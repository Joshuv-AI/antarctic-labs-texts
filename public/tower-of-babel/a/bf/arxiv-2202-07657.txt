Mobile impurity in a one-dimensional gas at finite temperatures
Oleksandr Gamayun, Miłosz Panfil, Felipe Taha Sant’Ana

Abstract
We consider the McGuire model of a one-dimensional gas of free fermions interacting with a single impurity. We compute the static one-body function and momentum distribution of the impurity at finite temperatures. The results involve averages over Fredholm determinants that we further analyse using the effective form factors approach. With this approach, we derive the large-distance behaviour of the one-body function, which takes the form of an averaged exponential decay. This method allows us to study an experimentally important regime of small momenta of the impurity’s momentum distribution. We also consider the one-body function at short distances and compute finite temperature Tan’s contact.

Introduction

Mobile impurities are ubiquitous in physical systems and are usually referred as polarons
lan 1965

Devreese and Alexandrov 2009

C. G. Kuper and G. D. Whitfield
(1963) eds

Recent years witness increase of the interest in mobile impurities due to the advances in fabrications and manipulations of systems of cold atoms

The mobile impurities appear there either in imbalanced mixtures of two different gases

;

;
Kohstall et al. 2012

;

To theoretically explore the properties of a quantum particle propagating in a one-dimensional medium different schemes and approximations were developed. For instance, various mean-field approaches

;

;

;

.
Finally, the properties of the polaron can be addressed by the Monte Carlo methods

In addition to numerical methods truly non-perturbative treatment of one-dimensional quantum system can be achieved for models that are solvable
by Bethe Ansatz methods.
An example of such model is the McGuire model
McGuire 1965
;
McGuire 1966

that describes a spin down particle interacting with the gas of spin up particles via the contact interaction. This model represents a specific sector of the fermionic Yang-Gaudin model

Gaudin 1967
;
Yang 1967
. Contrary to generic models the wave functions of the McGuire model can be written as a single determinant resembling the Slater determinant for the free Fermi
gas
Edwards 1990
;
Castella and Zotos 1993
. This allows one to find exact analytic expressions for the various physical quantities in the thermodynamic limit. In addition to the simplest correlation functions and the effective mass computed already by McGuire in Refs.
McGuire 1965
;
McGuire 1966
, one can compute the large time asymptotics of the average momentum of the impurity injected in the gas with some initial velocity

Gamayun et al. 2016

;
Gamayun et al. 2020

In this manuscript we consider impurity’s momentum distribution at finite temperature in the McGuire model.
It is computed as a Fourier transform of the one-body function.
We find that similar to
Gamayun et al. 2020
the answer in the thermodynamic limit can be expressed via the Fredholm determinants that additionally have to be integrated over
an additional degree of freedom related to the impurity’s momentum (the spin rapidity).
We also explore large distance asymptotic of these Fredholm determinants by employing the effective form factors methods
Gamayun et al. 2021
;
Zhuravlev et al. 2021
;
Chernowitz and Gamayun 2022
.
This allows us to find analytically the prefactor and the correlation length (before integration of the spin rapidity).
We also analytically compute Tan’s contact as a function of coupling constant and the the temperature.
In our derivations we never use the specific form of the thermal distribution and the same approach presented here can be used to compute the impurity’s correlation function for a system in generalized Gibbs ensemble, that could appear, for instance, after the quench protocol
De Nardis et al. 2014
.

II
The model and the correlation functions

The Hamiltonian of the McGuire model
McGuire 1965
;
McGuire 1966
is given by the following expression,

H=\frac{P_{\rm imp}^{2}}{2m}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{P_{j}^{2}}{2m}+g\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta(x_{j}-x_{\rm imp}).

(1)

The model describes a gas of spin up particles with momenta
P_{j}
and coordinates
x_{j}
interacting with a single mobile impurity (the spin down particle), with the momentum
P_{\rm imp}
and the coordinate
X_{\rm imp}
.
The gas particles are assumed to be either fermions or, equivalently, the hardcore bosons.
The impurity-gas coupling strength
g
in the dimensionless form is

\gamma=\frac{mg}{\rho_{0}},

(2)

where
\rho_{0}=N/L
is the gas density. We also set
m=1
.
Introducing the creation
\psi_{k,\downarrow}^{+}
and annihilation operators for the impurity
\psi_{k,\downarrow}
one can formally write an impurity’s momentum distribution in an eigenstate
|\{k_{j}\},\Lambda\rangle
as

n(k;\{k_{j}\},\Lambda)=\langle\{k_{j}\},\Lambda|\psi_{k,\downarrow}^{+}\psi_{k,\downarrow}|\{k_{j}\},\Lambda\rangle.

(3)

The eigenstate
|\{k_{j}\},\Lambda\rangle
of the Hamiltonian (
1
) is specified by a set of rapidities
\{k_{j}\}
and
\Lambda
. For a system of length
L
with periodic boundary conditions the rapidities obey the Bethe equations

k_{j}=\frac{2\pi}{L}\left(n_{j}-\frac{\delta(k_{j})}{\pi}\right),\qquad j=1,\dots,N+1,

(4)

where quantum numbers
n_{j}
are integers and obey the Pauli principle. The phase shift is

\delta(k)=\frac{\pi}{2}-{\rm arctan}\left(\Lambda-\alpha k\right),\qquad\alpha=\frac{2\pi}{\gamma}.

(5)

The rapidity
\Lambda
, called the spin rapidity, can be fixed by specifying values of other integrals of motions in this model. Traditionally, we require that the total momentum given by

P(\{k_{j}\},\Lambda)=\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}k_{j},

(6)

is fixed, i.e.
P(\{k_{j}\},\Lambda)=Q
. The
\Lambda
dependence in (
6
) is implicit through
k_{j}
as solutions to the Bethe equations. Therefore, for given
Q
and the set of integers
one can resolve condition (
6
) and thus solve the Bethe equations. Notice, however, that sometimes there are no solutions, therefore not all sets of integers are allowed for a fixed total momentum. For a detailed description of the spectrum of the McGuire model we refer to
McGuire 1965
. Finally, the energy of a given state is

E(\{k_{j}\},\Lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}k_{j}^{2}.

(7)

The impurity’s momentum distribution is a Fourier transform of the static one-body function

\rho(x;\{k_{j}\},\Lambda)\equiv\langle\{k_{j}\},\Lambda|\psi_{\downarrow}^{\dagger}(x)\psi_{\downarrow}(0)|\{k_{j}\},\Lambda\rangle.

(8)

This function was computed and thoroughly analyzed in Refs.

Recher and Kohler 2012
;
Gamayun et al. 2020
.

The aim of our work is to compute and study the impurity one-body function at finite temperatures and in the thermodynamic limit,

\rho_{T}(x)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}{\rm Tr}\left(e^{-\beta H}\psi_{\downarrow}^{\dagger}(x)\psi_{\downarrow}(0)\right),

(9)

where
\mathcal{Z}={\rm Tr}\exp(-\beta H)
is the partition function.

To do so we need to characterize the eigenstates of the thermodynamically large system. In a finite system the Hilbert space is spanned by choices of quantum numbers
\{n_{j}\}
and
Q
. Equivalently, by rapidities
\{k_{j}\}
and
\Lambda
. In the thermodynamic limit,
N,L\rightarrow\infty
such that
N/L=\rho_{0}
, we introduce a density function
\rho_{\rm p}(k)
such that
L\rho_{\rm p}(k){\rm d}k
gives the number of rapidities in the range
[k,k+{\rm d}k]
. In fact much more convenient is to deal with the distribution
\sigma=\rho_{\rm p}/\rho_{\rm tot}
normalized by the total density in the
rapidities space. In our case an impurity disturbs the underlying gas only in the subleading in the system size order such that
\rho_{\rm tot}=1/2\pi+\mathcal{O}(1/L)
. Instead, the quantum number
Q
remains as a single parameter specifying, by fixing the momentum of the system, the impurity. The extensive part of the Gibbs free energy
\mathcal{F}
depends only on distribution
\sigma(k)
and is independent of
\Lambda
,

\mathcal{F}[\sigma,\Lambda]=L\mathcal{F}_{\rm th}[\sigma]+\mathcal{F}_{0}[\sigma,\Lambda]+{\rm const}+\mathcal{O}(1/L),

(10)

where
{\rm const}
stands for intensive contributions to the free energy independent of
\Lambda
. The derivation of this relation is presented in Appendix
A
. The expression for the free energy implies that the impurity affects the thermodynamics at the subleading, in the system size, level. On the other hand, the leading thermodynamics is this of a free fermions. Therefore, at the thermal equilibrium the density
\sigma(k)
is just the usual Fermi-Dirac distribution.

Let us denote

\rho(x;\sigma,\Lambda)=Z(\sigma,\Lambda)\lim_{\rm th}\rho(x;\{k_{j}\},\Lambda),

(11)

where
\{k_{j}\}
is such that in the thermodynamic limit it corresponds to the distribution
\sigma(k)
and
{Z(\sigma,\Lambda)=\partial Q/\partial\Lambda}
the Jacobian of transformation between the quantum number
Q
and rapidity
\Lambda
. It is introduced here for future convenience. Then, in view of the above discussion on the free energy, the thermal expectation value is

\rho_{T}(x)=\frac{\sum_{Q}e^{-\beta\mathcal{F}_{0}[\sigma,\Lambda]}Z(\sigma,\Lambda)^{-1}\rho(x;\sigma,\Lambda)}{\sum_{Q}e^{-\beta\mathcal{F}_{0}[\sigma,\Lambda]}}.

(12)

For the partition function we have then

\sum_{Q}e^{-\beta\mathcal{F}_{0}[\sigma,\Lambda]}=\frac{L}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}\Lambda e^{-\beta\mathcal{F}_{0}[\sigma,\Lambda]}Z(\sigma,\Lambda).

(13)

The detailed account of how to perform summation over the eigenstates can be found in
Gamayun et al. 2015
;
Gamayun et al. 2016
. All the integrals, unless explicitly specified otherwise, extend over the real line. Performing the same transformation in the numerator of the correlation function (
12
) we find

\rho_{T}(x)=\frac{\int{\rm d}\Lambda e^{-\beta\mathcal{F}_{0}[\sigma,\Lambda]}\rho(x;\sigma,\Lambda)}{\int{\rm d}\Lambda e^{-\beta\mathcal{F}_{0}[\sigma,\Lambda]}Z(\sigma,\Lambda)}.

(14)

Later in this section we show that the denominator is responsible for the correct normalization of the one-body function giving
\rho_{T}(0)=1
.

Eq. (
14
) is the main result of this section. It expresses normalized one-body function of the impurity as an averaged one-body function over the spin rapidity. The various contributions are weighted by the correlation energy
\mathcal{F}_{0}
.
We discuss now the ingredients of this formula.

The intensive contribution to the free energy, the correlation energy, according to the derivation presented in Appendix
A
is

\mathcal{F}_{0}[\sigma,\Lambda]=-4\int\frac{{\rm d}k}{2\pi}\;k\sigma(k)\delta(k)\\
=-4\int\frac{{\rm d}k}{2\pi}\,k\left[\frac{\pi}{2}-\arctan\left(\Lambda-\alpha k\right)\right]\sigma(k),

(15)

where
\sigma(k)
is a Fermi-Dirac distribution, which we choose to parametrize as follows

\sigma(k)=\frac{1}{1+e^{\beta(k^{2}-\mu)}}.

(16)

An analogous expression for the correlation energy
\mathcal{F}_{0}
, for a lattice model, was also derived in Ref.
Castella et al. 1995
.

The expression for
\rho(x;\sigma,\Lambda)
can be deduced from the finite
N
results found in Ref.
Gamayun et al. 2020
.
There it was expressed in the forms of determinants and valid for any set of the momenta that specify the eigenstate.
Furthermore, the ensemble average can be replaced by the average in the typical state
Korepin et al. 1993
;
Panfil and Caux 2014
(see also appendix A in
Gamayun et al. 2016
).
In the thermodynamic limit this results in the Fredholm determinants with kernels multiplied by the Fermi distribution, namely we can present

\rho(x;\sigma,\Lambda)=\det\left(1+\sigma\hat{K}+\sigma\hat{W}\right)-\det\left(1+\sigma\hat{K}\right),

(17)

where operators act on
L^{2}(\mathds{R})
via the convolution, for example

\sigma\hat{K}u(q)=\sigma(q)\int dq^{\prime}K(q,q^{\prime})u(q^{\prime}).

(18)

The explicit form of the kernels can be found in
Gamayun et al. 2020
,

\displaystyle\hat{K}(q,q^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{e_{+}(q)e_{-}(q^{\prime})-e_{-}(q)e_{+}(q^{\prime})}{q-q^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\hat{W}(q,q^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}e_{-}(q)e_{-}(q^{\prime}),

(19)

\displaystyle e_{+}(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}e^{iqx/2+i\delta(q)},\quad e_{-}(q)=e^{-iqx/2}\sin\delta(q).

One can make kernels in (
17
) symmetric, that is
\sigma\hat{K}\to\sqrt{\sigma}\hat{K}\sqrt{\sigma}
and similarly for
\hat{W}
, by the conjugation with diagonal matrices.
The Fredholm determinants can be evaluated numerically by using methods from Ref.
Bornemann 2009
. Finally, we can evaluate the Jacobian by computing
\rho(0;\sigma,\Lambda)
by using an observation that at
x=0
the kernels
become rank-one operators.
This way, we obtain

Z(\sigma,\Lambda)={\rm Tr}\left[\sigma\hat{W}\right]=\int\frac{dk}{\pi}\frac{\sigma(k)}{1+(\alpha k-\Lambda)^{2}}.

(20)

Another justification of this formula comes directly from the formal form of the Jacobian
Z=\partial Q/\partial\Lambda
. Indeed, we can present it as
Z(\sigma,\Lambda)=-\partial_{\Lambda}\int dk\sigma(k)\delta(k)/\pi
, which is a derivative of
\Lambda
dependent part of the total momentum (
6
). We also note that
\rho(x;\sigma,-\Lambda)
is a complex function such that
\rho(x;\sigma,-\Lambda)=\rho^{*}(x;\sigma,\Lambda)
. Therefore the resulting one-body function
\rho_{T}(x)
is a real function.

In the zero temperature limit, the contributions to the integrals in (
14
) localize at the minimum of the correlation energy, that is at
\Lambda=0
. In the same time the Fermi-Dirac distribution becomes the ground state distribution and
\rho_{T=0}(x)
is given by the expectation value in the ground state of the McGuire model.

Finally, formula (
14
) expresses the finite temperature correlation function through averaging over correlation functions in different impurity states labelled by
\Lambda
. Different contributions are weighted with the correlation energy
\mathcal{F}_{0}[\sigma,\Lambda]
. The derivation of this formula presented here does not rely on the specific correlation functions and therefore this structure is universal for finite temperature impurity correlation functions. A relevant example, and a generalization of the static case considered here, is the finite temperature dynamic one-body function
\rho_{T}(x,t)
which then involves averaging over
\rho(x,t;\sigma,\Lambda)
given by straightforward adaptations of (
8
) and (
11
).

Effective form-factors and long distance asymptotic

\varphi(q)
in the complex plane for
\alpha=1
,
\mu=1
, and
\beta=1
. In the
|\Lambda|>\Lambda_{c}
case,
\varphi(q)
does not encircle the origin and therefore its winding number is
w=0
. Instead, for
|\Lambda|<\Lambda_{c}
,
\varphi(q)
winds once around the origin. On the right panel, we plot

.

In this section, we study the asymptotic expansion of the one-body function at large distances. The kernel
\hat{K}
in (
17
)
is nothing but a generalized sine-kernel and the asymptotic of
\det(1+\sigma\hat{K})
can be found by solving the corresponding Riemann-Hilbert problem (RHP)
Kitanine et al. 2009
;
Slavnov 2010
.
In principle, accounting for the
\hat{W}
can be also done via RHP, however, this approach is technically involved and we prefer to employ instead recently developed heuristic methods of the effective form factors

Gamayun et al. 2021
;
Zhuravlev et al. 2021
;
Chernowitz and Gamayun 2022
.
In this approach an exact form factor (spectral) series that describes the correlation function at finite temperature and that in the thermodynamic limit leads to
(
17
) is replaced with the effective one. The effective series formally corresponds to the zero temperature but the form factors depend on an effective phase shift in which information about the distribution
\sigma
is contained. To find this phase shift we perform summation of the effective form factor series and again express the result as Fredholm determinants of the similar kind as the exact one. Asymptotically identifying the kernels we compute the desired effective phase shift.
The advantage of the effective form factor series lies in the fact that the corresponding Fredholm determinants turn out to be an elementary functions, or integrals of elementary functions, thus, allowing us to find the desired asymptotic expression for the exact correlation function.

More explicitly, the effective form factors
|\langle{\bf k}|{\bf q}\rangle|
are

|\langle{\bf k}|{\bf q}\rangle|^{2}=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N+1}\frac{2e^{g(k_{i})}\sin^{2}\pi\nu(k_{i})}{L+2\pi\nu^{\prime}(k_{i})}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N}\frac{2e^{-g(q_{i})}}{L}(\det D)^{2},

(21)

with

\det D\equiv\begin{vmatrix}\frac{1}{k_{1}-q_{1}}&\dots&\frac{1}{k_{N+1}-q_{1}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{1}{k_{1}-q_{N}}&\dots&\frac{1}{k_{N+1}-q_{N}}\\
1&\dots&1\\
\end{vmatrix}.

(22)

Here
g(k)
and
\nu(k)
are arbitrary smooth functions that we are going to specify below. The momenta
{\bf k}=\{k_{i}\}
and
{\bf q}=\{q_{i}\}
are understood as solutions of

e^{ikL}=e^{-2\pi i\nu(k)},\qquad e^{iqL}=1,

(23)

respectively. They can be parametrized in terms of the quantum numbers as

\displaystyle k_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{L}\left(n^{(k)}_{j}-\nu(k_{j})\right),\quad j=1,\dots,N+1,

(24)

\displaystyle q_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{L}n^{(q)}_{j},\quad j=1,\dots,N.

(25)

In principle we could formulate the problem for any set of
\{k_{i}\}
. For the impurity problem the relevant scenario is when
\{k_{i}\}
takes the Fermi sea configuration at zero temperature. That is, the corresponding quantum numbers are

n^{(k)}_{j}=-\frac{N}{2}+j,\quad j=1,\dots,N+1.

(26)

The tau function of interest is defined as

\tau_{N}(x)=\sum_{\bf q}|\langle{\bf k}|{\bf q}\rangle|^{2}e^{-ix(P({\bf k})-P({\bf q}))},

(27)

where the summation extends over possible values of
\{q_{j}\}
or possible quantum numbers
\{n_{j}^{(q)}\}
keeping in mind the Pauli principle. The total momentum
P({\bf k})=\sum k_{j}
. The summation in (
27
) can be performed exactly, using a slight variation of the Cauchy–Binet formula
Shafarevich and Remizov 2013
and the result is

\tau_{N}(x)=\det_{N}(\delta_{ij}+A_{ij}+B_{ij})-\det_{N}(\delta_{ij}+A_{ij}),

(28)

with
A_{ij}=A(k_{i},k_{j})
and
B_{ij}=B(k_{i},k_{j})
where

\displaystyle A(q,q^{\prime})=

\displaystyle-\frac{e(q)-e(q^{\prime})}{q-q^{\prime}}B(q,q^{\prime}),

(29)

\displaystyle B(q,q^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{L}\exp\left({\frac{g(q)+g(q^{\prime})-ix(q+q^{\prime})}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\sin\pi\nu(q)\sin\pi\nu(q^{\prime}),

and

e(k)=\frac{2ie^{ikx-g(k)}}{e^{-2\pi i\nu(k)}-1}+\int\frac{dq}{\pi}\frac{e^{iqx-g(q)}}{k-q-i0}.

(30)

In the thermodynamic limit,
N,L\rightarrow\infty
with
N/L
fixed the determinants turn into Fredholm determinants acting on
L^{2}([-q_{F},q_{F}])
,

\tau(x)=\lim_{\rm th}\tau_{N}(x)=\det(1+A+B)-\det(1+A).

(31)

where
q_{F}=\pi N/L
is the Fermi momentum of the auxiliary problem.

We consider now the asymptotic expansion,
x\rightarrow\infty
. The integral in
e(k)
is then exponentially suppressed and

e(k)\approx\frac{2ie^{ikx-g(k)}}{e^{-2\pi i\nu(k)}-1},

(32)

with corrections exponentially small in
x
. Within this approximation the kernels can be presented in the following form

\displaystyle A(q,q^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\frac{E_{+}(q)E_{-}(q^{\prime})-E_{-}(q)E_{+}(q^{\prime})}{q-q^{\prime}},

(33)

\displaystyle B(q,q^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}E_{-}(q)E_{-}(q^{\prime}),

where

\displaystyle E_{+}(q)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}e^{iqx/2+i\pi\nu(q)-g(q)/2},

(34)

\displaystyle E_{-}(q)=

\displaystyle e^{-iqx/2+g(q)/2}\sin\pi\nu(q).

We now compare this asymptotic structure for
\tau(x)
with formula (
17
) for
\rho(x;\sigma,\Lambda)
. Recall that in
\tau(x)
functions
g(k)
and
\nu(k)
are arbitrary smooth functions. The two expressions match if
g(q)
and
\nu(q)
obey the following relations

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sigma(q)}

\displaystyle\sin\delta(q)=e^{g(q)/2}\sin\pi\nu(q),

(35)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sigma(q)}

\displaystyle e^{i\delta(q)}=e^{i\pi\nu(q)-g(q)/2},

which gives

\displaystyle\nu(q)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\ln\varphi(q),

(36)

\displaystyle g(q)=

\displaystyle\ln\varphi(q)-\ln\sigma(q)-2i\delta(q),

where

\varphi(q)\equiv 1+(e^{2i\delta(q)}-1)\sigma(q)=1+\frac{2i\sigma(q)}{\Lambda-\alpha q-i}.

(37)

With such chosen
\nu(k)
and
g(k)
we have

\rho(x;\sigma,\Lambda)\sim\lim_{q_{F}\rightarrow\infty}\tau(x),\quad{\rm as\;\;}x\rightarrow\infty.

(38)

On the other hand
\tau_{N}(x)
function has a spectral representation. We shall see that for
q_{F}\to\infty
, only small subset
of the spectral (form factor) sums matters, thus allowing us to find
\tau(x)
exactly, and in this way to understand the asymptotic expansion of
\rho(x;\sigma,\Lambda)
.

For the further understanding of the spectral series we discuss function
\nu(k)
.
This function enters the expression for the form factor and, more importantly, the rapidities
k_{j}
.
As we shall see its analytic properties of
\nu(q)
are determining for the form factor summation. This function, besides explicit dependence on
q
depends on all the parameters in the problem. Through
\delta(q)\equiv\delta(q;\Lambda,\alpha)
it depends on the coupling parameter
\alpha
and on the impurity rapidity
\Lambda
. It also depends on the thermodynamic properties of the system through the filling function
\sigma(q)
. At the thermal equilibrium these are the temperature
T
and the chemical potential
\mu
. The analytic properties of
\nu(q)
are determined by analytic properties of the complex logarithm, which is defined on a Riemann surface spiraling around the origin of the complex plane. On this surface
\nu(q)
is continuous but depends on the number of windings around the origin. More precisely, the plot of the
\varphi(q)
(the argument of the logarithm see (
36
), (
37
)) for all available
q
forms a loop with origin at
z=1
in the complex plane, see Fig. (
1
).
Whether the loop winds around the origin depends on the parameters of the system which we now inspect. The loop extends over the origin when, for some value
q_{*}
,
\varphi(q_{*})
has negative real part and zero imaginary part. This gives two conditions

\tanh(\beta\epsilon(q_{*})/2)<0,\quad\alpha q_{*}=\Lambda.

(39)

At the thermal equilibrium
\epsilon(q)=q^{2}-\mu
which leads to two cases, formulated in terms of
\Lambda
,

\displaystyle|\Lambda|\leq\Lambda_{c}:\quad{\rm winding}\,w=1

(40)

\displaystyle|\Lambda|>\Lambda_{c}:\quad{\rm winding}\,w=0,

(41)

where

\Lambda_{c}\equiv\alpha\sqrt{\mu}.

(42)

The winding number
w
is defined as (see Fig. (
1
))

w=\int dq\partial_{q}\nu(q)=\nu(\infty)-\nu(-\infty)

(43)

Note that the imaginary part of
\nu(q)
has always asymptote
0
as
q\to\pm\infty
.

Function
\nu(q)
enters the expression for the form factor through
\exp(i\pi\nu(q))
and therefore the non-zero winding has little effect on it. On the other hand
\nu(q)
enters directly the expression for rapidities
k_{j}
and affects the structure of the relevant excitations in the spectral sum of
\tau(x)
.
Namely, intuitively it is clear that the largest contributions come from those
q_{j}
whose quantum numbers are identical to
k_{j}
. In the non-winding case we cannot achieve this for all
q_{j}
, simply because the number of
k_{j}
in the set
|\textbf{k}\rangle
is larger by one that possible
q_{j}
in
|\textbf{q}\rangle
. So there will be at least one-hole as we demonstrate in Fig. (
2
a). In principle in the spectral sum there also different excitations, involving neccesarily particle-hole pairs. We will argue later that such excitations do not contribute in the limit
q_{F}\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore in the non-winding region
|\Lambda|>\Lambda_{c}
we obtain the exact expression for
\tau(x)
and asymptotic for
\rho(x;\sigma,\Lambda)

\rho(x;\sigma,\Lambda)\approx 2J(x)A(\Lambda)\exp\left(-ix\int k\nu^{\prime}(k){\rm d}k\right),

(44)

where

\ln A(\Lambda)=\int g(k)\nu^{\prime}(k)dk-\ln 2\\
+\int dq\int dp\,\nu^{\prime}(p)\nu^{\prime}(q)\ln|q-p|.

(45)

and

J(x)=\int\frac{dq}{\pi}\frac{\sigma(q)e^{-iqx}}{(\alpha q-\Lambda)^{2}+1}\exp\left(\fint dp\frac{2\nu(p)}{q-p}\right).

(46)

The appearance of the integral
J(x)
reflects the fact that we have to sum over the hole positions. Detailed derivation of this identity is given in Appendix
B.1
.
One can further analyze
J(x)
asymptotically by the saddle point method, which is done in Appendix
C
. For practical purposes, however, we leave it in the integral form.

If winding
w=1
then the state
|{\bf k}\rangle
is effectively compressed, see again Fig. (
2
b),
and contrary to the non-winding case, there is no extra space for a hole in the state
|{\bf q}\rangle
. The possible excitations are then only particle-hole excitations which we again can neglect in view of the
q_{F}\rightarrow\infty
limit. So in fact the sum (
31
) reduces to one term, which we evaluate in Appendix
B.2
. The asymptotic for
|\Lambda|<\Lambda_{c}

is

\rho(x;\sigma,\Lambda)=A(\Lambda)\exp\left(-ix\int k\nu^{\prime}(k){\rm d}k\right),

(47)

Here
A(\Lambda)
is given by the Eq. (
45
).

We discuss now the irrelevance of other excited states in the spectral sum of
\tau(x)
for
q_{F}\rightarrow\infty
. The other excited states necessarily involve particle-hole excitations. Given the Fermi sea structure of
|{\bf q}\rangle
the rapiditiy of the particle excitation is
|k|>q_{F}
. Therefore in the limit
q_{F}\rightarrow\infty
there is no space for such excitations and they do not contribute to the spectral sum. The formal proof of this argument, in the context of the XY spin chain can be found in
Gamayun et al. 2021
. Alternatively, one can numerically check that the result of our summation (Eqs. (
44
), (
47
)) is identical to the Fredholm determinants (
31
) for any
x
.

It might look as if the asymptotics (
44
) and (
47
) are discontinuous as function of
\Lambda
.
This is apparent and connected with the fact that the function
\nu(q)
behaves very differently for different winding numbers. In Appendix
C

we prove that while changing
\Lambda
over
\Lambda_{c}
one expression smoothly transforms into the other one.
This is somewhat similar to the gap dependence of the finite temperature correlation functions during the crossover over the critical point.
For the one-dimensional Ising model this can be seen in Ref.
Sachdev 1996
.

In Fig. (
3
) we compare the one-body function
\rho_{T}(x)
computed according to (
14
) with exact Fredholm determinant expression for
\rho(x;\sigma,\Lambda)
of Eq. (
17
), referred to as
\rho_{ex}
, with the results effective form-factors given by Eqs. (
44
) and (
47
), referred to as
\rho_{eff}
.
We see that the deviation happens only at small
x
and is less for smaller
\beta
(higher temperatures). The reason for this is that the effective form factors gives only the leading contribution with the smallest decay rate. At small temperatures, both leading and subleading decay rates become small so the latter cannot be ignored anymore. At exactly zero temperature all decay rates vanish and the correlation function has a
power law behavior with the separation distance
Gamayun et al. 2020
.

IV
Momentum distribution and Tan’s contact

The momentum distribution function of the impurity
n_{\rm imp}(k)
can be evaluated from the one-body function through its Fourier transform, which for the real and symmetric
\rho_{T}(x)
takes the following form

n_{\rm imp}(k)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}x}{\pi}\cos(kx)\rho_{T}(x).

(48)

In Fig.
4
we show the results for different temperatures and coupling constants. We compare the exact results with the results obtained from the effective form factors after
normalizing it such that for
k=0
values of the impurity’s distributions coincide.
We see that at small momenta this two distributions are almost identical. This reflects the fact that the effective description introduced in the previous section decently approximates the exact
\rho_{T}(x)
away from the origin.

The short distance expansion (for
{\mu x\ll 1}
) is however not captured by the effective form factors. For the momentum distribution
this behavior is responsible for the large
k
tails. Indeed, integrating by parts we see that the leading asymptotic expansion is governed by the
odd derivatives of
\rho_{T}(x)
and
x=0
. One can explicitly demonstrate that the first derivative at zero vanishes, so the leading asymptotics is giving by the third derivative, which gives rise to the famous

k^{-4}
decay

n_{\rm imp}(k)\approx\frac{\partial_{x}^{3}\rho_{T}}{\pi k^{4}}\Big|_{x=0}\equiv\frac{C(g,\beta)}{k^{4}}.

(49)

The constant
C(g,\beta)
is called the Tan’s contact and is related to the thermodynamic properties of the system
Tan 2008
;
Barth and Zwerger 2011
.
Such a constant was obtained in Ref.
Gamayun et al. 2020
by means of Taylor series of the kernels around
x=0
, rendering them into finite-rank expressions.
Expanding this argument by linearity we obtain that, at finite temperatures, the Tan’s contact reads

C(g,\beta)=\frac{\int d\Lambda e^{-\beta\mathcal{F}_{0}(\Lambda)}\left[S_{0}(\Lambda)S_{2}(\Lambda)-S_{1}(\Lambda)^{2}\right]}{\pi\int d\Lambda e^{-\beta\mathcal{F}_{0}(\Lambda)}S_{0}(\Lambda)},

(50)

where

S_{n}(\Lambda)=\int\frac{dk}{\pi}\frac{k^{n}\sigma(k)}{(\alpha k-\Lambda)^{2}+1}.

(51)

One can check that the contact (
50
), following the general principles
Tan 2008
;
Barth and Zwerger 2011
, can be written as a derivative of a thermodynamic quantity over the coupling constant. Indeed,
taking into account

\partial_{\Lambda}\mathcal{F}_{0}=2S_{1},\,\,\partial_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}_{0}=-2S_{2},\,\,\partial_{\alpha}S_{0}+\partial_{\Lambda}S_{1}=0,

(52)

and after an integration by parts, one can arrive at

C(g,\beta)=\frac{1}{2\pi\beta}\partial_{\alpha}\ln\int d\Lambda e^{-\beta\mathcal{F}_{0}(\Lambda)}S_{0}(\Lambda).

(53)

We plot Eq. (
50
) in Fig. (
5
) for three different interaction regimes. We observe the increase of the contact with both the temperature and the interaction strength at least to very low temperatures.

Figure 5
:
Tan’s contact from Eq. (
50
) as a function of inverse temperature for different interaction strengths.

Recently, the finite temperature polaron’s behavior in three-dimensional gases were addressed with the help of varional methods
Liu et al. 2020a
;
Liu et al. 2020b
;
Hu and Liu 2022
. In particular,
the temperature dependence of the Tan’s contact was studied. The qualitative behavior found in these papers is different from ours, and at least partially explainable by different dimensionalities of the problems.
At the very specific point of the infinite coupling constant, i.e.
\alpha=0
, the Tan’s contact is proportional to the average kinetic energy of in the Fermi gas

C(g=\infty,\beta)=\int\frac{dk}{2\pi^{2}}k^{2}\sigma(k)=-\frac{\rm{Li}_{\frac{3}{2}}\left(-e^{\beta\mu}\right)}{4(\pi\beta)^{3/2}},

(54)

which makes its growth with the temperature manifest. Similar behavior for the Tan’s contact in a one-dimensional gas was also observed in
Doggen and Kinnunen 2013
.

V
Conclusions

In this work we tackled the problem of calculating the one-body correlation function of an interacting mobile impurity submerged in a free fermionic gas at arbitrary finite temperatures. We formulated the problem exactly in terms of Fredholm determinants and inspected its large distance asymptotics through the effective form factors approach. We found that the effective form-factors provide an efficient way in extracting the asymptotics of the Fredholm determinant circumventing the need of studying the technically very involved matrix Riemann-Hilbert problem.

We observed that depending on the value of the spin rapidity
\Lambda
the asymptotic behavior looks structurally very different. The reason for this is topological as for
|\Lambda|<\Lambda_{c}
, the effective phase shift has the winding number
1
, while for
|\Lambda|>\Lambda_{c}
the winding is absent. Similar situation happens of the Ising model
Sachdev 1996
or the XY spin chain
Gamayun et al. 2021
where the role of
\Lambda
is played by the magnetic field. Our asymptotics are still continuous when crossing over
\Lambda_{c}
as there are no quantum phase transitions in one-dimensional systems at zero temperature
Sachdev 2011
.

With the one-body correlation function computed we investigated the momentum distribution of the impurity. This is the quantity directly accessible in cold-atoms experiments
Kuhnle et al. 2010
;
Mukherjee et al. 2019
;
Hoinka et al. 2013
;
Stewart et al. 2010
;
Sagi et al. 2012
. We observed the characteristic narrowing of the curve with the decrease of temperature.
Finally, by performing the short distance expansion of the Fredholm determinants, we evaluated the Tan’s contact of the impurity and showed its growth with both the temperature and the interaction strength.

The next step would be to generalize this approach to the time-dependent case and access the spectral functions.
It would be interesting to analyze not only the Green’s function of the mobile impurity
Gamayun et al. 2015
;
Gamayun et al. 2016
but also
the case of a static impurity in a three-dimensional gas. Indeed, it is known that various spectral observables can be expressed via the Fredholm determinants with kernels very similar to ours
Schmidt et al. 2018
, that allows to their analysis with the effective form factor approach. The time-dependent case is important from the experimental point of view
Cetina et al. 2016
but also
for the capturing universal contributions of the highly excited states in the generic correlation functions in quantum one-dimensional systems, within the non-linear Luttinger liquid paradigm

Imambekov and Glazman 2009
;
Imambekov et al. 2012
;
Markhof et al. 2019
. For fixed
\Lambda
we expect an additional power law prefactor for large times
Chernowitz and Gamayun 2022
;
Zhuravlev et al. 2021
.
In the infinite coupling constant case we expect to recover the predicted logarithmic diffusion
Zvonarev et al. 2007
;
Zvonarev et al. 2009
.

In this work we focused on thermal equilibrium as it is the most experimentally relevant case. However, the presented techniques do not rely on the gas distribution to be thermal and the presented results can be generalized to stationary non-equilibrium ensembles, such as the ones arising in the quench action
Caux 2016
or generalized hydrodynamics
Bastianello et al. 2022
.

Appendix A
Thermodynamics of the impurity

In this Appendix we evaluate the impurity dependent contribution to the free energy of the model. The main result is that the impurity affects only the intensive part of the free energy and the contribution comes purely from the energy. There is no impurity dependent contribution to the entropy. To derive this result we start by recalling the thermodynamic Bethe ansatz approach to the thermodynamics of integrable models.

A.1
Thermodynamic functions

In the thermodynamic limit,
N,L\rightarrow\infty
with
\rho_{0}=N/L
fixed. Following standard procedure
Korepin et al. 1993
we introduce function
k(x)
defined by the following relation

Lk(x)+2\delta(k(x))=2\pi Lx.

(55)

By the definition
k(n_{j}/L)=k_{j}
, see Bethe equations (
4
). It is customary to introduce two density functions. The total density
\rho_{\rm tot}(k)
and the particle density
\rho_{\rm p}(k)
. The former is defined through

\rho_{\rm tot}(k)=\frac{dx(k)}{dk},

(56)

and, including the subleading corrections in the system size, reads

\rho_{\rm tot,L}(k)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\rho_{\rm tot}(k)\left(1+\frac{2}{L}\partial_{k}\delta(k)\right).

(57)

The density of particles is defined as

\rho_{\rm p}(k_{j})=\lim_{\rm th}\frac{1}{L(k_{j+1}-k_{j})},

(58)

such that sum of over
k_{j}
becomes an integral over the density

\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j=1}^{N+1}f(k_{j})=\int{\rm d}k\rho_{\rm p}(k)f(k).

(59)

We derive now an expression for
\rho_{\rm p}(k)
, including
1/L
correction depending on
\Lambda
. To this end we consider again the sum and include corrections to
k_{j}
coming from the impurity. We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j=1}^{N+1}f(k_{j})=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j=1}^{N+1}f\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}n_{j}-\frac{2}{L}\delta_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j=1}^{N+1}\left(f\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}n_{j}\right)-\frac{2\delta_{j}}{L}f^{\prime}\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}n_{j}\right)\right).

(60)

Using that
\delta_{j}=\delta(k_{j})\approx\delta(2\pi n_{j}/L)
, we have

\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j=1}^{N+1}f(k_{j})=\int{\rm d}k\,\rho_{\rm p}(k)\left(f(k)-\frac{2}{L}\delta(k)f^{\prime}(k)\right)

(61)

We can now incorporate the
1/L
term as a correction to the particle density by integrating by parts. Neglecting here the boundary terms and obtain

\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j=1}^{N+1}f(k_{j})=\int{\rm d}k\,\rho_{\rm p,L}(k)f(k),

(62)

with

\rho_{\rm p,L}(k)=\rho_{\rm p}(k)+\frac{2}{L}\partial_{k}\left[\delta(k)\rho_{\rm p}(k)\right].

(63)

We will also need the filling function, which including the
1/L
correction, is

\sigma_{L}(k)=\frac{\rho_{\rm p,L}(k)}{\rho_{\rm tot,L}(k)}=\sigma(k)+\frac{2}{L}\delta(k)\left(\partial_{k}\sigma(k)\right).

(64)

Concluding, the states of the system in the thermodynamic limit are characterised by density functions which do not depend on the impurity. The dependence comes only in the subleading in the system size terms.

A.2
Thermal equilibrium

We consider now thermal equilibrium by minimising the free energy
F=E-TS
. Both energy and entropy have extensive parts, independent of the impurity, and intensive part which depends on it. There are also intensive parts that do not depend on the impurity. As we are interested in the physics of impurity, those can be neglected. As we shall see they don’t influence the saddle point distribution and lead only to a multiplicative constant for a partition function.

The energy (
7
), in the large system, becomes

E=LE_{\rm th}[\sigma]+E_{0}[\sigma,\Lambda]+\mathcal{O}(L^{0})

(65)

where

\displaystyle E_{\rm th}[\sigma]

\displaystyle=\int\frac{{\rm d}k}{2\pi}\,\sigma(k)\left(\frac{k^{2}}{2}-h\right),

(66)

\displaystyle E_{0}[\sigma,\Lambda]

\displaystyle=-2\int\frac{{\rm d}k}{2\pi}\,k\sigma(k)\delta(k).

(67)

We used here eq. (
7
) for the energy in a finite system and eq. (
62
) for
\rho_{\rm p,L}(k)
. We have also integrated by parts in
E_{0}[\rho_{\rm p},\Lambda]
.
The entropy has the same structure

S=LS_{\rm th}[\sigma]+S_{0}[\sigma,\Lambda]+\mathcal{O}(L^{0}),

(68)

where

\displaystyle S_{\rm th}[\rho_{\rm p},\rho_{\rm tot}]

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{{\rm d}k}{2\pi}\,G(\sigma(k)),

(69)

\displaystyle S_{0}[\sigma,\Lambda]

\displaystyle=0,

(70)

with

G(\sigma)=\sigma\ln\sigma+(1-\sigma)\ln(1-\sigma).

(71)

In the following we show that
S_{0}[\sigma,\Lambda]
, the subleading contribution depending on
\Lambda
, is zero and in the process we derive also the leading expression
S_{\rm th}[\rho_{\rm p},\rho_{\rm tot}]
.

Derivation of eq. (
68
):

The entropy density
dS(k)
in the interval
[k,k+{\rm d}k]
is
Korepin et al. 1993

\displaystyle dS(k)=\ln\left(\frac{\left[L\rho_{\rm tot,L}(k)dk\right]!}{\left[L\rho_{\rm p,L}(k)dk\right]!\left[L\rho_{\rm h,L}(k)dk\right]!}\right),

(72)

with the total entropy
S=\int{\rm d}S(k)
and with the holes density
\rho_{\rm h,L}(k)
defined as

\rho_{\rm h,L}(k)=\rho_{\rm tot,L}(k)-\rho_{\rm p,L}(k)

(73)

Using Stirling approximation for the factorial,
\ln n!\approx n\ln n-n+\dots
, we find

\displaystyle{\rm d}S(k)=-L\rho_{\rm tot,L}(k)G(\sigma_{L}(k)){\rm d}k,

(74)

from which
s_{\rm th}[\rho_{\rm p},\rho_{\rm tot}]
follows as the leading term in
L
.
We expand now
G(\sigma_{L}(k))
in
L
, the first two orders are

G(\sigma_{L}(k))=G(\sigma(k))+\frac{2}{L}\delta(k)\left(\partial_{k}G(\sigma(k))\right).

(75)

Using now the expression for
\rho_{\rm tot,L}(k)
, the subleading in system size contribution to
{\rm d}S(k)
is a total derivative with respect to
k
and therefore does not contribute to the total density. Therefore
S_{0}[\Lambda]=0
.

We consider now the free energy

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=L\mathcal{F}_{\rm th}+\mathcal{F}_{0}+\mathcal{O}(L^{0}),

(76)

where
\mathcal{F}_{0}
captures all system size independent contribution to the free energy that depends on
\Lambda
. According to the computation presented above

\mathcal{F}_{\rm th}=E_{\rm th}-TS_{\rm th},\qquad\mathcal{F}_{0}=E_{0}(\Lambda).

(77)

Therefore, in the thermodynamic limit, the saddle point configuration comes from minimising
(E_{\rm th}-TS_{\rm th})
which leads to the Fermi-Dirac distribution

\sigma(k)=\frac{1}{1+e^{\epsilon(k)}},\qquad\epsilon(k)=\frac{\frac{k^{2}}{2}-h}{T}.

(78)

The subleading contribution to the free energy, the correlation energy, is then

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{0}=-2\int\frac{{\rm d}k}{2\pi}\,k\sigma(k)\delta(k).

(79)

For further convenience we will redefine the temperature and the chemical potential such that
\epsilon(k)
takes the “standard” form

\epsilon(k)=\beta(k^{2}-\mu).

(80)

This rescaling of temperature affects the contribution from the correlation energy (
79
) which leads to formula (
15
) of the main text.

Appendix B
Thermodynamic limit of the form factors and spectral series

B.1
Non-winding case
w=0

In this appendix we study contribution of the single hole excitations to the sum (
27
).
Namely, let us denote the position of the hole by
h
and the corresponding set of
q
’s by
{\bf q}_{h}
, see Fig. (
2
a). By
{\bf\bar{q}}
we denote the set
{\bf q}_{h}
with the hole filled in.
Notice that the determinant in (
22
) can be presented as

\det D=\frac{\prod_{i>j}^{N+1}(k_{i}-k_{j})\prod_{i>j}^{N}(q_{i}-q_{j})}{\prod_{i=1}^{N+1}\prod_{j=1}^{N}(k_{i}-q_{j})},

(81)

so the effective form factor (
21
) reads

\frac{|\langle{\bf k}|{\bf q}_{h}\rangle|^{2}}{\widetilde{|\langle{\bf k}|{\bf\bar{q}}\rangle|}^{2}}=\frac{L}{2}e^{g(q_{h})}\left(\frac{2\pi\nu_{h}}{L}\right)^{2}\prod_{j\neq h}\left(\frac{k_{j}-q_{h}}{q_{j}-q_{h}}\right)^{2},

(82)

where we have introduced

\widetilde{|\langle{\bf k}|{\bf\bar{q}}\rangle|}^{2}=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N+1}\frac{4e^{g(k_{i})-g(q_{i})}\sin^{2}\pi\nu(k_{i})}{L^{2}(1+\frac{2\pi}{L}\nu^{\prime}(k_{i}))}\left(\det_{N+1}\frac{1}{k_{i}-q_{j}}\right)^{2},

(83)

which is the bulk contribution independent of the hole position.
First let us perform the summation over
h
. To this end we approximate the discrete product from
|\langle{\bf k}|{\bf q}_{h}\rangle|^{2}
in the following way

\displaystyle\prod_{j\neq h}\left(\frac{k_{j}-q_{h}}{q_{j}-q_{h}}\right)^{2}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=-M\\
j\neq h\end{subarray}}^{M}\left(1+\frac{\nu_{j}}{h-j}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=-M\\
j\neq h\end{subarray}}^{M}\left(1+\frac{\nu_{j}-\nu_{h}}{h-j+\nu_{h}}\right)^{2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=-M\\
j\neq h\end{subarray}}^{M}\left(1+\frac{\nu_{h}}{h-j}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=-M\\
j\neq h\end{subarray}}^{M}\left(1+\frac{\nu_{j}-\nu_{h}}{h-j}\right)^{2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=-M\\
j\neq h\end{subarray}}^{M}\left(1+\frac{\nu_{h}}{h-j}\right)^{2}.

(84)

In going to the third line we neglected
\nu_{h}
in the denominator. For
h-j
large it gives a subleading correction, whereas for
h-j
small the whole fraction vanishes. The first product turns then in the thermodynamic limit into an integral. The second product instead can be rewritten in terms of the
\Gamma
functions. The result is

\prod_{j\neq h}\left(\frac{k_{j}-q_{h}}{q_{j}-q_{h}}\right)^{2}\approx\left(\frac{\sin\pi\nu_{h}}{\pi\nu_{h}}\right)^{2}\exp\left(-2\int dp\frac{\nu(p)-\nu(q_{h})}{p-q_{h}}\right)\left(\frac{\Gamma(M-h-\nu_{h}+1)}{\Gamma(M-h+1)}\frac{\Gamma(M+h+\nu_{h}+1)}{\Gamma(M+h+1)}\right)^{2}.

(85)

The integration is over the range
[-q_{F},q_{F}]
but we have already taken
q_{F}\rightarrow\infty
limit in this part. Moreover, understanding the integral in the principal value sense, we obtain

\frac{|\langle{\bf k}|{\bf q}_{h}\rangle|^{2}}{\widetilde{|\langle{\bf k}|{\bf\bar{q}}\rangle|}^{2}}\approx\frac{2}{L}e^{g(q_{h})}\sin^{2}(\pi\nu_{h})\exp\left(\fint dp\frac{2\nu(p)}{q_{h}-p}\right)\\
\times\left(\frac{\Gamma(M-h-\nu_{h}+1)}{\Gamma(M-h+1)}\frac{\Gamma(M+h+\nu_{h}+1)}{\Gamma(M+h+1)}\right)^{2}.

(86)

It is important that this ratio is
O(1/L)
for all
h
, therefore in the summation we can consider only bulk contributions where the hole is far from the edges. That is
M\gg h\gg 1
, which implies that in the leading order the ratio of the
\Gamma
-functions is
1
. We then have

|\langle{\bf k}|{\bf q}_{h}\rangle|^{2}\approx\frac{2}{L}\widetilde{|\langle{\bf k}|{\bf{\bf q}}\rangle|}^{2}\sigma(q_{h})\sin^{2}(\delta(q_{h}))\Phi(q_{h})

(87)

where we used (
35
) to rewrite the formula in terms of
\delta(q)
and
\sigma(q)
instead of
g(q)
and
\nu(q)
, and denoted

\Phi(q_{h})=\exp\left(\fint dp\frac{2\nu(p)}{q_{h}-p}\right),

(88)

This way, the tau-function on the one-hole states reads

\tau(x)=\widetilde{|\langle{\bf k}|{\bf\bar{q}}\rangle|}^{2}\frac{2}{L}\sum_{q}\sigma(q)\sin^{2}(\delta(q))\times\\
\times\Phi(q)\exp\left(ix\int\nu(p){\rm d}p-ixq\right).

(89)

The sum over
q
in can be rewritten as an integral

J(x)=\int\frac{dq}{\pi}\sigma(q)\sin^{2}(\delta(q))\Phi(q)e^{-ixq},

(90)

and the overall factor
\widetilde{|\langle{\bf k}|{\bf\bar{q}}\rangle|}^{2}
, in the thermodynamic and
{q_{F}\rightarrow\infty}
limits, can be evaluated, for instance, as in Ref.
Chernowitz and Gamayun 2022
.
This leads to the final answer

\tau(x)=J(x)\exp\left(ix\int\nu(p)dp-\int\nu(q)g^{\prime}(q)dq\right)\times\\
\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int dkdq\left(\frac{\nu(k)-\nu(q)}{k-q}\right)^{2}\right).

(91)

Integrating by parts we arrive at the expression (
44
).
We compare the exact the Fredholm determinants and asymptotics in Fig. (
6
).
We see that for chosen typical parameters it is almost impossible to distinguish the exact and approximate expressions.

values,

\Lambda=1/2
- the winding region, and
\Lambda=2
- the non-winding region. Notice that the critical value of the spin rapidity for such parameters is
\Lambda_{c}=1
.

\rho_{\rm ex}
refers to the exact formulation in terms of Fredholm determinants and is shown in solid lines, while
\rho_{\rm eff}
refers to effective form-factors calculation and is shown with dots.

B.2
Winding case
w=1

If winding
w=1
then the state
|{\bf k}\rangle
is effectively compressed, see Fig. (
2
b).
and contrary to the non-winding case, there is no extra space for a hole in the state
|{\bf q}\rangle
. The possible excitations are then only particle-hole excitations which we neglect in view of the
q_{F}\rightarrow\infty
limit, as discussed in the main text. Therefore in the spectral sum there is only one state contributing

\rho(x,\Lambda)=|\langle{\bf k}|{\bf q}\rangle|^{2}\exp\left(-ix\int k\nu^{\prime}(k){\rm d}k\right),

(92)

where states
|{\bf k}\rangle
and
|{\bf q}\rangle
are both defined with the ground state quantum numbers and

\displaystyle q_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{L}\left(-\frac{N-1}{2}+j-1\right),\qquad j=1,\dots N,

(93)

\displaystyle k_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{L}\left(-\frac{N-1}{2}+j-1-\nu_{j}\right),\qquad j=1,\dots N+1,

(94)

with
\nu_{1}\approx 0
and
\nu_{N+1}\approx 1
. We present the overlap
|\langle{\bf k}|{\bf q}\rangle|^{2}
as

|\langle{\bf k}|{\bf q}\rangle|^{2}=\mathcal{D}\exp\left(\int(g(k)-1)\nu^{\prime}(k)dk\right),

(95)

where we have defined

\mathcal{D}=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N+1}\frac{2\sin^{2}\pi\nu_{i}}{L}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N}\frac{2}{L}(\det D)^{2}.

(96)

Further we can present it as
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{0}\mathcal{F}
, where

\mathcal{D}_{0}=\frac{2}{L}\sin^{2}(\pi\nu_{m})\frac{\prod\limits_{j\neq m}^{N+1}(k_{m}-k_{j})^{2}}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{N}(k_{m}-q_{j})^{2}},

(97)

and

\mathcal{F}=\left(\det\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\frac{\sin(\pi\eta_{i})}{\pi(i-j-\eta_{i})}\right)^{2}.

(98)

In the expression for
\mathcal{F}
we use
\eta_{i}

\eta_{i}=\begin{cases}\nu_{i},\qquad&i\leq m\\
\nu_{i}-1,\qquad&i>m,\end{cases}

(99)

which has a jump at
i=m
. In doing so we trade the continuous non-zero winding function
\nu(q)
into discontinuous but zero winding function
\eta(q)
. It turns out the in computing the thermodynamic limit it is easier to deal with a discontinuous rather that non-zero winding functions. The point
m
of the discontinuity is chosen arbitrarily (but far from the edges) and the final answer does not depend on it.

First we evaluate
\mathcal{D}_{0}
assuming that
N\gg m\gg 1
and
N\gg L\gg 1
. We find

\mathcal{D}_{0}\approx\frac{2}{L}(N-m)^{2}\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi\nu_{m})}{\nu_{m}^{2}}\prod\limits_{j\neq m}^{N}\left(\frac{1-\frac{\nu_{m}-\nu_{j}}{m-j}}{1-\frac{\nu_{m}}{m-j}}\right)^{2}.

(100)

The numerator in the product can be evaluated as

\prod\limits_{j\neq m}^{N}\left(1-\frac{\nu_{m}-\nu_{j}}{m-j}\right)=\exp\left(-\int\limits_{-q_{F}}^{q_{F}}\frac{\nu(p_{m})-\nu(k)}{p_{m}-k}{\rm d}k\right),

(101)

here
q_{F}=\frac{\pi N}{L}
,
p_{m}=2\pi/L(-N/2+m)
and the phase shifts are defined in the usual way
\nu_{i}=\nu(k_{i})=\nu(2\pi/L(-N/2+i))
.
Integrating this expression by parts we obtain

\prod\limits_{j\neq m}^{N}\left(1-\frac{\nu_{m}-\nu_{j}}{m-j}\right)=\frac{1}{q_{F}-p_{m}}\left(\frac{q_{F}-p_{m}}{q_{F}+p_{m}}\right)^{\nu_{m}}\times\\
\exp\left(\int\limits_{-q_{F}}^{q_{F}}\nu^{\prime}(k)\ln|p_{m}-k|{\rm d}k\right).

(102)

Note that in the integral we can already send
q_{F}\to\infty
. The product in the denominator of
\mathcal{D}_{0}
can be evaluated explicitly and the result reads

\prod\limits_{j\neq m}^{N}\left(1-\frac{\nu_{m}}{m-j}\right)=\frac{\Gamma(N-m+1+\nu_{m})}{\Gamma(1+\nu_{m})\Gamma(N-m+1)}\times\\
\frac{\Gamma(m-\nu_{m})}{\Gamma(1-\nu_{m})\Gamma(m)}\approx\frac{\sin(\pi\nu_{m})}{\pi\nu_{m}}\left(\frac{N-m}{m}\right)^{\nu_{m}}.

(103)

This way we obtain

\mathcal{D}_{0}=\frac{L}{2}\exp\left(2\int\nu^{\prime}(k)\ln|p_{m}-k|{\rm d}k\right).

(104)

Further for simplicity we can put
p_{m}=0
, which means that
m\approx N/2
, so

\mathcal{D}_{0}=\frac{L}{2}\exp\left(2\int\nu^{\prime}(k)\ln|k|{\rm d}k\right).

(105)

To estimate
\mathcal{F}
we use the result listed in Appendix B in
Chernowitz and Gamayun 2022
. It states that if
function
\eta(q)
is discontinuous at
q=0
and the discontinuity is
\delta
, that is

\lim_{q\rightarrow 0^{+}}\eta(q)-\lim_{q\rightarrow 0^{-}}\eta(q)=\delta,

(106)

smooth everywhere else and vanishing fast enough at
q\rightarrow\pm\infty
(in our case this vanishing is exponential due to
\sigma(q)
), then

\mathcal{F}=\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{\delta^{2}}G(1-\delta)^{2}\left(\frac{2\pi}{e}\right)^{\delta}\exp\left(\int{\rm d}q\int{\rm d}p[\eta^{\prime}](q)[\eta^{\prime}](p)\ln|q-p|+2\delta\int[\eta^{\prime}](q)\ln|q|{\rm d}q\right),

(107)

where
G(x)
is Barnes Gamma function and
[\eta]^{\prime}(q)
is the piece-wise derivative of
\eta(q)
,

[\eta]^{\prime}(q)=\Theta(-q)\eta^{\prime}(q)+\Theta(q)\eta^{\prime}(q).

(108)

In particular, if
\eta(q)
can be described with the help of smooth function
\nu(q)
such that

\eta(q)=\nu(q)+\delta\,\Theta(q),

(109)

then
[\eta^{\prime}](q)=\nu^{\prime}(q)
. In our case
\delta=-1
and
\eta(q)=\nu(q)-\Theta(q)
. This leads to

\mathcal{F}=\frac{e}{L}\exp\left(-2\int\nu^{\prime}(q)\ln|q|{\rm d}q\right)\times\\
\exp\left(\int{\rm d}q\int{\rm d}p\,\nu^{\prime}(p)\nu^{\prime}(q)\ln|q-p|\right).

(110)

The final answer for
\mathcal{D}
is then

\mathcal{D}=\frac{e}{2}\exp\left(\int{\rm d}q\int{\rm d}p\,\nu^{\prime}(p)\nu^{\prime}(q)\ln|q-p|\right).

(111)

Combining this with the prefactor (
95
) we arrive at the expression (
47
).

We compare the exact the Fredholm determinants and asymptotics in Fig. (
6
).
We see that the asymptotic expression works decently even for small distancnes.

Appendix C
Further analysis of the asymptotics

In this appendix we analyse further the asymptotics of
\rho_{T}(x)
discussed in Section
III
. Specifically, we compute an asymptotic expansion of
J(x)
in the non-winding region. We then use this result to analyse the behavior of
\rho(x,\Lambda)
for
\Lambda\approx\Lambda_{c}
and show that
\rho(x,\Lambda)
is a continuous function of
\Lambda
.

C.1
Asymptotic expansion of
J(x)

In this section we perform asymptotic expansion of
J(x)
defined in eq. (
46
) which for convenience we repeat here

J(x)=\int\frac{dq}{\pi}\frac{\sigma(q)e^{-iqx}}{(\alpha q-\Lambda)^{2}+1}\exp\left(\fint dp\frac{2\nu(p)}{q-p}\right).

(112)

To extract the asymptotics we rewrite the integral as an integral over a closed semicircle contour
\mathcal{C}_{-}
in the lower part of the complex plane. The large
x
behavior of
J(x)
is then determined by the pole (with negative imaginary part) closest to the real axis. To this end we first rewrite the principal value integral as

\fint dp\frac{\nu(p)}{q-p}=\int dp\frac{\nu(p)}{q-p-i\epsilon}-i\pi\nu(q),

(113)

where
\epsilon>0
is a small number taken to zero at the end of the computations. This integral, as a function of
q
, has now a simple pole above the real axis, outside of the contour
\mathcal{C}_{-}
. We also define function
w(q)
through the following relation

\frac{1}{w(q)}=\frac{\sigma(q)e^{-2\pi i\nu(q)}}{\alpha q-\Lambda+i}.

(114)

The formula for
w(q)
can be simplified to

w(q)=(\alpha q-\Lambda+i)e^{\beta(q^{2}-\mu)}+\alpha q-\Lambda-i.

(115)

For
J(x)
we then have

J(x)=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{-}}\frac{dz}{\pi}\frac{e^{-izx}}{w(z)}\frac{\exp\left(\int\frac{2\nu(p)dp}{z-p-i\epsilon}\right)}{\alpha z-\Lambda-i}.

(116)

where the potential poles with the negative imaginary part come solely from
w(z)=0
. Let us denote by
z_{*}
the solution to this equation with the smallest negative imaginary part. In the vicinity of this solution,
w(z)\approx w^{\prime}(z_{*})(z-z_{*})+\mathcal{O}((z-z_{*})^{2})
,
where

w^{\prime}(z_{*})=2i\frac{\alpha+iz_{*}\beta(1+(z_{*}\alpha-\Lambda)^{2})}{\alpha z_{*}-\Lambda+i}.

(117)

Therefore, the leading asymptotic contribution to
J(x)
is

J(x)=-\frac{\exp\left(-iz_{*}x-2i\delta(z_{*})+2\int\frac{\nu(p)dp}{z_{*}-p}\right)}{\alpha+iz_{*}\beta(1+(z_{*}\alpha-\Lambda)^{2})},

(118)

with the exponential decay rate set by the imaginary part of
z_{*}
, which therefore has to be negative. Note that in writing the integral we took
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
limit because
z_{*}
has a negative imaginary part which makes the integral well defined, see Fig.
7
.

Figure 7
:
Solution to
w(z)=0
as a function of
\Lambda
for
\alpha=1
,
\beta=1
and
\mu=1
. The critical value is
\Lambda_{c}=1
. For
\Lambda=\pm\Lambda_{c}
the solution is real. For large values of
\Lambda
the solution approaches finite asymptotes
\pm\sqrt{\beta\mu\pm i\pi}/\sqrt{\beta}
. For
\Lambda>\Lambda_{c}
, the imaginary part of
z_{*}
is negative.

C.2
Vicinity of the threshold
\Lambda_{c}

The formula for
J(x)
derived above is valid for
{|\Lambda|>\Lambda_{c}}
. In the following we will analyse it for
\Lambda
close to
\Lambda_{c}
. We start by solving for the pole
z_{*}
.

We put
\Lambda=\Lambda_{c}+\delta
with
\delta>0
. To find the pole
z_{*}
we put
z=\sqrt{\mu}+\delta v
. This way, in the first order in
\delta
, we obtain

w(z)=2\delta(v(\alpha+i\beta\sqrt{\mu})-1)+O(\delta^{2}),

(119)

and the pole is indeed located in the lower half plane for
\Lambda>\Lambda_{c}
,

z_{*}=\sqrt{\mu}+\frac{\Lambda-\Lambda_{c}}{\alpha+i\beta\sqrt{\mu}}+O\left((\Lambda-\Lambda_{c})^{2}\right).

(120)

We can now substitute
z_{*}
to formula (
118
). The integral appearing there requires a separate treatment because
\nu(p)
has a logarithmic singularity for
p=\sqrt{\mu}
when
{\Lambda=\Lambda_{c}}
. We denote the integral

\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)=2\int\frac{\nu(p){\rm d}p}{z^{*}-p},

(121)

and in the following we will show that for
\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda_{c}^{+}
,

\mathcal{I}(\Lambda_{c}^{+})=\ln\left(\alpha+i\sqrt{\mu}\beta\right)+\mathcal{J}(\Lambda_{c}^{+}),

(122)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\Lambda)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}p\,\left(\nu^{\prime}(\sqrt{\mu}-p)+\nu^{\prime}(\sqrt{\mu}+p)\right)\ln p.

(123)

The logarithmic term in
\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)
exactly cancels the prefactor in
J(x)
and the limiting expression for
J(x)
when
\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda_{c}^{+}
is

\displaystyle J(x)=e^{-i\sqrt{\mu}x+\mathcal{J}(\Lambda)}.

(124)

We now derive eq. (
122
). For convenience we introduce
v_{*}=z_{*}-\sqrt{\mu}
and define
\bar{\nu}_{\Lambda}(p)=\nu_{\Lambda}(\sqrt{\mu}+p)
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)=2\int{\rm d}p\frac{\bar{\nu}(p)}{v_{*}-p}.

(125)

We rewrite
\mathcal{I}
by first integrating by parts and then write it as a sum of two integrals

\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)=2\pi i\bar{\nu}(0)+\mathcal{I}_{+}(\Lambda)+\mathcal{I}_{-}(\Lambda),

(126)

where

\mathcal{I}_{\pm}(\Lambda)=2\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}p\,\bar{\nu}^{\prime}(\pm p)\ln(p\mp v_{*}).

(127)

In writing the second contribution we used
\ln(-z)=\ln z-i\pi
.
We rewrite now
\mathcal{I}_{\pm}(\Lambda)
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\pm}(\Lambda)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}p\,(p\pm v_{*})\bar{\nu}^{\prime}(\mp p)\partial_{p}\left(\ln(p\pm v_{*})\right)^{2},

(128)

which, upon integrating by parts, leads to

\mathcal{I}_{\pm}(\Lambda)=\pm v_{*}\bar{\nu}^{\prime}(0)\left(\ln(\pm v_{*})\right)^{2}+\mathcal{J}_{\pm}(\Lambda),

(129)

where

\mathcal{J}_{\pm}(\Lambda)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}p\,\partial_{p}\left[(p\pm v_{*})\bar{\nu}^{\prime}(\mp p)\right]\left(\ln(p\pm v_{*})\right)^{2}.

(130)

Collecting the integrals under
\mathcal{J}(\Lambda)
and other terms under
\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)
we find

\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)=\mathcal{C}+\mathcal{J}(\Lambda),

(131)

where
\mathcal{J}(\Lambda)=\mathcal{J}_{+}(\Lambda)+\mathcal{J}_{-}(\Lambda)
and

\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)=2\pi i\bar{\nu}(0)+v_{*}\bar{\nu}^{\prime}(0)(\ln v_{*})^{2}-v_{*}\bar{\nu}^{\prime}(0)(\ln(-v_{*}))^{2}.

(132)

We consider now
\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda_{c}^{+}
for both expressions separately. For
\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)
we use that

\lim_{\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda_{c}}v_{*}\bar{\nu}(0)=-\frac{1}{2\pi i},

(133)

to find
\mathcal{C}(\Lambda_{c})=\ln\left(\alpha+i\sqrt{\mu}\beta\right)
in agreement with the first part of (
122
). In the integral in
\mathcal{J}_{\pm}
there is only a square logarithmic singularity, the first part of the integrand is regular for
p\rightarrow 0
with
\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda_{c}
. Such singularity is integrable and therefore limit
\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda_{c}
of
\mathcal{J}_{\pm}(\Lambda)
can be safely taken. The result is

\mathcal{J}_{\pm}(\Lambda)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}p\,\partial_{p}\left[p\bar{\nu}^{\prime}(\mp p)\right]\left(\ln p\right)^{2}.

(134)

Considering now
\mathcal{J}(\Lambda)=\mathcal{J}_{+}(\Lambda)+\mathcal{J}_{-}(\Lambda)
, we integrate back by parts and obtain (
123
). This finishes the derivation of eq. (
122
).

C.3
Continuity of the asymptotics

In this section we show that
\rho(x,\Lambda)
is continuous across
\Lambda_{c}
, namely

\lim_{\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda_{c}^{+}}\rho(x,\Lambda)=\lim_{\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda_{c}^{-}}\rho(x,\Lambda).

(135)

We start by recalling the relevant expressions for
\rho(x,\Lambda)
in the winding and non-winding regions from Section
III
of the main text. In the former, for
|\Lambda|<\Lambda_{c}

\rho(x,\Lambda)\sim A(\Lambda)\exp\left(-ix\int k\nu_{\Lambda}^{\prime}(k){\rm d}k\right),

(136)

with

\displaystyle\ln A(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=\int g(k)\nu_{\Lambda}^{\prime}(k)dk-\ln 2

\displaystyle+\int dq\int dp\,\nu_{\Lambda}^{\prime}(p)\nu_{\Lambda}^{\prime}(q)\ln|q-p|.

(137)

Instead, in the non-winding region with
|\Lambda|>\Lambda_{c}
,

\rho(x,\Lambda)\sim 2A(\Lambda)J(x)\exp\left(-ix\int k\nu_{\Lambda}^{\prime}(k){\rm d}k\right).

(138)

In these expression we added
\Lambda
to
\nu_{\Lambda}(q)
to highlight its dependence on this parameter and
\nu_{\Lambda_{c}^{\pm}}(q)
means the limiting expression when
\Lambda
approaches
\Lambda_{c}
from either above or below.
As shown in the previous section, for
\Lambda\sim\Lambda_{c}^{+}
,
J(x)
can be approximated by formula (
130
). In this case we can write

\rho(x,\Lambda_{c}^{+})\sim B(\Lambda_{c}^{+})\exp\left(\!-ix\left(\sqrt{\mu}+\int k\nu_{\Lambda_{c}^{+}}^{\prime}(k){\rm d}k\right)\!\right),

(139)

where

\displaystyle B(\Lambda)=2A(\Lambda)e^{\mathcal{J}(\Lambda)}.

(140)

To confirm the continuity of
\rho(x,\Lambda)
we start with the
x
-dependent part. We should verify the following relation

\int k\nu_{\Lambda_{c}^{+}}^{\prime}(k){\rm d}k+\sqrt{\mu}=\int k\nu_{\Lambda_{c}^{-}}^{\prime}(k){\rm d}k.

(141)

To this end consider
\nu^{\prime}(q)
. From the definition (
36
),

\nu^{\prime}(q)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(q)}{\varphi(q)},

(142)

Function
\varphi(q)
defined in (
37
) is a bounded function for real
q
. Therefore
\varphi^{\prime}(q)
is also bounded and any non-analyticities of
\nu^{\prime}(q)
must come from points where
\varphi(q)=0
. We can rewrite
\varphi(q)
with the help of
w(q)
, defined in (
114
), as follows

w(q)=\frac{\varphi(q)\sigma(q)}{i+\alpha q-\Lambda},

(143)

which shows that for real
q
the set of zeroes of
\varphi(q)
is the same as the set of zeroes of
w(q)
. Specifically, if we extend to the complex plane, there is a special zero
z_{*}
that, as shown in the computation of the asymptotic of
J(x)
approaches real line from below as
\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda_{c}^{+}
and for
\Lambda=\Lambda_{c}
,
z_{*}=\sqrt{\mu}
.

Figure 8
:
Contours of integrations used in showing the continuity of
\rho(x,\Lambda)
.

The presence of this singularity can be taken into account by deforming the integration contours in (
141
). For the first integral, we avoid the pole from above whereas for the second integral we avoid the pole by deflecting the contour below the real axis, see fig.
8
. Then the difference of the integrals can be written as a single integral over a closed contour around the pole at
z=\sqrt{\mu}
,

\int k\nu_{\Lambda_{c}^{+}}^{\prime}(k){\rm d}k-\int k\nu_{\Lambda_{c}^{-}}^{\prime}(k){\rm d}k=-\oint{\rm d}zz\nu_{\Lambda_{c}}^{\prime}(z).

(144)

The minus sign appears to compensate for the clockwise orientation of the initial contour. The orientation of the contour in the final integral is then counterclockwise.
Performing now this integral with the help of the residue theorem we find

\oint{\rm d}zz\nu_{\Lambda_{c}}^{\prime}(z)=\sqrt{\mu},

(145)

and therefore, the
x
-dependent part is continuous.

We consider now the prefactors. For the ratio of them we have

\frac{B(\Lambda_{c}^{+})}{A(\Lambda_{c}^{-})}=\exp\left(\mathcal{I}_{1}+\mathcal{I}_{2}+\ln 2+\mathcal{J}\right),

(146)

where we defined

\mathcal{I}_{1}=\int g(k)\nu_{\Lambda_{c}^{+}}^{\prime}(k){\rm d}k-\int g(k)\nu_{\Lambda_{c}^{-}}^{\prime}(k){\rm d}k,

(147)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}=\int dq\int dp\,\nu_{\Lambda_{c}^{+}}^{\prime}(p)\nu_{\Lambda_{c}^{+}}^{\prime}(q)\ln|q-p|

\displaystyle-\int dq\int dp\,\nu_{\Lambda_{c}^{-}}^{\prime}(p)\nu_{\Lambda_{c}^{-}}^{\prime}(q)\ln|q-p|.

(148)

For the single integral we use that

g(k)=2\pi i\,\nu(k)-\ln a(k),\qquad a(k)=e^{2i\delta(k)}\sigma(k),

(149)

to find

\mathcal{I}_{1}=\pi i(w^{+}-w^{-})+\oint\ln a(k)\nu_{\Lambda_{c}}^{\prime}(k){\rm d}k.

(150)

Here
w^{+}=0
is the winding number of
\nu_{\Lambda}(k)
for
|\Lambda|>\Lambda_{c}
and
w^{-}=1
is the winding number for
|\Lambda|<\Lambda_{c}
.
The remaining integral can be again evaluated by the residue theorem with the result

\mathcal{I}_{1}=\pi i(w^{+}-w^{-})+\ln a(\sqrt{\mu})=-\ln 2,

(151)

where we used that
\sigma(\sqrt{\mu})=1/2
and
\delta(\sqrt{\mu})=\pi/2
for
\Lambda=\Lambda_{c}
.

We consider now a difference of the double integrals. We rewrite them as

\displaystyle\int dq\int dp\,\nu^{\prime}(p)\nu^{\prime}(q)\ln|q-p|

\displaystyle=2\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dp\,\ln p\int dq\,\nu^{\prime}(p+q)\nu^{\prime}(q).

(152)

The outer integral is the same in both regimes. Therefore

\mathcal{I}_{2}=2\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dp\,\ln p\;\mathcal{I}_{3}(p),

(153)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{3}(p)=\int dq\,\nu_{\Lambda_{c}^{+}}^{\prime}(q+p)\nu_{\Lambda_{c}^{+}}^{\prime}(q)

\displaystyle-\int dq\,\nu_{\Lambda_{c}^{-}}^{\prime}(q+p)\nu_{\Lambda_{c}^{-}}^{\prime}(q).

(154)

The difference of the two integrals can be now analysed in a similar manner as before. The integrands have two poles, at
q=\sqrt{\mu}
and
q=\sqrt{\mu}-p
, and

\mathcal{I}_{3}(p)=-\nu_{\Lambda_{c}}^{\prime}(\sqrt{\mu}+p)-\nu_{\Lambda_{c}}^{\prime}(\sqrt{\mu}-p).

(155)

Therefore
\mathcal{I}_{2}=-\mathcal{J}
and the ratio
B(\Lambda_{c}^{+})/A(\Lambda_{c}^{-})=1
showing that the
\rho(x,\Lambda)
is a continuous function of
\Lambda
.

C.4
Alternative representation

The presented about proof of continuity of
\rho(x,\Lambda)
suggest a possibility to write
\rho(x,\Lambda)
in a uniform manner for winding and non-winding regimes. In this section we demonstrate this for the
x
-dependent part. In the winding region the
x
-dependent part is given by

\xi^{-1}(\Lambda)=-\int{\rm d}k\,k\nu^{\prime}(k)=-\oint_{\mathcal{C}}{\rm d}zz\nu^{\prime}(z),

(156)

where the closed contour extends in the lower part of the complex plane. As we have seen in the previous section function
\nu^{\prime}(z)
has a simple pole which, for
\Lambda=\pm\Lambda_{c}
, is located at
z=\pm\sqrt{\mu}
and otherwise continuously depends on
\Lambda
, see Fig. (
7
). For
|\Lambda|<\Lambda_{c}
the pole is in the upper half of the complex plane and does not affect the integral in
156
.

In the non-winding region, the
x
-dependent part is

\xi^{-1}(\Lambda)=-z_{*}-\int{\rm d}k\,k\nu^{\prime}(k)=-z_{*}-\oint_{\mathcal{C}}{\rm d}zz\nu^{\prime}(z),

(157)

The extra contribution
-z_{*}
, can be now taken into the account by deforming the contour
\mathcal{C}
such that it excludes the point
z_{*}
. With the new contour
\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)
we have

\oint_{\mathcal{C}}{\rm d}zz\nu^{\prime}(z)=\oint_{\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)}{\rm d}zz\nu^{\prime}(z)-\oint_{z_{*}}{\rm d}zz\nu^{\prime}(z),

(158)

where the second integral is around the counterclockwise contour including point
z_{*}
and no other poles of
\nu^{\prime}(z)
. The latter integral evaluates to
z^{*}
such that in both regions, for the
x
-dependent part we have

\xi^{-1}(\Lambda)=-\oint_{\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)}{\rm d}zz\nu^{\prime}(z).

(159)

This is the sought after expression for
\xi^{-1}(\Lambda)
which is valid in the winding and non-winding regimes. As we change
\Lambda
it possible to adjust contour
\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)
in a continuous manner and as the result the integral varies in a smooth way.
