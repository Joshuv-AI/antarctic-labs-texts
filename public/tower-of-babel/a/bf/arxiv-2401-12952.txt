A unifying framework for perturbative exponential factorizations
Ana Arnal, Fernando Casas, Cristina Chiralt, José Angel Oteo

Abstract
We propose a framework where Fer and Wilcox expansions for the solution of differential equations are derived from two particular choices for the initial transformation that seeds the product expansion. In this scheme intermediate expansions can also be envisaged. Recurrence formulas are developed. A new lower bound for the convergence of the Wilcox expansion is provided as well as some applications of the results. In particular, two examples are worked out up to high order of approximation to illustrate the behavior of the Wilcox expansion. Keywords: Sequences of linear transformations; Wilcox expansion; Fer expansion; Zassenhaus formula; Bellman problem.

Introduction

Linear differential equations of the form

\dot{x}\equiv\frac{dx}{dt}=A(t)x,\qquad x(0)=x_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{d},

(1)

with
A(t)
a
d\times d
matrix whose entries are integrable functions of
t
, are ever-present in many branches of science,
the fundamental evolution equation of Quantum Mechanics, the Schrödinger equation, being a particular case. In consequence, solving
Eq. (
1
) is of the greatest importance.
In spite of their apparent simplicity, however, they are seldom solvable in terms of elementary functions, and so different procedures have been
proposed along the years to render approximate solutions. These are specially useful in the analytical treatment of perturbative problems, such
as those arising in the time evolution of quantum systems
[
1
]
, control theory or problems where time-ordered products
are involved
[
2
]
. Among them, exponential perturbative expansions have received a great deal of attention, due
to some remarkable properties they possess. In particular, if Eq. (
1
) is defined in a Lie group, the
approximations they furnish also evolve in the same Lie group. As a consequence, important qualitative properties of the exact solution are also
preserved by the approximations. Thus, if Eq. (
1
) represents the time-dependent Schrödinger equation, then the
approximate evolution operator is still unitary, and as a consequence the total sum of (approximate) transition probabilities is the unity, no matter where the
expansion is truncated. There are in fact many physical problems (in non-linear mechanics, optical spectroscopy, magnetic resonance, etc.)
involving periodic fast-oscillating external fields that are also modeled by Eq. (
1
), with
A(t)
periodic. In that case, especially
tailored expansions incorporating the well-known Floquet theorem
[
3
]
, such as the average Hamiltonian theory

[
4
]
and the Floquet–Magnus
expansion
[
5
,
6
]
have also been proposed.

When dealing with the general problem (
1
),
one of the most widely used exponential approximations corresponds to the Magnus expansion
[
7
]
,

x(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\Omega(t)}x_{0},

(2)

where
\Omega
is an infinite series,

\Omega(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Omega_{k}(t)

(3)

whose terms are linear combinations of time-ordered integrals of nested commutators of
A
evaluated at different times (see

[
8
]
for a review, including applications to several physical and mathematical problems). What is more interesting for our purposes here is that
this expansion can be related with a coordinate transformation
x\longmapsto X
rendering the original system (
1
) into the trivial equation

\frac{dX}{dt}=0,

(4)

with the
static
solution
X(t)=X(0)=x_{0}
, and that the transformation is given precisely by
x(t)=\exp(\Omega(t))X(t)

[
9
]
.

In contrast to the Magnus expansion, the Floquet-Magnus expansion gets the solution with two exponential transformations when
A(t)

is periodic, whereas
other exponential perturbative expansions are based on infinite product factorizations of
x(t)
,

x(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{1}(t)}\,\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{2}(t)}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{n}(t)}\cdots\,x(0),

(5)

such as those proposed by Fer and Wilcox.
In fact, as pointed out in
[
10
]
, both expansions have a
curious history worth to be sketched. It was Fer who proposed the expansion that bears his name in
[
11
]
, although
he never applied it to solve any specific problem. Bellman reviewed this paper in the
Mathematical Reviews
1
1

1

MR0104009.
,
and even proposed the expansion as an exercise
in
[
12
]
. Nevertheless, Wilcox identified it in
[
1
]
with an alternative factorization, Eq. (
5
), which was indeed a different
and new type of expansion. From them on, their historical trajectories move apart. Thus, Fer expansion was rediscovered by Iserles

[
13
]
as a tool for the numerical integration of linear differential equations and later on used in quantum mechanics
[
14
]

and solid-state
nuclear magnetic resonance
[
15
]
, but also as a Lie-group integrator
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
.
On the other hand, Wilcox expansion has been rediscovered several
times in the literature, in particular in
[
19
]
in the context of nonlinear control systems, and in
[
20
]
as a general tool for approximating the
time evolution operator in quantum mechanics.

The first goal in this work is to recast both infinite product expansions within a unifying framework. This is done by considering, instead of
just
one
exponential transformation as in the case of the Magnus expansion, a
sequence
of such transformations,

\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{1}(t)},\ldots,\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{k}(t)},\ldots
, chosen to satisfy certain requirements. To be more specific, suppose one
replaces
A(t)
in Eq. (
1
) by
\lambda A(t)
, where
\lambda>0
is a parameter. Then, if the transformations are chosen so that each

\Omega_{k}(t)
is proportional to
\lambda^{k}
, we recover the Wilcox expansion, whereas we end up with the Fer expansion when each

\Omega_{k}(t)
is an infinite series in
\lambda
whose first term is proportional to
\lambda^{2^{k-1}}
.

We also show that further alternative descriptions yield new factorizations. This additional degree of freedom can be indeed used
to deal better with the features of the matrix
A(t)
as in Floquet-Magnus, when
A(t)
is periodic.

One might then consider this sequence of linear transformations as a generalization of the concept of picture in Quantum Mechanics
when Eq. (
1
) refers to the Schrödinger equation.

Our second goal consists in obtaining, on the basis of this framework, new results concerning Wilcox expansion. Thus, we develop a recursive procedure to obtain every order of approximation in terms of nested commutators, as well as a convergence radius bound.
We also establish a formal connection of the Wilcox expansion with the Zassenhaus formula
[
7
,
21
]
.

Eventually, the important problem of expanding the exponential
\exp(A+\varepsilon B)
for
\varepsilon>0
and
A
,
B
, two generic non-commuting operators will be addressed, and two applications of the results given.

2
A sequence of transformations: the general case

Given the initial value problem (
1
), let us consider a linear change of variables
x\longmapsto X_{1}
of the form

x(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{1}(t)}X_{1}(t),\qquad\Omega_{1}(0)=0

(6)

transforming the original system into

\frac{dX_{1}}{dt}=B_{1}(t)X_{1}.

(7)

For the time being, the generator
\Omega_{1}(t)
of the transformation is not specified.
Then,
B_{1}(t)
can be expressed in terms of
A(t)
and
\Omega_{1}(t)
as follows. First, by inserting Eq. (
6
) into Eq.
(
1
), and
taking Eq. (
7
) into account, one can obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\exp(\Omega_{1})=A(t)\exp(\Omega_{1})-\exp(\Omega_{1})B_{1}(t),

(8)

whence

B_{1}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega_{1}}A(t)\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{1}}-\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega_{1}}\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{1}}.

(9)

The derivative of the matrix exponential can be written as
[
8
]

\frac{d}{dt}\exp(\Omega_{1}(t))=d\exp_{\Omega_{1}(t)}(\dot{\Omega}_{1}(t))\,\exp(\Omega_{1}),

(10)

where the symbol
d\exp_{\Omega}(C)
stands for the (everywhere convergent) power series

d\exp_{\Omega}(C)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(k+1)!}\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega}^{k}(C)\equiv\frac{\exp(\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega})-I}{\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega}}(C).

(11)

Here
\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega}^{0}C=C
,
\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega}^{k}C=[\Omega,\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega}^{k-1}C]
, and
[\Omega,C]
denotes the usual commutator.
Therefore

B_{1}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega_{1}}\left(A(t)-d\exp_{\Omega_{1}(t)}(\dot{\Omega}_{1}(t))\right)\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{1}}\equiv\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{1}}}(B_{0}-G_{1})

(12)

where

B_{0}(t)\equiv A(t),\qquad G_{1}(t)\equiv d\exp_{\Omega_{1}(t)}(\dot{\Omega}_{1}(t))

(13)

and

\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{1}}}F=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{1}}^{k}F=\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega_{1}}F\,\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{1}}.

(14)

Of course, nothing prevents us from repeating the whole procedure above and introduce a second transformation to Eq. (
7
) of the form

X_{1}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{2}(t)}X_{2}(t),\qquad\Omega_{2}(0)=0,

(15)

so that the new variables verify

\frac{dX_{2}}{dt}=B_{2}(t)X_{2}.

(16)

In general, for the
n
-th such linear transformation

X_{n-1}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{n}(t)}X_{n}(t),\qquad\Omega_{n}(0)=0

(17)

with

\frac{dX_{n}}{dt}=B_{n}(t)X_{n},

(18)

one has

B_{n}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{n}}}(B_{n-1}-G_{n}),\qquad\mbox{ with }\qquad G_{n}(t)=d\exp_{\Omega_{n}(t)}(\dot{\Omega}_{n}(t))

(19)

so that the solution of equation (
1
) is expressed as

x(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{1}(t)}\,\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{2}(t)}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{n}(t)}X_{n}(t).

(20)

Alternatively, we can write
B_{n}(t)
in Eq. (
19
) as follows. Since it is also true that
[
8
]

\displaystyle d\exp_{\Omega_{n}(t)}(\dot{\Omega}_{n}(t))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{n}}\right)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega_{n}}=\left(\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{x\Omega_{n}}\dot{\Omega}_{n}\mathrm{e}^{(1-x)\Omega_{n}}dx\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega_{n}}

(21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{x\,\Omega_{n}}\dot{\Omega}_{n}\mathrm{e}^{-x\,\Omega_{n}}dx,

we have

\displaystyle B_{n}=

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega_{n}}B_{n-1}\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{n}}-\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-u\,\Omega_{n}}\dot{\Omega}_{n}\mathrm{e}^{u\,\Omega_{n}}du

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(k+1)!}\left((k+1)\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{n}}^{k}B_{n-1}-\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{n}}^{k}\dot{\Omega}_{n}\right),\qquad n\geq 1.

The important point is of course how to choose
B_{n}
, or alternatively
\Omega_{n}
, i.e., the specific requirements each transformation has to satisfy in order to be useful to approximately solve Eq. (
1
). There are obviously many
possibilities and in the following we analyze two of them, leading to two different and well known exponential perturbation factorizations mentioned
in the Introduction,
namely the Wilcox
[
1
]
and Fer
[
11
]
expansions.

3
Wilcox expansion

3.1
Recurrences

Let us introduce the (dummy) parameter
\lambda
in Eq. (
1
) and replace
A
with
\lambda A
. This is helpful when collecting
coefficients, and at the end we can always take
\lambda=1
.

Since the solution of Eq.
(
1
) when
A
is constant, or more generally when
A(t_{1})A(t_{2})=A(t_{2})A(t_{1})
for all
t_{1}\neq t_{2}
, is
x(t)=\exp(\int_{0}^{t}A(u)du)
, it makes
sense to take the generator for the first transformation as

\Omega_{1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}B_{0}(u)\,du=\lambda\int_{0}^{t}A(u)\,du\equiv\lambda\,W_{1}(t).

(23)

Then, according to Eqs. (
12
)-(
13
), we have

C_{1}\equiv B_{0}-G_{1}=\sum_{k\geq 1}\lambda^{k}(b_{0,k}-g_{1,k})

(24)

where

\displaystyle b_{0,1}=A(t),\qquad\qquad b_{0,l}=0,\;\;l>1

(25)

\displaystyle g_{1,1}=\dot{W}_{1},\qquad\qquad\;g_{1,l}=\frac{1}{l!}\mathrm{ad}_{W_{1}}^{l-1}\dot{W}_{1},\;\;l>1.

With the choice
\dot{W}_{1}=A(t)
, it turns out that
B_{1}
is a power series in
\lambda
starting with
\lambda^{2}
,

B_{1}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{1}}}(B_{0}-G_{1})=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda\,\mathrm{ad}_{W_{1}}}C_{1}=\sum_{l\geq 2}\lambda^{l}\,b_{1,l},

(26)

where

b_{1,l}=\sum_{k=0}^{l-2}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\mathrm{ad}_{W_{1}}^{k}\big(b_{0,l-k}-g_{1,l-k}\big).

(27)

We can analogously choose the second transformation proportional to
\lambda^{2}
, i.e., as
\Omega_{2}(t)\equiv\lambda^{2}W_{2}(t)

for a given
W_{2}
to be determined.
Then a straightforward calculation shows that

C_{2}\equiv B_{1}-G_{2}=\sum_{l\geq 2}\lambda^{l}(b_{1,l}-g_{2,l})

(28)

with

g_{2,2}=\dot{W}_{2},\qquad g_{2,2l}=\frac{1}{l!}\mathrm{ad}_{W_{2}}^{l-1}\dot{W}_{2},\qquad g_{2,r}=0,\quad r\neq 2l.

(29)

The generator
W_{2}
is then obtained by imposing that
b_{1,2}-g_{2,2}=0
in Eq. (
28
), i.e.,

g_{2,2}=\dot{W}_{2}=b_{1,2}=b_{0,2}-g_{1,2}=-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{ad}_{W_{1}}\dot{W}_{1}.

(30)

In this way
B_{2}(t)
is a power series in
\lambda
starting with
\lambda^{3}
,

B_{2}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda^{2}\,\mathrm{ad}_{W_{2}}}C_{2}=\sum_{l\geq 3}\lambda^{l}\,b_{2,l}

(31)

with

b_{2,l}=\sum_{k=0}^{[(l-1)/2]-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\mathrm{ad}_{W_{2}}^{k}\big(b_{1,l-2k}-g_{2,l-2k}\big),

(32)

where
[\cdot]
stands for the integer part of the argument.
In general, the
n
-th transformation
\Omega_{n}(t)\equiv\lambda^{n}\,W_{n}(t)

is determined in such a way that the power series of
B_{n}(t)
starts with
\lambda^{n+1}
. This can be done as follows: from

B_{n-1}=\sum_{l\geq n}\lambda^{l}b_{n-1,l}
,
we compute

C_{n}\equiv B_{n-1}-G_{n}=\sum_{r=n}^{\infty}\lambda^{r}c_{n,r}=\sum_{r=n}^{\infty}\lambda^{r}(b_{n-1,r}-g_{n,r})

(33)

with

g_{n,r}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{1}{l!}\mathrm{ad}_{W_{n}}^{l-1}\dot{W}_{n},&r=n\,l\\
0,&r\neq n\,l\end{array}\right.\qquad l=1,2,\ldots

(34)

Then,
W_{n}
is obtained by taking
b_{n-1,n}-g_{n,n}=0
, i.e.,

\dot{W}_{n}=b_{n-1,n}

(35)

and finally
B_{n}
is determined as

B_{n}=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda^{n}\,\mathrm{ad}_{W_{n}}}C_{n}=\sum_{l=n+1}^{\infty}\lambda^{l}\,b_{n,l}

(36)

with

b_{n,l}=\sum_{k=0}^{[\frac{l-1}{n}]-1}\,\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\mathrm{ad}_{W_{n}}^{k}c_{n,l-n\,k}.

(37)

Notice that, in view of Eq. (
34
) and Eq. (
37
), equation (
35
) simplifies to

\dot{W}_{n}=b_{n-2,n}\qquad\mbox{ for }\qquad n\geq 3.

(38)

The solution of Eq. (
1
) is expressed, after
n
such transformations, as

x(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\lambda W_{1}(t)}\,\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{2}W_{2}(t)}\,\cdots\,\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{n}W_{n}(t)}X_{n}(t).

(39)

An approximation to the exact solution containing all the dependence up to
\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{n})
is obtained by
taking
X_{n}=x_{0}
in Eq. (
39
).

In spite of this, the truncated factorization
\mathrm{e}^{\lambda W_{1}(t)}\,\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{2}W_{2}(t)}\,\cdots\,\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{n}W_{n}(t)}
still shares relevant qualitative properties, such as orthogonality, unitarity, etc.

Recursion (
33
)-(
38
) allows one to construct
any
generator
W_{k}
of the expansion in terms of
W_{1},\ldots,W_{k-1}
.
For illustration, we next collect the first terms:

\displaystyle\dot{W}_{1}=A(t),\qquad\quad\dot{W}_{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{ad}_{W_{1}}\dot{W}_{1},\qquad\quad\dot{W}_{3}=\frac{1}{3}\mathrm{ad}_{W_{1}}^{2}\dot{W}_{1}

(40)

\displaystyle\dot{W}_{4}=-\frac{1}{8}\mathrm{ad}_{W_{1}}^{3}\dot{W}_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{ad}_{W_{2}}\dot{W}_{2},\qquad\quad\dot{W}_{5}=\frac{1}{30}\mathrm{ad}_{W_{1}}^{4}\dot{W}_{1}-\mathrm{ad}_{W_{2}}\dot{W}_{3}

This is the way the Wilcox expansion is built up.

3.2
Explicit expressions for
W_{n}(t)

Although the recursive procedure (
33
)-(
38
) turns out to be very computationally efficient to construct the exponents
W_{n}(t)
for a given
A(t)
in practice, it is clear that much insight about the expansion can be gained if an explicit expression for
any

W_{n}
can be constructed, thus generalizing the treatment done originally by
Wilcox up to
n=4

[
1
]
.

Such an expression could be obtained, in principle, by working out the recurrence (
33
)-(
38
), but
a more direct approach consists in
comparing the Dyson perturbation series of
U(t)

[
22
]
in the associated initial value problem

\dot{U}=\lambda\,A(t)U,\qquad U(0)=I,

(41)

i.e.,

U(t)=I+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{k}P_{k}(t),\qquad\mbox{ with }\qquad P_{k}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\cdots\int_{0}^{t_{k-1}}dt_{k}\,A(t_{1})\cdots A(t_{k})

(42)

with the expansion in
\lambda
of the factorization

U(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\lambda W_{1}(t)}\,\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{2}W_{2}(t)}\,\cdots\,\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{n}W_{n}(t)}\cdots.

(43)

Thus, for the first terms one has

\displaystyle P_{1}

\displaystyle=W_{1},\qquad\quad P_{2}=W_{2}+\dfrac{1}{2}W_{1}^{2},\qquad\quad P_{3}=W_{3}+W_{1}W_{2}+\frac{1}{3!}W_{1}^{3},

(44)

\displaystyle P_{4}

\displaystyle=W_{4}+W_{1}W_{3}+\frac{1}{2}W_{1}^{2}W_{2}+\dfrac{1}{2}W_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{4!}W_{1}^{4}.

In general, we can write

P_{n}=\sum_{p(n)\atop i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq\cdots\leq i_{k}}\frac{1}{r_{k}!}\,W_{i_{1}}W_{i_{2}}\cdots W_{i_{k}},\qquad i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq\cdots\leq i_{k},

(45)

where the sum is extended over the total number of partitions
p(n)
of the integer
n
. We recall that a partition
p(n)
of the integer
n

is an
n
-tuple
(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k})
such that
i_{1}+i_{2}+\cdots+i_{k}=n
, with ordering
i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq\cdots\leq i_{k}
. Thus, the
seven partitions of
n=5
with the chosen ordering are
(5)
,
(1,4)
,
(2,3)
,
(1,1,3)
,
(1,2,2)
,
(1,1,1,2)
and
(1,1,1,1,1)
.
In Eq. (
45
),

r_{k}
is the number of repeated indices in the partition considered.

By working out equation (
45
), one can invert the relations and express
W_{n}
in terms of
P_{1},\ldots,P_{n-1}
for any
n\geq 1
. Thus, i one obtains

\displaystyle W_{1}

\displaystyle=P_{1},\qquad\quad W_{2}=P_{2}-\frac{1}{2}P_{1}^{2},\qquad\quad W_{3}=P_{3}-P_{1}P_{2}+\frac{1}{3}P_{1}^{3},

(46)

\displaystyle W_{4}

\displaystyle=P_{4}-P_{1}P_{3}+\frac{3}{4}P_{1}^{2}P_{2}+\frac{1}{4}P_{2}P_{1}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}P_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}P_{1}^{4}.

Notice that
W_{n}
,
n\geq 2
, is expressed in terms of products of iterated integrals
P_{i_{1}}\cdots P_{i_{j}}
. Interestingly, it is possible to express these products as proper time-ordered integrals by
using a procedure developed in
[
23
]
. If we denote

A(i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{n})\equiv\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\cdots\int_{0}^{t_{n-1}}dt_{n}\,A(t_{i_{1}})A(t_{i_{2}})\cdots A(t_{t_{n}}),

(47)

so that
P_{n}(t)=A(12\ldots n)
, then

\displaystyle W_{2}=A(12)-\frac{1}{2}A(1)\cdot A(1)

(48)

\displaystyle W_{3}=A(123)-A(1)\cdot A(12)+\frac{1}{3}A(1)\cdot A(1)\cdot A(1),

etc. Taking into account Fubini’s theorem,

\int_{0}^{\alpha}dy\int_{y}^{\alpha}f(x,y)\,dx=\int_{0}^{\alpha}dx\int_{0}^{x}f(x,y)\,dy,

(49)

it is clear that
A(1)\cdot A(1)=A(12)+A(21)
, and thus

W_{2}=A(12)-\frac{1}{2}\big(A(12)+A(21)\big)=\frac{1}{2}\big(A(12)-A(21)\big).

(50)

We can proceed analogously with the following products

\displaystyle A(1)\cdot A(12)=A(123)+A(213)+A(312)

(51)

\displaystyle A(1)\cdot A(1)\cdot A(1)=A(123)+A(132)+A(213)+A(231)+A(312)+A(321),

so that

W_{3}=\frac{1}{3}\,A(123)+\frac{1}{3}\,A(132)-\frac{2}{3}\,A(213)+\frac{1}{3}\,A(231)-\frac{2}{3}\,A(312)+\frac{1}{3}\,A(321).

(52)

Carrying out this argument to any order, we can expand all the products of integrals appearing in
W_{n}
. As a result, each product is
replaced by the sum of all possible permutations of time ordering consistent with the time ordering in the factors of this product
[
24
]
.

At this point, it is illustrative to consider in detail some examples. Thus, the product
A(1)\cdot A(12)
gives the sum of
all permutations of three elements such
that the second index is less than the third
one. With respect to
A(1)\cdot A(1)\cdot A(1)
, since there is no special ordering, then all possible permutations have to be taken into account.
Finally, for the product
P_{1}P_{3}
appearing in
W_{4}
one has

P_{1}P_{3}=A(1)\cdot A(123)=A(4123)+A(3124)+A(2134)+A(1234).

(53)

Proceeding in a similar way, one can show that any product of iterated integrals can be expressed as a sum of iterated integrals. This property
is in fact related with a much deeper characterization of the group of permutations
[
23
]
. If
\mathfrak{S}Symm
denotes the graded

\mathbb{Q}
-vector space with fundamental basis given by the disjoint union of the symmetric groups
S_{n}
for all
n\geq 0
, then
it is possible to define a product
\ast
of permutations and a coproduct

\delta
in
\mathfrak{S}Symm
so that there exists a
one-to-one correspondence between iterated integrals and permutations:

A(\sigma)\cdot A(\tau)=A(\sigma\ast\tau).

(54)

The product
\ast
was introduced in
[
25
]
,
and together with the coproduct
\delta
endow
\mathfrak{S}Symm
with a structure of Hopf algebra
[
26
]
,
the so-called Malvenuto–Reutenauer Hopf algebra of permutations
[
27
]
.

In sum, the
general
structure of the Wilcox expansion terms reads

W_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n!}\omega_{k}^{(n)}A(\sigma_{k}),

(55)

where the summation extends over
all
the
n!
permutations
\sigma_{k}\in S_{n}
of
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
The weights
\omega_{k}^{(n)}
are given by rational numbers that can be determined algorithmically for any
n
, although, it seems not obvious
to find a general expression for them, in contrast with the Magnus expansion, for which such a closed formula exists
[
28
]
.
This can be then
considered as an open problem.

Moreover, if one
is interested in getting a compact expression for
W_{n}
in terms of independent nested commutators of
A(t)
, as is done in
[
1
]
up
to
n=4
, one can use the class of bases proposed by Dragt & Forest in
[
24
]
for the Lie algebra
generated by the operators
A(t_{1}),A(t_{2}),\ldots,A(t_{n})
. The same procedure carried out in
[
23
]
for the Magnus expansion can be
applied here, so that one gets the general formula

\displaystyle W_{n}(t)=\sum_{\tau_{k}}c_{\tau_{k}}^{(n)}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\cdots\int_{0}^{t_{n-1}}dt_{n}\,

(56)

\displaystyle[A(t_{\tau(2)}),[A(t_{\tau(3)})\cdots[A(t_{\tau(n)}),A(t_{1})]\cdots]].

Here the sum extends over the
(n-1)!
permutations
\tau_{k}
of the elements
\{2,3,\ldots,n\}
and
c_{\tau_{k}}^{(n)}
is
a rational number that depends on the
particular permutation. For a given permutation, say
\tau_{k}
, its value coincides with the prefactor in Eq. (
55
) of the particular
term
A(\sigma_{k})
corresponding to the permutation
\sigma_{k}\in S_{n}
such that

\{\sigma_{k}(1),\sigma_{k}(2),\ldots,\sigma_{k}(n-1),\sigma_{k}(n)\}=\{\tau_{k}(2),\tau_{k}(3),\ldots,\tau_{k}(n),1\}.

(57)

Thus, if we denote

A[i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{n}]\equiv\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\cdots\int_{0}^{t_{n-1}}dt_{n}[A(t_{i_{1}}),[A(t_{i_{2}}),\cdots[A(t_{i_{n-1}}),A(t_{i_{n}})]\cdots]]

(58)

we get for the first terms

\displaystyle W_{2}=-\frac{1}{2}A[21],

(59)

\displaystyle W_{3}=\frac{1}{3}A[231]+\frac{1}{3}A[321],

\displaystyle W_{4}=-\frac{1}{4}A[3241]-\frac{1}{4}A[4231]-\frac{1}{4}A[4321],

\displaystyle W_{5}=-\frac{2}{15}A[23451]-\frac{2}{15}A[23541]-\frac{2}{15}A[24351]-\frac{2}{15}A[24531]

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{15}A[25341]-\frac{2}{15}A[25431]+\frac{1}{5}A[32451]+\frac{1}{5}A[32541]-\frac{2}{15}A[34251]

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{15}A[34521]-\frac{2}{15}A[35241]-\frac{2}{15}A[35421]+\frac{1}{5}A[42351]+\frac{1}{5}A[42531]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{5}A[43251]+\frac{1}{5}A[43521]-\frac{2}{15}A[45231]-\frac{2}{15}A[45321]+\frac{1}{5}A[52341]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{5}A[52431]+\frac{1}{5}A[53241]+\frac{1}{5}A[53421]+\frac{1}{5}A[54231]+\frac{1}{5}A[54321]

In sum, the general structure of the Wilcox expansion terms
via
commutators
uses the same weights as in Eq. (
55
) and reads

W_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{(n-1)!}{\vphantom{\sum}}{}^{\prime}\omega_{k}^{(n)}A[\sigma_{k}],

(60)

where the primed sum requires the rightmost element in the permutation
\sigma(k)
to be invariant (as in Eq. (
56
)). This element may be chosen at will and, whatever it be that value, the permutations are build up with the remaining
n-1
elements. Different, but equivalent, expressions for
W_{n}
in terms of commutators are obtained depending on the value fixed at the rightmost position. We stress once again that, although only the first
terms have been collected here for simplicity, the whole procedure is algorithmic in nature and has been implemented in a computer algebra system
furnishing to evaluate explicitly
W_{n}(t)
for any
n

[
29
]
. Notice that
W_{n}(t)
involves a linear combination of
(n-1)!
iterated integrals.

3.3
Convergence of Wilcox expansion

Recursion (
33
)-(
38
) is also very useful to provide estimates for the radius of convergence of the Wilcox expansion when
Eq. (
1
) is defined in a
Banach algebra
\mathcal{A}
, i.e.,
an algebra that is also a complete normed linear space with a sub-multiplicative norm,

\|X\,Y\|\leq\|X\|\,\|Y\|.

(61)

If this is the case, then
\|\mathrm{ad}_{X}Y\|\leq 2\,\|X\|\,\|Y\|
and, in general,
\|\mathrm{ad}_{X}^{n}Y\|\leq 2^{n}\|X\|^{n}\,\|Y\|
.

As shown in
[
30
,
31
]
, if the series

M(\lambda;t)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{j}\,\|W_{j}(t)\|

(62)

has a certain radius of convergence
r_{c}
for a given
t
, then, for
\lambda<r<r_{c}
, the sequence of functions

\Psi_{n}\equiv\mathrm{e}^{\lambda W_{1}(t)}\,\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{2}W_{2}(t)}\,\cdots\,\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{n}W_{n}(t)}

(63)

converges uniformly on any compact subset of the ball
B(0,r_{c})
. Thus, studying the convergence of the Wilcox expansion reduces to
analyze the series
M(\lambda;t)
and in particular its radius of convergence
r_{c}
.

Let
k(t)
be a function such that
\|A(t)\|\leq k(t)
and denote
K(t)=\int_{0}^{t}k(s)\,ds
. Then, clearly

\|b_{0,1}\|\leq k(t);\qquad\quad\|b_{0,l}\|=0,\quad l=2,3,\ldots

(64)

and

\|W_{1}\|\leq K(t),\qquad\|W_{2}\|\leq\frac{1}{2}K^{2}(t).

(65)

In general, the following bounds can be established by induction:

\displaystyle\|g_{n,r}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\beta_{n,r}\,K^{r-1}(t)\,k(t),

\displaystyle\|b_{n,l}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\alpha_{n,l}\,K^{l-1}(t)\,k(t),\qquad\qquad n=1,2,\ldots;\quad l>n

\displaystyle\|W_{n}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{n}\,K^{n}(t),

(66)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{0,1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1;\qquad\alpha_{0,l}=0,\;\;l>1

\displaystyle\alpha_{n,l}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{[\frac{l-1}{n}]-1}\frac{1}{j!}\,2^{j}\,c_{n}^{j}\,(\alpha_{n-1,l-nj}+\beta_{n,l-nj})\quad\qquad n\geq 1,\;l>n

\displaystyle\beta_{n,r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{1}{l!}\,2^{l-1}\,n\,c_{n}^{l},&\left[\frac{r}{n}\right]=\frac{r}{n}=l\\
0,&\left[\frac{r}{n}\right]\neq\frac{r}{n}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1,\quad c_{2}=\frac{1}{2},\qquad c_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\,\alpha_{n-2,n},\qquad n>2.

It is clear that if the series
\sum_{j\geq 1}c_{j}\,K^{j}(t)
converges, so does
M(\lambda=1;t)
.
Therefore, a sufficient condition for convergence of the Wilcox
expansion is obtained by imposing

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{c_{n+1}K^{n+1}(t)}{c_{n}K^{n}(t)}=K(t)\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}D_{n}<1,

(70)

where

D_{n}\equiv\frac{n}{n+1}\frac{\alpha_{n-1,n+1}}{\alpha_{n-2,n}}.

(71)

We have computed this quantity up to
n=2000
and then extrapolated to the limit
(1/n)\rightarrow 0
. Then
D_{n}\rightarrow D_{\infty}=1.51868
,
as seen in Figure
1
,
and thus the convergence of the Wilcox expansion is ensured at least for values of time
t
such that

\int_{0}^{t}\|A(s)\|ds\leq K(t)<\xi_{W}=\frac{1}{D_{\infty}}\approx 0.65846

(72)

This type of extrapolation has also been used to estimate the convergence radius of the Magnus expansion
[
32
]
.
Although the estimate is not completely analytic, the same type of computation has provided accurate results in other settings. In particular,
for the Magnus expansion such an estimate fully agrees with a purely theoretically deduced bound
[
10
,
8
,
32
]
.

D_{n}
as a function of
1/n
, and linear extrapolation (red line).

4
Fer–like expansions

4.1
Standard Fer expansion

In forming the Wilcox expansion the first transformation is chosen in such a way that
\dot{\Omega}_{1}=B_{0}(t)
, whereas

\dot{\Omega}_{n}\neq B_{n-1}
for
n\geq 2
. It makes sense, then, to analyze what happens if we impose this condition at each step of the procedure

\dot{\Omega}_{n}(t)=B_{n-1}(t)\qquad\mbox{ or, equivalently, }\qquad\Omega_{n}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}B_{n-1}(u)du

(73)

for all
n\geq 1
.
In that way, expression (
22
) for
B_{n}
clearly simplifies to

B_{n}(t)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{k}k}{(k+1)!}\,\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{n}}^{k}B_{n-1},\qquad n\geq 1.

(74)

In doing so we recover precisely the Fer expansion, see
[
11
,
10
]
. Again, after
n
transformations, we get

x(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{1}(t)}\,\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{2}(t)}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{n}(t)}X_{n}(t)

(75)

so that if we impose
X_{n}(t)=x_{0}
we are left with another approximation to the exact solution. Notice that
this approximation clearly
differs from the previous Wilcox expansion for
n\geq 2
, as can be seen
by analyzing the dependence on
\lambda
of each transformation. Whereas
\Omega_{n}=\lambda^{n}W_{n}(t)
for
the Wilcox expansion, now
\Omega_{n}
contains terms of order
\lambda^{2^{n-1}}
and higher. This can be easily shown by induction:
\Omega_{1}
is
proportional to
\lambda
, so that
B_{1}
, according to Eq. (
74
) contains terms of order
\lambda^{2}
(coming from
[\Omega_{1},B_{0}]
) and higher.
In general,
B_{n-2}
and
\Omega_{n-1}
contain terms of order
\lambda^{2^{n-2}}
and higher, so the first term in the series (
74
) for
B_{n-1}
,
i.e., the commutator
[\Omega_{n-1},B_{n-2}]
, produces a term of order
(\lambda^{2})^{2^{n-2}}=\lambda^{2^{n-1}}
in
\Omega_{n}
.

Alternatively, expressing Eq. (
74
) as

B_{n}(t)=\int_{0}^{1}dx\int_{0}^{x}du\,\mathrm{e}^{-(1-u)\Omega_{n}}\,[B_{n-1},\Omega_{n}]\,\mathrm{e}^{(1-u)\Omega_{n}}

(76)

and taking norms, it is then possible to show that the Fer expansion converges for values of
t
such that
[
10
]

\int_{0}^{t}\|A(s)\|ds<0.8604065.

(77)

4.2
Intermediate Fer-like expansions

Notice that the
\lambda
-power series of
\Omega_{n}
in the Fer expansion contains infinite terms starting with

\lambda^{2^{n-1}}
, but the corresponding truncated factorization obtained from Eq. (
75
) by taking
X_{n}(t)=x_{0}

is correct only up to terms of order
\lambda^{2^{n-1}}
. One might then consider yet another sequence of transformations so that each
\Omega_{k}
contains
only those relevant terms leading to a correct approximation up to this order. Of course, both factorizations would be different, but nevertheless they would produce
the correct power series up to order
\lambda^{2^{n-1}}
. The corresponding factorization can be properly called a modified Fer expansion.

Our starting point is once again equation (
22
). Clearly, the first transformation is the same as in Fer (and Wilcox), i.e.,

\dot{\Omega}_{1}=B_{0}=\lambda A(t),

(78)

and thus

B_{1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}k}{(k+1)!}\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{1}}^{k}B_{0}=\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2}),

(79)

where the rightmost term points out the lowest
\lambda
contribution in the sum.

Next, to reproduce the same dependence on
\lambda
as the Fer expansion, we need to enforce that
B_{2}=\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4})
, and the question is how to
choose
\Omega_{2}
guaranteeing this feature. An analysis of Eq. (
22
) with
n=2
reveals that
this is achieved by taking
\dot{\Omega}_{2}
as the sum of terms in
B_{1}
in Eq. (
79
) contributing to
\lambda^{2}
and
\lambda^{3}
, i.e.,

\dot{\Omega}_{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{1}}B_{0}+\frac{1}{3}\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{1}}^{2}B_{0},

(80)

since the next term appearing in the expression of
B_{2}
involves the computation of
\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{1}}^{3}B_{0}=\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4})
. Thus

B_{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(k+1)!}\left((k+1)\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{2}}^{k}B_{1}-\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{2}}^{k}\dot{\Omega}_{2}\right)=\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4}).

(81)

Likewise,
\Omega_{3}

is to be designed so that

B_{3}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(k+1)!}\left((k+1)\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{3}}^{k}B_{2}-\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{3}}^{k}\dot{\Omega}_{3}\right)=\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{8}),

(82)

and this is guaranteed by taking
\Omega_{3}
as the sum all the terms in
B_{2}
contributing to powers from
\lambda^{4}
up to

\lambda^{7}
. From Eq. (
81
) it is clear that

B_{2}=\underbrace{-\frac{1}{2}\left(2\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{2}}B_{1}-\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{2}}\dot{\Omega}_{2}\right)}_{\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4})}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{3!}\left(3\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{2}}^{2}B_{1}-\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{2}}^{2}\dot{\Omega}_{2}\right)}_{\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{6})}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{8}),

(83)

where only the relevant terms in the expansion in
B_{1}
have to be taken into account. In this way, we can take

\dot{\Omega}_{3}=-\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{2}}B_{1}^{[4]}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{2}}\dot{\Omega}_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{2}}^{2}B_{1}^{[2]}-\frac{1}{6}\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{2}}^{2}\dot{\Omega}_{2}

(84)

with

B_{1}^{[j]}\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{j}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(k+1)!}k\,\mathrm{ad}_{\Omega_{1}}^{k}B_{0}.

(85)

Notice, however, that since the second term in
\Omega_{2}
in Eq. (
80
) is
\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{3})
, the expression (
84
) does contain some
contributions in
\lambda^{8}
and
\lambda^{9}
that in principle could be removed. We prefer, however, to maintain them just to have a more compact
expression.

For this modified Fer expansion,
\Omega_{n}
is chosen in general
so that
\dot{\Omega}_{n}
is precisely the sum of all terms of
B_{n-1}
containing terms of powers from

\lambda^{2^{n-1}}
up to
\lambda^{2^{n}-1}
and then truncating appropriately the series of
B_{n-2},\ldots,B_{1}
.

Other possibilities for choosing
B_{k}
at the successive stages clearly exist, and according with the particular election, different intermediate
Fer-like expansions result. In practice, one of those combinations of commutators could be more easily computed for a specific problem.

5
Applications

5.1
Wilcox expansion as the continuous analogue of Zassenhaus formula

The Zassenhaus formula may be considered as the dual of the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff (BCH) formula
[
33
]

in the sense that it relates the
exponential of the sum of two non-commuting operators
X
and
Y
with an infinite product of exponentials of these operators and their nested commutators. More
specifically,

\mathrm{e}^{X+Y}=\mathrm{e}^{X}\,\mathrm{e}^{Y}\,\prod_{n=2}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{C_{n}(X,Y)}=\mathrm{e}^{X}\,\mathrm{e}^{Y}\,\mathrm{e}^{C_{2}(X,Y)}\,\mathrm{e}^{C_{3}(X,Y)}\,\cdots\,\mathrm{e}^{C_{k}(X,Y)}\,\cdots,

(86)

where
C_{k}(X,Y)
is a homogeneous Lie polynomial in
X
and
Y
of degree
k

[
21
,
7
,
34
,
35
,
1
]
. A very efficient procedure to generate all the terms in Eq. (
86
)
is presented in
[
21
]
and allows to construct
C_{n}
up to a prescribed value of
n
directly in terms of the minimum number of
independent commutators involving
n
operators
X
and
Y
.

In view of the formal similarity between Eq. (
39
) and Eq. (
86
), Wilcox expansion has been also described as the
“continuous analogue of the Zassenhaus formula”
[
10
]
, just as the Magnus expansion is sometimes called
the continuous version of the BCH formula. To substantiate this claim, we next reproduce the Zassenhaus formula (
86
)
by applying the procedure of Section

3
to a particular initial value problem, namely the abstract equation

\dot{U}=\lambda(X+Y)\,U,\qquad U(0)=I,

(87)

where
X
and
Y
are two non-commuting constant operators.

The formal solution is of course

U(t)=\mathrm{e}^{t\lambda(X+Y)}
, but we can also solve Eq. (
87
) by first integrating
\dot{U}_{0}=\lambda XU_{0}
and factorizing
U(t)
as

U(t)=U_{0}\,U_{I}=\mathrm{e}^{t\lambda X}\,U_{I}
, where
U_{I}
obeys the equation

\dot{U}_{I}=\lambda\,\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda X}Y\mathrm{e}^{t\lambda X}\,U_{I}\equiv A_{\lambda}(t)\,U_{I},

(88)

and finally apply to Eq. (
88
) the sequence of transformations leading to the Wilcox expansion. Notice, however, that now the coefficient matrix

A_{\lambda}(t)
is an infinite series in
\lambda
,

A_{\lambda}(t)=\lambda\,\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda\mathrm{ad}_{X}}Y=\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}t^{j}\lambda^{j+1}\mathrm{ad}_{X}^{j}Y,

(89)

so that, when applying the recursion (
33
)-(
38
),
\dot{\Omega}_{1}
is no longer
B_{0}\equiv A_{\lambda}(t)
, but the term
in
A_{\lambda}(t)
which is proportional to
\lambda
.
In other words,

\dot{W}_{1}=Y,\qquad\mbox{ and thus }\qquad W_{1}(t)=t\,Y.

(90)

After some computation, one arrives at

b_{0,l}=\frac{(-1)^{l-1}}{(l-1)!}t^{l-1}\mathrm{ad}_{X}^{l-1}Y.

(91)

Since
\dot{W}_{1}=b_{0,1}=Y
, then clearly
g_{1,l}=0
for all
l>1
and

b_{1,l}=\sum_{k=0}^{l-2}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\mathrm{ad}_{W_{1}}^{k}b_{0,l-k}.

(92)

By imposing
b_{1,2}=g_{2,2}=\dot{W}_{2}
, we get

\dot{W}_{2}=-t\,\mathrm{ad}_{X}Y,\qquad\mbox{ so that }\qquad W_{2}(t)=-\frac{1}{2}\,t^{2}\,\mathrm{ad}_{X}Y.

(93)

In general,
g_{n,n}=\dot{W}_{n}
,
g_{n,r}=0
when
r\neq n
and the recurrence (
33
)-(
38
) reads now

\displaystyle\dot{W}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b_{0,1}

\displaystyle b_{n,l}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{[\frac{l-1}{n}]-1}\,\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\mathrm{ad}_{W_{n}}^{k}b_{n-1,l-nk},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots

(94)

\displaystyle\dot{W}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b_{n-2,n}\qquad n\geq 2

together with Eq. (
91
). Working out this recursion we obtain, for the first terms

\displaystyle W_{3}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{6}t^{3}\mathrm{ad}_{X}^{2}Y+\frac{1}{3}t^{3}\mathrm{ad}_{Y}\mathrm{ad}_{X}Y

\displaystyle W_{4}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{24}t^{4}\mathrm{ad}_{X}^{3}Y-\frac{1}{8}t^{4}\mathrm{ad}_{Y}\mathrm{ad}_{X}^{2}Y-\frac{1}{8}t^{4}\mathrm{ad}_{Y}^{2}\mathrm{ad}_{X}Y

\displaystyle W_{5}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{120}t^{5}\mathrm{ad}_{X}^{4}Y+\frac{1}{30}t^{5}\mathrm{ad}_{Y}\mathrm{ad}_{X}^{3}Y+\frac{1}{20}t^{5}\mathrm{ad}_{Y}^{2}\mathrm{ad}_{X}^{2}Y

(95)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{30}t^{5}\mathrm{ad}_{Y}^{3}\mathrm{ad}_{X}Y+\frac{1}{20}t^{5}\mathrm{ad}_{[X,Y]}\mathrm{ad}_{X}^{2}Y+\frac{1}{10}t^{5}\mathrm{ad}_{[X,Y]}\mathrm{ad}_{Y}\mathrm{ad}_{X}Y

One can see that this procedure agrees with the algorithm presented in
[
21
]
for every term
W_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, in

U(t)=\mathrm{e}^{t\lambda(X+Y)}=\mathrm{e}^{t\lambda X}\,\mathrm{e}^{\lambda W_{1}(t)}\,\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{2}W_{2}(t)}\,\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{3}W_{3}(t)}\,\cdots.

(96)

The Zassenhaus formula is recovered by taking
t=1
, i.e.,
C_{n}(X,Y)=W_{n}(t=1)
.

5.2
Expanding the exponential
\exp(A+\varepsilon B)

Bellman, in his classic book
[
36
]
, states that “one of the great challenges of modern physics is that of obtaining useful
approximate relations for
\mathrm{e}^{(A+\varepsilon B)t}
in the case where
AB\neq BA
”. One such approximation was proposed and left undisclosed in
[
12
, p. 175]
. Assuming that
\mathrm{e}^{A+\varepsilon B}
can be written in the form

\mathrm{e}^{A+\varepsilon B}=\mathrm{e}^{A}\,\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon C_{1}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon^{2}C_{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon^{3}C_{3}}\cdots,

(97)

Bellman proposed to determine the first three terms
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
,
C_{3}
, and pointed out that, contrary to other expansions, the product expansion
(
97
) is
unitary if
A
and
B
are skew-Hermitian.

It turns out that the Wilcox expansion can be used to provide explicit expressions for
C_{n}
for any two indeterminates
A
and
B
, as we will
see in the sequel.

Before proceeding, it is important to remark that this problem differs from the Zassenhauss formula, in the sense that the expansion parameter
affects only one of the operators in the exponential. The solution goes as follows. We write

U(t)\equiv\mathrm{e}^{t(A+\varepsilon B)}=\mathrm{e}^{tA}\,V,

(98)

and solve the differential equation satisfied by
V

\frac{dV}{dt}=\varepsilon e^{-tA}Be^{tA}\,V\equiv\varepsilon\tilde{B}(t)V,\qquad V(0)=I

(99)

with the Wilcox expansion, so that

V(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon W_{1}(t)}\,\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon^{2}W_{2}(t)}\,\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon^{3}W_{3}(t)}\cdots.

(100)

The operators
C_{i}
in Eq. (
97
) are then obtained by taking
t=1
.

The successive terms
W_{j}(t)
in Eq. (
100
) can be determined either by the recursion (
33
)-(
38
) or
the explicit expression (
56
). For the first term we get

W_{1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{B}(s)dt=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-s\,\mathrm{ad}_{A}}B\,ds=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}\,t^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\mathrm{ad}_{A}^{k}B

(101)

and so the expression for
C_{1}
is given by

C_{1}=\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{ad}_{A}}}{\mathrm{ad}_{A}}B=B-\frac{1}{2}[A,B]+\frac{1}{3!}[A,[A,B]]-\frac{1}{4!}[A,[A,[A,B]]]+\cdots.

(102)

Although it is possible in principle to construct explicit expressions for
W_{2}
,
W_{3}
, etc., it is perhaps more convenient to apply the recursion
(
33
)-(
38
) for each particular application.

5.3
Illustrative examples

We next particularize the
Bellman problem
(
97
) to matrices where
closed expressions for
C_{1},C_{2},\ldots
can be obtained.
The idea is to illustrate the behaviour of the product expansion by computing explicitly high order terms with matrices in the
\mathrm{SU(2)}

and the
\mathrm{SO(3)}
Lie algebras.

Matrices
X
and
Y
in
\mathrm{SU(2)}
.

In the first example we choose
A=i\,a\sigma_{z}
and
B=i\,\sigma_{x}
,
where
i=\sqrt{-1}
,
a
is a real parameter and

\sigma_{x}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right),\quad\sigma_{y}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-i\\
i&0\end{array}\right),\quad\sigma_{z}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right)

(103)

6
Conclusions

When a linear system of differential equations, defined by the coefficient matrix
\lambda A(t)
, is transformed under
\exp(\lambda\int_{0}^{t}A(t^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t^{\prime})
, the coefficient matrix in the new representation becomes an infinite power series in
\lambda
, say

\tilde{A}(t)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\lambda^{k}\,a_{k}(t)
.
That is the first step of all matrix exponential methods to approximate the time-evolution operator. In the framework that we have introduced it is the first move in a sequence of exponential transformations that change the linear system from a representation to another with the goal that dynamics will become less and less relevant. Choosing the transformation
\exp(\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{A}(x)\mathrm{d}x)
as the second move and iterating this procedure afterwards yields the Fer expansion. Instead, choosing the transformation
\exp(\lambda\int_{0}^{t}a_{1}(x)\mathrm{d}x)
, i.e. the leading term of the new coefficient matrix, opens up Wilcox expansion. The framework allows intermediate expansions taking
\exp(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda^{k}\int_{0}^{t}a_{k}(x)\mathrm{d}x)
as initiator, as well as jumping between schemes, according with the particular requirements of the problem at hand.

We have seen that the theory of linear transformations (or changes of picture in the language of Quantum Mechanics) provides a unified framework to deal
with many different exponential perturbative expansions: whereas only one linear transformation reducing the dynamics to the trivial equation (
4
) or
to a system with a constant matrix
renders the Magnus
[
9
]

and the Floquet-Magnus
[
37
]
expansion, respectively, a sequence of such transformations with different choices of the new matrices lead
to Wilcox and Fer factorizations. From this perspective, other factorizations are possible depending on the particular problem at hand: one only has to
select appropriately the successive transformations.

In the case of Wilcox expansion we have provided an efficient recursive procedure to compute it. In addition, we have developed a method to build up an explicit expression for any
W_{n}
in terms of commutators. This is possible by using similar tools as in the case of the Magnus expansion,
namely by relating products of iterated integrals with the structure of the Hopf algebra of permutations, and by using special bases of nested
commutators.
A sufficient condition for the expansion convergence has also been obtained.

We have presented some application examples of the results about Wilcox expansion. Firstly, we have shown how to obtain Zassenhaus formula from Wilcox expansion which, in turn, may be interpreted as its continuous analogue. Secondly, we point out that Wilcox expansion solves the problem of
expanding the exponential
\exp(A+\epsilon B)
when
A
and
B
are non-commuting operators. We refer to it as Wilcox–Bellman expansion. Two practical cases on this respect have been analyzed up to high order. Interestingly, in one of them the convergence seems not to be uniform.
For convenience, the interested reader can find in
[
29
]
a
Mathematica
code generating general
explicit expressions and recurrences for the Wilcox expansion.

While a full assessment of the Wilcox expansion in comparison with Fer expansion is not the main purpose of this work, we can still mention here some of their
most distinctive features. Both types of expansions construct the solution of Eq. (
1
) as an infinite exponential factorization, but
in Wilcox the exponent of each factor
is proportional to successive powers of the expansion parameter
\lambda
, whereas in Fer each exponent contains an infinite sum of powers of
\lambda
.
This means that, when truncated after a given number of transformations, say
n
, the Wilcox expansion differs from the exact solution in the power

\lambda^{n+1}
. In other words, each term
W_{k}(t)

in the Wilcox expansion collects the effect of the perturbation at order
k
. On the other hand,
the Fer expansion, when truncated after
n
transformations, provides a much more accurate approximation. This is true, of course, if the infinite sums
involved in each transformation
are exactly computed, an almost impossible task unless the time dependence of
A(t)
is simple enough.
By contrast, we have explicit expressions
for each exponent
W_{k}(t)
in the Wilcox expansion for a generic
A(t)
and, by using the same techniques as in the Magnus expansion, we can construct
appropriate approximations to the iterated integrals if necessary. As the examples collected here and some other studies show
[
20
]
,
Wilcox expansion can provide
accurate results after only a few such transformations.
