Algorithms and Complexity for Computing Nash Equilibria in Adversarial Team Games Correspondence to ipanagea@ics.uci.edu .
Ioannis Anagnostides, Fivos Kalogiannis, Ioannis Panageas, Emmanouil-Vasileios Vlatakis-Gkaragkounis, Stephen McAleer

Abstract
Adversarial team games model multiplayer strategic interactions in which a team of identically-interested players is competing against an adversarial player in a zero-sum game. Such games capture many well-studied settings in game theory, such as congestion games, but go well-beyond to environments wherein the cooperation of one team—in the absence of explicit communication—is obstructed by competing entities; the latter setting remains poorly understood despite its numerous applications. Since the seminal work of Von Stengel and Koller (GEB ‘97), different solution concepts have received attention from an algorithmic standpoint. Yet, the complexity of the standard Nash equilibrium has remained open. In this paper, we settle this question by showing that computing a Nash equilibrium in adversarial team games belongs to the class continuous local search ( 𝖢𝖫𝖲 {\mathsf{CLS}} ) , thereby establishing 𝖢𝖫𝖲 {\mathsf{CLS}} -completeness by virtue of the recent 𝖢𝖫𝖲 {\mathsf{CLS}} -hardness result of Rubinstein and Babichenko (STOC ‘21) in potential games. To do so, we leverage linear programming duality to prove that any ϵ \epsilon -approximate stationary strategy for the team can be extended in polynomial time to an O ⁡ ( ϵ ) O(\epsilon) -approximate Nash equilibrium, where the O ⁡ ( ⋅ ) O(\cdot) notation suppresses polynomial factors. As a consequence, we show that the Moreau envelop of a suitable best response function acts as a potential under certain natural gradient-based dynamics.



1
Introduction

Computing
Nash equilibria
has served as one of the most influential problems in the development of algorithmic game theory, with a myriad of applications in multi-agent systems. In the special case of
two-player zero-sum
games
(
von Neumann and Morgenstern 2007
)
, computing a Nash equilibrium is known to be equivalent to linear programming, thereby admitting efficient algorithms
(
Adler 2013
)
. In stark contrast, the problem becomes computationally intractable in general games
(
Daskalakis et al. 2009
;
Chen et al. 2009
)
, a hardness that persists even under approximate equilibrium concepts
(
Rubinstein 2016
;
Daskalakis 2013
)
. As a result, a central research endeavor is to identify and characterize classes of games that elude the aforedescribed intractability barriers.

In this paper, we study
adversarial team games
, a fundamental and well-studied setting in economics in which a team of identically-interested players is competing against an adversary in a zero-sum game.
Such games capture many important applications, including competition between firms, decision-making in distributed computer systems, diplomacy between political entities, and biological systems;
e.g.
, see
(
Coase 1937
;
Marschak 1955
;
Ohta 2002
;
Schulman and Vazirani 2019
)
. Furthermore, that class incorporates as special cases two-player zero-sum games and
potential games
, two of the most well-studied classes of games in the literature, but goes well-beyond to settings that feature both competing and shared interests. In those environments, it is understood that if players have the capacity to communicate without any restrictions, the game reduces to a standard two-player zero-sum interaction. However, coordination can be difficult or even infeasible to achieve for many applications, an impediment recognized as one of the major challenges in group decision-making
(
Marschak 1955
;
von Stengel and Koller 1997
;
Schulman and Vazirani 2019
)
. From an algorithmic standpoint, commencing from the pioneering work of
von Stengel and Koller 1997
, the complexity of different equilibrium concepts in adversarial team games has received extensive attention in recent years (see our overview in
Section

1.2
). Yet, surprisingly, the complexity of the standard Nash equilibrium has eluded prior investigations, bringing us to our central question:

Question 1
.

What is the complexity of computing Nash equilibria in adversarial team games?

1.1
Our Contributions

Our primary contribution is to establish that computing an approximate Nash equilibrium in adversarial team games lies in
continuous local search (
\mathsf{CLS}
)
, a complexity class introduced by
Daskalakis and Papadimitriou 2011
that has received extensive attention in recent years
(
Hubácek and Yogev 2020
;
Fearnley et al. 2020
;
Göös et al. 2018
;
Daskalakis et al. 2018
;
Fearnley et al. 2017
;
Gärtner et al. 2018
;
Göös et al. 2022a
;
Göös et al. 2022b
;
Tewolde et al. 2023
)
, culminating in the recent breakthrough result of
Fearnley et al. 2021
. Thus, when combined with the recent
{\mathsf{CLS}}
-hardness result of
Babichenko and Rubinstein 2021
for potential games—a member of adversarial team games (see
Section

4
), we settle
1
:

Theorem
.

Computing an
\epsilon
-approximate Nash equilibrium in adversarial team games is
{\mathsf{CLS}}
-complete.

We remark that, as is standard, the above theorem applies in the regime where
\log(1/\epsilon)
is a polynomial; we do not address the complexity of computing an
exact
Nash equilibrium, which is left as an interesting open problem (see
2
).

Given that computing Nash equilibria is well-known to be in
{\mathsf{PPAD}}

(
Daskalakis et al. 2009
;
Papadimitriou 1994
)
, membership in
{\mathsf{CLS}}
reduces to showing that the problem is in the class
polynomial local search (
{\mathsf{PLS}}
)

(
Johnson et al. 1988
)
by virtue of the recent collapse
{\mathsf{CLS}}={\mathsf{PPAD}}\cap{\mathsf{PLS}}

(
Fearnley et al. 2021
)
. To do so, the starting point is the fundamental
extendibility
of team-maxmin equilibria shown by
von Stengel and Koller 1997
; namely,
any team-maxmin profile can be extended to an equilibrium of the game
. Unfortunately, computing even an approximate team-maxmin profile is computationally prohibitive
(
Borgs et al. 2010
;
Hansen et al. 2008
)
, being
{\mathsf{FNP}}
-hard
(
Celli and Gatti 2018
)
, thereby necessitating a more refined approach.

In light of this, one of our main insigths is to relax the requirement of the extendibility result of
von Stengel and Koller 1997
. To be more precise, let us introduce some basic notation about our setting; the formal description is deferred to
Section

2
. For an adversarial team game
\Gamma
, we let
\mathcal{X}
be the joint strategy space of the team and
\mathcal{Y}
be the strategy space of the adversary. We further suppose that
U:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
is the (mixed extension) of the utility function. We will also write
{\mathsf{poly}}(\Gamma)
for factors that are polynomial in the natural parameters of the game. In this context, we show the following central characterization.

Proposition

(Formal Version in
Theorem

3.1
)
.

Let
\bm{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}
be an
\epsilon
-near stationary point of the function
\bm{x}\mapsto\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}U(\bm{x},\bm{y})
. Then, there exists
\bm{y}^{*}\in\mathcal{Y}
such that
(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})
is an
(\epsilon\cdot{\mathsf{poly}}(\Gamma))
-approximate Nash equilibrium of
\Gamma
. Further,
\bm{y}^{*}
can be computed in polynomial time.

This characterization is established via linear programming duality, similarly to the result of
von Stengel and Koller 1997
. However, unlike the extension of
von Stengel and Koller 1997
, the Proposition above is based on an approximate stationary point
\bm{x}^{*}
, which, crucially, can be efficiently computed. Indeed, we analyze a natural gradient-based algorithm (
Algorithm

1
), which additionally incorporates a suitable linear program that performs the extension from
\bm{x}^{*}
to
(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})
. Importantly, we show in
Theorem

3.3
that there exists a polynomially computable potential function—namely, the approximate value of the
Moreau envelope
(
Definition

2.1
) of
\bm{x}\mapsto\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}U(\bm{x},\bm{y})
. Along with the fact that each iteration of
Algorithm

1
can be implemented in polynomial time, this suffices for showing membership in
{\mathsf{PLS}}
, modulo some further technical issues that we address. Incidentally, our algorithm also leads to a (pseudo) fully polynomial-time approximation scheme for computing
\epsilon
-approximate Nash equilibria in adversarial team games (
Corollary

3.2
).

From a broader viewpoint, we find it surprising that computing Nash equilibria in adversarial team games has eluded prior research, although it subsumes many settings that have received tremendous interest in algorithmic game theory, such as congestion games. But, importantly, our setting also covers more uncharted territory; one interesting such example, discussed in
Section

4
, is that of congestion games with
adversarial costs
(
cf
.
(
Babaioff et al. 2009
)
). We hope that our results will encourage further research in those directions. Indeed, as we highlight in
Section

5
, there are still many exciting open problems. Perhaps most notably, while our paper focuses on the canonical setting where the team is facing a single adversary, we leave as a challenging open question the complexity of the more general problem in which both teams have multiple players
(
Schulman and Vazirani 2019
)
.

1.2
Further Related Work

The study of team games from an algorithm standpoint dates back at least to the pioneering work of
von Stengel and Koller 1997
. In particular, they introduced the
team-maxmin equilibrium (TME)
for games wherein a team of players—with identical payoffs—faces a single adversary. A TME profile prescribes a mixed strategy for each team member so that the minimal expected team payoff over all possible responses of the adversary (potentially knowing the play of the team) is the maximum possible. That is, in a TME profile the players of the team guarantee the best possible payoff, given the lack of coordination. Naturally, if enough communication is added between the team members, they would be able to coordinate all their actions, and the team-maxmin payoff would be identical to the value of the game when viewed as a two-player zero-sum game. However, prior work
(
von Stengel and Koller 1997
;
Schulman and Vazirani 2019
)
has provided ample motivation for studying team games with no coordination or communication. In other words, each player can only use a separate mixed (randomized) strategy, but correlated randomization (beyond the structure of the game) is not allowed.

TME enjoy a number of desirable properties. For one, a team-maxmin equilibrium has a
unique value
, a compelling antidote to the equilibrium selection problem in general games. Further, that value is the optimal payoff that individual players can guarantee: no equilibrium of the game can be better for the team than a team-maxmin equilibrium
(
von Stengel and Koller 1997
)
, while the worst equilibrium can be far from a TME in terms of efficiency
(
Basilico et al. 2017
)
. Unfortunately, computing a TME suffers from computationaly intractability, being
\mathsf{FNP}
-hard
(
Hansen et al. 2008
;
Borgs et al. 2010
)
. Nevertheless, several methods have been designed to tackle the problem in practice
(
Zhang and An 2020a
;
Zhang and An 2020b
;
Basilico et al. 2017
)
.

A different avenue for addressing the intractability of TME that has received attention in recent years is to incorporate some correlation device, thereby allowing players to coordinate
ex ante

(
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Farina et al. 2018
;
Farina et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Zhang and Sandholm 2021
;
Celli and Gatti 2018
;
Celli et al. 2019
;
Carminati et al. 2022
)
. Nevertheless, even with a correlation device, the problem is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard
(
Chu and Halpern 2001
)
, although it has been shown that efficient algorithms exist as long as the asymmetry between the players’ information is limited
(
Zhang et al. 2021
)
. Further, it is worth noting that team equilibria are also useful for extensive-form two-player zero-sum games where one of the players has
imperfect recall

(
Kaneko and Kline 1995
;
Piccione and Rubinstein 1997
)
.

Moreover, in a subsequent work,
Kalogiannis et al. 2022
extended our techniques to obtain an
{\mathsf{FPTAS}}
for computing Nash equilibria in the more general class of adversarial team
Markov
games. We also refer to the works of
Kartik et al. 2022
and
Lagoudakis and Parr 2002
, as well as the many references therein, for other pertinent considerations. Finally, we note that team games have also been recently analyzed from a mean-field perspective
(
Guan et al. 2023
)
.

two-team zero-sum game

U_{B}(\bm{a},\bm{b})=-U_{A}(\bm{a},\bm{b})=U(\bm{a},\bm{b})
. As a result, players in team

A
aim to minimize
U
(hereinafter, minimizers).

To ensure existence of equilibria, players are allowed to randomize, that is, select a probability distribution over their set of actions. We define the product distributions
\bm{x}=(\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n})
,
\bm{y}=(\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{m})
as the joint strategies of teams
A
and
B

\bm{x}_{i}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}_{i})

\bm{y}_{j}\in\Delta(\mathcal{B}_{j})

j\in\mathcal{M}

\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\prod_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{X}_{i}\coloneqq\prod_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\Delta(\mathcal{A}_{i})
and
\mathcal{Y}\coloneqq\prod_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{Y}_{j}\coloneqq\prod_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\Delta(\mathcal{B}_{j})
for the space of mixed strategy profiles of teams
A
and
B

U:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\ni(\bm{x},\bm{y})\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{a},\bm{b})\sim(\bm{x},\bm{y})}U(\bm{a},\bm{b})

Nash equilibrium.

In terms of solution concepts, our main focus is on computing
\epsilon
-approximate Nash Equilibria (NE)
, that is, a strategy profile
(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
such that for any possible unilateral deviation
\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{i}
from any player
i\in\mathcal{N}
or
\bm{y}_{j}\in\mathcal{Y}_{j}
from any player
j\in\mathcal{M}
,

\begin{array}[]{cc}U(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})\leq U(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{*}_{-i},\bm{y}^{*})+\epsilon\textrm{ and }U(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})\geq U(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}_{j},\bm{y}^{*}_{-j})-\epsilon.\end{array}

(NE)

Here, we used the standard shorthand notation
\bm{x}_{-i}=(\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{i-1},\bm{x}_{i+1},\bm{x}_{n})\in\prod_{i^{\prime}\neq i}\Delta(\mathcal{A}_{i^{\prime}})
, and similarly for
\bm{y}_{-j}\in\prod_{j^{\prime}\neq j}\Delta(\mathcal{B}_{j^{\prime}})
. The existence of such an equilibrium point is guaranteed by Nash’s theorem
(
Nash 1951
)
. We will say that the strategy profile
(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})
is
pure
if each player is selecting an action with probability
1
.

Team-maxmin equilibrium.

Another natural equilibrium concept tailored to two-team zero-sum games is the
team-maxmin equilibrium (TME)

(
von Stengel and Koller 1997
)
. If a joint strategy profile
(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
is a team-maxmin equilibrium, then

\bm{y}^{*}\in\arg\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}U(\bm{x},\bm{y})\textrm{ and }\bm{x}^{*}\in\arg\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}U(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{*}).

(Maxmin)

Any team-maxmin equilibrium yields the same value
(
von Stengel and Koller 1997
)
, denoted by
U_{\textrm{maxmin}}
. Similarly, a team-minmax equilibrium
(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
satisfies

\bm{x}^{*}\in\arg\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}U(\bm{x},\bm{y})\textrm{ and }\bm{y}^{*}\in\arg\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}U(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}).

(Minmax)

Any team-minmax equilibrium yields the same value, namely
U_{\textrm{minmax}}
. But, unlike two-player zero-sum games, team games do not enjoy a minimax theorem. That is, while
U_{\textrm{minmax}}\geq U_{\textrm{maxmin}}
(by weak duality), equality does not hold in general
(
Schulman and Vazirani 2019
)
.

Remark 1

(Succinct representation)
.

A standard issue encountered in multiplayer games is that the utility tensors describing the game have size that scales exponentially with the number of players. As is common, we will bypass this issue by focusing on multiplayer two-team zero-sum games that have the
polynomial expectation property

(
Papadimitriou and Roughgarden 2008
)
, formalized below (
1
). This property is known to hold for most classes of succinctly representable games
(
Papadimitriou and Roughgarden 2008
)
, albeit not for all
(
Daskalakis et al. 2006
)
.

To state the next assumption below, we will use the notation
|\bm{x}|
to represent the number of bits required to represent vector
\bm{x}
.

Assumption 1

(Polynomial Expectation Property)
.

For any (mixed) joint strategy profile
(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Delta(\mathcal{A}_{i})\times\prod_{j=1}^{m}\Delta(\mathcal{B}_{j})
, we can compute (exactly) the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{a},\bm{b})\sim(\bm{x},\bm{y})}U(\bm{a},\bm{b})\eqqcolon U(\bm{x},\bm{y})
in time
{\mathsf{poly}}(n,\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\mathcal{A}_{i}|,|\mathcal{B}|,|\bm{x}|,|\bm{y}|)
.

2.2
Basic Background on Optimization

First, it will be useful to note that any point
(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
that is a solution to the following variational inequality problem

\nabla_{\bm{x}}U(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})(\bm{x}^{*}-\bm{x})\leq\epsilon\;\forall\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}\textrm{ and }\nabla_{\bm{y}}U(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})(\bm{y}^{*}-\bm{y})\geq-\epsilon\;\forall\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y},

(VI)

must also be an
\epsilon
-approximate NE. Further, the notion of the
Moreau envelope
, introduced below, will be crucial for our potential argument in
Section

3
.

Definition 2.1

(Moreau Envelope.)
.

Consider any game
\Gamma
. We define the max-Moreau envelope with parameter
\lambda>0
as

\phi_{\lambda}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\min_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}U(\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{y})+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.

It is well-known that for a sufficiently small
\lambda
,
\phi_{\lambda}
defined above is a differentiable function;
e.g.
, see
(
Davis and Drusvyatskiy 2018
)
. In particular, if
\ell
is a smoothness parameter of
U
, it suffices to take
\lambda\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\ell}
. We will also need the following well-known properties.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
U(\bm{x},\bm{y})
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous, then the function
\bm{x}\mapsto\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}U(\bm{x},\bm{y})
is also
L
-Lipschitz continuous.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
U(\bm{x},\bm{y})
is
\ell
-smooth, then the function
\bm{x}\mapsto\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}U(\bm{x},\bm{y})
is
\ell
-weakly convex, in the sense that the function
\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}U(\bm{x},\bm{y})+\frac{\ell}{2}\|\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}
is convex.

Finally, we will need the definition of a stationary point for the nonsmooth function
\phi:\bm{x}\mapsto\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}U(\bm{x},\bm{y})
, formally introduced below.

Definition 2.2

(Approximate Stationarity)
.

We call a point
\bm{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}
an
\epsilon
-approximate stationary point of
\phi_{1/(2\ell)}
if

\min_{\bm{\delta}\in T_{\mathcal{X}}(\bm{x}^{*}),\left\|\bm{\delta}\right\|_{2}=1}\bm{\delta}^{\top}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\phi_{1/(2\ell)}(\bm{x}^{*})\geq-\epsilon,

(1)

where
T_{\mathcal{X}}(\bm{x}^{*})
is the tangent cone at
\bm{x}^{*}
.

A point
\bm{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}
that satisfies (
1
) will also sometimes be referred to as
\epsilon
-near stationary
point of
\phi
. The next lemma connects the closeness of a point to stationarity to the distance from its
proximal point
. More precisely, we recall that the proximal point of
\bm{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}
is defined as
\hat{\bm{x}}\coloneqq\arg\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{\phi(\bm{x})+\ell\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}
, and it is indeed well-defined
(
Lin et al. 2020
)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose that
\bm{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}
is an
\epsilon
-approximate stationary point of
\phi_{1/(2\ell)}
. If

\mathcal{X}\ni\hat{\bm{x}}\coloneqq\arg\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{\phi(\bm{x})+\ell\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}

is the proximal point of
\bm{x}^{*}
, then
\|\bm{x}^{*}-\hat{\bm{x}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2\ell}
.

The proof of this lemma is fairly standard; see, for example, the work of
Lin et al. 2020
.

3
The Complexity of Adversarial Team Games

In this section, we establish our main result: computing
\epsilon
-approximate Nash equilibria in adversarial team games is complete for the class
{\mathsf{CLS}}
, as formalized in
Theorem

3.4
. We organize our argument as follows.

•

First, in
Section

3.1
we show that an
\epsilon
-approximate stationary point
\bm{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}
of the Moreau envelope
\phi_{1/(2\ell)}

can be extended to an
O(\epsilon)
-approximate Nash equilibrium
(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})\in\mathcal{Y}
(
Theorem

3.1
). This extends a result of
von Stengel and Koller 1997
, and it is established via strong duality.

•

Next, in
Section

3.2
we construct an explicit polynomially computable potential function (
Theorem

3.3
) using gradient descent on a suitable function, along with the extendibility argument of
Theorem

3.1
.

3.1
From a Stationary Point to a Nash Equilibrium via Strong Duality

Suppose that
\bm{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}
is an
\epsilon
-approximate stationary point of
\phi_{1/(2\ell)}
. We will show (in
Theorem

3.1
) that
\bm{x}^{*}
can be extended to an
O(\epsilon)
-approximate Nash equilibrium profile. A similar in spirit extendibility argument was known from the work of
von Stengel and Koller 1997
, but that required that
\bm{x}^{*}
is a team-maxmin profile, which is computationally prohibitive for establishing membership in
{\mathsf{CLS}}
. On the other hand, as we formalize in
Section

3.2
, approximate stationary points of
\phi_{1/(2\ell)}
can be computed in time
{\mathsf{poly}}(\Gamma,1/\epsilon)
, where
{\mathsf{poly}}(\Gamma)\coloneqq{\mathsf{poly}}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\mathcal{A}_{i}|,|\mathcal{B}|,V)
; here,
V>0
denotes the maximum absolute value of a utility in the payoff tensor.

Let
L={\mathsf{poly}}(\Gamma)
be the Lipschitz parameter of
U(\bm{x},\bm{y})
(
Lemma

A.1
), and
\ell={\mathsf{poly}}(\Gamma)
be the smoothness parameter of
U(\bm{x},\bm{y})
(analogously to
Lemma

A.1
). The theorem below is the main result of this subsection. Our extendibility argument leverages strong duality, as in
(
von Stengel and Koller 1997
)
, but, crucially, our argument only requires
\bm{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}

Theorem 3.1

Definition 3.3

Theorem 3.2

Let function
V_{\bm{x}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined as
V_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})=\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}U(\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{y})+\ell\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}^{2}

{\mathsf{poly}}(n,\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\mathcal{A}_{i}|,|\mathcal{B}|,\log(1/\epsilon))

such that

\min_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}V_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\leq V_{\bm{x}}(\mathcal{A}lg(\bm{x}))\leq\min_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}V_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})+\epsilon.

Proof.

First, we will show that a subgradient of
V_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})
for any rational input
\bm{x}^{\prime}
is rational and can be computed both efficiently and accurately.
For the first summand, we recall Danskin’s theorem to compute the gradient of
\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}U(\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{y})
.

Fact 1

(Generalized Danskin Theorem,
(

\partial\phi(\bm{x}^{\prime})=\partial\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}U(\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{y})=\left\{\nabla_{\bm{x}}U(\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{y}^{\star}(\bm{x}^{\prime}))\Big|\ \ \bm{y}^{\star}\in\arg\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}U(\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{y})\right\}.

For a fixed
\bm{x}^{\prime}
, we can trivially compute a rational best response strategy
\bm{y}^{\star}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Y}
for the adversary (using
1

\phi(\bm{x}^{\prime})
. For the second summand, we have that
\partial_{\bm{x}}\{\ell\cdot(\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{2})\}=2\ell(\bm{x}-\bm{x^{\prime}})
.
Therefore, the latter argument implies that for a given
\bm{x}^{\prime}
, we can compute efficiently and accurately a subgradient of
V_{\bm{x}}(\cdot)
.
Additionally, for the constraint set
\mathcal{X}
we can have easily a strong separation oracle as it is a product of simplices. Therefore, either via subgradient cuts of central-cut ellipsoid method (see
(
Grötschel et al. 2012

with rational coordinates such

\min_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}V_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\leq V_{\bm{x}}(\hat{\bm{x}})\leq\min_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}V_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})+\epsilon
.

Remark 3

(Correlated Adversaries)
.

Another notable application of having a single adversary is the case where the adversary team has multiple players, but with the twist that the adversaries are allowed to correlate their strategies—
i.e.
, the team is facing a “virtual” player. However, in that case the action space of that virtual player grows exponentially with the number of adversaries
m
, and so establishing polynomial-time algorithms when
m\gg 1
requires further work.

4
Additional Applications of Two-Team Zero-Sum Games

In this section, we discuss about some additional interesting settings that can be cast as two-team zero-sum games. For the sake of generality, here we allow the adversary team to have more than a single player.

Adversarial potential games.

Consider a normal form game with two sets of players
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{M}
. Following the conventions of
Section

2.1
, the minimizers
\mathcal{N}
will be associated with cost functions, while the maximizers
\mathcal{M}
will be associated with utilities. Further, there exists an underlying
potential function

\Phi:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
, so that

(i)

for each minimizer
i\in\mathcal{N}
and strategies
\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{i}
,

c_{i}(\bm{x}_{i}^{\prime},\bm{x}_{-i},\bm{y})-c_{i}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{-i},\bm{y})=\Phi(\bm{x}_{i}^{\prime},\bm{x}_{-i},\bm{y})-\Phi(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{-i},\bm{y});

(25)

(ii)

for each maximizer
j\in\mathcal{M}
and strategies
\bm{y}_{j},\bm{y}_{j}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}_{j}
,

u_{j}(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{j}^{\prime},\bm{y}_{-j})-u_{j}(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{j},\bm{y}_{-j})=\Phi(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{j}^{\prime},\bm{y}_{-j})-\Phi(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{j},\bm{y}_{-j}).

(26)

While this setting is not a two-team zero-sum game, it can still be captured by one, namely
\Gamma(n,m,\Phi)
. More precisely, (
25
) implies that
c_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\Phi(\bm{x},\bm{y})+\Psi_{i}(\bm{x}_{-i},\bm{y})
, for any
i\in\mathcal{N}
and
(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, where
\Psi_{i}
does not depend on
\bm{x}_{i}
, and (
26
) implies that
u_{j}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\Phi(\bm{x},\bm{y})+\Psi^{\prime}_{j}(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{-j})
, where
\Psi_{j}
does not depend on
\bm{y}_{j}
. As a result, we arrive at the following conclusion.

Proposition 4.1
.

A strategy profile satisfying (
VI
) with respect to the two-team zero-sum game
\Gamma(n,m,\Phi)
will be an
\epsilon
-approximate Nash equilibrium of the original adversarial potential game.

Congestion games with adversarial costs.

A
congestion game
, dating back at least to the work of
Rosenthal 1973
, is defined by the tuple
\Gamma=(\mathcal{N};\mathcal{E};

(\mathcal{A}_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{N}};(c_{e})_{e\in\mathcal{E}})
, where

•

\mathcal{N}
is the set of
agents
with
n=|\mathcal{N}|
;

•

\mathcal{E}
is a set of
edges
(also known as
resources
or
bins
or
facilities
);

•

the action space
\mathcal{A}_{i}
of each player
i\in\mathcal{N}
is a collection of subsets of
\mathcal{E}
(
\mathcal{A}_{i}\subseteq 2^{\mathcal{E}}
); and

•

c_{e}:\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is the cost (
e.g.
, latency) function associated with edge
e\in\mathcal{E}
.

More precisely, for an action profile
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}_{i}
, the cost of player
i\in\mathcal{N}
is given by
c_{i}(\bm{a})\coloneqq\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}c_{e}(\ell_{e}(\bm{a}))
, where
\ell_{e}(\bm{a})
denotes the number of players using
e\in\mathcal{E}
under
\bm{a}
—the load of edge
e
.

Now we introduce a twist to this well-studied setting: there is an additional “adversarial” player who chooses the cost functions of the edges from a finite family of functions
\mathcal{F}_{e}=\{c_{e,1},...,c_{e,m_{e}}\}
, with
|\mathcal{F}_{e}|=m_{e}
. We denote by
\bm{b}
the action of the adversary, so that
b_{e}\in\mathcal{F}_{e}
denotes the cost function
c_{e,b_{e}}
the adversary chose for edge
e\in\mathcal{E}
. In this context, every agent is aiming at minimizing its cost, while the adversary is aiming at maximizing the potential function
\Phi(\bm{a},\bm{b})\coloneqq\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell_{e}(\bm{a})}c_{e,b_{e}}(j)
. A similar setting is the case of nonatomic congestion games with malicious players presented by
Babaioff et al. 2009
; apart from the players that strive to minimize their cost, part of the network’s flow is controlled by a malicious (adversarial) player that attempts to maximize the load experienced by the set of the cost-minimizing players.

This setting can be equivalently formulated as follows: there is a collection of congestion games (with respect to the team players), and the adversary has the power of selecting the underlying congestion game. As such, computing Nash equilibria is easily seen to be cast in the framework of
Section

3
. One caveat here is that the complexity of our algorithms depends polynomially on the number of actions, which could be exponential in the description of the congestion game. One promising approach to address this issue is to employ
multiplicative weights
instead of gradient-descent-type dynamics, as in
Algorithm

1
, in conjunction with the
kernel trick

(
Takimoto and Warmuth 2003
)
when the underlying action set is structured
(
Beaglehole et al. 2023
)
.

5
Conclusions and Open Problems

Our main contribution in this paper was to establish that computing an approximate Nash equilibrium in adversarial team games is
{\mathsf{CLS}}
-complete. This further expands the class of problems known to be complete for that class, and extends the recent
{\mathsf{CLS}}
-completeness of
Babichenko and Rubinstein 2021
(for potential games) to an important class of games that combines both cooperation and competition; we argue that understanding the complexity of such settings serves as an important step to demystify more realistic multi-agent environments.

While we have focused on the setting where the team is facing a single adversary, the canonical case treated in the literature starting from the work of
von Stengel and Koller 1997
(recall our overview in
Section

1.2
), a natural question is to investigate the complexity of computing Nash equilibria in the more general setting where both teams have multiple players. In this context, we make the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1
.

Computing an
\epsilon
-approximate Nash equilibrium, for a sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, in two-team zero-sum games with
n,m\gg 1
is
{\mathsf{PPAD}}
-complete.

Even the complexity status of the problem when
n,m=2
remains a challenging open problem. An important implication of any hardness result for that problem would be on the computation of equilibria in nonconvex-nonconcave min-max optimization
(
Daskalakis et al. 2021
)
, given that computing equilibria in two-team zero-sum games can be viewed as a special case of the former problem. In particular, the hardness of
Daskalakis et al. 2021
rests on the assumption that the players’ strategy sets are coupled, while answering
1
necessitates going from coupled to product constraints, which is recognized as a major challenge. An even more basic question: Is there a
{\mathsf{PTAS}}
for computing Nash equilibria when both teams have multiple players?

Returning to the canonical case of a single adversary, it would be interesting to understand the complexity of computing (approximate) Nash equilibria when
n=O(1)
. While our
{\mathsf{CLS}}
membership clearly applies in that case, one can no longer rely on the work of
Babichenko and Rubinstein 2021
to obtain a non-trivial hardness result given that Nash equilibria in identical-interest games with a constant number of players can be trivially computed in polynomial time (by simply identifying the maximum entry in the payoff tensor). In fact, even the case where
n=2
is a challenging open problem.

Moreover, the situation becomes even bleaker when insisting on an exact equilibrium, since one has to deal with the inherent irrationality of equilibria in adversarial team games
(
von Stengel and Koller 1997
)
. For this reason,
{\mathsf{FIXP}}
, introduced by
Etessami and Yannakakis 2010
, seems to be a plausible candidate for capturing the complexity of the problem:

Conjecture 2
.

Computing an exact Nash equilibrium in adversarial team games is
{\mathsf{FIXP}}
-complete.

Finally, our focus in this paper has been on games represented (succinctly) in normal form. It would be interesting to extend our
{\mathsf{CLS}}
membership to other classes of games which are not of
polynomial type
, in that the number of actions of each player is superpolynomial, most notably to extensive-form games. One major challenge that arises there is that the per-iteration complexity of gradient-based dynamics is no longer polynomial, thereby necessitating a more refined approach that leverages the underlying structure.
