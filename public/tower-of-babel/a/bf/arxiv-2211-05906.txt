A New Conjecture on Hardness of Low-Degree 2-CSP’s with Implications to Hardness of Densest k k -Subgraph and Other Problems
Julia Chuzhoy, Mina Dalirrooyfard, Vadim Grinberg, Zihan Tan

Abstract
We propose a new conjecture on hardness of low-degree 2 2 -CSP’s, and show that new hardness of approximation results for Densest k k -Subgraph and several other problems, including a graph partitioning problem, and a variation of the Graph Crossing Number problem, follow from this conjecture. The conjecture can be viewed as occupying a middle ground between the d d -to- 1 1 conjecture, and hardness results for 2 2 -CSP’s that can be obtained via standard techniques, such as Parallel Repetition combined with standard 2 2 -prover protocols for the 3SAT problem. We hope that this work will motivate further exploration of hardness of 2 2 -CSP’s in the regimes arising from the conjecture. We believe that a positive resolution of the conjecture will provide a good starting point for further hardness of approximation proofs. Another contribution of our work is proving that the problems that we consider are roughly equivalent from the approximation perspective. Some of these problems arose in previous work, from which it appeared that they may be related to each other. We formalize this relationship in this work.

Introduction

In this paper we consider several graph optimization problems, the most prominent and extensively studied of which is
Densest
k
-Subgraph
.
One of the main motivations of this work is to advance our understanding of the approximability of these problems. Towards this goal, we propose a new conjecture on the hardness of a class of 2-
CSP
problems, that we call
Low-Degree CSP Conjecture
, and we show that new hardness of approximation results for all these problems follow from this conjecture. We believe that the conjecture is interesting in its own right, as it can be seen as occupying a middle ground between the
d
-to-
1
conjecture, and the type of hardness of approximation results that one can obtain for 2-
CSP
problems via standard methods (such as using constant-factor hardness of approximation results for 3-SAT, combined with standard 2-prover protocols and Parallel Repetition). While our conditional hardness of approximation proofs are combinatorial and algorithmic in nature, we hope that this work will inspire complexity theorists to study the conjecture, and also lead to other hardness of approximation proofs that combine both combinatorial and algebraic techniques.

We prove a new conditional hardness of approximation result for
Densest
k
-Subgraph
based on
Low-Degree CSP Conjecture
.
In addition to the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem, we study three other problems. The first problem, called
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, recently arose in the hardness of approximation proof of the Node-Disjoint Paths problem of
[
CKN21
]
, who mention that the problem appears similar to
Densest
k
-Subgraph
, but could not formalize this intuition. We also study a new problem that we call
Dense
k
-Coloring
, that can be viewed as a natural middle ground between
Densest
k
-Subgraph
and
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
. The fourth problem that we study is a variation of the notoriously difficult
Minimum Crossing Number
problem, that we call
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
. This problem also arose implicitly in
[
CKN21
]
. We show that all four problems are roughly equivalent from the approximation perspective, in the regime where the approximation factors are somewhat large (but some of our reductions require quasi-polynomial time). We then derive conditional hardness of approximation results for all these problems based on these reductions and the conditional hardness of
Densest
k
-Subgraph
.

The main contribution of this paper is thus twofold: first, we propose a new conjecture on hardness of
CSP
’s and show that a number of interesting hardness of approximation results follow from it. Second, we establish a close connection between the four problems that we study. The remainder of the Introduction is organized as follows.
We start by providing a brief overview of the four problems that we study in this paper. We then state the
Low-Degree CSP Conjecture
and put it into context with existing results and well-known conjectures. Finally, we provide a more detailed overview of our results and techniques.

Densest
k
-Subgraph.

In the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem, given an
n
-vertex graph
G
and an integer
k>1
, the goal is to compute a subset
S
of
k
vertices of
G
, while maximizing the number of edges in
G[S]
.

Densest
k
-Subgraph
is one of the most basic graph optimization problems that has been studied extensively (see e.g.
[
KP93
,
FS+97
,
FPK01
,
FL01
,
Fei02
,
Kho06
,
GL09
,
BCC+10
,
AAM+11
,
BCG+12
,
Bar15
,
BKRW17
,
Man17
,
CDK+18
,
Man18
,
Lin18
,
Sot20
,
CCH+20
,
Han22
]
). At the same time it seems notoriously difficult, and despite this extensive work, our understanding of its approximability is still incomplete.
The best current approximation algorithm for
Densest
k
-Subgraph
, due to
[
BCC+10
]
, achieves, for every
\varepsilon>0
, an
O(n^{1/4+\varepsilon})
-approximation, in time
n^{O(1/\varepsilon)}
.
Even though the problem appears to be very hard, its hardness of approximation proof has been elusive.
For example, no constant-factor hardness of approximation proofs for
Densest
k
-Subgraph
are currently known under the standard
\mbox{\sf P}\neq\mbox{\sf NP}
assumption, or even the stronger assumption that
\mbox{\sf NP}\not\subseteq\mbox{\sf BPTIME}(n^{\textnormal{poly}\log n})
. In a breakthrough result, Khot
[
Kho06
]
proved a factor-
c
hardness of approximation for
Densest
k
-Subgraph
, for some small constant
c
, assuming that
\mbox{\sf NP}\not\subseteq\cap_{\varepsilon>0}\mbox{\sf BPTIME}(2^{n^{\varepsilon}})
. Several other papers proved constant and super-constant hardness of approximation results for
Densest
k
-Subgraph
under
average-case
complexity assumptions: namely that no efficient algorithm can refute random
3
-SAT or random
k
-AND formulas
[
Fei02
,
AAM+11
]
. Additionally,
a factor
2^{\Omega(\log^{2/3}n)}
-hardness of approximation was shown under
assumptions on solving Planted Clique
[
AAM+11
]
. In a recent breakthrough, Manurangsi
[
Man17
]
proved that, under the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH), the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem is hard to approximate to within factor
n^{1/(\log\log n)^{c}}
, for some constant
c
. Proving a super-constant hardness of
Densest
k
-Subgraph
under weaker complexity assumptions remains a tantalizing open question that we attempt to address in this paper. Unfortunately, it seems unlikely that the techniques of
[
Man17
]
can yield such a result. In this paper we show that, assuming the
Low-Degree CSP Conjecture
that we introduce,
Densest
k
-Subgraph
is
NP
-hard to approximate to within factor
2^{(\log n)^{\varepsilon}}
, for some constant
\varepsilon>0
.

The
(r,h)
-Graph Partitioning Problem.

A recent paper
[
CKN21
]
on the hardness of approximation of the Node-Disjoint Paths (
NDP
) problem formulated and studied a new graph partitioning problem, called
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
. The input to the problem is a graph
G
, and two integers,
r
and
h
. The goal is to compute
r
vertex-disjoint subgraphs
H_{1},\ldots,H_{r}
of
G
, such that for each
1\leq i\leq r
,
|E(H_{i})|\leq h
, while maximizing
\sum_{i=1}^{r}|E(H_{i})|
. A convenient intuitive way of thinking about this problem is that we are interested in obtaining a balanced partition of the graph
G
into
r
vertex-disjoint subgraphs, so that the subgraphs contain sufficiently many edges. Unlike standard graph partitioning problems, that typically aim to minimize the number of edges connecting the different subgraphs in the solution, our goal is to maximize the total number of edges that are contained in the subgraphs. In order to avoid trivial solutions, in which one of the subgraphs contains almost the entire graph
G
, and the remaining subgraphs are almost empty, we place an upper bound
h
on the number of edges that each subgraph may contribute towards the solution. Note that the subgraphs
H_{i}
of
G
in the solution need not be vertex-induced subgraphs.

The work of
[
CKN21
]
attempted to use
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
as a proxy problem for proving hardness of approximation of
NDP
. Their results imply that
NDP
is at least as hard to approximate as
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, to within polylogarithmic factors. In order to prove hardness of
NDP
, it would then be sufficient to show that
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
is hard to approximate.
Unfortunately,
[
CKN21
]
were unable to do so. Instead, they considered a generalization of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, called
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning with Bundles
. They showed that
NDP
is at least as hard as
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning with Bundles
, and then proved hardness of this new problem. In the
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning with Bundles
problem, the input is the same as in
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, but now graph
G
must be bipartite, and, for every vertex
v
, we are given a partition
{\mathcal{B}}(v)
of the set of edges incident to
v
into subsets that are called
bundles
. We require that, in a solution
(H_{1},\ldots,H_{r})
to the problem, for every vertex
v\in V(G)
, and every bundle
\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}(v)
, at most one edge of
\beta
contributes to the solution; in other words, at most one edge of
\beta
may lie in
\bigcup_{i}E(H_{i})
. This is a somewhat artificial problem, but this definition allows one to bypass some of the barriers that arise when trying to prove the hardness of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
from existing hardness results for
CSP
’s.

It was noted in
[
CKN21
]
that the
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
problem resembles the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem for two reasons. First, in
Densest
k
-Subgraph
, the goal is to compute a dense subgraph of a given graph, with a prescribed number of vertices. One can think of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
as the problem of computing many vertex-disjoint dense subgraphs of a given graph. Second, natural hardness of approximation proofs for both problems seem to run into the same barriers.
It is therefore natural to ask: (i) Can we prove that the
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
problem itself is hard to approximate? In particular, can the techniques of
[
CKN21
]
be exploited in order to obtain such a proof? and (ii) Can we formalize this intuitive connection between
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
and
Densest
k
-Subgraph
? In this paper we make progress on both these questions. Our conditional hardness result for
Densest
k
-Subgraph
indeed builds on the ideas from
[
CKN21
]
for proving hardness of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning with Bundles
. We also provide “almost” approximation-preserving reductions between
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
to
Densest
k
-Subgraph
: we show that, if there is an efficient factor
\alpha(n)
-approximation algorithm for
Densest
k
-Subgraph
, then there is a randomized efficient factor
O(\alpha(n^{2})\cdot\textnormal{poly}\log n)
-approximation algorithm to
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
. We also provide a reduction in the opposite direction: we prove that, if there is an efficient
\alpha(n)
-approximation algorithm for
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, then there is a randomized algorithm for
Densest
k
-Subgraph
, that achieves approximation factor
O\left((\alpha(n^{O(\log n)}))^{3}\cdot\log^{2}n\right)
, in time
n^{O(\log n)}
. Therefore, we prove that
Densest
k
-Subgraph
and
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
are roughly equivalent from the approximation perspective (at least for large approximation factors and quasi-polynomial running times).
Combined with our conditional hardness of approximation for
Densest
k
-Subgraph
, our results show that, assuming the
Low-Degree CSP Conjecture
, for some constant
0<\varepsilon\leq 1/2
, there is no efficient
2^{(\log n)^{\varepsilon}}
-approximation algorithm for
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, unless
\mbox{\sf NP}\subseteq\mbox{\sf BPTIME}(n^{O(\log n)})
.

Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph.

The third problem that we study is a variation of the classical
Minimum Crossing Number
problem. In the
Minimum Crossing Number
problem, given an input
n
-vertex graph
G
, the goal is to compute a drawing of
G
in the plane while minimizing the number of crossings in the drawing. We define the notions of graph drawing and crossings formally in the Preliminaries, but these notions are quite intuitive and the specifics of the definition are not important in this high-level overview.

The
Minimum Crossing Number
problem was initially introduced by Turán
[
Tur77
]
in 1944, and has been extensively studied since then (see, e.g.,
[
Chu11
,
CMS11
,
CH11
,
CS13
,
KS17
,
KS19
,
CMT20
]
, and also
[
RS09
,
PT00
,
Mat02
,
Sch12
]
for excellent surveys).
But despite all this work, most aspects of the problem are still poorly understood. A long line of work
[
LR99
,
EGS02
,
CMS11
,
Chu11
,
KS17
,
KS19
,
Chu15
,
CT22
]
has recently led to the first sub-polynomial approximation algorithm for the problem in
low degree graphs
. Specifically,
[
CT22
]
obtain a factor
O\left(2^{O((\log n)^{7/8}\log\log n)}\cdot\Delta^{O(1)}\right)
-approximation algorithm for
Minimum Crossing Number
, where
\Delta
is the maximum vertex degree. To the best of our knowledge, no non-trivial approximation algorithms are known for the problem when vertex degrees in the input graph
G
can be arbitrary. However, on the negative side, only APX-hardness is known for the problem
[
Cab13
,
AMS07
]
. As the current understanding of the
Minimum Crossing Number
problem from the approximation perspective is extremely poor, it is natural to study hardness of approximation of its variants.

Let us consider two extreme variations of the
Minimum Crossing Number
problem. The first variant is the
Minimum Crossing Number
problem itself, where we need to draw an input graph
G
in the plane with fewest crossings. The second variant is where we need to compute a subgraph
G^{\prime}
of the input graph
G
that is planar, while maximizing
|E(G^{\prime})|
. The latter problem has a simple constant-factor approximation algorithm, obtained by letting
G^{\prime}
be any spanning forest of
G
(this is since a planar
n
-vertex graph may only have
O(n)
edges).

In this paper we study a variation of the
Minimum Crossing Number
problem, that we call
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
, which can be viewed as an intermediate problem between these two extremes. In the
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
problem, given an
n
-vertex graph
G
and an integer
L>0
, the goal is to compute a subgraph
H\subseteq G
, such that
H
has a plane drawing with at most
L
crossings, while maximizing
|E(H)|
. This problem is only interesting when the bound
L
on the number of crossings is
\Omega(n)
. This is since, from the Crossing Number Inequality
[
ACNS82
,
Lei83
]
, if
|E(G)|\geq 4|V(G)|
, then the crossing number of
G
is at least
\Omega(|E(G)|^{3}/|V(G)|^{2})
. Therefore, for
L=O(n)
, a spanning tree provides a constant-factor approximation to the problem.
We emphasize that the focus here is on dense graphs, whose crossing number may be as large as
\Omega(n^{4})
.

The
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
problem was implicitly used in
[
CKN21
]
for proving hardness of approximation of
NDP
, as an intermediate problem, in the reduction from
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning with Bundles
to
NDP
. Their work suggests that there may be a connection between
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
and
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
, even though the two problems appear quite different. In this paper we prove that the two problems are roughly equivalent from the approximation perspective: if there is an efficient factor
\alpha(n)
-approximation algorithm for
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, then there is an efficient
O(\alpha(n)\cdot\textnormal{poly}\log n)
-approximation algorithm for
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
. On the other hand, an efficient
\alpha(n)
-approximation algorithm for
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
implies an efficient
O((\alpha(n))^{2}\cdot\textnormal{poly}\log n)
-approximation algorithm for
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
. Combined with our conditional hardness of approximation for
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, we get that, assuming the
Low-Degree CSP Conjecture
, for some constant
0<\varepsilon\leq 1/2
there is no efficient
2^{(\log n)^{\varepsilon}}
-approximation algorithm for
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
, unless
\mbox{\sf NP}\subseteq\mbox{\sf BPTIME}(n^{O(\log n)})
.

Dense
k
-Coloring.

The fourth and last problem that we consider is
Dense
k
-Coloring
. In this problem, the input is an
n
-vertex graph
G
and an integer
k
, such that
n
is an integral multiple of
k
. The goal is to partition
V(G)
into
n/k
disjoint subsets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n/k}
, of cardinality
k
each, so as to maximize
\sum_{i=1}^{n/k}|E(S_{i})|
. This problem can be viewed as an intermediate problem between
Densest
k
-Subgraph
and
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
. The connection to
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
seems clear: in both problems, the goal is to compute a large collection of subgraphs of the input graph
G
, that contain many edges of
G
. While in
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
we place a limit on the number of edges in each subgraph, in
Dense
k
-Coloring
we require that each subgraph contains exactly
k
vertices. The connection to the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem is also clear: while in
Densest
k
-Subgraph
the goal is to compute a single dense subgraph of
G
containing
k
vertices, in
Dense
k
-Coloring
we need to partition
G
into many dense subgraphs, containing
k
vertices each. We show reductions between the
Dense
k
-Coloring
and the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem in both directions, that provide very similar guarantees to the reductions between
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
and
Densest
k
-Subgraph
. In particular, our results show that, assuming the
Low-Degree CSP Conjecture
, for some constant
0<\varepsilon\leq 1/2
, there is no efficient
2^{(\log n)^{\varepsilon}}
-approximation algorithm for
Dense
k
-Coloring
, unless
\mbox{\sf NP}\subseteq\mbox{\sf BPTIME}(n^{O(\log n)})
.

The Low-Degree CSP Conjecture.

We now turn to describe our new conjecture on the hardness of 2-
CSP
’s.
We consider the following bipartite version of the Constraint Satisfaction Problem with 2 variables per constraint (2-
CSP
).
The input consists of two sets
X
and
Y
of variables, together with an integer
A\geq 1
. Every variable in
X\cup Y
takes values in
[A]=\{1,\ldots,A\}
. We are also given a collection
\mathcal{C}
of constraints, where each constraint
C(x,y)\in\mathcal{C}
is defined over a pair of variables
x\in X
and
y\in Y
. For each such constraint, we are given a truth table that, for every pair of assignments
a
to
x
and
a^{\prime}
to
y
, specifies whether
(a,a^{\prime})

satisfy
the constraint. The
value
of the
CSP
is the largest fraction of constraints that can be simultaneously satisfied by an assignment to the variables.
For given values
0<s<c\leq 1
, the
(c,s)
-
Gap-CSP
problem is the problem of distinguishing
CSP
’s of value at least
c
from those of value at most
s
.

We can associate, to each constraint
C=C(x,y)\in\mathcal{C}
, a bipartite graph
G_{C}=(L,R,E)
, where
L=R=[A]
, and there is an edge
(a,a^{\prime})
in
E
iff the assignments
a
to
x
and
a^{\prime}
to
y
satisfy
C
.
Notice that instance
{\mathcal{I}}
of the
Bipartite 2-CSP
problem is completely defined by
X,Y,A,\mathcal{C}
, and the graphs in
\{G_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{C}}
, so we will denote
{\mathcal{I}}=(X,Y,A,\mathcal{C},\{G_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{C}})
.
We let the
size
of instance
{\mathcal{I}}
be
\operatorname{size}({\mathcal{I}})=|\mathcal{C}|\cdot A^{2}+|X|+|Y|
. We sometimes refer to
A
as the
size of the alphabet for instance
{\mathcal{I}}
.
We say that instance
{\mathcal{I}}
of 2-
CSP
is
d
-to-
d^{\prime}
iff for every constraint
C
, every vertex of
G_{C}
that lies in
L
has degree at most
d
, and every vertex that lies in
R
has degree at most
d^{\prime}
. (We note that this is somewhat different from the standard definition, that requires that all vertices in
L
have degree exactly
d
and all vertices of
R
have degree exactly
d^{\prime}
. In the standard definition, the alphabet sizes for variables in
X
and
Y
may be different, that is, variables in
X
take values in
[A]
and variables of
Y
take values in
[A^{\prime}]
for some integers
A,A^{\prime}
. However, this difference is insignificant to our discussion, and it is more convenient for us to use this slight variation of the standard definition).

The famous Unique-Games Conjecture of Khot
[
Kho02
]
applies to
1
-to-
1
CSP’s. The conjecture states that, for any
0<\varepsilon<1
, there is a large enough value
A
, such that the
(1-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
-
Gap-CSP
problem is
NP
-hard for
1
-to-
1
instances with alphabet size
A
.
The conjecture currently remains open, though interesting progress has been made on the algorithmic side: the results of
[
ABS15
]
provide an algorithm for the problem with running time
2^{n^{O(1/\varepsilon^{1/3})}}
.

A conjecture that is closely related to the Unique-Games Conjecture is the
d
-to-
1
Conjecture of Khot
[
Kho02
]
. The conjecture states that, for every
0<\varepsilon<1
, and
d>0
, there is a large enough value
A
, such that the
(1,\varepsilon)
-
Gap-CSP
problem in
d
-to-
1
instances with alphabet size
A
is
NP
-hard.

Håstad
[
Hås01
]
proved the following nearly optimal hardness of approximation results for
CSP
’s: he showed that for every
0<\varepsilon<1
, there are values
d
and
A
, such that the problem of
(1,\varepsilon)
-
Gap-CSP
in
d
-to-
1
instances with alphabet size
A
is
NP
-hard. The value
d
, however, depends exponentially on
\textnormal{poly}(1/\varepsilon)
in this result.
In contrast, in the
d
-to-
1
Conjecture, both
d
and
\varepsilon
are fixed, and
d
may not have such a strong dependence on
1/\varepsilon
.

On the algorithmic side, the results of
[
ABS15
,
Ste
]
provide an algorithm for
(c,s)
-
Gap-CSP
on
d
-to-
1
instances. The running time of the algorithm is
2^{n^{O(1/(\log(1/s))^{1/2})}}
, where the
O(\cdot)
notation hides factors that are polynomial in
d
and
A
.

A recent breakthrough in this area is the proof of the
2
-to-
2
conjecture (now theorem), that builds on a long sequence of work
[
BGH+15
,
KS13
,
KMMS18
,
BKS19
,
KMS17
,
DKK+18a
,
DKK+18b
,
KMS18
]
. The theorem proves that for every
0<\varepsilon<1
, there is a large enough value
A
, such that the
(1-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
-
Gap-CSP
problem is
NP
-hard on
2
-to-
2
instances with alphabet size
A
.

In this paper, we propose the following conjecture, that we refer to as
Low-Degree CSP Conjecture

, regarding the hardness of
Gap-CSP
in
d
-to-
d
instances.

Conjecture 1

(
Low-Degree CSP Conjecture
)
.

There is a constant
0<\varepsilon\leq 1/2
, such that it is
NP
-hard to distinguish between
d(n)
-to-
d(n)
instances of 2-
CSP
of size
n
, that have value at least
1/2
, and those of value at most
s(n)
, where

d(n)=2^{(\log n)^{\varepsilon}}
and
s(n)=1/2^{64(\log n)^{1/2+\varepsilon}}
.

We now compare this conjecture to existing conjectures and results in this area that we are aware of.
First, in contrast to the
d
-to-
1
conjecture, we allow the parameter
d
and the soundness parameter
s
to be functions of
n
– the size of the input instance. Note that the size of the input instance depends on the alphabet size
A
, so, unlike in the setting of the
d
-to-
1
conjecture,
A
may no longer be arbitrarily large compared to
d
and
s
.

The hardness of approximation result of Håstad
[
Hås01
]

for
d
-to-
d

CSP
’s only holds when
d
depends exponentially on
\textnormal{poly}(1/s)
, (in particular it may not extend to the setting where
s(n)=1/2^{64(\log n)^{1/2+\varepsilon}}
, since the size
n
of the instance depends polynomially on
d(n)
).

We can also combine standard constant hardness of approximation results for
CSP
’s (such as, for example,
3
-SAT) with the Parallel Repetition theorem, to obtain
NP
-hardness of
(1,s(n))
-
Gap-CSP
on
d(n)
-to-
d(n)
instances. Using this approach, if we start from an instance of
CSP
of size
N
and a constant hardness gap (with perfect completeness), after
\ell
rounds of parallel repetition, we obtain hardness of
(1,s)
-
Gap-CSP
on
d
-to-
d
instances with
s=2^{O(\ell)}
,
d=2^{O(\ell)}
, and the resulting instance size
n=N^{O(\ell)}
.
By setting the number of repetition to be
\ell=\Theta\left((\log N)^{(1/2+\varepsilon)/(1/2-\varepsilon)}\right)
, we can ensure the desired bound
s(n)=1/2^{64(\log n)^{1/2+\varepsilon}}
. However, in this setting, we also get that
d(n)=2^{\Omega((\log n)^{1/2+\varepsilon})}
, which is significantly higher than the desired value
d(n)=2^{(\log n)^{\varepsilon}}
.

Lastly, one could attempt to combine the recent proof of the
2
-to-
2
conjecture with Parallel Repetition in order to reap the benefits of both approaches, but the resulting parameters also fall short of the ones stated in the conjecture.

From the above discussion, one can view the
Low-Degree CSP Conjecture
as occupying a middle ground between the
d
-to-
1
conjecture, and the results one can obtain via standard techniques of amplifying a constant hardness of a
CSP
, such as 3SAT, via Parallel Repetition.

We now proceed to discuss our results and techniques in more detail.

1.1
A More Detailed Overview of our Results and Techniques

In addition to posing the
Low-Degree CSP Conjecture
that we already described above, we prove conditional hardness of approximation of the four problems that we consider. We also prove that all four problems are roughly equivalent approximation-wise. We now discuss the conditional hardness of approximation for
Densest
k
-Subgraph
and the connections between the four problems that we establish.

Conditional Hardness of
Densest
k
-Subgraph
.

Our first result is a conditional hardness of
Densest
k
-Subgraph
. Specifically, we prove that, assuming
1
holds and
\mbox{\sf P}\neq\mbox{\sf NP}
, for some
0<\varepsilon\leq 1/2
, there is no efficient approximation algorithm for
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem that achieves approximation factor
2^{(\log N)^{\varepsilon}}
, where
N
is the number of vertices in the input graph.

We now provide a brief overview of our techniques. The proof of the above result employs a Cook-type reduction, and follows some of the ideas that were introduced in
[
CKN21
]
. We assume for contradiction that there is a factor-
\alpha
algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
for the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem, where
\alpha=2^{(\log N)^{\varepsilon}}
. Given an input instance
{\mathcal{I}}
of the
2
-
CSP
problem of size
n
, that is a
d(n)
-to-
d(n)
instance, we construct a constraint graph
H
representing
{\mathcal{I}}
. We gradually decompose graph
H
into a collection
{\mathcal{H}}
of disjoint subgraphs, such that, for each subgraph
H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}
, we can either certify that the value of the corresponding instance of
2
-
CSP
is at most
1/4
, or it is at least
\beta
, for some carefully chosen parameter
\beta
. In order to compute the decomposition, we start with
{\mathcal{H}}=\{H\}
. If, for a graph
H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}
, we certified that the corresponding instance of
2
-
CSP
has value at most
1/4
, or at least
\beta
, then we say that graph
H^{\prime}
is
inactive
. Otherwise, we say that it is
active
. As long as
{\mathcal{H}}
contains at least one active graph, we perform iterations. In each iteration, we select an arbitrary graph
H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}
to process. In order to process
H^{\prime}
, we consider an
assignment graph

G^{\prime}
associated with
H^{\prime}
, that contains a vertex for every variable-assignment pair
(x,a)
, where
x
is a variable whose corresponding vertex belongs to
H^{\prime}
. We view
G^{\prime}
as an instance of the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem, for an appropriately chosen parameter
k
, and apply the approximation algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
for
Densest
k
-Subgraph
to it. Let
S
be the set of vertices of
G^{\prime}
that Algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
computes as a solution to this instance. We exploit the set
S
of vertices in order to either (i) compute a large subset
E^{\prime}\subseteq E(H^{\prime})
of edges, such that, if we denote by
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
the set of constraints corresponding to
E^{\prime}
, then at most
1/4
of the constraints of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
can be simultaneously satisfied; or (ii) compute a large subset
E^{\prime}\subseteq E(H^{\prime})
of edges as above, and certify that at least a
\beta
-fraction of such constraints can be satisfied; or (iii) compute a subgraph
H^{\prime\prime}\subseteq H^{\prime}
, such that
|V(H^{\prime\prime})|\ll|V(H^{\prime})|
, and the number of edges contained in graphs
H^{\prime\prime}
and
H^{\prime}\setminus V(H^{\prime\prime})
is sufficiently large compared to
E(H^{\prime})
. In the former two cases, we replace
H^{\prime}
with graph
H^{\prime}[E^{\prime}]
in
{\mathcal{H}}
, and graph
H^{\prime}[E^{\prime}]
becomes inactive. In the latter case, we replace
H^{\prime}
with two graphs:
H^{\prime\prime}
and
H^{\prime}\setminus V(H^{\prime\prime})
, that both remain active. The algorithm terminates once every graph in
{\mathcal{H}}
is inactive. The crux of the analysis of the algorithm is to show that, when the algorithm terminates, the total number of edges lying in the subgraphs
H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}
is high, compared to
|E(H)|
. This algorithm for decomposing graph
H
into subgraphs and its analysis employ some of the techniques and ideas introduced in
[
CKN21
]
, and is very similar in spirit to the hardness of approximation proof of the
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning with Bundles
problem, though details are different. We employ this decomposition algorithm multiple times, in order to obtain a partition
(E_{0},E_{1},\ldots,E_{z})
of the set
E(H)
of edges into a small number of subsets, such that, among the constraints corresponding to the edges of
E_{0}
, at most a
1/4
-fraction can be satisfied by any assignment to
X\cup Y
, and, for all
1\leq i\leq z
, a large fraction of constraints corresponding to edges of
E_{i}
can be satisfied by some assignment. Depending on the cardinality of the set
E_{0}
of edges we then determine whether
{\mathcal{I}}
is a
Yes-Instance
or a
No-Instance
.

Reductions from
Dense
k
-Coloring
and
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
to
Densest
k
-Subgraph
.

We show that, if there is an efficient factor
\alpha(n)
-approximation algorithm for the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem, then there is an efficient
O(\alpha(n^{2})\cdot\textnormal{poly}\log n)
-approximation algorithm for
Dense
k
-Coloring
, and an efficient
O(\alpha(n^{2})\cdot\textnormal{poly}\log n)
-approximation algorithm for
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
. The two reductions are very similar, so we focus on describing the first one. We believe that the reduction is of independent interest, and uses unusual techniques.

We assume that there is an
\alpha(n)
-approximation algorithm for the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem. In order to obtain an approximation algorithm for
Dense
k
-Coloring
, we start by formulating a natural LP-relaxation for the problem. Unfortunately, this LP-relaxation has a large number of variables: roughly
n^{\Theta(k)}
, where
n
is the number of vertices in the
input graph and
k
is the parameter of the
Dense
k
-Coloring
problem instance. We then show an efficient algorithm, that, given a solution to the LP-relaxation, whose support size is bounded by
\textnormal{poly}(n)
, computes an approximate integral solution to the
Dense
k
-Coloring
problem.

The main challenge is that, since the LP relaxation has
n^{\Theta(k)}
variables, it is unclear how to solve it efficiently. We consider the dual linear program, that has
\textnormal{poly}(n)
variables and
n^{\Theta(k)}
constraints. Using the
\alpha(n)
-approximation algorithm for
Densest
k
-Subgraph
as a subroutine, we design an approximate separation oracle for the dual LP, that allows us to solve the original LP-relaxation for
Dense
k
-Coloring
, obtaining a solution whose support size is bounded by
\textnormal{poly}(n)
. By applying the LP-rounding approximation algorithm to this solution, we obtain the desired approximate solution to the input instance of
Dense
k
-Coloring
.

Reductions from
Densest
k
-Subgraph
to
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
and
Dense
k
-Coloring
.

We prove that, if there is an efficient
\alpha(n)
-approximation algorithm for
Dense
k
-Coloring
, then there is a randomized algorithm for the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem, whose running time is
n^{O(\log n)}
, that with high probability obtains an
O(\alpha(n^{O(\log n)})\cdot\log n)
-approximate solution to the input instance of the problem. We also show a similar reduction from
Densest
k
-Subgraph
to
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, but now the resulting approximation factor for
Densest
k
-Subgraph
becomes
O((\alpha(n^{O(\log N)}))^{3}\cdot\log^{2}n)
. By combining these reductions with our conditional hardness result for
Densest
k
-Subgraph
, we get that, assuming the
Low-Degree CSP Conjecture
, for some constant
0<\varepsilon\leq 1/2
, there is no efficient
2^{(\log n)^{\varepsilon}}
-approximation algorithm for
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
and for
Dense
k
-Coloring
, unless
\mbox{\sf NP}\subseteq\mbox{\sf BPTIME}(n^{O(\log n)})
.

The two reductions are very similar; we focus on the reduction to
Dense
k
-Coloring
in this overview. Our construction is inspired by the results of
[
KLS00
]
, and we borrow some of our ideas from them.
Assume that there is an efficient
\alpha(n)
-approximation algorithm for
Dense
k
-Coloring
. Let
G
be an instance of the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem. The main difficulty in the reduction is that it is possible that
G
only contains one very dense subgraph induced by
k
vertices, while the
Dense
k
-Coloring
problem requires that the input graph
G
can essentially be partitioned into many such dense subgraphs. To overcome this difficulty, we construct a random “inflated” bipartite graph
H
, that contains
n^{O(\log n)}
vertices, where
n=|V(G)|
. Every vertex of
G
is mapped to some vertex of
H
at random, while every edge of
G
is mapped to a large number of edges of
H
. This allows us to ensure that, if
G
contains a subgraph
G^{\prime}
induced by a set of
k
vertices, where
|E(G^{\prime})|=R
, then graph
H
can essentially be partitioned into a large number of subgraphs that contain
k
vertices each, and many of them contain close to
R
edges. Therefore, we can apply our
\alpha(n)
-approximation algorithm for
Dense
k
-Coloring
to the new graph
H
. The main challenge in the reduction is that, while this approximation algorithm is guaranteed to return a large number of disjoint dense subgraphs of
H
, since every edge of
G
contributes many copies to
H
, it is not clear that one can extract a single dense subgraph of
G
from dense subgraphs of
H
. The main difficulty in the reduction is to ensure that, on the one hand, a single
k
-vertex dense subgraph in
G
can be translated into
|V(H)|/k
dense subgraphs of
H
; and, on the other hand, a dense
k
-vertex subgraph of
H
can be translated into a dense subgraph of
G
on
k
vertices. We build on and expand the ideas from
[
KLS00
]
in order to ensure these properties.

Reductions between
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
and
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
.

Lastly, we provide reductions between
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
and
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
in both directions. First, we show that, if there is an efficient factor
\alpha(n)
-approximation algorithm for
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, then there is an efficient
O(\alpha(n)\cdot\textnormal{poly}\log n)
-approximation algorithm for
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
. On the other hand, an efficient
\alpha(n)
-approximation algorithm for
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
implies an efficient
O((\alpha(n))^{2}\cdot\textnormal{poly}\log n)
-approximation algorithm for
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
. Combined with our conditional hardness of approximation for
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, we get that, assuming the
Low-Degree CSP Conjecture
, for some constant
0<\varepsilon\leq 1/2
, there is no efficient
2^{(\log n)^{\varepsilon}}
-approximation algorithm for
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, unless
\mbox{\sf NP}\subseteq\mbox{\sf BPTIME}(n^{O(\log n)})
.

Both these reductions exploit the following connection between crossing number and graph partitioning: if a graph
G
has a drawing with at most
L
crossings, then there is a balanced cut in
G
, containing at most
O\left(\sqrt{L+\Delta\cdot|E(G)|}\right)
edges, where
\Delta
is maximum vertex degree in
G
. This result can be viewed as an extension of the classical Planar Separator Theorem of
[
LT79
]
.
Another useful fact exploited in both reductions is that any graph
G
with
m
edges has a plane drawing with at most
m^{2}
crossings. In particular, if
{\mathcal{H}}=\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{r}\}
is a solution to an instance of the
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
problem on graph
G
, then there is a drawing of graph
H=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}H_{i}
, in which the number of crossings is bounded by
r\cdot h^{2}
. These two facts establish a close relationship between the
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
and
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
problems, that are exploited in both our reductions.

We have now obtained a chain of reductions that show that all four problems,
Densest
k
-Subgraph
,
Dense
k
-Coloring
,
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, and
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
are almost equivalent from approximation viewpoint (if one considers sufficiently large approximation factors, and allows randomized quasi-polynomial time algorithms). We also obtain conditional hardness of approximation results for all four problems based on the
Low-Degree CSP Conjecture
.

Preliminaries

By default, all logarithms are to the base of
2
. For a positive integer
N
, we denote by
[N]=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
. All graphs are finite, simple and undirected. We say that an event holds with high probability if the probability of the event is
1-1/n^{c}
for a large enough constant
c
, where
n
is the number of vertices in the input graph.

2.1
General Notation

Let
G
be a graph and let
S
be a subset of its vertices. We denote by
G[S]
the subgraph of
G
induced by
S
.
For two disjoint subsets
A,B
of vertices of
G
, we denote by
E_{G}(A,B)
the set of all edges with one endpoint in
A
and the other endpoint in
B
, and we denote by
E_{G}(A)
the set of all edges with both endpoints in
A
.
Given a graph
G
and a vertex
v\in V(G)
, we denote by
\deg_{G}(v)
the degree of
v
in
G
. For a subset
S
of vertices of
G
, its
volume
is
\operatorname{vol}_{G}(S)=\sum_{v\in S}\deg_{G}(v)
.
We sometimes omit the subscript
G
if it is clear from the context.

Given a graph
G
, a drawing
\varphi
of
G
is an embedding of
G
into the plane, that maps
every vertex
v
of
G
to a point (called the
image of
v
and denoted by
\varphi(v)
), and every edge
e
of
G
to a simple curve (called the
image of
e
and denoted by
\varphi(e)
), that connects the images of its endpoints. If
e
is an edge of
G
and
v
is a vertex of
G
, then the image of
e
may only contain the image of
v
if
v
is an endpoint of
e
. Furthermore, if some point
p
belongs to the images of three or more edges of
G
, then
p
must be the image of a common endpoint of all edges
e
with
p\in\varphi(e)
. We say that two edges
e,e^{\prime}
of
G

cross
at a point
p
, if
p\in\varphi(e)\cap\varphi(e^{\prime})
, and
p
is not the image of a shared endpoint of these edges.
Given a graph
G
and a drawing
\varphi
of
G
in the plane, we use
\mathsf{cr}(\varphi)
to denote the number of crossings in
\varphi
, and the crossing number of
G
, denoted by
\mathsf{CrN}(G)
, is the minimum number of crossings in any drawing of
G
.

2.2
Problem Definitions and Additional Notation

In this paper we consider the following four problems:
Densest
k
-Subgraph
,
Dense
k
-Coloring
,
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
and
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
. We now define the problems, along with some additional notation.

Densest
k
-Subgraph.

In the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem, the input is a graph
G
and an integer
k>0
. The goal is to compute a subset
S\subseteq V(G)
of
k
vertices, maximizing
|E_{G}(S)|
. We denote an instance of the problem by
\mathrm{DkS}(G,k)
, and we denote the value of the optimal solution to instance
\mathrm{DkS}(G,k)
by
\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)
.

We also consider a bipartite version of the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem, called

Bipartite Densest
(k_{1},k_{2})
-Subgraph
.
This problem was first studied in
[
AAM+11
]
.
The input to the problem is a bipartite graph
G=(A,B,E)
and positive integers
k_{1},k_{2}
. The goal is to compute a subset
S\subseteq V(G)
of vertices with
|S\cap A|=k_{1}
and
|S\cap B|=k_{2}
, such that
|E_{G}(S)|
is maximized.
An instance of this problem is denoted by
\mathrm{BDkS}(G,k_{1},k_{2})
, and the value of the optimal solution to instance
\mathrm{BDkS}(G,k_{1},k_{2})
is denoted by
\mathrm{OPT_{BDkS}}(G,k_{1},k_{2})
.
The following lemma shows that the
Bipartite Densest
(k_{1},k_{2})
-Subgraph
problem and the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem are roughly equivalent from the approximation viewpoint. Similar results were also shown in prior work. For completeness, we provide the proof in
Appendix

A
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\alpha:\mathbb{Z^{+}}\to\mathbb{Z^{+}}
be an increasing function such that
\alpha(n)=o(n)
. Then the following hold:

•

If there exists an
\alpha(n)
-approximation algorithm for the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem with running time at most
T(n)
, where
n
is the number of vertices in the input graph, then there exists an
O(\alpha(N^{2}))
-approximation algorithm for the
Bipartite Densest
(k_{1},k_{2})
-Subgraph
problem, with running time
O(T(N^{2})\cdot\textnormal{poly}(N))
, where
N
is the number of vertices in the input graph. Moreover, if the algorithm for
Densest
k
-Subgraph
is deterministic, then so is the algorithm for
Bipartite Densest
(k_{1},k_{2})
-Subgraph
.

•

Similarly, if there exists an efficient
\alpha(N)
-approximation algorithm for the
Bipartite Densest
(k_{1},k_{2})
-Subgraph
problem, where
N
is the number of vertices in the input graph, then there exists an efficient
O(\alpha(2n))
-approximation algorithm for the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem, where
n
is the number of vertices in the input graph. Moreover, if the algorithm for
Bipartite Densest
(k_{1},k_{2})
-Subgraph
is deterministic, then so is the algorithm for
Densest
k
-Subgraph
.

Dense
k
-Coloring.

The input to the
Dense
k
-Coloring
problem consists of an
n
-vertex graph
G
and an integer
k>0
, such that
n
is an integral multiple of
k
. The goal is to compute a partition of
V(G)
into
n/k
subsets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n/k}
of cardinality
k
each, while maximizing
\sum_{i=1}^{n/k}|E_{G}(S_{i})|
.
An instance of the
Dense
k
-Coloring
problem is denoted by
\mathrm{DkC}(G,k)
, and the value of the optimal solution to instance
\mathrm{DkC}(G,k)
is denoted by
\mathrm{OPT_{DkC}}(G,k)
.

(r,h)
-Graph Partitioning.

The input to the
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
problem consists of a graph
G
, and integers
r,h>0
. The goal is to compute
r
vertex-disjoint subgraphs
H_{1},\ldots,H_{r}
of
G
, such that for all
1\leq i\leq r
,
|E(H_{i})|\leq h
, while maximizing
\sum_{i=1}^{r}|E(H_{i})|
.
An instance of the
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
problem is denoted by
\mathrm{GP}(G,r,h)
, and the value of the optimal solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r,h)
is denoted by
\mathrm{OPT_{GP}}(G,r,h)
.

Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph.

In the
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
problem, the input is a graph
G
and an integer
L>0
. The goal is to compute a subgraph
H\subseteq G
with
\mathsf{CrN}(H)\leq L
, while maximizing
|E(H)|
.
An instance of the
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
problem is denoted by
\mathrm{MBCS}(G,L)
, and the value of the optimal solution to instance
\mathrm{MBCS}(G,L)
is denoted by
\mathrm{OPT_{MBCS}}(G,L)
. We note that we can assume that
L\leq|V(G)|^{4}
, as otherwise the optimal solution is the whole graph

, since the crossing number of a simple graph

is at most
|E(G)|^{2}\leq|V(G)|^{4}
.

2.3
Chernoff Bound

We use the following standard version of Chernoff Bound (see. e.g.,
[
DP09
]
).

Lemma 2.2

(Chernoff Bound)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent randon variables taking values in
\{0,1\}
. Let
X=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}
, and let
\mu=\text{\bf E}\left[X\right]
. Then for any
t>2e\mu
,

\Pr\Big[X>t\Big]\leq 2^{-t}.

Additionally, for any
0\leq\delta\leq 1
,

\Pr\Big[X<(1-\delta)\cdot\mu\Big]\leq e^{-\frac{\delta^{2}\cdot\mu}{2}}.

2.4
Auxiliary Lemma

We use the following simple lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

There is an efficient algorithm, that, given a graph
G
, a subset
S
of its vertices, and a parameter
\frac{2}{|S|}<\beta<1
, computes a set
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
of vertices, such that
|S^{\prime}|\leq\beta\cdot|S|
, and
|E_{G}(S^{\prime})|\geq\Omega(\beta^{2}\cdot|E_{G}(S)|)
holds.

Proof:

Consider the graph
G^{\prime}=G[S]
and denote
|S|=k
.
We iteratively remove the lowest-degree vertex from
G^{\prime}
, until
G^{\prime}
contains
\left\lfloor\beta k\right\rfloor
vertices. Once the algorithm terminates, we output
S^{\prime}=V(G^{\prime})
. It now remains to show that
|E_{G^{\prime}}(S^{\prime})|\geq\Omega(\beta^{2}|E(G^{\prime})|)
.

Observe that, if
H
is an
n
-vertex graph, and
v
is a lowest-degree vertex of
H
, then the degree of
v
in
H
is at most
2|E(H)|/n
. Therefore, if vertex
v
is removed from
H
, then
|E(H)|
decreases by at most a factor
(1-2/n)
. Therefore,

0\leq i^{*}\leq q

|E_{i^{*}}|\geq\frac{|E(H)|}{2\log n}
. We let
\tilde{X}=S_{i^{*}}
, and we let
H_{1}
be the graph whose vertex set is
\tilde{X}\cup Y
, and edge set is
E_{i^{*}}
. We also define
d_{1}=2^{i^{*}}
. Clearly, for every vertex
x\in\tilde{X}
,
d_{1}\leq\deg_{H}(x)<2d_{1}
. This completes the first regularization step.

We now proceed to describe our second step, in which we consider the vertices of
y\in Y
one by one. We say that a vertex
y\in Y
is
bad
, if
\deg_{H_{1}}(y)<\frac{\deg_{H}(y)}{4\log n}
. Let
Y^{\prime\prime}\subseteq Y
be the set of all bad vertices, and let
Y^{\prime}=Y\setminus Y^{\prime\prime}
be the set of all remaining vertices of
Y
, that we refer to as
good vertices
.
We use the following observation.

Observation 3.14
.

\sum_{y\in Y^{\prime\prime}}\deg_{H_{1}}(y)\leq\frac{|E(H_{1})|}{2}
.

Proof:

Since, for every bad vertex
y
,
\deg_{H_{1}}(y)<\frac{\deg_{H}(y)}{4\log n}
, we get that:

\sum_{y\in Y^{\prime\prime}}\deg_{H_{1}}(y)<\sum_{y\in Y^{\prime\prime}}\frac{\deg_{H}(y)}{4\log n}\leq\frac{|E(H)|}{4\log n}.

Since, as observed above,
|E(H_{1})|\geq\frac{|E(H)|}{2\log n}
, the observation follows.

We let
H_{2}
be a graph that is obtained from
H_{1}
, by discarding the vertices of
Y^{\prime\prime}
from it. Therefore,
V(H_{2})=\tilde{X}\cup Y^{\prime}
. Additionally, from
3.14
,
|E(H_{2})|\geq\frac{|E(H_{1})|}{2}\geq\frac{|E(H)|}{4\log n}
.

Lastly, in our third step, we perform a geometric grouping of the vertices of
Y^{\prime}
by their degree in
H_{2}
.
Specifically, we let
r=\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil
, and we partition the vertices of
Y^{\prime}
into sets
S^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,S^{\prime}_{r}
, where for
0\leq j\leq r
,
S^{\prime}_{j}=\{y\in Y^{\prime}\mid 2^{j}\leq\deg_{H_{2}}(y)<2^{j+1}\}
.
As before, we also partition the set
E(H_{2})
of edges into subsets
E^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,E^{\prime}_{r}
, where for
0\leq j\leq r
set
E^{\prime}_{j}
contains all edges
e\in E(H_{2})
that are incident to the vertices of
S^{\prime}_{j}
. As before, there must be an index
0\leq j^{*}\leq r
with
|E^{\prime}_{j^{*}}|\geq\frac{|E(H_{2})|}{2\log n}\geq\frac{|E(H)|}{8\log^{2}n}
. We set
\tilde{Y}=S^{\prime}_{j^{*}}
,
d_{2}=2^{j^{*}}
, and we let
\tilde{H}
be the graph whose vertex set is
\tilde{X}\cup\tilde{Y}
, and edge set is
E^{\prime}_{j^{*}}
. We now verify that this graph has all required properties.

First, as observed already, for every vertex
x\in\tilde{X}
,
d_{1}\leq\deg_{H}(x)<2d_{1}
. Let
\tilde{E}=E(\tilde{H})
. As observed already,
|\tilde{E}|\geq\frac{|E(H)|}{8\log^{2}n}
. Moreover, since, for every vertex
x\in\tilde{X}
,
\deg_{H_{1}}(x)=\deg_{H}(x)\geq d_{1}
, we get that
|E(H_{1})|\geq d_{1}\cdot|\tilde{X}|
, and so
|\tilde{E}|\geq\frac{|E(H_{2})|}{2\log n}\geq\frac{|E(H_{1})|}{4\log n}\geq\frac{d_{1}}{4\log n}\cdot|\tilde{X}|
.

Consider now some vertex
y\in\tilde{Y}
. From the definition of graph
\tilde{H}
, it is immediate to verify that
\deg_{\tilde{H}}(y)=\deg_{H_{2}}(y)
. Therefore,
d_{2}\leq\deg_{\tilde{H}}(y)<2d_{2}
.
Clearly,
\deg_{H}(y)\geq\deg_{\tilde{H}}(y)\geq d_{2}
. Lastly, since vertex
y
is good, we get that:

\deg_{\tilde{H}}(y)=\deg_{H_{2}}(y)=\deg_{H_{1}}(y)\geq\frac{\deg_{H}(y)}{4\log n}.

Since
\deg_{\tilde{H}}(y)<2d_{2}
, we get that
\deg_{H}(y)\leq(4\log n)\deg_{\tilde{H}}(y)<8d_{2}\log n
. We conclude that

d_{2}\leq\deg_{H}(y)<8d_{2}\log n
.

3.4.2
Step 2: Assignment Graph and Reduction to
Bipartite Densest
(k_{1},k_{2})
-Subgraph

Recall that we have computed, in the first step, a subgraph
\tilde{H}=(\tilde{X},\tilde{Y},\tilde{E})
of the graph
H=H({\mathcal{I}})
. Since every edge of
H
is associated with a distinct constraint in
\mathcal{C}
, we can define a collection
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
of constraints corresponding to the edges of
\tilde{H}
:
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}=\{C\in\mathcal{C}\mid e_{C}\in\tilde{E}\}
.

Next, we define a bipartite graph
G=(U,V,\hat{E})
, called
assignment graph
, that is associated with graph
\tilde{H}
.
For every variable
z\in\tilde{X}\cup\tilde{Y}
, we define a set
R(z)=\{v(z,a)\mid 1\leq a\leq A\}
of vertices that represent the possible assignments to variable
z
.
We then set
U=\bigcup_{x\in\tilde{X}}R(x)
, and
V=\bigcup_{y\in\tilde{Y}}R(y)
. The set of vertices of
G
is defined to be
U\cup V
.

In order to define the edges,
consider any constraint
C=C(x,y)\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
. We define a set
E(C)
of at most
d(n)\cdot A
edges corresponding to
C
, as follows: we add an edge between vertex
v(x,a)
and vertex
v(y,a^{\prime})
to
E(C)
if assignments
a
to
x
and
a^{\prime}
to
y
satisfy the constraint
C
. Since instance
{\mathcal{I}}
is a
d(n)
-to-
d(n)
instance of Bipartite 2-CSP, every vertex of
R(x)\cup R(y)
is incident to at most
d(n)
edges of
E(C)
.
We then let
E(G)=\bigcup_{C\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}}E(C)
.

Let
k_{1}=|\tilde{X}|
and
k_{2}=|\tilde{Y}|
. We can then view graph
G
, together with parameters
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
as an instance of the
Bipartite Densest
(k_{1},k_{2})
-Subgraph
problem,
\mathrm{DkS}(G,k_{1},k_{2})
. Notice that
|V(G)|\leq|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|\cdot A^{2}\leq|\mathcal{C}|\cdot A^{2}\leq\operatorname{size}({\mathcal{I}})\leq n
. We apply Algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
to instance
\mathrm{DkS}(G,k_{1},k_{2})
of
Bipartite Densest
(k_{1},k_{2})
-Subgraph
, and we let
S
be the solution that the algorithm returns. Denote
G^{\prime}=G[S]
, so the value of the solution is
|E(G^{\prime})|
. Assume first that
|E(G^{\prime})|<\frac{|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|}{4\alpha(n)}
. We use the following observation to show that, in this case, the set
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
of constraints is bad.

Observation 3.15
.

If the set
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
of constraints is not bad, then the value of the optimal solution to instance
\mathrm{DkS}(G,k_{1},k_{2})
of
Bipartite Densest
(k_{1},k_{2})
-Subgraph
is at least
\frac{|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|}{4}
.

Proof:
Assume that the set
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
of constraints is not bad. Then there is an assignment
f
to variables of
\tilde{X}\cup\tilde{Y}
that satisfies more than
\frac{|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|}{4}
constraints of
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
. For each variable
z\in\tilde{X}\cup\tilde{Y}
, we denote the corresponding assignment by
f(z)
. Let
S^{\prime}
be the set of vertices of
G
that contains, for every variable
x\in\tilde{X}
, vertex
v(x,f(x))
, and for every variable
y\in\tilde{Y}
, vertex
v(y,f(y))
. Then
S^{\prime}
is a valid solution to instance
\mathrm{DkS}(G,k_{1},k_{2})
of
Bipartite Densest
(k_{1},k_{2})
-Subgraph
. Moreover, for every constraint
C\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
that is satisfied by the assignment
f
, an edge of
E(C)
must be contained in
G[S^{\prime}]
. Therefore, the value of solution
S^{\prime}
is at least
\frac{|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|}{4}
.

From
3.15
, if the set
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
of constraints is not bad, then algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
must have returned a solution whose value is at least
\frac{|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|}{4\alpha(n)}
. Therefore, if the value of the solution
S
that the algorithm returns is less than
\frac{|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|}{4\cdot\alpha(n)}
, then we terminate the algorithm, and return
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
as a bad set of constraints. Recall that, from
3.13
,
|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|=|E(\tilde{H})|\geq\frac{|E(H)|}{8\log^{2}n}=\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{8\log^{2}n}
.
From now on we assume that the value of the soution
S
is at least
\frac{|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|}{4\alpha(n)}
.
We will use the set
S
of vertices of
G
, in order to either correctly certify that graph
H
is
\beta^{3}
-good, or to compute a subgraph
H^{\prime}\subseteq H
with the required properties. It will be convenient for us to further regularize graph
G^{\prime}
, which we do in the next step.

3.4.3
Step 3: Further Regularization

It would be convenient for us to further regularize graph
G^{\prime}
, by computing a subgraph
G^{\prime\prime}\subseteq G^{\prime}
, with
|E(G^{\prime\prime})|
roughly comparable to
|E(G^{\prime})|
, that has the following additional properties. First, for all variables
x\in\tilde{X}
with
R(x)\cap V(G^{\prime\prime})\neq\emptyset
, the cardinalities of the sets
R(x)\cap V(G^{\prime\prime})
of vertices are roughly equal to each other (to within factor
2
). Similarly, for all variables
y\in\tilde{Y}
with
R(y)\cap V(G^{\prime\prime})\neq\emptyset
, the cardinalities of the sets
R(y)\cap V(G^{\prime\prime})
of vertices are roughly equal to each other. Lastly, for all constraints
C\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
with
E(C)\cap E(G^{\prime\prime})\neq\emptyset
,
the cardinalities of the sets
E(C)\cap E(G^{\prime\prime})
of edges are roughly equal to each other. In this step, we compute a subgraph
G^{\prime\prime}\subseteq G^{\prime}
with all these properties. The algorithm is summarized in the following claim.

Claim 3.16
.

There is an algorithm with running time
O(\textnormal{poly}(n))
, that computes a subgraph
G^{\prime\prime}\subseteq G^{\prime}
, subsets
X^{*}\subseteq\tilde{X}
,
Y^{*}\subseteq\tilde{Y}
of variables, a subset
\mathcal{C}^{*}\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
of constraints, and integers
q,q^{\prime},r\geq 1
, such that, if we denote, for every variable
z\in X\cup Y
,
R^{\prime}(z)=R(z)\cap V(G^{\prime\prime})
, and for every constraint
C\in\mathcal{C}
,
E^{\prime}(C)=E(C)\cap E(G^{\prime\prime})
, then the following hold:

•

for every variable
x\in X^{*}
,
2^{q}\leq|R^{\prime}(x)|<2^{q+1}
, and for
x\not\in X^{*}
,
R^{\prime}(x)=\emptyset
;

•

for every variable
y\in Y^{*}
,
2^{q^{\prime}}\leq|R^{\prime}(y)|<2^{q^{\prime}+1}
, and for
y\not\in Y^{*}
,
R^{\prime}(y)=\emptyset
;

•

for every constraint
C\in\mathcal{C}^{*}
,
2^{r}\leq|E^{\prime}(C)|<2^{r+1}
, and for
C\not\in\mathcal{C}^{*}
,
E^{\prime}(C)=\emptyset
; and

•

|\mathcal{C}^{*}|\geq\frac{|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|}{2^{r+6}\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{3}n}
.

Proof:

The proof follows a standard regularization process. Let
E^{0}=E(G^{\prime})
, so that
|E^{0}|\geq\frac{|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|}{4\cdot\alpha(n)}
.

Our first step regularizes the variables of
\tilde{X}
. We group the variables of
\tilde{X}
into sets
J_{0},J_{1},\ldots,J_{\left\lceil\log A\right\rceil}
. For all
0\leq i\leq\left\lceil\log A\right\rceil
, we let
J_{i}=\{x\in\tilde{X}\mid 2^{i}\leq|R(x)\cap V(G^{\prime})|<2^{i+1}\}
.
Note that, if
R(x)\cap V(G^{\prime})=\emptyset
, then variable
x
does not belong to any of the groups that we have defined. We also partition the edges of
E^{0}
into groups
E_{0},E_{1},\ldots,E_{\left\lceil\log A\right\rceil}
, where for all
0\leq i\leq\left\lceil\log A\right\rceil
, group
E_{i}
contains all edges
e\in E^{0}
that are incident to the vertices of
\bigcup_{x\in J_{i}}(R(x)\cap V(G^{\prime}))
. It is easy to verify that
(E_{0},\ldots,E_{\left\lceil\log A\right\rceil})
is indeed a partition of the set
E^{0}
of edges. Therefore, there is an index
0\leq q\leq\left\lceil\log A\right\rceil
with
|E_{q}|\geq\frac{|E^{0}|}{2\log A}\geq\frac{|E^{0}|}{2\log n}
. We then set
X^{*}=J_{q}
. For each such variable
x\in X^{*}
, we set
R^{\prime}(x)=R(x)\cap V(G^{\prime})
, and for each variable
x\in\tilde{X}\setminus X^{*}
, we set
R^{\prime}(x)=\emptyset
. We also let
E^{1}=E_{q}
. From the above discussion,
|E^{1}|\geq\frac{|E^{0}|}{2\log n}
, and, for every variable
x\in X^{*}
,
2^{q}\leq|R^{\prime}(x)|<2^{q+1}
. Note that all edges of
E^{1}
are incident to vertices of
\bigcup_{x\in X^{*}}R^{\prime}(x)
.

Our second step is to regularize the variables of
\tilde{Y}
, exactly as before.
We group the variables of
\tilde{Y}
into sets
J^{\prime}_{0},J^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,J^{\prime}_{\left\lceil\log A\right\rceil}
. For all
0\leq i^{\prime}\leq\left\lceil\log A\right\rceil
, we let
J^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}=\{y\in\tilde{Y}\mid 2^{i^{\prime}}\leq|R(x)\cap V(G^{\prime})|<2^{i^{\prime}+1}\}
.
As before, if
R(y)\cap V(G^{\prime})=\emptyset
, then variable
y
does not belong to any of the sets that we have defined. We also partition the edges of
E^{1}
into sets
E^{\prime}_{0},E^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,E^{\prime}_{\left\lceil\log A\right\rceil}
, where for all
0\leq i^{\prime}\leq\left\lceil\log A\right\rceil
, set
E_{i^{\prime}}
contains all edges
e\in E^{1}
that are incident to the vertices of
\bigcup_{y\in J_{i^{\prime}}}(R(y)\cap V(G^{\prime}))
. As before,
(E^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,E^{\prime}_{\left\lceil\log A\right\rceil})
is a partition of the set
E^{1}
of edges. Therefore, there is an index
0\leq q^{\prime}\leq\left\lceil\log A\right\rceil
with
|E^{\prime}_{q^{\prime}}|\geq\frac{|E^{1}|}{2\log A}\geq\frac{|E^{1}|}{2\log n}\geq\frac{|E^{0}|}{4\log^{2}n}
. We then set
Y^{*}=J^{\prime}_{q^{\prime}}
. For each variable
y\in Y^{*}
, we let
R^{\prime}(y)=R(y)\cap V(G^{\prime})
, and for each variable
y\in\tilde{Y}\setminus Y^{*}
, we set
R^{\prime}(y)=\emptyset
. We also let
E^{2}=E^{\prime}_{q^{\prime}}
. From the above discussion,
|E^{2}|\geq\frac{|E^{0}|}{4\log^{2}n}
, and, for every variable
y\in Y^{*}
,
2^{q^{\prime}}\leq|R^{\prime}(y)|<2^{y+1}
. Notice that every edge of
E^{2}
is incident to a vertex of
\bigcup_{x\in X^{*}}R^{\prime}(x)
and a vertex of
\bigcup_{y\in Y^{*}}R^{\prime}(y)
.

Our third and final step is to regularize the constraints. Recall that for each constraint
C=C(x,y)\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
, we defined a set
E(C)
of edges. Since
|R(x)|=A
and
|R(y)|=A
,
|E(C)|\leq A^{2}\leq n
must hold. We group all constraints
C\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
into
sets
\mathcal{C}_{0},\mathcal{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil}
, where for all
0\leq j\leq\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil
, set
\mathcal{C}_{j}
contains all constraints
C\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
with
2^{j}\leq|E(C)\cap E^{2}|<2^{j+1}
. Note that, if
E(C)\cap E^{2}=\emptyset
, then constraint
C
does not belong to any set. Next, we define a partition
E_{0}^{\prime\prime},E_{1}^{\prime\prime},\ldots,E_{\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil}^{\prime\prime}
of the set
E^{2}
of edges: for
0\leq j\leq\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil
, set
E^{\prime\prime}_{j}
contains all edges in
\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{j}}(E(C)\cap E^{2})
. It is easy to verify that
(E^{\prime\prime}_{0},\ldots,E_{\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil}^{\prime\prime})
is indeed a partition of
E^{2}
. Therefore, there must be an index
0<r\leq\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil
, with
|E^{\prime\prime}_{r}|\geq\frac{|E^{2}|}{2\log n}\geq\frac{|E^{0}|}{8\log^{3}n}
. We let
\mathcal{C}^{*}=\mathcal{C}_{r}
and
E^{3}=E^{\prime\prime}_{r}
.
Since every constraint
C\in\mathcal{C}^{*}
contributes at most
2^{r+1}
edges to
E^{3}
, we get that:

|\mathcal{C}^{*}|\geq\frac{|E^{3}|}{2^{r+1}}\geq\frac{|E^{0}|}{2^{r+4}\log^{3}n}\geq\frac{|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|}{2^{r+6}\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{3}n}.

For every constraint
C\in\mathcal{C}^{*}
, we let
E^{\prime}(C)=E(C)\cap E^{2}
, and for every constraint
C\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\mathcal{C}^{*}
, we let
E^{\prime}(C)=\emptyset
. We are now ready to define graph
G^{\prime\prime}
. Its vertex set is
\left(\bigcup_{x\in X^{*}}R^{\prime}(X)\right)\cup\left(\bigcup_{y\in Y^{*}}R^{\prime}(y)\right)
, and its edge set is
\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{C}^{*}}E^{\prime}(C)=E^{3}
.
It is immediate to verify, from the above discussion, that graph
G^{\prime}
, sets
X^{*},Y^{*}
of variables, and set
\mathcal{C}^{*}
of constraints have all required properties.

In the next observation, we establish some useful bounds on the cardinalities of the sets
X^{*},Y^{*}
of variables, and the set
\mathcal{C}^{*}
of constraints.

Observation 3.17
.

All of the following bounds hold:

•

|X^{*}|\leq\frac{|\tilde{X}|}{2^{q}}
;

•

|Y^{*}|\leq\frac{|\tilde{Y}|}{2^{q^{\prime}}}
;

•

|\mathcal{C}^{*}|\leq 2d_{1}|X^{*}|\leq\frac{2d_{1}|\tilde{X}|}{2^{q}}
; and

•

|\mathcal{C}^{*}|\leq 2d_{2}|Y^{*}|\leq\frac{2d_{2}|\tilde{Y}|}{2^{q^{\prime}}}
.

Proof:

From the definition of the instance
(G,k_{1},k_{2})
of
Bipartite Densest
(k_{1},k_{2})
-Subgraph
, graph
G^{\prime}
contains at most
k_{1}=|\tilde{X}|
vertices of
\bigcup_{x\in\tilde{X}}R(x)
, and at most
k_{2}=|\tilde{Y}|
vertices of
\bigcup_{y\in\tilde{Y}}R(y)
. Since, for every variable
x\in X^{*}
,
R^{\prime}(x)\subseteq V(G^{\prime})
and
|R^{\prime}(x)|\geq 2^{q}
, we get that
|X^{*}|\leq\frac{|\tilde{X}|}{2^{q}}
. Similarly,
|Y^{*}|\leq\frac{|\tilde{Y}|}{2^{q^{\prime}}}
.

Recall that, since
\tilde{H}
is a nice subgraph of
H
, for every vertex
x\in\tilde{H}
,
\deg_{\tilde{H}}(x)\leq\deg_{H}(x)\leq 2d_{1}
, and so
x
may participate in at most
2d_{1}
constraints of
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
. Since
\mathcal{C}^{*}\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
and
X^{*}\subseteq\tilde{X}
, every variable
x\in X^{*}
may participate in at most
2d_{1}
constraints of
\mathcal{C}^{*}
. Therefore,
|\mathcal{C}^{*}|\leq 2d_{1}|X^{*}|
.

Similarly, since
\tilde{H}
is a nice subgraph of
H
, for every vertex
y\in\tilde{Y}
,
\deg_{\tilde{H}}(y)\leq 2d_{2}
. Using the same arguments as before,
|\mathcal{C}^{*}|\leq 2d_{2}|Y^{*}|
.

Recall that, from
3.16
,
|\mathcal{C}^{*}|\geq\frac{|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|}{2^{r+5}\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{3}n}
. Since graph
\tilde{H}
is a nice subgraph of
H
, we get that, for every vertex
y\in\tilde{Y}
,
\deg_{\tilde{H}}(y)\geq d_{2}
. Therefore,
|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|\geq|\tilde{Y}|\cdot d_{2}
, and so:

|\mathcal{C}^{*}|\geq\frac{d_{2}\cdot|\tilde{Y}|}{2^{r+6}\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{3}n}.

(1)

Similarly, from the definition of a nice subgraph,
|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|\geq\frac{d_{1}}{4\log n}\cdot|\tilde{X}|
, and so:

|\mathcal{C}^{*}|\geq\frac{d_{1}\cdot|\tilde{X}|}{2^{r+8}\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{4}n}.

(2)

Lastly, we show that both
2^{q},2^{q^{\prime}}
are close to
2^{r}
, in the following corollary of
3.17
.

Corollary 3.18
.

The following inequalities hold:

•

\frac{2^{r}}{2d(n)}\leq 2^{q}\leq 2^{r+8}\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{4}n
; and

•

\frac{2^{r}}{2d(n)}\leq 2^{q^{\prime}}\leq 2^{r+6}\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{3}n
.

Proof:

Consider some constraint
C=C(x,y)\in\mathcal{C}^{*}
, and recall that
|E^{\prime}(C)|\geq 2^{r}
. From the definition of the
d
-to-
d
instances, every vertex
v(x,a)\in R(x)
may be incident to at most
d(n)
edges of
E(C)
. Since all edges of
E^{\prime}(C)
are incident to vertices of
R^{\prime}(x)
, and
|R^{\prime}(x)|\leq 2^{q+1}
, we get that
|E^{\prime}(C)|\leq d(n)\cdot|R^{\prime}(x)|\leq d(n)\cdot 2^{q+1}
. We conclude that
2^{r}\leq d(n)\cdot 2^{q+1}
.

Similarly, every vertex
v(y,a^{\prime})\in R(y)
may be incident to at most
d(n)
edges of
E(C)
. Since all edges of
E^{\prime}(C)
are incident to vertices of
R^{\prime}(y)
, and
|R^{\prime}(y)|\leq 2^{q^{\prime}+1}
, we get that
|E^{\prime}(C)|\leq d(n)\cdot 2^{q^{\prime}+1}
.
This proves the inequalities
\frac{2^{r}}{2d(n)}\leq 2^{q}
and
\frac{2^{r}}{2d(n)}\leq 2^{q^{\prime}}
.

Next, we prove that
2^{q^{\prime}}\leq 2^{r+6}\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{3}n
. Recall that, from Inequality
1
,
|\mathcal{C}^{*}|\geq\frac{d_{2}\cdot|\tilde{Y}|}{2^{r+6}\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{3}n}
.
On the other hand, from the definition of a nice subgraph, every variable

may participate in at most

constraints of
\mathcal{C}^{*}
, and, from

|Y^{*}|\leq\frac{|\tilde{Y}|}{2^{q^{\prime}}}

|\mathcal{C}^{*}|\leq 2d_{2}\cdot|Y^{*}|\leq\frac{2d_{2}|\tilde{Y}|}{2^{q^{\prime}}}.

(3)

Combining the two inequalities, we get that:

.

Lastly, we prove that

Recall that, from Inequality

,

|\mathcal{C}^{*}|\geq\frac{d_{1}\cdot|\tilde{X}|}{2^{r+7}\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{4}n}
holds.
As before, from the definition of a nice subgraph, every variable

may participate in at most

constraints of
\mathcal{C}^{*}
, and, from

|\mathcal{C}^{*}|\leq 2d_{1}\cdot|X^{*}|\leq\frac{2d_{1}\cdot|\tilde{X}|}{2^{q}}.

(4)

Combining the two inequalities together, we get that:
2^{q}\leq 2^{r+8}\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{4}n
.

Step 4: Certifying that

We consider two cases, depending on whether

\beta^{3}

Observation 3.19
.

If

, then graph

is
\beta^{3}
-good.

Proof:

We show that there exists an assignment to variables of
X\cup Y
that satisfies at least

constraints of
\mathcal{C}
. In order to do it, we show a randomized algorithm that computes assignments to variables of

The assignments are computed as follows. Consider a variable
x\in X
. If

, then we assign to

an arbitrary value from
[A]
. Assume now that
x\in X^{*}
. Recall that we have defined a set

of vertices, whose cardinality is at most

R^{\prime}(x)
of vertices naturally defines a collection

of assignments to variable

to

Assignments to variables of

are defined similarly. Consider a variable
y\in Y

y\not\in Y^{*}

y
an arbitrary value from
[A]
. Assume now that
y\in Y^{*}
. Recall that we have defined a set
R^{\prime}(y)\subseteq R(y)
of vertices, whose cardinality is at most
2^{q^{\prime}+1}
. Set
R^{\prime}(y)
of vertices naturally defines a collection
\hat{A}(y)=\{a\in[A]\mid v(y,a)\in R^{\prime}(y)\}

y

|\hat{A}(y)|\leq 2^{q^{\prime}+1}
. We choose an assignment
a^{\prime}\in\hat{A}(y)
uniformly at random, and assign value
a^{\prime}
to

.

Recall that we have computed, in
3.16
, a collection
\mathcal{C}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
of constraints, with
|\mathcal{C}^{*}|\geq\frac{|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|}{2^{r+6}\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{3}n}

must hold. Recall that graph
G^{\prime\prime}
contains a collection

wins

, and

is assigned value

is satisfied by the assignment that the algorithm chooses. Therefore, the probability that a constraint

is satisfied is at least
\frac{2^{r}}{2^{q+1}\cdot 2^{q^{\prime}+1}}
.

Overall, the expected number of constraints that are satisfied by the assignment is at least:

\frac{|\mathcal{C}^{*}|\cdot 2^{r}}{2^{q+1}\cdot 2^{q^{\prime}+1}}\geq\frac{|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|}{2^{8}\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot 2^{q}\cdot 2^{q^{\prime}}\cdot\log^{3}n}

Recall that, from
3.13

|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|=|E(\tilde{H})|\geq\frac{|E(H)|}{8\log^{2}n}=\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{8\log^{2}n}
, and, from

, since we have assumed that

.
Therefore, the expected number of constraints of

that are satisfied by the assignment is at least:

\frac{|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|}{2^{8}\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot 2^{q}\cdot 2^{q^{\prime}}\cdot\log^{3}n}\geq\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{2^{11}\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot 2^{q}\cdot 2^{q^{\prime}}\cdot\log^{5}n}\geq\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{2^{25}\cdot\beta^{2}\cdot(\alpha(n))^{3}\cdot\log^{12}n}\geq\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{\beta^{3}},

We conclude that there is an assignment to the variables of

is

-good.

If

is
\beta
-good.

From now on we assume that

of

, that is defined as follows:

,

, and

. We now verify that this graph has all required properties.

Recall that, from
3.17
,
|X^{*}|\leq\frac{|\tilde{X}|}{2^{q}}\leq\frac{|X|}{2^{q}}
, from
Corollary

3.18
,
2^{q}\geq\frac{2^{r}}{2d(n)}
, and, from our assumption,

. Therefore:

|X^{*}|\leq\frac{|X|}{2^{q}}\leq\frac{|X|\cdot 2d(n)}{2^{r}}\leq\frac{2d(n)}{\beta}\cdot|X|.

Similarly, from
3.17
,
|Y^{*}|\leq\frac{|\tilde{Y}|}{2^{q^{\prime}}}\leq\frac{|Y|}{2^{q^{\prime}}}
, from
Corollary

3.18
,
2^{q^{\prime}}\geq\frac{2^{r}}{2d(n)}
, and, from our assumption,

. Therefore:

|Y^{*}|\leq\frac{|Y|}{2^{q^{\prime}}}\leq\frac{|Y|\cdot 2d(n)}{2^{r}}\leq\frac{2d(n)}{\beta}\cdot|Y|.

It now only remains to show that
|E^{*}|\geq\frac{\operatorname{vol}_{H}(X^{*}\cup Y^{*})}{256d(n)\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{4}n}
.

Recall that, from Inequality
2
,

|\mathcal{C}^{*}|\geq\frac{d_{1}\cdot|\tilde{X}|}{2^{r+8}\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{4}n}

, since
|X^{*}|\leq\frac{|\tilde{X}|}{2^{q}}
from

. Therefore,

|E^{\prime}|\geq\frac{\operatorname{vol}_{H}(X^{*})}{1024d(n)\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{4}n}
.

Similarly, from Inequality
1
,
|\mathcal{C}^{*}|\geq\frac{d_{2}\cdot|\tilde{Y}|}{2^{r+6}\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{3}n}
.
Since, from

,

|E^{*}|\geq\frac{\operatorname{vol}_{H}(Y^{*})}{1024d(n)\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{3}n}

Altogether, we get that

.

Note that every step of the algorithm, except Step 2, has running time

, while the running time of Step 2 is

.

Reductions from

-Coloring
and

to

k
-Subgraph

In this section we provide reductions from the
Dense
k
-Coloring
and
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
problems to
Densest
k
-Subgraph
, by proving the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\alpha:\mathbb{Z^{+}}\to\mathbb{Z^{+}}
be an increasing function, such that
\alpha(n)\leq o(n)
.
Assume that there is an efficient
\alpha(n)
-approximation algorithm for the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem, where
n
is the number of vertices in the input graph. Then both of the following hold:

•

there is an efficient randomized algorithm that, given an instance of
Dense
k
-Coloring
whose graph contains
N
vertices, with high probability computes an
O(\alpha(N^{2})\cdot\textnormal{poly}\log N)
-approximate solution to this instance; and

•

there is an efficient randomized algorithm that, given an instance of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
whose graph contains
N
vertices, with high probability computes an
O(\alpha(N^{2})\cdot\textnormal{poly}\log N)
-approximate solution to this instance.

We provide the proof of the first assertion of the theorem, by showing a reduction from
Dense
k
-Coloring
to
Densest
k
-Subgraph
. The proof of the second assertion is similar and is deferred to Section
B
of Appendix.
We start by considering an LP-relaxation of the
Dense
k
-Coloring
problem, whose number of variables is at least
N\choose k
. Due to this high number of variables, we cannot solve it directly. We first show an algorithm, that, given an approximate fractional solution to this LP-relaxation, whose support size is polynomial in
N
, computes an approximate integral solution to the
Dense
k
-Coloring
problem instance. We then show an efficient algorithm that computes an approximate solution to the LP-relaxation, whose support is relatively small. In order to do so, we design an approximate separation oracle to the dual LP of the LP-relaxation.

4.1
An LP-Relaxation and Its Rounding

Let
\mathrm{DkC}(G,k)
be an input instance of the
Dense
k
-Coloring
problem, and denote
|V(G)|=N
.
We let
{\mathcal{R}}
the collection of all subsets of
V(G)
containing at most
k
vertices, that is:
{\mathcal{R}}=\{S\subseteq V(G)\mid|S|\leq k\}
. For every set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
of vertices, we denote by
m(S)=|E_{G}(S)|
.
We consider the following LP-relaxation of the
Dense
k
-Coloring
problem, that has a variable
x_{S}
for every set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
of vertices.

(LP-P)

\displaystyle\max

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}}m(S)\cdot x_{S}

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle S\in{\mathcal{R}}:}}{{v\in S}}}x_{S}\leq 1

\displaystyle\forall v\in V(G)

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}}x_{S}\leq N/k

\displaystyle x_{S}\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall S\in{\mathcal{R}}

It is easy to verify that (LP-P) is an LP-relaxation of the
Dense
k
-Coloring
problem. Indeed, consider a solution
(S_{1},\ldots,S_{r})
to the input instance
\mathrm{DkC}(G,k)
, where
r=N/k
. For all
1\leq i\leq r
, we set
x_{S_{i}}=1
, and for every other set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
, we set
x_{S}=0
. This provides a feasible solution to (LP-P), whose value is precisely
\sum_{i=1}^{r}|E_{G}(S_{i})|
. We denote the value of the optimal solution to (LP-P) by
\text{OPT}_{\textnormal{LP-P}}
. From the above discussion,
\text{OPT}_{\textnormal{LP-P}}\geq\mathrm{OPT_{DkC}}(G,k)
.

Note that the number of variables in (LP-P) is at least
N\choose k
, and so we cannot solve it directly. We will show below an efficient algorithm that provides an approximate solution to (LP-P), whose support is reasonably small. Before we do so, we provide an LP-rounding algorithm, by proving the following claim.

Claim 4.2
.

There is an efficient randomized algorithm, whose input consists of an instance
\mathrm{DkC}(G,k)
of the
Dense
k
-Coloring
problem with
N=|V(G)|
, such that
N
is greater than a large enough constant, and a solution
\{x_{S}\mid S\in{\mathcal{R}}\}
to (LP-P), in which the number of variables
x_{S}
with
x_{S}>0
is bounded by
\textnormal{poly}(N)
, and
\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}}m(S)\cdot x_{S}\geq\text{OPT}_{\textnormal{LP-P}}/\beta
, for some parameter
1\leq\beta\leq N^{3}
; the solution is given by only specifying values of variables
x_{S}
that are non-zero. The algorithm with high probability returns an integral solution
(S_{1},\ldots,S_{N/k})
to instance
\mathrm{DkC}(G,k)
, such that
\sum_{i=1}^{N/k}|E_{G}(S_{i})|\geq\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{DkC}}(G,k)}{2000\beta\log^{3}N}
.

Proof:

We assume that we are given a solution
\{x_{S}\mid S\in{\mathcal{R}}\}
to (LP-P), in which the number of variables
x_{S}
with
x_{S}>0
is bounded by
\textnormal{poly}(N)
. Denote
C=\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}}m(S)\cdot x_{S}
, so that
C\geq\frac{\text{OPT}_{\textnormal{LP-P}}}{\beta}\geq\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{DkC}}(G,k)}{\beta}
holds. We denote by
{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
the collection of all sets
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
with
x_{S}>0
. Assume first that there is any set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}
with
m(S)\geq\frac{C}{300\log^{3}N}
. Then we can obtain the desired solution
(S_{1},\ldots,S_{N/k})
as follows. We start with
S_{1}=S
and
S_{2}=\cdots=S_{N/k}=\emptyset
. We then iteratively add vertices of
V(G)\setminus S
to sets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{N/k}
arbitrarily, to ensure that the cardinality of each such set is exactly
k
. It is immediate to verify that
\sum_{i=1}^{N/k}|E_{G}(S_{i})|\geq\frac{C}{300\log^{3}N}\geq\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{DkC}}(G,k)}{300\beta\log^{3}N}
. Therefore, we assume from now on that, for every set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}
,
m(S)<\frac{C}{300\log^{3}N}
.

We construct another collection
{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime\prime}\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}
of subsets of vertices of
G
as follows. For every set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}
, we add
S
to
{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime\prime}
independently, with probability
x_{S}
. Clearly,
\text{\bf E}\left[\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime\prime}}m(S)\right]=\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}}m(S)\cdot x_{S}=C
.

We say that a bad event
{\cal{E}}_{1}
happens if some vertex
v\in V(G)
lies in more than
5\log N
sets in
{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime\prime}
. We say that a bad event
{\cal{E}}_{2}
happens if
|{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime\prime}|>(5N\log N)/k
. We say that a bad event
{\cal{E}}_{3}
happens if
\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime\prime}}m(S)<\frac{C}{8}
.
Lastly, we say that a bad event
{\cal{E}}
happens if either of the events
{\cal{E}}_{1},{\cal{E}}_{2}
, or
{\cal{E}}_{3}
happen.
We start with the following simple observation.

Observation 4.3
.

\text{\bf Pr}\left[{\cal{E}}\right]\leq\frac{2}{N^{3}}
.

Proof:

For every set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}
, let
Y_{S}
be the random variable whose value is
1
if
S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime\prime}
and
0
otherwise.

v\in V(G)

\text{\bf E}\left[Z_{v}\right]=\text{\bf E}\left[\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}:}}{{v\in S}}}Y_{S}\right]=\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}:}}{{v\in S}}}x_{S}\leq 1,

where the last inequality follows from the constraints of (LP-P).

By applying the Chernoff Bound from
Lemma

2.2
, we get that
\text{\bf Pr}\left[Z_{v}>5\log N\right]\leq 1/N^{5}
. Using the union bound over all vertices
v\in V(G)
, we get that
\text{\bf Pr}\left[{\cal{E}}_{1}\right]\leq 1/N^{4}
.

Notice that
\text{\bf E}\left[|{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime\prime}|\right]=\text{\bf E}\left[\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}}Y_{S}\right]=\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}}x_{S}\leq N/k

\text{\bf Pr}\left[{\cal{E}}_{2}\right]=\text{\bf Pr}\left[\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}}Y_{S}>(5N\log N)/k\right]\leq 1/N^{5}
.

Lastly, we bound the probability of Event
{\cal{E}}_{3}
.
Recall that we have assumed that, for every set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}
,

{\mathcal{R}}_{i}=\{S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}\mid 2^{i}\leq m(S)<2^{i+1}\}
.
For all
0\leq i<\rho

. Clearly,
\sum_{i=0}^{\rho-1}C_{i}=C
. We say that an index

is
bad

, and otherwise we say that
i
is a
good index
. Let
I^{g},I^{b}\subseteq\{0,\ldots,\rho-1\}
be the collections of good and bad indices, respectively. Since

\sum_{i\in I^{b}}C_{i}\leq(4\log N)\cdot\frac{C}{8\log N}\leq\frac{C}{2}.

Therefore,
\sum_{i\in I^{g}}C_{i}\geq\frac{C}{2}
holds. Consider now a good index

. Recall that, for every set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}
,
m(S)\leq\frac{C}{300\log^{3}N}

\frac{C}{8\log N}\leq\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}_{i}}m(S)x_{S}\leq 2^{i+1}\cdot\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}_{i}}x_{S}\leq\frac{C}{150\log^{3}N}\cdot\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}_{i}}x_{S}.

We conclude that
\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}_{i}}x_{S}\geq 16\log^{2}N
holds for every good index
i\in I^{g}
.

For a good index
i\in I^{g}
, we denote by
{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}_{i}={\mathcal{R}}_{i}\cap{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime\prime}
, and we let
\tilde{\cal{E}}_{i}
be the bad event that

From the Chernoff Bound (

2.2

\text{\bf Pr}\left[\tilde{\cal{E}}_{i}\right]\leq e^{-2\log^{2}N}<N^{-4}

By applying the Union Bound to all indices

, we get that the probability that any of the events in

. Note that, if neither of the events in

\begin{split}\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime\prime}}m(S)&\geq\sum_{i\in I^{g}}2^{i}\cdot|{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}_{i}|\\
&\geq\sum_{i\in I^{g}}2^{i}\cdot\frac{\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}_{i}}x_{S}}{2}\\
&\geq\sum_{i\in I^{g}}\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}_{i}}\frac{m(S)\cdot x_{S}}{4}\\
&\geq\sum_{i\in I^{g}}\frac{C_{i}}{4}\\
&\geq\frac{C}{8}.\end{split}

Therefore, if neither of the events in

also does not happen. We conclude that
\text{\bf Pr}\left[{\cal{E}}_{3}\right]\leq 1/N^{3}
.

Finally, from the Union Bound, we get that:

\text{\bf Pr}\left[{\cal{E}}\right]\leq\text{\bf Pr}\left[{\cal{E}}_{1}\right]+\text{\bf Pr}\left[{\cal{E}}_{2}\right]+\text{\bf Pr}\left[{\cal{E}}_{3}\right]\leq\frac{1}{N^{4}}+\frac{1}{N^{5}}+\frac{1}{N^{3}}\leq\frac{2}{N^{3}}.

Observe that we can efficiently check whether Event
{\cal{E}}
happened. If Event
{\cal{E}}
happens, then we terminate the algorithm with a FAIL. We assume from now on that Event
{\cal{E}}
did not happen.
In this case, we are guaranteed that
\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime\prime}}m(S)\geq\frac{C}{8}\geq\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{DkC}}(G,k)}{8\beta}
.
We denote
{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime\prime}=\{S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{z}\}
, where the sets are indexed according to their value
m(S)
, so that
m(S_{1})\geq m(S_{2})\geq\cdots\geq m(S_{z})
. We then let
{\mathcal{S}}=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{N/k}\}
(if
z<N/k
, then we set
S_{z+1}=\cdots=S_{N/k}=\emptyset
). For all
1\leq i\leq N/k
, we denote
E_{i}=E_{G}(S_{i})
, so
|E_{i}|=m(S_{i})
, and we denote
E^{\prime}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N/k}E_{i}
. Recall that, since Event
{\cal{E}}
did not happen,
|{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime\prime}|\leq(5N\log N)/k
holds. Therefore:

|E^{\prime}|\geq\frac{\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime\prime}}m(S)}{5\log N}\geq\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{DkC}}(G,k)}{40\beta\log N}.

Note that the vertex sets in the family
{\mathcal{S}}
may not be mutually disjoint. However, since Event
{\cal{E}}
did not happen, every vertex of
V(G)
may lie in at most
5\log N
such sets. We now construct a new collection
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}=\{S_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,S_{N/k}^{\prime}\}
of sets of vertices, as follows. Consider any vertex
v\in V(G)
, and let
S_{i_{1}},S_{i_{2}},\ldots,S_{i_{a}}\in{\mathcal{S}}
be the sets of
{\mathcal{S}}
containing
v
. Vertex
v
chooses an index
i^{*}\in\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{a}\}
at random, and is then added to
S^{\prime}_{i^{*}}
.

Note that for all
1\leq j\leq N/k
, for every vertex
v\in S_{j}
, the probability that
v\in S^{\prime}_{j}
is at least
1/(5\log N)
. We say that an edge
e=(u,v)\in E_{j}

survives
if both
u,v\in S^{\prime}_{j}
. We denote by
E^{\prime\prime}\subseteq E^{\prime}
the set of all edges that survive. Since
\text{\bf Pr}\left[u\in S^{\prime}_{j}\right]\geq 1/(5\log N)
,
\text{\bf Pr}\left[v\in S^{\prime}_{j}\right]\geq 1/(5\log N)
, and the two events are independent, we get that the probability that edge
e
survives is at least
1/(25\log^{2}N)
. Overall, we get that:

\text{\bf E}\left[|E^{\prime\prime}|\right]\geq\frac{|E^{\prime}|}{25\log^{2}N}\geq\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{DkC}}(G,k)}{1000\beta\log^{3}N}.

We obtain a final solution
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
to instance
\mathrm{DkC}(G,k)
of the
Dense
k
-Coloring
problem by starting with
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}={\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
, and then partitioning the vertices of
V(G)\setminus\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{N/k}S^{\prime}_{j}\right)
among the sets of
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
arbitrarily, until each such set contains exactly
k
vertices. Clearly, the value of the resulting solution is at least
|E^{\prime\prime}|
.

So far we have obtained a randomized algorithm that either returns FAIL (with probability at most
2/N^{3}
), or it returns a solution to instance
\mathrm{DkC}(G,k)
of the
Dense
k
-Coloring
problem, whose expected value is at least
\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{DkC}}(G,k)}{1000\beta\log^{3}N}
.

Let
p^{\prime}
be the probability that the algorithm returned a solution of value at least
\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{DkC}}(G,k)}{3000\beta\log^{3}N}
, given that it did not return FAIL. Note that the expected solution value, assuming the algorithm did not return FAIL, is at most
\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{DkC}}(G,k)}{2000\beta\log^{3}N}+p^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{OPT_{DkC}}(G,k)
. Since this expectation is also at least
\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{DkC}}(G,k)}{1000\beta\log^{3}N}
, we get that
p^{\prime}\geq\frac{1}{2000\beta\log^{3}N}
. Overall, the probability that our algorithm successfully returns a solution of value at least
\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{DkC}}(G,k)}{2000\beta\log^{3}N}
is
p^{\prime}\cdot\text{\bf Pr}\left[\neg{\cal{E}}\right]\geq\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\beta\log^{3}N}\right)
. By repeating the algorithm
\textnormal{poly}(N)
times we can ensure that it successfully computes a solution of value at least
\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{DkC}}(G,k)}{2000\beta\log^{3}N}
with high probability.

4.2
Approximately Solving the LP-Relaxation

As observed already, (LP-P) has
\binom{N}{k}
variables, and so we cannot solve it directly. Instead, we will use the Ellipsoids algorithm with an approximate separation oracle to its dual LP, that appears below. This LP has a variable
z
, and, additionally, for every vertex
v\in V(G)
, it has a variable
y_{v}
.

(LP-D)

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\frac{N}{k}\cdot z+\sum_{v\in V(G)}y_{v}

s.t.

\displaystyle z+\sum_{v\in S}y_{v}\geq m(S)

\displaystyle\forall S\in{\mathcal{R}}

\displaystyle z\geq 0

\displaystyle y_{v}\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall v\in V(G)

We denote the value of the optimal solution to (LP-D) by
\text{OPT}_{\textnormal{LP-D}}
.

Next, we define an approximate separation oracle, and provide such a separation oracle to (LP-D).

Approximate Separation Oracle.

Consider the following general minimization Linear Program, whose variables are
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
.

\displaystyle\mbox{(}P)

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}x_{i}

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{j,i}x_{i}\geq b_{j}

\displaystyle\forall 1\leq j\leq m

\displaystyle x_{i}\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall 1\leq i\leq n

Let
\beta:\mathbb{Z^{+}}\to\mathbb{Z^{+}}
be an increasing function. A
randomized
\beta(n)
-approximate separation oracle
for (P) is an efficient randomized algorithm (that is, the running time of the algorithm is bounded by a polynomial function of its input size). The input to the algorithm is a set
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of non-negative real values.
The algorithm either returns “accept”, or it returns an LP-constraint (called a
violated constraint
) that does not hold for the given values
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. We say that the algorithm
errs
if it returns “accept” and yet there is some index
1\leq j\leq m
for which
\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{j,i}x_{i}<b_{j}/\beta(n)
holds. We require that the probability that the algorithm errs is at most
2/3
.

For the case where a linear program has a very large number of constraints, or the constraints are not given explicitly, one can use an approximate separation oracle, combined with the Ellipsoids algorithm, in order to compute an approximate LP-solution in time polynomial in the number of variables of the LP, provided that there is an Ellipsoid containing the feasible region, whose volume is not too large. For the case where a linear program has a large number of variables, but a relatively small number of constraints, we can use an approximate separation oracle for its dual LP in order to solve the original LP approximately.
We start by providing an approximate separation oracle for (LP-D). We then show that this separation oracle can be used in order to obtain an approximate solution to (LP-P) in time
\textnormal{poly}(N)
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume that there is an efficient
\alpha(n)
-approximation algorithm for the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem, where
\alpha
is an increasing function, and
n
is the number of vertices in the input graph. Then there is a randomized
\beta(N)
-approximate separation oracle for (LP-D), where
N
is the number of variables in the input graph
G
, and
\beta(N)=O(\alpha(N^{2})\cdot\log^{2}N)
.

Proof:

Recall that we are given as input real values
z
and
\{y_{v}\mid v\in V(G)\}
. Clearly, we can efficiently check whether
z\geq 0
, and whether
y_{v}\geq 0
for all
v\in V(G)
. If this is not the case, we can return the corresponding violated constraint.

We say that a set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
of vertices is
bad
if
z+\sum_{v\in S}y_{v}<m(S)/\beta
holds, where
\beta=c\cdot\alpha(N^{2})\cdot\log^{2}N
, and
c
is a large enough constant whose value we set later.
Our goal is to design an efficient algorithm that either returns a violated constraint of the LP (that is, a set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
of vertices for which

z+\sum_{v\in S}y_{v}<m(S)
holds); or it returns “accept”. We require that, if there exists a bad set
S
of vertices, then the probability that the algorithm returns “accept” is at most
2/3
.

It will be convenient for us to slightly modify the input values in
\{y_{v}\mid v\in V(G)\}
, as follows. We let
m
be the smallest integral power of
2
that is greater than
|E(G)|
. First, for every vertex
v\in V(G)
with
y_{v}>m
, we let
y^{\prime}_{v}=m
, and for every vertex
v\in V(G)
with
y_{v}<1/4
, we set
y^{\prime}_{v}=0
. For each remaining vertex
v
, we let
y^{\prime}_{v}
be the smallest integral power of
2
that is greater than
4y_{v}
. Note that for every vertex
v
with
y^{\prime}_{v}\neq 0
,
1\leq y^{\prime}_{v}\leq 4m
holds, and
y^{\prime}_{v}
is an integral power of
2
. We also set
z^{\prime}=2z
. We say that a set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
of vertices is
problematic
if

z^{\prime}+\sum_{v\in S}y^{\prime}_{v}<8m(S)/\beta
holds. We
need the following two observations regarding the new values
\{y^{\prime}_{v}\mid v\in V(G)\}
.

Observation 4.5
.

If
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
is a bad set of vertices, then it is a problematic set of vertices.

Proof:

Recall that, if
S
is a bad set of vertices, then
z+\sum_{v\in S}y_{v}<m(S)/\beta
must hold. Since, for every vertex
v\in V(G)
,
y^{\prime}_{v}\leq 8y_{v}
holds, and
z^{\prime}=2z
, we get that:

z^{\prime}+\sum_{v\in S}y^{\prime}_{v}\leq 2z+8\sum_{v\in S}y_{v}\leq 8\left(z+\sum_{v\in S}y_{v}\right)<8m(S)/\beta.

Therefore, set
S
is problematic.

Observation 4.6
.

Assume that there exists a set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
of vertices, for which
z^{\prime}+\sum_{v\in S}y^{\prime}_{v}<m(S)
holds. Let
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
be the set of vertices obtained from
S
by deleting every vertex
v\in S
that has no neighbors in
S
. Then
z+\sum_{v\in S^{\prime}}y_{v}<m(S^{\prime})
holds.

Proof:

Since, for every vertex
v\in S\setminus S^{\prime}
, no neighbor of
v
lies in
S
, we get that
m(S^{\prime})=|E_{G}(S^{\prime})|=|E_{G}(S)|=m(S)
. We partition the vertices of
S^{\prime}
into two subsets: set
X
containing all vertices
v\in S^{\prime}
with
y_{v}<1/4
, and set
Y
containing all remaining vertices. Clearly,
\sum_{v\in X}y_{v}<\frac{|X|}{4}\leq\frac{m(S^{\prime})}{2}
(since
m(S^{\prime})\geq|S^{\prime}|/2\geq|X|/2
, as graph
G[S^{\prime}]
contains no isolated vertices).

Assume for contradiction that
z+\sum_{v\in S^{\prime}}y_{v}\geq m(S^{\prime})
. Then:

z+\sum_{v\in Y}y_{v}\geq m(S^{\prime})-\sum_{v\in X}y_{v}\geq m(S^{\prime})/2.

We now consider two cases. The first case is when there is some vertex
v\in Y
with
y_{v}\geq m
. In this case,
y^{\prime}_{v}\geq m
holds, and
z^{\prime}+\sum_{v\in S}y^{\prime}_{v}\geq m>m(S)
holds, a contradiction.

Otherwise, for every vertex
v\in Y
,
y^{\prime}_{v}\geq 4y_{v}
holds. Since
z^{\prime}=2z
also holds, we get that:

z^{\prime}+\sum_{v\in S}y^{\prime}_{v}\geq z^{\prime}+\sum_{v\in Y}y^{\prime}_{v}\geq 2z+4\sum_{v\in Y}y_{v}\geq m(S^{\prime})=m(S),

a contradiction.

From now on we focus on values
z^{\prime},\{y^{\prime}_{v}\mid v\in V(G)\}
. It is now enough to design an efficient randomized algorithm, that either computes a set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
of vertices, for which
z^{\prime}+\sum_{v\in S}y^{\prime}_{v}<m(S)
holds, or returns “accept”. It is enough to ensure that, if there is a problematic set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
of vertices, then the algorithm returns “accept” with probability at most
2/3
. Indeed, if there is a bad set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
of vertices, then, from
4.5
, there is a problematic set of vertices, and the algorithm will return “accept” with probability at most
2/3
. On the other hand, if the algorithm computes a set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
of vertices, for which
z^{\prime}+\sum_{v\in S}y^{\prime}_{v}<m(S)
holds, then we can return the set
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
of vertices from the statement of
4.6
, that defines a violated constraint with respect to the original LP-values.

Our algorithm computes a random partition
(A,B)
of the vertices of
G
, where every vertex
v\in V(G)
is independently added to
A
or to
B
with probability
1/2
each. Let
q=\log(8m)
. For all
1\leq i\leq q
, we define a set
A_{i}\subseteq A
of vertices:
A_{i}=\{v\in A\mid y^{\prime}_{v}=2^{i-1}\}
, and we let
A_{0}=\{v\in A\mid y^{\prime}_{v}=0\}
. Clearly,
(A_{0},\ldots,A_{q})
is a partition of the set
A
of vertices.

We compute a partition
(B_{0},\ldots,B_{q})
of the vertices of
B
similarly. For all
0\leq i,j\leq q
, we denote by
E_{i,j}
the set of all edges
e=(u,v)
with
u\in A_{i}
and
v\in B_{j}
, and we define a bipartite graph
G_{i,j}
, whose vertex set is
A_{i}\cup B_{j}
, and edge set is
E_{i,j}
.

Recall that we have assumed that there is an efficient
\alpha(n)
-approximation algorithm for the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem, where
n
is the number of vertices in the input graph. From
Lemma

2.1
, there exists an efficient
O(\alpha(\hat{n}^{2}))
-approximation algorithm for the
Bipartite Densest
(k_{1},k_{2})
-Subgraph
problem, where
\hat{n}
is the number of vertices in the input graph.
We denote this algorithm by
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
.

For every pair
0\leq i,j\leq q
of integers, and every pair
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 0
of integers with
k_{1}+k_{2}\leq k
, we apply Algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
for the
Bipartite Densest
(k_{1},k_{2})
-Subgraph
problem to graph
G_{i,j}
, with parameters
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
. Let
S_{i,j}^{k_{1},k_{2}}
be the output of this algorithm, and let
m_{i,j}^{k_{1},k_{2}}
be the number of edges in the subgraph of
G_{i,j}
that is induced by the set
S_{i,j}^{k_{1},k_{2}}
of vertices. We say that the application of algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
is
successful
if
z^{\prime}+\sum_{v\in S_{i,j}^{k_{1},k_{2}}}y^{\prime}_{v}<m_{i,j}^{k_{1},k_{2}}
, and otherwise it is
unsuccessful
. If, for any quadruple
(i,j,k_{1},k_{2})
of indices, the application of algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
was successful, then we return the resulting set
S=S_{i,j}^{k_{1},k_{2}}
of vertices. Clearly,
|S|\leq k_{1}+k_{2}\leq k
, so
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
holds. Moreover, we are guaranteed that
z^{\prime}+\sum_{v\in S}y^{\prime}_{v}<m_{i,j}^{k_{1},k_{2}}<m(S)
, as required. If every application of algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
is unsuccessful, then we return “accept”. The following observation will finish the proof of
Lemma

4.4
.

Observation 4.7
.

Suppose there is a problematic set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
of vertices. Then the probability that the algorithm returns “accept” is at most
2/3
.

Proof:

Let
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
be a problematic set of vertices, so
z^{\prime}+\sum_{v\in S}y^{\prime}_{v}<8m(S)/\beta
holds. Let
E^{\prime}=E_{G}[S]
, so
|E^{\prime}|=m(S)
. Denote
A_{S}=A\cap S,B_{S}=B\cap S
, and let
E^{\prime\prime}\subseteq E^{\prime}
be the set of edges
e
, such that exactly one endpoint of
e
lies in
A
. Clearly, for every edge
e\in E^{\prime}
,
\text{\bf Pr}\left[e\in E^{\prime\prime}\right]=1/2
. Therefore,
\text{\bf E}\left[|E^{\prime\prime}|\right]=|E^{\prime}|/2
. Let
{\cal{E}}^{\prime}
be the bad event that
|E^{\prime\prime}|<|E^{\prime}|/8
, and let
p=\text{\bf Pr}\left[{\cal{E}}^{\prime}\right]
. Clearly:

\text{\bf E}\left[|E^{\prime\prime}|\right]\leq p\cdot\frac{|E^{\prime}|}{8}+(1-p)\cdot|E^{\prime}|=|E^{\prime}|\left(1-\frac{7p}{8}\right).

Since
\text{\bf E}\left[|E^{\prime\prime}|\right]=|E^{\prime}|/2
, we get that
p\leq 2/3
. Next, we show that, if Event
{\cal{E}}^{\prime}
does not happen, then the algorithm does not return “accept”.

From now on we assume that Event
{\cal{E}}^{\prime}
did not happen, so
|E^{\prime\prime}|\geq|E^{\prime}|/8
.
Therefore:

z^{\prime}+\sum_{v\in S}y^{\prime}_{v}<\frac{8m(S)}{\beta}\leq\frac{64|E^{\prime\prime}|}{\beta}

holds.

Clearly, there must be a pair
0\leq i,j\leq q
of indices, such that
|E^{\prime\prime}\cap E_{i,j}|\geq\frac{|E^{\prime\prime}|}{4q^{2}}\geq\frac{|E^{\prime\prime}|}{128\log^{2}m}
. We now fix this pair
i,j
of indices, and denote
A^{\prime}_{i}=A_{i}\cap S
and
B^{\prime}_{j}=B_{j}\cap S
. We also denote
k_{1}=|A^{\prime}_{i}|
and let
k_{2}=|B^{\prime}_{j}|
.
Clearly,
k_{1}+k_{2}\leq k
holds.
Denote
M_{i,j}=|E^{\prime\prime}\cap E_{i,j}|
.
From our choice of indices
i,j
, we get that:

z^{\prime}+\sum_{v\in A^{\prime}_{i}\cup B^{\prime}_{j}}y^{\prime}_{v}\leq z^{\prime}+\sum_{v\in S}y^{\prime}_{v}\leq\frac{64|E^{\prime\prime}|}{\beta}\leq\frac{M_{i,j}}{\beta}\cdot(2^{13}\log^{2}m).

Notice that the set
S^{\prime}=A^{\prime}_{i}\cup B^{\prime}_{j}
of vertices provides a solution to the instance of the
Bipartite Densest
(k_{1},k_{2})
-Subgraph
problem on graph
G_{i,j}
with parameters
k_{1},k_{2}
, whose value is at least
M_{i,j}
. Let
S^{\prime\prime}=S_{i,j}^{k_{1},k_{2}}
be the set of vertices obtained by applying Algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
to graph
G_{i,j}
with parameters
k_{1},k_{2}
. Since
|V(G_{i,j})|\leq N
, and since
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
is an
O(\alpha(N^{2}))
-approximation algorithm for
Bipartite Densest
(k_{1},k_{2})
-Subgraph
, we are guaranteed that
|E_{G}(S^{\prime\prime})|\geq\Omega\left(\frac{M_{i,j}}{\alpha(N^{2})}\right)
. Recall that
|A\cap S^{\prime\prime}|\leq k_{1}
;
A\cap S^{\prime\prime}\subseteq A_{i}
, and all vertices
v\in A_{i}
have an identical value
y^{\prime}_{v}
. Therefore,
\sum_{v\in A\cap S^{\prime\prime}}y^{\prime}_{v}\leq\sum_{v\in A^{\prime}_{i}}y^{\prime}_{v}
. Using a similar reasoning,

\sum_{v\in B\cap S^{\prime\prime}}y^{\prime}_{v}\leq\sum_{v\in B^{\prime}_{j}}y^{\prime}_{v}
. Overall, we then get that:

\begin{split}z^{\prime}+\sum_{v\in S^{\prime\prime}}y^{\prime}_{v}&\leq z^{\prime}+\sum_{v\in S^{\prime}}y^{\prime}_{v}\\
&\leq\frac{M_{i,j}}{\beta}\cdot(2^{13}\cdot\log^{2}m)\\
&\leq O\left(\frac{|E_{G}(S^{\prime\prime})|\cdot\alpha(N^{2})\cdot 2^{13}\cdot\log^{2}m}{\beta}\right)\end{split}

Recall that
\beta=c\cdot\alpha(N^{2})\cdot\log^{2}N
. By letting the value of the constant
c
be high enough, we can ensure that
z^{\prime}+\sum_{v\in S^{\prime\prime}}y^{\prime}_{v}<|E_{G}(S^{\prime\prime})|
, and so the application of algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
to graph
G_{i,j}
with parameters
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
is guaranteed to be successful. Therefore, if Event
{\cal{E}}^{\prime}
does not happen, and we set
c
to be a large enough constant, then our algorithm does not return ”accept”. Since
\text{\bf Pr}\left[{\cal{E}}^{\prime}\right]\leq 2/3
, the observation follows.

Approximately Solving (LP-P).

We use standard methods for solving (LP-P) using approximate separation oracle for (LP-D).

For a collection
{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
of vertex subsets, we define a linear program (
\mbox{LP}({\mathcal{R}}^{\prime})
), which is obtained from (LP-D) by only including the constraints associated with the subsets in
{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}
:

(LP(
{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}
))

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\frac{N}{k}\cdot z+\sum_{v\in V(G)}y_{v}

s.t.

\displaystyle z+\sum_{v\in S}y_{v}\geq m(S)

\displaystyle\forall S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle z\geq 0

\displaystyle y_{v}\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall v\in V(G)

We denote by
\text{OPT}({\mathcal{R}}^{\prime})
the value of the optimal solution to (
\mbox{LP}({\mathcal{R}}^{\prime})
).
Since a solution to (LP-D) defines a solution to (
\mbox{LP}({\mathcal{R}}^{\prime})
), we get that
\text{OPT}({\mathcal{R}}^{\prime})\leq\text{OPT}_{\textnormal{LP-D}}

We use the following lemma that allows us to compute a small collection
{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
of vertex subsets, such that
\text{OPT}({\mathcal{R}}^{\prime})
is within a factor
\beta(N)=O(\alpha(N^{2})\cdot\log^{2}N)
of
\text{OPT}_{\textnormal{LP-D}}
.

Claim 4.8
.

Assume that there is an efficient
\alpha(n)
-approximation algorithm for
Densest
k
-Subgraph
, where
\alpha
is an increasing function of
n
, and
n
is the number of vertices in the input graph.
Then there is a randomized algorithm with running time
O(\textnormal{poly}(N))
, that computes a collection
{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
of subsets of vertices with
|{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}|\leq O(\textnormal{poly}(N))
, such that, with high probability,
\text{OPT}({\mathcal{R}}^{\prime})\geq\Omega\left(\text{OPT}_{\textnormal{LP-D}}/\beta(N)\right)
, where
\beta(N)=O(\alpha(N^{2})\cdot\log^{2}N)
.

We prove
4.8
below, after we provide an algorithm for approximately solving (LP-P) using it.

Recall that for every set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
of vertices, there is a variable
x_{S}
in the primal LP, (LP-P). We now consider the dual LP to
\mbox{LP}({\mathcal{R}}^{\prime})
, that is defined as follows:

(LP-P2)

\displaystyle\max

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}}m(S)\cdot x_{S}

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}:}}{{v\in S}}}x_{S}\leq 1

\displaystyle\forall v\in V(G)

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}}x_{S}\leq N/k

\displaystyle x_{S}\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall S\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}

Notice that this Linear Program can be obtained from (LP-P) by eliminating all variables
x_{S}
for
S\in{\mathcal{R}}\setminus{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}
. Since
|{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}|\leq\textnormal{poly}(N)
, this new linear program has at most
O(\textnormal{poly}(N))
variables, and it has at most
\textnormal{poly}(N)
constraints. Therefore, we can solve it in time

Proof of

.

We fix a value
0\leq C\leq\log M
, and consider the corresponding Linear Program (

. The idea of the proof is simple: we employ the Ellipsoids algorithm, together with the separation oracle from
Lemma

4.4
(after we reduce its error probability by repeating the algorithm a number of times). We then let
{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}(C)
be the collection of all vertex subsets
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
, such that the separation oracle returns the constraint associated with
S
over the course of the algorithm.

We now provide more details. Recall first the Ellipsoids algorithm for solving a feasibility Linear Program (
F
) on
N^{\prime}
variables. The algorithm proceeds in iterations. The input to the
i
th iteration is an
N^{\prime}
-dimensional Ellipsoid
E_{i}
, that contains the feasible region of (
F
). Let
x_{i}
denote the center point of the ellipsoid. If the algorithm is given a constraint
A^{j}
of the Linear Program
(F)
that is violated by point
x_{i}
, then it produces a new ellipsoid
E_{i+1}
, that contains the feasible region of (
F
), whose volume is at most
(1-1/\textnormal{poly}(N^{\prime}))
times the volume of
E_{i}
. The running time of a single iteration is
O(\textnormal{poly}(N^{\prime}))
.

Typically, we assume that there is an initial ellipsoid
E_{1}
, whose volume is at most
2^{\textnormal{poly}(N^{\prime})}
, that contains the feasible region of
(F)
, which needs to be supplied to the Ellipsoids algorithm. We can also typically assume that, if
(F)
has a feasible solution, then the volume of the feasible region of
(F)
is at least
L=2^{-\textnormal{poly}(N^{\prime})}
(if this is not the case, the feasible region can be slightly inflated artificially by adding a small amount of slack to the constraints; in our case, since we are only solving the LP approximately, this is immaterial). If the above two conditions hold, the algorithm can proceed for at most
\textnormal{poly}(N^{\prime})
iterations, before the volume of the current ellipsoid becomes smaller than
L
, and the algorithm then correctly declares that
(F)
does not have a feasible solution. In every iteration, the constraint violated by the center
x_{i}
of the current ellipsoid

is supplied by a separation oracle. If the separation oracle declares that
x_{i}
is (approximately) feasible solution, then the algorithm halts.

We now turn to consider the linear program (
F({\mathcal{R}},C))
. Since the LP constraints require that the values of all LP-variables are between
0
and
m
, it is easy to verify that the feasible region of the LP is contained in the
(N+1)

, whose radius is bounded by
\textnormal{poly}(m)
, and volume is at most
2^{\textnormal{poly}(m)}\leq 2^{\textnormal{poly}(N)}
. We initially set

. We apply the Ellipsoids algorithm to this LP, with the initial ellipsoid

.

We now consider the
i
th iteration of the algorithm, whose input is an ellipsoid

, together with its center point

. We manually check the constraints
\frac{N}{k}\cdot z^{i}+\sum_{v\in V(G)}y^{i}_{v}\leq 2^{C}

; and
0\leq y^{i}_{v}\leq m
for all

. If any of these constraints does not hold, then we return it as a violated constraint. Assume now that all these constraints hold. We apply the algorithm from
Lemma

4.4
to the current values
(z^{i},\{y^{i}_{v}\}_{v\in V(G)})

Assume now that in some application of the algorithm from

Claim 5.3

\text{\bf Pr}\left[{\cal{E}}^{g}\right]\geq 0.8

Proof:

For every index

\hat{E}(T_{j})=\{(u_{f(i)+j},u_{f(i^{\prime})+j})\mid(v_{i},v_{i^{\prime}})\in E_{G}(S)\}

We prove the following observation.

Observation 5.4
.

\text{\bf E}\left[\sum_{j\in I}|\hat{E}(T_{j})|\right]\geq 0.9\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{M}{k\log k}\right\rfloor\cdot\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k).

Assume first that the observation holds.
For simplicity of notation, denote
B=\left\lfloor\frac{M}{k\log k}\right\rfloor\cdot\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)
.
For all
j\in I
, we define a subgraph
H_{j}
of
H
, whose vertex set is
T_{j}
, and edge set is
\hat{E}(T_{j})
. Clearly, the graphs in

are disjoint, and, for all

|V(H_{j})|=|T|\leq k
holds. Additionally, from the above discussion, for all
j\in I

. If, additionally,
\sum_{j\in I}|\hat{E}(T_{j})|\geq 0.1B
holds, then Event
{\cal{E}}^{g}
happens. As observed above,
\sum_{j\in I}|\hat{E}(T_{j})|\leq B
must hold. Let

\text{\bf E}\left[\sum_{j\in I}|\hat{E}(T_{j})|\right]\leq(1-p)\cdot 0.1\cdot B+p\cdot B=0.1B+0.9Bp.

Since, from

,
\text{\bf E}\left[\sum_{j\in I}|\hat{E}(T_{j})|\right]\geq 0.9B
, we conclude that

, and so
\text{\bf Pr}\left[{\cal{E}}^{g}\right]\geq 0.8
, as required. In order to complete the proof of
5.3
, it is now enough to prove

Proof of
5.4
.

We associate a collection
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}\}
of random variables with the set
I
of indices. In order to do so, we view set
I
as being constructed as follows. For each
1\leq i\leq r
, sample a value
X_{i}
uniformly at random from
[M]
, and then let
I=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}\}
be the collection of these sampled values.
Consider now any index
1\leq i\leq r
, the corresponding set
T^{\prime}_{X_{i}}
of vertices of
H
, and any edge
e=(v_{a},v_{b})\in E_{G}(S)
. Edge
e^{\prime}=(u_{f(a)+X_{i}},u_{f(b)+X_{i}})
of
H
corresponding to

belongs to set
\hat{E}(T_{X_{i}})
if and only if neither of the vertices
u_{f(a)+X_{i}}
,
u_{f(b)+X_{i}}
lies in
\bigcup_{j\in I\setminus\{i\}}T^{\prime}_{X_{j}}
. Consider now an index
j\in I\setminus\{i\}
. The probability that a fixed vertex
u\in V(H)
lies in

is at most
k/M
(since for every vertex

, there is a single index

). From the union bound, the probability that a fixed vertex
u\in V(H)
lies in
\bigcup_{j\in I\setminus\{i\}}T^{\prime}_{X_{j}}
is at most
\frac{r\cdot k}{M}\leq\frac{1}{\log k}
. In particular, the probability that any of the endpoints of edge
e^{\prime}=(u_{f(a)+X_{i}},u_{f(b)+X_{i}})
lies in

\bigcup_{j\in I\setminus\{i\}}T^{\prime}_{X_{j}}
is at most
\frac{2}{\log k}
. Therefore,
\text{\bf E}\left[|\hat{E}(T_{j})|\right]\geq|E_{G}(S)|\cdot\left(1-\frac{2}{\log k}\right)\geq 0.9\cdot|E_{G}(S)|
. Altogether, from the linearity of expectation,

\text{\bf E}\left[\sum_{1\leq i\leq r}|\hat{E}(T_{X_{i}})|\right]\geq 0.9\cdot|E_{G}(S)|\cdot r=0.9\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{M}{k\log k}\right\rfloor\cdot\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)
.

Ensemble and Bad Event
{\cal{E}}^{b}
.

Next, we define the notion of an ensemble. Recall that
q=\left\lceil\log N\right\rceil
. An ensemble
{\mathcal{B}}
consists of a collection
I\subseteq[N]
of at most
2q
indices, and, for every index
i\in I
, an integer
-q\leq x_{i}\leq q
with
x_{i}\neq 0
. We denote the ensemble by
{\mathcal{B}}=\{I,\{x_{i}\}_{i\in I}\}
. We say that ensemble
{\mathcal{B}}=\{I,\{x_{i}\}_{i\in I}\}
is
bad
if
\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\cdot f(i)\equiv 0\text{ }(\textnormal{mod }M)
. We let
{\cal{E}}^{b}
be the bad event that there exists a bad ensemble. We start by showing that the probability of Event
{\cal{E}}^{b}
happening is low. Later, we show that, if Event
{\cal{E}}^{b}
does not happen, then graph
H
has some useful properties.

Observation 5.5
.

\text{\bf Pr}\left[{\cal{E}}^{b}\right]\leq\frac{1}{N^{q}}
.

Proof:

Consider any fixed ensemble
{\mathcal{B}}=\{I,\{x_{i}\}_{i\in I}\}
. Let
i^{*}\in I
be any fixed index. Consider now the following two-step process: in the first step, we select the values
f(i)
for all indices
i\in I\setminus\{i^{*}\}
independently uniformly at random from
[M]
. We then denote
S=\sum_{i\in I\setminus\{i^{*}\}}x_{i}\cdot f(i)
. In the second step, we select the value
a=f(i^{*})
at random from
[M]
.
Ensemble
{\mathcal{B}}
is bad if and only if
a\cdot x_{i^{*}}+S=0\mod M
. Since
M
is a prime number, there is exactly one value
a^{\prime}\in[M]
with
a^{\prime}\cdot x_{i^{*}}+S=0\mod M
(indeed, if two such values
a^{\prime},a^{\prime\prime}
exist, then
a^{\prime}\cdot x_{i^{*}}=a^{\prime\prime}\cdot x_{i^{*}}\mod M
, implying that
a^{\prime}=a^{\prime\prime}
must hold). The probability to choose
f(i^{*})=a^{\prime}
is then
1/M
, and so the probability that a fixed ensemble
{\mathcal{B}}
is bad is
1/M
.

Notice that the total number of ensembles is bounded by
\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{2q}\binom{N}{t}\Big)\cdot(2q)^{2q}\leq(2q)\cdot\binom{N}{2q}\cdot(2q)^{2q}\leq N^{4q}
. Using the Union Bound,
\text{\bf Pr}\left[{\cal{E}}^{b}\right]\leq\frac{N^{4q}}{M}\leq\frac{1}{N^{q}}
, since
M\geq N^{5q}
.

Next, we show that, if Event
{\cal{E}}^{b}
does not happen, then every edge

has a unique origin edge in

Observation 5.6
.

Assume that Event

is an origin edge of

j\equiv f(i_{1})+t_{1}\text{ }(\textnormal{mod }M),\quad\mbox{and}\quad j^{\prime}\equiv f(i^{\prime}_{1})+t_{1}\text{ }(\textnormal{mod }M).

(5)

k
remains unchanged. Note that, if Event
{\cal{E}}
did not happen, then, from the definition of Events
{\cal{E}}
and
{\cal{E}}^{g}
, there is a collection
\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{r}\}
of
r=\left\lfloor\frac{M}{k\log k}\right\rfloor
disjoint subsets of vertices of
H
, such that for all
1\leq j\leq r
,
|V(H_{j})|\leq k
holds, and additionally,
\sum_{1\leq j\leq r}|E(H_{j})|\geq 0.1\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{M}{k\log k}\right\rfloor\cdot\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)
. We can then define a solution
(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n/k})
to instance
\mathrm{DkC}(H,k)
of the
Dense
k
-Coloring
problem, as follows. For all
1\leq i\leq r
, we initially set
S_{i}=V(H_{i})
, and for all
r<i\leq n/k
, we set
S_{i}=\emptyset
. Let
U=V(H)\setminus\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}V(H_{i})\right)
. Next, we partition the vertices of
U
by adding them to sets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n/r}
arbitrarily, to ensure that the cardinality of each set is exactly
k
. From the above discussion, if Event
{\cal{E}}
did not happen, then:

\sum_{i=1}^{n/k}|E_{H}(S_{i})|\geq\sum_{i=1}^{r}|E(H_{i})|\geq 0.1\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{M}{k\log k}\right\rfloor\cdot\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)\geq\Omega\left(\frac{n}{k\log k}\right)\cdot\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k).

We conclude that, if Event
{\cal{E}}
did not happen, then
\mathrm{OPT_{DkC}}(H,k)\geq\Omega\left(\frac{n}{k\log k}\right)\cdot\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)
.

We apply the
\alpha(n)
-approximation algorithm for
Dense
k
-Coloring
to instance
\mathrm{DkC}(H,k)
, and we denote the resulting solution by
(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n/k})
. Note that:

\sum_{i=1}^{n/k}|E_{H}(U_{i})|\geq\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{DkC}}(H,k)}{\alpha(n)}\geq\Omega\left(\frac{n}{k\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log k}\right)\cdot\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)

must hold.
We let
U\in\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{n/k}\}
be a subset maximizing
|E_{H}(U_{i})|
, so that

|E_{H}(U)|\geq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n/k}|E_{H}(U_{i})|}{n/k}\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)}{\alpha(n)\cdot\log k}\right).

Let
H^{\prime}=H[U]
, and let
W=R(H^{\prime})
be the origin graph of
H^{\prime}
.
From
5.8
, if Event
{\cal{E}}
did not happen, then
|V(W)|\leq c^{*}\cdot|V(H^{\prime})|\leq c^{*}k
, for some universal constant
c^{*}
.

Lastly, we apply the algorithm from
Lemma

2.3
to graph
W
, to obtain a subgraph
W^{\prime}
of
W
with
|V(W^{\prime})|=k
and
|E(W^{\prime})|\geq\Omega(|E(W)|)
. We then return
S=|V(W^{\prime})|
as the solution to the input instance
\mathrm{DkS}(G,k)
of the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem. From the above discussion,
|E_{G}(S)|\geq\Omega(|E(W)|)\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)}{\alpha(n)\cdot\log k}\right)\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)}{\alpha\left(N^{O(\log N)}\right)\cdot\log N}\right)
.
Therefore, if the event
{\cal{E}}
does not happen, we obtain an
O(\alpha(N^{O(\log N)})\cdot\log N)
-approximate solution to the input instance of the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem. Recall that the probability of Event
{\cal{E}}
happening is at most
0.1
. Lastly, since
|V(H)|\leq N^{O(\log N)}
, it is easy to verify that the running time of the algorithm is at most
N^{O(\log N)}
.

5.3
Completing the Reduction from
Densest
k
-Subgraph
to
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning

Let
\mathrm{DkS}(G,k)
be an input instance of the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem with
|V(G)|=N
.
Our algorithm requires the knowledge of an estimate
h
on the value of
\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)
, with
h/2\leq\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)\leq h
. In order to overcome this difficulty, we run the algorithm for every value of
h
that is an integral power of
2
between
1
and
|E(G)|
, and output the best resulting solution. Therefore, it is now enough to provide a randomized algorithm that, given an estimate
h
with
h/2\leq\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)\leq h
, with a constant probability produces a solution to instance
\mathrm{DkS}(G,k)
of
Densest
k
-Subgraph
whose value is at least

\Omega\left(\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)}{(\alpha(N^{O(\log N)}))^{3}\cdot\log^{2}N}\right)
, such that the running time of the algorithm is
N^{O(\log N)}
. From now on we assume that we are given an integer
h
with
h/2\leq\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)\leq h
.

As before, we denote
V(G)=\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{N-1}\}
, and we construct the auxiliary graph
H
from instance
\mathrm{DkS}(G,k)
of
Densest
k
-Subgraph
. We denote
|V(H)|=n
, so
n\leq N^{O(\log N)}
holds. We then consider instance

\mathrm{GP}(H,r,h)
of the
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
problem, where
r=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{k\log k}\right\rfloor
.

Note that, if Event
{\cal{E}}
did not happen, then, from the definition of Events
{\cal{E}}
and
{\cal{E}}^{g}
, there is a collection
\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{r}\}
of
r=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{k\log k}\right\rfloor
disjoint subgraphs of
H
, such that for all
1\leq j\leq r
,
|E(H_{j})|\leq\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)\leq h
holds, and

\sum_{1\leq j\leq r}|E(H_{j})|\geq 0.1\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{n}{k\log k}\right\rfloor\cdot\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)
.
Therefore, we obtain a solution
(H_{1},\ldots,H_{r})
to instance
\mathrm{GP}(H,r,h)
of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, whose value is at least
0.1\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{n}{k\log k}\right\rfloor\cdot\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)
.
We conclude that, if Event
{\cal{E}}
did not happen, then
\mathrm{OPT_{GP}}(H,r,h)\geq\Omega\left(\frac{n}{k\log k}\right)\cdot\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)
.

We apply the
\alpha(n)
-approximation algorithm to instance
\mathrm{GP}(H,r,h)
of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, obtaining a solution
(H^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,H^{\prime}_{r})
, whose value is at least
\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{GP}}(H,r,h)}{\alpha(n)}
.
Denote
{\mathcal{H}}=\{H^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,H^{\prime}_{r}\}
. We partition set
{\mathcal{H}}
into two subsets: set
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
containing all graphs
H^{\prime}_{j}\in{\mathcal{H}}
with
|V(H^{\prime})|\leq 100\cdot\alpha(n)k\log k
, and set
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime\prime}
containing all remaining graphs.

Assume for now that Event
{\cal{E}}
did not happen. Then, as observed above:

\sum_{H^{\prime}_{j}\in{\mathcal{H}}}|E(H^{\prime}_{j})|\geq\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{GP}}(H,r,h)}{\alpha(n)}\geq\frac{n\cdot\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)}{20k\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log k}.

Clearly,
|{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime\prime}|\leq\frac{n}{100\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot k\log k}
, and so:

\sum_{H^{\prime}_{j}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime\prime}}|E(H^{\prime}_{j})|\leq\frac{n\cdot h}{100\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot k\log k}\leq\frac{n\cdot\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)}{100\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot k\log k}.

Altogether, we get that, if Event
{\cal{E}}
did not happen, then:

\sum_{H^{\prime}_{j}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}}|E(H)|\geq\frac{n\cdot\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)}{50k\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log k}.

We let
H^{*}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
be the graph maximizing the number of edges. Since
|{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}|\leq|{\mathcal{H}}|=r=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{k\log k}\right\rfloor
, from the above discussion, if Event
{\cal{E}}
did not happen, then:

|E(H^{*})|\geq\frac{n\cdot\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)}{r\cdot 50k\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log k}\geq\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)}{50\alpha(n)}.

From the definition of the collection
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
of graphs,
|V(H^{*})|\leq 100\cdot\alpha(n)k\log k
.

Let
W=R(H^{*})
be the origin graph of
H^{*}
.
From
5.8
, if Event
{\cal{E}}
did not happen, then
|V(W)|\leq c^{*}\cdot|V(H^{*})|\leq O(\alpha(n)k\log k)
.

Lastly, we apply the algorithm from
Lemma

2.3
to graph
W
, to obtain a subgraph
W^{\prime}
of
W
with
|V(W^{\prime})|\leq k
and
|E(W^{\prime})|\geq\Omega\left(\frac{|E(W)|}{(\alpha(n))^{2}\cdot\log^{2}k}\right)
. We then return
S=|V(W^{\prime})|
as the solution to the input instance
\mathrm{DkS}(G,k)
of the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem. From the above discussion,

|V(S)|\leq k
, and, if Event
{\cal{E}}
did not happen, then:

\begin{split}|E_{G}(S)|&\geq\Omega\left(\frac{|E(W)|}{(\alpha(n))^{2}\cdot\log^{2}k}\right)\\
&\geq\Omega\left(\frac{|E(H^{*})|}{(\alpha(n))^{2}\cdot\log^{2}k}\right)\\
&\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)}{(\alpha(n))^{3}\cdot\log^{2}k}\right)\\
&\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\mathrm{OPT_{DkS}}(G,k)}{(\alpha(N^{O(\log N)}))^{3}\cdot\log^{2}N}\right).\end{split}

Therefore, if the event
{\cal{E}}
does not happen, we obtain an
O((\alpha(N^{O(\log N)}))^{3}\cdot\log^{2}N)
-approximate solution to the input instance of the
Densest
k
-Subgraph
problem. Recall that the probability of Event
{\cal{E}}
happening is at most
0.1
. Lastly, since
|V(H)|\leq N^{O(\log N)}
, it is easy to verify that the running time of the algorithm is at most
N^{O(\log N)}
.

6
Reductions between
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
and
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph

In this section we establish a connection between the
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
and
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
problems, by proving the following two theorems.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\alpha:\mathbb{Z^{+}}\to\mathbb{Z^{+}}
be an increasing function with
\alpha(n)=o(n)
. Assume that there exists an efficient
\alpha(n)
-approximation algorithm for the
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
problem, where
n
is the number of vertices in the input graph. Then there exists an efficient
O(\alpha(N)\cdot\textnormal{poly}\log N)
-approximation algorithm for
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
, where
N
is the number of vertices in the input instance of
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\alpha:\mathbb{Z^{+}}\to\mathbb{Z^{+}}
be an increasing function with
\alpha(n)=o(n)
. Assume that there exists an efficient
\alpha(N)
-approximation algorithm for the
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
problem, where
N
is the number of vertices in the input graph. Then there exists an efficient
O((\alpha(n))^{2}\cdot\textnormal{poly}\log n)
-approximation algorithm for
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, where
n
is the number of vertices in the input instance of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
.

By combining
Theorem

6.2
with
Corollary

5.2
, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 6.3
.

Assume that
2
holds and that
\mbox{\sf NP}\not\subseteq\mbox{\sf BPTIME}(n^{O(\log n)})
. Then for some constant
0<\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq 1/2
, there is no efficient
2^{(\log n)^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}
-approximation algorithm for
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
.

Proof:

Assume that
2
holds and that
\mbox{\sf NP}\not\subseteq\mbox{\sf DTIME}(n^{O(\log n)})
. Then, from
Corollary

5.2
, for some constant
0<\varepsilon\leq 1/2
, there is no efficient factor-
2^{(\log n)^{\varepsilon}}
-approximation algorithm for
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, where
n
is the number of vertices in the input graph.

We let
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/c
, where
c
is a sufficiently large constant. We now prove that there is no efficient
2^{(\log n)^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}
-approximation algorithm for
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
. Indeed, assume for contradiction that there is an efficient
2^{(\log n)^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}
-approximation algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
for
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
. From

Theorem

6.2
, there is an efficient
c^{\prime}\cdot(\alpha(N))^{2}\cdot\textnormal{poly}\log N
-approximation algorithm for
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, where
N
is the number of vertices in the input instance of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
,
c^{\prime}
is some constant, and
\alpha(N)=2^{(\log N)^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}
. Notice however that
c^{\prime}\cdot(\alpha(N))^{2}\cdot\textnormal{poly}\log N\leq 2^{(\log N)^{\varepsilon}}
holds, if the constant
c
is large enough.

Therefore, we obtain an efficient factor-
2^{(\log n)^{\varepsilon}}
-approximation algorithm for
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, a contradiction.

In the remainder of this section, we prove Theorems
6.2
and
5.2
.
We start by proving two auxiliary lemmas that will be used in the proofs of both theorems. We then complete the proofs of
Theorem

6.1
and
Theorem

6.2
in sections
Section

6.2
and
Section

6.3
, respectively.

6.1
Auxiliary Lemmas

We start with the following definition, that will be used in the proofs of both theorems.

Definition 6.4
.

Let
\mathrm{GP}(G,r,h)
be an instance of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, and let
\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{r}\}
be a solution to this instance. We say that this solution is
good
, if for all
1\leq i\leq r
,
h/2\leq|E(H_{i})|\leq h
.

We are now ready to state the first auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 6.5
.

There is an efficient algorithm, that, given a graph
G
with
|V(G)|=n
, integers
r,h>0
and any solution
{\mathcal{H}}
to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r,h)
of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, computes positive integers
r^{*}\leq r,h^{*}\leq h
and a subset
{\mathcal{H}}^{*}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
of subgraphs of
G
, such that
{\mathcal{H}}^{*}
is a good solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r^{*},h^{*})
, of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, and
\sum_{H\in{\mathcal{H}}^{*}}|E(H)|\geq\frac{\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}}|E(H^{\prime})|}{4\log n}
.

Proof:

The idea of the proof is to partition the graphs in
{\mathcal{H}}
geometrically into groups by the cardinalities of their edge sets, and then select a group maximizing the total number of edges in its subgraphs.

Specifically, let
q=\left\lceil 2\log n\right\rceil
. For all
1\leq i\leq q
, we let
{\mathcal{H}}_{i}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
contain all graphs
H
with
2^{i-1}\leq|E(H)|<2^{i}
. It is easy to verify that
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\ldots,{\mathcal{H}}_{q}
partition
{\mathcal{H}}
. Clearly, there must be an index
1\leq i^{*}\leq q
, with
\sum_{H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{i^{*}}}|E(H)|\geq\frac{\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}}|E(H^{\prime})|}{q}\geq\frac{\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}}|E(H^{\prime})|}{4\log n}
. We set
r^{*}=|{\mathcal{H}}_{i^{*}}|
,
h^{*}=2^{i^{*}}
, and we let
{\mathcal{H}}^{*}={\mathcal{H}}_{i^{*}}
. It is immediate to verify that
{\mathcal{H}}^{*}
is a good solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r^{*},h^{*})
, of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, and, from the above discussion,
\sum_{H\in{\mathcal{H}}^{*}}|E(H)|\geq\frac{\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}}|E(H^{\prime})|}{4\log n}
.

We are now ready to prove our second auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 6.6
.

There is an efficient algorithm, whose input consists of an instance
\mathrm{MBCS}(G,L)
of the
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
problem with
|V(G)|=N
, where
N
is greater than a sufficiently large constant, together with a solution
H
to this instance, such that
|E(H)|\geq 4N\log^{6}N
holds. The algorithm computes integers
r,h>0
, such that
r\cdot h^{2}\leq L\cdot\log^{6}N
and
r\cdot h\geq\Omega(|E(H)|/\log N)
hold, together with a good solution
\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{r}\}
to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r,h)
of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
.

Proof:

Let
\hat{G}
be any graph. A
cut
in
\hat{G}
is a partition
(A,B)
of vertices of
\hat{G}
into two non-empty subsets. The
value
of the cut
(A,B)
is
|E(A,B)|
. For a parameter
1/2<\beta<1
, we say that cut
(A,B)
is
\beta
-balanced
, if
|E(A)|,|E(B)|\leq\beta\cdot|E(\hat{G})|
. We say that cut
(A,B)
is a
minimum
\beta
-balanced cut
if it is a
\beta
-balanced cut whose value is the smallest among all such cuts. We use the following theorem that follows from the results of
[
ARV09
]
, and was formally proved in
[
CT22
]
.

Theorem 6.7

(Theorem 4.11 in the full version of
[
CT22
]
)
.

There is an efficient algorithm, that, given a graph
\hat{G}
with
|V(\hat{G})|=\hat{N}
, computes a
\gamma
-balanced cut in
\hat{G}
, whose value is at most
O(\sqrt{\log\hat{N}})
times the value of the minimum
(3/4)
-balanced cut in
\hat{G}
, for some universal constant
3/4<\gamma<1
that does not depend on
\hat{N}
.

We use the following theorem, that is a simple corollary of the Planar Separator Theorem by Lipton and Tarjan
[
LT79
]
, and was formally proved in
[
CT22
]
. A variation of this theorem for vertex-balanced cuts was proved in
[
PSS96
]
.

Theorem 6.8

(Lemma 4.12 in the full version of
[
CT22
]
)
.

Let
\hat{G}
be a connected graph with
m
edges and maximum vertex degree
\Delta<\frac{m}{2^{40}}
. If
\mathsf{CrN}(\hat{G})\leq\frac{m^{2}}{2^{40}}
, then the value of the minimum
(3/4)
-balanced cut in
\hat{G}
is at most
O\left(\sqrt{\mathsf{CrN}(\hat{G})+\Delta\cdot m}\right)
.

We are now ready to complete the proof of
Lemma

6.6
. Recall that we are given an instance
\mathrm{MBCS}(G,L)
of
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
, where
|V(G)|=N
, together with a solution
H
to this instance, such that
|E(H)|\geq 4N\log^{6}N
.

The algorithm starts by iteratively decomposing graph
H
into smaller subgraphs. Throughout the decomposition procedure, we maintain a collection
{\mathcal{H}}
of connected subgraphs of
H
, that are all mutually disjoint. Each graph
H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}
is marked as either
active
or
inactive
. At the beginning of the algorithm, we let
{\mathcal{H}}
contain all connected components of
H
, which are all marked as active. The algorithm performs iterations, as long as at least one graph in
{\mathcal{H}}
is inactive.

In order to execute an iteration, we select an arbitrary active graph
H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}
. We apply the algorithm from
Theorem

6.7
to compute a
\gamma
-balanced cut
(A,B)
of
H^{\prime}
. If
|E_{H^{\prime}}(A,B)|\geq\frac{|E(H^{\prime})|}{\log^{2}N}
, then we mark
H^{\prime}
as inactive and continue to the next iteration. Otherwise, we remove graph
H^{\prime}
from
{\mathcal{H}}
, and we add all connected components of graphs
H^{\prime}[A]
and
H^{\prime}[B]
to
{\mathcal{H}}
, that are all marked as active graphs. We then continue to the next iteration. This completes the description of the decomposition procedure. Let
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
be the collection
{\mathcal{H}}
of subgraphs of
H
that we obtain at the end of the procedure. We prove the following simple observation.

Observation 6.9
.

\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}}|E(H^{\prime})|\geq|E(H)|/2
.

Proof:

We use a charging scheme. We observe the set
{\mathcal{H}}
of graphs over the course of the partitioning procedure. Throughout the execution of the partitioning procedure, we denote by
E^{\prime}=E(H)\setminus\left(\bigcup_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}}E(H^{\prime})\right)
, and we call the edges of
E^{\prime}

deleted edges
. Over the course of the partitioning procedure we maintain, for every edge
e\in E(H)
, a non-negative value
c(e)
, that we refer to as the
charge
of
e
. We will ensure that, at every point of the algorithm’s execution,
\sum_{e\in E(H)}c(e)\geq|E^{\prime}|
, and that, for every edge
e\in E(H)
,
c(e)\leq 1/2
always holds. We note that, even when an edge
e
is added to the set
E^{\prime}
of deleted edges, its charge
c(e)
may remain strictly positive. It is then easy to verify that, at the end of the algorithm,
|E^{\prime}|\leq\sum_{e\in E(H)}c(e)\leq|E(H)|/2
holds, and so
\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}}|E(H^{\prime})|\geq|E(H)|/2
.

It now remains to describe the assignment of the charge values
c(e)
to the edges
e\in E(H)
, for which the above properties hold. Initially,
E^{\prime}=\emptyset
, and we set
c(e)=0
for every edge
e\in E(H)
.

Consider now some iteration of the algorithm, and assume that, at the beginning of the iteration,
\sum_{e\in E(H)}c(e)\geq|E^{\prime}|
holds. Let
H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}
be the graph that was processed in the current iteration, and let
(A,B)
be the cut in
H^{\prime}
that the algorithm computed. If
|E_{H^{\prime}}(A,B)|\geq\frac{|E(H^{\prime})|}{\log^{2}N}
, then no new edges were added to
E^{\prime}
in the current iteration, and the charge values
c(e)
remain unchanged for all edges
e\in E(H)
. Assume now that
|E_{H^{\prime}}(A,B)|<\frac{|E(H^{\prime})|}{\log^{2}N}
holds, and denote
E^{\prime\prime}=E_{H^{\prime}}(A,B)
. Then in the current iteration, the edges of
E^{\prime\prime}
were added to set
E^{\prime}
. We increase the charge
c(e)
of every edge
e\in E(H^{\prime})
by
\frac{|E^{\prime\prime}|}{|E(H^{\prime})|}
, and leave all other edge charges unchanged. This ensures that
\sum_{e\in E(H)}c(e)\geq|E^{\prime}|
holds at the end of the iteration. Since
|E^{\prime\prime}|<\frac{|E(H^{\prime})|}{\log^{2}N}
, for every edge
e\in E(H^{\prime})
, the charge
c(e)
increases by at most
\frac{1}{\log^{2}N}
in the current iteration.

From the above discussion, at the end of the algorithm,
|E^{\prime}|\leq\sum_{e\in E(H)}c(e)
holds. It now remains to show that for every edge
e\in E(H)
,
c(e)\leq 1/2

Consider any edge
e\in E(H)
, and denote by
H_{1},H_{2},\ldots,H_{r}
the sequence of subgraphs of
H
that belonged to
{\mathcal{H}}
over the course of the algorithm, and contained

H_{1}=H
, and, for all

H_{i}
was obtained via a balanced cut from graph

has increased in at most
r+1

\frac{1}{\log^{2}N}
. Furthermore, for all
1<i\leq r

holds, and so
r\leq O(\log N)
as

c(e)\leq\frac{r+1}{\log^{2}N}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, since we have assumed that
N
is sufficiently large.

Consider now the final collection
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
of graphs. We say that a graph
H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
is

iff
|E(H^{\prime})|\geq|V(H^{\prime})|\cdot\log^{6}N
, and otherwise we say it is
sparse
. We partition the set
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
of graphs into a collection
{\mathcal{H}}^{d}
containing all dense graphs and a collection
{\mathcal{H}}^{s}
containing all sparse graphs. We need the following simple observation.

Observation 6.10
.

\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{d}}|E(H^{\prime})|\geq\frac{|E(H)|}{4}\geq N\log^{6}N
.

Proof:

Assume for contradiction that
\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{d}}|E(H^{\prime})|<\frac{|E(H)|}{4}
. Since, from
6.9

\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}}|E(H^{\prime})|\geq|E(H)|/2
, we get that
\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}}|E(H^{\prime})|>\frac{|E(H)|}{4}
. However:

\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}}|E(H^{\prime})|\leq\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}}|V(H^{\prime})|\cdot\log^{6}N\leq N\log^{6}N.

We then conclude that
|E(H)|<4\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}}|E(H^{\prime})|\leq 4N\log^{6}N
, contradicting the statement of
Lemma

6.6
.

We also need the following obsevation.

Observation 6.11
.

Let
H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{d}
be a dense graph. Then
\mathsf{CrN}(H^{\prime})\geq\Omega\left(\frac{|E(H^{\prime})|^{2}}{\log^{5}N}\right)

We provide the proof of
6.11
below, after we complete the proof of
Lemma

6.6
using it. Let
r^{\prime}=|{\mathcal{H}}^{d}|
and
h^{\prime}=\max_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{d}}\{|E(H^{\prime})|\}
. Clearly, collection
{\mathcal{H}}^{d}
of graphs is a valid solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r^{\prime},h^{\prime})
of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
. We apply the algorithm from

6.5
to the soluton
{\mathcal{H}}^{d}
to instance

of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
. Recall that the algorithm computes positive integers
r\leq r^{\prime},h\leq h^{\prime}
, and a subset
{\mathcal{H}}^{*}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}^{d}
of subgraphs of
G
, such that
{\mathcal{H}}^{*}
is a good solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r,h)
, of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, and
\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{*}}|E(H^{\prime})|\geq\frac{\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{d}}|E(H^{\prime})|}{4\log N}
.
It now remains to verify that
r\cdot h^{2}\leq L\cdot\log^{6}N
and
r\cdot h\geq\Omega(|E(H)|/\log N)
hold.

Observe first that:

r\cdot h\geq\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{*}}|E(H^{\prime})|\geq\frac{\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{d}}|E(H^{\prime})|}{4\log N}\geq\frac{|E(H)|}{16\log N},

from
6.10

Finally, since for each graph
H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{d}

\mathsf{CrN}(H^{\prime})\geq\Omega\left(\frac{|E(H^{\prime})|^{2}}{\log^{5}N}\right)
holds, and since, for every graph

|E(H^{\prime})|\geq h/2
holds, we get that:

r\cdot h^{2}\leq\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{*}}4\cdot|E(H^{\prime})|^{2}\leq\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{*}}O(\log^{5}N)\cdot\mathsf{CrN}(H^{\prime})\leq O(\log^{5}N)\cdot\mathsf{CrN}(H)\leq L\cdot\log^{6}N,

since
N
is large enough.

In order to complete the proof of
Lemma

6.6

6.11

Proof of
6.11
.

Since graph
H^{\prime}
is marked inactive by the algorithm, the

-balanced cut of

Let

denote the maximum vertex degree in

\Delta\leq V(H^{\prime})
must hold. On the other hand, from the definition of a dense graph,

\Delta\leq|V(H^{\prime})|\leq\frac{|E(H^{\prime})|}{\log^{6}N}<\frac{|E(H^{\prime})|}{2^{40}}
, if

is sufficiently large.

Recall that, from
Theorem

6.8
, either
\mathsf{CrN}(H^{\prime})\geq\frac{|E(H^{\prime})|^{2}}{2^{40}}

(3/4)
-balanced cut in

is at most
\sqrt{\mathsf{CrN}(H^{\prime})+\Delta\cdot|E(H^{\prime})|}
. In the former case, we immediately get that
\mathsf{CrN}(H^{\prime})\geq\Omega\left(\frac{|E(H^{\prime})|^{2}}{\log^{5}N}\right)
. In the latter case, since the value of the minimum
(3/4)
-balanced cut in

is
\Omega\left(\frac{|E(H^{\prime})|}{\log^{2.5}N}\right)
, we get that

\sqrt{\mathsf{CrN}(H^{\prime})+\Delta\cdot|E(H^{\prime})|}\geq\Omega\left(\frac{|E(H^{\prime})|}{\log^{2.5}N}\right)
. Moreover, since

\Delta\leq\frac{|E(H^{\prime})|}{\log^{6}N}

\mathsf{CrN}(H^{\prime})\geq\Omega\left(\frac{|E(H^{\prime})|^{2}}{\log^{5}N}\right)-\Delta\cdot|E(H^{\prime})|\geq\Omega\left(\frac{|E(H^{\prime})|^{2}}{\log^{5}N}\right)-\frac{|E(H^{\prime})|^{2}}{\log^{6}N}\geq\Omega\left(\frac{|E(H^{\prime})|^{2}}{\log^{5}N}\right),

since
N
is sufficiently large.

\frac{C^{*}}{4\log n}
. Since we use an
\alpha(N)
-approximation algorithm for
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
, and
|V(G)|=n
, we get that
|E(H)|\geq\frac{C^{*}}{4\alpha(n)\log n}
.

If
|E(H)|<\frac{C^{*}}{4\alpha(n)\log n}
, then we terminate the algorithm and return an empty solution: in this case, we are guaranteed that either
C^{*}
, or
r^{\prime},h^{\prime}
are guessed incorrectly. Therefore, we assume from now on that

|E(H)|\geq\frac{C^{*}}{4\alpha(n)\log n}
holds. Note that, since in Case 1,
C^{*}\geq 16n\alpha(n)\log^{7}n
holds, we are guaranteed that
|E(H)|\geq 4n\log^{6}n
.

Next, we apply the algorithm from
Lemma

6.6
to instance
\mathrm{MBCS}(G,L)
of
Maximum Bounded-Crossing Subgraph
, to compute integers
r^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime\prime}>0
, such that
r^{\prime\prime}\cdot(h^{\prime\prime})^{2}\leq L\cdot\log^{6}n=r^{\prime}\cdot(h^{\prime})^{2}\cdot\log^{6}n
, and
r^{\prime\prime}\cdot h^{\prime\prime}\geq\Omega\left(\frac{|E(H)|}{\log n}\right)\geq\Omega\left(\frac{C^{*}}{\alpha(n)\log^{2}n}\right)
. The algorithm also computes a good solution
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime\prime})
of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
.
Note that, while the value of the solution
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime\prime})
is guaranteed to be close to
C^{*}
, we are only guaranted that
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
is a valid solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime\prime})
of the problem, and it may not be a valid solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r^{\prime},h^{\prime})
. In our next steps, we will either correctly established that at least one of
C^{*},r^{\prime},h^{\prime}
was not guessed correctly; or we will slightly modify
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
to obtain a valid solution to intance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r^{\prime},h^{\prime})
of

, whose value remains close to that of
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
.
We start with the following observation.

Observation 6.14
.

There is a large enough constant
c^{\prime}
, such that, if
C^{*}=\mathrm{OPT_{GP}}(G,r,h)
,
r^{\prime}=r^{*}
and
h^{\prime}=h^{*}
hold, then
h^{\prime\prime}\leq c^{\prime}h^{\prime}\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{8}n
.

Proof:

Recall that we have established that:

r^{\prime\prime}\cdot h^{\prime\prime}\geq\Omega\left(\frac{C^{*}}{\alpha(n)\log^{2}n}\right).

Assume for contradicton that
h^{\prime\prime}>c^{\prime}h^{\prime}\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{8}n
, where
c^{\prime}
is a large enough constant. Then:

r^{\prime\prime}\cdot(h^{\prime\prime})^{2}\geq 4h^{\prime}\cdot C^{*}\log^{6}n.

Notice that
C^{*}\geq r^{\prime}h^{\prime}/2
must hold. Indeed, since
r^{\prime}\leq r
and
h^{\prime}\leq h
, any solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r^{\prime},h^{\prime})
of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
is also a feasible solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r,h)
. Since we have assumed that
r^{\prime}=r^{*}
and
h^{\prime}=h^{*}
, there is a good solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r^{\prime},h^{\prime})
of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, and the value of any such good solution is at least
r^{\prime}h^{\prime}/2
. Since we have assumed that
C^{*}=\mathrm{OPT_{GP}}(G,r,h)
, we get that
r^{\prime}h^{\prime}/2\leq C^{*}
must hold.
We conclude that, if
h^{\prime\prime}>h^{\prime}\cdot\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{8}n
, then:

r^{\prime\prime}\cdot(h^{\prime\prime})^{2}\geq 2r^{\prime}(h^{\prime})^{2}\log^{6}n.

But we have already established above that
r^{\prime\prime}\cdot(h^{\prime\prime})^{2}\leq r^{\prime}\cdot(h^{\prime})^{2}\cdot\log^{6}n
, a contradiction.

We will now slightly modify the collection
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
of subgraphs of
G
to obtain a feasible solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r,h)
, whose value is close to the value of
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
. First, for every cluster
H\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
, if
|E(H)|>h^{\prime}/2
, then we discard arbitrary edges from graph
H
, until
|E(H)|=h^{\prime}/2
holds. From
6.14
, if
C^{*}=\mathrm{OPT_{GP}}(G,r,h)
,
r^{\prime}=r^{*}
and
h^{\prime}=h^{*}
hold, then the total number of edges in the graphs of
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
decreases by at most factor
O(\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{8}n)
as the result of this transformation, and so
\sum_{H\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}}|E(H)|\geq\Omega\left(\frac{r^{\prime\prime}\cdot h^{\prime\prime}}{\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{8}n}\right)\geq\Omega\left(\frac{C^{*}}{(\alpha(n))^{2}\log^{10}n}\right)
holds. Also, if, at the end of this transformation,
\sum_{H\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}}|E(H)|>\frac{h^{\prime}\cdot r^{\prime}}{2}
holds, then we discard arbitrary edges from the graphs in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
until
\sum_{H\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}}|E(H)|\leq\frac{h^{\prime}\cdot r^{\prime}}{2}
holds. Since
h^{\prime}r^{\prime}\geq\frac{C^{*}}{4\log n}
,
\sum_{H\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}}|E(H)|\geq\Omega\left(\frac{C^{*}}{(\alpha(n))^{2}\log^{10}n}\right)
continues to hold.

If
|{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}|\leq r^{\prime}
, then we have obtained a valid solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r^{\prime},h^{\prime})
of value
\Omega\left(\frac{C^{*}}{(\alpha(n))^{2}\log^{10}n}\right)
. Otherwise, we perform further transformations to the set
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
of graphs as follows.

While
|{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}|>r^{\prime}
, we let
H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
be a pair of graphs with smallest number of edges, breaking ties arbitrarily. We remove
H^{\prime}
and
H^{\prime\prime}
from
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
, and we add a new graph
H=H^{\prime}\cup H^{\prime\prime}
to
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
instead. The procedure is terminated once
|{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}|=r^{\prime}
holds. We claim that at the end of this procedure, for every graph
H\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
,
|E(H)|\leq h^{\prime}
holds. Indeed, assume otherwise. Consider the first time when a graph
H
with
|E(H)|>h^{\prime}
was added to
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
. Then
H=H^{\prime}\cup H^{\prime\prime}
must hold, where
H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime}
are two graphs that belonged to
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
prior to this iterations. Then at least one of these two graphs must contain more than
h^{\prime}/2
edges. From the choice of the graphs
H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime}
, and from the fact that
|{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}|>r^{\prime}
held at the beginning of the iteration, we get that, at the beginning of the iteration, there were at least
r^{\prime}
graphs
\tilde{H}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
with
|E(\tilde{H})|>h^{\prime}/2
. But then
\sum_{\tilde{H}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}}|E(\tilde{H})|>\frac{r^{\prime}h^{\prime}}{2}
held at the beginning of the iteration. Since the total number of edges contained in the graphs of
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
does not change over the course of the algorithm, we reach a contradiction, since we have ensured that, at the beginning of the algorithm,
\sum_{\tilde{H}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}}|E(\tilde{H})|\leq\frac{h^{\prime}\cdot r^{\prime}}{2}
held. We return the resulting collection
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
of subgraphs of
G
, which is guaranteed to be a feasible solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r^{\prime},h^{\prime})
, of value at least
\Omega\left(\frac{C^{*}}{(\alpha(n))^{2}\log^{10}n}\right)
.

6.3.2
Case 2:
C^{*}<16n\alpha(n)\log^{7}n

In this case, we start by computing a maximal subgraph
F
of
G
, such that
F
is a forest, and maximum vertex degree in
F
is at most
h
. Such a graph
F
can be computed via a simple greedy algorithm. We start with graph
F
containing the set
V(F)=V(G)
of vertices and no edges. We then consider the edges of
G
one by one. For each such edge
e\in E(G)
, if graph
F\cup\{e\}
remains a forest with maximum vertex degree at most
h
, then we add
e
to
F
. Once every edge of
G
is processed, we obtain the final graph
F
. Let
S
be the set of all vertices of
G
that are adjacent to at least one edge of
F
, and denote
|S|=n^{\prime}
.

We consider two subcases of Case 2. The first subcase, Case 2a happens if
|E(F)|\geq\frac{C^{*}}{64\alpha(n^{\prime})\cdot\log^{7}n^{\prime}}
. In this case, we use the following simple observation, that will allow us to decompose the forest
F
to obtain a solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r^{\prime},h^{\prime})
of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, whose value is close to
|E(F)|
.

Observation 6.15
.

There is an efficient algorithm, that, given a tree
T
with maximum vertex degree at most
\Delta
and
|E(T)|\geq\Delta/2
, computes a collection
\mathcal{T}
of vertex-disjoint subgraphs of
T
, such that for each subgraph
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
,
\frac{\Delta}{2}\leq|E(T^{\prime})|\leq\Delta
, and
\sum_{T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}|E(T^{\prime})|\geq\frac{|E(T)|}{2}
.

Proof:

We root the tree
T
in an arbitrary vertex
v
. Initially, we let
\mathcal{T}=\emptyset
. As long as
|E(T)|>\Delta
, we perform iterations. In every iteration, we consider an arbitrary vertex
u
in the current tree
T
, that is a non-leaf vertex, but all children of
u
are leaf vertices. Let
T^{\prime}
be the subtree of
T
rooted at vertex
u
. Note that
1\leq|E(T^{\prime})|\leq\Delta-1
. We add graph
T^{\prime}
to
\mathcal{T}
, and we delete all vertices of
T^{\prime}
from
T
. Note that, as the result of this iteration, the unique edge
e
connecting
u
to its parent-vertex in the tree
T
is deleted from
T
, and it does not belong to any graph in
\mathcal{T}
. We let
e^{\prime}\in E(T)
be an arbitrary edge (which must exist since
|E(T)|\geq 1
), and we say that
e^{\prime}
is
responsible
for the deletion of the edge
e
. We then continue to the next iteration. The algorithm terminates once
|E(T)|\leq\Delta
holds. We then add graph
T
to the collection
\mathcal{T}
and terminate the algorithm. It is immediate to verify that, for every graph
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
,
|E(T^{\prime})|\leq\Delta
holds. Moreover, if an edge
e
belonged to the original graph
T
, and it does not belong to
\bigcup_{T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}E(T^{\prime})
, then some edge of
\bigcup_{T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}E(T^{\prime})
is designated as being responsible for deleting
e
. It is easy to verify that every edge
e^{\prime}\in\bigcup_{T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}E(T^{\prime})
may be responsible for the deletion of at most one edge. Therefore, at the end of the algorithm,
\sum_{T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}|E(T^{\prime})|\geq|E(T)|/2
holds.

Initially, we construct a collection
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
of subgraphs of
F
as follows. For every tree
T
of the forest
F
, if
|V(T)|\leq h
, then we add
T
to
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
. Otherwise, we apply

6.15
to tree
T
with parameter
\Delta=h
, and add the graphs in the resulting collection
\mathcal{T}
to
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
. At the end of this algorithm, for every graph
H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
,
|E(H^{\prime})|\leq h
holds, and
\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}}|E(H^{\prime})|\geq\frac{|E(F)|}{2}\geq\frac{C^{*}}{128\alpha(n^{\prime})\cdot\log^{7}n^{\prime}}
. For every graph
H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
, if
|E(H^{\prime})|>h/2
, then we delete edges from
H^{\prime}
until
|E(H^{\prime})|\leq h/2
holds. Clearly, after this transformation,
\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}}|E(H^{\prime})|\geq\frac{C^{*}}{256\alpha(n^{\prime})\cdot\log^{7}n^{\prime}}\geq\Omega\left(\frac{C^{*}}{\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{7}n}\right)
holds. If
\sum_{H\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}}|E(H)|>hr/2
, then we discard arbitrary edges from the graphs in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
, until
\sum_{H\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}}|E(H)|=hr/2
holds. Since, if
C^{*}=\mathrm{OPT_{GP}}(G,r,h)
,
C^{*}\leq rh
, we are still guaranteed that
\sum_{H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}}|E(H^{\prime})|\geq\Omega\left(\frac{C^{*}}{\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{7}n}\right)
holds.

If
|{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}|\leq r
, then we have obtained a valid solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r,h)
of value
\Omega\left(\frac{C^{*}}{\alpha(n)\cdot\log^{7}n}\right)
. Otherwise,
we proceed exactly like in Case 1 in order to transform
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
into a valid solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r,h)
of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, without changing the total number of edges that lie in the graphs of
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
.
While
|{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}|>r
, we let
H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
be a pair of graphs with smallest number of edges, breaking ties arbitrarily. We remove
H^{\prime}
and
H^{\prime\prime}
from
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
, and we add a new graph
H=H^{\prime}\cup H^{\prime\prime}
to
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
instead. The procedure is terminated once
|{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}|=r
holds. We claim that at the end of this procedure, for every graph
H\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
,
|E(H)|\leq h
holds. Indeed, assume otherwise. Consider the first time when a graph
H
with
|E(H)|>h
was added to
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
. Then
H=H^{\prime}\cup H^{\prime\prime}
must hold, where
H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime}
are two graphs that belonged to
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
prior to this iterations. Then at least one of these two graphs must contain more than
h/2
edges. From the choice of the graphs
H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime}
, and from the fact that
|{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}|>r
held at the beginning of the iteration, we get that, at the beginning of the iteration, there were at least
r
graphs
\tilde{H}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
with
|E(\tilde{H})|>h/2
. But then
\sum_{\tilde{H}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}}|E(\tilde{H})|>\frac{rh}{2}
held at the beginning of the iteration. Since the total number of edges contained in the graphs of
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
does not change over the course of the algorithm, we reach a contradiction, since we have ensured that, at the beginning of the algorithm,
\sum_{\tilde{H}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}}|E(\tilde{H})|\leq\frac{h\cdot r}{2}
held. We return the resulting set
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
of subgraphs of
G
, which is guaranteed to be a feasible solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r,h)
of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, of value at least
\Omega\left(\frac{C^{*}}{\alpha(n)\log^{7}n}\right)
.

It now remains to consider Case (2b), where
C^{*}<16n\alpha(n)\log^{7}n
and
|E(F)|<\frac{C^{*}}{64\alpha(n^{\prime})\cdot\log^{7}n^{\prime}}
. Recall that
S
is the set of all vertices of
G
that are adjacent to at least one edge of
F
, and recall that we have denoted
|S|=n^{\prime}
.

In this case, we let
G^{\prime}=G[S]
, and we consider instance
\mathrm{GP}(G^{\prime},r,h)
of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
. Notice that, if
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
is a valid solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G^{\prime},r,h)
of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
, then it is also a valid solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r,h)
of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
. We start by showing that
\mathrm{OPT_{GP}}(G^{\prime},r,h)
is close to
C^{*}
.

Observation 6.16
.

If
C^{*}=\mathrm{OPT_{GP}}(G,r,h)
, and Case (2b) happens, then
\mathrm{OPT_{GP}}(G^{\prime},r,h)\geq\frac{C^{*}}{2}
.

Proof:

Let
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
be the set of all vertices whose degree in
F
is
h
, and let
S^{*}
the set of all vertices of
F
that are isolated.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be the optimal solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r,h)
, and let
E^{\prime}=\bigcup_{H\in{\mathcal{H}}}E(H)
. We partition the set
E^{\prime}
of edges into two subsets: set
E^{\prime}_{1}
containing all edges that lie in
G^{\prime}=G[S]
, and set
E^{\prime}_{2}
containing all remaining edges. Clearly, for every edge
e=(x,y)\in E^{\prime}_{2}
, at least one endpoint of
e
must lie in
S^{*}
. Assume w.l.o.g. that
x\in S^{*}
. We claim that
y\in S^{\prime}
must hold. Indeed, otherwise
F\cup\{e\}
remains a forest, with maximum vertex degree at most
h
, contradicting the fact that
F
is a maximal subgraph of
G
with these properties.

Therefore, very edge of
E^{\prime}_{2}
connects a vertex of
S^{*}
to a vertex of
S^{\prime}
. We claim that
|E^{\prime}_{2}|\leq|S^{\prime}|\cdot h
. Indeed, from the definition of the
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
problem, for every graph
H\in{\mathcal{H}}
,
|E(H)|\leq h
, and all graphs in
{\mathcal{H}}
are disjoint in their vertices. Therefore, every vertex of
G
may be incident to at most
h
edges of
E^{\prime}
. Since every edge of
E^{\prime}_{2}
has a vertex of
S^{\prime}
as its endpoint, we get that
|E^{\prime}_{2}|\leq|S^{\prime}|\cdot h
.

Recall that, from our definition, every vertex
v\in S^{\prime}
has degree
h
in
F
. Therefore,
|E(F)|\geq(h-1)\cdot|S^{\prime}|
. We conclude that
|E(F)|\geq|E^{\prime}_{2}|/2
, and so
|E^{\prime}_{2}|\leq 2|E(F)|<\frac{C^{*}}{4}
. Since
|E^{\prime}|=C^{*}
, we get that
|E^{\prime}_{1}|\geq C^{*}/2
.

We now define a solution
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G^{\prime},h,r)
of
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
. For every graph
H\in{\mathcal{H}}
, we let
H^{\prime}
be a graph that is obtained from
H
by deleting all vertices of
S^{*}
from it, and we let
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}=\{H^{\prime}\mid H\in{\mathcal{H}}\}
. It is easy to verify that
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
is a valid solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G^{\prime},r,h)
, and that its value is at least
|E^{\prime}\setminus E^{\prime}_{2}|\geq\frac{C^{*}}{2}
. We conclude that
\mathrm{OPT_{GP}}(G^{\prime},r,h)\geq\frac{C^{*}}{2}
.

Denote
C^{\prime}=\mathrm{OPT_{GP}}(G^{\prime},r,h)
. From the above discussion
\frac{C^{*}}{2}\leq C^{\prime}\leq C^{*}
. Notice that for every tree
T
of
F
, if
T
is not a singleton vertex, then
|E(T)|\geq|V(T)|-1\geq\frac{|V(T)|}{2}
. Therefore,
|E(F)|\geq\frac{|S|}{2}=\frac{n^{\prime}}{2}
. On the other hand, from our assumpution,
|E(F)|<\frac{C^{*}}{64\alpha(n^{\prime})\cdot\log^{7}n^{\prime}}
. We then conclude that
C^{*}>32n^{\prime}\alpha(n^{\prime})\cdot\log^{7}n
.

We will now focus on solving instance
\mathrm{GP}(G^{\prime},r,h)
of the
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
problem. As before, we will try all guesses
C^{**}
on the value
C^{\prime}
of the optimal solution for this problem. Note that we only need to consider values
C^{**}
that are integers, with
\frac{C^{*}}{2}\leq C^{**}\leq C^{*}
. Furthermore, from the above discussion, for each such guess,
C^{**}\geq\frac{C^{*}}{2}\geq 16n^{\prime}\alpha(n^{\prime})\cdot\log^{7}n
holds, so Case 1 will occur. We execute the algorithm from Case 1 for each such guessed value
C^{**}
and output the best among the resulting solutions. We are then guaranteed to obtain a solution
{\mathcal{H}}
to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G^{\prime},r,h)
of value at least
\Omega\left(\frac{C^{\prime}}{(\alpha(n))^{2}\log^{10}n}\right)\geq\Omega\left(\frac{C^{*}}{(\alpha(n))^{2}\log^{10}n}\right)
. Clearly,
{\mathcal{H}}
is also a valid solution to instance
\mathrm{GP}(G,r,h)
. Overall, we obtain an efficient
O((\alpha(n))^{2}\cdot\textnormal{poly}\log n)
-approximation algorithm for
(r,h)-Graph Partitioning
.
