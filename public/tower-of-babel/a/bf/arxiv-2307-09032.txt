Isotonic conditional laws
Sebastian Arnold, Johanna Ziegel

Abstract
We introduce isotonic conditional laws (ICL) which extend the classical notion of conditional laws by the additional requirement that there exists an isotonic relationship between the random variable of interest and the conditioning random object. We show existence and uniqueness of ICL building on conditional expectations given σ \sigma -lattices. ICL corresponds to a classical conditional law if and only if the latter is already isotonic. ICL is motivated from a statistical point of view by showing that ICL emerges equivalently as the minimizer of an expected score where the scoring rule may be taken from a large class comprising the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS). Furthermore, ICL is calibrated in the sense that it is invariant to certain conditioning operations, and the corresponding event probabilities and quantiles are simultaneously optimal with respect to all relevant scoring functions. We develop a new notion of general conditional functionals given σ \sigma -lattices which is of independent interest.



Keywords:
Conditional law, isotonicity,
\sigma
-lattices, conditional functional, calibration.

1
Introduction

For a generic random vector
(Y,\xi)
defined on some probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, the conditional law of
Y
given
\xi
is the distribution of
Y
given ”all information about
\xi
”. Typically, the information carried by
\xi
is encoded by the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\xi
. In some situations such as isotonic regression, it is reasonable to assume that there exists an
isotonic
relationship between the random element
\xi
and the random variable
Y
in the sense that large (small) realizations of
\xi
make it more likely that
Y
will be large (small) as well. Evidently, in order to be able to speak about smaller and larger realizations, we have to assume that the random element
\xi
takes values in some measurable space
\mathcal{X}
which is equipped with a partial order
\preceq
. Throughout the article, we assume that the quantity of main interest,
Y
, is a real-valued random variable. We show that there exists a unique
isotonic conditional law (ICL) of
Y
given
\xi
, which takes the form of a Markov kernel and describes the best available explanation of the distribution of
Y
given all information of
\xi
under the assumption of isotonicity between
Y
and
\xi
.

While classical conditional distributions are constructed using conditional expectations given
\sigma
-algebras, our theory builds on conditional expectations given
\sigma
-lattices, which were first examined by
Brunk 1961
. A family of sets
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
is called a
\sigma
-lattice
if it contains
\Omega
, the empty set, and is closed under taking countable unions and intersections. In particular, any
\sigma
-algebra is a
\sigma
-lattice. In the Hilbert space of square integrable random variables, the family of all random variables which are measurable with respect to some
\sigma
-lattice
\mathcal{A}
form a closed convex cone, and the conditional expectation of any random variable
X
with respect to
\mathcal{A}
, for short
\mathbb{E}(X\mid\mathcal{A})
, is defined as the orthogonal projection onto this closed convex cone
(
Brunk 1965
)
. Conditional expectations given
\sigma
-lattices are strongly connected to isotonicity which will be illustrated in detail in Section
3
. In particular, the conditional expectation factorizes with respect to an increasing measurable function, that is, there exists an increasing measurable function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mathscr{L}(\xi))=f(\xi)\quad\textrm{almost surely},

where
\mathscr{L}(\xi)\subseteq\mathcal{F}
denotes the
\sigma
-lattice generated by
\xi
, see Section
3.1
. This result is analogous to the well-known factorization result for classical conditional expectations given
\sigma
-algebras
(
Bauer 2001
, Lemma 11.7)
, with the difference that, additionally,
f
has to be increasing. While the factorization result for conditional expectations given
\sigma
-lattices is already known in case of
\xi
being a random variable, see e.g.
Lee 1975
, we extend the result to more general random elements in Section
3.2
. Moreover, we will show that the isotonic conditional law of
Y
given
\xi
coincides with the conditional law of
Y
given
\xi
if and only if the latter is already isotonic in
\xi
.

For a random variable
Y
and a
\sigma
-lattice
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, we show in Theorem
4.2
that the ICL
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}
minimizes the expected score
\mathbb{E}{\rm S}(F,Y
) over all random distributions
F
which are isotonic, that is,
\mathcal{A}
-measurable, and where the scoring rule
{\rm S}
may be chosen from a class of proper scoring rules including the prominent continuous ranked probability score (CRPS;
Matheson and Winkler 1976
;
Gneiting and Raftery 2007
). This universal minimizing property of ICL shows that ICL can be interpreted as the theoretically optimal prediction for the distribution of
Y
given all information contained in
\mathcal{A}
, or, as the theoretically optimal prediction for the distribution of
Y
given all information of
\xi
under the assumption of isotonicity between
Y
and
\xi
if the
\sigma
-lattice
\mathcal{A}
is generated by the random element
\xi
. Moreover, in Section
4.2
, we show that ICL is the population version of isotonic distributional regression (IDR) as proposed by
Henzi et al. 2021
, see also
El Barmi and Mukerjee 2005
;
Mösching and Dümbgen 2020
. IDR is a powerful nonparametric distributional regression method under an isotonicity constraint that has found several applications in the statistical literature, see e.g.
Schulz and Lerch 2022
,
Henzi and Ziegel 2021
,
Henzi et al. 2023
.

A probabilistic forecast for
Y
is a (random) predictive distribution
F
that aims to be as close as possible to the conditional law of
Y
given the information at the time of forecasting. Calibration is a key requirement for probabilistic forecasts. Roughly speaking, probabilistic forecasts are
calibrated
(or
reliable
) if they are statistically compatible with the observations
(
Gneiting and Katzfuss 2014
)
. Numerous different notions of calibration are found in the literature, see e.g.
Dawid 1984
and
Diebold et al. 1998
for probabilistic calibration,
Strähl and Ziegel 2017
and
Arnold et al. 2023
for conditional and sequential notions of calibration,
Thorarinsdottir et al. 2016
and
Ziegel and Gneiting 2014
for multivariate notions of calibration, and
Guo et al. 2017
and
Gupta et al. 2022
for applications in machine learning. A recent extensive study of the logical dependencies between the main notions of calibration is given in
Gneiting and Resin 2022
.

Analogously to IDR, one can show that ICL automatically has certain calibration properties. We introduce the new notion of
isotonic calibration
which is satisfied by ICL, and show that isotonic calibration is weaker than auto-calibration and stronger than threshold- or quantile-calibration, see Section
5
. The calibration properties of ICL can alternatively be viewed as invariance properties of ICL under certain conditioning operations. Such invariance properties are well-known for classical conditional laws given
\sigma
-algebras.

IDR has the interesting property that it simultaneously fits optimal increasing quantile curves, and optimal increasing event probabilities. We show that the same property carries over to ICL, that is, for each threshold
z\in\mathbb{R}
, or quantile level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variables
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}(z)
and
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(\alpha)
are simultaneously optimal with respect to all relevant scoring functions for the mean, or quantile functional, respectively, see Theorem
4.2
. The difficulty is here that it is not obvious how one should naturally define conditional functionals, such as quantiles, given
\sigma
-lattices.

Fissler and Holzmann 2022
recently studied measurability conditions for compositions of Markov kernels and statistical functionals. An alternative approach lies in studying conditional functionals directly. Whereas conditional expectations are broadly studied and developed, the theoretical literature about conditional quantiles is by not as extensive. For a random variable
Y
and a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
,
Tomkins 1975
defines the conditional median of
Y
given
\mathcal{B}
as any
\mathcal{B}
-measurable random variable
X
which satisfies
\mathbb{P}(Y>X\mid\mathcal{B})\leq 1/2\leq\mathbb{P}(Y\geq X\mid\mathcal{B})
. Clearly, this definition may be extended to any quantile
but this approach does not readily generalize to
\sigma
-lattices. Instead, in Section
6
, we define conditional functionals based on identification functions as minimizers of expected scoring functions building on the work of
Jordan et al. 2022
. This approach is closely connected to the approach given by
Brunk and Johansen 1970
, where the authors characterize conditional expectations as generalized Radon-Nikodym derivatives but do not apply their considerations to quantiles. We apply our results on conditional functionals given
\sigma
-lattices to show the universality properties of ICL but the proposed construction is of independent interest.

Except for Theorem
4.2
and Proposition
5.2
, all proofs are collected in the appendix.

2
Existence and uniqueness of ICL

Throughout the article, we fix a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
be a
\sigma
-lattice
, that is
\mathcal{A}
is closed under countable intersections and unions and
\emptyset,\Omega\in\mathcal{A}
. A random variable
X
is called
\mathcal{A}
-measurable
if
\{X>x\}\in\mathcal{A}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Denote by
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
the family of all complements of elements in
\mathcal{A}
. It is straightforward to verify that
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
is also a
\sigma
-lattice. Let
L_{1}
be the space of all integrable random variables,
L_{1}(\mathcal{A})
the family of all
\mathcal{A}
-measurable integrable random variables, and
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbb{R}
. For a random variable
X
, we denote by
\sigma(X)
the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
X
, i.e.
\sigma(X)=\{X^{-1}(B)\mid B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\}
. Following
Brunk 1963
, we define:

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
be a
\sigma
-lattice. For any
Y\in L_{1}
, we call
X\in L_{1}(\mathcal{A})
a
conditional expectation of
Y
given
\mathcal{A}
, for short
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mathcal{A})
, if it satisfies

\mathbb{E}(Y\mathbbm{1}_{A})\leq\mathbb{E}(X\mathbbm{1}_{A})\quad\textrm{for all }A\in\mathcal{A}

(1)

and

\mathbb{E}(X\mathbbm{1}_{B})=\mathbb{E}(Y\mathbbm{1}_{B})\quad\textrm{for all }B\in\sigma(X).

(2)

Brunk 1963
shows existence and uniqueness of conditional expectations given
\sigma
-lattices (see Theorems 1 and 2 therein). Hence, we will denote by
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mathcal{A})
any
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variable
X
that satisfies (
1
) and (
2
) and know that
X=\tilde{X}
almost surely for any other random variable
\tilde{X}
with these properties. We call a conditional expectation given a
\sigma
-lattice sometimes simply
isotonic
conditional expectation and abbreviate
\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{B}\mid\mathcal{A})
by
\mathbb{P}(B\mid\mathcal{A})
for
B\in\mathcal{F}
.

Example 2.1
.

This example shows that the isotonic conditional expectation of a random variable on a finite and totally ordered probability space coincides with the solution to the isotonic regression problem for the mean functional (
Ayer et al. 1955
;
Barlow et al. 1972
).

Let
\Omega=\{1,2,3\}
be endowed with the power set
\mathcal{F}=2^{\Omega}
and
\mathbb{P}(A)=\#A/3
for
A\in\mathcal{F}
. Consider the
\sigma
-lattice
\mathcal{A}=\{\emptyset,\Omega,\{2,3\},\{3\}\}
and the random variable
Y(i)=y_{i}
, for
i=1,2,3
with
y_{2}<y_{1}<y_{3}
. Then, the conditional expectation
X=\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mathcal{A})
is given by
X(1)=X(2)=(y_{1}+y_{2})/2
and
X(3)=y_{3}
.

Some properties of classical conditional expectations do not hold any longer if we consider conditional expectations given
\sigma
-lattices rather than
\sigma
-algebras. In particular, we do not have linearity, the tower property fails to hold and
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variables can not be pulled out of the conditional expectation. Explicit counterexamples can be found e.g. in
Lee 1975
or
Arnold 2020
. For a comprehensive study of sufficient and necessary conditions for the conditional expectation to be linear see
Kuenzi 1969
.
Brunk 1965
shows that
\mathbb{E}(X\mid\mathcal{A})
is square integrable for any square integrable random variable
X
and defines
\mathbb{E}(X\mid\mathcal{A})
in this case as the projection of
X
onto the closed convex cone of
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variables in the space
L_{2}
of square integrable random variables with the inner product
\langle W,Z\rangle=\mathbb{E}(WZ)
for
W,Z\in L_{2}
. For square integrable random variables, there exists a nice geometrical interpretation why linearity and the tower property are violated as illustrated in Figure
1
. Moreover, the interpretation of isotonic conditional expectations as projections also illustrates why we do not require equality in (
1
) but rather only impose that
\mathbb{E}((Y-X)Z)\leq 0
for all
Z\in L_{2}(\mathcal{A})
(which is equivalent to condition (
1
)): Whereas
(Y-X)
and
Z
are orthogonal for any
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variable
Z
for classical conditional expectations (i.e.
\mathcal{A}
is a
\sigma
-algebra), the angle between
(Y-X)
and
Z
only has to be obtuse for any
Z\in L_{2}(\mathcal{A})
in the case of
\mathcal{A}
being a
\sigma
-lattice.

L_{2}(\mathcal{A})

\mathbb{E}(Y_{1}\mid\mathcal{A})+\mathbb{E}(Y_{2}\mid\mathcal{A})=X\neq Y=\mathbb{E}(Y_{1}+Y_{2}\mid\mathcal{A})

, the families

.

In this article, we study isotonic conditional laws, that is conditional laws given
\sigma
-lattices. Our first main result is the following theorem, which guarantees existence and uniqueness. Recall that a
Markov kernel
(also called a
stochastic kernel
or a
probability kernel
) from
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
to
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))
is a map
k:\Omega\times\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\to[0,1]
such that

(i)

for each
\omega\in\Omega
, the map
B\mapsto k(\omega,B)
,
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
, is a probability measure on
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))
,

(ii)

for each
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
, the map
\omega\mapsto k(\omega,B)
,
\omega\in\Omega
, is
\mathcal{F}
-
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
-measurable.

Theorem 2.1

(Existence and uniqueness of ICL)
.

Let
Y
be a random variable and
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
be a
\sigma
-lattice. Then there exists a conditional law of
Y
given
\mathcal{A}
, denoted by
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}
. More precisely, there exists a Markov kernel
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}
from
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
into
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))
such that
\omega\mapsto P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}(\omega,(y,\infty))
is a version of
\mathbb{P}(Y>y\mid\mathcal{A})
for any
y\in\mathbb{R}
. For any other Markov kernel
Q
with this property, it holds that
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}(\cdot,B)=Q(\cdot,B)

\mathbb{P}
-almost surely for all
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
.

We refer to the conditional law of
Y
given
\mathcal{A}
as the
isotonic conditional law (ICL) of
Y
given
\mathcal{A}
. By establishing the notion of a
\sigma
-lattice that is generated by some random element
\xi
it becomes clear why we refer to ICL as the
isotonic
conditional law of
Y
given
\xi
. Theorem
2.1
is analogous to the well-known result about existence and uniqueness of conditional laws given
\sigma
-algebras and is shown in a similar way, see e.g.
Bauer 1996
. For this reason we only give a sketch of proof in Appendix
A
and refer to the Master’s thesis by
Arnold 2020
for a detailed proof.

Remark 1
.

We only require
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}(\cdot,(a,\infty))=\mathbb{P}(Y>a\mid\mathcal{A})
for all
a\in\mathbb{R}
and not
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}(\cdot,B)=\mathbb{P}(Y\in B\mid\mathcal{A})
for any
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
for the following reason: For any
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
, we have
\mathbb{P}(Y\in B^{c}\mid\mathcal{A})=1-\mathbb{P}(Y\in B\mid\overline{\mathcal{A}})
by the general properties of isotonic conditional expectations, see e.g.
Brunk 1963
. If we imposed the stronger condition on
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}
, then also
\mathbb{P}(Y\in B^{c}\mid\mathcal{A})=1-\mathbb{P}(Y\in B\mid\mathcal{A})
since
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}(\cdot,B)=1-P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}(\cdot,B^{c})
almost surely for any
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
. This would imply
\mathbb{P}(Y\in B\mid\mathcal{A})=\mathbb{P}(Y\in B\mid\overline{\mathcal{A}})
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
which holds if and only if
\mathcal{A}
is a
\sigma
-algebra as explained by
Arnold 2020
.

3
Factorization and connection to classical conditional laws

3.1
Generated
\sigma
-lattices and measurability for random elements

We develop a notion of generated
\sigma
-lattices for random elements.
Our primary interest lies in random vectors
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and random cdfs
F\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
, but we will study more general random elements in the next section and illustrate in Examples
3.1
and
3.2
the connection to these two special cases.

Here,
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
denotes the set of all probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
. We treat elements of
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
interchangeably as probability measures or cumulative distribution functions (cdfs) and let
P
and
Q
be generic distributions and
F
and
G
be generic cdfs. For a cdf
F
, we denote the corresponding survival function (sf) by
\bar{F}
, i.e.
\bar{F}(x)=1-F(x)
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

For a nonempty set
\mathcal{X}
equipped with a partial order
\preceq
and a metric
d
, a triple
(\mathcal{X},d,\preceq)
is called an
ordered metric space
, see
Richmond 2020
for a comprehensive study of ordered metric spaces. Let
(\mathcal{X},d,\preceq)
be an ordered metric space, and denote the power set of
\mathcal{X}
by
2^{\mathcal{X}}
.
An arbitrary intersection of
\sigma
-lattices is again a
\sigma
-lattice. Hence, for any family of sets
\mathcal{E}\subseteq 2^{\mathcal{X}}
, we can define the
\sigma
-lattice generated by
\mathcal{E}
as the smallest
\sigma
-lattice that contains
\mathcal{E}
and denote it by
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{E})
. Let
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
denote the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathcal{X}
, that is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by all open sets and call
B\subseteq\mathcal{X}
an
upper set
if
x\in B
and
x\preceq y
implies
y\in B
. We denote by
\mathscr{U}
the family of all upper sets and leave it to the reader to show that
\mathscr{U}
is a
\sigma
-lattice.

Example 3.1

(Euclidean space)
.

Consider
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
with the usual
componentwise order

\boldsymbol{a}\leq\boldsymbol{b}
if
a_{i}\leq b_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq p
and the
Euclidean metric

d(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})=\lVert\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{b}\rVert
for
\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{i})_{i=1}^{p},\boldsymbol{b}=(b_{i})_{i=1}^{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

Example 3.2

(The space
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
)
.

We equip
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
with the stochastic order and the topology of weak convergence. Recall that for
F,G\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
we say that
F
is stochastically smaller or equal than
G
, for short
F\leq_{st}G
, if
F(x)\geq G(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Recall further that the topology of weak convergence can be metrisized by the
Lévy metric

d(F,G)=\inf\hskip 0.56905pt\big\{\epsilon>0\mid G(x-\epsilon)-\epsilon\leq F(x)\leq G(x+\epsilon)+\epsilon\textrm{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}\big\},\quad F,G\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}).

Let
(\mathcal{X}^{\prime},d^{\prime},\preceq^{\prime})
be a further nonempty ordered metric space and let
\mathcal{M}\subseteq 2^{\mathcal{X}},\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\subseteq 2^{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}
be
\sigma
-lattices. We call a function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}^{\prime}

isotonic
if
x\preceq y
implies
f(x)\preceq^{\prime}f(y)
, and
\mathcal{M}-\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
-
measurable
if
f^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal{M}
for all
B\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
. It is straightforward to check that
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
is increasing if and only if it is
\mathscr{U}-\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
-measurable, where
\mathscr{U}
and
\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
denote the family of all upper sets in
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
respectively. This key observation explains the close relation between
\sigma
-lattices and isotonicity. Since
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is Borel measurable and increasing if and only if
f^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
and
\{f>a\}\in\mathscr{U}
for all
a\in\mathbb{R}
, we propose the following definition.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\xi
be a random element in
(\mathcal{X},d,\preceq)
and let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
be a
\sigma
-lattice.

1.

\xi
is called
\mathcal{A}
-measurable
if
\xi^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal{A}
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\cap\mathscr{U}
.

2.

We define the
\sigma
-lattice generated by
\xi
as the family of all preimages of measurable upper sets in
\mathcal{X}
under
\xi
and denote it by
\mathscr{L}(\xi)
, that is

\mathscr{L}(\xi)=\big\{\xi^{-1}(B)\mid B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\cap\mathscr{U}\big\}.

Clearly,
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\cap\mathscr{U}
as the intersection of a
\sigma
-algebra with a
\sigma
-lattice is itself a
\sigma
-lattice. Thus saying that a random element
\xi
is
\mathcal{A}
-measurable is short for saying that
\xi
is
\mathcal{A}-(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\cap\mathscr{U})
-measurable.
Moreover,
\xi
is
\mathcal{A}
-measurable if and only if
\mathscr{L}(\xi)\subseteq\mathcal{A}
.

Definition
3.1
is consistent with the classical definition, where we call a random variable
X

\mathcal{A}
-measurable if
\{X>a\}\in\mathcal{A}
for all
a\in\mathbb{R}
. As it is usual convention to call a random variable
X
measurable with respect to the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}
if
X
is
\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
-measurable, we say that
X
is
\mathcal{A}
-measurable to abbreviate that
X
is
\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{R}}
-measurable, where
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{R}}
denotes the standard lattice on
\mathbb{R}
, that is the
\sigma
-lattice generated by all open upper sets in
\mathbb{R}
, i.e.
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{E})
for
\mathcal{E}=\{(a,\infty)\mid a\in\mathbb{R}\}
. Since any upper set in
\mathbb{R}
is of the form
(a,\infty)
or
[a,\infty)
for
a\in\mathbb{R}
, it follows directly that
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{R}}
is equal to the
\sigma
-lattice of all measurable upper sets in
\mathbb{R}
. Finally, it may be verified that the random variable
X
is
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{R}}
-measurable if and only if
X^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal{A}
for any
B\in\mathcal{E}
. The smallest
\sigma
-lattice
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
such that
X
is
\mathcal{M}
-measurable is
\mathscr{L}(X)
, see for example
Lee 1975
. Equivalently, it is the smallest
\sigma
-lattice containing
\{X>a\}
for all
a\in\mathbb{R}
, and it is not difficult to show that
\mathscr{L}(X)
basically only consists of its generator, that is,

\mathscr{L}(X)=\big\{\{X>a\}\mid a\in\mathbb{R}\big\}\cup\big\{\{X\geq a\}\mid a\in\mathbb{R}\big\}

(3)

(
Arnold 2020
, Lemma 5.2)
.

Under some regularity conditions on
(\mathcal{X},d,\preceq)
, which are explained in Appendix
B
, we can show the following result.

Lemma 3.1
.

Consider a random element
\xi
in an ordered metric space
(\mathcal{X},d,\preceq)
, which satisfies Assumption
B.1
. Then

\sigma(\xi)=\sigma\big(\mathscr{L}(\xi)\big).

In particular any
\mathscr{L}(\xi)
-measurable random variable
Z
is
\sigma(\xi)
-measurable.

The ordered metric spaces
\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
given in Examples
3.1
and
3.2
, respectively, satisfy Assumption
B.1
. Therefore, Lemma
3.1
implies that
\sigma(\boldsymbol{X})=\sigma(\mathscr{L}(\boldsymbol{X}))
for any random vector
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\sigma(F)=\sigma(\mathscr{L}(F))
for any random cdf
F\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
.

3.2
Factorization and connection to the classical conditional law

Consider two random variables
X
and
Y
. The classical factorization result states that a random variable
Y
is
\sigma(X)
-measurable if and only if there exists a measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
Y=f(X)

(
Bauer 2001
, Lemma 11.7)
. If we consider
\sigma
-lattices instead of
\sigma
-algebras, the factorization result remains valid with the additional requirement that the factorizing function
f
needs to be isotonic
(
Lee 1975
)
.
We generalize this result to random elements
\xi
with values in an ordered metric space.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\xi
be a random element in some ordered metric space
(\mathcal{X},d,\preceq)
and let
Z
be a random variable. Then
Z
is
\mathscr{L}(\xi)
-measurable if and only if there exists an increasing Borel measurable function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
Z=f(\xi)
. In particular, for any integrable random variable
Y
, there exists an increasing Borel measurable function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathbb{E}(Y\hskip 0.56905pt|\hskip 0.56905pt\mathscr{L}(\xi))=f(\xi).

Let
Y
be a random variable and
\xi\in\mathcal{X}
be a random element with distribution
P_{\xi}
. Recall that the conditional law of
Y
given
\xi
, which we denote by
P_{Y\mid\xi}
, is a Markov kernel from
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
to
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))
such that
P_{Y\mid\xi}(\cdot,B)
is a version of
\mathbb{P}(Y\in B\mid\xi)
for any
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
.
So for any
y\in\mathbb{Q}
, there exits a measurable function
g_{y}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
g_{y}(\xi)=\mathbb{P}(Y>y\mid\xi)
almost surely
(
Bauer 2001
, Lemma 11.7)
and hence
there exists a nullset
N\in\mathcal{F}
such that
g_{y}(\xi(\omega))=P_{Y\mid\xi}(\omega,(y,\infty))
for all
y\in\mathbb{Q}
and for all
\omega\in\Omega\setminus N
. Thus for
P_{\xi}
-almost all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the family
(g_{y})_{y\in\mathbb{Q}}
characterizes a deterministic law
P_{Y\mid\xi=x}
, which we call
conditional law of
Y
given
\xi=x
, by
P_{Y\mid\xi=x}((y,\infty))=g_{y}(x)
for all
y\in\mathbb{Q}
and imposing right-continuity.

The following theorem shows that the conditional law of
Y
given
\xi
corresponds to the isotonic conditional law of
Y
given
\xi
if, and only if, the conditional law is isotonic in
\xi
, where equality between the Markov kernels
P_{Y\mid\xi}
and
P_{Y\mid\mathscr{L}(\xi)}
is understood as usual in the sense that
P_{Y\mid\xi}(\cdot,B)=P_{Y\mid\mathscr{L}(\xi)}(\cdot,B)
almost surely for all
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
Y
be a random variable and let
\xi
be a random element in an ordered metric space
(\mathcal{X},d,\preceq)
that satisfies Assumption
B.1
. Then
P_{Y\mid\xi}=P_{Y\mid\mathscr{L}(\xi)}
if and only if the conditional law of
Y
given
\xi
is almost surely isotonic in
\xi
, that is for
P_{\xi}
-almost all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
with
x\preceq x^{\prime}
it holds that
P_{Y\mid\xi=x}\leq_{st}P_{Y\mid\xi=x^{\prime}}
.

For any
\sigma
-lattice
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
and random variable
Y
, we have shown existence and uniqueness of the ICL
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}
and its connection to isotonicity. In the next section, we show that
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}
equivalently emerges as a minimizer of some expected scoring rule (Proposition
4.1
) and that
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}
is even a universal minimizer in the sense that we may allow for a large class of scoring rules (Theorem
4.2
).

4
Universality of ICL

4.1
Identifiable functionals, scoring functions and scoring rules

In this section, we introduce identifiable functionals, scoring functions and scoring rules in order to show some universality properties of ICL.

Following
Jordan et al. 2022
, we call
V:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
an
identification function
, if
V(\cdot,y)
is increasing and left-continuous for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
. Similar to the classical procedure in
M
-estimation, an identification function weights negative values in the case of underprediction against positive values in the case of overprediction
(
Gneiting and Resin 2022
)
. For an identification function
V
, we define the
functional
T
induced by
V
as
T(F)=[T_{F}^{-},T_{F}^{+}]\subseteq\bar{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
, for any
F\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
, where the lower and upper bounds are given by

T_{F}^{-}=\sup\left\{x\hskip 2.84526pt\Big|\hskip 2.84526pt\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}V(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}F(y)<0\right\}\quad\textrm{and}\quad T_{F}^{+}=\inf\left\{x\hskip 2.84526pt\Big|\hskip 2.84526pt\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}V(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}F(y)>0\right\}.

A functional
T
is called
of singleton type
if
T(F)
is a singleton, and otherwise,
T
is called a
functional of interval type
. Relevant key examples for this paper are the mean functional with identification function
V(x,y)=x-y
, and the quantile functional at level
\alpha\in(0,1)
with identification function

V^{\alpha}(x,y)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{x>y\}}-\alpha.

(4)

Clearly, the mean functional is of singleton type but, in general, the quantile functional is of interval type. One may instead consider the singleton-valued
lower quantile

T_{F}^{-}
or
upper quantile

T_{F}^{+}
. For any distribution
F
, almost all quantiles are uniquely defined, i.e. the family of
\alpha\in(0,1)
, for which the set of
\alpha
-quantiles forms an interval, has Lebesgue measure zero. We will mainly consider the lower-quantile
T_{F}^{-}
, which corresponds to the quantile function
F^{-1}
evaluated at
\alpha\in(0,1)
, where
F^{-1}(\alpha)=\inf\{z\in\mathbb{R}\mid F(z)\geq\alpha\}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Point-valued forecasts for functionals of an unknown quantity should be compared using consistent scoring functions
(
Gneiting 2011
)
. A measurable map
S:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\bar{\mathbb{R}}
is a
consistent scoring function for the functional
T
relative to the class
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
if for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
F\in\mathcal{P}
, the integral
\int S(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}F(y)
exists and
\int S(t,y)\,\mathrm{d}F(y)\leq\int S(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}F(y)
for all
t\in T(F)
. The
Brier score
(or
quadratic score
)
\textrm{BS}(x,y)=(x-y)^{2}
,
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, and the
quantile score

\displaystyle\textrm{QS}_{\alpha}(x,y)

\displaystyle=(\mathbbm{1}_{\{y\leq x\}}-\alpha)(x-y),\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R},

are main examples of consistent scoring functions for the mean functional, and for the quantile at level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, respectively.

We adapt the methods of
Jordan et al. 2022
that rely on the mixture or Choquet representation of consistent scoring functions introduced by
Ehm et al. 2016
for the quantile and expectile functionals. Under some standard conditions, for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, consistent scoring functions for the
\alpha
-quantile have an integral representation
\int_{\mathbb{R}}S_{\alpha,\eta}^{Q}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}H(\eta)
, with respect to the elementary
\alpha
-quantile loss functions

S_{\alpha,\eta}^{Q}(x,y)=(1-\alpha)\mathbbm{1}_{\{y<\eta\leq x\}}+\alpha\mathbbm{1}_{\{x<\eta\leq y\}},\quad\eta\in\mathbb{R},

(5)

where
H
is a nonnegative
\sigma
-finite measure on
\mathbb{R}
. The quantile score
\textrm{QS}_{\alpha}(x,y)
arises when
H
is the Lebesgue measure. Similarly, any consistent scoring function for the mean is a Bregman scoring function and can be written as
\int S_{\eta}^{E}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}H(\eta)
, where

S_{\eta}^{E}(x,y)=(\eta-y)\mathbbm{1}_{\{y<\eta\leq x\}}+(y-\eta)\mathbbm{1}_{\{x<\eta\leq y\}},\quad\eta\in\mathbb{R},

(6)

and
\textrm{BS}(x,y)
arises by taking
H
to be the Lebesgue measure.

Probabilistic forecasts, that provide full predictive distributions over the possible values of the future outcome should be compared and evaluated using proper scoring rules
(
Gneiting and Raftery 2007
)
. A
proper scoring rule
is a function
{\rm S}:\mathcal{P}\times\mathbb{R}\mapsto\bar{\mathbb{R}}
for some class
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
such that
{\rm S}(F,\cdot)
is measurable for any
F\in\mathcal{P}
, the integral
\int{\rm S}(G,y)F(\,\mathrm{d}y)
exists, and

\int{\rm S}(F,y)\,\mathrm{d}F(y)\leq\int{\rm S}(G,y)\,\mathrm{d}F(y)

for all
F,G\in\mathcal{P}
. A key example is the
continuous ranked probability
score
(CRPS;
Matheson and Winkler 1976
), which is defined for all Borel probability measures, equivalently given by

\textrm{CRPS}(F,y)=\int\textrm{BS}(F(z),\mathbbm{1}_{\{y\leq z\}})\,\mathrm{d}z=2\int_{0}^{1}\textrm{QS}_{\alpha}(F^{-1}(\alpha),y)\,\mathrm{d}\alpha.

4.2
ICL as a scoring rule minimizer

We denote by
\mathscr{H}
the family of all Markov kernels from
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
into
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))
. Any Markov kernel
Q\in\mathscr{H}
can equivalently be considered as a random element in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
. If it is clear from the context that
Q
is random we write
Q(x)
or
Q(\cdot,x)
to abbreviate
Q(\cdot,(-\infty,x])
and
\bar{Q}(x)
or
\bar{Q}(\cdot,x)
to abbreviate
Q(\cdot,(x,\infty))
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
. The next Proposition shows that ICL equivalently results as a CRPS minimizer.

Proposition 4.1
.

For a random variable
Y
and a
\sigma
-lattice
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, the ICL
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}
minimizes

\mathbb{E}\textrm{CRPS}(G,Y)

(7)

over all random distributions
G
in the class

\mathscr{G}_{\mathcal{A}}=\{G\in\mathscr{H}\mid G(\cdot,(z,\infty))\textrm{ is }\mathcal{A}\textrm{-measurable for all }z\in\mathbb{R}\}.

The ICL
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}
is the unique minimizer in the sense that
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}(\cdot,B)=G(\cdot,B)
almost surely for all
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
, for any
G\in\mathscr{G}_{\mathcal{A}}
that minimizes (
7
) as well.

Remark 2

(The class
\mathscr{G}_{\mathcal{A}}
)
.

The class
\mathscr{G}_{\mathcal{A}}
can equivalently be defined as

\mathscr{G}_{\mathcal{A}}=\{G\in\mathscr{H}\mid G^{-1}(\alpha)\textrm{ is }\mathcal{A}\textrm{-measurable for all }\alpha\in(0,1)\}.

This may be seen by the equality

\Omega\setminus\{G(\cdot,(z,\infty))>1-\alpha\}=\{G(z)\geq\alpha\}=\{z\geq G^{-1}(\alpha)\}=\Omega\setminus\{G^{-1}(\alpha)>z\},

for any
z\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
and where the second equality follows by the fact that
G(z)\geq\alpha
if and only if
z\geq G^{-1}(\alpha)
, see e.g.
Embrechts and Hofert 2013
.

Suppose now that
\mathcal{A}
is generated by a random element
\xi
in some ordered metric space
(\mathcal{X},d,\preceq)
that satisfies Assumption
B.1
and let
P_{\xi}
denote the distribution of
\xi
. For any
G\in\mathscr{H}
,
G_{\xi}
and
G_{\mathscr{L}(\xi)}
are Markov kernels such that
G_{\xi}(\cdot,B)=\mathbb{E}(G(\cdot,B)\mid\xi)
almost surely for any
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
and
G_{\mathscr{L}(\xi)}(\cdot,(z,\infty))=\mathbb{E}(G(\cdot,(z,\infty))\mid\mathscr{L}(\xi))
almost surely for any
z\in\mathbb{R}
. Clearly, for any
\sigma
-lattice
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, we have
G=G_{\mathcal{A}}
if and only if
G(\cdot,(z,\infty))
is
\mathcal{A}
-measurable. By Proposition
3.2
, the class
\mathscr{G}_{\mathcal{A}}
may alternatively be characterized as

\mathscr{G}_{\mathcal{A}}=\{G\in\mathscr{H}\mid G_{\xi=x}\leq_{st}G_{\xi=x^{\prime}}\textrm{ for $P_{\xi}$-almost all }x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}\textrm{ with }x\preceq x^{\prime}\}.

Indeed, assume that for
G\in\mathscr{H}
it holds that
G(\cdot,(z,\infty))
is
\mathscr{L}(\xi)
-measurable for any
z\in\mathbb{R}
. By Proposition
3.2
, for any
z\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists an increasing Borel measurable function
g_{z}
such that
G(\cdot,(z,\infty))=g_{z}(\xi)
almost surely. Recall that for
P_{\xi}
-almost all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
G_{\xi=x}
denotes a deterministic law which is characterized by
G_{\xi=x}((z,\infty))=g_{z}(x)
for all
z\in\mathbb{Q}
and imposing right-continuity. Since
g_{z}
is isotonic for any
z\in\mathbb{R}
, it follows directly that
G_{\xi=x}\leq_{st}G_{\xi=x^{\prime}}
for
P_{\xi}
-almost all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
with
x\preceq x^{\prime}
.

Reversely, assume that for
G\in\mathscr{H}
it holds that
G_{\xi=x}\leq_{st}G_{\xi=x^{\prime}}
for
P_{\xi}
-almost all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
with
x\preceq x^{\prime}
. Then
G_{\xi}=G_{\mathscr{L}(\xi)}
by Theorem
3.3
. That is for any
z\in\mathbb{R}
,
G_{\xi}(\cdot,(z,\infty))
is a regular version of
\mathbb{E}(G(\cdot,(z,\infty)\mid\mathscr{L}(\xi))
and hence
\mathscr{L}(\xi)
-measurable.

Proposition
4.1
allows to show that ICL generalizes isotonic distributional regression (IDR) introduced by
Henzi et al. 2021
. IDR is a nonparametric distributional regression method that uses the shape constraint of isotonicity between the covariates and the responses: Consider
training data consisting of covariates
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
in a partially ordered set
(\mathcal{X},\preceq)
and real valued responses
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Henzi et al. 2021
show that there exists a unique minimizer of the criterion

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\textrm{CRPS}(G_{i},y_{i})

(8)

over all vectors of distributions
(G_{1},\dots,G_{n})
with
G_{i}\leq_{st}G_{j}
if
x_{i}\preceq x_{j}
for
i,j=1,\dots,n
and call this minimizer
Isotonic Distributional Regression (IDR) of
\boldsymbol{y}
given
\boldsymbol{x}
. The key observation in showing that ICL generalizes IDR is that any covariate vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
induces a
\sigma
-lattice
\mathcal{A}
on the set of indices
\{1,\dots,n\}
through the corresponding order relations, that is
A\in\mathcal{A}
for
A\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
if and only if
i\in A
and
x_{i}\preceq x_{j}
implies
j\in A
. Any vector of distributions
(G_{1},\dots,G_{n})
may be considered as a random cdf
G
defined on the discrete probability space
\{1,\dots,n\}
and
G\in\mathscr{G}_{\mathcal{A}}
if and only
G_{i}\leq_{st}G_{j}
if
x_{i}\preceq x_{j}
for any
i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Hence, we exactly obtain the minimizing criterion (
8
) if we consider the expectation with respect to the empirical measure of the observations in (
7
), and thus ICL generalizes IDR by Proposition
4.1
. Measurability poses no problem in the finite sample case. For this reason
Henzi et al. 2021
can simply consider partially ordered spaces but we have to study ordered metric spaces.

Analogously to Theorem 2.2 of
Henzi et al. 2021
, the next theorem shows that
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}
is not only optimal with respect to the CRPS but with respect to a broad class of proper scoring rules, and that for any
z\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the random variables
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}(z)
and
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(\alpha)
are optimal with respect to all consistent scoring functions for the mean and the
\alpha
-quantile, respectively.

Theorem 4.2

(Universality of ICL)
.

For a random variable
Y
and a
\sigma
-lattice
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, the ICL
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}
satisfies the following properties.

1.

The ICL
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}
minimizes
\mathbb{E}{\rm S}(G,Y)
over all
G\in\mathscr{G}_{\mathcal{A}}
uniquely in the sense of Proposition
4.1
under any scoring rule
{\rm S}
of the form

{\rm S}(F,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}S_{\eta}^{E}(F(z),\mathbbm{1}_{\{y\leq z\}})\,\mathrm{d}H(z,\eta),

where
S_{\eta}^{E}
is defined at (
6
) and
H
is a
\sigma
-finite Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, for every threshold value
z\in\mathbb{R}
,
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}(\cdot,(z,\infty))
is a minimizer of

\mathbb{E}S(X,\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y>z\}})

over all
{\mathcal{A}}
-measurable random variables
X
, where
S
is any consistent scoring function for the mean, that is,
S(x,y)=\int S_{\eta}^{E}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{H}(\eta)
for some
\sigma
-finite Borel measure
\tilde{H}
on
\mathbb{R}
.

2.

Suppose that the random vector
(P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(\alpha),Y)
satisfies Assumption
6.1
for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Then, the ICL
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}
minimizes
\mathbb{E}{\rm S}(G,Y)
over all
G\in\mathscr{G}_{\mathcal{A}}
uniquely in the sense of Proposition
4.1
under any scoring rule
{\rm S}
of the form

{\rm S}(F,y)=\int_{(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}}S_{\alpha,\eta}^{Q}(F^{-1}(\alpha),y)\,\mathrm{d}H(\alpha,\eta)

where
S_{\alpha,\eta}^{Q}
is defined at (
5
), and
H
is a
\sigma
-finite Borel measure on
(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, for every quantile level
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(\alpha)
minimizes

\mathbb{E}S(X,Y)

over all
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variables
X
, where
S
is any consistent scoring function for the
\alpha
-quantile, that is,
S(x,y)=\int S_{\alpha,\eta}^{Q}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{H}(\eta)
for a
\sigma
-finite Borel measure
\tilde{H}
on
\mathbb{R}
.

We strongly believe that the second part of the theorem also holds without Assumption
6.1
. However, we are currently not able to give a proof. We discuss the assumption in Section
6
.

For the proof of Theorem
4.2
, we use the fact that ICL is threshold and quantile calibrated as we will establish in the next section. Furthermore, we introduce a way to view conditional functionals as minimizers of consistent scoring functions in Section
6
by adapting the methods of
Jordan et al. 2022
, and this will allow us to conclude.

5
Calibration properties of ICL

Probabilistic predictions are called calibrated if they are statistically consistent with the observed outcomes.
Consider a random variable
Y
and a (random) predictive distribution for
Y
, denoted by
F
. The random forecast
F
is called
auto-calibrated

(
Tsyplakov 2013
)
, if the conditional law of
Y
given
F
equals
F
, that is
P_{Y\mid F}=F
, or equivalently

F(y)=\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y\mid F)\quad\textrm{almost surely for all }y\in\mathbb{R}.

(9)

For any
y\in\mathbb{R}
, we may condition on
F(y)
instead of
F
in (
9
), to obtain the weaker notion of threshold calibration, i.e. we call the forecast
F

threshold calibrated

(
Henzi et al. 2021
)
, if

F(y)=\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y\mid F(y))\quad\textrm{almost surely for all }y\in\mathbb{R}.

We adapt the definition of
Gneiting and Resin 2022
slightly and call the forecast
F

quantile calibrated
, if

F^{-1}(\alpha)=q_{\alpha}(Y\mid F^{-1}(\alpha))\quad\textrm{almost surely for all }\alpha\in(0,1),

(10)

where for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
q_{\alpha}(Y\mid F^{-1}(\alpha))
denotes the lower-
\alpha
-quantile of the conditional law of
Y
given
F^{-1}(\alpha)
. Restricting to the lower quantile ensures that all random quantities in (
10
) are real-valued.
Since almost all quantiles of the posited distribution
F
are point-valued, the restriction to lower quantiles in the definition of quantile calibration is not limiting. For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, let
X=q_{\alpha}(Y\mid F^{-1}(\alpha))
. Then
\mathbb{P}(Y<X\mid F^{-1}(\alpha))\leq\alpha\leq\mathbb{P}(Y\leq X\mid F^{-1}(\alpha))
almost surely, see e.g.
Tomkins 1975
. Hence, for any quantile calibrated forecast
F
,

\mathbb{P}(Y<F^{-1}(\alpha)\mid F^{-1}(\alpha))\leq\alpha\leq\mathbb{P}(Y\leq F^{-1}(\alpha)\mid F^{-1}(\alpha))\quad\textrm{almost surely for all }\alpha\in(0,1).

Gneiting and Resin 2022
provide a recent comprehensive analysis of the connections and implications between different notions of calibration found in the literature.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
be a
\sigma
-lattice. Clearly, the ICL
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}
may be interpreted as a predictive distribution for
Y
. In fact,
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}
is the theoretically optimal distributional prediction for
Y
given all information contained in
\mathcal{A}
. The following proposition shows that ICL is threshold- and quantile-calibrated.

Proposition 5.1
.

For any
\sigma
-lattice
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, the ICL
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}
is threshold calibrated, that is,

P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}(y)=\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y\mid P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}(y))\quad\textrm{almost surely for all }y\in\mathbb{R}.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
be a
\sigma
-lattice, and suppose that the random vector
(P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(\alpha),Y)
satisfies Assumption
6.1
for any

Example 5.1

To see that isotonic calibration does not imply auto-calibration, one can consider the equiprobable forecast

6.1
General construction

In this section, we propose a general notion of conditional (identifiable) functionals building on the work of
Jordan et al. 2022
. Although our main motivation is to consider conditional quantiles in order to show Theorem
4.2
and Proposition
5.2
, the considerations in this section are of independent interest. Moreover, the given recipe is perfectly applicable as well for
\sigma
-algebras instead of
\sigma
-lattices as illustrated in Remark
5
.

Consider a random variable
Y
and an identifiable functional
T
with identification function
V
. For a
\sigma
-lattice
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, our aim is to find an
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variable
X
that minimizes

\mathbb{E}(S(X,Y)),

(11)

over all
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variables
X
such that the expectation exists, where
S
is a consistent scoring function for
T
with representation
S(x,y)=\int S_{\eta}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}H(\eta)
for some
\sigma
-finite measure
H
on
\mathbb{R}
, and elementary loss functions

S_{\eta}(x,y)=V(\eta,y)\mathbbm{1}_{\{y<\eta\leq x\}}-V(\eta,y)\mathbbm{1}_{\{x<\eta\leq y\}},\quad\eta\in\mathbb{R}.

(12)

The minimizing criterion (
11
) can be motivated by the fact that the conditional expectation of any square integrable
Y
given
\mathcal{A}
minimizes
\mathbb{E}(X-Y)^{2}
over all square integrable
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variables
X
. Clearly, also conditional quantiles may be characterized as minimizers of some expected loss, see e.g.
Armerin 2014
.

Throughout this section, we assume that
\mathbb{E}(V(\eta,Y))
is finite for all
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
. If there exists an
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variable
X
that minimizes

\mathbb{E}(S_{\eta}(X,Y))

(13)

over all
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variables
X
simultaneously for all
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, then it follows that
X
is also a solution for the minimizing problem in (
11
). For a fixed
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, minimizing equation (
13
) over the class of all
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variables
X
is equivalent to minimizing

\int_{\{X\geq\eta\}}V(\eta,Y)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}

(14)

over all
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variables
X
. Since
X
is
\mathcal{A}
-measurable, it follows that
\{X\geq\eta\}\in\mathcal{A}
for all
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
. For
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
and
A\in\mathcal{A}
, we hence consider

s_{A}(\eta)=\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{A}V(\eta,Y)).

(15)

Although our aim is to minimize
s_{A}(\eta)
over all
A\in\mathcal{A}
for any
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, it will be useful to consider the same criterion but allow for general
A\in\mathcal{F}
. To point out this distinction, we define for
A\in\mathcal{F}

v_{A}(\eta)=\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{A}V(\eta,Y)).

That is
s_{A}(\eta)=v_{A}(\eta)
for
A\in\mathcal{A}
, while
s_{A}(\eta)
is not defined for
A\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{A}
. For
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, denote by
M(\eta)
the family of all sets
A\in\mathcal{A}
that minimize (
15
). The following lemma guarantees that
M(\eta)\neq\emptyset
for all
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

For
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, suppose that
A_{n}\in\mathcal{A}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
s_{A_{n}}(\eta)\to\inf_{A\in\mathcal{A}}s_{A}(\eta)
. Then
\liminf_{n\to\infty}A_{n}\in M(\eta)
.

We construct a
minimizing path
, that is a sequence
(A_{\eta})_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
with
A_{\eta}\in M(\eta)
for all
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, and will define the conditional functional of
Y
given
\mathcal{A}
as the inverse of the path. Firstly, we show that we can choose our path to be decreasing using Zorn’s Lemma.

Proposition 6.2
.

There exists a decreasing minimizing path, i.e. there exists a sequence
(A_{\eta})_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}}
with
A_{\eta}\in M(\eta)
for any
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
A_{\eta^{\prime}}\subseteq A_{\eta}
for any
\eta<\eta^{\prime}
.

For functionals with strictly increasing identification functions, any minimizing path will automatically be almost surely decreasing as shown in the next section.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
(A_{\eta})_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
be a decreasing minimizing path. We define the
conditional functional
T
of
Y
given
\mathcal{A}
as

T(Y\mid\mathcal{A})(\omega)=\sup\{\eta\in\mathbb{R}\mid\omega\in A_{\eta}\},\quad\omega\in\Omega.

(16)

Remark 3
.

Since we consider a decreasing minimizing path
(A_{\eta})_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}}
, we may write
T(Y\mid\mathcal{A})
equivalently by the inf-representation

T(Y\mid\mathcal{A})(\omega)=\inf\{\eta\in\mathbb{R}\mid\omega\notin A_{\eta}\},\quad\omega\in\Omega.

Remark 4
.

We note that

T(Y\mid\mathcal{A})(\omega)=\sup\{\eta\in\mathbb{Q}\mid\omega\in A_{\eta}\}

(17)

for all
\omega\in\Omega
since
\mathbb{Q}
lies dense in
\mathbb{R}
. Using equation (
17
) instead of (
16
) will be useful for treating null sets and show measurability conditions.

Some words of caution are necessary here. We defined
T(Y\mid\mathcal{A})
as the inverse of a particular minimizing path and hence
T(Y\mid\mathcal{A})
will be path dependent in general. This is no surprise if we consider that e.g. the quantile of a distribution is in general not unique either. In the next section, we will show that the conditional functional is almost surely unique if the corresponding identification function is strictly increasing in its first argument as it is the case for the mean functional. In Section
6.3
, we study conditional quantiles and show that we may obtain the lower quantile if we choose the minimizing path accordingly. In a first step, it is important to note that
T(Y\mid\mathcal{A})
as defined in (
16
) is actually a solution to our problem.

Proposition 6.3
.

The conditional functional
T(Y\mid\mathcal{A})
is
\mathcal{A}
-measurable and minimizes (
14
) over all
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variables
X
for all
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
. If the elementary loss functions
S_{\eta}
defined at (
12
) are nonnegative, then the expectation in (
11
) always exists, and
T(Y\mid\mathcal{A})
minimizes (
11
) over all
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variables
X
.

Remark 5
.

Since
\sigma
-algebras are also
\sigma
-lattices, the proposed construction of conditional functionals also applies if
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
is a
\sigma
-algebra. In this case, Proposition
6.3
shows that our construction provides an alternative characterization of the usual notion of conditional functionals defined as functionals of the conditional distribution.

Remark 6
.

The condition of nonnegative elementary loss funcitons is satisfied for all interesting identification functions we can think of, so in particular for the mean and for quantiles.

Finally assume that the
\sigma
-lattice, respectively the
\sigma
-algebra,
\mathcal{A}
is generated by some random element
\xi
that lies in an ordered metric space
(\mathcal{X},d,\preceq)
, that is
\mathcal{A}=\mathscr{L}(\xi)
or
\mathcal{A}=\sigma(\xi)
. By Proposition
3.2
and the classical factorization result, we know that there exists an increasing Borel measurable function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
and a Borel measurable function
g:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathbb{E}S(h(\xi),Y)

(18)

is minimized by
f
over the class of all Borel measurable and increasing functions
h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, and minimized by
g
over all Borel measurable functions
h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. Representation (
18
) of the underlying minimizing problem shows the connection to the work of
Jordan et al. 2022
more clearly, where the authors minimize the empirical version of (
18
) over all increasing functions
h
.

6.2
Strictly increasing identification functions and conditional expectations

Throughout this section, we assume that the identification function
V
is strictly increasing in its first component. Under this assumption, the conditional functional is almost surely unique and we use this fact to show the part of Theorem
4.2
concerning the mean functional. Let
(A_{\eta})_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}}
be any minimizing path with
A_{\eta}\in M(\eta)
for all
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 6.4
.

The sequence
(A_{\eta})_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}}
is almost surely decreasing, i.e.
\mathbb{P}(A_{\eta^{\prime}}\setminus A_{\eta})=0
for
\eta<\eta^{\prime}
.

Proposition 6.5
.

The conditional functional
T(Y\mid\mathcal{A})
as defined in (
16
) is almost surely unique. That is, for any minimizing paths
(A_{\eta})_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}},(\tilde{A}_{\eta})_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
with corresponding conditional functionals
X
and
\tilde{X}
defined at (
16
), it holds that
X=\tilde{X}
almost surely.

For the remainder of this section we focus on the mean functional with identification function
V(\eta,y)=\eta-y
. By Lemma
6.4
, any minimizing path
(A_{\eta})_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}}
is almost surely decreasing and by Proposition
6.5
, the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mathcal{A})
defined by (
16
) is almost surely unique. For any square integrable random variable
Y
,
Brunk 1965
defined the conditional expectation given
\mathcal{A}
as its projection onto the closed convex cone of square integrable and
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variables, that is as the minimizer of
\mathbb{E}(X-Y)^{2}
over all
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variables
X\in L_{2}
. Moreover,
Brunk 1965
shows that
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mathcal{A})
minimizes
\mathbb{E}S(X,Y)
over all over all
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variables
X\in L_{2}
for any Bregman scoring function
S
, where the class of Bregman scoring functions essentially comprises all consistent scoring functions for the mean functional, see
Jordan et al. 2022
. These considerations show that Definition
2.1
coincides with Definition
6.1
for the mean functional for square integrable random variables. For
X\in L_{1}\setminus L_{2}
, we may approximate
X
by
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq L_{2}
as it is done in the proof of Theorem 2 by
Brunk 1963
, to show that the definitions also correspond for integrable random variables whenever the given expectations exist.

We conclude this section by showing the first part of Theorem
4.2
.

Proof of the first part of Theorem
4.2
.

For
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, consider the elementary scoring function for the mean
S_{\eta}^{E}
as defined in (
6
). By the above considerations, it follows directly that
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}(\cdot,(z,\infty))=\mathbb{P}(Y>z\mid\mathcal{A})
minimizes
\mathbb{E}S_{\eta}(X,\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y>z\}})
over all
{\mathcal{A}}
-measurable random variables
X
for any
z\in\mathbb{R}
and thus the second claim of the first part follows by Fubini. Note that analogously one can show that for any
z\in\mathbb{R}
,
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}(z)
minimizes
\mathbb{E}S(X,\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y\leq z\}})
over all
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
-measurable random variables
X
. Consider now any
\sigma
-finite measure
H
on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
. By the disintegration theorem
(
Kallenberg 2017
, Theorem 1.23)
, there exists a
\sigma
-finite measure
P
on
\mathbb{R}
and a kernel
K
from
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))
to
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))
such that

. Hence, we may derive by Fubini

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\rm S}(F,Y)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}S_{\eta}^{E}(F(z),\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y\leq z\}})\,\mathrm{d}H(z,\eta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}S_{\eta}^{E}(F(z),\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y\leq z\}})K(z,\mathrm{d}\eta)\,\mathrm{d}P(\eta)

which concludes the proof of the first part.

6.3
Conditional quantiles and quantile calibration of ICL

Our main motivation to study conditional functionals was to show that ICL is quantile calibrated (Proposition
5.2
) and to show universality of ICL (Theorem
4.2
). Now, we turn our attention to quantiles and modify our notation slightly: For
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, denote by
M(\alpha,\eta)
the family of all
A\in\mathcal{A}
that minimize
\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{A}V^{\alpha}(\eta,Y))
, where
V^{\alpha}
denotes the identification function for the
\alpha
-quantile as given at (
4
). Firstly, we show that the minimizing paths may be chosen such that they additionally order with respect to
\alpha
.

Lemma 6.6
.

There exists a rectangular path

(A_{\eta}^{\alpha})^{\alpha\in(0,1)}_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
which is decreasing in
\eta
and increasing in
\alpha
, that is for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
A_{\eta}^{\alpha}\in M(\alpha,\eta)
, the sequence
(A_{\eta}^{\alpha})_{\alpha\in(0,1)}
is increasing and the sequence
(A_{\eta}^{\alpha})_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}}
is decreasing.

In the proof of Lemma
6.6
, we first construct a rectangular array
(\hat{A}^{\alpha}_{\eta})
for
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)\cap\mathbb{Q}
with the given monotonicity constraints and extend it in a second step to a rectangular array

A_{\eta}^{\alpha}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in(0,1)\cap\mathbb{Q}:\beta<\alpha\end{subarray}}\hat{A}_{\eta}^{\beta},\quad\alpha\in(0,1),\eta\in\mathbb{R}.

In the next lemma, we show that this particular extension implies that the resulting
\alpha
-quantiles are left-continuous. If we were interested in upper-quantiles rather than lower-quantiles we could extend
(\hat{A}^{\alpha}_{\eta})
by
A_{\eta}^{\alpha}=\bigcap_{\beta\in(\alpha,1)\cap\mathbb{Q}}\hat{A}_{\eta}^{\beta}
, to obtain right-continuous quantiles.

Lemma 6.7
.

Consider a rectangular path
(A_{\eta}^{\alpha})^{\alpha\in(0,1)}_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
with the monotonicity constraints as given in Lemma
6.6
which is left-continuous, that is for any
\alpha\in(0,1)

A_{\eta}^{\alpha}=\bigcup_{\beta\in(0,1):\beta<\alpha}A_{\eta}^{\beta}.

The corresponding conditional quantiles defined by (
16
), then define a random element
F\in\mathscr{G}_{\mathcal{A}}
given by

F^{-1}(\alpha)=q_{\alpha}(Y\mid\mathcal{A}),\quad\alpha\in(0,1).

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we denote by
q_{\alpha}(Y\mid X)
the lower
\alpha
-quantile of the conditional law
P_{Y\mid X}
, see e.g.
Tomkins 1975
. Equivalently,
q_{\alpha}(Y\mid X)
results as a minimizer of
\mathbb{E}\textrm{QS}_{\alpha}(Z,Y)
over all
\sigma(X)
-measurable random variables
Z
as shown by
Armerin 2014
, that is,
q_{\alpha}(Y\mid X)
may equivalently be constructed as proposed in this section. To show quantile calibration of ICL, we additionally need the following lemma, which is the analogue of Lemma
5.4
for conditional quantiles instead of conditional expectations.

Assumption 6.1
.

The joint distribution of the random vector
(X,Y)
is absolutely continuous, or, the marginal distribution of
X
has countable support without accumulation points, i.e. the support of
X
consists of countably many isolated points.

Lemma 6.8
.

For any random variables
X
and
Y
that satisfy Assumption
6.1
, we have
q_{\alpha}(Y\mid X)=X
if and only if
q_{\alpha}(Y\mid\mathscr{L}(X))=X
.

We are strongly convinced that Lemma
5.4
holds in full generality and not only under Assumption
6.1
, and we conjecture that the claim may be extended to any identifiable functional. However, currently we are only able to show the claim in the stated special cases. With Lemma
6.7
and Lemma

we are in position to show quantile calibration of ICL (Proposition

) and the remaining part of Theorem

.

Proof of Proposition
5.2
.

We apply Fubini to the quantile score based representation of the CRPS

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\textrm{CRPS}(G,Y)=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{1}\textrm{QS}_{\alpha}(G^{-1}(\alpha),Y)\,\mathrm{d}\alpha=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\textrm{QS}_{\alpha}(G^{-1}(\alpha),Y)\,\mathrm{d}\alpha.

By Proposition
6.3
, we know that the integrand can be minimized pointwise for each
\alpha\in(0,1)
and by Lemma

, it follows that the solutions may be chosen such that they define a random distribution

. By the uniqueness claim in Proposition

, it follows that
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}(\cdot,B)=F(\cdot,B)
almost surely for all

, and hence, for any

,

\displaystyle P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(\alpha)=F^{-1}(\alpha)=q_{\alpha}(Y\mid\mathcal{A}).

Using that
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(\alpha)
is
\mathcal{A}
-measurable, we see that

P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(\alpha)=q_{\alpha}\big(Y\mid\mathscr{L}(P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(\alpha))\big),\quad\textrm{for all }\alpha\in(0,1),

since
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(\alpha)
minimizes
\mathbb{E}(\textrm{QS}_{\alpha}(X,Y))
over all
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variables
X
, and hence, also over all
\mathscr{L}(P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(\alpha))
-measurable random variables. By Lemma

, it follows that

P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(\alpha)=q_{\alpha}(Y\mid P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(\alpha)),\quad\textrm{for all }\alpha\in(0,1),

that is,
P_{Y\mid\mathcal{A}}
is quantile calibrated.
∎

Proof of the second part of Theorem
4.2
.

In the proof of Proposition
5.2
, it was shown that for any

,

is a version of

and hence the second claim of the second part of Theorem

follows directly by Fubini. Consider now any
\sigma
-finite measure

on

. We may argue analogously as in Section

by the disintegration theorem
(
Kallenberg 2017
, Theorem 1.23)
, that there exists a

on

and a kernel

from

such that

Remark 7
.

For any random variable
Y
and
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
,

calls a random variable

a conditional median of

with respect to

, if

is
\mathcal{A}

. Whether this is also true in the case of

\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})

q\in\mathbb{Q}
, we let
P((q,\infty)\mid\mathcal{A})
be a version of
\mathbb{P}(Y>q\mid\mathcal{A})
. Using the properties of conditional expectations given
\sigma
-lattices, we first may construct a nullset
N_{0}\in\mathcal{F}
such that the mapping

\mathbb{Q}\rightarrow[0,1],\quad q\mapsto P((q,\infty)\mid\mathcal{A})(\omega)

is decreasing and nonnegative for all
\omega\in\Omega\setminus N_{0}
.
For
a,b\in\mathbb{Q}
with
a<b
, we define

\tilde{P}((a,b])=P((a,\infty)\mid\mathcal{A})-P((b,\infty)\mid\mathcal{A}).

We then extend
\tilde{P}
to any
A\in\mathfrak{R}
, where we have to show that
\tilde{P}(A)
does not depend on the representation of
A
(of disjoint half-open intervals), and write
P(\omega,A)
for
P(A)(\omega)
for
\omega\in\Omega
. Consider a further nullset
N_{1}
with
\tilde{P}(\omega,\mathbb{R})=1
for all
\omega\in N_{1}^{c}
. Then
N=N_{0}\cup N_{1}
is a nullset such that, for all
\omega\in N^{c}
, it holds that
B\mapsto\tilde{P}(\omega,B)
is a content on
\mathfrak{R}
with
\tilde{P}(\omega,\mathbb{R})=1
.

In a next step, we have to show that
B\mapsto\tilde{P}(\omega,B)
is
\sigma
-additive for all
\omega\in N^{c}
, i.e.
\tilde{P}
is almost surely a pre-measure on
\mathfrak{R}
. This can be achieved using the equivalence that a content is
\sigma
-additive if and only if it is continuous
(
Bauer 2001
, Theorem 3.2.)
.

By the extension theorem of Carathéodory, it follows that for
\omega\in N^{c}
the pre-measure
B\mapsto\tilde{P}(\omega,B)
may be uniquely extended to a probability measure
B\mapsto Q(\omega,B)
on
\sigma(\mathfrak{R})=\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
. If
N\neq\emptyset
, we let
Q(\omega,B)=\delta_{\omega_{N}}(B)
for
\omega\in N
and some fixed
\omega_{N}\in N
. Then,
Q
satisfies the first property of a Markov kernel, i.e.
B\mapsto Q(\omega,B)
is a probability measure for any
\omega\in\Omega
.

For any
B\in\mathfrak{R}
, the map
\omega\mapsto Q(\omega,B)
is by construction a linear combination of conditional probabilities given
\mathcal{A}
, and hence
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
-measurable. To show that
\omega\mapsto Q(\omega,B)
is
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
-measurable for any
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
, we define

\mathfrak{D}=\{B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\mid\omega\mapsto Q(\omega,B)\textrm{ is $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$-measurable}\}

and show that
\mathfrak{D}
forms Dynkin system. Since
\mathfrak{R}
is a ring, it follows that
\sigma(\mathfrak{R})=\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{R})
, where
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{R})
denotes the Dynkin system generated by
\mathfrak{R}
. Since
\mathfrak{R}\subseteq\mathfrak{D}
, we may conclude

\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})=\sigma(\mathfrak{R})=\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{R})\subseteq\mathfrak{D}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),

that is,
\mathfrak{D}=\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
and
Q
is indeed a Markov kernel. Moreover, it follows by construction that
\omega\mapsto Q(\omega,(y,\infty))
is a version of
\mathbb{P}(Y>y\mid\mathcal{A})
for any
y\in\mathbb{R}
and hence
Q
satisfies all requirements of Theorem
2.1
.

Finally, assume that
P
and
Q
are two Markov kernels from
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
into
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))
such that
\omega\mapsto P(\omega,(y,\infty))
and
\omega\mapsto Q(\omega,(y,\infty))
are versions of
\mathbb{P}(Y>y\mid\mathcal{A})
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
. Then it follows that
P(\cdot,A)=Q(\cdot,A)
almost surely for any
A\in\mathfrak{R}
, so for any
A\in\mathfrak{R}
there exists a nullset
N_{A}
such that
P(\omega,A)=Q(\omega,A)
for all
\omega\in N_{A}^{c}
. The countable union
N=\bigcup_{A\in\mathfrak{R}}N_{A}
is a nullset such that
P(\omega,A)=Q(\omega,A)
for all
\omega\in N^{c}
and for all
A\in\mathfrak{R}
. It follows by
Bauer 2001
that the equality holds even for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
, and hence, the conditional law of
Y
given
\mathcal{A}
is uniquely defined as specified in the theorem.
∎

Appendix B
Ordered metric spaces

Consider an ordered metric space
(\mathcal{X},d,\preceq)
. For
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we define the
upper closure of
x
as
\uparrow x=\{y\in\mathcal{X}\mid x\preceq y\}
and the
lower closure of
x
as
\downarrow x=\{y\in\mathcal{X}\mid y\preceq x\}
.
Following
Richmond 2020
, we call
(\mathcal{X},d,\preceq)

T_{1}
-ordered
if
\uparrow x
and
\downarrow x
are closed for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Equivalently, an ordered metric space is
T_{1}
-ordered if and only if the following continuity assumption is satisfied: If
x_{n}\preceq y
(
y\preceq x_{n}
) for some sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
with
x_{n}\to x
as
n\to\infty
, it holds that
x\preceq y
(
y\preceq x
). We call a set
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}

convex
if
a,c\in A
and
a\preceq b\preceq c
imply
b\in A
and say that an ordered metric space
(\mathcal{X},d,\preceq)
has a
convex topology
if the induced topology
\mathcal{T}
has a basis of convex sets. To assume that
(\mathcal{X},d,\preceq)
has a convex topology is a weaker assumption than to say that
\mathcal{X}
is a locally convex vector space, since we do not assume
\mathcal{X}
to be a vector space. Clearly, for any
a,b\in\mathcal{X}
with
a\preceq b
the interval
[a,b]=\uparrow a\cap\downarrow b=\{x\in\mathcal{X}\mid a\preceq x\preceq b\}
is convex. Recall that
\mathcal{X}
is called
separable
if there exists a dense countable subset
M\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, i.e. there exists a countable
M\subseteq\mathcal{X}
with
\overline{M}=\mathcal{X}
, where for any
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, we denote by
\overline{A}
the closure of
A
. For a sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we write
x_{n}\uparrow x
(
x_{n}\downarrow x
) if
x_{n}\to x
as
n\to\infty
and
x_{n}\preceq x_{n+1}
(
x_{n}\succeq x_{n+1}
) for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We have to impose the following assumption on
(\mathcal{X},d,\preceq)
in order to show Lemma
3.1
.

Assumption B.1
.

The ordered metric space
(\mathcal{X},d,\preceq)
is separable,
T_{1}
-ordered, possesses a convex topology and for each
x\in\mathcal{X}
, there exists sequences
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},(x^{\prime}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x\}
with
x_{n}\uparrow x
and
x^{\prime}_{n}\downarrow x
.

The following lemma is used to show Lemma
3.1
.

Lemma B.1
.

Consider an ordered metric space
(\mathcal{X},d,\preceq)
that satisfies Assumption
B.1
.
Then, the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathcal{X}
is generated by the family of intervals

\mathcal{E}=\big\{[x,y]\mid x,y\in\mathcal{X},x\preceq y\},

and by the family of all measurable upper sets, that is

\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})=\sigma(\mathcal{E})=\sigma(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\cap\mathscr{U}).

Proof.

For
\epsilon>0
and a set
B\subseteq\mathcal{X}
consider its
\epsilon
-envelope

B^{\epsilon}=\big\{x\in\mathcal{X}\mid d(x,B)<\epsilon\big\}\subseteq\mathcal{X},

which is an open set which contains
B
and where the distance of any element
x\in\mathcal{X}
to the set
B
is defined as
d(x,B)=\underset{y\in B}{\inf}\hskip 2.84544ptd(x,y)
.
Consider
(\epsilon_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
with
\epsilon_{n}\downarrow 0
. Since
\mathcal{X}
is
T_{1}
-ordered, we have for any
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
with
x\preceq y

[x,y]=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[x,y]^{\epsilon_{n}}\in\sigma(\mathscr{O})=\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}).

Conversely, consider
U\subseteq\mathcal{X}
open and pick for any
x\in U
a convex neighbourhood
C_{x}
that is contained in
U
, which exists by Assumption
B.1
. For any
x\in U
, there exist sequences
(x^{\prime}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},(\tilde{x}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x\}
such that
x^{\prime}_{n}\uparrow x
and
\tilde{x}_{n}\downarrow x
. Hence for any
x\in U
there exists
N_{x}\geq 0
such that
x^{\prime}_{n}\in C_{x}
and
\tilde{x}_{n}\in C_{x}
for all
n\geq N_{x}
. Hence,
[x_{N_{x}}^{\prime},\tilde{x}_{N_{x}}]
is a convex neighbourhood of
x
that is contained in
\sigma(\mathcal{E})
according to the considerations above. We have
U=\bigcup_{x\in U}[x^{\prime}_{N_{x}},\tilde{x}_{N_{x}}]
and by separability, we may pick a countable sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq U
such that
U=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[x^{\prime}_{N_{x_{n}}},\tilde{x}_{N_{x_{n}}}]\in\sigma(\mathcal{E})
. Therefore,
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})=\sigma(\mathcal{E})
.

Clearly,
[x,y]\in\sigma(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\cap\mathscr{U})
for any
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
with
x\preceq y
, since it is the intersection of a closed upper set and the complement of an open upper set. Hence,
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})=\sigma(\mathcal{E})\subseteq\sigma(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\cap\mathscr{U})
. The other inclusion holds trivially.
∎

It is easy to verify that
\mathbb{R}^{p}
with the componentwise order and the Euclidean metric and
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
with the stochastic order and the weak topology satisfy Assumption
B.1
, where we refer to
Hong 2014
or
Bogachev 2018
for the fact that the weak topology on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
is convex.

Statistical depth functions measure centrality in Euclidean space with respect to a given (empirical) distribution by assigning each point a value in
[0,1]
, see e.g.
Mosler and Mozharovskyi 2022
.
Hence, statistical depth functions may be used to identify points in some general multivariate space as elements in some bounded interval, which clearly satisfies Assumption
B.1
.

Appendix D
Probabilistic calibration of ICL

Let
Y
be a random variable and
F
a random distribution. Suppose that the distribution
F
is almost surely absolutely continuous and we consider it as a probabilistic forecast for
Y
. It is called
probabilistically calibrated
for
Y
if the probability integral transform
F(Y)
is following a standard uniform distribution
(
Gneiting and Ranjan 2013
)
. If the predictive distribution is not absolutely continuous, it is called
probabilistically calibrated
if, with a suitable randomization at jump points of
F
, the probability integral transform is standard uniform. The following result shows that this is the case for ICL, and is due to Alexander Henzi. The interested reader may like to compare the proposition to
Gneiting and Resin 2022
.

Adapting the definition of lower-quantile calibration given at (
10
), we call
F

upper-quantile calibrated
if

F_{u}^{-1}(\alpha)=\tilde{q}_{\alpha}\big(Y\mid F_{u}^{-1}(\alpha)\big)\quad\textrm{almost surely for all }\alpha\in(0,1),

where
F_{u}^{-1}
denotes the upper-quantile function
F_{u}^{-1}(\alpha)=\sup\{z\in\mathbb{R}\mid F(z)\leq\alpha\}
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and
\tilde{q}_{\alpha}(Y\mid F_{u}^{-1}(\alpha))
denotes the upper
\alpha
-quantile of the conditional law of
Y
given
F_{u}^{-1}(\alpha)
. One might show that ICL is upper-quantile calibrated exactly analogously as in Section
6.3
by choosing the sequence of minimizing sets given before Lemma
6.7
.

Proposition D.1
.

Let
Y
be a random variable and
F
a random predictive distribution for
Y
, which is lower- and upper-quantile calibrated. Then,

\mathbb{P}\big(F(Y)<\alpha\big)\leq\alpha\leq\mathbb{P}\big(F(Y-)\leq\alpha\big),\quad\textrm{for all }\alpha\in(0,1).

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Since
F
is lower-quantile calibrated and

if and only if
Y<F^{-1}(\alpha)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(F(Y)<\alpha\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(Y<F^{-1}(\alpha)\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(\mathbb{P}\big(Y<F^{-1}(\alpha)\mid F^{-1}(\alpha)\big)\big)\leq\alpha.

Analogously by upper-quantile calibration of

and since

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(F(Y-)\leq\alpha\big)
