On Optimal Coreset Construction for Euclidean ( k , z ) (k,z) -Clustering
Lingxiao Huang, Jian Li, Xuan Wu

Abstract
Constructing small-sized coresets for various clustering problems in Euclidean spaces has attracted significant attention for the past decade. A central problem in the coreset literature is to understand what is the best possible coreset size for ( k , z ) (k,z) -Clustering in Euclidean space. While there has been significant progress in the problem, there is still a gap between the state-of-the-art upper and lower bounds. For instance, the best known upper bound for k k -Means ( z = 2 z=2 ) is min ⁡ { O ⁡ ( k 3 / 2 ​ ε − 2 ) , O ⁡ ( k ​ ε − 4 ) } \min\{O(k^{3/2}\varepsilon^{-2}),O(k\varepsilon^{-4})\} [Cohen-Addad, Larsen, Saulpic, Schwiegelshohn, Sheikh-Omar. NeurIPS’22 [ 15 ] ], while the best known lower bound is Ω ⁡ ( k ​ ε − 2 ) \Omega(k\varepsilon^{-2}) [Cohen-Addad, Larsen, Saulpic, Schwiegelshohn. STOC’22 [ 14 ] ]. In this paper, we make significant progress on both lower and upper bounds on the coreset size for Euclidean ( k , z ) (k,z) -Clustering . For a wide range of parameters (i.e., ε , k \varepsilon,k ), we have a complete understanding of the optimal bound. In particular, we obtain the following results: (1) We present a new coreset lower bound Ω ⁡ ( k ​ ε − z − 2 ) \Omega(k\varepsilon^{-z-2}) for Euclidean ( k , z ) (k,z) -Clustering when ε ≥ Ω ⁡ ( k − 1 / ( z + 2 ) ) \varepsilon\geq\Omega(k^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{(z+2)}}}) . In view of the prior upper bound O ~ z ​ ( k ​ ε − z − 2 ) \tilde{O}_{z}(k\varepsilon^{-z-2}) [ 14 ] , the bound is optimal. The new lower bound is surprising since Ω ⁡ ( k ​ ε − 2 ) \Omega(k\varepsilon^{-2}) [ 14 ] is “conjectured” to be the correct bound in some recent works (see e.g., [ 14 , 15 ] ). Our new lower bound instance is a delicate construction with multiple clusters of points, which differs significantly from the previous construction in [ 14 ] that contains a single cluster of points. The new lower bound also implies improved lower bounds for ( k , z ) (k,z) -Clustering in doubling metrics. (2) For the upper bound, we provide efficient coreset construction algorithms for Euclidean ( k , z ) (k,z) -Clustering with improved coreset sizes. In particular, we provide an O ~ z ​ ( k 2 ​ z + 2 z + 2 ​ ε − 2 ) \tilde{O}_{z}(k^{\frac{2z+2}{z+2}}\varepsilon^{-2}) -sized coreset, with a unified analysis, for ( k , z ) (k,z) -Clustering for all z ≥ 1 z\geq 1 in Euclidean space. This upper bound improves upon the O ~ z ​ ( k 2 ​ ε − 2 ) \tilde{O}_{z}(k^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}) upper bound by [ 14 ] (when k ≤ ε − 1 k\leq\varepsilon^{-1} ), and matches the recent results [ 15 ] for k k -Median and k k -Means ( z = 1 , 2 z=1,2 ) and extends them to all z ≥ 1 z\geq 1 .

Introduction

We study the problem of constructing small-sized coresets for the classic
(k,z)
-Clustering
problem,
where
z\geq 1
is a given constant.
The
(k,z)
-Clustering
problem is defined as follows.

(k,z)
-Clustering
.

In the
(k,z)
-Clustering
problem, the input consists of a metric space
(\mathcal{X},d)
, where
\mathcal{X}
is a ground set (continuous or discrete) and
d:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a distance function, and a dataset
P\subseteq\mathcal{X}
of
n
points.
The goal is to find a set
C\subseteq\mathcal{X}
of
k
points, called
center set
,
that minimizes the objective function

\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P,C):=\sum_{x\in P}{d^{z}(x,C)},

(1)

where

d(x,C):=\min\left\{d(x,c):c\in C\right\}

is the distance from
x
to center set
C
and

d^{z}
denotes the distance raised to power
z\geq 1
.

This formulation captures several classical clustering problems,
including the well studied
k
-Median
(when
z=1
) and
k
-Means
(when
z=2
).
We mainly focus on the problem under Euclidean metric
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and more generally metrics with bounded doubling dimension.
The
(k,z)
-Clustering
problem has numerous applications in a variety of domains, including data analysis, approximation algorithms, unsupervised learning and computational geometry (see e.g.,
[
33
,
42
,
1
,
11
]
).

Coresets.

Motivated by the ever increasing volume of data, a powerful data-reduction technique, called
coresets
, has been developed for harnessing large datasets for various problems
[
23
,
20
,
21
]
.
Roughly speaking, for an optimization problem, a coreset is a small-sized subset of (weighted) data points that can be used to compute an approximation of the optimization objective for every possible solution.
In the context of
(k,z)
-Clustering
, we use
\mathcal{X}^{k}
to denote the collection of all solutions, i.e., all

k
-center sets in
\mathcal{X}
.
A coreset for
(k,z)
-Clustering
is formally defined as follows.

Definition 1.1
(Coreset
[
31
,
20
]
)

Given a metric space
(\mathcal{X},d)
together with a dataset
P\subseteq\mathcal{X}
of
n
points, an
\varepsilon
-coreset for the
(k,z)
-Clustering
problem is a weighted subset
S\subseteq P
with weight
w:S\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, such that

\forall C\in\mathcal{X}^{k},\qquad\sum_{x\in S}{w(x)\cdot d^{z}(x,C)}\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P,C).

(2)

From the above definition, one can see that if we run any existing approximation or exact algorithm on the coreset instead of the full dataset, the resulting solution provides almost the same performance guarantee in terms of the clustering objective, but the running time can be much smaller.
Hence, constructing small-sized coresets for
(k,z)
-Clustering
has been an important research topic and studied extensively in the literature, for various metric spaces including Euclidean metrics

1.2
Technical Overview

1.2.1
Lower bounds

For convenience, we present an overview of our lower bound for Euclidean
k
-Means
(
z=2
).

Prior lower bound instances
[
14
]
.

We first briefly review the construction for the best-known
lower bound
\Omega(k\varepsilon^{-2})
in
[
14
]
.
Their instance is simply the set of unit basis vectors
X=\left\{e_{i}:i\in[k\varepsilon^{-2}]\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k\varepsilon^{-2}}
.
To prove the lower bound, we assume by contradiction there exists an
\varepsilon
-coreset
S\subseteq X
of size at most
|X|/2
.
We arbitrarily partition
S
into
k
groups, each containing
roughly equal number of points (
\approx|S|/k
).
W.l.o.g., assume one group is
e_{1},\ldots,e_{|S|/k}
and we construct a center
c=\sqrt{k/|S|}\sum_{i\in[|S|/k]}e_{i}

for this group.
We can observe that

d^{2}(e_{1},c)=\cdots=d^{2}(e_{|S|/k},c)=1-\sqrt{k/|S|},

but for every
i>|S|/k
,
d^{2}(e_{i},c)=1+\sqrt{k/|S|}
.
We repeat the above construction for each group, and obtain a
k
-center set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which satisfies that 1)
d^{2}(p,C)=1-\sqrt{k/|S|}
for every
p\in S
and
2)
d^{2}(q,C)=1+\sqrt{k/|S|}
for every
q\in X\setminus S
.
Noting that
d^{2}(p,C)<(1-\varepsilon)d^{2}(q,C)
for any

p\in S
and
q\in X\setminus S
,
we can conclude that
\mathrm{cost}_{2}(S,C)<(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{2}(X,C)
,
and hence
S
is not an
\varepsilon
-coreset of
X
.

A failed attempt.

A natural idea to improve the lower bound
is to construct a dataset
P
that consists of
m\leq k/2
“independent” copies of the above instance
X
such that they are far away from each other, and attempt to argue that a constant proportion of points in every copy must be selected to form a coreset (see e.g.,
[
2
]
).
Again, by contradiction we assume there exists an
\varepsilon
-coreset
S\subseteq P
of size at most
|P|/2
.

We can assume without loss of generality that
w(S\cap X_{i})\approx|X_{i}|
holds for every copy
X_{i}
of
X
.
Otherwise, we can construct a center set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
that contains the origin of every copy
except
X_{i}
, and the remaining centers are far away from all points in
P
.
In this case, the clustering cost is mainly from
X_{i}
and
\frac{\mathrm{cost}_{2}(S,C)}{\mathrm{cost}_{2}(X,C)}\approx\frac{w(S\cap X_{i})}{|X_{i}|}\notin[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]
, which contradicts the assumption that
S
is a coreset.
Next, there must exist a copy
X_{i}
of
X
such that
|S\cap X_{i}|\leq|X_{i}|/2
.
We may construct a
k
-center set
C
as follows: first, construct

k-m+1
(
\geq k/2
) centers for this copy
X_{i}
such that the distance gap between
d(p,C)
for
p\in S\cap X_{i}
and

d(q,C)
for
q\in X_{i}\setminus S
is as large as possible;
we also place a center at the origin of every remaining copy of
X
except
X_{i}
.

Recall
|X_{i}|=k\varepsilon^{-2}
.
By applying the same construction of center set in
[
14
]
, we can construct
k-m+1
centers in
C
for
X_{i}
such that
the induced error on
X_{i}
is

\left|\mathrm{cost}_{2}(X_{i},C)-\mathrm{cost}_{2}(S\cap X_{i},C)\right|=\Omega(\sqrt{k/|S\cap X_{i}|}\cdot k\varepsilon^{-2})=\Omega(k\varepsilon^{-1}).

On the other hand, we can see that

\mathrm{cost}_{2}(P,C)=\Theta(mk\varepsilon^{-2})\gg\mathrm{cost}_{2}(X_{i},C)=k\varepsilon^{-2}
.
Hence, the induced error
\Omega(k\varepsilon^{-1})
on
X_{i}
is of order
much smaller than
\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{2}(P,C)
,
and we cannot conclude that
S
is not an
\varepsilon
-coreset.

Another attempt is to construct
k-m+1
centers for
X_{i}
such that

P1.

\mathrm{cost}_{2}(X_{i},C)=\Theta(mk\varepsilon^{-2})
, i.e., ensuring
\mathrm{cost}_{2}(X_{i},C)
and
\mathrm{cost}_{2}(P\setminus X_{i},C)
are in the same order;

P2.

\left|\mathrm{cost}_{2}(X_{i},C)-\mathrm{cost}_{2}(S\cap X_{i},C)\right|=\Theta(m\sqrt{k/|S\cap X_{i}|}\cdot k\varepsilon^{-2})=\Theta(mk\varepsilon^{-1})
, i.e., the induced error on
X_{i}
is proportional to that of
\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{2}(X_{i},C)
.

Unfortunately, under P1, all points in
X_{i}
are relatively close to each other (with distance
O(1)
), and hence there is no center set
C
that can make
|\mathrm{cost}_{2}(X_{i},C)-\mathrm{cost}_{2}(S\cap X_{i},C)|
large enough.

Our lower bound instance.

Motivated by the above attempts, we aim to construct an instance
X
such that both P1 and P2 hold.
Our key idea is to construct multiple clusters and ensure that each center can affect
\varepsilon^{-2}
points
from different clusters

(instead from one cluster as in
[
14
]
)!
For convenience, we discuss the case of
\varepsilon=\Theta(k^{-1/4})
and
describe the construction of an instance
P
for which the coreset size is at least

\Omega(k\varepsilon^{-4})=\Omega(k^{2})
.

We first construct a point set
X
and the instance
P
consists of
m=\Theta(\varepsilon^{-2})=\Theta(k^{1/2})
copies of
X
that are far apart.
Let
X
consist of
B=100k^{1/2}
clusters where the
j
-th cluster is centered at a scaled basis vector
a^{\star}_{j}=\varepsilon^{-1}e_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{B}
.
Next, we carefully select
k/2
unit vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k/2}\in\mathbb{R}^{B}
that are almost orthogonal to each other.
Every point in
X
is of the form
a^{\star}_{j}+v_{i}
for some
j\in[B]
and
i\in[k/2]
.
There are total
mB=k/2
clusters (for all copies).
We ensure that every
v_{i}
uniformly appears in
0.5k^{1/2}
clusters.
Now let
S\subseteq X
be any subset of size at most
|X|/2
.
We show it is possible to create a center
c_{i}
for every
i\in[k/2]
which affects all points in different clusters with the same offset
v_{i}
(
2.2
).
More concretely, for every
p=a^{\star}_{j}+v_{i}\in X
,

1.

c_{i}
is the closest center of
p
among all created centers
\left\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{k/2}\right\}
;

2.

For any
p\in S
and
q\in X\setminus S
,
d^{2}(p,c_{i})-d^{2}(q,c_{i})=\Theta(\varepsilon^{-1})
;

Consequently, the total induced error of these centers is

\left|\mathrm{cost}_{2}(X,C)-\mathrm{cost}_{2}(S,C)\right|=\Theta(\varepsilon^{-1}|X|)=\Theta(k^{7/4}),

while
\mathrm{cost}_{2}(X,C)=\Theta(\varepsilon^{-2}|X|)=\Theta(k^{2})
.
Hence both P1 and P2 hold, which implies that
S
is not an
\varepsilon
-coreset.

1.2.2
Upper Bounds

Next, we provide a high level overview for the analysis of
our upper bound in Theorem
1.4
.
Our algorithm is based on a recent importance sampling framework (Algorithm
1
), developed in
[
18
]
.
In the analysis, it suffices to bound the number of samples

\Gamma_{G}
for each group
G
, which consists of at most
k
rings, each having similar cost.

\Gamma_{G}
should be large enough to ensure the uniform convergence of
|\sum_{p\in S_{G}}w(p)\cdot d_{z}(p,C)-\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C)|
for all center sets
C
(see Lemma
A.12
).
To show the uniform convergence bound,

[
14
]
adopted a chaining argument
for tuples
u^{C}=(d(p,C))_{p\in G}
over all center sets
C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
in Euclidean metrics
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Roughly speaking, they construct
2^{-h}
-coverings
V_{h}\subset\mathbb{R}^{|G|}_{\geq 0}
at different scales
h\geq 0
, and bound the covering number
|V_{h}|
and the maximum variance of
\mathrm{Var}_{C,h}
for each scale.
Their method leads to an upper bound of the sample number
\Gamma_{G}
(ignoring polylog factor) being at most

k\varepsilon^{-2}\cdot\min\left\{\varepsilon^{-z},k\right\}
.
The unexpected term
\min\left\{\varepsilon^{-z},k\right\}
appears due to the upper bound for the variance

\mathrm{Var}_{C,h}\approx 2^{-2h}\cdot\min\left\{\varepsilon^{-z},k\right\}
.

A new chaining argument.

Our main improvement is to apply the chaining argument for
y^{C}=(d^{z}(p,C)-d^{z}(a^{\star}_{i},C))_{i,p}
instead of

u^{C}=(d(p,C))_{p\in G}
(Lemma
A.17
) and construct coverings
V_{h}
s for tuples
y^{C}
instead of
u^{C}
.
The benefit of introducing
y^{C}
is that
|d^{z}(p,C)-d^{z}(a^{\star}_{i},C)|\leq O(z)\cdot d(p,a^{\star}_{i})\cdot(d^{z-1}(p,C)+d^{z-1}(a^{\star}_{i},C))
by
triangle inequality, which can be much smaller than
d^{z}(p,C)
.
Moreover, considering the space of
y^{C}
enables us to design a
tighter and unified relative covering error

\mathsf{err}(p,C)
for all powers
z\geq 1
(see Definition
A.8
) than the previous one defined in
[
14
]
(which is
d^{z}(p,C)+d^{z}(p,A^{\star})
), and the new relative covering error is key to achieving a smaller variance upper bound (Lemma
A.22
) for the chaining argument.
Specifically, our variance
\mathrm{Var}_{C,h}
is exactly proportional to
2^{2h}

and avoids the additional term
\min\left\{\varepsilon^{-z},k\right\}
.

Bounding the covering number.

The remaining issue is to bound the covering number
|V_{h}|
s with respect to the new relative covering error
\mathsf{err}(p,C)
.
To bound the size of
2^{-h}
-coverings
V_{h}
,
we introduce a new dimension reduction notion, called

additive terminal embedding
(see Definition
3.1
),
which embeds a ring of radius
r>0
to a low dimensional space, and the required distance distortion should be an additive error
2^{-h}r
(proportional to the radius), instead of
a multiplicative error as in the ordinary terminal embedding.
Using the approach developed in
[
35
,
37
]
,
we can prove that the target dimension of additive terminal embedding can be bounded by
\tilde{O}(2^{-2h})
(Theorem
B.5
).
The new dimension reduction technique allows us to further reduce the target dimensions, which leads to better covering number
\exp(\tilde{O}(k^{\frac{2z+2}{z+2}}2^{2h}))
.
Consequently, the required sample number
\Gamma_{G}
is at most
\tilde{O}(k^{\frac{2z+2}{z+2}}\varepsilon^{-2})
(see Theorem
1.4
).
The notion of additive terminal embedding might be useful in
other contexts.

Proof of

In this subsection, we prove the first part of

(for

-coreset of

Proof:

For the convenience of analysis, we first consider the special case of
k
-Means
(

) and
\varepsilon=0.01k^{-1/4}
.
Later we show how to extend the proof to general

and
\varepsilon\geq\Omega(k^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{(z+2)}}})
.
We first show the construction of the lower bound instance
P
.

Construction of
P
.

W.l.o.g., assume
k^{1/4}
is an even integer
\geq 10000
.
Let
B=100k^{1/2}
and
t=k^{1/4}
.
Let
A^{\star}=\left\{a^{\star}_{j}=te_{j}:j\in[B]\right\}
be a collection of scaled basis vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{B}
.
We construct a family of
k/2
subsets
B_{1},\ldots,B_{k/2}\subseteq B
satisfying that

1.

for every

|B_{i}|=k^{1/2}

2.

for every

|B_{i}\cap B_{i^{\prime}}|\leq 0.1k^{1/2}

Indeed, the existence of such a family is well known (e.g.,
similar design is used the celebrated Nisan-Wigderson generator
[
39
]
), and it can be easily
constructed with nonzero probability by i.i.d. randomly sampling

’s from

. Then we know that for every
i\neq i^{\prime}\in[k/2]
, the expected size of intersection
\mathbb{E}\left[|B_{i}\cap B_{i^{\prime}}|\right]=0.01k^{1/2}
, which implies that

\Pr\left[|B_{i}\cap B_{i^{\prime}}|>0.1k^{1/2}\right]\leq e^{-10^{-4}k^{1/2}}

by Chernoff bound.
Consequently, we have that

\Pr\left[|B_{i}\cap B_{i^{\prime}}|\leq 0.1k^{1/2},\forall i\neq i^{\prime}\in[k/2]\right]\geq 1-10^{4}k\cdot e^{-10^{-4}k^{1/2}}>0.5

by the union bound and the assumption that

.
Hence, we can construct such a family of
k/2
subsets
B_{1},\ldots,B_{k/2}\subseteq B
as desired.

Given a subset
A\subseteq B
, let
e_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{B}
denote the vector with
e_{A,i}=1
if
i\in A
and
e_{A,i}=0
if
i\notin A
.
For every
i\in[k]
, arbitrarily divide
B_{i}
into
B_{i}=B_{i}^{+}\cup B_{i}^{-}
where
|B_{i}^{+}|=|B_{i}^{-}|=0.5k^{1/2}
.
We construct a dataset

to be

X:=\left\{p=a_{j}^{\star}+\frac{1}{t}(e_{B_{i}^{+}}-e_{B_{i}^{-}}):i\in[k/2],j\in B_{i}^{+}\right\},

i.e., we create a point for every
B_{i}
and every

.
By construction, we know that
|X|=0.25k^{3/2}
.
Also, note that every
p=a_{j}^{\star}+\frac{1}{t}(e_{B_{i}^{+}}-e_{B_{i}^{-}})\in X
satisfies

\|p\|_{2}^{2}=(t+\frac{1}{t})^{2}+(k^{1/2}-1)\cdot\frac{1}{t^{2}}=t^{2}+3\text{ and }\|p-a_{j}^{\star}\|^{2}=1.

Our final construction of
P
is to make
\frac{k}{2B}
copies of
X
in
\mathbb{R}^{B}
, say
X_{1},\ldots,X_{\frac{k}{2B}}
such that every two copies
X_{i},X_{j}
is sufficiently far from each other.
Let
o_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{B}
be the “origin” of each
X_{l}
.
Note that
|P|=\Theta(k^{2})
and
d(o_{i},o_{j})\rightarrow+\infty
for
i\neq j\in[\frac{k}{2B}]
.

Coreset lower bound of
P
.

Next, we show that any
\varepsilon
-coreset of

is of size at least
\Omega(k\varepsilon^{-z-2})=\Omega(k^{2})
.
By contradiction assume that
(S,w)
is an
\varepsilon
-coreset of
P
with
|S|=o(k^{2})
.
For every copy
X_{l}
(
l\in[\frac{k}{2B}]
), let

.
We first have the following claim.

Claim 2.1
(Weights of
S_{i}
)

For every
l\in[\frac{k}{2B}]
, we have
w(S_{l})\in(1\pm 2\varepsilon)\cdot|X_{l}|

Proof:

The proof is easy.
By contradiction assume w.l.o.g.
w(S_{1})\notin(1\pm 2\varepsilon)\cdot|X_{1}|
.
We construct a
k
-center set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
as follows:

1.

for every
l\in[\frac{k}{2B}]\setminus\{1\}
and
i\in[B]

c
at the copy of
a^{\star}_{j}
in
X_{l}
;

2.

for
X_{1}

c^{\star}
such that
d(o_{1},c^{\star})=100kt\varepsilon^{-1}
.

Note that the above procedure only constructs
\frac{k}{2}-B+1

We let all remaining centers of

be located at

.
Observe that
\mathrm{cost}_{2}(P,C)\approx|X_{1}|\cdot d^{2}(o_{1},c^{\star})

\square

By the pigeonhole principle, there must exist some
l\in[\frac{k}{2B}]
such that
|S_{l}|=o(|X_{l}|)=o(k^{3/2})

W.l.o.g., we assume

Now we construct a
k

We first construct
k/2-B

By construction, the points in the cluster around

.
It is not difficult to see that

\displaystyle\mathrm{cost}_{2}(X\setminus X_{1},C)\leq k^{2},\text{ and }\mathrm{cost}_{2}(S\setminus S_{1},C)\leq k^{2},

(3)

since there are at most

and each point

.
In the following, we only consider the case that

.
The argument for the reverse case is symmetric.

For every
i\in[k/2]
, we define
X_{1,i}:=\left\{p=a_{j}^{\star}+\frac{1}{t}(e_{B_{i}^{+}}-e_{B_{i}^{-}}):j\in B_{i}^{+}\right\}

for

.

We will construct
k/2

Now, we describe the construction of

For every

for the remaining

By construction, for every

We also have the following claim.

Claim 2.2
(Properties of

)

Let

for some

.

.

\square

New Lower Bounds for Doubling Spaces: Proof of Corollary
1.3

In this subsection, we extend the lower bounds in Theorem
1.2
to doubling metrics.

Proof:

[Proof of Corollary
1.3
]
We first show the coreset lower bound
\Omega(k\cdot\mathrm{ddim}\cdot\varepsilon^{-\max\{z,2\}}/\log k)
when
\varepsilon\geq\Omega(k^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{(z+2)}}})
.
For
1\leq z\leq 2
, it is known that
\Omega(k\cdot\mathrm{ddim}\cdot\varepsilon^{-2})
is a lower bound by
[
14
]
.
Thus, we only need to consider the case that
z>2
.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the instance constructed in the proof of Theorem
1.2
that consists of
\Theta(k\varepsilon^{-z-2})
points.
We perform an
O(z^{-1}\varepsilon)
-terminal embedding
f
on
P
(see Section
B.2
) and obtain an embedded dataset
f(P)\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfying that

1.

m=O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log(k\varepsilon^{-1}))=O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log k)
since
\varepsilon\geq\Omega(k^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{(z+2)}}})
;

2.

for every point
p\in P
and
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d^{z}(p,q)\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\cdot d^{z}(f(p),f(q))
.

It is well known that the doubling dimension of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is at most
\mathrm{ddim}\leq O(m)
(see e.g.,
[
45
]
).
We assume by contradiction that there exists an
\varepsilon
-coreset
f(S)\subseteq f(P)
of size at most

o(k\cdot\mathrm{ddim}\cdot\varepsilon^{-z}/\log k)\leq o(k\cdot m\cdot\varepsilon^{-z}/\log k)\leq o(k\cdot\varepsilon^{-z-2})

for the embedded instance
f(P)
for
(k,z)
-Clustering
.
Since
f
is an
O(z^{-1}\varepsilon)
-terminal embedding, we know that
S
is an
O(\varepsilon)
-coreset of the original instance

for
(k,z)
-Clustering

However,

1.2
.
Hence,
any
\varepsilon
-coreset of

\Omega(k\cdot\mathrm{ddim}\cdot\varepsilon^{-\max\{z,2\}}/\log k)
.

The argument for the second lower bound
\Omega(k^{\frac{2z+1}{z+2}}\cdot\mathrm{ddim}\cdot\varepsilon^{-1})

We can derive this lower bound from the proof of Theorem

3
Improved Coreset Upper Bounds for
(k,z)
-Clustering

In this section, we introduce the main ideas for our analysis of the upper bound and discuss some key technical differences between ours and prior work
[
14
,
15
]
.
For completeness, we provide all
technical details in the appendix.

We first briefly review the framework of prior work
[
14
,
15
]
.
Let
A^{\star}=\left\{a_{1}^{\star},\ldots,a_{k}^{\star}\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an
O(1)
-approximation of
P
for
(k,z)
-Clustering
.
For every
i\in[k]
, let
P_{i}:=\left\{p\in P:a_{i}^{\star}=\arg\min_{c\in A^{\star}}d(p,c)\right\}
denote the induced cluster of
P
whose closest center is
a_{i}^{\star}
.
Cohen-addad et al.
[
18
,
14
,
15
]
showed that we can make the following simplifying assumption without loss of generality.

1.

The number of distinct points
\|P\|_{0}
is at most
2^{O(z)}\cdot\operatorname{poly}(k\varepsilon^{-1})
;

2.

For every
i\in[k]
and every
p,q\in P_{i}
,
d^{z}(p,A^{\star})\leq 2d^{z}(q,A^{\star})
;

3.

For every
i,j\in[k]
,
d^{z}(P_{i},A^{\star})\leq 2d^{z}(P_{j},A^{\star})
.

The first assumption is via the iterative size reduction approach
[
7
]
.
The second assumption ensures that the distances of all points in
P_{i}
to
a_{i}^{\star}
are close.
This structure is called a
ring
in
[
18
,
14
]
.
The third assumption ensures that the clustering cost of every partition
P_{i}
to
A^{\star}
is close.
Combining these assumptions, dataset
P
consists of “balanced” rings, which is called
a main group
in
[
18
,
14
]
.
Cohen-addad et al.
[
18
]
showed that a general dataset can be decomposed into
\tilde{O}_{z}(1)
main groups, where
\tilde{O}_{z}(1)=\operatorname{poly}(z,\log k,\log\varepsilon^{-1})
.
Consequently, if there exists a coreset for a main group of size
t
, the coreset size without these data assumptions is at most
\tilde{O}_{z}(t)
.
Then the most essential part is to analyze the coreset size for a main group (under the aforementioned assumptions).

The key of our analysis is to show that a collection
S
of
\Gamma=2^{O(z)}\cdot\tilde{O}(k^{\frac{2z+2}{z+2}}\varepsilon^{-2})
samples from
P
is an
\varepsilon
-coreset, where each sample
p\in P
is selected with probability
\frac{d^{z}(p,A^{\star})}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P,A^{\star})}
and weighted by
w(p)=\frac{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P,A^{\star})}{\Gamma\cdot d^{z}(p,A^{\star})}
.
It suffices to bound the (expected) estimation error of the sample
S
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S}\sup_{C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}}\left[|\mathrm{cost}_{2}(P,C)-\mathrm{cost}_{2}(S,C)|\right]\leq\frac{\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P,C+A^{\star})}{\tilde{O}_{z}(1)},

(4)

where
\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P,C+A^{\star}):=\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P,C)+\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P,A^{\star})
.
Theorem
1.4
is a direct corollary of the above lemma by the Markov inequality.
To prove the above inequality, we also adopt a chaining argument, which is different from the one in
[
14
,
15
]
.

Define
r_{i}:=\min_{p\in P_{i}}d^{z}(p,a_{i}^{\star})
for every
i\in[k]
.
2
2

2

r_{i}
is referred to as
2^{j}\Delta_{i}
in Appendix
A.1
.

Given a subset
B\subseteq[k]
and an integer
\beta\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{X}^{k}_{B,\beta}
denote the collection of center sets
C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
satisfying
2^{\beta}\cdot r_{i}\leq d^{z}(a_{i}^{\star},C)<2^{\beta+1}\cdot r_{i}
for every
i\in B
.
We say a set
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{|S|}
of
cost vectors
is an
\alpha
-covering
of
S
if for each
C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}_{B,\beta}
, there exists a cost vector
v\in V
such that for any
i\in B
and
p\in P_{i}\cap S
, the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\left|d^{z}(p,C)-d^{z}(a^{\star}_{i},C)-v_{p}\right|\leq\alpha\cdot\mathsf{err}(p,C),

where
\mathsf{err}(p,C)
is the
relative covering error
of
p
to
C
defined as follows

\displaystyle\mathsf{err}(p,C):=\sqrt{d^{z}(p,C)\cdot d^{z}(p,A^{\star})}+d^{z}(p,A^{\star}))\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,A^{\star})}}.

The key is to bound the covering number
|V_{\alpha}|
.

We remark that our notion of covering is different from that in prior work
[
14
,
15
]
.
For instance, our definition has the following differences from
[
14
, Definition 3]
, which are crucial for improving the coreset sizes.
One difference is that our covering is constructed on the distance difference
d^{z}(p,C)-d^{z}(a^{\star}_{i},C)
instead of
d^{z}(p,C)
, which is upper bounded by
z\cdot d(p,a^{\star}_{i})\cdot(d^{z-1}(p,C)+d^{z-1}(p,a^{\star}_{i}))
by the triangle inequality.
The other one is that our relative covering error
\mathsf{err}(p,C)
is proportional to
\sqrt{d^{z}(p,C)\cdot d^{z}(p,A^{\star})}
, which is typically larger (especially when
d(p,C)\gg d(p,A^{\star})
).
For comparison, the covering error defined in
[
14
, Definition 3]
is
d^{z}(p,C)+d^{z}(p,A^{\star})
.
Compared to
[
15
]
, our notion contains an additional factor
\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,A^{\star})}}
that is at least 1.
Our smaller error bound is essential for the improvement of coreset sizes, since its scale affects the variance of our sampling scheme (Lemma
A.22
) and the variance bound directly appears in the coreset size (Lemma
A.21
).
Specifically, our covering notion enables us to bound the variance of our sampling scheme by
\mathrm{Var}_{\alpha}=O(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\Gamma})
(Lemma
A.22
), saving a factor of
\min\left\{\varepsilon^{-z},k\right\}
compared to that in
[
14
, Lemma 23]
.
We remark that
[
15
]
also introduces coverings of
S
for
k
-Median
and
k
-Means
, but uses different covering error bounds.
However, it is unclear to us how to use their error bounds to obtain the same results for general power
z\geq 1
as ours.

By a standard chaining argument, it suffices to prove the existence of an
\alpha
-covering
V_{\alpha}
of
S
with

\displaystyle\log|V_{\alpha}|\leq\tilde{O}_{z}(1)\cdot k\alpha^{-2}\cdot\min\left\{2^{\beta z},1+k2^{-2\beta}\right\}.

(5)

By simple calculation, we have
\min\left\{2^{\beta z},1+k2^{-2\beta}\right\}\leq k^{\frac{z}{z+2}}
, where the equality can be achieved when
2^{\beta}=k^{1/(z+2)}
.
Then we can select the sample number
\Gamma
to be

\varepsilon^{-2}\cdot\mathrm{Var}_{\alpha}\cdot\log(|V_{\alpha}|)\approx k^{\frac{2z+2}{z+2}}\varepsilon^{-2}

such that Inequality (
4
) holds.
It remains to prove Inequality (
5
).
Note that the first bound
\log|V_{\alpha}|\leq\tilde{O}_{z}(1)\cdot k\alpha^{-2}\cdot 2^{\beta z}
has been proved in the literature by applying terminal embedding (see e.g.,
[
15
, Lemma 4]
).
Thus, we only need to prove the second bound
\log|V_{\alpha}|\leq\tilde{O}_{z}(1)\cdot k\alpha^{-2}\cdot(1+k2^{-2\beta})
.
To this end, we introduce a new notion for dimension reduction, called

additive terminal embedding
.

Definition 3.1
(Additive terminal embedding)

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
r>0
, and
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a collection of
n
points within a ball
B(0,r)
with
0^{d}\in X
.
A mapping
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is called an
\alpha
-additive terminal embedding of
X
if for any
p\in X
and
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
|d(p,q)-d(f(p),f(q))|\leq\alpha\cdot r
.

The main difference of the above definition from the classic terminal embedding
[
37
,
9
]
is that we consider an additive error
\alpha\cdot r
instead of a multiplicative error
\alpha\cdot d(p,q)
.
Our error is smaller for remote points
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B(0,2r)
.
Since
p\in B(0,r)
, we know that
d(p,q)\geq r
by the triangle inequality.
Hence,
\alpha\cdot r
is a smaller error compared to
\alpha\cdot d(p,q)
.
We have the following theorem showing that the target dimension of an additive terminal embedding is the same as the multiplicative version, using the same mapping as in
[
35
,
37
]
.
The proof can be found in Appendix
B.2
.

Theorem 3.2
(Additive terminal embedding)

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
r>0
, and
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a collection of
n
points within a ball
B(0,r)
with
0^{d}\in X
.
There exists an
\alpha
-additive terminal embedding with a target dimension
O(\alpha^{-2}\log n)
.

Now we prove
\log|V_{\alpha}|\leq\tilde{O}_{z}(1)\cdot k\alpha^{-2}\cdot(1+k2^{-2\beta})
.
Fix
C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}_{B,\beta}
and we have
\frac{\mathrm{cost}_{1}(P,C)}{\mathrm{cost}_{1}(P,A^{\star})}\geq\frac{|B|\cdot 2^{\beta}}{4k}
by definition.
For each
i\in B
, let
f_{i}
be an
\sqrt{|B|k^{-1}}2^{\beta-5z}\alpha
-additive terminal embedding of
P_{i}
into
m=O(|B|^{-1}k2^{10z-2\beta}\alpha^{-2}\log\|P\|_{0})
dimensions given by Theorem
3.2
.
By definition and some standard calculation, we obtain that

\displaystyle\quad\left|(d^{z}(f_{i}(p),f_{i}(C))-d^{z}(f_{i}(a^{\star}_{i}),f_{i}(C)))-(d^{z}(p,C)-d^{z}(a^{\star}_{i},C))\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad O(\alpha)\cdot\sqrt{d^{z}(p,C)\cdot d^{z}(p,A^{\star})}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P,C)}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P,A^{\star})}}=\alpha\cdot\mathsf{err}(p,C),

which implies that the covering number for a single cluster
P_{i}
is at most
\exp(\tilde{O}_{z}(km))
.
Hence, the covering number
|V_{\alpha}|
over all
|B|
clusters satisfy that

\log|V_{\alpha}|\leq|B|\cdot\tilde{O}_{z}(km)\leq\tilde{O}_{z}(1)\cdot k\alpha^{-2}\cdot(1+k2^{-2\beta}).

We remark that the target dimension
m
is proportional to
2^{-2\beta}
, indicating that the covering number of a cluster
P_{i}
decreases as the distance
d(a_{i}^{\star},C)
to
C
increases.
This new geometric observation is essential for us to reduce the covering number, which may be of independent interest together with the notion of additive terminal embedding.

4
Final Remarks

In this paper, we make significant progress on understanding the
optimal coreset sizes for Euclidean
(k,z)
-Clustering
, in terms of both upper and lower bounds. In particular, for Euclidean space, when

\varepsilon\geq\Omega(k^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{(z+2)}}})
, we prove a new coreset lower bound
\Omega(k\varepsilon^{-z-2})
, which matches the upper bound in
[
14
]
(upto poly-log factors).
For the upper bound, we provide efficient coreset construction algorithms with improved or optimal coreset sizes, matching the previous bounds for
k
-Median
and
k
-Means

[
15
]
and extending them to all powers
z\geq 1
.
In fact, before discovering our lower bound, we have tried several other methods to improve the covering number bound, including various dimension notions
(such as Natarajan dimension
[
38
,
22
]
)
and covering number bounds in
multiclass prediction (e.g.,
[
46
]
).

3
3

3

The multi-class classification problem (e.g., in
[
22
]
) considers the setting
where each function
f
in the hypothesis class
\mathcal{F}
maps a point
x\in X
to a
k
-dimensional vector

\{f_{1}(x),\ldots,f_{k}(x)\}
and assign
x
to class
y\in[k]
if
f_{y}(x)>\max_{i\neq y}f_{i}(x)
.
To show learnability and uniform convergence, an important component in this line of work is to bound the covering number of
\mathcal{F}
via suitably defined notion of dimensions (which generalize VC-dimension or fat-shattering dimension for binary classification).
The setting and the goal are very similar to our problem where we also aim to bound certain
covering number for all
k
-center sets
C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
. Each
k
-center set corresponds to a function which maps a point
p
to the
k
-dimensional distance vector
\{d(p,c_{1}),\ldots,d(p,c_{k}\}

and
p
is assigned to the
i
-th center if
d(p,i)\leq\min_{j\neq i}d(p,j)
.

However, all techniques
we have tried led to more or less the same upper bound (we omit the details), which led us to suspect
that
O(k\varepsilon^{-2})
may not be the optimal coreset size bound.
In our lower bound instance, we allow a center to influence points from various clusters, which
may be useful in other contexts, e.g., understanding the covering number bound and sample complexity in multiclass prediction.

Our work reveals several avenues for future investigation. Firstly, addressing the outstanding gap for
\varepsilon<\Omega(k^{-\frac{1}{(z+2)}})
in the context of
(k,z)
-Clustering
in Euclidean space remains a significant unsolved problem.
Secondly, it has been established that the coreset size for a set of base vectors is
\Theta(k\varepsilon^{-2})
, which is notably smaller than our derived general coreset lower bound. Exploring instance-optimal coreset size limits constitutes an intriguing pursuit.
Another promising direction involves examining whether storing a coreset represents the most efficient compression method in terms of space complexity for clustering tasks. A recent finding
[
47
]
demonstrated that the space complexity for Euclidean
(k,z)
-Clustering
is
\Omega(kd\varepsilon^{-2})
, suggesting that coreset is indeed the optimal compression approach when
k
is constant.
For the general case of varying
k
, it is worthwhile to explore whether our coreset lower bound construction can be extended to establish a space lower bound of
\Omega(kd\varepsilon^{-z-2})
. This extension would imply that, under the condition
\varepsilon<\Omega(k^{-\frac{1}{(z+2)}})
, the coreset is also the optimal compression scheme.

Appendix A
The Coreset Construction Algorithm for
(k,z)
-Clustering

In this section,
we present a unified coreset construction algorithm (Algorithm
1
) for
(k,z)
-Clustering
via importance sampling for all metric spaces considered in this paper.
Our algorithm largely follows the one proposed in
[
18
,
14
]
, with some minor variations.
For preparation, we provide the following relaxed triangle inequality.

Lemma A.1
(Relaxed triangle inequality)

Let
a,b,c\in\mathcal{X}
and
z\geq 1
.
For every
t\in(0,1]
, the following inequalities hold:

d^{z}(a,b)\leq(1+t)^{z-1}d^{z}(a,c)+(1+\frac{1}{t})^{z-1}d^{z}(b,c).

We first introduce some useful notations.

A.1
Partition into Rings and Groups

Let
A^{\star}=\left\{a^{\star}_{1},\ldots,a^{\star}_{k}\in\mathcal{X}\right\}\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
be a constant approximation for the
(k,z)
-Clustering
problem.
Let
P_{i}=\left\{x\in P:\arg\min_{j\in[k]}d(x,a_{j})=i\right\}
be the
i
-th cluster induced by
A^{\star}
(breaking ties arbitrarily).
For each
i\in[k]
, denote
\Delta_{i}:=\frac{1}{|P_{i}|}\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P_{i},A^{\star})
to be the average cost of
P_{i}
to
A^{\star}
.

Ring structure and group structure.

Following
[
18
,
14
]
,
we first partition the points into a collection of rings, and then group
some rings into the group structure, based on
P_{i}
s and
A^{\star}
.
We first partition each clusters
P_{i}
into rings according to the ratio
\frac{d^{z}(p,A^{\star})}{\Delta_{i}}
for
p\in P_{i}
.

Definition A.2
(Ring structure
[
18
,
14
]
)

For each
i\in[k]
and
j\in\mathcal{Z}
, define
ring

R_{ij}:=\left\{p\in P_{i}:2^{j}\Delta_{i}\leq d^{z}(p,A^{\star})<2^{j+1}\Delta_{i}\right\}
to be the set of points in
P_{i}
whose
z
-th power distances to
A^{\star}
is within
[2^{j}\Delta_{i},2^{j+1}\Delta_{i})
.
For each
\in\mathcal{Z}
, define
R(j):=\bigcup_{i\in[k]}R_{ij}
to be the union of all
j
-th level rings
R_{ij}
.
For each
P_{i}
, we divide rings in the following way:

•

If
j\leq z\log(\varepsilon/z)
, we call
R_{ij}
an
inner ring
, where each point
p\in R_{ij}
satisfies
d(p,A^{\star})\leq\varepsilon\Delta_{i}
.

•

Let
R^{(o)}_{i}=\bigcup_{j\geq 2z\log(z\varepsilon^{-1})}R_{ij}
denote an
outer ring
, where each point
p\in R_{ij}
satisfies
d(p,A^{\star})\geq\varepsilon^{-2}\Delta_{i}
.
Let
R^{(o)}=\bigcup_{i\in[k]}R^{(o)}_{i}
denote the collection of all points in outer rings.

•

If
z\log(\varepsilon/z)<j<2z\log(z\varepsilon^{-1})
, we call
R_{ij}
a
main ring
.

By merging rings
R
at the same level from different clusters with similar
\mathrm{cost}_{z}(R,A^{\star})
, we propose the following group structure.

Definition A.3
(Group structure)

For each integer
z\log(\varepsilon/z)<j<2z\log(z\varepsilon^{-1})
and
b\leq 0
, we
denote the interval

I(j,b)=[2^{b}\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{z}(R(j),A^{\star}),2^{b+1}\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{z}(R(j),A^{\star})

and define a
main group

G^{(m)}(j,b):=\bigcup_{i\in[k]:\mathrm{cost}_{z}(R_{ij},A^{\star})\in I(j,b)}R_{ij}

as the collection of main rings
R_{ij}
with
2^{b}\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{z}(R(j),A^{\star})\leq\mathrm{cost}_{z}(R_{ij},A^{\star})<2^{b+1}\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{z}(R(j),A^{\star})
.
Let
\mathcal{G}^{(m)}(j):=\left\{G^{(m)}(j,b):z\log(\varepsilon/4z)-\log k<b\leq 0\right\}
be the collection of
j
-th level main groups.
Let
\mathcal{G}^{(m)}:=\left\{G\in\mathcal{G}^{(m)}(j):z\log(\varepsilon/z)<j<2z\log(z\varepsilon^{-1})\right\}
be the collection of all main groups.

Similarly, for each integer
b\leq 0
,
we define

I(o,b)=[2^{b}\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{z}(R^{(o)}(j),A^{\star}),2^{b+1}\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{z}(R^{(o)}(j),A^{\star})

and an
outer group

G^{(o)}(b):=\bigcup_{i\in[k]:\mathrm{cost}_{z}(R^{(o)}_{i},A^{\star})\in I(o,b)}R^{(o)}_{i}

to be the collection of outer rings
R_{i}^{(o)}
with
2^{b}\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{z}(R^{(o)},A^{\star})\leq\mathrm{cost}_{z}(R^{(o)}_{i},A^{\star})<2^{b+1}\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{z}(R^{(o)},A^{\star})
.
Let
\mathcal{G}^{(o)}:=\left\{G^{(o)}(b):z\log(\varepsilon/4z)-\log k<b\leq 0\right\}
be the collection of outer groups.

Let
\mathcal{G}:=\mathcal{G}^{(m)}\cup\mathcal{G}^{(o)}
be the collection of all groups.

By definition, we know that all groups in
\mathcal{G}
, including main groups
G^{(m)}(j,b)
s and outer groups
G^{(o)}(b)
s, are pairwise disjoint.
For main groups, we provide an illustration in Figure
2
.
We also have the following observation that lower bounds
\frac{d^{z}(p,A^{\star})}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,A^{\star})}
for main groups
G\in\mathcal{G}^{(m)}
.

Observation A.4
(Main group cost
[
14
]
)

Let
G\in\mathcal{G}^{(m)}
be a main group.
Let
i\in[k]
be an integer satisfying that
P_{i}\cap G\neq\emptyset
.
For any
p\in P_{i}\cap G
, we have

\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,A^{\star})\leq 2k\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P_{i}\cap G,A^{\star})\leq 4k\cdot|P_{i}\cap G|\cdot d(p,A^{\star}),\text{ and }|P_{i}\cap G|\cdot d(p,A^{\star})\leq 2\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P_{i}\cap G).

Note that
\mathcal{G}
may not contain all points in
P
– actually, we discard some “light” rings in Definition
A.3
.
We will see that we only need to take samples from groups in
\mathcal{G}
for coreset construction and the remaining points can be “represented by” points in

A^{\star}
with a small estimation error.
Our group structure is slightly different from that in
[
18
,
14
]
: they gather groups
G^{(m)}(j,b)
/
G^{(o)}(b)
with
-\log k\leq b\leq 0
as an entirety and sample from them together.

Figure 2:
An example of Definition
A.3

Lemma A.5
(Group number)

There exist at most
O(z^{2}\log(k\varepsilon^{-1})\log(z\varepsilon^{-1}))
groups in
\mathcal{G}
.

We also have the following lemma for the construction time of
\mathcal{G}
.

Lemma A.6
(Construction time of
\mathcal{G}
)

Given a constant approximation
A^{\star}\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
, it takes
O(nk)
time to construct
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof:

Firstly, it takes
O(nk)
time to compute all distances
d(p,A^{\star})
for
p\in P
and construct clusters
P_{i}
s.
Then it takes
O(n)
time to compute all
\Delta_{i}
s and construct all rings and their corresponding cost
\mathrm{cost}_{z}(R_{ij},A^{\star})
and
\mathrm{cost}_{z}(R^{(o)}_{i},A^{\star})
.
Finally, it takes at most
O(n)
time to construct all groups
G^{(m)}(j,b)
and
G^{(o)}(b)
.
Overall, we complete the proof.

\square

A.2
A Coreset Construction Algorithm for
(k,z)
-Clustering

[
18
,
14
]

Now we are ready to present our coreset construction algorithm
for
(k,z)
-Clustering
(see Algorithm
1
).
We use the same importance sampling procedure proposed in
[
18
,
14
]
.
The key is to perform an importance sampling procedure for each group
G\in\mathcal{G}
with a carefully selected number of samples
\Gamma_{G}
(Line 1), where
\Gamma_{G}
is defined in Theorem
A.11
.
We also weigh each center
a^{\star}_{i}\in A^{\star}
by the number of remaining points in
P_{i}\setminus\mathcal{G}
(We slightly abuse the notation
P_{i}\setminus\cal G
to denote

P_{i}\setminus(\cup_{G\in\mathcal{G}}G)
. See Line 2).

Next, we provide a new analysis for Algorithm
1
(Theorem
A.11
).

Algorithm 1

Coreset Construction Algorithm for
(k,z)
-Clustering

[
18
,
14
]

1:

a metric space
(\mathcal{X},d)
, a dataset
P\subseteq\mathcal{X}
,
z\geq 1
, integer
k\geq 1
;
A constant approximation
A^{\star}\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
for
P
, and the partition
P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}
of
P
according to
A^{\star}
;
The collection
\mathcal{G}
of groups as in Definition
A.3
together with
the number of samples
\Gamma_{G}
for each
G\in\mathcal{G}
.

2:

a weighted subset
(S,w)

3:

For each group
G\in\mathcal{G}
, sample a collection
S_{G}
of
\Gamma_{G}
points from
G
, where each sample
p\in G
is selected with probability
\frac{d^{z}(p,A^{\star})}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,A^{\star})}
and weighted by
w(p)=\frac{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,A^{\star})}{\Gamma_{G}\cdot d^{z}(p,A^{\star})}
.

4:

For each
i\in[k]
, set the weight of
a^{\star}_{i}\in A^{\star}
as
w(a^{\star}_{i})=|P_{i}\setminus\mathcal{G}|
.

5:

return

S:=A^{\star}\cup(\bigcup_{G\in\mathcal{G}}S_{G})
together with the weights
w
.

A.3
\alpha
-Covering and Covering Number of Groups

We first define
\alpha
-covering of groups in
\mathcal{G}
.
The new definition of
\alpha
-covering for main groups is crucial for improving the coreset size.

Coverings of main groups.

Recall that
A^{\star}=\left\{a^{\star}_{1},\ldots,a^{\star}_{k}\in\mathcal{X}\right\}\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
is a constant approximation for the
(k,z)
-Clustering
problem.
We define the following
huge subset
of main groups
G\in\mathcal{G}^{(m)}(j)
w.r.t. a
k
-center set
C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
:

H(G,C):=\left\{p\in R_{ij}\cap G:i\in[k],d^{z}(a^{\star}_{i},C)\geq 8z\varepsilon^{-1}2^{j}\Delta_{i}\right\},

i.e., the collection of rings
R_{ij}\in G
with
d^{z}(a^{\star}_{i},C)\geq 8z\varepsilon^{-1}2^{j}\Delta_{i}
.
Let
H(S,C):=H(G,C)\cap S
for every (weighted) subset
S\subseteq G
.
By construction, for all points
p\in H(G,C)
, the distances
d(p,C)
are “close” to each other, which is an important property. We summarize the property in the following observation.

Observation A.7

For a
k
-center set
C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
,
i\in[k]
and
p\in P_{i}\cap H(G,C)
, we have

d^{z}(p,C)\in(1\pm\frac{\varepsilon}{4})\cdot d^{z}(a^{\star}_{i},C)
.

By this observation, we can use a single distance
d^{z}(a^{\star},C)
to approximate
d^{z}(p,C)
with a small error for all points
p\in H(G,C)
.
For points in
G\setminus H(G,C)
, we define the following notions of coverings and covering numbers of main groups.

Definition A.8
(
\alpha
-Coverings of main groups)

Let
G\in\mathcal{G}^{(m)}
be a main group.
Let
S\subseteq G
be a subset and
\alpha>0
.
We say a set
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{|S|}
of
cost vectors
is an
\alpha
-covering
of
S
if for each
C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
, there exists a cost vector
v\in V
such that for any
i\in[k]
and
p\in P_{i}\cap S\setminus H(G,C)
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\left|d^{z}(p,C)-d^{z}(a^{\star}_{i},C)-v_{p}\right|\leq\alpha\cdot\mathsf{err}(p,C),

(6)

where
\mathsf{err}(p,C)
is called the
relative covering error
of
p
to
C
defined as follows

\mathsf{err}(p,C):=\left(\sqrt{d^{z}(p,C)\cdot d^{z}(p,A^{\star})}+d^{z}(p,A^{\star})\right)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,A^{\star})}},

where we use
\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})
as a shorthand notation of
\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C)+\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,A^{\star})
throughout.

Definition A.9
(Covering numbers of main groups)

Define
\mathcal{N}^{(m)}(S,\alpha)
to be the minimum cardinality
|V|
of any
\alpha
-covering
V
of
S
.
Given an integer
\Gamma\geq 1
, define the
(\Gamma,\alpha)
-covering number
of
G
to be

\mathcal{N}^{(m)}_{G}(\Gamma,\alpha):=\max_{S\subseteq G:|S|\leq\Gamma}\mathcal{N}^{(m)}(S,\alpha),

i.e., the maximum cardinality
\mathcal{N}^{(m)}(S,\alpha)
over all possible subsets
S\subseteq G
of size at most
\Gamma
.

Intuitively, the covering number is a complexity measure of the set of the all possible distance difference vectors
\left\{d^{z}(p,C)-d^{z}(a^{\star}_{i},C)\right\}_{p\in G}
.
As
\mathcal{N}^{(m)}_{G}(\Gamma,\alpha)
becomes larger, all possible center sets in
\mathcal{X}^{k}
can induce more types of such vectors (up to the relative covering error).

By the triangle inequality, we directly have for any
C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
,
i\in[k]
and
p\in P_{i}\cap G\setminus H(G,C)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\quad d^{z}(a^{\star}_{i},C)-d^{z}(p,C)\\
\leq&\quad(d^{z}(a^{\star}_{i},C)-d(p,C))\cdot z(d^{z-1}(p,C)+d^{z-1}(a^{\star}_{i},C))&\\
\leq&\quad 3zd(p,A^{\star})\cdot(8z\varepsilon^{-1}\cdot 2^{j}\Delta_{i})^{z-1}&(p\notin H(G,C))\\
\leq&\quad(8z)^{z}\varepsilon^{-z+1}d^{z}(p,A^{\star}),\quad&(p\in R_{ij}).\end{aligned}

(7)

Hence, for any
\alpha\geq(8z)^{z}\varepsilon^{-z+1}
,
\mathcal{N}^{(m)}_{G}(\Gamma,\alpha)=1
always holds by Definition
A.8
and we can trivially use
0^{|S_{G}|}
as an
\alpha
-covering of
S_{G}
.
Consequently, we only need to consider the range
0<\alpha\leq(8z)^{z}\varepsilon^{-z+1}
.

Coverings of outer groups.

Similarly, we define coverings of an outer group
G\in\mathcal{G}^{(o)}
.
The same as
[
14
]
, we define the following
far subset
of
G
:

F(G,C):=\left\{p\in R_{i}^{(o)}\cap G:i\in[k],\exists q\in R_{i}^{(o)}\cap G\text{ with }d(q,C)\geq 4\cdot d(q,A^{\star})\right\},

i.e., the collection of rings
R_{i}^{(o)}\in G
that contain at least one point
q
with
d(q,C)\geq 4\cdot d(q,A^{\star})
.
Note that for any
p\in G\setminus F(G,C)
, we have
\frac{d(p,C)}{d(p,A^{\star})}\leq 4
.
Again, we formally define coverings and covering numbers of outer groups, which is very similar to those for main groups.

Definition A.10
(Coverings and covering numbers of outer groups)

Let
G\in\mathcal{G}^{(o)}
be an outer group.
Let
S\subseteq G
be a subset and
\alpha>0
.
We say a set
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{|S|}
of
cost vectors
is an
\alpha
-covering
of
S
if for each
C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
, there exists a cost vector
v\in V
such that for any
p\in S\setminus F(G,C)
, the following inequality holds:

|d^{z}(p,C)-v_{p}|\leq\alpha\cdot(d^{z}(p,C)+d^{z}(p,A^{\star})).

Define
\mathcal{N}^{(o)}(S,\alpha)
to be the minimum cardinality
|V|
of an arbitrary
\alpha
-covering
V
of
S
.
Given an integer
\Gamma\geq 1
, define the
(\Gamma,\alpha)
-covering number
of
G
to be

\mathcal{N}^{(o)}_{G}(\Gamma,\alpha):=\max_{S\subseteq G:|S|\leq\Gamma}\mathcal{N}^{(o)}(S,\alpha),

i.e., the maximum cardinality
\mathcal{N}^{(o)}(S,\alpha)
over all possible subsets
S\subseteq G
of size at most
\Gamma
.

Our definition for outer groups is almost the same as
[
14
, Definition 3]
, except that we consider coverings for subsets
S\subseteq G
instead of
G
, which enables us to design a one-stage sampling algorithm.

A.4
The Main Theorem for Algorithm
1

Now we are ready to state the main theorem for Algorithm
1
.

Theorem A.11
(Coreset for
(k,z)
-Clustering
)

Let
(\mathcal{X},d)
be a metric space and
P\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be a set of

n
points.
Let integer
k\geq 1
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be the precision parameter.
We define the number
\Gamma_{G}
of samples for group
G

(in Algorithm
1
) as follows:

1.

For each main group
G\in\mathcal{G}^{(m)}
, let
\Gamma_{G}
be the smallest integer satisfying that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{G}\geq O\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}^{(m)}_{G}(\Gamma_{G},\alpha)}d\alpha\right)^{2}+k\varepsilon^{-2}\log(k\varepsilon^{-1})\right);

(8)

2.

For each outer group
G\in\mathcal{G}^{(o)}
, let
\Gamma_{G}
be the smallest integer satisfying that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{G}\geq O\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}^{(o)}_{G}(\Gamma_{G},\alpha)}d\alpha\right)^{2}+k\varepsilon^{-2}\log(k\varepsilon^{-1})\right);

(9)

With probability at least 0.9, Algorithm
1
outputs an
O(\varepsilon)
-coreset
(S,w)
of size

k+2^{O(z)}\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\Gamma_{G},

and the running time is
O(nk)
.

\Gamma_{G}
appears in the familiar form of the entropy integral
(or Dudley integral) commonly used in the chaining argument (see e.g.,
[
44
, Corollary 5.25]
).
Hence, the remaining task is to upper bound the entropy integrals.
Moreover, combining with Lemma
A.6
, we know that the coreset construction time is at most
O(nk)
time (for building the rings and groups), given
a constant factor approximation
A^{\star}
.

Compared to Theorem
A.11
, the coreset size for general
z\geq 1
has an additional multiplicative factor
2^{O(z)}
. Typically, we treat
z
as a constant
and thus this additional factor is also a constant.

A.5
Proof of Theorem
A.11
: Performance Analysis of Algorithm
1

The coreset size is directly from Line 1 of Algorithm
1
.
Thus, it remains to verify that
S
is an
O(\varepsilon)
-coreset.

The key is the following lemma that summarizes the estimation error induced by each group
G\in\mathcal{G}
and the remaining points
P\setminus\mathcal{G}
.
For a group
G\in\mathcal{G}
, we define
P^{G}:=\left\{p\in P:\exists i\in[k],p\in P_{i}\text{ and }P_{i}\cap G\neq\emptyset\right\}
to be the union of all clusters
P_{i}
that intersects with
G
. Recall that
\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})=\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C)+\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,A^{\star})
.

Lemma A.12
(Error analysis of groups and remaining points)

We have the following:

1.

For each main group
G\in\mathcal{G}^{(m)}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{S_{G}}\sup_{C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}}\left[\frac{1}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}\cdot\Bigl|\sum_{p\in S_{G}}w(p)\cdot d^{z}(p,C)-\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C)\Bigr|\right]\leq 3\varepsilon.

2.

For each outer group
G\in\mathcal{G}^{(o)}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{S_{G}}\sup_{C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}}\Bigl[\frac{1}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P^{G},C+A^{\star})}\cdot\left|\sum_{p\in S_{G}}w(p)\cdot d^{z}(p,C)-\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C)\Bigr|\right]\leq 2\varepsilon.

3.

For any center set
C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
, we have

\Bigl|\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P\setminus\mathcal{G},C)-\sum_{i\in[k]}w(a^{\star}_{i})\cdot d^{z}(a^{\star}_{i},C)\Bigr|\leq\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P,C+A^{\star}).

Here, the expectations above are taken over the randomness of the sample set
S_{G}
.

Note that the normalization term of a main group
G\in\mathcal{G}^{(m)}
is
\frac{1}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}
that only relates to
G
, but the normalization term of an outer group
G\in\mathcal{G}^{(o)}
is
\frac{1}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P^{G},C+A^{\star})}
that depends on a larger subset
P^{G}
.
This is because “remote” points in outer groups
G\in\mathcal{G}^{(o)}
introduce an even larger empirical error than
\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})
, but the total number of these remote points is small compared to
|P^{G}|
, which enables us to upper bound the empirical error by
\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P^{G},C+A^{\star})
.

The proof of Lemma
A.12
requires the chaining argument and the improved bound of variance
and is deferred to Appendix
A.6
.
Theorem
A.11
is a direct corollary of Lemma
A.12
. The argument is almost the same as the proof of Theorem 4 in
[
14
]
.
Intuitively, we can verify that for any center set
C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
, the expected estimation error of
(S,w)
is small, say

\mathbb{E}_{S}\sup_{C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}}\left[\frac{1}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P,C+A^{\star})}\cdot\Bigl|\sum_{p\in S}w(p)\cdot d^{z}(p,C)-\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P,C)\Bigr|\right]\leq O(\varepsilon).

Then we only need to apply the Markov inequality and use the fact that
A^{\star}
is an
O(1)
-approximate solution.
For completeness, we provide the proof in the following.

Proof:

[of Theorem
A.11
]
We have

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}_{S}\sup_{C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}}\left[\frac{1}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P,C+A^{\star})}\cdot\left|\sum_{p\in S}w(p)\cdot d^{z}(p,C)-\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P,C)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}_{S}\sup_{C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}}\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\left[\frac{1}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P,C+A^{\star})}\cdot\left|\sum_{p\in S_{G}}w(p)\cdot d^{z}(p,C)-\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\sup_{C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}}\left[\frac{1}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P,C+A^{\star})}\cdot\left|\sum_{i\in[k]}w(a^{\star}_{i})\cdot d^{z}(a^{\star}_{i},C)-\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P\setminus G,C)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}_{S}\sup_{C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}}\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P,C+A^{\star})}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{S_{G}}\sup_{C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}}\left|\frac{\sum_{p\in S_{G}}w(p)\cdot d^{z}(p,C)-\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C)}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}\right|\right]

as in the following lemma.

Lemma A.22
(Variance of
X_{C,h}
)

Fix a
k
-center set
C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
and an integer

.
The variance of

is always at most

\mathrm{Var}\left[X_{C,h}\right]=\sum_{p\in S_{G}\setminus H(S_{G},C)}\left(\frac{w(p)\cdot(v^{C,h+1}_{p}-v^{C,h}_{p})}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{2^{-2h+4}k}{\Gamma_{G}}.

Moreover, conditioned on event
\xi_{G}
(Inequality (
10
)), the variance of
X_{C,h}
is always at most

\mathrm{Var}\left[X_{C,h}\mid\xi_{G}\right]=\sum_{p\in S_{G}\setminus H(S_{G},C)}\left(\frac{w(p)\cdot(v^{C,h+1}_{p}-v^{C,h}_{p})}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{2^{-2h+6}}{\Gamma_{G}}.

The proof and analysis of the above lemma can be found in Appendix
A.7
.
Compared with
[

, Lemma 23]
, the above lemma provides a tighter variance bound, saving a
\min\left\{\varepsilon^{-z},k\right\}
factor.

Now we are ready to upper bound
\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\sup_{C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}}|X_{C,h}|
.
By law of total expectation, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\sup_{C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}}|X_{C,h}|=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\sup_{C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}}|X_{C,h}|\mid\xi_{G}\right]\cdot\Pr\left[\xi_{G}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\sup_{C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}}|X_{C,h}|\mid\overline{\xi_{G}}\right]\cdot\Pr\left[\overline{\xi_{G}}\right].

(18)

For each
C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
, we define
\psi^{(C,h)}=v^{C,h+1}-v^{C,h}
as a certificate of
C
.
Note that there are at most
|V_{h+1}|\cdot|V_{h}|
different certificates
\psi^{(C,h)}
s and let
\Psi_{h}=\left\{\psi^{(C,h)}:C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}\right\}
be the collection of all possible certificates
\psi^{(C,h)}
s.
For each certificate

, we select a

-center set

with

to be the representative center set of
\psi
.
By definition, we note that for any
C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
with
\psi^{(C,h)}=\psi
,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\left|\frac{1}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}\sum_{p\in S_{G}\setminus H(S_{G},C)}\xi_{p}\cdot w(p)\cdot(v^{C,h+1}_{p}-v^{C,h}_{p})\right|\\
\leq&\,\,\left|\frac{1}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C_{\psi}+A^{\star})}\sum_{p\in S_{G}\setminus H(S_{G},C_{\psi})}\xi_{p}\cdot w(p)\cdot(v^{C_{\psi},h+1}_{p}-v^{C_{\psi},h}_{p})\right|.\end{aligned}

Let
\mathcal{C}_{h}=\left\{C_{\psi}:\psi\in\Psi\right\}
be the collection of all such

s.
By construction, we have

.
Now we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\sup_{C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}}|X_{C,h}|\mid\xi_{G}\right]\cdot\Pr\left[\xi_{G}\right]\\
\leq&\,\,\,\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\sup_{C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}}\left|\frac{1}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}\sum_{p\in S_{G}\setminus H(S_{G},C)}\xi_{p}\cdot w(p)\cdot\left(v^{C,h+1}_{p}-v^{C,h}_{p}\right)\right|\mid\xi_{G}\right]\\
\\
=&\,\,\,\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\sup_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{h}}\left|\frac{1}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}\sum_{p\in S_{G}\setminus H(S_{G},C)}\xi_{p}\cdot w(p)\cdot\left(v^{C,h+1}_{p}-v^{C,h}_{p}\right)\right|\mid\xi_{G}\right]\\
\leq&\,\,\,2\sqrt{\sup_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{h}}\mathrm{Var}\left[X_{C,h}\mid\xi_{G}\right]}\cdot\sqrt{2\ln(|V_{h+1}|\cdot|V_{h}|)}\\
\leq&\,\,\,\frac{2^{6-h}}{\sqrt{\Gamma_{G}}}\cdot\sqrt{\ln|V_{h+1}|},\\
\end{aligned}

(20)

where the first inequality follows from the definition of
X_{C,h}
, the first equality holds since the supreme must achieve at some center set

due to Inequality (
19
), the second inequality follows from Lemma
A.21

Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\sup_{C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}}|X_{C,h}|\mid\overline{\xi_{G}}\right]\cdot\Pr\left[\overline{\xi_{G}}\right]\\
\leq&\,\,\,2\sqrt{\sup_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{h}}\sum_{p\in S_{G}\setminus H(S_{G},C)}\left(\frac{w(p)\cdot(v^{C,h+1}_{p}-v^{C,h}_{p})}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}\right)^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{2\ln(|V_{h+1}|\cdot|V_{h}|)}\cdot\Pr\left[\overline{\xi_{G}}\right]\\
\leq&\,\,\,\frac{2^{6-h}k}{\sqrt{\Gamma_{G}}}\cdot\sqrt{\ln|V_{h+1}|}\cdot\Pr\left[\overline{\xi_{G}}\right]\\
\leq&\,\,\,\frac{2^{6-h}\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\Gamma_{G}}}\cdot\sqrt{\ln|V_{h+1}|},&\\
\end{aligned}

(21)

where the first inequality follows from Inequality (

), the second inequality from Lemma
A.22
, and the last inequality from Lemma
A.13

Finally, we observe that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Gamma_{G}\geq&\quad\Omega\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\left(\int_{0}^{(8z)^{z}\varepsilon^{-z+1}}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}^{(m)}_{G}(\Gamma_{G},\alpha)}d\alpha\right)^{2}+k\varepsilon^{-2}\log(k\varepsilon^{-1})\right)&(\text{Defn. of $\Gamma_{G}$})\\
\geq&\quad\Omega\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\left(\int_{0}^{(8z)^{z}\varepsilon^{-z+1}}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}^{(m)}_{G}(\Gamma_{G},\alpha)+k\log\alpha^{-1}}d\alpha\right)^{2}\right)&\\
\geq&\quad\Omega(\int_{0}^{(8z)^{z}\varepsilon^{-z+1}}\sqrt{\log\widehat{\mathcal{N}}^{(m)}_{G}(\Gamma_{G},\alpha)}d\alpha).&(\text{Lemma\penalty\ \ref{lm:covering_relation}})\\
\end{aligned}

(22)

Using Eq. (

) and Ineqs. (

\displaystyle\sum_{h\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\sup_{C\in\mathcal{X}^{k}}|X_{C,h}|\leq

\displaystyle\,\,\,\sum_{h\geq 0}\frac{2^{7-h}}{\sqrt{\Gamma_{G}}}\cdot\sqrt{\ln|V_{h+1}|}

\displaystyle(\text{Eq.\penalty\ (\ref{ineq1:proof_main_small_y}), Ineqs.\penalty\ (\ref{ineq2:proof_main_small_y}) and (\ref{ineq3:proof_main_small_y})})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\,\,\frac{\varepsilon}{16}\cdot\frac{\sum_{h\geq 0}2^{-h}\cdot\sqrt{\ln|V_{h+1}|}}{\int_{0}^{(8z)^{z}\varepsilon^{-z+1}}\sqrt{\log\widehat{\mathcal{N}}^{(m)}_{G}(\Gamma_{G},\alpha)}d\alpha}

\displaystyle(\text{Ineq.\penalty\ (\ref{ineq4:proof_main_small_y})})

\displaystyle\,\,\,\frac{\varepsilon}{6},

\displaystyle(\text{Defn. of $V_{h}$})

Proof:

[of Lemma
A.22
]
Let
M_{C}\subseteq[k]
denote the collection of
i\in[k]
with
P_{i}\cap S_{G}\setminus H(S_{G},C)\neq\emptyset
.
By the definition of

, we note that for any

,
P_{i}\cap S_{G}\setminus H(S_{G},C)=P_{i}\cap S_{G}
.
For any
i\in[k]
and point

, let
q=\arg\min_{p^{\prime}\in P_{i}\cap G}d(p^{\prime},C)

Suppose

for some

We note that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} d^{z}(p,C)&\leq(d(q,C)+d(p,A^{\star})+d(q,A^{\star}))^{z}&(\text{triangle ineq.})\\
&\leq(d(q,C)+3d(p,A^{\star}))^{z}&(\text{Defn. of $R_{ij}$})\\
&\leq 2^{z}d(q,C)+6^{z}d^{z}(p,A^{\star})&(\text{Lemma\penalty\ \ref{lm:relaxed}})\\
&\leq\frac{2^{z}\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P_{i}\cap G,C)}{|P_{i}\cap G|}+6^{z}d^{z}(p,A^{\star})&(\text{Defn. of $q$}).\end{aligned}

(23)

Then we can bound the variance as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\sum_{p\in S_{G}\setminus H(S_{G},C)}\left(\frac{w(p)\cdot(v^{C,h+1}_{p}-v^{C,h}_{p})}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}\right)^{2}\\
=&\,\,\,\sum_{p\in S_{G}\setminus H(S_{G},C)}\left(\frac{w(p)\cdot(v^{C,h+1}_{p}-y^{C}_{p}+y^{C}_{p}-v^{C,h}_{p})}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}\right)^{2}&\\
\leq&\,\,\,\sum_{p\in S_{G}\setminus H(S_{G},C)}\frac{\left(w(p)\cdot(\sqrt{3}+1)\cdot 2^{-h}\cdot(\sqrt{d^{z}(p,C)\cdot d^{z}(p,A^{\star})}+d^{z}(p,A^{\star}))\right)^{2}}{(\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star}))\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,A^{\star})}\\
\leq&\,\,\,\frac{2^{-2h+3}}{\Gamma^{\prime}_{G}}\cdot\sum_{p\in S_{G}\setminus H(S_{G},C)}\frac{w(p)\cdot(d^{z}(p,C)+d^{z}(p,A^{\star}))}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}&\\
\leq&\,\,\,\frac{2^{-2h+3}}{\Gamma^{\prime}_{G}}\cdot\sum_{i\in M_{C}}\sum_{p\in P_{i}\cap S_{G}}\frac{w(p)}{{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}}\cdot\left(\frac{2^{z}\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P_{i}\cap G,C)}{|P_{i}\cap G|}+6^{z}d(p,A^{\star})\right)&\\
\leq&\,\,\,\frac{2^{-2h+5}}{\Gamma_{G}}\cdot\sum_{i\in M_{C}}\frac{1}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P_{i}\cap G,C)+\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P_{i}\cap G,A^{\star})}{|P_{i}\cap G|}\right)\cdot\sum_{p\in P_{i}\cap S_{G}}w(p)&\end{aligned}

(24)

where the first inequality holds due to the Defn. of
v^{C,h+1}_{p}
and
v^{C,h}_{p}
,
the second follows from the Definition of
w(p)
,
the third from Inequality (
23
) and the definition of
M_{C}
,
and the last from Observation
A.4
.
Recall that event
\xi_{G}
is defined as

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in P_{i}\cap S_{G}}w(p)=\sum_{p\in P_{i}\cap S_{G}}\frac{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,A^{\star})}{\Gamma^{\prime}_{G}\cdot d^{z}(p,A^{\star})}\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\cdot|P_{i}\cap G|.

(25)

Hence, conditioning on
\xi_{G}
and continue the derivation,
we can see that

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in S_{G}\setminus H(S_{G},C)}\left(\frac{w(p)\cdot(v^{C,h+1}_{p}-v^{C,h}_{p})}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\,\,\frac{2^{-2h+5}}{\Gamma_{G}}\cdot\sum_{i\in M_{C}}\frac{(1+\varepsilon)|P_{i}\cap G|}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}\left(\frac{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P_{i}\cap G,C)+\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P_{i}\cap G,A^{\star})}{|P_{i}\cap G|}\right)

\displaystyle(\text{Eq.\penalty\ (\ref{eq:xi_G})})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\,\,\frac{2^{-2h+6}}{\Gamma_{G}}\cdot\sum_{i\in M_{C}}\frac{(\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P_{i}\cap G,C)+\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P_{i}\cap G,A^{\star}))}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C)+\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,A^{\star})}

\displaystyle(\varepsilon\in(0,1))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\,\,\frac{2^{-2h+6}}{\Gamma_{G}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C)+\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,A^{\star})}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\,\,\frac{2^{-2h+6}}{\Gamma_{G}}.

In the general case without conditioning on
\xi_{G}

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in S_{G}\setminus H(S_{G},C)}\left(\frac{w(p)\cdot(v^{C,h+1}_{p}-v^{C,h}_{p})}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\,\,\frac{2^{-2h+2}}{\Gamma_{G}}\cdot\sum_{i\in M_{C}}\sum_{p\in P_{i}\cap S_{G}}\frac{w(p)}{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P_{i}\cap G,C)}{|P_{i}\cap G|}+4d^{z}(p,A^{\star})\right)

\displaystyle\,\,\,\frac{2^{-2h+2}}{\Gamma_{G}}\cdot\sum_{i\in M_{C}}\sum_{p\in P_{i}\cap S_{G}}\frac{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,A^{\star})}{\Gamma_{G}\cdot d^{z}(p,A^{\star})\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{z}(G,C+A^{\star})}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(P_{i}\cap G,C)}{|P_{i}\cap G|}+4d^{z}(p,A^{\star})\right)

\displaystyle\leq

where the first inequality follows from Ineq. (

), the first equality from the Definition of

.

(Coreset for Euclidean

\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and constant

, let

-Clustering

B.1
Proof of Theorem
B.1
: Coreset for Euclidean
(k,z)
-Clustering

Following the same reasoning as in
[
15
]
, we can make the following assumption without loss of generality.

Assumption 1
(Assumptions on Euclidean datasets)

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
We can assume that the given dataset
P
satisfies

•

The number of distinct points
\|P\|_{0}
is at most
2^{O(z)}\cdot\operatorname{poly}(k\varepsilon^{-1})
;

•

The dimension

;

•

P
is unweighted.

In light of Theorem
A.11
, it remains to upper bound the covering number
\mathcal{N}^{(m)}_{G}(\Gamma_{G},\alpha)
and
\mathcal{N}^{(o)}_{G}(\Gamma_{G},\alpha)
, as in the following lemma.

Lemma B.2
(Covering number in Euclidean metrics)

For each
G\in\mathcal{G}^{(m)}
,
0<\alpha\leq 1
, and integer
0\leq\beta<\log(z\varepsilon^{-1})
,

\log\mathcal{N}^{(m)}_{G}(\Gamma_{G},\alpha)=2^{O(z)}k\cdot\min\left\{k^{\frac{z}{z+2}}\alpha^{-2}\log\|P\|_{0}\log\varepsilon^{-1},d\right\}\cdot\log(\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha^{-1}).

For each
G\in\mathcal{G}^{(o)}
and any
0<\alpha\leq 1
,

\log\mathcal{N}^{(o)}_{G}(\Gamma_{G},\alpha)=O(z^{2}k\cdot\min\left\{\alpha^{-2}\log\|P\|_{0},d\right\}\cdot\log\alpha^{-1}).

The proof can be found in Appendix
B.3
.
Theorem
B.1
is a direct corollary of the above lemma.

Proof:

[of Theorem
B.1
]
Fix a main group
G\in\mathcal{G}^{(m)}(j)
for some integer
z\log(\varepsilon/z)<j<2z\log(z\varepsilon^{-1})
.
Recall that we only need to consider

.
To apply Theorem
A.11
, the key is to upper bound the entropy integral
\int_{0}^{(8z)^{z}\varepsilon^{-z+1}}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}^{(m)}_{G}(\Gamma_{G},\alpha)}d\alpha
.
We first have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{(8z)^{z}\varepsilon^{-z+1}}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}^{(m)}_{G}(\Gamma_{G},\alpha)}d\alpha=\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}^{(m)}_{G}(\Gamma_{G},\alpha)}d\alpha+\int_{\varepsilon}^{(8z)^{z}\varepsilon^{-z+1}}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}^{(m)}_{G}(\Gamma_{G},\alpha)}d\alpha.

(26)

Next, we apply Lemma
B.2
to upper bound the above two terms on the right side separately.

For the first term, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}^{(m)}_{G}(\Gamma_{G},\alpha)}d\alpha=\,\,\,&O(1)\cdot\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\sqrt{kd\log(\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha^{-1})}d\alpha&(\text{Lemma\penalty\ \ref{lm:Euclidean_covering_number}})\\

(27)

For the second term, we have the following upper bound

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\varepsilon}^{(8z)^{z}\varepsilon^{-z+1}}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}^{(m)}_{G}(\Gamma_{G},\alpha)}d\alpha&\\
=\,\,\,&O\left(\int_{\varepsilon}^{(8z)^{z}\varepsilon^{-z+1}}\sqrt{k^{\frac{2z+2}{z+2}}\alpha^{-2}\log\|P\|_{0}\log\varepsilon^{-1}\log(\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha^{-1})}d\alpha\right)&(\text{Lemma\penalty\ \ref{lm:Euclidean_covering_number}})\\
=\,\,\,&O(\sqrt{k^{\frac{2z+2}{z+2}}\log(k\varepsilon^{-1})\log\varepsilon^{-1}})\cdot\int_{\varepsilon}^{(8z)^{z}\varepsilon^{-z+1}}\sqrt{\alpha^{-2}\log(\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha^{-1})}d\alpha&(\text{letting $2^{\beta}=k^{\frac{1}{z+2}}$})\\
=\,\,\,&O(\sqrt{k^{\frac{2z+2}{z+2}}\log(k\varepsilon^{-1})\log\varepsilon^{-1}})\cdot\int_{\varepsilon}^{(8z)^{z}\varepsilon^{-z+1}}\sqrt{\alpha^{-2}\log(\varepsilon^{-2})}d\alpha&\\
=\,\,\,&O(\sqrt{k^{\frac{2z+2}{z+2}}\log(k\varepsilon^{-1})\log\varepsilon^{-1}})\cdot O(\sqrt{\log^{3}(\varepsilon^{-1})})&\\
=\,\,\,&O\left(\sqrt{k^{\frac{2z+2}{z+2}}\log(k\varepsilon^{-1})\log^{4}(\varepsilon^{-1})}\right),&\end{aligned}

(28)

Now we are ready to upper bound
\int_{0}^{(8z)^{z}\varepsilon^{-z+1}}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}^{(m)}_{G}(\Gamma_{G},\alpha)}d\alpha
.
Combining with Inequalities (
26
) to (

), we conclude that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{(8z)^{z}\varepsilon^{-z+1}}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}^{(m)}_{G}(\Gamma_{G},\alpha)}d\alpha

\displaystyle(\text{Eq.\penalty\ (\ref{ineq1:Euclidean_covering_number})})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O\left(\sqrt{k\log(k\varepsilon^{-1})\log\varepsilon^{-1}}\right)+O\left(\sqrt{k^{\frac{2z+2}{z+2}}\log(k\varepsilon^{-1})\log^{4}(\varepsilon^{-1})}\right)

\displaystyle(\text{Ineq.\penalty\ (\ref{ineq2:Euclidean_covering_number}) and (\ref{ineq3:Euclidean_covering_number})})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O\left(\sqrt{k^{\frac{2z+2}{z+2}}\log(k\varepsilon^{-1})\log^{4}(\varepsilon^{-1})}\right).

Similarly, we can prove that for an outer group

,

Consequently, we have that

satisfies Inequality (

).
This completes the proof.

B.2
Terminal Embedding with Additive Errors

Before proving Lemma
B.2
, we introduce two types of terminal embeddings that are useful for dimension reduction.

Terminal embedding.

Roughly speaking, a terminal embedding projects a point set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
to a low-dimensional space while approximately preserving all pairwise distances between
X
and
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Definition B.3
(Terminal embedding)

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a collection of
n
points.
A mapping
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is called an
\alpha
-terminal embedding of
X
if for any
p\in X
and
