Approximation and localized polynomial frame on double hyperbolic and conic domains
Yuan Xu

Abstract
. We study approximation and localized polynomial frames on a bounded double hyperbolic or conic surface and the domain bounded by such a surface and hyperplanes. The main work follows the framework developed recently in [ 30 ] for homogeneous spaces that are assumed to contain highly localized kernels constructed via a family of orthogonal polynomials. The existence of such kernels will be established with the help of closed form formulas for the reproducing kernels. The main results provide a construction of semi-discrete localized tight frame in weighted L 2 L^{2} norm and a characterization of best approximation by polynomials on our domains. Several intermediate results, including the Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund inequalities, positive cubature rules, Christoeffel functions, and Bernstein type inequalities, are shown to hold for doubling weights defined via the intrinsic distance on the domain.

1.
Introduction

Recently a general framework based on highly localized polynomial kernels is developed in
[
30
]
for
localizable homogeneous spaces and used for studying approximation and localized polynomial frame on
a finite conic surface and the domain bounded by such a surface and a hyperplane. In the present work,
we establish highly localized kernels and carry out analysis on bounded double hyperbolic or conic surface
and the domain bounded by such a surface and hyperplanes.

1.1.
Motivation

Let
\Omega
be a set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, either an algebraic surface or a domain with non-empty interior. A
homogeneous space is a measure space
(\Omega,\varpi,{\mathsf{d}})
, where
\varpi
is a nonnegative
doubling weight function with respect to the metric
{\mathsf{d}}(\cdot,\cdot)
on
\Omega
. We call a homogeneous
space
(\Omega,\varpi,{\mathsf{d}})

localizable
if it contains highly localized kernels.

The kernels are constructed using orthogonal polynomials. Let
\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}
be the Lebesgue measure on
\Omega
.
Assume that the weight function
\varpi
is regular so that

(1.1)

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{\varpi}:=\int_{\Omega}f(x)g(x)\varpi(x)\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(x)

is a well defined inner product on the space of polynomials restricted to
\Omega
. Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}(\Omega,\varpi)

be the space of orthogonal polynomials of degree
n
with respect to this inner product. The projection
operator
\operatorname{proj}_{n}:L^{2}(\Omega,\varpi)\mapsto{\mathcal{V}}_{n}(\Omega,\varpi)
can be written as

(1.2)

\operatorname{proj}_{n}(\varpi;f,x)=\int_{\Omega}f(y)P_{n}(\varpi;x,y)\varpi(y)\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(y),\quad f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\varpi).

where
P_{n}(\varpi;\cdot,\cdot)
is the reproducing kernel of the space
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}(\Omega,\varpi)
. Let

\widehat{a}
be a cut-off function, defined as a compactly supported function in
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
. Then
our highly localized kernels are of the form

L_{n}(\varpi;x,y):=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\widehat{a}\Big(\frac{j}{n}\Big)P_{j}(\varpi;x,y).

The kernel is highly localized if it decays at rates faster than any inverse polynomial rate away from the
main diagonal
y=x
in
\Omega\times\Omega
with respect to the distance
{\mathsf{d}}
on
\Omega
; see
the definition in the next section. These kernels provide important tools for analysis on regular domains,
such as the unit sphere and the unit ball, and are essential ingredient in recent study of approximation
and localized polynomial frames; see, for example,
[
3
,
8
,
11
,
14
,
17
,
18
,
19
]

for some of the results on the spheres and balls and
[
1
,
2
,
12
,
13
,
15
,
25
]

for various applications.

The reason that highly localized kernels are known only on a few regular domains lies in the
addition
formula
for orthogonal polynomials, which are closed form formulas for the reproducing kernels of
orthogonal polynomials. For spherical harmonics that are orthogonal on the unit sphere, which serve as
a quintessential example for our study, the closed form formula is given by
Z_{n}({\langle}x,y{\rangle})
, where
Z_{n}

is a Gegenbauer polynomial of one variable. The addition formulas are powerful tools when they exist.

Our recent work in
[
30
]
is prompted by two aspirations. The first one is the realization that, if highly
localized kernels are taken as granted, then much of the analysis can be developed within a general
framework of homogeneous spaces. The second one is the possibility of establishing highly localized kernels
and carrying out analysis thereafter on conic domains, which are domains largely untouched hitherto. The
latter is made possible by recently discovered new addition formulas for orthogonal polynomials on conic
domains
[
28
,
29
]
. Altogether there are four types of of conic domains and they are standardized as:

(1)

conic surface
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
defined by

{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}:=\left\{(x,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}:\|x\|=t,\,0\leq t\leq 1,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\right\};

(2)

solid cone
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
bounded by
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
and the hyperplane
t=1
;

(3)

two-sheets hyperbolic surface
{}_{\varrho}{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
defined by

{}_{\varrho}{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}:=\left\{(x,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}:\|x\|^{2}=t^{2}-\varrho^{2},\,\varrho\leq|t|\leq 1+\varrho,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\right\},

where
\varrho\geq 0
, which becomes the double conic surface
{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
when
\varrho=0
;

(4)

solid hyperboloid
{}_{\varrho}{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
bounded by
{}_{\varrho}{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
and the hyperplanes

t=\pm 1
, which becomes solid double cone
{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
when
\varrho=0
.

The first two types of conic domains are studied in
[
30
]
. The present paper deals with the other two
cases: double hyperbolic surfaces and hyperboloid.

1.2.
Main results

We will follow the framework established in
[
30
]
. Assuming several assertions on highly localized
kernels and on fast decaying polynomials, the framework provides a unified theory for two objectives.
The first one leads to localized polynomial frames constructed via a semi-continuous Calderón type decomposition

f=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}L_{2^{j}}\ast L_{2^{j}}\ast f,\qquad f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\varpi),

where
L_{n}*f
denotes the integral operator that has
L_{n}(\varpi;\cdot,\cdot)
as its kernel,

(1.3)

L_{n}*f(x):=\int_{\Omega}f(y)L_{n}(\varpi;x,y)\varpi(y)\mathrm{d}y.

Discretizing the integrals by appropriate positive cubature rules, we end up with a fame system
\{\psi_{\xi}\}_{\xi\in\Xi}
,
indexed by a discrete set
\Xi
of well-separated points in
\Omega
, where
\psi_{\xi}(x)=\sqrt{{\lambda}_{\xi}}L_{2^{j}}(\varpi;x,\xi)
with
{\lambda}_{\xi}>0
being the coefficients in the cubature, and the frame is tight in
the sense that, for all
f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\varpi)
,

f=\sum_{\xi\in\Xi}{\langle}f,\psi_{\xi}{\rangle}_{\varpi}\psi_{\varpi}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\int_{\Omega}|f(x)|^{2}\varpi(x)\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{\xi\in\Xi}|{\langle}f,\psi_{\xi}{\rangle}|^{2}.

This is an extension of the extensive work on the unit sphere and the unit ball in the literature that we have
described in the previous subsection. The second objective is to study the error of the best approximation by
polynomials

E_{n}(f)_{L^{p}(\Omega,\varpi)}=\inf_{\deg g\leq n}\|f-g\|_{L^{p}(\Omega,\varpi)}.

The aim is to provide a characterization via a modulus of smoothness, defined as a multiplier operator,
and an equivalent
K
-functional, defined by the spectral differential operator that has orthogonal polynomials as
eigenfunctions. Such a characterization is in line with those on the unit sphere, see
[
8
,
20
,
27
]
and
the references therein. The characterization consists of a direct estimate, using the fast decaying of

L_{n}(\varpi;\cdot,\cdot)
, and an inverse estimate, using a Bernstein inequality established via
highly localized kernels. It is worth mentioning that several intermediate results, such as the
Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund inequalities, positive cubature rules, Christoeffel functions, and Bernstein type
are of independent interests; furthermore, some of these intermediate results can be established
for doubling weights, which extends the results on the interval and on the unit sphere
[
4
,
8
,
16
]
.

In order for the framework to work on a particular domain, substantial work is needed to fulfill the
assertions and assumptions, which depends heavily on the geometry of the domain and the complexity
of the orthogonal structure on the domain. For each conic domain, this starts with identifying an appropriate
intrinsic distance on the domain. Establishing the highly localized kernels is delicate and fairly involved,
relying on the structure of addition formula and the distance function. The
{\varepsilon}
-separated set is assumed
conceptually in the framework, an explicit construction is needed on each domain, which is also necessary
for computational purpose. Even though the roadmap is outlined by the framework, the actual work on each
domain remains challenging and amounts to a thorough understanding of the intrinsic structure on the domain.

It should also be mentioned that the structures on double hyperbolic domains
{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
and

{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
, which degenerate to double cones when
\varrho=0
, are distinctively different from those
on the single conic domains
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
and
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
. Indeed, the distance functions for these
two typos of domains are incomparable, around the conic apex, and the orthogonal structure on the double
conic domains depends on the parity of the polynomials on the the variable
t
. For example, for the
double conic surface
{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
, the closed form formula for the reproducing kernels is stablished
for polynomials that are orthogonal with respect to
{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}(t)=|t|^{2{\beta}}(1-t^{2})^{{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}}
, but only for
the subspace of polynomials that are even in
t
variable or odd in
t
variable. The restriction requires us
to restrict to the class of functions that are even in
t
variable, but the entire framework can still be
carried out on the domain.
Using the reproducing kernels in
[
29
]
, we will show that
({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{0,{\gamma}},{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}})
is
a localizable homogeneous space. Similar result holds for the solid double cone
{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
with the weight
function
W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}(x,t)=|t|^{\beta}(1-t)^{\gamma}(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}
and its own distance function. For these
homogeneous spaces we shall show that the framework in
[
30
]
on localizable tight frame and on the
best approximation can be carried out completely.

1.3.
Organization and convention

The general framework on the localizable homogeneous space emerges from the study on the unit sphere
and the unit ball, as explained in
[
30
]
, and it consists of several topics. Because of its length, we shall
refer to its discussion and formulation to
[
30
]
. The present work, however, is self-contained otherwise
and can be read independently.

The paper is organized as follows. We will state the assertions and just enough background to state them
for the general framework in the next section. Whenever possible, we will not give definition and properties
for homogeneous spaces but only on conic domains and only when they are needed. For a more extensive
discussion on the background materials, we also refer to
[
30
]
. The cases of hyperbolic surface and
hyperboloid will be studied in Section 3 and Section 4, respectively, and the two sections will have parallel
structures, which is also the structure for the conic surface and the cone in
[
30
]
, to emphasis what
is required to carry out the program and for easier comparison between the cases. Each section will contain
several sections and its organization will be described in the preamble of the section.

Throughout this paper, we will denote by
L^{p}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
the weighted
L^{p}
space with respect to
the weight function
{\mathsf{w}}
defined on the domain
\Omega
for
1\leq p\leq\infty
. Its norm will be denote
by
\|\cdot\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}
for
1\leq p\leq\infty
with the understanding that the space is
C(\Omega)
with
the uniform norm when
p=\infty
.

Finally, we shall use
c
,
c^{\prime}
,
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
etc. to denote positive constants that depend on fixed parameters and
their values may change from line to line. Furthermore, we shall write
A\sim B
if
c^{\prime}A\leq B\leq cA
.

2.
Preliminaries

In the first subsection we collect all assertions needed for the general framework on the localized homogeneous
space. Basics of classical orthogonal polynomials that will be used latter are collected in the second subsection.

2.1.
Assertions for the general framework

Let
(\Omega,\varpi,{\mathsf{d}})
be a homogeneous space. For a given set
E\subset\Omega
, we define

\varpi(E)=\int_{E}\varpi(x)\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(x).

The weight function
\varpi
is a doubling weight if there exists a constant
L>0
such that

\varpi(B(x,2r))\leq L\varpi(B(x,r)),\qquad\forall x\in\Omega,\quad r\in(0,r_{0}),

where
r_{0}
is the largest positive number such that
B(x,r)=\{y\in\Omega:{\mathsf{d}}(x,t)<r\}\subset\Omega
.
The smallest
L
for doubling inequality to hold is called the doubling constant of
\varpi
.

Let
\varpi
be a nonnegative weight function on
\Omega
and let
{\langle}\cdot,\cdot{\rangle}_{\varpi}
be the
inner product defined by (
1.1
). For
n=0,1,2,\ldots
, let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}(\Omega,\varpi)
be the space
of orthogonal polynomials with respect to the inner product. If
\{P_{\nu,n}:1\leq\nu\leq\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}(\Omega,\varpi)\}

is an orthogonal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}(\Omega,\varpi)
, then the reproducing kernel of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}(\Omega,\varpi)

satisfies

(2.1)

P_{n}(\varpi;x,y)=\sum_{\nu=1}^{\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}(\Omega,\varpi)}\frac{P_{\nu,n}(x)P_{\nu,n}(y)}{{\langle}P_{\nu,n},P_{\nu,n}{\rangle}_{\varpi}}.

It is the kernel of the projection operator
\operatorname{proj}_{n}:L^{2}(\Omega,\varpi)\mapsto{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}(\Omega,\varpi)

stated in (
1.2
). The Fourier orthogonal series of
f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\varpi)
is given by

(2.2)

f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{proj}_{n}(\varpi;x,y).

2.1.1.
Highly localized kernels

A nonnegative function
\widehat{a}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
is said to be admissible if it obeys either one of the conditions

(a)

\mathrm{supp}\,\widehat{a}\subset[0,2]
and
\widehat{a}(t)=1
,
t\in[0,1]
; or

(b)

\mathrm{supp}\,\widehat{a}\subset[1/2,2]
.

Given such a cut-off function, we define a kernel
L_{n}(\varpi;\cdot,\cdot)
on
\Omega\times\Omega
by

L_{n}(\varpi;x,y):=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\widehat{a}\Big(\frac{j}{n}\Big)P_{j}(\varpi;x,y).

Definition 2.1
.

The kernels
L_{n}(\varpi;\cdot,\cdot)
,
n=1,2,\ldots
, are called highly localized if they satisfy the following
assertions:

Assertion 1
. For
{\kappa}>0
and
x,y\in\Omega
,

|L_{n}(\varpi;x,y)|\leq c_{\kappa}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varpi\!\left(B(x,n^{-1})\right)}\sqrt{\varpi\!\left(B(x,n^{-1})\right)}\left(1+n{\mathsf{d}}(x,y)\right)^{\kappa}}.

Assertion 2
. For
0<\delta\leq\delta_{0}
with some
\delta_{0}<1
and
x_{1}\in B(x_{2},\frac{\delta}{n})
,

\left|L_{n}(\varpi;x_{1},y)-L_{n}(\varpi;x_{2},y)\right|\leq c_{\kappa}\frac{n{\mathsf{d}}(x_{1},x_{2})}{\sqrt{\varpi\!\left(B(x_{1},n^{-1})\right)}\sqrt{\varpi\!\left(B(x_{2},n^{-1})\right)}\left(1+n{\mathsf{d}}(x_{2},y)\right)^{\kappa}}.

Assertion 3
. For sufficient large
{\kappa}>0
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\varpi(y)}{\varpi\!\left(B(y,n^{-1})\right)\big(1+n{\mathsf{d}}(x,y)\big)^{{\kappa}}}\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(y)\leq c.

The third assertion affirms the sharpness of the first two assertions, as can be seen from the following
inequality
[
30
, Lemma 2.1.4]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\varpi
be a doubling weight that satisfies Assertion 3 with
{\kappa}>0
. For
0<p<\infty
, let

\tau={\kappa}-\frac{p}{2}{\alpha}(\varpi)|1-\frac{p}{2}|>0
. Then, for
x\in\Omega
,

(2.3)

\int_{\Omega}\frac{\varpi(y)}{\varpi\!\left(B\left(y,n^{-1}\right)\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}\big(1+n{\mathsf{d}}(x,y)\big)^{\tau}}\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(y)\leq c\,\varpi\!\left(B\left(x,n^{-1}\right)\right)^{1-\frac{p}{2}}.

In particular, the highly localized kernel satisfies

(2.4)

\int_{\Omega}\left|L_{n}(\varpi;x,y)\right|^{p}\varpi(s)\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(y)\leq c\left[\varpi\!\left(B\!\left(x,n^{-1}\right)\right)\right]^{1-p}.

The homogeneous space
(\Omega,\varpi,{\mathsf{d}})
is called
localizable
if
\varpi
is a doubling weight that
admits highly localized kernels.

2.1.2.
Well-separated set of points

We will need well distributed points for discretization, such as in the Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund inequality
and the positive cubature rules, and in our localized polynomial frame. The precise definition is given
as follows.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\Xi
be a discrete set in
\Omega
.

(a)

A finite collection of subsets
\{S_{z}:z\in\Xi\}
is called a partition of
\Omega
if
S_{z}^{\circ}\cap S_{y}^{\circ}=\emptyset
when
z\neq y
and
\Omega=\bigcup_{z\in\Xi}S_{z}
.

(b)

Let
{\varepsilon}>0
. A discrete subset
\Xi
of
\Omega
is called
{\varepsilon}
-separated if
{\mathsf{d}}(x,y)\geq{\varepsilon}

for every two distinct points
x,y\in\Xi
.

(c)

\Xi
is called maximal if there is a constant
c_{d}>1
such that

(2.5)

1\leq\sum_{z\in\Xi}\chi_{B(z,{\varepsilon})}(x)\leq c_{d},\qquad\forall x\in\Omega,

where
\chi_{E}
denotes the characteristic function of the set
E
.

The existence of
{\varepsilon}
-separated points is assumed in the general framework for homogeneous spaces
in
[
30
]
. The construction of such points on a given domain depends on the geometry and the distance
function, and it is crucial for explicit formulation and practical computation of localized frames. For conic
domains
{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
and
{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
, we will give a construction of such points in latter sections.

2.1.3.
Fast decaying polynomials and cubature rules

Let
{\mathsf{w}}
be a doubling weight on
\Omega
. A cubature rule is a finite sum that discretize a given integral,

\int_{\Omega}f(z){\mathsf{w}}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\lambda}_{k}f(x_{k}),

where the equality holds for a given polynomials subspace. We will need positive cubature rules, for which
all
{\lambda}_{k}
are positive, over
{\varepsilon}
-separated points. To quantify the coefficients
{\lambda}_{k}
, which are used for
our localized polynomial frame, we need to show that the Christoffel function
{\lambda}_{n}({\mathsf{w}};\cdot)
, defined by

(2.6)

\displaystyle{\lambda}_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}g(x)=1\\
g\in\Pi_{n}(\Omega)\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega}|g(x)|^{2}{\mathsf{w}}(x)\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(x),

are bounded, where
\Pi_{n}(\Omega)
denotes the space of polynomials of degree
n
restricted on
\Omega
.
This is where our fourth assertion comes in, which ensures the existence of fast decaying polynomials on
the domain
\Omega
.

Assertion 4
.
Let
\Omega
be compact. For each
x\in\Omega
, there is a nonnegative
polynomial
T_{x}
of degree at most
n
that satisfies

(1)

T_{x}(x)=1
,
T_{x}(y)\geq\delta>0
for
y\in B(x,\frac{1}{n})
for some
\delta
independent of
n
,
and, for each
{\gamma}>1
,

0\leq T_{x}(y)\leq c_{\gamma}(1+n{\mathsf{d}}(x,y))^{-{\gamma}},\qquad y\in\Omega;

(2)

there is a polynomial
q_{n}
such that
q_{n}(x)T_{x}(y)
is a polynomial of degree at most
rn
,
for some positive integer
r
, in
x
-variable and
c_{1}\leq q_{n}(x)\leq c_{2}
for
x\in\Omega
for some
positive numbers
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
.

For conic domains, we will establish this assertion by explicitly construction.

2.1.4.
Bernstein inequality for the spectral operator

Our study of the best approximation by polynomials relies on the existence of a spectral operator
for orthogonal polynomials.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\varpi
be a weight function on
\Omega
. We denote by
{\mathfrak{D}}_{\varpi}
the second order
derivation operator that has orthogonal polynomials with respect to
\varpi
as eigenfunctions;
more precisely,

(2.7)

{\mathfrak{D}}_{\varpi}Y=-\mu(n)Y,\qquad\forall\,Y\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}(\Omega,\varpi),

where
\mu
is a nonnegative quadratic polynomial.

For the unit sphere, this is the Laplace-Beltrami operator. Its analogues exist on weighted sphere, ball
and simplexes (cf.
[
9
]
), as well as for conic domains
[
28
,
29
]
. We will need the Bernstein
inequality for the power of these operators. Since
\mu(k)\geq 0
, the operator
-{\mathfrak{D}}_{\varpi}
is a non-negative
operator. A function
f\in L^{p}(\Omega;\varpi)
belongs to the Sobolev space
W_{p}^{r}(\Omega;\varpi)

if there is a function
g\in L^{p}(\Omega;\varpi)
, which we denote by
(-{\mathfrak{D}}_{\varpi})^{\frac{r}{2}}f
, such that

(2.8)

\operatorname{proj}_{n}\left(\varpi;(-{\mathfrak{D}}_{\varpi})^{\frac{r}{2}}f\right)=\mu(n)^{\frac{r}{2}}\operatorname{proj}_{n}(\varpi;f),

where we assume that
f,g\in C(\Omega)
when
p=\infty
. The fractional differential operator

(-{\mathfrak{D}}_{\varpi})^{\frac{r}{2}}f
is a linear operator on the space
W_{p}^{r}(\Omega;\varpi)
defined by
(
2.8
).

For
r>0
, we denote by
L_{n}^{(r)}(\varpi;\cdot,\cdot)
the kernel defined by

(2.9)

L_{n}^{(r)}(\varpi;x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widehat{a}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)[\mu(k)]^{r/2}P_{k}(\varpi;x,y),

which is the kernel
{\mathfrak{D}}_{\varpi}^{r/2}L_{n}(x,y)
with
{\mathfrak{D}}_{\varpi}^{r/2}
applying on
x
variable. Our Bernstein
inequality is proved under the following assumption on the decaying of this kernel.

Assertion 5.

For
r>0
and
{\kappa}>0
, the kernel
L_{n}^{(r)}(\varpi)
satisfies, for
x,y\in\Omega
,

\left|L_{n}^{(r)}(\varpi;x,y)\right|\leq c_{\kappa}\frac{n^{r}}{\sqrt{\varpi(B(x,n^{-1}))}\sqrt{\varpi(B(y,n^{-1}))}}(1+n{\mathsf{d}}(x,y))^{-{\kappa}}.

For
r=0
, this reduces to the Assertion 1. We list it separately since Assertion 5 is not needed for
the localized polynomial frames.

2.2.
Classical orthogonal polynomials

In this subsection we collect a few results on classical orthogonal polynomials that we will need.
There are three families, Jacobi polynomials, spherical harmonics and classical orthogonal polynomials
on the ball. Each of three families comes from an example of a localized homogeneous space, as
explained in
[
30
]
, and has been extensively studied. We will recall only the basics.

2.3.
Jacobi polynomials

For
{\alpha},{\beta}>-1
, the Jacobi weight function is defined by

w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(t):=(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta},\qquad-1<x<1.

Its normalization constant
c^{\prime}_{{\alpha},{\beta}}
, defined by
c^{\prime}_{{\alpha},{\beta}}\int_{-1}^{1}w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(x)dx=1
, is given by

(2.10)

c^{\prime}_{{\alpha},{\beta}}=\frac{1}{2^{{\alpha}+{\beta}+1}}c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}\quad\hbox{with}\quad c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}:=\frac{\Gamma({\alpha}+{\beta}+2)}{\Gamma({\alpha}+1)\Gamma({\beta}+1)}.

The Jacobi polynomials
P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
satisfy the orthogonal relations

c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}^{\prime}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(x)P_{m}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(x)w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(x)\mathrm{d}x=h_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}\delta_{m,n},

where
h_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
is the square of the
L^{2}
norm that satisfies

h_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}=\frac{({\alpha}+1)_{n}({\beta}+1)_{n}({\alpha}+{\beta}+n+1)}{n!({\alpha}+{\beta}+2)_{n}({\alpha}+{\beta}+2n+1)}.

Let
\widehat{a}
be an admissible cut-off function. We defined a polynomial
L_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
by

(2.11)

L_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\widehat{a}\Big(\frac{j}{n}\Big)\frac{P_{j}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)P_{j}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(1)}{h_{j}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}}.

The estimate stated below (
[
3
]
and
[
8
, Theorem 2.6.7]
) will be used several times.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
\ell
be a positive integer and let
\eta
be a function that satisfy,
\eta\in C^{3\ell-1}({\mathbb{R}})
,

\mathrm{supp}\,\eta\subset[0,2]
and
\eta^{(j)}(0)=0
for
j=0,1,2,\ldots,3\ell-2
. Then,
for
{\alpha}\geq{\beta}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
,
t\in[-1,1]
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

(2.12)

\left|\frac{d^{m}}{dt^{m}}L_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)\right|\leq c_{\ell,m,{\alpha}}\left\|\eta^{(3\ell-1)}\right\|_{\infty}\frac{n^{2{\alpha}+2m+2}}{(1+n\sqrt{1-t})^{\ell}},\quad m=0,1,2,\ldots.

The Jacobi polynomials with equal parameters are the Gegenbauer polynomials
C_{n}^{\lambda}
, which
are orthogonal with respect to
w_{\lambda}(x)=(1-x^{2})^{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}}
,

(2.13)

c_{{\lambda}}\int_{-1}^{1}C_{n}^{{\lambda}}(x)C_{m}^{{\lambda}}(x)w_{\lambda}(x)\mathrm{d}x=h_{n}^{{\lambda}}\delta_{n,m},

where the normalization constant
c_{\lambda}
of
w_{\lambda}
and the norm square
h_{n}^{\lambda}
are given by

(2.14)

c_{{\lambda}}=\frac{\Gamma({\lambda}+1)}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})\Gamma({\lambda}+\frac{1}{2})}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad h_{n}^{\lambda}=\frac{{\lambda}}{n+{\lambda}}C_{n}^{\lambda}(1)=\frac{{\lambda}}{n+{\lambda}}\frac{(2{\lambda})_{n}}{n!}.

2.3.1.
Spherical harmonics

Spherical harmonics are homogeneous polynomials that are orthogonal on the unit sphere.
Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}})
be the space of spherical harmonics of degree
n
in
d
variables. Then

\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}})=\binom{n+d-2}{n}+\binom{n+d-3}{n-1}
.
For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
let
\{Y_{\ell}^{n}:1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}})\}
be an
orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}})
; then

\frac{1}{{\omega}_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}Y_{\ell}^{n}(\xi)Y_{\ell^{\prime}}^{m}(\xi)\mathrm{d}\sigma(\xi)=\delta_{n,m},

where
\mathrm{d}\sigma
is the surface measure of
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
and
{\omega}_{d}
denotes the surface area

{\omega}_{d}=2\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}/\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})
of
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
. The spherical harmonics satisfy two
characteristic properties. In terms of the orthonormal basis
\{Y_{\ell}^{n}\}
, its reproducing
kernel
{\mathsf{P}}_{n}(\xi,\eta)
satisfies the addition formula

(2.15)

{\mathsf{P}}_{n}(\xi,\eta)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}})}Y_{\ell}^{n}(\xi)Y_{\ell}^{n}(\eta)=Z_{n}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}({\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle}),\quad\xi,\eta\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}},

where
Z_{n}^{\lambda}
is defined in terms of the Gegenbauer polynomial by

(2.16)

Z_{n}^{\lambda}(t)=\frac{n+{\lambda}}{{\lambda}}C_{n}^{\lambda}(t),\qquad{\lambda}=\frac{d-2}{2}.

Furthermore, spherical harmonics are the eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta_{0}
,
which is the restriction of the Laplace operator
\Delta
on the unit sphere. More precisely (cf.
[
8
, (1.4.9)]
)

(2.17)

\Delta_{0}Y=-n(n+d-2)Y,\qquad Y\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}.

2.3.2.
Orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball

The classical weight function on the unit ball
{\mathbb{B}}^{d}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is defined by

(2.18)

W_{\mu}(x)=(1-\|x\|)^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad x\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d},\quad\mu>-\tfrac{1}{2}.

Its normalization constant is
b_{\mu}^{\mathbb{B}}=\Gamma(\mu+\frac{d+1}{2})/(\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}\Gamma(\mu+\frac{1}{2}))
.
Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},W_{\mu})
be the space of orthogonal polynomials of degree
n
with respect to

W_{\mu}
. An orthogonal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},W_{\mu})
can be given explicitly in terms of
the Jacobi polynomials and spherical harmonics. For
0\leq m\leq n/2
, let
\{Y_{\ell}^{n-2m}:1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n-2m}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}})\}
be an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{H}}_{n-2m}^{d}
. Define
[
9
, (5.2.4)]

(2.19)

P_{\ell,m}^{n}(x)=P_{m}^{(\mu-\frac{1}{2},n-2m+\frac{d-2}{2})}\left(2\|x\|^{2}-1\right)Y_{\ell}^{n-2m}(x).

Then
\{P_{\ell,m}^{n}:0\leq m\leq n/2,1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n-2m}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}})\}
is an orthogonal basis of

{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}(W_{\mu})
. Let
{\mathbf{P}}_{n}(W_{\mu};\cdot,\cdot)
denote the reproducing kernel of the
space
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},W_{\mu})
. This kernel satisfies an analogue of the addition formula
[
26
]
:
for
x,y\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d}
,

(2.20)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}_{n}(W_{\mu};x,y)=c_{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}Z_{n}^{\mu+\frac{d-1}{2}}

\displaystyle\Big({\langle}x,y{\rangle}+u\sqrt{1-\|x\|^{2}}\sqrt{1-\|y\|^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle\times(1-u^{2})^{\mu-1}\mathrm{d}u,

where
\mu>0
and it holds for
\mu=0
under the limit

(2.21)

\lim_{\mu\to 0+}c_{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}f(t)(1-t^{2})^{\mu-1}\mathrm{d}u=\frac{f(1)+f(-1)}{2}.

The orthogonal polynomials with respect to
W_{\mu}
on the unit ball are eigenfunctions of a second
order differential operator
[
9
, (5.2.3)]
: for all
u\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},W_{\mu})
,

(2.22)

\left(\Delta-{\langle}x,\nabla{\rangle}^{2}-(2\mu+d-1){\langle}x,\nabla{\rangle}\right)u=-n(n+2\mu+d-1)u

3.
Homogeneous space on double conic and hyperbolic surfaces

In this chapter we work in the setting of homogeneous space on the surface

{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}=\left\{(x,t):\|x\|^{2}=t^{2}-\varrho^{2},\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,\varrho\leq|t|\leq\sqrt{\varrho^{2}+1}\right\},

which is a hyperbolic surface of two sheets when
\varrho>0
and a double conic surface when
\varrho=0
.
We shall verify that this domain admits a localized homogeneous space for a family of weight functions

{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}
, which are related to the Gegenbauer weight functions and are even in
t
variable.

For
\varrho=0
, the upper part of
{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
with
t\geq 0
is exactly the conic surface
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
.
The analysis on
{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
, however, is of a different character. As a starter, the distance on

{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
is comparable to the Euclidean distance, in contrast to the distance on
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
.
Moreover, the space of orthogonal polynomials are divided naturally into two orthogonal subspaces, consisting
of polynomials even in
t
variable or odd in
t
variable. We consider only orthogonal polynomials even in

t
variable, since they alone satisfy the two characteristic properties: addition formula and the differential
operator having orthogonal polynomials as eigenfunctions. As a consequence, we consider approximation
and localized frames for functions that are even in
t
variable. Many estimates and computations are
easier to handle comparing with the conic surface because of simpler distance function and an addition
formula that is less cumbersome. The structure of the chapter follows that of the
previous two chapters, with contents arranging in the same order under similar section names.

3.1.
Distance on double conic and hyperbolic surfaces

Whenever it is necessary to emphasis the dependence on
\varrho
, we write
{}_{\varrho}{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}

instead of
{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
. In most places, however, we use
{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
to avoid excessive notations.

The surface
{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
can be decomposed as an upper part and a lower part,

{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}={\mathbb{X}}_{0,+}^{d+1}\cup{\mathbb{X}}_{0,-}^{d+1}=\{(x,t)\in{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}:t\geq 0\}\cup\{(x,t)\in{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}:t\leq 0\}.

For
\varrho=0
, the upper part
{\mathbb{X}}_{0,+}^{d+1}
is exactly the conic surface
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
.

As
{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
is a bounded domain, its distance function should take into account of the boundary
at the two ends, but it does not regard the apex as a boundary point because of symmetry. We first define
a distance on the double conic surface; that is, when
\varrho=0
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\varrho=0
. For
(x,t),(y,s)\in{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
, define

{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,s))=\arccos\left({\langle}x,y{\rangle}+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\right).

To see that
{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is indeed a distance on the double conic surface
{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
,
we let
X=\big(x,\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\big)
and
Y=\big(y,\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\big)
, so that
X,Y\in{\mathbb{S}}^{d}
if

x,y\in{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
. Then

{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,s))={\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{S}}^{d}}(X,Y),\quad(x,t),(y,s)\in{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},

from which it follows readily that
{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}
is indeed a distance on
{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
.

This distance is quite different from the distance
{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}}(\cdot,\cdot)
of
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}

defined by

{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,s))=\arccos\left(\sqrt{\frac{ts+{\langle}x,y{\rangle}}{2}}+\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s}\right),\quad(x,t),(y,s)\in{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}

in
[
30
]
and it resembles the distance
{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{B}}}(\cdot,\cdot)
of the unit ball which is of the same
form but with
t
and
s
replaced by
\|x\|
and
\|y\|
. The distance
{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}
is related to the
distance on the sphere, defined by

(3.1)

{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{S}}}(\xi,\eta)=\arccos{\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle},\qquad\xi,\eta\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}},

and the distance on the interval
[-1,1]
, defined by

(3.2)

{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)=\arccos\left(ts+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\right),\qquad t,s\in[-1,1].

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\varrho=0
and
d\geq 2
. For
(x,t),(y,s)\in{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
, write
x=t\xi
and
y=s\eta
with

\xi,\eta\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
, it holds

(3.3)

1-\cos{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,s))=1-\cos{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)+ts\left(1-\cos{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{S}}}(\xi,\eta)\right).

In particular,

(3.4)

c_{1}{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,s))\leq{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)+\sqrt{ts}\,{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{S}}}(\xi,\eta)\leq c_{2}{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,s)).

Proof.

Using
{\langle}x,y{\rangle}\mathrm{sign}(ts)=ts{\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle}
, we obtain

\displaystyle 1-\cos{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,s))=1-ts{\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle}-\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\sqrt{1-s^{2}}.

Hence, (
3.3
) follows immediately from (
3.2
) and (
3.1
). Moreover,
using (
3.3
), the estimate (
3.4
) follows from
1-\cos{\theta}=2\sin^{2}\frac{{\theta}}{2}
,

\frac{1}{\pi}{\theta}\leq\sin\frac{{\theta}}{2}\leq\frac{{\theta}}{2}
for
0\leq{\theta}\leq\pi
, and
(a+b)^{2}/2\leq a^{2}+b^{2}\leq(a+b)^{2}
for
a,b\geq 0
.
∎

In particular, the distance on the linear segment
l_{\xi}=\{(t\xi,t):-1\leq t\leq 1\}
for any fixed
\xi\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}

on the double conic surface becomes

{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}\big((t\xi,t),(s\xi,s)\big)={\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s).

We note that the line
l_{\xi}
passes through the origin when it passes from the upper conic surface
to the lower conic surface; in fact, the line passes through
(\xi,1)
on the rim of the upper conic surface
to
(-\xi,-1)
on the opposite rim of the lower conic surface.

Remark 3.1
.

In
[
30
, Remark 4.1]
, it is pointed out that the distance
{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is incompatible
with the Euclidean distance at around
t=0
since, for
t=s
,

{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,t))\sim\sqrt{t}\mathrm{d}_{{\mathbb{S}}}(\xi,\eta)\quad\hbox{and}\quad\|(x,t)-(y,t)\|\sim t\mathrm{d}_{{\mathbb{S}}}(\xi,\eta).

In contrast, the distance
{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}(\cdot,\cdot)
on the double conic surface satisfies,

{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,t))\sim|t|\mathrm{d}_{{\mathbb{S}}}(\xi,\eta)\sim\|(x,t)-(y,t)\|.

We will also need the following lemma in the proof of our estimates.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\varrho=0
and
d\geq 2
. If
(x,t),(y,s)
both in
{\mathbb{X}}_{0,+}^{d+1}
or both in
{\mathbb{X}}_{0,-}^{d+1}
, then

\big|t-s\big|\leq{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,s))\quad\hbox{and}\quad\big|\sqrt{1-t^{2}}-\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\big|\leq{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,s)).

Proof.

It suffices to consider the case
t,s\geq 0
. Let
t=\cos{\theta}
and
s=\cos\phi
with
0\leq{\theta},\phi\leq\pi/2
.
It follows readily that
\cos{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,s))\leq ts+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\sqrt{1-s^{2}}=\cos({\theta}-\phi)
, which is
equivalently to
|{\theta}-\phi|\leq{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,s))
. Hence, the stated inequalities follows from

\big|t-s\big|=|\cos{\theta}-\cos\phi|=2\sin\frac{{\theta}+\phi}{2}\sin\frac{|{\theta}-\phi|}{2}\leq|{\theta}-\phi|,

and, similarly,

\left|\sqrt{1-t^{2}}-\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\right|=|\sin{\theta}-\sin\phi|=2\cos\frac{{\theta}+\phi}{2}\sin\frac{|{\theta}-\phi|}{2}\leq|{\theta}-\phi|

for all
t,s\in[-1,1]
. This completes the proof.
∎

For
\varrho>0
, the hyperbolic surface
{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}={\mathbb{X}}_{0,+}^{d+1}\cup{\mathbb{X}}_{0,-}^{d+1}
consists of
two disjoint parts. For all practical purpose, it is sufficient to consider the distance between points that lie in
the same part. For
(x,t)
and
(y,t)
both in
{\mathbb{X}}_{0,+}^{d+1}
or both in
{\mathbb{X}}_{0,-}^{d+1}
, we define

(3.5)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}^{\varrho}((x,t),(y,s))

\displaystyle=\arccos\left({\langle}x,y{\rangle}+\sqrt{1+\varrho^{2}-t^{2}}\sqrt{1+\varrho^{2}-s^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}\left(\Big(x,\sqrt{t^{2}-\varrho^{2}}\Big),\Big(y,\sqrt{s^{2}-\varrho^{2}}\Big)\right).

It is easy to see that this is a distance function and, evidently, we can derive its properties as we did for
the distance on the double conic surface.

3.2.
A family of doubling weights

For
d\geq 2
,
{\beta}>-\frac{1}{2}
and
{\gamma}>-\frac{1}{2}
, let
{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\rho}
be the weight function defined on the
hyperbolic surface
{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
by

(3.6)

{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}(t)=|t|(t^{2}-\varrho^{2})^{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}(\varrho^{2}+1-t^{2})^{{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\varrho\geq 0,

for
\varrho\leq|t|\leq\sqrt{\varrho^{2}+1}
. When
\varrho=0
, or on the double conic surface, it becomes

{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{0}(t)=|t|^{2{\beta}}(1-t^{2})^{{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}},

which is integrable if
{\beta}>-\frac{d+1}{2}
on
{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
. When
{\beta}=0
,
{\mathsf{w}}_{0,{\gamma}}^{0}
is the classical
weight function for the Gegenbauer polynomial
C_{n}^{\gamma}
. Evidently, the two cases are related,

{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}(t)=|t|{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2},{\gamma}}^{0}\left(\sqrt{t^{2}-\varrho^{2}}\right).

Let
\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\varrho}
denote the surface measure on

{}_{\varrho}{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
. Then
\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\varrho}(x,t)=\mathrm{d}\omega_{\sqrt{t^{2}-\varrho^{2}}}(x)\mathrm{d}t
, where

\mathrm{d}\omega_{r}
denotes the surface measure on the sphere
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}_{r}
of radius
r
. It follows then

(3.7)

\displaystyle\int_{{}_{\varrho}{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}}f(x,t)|t|\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\varrho}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\varrho\leq|t|\leq\varrho+1}|t|\int_{\|x\|=\sqrt{t^{2}-\varrho^{2}}}f(x,t)\mathrm{d}\omega_{\sqrt{t^{2}-\varrho^{2}}}(x)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{|s|\leq 1}\int_{\|x\|=|s|}f(x,\sqrt{s^{2}+\varrho^{2}})|s|\mathrm{d}\omega_{|s|}(x)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{{}_{0}{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}}f(x,\sqrt{s^{2}+\varrho^{2}})|s|\mathrm{d}\sigma(x,s).

Using this relation, it is easy to verify that the normalization constant
{\mathsf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}
of the weight function

{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}
is given by

{\mathsf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}={\mathsf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}=\frac{\Gamma({\beta}+{\gamma}+\frac{d+1}{2})}{\sigma_{d}\Gamma({\beta}+\frac{d}{2})\Gamma({\gamma}+\frac{1}{2})}.

For
r>0
,
(x,t)
on the hyperbolic surface
{}_{\varrho}{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
and
(x,t)\neq(0,0)
, we denote the ball centered
at
(x,t)
with radius
r
by

{\mathsf{c}}_{\varrho}((x,t),r):=\left\{(y,s)\in{}_{\varrho}{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}:{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}^{\varrho}\big((x,t),(y,s)\big)\leq r\right\}.

Lemma 3.4
.

For
\varrho\geq 0
and
(x,t)\in{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
,

{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}\big({\mathsf{c}}_{\varrho}((x,t),r)\big)={\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{0}\left({\mathsf{c}}_{0}\left(\big(x,\sqrt{t^{2}-\varrho^{2}}\big),r\right)\right).

Proof.

By the definition of
{\mathsf{w}}(E)
and (
3.5
),

\displaystyle{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}\big({\mathsf{c}}_{\varrho}((x,t),r)\big)\,

\displaystyle={\mathsf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}\int_{{\mathsf{c}}_{\varrho}((x,t),r)}{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}(s)\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\varrho}(y,s)

\displaystyle={\mathsf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}\int_{{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}\big((x,\sqrt{t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}}),(y,\sqrt{s^{2}-\|y\|^{2}})\big)\leq r}{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}(s)\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\varrho}(y,s)

\displaystyle={\mathsf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}\int_{{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}\big((x,\sqrt{t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}}),(y,s)\big)\leq r}{\mathsf{w}}^{0}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}(s)\mathrm{d}\sigma_{0}(y,s)

\displaystyle={\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{0}\left({\mathsf{c}}_{0}\left(\big(x,\sqrt{t^{2}-\varrho^{2}}\big),r\right)\right),

where we have used (
3.7
) in the second to last step.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
r>0
and
(x,t)\in{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
. For
{\beta}>-\frac{d+1}{2}
and
{\gamma}>-\frac{1}{2}
,

(3.8)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{0}\big({\mathsf{c}}_{0}((x,t),r)\big)\,

\displaystyle={\mathsf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{0}\int_{{\mathsf{c}}_{0}((x,t),r)}{\mathsf{w}}^{0}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}(s)\mathrm{d}\sigma(y,s)

\displaystyle\sim r^{d}\big(t^{2}+r^{2}\big)^{{\beta}}\big(1-t^{2}+r^{2}\big)^{{\gamma}}.

In particular,
{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{0}
is a doubling weight on the double conic surface and the doubling index
is given by
{\alpha}({\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{0})=d+2\max\{0,{\beta}\}+2\max\{0,{\gamma}\}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume
r
is bounded by a small positive number, say
r\leq\frac{\pi}{12}
and

0\leq t\leq 1
. Since
{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{0}
is even in
t
, we only need to work on
{\mathbb{X}}_{0,+}^{d+1}={\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
.
Let
x=t\xi
and
y=s\eta
for
\xi,\eta\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
.
By the inequality (
3.3
), from
{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,s))\leq r
we obtain
{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)\leq r
; moreover, denote
\tau_{r}(t,s)=(\cos r-\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\sqrt{1-s^{2}})/(ts)
and
{\theta}_{r}(t,s)=\arccos\tau_{r}(t,s)
, we also have
{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{S}}}(\xi,\eta)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\arccos\tau_{r}(t,s)=\tfrac{1}{2}{\theta}_{r}(t,s)
. Then it is easy to see, since
\mathrm{d}\sigma(y,s)=s^{d-1}\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathbb{S}}(\eta)\mathrm{d}s
, that

\displaystyle{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{0}\big({\mathsf{c}}((x,t),r)\big)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)\leq r}s^{d-1}\int_{{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{S}}}(\xi,\eta)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}{\theta}_{r}(t,s)}{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{0}(s\eta,s)\mathrm{d}\sigma_{{\mathbb{S}}}(\eta)\mathrm{d}s.

By symmetry, we can choose
\xi=(1,0,\ldots,0)
and use the identity (cf.
[
8
, (A.5.1)]
)

(3.9)

\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}g({\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle})d\sigma(\eta)={\omega}_{d-1}\int_{0}^{\pi}g(\cos{\theta})(\sin{\theta})^{d-2}\mathrm{d}{\theta}

with
{\omega}_{d-1}
being the surface are of
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-2}
, to obtain that

\displaystyle{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{0}\big({\mathsf{c}}((x,t),r)\big)={\omega}_{d-1}\int_{{\mathsf{d}}_{[0,1]}(t,s)\leq r}s^{d-1}{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{0}(s)\int_{0}^{\tfrac{1}{2}{\theta}_{r}(t,s)}(\sin{\theta})^{d-2}\mathrm{d}{\theta}\mathrm{d}s.

Since
{\theta}\sim\sin{\theta}\sim\sqrt{1-\cos{\theta}}
, it follows then

(3.10)

{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{0}\big({\mathsf{c}}_{0}((x,t),r)\big)\sim\int_{{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)\leq r}s^{d-1}{\mathsf{w}}^{0}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}(s)\big(1-\tau_{r}(t,s)\big)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\mathrm{d}s.

We now need to consider three cases. If
3r<t<1-3r
, then we can use Lemma
3.3
to conclude
that
s^{2}\sim t^{2}+r^{2}
and
1-s^{2}\sim 1-t^{2}+r^{2}
, so that

\displaystyle{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{0}\big({\mathsf{c}}_{0}((x,t),r)\big)

\displaystyle\,\sim(t^{2}+r^{2})^{\beta}(1-t^{2}+r^{2})^{{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\times\int_{{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)\leq r}\big(\cos({\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s))-\cos r\big)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}.

Setting
t=\cos{\theta}
and
s=\cos\phi
so that
{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)=|{\theta}-\phi|
, then the last integral is easily
seen to be

\int_{|{\theta}-\phi|\leq r}\big(\cos({\theta}-\phi)-\cos r\big)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\mathrm{d}\phi=c\int_{|\zeta|\leq r}\big(\sin\tfrac{\zeta-r}{2}\sin\tfrac{\zeta+r}{2}\big)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\mathrm{d}\zeta\sim r^{d}.

This completes the proof of the first case. If
t\leq 3r
, then
|t-s|\leq d_{[-1,1]}(t,s)\leq r
so
that
s\leq 4r
. Evidently
1-s^{2}\sim 1-t^{2}\sim 1
in this case. Furthermore, let
t=\sin{\theta}
and
s=\sin\phi
;
then
|{\theta}-\phi|=d_{[-1,1]}(t,s)\leq r
, which is easily seen to be equivalent to
|\tau_{r}(t,s)|\leq 1
. Hence, using

1-\tau_{r}(t,s)\leq 2
and
s\leq 4r
, we obtain by (
3.10
) and
\sin\phi\sim\phi
,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}\big({\mathsf{c}}((x,t),r)\big)\leq c\int_{{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)\leq r}s^{d+2{\beta}-1}\mathrm{d}s\sim r^{2{\beta}+d},

which proves the upper bound in (
3.8
). For the lower bound, we consider a subset of
{\mathsf{c}}((x,t),r)

with
\mathrm{d}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)\leq r/2
. Using the upper bound of
s
and
t
, we then deduce

1-\tau_{r}(t,s)=\frac{\cos d_{[-1,1]}(t,s)-\cos r}{ts}\geq\frac{\cos\tfrac{r}{2}-\cos r}{12r^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}},

where in the last step we have used
\sin{\theta}\geq\frac{2}{\pi}{\theta}
, which shows that

\displaystyle{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}\big({\mathsf{c}}((x,t),r)\big)\,

\displaystyle\geq c\int_{{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)\leq r}s^{d+2{\beta}-1}\mathrm{d}s\sim r^{2{\beta}+d}.

Finally, if
t\geq 1-3r
, then we have
s\sim t\sim 1
for
(y,s)\in{\mathsf{c}}((x,t),r)
. Since

{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)={\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(\sqrt{1-t^{2}},\sqrt{1-s^{2}})
, changing variable
s\mapsto\sqrt{1-s^{2}}
in
(
3.10
), we see that this case can be reduced to that of the second case.
This completes the proof of (
3.8
).
∎

3.3.
Orthogonal polynomials on hyperbolic surfaces

For
{\gamma}>-\frac{1}{2}
, we define the inner product on the hyperbolic surface by

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}}={\mathsf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}\int_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}}f(x,t)g(x,t){\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}(t)\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\varrho}(x,t),

where
{\beta}>-\frac{1}{2}
if
\varrho>0
and
{\beta}>-\frac{d+1}{2}
if
\varrho=0
. The orthogonal polynomials
with respect to this inner product are studied in
[
29
]
.
Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho})
be the space of these polynomials with respect to
this inner product, which has the same dimension as the space of spherical harmonics
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d+1}
.
Because the weight function
{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}
is even in
t
, this space can be factored as

{\mathcal{V}}_{n}\left({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}\right)={\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{E}\big({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}\big)\bigoplus{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{O}\big({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}\big),

where the subspace
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{E}({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho})
consists of orthogonal polynomials that
are even in
t
variable, and the subspace
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{O}({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho})
consists of
orthogonal polynomials that are odd in
t
variable. Moreover,

\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{E}\big({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}\big)=\binom{n+d-1}{n},\quad\quad\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{O}\big({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}\big)=\binom{n+d-2}{n-1}.

It turns out that an orthogonal basis can be given in terms of the spherical harmonics and the Jacobi
polynomials for the subspace
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{E}({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho})
for all
\varrho\geq 0
, but
for the subspace
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{O}({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho})
only when
\varrho=0
. For example,
let
\{Y_{\ell}^{m}:1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{m}^{d}\}
denote an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{H}}_{m}^{d}
. Then the
polynomials

(3.12)

{\mathsf{C}}_{m,\ell}^{n}(x,t)=P_{k}^{({\gamma}-\frac{1}{2},n-2k+{\beta}+\frac{d-2}{2})}\big(2t^{2}-2\varrho^{2}-1\big)Y_{\ell}^{n-2k}(x),

where
1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n-2k}^{d}
and
0\leq k\leq n/2
, form an orthogonal basis of

{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{E}({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho})
. Since we will not work directly with explicit bases,
we refer to
[
29
]
for further information, where these polynomials are called, when
{\beta}=0
, the
Gegenbauer polynomials on the hyperbolic surface or on the double conic surface when
\varrho=0
.

Let
{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{E}({\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho};\cdot,\cdot)
be the reproducing kernel of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{E}({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho})
, which can be written in terms of the basis (
3.12
) as

{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{E}\left({\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho};(x,t),(y,s)\right)=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n-2m}^{d}}\frac{{\mathsf{C}}_{m,\ell}^{n}(x,t){\mathsf{C}}_{m,\ell}^{n}(y,s)}{{\langle}{\mathsf{C}}_{m,\ell}^{n},{\mathsf{C}}_{m,\ell}^{n}{\rangle}_{{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}}}.

Let
\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{E}\big({\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}\big):L^{2}\big({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}\big)\mapsto{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{E}\big({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}\big)
be the orthogonal projection operator. Then it
can be written as

\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{E}({\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho};f)={\mathsf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}\int_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}}f(y){\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{E}({\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho};\cdot,(y,s)){\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho}(s)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s.

If
f
is a function that is even in the variable
t
on
{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
, then its orthogonal projection on

{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{O}({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho})
becomes zero, so that its Fourier orthogonal expansion
is given by

(3.13)

f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{E}({\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho};f).

Hence, the kernel
{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{E}({\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}^{\varrho};\cdot,\cdot)
is meaningful for studying the Fourier
orthogonal expansions on the hyperboloid.

If
\varrho=0
, then the upper part
{\mathbb{X}}_{0,+}^{d+1}={\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
is the upper conic surface. The function

that is even in

variable can be regarded as defined on

or as the even extension
in
t
variable of a function defined on the upper conic surface. Consequently, the Fourier expansion
(
3.13
) works for the function
f
defined on the upper conic surface
{\mathbb{X}}_{0,+}^{d+1}
when

\varrho=0
. The latter, however, is different from the Fourier expansions in the Jacobi polynomials on the
conic surface
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
discussed in
[
30
]
.

The case

Theorem 3.9

Proof.

By (

\displaystyle L_{n}^{({\lambda}-\frac{1}{2},{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2})}\big(\zeta(x,t,y,s;v)\big)(1-v^{2})^{{\gamma}-1}\mathrm{d}v.

S_{n}(1)=1,\qquad 0\leq S_{n}(\cos{\theta})\leq c\big(1+n{\theta}\big)^{-2r},\quad 0\leq{\theta}\leq\pi.

To construct polynomials on hyperbolic surface, we define, for

\displaystyle S((x,t),(y,s))\,

\displaystyle=S_{n}\left({\langle}x,y{\rangle}+\sqrt{1+\varrho^{2}-t^{2}}\sqrt{1+\varrho^{2}-s^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle+S_{n}\left({\langle}x,y{\rangle}-\sqrt{1+\varrho^{2}-t^{2}}\sqrt{1+\varrho^{2}-s^{2}}\right).

n
in either

or

Then

T_{(x,t)}^{\varrho}(x,t)=1
. If
0\leq{\theta}\leq\frac{2\pi}{2m+1}

Hence, since

T_{(x,t)}^{\varrho}(y,s)\geq\frac{S_{n}\left({\langle}x,y{\rangle}+\sqrt{1+\varrho^{2}-t^{2}}\sqrt{1+\varrho^{2}-s^{2}}\right)}{1+S_{n}(2t^{2}-2\varrho^{2}-1)}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{2r}

\displaystyle 0\leq T_{(x,t)}^{\varrho}(y,s)\,

\displaystyle\leq c\left(1+n\sqrt{1-{\langle}x,y{\rangle}-\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}\right)^{-2r}

\displaystyle=c\left(1+n\sqrt{1-\cos{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,s))}\right)^{-2r}\sim\left(1+n{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,s))\right)^{-2r}

3.8.
Characterization of best approximation

For
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{0,{\gamma}}^{\varrho})

{\mathsf{E}}_{n}(f)_{p,{\mathsf{w}}^{\varrho}_{0,{\gamma}}}
the best

\Pi_{n}({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1})

n

on the surface
{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
, in the norm
\|\cdot\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}_{0,{\gamma}}^{\varrho}}

{\mathsf{E}}_{n}(f)_{p,{\mathsf{w}}_{0,{\gamma}}^{\varrho}}:=\inf_{g\in\Pi_{n}({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1})}\|f-g\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}_{0,{\gamma}}^{\varrho}},\qquad 1\leq p\leq\infty.

If
f
is even in
t

\left\|f(x,t)-\frac{1}{2}(g(x,t)+g(x,-t))\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}_{0,{\gamma}}^{\varrho}}\leq\|f-g\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}_{0,{\gamma}}^{\varrho}}.

Hence, we can choose the polynomial of best approximation from
\Pi_{n}^{E}({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1})
when

is
symmetric in
t
variable. Following the general framework in
[
30

t

We define a

and a doubling weight
{\mathsf{w}}

t

and

{\mathsf{K}}_{r}(f,t)_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}:=\inf_{g\in W_{p}^{r}({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}})}\left\{\|f-g\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}+t^{r}\left\|(-\Delta_{0,{\gamma}}^{\varrho})^{\frac{r}{2}}f\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\right\},

where the Sobolev space
W_{p}^{r}\big({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}\big)
is the space that consists of
g\in L^{p}({\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}})
,
even in
t
variable, so that
\left\|(-\Delta_{0,{\gamma}}^{\varrho})^{\frac{r}{2}}g\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}
is finite. The
K
-functional
is well defined, as shown in
[
30
, Section 3.3]

t

{\omega}_{r}(f;\rho)_{p,{\mathsf{w}}_{0,{\gamma}}^{\varrho}}=\sup_{0\leq{\theta}\leq\rho}\left\|\left(I-{\mathsf{S}}_{{\theta},{\mathsf{w}}_{0,{\gamma}}^{\varrho}}\right)^{r/2}f\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}_{0,{\gamma}}^{\varrho}},\quad 1\leq p\leq\infty,

and

\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{E}({\mathsf{w}}_{0,{\gamma}}^{\varrho};{\mathsf{S}}_{{\theta},{\mathsf{w}}_{0,{\gamma}}^{\varrho}}f)=\frac{C_{n}^{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}}(\cos{\theta})}{C_{n}^{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}}(1)}\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{E}({\mathsf{w}}_{0,{\gamma}}^{\varrho};f).

The operator is well defined since the above relations determine it uniquely among functions even in

t

and the modulus of smoothness satisfies
the usual properties under its name. Such a modulus of smoothness is in line with
those defined on the unit sphere and the unit ball

,
20
,
27
]
but its structure is more complicated.
There are recent results for modulus of smoothness on fairly general domains in

[
5
,
6
,
23
,
24
]
, defined via simple difference operators of functions, but conic domains are not
included.

By

For

1-\cos{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}}((x,t),(y,s))=1-\cos{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)+ts\left(1-\cos{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{B}}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\right).

In particular, if

and

have the same sign, then

The proof is similar to that of Proposition
3.2
, using (
3.2
) and (
4.2
).
In particular, it follows that the distance on the line segment
l_{x}=\{(tx^{\prime},t):-1\leq t\leq 1\}
, where
x^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d}
,
of the double cone becomes
{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)
as expected. We will also need the following lemma

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\varrho=0
and
d\geq 2
. For
(x,t),(y,s)
either both in
{\mathbb{X}}_{+}^{d+1}
or both in
{\mathbb{X}}_{-}^{d+1}

\big|t-s\big|\leq{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}}((x,t),(y,s))\quad\hbox{and}\quad\big|\sqrt{1-t^{2}}-\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\big|\leq{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}}((x,t),(y,s)).

and

\big|\sqrt{t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}}-\sqrt{s^{2}-\|y\|^{2}}\big|\leq(\sqrt{2}+\pi){\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}}((x,t),(y,s)).

Proof.

We consider only
t,s\geq 0
. Let
x=tx^{\prime}
and
y=sy^{\prime}
with
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d}
. Setting
t=\cos{\theta}

and

. Using the inequality
|{\langle}x^{\prime},y^{\prime}{\rangle}+\sqrt{1-\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}}\sqrt{1-\|y^{\prime}\|^{2}}|\leq 1
, it follows readily that

\cos{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,s))\leq ts+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\sqrt{1-s^{2}}=\cos({\theta}-\phi)
, which allows us to follow
the proof of Lemma
3.3
to establish the first two inequalities. For third inequality, we assume
without loss of generality that

. Then,

\displaystyle\big|\sqrt{t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}}-\sqrt{s^{2}-\|y\|^{2}}\big|

\displaystyle=\left||t|\sqrt{1-\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}}-|s|\sqrt{1-\|y^{\prime}\|^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq|t-s|\sqrt{1-\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}}+|s|\left|\sqrt{1-\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}}-\sqrt{1-\|y^{\prime}\|^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}}((x,t),(y,s))+\sqrt{ts}\,{\mathsf{d}}_{\mathbb{B}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),

where the last step uses the inequality
[

Hence, the third inequality follows from (

). This completes the proof.

4.2.
A family of doubling weights

For
d\geq 2
,
{\beta}>-\tfrac{1}{2}
,
{\gamma}>-\frac{1}{2}
and

, let
W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}
be the
weight function defined on the hyperboloid
{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
by

(4.7)

W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}(x,t)={\mathbf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}|t|(t^{2}-\varrho^{2})^{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}(1+\varrho^{2}-t^{2})^{{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}}(t^{2}-\varrho^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}.

When
\varrho=0
or the double cone, the weight function becomes

W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{0}(x,t)={\mathbf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{0}|t|^{2{\beta}}(1-t^{2})^{{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}}(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}},

which remains integrable over
{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
if
{\beta}>-\frac{d+1}{2}
. Using the identity

(4.8)

\displaystyle\int_{{}_{\varrho}{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}}f(x,t)|t|\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\varrho\leq|t|\leq\varrho+1}|t|\int_{\|x\|\leq\sqrt{t^{2}-\varrho^{2}}}f(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{|s|\leq 1}|s|\int_{\|x\|\leq|s|}f(x,\sqrt{s^{2}+\varrho^{2}})\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{{}_{0}{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}}f(x,\sqrt{s^{2}+\varrho^{2}})|s|\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}s,

it is easy to see that the normalization constant
{\mathbf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}
of
W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}(x,t)

satisfies

{\mathbf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}={\mathbf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{0}=b_{\mu}^{\mathbb{B}}\frac{\Gamma({\beta}+\mu+{\gamma}+\frac{d+1}{2})}{\Gamma({\beta}+\mu\frac{d}{2})\Gamma({\gamma}+\frac{1}{2})},

where
b_{\mu}^{\mathbb{B}}
is the normalization constant for the weigt function
\varpi_{\mu}
in (
2.18
)
on
{\mathbb{B}}^{d}
.

For
r>0
and
(x,t)
on the solid hyperboloid
{}_{\varrho}{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
, we denote the ball centered
at
(x,t)
with radius
r

{\mathbf{c}}_{\varrho}((x,t),r):=\left\{(y,s)\in{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}:{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}}^{\varrho}\big((x,t),(y,s)\big)\leq r\right\}.

The following lemma can be proved similarly as that of Lemma
3.4
.

Lemma 4.4
.

For
\varrho>0
and
(x,t)\in{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
r>0
and
(x,t)\in{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
. Then for
{\beta}>-\frac{d+1}{2}
and
{\gamma}>-1
and
\mu\geq 0
,

(4.9)

\displaystyle W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{0}\big({\mathbf{c}}_{0}((x,t),r)\big)

\displaystyle:={\mathbf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{0}\int_{{\mathbf{c}}_{0}((x,t),r)}W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{0}(y,s)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\,\sim r^{d+1}\left(t^{2}+r^{2}\right)^{{\beta}}\left(1-t^{2}+r^{2}\right)^{{\gamma}}\left(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}+r^{2}\right)^{\mu}.

In particular,
W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{0}
is a doubling weight on the double cone and the doubling index
is given by
{\alpha}(W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{0})=d+1+2\mu+2\max\{0,{\beta}\}+2\max\{0,{\gamma}\}
.

Proof.

Let
\tau_{r}(t,s)
and
{\theta}_{r}(t,s)=\arccos\tau_{r}(t,s)
be as in the proof of Proposition
3.5
.
From
{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}}((x,t),(y,s))\leq r
, we obtain
{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)\leq r
and, by (
4.1
)

{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{B}}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq\arccos\left(2[\tau_{r}(t,s)]^{2}-1\right)=\tfrac{1}{2}\arccos\tau_{r}(t,s)=\tfrac{1}{2}{\theta}_{r}(t,s),

where
{\mathsf{d}}_{\mathbb{B}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the distance on the unit ball
{\mathbb{B}}^{d}
. Hence, it follows that

\displaystyle W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{0}\big({\mathbf{c}}_{0}((x,t),r)\big)={\mathbf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{0}\int_{{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)\leq r}s^{d}\int_{{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{B}}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq\tfrac{1}{2}{\theta}_{r}(t,s)}W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{0}(y,s)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad\sim\int_{{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)\leq r}s^{2{\beta}+2\mu+d-1}(1-s^{2})^{{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}s\int_{{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{B}}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq\tfrac{1}{2}{\theta}_{r}(t,s)}(1-\|y^{\prime}\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}y.

For
\mu\geq 0
and
0<\rho\leq 1
, it is known
[
19
, Lemma 5.3]
or
[
8
, p. 107]
that

\int_{{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{B}}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq\rho}(1-\|y^{\prime}\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}\sim(1-\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}+\rho^{2})^{\mu}\rho^{d},

which implies, together with
{\theta}_{r}(t,s)\sim\sqrt{1-\tau_{r},t,s}
, that

\displaystyle W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{0}\big({\mathbf{c}}_{0}((x,t),r)\big)\sim

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)\leq r}s^{2{\beta}+2\mu+d-1}(1-s^{2})^{{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\times(1-\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}+1-\tau_{r}(t,s))^{\mu}(1-\tau_{r}(t,s))^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{d}s.

If
t\geq 3r
, then
s\sim t+r
and, by
1-\tau_{r}(t,s)=(\cos{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)-\cos r)/(ts)
, it follows that

s^{2\mu}\big(1-\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}+1-\tau_{r}(t,s)\big)^{\mu}\sim(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}+r^{2})^{\mu}.

With this term removed, the integral of the remaining integrand can be estimated by following the
estimates of Case 1 and Case 3 of the proof of Proposition
3.5
. If
t\leq 3r
, then

t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}+r^{2}\sim r^{2}
. We use
1-\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}+1-\tau_{r}(t,s)\leq 2
for the upper bound and

1-\tau_{r}(t,s)\geq 1/(8\pi^{2})
on the subset
{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)\leq r/2
, proved in the Case 2 of the proof
of Proposition
3.5
, to remove the term
\big(1-\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}+1-\tau_{r}(t,s)\big)^{\mu}
from the
integral. The rest of the proof then follows from that of the Case 2 of the proof of Proposition
3.5
.
This completes the proof.
∎

4.3.
Orthogonal polynomials on the hyperboloid

With respect to the weight function
W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}
, we defined the inner product

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{W}=\int_{{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}}f(x,t)g(x,t)W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho})
be the space of these orthogonal polynomials of degree
n
,
which has the dimension
\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W)=\binom{n+d}{n}
. Like the decomposition on the surface

{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
, this space satisfies

{\mathcal{V}}_{n}\left({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}\right)={\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{E}\big({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}\big)\bigoplus{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{O}\big({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}\big),

where the subspace
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{E}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho})
consists of orthogonal polynomials that
are even in
t
variable, whereas the subspace
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{O}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho})
consists of
orthogonal polynomials that are odd in
t
variable.

An orthogonal basis can be given explicitly in terms of the Jacobi polynomials and classical orthogonal
polynomials on the unit ball for the subspace
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{E}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho})
for all

\varrho\geq 0
, but for the subspace
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{O}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho})
only when
\varrho=0
.
For example, let
\{P_{\mathbf{k}}^{n-2k}:|{\mathbf{k}}|=n-2k,\,{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}\}
denote an orthonormal basis of

{\mathcal{V}}_{n-2k}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},W_{\mu})
. Then the polynomials

(4.10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{n-2k,{\mathbf{k}}}^{n}(x,t)=

\displaystyle\,P_{k}^{({\gamma}-\frac{1}{2},n-2k+{\beta}+\mu+\frac{d-2}{2})}(2t^{2}-2\varrho^{2}-1)

\displaystyle\times(t^{2}-\varrho^{2})^{\frac{n-2k}{2}}P_{\mathbf{k}}^{n-2k}\bigg(\frac{x}{\sqrt{t^{2}-\varrho^{2}}}\bigg)

with
|{\mathbf{k}}|=n-2k
and
0\leq k\leq n/2
form an orthogonal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{E}({\mathbb{V}}^{d+1},W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho})
.
We call these polynomials generalized Gegenbauer polynomials on the solid hyperboloid. We will not
work with the basis directly; see
[
29
]
for the basis in other cases.

Let
{\mathbf{P}}_{n}^{E}(W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho};\cdot,\cdot)
denote the reproducing kernel of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{E}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho})
. In terms of the basis of (
4.10
), we can write

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}_{n}^{E}(W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho};(x,t),(y,s))=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\sum_{|{\mathbf{k}}|=m}\frac{\mathbf{C}_{n-2k,{\mathbf{k}}}^{n}(x,t)\mathbf{C}_{n-2k,{\mathbf{k}}}^{n}(y,s)}{{\langle}\mathbf{C}_{n-2k,{\mathbf{k}}}^{n},\mathbf{C}_{n-2k,{\mathbf{k}}}^{n}{\rangle}_{W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}}}.

Just like on the surface
{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
, the kernel
{\mathbf{P}}_{n}^{E}\big(W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho};\cdot,\cdot\big)
can
be used to study the Fourier orthogonal series of any function
f
that is even in the variable
t
on

{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
. For such a function,
f(x,t)=f(x,-t)
, its projection on
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{O}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho})

becomes zero, so that its Fourier orthogonal expansion is given by

(4.11)

f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{E}(W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho};f),

where the projection
\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{E}\big(W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}\big):L^{2}\big({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}\big)\mapsto{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{E}\big({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}\big)
can be written in terms of the kernel

{\mathbf{P}}_{n}^{E}(W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho};\cdot,\cdot)
as

\operatorname{proj}_{n}(W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho};f)=c_{\mathsf{w}}\int_{{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}}f(y){\mathbf{P}}_{n}^{E}(W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho};\cdot,(y,s))W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}(s)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s.

Moreover, since
f
is even in
t
variable, we can regard it as the even extension of a function
f

defined on the upper hyperboloid
{\mathbb{X}}_{+}^{d+1}
, which is the cone
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
when
\varrho=0
.
In particular, this provides a Fourier orthogonal series for functions on the cone
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
, which is,
however, different from the Fourier orthogonal series in the Jacobi polynomials on the cone discussed in

[
30
]
.

The most interesting case on
{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
is
{\beta}=\frac{1}{2}
. To simplify the notation, we shall denote

W_{\frac{1}{2},{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}
by
W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}
throughout the rest of the section; that is,

The orthogonal polynomials with respect to

also possess two characteristic properties:
the first one is the spectral operator that has orthogonal polynomials as eigenfunctions

4.4.
Highly localized kernels

Let
\widehat{a}
be a cut-off function. For
(x,t)
,
(y,s)\in{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
, the localized kernel

{\mathbf{L}}_{n}^{E}(W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho};\cdot,\cdot)
is defined by

{\mathbf{L}}_{n}^{E}(W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho};(x,t),(y,s))=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\widehat{a}\left(\frac{j}{n}\right){\mathbf{P}}_{j}^{E}(W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho};(x,t),(y,s)).

We show that this kernel is highly localized when
(x,t)
and
(y,s)
are either both in
{\mathbb{X}}_{+}^{d+1}

or both in
{\mathbb{X}}_{-}^{d+1}
. For
\mu,{\gamma}\geq 0
, define

W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}(n;x,t):=\big(1+\varrho^{2}-t^{2}+n^{-2}\big)^{{\gamma}}\big(t^{2}-\varrho^{2}-\|x\|^{2}+n^{-2}\big)^{\mu}.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
\mu,{\gamma}\geq 0
. Let
\widehat{a}
be an admissible cutoff function. Then for
any
{\kappa}>0
, and
(x,t)
,
(y,s)
either both in
{\mathbb{X}}_{+}^{d+1}
or both in
{\mathbb{X}}_{-}^{d+1}
,

\left|{\mathbf{L}}_{n}^{E}(W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho};(x,t),(y,s))\right|\leq\frac{c_{\kappa}n^{d+1}}{\sqrt{W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}(n;x,t)}\sqrt{W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}(n;y,s)}}\big(1+n{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}}((x,t),(y,s))\big)^{-{\kappa}}.

Proof.

Again, it is sufficient to consider the case
\varrho=0
and we shall be brief. By (
4.14
)
we can write
{\mathbf{L}}_{n}^{E}\big(W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{0}\big)
in terms of the kernel for the Jacobi polynomials. Let

{\lambda}={\gamma}+\mu+\frac{d}{2}
. Then

\displaystyle{\mathbf{L}}_{n}^{E}\big(W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{0};(x,t),(y,s)\big)=b_{{\gamma},\mu}

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}L_{n}^{({\lambda}-\frac{1}{2},{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2})}\big(\xi(x,t,y,s;u,v)\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\times(1-v^{2})^{{\gamma}-1}(1-u^{2})^{\mu-1}\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v.

Hence, applying (
2.12
) with
m=1
and
\alpha={\beta}={\lambda}-1/2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbf{L}}_{n}^{E}(W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{0};(x,t),(y,s))\right|\leq cn^{2{\lambda}+1}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\left(1+n\sqrt{1-\xi(x,t,y,s;u,v)}\right)^{{\kappa}+3{\gamma}+3\mu+2}}

\displaystyle\times(1-v^{2})^{{\gamma}-1}(1-u^{2})^{\mu-1}\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v.

Since
t
and
s
have the same sign, it is easy to verify that

\displaystyle 1-\xi(x,t,y,s;u,v)\,

\displaystyle=1-\cos{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}}((x,t),(y,s))

\displaystyle+(1-u)\sqrt{t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}}\sqrt{s^{2}-\|y\|^{2}}+(1-v)\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\sqrt{1-s^{2}}.

The entries in both two lines in the right-hand side of the above identity are lower bounds of
1-\xi(x,t,u,s;u,v)
.
Using the first one, we obtain the estimate

{\lambda}_{n}^{E}\big(W;(x,t)\big)\leq c\,W\!\left({\mathbf{c}}_{\varrho}\left((x,t),\tfrac{1}{n}\right)\right).

Moreover, for
{\gamma}\geq 0
and
\mu\geq 0
,

{\lambda}_{n}^{E}\big(W_{{\gamma},\mu};(x,t)\big)\geq c\,W_{{\gamma},\mu}\!\left({\mathbf{c}}_{\varrho}\left((x,t),\tfrac{1}{n}\right)\right)=cn^{-d}W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}(n;x,t).

We are now in position to state the positive cubature rule for solid hyperboloid, following
which holds for polynomials in
\Pi_{n}^{E}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1})
and under the symmetry assumptions.

Theorem 4.19
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
\varrho\geq 0
. Let
W
be a doubling weight on
{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
such that

W(x,t)=W(x,-t)
for all
(x,t)\in{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
. Let
\Xi^{\varrho}
be a symmetric maximum

\frac{\delta}{n}
-separated subset of
{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
. There is a
\delta_{0}>0
such that for

0<\delta<\delta_{0}
there exist positive numbers
{\lambda}_{z,r}
,
(z,r)\in\Xi^{\varrho}
, so that

(4.20)

\int_{{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}}f(x,t)W(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{(z,r)\in\Xi^{\varrho}}{\lambda}_{z,r}f(z,r),\qquad\forall f\in\Pi_{n}^{E}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}).

Moreover,
{\lambda}_{z,r}\sim W\!\left({\mathbf{c}}_{\varrho}((z,r),\tfrac{\delta}{n})\right)
for all
(z,r)\in\Xi^{\varrho}
.

Again, this follows from
[
30
, Theorem 2.20]
when the domain becomes
{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}
and it
remains valid under symmetry assumptions on the weight and on the polynomials.

4.7.
Localized tight frames

The symmetry assumption for the weight and for the
{\varepsilon}
-separated subset also carries over to the
local frame on the solid hyperboloid. Let
\varrho\geq 0
and let
W
be a doubling weight on
{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}

that is even in
t
variable. Let
{\mathbf{L}}_{n}^{E}(W)*f
denote the operator in
\Pi_{2n}^{E}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1})
defined by

{\mathbf{L}}_{n}^{E}(W)*f(x):=\int_{{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}}f(y,s){\mathbf{L}}_{n}^{E}(W;(x,t),(y,s))W(y,s)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s,

where
{\mathbf{L}}_{n}^{E}(W;\cdot,\cdot)
is the highly localized kernel defined via a cut-off function
\widehat{a}
that
satisfies (
3.30
). For
j=0,1,\ldots,
let
\Xi_{j}^{\varrho}
be a symmetric maximal

\frac{\delta}{2^{j}}
-separated subset in
{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
, so that

\int_{{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}}f(x,t)W(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{(z,r)\in\Xi_{j}^{\varrho}}{\lambda}_{(x,r),j}f(z,r),\quad f\in\Pi_{2^{j}}^{E}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}).

For
j=1,2,\ldots,
define the operator
F_{j}^{\varrho}(W)
by

F_{j}^{\varrho}(W)*f={\mathbf{L}}_{2^{j-1}}^{E}(W)*f

and define the frame elements
\psi_{(z,r),j}
for
(z,r)\in\Xi_{j}^{\varrho}
by

\psi_{(z,r),j}(x,t):=\sqrt{{\lambda}_{(z,r),j}}F_{j}^{\varrho}((x,t),(z,r)),\qquad(x,t)\in{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}.

Then
\Phi=\{\psi_{(z,r),j}:(z,r)\in\Xi_{j}^{\varrho},\,j=1,2,3,\ldots\}
is a tight frame by

[
30
, Theorem 2.21]
.

Theorem 4.20
.

Let
W
be a doubling weight on
{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
even in its
t
variable. If

f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W)
and
f
is even in
t
variable, then

f=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{(z,r)\in\Xi_{j}^{\varrho}}\langle f,\psi_{(z,r),j}\rangle_{\mathsf{w}}\psi_{(z,r),j}\quad\mbox{in $L^{2}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W)$}

and

\|f\|_{2,W}=\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{(z,r)\in\Xi_{j}^{\varrho}}|\langle f,\psi_{(z,r),j}\rangle_{W}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Furthermore, for
{\gamma}\geq 0
and
\mu\geq 0
, the frame for
W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}
is highly localized in the
sense that, for every
{\kappa}>0
, there exists a constant
c_{\kappa}>0
such that

(4.21)

|\psi_{(z,r),j}(x,t)|\leq c_{\kappa}\frac{2^{j(d+1)/2}}{\sqrt{W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}(2^{j};x,t)}(1+2^{j}{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}}^{\varrho}((x,t),(z,r)))^{\kappa}},\quad(x,t)\in{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}.

Like the case on the hyperbolic surface, the frame elements involve only orthogonal polynomials even in

t
variable. The localization (
4.21
) follows from Theorem
4.9
and

{\lambda}_{(z,r),j}\sim 2^{-j(d+1)}W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}(2^{j};x,t)
which holds for

W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}

{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
.

4.8.
Characterization of best appoximation

For
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho})
, we denote by
{\mathbf{E}}_{n}(f)_{p,W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}}
the
best approximation to
f
from
\Pi_{n}^{d+1}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1})
in the norm
\|\cdot\|_{p,W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}}
; that is,

{\mathbf{E}}_{n}(f)_{p,W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}}:=\inf_{g\in\Pi_{n}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1})}\|f-g\|_{p,W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}},\qquad 1\leq p\leq\infty.

As in the case of hyperbolic surface, if
f
is even in
t
variable, then we can choose the polynomial of
best approximation from
\Pi_{n}^{E}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1})
. We can give a characterization of this quantity for
functions that are even in
t
variable.

For
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho})
and
r>0
, the modulus of smoothness is defined by

{\omega}_{r}(f;\rho)_{p,W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}}=\sup_{0\leq{\theta}\leq\rho}\left\|\left(I-{\mathbf{S}}_{{\theta},W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}}\right)^{r/2}f\right\|_{p,W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}},\quad 1\leq p\leq\infty,

where the operator
{\mathbf{S}}_{{\theta},W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}}
is defined by, for
n=0,1,2,\ldots
and
{\lambda}={\gamma}+\mu+\frac{d}{2}
,

\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{E}\Big(W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho};{\mathbf{S}}_{{\theta},W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}}f\Big)=R_{n}^{({\lambda}-\frac{1}{2},{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2})}(\cos{\theta})\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{E}\Big(W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho};f\Big).

In terms of the fractional differential operator
(-{\mathfrak{D}}_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho})^{\frac{r}{2}}
, the
K
-functional for a doubling
weight
W
, even in
t
variable on
{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
, is defined by

{\mathbf{K}}_{r}(f,t)_{p,W}:=\inf_{g\in W_{p}^{r}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W)}\left\{\|f-g\|_{p,W}+t^{r}\left\|(-{\mathfrak{D}}_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho})^{\frac{r}{2}}f\right\|_{p,W}\right\},

where the Sobolev space
W_{p}^{r}\big({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W\big)
is the space that consists of
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W)
,
even in
t
variable, so that
\left\|(-{\mathfrak{D}}_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho})^{\frac{r}{2}}f\right\|_{p,W}
is finite.

For
W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}
, Assertions 1 and 3 hold and we now verify that Assertion 5 holds as well.
By (
4.12
), the kernel
L_{n}^{(r)}(\varpi)
in Assertion 5 becomes

{\mathbf{L}}_{n}^{(r)}\big(W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho};(x,t),(y,s)\big)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\widehat{a}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)(k(k+2{\gamma}+2\mu+d))^{\frac{r}{2}}{\mathbf{P}}_{k}^{E}\big(W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho};(x,t),(y,s)\big).

Lemma 4.21
.

Let
{\gamma},\mu\geq-\frac{1}{2}
and
{\kappa}>0
. Then, for
r>0
and
(x,t),(y,s)\in{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
,

\left|{\mathbf{L}}_{n}^{(r)}\big(W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho};(x,t),(y,s)\big)\right|\leq c_{\kappa}\frac{n^{d+r+1}}{\sqrt{W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}(n;t)}\sqrt{W_{{\gamma},\mu}^{\varrho}(n;s)}}\big(1+n{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}}^{\varrho}((x,t),(y,s))\big)^{-{\kappa}}.

Proof.

This can be proved similarly as in the proof of Lemma
3.22
and using the estimate in
Theorem
4.9

following
[
30

Theorem 4.22
.

Let
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},W)
if
1\leq p<\infty
and
f\in C({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1})
if

Assume that

for

.

Let
