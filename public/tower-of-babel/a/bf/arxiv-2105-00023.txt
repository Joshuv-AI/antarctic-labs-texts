Dynamics of Open Quantum Systems II, Markovian Approximation
Marco Merkli

Abstract
A finite-dimensional quantum system is coupled to a bath of oscillators in thermal equilibrium at temperature T > 0 T>0 . We show that for fixed, small values of the coupling constant λ \lambda , the true reduced dynamics of the system is approximated by the completely positive, trace preserving markovian semigroup generated by the Davies-Lindblad generator. The difference between the true and the markovian dynamics is O ⁡ ( | λ | 1 / 4 ) O(|\lambda|^{1/4}) for all times, meaning that the solution of the Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad master equation is approximating the true dynamics to accuracy O ⁡ ( | λ | 1 / 4 ) O(|\lambda|^{1/4}) for all times. Our method is based on a recently obtained expansion of the full system-bath propagator. It applies to reservoirs with correlation functions decaying in time as slowly as 1 / t 4 1/t^{4} , which is a significant improvement relative to the previously required exponential decay.

Introduction

1.1
Motivation

The fundamental evolution equation of quantum theory is the Schrödinger equation

{\rm i}\frac{d}{dt}\psi(t)=H\psi(t),

(1.1)

where
\psi(t)
is a pure state (normalized vector) in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and
H
is the Hamiltonian, the operator representing the energy observable. (We choose units in which we have effectively
\hbar=1
.) Equivalently, density matrices
\rho(t)
, which are mixtures of pure states, evolve according to the von-Neumann equation

{\rm i}\frac{d}{dt}\rho(t)=[H,\rho(t)].

(1.2)

The state of a part, or subsystem, of a whole system, is obtained by ‘tracing out’ the degrees of freedom of the complement. This is formalized as follows. The Hilbert space is bipartite,
{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{H}}_{\rm S}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm R}
(
{\rm S}
corresponding to the subsystem one is interested in and
{\rm R}
being the remaining part) and the
reduced state of
{\rm S}
is

\rho_{\rm S}(t)={\rm tr}_{\rm R}\,\rho(t)={\rm tr}_{\rm R}\big(e^{-{\rm i}tH}\rho(0)e^{{\rm i}tH}\big),

(1.3)

where
{\rm tr}_{\rm R}
is the partial trace with respect to
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm R}
. What is the evolution equation for
\rho_{\rm S}(t)
? Generically, when
{\rm S}
and
{\rm R}
interact, which is expressed by the fact that
H
contains an interaction operator, the dynamics
\rho_{\rm S}(t)
is very complicated, as it contains all information about the dynamics of
{\rm R}
and the influence of
{\rm R}
on
{\rm S}
. Except in trivial situations, it is impossible to find a system operator
H_{\rm eff}
, s.t.
\rho_{\rm S}(t)
would equal
e^{-{\rm i}tH_{\rm eff}}\rho_{\rm S}(0)e^{{\rm i}tH_{\rm eff}}
.
1
1

1

Such an operator
H_{\rm eff}
, when it exists, could be called an ‘effective system Hamiltonian’, even though it might not be hermitian. The associated ‘dynamics’
e^{-{\rm i}tH_{\rm eff}}\rho_{\rm S}(0)e^{{\rm i}tH_{\rm eff}}\equiv e^{t{\mathcal{L}}}\rho_{\rm S}(0)
is called
unitary
if
H_{\rm eff}
is hermitian, and it is called
markovian
if
H_{\rm eff}
is hermitian or not.
Indeed, because
{\rm S}
and
{\rm R}
become entangled during the evolution (as they interact), the assignment
t\mapsto\rho_{\rm S}(t)
is not markovian, by which we mean that if we denote
\rho_{\rm S}(t)=V_{t}\rho_{\rm S}(0)
, where
V_{t}
is the ‘flow operator’ of the evolution, then
V_{s+t}\neq V_{s}V_{t}
. Equality here would hold if
V_{t}
was generated by an
H_{\rm eff}
, as mentioned above.

An important task in the study of open quantum systems is to identify under what circumstances the true reduced dynamical map
V_{t}
can be
approximated
as

V_{t}\approx e^{t\mathcal{L}},

(1.4)

by a (semi-)group
\,{\rm e}^{t{\mathcal{L}}}
, where
\mathcal{L}
is a ‘superoperator’ acting on density matrices and playing the role of an effective Hamiltonian (or, the operator taking the commutator with an effective Hamiltonian).
2
2

2

By an ‘effective Hamiltonian’, we mean a generally non-hermitian generator of dynamics – non-hermiticity is natural here since the open system dynamics will generically exhibit irreversible effects, such as decoherence, thermalization…
Analyzing the approximating dynamics
e^{t{\mathcal{L}}}
can then be done by standard means, namely by diagonalizing
\mathcal{L}
and expressing the propagator
e^{t\mathcal{L}}
as
\sum_{j}e^{t\epsilon_{j}}P_{j}
with eigenvalues
\epsilon_{j}
and eigenprojections
P_{j}
. The approximate dynamics is expressed equivalently as

\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{{\rm S},{\rm M}}(t)={\mathcal{L}}\rho_{{\rm S},{\rm M}}(t),

(1.5)

where the subscript “
\rm M
” stands for
markovian
. The latter equation is called the markovian master equation. Very often, one is interested in the situation where
{\rm R}
is a ‘very large’ quantum system, also called a ‘reservoir’. Then it is expected that the reduced system dynamics shows irreversibility. For instance, if
{\rm R}
is a thermal bath (a large number of quantum oscillators in thermal equilibrium) at a given temperature then one expects that
\rho_{{\rm S},\rm M}(t)
converges to an equilibrium state at the same temperature, when
t\rightarrow\infty
. Irreversibility of the system dynamics is then described by negative real parts
{\rm Re}\,\epsilon_{j}<0
, of the ‘effective energies’
\epsilon_{j}
which are the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}
.

Deriving a markovian effective evolution equation for open systems is an important task for theoretical and practical reasons
[
1
,
7
,
23
,
38
]
. The literature on the topic is huge and growing, and it encompasses research in mathematics, physics, chemistry, quantum information and biology
[
2
,
5
,
8
,
9
,
15
,
24
,
25
,
33
,
34
,
37
,
39
]
. In typical heuristic derivations, one explains that the markovian approximation should be valid under two main conditions
[
7
,
38
,
1
,
23
]
:

•

The reservoir correlation time (decay time of its correlation function) should be much shorter than the system relaxation time (typical time by which the open system
{\rm S}
changes significantly). This means that the reservoir loses its memory quickly relative to the pace at which the system moves, so information is carried away from the system and does not flow back to it, which effectively erases memory in the dynamics and makes it markovian.

•

The reservoir state, initially in equilibrium, does not significantly change during the course of time. This is plausible if the interaction between
{\rm S}
and
{\rm R}
is weak and the reservoir is much bigger than the system
{\rm S}
.

There are numerous papers deriving candidates for the generator
\mathcal{L}
obtained by making a small coupling expansion of the dynamics (weak
{\rm S}{\rm R}
interaction, gauged by an interaction constant
\lambda
). This is done both in general and specific open system models, and the forms and properties of the proposed
\mathcal{L}
vary. Over the course of time, the so-called “Davies generator” has emerged as the correct, or “proper” generator
[
14
]
. The (semi-)group
e^{t\mathcal{L}}
generated by the Davies generator is CPTP (completely positive, trace preserving), guaranteeing that under its evolution, an initial system density matrix stays a density matrix at all times – other generators do not have this property (but they still might approximate the true density matrix, at least on some restricted time-scales). The justification of the validity of the markovian approximation generated by the Davies generator was given in
[
10
,
11
]
. There it was shown that for time scales up to
O(1/\lambda^{2})
, where
\lambda
is the
{\rm S}{\rm R}
interaction constant, the difference between the true and the approximate dynamics vanishes in the limit
\lambda\rightarrow 0
. However, in any derivation up to and including
[
10
,
11
]
, the question of whether the markovian approximation is valid for all times (beyond
O(1/\lambda^{2})
) could not be answered with certainty. In particular, there were speculations as to what the correct asymptotic state is for the system (as
t\rightarrow\infty
). A detailed account of this point is given in the recent survey
[
35
]
. Over the last decade, the so-called quantum resonance theory was able to show that the markovian approximation (given by the Davies generator) is valid for all times
[
29
,
21
]
.

Our goal here is to improve those results and make them applicable for a larger class of models. In
[
29
,
21
]
, a crucial assumption was that the reservoir correlations decay exponentially quickly in time. This is equivalent to having an interaction operator which is very regular (namely, analytic; see Section 2.6 of
[
29
]
for a detailed discussion). Here we develop a method which can deal with less regular interactions, equivalent to only polynomially decaying reservoir correlations (as slow as
\sim t^{-4}
). We explain the details in Section
1.2
. It is not a mere technical exercise to see how much regularity, or correlation decay, is needed to guarantee the markovian approximation to be valid. Namely, it is known that eventually, when the interactions are too ‘rough’, the markovian approximation does
not
hold
[
37
,
39
,
22
]
.

1.2
Model

Consider an
N
-level system with Hilbert space
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
coupled to a reservoir of oscillators in equilibrium at temperature
T>0
. The Hamiltonian is given by

H_{\lambda}=H_{\rm S}+H_{\rm R}+\lambda G\otimes\varphi(g),

(1.6)

where
H_{\rm S}
is an
N\times N
hermitian matrix with (possibly degenerate) eigenvalues
E_{j}
and eigenvectors
\phi_{j}
,

H_{\rm S}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}E_{j}|\phi_{j}\rangle\langle\phi_{j}|

(1.7)

and
H_{\rm R}
is the environment, or reservoir Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\rm R}=\sum_{k}\omega_{k}a^{*}_{k}a_{k},

(1.8)

describing modes of a collection of harmonic oscillators, labelled by
k
, with frequencies
\omega_{k}>0
(put
\hbar=1
). The creation and annihilation operators
a^{*}_{k}
,
a_{k}
satisfy the canonical commutation relations
[a_{k},a^{*}_{\ell}]=\delta_{k,\ell}
(Kronecker symbol). The interaction term contains a coupling constant
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, an interaction operator
G
(hermitian
N\times N
matrix), and it is linear in the field operator

\varphi(g)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{k}g_{k}a^{*}_{k}+{\rm h.c.},

(1.9)

where
{\rm h.c.}
denotes the hermitian conjugate. The collection of the numbers
g_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
constitutes the form factor
g
. The size of
g_{k}
determines how strongly the oscillator
k
is coupled to the system. This model is ubiquitous, for
N=2
it is called the spin-Boson model.

In the limit of continuous modes (thermodynamic limit, infinite volume limit…), the values of frequencies
\omega_{k}
becomes a continuum, as do the values of the parameter
k
. For definiteness, we consider a reservoir modeling a (scalar) quantized field in three dimensions. This corresponds to momenta
k\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, and
\omega_{k}
,
g_{k}
,
a^{*}_{k}
and
a_{k}
become functions
\omega(k)
,
g(k)
,
a^{*}(k)
,
a(k)
with
[a(k),a^{*}(\ell)]=\delta(k-\ell)
(Dirac distribution). In the continuous mode limit, the reservoir Hamiltonian (
1.8
) and field operator (
1.9
) are

\displaystyle H_{\rm R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\omega(k)a^{*}(k)a(k)d^{3}k,

(1.10)

\displaystyle\varphi(g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\big(g(k)a^{*}(k)+{\rm h.c.}\big)d^{3}k.

(1.11)

We assume for convenience that
\omega(k)=|k|
. The Hilbert space on which the operators (
1.11
) act is the customary Bosonic Fock space over the single particle wave function space
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3},d^{3}k)
(momentum representation),

{\mathcal{F}}=\oplus_{n\geq 0}\,L_{\rm sym}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3n},d^{3n}k),

(1.12)

where the subscript sym refers to symmetric functions (Bosons) and the summand with
n=0
is interpreted to be
\mathbb{C}
.

In this paper, we understand that the continuous mode limit is performed to begin with, that is, we consider the Hamiltonian (
1.6
) with
H_{\rm S}
given in (
1.7
) and
H_{\rm R}
,
\varphi(g)
given in (
1.11
).
The Hilbert space of pure states of the coupled system is

{\mathfrak{h}}={\mathbb{C}}^{N}\otimes{\mathcal{F}}

(1.13)

with
{\mathcal{F}}
given by (
1.12
).

We consider initially disentangled system-servoir states of the form

\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R},0}=\rho_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta},

(1.14)

where
\rho_{\rm S}
is an arbitrary system (
N\times N
) density matrix and
\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}
is the equilibrium state of the reservoir at temperature
T=1/\beta>0
. It is not necessary to take initial product states as in (
1.14
) for our method to work – we study the case of correlated (classically or quantum) initial
{\rm S}{\rm R}
states in
[
31
]
. Since the Hamiltonian
H_{\rm R}
has continuous spectrum, the operator
e^{-\beta H_{\rm R}}
is not trace class and so we cannot define the reservoir Gibbs density matrix directly. Rather, one performs a thermodynamic limit: By constraining the reservoir particles to move in a finite volume
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
in (typically a box in direct, position) space, the momenta of particles are quantized and discrete and one can define the usual equilibrium Gibbs density matrix
\rho^{\Lambda}_{{\rm R},\beta}\propto\,{\rm e}^{-\beta H^{\Lambda}_{\rm R}}
for
H^{\Lambda}_{\rm R}
given in (
1.8
) and frequencies determined by
\Lambda
. In the limit
|\Lambda|\rightarrow\infty
of contiunous modes the ‘density matrix’
\propto e^{-\beta H^{\Lambda}_{\rm R}}
is ill defined. However, the two-point function

\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}^{\Lambda}(a^{*}(f)a(g))=\frac{{\rm tr}\big(e^{-\beta H_{\rm R}^{\Lambda}}a^{*}(f)a(g)\big)}{{\rm tr}\ e^{-\beta H_{\rm R}^{\Lambda}}}\quad\longrightarrow\quad\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}\big(a^{*}(f)a(g)\big),\qquad|\Lambda|\rightarrow\infty

has a well defined limit, which can be explicitly evaluated as

\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}\big(a^{*}(f)a(g)\big)=\langle g,(\,{\rm e}^{\beta\omega(k)}-1)^{-1}f\rangle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\frac{\bar{g}(k)f(k)}{\,{\rm e}^{\beta\omega(k)}-1}d^{3}k,

(1.15)

for any
f,g\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3},d^{3}k)
(where the overbar
\bar{\ }
denotes complex conjugation here). The state
\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}
,
a priori
defined only by (
1.15
), is extended to arbitrary products and sums of creation and annihilator operators by linearity and (Gaussian) quasi-freeness, meaning that averages of general
field (reservoir) observables
\mathcal{P}
, which are polynomials of creation and annihilation operators and limits thereof, are found using Wick’s theorem
[
6
]
. This state is the infinite volume, or continuous mode limit of the reservoir in thermal equilibrium, and it is the initial state for the reservoir in (
1.14
).

The state
\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}
is a linear functional on the observable algebra
{\mathcal{P}}
of polynomials in creation and annihilation operators, or the Weyl algebra, and it can also be represented by a density matrix
\rho_{{\rm R},\beta}
(having actually rank one), albeit in a Hilbert space different from
{\mathcal{F}}
. This is called a purification and we explain it in Section
2.1
below. It is not too surprising that the Fock space
\mathcal{F}
, (
1.12
), is not appropriate to describe a state with nonzero particle density in infinite volume, because every vector in
\mathcal{F}
obtained by applying an arbitrary (finite) number of creation operators to the vacuum represents a state with finitely many particles, which means a particle density of
zero
, in the infinite volume limit. The reduced system density matrix
\rho_{\rm S}(t)
is defined by the relation

{\rm tr}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}\big(\rho_{\rm S}(t)A\big)=\rho_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}\big(e^{{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}(A\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{\rm R})e^{-{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}\big)

(1.16)

valid for all system observables
A\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{C}}^{N})
(operators on
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
). On the right side of (
1.16
), we use the convenient notation
\rho_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}(A\otimes B)={\rm tr}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}(\rho_{\rm S}A)\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}(B)
, which extends by linearity to all (even non-product) system-reservoir observables. The mapping
\rho_{\rm S}\mapsto\rho_{\rm S}(t)
is linear and defines the so-called
dynamical map

V_{t}:\rho_{\rm S}\mapsto V_{t}\rho_{\rm S}=\rho_{\rm S}(t).

(1.17)

V_{t}
maps the initial system state to the state at time
t
, evolved according to the interacting dynamics.

A central question in the theory of open quantum systems is when one can approximate
V_{t}
by a
completely positive trace preserving
(CPTP) semi-group
e^{t{\mathcal{L}}}
. A classical result is that this can be done under some conditions: The works
[
10
,
11
,
36
]
show that

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\sup_{0\leq\lambda^{2}t<a}\|V_{t}-e^{t({\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}+\lambda^{2}K)}\|=0\qquad\mbox{for any $a>0$}.

(1.18)

Here,
{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}=-{\rm i}[H_{\rm S},\cdot]
is the free system dynamics and
K
describes the influence of the bath to second order in perturbation, consisting of a Hamiltonian as well as a dissipative term (see also
[
1
,
7
,
38
]
). Often, the superoperator
K
is called the
Davies generator
and
{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}+\lambda^{2}K
is the generator of a CPTP semigroup
[
8
,
14
,
29
]
. The Davies generator can be calculated by perturbation theory (and corresponds to the so-called level shift operator, see Section
2.3
and also the Appendix of
[
29
]
). As follows from (
1.18
), it can also be constructed from the relation

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\ V_{\tau/\lambda^{2}}\,\circ\,e^{-\tau{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}/\lambda^{2}}=e^{\tau K},\qquad\tau>0.

(1.19)

In differential form, the approximate markovian dynamics, defined as

\rho_{\rm M}(t)=e^{t({\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}+\lambda^{2}K)}\rho_{\rm M}(0),

(1.20)

takes the form of the ubiquitous
markovian master equation

\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{\rm M}(t)={\mathcal{L}}\rho_{\rm M}(t),\qquad{\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}+\lambda^{2}K.

(1.21)

An inconvenience of (
1.18
) is that the accuracy of the markovian approximation is only guaranteed for times which are not too large, satisfying
\lambda^{2}t<a
. This holds for arbitrary fixed
a
, but the deviation of the true dynamics
V_{t}
from its markovian approximation
e^{t({\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}+\lambda^{2}K)}
cannot be bounded uniformly in
a
– in other words, the speed of the convergence to zero in (
1.18
), as
\lambda\rightarrow 0
, depends on
a
. Said differently, (
1.18
) shows that the markovian approximation is guaranteed to hold for
t\rightarrow\infty
only if at the same time, one takes
\lambda\rightarrow 0
with a bounded value for
\lambda^{2}t
. For this reason, (
1.18
) is sometimes called the weak-coupling (or van Hove) limit. Recently, in
[
21
,
29
]
, the result (
1.18
) was improved to

\sup_{t\geq 0}\big\|V_{t}-e^{t({\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}+\lambda^{2}K)}\big\|\leq C\lambda^{2},

(1.22)

provided
|\lambda|\leq\lambda_{0}
for some
\lambda_{0}
. As (
1.22
) shows, the markovian approximation is now guaranteed to be accurate to
O(\lambda^{2})

uniformly in time

t\geq 0
, for fixed
\lambda
. The proof of (
1.22
) is given using the so-called dynamical resonance theory. In
[
21
,
29
]
, an analyticity condition on the form factor
g(k)
(see (
1.6
)) is imposed, which implies that the symmetrized reservoir correlation function

C_{\beta}(t)={\rm Re}\,c_{\beta}(t),

(1.23)

where

c_{\beta}(t)=\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}\big(\varphi(g)e^{{\rm i}tH_{\rm R}}\varphi(g)e^{-{\rm i}tH_{\rm R}}\big)

(1.24)

is the correlation function,
decays exponentially quickly in time, for large times. It is well known that decay of reservoir correlations is necessary for a markovian description of the system dynamics to be accurate, but demanding exponential decay is not necessary. The reason for the analyticity condition, and hence the assumption of exponential decay of correlations made in
[
21
,
29
]
, is that the analysis is based on so-called spectral deformation methods (pioneered in
[
4
,
18
]
in this setting). However, a technically more demanding approach, based on Mourre theory, was introduced in the current context in
[
27
,
16
]
and further developed in
[
19
,
20
]
. It can be viewed as an infinitesimal version of the deformation method and only requires the existence of
a few derivatives
(up to order four for us here), rather than
analyticity
of the form factor
g(k)
. Consequently, the Mourre theory approach allows us to deal with polynomially decaying reservoir correlation functions. In the present work, the decay has to be at least as quick as

C_{\beta}(t)\sim t^{-4},\qquad t\rightarrow\infty,

(1.25)

as we explain below, after (
3.4
).

The
main achievement
of the present work is a proof of the accuracy of the markovian approximation uniformly in time, assuming the above-mentioned weak regularity condition. Our main result is Theorem
1.1
, which gives (
1.22
) but, alas, with an inferior error estmate of
O(|\lambda|^{1/4})
. Prior to the current work, Mourre theory was not used to show the validity of the markovian approximation. In
[
19
]
it was used to show that the spin-Boson model has the property of return to equilibrium even in the strong coupling regime (in which one is forced to use Mourre theory as the analytic deformation cannot be applied). In
[
20
]
, that approach was further developed to examine the reduced dynamics of the spin-Boson model (large coupling), giving an expansion of the spin dynamics into the sum of a main term plus a remainder. The remainder was shown to decay to zero as
t\rightarrow\infty
but it was not shown to be small in
\lambda
, nor was it shown that the main term is a CPTP dynamics. In
[
30
]
an expansion of the system-reservoir dynamics was derived with an explicitly identified main term (in terms of resonance data) and a remainder which is small in
\lambda
for all times
t\geq 0
. Our current work uses the results of
[
30
]
to prove the validity of the markovian approximation.

1.3
Main result

Our main result is Theorem
1.1
below. We first present the assumptions and discuss them right below. Recall that the full Hamiltonian is given in (
1.6
), with the reservoir Hamiltonian (
1.10
) and the field operator
\varphi(g)
as in (
1.11
), and
g=g(k)
is called the form factor.

Assumptions

(1)

Smoothness of the form factor

g(k)\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3},d^{3}k)
.

We assume

g(k)=\frac{|k|^{p}}{1+|k|^{p+q}}h(k),

(1.26)

for some values

\qquad p=-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{3}{2}\mbox{\ or\ }p>2\mbox{\quad and\quad}q>2,

(1.27)

and where
h(k)
is a function four times differentiable with respect to
|k|
for
|k|>0
, such that
h
and its derivatives are bounded on
(0,\infty)
and
\lim_{|k|\rightarrow 0}\partial^{j}_{|k|}h(k)\neq 0
,
j=0,\ldots,4
.

(2)

Effective coupling
:

(a)

Fermi Golden Rule Condition. We assume that

\min_{\{1\leq m,n\leq N:E_{m}\neq E_{n}\}}\big|\langle\phi_{m},G\phi_{n}\rangle\big|^{2}\int_{S^{2}}\big|g\big(|E_{m}-E_{n}|,\Sigma\big)\big|^{2}d\Sigma\,>\,0,

(1.28)

where the minimum is over all distinct eigenvalue pairs of
H_{\rm S}
, see (
1.7
). Here,
S^{2}
is the unit sphere in momentum space
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and the function
g
is represented in spherical coordinates
k=(|k|,\Sigma)
,
|k|\geq 0
(length),
\Sigma\in S^{2}
(angles).

(b)

Simplicity of resonance energies. We assume that the so-called
level shift operators

\Lambda_{e}
,
e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{0}=\{E_{m}-E_{n}\ :\ m,n=1,\ldots,N\}
, have simple eigenvalues. The
\Lambda_{e}
are explicit matrices describing the second order (
\lambda^{2}
) corrections to the energy differences
e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
, given in Section
3.3
.

Discussion of the assumptions

(i)

In the open system literature, precise conditions on the form factor (coupling function)
g(k)
are often not presented. Rather, only the so-called reservoir spectral density,

J(\omega)=\tfrac{1}{2}\pi\omega^{2}\int_{S^{2}}|g(\omega,\Sigma)|^{2}d\Sigma,\qquad\omega\geq 0,

(1.29)

is specified. This is so because many physical quantities, such as decay rates, depend on
g
only in the form of
J
, see Section
1.3.1
. However, to derive those results rigorously, one will need direct assumptions on
g
. This is why Assumption (1) is phrased in terms of
g
and not
J
. This assumption says that
g
should be four times differentiable in the radial variable (energy)
|k|
and that it should have the infrared and ultraviolet behaviours
