Vector valued estimates for matrix weighted maximal operators and product BMO \mathrm{BMO}
Spyridon Kakaroumpas, Odí Soler i Gibert

Abstract
We consider maximal operators acting on vector valued functions, that is functions taking values on ℂ d , \mathbb{C}^{d}, that incorporate matrix weights in their definitions. We show vector valued estimates, in the sense of Fefferman–Stein inequalities, for such operators. These are proven using an extrapolation result for convex body valued functions due to Bownik and Cruz-Uribe. Finally, we show an H 1 \mathrm{H}^{1} - BMO \mathrm{BMO} duality for matrix valued functions and we apply the previous vector valued estimates to show upper bounds for biparameter paraproducts. For the reader’s convenience, we include an appendix explaining how to adapt the extrapolation for real convex body valued functions of Bownik and Cruz-Uribe to the setting of complex convex body valued functions that we treat.



1
Introduction

This paper deals with matrix weighted extensions of the Fefferman–Stein vector valued inequalities for the Hardy–Littlewood maximal function and the closely connected topic of matrix weighted extensions of the product BMO spaces. Before stating our main results, we briefly recall some historical background on each area.

The classical Fefferman–Stein vector valued inequalities, first proved by C. Fefferman and E. Stein
[
FS71
]
, state that for all
1<p,q<\infty
one has

\left\|\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|Mf_{k}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C(n,p,q)\left\|\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|f_{k}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

(1.1)

for any sequence
\{f_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
of (say) locally integrable complex valued functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n},
where
M
is the usual (uncentered) Hardy–Littlewood maximal function. That means

Mf(x)\coloneq\sup_{Q\ni x}\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|f(x)|\,\mathrm{dm}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where the supremum ranges over all cubes
Q
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(with faces parallel to the coordinate hyperplanes),
|E|
is the Lebesgue measure of a Lebesgue measurable set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n},
and
\,\mathrm{dm}(x)
denotes integration with respect to Lebesgue measure. The constant
C(n,p,q)
in (
1.1
) depends only on
n,

p
and
q.
In
[
FS71
]
also a weak type version of (
1.1
) is proved.

The estimate (
1.1
) is actually a special case of more general bounds for
vector valued extensions
of operators. That is, given some (not necessarily linear) operator
T
acting boundedly on
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for some
1<p<\infty,
we seek to find those
1<q<\infty
satisfying an estimate of the form

\left\|\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|Tf_{k}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C(T,n,p,q)\left\|\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|f_{k}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

(1.2)

for any sequence
\{f_{k}\}^{\infty}_{k=1}.
Such inequalities seem to have been studied for the first time systematically by J.-L. Rubio de Francia
[
Rub85
]
. For a thorough modern exposition of the methods in
[
Rub85
]
we refer to
[
Tao06
]
as well as
[
Gra14
]
.

The ideas in
[
Rub85
]
already hinted at an intimate connection between
extrapolation
and bounds for vector valued extensions as in (
1.2
). By an extrapolation problem one understands the following. Given an operator
T
acting on (suitable) functions on
\mathbb{R},
we assume that for some
1<p<\infty
it is already known or given that for all weights (that means, a.e. positive locally integrable functions)
w
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that belong to some class
C(p),
one has the estimate
\|Tf\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(w)}\leq C(T,n,p,w)\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(w)}.
Given this information, find all
1<q<\infty
and as well as associated classes
C(q)
of weights on
\mathbb{R}^{n},
such that for any
w\in C(q)
one has an estimate of the form
\|Tf\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(w)}\leq C(T,n,q,w)\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(w)}.
Rubio de Francia
[
Rub84
]
solved completely the extrapolation problem in the case that
C(p)
coincides with the
Muckenhoupt
A_{p}
class
, that is
w\in A_{p}
if and only if

[w]_{A_{p}}\coloneq\sup_{Q}\left(\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}w(x)\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\right)\left(\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}w(x)^{-1/(p-1)}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\right)^{p-1}<\infty,

where the supremum ranges again over all cubes
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}.
In this case, beginning with
any
fixed
1<p<\infty
and an extrapolation hypothesis holding for all weights
w\in A_{p},
the extrapolation problem is solvable for
any

1<q<\infty
and all weights
w\in A_{q}.
The extrapolation theorem of Rubio de Francia was subsequently further refined by various authors, until a sharp quantitative version of it was proved by J. Duoandikoetxea
[
Duo11
]
. A very thorough treatment of various forms of extrapolation with extensive historical background can be found in
[
CMP10
]
. In fact, this method is so powerful, that it naturally yields
weighted estimates
for vector valued extensions, that is

\left\|\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|Tf_{k}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(w)}\leq C(T,n,p,q,w)\left\|\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|f_{k}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(w)},

(1.3)

as explained in detail in
[
CMP10
]
.

Inequalities of the form (
1.3
) are a major tool for estimating operators arising naturally when decomposing
biparameter operators
or
bicommutators
in simpler, localized pieces. Such a decomposition for the so called Journeé operators was established by H. Martikainen
[
Mar12
]
(generalizing an analogous decomposition proved earlier by T. Hytönen
[
Hyt12
]
for Calderón–Zygmound operators in the context of the solution of the
A_{2}
problem). I. Holmes, S. Petermichl and B. Wick
[
HPW18
]
showed that these localized pieces can be estimated in terms of a
weighted product BMO
space. In the following, we recall the relevant definitions.

The classical space
\text{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
consists of all locally integrable functions
b
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\|b\|_{\text{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\coloneq\sup_{Q}\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|b(x)-\langle b\rangle_{Q}|\,\mathrm{dm}(x)<\infty,

where the supremum is taken over all cubes
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n},
and we denote
\langle b\rangle_{Q}\coloneq\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}b(x)\,\mathrm{dm}(x).
The importance of this space is two-fold. First, it is the dual space to the (real variable) Hardy space
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).
Second, the norm
\|b\|_{\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
is the “correct” quantity controlling the boundedness of commutators
[T,b]=[T,M_{b}],
where
M_{b}
denotes (pointwise) multiplication by
b
(called
symbol
of the commutator) and
T
is a Calderón–Zygmound operator. This was proved for the Hilbert transform by Z. Nehari
[
Neh57
]
and in full generality by R. R. Coifmann, Rochberg and G. Weiss
[
CRW76
]
. Moreover, the John–Nirenberg inequalities are an important intrinsic property of the space
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

B. Muckenhoupt and R. L. Wheeden
[
MW76
]
considered and studied the weighted BMO norm

\|b\|_{\mathrm{BMO}(\nu)}\coloneq\sup_{Q}\frac{1}{\nu(Q)}\int_{Q}|b(x)-\langle b\rangle_{Q}|\,\mathrm{dm}(x),

(1.4)

where the supremum ranges over all cubes
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\nu
is a
A_{2}
weight on
\mathbb{R}^{n}.
A characterization of the two weighted boundedness of commutators in terms of a weighted BMO norm of the symbol was established by S. Bloom
[
Blo85
]
for the Hilbert transform and later for arbitrary Calderón–Zygmund operators by I. Holmes, M. Lacey and B. Wick
[
HLW16
]
. In the latter work two weighted versions of (
1.4
) were introduced and associated John–Nirenberg inequalities were established. These played an important role in the commutator estimates in
[
HLW16
]
.

The study of
biparameter
BMO spaces on product spaces
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
was initiated by S. Y. A. Chang
[
Cha79
]
and R. Fefferman
[
Fef79
]
. Here, “biparameter” refers to invariance of the considered function spaces under rescaling each coordinate variable of the domain of definition separately. Works
[
Cha79
]
and
[
Fef79
]
introduced and investigated the biparameter product BMO space
\text{BMO}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R})
consisting of all locally integrable functions
b
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(considered as the product space
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
) such that

\|b\|_{\text{BMO}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R})}\coloneq\sup_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
R\subseteq\Omega\end{subarray}}\left|(b,w_{R})\right|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}<\infty,

where the supremum reanges over all (say) bounded Borel subsets
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with nonzero measure,
\bm{\mathcal{D}}
stands for the family of all dyadic rectangles of
\mathbb{R}^{2},

and
(w_{R})_{R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}}
is some (mildly regular) wavelet system adapted to
\bm{\mathcal{D}}.
Here and below we denote

(b,w_{R})\coloneq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}b(x)w_{R}(x)\,\mathrm{dm}(x).

The aforementioned works
[
Cha79
]
and
[
Fef79
]
established in particular that
\text{BMO}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R})
is the dual to the biparameter Hardy space
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}).
Moreover, a dyadic version of this product BMO space is the correct space for characterizing the boundedness of
bicommutators

[T_{1},[T_{2},b]],
where
T_{1},T_{2}
are Haar multipliers, as proved by
[
BP05
]
.

A weighted version of the Chang–Fefferman product BMO space was introduced and studied by Holmes–Petermichl–Wick
[
HPW18
]
in the context of proving two weight upper bounds for biparameter praproducts. Namely, given a biparameter dyadic grid
\bm{\mathcal{D}}
in the product space
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
and a biparameter
\bm{\mathcal{D}}
-dyadic
A_{2}
weight
w
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m},

[
HPW18
]
considers the
dyadic
Bloom type product space
\text{BMO}_{\text{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(\nu)
consisting of all locally integrable functions
b
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
with the property

\|b\|_{\text{BMO}_{\text{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(\nu)}\coloneq\sup_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{1}{\nu(\Omega)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}|b_{R}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\langle\nu^{-1}\rangle_{R}\bigg)^{1/2}<\infty,

(1.5)

where the supremum ranges over all Lebesgue-measurable subsets
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
of nonzero finite measure. We refer to Section
2
for a detailed explanation of the notation and the terminology. In
[
HPW18
]
an
\mathrm{H}^{1}
-BMO duality type result was established in this setting, which played a crucial role in the proofs of the upper bounds there. More recently, a two-weight version of (
1.5
) was defined in
[
KS22
]
and associated John–Nirenberg inequalities were established. These played an important role in
[
KS22
]
for characterizing the two weight boundedness of bicommutators with Haar multipliers, extending the aforementioned result of
[
BP05
]
to the two weight setting.

1.1
Main results

One of the main goals of this paper is to prove matrix weighted bounds for vector valued extensions of the Christ–Goldberg maximal function, which can be understood as a matrix weighted extension of the classical Fefferman–Stein vector valued inequalities for the Hardy–Littlewood maximal function.

Theorem 1
.

Let
1<p<\infty.
Consider a
(d\times d)
matrix
A_{p}
weight
W
and a sequence of vector valued functions
\{f_{n}\}^{\infty}_{n=1}.

Then, for each
1<q<\infty
it holds that

\left\|{\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|M_{W}f_{n}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}}\right\|_{{\mathrm{L}^{p}}}\leq C(n,d,p,q,[W]_{A_{p}})\left\|{\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|W(x)^{1/p}f_{n}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}}\right\|_{{\mathrm{L}^{p}}},

where

C(n,d,p,q,[W]_{A_{p}})=C(n,d,p,q)[W]_{A_{p}}^{\max\left\{\frac{1}{q-1},\frac{1}{p-1}\right\}},

and
M_{W}
denotes the Christ–Goldberg maximal function corresponding to the weight
W
and the exponent
p.

We refer to Subsection
3.1
for a detailed explanation of the notation in
Theorem

1
.
Theorem

1
yields readily a similar estimate for the so called
modified Christ–Goldberg maximal function
, as explained in
Theorem

7
below.

We deduce
Theorem

1
from a general principle for establishing matrix weighted bounds for vector valued extensions of operators acting on convex body valued functions, see
Theorem

5
below. Our method for deducing such bounds is inspired from
[
CMP10
]
: we use an analog of the Rubio de Francia extrapolation theorem for matrix weights proved in
[
BC22
]
, coupled with a trick of interpreting vector valued extensions of operators as operators whose values are convex body valued functions. It is worth noting that using the exact same method, the recent limited range extrapolation theorem for matrix weights proved in
[
KNV24
]
yields similar bounds as in
Theorem

5
that are valid only for a limited range of exponents. Note that the extrapolation for matrix weights and the other methods and techniques from
[
BC22
]
(which also belong to the foundations of the work in
[
KNV24
]
) concern the setting of real convex body valued functions. An extension to complex convex body valued functions presents nevertheless no difficulties thanks to previous work in
[
DKP24
]
. For the reader’s convenience we supply details in the appendix.

Theorem

1
and its counterpart for the modified Christ–Goldberg maximal function,
Theorem

7
, allow one to complete the proof of matrix weighted bounds for general (not necessarily paraproduct free) Journé operators given in
[
DKP24
]
for any
1<p<\infty
(not necessarily
p=2
), as explained in
[
DKP24
, Section 8]
(thus avoiding the use of extrapolation for biparameter matrix weights as in
[
Vuo23
]
). In this paper we focus on the application of
Theorem

1
for setting up the foundations of a theory of two matrix weighted product BMO. Namely, let
\bm{\mathcal{D}}=\mathcal{D}^{1}\times\mathcal{D}^{2}
be any product dyadic grid in the product space
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}.
Let
1<p<\infty,
and let
U,V
be biparameter
(d\times d)
matrix
\bm{\mathcal{D}}
-dyadic
A_{p}
weights on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}.
Let
B=\{B_{R}^{\varepsilon}\}_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}
be any sequence in
\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}).
We define

\|B\|_{\text{BMO}_{\text{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)}\coloneq\sup_{\Omega}\frac{1}{|\Omega|^{1/2}}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}|\mathcal{V}_{R}B_{R}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{U}_{R}^{-1}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2},

where the supremum ranges over all Lebesgue-measurable subsets
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
of nonzero finite measure. This definition is an extension of one of the equivalent definitions for the space of two matrix weighted one-parameter BMO, whose study was initiated in
[
IKP17
]
,
[
Isr17
]
and culminated in
[
IPT22
]
. Moreover, for every sequence
\Phi=\{\Phi_{R}^{\varepsilon}\}_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}
in
\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}),
we define

\|\Phi\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)}\coloneq\bigg\|\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}|V^{-1/p}\Phi_{R}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{U}_{R}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}}{|R|}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})},

which is the direct biparameter analog of the one parameter two matrix weighted
\mathrm{H}^{1}
norm from
[
Isr17
]
.
In this context, we prove the following result.

Theorem 2
.

Let
U,V,p,\bm{\mathcal{D}}
be as above. For any
B\in\emph{BMO}_{\emph{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p),
the linear functional
\ell_{B}:\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
given by

\ell_{B}(\Phi)\coloneq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{tr}((B_{R}^{\varepsilon})^{\ast}\Phi_{R}^{\varepsilon}),\quad\Phi\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)

is well-defined and bounded with
\|\ell\|\sim\|B\|_{\emph{BMO}_{\emph{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)},
where the implied constants depend only on
n,m,p,d
and
[V]_{A_{p},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}.
Conversely, for every bounded linear functional
\ell
on
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)
there is
B\in\emph{BMO}_{\emph{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)
with
\ell=\ell_{B}.

We note that our proof of Theorem
2
trivially works also in the one-parameter setting, thus answering to the positive the question posed in
[
Isr17
]
about the extension of the one parameter two matrix weighted
\mathrm{H}^{1}
-BMO duality proved there from
p=2
to arbitrary exponents
1<p<\infty.

The duality result of Theorem
2
coupled with Theorem
1
yields readily two-matrix weighted bounds for biparameter paraproducts, see Proposition
11
and Proposition
14
below. These yield in turn already some two matrix weighted upper bounds for bicommutators, see Subsection
4.3
below.

2
Background

In this section we review the central definitions for our work.
We also recall some basic facts about the various objects we deal with,
in most cases without proof.
References with further details are provided in this case.

2.1
Dyadic grids

For definiteness, in what follows intervals in
\mathbb{R}
will always be assumed to be left-closed, right-open and bounded.
A cube in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
will be a set of the form
Q=I_{1}\times\ldots\times I_{n}
, where
I_{k},\penalty\ k=1,\ldots,n
are intervals in
\mathbb{R}
of the same length,
which we denote by
\ell(Q)\coloneq|I_{1}|.

A rectangle in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
(with sides parallel to the coordinate axes) will be a set of the form
R=R_{1}\times R_{2}
, where
R_{1}
is a cube in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
R_{2}
is a cube in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

A collection
\mathcal{D}
of intervals in
\mathbb{R}
will be said to be a
dyadic grid
in
\mathbb{R}
if one can write
\mathcal{D}=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{D}_{k}
, such that the following hold:

•

for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathcal{D}_{k}
forms a partition of
\mathbb{R}
, and all intervals in
\mathcal{D}_{k}
have the same length
2^{-k}.

•

for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, every
J\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
can be written as a union of exactly
2
intervals in
\mathcal{D}_{k+1}.

We say that
\mathcal{D}
is the
standard dyadic grid
in
\mathbb{R}
if

\mathcal{D}\coloneq\left\{[m2^{k},(m+1)2^{k}):\penalty\ k,m\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

A collection
\mathcal{D}
of cubes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
will be said to be a
dyadic grid
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
if for some dyadic grids
\mathcal{D}^{1},\ldots,\mathcal{D}^{n}
in
\mathbb{R}
one can write
\mathcal{D}=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{D}_{k}
, where

\mathcal{D}_{k}=\{I_{1}\times\ldots\times I_{n}:\penalty\ I_{i}\in\mathcal{D}^{i},\penalty\ |I_{i}|=2^{-k},\penalty\ i=1,\ldots,n\}.

We say that
\mathcal{D}
is the
standard dyadic grid
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
if

\mathcal{D}\coloneq\{[m_{1}2^{k},(m_{1}+1)2^{k})\times\dots\times[m_{n}2^{k},(m_{n}+1)2^{k}):\penalty\ k,m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

If
\mathcal{D}
is a dyadic grid in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then we denote

\mathrm{ch}_{i}(Q)\coloneq\{K\in\mathcal{D}:\penalty\ K\subseteq Q,\penalty\ |K|=2^{-in}|Q|\},\qquad Q\in\mathcal{D},\penalty\ i=0,1,2,\ldots.

A collection
\bm{\mathcal{D}}
is said to be a
product dyadic grid
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
if for a dyadic grid
\mathcal{D}^{1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a dyadic grid
\mathcal{D}^{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
we have

\bm{\mathcal{D}}\coloneq\{R_{1}\times R_{2}:\penalty\ R_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{i},\penalty\ i=1,2\},

and in this case we write (slightly abusing the notation)
\bm{\mathcal{D}}=\mathcal{D}^{1}\times\mathcal{D}^{2}
.

If
\bm{\mathcal{D}}
is a product dyadic grid in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, then we denote

\mathrm{ch}_{i}(R)\coloneq\{Q_{1}\times Q_{2}:\penalty\ Q_{i}\in\mathrm{ch}_{i_{j}}(R_{j}),\penalty\ j=1,2\},

where

R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}},\penalty\ i=(i_{1},i_{2}),\penalty\ i_{1},i_{2}=0,1,2,\ldots.

We say that
R
is the
i
-th ancestor of
P
in
\bm{\mathcal{D}}
if
P\in\mathrm{ch}_{i}(R)
.

2.2
Haar systems

2.2.1
Haar system on
\mathbb{R}

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a dyadic grid in
\mathbb{R}
. For any interval
I\in\mathcal{D}
we denote by
h^{0}_{I},h^{1}_{I}
the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-normalized
cancellative
and
noncancellative
respectively Haar functions over the interval
I\in\mathcal{D}
, that is

h^{0}_{I}\coloneq\frac{\mathbf{1}_{I_{+}}-\mathbf{1}_{I_{-}}}{\sqrt{|I|}},\qquad h^{1}_{I}\coloneq\frac{\mathbf{1}_{I}}{\sqrt{|I|}}

(so
h^{0}_{I}
has mean 0). For simplicity we denote
h_{I}\coloneq h_{I}^{0}
. For any function
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
, we denote
f_{I}\coloneq(f,h_{I})
,
I\in\mathcal{D}
. It is well-known that the system
\{h_{I}\}_{I\in\mathcal{D}}
forms an orthonormal basis for
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Of course, all these notations and facts extend to
\mathbb{C}^{d}
-valued and
\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
-valued functions in the obvious entrywise way.

2.2.2
Haar system on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a dyadic grid in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We denote

\mathcal{E}\coloneq\{0,1\}^{n}\setminus\{(1,\ldots,1)\}.

We call
\mathcal{E}
the set of one-parameter signatures. For a cube
Q=I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{n}\in\mathcal{D}
and
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})\in\mathcal{E},
we denote by
h^{\varepsilon}_{Q}
the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-normalized cancellative Haar function over the cube
Q
corresponding to the signature
\varepsilon
defined by

h^{\varepsilon}_{Q}(x)\coloneq h^{\varepsilon_{1}}_{I_{1}}(x_{1})\dots h^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{I_{n}}(x_{n}),\qquad x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

For any function
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we denote
f^{\varepsilon}_{Q}\coloneq(f,h^{\varepsilon}_{Q})
. It is well-known that the system
\{h^{\varepsilon}_{Q}\colon\penalty\ Q\in\mathcal{D},\penalty\ \varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\}
forms an orthonormal basis for
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. All these notations and facts extend to
\mathbb{C}^{d}
-valued and
\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C})

2.2.3
Haar system on the product space
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}

Let
\bm{\mathcal{D}}=\mathcal{D}^{1}\times\mathcal{D}^{2}
be any product grid in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}.

We denote
\mathcal{E}\coloneq\mathcal{E}^{1}\times\mathcal{E}^{2}

\mathcal{E}^{1}\coloneq\{0,1\}^{n}\setminus\{(1,\ldots,1)\},\penalty\ \mathcal{E}^{2}\coloneq\{0,1\}^{m}\setminus\{(1,\ldots,1)\}.

We call
\mathcal{E}

R

\{h_{R}^{\varepsilon}\colon R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}},\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\}

For

\varepsilon_{2}\in\mathcal{E}^{2}

\mathbb{C}^{d}

\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C})

Finally, we remark that the dimensions

2.3

In the sequel we denote by
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}
the standard basis of
\mathbb{C}^{d}.
We will be often using the fact that

|A|\sim_{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}|Ae_{k}|,\quad\forall A\in\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}),

(2.1)

without explicitly mentioning it. Note also that for all

and for nonnegative numbers

\min(1,d^{p-1})\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{p}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\right)^{p}\leq\max(1,d^{p-1})\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{p}.

In particular, if

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|F_{k}(x)|^{r}\right)^{p}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\right)^{q}

\displaystyle\sim_{d,p,q,r}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{d}|F_{k}(x)e_{i}|^{r}\right)^{p}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\right)^{q}

\displaystyle\sim_{d,q}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|F_{k}(x)e_{i}|^{r}\right)^{p}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\right)^{q}.

2.4
Matrix weighted matrix-valued Lebesgue spaces

A function
W
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be a
d\times d
-matrix valued weight,
or just matrix weight, if
it is a locally integrable
\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
-valued function such that
W(x)
is a positive-definite matrix for a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Here, by locally integrable matrix valued function
W(x)
we mean that the scalar valued function
|W(x)|,

that is the matrix norm, is locally integrable.

Given a
d\times d
-matrix weight
W
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
1<p<\infty,

we define the norm

\|F\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(W)}\coloneq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|W(x)^{1/p}F(x)|^{p}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\right)^{1/p},

for all
\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
-valued measurable functions
F
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}.

This norm defines the matrix weighted matrix-valued Lebesgue space
\mathrm{L}^{p}(W).

2.5
Reducing operators

Let
1<p<\infty
. Let
E
be a bounded measurable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of nonzero measure. Let
W
be a
\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
-valued function on
E
that is integrable over
E

(meaning that its matrix norm is integrable, so
\int_{E}|W(x)|\,\mathrm{dm}(x)<\infty
) and that takes a.e. values in the set of positive-definite
(d\times d)
matrices.
It is proved in
[
Gol03
, Proposition 1.2]
that there exists a (not necessarily unique) positive-definite matrix
\mathcal{W}_{E}\in\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
, called
reducing operator
of
W
over
E
with respect to the exponent
p
, such that

\left(\frac{1}{|E|}\int_{E}|W(x)^{1/p}e|^{p}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\right)^{1/p}\leq|\mathcal{W}_{E}\,e|\leq\sqrt{d}\left(\frac{1}{|E|}\int_{E}|W(x)^{1/p}e|^{p}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\right)^{1/p}

for all
e\in\mathbb{C}^{d}.

If
d=1
, i.e.
W
is scalar-valued, then one can clearly take
\mathcal{W}_{E}\coloneq\langle W\rangle_{E}^{1/p},

where we denote the average of
W
over
E
by

\langle W\rangle_{E}=\frac{1}{|E|}\int_{E}W(x)\,\mathrm{dm}(x).

Moreover, if
p=2
, then

\frac{1}{|E|}\int_{E}|W(x)^{1/2}e|^{2}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)=\frac{1}{|E|}\int_{E}\langle W(x)e,e\rangle\,\mathrm{dm}(x),

which by linearity is equal to

\langle\langle W\rangle_{E}\,e,e\rangle=|\langle W\rangle_{E}^{1/2}e|^{2}

for all
e\in\mathbb{C}^{d}.

Thus in this special case one can take
\mathcal{W}_{E}\coloneq\langle W\rangle_{E}^{1/2}.

Assume in addition now that the function
W^{\prime}\coloneq W^{-1/(p-1)}
is also integrable over
E
.
Then we let
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}_{E}
be the reducing matrix of
W^{\prime}
over
E
corresponding to the exponent
p^{\prime}\coloneq p/(p-1)
, so that

|\mathcal{W}^{\prime}_{E}\,e|\sim_{d}\left(\frac{1}{|E|}\int_{E}|W^{\prime}(x)^{1/p^{\prime}}e|^{p^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\right)^{1/p^{\prime}}=\left(\frac{1}{|E|}\int_{E}|W(x)^{-1/p}e|^{p^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\right)^{1/p^{\prime}}

for all
e\in\mathbb{C}^{d}.

Note that one can take
\mathcal{W}^{\prime\prime}_{E}=\mathcal{W}_{E}
. Observe that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{W}_{E}\mathcal{W}^{\prime}_{E}|\sim_{p,d}\left(\frac{1}{|E|}\int_{E}\left(\frac{1}{|E|}\int_{E}|W(x)^{1/p}W(y)^{-1/p}|^{p^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{dm}(y)\right)^{p/p^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\right)^{1/p}.

For a detailed exposition of reducing operators we refer for example to
[
DKP24
]
.
Here we just state the following estimates for later convenience.

Lemma 3
.

Let
W
be a
\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
-valued function on
E
taking a.e. positive-definite values, and such that
W
and
W^{\prime}=W^{-1/(p-1)}
are integrable over
E
for some
1<p<\infty.
Set

\displaystyle C_{E}\coloneq\frac{1}{|E|}\int_{E}\left(\frac{1}{|E|}\int_{E}|W(x)^{1/p}W(y)^{-1/p}|^{p^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{dm}(y)\right)^{p/p^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{dm}(x).

We consider reducing operators of
W
with respect to exponent
p
and of
W^{\prime}
with respect to exponent
p^{\prime}.

(1)

There holds

|\mathcal{W}_{E}e|\lesssim_{p,d}C_{E}^{1/p}{\langle|W^{1/p}e|\rangle}_{E}

and

|\mathcal{W}_{E}^{-1}e|\leq|\mathcal{W}_{E}^{\prime}e|\lesssim_{p,d}C_{E}^{1/p}|\mathcal{W}_{E}^{-1}e|,

for all
e\in\mathbb{C}^{d}.

(2)

There holds

|{\langle W^{1/p}\rangle}_{E}e|\leq|\mathcal{W}_{E}e|\lesssim_{p,d}C_{E}^{d/p}|{\langle W^{1/p}\rangle}_{E}e|,

for all
e\in\mathbb{C}^{d}.

A proof of part (1) can be found, for example, in
[
DKP24
]
. A proof of part (2) can be found in
[
IKP17
]
.

Let
E,F
be measurable subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{m}
respectively with
0<|E|,|F|<\infty.
Let
1<p<\infty.
Let
W
be a
\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
-valued integrable function on
E\times F
taking a.e. values in the set of positive-definite
d\times d
-matrices. For all
x_{1}\in E,
set
W_{x_{1}}(x_{2})\coloneq W(x_{1},x_{2}),

x_{2}\in F.
For a.e.

denote by

with respect to the exponent

It is proved in

]
(see also

]

in a way that is measurable in

Set

for a.e.

Then

It is proved in

DKP24

(2.3)

2.7
Convex body valued functions

Here we consider functions taking values in the collection of closed bounded symmetric convex sets of
\mathbb{C}^{d}.

Take into account that by symmetric here we mean
complex symmetric
.
That is, a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d}
is complex symmetric (or just symmetric in this article) if
for every
u\in A
and every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\lambda|=1
it is also the case that
\lambda u\in A.

Of course, if the set
A
is convex in addition to symmetric, it will also be the case that,
for every
u\in A
and every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\lambda|\leq 1,
also
\lambda u\in A.
In other words, the “symmetric convex sets” as we have defined them are precisely the balanced convex sets.
We will denote the set of closed subsets of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
by
\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}^{d}),

or just
\mathcal{K}
when the dimension of the ambient space is clear by the context.
In addition, we define a
convex body
to be a closed bounded convex and symmetric subset of
\mathbb{C}^{d}.

We will use the symbol
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
to denote the set of convex bodies on
\mathbb{C}^{d}

and, whenever the dimension of the ambient space is unambiguous, simply by
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}.

We focus now on functions
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})

and we gather the definitions and basic properties that we will need for our results.
Some of these definitions can be found in more general forms in the texts that we cite,
we will restrict though to the cases that are of interest to us to avoid an excess of concepts.
For an introduction to such functions, their properties and how to define their integrals
see
[
BC22
,
Cru23
]
and
for a detailed exposition see
[
AF09
]
.

Since through the article we will consider both functions taking values in
\mathbb{C}^{d}

and functions taking values in
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}},

we will use a typographic convention to avoid confusion.
We will denote functions taking values in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with lowercase letters
f,g,h,\ldots

On the other hand, we will use uppercase letters
F,G,H,\ldots
to denote functions taking values in
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}(\mathbb{C}^{d}).

In any case, we will also explicitly state the target space of the functions we use.

Given a set
K\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d},
let us define its norm by

|K|\coloneq\sup\{|v|\colon v\in K\}.

For a matrix
A
we will denote its usual norm (given by its largest singular value) by
|A|.

The action of matrix weights on convex body valued functions is given by the next definition.
Given a convex body
K
and a positive definite matrix
A,
define the product

AK\coloneq\{Au\colon u\in K\}

and observe that
AK
will also be a convex body.

We will say that a function
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
is
measurable

if for every open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d}
it holds that the set

F^{-1}(U)\coloneq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon F(x)\cap U\neq\emptyset\}

is measurable (in the sense of Lebesgue).
A convex body valued function
F
is measurable if and only if there exists a sequence
\{f_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}

of measurable functions
f_{k}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that

F(x)=\overline{\{f_{k}(x)\colon k\geq 1\}}

(2.5)

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see
[
AF09
, Theorem 8.1.4]
).
The functions that form such sequences will be called
selection functions
for
F.

In addition, the set of all selection functions for
F
is denoted by
S^{0}(F).

Observe that this is the same as the set of all measurable functions
f
such that
f(x)\in F(x)

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Here we restrict to functions taking values on
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
due to the applications that we consider later.
Nonetheless, the previous definitions and concepts apply verbatim to functions taking values on
\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}^{d}).

When one is interested in the norm of
F,
it is possible to restrict to selection functions, as the following lemma shows
(see
[
BC22
, Lemma 3.9]
).
We include its proof for completeness, although the arguments of Bownik and Cruz-Uribe
are equally valid for complex convex bodies in this case.

Lemma 4
.

Consider a measurable function
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}(\mathbb{C}^{d}).

Then there exists
f\in S^{0}(F)
such that

|f(x)|=|F(x)|

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Proof.

Through this proof, we identify
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with
\mathbb{R}^{2d}

in the usual way.
Take the sequence
\{f_{k}\}
of selection functions satisfying (
2.5
).
Then, the function given by

g_{0}(x)=\sup\{|v|\colon v\in F(x)\}=\sup\{|f_{k}(x)|\colon k\geq 1\}

is also measurable.
This allows us to define the function

F_{0}(x)=\{v\in F(x)\colon|v|=g_{0}(x)\}=F(x)\cap\mathbf{{S}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{d}}}

taking values on
\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}^{d}),

where
\mathbf{{S}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{d}}}=\{v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\colon|v|=1\}
denotes the complex

(d-1)
-dimensional sphere.
Since both
F
and
g_{0}\mathbf{{S}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{d}}}
are measurable, so is
F_{0}

(see
[
AF09
, Theorems 8.2.2 and 8.2.4]
).

It is only left to choose
v(x)\in F_{0}(x)
in a measurable way.
This is done by choosing maximal vectors
v
in every other real coordinate iteratively (in the
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
sense).
Let
P_{1}
be the (continuous) projection onto the first complex coordinate and define

g_{1}(x)=\sup_{k}|P_{1}(f_{k}(x))|.

The function
g_{1}\colon\Omega\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is then measurable.
Next, use this function and the symmetry of
F(x)
to define
F_{1}\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
as

F_{1}(x)=\{v\in F_{0}(x)\colon P_{1}(v)=g_{1}(x)\}=F_{0}(x)\cap(\{g_{1}(x)\}\times\mathbb{C}^{d-1}).

As before,
F_{1}
is also a measurable function.
Assume that we have defined
F_{j}
measurable and taking values on
\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}^{d}).

Define
F_{j+1}\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
by taking the set of points
of
F_{j}(x)
with maximal modulus of the
j+1
complex coordinate, positive real part and zero imaginary part for the same coordinate.
Also
F_{j+1}
will be measurable because of the same reasons as before.
Eventually, we get the measurable function
F_{d}\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
and
F_{d}(x)
must be a singleton for every
x\in\Omega,

this is
F_{d}(x)=\{v(x)\}
for some
v(x)\in\mathbb{C}^{d},

because of the maximality of the modulus of each complex coordinate
and the restriction of having each complex coordinate lying on the positive real axis.
∎

In order to define integrals of convex body valued functions, consider first the set

S^{1}(F)\coloneq\{f\in S^{0}(F)\colon f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\}

of integrable selection functions for
F.

The
Aumann integral
of
F
is then defined as

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}F(x)\,\,\mathrm{dm}(x)=\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\,\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\colon f\in S^{1}(F)\right\}.

In this work, we will restrict to
integrably bounded
convex body valued functions,
that is to functions
F
such that
|F(x)|
is integrable.
In particular, in this case
S^{0}(F)=S^{1}(F).

If
|F(x)|
is only locally integrable, we will say that
F
is
locally integrably bounded
.
For further convenience, given a cube
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n},
we define the averaging operator
A_{Q}
by

A_{Q}F(x)\coloneq\frac{\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x)}{|Q|}\int_{Q}F(y)\,\,\mathrm{dm}(y),

where
F
is a locally integrably bounded convex body valued function.

Given
1\leq p<\infty,
we define the Lebesgue space of convex body valued functions
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})),

or just
\mathrm{L}^{p}
when there is no ambiguity, as the set of functions
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
such that

\left\|{F}\right\|_{{\mathrm{L}^{p}}}\coloneq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|F(x)|^{p}\,\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\right)^{1/p}<\infty.

The space of convex body valued functions
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})),
or just
\mathrm{L}^{\infty},
is defined as
the set of convex body valued functions
F
for which

\left\|{F}\right\|_{{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}}\coloneq\esssup\{|F(x)|\colon x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}<\infty.

Given a
(d\times d)
-matrix weight
W,
we define the weighted Lebesgue space
\mathrm{L}^{p}(W)
of convex body valued functions
as the set of functions
F
for which
W(x)^{1/p}F(x)\in\mathrm{L}^{p}.

3
Vector valued extensions of operators on convex body valued functions

The aim of this section is to prove vector valued estimates for convex body valued functions with matrix weights,
as well as to apply it to vector valued estimates for the matrix weighted maximal operators for vector valued functions
(see Subsection
3.1
for the definitions and details).
This will be a consequence of an extrapolation result on spaces of functions with values on
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}

and it is a minor modification of the analogous result for matrix weights and vector valued functions due to
Bownik and Cruz-Uribe (see
[
BC22
, Section 9]
).
Before stating the extrapolation theorem, we introduce the concept of families of extrapolation pairs
following the convention in
[
CMP10
]
.
A family of extrapolation pairs
\mathcal{F}
is a collection of pairs
(F,G)
of measurable functions
taking values in
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}
such that neither
F
nor
G
is equal to
\{0\}
almost everywhere.
In addition, given such a family
\mathcal{F},
we call each element
(F,G)\in\mathcal{F}
an extrapolation pair.
We are interested in inequalities of the form

\left\|{F}\right\|_{{\mathrm{L}^{p}(W)}}\leq C\left\|{G}\right\|_{{\mathrm{L}^{p}(W)}},\qquad(F,G)\in\mathcal{F},

in the sense that this holds for all pairs
(F,G)\in\mathcal{F}
for which the left-hand side is finite
and with the constant
C
depending on the characteristic
[W]_{A_{p}}
but not on the particular weight
W.

Theorem A

(Bownik, Cruz-Uribe
[
BC22
]
)
.

Consider a family of extrapolation pairs
\mathcal{F}.

Suppose that for some
p_{0},

1\leq p_{0}\leq\infty,
there exists an increasing function
C_{p_{0}}

such that for every matrix weight
W\in A_{p_{0}}
it holds that

\left\|{F}\right\|_{{\mathrm{L}^{p_{0}}(W)}}\leq C_{p_{0}}([W]_{A_{p_{0}}})\left\|{G}\right\|_{{\mathrm{L}^{p_{0}}(W)}},\qquad(F,G)\in\mathcal{F}.

(3.1)

Then, for all
1<p<\infty
and for every matrix weight
W\in A_{p}
it holds that

\left\|{F}\right\|_{{\mathrm{L}^{p}(W)}}\leq C_{p}(p_{0},n,d,[W]_{A_{p}})\left\|{G}\right\|_{{\mathrm{L}^{p}(W)}},\qquad(F,G)\in\mathcal{F},

where

C_{p}(p_{0},n,d,[W]_{A_{p}})=C(p,p_{0})C_{p_{0}}\left(C(n,d,p,p_{0})[W]_{A_{p}}^{\max\left\{1,\frac{p_{0}-1}{p-1}\right\}}\right)

if
p_{0}<\infty,
and

C_{p}(p_{0},n,d,[W]_{A_{p}})=C(p,p_{0})C_{p_{0}}\left(C(n,d,p,p_{0})[W]_{A_{p}}^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\right)

if
p_{0}=\infty.

The proof of this result is exactly the same as in
[
BC22
, Theorem 9.1]
,
up to the point that the calculations are done directly with the given convex body valued functions
F
and
G,

instead of taking
F(x)=\{\lambda f(x)\colon|\lambda|\leq 1\}
and
G(x)=\{\lambda g(x)\colon|\lambda|\leq 1\}

for given vector valued functions
f
and
g

(also note that in
[
BC22
]

F(x)
and
G(x)
are segments with endpoints
\pm f(x)
and
\pm g(x)

because in that article
real symmetric
convex bodies are used, instead of complex symmetric).
Since the proof in
[
BC22
]
follows closely that of the scalar case,
we also refer the reader to
[
CMP10
, Chapter I.3]
for a detailed exposition on the topic in the classical setting.

The main result of this section follows from Theorem
A
.
This is a vector valued estimate for sequences
\{(F_{n},G_{n})\}\subseteq\mathcal{F}

of a given family of extrapolation pairs
\mathcal{F}.

Theorem 5

(Vector valued estimates for families of extrapolation pairs)
.

Consider a family of extrapolation pairs
\mathcal{F}.

Suppose that for some
1\leq p_{0}\leq\infty
there exists an increasing function
C_{p_{0}}

such that for every matrix weight
W\in A_{p_{0}}
inequalities (
3.1
) hold.
Then, for any
p
and
q,

1<p,q<\infty,
and for every matrix weight
W\in A_{p}
it holds that

\left\|{\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|W^{1/p}F_{n}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}}\right\|_{{\mathrm{L}^{p}}}\leq C(n,d,p,p_{0},q,[W]_{A_{p}})\left\|{\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|W^{1/p}G_{n}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}}\right\|_{{\mathrm{L}^{p}}},

where
\{(F_{n},G_{n})\}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
and

\displaystyle C(n,d,p_{0},p,q,[W]_{A_{p}})=

\displaystyle C(d,p,q)

\displaystyle C_{p_{0}}\left(C(n,d,p_{0},p,q)[W]_{A_{p}}^{\left(\max\left\{1,\frac{p_{0}-1}{q-1}\right\}\max\left\{1,\frac{q-1}{p-1}\right\}\right)}\right)

if
p_{0}<\infty,
and

C(n,d,p_{0},p,q,[W]_{A_{p}})=C(d,p,q)C_{p_{0}}\left(C(n,d,p_{0},p,q)[W]_{A_{p}}^{\left(\frac{1}{q-1}\max\left\{1,\frac{q-1}{p-1}\right\}\right)}\right)

if
p_{0}=\infty.

Recall that one can define the Minkowski addition of
A,B\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}
(and for subsets of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
in general)
by

A+B\coloneq\{a+b\colon a\in A,b\in B\}.

It is easy to check that if
A,B\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}},
then also
A+B\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}.

One can extend the Minkowski addition of two convex body to that of
N
convex bodies by induction, with
N\geq 2.

We can also define the scalar multiplication, given
A\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C},
by

\lambda A\coloneq\{\lambda a\colon a\in A\},

and also
\lambda A\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}.

Keep in mind that these operations do not define a vector space structure, since the Minkowski addition has no inverse.

Given
1\leq q<\infty
and a sequence
\{K_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}
such that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|K_{n}|^{q}<\infty,

(3.2)

we define the infinite
\ell^{q}
Minkowski addition of
\{K_{n}\},
denoted by
\Sigma_{q}(\{K_{n}\}),
as

\Sigma_{q}(\{K_{n}\})\coloneq\bigcup\left\{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}v_{n}\colon v_{n}\in K_{n}\text{ for }n\geq 1,\{a_{n}\}\in\ell^{q^{\prime}},\left\|{\{a_{n}\}}\right\|_{{\ell^{q^{\prime}}}}\leq 1\right\}.

(3.3)

The sums in (
3.3
) are convergent due to
the summability condition (
3.2
) and Hölder’s inequality.
In particular, it holds that

\left|\Sigma_{q}(\{K_{n}\})\right|\leq\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|K_{n}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}

for any
1\leq q<\infty
whenever the right-hand side of this expression is finite.
Furthermore, given a
(d\times d)
matrix
A,
it also holds by linearity that

A\Sigma_{q}(\{K_{n}\})=\Sigma_{q}(\{AK_{n}\}).

For
1\leq q<\infty
it also happens that
\Sigma_{q}(\{K_{n}\})
is a convex symmetric bounded closed set,
which is proved in next lemma.

Lemma 6
.

Let
1\leq q<\infty
and let
\{K_{m}\}^{\infty}_{m=1}
be a sequence of sets in
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}
with

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}|K_{m}|^{q}<\infty.

Then
K=\Sigma_{q}(\{K_{n}\})

is a well-defined set in
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}
with

|K|\sim_{d,q}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}|K_{m}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}.

Proof.

First of all, for all sequences
\{v_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
with
v_{m}\in K_{m},
for all
m=1,2,\ldots
and for all complex numbers
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots
with
\left\|{\{a_{m}\}}\right\|_{{q^{\prime}}}\leq 1
we have by Hölder’s inequality

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}|a_{m}v_{m}|\leq\left\|{\{a_{m}\}}\right\|_{{q^{\prime}}}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}|v_{m}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}\leq\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}|K_{m}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}<\infty.

This shows that
K
is a well-defined bounded set with
|K|\leq\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}|K_{m}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}.

It is obvious that
K
is symmetric. Moreover, a standard weak star compactness argument yields that
K
is closed. Let us check that
K
is convex. For any sequences
\{v_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty},

\{u_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
with
v_{m},u_{m}\in K_{m},
for all
m=1,2,\ldots,
any complex numbers
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots
and
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots
with
\left\|{\{a_{m}\}}\right\|_{{q^{\prime}}}\leq 1
and
\left\|{\{b_{m}\}}\right\|_{{q^{\prime}}}\leq 1,
and any
t\in(0,1),
we can write

\displaystyle t\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}a_{m}v_{m}+(1-t)\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}b_{m}u_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(t|a_{m}|+(1-t)|b_{m}|)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\frac{t|a_{m}|}{t|a_{m}|+(1-t)|b_{m}|}\cdot\frac{a_{m}}{|a_{m}|}v_{m}+\frac{(1-t)|b_{m}|}{t|a_{m}|+(1-t)|b_{m}|}\cdot\frac{b_{m}}{|b_{m}|}u_{m}\right),

with the obvious modifications if
a_{m}=0
or
b_{m}=0
for some
m\geq 1,
thus it is clear that
t\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}a_{m}v_{m}+(1-t)\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}b_{m}u_{m}\in K.

We will now show that

|K|\gtrsim_{d,q}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}|K_{m}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}.

We can pick a sequence
\{v_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
with
v_{m}\in K_{m}
and
|v_{m}|\geq\frac{1}{2}|K_{m}|,
for all
m=1,2,\ldots.
It is clear that there exists a positive integer
N
with

\sum_{m=1}^{N}|v_{m}|^{q}\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}|v_{m}|^{q}.

For any vector
x\in\mathbb{C}^{d},
we denote by
x^{1},\ldots,x^{d}
its coordinates. Notice that

\sum_{m=1}^{N}|v_{m}|^{q}\sim_{d,q}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|v_{m}^{j}|^{q}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{m=1}^{N}|v_{m}^{j}|^{q},

therefore there exists
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
with

\sum_{m=1}^{N}|v_{m}^{j}|^{q}\gtrsim_{d,q}\sum_{m=1}^{N}|v_{m}|^{q}.

Clearly, one can find complex numbers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
with
\left\|{\{a_{m}\}_{m=1}^{N}}\right\|_{{q^{\prime}}}\leq 1
such that

\sum_{m=1}^{N}a_{m}v_{m}^{j}=\left(\sum_{m=1}^{N}|v_{m}^{j}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{m=1}^{N}a_{m}v_{m}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\left|\sum_{m=1}^{N}a_{m}v_{m}^{j}\right|=\left(\sum_{m=1}^{N}|v_{m}^{j}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle\gtrsim_{d,q}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{N}|v_{m}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}\gtrsim_{q}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}|K_{m}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}.

Since
\sum_{m=1}^{N}a_{m}v_{m}=\sum_{m=1}^{N}a_{m}v_{m}+\sum_{m=N+1}^{\infty}0\cdot 0\in K,
the proof is complete.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
5
.

Proof of Theorem
5
.

Fix first

We construct a new family

|W(x)^{1/p}F(x)|\sim_{d,p}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|W(x)^{1/p}F_{n}(x)|^{q}\right)^{1/q}

for any
1<p<\infty.
It is also easy to see that the convex body valued functions

) with exponent

\begin{split}\left\|{F}\right\|_{{\mathrm{L}^{q}(W)}}^{q}&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|W^{1/q}F(x)|^{q}\,\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\\

Here we have used Theorem

with the extrapolation paris

and with exponent

so that we have

C_{q}([W]_{A_{q}})=C(d,q,p_{0})C_{p_{0}}\left(C(n,d,q,p_{0})[W]_{A_{q}}^{\max\left\{1,\frac{p_{0}-1}{q-1}\right\}}\right).

\leq C_{p,q}(&[W]_{A_{p}})\left\|{\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|W(x)^{1/p}G_{n}(x)|^{q}\right)^{1/q}}\right\|_{{\mathrm{L}^{p}}}\end{split}

3.1
Fefferman–Stein vector valued inequalities for weighted maximal operators

Next, we give an application of Theorem
5
.
Fix
1<p<\infty.

Given a
(d\times d)
matrix valued weight
W
on
\mathbb{R}^{n},

one can define the pointwise matrix weighted maximal operator for vector valued functions by

M_{W}f(x)\coloneq\sup_{Q}\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|W(x)^{1/p}f(y)|\,\,\mathrm{dm}(y)\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x),

where
f
is a locally integrable function taking values on
\mathbb{C}^{d}

and where the supremum is taken over all cubes with sides parallel to the coordinate axes.
The maximal operator
M_{W}
is also called Christ–Goldberg maximal operator,
since it was studied by these two authors (see
[
CG01
]
and
[
Gol03
]
).
One can also define a weighted maximal operator with reducing operators as

\widetilde{M}_{W}f(x)\coloneq\sup_{Q}\int_{Q}|\mathcal{W}_{Q}f(y)|\,\,\mathrm{dm}(y)\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x),

where
f
is a locally integrable function with values on
\mathbb{C}^{d},

the supremum is taken over bounded cubes with sides parallel to the axes
and where
\mathcal{W}_{Q}
is the reducing operator of
W
over
Q
with exponent
p.

We do not make the dependence on the exponent
p
explicit since it will always be clear from the context.

Both weighted maximal operators play an important role in the theory of matrix weighted norm inequalities.
In addition, whenever
W
is a matrix
A_{p}
weight, both maximal operators are bounded
from weighted
\mathrm{L}^{p}(W)
to unweighted
\mathrm{L}^{p}.

Isralowitz and Moen
[
IM19
]
proved that

\left\|{M_{W}}\right\|_{{\mathrm{L}^{p}(W)\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{p}}}\lesssim_{n,d,p}[W]_{A_{p}}^{1/(p-1)},

(3.5)

while Isralowitz, Kwon and Pott
[
IKP17
]
showed that

\left\|{\widetilde{M}_{W}}\right\|_{{\mathrm{L}^{p}(W)\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{p}}}\lesssim_{n,d,p}[W]_{A_{p}}^{1/(p-1)}.

We will use a trick due to Bownik and Cruz-Uribe
[
BC22
]
so as to use
Theorem
5
(which is a statement about convex body valued functions)
to show statements about
\mathbb{C}^{d}
vector valued functions.
In order to apply the previous results for convex body valued operators to these weighted maximal operators,
we will also need to define the convex body valued analogue of
M_{W}.

Both definitions will be related by the following correspondence between vector valued and convex body valued functions.
Given a vector valued function
f,
that is taking values on
\mathbb{C}^{d},

we define the convex body valued function
F
by

F(x)\coloneq\{\lambda f(x)\colon|\lambda|\leq 1\}.

(3.6)

By construction,
F
takes values on
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
and also
|F(x)|=|f(x)|.

Thus, any estimate for operators depending on
|f(x)|
can be studied through
estimates for an analogous operator acting on
F(x)
and depending as well on
|F(x)|
uniquely.
It is straightforward to check that, for a locally integrable vector valued function
f
and a cube
Q,

it holds that the averaging operator
A_{Q}
applied to
F
given by (
3.6
) can be computed as

A_{Q}F(x)=\left\{\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}k(y)f(y)\,\,\mathrm{dm}(y)\colon k\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty},\left\|{k}\right\|_{{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}}\leq 1\right\}\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x).

(3.7)

For this reason, we will abuse notation and denote
A_{Q}f(x)=A_{Q}F(x).

Also note that, by an appropriate choice of the function
k
in (
3.7
), one can see that

|A_{Q}f(x)|\sim_{d}\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|f(y)|\,\,\mathrm{dm}(y),

(3.8)

and more generally

|A_{Q}F(x)|\sim_{d}\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|F(y)|\,\,\mathrm{dm}(y)

for convex body valued functions in general, which can be seen by choosing an appropriate selection function.
For fixed
1<p<\infty
and given a
(d\times d)
-matrix weight
W,

the convex body valued analogue of
M_{W}
acting on a locally integrably bounded convex body valued function
F

is defined by

M_{W}^{\mathcal{K}}F(x)\coloneq\overline{\mathrm{conv}\left(\bigcup_{Q}A_{Q}(W(x)^{1/p}F(x))\right)},

where the union is taken over all bounded cubes with sides parallel to the axes.
Moreover, given a locally integrable vector valued function
f,
we denote
M_{W}^{\mathcal{K}}f(x)=M_{W}^{\mathcal{K}}F(x),

where
F
is the convex body valued function given by (
3.6
).
In addition, if
W
is a matrix weight and
f
a vector valued function,
linearity and (
3.8
) yield that

|M_{W}^{\mathcal{K}}f(x)|=|W(x)^{1/p}M^{\mathcal{K}}f(x)|\sim_{d}M_{W}f(x),

(3.9)

where
M^{\mathcal{K}}
denotes the maximal operator weighted by the
(d\times d)
-identity matrix.

Theorem
1
.

Consider a
(d\times d)
matrix weight
W
and a sequence of vector valued functions
\{f_{n}\}.

Then, for each
1<p,q<\infty
it holds that

\left\|{\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|M_{W}f_{n}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}}\right\|_{{\mathrm{L}^{p}}}\leq C(n,d,p,q,[W]_{A_{p}})\left\|{\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|W(x)^{1/p}f_{n}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}}\right\|_{{\mathrm{L}^{p}}},

where

C(n,d,p,q,[W]_{A_{p}})=C(n,d,p,q)[W]_{A_{p}}^{\max\left\{\frac{1}{q-1},\frac{1}{p-1}\right\}}.

Proof.

In order to apply Theorem
5

4
Two matrix weighted biparameter product BMO

Let
\bm{\mathcal{D}}=\mathcal{D}^{1}\times\mathcal{D}^{2}
be any product dyadic grid in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}.
Let
1<p<\infty,
and let
U,V
be biparameter
(d\times d)

\bm{\mathcal{D}}
-dyadic
A_{p}
weights on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Let

\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}
be any sequence in
\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}).
We emphasize that

stands for the set of biparameter signatures,
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}^{1}\times\mathcal{E}^{2},

\mathcal{E}^{1}=\{0,1\}^{n}\setminus\{(1,\ldots,1)\}

We define

\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}|\mathcal{V}_{R}B_{R}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{U}_{R}^{-1}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2},

of nonzero finite measure, and all reducing operators are taken with respect to exponent
p.
Note that

|\mathcal{V}_{R}P\mathcal{U}^{-1}_{R}|=|\mathcal{U}^{-1}_{R}P^{\ast}\mathcal{V}_{R}|\sim_{p,d}|\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{R}P^{\ast}(\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{R})^{-1}|,\qquad\forall R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}},\penalty\ \forall P\in\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}).

Therefore

\|B\|_{\text{BMO}_{\text{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)}\sim_{p,d}\|B^{\ast}\|_{\text{BMO}_{\text{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(V^{\prime},U^{\prime},p^{\prime})}.

functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
with finitely many nonzero biparameter cancellative Haar coefficients, and we identify such functions with finitely supported sequences
\Phi=\{\Phi_{R}^{\varepsilon}\}_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}
in
\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
in the obvious way. Then, for all
\Phi\in\mathcal{H},
we have

\Phi=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}h_{R}^{\varepsilon}\Phi_{R}^{\varepsilon}

and therefore

\displaystyle\ell(\Phi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}\ell(h_{R}^{\varepsilon}\Phi_{R}^{\varepsilon})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\ell(h_{R}^{\varepsilon}\Phi_{R}^{\varepsilon}(i,j)E_{ij})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}h_{R}^{\varepsilon}\Phi_{R}^{\varepsilon}(i,j)\ell(E_{ij})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{tr}((B_{R}^{\varepsilon})^{\ast}\Phi_{R}^{\varepsilon})=(\Phi,B),

where
E_{ij}
is the
d\times d
-matrix with 1 at the
(i,j)
-entry and 0 at all other entries,
A(i,j)
is the
(i,j)
-entry of a matrix
A,
and

B_{R}^{\varepsilon}(i,j)\coloneq\overline{\ell(h_{R}^{\varepsilon}E_{ji})},\qquad\forall i,j=1,\ldots,d,\penalty\ \forall R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}},\penalty\ \forall\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}.

By Proposition
8
and since
\mathcal{H}
is dense in
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p),
it suffices only to prove that the defined sequence
B=\{B_{R}^{\varepsilon}\}_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}
is in
\text{BMO}_{\text{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)
with

\|B\|_{\text{BMO}_{\text{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)}\lesssim_{n,m,p,d}[V]_{A_{p},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}^{2}\|\ell\|.

(4.3)

Note that by the scalar, unweighted BMO equivalences we have

\displaystyle\|B\|_{\text{BMO}_{\text{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)}

\displaystyle\sim_{n,m,p,d}\sup_{\Omega}\frac{1}{|\Omega|^{1/p^{\prime}}}\bigg\|\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}|\mathcal{V}_{R}B_{R}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{U}_{R}^{-1}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}}{|R|}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})}=:\mathcal{C},

so it suffices only to prove that
\mathcal{C}\lesssim_{n,m,p,d}\|\ell\|.
By the Monotone Convergence Theorem, we can without loss of generality assume that
B
has only finitely many nonzero terms.

Let us denote by
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
the Hermitian product on
\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
given by

\langle A,B\rangle\coloneq\mathrm{tr}(B^{\ast}A).

The norm that
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
induces on
\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
is the Hilbert–Schmidt norm, which is of course equivalent to the usual matrix norm, up to constants depending only on
d.
Thus, by general facts about Lebesgue spaces of functions with values in a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, we have that the usual (unweighted)
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-
L^{p^{\prime}}
duality for
\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
-valued functions can be equivalently rewritten as

\displaystyle\|F\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m};\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}

\displaystyle\sim_{n,m,p,d}\left\{\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+m}}\langle F(x),G(x)\rangle\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\right|:\penalty\ G\in\mathcal{H},\penalty\ \|G\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m};\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}=1\right\}

\displaystyle=\bigg\{\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{tr}((G^{\varepsilon}_{R})^{\ast}F_{R}^{\varepsilon})\bigg|:\penalty\ G\in\mathcal{H},\penalty\ \|G\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m};\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}=1\bigg\},

(4.4)

for all
F\in\mathcal{H}.
We recall also the usual (unweighted) dyadic Littlewood–Paley estimates

\displaystyle\|F\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m};\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}

\displaystyle\sim_{n,m,p,d}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+m}}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}|F_{R}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)}{|R|}\bigg)^{p/2}dx\bigg)^{1/p},\qquad\forall F\in\mathcal{H}.

(4.5)

Fix now any Lebesgue-measurable subset
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
with finite nonzero measure. Then, we have

\displaystyle\bigg(\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}|\mathcal{V}_{R}B_{R}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{U}_{R}^{-1}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)}{|R|}\bigg)^{p^{\prime}/2}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\bigg)^{1/p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{Littlewood-Paley}}{\sim}_{n,m,p,d}\bigg\|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}h_{R}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{V}_{R}B_{R}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{U}_{R}^{-1}\bigg\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m};\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{Riesz representation}}{\sim}_{n,m,p,d}\sup_{A\in\mathcal{H}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{\|A\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m};\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{tr}\left((A_{R}^{\varepsilon})^{\ast}\mathcal{V}_{R}B_{R}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{U}_{R}^{-1}\right)\bigg|

\displaystyle=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{H}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{\|A\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m};\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{tr}\left((B_{R}^{\varepsilon})^{\ast}\mathcal{V}_{R}A_{R}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{U}_{R}^{-1}\right)\bigg|

\displaystyle=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{H}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{\|A\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}}|(\hat{A},B)|=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{H}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{\|A\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}}|\ell(\hat{A})|

\displaystyle\leq\|\ell\|\sup_{A\in\mathcal{H}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{\|A\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}}\|\hat{A}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)},

where

\hat{A}^{\varepsilon}_{R}\coloneq\begin{cases}\mathcal{V}_{R}A_{R}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{U}_{R}^{-1},\text{ if }R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\\
\\
0,\text{ otherwise}\end{cases},\quad R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}},\penalty\ \varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}.

It suffices now to prove that

\|\hat{A}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)}\lesssim_{n,m,p,d}[V]_{A_{p},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}^{2}|\Omega|^{1/p^{\prime}}\|A\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))},

for all
A\in\mathcal{H}.
We set

N_{\Omega}(x)\coloneq\sup_{R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)}|V(x)^{-1/p}\mathcal{V}_{R}|\mathbf{1}_{R}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}.

We define now functions
\widetilde{N}_{\Omega}^{(k)}
by applying
the strong dyadic Christ–Goldberg maximal operator
M_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},V^{\prime}}
with respect to the biparameter
(d\times d)
matrix valued
\bm{\mathcal{D}}
-dyadic
A_{p^{\prime}}
weight
V^{\prime}
on the function
V^{1/p}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}e_{k}
for each
k=1,\ldots,d
(where we recall that
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}
is the standard basis of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
).
That is, we define

\widetilde{N}_{\Omega}^{(k)}(x)\coloneq\sup_{R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}}\langle|V(x)^{-1/p}V^{1/p}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}e_{k}|\rangle_{R}\mathbf{1}_{R}(x).

If we consider
\widetilde{N}_{\Omega}\coloneq\sup_{k=1,\ldots,d}\widetilde{N}_{\Omega}^{(k)},
this satisfies

\widetilde{N}_{\Omega}(x)\gtrsim_{n,m,d,p}[V]_{A_{p},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}^{-1/p}N_{\Omega}(x).

Indeed, just observe that

\displaystyle N_{\Omega}(x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)}|V(x)^{-1/p}\mathcal{V}_{R}|\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)=\sup_{R}|\mathcal{V}_{R}V(x)^{-1/p}|\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)

\displaystyle\sim_{d}\sup_{R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)}\langle|V^{1/p}V(x)^{-1/p}|^{p}\rangle^{1/p}\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{n,m,d,p}[V]_{A_{p},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}^{1/p}\sup_{R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)}\langle|V^{1/p}V(x)^{-1/p}|\rangle_{R}\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)

\displaystyle=[V]_{A_{p},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}^{1/p}\sup_{R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)}\langle|V(x)^{-1/p}V^{1/p}|\rangle_{R}\mathbf{1}_{R}(x),

because the scalar weight
|V^{1/p}M|^{p}
is uniformly in the biparameter
\bm{\mathcal{D}}
-dyadic Muckenhoupt
A_{p}
class for every positive definite matrix
M
(see for example
[
DKP24
, Lemma 3.4]
).
Next, using the comparability between the matrix norm and the supremum of norms of matrix columns, we get

\displaystyle N_{\Omega}(x)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{n,m,d,p}[V]_{A_{p},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}^{1/p}\sup_{R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)}\sup_{k=1,\ldots,d}\langle|V(x)^{-1/p}V^{1/p}e_{k}|\rangle_{R}\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)

\displaystyle\leq[V]_{A_{p},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}^{1/p}\widetilde{N}_{\Omega}(x).

Finally, using the boundedness (
4.2
) of the strong dyadic Christ–Goldberg maximal operator,
we obtain

\|N_{\Omega}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{n,m,p,d}[V]_{A_{p},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}^{1/p}[V^{\prime}]_{A_{p^{\prime}},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}^{2/(p^{\prime}-1)}|\Omega|^{1/p^{\prime}}\sim_{p,d}[V]_{A_{p},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}^{2+1/p}|\Omega|^{1/p^{\prime}}.

Thus, for all
A\in\mathcal{H}
we have

\displaystyle\|\hat{A}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}|V(x)^{-1/p}\mathcal{V}_{R}A_{R}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{U}_{R}^{-1}\mathcal{U}_{R}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)}{|R|}\bigg)^{1/2}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}|V(x)^{-1/p}\mathcal{V}_{R}A_{R}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)}{|R|}\bigg)^{1/2}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}N_{\Omega}(x)\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}|A_{R}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)}{|R|}\bigg)^{1/2}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{n,m,p,d}\|N_{\Omega}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\|A\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m};\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{n,m,p,d}[V]_{A_{p},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}^{2+1/p}|\Omega|^{1/p^{\prime}}\|A\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m};\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))},

concluding the proof of (
4.3
).

Uniqueness of
B
follows immediately by testing
\ell
on sequences in
\mathcal{H}.

4.2
Two-matrix weighted bounds for paraproducts

Let
B:\mathbb{R}^{n+m}\to\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
be a locally integrable function. We define

\|B\|_{\text{BMO}_{\text{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)}\coloneq\sup_{\Omega}\frac{1}{|\Omega|^{1/2}}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}|\mathcal{V}_{R}B_{R}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{U}_{R}^{-1}|\bigg)^{1/2},

where
\{B_{R}^{\varepsilon}\}
is the sequence of biparameter Haar coefficients of
B
. Following the terminology of Holmes–Petermichl–Wick
[
HPW18
, Subsection 6.1]
we define the following so-called “pure” biparameter paraproducts acting on (suitable)
\mathbb{C}^{d}
-valued functions
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
:

\Pi_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}^{(11)}f\coloneq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}h^{\varepsilon}_{R}B_{R}^{\varepsilon}\langle f\rangle_{R},\quad\Pi_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}^{(00)}f\coloneq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}}{|R|}B^{\varepsilon}_{R}f^{\varepsilon}_{R},

\Gamma_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}f\coloneq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon,\delta\in\mathcal{E}\\
\varepsilon_{i}\neq\delta_{i},\penalty\ i=1,2\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|R|}}h_{R}^{1\oplus\varepsilon\oplus\delta}B_{R}^{\varepsilon}f_{R}^{\delta}.

Here we denote

1\oplus 1=0\oplus 0=0,\quad 1\oplus 0=0\oplus 1=1

and extend these operations in the obvious entrywise fashion to
\mathcal{E}^{1},

\mathcal{E}^{2}
and
\mathcal{E}.
Moreover, abusing notation we denote
(1,\ldots,1)
(where the number of entries is always clear from the context) by just
1.
Notice that
1\oplus\varepsilon_{i}\oplus\delta_{i}\neq 1,
for all
\varepsilon,\delta\in\mathcal{E}
with
\varepsilon_{i}\neq\delta_{i},

i=1,2.

Clearly
(\Pi_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}^{(00)})^{\ast}=\Pi_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B^{\ast}}^{(11)}
in the unweighted
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m};\mathbb{C}^{d})
sense. Observe also that a change of summation variables yields

\Gamma_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}f=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon,\delta\in\mathcal{E}\\
\varepsilon_{i}\neq\delta_{i},\penalty\ i=1,2\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|R|}}h_{R}^{\varepsilon}B_{R}^{1\oplus\varepsilon\oplus\delta}f_{R}^{\delta},

therefore
(\Gamma_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B})^{\ast}=\Gamma_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B^{\ast}}
in the unweighted
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m};\mathbb{C}^{d})
sense.

Proposition 11
.

Let
d,p,U,V
and
B
be as above.

(a)

There holds

\|\Pi_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}^{(11)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{p}(V)}\sim\|\Pi_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}^{(00)}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{p}(V)}\sim\|B\|_{\emph{BMO}_{\emph{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)},

where all implied constants depend only on
n,m,d,p,[U]_{A_{p},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}
and
[V]_{A_{p},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}.

(b)

There holds

\|\Gamma_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{p}(V)}\lesssim\|B\|_{\emph{BMO}_{\emph{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)},

where all implied constants depend only on
n,m,d,p,[U]_{A_{p},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}
and
[V]_{A_{p},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}.

Proof.

Throughout the proof
\lesssim,\gtrsim,\sim
mean that all implied inequality constants depend only on
n,m,p,d,[U]_{A_{p},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}
and
[V]_{A_{p},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}.

We adapt part of the proof of
[
Isr17
, Theorem 2.2]
. Note that by the John–Nirenberg inequalities for (unweighted) scalar dyadic product BMO we have

\displaystyle\|B\|_{\text{BMO}_{\text{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)}

\displaystyle\sim_{p,d,n,m}\sup_{\Omega}\frac{1}{|\Omega|^{1/p}}\bigg\|\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}|\mathcal{V}_{R}B_{R}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{U}_{R}^{-1}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}}{|R|}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})}\coloneq\mathcal{C},

where the supremum is taken over all Lebesgue-measurable subsets
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
of nonzero finite measure. Therefore, it suffices to prove that

\|\Pi_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}^{(11)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m};\mathbb{C}^{d})}\gtrsim\mathcal{C}

and

\|\Pi_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}^{(11)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m};\mathbb{C}^{d})}\lesssim\|B\|_{\text{BMO}_{\text{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)}.

Let us first see that
\mathcal{C}\lesssim\|\Pi_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}^{(11)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m};\mathbb{C}^{d})}.
Let
\Omega
be any Lebesgue-measurable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
of nonzero finite measure. Let also
e\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
be arbitrary. We test
\Pi_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}^{(11)}
on the function
f\coloneq\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}U^{-1/p}e.
Using Lemma 5.3 of
[
DKP24
]
we obtain

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}^{(11)}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m};\mathbb{C}^{d})}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+m}}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}|\mathcal{V}_{R}B_{R}^{\varepsilon}\langle f\rangle_{R}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)}{|R|}\bigg)^{p/2}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\bigg)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\geq\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+m}}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}|\mathcal{V}_{R}B_{R}\langle U^{-1/p}\rangle_{R}e|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)}{|R|}\bigg)^{p/2}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\bigg)^{1/p}.

Note also that
\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)}=|\Omega|^{1/p}\cdot|e|.
Therefore, we see that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Omega|^{1/p}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+m}}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}|\mathcal{V}_{R}B_{R}^{\varepsilon}\langle U^{-1/p}\rangle_{R}e|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)}{|R|}\bigg)^{p/2}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\bigg)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\Pi_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}^{(11)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m};\mathbb{C}^{d})}|e|,

for all
e\in\mathbb{C}^{d}.
In view of (
2.3
) we deduce

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Omega|^{1/p}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+m}}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}|\mathcal{V}_{R}B_{R}^{\varepsilon}\langle U^{-1/p}\rangle_{R}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)}{|R|}\bigg)^{p/2}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\bigg)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\Pi_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}^{(11)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m};\mathbb{C}^{d})}.

By part (2) of Lemma
3
we have

|P\langle U^{-1/p}\rangle_{R}|=|\langle U^{-1/p}\rangle_{R}P^{\ast}|\sim_{p,d}|\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{R}P|\sim_{p,d}|\mathcal{U}^{-1}_{R}P|=|P\mathcal{U}^{-1}_{R}|,\quad\forall P\in\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}),

yielding the desired result.

We now prove that
\|\Pi_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}^{(11)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m};\mathbb{C}^{d})}\lesssim\|B\|_{\text{BMO}_{\text{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)}.
Using Lemma 5.3 of
[
DKP24
]
we have

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}^{(11)}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m};\mathbb{C}^{d})}

\displaystyle\sim\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+m}}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}|\mathcal{V}_{R}B_{R}^{\varepsilon}\langle f\rangle_{R}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)}{|R|}\bigg)^{p/2}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\bigg)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+m}}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}|\mathcal{V}_{R}B_{R}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{U}_{R}^{-1}|^{2}|\mathcal{U}_{R}\langle f\rangle_{R}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)}{|R|}\bigg)^{p/2}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\bigg)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+m}}\bigg(\sum_{R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}}|\mathcal{V}_{R}B_{R}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{U}_{R}^{-1}|^{2}(\langle\widetilde{M}_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},U}f\rangle_{R})^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)}{|R|}\bigg)^{p/2}\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\bigg)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\sim_{p,n,m}\|\Pi_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},b}^{(11)}(\widetilde{M}_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},U}f)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})},

where
b=(b_{R}^{\varepsilon})_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}
is the sequence given by

b_{R}^{\varepsilon}\coloneq|\mathcal{V}_{R}B_{R}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{U}_{R}^{-1}|,\quad R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}},\penalty\ \varepsilon\in\mathcal{E},

and

\widetilde{M}_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},U}f\coloneq\sup_{R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}}\langle|\mathcal{U}_{R}f|\rangle_{R}\mathbf{1}_{R}.

By the well-known unweighted bounds for paraproducts in the scalar setting (see e. g.
[
BP05
]
) we have

\|\Pi_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},b}^{(11)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})}\lesssim_{p,n,m}\|b\|_{\text{BMO}_{\text{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}}.

By definition,
\|b\|_{\text{BMO}_{\text{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}}=\|B\|_{\text{BMO}_{\text{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)}.
From
[
DKP24
, Proposition 4.1]
we also have

\|\widetilde{M}_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},U}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})}\lesssim 1.

It follows that

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}^{(11)}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m};\mathbb{C}^{d})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|B\|_{\text{BMO}_{\text{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)}\|\widetilde{M}_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},U}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|B\|_{\text{BMO}_{\text{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)}\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)}.

Finally, by duality we obtain

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}^{(00)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{p}(V)}

\displaystyle=\|\Pi_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B^{\ast}}^{(11)}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(V^{\prime})\rightarrow L^{p^{\prime}}(U^{\prime})}\sim\|B^{\ast}\|_{\text{BMO}_{\text{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(V^{\prime},U^{\prime},p^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\sim_{p,d}\|B\|_{\text{BMO}_{\text{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)}.

We adapt the factorization trick from the proof of
[
HPW18
, Proposition 6.1]
. We have

\displaystyle(\Gamma_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}f,g)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon,\delta\in\mathcal{E}\\
\varepsilon_{i}\neq\delta_{i},\penalty\ i=1,2\end{subarray}}\langle B_{R}^{1\oplus\varepsilon\oplus\delta}f_{R}^{\varepsilon},g_{R}^{\delta}\rangle.

Observe that if
A\in\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
and
x,y\in\mathbb{C}^{d},
then

\displaystyle\langle Ax,y\rangle

\displaystyle=x^{T}A^{T}\bar{y}=\mathrm{tr}(x^{T}A^{T}\bar{y})=\overline{\mathrm{tr}(A^{\ast}y\bar{x}^{T})}.

It follows that

\displaystyle|(\Gamma_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}f,g)|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon,\delta\in\mathcal{E}\\
\varepsilon_{i}\neq\delta_{i},\penalty\ i=1,2\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|R|}}\text{tr}((B_{R}^{\varepsilon})^{\ast}g_{R}^{1\oplus\varepsilon\oplus\delta}\overline{f_{R}^{\delta}}^{T})\bigg|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|B\|_{\text{BMO}_{\text{prod},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)}\|\Phi\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)},

where

\Phi_{R}^{\varepsilon}\coloneq\frac{1}{\sqrt{|R|}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta\in\mathcal{E}\\
\delta_{i}\neq\varepsilon_{i},\penalty\ i=1,2\end{subarray}}g_{R}^{1\oplus\varepsilon\oplus\delta}\overline{f_{R}^{\delta}}^{T},\quad R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}},\penalty\ \varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}.

It suffices now to prove that

\|\Phi\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{p}(U)}\|g\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(V^{\prime})}.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}\bigg|V(x)^{-1/p}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta\in\mathcal{E}\\

\displaystyle\leq 2^{n+m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon,\delta\in\mathcal{E}\\
\varepsilon_{i}\neq\delta_{i},\penalty\ i=1,2\end{subarray}}|V(x)^{-1/p}g_{R}^{1\oplus\varepsilon\oplus\delta}\overline{f_{R}^{\delta}}^{T}\mathcal{U}_{R}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)}{|R|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{n+m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon,\delta\in\mathcal{E}\\
\varepsilon_{i}\neq\delta_{i},\penalty\ i=1,2\end{subarray}}|V(x)^{-1/p}g_{R}^{1\oplus\varepsilon\oplus\delta}|^{2}\cdot|\overline{f_{R}^{\delta}}^{T}\mathcal{U}_{R}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)}{|R|^{2}}

\displaystyle=2^{n+m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon,\delta\in\mathcal{E}\\
\varepsilon_{i}\neq\delta_{i},\penalty\ i=1,2\end{subarray}}|V(x)^{-1/p}g_{R}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)}{|R|}\cdot|\mathcal{U}_{R}f_{R}^{\delta}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}(x)}{|R|}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{n+m}S_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},V^{\prime}}g(x)\widetilde{S}_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},U}f(x),

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m},
where

S_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},V^{\prime}}g\coloneq\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}|V(x)^{-1/p}g_{R}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}}{|R|}\bigg)^{1/2},\quad\widetilde{S}_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},U}f\coloneq\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\
\delta\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}|\mathcal{U}_{R}f_{R}^{\delta}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{R}}{|R|}\bigg)^{1/2}.

Therefore

\|\Phi\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(U,V,p)}\leq 2^{n+m}\|S_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},V^{\prime}}g\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})}\|\widetilde{S}_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},U}f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})}.

It is proved in
[

, Lemma 5.2 and Corollary 5.4]
that

\|\widetilde{S}_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},U}f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)},\qquad\|S_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},V^{\prime}}g\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})}\lesssim\|g\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(V^{\prime})},

yielding the desired bound.
∎

Lemma 12
.

Let

Lemma 13
.

Let
1<p<\infty
and let
\{f_{P,\varepsilon}\}_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{D}^{2}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}^{2}\end{subarray}}
be a family of nonnegative measurable functions on
\mathbb{R}^{m}.
Then, it holds

\bigg\|\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{D}^{2}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}^{2}\end{subarray}}\langle f_{P,\varepsilon}\rangle_{P}^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{P}}{|P|}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}\lesssim_{m,p}\bigg\|\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{D}^{2}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}^{2}\end{subarray}}|f_{P,\varepsilon}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{P}}{|P|}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}.

Proof.

We use duality. Note that through an application of the monotone convergence theorem we may assume without loss of generality that only finitely many of the functions
\{f_{P,\varepsilon}\}_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{D}^{2}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}^{2}\end{subarray}}
are not identically equal to zero. Then, by the dyadic (unweighted) Littlewood–Payley estimates we have

\bigg\|\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{D}^{2}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}^{2}\end{subarray}}\langle f_{P,\varepsilon}\rangle_{P}^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{P}}{|P|}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}\sim_{m,p}\|F\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{m})},

where

F\coloneq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{D}^{2}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}^{2}\end{subarray}}\langle f_{P,\varepsilon}\rangle_{P}h_{P}^{\varepsilon}.

Let
g\in L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
be arbitrary. Then, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}|F(x)g(x)|\,\mathrm{dm}(x)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{D}^{2}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}^{2}\end{subarray}}\langle f_{P,\varepsilon}\rangle\cdot|g_{P}^{\varepsilon}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{D}^{2}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}^{2}\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}f_{P,\varepsilon}(x)h_{P}^{\varepsilon}(x)g_{P}^{\varepsilon}h_{P}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,\mathrm{dm}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\bigg\|\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{D}^{2}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}^{2}\end{subarray}}|f_{P,\varepsilon}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{P}}{|P|}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}\cdot\bigg\|\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{D}^{2}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}^{2}\end{subarray}}|g_{P}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{P}}{|P|}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}

\displaystyle\sim_{m,p}\bigg\|\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{D}^{2}\\
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}^{2}\end{subarray}}|f_{P,\varepsilon}|^{2}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{P}}{|P|}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}\cdot\|g\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}.

An appeal to the Riesz representation theorem concludes the proof.
∎

We adapt the factorization trick from the proof of

the proof for the other mixed paraproducts being similar or following by duality. We have

\displaystyle\left|(\Gamma_{\bm{\mathcal{D}},B}^{(10)}f,g)\right|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}\\

where

4.3

Two matrix weighted upper bounds for biparameter paraproducts lead naturally to two matrix weighed upper bounds for bicommutators. Here we overview very briefly the simplest case: that of bicommutators with Haar multipliers on
\mathbb{R}.
The argument is a straightforward adaptation of the one in the (unweighted)

Let

and

acting on functions

for a.e.

Let
B:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathrm{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
be a locally integrable function. For scalar valued locally integrable functions

\mathbb{R}^{2}
it is shown in
[

]
that

where

Applying (

[T^{1}_{\sigma_{1}},[T^{2}_{\sigma_{2}},B]]=[T^{1}_{\sigma_{1}},[T^{2}_{\sigma_{2}},\Lambda_{B}]],

Let now

matrix

-dyadic

In the following estimates, all implied constants depend only on

and
[V]_{A_{p},\bm{\mathcal{D}}}.
Using (
2.6.3

\|T^{j}_{\sigma_{j}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)\to\mathrm{L}^{p}(V)}\lesssim 1,\quad j=1,2.

Observe also that

11
and

\|\Lambda_{B}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)\to\mathrm{L}^{p}(V)}\lesssim 1.

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\|[T_{1},[T_{2},B]]\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)\to\mathrm{L}^{p}(V)}=\|[T_{1},[T_{2},\Lambda_{B}]]\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)\to\mathrm{L}^{p}(V)}

\displaystyle\leq\|T_{1}T_{2}\Lambda_{B}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)\to\mathrm{L}^{p}(V)}+\|T_{1}\Lambda_{B}T_{2}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)\to\mathrm{L}^{p}(V)}

Appendix

Through the article we have used the results of Bownik and Cruz-Uribe
[
BC22
]
in the complex setting.
The results in that paper are stated in the real setting and
it is not immediate that they work in the complex setting.
Some modifications are necessary and some steps require proper justification.
For instance, given a family of norms measurably parametrized (over
\mathbb{C}^{d}
),
it is not obvious that one can assign them a reducing matrix and a complex John ellipsoid
in a measurable way.
This is shown in detail in
[
DKP24
, Appendix A]
.
We devote this appendix to briefly discussing the necessary modifications
and where to find the details to get the results of
[
BC22
]

for complex valued matrix weights.

A.1
Convex sets and seminorms in the complex setting

We begin with the required changes in
[
BC22
, Section 2]
.
In both the present work and that of Bownik and Cruz-Uribe,
one considers symmetric sets.
The difference is that we substitute real symmetric sets
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d},

that is sets
E
such that
-u\in E
for every
u\in E,

by complex symmetric sets
E\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d},

i.e. sets
E
for which
\lambda u\in E
for every
u\in E
and for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\lambda|=1.
Let us note that the “symmetric convex sets” as we have defined them are precisely the balanced convex sets, meaning that
\lambda u\in E
, for all
u\in E
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\lambda|\leq 1.

Besides this, the rest of the definitions concerning convex bodies,
including that of the Minkowski addition of sets, is the same.
Of course, the definitions for seminorms are also the same,
only that we consider them to be defined on the vector space
\mathbb{C}^{d}

and to be complex homogeneous functions, which means that if
\rho

is a seminorm on
\mathbb{C}^{d},
then
\rho(\lambda v)=|\lambda|\rho(v)

for any
v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}.

Also, the fact that seminorms are in a one-to-one correspondence
with absorbing convex (complex) symmetric bodies
(the analogous result to
[
Cru23
, Theorem 2.4]
)
also holds with the same proof (see
[
Rud91
, Theorems 1.34 and 1.35]
).

Dual seminorms, their properties and their relation to
the polar of convex (complex) symmetric sets follow the same exposition
in the vector space
\mathbb{C}^{d}
as in
[
BC22
]
.
Here one only needs to keep in mind that we substitute the real Euclidean product
by the complex Hermitian product of
\mathbb{C}^{d}.

For this reason, in this context the support function
h_{K}
of
a set
K\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
has to be defined as

h_{K}(v)\coloneq\sup_{w\in K}|\langle v,w\rangle|,

(cf.
[
BC22
, Definition 2.12]
).
With this definition, the proof of
[
BC22
, Theorem 2.11]
,
which covers the properties of seminorms and their relation to convex bodies,
needs to be adapted.
For instance, to show that

p_{(K_{1}+K_{2})^{\circ}}=p_{K_{1}^{\circ}}+p_{K_{2}^{\circ}},

one first shows that

p_{(K_{1}+K_{2})^{\circ}}\leq p_{K_{1}^{\circ}}+p_{K_{2}^{\circ}}

by the triangle inequality.
Then, the reverse inequality is proved by taking
\varepsilon>0
arbitrary and

w_{1}\in K_{1}
and
w_{2}\in K_{2}
with
\langle v,w_{j}\rangle\geq h_{K_{j}}(v)-\varepsilon/2

for
j=1,2
(we can omit the moduli here because of complex symmetry).
This argument has to be repeated through the rest of the statements
of
[
BC22
, Theorem 2.11]
.
The properties of weighted geometric means of norms follow the same explanation
for norms defined on
\mathbb{C}^{d}.

For the rest of the section, the facts about positive definite matrices follow
by the same arguments,
since the facts that the authors of
[
BC22
]
use
hold in the complex setting (see
[
Bha07
, Chapter 6]
).
Here, one only needs to take into account that we consider hermitian matrices
instead of symmetric ones and that we use unitary matrices instead of orthogonal ones.

A.2
Convex-set valued functions in the complex setting

In this subsection we explain the necessary adaptations that have to be performed in Section 3 of
[
BC22
]
in the complex setting. In some places
\mathbb{R}^{d}
needs to be replaced by
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, which as a topological space, as a metric space, as a measure space and as a real vector space is the same as
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. In some other places,
\mathbb{R}^{d}
needs to be replaced directly by
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and “complex” versions of the ingredients of statements or proofs are necessary. We lay out the details below.

First of all, measurable maps
F:\Omega\to\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
from a positive,
\sigma
-finite, complete measure space
\Omega
into the set
\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
of closed subsets of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
are the same as
measurable maps
F:\Omega\to\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
. The various characterizations of measurability for maps
F:\Omega\to\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
given in
[
BC22
, Theorem 3.2]
with
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as the underlying space continue to hold with
\mathbb{R}^{d}
replaced by
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, and thus
\mathbb{C}^{d}
as the underlying space. Let us observe in particular that a measurable selection function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
of a map
F:\Omega\to\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
is the same as a measurable selection function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2d}
of the map
F:\Omega\to\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
.

Similarly, the measurability of closed convex hulls of countable unions
and the measurability of countable intersections of convex body valued functions
[
BC22
, Theorem 3.3]

hold in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and therefore also in
\mathbb{C}^{d}.

A similar remark applies to
[
BC22
, Theorem 3.5]
,
which states the equivalence between the definition of closed set valued functions
and the measurability as a mapping with respect to the Hausdorff topology.

Referring to
[
BC22
, Theorem 3.4]
proving the measurability of the polar of a measurable map, one replaces
\mathbb{R}^{d}
directly by
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. This entails that complex symmetric convex sets are considered, and that the real Euclidean product on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
needs to be replaced by the complex Hermitian product on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. A similar change applies to
[
BC22
, Theorem 3.6]
.
This result applies to measurable set valued functions
F
taking values on
the set of linear subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{d},

and it states that if
P(x)
is the orthogonal projection from
\mathbb{C}^{d}
to
F(x),

the mapping
P\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathrm{M}_{{d}}({\mathbb{C}})
is also measurable.
The necessary change is to replace
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, so that orthogonal projections and the Gram–Schmidt orthonormalization process are considered with respect to the Hermitian product on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Real linear subspaces are replaced by complex linear subspaces. At the last step of the proof of
[
BC22
, Theorem 3.6]
, a countable dense subset of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
is needed, one can take
\mathbb{Q}(i)^{d}
for example.

Next, one needs the existence and properties of John ellipsoids for bounded complex symmetric convex subsets of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. These are thoroughly established in
[
DKP24
, Appendix A]
and the distinction between complex and real ellipsoids is made precise. In fact, it is implicit in
[
DKP24
, Subsection A.4.7]
that a real ellipsoid in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is a complex ellipsoid in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
if and only if it is invariant under the real orthogonal map
\widetilde{L}_{z}
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
|z|=1
. For the reader’s convenience we lay out the details below.

We identify
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
with
\mathbb{C}^{d}
in the natural way, namely by the map
R:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{2d}
given by

R(x_{1}+ix_{2},\ldots,x_{2d-1}+ix_{2d}):=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{2d-1},x_{2d}).

We denote by
\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}
the closed unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and by
\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{d}}
the closed unit ball in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, so
\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}=R(\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{d}})
.

A
real ellipsoid
(more precisely, a
nondegenerate centrally symmetric ellipsoid
) in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is by definition a subset
E
of
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
of the form
E=A\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}
for some invertible real linear map
A:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. In this case, the polar decomposition yields
E=(AA^{*})^{1/2}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}
. It follows that for each ellipsoid
E
in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
there is a unique real positive definite linear map
A:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}^{2d}
with
E=A\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}
, which we denote by
A:=M_{\mathbb{R},2d}(E)
.

A
complex ellipsoid
(more precisely, a
nondegenerate centrally symmetric ellipsoid
) in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
is by definition a subset
E
of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
of the form
E=A\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{d}}
for some invertible complex linear map
A:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
. In this case, the polar decomposition yields
E=(AA^{*})^{1/2}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{d}}
. It follows that for each ellipsoid
E
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
there is a unique complex positive definite linear map
A:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
with
E=A\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{d}}
, which we denote by
A:=M_{\mathbb{C},d}(E)
.

If
A:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
is any complex linear map, then under our identification of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
,
A
corresponds to the real linear map
RAR^{-1}:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. Concretely,
RAR^{-1}
is obtained by
A
through replacing each complex entry
a_{ij}
of
A
with

H(a_{ij})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{Re}(a_{ij})&-\mathrm{Im}(a_{ij})\\
\mathrm{Im}(a_{ij})&\mathrm{Re}(a_{ij})\end{bmatrix}.

Observe that the map
H:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
is a ring monomorphism. We write
H(A):=RAR^{-1}
. It is then easy to see that if
A
is complex hermitian, respectively unitary, respectively positive definite, then
H(A)
is real symmetric, respectively orthogonal, respectively positive definite. In particular, if for each
z\in\mathbb{C}
,
L_{z}:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
denotes multiplication with
z
, then we can consider the corresponding linear map
\widetilde{L}_{z}:=RL_{z}R^{-1}:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. So for each
z\in\mathbb{C}
and for each nonempty subset
E
of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
we have
zE=E
if and only if
\widetilde{L}_{z}(R(E))=R(E)
. Moreover, if
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
|z|=1
, then
\widetilde{L}_{z}
is easily seen to be a real orthogonal map.

The precise relation between real and complex ellipsoids is explained in the following lemma.

Lemma 15

\mathcal{C}_{2d}(\mathbb{R})

\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and let
\mathcal{C}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
be the set of all complex ellipsoids in
\mathbb{C}^{d}

\mathcal{C}_{d}(\mathbb{C})=\{R^{-1}(E):\penalty\ E\in\mathcal{C}_{2d}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})\text{ and }\widetilde{L}_{z}(E)=E\}.

In particular, the complex ellipsoids in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
form a closed subset of the real ellipsoids in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}

Proof.

First of all, let
E
be a real ellipsoid in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
with
\widetilde{L}_{z}(E)=E
. Then, for each
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
|z|=1

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\widetilde{L}_{z}E=\widetilde{L}_{z}M_{\mathbb{R},2d}(E)\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}=(\widetilde{L}_{z}M_{\mathbb{R},2d}(E)(\widetilde{L}_{z}M_{\mathbb{R},2d}(E))^{*})^{1/2}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{L}_{z}M_{\mathbb{R},2d}(E)\widetilde{L}_{z}^{-1}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}},

where we used the fact that
\widetilde{L}_{z}
is an orthogonal linear map. Since
\widetilde{L}_{z}M_{\mathbb{R},2d}(E)\widetilde{L}_{z}^{-1}
is positive definite, by the uniqueness of
M_{\mathbb{R},2d}(E)
it follows that

M_{\mathbb{R},2d}(E)=\widetilde{L}_{z}M_{\mathbb{R},2d}(E)\widetilde{L}_{z}^{-1},

thus also

M_{\mathbb{R},2d}(E)=\widetilde{L}_{z}^{-1}M_{\mathbb{R},2d}(E)\widetilde{L}_{z}.

Now we set
\widetilde{E}:=R^{-1}(E)
and compute

\widetilde{E}=R^{-1}M_{\mathbb{R},2d}(E)\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}=R^{-1}M_{\mathbb{R},2d}(E)R\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{d}}.

In order to show that
\widetilde{E}\in\mathcal{C}_{d}(\mathbb{C})

R^{-1}M_{\mathbb{R},2d}(E)R:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
is in fact complex linear. To this end, it suffices to prove that

L_{z}R^{-1}M_{\mathbb{R},2d}(E)R=R^{-1}M_{\mathbb{R},2d}(E)RL_{z},

for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
in
|z|=1

z

\displaystyle L_{z}R^{-1}M_{\mathbb{R},2d}(E)RL_{z}^{-1}=L_{z}R^{-1}\widetilde{L}_{z}^{-1}M_{\mathbb{R},2d}(E)\widetilde{L}_{z}RL_{z}^{-1}

\displaystyle=L_{z}R^{-1}RL_{z}^{-1}R^{-1}M_{\mathbb{R},2d}(E)RL_{z}R^{-1}RL_{z}^{-1}

\displaystyle=R^{-1}M_{\mathbb{R},2d}(E)R.

This proves the inclusion
\supseteq
in (

).

We now show the inclusion
\subseteq
in (
A.1
). Let
\widetilde{E}
be any complex ellipsoid in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. We set
E:=R(\widetilde{E})
. Then, we compute

\displaystyle E=RM_{\mathbb{C},d}(\widetilde{E})R^{-1}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}.

The map
RM_{\mathbb{C},d}(\widetilde{E})R^{-1}:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is invertible real linear, thus

is a real ellipsoid in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. Moreover, for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
|z|=1
we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{L}_{z}E

\displaystyle=\widetilde{L}_{z}RM_{\mathbb{C},d}(\widetilde{E})R^{-1}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}=RL_{z}R^{-1}RM_{\mathbb{C},d}(\widetilde{E})R^{-1}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}

\displaystyle=RL_{z}M_{\mathbb{C},d}(\widetilde{E})R^{-1}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}=RM_{\mathbb{C},d}(\widetilde{E})L_{z}R^{-1}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}=RM_{\mathbb{C},d}(\widetilde{E})L_{z}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{d}}

\displaystyle=RM_{\mathbb{C},d}(\widetilde{E})\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{d}}=E,

where we used the fact that
L_{z}M_{\mathbb{C},d}(\widetilde{E})=M_{\mathbb{C},d}(\widetilde{E})L_{z}
due to the complex linearilty of

as well as the fact that the closed unit ball of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
remains invariant under multiplication with

.

In particular, the complex ellipsoids in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
form a closed subset of the real ellipsoids in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, for orthogonal maps induce isometries with respect to the Hausdorff metric.
∎

The previous exposition is related to
[
BC22
, Theorem 3.7]
.
The idea behind this result is the fact that
for any measurable convex body valued function
F,

the function
G(x)
defined as the John ellipsoid of the convex body
F(x)

is also measurable.
For the reader’s convenience, we include the precise statement
in the context of complex convex body valued functions.

Theorem 16

(Theorem 3.7 in
[
BC22
]
)
.

Suppose that
F\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
is measurable.
Then there exists a measurable matrix-valued mapping
W\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathrm{M}_{{d}}({\mathbb{C}})
such that

(i)

the columns of
W(x)
are mutually orthogonal,

(ii)

for every
x\in\Omega,
it holds that

W(x)\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{d}}\subseteq F(x)\subseteq\sqrt{d}W(x)\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{d}}.

The proof of this result is based on
[
BC22
, Lemma 3.8]
,

[
BC22
, Lemma 3.9]

and
[
BC22
, Lemma 3.10]
,
in a way analogous to that of the real convex body context.
Since this is one of the crucial points to get Theorem
A
,
we include the statements of these lemmata and their proofs.

Lemma 17

(Lemma 3.8 in
[
BC22
]
)
.

Given a measurable convex body valued function
F\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
such that
F(x)
has nonempty interior for every
x\in\Omega,

there exists a measurable convex body valued function
G\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
such that
G(x)
is an ellipsoid with nonempty interior and with

G(x)\subseteq F(x)\subseteq\sqrt{d}G(x)

(A.2)

for every
x\in\Omega.

Proof.

We follow the same approach as in
[
BC22
]
.
For every
x\in\Omega,
define
G(x)
as the unique John ellipsoid of
F(x),

which has nonempty interior.
This already satisfies (
A.2
).
It is only left to prove the measurability of
G.

To this end, consider a dense sequence
P_{1},P_{2},\ldots
of invertible matrices
in
\mathrm{M}_{{d}}({\mathbb{C}}).

In particular, we have that for any ellipsoid
E
with nonempty interior,
we can express
E=P\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{d}}}
with
P\in\mathrm{M}_{{d}}({\mathbb{C}})
and
for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
n\geq 1
such that

P_{n}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{d}}}\subseteq E\subseteq(1+\varepsilon)P_{n}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{d}}}.

We construct by induction a sequence of measurable convex body valued functions

G_{n}\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
such that for every
n\geq 1,

G_{n}(x)
is either an ellipsoid with nonempty interior or
G_{n}(x)=\{0\}
for every
x\in\Omega.

First, define

G_{1}(x)=\begin{cases}P_{1}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{d}}}&\text{ if }P_{1}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{d}}}\subseteq F(x),\\
\{0\}&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

The values of
G_{1}
are either an ellipsoid with nonempty interior or
\{0\}
by construction,
while it is easy to see that it is also measurable since the set

\{x\in\Omega\colon P_{1}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{d}}}\nsubseteq F(x)\}=\{x\in\Omega\colon F(x)\cap P_{1}(\mathbb{C}^{d}\setminus\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{d}}})\neq\emptyset\}

is measurable due to
F
being measurable.
Assume now that we have defined measurable functions
G_{1},\ldots,G_{n}
and define

G_{n+1}(x)=\begin{cases}P_{n+1}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{d}}}&\text{ if }P_{n+1}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{d}}}\subseteq F(x)\text{ and }m_{d}(P_{n+1}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{d}}})>m_{d}(G_{n}(x)),\\
G_{n}(x)&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Again, by construction
G_{n+1}(x)
is either an ellipsoid with nonempty interior
or just the trivial convex body
\{0\}.

We need to see that
G_{n+1}
is also measurable.
To do so, remember that the volume functional
K\mapsto m_{d}(K)

is a continuous mapping from
\mathcal{K}_{b}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
to
[0,\infty),

since this is the same as the volume functional
K\mapsto m_{2d}(K)

from
\mathcal{K}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
to
[0,\infty).

This and the measurability of
G_{n}
implies that
m_{d}(G_{n}(x))\colon\Omega\rightarrow[0,\infty)

is also a measurable mapping (see
[
BC22
, Theorem 3.5]
).
Now, for any given open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d},
we have that

\displaystyle\{x

\displaystyle\in\Omega\colon G_{n+1}(x)\cap U\neq\emptyset\}

\displaystyle=(\{x\in\Omega\colon m_{d}(G_{n}(x))\geq m_{d}(P_{n+1}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{d}}})\}

\displaystyle\cap\{x\in\Omega\colon G_{n}(x)\cap U\neq\emptyset\})

\displaystyle\cup(\{x\in\Omega\colon m_{d}(G_{n}(x))<m_{d}(P_{n+1}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{d}}})\}

\displaystyle\cap\{x\in\Omega\colon P_{n+1}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{d}}}\subseteq F(x)\text{ and }P_{n+1}\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{d}}}\cap U\neq\emptyset\}).

By the previous considerations, each of the sets appearing on the right-hand side is measurable,
so
G_{n+1}
is measurable as well.

The last step of the proof is to show that the sequence
G_{n}(x)
converges
to
G(x)
in the Hausdorff metric.
This will imply that
G
is measurable with respect to the Hausdorff topology,
which is equivalent to the definition that we have used of measurability of
convex body valued functions (see
[
BC22
, Theorem 3.5]
).
Assume this is not the case for a given
x\in\Omega.

By our construction, the density of the sequence
P_{1},P_{2},\ldots

and the maximality of the John ellipsoid
G(x),

we have that
m_{d}(G_{n})\to m_{d}(G(x))
as
n\to\infty.

The Blaschke selection theorem
[
Sch93
, Theorem 1.8.7]

asserts that any bounded sequence of convex bodies has a subsequence that converges to a convex body,
so we have a subsequence
G_{n_{k}}(x)
converging to an ellipsoid
E^{\prime}

with
m_{d}(E^{\prime})=m_{d}(G(x))
but with
E^{\prime}\neq G(x),

which contradicts the fact that the John ellipsoid is unique.
Note that
E^{\prime}
is a complex ellipsoid, since real ellipsoids are closed
in the Hausdorff topology
and complex ellipsoids are a closed subset of the real ellipsoids.
Thus
G_{n}(x)\to G(x)
and the last function is also measurable.
∎

]
for this result. An extension to the complex setting is performed in detail in
[

. Combining this with the complex version of
[

, we immediately deduce the complex version of
[

, since the composition of measurable maps remains measurable.

The statement and proof of

for complex convex bodies
correspond to Lemma

The proof included in Section

Finally,
[
BC22
, Lemma 3.10]
relates measurable complex ellipsoid valued mappings
G

to measurable matrix valued mappings with mutually orthogonal columns.

Lemma 19

(Lemma 3.10 in
[
BC22
]
)
.

Consider a measurable mapping
G\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})

such that
G(x)
is a complex ellipsoid for every
x\in\Omega

(possibly with empty interior).
Then there exists a measurable mapping
W\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathrm{M}_{{d}}({\mathbb{C}})
such that

(i)

the columns of the matrix
W(x)
are mutually orthogonal,

(ii)

it holds that
G(x)=W(x)\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{d}}}
for every
x\in\Omega.

Proof.

The proof consists in constructing the columns
v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}
of the matrix
W(x)

as measurable mappings
\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d}
and being mutually orthogonal
(with some of them possibly null at a given
x\in\Omega
).
Given our measurable mapping
G,
take the measurable mapping

v_{1}\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that
|G(x)|=|v_{1}(x)|
at every
x\in\Omega

given by Lemma
4
.
Then define the mappings
J_{1}\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}^{d})

and
J_{1}^{\perp}\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
given by

J_{1}(x)=\mathrm{span}\{v_{1}(x)\}
and
J_{1}^{\perp}(x)=(J_{1}(x))^{\perp},
respectively.
By considering the field of Gaussian rationals
\mathbb{Q}(i)=\{p+iq\colon p,q\in\mathbb{Q}\},

which is a dense set of
\mathbb{C},

we get the sequence of selection functions
\{\lambda v_{1}(x)\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}(i)}
for
J_{1},

yielding immediately that the latter is measurable.
A standard argument using the characterisations of measurability of closed set valued functions
(see
[
AF09
, Theorem 8.1.4]
)
shows that
J_{1}^{\perp}
is also measurable.

Assume now that
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
have already been defined for some
1\leq k<d.

As before, define the mappings

J_{k}(x)=\mathrm{span}\{v_{1}(x),\ldots,v_{k}(x)\},\qquad J_{k}^{\perp}(x)=(J_{k}(x))^{\perp},

which are again measurable by the same standard arguments.
Next, define the measurable mapping
G_{k}\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})

taking values on the set of complex ellipsoids given by

G_{k}(x)=G(x)\cap J_{k}^{\perp}(x)

(it might happen that
G_{k}(x)
is the trivial ellipsoid).
Applying Lemma
4
, one gets a new measurable vector valued function

v_{k+1}
such that it is orthogonal to
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}.

The vectors
v_{1}(x),\ldots,v_{d}(x)
constructed in this way are the semiaxes of an ellipsoid,
each one taken to be maximal in
G(x)
at each step and given in decreasing order.
Thus, these vectors are precisely the semiaxes of
G(x)
and,
if we define the measurable matrix valued function
W(x)
whose columns are the previous vectors,
we get the desired equality
G(x)=W(x)\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{d}}}.

∎

The proof of
[
BC22
, Theorem 3.7]
follows by replacing
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\mathbb{C}^{d}.

Also, the real inner product and associated notions are replaced by the Hermitian product and respective notions.
We include the details for completeness.

Proof of Theorem 3.7 in
[
BC22
]
.

Note that if
F(x)
is a convex body in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with nonempty interior for every
x\in\Omega,

then Lemmata
17
and
19

yield the result.
If that is not the case, one needs to divide
\Omega
into sets
\Omega_{0},\ldots,\Omega_{d}

such that
F(x)
is contained in a linear subspace of dimension
k
for every
x\in\Omega_{k}.

Given the measurable mapping
F\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}(\mathbb{C}^{d}),

define
J\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
by

J(x)=\mathrm{span}F(x)=\overline{\mathrm{conv}}\left(\bigcup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}(i)}\lambda F(x)\right),

which is measurable by a standard argument using the density of the Gaussian rationals in
\mathbb{C}.

Also, the mapping
P\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathrm{M}_{{d}}({\mathbb{C}})
with
P(x)
being
the orthogonal projection from
\mathbb{C}^{d}
to
J(x)
at every
x\in\Omega

is measurable as well (see
[
BC22
, Theorem 3.6]
,
which also holds in the complex setting by the previous discussion).
Then, the sets

\Omega_{k}=\{x\in\Omega\colon\mathrm{rank}(P(x))=k\},\qquad 0\leq k\leq d,

are also measurable since the rank can be computed taking the determinants of minors.
Now one only needs to construct the restrictions
W|_{\Omega_{k}}
satisfying the conclusions of the theorem
to get the desired function.

Let us fix
0\leq k\leq d.

One can find measurable functions
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\colon\Omega_{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d}

such that
v_{1}(x),\ldots,v_{k}(x)
are an orthonormal basis of
J(x)
for every
x\in\Omega_{k}

(see
[
Hel86
, Theorem 2 in Section 1.3]
,
which applies to complex Hilbert spaces).
Denote the set of
s\times t
-matrices by
\mathrm{M}_{{s\times t}}({\mathbb{C}}).

The matrix
M_{k}(x)\in\mathrm{M}_{{d\times k}}({\mathbb{C}})
whose columns are
v_{1}(x),\ldots,v_{k}(x)

is an isometry of
\mathbb{C}^{k}
onto
J(x)
for every
x\in\Omega_{k}

with inverse given by
M_{k}^{\ast}(x)=(M_{k}(x))^{\ast}
its conjugate transpose.
Thus, we can consider the measurable function
F_{k}\colon\Omega_{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bcs}}(\mathbb{C}^{k})
given by
F_{k}(x)=M_{k}^{\ast}(x)F(x),

which satisfies that
F_{k}(x)
is a convex body with nonempty interior in
\mathbb{C}^{k}.

Hence, Lemmata
17
and
19

yield a matrix valued function
W_{k}\colon\Omega_{k}\rightarrow\mathrm{M}_{{k}}({\mathbb{C}})

such that its columns are mutually orthogonal and with

W_{k}(x)\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{k}}}\subseteq F_{k}(x)\subseteq\sqrt{d}W_{k}(x)\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{k}}}

for every
x\in\Omega_{k}.

The actual mapping
W
restricted to
\Omega_{k}
is obtained as follows.
Take
P_{k}
the coordinate projection from
\mathbb{C}^{d}
to
\mathbb{C}^{k},

so
P_{k}\in\mathrm{M}_{{d}}({\mathbb{C}}).

Then define
W(x)=M_{k}(x)W_{k}(x)P_{k}(x)
for
x\in\Omega_{k}.

This mapping is measurable and its columns are mutually orthogonal by construction.
It is easy to check that it also satisfies the desired inclusions when applied to
\mathbf{\overline{B}}_{\mathbb{C}^{{d}}}.

∎

We now turn our attention to the second part of
[
BC22
, Section 3]
, which concerns integrals of convex-set valued maps. The definitions of the Aumann integral and integrable bounded functions there are valid with
\mathbb{R}^{d}
replaced by
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and thus also by
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

Lemma 3.13 in
[
BC22
]
yields,
for a given measurable real vector valued function
f,

a measurable real convex body valued function
F
with
|F(x)|=|f(x)|
at every
x\in\Omega.

For the complex version, we consider a measurable complex vector valued function
f

and we construct the corresponding measurable complex convex body valued function
F

with
|F(x)|=|f(x)|
at every point in the domain.
This is achieved performing one major change that is a recurring theme in the passage from the real to the complex case. Namely, given
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}^{d})
, one defines the convex-set valued map
F
by

F(x):=\{zf(x):\penalty\ z\in\mathbb{C}\text{ with }|z|\leq 1\},\quad x\in\Omega.

This map is measurable just as in the real case in
[
BC22
, Lemma 3.13]
because also the closed unit disk in the complex plane has a dense countable subset.

The validity of
[
BC22
, Theorem 3.14]
,
which states that the Aumann integral of a closed set valued function
with respect to a nonatomic measure is a convex (not necessarily closed) set,
is obvious in the complex case, because this theorem concerns only the topological and real vector space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, remaining valid for
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and thus also
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

To obtain a complex version of
[
BC22
, Theorem 3.15]
,
which states that the closure of the Aumann integral of a convex body valued function
is also a convex body,
we need once again to directly replace
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. The notion of real symmetric sets is accordingly replaced by the notion of complex symmetric sets. The Dunford–Pettis theorem and Mazur’s lemma hold equally well with the complex numbers as the underlying field of scalars.

The statement and proof of
[
BC22
, Theorem 3.16]
remain true verbatim in the complex case with
\mathbb{R}^{d}
replaced by
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, because as mentioned Mazur’s lemma remains true in complex Banach spaces.

It is obvious that
[
BC22
, Theorem 3.17, Corollary 3.18]
,
which deal with linearity and monotonicity of Aumann integrals of convex body valued functions,
and their proofs remain both true verbatim in the complex case with
\mathbb{R}^{d}
replaced by
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

Observe again that the definition of the Aumann integral makes use only of the topological space and real vector space structures of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Note also that complex symmetric sets are in particular real symmetric sets. Since
[
BC22
, Lemma 3.19]
,
which states that the Aumann integral of a convex body valued function
F
equals
\{0\}
only if
F(x)=0
almost everywhere,
holds equally well for
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, we deduce that it remains true also for
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

Finally, in the complex versions of
[
BC22
, Proposition 3.20, Proposition 3.21]
,
which are respectively analogues of Hölder’s inequality and Minkowski’s inequality
for Aumann integrals of convex body valued functions,
one replaces
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and then considers complex-symmetric sets instead of real-symmetric ones and complex homogeneous norms on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
instead of real homogeneous norms on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The statements and proofs remain in both cases otherwise completely unchanged, because complex symmetric sets are in particular real symmetric sets, complex homogeneous norms are in particular real homogeneous, and as remarked above,
[
BC22
, Theorem 3.17]
extends obviously to the complex case.

A.3
Seminorm functions

In this last section, we cover the adaptation of
[
BC22
, Section 4]
to the complex setting.
As mentioned in Subsection
A.1
,
the definition of seminorm functions itself only requires
substituting the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\mathbb{C}^{d}

and real homogeneity by complex homogeneity.

For the statement and proof of
[
BC22
, Theorem 4.2]
,
that gives a one-to-one correspondence between seminorm functions
and convex body valued functions,
one only needs the obvious modifications.
This is, one needs to substitute
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\mathbb{C}^{d},

consider the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}
on
\mathbb{C}^{d},

use countable dense sets of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
such as
\mathbb{Q}(i)^{d}

and use the complex version of Hahn-Banach Theorem.

Regarding
[
BC22
, Lemma 4.4]
,
which gives that every measurable convex body valued mapping is
the pointwise limit of simple measurable mappings (in the Hausdorff topology),
both its statement and its proof follow verbatim under the substitution
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\mathbb{C}^{d}.

Note that the characterization of the convergence of convex bodies used in
[
BC22
]

can be used in the same way in our context
because it actually applies to general nonempty compact convex sets (see
[
Sch93
, Theorem 1.8.7]
).

To get
[
BC22
, Theorem 4.5]
,
which characterises integrably boundedness of a measurable convex body valued function
in terms of the corresponding seminorm function,
one needs to follow the same argument
replacing
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and keeping in mind that
we have changed the definition of function
h_{F(x)}(v)
to

h_{F(x)}(v)\coloneq\sup_{w\in F(x)}|\langle v,w\rangle|

Next, all results about
\mathrm{L}^{p}
spaces of convex-set valued functions
follow with the replacement of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\mathbb{C}^{d},

since these are based on the topology given by the Hausdorff metric.

Finally, all considerations about matrix weights and seminorms also hold.
Here, in addition to the substitution of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\mathbb{C}^{d},

it is also necessary to replace orthogonal matrices by unitary ones and
symmetric matrices by those that are hermitian.

A.4
Main results including the Extrapolation Theorem with matrix weights

In the previous subsections we have explained how the theory developed to deal with real valued matrix weights
in
[
BC22
, Sections 2–4]

can be modified to be applied to complex valued matrix weights.
Once these tools have been conveniently adapted,
they can be used to get the complex version of the main results in that article
without further changes.
In other words, the exposition of
[
BC22
, Sections 5–9]

holds for the complex setting by using the results explained in the current appendix.
In particular, Theorem
A
holds.
