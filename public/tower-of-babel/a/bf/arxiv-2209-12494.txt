Frequency Distribution of Prime Numbers between an Integer and its Square: A Case Study
Tashreef Muhammad, G. M. Shahariar, Tahsin Aziz, Mohammad Shafiul Alam

Abstract
The chronicle of prime numbers travel back thousands of years in human history. Not only the traits of prime numbers have surprised people, but also all those endeavors made for ages to find a pattern in the appearance of prime numbers has been captivating them. Until recently, it was firmly believed that prime numbers do not maintain any pattern of occurrence among themselves. This statement is conferred not to be completely true. This paper is also an attempt to discover a pattern in the occurrence of prime numbers. This work intends to introduce some mathematical well-known equations that point to the existence of a simplistic pattern in the number of primes within the range of a number and its square. We assume that the rigorous evaluation of the perceived pattern may benefit in many aspects such as applications of encryption, algorithms concerning prime numbers, and many more.

4.1.3
Second Degree Polynomial

Second Degree polynomials can be expressed using the following general equation:

y=ax^{2}+bx+c

(5)

where
a
,
b
and
c
are constants and experimental value found for these constants are:

a=0.0376

A conditional equation is introduced to lessen the amount of error in the range
[x,x^{2}]

y=\begin{cases}x&,\text{if }x>ax^{2}+bx+c\\
ax^{2}+bx+c&,\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

4.1.4
Conics

Conics are special kind of second degree curves. The conics equation is derived from the general equation of second degree curve. The general equation of a second degree curve can be written as:

Ax^{2}+Bxy+Cy^{2}+Dx+Ey+F=0

(7)

Equation
7
can be simplified to find value of
y
as below:

y=\frac{-(Bx+E)\pm\sqrt{(Bx+E)^{2}-4C(Ax^{2}+Dx+F)}}{2C}

(8)

However, the experiment indicates that the expected values can be obtained using only "-" in the equation. Also, the count of primes in the range
[x,x^{2}]
is neither negative nor lower than
x
. Hence the equation can be modified as:

y=\begin{cases}x&,\text{if }x>\frac{-(Bx+E)-\sqrt{(Bx+E)^{2}-4C(Ax^{2}+Dx+F)}}{2C}\\
\frac{-(Bx+E)-\sqrt{(Bx+E)^{2}-4C(Ax^{2}+Dx+F)}}{2C}&,\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(9)

Here
A
,
B
,
C
,
D
,
E
and
F
are constants. The suitable values found by analyzing the data for the constants are:

A=3.11199927582249\times 10^{-09}

B=-9.33817244194697\times 10^{-15}

C=3.45730472758733\times 10^{-21}

D=0.0000515593800268165

E=-7.63287093993319\times 10^{-08}

F=-1

From the values of
A
,
B
,
C
,
D
,
E
and
F
we can say that Equation
9
is an
Ellipse
if the modification for negative value is removed.

4.1.5
Custom Ratio Equation

After plotting, the curve of Figure
2
looked like a logarithmic curve. So, we can write:

\frac{x^{2}-x}{y}=\kappa

(10)

Here,
\kappa
denotes a natural logarithmic equation used for fitting the Figure
2
. After several experiments the best value for
\kappa
was estimated using the following equation:

\kappa\approx 2.0038ln(x)-1.0932

(11)

From Equation
10
and Equation
11
we can write:

\frac{x^{2}-x}{y}\approx 2.0038ln(x)-1.0932

(12)

Considering the approximation as equal for the time being, Equation
12
can be modified to find the value of
y
as below:

y=\frac{x^{2}-x}{2.0038ln(x)-1.0932}

(13)

It is impracticable to ignore the error generated by considering the approximated value as equal value. That is why the value of
y
is not a precise match but very close in most cases.

4.1.6
Derivation of Bertrand’s Postulate

The following equation is derived from Bertrand’s Postulate to find the lower limit of number of primes in the range
[x,x^{2}]

[

]

\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}x^{2}=y

(14)

The derived equation claims to find the lower limit always. For evaluation purposes, it is considered that the equation predicts the actual count.
