Recursive construction of biorthogonal polynomials for handling polynomial regression
Laura Rebollo-Neira, Jason Laurie

Abstract
An adaptive procedure for constructing polynomials which are biorthogonal to the basis of monomials in the same finite-dimensional inner product space is proposed. By taking advantage of available orthogonal polynomials, the proposed methodology reduces the well-known instability problem arising from the matrix inversion involved in classical polynomial regression. The recurrent generation of the biorthogonal basis facilitates the upgrading of all its members to include an additional one. Moreover, it allows for a natural downgrading of the basis. This convenient feature leads to a straightforward approach for reducing the number of terms in the polynomial regression approximation. The merit of this approach is illustrated through a series of examples where the resulting biorthogonal basis is derived from Legendre, Laguerre, and Chebyshev orthogonal polynomials.



I
Introduction

Polynomial regression is becoming increasingly important for predictive modelling
Ost12
;
EO20
;
AVV22
, data analysis
MMR17
, signal and images processing
Im
, machine learning and neuron networks
SS22
;
SHL23
;
MBD22
. Thus, a renewed interest in efficient and effective techniques suitable for tackling common polynomial regression problems has arisen. Fundamentally, polynomial regression problems concern the modelling of a process represented by a function
f
by a polynomial
f_{k}
of degree
k
, i.e.,

\displaystyle f_{k}(x)=c_{0}+c_{1}x+\cdots+c_{k}x^{k},\quad\text{for $x\in{\cal{I}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}$},

and finding the coefficients
c_{n}
for
n=0,\dots,k
by minimising the
L^{2}
-norm of the approximation error
\|f-f_{k}\|_{2}
. Many applications using polynomial regression, e.g. such as the method of moments used in Ref.
MMR17
, approach the problem through the traditional method of solving the least squares equation via the normal equations. However, such an approach requires the inversion of a Gram matrix of Hankel-type which are notorious for being very badly conditioned
ZT01
, and can only provide a reliable solution for low-order polynomial approximations. This is unfortunate, because the Weierstrass approximation theorem states that every continuous function defined on a compact interval can be uniformly approximated by a polynomial up to any desired precision, even if the degree of the polynomial may be high. Moreover, because monomials on a compact interval are among the simplest functions to evaluate, integrate, and differentiate, polynomial approximations have enormous practical relevance in terms of both analytical and numerical computations of integrals and derivatives. This article introduces an adaptive biorthogonalisation technique which, by leveraging the orthogonality of classic orthogonal polynomials (OP), avoids the need for a matrix inversion and provides the coefficients of the polynomial approximation by simple computation of inner products. Additional advantages stem from the recursive nature of the approach that provides an efficient strategy for reducing the number of terms of the polynomial approximation i.e., it is convenient to iteratively increase or decrease the number of terms in the polynomial approximation to adjust the residual error.

The general theory of biorthogonal polynomials sequences (BPS) is not as extensively developed as the theory of orthogonal polynomials sequences (OPS). The initial research on the topic was conducted by Szegiö
Sze82
, Iserles and Nørsett
IN88
;
IN89
;
IN95
, and Brezinski
Bre92
. Since then, there have been few applications or further studies on BPS. One of the reasons for the extensive use of OP is the convenience of the orthogonality property for their derivation and applications. Another is the stability of the representation, which follows from the orthogonality property. However, when pursuing the construction of
K
-term polynomial approximations, releasing the condition of orthogonality may render approximations of equal or better quality involving fewer terms. We believe that the limited current applicability of BPS might be partly due to the absence of convenient methodologies for their practical construction. Therefore, our objective is to propose new methodologies for building BPS and enhance the comprehension that may expand the range of potential applications across diverse domains.

Since several definitions of biorthogonal polynomials (BP) exist, let us specify the definition we refer to here: Two polynomial bases
\{p_{n}\}_{n=0}^{k}
and
\{q_{n}\}_{n=0}^{k}
are biorthogonal on an interval
{\cal{I}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
if the inner product

\displaystyle\left\langle p_{n},q_{m}\right\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{I}}\,p_{n}(x)\,q_{m}(x)\,d\mu=\delta_{m,n},

where
d\mu=w(x)\,dx
is a measure with
w(x)
an acceptable weight function, and
\delta_{m,n}
is the Kronecker delta-function. More specifically, we refer to
classic-like biorthogonal polynomials
to be a set of polynomials
\left\{\beta_{n}^{k}\right\}_{n=0}^{k}
biorthogonal to the set of monomials
\{x^{n}\}_{n=0}^{k}
, with the additional property that
{\mbox{\rm{Span}}}\left(\{x^{n}\}_{n=0}^{k}\right)={\mbox{\rm{Span}}}\left(\{\beta_{n}^{k}\}_{n=0}^{k}\right)
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Here, the superscript of
\beta_{n}^{k}
indicates that all the elements
n=0,\dots,k
must be re-calculated if the number
k
of elements in the basis changes. This is a major difference with OP for if the degree
k
is increased, say to
k+1
, only a single new independent element needs to be added to the original orthogonal set, without modification of the previously calculated elements.

While recursive equations for generating biorthogonal functions were proposed in Refs.
RNL02
;
LRN02
;
LRN07
, the general nature of that framework misses the main advantage of dealing with the particular case of monomials, namely, the wealth of available results concerning the construction of OP. The central contribution of this work is to produce an adaptive methodology for constructing classic-like BP giving rise to representations of orthogonal projections for the subspace spanned by the monomials. In particular, we illustrate the method by deriving BP based on orthogonal bases built from Legendre, Laguerre, and Chebyshev polynomials.

The paper is organised as follows: Sec.
II
provides the necessary definitions and background material for the construction of classic-like BP. Sec.
III
proves the biorthogonal property of the proposed construction as well as their convenient adaption for upgrading and downgrading orthogonal projections. Examples involving biorthogonality with respect to particular weight functions and domains are also produced in this section. The conclusions and final remarks are presented in Sec.
IV
.

II
Preliminary Considerations

In this section we introduce the necessary background for a self-contained derivation of the proposed construction of BP. Since our purpose is to develop a methodology suitable for practical applications, we consider only biorthogonal representations of orthogonal projections, and therefore, throughout the paper we restrict ourselves to consider only
finite
-dimensional inner product spaces, which we denote as
V({\cal{I}},w)
to indicate that the inner product
\left\langle f,g\right\rangle
between two real functions in
V({\cal{I}},w)
is defined as

\displaystyle\left\langle f,g\right\rangle=\int_{{\cal{I}}}f(x)\,g(x)\,w(x)\,dx,

where
w(x)
is a weight function which satisfies
w(x)\geq 0
, for all
x\in{\cal{I}}
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the subspace
V_{k}({\cal{I}},w)\subseteq V({\cal{I}},w)
is specifically defined as the vector space spanned by the set of monomials
\{x^{n}\}_{n=0}^{k}
, i.e.

\displaystyle V_{k}={\mbox{\rm{Span}}}\left(\{x^{0},x,\ldots,x^{k}\}\right),\quad\text{for $x\in{\cal{I}}$}.

Type A and B orthonormal polynomial sequences

We introduce two definitions for the construction of orthonormal polynomial sequences (OPS). These two types, type A and type B, are general forms based on formulas used to construct sequences of orthogonal polynomials. Before, we define the two types, let us highlight a clarification of our notation. Unless otherwise specified, throughout the paper, superscripts are used as labels. The only exception is for the variable
x
where the superscript indicates the variable
x
raised to a power
i\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, i.e.,
x^{i}
.

Definition 1

(Type A Orthonormal Polynomial Sequence)
.

A type A orthonormal polynomial sequence is a sequence of functions generated by

\displaystyle p_{n}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}^{n}x^{i},\quad\text{with $a_{n}^{n}\neq 0$,}

(1)

such that
\left\langle p_{n},p_{m}\right\rangle=\delta_{n,m}
.

For the convenience of some particular cases we differentiate the above definition with a second type, type B of OPS.

Definition 2

(Type B Orthonormal Polynomial Sequence)
.

A type B orthonormal polynomial sequence is a sequence of functions generated by

\displaystyle p_{n}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}a_{i}^{n}x^{n-2i},\quad\text{with $a_{0}^{n}\neq 0$},

(2)

that satisfy
\left\langle p_{n},p_{m}\right\rangle=\delta_{n,m}
. Here
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
indicates the integer part of
n/2
.

The polynomials
\left\{p_{n}(x)\right\}_{n=0}^{k}
of Type A or B, form an orthonormal set that span the same subspace as the set of monomials
\{x^{n}\}_{n=0}^{k}
with respect to the inner product in
V({\cal{I}},w)
so that

\displaystyle V_{k}={\mbox{\rm{Span}}}\left(\{x^{n}\}_{n=0}^{k}\right)={\mbox{\rm{Span}}}\left(\{p_{n}\}_{n=0}^{k}\right)\subseteq V({\cal{I}},w).

Property 1
.

If
n\leq m
, then an OPS satisfies

\displaystyle\left\langle x^{n},p_{m}\right\rangle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{n,m}}{a_{m}^{m}}&\text{if $p_{m}$ is Type A,}\\
\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{n,m}}{a_{0}^{m}}&\text{if $p_{m}$ is Type B}.\end{cases}

(3)

Proof.

Since
x^{n}\in{\mbox{\rm{Span}}}\left(\{p_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}\right)
it can be expressed in terms of the first
n
orthonormal polynomials as

\displaystyle x^{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}d_{i}^{n}p_{i}(x),

(4)

where
d_{i}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}
for
i=0,\dots,n
. Hence, for any
m
satisfying
n<m
we have that
\left\langle x^{n},p_{m}\right\rangle=0
for both Type A and Type B OPS. For
n=m
, if
p_{m}
is of Type A, using Definition (
1
), we can express

\displaystyle a_{m}x^{m}=p_{m}-\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}a_{i}^{m}x^{i}

(5)

so that, from the previous case, it follows that
\left\langle x^{m},p_{m}\right\rangle=1/{a_{m}^{m}}
.

Similarly, if
p_{m}
is of Type B, with
n=m
, then using Definition (
2
) we have

\displaystyle a_{0}^{m}x^{m}=p_{m}-\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}a_{i}^{m}x^{m-2i},

(6)

so then from Eq. (
4
), we have
\left\langle x^{m},p_{m}\right\rangle=1/{a_{0}^{m}}
. This proves Property 1.
∎

Orthogonal Projections

In what follows, it is important to recall some properties of subspaces that allows us to use results regarding orthogonal projections.

Definition 3

(Direct sum of subspaces)
.

A vector space
V
is said to be the direct sum of two subspaces, say
V_{k}
and
U
, written as

\displaystyle V=V_{k}\oplus U

if each
f\in V
has a unique representation

\displaystyle f=f_{k}+g,\quad\text{where $f_{k}\in V_{k}$ and $g\in U$}.

If
V
is an inner product space
V({\cal{I}},w)
a case of much interest concerns complementary subspaces which are orthogonal.

Definition 4

(Orthogonal complement subspace)
.

A vector space
U
is said to be the orthogonal complement of
V_{k}
in
V({\cal{I}},w)
if

\displaystyle U=V_{k}^{\perp}=\left\{g\in V\ :\ \left\langle g,f_{k}\right\rangle=0,\forall f_{k}\in V_{k}\right\}.

(7)

Definition 5

(Orthogonal projector)
.

Let two vectors spaces
V_{k}
and
V_{k}^{\perp}
be orthogonal complements of each other. Then, given any
f\in V
, the map
\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}:V\to V_{k}
defined as

\displaystyle\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}f=f_{k}\in V_{k},

is called the orthogonal projection (or orthogonal projector) of
V
onto
V_{k}
if it has the following properties:

(i)

\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}
is idempotent, i.e.,
\hat{\operatorname{P}}^{2}_{V_{k}}=\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}
.

(ii)

If
V=V_{k}\oplus V_{k}^{\perp}
then
\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}f_{k}=f_{k}
if
f_{k}\in V_{k}
and
\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}g=0
if
g\in V_{k}^{\perp}.

As a consequence of the property of Definition
5
(ii) we can express
V_{k}^{\perp}=\hat{\operatorname{I}}_{V}V-\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}V=(\hat{\operatorname{I}}_{V}-\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}})V=\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}^{\perp}}V
, where
\hat{\operatorname{I}}_{V}
is the identity operator in
V
. Subsequently, it follows that every
f\in V
can be decomposed in terms of two contributions
f=\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}f+\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}^{\perp}}f
.

Property 2

(Self-adjoint operator)
.

It readily follows that
\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\langle f,\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}g\right\rangle=\left\langle\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}f,g\right\rangle.

An operator fulfilling the above property is said to be self-adjoint.

The next two theorems are well-known fundamental results concerning the optimal approximation in an inner product subspace, also known as the
least squares approximation
. For pedagogical reasons, we present the proofs tailored to our framework in Appendices A and B.

Theorem 1
.

Let
V({\cal{I}},w)
be an inner product space and
V_{k}
a finite-dimensional subspace of
V({\cal{I}},w)
. Then, the least squares approximation in
V_{k}
of any
f\in V
can be obtained by the projection of
f
onto the subspace
V_{k}
, i.e.,
\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}f
.

Proof.

The proof is presented in Appendix A.∎

The next theorem prescribes a method of constructing an orthogonal projector onto the finite-dimensional subspace
V_{k}={\mbox{\rm{Span}}}\left(\{x^{n}\}_{n=0}^{k}\right)
, with respect to the inner product defined on
V({\cal{I}},w)
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\{p_{n}\}_{n=0}^{k}
be an orthonormal basis for the inner product subspace
V_{k}({\cal{I}},w)
. Then the orthogonal projector operator
\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}:V\to V_{k}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}p_{i}\left\langle\cdot,p_{i}\right\rangle,

(8)

where
\left\langle\cdot,p_{i}\right\rangle
performs an inner product with the basis polynomial
p_{i}
, i.e., for any
f\in V

\displaystyle\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}f=\sum_{i=0}^{k}p_{i}\left\langle f,p_{i}\right\rangle.

Proof.

The proof is presented in Appendix B. ∎

III
Biorthogonal representation of orthogonal projectors

The next theorem establishes two distinctive features of the proposed BPS. (i) Their construction readily follows from known OPS of either Type A or B. (ii) The BPS provide us with the biorthogonal representation for the orthogonal projection operator
\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\{p_{n}\}_{n=0}^{k}
be a finite OPS. Then the orthogonal projector operator
\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}
can be represented as

\displaystyle\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}x^{i}\left\langle\cdot,\beta_{i}^{k}\right\rangle,

(9)

where the functions
\beta_{n}^{k}(x)
are defined as

(i)

\displaystyle\beta_{n}^{k}(x)=\sum_{j=n}^{k}a_{n}^{j}\ p_{j}(x),\,x\in{\cal{I}},

(10)

if
\{p_{n}\}_{n=0}^{k}
is an OPS of Type A as defined in Eq. (
1
), or

(ii)

\displaystyle\beta_{n}^{k}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor(k-n)/2\rfloor}a_{i}^{2i+n}\ p_{2i+n}(x),

(11)

if
\{p_{n}\}_{n=0}^{k}
is an OPS of Type B as defined in Eq. (
2
).

Proof.

For
n=k=0
we have
\beta_{0}^{0}=p_{0}
and
x^{0}=p_{0}
. Hence, operator
\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{0}}
as given by Eq. (
9
) for
k=0
is the orthogonal projection operator onto
V_{0}
. Assuming that
\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}
in Eq. (
9
) is the orthogonal projection operator onto
V_{k}
we will show that then
\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k+1}}
is the orthogonal projection operator onto
V_{k+1}
.

Case (i). Since
{\displaystyle{\beta_{n}^{k+1}=\beta_{n}^{k}+a_{n}^{k+1}p_{k+1}}}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{k+1}x^{n}\left\langle\cdot,\beta_{n}^{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{k}x^{n}\left\langle\cdot,\beta_{n}^{k+1}\right\rangle+x^{k+1}\left\langle\cdot,\beta_{k+1}^{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{k}x^{n}\left\langle\cdot,\beta_{n}^{k}\right\rangle+\sum_{n=0}^{k}x^{n}a_{n}^{k+1}\left\langle\cdot,p_{k+1}\right\rangle+x^{k+1}a_{k+1}^{k+1}\left\langle\cdot,p_{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}+\sum_{n=0}^{k+1}x^{n}a_{n}^{k+1}\left\langle\cdot,p_{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}+p_{k+1}\left\langle\cdot,p_{k+1}\right\rangle\quad{\text{using \eqref{eq:OPS}}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k+1}}.

Case (ii). From the definition of
\beta_{n}^{k}
given by Eq. (
11
) we have that

\displaystyle\beta_{n}^{k+1}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\beta_{n}^{k}+a^{k+1}_{(k-n+1)/2}\ p_{k+1}&\text{for $k-n+1$ even},\\
\beta_{n}^{k}&\text{for $k-n+1$ odd},\\
\beta_{k+1}^{k+1}=a^{k+1}_{0}\,p_{k+1}&\text{for $n=k+1$}.\end{cases}

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let us define two sets of numbers

\displaystyle{\cal{S}}_{e}

\displaystyle=\{n\leq k\ :\ k-n+1\ \text{even}\},\quad\text{and}\quad{\cal{S}}_{o}=\{n\leq k\ :\ k-n+1\ \text{odd}\}.

Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{k+1}x^{n}\left\langle\cdot,\beta_{n}^{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{k}x^{n}\left\langle\cdot,\beta_{n}^{k+1}\right\rangle+x^{k+1}\left\langle\cdot,\beta_{k+1}^{k+1}\right\rangle=\sum_{n\in{\cal{S}}_{o}}x^{n}\left\langle\cdot,\beta_{n}^{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\sum_{n\in{\cal{S}}_{e}}x^{n}\left\langle\cdot,\beta_{n}^{k+1}\right\rangle+x^{k+1}\left\langle\cdot,\beta_{k+1}^{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{k}x^{n}\left\langle\cdot,\beta_{n}^{k}\right\rangle+\sum_{n\in{\cal{S}}_{e}}x^{n}\left\langle\cdot,a_{(k-n+1)/2}^{k+1}p_{k+1}\right\rangle+x^{k+1}\left\langle\cdot,a_{0}^{k+1}p_{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}+\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}x^{k+1-2i}a_{i}^{k+1}\left\langle\cdot,p_{k+1}\right\rangle=\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}+p_{k+1}\left\langle\cdot,p_{k+1}\right\rangle=\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k+1}}.

∎

Remark III.1
.

It is worth noticing that Property

implies that

\displaystyle\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}x^{i}\left\langle\cdot,\beta_{i}^{k}\right\rangle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\beta_{i}^{k}\left\langle\cdot,x^{i}\right\rangle.

.

defined in (

The four cases to consider are

n=0,\dots,k
and
m=0,\dots,k
.

(ii)

n=k+1
and
m=0,\dots,k
.

(iii)

n=0,\dots,k
and
m=k+1
.

(iv)

and

so that from the assumption

and

Application to downgrading orthogonal projectors

Let us suppose that from knowing the orthogonal projection of
f

\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}f=f_{k}

\displaystyle{\cal{D}}_{k\backslash{\ell_{j}}}=\{x^{0},x^{1},\ldots,x^{\ell_{j}-1},x^{\ell_{j}+1},\ldots,x^{k}\}.

(13)

Remark III.2

Since the approximation error arising from removing the element

from the projection of

It follows that by removing the element

such that

\displaystyle\frac{\left|\left\langle f,\beta_{\ell_{j}}^{k}\right\rangle\right|^{2}}{\|\beta_{\ell_{j}}^{k}\|^{2}_{2}}\,\quad\text{is minimum}

the remaining downgraded approximation minimises the new approximation error norm.

Example 3: Sparse polynomial approximation

In this example we illustrate the construction of sparse polynomial approximations. Let us consider for instance a given

, where
{\cal{P}}_{19}
indicates the set of polynomial of degree at most
19

[-1,1]

(i)

A linear combination of Legendre polynomials

\displaystyle g(x)=d_{0}P_{0}+d_{1}P_{1}(x)+d_{2}P_{2}(x)+\cdots+d_{19}P_{19}(x),\quad x\in[-1,1],

-term approximations of

possibilities to be considered. Hence, for case (ii) we adopt an approximation

Remark III.3
.

It is worth commenting that the polynomial approximation of the functions (
27
) can not be realised by the standard way of solving the least squares problem. Indeed, the standard method would try to solve the normal equations:

\displaystyle\left\langle f,x^{j}\right\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{k}c_{n}^{k}\left\langle x^{n},x^{j}\right\rangle,\quad\text{for $j=0,\dots,k$},

which introduces the need to invert the Gram matrix of elements

\displaystyle G(n,j)=\left\langle x^{n},x^{j}\right\rangle=\frac{1-(-1)^{n+j+1}}{n+j+1},\quad\text{for $n=0,\dots,k$ and $j=0,\dots,k$}.

This is an Hankel-type matrix which is very badly conditioned even for small values of
k

ZT01
. In particular for
k=36
the determinant if close to zero and the condition number is
10^{18}
. Figs.
6
(c) and (d) show the functional approximation and absolute pointwise error when computing using the matrix inversion method respectively. Comparison to the error produced using our biorthogonal polynomials via Legendre polynomials is presented in Fig.
6

It is worth noticing that orthogonal polynomials can also be used as a pre-conditioner to decrease the condition number of the matrix to be inverted. Indeed, using Theorems 1 and 2 and letting
Q_{k},\,k=1,\ldots,36
be either Legendre or Chebyshev polynomials to represent the orthogonal projector, the least squares solution to the approximations by a polynomial of degree
36
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{36}}f(x)

\displaystyle=\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{36}}\sum_{n=0}^{36}c_{n}x^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{36}\sum_{n=0}^{36}c_{n}Q_{k}(x)\left\langle Q_{k},x^{n}\right\rangle,

or equivalently, after taking an inner product on both sides with
each
Q_{k}
,

\displaystyle\left\langle Q_{k},f\right\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{36}\left\langle Q_{k},x^{n}\right\rangle c_{n},\quad\text{for $k=0,\dots,36$}.

From (
3
) we gather that the new matrix to be inverted, of elements
\left\langle Q_{k},x^{n}\right\rangle
, is lower triangular. Its inverse is more stable than that of the Gram matrix and produces equivalent results to those achieved by the proposed biorthogonalisation.

IV
Conclusions

A recursive procedure for constructing dedicated sets of polynomials
\{\beta_{n}^{k}\}_{n=0}^{k},\,k\in\mathbb{N}
in inner product spaces
V({\cal{I}},w)
was proposed. Each set fulfils the following properties:

(i)

It is biorthogonal to the basis of monomials
\{x^{n}\}_{n=0}^{k}
in
V({\cal{I}},w)
.

(ii)

Span the same subspace
V_{k}
as
\{x^{n}\}_{n=0}^{k},\,k\in\mathbb{N}
.

These two properties amount to a representation of the orthogonal projection operation onto
V_{k}
, of an element
f\in V({\cal{I}},w)
, as given by

\displaystyle\hat{\operatorname{P}}_{V_{k}}f=\sum_{n=0}^{k}x^{n}\left\langle f,\beta_{n}^{k}\right\rangle.

Since the proposed construction is based on classic orthogonal polynomials there is no matrix inversion involved in the process. This feature is particularly convenient to decide the degree of a polynomial model by adjusting the residual error. Additionally, it is straightforward to realise a posterior reduction of the model by the removal of any monomial from the basis.

The procedure was illustrated by deriving biorthogonal polynomials based on Legendre, Laguerre and Chebyshev polynomials. The consideration of other orthogonal polynomials, such as Hermite polynomials, would readily follow.

The convenience of the proposal was highlighted by four numerical examples: (i) Polynomial regression modelling of noisy data. (ii) Derivation of analytic polynomial forms for the exponential decay function. (iii) Sparse polynomial approximation. (iv) The approximation of a continuous function which requires a polynomial of degree at least
k=36
to be well represented. Such an example, which cannot not be numerically realised by standard techniques involving the inversion of a Hankel-type matrix, gives an inside of the many applications that could benefit from the proposed construction of biorthogonal polynomials.

Proof.
