A useful inequality of inverse hyperbolic tangent
Kunal Marwaha

Abstract
We prove an inequality related to arctanh , resolving a conjecture of Gu and Polyanskiy [ GP23 ] .



In this note, we prove the following statement:

1

The heart of the proof relies on the following lemma:

8
.

Let
c,d\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that
0<c-d<\ln\frac{c}{d}
. Then
\ln\frac{(1-\lambda)+\lambda c}{(1-\lambda)+\lambda d}\leq\lambda\ln\frac{c}{d}
for all
\lambda\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

Let
h_{x}(\lambda)=\ln(1 + \lambda(x-1))-\lambda\ln x
. Suppose
0<c-d<\ln\frac{c}{d}
; our goal is to prove
h_{c}(\lambda)\leq h_{d}(\lambda)
for all
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Note that
h_{x}(0)=h_{x}(1)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, and
h_{1}(\lambda)=0
for all
\lambda\in[0,1]
.

When
x\neq 1
,
h^{\prime}_{x}(\lambda)=\frac{x-1}{1+\lambda(x-1)}-\ln x
. This has only one root at
\lambda^{*}_{x}=\frac{x-1-\ln x}{(x-1)\ln x}
.
Note that
h^{\prime}_{x}(0)=x-1-\ln x>0
for all
x\neq 1
; thus,
h_{x}(\lambda)
is increasing for
\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{x}^{*})
and decreasing for
\lambda\in(\lambda_{x}^{*},1]
.

We first consider when one of
c,d
equals
1
.
Suppose
c=1
; then
h_{d}(\lambda)\geq 0=h_{c}(\lambda)
for all
\lambda\in[0,1]
. When
d=1
, the premise
c-1<\ln c
is never satisfied.

Now consider any other
c,d\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that
0<c-d<\ln\frac{c}{d}
. Here,

\displaystyle h^{\prime}_{c}(\lambda)-h^{\prime}_{d}(\lambda)=\frac{\frac{1}{d-1}-\frac{1}{c-1}}{(\frac{1}{c-1}+\lambda)(\frac{1}{d-1}+\lambda)}-\ln\frac{c}{d}=\frac{c-d}{\left((c-1)\lambda+1\right)\left((d-1)\lambda+1\right)}-\ln\frac{c}{d}\,.

(5)

The roots of
h^{\prime}_{c}(\lambda)-h^{\prime}_{d}(\lambda)
are the roots of the quadratic
(c-1)(d-1)\lambda^{2}+(c+d-2)\lambda+1-\frac{c-d}{\ln c-\ln d}=0
.
Let the roots be
r_{1},r_{2}
such that
r_{1}\leq r_{2}
; then
r_{1}r_{2}=\frac{1}{(c-1)(d-1)}(1-\frac{c-d}{\ln c-\ln d})
. The extremum of the quadratic is at
\lambda^{\prime}=\frac{2-c-d}{2(c-1)(d-1)}=\frac{1}{2-2c}+\frac{1}{2-2d}
; note that
r_{1}\leq\lambda^{\prime}\leq r_{2}
.

We observe that at most one of
\{r_{1},r_{2}\}
is in
[0,1]
. When
c>1
and
d<1
,
r_{1}r_{2}<0
. Otherwise,
\lambda^{\prime}\notin[0,1]
: when
c,d<1
,
\lambda^{\prime}>1
, and when
c,d>1
,
\lambda^{\prime}<0
. So
h^{\prime}_{c}(\lambda)-h^{\prime}_{d}(\lambda)
has at most one root for
\lambda\in[0,1]
.

Note that
h^{\prime}_{c}(0)-h^{\prime}_{d}(0)=c-d-\ln\frac{c}{d}<0
by the premise.
This implies
h_{c}(\lambda)-h_{d}(\lambda)\leq 0
for
\lambda\in[0,1]
, since it equals
0
when
\lambda=0
and
\lambda=1
, is decreasing at
\lambda=0
, and has at most one local extremum for
\lambda\in[0,1]
.
∎

9
.

Let
\lambda\in[0,1]
and
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}\in(-1,1)
such that
0<(t_{1}+t_{2}+t_{3}+t_{1}t_{2}t_{3})<f(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})

\displaystyle F_{\lambda}(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{(1-\lambda)+\lambda\prod_{i\in[3]}(1+t_{i})}{(1-\lambda)+\lambda\prod_{i\in[3]}(1-t_{i})}\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}\ln\frac{\prod_{i\in[3]}(1+t_{i})}{\prod_{i\in[3]}(1-t_{i})}=\lambda f(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})\,.

Let
c:=\prod_{i\in[3]}(1+t_{i})
and

. Then
c-d=2(t_{1}+t_{2}+t_{3}+t_{1}t_{2}t_{3})

8

10
.

Let
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}\in(-1,1)
such that
0<(t_{1}+t_{2}+t_{3}+t_{1}t_{2}t_{3})
. Then
(t_{1}+t_{2}+t_{3}+t_{1}t_{2}t_{3})<f(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})
.

Proof.

When
t>0
, note that by Taylor expansion,
\textnormal{arctanh}(t)>t+\frac{t^{3}}{3}>t
. So when two variables are zero (let’s say
t_{1}=t_{2}=0
),
t_{3}>0
implies
t_{3}<\textnormal{arctanh}(t_{3})=f(0,0,t_{3})
.

Suppose exactly one variable is zero (let’s say
t_{1}=0
). Fix
t_{2}
. The equation
s(t_{3})=t_{2}+t_{3}-f(0,t_{2},t_{3})
has a root at
t_{3}=-t_{2}
. Its derivative is
s^{\prime}(t_{3})=1-\frac{1}{1-t_{3}^{2}}
, which is negative for all
t_{3}\neq 0
(and zero at
t_{3}=0
). So
t_{3}>-t_{2}
(i.e.
t_{2}+t_{3}>0
) implies
s(t_{3})<s(-t_{2})=0
.

Suppose all variables are nonzero. Fix
t_{1},t_{2}
. Consider
s(t_{3})=t_{1}+t_{2}+t_{3}+t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}-f(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})
. There is a root at
t_{3}^{*}:=-\frac{t_{1}+t_{2}}{1+t_{1}t_{2}}
, since at
t_{3}=t_{3}^{*}
,
\prod_{i\in[3]}(1+t_{i})=\prod_{i\in[3]}(1-t_{i})
. By the previous paragraph,
s(0)<0
. The derivative is
s^{\prime}(t_{3})=1+t_{1}t_{2}-\frac{1}{1-t_{3}^{2}}
, which has roots at
r_{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{t_{1}t_{2}}{1+t_{1}t_{2}}}
. We split the analysis into cases:

•

If
t_{1}t_{2}<0
, then
s^{\prime}(0)=t_{1}t_{2}<0
. Since
r_{\pm}
are not real,
s
is strictly decreasing. So
t_{3}>t_{3}^{*}
(i.e.
t_{1}+t_{2}+t_{3}+t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}>0
) implies
s(t_{3})<0
.

•

If
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
are both negative, then
t_{3}^{*}>0
. Note that
t_{3}^{*}>\max(r_{+},r_{-})
, since

(t_{1}+t_{2})^{2}>2t_{1}t_{2}\geq t_{1}t_{2}(1+t_{1}t_{2})
.
Since
\lim_{t_{3}\to 1}s(t_{3})=-\infty
,
s(t_{3})
is decreasing when
t_{3}>t_{3}^{*}
, and so
s(t_{3})<0
in this region.

•

If
t_{1},t_{2}
are both positive, then
t_{3}^{*}<0
. We know
s(t_{3})<0
for all
t_{3}^{*}<t_{3}<0
, since
s(0)<0
,
s^{\prime}(0)>0
, and only one root of
s^{\prime}
is negative. When
t_{3}>0
, Taylor expansion gives
s(t_{3})<t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}-\frac{1}{3}(t_{1}^{3}+t_{2}^{3}+t_{3}^{3})
. The right-hand side is equal to
-\frac{1}{3}(t_{1}+t_{2}+t_{3})(t_{1}^{2}+t_{2}^{2}+t_{3}^{2}-t_{1}t_{2}-t_{2}t_{3}-t_{3}t_{1})
, which is at most zero.

∎
