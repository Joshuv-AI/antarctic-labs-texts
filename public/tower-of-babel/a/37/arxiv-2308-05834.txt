Abstract
. The Bergman kernels of monomial polyhedra are explicitly computed. Monomial polyhedra are a class of bounded pseudoconvex Reinhardt domains defined as sublevel sets of Laurent monomials. Their kernels are rational functions and are obtained by an application of Bell’s transformation formula.

1.
Introduction

We will say that a
bounded
domain (open, connected subset)
\mathscr{U}_{B}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n},n\geq 2
is a
monomial polyhedron
if there is
an
n\times n
matrix of integers
B
such that

\mathscr{U}_{B}=\left\{(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}:\text{ for each }1\leq j\leq n,\quad\left|z_{1}\right|^{b^{j}_{1}}\dots\left|z_{n}\right|^{b^{j}_{n}}<1\right\},

(1.1)

where
b^{j}_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
denotes the entry at the
j
-th row and
k
-th column of
B
, where it is assumed in (
1.1
) that the power
\left|z_{k}\right|^{b^{j}_{k}}
is well-defined for each
z\in\mathscr{U}_{B}
and each
1\leq j,k\leq n
,
i.e., if
b^{j}_{k}<0
for some
j,k
, then
z_{k}\not=0
for each point
z\in\mathscr{U}_{B}
.
We summarize the situation by saying that
B
is the defining matrix of the domain
\mathscr{U}_{B}
, or simply that
\mathscr{U}_{B}
is defined by
B
. Monomial polyhedra are clearly Reinhardt, and looking at their log absolute image, we see immediately that they are also pseudoconvex.
If
B=I
is the
n\times n
identity matrix, then
\mathscr{U}_{I}
is the unit polydisc, which may be regarded as a trivial example.
A famous nontrivial example of a monomial polyhedron is the
classical Hartogs triangle
:

\{(z_{1},z_{2})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}:\left|z_{1}\right|<\left|z_{2}\right|<1\},

a venerable source
of counterexamples in complex analysis, which is easily seen to be a monomial polyhedron defined by the matrix
\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
0&\phantom{-}1\end{pmatrix}.

Pathologies of the Hartogs triangle (e.g., lack of Stein neighborhood bases) generalize to nontrivial monomial polyhedra, explaining the importance of these domains in complex analysis. Monomial polyhedra and domains closely associated to them have been studied extensively in complex and harmonic analysis (see
[
NP09
,
NP20
,
BCEM22
]
).

In the last decade, there has been activity surrounding domains generalizing the Hartogs triangle, their Bergman kernels, and the Bergman projection on these domains. (For general information on the Bergman kernel and projection, see, e.g.
[
Kra13
,
HKZ00
]
). The current interest began with the discovery in
[
CZ16
,
EM17
,
EM16
]
of remarkable
L^{p}
-mapping properties of the Bergman projection on the
Generalized Hartogs Triangle
, the domain
\Omega_{\gamma}=\{\left|z_{1}\right|^{\gamma}<\left|z_{2}\right|<1\},\gamma>0
, which is a monomial polyhedron if
\gamma
happens to be rational.
Since a first step in investigating the Bergman projection is to understand the Bergman kernel, a series of studies have been directed toward the goal of obtaining the Bergman kernel of various generalizations of the Hartogs triangle. The kernel of the classical Hartogs triangle has been known for a long time and occurs explicitly in
[
Bre55
]
.
In
[
Edh16b
,
Edh16a
]
, the kernel of the Generalized Hartogs Triangle
\Omega_{\gamma}
was obtained when
\gamma
is either a positive integer or the reciprocal of a positive integer. In
[
Par18
]
, the kernel of the domain
\{\left|z_{1}\right|^{k_{1}}<\left|z_{2}\right|^{k_{2}}<\left|z_{3}\right|^{k_{3}}\}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
was obtained. In
[
CKMM20
,
Che17
,
Zha21a
]
other types of generalizations of the Hartogs triangle were studied and explicit kernels were obtained. In each of these works, ad hoc methods based on Bell’s transformation formula were used and led to rather complicated expressions for the kernel.

The original motivating problem of determining the values of
p
for which the Bergman projection is bounded in
L^{p}
-norm can be studied once the kernel is known (see
[
Che17
,
Zha21a
,
Zha21b
,
CEM19
]
etc., see also the survey
[
Zey20
]
).
In
[
BCEM22
]
, the problem was studied in the context of the monomial polyhedra (
1.1
), using a representation of these domains as quotients, thus avoiding the question of computing the Bergman kernel (similar ideas are found in
[
CZ16
,
CKY20
]
). While the Bergman projection itself is not bounded in
L^{p}
on a monomial polyhedron if
p
does not lie in a certain bounded interval, it was shown in
[
CE23
]
how to construct an alternative bounded projection operator from
L^{p}(\mathscr{U}_{B})
to its holomorphic subspace.

In
[
BCEM22
]
, it was shown using Galois theory that the Bergman kernels of
the domains
\mathscr{U}_{B}
are rational functions of the coordinates (this is also related to the results of
[
Bel84
]
). The question naturally arises of explicitly computing the Bergman kernel of

\mathscr{U}_{B}
in terms of the
n\times n
integer matrix
B
. When
n=2
, this question was answered in the recent preprint
[
Alm23
]
. However, some of the ideas of
[
Alm23
]
are specific to the two-dimensional case and do not generalize easily to higher dimensions. In this paper, we explicitly compute the Bergman kernel of the domain (
1.1
) in terms of the matrix
B
for any
n\geq 2
, obtaining formula (
2.9
) below.
This formula is of interest from many points of view. First, it adds infinite examples to the list of domains for which it is at all possible to write down a fully explicit Bergman kernel, and it generalizes and simplifies the computations in the special cases mentioned above (see below in Section
5
for some examples).
The fact that the kernel is rational is significant in view of the continuing interest in the algebraic nature of the Bergman kernel (see
[
EXX21
]
for some recent results).
Second, the explicit kernel is essential to understanding the regularity of the Bergman projection in norms such as the Sobolev norms (see
[
EM20
]
, where the problem is studied on Generalized Hartogs Triangles starting from the precise form of the kernel). Third, the computation uses combinatorial and algebraic ideas which are of interest in themselves. We believe that these ideas may be of relevance in the study of Bergman kernels of other domains obtained as quotients, such as the domains in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
considered in
[
DM23
]
, which arise as quotients under the action of non-Abelian finite reflection groups.

2.
A formula for the Bergman kernel of
\mathscr{U}_{B}

2.1.
Some notation

We introduce, following
[
BCEM22
]
, some extended “multi-index” type
notation to simplify the writing of our formulas.

(1)

For positive integers
m,n
,
we let
\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
denote the collection of
m\times n
matrices with integer entries. Similarly,

{\mathbb{C}}^{m\times n}
is the space of complex
m\times n
matrices. For an
n\times n
square matrix
A
with
n\geq 2
, and for
1\leq j,k\leq n
, we denote:

[A]^{j}_{k}\text{ or }a^{j}_{k}:=\text{ the entry of $A$ at the intersection of the $j$-th row and the $k$-th column}.

(2.1)

(2)

For an
n\times n
matrix
A
, and
1\leq j\leq n
, we denote by
a^{j}
the
j
-th row of
A
, so that
a^{j}
is a row vector of length
n
. Similarly,
a_{j}
is the
j
-th column of
A

and is therefore a column vector of height
n
. If
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
, using the definition
(
2.1
), we can write for
1\leq j,k\leq n
:

a^{j}=(a^{j}_{1},\dots,a^{j}_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n},\quad\text{ and }a_{k}=(a_{k}^{1},\dots,a_{k}^{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times 1}.

(2.2)

(3)

For a matrix
M\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}

, denote by
\adj M\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
the
adjugate matrix
of
M
. Recall that, by definition, the entry
at the
j
-th row and
k
-th column of
\adj M
is given by

[\adj M]^{j}_{k}=(-1)^{j+k}\det\left(M[k,j]\right),

(2.3)

where
M[k,j]
denotes the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
submatrix of
M
obtained by removing the
k
-th row and
j
-th column of
M
. For
M
invertible,
\adj M=\det M\cdot M^{-1}
by Cramer’s rule.

(4)

Notice that according to our convention,
\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}

denotes the collection of integer row vectors of length
n
and

\mathbb{Z}^{n\times 1}
denotes the collection of integer column vectors of height
n
.
The elements of the complex Euclidean space
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
are thought of as
column
vectors, i.e., we identify
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\cong{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times 1}.

To simplify writing, we write column vectors as transposes of row vectors, where transposition is denoted by a superscript
T
:

\begin{pmatrix}z_{1}\\
\vdots\\
z_{n}\end{pmatrix}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})^{T}.

(5)

We use the standard multi-index power notation: if
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})^{T}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times 1}
is an
n\times 1

column vector and
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{1\times n}
is a
1\times n
row vector, we denote

z^{\alpha}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}z_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}=z_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots z_{n}^{\alpha_{n}},

(2.4)

where each power
z_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}
is assumed to be well-defined and where we use the convention
0^{0}=1
.

(6)

We denote by
\mathbb{N}
the collection of nonnegative integers.
Given a matrix
B\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
let
B_{+}\in\mathbb{N}^{n\times n}
and
B_{-}\in\mathbb{N}^{n\times n}
be matrices given by

(b_{+})^{j}_{k}=\max\{b^{j}_{k},0\},\quad(b_{-})^{j}_{k}=\max\{-b^{j}_{k},0\}.

More succinctly,
B_{+}=\max\{B,0\}
and

B_{-}=\max\{-B,0\}
, where the maxima are taken elementwise and
0
denotes the
n\times n
zero matrix.
As usual, we let
(b_{+})^{j},(b_{-})^{j}
be the rows of
B_{+},B_{-}
, and a similar notation is used for the columns.

2.2.
The function
\mathrm{D}

Let
k,r
be integers, with
k\geq 1
. The function
\mathrm{D}
, introduced in
[
CKMM20
]
(and occurring implicitly in
[
Par18
,
Zha21a
]
), is defined by the relation

\left(\frac{1-x^{k}}{1-x}\right)^{2}=\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{D}_{k}(r)x^{r}.

(2.5)

Since the left-hand side of the above equation is a polynomial, for a fixed
k\geq 1
the quantity
\mathrm{D}_{k}(r)
vanishes for all negative
r
and for all but finitely many positive values of
r
. A computation shows that

\mathrm{D}_{k}(r)=\begin{cases}1+r,&0\leq r\leq k-1,\\
2k-(1+r),&k\leq r\leq 2k-2,\\
0,&r<0\text{ or }r>2k-2.\end{cases}

(2.6)

2.3.
Two assumptions on
B

It is clearly no loss of generality to assume that the integer matrix
B\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
defining the bounded
domain
\mathscr{U}_{B}
has the following two properties:

(1)

The determinant of the defining matrix is positive, i.e.,

\det B>0.

(2.7a)

Indeed, we must have
\det B\not=0
since otherwise
\mathscr{U}_{B}
is not an open set. Further, if the rows of the matrix
B
are permuted, the new matrix continues to
define the same monomial polyhedron, so we may assume (
2.7a
) without loss of generality.

(2)

The
n
entries of each row of
B
are relatively prime. We write this as

\gcd(b^{j})=1,\quad 1\leq j\leq n.

(2.7b)

Indeed, for
1\leq j\leq n
, if
d
is a positive integer dividing each entry of the
j
-th row
b^{j}
of the matrix
B
, then dividing each entry
b^{j}_{k}
of this row
b^{j}
by
d
results in a matrix which continues to define the same domain.
Therefore, we may divide each
row of the defining matrix of a monomial polyhedron by the gcd of that row to obtain a new matrix that defines the same monomial polyhedron and whose rows now satisfy (
2.7b
).

2.4.
The main result

In the statement of this result, as well as in the sequel, we denote by
\mathbbm{1}
the
1\times n
row vector each of whose components is 1:

\mathbbm{1}=(1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}.

(2.8)

Theorem
.

Assume that the matrix
B
satisfies (
2.7b
) and (
2.7a
).
Denoting
t=(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})^{T}
with
t_{j}=p_{j}\cdot\overline{q_{j}}
, the Bergman kernel of
\mathscr{U}_{B}
is

K_{\mathscr{U}_{B}}(p,q)=\frac{1}{\pi^{n}\cdot{(\det B)^{n-1}}}\cdot\frac{\sum\limits_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{1\times n}}C_{B}(\nu)t^{\nu}}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}(t^{(b_{-})^{j}}-t^{(b_{+})^{j}})^{2}},

(2.9)

where

C_{B}(\nu)=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}\left(\left(\nu-2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}+\mathbbm{1}\right)[\adj B]_{j}-1\right),\quad\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n},

(2.10)

with
[\adj B]_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times 1}
being the
j
-th column of the adjugate matrix of
B
and
\mathrm{D}
as defined in (
2.5
).
Further, we have
C_{B}(\nu)=0
, except perhaps when
\nu=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n})
satisfies

-1+\xi_{j}\leq\nu_{j}\leq 2\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|b_{j}^{k}\right|-1-\xi_{j},\quad 1\leq j\leq n,

(2.11)

with
\xi_{j}
being the ceiling

\xi_{j}=\left\lceil\frac{1}{\det B}\cdot\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\left|b_{j}^{k}\right|\right\rceil.

(2.12)

The kernel
K_{\mathscr{U}_{B}}
is a rational function of the variables
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
, and the representation (
2.9
) is canonical in the sense that the numerator
\sum_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{1\times n}}C_{B}(\nu)t^{\nu}
and the denominator
\prod_{j=1}^{n}(t^{(b_{-})^{j}}-t^{(b_{+})^{j}})^{2}
are polynomials in
{\mathbb{C}}[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]
without a common factor.

Remark:
Geometrically, the boundary of a nontrivial monomial polyhedron has a non-Lipschitz singularity at the origin, and the rest of the boundary is piecewise Levi-flat
and consists of smooth Levi-flat pieces that meet transversely. Since we know from

[
BCEM22
]
that the kernel is rational, the known boundary behavior (see
[
Fu14
]
) of the diagonal kernel
K_{\mathscr{U}_{B}}(z,z)\sim\delta(z)^{-2}
near smooth Levi-flat boundary points, where
\delta
is the distance to the boundary, already predicts the form of the denominator. However, it does not seem easy to deduce information about the behavior of the kernel as
z\to 0
without actually computing it, and it is this behavior that is of greatest interest in applications to the mapping properties of the Bergman projection.

3.
Preliminaries

3.1.
Matrix powers of vectors

Let
A\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
be an
n\times n
matrix, and let
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})^{T}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times 1}

be an
n\times 1
column vector. We define a “matrix power”
z^{A}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times 1}
by the formal expression:

z^{A}=(z^{a^{1}},\dots,z^{a^{n}})^{T}.

(3.1)

For each
k
, on a domain
U_{k}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
,
if we choose for each
1\leq j\leq n
a local branch of
z_{k}\mapsto(z_{k})^{a^{j}_{k}}
for each entry
a^{j}_{k}

of the column
a_{k}
of the matrix
A
,
we obtain a locally defined holomorphic mapping formally given by
z\mapsto z^{A}
. This defines a
holomorphic mapping defined on
U_{1}\times\dots\times U_{n}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times 1}
and taking values in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}:

\phi_{A}(z)=z^{A}.

(3.2)

If
A\in\mathbb{N}^{n\times n}
is a matrix of nonnegative integers, then
z^{A}
is uniquely defined for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times 1}
and
\phi_{A}:{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times 1}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times 1}
is an entire holomorphic mapping.
The following is easily proved (see
[
BCEM22
, Lemma 3.8]
or
[
NP09
, Lemma 4.1]
) and can be thought to be a generalization of the formula
\dfrac{d}{dx}x^{n}=nx^{n-1}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\phi_{A}
be locally defined on some open set of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times 1}
, as in (
3.2
). We have

\det\phi_{A}^{\prime}(z)=\det A\cdot z^{\mathbbm{1}A-\mathbbm{1}}.

3.2.
Monomial maps

If it happens that
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
, then
\phi_{A}
is a globally defined single-valued map (except for a polar set), known as a
monomial map
.

To discuss the basic properties of monomial maps, we introduce some more notation.

(1)

Let

{\mathbb{C}}^{*}={\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}\quad\text{ and }\mathbb{T}=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:\left|z\right|=1\},

and note that these are groups under complex multiplication.

(2)

We let
\exp:{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times 1}\to({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n\times 1}
be the componentwise exponential map

\exp((z_{1},\dots,z_{n})^{T})=(e^{z_{1}},\dots,e^{z_{n}})^{T}.

(3)

Given matrices or vectors
z,w
of the same size, we denote by
z\odot w
the elementwise
(or Hadamard-Schur) product of
z
and
w
, which is therefore a matrix or vector of the same size as
z
and
w
. For example, if
z,w\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times 1}
are column vectors of height
n
,
then
z\odot w\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times 1}
is the column vector of height
n
whose
j
-th entry is

z_{j}w_{j}
.

We now summarize the properties of the monomial map
\phi_{A}
(for proof, see
[
BCEM22
]
).
Recall that a
regular
covering map
\pi:E\to B
is a covering map where the group
\Gamma
of
deck transformations acts transitively on each fiber
\pi^{-1}(x),x\in B
. One can then identify
B
to the topological quotient

E/\Gamma
, and identify
\pi
to the quotient map.

Proposition 3.4
.

Suppose that
A\in\mathbb{N}^{n\times n}
. Then the holomorphic mapping
\phi_{A}:{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
restricts to a regular covering map from
({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
to
({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
where
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}={\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
. The deck transformation group
\Gamma
of
the regular covering
\phi_{A}
is isomorphic to the group

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=\{\gamma\in\mathbb{T}^{n}:\gamma^{A}=\mathbbm{1}^{T}\}

(3.5a)

\displaystyle=\{\exp\left(2\pi iA^{-1}\nu\right),\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times 1}\},

(3.5b)

where the action of the group
\Gamma
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times 1}
is given by

(\gamma,z)\mapsto\gamma\odot z,\quad\gamma\in\Gamma,z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times 1}.

(3.6)

The order of the group
\Gamma
is given by

\left|\Gamma\right|=\left|\det A\right|.

(3.7)

Monomial polyhedra as quotient domains

Let

(1)

4.
Proof of the main theorem and formula (

4.1.
Application of Bell’s law

The following easy-to-verify formulas will be used without comment: if
z
is an
n\times 1
column vector,
\alpha
is a
1\times n
row vector, and
P
and
Q
are
n\times n

(z^{P})^{\alpha}=z^{\alpha P}
and
(z^{P})^{Q}=z^{QP}
provided all quantities are well-defined.

We apply Bell’s transformation law for the Bergman kernel
under a proper holomorphic map (see
[
Bel82
]
) to the monomial map
\phi_{A}
given in part (2) of Proposition
3.8
. Notice also that

\det A=\det(\det B\cdot B^{-1})=(\det B)^{n}\det(B^{-1})=(\det B)^{n-1}>0.

Since
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times 1}
is a product of
n

z\in\Omega
,

\det\phi_{A}^{\prime}(z)\cdot K_{\mathscr{U}_{B}}(\phi_{A}(z),q)=\sum_{j=1}^{\det A}K_{\mathbb{D}^{n}}(z,\Phi_{j}(q))\cdot\overline{\det\Phi_{j}^{\prime}(q)},\quad

(4.2)

where
\{\Phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\det A}
are the locally defined branches of the inverse to
\phi_{A}
, of which there are
\left|\Gamma\right|=\det A
. Let
C=A^{-1}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}
.
For each
1\leq j,k\leq n
, fix a local branch of
q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{n})\mapsto(q_{k})^{c^{j}_{k}}
near each point of
\mathscr{U}_{B}
. This gives us a local branch of

q\mapsto q^{A^{-1}}

(4.3)

near each point of
\mathscr{U}_{B}
. Since we have a regular covering map with deck group
\Gamma
, all the local branches of its inverse are given by

\Phi_{\gamma}(q)=q^{A^{-1}}\odot\gamma,\quad\gamma\in\Gamma.

(4.4)

Now thanks to Proposition
3.3
, we have
\det\phi^{\prime}_{A}(z)=\det A\cdot z^{\mathbbm{1}A-\mathbbm{1}},
and

\det\Phi_{\gamma}^{\prime}(q)=\det A^{-1}\cdot q^{\mathbbm{1}A^{-1}-\mathbbm{1}}\cdot\gamma^{\mathbbm{1}},

(4.5)

where
q^{\mathbbm{1}A^{-1}}
is defined to be
(q^{A^{-1}})^{\mathbbm{1}}
using the branch (

Inserting these expressions, (
4.2
) becomes

\det A\cdot z^{\mathbbm{1}A-\mathbbm{1}}\cdot K_{\mathscr{U}_{B}}(z^{A},q)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}K_{\mathbb{D}^{n}}(z,q^{A^{-1}}\odot\gamma)\cdot\det A^{-1}\cdot\overline{q}^{\mathbbm{1}A^{-1}-\mathbbm{1}}\cdot\overline{\gamma}^{\mathbbm{1}}.

(4.6)

Therefore,

K_{\mathscr{U}_{B}}(z^{A},q)=\frac{1}{(\det A)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\overline{q}^{\mathbbm{1}A^{-1}-\mathbbm{1}}}{z^{\mathbbm{1}A-\mathbbm{1}}}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\overline{\gamma}^{\mathbbm{1}}\cdot K_{\mathbb{D}^{n}}(z,q^{A^{-1}}\odot\gamma).

(4.7)

We introduce a change of variables

z=p^{A^{-1}},
where we use the same branch of the
A^{-1}
-th power as in (
4.3
). Then
z^{A}=p
. Further, recalling that

t=(p_{1}\overline{q_{1}},\dots,p_{n}\overline{q_{n}})=p\odot\overline{q}
, we have

\frac{\overline{q}^{\mathbbm{1}A^{-1}-\mathbbm{1}}}{z^{\mathbbm{1}A-\mathbbm{1}}}=\frac{\overline{q}^{\mathbbm{1}A^{-1}}\cdot\overline{q}^{-\mathbbm{1}}}{p^{\mathbbm{1}}\cdot p^{(-\mathbbm{1}A^{-1}})}=t^{\mathbbm{1}A^{-1}-\mathbbm{1}}.

Since
\mathscr{U}_{B}
is a Reinhardt domain, there is a function
k_{2}
such that

K_{\mathscr{U}_{B}}(p,q)=k_{2}(p_{1}\overline{q_{1}},\dots,p_{n}\overline{q_{n}})=k_{2}(p\odot\overline{q}).

We can also write

K_{\mathbb{D}^{n}}(z,w)=\frac{1}{\pi^{n}}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(1-z_{j}\overline{w_{j}})^{2}}=k_{1}(z_{1}\overline{w_{1}},\dots,k_{n}\overline{w_{n}})=k_{1}(z\odot\overline{w}),

where
\displaystyle{k_{1}(\tau)=\frac{1}{\pi^{n}}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(1-\tau_{j})^{2}}.}

Since

\displaystyle z\odot\overline{q^{A^{-1}}\odot\gamma}

\displaystyle=p^{A^{-1}}\odot\overline{q^{A^{-1}}\odot\gamma}=t^{A^{-1}}\odot\overline{\gamma}

\displaystyle=(\overline{\gamma_{1}}t^{c^{1}},\dots,\overline{\gamma_{n}}t^{c^{n}})^{T},\quad C=A^{-1},

in terms of
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
, we can rewrite formula (
4.7
) as

\displaystyle k_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{\mathbbm{1}A^{-1}-\mathbbm{1}}}{(\det A)^{2}}\cdot\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\overline{\gamma}^{\mathbbm{1}}\cdot k_{1}(t^{A^{-1}}\odot\overline{\gamma})

(4.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{\mathbbm{1}A^{-1}-\mathbbm{1}}}{\pi^{n}(\det{A})^{2}}\cdot\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\overline{\gamma}^{\mathbbm{1}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(1-t^{c^{j}}\overline{\gamma_{j}})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{-\mathbbm{1}}}{\pi^{n}(\det{A})^{2}}\cdot\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\overline{\gamma_{j}}t^{c^{j}}}{(1-\overline{\gamma_{j}}t^{c^{j}})^{2}}

(4.9)

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{-\mathbbm{1}}}{\pi^{n}(\det{A})^{2}}\cdot L(t^{A^{-1}}),

(4.10)

where for a column vector
E=(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})^{T}
of indeterminates we set

L(E)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\overline{\gamma_{j}}E_{j}}{(1-\overline{\gamma_{j}}E_{j})^{2}}.

(4.11)

4.2.
The group
\Gamma_{j}

Consider for
1\leq j\leq n
the set

\Gamma_{j}=\{\gamma_{j}\in\mathbb{T}:(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n})^{T}\in\Gamma\},

(4.12)

i.e., the projection of the group
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{T}^{n}
onto the
j
-th factor of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
. Since such a projection is a group homomorphism,
\Gamma_{j}
is a finite subgroup of
\mathbb{T}
and therefore cyclic. We compute its order:

Lemma 4.13
.

For each
1\leq j\leq n

\left|\Gamma_{j}\right|=\det B.

(4.14)

Proof.

Writing
C=A^{-1}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}
we can rewrite (
3.5b
)
as

\Gamma=\left\{(e^{2\pi ic^{1}\nu},\dots,e^{2\pi ic^{n}\nu})^{T},\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times 1}\right\}.

Therefore, denoting by
[\adj A]^{j}
the
j
-th row of the adjugate of
A
, that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{j}

\displaystyle=\{e^{2\pi ic^{j}\nu}\in\mathbb{T}:\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times 1}\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i}{\det A}\cdot[\adj A]^{j}\nu\right)\in\mathbb{T}:\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times 1}\right\}\quad\text{by Cramer's rule}

\displaystyle=\left\{\exp\left({\frac{2\pi i}{\det A}}\cdot\gcd([\adj A]^{j})\cdot k\right)\in\mathbb{T}:k\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

It follows that
the group
\Gamma_{j}
is a cyclic group
of order
\displaystyle{\frac{{\det A}}{\gcd([\adj A]^{j})}}
.
Since

\displaystyle\adj A

\displaystyle=\adj(\adj B)=\det(\adj B)\cdot(\adj B)^{-1}=(\det B)^{n-1}(\det B\cdot B^{-1})^{-1}

\displaystyle=(\det B)^{n-2}\cdot B,

(4.15)

we have

\gcd([\adj A]^{j})=\gcd((\det B)^{n-2}\cdot b^{j})=(\det B)^{n-2}\gcd(b^{j})=(\det B)^{n-2},

where we use the condition (
2.7b
). Therefore,

\left|\Gamma_{j}\right|=\frac{\det A}{\gcd([\adj A]^{j})}=\frac{(\det B)^{n-1}}{(\det B)^{n-2}}=\det B.

∎

4.3.
Simplification of
L

We now turn our attention to the function
L
of (
4.10
) and (
4.11
).
For each
1\leq j\leq n
, dividing and multiplying by
(1-E_{j}^{\det B})^{2}
, we write

\displaystyle\frac{\overline{\gamma_{j}}E_{j}}{(1-\overline{\gamma_{j}}E_{j})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\overline{\gamma_{j}}E_{j}}{(1-E_{j}^{\det B})^{2}}\cdot\dfrac{(1-E_{j}^{\det B})^{2}}{(1-\overline{\gamma_{j}}E_{j})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-E_{j}^{\det B})^{2}}\cdot\overline{\gamma_{j}}E_{j}\sum_{r_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}(r_{j})\cdot(\overline{\gamma_{j}}E_{j})^{r_{j}}

(4.16)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-E_{j}^{\det B})^{2}}\cdot\sum_{r_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}(r_{j})\overline{\gamma_{j}}^{r_{j}+1}E_{j}^{r_{j}+1},

where in (
4.16
) we use (
2.5
) with
x=\overline{\gamma_{j}}E_{j}
, remembering that
x^{\det B}=E_{j}^{\det B}
since
(\overline{\gamma_{j}})^{\det B}=1
, as
\overline{\gamma_{j}}\in\Gamma_{j}
and the group
\Gamma_{j}
has order
\det B
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle L(E)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\overline{\gamma_{j}}E_{j}}{(1-\overline{\gamma_{j}}E_{j})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(1-E_{j}^{\det B})^{2}}\cdot\sum_{r_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}(r_{j})\overline{\gamma_{j}}^{r_{j}+1}E_{j}^{r_{j}+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{r_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}(r_{j})\overline{\gamma_{j}}^{r_{j}+1}E_{j}^{r_{j}+1}}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-E_{j}^{\det B})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Lambda(E)}{\Delta(E)},

(4.17)

where
\Delta
and
\Lambda
are the polynomials in the indeterminates
E_{1},\dots,E_{n}
given by

\Delta(E)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-E_{j}^{\det B})^{2}

(4.18)

and

\displaystyle\Lambda(E)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{r_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}(r_{j})\overline{\gamma_{j}}^{r_{j}+1}E_{j}^{r_{j}+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\theta\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}}\left(\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}(\theta_{j}-1)\overline{\gamma_{j}}^{\theta_{j}}\right)E^{\theta}

(4.19)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\theta\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}}\Lambda_{\theta}E^{\theta},

(4.20)

where in (
4.19
) we have gathered all coefficients associated to each monomial
E^{\theta}
to put
\Lambda(E)
in the standard form, and consequently,

\Lambda_{\theta}=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\overline{\gamma}^{\theta}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}(\theta_{j}-1).

(4.21)

To simplify (
4.20
), we notice that for
\alpha\in\Gamma
, we have (recall that
\odot
stands for entrywise multiplication of vectors)

\displaystyle L(\alpha\odot E)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{j}\overline{\gamma_{j}}E_{j}}{(1-\alpha_{j}\overline{\gamma_{j}}E_{j})^{2}}=\sum_{\beta\in\Gamma}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\overline{\beta_{j}}E_{j}}{(1-\overline{\beta_{j}}E_{j})^{2}}

(4.22)

\displaystyle=L(E),

where in the last expression in (
4.22
), we set
\beta=\overline{\alpha}\odot\gamma
, i.e.,
\beta_{j}=\overline{\alpha_{j}}\gamma_{j}
, and reindex the sum over the group
\Gamma
.
Also,

\Delta(\alpha\odot E)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-(\alpha_{j}E_{j})^{\det B})^{2}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-\alpha_{j}^{\det B}\cdot E_{j}^{\det B})^{2}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-E_{j}^{\det B})^{2}=\Delta(E).

Since
\Lambda(E)=L(E)\cdot\Delta(E)
it follows that for each
\alpha\in\Gamma
,

\Lambda(\alpha\odot E)=L(\alpha\odot E)\cdot\Delta(\alpha\odot E)=\Lambda(E).

Using the representation (
4.20
) of
\Lambda(E)
, this is equivalent to the fact that for each
\alpha\in\Gamma
,

\sum_{\theta\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}}\Lambda_{\theta}\alpha^{\theta}E^{\theta}=\sum_{\theta\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}}\Lambda_{\theta}E^{\theta},\quad\text{ i.e.,}\quad\sum_{\theta\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}}\Lambda_{\theta}\cdot(\alpha^{\theta}-1)E^{\theta}=0.

Therefore, for
\theta\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}
, we can have
\Lambda_{\theta}\not=0
only if

\alpha^{\theta}=1
for each
\alpha\in\Gamma
, i.e., using the representation (
3.5b
) for the group
\Gamma
, for each
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times 1}
, we have

1=\left(\exp(2\pi iA^{-1}\nu)\right)^{\theta}=e^{2\pi i\theta A^{-1}\nu},

which is to say that

\theta A^{-1}\nu\in\mathbb{Z}\quad\text{ for each }\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times 1}.

(4.23)

We claim that (
4.23
) holds if and only if

\theta=mA\quad\text{for an }m\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}.

(4.24)

Indeed, if
\theta=mA
for an integer row vector
m
, then for each integer column vector

\nu
:

\theta A^{-1}\nu=(mA)A^{-1}\nu=m\nu\in\mathbb{Z}.

Conversely, suppose that for each
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times 1}
, we have
\theta A^{-1}\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
.
For
1\leq j\leq n
, let
e_{j}
denote the
j
-th standard basis vector in
\mathbb{Z}^{n\times 1}
, i.e.,
e_{j}
is a column vector with
n
entries, of which the
j
-th entry is 1 and the others are zeroes, and set
m_{j}=\theta A^{-1}e_{j},
so that
m_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
by hypothesis. But then
m_{j}
is the
j
-th entry of the row vector
\theta A^{-1}
and therefore this vector is in
\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}.
Setting
m=\theta A^{-1}
, we have
\theta=mA,
as needed.

Therefore,
\Lambda_{\theta}\not=0
for a
\theta\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}
if and only if (
4.24
) holds, and consequently, the expression (
4.20
) simplifies to

\Lambda(E)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta=mA\\
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{\theta}E^{\theta}=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}}\Lambda_{mA}E^{mA}=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}}\Lambda_{mA}\cdot(E^{A})^{m}.

(4.25)

Using the representation (
4.21
) of
\Lambda_{\theta}
, we see that

\displaystyle\Lambda_{mA}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\overline{\gamma}^{mA}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}(ma_{j}-1)

(4.26)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}(\overline{\gamma}^{A})^{m}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}(ma_{j}-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}(\mathbbm{1}^{T})^{m}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}(ma_{j}-1)

(4.27)

\displaystyle=\left|\Gamma\right|\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}(ma_{j}-1)

\displaystyle={\det A}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}(ma_{j}-1),

(4.28)

where in (
4.26
), we use the fact that the
j
-th entry of the row vector

mA\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}
is
ma_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
a_{j}
denotes the
j
-th column of the matrix
A=\adj B
. In (
4.27
) we use the characterization (
3.5a
) of the group
\Gamma
, and finally, in (
4.28
) we use the fact that
\Gamma
has
\left|\det A\right|
elements. Therefore, using (
4.25
), we have

\displaystyle\Lambda(t^{A^{-1}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}}\Lambda_{mA}\cdot((t^{A^{-1}})^{A})^{m}

\displaystyle={\det A}\cdot\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}(ma_{j}-1)t^{m}.

(4.29)

We also have, using (
4.18
),

\displaystyle{\Delta(t^{A^{-1}})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(1-(t^{c^{j}})^{\det B}\right)^{2}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(1-t^{\det B\cdot c^{j}}\right)^{2},}

where
c^{j}
denotes the
j
-th row of the matrix
C=A^{-1}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}
. Notice that

C=A^{-1}=(\adj B)^{-1}=(\det B\cdot B^{-1})^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det B}\cdot B,

so
\displaystyle{\det B\cdot c^{j}=\det B\cdot\frac{1}{\det B}\cdot b^{j}=b^{j}.}

Therefore, we obtain

\Delta(t^{A^{-1}})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(1-t^{{b^{j}}}\right)^{2}.

(4.30)

4.4.
An intermediate expression for the kernel

Since by definition (
4.17
) we have
L=\frac{\Lambda}{\Delta},
using
the representations (
4.29
) and (
4.30
) in (
4.10
), we obtain

\displaystyle k_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{-\mathbbm{1}}}{\pi^{n}(\det{A})^{2}}\cdot L(t^{A^{-1}})=\frac{t^{-\mathbbm{1}}}{\pi^{n}(\det{A})^{2}}\frac{\Lambda(t^{A^{-1}})}{\Delta(t^{A^{-1}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{-\mathbbm{1}}}{\pi^{n}(\det{A})^{2}}\cdot\frac{{\det A}\cdot\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}(ma_{j}-1)t^{m}}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(1-t^{{b^{j}}}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{n}\cdot{\det{A}}}\cdot\frac{\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}(ma_{j}-1)t^{m-\mathbbm{1}}}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(1-t^{{b^{j}}}\right)^{2}},

which taking into account (
4.1
), shows that

K_{\mathscr{U}_{B}}(p,q)=\frac{1}{\pi^{n}\cdot{(\det B)^{n-1}}}\cdot\frac{P(t)}{Q(t)},

(4.31)

where, recalling that
A=\adj B
,

P(t)=\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}}\left(\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}\left(m\,[\adj B]_{j}-1\right)\right)t^{m-\mathbbm{1}},

(4.32)

and

Q(t)=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}(1-t^{b^{j}})^{2}.

(4.33)

Notice that
Q
is a Laurent polynomial in the
n
variables
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
with integer coefficients and therefore
Q
a rational function. Also,
P
is a Laurent series in these
n
variables. In fact, a more careful book-keeping shows that
P
is also a Laurent polynomial with integer coefficients. We will show that
P/Q
is in fact a rational function by representing it as the ratio of two polynomials.

4.5.
Reduction to ratio of polynomials

Recall that
B_{+}=\max\{B,0\}
, and
B_{-}=\max\{-B,0\}
, where maxima of matrices are taken entrywise, and consequently,
B=B_{+}-B_{-}
.
Notice that the quantity
t^{2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}}
has two alternate representations:

\displaystyle t^{2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}}

\displaystyle=t^{2\sum_{j=1}^{n}(b_{-})^{j}}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}t^{2(b_{-})^{j}}

(4.34a)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}^{2\mathbbm{1}(b_{-})_{j}},

(4.34b)

where in (
4.34b
), we have used that
\mathbbm{1}B_{-}=(\mathbbm{1}(b_{-})_{1},\dots,\mathbbm{1}(b_{-})_{n}).

We multiply both the numerator and the denominator of (
4.31
)
by the monomial
t^{2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}}
. For the denominator, using representation (
4.34a
), we obtain

\displaystyle t^{2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}}\cdot Q(t)

\displaystyle=t^{2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}}\cdot\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}(1-t^{b^{j}})^{2}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}t^{2(b_{-})^{j}}\cdot\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}(1-t^{b^{j}})^{2}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(t^{(b_{-})^{j}}-t^{(b_{-})^{j}+b^{j}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(t^{(b_{-})^{j}}-t^{(b_{+})^{j}})^{2},

(4.35)

where in the last line we have used the fact that
b^{j}=(b_{+})^{j}-(b_{-})^{j}
. Now multiplying the numerator of (
4.31
) by
t^{2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}}
and using the representation (
4.34b
), we obtain:

\displaystyle t^{2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}}\cdot P(t)=

\displaystyle t^{2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}}\cdot\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}}\left(\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}\left(m\,[\adj B]_{j}-1\right)\right)t^{m-\mathbbm{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}^{2\mathbbm{1}(b_{-})_{j}}\cdot\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}\left(m\,[\adj B]_{j}-1\right)\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}^{m_{j}-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}\left(m\,[\adj B]_{j}-1\right)t_{j}^{m_{j}+2\mathbbm{1}(b_{-})_{j}-1}.

(4.36)

In the outer sum of (
4.36
), we reindex using a new summation index
\nu=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}
by setting

\nu_{j}=m_{j}+2\mathbbm{1}(b_{-})_{j}-1
for
1\leq j\leq n
,
or in vector notation,

\nu=m+2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}-\mathbbm{1}.

Notice that the mapping from
m
to
\nu
is nothing but a translation
in the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}
, so therefore
\nu
can be used as an index of summation instead of
m
in (
4.36
). Solving for
m
we obtain

m=\nu-2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}+\mathbbm{1},

(4.37)

or, written in terms of the components,

m_{j}=\nu_{j}-2\mathbbm{1}(b_{-})_{j}+1,\quad 1\leq j\leq n.

Reindexing, we obtain

(
4.36
)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}\left((\nu-2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}+\mathbbm{1})[\adj B]_{j}-1\right)t^{\nu}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}}C_{B}(\nu)t^{\nu},

(4.38)

with
C_{B}
as in (
2.10
). Therefore, using (
4.38
) and (
4.35
) in (
4.31
), we see that

\displaystyle K_{\mathscr{U}_{B}}(p,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{n}\cdot{(\det B)^{n-1}}}\cdot\frac{t^{2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}}\cdot P(t)}{t^{2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}}\cdot Q(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{n}\cdot{(\det B)^{n-1}}}\cdot\frac{\sum\limits_{\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}}C_{B}(\nu)t^{\nu}}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}(t^{(b_{-})^{j}}-t^{(b_{+})^{j}})^{2}},

which is the claimed formula (
2.9
), except the sum in the numerator is over
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}
rather than the finite set given by (
2.11
).

4.6.
Bounds on
\nu_{j}

To complete the proof, we need
to show that if
\nu
does not satisfy the conditions (
2.11
), then we have
C_{B}(\nu)=0
. Notice that the quantity

\xi_{j}
in (
2.12
) is an integer greater than or equal to 1,
since
\xi_{j}
is the ceiling of a positive number. Therefore, if (
2.11
) has been established, then from the left inequality we would know that
\nu_{j}\geq 0
for each
j
, and, consequently, the sum in the numerator will only have
\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{1\times n}
, i.e., the numerator is a polynomial.

From (
2.6
), we see that
\mathrm{D}_{k}(r)=0
for those
r
which do not satisfy

0\leq r\leq 2k-2.

Therefore,
\mathrm{D}_{\det B}\left((\nu-2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}+\mathbbm{1})[\adj B]_{j}-1\right)=0
except when we have

0\leq(\nu-2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}+\mathbbm{1})a_{j}-1\leq 2\det B-2,

(4.39)

using the notation
A=\adj B
introduced above in (
3.9
), and
a_{j}
being the
j
-th column of
A
.
From the definition (
2.10
) of the coefficients
C_{B}(\nu)
, it follows that
C_{B}(\nu)=0
provided
\nu
does not satisfy (
4.39
) for at least one
j
with
1\leq j\leq n
.
To manipulate the system of inequalities (
4.39
) in an efficient manner, we use
the elementwise inequality notation defined as follows: if
P,Q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
are real matrices or vectors
of the same size, then

P\preceq Q\quad\text{ means }p^{j}_{k}\leq q^{j}_{k},\quad 1\leq j\leq m\text{ and }1\leq k\leq n.

We will also write
Q\succeq P
to mean
P\preceq Q
if convenient.
Using this notation, we can say that
the set of
\nu
for which
C_{B}(\nu)\not=0
is contained in the set of
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}
given by

0\preceq(\nu-2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}+\mathbbm{1})A-\mathbbm{1}\preceq(2\det B-2)\mathbbm{1}.

(4.40)

which can be rearranged to read

\alpha\preceq\nu A\preceq\beta,

(4.41)

where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n}
are given by

\alpha=(2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}-\mathbbm{1})A+\mathbbm{1}

(4.42a)

and

\beta=(2\det B-1)\mathbbm{1}+(2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}-\mathbbm{1})A.

(4.42b)

Notice that since
B_{-}\succeq 0
and
B_{+}\succeq 0
by definition, we have from (
4.41
):

\alpha B_{+}\preceq\nu AB_{+}\preceq\beta B_{+}

(4.43a)

and

-\beta B_{-}\preceq-\nu AB_{-}\preceq-\alpha B_{-}.

(4.43b)

Adding (
4.43a
) and (
4.43b
) we obtain

\alpha B_{+}-\beta B_{-}\preceq\nu A(B_{+}-B_{-})\preceq\beta B_{+}-\alpha B_{-}.

(4.44)

To simplify (
4.44
), first note that since
B=B_{+}-B_{-}
, and

A=\adj B=\det B\cdot B^{-1},
we have for the middle term

\nu A(B_{+}-B_{-})=\nu AB=\nu\cdot(\det B\cdot I)=\det B\cdot\nu.

Let us denote
\left|B\right|=B_{+}+B_{-},

so that the entry at the
j
-th row and
k
-th column of
\left|B\right|
is
\left|b^{j}_{k}\right|.

For the leftmost term of (
4.44
), we have

\displaystyle\alpha B_{+}-\beta B_{-}

\displaystyle=\left((2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}-\mathbbm{1})A+\mathbbm{1}\right)B_{+}-\left((2\det B-1)\mathbbm{1}+(2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}-\mathbbm{1})A\right)B_{-}

\displaystyle=2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}AB_{+}-\mathbbm{1}AB_{+}+\mathbbm{1}B_{+}-2\det B\cdot\mathbbm{1}B_{-}+\mathbbm{1}B_{-}-2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}AB_{-}+\mathbbm{1}AB_{-}

\displaystyle=2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}A(B_{+}-B_{-})-\mathbbm{1}A(B_{+}-B_{-})+\mathbbm{1}(B_{+}+B_{-})-2\det B\cdot\mathbbm{1}B_{-}

\displaystyle=2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}AB-\mathbbm{1}AB+\mathbbm{1}\left|B\right|-2\det B\cdot\mathbbm{1}B_{-}

\displaystyle=2\det B\cdot\mathbbm{1}B_{-}-\det B\cdot\mathbbm{1}+\mathbbm{1}\left|B\right|-2\det B\cdot\mathbbm{1}B_{-}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\left|B\right|-\det B\cdot\mathbbm{1}.

For the rightmost term in (
4.44
) we have

\displaystyle\beta B_{+}-\alpha B_{-}

\displaystyle=\left((2\det B-1)\mathbbm{1}+(2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}-\mathbbm{1})A\right)B_{+}-\left((2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}-\mathbbm{1})A+\mathbbm{1}\right)B_{-}

\displaystyle=2\det B\cdot\mathbbm{1}B_{+}-\mathbbm{1}B_{+}+2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}AB_{+}-\mathbbm{1}AB_{+}-2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}AB_{-}+\mathbbm{1}AB_{-}-\mathbbm{1}B_{-}

\displaystyle=2\det B\cdot\mathbbm{1}B_{+}-\mathbbm{1}(B_{+}+B_{-})+2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}A(B_{+}-B_{-})-\mathbbm{1}A(B_{+}-B_{-})

\displaystyle=2\det B\cdot\mathbbm{1}B_{+}-\mathbbm{1}\left|B\right|+2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}AB-\mathbbm{1}AB

\displaystyle=2\det B\cdot\mathbbm{1}B_{+}-\mathbbm{1}\left|B\right|+2\det B\cdot\mathbbm{1}B_{-}-\det B\cdot\mathbbm{1}

\displaystyle=2\det B\cdot\mathbbm{1}(B_{+}+B_{-})-\mathbbm{1}\left|B\right|-\det B\cdot\mathbbm{1}

\displaystyle=(2\det B-1)\cdot\mathbbm{1}\left|B\right|-\det B\cdot\mathbbm{1}.

Putting together the three obtained expressions, (
4.44
) becomes

\mathbbm{1}\left|B\right|-\det B\cdot\mathbbm{1}\preceq\det B\cdot\nu\preceq(2\det B-1)\cdot\mathbbm{1}\left|B\right|-\det B\cdot\mathbbm{1}.

Since
\det B>0
by hypothesis, this is equivalent to

-\mathbbm{1}+\frac{1}{\det B}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\left|B\right|\preceq\nu\preceq-\mathbbm{1}+\left(2-\frac{1}{\det B}\right)\cdot\mathbbm{1}\left|B\right|.

In terms of components this takes the form

-1+\frac{1}{\det B}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|b^{k}_{j}\right|\leq\nu_{j}\leq-1+\left(2-\frac{1}{\det B}\right)\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|b^{k}_{j}\right|,\quad 1\leq j\leq n.

(4.45)

Since
\nu_{j}
is an integer, we can replace the leftmost member by its ceiling, and the rightmost member by its floor to obtain valid inequalities in terms of integers. Recalling the definition (
2.12
) of
\xi_{j}
, notice that the ceiling of the leftmost member is

\left\lceil-1+\frac{1}{\det B}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|b^{k}_{j}\right|\right\rceil=-1+\xi_{j},

(4.46a)

and the floor of the rightmost member is

\displaystyle\left\lfloor-1+\left(2-\frac{1}{\det B}\right)\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|b^{k}_{j}\right|\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=-1+2\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|b^{k}_{j}\right|+\left\lfloor-\frac{1}{\det B}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|b^{k}_{j}\right|\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=-1+2\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|b^{k}_{j}\right|-\xi_{j},

(4.46b)

where we use the fact that
\lfloor-x\rfloor=-\lceil x\rceil.

Plugging in (
4.46a
) and (
4.46b
) into (
4.45
),
the inequalities (
2.11
) follow.

4.7.
Canonicity of the representation

To complete the proof of the main theorem, we will now show that the representation (
2.9
) as the ratio of two polynomials is canonical, in the sense that there is no common irreducible factor of the numerator and denominator.

To see this, first, recall (see
[
Fu94
]
) that if
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is a bounded pseudoconvex
domain and
p\in b\Omega
is a boundary point in a neighborhood of which
b\Omega
is smooth, then there is a neighborhood
U
of
p
and a constant
C>0
such that

K_{\Omega}(z,z)\geq\frac{C}{\delta(z)^{2}}\quad\text{ for each }z\in U\cap\Omega,

(4.47)

where
\delta(z)
denotes the distance from the point
z
to
b\Omega
. This is clear in the case
n=1
, by comparing
K_{\Omega}(z,z)
with the diagonal Bergman kernel of a disk in
\Omega
tangent to
b\Omega
, and the general case follows by an inductive argument, using the famous Ohsawa-Takegoshi
L^{2}
-holomorphic extension theorem in the induction step.

To complete the proof, we use the following algebraic fact whose proof is postponed to the end of the section:

Lemma 4.48
.

For
1\leq k\leq n
, the polynomial
p_{k}(t)=t^{(b_{-})^{k}}-t^{(b_{+})^{k}}
is irreducible in

{\mathbb{C}}[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]
.

Assuming the lemma for the moment, suppose that the rational function
K_{\mathscr{U}_{B}}
is
not
in canonical form. Then, there is a
j
with
1\leq j\leq n
such that
p_{j}
divides the numerator

\sum_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{1\times n}}C_{B}(\nu)t^{\nu}
in the ring
{\mathbb{C}}[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]
. We can remove this common factor, leaving us with a denominator containing
p_{j}
to at most the first power. Now, let
q\not=0
be a point on the face
F_{j}=\{p_{j}(\left|z_{1}\right|^{2},\dots,\left|z_{n}\right|^{2})=0\}\subset b\mathscr{U}_{B}
which does not belong to any other face
F_{k},k\not=j
. Notice that the polynomial function
r(z)=p_{j}(\left|z_{1}\right|^{2},\dots,\left|z_{n}\right|^{2})
is a defining function of the domain
\mathscr{U}_{B}
near the smooth boundary point
q
, and so, since
p_{j}(t)
occurs in the denominator at most to the first power, from (
2.9
), there is a neighborhood
V
of
q

and a constant
C^{\prime}>0
such that

K_{\mathscr{U}_{B}}(z,z)\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{r(z)},\quad z\in V\cap\mathscr{U}_{B}.

(4.49)

However, shrinking
V
if needed, we have that
r
is comparable to
\delta
, the distance to the boundary, so using (
4.47
) we have that there is a constant
C^{\prime\prime}>0
such that

K_{\mathscr{U}_{B}}(z,z)\geq\frac{C^{\prime\prime}}{r(z)^{2}},\quad z\in V\cap\mathscr{U}_{B},

which contradicts (
4.49
) for
z
close enough to
q
, thus showing that
p_{j}
is not a factor of the numerator, and therefore the representation (
2.9
) is already in its lowest terms.

Proof of Lemma
4.48
.

Denote
\alpha=(b_{-})^{k},\beta=(b_{+})^{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{1\times n}

so that
b^{k}=\beta-\alpha
and
p_{k}(t)=t^{\alpha}-t^{\beta}=t_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots t_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}-t_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots t_{n}^{\beta_{n}}
. Since
\det B\not=0
, each row of
B
has at least one
nonzero entry and at least one of
\alpha,\beta
is nonzero. Therefore, renaming
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
, we can assume that

\alpha_{1}\not=0
. Further, the assumption (
2.7b
) means that

\gcd\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}\}=1.

Also, by definition of
B_{+},B_{-}
, we have the property

\alpha_{j}\not=0\Rightarrow\beta_{j}=0,\text{ and }\beta_{j}\not=0\Rightarrow\alpha_{j}=0.

(4.50)

Let
F
denote the rational function field
{\mathbb{C}}(t_{2},\dots,t_{n})
, the field of fractions of the UFD
R={\mathbb{C}}[t_{2},\dots,t_{n}]
.
Identifying
{\mathbb{C}}[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]
with
R[t_{1}]
it follows by Gauss’s Lemma
that the polynomial
p_{k}
in
R[t_{1}]
is irreducible in
R[t_{1}]
provided
it is primitive in
R[t_{1}]
and irreducible in
F[t_{1}]
. Notice that since

\alpha_{1}\not=0
, it follows that
p_{k}
is a polynomial of degree
\alpha_{1}\geq 1
as an element of either
R[t_{1}]
or
F[t_{1}].

Recall that a polynomial in
R[t_{1}]
is primitive if the gcd of its coefficients in
R
is 1. Since
\alpha_{1}\not=0
, it follows that
\beta_{1}=0
by (
4.50
), and we can write
p_{k}(t)=Pt_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}-Q
. We can factor the coefficients into
irreducibles of
R
as

Q=t_{2}^{\beta_{2}}\dots t_{n}^{\beta_{n}}\in R
and

P=t_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\dots t_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}\in R
. Since

t_{2},\dots,t_{n}
are irreducible in the UFD
R
, it follows from these factorizations that
\gcd(P,-Q)=1
, and therefore
p_{k}
is primitive in
R[t_{1}]
.

In
F[t_{1}]
, we can write
p_{k}(t)=P(t_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}-P^{-1}\cdot Q)
, so that we only need to show that
the polynomial
q(t_{1})=t_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}-P^{-1}\cdot Q
is irreducible in
F[t_{1}]
. This is obvious if
\alpha_{1}=1
, so assume that
\alpha_{1}\geq 2
.
We now claim that
P^{-1}\cdot Q\in F

is not a
d
-th power in
F
for any
d\geq 2
that divides
\alpha_{1}
.
Indeed, we have a factorization of
P^{-1}\cdot Q

into irreducible elements of
R

P^{-1}\cdot Q=t_{2}^{\beta_{2}-\alpha_{2}}\dots t_{n}^{\beta_{n}-\alpha_{n}}=\prod_{j=2}^{n}t_{j}^{b^{k}_{j}},

and since
\gcd(b^{k})=1
, it follows that if
d\geq 2
is
a divisor of
\alpha_{1}=b^{k}_{1}
, then there is at least one
2\leq j\leq n
such that
d
does not divide
b^{k}_{j}
. This means that
P^{-1}\cdot Q
is not a
d
-th power in
F
, since one of its irreducible factors
t_{j}
is raised to a power
b^{k}_{j}
not divisible by
d
.

Now let
c
be an
\alpha_{1}
-th root of
P^{-1}\cdot Q
in an extension field. Since by the above claim,
P^{-1}\cdot Q
is not an
\alpha_{1}
-th root, it follows that
c\not\in F
. More generally,
we have

c^{s}\not\in F,\quad 1\leq s<\alpha_{1}.

(4.51)

We already know this for
s=1
, so consider the smallest
s
with
2\leq s<\alpha_{1}
for which
c^{s}\in F
. We claim that
s
divides
\alpha_{1}
.
Let
s^{\prime}
be the remainder in dividing
\alpha_{1}
by
s
, so that
c^{s^{\prime}}=(c^{s})^{-T}\cdot c^{\alpha_{1}}\in F
for some positive integer
T
, which contradicts the minimality of
s
, since
s^{\prime}\not=1
. But then
c^{s}
is an
\frac{\alpha_{1}}{s}
-th root of
P^{-1}\cdot Q
, contradicting the fact that
P^{-1}\cdot Q
is not a
d
-th power in
F
for each divisor
d
of
\alpha_{1}
.

In the ring
F(c)[t_{1}]
, the polynomial
q(t_{1})
splits completely as

\displaystyle{t_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}-P^{-1}\cdot Q=\prod_{j=1}^{\alpha_{1}}(t_{1}-c\,\omega^{j})}
,
where
\omega=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{\alpha_{1}}}\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
If
q(t_{1})
has a nontrivial divisor
A(t_{1})
in
F[t_{1}]
, there is a subset
\emptyset\not=S\subsetneq\{1,\dots,n\}
and a
\lambda\in F
such that
A(t_{1})=\lambda\prod_{j\in S}(t-c\cdot\omega^{j})
. Looking at the constant term of
A
we see that

\lambda\prod_{j\in S}c\omega^{j}=\lambda\cdot c^{\left|S\right|}\cdot\omega^{\sum_{j\in S}j}\in F.

Since
\lambda\in F
and
\omega^{\sum_{j\in S}j}\in{\mathbb{C}}\subset F
, it follows that
c^{\left|S\right|}\in F
. But this contradicts (
4.51
), showing that
q(t_{1})
is irreducible in
F[t_{1}]

and completing the proof of the lemma.

∎

5.1.
Domains birational to the unit polydisc

When
\det B=1
, the map
\phi_{A}:\Omega\to\mathscr{U}_{B}
of Proposition
3.8
is a biholomorphism, and the formula (
2.9
) takes a simple form:

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
B\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
be such that
\det B=1
. Then

K_{\mathscr{U}_{B}}(z,w)=\frac{1}{\pi^{n}}\frac{t^{\mathbbm{1}\left|B\right|-\mathbbm{1}}}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}(t^{(b_{-})^{j}}-t^{(b_{+})^{j}})^{2}},\quad\text{ with }\left|B\right|=B_{+}+B_{-}.

(5.2)

Proof.

When
\det B=1
, the denominator of (
2.9
) and (
5.2
) are the same, so we need to show that
\sum_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{1\times n}}C_{B}(\nu)t^{\nu}=t^{\mathbbm{1}|B|-\mathbbm{1}}.

We use the bounds (
2.11
) to find the values of
\nu
for which
C_{B}(\nu)\not=0
. Using (
2.12
), we have, for each
1\leq j\leq n
, that

\displaystyle{\xi_{j}=\left\lceil\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\left|b_{j}^{k}\right|\right\rceil=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\left|b_{j}^{k}\right|,}

so the inequalities (
2.11
) become

-1+\xi_{j}\leq\nu_{j}\leq 2\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|b_{j}^{k}\right|-1-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\left|b_{j}^{k}\right|=-1+\xi_{j},

which means that if
C_{B}(\nu)\not=0
, we have
\nu_{j}=-1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|b_{j}^{k}\right|
,
i.e.,
\nu=\mathbbm{1}\left|B\right|-\mathbbm{1}.
Notice that if
\det B=1
, then
\adj B=\det B\cdot B^{-1}=B^{-1}
, so
B[\adj B]_{j}=e_{j}
, where
e_{j}
is the column vector with zeros everywhere except in the
j
-th spot. Therefore, we have by (
2.10
)

\displaystyle C_{B}(\mathbbm{1}\left|B\right|-\mathbbm{1})

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{D}_{1}\left(\left(\mathbbm{1}(B_{+}+B_{-})-\mathbbm{1}-2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}+\mathbbm{1}\right)[\adj B]_{j}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{D}_{1}\left(\mathbbm{1}B[\adj B]_{j}-1\right)=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{D}_{1}\left(\mathbbm{1}e_{j}-1\right)=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{D}_{1}(0)=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}1=1,

using (
2.6
) to compute
\mathrm{D}_{1}(0)
. Therefore

\displaystyle{\sum_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{1\times n}}C_{B}(\nu)t^{\nu}=C_{B}(\mathbbm{1}|B|-\mathbbm{1})t^{\mathbbm{1}|B|-\mathbbm{1}}=t^{\mathbbm{1}|B|-\mathbbm{1}}.}

∎

5.2.
Almughrabi’s formula for
n=2

We will now recapture the following result:

Proposition 5.3

(see
[
Alm23
]
)
.

Let
B\in\mathbb{Z}^{2\times 2}
satisfy (
2.7a
) and (
2.7b
). Then denoting
t_{j}=p_{j}\cdot\overline{q_{j}},j=1,2
, we have

K_{\mathscr{U}_{B}}(p,q)=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}\cdot\det A}\cdot\frac{g(t_{1},t_{2})}{\left(t_{2}^{a_{2}^{1}}-t_{1}^{a_{2}^{2}}\right)^{2}\left(t_{1}^{a_{1}^{2}}-t_{2}^{a_{1}^{1}}\right)^{2}},

(5.4)

where
a^{j}_{k}
indicates the element in the adjugate matrix
A=\adj B
of
B
at the
j
-th row and
k
-th column. The numerator
g(t_{1},t_{2})
is a polynomial given by

g(t_{1},t_{2})=\sum\limits_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{1\times 2}}\mathrm{D}_{\det A}(\zeta_{1}(\nu))\mathrm{D}_{\det A}(\zeta_{2}(\nu))t^{\nu_{1}}t^{\nu_{2}}

(5.5)

where

\zeta_{j}(\nu)=a^{1}_{j}\nu_{1}+a^{2}_{j}\nu_{2}-2(a^{2}_{1}a^{1}_{j}+a^{1}_{2}a^{2}_{j})+(a^{1}_{j}+a^{2}_{j}-1)
.

Proof.

Notice that

A=\begin{pmatrix}a^{1}_{1}&a^{1}_{2}\\

-b^{2}_{1}&b^{1}_{1}\end{pmatrix}.

By part (1) of Proposition
3.8
, we have
A=\adj B\succeq 0
, i.e. the entries of
A
are nonnegative. Consequently, we have from the above that
b^{1}_{1}\geq 0,b^{2}_{2}\geq 0,b^{1}_{2}\leq 0
and
b^{2}_{1}\leq 0.
Consequently, we have

B_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}b^{1}_{1}&0\\

Therefore, we have, for
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times 2}
,

\displaystyle\nu-2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}+\mathbbm{1}

\displaystyle=(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})-2(1,1)\begin{pmatrix}0&a^{1}_{2}\\
a^{2}_{1}&0\end{pmatrix}+(1,1)

\displaystyle=(\nu_{1}-2a^{2}_{1}+1,\nu_{2}-2a^{1}_{2}+1).

(5.6)

Using the above computations, with
n=2
, the formula (
2.9
) becomes

\displaystyle K_{\mathscr{U}_{B}}(p,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}\cdot{\det B}}\cdot\frac{\sum\limits_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{1\times 2}}C_{B}(\nu)t^{\nu}}{(t^{(b_{-})^{1}}-t^{(b_{+})^{1}})^{2}(t^{(b_{-})^{2}}-t^{(b_{+})^{2}})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}\cdot{\det B}}\cdot\frac{\sum\limits_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{1\times 2}}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{2}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}(\zeta_{j}(\nu))t^{\nu}}{(t^{(0,-b^{1}_{2})}-t^{(b^{1}_{1},0)})^{2}(t^{(-b^{2}_{1},0)}-t^{(0,b^{2}_{2})})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}\cdot{\det B}}\cdot\frac{\sum\limits_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{1\times 2}}\mathrm{D}_{\det B}(\zeta_{1}(\nu))\cdot\mathrm{D}_{\det B}(\zeta_{2}(\nu))t_{1}^{\nu_{1}}t_{2}^{\nu_{2}}}{\left(t_{2}^{-b^{1}_{2}}-t_{1}^{b^{1}_{1}}\right)^{2}\left(t_{1}^{-b^{2}_{1}}-t_{2}^{b^{2}_{2}}\right)^{2}}

(5.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}\cdot{\det A}}\cdot\frac{\sum\limits_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{1\times 2}}\mathrm{D}_{\det A}(\zeta_{1}(\nu))\cdot\mathrm{D}_{\det A}(\zeta_{2}(\nu))t_{1}^{\nu_{1}}t_{2}^{\nu_{2}}}{\left(t_{2}^{a^{1}_{2}}-t_{1}^{a^{2}_{2}}\right)^{2}\left(t_{1}^{a^{2}_{1}}-t_{2}^{a^{1}_{1}}\right)^{2}},

(5.8)

where (
5.8
) is obtained from (
5.7
) using the fact that
\det A=(\det B)^{n-1}=\det B
for
n=2
, the relation
A=\adj B
, and where,
using (
2.10
) and (
5.6
), we have

\displaystyle\zeta_{j}(\nu)

\displaystyle=\left(\nu-2\mathbbm{1}B_{-}+\mathbbm{1}\right)[\adj B]_{j}-1=(\nu_{1}-2a^{2}_{1}+1,\nu_{2}-2a^{1}_{2}+1)\begin{pmatrix}a^{1}_{j}\\
a^{2}_{j}\end{pmatrix}-1

\displaystyle=a^{1}_{j}\nu_{1}+a^{2}_{j}\nu_{2}-2(a^{2}_{1}a^{1}_{j}+a^{1}_{2}a^{2}_{j})+(a^{1}_{j}+a^{2}_{j}-1).

∎

5.3.
Comments on general formulas for
n\geq 3

Given the matrix
B\in\mathbb{Z}^{2\times 2}
defining a two-dimensional monomial polyhedron
\mathscr{U}_{B}
, using part (1) of Proposition
3.8
, we have

B_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}^{1}&0\\
0&b_{2}^{2}\end{pmatrix}
and

B_{-}=\begin{pmatrix}\phantom{-}0&-b^{1}_{2}\\
-b^{2}_{1}&\phantom{-}0\end{pmatrix}
.
So the decomposition into the positive and negative parts of the matrix
B
is unique, and this leads to the unique expression (
5.4
) for the Bergman kernel in its canonical form as the ratio of two coprime polynomials.

If
n\geq 3
, this is not the case. For example, consider the matrices

\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1\\
0&1&\phantom{-}0\\
0&0&\phantom{-}1\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&\phantom{-}1\\
0&\phantom{-}1&-1\\
0&\phantom{-}0&\phantom{-}1\end{pmatrix}.

Both of these have inverses with nonnegative entries and therefore define monomial polyhedra. Thus, the Bergman kernels are represented in canonical form by different formulas. However, it is possible to write
the kernels as
rational
functions using the same general formula in terms of the matrix
B
as we saw in (
4.31
). The possible sign patterns can be determined using methods described in
[
Joh83
]
.

5.4.
Symmetries of
\mathrm{D}

The following lemma will be used below:

Lemma 5.9
.

The function
\mathrm{D}_{k}
of (
2.5
) has the following properties:

(1)

for integers
k\geq 1
and
r
, we have
\mathrm{D}_{k}(r)=\mathrm{D}_{k}(2k-2-r).

(2)

for
k_{1},k_{2}
positive integers and each integer
r
we have

\mathrm{D}_{k_{1}k_{2}}(k_{2}(r+1)-1)=k_{2}\mathrm{D}_{k_{1}}(r).

Proof.

Part (1): Using (
2.5
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{D}_{k}(r)x^{r}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1-x^{k}}{1-x}\right)^{2}=\left(\frac{x^{k}(1-(x^{-1})^{k})}{x(1-x^{-1})}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=x^{2k-2}\cdot\left(\frac{(1-(x^{-1})^{k})}{(1-x^{-1})}\right)^{2}=x^{2k-2}\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{D}_{k}(r)(x^{-1})^{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{D}_{k}(r)x^{2k-2-r}=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{D}_{k}(2k-2-\lambda)x^{\lambda},

where the last expression is obtained by reindexing the sum using
\lambda=2k-2-r
. Upon comparing coefficients of the same degree, we get the result.

Part (2): Again, using (
2.5
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{D}_{k_{1}k_{2}}(\mu)x^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1-x^{k_{1}k_{2}}}{1-x}\right)^{2}=\left(\frac{1-(x^{k_{2}})^{k_{1}}}{1-x^{k_{2}}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1-x^{k_{2}}}{1-x}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{D}_{k_{1}}(\lambda_{1})x^{k_{2}{\lambda_{1}}}\cdot\sum_{\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{D}_{k_{2}}(\lambda_{2})x^{\lambda_{2}}

Fix
r\in\mathbb{Z}
and set

\mu=k_{2}(r+1)-1
. Equating coefficients of the same degree, we see that

\mathrm{D}_{k_{1}k_{2}}(k_{2}(r+1)-1)=\sum_{k_{2}\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}=k_{2}(r+1)-1}\mathrm{D}_{k_{1}}(\lambda_{1})\mathrm{D}_{k_{2}}(\lambda_{2}).

(5.10)

The linear Diophantine equation
k_{2}\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}=k_{2}(r+1)-1
for the integer unknowns
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
,
has the general solution
\lambda_{1}=(r+1)-\xi,\quad\lambda_{2}=-1+k_{2}\xi,\quad\xi\in\mathbb{Z}.

Thus, (
5.10
) becomes

\mathrm{D}_{k_{1}k_{2}}(k_{2}(r+1)-1)=\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{D}_{k_{1}}((r+1)-\xi)\mathrm{D}_{k_{2}}(-1+k_{2}\xi).

By (
2.6
), the function
\mathrm{D}_{k_{2}}
vanishes outside
[0,2k_{2}-2]
.
Therefore, the only value of
\xi
for which
\mathrm{D}_{k_{2}}(-1+k_{2}\xi)
is nonzero is
\xi=1
, so that we have

\mathrm{D}_{k_{1}k_{2}}(k_{2}(r+1)-1)=\mathrm{D}_{k_{1}}((r+1)-1)\mathrm{D}_{k_{2}}(-1+k_{2}\cdot 1)=\mathrm{D}_{k_{1}}(r)k_{2},

where we have used the fact that
\mathrm{D}_{k}(k-1)=k
from (
2.6
).
∎

5.6.
