Spin Liquid and Superconductivity emerging from Steady States and Measurements
Kaixiang Su, Abhijat Sarma, Marcus Bintz, Thomas Kiely, Yimu Bao, Matthew P. A. Fisher, Cenke Xu

Abstract
We demonstrate that, starting with a simple fermion wave function, the steady mixed state of the evolution of a class of Lindbladians, and the ensemble created by strong local measurement of fermion density without post-selection can be mapped to the “Gutzwiller projected” wave functions in the doubled Hilbert space – the representation of the density matrix through the Choi-Jamiołkowski isomorphism. A Gutzwiller projection is a broadly used approach of constructing spin liquid states. For example, if one starts with a gapless free Dirac fermion pure quantum state, the constructed mixed state corresponds to an algebraic spin liquid in the doubled Hilbert space. We also predict that for some initial fermion wave function, the mixed state created following the procedure described above is expected to have a spontaneous “strong-to-weak” U ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathrm{U}(1) symmetry breaking, which corresponds to the emergence of superconductivity in the doubled Hilbert space. We also design the experimental protocol to construct the desired physics of mixed states.



I
Introduction

Quantum spin liquids, a class of highly nontrivial disordered phase of quantum spins, have been
the subject of an extremely active subfield of condensed matter physics since their
early theoretical proposal
[
1
,
2
]
. Despite great progress made in the field, many open questions and challenges remain (for a review, please refer to
[
3
,
4
,
5

strongly coupled gauge theory, which is a formidable analytical problem except for certain theoretical limits.
Secondly, though it is certain that spin liquids do
exist in some elegant theoretical models, e.g. the Kitaev model
[
6
]
, numerical simulation of quantum spin liquid on more realistic models often suffers from sign problems as the models that potentially realize the spin liquid usually have geometric frustration. Hence controversies continue to persist about the existence or nature of the spin liquids in various important realistic models. Thirdly, the signal of quantum spin liquid in real correlated materials may be obscured by the inevitable disorders and impurities.

Rather than the parent Hamiltonian, one can instead focus on the spin liquid wave function. One standard construction of spin liquid wave function is the so-called Gutzwiller projected state
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,

,

]
:

\displaystyle|{\rm SL}\rangle=\prod_{i}\hat{{\cal P}}(\hat{n}_{i,\uparrow}+\hat{n}_{i,\downarrow}=1)|f_{i,\uparrow},f_{i,\downarrow}\rangle,

(1)

where
|f_{i,\uparrow},f_{i,\downarrow}\rangle
is a simple spin-1/2 fermion state, and it can be often taken as a free fermion state with on average one fermion per-site. The projection
\hat{{\cal P}}
ensures that there is one and only one fermion per site, which matches the onsite Hilbert space of a spin-1/2 system.

Though the Gutzwiller projection is broadly used as a trial mean field wave functions of spin liquids, it is never exactly realized in condensed matter systems. In this work we demonstrate that the Gutzwiller wave function can be realized in a completely different set-up: it can be constructed as the steady mixed state of a Lindbladian evolution, or as the ensemble created by strong measurements of local operators, starting with a simple fermion wave function. The mixed state density matrix obtained from both constructions in the doubled Hilbert space (the Choi-Jamiołkowski representation
[
14
,
15
]
), becomes exactly a Gutzwiller wave function. Predictions of the constructed mixed state will be made based on our theoretical understanding of quantum spin liquids.

We also demonstrate that, sometimes the constructed mixed state density matrix is expected to become a supercondutor in the doubled Hilbert space, which corresponds to the “strong-to-weak” spontaneous symmetry breaking, a subject that has attracted great interests very recently
[

,

,

II
Basic Formalism

II.1
Construction with Lindbladian

We consider the nonunitary Lindbladian evolution of a density matrix. Let us assume that the density matrix at
t=0
corresponds to a pure quantum state
\rho_{0}=|\Psi_{0}\rangle\langle\Psi_{0}|
of fermions. For simplicity we will start with a non-interacting spinless fermion wave function
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
, and we assume that
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
is the ground state of a Hamiltonian
H_{0}
on a
d-
dim lattice:
H_{0}=\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}-t_{ij}c^{\dagger}_{i}c_{j}+\mathrm{h.c.}
We will first assume that
H_{0}
is a
gapless
free fermion tight-binding model, later we will discuss interactions in
H_{0}
.

We consider the situation where the jump operators are the local fermion density operators
L_{i}=\hat{n}_{i}=c^{\dagger}_{i}c_{i}
. The nonunitary evolution of a density matrix under a general Lindbladian reads (we assume that the unitary part of the evolution is absent)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho(t)=\sum_{i}L_{i}\rho L_{i}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left(L_{i}^{\dagger}L_{i}\rho+\rho L_{i}^{\dagger}L_{i}\right).

(2)

Exploring physics of mixed-states using the Choi-Jamiołkowski isomorphism of the density matrix, i.e. representing the density matrix as a pure quantum state in the doubled Hilbert space
[
14
,
15
]
, has attracted a great deal of interests
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
. The Choi-Jamiołkowski isomorphism maps a density matrix
\rho=\sum_{n}p_{n}|\psi_{n}\rangle\langle\psi_{n}|
to a state
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle\sim\sum_{n}p_{n}|\psi_{n,L}\rangle|\psi_{n,R}\rangle
. In our current case it is also convenient to take the Choi-Jamiołkowski representation of the density matrix. The evolution of the Choi state in the doubled Hilbert space is given by

\displaystyle|\rho_{t}\rangle\!\rangle\sim e^{t{\cal L}}|\rho_{0}\rangle\!\rangle,

(3)

where

\displaystyle{\cal L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\hat{n}_{i,L}\hat{n}_{i,R}-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{n}_{i,L}^{2}+\hat{n}_{i,R}^{2})

(4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{n}_{i,L}-\hat{n}_{i,R})^{2}.

(6)

Here
L
and
R
label two copies of fermionic modes in the doubled Fock space of the Choi-Jamiołkowski isomorphism of the density matrix. The initial Choi state
|\rho_{0}\rangle\!\rangle
for the pure state
\rho_{0}=\outerproduct{\Psi_0}{\Psi_0}
is the free fermion state with the parent Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}

\displaystyle=H_{0}(c_{i,L})+H_{0}^{*}(c_{i,R})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}-t_{ij}c_{i,L}^{\dagger}c_{j,L}-t^{*}_{ij}c_{i,R}^{\dagger}c_{j,R}+\mathrm{h.c.}.

(7)

In the limit
t\to\infty
, the steady state
|\rho_{\infty}\rangle\!\rangle
satisfies the constraint
\hat{n}_{i,L}-\hat{n}_{i,R}=0
.

To more explicitly reveal the implication of the constraint
\hat{n}_{i,L}-\hat{n}_{i,R}=0
, let us perform a particle-hole (PH) transformation on
c_{i,R}
, and formally relabel the fermions in the
L
,
R
spaces as “
\uparrow
” and “
\downarrow
”:

\displaystyle c_{i,L}\rightarrow f_{i,\uparrow},\ \ c_{i,R}\rightarrow\eta_{i}f^{\dagger}_{i,\downarrow},

(8)

(9)

\displaystyle\hat{n}_{i,L}\rightarrow\hat{n}_{i,\uparrow},\ \ \hat{n}_{i,R}\rightarrow 1-\hat{n}_{i,\downarrow},

(10)

where
\eta_{i}=\pm 1
can be chosen to depend on the sites
i
. Then in the limit
t\rightarrow\infty
, the constraint
\hat{n}_{i,L}-\hat{n}_{i,R}=0
becomes

\displaystyle\hat{n}_{i,\uparrow}+\hat{n}_{i,\downarrow}=1,

(11)

which is precisely the
Gutzwiller projection
.
In this case, the steady Choi state is mapped to a spin wave function in the standard spin liquid literature:

\displaystyle|\rho_{\infty}\rangle\!\rangle=\prod_{i}\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\hat{n}_{i,\uparrow}+\hat{n}_{i,\downarrow}=1)|\rho_{0}\rangle\!\rangle,

(12)

where
\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\hat{n}_{i,\uparrow}+\hat{n}_{i,\downarrow}=1)
is the projector onto the subspace with single occupation.
Since now on every site there is one and only one fermion
f_{i,\alpha}
, the projected wave function
|\rho_{\infty}\rangle\!\rangle
lives in an effective spin-1/2 Hilbert space, with spin-1/2 operators
\hat{S}^{\mu}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}f^{\dagger}_{i,\alpha}\sigma^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}f_{i,\beta}
. Under certain conditions, for example, (1) the fermions in
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
are at half-filling, i.e. there are on average 1/2 fermions per site; and (2) under particle-hole transformation
H_{0}^{\ast}
becomes
H_{0}
with a certain choice of
\eta_{i}
, then the parent tight-binding Hamiltonian of
|\rho_{0}\rangle\!\rangle
in the doubled Hilbert space enjoys a full effective
\mathrm{SU}(2)
“spin” symmetry. For example, if
H_{0}
is a tight-binding model on a bipartite lattice with nearest neighbor hopping, then choosing
\eta_{i}=-1
on one sublattice will result in a
\mathrm{SU}(2)
symmetric
\mathcal{H}_{0}
in Eq.
7
.

II.2
Construction with measurements

When the jump operators are Hermitian and all commute with each other, the steady state density matrix also describes the ensemble of wave functions generated by strong measurements on the jump operators
without post-selection
. For example, when we perform strong measurement of local densities
\hat{n}_{i}
on a pure fermion quantum state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
,
without
post-selecting the measurement outcomes, the density matrix of the ensemble becomes

\displaystyle\rho=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\hat{P}_{\mathbf{n}}\rho_{0}\hat{P}_{\mathbf{n}},

(13)

where
\mathbf{n}
represents a configuration of fermion numbers
\hat{n}_{i}
, and
\hat{P}_{\mathbf{n}}
is a projection operator to the particular configuration. Such an ensemble can be obtained in fermi gas microscope with high spatial resolution
[
31
,

,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
]
. The SM includes a detailed experimental protocol for verifying the predictions made in this work based on
\rho
generated by measurement.

We also note that, construction of spin liquids as the doubled state representation of a mixed state was discussed before
[

,

,

]
, but to the best of our knowledge, the general connection to the Gutzwiller wave function, which is a broadly used construction of spin liquids, has not been explored.

When the emergent gauge symmetry is U(1), and the fermions
f_{i,\alpha}
have Dirac nodes at the Fermi level, the low energy action describing the spin liquid is a QED
3
with Dirac fermion matter fields coupled to a
\mathrm{U}(1)
gauge field

Dirac spin liquids have attracted great interests

]
. The dynamical gauge field
A_{\mu}
may lead to confinement and hence destabilize the spin liquid states. In our construction, to ensure a deconfined phase of the gauge field, one can start with a state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
that is the ground state of
N
flavors of degenerate fermions
c_{i,I}
at half-filling, where
I=1\cdots N
. For example, one can start with a state of the alkaline earth cold atoms, which enjoy a large flavor symmetry
[

. Then eventually the effective Gutzwiller projection imposed on the mixed state in the doubled space is

\displaystyle\sum_{I=1\cdots N}\hat{n}_{i,I,\uparrow}+\hat{n}_{i,I,\downarrow}=N,

(14)

which ensures that
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
is a wave function of an effective
\mathrm{SU}(2N)
spin system, with self-conjugate representation of
\mathrm{SU}(2N)
on each site.

Assuming the dispersion of
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
has Dirac nodes in the momentum space with
N-
fold flavor degeneracy, the spin liquid that
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
simulates is described by the following theory:

{\cal S}=\int d^{2}xd\tau\sum_{v=1}^{N_{v}}\sum_{a=1}^{2N}\bar{\psi}_{a,v}\gamma_{\mu}(\partial_{\mu}-\mathrm{i}A_{\mu})\psi_{a,v}+\frac{1}{4e^{2}}F_{\mu\nu}^{2}.

(15)

Here
v
labels the
N_{v}
Dirac nodes/valleys in the momentum space. For example, if
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
is the ground state of a nearest neighbor tight binding model on the honeycomb lattice at half-filling, or on the square lattice with
\pi-
flux per square, then
N_{v}=2
, i.e. there are two independent Dirac cones in the momentum space. It is known that for large enough
N
, Eq.
15
describes a stable algebraic liquid state which is also a
(2+1)d
conformal field theory. We refer to this liquid state in the doubled Hilbert space as the algebraic Choi-spin liquid.

Predictions for the Choi-spin liquid described above can be made based on our understanding of the spin liquid. Let us still start with a SU(
2N
) spin liquid on the square lattice with
\pi-
flux, whose low energy physics is described by QED
3
in Eq.
15
. The local spin operator
\hat{S}^{z}_{i}=\sum_{I=1\cdots N}\hat{n}_{i,I,\uparrow}-\hat{n}_{i,I,\downarrow}
has a nonzero overlap with the fermion-bilinear composite field
\bar{\psi}S^{z}\mu^{z}\psi
, where
\mu^{z}=\pm 1
denotes the two Dirac valleys.
\bar{\psi}S^{z}\mu^{z}\psi
is an
\mathrm{SU}(2N)
Néel order parameter. Then at long distance the spin correlation functions is given by

\displaystyle\langle\hat{S}^{z}_{0}\ \hat{S}^{z}_{{\bm{x}}}\rangle\sim(-1)^{{\bm{x}}}\frac{1}{|{\bm{x}}|^{2\Delta}}+\cdots.

(16)

Note that the sign of the correlation function above oscillates depending on the sublattice of
{\bm{x}}
. With sufficiently large
N
, the scaling dimension
\Delta
can be computed using the standard
1/N
expansion, and it is smaller than the scaling dimension of the free Dirac fermion
[

:

\displaystyle\Delta=2-\frac{32}{3NN_{v}\pi^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}\right).

(17)

There is also a non-oscillating (ferromagnetic) part of the correlation in Eq.
16

\Delta^{\prime}=2

It was shown that

17

The
\mathrm{SU}(2N)

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\rho|\delta\hat{n}_{0,\uparrow}\ \delta\hat{n}_{{\bm{x}},\uparrow}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle\sim\langle\!\langle\rho|\hat{S}^{z}_{0}\ \hat{S}^{z}_{\bm{x}}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle

(18)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\tr\left(\rho^{2}\delta\hat{n}_{0}\ \delta\hat{n}_{{\bm{x}}}\right)\sim(-1)^{{\bm{x}}}\frac{1}{|{\bm{x}}|^{2\Delta}}.

(20)

Here
\delta\hat{n}({\bm{x}})=\hat{n}({\bm{x}})-N/2
, and we have used the fact that
\delta n_{\uparrow}({\bm{x}})\sim\hat{S}^{z}_{{\bm{x}}}/2
, since
\hat{n}_{\uparrow}({\bm{x}})+\hat{n}_{\downarrow}({\bm{x}})
is a constant.
Since the Choi state
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
has a
\mathrm{SU}(2N)
symmetry, all the
\mathrm{SU}(2N)

\displaystyle\tr\left(\rho^{2}\ (\hat{O}_{0})^{I}_{J}\ (\hat{O}_{{\bm{x}}})^{J}_{I}\right)

(21)

is expected to decay in the same manner as Eq.

for sufficiently large

\hat{O}^{I}_{J}=c^{\dagger}_{I}c_{J}
with

is a
\mathrm{SU}(N)
operator that operates on the index

does not change the total number configuration

IV
SU(2) gauge symmetry and superconductivity

It is well-known in the field of spin liquid that (see for example Ref.
[
48
]
), a U(1) projection may actually lead to SU(2) gauge symmetry. In our context, for a class of free fermion states
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
with
N=1
whose parent Hamiltonian
H_{0}
only has real hopping
t_{ij}
, the seemingly
\mathrm{U}(1)
gauge projection in the doubled space
\prod_{i}\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\hat{n}_{i,\uparrow}+\hat{n}_{i,\downarrow}=1)
would also lead to a SU(2) gauge symmetry. For example, the
\pi-
flux state with
N=1
in Fig.
1
actually has SU(2) gauge symmetry.

To expose the explicit
\mathrm{SU}(2)
gauge symmetry, we no longer need the PH transformation of
H^{*}_{0}(c_{i,R})
. Instead, we just need to (trivially) relabel
L\rightarrow 1
,
R\rightarrow 2
, then the
\mathrm{U}(1)
gauge projection
\hat{n}_{i,1}-\hat{n}_{i,2}=0
automatically implies a SU(2) gauge constraint:

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}_{i,\alpha}\tau^{l}_{\alpha,\beta}c_{i,\beta}=0,\ \ \ l=1,2,3.

(22)

The reason is that, in the fock space of
c_{i,1}
and
c_{i,2}
, the only states that survive the projection are
|0,0\rangle_{i}
, and
c_{i,1}^{\dagger}c_{i,2}^{\dagger}|0,0\rangle_{i}
, both are singlets under a local SU(2) rotation on
[c_{i,1};c_{i,2}]
.

There are a few important points to keep in mind:

(1) This SU(2) gauge structure
only
emerges when
N=1
, i.e. when the original state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
is a spinless fermion state, and there are two flavors of fermions in the doubled Hilbert space.

(2) The SU(2) gauge symmetry is
independent
from the effective SU(2) global spin symmetry, i.e. one can explicitly break the SU(2) spin symmetry, but still keep the SU(2) gauge symmetry.

(3) The gauge symmetry may be broken down to a smaller gauge group, depending on the original state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
, or its parent Hamiltonian
H_{0}

[
48
]
. More specifically, the parent Hamiltonians for
|\rho_{0}\rangle\!\rangle
is
H_{0}(c_{i,L})+H_{0}^{\ast}(c_{i,R})
. If
H_{0}
is real (i.e. the hopping amplitudes
t_{ij}
are real), then the SU(2) gauge symmetry is preserved in the infrared.

V
Summary and Discussion

In this work, we demonstrated that starting with a simple fermion wave function, the steady state density matrix of the Lindbladian evolution, or the ensemble generated from strong measurement of local densities, is a Gutzwiller projected wave function in the doubled Fock space. The Gutzwiller projection has been broadly used as a numerical trial wave function of spin liquid. We propose that this new construction of spin liquids can be realized in real experimental platforms, e.g. the Fermi gas microscope.
We also predict that in certain scenarios, the constructed mixed state has a strong-to-weak spontaneous
\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry breaking, which corresponds to a superconductor in the doubled Fock space. Predictions made by our understanding of spin liquids, as well as the detailed measurement protocol of our predictions are described in the SM.

So far we have assumed that
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
is the ground state of a free fermion tight-binding model. We expect most of our predictions to be robust against a certain amount of short-range interaction in
H_{0}
. For example, when
H_{0}
is a nearest neighbor tight-binding model of
N
flavors of fermions
c_{I}
on the honeycomb lattice at half-filling, the Choi states
|\rho_{0}\rangle\!\rangle
and
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
should have an exact
\mathrm{SU}(2N)
symmetry, which guarantees that the correlations in Eq.
20
and Eq.
21
take the same form. If
H_{0}
has weak short-range interactions, then the Choi states no longer have an exact
\mathrm{SU}(2N)
symmetry. We expect the Dirac fermion
\psi
in Eq.
15
to inherit extra interactions from
H_{0}
. But since short-range interactions in Eq.
15
are perturbatively irrelevant for large enough
N
, the
\mathrm{SU}(2N)
symmetry is expected to emerge in the infrared, and the correlations in Eq.
20
and Eq.
21
should still have the same scaling dimension
\Delta
for large
{\bm{x}}
. For smaller
N
, some short-range interactions can become relevant due to the gauge fluctuation
[
77
,
78
,
79
,
80
,
81
,
82
]
.
For a non-interacting initial state
\rho_{0}=\outerproduct{\Psi_0}{\Psi_0}
, the correlator in the spin liquid state
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
actually stems from observables in the free fermion wave function
|\rho_{0}\rangle\!\rangle
, which can be computed on classical computers. For an initial gapless state with short-range interactions, we propose a combined classical-classical correlator and quantum-classical correlator to make predictions on Eq.
20
, Eq.
21
for smaller
N
(please refer to the SM).

In this work we have focused on mixed states prepared from initial fermion states
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
that are
gapless
. Gapped fermion states can also be very interesting – even free fermion insulators can have nontrivial topological nature, e.g. the Chern insulator. The Choi-representation of the Chern insulator in the doubled space is a quantum spin Hall state, and the procedure proposed in this work will lead to a Gutzwiller projection of the QSH wave function. We leave the generalizations of our work to topological insulators, including a discussion of the relation between Gutzwiller wave function and gauge theory to a future work.

Appendix A
Measurement of Rényi-2 correlation

In this appendix, we develop protocols to measure the Rényi-2 correlations in the Gutzwiller projected doubled state
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
,

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\rho|\delta\hat{n}_{0,\uparrow}\ \delta\hat{n}_{{\bm{x}},\uparrow}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\rho|\hat{S}^{z}_{0}\ \hat{S}^{z}_{\bm{x}}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle

(24)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\tr\left(\rho^{2}\ \delta\hat{n}_{0}\ \delta\hat{n}_{{\bm{x}}}\right),

(26)

here
\delta\hat{n}_{{\bm{x}}}=\hat{n}_{\bm{x}}-\bar{n}
. Our goal is to verify that
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
is an algebraic spin liquid state, meaning that the correlator above decays as
\sim 1/|{\bm{x}}|^{2\Delta}
, and we would like to extract the scaling dimension
\Delta
.

If state
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
is prepared from a
free
fermion state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
after measuring the fermion density configurations, i.e.
\rho
is a free fermion state subject to complete dephasing, the Rényi-2 correlator can be computed on a classical computer.
However, if
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
is an interacting fermion state,
directly computing the Rényi-2 correlator is generally challenging.
We here provide a scheme to verify the spin liquid prediction prepared from fermion states
with or without
short-range interactions. We note that experimentally one can prepare two identical copies of the state and measure the SWAP operator to evaluate Rényi-2 quantities
[
83
,
84
,
85
]
. We would like to propose a different method that avoids duplicating the system, and it only requires making measurement of local density configurations.

Our protocol makes use of the decomposition of the density matrix
\rho
as an ensemble of pure states generated by the measurements of local fermion occupation
\hat{n}_{i}=\sum_{\alpha}\hat{n}_{i,\alpha}

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}q_{\mathbf{n}}\outerproduct{\Psi_{\mathbf{n}}}{\Psi_{\mathbf{n}}}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\hat{P}_{\mathbf{n}}\outerproduct{\Psi_0}{\Psi_0}\hat{P}_{\mathbf{n}},

(27)

where
\mathbf{n}
is a vector that labels the fermion occupation on each site,
\ket{\Psi_{\mathbf{n}}}
is the normalized post-measurement state,
\hat{P}_{\mathbf{n}}
is the projector onto the subspace with occupation number
\mathbf{n}
, and
q_{\mathbf{n}}=\bra{\Psi_0}\hat{P}_{\mathbf{n}}\ket{\Psi_0}
is the probability of measurement outcome
\mathbf{n}
. To proceed, we need to first define the “classical-classical” correlator, and the “quantum-classical” correlator, motivated from Ref.
[
86
]
.

A.1
The “classical-classical” correlator

We first consider a “classical-classical” (CC) correlator defined with the projected
free fermion
state
|\tilde{\rho}\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\delta\hat{n}_{0}\ \delta\hat{n}_{{\bm{x}}}\rangle\!\rangle_{\tilde{\rho}}=\frac{\tr\tilde{\rho}^{2}\ \delta\hat{n}_{0}\ \delta\hat{n}_{{\bm{x}}}}{\tr\tilde{\rho}^{2}}=\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{n}}p_{\mathbf{n}}^{2}\ \delta n_{0}\ \delta n_{{\bm{x}}}}{\sum_{\mathbf{n}}p_{\mathbf{n}}^{2}},

(28)

where
\langle\!\langle\delta\hat{n}_{0}\ \delta\hat{n}_{{\bm{x}}}\rangle\!\rangle_{\tilde{\rho}}:=\mbox{$\langle\!\langle\tilde{\rho}|$}\delta\hat{n}_{0}\ \delta\hat{n}_{{\bm{x}}}\mbox{$|\tilde{\rho}\rangle\!\rangle$}
,
p_{\mathbf{n}}=\langle\tilde{\Psi}_{0}|\hat{P}_{\mathbf{n}}|\tilde{\Psi}_{0}\rangle
denotes the probability of outcome
\mathbf{n}
in the
free fermion
state
|\tilde{\Psi}_{0}\rangle
.
We stress that
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
in the previous subsection is the ground state of a gapless Hamiltonian
H_{0}
(which can include short-range interactions) for
N-
flavors of fermions
c_{I}
with an
\mathrm{SU}(N)
symmetry, while
|\tilde{\Psi}_{0}\rangle
is the free fermion ground state of the non-interacting part (fermion-bilinear part) of
H_{0}
.
Specifically, we first sample from
|\tilde{\Psi}_{0}\rangle
in the local occupation basis independently for
M
times, which can be efficiently performed on a classical computer
[
87
,
88
,
89
]
.
Given the outcome
\mathbf{n}_{m}
obtained in the
m
-th sample, we compute
p_{\mathbf{n}_{m}}
.
Taking
M
independent samples, one can obtain an estimate of the CC-correlator, i.e.

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\delta\hat{n}_{0}\ \delta\hat{n}_{{\bm{x}}}\rangle\!\rangle_{\tilde{\rho}}=\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}p_{\mathbf{n}_{m}}\delta n_{0,m}\ \delta n_{{\bm{x}},m}}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}p_{\mathbf{n}_{m}}}.

(29)

Using the same set of samples, we can compute
\langle\tilde{\Psi}_{0}|\hat{P}_{\mathbf{n}_{m}}(\hat{O}_{0})^{I}_{J}\ (\hat{O}_{{\bm{x}}})^{J}_{I}|\tilde{\Psi}_{0}\rangle
on the classical computer and obtain the Rényi-2 correlator in Eq.14,

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle(\hat{O}_{0})^{I}_{J}(\hat{O}_{{\bm{x}}})^{J}_{I}\rangle\!\rangle_{\tilde{\rho}}

(30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\langle\tilde{\Psi}_{0}|\hat{P}_{\mathbf{n}_{m}}(\hat{O}_{0})^{I}_{J}(\hat{O}_{{\bm{x}}})^{J}_{I}|\tilde{\Psi}_{0}\rangle}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}p_{\mathbf{n}_{m}}}.

(32)

Here, we use the fact that
(\hat{O}_{{\bm{x}}})_{J}^{I}=c_{I,{\bm{x}}}^{\dagger}c_{J,{\bm{x}}}
does not change the fermion occupation
\mathbf{n}
.
In Eq.
29
and Eq.
32
, since both sampling and computation are performed on classical computers, we refer to them as the classical-classical (CC) correlator.

A.2
The “quantum-classical” correlator

As the second step, we consider the following “quantum-classical” (QC) correlator, which is defined as the Rényi-2 correlator in the doubled state associated with

\displaystyle\sigma=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\hat{P}_{\mathbf{n}}\ket{\Psi_0}\langle\tilde{\Psi}_{0}|\hat{P}_{\mathbf{n}},

(33)

\displaystyle\mbox{$|\sigma\rangle\!\rangle$}=\prod_{i}\hat{\mathcal{P}}(n_{i,L}=n_{i,R})|\Psi_{0}\rangle_{L}|\tilde{\Psi}\rangle_{R}.

(34)

Note that in the doubled space,
|\Psi_{0}\rangle_{L}
is the
experimentally prepared
gapless fermion state which can be interacting; while
|\tilde{\Psi}\rangle_{R}
is the
free fermion state
that is the ground state of the noninteracting part of the parent Hamiltonian of
|\Psi_{0}\rangle_{L}
.
|\sigma\rangle\!\rangle
is a Gutzwiller projection on
2N
flavors of fermions, but
N
flavors of the fermions (in
|\tilde{\Psi}_{0}\rangle
) are non-interacting.

The Rényi-2 correlator of
\sigma
then takes the form

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\delta\hat{n}_{0}\ \delta\hat{n}_{{\bm{x}}}\rangle\!\rangle_{\sigma}=\frac{\tr\sigma^{\dagger}\sigma\ \delta\hat{n}_{0}\ \delta\hat{n}_{{\bm{x}}}}{\tr\sigma^{\dagger}\sigma}=\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{n}}q_{\mathbf{n}}p_{\mathbf{n}}\ \delta n_{0}\ \delta n_{{\bm{x}}}}{\sum_{\mathbf{n}}q_{\mathbf{n}}p_{\mathbf{n}}},

(35)

where
q_{\mathbf{n}}
is the probability of fermion occupation
\mathbf{n}
in the interacting wave function
\ket{\Psi_0}
prepared experimentally in quantum simulator.
The quantity can be evaluated by first sampling from the interacting wave function
\ket{\Psi_0}
on a quantum simulator (governed by the probability distribution
\{q_{\mathbf{n}}\}
) and then computing the corresponding
p_{\mathbf{n}}
in the free fermion state
|\tilde{\Psi}_{0}\rangle
on a classical computer.
We then have the following estimate of the quantum-classical correlator

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\delta\hat{n}_{0}\delta\hat{n}_{{\bm{x}}}\rangle\!\rangle_{\sigma}=\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}p_{\mathbf{n}_{m}}\delta n_{0,m}\delta n_{{\bm{x}},m}}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}p_{\mathbf{n}_{m}}}.

(36)

A.3
Comparison between the CC and QC correlators

To make predictions regarding the desired quantity Eq.
26
, we will need both the CC-correlator Eq.
28
and QC correlator Eq.
35
. Here we argue that, if the QC and CC correlators exhibit the same power-law scaling, this strongly indicates that the experimentally generated
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
is also a spin liquid state, with the same power-law correlations.

It is well-known that, for large enough
N
, all the perturbations of short-range four-fermion interactions in the QED
3
theory (Eq.11 of the main text) are irrelevant. But with smaller
N
, some short-range interactions can become relevant and destabilize the spin liquid described by Eq.11. The scaling dimensions of the short-range interactions depend on
N
, and also the symmetry of the four fermion interactions
[
77
,
78
,
79
,
80
,
81
,
82
]
.

Hence we need to compare the exact symmetry of the CC-correlator generated by
|\tilde{\rho}\rangle\!\rangle
, QC-correlator generated by
|\sigma\rangle\!\rangle
, and the quantum-quantum (QQ) correlator Eq.
26
which corresponds to the experimentally prepared
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
. As an example, let us still start with a
H_{0}
with
N
flavors of fermions and nearest neighbor hopping on the honeycomb lattice, as well as some local density-density interactions preserving the
\mathrm{SU}(N)
flavor symmetry. The CC-correlator based on the projected non-interacting state
|\tilde{\rho}\rangle\!\rangle
has an exact
\mathrm{SU}(2N)
symmetry on the lattice, as we discussed in the main text; the QQ-correlator which corresponds to the projected interacting state
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
has an exact
(\mathrm{SU}(N)_{L}\times\mathrm{SU}(N)_{R})\rtimes Z_{2}
symmetry, where the
Z_{2}
exchanges the left and right space. While the QC-correlator evaluated with the Choi state
|\sigma\rangle\!\rangle
has the
lowest

\mathrm{SU}(N)_{L}\times\mathrm{SU}(N)_{R}
symmetry. Though all three kinds of correlators may correspond to field theory Eq.11 with perturbations (extra four-fermion interactions), since
|\sigma\rangle\!\rangle
has the lowest symmetry, the field theory corresponding to
|\sigma\rangle\!\rangle
has
the most
four-fermion terms in addition to Eq.11. If the QC and the CC-correlator yield the same power-law decay with the same scaling dimension, the only natural possibility is that the local interactions in both QC and the CC-correlators are irrelevant; this would imply that the local interactions in QQ-correlator are also irrelevant since the QQ-correlator has higher symmetry than the QC-correlator, and must have fewer perturbations of local interaction. This means that
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
prepared experimentally should indeed be an algebraic spin liquid state controlled by the fixed point described by Eq.11.

We can also evaluate the Cooper pair correlation in Eq. 17 in a similar way. For example, the CC-correlator of Eq.17 reads

\displaystyle\frac{\tr c_{0}^{\dagger}\tilde{\rho}c_{0}c_{{\bm{x}}}^{\dagger}\tilde{\rho}c_{{\bm{x}}}}{\tr\tilde{\rho}^{2}}=\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}p_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}_{m}}}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}p_{\mathbf{n}_{m}}},

(37)

where, for an outcome
\mathbf{n}_{m}=(n_{0,m},\cdots,n_{{\bm{x}},m},\cdots)
,
\mathbf{n}^{\prime}_{m}=(n_{0,m}+1,\cdots,n_{{\bm{x}},m}-1,\cdots)
.
In this case, we note that the fermion on each site has a single flavor (
N=1
), and the mixed state
\tilde{\rho}
is classical, i.e. a diagonal matrix. The QC-correlator of Eq.17 can be constructed accordingly. The only difference is that, for an experimentally generated configuration
\mathbf{n}_{m}
, we need to compute the probability
p_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}_{m}}
from a classical computer, for a slightly different configuration
\mathbf{n}^{\prime}_{m}
.

A.4
Summary of Experimental Protocol

Here we make a summary of the proposed experimental protocol for measuring the desired quantity Eq.
26
:

•

Prepare a fermion state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
(cold atoms in optical lattice) that is the ground state of Hamiltonian
H_{0}
.
H_{0}
can have short-range interactions, and its spectrum is gapless.

•

In each experimental run, measure the fermion density on each site, obtain configuration
\mathbf{n}_{m}
.

•

Input
\mathbf{n}_{m}
in computer, find
p_{\mathbf{n}_{m}}=\langle\tilde{\Psi}_{0}|\hat{P}_{\mathbf{n}_{m}}|\tilde{\Psi}_{0}\rangle
, where
|\tilde{\Psi}_{0}\rangle
is the free fermion ground state of the noninteracting part of
H_{0}
.

•

We average
p_{\mathbf{n}_{m}}\delta n_{0,m}\delta n_{{\bm{x}},m}
over all experimental runs like Eq.
36
, and obtain the QC-correlator.

•

We separately compute the CC-correlator using
|\tilde{\Psi}_{0}\rangle
. If the CC-correlator and QC-correlator have the same power-law scaling, based on the arguments in the previous subsection, we verify that the experimentally prepared
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
is a spin liquid state, and its scaling is given by the QC and CC correlator.

B.1
Spinon Fermi surface state

The proposed experimental protocol discussed in the previous section also allows us to experimentally extract information of other gapless liquid states, for example, the spinon Fermi surface state, and the
d
-wave Bose liquid phase
[
13
,
90
]
.

The spinon Fermi surface state is among the most broadly studied spin liquid states, due to the compelling potential connection to condensed matter experiments. In our context, to access the physics of the gapless spinon Fermi surface state, we can follow the following protocol:

•

Prepare a spinless fermion state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
that is a gapless state at half-filling with a Fermi surface, for example, it can be chosen as the ground state of the tight-binding model on the triangular lattice at half-filling.
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
can have short-range interactions.

•

Again, in each experimental run, measure the fermion density on each site, obtain configuration
\mathbf{n}_{m}
.

•

For each experimentally produced configuration
\mathbf{n}_{m}
, we define
\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{m}
, and
\tilde{n}_{{\bm{x}},m}=1-n_{{\bm{x}},m}
.

•

Input
\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{m}
into computer, find
p_{\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{m}}=\langle\tilde{\Psi}_{0}|\hat{P}_{\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{m}}|\tilde{\Psi}_{0}\rangle
, where
|\tilde{\Psi}_{0}\rangle
is the free fermion ground state of the noninteracting part of the parent Hamiltonian of
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
.

•

We average
p_{\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{m}}\delta n_{0,m}\delta n_{{\bm{x}},m}
over all experimental runs, and this quantity should converge to the spin correlation function of the following state:

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{m}p_{\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{m}}\delta n_{0,m}\delta n_{{\bm{x}},m}}{\sum_{m}p_{\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{m}}}\sim\langle\!\langle\sigma|S^{z}_{0}\ S^{z}_{{\bm{x}}}|\sigma\rangle\!\rangle,

(38)

and state
|\sigma\rangle\!\rangle
is

\displaystyle|\sigma\rangle\!\rangle\sim\prod_{i}\hat{\mathcal{P}}(n_{i,L}+\tilde{n}_{i,R}=1)|\Psi_{0}\rangle_{L}|\tilde{\Psi}_{0}\rangle_{R}.

(39)

We can view
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
and
|\tilde{\Psi}_{0}\rangle
as the wave function for spin-up and spin-down spinons.
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
and
|\tilde{\Psi}_{0}\rangle
have the same Fermi surface. If
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
is a free fermion state with a finite Fermi surface, e.g. the ground state of a half-filling tight-binding model on the triangular lattice, the constructed
|\sigma\rangle
has a spin SU(2) symmetry. If
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
has short-range interactions,
|\sigma\rangle\!\rangle
is a special state where the spin-up and down spinons have different interactions, but still the same Fermi surface.

Ref.

\displaystyle b_{i}=d_{i,x}d_{i,y},

and

Appendix C
More general QCD
3
Choi-spin liquids

In the main text We have discussed that the QCD with an emergent SU(2) gauge field may be realized in the Choi state after a Lindbladian evolution when the jump operators are local density operators. In this section, we will demonstrate that QCD with a more general
\mathrm{SU}(k)
gauge field can also be realized in a similar manner, by choosing a different set of jump operators. Now let us consider the initial state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
which is a free fermion state of
kN
flavors of fermions. We label the fermions as
c_{i,l,J}
, where
l=1\cdots k
and
J=1\cdots N
. We choose the jump operator as

\displaystyle L^{\mu}_{i}=\hat{T}^{\mu}_{i}=c^{\dagger}_{i,l,J}T^{\mu}_{ll^{\prime}}c_{i,l^{\prime},J},

(42)

where
T^{\mu}
with
\mu=1\cdots k^{2}-1
are the generators of
\mathrm{SU}(k)
. Please note that
\hat{T}^{\mu}_{i}
are all Hermitian operators. We can design the Lindbladian in the Choi-Jamiołkowski representation as

{\cal L}=\sum_{i,\mu}-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{T}^{\mu}_{i,1}-(\hat{T}^{\mu}_{i,2})^{T})^{2}=\sum_{i,\mu}-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{T}^{\mu}_{i,1}-(\hat{T}^{\mu}_{i,2})^{\ast})^{2}.

(43)

Here
(\hat{T}^{\mu}_{i,2})^{\ast}=c^{\dagger}_{i,l,J}(T^{\mu}_{ll^{\prime}})^{\ast}c_{i,l^{\prime},J}
.

The fermions in the left and right spaces form a pair of complex-conjugate representations of
\mathrm{SU}(k)
, and in the long-time limit, the Lindbladian acts as a
\mathrm{SU}(k)
Gutzwiller projection in the doubled Hilbert space, as it demands
\hat{T}^{\mu}_{i,1}-(\hat{T}^{\mu}_{i,2})^{\ast}=0
on every site:

\displaystyle|\rho\rangle\!\rangle=\prod_{i}\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\mathrm{SU}(k))_{i}|\rho_{0}\rangle\!\rangle,

(44)

where
\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\mathrm{SU}(k))_{i}
is a projection to the
\mathrm{SU}(k)
singlet on every site
i
. It is hence expected that the steady state is a QCD with
2N
flavors of fermions coupled with a
\mathrm{SU}(k)
gauge field. Note that in
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle

N
flavors of the fermions (each carrying a “color” index
l=1\cdots k
) form the fundamental rep of the
\mathrm{SU}(k)
, while the other
N
flavors form an anti-fundamental representation.

Appendix D
Doubled space representation of fermion density matrix

Here, we detail the mapping from the fermion density matrix to the pure state in the doubled Fock space
[
91
]
.
The mapping is slightly more complicated than mapping the density operator in quantum spin models to the doubled space because two copies of the fermionic operators in the doubled Fock space have mutual fermionic statistics.

We first express the fermionic density matrix in the Fock basis as

\displaystyle\rho(c_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{n}_{L},\mathbf{n}_{R}}\rho_{\mathbf{n}_{L},\mathbf{n}_{R}}\ket{\mathbf{n}_L}\bra{\mathbf{n}_{R}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{n}_{L},\mathbf{n}_{R}}\rho_{\mathbf{n}_{L},\mathbf{n}_{R}}\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{n}_{L}}\outerproduct{\text{vac}}{\text{vac}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{n}_{R}},

(45)

where
\mathbf{n}_{L(R)}=(\{n_{i,L(R)}\})
is a binary vector representing the occupation number of fermionic modes,
\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{n}_{L}}:=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(c_{i}^{\dagger})^{n_{i,L}}
, and
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{n}_{R}}:=\prod_{i=N}^{1}(c_{i})^{n_{i,R}}
.

We now map the fermionic density matrix
\rho
to a pure state
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
in the doubled Fock space which contains two copies of the fermionic mode
c_{i,L}
and
c_{i,R}
.
We identify the fermonic mode
c_{i}
in
\rho
with
c_{i,L}
in the doubled Fock space and obtain the doubled state
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
by applying
\rho(c_{i,L})
as an operator to a reference state in the doubled Fock space, i.e.
\mbox{$|\rho\rangle\!\rangle$}:=\rho(c_{i,L})\mbox{$|\mathcal{I}\rangle\!\rangle$}
.
Here, we choose the following reference state

\displaystyle\mbox{$|\mathcal{I}\rangle\!\rangle$}:=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}(c_{i,L}^{\dagger})^{n_{i}}(c_{i,R}^{\dagger})^{n_{i}}\mbox{$|\text{vac}\rangle\!\rangle$}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(1+c_{i,L}^{\dagger}c_{i,R}^{\dagger}\right)\mbox{$|\text{vac}\rangle\!\rangle$}.

(46)

Accordingly, the doubled state
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
takes the form

\displaystyle\mbox{$|\rho\rangle\!\rangle$}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}_{L},\mathbf{n}_{R}}\rho_{\mathbf{n}_{L},\mathbf{n}_{R}}\left(\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{n}_{L}}\right)_{L}\left(\mathcal{O}^{T}_{\mathbf{n}_{R}}\right)_{R}\mbox{$|\text{vac}\rangle\!\rangle$}.

(47)

Here, we introduce the transpose operation
(\cdot)^{T}
, which acts linearly on the fermionic operators,

\displaystyle c_{i}^{T}=c_{i}^{\dagger},\quad(c_{i}^{\dagger})^{T}=-c_{i},

(48)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}c_{i}+\beta_{i}c_{i}^{\dagger}\right)^{T}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}c_{i}^{\dagger}-\beta_{i}c_{i}.

(49)

When acting on a product
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{n}_{1}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{n}_{2}}
with
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{n}_{1,2}}
individually being a product of fermionic operators defined above, we have

\displaystyle(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{n}_{1}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{n}_{2}})^{T}={\rm sgn}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{n}_{1}},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{n}_{2}})\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{n}_{2}}^{T}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{n}_{1}}^{T},

(50)

where
{\rm sgn}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{n}_{1}},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{n}_{2}})=\pm 1
, it takes
-1
value only if both
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{n}_{1}}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{n}_{2}}
are fermionic (i.e. contain odd number of fermion operators).
One can verify that for any operator
(\mathcal{O}^{T})^{T}=\mathcal{O}
.
According to this definition, we have the following relation for operators acting on the reference state

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{L}\mbox{$|\mathcal{I}\rangle\!\rangle$}=(\mathcal{O}^{T})_{R}\mbox{$|\mathcal{I}\rangle\!\rangle$}.

(51)

In this work, we focused on an initial pure Gaussian (noninteracting) fermionic state evolved under the Lindblad equation.
Specifically, the initial pure state is
\rho_{0}=\outerproduct{\Psi_0}{\Psi_0}
with

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_0}=\prod_{k=1}^{N}(c_{k}^{\dagger})^{n_{k}}\ket{\text{vac}}.

(52)

Here,
c_{k}^{\dagger}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{*}c_{i}^{\dagger}
(
c_{k}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}c_{i}
) is a linear combination of fermionic creation operators on each site, e.g. the creation operator of fermion with momentum
k
.
The corresponding doubled state is given by

\displaystyle\mbox{$|\rho_{0}\rangle\!\rangle$}=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\left(c_{k,L}^{\dagger}c_{k,R}^{T}\right)^{n_{k}}\mbox{$|\text{vac}\rangle\!\rangle$}.

(53)

Here,
c_{k}^{T}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}c_{i}^{\dagger}
.
Accordingly, the Lindblad evolution in the doubled Fock space is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mbox{$|\rho\rangle\!\rangle$}=\left(\sum_{i}(L_{i})_{L}(L_{i}^{\dagger})_{R}^{T}-\frac{1}{2}(L_{i}^{\dagger}L_{i})_{L}-\frac{1}{2}(L_{i}^{\dagger}L_{i})_{R}^{T}\right)\mbox{$|\rho\rangle\!\rangle$}.

(54)

Before closing, we remark that the choice of the reference state is not unique.
Alternatively, one can map
\rho\mapsto\rho(c_{i,L})\mbox{$|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle$}
with

\displaystyle\mbox{$|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle$}:=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}(c_{i,L}^{\dagger})^{n_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}(c_{i,R}^{\dagger})^{n_{i}}\mbox{$|\text{vac}\rangle\!\rangle$}.

However, such a choice is not convenient when considering the symmetry transformation that rotates between
c_{i,L}^{\dagger}
and
c_{i,R}^{\dagger}
.
More specifically, the reference state
|\mathcal{I}\rangle\!\rangle
has an
\mathrm{SU}(2)
symmetry generated by
\hat{S}^{\mu}:=\sum_{i}\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\sigma^{\mu}\mathbf{c}_{i}
, where
\mathbf{c}^{\dagger}_{i}=[c_{i,L}^{\dagger},c_{i,R}^{\dagger}]
.
Such a symmetry would have to act nonlocally in the alternative reference state
|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle
due to the mutual fermionic statistics between
c_{i,L}
and
c_{j,R}
.
To make this
\mathrm{SU}(2)
symmetry explicit and draw a direct connection to Gutzwiller projected wave function, we choose the reference state
|\mathcal{I}\rangle\!\rangle
throughout this paper.
