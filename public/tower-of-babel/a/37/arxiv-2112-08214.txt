Morse index for the ground state in the energy supercritical Gross–Pitaevskii equation
Dmitry E. Pelinovsky, Szymon Sobieszek

Abstract
. The ground state of the energy super-critical Gross–Pitaevskii equation with a harmonic potential converges in the energy space to the singular solution in the limit of large amplitudes. The ground state can be represented by a solution curve which has either oscillatory or monotone behavior, depending on the dimension of the system and the power of the focusing nonlinearity. We address here the monotone case for the cubic nonlinearity in the spatial dimensions d ≥ 13 d\geq 13 . By using the shooting method for the radial Schrödinger operators, we prove that the Morse index of the ground state is finite and is independent of the (large) amplitude. It is equal to the Morse index of the limiting singular solution, which can be computed from numerical approximations. The numerical results suggest that the Morse index of the ground state is one and that it is stable in the time evolution of the cubic Gross–Pitaevskii equation in dimensions d ≥ 13 d\geq 13 .



1.
Introduction

We consider the stationary Gross-Pitaevskii equation with a harmonic potential,

(-\Delta u+|x|^{2})u-|u|^{2p}u=\lambda u\,,

(1.1)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, and
u\in\mathbb{R}
. Existence of its ground state (a positive and radially decreasing solution) has been addressed before in the energy subcritical
[
7
,
13
]
, critical
[
20
]
, and supercritical
[
21
,
22
]
regimes, where the critical exponent is
p=\frac{2}{d-2}
if
d\geq 3
. We are concerned here with the energy supercritical case of the focusing Gross–Pitaevskii equation. Scattering in the defocusing version of energy supercritical equations
was studied in
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
.

The stationary Gross–Pitaevskii equation (
1.1
) is the Euler–Lagrange equation for the action functional
\Lambda_{\lambda}(u)=E(u)-\lambda M(u)
, where
E(u)
and
M(u)
are the energy and mass given by

M(w)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u|^{2}dx

(1.2)

and

E(w)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(|\nabla u|^{2}+|x|^{2}|u|^{2}-\frac{1}{p+1}|u|^{2p+2}\right)dx.

(1.3)

The energy and mass are formally the conserved quantities in the evolution of the time-dependent Gross–Pitaevskii equation. They are defined in the energy space
\mathcal{E}\cap L^{2p+2}_{r}
, where

\mathcal{E}:=\left\{u\in L^{2}_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{+}):\quad u^{\prime}\in L^{2}_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{+}),\quad ru\in L^{2}_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\right\}

(1.4)

and
L^{q}_{r}
denotes the space of radially symmetric
L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
functions.

The ground state of the stationary equation (
1.1
) can be obtained variationally in the energy subcritical case
p(d-2)<2
, but the variational methods are not applicable in the energy critical and supercritical cases
p(d-2)\geq 2
if
d\geq 3
. In all cases,
the ground state forms a family which appears as the curve on the
(\lambda,b)
plane, where
b:=u(0)\equiv\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}
is referred to as
the amplitude
. The large-amplitude limit is the limit as
b\to\infty
. In the energy supercritical regime, it was proven in
[
22
]
that there exists
\lambda_{\infty}\in(0,d)
such that
\lambda\to\lambda_{\infty}
as
b\to\infty
along the solution curve. It was discovered in our recent work
[
1
]
that there exists
d_{*}(p)
such that the solution curve is oscillatory for
2+\frac{2}{p}<d<d_{*}(p)
and monotone for
d>d_{*}(p)
.

For the sake of simplicity, we have been working with the cubic nonlinearity,
p=1
, for which

d_{*}(p=1)=8+2\sqrt{6}
. We will continue working with the cubic nonlinearity here. Figure
1.1
shows the dependence
of
\lambda(b)
, which is oscillatory for
5\leq d\leq 12

and monotone for
d\geq 13
.

Graph of
\lambda
versus
b
for the ground state of the boundary-value problem (
1.5
) for
d=5
(left) and
d=13
(right).

A similar duality between the oscillatory and monotone behaviors was
discovered for the classical Liouville–Bratu–Gerlfand problem in
[
12
]

and explored in
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
18
]
for the stationary focusing nonlinear Schrödinger equation in a ball and without a harmonic potential. The similarity is explained by the same linearization of the stationary equation near the origin after the Emden–Fowler transformation
[
6
]
. Another example of the oscillatory and monotone behaviors
was considered in
[
5
]
for the Schrödinger–Newton–Hooke model.

Let us define the ground state of the stationary equation (
1.1
)
in radial variable
r=|x|
as a solution of the following boundary-value problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathfrak{u}^{\prime\prime}(r)+\frac{d-1}{r}\mathfrak{u}^{\prime}(r)-r^{2}\mathfrak{u}(r)+\lambda\mathfrak{u}(r)+\mathfrak{u}(r)^{3}=0,&r>0,\\
\mathfrak{u}(r)>0,\qquad\qquad\mathfrak{u}^{\prime}(r)<0,&\\
\lim\limits_{r\to 0}\mathfrak{u}(r)<\infty,\quad\lim\limits_{r\to\infty}\mathfrak{u}(r)=0.&\end{array}\right.

(1.5)

Any solution of the boundary-value problem (
1.5
)
belongs to
\mathcal{E}\cap L^{\infty}
, since
\mathfrak{u}\in C^{2}(0,\infty)
and
\mathfrak{u}(r)\to 0
decays fast as
r\to\infty
.

As is well understood since the pioneering work in
[
12
]
,
the ground state of the boundary-value problem (
1.5
)
can be found from the family of solutions
to the following initial-value problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f_{b}^{\prime\prime}(r)+\frac{d-1}{r}f_{b}^{\prime}(r)-r^{2}f_{b}(r)+\lambda f_{b}(r)+f_{b}(r)^{3}=0,&r>0,\\
f_{b}(0)=b,\quad f_{b}^{\prime}(0)=0,&\end{array}\right.

(1.6)

where
b>0
is an arbitrary parameter. By Theorem 1.1 in our previous work
[
1
]
, for any
b>0
and
d\geq 4
, there exists some
\lambda\in(d-4,d)
such that the unique solution
f_{b}\in C^{2}(0,\infty)
to the initial-value problem (
1.6
) is monotonically decaying to zero as
r\to\infty
, making it a ground state
\mathfrak{u}\equiv\mathfrak{u}_{b}\in\mathcal{E}\cap L^{\infty}
of the boundary-value problem (
1.5
). That value of
\lambda
is denoted as
\lambda(b)
. The mapping
b\mapsto\lambda(b)
defines a solution curve on the
(\lambda,b)
plane. Uniqueness of
\lambda(b)
is an open problem for
d\geq 4
, whereas Figure
1.1
suggests that
\lambda(b)
is unique for every
b>0
.

The purpose of this work is to study the Morse index of the ground state
\mathfrak{u}_{b}
.
It is defined as the number of negative eigenvalues of the linearized operator
\mathcal{L}_{b}
given by

\mathcal{L}_{b}:=-\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}-\frac{d-1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}+r^{2}-\lambda(b)-3\mathfrak{u}_{b}^{2}(r).

(1.7)

Since
\mathcal{E}
is the form domain of
\mathcal{L}_{b}
, we can write

\mathcal{L}_{b}:\mathcal{E}\mapsto\mathcal{E}^{*}
, where
\mathcal{E}^{*}
is the dual of
\mathcal{E}
with respect to the scalar product in
L^{2}_{r}
.

Assuming
C^{1}
property of
\mathfrak{u}_{b}
in
b
and differentiating the initial-value problem (
1.6
) with
\lambda=\lambda(b)
in
b
, we can see that
\mathcal{L}_{b}\partial_{b}\mathfrak{u}_{b}=\lambda^{\prime}(b)\mathfrak{u}_{b}
, where
\partial_{b}\mathfrak{u}_{b}\in\mathcal{E}
. Hence, any value of
b
for which
\lambda^{\prime}(b)=0
corresponds to zero eigenvalue being in the spectrum
of
\mathcal{L}_{b}
in
L^{2}_{r}
.

Although the converse is not known, this property implies
that the oscillatory case is very different from the monotone case,
where the former has infinitely many crossing of zero eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}_{b}
in the parameter continuation in
b
as
b\to\infty
whereas the latter does not have any eigenvalue crossing as
b\to\infty
, see also Figure
1.1
. This suggests
that the Morse index should be well defined in the monotone case,
independently of
b
for large values of
b
. This is in fact the main result
which we formulate as the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every
d\geq 13
, there exists
b_{0}>0
such that the Morse index of
\mathcal{L}_{b}:\mathcal{E}\mapsto\mathcal{E}^{*}
is finite and is independent of
b
for every
b\in(b_{0},\infty)
.

Remark 1.1
.

Regarding the Morse index for the ground state in the energy supercrticial case,
we are only aware of the works
[
8
,
14
]
, where the Morse index was estimated in the monotone case for the limiting singular solutions of the Dirichlet problem in a ball. We believe that the conclusion of Theorem
1.1
and the technique behind its proof remain valid for other problems in the monotone case, e.g. for the nonlinear Schrödinger equation in a ball.

Remark 1.2
.

By the Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction technique (see, e.g.,
[
21
]
),
the solution curve satisfies
\lambda(b)\to d
and
\mathfrak{u}_{b}\to 0
as
b\to 0
, where the Morse index of
\mathcal{L}_{b}
in
L^{2}_{r}
is equal to one. If the Morse index is equal to one for
b>b_{0}
in Theorem
1.1
, then it is quite possible
that the Morse index remains one for every
b\in(0,\infty)
. Since the ground state under these conditions is orbitally stable in the time evolution of the Gross–Pitaevskii equation in
\mathcal{E}\cap L^{4}_{r}
if the mapping of
\lambda\mapsto\|\mathfrak{u}_{b}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{r}}
is monotonically decreasing (see, e.g., Theorem 4.8 in
[
19
]
), it is rather interesting
to observe that the transition from the oscillatory case for
5\leq d\leq 12
to the monotone case
d\geq 13
may enforce stability of the ground state.

We shall now explain the strategy to prove Theorem
1.1
.
We use the limiting singular solution
\mathfrak{u}_{\infty}\in\mathcal{E}\cap L^{4}_{r}
which exists
for a particular value of
\lambda=\lambda_{\infty}
if
d\geq 5

[
22
]
. It was also established in
[
22
]
that
\mathfrak{u}_{b}\to\mathfrak{u}_{\infty}
in
\mathcal{E}
and
\lambda(b)\to\lambda_{\infty}
as
b\to\infty
.
Uniqueness of
\lambda_{\infty}
is also an open problem, see the discussion in
[
1
]
.

Figure
1.2
shows the ground state
\mathfrak{u}_{b}(r)
for two values of
b
and the limiting singular solution
\mathfrak{u}_{\infty}(r)
for
d=13
. The discrepancy between the two solutions moves to smaller values of
r
if the value of
b
is increased. When
b=10
, the difference between
\mathfrak{u}_{b}
and
\mathfrak{u}_{\infty}
becomes invisible on the scale used in Figure
1.2
.

Figure 1.2.
Graph of the ground state
\mathfrak{u}_{b}
for
b=1
and
b=6
in comparison with the limiting singular solution

for

.

Since the limiting singular solution
\mathfrak{u}_{\infty}
satisfies the following divergent behavior

\mathfrak{u}_{\infty}(r)=\frac{\sqrt{d-3}}{r}\left[1+\mathcal{O}\left(r^{2}\right)\right],\quad\text{as }r\to 0,

(1.8)

it is natural to introduce
F_{\infty}(r):=r\mathfrak{u}_{\infty}(r)
and define it from the family of solutions to the following initial-value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}F^{\prime\prime}(r)+\frac{d-3}{r}F^{\prime}(r)-\frac{d-3}{r^{2}}F(r)-r^{2}F(r)+\lambda F(r)+\frac{1}{r^{2}}F(r)^{3}=0,&r>0,\\
F(0)=\sqrt{d-3},\quad F^{\prime}(0)=0.&\end{array}\right.

(1.9)

By Theorem 1.2 in
[
1
]
(based on the earlier work in
[
22
]
), for any
d\geq 5
, there exists some
\lambda_{\infty}\in(d-4,d)
such that the unique solution
F_{\infty}\in C^{2}(0,\infty)
to the initial-value problem (
1.9
) is monotonically decreasing to zero as
r\to\infty
, making it the limiting singular solution
\mathfrak{u}_{\infty}\in\mathcal{E}\cap L^{4}_{r}
by the transformation
\mathfrak{u}_{\infty}(r)=r^{-1}F_{\infty}(r)
.

By Theorem 1.3 in
[
1
]
, convergence of
\lambda(b)\to\lambda_{\infty}
as
b\to\infty
is oscillatory for
5\leq d\leq 12
and monotone for
d\geq 13
, see Figure
1.1
. The latter case is the only case we are interested in here.

In order to characterize the Morse index of
\mathcal{L}_{b}
, we use the Emden–Fowler transformation
[
6
]
for the nonlinear equation in (
1.9
) and study two families of solutions. One family is obtained from
F_{b}(r):=r\mathfrak{u}_{b}(r)
and is parametrized
by its parameter
b
from the behavior as
r\to 0
. The other family is
parametrized by another parameter
c
from the decaying behavior
as
r\to\infty
. The second family is considered in a local
neighborhood of the limiting singular solution
F_{\infty}(r)=r\mathfrak{u}_{\infty}(r)
. Both families have
C^{1}
property with respect to
their parameters and their derivatives with respect to these parameters are solutions of the homogeneous equation
\mathcal{L}_{b}v=0

after the inverse Emden–Fowler transformation, e.g.,
v(r)=r^{-1}\partial_{b}F_{b}(r)
. The proof of Theorem
1.1
is achieved from the Sturm’s Oscillation Theorem (see, e.g., Theorem 3.5 in
[
23
]
)
by showing that the two derivatives have finitely many oscillations
and there exists
b_{0}>0
such that the two derivatives are linearly
independent for every
b\in(b_{0},\infty)
.

As a by-product of our approach, we establish the equivalence of the
Morse index of
\mathcal{L}_{b}
in
L^{2}_{r}
with the Morse index
of the limiting operator
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}:=\lim\limits_{b\to\infty}\mathcal{L}_{b}
which is computed
at the limiting singular solution for
d\geq 5
:

\mathcal{L}_{\infty}:=-\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}-\frac{d-1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}+r^{2}-\lambda_{\infty}-3\mathfrak{u}_{\infty}^{2}(r).

(1.10)

Compared to
\mathcal{L}_{b}:\mathcal{E}\mapsto\mathcal{E}^{*}
, where the potential
-3\mathfrak{u}_{b}^{2}(r)
is bounded from below,
the potential
-3\mathfrak{u}_{\infty}^{2}(r)
is unbounded from below,
hence
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}:\mathcal{E}_{\infty}\mapsto\mathcal{E}_{\infty}^{*}
, where
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}=\{u\in\mathcal{E}:\;r^{-1}u\in L^{2}_{r}\}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}^{*}
is the dual of
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}
with respect to the scalar product in
L^{2}_{r}
.

The following theorem gives the precise result on the Morse index of the two linear operators.

Theorem 1.2
.

For every
d\geq 13
, there exists
b_{0}>0
such that the Morse index of
\mathcal{L}_{b}:\mathcal{E}\mapsto\mathcal{E}^{*}
coincides with
the Morse index of
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}:\mathcal{E}_{\infty}\mapsto\mathcal{E}^{*}_{\infty}
.

Remark 1.3
.

If the norm convergence of the resolvent for
\mathcal{L}_{b}
to the resolvent for
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
can be established as
b\to\infty
, this would imply the result of Theorem
1.2
. We do not study the norm convergence of resolvents here as our methods are based on analysis of differential equations.

Remark 1.4
.

The result of Theorem
1.2
suggests a simple way to obtain the Morse index for the ground state
\mathfrak{u}_{b}
in the monotone case for large

from the Morse index for the limiting singular solution

. The latter one can be approximated numerically with good accuracy.

2.
Preliminary results

We begin by introducing the Emden-Fowler transformation

r=e^{t},\quad F(r)=\Psi(t),\quad F^{\prime}(r)=e^{-t}\Psi^{\prime}(t),

(2.1)

which transforms the differential equation

F^{\prime\prime}(r)+\frac{d-3}{r}F^{\prime}(r)-\frac{d-3}{r^{2}}F(r)-r^{2}F(r)+\lambda F(r)+\frac{1}{r^{2}}F(r)^{3}=0,\quad r>0

(2.2)

to the equivalent form

\Psi^{\prime\prime}(t)+(d-4)\Psi^{\prime}(t)+(3-d)\Psi(t)+\Psi(t)^{3}=-\lambda e^{2t}\Psi(t)+e^{4t}\Psi(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.3)

For fixed
d\geq 5
and
\lambda\in(0,d)
, two one-parameter families of solutions to the second-order differential equation (
2.3
) have been constructed in
[
1
]
according to their asymptotic behaviors as
t\to-\infty
and
t\to+\infty
, respectively.

The first family of solutions to the differential equation (
2.3
), denoted as
\left\{\Psi_{b}\right\}_{b\in\mathbb{R}}
, corresponds to solutions of the initial-value problem (
1.6
) after applying the transformation
\Psi_{b}(t)=e^{t}f_{b}(e^{t})
. By Lemmas 3.2 and 3.4 in
[
1
]
,

\Psi_{b}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies the asymptotic behavior

\Psi_{b}(t)=be^{t}-(\lambda b+b^{3})(2d)^{-1}e^{3t}+\mathcal{O}(e^{5t}),\quad\text{as }t\to-\infty,

(2.4)

where the expansion can be differentiated in
t
.
These solutions depend on
\lambda
as well, and for
\lambda=\lambda(b)
and
b>0
,
\Psi_{b}(t)
gives a solution to the boundary-value problem (
1.1
), after the transformation
\mathfrak{u}_{b}(r)=r^{-1}\Psi_{b}(\log r)
. For other values of
\lambda
,
\Psi_{b}(t)
generally diverges as
t\to+\infty
.

The second family of solutions to the differential equation (
2.3
), denoted as
\left\{\Psi_{c}\right\}_{c\in\mathbb{R}}
,
decays to zero as
t\to+\infty
. By Lemmas 3.3 and 3.4 in
[
1
]
,

\Psi_{c}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies the asymptotic behavior

\Psi_{c}(t)\sim ce^{\frac{\lambda-d+2}{2}t}e^{-\frac{1}{2}e^{2t}},\quad\text{as }t\to+\infty,

(2.5)

where the sign
\sim
denotes the asymptotic correspondence which can be differentiated in
t
. Each
\Psi_{c}(t)
generally diverges as
t\to-\infty
, except when
\lambda=\lambda(b)
and
c=c(b)
for some value of
c(b)
for which it concides with
\Psi_{b}(t)=e^{t}\mathfrak{u}_{b}(e^{t})
:

\lambda=\lambda(b):\quad\Psi_{b}(t)=\Psi_{c(b)}(t),\quad\mbox{for all}\;\;t\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.6)

Each family of solutions is differentiable with respect to parameters
\lambda
and either
b
or
c
due to smoothness of the differential equation (
2.3
). Their derivatives decay to zero as
t\to-\infty
and
t\to+\infty
respectively, but generally diverge at the other infinities.

Let us define linearizations of the second-order equation (
2.3
) at the two families of solutions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{b}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}+(d-4)\frac{d}{dt}+(3-d)+3\Psi_{b}^{2}+\lambda e^{2t}-e^{4t},

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{c}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}+(d-4)\frac{d}{dt}+(3-d)+3\Psi_{c}^{2}+\lambda e^{2t}-e^{4t}.

(2.8)

Then, differentiating the second-order equation (
2.3
) with respect to
b
and
c
at fixed
\lambda
yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{b}\partial_{b}\Psi_{b}=0,\quad\mathcal{M}_{c}\partial_{c}\Psi_{c}=0,

where
\partial_{b}\Psi_{b}(t)\to 0
as
t\to-\infty
and

\partial_{c}\Psi_{c}(t)\to 0
as

.

The first family
\left\{\Psi_{b}\right\}_{b\in\mathbb{R}}
is defined
in a neighborhood of a heteroclinic orbit

connecting the saddle point
(0,0)
and the stable point

) given by

\Theta^{\prime\prime}(t)+(d-4)\Theta^{\prime}(t)+(3-d)\Theta(t)+\Theta(t)^{3}=0.

By Lemma 6.1 in
[
1
]
, there exists a heteroclinic orbit between
(0,0)
and
(\sqrt{d-3},0)

which is defined uniquely (module to the translation in

) by the asymptotic behavior

\Theta(t)=e^{t}-(2d)^{-1}e^{3t}+\mathcal{O}(e^{5t}),\quad\text{as }t\to-\infty.

(2.11)

The following proposition presents the main result of Lemmas 6.2, 6.5, and 6.8 in

]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Fix
d\geq 5
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. For every

, there exist

-independent constants

converges to

on the negative
t

to

for fixed

and

and for sufficiently large

at the expense of slower convergence rate.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
d\geq 13
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. For every
T>0
and
a\in(0,1)
, there exist
(T,a)
-independent constants
b_{0}>0
,
C_{0}>0

and
(T,a)
-dependent constants
b_{T,a}>0
,
C_{T,a}>0
such that

\partial_{b}\Psi_{b}
satisfies for every

\sup_{t\in(-\infty,0]}|\partial_{b}\Psi_{b}(t-\log b)-b^{-1}\Theta^{\prime}(t)|+\sup_{t\in(-\infty,0]}|\partial_{b}\Psi_{b}^{\prime}(t-\log b)-b^{-1}\Theta^{\prime\prime}(t)|\leq C_{0}b^{-3}

(3.1)

and for every
b\in(b_{T,a},\infty)

\sup_{t\in\left[0,T+a\log b\right]}|\partial_{b}\Psi_{b}(t-\log b)-b^{-1}\Theta^{\prime}(t)|+\sup_{t\in\left[0,T+a\log b\right]}|\partial_{b}\Psi_{b}^{\prime}(t-\log b)-b^{-1}\Theta^{\prime\prime}(t)|\leq C_{T,a}b^{-2(1-a)-1}.

(3.2)

Proof.

We begin by introducing
\gamma(t):=\partial_{b}\Psi_{b}(t-\log b)-b^{-1}\Theta^{\prime}(t)
, where
\Psi_{b}
is the unique solution to (
2.3
) with the asymptotic behavior (
2.4
) and
\Theta
is the unique solution
to (
2.10
) with the asymptotic behavior
(
2.11
). Since
\partial_{b}\Psi_{b}
satisfies
\mathcal{M}_{b}\partial_{b}\Psi_{b}=0

and
\Theta^{\prime}
satisfies
\mathcal{M}_{0}\Theta^{\prime}=0
, where

\mathcal{M}_{b}
is given by (
2.7
) and

\mathcal{M}_{0}:=\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}+(d-4)\frac{d}{dt}+(3-d)+3\Theta^{2},

(3.3)

the difference term
\gamma(t)
satisfies the following equation:

\mathcal{M}_{0}\gamma=f_{b}(b^{-1}\Theta^{\prime}+\gamma),

(3.4)

where

\displaystyle f_{b}(t):=3(\Theta(t)^{2}-\Psi_{b}(t-\log b)^{2})-\lambda b^{-2}e^{2t}+b^{-4}e^{4t}.

(3.5)

Note that
\mathcal{M}_{0}-f_{b}
gives
\mathcal{M}_{b}
after translation
t\mapsto t+\log b
.

Proof of the bound (
3.1
).
Two linearly independent solutions of
\mathcal{M}_{0}\gamma=0
are given by
\Theta^{\prime}(t)
and another function
\Xi(t)
, which can be found from the Wronskian relation

W(\Theta^{\prime},\Xi)(t)=\Theta^{\prime}(t)\Xi^{\prime}(t)-\Theta^{\prime\prime}(t)\Xi(t)=W_{0}e^{(4-d)t},

(3.6)

for some constant
W_{0}\neq 0
. We take
W_{0}=1
in order to normalize
\Xi(t)
uniquely. Since
\Theta^{\prime}(t)\to 0
as
t\to-\infty
according to the asymptotic expansion

\Theta^{\prime}(t)=e^{t}+\mathcal{O}(e^{3t}),\quad\text{as }t\to-\infty,

(3.7)

we have
\Xi(t)\to\infty
as
t\to-\infty
according to
the asymptotic expansion

\Xi(t)=(2-d)^{-1}e^{(3-d)t}+\mathcal{O}(e^{(5-d)t}),\quad\text{as }t\to-\infty.

(3.8)

In order to estimate the supremum-norm of
\gamma(t)
for

, we first rewrite the differential equation (
3.4
) as an integral equation

\gamma(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{(d-4)t^{\prime}}[\Theta^{\prime}(t^{\prime})\Xi(t)-\Theta^{\prime}(t)\Xi(t^{\prime})]f_{b}(t^{\prime})[b^{-1}\Theta^{\prime}(t^{\prime})+\gamma(t^{\prime})]dt^{\prime},

(3.9)

where the free solution

is set to zero from the requirement that
\gamma(t)=\mathcal{O}(e^{3t})
as
t\to-\infty
. The integral kernel in (
3.9
) becomes bounded if we introduce the transformation
\tilde{\gamma}(t)=e^{-t}\gamma(t)
. The integral equation corresponding to

\tilde{\gamma}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}K_{1}(t,t^{\prime})f_{b}(t^{\prime})[b^{-1}e^{-t^{\prime}}\Theta^{\prime}(t^{\prime})+\tilde{\gamma}(t^{\prime})]dt^{\prime},

(3.10)

where

K_{1}(t,t^{\prime}):=[e^{-t^{\prime}}\Theta^{\prime}(t^{\prime})][e^{(d-3)t}\Xi(t)]e^{(d-2)(t^{\prime}-t)}-[e^{-t}\Theta^{\prime}(t)][e^{(d-3)t^{\prime}}\Xi(t^{\prime})].

(3.11)

Since
\Theta^{\prime}(t)=\mathcal{O}(e^{t})
and
\Xi(t)=\mathcal{O}(e^{(3-d)t})

is a bounded function for all

.

By using bound (
2.12
) of Proposition
2.1
, we obtain from (

|f_{b}(t)|\leq C_{0}b^{-2}e^{2t},\quad t\in(-\infty,0],

(3.12)

where the constant

is independent of
b

and may change from one line to another line. Boundedness of

in the integral equation (

) allow us to estimate the supremum norm of

Due to smallness of

and boundness of

-independent

\sup_{t\in(-\infty,0]}|\tilde{\gamma}(t)|\leq C_{0}b^{-3}.

Since

). Since

3.1

3.10
) and using (
3.14

Proof of the bound (
3.2
).

In order to estimate
|\gamma(t)|
for sufficiently large positive
t

we need to define solutions to
\mathcal{M}_{0}\gamma=0
from their behavior as
t\to+\infty

(\sqrt{d-3},0)
is a stable node of the nonlinear equation (
2.10
), we can pick two linearly independent solutions
\gamma_{1}(t)
and
\gamma_{2}(t)

from their decaying behavior

(3.15)

where
\kappa_{-}<\kappa_{+}<0
are given by (
2.14

The Liouville’s formula yields the Wronskian relation

W(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})(t)=\gamma_{1}(t)\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(t)-\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(t)\gamma_{2}(t)=W_{0}e^{(4-d)t},

(3.16)

for some constant

\gamma_{1}(t)

and
\gamma_{2}(t)
we can assume that

. In order to derive supremum-norm estimates for

\gamma(t)=\gamma(0)[\gamma_{1}(t)\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(0)-\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(0)\gamma_{2}(t)]+\gamma^{\prime}(0)[\gamma_{1}(0)\gamma_{2}(t)-\gamma_{1}(t)\gamma_{2}(0)]\\
+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(d-4)t^{\prime}}[\gamma_{1}(t^{\prime})\gamma_{2}(t)-\gamma_{1}(t)\gamma_{2}(t^{\prime})]f_{b}(t^{\prime})[b^{-1}\Theta^{\prime}(t^{\prime})+\gamma(t^{\prime})]dt^{\prime},

The following lemma extends the estimate on the difference

Lemma 4.1

Fix

, and

|\partial_{c}\Psi_{c}(t)-\partial_{c}\Psi_{\infty}(t)|+|\partial_{c}\Psi_{c}^{\prime}(t)-\partial_{c}\Psi_{\infty}^{\prime}(t)|\leq C_{0}\epsilon e^{\kappa_{-}t}.

(4.3)

Proof.

Let

Since

satisfies
\mathcal{M}_{c}\partial_{c}\Psi_{c}=0

where

\displaystyle f_{c}(t)

\displaystyle:=3(\Psi_{\infty}(t)^{2}-\Psi_{c}(t)^{2})\partial_{c}\Psi_{\infty}(t)-(\lambda-\lambda_{\infty})e^{2t}\partial_{c}\Psi_{\infty}(t),

(4.5)

\displaystyle g_{c}(t)

\displaystyle:=3(\Psi_{\infty}(t)^{2}-\Psi_{c}(t)^{2})+(\lambda_{\infty}-\lambda)e^{2t}.

(4.6)

Note that
\mathcal{M}_{c}=\mathcal{M}_{\infty}-g_{c}
.

As in Appendix

, we pick two linearly independent solutions

,

to
\mathcal{M}_{\infty}r=0

(4.7)

where

are given by (

where we have used the normalization of the Wronskian

between the two solutions

and

as in (

In order to elliminate the divergent behavior of the kernel in (

The kernel is bounded for every

\int_{t}^{0}e^{-\kappa_{-}t^{\prime}}|f_{c}(t^{\prime})|dt^{\prime}\leq C_{0}\left(\epsilon b^{\kappa_{-}(1-a)}\int_{(a-1)\log b}^{0}e^{\kappa_{-}t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime}+|\lambda-\lambda_{\infty}|\right)\leq C_{0}\epsilon.

(4.12)

On the other hand, for every

(4.13)

Putting estimates (

∎

5.

We recall that

Lemma 5.1
.

Fix
d\geq 13
. Under Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
, for every

with

given by (

), there exists
b_{0}>0
such that for
every

, there exists no

C\partial_{c}\Psi_{c(b)}(t)=\partial_{b}\Psi_{b}(t),\quad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{R}.

(5.1)

Proof.

In order to get a contradiction, suppose that relation (
5.1
) holds for some constant
C\in\mathbb{R}
. The results of Corollaries
3.22
and
4.1
apply for
t=T+(a-1)\log b
for
fixed
a\in(0,a_{0})
,
T>0
, and sufficiently large
b
. Substituting bounds (

) and (
4.15
)
into (

CL_{\infty}e^{\kappa_{-}T}b^{-\kappa_{-}(1-a)}\left[1+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon,b^{-(\kappa_{+}-\kappa_{-})(1-a)},b^{-2(1-a)})\right]\\
=A_{0}\kappa_{+}b^{a\kappa_{+}-1}e^{\kappa_{+}T}\left[1+\mathcal{O}(b^{-2(1-a)-a\kappa_{+}},b^{-a(\kappa_{+}-\kappa_{-})},b^{a\kappa_{+}})\right],

(5.2)

where we recall that
2(1-a)+a\kappa_{+}>0
if

, where

is given by (
3.23
) in Corollary
3.22
.
Since
A_{0}\neq 0
and
L_{\infty}\neq 0
by Assumptions
2.1

and
2.2
, we obtain from (

C\left[1+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon,b^{-(\kappa_{+}-\kappa_{-})(1-a)},b^{-2(1-a)})\right]\\
=L_{\infty}^{-1}A_{0}\kappa_{+}b^{a(\kappa_{+}-\kappa_{-})+\kappa_{-}-1}e^{(\kappa_{+}-\kappa_{-})T}\left[1+\mathcal{O}(b^{-2(1-a)-a\kappa_{+}},b^{-a(\kappa_{+}-\kappa_{-})},b^{a\kappa_{+}})\right].

(5.3)

Since the remainder terms on both sides of (

, this gives a

which is a contradiction with the relation (

) for all

and hence for all

∎

From Lemma
5.1
, we can now prove Theorem
1.1
which
states that the Morse index of
\mathcal{L}_{b}:\mathcal{E}\mapsto\mathcal{E}^{*}
is finite and is independent of
b
for every
b\in(b_{0},\infty)
.

Proof

of Theorem
1.1

For every
b\in(0,\infty)
, the potential
-3\mathfrak{u}^{2}_{b}(r)
in
\mathcal{L}_{b}
is bounded from below on
[0,\infty)
. The Schrödinger operator
-\Delta_{r}+r^{2}:\mathcal{E}\mapsto\mathcal{E}^{*}
is strictly positive with a purely discrete spectrum. Since
\mathcal{L}_{b}=-\Delta_{r}+r^{2}-\lambda(b)-3\mathfrak{u}_{b}^{2}(r)
is bounded from below, the number of negative eigenvalues (the Morse index)
of
\mathcal{L}_{b}:\mathcal{E}\mapsto\mathcal{E}^{*}
is finite
by Theorem 10.7 in
[
11
]
.

It remains to show that the Morse index is independent of
b
for every
b\in(b_{0},\infty)
. Let us recall the Emden–Fowler transformation (
2.1
), which relates
solutions of
\mathcal{L}_{b}v=0
with solutions of
\mathcal{M}_{b}\gamma=0
by
v(r)=r^{-1}\gamma(\log r)
.
The spectrum of
\mathcal{L}_{b}:\mathcal{E}\mapsto\mathcal{E}^{*}
includes the zero eigenvalue if and only if there exists
v\in\mathcal{E}
satisfying
\mathcal{L}_{b}v=0
. This is impossible due to Lemma
5.1
according to the following arguments.

As
t\to-\infty
, there exist two linearly independent solutions to
\mathcal{M}_{b}\gamma=0
and the decaying solution is

\partial_{b}\Psi_{b}(t)=e^{t}+\mathcal{O}(e^{3t}),\quad\mbox{\rm as \;\;}t\to-\infty.

The other solution is growing as
e^{(3-d)t}
which corresponds to
v(r)\sim r^{2-d}
so that

r^{d-1}|v(r)|^{2}\sim r^{3-d}

is not integrable near
r=0
for
d\geq 4
. Hence, the corresponding
v(r)
is not in
L^{2}_{r}
and if there exists nonzero
v\in\mathcal{E}
satisfying
\mathcal{L}_{b}v=0
, then there exists a constant
C_{-}\neq 0
such that

v(r)=C_{-}r^{-1}\partial_{b}\Psi_{b}(\log r).

As
t\to+\infty
, there exist two linearly independent solutions to
\mathcal{M}_{b}\gamma=0
and the decaying solution is

\partial_{c}\Psi_{c(b)}(t)\sim e^{\frac{\lambda(b)-d+2}{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}e^{2t}},\quad\mbox{\rm as \;\;}t\to\infty.

The other solution is growing as
e^{\frac{1}{2}e^{2t}}
, which corresponds to
v(r)\sim e^{\frac{1}{2}r^{2}}
, clearly not in
L^{2}_{r}
. If there exists nonzero
v\in\mathcal{E}
satisfying
\mathcal{L}_{b}v=0
, then there exists a constant
C_{+}\neq 0
such that

v(r)=C_{+}r^{-1}\partial_{c}\Psi_{c(b)}(\log r).

Since
C_{-},C_{+}\neq 0
, if there exists nonzero
v\in\mathcal{E}
,
then
\partial_{b}\Psi_{b}
and
\partial_{c}\Psi_{c(b)}
are linearly dependent, which results in a contradiction with Lemma
5.1
for every
b\in(b_{0},\infty)
. Hence
0\notin\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{b})
for every
b\in(b_{0},\infty)
so that the Morse index of
\mathcal{L}_{b}:\mathcal{E}\mapsto\mathcal{E}^{*}
is independent of
b
for every
b\in(b_{0},\infty)
.

By Lemma
4.1
,
\partial_{c}\Psi_{c}(t)
converges to

\partial_{c}\Psi_{\infty}
on
[(a-1)\log b,\infty)
as
(\lambda,c)\to(\lambda_{\infty},c_{\infty})
. Each zero of either
\partial_{c}\Psi_{c}
or
\partial_{c}\Psi_{\infty}
is simple since they are solutions of the second-order linear homogeneous equations
\mathcal{M}_{c}\partial_{c}\Psi_{c}=0
and
\mathcal{M}_{\infty}\partial_{c}\Psi_{\infty}=0
. Consequently,
the number of nodal domains of
\partial_{c}\Psi_{c}
in
[(a-1)\log b,\infty)

coincides with that of
\partial_{c}\Psi_{\infty}
in
[(a-1)\log b,\infty)
.

The following lemma shows that
\partial_{c}\Psi_{c(b)}
does not have additional nodal domains in the interval
(-\infty,(a-1)\log b)

for sufficiently large
b
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Fix
d\geq 13
. Under Assumption
2.2
, for every
a\in(0,1)
, there exists
b_{0}>0
such that for every
b\in(b_{0},\infty)
, there exists
C_{0}>0
such that

|\partial_{c}\Psi_{c(b)}(t)|\geq C_{0},\quad t\in(-\infty,(a-1)\log b).

(5.4)

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{M}_{b}=\mathcal{M}_{c(b)}
for
\lambda=\lambda(b)
and

\mathcal{M}_{b}\partial_{b}\Psi_{b}=0
with

\partial_{b}\Psi_{b}(t)=e^{t}+\mathcal{O}(e^{3t}),\quad\mbox{\rm as}\;\;t\to-\infty.

(5.5)

Similar to the proof of Lemma
3.1
, we denote the second linearly independent solution of
\mathcal{M}_{b}\gamma=0
by
\Phi

and normalize it such that

\Phi(t)=(2-d)^{-1}e^{(3-d)t}+\mathcal{O}(e^{(5-d)t}),\quad\text{as }t\to-\infty.

(5.6)

We are interested in the behavior of
\partial_{c}\Psi_{c(b)}
for
t\in(-\infty,t_{0})
, where
t_{0}:=(a-1)\log b
. It follows from
(
2.25
) and (
4.3
) that for sufficiently large

, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{c}\Psi_{c(b)}(t_{0})

\displaystyle=L_{\infty}e^{\kappa_{-}t_{0}}\left[1+\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon,e^{(\kappa_{+}-\kappa_{-})t_{0}},e^{2t_{0}}\right)\right],

(5.7)

\displaystyle\partial_{c}\Psi^{\prime}_{c(b)}(t_{0})

\displaystyle=L_{\infty}\kappa_{-}e^{\kappa_{-}t_{0}}\left[1+\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon,e^{(\kappa_{+}-\kappa_{-})t_{0}},e^{2t_{0}}\right)\right].

(5.8)

Since
\partial_{c}\Psi_{c(b)}
is a linear combination of
\partial_{b}\Psi_{b}
and
\Phi
by the linear superposition principle, we can express
\partial_{c}\Psi_{c(b)}
as

\partial_{c}\Psi_{c(b)}(t)=e^{(d-4)t_{0}}\big[(\partial_{c}\Psi_{c(b)}(t_{0})\Phi^{\prime}(t_{0})-\partial_{c}\Psi^{\prime}_{c(b)}(t_{0})\Phi(t_{0}))\partial_{b}\Psi_{b}(t)\\
+\left(\partial_{c}\Psi^{\prime}_{c(b)}(t_{0})\partial_{b}\Psi_{b}(t_{0})-\partial_{c}\Psi_{c(b)}(t_{0})\partial_{b}\Psi^{\prime}_{b}(t_{0})\right)\Phi(t)\big],

(5.9)

where we have used the normalization
W(\partial_{b}\Psi_{b},\Phi)=e^{(4-d)t}

of the Wronskian between the two solutions
\partial_{b}\Psi_{b}
and
\Phi
. Since
t_{0}\to-\infty
as
b\to\infty
, we can use asymptotics (
5.5
) and (
5.6
) as well as the boundary condtions (
5.7
) and (
5.8
) to obtain for every

:

\partial_{c}\Psi_{c(b)}(t)=(d-2)^{-1}L_{\infty}e^{\kappa_{-}t_{0}}\left[(\kappa_{-}+d-3)e^{t-t_{0}}+(1-\kappa_{-})e^{(3-d)(t-t_{0})}\right]\\
\times\left[1+\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon,e^{(\kappa_{+}-\kappa_{-})t_{0}},e^{2t_{0}}\right)\right]\left[1+\mathcal{O}(e^{2t})\right],

(5.10)

where
1-\kappa_{-}>0
and

\kappa_{-}+d-3=\frac{1}{2}\left(d-2-\sqrt{d^{2}-16d+40}\right)>0

for every
d\geq 13
. Thus, the sign of
\partial_{c}\Psi_{c(b)}(t)

for every

coincides with the sign of
L_{\infty}

so that the bound (
5.4
) holds.
∎

From Lemmas

and

, we can now prove Theorem

which
states that the Morse index of

Proof

of Theorem

By Sturm’s Oscillation Theorem (see, e.g., Theorem 3.5 in

),
the Morse index of
\mathcal{L}_{b}:\mathcal{E}\mapsto\mathcal{E}^{*}
coincides with the number of zeros of the function

on

and

as

on

coincides
with the number of zeros of

and
\partial_{c}\Psi_{c(b)}(t)\to 0

and simplicity of the zeros of

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
2.2

Let
\Sigma(t):=\Psi_{c}(t)-\Psi_{\infty}(t)
. It follows from (
2.3

\Sigma
satisfies the following equation:

\mathcal{M}_{\infty}\Sigma=\mathcal{F}(\Sigma)(t),

(A.1)

where
\mathcal{M}_{\infty}
is defined by (
2.23

\mathcal{F}(\Sigma)(t):=-(\lambda-\lambda_{\infty})e^{2t}(\Psi_{\infty}(t)+\Sigma(t))-3\Psi_{\infty}(t)\Sigma(t)^{2}-\Sigma(t)^{3}.

Since
\Psi_{\infty}(t)\to\sqrt{d-3}
as

, as it follows from (
2.17
), we can pick two linearly independent solutions

,

to

such that

r_{1}(t)=\mathcal{O}(e^{\kappa_{-}t}),\quad r_{2}(t)=\mathcal{O}(e^{\kappa_{+}t}),\quad\text{ as }t\to-\infty,

(A.2)

where
\kappa_{-}<\kappa_{+}<0
are given by (
2.14
). Using the Liouville’s formula, we normalize the Wronskian according to the relation:

W(r_{1},r_{2})(t)=r_{1}(t)r_{2}^{\prime}(t)-r_{1}^{\prime}(t)r_{2}(t)=e^{-(d-4)t}.

(A.3)

By the variation of parameters method, we rewrite the differential equation (
A.1
) as an integral equation for every
t\in[(a-1)\log b,0]

\Sigma(t)=\Sigma(0)\left[r_{1}(t)r_{2}^{\prime}(0)-r_{1}^{\prime}(0)r_{2}(t)\right]+\Sigma^{\prime}(0)\left[r_{1}(0)r_{2}(t)-r_{1}(t)r_{2}(0)\right]\\
+\int_{t}^{0}e^{(d-4)t^{\prime}}\left[r_{1}(t)r_{2}(t^{\prime})-r_{1}(t^{\prime})r_{2}(t)\right]\mathcal{F}(\Sigma)(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}.

(A.4)

In order to use Banach fixed-point iterations, we introduce
\tilde{\Sigma}(t):=e^{-\kappa_{-}t}\Sigma(t)
, which satisfies

\tilde{\Sigma}(t)=\mathcal{A}(\tilde{\Sigma})(t)
, where

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{A}(\tilde{\Sigma})(t)&=\Sigma(0)e^{-\kappa_{-}t}\left[r_{1}(t)r_{2}^{\prime}(0)-r_{1}^{\prime}(0)r_{2}(t)\right]+\Sigma^{\prime}(0)e^{-\kappa_{-}t}\left[r_{1}(0)r_{2}(t)-r_{1}(t)r_{2}(0)\right]\\
&-(\lambda-\lambda_{\infty})\int_{t}^{0}K_{2}(t,t^{\prime})e^{2t^{\prime}}\left[e^{-\kappa_{-}t^{\prime}}\Psi_{\infty}(t^{\prime})+\tilde{\Sigma}(t^{\prime})\right]dt^{\prime}\\
&-\int_{t}^{0}K_{2}(t,t^{\prime})\left[3e^{\kappa_{-}t^{\prime}}\Psi_{\infty}(t^{\prime})\tilde{\Sigma}^{2}(t^{\prime})+e^{2\kappa_{-}t^{\prime}}\tilde{\Sigma}^{3}(t^{\prime})\right]dt^{\prime},\end{split}

(A.5)

where the kernel
K_{2}(t,t^{\prime})
is defined as

K_{2}(t,t^{\prime}):=e^{\kappa_{-}(t^{\prime}-t)-(\kappa_{+}+\kappa_{-})t^{\prime}}\left[r_{1}(t)r_{2}(t^{\prime})-r_{1}(t^{\prime})r_{2}(t)\right].

(A.6)

It follows from (
A.2
) that

K_{2}(t,t^{\prime})\sim 1+e^{(\kappa_{+}-\kappa_{-})(t-t^{\prime})}
as

, which means that there exists some constant
K_{0}>0
, such that

\sup_{-\infty<t\leq t^{\prime}\leq 0}|K_{2}(t,t^{\prime})|\leq K_{0}.

(A.7)

It follows from (
2.22
) that
there exists some constant
C_{0}>0
such that

|\Sigma(0)|+|\Sigma^{\prime}(0)|\leq C_{0}\left(|\lambda-\lambda_{\infty}|+|c-c_{\infty}|\right).

The integral operator
\mathcal{A}(\tilde{\Sigma})
in (

)
is estimated for every
\tilde{\Sigma}\in L^{\infty}((a-1)\log b,0)
as

\|\mathcal{A}(\tilde{\Sigma})\|_{\infty}\leq C_{0}\Big[|\lambda-\lambda_{\infty}|+|c-c_{\infty}|+|\lambda-\lambda_{\infty}|\|\tilde{\Sigma}\|_{\infty}\\
+b^{-\kappa_{-}(1-a)}\|\tilde{\Sigma}\|^{2}_{\infty}+b^{-2\kappa_{-}(1-a)}\|\tilde{\Sigma}\|^{3}_{\infty}\Big].

(A.8)

Similar estimate applies to
\|\mathcal{A}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{1})-\mathcal{A}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{2})\|_{\infty}

The estimates show that the integral operator

is closed and is a contraction in the ball

of the small radius

, provided that

satisfy the bound (

) and
\epsilon>0
is sufficiently small. By the Banach fixed-point theorem, there exists a unique fixed point of

satisfying

\|\tilde{\Sigma}\|_{\infty}\leq 2C_{0}\epsilon b^{\kappa_{-}(1-a)},

(A.9)

which proves the bound (

) after going back to the original variable
\Sigma
and redefining

.
