Generalization Bounds of Surrogate Policies for Combinatorial Optimization Problems
Pierre-Cyril Aubin-Frankowski, Yohann De Castro, Axel Parmentier, Alessandro Rudi

Abstract
Many real-world decision problems require solving, again and again, combinatorial optimization instances drawn from a common distribution. A recent line of structured learning methods exploits this regularity by learning policies that pair a statistical model with a tractable combinatorial oracle, instead of solving each instance independently. Training such policies is notoriously difficult, however: the resulting empirical risk is piecewise constant in the model parameters, which hinders gradient-based optimization, and only a few theoretical guarantees have been provided so far. We address this issue by analyzing smoothed (perturbed) policies: adding controlled random perturbations to the direction used by the linear oracle yields a differentiable surrogate risk and improves generalization. Our main contribution is a generalization bound that decomposes the excess risk into ( i ) (\mathit{i}) perturbation bias, ( 𝑖𝑖 ) (\mathit{ii}) statistical estimation error, and ( 𝑖𝑖𝑖 ) (\mathit{iii}) optimization error. The perturbation bias is controlled by the fan-crossing probability , a new geometric quantity measuring the likelihood that a perturbation changes the oracle solution. We introduce two complementary conditions to bound it — the Uniformly Bounded Density (UBD) property, yielding a sharp 𝒪 ⁡ ( λ ) \mathcal{O}(\lambda) bias, and the weaker Uniform Weak moment (UW) property, yielding a sub-linear bound — both capturing the geometric interaction between the statistical model and the normal fan of the feasible polytope. The statistical estimation error is controlled via a uniform deviation bound over the policy class, with rate 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / ( λ ​ n ) ) \mathcal{O}(1/(\lambda\sqrt{n})) that scales inversely in the smoothing parameter. Concerning the optimization error, we exploit kernel Sum-of-Squares methods to mitigate the curse of dimensionality of global optimization. We illustrate the scope of the results on applications such as stochastic vehicle scheduling, highlighting how smoothing enables both tractable training and controlled generalization. The framework is in particular applicable to contextual stochastic optimization.

Introduction

1.1
Learning policies instead of minimizing separately

Consider the following combinatorial optimization problem

\min_{{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}})}f^{0}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}})\,

(1.1)

where
\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}})
is the finite set of feasible solutions for instance
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
,
e.g.,
a path
{\bm{y}}
in a weighted graph
{\bm{x}}
. The
\mathds{R}
-valued objective
f^{0}
may model any complex phenomena, and is not restricted to any specific class of functions. We assume however that
f^{0}
comes with an oracle, that can be mildly expensive to compute,
i.e.
, can take a few seconds to evaluate and for instance be the result of a simulation. We will focus on settings where the problem defined by
f^{0}
is challenging in two ways: (i) its modelling is too complex to be cast as a mathematical programming formulation that can be solved with off-the-shelf algorithms; (ii) its computational cost prohibits the use of a local descent heuristic.

Instead of solving each instance independently, we consider a family
\mathcal{H}
of policies
h:{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}\mapsto{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}})
. Our goal is then to
learn
a mapping
h^{\star}
that minimizes the risk

\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{R}(h)\coloneqq\mathds{E}_{X}\big[f^{0}(h(X),X)\big],

(1.2)

where
X
is a random instance distributed according to some distribution
\mathds{P}_{X}
. If the set
\mathcal{H}
corresponds to all the possible mappings
\mathcal{X}\to(\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}}))_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}
, then we get back to the classical approach where each instance is solved independently by considering
{\bm{x}}\mapsto\argmin_{{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}})}f^{0}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}})
.

We instead restrict ourselves to a family of tractable policies parametrized by a vector
\bm{w}
belonging to a set
\mathcal{W}
, and we learn the parameter
\bm{w}
that minimizes the risk. This approach has proved empirically successful in the last few years on a variety of applications such as portfolio optimization
(
Elmachtoub and Grigas, 2022
)
, path finding in images
(
Pogančić et al., 2020
)
, vehicle scheduling
(
Parmentier, 2022
)
, route planning
(
Ferber et al., 2023
)
, machine scheduling
(
Parmentier and T’Kindt, 2023
)
, dynamic vehicle routing
(
Baty et al., 2024
)
. For such an approach to work, the policy mappings of
\mathcal{H}
need to be able to properly explore the large combinatorial set of solutions
\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}})
. The approach amounts to delegating the exploration of
\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}})
to the linear optimization problem

\hat{\bm{y}}_{\bm{x}}(\bm{\theta})\in\argmax_{{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}})}\,\langle{\bm{y}},\bm{\theta}\rangle\,,

(1.3)

where
\hat{\bm{y}}_{\bm{x}}\,:\,\bm{\theta}\in\mathds{R}^{d({\bm{x}})}\mapsto\hat{\bm{y}}_{\bm{x}}(\bm{\theta})\in\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}})
is an oracle returning an optimal solution of (
1.3
), vector
\bm{\theta}
is a direction in
\mathds{R}^{d({\bm{x}})}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard inner product. We assume in this article to have a practically efficient algorithm to solve (
1.3
). Our oracle for fixed
\bm{\theta}
is for instance a LP solver, a MILP solver, a shortest path algorithm,…The statistical model
{\psi}_{\bm{w}}
is then used to find a direction vector
\bm{\theta}
that leads to solutions
{\bm{y}}
with low

. In other words, we consider the following family of parametrized policy mappings

\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{W}}\coloneqq\big\{h_{\bm{w}}\,:\,{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}\mapsto\hat{\bm{y}}_{\bm{x}}({\psi}_{\bm{w}}({\bm{x}}))\in\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}})\,,\ {\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}\big\}\,,

(1.4)

for some parameter space
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathds{R}^{d_{\mathcal{W}}}
. Note that the objective of (
1.3
) nevertheless relies on an embedding of
\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}})
in
\mathds{R}^{d({\bm{x}})}
, whose dimension

As classical in machine learning, we then consider an empirical version
\mathcal{R}_{n}
of the risk
\mathcal{R}
in (

) which is approximated through samples
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
by

\min_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathcal{R}_{n}(h_{\bm{w}}),\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{R}_{n}(h_{\bm{w}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f^{0}\big(h_{\bm{w}}(X_{i}),X_{i}\big).

(1.5)

Unfortunately, due to the combinatorial nature of (
1.3
), this objective is piecewise constant in
{\bm{w}}
, which has two major issues: it makes the optimization problem intractable, and leads to poor generalization properties.
Consequently, we rely also on a perturbation strategy developed in

to turn the linear optimization problem (

) into a family of tractable distributions

, with

\min_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{\bm{w}}),\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{\bm{w}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{E}_{{\bm{y}}\sim p_{\lambda}({\bm{y}}|\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X_{i}))}\big[f^{0}\big({\bm{y}},X_{i}\big)\big],

(1.6)

where

p_{\lambda}({\bm{y}}|\bm{\theta})\coloneqq\mathds{P}_{Z}\big[{\bm{y}}\in\argmax_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}})}\langle{\bm{y}}^{\prime},\bm{\theta}+\lambda Z({\bm{x}})\rangle\big],

with
Z:\mathcal{X}\to\mathds{R}^{d({\bm{x}})}
an isotropic random variable,
e.g.,
Gaussian. For
\lambda=0
, through
p_{\lambda}
we recover (
1.3
), and (
1.6
) coincides with (
1.5
). We refer to the more formal Definitions
1
and
6
for the handling of non-unique optimal
{\bm{y}}^{\prime}
. In practice, we consider a Monte-Carlo approximation of the expectation in (
1.6
) by sampling over
Z

(
Berthet et al., 2020
;
Parmentier, 2025
)
. Through the regularization, we obtain a smooth objective (
1.6
) which is more amenable for optimization, whose error we will also have to control. In this paper, we choose to study the so-called kernel Sum-of-Squares method
(
Rudi et al., 2024
)
since it is among the few methods providing a bound on the optimization error for which the smoothness of the objective function
\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}
can mitigate the curse of dimensionality in the dimension
d_{\mathcal{W}}
of
\mathcal{W}
.

1.2
Discussion on the methodological choices

Why consider a learning problem?

The idea of transforming an optimization problem into a learning one is motivated by industrial practice.
In many real-world scenarios, the problem instances come from a specific but unknown distribution.
For example, an airline optimizes the routes of its airplanes based on a schedule and some operational constraints that do not vary much from one day to another. Learning approaches balance the computational burden between
learning
, when the policy is trained offline on historical data, and there is typically no industrial process deadline implying a limited time budget, and
inference
, when the calibrated policy is used on-the-fly to find a solution for a given instance, and a strong deadline must typically be respected for industrial settings.
While in classical approaches directly optimizing
f^{0}(\cdot,{\bm{x}})
, all the computation time occurs during inference, our approach makes most of the computation occur during learning: only the linear surrogate (
1.3
) needs to be solved during inference. This is helpful in industrial settings, where the full information on the instance is often known at the last minute, which strongly constrains the computing time and forces one to use only objective functions simple enough, such as (
1.3
), to allow for fast optimization algorithms.

Why not approximate
f^{0}
directly?

Since combinatorial algorithms must scale to be useful in OR, practitioners generally simplify (
1.1
) into

\min_{{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}})}\tilde{f}^{0}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}})\,

(1.7)

where
\tilde{f}^{0}
is a possibly-over-simplified deterministic objective amenable to classical algorithms. Our oracle (
1.3
) does fall within this category, however our crucial variable is
{\bm{w}}
, to coordinate between instances. Moreover we do not look to approximate the values of
f^{0}
by
\tilde{f}^{0}
, and we do not discard
f^{0}
since it is crucial in (
1.6
). We do wish nevertheless for
\hat{\bm{y}}_{\bm{x}}(\bm{\theta})
to be close to
\argmin f^{0}(\cdot,{\bm{x}})
, in terms of values of
f^{0}
. The learning approaches focus on optimizing the true objective
f^{0}
, while the estimate-then-optimize approach forgets about
f^{0}
and considers only its approximation
\tilde{f}^{0}
during the optimization phase of (
1.7
), and may overfit
\tilde{f}^{0}
.

Why consider a linear oracle in (
1.3
)?

The choice of the oracle (
1.3
) amounts to an architecture choice for a neural network. Indeed the oracle appears as a final composition in (
1.4
) (see
Figure

1
), hence we consider it as a combinatorial layer in our pipeline (see
Figure

2
below). This layer is application-dependent and critical for performance. A good surrogate model should be simple enough to have efficient algorithms, while being expressive enough to adapt to many settings. We consider a linear oracle because it provides such a balance.

Linear oracles are the de facto choice in optimization-augmented learning because the framework only needs a linear minimization oracle over the polytope
\mathcal{C}=\conv(\mathcal{Y})
, leaving the actual solver free. By Meyer’s theorem
(
Meyer, 1974
)
, the convex hull of feasible solutions of any bounded MILP is itself a polytope, so any MILP-formulable problem fits as a linear layer. Additionally, our approach only requires linearity in
{\bm{\theta}}
: for a layer
\max_{{\bm{z}}}{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}\phi({\bm{z}})
with non-linear mapping
\phi
, we can define
{\bm{y}}=\phi({\bm{z}})
to return to our setting.

Why not assume access to a training set?

Finding a suitable policy in
\mathcal{H}
requires designing a learning algorithm that selects a parameter
{\bm{w}}
that leads to a good performance in practice.
Two typologies of learning strategies have been contrasted in
(
Bengio et al., 2021
)
: supervised learning, which is also referred to as imitation learning or inverse optimization depending on the community, and risk minimization, a.k.a. learning by experience. In risk minimization as in (
1.5
), one does not have samples of near-optimal solutions. By contrast supervised learning approaches assume that the training set contains instance solution pairs
({\bm{x}}_{1},\bar{\bm{y}}_{1}),\ldots,({\bm{x}}_{n},\bar{\bm{y}}_{n})
of (
1.1
). In this setting, the original objective
f^{0}
is dismissed, and surrogate losses
\ell_{imitation}
evaluate how far the model output
\hat{\bm{y}}_{\bm{x}}({\psi}_{\bm{w}}({\bm{x}}_{i}))\in\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}}_{i})
is from the training set target
\bar{\bm{y}}_{i}
. Nevertheless this setting still fits our framework by setting
\tilde{\bm{x}}=({\bm{x}},\bar{\bm{y}})
, assuming the existence of a joint law on
(X,\bar{Y})
, and setting
f^{0}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{y}})\coloneqq\ell_{imitation}({\bm{x}}_{i},\bar{\bm{y}}_{i},{\bm{y}})
.

Why focus on the risk on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{W}}
rather than on any measurable
h
?

Our excess risk bounds are stated with respect to the best policy in the parametric class
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{W}}
(cf. (
1.4
)) instead of the Bayes optimal policy over all measurable mappings. This restriction is deliberate for several, complementary reasons. For any (possibly unattainable) measurable
h
, the classical risk decomposition writes

\mathcal{R}(\hat{h})-\inf_{h\,\text{meas.}}\mathcal{R}(h)=\underbrace{\big(\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{W}}}\mathcal{R}(h)-\inf_{h\,\text{meas.}}\mathcal{R}(h)\big)}_{\text{approximation / misspecification}}+\underbrace{\big(\mathcal{R}(\hat{h})-\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{W}}}\mathcal{R}(h)\big)}_{\text{estimation + optimization + smoothing (this paper)}}.

The first (misspecification) term depends intricately on the chosen architecture (depth, width, feature design, encoding of
\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}})
, etc.) and the combinatorial framework encapsulated in
f^{0}
. This term is largely orthogonal to the
geometric
phenomena (normal fan structure and perturbation smoothing) we study in this paper. By focusing on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{W}}
, we isolate the three components we can control: statistical estimation, optimization of
\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}
, and the perturbation bias under only a minimal assumption on
f^{0}
(boundedness). Handling the first (approximation) term lies outside the scope of this paper: it is architecture- and application-dependent and would require additional modelling of the instance distribution together with regularity assumptions on (near-)optimal solution paths.

Are there other frameworks for bounding the optimization error?

To the best of our knowledge, many global optimization algorithms converge because they provably do an exhaustive search, as is the case for DIRECT for instance
(
Jones et al., 1993
, Section 5)
. Consequently, their convergence bounds are most often affected by the curse of dimensionality. Here we have an advantage since
\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}
is a smooth function thanks to the parameter
\lambda>0
. This incites us to use a specific kernel-based approach recently suggested in
Rudi et al. (2024)
to leverage the smoothness of the function
\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}
in our theoretical bounds. In practice however, we recommend using SGD to leverage existing solvers.

Concerning SGD or other gradient-based solvers, the perturbation
p_{\lambda}
was introduced in
(
Berthet et al., 2020
, Definition 2.1)
to compute the needed gradient.

(
Dalle et al., 2022
, Proposition 4.1)
provides the formula for the gradient
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}
of the objective of (
1.6
), and
\nabla_{\bm{w}}
is then deduced by the chain rule.
However this is a score-based estimation of the gradient, which is known to have a high variance.
We refer to
Dalle et al. (2022)
for more insights on differentiating through combinatorial layers.

In a nutshell, instead of approximating the objective function
f^{0}
in (
1.1
) by a simpler
\tilde{f}^{0}
as in (
1.7
), we study an average error
\mathds{E}_{X}\big[f^{0}(h(X),X)\big]
as in (
1.2
). The latter involves a statistical model
\mathcal{H}
defined in (
1.4
) and derived from the linear combinatorial optimization problem (
1.3
). The role of the model is to explore efficiently the solution set
\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}})
and we do not seek to approximate
f^{0}
in any way. Our Theorem
3
provides error guarantees when
n
goes to
+\infty
to show the convergence of problem (
1.5
) to (
1.2
). Note that convergence is towards the best policy in the hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{W}}
defined in (
1.4
), which may be far from the optimal Bayes policy if
\mathcal{H}
has been poorly chosen.

1.3
Related works

Relevant applications

Parametrized policies
h_{{\bm{w}}}
defined in Equation (
1.4
) have demonstrated state-of-the-art empirical performance across several families of combinatorial decision problems. We briefly outline key application patterns while keeping the focus on why a
policy-learning with linear oracle
viewpoint is attractive. In
black-box optimization
, evaluating
f^{0}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}})
requires an expensive simulation (
e.g.,
delay propagation, stochastic congestion), and directly solving (
1.1
) is prohibitive when
|\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}})|
is large. Rather than constructing a global simplified objective
\tilde{f}^{0}
, we learn a direction
\psi_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}})
that is passed to a tractable linear oracle, using it as an
exploration mechanism
of
\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}})
rather than as a pointwise approximation of
f^{0}
,
e.g.,

Vu et al. (2017)
. This also allows for
scaling and fast heuristics
as, even if
f^{0}
is explicit, exact solvers or MILP formulations may fail to scale. Learning
{\bm{w}}
shifts instead the heavy computation to offline: one learns
h_{\bm{w}}
on moderate size instances (offline) and infers large size instance solutions using one linear oracle call. This yields high-quality fast heuristics in large-scale scheduling
(
Parmentier and T’Kindt, 2023
)
and bi-level contexts
(
Ferrarini et al., 2025
)
. Another example is
stochastic optimization
where
f^{0}
embeds expectations over uncertain parameters (
e.g.,
stochastic programming
(
Shapiro et al., 2021
)
), and traditional decomposition handles only moderate sizes. Recent studies on surrogate policy learning have shown strong performance in stochastic vehicle scheduling
(
Parmentier, 2022
)
, two-stage structured approximations
(
Dalle et al., 2022
;
Parmentier, 2025
)
, and routing
(
Ahmed et al., 2024
)
. Last but not least, for
contextual and multi-stage variants
of stochastic optimization
(
Sadana et al., 2024
)
(see Example
1
below), the side information replaces explicit conditional expectation estimation, and surrogate-policy extensions towards multi-stage or dynamic routing and equilibrium-inspired flow models appear in challenge-winning or emerging work
(
Baty et al., 2024
;
Jungel et al., 2022
;
Klein et al., 2022
;
Jungel et al., 2025b
;
Greif et al., 2024
;
Jungel et al., 2025a
)
.

The present work provides geometric and probabilistic principles (normal fan structure + perturbation smoothing) explaining these outcomes.

To illustrate our theoretical guarantees, we present three examples, one generic and two more industrial.

Example 1
.

(Contextual Stochastic Optimization)

Consider a stochastic optimization problem where the cost over a solution is influenced by some noise
\xi
. The decision maker does not observe
\xi
, but has access to some contextual data
\tilde{X}
that is correlated to
\xi
.
A solution is a policy
h
that maps any context
\tilde{\bm{x}}
to a feasible decision
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}(\tilde{\bm{x}})
given
\tilde{\bm{x}}
.
Given a joint distribution over
(\tilde{X},\xi)
and a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
for
h
, the goal of contextual stochastic optimization is to find the policy that minimizes the expected risk

\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\mathds{E}\Big[f^{\mathrm{c}}\big(h(\tilde{X}),\tilde{X},\xi\big)\Big],

where
f^{c}(\bm{y},\tilde{\bm{x}},\xi)
is the cost incurred if decision
\bm{y}
is taken under context
\tilde{\bm{x}}
and uncertainty
\xi
. An optimal policy is provided by the conditional distribution

h^{\star}:\tilde{\bm{x}}\longmapsto\argmin_{{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}(\tilde{\bm{x}})}\mathds{E}\Big[f^{\mathrm{c}}\big({\bm{y}},\tilde{X},\xi\big)\Big|\tilde{X}=\tilde{\bm{x}}\Big].

(1.8)

In contextual stochastic optimization however, see
Sadana et al. (2024)
for a survey of this emergent field, the joint distribution over
(\tilde{X},\xi)
is unknown and we only have access to a training set
(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{1},\bm{\xi}_{1}),\ldots,(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{n},\bm{\xi}_{n})
.
This makes the optimal policy (
1.8
) impractical.

Contextual stochastic optimization can be considered as a special case of our setting. It suffices to define
X
as
(\tilde{X},\xi)
, and
f^{0}(\bm{y},\bm{x})
as
f^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{y},\tilde{\bm{x}},\bm{\xi})
for
\bm{x}=(\tilde{\bm{x}},\bm{\xi})
. We then restrict ourselves to models
{\psi}_{\bm{w}}(\bm{x})
of the form
\tilde{\psi}_{\bm{w}}(\tilde{\bm{x}})
that exploit only the contextual information and not the noise. Similarly we can restrict the policies
h
not to depend on
\bm{\xi}
to preserve the fact that
\bm{\xi}
is uncertain at decision, i.e.
h(\tilde{\bm{x}},\bm{\xi})=\tilde{h}(\tilde{\bm{x}})
for some
\tilde{h}
. Common practice in stochastic optimization would have suggested defining
X
as
\tilde{X}
and

f^{0}(\bm{y},\bm{x})
as the objective of (
1.8
). However this would require the conditional distribution of
\xi
given
\tilde{X}={\bm{x}}_{i}
, which we do not know.

Example 2
.

(Stochastic Vehicle Scheduling
(
Parmentier, 2022
)
)
Consider a set of tasks with fixed begin and end times operated by a fleet of vehicles. When a task is late, the assigned vehicle may propagate the delay to the next tasks. The goal is to build the sequence of tasks operated by each vehicle in such a way that each task is operated by exactly one vehicle and the expected cost of delay
f^{0}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}})
is minimized.
The instance
{\bm{x}}
can typically be encoded as a digraph
D=(V,A)
where
V
is the set of tasks, and there is an arc
a
in
A
between
v
and
v^{\prime}
if these two tasks can be operated by the same vehicle in a sequence, and a vector
\bm{\phi}(a,{\bm{x}})
of additional information for each arc
a
. Since a solution corresponds to a partition of the vertices of an acyclic digraph into paths, the set of feasible solutions
\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}})
can be identified with the vertices of the flow polytope over
D
. As a statistical model, one can take
\psi_{\bm{w}}:\bm{x}\mapsto\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{a})_{a\in A}
with
\theta_{a}=\langle\bm{w},\bm{\phi}(a,{\bm{x}})\rangle
for each
a
.
The corresponding policy is illustrated on Figure
1
. Aircraft routing problems solved routinely by airlines are variants of this problem. In particular, the stochastic version aims at controlling delay propagation. In practice, solvers used in production typically address the linear oracle. Consequently, switching from the deterministic and delay-agnostic approaches currently in use to our policy that avoids delay propagation does not alter the inference algorithm in production; it only requires a change in the parametrization of the inference solver. While computing this new parametrization involves solving a computationally intensive learning problem, this step is carried out during the offline learning rather than during on-the-fly inference.

Example 3
.

(Single Machine Scheduling
(
Parmentier and T’Kindt, 2023
)
)
In single machine scheduling problems,
n
jobs have to be performed on a given machine. A solution is a permutation of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
giving the order in which the jobs are processed. Several objectives can be used. In the reference above, jobs have release times, before which they cannot be started, and processing times, which indicate the time needed by the machine to operate the job. The vector
\bm{y}
encodes a permutation, and the goal here is to minimize
f^{0}(\bm{y},{\bm{x}})
which is equal to the sum (the average) of the job completion times, taking into account the release time.
Permutations
\bm{y}
can be encoded by vectors
\bm{y}_{i}
giving the position of job
i
in the permutation. Optimizing a linear objective
\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{y}\rangle
amounts to sorting
\bm{\theta}
, and we can therefore use a sorting algorithm as a linear minimization oracle.

Learning algorithms and generalization bounds.

A prominent approach combining linear surrogates (
1.3
) and risk minimization is “Smart Predict then Optimize” (SPO)
(
Elmachtoub and Grigas, 2022
)
.
SPO replaces the discontinuous regret loss with a convex surrogate (SPO+), leveraging the duality of linear programming to convexify the decision boundary.
However, generalization guarantees in this framework
(
El Balghiti et al., 2023
)
rely heavily on the linearity of the objective and fixed feasible sets
\mathcal{Y}
independent of
{\bm{x}}
.
Consequently, the duality arguments underpinning SPO+ collapse when applied to generic, potentially black-box non-linear functions
f^{0}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}})
with instance-dependent decision boundaries, as there is no natural convex surrogate available.

In contrast, we investigate the risk minimization paradigm (
1.6
) where the loss is a smoothed version of the objective.
This approach has received limited attention as discussed in
(
Parmentier, 2025
)
, with exceptions found in
Qi et al. (2021)
,
Ferber et al. (2023)
, and recent works on contextual stochastic optimization
(
Bouvier et al., 2025
;
Hoppe et al., 2025
)
.
While we share the SPO goal of minimizing downstream decision regret rather than prediction error, we address the intractability of the empirical risk (
1.5
) via
randomized smoothing

(
Berthet et al., 2020
)
rather than convex relaxation.

This methodological divergence necessitates a distinct theoretical analysis.
We cannot rely on margin-based bounds such as the “distance to degeneracy” employed by
El Balghiti et al. (2019)
, as we do not bound the complexity of a discrete loss directly.
Instead, we must control the
smoothing bias

|\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}-\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon_{0}}|
.
To this end, we introduce the
Uniform Weak (UW) property
(Section
3
) and employ probabilistic assumptions (Assumption

(

UW

ε
0

)

) rather than Lipschitz continuity of the loss.
This allows us to bound the approximation error and ensure that minimizing the smoothed surrogate yields a performant solution for the true combinatorial problem.

Finally, our problem relates to data-driven algorithm design
(
Gupta and Roughgarden, 2016
;
Balcan, 2021
)
.
While
Balcan et al. (2021)
provide guarantees for piecewise decomposable risks, their results do not apply to our smoothed, regularized risk.
Our Theorem
3
provides the counterpart to their results, leveraging tools tailored to our smoothed non-linear context.

1.4
Contributions

In this paper, we build upon the normal fan geometry (see Section
2
) associated with the linear optimization program (
1.3
) and upon a perturbation approach to deliver a generalization bound (Theorem
1
) that controls the perturbation bias, the statistical learning error, and the optimization error.

Perturbation makes the learning problem smooth and amenable to gradient descent methods. Furthermore, we prove non-asymptotic guarantees on the performance of the learned policy, as well as convergence rates towards the best policy encoded by (
1.4
).

To obtain our performance guarantees, we introduce the
fan-crossing probability

q_{\bm{w}}(\lambda)
(see (
3.2
)), which measures the probability that a perturbation of scale
\lambda
changes the oracle solution. This quantity controls the perturbation bias. To bound it, we introduce two complementary conditions: the uniform weak (UW) moment property, which yields a sub-linear bound on the bias, and the uniformly bounded density (UBD) property, which yields a sharp linear bound. Both properties quantify the interplay of the statistical model and the normal fan of (
1.3
). We show that UW holds under mild assumptions on the statistical model
\psi_{\bm{w}}
and on
\mathds{P}_{X}
, and we illustrate this on several examples in Section
3.2.4
.

Definition 1

(Surrogate policy)
.

Let

, and define the internal cone radius as

\rho(\bm{\theta})\coloneqq\sup\Big\{r>0\ \Big|\ \forall{\bm{u}}\in B(0,1)\,,\exists{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}})\text{ s.t. }\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{F}_{\bm{y}}\text{ and }\bm{\theta}+r{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{F}_{\bm{y}}\Big\}\,,

(2.2)

where

is the standard Euclidean ball. We define the surrogate policy measure

\hat{\bm{y}}_{\bm{x}}(\bm{\theta})\coloneqq\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}})}p_{0}({\bm{y}}|\bm{\theta})\delta_{\bm{y}}

where

{\bm{y}}
and

p_{0}({\bm{y}}|\bm{\theta})\coloneqq\mathds{P}_{U}\big[\bm{\theta}+\rho(\bm{\theta})U\in\mathcal{F}_{\bm{y}}\big]

(2.3)

where

is uniformly distributed on

Note that

measures the proportion of the normal cone
\mathcal{F}_{\bm{y}}
locally around
\bm{\theta}
. The quantity
\rho(\bm{\theta})
will have an important role in our context of perturbations

\bm{\theta}+\mathbf{z}
can only point to a
{\bm{y}}\neq\hat{\bm{y}}_{\bm{x}}(\bm{\theta})
if
\|\mathbf{z}\|_{2}\geq\rho(\bm{\theta})
. Perturbations too small do not affect the outcome.

Remark 2

(Generic case and an abuse of notation)
.

Generically, a direction

and

\hat{\bm{y}}_{\bm{x}}(\bm{\theta})
is a Dirac mass at point
{\bm{y}}
and, by an abuse of notation, we denote by
\hat{\bm{y}}_{\bm{x}}(\bm{\theta})
the vector
{\bm{y}}\in\mathds{R}^{d({\bm{x}})}
. To ease notation, we will consider that, by convention,

f(\hat{\bm{y}}_{\bm{x}}(\bm{\theta}))\coloneqq\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}({\bm{x}})}p_{0}({\bm{y}}|\bm{\theta})f({\bm{y}})

for all functions

2.2
The regularized and empirical risks

In our algorithms, we optimize an empirical and regularized version of the risk
\mathcal{R}
, which we now introduce.
We observe
n

X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
drawn independently according to a (possibly unknown) distribution
\mathds{P}_{X}

{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}

h_{{\bm{w}}}\,:\,{\bm{x}}\mapsto\hat{\bm{y}}_{\bm{x}}(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}}))
and introduce the regularized population risk
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}
and its empirical version
\mathcal{R}_{n,\varepsilon}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(h_{\bm{w}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathds{E}_{X,Z}\big[f^{0}(\hat{\bm{y}}_{\bm{x}}({\psi}_{\bm{w}}(X)+\varepsilon Z(X)),X)\big]\,,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{n,\varepsilon}(h_{\bm{w}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{E}_{Z}\Big\{\big[f^{0}(\hat{\bm{y}}_{\bm{x}}({\psi}_{\bm{w}}(X_{i})+\varepsilon Z(X_{i})),X_{i})\big]\Big\}\,,

where

Definition 3

(Law of the perturbation)
.

Given

has the same law as

is uniformly distributed on the unit Euclidean sphere of

This perturbation is inspired by the one used in
Berthet et al. (2020)

Parmentier (2025)

allows one to control the tail of the distribution of

.

Remark 4

(On forthcoming Gaussian assumption)
.

When explicit constants are needed in bounds involving

(

Gauss

)

Remark 5

The perturbed vector

For later use in our analysis we introduce the following quantity.

The (non-perturbed)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(\mathcal{H})

2.3
Guarantees

Policy model misspecification error.

We define the policy model misspecification error as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(\mathcal{H})-\mathds{E}_{X}\big[f^{0}({\bm{y}}^{0}(X),X)\big]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}_{X,Z}\big[f^{0}(\hat{\bm{y}}_{\bm{x}}(\psi_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}}(X)+{\varepsilon_{0}}Z(X)),X)\big]-\mathds{E}_{X}\big[f^{0}({\bm{y}}^{0}(X),X)\big]\geq 0

where
{\bm{y}}^{0}({\bm{x}})
minimizes (
1.1
).

In
\mathds{P}_{X}
-average, the quantity
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{0}}
measures how far the policy
\hat{\bm{y}}_{\bm{x}}\big(\psi_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}}(X)+\varepsilon_{0}Z(X)\big)
is from matching the quality of
{\bm{y}}^{0}(X)
for the loss
f^{0}
. Equivalently, it quantifies how often the direction
\psi_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}}(X)+\varepsilon_{0}Z(X)
fails to fall inside normal cones
\mathcal{F}_{{\bm{y}}}
corresponding to solutions
{\bm{y}}
with small excess loss
f^{0}({\bm{y}},X)-f^{0}({\bm{y}}^{0}(X),X)
. Controlling the term
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(\mathcal{H})
would require additional regularity assumptions on
{\bm{y}}^{0}
and
f^{0}
, which lies beyond the scope of this paper; we therefore focus on the optimal risk in (
2.7
). In practice, choosing an appropriate architecture for
\psi_{\bm{w}}
is what keeps the model misspecification error small. Empirical evidence from the literature (see Section
1.3
) shows that, in many applications, the learned policy attains a small expected optimality gap, indicating that the policy model misspecification error is limited.

The model misspecification error can sometimes be
explicitly
bounded. If an approximation (or heuristic) algorithm
\mathsf{A}
for (
1.1
) with ratio
\alpha\geq 1
can be encoded as a policy

, then

\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(\mathcal{H})\leq\alpha\,\mathds{E}_{X}[f^{0}({\bm{y}}^{0}(X),X)]+\varepsilon_{0}\Delta_{\text{pert.}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(\mathcal{H})\leq(\alpha-1)\,\mathds{E}_{X}[f^{0}({\bm{y}}^{0}(X),X)]+\varepsilon_{0}\Delta_{\text{pert.}},

where
\Delta_{\text{pert.}}
is a constant which may depend on the law
Z
(perturbation). Hence a certified ratio inside
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{W}}
yields a constructive upper bound; choosing to enrich the features or the architecture can only tighten this bound. We refer to
(
Parmentier, 2025
, Section 4.3)
for this structured approximation viewpoint on the maximum weight two-stage spanning tree problem and the objective function approximation framework.

We focus on the

phenomena (normal fan structure and perturbation smoothing) by isolating the three components we can control: statistical estimation, optimization of
\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}
, and the perturbation bias.

We will introduce an estimator
h_{{\bm{w}}_{M,n,\lambda}}
which depends on some complexity integer parameter

, the sample size

, and a real valued tuning parameter

such that

. Assume that

exists, then one has the following decomposition of the error, with each term handled separately,

\displaystyle 0\leq\;

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(h_{{\bm{w}}_{M,n,\lambda}})-\mathds{E}_{X}\big[f^{0}({\bm{y}}^{0}(X),X)\big]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(h_{{\bm{w}}_{M,n,\lambda}})-\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}_{M,n,\lambda}})}_{\text{Theorem\penalty\ \ref{thm:reg_excess}}}+\underbrace{\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}_{M,n,\lambda}})-\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}_{M,n,\lambda}})}_{\text{Theorem\penalty\ \ref{thm:A3_statistical}}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}})-\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}})}_{\text{Theorem\penalty\ \ref{thm:A3_statistical}}}+\underbrace{\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}})-\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(h_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}})}_{\text{Theorem\penalty\ \ref{thm:reg_excess}}}

\displaystyle|\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(h_{{\bm{w}}})-\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}})|

\displaystyle\leq C\lambda^{\tau}\mathrm{polylog}(\lambda)

(Theorem

Improved performances via the Linear Perturbation Bias

As stated in Theorem
1
, the perturbation bias scales sub-linearly as
\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{\tau}\mathrm{polylog}(\lambda))
. This fractional rate stems from the generality of the Uniform Weak (UW) property
(
see Section

, which restricts the exponent to

to accommodate complex distributions.

However, this rate can be strictly improved to a

of

under slightly more regular geometric conditions (sharpness is proven in Proposition

uniformly bounded probability density on a compact support
, then the probability mass falling near the normal fan boundaries scales linearly with the perturbation
\lambda
. Under these conditions, the excess risk bound established in Theorem

improves to, for all

0\leq\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}_{M,n,\lambda}})-\mathds{E}_{X}\big[f^{0}({\bm{y}}^{0}(X),X)\big]\leq C\lambda+\mathcal{O}_{\mathds{P}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{\lambda\sqrt{n}}+\bigg(\frac{1}{\lambda(M/\log M)^{\frac{1}{d_{\mathcal{W}}}}}\bigg)^{s-\frac{d_{\mathcal{W}}}{2}}\Bigg]+\mathcal{E}_{0}(\mathcal{H})\,.

(2.8)

where
\mathcal{E}_{0}(\mathcal{H})=\inf_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathcal{R}_{0}(h_{\bm{w}})-\mathds{E}_{X}\big[f^{0}({\bm{y}}^{0}(X),X)\big]\geq 0
and the k-SoS bound is given again up to a small a posteriori error. We formalize this exact geometric behavior and prove the sharper
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
perturbation bias later in Proposition

(Section
3.2
) and Theorem

1
under Assumption

(

UBD

)

.

Perturbation level guidance.

Our excess risk bound is of the type
statistical to computational
, showing the effect of regularization
\lambda
, sampling
n
, and the complexity of the algorithm
M
, here the k-SoS algorithm. It shows that, when
\lambda
goes to zero, the excess risk goes to zero if
\sqrt{n}
dominates
1/\lambda
and
M
dominates
1/\lambda^{d_{\mathcal{W}}}
. The latter is a so-called curse of dimensionality, the complexity of the k-SoS algorithm being exponential in the dimension
{d_{\mathcal{W}}}
, while
1/\lambda
can then be interpreted as a smoothness parameter. Indeed,
the domination
1/\lambda
by
\sqrt{n}
shows quantitatively how many sample points the practitioner needs when diminishing the regularization
\lambda
. The optimization error is for its part based on the number of sampled points
M
on
\mathcal{W}
where the objective
\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}
is evaluated,
s
corresponding to which Sobolev space of smoothness
s
the function
\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}
belongs to, see
Section

3.4
. In practice, for a Gaussian smoothing,
s\gg d_{\mathcal{W}}
and could in principle be taken infinite. However some constants hidden in the
O_{\mathds{P}}
can depend exponentially on

, so we cannot take

(

3.2
Regularization by perturbation and perturbation bias

3.2.1
The fan-crossing probability and the perturbation bias

We now move on to the proof of Theorem
1
. A central role is played by the following quantity, which we call the
fan-crossing probability
:

q_{\bm{w}}(\lambda):=\mathds{E}_{X}\mathds{P}_{Z}\Big[\|Z(X)\|_{2}>\frac{\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X))}{\lambda}\,\big|\,X\Big]=\mathds{E}_{X}\mathds{P}_{R}\Big[{R(X)}>\frac{\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X))}{\lambda}\,\big|\,X\Big]\,,

(3.2)

for
\lambda>0
and
{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}
. By dominated convergence, note that
q_{\bm{w}}(\lambda)
tends to zero as
\lambda
goes to zero and that
q_{\bm{w}}(\lambda)
tends to one as
\lambda
goes to infinity. Note also that the function
\lambda\mapsto q_{\bm{w}}(\lambda)\in[0,1]
is non-decreasing. Intuitively,
q_{\bm{w}}(\lambda)
measures the probability that a perturbation of scale
\lambda
is large enough to push the direction
\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X)
across a boundary of the normal fan, thereby changing the oracle solution. The perturbation bias is controlled by
q_{\bm{w}}(\lambda)
, as the following proposition shows.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\varepsilon_{0}\geq 0
and
\lambda>0
be such that
\lambda\geq\varepsilon_{0}
. It holds that, for all
{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}
, with
\operatorname{osc}
defined in (
1.9
),

\big|\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{\bm{w}})-\mathcal{R}(h_{\bm{w}})\big|\leq 2\operatorname{osc}(f^{0})q_{\bm{w}}(\lambda)\,\quad\text{and}\quad\big|\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{\bm{w}})-\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(h_{\bm{w}})\big|\leq 4\operatorname{osc}(f^{0})q_{\bm{w}}(\lambda)\,.

Proof.

Recall that
p_{0}({\bm{y}}|\psi_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:def_p_0}}}{{=}}\mathds{P}[\psi_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}})+\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}}))U({\bm{x}})\in\mathcal{F}_{\bm{y}}]
where
U
is uniform on
B(0,1)
and

p_{\lambda}({\bm{y}}|\psi_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:def_p_lambda}}}{{=}}\mathds{P}_{Z}[\psi_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}})/\lambda+Z({\bm{x}})\in\mathcal{F}_{\bm{y}}]=\mathds{P}_{Z}[\psi_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}})/\lambda+R({\bm{x}})U({\bm{x}})\in\mathcal{F}_{\bm{y}}]\,.

Note that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{\bm{w}})-\mathcal{R}(h_{\bm{w}})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:reg_risk_solution}}}{{=}}\mathds{E}_{X}\big[\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}(X)}p_{\lambda}({\bm{y}}|\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X))f^{0}({\bm{y}},X)-\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}(X)}p_{0}({\bm{y}}|\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X))f^{0}({\bm{y}},X)\big]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}_{X,R,U}\big[\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}(X)}\underbrace{\Big(\mathbbm{1}\big(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X)/\lambda+R(X)U(X)\in\mathcal{F}_{\bm{y}}\big)-\mathbbm{1}\big(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X)+\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X))U(X)\in\mathcal{F}_{\bm{y}}\big)\Big)}_{\eqqcolon A(X,{\bm{y}},R,U)}f^{0}({\bm{y}},X)\big]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:def_rho}}}{{=}}\mathds{E}_{X,R,U}\big[\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}(X)}\Big(\mathbbm{1}\big(R(X)\leq\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X))/\lambda\big)+\mathbbm{1}\big(R(X)>\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X))/\lambda\big)\Big)A(X,{\bm{y}},R,U)f^{0}({\bm{y}},X)\big]

where we have introduced the random variable
A(X,{\bm{y}},R,U)
, using the definition of probabilities with indicators
\mathbbm{1}
. By a zero-sum argument, one can replace
f^{0}({\bm{y}},X)
by
f^{0}({\bm{y}},X)-\inf f^{0}
in the calculation above.

By definition of
\rho
, if
R(X)\leq\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X))/\lambda
then
\mathbbm{1}\big(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X)/\lambda+R(X)U(X)\in\mathcal{F}_{\bm{y}}\big)
is equal to
\mathbbm{1}\big(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X)+\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X))U(X)\in\mathcal{F}_{\bm{y}}\big)
. Hence
A(X,{\bm{y}},R,U)\mathbbm{1}\big(R(X)\leq\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X))/\lambda\big)=0
.

We therefore have:

\displaystyle\big|\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{\bm{w}})-\mathcal{R}(h_{\bm{w}})\big|

\displaystyle=\big|\mathds{E}_{X,R,U}\big[\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}(X)}\mathbbm{1}\big(R(X)>\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X))/\lambda\big)A(X,{\bm{y}},R,U)(f^{0}({\bm{y}},X)-\inf f^{0})\big]\big|

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}_{X,R,U}\big[\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}(X)}\mathbbm{1}\big(R(X)>\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X))/\lambda\big)\,|A(X,{\bm{y}},R,U)|\,(f^{0}({\bm{y}},X)-\inf f^{0})\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}_{X,R,U}\big[\operatorname{osc}(f^{0})\mathbbm{1}\big(R(X)>\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X))/\lambda\big)\underbrace{\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}(X)}|A(X,{\bm{y}},R,U)|}_{\leq 2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2\operatorname{osc}(f^{0})\mathds{E}_{X}\mathds{P}_{R}\big(R(X)>\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X))/\lambda|X\big)=2\operatorname{osc}(f^{0})q_{\bm{w}}(\lambda).

Using that the function
\lambda\mapsto q_{\bm{w}}(\lambda)\in[0,1]
is non-decreasing, we have

\displaystyle\big|\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{\bm{w}})-\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(h_{\bm{w}})\big|

\displaystyle\leq\big|\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{\bm{w}})-\mathcal{R}(h_{\bm{w}})\big|+\big|\mathcal{R}(h_{\bm{w}})-\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(h_{\bm{w}})\big|

\displaystyle\leq 2\operatorname{osc}(f^{0})q_{\bm{w}}(\lambda)+2\operatorname{osc}(f^{0})q_{\bm{w}}(\varepsilon_{0})

\displaystyle\leq 4\operatorname{osc}(f^{0})q_{\bm{w}}(\lambda)\,,

which concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3
.

Under Condition

(

Sol

)

, it holds that

\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{H})-\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{H})\leq 2\operatorname{osc}(f^{0})q_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}}(\lambda)\,.

Proof.

Observe that
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{H})=\mathds{E}_{X}\big[\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}(X)}p_{0}({\bm{y}}|\psi_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}}(X))f^{0}({\bm{y}},X)\big]
and remark that it holds

\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{H})\leq\mathds{E}_{X}\big[\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}(X)}p_{\lambda}({\bm{y}}|\psi_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}}(X))f^{0}({\bm{y}},X)\big]\,,

which gives the result by substracting the first equality.
∎

The remainder of this section is devoted to bounding the fan-crossing probability
q_{\bm{w}}(\lambda)
. We introduce two complementary conditions: the Uniform Weak (UW) moment property, which yields a sub-linear bound, and the Uniformly Bounded Density (UBD) property, which yields a sharp linear bound.

3.2.2
The uniform weak (UW) moment property

The following property provides a general framework for controlling
q_{\bm{w}}(\lambda)
.

Property
(
\mathrm{UW}_{\varepsilon_{0}}
)
.

For all
\tau\in(0,1)
, there exists a positive constant
C_{\varepsilon_{0},\tau}>0
such that

\forall{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}\,,\ \mathds{E}_{X,Z}\bigg[\Big(\frac{\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X)+\varepsilon_{0}Z(X))}{\sqrt{d(X)}}\Big)^{-\tau}\bigg]\leq C_{\varepsilon_{0},\tau}\,.

Remark 9
.

This property quantifies how likely the perturbed direction
\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X)+\varepsilon_{0}Z(X)
is close to the boundary of some cone of the normal fan. By Markov’s inequality, it holds

\displaystyle\forall t>0\,,\quad\mathds{P}_{X,Z}\Big[{\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X)+\varepsilon_{0}Z(X))}\leq t\sqrt{d(X)}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}_{X,Z}\Big[\Big(\frac{\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X)+\varepsilon_{0}Z(X))}{\sqrt{d(X)}}\Big)^{-\tau}\geq t^{-\tau}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq C_{\varepsilon_{0},\tau}t^{\tau}\,,

whenever

(

UW

ε
0

)

holds. It implies that

\displaystyle\forall t>0\,,\quad\mathds{P}_{X,Z}\Big[{\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X)+\varepsilon_{0}Z(X))}\leq t\sqrt{d(X)}\Big]\leq\max(1,C_{\varepsilon_{0},\tau}t)=\mathds{P}\big[\mathcal{U}(0,1)\leq C_{\varepsilon_{0},\tau}t\big]\,,

where
\mathcal{U}(0,1)
is the uniform distribution on
(0,1)
independent of
(X,Z)
. Hence, one gets the stochastic ordering

{\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X)+\varepsilon_{0}Z(X))}\leq\frac{\sqrt{d(X)}}{C_{\varepsilon_{0},\tau}}\,\mathcal{U}(0,1)\,.

If we further assume that
\lim\sup_{\tau\in(0,1)}C_{\varepsilon_{0},\tau}<\infty
, this latter condition would have been equivalent to Property

(

UW

ε
0

)

by the above reasoning. Hence, Property

(

UW

ε
0

)

guarantees that the distance to the boundary of normal cones of the perturbed direction
\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X)+\varepsilon_{0}Z(X)
is stochastically dominated by some uniform distribution and cannot concentrate too much mass close to
0
.

Nevertheless, Property

(

UW

ε
0

)

requires
\tau\in(0,1)
. To reach
\tau=1
, an alternative is to directly require a continuous uniformly bounded density, as discussed in
Section

3.2.3
; this also avoids the issues associated with concentrating mass at the boundary of the normal fans.

3.2.3
The uniformly bounded density (UBD) property

Under stronger regularity, the fan-crossing probability admits a sharper, linear bound. We single out the following condition.

Property
(
UBD
)
.

For each partition
G\in\mathcal{G}
, there exist a compact set
K_{G}\subset\mathds{R}^{d(G)}
and a constant
C_{\psi,G}>0
such that, for all
{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}
, the conditional push-forward law of
\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)
given
X\in\mathcal{X}_{G}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathds{R}^{d(G)}
, with density uniformly bounded by
C_{\psi,G}
, and supported on
K_{G}
.

Remark 11

(Examples satisfying Property

(

UBD

)

and other cases of Proposition

)
.

Practically, features are generally bounded, and assuming that
X
has a compact support is not restrictive.
For instance, in many transportation and routing problems, the instance

consists of continuous exogenous features (e.g., meteorological data, continuous traffic density) naturally bounded within a physical range.

However, assuming a density of
X
that is uniformly bounded and absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure might be more restrictive given that some labels might be integer-valued.
If the model
\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)
predicting the edge costs
\bm{\theta}\in\mathds{R}^{|E|}
is a sufficiently rich (full-rank) linear or smooth mapping, the induced distribution of the predicted costs naturally avoids singular concentrations and inherits a bounded density.
Property

(

UBD

)

—having a uniformly bounded density on a compact set—is therefore realistic for several standard architectures:

•

Generalized Linear Models:
Consider a generalized linear model where
\bm{\theta}=\Phi_{\bm{x}}{\bm{w}}
for a given matrix of features, such as the architecture used in the stochastic vehicle scheduling problem (Example
2
). Property

(

UBD

)

is satisfied when every feature is continuous and upper bounded, and the number of features is rich enough (i.e., full rank) so that the mapping does not collapse into a lower-dimensional subspace. In the stochastic vehicle scheduling problem, continuous features reflect properties of the delay or the distance, while discrete features can indicate combinatorial properties such as the number of arcs departing from the tail of an arc
a
.
When the number of features is small, the image of the instances through the model may be included in a hyperplane, which leads to Case
{\rm(iii)}
of Proposition
5
. Case
{\rm(ii)}
corresponds to features that take integer values and are finite.

•

Deterministic Neural Networks (via the Coarea Formula):
For a deterministic neural network with smooth activations, the coarea formula guarantees a bounded push-forward density provided the input
X
has a bounded density and the network’s Jacobian is uniformly lower-bounded (
|J\psi_{\bm{w}}|({\bm{x}})\geq\alpha>0
), see Proposition
16
. However, this precludes representations that collapse onto lower-dimensional manifolds, a usual phenomenon in deep learning. Using a sub-manifold instead of a hyperplane, Case
{\rm(iii)}
of Proposition
5
enables us to deal with such neural networks.

•

Stochastic Neural Networks and Randomized Smoothing:
To easily bypass the topological limitations of deterministic networks, one can employ randomized smoothing. By injecting a small, independent Gaussian noise
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d(X)})
at the final layer, the resulting model
\tilde{\psi}_{\bm{w}}(X)=\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)+\xi
produces a push-forward law that is the convolution of the deterministic network’s output distribution with a Gaussian. As we formally prove in Lemma
13
(Appendix

), this operation acts as a universal mollifier: it unconditionally guarantees that the density of
\tilde{\psi}_{\bm{w}}(X)
is absolutely continuous and uniformly bounded by
(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-d(X)/2}
for all
{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}
, regardless of the network’s internal Jacobian or potential manifold collapse.

Remark 12

(Universality of the bounded density via Randomized Smoothing)
.

For arbitrary deterministic statistical models
\psi_{\bm{w}}
, guaranteeing a uniformly bounded density over a compact parameter space
\mathcal{W}
as done in

(

UBD

)

is topologically restrictive. If the instance distribution
X
is discrete or supported on a lower-dimensional space, or if the model parametrization permits rank-deficient mappings (e.g.,
{\bm{w}}=\mathbf{0}
, or decision trees outputting finite discrete sets), the learned representations
\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)
will concentrate on lower-dimensional sub-manifolds in
\mathds{R}^{d(G)}
. In such cases, the push-forward law is singular with respect to the Lebesgue measure, and the bounded density assumption fails.

However, this theoretical bottleneck is
universally resolved
by adopting a randomized smoothing approach. As recalled in Lemma
13
(Appendix
A
), explicitly injecting an independent Gaussian perturbation
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\mathrm{Id}_{d(G)})
to the model’s output yields a smoothed direction
\tilde{\psi}_{\bm{w}}(X)=\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)+\xi
. The resulting convolution unconditionally guarantees that the push-forward law admits a probability density uniformly bounded by
C_{\psi,G}=(2\pi\varepsilon_{0}^{2})^{-d(G)/2}
for all
{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}
. Consequently, the sharp linear perturbation bias
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
is always accessible for any arbitrary combinatorial prediction architecture simply by incorporating a baseline stochasticity. In this case, note that
\mathcal{E}_{0}(\mathcal{H})
of (
2.8
) given for
\tilde{\psi}_{\bm{w}}
is equal to
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(\mathcal{H})
given for
{\psi}_{\bm{w}}
.

That said, this universality comes at a cost hidden in the constants. The constant
C
in Equation (
2.8
) depends linearly on the density bound
C_{\psi,G}=(2\pi\varepsilon_{0}^{2})^{-d(G)/2}
, which explodes exponentially with the ambient dimension
d(G)
as
\varepsilon_{0}\to 0
. Similarly, the fractional moment constant from Proposition
4
diverges as
C_{\varepsilon_{0},\tau}\propto\varepsilon_{0}^{-\tau}
. Consequently, the guarantee of (
2.8
) degrades for an artificially smoothed deterministic model unless
\varepsilon_{0}
is kept fixed, which in turn freezes the misspecification error
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(\mathcal{H})
.

Ultimately, the sharp rates are most practically meaningful for models where the conditional density naturally avoids singular concentrations—such as the contextual operations research settings highlighted in Remark
11
—without needing an artificial noise injection.

3.4
Optimization error bound in the Kernel-SoS case

The last part of the proof consists in bounding the optimization error when minimizing
\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}
. To alleviate notation, we set

R_{n,\lambda}({\bm{w}}):=\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}})\,,

which we want to globally minimize. It is a hard problem since
R_{n,\lambda}
is typically non-convex. The key idea in k-SoS is to find an (approximate) Sum-of-Squares representation for
R_{n,\lambda}
while minimizing
R_{n,\lambda}
. Fix
s>1+d_{\mathcal{W}}/2
and let
\mathcal{H}_{\phi}
be the Sobolev space over
\mathcal{W}

of smoothness
s
. Denote by
k
its reproducing kernel, i.e.
k(\cdot,{\bm{w}})\in\mathcal{H}_{\phi}
such that for all
g\in\mathcal{H}_{\phi},{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}
, we have
g({\bm{w}})=\left\langle{g},{k(\cdot,{\bm{w}})}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\phi}}
. Let
\phi:\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{H}_{\phi}
be the kernel embedding, i.e.
\phi({\bm{w}})=k(\cdot,{\bm{w}})
. Set
\tilde{s}=s-d_{\mathcal{W}}/2\geq 1
. Let
S^{+}(\mathcal{H}_{\phi})
be the set of self-adjoint
positive semidefinite operators from
\mathcal{H}_{\phi}
to
\mathcal{H}_{\phi}
. We consider an assumption inspired by
(
Rudi et al., 2024
, Assumption 1.a)
.

Assumption
(
k-SoS
)
.

We assume that
\mathcal{W}
is the closure of a union of closed balls
\cup_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathds{B}({\bm{w}},r)
for some
r>0
and a bounded set
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathds{R}^{d_{\mathcal{W}}}
. Assume furthermore that
R_{n,\lambda}\in C^{s+3}(\mathcal{W},\mathds{R})
and that
R_{n,\lambda}
has a kernel Sum-of-Square representation, i.e. there exists
A_{n,\lambda}\in S^{+}(\mathcal{H}_{\phi})
such that

R_{n,\lambda}({\bm{w}})-\min_{{\bm{w}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}}R_{n,\lambda}({\bm{w}}^{\prime})=\left\langle{\phi({\bm{w}})},{A_{n,\lambda}\phi({\bm{w}})}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\phi}},\,\forall{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}.

(3.9)

For the following, we fix the
A_{n,\lambda}
with minimal trace satisfying (
3.9
).

The requirement on
\mathcal{W}
is to use scattering bounds and is quite mild for compact sets. We mostly ensure
R_{n,\lambda}\in C^{s+3}(\mathcal{W},\mathds{R})
by regularizing
R_{n,0}
through
\lambda>0
and using a smooth feature map
{\bm{w}}\mapsto{\psi}_{\bm{w}}
, e.g.
{\psi}_{\bm{w}}({\bm{x}})=\Phi_{x}{\bm{w}}
with
\Phi_{x}\in\mathds{R}^{d(x)\times{d_{\mathcal{W}}}}
. By
Rudi et al. (2024, Theorem 3)
(see also
Marteau-Ferey et al. (2024)
), the existence of

is for instance guaranteed if
R_{n,\lambda}
and its second order partial derivatives belong to
\mathcal{H}_{\phi}
, and
R_{n,\lambda}
has a finite number of minimizers, all with positive Hessian. We will see an explicit example below.

Recall that a global optimization problem can always be cast as a convex one under the form

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\mathds{R},\end{subarray}}{\mathrm{max}}\quad c

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\mathds{R},\end{subarray}}}{\mathrm{max}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{max}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle\leq R_{n,\lambda}({\bm{w}}),\,\forall\,{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}.

We consider the kernel Sum-Of-Squares approximation of it, namely

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\mathds{R},\\
A\in S^{+}(\mathcal{H}_{\phi})\end{subarray}}{\mathrm{min}}\quad-c+\lambda_{\phi}\operatorname{Tr}(A)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\mathds{R},\\
A\in S^{+}(\mathcal{H}_{\phi})\end{subarray}}}{\mathrm{min}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{min}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle R_{n,\lambda}(\tilde{{\bm{w}}}_{m})-c

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\phi(\tilde{{\bm{w}}}_{m})},{A\phi(\tilde{{\bm{w}}}_{m})}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\phi}},\,\forall\,m\in[M]

where
\lambda_{\phi}>0
is some parameter, and the

are sampled uniformly at random in

.

Remark 14
.

A few words are in order. We want a
c\in\mathds{R}
such that
{\bm{w}}\mapsto R_{n,\lambda}({\bm{w}})-c\geq 0
. On the other hand, for any
A\in S^{+}(\mathcal{H}_{\phi})
, the function
{\bm{w}}\mapsto\left\langle{\phi({\bm{w}})},{A\phi({\bm{w}})}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\phi}}
is nonnegative, and under some assumptions on
\phi
and
\mathcal{H}_{\phi}

(
Marteau-Ferey et al., 2020
)
, these functions are pointwise dense in the nonnegative continuous functions. So we look for one such

for which the two functions are equal at least at the sampled points as in (
3.10a
), and we penalize the extra variable

by its trace. For (
3.10

Rudi et al. (2024, Section 6)
outputs the infimum value estimate

, the corresponding

and a candidate

.
Contrary to

, an extra term

appears in our analysis. One expects that this error goes to zero as

Theorem 4

(Theorem 6,

)
.

Fix

(

k-SoS

)

, there exist explicit constants

such that for

we have, with probability at least

|R_{n,\lambda}(\hat{\bm{w}}_{n,\lambda})-R_{n,\lambda}({\bm{w}}_{n,\lambda})|\leq R_{n,\lambda}(\hat{\bm{w}}_{n,\lambda})-\hat{R}+\lambda_{\phi}\left(\operatorname{Tr}\left(A_{n,\lambda}\right)+|R_{n,\lambda}|_{\mathcal{W},\lceil\tilde{s}\rceil}\right),

(3.11)

depending on the Sobolev norm
|R_{n,\lambda}|_{\mathcal{W},\lceil\tilde{s}\rceil}=\sup_{|\alpha|=\lceil\tilde{s}\rceil}\sup_{{\bm{w}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}}|D^{\alpha}R_{n,\lambda}({\bm{w}}^{\prime})|
.

The estimate (
3.11
) has the advantage that if
s\gg{d_{\mathcal{W}}}
then the smoothness mitigates the curse of dimensionality. Indeed, to achieve an a priori error
\epsilon
on the last term of (

), which we denote
\lambda_{\phi}C_{n,\lambda}
, we can take

rather than the more usual, and much larger,

. Note however that the constants
\bar{M}
and
\bar{C}
can still depend exponentially in the dimension, their closed-form expressions can be found in
Rudi et al. (2024, Theorem 5)
. Furthermore the Sobolev norm
|R_{n,\lambda}|_{\mathcal{W},\lceil s-{d_{\mathcal{W}}}/2\rceil}
and the cost of the Sum-of-Square representation

depend crucially on the noise parameter
\lambda
since the latter plays a role on the regularity of
R_{n,\lambda}
, and we expect both terms to diverge to
+\infty
when
\lambda
goes to
0
. On the other hand, as just said,
\lambda_{\phi}
can be taken very small when the number of sampling points
M
is large, so that it compensates the other terms. In other words the computational complexity, which is polynomial in
M
, of solving larger SDP problems on

will offset the cases when
\lambda
is taken small and the function
R_{n,\lambda}
is less smooth. We face thus a classical tradeoff between having a better estimate of
R_{n,0}
or doing fewer computations.

The example below showcases how a simple strategy for the embeddings, which was implemented in
Parmentier and T’Kindt (2023)
, does satisfy the technical assumptions of this Section.

The case of generalized linear embeddings

Assume
\bm{\theta}={\psi}_{\bm{w}}({\bm{x}})=\Phi_{x}{\bm{w}}
with
\Phi_{x}\in\mathds{R}^{d(x)\times{d_{\mathcal{W}}}}
and the added noise

is Gaussian with zero mean, then we can provide more explicit estimates for
\operatorname{Tr}\left(A_{n,\lambda}\right)
and
|R_{n,\lambda}|_{\mathcal{W},\lceil s-{d_{\mathcal{W}}}/2\rceil}
.

Decompose

with
Z^{\prime}
Gaussian over
\mathds{R}^{d_{\mathcal{W}}}
with covariance
\Sigma_{x}
and
Z^{\prime\prime}
Gaussian with values in the complement
\operatorname{Im}(\Phi_{x})^{\perp}
to the range of
\Phi_{x}
. Then

\mathds{E}_{Z(x)}\big[f^{0}(\hat{\bm{y}}({\psi}_{\bm{w}}({\bm{x}})+\lambda Z({\bm{x}})),{\bm{x}})\big]=\mathds{E}_{Z^{\prime}(x)}\mathds{E}_{Z^{\prime\prime}(x)}\big[f^{0}(\hat{\bm{y}}(\Phi_{x}({\bm{w}}+Z^{\prime}(x))+\lambda Z^{\prime\prime}(x)),{\bm{x}})\big]

Let

so
R_{n,\lambda}
is smooth. Moreover the convolution commutes with the derivation, and

for any functions

\tilde{s}=s-{d_{\mathcal{W}}}/2

\displaystyle|R_{n,\lambda}|_{\mathcal{W},\lceil\tilde{s}\rceil}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sup_{|\alpha|=\lceil\tilde{s}\rceil}\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Omega}|\underbrace{D^{\alpha}R_{n,\lambda,x_{i}}\star\mathcal{N}_{\lambda\Sigma_{x_{i}}}({\bm{w}})}_{=R_{n,\lambda,x_{i}}\star D^{\alpha}\mathcal{N}_{\lambda\Sigma_{x_{i}}}}|

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\big(\lambda^{-\tilde{s}}\big)\,.

Defining

and we can obtain a closed-form sum-of-squares expression of

for the chosen k-SoS feature map

\displaystyle R_{n,\lambda}({\bm{w}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle{\phi({\bm{w}})},{\left[\int_{\mathds{R}^{d_{\mathcal{W}}}}\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,i}({\bm{w}}^{\prime},\cdot)\otimes\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,i}({\bm{w}}^{\prime},\cdot)R_{n,0}({\bm{w}}^{\prime})d{\bm{w}}^{\prime}\right]\phi({\bm{w}})}\right\rangle_{H_{\phi}}

\displaystyle=:\left\langle{\phi({\bm{w}})},{\tilde{A}_{n,\lambda}\phi({\bm{w}})}\right\rangle_{H_{\phi}}\,.

and since

Since the RKHS associated with

Lemma 8

Let

be an integrable bounded function,
Z
a standard normal random vector of
\mathds{R}^{d}

\sigma>0
a positive real number, and

Proof.

Let

\|\nabla G({\bm{w}})\|_{2}\leq\|u\|_{\infty}\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\|\nabla h({\bm{v}})\|_{2}\mathrm{d}{\bm{v}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{d}\|u\|_{\infty}}{\sigma}\,,

which gives the result.

Proof.

Lemma 12
.

Let
Z
be a chi-square distribution with
d
degrees of freedom. Let
t>d
then

\mathds{P}[Z>t]\leq\exp(d/2)\exp(-t/4).

Proof.

From Markov’s inequality in its exponential form
[
Boucheron et al., 2013
]
, one has

\displaystyle\mathds{P}[Z>t]

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{0<u<1/2}\{\exp(-ut)\mathds{E}(\exp(uZ))\}

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{0<u<1/2}\{\exp(-ut)(1-2u)^{-d/2}\}.

Take
u=(1/2)(1-d/t)
to get

\displaystyle\mathds{P}[Z>t]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{t}{2}\big(1-\frac{d}{t}\big)+\frac{d}{2}\log\big(\frac{t}{d}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{d}{2}\big(\frac{t}{d}-1-\log\big(\frac{t}{d}\big)\big)\Big)

Set
v={t}/{d}-1>0
and note that

\displaystyle v-\log(1+v)=\int_{1}^{1+v}\frac{w-1}{w}\mathrm{d}w\geq\big(\frac{1}{1+v}\big)\int_{1}^{1+v}({w-1})\mathrm{d}w=\frac{v^{2}}{2+2v}=\frac{t}{2d}-1+\frac{d}{2t}\,,

hence

\mathds{P}[Z>t]\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{t}{4}+\frac{d}{2}-\frac{d^{2}}{4t}\Big)

which gives the result.
∎

Lemma 13
.

Let
d\geq 1
be an integer. Let
f_{\bm{w}}(X)
be any random vector in
\mathds{R}^{d}
generated by an arbitrary mapping
f_{\bm{w}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathds{R}^{d}
and instance distribution
X
, with probability law
\mu_{\bm{w}}
. Let
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\mathrm{Id}_{d})
be a Gaussian random vector independent of
X
, for some
\sigma>0
.

Define the randomized output
\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)=f_{\bm{w}}(X)+\xi
. Then the law of
\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, and its probability density function
g_{\bm{w}}(\theta)
is uniformly bounded such that

\|g_{\bm{w}}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{d/2}}\,,

unconditionally for all
{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}
, even if the distribution of
f_{\bm{w}}(X)
is singular or supported on a lower-dimensional manifold.

Proof.

Because
f_{\bm{w}}(X)
and
\xi
are independent, the law of their sum
\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)=f_{\bm{w}}(X)+\xi
is the convolution of the law of
f_{\bm{w}}(X)
(denoted by the probability measure
\mu_{\bm{w}}
) and the law of the Gaussian perturbation
\xi
. Since
\xi
is an isotropic Gaussian, its density with respect to the Lebesgue measure is given by

\varphi_{\sigma}({\bm{z}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{d/2}}\exp\Big(-\frac{\|{\bm{z}}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big)\,.

By the convolution formula, the probability density function
g_{\bm{w}}
of
\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)
at any point
\bm{\theta}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
exists and

g_{\bm{w}}(\bm{\theta})=\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\varphi_{\sigma}(\bm{\theta}-{\bm{u}})\mathrm{d}\mu_{\bm{w}}({\bm{u}})\,.

Observe that the Gaussian density attains its global maximum at the origin. Thus, for any
{\bm{z}}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
, it holds that
\varphi_{\sigma}({\bm{z}})\leq(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-d/2}
. We substitute this uniform upper bound into the integral to obtain

g_{\bm{w}}(\bm{\theta})\leq\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{d/2}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{\bm{w}}({\bm{u}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{d/2}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{\bm{w}}({\bm{u}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{d/2}}\,,

for all
\bm{\theta}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
and all
{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}
.

This bound depends strictly on the variance of the injected noise
\sigma^{2}
and the subspace dimension
d
, and holds completely independently of the deterministic mapping
f_{\bm{w}}
or the underlying distribution of
X
, concluding the proof.
∎

Remark 15

(On the conditions (
Orl.
) and (
Mom.
))
.

These conditions describe the tail behavior of the random variable
T:={\sqrt{d(X)}}/{\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X)+\varepsilon Z)}
. Condition (
Orl.
) is of Orlicz-norm type: it states that
T
belongs to the
\tau
-Orlicz space and, as such, it has
\tau
-exponentially light tail; see for instance
[
Chafaï et al., 2012
, Section 1.1]
for a review on Orlicz spaces. Hence, the distribution of the random variable
{\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X)+\varepsilon Z)}/{\sqrt{d(X)}}=1/T
is
\tau
-exponentially light at the edge support point zero, hence the random variable
1/T
is close to zero when switching from one normal cone to another. Roughly speaking, Condition (
Orl.
) means that the random direction
\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X)+\varepsilon Z
rarely falls close to a normal-cone boundary, i.e., the distribution of
\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X)+\varepsilon Z
places only a small mass near the decision boundaries (the borders of the normal cones).

Condition (
Mom.
) is a moment type assumption on
T
. It is weaker than Condition (
Orl.
). Indeed, by
[
Chafaï et al., 2012
, Theorem 1.1.5]
, note that (
Orl.
) is equivalent to

\forall p\geq\tau\,,\quad\bigg[\mathds{E}_{X}\Big(\frac{\rho(\psi_{{\bm{w}}}(X)+\varepsilon Z)}{\sqrt{d(X)}}\Big)^{-p}\bigg]^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq Cp^{\tau}\,,

for some positive constant
C>0
. Hence, Condition (
Orl.
) implies (
Mom.
). Condition (
Mom.
) gives however a slower rate of convergence of
q(\lambda)
than Condition (
Orl.
).

Note that both convergence rates are decreasing in
\tau
and one cannot have better rates than the sub-Gaussian type bound
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda\to 0}\big(\exp(-(2\lambda)^{-2})\big)
with this type of analysis.

Appendix C
Excess Risk Bound for bounded conditional push-forward laws

Theorem 5

(Excess Risk Bound)
.

Suppose Assumptions

(

Part

)

,

(

Gauss

)

,

(

Lip

)

, and

(

k-SoS

)

hold.
Furthermore, assume that Property

(

UBD

)

holds.
Then, for any
\lambda>0
, the learned policy
h_{{\bm{w}}_{M,n,\lambda}}
satisfies:

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}_{M,n,\lambda}})-\mathds{E}_{X}\big[f^{0}({\bm{y}}^{0}(X),X)\big]

\displaystyle\leq C\lambda+\mathcal{O}_{\mathds{P}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{\lambda\sqrt{n}}+\bigg(\frac{1}{\lambda(M/\log M)^{\frac{1}{d_{\mathcal{W}}}}}\bigg)^{s-\frac{d_{\mathcal{W}}}{2}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\big(\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}})-\hat{R}\big)}_{\text{a posteriori gap}}+\mathcal{E}_{0}(\mathcal{H})\,,

where
\mathcal{E}_{0}(\mathcal{H})=\inf_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathcal{R}_{0}(h_{\bm{w}})-\mathds{E}_{X}\big[f^{0}({\bm{y}}^{0}(X),X)\big]
is the model misspecification error,
\widehat{{\bm{w}}}={\bm{w}}_{M,n,\lambda}
is the parameter returned by the k-SoS algorithm, and
C>0
is the constant from Proposition
6
.

Proof.

Let
\widehat{{\bm{w}}}={\bm{w}}_{M,n,\lambda}
denote the parameter returned by the k-SoS algorithm. We first show that the unregularized risk
{\bm{w}}\mapsto\mathcal{R}_{0}(h_{\bm{w}})
is continuous. Let
({\bm{w}}_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be a sequence converging to
{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}
. By Assumption

(

Lip

)

, we have the convergence
\psi_{{\bm{w}}_{k}}(X)\to\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)
for all
X
.

By the bounded density assumption, the conditional push-forward law of
\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Thus, the probability of
\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)
falling exactly on the boundaries of any normal cone
\mathcal{F}_{\bm{y}}
is strictly zero. Consequently, for almost every
X
, the vector
\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)
lies strictly in the open interior of some normal cone. Since the interior of a normal cone is an open set, the convergence
\psi_{{\bm{w}}_{k}}(X)\to\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)
implies that for
k
sufficiently large,
\psi_{{\bm{w}}_{k}}(X)
falls into the exact same cone as
\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)
. Thus, the discrete choice locks in almost surely:
\hat{\bm{y}}_{X}(\psi_{{\bm{w}}_{k}}(X))=\hat{\bm{y}}_{X}(\psi_{\bm{w}}(X))
. By the Continuous Mapping Theorem,
f^{0}(\hat{\bm{y}}_{X}(\psi_{{\bm{w}}_{k}}(X)),X)\to f^{0}(\hat{\bm{y}}_{X}(\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)),X)
almost surely. Because the objective
|f^{0}|
is uniformly bounded by Assumption

(

Part

)

, we can invoke the Dominated Convergence Theorem to pass the limit inside the expectation:

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{R}_{0}(h_{{\bm{w}}_{k}})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathds{E}_{X}\big[f^{0}(\hat{\bm{y}}_{X}(\psi_{{\bm{w}}_{k}}(X)),X)\big]=\mathds{E}_{X}\big[f^{0}(\hat{\bm{y}}_{X}(\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)),X)\big]=\mathcal{R}_{0}(h_{\bm{w}})\,.

This proves
\mathcal{R}_{0}
is continuous. Since
\mathcal{W}
is a compact set, the infimum is attained at some global minimizer
{\bm{w}}^{\star}\in\mathcal{W}
.

We can now decompose the difference between the risk of our learned policy
\widehat{{\bm{w}}}
and the risk of the optimal parameter
{\bm{w}}^{\star}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}})-\mathcal{R}_{0}(h_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}})

\displaystyle=\big(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}})-\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}})\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\big(\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}})-\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}_{n,\lambda}})\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\big(\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}_{n,\lambda}})-\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}})\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\big(\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}})-\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}})\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\big(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}})-\mathcal{R}_{0}(h_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}})\big)\,.

We bound each term on the right-hand side:

•

Statistical Estimation Error:
The first and fourth terms are bounded by the uniform deviation over
\mathcal{W}
:

\big(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}})-\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}})\big)+\big(\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}})-\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}})\big)\leq 2\sup_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}}\big|\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{\bm{w}})-\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{\bm{w}})\big|\,.

By Theorem
3
, this supremum is bounded by
\mathcal{O}_{\mathds{P}}\big(\frac{1}{\lambda\sqrt{n}}\big)
.

•

Optimization Error:
The second term encapsulates the a posteriori gap and the approximation error from the k-SoS procedure. By Theorem
4
and the subsequent analysis, this is bounded by:

\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}})-\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}_{n,\lambda}})\leq\big(\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}})-\hat{R}\big)+\mathcal{O}_{\mathds{P}}\Bigg[\bigg(\frac{1}{\lambda(M/\log M)^{\frac{1}{d_{\mathcal{W}}}}}\bigg)^{s-\frac{d_{\mathcal{W}}}{2}}\Bigg]\,.

•

Empirical Minimization:
The third term is non-positive since
{\bm{w}}_{n,\lambda}
is the exact global minimizer of the empirical regularized risk
\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}
:

\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}_{n,\lambda}})-\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}})\leq 0\,.

•

Perturbation Bias:
The fifth term is the perturbation bias. Under the bounded density assumption, Proposition
6
guarantees that uniformly for all
{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}
:

\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}})-\mathcal{R}_{0}(h_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}})\leq\big|\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}})-\mathcal{R}_{0}(h_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}})\big|\leq C\lambda\,.

Summing these bounds, we obtain:

\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}})-\mathcal{R}_{0}(h_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}})\leq C\lambda+\mathcal{O}_{\mathds{P}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{\lambda\sqrt{n}}+\bigg(\frac{1}{\lambda(M/\log M)^{\frac{1}{d_{\mathcal{W}}}}}\bigg)^{s-\frac{d_{\mathcal{W}}}{2}}\Bigg]+\big(\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}})-\hat{R}\big)\,.

Finally, we add and subtract the population optimum
\mathds{E}_{X}\big[f^{0}({\bm{y}}^{0}(X),X)\big]
to introduce the misspecification error
\mathcal{E}_{0}(\mathcal{H})
. Recognizing that
\mathcal{R}_{0}(h_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}})=\inf_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathcal{R}_{0}(h_{\bm{w}})
, we get:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}})-\mathds{E}_{X}\big[f^{0}({\bm{y}}^{0}(X),X)\big]

\displaystyle=\Big(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}})-\mathcal{R}_{0}(h_{{\bm{w}}^{\star}})\Big)+\Big(\inf_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathcal{R}_{0}(h_{\bm{w}})-\mathds{E}_{X}\big[f^{0}({\bm{y}}^{0}(X),X)\big]\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C\lambda+\mathcal{O}_{\mathds{P}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{\lambda\sqrt{n}}+\bigg(\frac{1}{\lambda(M/\log M)^{\frac{1}{d_{\mathcal{W}}}}}\bigg)^{s-\frac{d_{\mathcal{W}}}{2}}\Bigg]+\big(\mathcal{R}_{n,\lambda}(h_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}})-\hat{R}\big)+\mathcal{E}_{0}(\mathcal{H})\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

To complete our theoretical analysis of the perturbation bias, we formally demonstrate that the linear rate
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
established in Theorem
5
and Proposition
6
is sharp. The following proposition provides a lower bound by constructing a simple, standard configuration satisfying the bounded density condition where the perturbation bias is strictly
\Omega(\lambda)
.

Proposition 15

(Sharpness of the Linear Perturbation Bias)
.

There exists a combinatorial optimization problem satisfying Assumption

(

Part

)

a Gaussian perturbation satisfying Assumption

(

Gauss

)

, and a statistical model
\psi_{\bm{w}}
satisfying Property

(

UBD

)

, such that the perturbation bias satisfies:

\big|\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{\bm{w}})-\mathcal{R}_{0}(h_{\bm{w}})\big|=\Omega(\lambda)\,.

Proof.

Consider a simplified 1-dimensional binary choice problem where the feasible set is
\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}\subset\mathds{R}
. The normal fan of the convex hull
\mathcal{C}=[0,1]
consists of two cones:
\mathcal{F}_{0}=\mathds{R}_{-}
and
\mathcal{F}_{1}=\mathds{R}_{+}
, separated by a single boundary point at the origin
H=\{0\}
.
Let the true objective function simply be
f^{0}(y,x)=y
.

Suppose the statistical model parametrizes the direction
\theta=\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)\in\mathds{R}
such that its push-forward law is the uniform distribution over the interval
[0,1]
. Its probability density function is
f_{\psi_{\bm{w}}}(\theta)=1
on a compact support, which perfectly satisfies Property

(

UBD

)

.

For the unregularized risk, because
\theta
is almost surely strictly positive, the linear oracle always selects
\hat{y}(\theta)=1
. The unregularized risk is therefore exactly:

\mathcal{R}_{0}(h_{\bm{w}})=\mathds{E}_{\theta}[f^{0}(\hat{y}(\theta))]=\int_{0}^{1}1\,\mathrm{d}\theta=1\,.

Now, consider the smoothed policy with a standard Gaussian perturbation
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. The oracle selects
\hat{y}(\theta+\lambda Z)=1
if and only if
\theta+\lambda Z>0
, which is equivalent to
Z>-\theta/\lambda
. Let
\Phi
denote the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution. The smoothed risk evaluates to:

\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{\bm{w}})=\mathds{E}_{\theta,Z}\big[f^{0}(\hat{y}(\theta+\lambda Z))\big]=\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{P}\left(Z>-\frac{\theta}{\lambda}\right)\mathrm{d}\theta=\int_{0}^{1}\Phi\left(\frac{\theta}{\lambda}\right)\mathrm{d}\theta\,.

The perturbation bias is the difference between the two risks:

\mathcal{R}_{0}(h_{\bm{w}})-\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{\bm{w}})=\int_{0}^{1}\left(1-\Phi\left(\frac{\theta}{\lambda}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\theta\,.

Applying the change of variables
u=\theta/\lambda
(so that
\mathrm{d}\theta=\lambda\,\mathrm{d}u
), we obtain:

\mathcal{R}_{0}(h_{\bm{w}})-\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{\bm{w}})=\lambda\int_{0}^{1/\lambda}\big(1-\Phi(u)\big)\mathrm{d}u\,.

As the perturbation scale goes to zero (
\lambda\to 0^{+}
), the upper limit of the integral approaches infinity. Using the identity
1-\Phi(u)=\mathds{P}(Z>u)
, the integral of the tail probabilities yields the expectation of the positive part of the standard normal:

\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{1/\lambda}\big(1-\Phi(u)\big)\mathrm{d}u=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathds{P}(Z>u)\mathrm{d}u=\mathds{E}[Z\mathbf{1}_{\{Z>0\}}]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,.

Consequently, as
\lambda\to 0
, the bias behaves asymptotically as:

\big|\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(h_{\bm{w}})-\mathcal{R}_{0}(h_{\bm{w}})\big|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\lambda-o(\lambda)\,,

which strictly proves that the perturbation bias is
\Omega(\lambda)
, concluding the proof.
∎

|J\psi_{\bm{w}}|({\bm{x}})\coloneqq\sqrt{\det(D\psi_{\bm{w}}({\bm{x}})D\psi_{\bm{w}}({\bm{x}})^{\top})}
is uniformly lower-bounded on
\mathcal{X}
by a constant
\alpha>0
.

2.

Bounded Level Sets:
The
(p-d)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{H}^{p-d}
of the level sets is uniformly bounded, meaning there exists a constant
V_{\mathrm{level}}<\infty
such that
\sup_{\theta\in\mathds{R}^{d}}\mathcal{H}^{p-d}\big(\psi_{\bm{w}}^{-1}(\theta)\cap\mathcal{X}\big)\leq V_{\mathrm{level}}
.

Then, for all
{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}
, the push-forward law of
\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)
satisfies Property

(

UBD

)

: its support is compact, it is absolutely continuous with respect to the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure, and its probability density function
g_{\bm{w}}(\theta)
is uniformly bounded by
C_{\psi}=\frac{\beta}{\alpha}V_{\mathrm{level}}
.

Proof.

Since
\psi_{\bm{w}}
is a continuous mapping (due to the smooth activations) and the instance space
\mathcal{X}
is compact, the image
K_{\bm{w}}=\psi_{\bm{w}}(\mathcal{X})\subset\mathds{R}^{d}
is compact. Furthermore,
because
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{X}
are compact, their product
\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{X}
is compact. Since the neural network mapping
({\bm{w}},{\bm{x}})\mapsto\psi_{\bm{w}}({\bm{x}})
is jointly continuous, its total image
K=\bigcup_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}}\psi_{\bm{w}}(\mathcal{X})
is compact, and therefore closed and uniformly bounded.
Thus, the compact support requirement of Case
{\rm(i)}
is satisfied.

To establish the existence and boundedness of the density
g_{\bm{w}}(\theta)
, we use the coarea formula. For any measurable set
A\subset\mathds{R}^{d}
, the probability that
\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)
falls in
A
is:

\mathds{P}_{X}(\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)\in A)=\int_{\mathcal{X}\cap\psi_{\bm{w}}^{-1}(A)}f_{X}({\bm{x}})\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\,.

Since
\psi_{\bm{w}}
is Lipschitz, Rademacher’s theorem ensures it is differentiable almost everywhere. By the coarea formula, for any integrable function
h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathds{R}
, it holds that:

\int_{\mathcal{X}}h({\bm{x}})|J\psi_{\bm{w}}|({\bm{x}})\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}=\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\left(\int_{\psi_{\bm{w}}^{-1}(\theta)\cap\mathcal{X}}h({\bm{x}})\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{p-d}({\bm{x}})\right)\mathrm{d}\theta\,.

We choose the test function
h({\bm{x}})=\frac{f_{X}({\bm{x}})}{|J\psi_{\bm{w}}|({\bm{x}})}\mathbf{1}_{A}(\psi_{\bm{w}}({\bm{x}}))
, which is well-defined almost everywhere because
|J\psi_{\bm{w}}|({\bm{x}})\geq\alpha>0
. Substituting
h({\bm{x}})
into the coarea formula yields:

\int_{\mathcal{X}\cap\psi_{\bm{w}}^{-1}(A)}f_{X}({\bm{x}})\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}=\int_{A}\left(\int_{\psi_{\bm{w}}^{-1}(\theta)\cap\mathcal{X}}\frac{f_{X}({\bm{x}})}{|J\psi_{\bm{w}}|({\bm{x}})}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{p-d}({\bm{x}})\right)\mathrm{d}\theta\,.

The left-hand side is exactly
\mathds{P}_{X}(\psi_{\bm{w}}(X)\in A)
. Because this holds for any measurable set
A
, the push-forward law is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, and the integrand of the outer integral on the right-hand side is exactly the probability density function
g_{\bm{w}}(\theta)
:

g_{\bm{w}}(\theta)=\int_{\psi_{\bm{w}}^{-1}(\theta)\cap\mathcal{X}}\frac{f_{X}({\bm{x}})}{|J\psi_{\bm{w}}|({\bm{x}})}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{p-d}({\bm{x}})\,.

We can now bound
g_{\bm{w}}(\theta)
uniformly. By hypothesis,
f_{X}({\bm{x}})\leq\beta
and
|J\psi_{\bm{w}}|({\bm{x}})\geq\alpha>0
. Thus:

g_{\bm{w}}(\theta)\leq\int_{\psi_{\bm{w}}^{-1}(\theta)\cap\mathcal{X}}\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{p-d}({\bm{x}})=\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\mathcal{H}^{p-d}\big(\psi_{\bm{w}}^{-1}(\theta)\cap\mathcal{X}\big)\,.

Finally, applying the bounded level sets assumption
\mathcal{H}^{p-d}\big(\psi_{\bm{w}}^{-1}(\theta)\cap\mathcal{X}\big)\leq V_{\mathrm{level}}
, we obtain:

g_{\bm{w}}(\theta)\leq\frac{\beta}{\alpha}V_{\mathrm{level}}\eqqcolon C_{\psi}\,.

Because this upper bound depends only on
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
V_{\mathrm{level}}
, and holds independently of
\theta
and
{\bm{w}}
, the density
g_{\bm{w}}
is uniformly bounded, completing the proof.
∎
