Engineering active motion in quantum matter
Yuanjian Zheng, Alexander P. Antonov, Benno Liebchen, Hartmut Löwen

Abstract
We introduce a framework for engineering active quantum matter that involves mimicking the role of self-propulsion through an external trapping potential that is moving along imposed trajectories traced by classical active dynamics. This approach in the presence of dissipation, not only recovers essential dynamical behavior of classical activity, including the ballistic to diffusive cross-over of its mean-square displacement, but also reveals additional features of activity that are of quantum origin. These quantum-active features are revealed in non-dissipative systems, and manifest as novel exponents of the mean-square displacement at short timescales.

Mimicking quantum propulsion –

We now consider a quantum particle trapped in a time-dependent potential which is uniquely parameterized by its minimum position
x_{c}(t)
. This position is externally programmed to follow random paths drawn from an ensemble of trajectories generated by classical active dynamics
SabhapanditMajumdar2024
;
UhlenbeckOrnstein1930
;
BechingerVolpe2016
;
RomanczukGeier2012
. Specifically, we consider Active Ornstein-Uhlenbeck particles (AOUP), where the velocities follow a classical stochastic process with both a random diffusive component controlled by the diffusion constant
D
and persistent motion governed by the persistence time
\tau

Bonilla2019
, given by the equation of motion:

\tau\ddot{x}_{c}=-\dot{x}_{c}+\sqrt{2D}\eta(t)

(1)

where
\eta(t)
is a zero-mean delta-correlated white noise. We now write the time-dependent Hamiltonian as

\hat{H}(t)=\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2m}+\hat{V}(\hat{x},t),

(2)

where
\hat{V}(\hat{x},t)
is the potential energy operator associated with a classical trapping potential
V(x,t)
;

\hat{p}=-i\hbar\partial_{x}
is the momentum operator, and
m
,
\omega
,
\hat{x}
are the particle mass, harmonic trap frequency and position operator respectively. In the following, we consider two cases, dissipative and non-dissipative. In the
dissipative
case, we assume that the quantum particle is weakly coupled to its environment such that the dynamics of its density matrix
\hat{\rho}(t)
is governed by the Lindblad equation
RivasHuelga2011
:

\dot{\hat{\rho}}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[\hat{H}(t),\hat{\rho}\right]+\hat{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{\rho},t)

(3)

and the (Markovian) dissipator is given by

\hat{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{\rho},t)=\nu_{+}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}\hat{a}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger},\hat{\rho}\right\}\right)+\nu_{-}\left(\hat{a}\hat{\rho}\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a},\hat{\rho}\right\}\right)

(4)

where
\hat{a}
(
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
) is the annihilation (creation) operator, while
\nu^{+}
and
\nu^{-}
are the gain and loss rates for the excitations of the quantum particle in the trap respectively, and correspond to an equilibrium temperature (
T
) of the thermal bath through
\nu_{+}/\nu_{-}=e^{-\hbar\omega/k_{B}T}
where
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant and
\hbar
is the reduced Planck constant
EnglertMorigi2002
. In the
non-dissipative
(or Hamiltonian) case, the dissipator in Eq. (
3
) vanishes,
\hat{\mathcal{D}}\equiv 0
.

We define the quantum MSD, or the spread of the time-dependent wavefunction
|\psi_{t}\rangle
, as the expectation value:

\text{MSD}\equiv\overline{\langle\psi_{t}|\hat{x}^{2}|\psi_{t}\rangle}-\langle\psi_{t=0}|\hat{x}^{2}|\psi_{t=0}\rangle=\overline{\textrm{Tr}({\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{x}^{2}})}-\textrm{Tr}({\hat{\rho}(0)\hat{x}^{2}}).

(5)

Here,
\overline{\vskip 8.61108pt\cdots}
denotes the average over prescribed stochastic trajectories, that we will specify below. In what follows, we analyze equation (
3
) both analytically and numerically, starting from the ground state
|\psi_{t=0}\rangle
, with further details of our analysis and methods provided in the Supplemental Material (SM)
supplement
. We choose
\sqrt{\hbar/m\omega}
and
\omega^{-1}
as units of length and time, respectively.

Non-dissipative case –
To further explore the significance of quantum-active effects, without partially masking it with contributions from the thermal bath, we now focus solely on the non-dissipative (Hamiltonian) case. We consider a family of power-law trapping potentials
V(x,t)

V(x,t)=V_{0}|x-x_{c}(t)|^{q},\ q>0,

(7)

with the prefactor
V_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\hbar^{1-\frac{q}{2}}m^{\frac{q}{2}}\omega^{\frac{q}{2}+1}
.

Without dissipation, the diffusive regime at very short times is absent in both classical and quantum cases. We now determine the short-time (
t\!\ll\!\tau
) behavior of the MSD for a quantum particle using time-dependent perturbation theory (see the SM, Sec. I B) to obtain:

\text{MSD}=A_{q}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}dt^{\prime\prime}\overline{x_{c}(t^{\prime})x_{c}(t^{\prime\prime})}.

(8)

This result has the following physical interpretation: the leading-order contribution to the MSD at short times is fully determined by the prescribed stochastic trajectory, i.e., by the active driving protocol of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process (
1
). In contrast, in the classical case, the scaling of the MSD is given by a Green–Kubo relation that involves velocity autocorrelations (see the SM):

\text{MSD}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}dt^{\prime\prime}\overline{\dot{x}(t^{\prime})\dot{x}(t^{\prime\prime})}.

(9)

We note that, in the quantum case, the trapping potential exponent
q
in Eq. (
7
) enters the prefactor
A_{q}
of Eq. (
8
), which is entirely quantum in origin. This prefactor determines whether short-time localization is stronger (negative

A_{q}
) or weaker (positive
A_{q}
). We note that the emergence of a negative MSD is possible by virtue of its definition (
5
), and its initial ground state condition. A negative MSD indicates that the wavefunction initially becomes narrower than its ground-state configuration. This occurs because, on one side of the maximum of the wave function (or, more precisely, of
\langle\psi_{t}|\psi_{t}\rangle
), the moving potential localizes the wave function, essentially by pushing it, whereas on the other side, delocalization is negligible due to the low wavefunction amplitude on that side. Such negative MSD cannot occur for a classical stochastic particle with a sharp initial position. Even when the classical system is initiated with a non-zero temperature equilibrium configuration which has a non-zero initial MSD, the MSD increases with a
t^{2}
-scaling (see the SM, Sec. III D). Thus, the occurrence of a negative MSD at short times is a pure quantum effect.

For a harmonic potential (
q=2
) the prefactor
A_{q}
vanishes (
A_{q}=0
), with the leading correction scaling as
t^{6}
arising from second-order perturbation theory (see the SM, Sec. I E). For
q\neq 2
(and thus
A_{q}\neq 0
), the leading-order contribution to the MSD (
8
) is
t^{4}
(see the SM, Sec. I C). In contrast, for the classical case, the potential exponent
q
directly enters the dynamical exponent, resulting in a
t^{2(q+1)}
-scaling (see the SM, Sec. III).

In this work, we introduced a generic approach for exploring quantum-active matter in continuum space. This approach involves mimicking the role of propulsion in active particles through imposing a trapping potential that is moving along imposed trajectories governed by classical active stochastic dynamics. We show that this framework for a single quantum degree of freedom, captures essential dynamical features of classical self-propelled active particles in the presence of any dissipation. These features include, a mean squared displacement (MSD) that scales diffusively (
\sim t
) at short times, ballistically (
\sim t^{2}
) at intermediate times, and again diffusively (
\sim t
) at times much longer than the persistence time
\tau
of the active dynamics. Notably, this approach also reveals quantum-active dynamical features with no classical equivalent and that are most pronounced in the absence of dissipation. These features manifest as anomalous dynamical exponents of the MSD at short timescales. Most remarkably, the MSD can even transiently acquire negative values for sharp trap geometries, representing an increased localization of the wavefunction with time transiently. This phenomenon cannot emerge classically, and is counter-intuitive given that active systems are typically super-diffusive at intermediate timescales.

We stress that this work focuses on establishing a conceptually new and generic approach to study quantum-active matter that is meant to inspire future simulations and experiments. This approach is generalizable, and can be combined with or extended to interacting forms of classical active matter. This may include alternate forms of microscopic activity including run-and-tumble or active Brownian dynamics
SolonCates2015
;
CatesTailleur2013
;
FreyKroy2005
. While the generalization to the many-body regime is beyond the scope of the present work, we would like to remark that: (i) experimental setups are now available to individually control more than
10^{3}
quantum particles
Lahaye2020
, which could be adapted in the future to mimic quantum active matter at the many-body level. (ii) In principle, a random but temporally and spatially correlated potential (a specific type of colored spatiotemporal noise) can be utilized to mimic quantum active motion at the many-body level at low to moderate densities and to predict a viable path toward realizing, e.g., flocking and motility-induced phase separation. On the other hand, the effect of quantum phenomena such as exchange-symmetry of indistinguishable quantum particles (e.g. fermions or bosons)
HyrkasManninen2013
;
AbrahamBonitz2012
;
ZhengPoletti2015
;
MyersDeffner2020
, or collective behavior of exotic quantum many-body ground-states (e.g. Bose-Einstein condensates)
GreinerBloch2002
, on the dynamics of an externally activated process could also be interesting avenues to explore within the current context presented. Importantly, our theoretical predictions can be tested using state of the art experimental techniques in ultra-cold atoms confined to optical traps where the trap minimum can be programmed on prescribed stochastic trajectories. As such, our framework of active quantum matter could provide a paradigm for uncovering phenomenology involving the non-trivial coupling of quantum fluctuations with non-equilibrium features of activity.

I.1
Introduction to time-dependent perturbation theory

We begin by reviewing basic results of time-dependent perturbation theory, following the pedagogy of
Griffiths2018_supp
. First, consider a time-dependent Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(t)
and corresponding potential
\hat{V}(\hat{x},t)
. The instantaneous state
\ket{\psi_{t}}_{I}
evolves from an initial state
\ket{\psi_{t=0}}_{I}
under a unitary operator
\hat{U}_{I}(t,t=0)
in the interaction picture. The interaction or Dirac picture is a framework in quantum mechanics where the dynamics of a system are split between the state vectors and operators. It is commonly used for problems involving a solvable free Hamiltonian and a time-dependent perturbation term. The equation of motion (i.e. akin to the Schrödinger equation in the Schrödinger picture) is equivalently written:

i\hbar\partial_{t}\ket{\psi_{t}}_{I}=\hat{V}_{I}(t)\ket{\psi_{t}}_{I}

(S1)

where
\hat{V}_{I}=e^{i\hat{H}_{t=0}t/\hbar}\hat{V}e^{-i\hat{H}_{t=0}t/\hbar}
. By expanding
\ket{\psi_{t}}_{I}=\sum_{n}c_{n}\ket{n}
in the initial energy orthogonal eigen-basis
\{\ket{n}\}
, with
\langle n|m\rangle=\delta_{nm}
, (
S1
) reduces for a general state to:

i\hbar\dot{c}_{m}=\sum_{n}V_{mn}(t)e^{i\frac{E_{m}-E_{n}}{\hbar}t}c_{n}(t)

(S2)

where
V_{mn}(t)=\braket{m|V(t)|n}
and
E_{n}
are the energy eigenvalues in the initial basis. Now, since unitary evolution
\ket{\psi_{t}}_{I}=\hat{U}_{I}\ket{\psi_{t=0}}_{I}
holds similarly in the interaction picture, and (
S1
) applies to any initial
\ket{\psi_{t=0}}_{I}
, it follows that

i\hbar\partial_{t}\hat{U}_{I}(t,t=0)=\hat{V}_{I}(t)\hat{U}_{I}(t,t=0)

(S3)

Upon solving, by time-integrating (
S3
), we obtain a closed-form expression for the unitary operator:

\hat{U}_{I}(t,t=0)=\mathbb{I}-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{t=0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\hat{V}_{I}(t^{\prime})\hat{U}_{I}(t^{\prime},t=0)

(S4)

Note that the identity –
\mathbb{I}
, comes from applying the (boundary) condition
\hat{U}_{I}(t=0,t=0)=\mathbb{I}
.

This expression can then be expanded by recursively substituting
\hat{U}
within the time integral by its definition (
S4
) to obtain a time-ordered integral:

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{I}(t,t=0)=\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\left(-\frac{i}{\hbar}\right)^{n}\int_{t=0}^{t}dt_{1}\dots\int_{t=0}^{t_{n-1}}dt_{n}\hat{V}_{I}(t_{1})\dots\hat{V}_{I}(t_{n})

(S5)

Lastly, since
\hat{U}_{I}(t,t=0)\ket{\psi_{t=0}}=\sum_{n}\ket{n}\bra{n}\hat{U}_{I}(t,t=0)\ket{\psi_{t=0}}\equiv\sum_{n}c_{n}(t)\ket{n}

\displaystyle c_{n}^{(1)}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{m}\int dt^{\prime}e^{i\frac{(E_{n}-E_{m})}{\hbar}t^{\prime}}\braket{n|\hat{\tilde{H}}(t^{\prime})|m}

(S6)

\displaystyle c_{n}^{(2)}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\sum_{m}\int dt^{\prime}\int dt^{\prime\prime}e^{i\frac{(E_{n}-E_{m})}{\hbar}t^{\prime}+i\frac{(E_{m}-E_{j})}{\hbar}t^{\prime\prime}}\braket{n|\hat{\tilde{H}}(t^{\prime})|m}\braket{m|\hat{\tilde{H}}(t^{\prime\prime})|j}

(S7)

where
\hat{\tilde{H}}(t)=\hat{H}(t)-\hat{H}_{t=0}
represents the perturbing change to the Hamiltonian at time

equivalent to
\hat{V}_{I}
in the interaction picture. In the following subsections, we shall make use of these two expressions to understand the short timescale dynamics of active quantum particles.

I.2
First-order perturbation theory

We consider a single non-relativistic quantum mechanical particle of mass

\hat{H}(t)=\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{1}{2}m\omega^{2}\left(\hat{x}-x_{c}(t)\right)^{2}

(S8)

where
\hat{p}=-i\hbar\partial_{x}
is the momentum operator.
We write the instantaneous Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_{t=0}+\hat{\tilde{H}}(t)
as a time-dependent perturbation to initial Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{t=0}

\hat{\tilde{H}}(t)=\frac{1}{2}m\omega^{2}\left[x_{c}^{2}(t)-2\hat{x}x_{c}(t)\right]

(S9)

The probability of occupying the

P_{j}(t)=|c_{j}(t)|^{2}

\displaystyle c_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}e^{i\frac{(E_{j}-E_{0})}{\hbar}t^{\prime}}\langle j|\hat{\tilde{H}}|0\rangle

(S10)

\displaystyle=-\frac{m\omega^{2}i}{2\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}e^{i\frac{(E_{j}-E_{0})}{\hbar}t^{\prime}}\left[x_{c}^{2}\langle j|0\rangle-2x_{c}\langle j|\hat{x}|0\rangle\right]

(S11)

and

Furthermore, to leading order in

\langle\psi_{t}|\hat{x}^{2}|\psi_{t}\rangle-\langle\psi_{t=0}|\hat{x}^{2}|\psi_{t=0}\rangle\sim\left[\int^{t}_{0}dt^{\prime}x_{c}(t^{\prime})\right]^{2}

\overline{\langle\psi_{t}|\hat{x}^{2}|\psi_{t}\rangle}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\int d\Gamma\langle\psi_{t}|\hat{x}^{2}|\psi_{t}\rangle_{\Gamma}

\overline{\langle\psi_{t}|\hat{x}^{2}|\psi_{t}\rangle}-\langle\psi_{t=0}|\hat{x}^{2}|\psi_{t=0}\rangle=A_{q=2}\overline{\left[\int^{t}_{0}dt^{\prime}x_{c}(t^{\prime})\right]^{2}}=A_{q=2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}dt^{\prime\prime}\overline{x_{c}(t^{\prime})x_{c}(t^{\prime\prime})}.

We note that this result holds for any

as the leading term in Eq. (

Finally, we reiterate that the result in Eq. (

II
Dissipative Quantum Active Dynamics

In this section, we demonstrate the short-time diffusive behavior (
\sim t
) of the dissipative quantum-active particle. This behavior is analogous to thermal diffusion in the classical case and is notably absent in the non-dissipative scenario. First, in Sec.
II.1
we make a brief introduction to the Lindblad master equation that models dissipation of Markovian open quantum systems. In particular, we provide intuitive understanding to the different terms that appear in the equation, but defer its full derivation and details to an extensive existing literature
RivasHuelga2011_supp
;
GardinerZoller2000_supp
;
BreuerPetruccione2002_supp
;
Manzano2020_supp
. In Sec.
II.2
, we demonstrate that the diffusive behavior observed at very short times originates from the dissipative term and is analogous to thermal diffusion in classical system.

II.1
Lindblad operators and open quantum systems

The Lindblad master equation generally describes the time-evolution of a quantum system
weakly
coupled to a heat bath. It represents Markovian dissipation that does not require explicit consideration for the bath-system interactions, and is capable of generating non-unitary dynamics while still ensuring that the trace of the density matrix
\hat{\rho}(t)
is preserved.
In general, the effective dynamics can be written as an additional dissipative component
\mathcal{D}(\hat{\rho},t)
to purely Hamiltonian dynamics of a closed system given by the von Neumann equation:

\dot{\hat{\rho}}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[\hat{H}(t),\hat{\rho}\right]+\hat{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{\rho},t)

(S34)

The terms of the dissipator, composing of the Bosonic annihilation and creation operators –
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
, act respectively to either add or remove excitations to
\hat{\rho}(t)
at rates controlled by
\nu_{+}
and
\nu_{-}
respectively.

\hat{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{\rho},t)=\nu_{+}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}\hat{a}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger},\hat{\rho}\right\}\right)+\nu_{-}\left(\hat{a}\hat{\rho}\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a},\hat{\rho}\right\}\right)\equiv\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{+}+\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{-}

(S35)

To gain an intuition for the roles of these terms, we consider individually the effect of
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{+}
and
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{-}
for a general eigenstate
\ket{n}
(i.e.
\hat{\rho}=\ket{n}\bra{n}
)

\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{+}(\hat{\rho})\sim\hat{a}^{\dagger}\ket{n}\bra{n}\hat{a}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\ket{n}\bra{n}+\ket{n}\bra{n}\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right)=(n+1)\ket{n+1}\bra{n+1}-(n+1)\ket{n}\bra{n}

(S36)

\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{-}(\hat{\rho})\sim\hat{a}\ket{n}\bra{n}\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\ket{n}\bra{n}+\ket{n}\bra{n}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\right)=n\ket{n-1}\bra{n-1}-n\ket{n}\bra{n}

(S37)

Hence, we see that the effect of
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{+}
is essentially to excite the system by transferring occupation from
\ket{n}
to
\ket{n+1}
, while conversely,
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{-}
destroys excitation by transferring occupation from
\ket{n}
to
\ket{n-1}
.

Conceivably, for a given
\nu_{+}<\nu_{-}
, the system reaches at long times to a steady state, characterized by a time-independent mean occupation
\langle\hat{n}\rangle
. In other words,
\partial_{t}\langle\hat{n}\rangle=0
, where
\langle n\rangle=\Tr\{\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\hat{\rho}\}
is the expectation of the number operator
\hat{n}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}
. For a time-independent Hamiltonian, this steady-state condition reduces the expectation value of (
S35
) to the relation

\nu_{-}\langle\hat{n}\rangle=\nu_{+}(1+\langle\hat{n}\rangle)

(S38)

By further assuming that the heat bath is in thermal equilibrium at inverse temperature
\beta=1/k_{B}T
such that the excitations are black-body (i.e.
\langle\hat{n}\rangle=(e^{\beta\hbar\omega}-1)^{-1}
), we arrive at the expression that connects the bath temperature to the dissipation rates stated in the main text.

\frac{\nu_{+}}{\nu_{-}}=e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}.

(S39)

II.2
Short time diffusive dynamics

In this section, we show that the initial diffusive regime discussed in the main text is always recovered in the presence of weak coupling to the environment when modeled as a Markovian Lindblad dissipator (
S35
).
We consider
\langle\hat{x}^{2}\rangle
under the dynamics of
\hat{\mathcal{D}}
for an infinitesimal
dt
starting from a pure state
\hat{\rho}_{0}=\ket{n}\bra{n}
:

\displaystyle\frac{d\langle\hat{x}^{2}(dt)\rangle}{dt}=\nu_{+}\left(\sum_{k}\braket{k|\hat{x}^{2}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{a}|k}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\braket{k|\hat{x}^{2}\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}_{0}-\hat{x}^{2}\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger}|k}\right)+\nu_{-}\left(\sum_{k}\braket{k|\hat{x}^{2}\hat{a}\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{a}^{\dagger}|k}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\braket{k|\hat{x}^{2}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\hat{\rho}_{0}-\hat{x}^{2}\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}|k}\right)

(S40)

The MSD contribution from the gain rate –
\langle\hat{x}^{2}_{gain}\rangle
reduces to

\frac{d\langle\hat{x}^{2}_{gain}\rangle}{dt}=\frac{\nu_{+}\hbar}{2m\omega}\left[(n+1)^{2}+\frac{n+1}{2}\right]

(S41)

and similarly for
\langle\hat{x}^{2}_{loss}\rangle
associated to the loss rate,

\frac{d\langle\hat{x}^{2}_{loss}\rangle}{dt}=\frac{\nu_{-}\hbar}{2m\omega}n^{2}

(S42)

Hence, we see that both contributions from the gain and loss terms for a pure state
\ket{n}
to
\langle\hat{x}^{2}\rangle
are linear in
t
. For an initial ground state (i.e.
n=0
), (
S42
) is vanishing, such that only non-trivial contribution to the MSD comes from the gain rate (
S41
), which upon integration yields

\langle\hat{x}^{2}\rangle=\frac{3\nu_{+}\hbar}{4m\omega}t

(S43)

More generally, we see that the same argument holds for any initial pure or mixed state at very short timescales, such that the MSD is always diffusive. For the quantum analogue of zero temperature (
\nu_{+}=0
), this diffusive behavior is absent, as demonstrated in Fig.
2
of the main text.

II.3
Numerical Details

In this section, we briefly discuss the numerical approach to solving the Lindblad equation (
S34
) for a moving harmonic potential. First we generate an ensemble of trajectories –
\{x_{c}(t)\}
driven by simulating classical AOUPs for a given diffusion constant –
D

\tau\ddot{x}_{c}=-\dot{x}_{c}+\sqrt{2D}\eta_{t},

(S44)

where
\eta(t)
is a zero-mean delta-correlated Gaussian white noise. This is solved using a first-order finite difference method with a time step
\delta t
typically smaller than the physical time-step
\Delta t\sim 10^{-2}
which is the discretized increment of time in the classical equation (
S44

\Delta t/\delta t=5
.

Now, for a given trajectory,

dictates the position of the minimum of a confining potential
V(x,t)
(
S17
) after each
\delta t

After each

III.1
Introduction to active motion

The dynamics of active particles are commonly described by the stochastic equation:

m\ddot{x}+\gamma\dot{x}=f_{a}+\!\sqrt{2D_{t}}\zeta(t),

(S49)

where
m
is the particle mass,
\gamma
is the damping coefficient,
D_{t}
is the thermal diffusion coefficient,
\zeta(t)
is a zero-mean delta-correlated Gaussian white noise and
f_{a}
is the represents the active force distinguishing active dynamics from passive Brownian motion.
The Active Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Particle (AOUP) model describes self-propelled particles where the active force
f_{a}
evolves according to an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process (Ornstein-Uhlenbeck) process
Bonilla2019_supp
;
MartinvanWijland2021_supp
;
nguyen2021active_supp
:

\tau\dot{f_{a}}=-f_{a}+\gamma_{c}\!\sqrt{2D}\eta(t),

(S50)

where
\tau
is the persistence time,
D
is the diffusive coefficient induced by the active force,
\gamma_{c}
is the amplitude of the active force
f_{a}=\gamma_{c}\dot{x}_{c}
and
\eta(t)
is a zero-mean delta-correlated Gaussian white noise. In terms of positional variable
x_{c}
, Eq. (
S50
) is identical to Eq. (
1
) of the main text:

\tau\ddot{x}_{c}=-\dot{x}_{c}+\!\sqrt{2D}\eta(t).

(S51)

The AOUP is widely used to study active matter because it effectively captures persistency of active motion while maintaining both mathematical and computational simplicity.

III.2
Short time AOUP dynamics

In the overdamped limit
m\to 0
, Equation (
S49
) with
\gamma=\gamma_{c}
reduces to:

\dot{x}=\dot{x}_{c}+\!\sqrt{2D_{t}}\zeta(t).

(S52)

This limit corresponds to the left column in Fig.
1
of the main text. Here, we calculated the MSD with the Green-Kubo relation:

\textrm{MSD}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}dt^{\prime\prime}\overline{\dot{x}(t^{\prime})\dot{x}(t^{\prime\prime})}=2D_{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}dt^{\prime\prime}\overline{\zeta(t^{\prime})\zeta(t^{\prime\prime})}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}dt^{\prime\prime}\overline{\dot{x}_{c}(t^{\prime})\dot{x}_{c}(t^{\prime\prime})}.

(S53)

For the velocity-correlation function of
x_{c}
given by Eq. (
S18
), we get

\textrm{MSD}=2D_{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}dt^{\prime\prime}\overline{\zeta(t^{\prime})\zeta(t^{\prime\prime})}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}dt^{\prime\prime}\overline{\dot{x}_{c}(t^{\prime})\dot{x}_{c}(t^{\prime\prime})}=2D_{t}t+2D(t+\tau[e^{-t/\tau}-1]).

(S54)

Equation (
S54
) is given by the sum of two contributions: i) a thermal noise contribution,
2D_{t}t
, and ii) a contribution due to the active force, which is
\propto D
. For
t\ll\tau
, we obtain

\textrm{MSD}\approx 2D_{t}t+\frac{D}{\tau}t^{2}.

(S55)

For short timescales the diffusive regime
\sim t
is solely controlled by the thermal noise amplitude
D_{t}
. This situation is analogous to a dissipative quantum case discussed in Sec.
II.2
, where the diffusive regime at short timescales is governed by the gain rate
\nu_{+}
associated with the equilibrium temperature of the thermal bath.

A ballistic regime
\sim t^{2}
due to the active force emerges at intermediate times, while for the long timescales
t\gg\tau
, the dynamics transitions back to a diffusive behavior:

\textrm{MSD}\approx 2(D_{t}+D)t.

(S56)

For the inertial (undamped) limit
\gamma=0
, Equation (
S49
) simplifies to

m\ddot{x}=\gamma_{c}\dot{x}_{c}.

(S57)

Similar to the quantum case discussed in Sec.
II
, we now express
x_{c}(t)
as a time integral of its velocity (
S19
) in the Green-Kubo relation, and obtain an expression for the classical MSD:

\textrm{MSD}=\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{c}^{2}}{m^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{3}\int_{0}^{t_{3}}dt_{4}\frac{D}{\tau}e^{-|t_{2}-t_{4}|/\tau}=\frac{D\gamma_{c}^{2}}{3m^{2}}\left(2t^{3}-3t^{2}\tau+6\tau^{3}-6e^{-t/\tau}\tau^{2}(t+\tau)\right).

(S58)

It exhibits quartic scaling for short timescales
t\ll\tau
,

\textrm{MSD}\approx\frac{D\gamma_{c}^{2}}{4\tau m^{2}}t^{4},

(S59)

while for long timescales
t\gg\tau
, it follows the cubic scaling:

\textrm{MSD}\approx\frac{2D\gamma_{c}^{2}}{3m^{2}}t^{3}.

(S60)

III.3
Mimicking active motion

Let us now consider a passive classical particle that is set in motion via a moving trapping potential
V(x,t)
, Eq. (
S17
). The resulting dynamics read:

m\ddot{x}+\gamma\dot{x}=-\nabla V(x,t)+\gamma\sqrt{2D_{t}}\zeta(t).

(S61)

This type of driving is effective for approximating the properties of the active motion, since (i) the thermal diffusive regime
\sim t
persists in the presence of the white noise term, and (ii) it recovers the MSD scalings for intermediate
\sim t^{2}
and long
\sim t
timescales. Thus, a passive particle in the moving trapping potential mimics the essential features of AOUP.

However, the imposed potential introduces an additional scaling regime for short timescales.

In the overdamped limit
m\to 0
with
\gamma=\gamma_{c}
and harmonic potential
q=2
, Equation (
S61
) reduces to

\gamma\dot{x}=-m_{c}\omega^{2}(x-x_{c}).

(S62)

Since
x_{c}
dominates
x
for
t\ll\tau
, at short timescales this equation is structurally identical to Eq. (
S57
), thus giving us
t^{4}
-scaling in between the diffusive regime at very short times and ballistic regime at intermediate times (center column of Fig.
1
in the main text).
For the inertial limit
\gamma\to 0
with
m=m_{c}
and harmonic potential
q=2
,

\ddot{x}+\omega^{2}x=\omega^{2}x_{c}(t),

(S63)

which is a non-homogeneous second-order linear differential equation representing an undamped driven harmonic oscillator. For the particle initialized in the potential minimum
x(0)=x_{c}(0)=0
(corresponding to the ground state in the quantum case), the solution is:

x(t)=\text{Re}\left[\omega^{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\,x_{c}(t_{2})\,e^{i\omega[(t_{2}-t_{1})+(t-t_{1})]}\right].

(S64)

We present
x_{c}(t)
as a time integral of its velocity (
S19
) in the solution (
S64
), with (
S18
) as the active velocity correlation, and obtain the following expression for the classical MSD:

\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle\sim\omega^{4}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}dt_{3}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{4}\int_{0}^{t_{4}}dt_{5}\int_{0}^{t_{5}}dt_{6}\frac{D}{\tau}e^{-|t_{3}-t_{6}|}.

(S65)

To the lowest order in
t
, this reduces to:

\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle\sim\omega^{4}\frac{D}{36\tau}t^{6}+O(t^{8}),

(S66)

corresponding to the short-time sextic scaling discussed in the main text (left column in Fig.
3
for
q=2

For an arbitrary
q
in the external potential
V(x,t)=V_{0}|x-x_{c}(t)|^{q}
, since
x_{c}
dominates
x
in the equation of motion for short times, we have

x(t)\sim\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{dt_{1}}dt_{2}[x_{c}(t_{2})]^{q-1},

(S67)

Generalizing Eq. (
S19

[x_{c}(t)]^{q-1}\sim\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\ldots\int_{0}^{t_{q-2}}dt_{q-1}[\dot{x}_{c}(t_{q})]^{q-1},

(S68)

we obtain

\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle\sim\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\ldots\int_{0}^{t_{q}}dt_{q+1}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{q+2}\int_{0}^{t_{q+2}}dt_{q+3}\ldots\int_{0}^{t_{2q+1}}dt_{2q+2}\frac{D}{\tau}e^{-q|t_{q+1}-t_{2q+2}|}\sim t^{2(q+1)},

(S69)

which is the result for short timescales for
q>0

III.4
Short time mean squared displacement of initially distributed particles.

For a more general scenario, where the initial position and velocity of the particle are non-zero, the equation of motion can be written as the integral equation

x(t)=v(0)t+x(0)-\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\partial_{x}V(x(t_{2}),t_{2})dt_{2},

(S70)

where the integral term is dominated by the initial conditions
x(0),v(0)
, at short times. We thus obtain the following expression:

\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle-\langle x^{2}(0)\rangle\sim\langle v^{2}(0)\rangle t^{2}+2\langle x(0)\rangle\langle v(0)\rangle t.

(S71)

For an initial equilibrium Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution of velocities at a finite temperature, the classical MSD is given by:

\textrm{MSD}\sim\frac{3k_{\rm B}T}{m}t^{2}.

(S72)

Therefore, a classical system with an initial equilibrium distribution at finite temperature has always a scaling that is different to the one obtained in the quantum case. In addition, more important, the negative MSD observed in the non-dissipative quantum case for
q>0
is not present in classical systems at finite temperatures.
