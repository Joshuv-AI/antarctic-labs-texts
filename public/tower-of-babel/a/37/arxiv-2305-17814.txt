The Realizability of Theta Graphs as Reconfiguration Graphs of Minimum Independent Dominating Sets
R. C. Brewster, C. M. Mynhardt ∗, L. E. Teshima

Abstract
The independent domination number i ⁡ ( G ) i(G) of a graph G G is the minimum cardinality of a maximal independent set of G G , also called an i ⁡ ( G ) i(G) -set. The i i -graph of G G , denoted ℐ ⁡ ( G ) \mathscr{I}(G) , is the graph whose vertices correspond to the i ⁡ ( G ) i(G) -sets, and where two i ⁡ ( G ) i(G) -sets are adjacent if and only if they differ by two adjacent vertices. Not all graphs are i i -graph realizable, that is, given a target graph H H , there does not necessarily exist a source graph G G such that H ≅ ℐ ⁡ ( G ) H\cong\mathscr{I}(G) . We consider a class of graphs called “theta graphs”: a theta graph is the union of three internally disjoint nontrivial paths with the same two distinct end vertices. We characterize theta graphs that are i i -graph realizable, showing that there are only finitely many that are not. We also characterize those line graphs and claw-free graphs that are i i -graphs, and show that all 3 3 -connected cubic bipartite planar graphs are i i -graphs.

Introduction

Our main topic is the characterization of theta graphs that are obtainable as reconfiguration graphs with respect to the minimum independent dominating sets of some graph.

In graph theory, reconfiguration problems are often concerned with solutions to a specific problem that are
vertex subsets of a graph. When this is the case, the reconfiguration problem can be viewed as
a token manipulation problem, where
a solution subset is represented by placing a token at each vertex of the subset. Each solution is
represented as a vertex of a new graph, referred to as a
reconfiguration graph
, where adjacency between vertices corresponds to a predefined token manipulation rule called the
reconfiguration step
. The reconfiguration step we consider here consists of sliding a single token along an edge between adjacent vertices
belonging to different solutions.

More formally, given a graph
G
, the
slide graph
of
G
is the graph
H
such that each vertex of
H
represents a solution of some problem
on
G
, and two vertices
u
and
v
of
H
are adjacent if and only if the solution in
G
corresponding
to
u
can be transformed into the solution corresponding to
v
by sliding a single
token along the edge
uv\in E(G)
. See
[
7
]
for a survey on reconfiguration of colourings and dominating sets in graphs.

We use the standard notation of
\alpha(G)
for the independence number of a graph
G
.
The
independent domination number

i(G)
of
G
is the
minimum cardinality of a maximal independent set of
G
, or, equivalently,
the minimum cardinality of an independent domination set of
G
. An independent dominating set of
G
of cardinality
i(G)
is also called an
i
-
set
of
G
, or an
i(G)
-
set
. An
\alpha
-
set
of
G
, or an
\alpha(G)
-
set
, is defined similarly. When
i(G)=\alpha(G)
, we say that
G
is
well-covered
.

Given a graph
G
, we consider the slide graph
\mathscr{I}(G)
of
G
, formally defined in Section
2
below, with respect to its
i
-sets. Our main result, Theorem
5.3
, concerns the class
\Theta
of “theta graphs” for which we characterize, in Sections
5
and
6
, those graphs

for which there exists a graph
G\in\Theta
such that
H\cong\mathscr{I}(G)
. We state known results required here in Section
3
. We introduce the technique we use for theta graphs in Section
4
, where we apply it to the simpler problem of characterizing line graphs and claw-free graphs that are realizable as
i
-graphs. In Section
7.1
we exhibit a graph that is neither a theta graph nor an
i
-graph, and in Section
7.2
we show that certain planar graphs are

-graphs and
\alpha
-graphs. We conclude with some open problems in Section

In general, we follow the notation of

]
. For other domination principles and

-Graphs and Theta Graphs

-set

of

.
Then

-set

.
Similarly, the

of a graph

We say

-graph

-

. Moreover, we refer to

of the

-graph

.
Going forward, we mildly abuse notation to denote both the

-set

and

In acknowledgment of the slide-action in

-graphs, given

More generally, we use

The study of

5
Theta Graphs From Graph Complements

Consider a graph
G
with
i(G)=3
. Each

-set of

is represented as a triangle (a

) in
\overline{G}
.
If
X
and

are two

-sets of

with

, then

and
\overline{G}[Y]
are triangles in
\overline{G}
, and have

. Although it is technically the induced subgraphs

and

that are the triangles of

, for notational simplicity we refer to

and

as triangles. In

, the triangle

can be transformed into the triangle

by removing the vertex

and adding in the vertex

(where

). Thus, we say that two triangles are

if they share exactly one edge. Moreover, since two

are adjacent if and only if their associated triangles in
\overline{G}
are adjacent, we use the same notation for

as triangle adjacency in
\overline{G}
; that is, the notation

represents both

and

being adjacent, and triangles

and

of
\overline{G}
being adjacent.

In the following sections we use triangle adjacency to construct
complement seed graphs
for the

-graphs of theta graphs; that is, a graph
\overline{G}
such that
\mathscr{I}(G)
is isomorphic to some desired theta graph. Before proceeding with these constructions, we note some observations which will help us with this process.

Observation 5.1

A graph

has

if and only if
\overline{G}
is nonempty and has an edge that does not lie on a triangle.

If
\overline{G}
has an edge

that does not lie on a triangle, then

is independent and dominating in

, and so

. When building our seed graphs

with

, it is therefore necessary to ensure that every edge of the complement

belongs to a triangle.

Observation 5.2

Let
G
be a graph with

. If

is a (possibly non-maximal) clique in

, then no

Suppose that

is such a clique of

. Then, for example,

in

, and so

is not an

-set of

.
Conversely, suppose that

is a triangle in a graph

with

. By attaching a new vertex

, we remove

as an

, while keeping all other

-sets of

(and hence

The use of triangle adjacency in a graph

to determine

translate to
\widetilde{G}
. Then, we construct the complement of
\widetilde{G}
, which we refer to as
H
. We claim that
H
is a seed graph of
G
; that is,
\mathscr{I}(H)
contains an induced copy of
G
.

Theorem 7.5

Every cubic
3
-connected bipartite planar graph is an
i
-graph and an
\alpha
-graph.

Proof.
Let
G
be a cubic 3-connected bipartite planar graph and consider the dual
\widetilde{G}
of
G
. Since
G
is bridgeless,
\widetilde{G}
has no loops. Moreover, since
G
is 3-connected, Theorem
7.2
implies that no two edges separate
G
; hence,
\widetilde{G}
has no multiple edges. Therefore,
\widetilde{G}
is a (simple) graph. Further, since
G
is cubic, each face of
\widetilde{G}
is a triangle, and so
\widetilde{G}
is a maximal planar graph.

We note the following two key observations. First, since each face of
\widetilde{G}
is a triangle,

(1)

each edge of
\widetilde{G}
belongs to a triangle.

For the second, note that since
G
is bipartite,
\widetilde{G}
is Eulerian (by Theorem
7.3
). Then, by Theorem
7.4
,
\chi(\widetilde{G})=3
, so
\widetilde{G}
does not contain a copy of
K_{4}
. Thus,

(2)

every triangle of
\widetilde{G}
is a maximal clique.

Now, by the duality of
\widetilde{G}
and
G
, there is a one-to-one correspondence between the facial triangles of
\widetilde{G}
and the vertices of
G
. Let
H
be the complement of
\widetilde{G}
. By
(1)
and
(2)
,
i(H)=\alpha(H)=3
, and every maximal independent set of
G
corresponds to a triangle of
\widetilde{G}
. But again, by duality, the facial triangles of
\widetilde{G}
and their adjacencies correspond to the vertices of
G
and their adjacencies. Therefore,
\mathscr{I}(H)
contains
G
as an induced subgraph. Any additional unwanted vertices of
\mathscr{I}(H)
can be removed by applying the Deletion Lemma (Lemma
3.10
).

We conclude with a few open problems. Although the problems are stated here for

-graphs, many are relevant to other reconfiguration graphs pertaining to domination-type parameters and are also mentioned in

Problem 2

Determine conditions on the graph
G
under which
\mathscr{I}(G)
is (a) connected (b) disconnected.

A graph
G
is
well-covered
if all its maximal independent sets have the same cardinality, that is, if
\alpha(G)=i(G)
.

Problem 3

(a)

Determine those
i
-graphs that are also
\alpha
-graph realizable.

(b)

Determine those
i
-graphs
H
for which there exists a well-covered seed graph
G
such that
\mathscr{I}(G)\cong H
.

Problem 4

We have already seen that classes of graphs trees, cycles, and, more generally, block graphs, are
i
-graphs.

As we build new graphs from these families of
i
-graphs, using tree structures, which of those are also
i
-graphs? For example, are cycle-trees
i
-graphs? Path-trees? For what families of graphs
H
are
H
-trees
i
-graphs?

Problem 5

Determine the structure of
i
-graphs of various families of trees. For example, consider

(a)

caterpillars in which every vertex has degree
1
or
3
,

(b)

spiders (
K_{1,r}

Problem 6

Find more classes of
i
-graphs that are Hamiltonian, or Hamiltonian traceable.

Problem 7

Suppose
G_{1},G_{2},\dots
are graphs such that
\mathscr{I}(G_{1})\cong G_{2}
,
\mathscr{I}(G_{2})\cong G_{3}
,
\mathscr{I}(G_{3})\cong G_{4},\dots

k

\mathscr{I}(G_{k})\cong G_{1}

As a special case of Problem
7
, note that for any

,

\mathscr{I}(C_{k})\cong C_{k}

Problem 8

Characterize the graphs
G
