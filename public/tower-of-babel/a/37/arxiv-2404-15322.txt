A phase-field fracture model in thermo-poro-elastic media with micromechanical strain energy degradation
Yuhao Liu, Keita Yoshioka, Tao You, Hanzhang Li, Fengshou Zhang

Abstract
This work extends the hydro-mechanical phase-field fracture model to non-isothermal conditions with micromechanics based poroelasticity, which degrades Biot’s coefficient not only with the phase-field variable (damage) but also with the energy decomposition scheme. Furthermore, we propose a new approach to update porosity solely determined by the strain change rather than damage evolution as in the existing models. As such, these poroelastic behaviors of Biot’s coefficient and the porosity dictate Biot’s modulus and the thermal expansion coefficient. For numerical implementation, we employ an isotropic diffusion method to stabilize the advection-dominated heat flux and adapt the fixed stress split method to account for the thermal stress. We verify our model against a series of analytical solutions such as Terzaghi’s consolidation, thermal consolidation, and the plane strain hydraulic fracture propagation, known as the KGD fracture. Finally, numerical experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the stabilization method and intricate thermo-hydro-mechanical interactions during hydraulic fracturing with and without a pre-existing weak interface. Keywords: Phase-field; Hydraulic fracturing; Thermo-hydro-mechanical coupling; Thermo-poroelasticity; Fixed stress split; Isotropic diffusion method

Phase-field models of fracture

For thermo-hydro-mechanical (THM) coupled modeling, which is the main topic in this study, Noii and Wick (2019)

first extended the hydro-mechanical phase-field fracture model to include thermal effects by introducing the work of temperature from thermo-poroelasticity in the total energy functional.
However, their model neglected spatial variations of the pressure field and, consequently, the convective heat transfer.
For a more complete coupling of thermo-hydro-mechanical processes, Li et al.

implemented the multi-physics interactions during the fracture propagation.
Suh and Sun (2021)

Despite all the recent developments in thermo-hydro-mechanical coupled phase-field fracture models, the existing works have not adequately addressed the following three aspects.
The first is the degradation of poro-thermo-elastic strain energy.
While the degradation of elastic strain energy has been more or less established, somewhat ad-hoc and various ways to degrade the poro-thermo-elastic strain energy have been employed in
[
54
,
61
,
66
,
74
]
.
The second is the transition of the hydraulic and thermal material parameters from intact to fractured material.
Although the most popular approach is to use a linear indicator function
[
42
,
26
]
, a linear interpolation does not conserve the mass or energy.
Lastly, the advection-dominated heat transfer in fractures can cause numerical oscillation
[
67
]
, and some stabilization methods may be required
[
66
]
.

In this study, we aim to address these three aspects.
First, we extend the existing hydro-mechanical phase-field fracture model to thermo-poro-elastic media by introducing the thermal strain.
The degradation of the thermo-poro-elastic strain energy is achieved through micromechanically derived Biot’s coefficient, and its degradation depends on the phase-field (damage) and the energy decomposition scheme.
Then, we propose a new way to update the porosity that only depends on the strain change rather than the damage.
From the degraded poro-elastic coefficients and porosity, we approximate hydraulic and thermal properties that transition from a fully-damaged to an intact state of the material.
Finally, we employ an isotropic diffusion method to stabilize the advective dominated heat flow in fracture.
For numerical implementation, we apply a hybrid staggered scheme and adapt the fixed stress split stabilization method by accounting for thermal stress.
Our proposed model is then tested by a series of analytical solutions verifying different implementation modules.

Section

In section

The accuracy of the newly proposed porosity update is highlighted against the existing hydro-mechanical phase-field model.
Section

2.1
Mechanical equilibrium with discrete cracks

The mechanical equilibrium and fracture evolution in the thermo-poroelastic medium can be derived based on the variational approach proposed by Francfort and Marigo (1998)
[
27
]
.
They defined the total energy of a homogeneous isotropic and linearly elastic material with a set of fractures as a sum of the strain energy, the surface energy of fracture, the body force
{\mathbf{b}}
and the traction
{\mathbf{\bar{t}}}
on the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{N}}
:

\mathcal{E}_{\Gamma}({\mathbf{u}},{\Gamma}):=\int_{{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma}\psi({\mathbf{u}})\,\mathrm{d}V+\int_{{\Gamma}}G_{c}\,\mathrm{d}S-\int_{{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma}{\mathbf{b}}\cdot{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}V-\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{N}}}{\mathbf{\bar{t}}}\cdot{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}V

(1)

where
G_{c}
is the critical surface energy release rate,
{\mathbf{u}}
is the displacement field, and
\psi({\mathbf{u}})
is the strain energy density described by the elastic stiffness tensor
\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{m}}
and linearized strain
\bm{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{u}})=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla{\mathbf{u}}+\nabla{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{T}})
:

\psi({\mathbf{u}})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{C}_{m}:\bm{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{u}}):\bm{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{u}}).

(2)

Following
[
48
,
61
]
, we propose the form of total energy in thermo-poroelastic medium

\mathcal{F}_{\Gamma}({\mathbf{u}},p,T,{\Gamma}):=W({\mathbf{u}},p,T)+\int_{{\Gamma}}G_{c}\,\mathrm{d}S-\int_{{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma}{\mathbf{b}}\cdot{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}V-\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{N}}}{\mathbf{\bar{t}}}\cdot{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}V,

(3)

where thermo-poro-elastic strain energy
W
is assumed to decompose into elastic, hydraulic, and thermal parts:

W({\mathbf{u}},p,T):=W_{\mathrm{elastic}}({\mathbf{u}},T)+W_{\mathrm{fluid}}({\mathbf{u}},\zeta)+W_{\mathrm{thermo}}(T).

(4)

The thermo-elastic strain energy is generated by the intergranular stress acting on the solid skeleton and is given as:

W_{\mathrm{elastic}}({\mathbf{u}},T):=\int_{{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma}\psi_{e}({\mathbf{u}},T)\,\mathrm{d}V.

(5)

Thermo-poro-elastic analyses in a small strain theory show that the total strain is decomposed into the elastic strain
\varepsilon_{e}
and the thermal strain
\varepsilon_{t}
induced by the temperature difference between the current temperature and the initial temperature
T_{0}
, i.e.,
\Delta T=T-T_{0}

[
59
]

\bm{\varepsilon}=\bm{\varepsilon_{e}}+\bm{\varepsilon_{t}}=\bm{\varepsilon_{e}}+\beta\Delta T{\mathbf{I}},

(6)

where
\beta
is the thermal expansion coefficient.
The elastic strain energy,
\psi({\mathbf{u}})
, is solely contributed by the elastic strain
[
2
]
and thus we have

\psi_{e}({\mathbf{u}},T)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{C}_{m}:\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}({\mathbf{u}}):\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}({\mathbf{u}})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{C}_{m}:\Big(\bm{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{u}})-\beta\Delta T{\mathbf{I}}\Big):\Big(\bm{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{u}})-\beta\Delta T{\mathbf{I}}\Big).

(7)

Following
[
18
,
42
,
77
,
74
]
, the energy of pore fluid can be defined as

W_{\mathrm{fluid}}({\mathbf{u}},\zeta):=\int_{{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma}\psi_{f}({\mathbf{u}}.\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}V=\int_{{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma}\frac{M_{p}}{2}\left[\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{\varepsilon_{e}}})-\zeta\right]^{2}\,\mathrm{d}V,

(8)

where
\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}
and
M_{p}
are Biot’s coefficient and Biot’s modulus, and

is the variation of fluid content

Neglecting the effect of thermal expansion of the pore fluid, we have

\zeta=\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\varepsilon_{e}})+\frac{p}{M_{p}}.

(9)

Assuming that the mechanical or hydraulic energy does not contribute to the thermal energy
[

, we have

W_{\mathrm{thermo}}(T):=\int_{{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma}\psi_{T}(T)\,\mathrm{d}V=\int_{{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma}(\rho c)_{m}\left[(T-T_{\mathrm{ref}})-T\ln\left(\frac{T}{T_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)\right]\,\mathrm{d}V.

(10)

With the local thermal equilibrium assumption,
T
represents the unified temperature in the domain, and
T_{\mathrm{ref}}
is the reference temperature.

(\rho c)_{m}
is the equivalent heat storage coefficient, and for porous medium, it is given by

(\rho c)_{m}=\phi c_{f}\rho_{p,f}+(1-\phi)c_{s}\rho_{p,s},

(11)

where

is the specific heat,
\rho
is the density, and
\phi
is the porosity.

Substituting Eq. (
5

8
), (
7
), (

) yields

\displaystyle W({\mathbf{u}},p,T)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma}\psi_{e}({\mathbf{u}},T)\,\mathrm{d}V+\int_{{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma}\frac{M_{p}}{2}\left[\alpha\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\varepsilon_{e}})-\zeta\right]^{2}\,\mathrm{d}V

(12)

\displaystyle+\int_{{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma}(\rho c)_{m}\left[(T-T_{\mathrm{ref}})-T\ln\left(\frac{T}{T_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)\right]\,\mathrm{d}V

\displaystyle=\int_{{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma}\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{C}_{m}:\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}({\mathbf{u}}):\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}({\mathbf{u}})\,\mathrm{d}V+\int_{{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma}\frac{M_{p}}{2}\left[\alpha\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{\varepsilon_{e}}})-\zeta\right]^{2}\,\mathrm{d}V

From Eq. (

Substituting Eq. (

2.2
Mechanical equilibrium with phase-field approximation

To alleviate the implementational difficulties of the sharp fractures, we apply the now-classical phase-field approach
[
10
,
11
]
.
Bourdin et al. (2000)
[
10
]
introduced a phase-field variable
\upsilon
that represents a state of the material from intact
(\upsilon=1)
to fully broken
(\upsilon=0)
and regularized the Francfort-Marigo energy functional as

\mathcal{E}({\mathbf{u}},\upsilon):=\int_{{\Omega}}\psi({\mathbf{u}},\upsilon)\,\mathrm{d}V+\int_{{\Omega}}\frac{G_{c}}{4c_{n}}\left[\frac{(1-\upsilon)^{n}}{\ell}+\ell\nabla\upsilon\cdot\nabla\upsilon\right]\,\mathrm{d}V-\int_{{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma}{\mathbf{b}}\cdot{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}V-\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{N}}}{\mathbf{\bar{t}}}\cdot{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}V,

(16)

where
c_{n}
is the normalizing parameter defined as
c_{n}=\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)^{n/2}\,\mathrm{d}S

[
46
,
62
]
.
For
n=1
, the model is called
AT
1
model, and for
n=2
,
AT
2
model
[
57
]
.
Also,
\ell
is the characteristic parameter with the dimension of a length that controls the phase-field profile transition.
The strain energy density
\psi({\mathbf{u}},\upsilon)
in Eq. (
16
) acknowledges the phase-field variable
\upsilon
and is continuous over
{\Omega}
for integration.

Now, extending the regularized functional in Eq. (
16
) to the thermo-poro-elastic medium, we can write

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{u}},p,T,\upsilon):=\int_{{\Omega}}\psi_{e}({\mathbf{u}},T,\upsilon)\,\mathrm{d}V+\int_{{\Omega}}\psi_{f}({\mathbf{u}},\zeta,\upsilon)\,\mathrm{d}V+\int_{{\Omega}}\psi_{T}(T,\upsilon)\,\mathrm{d}V

(17)

\displaystyle+\int_{{\Omega}}\frac{G_{c}}{4c_{n}}\left[\frac{(1-\upsilon)^{n}}{\ell}+\ell\nabla\upsilon\cdot\nabla\upsilon\right]\,\mathrm{d}V-\int_{{\Omega}}{\mathbf{b}}\cdot{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}V-\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{N}}}{\mathbf{\bar{t}}}\cdot{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}V.

The total stress can be found by taking the derivative of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to

as

\sigma=\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\upsilon):{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}({\mathbf{u}})-\alpha(\upsilon)p{\mathbf{I}}-3\beta K\Delta T{\mathbf{I}},

represent both intact and the fractured medium while

and

The expressions of

and

can be derived from the strain energy decomposition scheme

]

The elastic strain energy density is generally split into the positive (tension) and negative (compression) parts,
\psi=\psi_{+}+\psi_{-}
, to differentiate the contributions between positive (tension) and negative (compression) parts to the damage evolution.
With a degradation function

2

2

In this study, we employed

where

is a phase-field parameter representing residual stiffness, which keeps the system of equations well-conditioned for the partly-broken state

]
.
,
\psi_{e}({\mathbf{u}},T,\upsilon)
is rewritten as
[

,

]

\displaystyle\psi_{e}({\mathbf{u}},T,\upsilon)

\displaystyle=g(\upsilon)\psi_{+}({\mathbf{u}})+\psi_{-}({\mathbf{u}})

(19)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}g(\upsilon)\mathbb{C}_{+}:\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}:\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{C}_{-}:\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}:\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\upsilon):\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}:\bm{\varepsilon}_{e},

where
\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\upsilon)=g(\upsilon)\mathbb{C}_{+}+\mathbb{C}_{-}
.
Similarly, Biot’s coefficient evolves with the phase-field as the drained bulk modulus is degraded with damage as

]

\alpha(\upsilon)=1-\frac{K_{\mathrm{eff}}(\upsilon)}{K_{s}}.

(20)

The expressions of
\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\upsilon)
and

depend on the specific energy decomposition model applied as discussed in
[

]
.
We employed the volumetric-deviatoric (V-D) energy split
[

]
in this study and then the strain energy is split into
[

]

\psi^{\mathrm{vd}}(u,v)=g(v)\left[\frac{K_{\mathrm{m}}}{2}\langle\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{e})\rangle_{+}^{2}+\mu\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}:\mathbb{K}:\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}\right]+\frac{K_{\mathrm{m}}}{2}\langle\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{e})\rangle_{-}^{2},

where

Accordingly, we can derive the tangential stiffness (

\begin{aligned} \mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}(v)&=3\left[g(\upsilon)H(\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}))+H(\mathrm{Tr}(-\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}))\right]K_{\mathrm{m}}\mathbb{J}+2g(\upsilon)\mu\mathbb{K}\\
&=3K_{\mathrm{eff}}\mathbb{J}+2g(\upsilon)\mu\mathbb{K}\end{aligned},

(22)

with

K_{\mathrm{eff}}=\left[g(\upsilon)H(\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}))+H(\mathrm{Tr}(-\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}))\right]K_{\mathrm{m}},

(23)

where

is the Heaviside step function defined as

H(x):=\begin{cases}0,\quad&x<0\\
1,\quad&x\geq 0\end{cases}.

(24)

Substituting Eq. (

is

\displaystyle\alpha(v)

\displaystyle=1-\left[g(\upsilon)H(\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}\right))+H(\mathrm{Tr}\left(-\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}\right))\right]\frac{K_{\mathrm{m}}}{K_{s}}

(25)

\displaystyle=1-\left[g(v)H(\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}\right))+H(\mathrm{Tr}\left(-\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}\right))\right](1-\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}).

Eq. (

Otherwise, the fracture is closed, and
\alpha_{m}\mathrm{m}=1-(1-\alpha_{\mathrm{m}})=\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}

2.3
Fluid flow model

Considering the equivalent properties over
\Omega
, the mass balance is given as

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\alpha(\upsilon)\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{u}}+\frac{p}{M_{p}(\upsilon)}-\frac{T}{M_{T}(\upsilon)}\right)+\nabla\cdot\left({\mathbf{q}}_{f}\right)=Q_{f}\quad\mathrm{in}\quad\Omega,

(26)

where
M_{T}(\upsilon)
is the effective thermal storage coefficient that describes the thermal expansion in the incremental content of pore fluid.
Before we derive the expressions of
M_{p}(\upsilon)
and
M_{T}(\upsilon)
, we first discuss how the permeability tensor,
{\mathbf{K}}
, is obtained and the porosity
\phi
is updated in our model.
The fluid flux
{\mathbf{q}}_{f}
is given by Darcy’s law as

{\mathbf{q}}_{f}=-\frac{{\mathbf{K}}}{\mu}\nabla(p+\gamma_{f}z)\quad\mathrm{in}\quad\Omega,

(27)

with
\mu
the fluid viscosity,
\gamma_{f}
the specific weight of water and

the vertical coordinate, and
Q_{f}
the source term.
In the following derivation, the gravity can be neglected without loss of generality.

For permeability enhancement by fractures, we apply a formulation of anisotropic permeability which implicitly takes into account for the Poiseuille-type flow in fractures
[
50
,
51
]

{\mathbf{K}}={\mathbf{K_{m}}}{\mathbf{I}}+(1-\upsilon)^{\xi}\frac{w^{2}}{12}\left({\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{n}}_{{\Gamma}}\otimes{\mathbf{n}}_{{\Gamma}}\right),

(28)

where
{\mathbf{K_{m}}}
is the isotropic permeability,
\xi\geq 1
is a weighting exponent, and the fracture has an aperture of

{\mathbf{n}}_{{\Gamma}}
along the interface. The weighting exponent
\xi
controls the intensity of permeability enhancement to ensure numerical stability.

One may approximate the crack normal vector (
{\mathbf{n}}_{{\Gamma}}
) from the gradient of the phase-field
\nabla\upsilon
, but
\nabla\upsilon
deviates from the normal direction near the crack tip
[
9
]
.
Moreover,
\nabla\upsilon
is not defined on a fully broken element where
\upsilon=0
.
To avoid these problems, we decompose a strain tensor into principal strains as

\bm{\varepsilon}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\varepsilon_{i}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}

(29)

where
\varepsilon_{i}
are the principal strains (
\varepsilon_{1}>\varepsilon_{2}>\varepsilon_{3}
) and
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
are the associated eigenvectors.
Considering an internally pressurized crack, the crack normal deformation must be dominant, and thus we assign

{\mathbf{n}}_{{\Gamma}}={\mathbf{e}}_{1}.

(30)

This approach enables simple code parallelization because the computation is performed at each integration point, while some other methods like line integral
[
9
]
or level-set
[
41
]
based approach require a global phase-field profile.

To estimate the fracture width (
\omega
), Miehe et al. (2015)
[
51
]
proposed:

\omega=h_{e}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{vol}},

(31)

where
h_{e}
is the corresponding element size, and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}
represents the volumetric strain.
Eq. (
31
) can approximate the fracture width accurately as reported in
[
77
]
because the volumetric strain is almost identical to the strain in the crack normal opening direction for fluid-driven fractures.
However, this approximation can lead to spurious crack opening profiles when thermal stress is present.
Instead, in this study, we approximate the fracture width using the maximum principal strain:

\omega=h_{e}\varepsilon_{1}.

(32)

For further discussions, we refer to
C
, where we compared crack opening profiles from Eq. (
31
) with those from Eq. (
32
).

Regarding the porosity update, existing studies undertook various ways to account for evolving phase-field (damage).
Lee and Wick (2017)
[
41
]
first proposed a linear indicator function with threshold values to define the effective porosity distinguishing between the matrix and fracture

\phi(\upsilon)=\mathcal{X}_{R}\left(\frac{1}{M}p_{R}+\alpha\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{u}}\right)+\mathcal{X}_{F}\left(c_{f}p_{f}\right)

(33)

where
\mathcal{X}_{R}\in[0,1]
and
\mathcal{X}_{F}\in[0,1]
are two linear indicator functions for the reservoir domain and the fracture domain.

\mathcal{X}_{F}(\upsilon)
is 0 and
\mathcal{X}_{R}(\upsilon)
is 1 in the reservoir domain while
\mathcal{X}_{F}(\upsilon)
is 1
\mathcal{X}_{R}(\upsilon)
is 0 in the fracture domain.
And
\mathcal{X}_{F}(\upsilon)
and
\mathcal{X}_{R}(\upsilon)
vary linearly in the transition zone.
This concept has been used in may works
[
33
,
71
,
36
,
25
,
79
]
.
Similarly, Li et al. (2021)
[
42
]
linearly interpolated the matrix porosity (
\phi_{m}
) and the fracture porosity (1) as

\phi(\upsilon)=\mathcal{X}_{R}\phi_{m}+\mathcal{X}_{F}.

(34)

Zhou et al.
[
79
,
80
]
also applied a similar expression without considering the fluid compressibility

\phi(\upsilon)=\mathcal{X}_{R}\phi_{m}.

(35)

In
[
74
]
, the initial porosity
\phi_{m}
is degraded with the phase field
\upsilon
as

\phi(\upsilon)=\phi_{m}+(1-\phi_{m})(1-(1-\upsilon)^{2}).

(36)

Suh and Sun
[
61
]
additionally considered the effect of volumetric strain:

\phi(\upsilon)=\phi_{m}+g(\upsilon)(1-\phi_{m})(1-\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{u}}).

(37)

Lastly, You and Yoshioka (2023)
[
77
]
updated the porosity in a similar manner to Biot’s coefficient (Eq.(
25
)) as

\phi_{0}(\upsilon)=1-\left[g(\upsilon)H(\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}\right))+H(\mathrm{Tr}\left(-\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}\right))\right](1-\phi_{m}).

(38)

All of these approaches consider the transition of porosity from the matrix (
\phi_{\mathrm{m}}
) to fracture (
\phi=1
) relying on the phase-field profile,
\upsilon({\mathbf{x}})
. While the phase-field profile,
\upsilon({\mathbf{x}})
, is a diffused representation of crack, it does not physically represent the porosity at the corresponding location
{\mathbf{x}}
.
This is obvious because of the impacts of the characteristic length
\ell
on the phase-field transition.
The longer
\ell
, the wider the transition zone.
However, the porosity profile should not change with
\ell
.

Considering that a hydraulic fracture width is generally in the order of 10
-2
m, it is likely smaller than a computational element size
h_{e}
, which is typically in the order of 10
{}^{-1}\sim
10
0
m.
Therefore, one can consider that a physical crack opening is well contained within one element as depicted in Fig.
2
.
Accordingly, we can write the porosity change in a 2D quadrilateral element with the edge length
h_{e}
containing a line crack parallel to the edge as

\phi_{1}=\phi_{m}+\frac{\omega h_{e}}{{h_{e}}^{2}}=\phi_{m}+\frac{\omega}{h_{e}}.

(39)

Recalling Eq. (
32
), the porosity is given as:

\phi_{1}(\varepsilon)=\phi_{m}+\varepsilon_{1}.

(40)

Figure 2:
Physical crack opening within quadrilateral elements.

Using this porosity expression and effective Biot’s coefficient, we can write effective Biot’s modulus (
M_{p}(\upsilon)
) and the average thermal expansion coefficient (
M_{T}(\upsilon)
) as

\frac{1}{M_{p}(\upsilon,\varepsilon)}=\phi(\varepsilon)c_{f}+\frac{\alpha(\upsilon)-\phi(\varepsilon)}{K_{s}},

(41)

\frac{1}{M_{T}(\upsilon,\varepsilon)}=\phi(\varepsilon)\alpha_{f}+3\beta\left(\alpha(\upsilon)-\phi(\varepsilon)\right).

(42)

where
c_{f}
is the fluid compressibility,
K_{s}
is the solid phase’s intrinsic bulk modulus, and
\alpha_{f}
is the fluid volumetric thermal expansion coefficient.

2.4
Heat transfer model

Assuming the local thermal equilibrium and neglecting thermal effects due to deformation, we obtain the energy conservation equation in the porous medium as

(\rho c)_{m}\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot({\mathbf{q}}_{T})=Q_{T},

(43)

where
Q_{T}
is the thermal source term.
The total heat flux
{\mathbf{q}}_{T}
in Eq. (
44
) is decomposed into the advective and the conductive terms using Fourier’s law:

{\mathbf{q}}_{T}=\rho_{f}{\mathbf{q}}_{f}c_{f}T-{\mathbf{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{eff}}\nabla T,

(44)

with the effective thermal conductivity given as

{\mathbf{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\varepsilon)=\phi(\varepsilon){\mathbf{\lambda}}_{f}+(1-\phi(\varepsilon)){\mathbf{\lambda}}_{s}.

(45)

Similarly, the effective heat storage coefficient (Eq. (
11
)) is rewritten as

(\rho c)_{m}(\varepsilon)=\phi(\varepsilon)c_{p,f}\rho_{f}+(1-\phi(\varepsilon))c_{p,s}\rho_{s}.

(46)

Substituting Eq.(
44
) into Eq. (
43
), we have

\displaystyle(\rho c)_{m}\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}+c_{p,f}\rho_{f}{\mathbf{q}}_{f}\cdot\nabla T-\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{eff}}\nabla T=Q_{T}.

(47)

3
Numerical implementation

In a quasi-static setting, we obtain a solution pair of
({\mathbf{u}}_{i},\upsilon_{i})
that minimizes
\mathcal{F}
(Eq. (
17
)) each time as

(\mathbf{u}_{i},\upsilon_{i})=\argmin\left\{\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{u},\upsilon,p,T):\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}(t_{i}),\upsilon\in\mathcal{\upsilon}(t_{i},\upsilon_{i-1})\right\},

(48)

where
\mathcal{U}
is the kinematically admissible displacement set:

\mathcal{U}(t_{i})=\left\{\mathbf{u}\in H^{1}(\Omega):\mathbf{u}=0\quad\text{on}\quad\partial_{N}\Omega\right\}.

(49)

The kinematically admissible set of
\upsilon
requires an irreversible condition as:

\mathcal{V}(t_{i},\upsilon_{i-1})=\left\{\upsilon\in H^{1}(\Omega):0\leq\upsilon(x)\leq\eta\;\;\forall x\right\}.

(50)

where

\eta=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\quad\text{if}\quad\upsilon_{i-1}(x)\geq\upsilon_{ir}\\
\upsilon_{i-1}(x)&\quad\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

and
\upsilon_{ir}
is the irreversible threshold
\in[0,1]
(e.g. 0.05).
Following
[
10
]
, we apply an alternate minimization scheme which minimizes
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
{\mathbf{u}}
while fixing
\upsilon
and then minimizes
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
\upsilon
while fixing
{\mathbf{u}}
with the irreversible condition.
Therefore, the variations of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
{\mathbf{u}}
and
\upsilon
are given:

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathbf{u}}\mathcal{F}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}g(\upsilon){\mathbf{\sigma}}_{e}:\delta{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\,\mathrm{d}V-\int_{\Omega}\alpha(\upsilon)p\delta\varepsilon\,\mathrm{d}V-\int_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}\bar{{\mathbf{t}}}\cdot\delta{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}S-\int_{{\Omega}}{\mathbf{b}}\cdot{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}V

(51)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot(g(\upsilon){\mathbf{\sigma}}_{e})\cdot\delta{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}V+\int_{\partial_{N}\Omega}g(\upsilon)\mathbb{C}_{eff}:{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}_{e}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\delta{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}S-\int_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}\alpha(\upsilon)p{\mathbf{I}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\delta{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}S

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot(\alpha(\upsilon)p{\mathbf{I}})\cdot\delta{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}V-\int_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}\bar{{\mathbf{t}}}\cdot\delta{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}S-\int_{{\Omega}}{\mathbf{b}}\cdot\delta{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}V

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbf{0}},

and

\displaystyle\delta_{\upsilon}\mathcal{F}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}2(1-k)\upsilon\psi_{+}({\mathbf{u}})\delta\upsilon\,\mathrm{d}V+\int_{\Omega}\frac{p^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial 1/M_{p}(\upsilon,{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})}{\partial\upsilon}\delta\upsilon\,\mathrm{d}V

(52)

\displaystyle+\frac{G_{c}}{4c_{n}}\int_{\Omega}[-\frac{n}{\ell}(1-\upsilon)^{n-1}\delta\upsilon+2\ell\nabla\upsilon\cdot\nabla\delta\upsilon]\,\mathrm{d}V

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}2(1-k)\upsilon\psi_{+}({\mathbf{u}})\delta\upsilon dV+\int_{\Omega}\frac{p^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial 1/M_{p}(\upsilon,{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})}{\partial\upsilon}\delta\upsilon\,\mathrm{d}V

\displaystyle+\frac{G_{c}}{4c_{n}}\int_{\Omega}[-\frac{n}{\ell}(1-\upsilon)^{n-1}\delta\upsilon-2\ell\Delta\upsilon\delta\upsilon]\,\mathrm{d}V+\frac{G_{c}}{4c_{n}}\int_{\partial\Omega}2\ell\nabla\upsilon\cdot\bm{n}\delta\upsilon\,\mathrm{d}S

\displaystyle={\mathbf{0}}.

Note that the thermal energy
W_{thermo}
cancels because the porosity does not change with the phase-field
[
61
,
23
,
53
]
.
The derivative of the reciprocal of Biot’s modulus
M_{p}(\upsilon)
in Eq. (
52
) with respect to
\upsilon
writes

\frac{1/M_{p}(\upsilon,{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})}{\partial\upsilon}=\frac{2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}}{p}\upsilon(1-k)H(\mathrm{Tr}\left(\varepsilon\right))\left(1-\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}\right),

(53)

and its derivation details are given in
A
.

From Eqs. (
51
) and (
52
), we arrive at the strong forms of the coupled problem.
For the mechanical deformation, we have

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\nabla\cdot[g(\upsilon)\mathbb{C}_{+}:{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}_{e}-\alpha(\upsilon)p\bm{I}]+b=0&\text{in }\quad{\Omega}\\
g(\upsilon)\mathbb{C}_{+}:{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}_{e}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}-\bar{{\mathbf{t}}}=0\quad&\text{on}\quad\mathcal{C}_{N}\\
g(\upsilon)\mathbb{C}_{+}:{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}_{e}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}=0\quad&\text{on}\quad\partial\Gamma\\
\end{cases},

(54)

and for the phase-field evolution,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(1-k)\upsilon\mathbb{C}_{+}:{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}_{e}:{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}_{e}+\frac{G_{c}}{4c_{n}}\left[-\frac{n}{\ell}(1-\upsilon)^{n-1}-2\ell\Delta\upsilon\right]+\frac{p^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial 1/M_{p}(\upsilon)}{\partial\upsilon}=0&\text{in }\quad{\Omega}\\
\nabla\upsilon\cdot\bm{n}=0&\text{on}\quad\partial_{N}\Omega\end{cases}.

(55)

Multiplying Eqs. (
54
) and (
55
) with the weighting functions
{\mathbf{w}}_{u}\in H^{1}
and
{\mathbf{w}}_{\upsilon}\in H^{1}
and integrating over
{\Omega}
, we obtain the weak forms:

\int_{\Omega}\nabla{\mathbf{w}}_{u}\cdot\left[g(\upsilon){\mathbf{\sigma}}_{e}-\alpha(\upsilon)p{\mathbf{I}}\right]\,\mathrm{d}V-\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{b}}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}_{u}\,\mathrm{d}V-\int_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}\bar{{\mathbf{t}}}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}_{u}\,\mathrm{d}S={\mathbf{0}}

(56)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{w}}_{\upsilon}\left[2(1-k)\upsilon\psi_{+}({\mathbf{u}})-\frac{p^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial 1/M_{p}(\upsilon)}{\partial\upsilon}\right]\,\mathrm{d}V-\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{w}}_{\upsilon}\frac{G_{c}}{4c_{n}}\frac{n}{\ell}(1-\upsilon)^{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}V,

(57)

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}\frac{G_{c}}{2c_{n}}\ell\nabla{\mathbf{w}}_{\upsilon}\cdot\nabla\upsilon\,\mathrm{d}V={\mathbf{0}}.

Similarly, with
{\mathbf{w}}_{p}\in H^{1}
and
{\mathbf{w}}_{T}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, the weak forms of the mass and energy balances are

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\alpha(\upsilon)\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{u}}+\frac{1}{M_{p}(\upsilon,\varepsilon)}p-\frac{1}{M_{T}(\upsilon,\varepsilon)}T\right){\mathbf{w}}_{p}\,\mathrm{d}V+\int_{\Omega}\frac{{\mathbf{K}}}{\mu}\nabla p\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{w}}_{p}\,\mathrm{d}V

(58)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}Q_{f}{\mathbf{w}}_{p}\,\mathrm{d}V-\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{N}}}q_{n}{\mathbf{w}}_{p}\,\mathrm{d}S,

and

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\rho c)_{m}(\varepsilon)\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}{\mathbf{w}}_{T}\,\mathrm{d}V+\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{w}}_{T}c_{f}\rho_{f}{\mathbf{q}}_{f}\cdot\nabla T\,\mathrm{d}V+\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\varepsilon)\nabla T\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{w}}_{T}\,\mathrm{d}V

(59)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}Q_{T}{\mathbf{w}}_{p}\,\mathrm{d}V-\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{N}}}q_{Tn}{\mathbf{w}}_{T}\,\mathrm{d}S.

3.1
The staggered solution scheme

Eqs. (
56
), (
57
), (
58
) and (
59
) form the non-linear system of partial differential equations describing thermo-hydro-mechanical phase-field coupled problem.
We employed a staggered scheme based on
[
12
,
65
]
to split the system into four sub-problems in a sequence of
\upsilon-(T-p-{\mathbf{u}})
.
Globally, we have a staggered loop between the process of
\upsilon
and a paired process of
(T-p-{\mathbf{u}})
.
Within the
(T-p-{\mathbf{u}})
loop,
T
,
p
, and
{\mathbf{u}}
are solved in a staggered manner until convergence.

Specifically, at time step
k
, the backward Euler scheme for the time derivative is

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\frac{\partial(\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{u}})}{\partial t}=\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}^{k}-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}^{k-1}}{\Delta t}\\
&\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=\frac{p^{k}-p^{k-1}}{\Delta t}\\
&\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}=\frac{T^{k}-T^{k-1}}{\Delta t}\\
\end{cases}.

(60)

Then, the
m^{th}\>(m\geq 1)
iteration scheme reads

\bullet

\bm{\textbf{Step}\>1}
: Given
(T^{k,m-1},p^{k,m-1},{\mathbf{u}}^{k,m-1})
, solve for
\upsilon^{k,m}
:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{w}}_{\upsilon}\left[2(1-k)\upsilon^{k,m}\psi_{+}({\mathbf{u}})^{k,m-1}-\frac{\left(p^{k,m-1}\right)^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial 1/M_{p}}{\partial\upsilon}\right]\,\mathrm{d}V

(61)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{w}}_{\upsilon}\frac{G_{c}}{4c_{n}}\frac{n}{\ell}(1-\upsilon^{k,m})^{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}V-\int_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}\frac{G_{c}}{2c_{n}}\ell\nabla{\mathbf{w}}_{\upsilon}\cdot\nabla\upsilon^{k,m}\,\mathrm{d}V={\mathbf{0}}.

\bullet

\bm{\textbf{Step}\>2}
: Given
(p^{k,m-1},\upsilon^{k,m})
, find
T^{k,m}
independently:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\rho c)_{m}(\varepsilon)\frac{T^{k,m}-T^{k-1}}{\Delta t}{\mathbf{w}}_{T}\,\mathrm{d}V+\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{w}}_{T}c_{f}\rho_{f}{\mathbf{q}}_{f}^{k,m-1}\cdot\nabla T\,\mathrm{d}V+\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\varepsilon)\nabla T^{k,m}\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{w}}_{T}\,\mathrm{d}V

(62)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}Q_{T}{\mathbf{w}}_{p}\,\mathrm{d}V-\int_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}q_{Tn}{\mathbf{w}}_{T}\,\mathrm{d}S.

with

{\mathbf{q}}_{f}^{k,m-1}=-\frac{{\mathbf{K}}}{\mu}\nabla p^{k,m-1}

(63)

\bullet

\bm{\textbf{Step}\>3}
: Given
(T^{k,m},{\mathbf{u}}^{k,m-1},p^{k,m-1},\upsilon^{k,m})
, solve for
p^{k,m}
:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\alpha\frac{\varepsilon_{v}({\mathbf{u}}^{k,m})-\varepsilon_{v}({\mathbf{u}}^{k-1})}{\Delta t}+\frac{1}{M_{p}}\frac{p^{k,m}-p^{k-1}}{\Delta t}-\frac{1}{M_{T}}\frac{T^{k,m}-T^{k-1}}{\Delta t})\psi_{p}dV+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\bm{K}}{\mu}\nabla p^{k,m}\cdot\nabla\psi_{p}dV

(64)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}Q_{f}\psi_{p}dV-\int_{\partial_{N}\Omega}q_{n}\psi_{p}dS.

We apply the fixed-stress splitting method proposed by
[
38
]
for the stability of the fluid flow equation.
Freezing the volumetric stress, we eliminate the volumetric strain in the last iteration from the mass balance:

\displaystyle K_{\mathrm{eff}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}({\mathbf{u}}^{k,m})-\alpha p^{k,m}-3\alpha_{s}K_{\mathrm{eff}}(T^{k,m}-T_{0})

(65)

\displaystyle=K_{\mathrm{eff}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}({\mathbf{u}}^{k,m-1})-\alpha p^{k,m-1}-3\alpha_{s}K_{\mathrm{eff}}(T^{k,m-1}-T_{0}).

Then we have

K_{\mathrm{eff}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}({\mathbf{u}}^{k,m})=\frac{\alpha}{K_{\mathrm{eff}}}(p^{k,m}-p^{k,m-1})+3\alpha_{s}(T^{k,m}-T^{k,m-1}).

(66)

Substituting Eq. (
66
) into Eq. (
64
), the fluid flow equation now depends only on
p^{k,m}
as

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{K}\frac{p^{k,m}-p^{k,m-1}}{\Delta t}+3\alpha\alpha_{s}\frac{T^{k,m}-T^{k,m-1}}{\Delta t}\right){\mathbf{w}}_{p}dV

(67)

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{M_{p}}\frac{p^{k,m}-p^{k-1}}{\Delta t}-\frac{1}{M_{T}}\frac{T^{k,m}-T^{k-1}}{\Delta t}\right){\mathbf{w}}_{p}dV

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\frac{{\mathbf{K}}}{\mu}\nabla p^{k,m}\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{w}}_{p}dV=\int_{\Omega}\left(Q_{f}-\alpha\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}({\mathbf{u}}^{k,m-1})-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}({\mathbf{u}}^{k-1})}{\Delta t}\right){\mathbf{w}}_{p}dV-\int_{\partial_{N}\Omega}q_{n}{\mathbf{w}}_{p}dS.

\bullet

\bm{\textbf{Step}\>4}
: Given
(T^{k,m},p^{k,m},\upsilon^{k,m})
, solve for
{\mathbf{u}}^{k,m}
:

\int_{\Omega}\nabla{\mathbf{w}}_{u}\cdot\left[g(\upsilon^{k,m}){\mathbf{\sigma}}_{e}^{k,m}-\alpha(\upsilon^{k,m})p^{k,m}{\mathbf{I}}\right]\,\mathrm{d}V-\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{b}}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}_{u}\,\mathrm{d}V-\int_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}\bar{{\mathbf{t}}}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}_{u}\,\mathrm{d}S={\mathbf{0}}.

(68)

The proposed thermo-hydro-mechanical phase-field model has been implemented in an open source code, OpenGeoSys
[
5
]
, and discretization details are provided in
B
.

3.2
Isotropic diffusion stabilization method

For hydraulic fracturing problems, a mass source term imposes a strong advective term in heat transfer, and the standard Galerkin finite element method may generate non-symmetric coefficient matrices, which lead to numerical instabilities and spurious oscillations
[
63
,
24
]
.
To address this issue, Burman and Ern (2002) proposed the isotropic diffusion method
[
13
]
, which adds an artificial isotropic balancing dissipation to the diffusion coefficient and forces the Péclet number to be smaller than 1.
The isotropic balancing dissipation is defined as

\mathbf{K}_{s}=\frac{1}{2}s||{\mathbf{q}}||h_{e}\mathbf{I},

(69)

where
s\in[0,1]
is the tuning parameter and is set to
s=0.15
in this study.

4

No closed-form solution is known to couple all thermo-hydro-mechanical components with fracture propagation.

6
Conclusions

This study proposed the thermo-hydro-mechanical phase-field model based on the micromechanically derived strain energy degradation with a new approach to update the porosity and a modified fixed stress split scheme with the thermal stress.
Furthermore, our results demonstrated the isotropic diffusion method’s effectiveness in stabilizing the advection-dominated heat flux in hydraulic fracture.
Our model was verified against hydro-mechanical and thermo-hydro-mechanical problems and plane-strain hydraulic fracture propagation.
For hydraulic fracture propagation, we compared the results from our newly proposed porosity formulation against the existing model.
We found that the new porosity formulation more accurately represents the sharp fracture behavior than the existing one.

Then, we illustrated the impacts of thermal stress on hydraulic fracture propagation through the examples with a single fracture and its interactions with a pre-existing weak interface under various temperature differences.
The higher the temperature difference, the less the critical pressure and the more fracture growth in both length and width.
Moreover, because of this decrease in the critical pressure, higher temperature differences can suppress the hydro-mechanical attraction of the single fracture to the weak interface.

As a future study, the thermal equilibrium assumption between the fracture and matrix may need to be relaxed as in
[
31
,
32
]
, and some analytical approaches may be implemented to ensure the continuity on the fracture boundary
[
54
,
64
]
.
Furthermore, the model may be extended to account for various working fluids such as supercritical CO
2

[
47
]
or liquid nitrogen
[
35
]
.
These unconventional fracturing fluids will likely go through the phase transition at the crack tip
[
73
]
, which remains to be addressed in phase-field modelling of hydraulic fractures.
