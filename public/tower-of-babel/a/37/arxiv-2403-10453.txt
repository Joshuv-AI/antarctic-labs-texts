Stochastic integration with respect to cylindrical Lévy processes in Hilbert spaces
Gergely Bodó, Markus Riedle

Abstract
In this work, we present a comprehensive theory of stochastic integration with respect to arbitrary cylindrical Lévy processes in Hilbert spaces. Since cylindrical Lévy processes do not enjoy a semi-martingale decomposition, our approach relies on an alternative approach to stochastic integration by decoupled tangent sequences. The space of deterministic integrands is identified as a modular space described in terms of the characteristics of the cylindrical Lévy process. The space of random integrands is described as the space of predictable processes whose trajectories are in the space of deterministic integrands almost surely. The derived space of random integrands is verified as the largest space of potential integrands, based on a classical definition of stochastic integrability. We apply the introduced theory of stochastic integration to establish a dominated convergence theorem.

60H05, 60G20, 60G51, 28C20

Keywords and phrases:

cylindrical Lévy processes, stochastic integration, decoupled tangent sequence, dominated convergence theorem

1
Introduction

Cylindrical Lévy processes serve as a natural generalisation of cylindrical Brownian motion, providing a unified framework for modelling a wide variety of different random perturbations of infinite-dimensional systems. Analogously to cylindrical Brownian motion, these processes generally do not exist as stochastic processes in the usual sense as stochastic processes with values in the underlying infinite-dimensional space, and can only be interpreted in the generalised sense of Gel’fand and Vilenkin
[
7
]
or Segal
[
33
]
.

The first systematic treatment of the concept of cylindrical Lévy processes in Hilbert and Banach spaces was undertaken by Applebaum and Riedle in their work
[
1
]
. Leveraging the theory of cylindrical measures, as outlined by Badrikian and Chevet
[
2
]
and Schwartz
[
32
]
, the authors established a precise mathematical framework for understanding cylindrical Lévy processes. Within this framework, cylindrical Lévy processes has since found applications in modelling random perturbations of partial differential equations such as Bodó et all
[
4
]
and Kosmala and Riedle
[
14
]
. Specific instances of cylindrical Lévy processes as driving noise have also been explored in works by Priola and Zabczyk
[
28
]
and Peszat and Zabczyk
[
26
]
. However, all these applications were made under restrictive assumptions, such as additive noise or specific types of noise, owing to the absence of a fully developed general theory of stochastic integration.

The classical approach to stochastic integration relying on a semi-martingale decomposition cannot be applied to cylindrical Lévy processes since they do not enjoy a semi-martingale decomposition. For this reason, a stochastic integration theory has been developed almost exclusively only for cylindrical martingales. This has been achieved through a Doléans measure approach by Métivier and Pellaumail in
[
20
]
, via the construction of a family of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces by Mikulevičius and Rozovskiǐ in
[
21
]
, or alternatively by introducing a new type of quadratic variation for cylindrical continuous local martingales in UMD Banach spaces in Veraar and Yaroslavtsev
[
37
]
.

In this work, we present a comprehensive theory of stochastic integration for random integrands with respect to arbitrary cylindrical Lévy processes. The robustness of our developed theory is showcased through establishing a typical dominated convergence theorem. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the class of random integrands, described by the characteristics of the integrator, constitutes the largest class of potential integrands, based on the classical definition of stochastic integrability introduced by Urbanic and Woyczynski in
[
34
]
.

Our approach relies on a two-sided inequality for the metric of convergence in probability in Hilbert spaces applied to the sum of a decoupled tangent sequence representing the stochastic integral for simple integrands. This particular approach to stochastic integration in the real-valued case was pioneered in the late 1980s in a couple of publications by Kwapien and Woyczynski and cumulated in their monograph
[
17
]
. In the vector-valued setting, similar ideas were simultaneously pursued for stochastic integration in UMD-Banach spaces by McConnell in
[
19
]
. This work can be viewed as the precursor to the recently developed, comprehensive theory of stochastic integration with respect to cylindrical Brownian motion in UMD spaces by van Neerven, Veraar and Weis. This theory started with their work
[
36
]
and has led to various novel insights into stochastic partial differential equations, including sharp maximal inequalities.

The development of a stochastic integration theory for cylindrical Lévy processes was previously addressed only in the work
Jakubowski and Riedle
[
10
]
. There, the underlying approach was, similarly as here, based on studying the sum of the decoupled tangent sequence but only applied to conclude relatively compactness in the Skorokhod space of the uncoupled sum. This approach led only to an integration theory for stochastic processes that are continuous from the left and have right limits, a restriction often insufficient for practical applications. In particular, this approach could not be extended to include the important case of predictable integrands. Our publication
[
3
]
serves as a precursor to the present work, wherein we tested our methods in the specific case of a canonical
\alpha
-stable cylindrical process as the integrator. This restricted setting significantly simplifies the description of the space of admissible integrands. The current study builds upon this case study, offering a more comprehensive theory of stochastic integration for arbitrary cylindrical Lévy processes, overcoming limitations inherent in previous approaches.

The present work introduces the stochastic integral in two steps: firstly, for deterministic integrands and secondly, for random integrands. The largest space of deterministic integrands is derived in Theorem
4.4
as a modular space described in terms of the characteristics of the cylindrical Lévy process. The largest space of random integrands is derived in Theorem
7.1
, and can be described as the space of predictable processes whose trajectories are in the space of deterministic integrands almost surely.

We briefly summarise our article: in Section 2, some fundamental results on cylindrical Lévy processes and infinitely divisible probability measures in Hilbert spaces are collected. Section
3
introduces the modular space that characterises the space of deterministic integrands, establishing its completeness, metrisability, and linearity. The stochastic integral for deterministic integrands is presented in Section
4
. The role of Itô’s isometry for the classical stochastic integral with respect to a Brownian motion is taken by Lemma
4.5
. Although it does not establish an isometry, it guarantees that a a sequence of deterministic integrands is Cauchy if and only if the corresponding stochastic integrals are Cauchy in the semi-martingale topology. Section
5
sets the foundation for integrating random integrands by introducing necessary definitions and elementary results. The following Section
6
is devoted to the construction of the decoupled tangent sequence. In this section, we also briefly recall relevant definitions and results on decoupled tangent sequences from
[
17
]
. The space of random integrands is derived in Section
7
. Analogous to the deterministic integrands, Corollary
7.3
establishes a relationship between the Cauchyness in the space of random integrands and the corresponding Cauchyness of stochastic integrals.

Preliminaries

2.1
Cylindrical Lévy processes

Let
G
and
H
be separable Hilbert spaces with inner products
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and corresponding norms
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
. Let
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(b_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be orthonormal bases of
G
and
H
, respectively. We identify the dual of a Hilbert space by the space itself. The Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
H
is denoted by
\Borel(H)
and the open unit ball by
B_{H}:=\{h\in H:\left\lVert h\right\rVert<1\}
and the closed unit ball by
\bar{B}_{H}:=\{h\in H:\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq 1\}
.

The Banach space of bounded linear operators from
G
to
H
will be denoted by
L(G,H)
with the operator norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{G\rightarrow H}
. Its subspace
L_{2}(G,H)
of Hilbert-Schmidt operators is endowed with the norm

\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{\rm HS}^{2}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\lVert Fa_{k}\right\rVert^{2}
for
F\in L_{2}(G,H)
.

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,P)
be a complete probability space. We will denote by
L_{P}^{0}(\Omega,H)
the space of equivalence classes of measurable functions
X\colon\Omega\rightarrow H
, equipped with the topology of convergence in probability.

Let
S
be a subset of
G
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, elements
g_{1},...,g_{n}\in S
and Borel sets
A\in\Borel(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we define

C(g_{1},...,g_{n};A):=\{g\in G:(\langle g,g_{1}\rangle,...,\langle g,g_{n}\rangle)\in A\}.

Such sets are called cylindrical sets with respect to
A
. The collection of all these cylindrical sets is denoted by
\mathcal{Z}(G,S)
, and it is a
\sigma
-algebra if
S
is finite and otherwise an algebra. We write

\mathcal{Z}(G)
for
\mathcal{Z}(G,G)

A set function
\mu:\mathcal{Z}(G)\rightarrow[0,\infty]
is called a cylindrical measure on
\mathcal{Z}(G)
if for each finite dimensional subset
S\subseteq G
, the restriction of
\mu
to the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{Z}(G,S)
is a
\sigma
-additive measure. A cylindrical measure is said to be a cylindrical probability measure if
\mu(G)=1
.

A cylindrical random variable
X
in
G
is a linear and continuous mapping
X\colon G\rightarrow L_{P}^{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
. It defines a cylindrical probability measure
\mu_{X}
by

\displaystyle\mu_{X}\colon\mathcal{Z}(G)\to[0,1],\qquad\mu_{X}(Z)=P\big((Xg_{1},\dots,Xg_{n})\in A\big)

for cylindrical sets
Z=C(g_{1},...,g_{n};A)
. The cylindrical probability measure
\mu_{X}
is called the cylindrical distribution of
X
.
We define the characteristic function of the cylindrical random variable
X
by

\varphi_{X}\colon G\rightarrow\mathbb{C},\qquad\varphi_{X}(g)=E\big[e^{iXg}\big].

Let
T\colon G\to H
be a linear and continuous operator. By defining

\displaystyle TX\colon H\to L_{P}^{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}),\qquad(TX)h=X(T^{\ast}h)

we obtain a cylindrical random variable on
H
. In the special case when
T
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator and hence
0
-Radonifying by
[
35
, Th. VI.5.2]
, it follows from
[
35
, Pr. VI.5.3]
that the cylindrical random variable
TX
is induced by a genuine random variable
Y\colon\Omega\to H
, that is

(TX)h=\langle Y,h\rangle
for all
h\in H
. As shown in
[
3
, Le. 2.1]
, the inducing random variable
Y
depends continuously on the Hilbert-Schmidt operator.

A family
(L(t):t\geq 0)
of cylindrical random variables
L(t)\colon G\rightarrow L_{P}^{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
is called a cylindrical Lévy process if for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
g_{1},...,g_{n}\in G
, the stochastic process

\big(\big(L(t)g_{1},...,L(t)g_{n}\big):t\geq 0\big)

is a Lévy process in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The filtration generated by
(L(t):t\geq 0)
is defined by

\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\sigma(\{L(s)g\colon g\in G,s\in[0,t]\})\quad\text{for all}\;t\geq 0.

Denote by
\mathcal{Z}_{*}(G)
the collection

\big\{\{g\in G:(\langle g,g_{1}\rangle,...,\langle g,g_{n}\rangle)\in B\}:n\in\mathbb{N},g_{1},...,g_{n}\in G,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})\big\}

of cylindrical sets, which forms an algebra of subsets of
G
. For fixed
g_{1},...,g_{n}\in G
, let
\lambda_{g_{1},\dots,g_{n}}
be the Lévy measure of
((L(t)g_{1},...,L(t)g_{n}):t\geq 0)
. Define a function

\lambda\colon\mathcal{Z}_{*}(G)\rightarrow[0,\infty]
by

\displaystyle\lambda(C):=\lambda_{g_{1},\dots,g_{n}}(B)\quad\text{for }C=\{g\in G:\,(\langle g,g_{1}\rangle,...,\langle g,g_{n}\rangle)\in B\}\text{ and }B\in\Borel(\R^{n}).

It is shown in
[
1
]
that
\lambda
is well defined. The set function
\lambda
is called the cylindrical Lévy measure of
L
.

The characteristic function of a cylindrical Lévy process
L
in
G
takes for each
t\geq 0
the form

\varphi_{L(t)}:G\rightarrow\mathbb{C},\qquad\varphi_{L(t)}(g)=\exp\left(tS(g)\right),

where the mapping
S:G\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is called the cylindrical symbol of
L
, and satisfies

S(g)=ia(g)-\frac{1}{2}\langle Qg,g\rangle+\int_{G}\left(e^{i\langle g,h\rangle}-1-i\langle g,h\rangle\mathbb{1}_{\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}}}(\langle g,h\rangle)\right)\,\lambda({\rm d}h),

where
a:G\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuous mapping with
a(0)=0
,
Q:G\rightarrow G
is a positive and symmetric operator, and
\lambda
is a cylindrical Lévy measure on
G
. We call the triplet
(a,Q,\lambda)
the cylindrical characteristics of
L
. For this and related results see
[
29
]
.

2.2
Infinitely divisible measures and their characteristics

Infinitely divisible measures on a Hilbert space
H
can be defined as in the Euclidean space; see
[
25
]
.
As in finite dimensions, the characteristic function of any infinitely divisible measure
\mu
on
\Borel(H)
satisfies

\varphi_{\mu}(h)=\exp(S(h))
, where the symbol
S\colon H\to{\mathbb{C}}
is of the form

S(h)=i\langle b^{\kappa},h\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2}\langle Qh,h\rangle+\int_{H}\left(e^{i\langle h,g\rangle}-1-i\langle h,\kappa(g)\rangle\right)\,\lambda(dg),

where
b^{\kappa}\in H
, the mapping
Q\colon H\to H
is nuclear, symmetric and non-negative, the Lévy measure
\lambda
is a
\sigma
-finite measure on
\Borel(H)
satisfying

\int_{H}\big(\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1\big)\,\lambda({\rm d}h)<\infty
and
\kappa\colon H\to H
is a function which is bounded and satisfies
\kappa(h)=h
in a neighbourhood of
0
. Such a function
\kappa
is called a truncation function. The triplet
(b^{\kappa},Q,\lambda)
is called characteristics of
\mu
. For different truncation functions
\kappa
, one obtains the same representation of the symbol
S
but only the term
b^{\kappa}
depends on
\kappa
.
When dealing with limit theorems, for technical reasons, it is often preferable to use a continuous function
\kappa
. A specific example of a continuous truncation function, which will play an important role in the rest of this work, is the truncation function

\theta\colon H\rightarrow H,\qquad\theta(h)=\begin{cases}h&\text{if}\,\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq 1;\\
\frac{h}{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}&\text{if}\,\left\lVert h\right\rVert>1.\end{cases}

Let
\kappa\colon H\to H
be a continuous truncation function.
A sequence of infinitely divisible measures
\mu_{n}=(b_{n}^{\kappa},Q_{n},\lambda_{n})
with associated sequence
(T_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of so-called
S
-operators
T_{n}\colon H\to H
, which are defined by

\displaystyle\langle T_{n}h_{1},h_{2}\rangle=\langle Q_{n}h_{1},h_{2}\rangle+\int_{\left\lVert u\right\rVert\leq 1}\langle h_{1},u\rangle\langle h_{2},u\rangle\,\lambda_{n}({\rm d}u)\qquad\text{for all }h_{1},h_{2}\in H,

converges weakly to an infinitely divisible measure
\mu=(b^{\kappa},Q,\lambda)
if and only if the following conditions hold:

\displaystyle\displaystyle(1)\;b^{\kappa}=\lim_{n\to\infty}b_{n}^{\kappa};

(2.1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle(2)\;\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta}\langle h,u\rangle^{2}\,\lambda_{n}({\rm d}u)+(Q_{n}h,h)=(Qh,h)\text{ for all }h\in H;

(2.2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle(3)\;\lambda_{n}\to\lambda\text{ weakly outside of every closed neighbourhood of the origin;}

(2.3)

\displaystyle(4)\;\text{$(T_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is compact in the space of nuclear operators.}

Let
(\mathcal{I},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{0})

where

3
The modular space

Originally introduced by Nakano
[
22
]
, modular spaces serve as natural generalisations of metric spaces. Prominent and non-trivial examples are Orlicz spaces and generalised Musielak–Orlicz spaces. While numerous different definitions appear in the literature, in this work we will always use the following adaption of Nakano’s original definition of a generalised modular; see
[
23
]
. Our main objective in this section, apart from defining these spaces, is to establish them as complete, metrisable linear spaces and to demonstrate denseness of simple functions. Although various abstract conditions on the modular are known in the literature, guaranteeing one of these properties, we found it easier to establish these properties directly. The metrisability is achieved through recent results on
K
-quasi-metric spaces in
[
5
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
V
be a real vector space. A function
\Delta:V\rightarrow[0,\infty]
is called a modular if

(1)

\Delta(-v)=\Delta(v)
for all
v\in V
;

(2)

\displaystyle\inf_{\alpha>0}\Delta(\alpha v)=0
for all
v\in V
;

(3)

\Delta(\alpha v)\leq\Delta(\beta v)
for all
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta
and
v\in V
;

(4)

there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\Delta(v+w)\leq c\left(\Delta(v)+\Delta(w)\right)\quad\text{for all }v,w\in V.

A function satisfying Condition
{\rm(4)}
of Definition
3.1
is said to be of moderate growth.

As Hilbert-Schmidt operators between Hilbert spaces map cylindrical random variables to genuine random variables, they transform cylindrical Lévy processes to genuine Lévy processes.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(L(t):t\geq 0)
be a cylindrical Lévy process in
G
with cylindrical characteristics
(a,Q,\lambda)
, and let
F\in L_{2}(G,H)
be a Hilbert-Schmidt operator. Then there exists an
H
-valued Lévy process
(F(L)(t):t\geq 0)
satisfying
\langle F(L)(t),h\rangle=L(t)(F^{*}h)
for all
t\in[0,T]
and
h\in H
. Moreover,
F(L)
has characteristics
(b_{F},FQF^{*},\lambda\circ F^{-1})
, where for all
u\in H

\langle b_{F},u\rangle=a(F^{*}u)+\int_{H}\langle h,u\rangle\big(\mathbb{1}_{B_{H}}(h)-\mathbb{1}_{B_{\mathbb{R}}}(\langle h,u\rangle)\big)\,(\lambda\circ F^{-1})({\rm d}h).

Proof.

Existence of the
H
-valued Lévy process
F(L)
follows from
[
9
, Th.A]
. To derive the characteristics, first apply
[
30
, Le. 5.4]
to obtain the cylindrical characteristics of
F(L)
, and then use
[
30
, Le. 5.8]
to convert the cylindrical characteristics into genuine characteristics.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

It follows from the very definitions of

and

that for each fixed

Definition 3.7
.

For a measurable function
\psi:[0,T]\rightarrow L_{2}(G,H)

\displaystyle m_{L}^{\prime}(\psi):=\int_{0}^{T}\big(k_{L}(\psi(t))+l_{L}(\psi(t))\big)\,{\rm d}t;

\displaystyle m^{\prime\prime}(\psi):=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\left\lVert\psi(t)\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}^{2}\wedge 1\right)\,{\rm d}t;

.

Example 3.8
.

A cylindrical Lévy process
L
on
G
is called standard symmetric
\alpha
-stable if its characteristic function is of the form

\varphi_{L(t)}\colon G\to{\mathbb{C}},\qquad\varphi_{L(t)}(g)=\exp(-\left\lVert g\right\rVert^{\alpha}),

for some
\alpha\in(0,2)
, in which case the theory simplifies significantly. In particular, since
L
has cylindrical characteristics
(0,0,\lambda)
, we have that
l_{L}
and the second term of
k_{L}
are always zero. Moreover, the first term of
k_{L}
can be equivalently controlled by an

[
3

,
[
14

and
[
4
, Le. 2.1.]
, there exist constants
c_{\alpha},d_{\alpha}>0
such that for all measurable functions
\psi:[0,T]\rightarrow L_{2}(G,H)
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c_{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\psi(t)\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}^{\alpha}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{H}\big(\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1\big)\,(\lambda\circ\psi(t)^{-1})({\rm d}h)\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq d_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\psi(t)\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}^{\alpha}\,{\rm d}t.

Consequently, we obtain that
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}=L_{\rm Leb}^{\alpha}([0,T],L_{2}(G,H))
.

\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}

. One of the preliminary result, Lemma

As a first step towards this direction, the next lemma provides us with an alternative representation of

Lemma 3.9

Let

with characteristics
(a,Q,\lambda)

and

The term

must coincide with the corresponding term in the characteristics of the Lévy process which we obtain as the image of the Lévy process

under the map

.
∎

Remark 3.10
.

It follows from Lemma
3.9
that
l_{L}(F)
is finite for each
F\in L_{2}(G,H)
. To see this, we first note that for all
h\in\bar{B}_{H}
and
O\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}
we have
\left\lVert\theta(Oh)-O\theta(h)\right\rVert=0
, and for all
h\in H
and
O\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}
it holds that
\left\lVert\theta(Oh)-O\theta(h)\right\rVert\leq 2
. By combining these observations with Lemma
3.9
we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{O\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}}\left\lVert b_{OF}^{\theta}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert b_{F}^{\theta}\right\rVert+2(\lambda\circ F^{-1})(\bar{B}_{H}^{c})<\infty.

Before we could prove that our modular
m_{L}
is well-defined, we need to establish a relationship between weak convergence of infinitely divisible measures and convergence of the corresponding characteristics in the following sense:

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\mu_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{=}}(b_{n}^{\theta},Q_{n},\lambda_{n})
be a sequence of infinitely divisible measures on
\Borel(H)
converging weakly to
\mu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{=}}(b^{\theta},Q,\lambda)
. Then the following conditions hold:

(1)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\int_{H}\left(\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1\right)\;\lambda_{n}\,({\rm d}h)+{\rm Tr}(Q_{n})\right)=\int_{H}\left(\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1\right)\;\lambda\,({\rm d}h)+{\rm Tr}(Q)
;

(2)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert b_{n}^{\theta}-b^{\theta}\right\rVert=0
.

Proof.

The fact that
({\rm 2})
holds follows directly from Equation (
2.1
). To prove
({\rm 1})
, fix
\delta\in(0,1]
such that
\delta\in C(\lambda)
. By Equation (
2.3
) we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\left\lVert h\right\rVert>\delta}\Big(\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1\Big)\;\lambda_{n}({\rm d}h)=\int_{\left\lVert h\right\rVert>\delta}\Big(\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1\Big)\;\lambda({\rm d}h).

Therefore, it remains only to deal with the limit of the integrals over
\bar{B}_{H}(\delta)
. Let
\epsilon>0
be fixed. It follows from properties of the Lebesgue integral that there exists a
\delta_{1}\in(0,\delta]
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta_{1}}\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\;\lambda({\rm d}h)<\frac{\epsilon}{12}.

(3.2)

Let
\{e_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be an orthonormal basis of
H
. Since
Q
is a trace class operator, there exists
K_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\sum_{k={K_{1}+1}}^{\infty}\langle Qe_{k},e_{k}\rangle<\frac{\epsilon}{12}.

By compactness of the associated
S
-operators, see Condition (
2.4
), there exists
K_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

\sum_{k={K_{2}+1}}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta}\langle e_{k},h\rangle^{2}\;\lambda_{n}({\rm d}h)+\langle Q_{n}e_{k},e_{k}\rangle\right)<\frac{\epsilon}{4}.

(3.3)

Moreover, by an application of Condition (
2.2
), there exists a
\delta_{2}<\delta_{1}
and
N_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq N_{1}
and for all
k\leq K_{2}
we have that

\left|\int_{\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta_{2}}\langle e_{k},h\rangle^{2}\;\lambda_{n}({\rm d}h)+\langle Q_{n}e_{k},e_{k}\rangle-\langle Qe_{k},e_{k}\rangle\right|<\frac{\epsilon}{12K},

(3.4)

where
K:=\max\{K_{1},K_{2}\}
. Condition (
2.3
) guarantees that there exists
N_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq N_{2}
we have

\displaystyle\Bigg|\int_{\delta_{2}<\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta}\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\;\lambda_{n}({\rm d}h)-\int_{\delta_{2}<\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta}\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\;\lambda({\rm d}h)\Bigg|<\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

(3.5)

By splitting the integration domain, we obtain

\displaystyle\Bigg|

\displaystyle\int_{\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta}\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\;\lambda({\rm d}h)+{\rm Tr}(Q)-\int_{\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta}\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\;\lambda_{n}({\rm d}h)-{\rm Tr}(Q_{n})\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\Bigg|\int_{\delta_{2}<\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta}\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\;\lambda({\rm d}h)-\int_{\delta_{2}<\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta}\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\;\lambda_{n}({\rm d}h)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\qquad+\Bigg|\int_{\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta_{2}}\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\;\lambda({\rm d}h)+{\rm Tr}(Q)-\int_{\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta_{2}}\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\;\lambda_{n}({\rm d}h)-{\rm Tr}(Q_{n})\Bigg|.

(3.6)

By Parseval’s identity, Equations (
3.2
)-(
3.4
) and a repeated application of the triangle inequality, we obtain for all
n\geq N:=\max\{N_{1},N_{2}\}
that

\displaystyle\Bigg|\int_{\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta_{2}}\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\;\lambda({\rm d}h)+{\rm Tr}(Q)-\int_{\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta_{2}}\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\;\lambda_{n}({\rm d}h)-{\rm Tr}(Q_{n})\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Bigg|{\rm Tr}(Q)-\int_{\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta_{2}}\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\;\lambda_{n}({\rm d}h)-{\rm Tr}(Q_{n})\Bigg|+\left|\int_{\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta_{2}}\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\;\lambda({\rm d}h)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\langle Qe_{k},e_{k}\rangle-\int_{\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta_{2}}\langle h,e_{k}\rangle^{2}\;\lambda_{n}({\rm d}h)-\langle Q_{n}e_{k},e_{k}\rangle\right)\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}\left(\langle Qe_{k},e_{k}\rangle-\int_{\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta_{2}}\langle h,e_{k}\rangle^{2}\;\lambda_{n}({\rm d}h)-\langle Q_{n}e_{k},e_{k}\rangle\right)\right|+\left|\int_{\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta_{2}}\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\;\lambda({\rm d}h)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

(3.7)

Hence, if
n\geq N
then Equations (
3.5
)-(
3.7
) together imply

\Bigg|\int_{\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta}\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\;\lambda({\rm d}h)+{\rm Tr}(Q)-\int_{\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\delta}\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\;\lambda_{n}({\rm d}h)-{\rm Tr}(Q_{n})\Bigg|<\epsilon.

Since
\epsilon>0
was arbitrary, the result follows.
∎

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
k_{L},l_{L}:L_{2}(G,H)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be as in Definition
3.5
. Then we have:

(1)

k_{L}
is continuous;

(2)

l_{L}
is lower-semicontinuous and continuous at
0
.

Proof.

Continuity of
k_{L}
follows immediately from
[
3
, Le. 2.1]
and Lemma
3.11
. To prove that
l_{L}
is lower-semicontinuous, we fix
F\in L_{2}(G,H)
and a sequence
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq L_{2}(G,H)
satisfying
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert F_{n}-F\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}=0
. Let
\epsilon>0
be fixed. It follows from Remark
3.10
and the very definition of the supremum, that there exists
O_{\epsilon}\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}
such that
\sup_{O\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}}\left\lVert b_{OF}^{\theta}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert b_{O_{\epsilon}F}^{\theta}\right\rVert+\epsilon
. Since
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert b_{O_{\epsilon}F_{n}}^{\theta}\right\rVert=\left\lVert b_{O_{\epsilon}F}^{\theta}\right\rVert
by
[
3
, Le. 2.1]
and Equation (
2.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{O\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}}\left\lVert b_{OF}^{\theta}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert b_{O_{\epsilon}F}^{\theta}\right\rVert+\epsilon

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert b_{O_{\epsilon}F_{n}}^{\theta}\right\rVert+\epsilon\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{O\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}}\left\lVert b_{OF_{n}}^{\theta}\right\rVert+\epsilon.

As
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary, the above shows
l_{L}(F)\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}l_{L}(F_{n})

which proves lower-semicontinuity of
l_{L}
.

To show continuity of
l_{L}
at
0
, note that by
[
3
, Le. 2.1]
and Remark
2.1
, for all
\epsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that
\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}\leq\delta
implies
\left\lVert b_{F}^{\theta}\right\rVert\leq\epsilon
. Since
\left\lVert OF\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}\leq\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}
for all

, we conclude that

\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}\leq\delta
implies
\sup_{O\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}}\left\lVert b_{OF}^{\theta}\right\rVert\leq\epsilon
,
which concludes the proof.
∎

In preparation for showing that
m_{L}
is of moderate growth, see Definition

/(4), we prove the following technical lemmata.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be an orthonormal basis of
G
and let
P_{n}\colon G\rightarrow G
be the projection onto
\text{Span}\{e_{1},...,e_{n}\}
.
Then we have for all
F\in L_{2}(G,H)
that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert FP_{n}-F\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}=0.

Proof.

Since
P_{n}e_{i}=e_{i}
for
i\leq n
, and
P_{n}e_{i}=0
for
i>n
, we have

\|FP_{n}-F\|_{L_{2}(G,H)}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\|(FP_{n}-F)e_{i}\|_{H}^{2}=\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\|Fe_{i}\|_{H}^{2}\to 0\text{ as }n\to\infty,

by the Hilbert-Schmidt property of
F
.
∎

Lemma 3.14
.

For all
F,F_{1},F_{2}\in L_{2}(G,H)
we have

(1)

k_{L}(F_{1}+F_{2})\leq 2\left(k_{L}(F_{1})+k_{L}(F_{2})\right)
;

(2)

\displaystyle\sup_{O\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}}k_{L}(OF)\leq k_{L}(F)
.

Proof.

Let
P_{n}\colon G\rightarrow G
denote the projections from Lemma
3.13
. Using the inequality

(a+b)^{2}\wedge 1\leq 2\left[(a^{2}\wedge 1)+(b^{2}\wedge 1)\right]\;\text{for all}\;a,b\in\mathbb{R},

(3.8)

we observe for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
that

\displaystyle\int_{H}\left(\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1\right)\;\left(\lambda\circ\left((F_{1}+F_{2})P_{n}\right)^{-1}\right)({\rm d}h)

(3.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{G}\left(\left\lVert(F_{1}+F_{2})g\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1\right)\;\left(\lambda\circ P_{n}^{-1}\right)({\rm d}g)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\left(\int_{G}\left(\left\lVert F_{1}g\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1\right)\;\left(\lambda\circ P_{n}^{-1}\right)({\rm d}g)+\int_{G}\left(\left\lVert F_{2}g\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1\right)\;\left(\lambda\circ P_{n}^{-1}\right)({\rm d}g)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left(\int_{H}\left(\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1\right)\;\left(\lambda\circ\left(F_{1}P_{n}\right)^{-1}\right)({\rm d}h)+\int_{H}\left(\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1\right)\;\left(\lambda\circ\left(F_{2}P_{n}\right)^{-1}\right)({\rm d}h)\right).

Moreover, by symmetry and positivity of
Q
, and the very definition of the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product
\langle A,B\rangle_{L_{2}(G,H)}={\rm Tr}(AB^{*})
we obtain

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\left(((F_{1}+F_{2})P_{n})Q((F_{1}+F_{2})P_{n})^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\left\lVert(F_{1}+F_{2})P_{n}Q^{1/2}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\,2\left(\left\lVert F_{1}P_{n}Q^{1/2}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}^{2}+\left\lVert F_{2}P_{n}Q^{1/2}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\,2\left({\rm Tr}((F_{1}P_{n})Q(F_{1}P_{n})^{*})+{\rm Tr}((F_{2}P_{n})Q(F_{2}P_{n})^{*})\right).

(3.10)

By adding the Inequalities in (
3.9
) and (
3
) we get

k_{L}((F_{1}+F_{2})P_{n})\leq 2\left(k_{L}(F_{1}P_{n})+k_{L}(F_{2}P_{n})\right).

By taking limits on both sides, and using continuity of
k_{L}
, see Lemma
3.12
/(1), the first part of this Lemma is proved.

To prove the second part, we fix
F\in L_{2}(G,H)
and obtain for all
O\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
that

\displaystyle\int_{H}\left(\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1\right)\;\left(\lambda\circ\left(OFP_{n}\right)^{-1}\right)({\rm d}h)

\displaystyle=\int_{G}\left(\left\lVert(OF)g\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1\right)\;\left(\lambda\circ P_{n}^{-1}\right)({\rm d}g)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{G}\left(\left\lVert Fg\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1\right)\;\left(\lambda\circ P_{n}^{-1}\right)({\rm d}g)

\displaystyle=\int_{H}\left(\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1\right)\;\left(\lambda\circ\left(FP_{n}\right)^{-1}\right)({\rm d}h).

(3.11)

Moreover, using the relationship between the Hilbert-Schmidt norm and the trace operator, we obtain for all
O\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
that

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}((OFP_{n})Q(OFP_{n})^{*})

\displaystyle=\left\lVert OFP_{n}Q^{1/2}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert FP_{n}Q^{1/2}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}^{2}={\rm Tr}((FP_{n})Q(FP_{n})^{*}).

(3.12)

By adding Inequalities (
3
) and (
3
), and taking limits on both sides, the result follows from Lemmata
3.11
and
3.13
.
∎

Lemma 3.15
.

For all
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
we have

m_{L}(\psi_{1}+\psi_{2})\leq 4\left(m_{L}\left(\psi_{1}\right)+m_{L}\left(\psi_{2}\right)\right).

Proof.

Let
F_{1},F_{2}\in L_{2}(G,H)
and
(\pi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a nested normal sequence of partitions
\pi_{n}=(t_{i,n})_{i=1,\dots,N(n)}
of the interval
[0,1]
. In order to simplify the notation, we define for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in\left\{1,...,N(n)\right\}

A_{i,n}:=F_{1}(L)(t_{i,n})-F_{1}(L)(t_{i-1,n})\ \text{and}\ B_{i,n}:=F_{2}(L)(t_{i,n})-F_{2}(L)(t_{i-1,n}).

Since
(F_{1}+F_{2})(L)=F_{1}(L)+F_{2}(L)
, Theorem
2.2
implies

\displaystyle\left\lVert b_{F_{1}+F_{2}}^{\theta}\right\rVert

(3.13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}E\left[\theta\left((F_{1}+F_{2})(L)(t_{i,n})-(F_{1}+F_{2})(L)(t_{i-1,n})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}E\left[\theta(A_{i,n}+B_{i,n})\right]\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}E\left[\theta(A_{i,n}+B_{i,n})-\theta(A_{i,n})-\theta(B_{i,n})\right]\right\rVert+\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}E\left[\theta(A_{i,n})+\theta(B_{i,n})\right]\right\rVert\right).

Applying the inequality

\left\lVert\theta(h_{1}+h_{2})-\theta(h_{1})-\theta(h_{2})\right\rVert\leq 2\left(\theta(\left\lVert h_{1}\right\rVert)^{2}+\theta(\left\lVert h_{2}\right\rVert)^{2}\right)\quad\text{for all}\;h_{1},h_{2}\in H,

let us conclude that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}E\left[\theta(A_{i,n}+B_{i,n})-\theta(A_{i,n})-\theta(B_{i,n})\right]\right\rVert

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 2\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}E\left[\theta(\left\lVert A_{i,n}\right\rVert)^{2}\right]+2\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}E\left[\theta(\left\lVert B_{i,n}\right\rVert)^{2}\right].

(3.14)

Inequality (
3.13
) together with the triangle inequality imply
(
3.13
) that

\displaystyle\left\lVert b_{F_{1}+F_{2}}^{\theta}\right\rVert\leq

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Bigg(2\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}E\left[\theta(\left\lVert A_{i,n}\right\rVert)^{2}\right]+2\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}E\left[\theta(\left\lVert B_{i,n}\right\rVert)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}E\left[\theta(A_{i,n})\right]\right\rVert+\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}E\left[\theta(B_{i,n})\right]\right\rVert\Bigg).

By taking the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
and using the limit characterisation of Lévy characteristics from Theorem
2.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert b_{F_{1}+F_{2}}^{\theta}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\;2\left(\int_{H}\left(\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1\right)\left(\lambda\circ F_{1}^{-1}\right)({\rm d}h)+{\rm Tr}(F_{1}QF_{1}^{*})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\;2\left(\int_{H}\left(\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1\right)\left(\lambda\circ F_{2}^{-1}\right)({\rm d}h)+{\rm Tr}(F_{2}QF_{2}^{*})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left\lVert b_{F_{1}}^{\theta}\right\rVert+\left\lVert b_{F_{2}}^{\theta}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=2\left(k_{L}(F_{1})+k_{L}(F_{2})\right)+\left\lVert b_{F_{1}}^{\theta}\right\rVert+\left\lVert b_{F_{2}}^{\theta}\right\rVert.

Taking supremum and using Lemma
3.14
/(2) imply

\displaystyle l_{L}(F_{1}+F_{2})

\displaystyle=\sup_{O\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}}\left\lVert b_{O(F_{1}+F_{2})}^{\theta}\right\rVert\leq 2\left(k_{L}(F_{1})+k_{L}(F_{2})\right)+l_{L}(F_{1})+l_{L}(F_{2}),

which let us conclude from Lemma
3.14
/(1) that

\displaystyle k_{L}(F_{1}+F_{2})+l_{L}(F_{1}+F_{2})\leq 4\left(k_{L}(F_{1})+k_{L}(F_{2})+l_{L}(F_{1})+l_{L}(F_{2})\right).

(3.15)

Inequality (
3.8
) implies for all measurable functions
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
that

\displaystyle m_{L}(\psi_{1}+\psi_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}k_{L}\big(\psi_{1}(t)+\psi_{2}(t)\big)+l_{L}\big(\psi_{1}(t)+\psi_{2}(t)\big)\,{\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\left(\left\lVert\psi_{1}(t)+\psi_{2}(t)\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}^{2}\wedge 1\right)\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq 4\left(\int_{0}^{T}k_{L}(\psi_{1}(t)+l_{L}(\psi_{1}(t)\,{\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{T}k_{L}(\psi_{2}(t)+l_{L}(\psi_{2}(t)\,{\rm d}t\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+2\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left(\left\lVert\psi_{1}(t)\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}^{2}\wedge 1\right)\,{\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\left(\left\lVert\psi_{2}(t)\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}^{2}\wedge 1\right)\,{\rm d}t\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4\left(m_{L}(\psi_{1})+m_{L}(\psi_{2})\right),

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.16
.

For all
r>0
there exists
c_{r}>0
such that

\sup_{\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}\leq r}\left(k_{L}(F)+l_{L}(F)\right)\leq c_{r}.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.12
,
k_{L}+l_{L}
is continuous at
0
, from which it follows that there exists a
\delta>0
such that
\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}\leq\delta
implies
(k_{L}+l_{L})(F)\leq 1
. Let
r>0
be fixed. If we choose
N_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
to be large enough so that
\frac{r}{N_{r}}\leq\delta
, then by a repeated use of Equation (
3.15
), we obtain for some
c_{r}>0
that

\displaystyle\sup_{\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}\leq r}(k_{L}+l_{L})(F)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}\leq r}(k_{L}+l_{L})\left(N_{r}\frac{F}{N_{r}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq c_{r}\sup_{\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}\leq r}(k_{L}+l_{L})\left(\frac{F}{N_{r}}\right)\leq c_{r},

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.17
.

Lemma
3.16
guarantees that every bounded functions
\psi:[0,T]\rightarrow L_{2}(G,H)
is in
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
. Indeed, if
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert\psi(t)\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}\leq r
for some
r>0
, then Lemma
3.16
implies that there exists
c_{r}>0
such that
\sup_{\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}\leq r}\left(k_{L}(F)+l_{L}(F)\right)\leq c_{r}
. Hence we obtain

m_{L}^{\prime}(\psi):=\int_{0}^{T}\Big(k_{L}(\psi(t))+l_{L}(\psi(t))\Big)\,{\rm d}t\leq T\,c_{r}<\infty.

Since obviously
m^{\prime\prime}(\psi)<\infty
, it follows
m_{L}(\psi)<\infty
.

Having developed all the technical tools, we now present the main result of this section:

Theorem 3.18
.

\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
is a linear space and
m_{L}
is a modular on
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
.

Proof.

Lemma
3.15
shows for
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
that

m_{L}(\psi_{1}+\psi_{2})\leq 4\left(m_{L}(\psi_{1})+m_{L}(\psi_{2})\right)<\infty,

which implies that
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
is closed under addition. A similar argument as in Lemma
3.16
shows that
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
is closed under multiplication by scalars, which completes the proof that
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
is a vector space. Hence, it remains only to show that
m_{L}
satisfies the conditions of Definition
3.1
. It follows directly from the definition of
m_{L}
that
m_{L}(-\psi)=m_{L}(\psi)
for all
\psi\in\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
. Condition
{\rm(2)}
of Definition
3.1
is a consequence of Lemma
3.12
, Remark
3.6
and Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem. Condition
{\rm(3)}
of Definition
3.1
follows from an argument similar to Lemma
3.14
/(2) and the very definition of

. Finally, Condition
{\rm(4)}
of Definition

is a direct consequence of Lemma
3.15
.
∎

Remark 3.19
.

A sequence
(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
is said to converge to some
\psi\in\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
in the modular
m_{L}
if we have
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}m_{L}(\psi_{n}-\psi)=0
. Since
m_{L}(\psi)=0
if and only if
\psi(t)=0
for Lebesgue almost all
t\in[0,T]
, we have that limits of sequences in the modular are Lebesgue a.e. uniquely determined. For this and further properties of modular convergence, see Section 2 of Nakano
[
23
]
.

Later on, we will be interested in the space
L_{P}^{0}(\Omega,\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS})
of
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
-valued random elements, which we verify as a Polish space in the sequel.

Lemma 3.20
.

The modular space
(\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS},m_{L})
is complete, that is, each modular Cauchy sequence on
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
is modular convergent.

Proof.

Let
(\psi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
be such that
\lim_{i,j\rightarrow\infty}m_{L}(\psi_{i}-\psi_{j})=0
. Then, for all
\epsilon\in(0,1)
we have by Markov’s inequality that

\displaystyle\lim_{i,j\rightarrow\infty}{\rm Leb}\left(t\in[0,T]:\left\lVert\psi_{i}(t)-\psi_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}>\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{i,j\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\left\lVert\psi_{i}(t)-\psi_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}^{2}\wedge 1\right)\,{\rm d}t\leq\lim_{i,j\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}m_{L}(\psi_{i}-\psi_{j})=0,

which implies that the sequence
(\psi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is Cauchy in Lebesgue measure. Hence, there exists a subsequence
(\psi_{i_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converging Lebesgue almost everywhere to a measurable function
\psi:[0,T]\rightarrow L_{2}(G,H)
.

Let
\epsilon>0
be fixed. By assumption, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
i,j\geq N
we have
m_{L}(\psi_{i}-\psi_{j})<\epsilon/2
. Since by Lemma
3.12
,
k_{L}
is continuous and
l_{L}
is lower-semicontinuous, Fatou’s lemma implies for all
i\geq N
that

\displaystyle m_{L}^{\prime}(\psi_{i}-\psi)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}(k_{L}+l_{L})(\psi_{i}(t)-\psi(t))\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}(k_{L}+l_{L})(\psi_{i}(t)-\psi_{i_{n}}(t))\,{\rm d}t

(3.16)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}(k_{L}+l_{L})(\psi_{i}(t)-\psi_{i_{n}}(t))\,{\rm d}t\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}m_{L}(\psi_{i}-\psi_{i_{n}})<\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

Since
(\psi_{i_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges Lebesgue a.e. to
\psi
, using the dominated convergence theorem we obtain

\displaystyle m^{\prime\prime}(\psi_{i}-\psi)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\left\lVert\psi_{i}(t)-\psi(t)\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}^{2}\wedge 1\right)\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\left\lVert\psi_{i}(t)-\psi_{i_{n}}(t)\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}^{2}\wedge 1\right)\,{\rm d}t

(3.17)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\left\lVert\psi_{i}(t)-\psi_{i_{n}}(t)\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}^{2}\wedge 1\right)\,{\rm d}t\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}m_{L}(\psi_{i}-\psi_{i_{n}})<\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

Equations (
3
) and (
3
) establish that
\psi_{i}
converge to
\psi
in the modular topology.
Finally, to see that
\psi\in\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
, fix
i_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
m_{L}(\psi_{i_{0}}-\psi)\leq 1
.
It follows that

\displaystyle m_{L}(\psi)\leq 4\left(m_{L}(\psi-\psi_{i_{0}})+m_{L}(\psi_{i_{0}})\right)\leq 4\left(1+m_{L}(\psi_{i_{0}})\right)<\infty.

which concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.21
.

Note that Lemma
3.20
explains the role of
m^{\prime\prime}
in the modular
m_{L}
. In particular,
m^{\prime\prime}
is needed to establish completeness of the modular space
(\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS},m_{L})
by allowing the identification of a potential
m_{L}
-limit of an
m_{L}
-Cauchy sequence.

Our next goal is to establish that step functions are dense in the modular space
(\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS},m_{L})
. In particular, this will immediately yield that the modular space is separable.

Lemma 3.22
.

The collection of Hilbert-Schmidt operator-valued step functions of the form

\displaystyle\psi\colon[0,T]\rightarrow L_{2}(G,H),\qquad\psi(t)=F_{0}\mathbb{1}_{\{0\}}(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}F_{i}\mathbb{1}_{(t_{i},t_{i+1}]}(t),

where
0=t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}=T
,
F_{i}\in L_{2}(G,H)
for each
i\in\{0,...,n-1\}
, is dense in
(\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS},m_{L})
. Moreover, the modular space
(\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS},m_{L})
is separable.

Proof.

First, it follows from Remark
3.17
that each step function of the above form is an element of the modular space
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
. To prove the claimed result, we first assume that
\psi\in\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
is bounded, that is, there exists a constant
r>0
such that
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert\psi(t)\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}\leq r
. By
[
8
, Le. 1.2.19]
, there exists a sequence
(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of step functions satisfying:

(1)

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert\psi_{n}(t)\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}\leq r
;

(2)

(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
\psi
Lebesgue a.e.

Since
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t)\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}\leq 2r
,
Lemma
3.16
guarantees that there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}(k_{L}+l_{L})(\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t))\leq c.

(3.18)

Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem and Lemma
3.12
imply that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}(k_{L}+l_{L})(\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t))\,{\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(k_{L}+l_{L})(\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t))\,{\rm d}t=0.

Applying Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem to
m^{\prime\prime}(\psi_{n}-\psi)
shows that the step functions
\psi_{n}
converge to
\psi
in the modular
m_{L}
.

In the general case of an arbitrary
\psi
in
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
, we define a sequence of functions

\psi_{n}:[0,T]\rightarrow L_{2}(G,H),\qquad\psi_{n}(t)=\begin{cases}\psi(t)&\text{if }\left\lVert\psi(t)\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}\leq n,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

It follows from the very definition of
\psi_{n}
that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in[0,T]
we have

(k_{L}+l_{L})(\psi_{n+1}(t)-\psi(t))\leq(k_{L}+l_{L})(\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t))\leq(k_{L}+l_{L})(\psi(t)).

Since
m_{L}(\psi)<\infty
we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}(k_{L}+l_{L})(\psi_{1}(t)-\psi(t))\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}(k_{L}+l_{L})(\psi(t))\,{\rm d}t\leq m_{L}(\psi)<\infty.

The monotone convergence theorem implies

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}(k_{L}+l_{L})(\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t))\,{\rm d}t=0.

(3.19)

Since Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem shows
m^{\prime\prime}(\psi_{n}-\psi)\to 0
,
we obtain
m_{L}(\psi_{n}-\psi)\to 0
. By the first part of this lemma, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a sequence
(\psi_{n,i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of step functions converging to
\psi_{n}
in the modular
m_{L}
as
i\to\infty
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we can choose
i_{n}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
m_{L}(\psi_{n}-\psi_{n,i_{n}})<\frac{1}{n}
. It follows from Lemma
3.15
that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}m_{L}(\psi-\psi_{n,i_{n}})\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}4\left(m_{L}(\psi-\psi_{n})+m_{L}(\psi_{n}-\psi_{n,i_{n}})\right)=0.

Since one might require that the approximating sequence of step functions are defined on rational partitions of the time domain and, by separability of

, only take values in a countable dense subset of

, separability of
(\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS},m_{L})
follows.
∎

4
Stochastic integrals with deterministic integrands

The definition of the stochastic integral for deterministic integrands with respect to a cylindrical Lévy process
L
depends heavily on two classes of step functions. We give a precise definition of what is meant by a step function in the following.

Definition 4.1
.

(1)

An
L_{2}(G,H)
-valued step function is of the form

\displaystyle\psi\colon[0,T]\rightarrow L_{2}(G,H),\qquad\psi(t)=F_{0}\mathbb{1}_{\{0\}}(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}F_{i}\mathbb{1}_{(t_{i},t_{i+1}]}(t),

(4.1)

where
0=t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}=T
,
F_{i}\in L_{2}(G,H)
for each
i\in\{0,...,n-1\}
. The space of
L_{2}(G,H)
-valued step functions is denoted by
\mathcal{S}_{\rm det}^{\rm HS}:=\mathcal{S}_{\rm det}^{\rm HS}(G,H)
.

(2)

An
L(H)
-valued step function is of the form

\displaystyle\gamma\colon[0,T]\rightarrow L(H),\qquad\gamma(t)=F_{0}\mathbb{1}_{\{0\}}(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}F_{i}\mathbb{1}_{(t_{i},t_{i+1}]}(t),

(4.2)

where
0=t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}=T
and
F_{i}\in L(H)
for each
i\in\{0,...,n-1\}
. The space of
L(H)
-valued step functions with
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert\gamma(t)\right\rVert_{H\rightarrow H}\leq 1
is denoted by
\mathcal{S}^{\rm 1,op}_{\rm det}:=\mathcal{S}^{\rm 1,op}_{\rm det}(H,H)
.

Let
L(t_{i+1})-L(t_{i})
be an increment of the cylindrical Lévy process
L
and assume that
F_{i}\in L_{2}(G,H)
for each
i\in\{1,...,n-1\}
. Since Hilbert-Schmidt operators are
0
-Radonifying by
[
35
, Th. VI.5.2]
, it follows from
[
35
, Pr. VI.5.3]
that there exist genuine random variables
F_{i}\big(L(t_{i+1})-L(t_{i})\big)\colon\Omega\to H
for each
i\in\{1,...,n-1\}
satisfying

(L(t_{i+1})-L(t_{i}))(F_{i}^{*}h)=\langle F_{i}(L(t_{i+1})-L(t_{i})),h\rangle\quad\text{$P$-a.s.\ for all }h\in H.

We call the random variables
F_{i}\big(L(t_{i+1})-L(t_{i})\big)
Radonified increments for each
i\in\{1,...,n-1\}
.
The stochastic integral is defined for any
\psi\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm det}^{\rm HS}
with representation (
4.1
) as the sum of the Radonified increments

I(\psi):=\int_{0}^{T}\psi\,{\mathrm{d}}L=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}F_{i}(L(t_{i+1})-L(t_{i})).

Thus, the integral
I(\psi):\Omega\rightarrow H
is a genuine
H
-valued random variable.

The following definition of the stochastic integral can be traced back to the theory of vector measures, and was adapted to the probabilistic setting in
[
34
]
by Urbanik and Woyczyński.

Definition 4.2
.

A function
\psi\colon[0,T]\rightarrow L_{2}(G,H)
is
L
-integrable for a given cylindrical Lévy process
L
on
G

if there exists a sequence
(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of elements of
\mathcal{S}_{\rm det}^{\rm HS}
satisfying

(1)

(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
\psi
Lebesgue a.e.;

(2)

\displaystyle\lim_{m,n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{S}^{\rm 1,op}_{\rm det}}E\Bigg[\left\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\gamma(\psi_{m}-\psi_{n})\,{\mathrm{d}}L\right\rVert\wedge 1\Bigg]=0.

In this case, the stochastic integral of the deterministic function
\psi
is defined by

I(\psi):=\int_{0}^{T}\psi\,{\mathrm{d}}L:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\psi_{n}\,{\mathrm{d}}L\quad\text{in}\;L_{P}^{0}(\Omega,H).

The class of all deterministic
L
-integrable Hilbert-Schmidt operator-valued functions is denoted by
\mathcal{I}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}:=\mathcal{I}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}(G,H)
.

Remark 4.3
.

If Conditions (
1
) and (
2
) in Definition
4.2
are satisfied, then completeness of
L_{P}^{0}(\Omega,H)
implies the existence of the limit. Furthermore, it follows that the integral process
(\int_{0}^{t}\psi\,{\mathrm{d}}L)_{t\geq 0}
, defined by
\int_{0}^{t}\psi\,{\mathrm{d}}L:=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,t]}\psi\,{\mathrm{d}}L
has cádlág paths. To see this, note that for each
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
the process
(\int_{0}^{t}\left(\psi_{m}-\psi_{n}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}L)_{t\geq 0}
has cádlág paths. By an extension of
[
17
, Pr. 8.2.1]
to
H
-valued processes and Condition (
2
) above, we obtain

\lim_{m,n\rightarrow\infty}P\Bigg(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left\lVert\int_{0}^{t}\left(\psi_{m}-\psi_{n}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}L\right\rVert>\epsilon\Bigg)\\
\leq 3\lim_{m,n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}P\Bigg(\left\lVert\left(\int_{0}^{t}\psi_{m}-\psi_{n}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}L\right\rVert>\frac{\epsilon}{3}\Bigg)=0.

By passing on to a suitable subsequence if necessary, we obtain that there exists a subsequence
(\int_{0}^{\cdot}\psi_{n_{k}}\,{\mathrm{d}}L)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
that converges uniformly almost surely, which guarantees that the limiting process has cádlág paths.

The following is the main result of this section identifying the largest space of
L
-integrable Hilbert-Schmidt operator-valued functions with the modular space
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
.

Theorem 4.4
.

The space
\mathcal{I}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
of deterministic functions integrable with respect to the cylindrical Lévy process

4.7
implies

\displaystyle E\left[\left\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\gamma_{0}\psi_{n_{0}}\,dL\right\rVert\wedge 1\right]

\displaystyle=E\left[\left\lVert\sum_{i=0}^{N(n_{0})-1}O_{i,n_{0}}F_{i,n_{0}}\left(L(t_{i+1,n_{0}})-L(t_{i,n_{0}})\right)\right\rVert\wedge 1\right]<\epsilon.

This concludes the proof of the implication.
∎

The proof of the reverse implication
(\rm b)\Rightarrow(\rm a)
in Lemma
4.5
relies on two technical lemmata. In the next proof, we follow closely the argument in
[
24
, Le. 3.9]
.

Lemma 4.8
.

If
\psi\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm det}^{\rm HS}
then

\int_{0}^{T}\sup_{O\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}}\left\lVert b_{O\psi(t)}^{\theta}\right\rVert\,{\rm d}t=\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm det}^{\rm 1,op}}\left\lVert\int_{0}^{T}b_{\gamma\psi(t)}^{\theta}\,{\rm d}t\right\rVert,

where
\mathcal{S}_{\rm det}^{\rm 1,op}
was defined in Definition
4.1
/(2).

Proof.

Fix an element
e\in H
such that
\left\lVert e\right\rVert=1
. If
h\in H
is linearly independent of
e
, we define
A_{h}:={\rm Span}\{e,h\}
. For each
h\in H
we define the mapping
f(h):H\rightarrow\bar{B}_{L(H)}
by

f(h)(h^{\prime})=\begin{cases}R_{A_{h}}(h_{A_{h}}^{\prime})+h_{A_{h}^{\bot}}^{\prime},&if $h\in H\setminus{\rm Span}\{e\}$\\
{\rm sgn}(\lambda)h^{\prime},&if $h=\lambda e$,\end{cases}

(4.3)

where
h_{A_{h}}^{\prime}
and
h_{A_{h}^{\bot}}^{\prime}
denote the projections of
h^{\prime}
onto the subspace
A_{h}
and its orthogonal complement
A_{h}^{\bot}
, respectively, and
R_{A_{h}}
denotes the rotation on the plane
A_{h}
around the origin by the angle
\cos^{-1}(\langle h,e\rangle)/\left\lVert h\right\rVert
, that is, by the angle which rotates the vector
h
into
e
. We claim that
f
satisfies the following properties:

(1)

for each
h\in H
the mapping
f(h):H\rightarrow H
is a linear isometry;

(2)

for each
h\in H
and
F\in L_{2}(G,H)
we have
b_{f(h)F}^{\theta}=f(h)b_{F}^{\theta}
;

(3)

for each
h\in H
it holds that
\langle e,f(h)(h)\rangle=\left\lVert f(h)(h)\right\rVert
.

Proof of (1):
We first assume that
h\in H\setminus{\rm Span}\{e\}
. Then, since for any
h^{\prime}\in H
we have that
h^{\prime}=h_{A_{h}}^{\prime}+h_{A_{h}^{\bot}}^{\prime}
, and
h_{A_{h}}^{\prime}
is orthogonal to
h_{A_{h}^{\bot}}^{\prime}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert h_{A_{h}}^{\prime}+h_{A_{h}^{\bot}}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle h_{A_{h}}^{\prime}+h_{A_{h}^{\bot}}^{\prime},h_{A_{h}}^{\prime}+h_{A_{h}^{\bot}}^{\prime}\right\rangle=\left\lVert h_{A_{h}}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert h_{A_{h}^{\bot}}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}.

(4.4)

Since rotations are isometries, we have
\left\lVert R_{A_{h}}(h_{A_{h}}^{\prime})\right\rVert=\left\lVert h_{A_{h}}^{\prime}\right\rVert
. Moreover, it follows from the definition of the rotation
R_{A_{h}}
that
R_{A_{h}}(h_{A_{h}}^{\prime})
is orthogonal to
h_{A_{h}^{\bot}}^{\prime}
. Using these observations, a similar argument as above yields for all
h\in H\setminus{\rm Span}\{e\}
and
h^{\prime}\in H
that

\displaystyle\left\lVert f(h)(h^{\prime})\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert R_{A_{h}}(h_{A_{h}}^{\prime})+h_{A_{h}^{\bot}}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert R_{A_{h}}(h_{A_{h}}^{\prime})\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert h_{A_{h}^{\bot}}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert h_{A_{h}}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert h_{A_{h}^{\bot}}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}.

(4.5)

Hence, if
h\in H\setminus{\rm Span}\{e\}
then by Equations (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) we have for all
h^{\prime}\in H
that
\left\lVert f(h)(h^{\prime})\right\rVert=\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert
. If, on the other hand, we have
h=\lambda e
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, then it follows from the very definition of
f
that
\left\lVert f(h)(h^{\prime})\right\rVert=\left\lVert{\rm sgn}(\lambda)h^{\prime}\right\rVert=\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert
, which finishes the proof that for all
h\in H
the mapping
f(h):H\rightarrow H
is an isometry. Linearity of
f(h)
follows directly from the definition.

Proof of (2):
By Lemma
3.9
and the fact that by Step
{\rm(1)}
, for each
h_{0}\in H
the mapping
f(h_{0}):H\rightarrow H
is a linear isometry, we obtain for all
F\in L_{2}(G,H)
that

\displaystyle b_{f(h_{0})F}^{\theta}

\displaystyle=f(h_{0})b_{F}^{\theta}+\int_{H}\large(\theta(f(h_{0})h)-f(h_{0})\theta(h)\large)\,(\lambda\circ F^{-1})({\rm d}h)

\displaystyle=f(h_{0})b_{F}^{\theta}+\int_{H}\large(f(h_{0})\theta(h)-f(h_{0})\large)\theta(h)\,(\lambda\circ F^{-1})({\rm d}h)=f(h_{0})b_{F}^{\theta}.

Proof of (3):
Assume first that
h\in H\setminus{\rm Span}\{e\}
. Then, we have that
h_{A_{h}}=h
and
h_{A_{h}^{\bot}}=0
. By combining these observations with the fact that by its very definition,
R_{h}
rotates the vector
h
into
e
, we get

\displaystyle\langle e,f(h)(h)\rangle=\langle e,R_{h}(h)\rangle=\langle e,\left\lVert h\right\rVert e\rangle=\left\lVert h\right\rVert\langle e,e\rangle=\left\lVert h\right\rVert=\left\lVert R_{h}(h)\right\rVert=\left\lVert f(h)(h)\right\rVert.

(4.6)

If, on the other hand, we assume
h=\lambda e
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, then

\displaystyle\langle e,f(h)(h)\rangle=\langle e,{\rm sgn}(\lambda)\lambda e\rangle=|\lambda|=\left\lVert{\rm sgn}(\lambda)\lambda e\right\rVert=\left\lVert f(h)h\right\rVert.

(4.7)

Equations (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) establish the property (3).

To finish the proof of this lemma, let
\epsilon>0
be fixed. We define a set-valued function
g:L_{2}(G,H)\rightarrow 2^{\bar{B}_{L(H)}}
by

g(F)=\left\{O\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}:\sup_{Q\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}}\left\lVert b_{QF}^{\theta}\right\rVert-\left\lVert b_{OF}^{\theta}\right\rVert<\frac{\epsilon}{T}\right\}.

In order to prove the existence of a measurable selector for
g
, it suffices to show according to the Kuratowski-Ryll Nardzewski measurable selection theorem, see e.g.
[
15
]
, that for all open sets
S\subseteq\bar{B}_{L(H)}
we have

\displaystyle\left\{F\in L_{2}(G,H):g(F)\cap S\neq\emptyset\right\}\in\Borel(L_{2}(G,H)).

(4.8)

We define the mapping
\kappa:L_{2}(G,H)\times\bar{B}_{L(H)}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\kappa(F,O)=\sup_{Q\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}}\left\lVert b_{QF}^{\theta}\right\rVert-\left\lVert b_{OF}^{\theta}\right\rVert.

It follows from lower-semicontinuity of
l_{L}
, see Lemma
3.12
, that for each fixed
O\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}
the mapping
F\mapsto\kappa(F,O)
is lower-semicontinuous. Moreover, by
[
13
, Th.3]
,
[
3
, Le. 2.1]
and Equation (
2.1
), we have that for each fixed
F\in L_{2}(G,H)
the mapping
O\mapsto\kappa(F,O)
is continuous with the strong topology. By using these observations and noting that
S
is an open set in the strong topology, we obtain that

\displaystyle\left\{F\in L_{2}(G,H):g(F)\cap S\neq\emptyset\right\}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{O\in S}\left\{F\in L_{2}(G,H):\kappa(F,O)<\frac{\epsilon}{T}\right\}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{O\in S\cap\mathcal{D}^{1}}\left\{F\in L_{2}(G,H):\kappa(F,O)<\frac{\epsilon}{T}\right\},

where
\mathcal{D}^{1}
denotes a countable dense subset of
\bar{B}_{L(H)}
with the strong topology. Since for each fixed
O\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}
, the mapping
F\mapsto\kappa(F,O)
is lower-semicontinuous and hence measurable, for each fixed
O\in S
it holds that

\left\{F\in L_{2}(G,H):\kappa(F,O)<\frac{\epsilon}{T}\right\}\in\Borel(L_{2}(G,H)).

The Kuratowski-Ryll Nardzewski measurable selection theorem implies that there exists a measurable selector function
i\colon L_{2}(G,H)\rightarrow\bar{B}_{L(H)}
satisfying for all
F\in L_{2}(G,H)
that
i(F)\in g(F)
.

Finally, we define a function

\eta:[0,T]\rightarrow\bar{B}_{L(H)},\qquad\eta(t)=f\left(b_{i(\psi)\psi}^{\theta}\right)\big(i(\psi(t))\big),

where
\eta
is measurable since it is the composition of measurable functions. Using properties
{\rm(1)}-{\rm(3)}
of the mapping
f
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert\int_{0}^{T}b_{\eta\psi(t)}^{\theta}\,{\rm d}t\right\rVert

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{T}\langle b_{\eta\psi(t)}^{\theta},e\rangle\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert b_{i(\psi(t))\psi(t)}^{\theta}\right\rVert\,{\rm d}t\geq\int_{0}^{T}\left(\sup_{O\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}}\left\lVert b_{O\psi(t)}^{\theta}\right\rVert-\frac{\epsilon}{T}\right)\,{\rm d}t.

Since
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary, and by approximating
\eta
using processes from
\mathcal{S}_{\rm det}^{\rm 1,op}
we conclude

\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm det}^{\rm 1,op}}\left\lVert\int_{0}^{T}b_{\gamma\psi}^{\theta}\,{\rm d}t\right\rVert\geq\int_{0}^{T}\sup_{O\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}}\left\lVert b_{O\psi(t)}^{\theta}\right\rVert\,{\rm d}t.

As the reverse inequality follows directly from basic properties of the Bochner integral, the proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

If a sequence
(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\rm det}^{\rm HS}
satisfies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{S}^{\rm 1,op}_{\rm det}}E\left[\left\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\gamma\psi_{n}\,{\rm d}L\right\rVert\wedge 1\right]=0,

then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm det}^{\rm 1,op}}\left\lVert\int_{0}^{T}b_{\gamma\psi_{n}(t)}^{\theta}\,{\rm d}t\right\rVert=0.

Proof.

Assume, aiming for a contradiction, that this is not the case. Then, by passing on to a suitable subsequence if necessary, there exists an
\epsilon>0
and a sequence
(\gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\rm det}^{\rm 1,op}
satisfying for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
that

\left\lVert\int_{0}^{T}b_{\gamma_{n}\psi_{n}(t)}^{\theta}\,{\rm d}t\right\rVert>\epsilon.

(4.9)

On the other hand, the hypothesis
implies that the sequence
(I(\gamma_{n}\psi_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of infinitely divisible random variables

I(\gamma_{n}\psi_{n})
, whose first characteristics equals
\int_{0}^{T}b_{\gamma_{n}\psi_{n}(t)}^{\theta}\,{\rm d}t
, converges to
0
in probability. It follows from Equation (
2.1
) that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}b_{\gamma_{n}\psi_{n}(t)}^{\theta}\,dt=0,

which contradicts Equation (
4.9
). Hence, the result follows.
∎

Having provided all the necessary preliminary results, we now proceed to proving the reverse implication in Lemma
4.5
.

Proof of
(\rm b)\Rightarrow(\rm a)
in Lemma
4.5
.

Since for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\psi_{n}
has a representation of the form

\psi_{n}(t)=F_{0}^{n}\mathbb{1}_{\{0\}}(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)-1}F_{i}^{n}\mathbb{1}_{(t_{i}^{n},t_{i+1}^{n}]}(t),

where
0=t_{1}^{n}<...<t_{N(n)}^{n}=T
, and
F_{i}^{n}\in L_{2}(G,H)
for each
i\in\{0,...,N(n)-1\}
, the integral
I(\psi_{n})
takes the form

I(\psi_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)-1}F_{i}^{n}\left(L(t_{i+1}^{n})-L(t_{i}^{n})\right).

Since the integral
I(\psi_{n})
is the sum of independent infinitely divisible random variables,
I(\psi_{n})
is also infinitely divisible and has characteristics

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)-1}(t_{i+1}^{n}-t_{i}^{n})b_{F_{i}^{n}}^{\theta},\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)-1}(t_{i+1}^{n}-t_{i}^{n})F_{i}^{n}Q(F_{i}^{n})^{*},\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)-1}(t_{i+1}^{n}-t_{i}^{n})\left(\lambda\circ(F_{i}^{n})^{-1}\right)\right).

Since the hypothesis implies that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I(\psi_{n})=0
in
L_{P}^{0}(\Omega,H)
, we conclude from Lemma
3.11
and
[
3
, Le. 3.6]
that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}k_{L}(\psi(t))\,dt

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{H}\left(\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1\right)\left(\left(\lambda\otimes{\rm Leb}\right)\circ\kappa_{\psi_{n}}^{-1}\right)({\rm d}h)+{\rm Tr}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left(\psi_{n}(t)Q\psi_{n}(t)^{*}\right)\;{\rm d}t\right)=0.

(4.10)

Furthermore, the hypothesis implies by Lemmata
4.8
and
4.9
that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}l_{L}(\psi_{n}(t))dt

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\sup_{O\in\bar{B}_{L(H)}}\left\lVert b_{O\psi_{n}(t)}^{\theta}\right\rVert\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm det}^{\rm 1,op}}\left\lVert\int_{0}^{T}b_{\gamma\psi_{n}(t)}^{\theta}\,{\rm d}t\right\rVert=0.

(4.11)

Equations (
4
) and (
4
) together imply that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}m_{L}^{\prime}(\psi_{n})=0
. Since by assumption, we have
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}m^{\prime\prime}(\psi_{n})=0
, we obtain that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}m_{L}(\psi_{n})=0
.
∎

We are now ready to present the proof of the main result of this section characterising the largest space of deterministic Hilbert-Schmidt operator-valued functions which are integrable with respect to a cylindrical Lévy process
L
in Hilbert space.

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

If
\psi\in\mathcal{I}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
then by the very definition of integrability, see Definition
4.2
, there exists a sequence
(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteqq\mathcal{S}_{\rm det}^{\rm HS}
such that
\psi_{n}\rightarrow\psi
Lebesgue a.e. and
\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm det}^{1,{\rm op}}}E[\left\lVert I(\gamma(\psi_{n}-\psi_{m}))\right\rVert\wedge 1]\rightarrow 0
. By Lemma
4.5
, this implies that
m_{L}(\psi_{n}-\psi_{m})\rightarrow 0
. Completeness of the modular space
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
, see Lemma
3.20
, and the fact that
\psi_{n}\rightarrow\psi
Lebesgue a.e. allows us to conclude that
\psi\in\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
.

Conversely, if
\psi\in\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
, then Lemma
3.22
implies that there exists a sequence
(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of elements in
\mathcal{S}_{\rm det}^{\rm HS}
such that
\psi_{n}\rightarrow\psi
Lebesgue a.e. and
m_{L}(\psi_{n}-\psi)\rightarrow 0
. It follows that
m_{L}(\psi_{n}-\psi_{m})\rightarrow 0
, which implies
\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm det}^{\rm 1,op}}E[\left\lVert I(\gamma(\psi_{n}-\psi_{m}))\right\rVert\wedge 1]\rightarrow 0
by Lemma

and establishes that

∎

5
Stochastic integrals with predictable integrands

For the remainder of this section, we fix a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},P)
. As in the case of deterministic integrands, we begin by introducing two classes of functions on which our definition of the stochastic integral depends on.

Definition 5.1
.

(1)

An
L_{2}(G,H)
-valued predictable step process
\Psi\colon\Omega\times[0,T]\rightarrow L_{2}(G,H)
is of the form

\displaystyle\Psi(\omega,t)=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N(0)}F_{0,k}\mathbb{1}_{A_{0,k}}(\omega)\right)\mathbb{1}_{\{0\}}(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N(i)}F_{i,k}\mathbb{1}_{A_{i,k}}(\omega)\right)\mathbb{1}_{(t_{i},t_{i+1}]}(t),

(5.1)

where
0=t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}=T
,
A_{0,k}\in\mathcal{F}_{0}
and
F_{0,k}\in L_{2}(G,H)
for all
k=1,...,N(0)
,
A_{i,k}\in\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}}
and
F_{i,k}\in L_{2}(G,H)
for all
i=1,...,n-1
and
k=1,...,N(i)
. The space of all
L_{2}(G,H)
-valued predictable step processes is denoted by
\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{\rm HS}:=\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{\rm HS}(G,H)
.

(2)

An
L(H)
-valued predictable step process
\Gamma\colon\Omega\times[0,T]\rightarrow L(H,H)
is of the form

\displaystyle\Gamma(\omega,t)=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N(0)}O_{0,k}\mathbb{1}_{A_{0,k}}(\omega)\right)\mathbb{1}_{\{0\}}(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N(i)}O_{i,k}\mathbb{1}_{A_{i,k}}(\omega)\right)\mathbb{1}_{(t_{i},t_{i+1}]}(t),

(5.2)

where
0=t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}=T
,
A_{0,k}\in\mathcal{F}_{0}
and
O_{0,k}\in L(H)
for all
k=1,...,N(0)
,
A_{i,k}\in\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}}
and
O_{i,k}\in L(H)
for all
i=1,...,n-1
and
k=1,...,N(i)
. The space of all
L(H)
-valued predictable step processes with

\displaystyle\sup_{(\omega,t)\in\Omega\times[0,T]}\left\lVert\Gamma(\omega,t)\right\rVert_{H\rightarrow H}\leq 1

is denoted by
\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}:=\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}(H,H)
.

Let
\Psi\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{\rm HS}
be of the form (
5.1
) and
L
a cylindrical Lévy process in
G
. As explained after Definition
4.1
, there exists an
H
-valued random variable
F_{i,k}(L(t_{i+1})-L(t_{i}))\colon\Omega\to H
for each
i=1,...,n-1
and
k=1,...,N(i)
, satisfying

\big(L(t_{i+1})-L(t_{i})\big)(F_{i,k}^{*}h)=\langle F_{i,k}(L(t_{i+1})-L(t_{i})),h\rangle\quad\text{$P$-a.s.\ for all}\;h\in H.

In this case, the stochastic integral of
\Psi
is defined by

I(\Psi):=\int_{0}^{T}\Psi(t)\,{\rm d}L(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{k=1}^{N(i)}\mathbb{1}_{A_{i,k}}F_{i,k}(L(t_{i+1})-L(t_{i})).

Thus, the integral
I(\Psi):\Omega\rightarrow H
is a genuine
H
-valued random variable. In contrast to the deterministic case in Section
4
, the integral
I(\Psi)
is not necessarily infinitely divisible.

For the purposes of this section, it is convenient to introduce the measure space
\big(\Omega\times[0,T],\mathcal{P},P_{T}\big)
, where
\mathcal{P}
denotes the predictable
\sigma
-algebra and the measure
P_{T}
is defined by
P_{T}:=P\otimes{\rm Leb}|_{[0,T]}
.

Definition 5.2
.

We say that a predictable process
\Psi
is
L
-integrable if there exists a sequence
(\Psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of processes in
\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{\rm HS}
such that

(1)

(\Psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges
P_{T}
-a.e. to
\Psi
;

(2)

\displaystyle\lim_{m,n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}}E\Bigg[\left\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Gamma(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n})\;{\rm d}L\right\rVert\wedge 1\Bigg]=0.

In this case, the stochastic integral of
\Psi
is defined by

I(\Psi):=\int_{0}^{T}\Psi\;{\rm d}L=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\Psi_{n}\;{\rm d}L\quad\text{in}\;L_{P}^{0}(\Omega,H).

The class of all
L
-integrable
L_{2}(G,H)
-valued predictable processes will be denoted by
\mathcal{I}_{{\rm prd},L}^{\rm HS}:=\mathcal{I}_{{\rm prd},L}^{\rm HS}(G,H)
. As usual, for
t\in[0,T]
, we define
\int_{0}^{t}\Psi\;{\rm d}L:=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{1}_{\Omega\times(0,t]}\Psi\;{\rm d}L
.

Remark 5.3
.

An extension of
[
16
, Le. 2.3]
to
H
-valued random variables shows that Condition
{\rm(2)}
of Definition
5.2
implies

\lim_{m,n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}}E\Bigg[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert\int_{0}^{t}\Gamma(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n})\;{\rm d}L\right\rVert\wedge 1\Bigg]=0.

Hence, the notion of convergence introduced in Definition
5.2
coincides with convergence in the Émery topology. Since convergence in the Émery topology implies convergence in the ucp topology, this immediately gives that for each
\Psi\in\mathcal{I}_{{\rm prd},L}^{\rm HS}
the process
\left(\int_{0}^{t}\Psi\,{\rm d}L\right)_{t\in[0,T]}
has cádlág paths. For details, see the end of Remark
4.3
.

Let
L
be a cylindrical Lévy process in
G
and denote by
\rho_{L}
the metric on the modular space
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
making this space Polish; see Proposition
3.23
. Let
L_{P}^{0}(\Omega,\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS})
denote the collection of all Borel-measurable mappings
\Psi:\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
which we endow with the translation invariant metric

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\Psi_{1}-\Psi_{2}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L}:=E\left[\rho_{L}(\Psi_{1}-\Psi_{2})\wedge 1\right]\quad\text{for }\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}\in L_{P}^{0}(\Omega,\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}),

which guarantees its completeness.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\Psi
be a predictable stochastic process in
L_{P}^{0}(\Omega,\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS})
. Then there exists a sequence
(\Psi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of elements of
\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{\rm HS}

converging to
\Psi
both in the metric
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L}
and
P_{T}
-a.e.

Proof.

If
\Psi
is bounded, then
\Psi\in L_{P_{T}}^{\infty}\big(\Omega\times[0,T],L_{2}(G,H)\big)
. Since the algebra of sets

\mathcal{A^{\prime}}=\big\{(s,t]\times B:s<t,B\in\mathcal{F}_{s}\big\}\cup\big\{\{0\}\times B:B\in\mathcal{F}_{0}\big\}

generates
\mathcal{P}
, we conclude from
[
8
, Le. 1.2.19]
and
[
8
, Re. 1.2.20]
that there exists a sequence
(\Psi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of uniformly bounded processes in
\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{\rm HS}
such that
\Psi_{k}\rightarrow\Psi

P_{T}
-a.e. Thus, there exists a set
N\in\mathcal{P}
such that
P_{T}(N)=0
and
\big(\Psi_{k}(\omega,t)-\Psi(\omega,t)\big)\rightarrow 0
for all
(\omega,t)\in N^{c}
. Fubini’s theorem implies that

0=P_{T}(N)=P\otimes{\rm Leb}|_{[0,T]}(N)=\int_{\Omega}{\rm Leb}|_{[0,T]}(N_{\omega})\,P({\rm d}\omega),

where for each fixed
\omega\in\Omega
we define

N_{\omega}:=\Big\{t\in[0,T]\colon\big(\Psi_{k}(\omega,t)-\Psi(\omega,t)\big)_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\;\text{does not converge to 0}\Big\}.

The above implies that
{\rm Leb}|_{[0,T]}(N_{\omega})=0
for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
, that is, there exists an
\Omega_{0}\subseteq\Omega
with
P(\Omega_{0})=1
such that for all
\omega\in\Omega_{0}
we have

{\rm Leb}|_{[0,T]}\Big(t\in[0,T]\colon\big(\Psi_{k}(\omega,t)-\Psi(\omega,t)\big)_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\;\text{does not converge to 0}\Big)=0.

Because
(\Psi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly bounded and
\Psi
is bounded, we can conclude from Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}m_{L}\left(\Psi_{k}(\omega,\cdot)-\Psi(\omega,\cdot)\right)=0
for each
\omega\in\Omega_{0}
. Since
m_{L}
and
\rho_{L}
generate the same topology on
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
, we also have
\rho_{L}(\Psi_{k}(\omega,\cdot)-\Psi(\omega,\cdot))\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
for each
\omega\in\Omega_{0}
. Another application of Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem yields

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\Psi_{k}-\Psi\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\rho_{L}(\Psi_{k}(\omega,\cdot)-\Psi(\omega,\cdot))\wedge 1\Big)\,{\rm d}P=0,

which shows the claim if
\Psi
is bounded. In the case of a general
\Psi
, we define

\Psi_{n}:\Omega\times[0,T]\rightarrow L_{2}(G,H),\qquad\Psi_{n}(\omega,t)=\begin{cases}\Psi(\omega,t)&\text{if }\left\lVert\Psi(\omega,t)\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}\leq n,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Clearly,
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\Psi-\Psi_{n}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L}=0
. The first part of the proof shows that
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a sequence
(\Psi_{n,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{\rm HS}
converging to
\Psi_{n}
as
k\to\infty
in
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L}
and
P_{T}
-a.e. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
choose
k_{n}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\Psi_{n}-\Psi_{n,k_{n}})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L}<\frac{1}{n}
.
It follows that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\Psi-\Psi_{n,k_{n}})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L}\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\bigg({\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\Psi-\Psi_{n})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L}+{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\Psi_{n}-\Psi_{n,k_{n}})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L}\bigg)=0,

which completes the proof, since by passing on to a suitable subsequence, we also have convergence
P_{T}
-a.e.
∎

6
Construction of the decoupled tangent sequence

The technique of constructing decoupled tangent sequences is a powerful tool to obtain strong results on a sequence of possibly dependent random variables. In this section, we briefly recall the fundamental definition, see e.g. Kwapień and Woyczyński
[
17
]
or de la Peña and Giné
[
27
]
, and construct the decoupled tangent sequence in our setting which will enable us to identify the largest space of predictable integrands in the next section.

Remark 6.1
.

We repeatedly use the fact in the following that given a random variable
X
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
and another probability space
(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{F^{\prime}},P^{\prime})
, the random variable
X
can always be considered as a random variable on the product space
(\Omega\times\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{F}^{\prime},P\otimes P^{\prime})
by defining

X(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=X(\omega)\quad\text{for all}\;(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\in\Omega\times\Omega^{\prime}.

In this case, if
X
is real-valued and
P
-integrable we have
E_{P}[X]=E_{P\otimes P^{\prime}}[X]
.

In the next definition, we follow closely Chapter 4.3 of
[
17
]
.

Definition 6.2
.

Let
\big(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P,(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\big)
be a filtered probability space and
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
an
(\mathcal{F}_{n})
-adapted sequence of
H
-valued random variables. If
\big(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{F^{\prime}},P^{\prime},(\mathcal{F^{\prime}}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\big)
is another filtered probability space, then a sequence
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
H
-valued random variables defined on
\big(\Omega\times\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{F^{\prime}},P\otimes P^{\prime},(\mathcal{F}_{n}\otimes\mathcal{F^{\prime}}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\big)
is said to be a decoupled tangent sequence to
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
if

(1)

for each
\omega\in\Omega
, we have that
(Y_{n}(\omega,\cdot))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of independent random variables on
(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime},P^{\prime})
;

(2)

the sequences
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfy for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
that

\mathcal{L}(X_{n}|\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\otimes\mathcal{F^{\prime}}_{n-1})=\mathcal{L}(Y_{n}|\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\otimes\mathcal{F^{\prime}}_{n-1})\quad P\otimes P^{\prime}-\text{a.s.}

Remark 6.3
.

The importance of decoupled tangent sequences within the framework of stochastic integration lies in the existence of a collection of inequalities, frequently called decoupling inequalities, which relate convergence of an adapted sequence of random variables to convergence of their decoupled tangent sequence. In particular, by
[
17
, Pr. 5.7.1.(ii)]
, there exists a constant
c_{1}>0
such that for all finite adapted sequences
(X_{n})_{n=1,...,N}
of
H
-valued random variables with corresponding decoupled tangent sequence
(Y_{n})_{n=1,...,N}
it holds that

E_{P}\left[\left\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{N}X_{n}\right\rVert\wedge 1\right]\leq c_{1}E_{P\otimes P}\left[\left\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{N}Y_{n}\right\rVert\wedge 1\right].

Moreover, by
[
17
, Pr. 5.7.2]
, there exists
c_{2}>0
such that the following ”recoupling” inequality also holds

E_{P\otimes P}\left[\left\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{N}Y_{n}\right\rVert\wedge 1\right]\leq c_{2}\sup_{\epsilon_{n}\in\{\pm 1\}}E_{P}\left[\left\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{N}\epsilon_{n}X_{n}\right\rVert\wedge 1\right].

The main tool for establishing the stochastic integral in the next section is a cylindrical Lévy process
\widetilde{L}
on an enlarged probability space, whose Radonified increments are decoupled to the Radonified increments of the original cylindrical Lévy process. This cylindrical Lévy process
\widetilde{L}
is explicitly constructed in the following result.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
L
be a cylindrical Lévy process in
G
,
0=t_{0}\leq...\leq t_{N}=T
be a partition of
[0,T]
and for each
n=1,...,N
we define
\Theta_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{M(n)}F_{n,k}\mathbb{1}_{A_{n,k}}
, where
F_{n,k}\in L_{2}(G,H)
,
A_{n,k}\in\mathcal{F}_{t_{n-1}}
for all
k=1,...,M(n)
.
By defining cylindrical random variables

\widetilde{L}(t)\colon G\rightarrow L^{0}_{P\otimes P}(\Omega\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}),\qquad\Big(\widetilde{L}(t)g\Big)(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\Big(L(t)g\Big)(\omega^{\prime}),

it follows that
(\widetilde{L}(t):t\geq 0)
is a cylindrical Lévy process on
G
and the sequence of its Radonified increments

\left(\Theta_{n}\big(\widetilde{L}(t_{n})-\widetilde{L}(t_{n-1})\big)\right)_{n\in\{1,...,N\}}

defined on
\big(\Omega\times\Omega,\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{F},P\otimes P,(\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\otimes\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}})_{n\in\{0,...,N\}}\big)
is a decoupled tangent sequence to the sequence of Radonified increments

\Big(\Theta_{n}\big(L(t_{n})-L(t_{n-1})\big)\Big)_{n\in\{1,...,N\}}

defined on
\big(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P,(\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}})_{n\in\{0,...,N\}}\big).

Proof.

In order to make it easier to follow this proof, we define
\Omega^{\prime}=\Omega
,
\mathcal{F^{\prime}}=\mathcal{F}
,
P^{\prime}=P
and
\mathcal{F^{\prime}}_{t_{n}}=\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}
for all
n\in\{0,...,N\}
and instead of denoting the filtered product space by

\Big(\Omega\times\Omega,\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{F},P\otimes P,(\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\otimes\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}})_{n\in\{0,...,N\}}\Big),

we write

\Big(\Omega\times\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{F^{\prime}},P\otimes P^{\prime},(\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\otimes\mathcal{F^{\prime}}_{t_{n}})_{n\in\{0,...,N\}}\Big).

The fact that for each
t\geq 0
the mapping
\widetilde{L}(t)\colon G\rightarrow L_{P\otimes P^{\prime}}^{0}(\Omega\times\Omega^{\prime},\mathbb{R})
is continuous follows directly from the definition of
\widetilde{L}
and Remark
6.1
. Thus
\widetilde{L}
is a cylindrical stochastic process. To prove that it is in fact a cylindrical Lévy process, let us fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\in G
and consider the
n
-dimensional processes

Y
and
Z
defined by
Y(t)=(\widetilde{L}(t)g_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{L}(t)g_{n})
and
Z(t)=(L(t)g_{1},\ldots,L(t)g_{n})
.
It is enough to show that for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
and times
0\leq t_{0}<\cdots<t_{m}\leq T
the random variables
Y(t_{m})-Y(t_{m-1}),\ldots,Y(t_{1})-Y(t_{0})
and
Z(t_{m})-Z(t_{m-1}),\dots,Z(t_{1})-Z(t_{0})

have the same distribution. Here we only prove that for any
0\leq s<t\leq T
the random variables
Y(t)-Y(s)
and
Z(t)-Z(s)
have the same distribution. The general case follows analogously. To see this, let
A\in\Borel(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be arbitrary. The very definition of
\widetilde{L}
shows

\displaystyle(P\otimes P^{\prime})\left(Y(t)-Y(s)\in A\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=(P\otimes P^{\prime})\left((\widetilde{L}(t)g_{1}-\widetilde{L}(s)g_{1},...,\widetilde{L}(t)g_{n}-\widetilde{L}(s)g_{n})\in A\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=(P\otimes P^{\prime})\left(\Omega\times\left\{(L(t)g_{1}-L(s)g_{1},...,L(t)g_{n}-L(s)g_{n})\in A\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=P^{\prime}\left((L(t)g_{1}-L(s)g_{1},...,L(t)g_{n}-L(s)g_{n})\in A\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=P\left(Z(t)-Z(s)\in A\right).

To show that the Radonified increments of
\tilde{L}
satisfy Condition (1) of Definition
6.2
, fix some
\omega\in\Omega
. Then
\Theta_{n}(\omega)
is a (deterministic) Hilbert-Schmidt operator and
(\widetilde{L}(t)(\omega,\cdot):t\geq 0)
is a cylindrical Lévy process in
G
. Thus, for a fixed
\omega\in\Omega
and
n\in\{1,...,N\}
, the Radonified increment
\Theta_{n}(\omega)(\widetilde{L}(t_{n})(\omega,\cdot)-\widetilde{L}(t_{n-1})(\omega,\cdot))
is an
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{t_{n}}
-measurable
H
-valued random variable on
(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime},P^{\prime})
independent of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}}
. It follows for each
\omega\in\Omega
that

\left(\Theta_{n}(\omega)(\widetilde{L}(t_{n})(\omega,\cdot)-\widetilde{L}(t_{n-1})(\omega,\cdot))\right)_{n\in\{1,...,N\}}

is a sequence of independent random variables.

For establishing Condition (2) of Definition
6.2
, we define for each
n\in\{1,...,N\}

the
H
-valued random variables

\displaystyle X_{n}:=\Theta_{n}\big(L(t_{n})-L(t_{n-1})\big)=\sum_{k=1}^{M(n)}\mathbb{1}_{A_{n,k}}F_{n,k}\big(L(t_{n})-L(t_{n-1})\big),

\displaystyle Y_{n}:=\Theta_{n}\big(\widetilde{L}(t_{n})-\widetilde{L}(t_{n-1})\big)=\sum_{k=1}^{M(n)}\mathbb{1}_{A_{n,k}}F_{n,k}\big(\widetilde{L}(t_{n})-\widetilde{L}(t_{n-1})\big),

where
F_{n,k}\big(L(t_{n})-L(t_{n-1})\big)
and
F_{n,k}\big(\widetilde{L}(t_{n})-\widetilde{L}(t_{n-1})\big)
refer to the Radonified increments, and by taking another representation of
\Theta_{n}
if necessary, we may assume that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
the representation of
\Theta_{n}
satisfies
A_{n,k}\cap A_{n,l}=\emptyset
for
k\neq l
and
\cup_{k=1}^{M(n)}A_{n,k}=\Omega
. Choose regular versions of the conditional distributions

\displaystyle(P\otimes P^{\prime})_{X_{n}}\colon\Borel(H)\times(\Omega\times\Omega^{\prime})\rightarrow[0,1],

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad(P\otimes P^{\prime})_{X_{n}}\big(B,(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\big)=(P\otimes P^{\prime})(X_{n}\in B|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n-1}}\otimes\mathcal{F^{\prime}}_{t_{n-1}})(\omega,\omega^{\prime}),

\displaystyle(P\otimes P^{\prime})_{Y_{n}}\colon\Borel(H)\times(\Omega\times\Omega^{\prime})\rightarrow[0,1],

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad(P\otimes P^{\prime})_{Y_{n}}\big(B,(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\big)=(P\otimes P^{\prime})(Y_{n}\in B|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n-1}}\otimes\mathcal{F^{\prime}}_{t_{n-1}})(\omega,\omega^{\prime}).

Since
\widetilde{L}(t)
is a cylindrical Lévy process, and for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
A_{n,k}\cap A_{n,l}=\emptyset
for
k\neq l
and
\cup_{k=1}^{M(n)}A_{n,k}=\Omega
, we obtain for all
h\in H
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
that

\displaystyle E_{P\otimes P^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\Big[e^{i\langle Y_{n},h\rangle}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n-1}}\otimes\mathcal{F^{\prime}}_{t_{n-1}}\Big]

\displaystyle=E_{P\otimes P^{\prime}}\left[e^{i\left\langle\left(\sum_{k=1}^{M(n)}\mathbb{1}_{A_{n,k}\times\Omega^{\prime}}F_{n,k}\right)(\widetilde{L}(t_{n})-\widetilde{L}(t_{n-1})),h\right\rangle}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n-1}}\otimes\mathcal{F^{\prime}}_{t_{n-1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{M(n)}E_{P\otimes P^{\prime}}\Big[\mathbb{1}_{{A_{n,k}}\times\Omega^{\prime}}\,e^{{i\langle F_{n,k}(\widetilde{L}(t_{n})-\widetilde{L}(t_{n-1}))},h\rangle}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n-1}}\otimes\mathcal{F^{\prime}}_{t_{n-1}}\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{M(n)}\mathbb{1}_{{A_{n,k}}\times\Omega^{\prime}}\,E_{P\otimes P^{\prime}}\Big[e^{{i\langle F_{n,k}(\widetilde{L}(t_{n})-\widetilde{L}(t_{n-1}))},h\rangle}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n-1}}\otimes\mathcal{F^{\prime}}_{t_{n-1}}\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{M(n)}\mathbb{1}_{{A_{n,k}}\times\Omega^{\prime}}\,E_{P\otimes P^{\prime}}\Big[e^{{i\langle F_{n,k}(\widetilde{L}(t_{n})-\widetilde{L}(t_{n-1}))},h\rangle}\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{M(n)}\mathbb{1}_{{A_{n,k}}\times\Omega^{\prime}}\,E_{P^{\prime}}\Big[e^{{i\langle F_{n,k}({L}(t_{n})-{L}(t_{n-1}))},h\rangle}\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{M(n)}\mathbb{1}_{{A_{n,k}}\times\Omega^{\prime}}\,e^{{(t_{n}-t_{n-1})S(F_{n,k}^{*}h)}}

\displaystyle=e^{{(t_{n}-t_{n-1})S(\Theta_{n}^{*}h)}}\quad P\otimes P^{\prime}-\text{a.s.},

(6.1)

where
S
denotes the cylindrical Lévy symbol of
L
. In the same way we obtain

\displaystyle E_{P\otimes P^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\Big[e^{i\langle X_{n},h\rangle}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n-1}}\otimes\mathcal{F^{\prime}}_{t_{n-1}}\Big]=e^{{(t_{n}-t_{n-1})S(\Theta_{n}^{*}h)}}\quad P\otimes P^{\prime}-\text{a.s.}

(6.2)

It follows from (
6
) and (
6.2
) by calculating the conditional expectation from the conditional probability, see e.g.
[
11
, Th. 6.4]
, that for
P\otimes P^{\prime}
a.a.
(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\in\Omega\times\Omega^{\prime}
and for all
u\in H
we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{(P\otimes P^{\prime})_{X_{n}}(\cdot,(\omega,\omega^{\prime}))}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{H}e^{i\langle h,u\rangle}\,(P\otimes P^{\prime})_{X_{n}}\big({\rm d}h,(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle=E_{P\otimes P^{\prime}}\Big[e^{i\langle X_{n},u\rangle}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n-1}}\otimes\mathcal{F^{\prime}}_{t_{n-1}}\Big](\omega,\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=E_{P\otimes P^{\prime}}\Big[e^{i\langle Y_{n},u\rangle}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n-1}}\otimes\mathcal{F^{\prime}}_{t_{n-1}}\Big](\omega,\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{H}e^{i\langle h,u\rangle}\,(P\otimes P^{\prime})_{X_{n}}\big({\rm d}h,(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\big)=\varphi_{(P\otimes P^{\prime})_{Y_{n}}(\cdot,(\omega,\omega^{\prime}))}(u).

Since characteristic functions uniquely determine distributions on
\Borel(H)
, we obtain

(P\otimes P^{\prime})_{X_{n}}(\cdot,(\omega,\omega^{\prime}))=(P\otimes P^{\prime})_{Y_{n}}(\cdot,(\omega,\omega^{\prime}))\quad P\otimes P^{\prime}-\text{a.s.},

establishing Condition (2) of Definition
6.2
.
∎

7
Characterisation of random integrands

The following is the main result of this paper characterising the largest space of predictable integrands which are stochastically integrable with respect to a cylindrical Lévy process
L
in a Hilbert space
G
.

Theorem 7.1
.

The space
\mathcal{I}_{{\rm prd},L}^{\rm HS}
of predicable Hilbert-Schmidt operator-valued processes integrable
with respect to a cylindrical Lévy process
L
in
G
coincides with the class of predictable processes in
L_{P}^{0}(\Omega,\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS})
.

As in the case of deterministic integrands, the above characterisation of the space of
L
-integrable predictable processes
strongly relies on the equivalent notion of convergences in two spaces.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
L
be a cylindrical Lévy process in
G
, and
(\Psi_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
a sequence in
\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{\rm HS}
. Then the following are
equivalent:

(a)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\Psi_{n}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L}=0
;

(b)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\displaystyle\sup_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}}E\Bigg[\left\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Gamma\Psi_{n}\;{\rm d}L\right\rVert\wedge 1\Bigg]=0
and
\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}E\left[m^{\prime\prime}(\Psi_{n})\wedge 1\right]=0.

Proof.

To prove (a)
\Rightarrow
(b), let
\epsilon>0
be fixed. Lemma
4.5
and the fact that
m_{L}
and
\rho_{L}
generate the same topology on
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
enables us to choose

\delta>0
such that for every
\psi\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm det}^{\rm HS}
we have
the implication:

\displaystyle\rho_{L}(\psi)\leq\delta\;\Rightarrow\;\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{S}^{1,{\rm op}}_{\rm det}}P\left(\left\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\gamma\psi\;{\rm d}L\right\rVert>\epsilon\right)\leq\epsilon.

(7.1)

Since
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\Psi_{n}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L}=0
, there exists
n_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that the set

A_{n}:=\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\rho_{L}(\Psi_{n}(\omega))\leq\delta\right\}

satisfies
P(A_{n})\geq 1-\epsilon
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. By recalling the definition of
\widetilde{L}
and
(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime},P^{\prime})
from Proposition
6.4
, implication
(
7.1
) implies for all
\omega\in A_{n}
and
n\geq n_{0}
that

\sup_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}}P^{\prime}\left(\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega^{\prime}:\left\lVert\left(\int_{0}^{T}\Gamma(\omega)\Psi_{n}(\omega)\;{\rm d}\widetilde{L}(\omega,\cdot)\right)(\omega^{\prime})\right\rVert>\epsilon\right)\leq\epsilon.

Fubini’s theorem implies for all
n\geq n_{0}
and
\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}
that

\displaystyle(P\otimes P^{\prime})\left((\omega,\omega^{\prime})\in\Omega\times\Omega^{\prime}\colon\left\lVert\left(\int_{0}^{T}\Gamma\Psi_{n}\;{\rm d}\widetilde{L}\right)(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\right\rVert>\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}P^{\prime}\left(\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega^{\prime}:\left\lVert\left(\int_{0}^{T}\Gamma(\omega)\Psi_{n}(\omega)\;{\rm d}\widetilde{L}(\omega,\cdot)\right)(\omega^{\prime})\right\rVert>\epsilon\right)\,P({\rm d}\omega)\leq 2\epsilon

As
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary, we obtain

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}}E_{P\otimes P^{\prime}}\Bigg[\left\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Gamma\Psi_{n}\;{\rm d}\widetilde{L}\right\rVert\wedge 1\Bigg]=0.

(7.2)

By the ideal property of
L_{2}(G,H)
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}
the integrand
\Gamma\Psi_{n}
lies in
\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{\rm HS}
and has a representation of the from

\displaystyle\Gamma\Psi_{n}=\Gamma_{0}^{n}F_{0}^{n}\mathbb{1}_{\{0\}}+\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)-1}\Gamma_{i}^{n}F_{i}^{n}\mathbb{1}_{(t_{i}^{n},t_{i+1}^{n}]},

(7.3)

where
0=t_{1}^{n}\leq\cdots<t_{N(n)}^{n}=T
, and
\Gamma_{i}^{n}F_{i}^{n}
is an
\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{n}}
-measurable
L_{2}(G,H)
-valued random variable taking only finitely many values for each
i=0,...,N(n)-1
.
Proposition
6.4
guarantees for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
that the sequence of Radonified increments

\Big(\Gamma_{i}^{n}F_{i}^{n}(L(t_{i+1}^{n})-L(t_{i}^{n}))\Big)_{i=1,...,N_{n}-1}

has the decoupled tangent sequence

m\geq\max\{m_{0}(n,\epsilon),m_{1}(n,\epsilon),m_{2}(n,\epsilon)\}
that

\sup_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}}E\Bigg[\left\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Gamma\Psi_{n}^{m}\;{\rm d}L\right\rVert\wedge 1\Bigg]+E\left[m^{\prime\prime}(\Psi_{n}^{m})\wedge 1\right]<\delta(\epsilon),

which implies by (
7.13
) and (
7.15
) that

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\Psi_{n}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L}\leq{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\Psi_{n}-\Psi_{n}^{m}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L}+{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\Psi_{n}^{m}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L}<\epsilon.

As
\epsilon>0
was arbitrary, this concludes the proof.
∎

Having introduced the notion of the stochastic integral, we now show that stochastic integral processes, obtained by fixing an integrand and varying the upper limit of the stochastic integral, are in fact semimartingales.

Theorem 7.4
.

If
\Psi\in\mathcal{I}_{{\rm prd},L}^{\rm HS}
, then the integral process
\left(I(\Psi)(t):t\in[0,T]\right)
defined by

\displaystyle I(\Psi)(t):=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,t]}(s)\Psi(s)\,L({\rm d}s)\qquad\text{for }t\in[0,T],

is a semimartingale.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{1,\rm{op}}
be of the form

\Gamma(t)=\Gamma_{0}\mathbb{1}_{\{0\}}(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\Gamma_{k}\mathbb{1}_{(s_{k},s_{k+1}]}(t),

where
0=s_{1}<...<s_{N}=T
are deterministic times,
\Gamma_{0}:\Omega\rightarrow\bar{B}_{L(H)}
is
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable, and each
\Gamma_{k}:\Omega\rightarrow\bar{B}_{L(H)}
is an
\mathcal{F}_{s_{k}}
-measurable random variable taking only finitely many values for
k=1,...,N-1
.
Then we define the stochastic integral

\int_{0}^{T}\Gamma\,{\rm d}I(\Psi):=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\Gamma_{k}\bigg(I(\Psi)(s_{k+1})-I(\Psi)(s_{k})\bigg).

To prove the claim it suffices to show that the set

\big\{\int_{0}^{T}\Gamma\,{\rm d}I(\Psi):\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{1,\rm{op}}\big\}

is bounded in probability according to
[
9
, Th. 2.1]
. Suppose, aiming for a contradiction, that it is not the case. Then there exists an
\epsilon>0
and a sequence
(\Gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{1,\rm{op}}
satisfying for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
that

\displaystyle P\left(\left\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Gamma_{n}\,{\rm d}I(\Psi)\right\rVert>n\right)\geq\epsilon.

(7.18)

For each
\Psi\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{\rm HS}
and
\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{1,\rm{op}}
, the very definitions of stochastic integrals show

\int_{0}^{T}\Gamma\,{\rm d}I(\Psi)=\int_{0}^{T}\Gamma\Psi\,{\rm d}L.

This equality can be generalised to arbitrary
\Psi\in\mathcal{I}_{{\rm prd},L}^{\rm HS}
and
\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{1,\rm{op}}
by a standard approximation argument. Using this to rewrite Equation (
7.18
), we obtain for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
that

\displaystyle\epsilon\leq P\left(\left\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Gamma_{n}\,{\rm d}I(\Psi)\right\rVert>n\right)=P\left(\left\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{n}\Gamma_{n}\Psi\,{\rm d}L\right\rVert>1\right).

(7.19)

On the other hand, since
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\frac{1}{n}\Gamma_{n}\Psi\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, Corollary
7.3
implies

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}E\left[\left\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{n}\Gamma_{n}\Psi\,{\rm d}L\right\rVert\wedge 1\right]=0,

which contradicts (
7.19
) because of the equivalent characterisation of the topology in
L^{0}_{P}(\Omega,H)
.
∎

We finish this section with a stochastic dominated convergence theorem.

Theorem 7.5
.

Let
(\Psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of processes in
\mathcal{I}_{{\rm prd},L}^{\rm HS}
such that

(1)

(\Psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges
P_{T}
-a.e. to an
L_{2}(G,H)
-valued predictable process
\Psi
;

(2)

there exists a process
\Upsilon\in\mathcal{I}_{{\rm prd},L}^{\rm HS}
satisfying for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
that

(k_{L}+l_{L})(\Psi_{n}(\omega,t))\leq(k_{L}+l_{L})(\Upsilon(\omega,t))\quad\text{for $P_{T}$-a.a.\ $(\omega,t)\in\Omega\times[0,T]$.}

Then it follows that
\Psi\in\mathcal{I}_{{\rm prd},L}^{\rm HS}
and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\displaystyle\sup_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}}E\Bigg[\left\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Gamma(\Psi_{n}-\Psi)\;{\rm d}L\right\rVert\wedge 1\Bigg]=0.

Proof.

By assumption, there exists a set
N\subseteq\Omega\times[0,T]
with
P_{T}(N)=0
such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Psi_{n}(\omega,t)=\Psi(\omega,t)
and
(k_{L}+l_{L})(\Psi_{n}(\omega,t))\leq(k_{L}+l_{L})(\Upsilon(\omega,t))
for all
(\omega,t)\in N^{c}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Fubini’s theorem yields that

0=P_{T}(N)=\int_{\Omega}{\rm Leb}|_{[0,T]}(N_{\omega})\,P({\rm d}\omega),

where

\displaystyle N_{\omega}\!:=\!\left\{t\in[0,T]:\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Psi_{n}(\omega,t)\neq\Psi(\omega,t)\,\text{or}\,(k_{L}+l_{L})(\Psi_{n}(\omega,t))>(k_{L}+l_{L})(\Upsilon(\omega,t))\right\}.

It follows that there exists an
\Omega_{1}\subseteq\Omega
with
P(\Omega_{1})=1
such that
{\rm Leb}|_{[0,T]}(N_{\omega})=0
for all
\omega\in\Omega_{1}
. Consequently, for each
\omega\in\Omega_{1}
we have
(k_{L}+l_{L})(\Psi_{n}(\omega,t))\leq(k_{L}+l_{L})(\Upsilon(\omega,t))
and
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Psi_{n}(\omega,t)=\Psi(\omega,t)
for Lebesgue almost every
t\in[0,T]
. Theorem
7.1
guarantees
that there exists
\Omega_{2}\subseteq\Omega
with
P(\Omega_{2})=1
such that
m_{L}(\Upsilon(\omega,\cdot))<\infty
for all
\omega\in\Omega_{2}
. Continuity of
k_{L}
and
l_{L}
at
0
, see Lemma
3.12
, and the classical version of Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem implies that for all
\omega\in\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{m,n\rightarrow\infty}m_{L}(\Psi_{m}(\omega,\cdot)-\Psi_{n}(\omega,\cdot))

\displaystyle\qquad=\lim_{m,n\rightarrow\infty}\Bigg(\int_{0}^{T}k_{L}(\Psi_{m}(\omega,t)-\Psi_{n}(\omega,t))+l_{L}(\Psi_{m}(\omega,t)-\Psi_{n}(\omega,t))\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\Psi_{m}(\omega,t)-\Psi_{n}(\omega,t)\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G,H)}^{2}\wedge 1\,{\rm d}t\Bigg)=0.

Hence, for each
\omega\in\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}
the sequence
(\Psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is Cauchy in the modular topology, which by Lemma
3.20
and the fact that
\Psi_{n}(\omega)\rightarrow\Psi(\omega)
for Lebesgue a.a.
t\in[0,T]
allows us to conclude that
\Psi(\omega)\in\mathcal{M}_{{\rm det},L}^{\rm HS}
. Since
P(\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2})=1
, Theorem
7.1
shows
\Psi\in\mathcal{I}_{{\rm prd},L}^{\rm HS}
. Another application of Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem establishes that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\Psi_{n}-\Psi\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L}=0
, which completes the proof by an application of Corollary
7.3
.
∎
