Abstract

Associated to every closed, embedded submanifold
N
of a connected Riemannian manifold
M
, there is the distance function
d_{N}
which measures the distance of a point in
M
from
N
. We analyze the square of this function and show that it is Morse-Bott on the complement of the cut locus
\mathrm{Cu}(N)
of
N
, provided
M
is complete. Moreover, the gradient flow lines provide a deformation retraction of
M-\mathrm{Cu}(N)
to
N
. If
M
is a closed manifold, then we prove that the Thom space of the normal bundle of
N
is homeomorphic to
M/\mathrm{Cu}(N)
. We also discuss several interesting results which are either applications of these or related observations regarding the theory of cut locus. These results include, but are not limited to, a computation of the local homology of singular matrices, a classification of the homotopy type of the cut locus of a homology sphere inside a sphere, a deformation of the indefinite unitary group
U(p,q)
to
U(p)\times U(q)
and a geometric deformation of
GL(n,\mathbb{R})
to
O(n,\mathbb{R})
which is different from the Gram-Schmidt retraction.

If a compact Lie group
G
acts on a Riemannian manifold
M
freely then
M/G
is a manifold. In addition, if the action is isometric, then the metric of
M
induces a metric on
M/G
. We show that if
N
is a
G
-invariant submanifold of
M
, then the cut locus
\mathrm{Cu}(N)
is
G
-invariant, and
\mathrm{Cu}(N)/G=\mathrm{Cu}(N/G)
in
M/G
. An application of this result to complex projective hypersurfaces has been provided.

Chapter 1
Introduction

On a Riemannian manifold
M
, the distance function
d_{N}(\cdot)\vcentcolon=d(N,\cdot)
from a closed subset
N
is fundamental in the study of variational problems. For instance, the viscosity solution of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation is given by the flow of the gradient vector of the distance function
d_{N}
, when
N
is the smooth boundary of a relatively compact domain in manifolds; see
[
Li and Nirenberg, 2005
,
Mantegazza and Mennucci, 2003
]
. Although the distance function
d_{N}
is not differentiable at
N
, squaring the function removes this issue. Associated to
N
and the distance function
d_{N}
is a set
\mathrm{Cu}(N)
, the cut locus of
N
in
M
. The cut locus of a point (submanifold) consists of all points such that a distance minimal geodesic (see
2.3.2
and
3.1.2
) starting at the point (submanifold) fails its distance minimality property. The aim of the thesis is to explore the topological and geometric properties of cut locus of a submanifold.

1.1
A survey of the cut locus

This section is devoted to the literature survey and a discussion of some known results.

Cut locus of a point, a notion initiated by Henri Poincaré
[
Poincaré, 1905
]
, has been extensively studied (see
[
Kobayashi, 1967
]
for a survey as well as
[
Buchner, 1977
]
,
[
Myers, 1935
]
,
[
Sakai, 1996
]
, and
[
Wolter, 1979
]
). Prior to Poincaré it had appeared implicitly in a paper
[
von Mangoldt, 1881
]
. Other articles
[
Whitehead, 1935
]
and
[
Myers, 1935
,
Myers, 1936
]
describe topological behavior of the cut locus. Due to its topological properties, it became an important tool in the field of Riemannian geometry or Finsler geometry. We list a few references like
[
Klingenberg, 1959
]
,
[
Rauch, 1959
]
, and
[
Cheeger and Ebin, 1975
, Chapter 5]
for a detailed study of cut locus of a point. We also mention the work around the Blaschke conjecture which uses the geometry of the cut locus of a point, see
[
Besse, 1978
,
McKay, 2015
]
. A great source of reference for articles related to cut loci is
[
Sakai, 1984
, §4]
. Further, articles
[
Sakai, 1977
,
Sakai, 1978
,
Sakai, 1979
]
and
[
Takeuchi, 1978
,
Takeuchi, 1979
]
discussed cut loci in symmetric spaces. For questions on the triangulability of cut loci and differential topological aspects, see
[
Buchner, 1977
,
Singer and Gluck, 1976
,
Gluck and Singer, 1978
,
Wall, 1977
]
.

Cut locus of submanifolds was first studied by René Thom
[
Thom, 1972
]
. We mention some references for cut locus of submanifolds where it has been analyzed via the Eikonal equations and Hamilton-Jacobi equation, for example, see
[
Angulo Ardoy and Guijarro, 2011
,
Mantegazza and Mennucci, 2003
]
as well as analyzed via topological methods, for example, see
[
Flaherty, 1965
,
Ozols, 1974
,
Singh, 1987a
,
Singh, 1987b
,
Singh, 1988
]
.

1.2
Overview of results

Suitable simple examples indicate that
M-\mathrm{Cu}(N)
topologically deforms to
N
. One of our main results is the following (
4.3.5
).

Theorem A
.

Let
N
be a closed embedded submanifold of a complete Riemannian manifold
M
and
d:M\to\mathbb{R}
denote the distance function with respect to
N
. If
f=d^{2}
, then its restriction to
M-\mathrm{Cu}(N)
is a Morse-Bott function, with
N
as the critical submanifold. Moreover,
M-\mathrm{Cu}(N)
deforms to
N
via the gradient flow of
f
.

It is observed that this deformation takes infinite time. To obtain a strong deformation retract, one reparameterizes the flow lines to be defined over
[0,1]
. It can be shown (
Lemma

4.3.1
) that the cut locus
\mathrm{Cu}(N)
is a strong deformation retract of
M-N
. A primary motivation for
A
came from understanding the cut locus of
N=O(n,\mathbb{R})
inside
M=M(n,\mathbb{R})
, equipped with the Euclidean metric. We show in
Section

3.2
that the cut locus is the set
\mathrm{Sing}
of singular matrices and the deformation of its complement is not the Gram-Schmidt deformation but rather the deformation obtained from the polar decomposition, i.e.,
A\in GL(n,\mathbb{R})
deforms to
A\big(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\,\big)^{-1}
. Combining with a result of J. J. Hebda
[
Hebda, 1983
, Theorem 1.4]
we are able to compute the local homology of
\mathrm{Sing}
(cf
Lemma

3.2.4
and
3.2.1
).

Theorem B
.

For
A\in M(n,\mathbb{R})

H_{n^{2}-1-i}(\mathrm{Sing},\mathrm{Sing}-A;G)\cong\widetilde{H}^{i}(O(n-k,\mathbb{R});G)

where
A\in\mathrm{Sing}
has rank
k<n
and
G
is any abelian group.

When the cut locus is empty, we deduce that
M
is diffeomorphic to the normal bundle
\nu
of
N
in
M
. In particular,
M
deforms to
N
. Among applications, we discuss two families of examples. We reprove the known fact that
GL(n,\mathbb{R})
deforms to
O(n,\mathbb{R})
for any choice of left-invariant metric on
GL(n,\mathbb{R})
which is right-
O(n,\mathbb{R})
-invariant. However, this deformation is not obtained topologically but by Morse-Bott flows. For a natural choice of such a metric, this deformation (
5.2
) is not the Gram-Schmidt deformation, but one obtained from the polar decomposition. We also consider
U(p,q)
, the group preserving the indefinite form of signature
(p,q)
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We show (
5.2.1
) that
U(p,q)
deforms to
U(p)\times U(q)
for the left-invariant metric given by
\left\langle X,Y\right\rangle:=\textup{tr}(X^{\ast}Y)
. In particular, we show that the exponential map is surjective for
U(p,q)
(
5.2.1
). To our knowledge, this method is different from the standard proof.

For a Riemannian manifold we have the exponential map at
p\in M
,
\exp_{p}:T_{p}M\to M
. Let
\nu
denote the normal bundle of
N
in
M
. We will modify the exponential map (see §
4.3.2
) to define the
rescaled exponential

\widetilde{\exp}:D(\nu)\to M
, the domain of which is the unit disk bundle of
\nu
. The main result (
4.3.2
) here is the observation that there is a connection between the cut locus
\mathrm{Cu}(N)
and Thom space
\mathrm{Th}(\nu):=D(\nu)/S(\nu)
of
\nu
.

Theorem C
.

Let
N
be an embedded submanifold inside a closed, connected Riemannian manifold
M
. If
\nu
denotes the normal bundle of
N
in
M
, then there is a homeomorphism

\widetilde{\exp}:D(\nu)/S(\nu)\xrightarrow{\cong}M/\mathrm{Cu}(N).

This immediately leads to a long exact sequence in homology (see (
4.8
))

\cdots\to H_{j}(\mathrm{Cu}(N))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i_{*}}}{{\longrightarrow}}H_{j}(M)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle q}}{{\longrightarrow}}\widetilde{H}_{j}(\mathrm{Th}(\nu))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\partial}}{{\longrightarrow}}H_{j-1}(\mathrm{Cu}(N))\to\cdots.

This is a useful tool in characterizing the homotopy type of the cut locus. We list a few applications and related results.

Theorem D
.

Let
N
be a homology
k
-sphere embedded in a Riemannian manifold
M^{d}
homeomorphic to
S^{d}
.

1.

If
d\geq k+3
, then
\mathrm{Cu}(N)
is homotopy equivalent to
S^{d-k-1}
. Moreover, if
M,N
are real analytic and the embedding is real analytic, then
\mathrm{Cu}(N)
is a simplicial complex of dimension at most
d-1
.

2.

If
d=k+2
, then
\mathrm{Cu}(N)
has the homology of
S^{1}
. There exists homology
3
-spheres in
S^{5}
for which
\mathrm{Cu}(N)\simeq S^{1}
. However, for non-trivial knots
K
in
S^{3}
, the cut locus is not homotopy equivalent to
S^{1}
.

The above results are a combination of Theorem
4.3.3
, Theorem
4.3.1
and Example
4.3.3
. In general, the structure of the cut locus may be wild (see
[
Gluck and Singer, 1978
]
,
[
Itoh and Sabau, 2016
]
, and
[
Itoh and Vîlcu, 2015
]
). S. B. Myers
[
Myers, 1935
]
had shown that if
M
is a real analytic sphere, then
\mathrm{Cu}(p)
is a finite tree each of whose edge is an analytic curve with finite length. Buchner
[
Buchner, 1977
]
later generalized this result to cut locus of a point in higher dimensional manifolds.
4.3.1
, which states that the cut locus of an analytic submanifold (in an analytic manifold) is a simplicial complex, is a natural generalization of Buchner’s result (and its proof). We attribute it to Buchner, although it is not present in the original paper. This analyticity assumption also helps us to compute the homotopy type of the cut locus of a finite set of points in any closed, orientable, real analytic surface of genus
g
(
4.3.4
). In
4.3.3
we make some observations about the cut locus of embedded homology spheres of codimension
2
. This includes the case of real analytic knots in the round sphere
\mathbb{S}^{3}
.

Let
M
be a closed Riemannian manifold and
G
be any compact Lie group acting on
M
freely. Then it is known that
M/G
is a manifold. Further, if the action is isometric, then the metric on
M
induces a metric on
M/G
. If
N
is any
G
-invariant submanifold of
M
, then
N/G
is a submanifold of
M/G
. If the action is isometric, then we provide an equality between
\mathrm{Cu}(N)/G
and
\mathrm{Cu}(N/G)
(
6.1.1
).

Theorem E
.

Let
M
be a closed and connected Riemannian manifold and
G
be any compact Lie group which acts on
M
freely and isometrically. Let
N
be any
G
-invariant closed submanifold of
M
, then we have an equality

\mathrm{Cu}(N)/G=\mathrm{Cu}(N/G).

1.3
Outline of Chapter 2

The majority of this chapter is an overview of recalling some basic results in Riemannian geometry and differential topology. This chapter also deals with some known results for cut locus of a point. Although this chapter may be interesting to read and help clarify the concepts, the experts can skip the details.

§
2.1
Fermi coordinates

Fermi coordinates are important for studying the geometry of submanifolds. In this coordinate system the metric is rectangular and the derivative of metric vanishes at each point of a curve. It makes the calculations much simpler. This section is devoted to recalling the construction of Fermi coordinates in a tubular neighborhood of a submanifold of a Riemannian manifold. This requires us to define the exponential map restricted to the normal bundle. We have recollected some results which will be used to study the distance squared function from a submanifold. For example, it is shown that the distance squared function from a submanifold is sum of squares of Fermi coordinates in a tubular neighborhood of the submanifold.

§
2.2
Morse-Bott theory

In order to study the space via critical points of some real valued function on that space, Morse theory plays an important role. If non-degenerate critical points are replaced by non-degenerate critical submanifolds (see
2.2.5
), then a generalization of Morse theory comes into the picture – Morse-Bott theory. In this section, we have recalled the definition of a Morse function and some examples of Morse functions. In §
2.2.2
we have discussed Morse-Bott theory motivated by an example.

§
2.3
Cut locus and conjugate locus

In a Riemannian manifold
M
a geodesic
\gamma
joining
p,q\in M
is said to be
distance minimal
if
l(\gamma)=d(p,q)
, where
d
is the Riemannian distance. Cut locus of a point captures all points in
M
beyond which geodesics fail to be distance minimal. In §
2.3.1
we have discussed numerous example of cut locus of a point. Characterizations of cut locus has been discussed in terms of conjugate points (points
p
and
q
are said to be conjugate along a geodesic
\gamma
if there exists a non-vanishing Jacobi field vanishes at
p
and
q
) and number of geodesics joining the two points (
2.3.1
). In particular, it says that a cut point is either the first conjugate point or there exists more than one geodesic joining the point and the cut point. We also have a characterization which shows the existence of a closed geodesic (
2.3.6
). One of the result
[
Wolter, 1979
, Theorem 1]
is very important to find the cut points, which says that the cut locus of a point is the closure of points which can be joined by more than one geodesic (
2.3.3
).

Outline of Chapter 3

This chapter serves as a motivation for the results of the subsequent chapters. It includes a detailed discussion of cut locus of submanifolds with numerous examples.

§
3.1
Cut locus of submanifolds

To define cut locus of subset of a Riemannian manifold, one needs to define distance minimal geodesic starting from the subset. This section starts with defining the same (
3.1.1
) and then the cut locus of a subset is similarly.
3.1.9
shows that the cut locus need not be a manifold.
3.1.5
shows that the topological join of
\mathbb{S}^{k}
and
\mathbb{S}^{n-k-1}
is induced from cut locus by showing that
\mathrm{Cu}(\mathbb{S}^{k}_{i})=\mathbb{S}^{n-k-1}_{l}
, where
\mathbb{S}_{i}^{k}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{n}
denote the embedding of the
k
-sphere in the first
k+1
coordinates and
\mathbb{S}^{n-k-1}_{l}
denote the embedding of the
(n-k-1)
-sphere in the last
n-k
coordinates. In §
3.1.1
we have defined the separating set of a subset which consists of all points which have more than one distance minimal geodesic joining the subset. In
3.1.6
we have shown that the cut locus is strictly bigger than the separating set.

§
3.2
An illuminating example

The main aim of this section is to find the cut locus of the set of all
n\times n
orthogonal matrices. We have shown that the cut locus is the set of all singular matrices by showing that it is the separating set. We also analyzed the regularity of distance squared function on the singular set and outside the singular set, set of all invertible matrices. In fact, we have shown that the distance squared function is differentiable at
A
if and only if
A\in GL(n,\mathbb{R})
In this section we have also shown that
GL(n,\mathbb{R})
deforms to the set of all orthogonal matrices, but we noted that this deformation is different from one we obtained via Gram-Schmidt. We will also prove
B
.

4.1
Regularity of distance squared function

This section is motivated by the example of cut locus of
O(n,\mathbb{R})

Lemma

§
4.2
Characterizations of
\mathrm{Cu}(N)

We have discussed two characterizations of cut locus. One in terms of first focal points (
4.2.2
) and number of geodesics joining the submanifold to the cut points (
4.2.1
) and other is in terms of separating set (
4.2.2
). The latter one is important for computation viewpoint. Let
\nu
denotes the normal bundle of
N
and
S(\nu)
be the unit sphere bundle. Consider a map

\displaystyle\rho:S(\nu)\to[0,\infty),

\displaystyle v\mapsto\sup\{t\in[0,\infty):\gamma_{v}|_{[0,t]}\text{ is a distance minimal geodesic from $N$}\}{\@index{\@indexphrase{$\rho$}}}

where
\gamma_{v}
means
\gamma^{\prime}(0)=v
(also see (
4.4
)).

Theorem
.

Let
u\in S(\nu)
. A positive real number
T
is
\rho(u)
if and only if
\gamma_{u}:[0,T]
is a distance minimal geodesic from
N
and at least one of the following holds:

(i)

\gamma_{u}(T)
is the first focal point of
N
along
\gamma_{u}
,

(ii)

there exists
v\in S(\nu)
with
v\neq u
such that
\gamma_{v}(T)=\gamma_{u}(T)
.

Theorem
.

Let
\mathrm{Cu}(N)
be the cut locus of a compact submanifold
N
of a complete Riemannian manifold
M
. The subset
\mathrm{Se}(N)
, the set of all points in
M
which can be joined by at least two distance minimal geodesic starting from
N
, of
\mathrm{Cu}(N)
is dense in
\mathrm{Cu}(N)
.

[

]

Outline of Chapter 5

We apply our study of gradient of distance squared function to two families of Lie groups -

and

and
\tilde{X}(d)\vcentcolon=\pi^{-1}(X(d))

.

The cut locus of

\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{\star(n+1)}\times_{\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\mathbb{S}^{1}

2.1
Fermi coordinates

In this section we give a brief overview of the Fermi coordinates which are generalizations of normal coordinates in Riemannian geometry. To study the distance squared function from a submanifold
N
of a Riemannian manifold
M
, it is essential to analyze the local geometry of
M
around
N
. For this the Fermi coordinates are the most convenient tool. In 1922, Enrico Fermi
[
Fermi, 1922
]
came up with a coordinate system in which the Christoffel symbols vanish along geodesics which makes the metric simpler. For an extensive reading we refer to the book
[
Gray, 2004
, Chapter 2]
and an article
[
Manasse and Misner, 1963
]
.

2.1.1
Normal exponential map

Let
N
be an embedded submanifold of a Riemannian manifold
M
. We define the
normal bundle
, denoted by
\nu
,

\nu\vcentcolon=\left\{(p,v):p\in N\text{ and }v\in\left(T_{p}N\right)^{\perp}\right\},{\@index{\@indexphrase{$\nu$}}}

where
(T_{p}N)^{\perp}
is the orthogonal complement of
T_{p}N
. Indeed,
\nu
is a subbundle of the restriction of the tangent bundle
TM
to
N
. We can restrict the usual exponential map of the Riemannian manifold to the normal bundle to define the exponential map of the normal bundle. We define the
exponential map of the normal bundle
as follows:

\exp_{\nu}:\nu\to M,~(p,v)\mapsto\exp_{p}(v),

(2.1)

where
\exp_{p}:T_{p}M\to M
is the exponential map of
M
. We may write
\exp_{\nu}(v)
in short and call this the
normal exponential map
. Note that we can identify
N
as the zero section of the normal bundle and hence
N
can be assumed to be submanifold of
\nu
.

Lemma 2.1.1
.

[
Gray, 2004
, Lemma 2.3]

Let
M
be a Riemannian manifold and
N
be any embedded submanifold. Then the normal exponential map
\exp_{\nu}:\nu\to M
is a diffeomorphism from a neighbourhood of
N\subseteq\nu
onto a neighbourhood of
N\subseteq M
.

Using the above lemma, let
\mathcal{U}_{N}
be the largest open neighbourhood of
N\subseteq\nu
for which
\exp_{\nu}
is a diffeomorphism. We shall later be able to describe this neighbourhood in terms of a function
\rho
(
4.4
). We now ready to define the Fermi coordinates.

2.1.2
Fermi coordinate system

To define a system of Fermi coordinates, we need an arbitrary system of coordinates
\left(y_{1},\cdots,y_{k}\right)
defined in a neighborhood
\mathcal{O}\subseteq N
of
p\in N
together with orthogonal sections
\mathcal{E}_{k+1},\cdots,\mathcal{E}_{n}
of the restriction on
\nu
to
\mathcal{O}
.

Definition 2.1.1

(Fermi coordinates)
.

The
Fermi coordinates

\left(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\right)
of
N\subseteq M
centered at
p
(relative to a given coordinate
\left(y_{1},\cdots,y_{k}\right)
on
N
and given orthogonal sections
\mathcal{E}_{k+1},\cdots,\mathcal{E}_{n}
of

) are defined by

\displaystyle x_{l}\left(\exp_{\nu}\bigg(\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}\tau_{i}\mathcal{E}_{i}\left(p^{\prime}\right)\bigg)\right)=y_{l}\left(p^{\prime}\right),~l=1,\cdots,k

\displaystyle x_{m}\left(\exp_{\nu}\bigg(\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}\tau_{i}\mathcal{E}_{i}\left(p^{\prime}\right)\bigg)\right)=\tau_{m},~m=k+1,\cdots,n

for
p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}
provided the numbers
\tau_{k+1},\cdots,\tau_{n}
are small enough so that
\tau_{k+1}\mathcal{E}_{k+1}\left(p^{\prime}\right)+\cdots+\tau_{n}\mathcal{E}_{n}\left(p^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{U}_{N}
.

As the normal exponential map is a diffeomorphism on the set
~\mathcal{U}_{N}
,
\left(x_{1},\cdots,x_{k},x_{k+1}\right.

\left.,\cdots,x_{n}\right)
defines a coordinate system near
p
. In fact, the restrictions to

of coordinate vector fields
\allowbreak\partial/\partial x_{k+1},\ldots,\partial/\partial x_{n}
are orthonormal.

Lemma 2.1.2
.

Let
\gamma
be a unit speed geodesic normal to
N
with
\gamma(0)=p\in N
. If
v=\gamma^{\prime}(0)
, then there exists a system of Fermi coordinates
(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
such that whenever
(p,tv)\in\mathcal{U}_{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\kern 28.45274pt\left.\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{k+1}}\right|_{\gamma(t)}=\gamma^{\prime}(t),

\displaystyle\left.\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{l}}\right|_{p}\in T_{p}N,\text{ and }\left.\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\right|_{p}\in(T_{p}N)^{\perp}

for
1\leq l\leq k
and
k+1\leq i\leq n.
Furthermore, for
1\leq j\leq n

(x_{j}\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}}\gamma)(t)=t\delta_{j(k+1)}.

The following object will be useful while studying the distance squared function from a submanifold
N
.

Definition 2.1.2
.

Let
N
be a submanifold of a Riemannian manifold and let
(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
be a system of Fermi coordinates for
N
. We define
\Delta(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
to be the non-negative number satisfying

\Delta^{2}=\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}.

Lemma 2.1.3
.

Let
p\in N
. The
\Delta
is independent of the choice of Fermi coordinates at
p
.

Proof.

Let
(x_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,x_{n}^{\prime})
be another system of Fermi coordinates at
p
, and let
\left\{\mathcal{E}_{k+1}^{\prime},\cdots,\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\prime}\right\}
be the orthonormal sections of
\nu
that give rise to it. We can write

\mathcal{E}_{j}^{\prime}=\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}a_{ij}\mathcal{E}_{i}

where
(a_{ij})
is a matrix of functions in the orthogonal group
O(n-k)
with each
a_{ji}
being a smooth function on
N.
Now,

\displaystyle x_{m}\left(\exp_{\nu}\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}\tau_{j}^{\prime}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathcal{E}_{j}^{\prime}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=x_{m}\left(\exp_{\nu}\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}\tau_{j}^{\prime}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}a_{ij}\mathcal{E}_{i}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=x_{m}\left(\exp_{\nu}\bigg(\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}\bigg(\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}a_{ij}\tau_{j}^{\prime}\bigg)\mathcal{E}_{i}\bigg)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=k+1}^{n}a_{ml}{\color[rgb]{0.0234,0.6406,0.7031}\tau_{l}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=k+1}^{n}a_{ml}{\color[rgb]{0.0234,0.6406,0.7031}x_{l}^{\prime}\left(\exp_{\nu}\bigg(\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}\tau_{j}^{\prime}\mathcal{E}_{j}^{\prime}\bigg)\right)}.

Therefore, we have

x_{m}=\sum_{l=k+1}^{n}a_{ml}x_{l}^{\prime},~m=k+1,\cdots,n.

(2.2)

Now consider,

\displaystyle\sum_{m=k+1}^{n}x_{m}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=k+1}^{n}\left(\sum_{l=k+1}^{n}a_{ml}x_{l}^{\prime}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=k+1}^{n}\left(\sum_{l=k+1}^{n}\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}(a_{ml}x_{l}^{\prime})(a_{mj}x_{j}^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=k+1}^{n}\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}\left(\sum_{m=k+1}^{n}a_{ml}a_{mj}\right)x_{l}^{\prime}x_{j}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=k+1}^{n}\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}\delta_{lj}x_{l}^{\prime}x_{j}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=k+1}^{n}\left(x_{m}^{\prime}\right)^{2}.

∎

2.2
Morse-Bott theory

This section will be devoted to a generalization of Morse function in which we study the space by looking at the critical points of a smooth real valued function. We will briefly recall Morse functions with a couple of examples, and then we will define Morse-Bott functions. The reference for this section will be the original article by Raoul Bott
[
Bott, 1954
]
and the book
[
Banyaga and Hurtubise, 2004
, Section 3.5]
.

2.2.1
Morse functions

Broadly the “functions” and “spaces” are objects of study in analysis and geometry respectively. However, these two objects are related to each other. For example, on a line we can have functions like
f(x)=x,~g(x)=x^{2}
which takes arbitrarily large values, whereas on the circle there does not exist any function which takes arbitrarily large value. In this way, we are able to differentiate circles with lines by seeing functions on them. Morse theory studies relations between shape of space and function defined on this space. We study the critical points of a function defined on spaces to find out information on the space. More specifically, in Morse theory we study the topology of smooth manifolds by analyzing the critical point of a smooth real valued function. If
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function on a smooth manifold
M
, then using Morse theory we can find a CW-complex which is homotopy equivalent to
M
and the CW-complex has one cell for each critical point of
f
. For a detailed study of Morse theory we refer to the book
[
Milnor, 1963
]
by John Milnor.

Definition 2.2.1

(Critical Points)
.

Let
M
and
N
be two smooth manifolds of dimension
m
and
n
respectively. A point
p\in M
is said to be
critical point
of a smooth function
f:M\to N
if the differential map

df_{p}:T_{p}M\to T_{f(p)}N{\@index{\@indexphrase{$df_{p}$}}}

does not have full rank.

We confine our study to real-valued functions. In this case the above is equivalent to
df_{p}\equiv 0
. In a coordinate neighborhood
(\phi=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}),U)
around
p
, we have

\dfrac{\partial(f\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}}\phi^{-1})}{\partial x_{j}}(\phi(p))=0,~j=1,\cdots,n.

(2.3)

At a critical point of
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
, we define the Hessian which is similar to the second derivative of the function.

Definition 2.2.2

(Hessian of
f
at
p
)
.

Let
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
be any smooth real valued function and
p
be any critical point of
f
. The
Hessian of
f
at
p
is the map

\mathrm{Hess}_{p}(f):T_{p}M\times T_{p}M\to\mathbb{R},~\mathrm{Hess}_{p}(f)(V,W)=\tilde{V}\cdot\left(\tilde{W}\cdot f\right)(p),{\@index{\@indexphrase{$\mathrm{Hess}_{p}(f)$}}}

(2.4)

where
\tilde{V}
and
\tilde{W}
are any extensions of
V
and
W
respectively.

Note that the Hessian is a bilinear form of
V
and
W
. Consider

\displaystyle V\cdot\left(\tilde{W}\cdot f\right)(p)-W\cdot\left(\tilde{V}\cdot f\right)(p)

\displaystyle=\left[\tilde{V},\tilde{W}\right]_{p}\cdot f

\displaystyle=df_{p}\left(\left[\tilde{V},\tilde{W}\right]_{p}\right)

\displaystyle=0.

Thus, Hessian is a symmetric bilinear form on
T_{p}M\times T_{p}M
. The above computation, in particular, also proves that the definition is well defined, that is, it is independent of the choice of extension.

Any critical point is categorized by looking at the value of Hessian at that point.

Definition 2.2.3
.

A critical point
p\in M
of a smooth function
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
non-degenerate
if the Hessian is non-degenerate. Otherwise, we call
p
to be a
degenerate
critical point. The
index
of a non-degenerate critical point
p
is the dimension of the subspace of the maximum dimension on which
\mathrm{Hess}_{p}f
is negative definite.

For example, the function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}~x\mapsto x^{2}
has
0
a critical point which is non-degenerate but
0
is the degenerate critical point of the function
f(x)=x^{3}
.

Definition 2.2.4
.

A smooth function
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be a
Morse function
if all its critical points are non-degenerate.

Example 2.2.1
.

The function

f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R},~(x,y)\mapsto x^{2}-3xy^{2}

is not a Morse function, as the critical point
(0,0)
is not non-degenerate.

(Height function on sphere)
.

The height function on the

2.2.2
Morse-Bott functions

Morse-Bott functions are generalizations of Morse functions where we are allowed to have critical set need not be isolated but may form a submanifold. For example, let a torus be kept horizontally (a donut is kept in a plate). If
f
is the height function on the torus, then there are two critical submanifolds, the top and bottom circles.

Let
M
be a Riemannian manifold and
f
be any real valued smooth function on
M
. Let
\mathrm{Cr}(f)
denotes the set of all critical points of
f
and
N
be any submanifold of
M
which is contained in
\mathrm{Cr}(f)
. For any point
p\in M
we have the following decomposition:

T_{p}M=T_{p}N\oplus\nu_{p}N,

where
\nu_{p}N
is the normal bundle at
p
. Note that if
p\in N
then for any
V\in T_{p}N
and
W\in T_{p}M
the Hessian vanishes, i.e.,
\mathrm{Hess}_{p}(f)(V,W)=0
. Therefore,
\mathrm{Hess}_{p}(f)
induces a symmetric bilinear form on
\nu_{p}N
. Now we can define non-degenerate critical submanifold similar to the non-degenerate critical points.

Definition 2.2.5

(Non-degenerate critical submanifold)
.

Let
N\subset M
be a submanifold of a Riemannian manifold
M
. Then
N
is said to be
non-degenerate critical submanifold
of
f
if
N\subseteq\mathrm{Cr}(f)
and for any
p\in N
the Hessian,
\mathrm{Hess}_{p}(f)
is non-degenerate in the direction normal to
N
at
p
.

In the above definition, by
\mathrm{Hess}_{p}(f)
is non-degenerate in the direction normal to
N
at
p
we mean that for any
V\in\nu_{p}N
there exists
W\in\nu_{p}N
such that
\mathrm{Hess}_{p}(f)(V,W)\neq 0
.

Definition 2.2.6

(Morse-Bott functions)
.

The function
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
Morse-Bott
if the connected components of
\mathrm{Cr}(f)
are non-degenerate critical submanifolds.

Example 2.2.4
.

Let
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
be a Morse function. Then the critical submanifolds are zero-dimensional and hence the Hessian
\mathrm{Hess}_{p}(f)
at any critical point
p
is non-degenerate in every direction as all the directions are normal. So
f
is Morse-Bott with critical submanifolds as critical points.

Example 2.2.5
.

Any constant function defined on a smooth manifold
M
is a Morse-Bott function with critical submanifold
M
.

Example 2.2.6
.

Let
M=\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Define

f:M\to\mathbb{R},~(x,y)\mapsto x^{4}.

Then the derivative map

df_{(x,y)}=\left(4x^{3},0\right)=(0,0)\implies x=0.

Thus, the critical set is
\{(x,y):x=0\}
which is
y

(0,y)
will be

\displaystyle\mathrm{Hess}_{(0,y)}(f)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

which is degenerate in every direction and hence it is not a Morse-Bott function.

Let

\displaystyle d\phi_{1_{\mathbf{p}}}\left(\mathbf{v}_{2}\right)=\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\phi_{1}\circ\eta\right)(t)\big|_{t=0}=(-x,-y).

So, we can define an isomorphism between

Now note that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Hess}_{\mathbf{p}}((-x,-y),(-x,-y))

\displaystyle=(-x,-y)\begin{pmatrix}2x^{2}&2xy\\
2xy&2y^{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-x\\
-y\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=(-x,-y)\begin{pmatrix}-2x^{3}&-2xy^{2}\\

\displaystyle=2x^{4}+2x^{2}y^{2}+2x^{2}y^{2}+2y^{4}

\displaystyle=2\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)^{2}=2.

Thus, Hessian is non-degenerate in the normal direction and hence it is a Morse-Bott functions.

The above example, in particular, shows that the critical submanifolds may have different dimensions.

2.3
Cut locus and conjugate locus

Let
M
be a complete Riemannian manifold and
p\in M
. Let
\gamma
be a geodesic such that
\gamma(0)=p
. A
cut point
of
p
along the geodesic
\gamma
is the first point
q
on
\gamma
such that for any point
\tilde{q}
on
\gamma
beyond
q
, there exists a geodesic
\tilde{\gamma}
joining
p
to
\tilde{q}
such that
l\left(\tilde{\gamma}\right)<l(\gamma)
, where
l(\gamma)
is the length of
\gamma
. In simple words,
q
is the first point beyond which
\gamma
stops to minimize the distance. In this section we will recall the definition of cut locus of a point with some examples. We will also mention some important results which will be generalized in the upcoming chapters. The main references for this section are books
[
Sakai, 1996
, Chapter 3, Section 4]
and
[
Cheeger and Ebin, 1975
, Chapter 5]
.

2.3.1
Cut locus of a point

Let
M
be a Riemannian manifold and
p,q\in M
be two points. If there exists a piecewise differentiable curve joining them, then using the Riemannian metric we can measure the length of the curve. We now consider all possible curves joining these points. Then the distance between
p
and
q
is the infimum of the length of all (piecewise differentiable) curves joining
p
and
q
. This distance induces a metric. We call
M
to be
complete Riemannian manifold
if
(M,d)
is a complete metric space. From now onwards, we always consider
M
to be a complete Riemannian manifold. A geodesic
\gamma(t),~t\in[a,b]
is said to be
extendable
if it can be extended to a geodesic
\gamma(t),~t\in[c,d]\supsetneq[a,b]
. A Riemannian manifold is said to be
geodesically complete
if any geodesic can be extendable for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then the Hopf-Rinow Theorem
[
Hopf and Rinow, 1931
]
says that these two notions of completeness are equivalent. If a manifold is not complete, then we can not always extend a geodesic. For example,
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
is not complete and the geodesic
\gamma(t)=t,~t>0
is not extendable in the negative
x
-axis. This problem does not arise if the manifold is complete. The more is true which says that
M
is complete if and only if every geodesic can be extended for infinite time. The completeness of
M
also guarantees that any two points can be joined by a distance minimal geodesic which is defined as follows.

Definition 2.3.1

(Distance minimal geodesic)
.

A geodesic joining
p
and
q
is said to be
distance minimal
if the length of the geodesic is equal to the distance between these points, i.e.,
l(\gamma)=d(p,q)
.

We shall now define the cut locus,
\mathrm{Cu}(p)
of a point
p
in a complete Riemannian manifold
M
. The notion of cut locus was first introduced for convex surfaces by Henri Poincaré
[
Poincaré, 1905
]
in 1905 under the name
la ligne de partage
meaning
the dividing line
.

Definition 2.3.2

(Cut locus of a point)
.

Let
M
be a complete Riemannian manifold and

. If

of

to

Consider the set

If

is the cut point of

along

, and if

, then the point

.

Note that if

2.3.2
Conjugate locus of a point

Let
M
be a Riemannian manifold and
\gamma
be any curve defined on
[a,b]
. A
variation of
\gamma
is a function
\Gamma:[a,b]\times(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to M
such that
\Gamma(t,0)=\gamma(t)
. So
\Gamma
is a one-parameter family of curves
\gamma_{s}(t)\vcentcolon=\Gamma(t,s)
. If each of
\gamma_{s}
is a geodesic, then we call it is a
geodesic variation
. In this section by variation we mean the geodesic variation. The variation field
\dfrac{\partial\Gamma}{\partial s}(t,0)
is called a
Jacobi field
and we will denote it by
J(t)
.

Let
\gamma
be a geodesic. We say that a point
q\in M
on
\gamma
is
conjugate to
p\in M
if we can find a variation
\gamma_{s}
of
\gamma
such that
\gamma_{s}(0)=p
for
s\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
and each of the geodesic
\gamma_{s}
meet
infinitesimally
at
q
. That is, if
\gamma(t_{0})=q
, then

\left.\dfrac{\partial\gamma_{s}}{\partial t}\right|_{(t,s)=(0,0)}=0=\left.\dfrac{\partial\gamma_{s}}{\partial t}\right|_{(t,s)=(t_{0},0)}.

The conjugate points can be defined in two more equivalent ways. One of them uses the Jacobi field and other uses the exponential map. Recall that a Jacobi field along a geodesic
\gamma
satisfies

\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}}\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}}J+R\left(\dot{\gamma},J\right)\dot{\gamma}=0,

where
R
is the Riemann curvature tensor.

Definition 2.3.3

(Conjugate points in terms of Jacobi fields)
.

A point
p
is said to be
conjugate to q along a geodesic
\gamma
if there exists a non-vanishing Jacobi field along
\gamma
which vanishes at
p
and
q
.

Figure 2.13
:
p
and
q
are conjugate to each other along
\gamma

Definition 2.3.4

(Conjugate points in terms of exponential map)
.

For a point
p\in M
we say that
\mathbf{v}\in T_{p}M
is a
tangent conjugate point of
p
if the derivative of the exponential map is singular at
\mathbf{v}
i.e.,
\det\left(d\left(\exp_{p}\right)_{\mathbf{v}}\right)=0
. The point
q\vcentcolon=\exp_{p}(\mathbf{v})
is said to be
conjugate point of
p
along the geodesic
\gamma(t)=\exp_{p}(t\mathbf{v})
.

For a proof of equivalence of these three definitions we refer the reader to books on Riemannian geometry, for example see
[
do Carmo, 1992
]
.

The
multiplicity of a conjugate point
is defined to be the nullity of
d(\exp_{p})_{\mathbf{v}}
. If nullity is one, then we say it is first conjugate point.

Example 2.3.6
.

Let
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the Euclidean metric, then there are no conjugate points along any geodesic.

Example 2.3.7
.

If
M=\mathbb{S}^{n}
with the round metric, then any antipodal points are conjugate to each other along any great circles. In particular, south pole and north poles are conjugate to each other.

Example 2.3.8
.

If
M
is a flat torus, then there are no conjugate points along any geodesic. Recall that metric is flat if and only if the Riemann curvature tensor
R
vanishes, which implies the Jacobi field is affine and vanishes at two points forced it to be zero everywhere. This example, in particular, proves that if
M
is flat, then there are no conjugate points along any geodesic.

Example 2.3.9
.

Let
M
be the real projective plane,
\mathbb{RP}^{n}
, with the metric induced from
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. Here any point
p
is conjugate to itself along any geodesic.

2.3.3
Some results involving cut and conjugate locus

We will present some results related to the two concepts. As most of the results are standard, we will not provide proofs. Instead, we will mention references for each.

The following result is one of the most important characterization of cut locus in terms of first conjugate point.

Theorem 2.3.1
.

[
Sakai, 1996
, Chapter 3, Proposition 4.1]

Let
\gamma
be a unit speed geodesic. Then
q=\gamma(t_{0})
is a cut point of
p=\gamma(0)
along
\gamma
if either of the following holds.

(i)

The point
q=\gamma(t_{0})
is the first conjugate point of
p
along
\gamma
.

(ii)

There exists at least two distance minimal geodesic joining
p
to
q
.

The next result is also a relation between the two loci. In particular, it states that the cut point of
p
always comes before (if not the same) the conjugate point.

Theorem 2.3.2
.

[
Kobayashi, 1967
, Theorem 4.1]

Let
\gamma
be a unit speed geodesic starting at
p
. Let
q=\gamma(t_{0})
be the first conjugate point along
\gamma
. Then
\gamma
is not a distance minimal geodesic beyond
q
.

There is one more characterization of the cut locus in terms of number of geodesics joining the point to the cut point. For
p\in M
we define the set
\mathrm{Se}(p)
as

\mathrm{Se}(p)\vcentcolon=\left\{q\in M~\middle|~\begin{aligned} &\text{ there exists at least two distance }\\
&\text{minimal geodesics joining $p$ to $q$}\end{aligned}\right\}

(2.5)

Note that
\mathrm{Se}(p)\subseteq\mathrm{Cu}(p)
. Franz-Erich Wolter in 1979 showed that the closure of
\mathrm{Se}(p)
is the cut locus.

Theorem 2.3.3
.

[
Wolter, 1979
, Theorem 1]

Let
M
be a complete Riemannian manifold and
p
be any point in
M
. Then

\overline{\mathrm{Se}(p)}=\mathrm{Cu}(p).

The above theorem, in particular, shows that the cut locus of a point is a closed set. He also proved that the distance squared function from the point
p
is not differentiable on the set
\mathrm{Se}
.

Theorem 2.3.4
.

[
Wolter, 1979
, Lemma 1]

Let
d^{2}(p,\cdot)
denotes the square of the distance from the point
p
. Let
q\in\mathrm{Se}(p)
and let
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
be two distance minimal geodesics joining
p
to
q
. Then the directional derivative of
d^{2}(p,\cdot)
does not exist at
q
in the direction of
\gamma_{i},~i=1,2
.

For some special point of the cut locus of
p
we can improve
2.3.1
.

Theorem 2.3.5
.

[
Kobayashi, 1967
, Theorem 4.4]

Let
q
be a cut point of
p
and we assume that it is the closest point of
p
. Then
q
is either conjugate to
p
along a minimal geodesic joining these two points, or
q
is the mid-point of a closed geodesic starting and ending at
p
.

We can even make the above theorem sharper if we provide an additional condition.

Theorem 2.3.6
.

[
Kobayashi, 1967
, Theorem 4.5]

Let
p
be any point in
M
such that
d(p,\mathrm{Cu}(p))
is the smallest and
q
be any cut point closest to
p
. Then either
q
is conjugate to
p
with respect to a distance minimal geodesic joining
p
and
q
or
q
is the mid-point of a closed geodesic starting and ending smoothly at
p
.

Chapter 3
Cut locus of a submanifold

The cut locus of a point plays an important role in analyzing the local structure of a Riemannian manifold
M
. In the last chapter we had studied cut locus of a point, conjugate locus and some of their properties. Similarly, one can ask about the notion of cut locus for a non-empty subset of
M
. In order to give a similar definition, we need to first define distance minimal geodesics joining a point
p\in M
to a subset
N\subseteq M
. If
q\in N
such that the geodesic is distance minimal geodesic joining
p
and
q
, and it minimizes the distance between the set
N
and the point
p
then we call such a geodesic a distance minimal geodesic. Now the cut locus of
N
consists of all points
q\in M
such that there exists a distance minimal geodesic which fails to be minimal beyond
q
. Conjugate locus is termed as focal locus if we replace point with submanifold. In this chapter we will study the cut locus of a subset, in particular, a submanifold. We will motivate the results based on some examples and the proofs will be discussed in the subsequent chapters.

3.1
Cut locus of submanifolds

In order to have a definition of the cut locus for a submanifold (or a subset), we need to generalize the notion of a minimal geodesic.

Definition 3.1.1
.

A geodesic
\gamma
is called a
distance minimal geodesic
joining
N
to
p
if there exists
q\in N
such that
\gamma
is a minimal geodesic joining
q
to
p
and
l(\gamma)=d(p,N)
. We will refer to such geodesics as
N
-geodesics
.

If
N
is an embedded submanifold, then an
N
-geodesic is necessarily orthogonal to
N
. This follows from the first variational principle. We are ready to define the cut locus for
N\subset M
.

Definition 3.1.2

(Cut locus of a subset)
.

Let
M
be a Riemannian manifold and
N
be any non-empty subset of
M.
If
\mathrm{Cu}(N)
denotes the
cut locus of
N
, then we say that
q\in\mathrm{Cu}(N)
if and only if there exists a distance minimal geodesic joining
N
to
q
such that any extension of it beyond
q
is not a distance minimal geodesic.

Separating set

In all the examples in the previous section, we observed the cut locus of any submanifold

Let

3.2
An illuminating example

Let
M=M(n,\mathbb{R})
be the set of
n\times n
matrices, and
N=O(n,\mathbb{R})
be the set of all orthogonal
n\times n
matrices. Let
A,B\in M(n,\mathbb{R})
. We fix the standard flat Euclidean metric on
M(n,\mathbb{R})
by identifying it with
\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
. This induces a distance function given by

d(A,B)\vcentcolon=\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}\left((A-B)^{T}(A-B)\right)}

Consider the distance squared function

f:GL(n,\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R},~~A\mapsto d^{2}(A,O(n,\mathbb{R})).

In order to study this function, we want a closed formula for it.

Lemma 3.2.1
.

The function
f
can be explicitly expressed as

f(A)=n+\mathrm{tr}\left(A^{T}A\right)-2\mathrm{tr}\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right).

(3.1)

Proof.

Let
A\in GL(n,\mathbb{R})
be any invertible matrix. Then,

\displaystyle f(A)

\displaystyle=\inf_{B\in O(n,\mathbb{R})}d^{2}(A,B)

\displaystyle=\inf_{B\in O(n,\mathbb{R})}\|A-B\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\inf_{B\in O(n,\mathbb{R})}\mathrm{tr}\left((A-B)^{T}(A-B)\right)

\displaystyle=\inf_{B\in O(n,\mathbb{R})}\mathrm{tr}\left(A^{T}A-A^{T}B-B^{T}A+B^{T}B\right)

\displaystyle=\inf_{B\in O(n,\mathbb{R})}\big[\mathrm{tr}\left(A^{T}A\right)-\mathrm{tr}\left(A^{T}B\right)-\mathrm{tr}\left(B^{T}A\right)+\mathrm{tr}\left(B^{T}B\right)\big]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(A^{T}A\right)+\inf_{B\in O(n,\mathbb{R})}\big[-2\mathrm{tr}\left(A^{T}B\right)\big]+n

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(A^{T}A\right)-2\sup_{B\in O(n,\mathbb{R})}\mathrm{tr}\left(A^{T}B\right)+n.

(3.2)

The problem of computing
f(A)
is equivalent to maximizing the function

h_{A}:O(n,\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R},B\mapsto\mathrm{tr}\left(A^{T}B\right).

Case I:

A
is a diagonal matrix with positive entries. Then,

\displaystyle\left|h_{A}(B)\right|=\left|\mathrm{tr}\left(A^{T}B\right)\right|=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii}b_{ii}\right|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|a_{ii}b_{ii}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii}=\mathrm{tr}\left(A^{T}\right)=h_{A}(I).

Thus, one of the maximizer is
B=I.

Case II:

For any non-singular matrix
A
, we will use the
singular value decomposition
(SVD). Write
A=UDV^{T}
, where
U
and
V
are
n\times n
orthogonal matrices and
D
is a diagonal matrix with positive entries. For any
B\in O(n,\mathbb{R})
using the cyclic property of the trace we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left(A^{T}B\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(VDU^{T}B\right)=\mathrm{tr}\left(D(U^{T}BV)\right).

(3.3)

Since
U^{T}BV
is an orthogonal matrix, maximizing over
B
reduces to the earlier observation that
B
will be a maximizer if
U^{T}BV=I
, which implies
B=UV^{T}
.

Since
A
is invertible, by the polar decomposition, there exists an orthogonal matrix
Q
and a symmetric positive definite matrix
S=\sqrt{A^{T}A}
such that
A=QS
. As
S
is symmetric matrix we can diagonalize it, that is,
S=P\tilde{D}P^{T}
, where
P\in O(n,\mathbb{R})
and
\tilde{D}
is a diagonal matrix. Thus,

A=QS=QP\tilde{D}P^{T}.

Set
U=QP
,
V=P
to obtain the SVD of
A
. In particular, the minimizer is given by

B=Q=A\big(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\big)^{-1}.

Therefore,

f(A)=n+\mathrm{tr}\left(A^{T}A\right)-2\,\mathrm{tr}\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)

for invertible matrices.

To find out
f(A)
for a non-invertible matrix
A
, we note that
GL(n,\mathbb{R})
is dense in
M(n,\mathbb{R})
and that
\sqrt{A^{T}A}
is well-defined for
A\in M(n,\mathbb{R})
. The continuity of the map
A\mapsto\sqrt{A^{T}A}
on
M(n,\mathbb{R})
implies that the same formula (
3.1
) for
f
applies to
A
as well.
∎

In order to understand the differentiability of
f
, it suffices to analyze the function
A\mapsto\mathrm{tr}\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)
.

Lemma 3.2.2
.

The map
g:M(n,\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R},~A\mapsto\mathrm{tr}\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)
is differentiable if and only if
A
is invertible.

Proof.

Let
A
be an invertible matrix. We will prove that the function
g
is differentiable at
A
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of all positive definite matrices which is an open subset of the set of all symmetric matrices
\mathcal{S}
. We will prove that the map

r:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{P},~A\mapsto\sqrt{A}

is differentiable. Define a function

s:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{P},~A\mapsto A^{2}.

We will show that
s
is a diffeomorphism and from the inverse function theorem
r
will be differentiable. In order to show that
s
is a diffeomorphism, we claim that for
A\in\mathcal{P},~ds_{A}:T_{A}\mathcal{P}\to T_{A^{2}}\mathcal{P}
is injective. Note that
\mathcal{P}
is an open subset of a vector space
\mathcal{S}
and therefore,
T_{A}\mathcal{P}\cong\mathcal{S}\cong T_{A^{2}}\mathcal{P}.
So, take
B\in\mathcal{S}
such that
ds_{A}(B)=0
. We will show that
B=0.
Recall that
ds_{A}(B)=AB+BA.
Now choose an orthonormal basis
\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{v}_{n}\}
of eigenspace of
A
and
A\mathbf{v}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}
(
\lambda_{i}>0
). Then,

A(B\mathbf{v}_{i})=-BA\mathbf{v}_{i}=-B\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}=-\lambda i(B\mathbf{v}_{i})

which implies
Bv_{i}
is also an eigenvector of
A
with eigenvalue
-\lambda_{i}<0
. Hence,
Bv_{i}=0
which implies
B=0
.

For the converse, we will show that if
A
is a singular matrix, then the map
g
is not directional differentiable. Let
A
be a singular matrix. Using the singular value decomposition, we write

A=U\begin{pmatrix}D&0\\
0&0_{k}\end{pmatrix}V^{T},

where
D
is a
(n-k)\times(n-k)
diagonal matrix with positive entries. If

B=U\begin{pmatrix}0_{n-k}&0\\
0&I_{k}\end{pmatrix}

then we claim that
g
is not differentiable in the direction of
B
. Since

\sqrt{(A+tB)^{T}(A+tB)}=V\begin{pmatrix}D&0\\
0&I_{k}|t|\end{pmatrix}V^{T}

the limit

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\dfrac{g(A+tB)-g(A)}{t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0}\dfrac{\mathrm{tr}\left(V\begin{pmatrix}D&0\\
0&I_{k}|t|\end{pmatrix}V^{T}\right)-\mathrm{tr}\left(V\begin{pmatrix}D&0\\
0&0_{k}\end{pmatrix}V^{T}\right)}{t}

\displaystyle=k\lim_{t\to 0}\dfrac{|t|}{t}

does not exist and hence the function
g
is not differentiable.
∎

Proposition 3.2.1
.

If
A
is an invertible matrix, then

dg_{A}(H)=\left\langle A\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)^{-1},H\right\rangle,

(3.4)

where
H
is a symmetric matrix of order
n
.

The following lemma along with chain rule will prove the above proposition.

Lemma 3.2.3
.

Let
A
be a positive definite matrix and
\psi:A\mapsto\sqrt{A}
. Then

d\psi_{A}(H)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t\sqrt{A}}He^{-t\sqrt{A}}~dt,

for any symmetric matrix
H.

Proof.

As
\psi(A)\cdot\psi(A)=A
, differentiating at
A
we obtain

d\psi_{A}(H)\psi(A)+\psi(A)d\psi_{A}(H)=H.

(3.5)

We will show the following:

(i)

For any positive definite matrix
X
and for any symmetric matrix
Y
the integral

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tX}Ye^{-tX}~\mathrm{d}t

(3.6)

converges. We note that the eigenvalues of
e^{-tX}
are
e^{-t\lambda_{j}}
, where
\lambda_{j}
are the eigenvalues of
X
. Since
X
is a positive definite matrix, each of the
\lambda_{j}
is positive. Without loss of generality, we assume that
\lambda=\lambda_{1}
is the smallest eigenvalue of
X
. Then we have

\displaystyle e^{-t\lambda_{j}}\leq e^{-t\lambda}

\displaystyle\implies\left\|e^{-tX}\right\|=e^{-t\lambda}.

where
\|\cdot\|
is the operator norm. Therefore, the operator norm of the integrand in (
3.6
) is bounded by
2e^{-t\lambda}\|Y\|
, which is an integrable function. Hence, the integral given by (
3.6
) converges.

(ii)

The matrix
d\psi_{A}(H)
satisfies (
3.5
). Observe that

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t\sqrt{A}}\cdot H\cdot e^{-t\sqrt{A}}\mathrm{d}t\right)\sqrt{A}+\sqrt{A}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t\sqrt{A}}\cdot H\cdot e^{-t\sqrt{A}}~\mathrm{d}t\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(e^{-t\sqrt{A}}\cdot H\cdot e^{-t\sqrt{A}}\sqrt{A}+\sqrt{A}e^{-t\sqrt{A}}\cdot H\cdot e^{-t\sqrt{A}}\right)~\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}-\left(e^{-t\sqrt{A}}He^{-t\sqrt{A}}\right)^{\prime}~\mathrm{d}t=H.

From (i), (ii) and the uniqueness of the derivative, the lemma is proved.
∎

We now give a proof of
3.2.1
.

Proof of
3.2.1
.

Note that using
Lemma

3.2.3
, for any symmetric matrix
H
we have

dg_{A}(H)=\mathrm{tr}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t\sqrt{A^{T}A}}\left(A^{T}H+H^{T}A\right)e^{-t\sqrt{A^{T}A}}~dt\right).

(3.7)

Let us simplify the above expression to get the desired result.

\displaystyle dg_{A}(H)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\mathrm{tr}\left(e^{-2t\sqrt{A^{T}A}}H^{T}A\right)+\mathrm{tr}\left(e^{-2t\sqrt{A^{T}A}}A^{T}H\right)\right]~dt

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2t\sqrt{A^{T}A}}H^{T}A~dt\right)+\mathrm{tr}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2t\sqrt{A^{T}A}}A^{T}H~dt\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2t\sqrt{A^{T}A}}~dt\right]H^{T}A\right)+\mathrm{tr}\left(\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2t\sqrt{A^{T}A}}~dt\right]A^{T}H\right)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\mathrm{tr}\left(\left[-\dfrac{\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)^{-1}}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{d}{dt}e^{-2t\sqrt{A^{T}A}}~dt\right]H^{T}A\right)\\[2.15277pt]
&\kern 28.45274pt+\mathrm{tr}\left(\left[-\dfrac{\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)^{-1}}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{d}{dt}e^{-2t\sqrt{A^{T}A}}~dt\right]A^{T}H\right)\end{split}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(\dfrac{\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)^{-1}}{2}H^{T}A\right)+\mathrm{tr}\left(\dfrac{\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)^{-1}}{2}A^{T}H\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}\left(\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)^{-1}A^{T}H\right)+\dfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}\left(A^{T}H\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)^{-1}A^{T}H\right)=\left\langle A\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)^{-1},H\right\rangle

Thus,

dg_{A}(H)=\left\langle A\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)^{-1},H\right\rangle.

∎

For any
A\in GL(n,\mathbb{R})

df_{A}=2A-2A\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)^{-1}=-2A\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}^{-1}-I\right).

Hence, the negative gradient of the function
f
, restricted to
GL(n,\mathbb{R})
is given by

-\nabla f\big|_{A}=2A\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}^{-1}-I\right).

The critical points are orthogonal matrices. If
\gamma(t)
is an integral curve of
-\nabla f
initialized at
A
, then
\gamma(0)=A
and

\dfrac{d\gamma}{dt}=-2\gamma(t)+2\gamma(t)\left(\sqrt{\gamma(t)^{T}\gamma(t)}\right)^{-1}=-2\gamma(t)+2\left(\gamma(t)^{T}\right)^{-1}\sqrt{\gamma(t)^{T}\gamma(t)}.

(3.8)

Take the test solution of (
3.8
) given by

\gamma(t)=Ae^{-2t}+(1-e^{-2t})\left(A^{T}\right)^{-1}\sqrt{A^{T}A}=Ae^{-2t}+(1-e^{-2t})A\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)^{-1}.

(3.9)

In order to show that
\gamma(t)
satisfies (
3.8
), note that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\gamma(t)^{T}\gamma(t)&=\left[Ae^{-2t}+(1-e^{-2t})\left(A^{T}\right)^{-1}\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right]^{T}\\
&\qquad\qquad\left[Ae^{-2t}+(1-e^{-2t})\left(A^{T}\right)^{-1}\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right]\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\left[A^{T}e^{-2t}+(1-e^{-2t})\sqrt{A^{T}A}A^{-1}\right]\\
&\qquad\qquad\left[Ae^{-2t}+(1-e^{-2t})\left(A^{T}\right)^{-1}\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right]\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=A^{T}Ae^{-4t}+2e^{-2t}(1-e^{-2t})\sqrt{A^{T}A}\\
&\qquad\qquad+(1-e^{-2t})^{2}\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}A^{-1}\left(A^{T}\right)^{-1}\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=A^{T}Ae^{-4t}+2e^{-2t}(1-e^{-2t})\sqrt{A^{T}A}\\
&\qquad\qquad+(1-e^{-2t})^{2}\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\left(A^{T}A\right)^{-1}\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=A^{T}Ae^{-4t}+2e^{-2t}(1-e^{-2t})\sqrt{A^{T}A}\\
&\qquad\qquad+(1-e^{-2t})^{2}\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)^{-1}\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)^{-1}\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)\end{split}

\displaystyle=A^{T}Ae^{-4t}+2e^{-2t}(1-e^{-2t})\sqrt{A^{T}A}+\left(1-e^{-2t}\right)^{2}I

\displaystyle=\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}~e^{-2t}+\left(1-e^{-2t}\right)I\right)^{2}.

Thus,

\gamma(t)^{T}\gamma(t)=\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}~e^{-2t}+\left(1-e^{-2t}\right)I\right)^{2}

and hence

\left(\sqrt{\gamma(t)^{T}\gamma(t)}\right)^{T}=\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}A^{-1}\gamma(t)\right)^{T}=\gamma(t)^{T}\left(A^{T}\right)^{-1}\sqrt{A^{T}A}

This implies that

\displaystyle\sqrt{\gamma(t)^{T}\gamma(t)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{A^{T}A}\left(e^{-2t}I+\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)^{-1}\left(1-e^{-2t}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\implies\sqrt{\gamma(t)^{t}\gamma(t)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{A^{T}A}A^{-1}\gamma(t)

\displaystyle\implies\left(\sqrt{\gamma(t)^{T}\gamma(t)}\right)^{T}

\displaystyle=\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}A^{-1}\gamma(t)\right)^{T}=\gamma(t)^{T}\left(A^{T}\right)^{-1}\sqrt{A^{T}A}

\displaystyle\implies\left(\gamma(t)^{T}\right)^{-1}\sqrt{\gamma(t)^{T}\gamma}

\displaystyle=\left(A^{T}\right)^{-1}\sqrt{A^{T}A}.

The right hand side of (
3.8
), with the test solution, can be simplified to

-2Ae^{-2t}+2e^{-2t}\left(A^{T}\right)^{-1}\sqrt{A^{T}A}

which is the derivative of
\gamma
. Thus,
\gamma(t)
, as defined in (
3.9
), is the required flow line which deforms
GL(n,\mathbb{R})
to
O(n,\mathbb{R}).
In particular,
GL^{+}(n,\mathbb{R})
deforms to
SO(n,\mathbb{R})
and other component of
GL(n,\mathbb{R})
deforms to
O(n,\mathbb{R})\setminus SO(n,\mathbb{R})
. We note, however, that this deformation takes infinite time to perform the retraction.

Remark 3.2.1
.

A modified curve

\eta(t)=A(1-t)+tA\left(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right)^{-1}

(3.10)

with the same image as
\gamma
, defines an actual deformation retraction of
GL(n,\mathbb{R})
to
O(n,\mathbb{R})
. Apart from its origin via the distance function, this is a geometric deformation in the following sense. Given
A\in GL(n,\mathbb{R})
, consider its columns as an ordered basis. This deformation deforms the ordered basis according to the length of the basis vectors and mutual angles between pairs of basis vectors in a geometrically uniform manner. This is in sharp contrast with Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization, also a deformation of
GL(n,\mathbb{R})
to
O(n,\mathbb{R})
, which is asymmetric as it never changes the direction of the first column, the modified second column only depends on the first two columns and so on.

We now show that
f
is Morse-Bott. The tangent space
T_{I}O(n,\mathbb{R})
consists of skew-symmetric matrices while the normal vectors at
I_{n}
are the symmetric matrices. As left translation by an orthogonal matrix is an isometry of
M(n,\mathbb{R})
, normal vectors at
A\in O(n,\mathbb{R})
are of the form
AW
for symmetric matrices
W
. Since

df_{A}(H)=2\left\langle A,H\right\rangle-2\left\langle A\big(\sqrt{A^{T}A}\big)^{-1},H\right\rangle

the relevant Hessian is

\mathrm{Hess}(f)_{A}(H,H^{\prime})=\lim_{t\to 0}\dfrac{df_{A+tH^{\prime}}(H)-df_{A}(H)}{t}

with
H=AW,H^{\prime}=AW^{\prime}
and symmetric matrices
W,W^{\prime}
. Solving the Hessian expression, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Hess}(f)_{A}(H,H^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0}\left(\dfrac{2\left\langle A+tH^{\prime},H\right\rangle-2\left\langle(A+tH^{\prime})\sqrt{(A+tH^{\prime})(A+tH^{\prime})^{T}}^{-1},H\right\rangle}{t}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.-\dfrac{2\left\langle A,H\right\rangle-2\left\langle A\sqrt{A^{T}A}^{-1},H\right\rangle}{t}\right)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\lim_{t\to 0}\left(\dfrac{2\left\langle A,H\right\rangle+2t\left\langle H^{\prime},H\right\rangle-2\left\langle A\sqrt{(A+tH^{\prime})^{T}(A+tH^{\prime})}^{-1},H\right\rangle}{t}\right.\\
&\kern-14.22636pt\left.-\dfrac{2t\left\langle H^{\prime}\sqrt{(A+tH^{\prime})^{T}(A+tH^{\prime})}^{-1},H\right\rangle+2\left\langle A,H\right\rangle-2\left\langle A\sqrt{A^{T}A}^{-1},H\right\rangle}{t}\right)\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\lim_{t\to 0}\left(\dfrac{2t\left\langle H^{\prime},H\right\rangle-2t\left\langle H^{\prime}\sqrt{(A+tH^{\prime})^{T}(A+tH^{\prime})}^{-1},H\right\rangle}{t}\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad\left.\dfrac{-2\left\langle A\sqrt{(A+tH^{\prime})^{T}(A+tH^{\prime})}^{-1},H\right\rangle+2\left\langle A\sqrt{A^{T}A}^{-1},H\right\rangle}{t}\right)\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\lim_{t\to 0}2\cancel{t}\left(\dfrac{\left\langle H^{\prime},H\right\rangle-\left\langle H^{\prime}\sqrt{(A+tH^{\prime})^{T}(A+tH^{\prime})}^{-1},H\right\rangle}{\cancel{t}}\right)\\
&\qquad-2\lim_{t\to 0}\left(\dfrac{\left\langle A\sqrt{(A+tH^{\prime})^{T}(A+tH^{\prime})}^{-1},H\right\rangle-\left\langle A\sqrt{A^{T}A}^{-1},H\right\rangle}{t}\right)\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=2\left\langle H^{\prime},H\right\rangle-2\left\langle H^{\prime}\cancelto{I}{\sqrt{A^{T}A}^{-1}},H\right\rangle-2\left\langle A\left(Dg^{-1}\right)_{A}(H^{\prime}),H\right\rangle\end{split}

\displaystyle=-2\left\langle A\cdot\frac{A^{T}H^{\prime}+H^{\prime T}A}{2},H\right\rangle=\left\langle H^{\prime},H\right\rangle+\left\langle AH^{\prime T}A,H\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle H^{\prime},H\right\rangle+\left\langle A(AW^{\prime})^{T}A,AW\right\rangle=\left\langle H^{\prime},H\right\rangle+\left\langle AW^{\prime T},AW\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(H^{\prime T}H\right)+\mathrm{tr}\left(W^{\prime T}W\right)=\mathrm{tr}\left(H^{\prime T}H\right)+\mathrm{tr}\left(H^{\prime T}H\right)=2~\mathrm{tr}\left(H^{\prime T}H\right).

Thus, the Hessian is,

\mathrm{Hess}(f)_{A}(H,H^{\prime})=2\,\mathrm{tr}\left(H^{T}H^{\prime}\right)=2\left\langle H,H^{\prime}\right\rangle.

Therefore, the Hessian matrix restricted to
(T_{A}O(n,\mathbb{R}))^{\perp}
is
2I_{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}
. This is a recurring feature of distance squared functions associated to embedded submanifolds (see Proposition
4.1.1
).

There is a relationship between the local homology of cut loci and the reduced
\check{\textup{C}}
ech cohomology of the

of a point in the cut locus. This is due to Hebda
[

, Theorem 1.4 and the remark following it]
.

Definition 3.2.1
.

Let
N
be an embedded submanifold of a complete smooth Riemannian manifold
M
. For each
q\in\mathrm{Cu}(N)
, consider the set
\Lambda(q,N)
of unit tangent vectors at
q
so that the associated geodesics realize the distance between
q
and
N
. This set is called the
link
of
q
with respect to
N
.

The set of points in
N
obtained by the end points of the geodesics associated to
\Lambda(q,N)
will be called the
equidistant set
, denoted by
\mathrm{Eq}(q,N)
, of
q
with respect to
N
.

Since the equidistant set
\mathrm{Eq}(q,N)
, consisting of points which realize the distance
d(q,N)
, is obtained by exponentiating the points in
\Lambda(q,N)
, there is a natural surjection map from
\Lambda(q,N)
to
\mathrm{Eq}(q,N)
.

Theorem 3.2.1

(Hebda 1983)
.

Let
N
be a properly embedded submanifold of a complete Riemannian manifold
M
of dimension
n
. If
q\in\mathrm{Cu}(N)
and
v
is an element of
\Lambda:=\Lambda(q,N)
, then for any abelian group
G
there is an isomorphism

\check{H}^{i}(\Lambda,v;G)\cong H_{n-1-i}(\mathrm{Cu}(N),\mathrm{Cu}(N)-q;G).

(3.11)

We are interested in computing
\Lambda(A,O(n,\mathbb{R}))
for singular matrices
A
. Note that geodesics in
M(n,\mathbb{R})
, initialized at
A
, are straight lines and any two such geodesics can never meet other than at
A
. Therefore, there is a natural identification between the link and the equidistant set of
A
.

Lemma 3.2.4
.

[
Basu and Prasad, 2021
, Lemma 2.15]

If
A\in M(n,\mathbb{R})
is singular of rank
k
, then
\mathrm{Eq}(A,O(n,\mathbb{R}))
is homeomorphic to
O(n-k,\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Using the singular value decomposition, we write
A=UDV^{T}
, where
U,V\in O(n,\mathbb{R})
and
D
is a diagonal matrix with entries the eigenvalues of
\sqrt{A^{T}A}
. If we specify that the diagonal entries of
D
are arranged in decreasing order, then
D
is unique. Moreover, as
A
has rank
k<n
, the first
k
diagonal entries of
D
are positive while the last
n-k
diagonal entries are zero. In order to find the matrices in
O(n,\mathbb{R})
which realize the distance
d(A,O(n,\mathbb{R}))
, by (
3.2
), it suffices to find
B\in O(n,\mathbb{R})
such that

\sup_{B\in O(n,\mathbb{R})}\mathrm{tr}\left(A^{T}B\right)=\sup_{B\in O(n,\mathbb{R})}\mathrm{tr}\left(VDU^{T}B\right)=\sup_{B\in O(n,\mathbb{R})}\mathrm{tr}\left(DU^{T}BV\right)

is maximized. However,
U^{T}BV\in O(n,\mathbb{R})
has orthonormal rows and the specific form of
D
implies that the maximum is attained if and only if
U^{T}BV
has
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}
as the first
k
rows, in order. Therefore,
U^{T}BV
is a block orthogonal matrix, with blocks of
I_{k}
and
C\in O(n-k,\mathbb{R})
, i.e.,
B\in U(I_{k}\times O(n-k,\mathbb{R}))V^{T}
.
∎

Corollary 3.2.1
.

Let
\mathrm{Sing}
denote the space of singular matrices in

. If

is of rank

, then for any abelian group
G
there is an isomorphism

H_{n^{2}-1-i}(\mathrm{Sing},\mathrm{Sing}-A;G)\cong\widetilde{H}^{i}(O(n-k,\mathbb{R});G).

(3.12)

Proof.

It follows from

that
\Lambda(A,O(n,\mathbb{R}))\cong O(n-k,\mathbb{R})
if

. Since

is a manifold,
\check{\textup{C}}

is a star-convex set, whence all homotopy and homology groups are that of a point. Applying (

) in our case, we obtain an isomorphism

H_{n^{2}-1-i}(\mathrm{Sing},\mathrm{Sing}-A)\cong\widetilde{H}^{i}(O(n-k,\mathbb{R}))

between reduced cohomology and local homology groups. In particular, the local homology of the cut locus at

detects the rank of

.
∎

.

Note that for a smooth manifold, the relative homology group

Chapter 4
Geometric and topological nature of cut locus

The objective of this chapter is to analyze the geometric and topological properties of cut locus of submanifolds. In particular, we will study relations between the distance squared function from a submanifold, the cut locus of submanifold and Thom space of the normal bundle of the submanifold. We will also prove that the distance squared function is a Morse-Bott function. Results in this chapter are based on joint work with Basu
[

]

A result due to Wolter

Proposition 4.1.1

Let

Since the expression of

(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
. For

, choose the unique unit speed

-geodesic
\gamma
joining

to

Towards the regularity of distance squared function, the following observation will be useful. It is a routine generalization of
[
Wolter, 1979

Lemma 4.1.1
.

[
Basu and Prasad, 2021
, Lemma 3.7]

Let
M
be a connected, complete Riemannian manifold and
N
be an embedded submanifold of
M
. Suppose two
N
-geodesics exist joining
N
to
q\in M
. Then
d^{2}(N,\cdot):M\to\mathbb{R}
has no directional derivative at
q
for vectors in direction of those two
N
-geodesics.

Proof.

Let us assume that all the geodesics are arc-length parametrized. Let
\gamma_{i}:[0,\hat{t}]\to M,~~i=1,2
, be two distinct geodesics with
\gamma_{1}(0),\gamma_{2}(0)\in N
and
\gamma_{1}(l)=q=\gamma_{2}(l)
, where
l=d(N,q)
and
0<l<\hat{t}
. Suppose that the two geodesics start at
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
and so
d(p_{1},q)=l=d(p_{2},q)
. Note that the directional derivative of
d^{2}
at
q
in the direction of
\gamma_{i}^{\prime}(q)
from the left is given by

\displaystyle(d^{2})^{\prime}_{-}(q)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\dfrac{(d(N,\gamma_{i}(l)))^{2}-(d(N,\gamma_{i}(l-\varepsilon)))^{2}}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\dfrac{(d(p_{i},\gamma_{i}(l)))^{2}-(d(p_{i},\gamma_{i}(l-\varepsilon)))^{2}}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\dfrac{l^{2}-(l-\varepsilon)^{2}}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\dfrac{l^{2}-l^{2}+2l\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{2}}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\dfrac{2l\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{2}}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=2l.

Next, we claim that the derivative of the same function from the right is strictly bounded above by
2l
. Let
\omega\in(0,\pi]
be the angle between the two geodesics
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
at
q
. Define the function,

u(\tau)\vcentcolon=d(N,\gamma_{1}(l-\varepsilon))+d(\gamma_{1}(l-\varepsilon),\gamma_{2}(\tau+l)).

Figure 4.2
:
When two
N
-geodesics meet

By triangle inequality, we observe that

f(\tau)\vcentcolon=(u(\tau))^{2}\geq d^{2}(p_{1},\gamma_{2}(\tau+l))\geq d^{2}(N,\gamma_{2}(\tau+l)),

and equality holds at
\tau=0
and
(u(0))^{2}=d^{2}(N,q)=l^{2}
. Thus, in order to prove the claim, it suffices to show that the derivative of
f
from right, at
\tau=0
, is bounded below by
2l
. We need to invoke a version of the cosine law for small geodesic triangles. Although this may be well-known to experts, we will use the version that appears in
[
Sharafutdinov, 2007
]
(also see
[
Daniilidis et al., 2018
, Lemma 2.4]
for a detailed proof). In our case, this means that

d^{2}(\gamma_{1}(l-\varepsilon),\gamma_{2}(\tau+l))=\varepsilon^{2}+\tau^{2}+2\varepsilon\tau\cos\omega+K(\tau)\varepsilon^{2}\tau^{2}

where
|K(\tau)|
is bounded, and the side lengths are sufficiently small. Note that we are considering geodesic triangles with two vertices constant and the varying vertex being
\gamma_{2}(l+\tau)
. It follows from taking a square root and then expanding in powers of
\tau
that

d(\gamma_{1}(l-\varepsilon),\gamma_{2}(\tau+l))=\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}+\tau^{2}+2\varepsilon\tau\cos\omega}~(1+O(\tau^{2})).

It follows that

u(\tau)=l-\varepsilon+\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}+\tau^{2}+2\varepsilon\tau\cos\omega}~(1+O(\tau^{2})).

Therefore,
u^{\prime}_{+}(0)=\cos\omega=d^{\prime}_{+}(\gamma_{1}(l-\varepsilon),\gamma_{2}(l))
. Observe that

\displaystyle f^{\prime}_{+}(\tau)\Big|_{\tau=0}

\displaystyle=2d(N,\gamma_{1}(l-\varepsilon))d^{\prime}_{+}(\gamma_{1}(l-\varepsilon),\gamma_{2}(l))

\displaystyle\kern 85.35826pt+2d(\gamma_{1}(l-\varepsilon),\gamma_{2}(l))d^{\prime}_{+}(\gamma_{1}(l-\varepsilon),\gamma_{2}(l))

\displaystyle=2d(N,\gamma_{1}(l-\varepsilon))\cos\omega+2d(\gamma_{1}(l-\varepsilon),\gamma_{2}(l))\cos\omega

\displaystyle=2\cos\omega\big[d(N,\gamma_{1}(l-\varepsilon))+d(\gamma_{1}(l-\varepsilon),\gamma_{2}(l)))\big]

\displaystyle=2\cos\omega\big[d(N,\gamma_{1}(l-\varepsilon))+d(\gamma_{1}(l-\varepsilon),\gamma_{1}(l)))\big]

\displaystyle=2d(N,\gamma_{1}(l))\cos\omega<2l.

Thus, we have proved the claim and subsequently the result.
∎

On the other hand, for
\varepsilon<0

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{-}}\frac{d^{2}(P_{\varepsilon}(\alpha,\beta),Q_{\varepsilon}(\alpha,\beta))-d^{2}(P_{0}(\alpha,\beta),Q_{0}(\alpha,\beta))}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{-}}\frac{d^{2}_{-\varepsilon}(-\alpha,\beta)-d^{2}_{0}(-\alpha,\beta)}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=-\lim_{-\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{d^{2}_{-\varepsilon}(-\alpha,\beta)-d^{2}_{0}(-\alpha,\beta)}{-\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2b^{2}\alpha}{a},

where the last equality follows from the right hand derivative of

, as computed previously.

When

(4.2)

On the other hand, if

We may compute the distance

d^{2}_{\varepsilon}(\alpha,0):=(d(P_{\varepsilon},Q_{\varepsilon}))^{2}=\frac{b^{4}}{a^{2}}-\frac{2b^{2}\alpha\varepsilon}{a}-\frac{b^{2}\alpha^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{a^{2}-b^{2}},

where

4.3
Topological properties

In this section we will study the structure of the cut locus and the relation of cut locus to the Thom space. We will also see various applications of this relation.

4.3.1
Structure of the cut locus

On a complete Riemannian manifold it is very difficult to analyze the structure of cut locus of a point or a submanifold. The main problem is that cut locus is not
C^{1}
-smooth. For example, the authors in
[
Gluck and Singer, 1978
]
showed that cut locus of a point is not always triangulable. Regarding the question of cut loci being triangulable, we recall the result
[
Buchner, 1977
]
that the cut locus (of a point) of a real analytic Riemannian manifold (of dimension
d
) is a simplicial complex of dimension at most
d-1
. It follows, without much changes, that the result holds for cut loci of submanifolds as well. Hence, we attribute the following result to Buchner.

Theorem 4.3.1

(Buchner 1977)
.

Let

is a simplicial complex of dimension at most

.

The obvious modifications to the proof by Buchner are the following:

(i)

Choose

, then there is a unique

-geodesic to

(ii)

Consider the set

, the space of piecewise broken geodesics starting at

, and define
\Omega_{N}(t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})^{s}
analogously;

(iii)

The map

determines an analytic structure on

The remainder of the proof works essentially verbatim.

Remark 4.3.1
.

As we have seen in
3.1.5
, the dimension of the cut locus of a
k
-dimensional submanifold can be of dimension
d-k-1
. However, generically, we may not expect this to be true. In fact, for real analytic knots (except the unknot) in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, it is always the case that the cut locus cannot be homotopic to a (connected)
1
-dimensional simplicial complex (cf
4.3.3
).

4.3.2
Thom space via cut locus

Recall that the
Thom space

\textup{Th}(E)
of a real vector bundle
E\to B
of rank
k
is
D(E)/S(E)
, where it is understood that we have chosen a Euclidean metric on
E
. If
B
is compact, then the Thom space
\textup{Th}(E)
is the one-point compactification of
E
. In general, we compactify the fibres and then collapse the section at infinity to a point to obtain
\textup{Th}(E)
. Thus, Thom spaces obtained via two different metrics are homeomorphic. We start with a similar exponential map which is obtained by the
\rho
map, and we named it
rescaled exponential map
.

Definition 4.3.1

(Rescaled Exponential)
.

The
rescaled exponential
or
\rho
-exponential map is defined to be

\widetilde{\textup{exp}}:D(\nu)\to M,\,\,(p,v)\mapsto\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\textup{exp}_{p}(\rho(\hat{v})v)&\textup{if $v=\|v\|\hat{v}\neq 0$}\\
p&\textup{if $v=0$.}\end{array}\right.

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.3.2
.

Let
N
be an embedded submanifold inside a closed, connected Riemannian manifold
M
. If
\nu
denotes the normal bundle of
N
in
M
, then there is a homeomorphism

\widetilde{\textup{exp}}:D(\nu)/S(\nu)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}M/\mathrm{Cu}(N).

Proof.

It follows from
4.2.2
that the rescaled exponential is continuous. Moreover,
\widetilde{\textup{exp}}
is surjective and
\widetilde{\textup{exp}}(S(\nu))=\mathrm{Cu}(N)
. If there exists
(p,v)\neq(q,w)\in D(\nu)
such that

\widetilde{\textup{exp}}(p,v)=\widetilde{\textup{exp}}(q,w)=p^{\prime},

then
d(p^{\prime},N)
can be computed in two ways to obtain

d(p^{\prime},N)=\rho(\hat{v})\|v\|=\rho(\hat{w})\|w\|.

Thus,
T=d(p^{\prime},N)
is a number such that
\gamma_{v}:[0,T]
is an
N
-geodesic and
\gamma_{v}(T)=\gamma_{w}(T)=p^{\prime}
. By
4.2.1
, we conclude that
T=\rho(\hat{v})=\rho(\hat{w})
, whence
\|v\|=\|w\|=1
. Therefore,
\widetilde{\textup{exp}}
is injective on the interior of
D(\nu)
.

As
\mathrm{Cu}(N)
is closed and
M
is a compact metric space, the quotient space
M/\mathrm{Cu}(N)
is Hausdorff. As the quotient
D(\nu)/S(\nu)
is compact, standard topological arguments imply the map induced by the rescaled exponential is a homeomorphism.
∎

We will now revisit a basic property of Thom space via its connection to the cut locus. It can be seen that

\mathrm{Cu}(N_{1}\times N_{2})=(\mathrm{Cu}(N_{1})\times M_{2})\cup(M_{1}\times\mathrm{Cu}(N_{2}))

(4.5)

for an embedding

\displaystyle\textup{Th}(\nu_{1}\oplus\nu_{2})

\displaystyle\cong\frac{M_{1}\times M_{2}}{(M_{1}\times\mathrm{Cu}(N_{2}))\cup(\mathrm{Cu}(N_{1})\times M_{2})}

Example 4.3.1
.

Consider the two circles

. As it is closed, the claim will follow by using

\gamma(t)=P\cos t+\mathbf{v}\sin t,~=(a\cos t,b\cos t,\cos\theta\sin t,\sin\theta\sin t),~~0\leq t\leq\pi.

We have

d(\gamma(t),N)=\inf_{X\in N}d(\gamma(t),X)=\min\left\{\inf_{X\in N_{1}}d(\gamma(t),X),\inf_{X\in N_{2}}d(\gamma(t),X),\right\}.

Look at
Figure

4.6

Figure 4.6

Distance of

Therefore, the problem of finding the distance of

Let

Maximizing the above such that

Note that

There are some topological similarities between

Lemma 4.3.1
.

The cut locus
\mathrm{Cu}(N)

Proof.

Consider the map

\displaystyle H(q,t)=\begin{cases}\exp_{\nu}\left[\left\{t\cdot\rho\left(\frac{\exp_{\nu}^{-1}(q)}{\left\|\exp_{\nu}^{-1}(q)\right\|}\right)+(1-t)\left\|\exp_{\nu}^{-1}(q)\right\|\right\}\frac{\exp_{\nu}^{-1}(q)}{\left\|\exp_{\nu}^{-1}(q)\right\|}\right]&\text{ if }q\notin\mathrm{Cu}(N)\\[4.30554pt]

If

∎

Remark 4.3.2
.

The above lemma, in particular, shows that the homotopy type of the cut locus of a submanifold is independent of the choice of the Riemannian metric.

Corollary 4.3.1
.

If two embeddings
f,g:N\to M
are ambient isotopic, then
\mathrm{Cu}(f(N))
and
\mathrm{Cu}(g(N))
are homotopy equivalent.

Proof.

The hypothesis implies that there is a diffeomorphism
\varphi:M\to M
such that
\varphi(f(N))=g(N)
. Thus,
M-\mathrm{Cu}(f(N))
is homeomorphic to
M-\mathrm{Cu}(g(N))
and the claim follows from the lemma above. Note that in the smooth category, the notion of isotopic and ambient isotopic are equivalent (refer to §8.1 of the book
[
Hirsch, 1976
]
). Thus, the same conclusion holds if we assume that the embeddings are isotopic.
∎

Remark 4.3.3

Remark 4.3.4

The long exact sequence (

Remark 4.3.6
.

Cut locus can be very hard to compute. For a general space, we have the notion of topological dimension. This notion coincides with the usual notion if the space is triangulable. However, in
[
Barratt and Milnor, 1962
]
, the authors proved that the singular homology of a space may be non-zero beyond its topological dimension. Čech (co)homology is better equipped to detect topological dimension and is the reason why one may prefer it over singular homology due to the generic fractal like nature of cut loci (see the remarks following
C
in
Chapter

1
). Although the topological dimension of
\mathrm{Cu}(N)
is at most
d-1
, it is not apparent that
H_{d-1}(\mathrm{Cu}(N))
is a free abelian group.

There are several applications of this discussion.

Theorem 4.3.3
.

Let

be a smooth homology

-sphere embedded in a Riemannian manifold homeomorphic to

As

is
(d-k-2)

If

\Sigma
is non-orientable, then
\Sigma\cong(\mathbb{RP}^{2})^{\#k}
has non-orientable genus
k
and the oriented double cover of
\Sigma
has genus
g=k-1
. Recall that
H_{1}(\Sigma)\cong\mathbb{Z}^{k-1}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and
H_{2}(\Sigma)=0
. Looking at (
4.8
) with
j=2
we obtain

0\to\mathbb{Z}\to H_{1}(\mathrm{Cu}(p))\to\mathbb{Z}^{k-1}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{2}\to 0.

Thus,
H_{1}(\mathrm{Cu}(p))\cong\mathbb{Z}^{k}
as homology of graphs are free abelian. Let
B_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{Cu}(p))
denote the
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood of
\mathrm{Cu}(p)
in
\Sigma
. For
\varepsilon
sufficiently small, this is a surface such that
\overline{B_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{Cu}(p))}
has one boundary component. The compact surface
B_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{Cu}(p))
is reminiscent of ribbon graphs. The surface
\Sigma
can be obtained as the connect sum of a disk centered at
p
and the closure of
B_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{Cu}(p))
. Therefore, non-orientability of
\Sigma
is equivalent to non-orientability of
B_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{Cu}(p))
. A similar observation appears in the unpublished article
[
Itoh and Vîlcu, 2011
, Theorem 3.7]
.

Example 4.3.3

(Homology spheres of codimension two)
.

In continuation of
4.3.3
, let
N\hookrightarrow S^{k+2}
be a homology sphere of dimension
k\geq 1
. Since
N
has codimension two,
S^{k+2}-N
is path connected and so is
\mathrm{Cu}(N)
. We are not assuming that the metric on
S^{k+2}
is real analytic. Using (
4.10
) and the long exact sequence in cohomology of
(S^{k+2},N)
, we infer that
H_{1}(\mathrm{Cu}(N))\cong\mathbb{Z}
and all higher homology groups vanish. However, the Hurewicz Theorem cannot be used here to establish that
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Cu}(N))\cong\mathbb{Z}
.

In particular cases, we may conclude that
\mathrm{Cu}(N)
is homotopic to a circle. It was proved in
[
Plotnick, 1982
]
that certain homology
3
-spheres
N
, obtained by a Dehn surgery of type
\frac{1}{2a}
on a knot, smoothly embed in
S^{5}
with complement a homotopy circle. Since
M-N
deforms to
\mathrm{Cu}(N)
, it follows that there is a map
\alpha:S^{1}\to\mathrm{Cu}(N)
inducing isomorphisms on homotopy and homology groups.

If
k=1
, then a homology
1
-sphere is just a knot
K
in
S^{3}
. Since
S^{3}-K
deforms to
\mathrm{Cu}(K)
, the fundamental group of the cut locus is the knot group. Moreover, in the case of real analytic knots in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, the cut locus is a finite simplicial complex of dimension at most
2
(cf
4.3.1
). Except for the unknot, the knot group is never a free group while the fundamental group of a connected, finite graph is free. This observation establishes that
\mathrm{Cu}(K)
is always a
2
-dimensional simplicial complex, whenever
K
is a non-trivial (real analytic) knot in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
.

Finally, we will end this chapter by proving that the complement of cut locus deforms to the submanifold.

Theorem 4.3.5
.

Let
N
be a closed embedded submanifold of a complete Riemannian manifold
M
. Let
d:M\to\mathbb{R}
be the distance function with respect to
N
. If
f=d^{2}
, then its restriction to
M-\mathrm{Cu}(N)
is a Morse-Bott function, with
N
as the critical submanifold. Moreover, the gradient flow of
f
deforms
M-\mathrm{Cu}(N)
to
N
.

Proof.

It follows from
4.2.1
the map
\exp_{\nu}^{-1}:M-\left(\mathrm{Cu}(N)\cup N\right)\to\nu-\{0\}
is an (into) diffeomorphism and
\operatorname{dist}(N,q)=\left\|\exp^{-1}_{\nu}(q)\right\|
and hence the distance function is of class
C^{\infty}
at
q\in M-\left(\mathrm{Cu}(N)\cup N\right)
. Using Fermi coordinates (cf
4.1.1
), we have seen that the distance squared function is smooth around
N
and therefore it is smooth on
M-\mathrm{Cu}(N)
. By
4.1.1
, the Hessian of this function at
N
is non-degenerate in the normal direction. It is well-known
[
Sakai, 1996
, Proposition 4.8]
that
\|\nabla d(q)\|=1
if
d
is differentiable at
q\in M
. Thus, for
q\in M-(\mathrm{Cu}(N)\cup N)
we have

\|\nabla f(q)\|=2d(q)\|\nabla d(q)\|=2d(q).

(4.12)

Let
\gamma
be the unique unit speed
N
-geodesic that joins
N
to
q
, i.e.,

\gamma:[0,d(q)]\to M,\,\,\gamma(0)=p,\,\gamma(d(q))=q,\,\|\gamma^{\prime}\|=1.

We may write
\nabla f(q)=\lambda\gamma^{\prime}(d(q))+w
, where
w
is orthogonal to
\gamma^{\prime}(d(q))
. But

\left\langle\nabla f\big|_{q},\gamma^{\prime}(d(q))\right\rangle=\frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma(d(q)+t))\Big|_{t=0}=\frac{d}{dt}(d(q)^{2}+2d(q)t+t^{2})\Big|_{t=0}=2d(q).

Thus,
\lambda=2d(q)
and combined with (
4.12
), we conclude that
\nabla f(q)=2d(q)\gamma^{\prime}(d(q))
. Therefore, the negative gradient flow line initialized at
q\in M-\mathrm{Cu}(N)
is given by

\eta(t)=\gamma(d(q)e^{-2t}).

These flow lines define a flow which deform
M-\mathrm{Cu}(N)
to
N
in infinite time.
∎

The reader may choose to revisit the example of
GL(n,\mathbb{R})
discussed in
Section

3.2
and treat it as a concrete illustration of the Theorem above.

Chapter 5
Application to Lie groups

Due to the classical results of Cartan, Iwasawa and others, we know that any connected Lie group
G
is diffeomorphic to the product of a maximally compact subgroup
K
and the Euclidean space. In particular,
G
deforms to
K
. For semisimple groups, this decomposition is stronger and is attributed to Iwasawa. The Killing form on the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is non-degenerate and negative definite for compact semi-simple Lie algebras. For such a Lie group
G
, consider the Levi-Civita connection associated to the bi-invariant metric obtained from negative of the Killing form. This connection coincides with the Cartan connection.

We will consider two examples, both of which are non-compact and non semisimple. We prove that these Lie groups
G
deformation retract to maximally compact subgroups
K
via gradient flows of appropriate Morse-Bott functions. This requires a choice of a left-invariant metric which is right-
K
-invariant, and a careful analysis of the geodesics associated with the metric. In particular, we provide a possibly new proof of the surjectivity of the exponential map for
U(p,q)
. The results of this chapter is based on joint work with Basu
[
Basu and Prasad, 2021
, §4]
.

5.1
Matrices with positive determinant

Let
g
be a left-invariant metric on
GL(n,\mathbb{R})
, the set of all invertible matrices. Recall that a left-invariant metric
g
on a Lie group is determined by its restriction at the identity. For
A\in GL(n,\mathbb{R})
, consider the left multiplication map
l_{A}:GL(n,\mathbb{R})\to GL(n,\mathbb{R}),~B\mapsto AB
. This extends to a linear isomorphism from
M(n,\mathbb{R})
to itself. Thus, the differential
(Dl_{A})_{I}:T_{I}GL(n,\mathbb{R})\to T_{A}GL(n,\mathbb{R})
is an isomorphism and given by
l_{A}
itself. For
X,Y\in T_{I}GL(n,\mathbb{R})
,

g_{I}(X,Y)=g_{A}((Dl_{A})_{I}X,(Dl_{A})_{I}Y)=g_{A}(AX,AY).

We choose the left-invariant metric on
GL(n,\mathbb{R})
generated by the Euclidean metric at
I
. Therefore,

g_{A^{-1}}(X,Y)=\left\langle AX,AY\right\rangle_{I}:=\mathrm{tr}\left((AX)^{T}\!AY\right)=\mathrm{tr}\left(X^{T}\!A^{T}\!AY\right).

Note that this metric is right-
O(n,\mathbb{R})
-invariant. We are interested in the distance between an invertible matrix
A
(with
\det(A)>0
) and
SO(n,\mathbb{R})
. Since
SO(n,\mathbb{R})
is compact, there exists
B\in SO(n,\mathbb{R})
such that
d(A,B)=\operatorname{dist}(A,SO(n,\mathbb{R}))
.

Lemma 5.1.1
.

If
D
is a diagonal matrix with positive diagonal entries
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n}
, then

\operatorname{dist}(D,SO(n,\mathbb{R}))=d(D,I).

Moreover,
I
is the unique minimizer and the associated minimal geodesic is given by
\gamma(t)=e^{t\log D}
.

Proof.

Let
B\in SO(n,\mathbb{R})
satisfying
d(D,B)=\operatorname{dist}(D,SO(n,\mathbb{R}))
. Since with respect to the left-invariant metric
GL^{+}(n,\mathbb{R})
is complete, there exists a minimal geodesic
\gamma:[0,1]\to GL^{+}(n,\mathbb{R})
joining
B
to
D
, i.e.,

\gamma(0)=B,~~\gamma(1)=D,~~\text{ and }~~l(\gamma)=d(D,B).

The first variational principle implies that
\gamma^{\prime}(0)
is orthogonal to
T_{B}SO(n,\mathbb{R})
. It follows from
[
Martin and Neff, 2016
, §2.1]
that
\eta(t)=e^{tW}
is a geodesic if
W
is a symmetric matrix. Moreover,
\eta^{\prime}(0)=W
is orthogonal to
T_{I}SO(n,\mathbb{R})
. As left translation is an isometry and isometry preserves geodesic, it follows that
\gamma(t)=Be^{tW}
is a geodesic with
\gamma^{\prime}(0)
orthogonal to
T_{B}SO(n,\mathbb{R})
. By the defining properties of
\gamma
,
D=\gamma(1)=Be^{W}
. Since
e^{W}
is symmetric positive definite, we obtain two polar decompositions of
D
, i.e.,
D=ID
and
D=Be^{W}
. By the uniqueness of the polar decomposition for invertible matrices,
B=I
and
D=e^{W}
.

In order to compute
d(I,D)
, note that

e^{W}=D=e^{\log D},

where
\log D
denotes the diagonal matrix with entries
\log\lambda_{1},\cdots,\log\lambda_{n}
. As
W
and
\log D
are symmetric, and matrix exponential is injective on the space of symmetric matrices, we conclude that
W=\log D
. The geodesic is given by
\gamma(t)=e^{t\log D}
and

\operatorname{dist}(D,SO(n,\mathbb{R}))=\left\|\gamma^{\prime}(0)\right\|_{I}=\left\|\log D\right\|_{I}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\log\lambda_{i})^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(5.1)

Thus, the distance squared function will be given by
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\log\lambda_{i})^{2}
.
∎

Now for any
A\in GL^{+}(n,\mathbb{R})
we can apply the SVD decomposition, i.e.,
A=UDV^{T}
with
\sqrt{A^{T}A}=VDV^{T}
and
\log\sqrt{A^{T}A}=V(\log D)V^{T}
. Note that
U,V\in SO(n,\mathbb{R})
and
D
is a diagonal matrix with positive entries. The left-invariant metric is right-invariant with respect to orthogonal matrices. Thus,

\operatorname{dist}(A,SO(n,\mathbb{R}))=\operatorname{dist}(D,SO(n,\mathbb{R}))=\left\|\log D\right\|_{I},

where the last equality follows from the lemma (see (
5.1
)). As

\left\|\log D\right\|_{I}=\left\|V(\log D)V^{T}\right\|_{I}=\left\|\log\sqrt{A^{T}A}\right\|_{I},

it follows from the arguments of the lemma and the metric being bi-
O(n,\mathbb{R})
-invariant that

\gamma(t)=Ue^{t\log D}V^{T}

is a minimal geodesic joining
UV^{T}
to
A
, realizing
\operatorname{dist}(A,SO(n,\mathbb{R}))
. As the minimizer
UV^{T}
is unique,
\mathrm{Se}(SO(n,\mathbb{R}))
is empty, implying that
\mathrm{Cu}(SO(n,\mathbb{R}))
is empty as well. In fact,
UV^{T}=A\sqrt{A^{T}A}^{-1}
and

\gamma(t)=Ue^{t\log D}V^{T}=UV^{T}Ve^{t\log D}V^{T}=A\sqrt{A^{T}A}^{-1}e^{t\log\sqrt{A^{T}A}}.

(5.2)

If we compare (
3.10
), the deformation of
GL(n,\mathbb{R})
to
O(n,\mathbb{R})
inside
M(n,\mathbb{R})
, with (
5.2
), then in both of the cases, an invertible matrix
A
deforms to
A\sqrt{A^{T}A}^{-1}
. Finally, observe that the normal bundle of
SO(n,\mathbb{R})
is diffeomorphic to
GL^{+}(n,\mathbb{R})
.

5.2
Indefinite unitary groups

Let
n
be a positive integer with
n=p+q
. Consider the inner product on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
given by

\left\langle(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}),(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\right\rangle=z_{1}\overline{w_{1}}+\cdots+z_{p}\overline{w_{p}}-z_{p+1}\overline{w_{p+1}}-\cdots-z_{n}\overline{w_{n}}.

This is given by the matrix
I_{p,q}
in the following way:

\left\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle=\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{t}I_{p,q}\mathbf{z}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\overline{w}_{1}&\cdots&\overline{w}_{n}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{p}&0\\
0&-I_{q}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}z_{1}\\
\vdots\\
z_{n}\end{array}\right)

Let
U(p,q)
denote the subgroup of
GL(n,\mathbb{C})
preserving this indefinite form, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}\in U(p,q)
if and only if
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}I_{p,q}\mathcal{A}=I_{p,q}
. In particular,
\det\mathcal{A}
is a complex number of unit length. By convention,
I_{n,0}=I_{n}
and
I_{0,n}=-I_{n}
, both of which corresponds to
U(n,0)=U(n)=U(0,n)
, the unitary group. In all other cases, the inner product is indefinite.

The group
U(1,1)
is given by matrices of the form

\mathcal{A}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha&\beta\\
\lambda\overline{\beta}&\lambda\overline{\alpha}\end{array}\right),\,\,\lambda\in S^{1},\,\,|\alpha|^{2}-|\beta|^{2}=1.

More generally, we shall use

\mathcal{A}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A&B\\
C&D\end{array}\right)

to denote an element of
U(p,q)
. It follows from the definition that
\mathcal{A}\in U(p,q)
if and only if

\displaystyle A^{\ast}A-C^{\ast}C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{p}

\displaystyle A^{\ast}B-C^{\ast}D

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0_{p\times q}

\displaystyle B^{\ast}B-D^{\ast}D

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-I_{q}.

Observe that if
Av=0
, then

0=A^{\ast}Av=C^{\ast}Cv+v,

which implies that
C^{\ast}C
, a positive semi-definite matrix, has
-1
as an eigenvalue unless
v=0
. Therefore,
A
is invertible, and the same argument works for
D
.

Lemma 5.2.1
.

The intersection of
U(p+q)
with
U(p,q)
is
U(p)\times U(q)
. Moreover, if
\mathcal{A}\in U(p,q)
, then
\mathcal{A}^{\ast},\sqrt{\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}}\in U(p,q)
.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{A}\in U(p)\times U(q)
, then

\displaystyle A^{\ast}A+C^{\ast}C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{p}

\displaystyle B^{\ast}B+D^{\ast}D

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{q}.

This implies that both
B
and
C
are zero matrices. If
\mathcal{A}\in U(p,q)
, then
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}=I_{p,q}\mathcal{A}^{-1}I_{p,q}
and

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A})^{\ast}I_{p,q}(\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A})I_{p,q}(\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=I_{p,q}\mathcal{A}^{-1}I_{p,q}\mathcal{A}I_{p,q}I_{p,q}\mathcal{A}^{-1}I_{p,q}\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=I_{p,q}=\mathcal{A}^{\ast}I_{p,q}\mathcal{A}.

This also implies that
\mathcal{A}I_{p,q}\mathcal{A}^{\ast}=I_{p,q}
.

All the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}
are positive. Moreover, if
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}
with eigenvector
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{p},v_{p+1},\ldots,v_{n})
, then

I_{p,q}\mathbf{v}=\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}\,I_{p,q}\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}\mathbf{v}=\lambda(\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}\,I_{p,q}\mathbf{v}),

which implies that
\lambda^{-1}
is also an eigenvalue with eigenvector
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{p},-v_{p+1},\ldots,-v_{n})
. If
\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n}\}
is an eigenbasis of
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}
with (possibly repeated) eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
, then

\sqrt{\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}}\,I_{p,q}\sqrt{\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}}\mathbf{v}_{j}=\sqrt{\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}}\,I_{p,q}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\mathbf{v}_{j}=\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\sqrt{\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{\prime}=\mathbf{v}_{j}^{\prime}=I_{p,q}\mathbf{v}_{j}.

Thus,
\sqrt{\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}}
satisfies the defining relation for a matrix to be in
U(p,q)
.
∎

We may use the polar decomposition (for matrices in
GL(n,\mathbb{C})
) to write

\mathcal{A}=U|\mathcal{A}|,\,\,\textup{where}\,\,U=\mathcal{A}\left(\sqrt{\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}}\right)^{-1},|\mathcal{A}|=\sqrt{\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}},

where
U,|\mathcal{A}|\in U(p,q)
. For
\textup{U}(1,1)
this decomposition takes the form

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha&\beta\\
\lambda\overline{\beta}&\lambda\overline{\alpha}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\alpha}{|\alpha|}&0\\
0&\lambda\frac{\overline{\alpha}}{|\alpha|}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}|\alpha|&\frac{|\alpha|\beta}{\alpha}\\
\frac{|\alpha|\overline{\beta}}{\overline{\alpha}}&|\alpha|\end{array}\right)

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{u}_{p,q}
is given by matrices
X\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
such that

X^{\ast}I_{p,q}+I_{p,q}X=0.

This is real Lie subalgebra of
M_{p+q}(\mathbb{C})
. It contains the subalgebras
\mathfrak{u}_{p},\mathfrak{u}_{q}
as Lie algebras of the subgroups
U(p)\times I_{q}
and
I_{p}\times U(q)
. Consider the inner product

\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle:\mathfrak{u}_{p,q}\times\mathfrak{u}_{p,q}\to\mathbb{R},\,\,\,\left\langle X,Y\right\rangle:=\textup{trace}(X^{\ast}Y).

Lemma 5.2.2
.

The inner product is symmetric and positive-definite.

Proof.

Note that

\left\langle X,Y\right\rangle=\textup{trace}(-I_{p,q}XI_{p,q}Y)=\textup{trace}(-I_{p,q}YI_{p,q}X)=\left\langle Y,X\right\rangle.

Since
\overline{\left\langle X,Y\right\rangle}=\left\langle Y,X\right\rangle
due to the invariance of trace under transpose, we conclude that the inner product is real and symmetric. It is positive-definite as
\left\langle X,X\right\rangle=\textup{trace}(X^{\ast}X)\geq 0
and equality holds if and only if
X
is the zero matrix.
∎

The Riemannian metric obtained by left translations of
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
will also be denoted by
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
. We shall analyze the geodesics for this metric. The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{u}_{p}\oplus\mathfrak{u}_{q}
of
U(p)\times U(q)
consists of

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A&0\\
0&D\end{array}\right),\,\,A+A^{\ast}=0,\,D+D^{\ast}=0.

Let
\mathfrak{n}
denote the orthogonal complement of
\mathfrak{u}_{p}\oplus\mathfrak{u}_{q}
inside
\mathfrak{u}_{p,q}
. As
\mathfrak{n}
is of (complex) dimension
pq
, and

\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&B\\
B^{\ast}&0\end{array}\right)\,\Big|\,B\in M_{p,q}(\mathbb{C})\right\}

is contained in
\mathfrak{n}
, this is all of it. We may verify that

\displaystyle\left[\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A&0\\
0&D\end{array}\right),\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&B\\
B^{\ast}&0\end{array}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&AB-BD\\
DB^{\ast}-B^{\ast}A&0\end{array}\right)\in\mathfrak{n}

\displaystyle\left[\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&B\\
B^{\ast}&0\end{array}\right),\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&C\\
C^{\ast}&0\end{array}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}BC^{\ast}-CB^{\ast}&0\\
0A&B^{\ast}C-C^{\ast}B\end{array}\right)\in\mathfrak{u}_{p}\oplus\mathfrak{u}_{q}.

Lemma 5.2.3
.

Let
\gamma
be the integral curve, initialized at
e
, for a left-invariant vector field
Y
. This curve is a geodesic if
Y(e)
either belongs to
\mathfrak{n}
or to
\mathfrak{u}_{p}\oplus\mathfrak{u}_{q}
.

Proof.

The Levi-Civita connection
\nabla
is given by the Koszul formula

2\left\langle X,\nabla_{Z}Y\right\rangle=Z\left\langle X,Y\right\rangle+Y\left\langle X,Z\right\rangle-X\left\langle Y,Z\right\rangle+\left\langle Z,[X,Y]\right\rangle+\left\langle Y,[X,Z]\right\rangle-\left\langle X,[Y,Z]\right\rangle.

Putting
Z=Y
and
X
, two left-invariant vector fields, in the above, we obtain

\left\langle X,\nabla_{Y}Y\right\rangle=\left\langle Y,[X,Y]\right\rangle.

To prove our claim, it suffices to show that
\nabla_{Y}Y=0
, ie,
\left\langle Y,[X,Y]\right\rangle=0
for any
X
. Let us assume that
Y(e)\in\mathfrak{n}
. If
X(e)\in\mathfrak{n}
, then
[X(e),Y(e)]\in\mathfrak{u}_{p}\oplus\mathfrak{u}_{q}
, which implies that
\left\langle Y(e),[X(e),Y(e)]\right\rangle=0
. If
X(e)\in\mathfrak{u}_{p}\oplus\mathfrak{u}_{q}
, then

\displaystyle\left\langle Y,[X,Y]\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&B\\
B^{\ast}&0\end{array}\right),\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&AB-BD\\
DB^{\ast}-B^{\ast}A&0\end{array}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textup{trace}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}B(DB^{\ast}-B^{\ast}A)&0\\
0&B^{\ast}(AB-BD)\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textup{trace}(BDB^{\ast}-BB^{\ast}A)+\textup{trace}(B^{\ast}AB-B^{\ast}BD)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

by the cyclic property of trace. Thus,
\nabla_{Y}Y=0
if
Y(e)\in\mathfrak{n}
; similar proof works if
Y(e)\in\mathfrak{u}_{p}\oplus\mathfrak{u}_{q}
.
∎

Remark 5.2.1
.

An integral curve of a left-invariant vector field (also called
1
-parameter subgroups) need not be a geodesic in
U(p,q)
. For instance, if
X+Y
is a left-invariant vector field given by
X(e)\in\mathfrak{u}_{p}\oplus\mathfrak{u}_{q}
and
Y(e)\in\mathfrak{n}
, then
\nabla_{X+Y}(X+Y)=0
if and only if
\nabla_{X}Y=\frac{1}{2}[X,Y]
and
\nabla_{Y}X=\frac{1}{2}[Y,X]
. This happens if and only if the metric is bi-invariant, i.e.,

\left\langle[X,Z],Y\right\rangle=\left\langle X,[Z,Y]\right\rangle.

This is not true in general; for instance, with
X(e)\in\mathfrak{u}_{p}\oplus\mathfrak{u}_{q}
and linearly independent
Y(e),Z(e)\in\mathfrak{n}
, we get
\left\langle[X,Z],Y\right\rangle-\left\langle X,[Z,Y]\right\rangle\neq 0
.

Consider the matrix

Y=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&B\\
B^{\ast}&0\end{array}\right)\in\mathfrak{n}.

Let
B=U\sqrt{B^{\ast}B}
and
B^{\ast}=\sqrt{B^{\ast}B}\,U^{\ast}
be the polar decompositions for the rectangular matrices. It follows from direct computation that

\displaystyle e^{Y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{p}+\frac{BB^{\ast}}{2!}+\frac{(BB^{\ast})^{2}}{4!}+\cdots&\frac{B}{1!}+\frac{B(B^{\ast}B)}{3!}+\frac{B(B^{\ast}B)^{2}}{5!}+\cdots\\
\frac{B^{\ast}}{1!}+\frac{(B^{\ast}B)B^{\ast}}{3!}+\frac{(B^{\ast}B)^{2}B^{\ast}}{5!}+\cdots&I_{q}+\frac{B^{\ast}B}{2!}+\frac{(B^{\ast}B)^{2}}{4!}+\cdots\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cosh(\sqrt{BB^{\ast}})&U\sinh(\sqrt{B^{\ast}B})\\
\sinh(\sqrt{B^{\ast}B})U^{\ast}&\cosh(\sqrt{B^{\ast}B})\end{array}\right).

It can be checked that

e^{\mathfrak{n}}\cap\left(U(p)\times U(q)\right)=\{I_{n}\}.

It is known that the non-zero eigenvalues of
Y
are the non-zero eigenvalues of
\sqrt{BB^{\ast}}
and their negatives.

Theorem 5.2.1
.

For any element
\mathcal{A}\in U(p,q)
, the associated matrix
\sqrt{\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}}
can be expressed uniquely as
e^{Y}
for
Y\in\mathfrak{n}
. Moreover, there is a unique way to express
\mathcal{A}
as a product of a unitary matrix and an element of
e^{\mathfrak{n}}
, and it is given by the polar decomposition.

In order to prove the result, we discuss some preliminaries on logarithm of complex matrices. In general, there is no unique logarithm. However, the Gregory series

\log A=-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{2}{2m+1}\left[(I-A)(I+A)^{-1}\right]^{2m+1}

converges if all the eigenvalues of
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
have positive real part, see
[
Higham, 2008
, §11.3, page 273]
. In particular,
\log A
is well-defined for Hermitian positive-definite matrix. This is often called the
principal logarithm
of
A
. This logarithm satisfies
e^{\log A}=A
. There is an integral form of logarithm that applies to matrices without real or zero eigenvalues; it is given by

\log A=(A-I)\int_{0}^{1}\left[s(A-I)+I\right]^{-1}ds.

Lemma 5.2.4
.

The inverse of
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}+I_{n}
for
\mathcal{A}\in U(p,q)
is given by

\left[\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}+I_{n}\right]^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{p}&-A^{-1}B\\
-B^{\ast}(A^{\ast})^{-1}&I_{q}\end{array}\right).

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}
has only positive eigenvalues,
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}+I_{n}
has no kernel. We note that

\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}+I_{n}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}2C^{\ast}C+2I_{p}&2A^{\ast}B\\
2B^{\ast}A&2B^{\ast}B+2I_{q}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}2A^{\ast}A&2A^{\ast}B\\
2B^{\ast}A&2D^{\ast}D\end{array}\right).

The inverse matrix satisfies

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}2A^{\ast}A&2A^{\ast}B\\
2B^{\ast}A&2D^{\ast}D\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}E&F\\
F^{\ast}&G\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{p}&0\\
0&I_{q}\end{array}\right).

As the matrices are Hermitian, the three constraints that
E,F,G
must satisfy (and are uniquely determined by) are

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}(A^{\ast}A)^{-1}-A^{-1}BF^{\ast}

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}(D^{\ast}D)^{-1}-D^{-1}CF

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-A^{-1}BG.

We note that
E=\frac{1}{2}I_{p}
,
G=\frac{1}{2}I_{q}
and
F=-\frac{1}{2}A^{-1}B
satisfy the above equations. For instance,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(A^{\ast}A)^{-1}-A^{-1}BF^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(A^{\ast}A)^{-1}+\frac{1}{2}A^{-1}BB^{\ast}(A^{\ast})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(A^{\ast}A)^{-1}+\frac{1}{2}A^{-1}(AA^{\ast}-I_{p})(A^{\ast})^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}I_{p},

where
BB^{\ast}=AA^{\ast}-I_{p}
is a consequence of
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\in U(p,q)
. Yet another consequence is
AC^{\ast}=BD^{\ast}
, which is equivalent to

A^{-1}B=(D^{-1}C)^{\ast}.

In a similar vein,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(D^{\ast}D)^{-1}-D^{-1}CF

\displaystyle==\frac{1}{2}(D^{\ast}D)^{-1}+\frac{1}{2}D^{-1}CC^{\ast}(D^{\ast})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(D^{\ast}D)^{-1}+\frac{1}{2}D^{-1}(DD^{\ast}-I_{q})(D^{\ast})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}I_{q},

where
CC^{\ast}=DD^{\ast}-I_{q}
is due to
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\in U(p,q)
.
∎

Proof of
5.2.1
.

We use Gregory series expansion for computing the principal logarithm of
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}
along with
Lemma

5.2.4
:

\displaystyle\log(\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}{\frac{2}{2m+1}}\left[2\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A^{\ast}A-I_{p}&A^{\ast}B\\
B^{\ast}A&D^{\ast}D-I_{q}\end{array}\right)\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{p}&-A^{-1}B\\
-B^{\ast}(A^{\ast})^{-1}&I_{q}\end{array}\right)\right]^{2m+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}{\frac{2}{2m+1}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&A^{-1}B\\
B^{\ast}(A^{\ast})^{-1}&0\end{array}\right)^{2m+1}.

We set
Y=\frac{1}{2}\log(\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A})
. It is clear that
Y\in\mathfrak{n}
and
e^{Y}=\sqrt{\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}}
. It is known that the exponential map is injective on Hermitian matrices. This implies the uniqueness of
Y
.

If
U_{1}e^{Y_{1}}=U_{2}e^{Y_{2}}
are two decompositions of
\mathcal{A}\in U(p,q)
with
U_{i}\in\textup{U}(p)\times\textup{U}(q)
and
Y_{i}\in\mathfrak{n}
, then

e^{2Y_{1}}=e^{Y_{1}}U_{1}^{\ast}U_{1}e^{Y_{1}}=e^{Y_{2}}U_{2}^{\ast}U_{2}e^{Y_{2}}=e^{2Y_{2}}.

By the injectivity of the exponential map (on Hermitian matrices), we obtain
Y_{1}=Y_{2}
, which implies that
U_{1}=U_{2}
.
∎

We infer the following (see
[
Yakubovich and Starzhinskii, 1975
, Lemma 1, page 211]
for a different proof) result.

Corollary 5.2.1
.

The exponential map
\textup{exp}:\mathfrak{u}_{p,q}\to U(p,q)
is surjective.

Proof.

Using the polar decomposition and
5.2.1
,

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}\big(\sqrt{\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}}\big)^{-1}\sqrt{\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{A}\big(\sqrt{\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}}\big)^{-1}e^{Y}.

Since the matrix exponential is surjective for
U(p)\times U(q)
, choose
Z\in\mathfrak{u}_{p}\oplus\mathfrak{u}_{q}
such that
e^{Z}=\mathcal{A}(\sqrt{\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}})^{-1}
. By Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula, we may express
e^{Z}e^{Y}
as exponential of an element in
\mathfrak{u}_{p,q}
.
∎

The distance from any matrix
\mathcal{A}\in U(p,q)
to
U(p)\times U(q)
is given by the length of the curve

\gamma(t)=\mathcal{A}\big(\sqrt{\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}}\big)^{-1}e^{tY},

which can be computed (and simplified via left-invariance) as follows

\ell(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}\|\gamma^{\prime}(t)\|_{\gamma(t)}\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}\|Y\|\,dt=\|Y\|.

Note that

\|Y\|^{2}=\textup{trace}(Y^{\ast}Y)=\textup{trace}\left[\textstyle{\frac{1}{4}}(\log(\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}))^{2}\right].

Thus, the distance squared function is given by

d^{2}:U(p,q)\to\mathbb{R},\,\,\mathcal{A}\mapsto\textstyle{\frac{1}{4}}\textup{trace}\left[\left(\log(\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A})\right)^{2}\right].

6.2
Proof of the equivariant cut locus theorem

In this section we will recall some results on Riemannian submersion and most of the results can be found in the book
[
Michor, 2008

Definition 6.2.1
.

Let
\pi:E\to B
be a smooth principal
G
-bundle. An
Ehresmann connection
on
E
is a smooth subbundle
\mathcal{H}
of
TE
, called the
horizontal bundle
of the connection, such that
TE=\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{V}
, where
\mathcal{V}_{p}=\ker\left(d\pi_{p}:T_{p}E\to T_{\pi(p)}B\right)
.

The bundle
\mathcal{V}
is called the
vertical bundle
, and it is independent of the connection chosen. It follows from the definition that
\mathcal{H}_{p}
depends smoothly on
p
and
\mathcal{H}_{p}\cap\mathcal{V}_{p}=\{0\}
. Moreover, the map
d\pi_{p}
restricts to
\mathcal{H}_{p}
is an isomorphism on
T_{\pi(p)}B
. If
E
is a Riemannian manifold with metric
g
, then by choosing a horizontal bundle
\mathcal{H}
we have
\mathcal{H}_{p}=\mathcal{V}_{p}^{\perp}
.

Definition 6.2.2
.

A smooth submersion
\pi:(E,g)\to(B,g^{\prime})
is called a
Riemannian submersion
if the linear map
d\pi_{p}
preserves the length of the horizontal vectors for each point
p\in E
. Equivalently,
d\pi_{p}
is a linear isometry between
\mathcal{H}_{p}
and
T_{\pi(p)}B
.

Using the above definitions we can define the following type of vectors.

Definition 6.2.3
.

Let
\pi:E\to B
be a Riemannian submersion. A vector field
X\in\mathfrak{X}(E)
is called

•

vertical
if for any
p\in E
,
X_{p}\in\mathcal{V}_{p}
, denoted by
X^{\text{ver}}
, and

•

horizontal
if for any
p\in E
,
X_{p}\in\mathcal{H}_{p}
, denoted by
X^{\text{hor}}
.

We can uniquely decompose any vector field
X\in\mathfrak{X}(E)
as

X=X^{\text{ver}}+X^{\text{hor}}

into its horizontal and vertical components.

Once we have a connection, we now have a preferred way of lifting vectors from
TB
to
TE
. Recall that
a vector
\tilde{X}\in T_{e}E
is a
lift
of
X\in T_{\pi(e)B}
if
T_{e}\pi(\tilde{X})=X
. In absence of a connection, there are many different choices of lifts of a vector, and any two choices differ by a vertical vector. That is, if
\tilde{X},\tilde{X}^{\prime}
are lifts of
X
, then
\tilde{X}-\tilde{X}^{\prime}
is vertical. Once we have a connection, we can define the
horizontal lift
(with respect to a connection
\mathcal{H}
) of
X
as the horizontal component of any lift of
X
. This definition
is, of course, independent of the choice of lift, since any two differ by a vertical vector, whose horizontal
component vanishes. Similarly, we can lift vector fields by lifting them in a pointwise fashion.

Definition 6.2.4

(Horizontal lift of vector fields)
.

Let

be a vector field and
\mathcal{H}\subset TE

.

Suppose that we have a curve

-geodesics maps to
N/G
-geodesics

On the other hand, if

will be a geodesic. In fact, it will be an

-geodesic. If not, let

\displaystyle l(\gamma)

\displaystyle=d\left(p,N/G\right)=l(\eta)

\displaystyle=l\left(\tilde{\eta}\right)<l\left(\tilde{\gamma}\right)=l(\gamma),

a contradiction.
Thus
\tilde{p}\in\mathrm{Se}(N)
. This proves that
\mathrm{Se}(N)/G=\mathrm{Se}(N/G)
. In order to prove the theorem, note that we have the following relation.

\mathrm{Se}(N/G)\subseteq\overline{\mathrm{Se}(N)}/G\subseteq\overline{\mathrm{Se}(N/G)}.

As
\overline{\mathrm{Se}(N)}
is a closed set and

is a compact Lie group, so
\overline{\mathrm{Se}(N)}/G
is closed. Thus, we have

\overline{\mathrm{Se}(N)}/G=\overline{\mathrm{Se}(N/G)}\implies\mathrm{Cu}(N)/G=\mathrm{Cu}(N/G).

∎

implies that
\mathbb{CP}^{n}\setminus X(2)
deformation retracts to
\mathrm{Cu}(X(2))
. Hence,
\mathrm{Cu}(X(2))
and
\mathbb{RP}^{n}
have the same homotopy type. However, it’s not a priori clear whether they are equal. Moreover, our calculation shows a computation of
\mathrm{Cu}(T_{1}\mathbb{S}^{n})\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{2n+1}
, where
T_{1}\mathbb{S}^{n}
is the unit tangent bundle of
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. We deduce that the cut locus is the unique non-trivial
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-bundle of
\mathbb{RP}^{n}
and this is a new computation.
