An Efficient Container Lemma
Wojciech Samotij

Abstract
We prove a new, efficient version of the hypergraph container theorems that is suited for hypergraphs with large uniformities. The main novelty is a refined approach to constructing containers that employs simple ideas from high-dimensional convex geometry. The existence of smaller families of containers for independent sets in such hypergraphs, which is guaranteed by the new theorem, allows us to improve upon the best currently known bounds for several problems in extremal graph theory, discrete geometry, and Ramsey theory. One of the applications of our efficient container lemma, a structural characterisation of n n -vertex graphs with clique number o ⁡ ( log ⁡ n / log ⁡ log ​ n ) o(\log n/\log\log n) , suggests that the new lemma is nearly best-possible for general hypergraphs of large uniformities.

Introduction

The hypergraph container theorems, proved several years ago by Balogh, Morris, and Samotij
[
6
]
and, independently, by Saxton and Thomason
[
42
]
, state that the family of independent sets of any uniform hypergraph whose edges are distributed somewhat evenly may be covered by (the families of subsets of) a small collection of sets, called containers, each of which is nearly independent. The original motivation for these results were several specific questions concerning enumeration of graphs avoiding a given subgraph and of sets of integers contining no arithmetic progressions of a given length. (The idea of considering these problems in the general context of independent sets in hypergraphs had been successfully pursued earlier in the breakthrough works of Conlon–Gowers
[
14
]
and Schacht
[
44
]
on extremal properties of random graphs and random sets of integers.) However, over the years, the scope of applicability of the container theorems has grown quite substantially (see, for example, the survey
[
7
]
and references therein). The two major reasons for this are the general form of the theorems (many problems can be cast in the language of independent sets in auxiliary hypergraphs) and the explicit, optimal dependence between the various parameters disguised under the vague phrases ‘small collection’, ‘evenly distributed’, and ‘nearly independent’ above.

The vast majority of applications of the container theorems concern sequences of hypergraphs of fixed uniformity and growing order and size. As a result, the explicit dependence of the various parameters involved in the statements of the container theorems on the uniformity of the hypergraph is merely a minor detail in all these works. Recently, however, the container theorems have been used to analyse sequences of hypergraphs whose uniformities grow with the numbers of vertices and edges. In these applications of the container method to questions in Ramsey theory
[
12
,
40
]
, discrete geometry
[
9
]
, and extremal graph theory
[
5
,
33
]
, the explicit dependence between uniformity and other parameters turned out to lie at the heart of the matter, obstructing the way to obtaining optimal bounds for several well studied functions. It is only fair to note here that this dependence is more favourable in the version of the theorem proved by Saxton and Thomason
[
42
]
. Having said that, the two constructions of containers presented in
[
6
]
and
[
42
]
are essentially equivalent and the differences between the final results reflect merely the differences in their analyses. This analysis was performed more carefully, and with a wiser choice of parameters, by the authors of
[
42
]
.

The basic container lemma, which is the building block of both proofs that really lies at the heart of the matter, is a statement that asserts the existence of a small family
\mathcal{C}
of containers for independent sets of an
s
-uniform hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
that satisfies
|C|\leqslant(1-\delta)v(\mathcal{H})
for every
C\in\mathcal{C}
and some positive constant
\delta
; see
[
6
, Proposition 3.1]
and
[
42
, Theorem 3.4]
. The stronger form of the theorem described in the first paragraph is then derived by recursively applying this basic lemma to the subhypergraphs of
\mathcal{H}
induced by the sets
C\in\mathcal{C}
as long as
C
still contains many edges of
\mathcal{H}
. The caveat here is that the proof methods used in both
[
6
]
and
[
42
]
necessarily yield
\delta\leqslant 1/s!
. (The short, non-algorithmic proof of the basic container lemma given recently by Bernshteyn, Delcourt, Towsner, and Tserunyan
[
10
]
seems to yield
\delta
that is doubly-exponentially small in
s
.) Since one typically requires the ratio
|C|/v(\mathcal{H})
to be bounded away from one for each final container
C
, at least
\exp\big(\Omega(s\log s)\big)
iterations are required; this substantially blows up the final number of containers when
s
is no longer a fixed constant. Finally, we remark that a different method of building containers for independent sets in hypergraphs was proposed and analysed by Saxton and Thomason
[
43
]
. Even though the parameter
\delta
in the basic container lemma proved in
[
43
]
is only polynomially small in the uniformity, the upper bound on the number of containers is far from optimal. Moreover, the lemma applies only to simple hypergraphs (i.e., hypergraphs whose every pair of vertices is contained in at most one edge) whereas the hypergraphs considered in most applications of the container method are far from being simple.

The main result of this work is a new, more efficient version of the basic container lemma in which the parameter
\delta
is only polynomially small in the uniformity. We postpone stating the strongest form of our new lemma until Section
2
and state here only its corollary that can be easily compared with
[
6
, Proposition 3.1]
. Following the notational convention of
[
6
]
, given a nonempty
s
-uniform hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
, we shall denote the numbers of its vertices and edges by
v(\mathcal{H})
and
e(\mathcal{H})
, respectively. Moreover, for every
T\subseteq V(\mathcal{H})
, we define

\deg_{\mathcal{H}}T=|\{A\in E(\mathcal{H}):T\subseteq A\}|

and, for every
t\in\{1,\dotsc,s\}
, we let

\Delta_{t}(\mathcal{H})=\max\big\{\deg_{\mathcal{H}}T:T\subseteq V(\mathcal{H})\text{ and }|T|=t\big\}.

The following theorem, an efficient basic container lemma, is a simplified version of our main technical result, Theorem
2.1
below.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
s
be a positive integer and let
\mathcal{H}
be a nonempty
s
-uniform hypergraph.
Suppose that
q\in(0,1)
and
K>0
are such that
q\cdot v(\mathcal{H})\geqslant 10^{8}s^{6}K
and, for every
t\in\{1,\dotsc,s\}
,

\Delta_{t}(\mathcal{H})\leqslant K\cdot\left(\frac{q}{10^{6}s^{5}}\right)^{t-1}\cdot\frac{e(\mathcal{H})}{v(\mathcal{H})}.

(1)

Then, there exist a family
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\binom{V(\mathcal{H})}{\leqslant q\cdot v(\mathcal{H})}
and functions
f\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{P}(V(\mathcal{H}))
and
g\colon\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{S}
such that, for every
I\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{H})
,

g(I)\subseteq I\subseteq g(I)\cup f(g(I))\qquad\text{and}\qquad|f(g(I))|\leqslant(1-\delta)\cdot v(\mathcal{H}),

where
\delta=(10^{3}s^{4}K)^{-1}
. Moreover, if
g(I)\subseteq I^{\prime}
and
g(I^{\prime})\subseteq I
for some
I,I^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{H})
, then
g(I)=g(I^{\prime})
.

Qualitatively, Theorem
1.1
is identical to the original basic container lemmas
[
6
, Proposition 3.1]
and
[
42
, Theorem 3.4]
. Quantitively, however, it is a significant improvement of these results. In order to demonstrate this, we shall present four applications of our new theorem to problems in extremal graph theory, discrete geometry, and Ramsey theory that had previously been attacked using the original container theorems, obtaining an essential improvement of the state-of-the-art result in each case. We discuss these applications and the relevant background in detail in the next three sections. We just point out here that one of these applications, an efficient version of the classical theorem of Kolaitis, Prömel, and Rothschild
[
28
]
, Theorem
1.2
below, strongly suggests that Theorem
1.1
is, up to lower-order terms, optimal for general hypergraphs of large uniformities (see the discussion below the statement of Theorem
1.2
).

1.1
The typical structure of graphs with no large cliques

Given graphs
G
and
H
, we say that
G
is
H
-free
if
G
does not contain
H
as a (not necessarily induced) subgraph. The study of typical properties of
H
-free graphs goes back to the seminal work of Erdős, Kleitman, and Rothschild
[
20
]
, who proved that almost all triangle-free graphs are bipartite.
1
1

1

That is, the probability that a uniformly random
K_{3}
-free subgraph of
K_{n}
is bipartite tends to one as
n
tends to infinity.
We shall consider here almost exclusively the case where
H
is a clique; for a wealth of information regarding other graphs, we refer the reader to
[
4
]
. The result of
[
20
]
was generalised by Kolaitis, Prömel, and Rothschild
[
28
]
, who showed that, for every fixed
r\geqslant 2
, almost all
K_{r+1}
-free graphs are
r
-partite.

Let us point out that this ‘structural’ characterisation of a typical
K_{r+1}
-free graph is in fact an enumeration result in disguise. Since every
r
-partite graph is
K_{r+1}
-free, the main result of
[
28
]
is that the number of
K_{r+1}
-free subgraphs of
K_{n}
is asymptotically equal to the number of
r
-partite subgraphs of
K_{n}
. Taking this point of view, one can say that the result of
[
28
]
was anticipated by the aforementioned work of Erdős, Kleitman, and Rothschild
[
20
]
, who also showed that, for every fixed
r
, there are
2^{\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r+1})+o(n^{2})}
many
K_{r+1}
-free subgraphs of
K_{n}
.
2
2

2

We write
\mathrm{ex}(n,H)
to denote the Turán number of a graph
H
, that is, the largest number of edges in an
H
-free graph with
n
vertices.
This estimate was generalised by Erdős, Frankl, and Rödl
[
17
]
, who proved that, for every fixed graph
H
, there are
2^{\mathrm{ex}(n,H)+o(n^{2})}
many
H
-free subgraphs of
K_{n}
.

In the above discussion, we have tried to stress that the forbidden graph
H
is fixed whereas
n
, the number of vertices in the host graphs, tends to infinity. Much less is known if one allows the order/size of
H
to grow with
n
. This more general question was considered only fairly recently by Bollobás and Nikiforov
[
11
]
; however, the results of
[
11
]
are only meaningful when the chromatic number of
H
stays bounded. A few years later, Mousset, Nenadov, and Steger
[
33
]
, extending the Erdős–Kleitman–Rothschild bound, proved that there are
2^{\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r+1})+o(n^{2}/r)}
many
K_{r+1}
-free subgraphs of
K_{n}
as long as
r\leqslant(\log n)^{1/4}/2
.
3
3

3

The
o(n^{2}/r)
error term is natural here as
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r+1})=\binom{n}{2}-\Theta(n^{2}/r)
.
Somewhat later, Balogh, Bushaw, Collares, Liu, Morris, and Sharifzadeh
[
5
]
strengthened this result considerably by showing that, under the slightly weaker assumption
2\leqslant r\leqslant(\log n)^{1/4}
, almost all
K_{r+1}
-free subgraphs of
K_{n}
are
r
-partite. (Both
[
33
]
and
[
5
]
relied on the original hypergraph container theorems.) Our first application of the new, efficient container lemma is the following strengthening of this result.

Theorem 1.2
.

If a function
r\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
satisfies
2\leqslant r(n)\leqslant\log n/(121\log\log n)
, then almost all
K_{r+1}
-free subgraphs of
K_{n}
are
r
-partite.

We point out that the assumption on the growth rate of
r
in Theorem
1.2
is nearly optimal. Indeed, a standard first-moment calculation shows that, for every positive constant
\varepsilon
, a uniformly random subgraph
G\subseteq K_{n}
contains no clique with
\lfloor(2+\varepsilon)\log_{2}n\rfloor
vertices whereas
\chi(G)\geqslant\Omega(n/\log n)
. On the other hand, it may well be that the assertion of the theorem remains true as long as
r(n)\leqslant(2-\varepsilon)\log_{2}n
for some positive constant
\varepsilon
. However, even removing the doubly-logarithmic term from the denominator in the assumed upper bound on
r(n)
will likely require significantly new ideas.

1.2
Lower bounds on
\varepsilon
-nets

Suppose that
X
is a finite set and let
\mathcal{R}
be an arbitrary collection of subsets of
X
. For a positive number
\varepsilon
, an
\varepsilon
-net
in
\mathcal{R}
is any set
N\subseteq X
that intersects every element of
\mathcal{R}
with cardinality at least
\varepsilon|X|
. In other words,
N
is an
\varepsilon
-net if
N\cap A\neq\emptyset
for every
A\in\mathcal{R}
with
|A|\geqslant\varepsilon|X|
.

One is usually interested in finding a small
\varepsilon
-net. However, this is not always possible. For example, if
\mathcal{R}
comprises all subsets of
X
, then every
\varepsilon
-net in
\mathcal{R}
must have more than
(1-\varepsilon)|X|
elements. One can rule out such ‘pathological’ examples by imposing a natural assumption on a measure of complexity of the family
\mathcal{R}
called the VC dimension. We say that a set
S
is
shattered
by a family
\mathcal{R}
if
\{A\cap S:A\in\mathcal{R}\}
contains all
2^{|S|}
subsets of
S
. The
VC dimension
(a shorthand for
Vapnik–Chervonenkis dimension
) of
\mathcal{R}
is the largest cardinality of a set that
\mathcal{R}
shatters. A seminal result of Haussler and Welzl
[
26
]
states that every family of subsets whose VC dimension is at most
d
admits an
\varepsilon
-net with at most
\lceil(8d/\varepsilon)\log(8d/\varepsilon)\rceil
elements, for every
\varepsilon>0
. Komlós, Pach, and Woeginger
[
29
]
improved this upper bound on the smallest size of an
\varepsilon
-net to
\big(d+o(1)\big)\cdot(1/\varepsilon)\log(1/\varepsilon)
, where
o(1)
denotes some function tending to zero with
\varepsilon
. Moreover, they constructed, for every
d\geqslant 2
, (random) families with VC dimension
d
that have no
\varepsilon
-net smaller than
\big(d-2+2/(d+1)-o(1)\big)\cdot(1/\varepsilon)\log(1/\varepsilon)
.

On the other hand, it was proved that various set families arising in geometry admit
\varepsilon
-nets of cardinality merely
O(1/\varepsilon)
, see
[
29
,
31
]
. In view of this, many researchers believed that in ‘geometric scenarios’ (with bounded VC dimension), there always exists an
\varepsilon
-net of size
O(1/\varepsilon)
. This belief was shown to be wrong by Alon
[
1
]
, who proved that, for arbitrary small
\varepsilon
, there are finite sets
X
of points in the plane such that every
\varepsilon
-net for the family comprising the intersections of
X
with straight lines (the
range space
of lines on
X
) must have at least
(1/\varepsilon)\cdot\omega(1/\varepsilon)
points, for some (very slowly growing) function
\omega
with
\lim_{x\to\infty}\omega(x)=\infty
. Alon speculated that there are planar sets of points
X
for which the factor
\omega(1/\varepsilon)
in the above statement could be replaced by
\Omega\big(\log(1/\varepsilon)\big)
.

In a paper that served as the main motivation for this work, Balogh and Solymosi
[
9
]
showed that, for arbitrarily small
\varepsilon>0
, there are sets
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that the range space of lines on
X
does not have
\varepsilon
-nets with fewer than
(1/\varepsilon)\big(\log(1/\varepsilon)\big)^{1/3-o(1)}
points; their proof relied on the hypergraph container theorems. We review the construction of Balogh and Solymosi
[
9
]
and, using our new, efficient container lemma, we further improve their lower bound, replacing the constant
1/3
in the exponent with
1/2
.

Theorem 1.3
.

The following holds for every
\varepsilon_{0}>0
. There exists an
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
and a finite set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that the smallest size of an
\varepsilon
-net for the family of intersections of straight lines with
X
is at least

\frac{1}{80\varepsilon}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\log\log(1/\varepsilon)}}.

We should mention here that, several years prior to
[
9
]
, Pach and Tardos
[
36
]
showed that the families defined by intersections of finite point sets with axis parallel rectangles (in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
) and axis-parallel boxes in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
may require
\varepsilon
-nets of sizes
\Omega\big((1/\varepsilon)\log\log(1/\varepsilon)\big)
and
\Omega\big((1/\varepsilon)\log(1/\varepsilon)\big)
, respectively; both these lower bounds are tight up to multiplicative constants, see
[
3
]
.

1.3
Upper bounds on Ramsey numbers

Given graphs
G
and
H
and a positive integer
k
, we write
G\rightarrow(H)_{k}
, and say that
G
is Ramsey for
H
in
k
colours
, if every
k
-colouring of the edges of
G
contains a monochromatic copy of
H
. In other words,
G\rightarrow(H)_{k}
if, for every
c\colon E(G)\to\llbracket{k}\rrbracket
, there is some
i\in\llbracket{k}\rrbracket
such that the graph
c^{-1}(i)
contains
H
as a subgraph.
4
4

4

Throughout the paper, we write
\llbracket{k}\rrbracket
as a shorthand for
\{1,\dotsc k\}
.
The famous theorem of Ramsey
[
38
]
states that, for all positive integers
n
and
k
, there is an integer
N
such that
K_{N}\rightarrow(K_{n})_{k}
; we shall denote the smallest such
N
, the
k
-colour Ramsey number of
K_{n}
, by
R(n;k)
. It is well-known that
R(n;k)\leqslant(kn)!/(n!)^{k}\leqslant k^{kn}
, see
[
19
,
25
]
.

Fifty years ago, Folkman
[
21
]
proved that, for every
n
, there exists a graph
G
such that
G\nsupseteq K_{n+1}
but, nevertheless,
G\rightarrow(K_{n})_{2}
and Nešetřil and Rödl
[
35
]
generalised this result to an arbitrary number of colours. Define the
k
-colour Folkman number for
K_{n}
by

F(n;k)=\min\big\{N\in\mathbb{N}:G\rightarrow(K_{n})_{k}\text{ for some $K_{n+1}$-free $G\subseteq K_{N}$}\big\}.

The constructions given in
[
21
,
35
]
yielded upper bounds on
F(n;k)
that are tower functions of height polynomial in
n
and
k
. On the other hand, the strongest lower bound on
F(n;k)
, due to Lefmann
[
30
]
, is only exponential in
kn
. In the recent years, the transference theorems of Conlon–Gowers
[
14
]
and Schacht
[
44
]
(see also
[
23
]
) were used by Rödl, Ruciński, and Schacht
[
41
]
and by Conlon and Gowers (unpublished) to give improved upper bounds on
F(n;k)
that were merely doubly-exponential in
n
and
k
. Soon afterwards, the first of these two groups of authors
[
40
]
used the hypergraph container theorems to give the first exponential bound
F(n;k)\leqslant\exp\big(O(n^{4}\log n+n^{3}k\log k)\big)
. Our next application of the efficient container lemma is the following improvement of this result.

Theorem 1.4
.

There exists a constant
C
such that, for all positive integers
n
and
k
,

F(n;k)\leqslant\big(CknR(n;k)\big)^{21n^{2}}\leqslant\exp\left(Ckn^{3}\log k\right).

Another well studied variation of the classical Ramsey numbers are induced Ramsey numbers. Given graphs
G
and
H
and a positive integer
k
, we write
G\rightarrow_{\mathrm{ind}}(H)_{k}
, and say that
G
is induced-Ramsey for
H
in
k
colours
, if every
k
-colouring of the edges of
G
contains a monochromatic induced copy of
H
. In other words,
G\rightarrow_{\mathrm{ind}}(H)_{k}
if, for every
c\colon E(G)\to\llbracket{k}\rrbracket
, there are an
i\in\llbracket{k}\rrbracket
and an injection
\varphi\colon V(H)\to V(G)
such that
\varphi\big(E(H)\big)\subseteq c^{-1}(i)
and
\varphi\big(E(H)^{c}\big)\cap E(G)=\emptyset
. The existence of induced-Ramsey graphs for every
H
and any number of colours
k
was established, independently, by Deuber
[
15
]
, by Erdős, Hajnal, and Pósa
[
18
]
, and by Rödl
[
39
]
. We may thus define the
k
-colour Ramsey number of
H
by

R_{\mathrm{ind}}(H;k)=\min\big\{N\in\mathbb{N}:G\rightarrow_{\mathrm{ind}}(H)_{k}\text{ for some }G\subseteq K_{N}\big\}.

The upper bounds on
R_{\mathrm{ind}}(H;k)
implied by the constructions of
[
15
,
18
,
39
]
were enormous. In spite of that, Erdős
[
16
]
conjectured that, for every
n
-vertex graph
H
, the
2
-colour induced Ramsey number
R_{\mathrm{ind}}(H;2)
is only exponential in
n
. The best-known result to date was obtained by Conlon, Fox, and Sudakov
[
13
]
, who proved that
R_{\mathrm{ind}}(H;2)\leqslant\exp\big(O(n\log n)\big)
for every
n
-vertex graph
H
. However, the method of
[
13
]
does not work when the number of colours is larger than two. The strongest general upper bound for
k
-colour induced Ramsey numbers in the case
k>2
that can be found in the literature is due to Fox and Sudakov
[
22
]
, who showed that
R_{\mathrm{ind}}(H;k)\leqslant\exp(C_{k}n^{3})
for every
n
-vertex
H
, where
C_{k}
depends only on
k
. However, Fox (private communication) informed us that the methods of
[
22
]
, which were optimised for sparse graphs
H
, may be used to prove that
R_{\mathrm{ind}}(H;k)\leqslant\exp(Ckn^{2}\log k)
. Our final application of the efficient container lemma is a short derivation of this bound.

Theorem 1.5
.

There exists a constant
C
such that, for every positive integer
k
and every
n
-vertex graph
H
,

R_{\mathrm{ind}}(H;k)\leqslant\big(Cn^{2}kR(n;k)\big)^{7n}\leqslant\exp\left(Ckn^{2}\log k\right).

Finally, let us mention that Conlon, Dellamonica Jr., La Fleur, Rödl, and Schacht
[
12
]
used the original container theorems to prove strong bounds on the induced Ramsey numbers of uniform hypergraphs.

1.4
Packaged statement

Each of the four illustrations of Theorem
1.1
presented in this paper requires iterative/recursive applications of the theorem. In order to save ourselves (and the reader) from repeating similar, routine arguments and calculations several times, it will be convenient for us to work with the following ‘packaged’ version of the theorem that is analogous to
[
6
, Theorem 2.2]
and
[
42
, Corollary 3.6]
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
s
be a positive integer and let
\mathcal{H}
be a nonempty
s
-uniform hypergraph.
Suppose that
\alpha,\beta,q\in(0,1)
and
E\geqslant v(\mathcal{H})
are such that
\alpha\beta q\cdot v(\mathcal{H})\geqslant 10^{9}s^{7}
and
10^{4}s^{5}q\leqslant\beta
and, for every
t\in\{2,\dotsc,s\}
,

\Delta_{t}(\mathcal{H})\leqslant\left(\frac{q}{10^{6}s^{5}}\right)^{t-1}\cdot\frac{E}{v(\mathcal{H})}.

Then there is a family
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(V(\mathcal{H}))
of at most
\exp\left(10^{4}s^{5}\beta^{-1}\log(e/\alpha)\cdot q\log(e/q)\cdot v(\mathcal{H})\right)
sets such that:

(i)

For every
I\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{H})
, there is a
C\in\mathcal{C}
such that
I\subseteq C
.

(ii)

For every
C\in\mathcal{C}
, either
|C|\leqslant\alpha v(\mathcal{H})
or there is a subset
W\subseteq C
with
|W|\geqslant(1-\beta)|C|
such that
e(\mathcal{H}[W])<E
.

1.6
from Theorem
1.1
is presented in Section

1.5
Organisation of the paper

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the crucial concept of degree measures, state our main technical result, Theorem
2.1
, and derive from it Theorems
1.1
and
1.6
. Section
3
is devoted to establishing key properties of degree measures; these properties are used in the subsequent Section
4

2
The main technical result

2.1
A word of motivation

The key idea behind the proof of the container lemma due to Morris and the authors
[
6
]
is to, given an
(r+1)
-uniform hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
and an independent set
I\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{H})
, consider a sequence of vertices of
\mathcal{H}
for inclusion in a small ‘signature’ set
S
and construct an
r
-uniform hypergraph
\mathcal{G}
from the neighbourhoods (link hypergraphs) of those among the considered vertices that belong to
I
. Crucially, each element of this sequence is allowed to depend only on the intersection of
I
with the set of its predecessors; this guarantees that
\mathcal{G}
depends solely on
S
. Since
\mathcal{G}
comprises only neighbourhoods of vertices in
I
, we have
I\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{G})
. This facilitates induction on the uniformity of the hypergraph.

Whereas there is essentially one way to define containers for independent sets in a
1
-uniform hypergraph, the general description of the inductive step given above leaves plenty of room for manoeuvre. The approach taken in
[
6
]
was, roughly speaking, to cap the degrees of all vertices of
\mathcal{G}
at some predefined value
\Delta
and, at the same time, make sure that
e(\mathcal{G})\geqslant\beta\Delta v(\mathcal{G})
for some constant
\beta
; this way, the ratio of the maximum and the average degrees of the constructed hypergraph
\mathcal{G}
remained bounded by a constant. The advantage of this approach was its relative simplicity. However, this simplicity came at a price; the gap between the maximum and the average degrees was forced to grow by a factor of at least
r+1
, at each step of the induction (reducing uniformity from
r+1
to
r
).
5
5

5

For those readers who are somewhat familiar with the proof in
[
6
]
, the essence of the above shortcoming was the following. Only one vertex of degree
\Delta
in
\mathcal{G}
forced us to remove an edge from
\mathcal{H}
, but while counting the edges of
\mathcal{G}
that contain some vertex with degree
\Delta
, we accounted for the possibility that every edge of
\mathcal{G}
contains
r
vertices of degree
\Delta
.
As a result, the crucial parameter
\delta
in the basic container lemma could not exceed
1/s!
, where
s
is the uniformity of the original hypergraph.

Here, we use a similar high-level inductive strategy. However, we take a refined approach to choosing a sequence of vertices of the
(r+1)
-uniform
\mathcal{H}
while constructing the
r
-uniform
\mathcal{G}
; this yields a much more favourable dependence of the parameter
\delta
on the uniformity
s
. The key new idea is to abandon the wish to control the maximum degree of
\mathcal{G}
and instead focus on the
\ell^{2}
-norm of its degree sequence. In other words, we measure hypergraphs with
\ell^{2}
-norms, rather than
\ell^{\infty}
-norms, of their degree sequences. Viewing hypergraphs as vectors in high-dimensional Euclidean spaces allows us to reduce the problem of constructing a sequence of vertices to be considered for inclusion in the ‘signature’ to an elementary problem in convex geometry.

2.2
Degree measures

We begin by extending the notion of the
degree measure
of a hypergraph, which was introduced by Saxton and Thomason
[
42
]
. For a non-empty
r
-uniform hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
with vertex set
V
and a
t\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket
, we define the
t
-degree measure
of
\mathcal{H}
, denoted by
\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}
, to be the probability distribution on
\binom{V}{t}
, the family of all
t
-element subsets of
V
, given by

\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}(T)=\deg_{\mathcal{H}}T\cdot\left(\sum_{U\in\binom{V}{t}}\deg_{\mathcal{H}}U\right)^{-1}=\frac{\deg_{\mathcal{H}}T}{\binom{r}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}.

In other words,
\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}
is the probability distribution induced by the following random experiment. Select an edge
A
of
\mathcal{H}
uniformly at random and output a
t
-element subset
T\subseteq A
chosen uniformly at random from
\binom{A}{t}
.

Throughout this paper, we shall identify (as we already did in the above definition) the measure
\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}
with its density (with respect to the counting measure), which we shall view as an element of the
\binom{|V|}{t}
-dimensional vector space of
\mathbb{R}
-valued functions on
\binom{V}{t}
. Since the
1
-degree measure will be of particularly high importance, we shall refer to it simply as the degree measure and often suppress the superscript
(1)
from the notation, denoting it by
\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}
. Given a positive integer
d
and a vector
\xi=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote by
\|\xi\|
its
\ell^{2}
-norm, so that

\|\xi\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\xi_{i}^{2}.

2.3
The main technical result

We are now ready to state the main technical result of this paper, Theorem
2.1
below. We postpone the proof of the theorem to Section
4
; the proof will use several simple properties of degree measures that will be derived in Section
3
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose that a nonempty
s
-uniform hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
and reals
p,\delta\in(0,1)
satisfy

300s^{4}\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{s}\binom{s-1}{t-1}\left(\frac{5000s^{3}}{p}\right)^{t-1}\|\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}\|^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{\delta\cdot v(\mathcal{H})}\leqslant\frac{p}{500}.

(2)

Then, there exist a family
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\binom{V(\mathcal{H})}{\leqslant 30s^{2}p\cdot v(\mathcal{H})}
and functions
f\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{P}(V(\mathcal{H}))
and
g\colon\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{S}
such that, for every
I\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{H})
,

g(I)\subseteq I\subseteq g(I)\cup f(g(I))\qquad\text{and}\qquad|f(g(I))|\leqslant(1-\delta)\cdot v(\mathcal{H}).

Moreover, if
g(I)\subseteq I^{\prime}
and
g(I^{\prime})\subseteq I
for some
I,I^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{H})
, then
g(I)=g(I^{\prime})
.

2.4
The simple and packaged versions

In this section, we derive Theorems
1.1
and
1.6
from our main technical result, Theorem
2.1
above. We start with a short proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Derivation of Theorem
1.1
from Theorem
2.1
.

Let
p=q/(30s^{2})
and let
\delta=(10^{3}s^{4}K)^{-1}
. It suffices to verify that
\mathcal{H}
,
p
, and
\delta
satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
2.1
, which will give us the claimed family
\mathcal{S}
and functions
g
and
f
. To this end, note that

\|\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}\|^{2}=\sum_{T\in\binom{V(\mathcal{H})}{t}}\left(\frac{\deg_{\mathcal{H}}T}{\binom{s}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}\right)^{2}\leqslant\frac{\Delta_{t}(\mathcal{H})}{\binom{s}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}\cdot\sum_{T\in\binom{V(\mathcal{H})}{t}}\frac{\deg_{\mathcal{H}}T}{\binom{s}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}=\frac{\Delta_{t}(\mathcal{H})}{\binom{s}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}

for every

and thus the assumptions of the theorem imply that

\begin{split}\sum_{t=1}^{s}\binom{s-1}{t-1}\left(\frac{5000s^{3}}{p}\right)^{t-1}\|\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}\|^{2}&\leqslant\sum_{t=1}^{s}\binom{s-1}{t-1}\left(\frac{150000s^{5}}{q}\right)^{t-1}\cdot\frac{\Delta_{t}(\mathcal{H})}{\binom{s}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}\\
&\leqslant\frac{K}{v(\mathcal{H})}\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{s}\frac{t}{s}\cdot\left(\frac{150000s^{5}}{10^{6}\cdot s^{5}}\right)^{t-1}\leqslant\frac{2K}{v(\mathcal{H})}.\end{split}

Moreover,
300s^{4}\cdot 2K\leqslant 1/\delta
and
p\cdot\delta\cdot v(\mathcal{H})=q\cdot(30s^{2}\cdot 10^{3}s^{4}K)^{-1}\cdot v(\mathcal{H})\geqslant 500
.
∎

We now turn to the proof of Theorem
1.6
. The key ingredient here is the following lemma, which, roughly speaking, states that a hypergraph that is ‘robustly dense’ contains a large subhypergraph whose maximum degree is not much larger than its average degree. The statement and the proof of the lemma are inspired by the work of Morris and Saxton
[
32
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be an
s
-uniform hypergraph and suppose that, for some positive
\beta
and
M
, every set
W\subseteq V(\mathcal{H})
with
|W|\geqslant(1-\beta)v(\mathcal{H})
satisfies
e(\mathcal{H}[W])\geqslant M
. Then, there is a subhypergraph
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
with at least
M
edges that satisfies

\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})\leqslant\left\lceil\frac{s}{\beta}\cdot\frac{e(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})}{v(\mathcal{H})}\right\rceil.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
be a largest (in terms of the number of edges) subhypergraph of
\mathcal{H}
satisfying
\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})\leqslant\lceil\frac{sM}{\beta v(\mathcal{H})}\rceil
and let
X\subseteq V(\mathcal{H})
be the set of vertices of
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
whose degree achieves the bound
\lceil\frac{sM}{\beta v(\mathcal{H})}\rceil
. Observe that every edge of
\mathcal{H}
that is disjoint from
X
must belong to
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
and, consequently,
e(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})\geqslant e(\mathcal{H}-X)
. If
|X|\leqslant\beta v(\mathcal{H})
, then
e(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})\geqslant M
by our assumption on
\mathcal{H}
. Otherwise, if
|X|>\beta v(\mathcal{H})
,

e(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{v\in V(\mathcal{H})}\deg_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}v\geqslant\frac{|X|}{s}\cdot\left\lceil\frac{sM}{\beta v(\mathcal{H})}\right\rceil>M.

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

We shall say that a set
C\subseteq V(\mathcal{H})
is a
good container
if either
|C|\leqslant\alpha v(\mathcal{H})
or if there is a subset
W\subseteq C
with
|W|\geqslant(1-\beta)|C|
such that
e(\mathcal{H}[W])<E
. We will construct a rooted tree
\mathcal{T}
whose vertices are subsets of
V(\mathcal{H})
that has the following properties:

(i)

The root of
\mathcal{T}
is
V(\mathcal{H})
.

(ii)

If an independent set
I\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{H})
is contained in a non-leaf vertex of
\mathcal{T}
, then
I
is contained in some child of this vertex in
\mathcal{T}
.

(iii)

Every leaf of
\mathcal{T}
is a good container.

(iv)

Every non-leaf vertex of
\mathcal{T}
has at most
(e/q)^{q\cdot v(\mathcal{H})}
children.

(v)

The height of
\mathcal{T}
is at most
10^{4}s^{5}\beta^{-1}\cdot\log(e/\alpha)
.

The set of leaves of
\mathcal{T}
will then form a collection of containers for the independent sets of
\mathcal{H}
that has the desired properties.

We build such a tree starting from the root
V(\mathcal{H})
by iteratively applying Theorem
1.1
to (a carefully chosen subhypergraph of) the subhypergraph of
\mathcal{H}
induced by a leaf
C
of
\mathcal{T}
that is not yet a good container and attaching the resulting family of containers (for the independent sets of
\mathcal{H}[C]
, that is, the independent sets of
\mathcal{H}
that are contained in
C
) as children of
C
(which, as a result, ceases to be a leaf of
\mathcal{T}
) until no such leaves are left. This way, properties
(i)
–
(iii)
are clearly satisfied. However, we still need to show that the final tree has properties
(iv)
and
(v)
.

To this end, suppose that
C\subseteq V(\mathcal{H})
is not a good container, that is,
|C|>\alpha v(\mathcal{H})
and every
W\subseteq C
with
|W|\geqslant(1-\beta)|C|
satisfies
e(\mathcal{H}[W])\geqslant E
. Lemma
2.2
invoked with
\mathcal{H}\leftarrow\mathcal{H}[C]
supplies a subhypergraph
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{H}[C]
with at least
E
edges that satisfies

\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})\leqslant\left\lceil\frac{s}{\beta}\cdot\frac{e(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})}{|C|}\right\rceil\leqslant\frac{2s}{\beta}\cdot\frac{e(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})}{|C|}=\frac{2s}{\beta}\cdot\frac{e(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})}{v(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})},

where the second inequality follows from our assumption that
e(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})\geqslant E\geqslant v(\mathcal{H})\geqslant|C|
. Since, for every
t\in\{2,\dotsc,s\}
,

\Delta_{t}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})\leqslant\Delta_{t}(\mathcal{H})\leqslant\left(\frac{q}{10^{6}s^{5}}\right)^{t-1}\cdot\frac{E}{v(\mathcal{H})}\leqslant\left(\frac{q}{10^{6}s^{5}}\right)^{t-1}\cdot\frac{e(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})}{v(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})},

Theorem
1.1
invoked with
\mathcal{H}\leftarrow\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
and
K\leftarrow 2s/\beta
supplies sets
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\binom{C}{\leqslant q\cdot|C|}
and functions
f\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{P}(C)
and
g\colon\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})\to\mathcal{S}
such that, for every
I\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})

I\subseteq g(I)\cup f(g(I))\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{|f(g(I))|}{|C|}\leqslant 1-\frac{\beta}{2\cdot 10^{3}s^{5}}.

Since
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{H}[C]
, we have
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{H}[C])\subseteq\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
and we may define

\mathcal{C}_{C}=\big\{g(I)\cup f(g(I)):I\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{H}[C])\big\}.

By construction, the family

\mathcal{H}[C]
and

|\mathcal{C}_{C}|\leqslant|\mathcal{S}|\leqslant\sum_{i=0}^{q\cdot|C|}\binom{|C|}{i}\leqslant\left(\frac{e}{q}\right)^{q\cdot|C|}\leqslant\left(\frac{e}{q}\right)^{q\cdot v(\mathcal{H})},

(iv)
. Finally, for every

\frac{|D|}{|C|}\leqslant q+1-\frac{\beta}{2s}\cdot\frac{1}{10^{3}s^{4}}\leqslant 1-\frac{\beta}{10^{4}s^{5}},

since we assumed that

. In particular, if a set
C

\mathcal{T}
that lies at distance

\alpha\leqslant\frac{|C|}{v(\mathcal{H})}\leqslant\left(1-\frac{\beta}{10^{4}s^{5}}\right)^{d}\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\beta d}{10^{4}s^{5}}\right).

This implies that

0pt(\mathcal{T})\leqslant\frac{10^{4}s^{5}}{\beta}\cdot\log\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)+1\leqslant\frac{10^{4}s^{5}}{\beta}\cdot\log\left(\frac{e}{\alpha}\right),

establishing
(v)
.

3
Properties of degree measures

3.1
Norms of degree measures

As we shall be estimating the
\ell^{2}
-norms of
t
-degree measures of various uniform hypergraphs, we collect here several useful properties of this quantity. We first give general lower and upper bounds on the
\ell^{2}
-norm of the
t
-degree measure of a hypergraph in terms of the numbers of its vertices and edges and its maximum
t
-degree. Throughout this section,
r
is a positive integer. We stress here that all of our hypergraphs are allowed to have multiple edges, that is, every edge can have an arbitrary positive multiplicity. (This idea was first introduced by Saxton and Thomason
[
42
]
.) Moreover, when computing
\deg_{\mathcal{H}}
and
e(\mathcal{H})
, we always count edges with multiplicities.

Fact 3.1
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{H}
is a nonempty
r
-uniform hypergraph. For every
t\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket
,

\max\left\{\frac{1}{\binom{v(\mathcal{H})}{t}},\frac{1}{\binom{r}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}\right\}\leqslant\|\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}\|^{2}\leqslant\frac{\Delta_{t}(\mathcal{H})}{\binom{r}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}.

Proof.

The upper bound is straightforward:

\|\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}\|^{2}=\sum_{T\in\binom{V(\mathcal{H})}{t}}\left(\frac{\deg_{\mathcal{H}}T}{\binom{r}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}\right)^{2}\leqslant\frac{\Delta_{t}(\mathcal{H})}{\binom{r}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}\cdot\sum_{T\in\binom{V(\mathcal{H})}{t}}\frac{\deg_{\mathcal{H}}T}{\binom{r}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}=\frac{\Delta_{t}(\mathcal{H})}{\binom{r}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}.

For the lower bound, let

\mathcal{T}=\left\{T\in\binom{V(\mathcal{H})}{t}:\deg_{\mathcal{H}}T>0\right\}

and observe that

|\mathcal{T}|\leqslant\min\left\{\binom{v(\mathcal{H})}{t},\binom{r}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})\right\}.

It follows form the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality that

\|\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}\|^{2}=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\left(\frac{\deg_{\mathcal{H}}T}{\binom{r}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}\right)^{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\cdot\left(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\frac{\deg_{\mathcal{H}}T}{\binom{r}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|},

implying the lower bound.
∎

Our second observation states that the
\ell^{2}
-norm of a
t
-degree measure of a hypergraph cannot increase much when one deletes from it a small proportion of its edges.

Fact 3.2
.

If
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is a nonempty subhypergraph of an
r
-uniform hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
, then, for every
t\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket
,

\|\sigma_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}^{(t)}\|\leqslant\frac{e(\mathcal{H})}{e(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})}\cdot\|\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}\|.

Proof.

The assertion follows simply because
\deg_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}T\leqslant\deg_{\mathcal{H}}T
for every
T\subseteq V(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
and hence

\sigma_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}^{(t)}(T)\leqslant\frac{e(\mathcal{H})}{e(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})}\cdot\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}(T).\qed

Our final lemma relates the
\ell^{2}
-norm of the degree measure of a uniform hypergraph to a simple property of its edge distribution.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{H}
is a nonempty
r
-uniform hypergraph. If a set
D\subseteq V(\mathcal{H})
satisfies
e(\mathcal{H}-D)\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\cdot e(\mathcal{H})
for some
\varepsilon>0
, then

|D|\geqslant\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{r}\right)^{2}\cdot\|\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\|^{-2}.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{1}_{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(\mathcal{H})}
be the characteristic vector of
D
. It follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality that

\left\langle\mathbf{1}_{D},\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\right\rangle^{2}\leqslant\|\mathbf{1}_{D}\|^{2}\cdot\|\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\|^{2}=|D|\cdot\|\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\|^{2}.

Since at least an
\varepsilon
-proportion of edges of
\mathcal{H}
contain at least one vertex of
D
,

\left\langle\mathbf{1}_{D},\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{r\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}\cdot\sum_{v\in D}\deg_{\mathcal{H}}v\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{r},

giving the desired lower bound on
|D|
.
∎

3.2
Degree measures and link hypergraphs

Suppose that
\mathcal{H}
is an
(r+1)
-uniform hypergraph with vertex set
V
. Given a
v\in V
, we shall denote by
\mathcal{H}_{v}
the
link hypergraph
of
v
(the
neighbourhood
of
v
in
\mathcal{H}
), that is, the
r
-uniform hypergraph with vertex set
V
whose edges are all the
r
-element sets
A
such that
\{v\}\cup A
is an edge of
\mathcal{H}
. A property of crucial importance for us is that, for each
t\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket
, the
t
-degree measure of
\mathcal{H}
is a convex combination of the
t
-degree measures of the link hypergraphs of its vertices. Moreover, each of these convex combinations has the same coefficients – the coordinates of the
1
-degree measure vector
\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}
.

Remark
.

Even though
\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}
was defined only for nonempty hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}
, for the sake of brevity, we shall often write
0\cdot\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}
even if
\mathcal{H}
has no edges. In this case,
0\cdot\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}
should be interpreted as the zero vector of appropriate dimension.

Fact 3.4
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{H}
is a nonempty
(r+1)
-uniform hypergraph with vertex set
V
. For every
t\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket
,

\sum_{v\in V}\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}(v)\cdot\sigma_{\mathcal{H}_{v}}^{(t)}=\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}.

Proof.

It follows from our definition of a link hypergraph that
e(\mathcal{H}_{v})=\deg_{\mathcal{H}}v
for each
v\in V
and, more generally, for every
T\in\binom{V}{t}
,

\deg_{\mathcal{H}_{v}}T=\begin{cases}\deg_{\mathcal{H}}(T\cup\{v\})&\text{if $v\notin T$},\\
0&\text{if $v\in T$}.\end{cases}

Consequently,

\begin{split}\sum_{v\in V}\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}(v)\cdot\sigma_{\mathcal{H}_{v}}^{(t)}(T)&=\sum_{v\in V}\frac{\deg_{\mathcal{H}}v}{(r+1)\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}\cdot\frac{\deg_{\mathcal{H}_{v}}T}{\binom{r}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H}_{v})}\\
&=\frac{1}{(r+1)\binom{r}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}\cdot\sum_{v\in V\setminus T}\deg_{\mathcal{H}}(T\cup\{v\})\\
&=\frac{(r+1-t)\cdot\deg_{\mathcal{H}}T}{(r+1)\binom{r}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}=\frac{\deg_{\mathcal{H}}T}{\binom{r+1}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}=\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}(T),\end{split}

where we used the identity
(r+1)\binom{r}{t}=\binom{r+1}{t}(r+1-t)
.
∎

In our arguments, we shall employ the following relation between the
\ell^{2}
-norm of the
(t+1)
-degree measure of a hypergraph and the
\ell^{2}
-norms of the
t
-degree measures of the link hypergraphs of its vertices.

Fact 3.5
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{H}
is a nonempty
(r+1)
-uniform hypergraph with vertex set
V
. For every
t\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket
,

\sum_{v\in V}\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}(v)^{2}\cdot\|\sigma_{\mathcal{H}_{v}}^{(t)}\|^{2}=\frac{\|\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t+1)}\|^{2}}{t+1}.

Proof.

A quick way to verify the claimed identity is to observe that both the left- and the right-hand sides of the claimed equality express the probability of obtaining the same outcome in two independent executions of the following random process: Pick an edge
A
of
\mathcal{H}
uniformly at random, choose a
(t+1)
-element subset
S
of
A
uniformly at random, mark a vertex
v\in S
chosen uniformly at random, and return the pair
(v,S)
.

More explicitly, using the identities
\deg_{\mathcal{H}}v=e(\mathcal{H}_{v})
, valid for every
v\in V
, and
\deg_{\mathcal{H}_{v}}T=\deg_{\mathcal{H}}(T\cup\{v\})
, valid for each
T\in\binom{V}{t}
and
v\in V\setminus T
, and
(r+1)\binom{r}{t}=(t+1)\binom{r+1}{t+1}
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}(v)^{2}\cdot\|\sigma_{\mathcal{H}_{v}}^{(t)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}\left(\frac{\deg_{\mathcal{H}}v}{(r+1)\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}\right)^{2}\cdot\sum_{T\in\binom{V}{t}}\left(\frac{\deg_{\mathcal{H}_{v}}T}{\binom{r}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H}_{v})}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{(r+1)\binom{r}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}\right)^{2}\cdot\sum_{T\in\binom{V}{t}}\sum_{v\in V\setminus T}\big(\deg_{\mathcal{H}}(T\cup\{v\})\big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{(t+1)\binom{r+1}{t+1}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}\right)^{2}\cdot\sum_{S\in\binom{V}{t+1}}\sum_{v\in S}\left(\deg_{\mathcal{H}}S\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t+1}\cdot\sum_{S\in\binom{V}{t+1}}\left(\frac{\deg_{\mathcal{H}}S}{\binom{r+1}{t+1}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}\right)^{2}=\frac{\|\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t+1)}\|^{2}}{t+1}.\qed

r

The bottom line is that there is a way to choose a sequence of vertices to be queried for membership in

r
-uniform hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
and
t\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket
,

\hat{\Delta}_{t}(\mathcal{H})=\frac{r}{t}\cdot e(\mathcal{H})\cdot\|\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}\|^{2};

(4)

one should think of
\hat{\Delta}_{t}(\mathcal{H})
as a robust analogue of the maximum degree
\Delta_{t}(\mathcal{H})
. In particular, Fact
3.1
implies that
\hat{\Delta}_{1}(\mathcal{H})\leqslant\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{H})
; even though equality sometimes holds (when
\mathcal{H}
is regular), in general the ratio
\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{H})/\hat{\Delta}_{1}(\mathcal{H})
can be arbitrarily large. Our next lemma shows that this inequality becomes nearly tight, up to a multiplicative factor of
O(r)
, after we delete a small proportion of the edges of
\mathcal{H}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{H}
is a nonempty
r
-uniform hypergraph. Then, for every
R\geqslant r
, there is an
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
with
e(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})>(1-\frac{r}{R})\cdot e(\mathcal{H})
such that
\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})\leqslant R\cdot\hat{\Delta}_{1}(\mathcal{H})
.

Proof.

Given a nonempty
r
-uniform hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
and
R\geqslant r
, define

X=\left\{v\in V(\mathcal{H}):\deg_{\mathcal{H}}v>R\cdot\hat{\Delta}_{1}(\mathcal{H})\right\}.

By the definition of
X
and
\hat{\Delta}_{1}(\mathcal{H})
, we have

\|\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\|^{2}\geqslant\sum_{v\in X}\left(\frac{\deg_{\mathcal{H}}v}{r\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}\right)^{2}>\frac{R\cdot\hat{\Delta}_{1}(\mathcal{H})}{r\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}\cdot\sum_{v\in X}\frac{\deg_{\mathcal{H}}v}{r\cdot e(\mathcal{H})}=R\cdot\|\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\|^{2}\cdot\sum_{v\in X}\frac{\deg_{\mathcal{H}}v}{r\cdot e(\mathcal{H})},

which implies that

\sum_{v\in X}\deg_{\mathcal{H}}v<\frac{r}{R}\cdot e(\mathcal{H}).

In particular, deleting from
\mathcal{H}
all edges containing at least one vertex of
X
yields a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
satisfying the assertion of this lemma.
∎

Since the degree measures
\sigma^{(t)}(\mathcal{H})
are not defined when
\mathcal{H}
is an empty hypergraph, in order to streamline our analysis, we will start building the hypergraph
\mathcal{H}^{(r)}
by seeding it with a fixed well-behaved
r
-uniform hypergraph. In order to guarantee that, at the end of the algorithm, this initial seed constitutes only a negligible proportion of
\mathcal{H}^{(r)}
, we need to make sure that the link hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}_{v}^{(r+1)}
that the algorithm adds to
\mathcal{H}^{(r)}
are somewhat large. We will achieve this by (temporarily) removing vertices of very small degree from various subhypergraphs of
\mathcal{H}^{(r+1)}
.

Fact 4.6
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{H}
is a nonempty hypergraph with vertex set
V
. Then, for every
\beta>0
, there is a spanning
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
such that
e(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})\geqslant(1-\beta)\cdot e(\mathcal{H})
and, for each
v\in V
,
\deg_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}v
is either zero or at least
\beta\cdot e(\mathcal{H})/|V|
.

Proof.

Form a spanning subgraph
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{H}
by iteratively deleting all edges containing some vertex with degree smaller than
\beta\cdot e(\mathcal{H})/|V|
. Clearly, each edge of
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
contains only vertices with degrees at least
\beta\cdot e(\mathcal{H})/|V|
. Moreover, the number of edges deleted from
\mathcal{H}
while forming
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
cannot exceed
\beta\cdot e(\mathcal{H})
.
∎

4.5
The algorithm

We are ready to present the algorithm that underlies the proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Input

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an
(r+1)
-uniform hypergraph with vertex set
V
. Let
\varepsilon\in\big(0,(9(r+1))^{-1}\big)
and
p\in(0,1)
, define

a=\frac{25}{\varepsilon^{2}},

and suppose that

\|\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}\|^{2}\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{3}p}{50(r+1)}=\frac{\varepsilon p}{2a(r+1)}.

(5)

Observe that uniformly scaling the multiplicities of all edges of
\mathcal{G}
by a positive integer factor
k
does not affect the
t
-degree measure
\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}^{(t)}
, for any
t\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket
, nor does it change the family
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{G})
of independent sets of
\mathcal{G}
. It does, however, increase the value of
e(\mathcal{G})
, and thus also the value of
\hat{\Delta}_{t}(\mathcal{G})
, for each
t\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket
, by the same multiplicative factor
k
. Consequently, we may assume, without loss of generality, that there is a (large) positive integer
m
such that

\frac{m}{2a}\cdot\binom{|V|}{r}\leqslant\hat{\Delta}_{1}(\mathcal{G})\leqslant\frac{m}{a}\cdot\binom{|V|}{r}.

(6)

Finally, let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
be a vector with nonnegative coordinates and let
I
be an independent set of
\mathcal{G}
.

Setup

Let
L
be the empty set and let
\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(0)}
be the hypergraph obtained from the complete
r
-uniform hypergraph with vertex set
V
by changing the multiplicities of all of its edges to
m
, so that
e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(0)})=m\binom{|V|}{r}
.
Further, apply Lemma
4.5
, with
R\leftarrow\frac{r+1}{\varepsilon}
, to find an
\mathcal{A}^{(0)}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
satisfying

e(\mathcal{A}^{(0)})\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\cdot e(\mathcal{G})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{A}^{(0)})\leqslant\frac{r+1}{\varepsilon}\cdot\hat{\Delta}_{1}(\mathcal{G}).

(7)

Finally, let

b=\left\lceil\frac{2p}{\varepsilon}\cdot|V|\right\rceil\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma=(1-3\varepsilon)^{-1}\cdot\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G})\|.

Main loop

Do the following for
j=0,1,\ldots
:

(S1)

If
|L|=b
or
e(\mathcal{A}^{(j)})<(1-2\varepsilon)\cdot e(\mathcal{G})
, then let
J=j
and
STOP
.

(S2)

Let
\hat{\mathcal{A}}^{(j)}
be a canonically chosen spanning subgraph of
\mathcal{A}^{(j)}
satisfying the assertion of Fact
4.6
with
\beta\leftarrow\varepsilon
. For each
v\in V
, let
\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j,v)}=\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j)}\cup\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{v}^{(j)}
and let
v_{j}
be a canonically chosen vertex that minimises the quantity

e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j,v)})\cdot\left(\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j,v)})\|^{2}-(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\sigma^{2}\right)

over all
v\in V
whose degree in
\hat{\mathcal{A}}^{(j)}
is nonzero.

(S3)

If
v_{j}\in I
, then add
j
to the set
L
and let

\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j+1)}=\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j,v_{j})}=\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j)}\cup\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{v_{j}}^{(j)}.

Otherwise, let
\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j+1)}=\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j)}
.

(S4)

Let
\mathcal{A}^{(j+1)}
be the hypergraph obtained from
\mathcal{A}^{(j)}
by removing all edges containing
v_{j}
.

Output

After
STOP
is called in the main loop, let
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}^{(J)}
and
\mathcal{G}_{*}=\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(J)}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(0)}
, that is,
\mathcal{G}_{*}
is the hypergraph satisfying
\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(J)}=\mathcal{G}_{*}\cup\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(0)}
. (Recall once more that all our hypergraphs contain edges with multiplicities.)

4.6
Basic properties of the algorithm and the key dichotomy

In this section, we establish several basic properties of the algorithm and state its key ‘dichotomy’ property, which we shall derive in later sections. Moreover, we explain how to use the algorithm to prove Lemma
4.1
. We start by showing that the algorithm terminates on every input and that the output hypergraph
\mathcal{G}_{*}
and the final set
L
retain important information about the input set
I
.

Observation 4.7
.

For every
I\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{G})
, the map
\{0,\dotsc,J-1\}\ni j\mapsto v_{j}\in V
is injective and hence the algorithm terminates. Moreover,
I\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{G}_{*})
and
L=\big\{j\in\{0,\dotsc,J-1\}:v_{j}\in I\big\}
.

Proof.

The first assertion holds because in step
(S4)
, all edges containing
v_{j}
are removed, and hence its degree remains zero in each
\mathcal{A}^{(j^{\prime})}
with
j^{\prime}>j
. Therefore, the algorithm stops after at most
|V|
iterations of the main loop. The second assertion holds because
\mathcal{G}_{*}
comprises only edges of the link hypergraphs
\mathcal{G}_{v}
for which
v\in I
and because
j\in L
if and only if
v_{j}\in I
.
∎

We next observe that the set
L
contains all the information about the input set
I
that is needed to reconstruct the execution of the algorithm.

Observation 4.8
.

If the algorithm produces the same set
L
for two inputs
I,I^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{G})
, then also the hypergraphs
\mathcal{G}_{*}
, the numbers
J
, and the sequences
v_{0},\dotsc,v_{J-1}
are the same for both inputs.

Proof.

The only decisions that depend on the input set
I
are taken in step
(S3)
of the algorithm. Each time this step is executed, the decision taken is encoded in the set
L
by placing, or not placing, the index
j
in
L
.
∎

The function
S\colon\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathcal{P}(V)
whose existence is asserted by Lemma
4.1
will be defined as follows:

S_{I}=\{v_{j}\colon j\in L\}=\{v_{j}\colon 0\leqslant j<J\text{ and }v_{j}\in I\}.

(8)

In other words,
S_{I}
comprises precisely those among the queried vertices
v_{0},\dotsc,v_{J-1}
that belong to the input set
I
. Note that
|S_{I}|=|L|\leqslant b
, since the algorithm terminates in step
(S1)
as soon as
L
has
b
elements. We shall now show that the knowledge of the set
S_{I}
is enough to reconstruct the final set
L
and hence, as stated in Observation
4.8
, the entire execution of the algorithm. In fact, the following stronger statement is true.

Lemma 4.9
.

Suppose that, for two inputs
I,I^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{G})
, we have
S_{I}\subseteq I^{\prime}
and
S_{I^{\prime}}\subseteq I
. Then, for both these inputs, the algorithm outputs the same set
L
.

Suppose that
S_{I}=S_{I^{\prime}}
for some
I,I^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{G})
. As
S_{I}\subseteq I
and
S_{I^{\prime}}\subseteq I^{\prime}
, by construction, Lemma
4.9
implies that the output set
L
must be the same for both
I
and
I^{\prime}
.

Proof of Lemma
4.9
.

Suppose that two inputs
I
and
I^{\prime}
yield sets
L
and
L^{\prime}
, respectively, with
L\neq L^{\prime}
. Let
j
be the smallest index such that
j\in(L\setminus L^{\prime})\cup(L^{\prime}\setminus L)
; without loss of generality, we may assume that
j\in L\setminus L^{\prime}
. Since
L\cap\{0,\dotsc,j-1\}=L^{\prime}\cap\{0,\dotsc,j-1\}
, the algorithm produces the same sequences
v_{0},\dotsc,v_{j}
while working with inputs
I
and
I^{\prime}
. Since
j\in L\setminus L^{\prime}
, we must have
v_{j}\in S_{I}
and
v_{j}\notin I^{\prime}
. In particular,
S_{I}\nsubseteq I^{\prime}
.
∎

Finally, define the vector
\alpha^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{r+1}
as in (
3
):

\alpha^{*}=(1+\varepsilon)^{10}\cdot(\alpha,0)+\frac{50(r+1)}{\varepsilon^{2}p}\cdot(0,\alpha).

The key dichotomy property, stated in our next lemma, is that either the algorithm inspects many vertices of the hypergraph (before encountering the
b
th vertex of
I
) or the final hypergraph
\mathcal{G}_{*}
is a good ‘model’ of
\mathcal{G}
, in the sense that the
\ell^{2}
-norm of
\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*})
does not exceed the
\ell^{2}
-norm of
\sigma_{\alpha^{*}}(\mathcal{G})
.

Lemma 4.10
.

At least one of the following holds:

(1)

J\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{(r+1)^{2}}\cdot\|\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}\|^{-2}
,

(2)

\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*})\|\leqslant\|\sigma_{\alpha^{*}}(\mathcal{G})\|
.

We shall prove Lemma
4.10
, which lies at the heart of the matter, in the next two sections. We finish the current section with a short derivation of Lemma
4.1
, which is now straightforward. Given an
(r+1)
-uniform hypergraph
\mathcal{G}
and numbers
\varepsilon
and
p
as in the statement of the lemma, we may define the function
S\colon I(\mathcal{G})\to\mathcal{P}(V)
as in (
8
), by running the algorithm on each input
I\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{G})
. If
J\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{(r+1)^{2}}\cdot\|\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}\|^{-2}
, we place
S_{I}
in the family
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
and let
C(S_{I})=V\setminus\{v_{j}:0\leqslant j<J\}
; note that
I\setminus S_{I}\subseteq C(S_{I})
as
S_{I}=I\cap(V\setminus C(S_{I}))
by (
8
). Otherwise, we place
S_{I}
in the family
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}
and let
\mathcal{F}(S_{I})=\mathcal{G}_{*}
; Lemma
4.10
implies the desired property of each such hypergraph
\mathcal{F}(S_{I})
. Lemma
4.9
and Observation
4.8
guarantee that the set
C(S_{I})
or the hypergraph
\mathcal{F}(S_{I})
depend only on the set
S_{I}
, and not on
I
itself, and that the function
S
has the claimed consistency property.

4.7
The geometric lemma

The most important elementary operation performed by the algorithm described in Section
4.5
is to choose some
v\in V
and add the
r
-uniform link hypergraph
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{v}^{(j)}
to the hypergraph
\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j)}
, obtaining a new hypergraph
\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j,v)}=\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j)}\cup\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{v}^{(j)}
. Since we want the final hypergraph
\mathcal{G}_{*}
to have small
\ell^{2}
-norm of
\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*})
, in step
(S2)
of the algorithm, we consider a vertex
v
that, essentially, minimises the
\ell^{2}
-norm of
\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j,v)})
over all eligible
v\in V
. The following proposition, which is the core of the proof of Theorem
2.1
, bounds the minimum of
\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j,v)})\|
from above. Given an
(r+1)
-uniform hypergraph
\mathcal{A}
and a vector
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
, we define

\hat{\Delta}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{t=1}^{r}\alpha_{t}\cdot\hat{\Delta}_{t+1}(\mathcal{A}).

Proposition 4.11
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{A}
is an
(r+1)
-uniform hypergraph with vertex set
V
and that
\mathcal{G}_{*}
is an
r
-uniform hypergraph with the same vertex set. Suppose that
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
has nonnegative coordinates. Then, there exists a vertex
v\in V
with nonzero degree in
\mathcal{A}
such that the hypergraph
\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v}=\mathcal{G}_{*}\cup\mathcal{A}_{v}
satisfies

\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v})\|^{2}\leqslant\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*})\|^{2}+\frac{\deg_{\mathcal{A}}v}{e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v})}\cdot\left(\left(2\cdot\frac{\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})\|}{\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*})\|}-2+\frac{\hat{\Delta}_{1}(\mathcal{A})}{e(\mathcal{G}_{*})}\right)\cdot\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*})\|^{2}+\frac{\hat{\Delta}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})}{e(\mathcal{G}_{*})}\right).

(9)

Since the right-hand side of (
9
) is rather complicated, let us explain the underlying intuition. The two terms
\hat{\Delta}_{1}(\mathcal{A})/e(\mathcal{G}_{*})
and
\hat{\Delta}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})/e(\mathcal{G}_{*})
should be viewed as ‘error terms’. If we assumed that they are both zero, inequality (
9
) would simplify to

\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v})\|^{2}\leqslant\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*})\|^{2}+\frac{2\deg_{\mathcal{A}}v}{e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v})}\cdot\big(\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})\|-\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*})\|\big)\cdot\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*})\|.

(10)

This simplified inequality (
10
) states that, as long as the
\ell^{2}
-norm of
\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*})
exceeds that of
\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})
, there is a vertex
v\in V
such that the
\ell^{2}
-norm of
\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v})
is strictly smaller than that of
\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*})
. Moreover, the difference
\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*})\|-\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v})\|
is proportional to the difference
\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})\|-\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*})\|
. Proposition
4.11
will allow us to show that, as we repeatedly update
\mathcal{G}_{*}\leftarrow\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v}
in step
(S3)
of the algorithm, the value
\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*})\|
drifts, rather quickly, towards
\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})\|
.

The reason why Proposition
4.11
is true stems from Fact
3.6
, which states that the vector
\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})
is a convex combination of the vectors
\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A}_{v})
, where
v
ranges over
V
, and the coefficient of each
\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A}_{v})
in this combination is proportional to
\deg_{\mathcal{A}}v
. This basic property of the degree measures enables us to express the problem of minimising
\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v})\|
, solved by the proposition, in a simple, abstract way, as we now do in the next lemma.

Lemma 4.12
.

Suppose that
\nu_{1},\dotsc,\nu_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
all have nonnegative coordinates and
\|\lambda\|_{1}=\lambda_{1}+\dotsb+\lambda_{k}=1
. Define

\nu=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\cdot\nu_{i}.

For every positive
x
, every
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with nonnegative coordinates, and all
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{k}\in(0,x]
, there exists an
i\in\llbracket{k}\rrbracket
such that
\lambda_{i}>0
and the vector
\mu_{i}
defined by

\mu_{i}=(1-x_{i}\lambda_{i})\cdot\mu+x_{i}\lambda_{i}\cdot\nu_{i}

satisfies

\|\mu_{i}\|^{2}\leqslant\|\mu\|^{2}+\lambda_{i}x_{i}\cdot\left(\left(2\cdot\frac{\|\nu\|}{\|\mu\|}-2+x\cdot\|\lambda\|^{2}\right)\cdot\|\mu\|^{2}+x\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}^{2}\|\nu_{j}\|^{2}\right).

(11)

Proof.

Note first that, for every
i\in\llbracket{k}\rrbracket
,

\|\mu_{i}\|^{2}=\|\mu+x_{i}\lambda_{i}\cdot(\nu_{i}-\mu)\|^{2}=\|\mu\|^{2}+2x_{i}\lambda_{i}\cdot\left\langle\nu_{i}-\mu,\mu\right\rangle+x_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}\cdot\|\nu_{i}-\mu\|^{2}.

(12)

Since
\left\langle\nu_{i},\mu\right\rangle\geqslant 0
, by our assumption on non-negativity of the coordinates, we have

\|\nu_{i}-\mu\|^{2}=\|\nu_{i}\|^{2}-2\left\langle\nu_{i},\mu\right\rangle+\|\mu\|^{2}\leqslant\|\mu\|^{2}+\|\nu_{i}\|^{2}.

Substituting this inequality into (
12
), dividing both sides by
x_{i}
, and summing over
i\in\llbracket{k}\rrbracket
yields

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\|\mu_{i}\|^{2}-\|\mu\|^{2}}{x_{i}}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k}2\lambda_{i}\cdot\left\langle\nu_{i}-\mu,\mu\right\rangle+\max_{i}x_{i}\cdot\left(\|\lambda\|^{2}\|\mu\|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}^{2}\|\nu_{i}\|^{2}\right).

(13)

The definition of
\nu
, the assumption
\|\lambda\|_{1}=1
, and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality give

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\cdot\left\langle\nu_{i}-\mu,\mu\right\rangle=\left\langle\nu-\mu,\mu\right\rangle=\left\langle\nu,\mu\right\rangle-\|\mu\|^{2}\leqslant\|\nu\|\cdot\|\mu\|-\|\mu\|^{2}.

Substituting this inequality into (
13
), recalling the assumption that
\max_{i}x_{i}\leqslant x
, yields

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\|\mu_{i}\|^{2}-\|\mu\|^{2}}{x_{i}}\leqslant 2\left(\|\nu\|\cdot\|\mu\|-\|\mu\|^{2}\right)+x\cdot\left(\|\lambda\|^{2}\|\mu\|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}^{2}\|\nu_{i}\|^{2}\right).

(14)

Finally, as
\|\lambda\|_{1}=1
, there must exist an
i\in\llbracket{k}\rrbracket
such that
\lambda_{i}>0
and the
i
th summand in the left-hand side of (
14
) is at most
\lambda_{i}
times the right-hand side of (
14
). This gives

\|\mu_{i}\|^{2}-\|\mu\|^{2}\leqslant\lambda_{i}x_{i}\cdot\left(2\left(\|\nu\|\cdot\|\mu\|-\|\mu\|^{2}\right)+x\left(\|\lambda\|^{2}\|\mu\|^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}^{2}\|\nu_{j}\|^{2}\right)\right),

which is easily seen to be equivalent to the desired inequality (
11
).
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.11
.

For every
v\in V
, let
\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v}=\mathcal{G}_{*}\cup\mathcal{A}_{v}
. We claim that, for each
t\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket
, the
t
-degree measure of
\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v}
is a convex combination of the
t
-degree measures of
\mathcal{G}_{*}
and
\mathcal{A}_{v}
and the coefficients in this convex combination are proportional to
e(\mathcal{G}_{*})
and
\deg_{\mathcal{A}}v
, respectively. Indeed, since for every
T\subseteq V
, the degree of
T
in
\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v}
is simply the sum of the degrees of
T
in
\mathcal{G}_{*}
and
\mathcal{A}_{v}
, we have

e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v})\cdot\sigma_{\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v}}^{(t)}=e(\mathcal{G}_{*})\cdot\sigma_{\mathcal{G}_{*}}^{(t)}+e(\mathcal{A}_{v})\cdot\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{v}}^{(t)}=e(\mathcal{G}_{*})\cdot\sigma_{\mathcal{G}_{*}}^{(t)}+\deg_{\mathcal{A}}v\cdot\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{v}}^{(t)}.

Dividing the above equality through by
e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v})=e(\mathcal{G}_{*})+\deg_{\mathcal{A}}v
, we obtain

\sigma_{\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v}}^{(t)}=\left(1-\frac{\deg_{\mathcal{A}}v}{e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v})}\right)\cdot\sigma_{\mathcal{G}_{*}}^{(t)}+\frac{\deg_{\mathcal{A}}v}{e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v})}\cdot\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{v}}^{(t)}.

Define, for each
v\in V
,

x_{v}=\frac{(r+1)\cdot e(\mathcal{A})}{e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v})}\leqslant\frac{(r+1)\cdot e(\mathcal{A})}{e(\mathcal{G}_{*})}.

(15)

Since
\mathcal{A}
is
(r+1)
-uniform, we have, for each
v\in V
,

x_{v}\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(v)=\frac{\deg_{\mathcal{A}}v}{e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v})}

and, consequently, for each
t\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket
,

\sigma_{\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v}}^{(t)}=\big(1-x_{v}\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(v)\big)\cdot\sigma_{\mathcal{G}_{*}}^{(t)}+x_{v}\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(v)\cdot\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{v}}^{(t)}.

(16)

We now invoke Lemma
4.12
with
k=|V|
, the vectors
\nu_{1},\dotsc,\nu_{k}
replaced by
\{\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A}_{v}):v\in V\}
, the vector
\lambda
replaced by
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}
, the vector
\mu
replaced by
\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*})
, the numbers
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
replaced by
\{x_{v}:v\in V\}
, and
x
replaced by
(r+1)\cdot e(\mathcal{A})/e(\mathcal{G}_{*})
; note that
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{k}\leqslant x
, see (

). Fact
3.6
implies that

\nu=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\cdot\nu_{i}=\sum_{v\in V}\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(v)\cdot\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A}_{v})=\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A}),

and, if
i\in\llbracket{k}\rrbracket
corresponds to

, then
\lambda_{i}x_{i}=x_{v}\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(v)=\deg_{\mathcal{A}}v/e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v})

\mu_{i}=\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{v})
. Recalling the definition of

x\cdot\|\lambda\|^{2}=\frac{(r+1)\cdot e(\mathcal{A})}{e(\mathcal{G}_{*})}\cdot\|\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}\|^{2}=\frac{\hat{\Delta}_{1}(\mathcal{A})}{e(\mathcal{G}_{*})}.

Finally, Fact

\begin{split}x\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}^{2}\|\nu_{i}\|^{2}&=\frac{(r+1)\cdot e(\mathcal{A})}{e(\mathcal{G}_{*})}\cdot\sum_{v\in V}\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(v)^{2}\cdot\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A}_{v})\|^{2}\\
&=\frac{(r+1)\cdot e(\mathcal{A})}{e(\mathcal{G}_{*})}\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{r}\alpha_{t}\cdot\sum_{v\in V}\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(v)^{2}\cdot\|\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{v}}^{(t)}\|^{2}\\

It is now straightforward to verify that Lemma

4.8
Proof of the key dichotomy property

In this section, we use Proposition
4.11
to bound the expression from step
(S2)
in the description of our algorithm. This is the most technically demanding part of the proof. Throughout this section, we use the notation introduced in Section
4.5
. We start with an easy dichotomy.

Lemma 4.13
.

If
e(\mathcal{A})<(1-2\varepsilon)\cdot e(\mathcal{G})
, then

J\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{(r+1)^{2}}\cdot\|\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}\|^{-2}.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{A}
is obtained from
\mathcal{A}^{(0)}
by removing all edges that contain at least one of the vertices
v_{0},\dotsc,v_{J-1}
, we have

e(\mathcal{A}^{(0)})-e(\mathcal{A})\leqslant\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\deg_{\mathcal{A}^{(0)}}v_{J}\leqslant J\cdot\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{A}^{(0)}).

Consequently, it follows from (
7
) and our upper bound on
e(\mathcal{A})
that

J\geqslant\frac{e(\mathcal{A}^{(0)})-e(\mathcal{A})}{\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{A}^{(0)})}\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\cdot e(\mathcal{G})}{(r+1)\cdot\hat{\Delta}_{1}(\mathcal{G})}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{(r+1)^{2}}\cdot\|\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}\|^{-2}.\qed

Lemma 4.14
.

If
e(\mathcal{A})\geqslant(1-2\varepsilon)\cdot e(\mathcal{G})
, then
e(\mathcal{G}_{*})\geqslant p\cdot e(\mathcal{G})
.

Proof.

By construction,

e(\mathcal{G}_{*})=e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(J)})-e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(0)})=\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\left(e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j+1)})-e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j)})\right)=\sum_{j\in L}\deg_{\hat{\mathcal{A}}^{(j)}}v_{j}\geqslant\sum_{j\in L}\frac{\varepsilon\cdot e(\mathcal{A}^{(j)})}{|V|}.

Our assumption
e(\mathcal{A})\geqslant(1-2\varepsilon)\cdot e(\mathcal{G})
implies that the algorithm terminated with
|L|=b\geqslant 2p/\varepsilon\cdot|V|
, see step
(S1)
. Consequently, recalling that
\varepsilon<1/4
,

e(\mathcal{G}_{*})\geqslant|L|\cdot\frac{\varepsilon\cdot e(\mathcal{A})}{|V|}\geqslant\frac{2p\cdot|V|}{\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon\cdot(1-2\varepsilon)\cdot e(\mathcal{G})}{|V|}\geqslant p\cdot e(\mathcal{G}).\qed

Our next lemma lies at the heart of the matter. For brevity, define

\hat{\Delta}:=3\cdot\hat{\Delta}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}).

Lemma 4.15
.

For every
j\in\{0,\dotsc,J\}
,

\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j)})\|^{2}\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\sigma^{2}+\frac{a\cdot\hat{\Delta}}{e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j)})}.

(17)

Proof.

We prove (
17
) by induction on
j
. Since
\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(0)}
is (an integer multiple of) the complete
r

\sigma_{\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(0)}}^{(t)}
is the uniform probability measure on
\binom{V}{t}
and, consequently,

\|\sigma_{\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(0)}}^{(t)}\|^{2}=\binom{|V|}{t}^{-1}

for every
t\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket
. On the other hand, Fact
3.5
implies that, for every
t\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket

\hat{\Delta}_{t+1}(\mathcal{G})=\frac{r+1}{t+1}\cdot e(\mathcal{G})\cdot\|\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}^{(t+1)}\|^{2}=(r+1)\cdot e(\mathcal{G})\cdot\sum_{v\in V}\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}(v)^{2}\cdot\|\sigma_{\mathcal{G}_{v}}^{(t)}\|^{2}.

Fact
3.1
implies that, for every

such that
\mathcal{G}_{v}

\|\sigma_{\mathcal{G}_{v}}^{(t)}\|^{2}\geqslant\binom{|V|}{t}^{-1}=\|\sigma_{\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(0)}}^{(t)}\|^{2}

\hat{\Delta}_{t+1}(\mathcal{G})\geqslant(r+1)\cdot e(\mathcal{G})\cdot\sum_{v\in V}\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}(v)^{2}\cdot\|\sigma_{\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(0)}}^{(t)}\|^{2}=\hat{\Delta}_{1}(\mathcal{G})\cdot\|\sigma_{\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(0)}}^{(t)}\|^{2}.

Recall from (
6

m

\hat{\Delta}_{1}(\mathcal{G})\geqslant\frac{m}{2a}\cdot\binom{|V|}{r}=\frac{e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(0)})}{2a},

which, substituted into the previous inequality, implies that

\|\sigma_{\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(0)}}^{(t)}\|^{2}\leqslant\frac{2a}{e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(0)})}\cdot\hat{\Delta}_{t+1}(\mathcal{G}).

(18)

After we multiply both sides of (

\alpha_{t}
and sum the resulting inequalities over all

, we obtain

\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(0)})\|^{2}\leqslant 2a\cdot\frac{\hat{\Delta}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G})}{e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(0)})},

which implies (

) when
j=0

Suppose now that
j\geqslant 0
and assume that (

) holds; we shall show that this inequality remains true after we replace

with
j+1
. We may assume that
\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j+1)}=\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j)}\cup\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{v_{j}}^{(j)}
, as otherwise
\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j+1)}=\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j)}
and there is nothing to prove. Let
v
be a vertex satisfying the assertion of Proposition
4.11
with

and

so that

e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j+1)})\cdot\left(\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j+1)})\|^{2}-(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\sigma^{2}\right)\leqslant e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j,v)})\cdot\left(\|\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j,v)})\|^{2}-(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\sigma^{2}\right),

(19)

so it suffices to bound the right-hand side of (

.

The assertion of Proposition

Furthermore, recall from (

\frac{\hat{\Delta}_{1}(\hat{\mathcal{A}}^{(j)})}{e(\mathcal{G}_{*}^{(j)})}\leqslant\frac{(1-3\varepsilon)^{-2}}{a}\leqslant\frac{3}{a}

\begin{split}\Delta_{1}(\hat{\mathcal{A}}^{(j)})&\leqslant\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{A}^{(j)})\leqslant\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{A}^{(0)})\leqslant\frac{r+1}{\varepsilon}\cdot\hat{\Delta}_{1}(\mathcal{G})\\

Proof of Lemma
4.10
.

immediately gives

5
Probabilistic inequalities

The proofs of Theorems
1.2
,
1.3
, and
1.4
make use of well-known probabilistic inequalities. The first of them are standard tail bounds for binomial distributions.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
n
be a positive integer, let
p\in[0,1]
, and suppose that
X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)
. Then, for every
\delta\in[0,1]
,

\Pr\big(X\leqslant(1-\delta)np\big)\leqslant\exp(-\delta^{2}np/2)\quad\text{and}\quad\Pr\big(X\geqslant(1+\delta)np\big)\leqslant\exp(-\delta^{2}np/3).

We shall also need the following version of Janson’s inequality
[
27
]
, which can be easily deduced from the statements found in
[
2
, Chapter 8]
.

Theorem 5.2

(Janson’s inequality)
.

Suppose that
\Omega
is a finite set and let
B_{1},\dotsc,B_{k}
be arbitrary subsets of
\Omega
. Form a random subset
R\subseteq\Omega
by independently keeping each
\omega\in\Omega
with probability
p_{\omega}\in[0,1]
. For each
i\in\llbracket{k}\rrbracket
, let
X_{i}
be the indicator of the event that
B_{i}\subseteq R
and define

\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Delta=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i<j\leqslant k\\
B_{i}\cap B_{j}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}X_{j}].

Then,

\Pr\big(\text{$B_{i}\nsubseteq R$ for all $i\in\llbracket{k}\rrbracket$}\big)\leqslant\exp\left(-\min\left\{\frac{\mu}{2},\frac{\mu^{2}}{4\Delta}\right\}\right).

6
The typical structure of
K_{r+1}
-free graphs

6.1
Outline

The first, key part of the proof of Theorem
1.2
is showing that, for sufficiently small
\delta
, the number of
K_{r+1}
-free subgraphs of
K_{n}
that are not
\delta n^{2}
-close to being
r
-partite is much smaller than
2^{\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r+1})}
, which is a trivial lower bound on the number of
K_{r+1}
-free graphs. This statement is derived from a container lemma for
K_{r+1}
-free graphs (Proposition
6.1
below), which is obtained by applying Theorem
1.6
to the
\binom{r+1}{2}
-uniform hypergraph that encodes copies of
K_{r+1}
in
K_{n}
, and the ‘supersaturated’ version of the stability theorem of Erdős and Simonovits proved in
[
5
]
and stated as Lemma
6.3
below. Proposition
6.1
, which is the main result of this section, supplies a covering of all
K_{r+1}
-free subgraphs of
K_{n}
with few containers, each of which is a subgraph of
K_{n}
with either fewer than
n^{2}/8
edges or fewer than
n^{r+1/2}
copies of
K_{r+1}
(after we delete from it some
n^{2-1/(8r)}
edges), whereas Lemma
6.3
is used to show that all containers with nearly
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r+1})
edges must be close to being
r
-partite.

The remainder of the proof is showing that all but an
2^{-n/(10r)^{4}}
-proportion of
K_{r+1}
-free subgraphs of
K_{n}
that are
\delta n^{2}
-close to being
r
-partite are in fact
r
-partite. Our three-step argument is loosely based on the methods of
[
8
]
. First, we show that all but a tiny fraction of graphs in our collection admit an optimal, balanced
r
-partition with at most
\delta n^{2}
monochromatic edges (i.e., edges whose both endpoints belong to the same part of the partition); an
r
-partition is optimal if it minimises the number of monochromatic edges and it is balanced if each partite set comprises at least
n/(2r)
vertices. Second, we bound from above the number of remaining graphs whose associated
r
-partition induces a monochromatic copy of
K_{1,D}
in one of the parts, where
D=\lfloor n/(2^{14}r^{5}\log n)\rfloor
. Third, we bound from above the number of remaining graphs whose associated
r
-partition induces a monochromatic matching with a given number of edges in one of the parts. The second and third steps complement one another as every graph with
t
edges contains either a copy of
K_{1,D}
or a matching with at least
t/D
edges.

6.2
An efficient container lemma for
K_{r+1}
-free graphs

The following statement, which is the main technical result of this section, is an efficient container lemma for
K_{r+1}
-free subgraphs of
K_{n}
. It is obtained by applying Theorem
1.6
to the
\binom{r+1}{2}
-uniform hypergraph that encodes copies of
K_{r+1}
in
K_{n}
.

Proposition 6.1
.

For almost all
n
and every
r
satisfying
2\leqslant r\leqslant\log n/(121\log\log n)
, there exists a collection
\mathcal{G}
of at most
\exp\left(n^{2-1/(8r)}\right)
subgraphs of
K_{n}
such that:

(i)

Each
K_{r+1}
-free subgraph of
K_{n}
is contained in some member of
\mathcal{G}
.

(ii)

Each
G\in\mathcal{G}
either has fewer than
n^{2}/8
edges or it contains a subgraph
G^{\prime}
with
e(G^{\prime})\geqslant e(G)-n^{2-1/(8r)}
that has fewer than
n^{r+1/2}
copies of
K_{r+1}
.

Proof.

Let
n
be a large integer and suppose that
r
satisfies
2\leqslant r\leqslant\log n/(121\log\log n)
. Let
\gamma=1/(8r)
and observe that

n^{\gamma}=\exp\left(\frac{\log n}{8r}\right)\geqslant\exp(12\log\log n)=(\log n)^{12}.

(30)

Let
\mathcal{H}
be the
\binom{r+1}{2}
-uniform hypergraph with vertex set
E(K_{n})
whose edges are the edge sets of all copies of
K_{r+1}
in
K_{n}
and set

s=\binom{r+1}{2},\qquad q=n^{-3\gamma},\qquad\text{and}\qquad E=n^{r+1/2}.

We now verify that we may apply Theorem
1.6
, with
\alpha\leftarrow 1/4
and
\beta\leftarrow n^{-\gamma}
, to the hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
. First, as
s\leqslant r^{2}\leqslant(\log n)^{2}
, we have

\alpha\beta q\cdot v(\mathcal{H})=\frac{n^{-4\gamma}}{4}\cdot\binom{n}{2}\geqslant n\geqslant 10^{9}s^{7}

and, using (
30
),

10^{4}s^{5}q=10^{4}s^{5}n^{-3\gamma}\leqslant 10^{4}(\log n)^{10}n^{-3\gamma}\leqslant n^{-\gamma}=\beta,

provided that
n
is sufficiently large. Second, suppose that
t\in\{2,\dotsc,s\}
and let
\ell\in\{3,\dotsc,r+1\}
be the unique integer satisfying
\binom{\ell-1}{2}<t\leqslant\binom{\ell}{2}
, so that

t-1\leqslant\binom{\ell}{2}-1=\frac{(\ell+1)(\ell-2)}{2}\leqslant r(\ell-2).

Since a graph with
t
edges must have at least
\ell
vertices, we have

\Delta_{t}(\mathcal{H})=\binom{n-\ell}{r+1-\ell}\leqslant n^{r+1-\ell}

and, consequently,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{q}{10^{6}s^{5}}\right)^{t-1}\cdot\frac{E}{v(\mathcal{H})}\cdot\frac{1}{\Delta_{t}(\mathcal{H})}

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(\frac{q}{10^{6}s^{5}}\right)^{r(\ell-2)}\cdot\frac{n^{r+1/2}}{n^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{r+1-\ell}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{n^{-3\gamma}}{10^{6}s^{5}}\right)^{r(\ell-2)}\cdot n^{\ell-5/2}\geqslant\left(\frac{n^{1/(2r)-3\gamma}}{10^{6}s^{5}}\right)^{r(\ell-2)}

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(\frac{n^{\gamma}}{10^{6}(\log n)^{10}}\right)^{r(\ell-2)}\geqslant 1,

where the last inequality follows from (
30
). Theorem
1.6
supplies a collection
\mathcal{G}
of containers for the independent sets of
\mathcal{H}
(that is,
K_{r+1}
-free subgraphs of
K_{n}
) satisfying

\begin{split}|\mathcal{G}|&\leqslant\exp\left(10^{4}s^{5}\beta^{-1}\log(e/\alpha)\cdot q\log(e/q)\cdot v(\mathcal{H})\right)\\
&\leqslant\exp\left(10^{5}(\log n)^{10}n^{\gamma}\cdot n^{-3\gamma}\log n\cdot n^{2}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(n^{2-\gamma}\right),\end{split}

where the last inequality follows from (
30
), such that, for every
G\in\mathcal{G}
, either
e(G)\leqslant\alpha\cdot v(\mathcal{H})\leqslant n^{2}/8
or there is a subgraph
G^{\prime}\subseteq G
with
e(G^{\prime})\geqslant(1-\beta)e(G)\geqslant e(G)-n^{2-\gamma}
and
e(\mathcal{H}[G^{\prime}])<E
(that is,
G^{\prime}
contains fewer than
E
copies of
K_{r+1}
).
∎

6.3
Almost all
K_{r+1}
-free graphs are almost
r
-partite

The following theorem, which is a rather straightforward consequence of our container lemma for
K_{r+1}
-free graphs (Proposition
6.1
) and the ‘supersaturated’ version of the stability theorem of Erdős and Simonovits (Lemma
6.3
below) proved by Balogh, Bushaw, Collares, Liu, Morris, and Sharifzadeh
[
5
]
, may be viewed as an approximate version of Theorem
1.2
. It states that, under the assumptions of Theorem
1.2
, almost all
K_{r+1}
-free subgraphs of
K_{n}
are almost
r
-partite. To make this notion precise, given nonnegative integers
r
and
t
with
r\geqslant 2
, we shall say that a graph
G
is
t
-close to being
r
-partite
if
G
can be made
r
-partite by removing from it at most
t
edges. In other words,
G
is
t
-close to being
r
-partite if
G
contains an
r
-partite subgraph
G^{\prime}
with
e(G^{\prime})\geqslant e(G)-t
. Conversely, we shall say that a graph
G
is
t
-far from being
r
-partite
if
G
is not
t
-close to being
r
-partite or, in other words, if
\chi(G^{\prime})>r
for every
G^{\prime}\subseteq G
with
e(G^{\prime})\geqslant e(G)-t
.

Theorem 6.2
.

The following holds for sufficiently large
n
and all
r
satisfying
2\leqslant r\leqslant\log n/(121\log\log n)
. Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the family of
K_{r+1}
-free subgraphs of
K_{n}
that are
(8\log n)^{-15}n^{2}
-far from being
r
-partite. Then

|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant 2^{\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r+1})-n}.

Lemma 6.3

(
[
5
]
)
.

Suppose that
n
,
r
, and
t
are positive integers. Every
n
-vertex graph
G
that is
t
-far from being
r
-partite contains at least

\frac{n^{r-1}}{e^{2r}\cdot r!}\left(e(G)+t-\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)\frac{n^{2}}{2}\right)

copies of
K_{r+1}
.

Proof of Theorem
6.2
.

Let
\delta=(8\log n)^{-15}
so that
\mathcal{F}
is the family of
K_{r+1}
-free subgraphs of
K_{n}
that are
\delta n^{2}
-far from being
r
-partite. Let
\mathcal{G}
be the family of containers for
K_{r+1}
-free graphs supplied by Proposition
6.1
. We partition
\mathcal{G}
into two parts as follows:

\mathcal{G}_{1}=\left\{G\in\mathcal{G}:e(G)\geqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)\frac{n^{2}}{2}-2n^{2-1/(8r)}\right\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{G}_{2}=\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{1}.

Fix an arbitrary
G\in\mathcal{G}_{1}
. Since
e(G)>n^{2}/8
, it must be the case that
G
contains a subgraph
G^{\prime}
with

e(G^{\prime})\geqslant e(G)-n^{2-1/(8r)}\geqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)\frac{n^{2}}{2}-3n^{2-1/(8r)}

(31)

that contains fewer than
n^{r+1/2}
copies of
K_{r+1}
. Let
G^{\prime}
be any such subgraph of
G
and let
t^{\prime}
be the smallest number of edges one can delete from
G^{\prime}
to make it
r
-partite. Lemma
6.3
implies that

\frac{n^{r-1}}{e^{2r}\cdot r!}\left(e(G^{\prime})+t^{\prime}-1-\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)\frac{n^{2}}{2}\right)<n^{r+1/2}

and hence, by (
31
),

t^{\prime}-3n^{2-1/(8r)}\leqslant e^{2r}\cdot r!\cdot n^{3/2}+1\leqslant r^{4r}\cdot n^{3/2}\leqslant n^{7/4}.

Since
G^{\prime}
is
t^{\prime}
-close to being
r
-partite,
G
is
t
-close from being
r
-partite for all
t\geqslant 5n^{2-1/(8r)}
, since

5n^{2-1/(8r)}>n^{7/4}+4n^{2-1/(8r)}\geqslant t^{\prime}+e(G)-e(G^{\prime}).

In particular, since

n^{-1/(8r)}=\exp\left(-\frac{\log n}{8r}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{121}{8}\cdot\log\log n\right)\ll\frac{1}{(\log n)^{15}},

neither
G
nor any of its subgraphs can be
\delta n^{2}
-far from being
r
-partite.

Thus, every graph in
\mathcal{F}
must be contained in some element of
\mathcal{G}_{2}
. Since

\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r+1})\geqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)\binom{n}{2}\geqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)\frac{n^{2}}{2}-n,

we have

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{2^{\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r+1})}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{2}}2^{e(G)-\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)\frac{n^{2}}{2}+n}\leqslant|\mathcal{G}|\cdot 2^{n-2n^{2-1/(8r)}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(n^{2-1/(8r)}+2\log 2\cdot\left(n-n^{2-1/(8r)}\right)\right)\leqslant 2^{-n},

as claimed.
∎

6.4
Balanced and unbalanced
r
-partitions

Let
\Pi
be an arbitrary
r
-partition of
\llbracket{n}\rrbracket
. We shall say that
\Pi
is
balanced
if
\min_{P\in\Pi}|P|\geqslant\frac{n}{2r}
and that it is
unbalanced
otherwise. In the sequel, we denote by
K_{\Pi}
the complete
r
-partite graph whose colour classes are the parts of
\Pi
.

Fact 6.4
.

Suppose that
r\geqslant 2
and let
\Pi
be an unbalanced
r
-partition of
\llbracket{n}\rrbracket
. Then

e(K_{\Pi})\leqslant\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r+1})-\frac{n^{2}}{16r^{2}}+n.

Proof.

Let
P\in\Pi
be an arbitrary part satisfying
|P|<\frac{n}{2r}
and let
Q\in\Pi
be an arbitrary part satisfying
|Q|\geqslant\frac{n}{r}
. Set

m=\left\lfloor\frac{|Q|-|P|}{2}\right\rfloor,

let
\Pi^{\prime}
be the partition obtained from
\Pi
by moving some
m
vertices from
Q
to
P
, and observe that

e(K_{\Pi^{\prime}})-e(K_{\Pi})=(|P|+m)(|Q|-m)-|P||Q|=(|Q|-|P|)\cdot m-m^{2}\geqslant m^{2}.

Since
|Q|-|P|>\frac{n}{2r}
, then
m\geqslant\left\lfloor\frac{n}{4r}\right\rfloor
and, consequently,
m^{2}\geqslant\frac{n^{2}}{16r^{2}}-n
. Finally, since
K_{\Pi^{\prime}}
is an
r
-partite graph, we have
e(K_{\Pi^{\prime}})\leqslant\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r+1})
and the claimed upper bound on
e(K_{\Pi})
follows.
∎

Our next lemma bounds from above the number of subgraphs of
K_{n}
that admit an unbalanced
r
-partition with few monochromatic edges.

Lemma 6.5
.

The following holds for all sufficiently large
n
and all
r
satisfying
2\leqslant r\leqslant\log n
. Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the family of all
G\subseteq K_{n}
that satisfy
e(G\setminus K_{\Pi})\leqslant\frac{n^{2}}{(r\log n)^{2}}
for some unbalanced
r
-partition
\Pi
. Then

|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant 2^{\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r+1})-n}.

Proof.

Denote by
\mathcal{P}_{u}
the family of all unbalanced
r
-partitions of
\llbracket{n}\rrbracket
. For every
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{u}
, let
\mathcal{F}_{\Pi}
denote the family of all graphs
G\subseteq K_{n}
that satisfy
e(G\setminus K_{\Pi})\leqslant\frac{n^{2}}{(r\log n)^{2}}
. We have

|\mathcal{F}_{\Pi}|\leqslant\sum_{t=0}^{n^{2}/(r\log n)^{2}}\binom{\binom{n}{2}}{t}\cdot 2^{e(K_{\Pi})}\leqslant\left(n^{2}\right)^{\frac{n^{2}}{(r\log n)^{2}}}\cdot 2^{e(K_{\Pi})}\leqslant 2^{e(K_{\Pi})+\frac{4n^{2}}{r^{2}\log n}}.

Since
|\mathcal{P}_{u}|\leqslant r^{n}\leqslant 2^{2n\log n}
, Fact
6.4
gives

|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{u}}|\mathcal{F}_{\Pi}|\leqslant 2^{\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r+1})-\frac{n^{2}}{16r^{2}}+\frac{4n^{2}}{r^{2}\log n}+n+2n\log n}\leqslant 2^{\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r+1})-n},

provided that
n
is sufficiently large.
∎

Let
\mathrm{Col}_{r}(n)
denote the family of all
r
-partite subgraphs of
K_{n}
. Even though some graphs in
\mathrm{Col}_{r}(n)
admit many different proper
r
-colourings, our next lemma, which is implicit in the work of Prömel and Steger
[
37
]
, shows that the average number of proper
r
-colourings of a graph in
\mathrm{Col}_{r}(n)
is only slightly larger than one. Our proof of the lemma is an adaptation of the argument underlying the proof of
[
8
, Proposition 5.5]
.

Lemma 6.6
.

The following holds for all sufficiently large
n
and all
r
satisfying
2\leqslant r\leqslant\log n
. Denoting by
\mathcal{P}
the family of all
r
-partitions of
\llbracket{n}\rrbracket
, we have

\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}}2^{e(K_{\Pi})}\leqslant\left(1+2^{-\frac{n}{5r^{4}}}\right)\cdot|\mathrm{Col}_{r}(n)|.

Proof.

Denote by
\mathcal{P}_{b}
the family of all balanced
r
-partitions of
\llbracket{n}\rrbracket
and let
\mathcal{P}_{u}=\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{P}_{b}
. Since
|\mathcal{P}_{u}|\leqslant r^{n}\leqslant 2^{2n\log n}
, Fact
6.4
gives

\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{u}}2^{e(K_{\Pi})}\leqslant 2^{\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r+1})-\frac{n^{2}}{16r^{2}}+n+2n\log n}\leqslant 2^{\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r+1})-n},

provided that
n
is sufficiently large. (This bound is also a consequence of Lemma
6.5
.) As
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r+1})
is the (largest) number of edges of an
n
-vertex
r
-colourable graph, we have

\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{u}}2^{e(K_{\Pi})}\leqslant 2^{-n}\cdot|\mathrm{Col}_{r}(n)|.

Since, for every pair
\Pi,\Pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
, there are exactly
2^{e(K_{\Pi}\cap K_{\Pi^{\prime}})}
subgraphs of
K_{n}
that are properly
r
-coloured by both
\Pi
and
\Pi^{\prime}
, Bonferroni’s inequality (the inclusion-exclusion principle) gives

|\mathrm{Col}_{r}(n)|\geqslant\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{b}}2^{e(K_{\Pi})}-\sum_{\{\Pi,\Pi^{\prime}\}\in\binom{\mathcal{P}_{b}}{2}}2^{e(K_{\Pi}\cap K_{\Pi^{\prime}})}.

The claimed inequality will thus follow once we establish the following claim.

Claim 6.7
.

For every
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{b}
,

\sum_{\Pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{b}\setminus\{\Pi\}}2^{e(K_{\Pi}\cap K_{\Pi^{\prime}})}\leqslant 2^{e(K_{\Pi})-\frac{n}{4r^{4}}}.

Fix distinct
\Pi,\Pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{b}
. Suppose that
\Pi=\{P_{1},\dotsc,P_{r}\}
and
\Pi^{\prime}=\{P_{1}^{\prime},\dotsc,P_{r}^{\prime}\}
and, for all
i,j\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket
, let
P_{i,j}=P_{i}\cap P_{j}^{\prime}
. We will say that the vertices in
P_{i,j}
are
moved
from
P_{i}
to
P_{j}^{\prime}
. For every
i\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket
, define
L_{i}
and
S_{i}
as the largest and the second largest subclasses of
P_{i}
, respectively (with ties broken arbitrarily). Note that
|P_{i}|\geqslant\frac{n}{2r}
implies that
|L_{i}|\geqslant\frac{n}{2r^{2}}
. Set
s=\max_{j\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket}|S_{j}|
and let
S=S_{j}
for the smallest
j
for which the maximum in the definition of
s
is achieved. Note that
1\leqslant s\leqslant n/2
, as
s=0
would imply that
(P_{1}^{\prime},\dotsc,P_{r}^{\prime})
is a permutation of
(P_{1},\dotsc,P_{r})
, and therefore
\Pi=\Pi^{\prime}
.

By the pigeonhole principle, either some pair
\{L_{i},L_{j}\}
of largest subclasses or some largest subclass
L_{i}
and
S
, where
S\nsubseteq P_{i}
, are moved to the same vertex class
P_{k}^{\prime}
. Since
P_{k}^{\prime}
is an independent set in
K_{\Pi^{\prime}}
, it follows that
K_{\Pi}\cap K_{\Pi^{\prime}}
has no edges between the sets
L_{i}
and
L_{j}
or
L_{i}
and
S
. Since,

\min\{|L_{i}|\cdot|L_{j}|,|L_{i}|\cdot|S|\}\geqslant\min\left\{\left(\frac{n}{2r^{2}}\right)^{2},\frac{n}{2r^{2}}\cdot s\right\}\geqslant\frac{sn}{2r^{4}},

we have
e(K_{\Pi}\cap K_{\Pi^{\prime}})\leqslant e(K_{\Pi})-\frac{sn}{2r^{4}}
.

Observe that, given a
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}
, we can describe any
\Pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}\setminus\{\Pi\}
by first picking the (ordered) partitions
(P_{i,j})_{j\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket}
for every
i
and then setting
P_{j}^{\prime}=\bigcup_{i\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket}P_{i,j}
. We claim that, for every
s
, the number of ways to choose all
P_{i,j}
in such a way that
\max_{i\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket}|S_{i}|=s
is at most
n^{r^{2}}\cdot n^{sr^{2}}
. Indeed, one may first specify the sequence
\big(|P_{i,j}|\big)_{i,j\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket}
and then specify, for each
i\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket
, the elements of each
P_{i,j}
with
j\in\llbracket{r}\rrbracket
, apart from
L_{i}
(which will comprise all the remaining, unspecified elements of
P_{i}
).

We may thus conclude that

\begin{split}\sum_{\Pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{b}\setminus\{\Pi\}}2^{e(K_{\Pi}\cap K_{\Pi^{\prime}})}&\leqslant\sum_{s\geqslant 1}n^{(s+1)r^{2}}\cdot 2^{e(K_{\Pi})-\frac{sn}{2r^{4}}}\\
&\leqslant 2^{e(K_{\Pi})}\cdot\sum_{s\geqslant 1}2^{4sr^{2}\log n-\frac{sn}{2r^{4}}}\leqslant 2^{e(K_{\Pi})-\frac{n}{4r^{4}}},\end{split}

as claimed.
∎

6.5
The number of
K_{r+1}
-free graphs with a monochromatic star

The following lemma will be used to bound from above the number of
K_{r+1}
-free graphs whose optimal
r
-partition induces a monochromatic copy of
K_{1,D}
in one of the parts.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
D
be an integer satisfying
D\geqslant 2^{r}r
. Suppose that
\Pi
is an
r
-partition of
\llbracket{n}\rrbracket
and that
S
is a copy of
K_{1,D}
with
V(K_{1,D})\subseteq P
for some
P\in\Pi
. If
v\in P
is the centre vertex of
S
, then

\left|\big\{G\subseteq K_{\Pi}:\text{$G\cup S\nsupseteq K_{r+1}$ and $\deg_{G}(v,Q)\geqslant D$ for all $Q\in\Pi\setminus\{P\}$}\big\}\right|\leqslant 2^{e(K_{\Pi})-\frac{D^{2}}{8r^{2}}}.

Proof.

Let
G
be a uniformly chosen random subgraph of
K_{\Pi}
. Expose
G
on all the edges of
K_{\Pi}
that have an endpoint in
P
and condition on
\deg_{G}(v,Q)\geqslant D
for all
Q\in\Pi\setminus\{P\}
. It suffices to show that, for every such conditioning,
\Pr(G\cup S\nsupseteq K_{r+1})\leqslant 2^{-\frac{D^{2}}{8r^{2}}}
. For each
Q\in\Pi\setminus\{P\}
, choose an arbitrary set of
D
neighbours of
v
in
Q
and let
\mathcal{K}
be the family of all
D^{r}
copies of
K_{r}
in
K_{\Pi}
whose vertices belong to the chosen
D
-element sets or to
V(K_{1,D})\setminus\{v\}\subseteq P
. Since, under our conditioning,
v
is adjacent (in
G\cup S
) to all the vertices of each
K\in\mathcal{K}
, we have
\Pr(G\cup S\nsupseteq K_{r+1})\leqslant\Pr(K\nsubseteq G\text{ for all }K\in\mathcal{K})
. We may bound the latter probability from above using Janson’s inequality. Define, as in the statement of Theorem
5.2
,

\mu=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{K}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{e(K)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Delta=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{K,K^{\prime}\}\in\binom{\mathcal{K}}{2}\\
E(K)\cap E(K^{\prime})\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{e(K\cup K^{\prime})}.

Observe that the function
\{2,\dotsc,r\}\ni\ell\mapsto D^{\ell}\cdot 2^{-\binom{\ell}{2}}
is increasing. Indeed, our assumption implies that, for each
\ell\leqslant r-1
, we have
D\cdot 2^{\binom{\ell}{2}-\binom{\ell+1}{2}}\geqslant D\cdot 2^{-r}\geqslant 1
. It follows that

\frac{\mu}{2}=\frac{D^{r}}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\binom{r}{2}}\geqslant\frac{D^{2}}{4}.

We now estimate
\Delta
. To this end, fix some
K\in\mathcal{K}
and observe that, for each
k\in\{2,\dotsc,r-1\}
, there are at most
\binom{r}{k}\cdot D^{r-k}
many
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}
that share exactly
k
vertices with
K
and that
e(K\cup K^{\prime})=2\binom{r}{2}-\binom{k}{2}
for each such
K^{\prime}
. We conclude that

2\Delta\leqslant\mu\cdot\sum_{k=2}^{r-1}\binom{r}{k}\cdot D^{r-k}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\binom{r}{2}-\binom{k}{2}}\leqslant\mu^{2}\cdot\sum_{k=2}^{r-1}\frac{2^{\binom{k}{2}}r^{k}}{D^{k}}.

Our assumption implies that, for every
k\in\{2,\dotsc,r-2\}
,

\frac{2^{\binom{k+1}{2}}r^{k+1}}{D^{k+1}}=\frac{2^{k}r}{D}\cdot\frac{2^{\binom{k}{2}}r^{k}}{D^{k}}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{2^{\binom{k}{2}}r^{k}}{D^{k}}

and hence,

\frac{2\Delta}{\mu^{2}}\leqslant 2\cdot\frac{2r^{2}}{D^{2}}.

We conclude that

\Pr(G\cup S\nsubseteq K_{r+1})\leqslant\exp\left(-\min\left\{\frac{\mu}{2},\frac{\mu^{2}}{4\Delta}\right\}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{D^{2}}{8r^{2}}\right),

as claimed.
∎

6.6
The number of
K_{r+1}
-free graphs with a monochromatic matching

The following lemma will be used to bound from above the number of
K_{r+1}
-free graphs whose optimal
r
-partition induces a monochromatic matching with a given number of edges in one of the parts.

Lemma 6.9
.

Suppose that
\Pi
is a balanced
r
-partition of
\llbracket{n}\rrbracket
and that
M
is a matching with
m
edges such that
V(M)\subseteq P
for some
P\in\Pi
. If
r^{2}\cdot 2^{r+3}\leqslant n
, then

\left|\big\{G\subseteq K_{\Pi}:G\cup M\nsupseteq K_{r+1}\big\}\right|\leqslant 2^{e(K_{\Pi})-\frac{mn}{2^{10}r^{4}}}.

Proof.

Let
G
be a uniformly chosen random subgraph of
K_{\Pi}
, so that the assertion of the lemma becomes equivalent to the inequality
\Pr(G\cup M\nsupseteq K_{r+1})\leqslant 2^{-\frac{mn}{2^{10}r^{4}}}
. Let
N=\prod_{Q\in\Pi\setminus\{P\}}|Q|
and note that the assumption that
\Pi
is balanced implies that

. Denote by

the graph obtained from

be the collection of all copies of

that form a

with an edge of

and that
\Pr(G\cup M\nsupseteq K_{r+1})=\Pr(K\nsubseteq G\text{ for all }K\in\mathcal{K})
. We may thus bound this probability from above using Janson’s inequality. Define, as in the statement of Theorem
5.2
,

\mu=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{K}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{e(K)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Delta=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{K,K^{\prime}\}\in\binom{\mathcal{K}}{2}\\
E(K)\cap E(K^{\prime})\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{e(K\cup K^{\prime})}.

Observe that the function
\{2,\dotsc,r\}\ni\ell\mapsto\left(\frac{n}{2r}\right)^{\ell-1}\cdot 2^{-\binom{\ell+1}{2}}
is increasing. Indeed, our assumption on
r
implies that, for each
\ell\leqslant r-1
, we have
\frac{n}{2r}\cdot 2^{\binom{\ell}{2}-\binom{\ell+1}{2}}\geqslant\frac{n}{2r}\cdot 2^{-r}\geqslant 1
. It follows that

\frac{\mu}{2}=\frac{mN}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\binom{r+1}{2}-1}\geqslant m\cdot\left(\frac{n}{2r}\right)^{r-1}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\binom{r+1}{2}}\geqslant\frac{mn}{16r}.

We now estimate
\Delta
. To this end, fix some
K\in\mathcal{K}
and observe that:

(a)

For each
k\in\{1,\dotsc,r-2\}
, there are at most
\binom{r-1}{k}\cdot N\cdot\left(\frac{n}{2r}\right)^{-k}
many
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}
that share with
K
the two vertices in
P
and exactly
k
other vertices; we have
e(K\cup K^{\prime})=2\binom{r+1}{2}-\binom{k+2}{2}-1
for each such
K^{\prime}
.

(b)

For each
k\in\{2,\dotsc,r-1\}
, there are at most
m\cdot\binom{r-1}{k}\cdot N\cdot\left(\frac{n}{2r}\right)^{-k}
many
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}
that share with
K
only some
k
vertices outside of
P
; we have
e(K\cup K^{\prime})=2\binom{r+1}{2}-\binom{k}{2}-2
for each such
K^{\prime}
.

We conclude that

\begin{split}\frac{2\Delta}{\mu^{2}}&\leqslant\frac{1}{m}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{r-2}\binom{r-1}{k}\cdot\left(\frac{n}{2r}\right)^{-k}\cdot 2^{\binom{k+2}{2}-1}+\sum_{k=2}^{r-1}\binom{r-1}{k}\cdot\left(\frac{n}{2r}\right)^{-k}\cdot 2^{\binom{k}{2}}\\
&\leqslant\frac{1}{m}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{r-2}\frac{(2r^{2})^{k}\cdot 2^{\binom{k+2}{2}-1}}{n^{k}}+\sum_{k=2}^{r-1}\frac{(2r^{2})^{k}\cdot 2^{\binom{k}{2}}}{n^{k}}\\
&\leqslant\frac{8r^{2}}{mn}+\sum_{k=2}^{r}\frac{(2r^{2})^{k}\cdot 2^{\binom{k+2}{2}}}{n^{k}}.\end{split}

Our assumption on
r
implies that, for every
k\in\{2,\dotsc,r-1\}
,

\frac{(2r^{2})^{k+1}2^{\binom{k+3}{2}}}{n^{k+1}}=\frac{r^{2}2^{k+3}}{n}\cdot\frac{(2r^{2})^{k}2^{\binom{k+2}{2}}}{n^{k}}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{(2r^{2})^{k}2^{\binom{k+2}{2}}}{n^{k}}

and hence, as
m\leqslant n/2
and
r\geqslant 2
,

\frac{2\Delta}{\mu^{2}}\leqslant\frac{8r^{2}}{mn}+2\cdot\frac{4r^{4}\cdot 2^{6}}{n^{2}}\leqslant\frac{258r^{4}}{mn}.

We conclude that

\Pr(G\cup M\nsubseteq K_{r+1})\leqslant\exp\left(-\min\left\{\frac{\mu}{2},\frac{\mu^{2}}{4\Delta}\right\}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{mn}{2^{10}r^{4}}\right),

as claimed.
∎

7
Lower bounds for
\varepsilon
-nets

7.1
Outline

Our (randomised) construction of planar point sets
X
without a small
\varepsilon
-net for the range space of lines on
X
is a slight simplification of the construction of Balogh and Solymosi
[
9
]
. The high-level idea of both constructions, which can be traced back to the work of Alon
[
1
]
, may be summarised as follows. We find an integer
s
, a finite set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and a sub-collection
\mathcal{L}
of all lines in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the following property: Let
\mathcal{H}
be the
s
-uniform hypergraph with vertex set
X
whose edges are all intersections of the lines in
\mathcal{L}
with
X
that have exactly
s
points. The independence number of
\mathcal{H}
is at most
(1-c)|X|
, for some constant
c>0
.

Given such
s
,
X
, and
\mathcal{L}
, we set
\varepsilon=s/|X|
and observe that the complement of every
\varepsilon
-net
N
for the range space of lines on
X
is an independent set of
\mathcal{H}
. Indeed, every such
N
intersects every line that contains at least
s
points of
X
; in particular,
N
must intersect every line in
\mathcal{L}
that contains exactly
s
points of
X
. This means that
|N|\geqslant c|X|=cs/\varepsilon
, which improves upon the trivial bound
|N|\geqslant\Omega(1/\varepsilon)
if
s
can be made arbitrarily large. The challenge is to make
s
as large as possible, as a function of
|X|
.

In the construction of Alon
[
1
]
, the set
X
is a generic projection of the
d
-dimensional grid
\llbracket{s}\rrbracket^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\mathcal{L}
is the image of all combinatorial lines in
\llbracket{s}\rrbracket^{d}
via this projection; the key property is guaranteed, for large enough
d
, by the density version of the Hales–Jewett theorem proved by Furstenberg and Katznelson
[
24
]
. In Balogh and Solymosi’s
[
9
]
construction,
X
was a generic projection of a random subset of a larger, high-dimensional integer grid, trimmed appropriately (so that each line in
\mathcal{L}
contains no more than
s
points of
X
), and the key property of
\mathcal{H}
was established, for a careful choice of
\mathcal{L}
, with the use of the hypergraph container theorem of Saxton and Thomason
[
42
]
. Here, we take
X
to be a random subset of
\llbracket{n}\rrbracket^{2}
, trimmed appropriately (as in
[
9
]
), and establish the key property of
\mathcal{H}
, for a careful choice of
\mathcal{L}
, using our efficient container lemma, Theorem
1.6
.

We shall find an

\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{h}}|\ell\cap Q|\geqslant|Q|-\frac{n^{2}}{10}\geqslant\frac{n^{2}}{5}

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{L}|}\cdot\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}|\ell\cap Q|=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{L}|}\cdot\sum_{h=1}^{h_{\max}}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{h}}|\ell\cap Q|\geqslant\frac{h_{\max}}{|\mathcal{L}|}\cdot\frac{n^{2}}{5}\geqslant\frac{m}{5}.

Consequently,

e\big(\mathcal{H}[Q]\big)\geqslant|\mathcal{L}|\cdot b_{s}\big(m/5\big)=|\mathcal{L}|\cdot\binom{m/5}{s}\geqslant|\mathcal{L}|,

as claimed.
∎

Proof of Lemma
7.2
.

Set

q=\frac{p}{300m^{5}\log n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad E=|\mathcal{L}|.

We now verify that we may apply Theorem
1.6
, with
\alpha\leftarrow 1/2
and
\beta\leftarrow 1/3
, to the hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
. First, we have

\alpha\beta q\cdot v(\mathcal{H})=\frac{pn^{2}}{1800m^{5}\log n}\geqslant 10^{9}s^{7}\qquad\text{and}\qquad 10^{4}s^{5}q=\frac{10^{4}s^{5}p}{300m^{5}\log n}\leqslant\frac{1}{\log n}\leqslant\beta,

provided that
n
is sufficiently large. Second, for every
t\in\{2,\dotsc,s\}
, by (
35
) and (
36
),

\left(\frac{q}{10^{6}s^{5}}\right)^{t-1}\cdot\frac{E}{v(\mathcal{H})}=\left(\frac{p}{3\cdot 10^{8}s^{5}m^{5}\log n}\right)^{t-1}\cdot\frac{|\mathcal{L}|}{n^{2}}\geqslant\left(\frac{K\cdot m^{5}\cdot n^{-1/(s-1)}}{3\cdot 10^{8}s^{5}}\right)^{s-1}\cdot\frac{n}{12m^{2}}\\
=\left(\frac{K}{3\cdot 10^{3}}\right)^{s-1}\cdot\frac{1}{12m^{2}}\geqslant 2^{m}\geqslant\binom{m-t}{s-t}=\Delta_{t}(\mathcal{H}),

provided that
K
is sufficiently large. The theorem supplies a collection
\mathcal{C}
of containers for the independent sets of
\mathcal{H}
such that

\begin{split}|\mathcal{C}|&\leqslant\exp\left(10^{4}s^{5}\beta^{-1}\log(e/\alpha)\cdot q\log(e/q)\cdot v(\mathcal{H})\right)\\
&\leqslant\exp\left(m^{5}\cdot q\log n\cdot n^{2}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(pn^{2}/300\right),\end{split}

where we used the inequality
q\geqslant e/n
, which holds if
K
is sufficiently large, and, for every
C\in\mathcal{C}
, either
|C|\leqslant\alpha\cdot v(\mathcal{H})=n^{2}/2
or there is a subset
W\subseteq C
with
|W|\geqslant(1-\beta)|C|=2|C|/3
such that

. We claim that, in fact,
|C|\leqslant n^{2}/2
for every
C\in\mathcal{C}
. Indeed, if
|C|>n^{2}/2
and
W\subseteq C
satisfies
|W|\geqslant 2|C|/3>n^{2}/3
, then
e(\mathcal{H}[W])\geqslant|\mathcal{L}|
, by Lemma
7.3
.
∎

8
Upper bounds on Ramsey numbers

In this section, we derive the upper bounds on Folkman numbers and on induced Ramsey numbers stated in Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
. We shall do this by building containers for non-Ramsey colourings of subgraphs of a large complete graph
K_{N}
and examining how a random subgraph of
K_{N}
, drawn with an appropriately chosen distribution for each of the two theorems, intersects these containers. This approach to studying Ramsey properties of random graphs was introduced in the work of Nenadov and Steger
[
34
]
. The only Ramsey-theoretic ingredient in our proof is the following supersaturated version of Ramsey’s theorem, which is a refinement of
[
34
, Corollary 2.2]
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Suppose that
n
and
k
are positive integers and let
R=R(n;k)
. If
N\geqslant R
, then every colouring
c\colon E(K_{N})\to\llbracket{k+1}\rrbracket
either assigns the colour
k+1
to at least
(1/2)\cdot(N/R)^{2}
edges or it contains at least
(1/2)\cdot(N/R)^{n}
monochromatic copies of
K_{n}
in colours
1,\dotsc,k
.

Proof.

The choice of
R
guarantees that the edge-colouring induced by every subset of
R
vertices of
K_{N}
contains either an edge coloured
k+1
or a monochromatic copy of
K_{n}
in one of the remaining
k
colours. On the other hand, each edge and each copy of
K_{n}
are contained in, respectively,
\binom{N-2}{R-2}
and
\binom{N-n}{R-n}
such subsets. Denoting by
M
the total number of monochromatic copies of
K_{n}
in colours
1,\dotsc,k
, we thus have

\binom{N}{R}\leqslant\left|c^{-1}(k+1)\right|\cdot\binom{N-2}{R-2}+M\cdot\binom{N-n}{R-n}.

(37)

In particular, since, for every
\ell\in\{2,n\}
,

\binom{N}{R}\cdot\binom{N-\ell}{R-\ell}^{-1}=\binom{N}{\ell}\cdot\binom{R}{\ell}^{-1}\geqslant\left(\frac{N}{R}\right)^{\ell}

inequality (
37
) implies that either
|c^{-1}(k+1)|\geqslant(1/2)\cdot(N/R)^{2}
or
M\geqslant(1/2)\cdot(N/R)^{n}
.
∎

8.1
Folkman numbers (proof of Theorem
1.4
)

Let
k
and
n
be positive integers, let
R=R(n;k)
and suppose that an integer
N
satisfies

N\geqslant(\Gamma knR)^{21n^{2}}

(38)

for some large constant
\Gamma
. We shall give a randomised construction of a
K_{n+1}
-free subgraph of
K_{N}
that satisfies
G\rightarrow(K_{n})_{k}
, proving that
F(n;k)\leqslant N
. We shall from now on assume that
k\geqslant 2
and
n\geqslant 3
, as otherwise the assertion of the theorem is trivial.

Suppose that
G\subseteq K_{N}
. We shall identify a
k
-colouring
c\colon E(G)\to\llbracket{k}\rrbracket
of the edges of
G
with the set

\big\{(e,c_{e}):e\in E(G)\big\}\subseteq E(K_{N})\times\llbracket{k}\rrbracket.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be the hypergraph with vertex set
E(K_{N})\times\llbracket{k}\rrbracket
whose edges are all sets of the form

\varphi\big(E(K_{n})\big)\times\{i\},

where
\varphi\colon V(K_{n})\to V(K_{N})
is an arbitrary injection and
i\in\llbracket{k}\rrbracket
. If a graph
G\subseteq K_{N}
admits a colouring
c\colon E(G)\to\llbracket{k}\rrbracket
with no monochromatic copy of
K_{n}
, then
c
, when viewed as a subset of
E(K_{N})\times\llbracket{k}\rrbracket
, is an independent set of
\mathcal{H}
.

We shall say that a graph
G\subseteq E(K_{N})
is
compatible
with a set
C\subseteq E(K_{N})\times\llbracket{k}\rrbracket
if there exists a colouring
c\colon E(G)\to\llbracket{k}\rrbracket
that is contained in
C
. Equivalently,
G
is compatible with
C
if and only if
\big(\{e\}\times\llbracket{k}\rrbracket\big)\cap C\neq\emptyset
for every
e\in E(G)
. In other words, defining

X(C)=\big\{e\in E(K_{N}):\big(e\times\llbracket{k}\rrbracket\big)\cap C=\emptyset\big\},

G
is compatible with
C
if and only if
X(C)\cap E(G)=\emptyset
.

Suppose that
p
satisfies

D\cdot(knR)^{20}\cdot N^{-2/(n+1)}\log N\leqslant p\leqslant\frac{N^{-2/(n+2)}}{DR}

(39)

for some large constant
D
; such a number does indeed exist as (
38
) implies that

\frac{N^{2/(n+1)-2/(n+2)}}{\log N}\geqslant N^{1/n^{2}}\geqslant D^{2}\cdot(knR)^{21},

provided that
\Gamma
is sufficiently large. The following lemma is key.

Lemma 8.2
.

There is a family
\mathcal{C}
of at most
\exp\left(\frac{pN^{2}}{256R^{2}}\right)
containers for the independent sets of
\mathcal{H}
such that
|X(C)|\geqslant\left(\frac{N}{4R}\right)^{2}
for every
C\in\mathcal{C}
.

We first show how Lemma
8.2
implies the assertion of the theorem. To this end, suppose that
G\sim G_{N,p}
and denote by
Z
the number of copies of
K_{n+1}
in
G
. The upper bound in (
39
) implies that

\mathbb{E}[Z]\leqslant p^{\binom{n+1}{2}}N^{n+1}=pN^{2}\cdot\left(p^{(n+2)/2}N\right)^{n-1}\leqslant\frac{pN^{2}}{(DR)^{(n+2)(n-1)/2}}\leqslant\frac{pN^{2}}{64R^{2}},

provided that
D
is sufficiently large. Let
G^{\prime}
be the subgraph obtained from
G
by deleting an arbitrary edge from every copy of
K_{n+1}
; observe that
K_{n+1}\nsubseteq G^{\prime}
and
e(G^{\prime})\geqslant e(G)-Z
.

Suppose that
G^{\prime}\not\rightarrow(K_{n})_{k}
. This means that there is a colouring
c\colon E(G^{\prime})\to\llbracket{k}\rrbracket
that is an independent set of
\mathcal{H}
. Therefore,
G^{\prime}
must be compatible with some container from
\mathcal{C}
. In other words, there is some
C\in\mathcal{C}
such that
X(C)\cap E(G^{\prime})=\emptyset
and, consequently,

|X(C)\cap E(G)|\leqslant e(G)-e(G^{\prime})\leqslant Z.

We may conclude that

\Pr\big(G^{\prime}\not\rightarrow(K_{n})_{k}\big)\leqslant\Pr\big(Z>2\mathbb{E}[Z]\big)+\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\Pr\big(|X(C)\cap E(G)|\leqslant 2\mathbb{E}[Z]\big).

(40)

Fix an arbitrary
C\in\mathcal{C}
. Since
|X(C)|\geqslant\frac{N^{2}}{16R^{2}}
, standard estimates on the lower tails of binomial distributions (Lemma
5.1
) yield

\Pr\big(|X(C)\cap E(G)|\leqslant 2\mathbb{E}[Z]\big)\leqslant\Pr\left(\mathrm{Bin}\left(\frac{N^{2}}{16R^{2}},p\right)\leqslant\frac{pN^{2}}{32R^{2}}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{pN^{2}}{128R^{2}}\right).

Substituting this estimate and the inequality
\Pr(Z>2\mathbb{E}[Z])<1/2
into (
40
) yields

\Pr\big(G^{\prime}\not\rightarrow(K_{n})_{k}\big)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}+\exp\left(-\frac{pN^{2}}{256R^{2}}\right)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}+e^{-N}\leqslant\frac{3}{4}.

In particular, there is a graph
G^{\prime}\subseteq K_{N}
such that
G^{\prime}\nsupseteq K_{n+1}
and
G^{\prime}\rightarrow(K_{n})_{k}
, as claimed.

Proof of Lemma
8.2
.

Set

We now verify that we may apply Theorem
1.6
, with
\alpha\leftarrow\frac{1}{2k}
, to the hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
. First, as

\alpha\beta q\cdot v(\mathcal{H})\geqslant\frac{D\cdot(knR)^{10}\cdot N^{-2/(n+1)}}{10^{10}}\cdot\binom{N}{2}\geqslant N\geqslant(\Gamma n)^{21}\geqslant 10^{9}s^{7}

and

10^{4}s^{5}q\leqslant 10^{4}s^{5}\cdot N^{-2/(n+2)}\leqslant\frac{10^{4}n^{10}}{(knR)^{21n}}\leqslant\frac{1}{16kR^{2}}=\beta,

provided that
n
is sufficiently large. Second, suppose that

and let

Since a graph with

vertices, we have

\Delta_{t}(\mathcal{H})=k\cdot\binom{N-\ell}{n-\ell}\leqslant kN^{n-\ell}

and, consequently,

where the last inequality follows from (
39
). The theorem supplies a collection
\mathcal{C}
of containers for the independent sets of

such that, for every
C\in\mathcal{C}
, either
|C|\leqslant\alpha\cdot v(\mathcal{H})
or there is a subset
W\subseteq C
with
|W|\geqslant(1-\beta)|C|\geqslant|C|-\beta k\binom{N}{2}
and
e(\mathcal{H}[W])<E
. We now turn to bounding the right-hand side of (

) from above. To this end, observe first that
v(\mathcal{H})=k\binom{N}{2}\leqslant kN^{2}
and that

\frac{e}{q}=\frac{e(10knR)^{10}\log N}{p}\leqslant N^{2/(n+1)}\leqslant N

. Substituting these three estimates into (

|\mathcal{C}|\leqslant\exp\left(10^{4}\cdot n^{10}R^{2}k\log(2ek)\cdot\frac{p}{(10knR)^{10}}\cdot kN^{2}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(\frac{pN^{2}}{256R^{2}}\right),

It remains to show that
|X(C)|\geqslant\left(\frac{N}{4R}\right)^{2}
for every
C\in\mathcal{C}
. Suppose that this were not true. If
|C|\leqslant\alpha v(\mathcal{H})=\frac{k}{2k}\binom{N}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\binom{N}{2}

|X(C)|\geqslant\binom{N}{2}-|C|\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\binom{N}{2}\geqslant\left(\frac{N}{4R}\right)^{2},

so we may assume that

W\subseteq C
with
|C\setminus W|\leqslant\beta k\binom{N}{2}
such that
e(\mathcal{H}[W])<E
. Observe that

|X(W)|\leqslant|X(C)|+|C\setminus W|\leqslant\left(\frac{N}{4R}\right)^{2}+\beta k\binom{N}{2}\leqslant\frac{N^{2}}{8R^{2}}

(42)

and let

and

otherwise. By Lemma

or the colouring

has at least
(1/2)\cdot(N/R)^{n}
monochromatic copies of
K_{n}
in colours

. However, the former inequality contradicts (

) and thus the latter must hold. Finally, note that, if
K
is an arbitrary copy of
K_{n}
in

that

colours with some

, then

. This implies that
e(\mathcal{H}[W])\geqslant(1/2)\cdot(N/R)^{n}\geqslant E
, contradicting our assumption.
∎

K_{n}
in colours
1,\dotsc,k
. However, the former inequality contradicts (
44
) and thus the latter must hold. Let
K
be an arbitrary copy of
K_{n}
in
K_{N}
that
c
colours with some
i\in\llbracket{k}\rrbracket
. Since
E(K)\cap X_{1}(W)=\emptyset
, then
E(K)\times\{0,i\}\subseteq W
and, as a result, any injection
\varphi\colon V(H)\to V(K)
corresponds to an edge of
\mathcal{H}[W]
. This implies that
e(\mathcal{H}[W])\geqslant(1/2)\cdot(N/R)^{n}\geqslant E
, contradicting our assumption.
