Abstract
. We introduce 3 3 -dimensional generalizations of Postnikov’s plabic graphs and use them to establish cluster structures for type A A braid varieties. Our results include known cluster structures on open positroid varieties and double Bruhat cells, and establish new cluster structures for type A A open Richardson varieties.

1.
Introduction

Braid varieties

\accentset{\circ}{R}_{{u,\beta}}
are smooth, affine, complex algebraic varieties associated to a permutation
u
and a braid word
\beta
, that is, a word representing an element of the positive braid monoid. The purpose of this work is to construct a cluster algebra structure
[
FZ02
]
on the coordinate ring of a braid variety.

Theorem 1.1
.

The coordinate ring
{\mathbb{C}}[\accentset{\circ}{R}_{u,\beta}]
of a braid variety is a cluster algebra.

The braid varieties we consider were studied, rather recently, in
[
Mel19
,
CGGS20
]
, generalizing varieties considered previously in
[
Deo85
,
MR04
,
WY07
]
.
Theorem

1.1
resolves conjectures of
[
Lec16
,
CGGS21
]
.

Certain special cases of braid varieties, namely open Richardson varieties and open positroid varieties, have played a key role in the combinatorial geometry of flag varieties and Grassmannians.
Open Richardson varieties

\accentset{\circ}{R}_{u,w}
are subvarieties of the variety
\operatorname{SL}_{n}/B_{+}
of complete flags in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, arising in the study of total positivity and Poisson geometry
[
Deo85
,
Lus98
,
MR04
,
Pos06
,
BGY06
,
KLS13
]
. An explicit cluster structure for open Richardson varieties was conjectured by Leclerc
[
Lec16
]
. Ménard
[
Mé22
]
gave an alternative conjectural cluster structure, which was proved by Cao and Keller
[
CK22
]
to be an upper cluster structure; Ménard’s and Leclerc’s cluster structures are expected to coincide. Another upper cluster structure was constructed by Ingermanson
[
Ing19
]
. The cluster structure of
Theorem

1.1
, in the case of open Richardson varieties, agrees with that of Ingermanson. It is related to the cluster structure of Leclerc
[
Lec16
]
by the twist automorphism
[
GL22b
,
SSB22
]
.

We also prove that the cluster varieties in
Theorem

1.1
are locally acyclic
[
Mul13
]
; in the case of open Richardson varieties we use this to establish a variant of a conjecture of Lam and Speyer
[
LS16
]
. In particular, the cohomology of braid varieties satisfies the curious Lefschetz phenomenon (
Theorem

10.1
).

Theorem

1.1
generalizes the (type A) results of
[
FZ99
,
GY20
]
on double Bruhat cells and
[
SW21
]
on double Bott–Samelson cells, and also the results of Ingermanson
[
Ing19
]
and Cao and Keller
[
CK22
]
, who found upper cluster structures on open Richardson varieties (see also
[
Mé22
]
). Furthermore,
Theorem

1.1
generalizes the main result of
[
GL19
]
(see also
[
Sco06
,
MS16
,
SSBW19
]
), where the same statement was proved for
open positroid varieties

[
KLS13
]
, which are special cases of open Richardson varieties. Positroid varieties are parametrized by
plabic graphs

[
Pos06
]
, whose planar dual quivers describe the cluster algebra structure on the associated open positroid varieties.

Ever since the completion of
[
GL19
]
, it has been our hope that constructing a cluster structure for open Richardson varieties would lead to a meaningful generalization of Postnikov’s plabic graphs; indeed, discovering such a generalization turned out to be a crucial step in our proof of
Theorem

1.1
. We associate a
3D plabic graph
to each pair
(u,\beta)
consisting of a permutation
u
and a (double) braid word
\beta
, and use the combinatorics of this graph to construct our cluster structure. The reader is invited to look forward at the examples in
Figs.

1
–
6
. Code for computing 3D plabic graphs and the associated seeds is available at
[
Gal23
]
.

An important geometric ingredient in our approach is the study of the Deodhar geometry of braid varieties, originally used by Deodhar
[
Deo85
]
in the flag variety setting. We define an open Deodhar torus
T_{u,\beta}\subset\accentset{\circ}{R}_{u,\beta}
, and our cluster variables are interpreted as characters of
T_{u,\beta}
that have certain orders of vanishing along the Deodhar hypersurfaces in the complement of the Deodhar torus. We expect this geometric approach to have applications to other settings where cluster structures are expected to make an appearance.

Our work has a number of applications. Our cluster structure implies, via
[
LS16
]
, a curious Lefschetz phenomenon for the cohomology of braid varieties. Our approach is closely related to the combinatorics of
braid Richardson links
that are associated to a braid variety; in particular, we relate certain quiver point counts to the HOMFLY polynomial of these links.

We learned at the final stages of completing this manuscript that a cluster structure for braid varieties (of arbitrary Lie type) was independently announced in a recent preprint
[
CGG+22
]
. It would be interesting to investigate the relation between our 3D plabic graphs and the approach of
[
CGG+22
]
.

Our construction in other Lie types

The first three authors have continued this line of research to encompass braid varieties of arbitrary Lie type
[
GLSB23
]
, again using the Deodhar decomposition as the main geometric tool.
The cluster structure that we construct in type
A
is much more explicit and combinatorial than the ones that we construct in other Lie types.
In particular, our type
A
structure has clear connections to
the plabic graphs of
[
Pos06
]
. In all Lie types, there is a family of “chamber minors” (generalizing those appearing in
[
FZ99
,
MR04
]
) which are monomials in the cluster variables. In type
A
, the matrix transforming cluster variables to chamber minors is a
\{0,1\}
matrix; see
Section

7.3
and
Proposition

7.7
.
However, in other Lie types, this matrix can have entries larger than
1
, and the combinatorics of these matrix entries is complicated; see
Remark

7.8
and
[
GLSB23
, Section 7]
.

On the other hand, there are arguments which are simpler and clearer when presented in all Lie types.
We have chosen to place those arguments in
[
GLSB23
]
, and removed longer computations which establish them only in type
A
from this paper.

Let

2.3.
The quiver

A

Q
is a directed graph without directed cycles of length
1

2

The procedure in

decorated with a family

of relative cycles. To this data, we associate an ice quiver
\widetilde{Q}_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}

[

GK13

2.4.
The seed

To each
c\in J_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}
we associate a
cluster variable

x_{c}\in{\mathbb{C}}[\accentset{\circ}{R}_{u,w}]
. Let
\mathbf{Ch}_{c}
be the
chamber
(i.e., a connected component of the complement of
G_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}
in the plane) located immediately to the left of the bridge
b_{c}
. To this data, one can associate a regular function on
\accentset{\circ}{R}_{u,w}
called a
chamber minor

\Delta_{c}\in{\mathbb{C}}[\accentset{\circ}{R}_{u,w}]
. Variants of these functions appear in
[
MR04
,
Lec16
,
Ing19
]
.

We now define these chamber minors.
For
v\in S_{n}
and
0\leq i\leq n
, we write
v[i]:=\{v(1),\dots,v(i)\}
. For a matrix
g\in{\rm GL}_{n}
, we write
\Delta_{A,B}(g)
for the matrix minor with row set
A
and column set
B
.
Given an element
gB_{+}\in\accentset{\circ}{R}_{u,w}
, one can find a matrix
z\in B_{+}
such that
gB_{+}=zwB_{+}
and
\Delta_{u[i],w[i]}(z)=1
for all
i=1,2,\dots,n
. The matrix
z
is not unique, but is unique up to right multiplication by elements of
U_{+}\cap wU_{+}w^{-1}
(where
U_{+}\subset B_{+}
is the unipotent subgroup; see
Section

6.1
), and all the minors of
z
which we consider are invariant under this multiplication. The wiring diagram of
w
is obtained from
G_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}
by replacing every bridge with a crossing, and the wiring diagram of
u
is obtained by erasing all bridges from
G_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}
. Given any chamber
\mathbf{Ch}
, let
A
be the set of left endpoints of
u
-strands (i.e., the strands in the wiring diagram of
u
) passing below
\mathbf{Ch}
, and let
B
be the set of left endpoints of
w
-strands passing below
\mathbf{Ch}
. The chamber minor for
\mathbf{Ch}
is then given by
\Delta_{A,B}(z)
.

For
d\in J_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}
, we say that
\mathbf{Ch}_{c}
is
inside
C_{d}
if it is contained inside the projection of the disk
D_{d}
to the plane.
Then the cluster variables
(x_{c})_{c\in J_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}}
are uniquely defined by the invertible monomial transformation

(2.1)

\Delta_{c}=\prod_{d\in J_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}:\ \text{$\mathbf{Ch}_{c}$ is inside $C_{d}$}}x_{d}.

See
Fig.

5

w=s_{3}s_{2}s_{1}s_{4}s_{3}s_{2}s_{3}s_{4}
and

, and see
Section

for further details.

The cluster
\{x_{c}\}_{c\in J_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}}
together with the quiver
\widetilde{Q}_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}
is a seed
\Sigma_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}
in
{\mathbb{C}}(\accentset{\circ}{R}_{u,w})
. To prove
Theorem

2.1
, we show that
{\mathbb{C}}[\accentset{\circ}{R}_{u,w}]={\mathcal{A}}(\Sigma_{u,{\mathbf{w}}})
.

2.5.
Deodhar geometry

In
[
Deo85
]
, Deodhar constructed a stratification of
\accentset{\circ}{R}_{u,w}
for each reduced word
{\mathbf{w}}
of
w
. The strata are of the form
{\mathbb{C}}^{a}\times({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{b}
. The dense open stratum in Deodhar’s stratification is called the
Deodhar torus
, and denoted
T_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}\subset\accentset{\circ}{R}_{u,w}
. The Deodhar torus is the initial cluster torus in our cluster structure. We show in
Proposition

7.3
that the chamber minors
\{\Delta_{c}\}_{c\in J_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}}
are a basis of characters for
T_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}
.

We show that the cluster variables
\{x_{d}\}_{d\in J_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}}
are certain distinguished characters on
T_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}
.
Namely, the complement
\accentset{\circ}{R}_{u,w}\setminus T_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}
is a union of irreducible components called
(mutable) Deodhar hypersurfaces

V_{d}
. We deduce from (
2.1
) that the mutable cluster variable
x_{d}
is the unique character of
T_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}
which vanishes to order one along
V_{d}
and has no zeroes along other Deodhar hypersurfaces. Frozen Deodhar hypersurfaces and frozen cluster variables are constructed using a slightly different geometric approach.

2.6.
Comparison to known cluster structures

Our construction simultaneously includes several known cluster structures.

When
w
is a
k
-Grassmannian permutation, the open Richardson variety
\accentset{\circ}{R}_{u,w}
is an open positroid variety in the sense of
[
Pos06
,
KLS13
]
.
In this case, Karpman
[
Kar18
]
showed that the graph
G_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}
is one of Postnikov’s reduced plabic graphs
[
Pos06
]
with
n
extra leaves attached.
In particular, in this case
G_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}
is planar and each cycle
C_{c}
bounds a single face of
G_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}
, which is not true for general
w\in S_{n}
.
In this case, the first two authors
[
GL19
]
showed that the open positroid variety is a cluster variety, with quiver coming from the plabic graph; this gives the quiver
\widetilde{Q}_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}
.

Many of our ideas appeared in the unpublished Ph.D. dissertation of Ingermanson
[
Ing19
]
, which constructs an upper cluster structure on
\accentset{\circ}{R}_{u,w}
. The fourth author was Ingermanson’s advisor and is grateful for the ideas he learned from her. We summarize the constructions in our paper that appear in
[
Ing19
]
. Ingermanson writes down the same monomial transformation as (
2.1
), but defines it via a much more involved recursion.
Ingermanson constructs a bridge diagram (in the case that
{\mathbf{w}}
is a particular reduced word called the “unipeak word”) which is isomorphic to our graph
G_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}
but is defined as an abstract graph rather than embedded in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. Ingermanson’s quiver is identical to our
\widetilde{Q}_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}
, but it is described in a very different way, along the lines of
Section

3.7
.

2.7.
Braid Richardson varieties

Open Richardson varieties generalize to
braid Richardson varieties
. We say that two flags
B_{1},B_{2}\in G/B_{+}
are in
relative position
w\in S_{n}
if there exists a matrix
g\in G
such that
(gB_{1},gB_{2})=(B_{+},wB_{+})
. In this case, we write
B_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\longrightarrow}}B_{2}
. Consider a (not necessarily reduced) word
\beta=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{m})\in[n-1]^{m}
and let
u\in S_{n}
. Following the nomenclature of
[
GLTW22
]
, define the
braid Richardson variety

(2.2)

\accentset{\circ}{R}_{u,\beta}=\left\{(B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{m})\in(G/B_{+})^{m}\mid B_{+}\xrightarrow{s_{i_{1}}}B_{1}\xrightarrow{s_{i_{2}}}\cdots\xrightarrow{s_{i_{m}}}B_{m}\xleftarrow{w_{\circ}u}B_{-}\right\}.

This variety is nonempty whenever
u\leq\beta
in the sense of
Definition

3.1
. In the case
u=w_{\circ}
, the definition (
2.2
) is the same as the definition of the braid variety
X(\beta)
considered in
[
CGG+22
]
, and is isomorphic to the braid variety
X(\beta,w_{\circ})
in
[
CGGS20
]
. We shall show that braid Richardson varieties are isomorphic to the braid varieties we study in
Section

6
.

When
\beta
is a reduced word for some
w\in S_{n}
then using a variant of Lemma
6.2
(2–3)
, we see that
\accentset{\circ}{R}_{u,\beta}
is isomorphic to the space

\left\{B\in G/B_{+}\mid B_{+}\xrightarrow{w}B\xleftarrow{w_{\circ}u}B_{-}\right\}

which can be identified with the Richardson variety
\accentset{\circ}{R}_{u,w}\subset G/B_{+}
. Thus, braid Richardson varieties generalize open Richardson varieties.
Theorem

2.1
extends to the setting of braid Richardson varieties.

In
Section

3.1
, we describe our construction in the most general setting of double braid varieties, and define a 3D plabic graph

and a quiver
\widetilde{Q}_{u,\beta}
for a pair

consisting of a permutation
u

In this case, if we impose that the double word

3.
Double braid quivers

The goal of this section is to define 3D plabic graphs, conjugate surfaces, relative cycles, and the associated quivers in the extended generality of
double braid words
.

3.1.
Double braid words

Let
I:=[n-1]
. A
double braid word
is a word
\beta=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{m})\in(\pm I)^{m}
in the alphabet

\pm I:=\{-1,-2,\dots,-(n-1)\}\sqcup\{1,2,\dots,n-1\}.

We denote the set of double braid words by
(\pm I)^{m}
. We will usually abbreviate
\beta=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{m})
as
\beta=i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{m}
. For
\beta\in(\pm I)^{m}
, we write
\ell(\beta):=m
. The goal of this section is to associate a quiver
\widetilde{Q}_{u,\beta}
to a pair
(u,\beta)
, where
u\in S_{n}
and
\beta\in(\pm I)^{m}
.

The word
\beta\in(\pm I)^{m}
should be considered as a
shuffle
of two positive braid words in the commuting alphabets
I,-I
. We emphasize that the letter
-i
does
not
correspond to
\sigma_{i}^{-1}
, the inverse of the braid group generator
\sigma_{i}
. We call elements of
I

red
and elements of
-I

blue
.

For
i\in\pm I
, let

s_{i}^{+}:=\begin{cases}s_{i},&\text{if }i>0,\\
{\operatorname{id}},&\text{if }i<0,\end{cases}\quad s_{i}^{-}:=\begin{cases}{\operatorname{id}},&\text{if }i>0,\\
s_{-i},&\text{if }i<0.\\
\end{cases}

We use the convention that the positive indices act on the right while the negative indices act on the left; for a permutation
u\in S_{n}
and
i\in\pm I
, we write this action as
u\mapsto s_{i}^{-}us_{i}^{+}
.

Let
\ast
denote the standard Demazure product on
S_{n}
: for
v\in S_{n}
and
i\in I
, let
v\ast s_{i}
be equal to
vs_{i}
if
v<vs_{i}
and to
v
otherwise. This operation is associative. For a double braid word
\beta
, its
Demazure product
is defined by

\pi(\beta):=s_{i_{m}}^{-}*s_{i_{m-1}}^{-}*\cdots*s_{i_{1}}^{-}*s_{i_{1}}^{+}*s_{i_{2}}^{+}*\cdots*s_{i_{m}}^{+}\quad\in S_{n}.

Definition 3.1
.

For
u\in S_{n}
and a double braid word
\beta
, we write
u\leq\beta
if
u\leq\pi(\beta)
in the Bruhat order on
S_{n}
.

Let
\beta=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{m})\in(\pm I)^{m}
and
u\in S_{n}
. A
u
-subexpression
of
\beta
is a sequence

{\mathbf{u}}=(u_{0},u_{1},\dots,u_{m})\in S_{n}^{m+1}
such that
u_{0}={\operatorname{id}}
,
u_{m}=u
, and such that for each
c\in[m]
, we have either
u_{c}=u_{c-1}
or
u_{c}=s_{i_{c}}^{-}u_{c-1}s_{i_{c}}^{+}
. It is clear that
\beta
contains a
u
-subexpression if and only if
u\leq\beta
.

Suppose that
u\leq\beta
. Out of all
u
-subexpressions of
\beta
, there exists a unique “rightmost” one, called the
u
-positive distinguished subexpression (
u
-PDS)
. It can be computed explicitly using the following operation which we call
Demazure quotient
: for
u\in S_{n}
and
i\in I
, set

s_{i}\vartriangleright u=\begin{cases}s_{i}u,&\text{ if }s_{i}u<u,\\
u,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad u\vartriangleleft s_{i}=\begin{cases}us_{i},&\text{ if }us_{i}<u,\\
u,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

By convention, we set
{\operatorname{id}}\vartriangleright u=u=u\vartriangleleft{\operatorname{id}}
. The
u
-PDS
{\mathbf{u}}=(u_{0},u_{1},\dots,u_{m})
is computed iteratively starting from
u_{m}=u
. For
c=m,m-1,\dots,1
, we set

(3.1)

u_{c-1}:=s_{i_{c}}^{-}\vartriangleright u_{c}\vartriangleleft s_{i_{c}}^{+}.

Since
u\leq\beta
, we have
u_{0}={\operatorname{id}}
.
We set
J_{u,\beta}:=\{c\in[m]\mid u_{c}=u_{c-1}\}
. We refer to the indices in
J_{u,\beta}
as
solid crossings
and to the indices in
[m]\setminus J_{u,\beta}
as
hollow crossings
.
The indices in
[m]\setminus J_{u,\beta}
form a reduced word for
u
, i.e., we have
|J_{u,\beta}|=\ell(\beta)-\ell(u)
.

Remark 3.2
.

When
\beta\in I^{m}
is a reduced word for a permutation and

(u_{0},\dots,u_{m})
is a positive distinguished subexpression in the sense of
[

The terminology of “solid” and “hollow” crossings is drawn from
[

When the positive and negative subwords of

are both reduced and

(u_{0},\dots,u_{m})
is a positive double distinguished subexpression in the sense of
[
WY07
]
.

For the rest of this section, we fix a pair
(u,\beta)\in S_{n}\times(\pm I)^{m}
satisfying

, and let
{\mathbf{u}}
be the

-PDS of

Remark 3.3

All our constructions (including quivers, 3D plabic graphs, cluster algebra structures, and braid varieties) will be invariant under the following operation of appending hollow crossings on the right: if

. When passing from

Definition 3.16

4.2.
Moves for double braid words

Two double braid words are
(double braid) equivalent
if they are related by a sequence of the following double braid moves:

(B1)

ij\leftrightarrow ji
if
i,j\in\pm I
have different signs;

(B2)

ij\leftrightarrow ji
if
i,j\in\pm I
have the same sign and
|i-j|>1
;

(B3)

iji\leftrightarrow jij
if
i,j\in\pm I
have the same sign and
|i-j|=1
.

Definition 4.2
.

We say that a double braid move is
fully solid
if all of the indices involved are solid. Suppose that
i_{d-1}=-j
and
i_{d}=i
for some
i,j\in I
and
2\leq d\leq m
. Then we say that the move (
B
1
) swapping these two indices is
special
if
u_{d-1}s_{i}=s_{j}u_{d-1}
, and
solid-special
if it is special and fully solid. Motivated by the following theorem, we call (
B
1
) (solid-special) and (
B
3
) (fully solid)
mutation moves
. All other braid moves are
non-mutation moves
.

Theorem 4.3
.

The mutation type of
\widetilde{Q}_{u,\beta}
is invariant under double braid moves
(
B
1
)
–
(
B
3
)
on
\beta
. More precisely:

(1)

Under mutation moves, the quiver
\widetilde{Q}_{u,\beta}
changes by a mutation. The relative cycles
(C_{c})_{c\in J_{u,\beta}}
change according to (
3.4
). The graph
G_{u,\beta}
changes by a square move
(
M2
)
.

(2)

Under non-mutation moves, the quiver
\widetilde{Q}_{u,\beta}
and the relative cycles
(C_{c})_{c\in J_{u,\beta}}
are unchanged, up to relabeling. The graph
G_{u,\beta}
changes by a sequence of contraction-uncontraction moves
(
M1
)
.

In both cases, the surface
\mathbf{S}_{u,\beta}
is unchanged.

We prove
Theorem

4.3
in the next two subsections. Throughout, we let
\beta
and

be two braids related by one of the moves in

4.3

and

Let
\mathcal{F}\cong{\mathbb{C}}(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})
be isomorphic to the field of rational functions in
r
algebraically independent variables. A
seed
in
\mathcal{F}
is a pair
\Sigma=({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})
where
\widetilde{Q}
is an ice quiver with
r
vertices
\widetilde{V}=V^{\operatorname{mut}}\sqcup V^{\operatorname{fro}}
and
{\mathbf{x}}=\{x_{v}\}_{v\in\widetilde{V}}
is a transcendence basis of
\mathcal{F}
. The tuple
{\mathbf{x}}
is the
cluster
, its elements are
cluster variables
and
x_{v}
is
mutable
if
v\in V^{\operatorname{mut}}
and
frozen
otherwise.

Given a seed
({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})
in
\mathcal{F}
and
d\in V^{\operatorname{mut}}
, one can
mutate
in direction
d
to obtain a new seed
\mu_{d}({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})=({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},\mu_{d}(\widetilde{Q}))
. The quiver of the mutated seed is the mutation of
\widetilde{Q}
in direction
d
(see
Definition

3.13
). The cluster
{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}=\{x^{\prime}_{v}\}_{v\in\widetilde{V}}
of the new seed satisfies
x^{\prime}_{v}=x_{v}
for
v\neq d
and

x^{\prime}_{d}=\frac{\prod_{v\to d}x_{v}+\prod_{d\to v}x_{v}}{x_{d}}\in\mathcal{F}.

By repeatedly mutating, we generate (possibly infinitely) many seeds and cluster variables.
We denote by
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})
the
cluster algebra
associated to the seed
({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})
. This is the
{\mathbb{C}}
-subalgebra of
\mathcal{F}
generated by all cluster variables and the inverses of frozen variables. We call
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})

isolated
(resp.,
really full rank
) if
\widetilde{Q}
is.

We denote by
\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})
the
upper cluster algebra
associated to the seed
({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})
; see
[
BFZ05
]
. It is given by

\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})=\bigcap_{({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},\widetilde{Q}^{\prime})}{\mathbb{C}}[(x_{v}^{\prime})^{\pm 1}:v\in\widetilde{V}]\subset\mathcal{F},

where the intersection is taken over all seeds
({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},\widetilde{Q}^{\prime})
which can be obtained from
({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})
by a sequence of mutations.

Note that we have isomorphisms
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})\cong{\mathcal{A}}(\mathbf{y},\widetilde{Q})
and
\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})\cong\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{y},\widetilde{Q})
for any two clusters
{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{y}
.
Thus, we may occasionally write
{\mathcal{A}}(\widetilde{Q})
and
\mathcal{U}(\widetilde{Q})
if the particular choice of initial cluster does not matter.

In general, we have
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})\subseteq\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})
, and the containment may be strict. However, we have equality if
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})
is
locally acyclic
, a property introduced by Muller
[
Mul13
]
. We need a few definitions before defining local acyclicity; we follow the presentation of
[
Mul14
]
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})
be a cluster algebra and let
S\subset V^{\operatorname{mut}}
. Then the cluster algebra
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q}[S])
obtained by freezing
S
is a
cluster localization
of
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})
if

(5.1)

{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q}[S])={\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})[x_{v}^{-1}:v\in S].

(In general, the left-hand side is contained in the right.) Lemma 4.3 of
[
Mul13
]
states that if
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q}[S])=\mathcal{U}(x,\widetilde{Q}[S])
, then (
5.1
) automatically holds.

A collection of cluster localizations
\{{\mathcal{A}}_{i}\}
of a cluster algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
is a
cover
if for every prime ideal
P
of
{\mathcal{A}}
, there is some
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
such that
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}P\subsetneq{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
.
A cluster algebra is
locally acyclic
if it has a cover by isolated cluster algebras. We say that
\widetilde{Q}
is
locally acyclic
if
{\mathcal{A}}(\widetilde{Q})
is.
The motivation for this definition is that, in this case, the collection of varieties
\text{Spec}{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
forms an open cover of
\text{Spec}{\mathcal{A}}
.

We will use the following facts about locally acyclic cluster algebras.

Proposition 5.2

(
[
Mul13
, Proposition 3.10]
)
.

Let
\widetilde{Q}
be an ice quiver and let
Q
denote its mutable part, as usual. Then
{\mathcal{A}}(\widetilde{Q})
is locally acyclic if and only if
{\mathcal{A}}(Q)
is locally acyclic.

Theorem 5.3

(
[
Mul13
, Theorem 4.1]
)
.

If
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})
is locally acyclic, then
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})=\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})
.

One frequently studied class of locally acyclic quivers are
Louise
quivers
[
LS16
]
. We focus instead on
sink-recurrent
quivers, defined below, which may not be Louise but are locally acyclic. In the next subsection, we show that our quivers of interest,
\widetilde{Q}_{u,\beta}
, are sink-recurrent (see
Theorem

5.6
).

Let
Q
be a quiver with no frozen vertices. A vertex
s
of
Q
is a
sink
if it has no outgoing arrows. We let
N^{\operatorname{in}}_{s}(Q)
be the set of vertices of
Q
that have an arrow pointing to
s
. The following notion is analogous to the class of
leaf-recurrent quivers
from
[
GL22a
, Section 5.4]
; see also
[
GL22a
, Remark 5.14]
.

Definition 5.4
.

The class of
sink-recurrent quivers
is defined recursively as follows.

—

Any isolated quiver
Q
is sink-recurrent.

—

Any quiver that is mutation equivalent to a sink-recurrent quiver is sink-recurrent.

—

Suppose that a quiver
Q
has a sink vertex
s
such that the quivers
Q-\{s\}
and
Q-(N^{\operatorname{in}}_{s}(Q)\cup\{s\})
are sink-recurrent. Then
Q
is sink-recurrent.

The above definition refers to mutable quivers (without frozen vertices). We say that an ice quiver
\widetilde{Q}
is
sink-recurrent
if its mutable part
Q
is sink-recurrent.

Proposition 5.5
.

If
\widetilde{Q}
is a sink-recurrent quiver, then
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{Q})
is locally acyclic.

Proof.

If
\widetilde{Q}
is isolated, we are done. Otherwise, since local acyclicity is a property of the cluster algebra rather than the quiver, we may assume that
Q
has a sink
s
so that
Q-\{s\}
and
Q-(N^{\operatorname{in}}_{s}(Q)\cup\{s\})
are sink-recurrent (by mutating if necessary). Consider the freezings
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}={\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{x}},Q[s])
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{2}={\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{x}},Q[N^{\operatorname{in}}_{s}(Q)])
. The mutable parts of
Q[s]
and
Q[N^{\operatorname{in}}_{s}(Q)]
are
Q-\{s\}
and
Q-N^{\operatorname{in}}_{s}(Q)
, respectively, which are both sink-recurrent, the former by definition, and the latter because
Q-N^{\operatorname{in}}_{s}(Q)
differs from the sink-recurrent quiver
Q-(N^{\operatorname{in}}_{s}(Q)\cup\{t\})
by an isolated vertex. So
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
are locally acyclic by induction, and thus are cluster localizations.

Let
t
be any neighbor of
s
. Then the pair
(s,t)
forms a
covering pair
in the sense of
[
Mul13
]
, and by
[
Mul13
, Lemma 5.3]
it follows that
x_{s}
and
x_{t}
cannot simultaneously vanish on
{\mathcal{A}}(\widetilde{Q})
. It follows that
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
cover
{\mathcal{A}}
. The union of the covers of
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
by isolated cluster algebras is a cover of
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{x}},Q)
by isolated cluster algebras. ∎

5.3.
Really full rank

Our goal is to show the following result; cf.
Definition

5.1
.

Theorem 5.9
.

The quiver
\widetilde{Q}_{u,\beta}
is really full rank.

We recall what we have described before: We defined
\Lambda_{u,\beta}=H_{1}(\mathbf{S}_{u,\beta},\mathbf{M})
,
\Lambda^{\ast}_{u,\beta}=H_{1}(\mathbf{S}_{u,\beta}\setminus\mathbf{M},\partial\mathbf{S}_{u,\beta}\setminus\mathbf{M})
and
\Lambda^{\operatorname{closed}}_{u,\beta}=H_{1}(\mathbf{S}_{u,\beta})
. The lattice
\Lambda^{\operatorname{closed}}_{u,\beta}
is a sublattice of both
\Lambda_{u,\beta}
and
\Lambda^{\ast}_{u,\beta}
, and non-canonically splits off as a direct summand of either (
Lemma

3.6
).
We have a perfect pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\Lambda_{u,\beta}\times\Lambda^{\ast}_{u,\beta}\to\mathbb{Z}
.
For
c\in J_{u,\beta}
, we have constructed relative cycles
C_{c}
in
\Lambda_{u,\beta}
; if
c\in J_{u,\beta}^{\operatorname{mut}}
then
C_{c}\in\Lambda^{\operatorname{closed}}_{u,\beta}
.
The matrix
\widetilde{B}
has entries
\langle C_{c},C_{d}\rangle
for
c\in J_{u,\beta}
and
d\in J_{u,\beta}^{\operatorname{mut}}
.
We want to show that the rows of this matrix (indexed by
J_{u,\beta}
) span
{\mathbb{Z}}^{J_{u,\beta}^{\operatorname{mut}}}
over
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Lemma 5.10
.

The relative cycles
(C_{c})_{c\in J_{u,\beta}}
are a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-basis of
\Lambda_{u,\beta}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{S}_{u,\beta}
deformation retracts onto
G_{u,\beta}
, we see that
\Lambda_{u,\beta}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{|J_{u,\beta}|}
is a free abelian group whose rank is the number of bridges in
G_{u,\beta}
.
Thus, it is enough to show that the cycles
(C_{c})_{c\in J_{u,\beta}}
span
\Lambda_{u,\beta}
over
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Let
\gamma=\sum a_{e}e
be any relative cycle in
\Lambda_{u,\beta}
. Removing all bridges from
G_{u,\beta}
leaves
n
disjoint paths so, if
\gamma
is nonzero, there must be a bridge
c
with
a_{c}\neq 0
; let
c
be the rightmost such bridge. Replacing
\gamma
by
\gamma-a_{c}C_{c}
, we replace
\gamma
by a cycle supported on bridges to the left of
c
. Continuing in this manner, we eventually obtain a cycle supported on no bridges, which must be
0
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

5.9
.

If the rows of the matrix
\widetilde{B}
spanned a proper sublattice of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{J_{u,\beta}^{\operatorname{mut}}}
, then there would be a prime
p
, and integers
\bm{a}:=(a_{d})_{d\in J_{u,\beta}^{\operatorname{mut}}}
, not all
0\bmod p
, such that the element
C_{\bm{a}}:=\sum_{d\in J_{u,\beta}^{\operatorname{mut}}}a_{d}C_{d}\in\Lambda^{\operatorname{closed}}_{u,\beta}\subset\Lambda^{\ast}_{u,\beta}
satisfies

\langle C_{c},C_{\bm{a}}\rangle\equiv 0\bmod p
for all
c\in J_{u,\beta}
.
So, using
Lemma

5.10
and the fact that the pairing
{\langle}\cdot,\cdot{\rangle}
is perfect, we get
C_{\bm{a}}\in p\Lambda^{\ast}_{u,\beta}
. But
\Lambda^{\operatorname{closed}}_{u,\beta}
is a direct summand of
\Lambda^{\ast}_{u,\beta}
by
Lemma

3.6
, and thus
C_{\bm{a}}\in p\Lambda^{\operatorname{closed}}_{u,\beta}
. Using the
Lemma

5.10
again to note that the
C_{d}
,
d\in J_{u,\beta}^{\operatorname{mut}}
, are linearly independent modulo
p
, this shows that all the
a_{d}
are
0\bmod p
, contrary to assumption.
∎

We also take the opportunity to verify a claim made earlier in
Remark

3.15
.

Lemma 5.11
.

The cycles
(C_{c})_{c\in J_{u,\beta}^{\operatorname{mut}}}
can be extended to a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-basis of
\Lambda^{\operatorname{closed}}_{u,\beta}
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

5.10
, the cycles
(C_{c})_{c\in J_{u,\beta}^{\operatorname{mut}}}
are linearly independent, so it remains to check that
\Lambda^{\operatorname{closed}}_{u,\beta}/{\mathbb{Z}}\cdot\{C_{c}:c\in J_{u,\beta}^{\operatorname{mut}}\}
is torsion free.
This embeds in the larger abelian group

which, using

∎

6.1.
Notation

Let
G=\operatorname{SL}_{n}
. Recall that
I:=[n-1]
indexes the simple transpositions of the Weyl group
W=S_{n}
, and that for
i\in I
, we denote
i^{*}:=n-i
. For
i\in I
, we set
(-i)^{*}:=-i^{*}
. Let
B_{+}
and
B_{-}
denote the subgroup of upper triangular and lower triangular matrices, respectively, and let
U_{+}
and
U_{-}
denote the respective unipotent subgroups. We use
H
to denote the torus of diagonal matrices.

For
i\in I
, let
\phi_{i}:\operatorname{SL}_{2}\to G
denote the homomorphism where
\phi_{i}(g)
is the matrix which has
g
as the
2\times 2
submatrix on rows and columns
i,i+1
and otherwise agrees with the identity matrix.

We use
\phi_{i}
to lift
S_{n}
to
G
. Let

\dot{s}_{i}:=\phi_{i}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

For
w\in S_{n}
, we define
\dot{w}:=\dot{s}_{i_{1}}\dots\dot{s}_{i_{n}}
where
s_{i_{1}}\dots s_{i_{n}}
is a reduced expression for
w
. The map
w\mapsto\dot{w}
is not a homomorphism, but if
\ell(vw)=\ell(v)+\ell(w)
, then
(vw)^{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}=\dot{v}\dot{w}
. In particular,
\dot{w}
does not depend on the choice of reduced expression. Explicitly,

(\dot{w})_{i,j}=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }i\neq w(j),\\
(-1)^{|\{a<j:w(a)>w(j)\}|}&\text{ if }i=w(j).\end{cases}

We omit the dot when the choice of the signs in the permutation matrix of
w
does not matter: e.g., we write
B_{+}wB_{+}
and
Hw
in place of
B_{+}\dot{w}B_{+}
and
H\dot{w}
.

We will also need the generators

(6.1)

x_{i}(t):=\phi_{i}\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad y_{i}(t):=\phi_{i}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
t&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\alpha^{\vee}_{i}(t):=\phi_{i}\begin{pmatrix}t&0\\
0&1/t\end{pmatrix},

and the
braid matrices

z_{i}(t):=\phi_{i}\begin{pmatrix}t&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}=x_{i}(t)\dot{s}_{i}=\dot{s}_{i}y_{i}(-t);

\bar{z}_{i}(t):=\phi_{i}\begin{pmatrix}t&1\\

6.2.
Weighted flags

A
weighted flag
is an element
F=gU_{+}\in G/U_{+}
. Associated to a weighted flag
F
is the flag
\pi(F)=gB_{+}
, the image of
F
in
G/B_{+}
.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
w\in S_{n}
. Two weighted flags
(F,F^{\prime})
are called
(weakly)
w
-related
if there exist
g\in G
and
h\in H
such that
(gF,gF^{\prime})=(U_{+},h\dot{w}U_{+})\in G/U_{+}\times G/U_{+}
. Equivalently,
F=g_{1}U_{+}
and
F^{\prime}=g_{2}U_{+}
are weakly

-related if and only if
g_{1}^{-1}g_{2}\in B_{+}\dot{w}B_{+}
. We write this as
F\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\Longrightarrow}}F^{\prime}
.

Two weighted flags
(F,F^{\prime})
are called
strictly
w
-related
if there exists
g\in G
such that
(gF,gF^{\prime})=(U_{+},\dot{w}U_{+})\in G/U_{+}\times G/U_{+}
. Equivalently,
F=g_{1}U_{+}
and
F^{\prime}=g_{2}U_{+}
are strictly
w
-related if and only if
g_{1}^{-1}g_{2}\in U_{+}\dot{w}U_{+}
. We write this as
F\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\longrightarrow}}F^{\prime}
.

Let
F,F^{\prime}
be two weighted flags and let
B,B^{\prime}
be their images in
G/B_{+}
. Then

(6.2)

F\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\longrightarrow}}F^{\prime}
implies
F\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\Longrightarrow}}F^{\prime}
, which in turn implies

(see

).

We collect some elementary facts about relative position.

.

Let

(1)

F\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\operatorname{id}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}F^{\prime}
if and only if

.

(2)

If
F\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\longrightarrow}}F^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\longrightarrow}}F^{\prime\prime}

F\stackrel{{\scriptstyle vw}}{{\longrightarrow}}F^{\prime\prime}
.

(3)

If
F\stackrel{{\scriptstyle vw}}{{\longrightarrow}}F^{\prime\prime}
, then there exists a unique

. If

, there exist unique

.

Proof.

Parts (1), (2) and the first sentence of (3) can be found in
[

, Appendix]
. We show the existence of

in (3); the argument for

is similar. The images
\pi(F)
and

in

uniquely determine a flag

satisfying

, and

to

such that

.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

7.
Deodhar geometry and seeds

Let

. We will use the

-positive distinguished subexpression (cf.

-almost positive sequences (cf.

7.1.
The Deodhar torus

We continue to denote the
u
-PDS by
{\mathbf{u}}=(u_{0},\dots,u_{m})
.

Definition 7.1
.

The
Deodhar torus

T_{{u,\beta}}\subset\accentset{\circ}{R}_{{u,\beta}}
is the subset of
\accentset{\circ}{R}_{{u,\beta}}
given by the conditions

(7.1)

X_{c}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w_{\circ}u_{c}}}{{\Longleftarrow}}Y_{c}\qquad\mbox{ for $c=0,1,\ldots,m$.}

Remark 7.2
.

Points in
T_{{u,\beta}}
lift to walks of the sort described in
Remark

6.5
where at each step, one greedily increases the relative position of
X_{c}
and
Y_{c}
.

Recall from the proof of
Lemma

6.20
that all grid minors are nonvanishing on the Deodhar torus
T_{{u,\beta}}
.

As the next proposition shows, the Deodhar torus is in fact a torus, and the solid chamber minors form a basis of characters.

Proposition 7.3

(
[
GLSB23
, Corollary 2.8, Proposition 2.12]
)
.

(1)

The Deodhar torus
T_{{u,\beta}}
is an open subset of
\accentset{\circ}{R}_{{u,\beta}}
, isomorphic to an algebraic torus of dimension
d({u,\beta})
.

(2)

The character lattice of
T_{{u,\beta}}
consists of Laurent monomials in the solid chamber minors
\Delta_{c}
,
c\in J_{u,\beta}
. The ring of regular functions on
T_{{u,\beta}}
is the ring of Laurent polynomials in the chamber minors.

(3)

The grid minors are characters of
T_{{u,\beta}}
.

While the braid variety
\accentset{\circ}{R}_{{u,\beta}}
is unchanged by braid moves, the Deodhar torus
T_{{u,\beta}}
may change. In particular, we will show later that non-mutation moves preserve the Deodhar torus, while mutation moves change it.

For

Definition 7.4

Remark 7.5
.

Points in the locus satisfying (

and

The Deodhar hypersurfaces form the complement of the Deodhar torus, as the next proposition shows. We denote by

under the quotient map

Proposition 7.6

(
[

, Proposition 2.19]
)
.

The closed subset

for

is irreducible and has codimension

For a grid minor

and

is regular on

, so

exactly, using the

-PDS and

-APS.

Proposition 7.7
.

Fix

. For

{\mathrm{ord}}_{V_{d}}\Delta_{c,j}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }u_{c}[j]\neq u_{c}^{{\langle}d{\rangle}}[j],\\
0&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathrm{ord}}_{V_{d}}\Delta_{c,-j}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }u^{-1}_{c}[j]\neq(u_{c}^{{\langle}d{\rangle}})^{-1}[j],\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Remark 7.8
.

In the case of braid varieties of arbitrary type treated in
[
GLSB23
]
, the analog of

7.7
becomes more complicated. It is still the case that
{\mathrm{ord}}_{V_{d}}\Delta_{c,j}=0
if and only if

; see
[
GLSB23
, Proposition 2.21]
. However, if
u_{c}[j]\neq u_{c}^{{\langle}d{\rangle}}[j]
then we have
{\mathrm{ord}}_{V_{d}}\Delta_{c,j}\geq 1
, and determining the exact value of
{\mathrm{ord}}_{V_{d}}\Delta_{c,j}
becomes a highly nontrivial problem; see
[
GLSB23
, Section 7]
for a combinatorial algorithm.

Proof.

We compute the order of vanishing inside the space
S\subset\mathcal{Y}_{u,\beta}
of tuples
(X_{\bullet},Y_{\bullet})
of the form (
6.5
) (i.e., the space where
X_{0}
has been gauge-fixed to
U_{+}
). By
Lemma

6.3
, this space

is an affine space with coordinates
{\mathbf{t}}=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots)
, as each
X_{i}
or
Y_{i}
is of the form

\bar{z}_{i_{j_{1}}}(t_{j_{1}})\cdots\bar{z}_{i_{j_{r}}}(t_{j_{r}})U_{+}
for some
r
. (These coordinates differ from those given by (
6.4
).) The
Z_{c}
are then also products of inverses of braid matrices in some subset of the parameters
{\mathbf{t}}
.

Let
Z
be any matrix that is a product of braid matrices or their inverses with parameters from
{\mathbf{t}}
, each parameter used at most once. Then it is easy to see that every minor of

is linear or constant in each variable

. The Deodhar hypersurface

is cut out by a minor of

, and this minor has degree one in some parameter

. Any grid minor
\Delta_{c,j}
is at most degree one in

V_{d}
.

Suppose
j\in I
. Then
u_{c}[j]=u_{c}^{{\langle}d{\rangle}}[j]
if and only if for a generic point in

, the

has the correct relative position (given by

. This holds if and only if

does not vanish on

. As we showed above,

we obtain the stated formula for

is identical.
∎

Proposition 7.9
.

The map

(7.3)

f\mapsto({\mathrm{ord}}_{V_{c}}f)_{c\in J_{u,\beta}}

is an isomorphism from the character lattice of

to

Proof.

Let

be the image of

vanishes to order

∎

10.1.
Curious Lefschetz

Let
X
be a smooth, affine, complex algebraic variety of dimension
d
. Then the cohomology
H^{*}(X)=H^{*}(X,{\mathbb{C}})
has a mixed Hodge structure, which gives a Deligne (weak) splitting
H^{k}(X)=\bigoplus_{p,q}H^{k,(p,q)}(X)
. We say that
X
is of
mixed Tate type
if
H^{k}(X)=\bigoplus_{p}H^{k,(p,p)}(X)
. We say that
X
satisfies the
curious Lefschetz property
if
X
is of mixed Tate type and there is a class
[\gamma]\in H^{2,(2,2)}(X)
such that cup product induces isomorphisms

[\gamma]^{d-p}:H^{p+s,(p,p)}(X)\xrightarrow{\sim}H^{2d-p+s,(2d-p,2d-p)}(X)

for all
p
and
s
. Curious Lefschetz implies the
curious Poincaré symmetry

\dim H^{p+s,(p,p)}(X)=\dim H^{2d-p+s,(2d-p,2d-p)}(X).

In
[
LS16
]
it is shown that a large class of cluster varieties satisfy curious Lefschetz, and the following result is a consequence.

Theorem 10.1
.

Suppose that
\widetilde{Q}
is an ice quiver that is sink-recurrent, full rank, and has an even number of vertices. Then the cluster variety
X(\widetilde{Q})
satisfies curious Lefschetz.

The even-dimensional hypothesis is not a significant restriction since we can always add an extra isolated frozen vertex. See also
[
LS16
, Theorem 8.3]
.

Proof.

Let
X
be a smooth, affine complex algebraic variety, and
X=U\cup V
be an open covering of
X
, and
[\gamma]\in H^{2,(2,2)}(X)
. By
[
LS16
, Theorem 3.5]
, if all three
(U,[\gamma]),(V,[\gamma]),(U\cap V,[\gamma])
satisfy curious Lefschetz then so does
(X,[\gamma])
.

By
[
LS16
, Proposition 8.2]
, full rank isolated cluster varieties satisfy the curious Lefschetz property with respect to a (nearly canonical)
GSV form

[\gamma]
; cf.
[
GSV10
]
.

Let
X=\operatorname{Spec}{\mathcal{A}}(\widetilde{Q})
be a sink-recurrent, full rank cluster variety and let
X_{1}=\operatorname{Spec}{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
and
X_{2}=\operatorname{Spec}{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
give an open cover of

, as in the proof of

5.5
. Both

and

are sink-recurrent, and

∎

We do not know whether the Louise condition

is satisfied for the quivers in this work. The cohomology of open Richardson varieties are particularly interesting because of the relations to both Category

10.4.
Relation of 3D plabic graphs to Postnikov’s plabic graphs

We explain how our 3D plabic graphs generalize the plabic graphs of
[
Pos06
]
. A permutation

is called

if

and

. We denote by

the set of

-Grassmannian permutations. Thus, any reduced word for

, and all reduced words for

are related by commutation moves. It is well known that

rectangle. We draw Young diagrams in English notation.
A

is a way of placing a dot in some of the boxes of
{\lambda}
so that whenever a box of
{\lambda}
has a dot above it in the same column and to the left of it in the same row, it must also contain a dot. We will find it useful to note the contrapositive formulation: If a box of
{\lambda}
is empty, then either all the boxes to its left are empty, or all the boxes above it are empty (or both).

For each dot, we draw horizontal and vertical line segments connecting it to the southeastern boundary of
{\lambda}
. An example of a Le-diagram
\Gamma
is shown in Figure
28
(left)
. One can convert a Le-diagram
\Gamma
into a plabic graph

using the local rule
. The graph

is drawn in a disk which we identify with the Young diagram
{\lambda}
. Thus,

has
n
boundary vertices on the southeastern boundary of
{\lambda}
, and it has a unique northwestern boundary face which we denote
F_{0}
. Rotating
\Gamma
by
135^{\circ}
clockwise, replacing all empty boxes with crossings, and all dots with black-white bridges, we obtain a graph
G_{u,{\mathbf{w}}}
, where
{\mathbf{w}}
is a reduced word for
w
and

in the Bruhat order on

; see

10.5.2.

For

Remark 10.12
