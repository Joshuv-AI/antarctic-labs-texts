Diffusion equations with general nonlocal time and space derivatives
Chung-Sik Sin, Hyong-Chol O, Sang-Mun Kim

Abstract
In the present study, firstly, based on the continuous time random walk (CTRW) theory, general diffusion equations are derived. The time derivative is taken as the general Caputo-type derivative introduced by Kochubei and the spatial derivative is the general Laplacian defined by removing the conditions (1.5) and (1.6) from the definition of the general fractional Laplacian proposed in the paper (Servadei and Valdinoci, 2012). Secondly, the existence of solutions of the Cauchy problem for the general diffusion equation is proved by extending the domain of the general Laplacian to a general Sobolev space. The results for positivity and boundedness of the solutions are also obtained. In the last, the existence result for solutions of the initial boundary value problem (IBVP) for the general diffusion equation on a bounded domain is established by using the Friedrichs extension of the general fractional Laplacian introduced in the book (Bisci, Radulescu and Servadei, 2016).

Introduction

Anomalous diffusion phenomena have been observed in many physical processes such as diffusion processes inside living biological cells
[
1
,

,

, motion of charge carrier in amorphous semiconductors

]
and motion of suspended particle in ground water

In particular, with the help of modern single particle superresolution techniques, motion of anomalously diffusing particle in complex crowded environment has been intensively investigated
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
. Recently, in
[
10
]
, anomalous diffusion processes were successfully simulated by Monte Carlo method.

It is a very interesting matter that the anomalous diffusion process can be described by means of a CTRW model.
The probability density function (PDF) of the CTRW process is derived from the waiting time PDF and the jump length PDF
[
11
]
.
When the waiting time PDF
\phi(t)
of moving particle follows the relation:
\phi(t)\approx t^{-1-\alpha},t\rightarrow\infty
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then the random walk process, whose mean squared displacement (MSD) is
<x^{2}(t)>\approx t^{\alpha},t\rightarrow\infty
, corresponds to the Caputo/Riemann-Liouville time fractional diffusion equation
[
12
]
. If the jump length PDF
\lambda(x)
satisfies the relation:
\lambda(x)\approx|x|^{-1-\beta},|x|\rightarrow\infty
for some
\beta\in(0,2)
, then the Lévy process, whose MSD diverges, is modeled by the Riesz space fractional diffusion equation
[
12
]
.
However, classical fractional derivatives such as Caputo, Riemann-Liouville and Riesz fractional derivatives are insufficient to capture all of the anomalous diffusion phenomena.
In fact, in
[
13
,
14
]
, the authors found the ultraslow diffusive processes, whose MSDs don’t follow the power law in time but have logarithmic dependence.
In
[
15
]
, the authors used the fractional derivative of distributed order, which generalizes single-term and multi-term fractional derivatives, to model the ultraslow diffusion process effectively.

The diversity of the anomalous diffusion process needs a more general framework for modeling the complex diffusion phenomena. Kochubei introduced a general derivative which generalizes the previous fractional derivatives such as single-term, multi-term and distributed order Caputo-type derivatives
[
16
]
.
It has been shown in the papers
[
17
,
18
]
that the general derivative is very appropriate as time derivative in describing the anomalous diffusion process. On the other hand, in order to define a general space derivative, Servadei and Valdinoci proposed a generalization of the fractional Laplacian and considered an equation involving the generalized operator
[
19
]
. In
[
20
]
, the auhors investigated nonlocal elliptic equations involving the operator defined by removing a condition for the kernel function from the definition of the generalization of the fractional Laplacian introduced in
[
19
]
.
However, the MSDs of the random walk processes described by the anomalous diffusion equations involving the generalizations of the fractional Laplacian are infinite. The most important problem which affects the applicability of Lévy statistics is the divergence of the MSD due to slowly decaying tails
[
21
]
. In case of the exponentially tempered Lévy flights, which have been found in plasma physics and fluid mechanics, the divergence problem has been solved in
[
21
]
.

With the rapid development of mathematical modeling of anomalous diffusion processes, mathematical aspects for diffusion equations involving nonlocal derivatives have been widely investigated.
Eidelman and Kochubei studied the fundamental solution of the Cauchy problem of the Caputo-type time fractional diffusion equation by employing the H-functions
[
22
]
.
In
[
23
]
, a maximum principle of the IBVP of the single-term Caputo-type time fractional diffusion equation was proved.
In
[
24
]
, the authors used the eigenfunction expansion of the symmetric uniformly elliptic operator and the properties of the two-parameter Mittag-Leffler function to establish the existence of weak solutions of the IBVP of the single-term time fractional diffusion equation.
In
[
25
]
, existence results for a suitably defined weak solution of the IBVP of the single-term time fractional diffusion equation were obtained in the fractional Sobolev space.
In
[
26
,
27
]
, existence and uniqueness of solutions of the multi-term Caputo-type time fractional diffusion equation were proved by deriving the maximum principle for the IBVP and the new properties of the multivariate Mittag-Leffler function.
In
[
28
,
29
]
, the authors considered the distributed order time fractional diffusion equations.
In
[
30
]
, the fundamental solution of the Cauchy problem for the fractional diffusion
equation with the Caputo fractional derivative in time and the Riesz-Feller derivative in space was investigated.
In
[
31
]
, existence and uniqueness of solutions of the IBVP for the fractional diffusion equation with the general Caputo-type derivative in time were established.

In this paper, we try to develop a more general framework for describing more complex anomalous diffusion phenomena.
By using the CTRW theory, we obtain a general diffusion equation involving the general Caputo-type differential operator and a nonlocal operator more general than the general fractional Laplacians proposed in
[
19
,
20
]
.
The advantage of the general diffusion equation is that it can capture the Lévy process whose MSD is finite.

The organization of the rest paper is as follows. In Section 2, the general nonlocal derivatives are discussed and the general nonlocal diffusion equations are derived from the CTRW theory.
In Section 3, we establish the existence, positivity and boundedness of solutions of the Cauchy problem for the general diffusion equation. For special cases of the general diffusion equation, the analytical solutions are represented in terms of Mittag-Leffler type functions.
In Section 4, we obtain the existence result for the IBVP of the general diffusion equation on a bounded domain.

Definition 2.1

\mathscr{D}_{(g)}v(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{t}g(t-\tau)v(\tau)d\tau-g(t)v(0).

\Lambda g(s)=\hat{g}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-ts}g(t)dt

(C2)

(C3)

It was proved in

Meanwhile, by the results in

34
]

Lemma 2.1
.

Let

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{(g)}v(t)

\displaystyle=-\lambda v(t),t>0,

(2.2)

\displaystyle v(0)=1

2.2
General Laplacian

Definition 2.2
.

Let the function

\min\{|x|^{2},1\}k(x)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

Then the general Laplacian

\mathscr{L}_{(k)}v(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big(v(x+y)+v(x-y)-2v(x)\big)k(y)dy,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(2.4)

We remark that Definition

An example of the general Laplacian

In the following lemma, an alternative definition of the general Laplacian

is introduced

Lemma 2.2
.

Let the function

satisfy the condition:

\min\{|x|^{2},1\}k(x)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

and the function

\zeta(x)=2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1-\cos(xy))k(y)dy,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

where

\displaystyle Fv(\xi)=\tilde{v}(\xi)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v(x)e^{-ix\xi}dx,

\displaystyle F^{-1}v(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v(\xi)e^{ix\xi}d\xi.

Here

means the Schwartz space of rapidly decreasing smooth functions

\sup_{x=(x_{1},...x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bigg|x_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{i_{n}}\frac{\partial^{j_{1}}v(x)}{\partial x_{1}^{j_{1}}}\cdots\frac{\partial^{j_{n}}v(x)}{\partial x_{n}^{j_{n}}}\bigg|<+\infty

for any
i_{1},...,i_{n},j_{1},...,j_{n}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.

Proof.

For

, we have

\big(v(x+y)+v(x-y)-2v(x)\big)k(y)\leq\|D^{2}v\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}|y|^{2}k(y).

Thus
\mathscr{L}_{(k)}v
exists.
For
v\in S(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle F\{\mathscr{L}_{(k)}v\}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k(y)F\big(v(x+y)+v(x-y)-2v(x)\big)(\xi)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k(y)\big(e^{i\xi y}+e^{-i\xi y}-2\big)dyFv(\xi)

\displaystyle=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k(y)\big(1-\cos(\xi y)\big)dyFv(\xi)

\displaystyle=-\zeta(\xi)Fv(\xi).

∎

General diffusion equation

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\|g\|_{L^{1}(0,t)}}{t}=0.

Proof.

Firstly, we prove the part (a) of the lemma.
Since the function
g
is completely monotone, by Bernstein’s theorem
[
39
]
, there exists a nondecreasing function
\sigma:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

g(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-ts}d\sigma(s),t>0,

where
\sigma(0)=0
and
\sigma(t)
is continuous from the left.
Moreover,

(-1)^{m}g^{(m)}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{m}e^{-ts}d\sigma(s),m=0,1,2,...,t>0,

and

(-1)^{m}g^{(m)}(0+)=\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{m}d\sigma(s).

Then, for
m=0
, we have

g(0+)=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\sigma(s)=\sigma(\infty).

We can easily see that

\sup_{s\in[0,\infty)}s^{m}e^{-ts}=\bigg(\frac{m}{et}\bigg)^{m},m\in\mathbb{N},t>0.

Thus, we have

|g^{(m)}(t)|=\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{m}e^{-ts}d\sigma(s)<g(0+)\bigg(\frac{m}{et}\bigg)^{m},m\in\mathbb{N},t>0.

Secondly, we prove the part (b) of the lemma.
By Fubini’s theorem, for
s>0
, we have

\displaystyle g(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-sw}d\sigma(w)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{w}^{\infty}se^{-s\tau}d\tau d\sigma(w)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\tau}se^{-s\tau}d\sigma(w)d\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}se^{-s\tau}\sigma(\tau)d\tau.

For
t>0
, we deduce

\displaystyle\|g\|_{L^{1}(0,t)}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}se^{-s\tau}\sigma(\tau)d\tau ds=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}se^{-s\tau}ds\sigma(\tau)d\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}(1-e^{-t\tau}-t\tau e^{-t\tau})\sigma(\tau)d\tau.

Then, for
t>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{\|g\|_{L^{1}(0,t)}}{t}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-e^{-t\tau}-t\tau e^{-t\tau}}{t\tau^{2}}\sigma(\tau)d\tau\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-e^{-t\tau}-t\tau e^{-t\tau}}{t\tau^{2}}\sigma(\infty)d\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-e^{-w}-we^{-w}}{w^{2}}\sigma(\infty)dw.

Since

\lim_{w\to 0}\frac{1-e^{-w}-we^{-w}}{w^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}

and

\sup_{w\in[0,\infty)}\{1-e^{-w}-we^{-w}\}=1,

we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-e^{-w}-we^{-w}}{w^{2}}dw=\int_{0}^{\delta}dw+\int_{\delta}^{\infty}\frac{1}{w^{2}}dw<\infty,

where
\delta>0
is a real number such that

\frac{1-e^{-w}-we^{-w}}{w^{2}}<1,w\in(0,\delta).

It follows from Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\|g\|_{L^{1}(0,t)}}{t}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-e^{-t\tau}-t\tau e^{-t\tau}}{t\tau^{2}}\sigma(\tau)d\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1-e^{-t\tau}-t\tau e^{-t\tau}}{t\tau^{2}}\sigma(\tau)d\tau=0.

∎

Theorem 3.4

(3.8)

Proof.

Taking the Fourier transform to (

) and (

\mathscr{D}_{(g)}\tilde{p}(t,\xi)=-\zeta{(\xi)}\tilde{p}(t,\xi),t>0,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\|p(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\|\tilde{p}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\|\tilde{f}(\cdot)Z(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|\tilde{f}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

\displaystyle\|D_{(g)}p(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\|D_{(g)}\tilde{p}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\|\zeta(\cdot)\tilde{p}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\zeta{(\xi)}tZ(t,\xi)\leq\|\zeta{(\xi)}Z(\cdot,\xi)\|_{L^{1}(0,t)}=\|\mathscr{D}_{(g)}Z(\cdot,\xi)\|_{L^{1}(0,t)}

Z(t,\xi)\leq\frac{1}{1+\frac{t\zeta(\xi)}{\|g\|_{L^{1}(0,t)}}},t>0,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

It follows from (b) of Lemma
3.1

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\|p(t,\cdot)\|_{M_{(k)}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\|(1+\zeta(\cdot))\tilde{p}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle=\|(1+\zeta(\cdot))\tilde{f}(\cdot)\lim_{t\to\infty}Z(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=0.

Since the function
\hat{g}(s)
is a Stieltjes function, by Theorem 6.2 in
[
33
]

is a complete Bernstein function.
It follows from Theorem 7.5 in
[
33

By the conditions (C3) and (C4), we deduce

\lim_{s\to\infty}e^{-s\hat{g}(s)}=0,\lim_{s\to 0}se^{-s\hat{g}(s)}=0.

Thus, the function

is a Laplace transform of a completely monotone function.
Then

\Lambda^{-1}\{\hat{g}(s)e^{-s\hat{g}(s)}\}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}g(t-\tau)\Lambda^{-1}\{e^{-s\hat{g}(s)}\}(\tau)d\tau.

\phi(t,\tau)
by

\phi(t,\tau)=\Lambda^{-1}\{\hat{g}(s)e^{-\tau s\hat{g}(s)}\}(t),t,\tau>0.

Since the set of completely monotone functions is closed under multiplication, the function
\hat{g}(s)e^{-s\hat{g}(s)}
is also completely monotone.
Then it follows from Bernstein’s theorem that for
t,\tau>0,

\phi(t,\tau)>0.

Using the complex inversion formula, for
t>0

\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(t,\tau)d\tau=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{r-i\infty}^{r+i\infty}\hat{g}(s)e^{st-\tau s\hat{g}(s)}dsd\tau=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{r-i\infty}^{r+i\infty}\frac{e^{st}}{s}ds=1,

where

Meanwhile, we deduce

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-st}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(t,\tau)e^{-\zeta(\xi)\tau}d\tau dt=\hat{g}(s)\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\tau s\hat{g}(s)}e^{-\zeta(\xi)\tau}d\tau

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{g}(s)}{s\hat{g}(s)+\zeta(\xi)}=\hat{Z}(s,\xi),s>0,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

By the uniqueness of the Laplace transform,

has the form:

Since

\xi

\xi

Then, by Bochner’s theorem
[
37

on

The solution of the equation (
2.13

.

) with the conditions (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) if and only if it is a solution of the equation (
4.1
) with the conditions (
4.2
) and (
4.3
).

Proof.

Suppose that
p
is a solution of the equation (
4.4
) with the conditions (
4.2
) and (
4.3
). If
x\in B
and
|y|>2H
, then
|x+y|>H
and
|x-y|>H
.
Then, for
t>0
and
x\in B
, we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{(k^{*})}p(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big(p(t,x+y)+p(t,x-y)-2p(t,x)\big)k^{*}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\big(p(t,x+y)+p(t,x-y)-2p(t,x)\big)k^{*}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\big(p(t,x+y)+p(t,x-y)-2p(t,x)\big)k(y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big(p(t,x+y)+p(t,x-y)-2p(t,x)\big)k(y)dy=\mathscr{L}_{(k)}p(t,x),

which yields that
p
is also a solution of the equation (
4.1
) with the conditions (
4.2
) and (
4.3
).

Similarly, we can prove the other direction of the lemma.
∎

Lemma
4.1
shows that the condition (C7) is equivalent to the condition (C5) when solving the IBVP
(
4.1
)-(
4.3
).
From now on, we suppose that the kernel function
k
satisfies the condition (C5).

4.1
Friedrichs extension of general fractional Laplacian

In this subsection, based on the results in
[
20
]
, the Friedrichs extension of the general fractional Laplacian
\mathscr{L}_{(k)}
is discussed.

For convenience of notation, we denote
Q:=(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})\setminus(B^{C}\times B^{C})
.
Firstly, we recall concepts and properties of the functional spaces
X^{\beta}(B)
and
X_{0}^{\beta}(B)
, which are very crucial in our study.

Definition 4.1

(
[
20
]
)
.

The functional space
X^{\beta}(B)
is the linear space of Lebesgue measurable functions from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}
such that the restriction to
B
of any function
v\in X^{\beta}(B)
belongs to
L^{2}(B)
and the map

(x,y)\rightarrow(v(x)-v(y))\sqrt{k(x-y)}
is in
L^{2}(Q,dxdy)
.
The norm in
X^{\beta}(B)
is defined as follows:

\|v\|_{X^{\beta}(B)}=\|v\|_{L^{2}(B)}+\bigg(\int_{Q}|v(x)-v(y)|^{2}k(x-y)dxdy\bigg)^{1/2}.

Definition 4.2

(
[
20
]
)
.

The functional space
X_{0}^{\beta}(B)
is defined by

X_{0}^{\beta}(B)=\{u\in X^{\beta}(B):u=0\text{ a.e.in }B^{C}\}.

(4.5)

Lemma 4.2

(Lemma 1.29 in
[
20
]
)
.

X_{0}^{\beta}(B)
is a Hilbert space with the scalar product

\langle u,v\rangle_{X_{0}^{\beta}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))k(x-y)dxdy.

Lemma 4.3

(Theorem 2.6 in
[
20
]
)
.

The functional space
C_{0}^{\infty}(B)
is dense in
X_{0}^{\beta}(B)
.

Now we consider some properties of the operator
-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}
.

Lemma 4.4

(Theorem 1.26 in
[
20
]
)
.

The operator
-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}:C_{0}^{\infty}(B)\subset L^{2}(B)\rightarrow L^{2}(B)
is a linear symmetric operator on
L^{2}(B)
.
In particular, for
u,v\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B)
, the following equalities hold.

\displaystyle\langle-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}u,v\rangle_{L^{2}(B)}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\big(u(x+y)+u(x-y)-2u(x)\big)v(x)k(y)dxdy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y)k(x-y)dxdy=\langle u,v\rangle_{X_{0}^{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\big(v(x+y)+v(x-y)-2v(x)\big)u(x)k(y)dxdy=\langle u,-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}v\rangle_{L^{2}(B)}.

Lemma 4.5

(Lemma 1.28 in
[
20
]
)
.

There exists a constant
C>1
, depending only on
n,s,\theta
and
B
such that for any
u\in X_{0}^{\beta}(B)
,

\displaystyle\int_{Q}|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}k(x-y)dxdy\leq\|u\|^{2}_{X^{\beta}(B)}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{Q}|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}k(x-y)dxdy.

Therefore, the operator
-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}:C_{0}^{\infty}(B)\subset L^{2}(B)\rightarrow L^{2}(B)
is a strongly monotone operator on
L^{2}(B)
.

Theorem 4.1
.

The energetic space of the operator
-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}:C_{0}^{\infty}(B)\subset L^{2}(B)\rightarrow L^{2}(B)
is
X_{0}^{\beta}(B)
.

Proof.

It follows Lemma
4.4
and Lemma
4.5
that the operator
-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}:C_{0}^{\infty}(B)\subset L^{2}(B)\rightarrow L^{2}(B)
is a linear symmetric, strongly monotone operator on
L^{2}(B)
.
Thus, there exists an energetic extension
-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}^{E}
of the operator
-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}
.
By using Lemma
4.2
and Lemma
4.3
, we can easily prove the desired result.
∎

As usual, the Friedrichs extension
-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}^{F}
of the operator
-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}
is defined by

-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}^{F}u=-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}^{E}u\text{ for all }u\in D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}^{F}),

where
D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}^{F})=\{u\in X_{0}^{\beta}(B):-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}^{E}u\in L^{2}(B)\}
.
This means that
u\in D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}^{F})
iff
u\in X_{0}^{\beta}(B)
and there exists a function
g\in L^{2}(B)
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y)k(x-y)dxdy=\int_{B}g(x)v(x)dx\text{ for all }v\in X_{0}^{\beta}(B).

The Friedrichs extension
-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}^{F}
of the operator
-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}
is self-adjoint, bijective.

Lemma 4.6

(Lemma 1.31 in
[
20
]
)
.

The embedding
X_{0}^{\beta}(B)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(B)
is compact.

The eigenvalue problem of the Friedrichs extension
-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}^{F}
is equivalent to the problem that is to find a solution
u\in X_{0}^{\beta}(B),\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
of the following equation

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y)k(x-y)dxdy=\lambda\int_{B}u(x)v(x)dx\text{ for all }v\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B).

(4.6)

The following results for the eigensolutions of the Friedrichs extension
-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}^{F}
are standard in references of functional analysis (for example, see
[
42
]
).

(P1) The operator
-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}^{F}
has a countable system
\{\psi_{j},\lambda_{j}\}
of eigensolutions that contain all the eigensolutions of
-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}^{F}
.

(P2) The eigenvectors
\{\psi_{j}\}
form a complete orthonormal system in
L^{2}(B)
. In addition,
\psi_{j}\in X_{0}^{\beta}(B)
for all
j\in N
.

(P3) All the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}
have finite multiplicity. Furthermore,

0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots
and
\lambda_{j}\rightarrow+\infty
as
j\rightarrow\infty
.

(P4)

\displaystyle D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}^{F})=\bigg\{u\in L^{2}(B):\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{j}\langle\psi_{j},u\rangle_{L_{2}(B)}|^{2}<\infty\bigg\}.

\displaystyle\text{ For any }u\in D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}^{F}),-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}^{F}u=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}\langle\psi_{j},u\rangle_{L_{2}(B)}\psi_{j}.

(4.7)

In particular,
D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}^{F})
is a Hilbert space with the norm

\|u\|_{D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}^{F})}=\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{j}\langle\psi_{j},u\rangle_{L_{2}(B)}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}.

(4.8)

For convenience of notation, from now on,
-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}
will mean the Friedrichs extension
-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}^{F}
.

4.2
Existence and properties of solutions

We use the eigenfunction expansion method to solve the initial boundary value problem.
Let

p(t,x)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\omega_{j}(t)\psi_{j}(x)

(4.9)

be the solution of the general diffusion equation (
4.1
) with the conditions
(
4.2
) and (
4.3
).
By substituting (
4.9
) into (
4.1
), for any
j\in N
,
we have

\displaystyle D_{(g)}\omega_{j}(t)+\lambda_{j}\omega_{j}(t)=0,t>0,

\displaystyle\omega_{j}(0)=\langle\psi_{j},f\rangle_{L^{2}(B)},

which, by Lemma
2.1
, has a unique solution
\omega_{j}(t)
in
C[0,\infty)
.
In particular, the solution
\omega_{j}(t)
is a completely monotone function.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
f\in D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)})
. Then the general diffusion equation (
4.1
) with the conditions
(
4.2
) and (
4.3
) has a unique solution
p
in
C([0,\infty);D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}))
.
In particular, the following relations hold.

\displaystyle\|p(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(B)}\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}(B)},t\geq 0.

(4.10)

\displaystyle\|p(t,\cdot)\|_{D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}))}\leq\|f\|_{D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)})},t\geq 0.

(4.11)

\displaystyle\|D_{(g)}p(t,\cdot)\|_{D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)}))}\leq\|f\|_{D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)})},t>0.

(4.12)

\displaystyle\bigg\|\frac{\partial^{m}p(t,\cdot)}{\partial t^{m}}\bigg\|_{L^{2}(B)}\leq\bigg(\frac{m}{et}\bigg)^{m}\|f\|_{L^{2}(B)},m\in\mathbb{N},t>0.

(4.13)

\displaystyle\bigg\|\frac{\partial^{m}p(t,\cdot)}{\partial t^{m}}\bigg\|_{D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)})}\leq\bigg(\frac{m}{et}\bigg)^{m}\|f\|_{D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)})},m\in\mathbb{N},t>0.

(4.14)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\|p(t,\cdot)-f\|_{D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)})}=0.

(4.15)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\|p(t,\cdot)\|_{D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)})}=0.

(4.16)

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
, the function
|\omega_{j}(t)|
is completely monotone on
(0,\infty)
. Therefore
|\omega_{j}(t)|
is monotone decreasing on
[0,\infty)
.
For any
t\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\|p(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(B)}=\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\omega_{j}(t)|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\leq\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\langle\psi_{j},f\rangle_{L^{2}(B)}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}=\|f\|_{L^{2}(B)},

\displaystyle\|p(t,\cdot)\|_{D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)})}=\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{j}\omega_{j}(t)|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\leq\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{j}\langle\psi_{j},f\rangle_{L^{2}(B)}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}=\|f\|_{D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)})}.

For
t>0
, we estimate

\|D_{(g)}p(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(B)}=\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|D_{(g)}\omega_{j}(t)|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}=\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{j}\omega_{j}(t)|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\leq\|f\|_{D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)})}.

By (a) of Lemma
3.1
, for
m\in\mathbb{N},t>0,
we deduce

\displaystyle\bigg\|\frac{\partial^{m}p(t,\cdot)}{\partial t^{m}}\bigg\|_{L^{2}(B)}=\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\bigg|\frac{\partial^{m}\omega_{j}(t)}{\partial t^{m}}\bigg|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\leq\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\bigg(\frac{m}{et}\bigg)^{2m}|\omega_{j}(t)|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\frac{m}{et}\bigg)^{m}\|f\|_{L^{2}(B)}.

Similarly, we can easily prove the inequality (
4.14
).
In order to find the asymptotic behavior near
t=0
, for
t>0
, we have

\displaystyle\|p(t,\cdot)-f\|_{D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)})}=\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{j}(\omega_{j}(t)-\langle\psi_{j},f\rangle_{L^{2}(B)})|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{j}(2\langle\psi_{j},f\rangle_{L^{2}(B)})|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}=2\|f\|_{D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)})}.

Then by Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem, we estimate

\lim_{t\to 0}\|p(t,\cdot)-f\|_{D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)})}=\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lim_{t\to 0}|\lambda_{j}(\omega_{j}(t)-\langle\psi_{j},f\rangle_{L^{2}(B)})|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}=0.

By Young’s inequality, we deduce

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}t\omega_{j}(t)\leq\|\lambda_{j}\omega_{j}\|_{L^{1}(0,t)}=\|\mathscr{D}_{(g)}\omega_{j}\|_{L^{1}(0,t)}\leq\|g\|_{L^{1}(0,t)}\bigg\|\frac{d\omega_{j}(t)}{dt}\bigg\|_{L^{1}(0,t)}

\displaystyle=\|g\|_{L^{1}(0,t)}(1-\omega_{j}(t)).

Then

\omega_{j}(t)\leq\frac{1}{1+\frac{t\lambda_{j}}{\|g\|_{L^{1}(0,t)}}}.

It follows from (b) of Lemma
3.1
and Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\|p(t,\cdot)\|_{D(-\mathscr{L}_{(k)})}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{j}\omega_{j}(t)|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}=\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{j}\lim_{t\to\infty}\omega_{j}(t)|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}=0.

∎

5
Conclusion

In the paper, we used the CTRW theory to derive the nonlocal diffusion equation with the general Caputo derivative and the general Laplacian.
The general nonlocal diffusion equation can describe the Lévy process whose MSD is finite.

By employing the Fourier analysis technique, we investigate the exstence, nonnegativity and boundedness of solutions of the Cauchy problem for the general nonlocal diffusion equation. In particular, for the special cases of the general diffusion equation, the analytical solutions were expressed in terms of the Mittag-Leffler type functions.

In addition, the existence result for the IBVP of the nonlocal diffusion equation was obtained.

In the future work, we will consider the existence for a more general class of functions and the optimal estimation for the large time behaviour of solutions.
