Average CM-values of Higher Green’s Function and Factorization Thanks: The author is partially supported by the DFG grant BR-2163/4-2, an NSF postdoctoral fellowship and the LOEWE research unit USAG.
Yingkun Li

Abstract
. In this paper, we prove an averaged version of an algebraicity conjecture in [ 16 ] concerning the values of higher Green’s function at CM points. Furthermore, we give the factorization of the ideal generated by such algebraic value in the spirit of the famous work of Gross and Zagier on singular moduli.



1.
Introduction

Let
j(z)
be the modular
j
-invariant on the modular curve
X_{\Gamma}:=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
with
\Gamma:={\mathrm{SL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
\mathbb{H}
the upper-half complex plane.
Its value at a CM point
z\in\mathbb{H}
, i.e. an algebraic number in an imaginary quadratic field
K\subset\mathbb{C}
, is called a singular moduli.
The theory of complex multiplication tells us such a singular moduli is algebraic and generates an abelian extension of
K
. This is the extension of the well-known theorem of Kronecker-Weber from
\mathbb{Q}
to
K
, and part of Kronecker’s Jugendtraum.

On the other hand, the modular
j
-invariant also plays an important role in arithmetic intersection theory as the function
G_{1}(z_{1},z_{2}):=2\log|j(z_{1})-j(z_{2})|
is the automorphic Green’s function on
X_{\Gamma}^{2}
with logarithm singularity along the diagonal
T_{1}\subset X_{\Gamma}^{2}
. Values of
G_{1}
at a divisor
Z
on
X_{\Gamma}^{2}\backslash T_{1}
then give the archimedean part of the arithmetic intersection between
T_{1}
and
Z
. When
Z=Z_{\chi}
is the average of all pairs of CM points with discriminants
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
(see (
3.3.1
)), this arithmetic intersection is related to the Fourier coefficients of an incoherent Eisenstein series associated to
Z_{\chi}
.
This is the essential ingredient behind the analytic proof of the factorization of the norm of difference of singular moduli by Gross and Zagier in
[
17
]
.

The Green’s function
G_{1}
is a member of a family of
higher Green’s functions

G_{k}
on
X_{\Gamma}^{2}\backslash T_{1}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
(see (
4.2.3
)), which are eigenfunctions under the hyperbolic Laplacians of both
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
with the same eigenvalue
k(1-k)
.
They also play an important role in the calculation of arithmetic intersections by giving the archimedean height paring of CM-cycles in Kuga-Sato varieties
[
33
]
.
Even though complex multiplication does not apply to special values of higher Green’s functions, one still expects them to be of algebraic nature.
In
[
18
]
, Gross and Zagier made the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1
.

Let
G_{k,f}
be the higher Green’s function associated to a weakly holomorphic modular form
f\in M^{!}_{2-2k}
for
k\geq 2
(see (
4.2.4
)).
Suppose
f
has rational Fourier coefficients.
For a CM point
Z=(z_{1},z_{2})\in X_{\Gamma}^{2}
not on the singularity
T_{f}
of
G_{k,f}
(see (
4.2.5
)), there exists
\alpha=\alpha_{\chi,f}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\subset\mathbb{C}
such that

G_{k,f}(Z)=(d_{1}d_{2})^{\tfrac{1-k}{2}}\log|\alpha|,

where
d_{j}
is the discriminant of
z_{j}
.

The first instance of this conjecture was proved by Gross, Kohnen and Zagier
[
16
]
, where they consider the value of
G_{k,f}
at
Z_{\chi}
for
k

odd
and
\sqrt{\Delta}\not\in\mathbb{Q}
with
\Delta:=d_{1}d_{2}
. In this case,
\alpha
can be taken to be a positive rational number, and has an explicit factorization generalizing
[
17
]
.
To state this result, let
F
be the real quadratic field
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{\Delta})\subset\mathbb{R}
with ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{F}
. Denote
\chi
the genus character corresponding to the CM extension
K:=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d_{1}},\sqrt{d_{2}})
over
F
.
For each integral ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathcal{O}_{F}
, we can attach an integral ideal
I^{+}_{\chi}(\mathfrak{a})\subset\mathcal{O}_{F}
given by

(1.0.1)

I^{+}_{\chi}(\mathfrak{a}):=\prod_{\mathfrak{b}\mid\mathfrak{a}}(\mathfrak{b}\cdot\mathfrak{b}^{\prime})^{\chi(\mathfrak{b})},

where
′
denotes the real conjugation in
{\mathrm{Gal}}(F/\mathbb{Q})
. Notice that
I^{+}_{\chi}(\mathfrak{a})
is
{\mathrm{Gal}}(F/\mathbb{Q})
-invariant, hence principal, and has a unique positive integer as its generator. If
\mathfrak{a}=(\lambda)
is principal, we simply write
I^{+}_{\chi}(\lambda)
for
I^{+}_{\chi}(\mathfrak{a})
.

For each positive integer
m
, we have a finite set
\mathrm{S}_{m}
of elements in
\mathcal{O}_{F}
defined by

(1.0.2)

\mathrm{S}_{m}:=\{\lambda\in\mathcal{O}_{F}:\lambda\sqrt{\Delta}\gg 0,~\lambda\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}+\tfrac{m}{2}\sqrt{\Delta}\}.

For each
\lambda\in\mathcal{O}_{F}
and integers
m\in\mathbb{Z},k\geq 1
, denote

(1.0.3)

C_{k}(\lambda,m):=-(-m\sqrt{\Delta})^{k-1}P_{k-1}\left(\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\lambda)}{m\sqrt{\Delta}}\right)\in\mathbb{Z},

where
P_{k-1}(t)
is the
(k-1)
th
Legendre polynomial (see (
A.2.1
)).
The result of Gross-Zagier and Gross-Kohnen-Zagier can be phrased as follows.

Theorem A

(Theorem 1.3 in
[
17
]
and Theorem 2 in Section V of
[
16
]
)
.

Suppose
d_{1},d_{2}<0
are co-prime, fundamental discriminants. For odd
k\geq 1
and
f\in M^{!}_{2-2k}
with integral Fourier coefficients
c_{f}(n)
, let
\alpha^{+}=\alpha^{+}_{\chi,f}\in\mathbb{Q}
be the unique positive generator of the ideal

\prod_{m\geq 1}\prod_{\lambda\in\mathrm{S}_{m}}I^{+}_{\chi}(\lambda)^{c_{f}(-m)C_{k}(\lambda,m)}.

Then
G_{k,f}(Z_{\chi})=\Delta^{(1-k)/2}\log(\alpha^{+})
.

Remark 1.1
.

When
k=1
, the result above is the factorization of norm of difference of singular moduli by Gross and Zagier.

Since the work of Gross-Kohnen-Zagier, there has been a lot of progress proving conjecture
1
.
When the discriminants
\Delta=d_{1}d_{2}
is a perfect square, this was proved by Zhang
[
33
]
conditional on the positivity of the height parings of Heegner cycles on Kuga-Sato varieties.
Later in
[
30
]
, Viazovska gave an unconditional proof using the machinery of theta liftings.
When
\Delta
is not a perfect square, i.e.
F
is a real quadratic field, Mellit proved the conjecture for
k=2
,
z_{1}=i
,
z_{2}
arbitrary in his thesis
[
27
]
.
In a recent work
[
5
]
, the conjecture is proved when one averages over all CM points
(z_{1},z_{2})
with
z_{1}
having discriminant
d_{1}
.
Most recently, conjecture
1
is proved in
[
26
]
for any CM point
Z=(z_{1},z_{2})
and
odd

k\geq 3
.
In these cases, the algebraic number
\alpha
is in an abelian extension of an imaginary quadratic field. In general, one expects
\alpha
to be in an abelian extension of the CM field
K
.

With the (sometimes conjectural) knowledge of the algebraicity of
\alpha
, it is very natural to ask for the factorization of the ideal generated by it.
For
even

k\geq 2
, this is not known even when one takes the average
Z=Z_{\chi}
.
The goal of this work is to provide a result in this setting, which complements Theorem
A
dealing with the cases that
k
is odd.
The subtlety created by the parity of
k
is made clear in the following example by Mellit
[
27
]

G_{2}\left(i,\frac{-1+\sqrt{-7}}{2}\right)=\frac{8}{\sqrt{4\cdot 7}}\log\frac{8-3\sqrt{7}}{8+3\sqrt{7}},

where
d_{1}=-4,d_{2}=-7
.
The algebraic number
\frac{8-3\sqrt{7}}{8+3\sqrt{7}}
is not rational. Furthermore, it is a unit and its norm to
\mathbb{Q}
is 1.
It turns out that the right modification for even
k
is to replace the integral ideal
I^{+}_{\chi}(\mathfrak{a})
with the fractional ideal

(1.0.4)

I^{-}_{\chi}(\mathfrak{a}):=\prod_{\mathfrak{b}\mid\mathfrak{a}}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{b}}{\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}}\right)^{\chi(\mathfrak{b})}

for every integral ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathcal{O}_{F}
.
Since
I^{-}_{\chi}(\mathfrak{a})^{\prime}=I^{-}_{\chi}(\mathfrak{a})^{-1}
, the ideal
I^{-}_{\chi}(\mathfrak{a})
is not necessarily principal.
One can overcome this by raising it to a suitable power (for example the class number of
F
).
With this minor difference, we can now state the analog of Theorem
A
for even
k
as follows.

Theorem B

(Theorem
4.4
)
.

Suppose
d_{1},d_{2}<0
are co-prime, fundamental discriminants.
For even
k\geq 2
and
f\in M^{!}_{2-2k}
with integral Fourier coefficients
c_{f}(n)
, there exists
\kappa=\kappa_{f,F}\in\mathbb{N}
and a positive generator
\alpha^{-}=\alpha^{-}_{\chi,f,\kappa}\in F
of the fractional ideal

\prod_{m\geq 1}\prod_{\lambda\in\mathrm{S}_{m}}I^{-}_{\chi}(\lambda)^{c_{f}(-m)C_{k}(\lambda,m)\kappa}

such that
\kappa\cdot G_{k,f}(Z_{\chi})=\Delta^{(1-k)/2}\log(\alpha^{-})
.

Remark 1.2
.

Even though the result is an existence statement, the algebraic number
\alpha^{-}
is
explicitly
constructed.
This theorem then also gives a constructive proof of Conjecture
1
when
Z_{\chi}
consists of just one point.

To give a numerical example, we take
k=4
and
f(\tau)=q^{-1}+O(1)\in M_{-6}
. Such
f
exists (and is unique) since there is no non-trivial cusp form in
S_{8}
. If we take the discriminants
-7
and
-23
, then the CM points are
(z_{7},z_{23,0}),(z_{7},z_{23,1})
and
(z_{7},z_{23,-1})
, where
z_{d}=z_{d,0}:=\frac{1+\sqrt{-d}}{2}
and
z_{23,\pm 1}=\frac{\pm 1+\sqrt{-23}}{4}
.
Theorem
B
above gives us
\alpha^{-}\in F
and
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\sum_{j=-1,0,1}G_{4,f}(z_{7},z_{23,j})=\frac{1}{{161}^{3/2}\kappa}\log(\alpha^{-}).

The factorization tells us that
\alpha^{-}
generates the fractional ideal

\left(\left(\frac{\mathfrak{p}_{5}}{\mathfrak{p}_{5}^{\prime}}\right)^{-2878}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{p}_{17}}{\mathfrak{p}_{17}^{\prime}}\right)^{-3580}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{p}_{19}}{\mathfrak{p}_{19}^{\prime}}\right)^{2628}\right)^{\kappa},

where
\mathfrak{p}_{\ell}=(\pi_{\ell})
are prime ideals in
F=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{161})
with

\pi_{5}=38+3\sqrt{161},\pi_{17}=12+\sqrt{161},\pi_{19}=25+2\sqrt{161}.

Numerically, we have computed the left hand side to be
-4.157888612785..
and bounded
\kappa
explicitly.
This helps us to see that we can take
\kappa=1
and

\alpha^{-}=\left(\frac{\pi_{5}}{\pi_{5}^{\prime}}\right)^{-2878}\left(\frac{\pi_{17}}{\pi_{17}^{\prime}}\right)^{-3580}\left(\frac{\pi_{19}}{\pi_{19}^{\prime}}\right)^{2628}\varepsilon_{F}^{1168},

where
\varepsilon_{F}=11775+928\sqrt{161}
is the fundamental unit of
F
.

In
[
16
]
, the authors proved Theorem
A
by expressing the special value of higher Green’s function at CM points as linear combinations of the holomorphic part Fourier coefficients of certain real-analytic Eisenstein series, which are
incoherent
in the sense of Kudla
[
21
]
. These Fourier coefficients can be computed exactly and are rational multiples of logarithms of integers.

Comparing the theorems above, it is then clear that one does not expect the value
G_{k,f}(Z_{\chi})
to be directly related to these Fourier coefficients when
k
is even. In fact, the method in
[
16
]
applies to all
k\geq 1
with
G_{k,f}
replaced by a function which is odd under the Atkin-Lehner involution when
k
is even (see page 502 in
[
16
]
). For level one, this returns the trivial equation.

To prove Theorem
B
, we take this analytic approach and replace the incoherent Eisenstein series with the
\mathrm{O}(2,2)
theta lift of a weight one real-analytic modular form on
{\mathrm{SL}}_{2}
over
\mathbb{Q}
.
In the context of the Siegel-Weil formula
[
24
,
25
]
, the incoherent Hilbert Eisenstein series used in
[
17
]
and
[
12
]
lies on
{\mathrm{SL}}_{2}
over the real quadratic field
F
and is the integral of a theta function over the orthogonal group, i.e. sum of big CM points.
Even though our lift goes in the opposite direction, the exceptional isomorphism between
\mathrm{O}(2,2)
over
\mathbb{Q}
and
{\mathrm{SL}}_{2}
over
F
makes it a viable idea.
The key step then is to find the right input and theta kernel.

The input is a weight one modular form, which was studied in
[
14
]
and used to construct weight one harmonic Maass forms associated to real quadratic fields.
The form is constructed by deforming a theta integral from
\mathrm{O}(1,1)
to
{\mathrm{SL}}_{2}
, and has no singularity. Therefore, it can be fed into the theta lifting machinery without regularization and the output will have no singularity either. Unlike the incoherent Eisenstein series in
[
16
]
, our replacement will not behave nicely under the Laplacian. Fortunately, it still has a holomorphic part, which can be explicitly calculated.
This, along with an error term arising from the non-holomorphic part, equals to
G_{k,f}(Z_{\chi})
(see Theorem
4.3
). One then needs to carefully analyze the error term to see that it contributes a rational multiple of
\log\varepsilon_{F}
with
\varepsilon_{F}
the fundamental unit of
F
. This is the reason we need
\kappa
in Theorem
B
.

The theta kernel we used are also quite interesting.
For each
k\in 2\mathbb{N}
, we use a different Schwartz function formed from the Gaussian with a suitable polynomial
\tilde{p}_{k-1}
in the sense of
[
3
]
(see section
5.3
). They are easy to describe in the polynomial Fock space, and particular cases are related to the Schwartz forms studied by Kudla and Millson
[
23
]
.
To evaluate such a theta lift, we require a formula by Borcherds
[
3
, Theorem 7.1]
with polynomials in both the plus and minus parts.
In addition, we also need the recent work
[
1
]
on the Fourier expansion of Millson theta lifts.

It would be interesting to see how our formula fits into the context of arithmetic intersection theory, i.e. Gross-Zagier formula and various generalizations
[
33
,
31
,
13
,
8
,
7
,
2
]
.
In the Gross-Zagier type formulas, one has an equality between a special value of the derivative of a suitable
L
-function and an appropriate arithmetic intersection number, which has contributions from the archimedean and non-archimedean parts. The former are special values of Green’s functions and the latter are local intersection numbers.
After explicit calculations, one can match the non-archimedean part with Fourier coefficients of incoherent Eisenstein series.
When the
L
-function is identically zero, e.g. attached to the trivial cusp form, then one obtains an equality (up to sign) between the archimedean and non-archimedean contributions.
This, for example, gives the factorization of singular moduli. Our result suggests that there should be a similar interpretation of Fourier coefficients of our replacement in this setting, with which one can then prove a Gross-Zagier type formula by replacing
f
with a harmonic Maass form.
It would then be interesting to generalize our proof to the cases when
f
has higher level.

We organize the paper into three parts.
The first part consists of sections
2
and
3
, where we discuss some preliminary notions about theta lifts, real quadratic fields and the deformed theta integral studied in
[
14
]
. The main results are the two counting propositions
3.11
and
3.13
.
This can be considered as an analysis of the information contained in the non-archimedean component of the theta lift.
The archimedean component is contained in the last part of the paper. It consists of the last section and the appendix, where we give the relevant theta functions and study in details various theta lifts involved.
Between these two technical parts is section
4
, which is the middle part of the paper. It consists of information about higher Green’s function, the main result (Theorem
4.4
), and its proof. Assuming the first and last parts, the reader can read through the middle part to get the rough idea of the proof.

2.
Preliminary Facts

2.1.
Weil Representation and Modular Forms

We first follow the convention of
[
3
]
to recall the Weil representation arising from finite quadratic modules.
Let
{{\mathrm{Mp}}}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
be the metaplectic two-fold cover of
{\mathrm{SL}}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
consisting of elements
(A,\phi(\tau))
with
A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in{\mathrm{SL}}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
\phi:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
holomorphic function satisfying
\phi(\tau)^{2}=c\tau+d
.
Let
{\mathrm{Mp}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\subset{{\mathrm{Mp}}}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
be the inverse image of
{\mathrm{SL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\subset{\mathrm{SL}}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
It is generated by
T:=(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right),1)
and
S=(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right),\sqrt{\tau})
, where
\sqrt{\cdot}
denotes the principal branch of the holomorphic square root.

Let
\Gamma\subset{\mathrm{Mp}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
be a subgroup of finite index and
\rho:\Gamma\to{\mathrm{GL}}(W)
a representation on a finite dimensional
\mathbb{C}
-vector space
W
.
A real-analytic function
f:\mathbb{H}\to W
is called
modular
with respect to
\rho
and weight
k\in{\tfrac{1}{2}}\mathbb{Z}
if

(2.1.1)

(f\mid_{k}(A,\phi))(\tau):=\phi(\tau)^{-2k}f(A\cdot z)=\rho((A,\phi))\cdot f(\tau)

for all
(A,\phi)\in\Gamma
and
\tau\in\mathbb{H}
.
We denote the space of all such functions and the usual subspace of weakly holomorphic, holomorphic and cuspidal forms by
{\mathcal{A}}_{k,\rho}(\Gamma),M^{!}_{k,\rho}(\Gamma),M_{k,\rho}(\Gamma),S_{k,\rho}(\Gamma)
respectively. We sometimes omit
\rho
, resp.
\Gamma
, if it is trivial, resp.
{\mathrm{Mp}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.
Given
f\in{\mathcal{A}}_{k,\rho}(\Gamma)
, we denote

(2.1.2)

f^{c}(\tau):=v^{k}\overline{f(\tau)}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{-k,\overline{\rho}}(\Gamma)

for convenience.

The representations that we are mainly interested in come from arithmetic.
Let
L
be an even, integral lattice with quadratic form
Q
of signature
(n^{+},n^{-})
, the dual lattice
L^{\vee}
and the associated finite quadratic module

(2.1.3)

A_{L}:=L^{\vee}/L,

on which
Q
becomes a quadratic form valued in
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
.
Let
\mathrm{B}(\cdot,\cdot)
be the associated bilinear form.
The vector space
\mathbb{C}[A_{L}]:=\bigoplus_{h\in A_{L}}\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{e}_{h}
is naturally an
{\mathrm{Mp}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-module via the Weil representation
\rho_{L}
defined by

(2.1.4)

\begin{split}\rho_{L}(T)(\mathfrak{e}_{h})&:={\mathbf{e}}(Q(h))\mathfrak{e}_{h},\\
\rho_{L}(S)(\mathfrak{e}_{h})&:=\frac{{\mathbf{e}}(-(n^{+}-n^{-})/8)}{\sqrt{|A_{L}|}}\sum_{\mu\in A_{L}}{\mathbf{e}}(-\mathrm{B}(h,\mu))\mathfrak{e}_{\mu}.\end{split}

Despite the notation,
\rho_{L}
only depends on the finite quadratic module
A_{L}
.
There is a natural hermitian pairing
\langle,\rangle
on
\mathbb{C}[A_{L}]
given by
\langle\mathfrak{e}_{h_{1}},\mathfrak{e}_{h_{2}}\rangle:=1
if
h_{1}=h_{2}
and zero otherwise. With respect to this pairing
\rho_{L}
is a unitary representation.
When
n^{+}+n^{-}
is even,
\rho_{L}
factors through
\Gamma:={\mathrm{SL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.
To understand
A_{L}
, it is sometimes useful to consider the
p
-component

(2.1.5)

A_{L,p}:=A_{L}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}_{p}

with
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
the
p
-adic integers for a prime
p\in\mathbb{N}
.
It is a finite quadratic module with value in
\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1}{p}]/\mathbb{Z}
.
Through the natural map
A_{L}\to A_{L,p}
given by
h\mapsto h\otimes 1
, we have

(2.1.6)

A_{L}=\bigoplus_{p\text{ prime}}A_{L,p}

as finite quadratic modules.

Suppose
M\subset L
is a sublattice of finite index and
s:L^{\vee}/M\to A_{L}
the natural surjection. Then
L^{\vee}\subset M^{\vee}
and there is a linear map
\psi:\mathbb{C}[A_{M}]\to\mathbb{C}[A_{L}]
defined by

(2.1.7)

\psi(\mathfrak{e}_{h}):=\begin{cases}\mathfrak{e}_{s(h)},&h\in L^{\vee}/M,\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The adjoint of
\psi
with respect to the hermitian pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is

(2.1.8)

\begin{split}\phi:\mathbb{C}[A_{L}]&\to\mathbb{C}[A_{M}]\\
\mathfrak{e}_{h}&\mapsto\sum_{\mu\in L^{\vee}/M,s(\mu)=h}\mathfrak{e}_{\mu}.\end{split}

It is straightforward to check that
\psi
and
\phi
are
\Gamma
-linear with respect to the Weil representations above and
\psi\circ\phi
is
[L:M]
times the identity map on
\mathbb{C}[A_{L}]
.

2.2.
Symmetric Space and Theta Function

We will now introduce the two-variable theta function associated to a suitable Schwartz function (see (
2.2.4
)). This can be done either adelically as in
[
24
]
or classically as in
[
3
]
, and the connection between the two are spelled out clearly in section 1 of
[
22
]
. We take an approach closer to that of Borcherds, while keeping the adelic perspective visible in the setup as well.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the Euclidean space with the metric

|\underline{x}|^{2}:=\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}x_{j}^{2}

for
\underline{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
With respect to the quadratic form
Q_{n}(\underline{x}):=\frac{1}{2}|\underline{x}|^{2}
, the space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
becomes a real quadratic space.
Let
\Delta_{\mathbb{R}}:=\sum_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}^{2}
be the Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and define for
\alpha>0
the following operator

(2.2.1)

{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha}(f):=e^{-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}}/(8\pi\alpha)}f.

on a smooth function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
.
In particular, we consider
f=p
a polynomial homogeneous of degree
m
.
It is easy to check that

(2.2.2)

{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(p)(\alpha\cdot\underline{x})=\alpha^{m}{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha^{2}}(p)(\underline{x}).

for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\underline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Using such a polynomial, we define the following Schwartz function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

(2.2.3)

\varphi(\underline{x};p):={\mathcal{H}}_{1}(p)(\underline{x})e^{-{\pi}|\underline{x}|^{2}},

which is an eigenfunction of eigenvalue
i^{n}
with respect to Fourier transform on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Suppose
n^{+},n^{-}\in\mathbb{N}
with
n=n^{+}+n^{-}
.
Let
\mathbb{R}^{n^{+},n^{-}}:=\mathbb{R}^{n^{+}}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{n^{-}}
be the real quadratic space with the quadratic form
Q_{0}:=Q_{n^{+}}-Q_{n^{-}}
.
Suppose
p
is a polynomial on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
homogeneous of degrees
m^{+}
and
m^{-}
on
\underline{x}^{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{+}}
and
\underline{x}^{-}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{-}}
respectively.
Then we can form the Schwartz function

(2.2.4)

\varphi(\underline{x};p):={\mathcal{H}}_{1}(p)(\underline{x})e^{-2\pi Q_{0}(\underline{x})}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n^{+},n^{-}})

for
\underline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{+},n^{-}}
, which is also an eigenfunction under the Fourier transform on
\mathbb{R}^{n^{+},n^{-}}
. Furthermore, it is in the subspace of
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n^{+},n^{-}})
called the polynomial Fock space
[
23
]
.

For a real quadratic space
(V,Q)
with signature
(n^{+},n^{-})
, denote
{\mathcal{D}}_{V}
the oriented Grassmannian of
V
, i.e. the set of oriented, positive definite
n^{+}
-dimensional subspaces of
V
.
Then every
w\in{\mathcal{D}}_{V}
induces an isometry

(2.2.5)

\begin{split}\nu_{w}:V_{\mathbb{R}}&\to\mathbb{R}^{n^{+},n^{-}}\end{split}

by projecting
\lambda\in V
to
w
and its orthogonal complement
w^{\perp}\subset V
. We denote these projections by
\lambda_{w}
and
\lambda_{w^{\perp}}
.
For any function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n^{+},n^{-}}\to\mathbb{C}
, we use
f_{w}:V_{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{C}
to denote the composition
f\circ\nu_{w}
. The quadratic form
Q_{w}:=Q_{0}\circ\nu_{w}
is half the majorant
(\cdot,\cdot)_{w}
defined by

(2.2.6)

(\lambda,\lambda)_{w}:=(\lambda,\lambda)-2(\lambda_{w^{\perp}},\lambda_{w^{\perp}})

for
\lambda\in V
.
Given
\tau=u+iv\in\mathbb{H}
, we can now define a Schwartz function

(2.2.7)

\begin{split}\varphi(\lambda;\tau,w;p)&:={v}^{n/4-k/2}\varphi_{w}(\sqrt{v}\lambda;p){\mathbf{e}}(Q(\lambda)u)\\
&=v^{n^{-}/2+m^{-}}e^{-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}}/(8\pi v)}(p)(\nu_{w}(\lambda)){\mathbf{e}}\left(Q(\lambda_{w})\tau+Q(\lambda_{w^{\perp}})\overline{\tau}\right)\end{split}

for
\lambda\in V
with
k:=\frac{n^{+}-n^{-}}{2}+m^{+}-m^{-}
.

Let
L\subset V
be an even, integral lattice of rank
n
with dual lattice
L^{\vee}
and finite quadratic module
A_{L}:=L^{\vee}/L
. We can define the theta function
\Theta_{L}(\tau,w,p)
valued in
\mathbb{C}[A_{L}]
by

(2.2.8)

\Theta_{L}(\tau,w;p)=\sum_{h\in A_{L}}\mathfrak{e}_{h}\sum_{\lambda\in L+h}\varphi(\lambda;\tau,w;p),

and denote the
\mathfrak{e}_{h}
component of
\Theta_{L}
by
\Theta_{L,h}
.
We omit
p
in the notation if it is the constant 1.
Let
\Gamma_{L}
be the kernel of the surjection
{\mathrm{SO}}(L)\to{\mathrm{Aut}}(A_{L})
.
Then
\Theta_{L}(\tau,w;p)
is clearly
\Gamma_{L}
invariant in
w\in{\mathcal{D}}_{V}
.
By Poisson summation, one can show that
\Theta_{L}(\tau,w;p)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{k,\rho_{L}}
(see
[
3
, Theorem 4.1]
).
For
m\in\mathbb{Q}
and
h\in A_{L}
, denote as usual

(2.2.9)

L_{m,h}:=\{\lambda\in L+h:Q(\lambda)=m\},~L(m,h):=\Gamma_{L}\backslash L_{m,h}.

Then
\Theta_{L,h}
has the Fourier expansion

\Theta_{L,h}(\tau,w;p)=v^{n/4-k/2}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Q}}{\mathbf{e}}(mu)\sum_{\lambda\in L_{m,h}}\varphi_{w}(\sqrt{v}\lambda;p)

For a finite index sublattice
M\subset L
, it is easy to check that

(2.2.10)

\psi(\Theta_{M}(\tau,w;p))=\Theta_{L}(\tau,w;p),

where
\psi
is the map in (
2.1.7
).

Example 2.1
.

Suppose
V=\mathbb{R}^{1,0}
and
p(x)=p_{b}(x)=x^{b}
for
b\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the polynomial in the theta function above can be expressed in terms of the Hermite polynomials.
In particular, we have

(2.2.11)

{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(p_{b})(x)e^{-\pi x^{2}}=(-4\pi)^{-b}(\partial_{x}-2\pi x)^{b}e^{-\pi x^{2}}.

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the lattice
\mathbb{Z}
is even integral with respect to the quadratic form
Q_{N}(x):=Nx^{2}
, and its dual lattice is given by
\frac{1}{2N}\mathbb{Z}
.
We denote this lattice and the associated Weil representation by
P_{N}
and
\rho_{N}
respectively.
Since
P_{N}
is definite, the Grassmannian consists of one point
o
, which induces

\displaystyle\nu_{o}:P_{N}\otimes\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}^{1,0}

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle\mapsto\sqrt{2N}r.

Using this lattice, we can form the theta series

(2.2.12)

\theta_{N}(\tau;b):=\Theta_{P_{N}}(\tau,o;p_{b})=\sum_{h\in\frac{1}{2N}\mathbb{Z}/\mathbb{Z}}\mathfrak{e}_{h}\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}+h}{\mathcal{H}}_{v}(p_{b})(\sqrt{2N}r){\mathbf{e}}(Nr^{2}\tau)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{b+1/2,\rho_{N}}.

For
b=0,1
,
\theta_{N}(\tau;b)
is holomorphic in
M_{1/2,\rho_{N}}
and
S_{3/2,\rho_{N}}
respectively. If
P_{N}^{-}
denotes the lattice
(\mathbb{Z},-Q_{N})
, then
\Theta_{P^{-}_{N}}(\tau,o;p_{b})=\theta^{c}_{N}({\tau};b)
.

Example 2.2
.

Let
(V,Q)=(M_{2}(\mathbb{R}),\det)
be the real quadratic space of signature
(2,2)
with
M_{2}(\mathbb{R})
the vector space of 2 by 2 matrices with entries in
\mathbb{R}
. The Grassmannian
{\mathcal{D}}_{V}
is identified with
\mathbb{H}^{2}
via

(2.2.13)

\underline{z}=(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{H}^{2}\mapsto w(\underline{z}):=\mathbb{R}\Re Z(\underline{z})\oplus\mathbb{R}\Im Z(\underline{z})\in{\mathcal{D}}_{V},

where we denote

(2.2.14)

Z(\underline{z})=X(\underline{z})+iY(\underline{z}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2y_{1}y_{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}z_{1}\overline{z_{2}}&\overline{z_{2}}\\
z_{1}&1\end{pmatrix},\;Z^{\perp}(\underline{z})=X^{\perp}(\underline{z})+iY^{\perp}(\underline{z}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2y_{1}y_{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}z_{1}z_{2}&z_{2}\\
z_{1}&1\end{pmatrix}.

It will also be important later for us to consider the diagonally embedded
\mathbb{H}
in
\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}
. In that case, we use
z\in\mathbb{H}
to denote its image
(z,z)\in\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}
and also the point
w((z,z))\in{\mathcal{D}}_{V}
.
The vector
Y:=Y(z)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
is independent of
z
and the isometry
\nu_{z}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{2,2}
is then given by

(2.2.15)

\nu_{z}(\Lambda):=(\Lambda_{z},\mathrm{B}(\Lambda,Y),\Re(\Lambda_{z^{\perp}}),\Im(\Lambda_{z^{\perp}}))\in\mathbb{R}^{2,2},

where we denote

(2.2.16)

\Lambda_{z}:=\mathrm{B}(\Lambda,X(z)),\;\Lambda_{z^{\perp}}:=\mathrm{B}(\Lambda,Z^{\perp}(z)).

The lattice
L=M_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\subset V
is even and unimodular. The theta function
\Theta_{L}(\tau,w(\underline{z}))
has the explicit form

(2.2.17)

\Theta_{L}(\tau,w(\underline{z}))=v\sum_{a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbf{e}}\left(\frac{|az_{1}z_{2}+bz_{1}+cz_{2}+d|^{2}\tau-|az_{1}{\overline{z}}_{2}+bz_{1}+c{\overline{z}}_{2}+d|^{2}\overline{\tau}}{4y_{1}y_{2}}\right).

From the result above, we know that
\Theta_{L}(\tau,w(\underline{z}))\in{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
as a function of
\tau\in\mathbb{H}
for any
\underline{z}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
.

Suppose
f(\tau)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{k,\rho_{L}}
is regular on
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
and has sufficient decay near the cusp, then one can integrate
\langle f(\tau),\Theta_{L}(\tau,w;p)\rangle v^{k}d\mu(\tau)
on
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
to obtain a function on
{\mathcal{D}}_{V}
.
If
f
is allowed to have linear exponential growths at the cusps and be nearly holomorphic, i.e.
L^{n}_{\tau}f=0
for
n\gg 0
, then Borcherds defined regularized theta integral

(2.2.18)

\begin{split}\Phi_{L}(w;p,f)&=\int^{\mathrm{reg}}_{\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}}\langle f(\tau),\Theta_{L}(\tau,w;p)\rangle v^{k}d\mu(\tau)\\
&:=\mathrm{Const}_{s=0}\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{{\mathcal{F}}_{T}}\langle f(\tau),\Theta_{L}(\tau,w;p)\rangle v^{k-s}d\mu(\tau)\end{split}

following the regularization idea of Harvey and Moore
[
19
]
.
This is defined by integrating first over the truncated fundamental domain
{\mathcal{F}}_{T}:=\{\tau=u+iv\in\mathbb{H}:|\tau|>1,|u|<1/2,v<T\}
, before taking the limit
T\to\infty
for
\Re(s)\gg 0
, analytically continuing this to
s\in\mathbb{C}
and taking the constant term in the Laurent expansion in
s\in\mathbb{C}
.
The same integral can be defined for harmonic Maass forms as well
[
4
,
6
]
.

On the other hand, one could also integrate orthogonal modular forms in the variable
w
to produce symplectic modular forms on
\mathbb{H}
. In
[
20
]
, Hecke computed one of the first such theta integral and associated weight one cusp forms to real quadratic fields.

This is a special case of the Siegel-Weil formula
[
24
,
25
]
.
In the next section, we will review Hecke’s construction along with its deformation.

2.3.
Real Quadratic Field

From now on, let
F\subset\mathbb{R}
be a real quadratic field with fundamental discriminant
\Delta>1
.
Its ring of integer and inverse different are given by
\mathcal{O}_{F}:=\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\omega_{\Delta},\omega_{\Delta}:=\frac{\Delta+\sqrt{\Delta}}{2}
and
\mathfrak{d}^{-1}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\mathcal{O}_{F}
respectively.
We also denote
\Delta_{0}
the square-free part of
\Delta
, i.e.
\Delta=\Delta_{0}\cdot c^{2}
for some
c\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
\Delta
is fundamental, it is in one of the following forms

•

\Delta\equiv 1\bmod{4}
and
\Delta=\Delta_{0}
,

•

\Delta\equiv 0\bmod{4}
and
\Delta/4=\Delta_{0}\equiv 2,3\bmod{4}
.

We denote the (narrow) class group of
F
by
{\mathrm{Cl}}^{(+)}(F)
, which is the quotient of

(2.3.1)

I_{\mathfrak{d}}:=\{\mathcal{O}_{F}\text{-fractional ideal }\mathfrak{a}\subset F:\mathfrak{a}\text{ and }\mathfrak{d}\text{ are relatively prime to each other.}\}

by its subgroup of principal ideals (with totally positive generators).
By the Chebotarev density theorem, every class in
{\mathrm{Cl}}^{+}(F)
has infinitely many prime representatives in
I_{\mathfrak{d}}
.
For each prime
p\mid\Delta
, let
\mathfrak{d}_{p}
denote the unique (ramified) prime ideal in
\mathcal{O}_{F}
above
p
. They are the only prime ideals dividing
\mathfrak{d}
.
We use
′
to denote the non-trivial Galois conjugation, with which we can define the norm map

{\mathrm{Nm}}(\alpha):=\alpha\alpha^{\prime}

on
F
. Denote

(2.3.2)

{\mathrm{Nm}}(F)_{\pm}:=\{m\in\mathbb{Q}:\pm m>0\text{ and there exists }\alpha\in F\text{ with }{\mathrm{Nm}}(\alpha)=m\}.

If
F
does not have an element of norm
-1
, then
{\mathrm{Nm}}(F)_{+}
and
{\mathrm{Nm}}(F)_{-}
are disjoint. This happens when
\Delta
is divisible by a prime congruent to
3
modulo
4
.

The group of units
\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times}
is generated by the fundamental unit
\varepsilon_{F}>1
.
If
\Delta
is divisible by a prime congruent to 3 modulo 4, then
\varepsilon_{F}
is totally positive, and the canonical map
{\mathrm{Cl}}^{+}(F)\to{\mathrm{Cl}}(F)
is a 2-1 surjection.
Besides the usual norm map, we will also need the map on
\mathcal{O}_{F}
-fractional ideals

(2.3.3)

{\mathrm{Nm}}^{-}(\mathfrak{a}):=\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}},

whose image is exactly the kernel of
{\mathrm{Nm}}
.

Suppose
Q={\mathrm{Nm}}
is the quadratic form. Then
\mathcal{O}_{F}
is an even integral lattice of signature
(1,1)
, whose discriminant kernel is denoted by
\Gamma_{\Delta}
.
The Galois conjugation is an element in
\mathrm{O}(\mathcal{O}_{F})
.
On the finite quadratic module

(2.3.4)

A_{\Delta}:=\mathfrak{d}^{-1}/\mathcal{O}_{F},

which is identified with
\mathbb{Z}/(\Delta/\gcd(\Delta,2))\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/(\gcd(\Delta,2))\mathbb{Z}
by the map

(2.3.5)

\frac{a+b\omega_{\Delta}}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\mapsto(a,b),

the Galois conjugation acts as the map sending
h\in A_{\Delta}
to
-h
.
The multiplication on
F
also induces a multiplication
A_{\Delta}\times A_{\Delta}\to A_{\Delta}
.
For us later, it will be convenient to work with the
p
-components
A_{\Delta,p}
. If
p\nmid\Delta
, then
A_{\Delta,p}
is trivial.
When
p\mid\Delta
is an odd prime, it is easily seen that

(2.3.6)

A_{\Delta,p}\cong(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z},a\mapsto\alpha_{\Delta,p}\tfrac{a^{2}}{p}).

for some
\alpha_{\Delta,p}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
depending on
\Delta
.
For
p=2\mid\Delta
, there are two possibilities

(2.3.7)

A_{\Delta,2}\cong\begin{cases}((\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{2},(a,b)\mapsto\tfrac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{4}),&{\mathrm{ord}}_{2}(\Delta)=2,\\
(\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z},(a,b)\mapsto\tfrac{\pm a^{2}+6b^{2}}{8}),&{\mathrm{ord}}_{2}(\Delta)=3,\end{cases}

since
\Delta
is a fundamental discriminant.
Note that the
\pm
’s must be chosen consistently and the choices depend on
\Delta
.

For every prime
p\mid\Delta
, we define an automorphism
\sigma_{p}
on
A_{\Delta,p}
by

(2.3.8)

\sigma_{p}(h):=\begin{cases}(b,a),&\text{if }p=2,{\mathrm{ord}}_{2}(\Delta)=2,\text{ and }h=(a,b)\in(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{2}\cong A_{\Delta,2},\\
-h,&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

which has order 2. It is easy to check that for every prime
p\mid\Delta
and distinct
h,\tilde{h}\in A_{\Delta,p}

(2.3.9)

Q_{p}(h)=Q_{p}(\tilde{h})\Leftrightarrow\sigma_{p}(h)=\tilde{h}.

Since
A_{\Delta}=\oplus_{p\mid\Delta\text{ prime}}A_{\Delta,p}
as a finite quadratic modules in (
2.1.6
), we also use
\sigma_{p}
to denote the isometry on
A_{\Delta}
that acts as
\sigma_{p}
on
A_{\Delta,p}
, and trivially on the other components.
These automorphisms all have order 2 and commute with each other.
We denote the group they generate by
\mathrm{G}_{\Delta}
, which contains the subgroup

(2.3.10)

\mathrm{G}_{\Delta,h}:=\{\sigma\in\mathrm{G}_{\Delta}:\sigma(h)=h\}

and has the following property.

Lemma 2.3
.

In the notations above,
h
is in
\mathfrak{d}_{p}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}/\mathcal{O}_{F}\subset A_{\Delta}
if and only if
\sigma_{p}
fixes
h
.
Suppose
Q(h)=\frac{n}{\Delta}
with
n\in\mathbb{Z}/\Delta\mathbb{Z}
, then
|\mathrm{G}_{\Delta,h}|=2^{\omega(\gcd(n,\Delta))}
, where
\omega(n)
is the number of distinct prime factors of
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Furthermore, if there exists
\tilde{h}\in A_{\Delta}
satisfying
Q(h)=Q(\tilde{h})
, then
\tilde{h}=\sigma(h)
for some
\sigma\in\mathrm{G}_{\Delta}
.

Remark 2.4
.

For convenience, we define

(2.3.11)

G_{\Delta}:=\{|d|:d\text{ fundamental discriminant dividing }\Delta\text{ and }\gcd(d,\Delta/d)=1\},

which has a group structure under the multiplication
d_{1}\cdot d_{2}:=d_{1}d_{2}/\gcd(d_{1},d_{2})^{2}
.
We canonically identify it with
\mathrm{G}_{\Delta}
via the map

(2.3.12)

d\in G_{\Delta}\mapsto\sigma_{d}:=\prod_{p\mid d\text{ prime}}\sigma_{p}\in\mathrm{G}_{\Delta}.

Consider the subgroups of
G_{\Delta}
of the form

(2.3.13)

G_{\Delta,d}:=\left\{\tilde{d}\in G_{\Delta}:\tilde{d}\mid d\right\}

for some
d\in G_{\Delta}
.
For any
h\in A_{\Delta}
,
\sigma_{d}\in\mathrm{G}_{\Delta,h}
if and only if
\sigma_{p}\in\mathrm{G}_{\Delta,h}
for all primes
p\mid d
.
Therefore, we can define a function
\mathrm{d}:A_{\Delta}\to G_{\Delta}
such that

(2.3.14)

G_{\Delta,\mathrm{d}(h)}=\mathrm{G}_{\Delta,h},

i.e.
\mathrm{d}(h)
and
\gcd(\Delta,n)
have the same prime factors. For any prime
p\mid\Delta
and
h\in A_{\Delta}
, we have

(2.3.15)

\mathrm{d}(ph)=\mathrm{lcm}(\mathrm{d}(h),p).

Remark 2.5
.

The following set

(2.3.16)

A_{\Delta}^{*}:=\left\{h\in A_{\Delta}:\#\mathrm{G}_{\Delta,h}=1\right\}=\left\{h\in A_{\Delta}:\mathrm{d}(h)=1\right\}

will be useful for us later.

Remark 2.6
.

One can check that
\sigma_{\Delta_{0}}(h)=-h
, where
\Delta_{0}
is the square-free part of
\Delta
. Therefore,
2h=0\in A_{\Delta}
if and only if
\sigma_{\Delta_{0}}\in\mathrm{G}_{\Delta,h}
, i.e.
\Delta_{0}\mid\mathrm{d}(h)
.

Proof.

The first claim can be verified directly, though one needs to be slightly careful when
p=2
divides
\Delta
.
The second claim follows from the first since
h\in\mathfrak{d}_{p}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}/\mathcal{O}_{F}
if and only if
p\mid m
.
For the last claim, notice that
Q(h)=Q(\tilde{h})
is equivalent to
Q_{p}(h_{p})=Q_{p}(\tilde{h}_{p})
for all primes
p\mid\Delta
, where
h_{p}
and
\tilde{h}_{p}
are the
p
-components of
h
and
\tilde{h}
respectively.
Therefore,
\tilde{h}_{p}
is either
h_{p}
or
\sigma_{p}(h_{p})
by (
2.3.9
), and we are done after setting
\sigma:=\prod_{p\mid\Delta\text{ prime,}~\tilde{h}_{p}=\sigma_{p}(h_{p})}\sigma_{p}\in\mathrm{G}_{\Delta}
.
∎

We can now replace
\mathcal{O}_{F}
with any fractional
\mathcal{O}_{F}
-ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subset F
to obtain an even integral lattice
L_{\mathfrak{a}}
with
Q_{\mathfrak{a}}={\mathrm{Nm}}/{\mathrm{Nm}}(\mathfrak{a})
and
L^{\vee}_{\mathfrak{a}}=\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}
.
We denote the finite quadratic module
(L_{\mathfrak{a}}^{\vee}/L_{\mathfrak{a}},Q_{\mathfrak{a}})
and the Weil representation associated to it by
A_{\mathfrak{a}}
and
\rho_{\mathfrak{a}}
respectively.
Their isomorphism classes only depend on the class of
\mathfrak{a}
in
{\mathrm{Cl}}^{+}(F)
.
The finite abelian groups
A_{\mathfrak{a}}
and
A_{\Delta}
are always isomorphic.
Furthermore, suppose that
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\in I_{\mathfrak{d}}
with
\mathfrak{b}\subset\mathfrak{a}
. Then one can use this inclusion to canonically identify the abelian groups
A_{\mathfrak{a}}
and
A_{\mathfrak{b}}
.
By taking
\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{a}\cap\mathcal{O}_{F}
, we then have the canonical identification.

(2.3.17)

A_{\Delta}=A_{\mathfrak{a}\cap\mathcal{O}_{F}}=A_{\mathfrak{a}}.

However, the quadratic form is given by
Q_{\mathfrak{a}}={\mathrm{Nm}}(\mathfrak{a})^{-1}Q
under this group isomorphism.
Since the quadratic forms only differ by a factor invertible in
\mathbb{Z}/\Delta\mathbb{Z}
, one can define the automorphism
\sigma_{p}
on
A_{\mathfrak{a},p}
and
A_{\mathfrak{a}}
as in (
2.3.16
) and (
2.3.8
) respectively.
Therefore the group
\mathrm{G}_{\Delta}=G_{\Delta}
also acts
A_{\mathfrak{a}}
, and one can define the subgroup
\mathrm{G}_{\Delta,h}
for
h\in A_{\mathfrak{a}}
and the subset
A_{\mathfrak{a}}^{*}\subset A_{\mathfrak{a}}
as in (
2.3.10
) and (
2.3.16
) respectively.

We are in a particularly nice situation if
Q_{\mathfrak{a}}=Q
, i.e.
\mathfrak{a}\in\ker({\mathrm{Nm}})=\mathrm{im}({\mathrm{Nm}}^{-})

, since (
2.3.17
) is an equality of finite quadratic modules, which is
\Gamma
-linear on
\mathbb{C}[A_{\mathfrak{a}}]=\mathbb{C}[A_{\Delta}]
with respect to
\rho_{\mathfrak{a}}
and
\rho_{\Delta}
after extending by linearity.
On the other hand, if
\mathfrak{a}=(\alpha)
for some
\alpha\in F
coprime to
\mathfrak{d}
, then there is another group isomorphism

(2.3.18)

\begin{split}A_{\Delta}=\mathfrak{d}^{-1}/\mathcal{O}_{F}&\cong A_{\mathfrak{a}}=\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}/\mathfrak{a}\\
h&\mapsto\alpha h.\end{split}

When
A_{\Delta}
is equipped with the quadratic form
Q_{\alpha}:={\mathrm{Nm}}/{\mathrm{Nm}}(\alpha)
, this is an isomorphism between finite quadratic modules.
If
{\mathrm{Nm}}(\alpha)=1
, then
A_{\mathfrak{a}}=A_{\Delta}
and
(\alpha)={\mathrm{Nm}}^{-}(\mathfrak{b})
with
\mathfrak{b}\in I_{\mathfrak{d}}
having order at dividing 2 in
{\mathrm{Cl}}^{+}(F)
.
When
[\mathfrak{b}]
is trivial in
{\mathrm{Cl}}^{+}(F)
, i.e.
\mathfrak{b}=(\beta)
with
\beta\in F
totally positive, then
{\mathrm{Nm}}^{-}(\beta)=1\bmod{\mathfrak{d}}
and the composition
A_{\Delta}\cong A_{\mathfrak{a}}=A_{\Delta}
is the identity map.
Therefore, the composition
A_{\Delta}\cong A_{\mathfrak{a}}=A_{\Delta}
only depends on the class of
\mathfrak{b}
in
{\mathrm{Cl}}^{+}(F)
.
It is straightforward to verify that this is the automorphism
\sigma_{p}
defined in (
2.3.8
) when
[\mathfrak{b}]=[\mathfrak{d}_{p}]
for any prime
p\mid\Delta
.

2.4.
Genus Theory

We make a quick digression to the genus theory of quadratic fields.
The map
{\mathrm{Nm}}^{-}
defined in (
2.3.3
) induces the squaring map on
{\mathrm{Cl}}^{+}(F)
, whose kernel and image are denoted by

(2.4.1)

{\mathrm{Cl}}^{+}_{2}(F):=\ker({\mathrm{Nm}}^{-})\subset{\mathrm{Cl}}^{+}(F),\quad{\mathrm{Cl}}^{+,2}(F):=\mathrm{im}({\mathrm{Nm}}^{-})\subset{\mathrm{Cl}}^{+}(F)

respectively.
Then
{\mathrm{Cl}}^{+}_{2}(F)
is exactly the group of 2-torsion elements in
{\mathrm{Cl}}^{+}(F)
and is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{\omega(\Delta)-1}
by genus theory.
It is easy to see that
[\mathfrak{d}_{p}]\in{\mathrm{Cl}}^{+}_{2}(F)
for every prime
p\mid\Delta
.
With the group
G_{\Delta}
defined in (
2.3.11
) from the previous section, we can now describe
{\mathrm{Cl}}^{+}_{2}(F)
precisely in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.7
.

There is a unique
d_{0}\in G_{\Delta}\backslash\{1\}
such that the class of

(2.4.2)

\mathfrak{d}_{0}:=\prod_{p\mid d_{0}\text{ prime}}\mathfrak{d}_{p}

in
{\mathrm{Cl}}^{+}(F)
is trivial.
Let
\varepsilon_{F}^{+}>1
be the generator of the subgroup of totally positive units in
\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times}
.
Then multiplying by

Proof.

For every

Then

\mathfrak{d}_{d}=\mathfrak{d}_{\tilde{d}}
.
The unit
\varepsilon_{F}^{+}
is either
\varepsilon_{F}
or
\varepsilon_{F}^{2}
depending on whether
\varepsilon_{F}
has norm
1
or
-1
.
By Hilbert’s Theorem 90, there exists an
\alpha_{0}\in\mathcal{O}_{F}
such that
\varepsilon^{+}_{F}=\alpha_{0}/\alpha^{\prime}_{0}
. Since
\varepsilon^{+}_{F}
is a unit, we can choose
\alpha_{0}
such that
(\alpha_{0})=\mathfrak{d}_{d_{0}}
for some

.
Suppose

such that
\mathfrak{d}_{d}=(\alpha_{d})
with
\alpha_{d}
totally positive, then
\alpha_{d}^{\prime}/\alpha_{d}=(\varepsilon^{+}_{F})^{r}=(\alpha_{0}/\alpha^{\prime}_{0})^{r}
for some

, which implies

. If

has a generator in
\mathbb{Z}
, meaning

must be 1. If

has a generator in
\mathbb{Z}
, implying that
\mathfrak{d}_{d}
and
\mathfrak{d}_{d_{0}}

. Note that

if
\varepsilon_{F}
has norm
-1

We denote
\mathfrak{d}_{0}:=\mathfrak{d}_{d_{0}}

Using
\varepsilon_{F}^{+}=\alpha_{0}/\alpha^{\prime}_{0}
with
(\alpha_{0})=\mathfrak{d}_{0}
and
\alpha_{0}-\alpha^{\prime}_{0}\in\mathfrak{d}

(2.4.5)

\varepsilon^{+}_{F}\equiv 1\bmod{\mathfrak{d}/\mathfrak{d}_{0}},~\varepsilon^{+}_{F}\equiv\begin{cases}-1\bmod{\mathfrak{d}_{p}},&p\mid d_{0}\text{ odd,}\\
-1\bmod{\mathfrak{d}_{2}},&2\mid d_{0},{\mathrm{ord}}_{2}(\Delta)=2,\\
-1\bmod{\mathfrak{d}^{3}_{2}},&2\mid d_{0},{\mathrm{ord}}_{2}(\Delta)=3,\end{cases}

which implies the second claim.
Note that

and
\Delta
is divisible by a prime congruent to 3 modulo 4. In that case, the fundamental unit
\varepsilon_{F}
has norm

\varepsilon_{F}^{+}=\varepsilon_{F}
.
Since

,
\mathfrak{d}_{0}
is non-trivial and
\varepsilon^{+}_{F}\not\in\Gamma_{\Delta}
.
As the group

\pm\varepsilon^{+}_{F}

∎

Using the embedding

Lemma 2.9

Let

Remark 2.10

Since

\mathrm{s}_{h}=1

Proof.

It is clear

.
Now suppose

Now we can attach an element of the group ring

\mathrm{sqrt}(\mathfrak{a},h):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{B}=[\mathfrak{b}]\in{\mathrm{Cl}}^{+}(F)\\

\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\in\mathcal{O}_{F}+h\subset\mathfrak{d}^{-1}\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{B}\in\mathbb{Z}[{\mathrm{Cl}}^{+}(F)].

\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{\prime}}\equiv 1\bmod{\mathfrak{d}}

\mathfrak{d}
, we know that the condition in the definition is independent of the choice of the representative
\mathfrak{b}\in I_{\mathfrak{d}}

However, there is a choice between
\mu

\varepsilon_{F}^{+}\mu

2.7

(2.4.10)

\mathrm{sqrt}(\mathfrak{a},h)=\mathrm{sqrt}(\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{d_{0}}(h))

and

For other distinct

\mathrm{sqrt}(\mathfrak{a},h_{1})
and

The action of an element

For any positive
\alpha

\mathrm{supp}(\mathrm{sqrt}(\mathfrak{a},h))\subset\mathrm{supp}(\mathrm{sqrt}((\alpha)\mathfrak{a},\alpha h)),

which is an equality if

.
Using the analysis above, we have the following precise result.

Lemma 2.11
.

For any positive
\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_{F}
and integral ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathcal{O}_{F}
, let
d\in G_{\Delta}
be the smallest element such that

Then for any

, we have

\mathrm{sqrt}((\alpha)\mathfrak{a},\alpha h)=\frac{1}{\#(G_{\Delta,d}\cap\mathrm{G}_{\Delta,\overline{h}})}\sum_{\sigma\in G_{\Delta,d}}\mathrm{sqrt}(\mathfrak{a},\sigma(h)).

Proof.

The choice of

implies that

for all
\sigma\in G_{\Delta,d}
. In fact, every
\tilde{h}\in A_{\Delta}
with the property

is of the form
\sigma_{\tilde{d}}(h)
for some

).
On the other hand,
\mathrm{sqrt}(\mathfrak{a},h)=\mathrm{sqrt}(\mathfrak{a},\sigma(h))
if and only if

or

, i.e.
\sigma\in\mathrm{G}_{\Delta,\overline{h}}
.
Therefore, we have an equality of both sides.
∎

Finally, recall that the genus field

.
If we factor

From this proposition, we can deduce some the following properties of the coefficient
c_{\chi}(m,h)
.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
p\mid\Delta
be a prime such that
\chi([\mathfrak{d}_{p}])=-1
with
\mathfrak{d}_{p}
the unique prime ideal in
\mathcal{O}_{F}
above
p
.
Then
c_{\chi}(m,h)=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{Q}
and
h\in A_{\Delta}
satisfying
p\mid\mathrm{d}(h)
, where
\mathrm{d}
is the function defined in (
2.3.14
).

Proof.

This easily follows from the proposition above and (
2.4.11
).
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
be a prime such that there is only one prime
\mathfrak{l}
in
\mathcal{O}_{F}
above it.
Then

(3.1.11)

c_{\chi}\left(\frac{\ell^{2}n}{\Delta},\ell h\right)=\begin{cases}c_{\chi}\left(\frac{n}{\Delta},h\right),&\text{ if }\ell\nmid\Delta\text{ or }\ell\mid\gcd(\Delta,n),\\
(1+\chi([\mathfrak{l}]))c_{\chi}\left(\frac{n}{\Delta},h\right),&\text{ if }\ell\mid\Delta\text{ and }\ell\nmid n,\end{cases}

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
h\in A_{\Delta}
.

Proof.

By Prop.
3.4
, we can suppose that
n\in{\mathrm{Nm}}(F)_{+}
and
\chi
is odd, otherwise both sides are identically zero.
Applying Prop.
3.4
gives us

\displaystyle c_{\chi}\left(\frac{\ell^{2}n}{\Delta},\ell h\right)

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{s}_{\ell h}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathcal{O}_{F},~{\mathrm{Nm}}(\ell\mathfrak{a})=\ell^{2}n\end{subarray}}{\chi}(\mathrm{sqrt}(\ell\mathfrak{a},\ell h))

since there is only one prime in
\mathcal{O}_{F}
above
\ell
.
If
\ell
is inert, i.e.
\ell\nmid\Delta
, then
\mathrm{s}_{\ell h}=\mathrm{s}_{h}
and
\mathrm{sqrt}(\ell\mathfrak{a},\ell h)=\mathrm{sqrt}(\mathfrak{a},h)
by Lemma
2.11
, which completes the proof.
Suppose
\ell
is ramified, i.e.
\ell\mid\Delta
, then Lemma
2.11
implies that

\displaystyle c_{\chi}\left(\frac{\ell^{2}n}{\Delta},\ell h\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\mathrm{s}_{\ell h}}{\#(G_{\Delta,\ell}\cap\mathrm{G}_{\Delta,\overline{h}})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathfrak{a}}\subset\mathcal{O}_{F}\\
{\mathrm{Nm}}({\mathfrak{a}})=n\end{subarray}}\sum_{\sigma\in G_{\Delta,\ell}}\chi(\mathrm{sqrt}(\mathfrak{a},\sigma(h))).

Let
d_{0}
and
\Delta_{0}
be as in Lemma
2.9
.
We have now several cases

Case 1:

\ell\mid\mathrm{d}(h)
.

Case 2:

\ell\nmid\mathrm{d}(h)
and
\chi([\mathfrak{l}])=-1
.

Case 3:

\ell\nmid\mathrm{d}(h)
,
\chi([\mathfrak{l}])=1
and
\Delta_{0}\mid\mathrm{d}(\ell h)
.

Case 4(a):

\ell\nmid\mathrm{d}(h)
,
\chi([\mathfrak{l}])=1
,
\Delta_{0}\nmid\mathrm{d}(\ell h)
and
d_{0}\mid\mathrm{d}(h)
.

Case 4(b):

\ell,d_{0}\nmid\mathrm{d}(h)
,
\chi([\mathfrak{l}])=1
,
\Delta_{0}\nmid\mathrm{d}(\ell h)
and
d_{0}\mid\mathrm{d}(\ell h)
.

Case 5:

Otherwise, i.e.
\ell\nmid\mathrm{d}(h)
,
\chi([\mathfrak{l}])=1
and
d_{0},\Delta_{0}\nmid\mathrm{d}(\ell h)
.

For the elements
\mathrm{sqrt}(\mathfrak{a},h),\mathrm{sqrt}(\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\ell}(h))
to be non-zero, we can suppose that
\ell\mid n
in Case 1 and
\ell\nmid n
for the other cases.
In Case 1, the proof of the inert case carries through since
\mathrm{s}_{\ell h}=\mathrm{s}_{h}
and
G_{\Delta,\ell}\cap\mathrm{G}_{\Delta,\overline{h}}=G_{\Delta,\ell}
.
For Case 2, the left hand side vanishes by Corollary
3.5
, and equals the right hand side.
For Case 3, since
\chi
is odd, i.e.
\chi([\mathfrak{d}])=-1
, there is a prime
p\mid\Delta_{0}
such that
\chi([\mathfrak{d}_{p}])=-1
, which implies that
p\neq\ell
.
Therefore,
p\mid\mathrm{d}(h)
and both sides vanish again by Corollary
3.5
.

For the other cases, it suffices to establish the equality

\frac{2\mathrm{s}_{\ell h}}{\#(G_{\Delta,\ell}\cap\mathrm{G}_{\Delta,\overline{h}})}=2\mathrm{s}_{h}

and the rest follows as in the previous cases.
In Case 4(a), we have
\mathrm{s}_{\ell h}=\mathrm{s}_{h}=2
from Lemma
2.9
. The conditions
\ell\nmid\mathrm{d}(h)
and
d_{0}\mid\mathrm{d}(h)\mid\mathrm{d}(\ell h)
together imply that
\sigma_{\ell}(h)\neq h=\sigma_{d_{0}}(h)
, i.e.
G_{\Delta,\ell}\cap\mathrm{G}_{\Delta,\overline{h}}
is trivial.
In Case 4(b), Lemma
2.9
again implies that
\mathrm{s}_{\ell h}=2\mathrm{s}_{h}=2
.
On the other hand, we have
\ell h=\ell\sigma_{d_{0}}(h)=\ell\sigma_{\ell}(h)
, which means
h,\sigma_{d_{0}}(h)
and
\sigma_{\ell}(h)
all have the same
p
-component for
p\neq\ell
. Since
\ell\nmid\mathrm{d}(h)
implies that
h\neq\sigma_{\ell}(h)
, we must have
\sigma_{d_{0}}(h)=h
or
\sigma_{\ell}(h)
by (
2.3.9
). The former cannot happen precisely since
d_{0}\nmid\mathrm{d}(h)
. Therefore
\sigma_{d_{0}}(h)=\sigma_{\ell}(h)
and
\sigma_{\ell}\in\mathrm{G}_{\Delta,\overline{h}}
by definition.
In the last case, we again have
\mathrm{s}_{\ell h}=\mathrm{s}_{h}
and
G_{\Delta,\ell}\cap\mathrm{G}_{\Delta,\overline{h}}
being trivial.
This finishes the proof.
∎

3.2.
Deformed Integral

Let
(L,Q)=(\mathfrak{a},{\mathrm{Nm}}/A)
as in the previous section.
In
[
14
]
, we considered the following deformed integral

(3.2.1)

\hat{\vartheta}_{\mathfrak{a}}(\tau):=\int_{0}^{\log\varepsilon_{\Delta}}\Theta_{\mathfrak{a}}(\tau,w)wdw\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1,\rho_{\mathfrak{a}}}.

Note that this function is real-analytic on
\mathbb{H}
with exponential decay near the cusp.
Furthermore, it depends on the choice of the fundamental domain.
In Prop. 5.5 in
[
14
]
, we have calculated its Fourier expansion, which we recall here.
For each
\lambda\in F^{\times}
, denote

(3.2.2)

r(\lambda):=\left|\frac{\lambda}{\lambda^{\prime}}\right|.

For each
\Gamma_{\Delta}
-orbit
\Lambda\subset F^{\times}
, choose a representative
\lambda_{0}\in\Lambda
such that
1\leq r(\lambda_{0})<\varepsilon_{\Delta}^{2}
.
Now define

(3.2.3)

a(\Lambda):=\begin{cases}\mathrm{sgn}(\lambda_{0})\log r(\lambda_{0}),&r(\lambda_{0})\neq 1,\\
-\log\varepsilon_{\Delta},&r(\lambda_{0})=1.\end{cases}

These are the “holomorphic” part coefficients of
\hat{\vartheta}_{\mathfrak{a},h}
with
h\in\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}/\mathfrak{a}
. For the non-holomorphic part, we first define incomplete Gamma function

(3.2.4)

\Gamma(s,x):=\int^{\infty}_{x}e^{-t}t^{s}\frac{dt}{t}.

for
s\in\mathbb{C}
and
x>0
. Then we can define the following non-holomorphic function on
\mathbb{H}

(3.2.5)

\begin{split}\tilde{\vartheta}_{\mathfrak{a},h}^{*}(\tau)&:=\sum_{\Lambda\in\Gamma_{\Delta}\backslash(\mathfrak{a}+h),Q(\Lambda)<0}\mathrm{sgn}(\Lambda)\Gamma(0,-4\pi Q(\Lambda)v)q^{Q(\Lambda)},\\
\tilde{\Theta}^{*}_{\mathfrak{a},h}(\tau)&:=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}+h}\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Gamma\left({\tfrac{1}{2}},\frac{\pi v|\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}|^{2}}{A}\right)q^{Q(\lambda)}.\end{split}

Furthermore, under the lowering operator
L_{\tau}
, these functions become modular and satisfy

(3.2.6)

L_{\tau}\tilde{\vartheta}_{\mathfrak{a},h}^{*}(\tau)=-v\overline{\vartheta^{-}_{\mathfrak{a},h}(\tau)},\;L_{\tau}\tilde{\Theta}^{*}_{\mathfrak{a},h}(\tau)=-v\overline{\Theta^{-}_{\mathfrak{a},h}(\tau,0)}.

They turn out to form the “non-holomorphic” part of
\hat{\vartheta}_{\mathfrak{a},h}(\tau)
.

Proposition 3.7

(Prop. 5.1 in
[
14
]
)
.

The function
\hat{\vartheta}_{\mathfrak{a}}(\tau)=\sum_{h\in\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}/\mathfrak{a}}\mathfrak{e}_{h}\hat{\vartheta}_{\mathfrak{a},h}(\tau)
has the Fourier expansion

(3.2.7)

2\hat{\vartheta}_{\mathfrak{a},h}(\tau)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}}\tilde{c}_{\mathfrak{a}}(m,h)q^{m}-\tilde{\vartheta}^{*}_{\mathfrak{a},h}(\tau)-\log\varepsilon_{\Delta}\cdot\tilde{\Theta}^{*}_{\mathfrak{a},h}(\tau),

where
\tilde{c}_{\mathfrak{a}}(m,h):=\sum_{\Lambda\in\Gamma_{\Delta}\backslash L(m,h)}a(\Lambda)
.

Let
\chi
be a genus character of
F
and
S_{F}
as in (
3.1.8
).
As before, we can sum
\chi(\mathfrak{b})\hat{\vartheta}_{{\mathrm{Nm}}^{-}(\mathfrak{b})}
over
\mathfrak{b}\in S_{F}
to define

(3.2.8)

\begin{split}\hat{\vartheta}_{\chi}(\tau;S_{F})&:=\sum_{\mathfrak{b}\in S_{F}}\chi(\mathfrak{b})\hat{\vartheta}_{{\mathrm{Nm}}^{-}(\mathfrak{b})}(\tau)=\sum_{h\in A_{\Delta}}\mathfrak{e}_{h}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}}\hat{c}_{\chi}(m,h,v){\mathbf{e}}(mu)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1,\rho_{\Delta}},\\
\tilde{c}_{\chi}(m,h)&:=\lim_{v\to\infty}\hat{c}_{\chi}(m,h,v)e^{2\pi mv}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathfrak{b}\in S_{F}}\chi(\mathfrak{b})\tilde{c}_{{\mathrm{Nm}}^{-}(\mathfrak{b})}(m,h)\in\mathbb{R}.\end{split}

The lemma below gives a bound of the Fourier coefficients
\hat{c}_{\chi}(m,h,v)
.

Lemma 3.8
.

For every
h\in A_{\Delta}
and
\epsilon>0
, we have the asymptotic

(3.2.9)

\hat{c}_{\chi}(m,h,v)e^{2\pi|m|v}=\tilde{c}_{\chi}(m,h)+O_{F,S_{F},\epsilon}\left(|m|^{\epsilon}\frac{v+1}{v^{3/2}}\right),

where
S_{F}
is the set of ideals as in (
3.1.8
) and
\tilde{c}_{\chi}(m,h)=0
for
m\leq 0
.

Remark 3.9
.

When
m>0
, it is easy to check that
\tilde{c}_{\chi}(m,h)=O_{F,\epsilon}(m^{\epsilon})
for any
\epsilon>0
.

Proof.

When
m<0
, we see from the definition that
\tilde{c}_{\chi}(m,h)=0
and

(3.2.10)

|\hat{c}_{\chi}(m,h,v)|\ll_{F}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{b}\in S_{F}\\
(\lambda)\subset{\mathrm{Nm}}^{-}(\mathfrak{b})\mathfrak{d}^{-1}\\
{\mathrm{Nm}}(\lambda)=m\end{subarray}}\left(\Gamma(0,-{4\pi mv})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{Z}\\
\lambda_{n}\neq\lambda_{n}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\Gamma\left({\tfrac{1}{2}},\pi v{(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{n}^{\prime})^{2}}{}\right)\right)e^{-{2\pi mv}},

where we have denote
\lambda_{n}:=\lambda\varepsilon^{n}
and chosen the index
n
such that
r(\lambda_{n})\geq 1
if and only if
n\geq 0
.
Using the bounds
\Gamma(s,x)\ll_{s}x^{s-1}e^{-x}
, we can write

\Gamma(0,-4\pi mv)e^{-2\pi mv}\ll\frac{e^{-2\pi|m|v}}{|m|v},~\Gamma\left({\tfrac{1}{2}},\pi v{(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{n}^{\prime})^{2}}{}\right)e^{-2\pi mv}\ll\frac{e^{-{\pi|m|v(r(\lambda_{n})+r(\lambda_{n})^{-1})}}}{\sqrt{v}|\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{n}^{\prime}|}.

Since
\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{n}^{\prime}\neq 0
and
\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}\mathfrak{d}(\lambda_{n})\subset\mathcal{O}_{F}
, we have
|\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{n}^{\prime}|^{-1}\ll_{S_{F}}1
.
Since the function
x+x^{-1}
is monotonically increasing for
x\geq 1
and
, we have
r(\lambda_{n})+r(\lambda_{n})^{-1}-2>C\cdot|n|
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
with some constant
C=C(S_{F})>0
depending on
S_{F}
.
Summing over
n\in\mathbb{Z}
gives us

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Gamma\left({\tfrac{1}{2}},\pi v{(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{n}^{\prime})^{2}}{}\right)e^{-{2\pi|m|v}}

\displaystyle\ll_{S_{F}}\frac{e^{-2\pi|m|v}}{\sqrt{v}}\sum_{n\geq 0}e^{-\pi|m|vCn}\ll_{S_{F}}\frac{v+1}{v^{3/2}}e^{-2\pi|m|v},

since
\frac{1}{1-e^{-x}}\leq\frac{x+1}{x}
for
x\geq 0
.
The sum on the right hand side of (
3.2.10
) counts the number of ideals in
\mathcal{O}_{F}
with norm
\Delta m
, which is bounded above by
C_{\epsilon}\cdot(\Delta m)^{\epsilon}
for any
\epsilon>0
and a constant
C_{\epsilon}>0
.
Putting everything together, we obtain the bound (
3.2.9
) for
m<0
.
When
m>0
, we have

|\hat{c}_{\chi}(m,h,v)-\tilde{c}_{\chi}(m,h)e^{-2\pi mv}|\ll_{F}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{b}\in S_{F}\\
(\lambda)\subset{\mathrm{Nm}}^{-}(\mathfrak{b})\mathfrak{d}^{-1}\\
{\mathrm{Nm}}(\lambda)=m\end{subarray}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Gamma\left({\tfrac{1}{2}},\pi v{(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{n}^{\prime})^{2}}\right)e^{-{2\pi mv}}.

Proceeding as before finishes the proof.
∎

The function
\hat{\vartheta}_{\chi}(\tau,S_{F})
will be the input of the theta lift that constructs the preimage of an Eisenstein series under the lowering operator since

where

3.1.9
).

3.4.
Counting Result I

In the notations of sections
2.3
,
3.1
and
3.3
, we can define a quantity

(3.4.1)

C_{\chi}(\mu_{0})=\sum_{t\mid(\mu_{0}),\,t\in\mathbb{N}}c_{\chi}\left(\frac{{\mathrm{Nm}}(\mu_{0}/t)}{\Delta},\frac{\mu_{0}/t}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\right)

for any
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{O}_{F}
.
This turns out to be related to the ideal counting function
\rho_{K/F}((\mu_{0}))
defined in Eq. (
3.3.2
).

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
\chi
be an odd genus character of
F
and
K/F
the corresponding CM extension.
For any totally positive
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{O}_{F}
,

(3.4.2)

C_{\chi}(\mu_{0})=2\sigma_{\chi}((\mu_{0}))=2\rho_{K/F}((\mu_{0})),

where
\rho_{K/F}(\mathfrak{a})
is the ideal-counting function defined in Eq. (
3.3.2
).

Proof.

We will proceed by considering the prime factorization of the ideal
(\mu_{0})
.
Let
\ell
be a rational prime and
{\mathrm{ord}}_{\ell}({\mathrm{Nm}}(\mu_{0}))=a
.
If
\ell
is inert in
\mathcal{O}_{F}
, then
2\mid a
,
\chi([\ell])=1
and

C_{\chi}(\mu_{0})=(a+1)C_{\chi}(\mu_{0}/\ell^{a/2})=\rho_{K/F}(\ell^{a})C_{\chi}(\mu_{0}/\ell^{a/2})

by Prop.
3.6
.
If
\ell
is ramified in
\mathcal{O}_{F}
, i.e.
\ell\mathcal{O}_{F}=\mathfrak{l}^{2}
, then there are two cases depending on the parity of
a
. When
a
is even, Prop.
3.6
again implies that

\displaystyle C_{\chi}(\mu_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\mid(\mu_{0}/\ell^{a/2})}\sum_{r=0}^{a/2}c_{\chi}\left(\frac{{\mathrm{Nm}}(\mu_{0}/(t\ell^{r}))}{\Delta},\frac{\mu_{0}/(t\ell^{r})}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{a}{2}(1+\chi([\mathfrak{l}]))+1\right)\sum_{t\mid\widetilde{\mu_{0}}}c_{\chi}\left(\frac{{\mathrm{Nm}}(\widetilde{\mu_{0}}/t)}{\Delta},\frac{\widetilde{\mu_{0}}/t}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\right)=\rho_{K/F}(\mathfrak{l}^{a})C_{\chi}(\widetilde{\mu_{0}}),

where
\widetilde{\mu_{0}}:=\mu_{0}/\ell^{a/2}
.
When
a
is odd and
\chi([\mathfrak{l}])=-1
, Corollary
3.5
implies that
C_{\chi}(\mu_{0})=0=2\rho_{K/F}((\mu_{0}))
.
Suppose that
a
is odd and
\chi([\mathfrak{l}])=1
. Applying Prop.
3.6
as before yields

(3.4.3)

C_{\chi}(\mu_{0})=\sum_{t\mid(\widetilde{\mu_{0}})}\sum_{r=0}^{(a-1)/2}c_{\chi}\left(\frac{\ell^{r}{\mathrm{Nm}}(\widetilde{\mu_{0}}/t)}{\Delta},\frac{\ell^{r}\widetilde{\mu_{0}}/t}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\right)=\frac{a+1}{2}\sum_{t\mid\widetilde{\mu_{0}}}c_{\chi}\left(\frac{{\mathrm{Nm}}(\widetilde{\mu_{0}}/t)}{\Delta},\frac{\widetilde{\mu_{0}}/t}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\right),

where
\widetilde{\mu_{0}}:=\mu_{0}/\ell^{(a-1)/2}
and
{\mathrm{ord}}_{\mathfrak{l}}(\widetilde{\mu_{0}})=1
.
Now we can apply the Chebotarev density theorem to choose a prime
\tilde{\mathfrak{l}}
relatively prime to
\mu_{0}
in the class
[\mathfrak{l}]\in{\mathrm{Cl}}^{+}(F)
.
That means
\mathfrak{l}\tilde{\mathfrak{l}}=(\alpha)
with
\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_{F}
totally positive.
Mimicking the proof of Prop.
3.6
, we have

\displaystyle c_{\chi}\left(\frac{n}{\Delta},h\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathcal{O}_{F}\\
{\mathrm{Nm}}(\mathfrak{a})=n\end{subarray}}\chi(\mathrm{sqrt}(\mathfrak{a},h))=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathcal{O}_{F}\\
{\mathrm{Nm}}(\mathfrak{a})=n\end{subarray}}\chi(\mathrm{sqrt}(\mathfrak{a}(\alpha),\alpha h))+\chi(\mathrm{sqrt}(\mathfrak{a}(\alpha^{\prime}),\alpha^{\prime}h))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}\subset\mathcal{O}_{F}\\
{\mathrm{Nm}}(\tilde{\mathfrak{a}})=n{\mathrm{Nm}}(\alpha)\end{subarray}}\chi(\mathrm{sqrt}(\tilde{\mathfrak{a}},\alpha h))=\frac{1}{2}c_{\chi}\left(\frac{n{\mathrm{Nm}}(\alpha)}{\Delta},\alpha h\right)

for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
h\in A_{\Delta}
with
\sigma_{\ell}(h)=h
. Note that the second step follows from Lemma
2.11
and
\alpha h=\alpha^{\prime}h\in A_{\Delta}
since
\sigma_{\ell}(h)=h
.
Substituting this into (
3.4.3
) gives us

\displaystyle C_{\chi}(\mu_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{a+1}{4}\sum_{t\mid\widetilde{\mu_{0}}}c_{\chi}\left(\frac{{\mathrm{Nm}}(\widetilde{\mu_{0}}\alpha/t)}{\Delta},\frac{\widetilde{\mu_{0}}\alpha/t}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\right)=\frac{a+1}{2}\sum_{t\mid\widetilde{\mu_{0}}\alpha/\ell}c_{\chi}\left(\frac{{\mathrm{Nm}}(\widetilde{\mu_{0}}\alpha/t)/\ell^{2}}{\Delta},\frac{\widetilde{\mu_{0}}\alpha/(t\ell)}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{K/F}(\mathfrak{l}^{a})}{\rho_{K/F}(\tilde{\mathfrak{l}})}C_{\chi}(\widetilde{\mu_{0}}\alpha/\ell).

Notice that
(\widetilde{\mu_{0}}\alpha/\ell)
is not divisible by
\mathfrak{l}
.

From this, we can suppose that
\mu_{0}
is only divisible by split primes in
\mathcal{O}_{F}
, i.e.

(3.4.4)

(\mu_{0})=\prod\underline{\mathfrak{l}}^{\underline{a}}(\underline{\mathfrak{l}^{\prime}})^{\underline{b}}

where
\underline{\mathfrak{l}}=(\mathfrak{l}_{j})_{1\leq j\leq J}
with
{\mathrm{Nm}}(\underline{\mathfrak{l}})=\underline{\ell}=(\ell_{j})_{1\leq j\leq J}
a set of distinct rational primes and
\underline{a},\underline{b}\in\mathbb{N}^{J}
.
Here, the operations on vectors are carried out componentwisely and
\prod
sends a vector to the product of its components.
WLOG, we take
\underline{a}\leq\underline{b}
.
Then the divisors of
\mu_{0}
in
\mathbb{N}
are of the form

t(\underline{s}):=\prod\underline{\ell}^{\underline{s}},~\underline{s}\leq\underline{a}.

For each such
t(\underline{s})
, the ideals with norm
{\mathrm{Nm}}(\mu_{0}/t(\underline{s}))
are exactly given by

(3.4.5)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\underline{s}\in\mathbb{N}^{J}\\
\underline{s}\leq\underline{a}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\underline{r}\in\mathbb{Z}^{J}\\

Higher Green’s Function

In this section, we will state and prove the main result (Theorem

, the theta kernel becomes the Eisenstein series

4.1.
Differential Operators

For any
k\in{\tfrac{1}{2}}\mathbb{Z}
and
\tau=u+iv\in\mathbb{H}
, define the following differential operators on real-analytic
\mathbb{C}
-valued function

on
\mathbb{H}

(4.1.1)

\begin{split}R_{\tau,k}&=2i\partial_{\tau}+\frac{k}{v},\;L_{\tau,k}=-2iv^{2}\partial_{\overline{\tau}},\\
\Delta_{\tau,k}&=-R_{\tau,k-2}L_{\tau,k}=-L_{\tau,k+2}R_{\tau,k}-k=-v^{2}\left(\partial_{u}^{2}+\partial_{v}^{2}\right)+ikv(\partial_{u}+i\partial_{v}).\end{split}

We omit the subscript
\tau
when it is clear from the context.
For any

, we also know that

(4.1.2)

R^{n}_{k}f:=R_{k+2n-2}\circ R_{k+2n-4}\circ\dots\circ R_{k}f=\sum_{r=0}^{n}(2i)^{r}\binom{n}{r}\frac{(k+r)_{n-r}}{v^{n-r}}(\partial_{\tau}^{r}f)(\tau),

from equation (56) in
[
11
]

Suppose that

for any
\Gamma\subset{\mathrm{Mp}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
of finite index and representation
\rho:\Gamma\to{\mathrm{GL}}(V)
. Then applying the differential operators
R_{k}
and

componentwisely to

preserves its modularity by raising and lowering the weight by 2 respectively, i.e.
R_{k}(f)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{k+2,\rho}(\Gamma),L_{k}(f)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{k-2,\rho}(\Gamma)
.
Furthermore, it is a simple consequence of the commutation of differential operators that

(4.1.3)

\Delta_{k+2n}R^{n}_{k}f=(k+n-1)nR^{n}_{k}f

if
\Delta_{k}f=0
.
For
N,n\in\mathbb{N}
, recall the unary theta function
\theta_{N}(\tau,n)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{n+1/2}(\rho_{N})
from (

). It is easy to check that

(4.1.4)

R_{n+1/2}\theta_{N}(\tau;n)=-2\pi\theta_{N}(\tau;n+2).

From Stokes’ theorem, we can deduce the following consequence.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f,g
be smooth functions on
\mathbb{H}
and
S\subset\mathbb{H}
a compact domain.
Then

(4.1.5)

\begin{split}\int_{S}f(R_{-2-k}g)d\mu(\tau)+\int_{S}(R_{k}f)gd\mu(\tau)&=-\oint_{\partial S}\frac{fg}{v^{2}}d\overline{\tau},\\
\int_{S}f(L_{k_{1}}g)d\mu(\tau)+\int_{S}(L_{k_{2}}f)gd\mu(\tau)&=-\oint_{\partial S}{fg}{}d{\tau},\end{split}

with counterclockwise orientation on
\partial S
for any

Proof.

Note that
2i\frac{\partial(fg/v^{2})}{\partial\tau}=\frac{R_{-2}(fg)}{v^{2}}=v^{-2}((R_{k}f)g+f(R_{-k-2}g))

In order to maintain both holomorphicity and modularity of derivatives of modular forms, one can take linear combinations of products of repeatedly raised modular forms. This is the idea behind the following operation (see e.g. section 5.2 of part 1 of

5.

This section contains the construction of the preimage of the kernel

5.1.
Basic Setup

Let
F=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{\Delta})\subset\mathbb{R}
be a real quadratic field with fundamental discriminant
\Delta>0

Consider the lattice

(5.1.1)

M:=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&\lambda\\
\lambda^{\prime}&b\end{pmatrix}\in M_{2}(F):a,b\in\mathbb{Z},\lambda\in\mathcal{O}_{F}\right\}

The dual lattice

M^{\vee}:=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&\lambda\\
\lambda^{\prime}&b\end{pmatrix}\in M_{2}(F):a,b\in\mathbb{Z},\lambda\in\mathfrak{d}^{-1}\right\}.

Then the lattice
L^{-}=(\mathcal{O}_{F},-{\mathrm{Nm}})

\lambda\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}0&\lambda\\
\lambda^{\prime}&0\end{pmatrix}

as
\Gamma
-modules with respect to the Weil representation

in section
3.1

We also denote

\lambda^{\prime}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right)=-{\mathrm{Nm}}(\lambda)
for
\lambda\in F
.

Via the two embeddings of

into
\mathbb{R}
, the real quadratic space

is isomorphic to
(M_{2}(\mathbb{R}),\det)

As in section
A
, we identify
M_{2}(\mathbb{R})
with
\mathbb{R}^{2,2}
and the symmetric space
{\mathcal{D}}_{V}
with
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.
For our purpose, it suffices here to consider the diagonal
\mathbb{H}\subset\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}

For
a,b,c\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the polynomial
p_{a,b,c}
on

defined in (
A.1.9

{\mathcal{A}}_{a+b-c,\rho_{M}}

Suppose

(5.1.2)

\Phi(z;p_{a,b,c},f):=\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{{\mathcal{F}}_{T}}\langle f(\tau),\Theta_{M}(\tau,z;p_{a,b,c})\rangle v^{a+b-c}d\mu(\tau).

We would like to express its Fourier expansion in terms of

[

.
The result is as follows.

Proposition 5.1

In the notations above, the function

(5.1.3)

\begin{split}y^{-1}\Phi(z;p_{a,b,c},f)&=c(f;p_{a,b,c})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq h\leq h^{-}\leq c\\

Lemma 5.2

For

(5.1.4)

5.2.
Lift 1

\Phi(z;p_{0,1,0},\vartheta_{\chi})=-\sqrt{2}{y}\left\{\begin{split}2c_{0}&+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\mathfrak{d}^{-1}\\

When

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}_{0}(0,T,T^{\prime};b)=\int_{1}^{T}\int_{1}^{T^{\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\mathbb{Z}+h_{1}\\

\displaystyle{\mathrm{p}_{k,b}(0,c,v)}c(r,h_{2},v;b)e^{-2\pi v\left(\Delta r_{1}^{2}+B^{2}r_{2}^{2}+\Delta B^{2}r_{3}^{2}+c^{2}\right)}\frac{dvdy}{v^{k-b}y^{k}}.

\displaystyle c(r,h_{2},v;b)e^{-2\pi v\left(\Delta r_{1}^{2}+B^{2}r_{2}^{2}+\Delta B^{2}r_{3}^{2}\right)}\times

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z},2nc=m}

\displaystyle{\mathrm{p}_{k,b}(ny,c,v)}e^{-\frac{\pi y^{2}n^{2}}{v}-2\pi my-2\pi c^{2}v}\frac{dv}{v^{k-b}}\frac{dy}{y^{k}}.

for all

Legendre Polynomials

For

(n+1)P_{n+1}(x)=(2n+1)xP_{n}(x)-nP_{n-1}(x),\;P_{0}(x)=1,P_{1}(x)=x,

(A.2.1)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n}(x)t^{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2xt+t^{2}}}.

(A.2.2)

\binom{a}{m}:=\frac{a(a-1)\dots(a-m+1)}{m!}

and
a\in\mathbb{R}
