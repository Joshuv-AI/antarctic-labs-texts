Multiplicatively spectrum-preserving maps on C ∗ C^{*} -algebras
Michiya Mori, Shiho Oi

Abstract
. We study surjective maps between the sets of all self-adjoint elements of unital C ∗ C^{*} -algebras which satisfy the multiplicatively spectrum-preserving property. We show that such maps are characterized by Jordan isomorphisms and central symmetries. This is an answer to a problem posed by Molnár.

1.
Introduction

Let
A
and
B
be unital
C^{*}
-algebras and let
\sigma(\cdot)
denote the spectrum of elements.
For any
a,b\in A
, we define the Jordan product
\circ
by
a\circ b=\frac{1}{2}(ab+ba)
. We denote the set of all self-adjoint elements of
A
(resp.
B
) by
A_{sa}
(resp.
B_{sa}
). As we have
a\circ b\in A_{sa}
for any
a,b\in A_{sa}
,
the self-adjoint part
A_{sa}
of any
C^{*}
-algebra
A
is a Jordan algebra, which is a real commutative (non-associative) algebra whose product
\circ
satisfies
a\circ(b\circ a^{2})=(a\circ b)\circ a^{2}
. We call a real-linear map
J\colon A_{sa}\to B_{sa}
a Jordan homomorphism if
J(a\circ b)=J(a)\circ J(b)
holds for any
a,b\in A_{sa}
. Moreover, if
J\colon A_{sa}\to B_{sa}
is bijective, then it is called a Jordan isomorphism. For any Jordan homomorphism
J\colon A_{sa}\to B_{sa}
, one may uniquely obtain the complexification of
J
, which is a complex-linear map
J_{\mathbb{C}}
from
A
into
B
that satisfies
J=J_{\mathbb{C}}
on
A_{sa}
.
Then
J_{\mathbb{C}}
is a Jordan
*
-isomorphism, that is, it satisfies
J_{\mathbb{C}}(a\circ b)=J_{\mathbb{C}}(a)\circ J_{\mathbb{C}}(b)
, and
J_{\mathbb{C}}(a^{*})=J_{\mathbb{C}}(a)^{*}
for any
a,b\in A
.
Jordan
*
-isomorphisms appear ubiquitously when studying mappings between
C^{*}
-algebras preserving a certain structure. See for example
[
7
]
.
On the other hand, the quantum-mechanical observables are expected to be self-adjoint in quantum theory. From this perspective, studying Jordan isomorphisms between the self-adjoint parts of
C^{*}
-algebras is equally interesting.

Størmer
[
8
, Theorem 3.3]
showed that any Jordan
*
-homomorphism between two unital
C^{*}
-algebras is the sum of a
*
-homomorphism and a
*
-anti-homomorphism. This implies that any Jordan isomorphism
J\colon A_{sa}\to B_{sa}
satisfies

\sigma(J(a)J(b))=\sigma(ab),\quad a,b\in A_{sa}.

(1)

For a detailed proof, see the first part of the proof of Theorem 2.2 in
[
6
]
.
We call (
1
) the multiplicatively spectrum-preserving property of a Jordan isomorphism.

Molnár initiated the study of the multiplicatively spectrum-preserving property in
[
5
]
. In
[
6
]
, he considered whether a surjection (not assumed to be linear) between self-adjoint parts of unital
C^{*}
-algebras which has the multiplicatively spectrum-preserving property induces a Jordan isomorphism or not.
He obtained a positive solution in the contexts of von Neumann algebras (
[
6
, Theorem 2.2]
) and standard
C^{*}
-algebras (
[
6
, Theorem 2.5]
). Indeed, in order to prove the theorem
[
6
, Theorem 2.2]
, he applied the lemmas
[
6
, Lemma 2.3]
(
[
7
, Lemma 22]
) and
[
6
, Lemma 2.4]
(
[
7
, Lemma 23]
), which provide spectral characterizations of elements. However, he noted that the proofs of the lemmas were valid for only von Neumann algebras. Consequently, he posed a problem of whether the theorem
[
6
, Theorem 2.2]
can survive for general
C^{*}
-algebras (see
[
6
, p.194]
,
[
7
, Problem 4]
).
It can be seen from
[
7
, p.35]
that the answer is yes if
one may obtain extensions of the lemmas
[
6
, Lemma 2.3]
(
[
7
, Lemma 22]
) and
[
6
, Lemma 2.4]
(
[
7
, Lemma 23]
) to general
C^{*}
-algebras. The purpose of this paper is to actually carry it out in Lemma
3
and Lemma
6
below. This gives a positive answer to the problem, which reads as follows.
Recall that a self-adjoint unitary is called a symmetry.

Theorem 1
.

Let
A
and
B
be unital
C^{*}
-algebras. Suppose that a surjective map
\phi\colon A_{sa}\to B_{sa}
satisfies

\sigma(\phi(a)\phi(b))=\sigma(ab),\ \ a,b\in A_{sa}.

Then there is a central symmetry
s\in B_{sa}
and a Jordan isomorphism
J\colon A_{sa}\to B_{sa}
such that

\phi(a)=sJ(a),\quad a\in A_{sa}.

Note that the problem we study is one of two problems posed by Molnár in
[
6
, p.194]
. Another problem has an affirmative solution, too (see
[
7
, p.36]
and
[
4
, Theorem 5.5]
).
Also note that multiplicatively spectrum-preserving mappings defined on the whole algebra (instead of the self-adjoint part) are studied in
[
1
]
in the setting of unital semisimple Banach algebras.

2.
Results

We present notations to be used throughout the paper. For a unital
C^{*}
-algebra
A
,
1
stands for its unit.
The symbols
A_{+}
and
A_{+}^{-1}
denote the set of all positive elements and the set of all positive invertible elements of
A
, respectively.
For
x\in A_{sa}
, let
x=x_{+}-x_{-}
denote the decomposition into its positive and negative parts. Note that
x_{+}x_{-}=x_{-}x_{+}=0
holds.
If
x\in A_{+}
, then
x^{1/2}\in A_{+}
stands for its positive square root.

Lemma 2
.

Let
x\in A_{+}
and
y\in A
.
If
xy=0
, then
x^{1/2}y=0
.
If
yx=0
, then
yx^{1/2}=0
.

Proof.

This follows from the basic fact that there is a sequence of polynomials
P_{n}
satisfying
P_{n}(0)=0
and
P_{n}(x)\to x^{1/2}
as
n\to\infty
.
∎

Throughout the paper, we use the following well-known fact several times.

\sigma(ab)\setminus\{0\}=\sigma(ba)\setminus\{0\}\text{ whenever }a,b\in A.

(2)

This is called Jacobson’s lemma.

The next lemma is an extension of
[
6
, Lemma 2.3]
(
[
7
, Lemma 22]
). This might be of independent interest.

Lemma 3
.

Let
x\in A_{sa}
. Then the following two conditions are equivalent.

•

\sigma(xy)\subset\mathbb{R}
for every
y\in A_{sa}
.

•

x_{+}yx_{-}=0
for every
y\in A
.

Proof.

Firstly we assume that
x_{+}yx_{-}=0
for every
y\in A
.
Let
y\in A_{sa}
.
By Lemma
2
, we have
x_{+}-x_{-}=(x_{+}^{1/2}+x_{-}^{1/2})(x_{+}^{1/2}-x_{-}^{1/2})
and
x_{+}^{1/2}yx_{-}^{1/2}=0
.
We also have
x_{-}^{1/2}yx_{+}^{1/2}=(x_{+}^{1/2}yx_{-}^{1/2})^{*}=0
.
Thus we get

xy=(x_{+}-x_{-})y=(x_{+}^{1/2}+x_{-}^{1/2})(x_{+}^{1/2}-x_{-}^{1/2})y

and

(x_{+}^{1/2}-x_{-}^{1/2})y(x_{+}^{1/2}+x_{-}^{1/2})=x_{+}^{1/2}yx_{+}^{1/2}-x_{-}^{1/2}yx_{-}^{1/2}\in A_{sa}.

Combining these with (
2
), we get
\sigma(xy)\subset\mathbb{R}
.

Conversely, assume that
\sigma(xy)\subset\mathbb{R}
for every
y\in A_{sa}
.
Let
y\in A
. We prove
x_{+}yx_{-}=0
.
It suffices to consider the case
y\in A_{sa}
.
In this case, we have
z:=x_{+}^{1/2}yx_{-}^{1/2}+x_{-}^{1/2}yx_{+}^{1/2}\in A_{sa}
.
From
\sigma(xz)\subset\mathbb{R}
,

xz=(x_{+}-x_{-})z=(x_{+}^{1/2}+x_{-}^{1/2})(x_{+}^{1/2}-x_{-}^{1/2})z,

(x_{+}^{1/2}-x_{-}^{1/2})z(x_{+}^{1/2}+x_{-}^{1/2})=x_{+}yx_{-}-x_{-}yx_{+}\in iA_{sa},

and (
2
), we get
\sigma(x_{+}yx_{-}-x_{-}yx_{+})=\{0\}
and
x_{+}yx_{-}=x_{-}yx_{+}
.
Hence
x_{+}yx_{-}
is self-adjoint and

(x_{+}yx_{-})^{2}=(x_{+}yx_{-})(x_{+}yx_{-})=x_{+}y\cdot 0\cdot yx_{-}=0,

so we obtain
x_{+}yx_{-}=0
as desired.
∎

Our proofs of Lemma
4
and Lemma
6
are similar to the proof of Theorem 2.2 in
[

]
, which shows that for any semisimple Banach algebra
X

\sigma(ax)=\sigma(bx)
for all elements

Lemma 4
.

Let
a,b\in A_{sa}
. Suppose that
\sigma(ay)=\sigma(by)
for any
y\in A_{+}^{-1}
. Let
x\in A_{sa}
and let
\lambda
be a real number with
|\lambda|>\lVert x\rVert
. Then
\lambda\in\sigma(x+a)
if and only if
\lambda\in\sigma(x+b)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\lambda>\lVert x\rVert
. Then

\lambda-x\in A_{+}^{-1}
. We have
\lambda-(x+a)=(\lambda-x)[1-(\lambda-x)^{-1}a]
. This implies that
\lambda\in\sigma(x+a)\iff 1\in\sigma((\lambda-x)^{-1}a)\iff 1\in\sigma((\lambda-x)^{-1}b)\iff\lambda\in\sigma(x+b)
.
Suppose that
-\lambda>\lVert x\rVert
. Then

-\lambda+x\in A_{+}^{-1}
. Thus the conclusion follows from a similar procedure as described above.
∎

Corollary 5
.

Let
a,b\in A_{sa}
. Suppose that
\sigma(ay)=\sigma(by)
for any
y\in A_{+}^{-1}
. Let
x\in A_{sa}
. Then either
\max\{\lVert x+a\rVert,\lVert x+b\rVert\}\leq\lVert x\rVert
or
\lVert x+a\rVert=\lVert x+b\rVert
holds.

The next lemma extends
[
6
, Lemma 2.4]
(
[
7
, Lemma 23]
).

Lemma 6
.

Let
a,b\in A_{sa}
. If
\sigma(ay)=\sigma(by)
for any
y\in A_{+}^{-1}
, then we have
a=b
.

Proof.

We prove by induction that

\lVert nb-(n-1)a\rVert\leq\lVert b\rVert\text{ and }\lVert n(b-a)\rVert\leq\lVert b\rVert

(3)

for
n\geq 1
.
Applying Corollary
5
with
x=-b\in A_{sa}
, we get either
\lVert-b+a\rVert\leq\lVert-b\rVert
or
\lVert-b+a\rVert=\lVert-b+b\rVert=0
. In both cases, we get
\lVert b-a\rVert\leq\lVert b\rVert
.
Thus we get (
3
) when
n=1
.

Let
k\geq 1
and suppose that (
3
) holds with
n=k
. Applying Corollary
5
with
x=k(b-a)\in A_{sa}
, we get either

\lVert k(b-a)+b\rVert\leq\lVert k(b-a)\rVert
or
\lVert k(b-a)+a\rVert=\lVert k(b-a)+b\rVert
.
Therefore, in both cases, the assumption implies

\lVert(k+1)b-ka\rVert\leq\lVert b\rVert.

(4)

Applying Corollary
5
with
x=ka-(k+1)b\in A_{sa}
, we have either

\lVert ka-(k+1)b+a\rVert\leq\lVert ka-(k+1)b\rVert
or
\lVert ka-(k+1)b+a\rVert=\lVert ka-(k+1)b+b\rVert
.
In both cases, the assumption together with (
4
) shows

\lVert(k+1)(b-a)\rVert\leq\lVert b\rVert.

Thus we have obtained the equation
\lVert n(b-a)\rVert\leq\lVert b\rVert
for any
n\geq 1
. This implies
a=b
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Let
s=\phi(1)
. Then
s\in B_{sa}
. We have
\sigma(s^{2})=\sigma(1)=\{1\}
, thus we obtain that
\sigma(s)\subset\{1,-1\}
, so
s
is a symmetry. As
\phi
is a surjection, for any
y\in B_{sa}
there is
x\in A_{sa}
such that
\phi(x)=y
. We have
\sigma(sy)=\sigma(\phi(1)\phi(x))=\sigma(x)\subset\mathbb{R}
. By Lemma
3
, we obtain
s_{+}ys_{-}=0
for any
y\in B
. For any
y\in B_{sa}
, we get
s_{+}ys_{-}=0
and
s_{-}ys_{+}=(s_{+}ys_{-})^{*}=0
, thus

sy=(s_{+}-s_{-})y(s_{+}+s_{-})=s_{+}ys_{+}-s_{-}ys_{-}\in B_{sa}.

It follows that
sy=(sy)^{*}=ys
. Hence
s
is central.

We define a map
T\colon A_{sa}\to B_{sa}
by
T(x)=s\phi(x)
for any
x\in A_{sa}
.
For any
y\in B_{sa}
, as
sy\in B_{sa}
and
\phi
is surjective, there is
x\in A_{sa}
such that
\phi(x)=sy
. Thus
T(x)=ssy=y
.
Therefore,
T\colon A_{sa}\to B_{sa}
is a surjection. We have

\sigma(T(x)T(y))=\sigma(s\phi(x)s\phi(y))=\sigma(\phi(x)\phi(y))=\sigma(xy)

(5)

for any
x,y\in A_{sa}
.
By taking
y=1
, we get
\sigma(T(x))=\sigma(x)
for any
x\in A_{sa}
.
It follows that
x\in A_{+}^{-1}\iff T(x)\in B_{+}^{-1}
.
Therefore,
T|_{A_{+}^{-1}}\colon A_{+}^{-1}\to B_{+}^{-1}
is a surjective map which satisfies (
5
).

For any
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
we have

\{1\}=\sigma(xx^{-1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{multiplicative}}}{{=}}\sigma(T(x)T(x^{-1}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{abba}}}{{=}}\sigma(T(x)^{1/2}T(x^{-1})T(x)^{1/2}).

Thus we obtain
T(x)^{1/2}T(x^{-1})T(x)^{1/2}=1
and

T(x)^{-1}=T(x^{-1}).

(6)

It follows that

\begin{split}\sigma(x^{-{1/2}}yx^{-{1/2}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{abba}}}{{=}}\sigma(yx^{-1})&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{multiplicative}}}{{=}}\sigma(T(y)T(x^{-1}))\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{-1}}}{{=}}\sigma(T(y)T(x)^{-1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{abba}}}{{=}}\sigma(T(x)^{-{1/2}}T(y)T(x)^{-{1/2}})\end{split}

for any pair
x,y\in A_{+}^{-1}
.
Combining this with a fact that the Thompson metric
d_{T}
on
A_{+}^{-1}
satisfies
d_{T}(x,y)=\|\log(x^{-{1/2}}yx^{-{1/2}})\|
for any
x,y\in A_{+}^{-1}
, we have
T|_{A_{+}^{-1}}\colon A_{+}^{-1}\to B_{+}^{-1}
is a surjective Thompson isometry with
T(1)=1
. For any
t>0
and
x,y\in A_{+}^{-1}
, we see that
\sigma(T(x)T(y))=\sigma(xy)
and
\sigma(txy)=\sigma(T(tx)T(y))
by (
5
), hence

\sigma(tT(x)T(y))=\sigma(txy)=\sigma(T(tx)T(y)).

Thus, Lemma
6
shows that
tT(x)=T(tx)
for any
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
. In other words,
T|_{A_{+}^{-1}}
is positive homogeneous.
By applying
[
3
, Theorem 9]
(
[
7
, Theorem 8]
) with
T|_{A_{+}^{-1}}
, we see that there is a Jordan
*
-isomorphism
J\colon A\to B
such that
T=J
on
A_{+}^{-1}
. Fix
a\in A_{sa}
. For any
y\in B_{+}^{-1}
, there is
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
such that
T(x)=J(x)=y
. We have

\sigma(J(a)y)=\sigma(J(a)J(x))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{jajb}}}{{=}}\sigma(ax)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{multiplicative}}}{{=}}\sigma(T(a)T(x))=\sigma(T(a)y).

Since
J(a),T(a)\in B_{sa}
, Lemma
6
implies that
J(a)=T(a)
. Thus
\phi(a)=sJ(a)
for any
a\in A_{sa}
.
∎
