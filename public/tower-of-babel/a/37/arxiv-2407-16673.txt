Reconstructing dynamical systems as zero-noise limits
Suddhasattwa Das

Abstract
A dynamical system may be defined by a simple transition law - such as a map or a vector field. The objective of most learning techniques is to reconstruct this dynamic transition law. This is a major shortcoming, as most dynamic properties of interest are asymptotic properties such as an attractor or invariant measure. Thus approximating the dynamical law may not be sufficient to approximate these asymptotic properties. This article presents a method of representing a discrete-time deterministic dynamical system as the zero-noise limit of a Markov process. The Markov process approximation is completely data-driven. Besides proving a low-noise approximation of the dynamics the process also approximates the invariant set, via the support of its stationary measures. Thus invariant sets of arbitrary dynamical systems, even with complicated non-smooth topology, can be approximated by this technique. Under further assumptions, we show that the technique performs a convergent statistical approximation as well as approximations of true orbits.



Key words.
Markov kernel, Markov process, convex approximation, invariant measure

Dynamical systems are known to have multiple, even uncountably infinitely many co-existing invariant sets

Given any point

-limit set is the collection of accumulation points of the sequence
\left\{f^{n}z\right\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
. It is thus the collection of all those points which are approached arbitrarily closely infinitely many times by the trajectory of

. An
\omega
-limit set can be shown to be an invariant closed set itself. The

of an invariant set

is the set of points

whose
\omega

. In other words, it is the collection of points which converge towards

as the dynamics enfolds. We will call the set

visible

\mathcal{M}
.

The concepts of visibility and stability are strongly tied to the outcome of physical experiments. For simplicity, a physical experiment may be interpreted as a simulation of the system by randomly selecting an initial point
z
. The location of this initial point is evenly distributed with respect to some volume measure on
\mathcal{M}
which is independent from the map
f
itself. The outcome of such an experiment will be an orbit
\left\{f^{n}z\right\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
. Being visible means that with positive probability, this orbit converges to
X
. Note that the question of probability arises because we view the selection of the initial point

as a random event. Similarly, being stable means that
X
will continue to remain visible if the initial point is slightly perturbed from
X
. Stability implies visibility, but the converse is not true. The focus of our numerical methods is the issue of visibility and stability for the reconstructed dynamics.

One can have a similar discussion in a measure theoretic context. A measure
\mu

\mathcal{M}
is said to be

is the same as its push-forward

. Note that as the point

\mu
. One can then similarly define the properties of stability and visibility for the measure
\mu

A dynamical system may have an infinite collection

invariant measures. Moreover, any convex combination of invariant measures is again an invariant measure. The sub-collection

There is a manifold

, an invariant ergodic measure

Assumption

Assumption 2

There is an injective map

\mu

Assumptions

Preserving the stability under a reconstruction has been an elusive goal in most learning techniques. We present a means of approximating the dynamics as an inherently stochastic system. This will lead to a loss of the determinism of
f
, but shall guarantee an approximation of
\mu
and
X
which is both close and stable. This notion of stability is made more precise using Arnold’s paradigm
[
10

Let
\left(\Omega,\Sigma\right)
be a measurable space, and
\tau:\Omega\to\Omega
be a measurable map. Now suppose there is a map

. For each
\omega\in\Omega
, one gets a different self-map

on
\Omega
. If the choice of
\omega
is random, then

provides a parametric description of a stochastic process on
\mathcal{M}
. Now consider the following dynamical system on the product space
\Omega\times\mathcal{M}
:

\begin{split}\omega_{n+1}&=\tau(\omega_{n})\\
y_{n+1}&=g\left(\omega_{n},y_{n}\right)\end{split}

(2)

This is called a
skew-product
system as the first variable evolves independently and continues to drive the dynamics in
\Omega
. Such skew-product systems (
2
) provide a universal description of discrete-time stochastic dynamics as a deterministic dynamical system
[
10
]
. The stochasticity in the dynamics of the

\omega_{0}
.

Suppose that the deterministic map
f
from Assumption

corresponds to

for some point
\omega_{0}\in\Omega
. Now suppose that the function

depends on a third parameter

is an invariant measure for the

converge weakly to the Dirac-delta measure

(z.n.l.) of the parameterized family

of stochastic processes. Following
[
11

, the ergodic system

is said to be

if for any parameterized family
\left\{g_{t}\;:\;t>0\right\}

converges to

must converge weakly to

The projection

characterizes the spread in the parameter space

\mu
. The concept of stochastic stability provides a rigorous platform on which to assess the visibility and stability of ergodic measures. Stochastic stability is hard to be established in general, and has only been demonstrated for a special class of ergodic systems called

-systems
[
12
]
.

Goal

Reconstruct the deterministic dynamics
f
in Assumption
1
as a parameterized Markov stochastic process of which

is a zero-noise limit. The reconstruction procedure would be entirely data-driven, and based on Assumption

Our plan of approximation is to construct a special instance of (

. The driving dynamics will be modeled as a finite-state discrete-time Markov process. This is part of a broader effort to find alternative ways to describe a dynamical system, instead of the dynamics law alone. The transformation law induces many other phenomenon

6

such as invariant sets, almost Markov processes and the Koopman operator. Many of these properties are asymptotic, hence an approximation of the dynamics law alone does not guarantee an approximation of these properties. We shall prove that

Suppose there is a dynamical system as in Assumption

is the zero-noise limit of a stochastic dynamics on

. Moreover, the stochastic dynamics may be chosen so that the support of any stationary measure of the process is within (Hausdorff) distance

Corollary

as a consequence of our main technical result Theorem

. A Markov process is stochastic while our targeted system is deterministic. A stochastic and a deterministic process may only be compared in terms of their invariant measures, and their statistical properties. Chaotic dynamical systems have a statistical profile which is extremely complicated
[

,

,
16
,
17
]
and difficult to estimate reliably. Theoretical results and numerical experiments seem to suggest that a small amount of noise retains the statistical properties of a dynamical system. The loss of determinism is compensated for by an easier mathematical description
(

,

,
20

Corollary 2

Let the same assumptions as Corollary

Corollaries

If the ergodic system

Corollaries

2  The technique

Throughout this section we assume Assumptions
1
and
2
. We shall also assume

Assumption 3
.

There is a minimal, finite measurable cover
\mathcal{U}=\left\{U_{i}\;:\;1\leq i\leq m\right\}
for
h(X)
.

A cover is called
minimal
if it has no proper sub-cover. The sets
U_{i}
will be called the cells of the cover. A cover is called
measurable
if its cells are measurable sets with non-zero
\mu
-measure. Open covers provide a coarse graining of the phase space or invariant space. Our goal is to keep track of the transitions between the cells to obtain an outer approximation of the dynamics. This technique has been extensively studied
(
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
, e.g.)
in the special case when
\mathcal{U}
is a partition. Note that such a cover creates a cover
\left\{V_{i}:=h^{-1}(U_{i})\;:\;1\leq i\leq m\right\}
for
X
in
\mathcal{M}
.

Transitions

Let
h_{*}\mu
denote the push-forward of the measure
\mu
onto
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Given any cover as above, one can compute transition probabilities
\tilde{\beta}_{j}
for each cell
j
:

\tilde{\beta}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad\left(\tilde{\beta}_{j}\right)_{i}:=\mu\left(f(x)\in V_{i}\,\rvert\,x\in V_{j}\right)=\mu\left(f^{-1}\left(V_{i}\right)\cap V_{j}\right)/\mu\left(V_{j}\right).

(3)

We normalize this vector of conditional probabilities based at the cell
j
, into a probability distribution

\beta_{j}\in\Prob\left\{1,\ldots,M\right\},\left(\beta_{j}\right)_{i}:=\left(\tilde{\beta}_{j}\right)_{i}/\sum_{l=1}^{M}\left(\tilde{\beta}_{j}\right)_{l}.

(4)

Our construction shall contain as a sub-system a discrete state Markov process on the set
\mathcal{S}:=\left\{1,\ldots,m\right\}
. The power-set of
\mathcal{S}
is its assigned sigma-algebra. Consider the
m\times m
matrix
\mathbb{P}
whose
j
-th column is
\beta_{j}
. Let
\vec{1}_{m}
denote the
m
-dimensional vector with all entries
1
. By design we have
\mathbb{P}^{T}\vec{1}_{m}=\vec{1}_{m}
, i.e., the matrix
\mathbb{P}
is column-stochastic.
Any probability measure
\alpha
on
\mathcal{S}
is a non-negative entried
m
-dimensional vector whose entries sum to
1
. The latter condition can be stated concisely as
\left\langle\vec{1}_{m},\alpha\right\rangle=1
. Now note that

\left\langle\vec{1}_{m},\mathbb{P}\alpha\right\rangle=\left\langle\mathbb{P}^{*}\vec{1}_{m},\alpha\right\rangle=\left\langle\vec{1}_{m},\alpha\right\rangle=1.

Thus the matrix
\mathbb{P}
converts any probability measure
\beta
into another probability measure. Thus this matrix can be interpreted as a Markov transition function on
\mathcal{S}
. For each
j\in\mathcal{S}
we shall denote by
p(j)
the (discrete) probability measure
\beta_{j}
. Note that
p(j)
also corresponds to the
j
-th column of
\mathbb{P}
.

The Markov process on
\mathcal{S}
generated by
\mathbb{P}
has been shown to approximate statistical properties of the original ergodic system
(\Omega,\mu,f)
under a variety of different assumptions
[
20
]
. A condition typically relied on is quasi-compactness
(
26
, se)
of the transfer operator
\mathcal{P}
. The latter is a property borne by the spectrum of
\mathcal{P}
. If
\mathcal{P}
is quasi-compact, then it has a unique fixed point. Moreover all ergodic sums converge uniquely to the unique fixed point
(
27
, Thm 2.3)
. Techniques such as the simplicial triangulation method
[
27
]
are shown to provide good convergence to the fixed stationary density. Another important property concerns the iterations of
\mathcal{P}
and is said to be of
Lasota Yorke type

(
28
, e.g.)
. Simple techniques such as continuous piecewise linear approximations are shown to be convergent
(
29
, see)
. However we avoid the need for any of these conditions as our goal is a z.n.l. approximation. A z.n.l. approximation is not necessarily a stochastic approximation. We intend to do so by constructing a Markov process not on the discrete space
\mathcal{S}
alone but on the product space
\mathcal{S}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The Markov process

Recall that by Assumption
2

h
is an injective map. Thus there is at least one function
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that it satisfies (
1
) when restricted to
h(X)
. We now describe a low-noise approximation of
F
. The key components in the design are the functions
\phi_{j\to i}
for various transitions
j\to i\in\mathcal{S}
. Consider the subsets

X_{j\to i}:=h\left(V_{j}\cap f^{-1}\left(V_{i}\right)\right)=U_{j}\cap F^{-1}(U_{i}).

We define
\phi_{j\to i}
to be any continuous extension of
F
restricted to
X_{j\to i}
. Being extensions, they satisfy

\phi_{j\to i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{U_{i}},\quad\phi_{j\to i}(x)=f(x),\quad\mu-a.e.\,x\in X_{j\to i}.

(5)

The functions
\phi_{j\to i}
has two important features. Firstly, its range is confined to the closure of
U_{i}
. Secondly it agrees with the original map on a subset of the domain.
We now have all the components for the Markov process - the transition probabilities
\beta_{j}
(
4
) and the maps
\phi_{j\to i}
(
5
). They create the following Markov process on the product space
\mathcal{S}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\boxed{\begin{aligned} s_{n+1}&\sim p(s_{n})\\
x_{n+1}&=\phi_{s_{n}\to s_{n+1}}(x_{n})\end{aligned}}

(6)

The functions
\phi_{s_{n}\to s_{n+1}}
are continuous functions from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
U_{s_{n+1}}
, one of the cells in the cover
\mathcal{U}
. This is a skew product system in which the first set of coordinates (namely
s
) evolves autonomously, and drives the second set of coordinates. This particular type of skew products is closely related to
step-skew products
which have been used to demonstrate a variety of robust and non-intuitive behavior in dynamical systems
(
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
, e.g.)
. To specify the Markov transition function for this system, consider the map

G:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad(s,s^{\prime},x)\mapsto\phi_{s\to s^{\prime}}(x).

(7)

This function contains the collective actions of the transition functions
\phi_{j\to i}
. Note that for each fixed
(x,s)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
G(s,\cdot,x)
is a function from
\mathcal{S}
into
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Thus this function pushes forward the probability measure
p(s)
on
\mathcal{S}
into the probability measure
G(s,\cdot,x)_{*}p(s)
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This leads to the following Markov transition function on the space
\mathcal{S}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

Q:\mathcal{S}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\Prob\left(\mathcal{S}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\right),\quad(s,x)\mapsto p(s)\times\left[G(s,\cdot,x)_{*}p(s)\right].

(8)

The Markov process (
6
) thus has the transition function
Q
in (
8
).

The
y
-coordinates of the Markov process takes a random walk in the neighborhood
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}:=\cup_{i=1}^{m}U_{i}
of
h(X)
. To establish this walk as a Markov process we make the simple assumption

Assumption 4
.

The cover
\mathcal{U}
from Assumption
3
is a partition, i.e., its cells are disjoint.

Under this assumption
\mathcal{U}
is a disjoint union of the
U_{j}
and each point
x\in\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
lies within a unique cell of the partition. We show later in Lemma
4.2
that the Markov transition on
\mathcal{S}
has a unique stationary measure
\nu
. The Markov process on
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
has the transition function :

\hat{f}:\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\to\Prob\left(\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\right),\quad\hat{f}(x):=\sum_{i:j\to i}\delta_{\phi_{j\to i}(x)}\mathbb{P}_{j,i},\quad\mbox{ if }x\in U_{j}.

(9)

Equation (
6
) presents the Markov process that approximates any dynamical system using an arbitrary cover. If that cover is a partition then the Markov process on the joint space
\mathcal{S}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be interpreted as a Markov process (
9
) on
\mathcal{U}
, which is a neighborhood of
h(X)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Note that
\hat{f}
in (
9
) assigns to each point
x
in
\mathcal{U}
a discrete measure, i.e., a weighted sum of atomic probability measures. Equations (
6
) and (
9
) are both equivalent descriptions. The former will be the target of our numerical approximation, whereas the latter will be useful in the convergence analysis.

Recall that the map
F
in (
1
) is defined uniquely only on the image set
h(X)
. In a data-driven scheme, in the absence of
\mathcal{M}
, our goal is to approximate
F
as well as
h(X)
by confining
\mathcal{U}
to some small neighborhood of
h(X)
.
Given any cover such as
\mathcal{U}
, its
mesh-size
is the largest diameter of any of its cell
\mathcal{U}
. Thus the mesh size represents the spread of the cover around the target set
h(X)
. In the following statement, given any set
A
and
\delta>0
,
\bar{B}(A,\delta)
shall denote the closed
\delta
-neighborhood of the set
A
.

Theorem 4

(Markov approximation of dynamics)
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
2
and
3
hold. Suppose that the mesh-size of the cover is some
\delta>0
. Then

1.

Let
\mu_{\delta}
be any stationary measure of the Markov process (
6
). Then

\support(\mu_{\delta})\subset\bar{B}\left(h(X),\delta\right),\quad h(X)\subset\bar{B}\left(\support(\mu_{\delta}),2\delta\right).

2.

Suppose Assumption
4
also holds. Then the projection of the ergodic system
(\Omega,f,\mu)
via
h
is the zero noise limit of the stochastic dynamics (
6
), as
\delta
approaches zero.

Theorem
4
is proved in Section
4
. Theorem
4
(ii) thus establishes any dynamical system universally as a zero noise limit of a stochastic dynamical system. The first claim implies that the support of the
\mu_{\delta}
converges in Hausdorff metric to the targeted attractor
X
. This was one one of our primary goals, creating a dynamical system whose invariant region as well as iterations are a close approximation to the original. We next look at alternate way of describing the driving dynamics, as a deterministic system instead of a Markov process.

3  Symbolic dynamics

Throughout this section we assume Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
and
4
. There is an index function
\mathfrak{j}:X\to\mathcal{S}
which assigns to every
x\in X
the number
j=\mathfrak{j}(x)
which is the index of the cell of the partition in which it lies. Then for every
x\in X
and integer
N>0
there is a sequence of indexes

\mathcal{U}(x,N):=\left(\mathfrak{j}\circ h\circ f^{0}(x),\ldots,\mathfrak{j}\circ h\circ f^{N}(x)\right),

which records the sequence of cells the point visits. We call this the

N
-itinerary
of the point.

Symbolic sequences

Given any metric space
\mathcal{A}
, one can associate to it a metric space called a
symbolic space
. Its points are all infinite sequences
x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\ldots
of points from
\mathcal{A}
. The metric structure is given by

\dist\left(\left\{a_{n}\right\}_{n=0}^{\infty}\,,\,\left\{a^{\prime}_{n}\right\}_{n=0}^{\infty}\right):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{-n}\dist_{\mathcal{A}}(a_{n},a^{\prime}_{n}).

We denote this metric space simply by
\mathcal{A}^{\omega}
. This space has a natural transform on it, called the
shift
-map :

\sigma:\mathcal{A}^{\omega}\to\mathcal{A}^{\omega},\quad\left\{a_{n}\right\}_{n=0}^{\infty}\mapsto\left\{a_{n+1}\right\}_{n=0}^{\infty}.

Of special interest is the case when
\mathcal{A}
is a discrete set. In that case
\mathcal{A}
can be equipped with the Dirac-delta metric. This is the metric
\delta(x,y)
which equals
0
if
x=y
, and is
1
otherwise. With this choice the metric on its symbolic space becomes

\dist\left(\left\{a_{n}\right\}_{n=0}^{\infty}\,,\,\left\{a^{\prime}_{n}\right\}_{n=0}^{\infty}\right):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{-n}\delta(a_{n},a^{\prime}_{n}).

Symbolic spaces are of great importance in studying the computational aspects of dynamical systems. The space
\mathcal{A}
represents a discrete valued measurement function. However it is rarely the case that all the sequences of
\mathcal{A}^{\omega}
are observed. One is usually interested in a smaller portion of this collection.

Sub-shifts

The pair
\left(\mathcal{A}^{\omega},\sigma\right)
creates a continuous dynamical system of its own. Any
\sigma
-invariant subspace of
\mathcal{A}^{\omega}
will be called a
sub-shift
. Sub-shifts are created whenever there is a function
\phi:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{A}
on a dynamical system such as in Assumption
1
. In that case the collection of sequences

\left\{a_{n}:=\phi\left(f^{n}x\right)\;:\;n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\right\}

generated by all possible choices of
x\in X
creates a sub-shift of the total symbolic space
\mathcal{X}^{\omega}
.

In our case, given a finite open cover
\mathcal{U}
satisfying Assumptions
3
,
4
, the role of
\phi
is played by the index function
\mathfrak{j}:X\to\mathcal{S}
which assigns to every
x\in X
and number
j=\mathfrak{j}(x)
which is the index of the cell in which it lies. Any point
x\in X
has its own trajectory
x_{n}=f^{n}(x)
. This trajectory generates a sequence of states from
\mathcal{S}
based on the index of the cell that the trajectory currently is at, namely the sequence
\left\{s_{n}=\mathfrak{j}\circ h\circ f^{n}(x)\;:\;n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
. Thus for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x_{n}\in U_{s_{n}}
. This is called the
itinerary
of the point
x
, with respect to (w.r.t.) the cover
\mathcal{U}
. The collection of itineraries is a sub-shift of the symbolic space
\mathcal{S}^{\omega}
. We call this space
\Omega
. Sub-shifts provide a discrete description of continuum dynamics, and enable concepts such as information, entropy and complexity to be associated to dynamical systems
(
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
, e.g.)
.

On the other hand, the Markov process on
\mathcal{S}
generated by
\mathbb{P}
creates its own sub-shift. It is the collection of all sequences
\left\{s_{n}\right\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
such that for every
n
,
s_{n}\to s_{n+1}
is a feasible transition according to the transition probabilities stored in
\mathbb{P}
. Equivalently, for every
n
,
\mathbb{P}_{s_{n},s_{n+1}}>0
. We call this sub-shift
\Omega^{\prime}
. A good zero noise limit would ensure that
\Omega^{\prime}
is close to
\Omega
. This comparison can be done more precisely using the topology of the symbolic space.

Cylinders

Given any finite sequence
s_{1},\ldots,s_{N}
, the
cylinder
created by this sequence is the collection
\Cylndr\left(s_{1},\ldots,s_{N}\right)
of all points in
\mathcal{S}^{\omega}
whose first
N
coordinates coincide with this sequence. Such a cylinder is an open set, and we declare its
length
to be
N
. The collection of all such cylinders form a countable basis for
\mathcal{S}^{\omega}
. Moreover, for each
N
, the
N
-length cylinders form a partition of
\mathcal{S}^{\omega}
. All sub-shifts of
\mathcal{S}^{\omega}
inherit its topology and thus the cylinders form a basis for sub-shifts too. With these notations in mind (
6

\begin{split}&\sigma:\Omega^{\prime}\to\Omega^{\prime}\\
&g:\Omega^{\prime}\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{D}\end{split}

where
g
is the map

g:\left(\left\{s_{n}\right\}_{n=0}^{\infty},y\right)\mapsto\phi_{s_{0}\to s_{1}}(y).

Consider the cylinder

\mathcal{C}(x,N):=\Cylndr\left(\mathcal{U}(x,N)\right).

It is therefore an open subset of all those sequences in the sub-shift
\Omega^{\prime}
, whose first
N+1

N

x

\mathcal{C}(x,N)

f
. Suppose we fix an
x\in X
and a sample path
\vec{s}=\left\{s_{n}\right\}_{n}\in\mathcal{C}(x,N)

\begin{split}\tilde{f}^{N}\left(\vec{s},h(x)\right)&:=\phi_{s_{N-1}\to s_{N}}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{s_{1}\to s_{2}}\circ\phi_{s_{0}\to s_{1}}(x)=\phi_{s_{N-1}\to s_{N}}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{s_{1}\to s_{2}}\circ F|\alpha_{s_{0}\to s_{1}}h(x)\\

&=\tilde{f}^{N-1}\left(\sigma\vec{s},h(f(x))\right).\end{split}

Iterating this identity

\tilde{f}^{N}\left(\vec{s},h(x)\right)=\tilde{f}^{0}\left(\sigma^{N}\vec{s},h\left(f^{N}(x)\right)\right)=h\left(f^{N}(x)\right),\quad\forall\vec{s}\in\mathcal{C}(x,N).

x
, given that its first coordinate is
\mathfrak{j}(x)
. Thus
\theta(N)
is the total probability that an iteration of (
9

N

increases. However for a fixed

Towers

One of the most fundamental discoveries in the measure theoretic study of dynamical systems was the concept of
tower
s and their utility in approximating the dynamics and constructing a variety of non-intuitive behavior
[
38
,
39
,
40
,
41
,
42
]
. A
Rokhlin tower

[
43
,
44
]
with base
B
, height
N
and error
\epsilon
consists of a measurable set
B
such that

1.

the sets
\left\{B_{n}:=f^{n}(B)\;:\;0\leq n<N\right\}
are disjoint;

2.

the remainder set
B_{N}:=X\setminus\cup_{n=0}^{\infty}B_{n}
has
\mu
-measure less than
\epsilon
.

If we create a partition using these sets, then the only transition from cell
s
would be to cell
s+1
for
0\leq s<N
. Such a partition will thus create a simple Markov transition, in which the transition is mostly deterministic except in the last cell
f^{N-1}(B)
of the Rokhlin tower. To understand the utility of this simple form we consider the
symbolic dynamics
induced by the original map
f
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds, and
\mathcal{U}
is a partition created out of a Rokhlin tower of height
N^{\prime}+N
and remainder
\epsilon
. Then the probability
\theta(N)
from (
12
) is at least
(1-\epsilon)\frac{N^{\prime}-1}{N+N^{\prime}}
.

Let
B
be the base of the tower. We shall denote the cell
f^{t}(B)
by
B_{t}
. The key observation is that

\theta(x,N)\equiv 1,\quad\forall 0\leq t\leq N^{\prime},\;\forall x\in B_{t}.

Using this we can draw the following estimate :

\begin{split}\theta(N)&:=\int_{x\in X}\theta(x,N)d\mu(x)\geq\sum_{t=0}^{N^{\prime}-1}\int_{x\in B_{t}}\theta(x,N)d\mu(x)=\sum_{t=0}^{N^{\prime}-1}\int_{x\in B_{t}}1d\mu(x)\\
&=\sum_{t=0}^{N^{\prime}-1}\mu\left(B_{t}\right)=\sum_{t=0}^{N^{\prime}-1}\frac{1-\epsilon}{N+N^{\prime}}=(1-\epsilon)\frac{N^{\prime}-1}{N+N^{\prime}}\end{split}

This completes the proof of Lemma
3.1
. ∎

Note that the choice of
N,N^{\prime}
and
\epsilon
were arbitrary. We also have that

\lim_{\begin{array}[]{c}\epsilon\to 0^{+}\\
N^{\prime}\to\infty\end{array}}(1-\epsilon)\frac{N^{\prime}-1}{N+N^{\prime}}=1.

This is the precise statement of Corollary
2
stated before.

4  Convergence analysis

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
4
. For the sake of simplicity we shall assume throughout this section that the manifold
\mathcal{M}
is a sub-manifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that
h
from Assumption
2
is an inclusion map. We shall need a few key observations. We begin by familiarizing ourselves with some theoretical background.

Markov transitions

Let
Y
be a metric space, equipped with its Borel sigma-algebra. Given any Markov transition function
p:Y\to\Prob(Y)
, we define the
spread
of
p
to be

\text{spread}(p):=\sup_{x\in Y}\diam\support p(x).

The spread is an indication of the uncertainty associated with the Markov process. It is weaker than the more widely used notion of entropy
(
37
,
36
, e.g.)
. However it is well suited to our Analysis.

Note that a dynamical process is a deterministic process generated by a map
T:Y\to Y
. Such a map is also a Markov transition function, since it assigns to each point
x
the Dirac delta measure
\delta_{Tx}
. Such a transition function has zero spread. The following lemma is an easy consequence of Analysis :

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a metric space and
\left\{\beta_{t}\;:\;t>0\right\}
be a family of measures such that
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\text{spread}(\beta_{t})=0
. Suppose there is a point
x
in the intersection
\cap_{t>0}\support(\beta_{t})
. Then the
\beta_{t}
converge weakly to the Dirac-delta measure
\delta_{x}
.

The notion of a zero noise limit is dependent on a choice of measure
\beta
for the driving dynamics. Lemma
4.1
provides a means of establishing a zero noise limit without a knowledge of
\beta
. It is based on metric considerations alone.

Ergodicity

An invariant measure
\mu
for a dynamical system is said to be ergodic if any invariant subset
S
of the dynamics is either null w.r.t.
\nu
, or is of full
\nu
-measure. An ergodic measure is thus an indecomposable measure. It allowed no non-trivial partition into smaller invariant sets each carrying a fraction of the total measure. The space of invariant measures is a convex set and the ergodic probability measures are its extreme points. Ergodicity is an important part of our Assumption
1
. It is a natural assumption to make, as any dynamical system with bounded trajectories have at least one ergodic measure
(
50
,
16
, see)
. In fact, the convergence of data-driven techniques can seldom be justified without the aid of ergodicity
[
8
,
51
,
52
,
17
]
. Ergodicity leads to the following algebraic consequence :

Lemma 4.2
.

If the measure
\mu
is ergodic then the transition matrix
\mathbb{P}
of the partition is transitive, i.e., it has
1
as a simple eigenvalue.

Note that Lemma
4.2
holds irrespective of the choice of
\delta
or cover
\mathcal{U}
. The only requirement is ergodic. The matrix
\mathbb{P}
is non-negative entried and column-stochastic. Moreover
\mathbb{P}
is irreducible, i.e. it has no invariant subspace spanned by a proper subset of the coordinate vectors. To see why, let the contrary be assumed. So there are indices
i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}
in
\left\{1,\ldots,m\right\}
such that
\mathbb{P}
preserved the subspace spanned by the coordinate vectors corresponding to these vectors. This would mean that the set
\tilde{U}:=\cup_{j=1}^{k}U_{i_{j}}
is an invariant set of
f
. Note that
X\cap\tilde{U}
is a proper subset of
X
by the minimality of
\mathcal{U}
. This violates the ergodicity of
\mu
. Thus there is no such invariant subspace.

Thus
\mathbb{P}
is irreducible. From the theory of non-negative matrices
(
53
,
54
, e.g.)
it now follows that the eigenvalue of maximum magnitude of
\mathbb{P}
is
1
, and
1
is a simple eigenvalue. This completes the proof of Lemma
4.2
. The vector
\left(\frac{1}{m},\ldots,\frac{1}{m}\right)
is a left eigenvector of
\mathbb{P}
corresponding to eigenvalue
1
. It is also the unique stationary probability measure of the Markov process represented by
\mathbb{P}
.

Spread of the Markov process

We next establish such a metric bound for the Markov process formulation (
9
)

Lemma 4.3
.

The Markov process (
9
) has spread less than
4\delta(1+\|f\|_{Lip})
.

Fix any function
x\in x
and an index
j
such that
x\in U_{j}
. Consider any transition
j\to i
from
j
. There must a point
x^{\prime}\in U_{j}
such that
f(x)\in U_{i}
. Then note that

\begin{split}\dist\left(f(x),\phi_{j\to i}(x)\right)&\leq\dist\left(f(x),f(x^{\prime})\right)+\dist\left(f(x^{\prime}),\phi_{j\to i}(x)\right)\leq\|f\|_{Lip}\dist(x,x^{\prime})+\diam(U_{j})\\
&\leq\|f\|_{Lip}(2\delta)+2\delta=2\delta(1+\|f\|_{Lip}).\end{split}

Thus every point in the support of the probability
\hat{f}(x)
is within distance
2\delta(1+\|f\|_{Lip})
of
f(x)
. Thus the total spread of
\hat{f}(x)
is not more than
4\delta(1+\|f\|_{Lip})
. This completes the proof of Lemma
4.3
. ∎

Proof of Theorem
4
(ii)

Lemmas

and

is indeed a zero-noise limit of the Markov process (

Proof of Theorem

(i)

Note that since the domain of each

is

, the supports of any stationary measure

, which is within a

Stationary measure

A Markov process such as (
6

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\tilde{\beta}
be any stationary measure of the Markov process (
6
). Then the projection of
\tilde{\beta}
to
\mathcal{S}
must be a stationary measure of the process generated by
\mathbb{P}
.

Thus any stationary measure
\tilde{\beta}
for the process (
6
) must project along the
\mathcal{S}
coordinates into
\beta
, the unique stationary measure on
\mathcal{S}
. Thus any integral of any continuous function
\zeta
with respect to
\tilde{\beta}
can be rewritten as

\int_{\mathcal{S}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\zeta d\tilde{\beta}=\int_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\zeta(s,x)d\tilde{\beta}(x|s)\right]d\beta(s).

(13)

Here
\tilde{\beta}(\cdot|s)
denotes the conditional of
\tilde{\beta}
along the fibre
\{s\}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Recall that the process (
6
) thus has the transition function
Q
in (
8
). The following formula describes how the Markov process transforms any measure
\tilde{\beta}
on
\mathcal{S}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, stationary or not.

\begin{split}Q_{*}(\tilde{\beta})&=\int_{\mathcal{S}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}Q(x,s)d\tilde{\beta}(x,s)=\int_{\mathcal{S}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(s)\times\left[G(s,\cdot,x)_{*}p(s)\right]d\tilde{\beta}(x,s)\\
&=\int_{\mathcal{S}}p(s)\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[G(s,\cdot,x)_{*}p(s)\right]d\tilde{\beta}(x|s)\right]d\beta(s)\end{split}

(14)

If
\tilde{\beta}
is indeed a stationary measure, then both sides of (
14
) are equal to
\tilde{\beta}
itself.

Follower-predecessor

It will be useful to establish some terminology to describe the causal relations created by the Markov process on
\mathcal{S}
. Given two states
s,j\in\mathcal{S}
say that a state
s
is a
\mathcal{S}
-
predecessor
of a state

within
\mathcal{S}
if
\mathbb{P}_{s,j}>0
. In other words, there is a Markov transition from
s
to
j
according to the process generated by
\mathbb{P}
. In that case
j
will be called an
\mathcal{S}
-follower of
s
. A state
i
will be called an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-follower of

if there is some state
s^{\prime\prime}
such that
\phi_{s\to s^{\prime\prime}}^{-1}\left(U_{s^{\prime\prime}}\cap U_{i}\right)
has non-zero
\tilde{\beta}(\cdot|s)
measure. Note that if
j
is an
\mathcal{S}
-predecessor of

, then

must be an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-follower of
j
. Also note that the notions of predecessor and follower for
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d}
coincide if Assumption
3
holds.

Now substitute
\zeta(x,s)=\lambda(s)\xi(x)
in (
13
) and apply (
14
) to get

\begin{split}\int\zeta d\tilde{\beta}&=\int\zeta d\left[Q_{*}(\tilde{\beta})\right]\\
&=\int_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\int_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\lambda(s^{\prime})dp(s)(s^{\prime})\left[\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[\int_{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\xi(x^{\prime})d\left[G(s,\cdot,x)_{*}p(s)\right](x^{\prime})\right]d\tilde{\beta}(x|s)\right]d\beta(s).\end{split}

The inner-most integral can be simplified using the change of variables formula to get

\int_{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\xi(x^{\prime})d\left[G(s,\cdot,x)_{*}p(s)\right](x^{\prime})=\int_{s^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\xi\circ G(s,s^{\prime\prime},x)dp(s)(s^{\prime\prime}).

Using this substitution we get

\int\zeta d\tilde{\beta}=\int_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[\int_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\lambda(s^{\prime})\left[\int_{s^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\xi\circ G(s,s^{\prime\prime},x)dp(s)(s^{\prime\prime})\right]dp(s)(s^{\prime})\right]d\tilde{\beta}(x|s)d\beta(s).

(15)

Now take
\lambda=1_{j}
and
\xi=1_{U_{i}}
. Then (

\begin{split}\tilde{\beta}\left(\{j\}\times U_{i}\right)&=\int 1_{j}1_{U_{i}}d\left[Q_{*}(\tilde{\beta})\right]\\
&=\int_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[\int_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}1_{j}(s^{\prime})\left[\int_{s^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}1_{U_{i}}\circ G(s,s^{\prime\prime},x)dp(s)(s^{\prime\prime})\right]dp(s)(s^{\prime})\right]d\tilde{\beta}(x|s)d\beta(s)\\
&=\int_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(s)(j)\left[\int_{s^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left(1_{U_{i}}\circ G\right)(s,s^{\prime\prime},x)dp(s)(s^{\prime\prime})\right]d\tilde{\beta}(x|s)d\beta(s)\end{split}

The probability weight

. The quantity
\left(1_{U_{i}}\circ G\right)(s,s^{\prime\prime},x)

\left(1_{U_{i}}\circ G\right)(s,s^{\prime\prime},x)

. Substituting we get

\tilde{\beta}\left(\{j\}\times U_{i}\right)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{P}_{s,j}\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{P}_{s,s^{\prime\prime}}\tilde{\beta}(\cdot|s)\left(\phi_{s\to s^{\prime\prime}}^{-1}\left(U_{s^{\prime\prime}}\cap U_{i}\right)\right)\right]d\tilde{\beta}(x|s)d\beta(s).

All the terms in the summation are nonzero. The LHS

\mathcal{S}

\mathbb{R}^{d}

In particular this means that

5  Kernel methods

Any function
k:Y\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
on a topological space
Y
is a kernel function. One imposes various additional constraints so that deeper geometric properties of the underlying space
Y
may be identified, or to generate suitable function space. We shall assume that

Assumption 5
.

There is a strictly positive definite, symmetric kernel
k:\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
, which is
C^{r}
for some
r\geq 1
, and strictly positive valued.

A kernel is said to be
symmetric
if it is symmetric w.r.t. its two variables. A kernel
k
is said to be
positive semi-definite
if for every distinct points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in Y
and scalars
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
,
\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}c_{i}^{*}k(x_{i},x_{j})c_{j}\geq 0
. It will be called
strictly positive definite
when equality holds in this equation if and only if
c_{i}=0\quad\forall i=1,\dots,n
. The main utility of kernels in Learning theory is the fact that for each
y\in Y
,
k(y,\cdot)
is a function on
Y
. It is called the
section
of
k
at
y
. Thus the first variable in a kernel can be viewed as a parameter to generate an family of functions. The span of the kernel sections create useful interpolation spaces. If the kernel is symmetric and s.p.d. the span is called a reproducing kernel Hilbert space or RKHS. RKHSs are collections of smooth functions which are universal approximators and also endowed with a Hilbert space structure. The span of a general kernel function can be described using the language of
integral operators
.

Kernel integral operators

Let
\nu
be a probability measure on
Y
. Associated with the kernel
k
and measure
\nu
is a kernel integral operator
K^{\nu}
which transforms every function
\phi\in L^{2}(\mu)
as

K^{\nu}\phi(x):=\int_{\mathcal{M}}k(x,y)\phi(y)d\mu(y),

If
\phi
lies in
L^{2}(\nu)
then so does
K^{\nu}\phi
. If the kernel function is continuous so is
K^{\nu}\phi
. If
Y
is a manifold and if the kernel
k
is
C^{r}
, then the functions in the image of the integral operator
K
will also consist of
C^{r}
functions. Kernel integral operators and associated Hilbert spaces have found widespread use in the mathematical theory of learning
[
55
]
, and more recently in ergodic theory
[
51
,
2
,
17
,
52
]
, conditional expectation
[
56
,
57
]
, and Algebra
[
58
]
.

Suppose
k
is square integrable with respect to
\nu
, and is strictly positive valued. Then
k
can be modified by a process called
Markov normalization
:

k^{\nu}_{Markov}(x,y):=k(x,y)/\rho^{\nu}(x),\quad\rho^{\nu}(x):=\int_{Y}k(x,z)d\nu(z).

The function
\rho^{\nu}
is known as the
degree-vector
. Markov kernels have the property that

\int_{Y}k^{\nu}_{Markov}(x,y)d\nu(y)=1,\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{M}.

This allows such kernels to be interpreted as mimicking a Markov transition function. Hence the name.

Range of Markov kernels

Since we assume the kernel
k
to be strictly positive valued and
C^{r}
, the degree vector
\rho^{\nu}
will be strictly positive valued and
C^{r}
too, for every choice of
\nu
. Consider the
multiplication operator

M_{\rho}^{\nu}:L^{2}(\nu)\to L^{2}(\nu),\quad\phi\mapsto\phi\rho^{\nu}.

The operator
M_{\rho}^{\nu}
is therefore merely pointwise multiplication by the degree vector. This operator is bounded with a bounded inverse. In fact
M_{\rho}^{\nu}
is a symmetric, strictly positive definite bounded operator with a bounded inverse. Now consider the operator

\tilde{K}^{\nu}:=\left[M_{\rho}^{\nu}\right]^{1/2}K_{Markov}^{\nu}\left[M_{\rho}^{\nu}\right]^{-1/2}:L^{2}(\nu)\to L^{2}(\nu)

Thus
\tilde{K}^{\nu}
is a similarity transform of the Markov operator. Now note that

\begin{split}\left(\tilde{K}^{\nu}\phi\right)(x)&=\rho^{\nu}(x)^{1/2}\int k^{\nu}_{Markov}(x,y)\rho^{\nu}(y)^{-1/2}\phi(y)d\nu(y)\\
&=\rho^{\nu}(x)^{1/2}\int\frac{k(x,y)}{\rho^{\nu}(x)\rho^{\nu}(y)^{1/2}}\phi(y)d\nu(y)=\int\frac{k(x,y)}{\rho^{\nu}(x)^{1/2}\rho^{\nu}(y)^{1/2}}\phi(y)d\nu(y)\\
&=\int\tilde{k}(x,y)\phi(y)d\nu(y).\end{split}

Here
\tilde{k}(x,y)
is the symmetric kernel
\frac{k(x,y)}{\rho^{\nu}(x)^{1/2}\rho^{\nu}(y)^{1/2}}
. In fact the kernel
\tilde{k}
satisfies Assumption
5
, see
(
17
, Sec 4)
for a proof. Thus the Markov normalized kernel is similar to the kernel integral transform corresponding to a kernel satisfying Assumption
5
. This means among other things, that the range of
K^{\nu}_{Markov}
is dense in the space of continous functions on the support of
\nu
.

The algorithms we develop in the next section utilize Markov kernels to construct the transition functions
\phi_{j\to i}
as in (
5
). The measure
\nu
will be a discrete measure supported on a small portion of the dataset. A crucial feature of Markov kernels that we utilize is the control it provides over its range.

Convexity

Let
\phi
be a continuous function on a compact set
Y
. Then the
convex-hull
of
\phi
is the collection

\mbox{convex-hull}(\phi):=\left\{\int\phi d\gamma\;:\;\gamma\in\Prob(Y)\right\}.

Let
p
be a Markov kernel and
P^{\nu}
denote the integral operator corresponding to
p
and probability measure
\nu
. Then note that

\left(P^{\nu}\phi\right)(x)=\int p(x,x^{\prime})\phi(x^{\prime})d\nu(x^{\prime})=\int\phi p(x,\cdot)d\nu,\quad\forall\phi\in L^{2}(\nu),\,\forall x\in Y.

The value
\left(P^{\nu}\phi\right)(x)
of
P^{\nu}\phi
at
x
is thus a weighted average of
\phi
. The weight is provided by the Markov kernel section
p(x,\cdot)
. This means that

\ran\left(P^{\nu}\phi\right)\subseteq\mbox{convex-hull}(\phi).

This relation is important from a numerical point of view. Typically
\nu
represents an empirical measure borne by data. Thus reconstructing a function as a function of the form
P^{\nu}\phi
ensures that the range of the function lies within the convex hull of the output values used in its training phase.

Bandwidth selection

We now present our method of selecting a bandwidth
\theta
for constructing a kernel over a dataset
\mathcal{D}

\eta\in(0,1)
, and a zero threshold
\theta_{zero}=10^{-14}

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
of the data set
\mathcal{D}
and construct the set
\mathcal{S}
of all possible pairwise squared distances. Usually, it suffices to choose
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}

\mathcal{D}

\theta=-1/\ln\left(\theta_{zero}\right).

Given the

\eta

are less than

We next discuss various ways to evaluate the results.

Comparing topology

Our primary motivation was to recreate the dynamics as well as the invariant set
X
supporting the measure
\mu
. Some care is needed to compare an invariant region of the reconstructed system
F
with
h(X)
. They may not always be comparable as manifolds. One of the characteristic features of chaotic attractors
(
62
,
63
,
64
, e.g.)
is that they have a fractal geometry, which is self-similar at all scales, and almost nowhere differentiable. A general mathematical description of chaotic sets is elusive, and there is no reliable numerical method to characterize chaotic sets from data. We avoid this issue by comparing the two subsets by the Hausdorff metric
\dist_{\text{Hauss}}
:

\begin{split}\dist_{\to}\left(X,Y\right):=\sup_{x\in X}\inf_{y\in Y}\dist(X,Y),\\
\dist_{\text{Hauss}}\left(A,B\right)&:=\left(\dist_{\to}\left(A,B\right),\quad\dist_{\to}\left(B,A\right)\right)\end{split}

(21)

The Hausdorff metric is a proper metric on the collection of compact sets in any Hausdorff space. One can similarly define an
L^{1}
-metric

\begin{split}\dist_{L1,\to}\left(X,Y\right):=\int_{x\in X}\inf_{y\in Y}\dist(X,Y)d\mu(x),\\
\dist_{\text{Hauss},L1}\left(A,B\right)&:=\left(\dist_{L1,\to}\left(A,B\right),\quad\dist_{L1,\to}\left(B,A\right)\right)\end{split}

(22)

The
L^{1}
Hausdorff metric will be our choice of metric to evaluate the accuracy of our Markov approximation.

Comparing invariant measures

An invariant measure of a dynamical system bears many characteristics of the dynamics otherwise not available from just the topology of the invariant set. Invariant measures are the basis of the ergodic theorem, which essentially says that time average of a function along a typical trajectory must equal its space average with respect the invariant measure. In fact this phenomenon of convergence can provide a complete phase portrait of the multiple invariant sets co-existing with in
\mathcal{M}

(
65
,
66
, e.g.)
. Ergodic convergence is special case of a property called decay of correlations. Given two square integrable functions
\alpha,\beta:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, their correlation is a function of time given by :

\Corel(n;\alpha,\beta):=\left\langle\alpha,U^{n}\beta\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}=\int_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\left\langle\alpha(x)\beta\left(f^{n}x\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}d\mu(x).

Suppose
\alpha,\beta
are the same functions
\Phi
. Then the correlation function is named
auto-correlation
:

\AutoCorel(n;\Phi):=\Corel(n;\Phi,\Phi)

The decay of the correlation and auto-correlation functions with time
n
indicates the level of mixing in the system. Mixing is the measure theoretic analog of chaos. Moreover, the collection of all correlation functions determine a construct called the
spectral-measure
of the dynamics
(
17
, see)
. The spectral measure is an equivalent description of the dynamics using the language of operators and operator families. Thus in conclusion, autocorrelation functions form a good metric to compare the invariant measures of two different processes.

In a numerical setup we cannot compute the autocorrelation function for ever function
\Phi
. Instead we shall concentrate on the cases when
\Phi
is the collection of coordinate projects. This autocorrelation function is easy to evaluate on signals. Given a signal
\vec{X}=\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\right)
, its autocorrelation can be approximated as

\Delta(\vec{x})(t):=\frac{1}{N-T}\sum_{n=1}^{N-T}\left\langle x_{n}-\bar{x},x_{n+t}-\bar{x}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{d}},\quad 1\leq t\leq T.

(23)

The quantity in (
23
) will can be computed both for the original timeseries as well as the timeseries obtained by simulating the Markov process, for various lead-times
T
.

8  Conclusions

We have thus presented how an ergodic dynamical system
(\Omega,\mu,f)
may be realized as the zero-noise limit of a Markov process. If the system is stochastically stable, then the Markov process also provides an approximation of its ergodic / statistical properties. These Markov processes can be implemented though data-driven algorithms. The experiments conducted with these algorithms confirmed the convergence claimed and proved in Theorem
4
.

There are two main requirements in any data driven approach to dynamical system. Firstly, the methods must not rely on a fore-knowledge of the original phase space
\Omega
or of the map
\mu
. The reconstruction must be entirely in a space created out of data, such as Euclidean spaces. Secondly the methods must preserve the invariant region and measures as seen from from observation maps. The loss of stability of the targeted invariant region is a common challenge faced by most numerical methods.

The procedure we have described fulfills both these requirements. Moreover, it has the following useful features :

1.

The techniques are based on kernel methods. Kernel methods compute pairwise distances between data points, and convert the problem into an analysis of the kernel matrix. As a result, dimensionality issues are averted. One may have a low dimensional invariant set
X
embedded in a high dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The performance of kernel methods are not affected by the magnitude of
d
.

2.

The technique places no strict requirement on the creation of the open covers. This allows many other possibilities, such as coverings using cubes and simplexes.

3.

The main result Theorem
4
is stated in the context of the continuum measure
\mu
. This measure needs to be well sampled by data. For any choice of mesh size
\delta
, each cell
U_{j}
of the cover must contain at least two data points. This simple requirement significantly reduces the demand for large data for larger and larger estimates.

4.

One of the key ideas used in the technique was the use of Markov kernels to ensure that the ranges of the
\phi_{j\to i}
remain confined to a convex hull. See their definition in (
5
). This simple idea ensures the Hausdorff convergence of the support of the invariant measure for the stochastic process to the targeted invariant set
h(X)
.
