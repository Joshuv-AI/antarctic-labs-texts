Relating Interleaving and Fréchet Distances via Ordered Merge Trees
Thijs Beurskens, Tim Ophelders, Bettina Speckmann, Kevin Verbeek

Abstract
Merge trees are a common topological descriptor for data with a hierarchical component, such as terrains and scalar fields. The interleaving distance, in turn, is a common distance for comparing merge trees. However, the interleaving distance for merge trees is solely based on the hierarchical structure, and disregards any other geometrical or topological properties that might be present in the underlying data. Furthermore, the interleaving distance is NP-hard to compute. In this paper, we introduce a form of ordered merge trees that can capture intrinsic order present in the data. We further define a natural variant of the interleaving distance, the monotone interleaving distance , which is an order-preserving distance for ordered merge trees. Analogously to the regular interleaving distance for merge trees, we show that the monotone variant has three equivalent definitions in terms of two maps, a single map, or a labelling. Furthermore, we establish a connection between the monotone interleaving distance of ordered merge trees and the Fréchet distance of 1D curves. As a result, the monotone interleaving distance between two ordered merge trees can be computed exactly in near-quadratic time in their complexity. The connection between the monotone interleaving distance and the Fréchet distance builds a new bridge between the fields of topological data analysis, where interleaving distances are a common tool, and computational geometry, where Fréchet distances are studied extensively.

Introduction

Topological descriptors, such as persistence diagrams, Reeb graphs, Morse-Smale complexes, and merge trees, lie at the heart of topological data analysis; they support the analysis and visualisation of complex, real-world data.
In this paper, we focus in particular on merge trees.
Merge trees are frequently defined on a scalar field, where they capture connected components of sublevel sets; they are generally used to represent hierarchical structures.
Merge trees closely resemble phylogenetic trees in computational biology, and dendrograms in hierarchical clustering.

Figure 1
:
The Waimakariri River in New Zealand is a braided river. Photo by Greg O’Beirne
[
28
]
.

Standard merge trees focus solely on the hierarchy and do not represent other salient geometric features.
However, there are applications where there is an intrinsic order present in the data which we would like to capture in the merge tree as well.
Our work, specifically, is motivated by the study of braided rivers (see Figure
1
).
A braided river is a multi-channel river system, known to evolve rapidly
[
21
,
24
]
. It is our goal to analyse the evolution of such a river system over time.
Since the water level in a river, and hence the number of recognisable channels, is highly variable, geomorphologists frequently model river networks based solely on bathymetry, that is, terrain models of the river bed
[
20
,
22
]
.
A river network is then characterised by the topological features of this river bed, most notably, by the so-called
braid bars
that correspond to local maxima of the terrain.
For the purpose of studying river networks over time, we use a simple topological descriptor, namely a merge tree based on these bars: each leaf in our merge tree represents a local maximum (bar), and each internal vertex represents two bars merging as we move downward in the terrain (see Figure
2
).
We use these river networks and merge trees in the software package TopoTide
[
30
]
that we have been developing with domain scientists.

Figure 2
:
A schematic river, at different levels of the terrain.
We use a merge tree to represent the bars: leaves correspond to local maxima, and internal vertices correspond to two bars merging.
We draw the tree upside down, following the convention that merge trees are rooted at positive infinity.

To track the evolution of a river over time via a representation with merge trees, we need a geomorphologically meaningful way to match consecutive merge trees while respecting the intrinsic order on the bars induced by the river (upstream towards downstream and from bank to bank).
Hence we need to encode such orders into our merge trees and then match these merge trees in a way that respects the orders.
The standard way to compare and match two merge trees is the interleaving distance
[
26
]
.
However, the regular interleaving distance is unable to capture orders and there is no known efficient algorithm to compute even an constant-factor approximation of the interleaving distance, rendering it effectively useless in practice.
In fact, computing the interleaving distance is NP-hard
[
1
]
.
1
1

1

Agarwal et al.
[
1
]
actually prove that approximating the Gromov-Hausdorff distance with a factor better than 3 is NP-hard. As many have observed, this proof also applies to the interleaving distance.
For completeness, we present the modified proof in Appendix
A
.

We hence introduce a class of ordered merge trees that can capture the intrinsic order present among river bars and an accompanying
monotone interleaving distance
which preserves these orders.

Contributions.

We introduce the
monotone interleaving distance
, an extension of the interleaving distance for ordered merge trees based on order-preserving maps.
We show that an ordered merge tree induces a 1D curve and use this to compute the monotone interleaving distance efficiently.
In particular, we show that the monotone interleaving distance between two ordered merge trees is equal to the Fréchet distance between their induced 1D curves.
Hence, we can use any algorithm for the Fréchet distance between 1D curves to compute the monotone interleaving distance between ordered merge trees.
Furthermore, we show that the Fréchet distance, and therefore also the monotone interleaving distance, is an interleaving distance for a specific category.
Lastly, we give two alternative definitions for the monotone interleaving distance, in terms of
\delta
-good maps and in terms of labelled merge trees.
This last result is analogous to a similar result for the regular interleaving distance for merge trees.

In Section
2
we discuss the necessary background from computational topology.
We introduce our class of ordered merge trees in Section
3
and show how to define them in two equivalent ways.
Here, we also define the monotone interleaving distance.
Section
4
focuses on the relation between the Fréchet distance and the monotone interleaving distance.
In Section
5
we switch to a categorical point of view, and we show that the Fréchet distance is an interleaving distance for a specific category.
Lastly, in Section
6
we discuss the two alternative definitions:
monotone
\delta
-good interleaving distance
and the
monotone label interleaving distance
.

Preliminaries

In this section, we review the necessary mathematical notions in computational topology and geometry, including merge trees, the interleaving distance, and the Fréchet distance.

Merge trees.

A
rooted tree
is a tree where one vertex is identified as the
root
.
Let
T
denote a rooted tree, with vertices
V(T)
, edges
E(T)
, and leaves
L(T)\subseteq V(T)
.
In the remainder of the paper, we view
T
not necessarily as a combinatorial tree, but rather as a topological space.
We note here that the distances we consider are defined not only on the vertices of the tree, but also on the interior of the edges.
To that end, we use a continuous representation of
T
, which is commonly referred to as the
geometric realisation
, or, more accurately, the
topological realisation
of
T
.
Formally, the topological realisation of
T
is a topological space, which is defined as the union of segments that each represent an edge.
Specifically, we represent each edge
e\in E(T)
by a copy of the unit interval
[0,1]
and connect these intervals according to their adjacencies.
We refer to the elements of
T
as
points
and to the elements of
V(T)
as
vertices
.
For ease of explanation, we identify
T
with its topological realisation.

Definition 1
.

A
merge tree
is a pair
(T,f)
, where
T
is a rooted tree and
f\colon T\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is a continuous height function that is strictly increasing towards the root, with
f(v)=\infty
if and only if
v
is the root.

A merge tree
(T,f)
naturally induces a partial order on its points.
For two points
x_{1},x_{2}\in T
, the point
x_{2}
is called an
ancestor
of
x_{1}
, denoted
x_{1}\preceq x_{2}
, if there is a path from
x_{1}
to
x_{2}
that is monotonically increasing under
f
.
We call
x_{2}
a
strict ancestor
of
x_{1}
if
x_{1}\prec x_{2}
.
Correspondingly, the point
x_{1}
is called a
(strict) descendant
of
x_{2}
if
x_{2}
is a (strict) ancestor of
x_{1}
.
For a point
x\in T
, we denote by
T_{x}
the subtree of
T
rooted at
x
.
Furthermore, for a given value
\delta\geq 0
, we denote by
x^{\delta}
the unique ancestor of
x
with
f(x^{\delta})=f(x)+\delta
.

\delta
-interleaving. Mapping a point

to
T^{\prime}
through
\alpha
(in blue), and mapping it back to

\beta
(in red) gives the unique ancestor

.

Interleaving distance.

For two merge trees
(T,f)
and
(T^{\prime},f^{\prime})
, we are now ready to define the
interleaving distance
.
Let
\delta\geq 0
.
Intuitively, a
\delta
-interleaving describes a mapping
\alpha
from
T
to
T^{\prime}
that sends points exactly
\delta
upwards, and a similar map
\beta
from
T^{\prime}
to
T
, such that the compositions of
\alpha
and
\beta
send any point to its unique ancestor
2\delta
higher.
Figure
3
shows an example of a
\delta
-interleaving.

Definition 2

(Morozov, Beketayev and Weber
[
26
]
)
.

Given two merge trees
(T,f)
and
(T^{\prime},f^{\prime})
, a pair of continuous maps
\alpha\colon T\to T^{\prime}
and
\beta\colon T^{\prime}\to T
is called a
\delta
-interleaving
if for all
x\in T
and
y\in T^{\prime}
:

[(C1)]

1.

f^{\prime}(\alpha(x))=f(x)+\delta
,

2.

\beta(\alpha(x))=x^{2\delta}
,

3.

f(\beta(y))=f^{\prime}(y)+\delta
, and

4.

\alpha(\beta(y))=y^{2\delta}
.

The
interleaving distance

d_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{}}((T,f),(T^{\prime},f^{\prime}))
is defined as the infimum of all
\delta
such that there exists a
\delta
-interleaving.

Gasparovich et al.
[
18
]
showed that for finite trees there always exists an interleaving that achieves the infimum.
Hence, we can replace infimum with minimum.
The individual maps
\alpha
and
\beta
of a
\delta
-interleaving
(\alpha,\beta)
are both
\delta
-shift maps
, i.e. continuous maps that send points exactly
\delta
higher.
We say a
\delta
-interleaving is
optimal
for two merge trees
T
and
T^{\prime}
if their interleaving distance
d_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{}}(T,T^{\prime})
is
\delta
.

Fréchet distance.

The
Fréchet distance
is a distance for curves.
We are specifically interested in the Fréchet distance for 1D curves.
A
1D curve
is a continuous map
P\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
.
The Fréchet distance between two 1D curves is defined in terms of matchings between the two curves.
A
matching
between two curves
P
and
Q
is a continuous and increasing bijection
\mu\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]
.
We say a matching
\mu
is a
\delta
-matching
if
|P(t)-Q(\mu(t))|\leq\delta
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
The Fréchet distance is defined as the infimum value
\delta
for which a
\delta
-matching exists:

d_{\mathrm{F}}(P,Q)=\inf_{\mu}\max_{t\in[0,1]}|P(t)-Q(\mu(t))|.

2.1
Binary relations.

In the remainder of the paper we use the following standard definitions from order theory.
A
total order

\leq
on a set
X
is a binary relation that satisfies the following four properties.

•

Reflexivity.
For any
x\in X
, the relation
x\leq x
holds.

•

Antisymmetry.
For any
x,y\in X
, if
x\leq y
and
y\leq x
then
x=y
.

•

Transitivity.
For any
x,y,z\in X
, if
x\leq y
and
y\leq z
then
x\leq z
.

•

Totality.
For any
x,y\in X
, either
x\leq y
or
y\leq x
holds.

A total order
\leq
on a set
X
induces a
strict order

<
as follows.
For two points
x,y\in X
, we have
x<y
if and only if
x\leq y
and not
y\leq x
, or, equivalently,
x\leq y
and not
x=y
.

An Order-Preserving Interleaving Distance

In this section, we introduce a form of
ordered merge trees
and define an order-preserving distance on them.
We first introduce two classes of ordered merge trees and we show that they are equivalent.
In Section
3.2
, we then define the monotone interleaving distance.

3.1
Ordered merge trees.

We now define a new class of merge trees, which we call
ordered merge trees
.
An ordered merge tree is a merge tree equipped with a total order on each of its level sets.
Specifically, consider a merge tree
(T,f)
and assume that its lowest leaf has height exactly
0
.
If not, we can simply shift
f
by a constant term.
Fix a height value
h\geq 0
.
The level set of
T
at height
h
is defined as
f^{-1}({h})\coloneqq\{x\in T\mid f(x)=h\}
.
For a point
x\in T
with
f(x)\leq h
, we denote by
x|^{h}
the unique ancestor of
x
at height
h
.
We define a
layer-order
as a set of total orders
(\leq_{h})_{h\geq 0}
on the level sets of
T
, such that it is
consistent
: for two heights
h_{1}\leq h_{2}
and two points
x_{1},x_{2}
in
f^{-1}({h_{1}})
, we require that
x_{1}\leq_{h_{1}}x_{2}
implies
x_{1}|^{h_{2}}\leq_{h_{2}}x_{2}|^{h_{2}}
.

Definition 3
.

An
ordered merge tree
is a triple
(T,f,(\leq_{h})_{h\geq 0})
, with
(T,f)
a merge tree and
(\leq_{h})_{h\geq 0}
a layer-order.

Lemma 1
.

Fix
h\geq 0
and let
x_{1},x_{2}\in f^{-1}({h})
be two distinct points such that
x_{1}<_{h}x_{2}
.
Then also
\bar{x}_{1}<_{\bar{h}}\bar{x}_{2}

\bar{x}_{2}\in T_{x_{2}}

.

Proof.

Consider two points
\bar{x}_{1}\in T_{x_{1}}
and
\bar{x}_{2}\in T_{x_{2}}
at height
\bar{h}\leq h
.
Assume for the sake of a contradiction that not
\bar{x}_{1}<_{\bar{h}}\bar{x}_{2}

It follows that then
\bar{x}_{2}<_{\bar{h}}\bar{x}_{1}

We know that

However, then by consistency of

If all leaves of an ordered merge tree are at the same height, say

, then by the consistency property, the order
\leq_{0}
is a total order on the leaves and completely and uniquely defines the layer-order

For trees that do not have their leaves all at the same height, we wish to similarly use the layer-order to infer a single total order on the leaves from which we can recover the layer-order.
For this purpose, we introduce a second class of ordered merge trees, which we call

Specifically, denote the

We define a

as a total order
\sqsubseteq_{L}

That is, for every leaf
u\in L(T)
, we require that if there exist leaves
u_{1},u_{2}\in L(T)

u_{1}\sqsubseteq_{L}u\sqsubseteq_{L}u_{2}
, then

(see Figure

Definition 4
.

A
leaf-ordered merge tree
is a triple
(T,f,\sqsubseteq_{L})
with
(T,f)
a merge tree and
\sqsubseteq_{L}
a leaf-order.

We show in Theorem

Induced leaf-ordered merge trees.

Consider an ordered merge tree
(T,f,(\leq_{h}))
.
First, we define the

\mathcal{L}((T,f,(\leq_{h})))\coloneqq(T,f,\sqsubseteq_{L})
, where
\sqsubseteq_{L}
is defined as follows:
for

u_{1}|^{h}\leq_{h}u_{2}|^{h}

.

Lemma 2
.

The induced leaf-order
\sqsubseteq_{L}

Proof.

Reflexivity follows from the definition.
To see that
\sqsubseteq_{L}

u_{1},u_{2}\in L(T)

u_{1}\sqsubseteq_{L}u_{2}

u_{2}\sqsubseteq_{L}u_{1}

Let

By definition, we have
u_{1}|^{h}\leq_{h}u_{2}|^{h}
and
u_{2}|^{h}\leq_{h}u_{1}|^{h}

Since
\leq_{h}

u_{1}|^{h}=u_{2}|^{h}

One of
u_{1}
,

Next, to show

.
We need to show that
u_{1}\sqsubseteq_{L}u_{3}

Define

By consistency of

Using transitivity of

Now, set

Else, if

.

u_{2}<_{h}u_{3}|^{h}

Now, we can use Lemma

In particular, for

Lemma 3
.

The induced leaf-order
\sqsubseteq_{L}
separates subtrees.

Proof.

Consider distinct leaves
u,u_{1},u_{2}\in L(T)
such that
u_{1}\sqsubseteq_{L}u\sqsubseteq_{L}u_{2}
.
Define
v=\mathrm{lca}(u_{1},u_{2})
.
Set
h=\max(f(v),f(u))
, and observe that both
u_{1}|^{h}=v|^{h}
and
u_{2}|^{h}=v|^{h}
.
Since
u_{1}\sqsubseteq_{L}u
, we get
v|^{h}=u_{1}|^{h}\leq_{h}u|^{h}
.
Similarly, since
u\sqsubseteq_{L}u_{2}
, we obtain
u|^{h}\leq_{h}v|^{h}
.
Combining these inequalities, and using the antisymmetry of
\leq_{h}
, we thus have
u|^{h}=v|^{h}
.
We now claim that
h=f(v)
, so that
u|^{h}=v
, implying
u\in T_{v}
.
Assume for the sake of a contradiction that
h=f(u)
.
Then, we get
v|^{h}=u|^{h}=u
, implying that
u_{1}\prec v\preceq u
.
However,
u
is a leaf, and can not have any descendants. ∎

Combining Lemmas

and
3
, we see that
\mathcal{L}
induces a leaf-ordered merge tree.

Corollary 4
.

\mathcal{L}(T,f,(\leq_{h}))
is a leaf-ordered merge tree.

Induced ordered merge trees.

Consider a leaf-ordered merge tree
(T,f,\sqsubseteq_{L})
.
For two sets of leaves
U_{1},U_{2}\subset L(T)
, we write
U_{1}\sqsubseteq_{L}U_{2}
if for all
u_{1}\in U_{1}
and for all
u_{2}\in U_{2}
, we have
u_{1}\sqsubseteq_{L}u_{2}
.
We now define the
induced ordered merge tree

\mathcal{T}(T,f,\sqsubseteq_{L})\coloneqq(T,f,(\leq_{h}))
, where
\leq_{h}
is defined as follows: for height
h\geq 0
and points
x_{1},x_{2}\in f^{-1}({h})
, we set
x_{1}\leq_{h}x_{2}
if and only if
x_{1}=x_{2}
or if
L(T_{x_{1}})\sqsubseteq_{L}L(T_{x_{2}})
.
We refer to
(\leq_{h})
as the
induced layer-order
.

Lemma 5
.

For each
h\geq 0
, the relation
\leq_{h}
is a total order.

Proof.

Fix
h\geq 0
.
Reflexivity follows immediately.
To show
\leq_{h}
is antisymmetric, take
x_{1},x_{2}\in f^{-1}({h})
and assume
x_{1}\leq_{h}x_{2}
and
x_{2}\leq_{h}x_{1}
.
Assume for the sake of a contradiction
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
.
By construction, we then must have both
L(T_{x_{1}})\sqsubseteq_{L}L(T_{x_{2}})
and
L(T_{x_{2}})\sqsubseteq_{L}L(T_{x_{1}})
.
This means that for
u_{1}\in L(T_{x_{1}})
and
u_{2}\in L(T_{x_{2}})
, we have
u_{1}\sqsubseteq_{L}u_{2}
and
u_{2}\sqsubseteq_{L}u_{1}
, implying
u_{1}=u_{2}
.
However, then
x_{1}=u_{1}|^{f(x_{1})}=u_{2}|^{f(x_{2})}=x_{2}
, contradicting our assumption
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
.
To show transitivity, take distinct points
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in f^{-1}({h})
and assume
x_{1}\leq_{h}x_{2}
and
x_{2}\leq_{h}x_{3}
.
We need to show that
x_{1}\leq_{h}x_{3}
, i.e. that
L(T_{x_{1}})\sqsubseteq_{L}L(T_{x_{3}})
.
Let
u_{k}\in T_{x_{k}}
for
k=1,2,3
.
By assumption, we have
u_{1}\sqsubseteq_{L}u_{2}
and
u_{2}\sqsubseteq_{L}u_{3}
, so by transitivity of
\sqsubseteq_{L}
we obtain
u_{1}\sqsubseteq_{L}u_{3}
.
As these leaves were chosen arbitrarily, we obtain
L(T_{x_{1}})\sqsubseteq_{L}L(T_{x_{3}})
, implying that indeed
x_{1}\leq_{h}x_{3}
.

Next, take two points
x_{1},x_{2}\in f^{-1}({h})
and assume not
x_{1}\leq_{h}x_{2}
.
Then we know that
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
and not
L(T_{x_{1}})\sqsubseteq_{L}L(T_{x_{2}})
.
Since
\sqsubseteq_{L}
is a total order, this means that there are leaves
u_{1}\in L(T_{x_{1}})
and
u_{2}\in L(T_{x_{2}})
such that
u_{2}\sqsubseteq_{L}u_{1}
.
We now show that
L(T_{x_{2}})\sqsubseteq_{L}L(T_{x_{1}})
.
Consider a leaf
u_{2}^{\prime}\in L(T_{x_{2}})
.
Assume, towards a contradiction, that
u_{1}\sqsubseteq_{L}u_{2}^{\prime}
.
Then we have
u_{2}\sqsubseteq_{L}u_{1}\sqsubseteq_{L}u_{2}^{\prime}
.
By the separating subtree property of
\sqsubseteq_{L}
, we obtain
u_{1}\in T_{\mathrm{lca}(u_{2},u_{2}^{\prime})}
.
As
u_{2},u_{2}^{\prime}
are both leaves in
T_{x_{2}}
, it follows that
\mathrm{lca}(u_{2},u_{2}^{\prime})\preceq x_{2}
, and thus
u_{1}\in T_{x_{2}}
.
However, as
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
, the subtrees
T_{x_{1}}
and
T_{x_{2}}
are disjoint, and we reach a contradiction.
Hence,
L(T_{x_{2}})\sqsubseteq_{L}u_{1}
.
Secondly, consider another leaf
u_{1}^{\prime}\in L(T_{x_{2}})
.
We now show that
u_{2}^{\prime}\sqsubseteq_{L}u_{1}^{\prime}
.
Assume, towards a contradiction, that
u_{1}^{\prime}\sqsubseteq_{L}u_{2}^{\prime}\sqsubseteq_{L}u_{1}
.
Using a similar argument as before, we obtain
u_{2}^{\prime}\in T_{x_{1}}
, contradicting our assumption
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
.
Hence, we conclude that indeed
L(T_{x_{2}})\sqsubseteq_{L}L(T_{x_{1}})
, and thus
x_{2}\leq_{h}x_{1}
.
This shows that
\leq_{h}
is a total order.
∎

Lemma 6
.

The induced layer-order
(\leq_{h})
is consistent.

Proof.

We need to show that for
h_{1}\geq 0
and any two points
x_{1},x_{2}\in f^{-1}({h_{1}})
with
x_{1}\leq_{h_{1}}x_{2}
we also have
x_{1}|^{h_{2}}\leq_{h_{2}}x_{2}|^{h_{2}}
for all
h_{2}\geq h_{1}
.
If we assume that
x_{1}\leq_{h_{1}}x_{2}
, then by construction we know that
L(T_{x_{1}})\sqsubseteq_{L}L(T_{x_{2}})
.
Let
h_{2}\geq h_{1}
and set
\hat{x}_{1}=x_{1}|^{h_{2}}
and
\hat{x}_{2}=x_{2}|^{h_{2}}
.
If
x_{1}=x_{2}
, it immediately follows that
\hat{x}_{1}=\hat{x}_{2}
.
Else, towards a contradiction, assume that we do not have
\hat{x}_{1}\leq_{h_{2}}\hat{x}_{2}
.
We have already shown that
\leq_{h_{2}}
is a total order, so we must have
\hat{x}_{2}<_{h_{2}}\hat{x}_{1}
.
By construction, this means that
L(T_{\hat{x}_{2}})\sqsubseteq_{L}L(T_{\hat{x}_{1}})
.
Observe that because
x_{1}\preceq\hat{x}_{1}
, we have
L(T_{x_{1}})\subseteq L(T_{\hat{x}_{1}})
.
Similarly, we have
L(T_{x_{2}})\subseteq L(T_{\hat{x}_{2}})
.
It follows that
L(T_{x_{2}})\sqsubseteq_{L}L(T_{x_{1}})

Combining with our initial assumption, we get
L(T_{x_{1}})=L(T_{x_{2}})
.
Since
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are from the same layer, this implies that

.
However, this contradicts our assumption that
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are distinct points.
∎

Combining Lemmas

, we see that

induces an ordered merge tree.

Corollary 7
.

\mathcal{T}(T,f,\sqsubseteq_{L})
is an ordered merge tree.

Lemma 8

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L}((T,f,(\leq_{h}))))=(T,f,(\leq_{h}))

Proof.

By Corollaries
4

7

From the definitions of
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{T}

and

, so it suffices to show that

equals
(\leq^{\prime}_{h})

Fix

and let

Assume that
x_{1}\leq_{h}x_{2}

If

Lemma 9
.

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{T}((T,f,\sqsubseteq_{L})))=(T,f,\sqsubseteq_{L})
.

Proof.

By Corollaries
7
and
4

\mathcal{T}((T,f,\sqsubseteq_{L}))=(T^{\prime},f^{\prime},(\leq_{h}))

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{T}((T,f,\sqsubseteq_{L})))=(T^{\prime\prime},f^{\prime\prime},\sqsubseteq_{L}^{\prime})

It immediately follows that
T=T^{\prime}=T^{\prime\prime}
and

, so it suffices to show that

Take

Set

, and set

The maps

3.2
Monotone interleaving distance.

We are now ready to define an order-preserving distance on ordered merge trees.
Specifically, consider two ordered merge trees
(T,f,(\leq_{h}))
and
(T^{\prime},f^{\prime},(\leq^{\prime}_{h}))
.
We say a
\delta
-shift map
\alpha\colon T\to T^{\prime}
is
layer-monotone
, or simply
monotone
, if for all height values
h\geq 0
, and for any two points
x_{1},x_{2}\in f^{-1}({h})
it holds that if
x_{1}\leq_{h}x_{2}
then also
\alpha(x_{1})\leq^{\prime}_{h+\delta}\alpha(x_{2})
.
A
monotone
\delta
-interleaving
, in turn, is a
\delta
-interleaving
(\alpha,\beta)
such that the maps
\alpha
and
\beta
are both monotone.

:

0
, since the rivers induce the same merge trees. The monotone interleaving distance (bottom) is greater than
0
and captures the left-to-right order of the bars better.

Definition 5
.

The
monotone interleaving distance

d_{\mathrm{MI}}^{\mathrm{}}
is the minimum
\delta
that admits a monotone
\delta
-interleaving.

As a monotone interleaving is still an interleaving, the monotone interleaving distance is bounded from below by the regular interleaving distance.
On the contrary, Figure
6
illustrates that an optimal interleaving is not necessarily an optimal monotone interleaving.

4
Relation to 1D Curves

In this section, we establish a connection between the monotone interleaving distance and the Fréchet distance.
An in-order tree walk on an ordered merge tree induces a particular (in-order) curve on the tree, which in turn induces a particular 1D curve.
We show that the monotone interleaving distance between two ordered merge trees equals the Fréchet distance between their induced 1D curves.

Curves.

Let
(T,f,(\leq_{h}))
be an ordered merge tree.
We define a 1D curve

For technical reasons, we require that such a walk starts and ends at the root (at infinity) of

We use

Here,

for points interior of edges of

\mathrm{deg}(x)=0
for leaves of
T

For a curve
\sigma\colon[0,1]\to T
on a tree
T
and a point

\sigma

visits

x
is the number of connected components of
\sigma^{-1}(x)
. If
\sigma^{-1}(x)
is empty for some

, then we say that
x
is unvisited by
\sigma
.
For a vertex

and a strict ancestor

We say that a planted subtree

.
Lastly, we define the
\sigma
-unvisited degree

Definition 6

Let

Let

Matched curves.

Let
(T,f,(\leq_{h}))
and

be two in-order curves on
T

T^{\prime}

The Fréchet distance between the induced 1D curves

and
Q_{\tau^{\prime}}
is the infimum value
\delta
for which a matching
\mu

|f(\tau(t))-f^{\prime}(\tau^{\prime}(\mu(t)))|\leq\delta
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
The composition
\tau^{\prime}\circ\mu
is a reparameterisation of
\tau^{\prime}
, and therefore also an in-order curve of
T^{\prime}
.
Instead of considering matchings
\mu

\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
on
T

T^{\prime}
.
A pair of in-order curves

\delta

for all

\delta

d_{\mathrm{F}}(P,Q)=\inf_{(\tau,\tau^{\prime})}\max_{t\in[0,1]}|f(\tau(t))-f^{\prime}(\tau^{\prime}(t))|.

Theorem 13

d_{\mathrm{F}}(P,Q)=d_{\mathrm{MI}}^{\mathrm{}}(T,T^{\prime})

4.1
From matched in-order curves to monotone interleavings.

Let
\tau\colon[0,1]\to T

\tau^{\prime}\colon[0,1]\to T^{\prime}

\delta
-matched pair of in-order curves on

and
T^{\prime}
respectively.
Then
|f(\tau(t))-f^{\prime}(\tau^{\prime}(t))|\leq\delta
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
We define a map
\alpha\colon T\to T^{\prime}
as follows.
For
x\in T

arbitrarily and set
\alpha(x)=\tau^{\prime}(t)|^{f(x)+\delta}
.
To see that such an ancestor exists, it suffices to show that

\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
are
\delta
-matched.
We now first show that the definition of
\alpha(x)
is independent from the choice of

Lemma 14
.

Fix
x\in T
and let
h=f(x)

For all
t_{1},t_{2}\in\tau^{-1}(x)

\tau^{\prime}(t_{1})|^{h+\delta}=\tau^{\prime}(t_{2})|^{h+\delta}

Proof.

Define
y\coloneqq\tau^{\prime}(t_{1})|^{h+\delta}
.
By Lemma
11

[t_{1},t_{2}]
lies in
T_{x}
.
So, for all
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
we have
f(\tau(t))\leq h
, and thus also
f^{\prime}(\tau^{\prime}(t))\leq h+\delta=f^{\prime}(y)
.
By continuity of
\tau^{\prime}
, it follows that
\tau^{\prime}(t)\preceq y

In this section, we describe two alternative ways to define the monotone interleaving distance.
This result is analogous to the regular setting, where the original interleaving distance by Morozov et al.

7
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we introduced the monotone interleaving distance: an order-preserving distance for a class of ordered merge trees.
The monotone interleaving distance captures more geometric facets of data than the regular interleaving distance, and can hence be a more powerful tool for data that has a geometric interpretation.
We showed that the monotone interleaving distance between two ordered merge trees can be computed in near-quadratic time, by exploiting a relation with the Fréchet distance on 1D curves.
Additionally, we demonstrated that the monotone interleaving distance is an interleaving distance in the categorical sense.
Lastly, analogously to the regular setting, we gave three ways to define the monotone interleaving distance: in terms of an interleaving, in terms of a
\delta
-good map, and in terms of a labelling.

We plan to explore various directions for future work.
Firstly, given that the regular interleaving distance for merge trees is NP-hard to compute, and the monotone interleaving distance can be computed in polynomial time for ordered merge trees, it would be interesting to explore which intermediate variants can still be computed in polynomial time.
Specifically, we could relax the total orders on merge tree layers to partial orders.
Furthermore, it would be interesting to explore more connections between variants of the Fréchet distance and variants of the (monotone) interleaving distance, which may lead to efficient algorithms or approximations for various types of (monotone) interleaving distances.
Finally, we plan to engineer our results to be applicable to real-world river networks.
As the interleaving distance is a bottleneck distance, an optimal interleaving does not give much information about the parts of the trees that are not the bottleneck.
Therefore, to make the distance more meaningful on real data, we consider developing a
locally adaptive variant
of the (monotone) interleaving distance.

NP-Hardness of Interleaving Distance

In this section, we show NP-hardness of computing the interleaving distance.
In existing literature it is commonly accepted that computing the interleaving distance is NP-hard
[
18
,
31
]

[
1
]
to prove NP-hardness of the Gromov-Hausdorff distance would also work for the interleaving distance.
For completeness, we slightly alter their proof and show that computing the interleaving distance is indeed NP-hard.

They describe a reduction from the decision problem called balanced partition.

|L(T)|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\eqqcolon\overline{a}
.
Similarly, we have
|f^{-1}({\delta})^{\prime}|=m\cdot\mu=\overline{a}
.
As
|L(T)|=|f^{-1}({\delta})^{\prime}|
, and
\chi
is surjective, it follows that
\chi
is injective, and hence bijective.

We can now use this bijection to partition
X
into
m
subsets.
For
i\in[n]
, let
u\in T_{p^{\prime}_{i}}
be an arbitrary leaf.
Now, let
q_{j}
be the unique ancestor of
\alpha(u)
at height
\lambda+1
.
We claim that
q_{j}
is independent of the choice for
u
.
Consider two leaves
u_{1},u_{2}\in T_{p^{\prime}_{i}}
, and set
y_{1},y_{2}
to be their respective images under
\alpha
.
Note that
u_{1}^{\lambda}=p^{\prime}_{i}=u_{2}^{\lambda}
.
Then, by continuity, we must have
y\coloneqq y_{1}^{\lambda}=y_{2}^{\lambda}
.
Since
1\leq\delta<3
, we have
\lambda+1\leq f^{\prime}(x_{1})^{\lambda}<\lambda+3
, which means
y
lies on the edge from
q_{j}
to
r
for some
j
.
Hence,
y_{1},y_{2}
lie in the same subtree
T^{\prime}_{q_{j}}
, meaning
q_{j}
is indeed uniquely determined by
i
.
so, we can safely add
a_{i}
to
X_{j}
.
Since
\chi
is a bijection, there are exactly
\mu
leaves that map to each subtree in
T^{\prime}
, meaning that the partition is valid.
∎

Appendix B
Good Maps

The original definition of a
\delta
-good map was given by Touli and Wang
[
31
]
.

Definition 48

(Definition 3 from
[
31
]
)
.

Given two merge trees
(T,f)
and
(T^{\prime},f^{\prime})
, a map
\alpha\colon T\to T
is called
\delta
-good if it is a continuous map such that

[(T1)]

1.

f^{\prime}(\alpha(x))=f(x)+\delta
for all
x\in T
,

2.

if
\alpha(x_{1})\succeq\alpha(x_{2})
, then
x_{1}^{2\delta}\succeq x_{2}^{2\delta}
,

3.

|f^{\prime}(y^{F})-f^{\prime}(y)|\leq 2\delta
for
y\in T^{\prime}\setminus\mathrm{Im}(\alpha)
, where
y^{F}
is the lowest ancestor of
y
in
\mathrm{Im}(\alpha)
.

Gasparovich et al.
[
18
]
use the concept of a
\delta
-good map to show that the label interleaving distance is equal to the interleaving distance.
However, they use a slightly different definition for a
\delta
-good map, and they do not provide a proof that their definition is equivalent.

Definition 49

(Definition 2.11 from
[
18
]
)
.

Given two merge trees
(T,f)
and
(T^{\prime},f^{\prime})
, a map
\alpha\colon T\to T^{\prime}
is called
\delta
-good if it is a continuous map such that

[(G1)]

1.

for all
x\in T
,
f^{\prime}(\alpha(x))=f(x)+\delta
,

2.

for all
y\in\mathrm{Im}(\alpha)
,
f(x^{\prime})-f(x)\leq 2\delta
, where
x^{\prime}=\mathrm{lca}(\alpha^{-1}(y))
, for all
x\in\alpha^{-1}(y)
.

3.

for all
y\in T^{\prime}\setminus\mathrm{Im}(\alpha)
,
0pt(y)\leq 2\delta
, where
0pt(y)
is defined as
f^{\prime}(y)-f^{\prime}(y^{L})
, and where
y^{L}
is the lowest descendant of
y
.

For completeness, we show these definitions are equivalent.

Lemma 50
.

A continuous map
\alpha\colon T\to T^{\prime}
satisfies
(T1)
-
(T3)
iff it satisfies
(G1)
-
(G3)
.

Proof.

We show both directions.

\Rightarrow

First, suppose
\alpha
satisfies
(T1)
-
(T3)
.

[(G1)]

1.

This immediately follows from
(T1)
.

2.

Let
y\in\mathrm{Im}(\alpha)
, set
x^{\prime}=\mathrm{lca}(\alpha^{-1}(y))
and take
x_{1}\in\alpha^{-1}(y)
.
We consider two cases.
If
x_{1}
is the only point in
\alpha^{-1}(y)
, then
x^{\prime}=x_{1}
and thus
f(x^{\prime})-f(x_{1})=0\leq 2\delta
.
Else, let
x_{2}\in\alpha^{-1}(y)
such that
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
.
Observe that
\alpha(x_{1})=\alpha(x_{2})
, so both
\alpha(x_{1})\succeq\alpha(x_{2})
and
\alpha(x_{2})\succeq\alpha(x_{1})
.
By (T2) it then follows that both
x_{1}^{2\delta}\succeq x_{2}^{2\delta}
and
x_{2}^{2\delta}\succeq x_{1}^{2\delta}
, and thus
x_{1}^{2\delta}=x_{2}^{2\delta}
.
Since this must hold for any two points
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
in
\alpha{-1}(y)
, we get
x_{1}^{2\delta}\succeq x^{\prime}
.
In particular, this means
f(x^{\prime})-f(x_{1})\leq 2\delta
.

3.

Let
y\in T^{\prime}\setminus\mathrm{Im}(\alpha)
and towards a contradiction assume
0pt(y)>2\delta
.
Then there is
y_{L}\in T^{\prime}
such that
f^{\prime}(y)-f^{\prime}(y_{L})>2\delta
.
By continuity of
\alpha
and tree properties, it must be the case that
y_{L}\notin\mathrm{Im}(\alpha)
, and that
y_{L}^{F}\succeq y
.
But that means we get
|f^{\prime}(y_{L}^{F})-f^{\prime}(y_{L})|>2\delta
, contradicting property
(T3)
.

\Leftarrow

Secondly, suppose
\alpha
satisfies
(G1)
-
(G3)
.

[(T1)]

1.

This immediately follows from
(G1)
.

2.

Suppose
\alpha(x_{1})\succeq\alpha(x_{2})
.
We consider two cases.
If
x_{1}\succeq x_{2}
, then
x_{1}^{2\delta}\succeq x_{2}^{2\delta}
follows by tree properties.
Else, we know that
x_{1}\neq\mathrm{lca}(x_{1},x_{2})\neq x_{2}
.
Set
y=\mathrm{lca}(\alpha(x_{1}),\alpha(x_{2}))
and observe that
y=\alpha(x_{1})
, as
\alpha(x_{1})\succeq\alpha(x_{2})
.
Define
h=f^{\prime}(\alpha(x_{1}))-f^{\prime}(\alpha(x_{2}))\geq 0
, so that by continuity of
\alpha
, we have
\alpha(x_{2}^{h})=\alpha(x_{1})
.
This means that both
x_{1}\in\alpha^{-1}(y)
and
x_{2}^{h}\in\alpha^{-1}(y)
.
Set
x^{\prime}=\mathrm{lca}(\alpha^{-1}(y))
, so that
x^{\prime}\succeq x_{1}
and
x^{\prime}\succeq x_{2}^{h}\succeq x_{2}

By property
(G2)
, we then obtain
f(x^{\prime})-f(x_{1})\leq 2\delta
.
So, lifting
x_{1}
by
2\delta
yields
x_{1}^{2\delta}\succeq x^{\prime}\succeq x_{2}
.
As
\alpha(x_{1})\succeq\alpha(x_{2})
, and by
(G1)
, we know that
f(x_{1})\geq f(x_{2})
.
Hence, we get our desired result:
x_{1}^{2\delta}\succeq x_{2}^{2\delta}
.

3.

Let
y\in T^{\prime}\setminus\mathrm{Im}(\alpha)
.
Then
|f^{\prime}(y^{F})-f^{\prime}(y)|\leq 0pt(y^{F})\leq 2\delta
by
(G3)
.

This proves the claim.
∎
