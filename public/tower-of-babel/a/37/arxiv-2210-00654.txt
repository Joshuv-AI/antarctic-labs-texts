Relational Models for the Lambek Calculus with Intersection and Constants \rsuper * Thanks: The author is grateful to Daniel Rogozin for fruitful discussions. The work was supported by the Theoretical Physics and Mathematics Advancement Foundation “BASIS”
Stepan L. Kuznetsov ORCID:0000-0003-0025-0133

Abstract
. We consider relational semantics (R-models) for the Lambek calculus extended with intersection and explicit constants for zero and unit. For its variant without constants and a restriction which disallows empty antecedents, Andréka and Mikulás (1994) prove strong completeness. We show that it fails without this restriction, but, on the other hand, prove weak completeness for non-standard interpretation of constants. For the standard interpretation, even weak completeness fails. The weak completeness result extends to an infinitary setting, for so-called iterative divisions (Kleene star under division). We also prove strong completeness results for product-free fragments.

Introduction

The
Lambek calculus

\mathbf{L}
was originally introduced back in 1958 for modelling natural language syntax
[
Lam58
]
. From a modern point of view, the Lambek calculus is considered as the most basic associative substructural logic, being the algebraic logic of
residuated semigroups

[
GJKO07
]
. On the other hand, as noticed by Abrusci
[
Abr90
]
, the Lambek calculus can be viewed as a non-commutative, multiplicative-only variant of Girard’s
[
Gir87
]

linear logic.

A residuated semigroup is a semigroup with a partial order
\preceq
and division operations
\mathop{\backslash}
and
\mathop{/}
obeying the following equivalences:

b\preceq a\mathop{\backslash}c\iff a\cdot b\preceq c\iff a\preceq c\mathop{/}b.

Notice that here divisions and multiplication are connected via partial order, not equality. The idea of such divisions, also called
residuals,
goes back to the works of Krull
[
Kru24
]
and Ward and Dilworth
[
WD39
]
.

Formulae of the Lambek calculus are built from a variables using the operations of residuated semigroups:
\cdot
,
\mathop{\backslash}
,
\mathop{/}
. From the point of view of first-order logic, such formulae are terms in the signature of residuated semigroups. Furthermore,
\mathbf{L}
operates expressions of the form
A\to B
, where
A
and
B
are formulae. Here
\to
corresponds to
\preceq
, thus, from the first-order point of view, these are atomic formulae. We call such expressions
atomic sequents.

The set of theorems of
\mathbf{L}
can be semantically described as the set of all sequents which are true on any residuated semigroup under any interpretation of variables. Syntactically,
\mathbf{L}
is axiomatised as a Gentzen-style sequent calculus
[
Lam58
]
. This calculus is presented in Section
1
below. Derivable objects of this sequent calculus are
sequents
of the form
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\to B
, which generalise atomic sequents defined above.

Natural language applications, connections to linear logic, and algebraic interpretations on residuated structures suggest extending the Lambek calculus with extra operations.
Adding new operations and constants to the algebraic construction of residated semigroups results in extending the Lambek calculus. In this article, we focus on extending
\mathbf{L}
with the following:

•

the unit constant
\mathbf{1}
, which is the unit for multiplication;

•

the zero constant
\mathbf{0}
, which is the smallest element for
\preceq
(using division operations one can also prove that
\mathbf{0}
is the zero element for multiplication);

•

the intersection, or
meet
operation
\wedge
:
a\wedge b=\inf_{\preceq}\{a,b\}
; meet turns the preorder into a lower semilattice.

Note that extending
\mathbf{L}
with
\mathbf{1}
and
\wedge
is also due to Lambek
[
Lam61
,
Lam69
]
.

An intricate issue arises when extending
\mathbf{L}
with the unit constant
\mathbf{1}
: unlike most others, this extension is not a
conservative
one. Consider the atomic sequent
(p\mathop{\backslash}p)\mathop{\backslash}q\to q
, which belongs to the original language of
\mathbf{L}
. This atomic sequent is true on all residuated monoids (i.e., residuated semigroups with the unit). However, it fails to be true on all residuated semigroups (which form a larger class of algebraic structures).

Thus, even in the language without
\mathbf{1}
, the algebraic logic of all residuated monoids differs from
\mathbf{L}
. This logic is called
the Lambek calculus allowing empty antecedents

[
Lam61
]
and is commonly denoted by
\mathbf{L}^{\!\mathbf{*}}
. We prefer, however, an alternative notation,
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}
, in order to avoid notation conflicts with Kleene star which we shall use, in the form of iterative divisions, in one of the sections of this article. The term “… allowing empty antecedents” comes from the Gentzen-style formulation, see Section
1
below.

Residuated algebraic structures provide a very abstract and general framework for semantics of the Lambek calculus, its variants and extensions. This is traditional
provability
semantics, which interprets theoremhood and entailment, not proofs. More modern
semantics of proofs
(see, e.g.,
[
AH96
,
BS96
,
CGS13
,
dPE18
]
) are beyond the scope of this article.

As usual, proving completeness results for abstract algebraic semantics is a simple application of the Lindenbaum – Tarski construction (cf.
[
BP89
]
). This construction gives
strong completeness,
that is, completeness not only for theoremhood, but also for the entailment relation (derivability vs. semantic entailment from sets of hypotheses).
More interesting issues arise when one starts
concretising
algebraic semantics, that is, considers specific classes of algebras. Completeness theorems for such classes, if they hold, are non-trivial results. We shall also see cases where completeness fails, or holds only in the weak sense (for theoremhood, not for entailment).

This article concentrates on
relational models,
or
R-models.
In R-models, variables and formulae are interpreted as binary relations over a non-empty set
W
.
The set
\mathcal{P}(W\times W)
of
all
binary relations over
W
has a natural structure of residuated monoid. Multiplication is relation composition:

R\cdot S=R\circ S=\{(x,z)\mid(\exists y\in W)\,((x,y)\in R\mbox{ and }(y,z)\in S)\}.

Divisions are defined as follows:

\displaystyle R\mathop{\backslash}S=\{(y,z)\mid(\forall x\in W)\,((x,y)\in R\Rightarrow(x,z)\in S\},

\displaystyle S\mathop{/}R=\{(x,y)\mid(\forall z\in W)\,((y,z)\in R\Rightarrow(x,z)\in S\}.

The rôle of the unit is played by the diagonal relation
\delta=\{(x,x)\mid x\in W\}
.
The preorder is the set inclusion relation
\subseteq
. Meet is set-theoretic intersection, and zero is the empty relation
\varnothing
.

Interpretations of the Lambek calculus on residuated monoids of all binary relations on a non-empty set
W
are called
unrelativised
or
square
R-models. Unrelativised R-models give natural semantics for
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}
; the completeness theorem was proved by Andréka and Mikulás
[
AM94
]
. Notice that the argument used for proving this completeness result does not easily extend to
\mathbf{0}
,
\mathbf{1}
, and
\wedge
. Issues with R-models for these extensions form the main topic of this article.

More precisely, constants
\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{1}
ruin completeness w.r.t. square R-models, even in the weak sense (see Section
2
below). In order to overcome this, we introduce variations of R-models with non-standard interpretation of constants.

As for the extension of
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}
with intersection, Mikulás
[
Mik15a
,
Mik15b
]
proves completeness w.r.t. square R-models, but only in the weak sense. We show (Section
4
) that this is essential, and strong completeness fails. On the other hand, we strengthen Mikulás’ result by adding constants
\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{1}
, with non-standard interpretations. Our argument also gives an alternative proof of Mikulás’ result (without constants), which happens to be extendable to an infinitary extension of the calculus, with so-called iterative divisions (Section
5
).
Finally, we show that strong completeness restores for the variant of
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}
with meet (intersection) but without product (Section
7
). Thus, the results presented in this article fill some gaps in the theory of R-models for extensions of
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}
.

In order to provide adequate semantics for the original Lambek calculus
\mathbf{L}
, one needs to relax the definition of R-model. This is done by
relativising
it. Namely, instead of all binary relations on
W
one may now consider only subsets of a fixed transitive relation
U
, which is called the “universal” one. By transitivity,
composition (product) keeps this relativisation in place; for divisions, again, one needs to impose it implicitly:

R\mathop{\backslash}\nolimits_{U}S=\{(y,z)\in U\mid(\forall x\in W)\;((x,y)\in R\Rightarrow(x,z)\in S)\},

and similarly for
S\mathop{/}\nolimits_{\!U}R
. This definition of division operations significantly depends on the choice of
U
. In particular, replacing
U
by a superset might alter
\mathop{\backslash}\nolimits_{U}
and
\mathop{/}\nolimits_{\!U}
. Square R-models are a particular case of relativised ones, with
U=W\times W
.

Since
U
is not required to be reflexive, in relativised R-models we deal with residuated semigroups, not monoids, that is, a class of models for
\mathbf{L}
, not
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}
. And indeed, as proved also by Andréka and Mikulás
[
AM94
]
,
\mathbf{L}
is complete w.r.t. relativised R-models. Moreover, unlike the
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}
case, this result keeps valid for the extension of
\mathbf{L}
with
\wedge
.

Before going further, let us briefly compare R-models with other classes of models for the Lambek calculus and its extensions, which fit into the general algebraic framework.

The original linguistic motivation of the Lambek calculus suggests interpretations on the algebra of formal languages. Such models are called
language models
or
L-models.

In L-models, multiplication is pairwise concatenation, and divisions are defined in a natural way. The difference between
\mathbf{L}
and
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}
is reflected by absence or presence of the empty word in the languages considered. Refraining from the empty word (which is, by the way, motivated by linguistic applications
[
MR12
, § 2.5]
) modifies the definition of divisions, just like relativisation in R-models. Completeness theorems for
\mathbf{L}
and
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}
w.r.t. corresponding versions of L-models were proved by Pentus
[
Pen95
,
Pen98
]
.
Language models might look quite similar to relational ones. However, there is the following significant difference: while any two words can be concatenated, for arrows in relations (i.e., pairs of elements of
W
) this is possible only if the end of the first one coincides with the beginning of the second one. Thus, unlike algebras of formal languages, algebras of relations have
divisors of zero:
if
|W|>1
, there exist non-empty relations
R
and
S
over
W
such that
R\circ S=\varnothing
.

Language models may be viewed as models on powersets of free semigroups (for
\mathbf{L}
) or free monoids (for
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}
). This suggests considering models on powersets of arbitrary semigroups or monoids (see
[
Bus86
,
Bus96
]
), since all of them have well-defined division operations, and also zero, unit, and intersection.

The well-known
phase semantics
for linear logic
[
Gir95
,
Abr91
,
dG05
,
KOT06
]
, which is also connected to denotational semantics
[
BE01
]
, is another species of semantics based on powersets of monoids. The crucial difference from L-models, however, is the usage of a
closure operator,
which in case of phase semantics is the operator taking the biorthogonal of a subset. Another variation of L-models which also uses a closure operation, but of a different nature, is the
syntactic concept lattice
semantics proposed by Wurm
[
Wur17
]
. In the same article Wurm also proposed a similar variant of R-models, also augmented with closure operations.

In the presence of closure operations,
completeness proofs run more smoothly and cover richer extensions of the original system. In particular, besides meet one can also consider join (additive disjunction). For relational or language semantics without closure operations, where meet and join are set-theoretic intersection and union, adding join immediately leads to incompleteness, since the distributivity law (and its corollaries with only divisions and join, but not meet) is generally true, but not derivable in the calculus
[
OK85
,
KKS22
]
. With closure operators, distributivity is no longer generally true, which opens the way to completeness in the presence of join. In this article, we consider R-models without closure operators, and therefore consider only meet, not join.

Finally, relational models considered in this article should not be confused with
ternary
relational semantics for substructural logics (in particular, variants of the Lambek calculus), which offer a more Kripke-style approach (see
[
Doš92
,
CGvR14
]
).

, we give accurate definitions of the calculus in question, denoted by
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}
, and R-models for it. Here we also formulate known completeness results, by Andréka and Mikulás
[
AM94
,
Mik15a
,
Mik15b
]
.

•

In Section

, we provide examples showing that each of the explicit constants
\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{1}
ruins completeness, even in the weak sense (the example for
\mathbf{1}
is not a new one). As a workaround, we relax the definition of R-models and define
R-models with non-standard interpretations of constants.

•

For these non-standard models, in Section
3
we prove weak completeness of
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}
. As a corollary, we obtain an alternative proof of Mikulás’ completeness result
[
Mik15a
,
Mik15b
]
for the fragment of
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}
without constants.

•

Strong completeness fails, as proved in Section
4
. The counterexample used in this section was suggested by Mikulás
[
Mik15b
]
, but without proof. Here we fill this gap.

•

In Section
5
, we extend our weak completeness result to an infinitary setting. Namely, we consider so-called
iterative divisions,
which are Kleene stars in denominators of division operations. Being axiomatised by
\omega
-rules, iterative divisions are in fact infinite intersections. The result of Section
5
solves a natural question suggested by the earlier work
[
KR20
]
. Namely, while
[
KR20
]
features R-completeness for the calculus with positive iterative divisions under Lambek’s restriction, Theorem
16
of Section
5
provides the corresponding completeness result without Lambek’s restriction.

•

The last two sections are devoted to the product-free fragment of
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}
. We show (Section
7
) that, if one removes the product, then strong completeness can be restored, but w.r.t. a modified class of models. However, inside the proof we essentially use the product, which raises a conservativity issue. Conservativity is proved in Section
6
in the strong form, i.e., for derivability from hypotheses, using an extension of
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}
with the exponential modality (which allows a modalised variant of deduction theorem, cf.
[
LMSS92
]
). An important corollary of the strong completeness result is that if we consider the product-free fragment without constants (that is, in the language of
\mathop{\backslash}
,
\mathop{/}
, and
\wedge
), then it will be strongly complete w.r.t. square R-models in the standard sense.

\mathbf{0}

1.
The Lambek Calculus with Intersection and R-Models

Let us recall the formulation of
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}
, the Lambek calculus with intersection and constants, in the form of a Gentzen-style sequent calculus. Formulae are constructed from variables (
p,q,r,\ldots
) and constants
\mathbf{0}
(zero) and
\mathbf{1}
(unit) using four binary connectives:
\cdot
(multiplication),
\mathop{\backslash}
(left division),
\mathop{/}
(right division), and
\wedge
(intersection). The set of all formulae is denoted by
\mathrm{Fm}
. Formulae are denoted by capital Latin letters. Capital Greek letters denote sequences of formulae.
Sequents are expressions of the form
\Pi\to B
. (Due to the non-commutative nature of the Lambek calculus, order in
\Pi
matters.) Here
\Pi
is called the antecedent and
B
the succedent of the sequent.

Letter
\Lambda
, which appears in the name of the calculus, denotes the empty sequence of formulae. However, in sequents with empty antecedents we omit it and write just
{\to B}
instead of
\Lambda\to B
.

The axioms and inference rules are as follows:

\Gamma,\Pi,A\mathop{\backslash}B,\Delta\to C\lx@proof@logical@and\Pi\to A\Gamma,B,\Delta\to C\qquad\Pi\to A\mathop{\backslash}BA,\Pi\to B\qquad\Gamma,A\cdot B,\Delta\to C\Gamma,A,B,\Delta\to C

\Gamma,B\mathop{/}A,\Pi,\Delta\to C\lx@proof@logical@and\Pi\to A\Gamma,B,\Delta\to C\qquad\Pi\to B\mathop{/}A\Pi,A\to B\qquad\Pi,\Delta\to A\cdot B\lx@proof@logical@and\Pi\to A\Delta\to B

\Gamma,A\wedge B,\Delta\to C\Gamma,A,\Delta\to C\qquad\Gamma,A\wedge B,\Delta\to C\Gamma,B,\Delta\to C\qquad\Pi\to A\wedge B\lx@proof@logical@and\Pi\to A\Pi\to B

\Gamma,\mathbf{0},\Delta\to C\qquad\Gamma,\mathbf{1},\Delta\to C\Gamma,\Delta\to C\qquad{}\to\mathbf{1}

We consider both
pure derivability
of sequents and
derivability from hypotheses.
For the latter, having a set of sequents
\mathcal{H}
, we add them as extra non-logical axioms, and derive a given sequent
\Pi\to B
using both the new axioms and axioms and inference rules of the original calculus. In this case, we say that
\Pi\to B
is derivable, or
syntactically follows
from
\mathcal{H}
.
Pure derivability is derivability from an empty
\mathcal{H}
, i.e., using only axioms and rules of the original calculus.

For pure derivability,
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}
enjoys cut elimination, that is, any derivable sequent can be derived without using
Cut
. The proof of cut elimination is standard, being an easy extension of Lambek’s argument
[
Lam58
]
for the Lambek calculus. For derivability from hypotheses, cut elimination, in general, does not hold.

Besides
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}
itself, we consider its
elementary fragments
with restricted sets of connectives and constants. Such a fragment is obtained by merely removing all axioms and rules which operate the connectives and constants which do not belong to the restricted set. In particular, we shall use the notation
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}
for the fragment of
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}
without constants
\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{1}
and
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}
for the fragment with only
\mathop{\backslash}
,
\mathop{/}
, and
\cdot
.

Such a definition of elementary fragment raises issues of
conservativity.
For pure derivability, these issues are resolved by cut elimination. All rules, except
Cut
, enjoy the subformula property, therefore the set of theorems of an elementary fragment coincides with the set of sequents in the corresponding restricted language which are derivable in the full system
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}
. For derivability from hypotheses, the issue is more subtle. In principle, a derivation of
\Pi\to B
from
\mathcal{H}
which uses
Cut
could involve connectives or constants which do not belong to the restricted language of
\mathcal{H}
and
\Pi\to B
. We address this question below in Section
6
.

The original Lambek calculus
\mathbf{L}
is obtained from
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}
by imposing the so-called
Lambek’s non-emptiness restriction,
that is, requiring non-emptiness of
\Pi
in the
\mathop{\backslash}R
and
\mathop{/}R
rules. This condition ensures that antecedents of all sequents in
\mathbf{L}
are non-empty (
\mathop{\backslash}R
and
\mathop{/}R
are the only two rules which could possibly produce an empty antecedent). As noticed in the introduction, Lambek’s restriction has linguistic motivation
[
MR12
, § 2.5]
. One can also consider a version of
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}
with Lambek’s restriction, denoted by
\mathbf{L}{\wedge}
. On the other hand, the constant
\mathbf{1}
is incompatible with Lambek’s restriction, so we shall not add constants to
\mathbf{L}
or
\mathbf{L}{\wedge}
.

It is important to keep in mind that
\mathbf{L}
is not a conservative fragment of
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}
, neither
\mathbf{L}{\wedge}
is such of
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}
. Even if the sequent has a non-empty antecedent, empty antecedents could be necessary inside its derivation. As noticed in the introduction, an example is
(p\mathop{\backslash}p)\mathop{\backslash}q\to q
, which is derivable in
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}
, but not in
\mathbf{L}
. Therefore, there is no easy way of translating results between
\mathbf{L}
and
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}
, and certain properties of these systems differ, as we shall see below.

Now let us define the
relational semantics.
Here we define it for calculi without constants
\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{1}
. Those are handled in the next section, since the most natural ways to interpret constants lead to incompleteness, and therefore non-standard models will be introduced.

{defi}

A relativised relational model (R-model) is a triple
\mathcal{M}=(W,U,v)
, where
W
is a non-empty set,
U\subseteq W\times W
is a transitive relation on
W
called the universal one, and
v\colon\mathrm{Fm}\to\mathcal{P}(U)
is a valuation function mapping formulae to subrelations of
U
. The valuation function should obey the following conditions:

\displaystyle v(A\cdot B)=v(A)\circ v(B)=\{(x,z)\mid\exists y\in W\>\bigl((x,y)\in v(A)\mbox{ and }(y,z)\in v(B)\bigr)\};

\displaystyle v(A\mathop{\backslash}B)=v(A)\mathop{\backslash}\nolimits_{U}v(B)=\{(y,z)\in U\mid\forall x\in W\>\bigl((x,y)\in v(A)\Rightarrow(x,z)\in v(B)\bigr)\};

\displaystyle v(B\mathop{/}A)=v(B)\mathop{/}\nolimits_{\!U}v(A)=\{(x,y)\in U\mid\forall z\in W\>\bigl((y,z)\in v(A)\Rightarrow(x,z)\in v(B)\bigr)\};

\displaystyle v(A\wedge B)=v(A)\cap v(B)=\{(x,y)\mid(x,y)\in v(A)\mbox{ and }(x,y)\in v(B)\}.

{defi}

An R-model
\mathcal{M}=(W,U,v)
is a square one if
U=W\times W
.

Arbitrary R-models and square R-models form natural classes of models for
\mathbf{L}{\wedge}
and
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}
respectively (and, thus, for
\mathbf{L}
and
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}
). Let us define the truth condition of sequents in R-models.

{defi}

A sequent of the form
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\to B
is true in model
\mathcal{M}=(W,U,v)
, if

v(A_{1})\circ\ldots\circ v(A_{n})\subseteq v(B)
. For sequents with empty antecedents, truth is defined only in square R-models:
{\to B}
is true in
\mathcal{M}=(W,W\times W,v)
, if
\delta=\{(x,x)\mid x\in W\}\subseteq v(B)
.

Let us also recall the general notion of
strong
soundness and completeness of a logic
\mathcal{L}
(formulated as a sequent calculus) w.r.t. a class of models
\mathcal{K}
.

{defi}

Let
\Pi\to B
and
\mathcal{H}
be, respectively, a sequent and a set of sequents in the language of
\mathcal{L}
. The sequent
\Pi\to B
semantically follows from
\mathcal{H}
on the class of models
\mathcal{K}
, if for any model from
\mathcal{K}
in which all sequents from
\mathcal{H}
are true, the sequent
\Pi\to B
is also true. This is denoted by
\mathcal{H}\vDash_{\mathcal{K}}\Pi\to B
.

{defi}

In the notations of the previous definition,
\Pi\to B
syntactically follows from
\mathcal{H}
in the logic
\mathcal{L}
, if
\Pi\to B
is derivable in the calculus for
\mathcal{L}
extended with sequents from
\mathcal{H}
as extra axioms. This is denoted by
\mathcal{H}\vdash_{\mathcal{L}}\Pi\to B
.

{defi}

The logic
\mathcal{L}
is strongly sound w.r.t. the class of models
\mathcal{K}
, if
\mathcal{H}\vdash_{\mathcal{L}}\Pi\to B
entails
\mathcal{H}\vDash_{\mathcal{K}}\Pi\to B
for any
\Pi\to B
and
\mathcal{H}
.

{defi}

The logic
\mathcal{L}
is strongly complete w.r.t. the class of models
\mathcal{K}
, if
\mathcal{H}\vDash_{\mathcal{K}}\Pi\to B
entails
\mathcal{H}\vdash_{\mathcal{L}}\Pi\to B
for any
\Pi\to B
and
\mathcal{H}
.

The Lambek calculus is a substructural system which does not enjoy a deduction theorem. Therefore, strong soundness and completeness, even for finite sets
\mathcal{H}
, are significantly different from their more usual weak counterparts (that derivability of a sequent without hypotheses yields its truth in all models from the given class, and the other way round).

One can easily check that
\mathbf{L}{\wedge}
and
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}
are strongly sound w.r.t. the corresponding class of R-models: namely, all R-models for
\mathbf{L}
and square ones for
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}

2.
Constants Zero and One: Nonstandard Models

The situation with constants
\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{1}
is tricky. Naturally,
\mathbf{1}
should be interpreted as the neutral element for multiplication (since
A\cdot\mathbf{1}\leftrightarrow A\leftrightarrow\mathbf{1}\cdot A
) and
\mathbf{0}
as the smallest element in the preorder (due to derivability of
\mathbf{0}\to A
). This suggests the following interpretation of constants in R-models, which we call the
standard interpretation:

\displaystyle v(\mathbf{0})=\varnothing;

\displaystyle v(\mathbf{1})=\delta=\{(x,x)\mid x\in W\}.

Unfortunately, under the standard interpretation completeness fails, even in the weak sense, for each of the constants. Let us start with constant
\mathbf{0}
.

Proposition 4
.

The sequent

\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}p),\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}q)\to(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}q))\cdot(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}p))

is true in all square R-models (under the standard interpretation of
\mathbf{0}
), but not derivable in
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}
.

Proof 2.1
.

Let us first check that sequent is generally true on R-models. For an arbitrary relation
R
we have:

\varnothing\mathop{/}R=\{(x,y)\in W\times W\mid\forall z\in W\>\bigl((y,z)\in R\Rightarrow(x,z)\in\varnothing\bigr)\}=\\
\{(x,y)\in W\times W\mid\forall z\in W\>(y,z)\notin R\}.

Further,

\varnothing\mathop{/}(\varnothing\mathop{/}R)=\{(x,y)\in W\times W\mid\forall z\in W\>(y,z)\notin\varnothing\mathop{/}R\}=\\
\{(x,y)\in W\times W\mid\forall z\in W\>\exists w\in W\>(z,w)\in R\}.

The condition on
(x,y)
here does not depend on
(x,y)
itself. Hence,
\varnothing\mathop{/}(\varnothing\mathop{/}R)
is either
\varnothing
or
W\times W
, for any relation
R
. For these two relations, composition is commutative:

\varnothing\circ(W\times W)=\varnothing=(W\times W)\circ\varnothing.

This yields
(\varnothing\mathop{/}(\varnothing\mathop{/}P))\circ(\varnothing\mathop{/}(\varnothing\mathop{/}Q))\subseteq(\varnothing\mathop{/}(\varnothing\mathop{/}Q))\circ(\varnothing\mathop{/}(\varnothing\mathop{/}P))
for any
P,Q
. Taking
P=v(p)
,
Q=v(q)
, and recalling that
v(\mathbf{0})=\varnothing
(standard interpretation), we conclude that the desired sequent is true.

Now let us perform cut-free proof search in order to show that this sequent is not derivable in
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}
. Two possible cases for the lowermost rule are
\mathop{/}L
and
\cdot R
. In the first case, the left premise of this rule should be
\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}q)\to\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}p
(since
{}\to\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}p
and
{}\to\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}q
are obviously not derivable). The only non-trivial attempt to derive this sequent is as follows:

\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}q)\to\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}p\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}q),p\to\mathbf{0}\lx@proof@logical@and p\to\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}qp,q\to\mathbf{0}\mathbf{0}\to\mathbf{0}

and it fails.

The second possibility is
\cdot R
. One can easily see that
{\to\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}q)}
is not derivable, neither is
{\to\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}p)}
. Hence, the only remaining attempt is as follows:

\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}p),\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}q)\to(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}q))\cdot(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}p))\lx@proof@logical@and\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}p)\to\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}q)\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}p),\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}q\to\mathbf{0}\lx@proof@logical@and\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}q\to\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}p\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}q,p\to\mathbf{0}\mathbf{0}\to\mathbf{0}\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}q)\to\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}p)

The top sequent
\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}q,p\to\mathbf{0}
is not derivable.

Next, we recall two previously known counterexamples to weak completeness with constant
\mathbf{1}
, in square R-models with standard interpretation of this constant.

One example,

\mathbf{1}\wedge p\wedge q\to(\mathbf{1}\wedge p)\cdot(\mathbf{1}\wedge q),

was given by Andréka and Mikulás
[
AM11
]
. In the particular case of
p=q
, this yields the contraction (“doubling”) principle for formulae of the form
\mathbf{1}\wedge A
, that is,
\mathbf{1}\wedge A\to(\mathbf{1}\wedge A)\cdot(\mathbf{1}\wedge A)
. Another such example was given by Buszkowski
[
Bus06
]
:

\mathbf{1}\mathop{/}(p\mathop{/}p)\to(\mathbf{1}\mathop{/}(p\mathop{/}p))\cdot(\mathbf{1}\mathop{/}(p\mathop{/}p)).

This
example uses division instead of intersection, and again it is a form of contraction.

Thus, constructing a complete axiomatisation for the standard interpretation of constants
\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{1}
in R-models, even if this is possible, is a non-trivial open question.

We overcome this issue by extending the class of models being considered,
thus restoring completeness for the original system
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}
. The idea is as follows:
while
\delta
is the only neutral element for the set of
all
binary relations on
W
, for
a set which includes only some relations (and does not include
\delta
), the neutral
element could be a different relation. Similarly for zero, a designated subclass of binary relations could have the smallest element which is not
\varnothing
. This leads to the following definition.

{defi}

Let
\mathfrak{A}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(W\times W)
be a family of binary relations over
W
, closed under
\circ
,
\mathop{\backslash}
,
\mathop{/}
, and
\cap
. Relation
\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}\in\mathfrak{A}
is called the
\mathfrak{A}
-unit if
\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}\circ R=R\circ\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}=R
for any
R\in\mathfrak{A}
. Relation
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}\in\mathfrak{A}
is called the
\mathfrak{A}
-zero if
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}\subseteq R
for any
R\in\mathfrak{A}
.

A standard algebraic argument shows that the
\mathfrak{A}
-unit, if it exists, is unique. Indeed, for another
\mathfrak{A}
-unit
\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{A}
we have
\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime}=\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime}\circ\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}=\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}
. For the
\mathfrak{A}
-zero, uniqueness is obvious.

However,
\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}
is not necessarily the diagonal relation
\delta=\{(x,x)\mid x\in W\}
, as the latter may be outside
\mathfrak{A}
. For example, let
W
be a non-empty set and let
W^{\prime}=W\times\{1,2\}
. For each relation
R
on
W
let us define a relation
R^{\prime}
as follows:
(x,i)R^{\prime}(y,j)
, if
xRy
and
i\leq j
. Let
\mathfrak{A}
be the class of relations of the form
R^{\prime}
. Then
\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}=\delta^{\prime}=\{((x,i),(x,j))\mid i\leq j\}
. Similarly,
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}
is not necessarily
\varnothing
; in fact, it is just the intersection of all relations from
\mathfrak{A}
.

Let us prove two properties of
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}
and
\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}
.

Lemma 5
.

If
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}
is the
\mathfrak{A}
-zero, then it is the zero for relation composition, i.e.,
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}\circ R=R\circ\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}=\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}
for any
R\in\mathfrak{A}
.

Proof 2.2
.

Let us show
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}\circ R=\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}
, the other equality is symmetric. First, since
\mathfrak{A}
is closed under composition, we have
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}\circ R\in\mathfrak{A}
, and therefore
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}\subseteq\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}\circ R
. For the opposite inclusion, we notice that
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}\mathop{/}R
is also in
\mathfrak{A}
, whence
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}\subseteq\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}\mathop{/}R
, which is equivalent to
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}\circ R\subseteq\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}
.

Remark 6
.

Lemma
5
is actually a general algebraic fact that, in residuated partially ordered structures, the least object of the partial order should always be the zero for the multiplication operation. Division operations are crucial here.

Lemma 7
.

If
R\in\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}
is the
\mathfrak{A}
-unit, then
\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}\subseteq R
if and only if
\delta\subseteq R
. In particular,
\delta\subseteq\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}
.

Proof 2.3
.

Let us first show that for any
R\in\mathfrak{A}
we have
R=R\mathop{/}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}
. One inclusion is easy:
R\circ\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}\subseteq R
yields
R\subseteq R\mathop{/}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}
. For the other inclusion, we first notice that
(R\mathop{/}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}})\circ\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}\subseteq R
(this follows from
R\mathop{/}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}\subseteq R\mathop{/}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}
). Now, since
(R\mathop{/}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}})\in\mathfrak{A}
, we have
R\mathop{/}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}=(R\mathop{/}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}})\circ\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}\subseteq R
.
Using
R=R\mathop{/}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}
, we build a chain of equivalences:

\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}\subseteq R\iff\delta\circ\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}\subseteq R\iff\delta\subseteq R\mathop{/}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}\iff\delta\subseteq R.

Due to this lemma, we may keep the truth definition for sequents with empty antecedents the same. That is, we do not need to replace
\delta
with
\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}
.

Remark 8
.

It is important to note that computations in the proof of Lemma
7
go beyond the family
\mathfrak{A}
, since it is possible that
\delta\notin\mathfrak{A}
. This does not cause any problems, because the introduction of
\mathfrak{A}
does not change the definitions of operations, in particular, divisions. They are still computed on the whole
\mathcal{P}(W\times W)
, as in a standard square R-model.

Remark 9
.

One of the reviewers noticed that
\mathfrak{A}
-units may be viewed as “super-identities,” as opposed to more well-known subidentities, which appear, e.g., in the theory of domain semirings
[
DS11
]
. The behaviour of such “super-identities” in more general algebraic settings might be a topic for further study.

Now we are ready to define our non-standard models.

{defi}

A non-standard square R-model is a structure
\mathcal{M}^{\mathfrak{A}}=(W,\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}},\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}},v)
, where
W
is a non-empty set;
\mathfrak{A}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(W\times W)
is a family of binary relations on
W
, closed under
\circ
,
\mathop{\backslash}
,
\mathop{/}
, and
\cap
;
\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}
is the
\mathfrak{A}
-unit;
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}
is the
\mathfrak{A}
-zero;
v\colon\mathrm{Fm}\to\mathfrak{A}
is a valuation function mapping formulae to relations from the family
\mathfrak{A}
. The valuation function should obey the conditions from Definition
1
, with
U=W\times W
, and, additionally,
v(\mathbf{1})=\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}
and
v(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}
. The truth of a sequent in a non-standard square R-model is defined exactly as in Definition

.

Proposition 10
.

The calculus
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}
is strongly sound w.r.t. the class of non-standard square R-models.

Proof 2.4
.

As usual, we proceed by induction on the derivation. The interesting cases are
\mathbf{1}L
,
\mathbf{1}R
, and
\mathbf{0}L
, as others are copied from the standard strong soundness proof of
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}
w.r.t. square R-models (without constants
\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{1}
).

For
\mathbf{1}R
, we have to show that
{\to\mathbf{1}}
is true, that is,
\delta\subseteq v(\mathbf{1})=\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}
. This is a particular case of Lemma
7
.

For
\mathbf{1}L
, we consider two cases. If both
\Gamma
and
\Delta
are empty, then our induction hypothesis gives
\delta\subseteq v(C)
. By Lemma
7
, this is equivalent to
\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}\subseteq v(C)
(recall that
v(C)\in\mathfrak{A}
), which is the truth of
\mathbf{1}\to C
. If, say,
\Delta
is non-empty, then let
D_{1}
be the first formula of
\Delta
. By definition of the
\mathfrak{A}
-unit, we have
v(\mathbf{1})\circ v(D_{1})=\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}\circ v(D_{1})=v(D_{1})
. Thus, interpretations of left-hand sides of the premise and the conclusion are identical. The case of non-empty
\Gamma
is symmetric.

For
\mathbf{0}L
, since the antecedent includes
\mathbf{0}
, its interpretation is
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}
by Lemma

. By definition of the
\mathfrak{A}
-zero,
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}\subseteq v(C)
, where

is the succedent.

As for completeness, we prove only its weak version (Section
3
). For strong completeness, there is a counterexample (Section
4
).

3.
Weak Completeness

We prove weak completeness of
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}
w.r.t. the class of non-standard square R-models, as defined in the previous section.

Theorem 11
.

If a sequent (in the language of

Remark 13
.

Andréka and Mikulás
[
AM94
]
in their proof of Theorem
1
use a more abstract algebraic framework: labels on graph edges are not formulae but elements of a residuated semi-lattice (that is, algebraic model for
\mathbf{L}
, see
[
GJKO07
]
for details). In other words, strong completeness appears as a corollary of a purely algebraic representation theorem. In our case, however, the representation theorem holds only for the Lindenbaum–Tarski algebra, which consists of equivalence classes of formulae. Indeed, the representation theorem for arbitrary algebras would have yielded strong completeness, which does not hold (see Section
4
below). Thus, it does not matter whether to use formulae (as we do) or elements of this algebra as labels.

Remark 14
.

If one takes the reduct of a non-standard square R-model by removing constants
\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{1}
, the result will be a square R-model in the usual sense.
In particular, this holds for our universal model
\mathcal{M}_{0}^{\mathfrak{A}}
.
Thus, we obtain an alternative proof of Mikulás’ Theorem
1
. In our construction, labels on edges are formulae, while Mikulás used filters, which are sets of formulae. This is achieved by using the explicit unit constant: without
\mathbf{1}
, there are incompatible formulae which should be labels of the same loop (e.g.,

and

for different variables

). The tradeoff for this simplification is the use of a more complicated, non-standard interpretations of constants. Below (Section

4.

Unlike the case with Lambek’s restriction, for

\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}

In order to make our syntax simpler, we shall not consider arbitrary infinite conjunctions, since this would require development of an infinitary formula language. We concentrate on one important particular case, in which the formula language is finitary (while proofs could be infinitary). This particular case of infinitary conjunction is connected to the Kleene star.

In relational models, the Kleene star is the operation of taking the reflexive-transitive closure of a relation. Thus, it can be represented as an infinite union:

\Pi\to B
also do not contain constants
\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{1}
, we use statement (
3
) and get derivability of
\Pi\to B
from
\mathcal{H}
in
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}_{\mathop{\backslash},\mathop{/}}{\wedge}
.

7.
Strong Completeness without Product

In this section we are going to prove strong completeness results for the product-free fragments of
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}
and
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}
. For the first one, denoted by
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}_{\mathop{\backslash},\mathop{/}}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}
, strong completeness will be proved for a
modified
class of non-standard square R-models, which we call
product-free non-standard square R-models.
This class could look
ad hoc
and artificial, but using such models will allow proving strong completeness of
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}_{\mathop{\backslash},\mathop{/}}{\wedge}
(the product-free calculus without constants
\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{1}
) w.r.t. square R-models in the standard sense.

In order to define product-free non-standard square R-models, we first relax the notions of
\mathfrak{A}
-unit and
\mathfrak{A}
-zero (Definition
2
) by allowing
\mathfrak{A}
not to be closed under composition. (At the same time,
\mathfrak{A}
should still be closed under
\mathop{\backslash}
,
\mathop{/}
,
\cap
.) The definition of
\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}
and
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}
remains the same:
\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}\circ R=R\circ\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}=R
and
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}\subseteq R
for any
R\in\mathfrak{A}
. Uniqueness of
\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}
and
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}
(if they exist) still holds.

As for properties of
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}
and
\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}
, the situation is trickier. Lemma
7
still holds, with the same proof. (Note that Remark
8
also applies here.) As for Lemma
5
, we prove only one inclusion:

Lemma 21
.

If
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}
is the
\mathfrak{A}
-zero, where
\mathfrak{A}
is not necessarily closed under composition, then
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}\circ R\subseteq\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}
and
R\circ\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}\subseteq\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}
for any
R\in\mathfrak{A}
.

Proof 7.1
.

Exactly as the corresponding part of the proof of Lemma
5
.

Corollary 22
.

If
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}
is the
\mathfrak{A}
-zero, where
\mathfrak{A}
is not necessarily closed under composition,
then
R_{1}\circ\ldots\circ R_{m}\circ\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}\circ S_{1}\circ\ldots\circ S_{k}\subseteq\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}
for any
R_{1},\ldots,R_{m},S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\in\mathfrak{A}
.

Proof 7.2
.

By Lemma
21
, using monotonicity of
\circ
w.r.t.
\subseteq
.

The definition of our modified models, product-free non-standard square R-models, is the same as Definition
2
, but
\mathfrak{A}
is not required to be closed under composition, and the
\mathfrak{A}
-unit and
\mathfrak{A}
-zero are understood also in this relaxed version of definition.

Proposition 23
.

The calculus
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}_{\mathop{\backslash},\mathop{/}}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}
is strongly sound w.r.t. the class of product-free non-standard square R-models.

Proof 7.3
.

Again, we basically repeat the proof of Proposition
10
. First we notice that for any product-free formula
A
we have
v(A)\in\mathfrak{A}
. (For formulae with product, this is now, in general, not true.) This allows considering the cases of
\mathbf{1}R
and
\mathbf{1}L
exactly as in the proof of Proposition
10
, using Lemma
7
. For
\mathbf{0}L
, we use Corollary
22
and show that the interpretation
R
of the antecedent is a subset of
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}
. Since
C
, the succedent, is product-free, we have
R\subseteq\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}\subseteq v(C)
.

Notice that the original calculus
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}
is, in general, not sound w.r.t. these models even in the weak sense. For example, Lemma
21
lacks the other inclusion,
\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}\subseteq\mathbf{0}_{\mathfrak{A}}\circ R
, which prevents from validating the sequent
\mathbf{0}\to\mathbf{0}\cdot r
. Fortunately, this sequent is not a product-free one.

Now we are ready to prove strong completeness for
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}_{\mathop{\backslash},\mathop{/}}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}
.

Theorem 24

The calculus
\mathbf{L}^{\!\Lambda}_{\mathop{\backslash},\mathop{/}}{\wedge}\mathbf{0}\mathbf{1}

As mentioned in the previous section, inside our proofs we shall use multiplication. Due to this reason, the set

Concluding Remarks

The completeness and incompleteness results presented in this article reveal the following peculiarities of relational semantics for the Lambek calculus and its extensions. First, Lambek’s non-emptiness restriction indeed matters, and without it strong completeness surprisingly fails when the system includes both multiplicative and additive conjunctions (i.e., product and intersection). Weak completeness still holds. One of the conjunctions is fine for strong completeness. Second, adding one or both explicit constants
\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{1}
ruins completeness even in the weak sense. Fortunately, there are easy and arguably natural ways of modifying the models which restore completeness.

The counterexamples to weak completeness with constants are actually very deep ones. They show that these constants allow building constructions (e.g.,
\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}A)
,
\mathbf{1}\wedge B
, or
\mathbf{1}\mathop{/}(C\mathop{/}C)
) which obey structural rules not available for arbitrary formulae. In particular, formulae of the form
\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}(\mathbf{0}\mathop{/}A)
even obey classical Boolean logic! This suggests that axiomatising the
standard
interpretations for these constants is probably a hard problem. Here one would have to somehow hybridise the Lambek calculus itself and another calculus with more structural rules. (For comparison, see
[
Kuz14
]
where such attempts were made for the case of language semantics.)

From the point of view of complexity, we see that these constructions using constants behave much like exponential or subexponential modalities (i.e., enable structural rules, including contraction), which would probably make the complete theories for standard interpretations undecidable, cf.
[
LMSS92
,
CH16
]
. These undecidability conjectures are supported by the analogous situation with constant
\mathbf{1}
in language models, where undecidability was established
[
KKS22
]
. Even more interesting things in complexity could happen if we add some sort of infinitary operations, like Kleene star or iterated divisions, cf.
[
KS22
]
. By now, we leave these questions aside as an interesting area of further research.
