1.1.
Principal ideas for the proof of Theorem
1

It turns out that for real elliptic matrices
B
the maximal operator
{\mathscr{M}}^{B}
is bounded in
L^{p}(\Omega)
for all
p\in(1,\infty]
(see Corollary
4
).

The
first idea
for proving Theorem
1
comes from afar (for instance, see
[
34
]
): by triangular inequality,

(5)

{\mathscr{M}}^{A}f(x)\leqslant{\mathscr{M}}^{B}f(x)+\sup_{t>0}\left|{T^{A}_{t}f(x)-T^{B}_{t}f(x)}\right|,

therefore we reduce the problem to proving that the second maximal operator on the right-hand side is bounded in
L^{p}(\Omega)
. The point is that the maximal operator

{\mathscr{M}}^{A,B}f(x)=\sup_{t>0}\left|{T^{A}_{t}f(x)-T^{B}_{t}f(x)}\right|

is expected to behave much better than
{\mathscr{M}}^{A}
. As an example, consider the case of the two semigroups
\exp(-tzL)
and
\exp(-tL)
, where
\operatorname{Re}z>0
and
L=-\Delta
is the positive Euclidean Laplacian. The Fourier multiplier
m_{z}(\xi):=\exp(-z\left|{\xi}\right|^{2})-\exp(-\left|{\xi}\right|^{2})
satisfies the estimates
\left|{m_{z}(\xi)}\right|\,\text{\posebni\char 46\relax}\,\min(|\xi|,|\xi|^{-1})
and
\left|{\xi\cdot\nabla m_{z}(\xi)}\right|\,\text{\posebni\char 46\relax}\,1
, from which one can prove
[
4
,
6
,
5
]
that the square function

f\mapsto\left(\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\left|{\exp(-2^{n}zL)f-\exp(-2^{n}L)f}\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

and, consequently, the maximal operator

f\mapsto\sup_{t>0}\left|{\exp(-tL)f-\exp(-tzL)f}\right|

are bounded in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
p\in(1,\infty)
.

The
second idea
used in the proof of Theorem
1
comes from
[
15
]
, where the author developed a subordination method for studying the maximal operator
\sup_{t>0}\left|{m(t{\mathscr{A}})f}\right|
associated with, say, a generator
{\mathscr{A}}
of a holomorphic semigroup with bounded imaginary powers on
L^{p}
and a multiplier
m\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\,\text{\rm d}\lambda/\lambda)
: at least formally we have

(6)

m(t{\mathscr{A}})f=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}t^{iu}[{\mathcal{M}}m](u){\mathscr{A}}^{iu}f\,\text{\rm d}u,

where
{\mathcal{M}}m
denotes the Mellin transform of
m
, which is defined by rule

(7)

[{\mathcal{M}}m](u)=\int^{\infty}_{0}m(\lambda)\lambda^{-iu}\frac{\,\text{\rm d}\lambda}{\lambda},\quad u\in\mathbb{R}.

It follows from (
6
) that

(8)

{\left\|{\sup_{t>0}\left|{m(t{\mathscr{A}})f}\right|}\right\|_{p}}\leqslant\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|{[{\mathcal{M}}m](u)}\right|{\left\|{{\mathscr{A}}^{iu}f}\right\|_{p}}\,\text{\rm d}u.

This reduces the estimate of the
L^{p}
-norm of maximal function on the left-hand side of (
6
) to finding poinwise estimates of
{\mathcal{M}}m
and
L^{p}
-estimates of imaginary powers of
{\mathscr{A}}
.

If we apply this method to the multiplier

(9)

m_{\pm\theta}(\lambda):=\exp(-e^{\pm i\theta}\lambda)-\exp(-\lambda),\quad\lambda>0,\quad 0<\theta<\pi/2,

and the generator
L_{B}
where
B\in{\mathcal{A}}(\Omega)
is real, then it follows from
[
10
, Theorem 3]
that
{\mathscr{M}}^{e^{\pm i\theta}B,B}
is bounded in
L^{p}(\Omega)
whenever the matrix
A=e^{\pm i\theta}B
is
p
-elliptic (see Section
2.2
for details). This argument breaks down when the complex rotations of
B
are replaced by a general
A\in{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\Omega)
, because the two generators
L_{A}
and
L_{B}
, in general, are not related via functional calculus (see also the comments in Section
3
). Let us explain how we get around this.

Let
A,B\in{\mathcal{A}}(\Omega)
, where
B
is real. We have the Duhamel’s formula:

(10)

T^{A}_{t}f-T^{B}_{t}f=\int^{t}_{0}T^{B}_{s}(L_{B}-L_{A})T^{A}_{t-s}f\,\text{\rm d}s,\quad\forall f\in(L^{2}\cap L^{p})(\Omega).

Duhamel’s formula is typically used for showing that the difference of the two semigroups is small (with respect to some topology) whenever
A-B
is small, for example, with respect to the
L^{\infty}
-norm. So it would seem futile to use the estimate

(11)

\left|{T^{A}_{t}f-T^{B}_{t}f}\right|\leqslant\left|{\int^{t}_{0}L_{B}T^{B}_{s}T^{A}_{t-s}f\,\text{\rm d}s}\right|+\left|{\int^{t}_{0}T^{B}_{s}L_{A}T^{A}_{t-s}f\,\text{\rm d}s}\right|,

but actually it works.

For simplicity consider the first term on the right-hand side (
11
). Our idea is to transfer a fractional power of
L_{B}
from the first semigroup to the second one as follows:

L_{B}T^{B}_{s}T^{A}_{t-s}=L^{1-\alpha}_{B}T^{B}_{s}L^{\alpha}_{B}T^{A}_{t-s},\quad\alpha\in(0,1).

Now we would like to transform the fractional power of
L_{B}
into a fractional power of
L_{A}
, more precisely we aim at the decomposition

L^{\alpha}_{B}=U^{B,A}_{\alpha}L^{\alpha}_{A},

where
U^{B,A}_{\alpha}
is a bounded operator on
L^{p}(\Omega)
.
Yet this is not possible for all
A,B,p,\alpha
, because for, say,
1/2\leqslant\alpha<1
and
p=2
, the domains of
L^{\alpha}_{A}
and
L^{\alpha}_{B}
are either unrelated or unknown. This procedure with
B=I
,
\alpha=1/2
and
p=2
would require a solution to the Kato problem for the square root of
L_{A}
which is still an open problem in the general setting we consider in this paper.

However, by combining a classic result of Kato
[
26
]
with
[
10
, Theorem 3]
and a classic complex interpolation argument, we are able to make the procedure described above work for a small positive power
\alpha=\alpha(p)<1/2
whenever
A\in{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\Omega)
(see Proposition
8
). This gives

\int^{t}_{0}L_{B}T^{B}_{s}T^{A}_{t-s}f\,\text{\rm d}s=\int^{t}_{0}\psi_{1-\alpha}(sL_{B})U^{B,A}_{\alpha}\psi_{\alpha}((t-s)L_{A})f\,\text{\rm d}\mu^{t}_{\alpha}(s)

with
U^{B,A}_{\alpha}
as above,

(12)

\,\text{\rm d}\mu^{t}_{\alpha}(s)=s^{\alpha-1}(t-s)^{-\alpha}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,t]}(s)\,\text{\rm d}s,\quad t>0,

and

(13)

\psi_{\beta}(\lambda)=\lambda^{\beta}e^{-\lambda},\quad\lambda>0,\quad\beta>0.

The advantage of this decomposition is that
\mu^{t}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R})
is a finite measure on
\mathbb{R}
with total mass independent of
t
. More precisely,

(14)

\mu^{t}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R})=B(\alpha,1-\alpha)=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\alpha\pi)},\quad\forall t>0.

Then, by using Cowling’s subordination
[
15
]
twice in combination with our functional calculus result in
[
10
, Proposition 3]
, we obtain that the two-variable maximal operator

\sup_{0<s\leqslant t}\left|{\psi_{1-\alpha}(sL_{B})U^{B,A}_{\alpha}\psi_{\alpha}((t-s)L_{A})f}\right|

is bounded in
L^{p}(\Omega)
, whenever
A\in{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\Omega)
(see Section
3.1
). In this way we can prove that
{\mathscr{M}}^{A,B}
— and thus
{\mathscr{M}}^{A}
— is bounded in
L^{p}(\Omega)
whenever
A\in{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\Omega)
.

2.1.
Cowling’s subordination technique

It is by now a classic result in semigroup theory that for a semigroup
(S_{t})_{t>0}
of linear transformations on some Lebesgue spaces
L^{p}
, the boundedness of the associated maximal ergodic operator in
L^{p}
together with a
H^{\infty}
-calculus of angle
<\pi/2
in
L^{p}
for the negative generator implies the boundedness of the associated maximal operator in
L^{p}
. One way to prove this result is to use Cowling’s subordination technique
[
15
]
(see also
[
31
,
8
]
) which we briefly describe below in the specific case we are interested in here (see (
17
), Proposition
3
and Corollary
4
). Note that Cowling originally formulated his argument only for symmetric semigroups. Note also that as an alternative to Cowling’s subordination method, we can slightly modify an argument of Stein
[
33
, Chapter III]
that exploits complex interpolation and fractional integration.

Fix
A\in{\mathcal{A}}(\Omega)
. Define the associated
maximal ergodic operator
by the rule

(16)

{\mathscr{E}}^{A}f(x)=\sup_{t>0}\left|{\frac{1}{t}\int^{t}_{0}T^{A}_{s}f(x)\,\text{\rm d}s}\right|,\quad f\in L^{2}(\Omega).

A rapid calculation shows that for all
f\in(L^{2}\cap L^{p})(\Omega)
and
t>0
we have

T^{A}_{t}f=\frac{1}{t}\int^{t}_{0}T^{A}_{s}f\,\text{\rm d}s-\frac{1}{t}\int^{t}_{0}\psi_{1}(sL_{A})f\,\text{\rm d}s,

where
\psi_{\alpha}
,
\alpha>0
, is defined by (
13
) and the bounded operator
\psi_{1}(tL_{A})
on
L^{2}(\Omega)
is defined by means of McIntosh’s
L^{2}
-functional calculus
[
30
]
.
Therefore,

(17)

\sup_{t>0}\left|{T^{A}_{t}f(x)}\right|\leqslant{\mathscr{E}}^{A}f(x)+\sup_{t>0}\left|{\psi_{1}(tL_{A})f(x)}\right|,\quad\forall f\in(L^{2}\cap L^{p})(\Omega).

Proposition 3
.

Let
p>1
. Suppose that
A\in{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\Omega)
. For every
\alpha>0
there exists
C>0
, which depends only on
\alpha
,
p
and the
p
-ellipticity constants of
A
, such that

{\left\|{\sup_{t>0}\left|{\psi_{\alpha}(tL_{A})f}\right|}\right\|_{p}}\leqslant C{\left\|{f}\right\|_{p}},\quad\forall f\in L^{p}(\Omega).

Proof.

We use Cowling’s subordination (
8
). The Mellin transform (
7
) of
\psi_{\alpha}
is

(18)

[{\mathcal{M}}\psi_{\alpha}](u)=\Gamma(\alpha-iu),\hskip 30.0pt\text{ for all }u\in\mathbb{R},

where
\Gamma
denotes the Euler gamma function. Stirling’s formula gives

(19)

\left|{\Gamma(\alpha-iu)}\right|\sim\sqrt{2\pi}\,|u|^{\alpha-1/2}e^{-\frac{\pi}{2}|u|},\quad\textrm{for }|u|\rightarrow\infty.

Therefore,

{\left\|{\sup_{t>0}\left|{\psi_{\alpha}(tL_{A})f}\right|}\right\|_{p}}\,\text{\posebni\char 46\relax}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+|u|)^{\alpha-1/2}e^{-\frac{\pi}{2}|u|}{\left\|{L^{iu}_{A}f}\right\|_{p}}\,\text{\rm d}u.

Now the desired result follows from Proposition
2
.
∎

Corollary 4
.

Let

be a real matrix. Then
{\mathscr{M}}^{B}
is bounded in

, for every

Proof.

As remarked before, when

is positive and contractive on

and on

by the duality
(T^{B}_{t})^{*}=T^{B^{*}}_{t}

[

. Hence, by the Hopf–Dunford–Schwartz theorem,
{\mathscr{E}}^{B}
is bounded in
L^{p}(\Omega)
for all

. Moreover,
B\in{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\Omega)
for all

[

, therefore by Proposition

the maximal operator

is bounded in

for all

. The desired result for

now follows from (

.
∎

Proposition 5
.

Let

be a real matrix. Suppose that

Recall the definition (

As for the maximal operator associated with

Now use the assumptions, the fact that

Proof of Theorem

The proof of Proposition

-elliptic matrix

and
T^{B}_{t}
may not commute and the generators
L_{A}
and
L_{B}
may not have a joint functional calculus. As we already explained in Section

, despite this difficulty, we prove Theorem
1

by combining Corollary
4
with Proposition
2
and using Cowling’s subordination (
6
) appropriately. To do that, another ingredient is needed: a classic result of Kato
[
26
]
concerning the domain of the fractional powers of Kato-sectorial operators, which we state below in the particular case we will use in this paper.

For every
\alpha\in[0,1/2]
denote by

{\mathscr{V}}_{\alpha}=\left[L^{2}(\Omega),{\mathscr{V}}\right]_{2\alpha}

the complex interpolation space of index
2\alpha
between
L^{2}(\Omega)
and the domain
{\mathscr{V}}
of the sesquilinear form associated with
L_{A}
,
A\in{\mathcal{A}}(\Omega)
.

Lemma 7

(Kato
[
26
]
and Lions
[
28
]
)
.

Let
A,B\in{\mathcal{A}}(\Omega)
and
0<\alpha<1/2
. Then

\operatorname{D}_{2}(L^{\alpha}_{A})=\operatorname{D}_{2}(L^{\alpha}_{B})={\mathscr{V}}_{\alpha},

and there exist
C_{\alpha},C^{\prime}_{\alpha}>0
depending only on
\alpha
and the ellipticity constants of
A
and
B
such that

(20)

C_{\alpha}{\left\|{L^{\alpha}_{A}f}\right\|_{2}}\leqslant{\left\|{L^{\alpha}_{B}f}\right\|_{2}}\leqslant C^{\prime}_{\alpha}{\left\|{L^{\alpha}_{A}f}\right\|_{2}},\quad\forall f\in{\mathscr{V}}_{\alpha}.

Moreover, for every
\alpha_{0}\in[0,1/2)
we have
\sup_{0\leqslant\alpha\leqslant\alpha_{0}}\left(\left|{C_{\alpha}}\right|^{-1}+\left|{C^{\prime}_{\alpha}}\right|\right)<+\infty
.

Recall that for every
A\in{\mathcal{A}}(\Omega)
we have
{\rm N}_{2}(L_{A})={\rm N}_{2}(L_{I})
and that for simplicity in this paper we always assume that
\Omega
and
{\mathscr{V}}
are such that
{\rm N}_{2}(L_{I})=\{0\}
. Therefore
\overline{{\rm R}}_{2}(L_{A})=L^{2}(\Omega)
and (
20
) is equivalent to the fact that

U^{B,A}_{\alpha}:=L^{\alpha}_{B}L^{-\alpha}_{A}

extends to a bounded (invertible) operator in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, for all
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
and all
A,B\in{\mathcal{A}}(\Omega)
. This together with a standard complex interpolation argument based on (
15
) gives the next proposition which extends the
L^{2}
-boundedness of
U^{B,A}_{\alpha}
to the
L^{p}
-boundedness in the range of
p
-ellipticity of
A
and
B
.

Proposition 8
.

Let
p>1
. Suppose that
A,B\in{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\Omega)
. Then there exists
\alpha=\alpha(p)\in(0,1/2)
such that
U^{B,A}_{\alpha}
extends to a bounded operator in
L^{p}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

For simplicity, suppose that
1<p<2
. For every
\alpha>0
define the strip

\Sigma_{\alpha}=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:\ 0<\operatorname{Re}z<\alpha\}.

Fix
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1/2)
and consider the family of operators

S_{z}=e^{z^{2}}L^{i\operatorname{Im}z}_{B}U^{B,A}_{\operatorname{Re}z}L^{-i\operatorname{Im}z}_{A},\quad z\in\Sigma_{\alpha_{0}}.

The imaginary powers of
L_{A}
and
L_{B}
are bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega)
(this is a consequence of
[
16
]
, but it also follows from (
15
) applied with
p=2
) and, by Lemma
7
, the operators
U^{B,A}_{\operatorname{Re}z}
are bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega)
uniformly in
z\in\overline{\Sigma}_{\alpha_{0}}
. Hence,

(21)

{\left\|{S_{z}}\right\|_{2}}\leqslant C(\alpha_{0}),\quad\forall z\in\overline{\Sigma}_{\alpha_{0}}.

Moreover, by (
15
) we have

{\left\|{S_{i\sigma}}\right\|_{p}}\leqslant C_{p},\quad\forall\sigma\in\mathbb{R}.

Suppose we have already proved that for all
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
the function

z\mapsto S_{z}f\in L^{2}(\Omega)

is continuous and bounded on
\overline{\Sigma}_{\alpha_{0}}
, and holomorphic in the interior of the strip. Then, by Stein’s complex interpolation theorem, for every
q\in(p,2)
there exist
\alpha(q)\in(0,\alpha_{0})
and
C_{q}>0
such that

{\left\|{U^{B,A}_{\alpha(q)}}\right\|_{q}}\leqslant C_{q}.

This proves the proposition, because the interval of
p
-ellipticity of
A
and
B
is open
[
11
]
.

It remains to show that
z\mapsto S_{z}f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is continuous and bounded on
\overline{\Sigma}_{\alpha_{0}}
, and holomorphic in the interior of the strip. We first show that

(22)

S_{z}f=e^{z^{2}}L^{z}_{B}L^{-z}_{A}f,\quad\forall z\in\Sigma_{1/2},\quad\forall f\in\operatorname{R}_{2}(L_{A}).

Indeed, since the imaginary powers of
L_{B}
are bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, we have
\operatorname{D}(L^{z}_{B})=\operatorname{D}(L^{\operatorname{Re}z}_{B})
and
L^{z}_{B}=L^{i\operatorname{Im}z}_{B}L^{\operatorname{Re}z}_{B}
; see, for example,
[
24
, Proposition 3.2.1 (c)]
applied with
\alpha=i\operatorname{Im}z
and
\beta=\operatorname{Re}z
. Therefore, by Lemma
7
we have

(23)

L^{z}_{B}=L^{i\operatorname{Im}z}_{B}L^{\operatorname{Re}z}_{B}L^{-\operatorname{Re}z}_{A}L^{\operatorname{Re}z}_{A}=L^{i\operatorname{Im}z}_{B}U^{B,A}_{\operatorname{Re}z}L^{\operatorname{Re}z}_{A}.

Using once again
[
24
, Proposition 3.2.1 (c)]
we deduce that

(24)

L^{\operatorname{Re}z}_{A}L^{-z}_{A}\subset\overline{L^{\operatorname{Re}z}_{A}L^{-z}_{A}}=L^{\operatorname{Re}z-z}_{A}=L^{-i\operatorname{Im}z}_{A}.

By
[
24
, Proposition 3.1.1 (c)]
we have
L_{A}=L^{z+(1-z)}_{A}=L^{1-z}_{A}L^{z}_{A}=L^{z}_{A}L^{1-z}_{A}
, which implies

(25)

\operatorname{R}_{2}(L_{A})\subseteq\operatorname{R}_{2}(L^{z}_{A}),\quad\forall z\in\overline{\Sigma}_{1}.

Now (
22
) follows by combining (
23
) with (
24
) and (
25
).

We are now ready to study the regularity of
z\mapsto S_{z}f
. Fix
f\in\operatorname{D}_{2}(L_{A})\cap\operatorname{R}_{2}(L_{A})
and
g\in\operatorname{D}_{2}(L_{B^{*}})\cap\operatorname{R}_{2}(L_{B^{*}})
. Let
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
be such that
0\leqslant\operatorname{Re}z<1/2
. By Lemma
7
we have
\operatorname{D}_{2}(L^{z}_{A})=\operatorname{D}_{2}(L^{z}_{B})
, which together with (
25
) gives

\left\langle L^{z}_{B}L^{-z}_{A}f,g\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=\left\langle L^{-z}_{A}f,L^{\bar{z}}_{B^{*}}g\right\rangle_{L^{2}}.

By
[
24
, Proposition 3.2.1 (f)]
(applied with
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{1}=1/2
) the maps
z\mapsto L^{-z}_{A}f
and
z\mapsto\overline{L^{\bar{z}}_{B^{*}}g}
are analytic in the strip
-1/2<\operatorname{Re}z<1/2
.

We now observe that, by assumption,
L^{2}(\Omega)=\overline{\operatorname{R}}_{2}(L_{B^{*}})
. Hence
\operatorname{D}_{2}(L_{B^{*}})\cap\operatorname{R}_{2}(L_{B^{*}})
is dense in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. In order to see this, consider
g_{n}\in\operatorname{D}(L_{B^{*}})
such that
L_{B^{*}}g_{n}\rightarrow f
and set
f_{n}=n\int^{1/n}_{0}T^{B^{*}}_{s}L_{B^{*}}g_{n}\,\text{\rm d}s
. It follows form (
22
) and the previous considerations that for every
f\in\operatorname{D}_{2}(L_{A})\cap\operatorname{R}_{2}(L_{A})
the map
z\mapsto S_{z}f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is continuous in
\overline{\Sigma}_{\alpha_{0}}
and holomorphic in the interior of the strip. By the uniform boundedness of the family
\{S_{z}:z\in\overline{\Sigma}_{\alpha_{0}}\}
(see (
21
)) and the density of
\operatorname{D}_{2}(L_{A})\cap\operatorname{R}_{2}(L_{A})
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, we deduce that the same is true for every
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.
∎

5.
Two-parameter maximal operator

Suppose that
A_{1},A_{2}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\Omega)
. Define the associated two-parameter maximal operator by the rule

{\text{\got M}}^{A_{1},A_{2}}f=\sup_{w,t>0}\left|{T^{A_{1}}_{w}T^{A_{2}}_{t}f}\right|.

Theorem 13
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and
A_{1},A_{2}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\Omega)
. Then
{\text{\got M}}^{A_{1},A_{2}}
is bounded on
L^{p}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

In the special case when
A_{1}
is real-valued, the associated semigroup is nonnegative and, therefore, we have

(29)

\left|{T^{A_{1}}_{w}T^{A_{2}}_{t}f}\right|\leqslant T^{A_{1}}_{w}{\mathscr{M}}^{A_{2}}f\leqslant{\mathscr{M}}^{A_{1}}{\mathscr{M}}^{A_{2}}f.

It follows from Theorem
1
that
{\text{\got M}}^{A_{1},A_{2}}
is bounded on
L^{p}(\Omega)
.

When
A_{1}
is not real-valued, the domination in (
29
) does not necessarily hold and we need another argument for proving the boundedness of
{\text{\got M}}^{A_{1},A_{2}}
. It is a variant of the proof of Theorem
1
.

Fix
f\in L^{p}(\Omega)
and write

T^{A_{1}}_{w}T^{A_{2}}_{t}f=T^{A_{1}}_{t+w}f+T^{A_{1}}_{w}(T^{A_{2}}_{t}f-T^{A_{1}}_{t}f).

We clearly have

{\text{\got M}}^{A_{1},A_{2}}f\leqslant{\mathscr{M}}^{A_{1}}f+\sup_{w,t>0}\left|{T^{A_{1}}_{w}(T^{A_{2}}_{t}f-T^{A_{1}}_{t}f)}\right|

By Theorem
1
the maximal operator
{\mathscr{M}}^{A_{1}}
is bounded in
L^{p}(\Omega)
. As for the second term in the right hand side, we use Duhamel’s (
10
) formula and write

T^{A_{1}}_{w}(T^{A_{2}}_{t}f-T^{A_{1}}_{t}f)=\int^{t}_{0}T^{A_{1}}_{w+s}(L_{A_{1}}-L_{A_{2}})T^{A_{2}}_{t-s}f\,\text{\rm d}s=I_{w,t}(f)-J_{w,t}(f),

where

I_{t,w}(f)=\int^{t}_{0}L_{A_{1}}T^{A_{1}}_{w+s}T^{A_{2}}_{t-s}f\,\text{\rm d}s;\quad J_{t,w}(f)=\int^{t}_{0}T^{A_{1}}_{w+s}L_{A_{2}}T^{A_{2}}_{t-s}f\,\text{\rm d}s.

We now proceed as we did in Section
3.1.1
. We write below the details only for the first integral.

Let
\alpha=\alpha(p)\in(0,1/2)
be as in Proposition
8
. Then,

I_{t,w}(f)=\int^{t}_{0}\psi_{1-\alpha}((w+s)L_{A_{1}})U^{A_{1},A_{2}}_{\alpha}\psi_{\alpha}((t-s)L_{A_{2}})f\,\text{\rm d}\mu^{w,t}_{\alpha}(s),

where

\,\text{\rm d}\mu^{w,t}_{\alpha}(s)=(w+s)^{\alpha-1}(t-s)^{-\alpha}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,t]}(s)\,\text{\rm d}s.

By (
14
) we have

\mu^{w,t}_{\alpha}([0,t])\leqslant\mu^{t}_{\alpha}([0,t])=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\alpha\pi)},\quad\forall w,t>0.

Therefore,

\sup_{w,t>0}\left|{I_{t,w}(f)}\right|\leqslant\frac{\pi}{\sin(\alpha\pi)}\sup_{s,t>0}\left|{\psi_{1-\alpha}(sL_{A_{1}})U^{A_{1},A_{2}}_{\alpha}\psi_{\alpha}(tL_{A_{2}})f}\right|.

By Cowling’s subordination, the maximal operator on the right-hand side is dominated by the sublinear operator

\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|{\Gamma(1-\alpha-iu)}\right|\left|{\Gamma(\alpha-iv)}\right|\left|{L^{iu}_{A_{1}}U^{A_{1},A_{2}}_{\alpha}L^{iv}_{A_{2}}f}\right|\,\text{\rm d}u\,\text{\rm d}v.

It follows from Stirling’s formula (
19
) in combination with Propositions
8
and
2
that the sub-linear operator above is bounded in
L^{p}(\Omega)
.
∎

Appendix A
Proof of Kato’s theorem (Lemma
7
) via square functions

In this appendix we give a proof of Lemma
7
which somewhat differs from the original one by Kato
[
26
]
.
We find it likely that this proof may already be known, yet we where not able to find references that would confirm this.

Let
H
be a complex Hilbert space. Let
{\text{\got a}}_{1},{\text{\got a}}_{2}
be two densely defined, closed and sectorial (thus continuous) sesquilinear forms on
H

[
27
,
32
,
24
]
. Denote by
{\mathscr{L}}_{{\text{\gotm a}}_{1}}
, and
{\mathscr{L}}_{{\text{\gotm a}}_{2}}
, respectively, the two associated Kato-sectorial operators on
H
. Suppose further that

\operatorname{D}({\text{\got a}}_{1})=\operatorname{D}({\text{\got a}}_{2})={\mathcal{V}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\operatorname{Re}{\text{\got a}}_{1}(h)\sim\operatorname{Re}{\text{\got a}}_{2}(h),

uniformly in
h\in{\mathcal{V}}
. This is equivalent to
{\left\|{h}\right\|_{{\text{\gotm a}}_{1}}}\sim{\left\|{h}\right\|_{{\text{\gotm a}}_{2}}}
uniformly in
h\in{\mathcal{V}}
, where

\|h\|^{2}_{{\text{\gotm a}}_{j}}=\|h\|^{2}_{H}+\operatorname{Re}{\text{\got a}}_{j}(h),\quad h\in H,\ j=1,2.

Moreover, we assume that
{\mathscr{L}}_{{\text{\gotm a}}_{1}}
, and thus also
{\mathscr{L}}_{{\text{\gotm a}}_{2}}
, are injective.

Kato’s theorem on fractional powers
[
26
]
, which is a part of Lemma
7
, can be rephrased by saying that, for every
\beta\in[0,1/2)
we have

(30)

{\mathcal{V}}_{\beta}:=\operatorname{D}({\mathscr{L}}^{\beta}_{{\text{\gotm a}}_{1}})=\operatorname{D}({\mathscr{L}}^{\beta}_{{\text{\gotm a}}_{2}})\quad\textrm{and}\quad{\left\|{{\mathscr{L}}^{\beta}_{{\text{\gotm a}}_{1}}h}\right\|}\sim{\left\|{{\mathscr{L}}^{\beta}_{{\text{\gotm a}}_{2}}h}\right\|},\quad\forall h\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\beta}.

Once we have this result, Lions’s improvement,
{\mathcal{V}}_{\beta}=\left[H,{\mathcal{V}}\right]_{2\beta}
for all
\beta\in(0,1/2)

We now prove (

Proof.

Define

,

\displaystyle\left|{\left\langle x,{\mathscr{L}}^{\beta}_{{\text{\gotm a}}^{*}_{2}}y\right\rangle}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{\int^{\infty}_{0}-\frac{\,\text{\rm d}}{\,\text{\rm d}t}\left\langle T^{{\text{\gotm a}}_{1}}_{t}x,T^{{\text{\gotm a}}^{*}_{2}}_{t}{\mathscr{L}}^{\beta}_{{\text{\gotm a}}^{*}_{2}}y\right\rangle\,\text{\rm d}t}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\int^{\infty}_{0}\left|{\left\langle{\mathscr{L}}_{{\text{\gotm a}}_{1}}T^{{\text{\gotm a}}_{1}}_{t}x,T^{{\text{\gotm a}}^{*}_{2}}_{t}{\mathscr{L}}^{\beta}_{{\text{\gotm a}}^{*}_{2}}y\right\rangle}\right|\,\text{\rm d}t+\int^{\infty}_{0}\left|{\left\langle T^{{\text{\gotm a}}_{1}}_{t}x,{\mathscr{L}}^{\beta+1}_{{\text{\gotm a}}^{*}_{2}}T^{{\text{\gotm a}}^{*}_{2}}_{t}y\right\rangle}\right|\,\text{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=I+J.

It follows from Lemma

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{0}\left|{\left\langle\psi_{1-\beta}(t{\mathscr{L}}_{{\text{\gotm a}}_{1}}){\mathscr{L}}^{\beta}_{{\text{\gotm a}}_{1}}x,\psi_{\beta}(t{\mathscr{L}}_{{\text{\gotm a}}^{*}_{2}})y\right\rangle}\right|\frac{\,\text{\rm d}t}{t}
