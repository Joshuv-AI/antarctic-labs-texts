1.
Bohr neighborhoods in iterated difference sets

Let
\mathcal{S}^{1}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}
be the group of complex numbers with modulus
1
, with the usual topology and the operation of multiplication.

Let
G
be a topological abelian group. A
character
of
G
is a continuous homomorphism
\chi:G\to\mathcal{S}^{1}
. Of course, when
G
is discrete, every such homomorphism is continuous. A
trigonometric polynomial
is linear combination of characters, i.e. a function of the form
\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}\chi_{j}
, where
\chi_{j}\in\widehat{G}
. A function
\psi
on
G
is
uniformly almost periodic
if it is a uniform limit of trigonometric polynomials.

The set
\widehat{G}
of characters of
G
form an abelian group under pointwise multiplication. When
G
is discrete and
\widehat{G}
is given the topology of pointwise convergence,
\widehat{G}
is a compact abelian group. When
G
is compact and
\widehat{G}
is given the topology of uniform convergence,
\widehat{G}
is discrete.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{d}\in\widehat{G}
. The
Bohr neighborhood of
0
in
G
having rank
d
and radius
\varepsilon
determined by
\{\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{d}\}
is

\operatorname{Bohr}(\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{d};\varepsilon):=\{g:|\chi_{i}(g)-1|<\varepsilon,1\leq i\leq d\}.

Remark 1.1
.

If
\psi
is a real valued trigonometric polynomial with
\psi(0)>0
, it is easy to verify that
\operatorname{supp}(\phi):=\{g\in G:\psi(g)\neq 0\}
contains a Bohr neighborhood of
0
. Since almost periodic functions may be uniformly approximated by trigonometric polynomials, the same holds when
\psi
is uniformly almost periodic.

A classical theorem of Bogoliouboff
[
9
]
states that if
G
is a countable abelian group and
G=C_{1}\cup C_{2}\cup\dots\cup C_{r}
is a finite cover of
G
, then for at least one
i
, the iterated difference set
(C_{i}-C_{i})-(C_{i}-C_{i}):=\{(x-y)-(z-w):x,y,z,w\in C_{i}\}
contains a Bohr neighborhood of
0
with radius and rank depending only on
r
. Note that Bogoliouboff’s theorem implies that if finitely many translates of
C
cover
G
, then
(C-C)-(C-C)
contains a Bohr neighborhoof of
0
. Følner
[
11
]
generalizes this to say that
(A-A)-(A-A)
contains a Bohr neighborhood of
0
under the weaker hypothesis that
d^{*}(A)>0
, where
d^{*}
denotes upper Banach density (see §
3.1
for the definition). See Theorem 5.7.2 of Part II of
[
15
]
for exposition of Bogoliouboff’s and Følner’s results.

Bergelson and Ruzsa extended Følner’s theorem as follows.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
5
]
, Theorem 6.1)
.

Let
r,s,t
be nonzero integers with
r+s+t=0
, and let
A\subset\mathbb{Z}
have
d^{*}(A)>0
. Then
rA+sA+tA:=\{ra_{1}+sa_{2}+ta_{3}:a_{i}\in A\}
contains a Bohr neighborhood of
0
, with rank and radius depending only on
r,s,t
, and
d^{*}(A)
.

Our main result is Theorem
1.3
, a partial generalization of Theorem
1.2
to arbitrary countable abelian groups and more summands. We say “partial generalization” because we do not bound the rank and radius of the Bohr neighborhood.

Let
G
be a countable (discrete) abelian group. Let
q
be the exponent of
G
, meaning
q
is the least
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
mg=0
for every
g\in G
. If there is no such
m
, we say
G
has exponent
0
.

If
\vec{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
A\subset G
, we define

\vec{c}\cdot A:=\Bigl\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}a_{i}:a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\in A\text{ {are mutually distinct}}\Bigr\}\cup\{0\}

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
d\geq 3
, and let
c_{1},\dots,c_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}
be such that

(1)

c_{i}G
has finite index in
G
for each
i
,

(2)

(c_{d-1}+c_{d})G
, has finite index in
G
, and

(3)

q
divides
\sum c_{i}
.

If
A\subset G
has
d^{*}(A)>0
, then
\vec{c}\cdot A
contains a Bohr neighborhood of
0
in
G
.

Letting
p
be an odd prime,
G=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\omega}:=
the direct sum of countably many copies of
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, and
c_{1}=\dots=c_{d}=1
, we get the following corollary as a special case.

Corollary 1.4
.

If
p
is an odd prime,
p|d\in\mathbb{N}
, and
A\subset\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\omega}
has

, then

\{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{d}:a_{i}\in A\text{ are mutually distinct}\}

contains a Bohr neighborhood of
0
.

Remark 1.5
.

The rank and radius of

in the conclusion of Theorem
1.3
can be bounded by functions of the indices of

in

and

, but we will not prove such bounds in this article.

While Theorem
1.2
does not insist that the sums be formed with mutually distinct

make it convenient to do so.

The hypothesis (

such that

has finite index in

,” since the

can be reordered without changing the value of

.

Remark 1.6
.

We are currently unable to decide whether the hypothesis (

. The other hypotheses cannot be omitted, as the following three examples demonstrate.

(i)

The hypothesis that

(ii)

The next lemma deals with a technicality that arises from our approach.

Lemma 1.7
.

If
B
is a Bohr neighborhood of
0
in an abelian group
G
and
c\in\mathbb{Z}
is such that
cG
has finite index in
G
, then
cB
is also a Bohr neighborhood of
0
in
G
.

Proof.

This is somewhat cumbersome to verify directly, but easy to see as a consequence of Bogoliouboff’s theorem. If
B
is a Bohr neighborhood of
0
in
G
, then
B
contains a set of the form
(A-A)-(A-A)
, where
A
is a Bohr neighborhood of
0
in
G
having radius smaller than the radius of
B
. In particular, finitely many translates of
A
cover
G
. Since
cG
has finite index in
G
, we have that finitely many translates of
cA
cover
G
. Now
cB
contains
(cA-cA)-(cA-cA)
, which must contain a Bohr neighborhood of

, by Bogoliouboff’s theorem.
∎

Notation for integrals

We adopt the usual notation for integrals:
\int f\,d\mu
is the integral of
f
with respect to
\mu
, or
\int f(x)\,d\mu(x)
, if we need to specify a variable. When
\mu
is Haar measure on a compact abelian group
K
, we may write
\int f(x)\,dx
in place of
\int f(x)\,d\mu(x)
, to shorten the appearance of iterated integrals.

We will prove Theorem
1.3
by proving that there is an almost periodic function
\phi
with
\phi(0)>0
and
c_{d}\operatorname{supp}(\phi)\subset\vec{c}\cdot A
(cf. Remark
1.1
and Lemma
1.7
). The following lemma specifies the form of
\phi
.

Lemma 1.8
.

Let
A\subset G
and let
c_{1},\dots,c_{d}
satisfy the hypothesis of Theorem
1.3
. Then there is a compact abelian group
K
with Haar probability measure
m
, an
m
-measurable
\tilde{f}:K\to[0,1]
with
\int\tilde{f}\,dm=d^{*}(A)
, and a homomorphism
\rho:G\to K
with
\overline{\rho(G)}=K
such that

\phi(t):=\int_{K^{d}}\tilde{f}(k+c_{d}s_{1})\tilde{f}(k+c_{d}s_{2})\cdots\tilde{f}(k+c_{d}s_{d-1})\tilde{f}\Bigl(k+\rho(t)-\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}c_{i}s_{i}\Bigr)\,dk\,ds_{1}\,ds_{2}\,\dots\,ds_{d-1}

satisfies
c_{d}\operatorname{supp}(\phi)\subset\vec{c}\cdot A
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Assuming Lemma
1.8
, it suffices to prove that the
\phi
defined therein is almost periodic and satisfies
\phi(0)>0
. The almost periodicity of
\phi
follows by approximating (in
L^{2}(\mu)
) the function
\tilde{f}
in the definition by a trigonometric polynomial
p:K\to[0,1]
, so that
\|\tilde{f}-p\|<\varepsilon/{2^{d}}
and observing that the resulting function of
t
is a trigonometric polynomial on
G
differing (uniformly) from
\phi
by at most
\varepsilon.

To prove that
\phi(0)>0
, note that the function on
K^{d-1}
defined by

(s_{1},\dots,s_{d-1})\mapsto\int\tilde{f}(k+s_{1})\cdots\tilde{f}(k+s_{d-1})\tilde{f}\Bigl(k-\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}c_{i}s_{i}\Bigr)\,dk

is continuous, and is equal to
\int\tilde{f}(x)^{d}\,dx
when
s_{1}=s_{2}=\cdots=s_{d}=0
. The integrand in the definition of
\phi
is therefore positive for a neighborhood of values
(s_{1},\dots,s_{d-1})
near
0
. Consequently, the integral defining
\phi
does not vanish at
t=0
. ∎

Remark 1.9
.

The argument in the second paragraph of the above proof is a key ingredient in ergodic theoretic proofs of Roth’s theorem on three-term arithmetic progressions. In fact, Roth’s theorem is a corollary of our proofs, although we do not discuss this implication here. The proof of Theorem 6.1 in
[
5
]
uses Roth’s theorem in a key step.

2.
Sumsets as supports of convolutions

In this section we state Theorem
2.2
, an ergodic theoretic version of Lemma
1.8
, and use it to prove the lemma. Theorem
2.2
will be proved in §
3.7
, after some preliminary machinery is developed.

Our first step in the proof of Lemma
1.8
is to find a kind of convolution supported on the sumset in question. For the sake of explanation, we first consider two summands.

Given
A,B\subset G
, consider their characteristic functions
1_{A}
and
1_{B}
. Note that a fixed
g
lies in
A+B
if and only if there is an
h\in G
such that
1_{A}(h)1_{B}(g-h)>0
. Often the existence of such an

is proved by averaging, i.e. proving that there is a Følner sequence
F_{n}
with
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|F_{n}|}\sum_{h\in F_{n}}1_{A}(h)1_{B}(g-h)>0
. This limit is a kind of convolution of
1_{A}
and
1_{B}
.

We want to find a convolution supported on the set
\vec{c}\cdot A
and write it in a form suited to the hypotheses of Theorem
1.3
. The next observation does so in a somewhat cumbersome way.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\vec{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
satisfy hypothesis (3) of Theorem
1.3
, let

, and write
1_{A}
for its characteristic function. Define

and
J_{0}\subset G
by

\displaystyle J(h,g_{1},\dots,g_{d-1};t):=1_{A}(h+c_{d}g_{1})1_{A}(h+c_{d}g_{2})\cdots 1_{A}(h+c_{d}g_{d-1})1_{A}\Bigl(h+t-\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}c_{i}g_{i}\Bigr)

\displaystyle J_{0}:=\{t\in G:\exists h,g_{1},\dots,g_{d-1}\in G\text{ with }J(h,g_{1},\dots,g_{d-1};t)>0,c_{i}g_{i}\text{ mutually distinct}\},

we have

(2.1)

c_{d}J_{0}\subset\vec{c}\cdot A.

Furthermore, setting

\displaystyle\tilde{J}(g_{1},\dots,g_{d-1};t):=d^{*}(\{h:J(h,g_{1},\dots,g_{t-1};t)>0\})

\displaystyle\tilde{J}_{0}:=\{t\in G:\exists g_{1},\dots,g_{d-1}\in G\text{ with }\tilde{J}(g_{1},\dots,g_{d-1};t)>0,c_{i}g_{i}\text{ mutually distinct}\},

we have
c_{d}\tilde{J}_{0}\subset\vec{c}\cdot A
.

Proof.

We first prove the containment (

). Suppose

,

, and

. Then each of

, and

lie in

, and

\displaystyle c_{1}a_{1}+\cdots+c_{d}a_{d}

\displaystyle=(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{d})h+c_{d}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}c_{i}g_{i}\Bigr)+c_{d}\Bigl(t-\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}c_{i}g_{i}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=c_{d}t,

so

.

The second assertion of the lemma follows from the observation that if

, then there are infinitely many

.
∎

Before proving Proposition
2.4
, we summarize some background on invariant means; readers can consult
[
6
]
and
[
1
]
for further details.

An
invariant mean
on
\ell^{\infty}(G)
is a positive linear functional
\eta
satisfying
\eta(1_{G})=1
; and for every
\phi\in\ell^{\infty}(G)
and
t\in G
, we have
\eta(\phi_{t})=\eta(\phi)
, where
\phi_{t}
is the translate of
\phi
by
t
(i.e.
\phi_{t}(g)=\phi(g+t)
). It is easy to prove that for every Følner sequence
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, there is an invariant mean
\eta
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{n}|}\sum_{g\in\Phi_{n}}\phi(g)=\eta(\phi)
for every bounded
\phi:G\to\mathbb{C}
for which the limit exists. This yields the following characterization of upper Banach density: for all
A\subset G
, we have

d^{*}(A)=\max\{\eta(1_{A}):\eta\text{ is an invariant mean on }\ell^{\infty}(G)\}

Since the set of invariant means is a compact convex set in the weak
∗
topology on the dual of
\ell^{\infty}(G)
, it contains extreme points. In other words, there are invariant means which cannot be written as a nontrivial convex combination of other invariant means. Furthermore, every invariant mean can be written as a convex combination of extreme invariant means, and it follows that for every
A\subset G
, there is an extreme invariant mean
m
with
m(1_{A})=d^{*}(A)
.

The key identity for ergodicity is most easily expressed using the following notation: given an invariant mean
\eta
and
\phi\in\ell^{\infty}(G)
, we write
\int\phi(g)\,d\eta(g)
for
\eta(\phi)
(even though the “integral” is only finitely additive). This allows us to write expressions such as
\int\int\psi(g)\phi(g+h)\,d\eta(g)\,d\kappa(h)
in place of the more cumbersome “
\kappa(\Phi)
, where
\Phi(h):=\eta(\psi\cdot\phi_{h})
.” The key identity is stated as Scholium 4.4 of
[
6
]
: if
m
is an extreme invariant mean and
\eta
is any invariant mean then for all
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in\ell^{\infty}(G)
, we have

(2.7)

\int\phi_{1}(g)\phi_{2}(g+h)\,dm(g)\,d\eta(h)=m(\phi_{1})m(\phi_{2}).

To prove ergodicity of

3.
Ergodic theoretic machinery

3.1.
Densities on
G

A sequence
\mathbf{F}=(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of finite subsets of
G
is a
Følner sequence
if for all
g\in G
,
\lim_{n\to\infty\frac{|F_{n}\triangle(g+F_{n})|}{|F_{n}|}}=0
. If
\mathbf{F}
is a Følner sequence and
A\subset G
the
upper density of
A
with respect to
\mathbf{F}
is
\bar{d}_{\mathbf{F}}(A):\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{|A\cap F_{n}|}{|F_{n}|}
. The
upper Banach density of
A
is
d^{*}(A):=\sup\{d_{\mathbf{F}}(A):\mathbf{F}\text{ is a F{\o}lner sequence}\}
.

3.2.
Measure preserving systems

We use the usual ergodic theoretic setup for dealing with problems involving configurations in dense subsets of abelian groups; see any of
[
13
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
23
]
,
[

]

Fix a countable abelian group

. We say that

is a

-system
(or “

-system”) if
(X,\mu)
is a probability measure space, and

by transformations

which preserve

and

for the element

on

by unitary operators

.

We say that

for
\mu
-almost every

and all

.

A subset

is

-invariant
if
\mu(B\triangle T_{g}B)=0
for every

.

The system
(X,\mu,T)
is

if the only
T
-invariant sets

have
\mu(B)=0
or
\mu(B)=1
.

We say that
(X,\mu,T)
has

if the closed subspace of
L^{2}(\mu)
consisting of
T
-invariant functions is finite dimensional.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
(X,\mu,T)
is ergodic and
H
is a finite index subgroup of
G
, then the
H
-action given by restricting
T_{g}
to

has finitely many ergodic components.

Proof.

Let
B\subset X
be
H
-invariant, and let
g_{1},\dots,g_{k}
be coset representatives of
H
. Now

X\sim_{\mu}\bigcup_{g\in G}T_{g}B\sim_{\mu}\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}T_{g_{i}}B,

so
\mu(B)\geq\frac{1}{k}
. Thus
\inf\{\mu(B):B\subset X\text{ is }H\text{-invariant}\}>0
, and the algebra
\mathcal{I}_{H}
of
H
-invariant sets is generated (up to
\mu
-measure
0
) by finitely many atoms
B_{1},\dots,B_{r}
. Now every
H
-invariant
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
is
\mathcal{I}_{H}
-measurable, so the functions
\psi_{i}:=\frac{1}{\mu(B_{i})}1_{B_{i}}
form an orthonormal basis of the space of
H
-invariant elements of
L^{2}(\mu)
.
∎

We will use the Mean Ergodic Theorem for unitary actions; see
[
20
, Theorem 4.22]
for exposition.

Theorem 3.2

(Mean ergodic theorem)
.

Let
U
be an action of
G
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
by unitary operators and
x\in\mathcal{H}
. If
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Følner sequence, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{F_{n}}\sum_{g\in F_{n}}U_{g}x=P_{I}x,

where
P_{I}x
is the orthogonal projection of
x
onto the closed subspace of
\mathcal{H}
consisting of
U
-invariant vectors.

Specializing to the unitary action associated to a
G
-system yields the following.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be a
G
-system and
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{|F_{n}|}\sum_{g\in F_{n}}T_{g}f=P_{\mathcal{I}}f,

where
P_{\mathcal{I}}
is the orthogonal projection onto the closed subspace of
L^{2}(\mu)
consisting of
T
-invariant functions.

Observation 3.4
.

If
(X,\mu,T)
has only finitely many ergodic components, then
X
can be partitioned into
\mu
-measurable
G
-invariant subsets

, and for each

, the projection

can be written as

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\mu(X_{i})}\int f\,1_{X_{i}}\,d\mu\Bigr)1_{X_{i}}.

3.3.
Eigenfunctions

We say that

is an

if there is a character

such that

for every

. Let

in

Given a function

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{|F_{n}|}\sum_{g\in F_{n}}|\psi(g)|=0

Let

(i)

(ii)

Proof.

Part (i) here follows from Corollary 1.5 of
[

]
(cf. Proposition 2.20 and Propsition D.17 of

). Part (ii) follows from Part (i) and Observation

As usual for ergodic theoretic proofs of Roth’s theorem, we need a van der Corput lemma. The version we use is

If
