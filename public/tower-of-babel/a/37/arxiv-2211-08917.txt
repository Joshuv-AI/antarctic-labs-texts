A simple formula for the x x - y y symplectic transformation in topological recursion
Alexander Hock

Abstract
. Let W g , n W_{g,n} be the correlators computed by Topological Recursion for some given spectral curve ( x , y ) (x,y) and W g , n ∨ W^{\vee}_{g,n} for ( y , x ) (y,x) , where the role of x , y x,y is inverted. These two sets of correlators W g , n W_{g,n} and W g , n ∨ W^{\vee}_{g,n} are related by the x x - y y symplectic transformation. Bychkov, Dunin-Barkowski, Kazarian and Shadrin computed a functional relation between two slightly different sets of correlators. Together with Alexandrov, they proved that their functional relation is indeed the x x - y y symplectic transformation in Topological Recursion. This article provides a fairly simple formula directly between W g , n W_{g,n} and W g , n ∨ W^{\vee}_{g,n} which holds by their theorem for meromorphic x x and y y with simple and distinct ramification points. Due to the recent connection between free probability and fully simple vs ordinary maps, we conclude a simplified moment-cumulant relation for moments and higher order free cumulants.



1.
Introduction

Invented in 2007 by Chekhov, Eynard and Orantin
[
CEO06
,
EO07a
]
, the theory of Topological Recursion (TR) reaches more and more applications in mathematical physics and pure mathematics. TR is a universal recursive procedure to compute from a given initial data
(\Sigma,x,y,B)
, called the
spectral curve
, a family of differential forms
\omega_{g,n}
labelled by two integers

. The application of TR ranges from enumerative geometry and random matrix theory over string theory to knot theory. We refer to
[

]
for a short overview.

We will stick to the following setup: Let
\omega_{g,n}
be a family of

n
products of Riemann surfaces

These
\omega_{g,n}
are labeled by the genus

and the number

of marked points of a compact complex curve. These objects occur as differential forms
on a complex curve

, understood in parametric
representation

and

. For simplicity, we will assume that the complex curve is of genus zero.

The initial data is the
spectral curve

(\Sigma,x,y,B)
consisting of ramified coverings

x,y:\Sigma\to\Sigma_{0}
of Riemann surfaces.
Let
\omega_{0,1}(z)=y(z)dx(z)
be a differential 1-form and the

. From the initial data,
TR constructs a family of meromorphic differentials
\omega_{g,n+1}(z_{1},...,z_{n},z)
with

2g+n-2\geq 0
via the following universal formula
(in which we abbreviate
I=\{z_{1},...,z_{n}\}
):´

\displaystyle\omega_{g,n+1}(I,z)

(1.1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha_{i}}\Res\displaylimits_{q\to\alpha_{i}}K_{i}(z,q)\bigg(\omega_{g-1,n+2,0}(I,q,\sigma_{i}(q))+\hskip-28.45274pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g_{1}+g_{2}=g\\
I_{1}\uplus I_{2}=I\\
(g_{1},I_{1})\neq(0,\emptyset)\neq(g_{2},I_{2})\end{subarray}}\hskip-31.2982pt\omega_{g_{1},|I_{1}|+1}(I_{1},q)\omega_{g_{2},|I_{2}|+1}(I_{2},\sigma_{i}(q))\!\bigg)\;.

This construction proceeds recursively
in minus the Euler characteristic

. Further, we need to define:

•

The sum over the

•

We also assume that

All

are symmetric differential forms with poles just located at the ramification points of

If we interchange the role of

, the differential forms are denoted by

. For instance, we have

1.1.
Symplectic Transformation

For any spectral curve
(\Sigma,x,y,B)
, the
free energy
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{g}=\frac{1}{2-2g}\sum_{\alpha_{i}}\Res\displaylimits_{q\to\alpha_{i}}\Phi_{0,1}(x(q))\omega_{g,1}(q)

(1.2)

for
g>1
, where
\Phi_{0,1}(x(q))=\int_{o}^{x(q)}\omega_{0,1}(q^{\prime})
, i.e.
d\Phi_{0,1}(x(z))=\omega_{0,1}(z)
. For
g\in\{0,1\}
, the free energies are a bit subtle (see
[
EO07a
]
).

It is conjectured that the free energies
\mathcal{F}^{g}
are invariant under any symplectic transformation of
(x,y)
, i.e. any transformation which leaves
|dx\wedge dy|
invariant. Due to this observation, the free energies
\mathcal{F}^{g}
are also called
symplectic invariants
.

We list some transformations

•

(x,y)\to\big(\frac{ax+b}{cx+d},\frac{(cx+d)^{2}}{ad-bc}y\big)
, where
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in PSL_{2}(\mathbb{C})

•

(x,y)\to\big(x,y+R(x)\big)
, where
R(x)
is any rational function

•

(x,y)\to(y,x)
.

The first and the second transformations actually also leave all
\omega_{g,n}
invariant (not just
\mathcal{F}^{g}
). To see this, take the recursion kernel
K_{i}(z,q)
of (
1.1
), the only dependence on
(x,y)
is in the denominator, which is of the form

\displaystyle\omega_{0,1}(q)-\omega_{0,1}(\sigma(q))=(y(q)-y(\sigma(q)))dx(q).

This is indeed invariant under the first two transformations, since for any rational function we have
R(x(q))=R(x(\sigma(q)))
. Note also that the first transformation conserves the ramification points.

However, the third transformation
(x,y)\to(y,x)
, which we call the
x
-
y

symplectic transformation
, changes the
\omega_{g,n}
’s completely, but
\mathcal{F}^{g}
is conjectured to be invariant. As defined before, we denote the differential forms after the third transformation
\omega_{g,n}^{\vee}
to distinguish them from
\omega_{g,n}
. Therefore, it is of considerable interest to understand the transformation of
\omega_{g,n}
under the
x
-
y
symplectic transformation.

There are essentially two main examples were the
x
-
y
symplectic transformation relates two different interesting families of differential forms related to combinatorial problems:

•

2-Matrix model
[
CEO06
,
EO07b
]
: the differential forms
\omega_{g,n}
are related to genus
g
bicoloured maps with
n
boundaries in the first colour, whereas
\omega_{g,n}^{\vee}
are related to genus
g
bicoloured maps with
n
boundaries in the second colour

•

fully simple vs ordinary maps
[
BDBKS21
,
BCGF21a
]
: the differential
\omega_{g,n}
are related to genus
g
maps with
n
ordinary boundaries, whereas
\omega_{g,n}^{\vee}
are related to genus
g
maps with
n
fully simple boundaries.

Due to the last observation that the
x
-
y
symplectic transformation relates ordinary and fully simple maps, there is a tremendous connection to the theory of free probability
[
Voi86
,
CMSS07
]
. In short, ordinary maps correspond to the generating series of higher order moments, whereas fully simple maps to the generating series of higher order free cumulants. This means that the relation between
\omega_{g,n}
and
\omega_{g,n}^{\vee}
corresponds to a moment-cumulant relation in free probability.

2.3.
Examples

To show how the formula of Theorem
2.8
works and how simple the functional relation of Theorem
2.8
is, we want to give some examples.

First, we add some information about the different orders of
\hbar
contributing from different graphs. The set of graphs
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is an infinite set, but as stated already in
[
BCGF+21b
]
just a finite number of graphs contribute for a fixed genus. This finite set is restricted by the number of loops
L
(first Betti number), which has to be smaller or equal to the genus
L\leq g
. From the weights
\hat{c}
of (
2.25
), the smallest order in
\hbar
is
\prod_{I\in\mathcal{I}(\Gamma)}\hbar^{2|I|-2}
. This happens because at leading order we have
\hbar^{|I|}
coming from
\prod_{i\in I}\hbar u_{i}S(\hbar u_{i}\partial_{x_{i}})
and
\hbar^{|I|-2}
from
W_{|I|}(x_{I})=\sum_{g}\hbar^{2g+|I|-2}W_{g,|I|}(x_{I})
for each weight
\hat{c}(u_{I},x_{I})
. Note that for a connected graph
L=1+E-V
holds, where
E
is the number of edges and
V
the number of vertices. The number of edges for a graph
\Gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
defined by Definition
2.1
is
E=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}(\Gamma)}|I|
and the number of vertices
V=n+|\mathcal{I}|
. Therefore, the number of loops is equal to
L=1+\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}(\Gamma)}|I|-n+|\mathcal{I}|=1+\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}(\Gamma)}(|I|-1)-n
. Inserting this into the leading order in
\hbar
for a given graph
\Gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
, we get

\displaystyle\prod_{I\in\mathcal{I}(\Gamma)}\hbar^{2|I|-2}=\hbar^{2\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}(\Gamma)}(|I|-1)}=\hbar^{2L-2+2n}.

The denominator of (
2.27
) divides by
\hbar^{n}
which gives in total a leading order of
\hbar^{2L-2+n}
. This proves that for each loop we are getting an additional factor of
\hbar^{2}
. Furthermore, the genus is an upper bound for the number of possible loops we have to consider for the graphs
\Gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
.

This section aims to apply the

To understand this, we first recall some facts about the combinatorics of ordinary and fully simple maps. The primary example of TR is the combinatorial problem of ordinary maps. It is carried out gently in the introductory book of TR by Eynard
[

]
. It can be represented as random

-hermitian unitary invariant matrix model with the partition function

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}:=\int_{H_{N}}dM\,e^{-N\mathrm{Tr}V(M)}

and

and
\langle\mathcal{O}(M)\rangle:=\int_{H_{N}}\mathcal{O}(M)d\mu

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{g,n}(x_{1},...,x_{n}):=

\displaystyle N^{2g+n-2}\bigg\langle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Tr}\frac{1}{x_{i}-M}\bigg\rangle_{c}=N^{2g+n-2}\sum_{k_{1},...,k_{n}\geq 0}\frac{\bigg\langle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Tr}M^{k_{i}}\bigg\rangle_{c}}{x_{1}^{k1+1}...x_{n}^{k_{n}+1}},

corresponds exactly to

considered before with a very explicit spectral curve (see
[

]
for more details).

For a cycle

of length

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{(k)}_{\gamma}(M):=\prod_{m=1}^{k}M_{c_{m},\gamma(c_{m})}.

Then, we define for pairwise disjoint cycles

the correlators

\displaystyle\hat{W}^{\vee}_{g,n}(y_{1},...,y_{n}):=

\displaystyle=N^{2g+n-2}\sum_{k_{1},...,k_{n}\geq 0}N^{k_{1}+...+k_{n}}\bigg\langle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{P}^{(k_{i})}_{\gamma_{i}}(M)\bigg\rangle_{c}y_{1}^{k_{1}-1}...y_{n}^{k_{n}-1}.

(3.3)

This correlators
