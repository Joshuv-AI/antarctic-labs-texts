A Recursive Relation for Bipartition Numbers
Yen-Chi Roger Lin, Shu-Yen Pan

Abstract
. We establish a recursive relation for the bipartition number p 2 ​ ( n ) p_{2}(n) which might be regarded as an analogue of Euler’s recursive relation for the partition number p ⁡ ( n ) p(n) . Two proofs of the main result are proved in this article. The first one is using the generating function, and the second one is using combinatoric objects (called “symbols”) created by Lusztig for studying representation theory of finite classical groups.

Primary: 05A17, 11P87; Secondary: 20C33

1.
Introduction

For a positive integer
n
, let
p(n)
(resp.
p_{2}(n)
) denote the number of partitions
(resp. bipartitions) of
n
.
By convention, we define
p(0)=p_{2}(0)=1
, and

p(x)=p_{2}(x)=0
if
x
is not a non-negative integer.
For
n\geq 1
, Euler obtained the following recursive relation for
p(n)
:

p(n)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\smallsetminus\{0\}}(-1)^{k-1}p\bigl(n-\tfrac{1}{2}k(3k-1)\bigr)

(cf.
[
And76
]
corollary 1.8).
Our result in this article is the following recursive relation for
p_{2}(n)
:

Theorem 1.1
.

For
n\geq 0
, we have

p_{2}(n)=p\bigl(\tfrac{n}{2}\bigr)+\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\smallsetminus\{0\}}(-1)^{k-1}p_{2}\bigl(n-k^{2}\bigr).

We will provide two proofs for the above recursive formula from different point of views.
In Section 2, the theorem is first proved by manipulating the respective generating functions.
In Section 3, we will derive the recursive relation from Lusztig’s theory of “symbols” and the binomial theorem.

It is well known that the irreducible characters of the Coxeter group of type
B_{n}
is parametrized by the set of
bipartitions of
n
(
cf
.
[
GP00
]
§5.5),
just like the set of irreducible characters of the Coxeter group of type
A_{n}
(i.e., the symmetric group
S_{n}
)
is parametrized by the set of partitions of
n
.
It is not surprising that Theorem
1.1
has applications to the representation theory of finite classical groups.
Two consequences are given in Remark
3.9
and Remark
3.10
.

In the appendix, we provide a new proof of a congruence relation for the bipartition number
p_{2}(n)
as a reference.

2.
Generating Functions

2.1.
Partitions and bipartitions

For a nonnegative integer
n
,
let
{\mathcal{P}}(n)
denote the set of partitions of
n
.
By convention, we define
{\mathcal{P}}(0)
to be the set of “the empty partition”,
and
{\mathcal{P}}(x)
to be the empty set if
x
is not a nonnegative integer.
Let
p(n)=|{\mathcal{P}}(n)|
where
|X|
denotes the number of elements of a finite set
X
.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}(n)
denote the set of
bi-partitions
of
n
, i.e.,

\textstyle{\mathcal{P}}_{2}(n)=\left\{\,\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{\lambda}{\mu}\mid|\lambda|+|\mu|=n\,\right\}

where
|\lambda|
means the sum of parts of
\lambda
.
Let
p_{2}(n)=|{\mathcal{P}}_{2}(n)|
.
We know that
p_{2}(0)=1
, and
p_{2}(x)=0
if
x
is not a nonnegative integer.
For a bipartition
\Sigma=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{\lambda}{\mu}
,
its
transpose
is given by
\Sigma^{\rm t}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{\mu}{\lambda}
.
A bipartition
\Sigma
is called
degenerate
if
\Sigma^{\rm t}=\Sigma
, it is called
non-degenerate
, otherwise.
If
\Sigma\in{\mathcal{P}}_{2}(n)
is degenerate, then
\Sigma=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{\lambda}{\lambda}
for some
\lambda\in{\mathcal{P}}(\tfrac{n}{2})
.
Thus, the number of degenerate bipartitions in
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}(n)
is equal to
p(\tfrac{n}{2})
.

2.2.
First proof of Theorem
1.1

Lemma 2.1
.

We have

\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(-1)^{n}z^{n^{2}}=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-z^{k})^{2}}{1-z^{2k}}.

Proof.

We start with the
Jacobi’s triple product identity
(
cf
.
[
And76
]
theorem 2.8)

(2.2)

\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}z^{n}=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}(1+zq^{k})(1+z^{-1}q^{k-1})(1-q^{k}).

In both sides of (
2.2
), we replace
z
by
z^{-1}
, and replace
q
by
z^{2}
to obtain

\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}z^{n^{2}}=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}(1+z^{2k-1})^{2}(1-z^{2k}).

Then we replace
z
by
-z
to get

\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(-1)^{n}z^{n^{2}}=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}(1-z^{2k-1})^{2}(1-z^{2k})=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}(1-z^{2k-1})(1-z^{k}).

Now for the right-hand side,
we need the
Euler’s identity
:
p_{\rm d}(n)=p_{\rm o}(n)

where
p_{\rm d}(n)
is the number of partitions into distinct parts of
n
,
and
p_{\rm o}(n)
is the number of partitions into odd parts.
In terms of generating functions, we have

\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}(1+z^{k})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{\rm d}(n)z^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{\rm o}(n)z^{n}=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1-z^{2k-1}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}(1-z^{2k-1})(1-z^{k})=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1-z^{k}}{1+z^{k}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-z^{k})^{2}}{(1+z^{k})(1-z^{k})}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-z^{k})^{2}}{1-z^{2k}},

and the lemma is proved.
∎

First Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Recall that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p(n)z^{n}=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1-z^{k}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{2}(n)z^{n}=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-z^{k})^{2}}.

By Lemma
2.1
, we have

\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{2}(n)z^{n}\right)\left(\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(-1)^{k}z^{k^{2}}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p(n)z^{2n},

which implies that

\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(-1)^{k}p_{2}(n-k^{2})=p\bigl(\tfrac{n}{2}\bigr),

i.e., the theorem is proved.
∎

3.
Lusztig’s Symbols

3.1.
Definition of the symbols

In this subsection and the next subsection, we recall the notions of “symbols” by Lusztig.
The materials are modified from
[
Lus77
]
§2, §3 (see also
[
Pan24
]
§2).

A
symbol
is an ordered pair

\Lambda=\binom{A}{B}=\binom{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m_{1}}}{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{m_{2}}}

of two finite subsets
A,B
of nonnegative integers.
The elements in each of
A
and
B
are written in decreasing order, i.e.,

a_{1}>a_{2}>\cdots>a_{m_{1}}
and
b_{1}>b_{2}>\cdots>b_{m_{2}}
.
The sets
A,B
in
\Lambda
are also denoted by
\Lambda^{*},\Lambda_{*}
, respectively,
and called the
first row
and the
second row
of
\Lambda
.
An element in
\Lambda^{*}
or
\Lambda_{*}
is called an
entry
of
\Lambda
.
A symbol
\Lambda^{\prime}
is called a
subsymbol
of
\Lambda
and denoted by
\Lambda^{\prime}\subset\Lambda

if
\Lambda^{\prime*}\subset\Lambda^{*}
and
\Lambda^{\prime}_{*}\subset\Lambda_{*}
.
For a subsymbol
\Lambda^{\prime}\subset\Lambda
, we define the substraction

\Lambda\smallsetminus\Lambda^{\prime}
to be
\binom{\Lambda^{*}\smallsetminus\Lambda^{\prime*}}{\Lambda_{*}\smallsetminus\Lambda^{\prime}_{*}}
.
For two symbols
\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}
, their

union
is defined by
\Lambda\cup\Lambda^{\prime}=\binom{\Lambda^{*}\cup\Lambda^{\prime*}}{\Lambda_{*}\cup\Lambda^{\prime}_{*}}
.

For a symbol
\Lambda=\binom{A}{B}=\binom{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m_{1}}}{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{m_{2}}}
,
its
rank

{\rm rk}(\Lambda)
,
defect

{\rm def}(\Lambda)
and
transpose

\Lambda^{\rm t}
are defined by

\displaystyle{\rm rk}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}a_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{m_{2}}b_{j}-\biggl\lfloor\biggl(\frac{|A|+|B|-1}{2}\biggr)^{2}\biggr\rfloor,

\displaystyle{\rm def}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=|A|-|B|,

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\rm t}

\displaystyle=\binom{B}{A}

where
\lfloor x\rfloor
denotes the floor function of a real number
x
.
It is not difficult to check that
{\rm rk}(\Lambda)\geq\bigl\lfloor\bigl(\frac{{\rm def}(\Lambda)}{2}\bigr)^{2}\bigr\rfloor

for any symbol
\Lambda
.
A symbol
\Lambda
is called
degenerate
if
\Lambda^{\rm t}=\Lambda
;
it is called
non-degenerate
, otherwise.

On the set of symbols , we define an equivalence relation generated by

\binom{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m_{1}}}{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{m_{2}}}\sim\binom{a_{1}+1,a_{2}+1,\ldots,a_{m_{1}}+1,0}{b_{1}+1,b_{2}+1,\ldots,b_{m_{2}}+1,0}.

It is easy to see that the ranks and the defects are invariant in a similarity class.
Let
\Phi_{n,d}
denote the set of similarity classes of symbols of rank
n
and defect
d
.
Note that
\Phi_{n,d}=\emptyset
if
\bigl\lfloor(\frac{d}{2})^{2}\bigr\rfloor>n
.

We define a mapping

\binom{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m_{1}}}{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{m_{2}}}\mapsto\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{a_1-(m_1-1),a_2-(m_1-2),\ldots,a_{m_1-1}-1,a_{m_1}}{b_1-(m_2-1),b_2-(m_2-1),\ldots,b_{m_2-1}-1,b_{m_2}}

from symbols to bipartitions.
It is easy to see that two symbols in the same similarity class has the same image.
So the above mapping will be regarded as a mapping from the set of similarity classes of symbols to the set of
bipartitions.
Moreover, it is not difficult to check that the above mapping gives a bijection

(3.1)

\Upsilon\colon\Phi_{n,d}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{P}}_{2}\bigl(n-\bigl\lfloor\bigl(\tfrac{d}{2}\bigr)^{2}\bigr\rfloor\bigr).

In particular,
|\Phi_{n,d}|=p_{2}\bigl(n-(\tfrac{d}{2})^{2}\bigr)
if
d
is even.
Note that if a symbol
\Lambda
of rank
n
is degenerate, then
{\rm def}(\Lambda)=0
and
\Upsilon(\Lambda)
is a degenerate
bipartition in
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}(n)
.
Finally, we define

(3.2)

\Phi^{+}_{n}=\bigcup_{d\equiv 0\pmod{4}}\Phi_{n,d},\qquad\Phi^{-}_{n}=\bigcup_{d\equiv 2\pmod{4}}\Phi_{n,d},\qquad\Phi_{n}=\Phi^{+}_{n}\cup\Phi^{-}_{n}=\bigcup_{d\text{ even}}\Phi_{n,d}.

For a special symbol

Z_{\rm I}=Z\smallsetminus\binom{Z^{*}\cap Z_{*}}{Z^{*}\cap Z_{*}}

.
Clearly,
{\rm def}(Z_{\rm I})=0

The

\deg(Z)

|(Z_{\rm I})^{*}|=|(Z_{\rm I})_{*}|

Second proof of Theorem
1.1

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
Z\in\Phi_{n,0}
be a special symbol,
and let
\delta=\deg(Z)
.
Then

\left|\Phi_{Z}\cap\left(\bigcup_{d\equiv 0\pmod{4}}\Phi_{n,d}\right)\right|-\left|\Phi_{Z}\cap\left(\bigcup_{d\equiv 2\pmod{4}}\Phi_{n,d}\right)\right|=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $\delta=0$};\\
0,&\text{if $\delta\geq 1$}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Note that
|(Z_{\rm I})^{*}|+|(Z_{\rm I})_{*}|=2\delta
,
and
{\rm def}(M)\equiv|M|\pmod{2}
.
Then from (
3.4
), we have

\displaystyle\left|\Phi_{Z}\cap\left(\bigcup_{d\equiv 0\pmod{4}}\Phi_{n,d}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=|\{\,M\subset Z_{\rm I}\mid|M^{*}|+|M_{*}|\text{ even}\,\}|=\sum_{k\geq 0,\text{ even}}C^{2\delta}_{k},

\displaystyle\left|\Phi_{Z}\cap\left(\bigcup_{d\equiv 2\pmod{4}}\Phi_{n,d}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=|\{\,M\subset Z_{\rm I}\mid|M^{*}|+|M_{*}|\text{ odd}\,\}|=\sum_{k\geq 1,\text{ odd}}C^{2\delta}_{k}

where
C^{l}_{m}=\frac{l!}{m!(l-m)!}
is a binomial coefficient.
Note that by convention
C^{l}_{m}=0
if
l,m\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
and
l<m
.
It is well known from the binomial theorem that

\sum_{k\geq 0,\text{ even}}C^{2\delta}_{k}-\sum_{k\geq 1,\text{ odd}}C^{2\delta}_{k}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $\delta=0$};\\
0,&\text{if $\delta\geq 1$}.\end{cases}

Then the lemma is proved.
∎

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
n
be a nonnegative integer.
Then
|\Phi_{n}^{+}|-|\Phi^{-}_{n}|=p(\tfrac{n}{2})
.

Proof.

From (
3.2
) and (
3.4
), we have

\displaystyle\Phi^{+}_{n}=\bigcup_{Z\in\Phi_{n,0},\text{ special}}\left(\Phi_{Z}\cap\bigcup_{d\equiv 0\pmod{4}}\Phi_{n,d}\right),

\displaystyle\Phi^{-}_{n}=\bigcup_{Z\in\Phi_{n,0},\text{ special}}\left(\Phi_{Z}\cap\bigcup_{d\equiv 2\pmod{4}}\Phi_{n,d}\right).

Then by Lemma
3.7
, we have

|\Phi_{n}^{+}|-|\Phi^{-}_{n}|=\sum_{Z\in\Phi_{n,0},\ \deg(Z)=0}1=p(\tfrac{n}{2})
.
∎

Second Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

From (

Appendix A
A Congruence Relation for
p_{2}(n)

The following theorem on a congruence relation for
p_{2}(n)
has been proved in
[
HL04
]
and
[
CJW06
]
.
Below we provide another proof along the approach taken by Michael Hirschhorn
[
Hir00
]
.

Theorem A.1
.

We have

p_{2}(5n+2)\equiv p_{2}(5n+3)\equiv p_{2}(5n+4)\equiv 0\pmod{5}

for any nonnegative integer
n
.

Before the proof, we first set up some notations.
Let

(a;q)=\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}(1-aq^{k}),

and define

R(q)=\frac{(q^{2};q^{5})(q^{3};q^{5})}{(q;q^{5})(q^{4};q^{5})}\qquad\text{and}\qquad c=R(q^{5}).

Then Hirschhorn obtained (see also
[
Aig07
]
p.140)

(A.2)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p(n)q^{n}=\frac{(q^{25};q^{25})^{5}}{(q^{5};q^{5})^{6}}f(q),

where

f(q)=c^{4}+c^{3}q+2c^{2}q^{2}+3cq^{3}+5q^{4}-3c^{-1}q^{5}+2c^{-2}q^{6}-c^{-3}q^{7}+c^{-4}q^{8}.

From (
A.2
) and the fact that the expansion of
c
only involves powers of
q^{5}
,
Hirschhorn obtained

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p(5n+4)q^{5n+4}=5\frac{(q^{25};q^{25})^{5}}{(q^{5};q^{5})^{6}}q^{4}

and concluded the famous congruence by Ramanujan:

p(5n+4)\equiv 0\pmod{5}
.

Proof of Theorem
A.1
.

We square both sides of (
A.2
) and reach

(A.3)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{2}(n)q^{n}=\left(\sum_{n\geqslant 0}p(n)q^{n}\right)^{2}=\frac{(q^{25};q^{25})^{10}}{(q^{5};q^{5})^{12}}f(q)^{2},

and

f(q)^{2}=c^{8}+2c^{7}q+5c^{6}q^{2}+10c^{5}q^{3}+20c^{4}q^{4}+16c^{3}q^{5}\\
+27c^{2}q^{6}+20cq^{7}+15q^{8}-20c^{-1}q^{9}+27c^{-2}q^{10}-16c^{-3}q^{11}\\
+20c^{-4}q^{12}-10c^{-5}q^{13}+5c^{-6}q^{14}-2c^{-7}q^{15}+c^{-8}q^{16}.

Using the fact again that the expansion of
c
only involves powers of
q^{5}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{2}(5n+2)q^{5n+2}

\displaystyle=5\frac{(q^{25};q^{25})^{10}}{(q^{5};q^{5})^{12}}\left(c^{6}q^{2}+4cq^{7}+4c^{-4}q^{12}\right),

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{2}(5n+3)q^{5n+3}

\displaystyle=5\frac{(q^{25};q^{25})^{10}}{(q^{5};q^{5})^{12}}\left(2c^{5}q^{3}+3q^{8}-2c^{-5}q^{13}\right),

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{2}(5n+4)q^{5n+4}

\displaystyle=5\frac{(q^{25};q^{25})^{10}}{(q^{5};q^{5})^{12}}\left(4c^{4}q^{4}-4c^{-1}q^{9}+c^{-6}q^{14}\right).

The theorem now follows from these identities immediately.
∎
