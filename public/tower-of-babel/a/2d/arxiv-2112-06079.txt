Gábor Domokos, Zsolt Lángi, Péter L. Várkonyi

Abstract
. Answering a question of Conway and Guy in a 1968 paper, Lángi in 2021 proved the existence of a monostable polyhedron with n n -fold rotational symmetry for any n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 , and arbitrarily close to a Euclidean ball. In this paper we strengthen this result by characterizing the possible symmetry groups of all mono-monostatic smooth convex bodies and convex polyhedra. Our result also answers a stronger version of the question of Conway and Guy, asked in the above paper of Lángi.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Monostatic shapes

Not only was the general study of the relationship between geometric shape and the number of static balance points initiated by Archimedes
[
12
]
, his design of ship hulls immediately highlighted the special role of
monostatic
shapes, i.e. bodies having just one stable or one unstable static balance position. In the former case monostatic objects are also referred to as
monostable
while in the latter case as
mono-unstable
. The same idea was re-visited in 1966 by Conway and Guy
[
1
]

who conjectured that there is no homogeneous tetrahedron which can stand in rest only on one of its faces when placed on a horizontal plane (i.e. homogeneous, monostable tetrahedra do not exist), but there is a homogeneous convex polyhedron with the same property (i.e. homogeneous, monostable polyhedra do exist). The full proof of the first conjecture was published by Dawson
[
3
]
who credited Conway with the idea. The second conjecture was resolved in
[
11
]
by an explicit monostable polyhedral construction with
D_{2}
-symmetry, which has been dubbed the Conway-Guy polyhedron. In the same paper Guy presented some problems regarding monostable polyhedra, stating that three of them are due to Conway. These three questions (which we list below) appear also in the problem collection of Croft, Falconer and Guy
[
2
]
as Problem B12, as well as in the collection of Klamkin
[
13
]
. Problem
1
and some other problems for monostable polyhedra appear in a 1968 collection of geometry problems of Shephard
[
16
]
, who described these objects as ‘a remarkable class of convex polyhedra’ whose properties ‘it would probably be very rewarding and interesting to make a study of’.

Problem 1
.

Can a monostable polyhedron in the Euclidean
3
-space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
have an
n
-fold axis of symmetry for
n>2
?

Before the next problem, recall that the
girth
of a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the minimum perimeter of an orthogonal projection of the body onto a plane
[
8
]
.

Problem 2
.

What is the smallest possible ratio of diameter to girth for a monostable polyhedron?

Problem 3
.

What is the set of convex bodies uniformly approximable by monostable polyhedra, and does this contain the sphere?

The second-named author of the current paper answered in
[
14
]
the first two questions by showing Theorem
1
, where
d_{H}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\mathbf{B}^{3}
denotes the Hausdorff distance of convex bodies, and the closed unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
centered at the origin
o
, respectively.

Theorem 1

(Lángi, 2021)
.

For any
n\geq 3
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\varepsilon>0
there is a homogeneous monostable polyhedron
P
such that
P
has an
n
-fold rotational symmetry and

d_{H}(P,\mathbf{B}^{3})<\varepsilon
.

We remark that among all convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the ratio of diameter to girth is minimal for the Euclidean ball (see, e.g.
[
14
]
), hence Theorem
1
implies that the answer to Problem
2
is
\frac{1}{\pi}
.

1.2.
Mono-monostatic shapes

While the class of monostatic polyhedra already appears to have intriguing geometric properties, the study of an even more elusive class of convex bodies was initiated by Arnold
[
4
]
who conjectured that smooth, homogeneous, convex bodies with just two static balance points (one stable, one unstable) may exist. His conjecture was proven in
[
17
]
where this class of bodies was dubbed
mono-monostatic
and an explicit construction with
D_{2}
-symmetry was presented. On the other hand, the general symmetry properties of smooth mono-monostatic shapes have not been explored.
Little is known about the polyhedral case either. In
[
14
]
, the author asked the following question, as a stronger version of Problem
1
.

Question 1

(Lángi, 2021)
.

What are the positive integers
n\geq 2
such that there is a mono-monostatic convex polyhedron with an
n
-fold axis of symmetry?

In this paper we answer a more general question which goes beyond Question
1
and also encompasses the smooth case: our aim is to characterize all
the possible symmetry groups of mono-monostatic bodies, both smooth and polyhedral.

1.3.
Basic concepts

Before formally stating our main result, we recall some basic concepts from the theory of equilibrium points.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a smooth convex body, i.e. having
C^{1}
-class boundary, and let the center of mass of
K
be
p
. Let
\delta_{K}:\bd(K)\to\mathbb{R}
be the Euclidean distance function measured from the point
p
, where
\bd(K)
denotes the boundary of
K
. The critical points of
\delta_{K}
are called
equilibrium points
of
K
. To avoid degeneracy, it is usually assumed that
\delta_{K}
is a Morse function; i.e. it has finitely many critical points,
\bd(K)
is twice continuously differentiable at least in a neighborhood of each critical point, and at each such point the Hessian of
\delta_{K}
is nondegenerate. Depending on the number of negative eigenvalues of the Hessian, we distinguish between
stable
,
unstable
and
saddle-type
equilibrium points, corresponding to the local minima, maxima and saddle points of
\delta_{K}
, respectively. The Poincaré-Hopf Theorem implies that under these conditions, the numbers
S
,
U
and
H
of the stable, unstable and saddle points of
K
, respectively, satisfy the equation
S-H+U=2
.
For any
S,U\geq 1
we define the set
(S,U)_{c}
as the family of smooth convex bodies
K
having
S
stable and
U
unstable equilibrium points, where
K
has no degenerate equilibrium point, and at each such point
\bd(K)
has a positive Gaussian curvature.

We define the class
(S,U)_{p}
analogously for convex polyhedra. More specifically, let
P
be a convex polyhedron in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
p
as its center of mass. A point
q\in\bd(P)
is called an equilibrium point of
P
if the plane through
q
with normal vector
q-p
supports
P
. Note that in this case there is a unique vertex, edge or face of
P
that contains
q
in its relative interior, where by the relative interior of a vertex we mean the vertex itself. Let
F
denote this vertex, edge or face, and let
H
be the supporting plane of
P
through
q
that is perpendicular to
q-p
. Observe that
F\subset P\cap H
. We say that
q
is
nondegenerate
if
F=P\cap H
, and
P
is nondegenerate if all its equilibrium points are nondegenerate. A nondegenerate equilibrium point
q
is called a
stable, saddle-type
or
unstable
point of
P
if the dimension of
F
is
2,1
or
0
, respectively
[
6
]
. Again, we may apply the Poincaré-Hopf Theorem and obtain that if
P
is nondegenerate, then the numbers
S
,
U
and
H
of the stable, unstable and saddle points of
K
, respectively, satisfy the equation
S-H+U=2
. Thus, for any
S,U\geq 1
, we define the class
(S,U)_{p}
as the family of nondegenerate convex polyhedra with
S
stable and
U
unstable points (see also
[
6
]
or
[
14
]
). We remark that by
[
17
]
and
[
14
]
, the classes
(1,1)_{c}
and
(1,1)_{p}
are not empty.

Finally, we call a convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

centered
if its center of mass is the origin
o
.

1.4.
Main result

Using the concepts of Subsection
1.3
, we formulate our main result about the possible symmetry groups of mono-monostatic bodies as

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(1,1)_{c}
or
\mathcal{F}=(1,1)_{p}
. Then

(i)

the symmetry group of any element of
\mathcal{F}
is a discrete
2
-dimensional point group and

(ii)

for any discrete
2
-dimensional point group
G
and any
\varepsilon>0
, there is an element
K\in\mathcal{F}
whose symmetry group is
G
and which satisfies
d_{H}(K,\mathbf{B}^{3})<\varepsilon
.

Remark 1
.

We remark that any discrete
2
-dimensional point group is either a dihedral group
D_{n}
for some
n\geq 1
, or a rotation group
C_{n}
for some
n\geq 1
; here the latter group is isomorphic to the
n
-element cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
(see e.g.
[
10
]
).

The proof of Theorem
2
has two parts. In the first part, contained in Section
2
, we show that for any
\varepsilon>0
and
n\geq 2
, the family
(1,1)_{c}
contains an element
K
with symmetry group
D_{n}
and satisfying
d_{H}(K,\mathbf{B}^{3})<\varepsilon
. In the second part we show how the existence of such a body implies Theorem
2
.

2.
The construction of a smooth mono-monostatic convex body with
D_{n}
symmetry

In this section we fix
n\geq 2
. Our construction roughly follows the construction in
[
17
]
with suitable modifications to obtain a mono-monostatic convex body with
D_{n}
as its symmetry group, and belonging to class
\mathcal{C}^{2}_{+}
, i.e. having
C^{2}
-class boundary and positive Gaussian curvature at every boundary point (cf. e.g.
[
15
]
).

To achieve our goal, we define a two-parameter family
\mathcal{F}_{n}
of star-shaped bodies
K_{n}(c,d)
such that for any choice of the parameters

(A)

the symmetry group of
K_{n}(c,d)
is
D_{n}
;

(B)

K_{n}(c,d)
has
C^{2}
-class boundary;

(C)

K_{n}(c,d)
has exactly one stable and one unstable point with respect to
o
.

In the next part of the construction, we show that for any
\varepsilon>0
, there is a suitable choice of the parameters
c,d
such that

(D)

K_{n}(c,d)
has positive Gaussian curvature at every boundary point, implying also that it is convex;

(E)

the center of mass of
K_{n}(c,d)
is
o
;

(F)

d_{H}(K_{n}(c,d),\mathbf{B}^{3})<\varepsilon
.

We note that a convex body satisfying the properties (A)-(F) also satisfies the conditions in (ii) of Theorem
2
in the case
\mathcal{F}=(1,1)_{c}
and
G=D_{n}
.

We describe the construction of the family
\mathcal{F}_{n}
in Subsection
2.1
. Then we prove that the properties (A)-(C) are satisfied for all members of this family in Subsection
2.2
. In Subsection
2.3
we show that a suitable member of this family also satisfies the properties (D)-(F).

2.1.
Construction of
\mathcal{F}_{n}

Let us define the function
F_{c}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
,

(1)

F_{c}(x)=\frac{cx^{2}+(1-c)(1-x)^{2}\cdot\frac{cx}{c+x}}{cx+(1-c)(1-x)^{2}},

where
0<c\leq 1
(see Fig.
1
).

In the next lemma, we collect some properties of
F_{c}
.

Lemma 1
.

For any
c\in(0,1)
,

(a)

F_{c}
is smooth.

(b)

F_{c}
is strictly increasing on its domain.

(c)

F_{c}(0)=0
and
F_{c}(1)=1
.

(d)

(F_{c})^{\prime}_{+}(0)=(F_{c})^{\prime}_{-}(1)=1
.

Furthermore,

(e)

F_{1}(x)=x
for all
x\in[0,1]
.

(f)

\lim_{c\to 0+0}F_{c}(x)=0
for all
x\in[0,1)
.

Proof.

To show (a), it is sufficient to show that the denominator of the fraction in (
1
) is not zero.
But this fact follows from the observation that the denominator is clearly nonnegative for any
x,c\in[0,1]
, and it is zero if and only if
xc=0
and
(1-x)(1-c)=0
are satisfied simultaneously. The solution for this system of equations is
x=0
and
c=1
, or
x=1
and
c=0
, neither of which satisfies the conditions in the lemma.

To prove (b), we show that
F^{\prime}_{c}(x)>0
for any
x\in(0,1)
and
c\in(0,1)
, and remark that for
c=1
(b) follows from (e) in a straightforward way.
Note that
F_{c}(x)
can be recast as a weighted average of two functions

F_{c}(x)=w(x)F_{1}(x)+(1-w(x))F_{2}(x)

with
F_{1}(x)=x
,
F_{2}=(c^{-1}+x^{-1})^{-1}
, and

w(x)=\left(1+\frac{(1-c)(1-x^{2})}{cx}\right)^{-1}.

Then, we have

F_{c}^{\prime}(x)=w(x)F_{1}^{\prime}(x)+(1-w(x))F_{2}^{\prime}(x)+w^{\prime}(x)(F_{1}(x)-F_{2}(x))

It is straightforward to show that the terms
w(x)
,
1-w(x)
,
F_{1}^{\prime}(x)
,
F_{2}^{\prime}(x)
,
F_{1}(x)-F_{2}(x)
are positive, which proves (b).

The statements in (c), (d), (e) and (f) can be obtained by substituting into
F_{c}
and its derivative, and in case of (f), also by using the continuity of elementary functions.
∎

We remark that by Lemma
1
, the range of
F_{c}
is
[0,1]
for all
c\in(0,1)
.

In the next step, we define
f_{c}:\left[-\frac{\pi}{2},+\frac{\pi}{2}\right]\to\left[-\frac{\pi}{2},+\frac{\pi}{2}\right]
by

(2)

f_{c}(\theta)=\pi F_{c}\left(\frac{\theta}{\pi}+\frac{1}{2}\right)-\frac{\pi}{2}.

Note that for any
0<c<1
,
f_{c}
is a linear image of
F_{c}
.

Now, set
g_{c}(\theta)=-f_{c}(-\theta)
. Clearly, the domain and the range of
g_{c}
is
\left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]
. In Remark
2
we summarize the elementary properties of
f_{c}
and
g_{c}
.

Remark 2
.

For any
0<c\leq 1
, we have

f_{c}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=g_{c}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=\pi/2
,

f_{c}\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=g_{c}\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=-\pi/2
,

(f_{c})^{\prime}_{\mp}\left(\pm\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=(g_{c})^{\prime}_{\mp}\left(\pm\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=1
,
(f_{c})^{\prime\prime}_{--}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=-\frac{2(1-c)}{\pi c(1+c)},(f_{c})^{\prime\prime}_{++}\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=-\frac{2}{\pi c}
,
(g_{c})^{\prime\prime}_{--}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=\frac{2}{\pi c},(g_{c})^{\prime\prime}_{++}\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=\frac{2(1-c)}{\pi c(1+c)}
.

For any
0<c\leq 1
,
0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi
and
\varphi\in\mathbb{R}
, we set

(3)

a_{c}(\theta,\varphi)=\frac{\cos^{2}\frac{n\varphi}{2}\cos^{2}f_{c}(\theta)}{\cos^{2}\frac{n\varphi}{2}\cos^{2}f_{c}(\theta)+\sin^{2}\frac{n\varphi}{2}\cos^{2}g_{c}(\theta)},

as illustrated by Fig.
2
. Note that if
\cos\frac{n\varphi}{2}\neq 0
(i.e. if
\varphi
is not an integer multiple of
\frac{2\pi}{n}
), then

a_{c}(\theta,\varphi)=\frac{1}{1+\tan^{2}\frac{n\varphi}{2}\frac{\cos^{2}f_{c}(\theta)}{\cos^{2}g_{c}(\theta)}}
.

Using polar coordinates on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, we define the function
\rho_{c}:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
by

(4)

\rho_{c}(u)=a_{c}(\theta,\varphi)\sin f_{c}(\theta)+(1-a_{c}(\theta,\varphi))\sin g_{c}(\theta),

for any

(see Fig.

(5)

R_{c,d}(u)=1+d\rho_{c}(u).

Observe that by Remark

Finally, we define the star-shaped set
K_{n}(c,d)

\rho_{c}(u)

f_{c}(\theta)

, implies that
-1\leq\rho_{c}(u)\leq 1

1-d\leq R_{c,d}(u)\leq 1+d

K_{n}(c,d)

.

2.2.
Proof of properties (A)-(C)

In this subsection we prove that the properties described in (A)-(C) are satisfied for
K_{n}(c,d)
for all
0<d<1
and
0<c\leq 1
.

First, observe that by its definition, the symmetry group of
K_{n}(c,d)
is the group
D_{n}
, and thus, (A) is satisfied.
To show (B), first we prove Lemma
2
, which plays a crucial role in the proof of (B).

Lemma 2
.

Let
\theta_{0}\in\left\{\frac{\pi}{2}-0,-\frac{\pi}{2}+0\right\}
. Then, as
\theta\to\theta_{0}
, we have

\frac{(\rho_{c})^{\prime}_{\theta}}{\cos\theta}\to 1,\hbox{ and }\frac{(\rho_{c})^{\prime}_{\varphi}}{\cos^{2}\theta},\frac{(\rho_{c})^{\prime\prime}_{\theta\varphi}}{\cos\theta},\frac{(\rho_{c})^{\prime}_{\varphi\varphi}}{\cos^{2}\theta}\to 0.

Furthermore,

\lim_{\theta\to\frac{\pi}{2}-0}(\rho_{c})^{\prime\prime}_{\theta\theta}=-\lim_{\theta\to-\frac{\pi}{2}+0}(\rho_{c})^{\prime\prime}_{\theta\theta}=-1.

Proof.

We prove Lemma
2
only for
\theta_{0}=\frac{\pi}{2}-0
, since if
\theta_{0}=-\frac{\pi}{2}+0
, then a similar consideration can be applied.

First, observe that by definition,
\rho_{c}(\theta,\varphi)=a_{c}(\theta,\varphi)(\sin f_{c}-\sin g_{c})+\sin g_{c}
.
Furthermore,
g_{c}
is independent of
\varphi
, and by Remark
2
an elementary computation shows that if
\theta\to\theta_{0}\left(=\frac{\pi}{2}-0\right)
, then
\frac{(\sin g_{c})^{\prime}}{\cos\theta}\to 1
and
(\sin g_{c})^{\prime\prime}\to-1
. Thus, letting
\Psi(\theta,\varphi)=\rho_{c}(\theta,\varphi)-\sin g_{c}=a_{c}(\theta,\varphi)(\sin f_{c}-\sin g_{c})
, it is sufficient to show that as
\theta\to\theta_{0}
, we have

\frac{\Psi^{\prime}_{\theta}}{\cos\theta},\frac{\Psi^{\prime}_{\varphi}}{\cos^{2}\theta},\frac{\Psi^{\prime\prime}_{\theta\varphi}}{\cos\theta},\frac{\Psi^{\prime}_{\varphi\varphi}}{\cos^{2}\theta},\Psi^{\prime\prime}_{\theta\theta}\to 0.

Since the computations to prove these limits are fairly tedious and follow similar ideas, we present the proof of only the first one.

Applying the elementary differentiation rules, we obtain

(a_{c})^{\prime}_{\theta}(\theta,\varphi)=\frac{2\cos^{2}\frac{n\varphi}{2}\sin^{2}\frac{n\varphi}{2}\left(\tan g_{c}\cdot(g_{c})^{\prime}-\tan f_{c}\cdot(f_{c})^{\prime}\right)}{\left(\cos^{2}\frac{n\varphi}{2}\cdot\frac{\cos f_{c}}{\cos g_{c}}+\sin^{2}\frac{n\varphi}{2}\cdot\frac{\cos g_{c}}{\cos f_{c}}\right)^{2}}.

By L’Hospital’s rule, we have
\lim_{\theta\to\theta_{0}}\frac{\cos f_{c}}{\cos g_{c}}=\lim_{\theta\to\theta_{0}}\frac{\cos f_{c}}{\cos\frac{f_{c}+g_{c}}{2}}=1
.
Thus, the denominator of
(a_{c})^{\prime}_{\theta}
tends to
1
as
\theta\to\theta_{0}
. On the other hand, since
\sin f_{c}-\sin g_{c}=2\sin\frac{f_{c}-g_{c}}{2}\cos\frac{f_{c}+g_{c}}{2}
and
\frac{\sin\frac{f_{c}-g_{c}}{2}}{\cos\theta}\to 0
as
\theta\to\theta_{0}
, the fact that

\left(\tan g_{c}\cdot(g_{c})^{\prime}-\tan f_{c}\cdot(f_{c})^{\prime}\right)(\sin f_{c}-\sin g_{c})=

2\sin\frac{f_{c}-g_{c}}{2}\left(\sin g_{c}\cdot\frac{\cos\frac{f_{c}+g_{c}}{2}}{\cos g_{c}}\cdot g_{c}^{\prime}-\sin f_{c}\cdot\frac{\cos\frac{f_{c}+g_{c}}{2}}{\cos f_{c}}\cdot f_{c}^{\prime}\right)

yields that
\frac{(a_{c})^{\prime}_{\theta}(\sin f_{c}-\sin g_{c})}{\cos\theta}\to 0
. Thus, the desired limit follows from
\Psi^{\prime}_{\theta}=(a_{c})^{\prime}_{\theta}(\sin f_{c}-\sin g_{c})+a_{c}(\cos f_{c}\cdot(f_{c})^{\prime}-\cos g_{c}\cdot(g_{c})^{\prime})
.
∎

Now we can prove property (B) via the following statement:

Lemma 3
.

For any
0<c<1
and
0<d<1
, the function
R_{c,d}:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is
C^{2}
-class differentiable.

Proof.

Clearly, by (
5
), it is sufficient to prove that the function
\rho_{c}:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is
C^{2}
-class differentiable.
Furthermore, since the function
\rho_{c}
is defined by smooth functions of the polar coordinates
\theta
and
\varphi
in (
4
), we need to prove its differentiability properties at the two poles
u_{N}=(0,0,1),u_{S}=(0,0,-1)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, associated to the parameter values
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
and
\theta=-\frac{\pi}{2}
of the polar coordinate system, respectively. We do it only for the North Pole
u_{N}
, as the proof in the other case is analogous.

Let
U
be a neighborhood of
u_{N}
contained in the open hemisphere defined by the inequality
\theta>0
(i.e. the northern hemisphere), and let
\pi_{xy}:U\to H_{xy}
denote the orthogonal projection onto the
(x,y)
-plane
H_{xy}
. Then
\pi_{xy}
is a bijection between
U
and
V=\pi_{xy}(U)
, and for any
u\in U
with
u=(\cos\theta\cos\varphi,\cos\theta\sin\varphi,\sin\theta)
, the image of
u
is

\pi_{xy}(u)=(\cos\theta\cos\varphi,\cos\theta\sin\varphi,0).

For simplicity, we identify the
(x,y)
-plane
H_{xy}
by
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and ignore the
z
-coordinate of the above point.
Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be defined by
h(\theta,\varphi)=(\cos\theta\cos\varphi,\cos\theta\sin\varphi)
, and note that the Jacobian of
h
is

J_{h}(\theta,\varphi)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}-\sin\theta\cos\varphi&-\sin\theta\sin\varphi\\
-\cos\theta\sin\varphi&\cos\theta\cos\varphi\end{array}\right],
