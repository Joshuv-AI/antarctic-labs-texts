Space-grid approximations of hybrid stochastic differential equations and first passage properties
Hansjoerg Albrecher, Oscar Peralta

Abstract
. Hybrid stochastic differential equations are a useful tool to model continuously varying stochastic systems which are modulated by a random environment that may depend on the system state itself. In this paper, we establish the pathwise convergence of the solutions to hybrid stochastic differential equations via space-grid discretizations. While time-grid discretizations are a classical approach for simulation purposes, our space-grid discretization provides a link with multi-regime Markov modulated Brownian motions, leading to computational tractability. We exploit our convergence result to obtain efficient approximations to first passage probabilities and expected occupation times of the solutions hybrid stochastic differential equations, results which are the first of their kind for such a robust framework. We finally illustrate the performanced of the resulting approximations in numerical examples.



1.
Introduction

Stochastic systems that enjoy some sort of modulation have attracted considerable attention in probability, both from applied and theoretical perspectives. Examples of these are Markov-modulated Poisson processes
[
14
]
, Markov additive processes
[
5
, Chapter XI]
and regime-switching stochastic differential equations
[
28
, Section II.2]
, which have found applications in risk theory
[
6
, Chapter VII]
, queueing
[
27
]
and finance
[
13
]
, just to name a few. The main appeal behind this framework comes from the flexibility when modelling phenomena whose parameters depend on random environmental factors. For instance, modulation may be used to model correlated catastrophic events in insurance, different workload regimes in a queue, or seasonal sentiment changes in financial markets. In case the modulation is Markovian, a robust toolbox of matrix-analytic methods has been developed to compute relevant qualities; see e.g.
[
9
]
and references therein.

In this manuscript, we are interested in a class of processes whose dynamics are described by a continuously varying component which arises as the solution of a stochastic differential equation, and an environmental finite-state component which is modelled after a jump process that may or may not be Markovian. Such a class of processes is referred to in the literature as
hybrid stochastic differential equations
(hybrid SDEs), which generically take the form

(1.1)

\mathrm{d}X(t)=\mu(J(t),X(t))\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(J(t),X(t))\mathrm{d}B(t),

where
B
is a Brownian motion,
J
is the environmental process, and
\mu
and
\sigma
are real functions which satisfy certain regularity conditions.

In particular, we will be interested in hybrid SDEs which exhibit the following characteristics:

•

The process
X
is continuous and one-dimensional.

•

At a given instant, the environmental process switches state with an intensity that is dependent on the main component.

In simpler words, the class of hybrid SDEs we are interested in has components whose evolution is interlaced and dependent on each other. This contrasts with the Markov-modulated case, where one is able to draw a path of the environmental component without any knowledge of the main one (see
[
25
]
). In order to highlight this interlacing feature, in the literature such a model is referred to as hybrid SDEs with state-dependent switching; here we will simply call them hybrid SDEs for brevity.

Existence, uniqueness and stability properties for hybrid SDEs have been extensively studied in recent years
[
33
,
24
,
34
]
. Another stream of research lies in investigating efficient simulation methods of hybrid SDEs, most of them relying on adapating well-known convergence results into this considerably more challenging scenario
[
32
,
25
]
. However, computing explicit probabilistic descriptors for such a class of processes has been proven challenging, even in simple scenarios. For instance, several descriptors for Markov-modulated Brownian motion have been explicitly obtained in the literature (see e.g.
[
4
,
20
,
11
,
12
,
26
]
), but virtually none of these results have been extended to hybrid SDEs, even in the Markovian case. Such a task seems intractable simply because the toolbox for general diffusions is considerably more limited than the one available for the Brownian motion.

Our contributions to the literature of hybrid SDEs are the following. In a first instance, we provide a novel pathwise approximation technique by means of a space-grid discretization, resulting in approximations which belong to the class of multi-regime Markov-modulated Brownian motions. The latter are processes that, when restricted to each band of the space-grid, behave like a Markov-modulated Brownian motion. While Wong-Zakai or Euler-Maruyama are the most common approximation methods for simulation of hybrid SDEs in the literature, our proposed approximation sets the course to exploit aspects that are currently only knowm for multi-regime Markov-modulated Brownian motions. As an example, we employ our pathwise approximation result to provide approximations for the descriptors for first exit times of a hybrid SDEs over a band
[0,a]
,
a>0
, using recent results on the stationary measures of multi-regime Markov-modulated Brownian motion in queueing theory
[
18
,
1
]
. We remark that, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt to compute first passage probabilities and expected occupation times for hybrid SDEs, even when reduced to the case in which the environmental process is Markovian.

The structure of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we provide the proper framework to construct strong solutions to hybrid SDEs via the uniformization method and concatenation of paths. Later, in Section
3
, we construct the proposed multi-regime Markov-modulated Brownian motion approximation, and prove its uniform convergence in probability to the original hybrid SDE over increasing compact intervals. This approximation result is carried over in two steps by considering an auxiliary process, which serves as a middle point to prove our main result in Theorem
3.6
. In Section
4
we show how our convergence result can be applied to approximate first passage probabilities of hybrid SDEs, as well as its expected occupation times; some numerical examples of such approximations are explored as well. Finally, in Section
5
we provide a brief summary of our findings, along with a discussion on some avenues of further research.

2.
Hybrid stochastic differential equations

Let us provide a precise description of a hybrid SDE and its solution
(J,X)=\{(J(t),X(t))\}_{t\geq 0}
, for which we require a complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathbb{P},\mathcal{F})
that supports the following independent components:

•

A standard Brownian motion
{B}=\{B(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
; this will dictate the continuously-varying nature of
X
.

•

A Poisson process
{N}=\{N(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
of a sufficiently large parameter
\gamma>0
, and a sequence
\{U_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
of
\mbox{Unif}(0,1)
i.i.d. random variables: these will be used to describe the jump dynamics of
J
, with
N
marking the epochs at which jumps occur, and
U_{\ell}
dictating where the
\ell
th jump leads to.

Our first goal is to rigorously construct a pair
(J,X)
that solves the hybrid SDE

(2.1)

\mathrm{d}X(t)=\mu(J(t),X(t))\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(J(t),X(t))\mathrm{d}B(t),\quad J(0)=i_{0},\;X(0)=x_{0},

where
X
is an a.s. continuous real process,

{J}
is a càdlàg jump process with finite state space
\mathcal{E}=\{1,\dots,p\}
,
\mu:\mathcal{E}\times\mathds{R}\mapsto\mathds{R}
,
\sigma:\mathcal{E}\times\mathds{R}\mapsto\mathds{R}
,
i_{0}\in\mathcal{E}
and
x_{0}\in\mathds{R}
.
Furthermore, we require that
(J,X)
is adapted to the
\mathds{P}
-completed filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}
generated by
\big\{B(s),N(s),U_{N(s)};s\leq t\big\}
. A pair
(J,X)
satisfying the aforementioned characteristics is called a
strong solution
of (
2.1
).

Remark 2.1
.

In the context of classic Itô diffusions, a strong solution to an SDE needs to be adapted to the filtration generated by the driving noise component
B
only. In the case of hybrid stochastic differential equations, there is an additional stochastic component, the environmental process
J
, which is why the term ‘strong solution’ needs to be modified in the hybrid SDE framework.

In this paper we are interested in hybrid SDEs whose environmental process
{J}
switches at a state-dependent rate. Specifically, we let
J
evolve according to

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(J(t+h)=j|X(t),J(t)=i)=\delta_{ij}+\Lambda_{ij}(X(t))h+o(h)

for some family of intensity matrices
\{\bm{\Lambda}(x)\}_{x\in\mathds{R}}
which, at this stage, we assume to be continuous w.r.t.
x
.

Note that (
2.1
) characterises
part
of the pathwise construction of
{X}
. Indeed if
{J}
was given, then we could solve
{X}
during sojourn times of
{J}
with fixed drift and diffusion coefficients, updating them at each jump time of
{J}
. However, (
2.2
) does
not
tell us how
{J}
is meant to be constructed, it only provides a distributional property, implying that one needs a construction whose characteristics coincide with (
2.2
). This would not be a problem if, for instance,
\bm{\Lambda}(x)\equiv\bm{\Lambda}
,
x\in\mathds{R}
, for some intensity matrix
\bm{\Lambda}
; in such a case of a
Markov-modulated diffusion
, the evolution of
{J}
does not depend on the values of
{X}
, and thus can be constructed via uniformization arguments using the Poisson process
{N}
and the sequence
\{U_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
only (see
[
25
]
for more details). In the general case of hybrid SDEs, the construction is more involved since
{J}
evolves using information provided by
{X}
. Below we discuss a construction developed in
[
3
]
which relies on using a related inhomogeneous uniformization argument. First, we state some assumptions needed.

Assumption 1
.

For all
i\in\mathcal{E}
,
\mu(i,\cdot)
and
\sigma(i,\cdot)
are Lipschitz-continuous. i.e. there exists some
K>0
such that

\displaystyle|\mu(i,x)-\mu(i,y)|\vee|\sigma(i,x)-\sigma(i,y)|\leq K|x-y|\quad\mbox{for all}\quad x,y\in\mathds{R}.

Assumption 2
.

The family
\{\bm{\Lambda}(x)\}_{x\in\mathds{R}}
is uniformly bounded, i.e.

\gamma:=\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}}|\Lambda_{ii}(x)|<\infty.

Under Assumption
1
, we can guarantee the existence of a unique and strong solution to the (ordinary) stochastic differential equation (SDE)

(2.3)

X^{[i,x,v]}(t)=x+\int_{0}^{t}\mu(i,X^{[i,x,v]}(t))\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(i,X^{[i,x,v]}(t))\mathrm{d}B^{[v]}(r),

for all
i\in\mathcal{E}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
v,t\geq 0
, where
B^{[v]}(t):=B(v+t)-B(v)
is a time-shifted version of
{B}
. In other words,
{X}^{[i,x,v]}
corresponds to the solution of the SDE driven by
{B}^{[v]}
, with coefficients
\mu(i,\cdot)
and
\sigma(i,\cdot)
, and starting point
x
. On the other hand, Assumption
2
tells us that the jumps of
{J}
are
dominated
by those of a Poisson process of intensity
\gamma
, meaning that we can use the arrival points of
{N}
as the (possible) jump epochs of
{J}
.

The precise construction of
(J,X)
is as follows. Let
\{\theta_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 0}
denote the arrival times of
{N}
(with
\theta_{0}=0
). Define
X(0)=x_{0}
and
J(0)=i_{0}
, and for
t\in(0,\theta_{1})
, let
J(t)=i_{0}
and
X(t)=X^{[i_{0},x_{0},0]}(t)
: we have defined the processes
{X}
and
{J}
in
[0,\theta_{1})
. Since we want
{X}
to be continuous, we ought to define
X(\theta_{1})=X(\theta_{1}-)
. For
{J}
, we decide if it jumps or not at time
\theta_{1}
employing the values of
U_{1}
and
X(\theta_{1})
as follows:

(2.4)

J(\theta_{1})=k\quad\mbox{if}\quad U_{1}\in\left[\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\delta_{i_{0}j}+\frac{\Lambda_{i_{0}j}(X(\theta_{1}))}{\gamma},\sum_{j=1}^{k}\delta_{i_{0}j}+\frac{\Lambda_{i_{0}j}(X(\theta_{1}))}{\gamma}\right).

Although at a first look (
2.4
) may look complex, we are simply using
U_{1}
and
X(\theta_{1})
in such a way that
{J}
jumps to State
k
at time
\theta_{1}
with a mass given by the
(i_{0},k)
-th entry of the probability matrix
\bm{I}+\bm{\Lambda}(X(\theta_{1}))/\gamma
, the uniformized version of
\bm{\Lambda}(X(\theta_{1}))
(which we rigorously verify in Lemma
2.1
). After having defined
(J,X)
in
[0,\theta_{1}]
, we construct
{X}
in subsequent intervals
(\theta_{\ell},\theta_{\ell+1}]
by
concatenating
strong solutions of the type (
2.3
) with appropriate chosen values for
(i,x,v)
, as well as using a decision rule similar to (
2.4
) to establish which states
{J}
visits. More specifically, in a recursive manner, for
\ell=1,2,3,\dots
and
t\in(0,\theta_{\ell+1}-\theta_{\ell})
let

(2.5)

\displaystyle X(\theta_{\ell}+t)

\displaystyle=X^{[i_{\ell},x_{\ell},\theta_{\ell}]}(t),

(2.6)

\displaystyle X(\theta_{\ell+1})

\displaystyle=X(\theta_{\ell+1}-),

(2.7)

\displaystyle J(\theta_{\ell}+t)

\displaystyle=i_{\ell},

(2.8)

\displaystyle J(\theta_{\ell+1})

\displaystyle=k\quad\mbox{if}\quad U_{\ell}\in\left[{C}_{\ell}(k),{C}_{\ell}(k)+{D}_{\ell}(k)\right),

where
x_{\ell}=X(\theta_{\ell})
,
i_{\ell}=J(\theta_{\ell})
and

{C}_{\ell}(k)=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\delta_{i_{\ell}j}+\frac{{\Lambda}_{i_{\ell}j}({X}(\theta_{\ell+1}))}{\gamma},\quad{D}_{\ell}(k)=\delta_{i_{\ell}k}+\frac{{\Lambda}_{i_{\ell}k}({X}(\theta_{\ell+1}))}{\gamma}.

A few aspects that are straightforward to verify about this construction:

•

{X}
and
{J}
are
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted,

•

{X}
is continuous at
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots
, and thus, has a.s. continuous paths,

•

{J}
is càdlàg,

•

{X}
solves the hybrid SDE (
2.1
) on every interval
(\theta_{\ell},\theta_{\ell+1})
,
\ell=0,1,2,\dots
.

Thus,
(J,X)
is indeed a strong solution to (
2.1
).

Remark 2.2
.

Note that
{X}
is the unique solution to (
2.1
) under this
particular
construction of
{J}
. Indeed, if we choose a different construction of
{J}
, the process
{X}
may take another form. For instance, use the r.v.’s
U_{\ell}^{\prime}=1-U_{\ell}
instead of
U_{\ell}
in (
2.8
); the process
{J}
will then be different and so will
{X}
.

We still need to verify that (
2.2
) holds, which we prove in a slightly more general scenario next. Recall that (
2.2
) only makes sense if
\bm{\Lambda}(x)
is continuous w.r.t.
x
. A generalization of this property for discontinuous
\bm{\Lambda}(\cdot)
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(J(t+v)=J(t)\mbox{ for all }v\in[0,s]\;\mid\;\mathcal{F}_{t}\otimes\mathcal{F}^{{B}}_{t+s},X(t)=x,J(t)=i\right)

(2.9)

\displaystyle\quad=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{s}\Lambda_{ii}(X^{[i,x,t]}(v))\mathrm{d}v\right),

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(J(\theta_{\ell})=j\;\mid\;J(\theta_{\ell}-)=i,J(\theta_{\ell}-)\neq J(\theta_{\ell}),X(\theta_{\ell})\big)=\frac{\Lambda_{ij}(X(\theta_{\ell}))}{|\Lambda_{ii}(X(\theta_{\ell}))|},\quad i\neq j,

where
\mathcal{F}^{{B}}_{t}
is the
\mathbb{P}
-completion of the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\{B(s):0\leq s\leq t\}
. In essence, (
2.9
) and (
2.10
) correspond to how inhomogeneous Markov jump processes are classically constructed through their integrated jump intensities/hazard rates (see e.g.
[
29
, Ch. 13]
). That (
2.9
) and (
2.10
) imply (
2.2
) for continuous
\bm{\Lambda}(\cdot)
is readily obtained by noting that

\exp\left(\int_{0}^{h}\Lambda_{ii}(X^{[i,x,t]}(v))\mathrm{d}v\right)=1+\Lambda_{ii}(x)+o(h),

where we employed the continuity of
X^{[i,x,t]}
too. For sake of completeness, we now show that (
2.9
) and (
2.10
) indeed hold; this proof is a simplified version of an analogous result found in
[
3
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(J,X)
be constructed via (
2.5
)-(
2.8
). Then (
2.9
) and (
2.10
) hold.

Proof.

Integrating with respect to the number and position of arrivals
{N}
in
[t,t+s]
, say
\{\theta^{*}_{\ell}\}_{\ell}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(J(t+v)=J(t)\mbox{ for all }v\in[0,s]\;\mid\;\mathcal{F}_{t}\otimes\mathcal{F}^{{B}}_{t+s},X(t)=x,J(t)=i\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\gamma s)^{k}}{k!}e^{-\gamma s}\int_{0}^{s}\cdots\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathds{P}(J(\theta^{*}_{j})=J(\theta^{*}_{j}-)\mid\theta^{*}_{j}=t+v_{j},X^{[i,x,t]}(v_{j}),J(\theta^{*}_{j})=i)}{s^{k}}\mathrm{d}v_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}v_{k},

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\gamma s)^{k}}{k!}e^{-\gamma s}\int_{0}^{s}\cdots\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(1+\Lambda_{ii}(X^{[i,x,t]}(v_{j}))/\gamma\right)}{s^{k}}\mathrm{d}v_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}v_{k}

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\gamma s)^{k}}{k!}e^{-\gamma s}\left(1+\frac{\int_{0}^{s}\Lambda_{ii}(X^{[i,x,t]}(v))\mathrm{d}v}{\gamma s}\right)^{k}=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{s}\Lambda_{ii}(X^{[i,x,t]}(v))\mathrm{d}v\right),

where in the first equality we used that on the event
\{J(t+v)=J(t)\mbox{ for all }v\in[0,s],J(t)=i,X(t)=t\}
,
X(t+v)=X^{[i,x,t]}(v)
for all
v\in[0,s]
by construction. Finally, for
i\neq j\in\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(J(\theta_{\ell})=j\;\mid\;J(\theta_{\ell}-)=i,J(\theta_{\ell}-)\neq J(\theta_{\ell}),X(\theta_{\ell})\big)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(J(\theta_{\ell})=j\;\mid\;J(\theta_{\ell}-)=i,X(\theta_{\ell})\big)}{\mathbb{P}\big(J(\theta_{\ell}-)\neq J(\theta_{\ell})\;\mid\;J(\theta_{\ell}-)=i,X(\theta_{\ell})\big)}=\frac{\Lambda_{ij}(X(\theta_{\ell}))}{|\Lambda_{ii}(X(\theta_{\ell}))|},

completing the proof.
∎

We note that the construction of strong solutions for hybrid SDEs can be carried on in more general scenarios. For instance, Brownian-driven multidimensional hybrid SDEs with unbounded jump intensities are considered in
[
24
]
, while the construction discussed in
[
3
]
concerns multidimensional past-dependent hybrid SDEs driven by Lévy processes.

3.
Space-grid approximation of hybrid SDEs

Although solutions to state-dependent hybrid SDEs are easy to simulate following the steps in (
2.5
)-(
2.8
), studying their distributional properties is a challenging task (see
[
33
]
and references therein). Here we advocate to study hybrid SDEs using an approximation method via discretizing the space-grid.

Let
(\Omega,\mathbb{P},\mathcal{F})
be as in Section
2
. For each
i\in\mathcal{E}
, let
\widehat{\mu}(i,\cdot)
and
\widehat{\sigma}(i,\cdot)
be a piecewise constant approximation of
{\mu}(i,\cdot)
and
{\sigma}(i,\cdot)
on a space-grid
\{\zeta_{m}\}
of
\mathds{R}
. In this paper, we assume the following properties.

Assumption 3
.

For each
i\in\mathcal{E}
:

•

\widehat{\mu}(i,\cdot)
and
\widehat{\sigma}(i,\cdot)
are right-continuous with left limits,

•

|\widehat{\mu}(i,\cdot)|\leq|\mu(i,\cdot)|
and
|\widehat{\sigma}(i,\cdot)|\leq|\sigma(i,\cdot)|
,

•

\widehat{\mu}
and
\widehat{\sigma}
are such that the stochastic differential equation

(3.1)

\widehat{X}^{[i,x,v]}(t)=x+\int_{0}^{t}\widehat{\mu}(i,\widehat{X}^{[i,x,v]}(t))\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{t}\widehat{\sigma}(i,\widehat{X}^{[i,x,v]}(t))\mathrm{d}B^{[v]}(r)

admits a strong and unique solution for all
x\in\mathds{R}
and
v\geq 0
,

•

the space-grid
\{\zeta_{m}\}
has a finite cardinality.

Below we present an elementary stability condition that is useful for our forthcoming developments.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\widehat{X}^{[i,x,v]}
and
\widehat{X}^{[i,x,v]}
be the solutions of (
2.3
) and (
3.1
), respectively, where
\mu
,
\sigma
,
\widehat{{\mu}}
and
\widehat{{\sigma}}
attain Assumptions
1
and
3
. Then,

\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\leq t}|X^{[i,x,v]}(s)|^{2}\right)<\infty\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\leq t}|\widehat{{X}}^{[i,x,v]}(s)|^{2}\right)<\infty.

Proof.

The
L_{2}
-uniform boundedness over compact intervals of
{X}^{[i,x,v]}
is a standard result, which we briefly replicate below for the sake of completeness. Let
\rho_{m}:=\inf\{s>0\;:\;|{X}^{[i,x,v]}(s)|>m\}
, so that

\frac{1}{3}\left|{X}^{[i,x,v]}(s\wedge\rho_{m})\right|^{2}\leq|x|^{2}+\left|\int_{0}^{s\wedge\rho_{m}}\mu\left(i,{X}^{[i,x,v]}(r)\right)\mathrm{d}r\right|^{2}+\left|\int_{0}^{s\wedge\rho_{m}}\sigma\left(i,{X}^{[i,x,v]}(r)\right)\mathrm{d}B^{[v]}(r)\right|^{2}.

By Cauchy-Schwartz,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{s\wedge\rho_{m}}\mu\left(i,{X}^{[i,x,v]}(r)\right)\mathrm{d}r\right|^{2}\leq(s\wedge\rho_{m})\int_{0}^{s\wedge\rho_{m}}\left|\mu\left(i,{X}^{[i,x,v]}(r)\right)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}r.

By Doob’s inequality and Itô isometry,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\leq t}\left|\int_{0}^{s\wedge\rho_{m}}\sigma\left(i,{X}^{[i,x,v]}(r)\right)\mathrm{d}B^{[v]}(r)\right|^{2}\right)\leq 2^{2}\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{s\wedge\rho_{m}}\left|\sigma\left(i,{X}^{[i,x,v]}(r)\right)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}B^{[v]}(r)\right).

By the Assumption
1
,
\mu(i,\cdot)
and
\sigma(i,\cdot)
must be at most linearly increasing, meaning that there exists some
K^{\prime}>0
such that

|\mu(i,x)\vee\sigma(i,x)|\leq K^{\prime}(1+|x|).

Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\leq t\wedge\rho_{m}}\left|{X}^{[i,x,v]}(s)\right|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 3|x|^{2}+3t\int_{0}^{t}K^{\prime}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\leq r\wedge\rho_{m}}\left|{X}^{[i,x,v]}(s)\right|^{2}\right)+1\right)\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\quad+3\times 2^{2}\int_{0}^{t}K^{\prime}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\leq r\wedge\rho_{m}}\left|{X}^{[i,x,v]}(s)\right|^{2}\right)+1\right)\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=K_{1}^{\prime}+K_{2}^{\prime}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\leq r\wedge\rho_{m}}\left|{X}^{[i,x,v]}(s)\right|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}r,

where
K_{1}^{\prime}=3|x|^{2}+3t^{2}+3\times 2^{2}t
and
K_{2}^{\prime}=K^{\prime}(3t+2^{2})
. Gronwall’s lemma then yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\leq t\wedge\rho_{m}}\left|{X}^{[i,x,v]}(s)\right|^{2}\right)\leq K_{1}^{\prime}e^{K_{2}^{\prime}t};

by letting
m\rightarrow\infty
, we get that
\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\leq t}\left|{X}^{[i,x,v]}(s)\right|^{2}\right)<\infty
. Now, since
|\widehat{\mu}|\leq|\mu|
and
|\widehat{\sigma}|\leq|\sigma|
, then
\widehat{\mu}
and
\widehat{\sigma}
are at most linearly increasing as well. Given this, the statement concerning
\widehat{X}^{[i,x,v]}
follows by analogous arguments to the previously presented.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

Finding conditions under which a stochastic differential equation with discontinuous coefficients has a unique strong solution is an active area of research in recent years. For instance, in
[
21
]
it is established that (
3.1
) has a unique and strong solution for
\widehat{\sigma}(i,\cdot)=1
and general measurable function
\widehat{\mu}(i,\cdot)
that attains certain integrability properties. In
[
22
]
, the same result is established for Lipschitz-continuous functions
\widehat{\sigma}(i,\cdot)
and piecewise Lipschitz-continuous functions
\widehat{\mu}(i,\cdot)
, both assumed to be bounded. Given the nature of
\widehat{\mu}(i,\cdot)
and
\widehat{\sigma}(i,\cdot)
as piecewise constant approximations of
\mu(i,\cdot)
and
\sigma(i,\cdot)
, and taking into account the existing results in
[
21
]
and
[
22
]
, our setup allows for the case in which
\widehat{\sigma}(i,\cdot)
and
\sigma(i,\cdot)
are constant, and
\widehat{\mu}(i,\cdot)
and
\mu(i,\cdot)
are bounded functions. Other possible setups that produce strong and unique solutions to (
3.1
) ought to be studied on a case by case basis, a topic which goes beyond the scope of this manuscript.

Now, let
\widehat{{{J}}}
be a càdlàg
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted jump process whose jump intensities are of the form

(3.2)

\mathbb{P}(\widehat{{J}}(t+h)=j|\widehat{{X}}(t),\widehat{{J}}(t)=i)=\delta_{ij}+\widehat{{\Lambda}}_{ij}(\widehat{{X}}(t))h+o(h)

with
\widehat{{\bm{\Lambda}}}(\cdot)
being a piecewise constant approximation of
\bm{\Lambda}(\cdot)
over the space-grid
\{\zeta_{m}\}
.
Under Assumption
3
and supposing that the path
\widehat{J}
is
somehow
known, the solution
\widehat{X}
to the hybrid SDE

(3.3)

\mathrm{d}\widehat{{X}}(t)=\widehat{{\mu}}(\widehat{{J}}(t),\widehat{{X}}(t))\mathrm{d}t+\widehat{{\sigma}}(\widehat{{J}}(t),\widehat{{X}}(t))\mathrm{d}B(t),\quad\widehat{{J}}(0)=i_{0},\;\widehat{{X}}(0)=x_{0},

can be constructed by a piecewise concatenation of paths, analogously to (
2.5
)-(
2.6
). The resulting process is such that, when restricted to a space interval
(\zeta_{m},\zeta_{m+1})
and to the time intervals for which
\widehat{J}
is equal to
i
,
\widehat{X}
behaves like a Brownian motion with drift
\widehat{\mu}(i,\zeta_{m})
and noise coefficient
\widehat{\sigma}(i,\zeta_{m})
. In fact, the process
\widehat{X}
falls within the class of
multi-regime Markov-modulated Brownian motions
studied in
[
18
]
(see
[
23
]
for an earlier reference in the case
\widehat{\sigma}\equiv 0
). Heuristically speaking, since the functions
\widehat{\mu}
,
\widehat{\sigma}
and
\widehat{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
are space-grid approximations of
\mu
,
\sigma
and
\bm{\Lambda}
, one can expect that
\widehat{{X}}
approximates the original solution

. A main purpose of this paper is to formalize this statement.

Remark 3.2
.

In the previous paragraph we did not specify how
\widehat{{J}}
is constructed. This was on purpose: while it is straightforward to follow analogous steps to those in (
2.7
)-(
2.8
) to define
\widehat{{J}}
, here we need to follow slightly different steps in order to guarantee the pathwise convergence of
(\widehat{J},\widehat{X})
to
(J,X)
. The specifics of such a construction are contained in Subsection
3.2
.

To prove pathwise convergence of
(\widehat{{J}},\widehat{{X}})
to
({J},{X})
as some of its parameters go to
\infty
, we need to proceed with care. First we study the unique continuous strong solution
\tilde{{X}}
to

(3.4)

\mathrm{d}\tilde{X}(t)=\widehat{{\mu}}({J}(t),\tilde{X}(t))\mathrm{d}t+\widehat{{\sigma}}({J}(t),\tilde{X}(t))\mathrm{d}B(t),\quad{J}(0)=i_{0},\;\tilde{X}(0)=\tilde{x}_{0},

where
{J}
is the jump process constructed through (
2.5
)-(
2.8
), while
X
is recursively defined by

(3.5)

\displaystyle\tilde{X}(\theta_{\ell}+t)=\widehat{X}^{[i_{\ell},\tilde{x}_{\ell},\theta_{\ell}]}(t)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\tilde{X}(\theta_{\ell+1})=\widehat{X}(\theta_{\ell+1}-)\quad\mbox{where}\quad\tilde{x}_{\ell}=\tilde{X}(\theta_{\ell}).

The
practicality
of the process
\tilde{{X}}
is essentially null.
\tilde{{X}}
is an approximation to
{X}
; however, it needs
{J}
and
{X}
to be constructed. This does not mean that it is ill-defined; in fact, once
(J,X)
is constructed (which can be done without issues following (
2.5
)-(
2.8
)), then
\tilde{{X}}
simply arises as a concatenation of solution paths. Instead, the real usefulness of
\tilde{{X}}
is in providing an intermediate step towards quantifying the approximation error
|{X}-\widehat{{{X}}}|
: we will first measure
|{X}-\tilde{{X}}|
(discretization error brought by
\widehat{\mu}
and
\widehat{\sigma}
) and then the discrepancy between
\tilde{{X}}
and
\widehat{{{X}}}
(discretization error brought by
\widehat{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
).

3.1.
Measuring
|{X}-\tilde{{X}}|
.

In order to quantify the approximation error of
\tilde{{X}}
to
X
, let us first measure the uniform mean-square distance over compact time intervals between
{X}
and
\tilde{{X}}
in the case
\mathcal{E}=\{1\}
(that is, no switching occurs). Abusing notation for a brief moment, let us write
\mu
,
\sigma
,
\widehat{\mu}
and
\widehat{\sigma}
instead of
\mu(1,\cdot)
,
\sigma(1,\cdot)
,
\widehat{\mu}(1,\cdot)
and
\widehat{\sigma}(1,\cdot)
, respectively.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{E}=\{1\}
and let
t\geq 0
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\leq t}\left|X(s)-\tilde{X}(s)\right|^{2}\right)\leq C(t)\left(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mu(x)-\widehat{\mu}(x)\right|^{2}+\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\sigma(x)-\widehat{\sigma}(x)\right|^{2}+|x_{0}-\tilde{x_{0}}|^{2}\right),

where
C(t)
takes the form (
3.12
).

Proof.

For
t\geq 0
, define

Z(t)=\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\leq t}|X(s)-\tilde{X}(s)|^{2}\right).

By Lemma
3.1
,
Z(t)
is finite for all
t\geq 0
. Using the inequality
(a+b+c)^{2}\leq 3a^{2}+3b^{2}+3c^{2}
,

(3.6)

\displaystyle Z(t)

\displaystyle\leq 3\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\leq t}\left|\int_{0}^{s}\mu(X(r))-\widehat{\mu}(\tilde{X}(r))\mathrm{d}r\right|^{2}\right)

(3.7)

\displaystyle\quad+3\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\leq t}\left|\int_{0}^{s}\sigma(X(r))-\widehat{\sigma}(\tilde{X}(r))\mathrm{d}B(r)\right|^{2}\right)+3|x_{0}-\tilde{x_{0}}|^{2}.

Employing the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\leq t}\left|\int_{0}^{s}\mu(X(r))-\widehat{\mu}(\tilde{X}(r))\mathrm{d}r\right|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\int_{0}^{t}1\times\left|\mu(X(r))-\widehat{\mu}(\tilde{X}(r))\right|\mathrm{d}r\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\int_{0}^{t}1^{2}\mathrm{d}r\right)\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left|\mu(X(r))-\widehat{\mu}(\tilde{X}(r))\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}r\right)\right)

(3.8)

\displaystyle=t\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\mu(X(r))-\widehat{\mu}(\tilde{X}(r))\right|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}r,

while the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\leq t}\left|\int_{0}^{s}\sigma(X(r))-\widehat{\sigma}(\tilde{X}(r))\mathrm{d}B(r)\right|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{*}\mathds{E}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left|\sigma(X(r))-\widehat{\sigma}(\tilde{X}(r))\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}r\right)

(3.9)

\displaystyle=C_{*}\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{E}\left(\left|\sigma(X(r))-\widehat{\sigma}(\tilde{X}(r))\right|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}r

for some universal constant
C_{*}>0
. Furthermore, the Lipschitz-continuity of
\mu
implies

\displaystyle\left|\mu(X(r))-\widehat{\mu}(\tilde{X}(r))\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left|\mu(X(r))-\mu(\tilde{X}(r))\right|^{2}+2\left|\mu(\tilde{X}(r))-\widehat{\mu}(\tilde{X}(r))\right|^{2}

(3.10)

\displaystyle\leq 2K^{2}\left|X(r)-\tilde{X}(r)\right|^{2}+2\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mu(x)-\widehat{\mu}(x)\right|^{2};

similarly,

(3.11)

\displaystyle\left|\sigma(X(r))-\widehat{\sigma}(\tilde{X}(r))\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2K^{2}\left|X(r)-\tilde{X}(r)\right|^{2}+2\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\sigma(x)-\widehat{\sigma}(x)\right|^{2}.

By virtue of (
3.7
)-(
3.11
), we get

\displaystyle Z(t)

\displaystyle\leq 6K^{2}(t+C_{*})\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left(\left|X(r)-\tilde{X}(r)\right|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}r+6t\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mu(x)-\widehat{\mu}(x)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+6t\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\sigma(x)-\widehat{\sigma}(x)\right|^{2}+3|x_{0}-\tilde{x_{0}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 6K^{2}(t+C_{*})\int_{0}^{t}Z(r)\mathrm{d}r+6t\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mu(x)-\widehat{\mu}(x)\right|^{2}+6t\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\sigma(x)-\widehat{\sigma}(x)\right|^{2}+3|x_{0}-\tilde{x_{0}}|^{2}.

Finally, Gronwall’s lemma implies that

\displaystyle Z(t)

\displaystyle\leq C(t)\left(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mu(x)-\widehat{\mu}(x)\right|^{2}+\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\sigma(x)-\widehat{\sigma}(x)\right|^{2}+|x_{0}-\tilde{x_{0}}|^{2}\right)

where

(3.12)

C(t)=(6t\vee 3)e^{6K^{2}(t+C_{*})t}.

∎

As usual, mean-square convergence implies convergence in probability, which in our case takes the following explicit form.

Corollary 3.3
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{E}=\{1\}
and that there exists some
\alpha>0
such that

(3.13)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mu(x)-\widehat{\mu}(x)\right|\vee\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\sigma(x)-\widehat{\sigma}(x)\right|\vee|x_{0}-\tilde{x}_{0}|\leq\alpha.

Then, for any
n\geq 2
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s\leq t}\left|X(s)-\tilde{X}(s)\right|\geq\sqrt{3C(t)(\log n)}\alpha\right)\leq(\log n)^{-1}.

Proof.

Markov’s inequality and Theorem
3.2
imply that for any
\Delta_{n,t,\alpha}>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left(\sup_{s\leq t}\left|X(s)-\tilde{X}(s)\right|\geq\Delta_{n,t,\alpha}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\leq t}|X(s)-\tilde{X}(s)|^{2}\right)}{\Delta_{n,t,\alpha}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(t)\left(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mu(x)-\widehat{\mu}(x)\right|^{2}+\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\sigma(x)-\widehat{\sigma}(x)\right|^{2}+|x_{0}-\tilde{x_{0}}|^{2}\right)}{\Delta_{n,t,\alpha}^{2}}=\frac{3C(t)\alpha^{2}}{\Delta_{n,t,\alpha}^{2}}.

The result follows by choosing
\Delta_{n,t,\alpha}=\sqrt{3C(t)(\log n)}\alpha
.
∎

Corollary
3.3
implies that we can choose appropriate values of
t
and
\alpha
which are dependent on
n
, say
t_{n}
and
\alpha_{n}
, such that

(3.14)

\displaystyle t_{n}\rightarrow\infty\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sqrt{3C(t_{n})(\log n)}\alpha_{n}\rightarrow 0,

and then conclude that the path
\tilde{{X}}
converges in probability to
{X}
uniformly over increasing compact intervals. Here we choose
t_{n}=\sqrt{\log\log n}
and
\alpha_{n}=(\log n)^{\beta}n^{-\gamma}
for some
\beta,\gamma>0
. In such a case, there exists some
n_{0}\geq 1
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{3C(t_{n})(\log n)}\alpha_{n}

\displaystyle\leq(6\sqrt{\log\log n})e^{12K^{2}\log\log n}(\log n)^{\beta}n^{-\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq(\log n)(\log n)^{12K^{2}}(\log n)^{\beta}n^{-\gamma}

(3.15)

\displaystyle=(\log n)^{\beta_{*}+\beta}n^{-\gamma},

where
\beta_{*}=1+12K^{2}
; note that
n_{0}
does not depend on the choice of
\beta
or
\gamma
. In light of this, we assume the following.

Assumption 4
.

There exists some
\beta\geq 0
and
\gamma>0
such that for each
n\geq 2
, the approximation
\tilde{{X}}^{(n)}
(constructed as
\tilde{{X}}
in (
3.4
) where the coefficients
\widehat{\mu}
and
\widehat{\sigma}
now show an explicit dependence on
n
via their superscript) satisfies

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R},i\in\mathcal{E}}\left|\mu(i,x)-\widehat{\mu}^{(n)}(i,x)\right|\vee\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R},i\in\mathcal{E}}\left|\sigma(i,x)-\widehat{\sigma}^{(n)}(i,x)\right|\vee|x_{0}-\tilde{x}^{(n)}_{0}|\leq(\log n)^{\beta}n^{-\gamma}.

While it might seem that the proof in Theorem
3.2
can be easily modified to handle the general case
\mathcal{E}=\{1,\dots,p\}
, the switching caused by
{J}
destroys the local martingale property of the involved stochastic integrals, and thus, the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality no longer applies. For this reason, our approach is to study the discrepancy between
{X}
and
\tilde{{X}}^{(n)}
by concatenating paths at the time epochs
\{\theta_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 0}
for which switching can occur. We explicitly do this in the proof below.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{E}=\{1,\dots,p\}
and fix some small
\epsilon_{1}>0
. As
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s\leq\xi_{n}}\left|X(s)-\tilde{X}^{(n)}(s)\right|>n^{-\gamma+\epsilon_{1}}\right)=o(1).

where
\xi_{n}=\sqrt{\log\log\log n}
.

Proof.

First, we claim that

(3.16)

\mathds{P}(N(\xi_{n})>\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor)=o(1)\quad\mbox{as}\quad n\rightarrow\infty.

Indeed, using
[
16
, Proposition 1]
, for sufficiently large
n

(3.17)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}(N(\xi_{n})>\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor)\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\gamma\xi_{n}}{\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor+1}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{(\gamma\xi_{n})^{\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor}}{\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor!}e^{-\gamma\xi_{n}}\right):

the first element in the product of the r.h.s. in (
3.17
) clearly converges to
1
, while the second element is bounded by

\displaystyle\frac{(\gamma\xi_{n})^{\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor}}{\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor!}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left((\gamma\xi_{n})^{\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor}\right)\left(\sqrt{2\pi\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor}\left(\frac{\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor}{e}\right)^{\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor}e^{\tfrac{1}{12\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor+1}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\quad=O\left(\left(\frac{e\gamma\xi_{n}}{\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor}\right)^{\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor}\right)=o(1).

Now, fix some large
n\geq 1
and for all
m\geq 0
let

\displaystyle A_{m}

\displaystyle=\left\{\sup_{s\leq\xi_{n}\wedge\theta_{m}}\left|X(s)-\tilde{X}^{(n)}(s)\right|>(\log n)^{m\beta_{*}+\beta}n^{\gamma}\right\}.

Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(A_{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(A_{m-1}\cap A_{m})+\mathbb{P}(A_{m-1}^{c}\cap A_{m})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(A_{m-1})+\mathbb{P}(A_{m}\mid A_{m-1}^{c})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(A_{\ell}\mid A_{\ell-1}^{c})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s\leq\xi_{n}\wedge\theta_{\ell}}\left|X(s)-\tilde{X}^{(n)}(s)\right|>(\log n)^{\ell\beta_{*}+\beta}n^{\gamma}\mid A_{\ell-1}^{c}\right)\leq m(\log n)^{-1},

where the last inequality follows by using Corollary
3.3
and (
3.15
) for all
\widehat{\mu}(i,\cdot)
and
\widehat{\sigma}(i,\cdot)
,
i\in\mathcal{E}
. Finally, for sufficiently large
n
(large enough that
(\log\ell)^{\lfloor\log\log\ell\rfloor\beta_{*}+\beta}\geq n^{\epsilon_{1}}
for all
\ell\geq 1
),

\displaystyle\left\{\sup_{s\leq\xi_{n}}\left|X(s)-\tilde{X}^{(n)}(s)\right|>n^{-\gamma+\epsilon_{1}}\right\}\subset\left\{N(\xi_{n})>\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor\right\}\cup\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor}A^{(n)}_{\ell},

so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s\leq\xi_{n}}\left|X(s)-\tilde{X}^{(n)}(s)\right|>n^{-\gamma+\epsilon_{1}}\right)\leq o(1)+(\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor)^{2}(\log n)^{-1}=o(1).

∎

Now that we have assessed the convergence of
\tilde{X}
to
X
, we will continue with the discrepancy between
\tilde{X}
and
\widehat{{X}}
in the following section, which will ultimately lead to measuring the final error
|{X}-\widehat{{{X}}}|
.

3.2.
Measuring
|\tilde{X}-\widehat{{X}}|
.

Assessing the discrepancy between
\tilde{X}
and
\widehat{{X}}
relies on the following key observation:
\widehat{X}
is
identical
to
\tilde{X}
in a compact interval
[0,t]
if
\widehat{J}
coincides with
J
in that interval. Thus, the question of convergence of
\widehat{X}
to
\tilde{X}
may be posed as a question of if/when the process
\widehat{J}
differs from
J
. In order to answer this, we borrow the
decoupling
idea introduced in
[
3
]
. This concept relies on constructing the approximation
\widehat{J}^{(n)}
(understood as the process
\widehat{J}
with an explicit dependence on some parameter
n
; we omit such dependence in some places for notational convenience) in such a way that this process decouples or stops being identical to
J
in an interval
[0,t]
with a probability that tends to
0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. As announced in Remark
3.2
, instead of constructing
\widehat{J}
via (
2.7
)-(
2.8
), below we describe an alternative (but similar) technique that yields the distributional property (
3.2
) as well.

First, let us add an extra process
\{\widehat{H}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 0}
which tracks the
decoupling
event of
\widehat{J}
w.r.t.
J
at the time epochs
\{\theta_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 0}
. Roughly speaking, we say a decoupling occurs at time
\theta_{\ell}
if we can no longer guarantee that
\widehat{J}(\theta_{\ell})=J(\theta_{\ell})
, but we can still guarantee that
\widehat{J}
is identical to
J
on
[0,\theta_{\ell})
. To properly define this, we consider a process
\{\widehat{H}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 0}
taking values in
\{0,1,2\}
, where
\{\widehat{H}_{\ell}=0\}
will signify that the decoupling has not occurred by time
\theta_{\ell}
,
\{\widehat{H}_{\ell}=1\}
will signify that the decoupling occured exactly at time
\theta_{\ell}
, and
\{\widehat{H}_{\ell}=2\}
will signify that a decoupling occurred before time
\theta_{\ell}
. More specifically, let
\widehat{X}=x_{0}
,
\widehat{J}(\theta_{0})=i_{0}
,
\widehat{H}_{0}=0
,
and recursively define for
\ell=0,1,2,\dots
and
t\in(0,\theta_{\ell+1}-\theta_{\ell})
,

(3.18)

\displaystyle\widehat{X}(\theta_{\ell}+t)

\displaystyle=\widehat{X}^{[\widehat{i}_{\ell},\widehat{x}_{\ell},\theta_{\ell}]}(t),

(3.19)

\displaystyle\widehat{X}(\theta_{\ell+1})

\displaystyle=\widehat{X}(\theta_{\ell+1}-),

(3.20)

\displaystyle\widehat{J}(\theta_{\ell}+t)

\displaystyle=\widehat{i}_{\ell},

\displaystyle\widehat{J}(\theta_{\ell+1})

\displaystyle=k,\;\widehat{H}_{\ell+1}=0

(3.21)

\displaystyle\quad\mbox{if}\quad U_{\ell}\in\left[{C}_{\ell}(k),{C}_{\ell}(k)+{D}_{\ell}(k)\wedge\widehat{D}_{\ell}(k)\right),\widehat{H}_{\ell}=0,

\displaystyle\widehat{J}(\theta_{\ell+1})

\displaystyle\sim(\widehat{D}_{\ell}(k)-{D}_{\ell}(k)\wedge\widehat{D}_{\ell}(k))_{k\in\mathcal{E}},\;\widehat{H}_{\ell+1}=1

(3.22)

\displaystyle\quad\mbox{if}\quad U_{\ell}\notin\cup_{k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}}\left[{C}_{\ell}(k^{\prime}),{C}_{\ell}(k^{\prime})+{D}_{\ell}(k^{\prime})\wedge\widehat{D}_{\ell}(k^{\prime})\right),\widehat{H}_{\ell}=0,

(3.23)

\displaystyle\widehat{J}(\theta_{\ell+1})

\displaystyle\sim(\widehat{D}_{\ell}(k))_{k\in\mathcal{E}},\;\widehat{H}_{\ell+1}=2\quad\mbox{if}\quad\widehat{H}_{\ell}\in\{1,2\},

where
\widehat{x}_{\ell}=\widehat{X}(\theta_{\ell})
,
\widehat{i}_{\ell}=\widehat{{J}}(\theta_{\ell})
and
\widehat{D}_{\ell}(k)=\delta_{i_{\ell}k}+\frac{\widehat{\Lambda}_{i_{\ell}k}(\widehat{X}(\theta_{\ell+1}))}{\gamma}
.
Here the notation ‘
Y\sim\bm{\alpha}
’ for
\bm{\alpha}\neq\bm{0}
denotes ‘
Y
is sampled from
\bm{\alpha}/|\bm{\alpha}|
’.

While Equations (
3.18
)-(
3.20
) are analogous to their counterpart (
2.5
)-(
2.7
), note that (
3.21
)-(
3.23
) are slightly more involved than (
2.8
). Nevertheless, the idea is similar:
\widehat{J}
is constructed in such a way that at the uniformization epoch
\theta_{\ell+1}
, it lands in State
k
with probability
\widehat{D}_{\ell}(k)
. Indeed, if
\widehat{H}_{\ell}=0
, then in order to land in
k
, either

•

U_{\ell}
is in
\left[{C}_{\ell}(k),{C}_{\ell}(k)+{D}_{\ell}(k)\wedge\widehat{D}_{\ell}(k)\right)
(with probability
{D}_{\ell}(k)\wedge\widehat{D}_{\ell}(k)
), or

•

U_{\ell}
lands in
[0,1]\setminus\left(\cup_{k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}}\left[{C}_{\ell}(k^{\prime}),{C}_{\ell}(k^{\prime})+{D}_{\ell}(k^{\prime})\wedge\widehat{D}_{\ell}(k^{\prime})\right)\right)
and then
k
gets sampled from the vector
\left(\widehat{D}_{\ell}(k^{\prime})-{D}_{\ell}(k^{\prime})\wedge\widehat{D}_{\ell}(k^{\prime})\right)_{k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}}
(with probability
\widehat{D}_{\ell}(k)-{D}_{\ell}(k)\wedge\widehat{D}_{\ell}(k)
).

In the second case, a decoupling is declared at that step and we let
\widehat{H}_{\ell+1}=1
; under these circumstances, by construction we can still guarantee that
\{\widehat{J}(t)\}_{t<\theta_{\ell+1}}=\{J(t)\}_{t<\theta_{\ell+1}}
(compare (
2.8
) and (
3.21
)-(
3.23
)) and
\{\widehat{X}(t)\}_{t\leq\theta_{\ell+1}}=\{\tilde{X}(t)\}_{t\leq\theta_{\ell+1}}
(compare (
3.5
) and (
3.18
)-(
3.19
)). Once a decoupling occurs, further jumps of
J
at the uniformization epochs occur by sampling directly from the vector
(\widehat{D}_{\ell}(k^{\prime}))_{k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}}
, which are points in time at which we can no longer guarantee that
\widehat{J}
and
J
are identical (ditto
\widehat{X}
and
\tilde{X}
). Fortunately, since
\widehat{\Lambda}
is thought to be close to
\bm{\Lambda}
, so is
\widehat{D}_{\ell}(k)
to
{D}_{\ell}(k)
, suggesting that the probability of a decoupling at a given step must be low. In the rest of the section, we investigate a stronger statement: decoupling occurs with low probability in any compact time interval.

Similarly to Assumption
4
, let us now make explicit the dependence of
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}
,
\widehat{X}
and
\widehat{J}
on
n
via a superscript. In order to obtain convergence rates of the decoupling of
\widehat{J}^{(n)}
w.r.t.
J
, we impose the following regularity condition on
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{(n)}
.

Assumption 5
.

For a square matrix
A=\{a_{ij}\}
, let
\|\cdot\|
denote the norm defined by

\|A\|=\sup_{i}\left\{\sum_{j}a_{ij}\right\}.

The intensity matrix
\bm{\Lambda}(\cdot)
is
\log
-Hölder continuous, in the sense that there exists a constant
G>0
such that for any
x,y\in\mathds{R}
,

\left\|\bm{\Lambda}(x)-\bm{\Lambda}(y)\right\|\leq\frac{G}{-\log|x-y|}.

Furthermore,
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{(n)}=\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}
is right-continuous with left limits and converges uniformly to

at a

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\left\|\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{(n)}(z)-\bm{\Lambda}(z)\right\|\leq\frac{G}{\log n}.

Remark 3.3

Under Assumption

does not admit any discontinuities, however,
recall that

-Hölder continuity is considerably less restrictive than Hölder- or Lipschitz-continuity. This means that we can

Lemma 3.5
.

Fix some small
\epsilon_{1}>0
. Then,

(3.24)

\mathbb{P}\left(N(\xi_{n})\leq\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor,\widehat{H}_{N({\xi_{n}})}\neq 0,\sup_{s\leq\xi_{n}}\left|X(s)-\widehat{X}^{(n)}(s)\right|\leq n^{-\gamma+\epsilon_{1}}\right)=o(1)\quad\mbox{as}\quad n\rightarrow\infty.

Additionally, Equation (

is replaced by

Proof.

For notational convenience, let us write
\widehat{X}
instead of
\widehat{X}^{(n)}
, and
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}
instead of
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{(n)}
. Note that

\displaystyle\left\{N(\xi_{n})\leq\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor,\widehat{H}_{N({\xi_{n}})}\neq 0,\sup_{s\leq\xi_{n}}\left|X(s)-\widehat{X}(s)\right|\leq n^{-\gamma+\epsilon_{1}}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad=\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor}\bigcup_{k=1}^{\ell}\left\{N(\xi_{n})=\ell,\widehat{H}_{k}=1,\sup_{s\leq\xi_{n}}\left|X(s)-\widehat{X}(s)\right|\leq n^{-\gamma+\epsilon_{1}}\right\}

(3.25)

\displaystyle\quad\subseteq\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor}\bigcup_{k=1}^{\ell}\left\{N(\xi_{n})=\ell,\;\widehat{H}_{k}=1,\;\sup_{s\leq\theta_{k}}\left|X(s)-\widehat{X}(s)\right|\leq n^{-\gamma+\epsilon_{1}}\right\}.

By conditioning on the history of the processes up to time
\theta_{k}
and letting

F=\{{N(\xi_{n})=\ell,\;\widehat{H}_{k-1}=0,\;\sup_{s\leq\theta_{k}}\left|X(s)-\widehat{X}(s)\right|\leq n^{-\gamma+\epsilon_{1}}}\},

we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N(\xi_{n})=\ell,\widehat{H}_{k}=1,\sup_{s\leq\theta_{k}}\left|X(s)-\widehat{X}(s)\right|\leq n^{-\gamma+\epsilon_{1}}\;|\;N(\xi_{n}),\widehat{H}_{k-1},\{X(s)\}_{s\leq\theta_{k}},\{\widehat{X}(s)\}_{s\leq\theta_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{H}_{k}=1\;|\;N(\xi_{n}),\{X(s)\}_{s\leq\theta_{k}},\{\widehat{X}(s)\}_{s\leq\theta_{k}}\right)\times\mathds{1}_{F}

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{P}\left(U_{\ell-1}\notin\cup_{k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}}\left[{C}_{\ell-1}(k^{\prime}),{C}_{\ell-1}(k^{\prime})+{D}_{\ell-1}(k^{\prime})\wedge\widehat{D}_{\ell-1}(k^{\prime})\right)\;|\;N(\xi_{n}),\{X(s)\}_{s\leq\theta_{k}},\{\widehat{X}(s)\}_{s\leq\theta_{k}}\right)\times\mathds{1}_{F}

\displaystyle\quad=\left(\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}}(\widehat{D}_{\ell-1}(k^{\prime})-{D}_{\ell-1}(k^{\prime})\wedge\widehat{D}_{\ell-1}(k^{\prime}))\right)\times\mathds{1}_{F}\leq\left\|\frac{{\bm{\Lambda}}({X}(\theta_{k}))}{\gamma}-\frac{\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}(\widehat{X}(\theta_{k}))}{\gamma}\right\|\times\mathds{1}_{F}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\left[\left\|{{\bm{\Lambda}}({X}(\theta_{k}))}-{{\bm{\Lambda}}(\widehat{X}(\theta_{k}))}\right\|+\left\|{{\bm{\Lambda}}(\widehat{X}(\theta_{k}))}-{\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}(\widehat{X}(\theta_{k}))}\right\|\right]\times\mathds{1}_{F}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\left[\frac{G}{-\log|{X}(\theta_{k})-\widehat{X}(\theta_{k})|}+\frac{G}{\log n}\right]\times\mathds{1}_{F}\leq\frac{G}{\gamma}\left[\frac{1}{\gamma-\epsilon_{1}}+1\right](\log n)^{-1}.

From (
3.25
) we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N(\xi_{n})\leq\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor,\widehat{H}_{N({\xi_{n}})}\neq 0,\sup_{s\leq\xi_{n}}\left|X(s)-\widehat{X}^{(n)}(s)\right|\leq n^{-\gamma+\epsilon_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{P}\left(N(\xi_{n})=\ell,\;\widehat{H}_{k}=1,\;\sup_{s\leq\theta_{k}}\left|X(s)-\widehat{X}(s)\right|\leq n^{-\gamma+\epsilon_{1}}\right)

(3.26)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{G}{\gamma}\left[\frac{1}{\gamma-\epsilon_{1}}+1\right](\log n)^{-1}\leq\frac{G}{\gamma}\left[\frac{1}{\gamma-\epsilon_{1}}+1\right]\frac{\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor^{2}}{\log n}=o(1),

which proves (

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N(\xi_{n})\leq\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor,\widehat{H}_{N({\xi_{n}})}\neq 0,\sup_{s\leq\xi_{n}}\left|X(s)-\tilde{X}^{(n)}(s)\right|\leq n^{-\gamma+\epsilon_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{P}\left(N(\xi_{n})=\ell,\;\widehat{H}_{k}=1,\;\sup_{s\leq\theta_{k}}\left|X(s)-\tilde{X}(s)\right|\leq n^{-\gamma+\epsilon_{1}}\right);

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N(\xi_{n})=\ell,\;\widehat{H}_{k}=1,\;\sup_{s\leq\theta_{k}}\left|X(s)-\tilde{X}(s)\right|\leq n^{-\gamma+\epsilon_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(N(\xi_{n})=\ell,\;\widehat{H}_{k}=1,\;\sup_{s\leq\theta_{k}}\left|X(s)-\widehat{X}(s)\right|\leq n^{-\gamma+\epsilon_{1}}\right),

so the result for the case

follows by identical steps to those in (

Theorem

4.
First passage probabilities and expected occupation times for solutions of hybrid SDEs

Define the first passage times
\tau_{0}^{-}=\inf\{t>0:X(t)<0\}
,
\tau_{a}^{+}=\inf\{t>0:X(t)>a\}
(
a>0
). For
q\geq 0
, we are interested in providing approximations for the Laplace transform of the first passage times,

m^{-}_{ij}(u,q,a)=\mathds{E}(e^{-q(\tau_{0}^{-}\wedge\tau_{a}^{+})}\mathds{1}\left\{J(\tau_{0}^{-}\wedge\tau_{a}^{+})=j,X(\tau_{0}^{-}\wedge\tau_{a}^{+})=0\right\}\,\mid\,J(0)=i,X(0)=u),

m^{+}_{ij}(u,q,a)=\mathds{E}(e^{-q(\tau_{0}^{-}\wedge\tau_{a}^{+})}\mathds{1}\left\{J(\tau_{0}^{-}\wedge\tau_{a}^{+})=j,X(\tau_{0}^{-}\wedge\tau_{a}^{+})=a\right\}\,\mid\,J(0)=i,X(0)=u),

for
0<u<a\leq\infty
and
i,j\in\mathcal{E}
. In essence, the functions
m^{+}_{ij}
and
m^{-}_{ij}
characterize the distributional law of the exit times of
X
from the band
[0,a]
. Employing the law of total probability, it is straightforward to reinterpret
m^{-}_{ij}
and
m^{-}_{ij}
as

m^{-}_{ij}(u,q,a)=\mathds{P}(\tau_{0}^{-}\wedge\tau_{a}^{+}<e_{q},J(\tau_{0}^{-}\wedge\tau_{a}^{+})=j,X(\tau_{0}^{-}\wedge\tau_{a}^{+})=0\,\mid\,J(0)=i,X(0)=u),

m^{+}_{ij}(u,q,a)=\mathds{P}(\tau_{0}^{-}\wedge\tau_{a}^{+}<e_{q},J(\tau_{0}^{-}\wedge\tau_{a}^{+})=j,X(\tau_{0}^{-}\wedge\tau_{a}^{+})=a\,\mid\,J(0)=i,X(0)=u),

where
e_{q}
is an
\mbox{Exp}(q)
random variable independent of everything else; from now on, we adopt this reinterpretation.

In the existing literature, explicit solutions to
m^{+}_{ij}
and
m^{-}_{ij}
only exist in very simple scenarios: either
q=0
and
\mathcal{E}=\{1\}
(see e.g.
[
19
, Chapter 4]
), or
\mu_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}
are constant for all
i\in\mathcal{E}
(see e.g.
[
20
]
). Moreover, we are as well interested in the present value of the expected ocupation times in State
j\in\mathcal{E}
while below level
b\in(0,a)
, defined as

O_{ij}(u,q,a,b)=\mathds{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}^{-}\wedge\tau_{a}^{+}}e^{-qs}\mathds{1}\left\{J(s)=j,\,0<X(s)\leq b\right\}\mathrm{d}s\,\mid\,J(0)=i,X(0)=u\right).

Similarly to
m^{-}_{ij}
and
m^{+}_{ij}
, here we reinterpret
O_{ij}
as

O_{ij}(u,q,a,b)=\mathds{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}^{-}\wedge\tau_{a}^{+}\wedge e_{q}}\mathds{1}\left\{J(s)=j,\,0<X(s)\leq b\right\}\mathrm{d}s\,\mid\,J(0)=i,X(0)=u\right).

In this section we propose to exploit the approximations laid out in Theorem
3.6
, along with the existing theory for multi-regime Markov-modulated Brownian motions, to provide an efficient approximation scheme based on computing

\widehat{m}^{-}_{ij}(u,q,a)=\mathds{P}(\widehat{\tau}^{-}_{0}\wedge\widehat{\tau}^{+}_{a}<e_{q},\widehat{J}(\widehat{\tau}^{-}_{0}\wedge\widehat{\tau}_{a}^{+})=j,\widehat{X}(\widehat{\tau}^{-}_{0}\wedge\widehat{\tau}_{a}^{+})=0\,\mid\,\widehat{J}(0)=i,\widehat{X}(0)=u),

\widehat{m}^{+}_{ij}(u,q,a)=\mathds{P}(\widehat{\tau}^{-}_{0}\wedge\widehat{\tau}^{+}_{a}<e_{q},\widehat{J}(\widehat{\tau}^{-}_{0}\wedge\widehat{\tau}_{a}^{+})=j,\widehat{X}(\widehat{\tau}^{-}_{0}\wedge\widehat{\tau}_{a}^{+})=a\,\mid\,\widehat{J}(0)=i,\widehat{X}(0)=u),

\widehat{O}_{ij}(u,q,a,b)=\mathds{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\widehat{\tau}_{0}^{-}\wedge\widehat{\tau}_{a}^{+}\wedge e_{q}}\mathds{1}\left\{\widehat{J}(s)=j,\,0<\widehat{X}(s)\leq b\right\}\mathrm{d}s\,\mid\,\widehat{J}(0)=i,\widehat{X}(0)=u\right),

where
\widehat{\tau}_{0}^{-}=\inf\{t>0:\widehat{X}(t)<0\}
and
\widehat{\tau}_{a}^{+}=\inf\{t>0:\widehat{X}(t)>a\}
. Indeed, the convergence in probability over increasing compact intervals of Theorem
3.6
yields the convergence in probability of

and

to
\tau_{0}^{-}
and

and

and
\widehat{m}^{+}_{ij}
to
{m}^{-}_{ij}
and
{m}^{+}_{ij}

.

While the functions

on the strip
[0,a]

]

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathds{P}(L(t)=\partial_{0},Y(t)=u).

•

the steady state probability distribution function while on State
j\in\mathcal{E}
over
(0,a)
, say
F_{j}
where

F_{j}(b)=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathds{P}(L(t)=j,0<Y(t)\leq b),\quad b\in(0,a).

Below we link these quantities with the first passage probabilities
\widehat{m}^{+}_{ij}
and
\widehat{m}^{-}_{ij}
, as well as with the expected occupation times
\widehat{O}_{ij}

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that
\mathds{E}(\widehat{\tau}_{0}^{-}\wedge\widehat{\tau}_{a}^{+}\wedge e_{q}\,\mid\,\widehat{J}(0)=i,\widehat{X}(0)=u)<\infty
. Then,

(4.1)

\widehat{m}^{-}_{ij}(u,q,a)=\frac{(\bm{p}_{-M})_{j}}{p_{0}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\widehat{m}^{+}_{ij}(u,q,a)=\frac{(\bm{p}_{M})_{j}}{p_{0}}.

Moreover,

(4.2)

\widehat{O}_{ij}(u,q,a,b)=\frac{F_{j}(b)}{p_{0}},\quad b\in(0,a).

Proof.

The process
(L,Y)
regenerates at the epochs
\{\nu_{4}^{\{\ell\}}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
, all of which have a (common) finite first moment. This in turn implies that
(L,Y)
is positive recurrent, so by
[
17
, Theorem 1]
,

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{M}\bm{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathds{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\nu_{4}^{\{1\}}}\mathds{1}\{Y(s)=u,L(s)\in\mathcal{E}\}\mathrm{d}s\right)}{\mathds{E}\left(\nu_{4}^{\{1\}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathds{P}(Y(\nu_{1}^{\{1\}})=u,L(\nu_{1}^{\{1\}})\in\mathcal{E})\mathds{E}\left(\rho_{0}^{\{1\}}\right)}{\mathds{E}\left(\nu_{4}^{\{1\}}\right)}=\frac{\widehat{m}^{+}(u,q,a)}{\mathds{E}\left(\nu_{4}^{\{1\}}\right)},

where we used that
\mathds{E}\left(\rho_{0}^{\{1\}}\right)=1
.
Employing similar arguments we get

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{-M}\bm{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\widehat{m}^{-}(u,q,a)}{\mathds{E}\left(\nu_{4}^{\{1\}}\right)}\quad\mbox{and}\quad p_{0}=\frac{1}{\mathds{E}\left(\nu_{4}^{\{1\}}\right)},

so that (
4.1
) follows. Additionally,

\displaystyle F_{j}(b)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathds{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\nu_{4}^{\{1\}}}\mathds{1}\{L(s)=j,\,0<Y(s)\leq b\}\mathrm{d}s\right)}{\mathds{E}\left(\nu_{4}^{\{1\}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathds{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\widehat{\tau}_{0}^{-}\wedge\widehat{\tau}_{a}^{+}\wedge e_{q}}\mathds{1}\left\{\widehat{J}(s)=j,\,0<\widehat{X}(s)\leq b\right\}\mathrm{d}s\right)}{\mathds{E}\left(\nu_{4}^{\{1\}}\right)}=\frac{\widehat{O}_{j,b}(u,q,a)}{\mathds{E}\left(\nu_{4}^{\{1\}}\right)},

which in turn implies (
4.2
).
∎

5.
Summary and extensions

Under Assumptions
1
-
5
, we considered the class of hybrid SDEs and their space-grid approximations, the latter belonging to a the family of multi-regime Markov-modulated Brownian motions. We provided a rigorous proof of their pathwise convergence, which holds in a probability sense uniformly over increasing compact intervals. As an application of our convergence result, we considered the challenging problem of identifying first passage probabilities and expected occupation times for solutions of hybrid SDEs, for which we suggest an approximate and computationally efficient answer which employs developments in the area of multi-regime Markov-modulated queues.

We point out that applications of our convergence result may also be used to approximate other (and possibly more complex) descriptors of hybrid SDEs. For instance, one can easily build hybrid SDEs with phase-type jumps by means of the fluidization method
[
8
]
. Other modifications and extensions which are straightforward to implement in our framework are the Omega model
[
2
,
15
]
, the Erlangian approximations for finite-time probabilities of ruin
[
7
]
, and Parisian ruin problems with phase-type clocks
[
10
]
.
