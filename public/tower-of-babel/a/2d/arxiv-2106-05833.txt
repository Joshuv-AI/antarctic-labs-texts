Incremental space-filling design based on coverings and spacings: improving upon low discrepancy sequences
A. Nogales Gómez, L. Pronzato, M.-J. Rendas

Abstract
The paper addresses the problem of defining families of ordered sequences { 𝐱 i } i ∈ ℕ \{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathds{N}} of elements of a compact subset 𝒳 {\mathscr{X}} of ℝ d \mathds{R}^{d} whose prefixes 𝐗 n = { 𝐱 i } i = 1 n \mathbf{X}_{n}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n} , for all orders n n , have good space-filling properties as measured by the dispersion (covering radius) criterion. Our ultimate aim is the definition of incremental algorithms that generate sequences 𝐗 n \mathbf{X}_{n} with small optimality gap, i.e., with a small increase in the maximum distance between points of 𝒳 {\mathscr{X}} and the elements of 𝐗 n \mathbf{X}_{n} with respect to the optimal solution 𝐗 n ⋆ \mathbf{X}_{n}^{\star} . The paper is a first step in this direction, presenting incremental design algorithms with proven optimality bound for one-parameter families of criteria based on coverings and spacings that both converge to dispersion for large values of their parameter. The examples presented show that the covering-based method outperforms state-of-the-art competitors, including coffee-house, suggesting that it inherits from its guaranteed 50% optimality gap. keywords Covering; Spacing; Submodularity; Greedy algorithm; Computer experiments; Space-filling design MSC 62K99, 65D99

Introduction and motivation

The paper discusses algorithmic constructions to define space-filling designs. Given
a compact subset
{\mathscr{X}}\subset\mathds{R}^{d}
, we say that a finite subset
\mathbf{Z}_{n}\subset{\mathscr{X}}
is a space-filling design if
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
fills
{\mathscr{X}}
evenly. Several mathematical definitions of this intuitive notion have been proposed in the literature, and we refer the interested reader to the reviews
[
28
,
30
]
and the books
[
6
]
,
[
35
, Chap. 5]
for a comprehensive presentation and discussion. Generically, a space-filling criterion is a set function, that maps (possibly finite) subsets of
{\mathscr{X}}
to
\mathbb{R}
.

In this work, the space-filling quality of a design
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
is measured through the distances from the domain points to their closest point in
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
, i.e., their distance to the design. Motivated by interpolation problems, the covering radius, equal to the maximum of these distances, is often considered to be the ultimate measure of the space-filling quality of
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
: good space-filling sequences should have small covering radius. In colloquial terms, “good designs leave no large holes in
{\mathscr{X}}
”.

The packing radius is in some sense a dual space-filling metric, assessing how well
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
is spread inside
{\mathscr{X}}
through the distance between its closest points. In terms of this criterion, a design is space-filling if it has a large packing radius. It is easy to see that the designs optimal for the covering radius are not optimal according to the packing radius: the latter must have some points in the boundary of
{\mathscr{X}}
(otherwise a larger packing radius would be obtained by expanding the design uniformly), while this clearly does not lead to minimal covering radius. Nevertheless, the packing radius is widely used as a space-filling criterion, given the much smaller numerical complexity involved in its computation when compared to the covering radius, and in the paper we also consider the optimization of the packing radius.

Both the covering and packing radii have important limitations as space-filling criteria, since they only reflect local characteristics. Indeed, they are generally determined by a few points of
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
, being insensitive to perturbations of the other points, with the consequence that many extensions of a given design may keep these indicators constant. Moreover, when
d
is large the numerical estimation of the covering radius is difficult and, as we shall see, very sensitive to the presence of vertices in
{\mathscr{X}}
. As already discussed in
[
26
]
, the
\alpha
-quantile of the distribution of the distance of a random point of
{\mathscr{X}}
to
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
, which we shall call the covering
\alpha
-quantile of the design, with
\alpha
close to 1, is a more stable space-filling indicator. For completeness, we will also compare designs according to this performance measure.

Most literature on space-filling design considers the construction of designs of a given target size. We are instead interested in the more complex situation where the number of design points that will effectively be used,
n\leq n_{\max}
, is to be decided in a later step, and our goal is to specify an ordered design
\mathbf{X}_{n_{\max}}
such that all prefix designs
\mathbf{X}_{n}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
n\leq n_{\max}
, have good space-filling properties. Such a situation typically occurs in the construction of the initial design on which the metamodel subsequently used in a computer experiment will be identified. In this context, performance is no longer assessed through a single scalar, but rather by the trajectory of criterion values for increasing design sizes.

Background.

Optimization of the covering radius is a highly non-linear (the cost function involves a maximum) and inherently multi-dimensional (the search space has dimension
n\times d
) combinatorial problem. Actually, the problem is known to be NP-hard, see
[
16
, p. 414]
, meaning that except for toy-problems we can only hope to find tractable algorithms that produce reasonably good solutions. In these circumstances, it is important to know how far the solutions found by a given algorithm can be from optimality, commonly designated in algorithmics by optimality gap. The definition of algorithms with guaranteed optimality gap for NP-hard problems is an active research topic which has produced important achievements for many combinatorial optimization problems, and our work is a contribution in this sense.

Algorithms which define the solution by incrementally adding a point at a time are specially interesting given their more manageable complexity: at each iteration an optimization problem in
{\mathscr{X}}
, i.e., in only
d
variables, needs to be solved. It is a well known — and easily verified — fact that besides additive cost functions, for which the optimal solution can be found by greedily appending the point in
{\mathscr{X}}
that produces the largest improvement, the optimality gap of greedy algorithms can be bounded if the criterion optimized is a submodular set function (this notion will be made precise below). Unfortunately, neither the covering radius nor the packing radius are submodular set functions.

Claims and hint of the contents.

In this paper, we present a parameterized submodular design criterion, based of the cumulative distribution function (c.d.f.) of the distance of a random point to the design, which is asymptotically (for large values of its parameter) related to the covering radius, submodularity implying that its greedy solutions have a bounded optimality gap. We show numerically that the performance of the designs produced is robust with respect to the precise tuning parameters of the criterion. Interestingly, our construction is free from knowledge of the detailed geometry of the domain
{\mathscr{X}}
, requiring only the specification of two (possibly coincident) sets: a set of eligible design points and a set of points used to compute the c.d.f., which must finely cover
{\mathscr{X}}
.

Despite its simplicity, the incremental construction of designs by greedy maximization of the packing radius, sometimes called coffee-house design in the literature
[
22
,
23
]
, ideally ensures an optimality gap of 50% for the covering radius
[
10
]
. Practical constructions use a finite set of candidates
{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
at each iteration, and the 50% guarantee above holds for
{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
only. As explained in the paper, the quality of the resulting design heavily depends on the imbedding of
{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
in
{\mathscr{X}}
. We show that a slight modification of the original coffee-house algorithm based on the notion of spacings, which keeps the design points away from the boundary of
{\mathscr{X}}
with the aim of reducing the covering radius, improves robustness with respect to the choice of
{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
.

Our numerical studies indicate that the greedy optimization of our c.d.f.-based criterion yields design with better covering properties than all alternative techniques tested: low-discrepancy sequences (LDS), variants of coffee-house design, and incremental constructions based on two other relaxed versions of the covering radius

The covering radius

\displaystyle\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=\CR_{[{\mathscr{X}}]}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=\max_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}}d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Z}_{n})\,.

It is called dispersion in the theory of quasi-Monte Carlo methods
[
25

and coincides with the minimax-distance, or fill criterion, used in computer experiments and function interpolation; see
[
5
,
14
,
28
,
30
]
.
When the design objective is to ensure an accurate prediction of the values of an unknown function

over all

\mathbf{x}

there always exists a
\mathbf{z}_{i}
at proximity of
\mathbf{x}
where

has been evaluated (see, e.g.,
[
36

Let
{\mathscr{X}}_{N}=\{\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\mathbf{x}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(N)}\}
be a finite subset of
{\mathscr{X}}
, with
N\gg n
, that is well spread over
{\mathscr{X}}
; it may be a regular grid when

) the first

points of a LDS in

. With a slight abuse of notation, we assume that
{\mathscr{X}}_{N}
and

do not necessarily share any elements when

. Note that the value

\displaystyle\CR_{[{\mathscr{X}}_{N}]}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=\max_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}_{N}}d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Z}_{n})

(1)

underestimates

. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\CR_{[{\mathscr{X}}_{N}]}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})\leq\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})\leq\max_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}}\min_{\mathbf{z}_{i}\in\mathbf{Z}_{n},\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\in{\mathscr{X}}_{N}}\left(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\|+\|\mathbf{x}^{(j)}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\|\right)\leq\CR_{[{\mathscr{X}}_{N}]}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})+\CR({\mathscr{X}}_{N})\,.

(2)

In the appendix we give an upper bound on

is the prefix of a LDS.

The packing radius (also designated by separating radius, or maximin-distance)

\displaystyle\PR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=\min_{\mathbf{z}_{i}\neq\mathbf{z}_{j}\in\mathbf{Z}_{n}}\frac{1}{2}\,\|\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{z}_{j}\|\ (n\geq 2)\,,

is also often used as a space-filling characteristic of a design
\mathbf{Z}_{n}

[
5
,
14
,
28
,
30
]
, in particular due to the simplicity of its calculation when compared to
\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
. Good designs should have a large packing radius. Notice, however, that
\PR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
is an even more local space-filling characteristic of

than
\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})

of
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
to make it coincide with another

sets

to zero whereas
\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
only increases to
\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{z}_{i}\})

We reserve the notation
\mathbf{X}_{n}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}]
to designs that are constructed incrementally, our objective being that all prefix designs
\mathbf{X}_{m}

A special notation is used for the Sobol’ and Halton sequences, with

and
\mathbf{H}_{n,d}
respectively denoting their first

When using Gaussian process modeling and kriging, the number of design points required for a given prediction accuracy can be related to the correlation length of the model

. However, this requires prior knowledge on the behavior of the function to be approximated, or that preliminary experimentation be performed with an initial design. When this initial design is suitably ordered, function evaluations can be interrupted when the confidence in the identified model is deemed sufficient to proceed to a second design phase, where complementary design points are added to improve the precision of the model predictions. In this situation, the size

of the initial design is small, much smaller than

when

Interest in incremental constructions brings an important constraint to the optimization problem, since designs with minimum covering radius are not nested. The consequences are well known in the large

Performance guarantee for submodular function maximization

This section recalls the notion of submodular set functions and a well known theorem establishing, when they are non-decreasing, bounds on the optimality gap of their greedy maximization.

2.1
Submodularity and the greedy algorithm

Let
{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
be a finite candidate set with
C=|{\mathscr{X}}_{C}|
elements and denote by
2^{{\mathscr{X}}_{C}}
its power set. A (scalar real valued) set-function is an application of
2^{{\mathscr{X}}_{C}}
on
\mathds{R}
. Space-filling criteria are set-functions. We say that a set-function
f
is non-decreasing when
f(\mathbf{A}\cup\{\mathbf{x}\})\geq f(\mathbf{A})
for any
\mathbf{A}\subset{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
and any
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
.

Definition 1

(Submodular function)

A set-function
f:2^{{\mathscr{X}}_{C}}\rightarrow\mathds{R}
is submodular if and only if it satisfies the following three equivalent conditions, see e.g.,
[
1
]
.

\displaystyle(a)\quad\quad

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{A})+f(\mathbf{B})\geq f(\mathbf{A}\cup\mathbf{B})+f(\mathbf{A}\cap\mathbf{B}),\qquad\forall\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in 2^{{\mathscr{X}}_{C}}\,;

\displaystyle(b)\quad\quad

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{A}\cup\{\mathbf{x}\})-f(\mathbf{A})\geq f(\mathbf{B}\cup\{\mathbf{x}\})-f(\mathbf{B}),\qquad\forall\mathbf{A}\subset\mathbf{B}\in 2^{{\mathscr{X}}_{C}},\ \mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}_{C}\setminus\mathbf{B}\,;

(3)

\displaystyle(c)\quad\quad

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{A}\cup\{\mathbf{x}\})-f(\mathbf{A})\geq f(\mathbf{A}\cup\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\})-f(\mathbf{A}\cup\{\mathbf{y}\}),\qquad\forall\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in 2^{{\mathscr{X}}_{C}},\ \mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in{\mathscr{X}}_{C}\setminus\mathbf{A}\,.

Inequality (
3
) is known as the
diminishing returns
property, stating that the increment resulting of the addition of an element
\mathbf{x}
to a set is a decreasing function of the set to which it is added.

The problem of maximizing a set-function is in general NP-hard, its exact resolution requiring the evaluation of
f
over all the
2^{C}
elements of the power set, which is infeasible except in trivial cases of little practical interest. In this paper we concentrate on the greedy (one-step-ahead) algorithm below to efficiently find approximate maximizers of size
k
of a given set-function
f
.

Algorithm 1

(Greedy Algorithm)

1:

set
\mathbf{X}=\emptyset

2:

while

|\mathbf{X}|<k

do

3:

find
\mathbf{x}
in
{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
such that
f(\mathbf{X}\cup\{\mathbf{x}\})
is maximal

4:

\mathbf{X}\leftarrow\mathbf{X}\cup\{\mathbf{x}\}

5:

end

while

6:

return

\mathbf{X}

When several solutions exist at Step 3, a single one is selected (e.g. randomly).
In spite of its simplicity, under mild conditions on
f
Algorithm 1 can be fairly efficient when applied to a non-decreasing submodular function as stated by the following theorem
[
24
]
.

Theorem 1

Let
f
be a non-decreasing submodular set function, then, for any given
k
,
1\leq k\leq C
, Algorithm 1 returns a set
\mathbf{X}_{k}
with bounded optimality gap

\frac{f^{\star}_{k}-f(\mathbf{X}_{k})}{f^{\star}_{k}-f(\emptyset)}\leq(1-1/k)^{k}\leq 1/\mathsf{e}<0.3679\,,

(4)

where
f^{\star}_{k}=\max_{\mathbf{X}\subset{\mathscr{X}}_{C}:|\mathbf{X}|\leq k}f(\mathbf{X})
and
\mathsf{e}=\exp(1)
.

Note that (
4
) implies the efficiency bound

\displaystyle\frac{f(\mathbf{X}_{k})-f(\emptyset)}{f^{\star}_{k}-f(\emptyset)}\geq 1-\frac{1}{\mathsf{e}}>63.2\%\,,\ \ \forall k\geq 1\,.

(5)

2.2
The lazy-greedy algorithm

At each iteration of the greedy algorithm the maximum of
f(\mathbf{X}\cup\{\mathbf{x}\})
over all
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}_{C}\setminus\mathbf{X}
must be found. As presented in
[
21

can be further exploited to restrict actual evaluation of

to a proper subset of
{\mathscr{X}}_{C}\setminus\mathbf{X}
(i.e., strictly included in
{\mathscr{X}}_{C}\setminus\mathbf{X}

\mathcal{O}(k\,C)

Denote by
\delta_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{X}\cup\{\mathbf{x}\})-f(\mathbf{X})\geq 0

is added to
\mathbf{X}

\delta_{\mathbf{X}_{n}}(\mathbf{x})

By construction,
\mathbf{X}_{i}\subset\mathbf{X}_{n}
for all

is submodular, for all

\mathbf{x}

\mathbf{X}_{i}

\mathbf{X}_{0}=\emptyset

Covering measures

3.1
Definitions

For any positive scalar
r
, we define the covering measure
\Phi_{r}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
of
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
by

\Phi_{r}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=\frac{\mathrm{vol}\{{\mathscr{X}}\cap\left[\cup_{i=1}^{n}{\mathscr{B}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},r)\right]\}}{\mathrm{vol}({\mathscr{X}})}\,,

with
{\mathscr{B}}(\mathbf{z},r)
the closed ball with center
\mathbf{z}
and radius
r
. For a given
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
, consider also the function
r\in\mathds{R}^{+}\rightarrow F_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}(r)=\Phi_{r}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
.
F_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}
is non-decreasing and satisfies
F_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}(0)=0
and
F_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}(r)=1
for any
r\geq\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
. If
X
is distributed with the uniform probability measure
\mu
on
{\mathscr{X}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Prob}\left\{X\in\cup_{i=1}^{n}{\mathscr{B}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},r)\right\}=\mathrm{Prob}\{d(X,\mathbf{Z}_{n})\leq r\}=\int_{\{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}:\,d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Z}_{n})\leq r\}}\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})=F_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}(r)\,,

(6)

and
F_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}
is the c.d.f. of the random variable
d(X,\mathbf{Z}_{n})
, supported on
[0,\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})]
. We will call
F_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}
the distance c.d.f. and denote by
Q_{\alpha}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
its
\alpha
-quantile:

\displaystyle Q_{\alpha}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=\inf\{t:F_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}(t)\geq\alpha\}\,,

with
Q_{1}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
.
The set function
\mathbf{Z}_{n}\subset{\mathscr{X}}\rightarrow\Phi_{r}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
is non-decreasing and satisfies
\Phi_{r}(\emptyset)=0
. Moreover, for any
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}
, the difference
\Phi_{r}(\mathbf{Z}_{n}\cup\{\mathbf{x}\})-\Phi_{r}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
is non-increasing with respect to
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
, so that
\Phi_{r}
is submodular.

In
[
26
]
, the authors also define design criteria based on the distribution of distances to design points, in particular the
(1-\gamma)
-covering radius which corresponds to
Q_{1-\gamma}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
. Using approximations valid for large
d
(see Remark
2
), they investigate the properties of
Q_{1-\gamma}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
for different random designs, in particular designs formed by
n
points i.i.d. in
{\mathscr{C}}_{d}=[0,1]^{d}
with a beta distribution. In
[
27
]
, they consider full-factorial and
2^{d-1}
fractional factorial designs, also for large
d
. We follow a different route, considering the values of
F_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}(r)
taken at different
r
and exploiting the submodularity of
\Phi_{r}
to construct deterministic incremental designs
\mathbf{X}_{n}
which, thanks to Theorem
1
, have guaranteed efficiency for all
n
for the criterion considered. In contrast with
[
26
]
, our constructions are effective also for small
d
, as several examples will illustrate. In our numerical study, additionally to the covering radius, we will also assess performance through the covering
\alpha
-quantile
Q_{\alpha}
for
\alpha
close to 1.

3.2
A c.d.f.-based submodular covering criterion

Denote by
f_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}
the probability density function (p.d.f.) corresponding to
F_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}
. For any
B>0
,
q>-1
and
\mathbf{Z}_{n}\neq\emptyset
, define the integrated covering measure

\displaystyle I_{B,q}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{B}r^{q}\,F_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}(r)\,\mathrm{d}r

(7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q+1}\left\{B^{q+1}F_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}(B)-\int_{0}^{B}r^{q+1}\,f_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}(r)\,\mathrm{d}r\right\}\,,

(8)

and set
I_{B,q}(\emptyset)=0
.

The set function
I_{B,q}:\,\mathbf{Z}_{n}\rightarrow I_{B,q}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
is non-decreasing and submodular, and satisfies
I_{B,q}(\emptyset)=0
. As
I_{B,q}
satisfies the conditions of Theorem 1, its greedy maximization with Algorithm 1 provides sequences of incremental designs
\mathbf{X}_{n}
with a
I_{B,q}
-efficiency of at least 63.2% for any
n
, see (
5
).
Since
F_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}(B)=1
for any
B\geq\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
, maximizing
I_{B,q}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
with respect to
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
for
B\geq\mathrm{diam}({\mathscr{X}})
is equivalent to minimizing
\int_{0}^{B}r^{q+1}\,f_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}(r)\,\mathrm{d}r=\mathsf{E}_{n}\{R^{q+1}\}
in (
8
), where the random variable
R
has the p.d.f.
f_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}
and where

\displaystyle(\mathsf{E}_{n}\{R^{q+1}\})^{1/(q+1)}=E_{q+1}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})

(9)

is the
L^{q+1}
-mean quantization error induced by
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
, see
[
11
]
.
It satisfies

E_{q+1}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})\nearrow\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
as
q\rightarrow\infty
. For
B
and
q
large enough, maximizing
I_{B,q}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
should therefore provide designs with small values of
\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
; moreover, the greedy maximization of
I_{B,q}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
is equivalent to the greedy minimization of
E_{q+1}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
, which is proved in
[
20
]
to ensure that
E_{q+1}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
tends to zero at rate
n^{-1/d}
.
These observations motivate the investigations on the properties of
I_{B,q}
and its numerical implementation presented in the paper.

3.3
Numerical implementation

To evaluate
I_{B,q}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
we substitute the empirical c.d.f.
\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}
for
F_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}(r)
in (
7
), where
\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}
is obtained by replacing
\mu
in (
6
) by the uniform measure
\mu_{Q}
supported on a finite subset
{\mathscr{X}}_{Q}
of
{\mathscr{X}}
,

\displaystyle{\mathscr{X}}_{Q}=\{\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\mathbf{x}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(Q)}\}\,.

The set
{\mathscr{X}}_{Q}
must be well spread over
{\mathscr{X}}
; it may be a regular grid, or correspond to the first
Q
points of a LDS in
{\mathscr{X}}
.
The empirical distance c.d.f.
\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}
is based on the
Q
distances
d_{j}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=d(\mathbf{x}^{(j)},\mathbf{Z}_{n})
,
j=1,\ldots,Q
. Denoting by
\overline{d}_{j}
the truncated version of
d_{j}
,
\overline{d}_{j}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=\min\{d_{j}(\mathbf{Z}_{n}),B\}
,
j=1,\ldots,Q
, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{B,q}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})\approx\widehat{I}_{B,q}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=\frac{B^{q+1}}{q+1}-\frac{1}{Q(q+1)}\,\sum_{j=1}^{Q}\overline{d}_{j}^{q+1}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})\,.

(10)

Remark 1
(A connection with clustering)

When
B\geq\mathrm{diam}({\mathscr{X}})
(
B\geq\mathrm{diam}({\mathscr{X}})/2
when
{\mathscr{X}}
is convex and
\mathbf{x}_{1}
is its Chebyshev center), the greedy maximization of
I_{B,q}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
,
q>-1
, selects

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{n+1}\in\mathrm{Arg}\max_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}}\int_{{\mathscr{C}}(\mathbf{x})}\left[d^{q+1}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{X}_{n})-\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\|^{q+1}\right]\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z})

at iteration
n
, with
{\mathscr{C}}(\mathbf{x})
the cell with generator
\mathbf{x}
in the Voronoi partition of
{\mathscr{X}}
associated with
\mathbf{X}_{n}\cup\{\mathbf{x}\}
.

One may notice that (
10
) implies that, when
B
is large enough, the maximization of
\widehat{I}_{B,q}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
is equivalent to the center location problem
[
21
]
defined by the minimization of
\sum_{i}d_{j}^{q+1}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
. It also corresponds to the minimization of the
L^{q+1}
-mean quantization error induced by
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
, which, in this discrete setting where
\mu_{Q}
is substituted for
\mu
, can be solved by clustering algorithms, see
[
18
]
, including
kmeans
and variants based of the Chebyshev centers of the Voronoi cells defined by the elements of
\mathbf{Z}_{n}

[
28
]
.

\Box

Iterative update of the
d(\mathbf{x}^{(j)},\mathbf{X}_{n})
.

Consider the greedy maximization of
\widehat{I}_{B,q}
. The criterion
\widehat{I}_{B,q}(\mathbf{X}_{n}\cup\{\mathbf{x}\})
optimized at step
n
of the incremental construction involves the distances
d_{j}(\mathbf{X}_{n}\cup\{\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\})
for the current design
\mathbf{X}_{n}
and all
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\in{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
, the set of eligible design points. The
Q\times C
matrix
\mathbf{D}_{n}
with elements
\{\mathbf{D}_{n}\}_{j,k}=d_{j}(\mathbf{X}_{n}\cup\{\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\})
,
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\in{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
, can be computed recursively as follows. At initialization of Algorithm 1,
\{\mathbf{D}_{0}\}_{j,k}
is the
Q\times C
matrix of inter-distances between the points of
{\mathscr{X}}_{Q}
and those of
{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
. Then, when
\mathbf{X}_{n+1}=\mathbf{X}_{n}\cup\{\mathbf{x}^{(k^{\prime})}\}
for some
\mathbf{x}^{(k^{\prime})}\in{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
, each column
\{\mathbf{D}_{n+1}\}_{\cdot,\ell}
of
\mathbf{D}_{n+1}
is given by
\min\{\{\mathbf{D}_{n}\}_{\cdot,\ell},\{\mathbf{D}_{0}\}_{\cdot,k^{\prime}}\}
, where the minimum is taken element-wise, for
\ell=1,\ldots,C
.
In practice, it is computationally more efficient to calculate
\mathbf{D}_{0}^{(q+1)}
and directly update
\mathbf{D}_{n}^{(q+1)}
, with
\{\mathbf{D}_{n}^{(q+1)}\}_{j,k}=[d_{j}(\mathbf{X}_{n}\cup\{\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\})]^{q+1}
for any
n\geq 0
.

The complexity of the evaluation of
\widehat{I}_{B,q}(\mathbf{X}_{n}\cup\{\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\})
for an
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\in{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
is of order
\mathcal{O}(Q)
. The complexity of the greedy maximization of
\widehat{I}_{B,q}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
is thus of order
\mathcal{O}((\kappa+n)\,CQ)
, where
\kappa\,CQ
counts for the initial calculation of
\mathbf{D}_{0}^{(q+1)}
; it becomes
\mathcal{O}((\kappa+n\,\overline{\gamma}_{n})\,CQ)
for the lazy-greedy algorithm, with
\overline{\gamma}_{n}\ll 1
, see Section
2.2
.

Remark 2
(Large
d
approximation)

Using an Edgeworth expansion of the Central Limit Theorem, in
[
40
]
the authors derive approximations of
F_{\mathbf{x}}(r)
that are valid for large
d
, with
F_{\mathbf{x}}
the distance c.d.f. for the one-point design
\{\mathbf{x}\}
. For
X
distributed with the uniform probability measure on
{\mathscr{X}}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
a random design with
n
i.i.d. elements
\mathbf{z}_{i}
, we have
F_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}(r)=\mathrm{Prob}\{d(X,\mathbf{Z}_{n})\leq r\}=1-\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[1-F_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}(r)\right]
; this is also exploited in
[
26
]
. However, the approximation of
F_{\mathbf{x}}
is not directly exploitable for the estimation of
I_{B,q}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})

for a given

\mathbf{Z}_{n}
since in that case
F_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}(r)\neq 1-\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[1-F_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}(r)\right]
.

Remark 3
(Initialization)

When one is not interested in the performance of the smallest designs, with
n
less than some
n_{\min}
, it seems preferable to directly choose the first
n_{\min}
points by minimizing
\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n_{\min}})
, and initialize the greedy algorithm at this
n_{\min}
-point design. The batch optimization of
\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n_{\min}})
is a difficult task, but several methods available are able to produce designs with reasonably good performance; see, e.g.,
[
28
]
. The investigation of properties and performance of incremental constructions initialized in this way is not considered in the present paper, where we consider the entire nested sequence, starting at
n=1
. This will be the subject of future studies, together with backwards constructions that start with a nearly optimal
n_{\max}
-point design and iteratively eliminate design points until an
n_{\min}
-points design is reached, for some
n_{\min}<n_{\max}
.

Choice of

Large enough values of

for

, guaranteeing that

for any
\mathbf{Z}_{n}

of the order of magnitude of the largest possible value of

over the range

of design sizes of interest, i.e.,
\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n_{\min}})
.

When

and

Remark 4
(Truncation of

For

Remark 5
(A positive lower integration limit in
I_{B,q}
)

We discussed above the possibility of restricting the largest distances taken into account in
I_{B,q}
. We can also think of restricting the contribution of the smallest distances by using a strictly positive lower integration limit
b
. The criterion is then more focussed on the influence of points of
{\mathscr{X}}_{Q}
at large distances from the design. Parameter
b
must be taken larger than the smallest covering radius for the design sizes of interest, and we suggest to relate
b
to a lower bound on
\CR_{n_{\max}}^{\star}
. Consider designs of size
n
in
{\mathscr{X}}={\mathscr{C}}_{d}
. Since the
n

centered at the optimal design points cover
{\mathscr{X}}
,

, with
V_{d}
the volume of the
d
-dimensional unit ball
{\mathscr{B}}(\mathbf{0},1)
,
V_{d}=\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2+1)
.
This implies the following classical lower bound
[
25
, p. 150]
:

\displaystyle\CR_{n}^{\star}\geq R_{\star}(n,d)=(nV_{d})^{-1/d}\,.

(12)

We may note that
V_{d}
is maximum for
d=5
(with
V_{5}\simeq 5.2638
) and that
n^{1/d}\,R_{\star}(n,d)=\sqrt{d}/\sqrt{2\,\pi\,\mathsf{e}}\,+\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{d})
,
d\rightarrow\infty
.
A reasonable choice is then
b=R_{\star}(n_{\max},d)
.
In practice, our numerical investigations indicate that this modification has marginal influence on performance, although it may slightly simplify the computations since the summation in (
10
) involves a smaller number of terms. It will not be used in the numerical experiments of Section
5
.

Choices of

The problem considered may impose constraints on the set

by

. Our numerical experiments suggest using
{\mathscr{X}}_{C}\subset{\mathscr{X}}_{Q}
with the rule of thumb

4
Spacings and coffee-house design

Coffee-house design.

The algorithmic construction presented in the previous section is based on the distance c.d.f.
F_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}
.
In this section we present a second family of incremental algorithms, directly related to the most common geometrical criteria, the covering and packing radii. Even if these criteria are not submodular, and thus Theorem
1
cannot be invoked to guarantee a lower bound on the efficiency of their greedy optimization, an efficiency of 50% can still be guaranteed.

Coffee-house designs
[
22
]
,
[
23
, Chap. 4]
greedily maximize
\PR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
for
n>1
; see
[
15
]
for an early suggestion. When
{\mathscr{X}}
is convex, the first design point
\mathbf{x}_{1}
is usually chosen at the Chebyshev center of
{\mathscr{X}}
(the center of the minimal-radius ball enclosing
{\mathscr{X}}
); then, at any
n\geq 1
,
\mathbf{x}_{n+1}\in\mathrm{Arg}\max_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}}d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{X}_{n})
. Denote by
\PR_{n}^{\star}
the optimal (maximum) value of
\PR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
for a
n
-point design in
{\mathscr{X}}
. The two ratios
\CR_{n}^{\star}/\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
and
\PR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})/\PR_{n}^{\star}
are less than one by construction and quantify the efficiency of a design
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
in terms of its covering and packing radii.
It is remarkable that the simple greedy coffee-house construction ensures the following property (irrespectively of the choice made for
\mathbf{x}_{1}
):

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\leq\frac{\CR_{n}^{\star}}{\CR(\mathbf{X}_{n})}\leq 1\ (n\geq 1)\ \ \mbox{ and }\ \ \frac{1}{2}\leq\frac{\PR(\mathbf{X}_{n})}{\PR_{n}^{\star}}\leq 1\ (n\geq 2)\,.

(13)

Indeed, by construction
\PR(\mathbf{X}_{n+1})=d(\mathbf{x}_{n+1},\mathbf{X}_{n})/2=\CR(\mathbf{X}_{n})/2
for all
n\geq 1
.
Take any
n
-point design
\mathbf{Z}_{n}=\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{n}\}
in
{\mathscr{X}}
. From the pigeonhole principle, one of the balls
{\mathscr{B}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n}))
must contain two points
\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}
of
\mathbf{X}_{n+1}
, implying that
\PR(\mathbf{X}_{n+1})\leq\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|/2\leq\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
. Therefore,
\CR_{n}^{\star}\geq\PR(\mathbf{X}_{n+1})=\CR(\mathbf{X}_{n})/2
. Similarly,
\PR_{n+1}^{\star}\leq\CR_{n}^{\star}\leq\CR(\mathbf{X}_{n})=2\,\PR(\mathbf{X}_{n+1})
. The original proof is given in
[
10
]
.

As for the maximization of
I_{B,q}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
in Section
3
, the implementation of Algorithm 1 is much facilitated when

\mathbf{x}_{n+1}\in\mathrm{Arg}\max_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}_{C}}d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{X}_{n})
with
{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
a finite set of candidates. The efficiencies given in (
13
) remain valid provided that
\CR(\mathbf{X}_{n})
is approximated by
\CR_{[{\mathscr{X}}_{C}]}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
and that
\CR_{n}^{\star}
and
\PR_{n}^{\star}
are relative to optimal
n
-point designs in
{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
. Contrary to the maximization of
\widehat{I}_{B,q}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
in Section
3.3
, the greedy maximization of
\PR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
does not require computing a
Q\times C
matrix of inter-distances, but only the update of the
C
distances
d(\mathbf{x}^{(i)},\mathbf{Z}_{n})
for the
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
in
{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
. For that reason, the size
C
of the candidate set can be taken much larger than in Section
3.4
.

Denote by
\rho(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
the mesh-ratio of
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
, defined by

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=\frac{\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})}{\PR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})}\ (n\geq 2)\,.

If a sequence of
n
-points designs
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
is such that
\rho(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
is uniformly bounded, then the sequence is said to be quasi-uniform; see, e.g.,
[
4
]
.
The inverse of
\rho(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
is sometimes called uniformity measure
[
5
]
.
When
{\mathscr{X}}
is convex it cannot be covered by two or more non-overlapping balls having their centers in
{\mathscr{X}}
, and
\PR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})<\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
, implying that
\rho(\mathbf{Z}_{n})>1
. When
\mathbf{X}_{n}
is a coffee-house design,
\PR(\mathbf{X}_{n+1})=\CR(\mathbf{X}_{n})/2
for
n\geq 1
, and since
\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})\geq\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n+1})
for all
n
and any nested designs
\mathbf{Z}_{n}\subset\mathbf{Z}_{n+1}
,
\rho(\mathbf{X}_{n})
satisfies

\displaystyle 1\leq\rho(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq 2\ (n\geq 2)\,.

That
\rho(\mathbf{X}_{n})
is upper-bounded by 2 is a very strong guarantee of coffee-house design, which does not hold for our c.d.f.-based construction. This powerful property is certainly related to the bounded optimality gaps for both the covering and packing radii. The method presented in the previous section, tailored to control
\CR
only, is unable to guarantee a large spacing between design points, which can translate into large values
5
5

5

Values larger than 3 have been observed in Example 1 of Section
5
.
of
\rho(\mathbf{X}_{n})
.

Spacings and boundary-phobic coffee-house design.

By construction, a coffee-house design places design points on the boundary
\partial{\mathscr{X}}
of
{\mathscr{X}}
, in contradiction with the objective of constructing designs with low values of
\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
. For that reason, we consider below a modified method that forces design points to stay away from
\partial{\mathscr{X}}
.

Following
[
13
]
6
6

6

In
[
13
]
,
{\mathscr{X}}
is only assumed to be bounded and bounded convex sets other than
{\mathscr{B}}(\mathbf{0},1)
are also considered.
, we define the maximal spacing
S(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
as the radius of the largest ball contained in
{\mathscr{X}}
and not intersecting
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
,

\displaystyle S(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=\sup\left\{r:\exists\mathbf{x}\mbox{ such that }\mathbf{x}\oplus r\,{\mathscr{B}}(\mathbf{0},1)\subset{\mathscr{X}}\setminus\mathbf{Z}_{n}\right\}\,,

with
\oplus
denoting the Minkowski sum (for two subsets
{\mathscr{A}}_{1}
and
{\mathscr{A}}_{2}
of
\mathds{R}^{d}
,
{\mathscr{A}}_{1}\oplus{\mathscr{A}}_{2}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1}+\mathbf{x}_{2}:\,\mathbf{x}_{1}\in{\mathscr{A}}_{1},\,\mathbf{x}_{2}\in{\mathscr{A}}_{2}\}
.)
We slightly extend this notion by introducing a parameter
\beta
that controls the ratio between the distance to the design and the distance
7
7

7

When
{\mathscr{X}}={\mathscr{C}}_{d}
, an anonymous referee suggested to penalize differently distances to the vertices and distances to the boundary of
{\mathscr{C}}_{d}
(or more generally to the
d^{\prime}
-dimensional faces of
{\mathscr{C}}_{d}
,
1<d^{\prime}<d
), using different metrics; we have not explored this possibility here.
to
\partial{\mathscr{X}}
, and define the
\beta
-spacing of
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
, for
\beta>0
, as

\displaystyle S_{\beta}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=\sup\left\{r:\exists\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}\mbox{ such that }d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Z}_{n})\geq r\mbox{ and }d(\mathbf{x},\partial{\mathscr{X}})\geq\frac{r}{\beta}\right\}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}}D_{\beta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Z}_{n})\,,

where

\displaystyle D_{\beta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Z}_{n})=\min\left\{d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Z}_{n})\,,\ \beta\,d(\mathbf{x},\partial{\mathscr{X}})\right\}\,,\ \mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}\,,

(14)

and
d(\mathbf{x},\partial{\mathscr{X}})=\inf_{\mathbf{z}\in\partial{\mathscr{X}}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\|
. Figure
5
shows the variations of
D_{\beta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Z}_{n})
for a 4-point design
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
in the square
[0,1]^{2}
for two different values of
\beta
. We have
S_{1}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=S(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
and define
S_{\infty}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=\CR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
.
We also define

\displaystyle P_{\beta}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=\min_{\mathbf{z}_{i}\neq\mathbf{z}_{j}\in\mathbf{Z}_{n}}\frac{1}{2}\,\min\left\{\|\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{z}_{j}\|\,,\ \beta\,d(\mathbf{z}_{i},\partial{\mathscr{X}})\right\}\mbox{ and }\rho_{\beta}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=\frac{S_{\beta}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})}{P_{\beta}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})}\ (n\geq 2)\,,

(15)

and set
P_{\infty}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=\PR(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
, so that
\rho_{\infty}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=\rho(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
.

Boundary-phobic compromise distance
D_{\beta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Z}_{n})
for a 4-point design (yellow stars) in
{\mathscr{X}}=[0,1]^{2}
.

The coffee-house algorithm can be straightforwardly extended to the greedy maximization of
P_{\beta}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
,
\beta>0
, using
\mathbf{x}_{n+1}\in\mathrm{Arg}\max_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}}D_{\beta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{X}_{n})
for any
n\geq 1
(and if we use the convention
\mathbf{X}_{0}=\emptyset
and
d(\mathbf{x},\emptyset)=+\infty
for any
\mathbf{x}
, the same rule sets
\mathbf{x}_{1}
at the Chebyshev center of
{\mathscr{X}}
when
{\mathscr{X}}
is convex and to a point of the medial axis
[
3
]
at maximum distance from
\partial{\mathscr{X}}
in general).
Following the same steps as for coffee-house design, we obtain the following property, whose proof is given in the appendix.

Theorem 2

Let
{\mathscr{X}}
be a compact and convex subset of
\mathds{R}^{d}
and let
\mathbf{X}_{n}
be a design obtained with the following greedy construction:
\mathbf{X}_{0}=\emptyset
and
\mathbf{x}_{k+1}\in\mathrm{Arg}\max_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}}D_{\beta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{X}_{k})
,
k=0,1,\ldots,n-1
, where
D_{\beta}
is defined in (
14
),
\beta>0
, and
d(\mathbf{x},\emptyset)=+\infty
. Then
P_{\beta}(\mathbf{X}_{n+1})=(1/2)\,S_{\beta}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
for
n\geq 1
and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\leq\frac{S_{\beta,n}^{\star}}{S_{\beta}(\mathbf{X}_{n})}\leq 1\ (n\geq 1)\ \ \mbox{ and }\ \ \frac{1}{2}\leq\frac{P_{\beta}(\mathbf{X}_{n})}{P_{\beta,n}^{\star}}\leq 1\ \,,\ 1\leq\rho_{\beta}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq 2\ (n\geq 2)\,,

(16)

where
S_{\beta,n}^{\star}=\min_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}\subset{\mathscr{X}}}S_{\beta}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
and
P_{\beta,n}^{\star}=\max_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}\subset{\mathscr{X}}}P_{\beta}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
.

Algorithm 2 of
[
37
]
, also involving the domain boundary, corresponds to the greedy maximization of
P_{\beta}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
in
{\mathscr{X}}={\mathscr{C}}_{d}
, for the value
\beta=2\,\sqrt{2\,d}
(chosen in
[
37
]
by trial and error). Considering that the covering radius is of special importance, we recommend a different choice for
\beta
when
{\mathscr{X}}={\mathscr{C}}_{d}
. Since
\mathbf{x}_{1}=\mathbf{1}_{d}/2
, the center of
{\mathscr{X}}
, when
\beta=\infty
the second point
\mathbf{x}_{2}
coincides with a vertex of
{\mathscr{X}}
. We suggest to choose
\beta
depending on the maximum target design size
n_{\max}
, such that
\mathbf{x}_{2}
be at distance
R_{\star}(n_{\max},d)
from a vertex of
{\mathscr{X}}
, with
R_{\star}(n_{\max},d)
the lower bound on
\CR_{n_{\max}}^{\star}
given by (
12
).
This implies
d(\mathbf{x}_{2},\partial{\mathscr{X}})=R_{\star}(n_{\max},d)/\sqrt{d}=\|\mathbf{x}_{2}-\mathbf{x}_{1}\|/\beta
and gives

\displaystyle\beta=\beta_{\star}(n_{\max},d)=\frac{d}{2\,R_{\star}(n_{\max},d)}-\sqrt{d}\,.

(17)

The left panel of Figure
13
in the appendix shows
\beta_{\star}(n,d)
as a function of
d
for
n=50
(red
\bigstar
),
n=100
(blue
\triangledown
) and
n=200
(black
\circ
); the curve with magenta
\times
corresponds to the values suggested in
[
37
]
. We see that as
n
grows
\beta_{\star}(n,d)
increases (since we expect designs to better cover
{\mathscr{X}}
), and as
d
becomes large the value of
\beta_{\star}(n,d)
exhibits a very slow growth after an initial fast decay (being almost constant for large values of
n
).

5
Numerical study

This section assesses the performance of the design algorithms presented in the previous sections.
Designs are compared in terms of the covering radius
\CR(\mathbf{X}_{n})
and of the covering
\alpha
-quantile
Q_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
, with
\alpha=0.99
; we will show plots of the evolution of these criteria as the greedy constructions unfold.
We stress that for the incremental design methods studied in this paper, the entire evolution of the performance indicators over a target range of design sizes is of interest, and not just their value for a given final design size. Since
\CR(\mathbf{X}_{n})
and
Q_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
ideally decrease as
n^{-1/d}
all our plots will show normalized versions,

\CR(\mathbf{X}_{n})/R_{\star}(n,d)
, see (
12
), and
n^{1/d}\,Q_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
. An ideal design method should lead to trajectories close to an horizontal line at a small value.

We compare the performance of designs
\mathbf{X}_{n}^{\star}
that incrementally maximize
\widehat{I}_{B,q}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
to three families of constructions: (
i
) prefixes of low discrepancy Halton (
\mathbf{H}_{n,d}
) and Sobol’ (
\mathbf{S}_{n,d}
) sequences; (
ii
)
\mathbf{X}_{n}^{CH,\beta}
obtained by greedy maximization of
P_{\beta}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
given by (
15
), with
\beta\in\{\beta_{\star}(n_{\max},d),\,2\sqrt{2},\,\infty\}
; and (
iii
) two incremental constructions (designs
\mathbf{X}_{n}^{VD}
and
\mathbf{X}_{n}^{RD}
) based on a direct relaxation of the covering radius recently proposed in
[
32
]
, see precise definitions in the appendix.

Two case-studies are considered. In Example 1 the design space has a simple geometry embedded in a relatively large dimensional space,
{\mathscr{X}}={\mathscr{C}}_{10}=[0,1]^{10}
, while
Example 2 considers a more challenging topology involving a non-convex (annular) domain
{\mathscr{X}}\subset\mathds{R}^{2}
. In both examples, the parameters of
\widehat{I}_{B,q}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
are

and
q=10
(we also use

for

6
Conclusions

This paper proposes a novel space-filling c.d.f.-based criterion
I_{B,q}
tailored to the minimization of the covering radius. This set function satisfies the conditions of the celebrated Nemhauser’s Theorem on the greedy maximization of submodular functions, thereby opening the possibility to challenge the simple but 50%-efficient coffee-house design methodology. Although the performance guarantees provided by the submodularity of
I_{B,q}
do not translate into performance guarantees for the covering radius, we verified experimentally, over a larger set of case studies than those presented in the paper, that the design sequences obtained by its greedy maximization have smaller
\CR
values than
competing state-of-the-art incremental design methods. The fact that it almost always outperforms coffee-house which has a guaranteed 50% efficiency suggests that an even stronger guarantee may hold for our method.

By fully taking into account how the points of
\mathbf{X}_{n}
are collectively embedded in the domain
{\mathscr{X}}
,
I_{B,q}
overcomes the limitations due to the locality of
\CR(\mathbf{X}_{n})
mentioned in the introduction. It involves a regularization parameter
q
and a range parameter
B
, whose precise values do not significantly impact its ability to express the space-fillingness of a design. The method also requires the specification of a set of points
{\mathscr{X}}_{Q}
to compute the distance c.d.f. and of a candidate set
{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
of eligible design points. For finite
q
, the method greedily minimizes the
L^{q+1}
-mean quantization error evaluated on
{\mathscr{X}}_{Q}
, which, when
B=\mathrm{diam}({\mathscr{X}})
and
q\to\infty
, tends to an underestimate of
\CR(\mathbf{X}_{n})
(since based on
{\mathscr{X}}_{Q}
only). We verified that setting
q\in[5,25]
and
B=\mathrm{diam}({\mathscr{X}})
yields good designs in a wide variety of situations. The choice of
{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
is not critical, even in the challenging situation where
{\mathscr{X}}
is the hypercube
{\mathscr{C}}_{d}
, for which the presence of vertices is easily problematic for large
d
: the method never tries to select points near the vertices and, more generally, is not sensitive to the proximity of points of
{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
to the boundary of
{\mathscr{X}}
.

The coffee-house algorithm only requires the specification of a candidate set
{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
and runs much faster. However, when
{\mathscr{X}}={\mathscr{C}}_{d}
it naturally tends to select points as near as possible to the vertices of
{\mathscr{X}}
, thereby exhibiting a lack of robustness to the choice of
{\mathscr{X}}_{C}
. Boundary avoidance can be enforced through the notion of spacings, resulting in simple and efficient algorithms when
{\mathscr{X}}
has a simple enough geometry.

Overall, the geometry of
{\mathscr{X}}
appears as being a key factor, including in terms of the relevance of
\CR
to measure the space-filling quality of a design. We thus concur with the authors of
[
26
]
on the importance of using a more global characteristic like the covering
\alpha
-quantile
Q_{\alpha}
. Incremental design constructed with
I_{B,q}
are also competitive in terms of
Q_{\alpha}
compared to other incremental design methods.

Note finally that the development of greedy algorithms for the minimization of
Q_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
itself forms another challenging but promising objective towards the incremental construction of efficient space-filling designs, in particular in the hypercube
{\mathscr{C}}_{d}
.
