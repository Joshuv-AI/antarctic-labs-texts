Provably Accelerating Ill-Conditioned Low-rank Estimation via Scaled Gradient Descent, Even with Overparameterization
Cong Ma, Xingyu Xu, Tian Tong, and Yuejie Chi

Abstract
Many problems encountered in science and engineering can be formulated as estimating a low-rank object (e.g., matrices and tensors) from incomplete, and possibly corrupted, linear measurements. Through the lens of matrix and tensor factorization, one of the most popular approaches is to employ simple iterative algorithms such as gradient descent (GD) to recover the low-rank factors directly, which allow for small memory and computation footprints. However, the convergence rate of GD depends linearly, and sometimes even quadratically, on the condition number of the low-rank object, and therefore, GD slows down painstakingly when the problem is ill-conditioned. This chapter introduces a new algorithmic approach, dubbed scaled gradient descent (ScaledGD), that provably converges linearly at a constant rate independent of the condition number of the low-rank object, while maintaining the low per-iteration cost of gradient descent for a variety of tasks including sensing, robust principal component analysis and completion. In addition, ScaledGD continues to admit fast global convergence to the minimax-optimal solution, again almost independent of the condition number, from a small random initialization when the rank is over-specified in the presence of Gaussian noise. In total, ScaledGD highlights the power of appropriate preconditioning in accelerating nonconvex statistical estimation, where the iteration-varying preconditioners promote desirable invariance properties of the trajectory with respect to the symmetry in low-rank factorization without hurting generalization.



1
Introduction

Low-rank matrix and tensor estimation plays a critical role in fields such as machine learning, signal processing, imaging science, and many others. The central task can be regarded as recovering an
d
-dimensional object
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}
from its highly incomplete observation
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
given by

\displaystyle\bm{y}\approx\mathcal{A}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}).

Here,
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{m}
represents a certain linear map modeling the data collection process. Importantly, the number
m
of observations is often much smaller than the ambient dimension
\prod_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}
of the data object due to resource or physical constraints, necessitating the need of exploiting low-rank structures to allow for meaningful recovery.
It is natural to minimize the least-squares loss function

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}}\;f(\bm{\mathcal{X}})\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{A}(\bm{\mathcal{X}})-\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}

(1)

subject to some rank constraint. However, naively imposing the rank constraint is computationally intractable, and moreover, as the size of the object increases, the costs involved in optimizing over the full space (i.e.,
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}
) are prohibitive in terms of both memory and computation.

To cope with these challenges, one popular approach is to represent the object of interest via its low-rank factors, which take a more economical form, and then optimize over the factors instead.
Although this leads to a nonconvex optimization problem over the factors, recent breakthroughs have shown that simple iterative algorithms such as vanilla gradient descent (GD), when properly initialized (e.g., via the spectral method), can provably converge to the true low-rank factors under mild statistical assumptions; see
chi2019nonconvex
for an overview. This enables us to tap into the scalability of gradient descent in solving large-scale problems due to its amenability to computing advances such as parallelism

bottou2018optimization
.

However, upon closer examination, the computational cost of vanilla gradient descent is still expensive, especially for ill-conditioned objects. Although the per-iteration cost is small, the iteration complexity of gradient descent scales linearly with respect to the condition number of the low-rank matrix
tu2015low
, which degenerates even worse for higher-order tensors
han2020optimal
.
In fact, the issue of ill-conditioning is quite ubiquitous in real-world data modeling with many contributing factors. One one end, extracting fine-grained and weak information often manifests to estimating ill-conditioned object of interest, when the goals are to separate close-located sources of intelligence, to identify a weak mode nearby a strong one, to predict individualized responses for similar objects, and so on. While the impact of condition numbers on the computational efficacy cannot be ignored in practice, it unfortunately has not been properly addressed in recent algorithmic advances, which often assume the problem is well-conditioned.
These together raise an important question:

{svgraybox}

Is it possible to design a first-order algorithm with a comparable per-iteration cost as gradient descent, but converges much faster at a rate that is independent of the condition number in a provable manner for a wide variety of low-rank matrix and tensor estimation tasks?

1.1
An overview of ScaledGD

In this chapter, we answer this question affirmatively by setting forth an algorithmic approach dubbed scaled gradient descent (
ScaledGD
), which is instantiated for an array of low-rank matrix and tensor estimation tasks.

\runinhead

ScaledGD for low-rank matrix estimation.
By parametrizing the matrix object
\bm{X}=\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\top}
via two low-rank factors
\bm{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times r}
and
\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times r}
in (
1
), where
r
is the rank of the true low-rank object
\bm{X}_{\star}
, we arrive at the objective function

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}_{\bm{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times r},\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times r}}\;\mathcal{L}(\bm{L},\bm{R})\coloneqq f(\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\top}).

(2)

Given an initialization
(\bm{L}_{0},\bm{R}_{0})
,
ScaledGD
proceeds as follows

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{L}_{t+1}&=\bm{L}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\bm{L}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{L}_{t},\bm{R}_{t})(\bm{R}_{t}^{\top}\bm{R}_{t})^{-1},\\
\bm{R}_{t+1}&=\bm{R}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\bm{R}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{L}_{t},\bm{R}_{t})(\bm{L}_{t}^{\top}\bm{L}_{t})^{-1},\end{split}

(3)

where
\eta>0
is the step size and
\nabla_{\bm{L}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{L}_{t},\bm{R}_{t})
(resp.
\nabla_{\bm{R}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{L}_{t},\bm{R}_{t})
) is the gradient of the loss function
\mathcal{L}
with respect to the factor
\bm{L}_{t}
(resp.
\bm{R}_{t}
) at the
t
-th iteration. Comparing to vanilla gradient descent, the search directions of the low-rank factors
\bm{L}_{t},\bm{R}_{t}
in (
3
) are
scaled
by
(\bm{R}_{t}^{\top}\bm{R}_{t})^{-1}
and
(\bm{L}_{t}^{\top}\bm{L}_{t})^{-1}
respectively.
Intuitively, the scaling serves as a preconditioner as in quasi-Newton type algorithms, with the hope of improving the quality of the search direction to allow larger step sizes. Since computing the Hessian is extremely expensive, it is necessary to design preconditioners that are both theoretically sound and practically cheap to compute. Such requirements are met by
ScaledGD
, where the preconditioners are computed by inverting two
r\times r
matrices, whose size is much smaller than the dimension of matrix factors.
Theoretically, we confirm that
ScaledGD
achieves linear convergence at a rate
independent of
the condition number of the matrix when initialized properly, e.g., using the standard spectral method, for several canonical problems: low-rank matrix sensing, robust PCA, and matrix completion.

(4)

Given an initialization
\bm{F}_{0}=(\bm{U}_{0},\bm{V}_{0},\bm{W}_{0},\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{0})

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{t+1}

\displaystyle\bm{V}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\bm{V}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\bm{V}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{F}_{t})\big(\breve{\bm{V}}_{t}^{\top}\breve{\bm{V}}_{t}\big)^{-1},

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\bm{W}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\bm{W}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{F}_{t})\big(\breve{\bm{W}}_{t}^{\top}\breve{\bm{W}}_{t}\big)^{-1},

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{t}-\eta\left((\bm{U}_{t}^{\top}\bm{U}_{t})^{-1},(\bm{V}_{t}^{\top}\bm{V}_{t})^{-1},(\bm{W}_{t}^{\top}\bm{W}_{t})^{-1}\right)\bcdot\nabla_{\bm{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{F}_{t}),

(5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\breve{\bm{U}}_{t}&\coloneqq\mathcal{M}_{1}\left((\bm{I}_{r_{1}},\bm{V}_{t},\bm{W}_{t})\bcdot\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{t}\right)^{\top}=(\bm{W}_{t}\otimes\bm{V}_{t})\mathcal{M}_{1}(\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{t})^{\top},\\
\breve{\bm{V}}_{t}&\coloneqq\mathcal{M}_{2}\left((\bm{U}_{t},\bm{I}_{r_{2}},\bm{W}_{t})\bcdot\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{t}\right)^{\top}=(\bm{W}_{t}\otimes\bm{U}_{t})\mathcal{M}_{2}(\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{t})^{\top},\\

(6)

Clearly, the iteration complexity of GD deteriorates at a super linear rate with respect to the condition number
\kappa
, while
ScaledGD
enjoys an iteration complexity that is independent of
\kappa
as predicted by our theory. Indeed, with a seemingly small modification,
ScaledGD
takes merely
17
iterations to achieve the desired accuracy over the entire range of
\kappa
, while GD takes thousands of iterations even with a moderate condition number!

To highlight,
ScaledGD
possesses many desirable properties appealing to practitioners.

•

Low per-iteration cost:
as a preconditioned GD or quasi-Newton algorithm,
ScaledGD
updates the factors along the descent direction of a scaled gradient, where the preconditioners can be viewed as the inverse of the diagonal blocks of the Hessian for the population loss (i.e., matrix factorization and tensor factorization). As the sizes of the preconditioners are proportional to the rank rather than the ambient dimension, the matrix inverses are cheap to compute with a minimal overhead and the overall per-iteration cost is still low and linear in the time it takes to read the input data.

•

Equivariance to parameterization:
one crucial property of
ScaledGD
is that if we reparameterize the factors by some invertible transformation, the entire trajectory will go through the same reparameterization, leading to an
invariant
sequence of low-rank updates regardless of the parameterization being adopted.

•

Implicit balancing:

ScaledGD
optimizes the natural loss function in an
unconstrained
manner without requiring additional regularizations or orthogonalizations used in prior literature
han2020optimal
;
frandsen2020optimization
;
kasai2016low
, and the factors stay balanced in an automatic manner as if they are implicitly regularized
ma2021beyond
.

In total, the fast convergence rate of
ScaledGD
, together with its low computational and memory costs by operating in the factor space, makes it a highly scalable and desirable method for low-rank estimation tasks.

2.1
Assumptions

Denote by
\bm{U}_{\star}\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}\bm{V}_{\star}^{\top}
the compact singular value decomposition (SVD) of the rank-
r
matrix
\bm{X}_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
, i.e.,

\bm{X}_{\star}=\bm{U}_{\star}\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}\bm{V}_{\star}^{\top}.

Here,
\bm{U}_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times r}
and
\bm{V}_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times r}
are composed of
r
left and right singular vectors, respectively, and
\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
is a diagonal matrix consisting of
r
singular values of
\bm{X}_{\star}
organized in a non-increasing order, i.e.
\sigma_{1}(\bm{X}_{\star})\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{r}(\bm{X}_{\star})>0
. Define the ground truth low-rank factors as

\displaystyle\bm{L}_{\star}\coloneqq\bm{U}_{\star}\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}^{1/2},\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\bm{R}_{\star}\coloneqq\bm{V}_{\star}\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}^{1/2},

(8)

so that
\bm{X}_{\star}=\bm{L}_{\star}\bm{R}_{\star}^{\top}
. Correspondingly, denote the stacked factor matrix as

\displaystyle\bm{F}_{\star}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\bm{L}_{\star}\\
\bm{R}_{\star}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n_{1}+n_{2})\times r}.

(9)

\runinhead

Key parameters. The condition number of
\bm{X}_{\star}
is defined as follows.

Definition 1
(Matrix condition number)

Define

\displaystyle\kappa\coloneqq\frac{\sigma_{1}(\bm{X}_{\star})}{\sigma_{r}(\bm{X}_{\star})}

(10)

as the condition number of
\bm{X}_{\star}
.

We next introduce the incoherence condition, which is known to be crucial for reliable estimation of the low-rank matrix
\bm{X}_{\star}
in matrix completion and robust PCA
chen2015incoherence
.

Definition 2
(Matrix incoherence)

A rank-
r
matrix
\bm{X}_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
with compact SVD as
\bm{X}_{\star}=\bm{U}_{\star}\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}\bm{V}_{\star}^{\top}
is said to be
\mu
-incoherent if

\displaystyle\|\bm{U}_{\star}\|_{2,\infty}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{n_{1}}}\|\bm{U}_{\star}\|_{\fro}=\sqrt{\frac{\mu r}{n_{1}}},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|\bm{V}_{\star}\|_{2,\infty}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{n_{2}}}\|\bm{V}_{\star}\|_{\fro}=\sqrt{\frac{\mu r}{n_{2}}}.

2.2
Matrix sensing

\runinhead

Observation model. Assume that we have collected a set of linear measurements about a rank-
r
matrix
\bm{X}_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
, given as

\displaystyle\bm{y}=\mathcal{A}(\bm{X}_{\star})\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

(11)

where
\mathcal{A}(\bm{X})=\{\langle\bm{A}_{i},\bm{X}\rangle\}_{i=1}^{m}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the linear map modeling the measurement process. The goal of low-rank matrix sensing is to recover
\bm{X}_{\star}
from
\bm{y}
when the number of measurements
m\ll n_{1}n_{2}
, which
has wide applications in medical imaging, signal processing, and data compression
candes2011tight
.

\runinhead

Algorithm development. Writing
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
into a factored form
\bm{X}=\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\top}
, we consider the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}_{\bm{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n_{1}+n_{2})\times r}}\;\mathcal{L}(\bm{F})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathcal{A}(\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\top})-\bm{y}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(12)

Here as before,
\bm{F}
denotes the stacked factor matrix
[\bm{L}^{\top},\bm{R}^{\top}]^{\top}
. We suggest running
ScaledGD
(
3
) with the spectral initialization to solve (
12
), which performs the top-
r
SVD on
\mathcal{A}^{*}(\bm{y})
, where
\mathcal{A}^{*}(\cdot)
is the adjoint operator of
\mathcal{A}(\cdot)
. The full algorithm is stated in Algorithm
1
. The low-rank matrix can be estimated as
\bm{X}_{T}=\bm{L}_{T}\bm{R}_{T}^{\top}
after running
T
iterations of
ScaledGD
.

ScaledGD

for

Theoretical guarantee. To understand the performance of
ScaledGD
for low-rank matrix sensing, we adopt a standard assumption on the sensing operator
\mathcal{A}(\cdot)
, namely the Restricted Isometry Property (RIP).

Definition 3
(Matrix RIP

)

The linear map
\mathcal{A}(\cdot)
is said to obey the rank-
r
RIP with a constant
\delta_{r}\in[0,1)
, if for all matrices
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
of rank at most
r
, one has

\displaystyle(1-\delta_{r})\|\bm{M}\|_{\fro}^{2}\leq\|\mathcal{A}(\bm{M})\|_{2}^{2}\leq(1+\delta_{r})\|\bm{M}\|_{\fro}^{2}.

It is well-known that many measurement ensembles satisfy the RIP property

;
candes2011tight
. For example, if the entries of
\bm{A}_{i}
’s are composed of i.i.d. Gaussian entries
\mathcal{N}(0,1/m)
, then the RIP is satisfied for a constant
\delta_{r}
as long as
m
is on the order of
(n_{1}+n_{2})r/\delta_{r}^{2}
. With the RIP condition in place, the following theorem demonstrates that
ScaledGD
converges linearly at a constant rate as long as the sensing operator
\mathcal{A}(\cdot)
has a sufficiently small RIP constant.

Theorem 2.1

Suppose that
\mathcal{A}(\cdot)
obeys the
2r
-RIP with

. If the step size obeys

, then for all
t\geq 0
, the iterates of the
ScaledGD
method in Algorithm
1
satisfy

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{L}_{t}\bm{R}_{t}^{\top}-\bm{X}_{\star}\right\|_{\fro}

\displaystyle\leq(1-0.6\eta)^{t}0.15\sigma_{r}(\bm{X}_{\star}).

Theorem
2.1
establishes that the reconstruction error
\|\bm{L}_{t}\bm{R}_{t}^{\top}-\bm{X}_{\star}\|_{\fro}
contracts linearly at a constant rate, as long as the sample size satisfies
m=O(nr^{2}\kappa^{2})
with Gaussian random measurements

, where we recall that
n=n_{1}\vee n_{2}
.
To reach
\epsilon
-accuracy, i.e.
\|\bm{L}_{t}\bm{R}_{t}^{\top}-\bm{X}_{\star}\|_{\fro}\leq\epsilon\sigma_{r}(\bm{X}_{\star})
,

takes at most
T=O(\log(1/\epsilon))
iterations, which is
independent
of the condition number
\kappa
of
\bm{X}_{\star}
.
In comparison, GD with spectral initialization in
tu2015low
converges in

iterations as long as

.
Therefore,

converges at a much faster rate than GD at the same sample complexity while maintaining a similar per-iteration cost (cf. Table

2.3
Matrix robust principal component analysis

\runinhead

Observation model. Assume that we have observed the data matrix

\displaystyle\bm{Y}=\bm{X}_{\star}+\bm{S}_{\star},

which is a superposition of a rank-
r
matrix
\bm{X}_{\star}
, modeling the clean data, and a sparse matrix
\bm{S}_{\star}
, modeling the corruption or outliers.
The goal of robust PCA
candes2009robustPCA
;
chandrasekaran2011siam
is to separate the two matrices
\bm{X}_{\star}
and
\bm{S}_{\star}
from their mixture
\bm{Y}
.

Following
chandrasekaran2011siam
;
netrapalli2014non
;
yi2016fast
, we consider a deterministic sparsity model for
\bm{S}_{\star}
, in which
\bm{S}_{\star}
contains at most
\alpha
-fraction of nonzero entries per row and column for some
\alpha\in[0,1)
, i.e.
\bm{S}_{\star}\in\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
, where we denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}\coloneqq\{\bm{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}:\|\bm{S}_{i,\cdot}\|_{0}\leq\alpha n_{2}\mbox{ for all }i,\mbox{ and }\|\bm{S}_{\cdot,j}\|_{0}\leq\alpha n_{1}\mbox{ for all }j\}.

(15)

\runinhead

Algorithm development. Writing
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
into the factored form
\bm{X}=\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\top}
, we consider the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}_{\bm{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n_{1}+n_{2})\times r},\bm{S}\in\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}}\;\mathcal{L}(\bm{F},\bm{S})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\top}+\bm{S}-\bm{Y}\right\|_{\fro}^{2}.

(16)

It is thus natural to alternatively update
\bm{F}=[\bm{L}^{\top},\bm{R}^{\top}]^{\top}
and
\bm{S}
, where
\bm{F}
is updated via the proposed
ScaledGD
algorithm, and
\bm{S}
is updated by hard thresholding, which trims the small entries of the residual matrix
\bm{Y}-\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\top}
. More specifically, for some truncation level
0\leq\bar{\alpha}\leq 1
, we define the sparsification operator that only keeps
\bar{\alpha}
fraction of largest entries in each row and column:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}_{\bar{\alpha}}[\bm{A}])_{i,j}=\begin{cases}\bm{A}_{i,j},&\mbox{if }|\bm{A}|_{i,j}\geq|\bm{A}|_{i,(\bar{\alpha}n_{2})},\mbox{ and }|\bm{A}|_{i,j}\geq|\bm{A}|_{(\bar{\alpha}n_{1}),j}\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases},

(17)

where
|\bm{A}|_{i,(k)}
(resp.
|\bm{A}|_{(k),j}
) denote the
k
-th largest element in magnitude in the
i
-th row (resp.
j
-th column).
The
ScaledGD
algorithm with the spectral initialization for solving robust PCA is formally stated in Algorithm

. Note that, comparing with
yi2016fast
, we do not require a balancing term
\|\bm{L}^{\top}\bm{L}-\bm{R}^{\top}\bm{R}\|_{\fro}^{2}
in the loss function (

), nor the projection of the low-rank factors onto the

ball in each iteration.

2.4
Matrix completion

\runinhead

Observation model. Assume that we have observed a subset
\Omega
of entries of
\bm{X}_{\star}
given as

\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\bm{X}_{\star})
, where
\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
is a projection such that

\displaystyle(\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\bm{X}))_{i,j}=\begin{cases}\bm{X}_{i,j},&\mbox{if }(i,j)\in\Omega,\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

(20)

Here,
\Omega
is generated according to the Bernoulli model in the sense that each
(i,j)\in\Omega
independent with probability
p\in(0,1]
.
The goal of matrix completion is to recover the matrix
\bm{X}_{\star}
from its partial observation
\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\bm{X}_{\star})
.

\runinhead

Algorithm development. Again, writing
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
into the factored form
\bm{X}=\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\top}
, we consider the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}_{\bm{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n_{1}+n_{2})\times r}}\;\mathcal{L}(\bm{F})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2p}\left\|\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\top}-\bm{X}_{\star})\right\|_{\fro}^{2}.

(21)

Similarly to robust PCA, the underlying low-rank matrix
\bm{X}_{\star}
needs to be incoherent (cf. Definition
2
) to avoid ill-posedness. One typical strategy to ensure the incoherence condition is to perform projection after the gradient update, by projecting the iterates to maintain small
\ell_{2,\infty}
norms of the factor matrices. However, the standard projection operator
chen2015fast
is not covariant with respect to invertible transforms, and consequently, needs to be modified when using scaled gradient updates. To that end, we introduce the following new projection operator: for every
\widetilde{\bm{F}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n_{1}+n_{2})\times r}=[\widetilde{\bm{L}}^{\top},\widetilde{\bm{R}}^{\top}]^{\top}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{P}_{B}(\widetilde{\bm{F}})=&\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n_{1}+n_{2})\times r}}\;\big\|(\bm{L}-\widetilde{\bm{L}})(\widetilde{\bm{R}}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{R}})^{1/2}\big\|_{\fro}^{2}+\big\|(\bm{R}-\widetilde{\bm{R}})(\widetilde{\bm{L}}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{L}})^{1/2}\big\|_{\fro}^{2}\\
&\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad\sqrt{n_{1}}\big\|\bm{L}(\widetilde{\bm{R}}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{R}})^{1/2}\big\|_{2,\infty}\vee\sqrt{n_{2}}\big\|\bm{R}(\widetilde{\bm{L}}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{L}})^{1/2}\big\|_{2,\infty}\leq B\end{split},

(22)

which finds a factored matrix that is closest to
\widetilde{\bm{F}}
and stays incoherent in a weighted sense. Luckily, the solution to the above scaled projection admits a simple closed-form solution, given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{P}_{B}(\widetilde{\bm{F}})\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\bm{L}\\
\bm{R}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mbox{where}\,\,\bm{L}_{i,\cdot}&\coloneqq\left(1\wedge\frac{B}{\sqrt{n_{1}}\|\widetilde{\bm{L}}_{i,\cdot}\widetilde{\bm{R}}^{\top}\|_{2}}\right)\widetilde{\bm{L}}_{i,\cdot},\,1\leq i\leq n_{1},\\
\bm{R}_{j,\cdot}&\coloneqq\left(1\wedge\frac{B}{\sqrt{n_{2}}\|\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{j,\cdot}\widetilde{\bm{L}}^{\top}\|_{2}}\right)\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{j,\cdot},\,1\leq j\leq n_{2}.\end{split}

(23)

With the new projection operator in place, we propose the following
ScaledGD
method with spectral initialization for solving matrix completion, formally stated in Algorithm
3
.

2.5
A glimpse of the analysis

At the heart of our analysis is a proper metric to measure the progress of the
ScaledGD
iterates
\bm{F}_{t}\coloneqq[\bm{L}_{t}^{\top},\bm{R}_{t}^{\top}]^{\top}
.
Obviously, the factored representation is not unique in that for any invertible matrix
\bm{Q}\in\GL(r)
, one has
\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\top}=(\bm{L}\bm{Q})(\bm{R}\bm{Q}^{-\top})^{\top}
. Therefore, the reconstruction error metric needs to take into account this identifiability issue. More importantly, we need a diagonal scaling in the distance error metric to properly account for the effect of preconditioning. To provide intuition, note that the update rule (
3
) can be viewed as finding the best local quadratic approximation of
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
in the following sense:

\displaystyle(\bm{L}_{t+1},\bm{R}_{t+1})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{L},\bm{R}}\;\mathcal{L}(\bm{L}_{t},\bm{R}_{t})+\langle\nabla_{\bm{L}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{L}_{t},\bm{R}_{t}),\bm{L}-\bm{L}_{t}\rangle+\langle\nabla_{\bm{R}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{L}_{t},\bm{R}_{t}),\bm{R}-\bm{R}_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2\eta}\left(\left\|(\bm{L}-\bm{L}_{t})(\bm{R}_{t}^{\top}\bm{R}_{t})^{1/2}\right\|_{\fro}^{2}+\left\|(\bm{R}-\bm{R}_{t})(\bm{L}_{t}^{\top}\bm{L}_{t})^{1/2}\right\|_{\fro}^{2}\right),

where it is different from the common interpretation of gradient descent in the way the quadratic approximation is taken by a scaled norm. When
\bm{L}_{t}\approx\bm{L}_{\star}
and
\bm{R}_{t}\approx\bm{R}_{\star}
are approaching the ground truth, the additional scaling factors can be approximated by
\bm{L}_{t}^{\top}\bm{L}_{t}\approx\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}
and
\bm{R}_{t}^{\top}\bm{R}_{t}\approx\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}
, leading to the following error metric

\displaystyle\dist^{2}(\bm{F},\bm{F}_{\star})\coloneqq\inf_{\bm{Q}\in\GL(r)}\;\left\|(\bm{L}\bm{Q}-\bm{L}_{\star})\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}^{1/2}\right\|_{\fro}^{2}+\left\|(\bm{R}\bm{Q}^{-\top}-\bm{R}_{\star})\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}^{1/2}\right\|_{\fro}^{2}.

(26)

The design and analysis of this new distance metric are of crucial importance in obtaining the improved rate of
ScaledGD
.
In comparison, the previously studied distance metrics (proposed mainly for GD) either do not include the diagonal scaling
ma2021beyond
;
tu2015low
, or only consider the ambiguity class up to orthonormal transforms
tu2015low
, which fail to unveil the benefit of
ScaledGD
.

This section is devoted to introducing

and establishing its statistical and computational guarantees for various low-rank tensor estimation problems; the majority of the results are based on

;

.

3.1
Assumptions

Suppose the ground truth tensor
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}=[\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3})]\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
admits the following Tucker decomposition

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3})=\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{r_{1}}\sum_{j_{2}=1}^{r_{2}}\sum_{j_{3}=1}^{r_{3}}\bm{U}_{\star}(i_{1},j_{1})\bm{V}_{\star}(i_{2},j_{2})\bm{W}_{\star}(i_{3},j_{3})\bm{\mathcal{G}}_{\star}(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})

(27)

for
1\leq i_{k}\leq n_{k}
, or more compactly,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}

\displaystyle=(\bm{U}_{\star},\bm{V}_{\star},\bm{W}_{\star})\bcdot\bm{\mathcal{G}}_{\star},

(28)

where
\bm{\mathcal{G}}_{\star}=[\bm{\mathcal{G}}_{\star}(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})]\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}\times r_{2}\times r_{3}}
is the core tensor of multilinear rank
\bm{r}=(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})
, and
\bm{U}_{\star}=[\bm{U}_{\star}(i_{1},j_{1})]\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times r_{1}}
,
\bm{V}_{\star}=[\bm{V}_{\star}(i_{2},j_{2})]\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times r_{2}}
,
\bm{W}_{\star}=[\bm{W}_{\star}(i_{3},j_{3})]\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}\times r_{3}}
are the factor matrices of each mode. Letting
\mathcal{M}_{k}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star})
be the mode-
k
matricization of
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{1}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star})

\displaystyle=\bm{U}_{\star}\mathcal{M}_{1}(\bm{\mathcal{G}}_{\star})(\bm{W}_{\star}\otimes\bm{V}_{\star})^{\top},

(29a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star})

\displaystyle=\bm{V}_{\star}\mathcal{M}_{2}(\bm{\mathcal{G}}_{\star})(\bm{W}_{\star}\otimes\bm{U}_{\star})^{\top},

(29b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{3}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star})

\displaystyle=\bm{W}_{\star}\mathcal{M}_{3}(\bm{\mathcal{G}}_{\star})(\bm{V}_{\star}\otimes\bm{U}_{\star})^{\top}.

(29c)

It is straightforward to see that the Tucker decomposition is not uniquely specified: for any invertible matrices
\bm{Q}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{k}\times r_{k}}
,
k=1,2,3
, one has

\displaystyle(\bm{U}_{\star},\bm{V}_{\star},\bm{W}_{\star})\bcdot\bm{\mathcal{G}}_{\star}=(\bm{U}_{\star}\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{V}_{\star}\bm{Q}_{2},\bm{W}_{\star}\bm{Q}_{3})\bcdot((\bm{Q}_{1}^{-1},\bm{Q}_{2}^{-1},\bm{Q}_{3}^{-1})\bcdot\bm{\mathcal{G}}_{\star}).

We shall fix the ground truth factors such that
\bm{U}_{\star}
,
\bm{V}_{\star}
and
\bm{W}_{\star}
are orthonormal matrices consisting of left singular vectors in each mode. Furthermore, the core tensor
\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{\star}
is related to the singular values in each mode as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{k}(\bm{\mathcal{G}}_{\star})\mathcal{M}_{k}(\bm{\mathcal{G}}_{\star})^{\top}=\bm{\Sigma}_{\star,k}^{2},\qquad k=1,2,3,

(30)

where
\bm{\Sigma}_{\star,k}\coloneqq\diag[\sigma_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{k}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star})),\dots,\sigma_{r_{k}}(\mathcal{M}_{k}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}))]
is a diagonal matrix where the diagonal elements are composed of the nonzero singular values of
\mathcal{M}_{k}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star})
and
r_{k}=\rank(\mathcal{M}_{k}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}))
for
k=1,2,3
.

\runinhead

Key parameters.
Of particular interest is a sort of condition number of
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}
, which plays an important role in governing the computational efficiency of first-order algorithms.

Definition 4
(Tensor condition number)

The condition number of
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}
is defined as

\displaystyle\kappa\coloneqq\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star})}{\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star})}=\frac{\max_{k=1,2,3}\sigma_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{k}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}))}{\min_{k=1,2,3}\sigma_{r_{k}}(\mathcal{M}_{k}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}))}.

(31)

Another parameter is the incoherence parameter, which plays an important role in governing the well-posedness of low-rank tensor RPCA and completion.

Definition 5
(Tensor incoherence)

The incoherence parameter of
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mu\coloneqq\max\left\{\frac{n_{1}}{r_{1}}\|\bm{U}_{\star}\|_{2,\infty}^{2},\;\frac{n_{2}}{r_{2}}\|\bm{V}_{\star}\|_{2,\infty}^{2},\;\frac{n_{3}}{r_{3}}\|\bm{W}_{\star}\|_{2,\infty}^{2}\right\}.

(32)

Roughly speaking, a small incoherence parameter ensures that the energy of the tensor is evenly distributed across its entries, so that a small random subset of its elements still reveals substantial information about the latent structure of the entire tensor.

3.2
Tensor sensing

\runinhead

Observation model. We first consider tensor sensing — also known as tensor regression — with Gaussian design. Assume that we have access to a set of observations given as

\displaystyle y_{i}=\langle\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{i},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}\rangle,\quad i=1,\dots,m,\quad\mbox{ or concisely, }\qquad\bm{y}=\mathcal{A}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}),

(33)

(34)

3.3
Tensor robust principal component analysis

\runinhead

Observation model. Suppose that we collect a set of corrupted observations of
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}
as

\bm{\mathcal{Y}}=\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}+\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{\star},

(36)

where
\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{\star}
is the corruption tensor. The problem of tensor RPCA seeks to separate
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}
and
\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{\star}
from their sum
\bm{\mathcal{Y}}
as efficiently and accurately as possible. Similar to the matrix case, we consider a deterministic sparsity model following the matrix case
chandrasekaran2011siam
;
netrapalli2014non
;
yi2016fast
, where
\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{\star}
contains at most a small fraction of nonzero entries per fiber. Formally, the corruption tensor
\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{\star}
is said to be
\alpha
-fraction sparse, i.e.,
\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{\star}\in\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{\alpha}
, where

\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{\alpha}\coloneqq\Big\{\bm{\mathcal{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}:\;\|\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{i_{1},i_{2},:}\|_{0}\leq\alpha n_{3},\;\|\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{i_{1},:,i_{3}}\|_{0}\leq\alpha n_{2},\;\\
\|\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{:,i_{2},i_{3}}\|_{0}\leq\alpha n_{1},\mbox{ for all }1\leq i_{k}\leq n_{k},\quad k=1,2,3\Big\}.

(37)

\runinhead

ScaledGD algorithm.
It is natural to optimize the following objective function:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}_{\bm{F},\bm{\mathcal{S}}}\;\mathcal{L}(\bm{F},\bm{\mathcal{S}})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left\|\big(\bm{U},\bm{V},\bm{W}\big)\bcdot\bm{\mathcal{G}}+\bm{\mathcal{S}}-\bm{\mathcal{Y}}\right\|_{\fro}^{2},

(38)

where
\bm{F}=(\bm{U},\bm{V},\bm{W},\bm{\mathcal{G}})
and
\bm{\mathcal{S}}
are the optimization variables for the tensor factors and the corruption tensor, respectively.
Our algorithm alternates between corruption removal and factor refinements, as detailed in Algorithm
5
. To remove the corruption, we use the following soft-shrinkage operator that trims the magnitudes of the entries by the amount of some carefully pre-set threshold.

Definition 6
(Soft-shrinkage operator)

For an order-
3
tensor
\bm{\mathcal{X}}
, the soft-shrinkage operator
\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{soft}}_{\zeta}\left[\cdot\right]:\,\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
with threshold
\zeta>0
is defined as

\displaystyle\big[\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{soft}}_{\zeta}\left[\bm{\mathcal{X}}\right]\big]_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}}:=\sgn\big([\bm{\mathcal{X}}]_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}}\big)\,\cdot\,\max\big(0,\big|[\bm{\mathcal{X}}]_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}}\big|-\zeta\big).

The soft-shrinkage operator sets entries with magnitudes smaller than
\zeta
to
0
, while uniformly shrinking the magnitudes of the other entries by
\zeta
. At the beginning of each iteration, the corruption tensor is updated via applying the soft-thresholding operator to the current residual
\bm{\mathcal{Y}}-\big(\bm{U}_{t},\bm{V}_{t},\bm{W}_{t}\big)\bcdot\bm{\mathcal{G}}_{t}
using some properly selected threshold
\zeta_{t}
, followed by updating the tensor factors
\bm{F}_{t}
via scaled gradient descent with respect to
\mathcal{L}(\bm{F}_{t},\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{t+1})
in (
38

\bm{r}

Fortunately, the

3.4
Tensor completion

\runinhead

Observation model. Assume that we have observed a subset of entries in
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}
, given as

\bm{\mathcal{Y}}=\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star})
, where
\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
is a projection such that

\displaystyle[\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star})](i_{1},i_{2},i_{3})=\begin{cases}\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}),&\mbox{if }(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3})\in\Omega,\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(41)

Here,
\Omega
is generated according to the Bernoulli observation model in the sense that

\displaystyle(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3})\in\Omega~~\mbox{ independently with probability}~p\in(0,1].

(42)

The goal is to recover the tensor
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}
from its partial observation
\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star})
, which can be achieved by minimizing the loss function

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{F}=(\bm{U},\bm{V},\bm{W},\bm{\mathcal{S}})}\;\mathcal{L}(\bm{F})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2p}\left\|\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}\big((\bm{U},\bm{V},\bm{W})\bcdot\bm{\mathcal{S}}\big)-\bm{\mathcal{Y}}\right\|_{\fro}^{2}.

(43)

\runinhead

Algorithm development.
To guarantee faithful recovery from partial observations, the underlying low-rank tensor
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}
needs to be incoherent (cf. Definition
5
) to avoid ill-posedness. One typical strategy, as employed in the matrix setting, to ensure the incoherence condition is to trim the rows of the factors after the scaled gradient update. We introduce the scaled projection as follows,

\displaystyle(\bm{U},\bm{V},\bm{W},\bm{\mathcal{S}})=\mathcal{P}_{B}(\bm{U}_{+},{\bm{V}}_{+},{\bm{W}}_{+},{\bm{\mathcal{S}}}_{+}),\

(44)

where
B>0
is the projection radius, and

\displaystyle\bm{U}(i_{1},:)

\displaystyle=\left(1\wedge\frac{B}{\sqrt{n_{1}}\|{\bm{U}}_{+}(i_{1},:)\breve{\bm{U}}_{+}^{\top}\|_{2}}\right)\bm{U}_{+}(i_{1},:),\qquad 1\leq i_{1}\leq n_{1};

\displaystyle\bm{V}(i_{2},:)

\displaystyle=\left(1\wedge\frac{B}{\sqrt{n_{2}}\|{\bm{V}}_{+}(i_{2},:)\breve{\bm{V}}_{+}^{\top}\|_{2}}\right)\bm{V}_{+}(i_{2},:),\qquad 1\leq i_{2}\leq n_{2};

\displaystyle\bm{W}(i_{3},:)

\displaystyle=\left(1\wedge\frac{B}{\sqrt{n_{3}}\|\bm{W}_{+}(i_{3},:)\breve{\bm{W}}_{+}^{\top}\|_{2}}\right){\bm{W}}_{+}(i_{3},:),\qquad 1\leq i_{3}\leq n_{3};

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{+}.

Here, we recall
\breve{\bm{U}}_{+}
,
\breve{\bm{V}}_{+}
,
\breve{\bm{W}}_{+}
are analogously defined in (
6
) using
(\bm{U}_{+},\bm{V}_{+},\bm{W}_{+},\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{+})
.
As can be seen, each row of
{\bm{U}}_{+}
(resp.
{\bm{V}}_{+}
and
{\bm{W}}_{+}
) is scaled by a scalar based on the row
\ell_{2}
norms of
{\bm{U}}_{+}\breve{\bm{U}}_{+}^{\top}
(resp.
{\bm{V}}_{+}\breve{\bm{V}}_{+}^{\top}
and
{\bm{W}}_{+}\breve{\bm{W}}_{+}^{\top}
), which is the mode-1 (resp. mode-2 and mode-3) matricization of the tensor
(\bm{U}_{+},\bm{V}_{+},\bm{W}_{+})\bcdot\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{+}
. It is a straightforward observation that the projection can be computed efficiently.

With the scaled projection
\mathcal{P}_{B}(\cdot)
defined in hand, we are in a position to describe the details of the proposed
ScaledGD
algorithm, summarized in Algorithm
6
. It consists of two stages: spectral initialization followed by iterative refinements using the scaled projected gradient updates in
(
45
). For the spectral initialization, we take advantage of the subspace estimators proposed in
cai2019subspace
;
xia2021statistically
for highly unbalanced matrices. Specifically, we estimate the subspace spanned by
\bm{U}_{\star}
by that spanned by top-
r_{1}
eigenvectors
\bm{U}_{+}
of the diagonally-deleted Gram matrix of
p^{-1}\mathcal{M}_{1}(\bm{\mathcal{Y}})
, denoted as

\displaystyle\Poffdiag(p^{-2}\mathcal{M}_{1}(\bm{\mathcal{Y}})\mathcal{M}_{1}(\bm{\mathcal{Y}})^{\top}),

and the other two factors
\bm{V}_{+}
and
\bm{W}_{+}
are estimated similarly. The core tensor is then estimated as

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{+}=p^{-1}(\bm{U}_{+}^{\top},\bm{V}_{+}^{\top},\bm{W}_{+}^{\top})\bcdot\bm{\mathcal{Y}}.

To ensure the initialization is incoherent, we pass it through the scaled projection operator to obtain the final initial estimate:

\displaystyle(\bm{U}_{0},\bm{V}_{0},\bm{W}_{0},\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{0})=\mathcal{P}_{B}\big(\bm{U}_{+},\bm{V}_{+},\bm{W}_{+},\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{+}\big).

4
Preconditioning Meets Overparameterization

In this section we treat a more complicated scenario,
where the correct rank
r
of the ground truth
\bm{X}_{\star}
is not known
a priori
. In this case, a practical solution is to
overparameterize
, i.e., to choose some
r^{\prime}>r
, and proceed as if
r^{\prime}
is the correct rank.
It turns out that
ScaledGD
needs some simple modification to work robustly in this setting. The modified algorithm, which we call
ScaledGD(
\lambda
)
, will be introduced in the rest of this section, along with theoretical analysis on its global convergence.

\runinhead

Motivation.
We begin with inspecting the behavior of
ScaledGD
in the overparameterized setting. Assume we already find some
\bm{L}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times r^{\prime}},\bm{R}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times r^{\prime}}
that are close to the ground truth: the first
r
columns of
\bm{L}_{t}

are close to
\bm{L}_{\star}
, while the rest
r^{\prime}-r
columns are close to zero;
mutatis mutandis
for
\bm{R}_{t}
.

Recall that in the update equation (
3
) of
ScaledGD
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{L}_{t+1}&=\bm{L}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\bm{L}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{L}_{t},\bm{R}_{t})(\bm{R}_{t}^{\top}\bm{R}_{t})^{-1},\\
\bm{R}_{t+1}&=\bm{R}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\bm{R}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{L}_{t},\bm{R}_{t})(\bm{L}_{t}^{\top}\bm{L}_{t})^{-1},\end{split}

the preconditioners are chosen to be the inverse of the
r^{\prime}\times r^{\prime}
matrices
\bm{L}_{t}^{\top}\bm{L}_{t}
,
\bm{R}_{t}^{\top}\bm{R}_{t}
. However, since the last
r^{\prime}-r
columns for
\bm{L}_{t}
(respectively,
\bm{R}_{t}
) are close to zero, it is clear that
\bm{L}_{t}^{\top}\bm{L}_{t}
(respectively,
\bm{R}_{t}^{\top}\bm{R}_{t}
) are approximately of rank at most
r
. When
r^{\prime}>r
, this means
\bm{L}_{t}^{\top}\bm{L}_{t}
and
\bm{R}_{t}^{\top}\bm{R}_{t}
are close to being degenerate since their approximate ranks (no larger than
r
) are smaller than their dimensions
r^{\prime}
, thus taking inverse of them are numerically unstable.

\runinhead

ScaledGD(
\lambda
)
:
ScaledGD
with overparameterization.
One of the simplest remedies to such instability is to
regularize
the preconditioner. Before taking inverse of
\bm{L}_{t}^{\top}\bm{L}_{t}
and
\bm{R}_{t}^{\top}\bm{R}_{t}
, we add a regularizer
\lambda\bm{I}
to avoid degeneracy, where
\lambda>0
is a regularization parameter; the preconditioner thus becomes
(\bm{L}_{t}^{\top}\bm{L}_{t}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}
and
(\bm{R}_{t}^{\top}\bm{R}_{t}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}
.
The new update rule is specified by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{L}_{t+1}&=\bm{L}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\bm{L}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{L}_{t},\bm{R}_{t})(\bm{R}_{t}^{\top}\bm{R}_{t}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1},\\
\bm{R}_{t+1}&=\bm{R}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\bm{R}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{L}_{t},\bm{R}_{t})(\bm{L}_{t}^{\top}\bm{L}_{t}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}.\end{split}

(46)

This regularized version of
ScaledGD
is called
ScaledGD(
\lambda
)
.
It turns out that the simple regularization trick works out well:
ScaledGD(
\lambda
)
not only (almost) inherits the
\kappa
-free convergence rate, but also has the advantage of being robust to overparameterization and enjoying provable global convergence to any prescribed accuracy level, when initialized by a small random initialization. This is established in
xu2023power
formally for the matrix sensing setting studied in Section
2.4
, where the two factors
\bm{L}_{\star}
,
\bm{R}_{\star}
in
\bm{X}_{\star}=\bm{L}_{\star}\bm{R}_{\star}^{\top}
are equal, i.e.
\bm{X}_{\star}=\bm{L}_{\star}\bm{L}_{\star}^{\top}
. Under such situation,
ScaledGD(
\lambda
)
instantiates to

\bm{L}_{t+1}=\bm{L}_{t}-\eta\mathcal{A}^{*}(\mathcal{A}(\bm{L}_{t}\bm{L}_{t}^{\top})-\bm{y})\bm{L}_{t}(\bm{L}_{t}^{\top}\bm{L}_{t}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}.

(47)

Similar to
li2018algorithmic
;
stoger2021small
,
we set the initialization
\bm{L}_{0}
to be a small random matrix, i.e.,

\bm{L}_{0}=\alpha\bm{G},

(48)

where
\bm{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r^{\prime}}
is some matrix considered to be normalized
and
\alpha>0
controls the magnitude of the initialization.
To simplify exposition, we take
\bm{G}
to be a standard random Gaussian matrix,
that is,
\bm{G}
is a random matrix with i.i.d. entries distributed as
\mathcal{N}(0,1/n)
.

Theorem

shows that by choosing an appropriate

\lambda

finds an

, in no more than an order of

\log\kappa\cdot\log(\kappa n)+\log(1/\epsilon)

ScaledGD(
\lambda
)
converges at a constant linear rate independent of the condition number
\kappa
after an initial phase of approximately
O(\log\kappa\cdot\log(\kappa n))

is much slower than
ScaledGD(
\lambda

even for mildly ill-conditioned matrices. Furthermore,
our sample complexity depends only on the true rank
r

r^{\prime}
— a crucial feature in order to provide meaningful guarantees when the overparameterized rank
r^{\prime}
is uninformative of the true rank, i.e., close to the full dimension
n
.
The dependency on
\kappa
in the sample complexity, on the other end, has been generally unavoidable in nonconvex low-rank estimation
chi2019nonconvex
.

The experimental results help to explain the motivation of using
ScaledGD
with mixed initialization. The reason for the instability of spectrally initialized
ScaledGD
for large

stems from the fact that spectral initialization does not cope well with overparameterization. On the other hand, small random initialization is known to help stabilize with overparameterization

ScaledGD

\bm{L}_{0}

\lambda
)
, which is provably robust against overparameterization and can be integrated perfectly into
ScaledGD
, becomes a reasonable choice.

6
Conclusions

This chapter highlights a novel approach to provably accelerate ill-conditioned low-rank estimation via
ScaledGD
. Its fast convergence, together with low computational and memory costs by operating in the factor space, makes it a highly scalable and desirable method in practice. The performance of
ScaledGD
is also robust when the data matrix is only approximately low-rank and the observations are noisy; we refer interested readers to
xu2023power
for further details.
In terms of future directions, it is of great interest to explore the design of effective preconditioners for other statistical estimation and learning tasks, as well as further understand the implications of preconditioning in the presence of overparameterization for the asymmetric setting.
