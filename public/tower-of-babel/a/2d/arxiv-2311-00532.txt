Smooth Koopman eigenfunctions
Suddhasattwa Das

Abstract
Any dynamical system, whether it is generated by a differential equation or a transformation map on a manifold, induces a dynamics on functional-spaces. The choice of functional-space may vary, but the induced dynamics is always linear, and codified by the Koopman operator. The eigenfunctions of the Koopman operator are of extreme importance in the study of the dynamics. They provide a clear distinction between the mixing and non-mixing components of the dynamics, and also reveal embedded toral rotations. The usual choice of functional-space is L 2 L^{2} , a class of square integrable functions. A fundamental problem with eigenfunctions in L 2 L^{2} is that they are often extremely discontinuous, particularly if the system is chaotic. There are some prototypical systems called skew-product dynamics in which L 2 L^{2} Koopman eigenfunctions are also smooth. The article shows that under general assumptions on an ergodic system, these prototypical examples are the only possibility. Moreover, the smooth eigenfunctions can be used to create a change of variables which explicitly characterizes the weakly mixing component too.



Key words.

Koopman operator, skew-product, quasiperiodicity, Discrete spectrum, submersion

1  Introduction

Dynamical systems theory involves the study of a map
\Phi:M\to M
on a topological space
M
or a continuous family of maps
\Phi^{t}:M\to M
. Although this branch of mathematics had started out as a descriptive tool for various physical phenomenon, the realization that the map(s) can induce a variety of different mathematical behavior within
M
lead to a great diversification of the theory. The following will be our standing assumption :

Assumption 1
.

M
is an
n
-dimensional
C^{1}
manifold equipped with the the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}
.
\Phi^{t}
is a
C^{1}
flow on
M
generated by a vector field
V
. The flow has an invariant probability measure
\mu
with a compact support
X\subset M
.

There has been mainly three means of studying dynamics - as a topological system
(
AkinEtAl1996
;
Blender2
;
DasJim17_chaos
, e.g.)
, as a measure preserving system
(
Halmos1944mixing
;
Halmos1944
;
Halmos1956
;
Walters2000
, e.g.)
, or indirectly as a group of linear transformations induced on some function space
(
Nadkarni
;
DasGiannakis_RKHS_2018
, e.g.)
. In all these approaches, one could encounter different facets of the phenomenon of
chaos

(
DGJ_compactV_2018
;
Katok_periodic_1980
;
Bowen_AxiomAflow_1975
;
Bowen_equi_1975
;
Robinson1998
;
Bowen_entropy_1970
, e.g.)
. In a series of independent works spanning decades, it has been discovered that chaotic systems have a dense collection of unstable hyperbolic periodic orbits
(
Katok_periodic_1980
, e.g.)
, and these points typically have dense stable and unstable foliation
(
Pesin_families_1976
, e.g.)
. This results in a non-smooth geometry of the attractor which is locally dense with expanding and contracting regions, and in spite of this great complexity, can be approximated well by arbitrarily long periodic orbits
(
WangSun2010
;
LiaoEtAl2018
, e.g.)
embedded within the dynamics.

There is yet another broad category of dynamical systems which are completely free of these traits of chaos. These are called
quasiperiodic systems

(
Dioph_Herman_1979
;
DasJim2017_SuperC
;
DSSY2017_QQ
, e.g.)
. They are characterized by zero Lyapunov exponents, absence of stable or unstable manifolds, lack of mixing, and every single trajectories being dense. Topologically they are conjugate to a rotation on a torus. One of the long standing conjectures in dynamics
SanderYorkeChaos2015
is that a typical non-periodic, minimal dynamical system is either quasiperiodic or chaotic set. The operator theoretic formulation of dynamical systems provides a clearer distinction between these two types of dynamics.

The Koopman operator.

Koopman operators
Nadkarni
act on observables by composition with the flow map. Given a function
f:M\to\mathbb{C}
or
f:X\to\mathbb{C}
, and a time
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the action of the Koopman operator
U^{t}
is defined as the time shifted map

(U^{t}f)(x):=f\left(\Phi^{t}x\right).

(1)

This makes
U^{t}
a linear operator on the vector spaces of functions on
X
or
M
. This action can be factored into the space of Borel functions on
X
or
M
.
U^{t}
also preserves the Banach spaces
C(X)
and
C^{r}_{c}(M)
, where
C^{r}_{c}(M)
is the space of compactly supported
C^{r}
functions on
M
.
U^{t}
also preserves the Banach spaces
L^{p}(\mu)
for
p>0
and the invariant measure
\mu
. Moreover,
U^{t}
is a norm-preserving operator on these Banach spaces. Let
V
be the vector field generating the flow.
V
acts as a differential operator
V:C^{1}(M)\to C(M)
, defined as

Vf:=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{t}\left(U^{t}f-f\right),\quad f\in C^{1}(M).

When
p=2
,
L^{2}(\mu)
is a Hilbert space, and
\{U^{t}:t\in\mathbb{R}\}
is a 1-parameter unitary evolution group on
L^{2}(\mu)

DasGiannakis_delay_2019
;
DGJ_compactV_2018
. Such a group has a generator
\hat{V}
acting on some dense subspace of
L^{2}(\mu)
, and the action of
\hat{V}
on
C^{1}(M)\cap L^{2}(\mu)
coincides with that of
V
. This space
L^{2}(\mu)
will be our primary choice of functional space, in which we study the spectrum of the Koopman operator. The Hilbert space property provides a powerful array of tools to model the Koopman operator, both theoretically and numerically
(
FroylandEtAl14
;
FroylandEtAl_coherent_2010
;
DasGiannakis_delay_2019
;
DGJ_compactV_2018
;
Philipp2024error
, e.g.)
. An important property to consider for both the Koopman group and its generator, is the presence of eigenfunctions.

Koopman eigenfunctions

Since
U^{t}
is a linear operator on
C^{r}(M)
,
C(X)
and
L^{2}(\mu)
, one can look for Koopman eigenfunctions in either of these spaces. Based on (
1
), an eigenfunction
z:M\to\mathbb{C}
of
U^{t}
, with eigenvalue
\lambda
should satisfy an equation of the form

U^{t}z(x)=z(\Phi^{t}x)=\lambda z(x)

(2)

The function
z
above will be called a
C^{r}(M)
-eigenfunction if
z\in C^{r}(M)
and (
2
) holds for every
x\in M
. Similarly,
z
will be called a
L^{2}(\mu)
-eigenfunction if
z\in L^{2}(\mu)
and (
2
) holds in
L^{2}(\mu)
sense, i.e.,
\left\|U^{t}z-\lambda z\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}=0
. In this Hilbert space setting, every eigenfunction has associated to it a quantity
\omega
called its
eigenfrequency
, which is independent of the time
t
, and which satisfies

U^{t}z(x)=z(\Phi^{t}x)=e^{\iota\omega t}z(x),\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R},\quad\mu-\text{a.e.}\ \ x\in M

(3)

This is consistent with the fact that
U^{t}
is a unitary operator on
L^{2}(\mu)
and all its eigenvalues must have norm
1
. Now note that if
z
is a
C(M)
-eigenfunction, then it is an
L^{2}(\mu)
eigenfunction too for every finite invariant measure
\mu
, thus (
2
) would still hold with
\lambda=e^{\iota\omega t}
. An important link between the unitary group
U^{t}
and its generator
V
is that they share the same
L^{2}(\mu)
eigenfunctions :

U^{t}z=\exp(\iota\omega t)z\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad Vz=\iota\omega z.

Koopman eigenfunctions carry significance in several real world phenomenon, such as latent seasonal sources in the study of timeseries
DasMustAgar2023_qpd
;
DasEtAl2023traffic
, as indicators of coherence in fluid flows
FroylandEtAl14b
;
DellnitzEtAl00
, and even as states in the operator theoretic description of quantum mechanics
(
Eisner2017pointwise
, e.g.)
, and in the study of general evolution semigroups
Suchanecki2010evolution
;
DGJ_compactV_2018
;
EisnerEtAl15
. Note that the constant functions are always eigenfunctions, corresponding to eigenvalue 1 (or eigenfrequency 0). Non-trivial eigenfunctions may or may not be present. If they happen to be present, they provide a meaningful splitting of the function space.

Spectral splitting

Let
\mathcal{D}
denote the subspace of
L^{2}(\mu)
spanned by the eigenfunctions of
V
with non-zero eigenvalues,
\mathcal{D}^{\bot}
denote its orthogonal complement.

L^{2}(\mu)=\mathcal{D}\oplus\mathcal{D}^{\bot}.

(4)

Note that
\mathcal{D}
always contains the constant functions, which correspond to eigenfrequency
0
. If
d\geq 1
, there is at least one non-trivial Koopman eigenfunction. The eigenvalues of
V
are closed under integer linear combinations and is generated by a finite set of rationally independent eigenvalues
\iota\omega_{1},\ldots,\iota\omega_{d}
. Thus every eigenvalue of
V
is of the form
\Sigma_{j\in\left\{1,\ldots,d\right\}}a_{j}\omega_{j}
, for some
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. If
d>1
, then the eigenvalues are dense on the imaginary axis. For every
\vec{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
z_{\vec{a}}
will denote the eigenfunction
\Sigma_{j\in\left\{1,\ldots,d\right\}}a_{j}\omega_{j}
. The corresponding eigenvalue is
i\vec{a}\cdot\vec{\omega}
, where
\vec{\omega}
=
(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{d})
.

The splitting in (
4
) is yet another manifestation of the dichotomy of chaotic and quasiperiodic dynamics. By a reuse of terminology, the subspaces
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\bot}
will be called the quasiperiodic and chaotic components of the dynamics respectively. The action of
U^{t}
on the quasiperiodic component is characterized by absence of mixing, unique ergodicity and superior ergodic convergence properties
(
DasJim2017_SuperC
;
DSSY2017_QQ
;
DasYorkeQuasiR2016
, e.g.)
. Chaotic motion is opposite in nature to quasiperiodicity
(
DasJim17_chaos
;
DasToungue2018
;
Blender2
, e.g.)
, it is characterized by infinitely many invariant subsets, and ergodic properties like arbitrarily small rate of decay of Birkhoff averages, mixing, and decay of correlations. We next further explore the connection of
\mathcal{D}
with toral rotations.

Embedded toral rotations.

In what follows, the circle
S^{1}
is identified with the unit circle
\{e^{i\theta}\;:\;\theta\in[0,2\pi)\}
in the complex plane. If
z:M\to\mathbb{C}
is a Koopman eigenfunction nonzero everywhere, then
z/|z|
is a Koopman eigenfunction too, with the same eigenfrequency. Thus without loss of generality (WLOG),
z
can be considered to map into the circle, i.e.,
z:M\to S^{1}
. For every
d\in\mathbb{N}
, the
d
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
is the
d
-times Cartesian product
S^{1}\times\cdots\times S^{1}
. An important property of Koopman eigenfunctions is that they factor the dynamics onto a rotation on
S^{1}
with frequency
\omega
. This is shown on the left below :

Smooth eigenfunctions

So far we have seen the several utilities of studying the Koopman operator in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mu)
. The spectral theory for 1-parameter group of unitary operators is extremely powerful and complete in Hilbert spaces
EisnerEtAl15
;
DGJ_compactV_2018
;
ValvaGiannakis2023cnstnt

L^{2}(\mu)

-eigenfunctions. A dynamical system can have multiple or even uncountably many coexisting invariant regions, each supporting its own ergodic measure and displaying a different type of dynamics. A classical example is the standard Chirikov map

;

;

eigenfunctions are globally defined, and independent of a choice of ergodic measure. Consider the following system

\begin{split}\frac{d\theta}{dt}&=\vec{\omega}\\

Generating frequencies

Given any two eigenfunctions
z_{1},z_{2}
with eigenfrequencies
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}
, and any two integers
a,b
, the number
a\omega_{1}+b\omega_{2}
is also an eigenfrequency. This is because

\begin{split}U^{t}\left(z_{1}^{a}z_{2}^{b}\right)&=\left(z_{1}^{a}z_{2}^{b}\right)\circ\Phi^{t}=\left(z_{1}^{a}\circ\Phi^{t}\right)\left(z_{2}^{b}\circ\Phi^{t}\right)=\left(z_{1}\circ\Phi^{t}\right)^{a}\left(z_{2}\circ\Phi^{t}\right)^{b}=\left(U^{t}z_{1}\right)^{a}\left(U^{t}z_{2}\right)^{b}=e^{\iota a\omega_{1}}z_{1}^{a}e^{\iota b\omega_{2}}z_{2}^{b}\\
&=e^{\iota(a\omega_{1}+b\omega_{2})}\left(z_{1}^{a}z_{2}^{b}\right).\end{split}

Thus integer linear combinations of frequencies are again frequencies, and products of eigenfunctions are again eigenfunctions. This makes the eigenfrequencies a module over the ring of integers. In particular, if the system has at least one nonzero frequency, then it has all harmonics of that frequency and thus infinitely many frequencies. A collection of eigenfrequencies are said to be independent if no integer linear combination of them is an integer.
If the system has two independent frequencies, then all its frequencies are together dense on the real line.

A collection of frequencies will be called a
basis
or
generating
set of eigenfrequencies if they are independent and all frequencies of the system can be generated by taking integer linear combinations of frequencies from this set. There is no unique choice of a basis, but all bases will have the same dimension
d
, called the quasiperiodicity dimension
d
. In finite-dimensional manifolds, the number
d
is usually observed to be finite
(e.g.
DasGiannakis_delay_2019
, Sec 3)
.
A set of eigenfunctions corresponding to a generating set of frequencies form a
generating set of eigenfunctions

Mixing

A function

is said to be (strongly)
mixing
w.r.t. the ergodic dynamics
\left(\mu,\Phi^{t}\right)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\left\langle U^{t}f,g\right\rangle_{\mu}=0,\quad\forall g\in L^{2}(\mu).

The function

is said to be

w.r.t. the ergodic dynamics
\left(\mu,\Phi^{t}\right)
if

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{t=0}^{T}\left\langle U^{t}f,g\right\rangle_{\mu}dt=0,\quad\forall g\in L^{2}(\mu).

The ergodic dynamics
\left(\mu,\Phi^{t}\right)
will be called weakly / strongly mixing if

is weakly / strongly mixing for every

. Similar definitions can be made for discrete time dynamics, which are described by a map instead of a flow.
Usually if the context is clear, we drop either
\mu

\Phi^{t}
when making a claim about mixing.

Let

be a positive function such that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\alpha(t)=0^{+}
. Then
\alpha
is said to be a rate of (strong) mixing for the flow if

\left|\left\langle U^{t}f,g\right\rangle_{\mu}\right|=O\left(\alpha(t)\right),\quad\mbox{ as }t\to\infty,\quad\forall f,g\in L^{2}(\mu).

One can similarly derive notions of rates of strong and weak mixing for discrete and continuous time maps. Note that rate of mixing so defined, is not unique, but merely an upper bound for the asymptotic rate of decay. Our next result assumes :

As shown in

We next prove Claim (ii). Since the flow

Therefore, since
\tilde{V}
is a linear combination of the two flow invariant vector fields
V
and
\nabla_{\tau}z
,
\tilde{V}
is also invariant under
\Phi^{t}
. We summarize all these invariances below.

\Phi^{t}_{*}|_{x}\tilde{V}(x)=\tilde{V}(\Phi^{t}x),\quad\Phi^{t}_{*}|_{x}V(x)=V(\Phi^{t}x),\quad\Phi^{t}_{*}|_{x}\nabla_{\tau}z(x)=\nabla_{\tau}z(\Phi^{t}x),\quad\forall x\in M.

(29)

We will next show that the two flows commute, i.e.,
\tilde{\Phi}^{t}\Phi^{s}
=
\Phi^{s}\tilde{\Phi}^{t}
for every
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
x\in M
, and define
x^{\prime}(s):=\tilde{\Phi}^{s}(x)
,
x^{\prime\prime}(s):=\Phi^{t}(x^{\prime\prime}(s))
and
x^{\prime\prime\prime}(s):=\tilde{\Phi}^{-s}(x^{\prime}(s))
. We have to show that
x^{\prime\prime\prime}(s)=\Phi^{t}(x)
for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Since this is true for
s=0
, it is equivalent to prove that
\frac{d}{ds}x^{\prime\prime\prime}(s)=0
. This follows from

\begin{split}\frac{d}{ds}x^{\prime\prime\prime}(s)&=\frac{d}{ds}\tilde{\Phi}^{-s}\Phi^{t}\tilde{\Phi}^{s}(x)=\frac{d}{du}|_{u=s}\tilde{\Phi}^{-u}\Phi^{t}\tilde{\Phi}^{s}(x)+\frac{d}{dv}|_{v=s}\tilde{\Phi}^{-s}\Phi^{t}\tilde{\Phi}^{v}(x)\\
&=-\tilde{V}(x^{\prime\prime\prime}(s))+\tilde{\Phi}^{-s}_{*}|_{x^{\prime\prime}(s)}\Phi^{t}_{*}|_{x^{\prime}(s)}\tilde{V}(x^{\prime}(s))\\
&=-\tilde{V}(x^{\prime\prime\prime}(s))+\tilde{\Phi}^{-s}_{*}|_{x^{\prime\prime}(s)}\tilde{V}(x^{\prime\prime}(s)),\quad\mbox{by }\eqref{eqn:inv_vector},\\
&=-\tilde{V}(x^{\prime\prime\prime}(s))+\tilde{V}(x^{\prime\prime\prime}(s))=0.\end{split}

The last equality above again follows from the invariance of the vector field
\tilde{V}
under the flow
\tilde{\Phi}^{t}
that it generates. This completes the proof of Claim (ii). This completes the proof of Theorem
6
. See Figure
4
for an illustration. ∎

7.6  Proof of Theorem
7

The proof of Claim (i) is analogous to the proof of Theorem
3
(iii), and will be omitted. We next prove Claim (iv). By (
13
) we have

V\zeta=\tilde{V}\zeta+\omega\nabla_{\tau}z\zeta.

The first differential operator acts only along the tangent subspaces of the leaves of
z
. The second differential operator acts along the vector field normal to the flow. Due to the local product structure,

V\zeta=\iota\omega^{\prime}\zeta+\iota\omega z\zeta.

Thus
\zeta
satisfies the eigenfunction equation with eigenfrequency
\omega+\omega^{\prime}
. If
\tilde{\zeta}
is a
C^{r}
eigenfunction, then so is
\zeta
. Suppose
\tilde{\zeta}
is an
L^{2}(\tilde{\mu})
eigenfunction. By Theorem
3
(iii) , since the flow
\Phi^{t}
preserves the conditional measures of
\mu
along the fibres of
\mathcal{F}
, the set
M^{\prime}
on which the eigenvalue equation holds has full
\mu
-measure. Thus,
\zeta
is an
L^{2}(\mu)
eigenfunction.

By Lemma
4.1
, the
d
vector fields
\nabla_{\tau}z_{1},\ldots,\nabla_{\tau}z_{d}
are linearly independent everywhere on
M
, for any choice of a Riemannian metric
\tau
. Thus
\tau
can be chosen so that these vector fields are mutually orthogonal and each of constant unit norm. As a result, using the identity (
12
) for the gradient, we get

\tilde{V}(z_{j})=V(z_{j})-\omega\nabla_{\tau}z_{1}(z_{j})=\iota\omega_{j}z_{j}-i\omega\langle\nabla_{\tau}z_{1},\nabla_{\tau}z_{j}\rangle_{\tau}z_{j}=\iota\omega_{j}z_{j},\quad j=2,\ldots,d.

This completes the proof of Claim (iii) and of Theorem
7
. ∎
