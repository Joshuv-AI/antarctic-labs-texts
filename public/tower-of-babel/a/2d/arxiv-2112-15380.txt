Tail processes and tail measures: An approach via Palm calculus
Günter Last

Abstract
Using an intrinsic approach, we study some properties of random fields which appear as tail fields of regularly varying stationary random fields. The index set is allowed to be a general locally compact Hausdorff Abelian group 𝔾 {\mathbb{G}} . The values are taken in a measurable cone, equipped with a pseudo norm. We first discuss some Palm formulas for the exceedance random measure ξ \xi associated with a stationary (measurable) random field Y = ( Y s ) s ∈ 𝔾 Y=(Y_{s})_{s\in{\mathbb{G}}} . It is important to allow the underlying stationary measure to be σ \sigma -finite. Then we proceed to a random field (defined on a probability space) which is spectrally decomposable, in a sense which is motivated by extreme value theory. We characterize mass-stationarity of the exceedance random measure in terms of a suitable version of the classical Mecke equation. We also show that the associated stationary measure is homogeneous, that is a tail measure. We then proceed with establishing and studying the spectral representation of stationary tail measures and with characterizing a moving shift representation. Finally we discuss anchoring maps and the candidate extremal index.



Keywords:
tail process, exceedances, tail measure,
spectral representation, random measure,
Palm measure, stationarity, mass-stationarity, locally compact Abelian group,
anchoring map, candidate extremal index

2
Some Palm calculus

Assume that
{\mathbb{G}}
is a locally compact Hausdorff group with Borel
\sigma
-field

{\mathcal{G}}
and (non-trivial) Haar measure
\lambda
.
Important special cases are
{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
with
\lambda
being the counting measure
and
{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
\lambda
being the Lebesgue measure.
Let
{\mathbf{M}}
denote the space of measures on
{\mathbb{G}}
which are locally finite
(that is, finite on compact sets) and let
{\mathcal{M}}
be the smallest
\sigma
-field
on
{\mathbf{M}}
making the mappings
\mu\mapsto\mu(B)

measurable for all
B\subset{\mathbb{G}}
.
Let
{\mathbf{N}}
be the measurable subset of
{\mathbf{M}}
of those
\mu\in{\mathbf{M}}

which are integer-valued on relatively compact Borel sets.
Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
be a
\sigma
-finite measure space.
At the moment the reader might think of
\mathbb{P}
as of a probability measure.
However, for our later purposes it is important to allow for
\mathbb{P}(\Omega)=\infty
.
Still we shall use a probabilistic language.
A
random measure
(resp.
point process
)
\xi
on
{\mathbb{G}}
is
a measurable mapping
\xi\colon\Omega\rightarrow{\mathbf{M}}

(resp.
\xi\colon\Omega\rightarrow{\mathbf{N}}
). We find it convenient
to use this terminology even without reference to a (probability) measure
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
. We often use the kernel notation

\xi(\omega,B):=\xi(\omega)(B)
,
(\omega,B)\in\Omega\times\mathcal{G}
.
A point process
\xi
is said to be
simple
, if

\xi(\omega,\{s\})\leq 1
for all
(\omega,s)\in\Omega\times{\mathbb{G}}
.

Next we give a short but self-contained introduction
into Palm calculus, using the setting from
[
16
]
and
[
14
]
.
A more comprehensive summary can be found in
[
12
]
.
Assume that
{\mathbb{G}}
acts measurably on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
.
This means that there is a family
of measurable mappings
\theta_{s}\colon\Omega\to\Omega
,
s\in{\mathbb{G}}
,
such that
(\omega,s)\mapsto\theta_{s}\omega
is measurable,

\theta_{0}
is the identity on
\Omega
and

\displaystyle\theta_{s}\circ\theta_{t}=\theta_{s+t},\quad s,t\in{\mathbb{G}},

(2.1)

where
\circ
denotes composition. The family
\{\theta_{s}:s\in{\mathbb{G}}\}

is said to be (measurable)
flow
on
\Omega
.
A random measure on
{\mathbb{G}}

is said to be
invariant
(w.r.t. to the flow) or
flow-adapted
if

\displaystyle\xi(\omega,B+s)=\xi(\theta_{s}\omega,B),\quad\omega\in\Omega,\,s\in{\mathbb{G}},B\in{\mathcal{G}}.

(2.2)

Let us illustrate these concepts with two examples.

Example 2.1
.

Assume that
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})=({\mathbf{M}},\mathcal{M})

and define
\theta_{s}\mu:=\mu(\cdot+s)
, for
\mu\in{\mathbf{M}}
and
s\in{\mathbb{G}}
.
Then
\{\theta_{s}:s\in{\mathbb{G}}\}
is a flow and the identity on
{\mathbf{M}}
is
invariant.

Example 2.2
.

Let
{\mathbb{H}}
be a (non-empty) Polish space equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{H}

and consider the space
{\mathbb{H}}^{\mathbb{G}}
of all functions
\omega\colon{\mathbb{G}}\to{\mathbb{H}}
.
For each
s\in{\mathbb{G}}
we define the shift-operator

\theta_{s}\colon{\mathbb{H}}^{\mathbb{G}}\to{\mathbb{H}}^{\mathbb{G}}
by
\theta_{s}\omega:=\omega(\cdot+s)
.
Assume now that
{\mathbf{F}}
is shift-invariant subset of
{\mathbb{H}}^{\mathbb{G}}
equipped with a
\sigma
-field

\mathcal{F}
such that
(\omega,s)\mapsto(\theta_{s}\omega,\omega(0))
is
measurable with respect to
\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{H}
. For instance we can take

{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
{\mathbb{H}}={\mathbb{R}}
,
{\mathbf{F}}
as the
Skorohod space
of all càdlàg functions
(see e.g.
[
8
]
)
and
\mathcal{F}
as the smallest
\sigma
-field rendering the mappings

\omega\mapsto\omega(t)
,
t\in{\mathbb{G}}
, measurable.
Then even
(\omega,t)\mapsto\omega(t)
is measurable and
therefore also
(\omega,s)\mapsto\theta_{s}\omega
, as required.
An example of an invariant random measure (defined on
{\mathbf{F}}
) is

\xi(\omega):=\int{\bf 1}\{t\in\cdot\}f(\theta_{t}\omega)\,\lambda(dt)
,
where
f\colon{\mathbf{F}}\to[0,\infty)
is measurable and bounded.

In view of the preceding examples it is helpful
to think of
\theta_{s}\omega
as of
\omega

shifted
by
s
.
A measure
\mathbb{P}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
is called

stationary
if it is invariant under the flow, i.e.

\mathbb{P}\circ\theta_{s}=\mathbb{P},\quad s\in{\mathbb{G}},

where
\theta_{s}
is interpreted as a mapping from
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{A}

in the usual way:

\theta_{s}A:=\{\theta_{s}\omega:\omega\in A\},\quad A\in\mathcal{A},\,s\in{\mathbb{G}}.

Throughout the paper
\mathbb{P}
will denote a
\sigma
-finite stationary measure
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
.

Let
B\in{\mathcal{G}}
be a set with positive and finite Haar measure
\lambda(B)

and
\xi
be an invariant random measure on
{\mathbb{G}}
.
The measure

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\xi}(A):=\lambda(B)^{-1}\iint{\bf 1}_{A}(\theta_{s}\omega){\bf 1}_{B}(s)\,\xi(\omega,ds)\,\mathbb{P}(d\omega),\quad A\in\mathcal{A},

(2.3)

is called the
Palm measure
of
\xi
(with respect to
\mathbb{P}
).

For discrete groups the previous definition becomes very simple:

Example 2.3
.

Assume that
{\mathbb{G}}
is discrete.
Then we can take
B:=\{0\}
and obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\xi}(A)=\mathbb{E}{\bf 1}_{A}\xi(\{0\}).

The
intensity
of
\xi
is the number
\gamma_{\xi}:=\mathbb{E}[\xi(B)]=\mathbb{P}_{\xi}(\Omega)
.
If this intensity is positive and finite then the normalized Palm measure

\mathbb{P}^{0}_{\xi}:=\gamma_{\xi}^{-1}\mathbb{P}_{\xi}

is called
Palm probability measure
of
\xi
(w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}
).
Note that
\mathbb{P}_{\xi}
and
\mathbb{P}^{0}_{\xi}

are defined on the underlying space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
.
The
Palm distribution
of
\xi
is the distribution
\mathbb{P}^{0}_{\xi}(\xi\in\cdot)

of
\xi
under
\mathbb{P}^{0}_{\xi}
.
If
\xi
is a
simple
point process (that is
\xi(\{s\})\leq 1
for all
s\in{\mathbb{G}}
),
the number
\mathbb{P}_{\xi}^{0}(A)
can be interpreted as the conditional probability of

A\in\mathcal{A}
given that
\xi
has a point at
0\in{\mathbb{G}}
.

In the general case the Palm measure
\mathbb{P}_{\xi}
is
\sigma
-finite.
Moreover, if
\gamma_{\xi}>0
and
A\in\mathcal{A}
is flow-invariant
(that is
\theta_{s}A=A
for each
s\in{\mathbb{G}}
), then

\mathbb{P}(A)=0
iff
\mathbb{P}_{\xi}(A)=0
.
Since the definition (
2.3
)
does not depend on
B
, we have the
refined Campbell theorem

\displaystyle\iint f(\theta_{s}\omega,s)\,\xi(\omega,ds)\,\mathbb{P}(d\omega)=\iint f(\omega,s)\,\lambda(ds)\,\mathbb{P}_{\xi}(d\omega)

(2.4)

for all measurable
f\colon\Omega\times{\mathbb{G}}\to[0,\infty]
.
We write this as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int f(\theta_{s},s)\,\xi(ds)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\xi}}\left[\int f(\theta_{0},s)\,\lambda(ds)\right],

(2.5)

where
\mathbb{E}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\xi}}
denote integration with respect to
\mathbb{P}

and
\mathbb{P}_{\xi}
, respectively. Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\xi}(\xi({\mathbb{G}})=0)=0.

(2.6)

If
\xi
is a point process, then
\mathbb{P}_{\xi}
is concentrated on the event

\{\omega\in\Omega:\xi(\omega,\{0\})\geq 1\}
.

Let
\xi
be an invariant random measure on
{\mathbb{G}}
and
let
\tilde{h}\colon\Omega\times{\mathbb{G}}\to[0,\infty]
be a measurable function such

\int\tilde{h}(\theta_{0},s)\,\xi(ds)={\bf 1}\{\xi({\mathbb{G}})>0\}

\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
Then the refined Campbell theorem implies the

inversion formula

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\bf 1}\{\xi({\mathbb{G}})>0\}f=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\xi}}\int f(\theta_{-s})\tilde{h}(\theta_{-s},s)\,\lambda(ds),

(2.7)

for each measurable
f\colon\Omega\to[0,\infty]
;
see also
[
15
]
.
This shows that the
restriction of the measure
\mathbb{P}
to
\{\xi({\mathbb{G}})>0\}

is uniquely determined by
\mathbb{Q}
.

Let
\xi
and
\eta
be two invariant random measures on
{\mathbb{G}}

and
g\colon\Omega\times{\mathbb{G}}\to[0,\infty]
be measurable.
Neveu’s
[
16
]

exchange formula
says that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\xi}}\left[\int g(\theta_{0},s)\,\eta(ds)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\eta}}\left[\int g(\theta_{s},-s)\,\xi(ds)\right].

(2.8)

Allocations, point- and mass-stationarity

As in Section
2
we consider a measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})

equipped with a measurable flow
\{\theta_{s}:s\in{\mathbb{G}}\}
and a stationary

\sigma
-finite measure
\mathbb{P}
.

A measurable function
\tau\colon\Omega\times{\mathbb{G}}\to{\mathbb{G}}\cup\{\infty\}

(it is understood here that
\infty\notin{\mathbb{G}}
)
is said to be an
allocation
, if it satisfies
the covariance property

\displaystyle\tau(\theta_{t}\omega,s-t)=\tau(\omega,s)-t,\quad s,t\in{\mathbb{G}},\,\omega\in\Omega,

(3.1)

where
\infty-t:=\infty
.
Given such an allocation we define the (random) sets

\displaystyle C_{\tau}(s):=\{t\in{\mathbb{G}}:\tau(t)=s\},\quad s\in{\mathbb{G}},

(3.2)

where, as usual,
\tau(t):=\tau(\cdot,t)
.

The following result generalizes
[
12
, Theorem 4.1]
.
The latter arises in the special case where
{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and

\xi
equals Lebesgue measure. We denote by
\supp\mu
the
support

of a measure
\mu
on
{\mathbb{G}}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose that
\xi
is an invariant random
measure and that
\eta
is a simple invariant point process.
Let
\tau
be an allocation satisfying

\displaystyle\tau(s)\in\supp\eta\cup\{\infty\},\quad\xi\text{-a.e.\ $s\in{\mathbb{G}}$},\,\mathbb{P}\text{-a.e.}

Let
h\colon\Omega\times\Omega\to[0,\infty]
be
measurable. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\xi}}{\bf 1}\{\tau(0)\neq\infty\}h(\theta_{0},\theta_{\tau(0)})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\eta}}\int_{C_{\tau}(0)}h(\theta_{s},\theta_{0})\,\xi(ds).

(3.3)

Proof.

It follows from (
2.2
) and (
3.1
)
that the event consisting of all
\omega\in\Omega
satisfying

\displaystyle\xi(\omega,\{s\in{\mathbb{G}}:\tau(\omega,s)\notin(\supp\eta(\omega)\cup\{\infty\}\})=0

is shift-invariant. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\xi}(\tau(0)\notin(\supp\eta\cup\{\infty\}))=0.

(3.4)

The proof proceeds now as the one of
[
12
, Theorem 4.1]
, applying
the exchange formula (
2.8
)
instead of the refined Campbell theorem.
We apply (
2.8
)
with the function
(\omega,s)\mapsto h(\omega,\theta_{s}\omega){\bf 1}\{\tau(\omega,0)=s\}
.
In view of (
3.4
), the left-hand coincides with the left-hand side of
(
3.3
). The right-hand side equals

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\eta}}\int h(\theta_{s},\theta_{0}){\bf 1}\{\tau(\theta_{s},0)=-s\}\,\xi(ds).

Since
\tau(\theta_{s},0)=\tau(\theta_{0},s)-s
, this equals the
right-hand side of (
3.3
).
∎

Let
\xi
,
\eta
and
\tau
be as in Proposition
3.1

and assume moreover that
\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
\xi(C_{\tau}(s))=1
for all
s\in\supp\eta
.
Then (
3.3
) implies the
shift-coupling

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\xi}}{\bf 1}\{\tau(0)\neq\infty\}{\bf 1}\{\theta_{\tau(0)}\in\cdot)=\mathbb{P}_{\eta}.

(3.5)

The additional assumption on
\tau
is equivalent to the
balancing
property

\displaystyle\int{\bf 1}\{\tau(s)\neq\infty,\tau(s)\in\cdot\}\,\xi(ds)=\eta,\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.e.}

(3.6)

Since the above balancing event is easily seen to be flow-invariant,
equation (
3.6
) does also hold
\mathbb{P}_{\xi}
-a.e. and
\mathbb{P}_{\eta}
-a.e. Of particular interest is the case
\xi=\eta
.
Then (
3.6
) implies
\mathbb{P}
-a.e. that
\tau(s)\neq\infty
for all
s\in\supp\xi
and (
3.6
)
means that
\tau(\omega,\cdot)
induces for
\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
\omega
a
bijection between the points of
\supp\xi
. We say that

\tau
is a
bijective point map
for
\xi
w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}
(see
[
21
,
7
]
)
and use this terminology also for other measures
\mathbb{P}
.

Given an invariant simple point process
\xi
and a measure
\mathbb{Q}

on
\Omega
, we call
\xi

point-stationary
if
\mathbb{Q}(0\notin\supp\xi)=0

and
\mathbb{Q}(\theta_{\tau(0)}\in\cdot)=\mathbb{Q}
holds for each
bijective point map
\tau
for
\xi
w.r.t.
\mathbb{Q}
.
It was proved in
[
7
]
that a
\sigma
-finite measure

\mathbb{Q}
on
\Omega
is point-stationary iff it is the Palm measure
of
\xi
with respect to some
\sigma
-finite stationary measure
on
\Omega
. A key ingredient of the proof is the following
intrinsic characterization of general Palm measures; see
[
15
, Satz 2.5]
.
Mecke proved his fundamental result in a canonical setting.
As discussed in
[
14
]
his proof applies in our more general framework.

Theorem 3.2

(Mecke 1967)
.

Let
\xi
be an invariant
random measure on
{\mathbb{G}}
and
\mathbb{Q}
be a
\sigma
-finite measure
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
. Then
\mathbb{Q}
is the Palm measure of
\xi

w.r.t. a
\sigma
-finite stationary measure on
\Omega
iff
\mathbb{Q}(\xi({\mathbb{G}})=0)=0
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int g(\theta_{s},-s)\,\xi(ds)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int g(\theta_{0},s)\,\xi(ds)

(3.7)

for all measurable
g\colon\Omega\times{\mathbb{G}}\to[0,\infty]
.
Equation (
3.7
) determines the stationary measure
on
\{\xi({\mathbb{G}})>0\}
.

The final assertion of Theorem
3.2
follows from
the inversion formula (
2.7
).
.

Point stationarity was extended in
[
14
]
to

mass-stationarity
of an invariant random measure
\xi
w.r.t. a given

\sigma
-finite measure
\mathbb{Q}
on
\Omega
.
Roughly speaking, mass-stationarity of
\xi
can be described
as follows. Let
C\in\mathcal{G}
be a relatively compact set
with positive Haar measure whose boundary is not charged by
\lambda
.
Let
U
be a random element of
{\mathbb{G}}
, independent of
\xi

and with distribution
\lambda(C)^{-1}\lambda(C\cap\cdot)
.
Given
(\xi,U)
pick a random point
V
according to the normalized
restriction of
\lambda
to
C-U
. If
\mathbb{Q}
is the Palm measure
of
\xi
w.r.t. some stationary measure, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}((\xi\circ\theta_{V},U+V)\in\cdot)=\mathbb{Q}((\xi,U)\in\cdot).

As shown by
[
14
, Theorem 6.3]
, a version of this property
(assumed to be true for all
C
as above) is equivalent
to (
3.7
) and hence provides another intrinsic chracterization
of Palm measures.
Justified by this result we call
\xi

mass-stationary
(w.r.t.
\mathbb{Q}
)
if (
3.7
) holds.
In this paper
\mathbb{Q}
will always denote a probability measure
while, as a rule,
\mathbb{P}
is only
\sigma
-finite.

4
Exceedance random measures

Let
{\mathbb{H}}
be a (non-empty) Polish space equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{H}
.
Assume that
|\cdot|\colon{\mathbb{H}}\to[0,\infty)
is a measurable mapping.
One might think of
{\mathbb{H}}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
equipped with the Euclidean norm.

In this and later sections we consider a measurable mapping

Y\colon\Omega\times{\mathbb{G}}\to{\mathbb{H}}
. For
s\in{\mathbb{G}}
we write
Y_{s}

for the random variable
\omega\mapsto Y_{s}(\omega)
.
Then
Y
can be considered as a (measurable) random field
(Y_{s})_{s\in{\mathbb{G}}}
.
We assume the shift-covariance

\displaystyle Y_{s}(\theta_{t}\omega)=Y_{s+t}(\omega),\quad(\omega,s,t)\in\Omega\times{\mathbb{G}}\times{\mathbb{G}}.

(4.1)

We call

\displaystyle\xi:=\int{\bf 1}\{s\in\cdot,|Y_{s}|>1\}\,\lambda(ds)

(4.2)

the
exceedance measure
of
Y
.
By (
4.1
) this is an invariant random measure.
If
{\mathbb{G}}
is discrete,
\xi
is a simple point process.
The Palm measure of
\xi
takes a rather simple form:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathbb{P}
be
a
\sigma
-finite stationary measure on
\Omega
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\xi}=\mathbb{P}(\cdot\cap\{|Y_{0}|>1\}).

(4.3)

Proof.

Let
B\in\mathcal{G}
satisfy
\lambda(B)=1
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\xi}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\int{\bf 1}\{\theta_{s}\in\cdot\}{\bf 1}\{s\in B\}\,\xi(ds)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\int{\bf 1}\{\theta_{s}\in\cdot\}{\bf 1}\{s\in B,|Y_{s}|>1\}\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\int_{B}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[{\bf 1}\{\theta_{s}\in\cdot,|Y_{s}|>1\}]\,\lambda(ds)=\int_{B}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[{\bf 1}\{\theta_{0}\in\cdot,|Y_{0}|>1\}]\,\lambda(ds),

where we have used stationarity of
\mathbb{P}
to get the final equation.
This proves the assertion.
∎

In this paper we will mostly be concerned
with a probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})

such that
\xi
is mass-stationary w.r.t.
\mathbb{Q}
.
In this case Theorem
3.2
shows that
there exists a unique
\sigma
-finite stationary measure

\mathbb{P}
on
\Omega
satisfying
\mathbb{P}_{\xi}=\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{P}(\xi({\mathbb{G}})=0)=0
.
If
\mathbb{Q}(\xi({\mathbb{G}})<\infty)=1
then
\mathbb{P}
cannot be finite:

Remark 4.2
.

Let
\mathbb{Q}
be as above and
assume that
\mathbb{Q}(0<\xi({\mathbb{G}})<\infty)=1
.
Define a measure
\mathbb{P}
on
\Omega
by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\xi({\mathbb{G}})^{-1}\int{\bf 1}\{\theta_{s}\in\cdot\}\,\lambda(ds).

(4.4)

Since
\xi({\mathbb{G}})
is invariant under shifts, this measure is stationary.
Let
f\colon{\mathbf{M}}\to[0,\infty]
be measurable and
B\in{\mathcal{G}}
with

\lambda(B)=1
. A simple calculation (using invariance of
\xi

and Fubini’s theorem) shows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\int{\bf 1}\{s\in B\}f\circ\theta_{s}\,\xi(ds)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\xi({\mathbb{G}})^{-1}\int f\circ\theta_{s}\,\xi(ds).

Since we have assumed
\xi
to be mass-stationary,
we can use the Mecke equation (
3.7
) to find that
the latter expression equals
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}f
.
Hence
\mathbb{Q}
is the Palm measure of
\xi
.
Note that
\mathbb{P}(\Omega)=\infty
, unless
\lambda({\mathbb{G}})<\infty
.

5
Spectrally decomposable fields

In this and later sections we take
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
as the function space

({\mathbf{F}},\mathcal{F})
satisfying the assumptions of Example
2.2
.
In addition we assume that
{\mathbb{H}}
is a
measurable cone
, that
is, there exists a measurable mapping
(u,x)\mapsto u\cdot x

from
(0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{H}}

{\mathbb{H}}
such that
1\cdot x=x
and

u\cdot(v\cdot x)=(uv)\cdot x
for all
x\in{\mathbb{H}}
and
u,v\in(0,\infty)
.
We mostly write
ux
instead of
u\cdot x
.
The function
|\cdot|
is assumed to be homogeneous,
that is
|ux|=u|x|
for all
u>0
and
x\in{\mathbb{H}}
.
If
\omega\in{\mathbb{H}}^{\mathbb{G}}

u\cdot\omega\equiv u\omega
is the function in

{\mathbb{H}}^{\mathbb{G}}
given by

,
s\in{\mathbb{G}}
.
We assume that
{\mathbf{F}}
is closed under the action of
(0,\infty)
. The

\sigma
-field
\mathcal{F}
has been assumed to render the mapping

we assume now in addition that
the mapping
(\omega,u)\mapsto u\cdot\omega

is measurable on
{\mathbf{F}}\times(0,\infty)
.
If

is discrete, then we take
{\mathbf{F}}={\mathbb{H}}^{\mathbb{G}}
and equip

\sigma
-algebra.
We write
Y_{s}
for the mapping
\omega\mapsto\omega(s)
,

s\in{\mathbb{G}}
, and note that
(\omega,s)\mapsto Y_{s}(\omega)
is measurable.
We also write
Y:=(Y_{s})_{s\in{\mathbb{G}}}
, which is simply the identity on
{\mathbf{F}}
.
We define another random field

, by
W_{s}:=|Y_{0}|^{-1}Y_{s}
if

and by

W_{s}:=x_{0}
otherwise, where

is some fixed element of

with

. Since we do not assume

to contain a zero element

whenever

and

.

Remark 5.1
.

Assume that
{\mathbf{F}}^{\prime}\subset{\mathbb{H}}^{\mathbb{G}}
is shift-invariant
and closed under the action of
(0,\infty)
. Assume that

{\mathbf{F}}^{\prime}
is equipped with the Kolmogorov product

\sigma
-field, that is, the smallest
\sigma
-field making
the mappings
\omega\mapsto\omega(s)
(from
{\mathbf{F}}^{\prime}
to
{\mathbb{H}}
) measurable for each
s\in{\mathbb{G}}
.
Assume, moreover, that

(\omega,s)\mapsto\omega(s)
is a (jointly) measurable mapping
on
{\mathbf{F}}^{\prime}\times{\mathbb{G}}
. Then it is easy to see that

(\omega,s,u)\mapsto(\theta_{s}\omega,u\cdot\omega)
is a measurable
function on
{\mathbf{F}}^{\prime}\times{\mathbb{G}}\times(0,\infty)
. This shows that
for a proper choice of

, the product
\sigma
-field is a natural candidate for

.

We often consider a probability measure

on

with the following properties.
The probability measure
\mathbb{Q}(|Y_{0}|\in\cdot)
is a Pareto distribution
on

with parameter

and

is independent of
|Y_{0}|
. To achieve this,
we take a probability measure
\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}
on

such that

\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}(|Y_{0}|=1)=1

and define

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}:=\iint{\bf 1}\{u\omega\in\cdot,u>1\}\,\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}(d\omega)\,du.

(5.1)

For the special groups
{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and
{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{R}}
such processes
occur in extreme value theory; see the seminal paper
[
1
]

(treating

) and
[

,
3
,
20
]
.
Note that

is a measurable function of

, that

and that the pair

has the desired properties. We say that

is

(w.r.t.
\mathbb{Q}
)
or, synonomously, that

is spectrally decomposable.

Define the exceedance random measure
\xi
by (

).
If
\mathbb{Q}
is given as in (

), it is natural to characterize
mass-stationarity of
\xi
(w.r.t.
\mathbb{Q}
) in terms
of suitable invariance properties of the field
W
.
In the context of tail processes the following property
(
5.2
) was proved in
[
1
]
in the case
{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{Z}}

(see also
[
3
,
18
]
) and in the case
{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{R}}

in
[
20
]
.
The fact that (
5.2
) implies mass-stationarity
in the case
{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
was derived in
[
17
]
, exploiting the connection to
regularly varying random fields. We use here an intrinsic
non-asymptotic approach. It is worth noticing that
[
9
]

identifies (

) (in the case
{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{Z}}
) as being characteristic
for the tail processes introduced in
[
1
]
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Assume that
Y
is spectrally decomposable with index
\alpha
.
Then the exceedance random measure
\xi
is mass-stationary if
and only if

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int g(\theta_{s}W,-s){\bf 1}\{|W_{s}|>0\}\,\lambda(ds)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int g(|W_{s}|^{-1}W,s)|W_{s}|^{\alpha}\,\lambda(ds),

(5.2)

holds for all measurable
g\colon{\mathbf{F}}\times{\mathbb{G}}\to[0,\infty]
.

Proof.

Let us first assume that
\xi
is mass-stationary.
We generalize the arguments from the proof of Lemma 2.2 in
[
18
]
.
Let
h\colon{\mathbf{F}}\times{\mathbb{G}}\to[0,\infty]
be measurable
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. Then

\displaystyle I:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}

\displaystyle\int h(\theta_{s}Y,-s){\bf 1}\{|Y_{s}|>\varepsilon\}\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\iint h(u\theta_{s}W,-s){\bf 1}\{u|W_{s}|>\varepsilon\}{\bf 1}\{u>1\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\iint h(\varepsilon v\theta_{s}W,-s){\bf 1}\{v|W_{s}|>1\}{\bf 1}\{v>1/\varepsilon\}\alpha v^{-\alpha-1}\,dv\,\lambda(ds),

where we have made the change of variables
v:=u/\varepsilon
to
get the second identity. Since
1/\varepsilon>1
we obtain that

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int h(\varepsilon\theta_{s}Y,-s){\bf 1}\{|Y_{s}|>1\}{\bf 1}\{|Y_{0}|>1/\varepsilon\}\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int h(\varepsilon\theta_{s}Y,-s){\bf 1}\{|Y_{0}|>1/\varepsilon\}\,\xi(ds).

Using now the assumption (
3.7
) together with

Y_{0}=(Y\circ\theta_{s})_{-s}
we arrive at

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int h(\varepsilon Y,s){\bf 1}\{|Y_{s}|>1/\varepsilon\}\,\xi(ds),

that is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}

\displaystyle\int h(\theta_{s}Y,-s){\bf 1}\{|Y_{s}|>\varepsilon\}\,\lambda(ds)=\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int h(\varepsilon Y,s){\bf 1}\{|Y_{s}|>1/\varepsilon\}\,\lambda(ds).

(5.3)

We apply this with
h(Y,s):=g(|Y_{s}|^{-1}Y,s)
for some measurable function
g\colon{\mathbf{F}}\times{\mathbb{G}}\to[0,\infty]
,
noting that

h(\theta_{s}Y,-s)=g(|(\theta_{s}Y)_{-s}|^{-1}\theta_{s}Y,-s)=g(|Y_{0}|^{-1}\theta_{s}Y,-s)=g(\theta_{s}W,-s).

Using monotone convergence this yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int g(\theta_{s}W,-s)

\displaystyle{\bf 1}\{|W_{s}|>0\}\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int g(|W_{s}|^{-1}W,s){\bf 1}\{|Y_{0}||W_{s}|>1/\varepsilon\}\,\lambda(ds).

We have that

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}

\displaystyle\int g(|W_{s}|^{-1}W,s){\bf 1}\{|Y_{0}||W_{s}|>1/\varepsilon\}\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int g(|W_{s}|^{-1}W,s){\bf 1}\{u|W_{s}|>1/\varepsilon\}{\bf 1}\{u>1\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int g(|W_{s}|^{-1}W,s)\min\{|W_{s}|^{\alpha}\varepsilon^{\alpha},1\}\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int g(|W_{s}|^{-1}W,s)\min\{|W_{s}|^{\alpha},\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\}\,\lambda(ds).

As
\varepsilon\to 0
the latter term tends to

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int g(|W_{s}|^{-1}W,s)|W_{s}|^{\alpha}\,\lambda(ds)
,
yielding (
5.2
).

To prove the converse implication we assume that
(
5.2
) holds. We take a measurable

g\colon{\mathbf{F}}\times{\mathbb{G}}\to[0,\infty]
and aim at
establishing (
3.7
). We have that

\displaystyle I^{\prime}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int

\displaystyle g(\theta_{s}Y,-s)\,\xi(ds)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int g(\theta_{s}Y,-s){\bf 1}\{|Y_{s}|>1\}\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\int\bigg[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int g(u\theta_{s}W,-s){\bf 1}\{u|W_{s}|>1\}\,\lambda(ds)\bigg]{\bf 1}\{u>1\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du.

For each
u>1
we can apply (
5.2
) with the function

\tilde{h}(\omega,s)=g(u\omega,s){\bf 1}\{u|\omega(0)|>1\}
. Then

\tilde{h}(\theta_{s}W,-s)=g(u\theta_{s}W,-s){\bf 1}\{u|W_{s}|>1\}

and

\tilde{h}(|W_{s}|^{-1}W,s)=g(u|W_{s}|^{-1}W,s){\bf 1}\{u|W_{s}|^{-1}>1\}

Therefore

\displaystyle I^{\prime}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\iint g(u|W_{s}|^{-1}W,s)|W_{s}|^{\alpha}{\bf 1}\{u|W_{s}|^{-1}>1,u>1\}\,\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du\,\lambda(ds).

In the above inner integral we can assume that
|W_{s}|>0
.
After the change of variables
v:=|W_{s}|^{-1}u
we obtain that

\displaystyle I^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\iint g(vW,s){\bf 1}\{v>1,v|W_{s}|>1\}\,\alpha v^{-\alpha-1}\,dv\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\iint g(Y,s){\bf 1}\{|Y_{s}|>1\}\,\lambda(ds),

establishing (
3.7
).
∎

Remark 5.3
.

The equations (
5.2
) are clearly equivalent
to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}h(\theta_{-s}W){\bf 1}\{|W_{-s}|>0\}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}h(|W_{s}|^{-1}W)|W_{s}|^{\alpha},\quad\lambda\text{-a.e.\ $s\in{\mathbb{G}}$},

(5.4)

for all measurable
h\colon{\mathbf{F}}\to[0,\infty]
.
They are also equivalent to the equations

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int g(W,-s){\bf 1}\{|W_{s}|>0\}\,\lambda(ds)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int g(|W_{s}|^{-1}\theta_{s}W,s)|W_{s}|^{\alpha}\,\lambda(ds).

(5.5)

as well as to the equations

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int g(W,-s)|W_{s}|^{\alpha}\,\lambda(ds)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int g(|W_{s}|^{-1}\theta_{s}W,s){\bf 1}\{|W_{s}|>0\}\,\lambda(ds).

(5.6)

To see the latter equivalence, we can use the function

\tilde{h}\colon{\mathbf{F}}\times{\mathbb{G}}\to[0,\infty)
given by

\tilde{h}(\omega,s):=|\omega(-s)|
.
If

we have that

\tilde{h}(|W_{s}|^{-1}\theta_{s}W,s)=|W_{s}|^{-1}
.
Applying (
5.5
) with

instead of

yields (
5.6
).

Remark 5.4
.

Assume that
{\mathbb{G}}
is discrete
and that
Y
is spectrally decomposable with index
\alpha
.
Then
\xi
is mass-stationary iff

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}g(\theta_{-s}W){\bf 1}\{|W_{-s}|>0\}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}g(|W_{s}|^{-1}W)|W_{s}|^{\alpha}

Palm theory would still guarantee
the existence of stationary measure
\mathbb{P}
(uniquely determined
on
\{\xi({\mathbb{G}})>0\}
) such that
\mathbb{P}_{\xi}=\mathbb{P}(\cdot\cap\{|Y_{0}|>1\})=\mathbb{Q}
.

6
Tail measures

In this section we let
({\mathbf{F}},\mathcal{F})
be as in Section
5
.
Throughout we work with the exceedance random measure
\xi
(defined by
(
4.2
)) and the random measure
\xi^{\prime}
, defined by
(
5.9
).
We say that a measure
\nu
on
{\mathbf{F}}
is a
tail measure
if

\displaystyle\int{\bf 1}\{|Y_{s}|>0\}\,\lambda(ds)>0,\quad\nu\text{-a.e.},

(6.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\xi(B)<\infty,\quad\text{$B\in{\mathcal{G}}$ compact},

(6.2)

and if there exists an
\alpha>0
such that
\nu
is

\alpha
-homogeneous
, that is

\displaystyle\nu(uY\in\cdot)=u^{\alpha}\nu(Y\in\cdot),\quad u>0.

(6.3)

In accordance with the literature we call
\alpha
the
index
of
\nu
.

This definition extends the one in
[
3
]
.
A rather general (but slightly different) definition of a tail measure has
very recently been given in
[
2
]
.
In this paper we are mostly interested in stationary tail measures.
In this case (
6.2
) implies that

B\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\xi(B)
(the intensity measure of
\xi
) is a finite multiple
of the Haar measure
\lambda
. In accordance with the
literature we shall always assume then, that this
multiple equals
1
, that is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\int{\bf 1}\{s\in B,|Y_{s}|>1\}\,\lambda(ds)=\lambda(B),\quad B\in{\mathcal{G}},

(6.4)

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle\nu(|Y_{0}|>1)=1.

(6.5)

Up to Remark
6.1
our definition of a stationary
tail measure generalizes the one given
[
20
]
in the case
{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{R}}
.

If
\mathbb{Q}
is a probability measure on
{\mathbf{F}}
such that
\mathbb{Q}(\xi({\mathbb{G}})=0)=0
and

\xi
is mass-stationary w.r.t.
\mathbb{Q}
, then Theorem
3.2

shows that there exists
a stationary measure
\nu
on
{\mathbf{F}}
(uniquely determined on
\{\xi({\mathbb{G}})>0\}
)
such that
\mathbb{Q}=\nu_{\xi}
is the Palm measure of
\xi
w.r.t.
\nu
.
The main purpose of this section is to show that, if

\mathbb{Q}
is spectrally decomposable, then
\nu
is a tail measure.

Remark 6.1
.

Condition (
6.1
) means that

\nu(\xi^{\prime}({\mathbb{G}})=0)=0
and should be compared
with the condition
\nu(Y\equiv 0)=0
, made in

[
20
]
. Our assumption is (slightly) stronger, also
in the stationary case.
Without any topological structure of
{\mathbf{F}}
such
a stronger assumption appears to be appropriate.
(The set
\{Y\equiv 0\}
does not even need to be measurable.)

If
\nu
is a
\sigma
-finite measure on
{\mathbf{F}}
and
\eta
a random measure on
{\mathbb{G}}

we define the
Campbell measure

\displaystyle C_{\nu,\eta}:=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\int{\bf 1}\{(Y,s)\in\cdot\}\,\eta(ds),

which is a measure on
{\mathbf{F}}\times{\mathbb{G}}
.
It is well-known (and easy to prove) that
C_{\nu,\xi^{\prime}}
determines

\mathbb{P}
on the event
\{\xi^{\prime}({\mathbb{G}})>0\}
. For tail measures this can be
refined as follows.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\nu
be a tail measure on
{\mathbf{F}}
.
Then
\nu
is
\sigma
-finite and uniquely determined by
C_{\nu,\xi}
.

Proof.

It follows from (
6.1
) and (
6.2
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\int{\bf 1}\{s\in B,|Y_{s}|>c\}\,\lambda(ds)<\infty

for each
c>0
and whenever
B\subset{\mathbb{G}}
is compact.
Take a sequence
B_{k}
,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, of compact sets increasing towards
{\mathbb{G}}
.
Then
\nu
is finite on the sets

\displaystyle U_{k}:=\Big\{\omega\in{\mathbf{F}}:\int{\bf 1}\{s\in B_{k},|\omega(s)|\geq 1/k\}\,\lambda(ds)\geq 1/k\Big\},\quad k\in{\mathbb{N}},

(6.6)

which increase towards
\big\{\omega\in{\mathbf{F}}:\int{\bf 1}\{|\omega(s)|>0\}\,\lambda(ds)>0\big\}
.
In view of (
6.1
) we obtain that
\nu
is
\sigma
-finite.

By homogeneity the Campbell measure
C_{\nu,\xi}
determines
the Campbell measures

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\int{\bf 1}\{(Y,s)\in\cdot\}{\bf 1}\{|Y_{s}|>c\}\,\lambda(ds)

for each
c>0
and hence also
C_{\nu,\xi^{\prime}}
.
Take a measurable
\tilde{h}\colon{\mathbf{F}}\times{\mathbb{G}}\to[0,\infty)

such that
\int\tilde{h}(\omega,s)\xi^{\prime}(ds)=1
,
whenever
\xi^{\prime}(\omega,{\mathbb{G}})>0
; see Remark
6.11
.
Then we obtain for each measurable
g\colon{\mathbf{F}}\to[0,\infty]
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}{\bf 1}\{\xi^{\prime}({\mathbb{G}})>0\}g(Y)=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\int g(Y)\tilde{h}(Y,s)\,\xi^{\prime}(ds)=\int g(\omega)\tilde{h}(\omega,s)\,C_{\nu,\xi^{\prime}}(d(\omega,s)).

Since
\nu(\xi^{\prime}({\mathbb{G}})=0)=0
, this proves the second assertion.
∎

Given a
\sigma
-finite stationary measure
\nu
on
{\mathbf{F}}
,
we recall that
\nu_{\xi}=\nu(\cdot\cap\{|Y_{0}|>1\})

is the Palm measure of
\xi
w.r.t.
\nu
.
If
\nu(\xi^{\prime}({\mathbb{G}})=0)=0
(e.g. if
\nu
is a tail measure),
then the definition (
2.3
) and the shift-invariance
of the event
\{\xi^{\prime}=0\}
show that

\displaystyle\nu_{\xi}(\xi^{\prime}=0)=0.

(6.7)

Corollary 6.3
.

A stationary tail measure
\nu
is
uniquely determined by
\nu_{\xi}
.

Proof.

Let
\nu^{\prime}
be another stationary tail measure with

\nu_{\xi}=\nu^{\prime}_{\xi}
. By the refined Campbell theorem (
2.4
)
we obtain that
C_{\nu,\xi}=C_{\nu^{\prime},\xi}
.
Lemma
6.2
shows that
\nu=\nu^{\prime}
, as asserted.
∎

Next we connect tail measures with
spectrally decomposable fields.
The first part of the following proposition is
a classical result.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
\nu
be a stationary tail measure with index
\alpha>0
.
Then there exists a probability measure
\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}
on
{\mathbf{F}}

such that
\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}(|Y_{0}|=1)=1
and

\displaystyle\nu(\cdot\cap\{|Y_{0}|>0\})=\iint{\bf 1}\{u\omega\in\cdot\}{\bf 1}\{u>0\}\,\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}(d\omega)\,du.

(6.8)

Moreover,
\xi
is mass-stationary with respect to the probability
measure

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}:=\iint{\bf 1}\{u\omega\in\cdot\}{\bf 1}\{u>1\}\,\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}(d\omega)\,du.

(6.9)

Further we have
\nu_{\xi}=\mathbb{Q}
.

Proof.

The first part follows by a classical argument;
see also
[
5
]
for a general version.
For the convenience of the reader we give the short proof.
Define

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}:=\nu(\{\omega\in{\mathbf{F}}:|\omega(0)|>1,|\omega(0)|^{-1}\omega\in\cdot\}).

By (
6.5
) (and stationarity), this is a probability measure
and we have that
\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}(|Y_{0}|=1)=1
by definition.
Take
u>0
and
A\in\mathcal{F}
. By (
6.3
),

\displaystyle\nu(\{\omega\in{\mathbf{F}}:|\omega(0)|>u,|\omega(0)|^{-1}\omega\in A\}=u^{-\alpha}\,\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}(A).

This implies (
6.8
).

To prove the second assertion we proceed similarly as in the
first part of the proof of Theorem
5.2
.
Let us first note, that

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}=\nu(\cdot\cap\{|Y_{0}|>1\}).

(6.10)

Let
h\colon{\mathbf{F}}\times{\mathbb{G}}\to[0,\infty]
be measurable
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. Then

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int h(\theta_{s}Y,-s){\bf 1}\{|Y_{s}|>\varepsilon\}\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}}\iint h(u\theta_{s}W,-s){\bf 1}\{u|W_{s}|>\varepsilon,u>1\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}}\iint h(\varepsilon v\theta_{s}W,-s){\bf 1}\{v|W_{s}|>1,v>\varepsilon^{-1}\}\alpha v^{-\alpha-1}\,dv\,\lambda(ds).

By (
6.8
) and
\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}(|W_{0}|=1)=1
,

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\iint h(\varepsilon\theta_{s}Y,-s){\bf 1}\{|Y_{s}|>1,|Y_{0}|>\varepsilon^{-1}\}\alpha v^{-\alpha-1}\,dv\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\iint h(\varepsilon Y,-s){\bf 1}\{|Y_{0}|>1,|Y_{-s}|>\varepsilon^{-1}\}\alpha v^{-\alpha-1}\,dv\,\lambda(ds),

where we have used stationarity, to obtain the second equality.
From here we can proceed as in the proof of Theorem
5.2

to obtain (
5.2
).

The final assertion
\nu_{\xi}=\mathbb{Q}
follows from
(
6.10
) and Lemma
4.1
.
∎

Remark 6.5
.

Let
\nu
be a tail measure.
By Proposition
6.4
,
\nu_{\xi}(W\in\cdot)
(
=\mathbb{Q}
) determines

\nu_{\xi}
and hence, by Corollary
6.3
also
\nu
.

Generalizing
[
3
, Theorem 2.9]
(treating
{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{Z}}
) and

[
20
, Theorem 2.3]

(treating
{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{R}}
) we next provide a construction of a stationary tail measure
\nu
,
assuming the space shift formula (
5.2
) to hold
for some given probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
.
This measure
\nu
satisfies
\nu_{\xi}=\mathbb{Q}
.
A function
h
from
{\mathbf{F}}
into some space is said
to be
0
-homogeneous
if
h(u\omega)=h(\omega)

for each
\omega\in{\mathbf{F}}
and each
u>0
.

Theorem 6.6
.

Assume that

\mathbb{Q}
is a spectrally decomposable probability measure on
{\mathbf{F}}
such that the space shift formula
(
5.2
) holds for some
\alpha>0
. Assume that
H\colon{\mathbf{F}}\times{\mathbb{G}}\to[0,\infty]

is a measurable function,
0
-homogeneous in the first coordinate
and such that

\displaystyle\int H(\theta_{t}W,s-t){\bf 1}\{|W_{s}|>0\}\,\lambda(ds)=1,\quad\lambda\text{-a.e.\ $t$},\,\mathbb{Q}\text{-a.s}.

(6.11)

Define a measure
\nu^{H}
on
{\mathbf{F}}
by

\displaystyle\nu^{H}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\iint{\bf 1}\{u\theta_{-s}W\in\cdot\}H(\theta_{-s}W,s){\bf 1}\{u>0\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du\,\lambda(ds).

(6.12)

Then
\nu^{H}
is a stationary tail measure satisfying
(\nu^{H})_{\xi}=\mathbb{Q}
.

Proof.

For the proof we generalize some of the arguments
from
[
3
,
20
]
.
The fact that
\nu^{H}
is
\alpha
-homogeneous
is an immediate consequence of the definition.
Assumption (
6.11
) implies that
\mathbb{Q}(\xi^{\prime}({\mathbb{G}})=0)=0
.
Since
\{\xi^{\prime}({\mathbb{G}})=0\}
is shift and scale invariant,
we obtain again directly from the definition of
\nu^{H}

that
\nu^{H}(\xi^{\prime}({\mathbb{G}})=0)=0
, that is (
6.1
).

Let
f\colon{\mathbf{F}}\times{\mathbb{G}}\to[0,\infty]
be measurable
and set
\rho(du):={\bf 1}\{u>0\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}du
.
Then

\displaystyle I:=\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{H}}

\displaystyle\int f(\theta_{t}Y,t)\,\xi(dt)=\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{H}}\int f(\theta_{t}Y,t){\bf 1}\{|Y_{t}|>1\}\,\lambda(dt)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\iiint f(u\theta_{t-s}W,t){\bf 1}\{u|W_{t-s}|>1,H(\theta_{-s}W,s)\,\rho(du)\,\lambda(ds)\,\lambda(dt)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\iiint f(v|W_{t-s}|^{-1}\theta_{t-s}W,t)|W_{t-s}|^{\alpha}H(\theta_{-s}W,s)\}{\bf 1}\{v>1\}\,\lambda(ds)\,\lambda(dt)\,\rho(dv),

where we have used the homogeneity of
H
and a change of variables.
By the invariance properties of Haar measure (set
r:=t-s
in the inner integral),

\displaystyle I=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\iiint f(v|W_{r}|^{-1}\theta_{r}W,t)|W_{r}|^{\alpha}H(\theta_{r-t}W,t-r)\}{\bf 1}\{v>1\}\,\lambda(dr)\,\lambda(dt)\,\rho(dv).

Now we can use assumption (
5.2
) (and again the
homogeneity of
H
) to obtain that

\displaystyle I=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\iiint f(vW,t)H(\theta_{-t}W,r+t)\}{\bf 1}\{|W_{r}|>0\}{\bf 1}\{v>1\}\,\lambda(dr)\,\lambda(dt)\,\rho(dv).

By assumption (
6.11
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{H}}\int f(\theta_{t}Y,t)\,\xi(dt)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\iint f(vW,t){\bf 1}\{v>1\}\,\lambda(dt)\,\rho(dv).

(6.13)

From (
6.13
) we conclude that (
6.4
) holds for
\nu=\nu^{H}
.
The right-hand side of (
6.13
)
does not depend on the specific choice of
H
. Take
r\in{\mathbb{G}}

and apply (
6.13
) with
H^{r}
instead of
H
,
where
H^{r}(\omega,s):=H(\theta_{r}\omega,s-r)
. Lemma
6.2
yields
that
\nu^{H}=\nu^{H_{r}}
. On the other hand
we obtain for each measurable
g\colon{\mathbf{F}}\to[0,\infty]
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{H^{r}}}g(\theta_{r}Y)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\iint{\bf 1}\{u\theta_{r-s}W\in\cdot\}H(\theta_{r-s}W,s-r){\bf 1}\{u>0\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du\,\lambda(ds),

which equals
\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{H}}g(Y)
. Hence
\nu^{H}
is stationary
and (
6.13
) shows that
(\nu^{H})_{\xi}=\mathbb{Q}
.
∎

Next we discuss some special cases of Theorem
6.6
.
Given a measurable function
G\colon{\mathbb{G}}\to[0,\infty]

we define a measurable function

J_{G}\colon{\mathbf{F}}\to[0,\infty]
by

\displaystyle J_{G}(\omega):=\int|\omega(s)|^{\alpha}G(s)\,\lambda(ds),\quad\omega\in{\mathbf{F}}.

(6.14)

and a measure
\mathbb{Q}^{G}
on
{\mathbf{F}}
by

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{G}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int{\bf 1}\{J_{G}(\theta_{-s}W)^{-1/\alpha}\theta_{-s}W\in\cdot\}G(s)\,\lambda(ds).

(6.15)

Corollary 6.7
.

Let
\mathbb{Q}
satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
6.6
.
Let
G\colon{\mathbf{F}}\to[0,\infty]
be a measurable function satisfying

\displaystyle 0<\int|W_{s}|^{\alpha}G(s+r)\,\lambda(ds)<\infty,\quad\lambda\text{-a.e.\ $r$},\,\text{$\mathbb{Q}$-a.s.}

(6.16)

Then

\displaystyle\nu^{G}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{G}}\int{\bf 1}\{uY\in\cdot,u>0\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du

(6.17)

is a stationary tail measure satisfying
(\nu^{G})_{\xi}=\mathbb{Q}
.

Proof.

We wish to apply Theorem
6.6
with the
function

\displaystyle H(\omega,t):=J_{G}(\omega)^{-1}|\omega(t)|^{\alpha}G(t).

For each
t\in{\mathbb{G}}
we have

\displaystyle\int H(\theta_{t}W,s-t){\bf 1}\{|W_{s}|>0\}\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=J_{G}(\theta_{t}W)^{-1}\int|W_{s}|^{\alpha}G(s-t)\,\lambda(ds).

By assumption (
6.16
) this equals
1
for
\lambda
-a.e.
t
.
Since
|(\theta_{-s}W)_{s}|=|W_{0}|=1
we obtain that
\nu^{H}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\iint{\bf 1}\{v\theta_{-s}W\in\cdot,v>0\}J_{G}(\theta_{-s}W)^{-1}G(s)\alpha v^{-\alpha-1}\,dv\,\lambda(ds).

Changing variables
u:=J_{G}(\theta_{-s}W)v
yields the assertion.
∎

Corollary 6.8
.

Let
\mathbb{Q}
be a spectrally decomposable probability measure on
{\mathbf{F}}

such that
\mathbb{Q}(\xi^{\prime}({\mathbb{G}})=0)=0
. Assume that the space shift formula
(
5.2
) holds for some
\alpha>0
. Let
G\colon{\mathbf{F}}\to(0,\infty)

be measurable with
\int Gd\lambda=1
. Define a probability measure

\mathbb{Q}^{G}
by (
6.15
). Then
\nu^{G}
defined
by (
6.17
) is a stationary tail measure satisfying
(\nu^{G})_{\xi}=\mathbb{Q}
.

Proof.

We wish to apply Corollary
6.7
. The first inequality
in (
6.16
) follows from our assumptions
\mathbb{Q}(\xi^{\prime}({\mathbb{G}})>0)=1

and
G>0
. Assumption (
5.2
) implies
for each
r\in{\mathbb{G}}
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int|W_{s}|^{\alpha}G(s+r)\,\lambda(ds)\leq\int G(t+s)\,\lambda(ds)\leq 1.

Hence the second inequality in (
6.16
) holds as well, proving the result.
∎

Remark 6.9
.

The assumption
\mathbb{Q}(\xi^{\prime}({\mathbb{G}})>0)=1

).
This assumption is very natural (see Remark

Remark 6.10
.

Consider the assumptions of Theorem
6.6

and assume moreover that
\mathbb{Q}(|Y_{s}|>0)=1
for

-a.e.

.
Then we can choose
G={\bf 1}_{B}
for any

with

.

Remark 6.11
.

We can follow
[
15
]

to construct a function
H
satisfying the assumptions of
Theorem
6.6
. Take a measurable partition

\{B_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
of
{\mathbb{G}}
into relatively compact Borel sets.
Define
\tilde{H}\colon{\mathbf{F}}\times{\mathbb{G}}\to(0,\infty)

\displaystyle\tilde{H}(\omega,s):=\sum_{n}2^{-n}(\xi^{\prime}(\omega,B_{n})+1)^{-1}{\bf 1}\{s\in B_{n}\}.

Since
\xi^{\prime}(\omega)=\xi^{\prime}(W(\omega))

we have that
\tilde{H}(\omega,s)=\tilde{H}(W(\omega),s)
.
Define a random variable
S
by
S(\omega):=\int\tilde{H}(\omega,s)\,\xi^{\prime}(\omega,ds)
,

\omega\in\Omega
.
Then
S\leq 1
and
S>0
, whenever
\xi^{\prime}({\mathbb{G}})>0
.
Define a function
H
by

H(\omega,s):=S^{-1}(\omega)\tilde{H}(\omega,s)
.
By definition of
\xi^{\prime}
,

\tilde{H}
and hence also
H
is

-homogeneous in the first
argument. Furthermore we have for

that

\displaystyle\int

\displaystyle\tilde{H}(\theta_{t}W(\omega),s-t){\bf 1}\{|W_{s}(\omega)|>0\}\,\lambda(ds)=\int\tilde{H}(\theta_{t}W(\omega),s-t)\,\xi^{\prime}(W(\omega),ds)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}2^{-n}(\xi^{\prime}(\theta_{t}W(\omega),B_{n})+1)^{-1}{\bf 1}\{s-t\in B_{n}\}\,\xi^{\prime}(W(\omega),ds)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}2^{-n}(\xi^{\prime}(W(\omega),B_{n}+t)+1)^{-1}\xi^{\prime}(W(\omega),B_{n}+t).

This is positive as soon as

.
Since

), provided that

Remark 6.14
.

Suppose that

Example 6.15

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{G}}}{\bf 1}\{T(\theta_{t}W)=s+t,|W_{s+t}|>0\}=1,\quad\mathbb{Q}\text{-a.s.},\,t\in{\mathbb{G}},

\displaystyle\nu^{T}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{G}}}\int{\bf 1}\{u\theta_{-s}W\in\cdot,T(\theta_{-s}W)=s\}{\bf 1}\{u>0\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du,

(6.19)

providing a modest generalization of
[
3
, Proposition 2.12]
.
Using the arguments in
[
3
, Section 2.4]

(and assuming
\mathbb{Q}(Y\equiv 0)=0
)
it is possible to construct a mapping
T
with the preceding properties.

Remark 6.16
.

We can extend the mapping
T
from

to an allocation by setting

\tau(\omega,s):=T(\theta_{s}\omega,0)+s
. Then the formulas

5.14
) and (

) look very similar.
The crucial difference is that the allocation in the first

.
A similar remark applies to Remark
5.7

and Theorem

.

7
Spectral representation

Again we establish the canonical setting of Section
5
.
Let
\nu
be a measure on
{\mathbf{F}}
. In accordance with
the literature we say that
\nu
has a
spectral representation
, if
there exists a probability measure
\mathbb{Q}^{*}
on
{\mathbf{F}}
and
an
\alpha>0
satisfying

\displaystyle\nu=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\int{\bf 1}\{uY\in\cdot,u>0\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du.

(7.1)

In this case we refer to
\mathbb{Q}^{*}
as a
spectral measure
of
\nu

and to
\alpha
as the index of
\nu
.
Our previous results will show rather quickly that any stationary tail measure
has a spectral representation. In a sense this section is dual to the previous one.
We start with the non-probabilistic object
\nu
and derive
the probabilistic representation (
7.1
).

First we will state a few basic properties of a spectral representation,
to be found (in special cases) in
[
3
,
20
]

and in the recent preprint
[
2
]
dealing with more
general fields. Recall that a stationary tail measure is assumed
to be normalized as in (
6.4
).

Proposition 7.1
.

Suppose that
\nu

admits a spectral representation with spectral measure

\mathbb{Q}^{*}
and index
\alpha>0
. Assume that
\mathbb{Q}^{*}(\xi^{\prime}({\mathbb{G}})>0)=1
.
Then we have:

(i)

\nu
is a tail measure iff

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\int{\bf 1}\{s\in B\}|Y_{s}|^{\alpha}\,\lambda(ds)<\infty,\quad\text{$B\in{\mathcal{G}}$ compact}.

(7.2)

(ii)

Assume in addition that (
7.2
) holds.
Then
\nu
is stationary iff

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\int g(Y,s)|Y_{s}|^{\alpha}\,\lambda(ds)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\int g(\theta_{-s}Y,s)|Y_{0}|^{\alpha}\,\lambda(ds),

(7.3)

holds for all measurable
g\colon{\mathbf{F}}\times{\mathbb{G}}\to[0,\infty]

which are
0
-homogeneous in the first argument.
If these conditions hold, then
\nu
is a stationary tail measure iff

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}|Y_{s}|^{\alpha}=1,\quad\lambda\text{-a.e.\ $s\in{\mathbb{G}}$}.

(7.4)

Proof.

For the proof we generalize the arguments
in
[
3
]
(given for
{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{Z}}
) in a straightforward manner.
Clearly
\nu
is
\alpha
-homogeneous. By

\mathbb{Q}^{*}(\xi^{\prime}({\mathbb{G}})>0)=1
and (
7.1
),
\nu

satisfies property (
6.1
).

(i) Let
B\in{\mathcal{G}}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\int{\bf 1}\{s\in B,|Y_{s}|>1\}\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\iint{\bf 1}\{s\in B,u|Y_{s}|>1\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\int{\bf 1}\{s\in B\}|Y_{s}|^{\alpha}\,\lambda(ds).

(7.5)

Hence (
6.4
) and (
7.2
) are equivalent.

(ii) Assume that
\nu
is stationary and take
a measurable
g\colon{\mathbf{F}}\times{\mathbb{G}}\to[0,\infty]

which is
0
-homogeneous in the first argument.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\int g(Y,s)|Y_{s}|^{\alpha}\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\iint g(uY,s){\bf 1}\{u|Y_{s}|>1\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\int g(Y,s){\bf 1}\{|Y_{s}|>1\}\,\lambda(ds).

By stationarity of
\nu
this equals

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\int g(\theta_{-s}Y,s){\bf 1}\{|Y_{0}|>1\}\,\lambda(ds)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\iint g(\theta_{-s}Y,s){\bf 1}\{u|Y_{0}|>1\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du\,\lambda(ds).

This equals the right-hand side of (
7.3
).

Assume now that (
7.3
) holds.
Take a measurable
f\colon{\mathbf{F}}\times{\mathbb{G}}\to[0,\infty]
and
t\in{\mathbb{G}}
.
Then

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\int f(\theta_{t}Y,s){\bf 1}\{|Y_{s+t}|>1\}\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\iint f(u\theta_{t}Y,s){\bf 1}\{u|Y_{s+t}|>1\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\iint f(u\theta_{t}Y,s-t){\bf 1}\{u|Y_{s}|>1\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\iint f(|Y_{s}|^{-1}v\theta_{t}Y,s-t)|Y_{s}|^{\alpha}{\bf 1}\{v>1\}\alpha v^{-\alpha-1}\,dv\,\lambda(ds).

By the homogeneity of
|Y_{s}|^{-1}Y
and (
7.3
),

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\iint f(|Y_{0}|^{-1}v\theta_{t-s}Y,s-t)|Y_{0}|^{\alpha}{\bf 1}\{v>1\}\alpha v^{-\alpha-1}\,dv\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\iint f(u\theta_{t-s}Y,s-t){\bf 1}\{u|Y_{0}|>1\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\iint f(u\theta_{-s}Y,s){\bf 1}\{u|Y_{0}|>1\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du\,\lambda(ds).

This shows that the Campbell measures
C_{\nu\circ{\theta_{t}},\xi^{\prime}}
do not depend on
t\in{\mathbb{G}}
.
But
\nu\circ{\theta_{t}}
does also satisfy (
6.1
) and
(
6.3
) along with the assumption of (ii).
Hence Lemma
6.2
implies that
\nu
is stationary.

The final assertion follows from (
7
).
∎

Let us mention the following fact; cf.
[
20
, (2.8)]
.

Corollary 7.2
.

Suppose that
\nu
is a stationary tail measure
with index
\alpha>0
. Let
\mathbb{Q}^{*}
be a spectral measure of
\nu
.
Then

\displaystyle\nu_{\xi}(W\in\cdot)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}{\bf 1}\{|Y_{s}|^{-1}\theta_{s}Y\in\cdot\}|Y_{s}|^{\alpha},\quad\lambda\text{-a.e.\ $s$}.

Proof.

In the proof of Proposition
7.1
we have seen that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\int g(Y,s)|Y_{s}|^{\alpha}\,\lambda(ds)=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\int g(\theta_{-s}Y,s){\bf 1}\{|Y_{0}|>1\}\,\lambda(ds).

holds, provided that
g
is 0-homogeneous in the first
argument.
The right-hand side equals
\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\xi}}\int g(\theta_{-s}Y,s)\lambda(ds)
.
Equivalently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\int g(\theta_{s}Y,s)|Y_{s}|^{\alpha}\,\lambda(ds).=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\xi}}\int g(Y,s)\,\lambda(ds)

Applying this with
g(Y,s):=h(|Y_{0}|^{-1}Y,s)
for a
measurable
h\colon{\mathbf{F}}\times{\mathbb{G}}\to[0,\infty]
yields the assertion.
∎

.

.

Suppose
that
\nu
is a stationary tail measure
with index

. Then
\nu
has a spectral

satisfying (

) and (

By Theorem

Remark 7.4
.

The existence of a spectral representation

Remark 7.5
.

Let
\nu
be a stationary tail measure.
Then

is said to be the distribution of the

associated with

; see
[

,

]
.

\displaystyle\nu=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\iint{\bf 1}\{u\theta_{s}Y\in\cdot,u>0\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du\,\lambda(ds).

Theorem 7.6
.

Suppose that
\nu
is a stationary tail measure
with index
\alpha>0
. Then there exists a probability

\mathbb{Q}^{*}
on
{\mathbf{F}}
such that (

)
holds iff

8
Anchoring maps

In this section we let
Y
and
\xi
be as in Section
4

and suppose that
\mathbb{Q}
is a probability measure on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})

such that
\xi
is mass-stationary w.r.t.
\mathbb{Q}
.

Following
[
17
,
20
]
we say that a measurable mapping

T\colon{\mathbf{F}}\to{\mathbb{G}}
is an
anchoring map
if

\displaystyle T(\theta_{s}\omega)=T(\omega)-s,\quad s\in{\mathbb{G}},\,\text{if $0<\xi(\omega,{\mathbb{G}})<\infty$}.

(8.1)

In stochastic geometry such functions are known as
center functions
;
see e.g.
[
13
, Chapter 17]
.

In the following the number

\displaystyle\vartheta:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\xi({\mathbb{G}})^{-1}.

(8.2)

will play an important rule. If
\mathbb{Q}(\xi({\mathbb{G}})<\infty)>0
, then
\vartheta>0
.

Proposition 8.1
.

Assume that
\xi
is mass-stationary w.r.t.
\mathbb{Q}
.
Assume also that

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(0<\xi({\mathbb{G}})<\infty)=1

(8.3)

and
\vartheta<\infty
.
Let
T
be an anchoring map and define the probabiliy measure

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{T}:=\vartheta^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\xi({\mathbb{G}})^{-1}{\bf 1}\{\theta_{T}\in\cdot\}

(8.4)

Then we have for all
measurable
g\colon\Omega\to[0,\infty]
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}g

\displaystyle=\vartheta\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{T}}\int g\circ\theta_{s}\,\xi(ds).

(8.5)

Proof.

Let
\nu
be the
\sigma
-finite
stationary measure on
\Omega
such that
\mathbb{Q}=\nu_{\xi}
and
\nu(\xi({\mathbb{G}})=0)=0
.
Define an allocation
\tau
by
\tau(\omega,s):=T(\theta_{s}\omega)+s
.
By assumption
\tau(\omega,s)=T(\omega)
for each
s\in{\mathbb{G}}
, provided that

0<\xi(\omega,{\mathbb{G}})<\infty
. Moreover,

\eta:={\bf 1}\{0<\xi(\omega,{\mathbb{G}})<\infty\}{\bf 1}\{T\in\cdot\}

is an invariant simple point process.
By Proposition
3.1
we have for each measurable
h\colon\Omega\times\Omega\to[0,\infty]

that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}h(\theta_{0},\theta_{\tau(0)})=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\eta}}\int{\bf 1}\{\tau(s)=0\}h(\theta_{s},\theta_{0})\,\xi(ds).

Since
\nu_{\eta}(\xi({\mathbb{G}})\in\{0,\infty\})=0
, this means

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}h(\theta_{0},\theta_{T})=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\eta}}\int{\bf 1}\{T=0\}h(\theta_{s},\theta_{0})\,\xi(ds).

It follows straight from the definition (
2.3
)
that
\nu_{\eta}(T\neq 0)=0
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}h(\theta_{0},\theta_{T})=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\eta}}\int h(\theta_{s},\theta_{0})\,\xi(ds).

(8.6)

Applying this
to the function
(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\mapsto\xi(\omega^{\prime},{\mathbb{G}})^{-1}g(\omega^{\prime})

(for some measurable
g\colon\Omega\to[0,\infty]
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\xi({\mathbb{G}})^{-1}g(\theta_{T})=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\eta}}g

and therefore
\mathbb{Q}_{T}=\vartheta^{-1}\nu_{\eta}
. Hence (
8.5
)
follows from (
8.6
).
∎

Example 8.2
.

Assume that
{\mathbb{G}}
is discrete as in Example
2.3
,
so that
\xi
is a simple point process on
{\mathbb{G}}
.
Under assumption (
8.3
) we have
\vartheta\leq 1

with equality iff
\mathbb{Q}(\xi({\mathbb{G}})=1)=1
.
From (
8.5
) we obtain
for each measurable
f\colon{\mathbf{F}}\to[0,\infty]
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}f{\bf 1}\{T=0\}

\displaystyle=\vartheta\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{T}}\int f\circ\theta_{s}{\bf 1}\{T\circ\theta_{s}=0\}\,\xi(ds)

\displaystyle=\vartheta\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{T}}\int f\circ\theta_{s}{\bf 1}\{T=s\}\,\xi(ds)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{T}}f{\bf 1}\{|Y_{0}|>1\},

where we have used that
\mathbb{Q}_{T}(T=0)=1
. As in
[
17
]

it is natural to assume that

\displaystyle|Y_{T}|>1.

(8.7)

Then
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}f{\bf 1}\{T=0\}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{T}}f
.
Hence
\vartheta=\mathbb{Q}(T=0)
and

\mathbb{Q}_{T}=\mathbb{Q}(\cdot\mid T=0)
.
Therefore Proposition
8.1
extends
[
17
, Proposition 3.2]
.
Further formulas in the spectrally decomposable case can be found in Subsection 3.3 of

[
17
]
.

In the remainder of this section we establish the canonical setting
of Section
5
. We assume that
\mathbb{Q}
is spectrally
decomposable and satisfies
\mathbb{Q}(\xi^{\prime}({\mathbb{G}})=0)=0
.
By Corollary
6.8
we can associate with
\mathbb{Q}
a unique
stationary tail measure
\nu
such that
\nu_{\xi}=\mathbb{Q}
.
We assume moreover that

\displaystyle\int|Y_{s}|^{\alpha}\lambda(ds)<\infty,\quad\mathbb{Q}\text{-a.e.}

(8.8)

Note that

\displaystyle\xi({\mathbb{G}})\leq\int{\bf 1}\{|Y_{s}|>1\}|Y_{s}|^{\alpha}\,\lambda(ds)\leq\int|Y_{s}|^{\alpha}\,\lambda(ds),

so that
\mathbb{Q}(0<\xi({\mathbb{G}})<\infty)=1
; see also Corollary
6.13
.
Since (
8.8
) does also hold
\nu
-a.e., we can
and will use the moving shift representation (

)
with
\mathbb{Q}^{*}
given by (

).

For each
\omega\in{\mathbf{F}}
the function

u\mapsto\int{\bf 1}\{|\omega(s)|>u^{-1}\}\,\lambda(ds)

from

to

is increasing and left-continuous.
Therefore we can define a random variable
\kappa
by

\displaystyle\kappa:=\inf\{u>0:\xi(uZ^{-1/\alpha}W,{\mathbb{G}})>0\}.

(8.9)

The following lemma gives an alternative expression
for the number
\vartheta
defined by (

).

Lemma 8.3
.

Assume that
\mathbb{Q}
is spectrally decomposable, that
\xi
is mass-stationary
w.r.t.
\mathbb{Q}
and that (
8.8
) holds. Then

\vartheta=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\kappa^{\alpha}
.

Proof.

We start with a preliminary comment. Since
\mathbb{Q}(|Y_{0}|>0)=1
we have
\mathbb{Q}
-a.e.

Z^{-1/\alpha}W=\tilde{Z}^{-1/\alpha}Y
, where

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}:=\int|Y_{s}|^{\alpha}\,\lambda(ds).

In particular,

\displaystyle\kappa=\inf\{u>0:\xi(u\tilde{Z}^{-1/\alpha}Y,{\mathbb{G}})>0\}.

(8.10)

By our assumptions,
\mathbb{Q}(0<\tilde{Z}<\infty)=1
.
Since for each
v>0

\displaystyle\int{\bf 1}\{v|Y_{s}|>1\}\,\lambda(ds)\leq v^{\alpha}\int|Y_{s}|^{\alpha}\,\lambda(ds),

we obtain

\displaystyle\xi(u\tilde{Z}^{-1/\alpha}Y,{\mathbb{G}})<\infty,\quad u>0,\,\mathbb{Q}\text{-a.e.}

(8.11)

Since
\mathbb{Q}
is the Palm measure of
\xi
w.r.t.
\nu

we have
\vartheta=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}{\bf 1}\{|Y_{0}|>1\}\xi({\mathbb{G}})^{-1}
.
Hence we obtain from (

) and (

) that

\displaystyle\vartheta=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\iint{\bf 1}\{u\tilde{Z}^{-1/\alpha}|Y_{s}|>1\}\xi(u\tilde{Z}^{-1/\alpha}Y,{\mathbb{G}})^{-1}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du\,\lambda(ds).

By Fubini’s theorem and (
8.11
),

\displaystyle\vartheta=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int{\bf 1}\{\xi(u\tilde{Z}^{-1/\alpha}Y,{\mathbb{G}})>0\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du.

By (
8.10
) this yields

\vartheta=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int{\bf 1}\{u>\kappa\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du

and hence the asserted formula.
∎

Let
\tau
be an allocation such that

\displaystyle\tau(u\omega,s)=\tau(u\omega,0)\in{\mathbb{G}},\quad\mathbb{Q}\otimes\lambda\otimes\lambda_{+}\text{-a.e. $(\omega,s,u)$},

(8.12)

where
\lambda_{+}
denotes Lebesgue measure on
(0,\infty)
.
In view of (

) we can interpret
\tau(0)
as an almost every version
of an anchoring map.
Motivated by
[
20
, Section 2.3]
we collect some (preliminary) information
on the distribution of
(\tau(Y,0),Y)
.
Though the principal calculations are similar, we cannot use the specific
moving shift representation from
[
20
, Theorem 2.9]
.

Lemma 8.4
.

Assume that the assumptions of Lemma
8.3
hold.
Let
\tau
be an allocation satisfying (
8.12
) and
suppose that
g\colon{\mathbf{F}}\times{\mathbb{G}}\to[0,\infty]

is measurable and shift-invariant in the first coordinate. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}g(Y,\tau(0))=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\iint{\bf 1}\{u>\kappa\}{\bf 1}\{u|Y_{s+\tau(uY,0)}|>1\}g(uY,-s)\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du\,\lambda(ds).

(8.13)

In particular
\mathbb{Q}(\tau(0)\in\cdot)
has the
\lambda
-density

\displaystyle f_{\tau}(s):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\int{\bf 1}\{u>\kappa,u|Y_{-s+\tau(uY,0)}|>1\}\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du,\quad s\in{\mathbb{G}}.

(8.14)

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}g(Y,\tau(0))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}{\bf 1}\{|Y_{0}|>1\}g(Y,\tau(0))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\iint{\bf 1}\{u|Y_{s}|>1\}g(u\theta_{s}Y,\tau(u\theta_{s}Y,0))\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du\,\lambda(ds)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{*}}\iint{\bf 1}\{u|Y_{s}|>1\}g(uY,\tau(uY,0)-s)\alpha u^{-\alpha-1}\,du\,\lambda(ds),

where we have used that
\tau(u\theta_{s}Y,0)=\tau(uY,s)-s=\tau(uY,0)-s

holds for
\lambda\otimes\lambda_{+}
-a.e.
(s,u)
and
\mathbb{Q}^{*}
-a.e. Changing variables gives

9
Concluding remarks

The results from Sections
5
-
7

generalize to the setting
described in Remark
5.9
. This would mean, for instance, that
tail measures are then defined on a more general space
\Omega

and not just on the function space
{\mathbf{F}}
.
To avoid an abstract (and potentially confusing)
notation we have chosen to stick to the present more
specific setting.

Given the results of this paper, one might define a tail process
in an intrinsic way, namely as a spectrally decomposable random
field
Y=(Y_{s})_{s\in{\mathbb{G}}}
such that the exceedance random
measure is mass-stationary. It would be interesting to identify
such processes as the tail processes of regularly varying stationary fields,
beyond the known special cases. It would also be interesting to
further study tail measures of such fields, as introduced in great generality
in
[
19
]
. In particular it might be worthwhile exploring
further relationships between tail measures, tail processes and
Palm calculus.
