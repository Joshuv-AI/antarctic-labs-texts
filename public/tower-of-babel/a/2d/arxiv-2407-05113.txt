Emergent Mesoscale Correlations in Active Solids with Noisy Chiral Dynamics
Amir Shee, Silke Henkes, Cristián Huepe

Abstract
We present the linear response theory for an elastic solid composed of active Brownian particles with intrinsic individual chirality, deriving both a normal mode formulation and a continuum elastic formulation. Using this theory, we compute analytically the velocity correlations and energy spectra under different conditions, showing an excellent agreement with simulations. We generate the corresponding phase diagram, identifying chiral and achiral disordered regimes (for high chirality or noise levels), as well as chiral and achiral states with mesoscopic-range order (for low chirality and noise). The chiral ordered states display mesoscopic spatial correlations and oscillating time correlations, but no wave propagation. In the high chirality regime, we find a peak in the elastic energy spectrum that leads to a non-monotonic behavior with increasing noise strength that is consistent with the emergence of the ‘hammering’ state recently identified in chiral glasses. Finally, we show numerically that our theory, despite its linear response nature, can be applied beyond the idealized homogeneous solid assumed in our derivations. Indeed, by increasing the level of activity, we show that it remains a good approximation of the system dynamics until just below the melting transition. In addition, we show that there is still an excellent agreement between our analytical results and simulations when we extend our results to heterogeneous solids composed of mixtures of active particles with different intrinsic chirality and noise levels. The derived linear response theory is therefore robust and applicable to a broad range of real-world active systems. Our work provides a thorough analytical and numerical description of the emergent states in a densely packed system of chiral self-propelled Brownian disks, thus allowing a detailed understanding of the phases and dynamics identified in a minimal chiral active system.

In this paper, we investigate the collective dynamics of chiral active Brownian disks with elastic repulsive interactions at high densities, in the solid state. We identify and describe the different phases, finding an emergent mesoscopic length scale that can display or not oscillatory time correlations, depending on the ratio of chiral motion to rotational noise.
We derive an analytical active solid theory to describe these phases, using a normal mode approach and a continuum elasticity approach, both of which match our simulations.
In addition, we show that these results remain valid well into the nonlinear regime, just below the melting transition, and inform the dynamics of the fluid state. They also extend to different kinds of active binary mixtures, including mixtures of chiral and achiral particles, of chiral particles with different rotational speeds, and of chiral particles with different levels of rotational diffusion.

II
Model

We consider a system of
N
densely packed soft chiral self-propelled disks following overdamped dynamics in a two dimensional periodic box of size
L\times L
.
Disregarding passive translational diffusion, the dynamics of the position
{\bf{r}}_{i}\equiv(x_{i},y_{i})
and heading direction
{\hat{n}}_{i}\equiv(\cos(\theta_{i}),\sin(\theta_{i}))
of the
i
-th disk will be given by

\displaystyle\dot{{\bf{r}}}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{0}{\hat{n}}_{i}+\mu{\bf{F}}_{i}~,

(1)

\displaystyle\dot{{\hat{n}}}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\Omega+\sqrt{2D_{r}}~\eta_{i}(t)\right]{\hat{n}^{\perp}}_{i}~.

(2)

Here,
v_{0}
is the self-propulsion speed,
\mu
is the mobility (inverse damping coefficient),
\Omega
is the chiral angular speed of the disks,
D_{r}
is their rotational diffusion coefficient, and
{\hat{n}^{\perp}}_{i}
is a unit vector perpendicular to
{\hat{n}}_{i}
.
Noise is introduced through the random variable
\eta_{i}(t)
, following Gaussian white noise with
\langle\eta_{i}(t)\rangle=0
and

\langle\eta_{i}(t)\eta_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
.
The sum of all contact forces over disk
i
is

{\bf{F}}_{i}=\sum_{j\in S_{i}}{\bf{f}}_{ij}
,
where
S_{i}
is the set of indexes of all disks that overlap disk
i
.
These forces are modeled as linear repulsion, with

{\bf{f}}_{ij}=k(|{\bf{r}}_{ij}|-l_{0}){\bf{r}}_{ij}/|{\bf{r}}_{ij}|
if
|{\bf{r}}_{ij}|\leq l_{0}

and
{\bf{f}}_{ij}=0
otherwise, were
{\bf{r}}_{ij}={\bf{r}}_{j}-{\bf{r}}_{i}
and
l_{0}=2r_{0}
is the equilibrium center-to-center distance between two neighboring disks of radius
r_{0}
.
We note that, in real-world scenarios,
\Omega
and
D_{r}
will not be the same for all disks.
In Section
VII
, we thus conduct a comprehensive investigation into binary and complex mixtures of disks with different
D_{r}
and
\Omega
values, substituting
D_{r}
by
D_{r}^{i}
and
\Omega
by
\Omega^{i}
in Eq. (
2
).

We note that the orientation dynamics in Eq. (
2
) are decoupled from the position dynamics in Eq. (
1
), and result from the interplay between deterministic chirality and angular diffusion.
The deterministic angular speed
\Omega
sets a rotational timescale

\tau_{{\scriptscriptstyle\Omega}}=\Omega^{-1}
; the diffusion constant
D_{r}
sets a persistence timescale
\tau_{\text{r}}=D_{r}^{-1}
.
We will show below that the interplay between rotational, persistence, and elastic timescales can generate different collective states.

IV
Analytical Results

In this section, we present the analytical formulations used to describe the system.
We begin by computing the orientation dynamics of the heading direction in Subsection A, since they are not coupled to the positions.
We then formulate a linear response theory, adopting the method in Henkes et al.
[
89
]
to describe the linear response in terms of normal modes in Subsection B, to then calculate the energy per mode and spatial velocity correlations.
We further simplify the analytical description by implementing a continuum elasticity framework in Subsection C, to compute mean-squared velocity and velocity autocorrelation functions.

IV.1
Orientation Dynamics

Given that the orientation evolves independently in Eq. (
2
), the probability distribution
P({\hat{n}},t)
for the heading direction
{\hat{n}}
as a function of time will follow the Fokker-Planck equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P({\hat{n}},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{r}\nabla_{{\hat{n}}}^{2}P-\Omega{\hat{n}}^{\perp}\cdot\nabla_{{\hat{n}}}P~,

(3)

where
\nabla_{{\hat{n}}}
is the Laplacian in orientation space.
Using a Laplace transformation approach described in detail in Appendix-
A
, we can compute an exact expression for the heading orientation autocorrelation, which is given by

\displaystyle\langle{\hat{n}}(t)\cdot{\hat{n}}(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{e}^{-D_{r}t}\cos(\Omega~t).

(4)

Here, the decay rate of the exponential term is given by
\tau_{r}=D_{r}^{-1}
and the period of chiral rotation, by
\tau_{{\scriptscriptstyle\Omega}}=\Omega^{-1}
.
We thus define
D_{r}=\Omega
as the critical line between a regime dominated by the angular noise and a regime dominated by the deterministic chirality, which we highlighted as a solid red line in Fig.
1
(a).
Note that this boundary is formally analogous in Eq. (
4
) to the limit between damped and overdamped oscillations, where the high angular diffusion case corresponds to the overdamped regime, as the mean temporal heading correlations display no oscillatory component.

IV.2
Normal Mode Formulation

In order to express the dynamics in terms of the normal modes of vibration of the passive system, we first define as
{\bf{r}}_{i}^{0}
the equilibrium position of disk
i
for
v_{0}=0
, which corresponds to a minimum of the elastic energy.
Using Eq. (
1
), we then find that the dynamics of small displacements
\delta{\bf{r}}_{i}={\bf{r}}_{i}-{\bf{r}}_{i}^{0}
around these equilibrium positions are described by

\displaystyle\delta\dot{{\bf{r}}}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{0}{\hat{n}}_{i}-\sum_{j}\mathbb{K}_{ij}\cdot\delta{\bf{r}}_{j},

(5)

where each
\mathbb{K}_{ij}
corresponds to a
2\times 2
block of the
2N\times 2N
dynamical matrix.
We are interested in expressing the dynamics over the normal elastic modes of the system, i.e., over the eigenvectors of the dynamical matrix.
Each of these
2N
normal modes corresponds to a
2N
-dimensional eigenvector that can be written as a list of
N
two-dimensional vectors, given by
({\bf\xi}_{1}^{\nu},...,{\bf\xi}_{N}^{\nu})
, where
\nu=1,...,2N
labels the eigenvector mode associated to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{\nu}
.

We can formally write the displacements in terms of the eigenmodes described above as

\delta{\bf{r}}_{i}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{2N}a_{\nu}\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\nu}
.
Projecting Eq. (
5
) onto the normal modes, we then obtain the following uncoupled equations for the dynamics of the normal mode amplitudes:

\displaystyle\dot{a}_{\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{\nu}-\lambda_{\nu}a_{\nu},

(6)

where
\eta_{\nu}
is the projection of the self-propulsion force onto the normal mode
\nu
, given by

\displaystyle\eta_{\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\hat{n}}_{i}\cdot\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\nu}.

(7)

We note that
\eta_{\nu}
is the sum of
N
statistically independent contributions with bounded moments, each one resulting from the correlated noise dynamics in time that is followed by its corresponding
{\hat{n}}_{i}
.
The Central Limit Theorem then implies that
\eta_{\nu}
must follow a Gaussian distribution, here with
\langle\eta_{\nu}(t)\rangle=0
.
Additionally, since the eigenvectors form an orthonormal basis where

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\nu}\cdot\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\nu^{\prime}}=\delta_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}
,
the corresponding two-time correlation function will be given by

\langle\eta_{\nu}(t)\eta_{\nu^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle=(v_{0}^{2}/2)\langle{\hat{n}}(t)\cdot{\hat{n}}(t^{\prime})\rangle\delta_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}
.
Replacing the heading autocorrelation expression in Eq. (
4
), we finally obtain

\langle\eta_{\nu}(t)\eta_{\nu}(0)\rangle=(v_{0}^{2}/2)\text{e}^{-D_{r}t}\cos{(\Omega~t)}
, which implies that the statistical properties of the noise
\eta_{\nu}
are the same for any mode
\nu
.

We now calculate the mean potential energy stored in each mode (see Supplemental Material, Section II for details
[
88
]
).
By solving Eq. (
6
), we first find

\displaystyle a_{\nu}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{\nu}(0)\text{e}^{-\lambda_{\nu}t}+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\eta_{\nu}(t^{\prime})\text{e}^{-\lambda_{\nu}(t-t^{\prime})}~.

(8)

From here, we can obtain the steady state mean squared value of
a_{\nu}(t)
by computing

\Lim\langle a_{\nu}^{2}(t)\rangle
to obtain

\displaystyle\langle a_{\nu}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{v_{0}^{2}(D_{r}+\lambda_{\nu})}{2\lambda_{\nu}\left[(D_{r}+\lambda_{\nu})^{2}+\Omega^{2}\right]}~.

(9)

The mean energy per mode is given by
E_{\nu}=\lambda_{\nu}\langle a_{\nu}^{2}\rangle/2
and can thus be expressed as

\displaystyle E_{\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{v_{0}^{2}(D_{r}+\lambda_{\nu})}{4\left[(D_{r}+\lambda_{\nu})^{2}+\Omega^{2}\right]}~.

(10)

We note in this equation that there is a critical curve in the
D_{r}-\Omega
plane that maximizes the mean potential energy injected into the system by the combined activity of all modes (as shown in Figure S2 of the Supplemental Material
[
88
]
).
We can obtain an approximate expression for this curve by finding the conditions that maximize the energy of the stiffest mode only (i.e., the mode least excited by the activity), which we identified as the main responsible for the maximum in the total potential energy.
Since the stiffest mode corresponds to the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\nu}=\lambda_{max}
, its energy will be
E^{*}=E_{\nu}|_{\lambda_{\nu}=\lambda_{max}}
and its maximum can be computed using
\partial E^{*}/\partial D_{r}=0
.
We thus find that the potential energy injected by activity is approximately maximized for

D_{r}^{*}=\Omega-\lambda_{max}
, corresponding to the dominant mode, i.e. the maximum eigenvalue
\lambda_{max}=5.93\pm 0.01
.
This curve is displayed as the green open diamonds in Fig.
1
(a).

In Fig.
2
(c), we visualize a color map of the energy
E/Nv_{0}^{2}=\sum_{\nu}E_{\nu}/Nv_{0}^{2}
on the
D_{r}-\Omega
plane that clearly shows an increase of elastic energy in the low
D_{r}
and low
\Omega
regimes.
Figure

\Omega=10,20

Equation (
10

In the high noise case,
D_{r}/\lambda_{\nu}\gg 1
and the mean energy per mode reduces to
E_{\nu}\approx v_{0}^{2}D_{r}/4(D_{r}^{2}+\Omega^{2})
, which gives rise to two limits:
(
i
) a low chirality limit
\Omega\to 0
, where
E_{\nu}\to v_{0}^{2}/4D_{r}
, and
(
ii
) a high chirality limit
\Omega\to\infty
, where
E_{\nu}\to 0
.
In the low noise case
D_{r}\to 0
, we find
E_{\nu}=v_{0}^{2}/4[\lambda_{\nu}^{2}+\Omega^{2}]
, which also gives rise to two limits:
(
i
) a low chirality limit with
E_{\nu}\to v_{0}^{2}/4\lambda_{\nu}^{2}
, where the lowest modes with
\lambda_{\nu}\ll 1
are enhanced,
and (
ii
) a high chirality limit with
E_{\nu}\to v_{0}^{2}/4\Omega^{2}
.
On the other hand, for any noise value, in the
\Omega\to 0
limit, we recover from Eq. (
10
) the same expressions previously obtained in
[
69
,
89
]
for standard (non-chiral or achiral) active particles, as expected.

Finally, in order to identify the emergence of mesoscopic order, we are interested in finding the mean velocity spectrum (see Supplemental Material, Section II A for details
[
88
]
).
We begin by expressing the velocity in Fourier space, computing its discrete Fourier transform

{\bf{v}}({\bf q})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\text{e}^{i{\bf q}\cdot{\bf{r}}_{j}^{0}}\delta\dot{{\bf{r}}}_{j}/N

in terms of the
{\bf{r}}_{i}^{0}
equilibrium reference positions of the disks.
Expanding
\delta\dot{{\bf{r}}}_{j}
in the normal mode basis, we find

\displaystyle\langle|{\bf{v}}({\bf q})^{2}|\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{\bf{v}}({\bf q})\cdot{\bf{v}}^{*}({\bf q})\rangle=\sum_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}\langle\dot{a}_{\nu}\dot{a}_{\nu^{\prime}}\rangle\bm{\xi}_{\nu}({\bf q})\cdot\bm{\xi}^{*}_{\nu^{\prime}}({\bf q})

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}\langle\dot{a}_{\nu}^{2}\rangle|\bm{\xi}_{\nu}({\bf q})|^{2}\delta_{\nu\nu^{\prime}},

where we defined

\bm{\xi}_{\nu}({\bf q})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\text{e}^{i{\bf q}\cdot{\bf{r}}_{j}^{0}}\bm{\xi}_{j}^{\nu}/N

as the discrete Fourier transform of the eigenvectors.
Using Eq. (
6
), we then replace

\langle\dot{a}_{\nu}^{2}\rangle=\lambda_{\nu}^{2}\langle a_{\nu}^{2}\rangle-2\lambda_{\nu}\langle a_{\nu}\eta_{\nu}\rangle+\langle\eta_{\nu}^{2}\rangle

into Eq. (
11
).
Here, the
\langle a_{\nu}^{2}\rangle
term is known from Eq. (
9
),
the equal-time correlation
\langle\eta_{\nu}^{2}\rangle=v_{0}^{2}/2
can be computed from
Eq. (
7
),
and the expression for

\langle a_{\nu}\eta_{\nu}\rangle=v_{0}^{2}(D_{r}+\lambda_{\nu})/2[(D_{r}+\lambda_{\nu})^{2}+\Omega^{2}]

in the steady-state (
t\to\infty
)
can be obtained from Eq. (
8
).
This leads to the following explicit expression for the velocity correlation function:

\langle|{\bf{v}}({\bf q})^{2}|\rangle\!=\!\frac{v_{0}^{2}}{2}\sum_{\nu}\left[1\!-\!\frac{\lambda_{\nu}(D_{r}+\lambda_{\nu})}{(D_{r}\!+\!\lambda_{\nu})^{2}\!+\!\Omega^{2}}\right]\!|\bm{\xi}_{\nu}({\bf q})|^{2}.

(12)

This equation allows us examine different limits.
For
\Omega=0
, it simplifies to the velocity correlation function

\langle|{\bf{v}}({\bf q})^{2}|\rangle=(v_{0}^{2}/2)\sum_{\nu}[D_{r}/(D_{r}+\lambda_{\nu})]|\bm{\xi}_{\nu}({\bf q})|^{2}
,
previously obtained for achiral active particles in
[
89
]
.
For
D_{r}=0
, it simplifies to the velocity correlation function
\langle|{\bf{v}}({\bf q})^{2}|\rangle=(v_{0}^{2}/2)\sum_{\nu}[\Omega^{2}/(\Omega^{2}+\lambda_{\nu}^{2})]|\bm{\xi}_{\nu}({\bf q})|^{2}
for disordered deterministic rotators.

In most experimental contexts, the extraction of the normal modes or their eigenvalues is unfeasible, except in specific scenarios like colloidal particle experiments
[
90
,
91
,
92
]
.
Current methods are often restricted to measuring in thermal equilibrium conditions and necessitate extensive data gathering. In the next subsection, we will therefore extend our findings to the framework of continuum elasticity theory, which only requires knowing the elastic constants of the material and is thus much easier to compute for real-world systems.

IV.3
Continuum Elastic Formulation

To derive the continuum formulation, we begin by writing the equation of motion for the displacement vector field
{\bf u}({\bf{r}})={\bf{r}}^{\prime}({\bf{r}})-{\bf{r}}
, which describes the deformed state
{\bf{r}}^{\prime}({\bf{r}})
with respect to the equilibrium reference state
{\bf{r}}
.
As detailed in the Supplemental Material
[
88
]
, in the presence of active forces this equation is given by

\displaystyle\dot{{\bf u}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathcal{\sigma}+{\bf{f}}_{\text{act}}.

(13)

Here,
\sigma
is the passive stress tensor, with components
\sigma_{\alpha\beta}=B\delta_{\alpha\beta}u_{\gamma\gamma}+2G(u_{\alpha\beta}-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\alpha\beta}u_{\gamma\gamma})
,
and activity is introduced through self-propulsion forces defined by

{\bf{f}}_{\text{act}}({\bf{r}},t)=v_{0}{\hat{n}}({\bf{r}},t)
.
In this expression for the stress tensor,
B
and
G
correspond respectively to the bulk and shear moduli of the isotropic solid,
the strain tensor components

u_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\partial_{\alpha}u_{\beta}+\partial_{\beta}u_{\alpha}\right]

are written in terms of spatial derivatives of the displacement vectors
{\bf u}({\bf{r}})
with respect to
\alpha,\beta\in\{x,y\}
, and the summation over repeated indexes is assumed.
We can see explicitly in Eq. (
13
) that this active solid is distinct from odd active matter, which only considers internal active stresses that can be written in terms of effective moduli
[
57
]
.

To proceed with the computations, we define the direct and inverse spatiotemporal Fourier transforms as

\displaystyle{\bf u}({\bf{r}},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int d^{2}{\bf q}\int d\omega~\tilde{{\bf u}}({\bf q},\omega)\text{e}^{-i({\bf q}\cdot{\bf{r}}+\omega t)},

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bf u}}({\bf q},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{2}{\bf{r}}\int dt~{\bf u}({\bf{r}},t)\text{e}^{i({\bf q}\cdot{\bf{r}}+\omega t)},

and write the continuum equation of motion (
13
) in Fourier space as

\displaystyle-i\omega\tilde{{\bf u}}({\bf q},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bf{f}}}_{\text{act}}({\bf q},\omega)-\mathbb{D}({\bf q})\tilde{{\bf u}}({\bf q},\omega).

(14)

Here,
\mathbb{D}({\bf q})
is a
2\times 2
dynamic matrix in Fourier space, given by

\mathbb{D}({\bf q})=\begin{bmatrix}Bq_{x}^{2}+Gq^{2}&Bq_{x}q_{y}\\
Bq_{x}q_{y}&Bq_{y}^{2}+Gq^{2}\\
\end{bmatrix},

where
q^{2}=q_{x}^{2}+q_{y}^{2}
(see Supplemental Material
[
88
]
for a detailed derivation), and we defined the active force
{\bf{f}}_{\text{act}}({\bf{r}},t)
in Fourier space as

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bf{f}}}_{\text{act}}({\bf q},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{0}\int d^{2}{\bf{r}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt~{\hat{n}}({\bf{r}},t)~\text{e}^{i({\bf q}\cdot{\bf{r}}+\omega t)}~.

(15)

We are interested in computing the velocity correlation functions. To do this, we begin by writing the orientational correlation in the continuum limit, replacing
{\hat{n}}_{i}(t)
by a continuous field
{\hat{n}}({\bf{r}},t)
, with
\langle{\hat{n}}_{i}(t)\cdot{\hat{n}}_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{i,j}\langle{\hat{n}}(t)\cdot{\hat{n}}(t^{\prime})\rangle
.
We then substitute the Kronecker delta
\delta_{i,j}
by its Dirac counterpart, using
\delta_{i,j}\to a^{2}\delta({\bf{r}}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime})
, where
a
is the smallest characteristic length scale of the system,
to obtain
\langle{\hat{n}}({\bf{r}},t)\cdot{\hat{n}}({\bf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=a^{2}\delta({\bf{r}}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime})\langle{\hat{n}}(t)\cdot{\hat{n}}(t^{\prime})\rangle
.
From Eq. (
15
), it is then clear that
\langle\tilde{{\bf{f}}}_{\text{act}}({\bf q},\omega)\rangle=0
, and the second order correlation function
C_{\tilde{F}}=\langle\tilde{{\bf{f}}}_{\text{act}}({\bf q},\omega)\cdot\tilde{{\bf{f}}}_{\text{act}}({\bf q}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\rangle
is simply given by

\displaystyle C_{\tilde{F}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2(2\pi)^{3}a^{2}v_{0}^{2}D_{r}}{(\omega-\Omega)^{2}+D_{r}^{2}}\delta({\bf q}+{\bf q}^{\prime})\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})~.

(16)

If we now consider a finite system (a square of side
L
, for simplicity), the wave vector becomes discretized.
We can thus replace the Dirac delta by the Kronecker delta,
\delta({\bf q}+{\bf q}^{\prime})\to\frac{1}{(\Delta q)^{2}}\delta_{{\bf q}^{\prime},-{\bf q}}
,
with
\Delta q\equiv 2\pi/L
.
This also leads us to define the spatially discrete Fourier transform
{\bf{f}}_{\text{act}}({\bf q},\omega)=\tilde{{\bf{f}}}_{\text{act}}({\bf q},\omega)/a^{2}
for discrete spatial wave vectors
{\bf q}
but continuous frequency
\omega
.
The correlation function for this discrete Fourier transform, given by
C_{F}=\langle{\bf{f}}_{\text{act}}({\bf q},\omega)\cdot{\bf{f}}_{\text{act}}({\bf q}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\rangle
, will be equal to

\displaystyle C_{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N\pi^{2}v_{0}^{2}D_{r}}{\phi\left[(\omega-\Omega)^{2}+D_{r}^{2}\right]}\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})~.

(17)

Finally, by decomposing Eq. (
15
) into a its
longitudinal and transverse components

\tilde{{\bf u}}=\tilde{u}_{\text{L}}({\bf q},\omega)\hat{{\bf q}}+\tilde{u}_{\text{T}}({\bf q},\omega)\hat{{\bf q}}_{\perp}
, with respect to the wave vector
{\bf q}
,
we can use
\tilde{{\bf{v}}}({\bf q},\omega)=-\text{i}\omega\tilde{{\bf u}}({\bf q},\omega)
to obtain the following expression for the mean squared velocity in Fourier space

\displaystyle\langle|{\bf{v}}({\bf q})|^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Nv_{0}^{2}}{2}\left[\frac{1+\chi(\xi_{\text{L}}q)^{2}}{1+2\chi(\xi_{\text{L}}q)^{2}+(\xi_{\text{L}}q)^{4}}\right.

(18)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\frac{1+\chi(\xi_{\text{T}}q)^{2}}{1+2\chi(\xi_{\text{T}}q)^{2}+(\xi_{\text{T}}q)^{4}}\right]~.

Here, we have respectively defined the longitudinal and transverse characteristic length scales as

\displaystyle\xi_{\text{L}}=\sqrt{\frac{B+G}{\sqrt{D_{r}^{2}+\Omega^{2}}}}~,~\xi_{\text{T}}=\sqrt{\frac{G}{\sqrt{D_{r}^{2}+\Omega^{2}}}},

(19)

and the control parameter as
\chi=D_{r}/\sqrt{D_{r}^{2}+\Omega^{2}}
.

Figures
2
(a) and
2
(b) display how the longitudinal and transverse characteristic length scales described by Eqs. (
19
) change across the different regimes on the
D_{r}-\Omega
plane.
We observe that the largest characteristic length scales are found for small
D_{r}
and
\Omega
values.
Looking back at figure

(a), we now identify the thin blue line as the
D_{r}
and
\Omega
values for which the smallest characteristic length scale
\xi_{\text{T}}
is equal to the typical equilibrium distance between particles
l_{0}
.
Below this line, the system develops mesoscopic scale correlations.

Next, we proceed to compute the mean-squared velocity
\langle|{\bf{v}}|^{2}\rangle
of our system in real space.
We can directly write the mean-squared velocity of all particles,
\langle|{\bf{v}}|^{2}\rangle=\langle\sum_{i}|{\bf{v}}_{i}|^{2}\rangle/N
, in continuum form as
\langle|{\bf{v}}|^{2}\rangle=[a^{2}/N(2\pi)^{2}]\int d^{2}{\bf q}~\langle|{\bf{v}}({\bf q})|^{2}\rangle
, where
\langle|{\bf{v}}({\bf q})|^{2}\rangle
was already evaluated in Eq. (
18
).
The upper limit of this integral is set by the inverse particle size, i.e., by the maximum wave number
q_{\mathrm{max}}=2\pi/a
where
a
is of the order of the particle size (this corresponds to
a=l_{0}
for the simulation). Using
\int d^{2}q=2\pi\int q~dq
, we thus write

\displaystyle\langle|{\bf{v}}|^{2}\rangle=\frac{a^{2}}{2\pi N}\int dq~q~\langle|{\bf{v}}({\bf q})|^{2}\rangle.

(20)

This expression can then be integrated numerically to compute the mean-squared velocity. In Fig.

(d), we visualize a color map of the mean-squared velocity
\langle|{\bf{v}}|^{2}\rangle/v_{0}^{2}
on the
D_{r}-\Omega
plane, which shows that the mean-squared velocity is small for low
D_{r}
and low
\Omega
values, in regimes displaying mesoscopic coherent motion.

\langle|{\bf{v}}|^{2}\rangle/v_{0}^{2}

for three different values of

\langle|{\bf{v}}|^{2}\rangle/v_{0}^{2}
value at intermediate

noise strengths, for high chirality (

(h) displays
\langle|{\bf{v}}|^{2}\rangle/v_{0}^{2}
as function of

for three different values of

|\langle{\bf{v}}^{2}\rangle|/v_{0}^{2}
(as apparent in Fig.

This curve can be directly computed through a numerical integration of Eq. (

Finally, we will investigate the collective temporal behavior of the system using the same continuum approach. Please see the Supplementary Material sections III B-C for details
[
88
]
. We can directly calculate the velocity autocorrelation function defined by the integral over Fourier space as

\displaystyle\langle{\bf{v}}(t)\cdot{\bf{v}}(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{4}}\int d^{2}{\bf q}\int d^{2}{\bf q}^{\prime}~\langle\tilde{{\bf{v}}}({\bf q},t)\cdot\tilde{{\bf{v}}}({\bf q}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle.

Here,
\langle\tilde{{\bf{v}}}({\bf q},t)\cdot\tilde{{\bf{v}}}({\bf q}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle
can be written in terms of the Fourier integral of
\langle\tilde{{\bf{v}}}({\bf q},\omega)\cdot\tilde{{\bf{v}}}({\bf q}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\rangle
over the frequencies
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
.
A long straightforward calculation then leads to the following expression for the velocity autocorrelation function

\displaystyle\langle{\bf{v}}(t)\cdot{\bf{v}}(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a^{2}v_{0}^{2}}{4\pi}\int_{q_{\mathrm{min}}}^{q_{\mathrm{max}}}dq~q~\left[\frac{\left[\left[(B+G)^{2}q^{4}(\Omega^{2}-D_{r}^{2})+(\Omega^{2}+D_{r}^{2})^{2}\right]\cos(\Omega~t)-2\Omega D_{r}(B+G)^{2}q^{4}\sin(\Omega~t)\right]\text{e}^{-D_{r}t}}{\left[((B+G)^{2}q^{4}+\Omega^{2}-D_{r}^{2})^{2}+4D_{r}^{2}\Omega^{2}\right]}\right.

(22)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\left[\left[G^{2}q^{4}(\Omega^{2}-D_{r}^{2})+(\Omega^{2}+D_{r}^{2})^{2}\right]\cos(\Omega~t)-2\Omega D_{r}G^{2}q^{4}\sin(\Omega~t)\right]\text{e}^{-D_{r}t}}{\left[(G^{2}q^{4}+\Omega^{2}-D_{r}^{2})^{2}+4D_{r}^{2}\Omega^{2}\right]}\right.

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left.\frac{(B+G)q^{2}D_{r}(D_{r}^{2}+\Omega^{2}-(B+G)^{2}q^{4})\text{e}^{-(B+G)q^{2}t}}{\left[(D_{r}^{2}+\Omega^{2}-(B+G)^{2}q^{4})^{2}+4(B+G)^{2}q^{4}\Omega^{2}\right]}-\frac{Gq^{2}D_{r}(D_{r}^{2}+\Omega^{2}-G^{2}q^{4})\text{e}^{-Gq^{2}t}}{\left[(D_{r}^{2}+\Omega^{2}-G^{2}q^{4})^{2}+4G^{2}q^{4}\Omega^{2}\right]}\right].

We note that the integral in this expression must be computed numerically and that the integration limits
q_{\mathrm{min}}
and
q_{\mathrm{max}}
are determined by the largest and smallest scales in the system, respectively. Figure S3 in the Supplemental Material
[

(panel c) and for a fixed

as a function of

. In all cases where

, oscillations with frequency

appear.

We finalize this section by exploring the scaling of the mean squared velocity in Eq. (

) in two limiting cases: the achiral active limit for
\chi=1
(setting
\Omega=0
) and the limit with no noise
\chi=0
(setting
D_{r}=0
).
In the achiral active case,
\langle|{\bf{v}}({\bf q})|^{2}\rangle
scales as
\sim(\xi_{\text{T}}q)^{-2}
and in the no noise case, it scales as
\langle|{\bf{v}}({\bf q})|^{2}\rangle\sim(\xi_{\text{T}}q)^{-4}
.
In both cases,
\langle|{\bf{v}}({\bf q})|^{2}\rangle
thus diverges for low
q
.

In these two limiting cases, we can also compute the closed-form analytic mean-squared velocity.
In the achiral active limit (
\Omega=0

[

\displaystyle\langle|{\bf{v}}|^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

This expression shows that the dominant scaling,

\langle|{\bf{v}}|^{2}\rangle\sim\xi_{\text{T}}^{-2}
or
\langle|{\bf{v}}|^{2}\rangle\sim\xi_{\text{L}}^{-2}
,
will follow the scaling Ansatz used for highly dense collective cellular motion in a monolayer
[

]
.
In the limit of no noise (
D_{r}=0
), we find

\displaystyle\langle|{\bf{v}}|^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a^{2}v_{0}^{2}}{8\pi}\left[\frac{\tan^{-1}(\xi_{\text{L}}^{2}q_{\mathrm{max}}^{2})}{\xi_{\text{L}}^{2}}+\frac{\tan^{-1}(\xi_{\text{T}}^{2}q_{\mathrm{max}}^{2})}{\xi_{\text{T}}^{2}}\right],

which also shows the dominant scaling
\langle|{\bf{v}}|^{2}\rangle\sim\xi_{\text{T}}^{-2}
or
\langle|{\bf{v}}|^{2}\rangle\sim\xi_{\text{L}}^{-2}
.
Note that the mean-squared velocity will have small values in regimes of low chirality and low noise, which matches the regime where elastic energy is stored in the sheet, leading to the emergence of the mesoscopic correlated motion in the velocity fields.

VII
Extension to Heterogeneous Systems

We now extend our results to heterogeneous systems of active particles, where they do not all have the same dynamical properties.
We will first study binary mixtures of particles with different chiralities and rotational diffusion constants in Subsection
VII.1
, and then analyze cases where the particles have a distribution of chirality and diffusion values in Subsection
VII.2
.

VII.1
Binary Mixtures

We consider active solids composed of two particle species,
A
and
B
, differentiated by their
D_{r}
and
\Omega
values. Each represents a fraction of the total, corresponding to their respective packing fractions,
\phi_{A}
and
\phi_{B}
. Since the active driving acts as a time-correlated but single-particle noise (see Eq. (
7
) and Eq. (
16
)), the total driving noise from two species can be computed by simple superposition, with no particle-particle cross-correlations.
Then quantities such as
\langle|{\bf{v}}({\bf q})|^{2}\rangle
and
\langle{\bf{v}}(t)\cdot{\bf{v}}(0)\rangle
, that we analytically derived in Sec.
IV
can be expressed as the average of the
A
and
B
contributions, weighted by their respective fractions of the total.
In the linear response regime, the general expression for

f=|{\bf{v}}({\bf q})|^{2}
or
f={\bf{v}}(t)\cdot{\bf{v}}(0)

is thus given by the following superposition formula

\displaystyle\langle f\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\phi_{A}}{\phi}\langle f\rangle_{A}+\frac{(\phi-\phi_{A})}{\phi}\langle f\rangle_{B}~,

(23)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle_{A}
and
\langle\cdot\rangle_{B}
represent the mean over populations
A
or
B
, respectively, and
\phi
is the total packing fraction.
We note that a mixture of positive and negative chiral active particles, with the same absolute chirality but different sign, behaves just as a system with uniform chirality due to the symmetry
\langle f\rangle_{A}(\Omega)=\langle f\rangle_{B}(-\Omega)
.
Despite this, the CMRO state shows the emergence of patches of uniform positive and negative chirality, in a proportion controlled by the ratio of
\phi_{A}/\phi_{B}
(see Supplemental Material Movie 8
[
88
]
).
We also note that in the case of a random mixture of chiral active and passive particles, where
v_{0}^{\mathrm{A}}>0
and
v_{0}^{\mathrm{B}}=0
, the dynamics of the CMRO state is controlled by
\phi_{A}/\phi
.

To illustrate the effects of having binary mixtures of particles, we present simulations and analytical results for three different species combinations, all displaying chiral mesoscopic range order.

VII.2
Heterogeneous Particles

We now further extend our analyses to investigate heterogeneous active solids composed of particles that follow a distribution of dynamical properties.
To do this, we consider a complex mixture of

With this general superposition expression, we can analytically predict the dynamics for active solids composed of diverse combinations of active particles, in the linear response regime.

VIII
Conclusions

In this work, we formulated the analytic linear response theory for an active solid composed of self-propelled particles with noisy chiral dynamics. We considered a minimal model with the potential for describing a broad range of systems, ranging from artificial active solids made of chiral self-propelled robots to biological tissues with emergent macroscopic chiral order. We developed an analytic formulation that allowed us to fully describe all the observed dynamics in the linear response regime, perfectly matching our numerical results.

We described the emergence of four different regimes in the phase space formed by the chirality
\Omega
and the rotational diffusion coefficient
D_{r}
. For small enough
D_{r}
and
\Omega
, we observe chiral (CMRO) and achiral (MRO) self-organized states displaying mesoscopic range order.
For larger
D_{r}
and
\Omega
, we only find chiral (CD) and achiral (DD) disordered states.
In all cases, the chiral sates appear for
D_{r}<\Omega
and the achiral states, for
D_{r}>\Omega
.
We then explored the dynamics of the melting regime by increasing the activity
v_{0}
, showing that our analytic results for the spatial velocity correlations and the temporal velocity autocorrelations agree with simulations up to an active speed
v_{0}\approx 0.1
, just below
v_{0}=0.12
, the melting point predicted by our active solid theory.
Finally, we showed that our analytic approaches can be extended to consider particles with heterogeneous dynamical features, including different chirality and rotational diffusion levels. We derived analytic superposition expressions for binary and more complex mixtures of heterogeneous active particles, demonstrating their excellent agreement with simulations.

Our work is consistent with the (sparse) literature on chiral ABP solids. Notably we recover the oscillatory correlations and ‘hammering’ resonance observed by
[
86
]
in the glassy state.
Recently, Caprini et al.
[
87
]
explored the emergence of self-reverting vortices in the absence of alignment interactions as a result of the interplay between attractive interactions and chirality. They observed two kinds of vortices with persistent and oscillatory behavior, similar to our observation that the correlated velocity fields in the mesoscopic range can either be persistent (MRO) or oscillatory (CMRO).
However, due to subtly different equations that include inertia and spatial noise, we cannot currently compare results quantitatively.

We hope that our analytical descriptions of dense, solid chiral active systems can help establish a foundation for the systematic understanding of the emergent dynamics in this type of systems. Future research could explore the scalability of our theory, its practical applications in synthetic biology, and its potential impact on the design and control of rotating correlated velocity fields in active solids.

Appendix A
Orientation Autocorrelation

We adopt the method of exact moments calculation for stiff chains, as described in Hermans et al.
[
105
]
, to determine the exact moments for active dynamics. This approach, previously studied in
[
106
,
107
,
108
]
, is utilized to obtain the exact dynamics for a chiral active Brownian particle in a harmonic trap, as also detailed in Debets et al.
[
86
]
. Utilizing the Laplace transform
\tilde{P}({\hat{n}},s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\text{e}^{-st}\,P({\hat{n}},t)
in Eq. (
3
) and defining the mean of an observable
\langle\psi\rangle_{s}=\int d{\hat{n}}\,\psi({\hat{n}})\tilde{P}({\hat{n}},s)
, multiplying by
\psi({\hat{n}})
and integrating over all possible
{\hat{n}}
, we find

\displaystyle-\langle\psi\rangle_{0}+s\langle\psi\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{r}\langle\nabla_{\hat{n}}^{2}\psi\rangle_{s}+\Omega~\langle{\hat{n}}^{\perp}\cdot\nabla_{{\hat{n}}}\psi\rangle_{s}~,

(25)

where the initial condition sets
\langle\psi\rangle_{0}=\int d{\hat{n}}\,\psi({\hat{n}})P({\hat{n}},0)
. Without any loss of generality, we consider the initial condition to follow
P({\hat{n}},0)=\delta({\hat{n}}-{\hat{n}}_{0})
, where
{\hat{n}}_{0}
is the initial orientation of the particle.

We utilize Eq. (
25
) to compute exact orientation correlation as a function of time.
We consider the initial orientation of the particles along
\langle{\hat{n}}\rangle={\hat{n}}_{0}
. To calculate
\langle{\hat{n}}\rangle
, we use
\psi={\hat{n}}
in the Eq. (
25
), leads to

\displaystyle\langle{\hat{n}}\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\hat{n}}_{0}+\Omega~\langle{\hat{n}}^{\perp}\rangle_{s}}{s+D_{r}}~,

(26)

where we can calculate
\langle{\hat{n}}^{\perp}\rangle_{s}
with initial perpendicular orientation
\langle{\hat{n}}^{\perp}\rangle={\hat{n}}^{\perp}_{0}
, using
\psi={\hat{n}}^{\perp}
in the Eq. (
25
) gives
\langle{\hat{n}}^{\perp}\rangle_{s}=({\hat{n}}^{\perp}_{0}-\Omega\langle{\hat{n}}\rangle_{s})/(s+D_{r})
, substituting back into the Eq. (
26
)

\displaystyle\langle{\hat{n}}\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\hat{n}}_{0}(s+D_{r})+\Omega~{\hat{n}}^{\perp}_{0}}{(s+D_{r})^{2}+\Omega^{2}}~.

(27)

Inverse Laplace transform leads to
\langle{\hat{n}}(t)\rangle=\text{e}^{-D_{r}t}[{\hat{n}}_{0}\cos(\Omega t)+{\hat{n}}^{\perp}_{0}\sin(\Omega t)]
. Taking the dot product with the initial orientation, we get the orientation autocorrelation in Eq. (
4
).

Appendix C
Determining the Elastic Moduli and Continuum Theory Estimation to Compare with Simulations

We first equilibrate the non-equilibrium steady state configurations by letting
v_{0}=0
in Eq. (
1
). The positions reaches to its equilibrium positions at long time. Utilizing the equilibrated positions, we construct dynamical Hessian matrix. We transform this dynamical matrix to the Fourier space with appropriate grid space of
{\bf q}
. The longitudinal and transverse eigenvalues are
(B+G)q^{2}
and
Gq^{2}
, respectively.

We determine the bulk modulus
B
and shear modulus
G
by performing a linear fit of the radially averaged longitudinal and transverse eigenvalues against
q^{2}
, focusing on the limit where
q^{2}\leq 1
. Our calculations for soft disks yield the moduli values: :
G=0.61
,
B=2.03
, and
(B+G)/G=4.33
with relative error
<1\%
(averaging over 10 independent estimations).

When we compare numerical results with the continuum theory, the simulations are done for relatively large but finite systems of size
L
, with minimum length scale given by the particle size
a=2r_{0}
, where
r_{0}
is the radius of the particle.
This guide us to perform discrete space Fourier transformation in numerical analysis. On the other hand, by setting
L\to\infty
and
a\to 0
in the analytic calculation give the results in hydrodynamic limit.

For consistency between two approaches, we use the following space continuous Fourier transform

\displaystyle{\bf u}({\bf{r}},t)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int d^{2}{\bf q}~\tilde{{\bf u}}({\bf q},t)\text{e}^{-i{\bf q}\cdot{\bf{r}}}.

(31)

By considering the finite system and particle sizes, we discretize the integral into

\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int d^{2}{\bf q}\to\frac{1}{Na^{2}}\sum_{{\bf q}}~~\text{and}~~\int d^{2}{\bf{r}}\to a^{2}\sum_{r},

where
N=4\phi L^{2}/\pi a^{2}
,
\phi
is the packing fraction of the system close to
1
for dense systems. In the sum
{\bf q}
takes discrete values defined by the geometry of the problem. For instance a square lattice of linear size
L
,
{\bf q}\equiv(q_{x},q_{y})=2\pi/L(m,n)
where integers
m,n
satisfying
0\leq m,n\leq L/a-1
. Thus, the discrete space Fourier transform
{\bf u}({\bf q},t)
is related to the continuous Fourier transform
\tilde{{\bf u}}({\bf q},t)
through
\tilde{{\bf u}}({\bf q},t)=a^{2}{\bf u}({\bf q},t)
.
