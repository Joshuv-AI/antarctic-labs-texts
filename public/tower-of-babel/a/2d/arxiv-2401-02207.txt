A noise-limiting quantum algorithm using mid-circuit measurements for dynamical correlations at infinite temperature
Etienne Granet and Henrik Dreyer

Abstract
It is generally considered that the signal output by a quantum circuit is attenuated exponentially fast in the number of gates. This letter explores how algorithms using mid-circuit measurements and classical conditioning as computational tools (and not as error mitigation or correction subroutines) can be naturally resilient to complete decoherence, and maintain quantum states with useful properties even for infinitely deep noisy circuits. Specifically, we introduce a quantum channel built out of mid-circuit measurements and feed-forward, that can be used to compute dynamical correlations at infinite temperature and canonical ensemble expectation values for any Hamiltonian. The unusual property of this algorithm is that in the presence of a depolarizing channel it still displays a meaningful, non-zero signal in the large depth limit. We showcase the noise resilience of this quantum channel on Quantinuum’s H1-1 ion-trap quantum computer.



Introduction.
— The main obstacle to large-scale quantum computing is the imperfect realization of quantum operations
Preskill 2018
;
Knill 2005
. In the presence of noise, the signal output by a quantum circuit is generically exponentially attenuated with the number of gates
Emerson et al. 2005
;
Moses et al. 2023
;
Mayer et al. 2021
;
Proctor et al. 2022
;
Baldwin et al. 2022
, causing exponential overheads which threaten the prospects of the utility of quantum computers.
Quantum error correcting codes are particular circuits fundamentally relying on mid-circuit measurements and classical conditioning on the measurement outcomes (also called
feed-forward
) that delay this complete depolarizing for very deep circuits
Shor 1995
;
Knill et al. 1998
;
Calderbank and Shor 1996
;
Steane 1996
;
Kitaev 2003
;
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
. However, these circuits only serve as subroutines within unitary circuits and do not perform a computational task themselves. The purpose of this work is to investigate whether algorithms that use mid-circuit measurements and feed-forward directly as computational tools (rather than error correcting subroutines) are naturally
noise-resilient
in the sense that in the limit of an infinitely deep circuit there remains a non-zero signal that contains information on the task fulfilled by the algorithm.

In this Letter, we answer this question in the affirmative. Specifically, we introduce a simple “shuffling” quantum channel based on mid-circuit measurements and feed-forward that outputs a non-zero signal when repeated an arbitrary number of times, even in the presence of noise. Crucially, this non-zero signal also carries non-trivial, physically interesting information: We show that this quantum channel can be used to compute finite temperature expectation values for any Hamiltonian
H
, as well as dynamical correlations at infinite temperature
{\rm tr}\,[Se^{iHt}Se^{-iHt}]
where
S
is any Pauli string. We also show that it can prepare certain density matrices in a noise-resilient way as fixed points of a quantum channel.

;

;

;

;

;
Tantivasadakarn et al. 2021
;
Tantivasadakarn et al. 2023
;
Foss-Feig et al. 2023
;
Iqbal et al. 2023b
, or on top of another algorithm to mitigate the effect of noise
McArdle et al. 2019
;
Botelho et al. 2022
;
Bonet-Monroig et al. 2018
. Some algorithms use a weak form of feed-forward where measurement outcomes are used as a stopping criterion or to partially reset the state

The shuffling quantum channel.
— Let us start with introducing the main quantum channel that we will study in this Letter. The effect of the channel on expectation values is given by Eq (
2

For a generic Hamiltonian
H
on
L
qubits, we define the quantum channel
Shuffle
(H)
by the three following steps.

1.

Pick a Pauli matrix at each site, i.e.
M_{1},...,M_{L}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}
uniformly at random.

2.

Prepare an ancilla in state

(1)

3.

Measure the ancilla. Only if the measurement outcome is

Let us denote

the initial density matrix of the

(3)

Moreover, the probability

of measuring

p=\frac{{\rm tr}\,[\cos^{2}(H)]}{2^{L}}\,.

U_{n}=\left(\begin{matrix}\cos E_{n}&i\sin E_{n}\\
i\sin E_{n}&\cos E_{n}\end{matrix}\right)\,,

(5)

with

|0\rangle

L
qubits with the operator
\langle 0|U|0\rangle=\cos(H_{\boldsymbol{M}})
if we measure

\langle 1|U|0\rangle=i\sin(H_{\boldsymbol{M}})
if we measure

\rho^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{M}}
that we obtain after the steps (2) and (3), assuming

have been picked at step (1), is

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{M}}

\displaystyle=\sin(H_{\boldsymbol{M}})\rho\sin(H_{\boldsymbol{M}})+\boldsymbol{M}\cos(H_{\boldsymbol{M}})\rho\cos(H_{\boldsymbol{M}})\boldsymbol{M}

(6)

\displaystyle=\sin(H_{\boldsymbol{M}})\rho\sin(H_{\boldsymbol{M}})+\cos(H)\boldsymbol{M}\rho\boldsymbol{M}\cos(H)\,.

Taking into account all the

M_{1},...,M_{L}
at step (1), the density matrix

It follows

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4^{L}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{M}\in\mathcal{M}\end{subarray}}\sin(H_{\boldsymbol{M}})\rho\sin(H_{\boldsymbol{M}})+\frac{\cos^{2}(H)}{2^{L}}\,.

(8)

We note that the feed-forward in step (3) is crucial to obtain a non-trivial density matrix. Had we always applied
\boldsymbol{M}
on the qubits independently of the measurement outcome of step (3), the density matrix
\rho^{\prime}
would have been the completely depolarized state
1/2^{L}
.

Let us now consider a given Pauli string operator
\boldsymbol{M^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{M}
, and denote the expectation values

m={\rm tr}\,[\boldsymbol{M^{\prime}}\rho]\,,\qquad m^{\prime}={\rm tr}\,[\boldsymbol{M^{\prime}}\rho^{\prime}]\,.

(9)

Multiplying (
8

\boldsymbol{M^{\prime}}
and taking the trace, we make appear in the sum the terms
{\rm tr}\,[\boldsymbol{M^{\prime}}\sin(H_{\boldsymbol{M}})\rho\sin(H_{\boldsymbol{M}})]

\rho=\sum_{\boldsymbol{M^{\prime\prime}}\in\mathcal{M}}c_{\boldsymbol{M^{\prime\prime}}}\boldsymbol{M^{\prime\prime}}\,,

(10)

we write

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle tr}\,[\boldsymbol{M^{\prime}}\sin(H_{\boldsymbol{M}})\rho\sin(H_{\boldsymbol{M}})]

(11)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{M^{\prime\prime}}\in\mathcal{M}}c_{\boldsymbol{M^{\prime\prime}}}{\rm tr}\,[\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{M^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{M}\sin(H)\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{M^{\prime\prime}}\boldsymbol{M}\sin(H)]\,.

Let us perform first the sum over
M_{i}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}
appearing in (
8
). We note that
M_{i}M_{i}^{\prime}M_{i}
is either
M_{i}^{\prime}
(if
M_{i}=I
, or
M_{i}^{\prime}=I
, or
M_{i}=M_{i}^{\prime}
), or
-M_{i}^{\prime}
(in the remaining cases). If

, then at least one of the two is not

M_{i}^{\prime}\neq I
. If
M_{i}^{\prime\prime}=I
, then one sees that one gets an amplitude
{\rm tr}\,[\boldsymbol{M^{\prime}}\sin(H)\boldsymbol{M^{\prime\prime}}\sin(H)]
with a

and
M_{i}=M_{i}^{\prime}
, and with a

. If
M_{i}^{\prime}=I

M_{i}=I
and
M_{i}\neq M_{i}^{\prime},M_{i}^{\prime\prime}
, and a
-
sign in the two remaining cases. Hence this term also vanishes once summed over
M_{i}
. It follows that once we sum over all the

, only the terms where

give a non-zero contribution. This yields

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{M}\in\mathcal{M}\end{subarray}}{\rm tr}\,[\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{M^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{M}\sin(H)\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{M^{\prime\prime}}\boldsymbol{M}\sin(H)]

\displaystyle=4^{L}{\rm tr}\,[\boldsymbol{M^{\prime}}\sin(H)\boldsymbol{M^{\prime\prime}}\sin(H)]\prod_{i=1}^{L}\delta_{M_{i}^{\prime},M_{i}^{\prime\prime}}\,.

Noting that we have

\displaystyle m\frac{{\rm tr}\,[\boldsymbol{M^{\prime}}\sin(H)\boldsymbol{M^{\prime}}\sin(H)]}{2^{L}}+\frac{{\rm tr}\,[\boldsymbol{M^{\prime}}\cos^{2}(H)]}{2^{L}}\,,

(13)

which is precisely (

). To compute the probability

has been picked at step (1), then this probability

Summing over

Dynamical correlations at infinite temperature.
— Let us show how the shuffling quantum channel can be used to compute dynamical correlations of Pauli strings
S=M_{1}...M_{L}
at infinite temperature, defined as

C_{S}(t)=\frac{{\rm tr}\,[e^{-iHt}Se^{iHt}S]}{2^{L}}\,.

(16)

These quantities are relevant for transport properties and have attracted particular interest recently
Rosenberg et al. 2023
;
Gopalakrishnan et al. 2019
;
De Nardis et al. 2020
;
Kanász-Nagy et al. 2017
;
Scheie et al. 2021
;
Granet and Essler 2020
;
Granet et al. 2022
;
Rakovszky et al. 2022
;
Žnidarič 2011
;
Dupont and Moore 2020
;
Parker et al. 2019
.
To compute this quantity with the shuffling quantum channel, we write

C_{S}(t)=\frac{{\rm tr}\,[S\cos(Ht)S\cos(Ht)]}{2^{L}}+\frac{{\rm tr}\,[S\sin(Ht)S\sin(Ht)]}{2^{L}}\,.

(17)

Let us consider
\rho
an arbitrary initial density matrix for which we know
{\rm tr}\,[\rho S]
. We then apply on
\rho
either
Shuffle
(Ht)
with probability
1/2
, or
Shuffle
(Ht+\tfrac{\pi}{2})
with probability
1/2
. We measure the Pauli string
S
and denote
{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime}S]
the average outcome. Using formula (
2
), we have thus

{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime}S]=\frac{A_{0}+A_{\pi/2}}{2}{\rm tr}\,[\rho S]+\frac{B_{0}+B_{\pi/2}}{2}\,,

(18)

with
A_{x},B_{x}
the coefficients (
3
) corresponding to the Hamiltonian
Ht+x
. From (
3
) we find thus for
S\neq I

{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime}S]=\frac{C_{S}(t){\rm tr}\,[\rho S]}{2}\,.

(19)

Hence, one can directly compute the value of
C_{S}(t)
provided we chose
\rho
such that
{\rm tr}\,[\rho S]\neq 0
. We note there are other similar ways of extracting
C_{S}(t)
from this quantum channel
sup
.

This algorithm for dynamical correlations at infinite temperature presents advantages compared to previously existing techniques. Computing the trace over the Hilbert space through a purification requires to double the number of qubits, whereas here there is only one ancilla qubit overhead. Implementing the trace through a Haar random state preparation
Summer et al. 2023
has a gate overhead to prepare the Haar random state, and also a shot overhead because it computes the square of the dynamical correlations and can only treat one site at a time, which increases considerably the variance. Finally, for
S
single Pauli matrices, evaluating the trace as a sum over product states in the basis of
S
can have a large statistical sampling overhead if dynamical correlations are small in this basis, whereas our algorithm is basis-independent.

Mixed state preparation.
— The density matrix constructed previously in (
19
) contains information about the physics of the system through
C_{S}(t)
, but also on the initial density matrix through
{\rm tr}\,[\rho S]
. This can be problematic for strings
S
involving different Pauli matrices at different sites, since the preparation of
\rho
such that
{\rm tr}\,[\rho S]\neq 0
for multiple such
S
would become non-trivial.

The shuffling quantum channel through the relation (
2
) actually enables one to prepare non-trivial density matrices independently of the initial density matrix. Let us repeat
n
times the channel
Shuffle
(H)
on an initial density matrix
\rho_{0}
, denoting
\rho_{n}
the resulting density matrix. Eq (
2
) yields a geometric series for any string
S

{\rm tr}\,[S\rho_{n}]=A^{n}{\rm tr}\,[S\rho_{0}]+\frac{1-A^{n}}{1-A}B\,,

(20)

with
A,B
given in (
3
). In particular, since
|A|<1
(except for very specific choices of
H,S
where we would have
|A|=1
) when
n\to\infty
we obtain

\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}\,{\rm tr}\,[S\rho_{n}]=\frac{B}{1-A}\,,

(21)

which does not depend on the initial density matrix. Moreover the convergence is exponentially fast in
n
with rate
\log 1/|A|
. We thus prepare a non-trivial density matrix whose expectation values contain physical information about the system studied.

Noise-limiting behaviour: analytics.
— We now argue that the algorithms based on the repetition of the shuffling quantum channel are to some extent resilient to noise. Let us consider a noise model where a global depolarizing channel with amplitude
\lambda
is applied on the
L
qubits every time we apply the
Shuffle
(H)
channel. This depolarizing channel transforms a density matrix
\rho
into

\rho\mapsto(1-\lambda)\rho+\frac{\lambda}{2^{L}}\,.

(22)

Hence, using (
2
), the (noisy) density matrix
\rho^{\prime}_{\rm noisy}
that describes the
L
qubits after one round of
Shuffle
(H)
and one global depolarizing channel satisfies for
S\neq I

{\rm tr}\,[S\rho^{\prime}_{\rm noisy}]=(1-\lambda)(A{\rm tr}\,[S\rho]+B)\,.

(23)

Denoting
\rho_{{\rm noisy},n}
the density matrix obtained after
n
noisy applications of
Shuffle
(H)
, the limiting value reached in the limit of an infinite number of applications is

\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}{\rm tr}\,[S\rho_{{\rm noisy},n}]=\frac{(1-\lambda)B}{1-(1-\lambda)A}\,,

(24)

which is
finite
, even if an infinite number of gates and noise channels have been applied. One notes that none of the
L
qubits are ever measured or re-initialized. This shows the noise resilience of the preparation of density matrices through the shuffling quantum channel.

Let us explain why this noise-limiting behaviour is directly related to the use of mid-circuit measurements and feed-forward. A circuit involving only unitary operations and measurements without feed-forward can always be written as the composition of density matrix maps
T(\rho)=U\rho U^{\dagger}
with
U
unitary or
T(\rho)=\sum_{m}P_{m}\rho P_{m}^{\dagger}
with
\sum_{m}P_{m}P_{m}^{\dagger}={\rm Id}
. These maps both satisfy
T({\rm Id})={\rm Id}
(also called “unital” maps), so the completely mixed state
\frac{{\rm Id}}{2^{L}}
is always a fixed point of such circuits. However, with feed-forward these density matrix maps are in general
T(\rho)=\sum_{m}U_{m}P_{m}\rho P_{m}^{\dagger}U_{m}^{\dagger}
with
U_{m}
unitary, for which the identity is (generically) not a fixed point. This prevents thus the completely depolarized state to be reached in the limit of large number of gates.

This explanation yields the following nice interpretation of the noise resilience of our algorithm. The resulting density matrix after a large number of rounds converges to the fixed point of the quantum channel. Because of the presence of mid-circuit measurements and feed-forward, this fixed point cannot be (in general) the completely depolarized channel. Now, incorporating noise in the process (under any form) will only smoothly change the quantum channel, and so only smoothly change the fixed point, instead of sending it to the completely depolarized state. The noise-resilience of the prepared density matrix is thus built into the algorithm itself.

Canonical ensemble expectation values.
— Finally, we show that using a shuffling quantum channel with multiple ancillas, one can compute expectation values at finite temperature. Although this application is not of practical interest (as we will show, it is exponential in system size runtime), it shows the versatility of our channel in extracting physical information about a system. Let us consider a Hamiltonian
H
on
L
qubits, an observable
O
and an inverse temperature
\beta
. We define

H^{\prime}=\arctan\theta+\frac{\beta H-\epsilon Z_{v}O}{2\theta N}\,,

(26)

on the system of
L

, where
Z_{v}
denotes the Pauli matrix
Z
applied on the virtual qubit

, and where we introduced parameters
\epsilon>0
and

, as well as an integer

. As shown in the SM

, the shuffling quantum channel can be generalized into a channel

(H,N)
that uses

N
ancillas, and then in step (3) one applies

on the
L
qubits only if

N
ancillas have been measured to be in state

. An identical relation to (
2
) follows then with precise coefficients

and
B

sup
. In the particular case of

in (
26
), because

commutes with

, we have

{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime}Z_{v}]=A{\rm tr}\,[\rho Z_{v}]+B\,,

(27)

with

A=1-\frac{{\rm Tr}\,[\cos^{2N}(H^{\prime})]}{2^{L+1}}\,,\quad B=\frac{{\rm Tr}\,[Z_{v}\cos^{2N}(H^{\prime})]}{2^{L+1}}\,,

where

denotes the trace on the
L

\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}{\rm Tr}\,[Z_{v}\rho_{n}]=\frac{{\rm Tr}\,[Z_{v}\cos^{2N}(H^{\prime})]}{{\rm Tr}\,[\cos^{2N}(H^{\prime})]}\,.

This prepares the virtual qubit in an ensemble that is close to a canonical ensemble. Indeed, for

, we have

\cos^{2N}(H^{\prime})\sim\frac{e^{-\beta H+\epsilon Z_{v}O}}{(1+\theta^{2})^{N}}\,.

Hence, performing the trace over the virtual qubit

\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}{\rm Tr}\,[Z_{v}\rho_{n}]=\epsilon\frac{{\rm tr}\,[Oe^{-\beta H}]}{{\rm tr}\,[e^{-\beta H}]}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2},\tfrac{\beta||H||}{N\theta})\,,

to denote the trace over the

of traces
{\rm tr}\,[Oe^{-\beta H}]/2^{L}
and
{\rm tr}\,[e^{-\beta H}]/2^{L}
that can each of them be exponentially small. Using (
28
) and (
30
), the number of rounds
n
required to reach this limit is
1/\log(1-\frac{{\rm tr}\,[e^{-\beta H}]}{2^{L}(1+\theta^{2})^{N}})
. The time complexity is thus exponential in the system size
L
, which is the expected scaling for a finite-temperature algorithm that applies to generic Hamiltonians.

Discussion.
—
In this Letter we have provided a first example of how non-error-corrected algorithms can avoid total depolarisation in the infinite-depth limit. This proof of principle opens up a number of possible future directions. For example, the algorithms presented here for finite temperature expectation values have an exponential time complexity, which is unavoidable without further assumption on
H
. It would be very valuable to investigate whether these features can be improved for local Hamiltonians
H
.

II
Generalization of the channel to multiple ancillas.

In this Section we generalize the shuffling quantum channel to multiple ancillas. Namely, for an integer
N
we define
Shuffle
(H,N)
through the following steps.

1.

Pick a Pauli matrix at each site, i.e.
M_{1},...,M_{L}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}
uniformly at random.

2.

Prepare
N
ancillas in state
|0\rangle_{a_{1}}...|0\rangle_{a_{N}}
. For
\ell=1,...,N
, apply
e^{iH_{M_{1}...M_{L}}X_{a_{\ell}}}
.

3.

Measure the
N
ancillas. Only if the measurement outcome is
0
for all the
N
ancillas, apply
M_{1}...M_{L}
on the
L
qubits.

The analysis of
Shuffle
(H)
is straightforwardly generalized to the case
N>1
. For any Pauli string operator
S=M^{\prime}_{1}...M^{\prime}_{L}
with
M^{\prime}_{i}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}
, we have

{\rm tr}\,[S\rho^{\prime}]=A{\rm tr}\,[S\rho]+B\,,

(32)

with

\displaystyle A=\frac{1}{2^{L}}\sum_{p=0}^{N-1}\binom{N}{p}{\rm tr}\,[

\displaystyle S\cos^{p}(H)\sin^{N-p}(H)

(33)

\displaystyle\times S\cos^{p}(H)\sin^{N-p}(H)]\,,

and

B=\frac{{\rm tr}\,[S\cos^{2N}(H)]}{2^{L}}\,.

(34)

Moreover, the probability
p
of measuring
0
in all the
N
ancillas is

p=\frac{{\rm tr}\,[\cos^{2N}(H)]}{2^{L}}\,.

(35)

When
S
commutes with
H
, we obtain

A=1-\frac{{\rm tr}\,[\cos^{2N}(H)]}{2^{L}}\,.

(36)

In the main text, the system on which we apply this quantum channel contains
L
qubits and one extra ancilla
v
. It is thus a system with
L+1
qubits. Moreover the Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
commutes with the particular choice of Pauli string
S=Z_{v}
that we consider. This yields thus formula (28) in the main text.

III
Alternative way of extracting the coefficients
A
and
B
.

In this Section we present an alternative way of computing coefficients
A
and
B
appearing in the shuffling quantum channel output in Eq (2) of the main text. By applying twice
Shuffle
(H)
on a density matrix
\rho
, and denoting
\rho^{\prime\prime}
the output density matrix, we have for any Pauli string
S

{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime\prime}S]=A^{2}{\rm tr}\,[\rho S]+AB+B\,.

(37)

It follows thus that we can extract
A
from the ratio

A=\frac{{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime\prime}S]-{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime}S]}{{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime}S]-{\rm tr}\,[\rho S]}\,,

(38)

where we recall that
\rho^{\prime}
denotes the density matrix after one single run of
Shuffle
(H)
. Then,
B
is given by

B={\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime}S]-A{\rm tr}\,[\rho S]\,.

(39)

Applying this process to the Hamiltonians
Ht
and
Ht+\tfrac{\pi}{2}
, one can then sum the resulting
A
’s and obtain the dynamical correlation
C_{S}(t)
. This is the technique we used in Fig 2 of the main text to compute dynamical correlations at infinite temperature.

This way of computing
C_{S}(t)
presents sometimes a lower variance compared to the one quoted in the main text, although more involved. Let us indeed compute the variance
\Delta
in the estimation of
A
. The variance in estimating
{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime}S]
using
K
shots is
\frac{1-{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime}S]^{2}}{K}
. Hence, using Gaussian error propagation, at large
K
the variance
\Delta
in estimating
A
through formula (
38
) is

\displaystyle\Delta=

\displaystyle\frac{1-{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime}S]^{2}}{K}\frac{1}{({\rm tr}\,[\rho S]-{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime}S])^{2}}+\frac{1-{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime\prime}S]^{2}}{K}\frac{1}{({\rm tr}\,[\rho S]-{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime}S])^{2}}

(40)

\displaystyle+\frac{1-{\rm tr}\,[\rho S]^{2}}{K}\left(\frac{{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime}S]-{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime\prime}S]}{({\rm tr}\,[\rho S]-{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime}S])^{2}}\right)^{2}+\frac{1-{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime}S]^{2}}{K}\left(\frac{{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime}S]-{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime\prime}S]}{({\rm tr}\,[\rho S]-{\rm tr}\,[\rho^{\prime}S])^{2}}\right)^{2}\,.

Expressed in terms of
m\equiv{\rm tr}\,[\rho S]
and
A,B
, this is

\displaystyle\Delta=\frac{1}{K(m(A-1)+B)^{2}}\left[2+2A^{2}-(mA+B)^{2}-(mA^{2}+AB+B)^{2}-m^{2}A^{2}-(mA+B)^{2}A^{2}\right]\,.

(41)

Dynamical correlations typically become small as
t
increases, meaning that
A
is typically small, whereas
B
remains of order
1
since the two
B
’s obtained from
Ht
and
Ht+\tfrac{\pi}{2}
have to add up to
1
. When
A\to 0
and
B\to 1
, we see that we have
\Delta\to 0
at fixed
K
. When
A\to 0
for
B=1/2
we have
\Delta=\frac{2}{3K}
if we choose
m=-1
. For the method stated in the text, the variance in estimating
C_{S}(t)
is
\frac{4}{K}
when
C_{S}(t)\to 0
. This approach yields thus typically a lower variance in these situations.
