Colouring random subgraphs
Boris Bukh, Bhargav Narayanan

Abstract
. We study several basic problems about colouring the p p -random subgraph G p G_{p} of an arbitrary graph G G , focusing primarily on the chromatic number and colouring number of G p G_{p} . In particular, we show that there exist infinitely many k k -regular graphs G G for which the colouring number (i.e., degeneracy) of G 1 / 2 G_{1/2} is at most k / 3 + o ⁡ ( k ) k/3+o(k) with high probability, thus disproving the natural prediction that such random graphs must have colouring number at least k / 2 − o ⁡ ( k ) k/2-o(k) .

1.
Introduction

For a graph
G
and
p\in(0,1)
, let
G_{p}
denote the random subgraph of
G
obtained by randomly including each edge of
G
independently each with probability
p
. Here, we shall study some basic questions about properly colouring the vertices of
G_{p}
for fixed
p\in(0,1)
, motivated primarily by the following old question of Erdős and Hajnal
[
9
,
10
]
that remains frustratingly open: is it true that for every pair
t,g\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a
k=k(t,g)\in\mathbb{N}
such that any graph with chromatic number at least
k
contains a subgraph with chromatic number at least
t
and girth at least
g
? A natural step towards this question of Erdős and Hajnal — motivated by Erdős’ randomised construction (see
[
2
]
) of graphs of large girth and chromatic number — is to study the colouring-related properties of the random subgraph
G_{p}
of an arbitrary graph
G
of large chromatic number.

Concretely, we shall focus on the two problems that we next describe. First, we study the following ‘chromatic number problem’: for
k\to\infty
, given an arbitrary graph
G
with chromatic number
\chi(G)=k
, what can we say (asymptotically) about the chromatic number
\chi(G_{p})
of the random graph
G_{p}
? Second, as a more approachable weakening of the chromatic number problem where we restrict our attention to
greedy
colourings, we also study the following ‘colouring number problem’: for
k\to\infty
, given an arbitrary graph
G
with minimum degree
\delta(G)=k
, what can we say (again, asymptotically) about the colouring number
\mathscr{C}(G_{p})
of the random graph
G_{p}
?

Towards the first of our two primary questions, an old (unpublished) problem of the first author asks the following.

Problem 1.1
.

As
k\to\infty
, is it true that for any graph
G
with
\chi(G)=k
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\chi(G_{1/2})]=\Omega(k/\log k)?

The lower bound of
k/\log k
in
1.1
is natural, and best-possible if true; indeed, for the complete graph
G=K_{k}
, the classical result of Bollobás
[
5
]
pinning down the chromatic number of dense Erdős–Rényi random graphs asserts that
\chi(G_{1/2})\sim k/(2\log_{2}k)
with high probability.

1.1
strikes us a rather basic question; however, not much appears to be known, and the state of the art is as follows. First, for any graph
G
with
\chi(G)=k
, since
G_{1/2}
and its complement (in
G
) have the same distribution, it follows from a simple product-colouring argument that
\mathbb{E}[\chi(G_{1/2})]\geq k^{1/2}
; a similar argument (using a random partition into
r
parts) shows that
\mathbb{E}[\chi(G_{1/r})]\geq k^{1/r}
for any
r\in\mathbb{N}
. The argument in
[
1
]
shows that
\chi(G_{1/2})=\Omega(k/\log n)
holds with high probability, where
n
is the number of vertices of
G
. Finally, Mohar and Wu
[
16
]
have settled the fractional analogue of
1.1
in the affirmative.

Our primary contribution towards the chromatic number problem is a modest extension of the work of Shinkar
[
18
]
studying ‘large deviations’ of
\chi(G_{1/2})
. Taken together, our results give bounds for the entire lower tail of
\chi(G_{1/2})
; the first and the third bounds in the result below are due to Shinkar, while our contribution here is a proof of the second bound.

Theorem 1.2
.

For any graph
G
with
\chi(G)=k
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\chi(G_{1/2})\leq d\right)\leq\begin{cases}\exp(-\Omega((k^{1/2}-d)^{2}/k^{1/2})&\text{for }k^{1/2}/2\leq d\leq k^{1/2},\\
\exp(-\Omega(k/d^{2}))&\text{for }k^{1/3}\leq d\leq k^{1/2}/2,\text{ and}\\
\exp(-\Omega(k(k-d^{3}))/d^{3})&\text{for }d\leq k^{1/3}.\end{cases}

Towards the second of our two primary questions, we raise the following problem.

Problem 1.3
.

As
k\to\infty
, determine the largest
\mathfrak{D}(k)
for which we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathscr{C}(G_{1/2})\geq\mathfrak{D}(k)\right)\geq 1/2

for all graphs
G
with
\delta(G)\geq k
.

In other words,
1.3
asks the following: as
k\to\infty
, what is the best-possible lower bound on the colouring number
\mathscr{C}(G_{1/2})
that holds for all graphs
G
with minimum degree
\delta(G)\geq k
?
1.3
is the ‘degree-analogue’ of
1.1
, replacing proper colourings with (the more tractable) proper
greedy
colourings, and the chromatic number with the minimum degree. This is motivated in large part by the degree-analogue — due to Thomassen
[
19
]
and also wide open — of the aforementioned problem of Erdős–Hajnal
[
9
,
10
]
: is it true that for every pair
t,g\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a
k=k(t,g)\in\mathbb{N}
such that any graph with average degree at least
k
contains a subgraph with average degree at least
t
and girth at least
g
?

Let us point out that
1.3
and its variants arise naturally in some other (non-mathematical) contexts as well. First, several variants of
1.3
have been studied by biologists, sociologists and theoretical computer scientists as models of ‘cascading failures’ in networks; see
[
4
,
20
]
and the references therein, for example. Second, we note that
1.3
can also be recast in the language of bootstrap percolation
[
7
]
. Bootstrap percolation on a graph
G
is a model — originating in statistical physics — for the spread of infection on
G
defined as follows: starting with an initially infected set of vertices
A
, infection spreads along the edges of
G
, where a vertex of
G
gets infected if the number of its (previously) infected neighbours in
G
exceeds a specified threshold, and
A
is said to percolate if all the vertices of
G
are eventually infected. There is by now a large body of (mathematical) work devoted to understanding the percolating sets for various graph families (see
[
3
,
14
]
, for example), and
1.3
may also be rephrased in this language: given a graph
G
with
\delta(G)=k
, we are looking to understand for what
t=t(k)
we can guarantee that the set
A
of vertices of degree at most
t
in
G_{1/2}
percolates in bootstrap percolation on
G_{1/2}
with the threshold
(\deg(v,G_{1/2})-t)
at each vertex
v
of
G_{1/2}
(or in other words, for what
t=t(k)
we can guarantee that the
t
-core of
G_{1/2}
is non-empty).

It is clear from considering the complete graph
G=K_{k+1}
that we have the upper bound
\mathfrak{D}(k)\leq k/2+o(k)
, and using the well-known fact that any graph of average degree
d
contains a subgraph of minimum degree at least
d/2
, it is also easy to see (using a simple Chernoff bound) that
\mathfrak{D}(k)\geq k/4-o(k)
.

While the upper bound of
\mathfrak{D}(k)\leq k/2+o(k)
seems like the natural guess for the truth, the following result — our main contribution towards the colouring number problem, and our most significant result here — shows that this is not the case.

Theorem 1.4
.

As
k\to\infty
, we have
\mathfrak{D}(k)\leq k/3+o(k)
.

In more detail, the proof of
Theorem

1.4
shows (for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
divisible by 3) that there exist large
k
-regular graphs
G
for which the
t
-core of
G_{1/2}
is empty with high probability for some
t=k/3+o(k)
. Our next result, stated below, serves to illustrate some of the subtleties that arise in studying
1.3
.

Theorem 1.5
.

For every
\alpha>0
, there exists a
\beta>0
such that for infinitely many
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist arbitrarily large
k
-regular graphs
G
for which the following holds with high probability (as
k\to\infty
): any induced subgraph
H
of
G_{1/2}
with
\delta(H)\geq k/4+\alpha k
satisfies
|V(H)|/|V(G)|=O((1-\beta)^{k^{2}})
.

In other words,
Theorem

1.5
asserts (for infinitely many
k\in\mathbb{N}
) that there exist large
k
-regular graphs
G
for which the
t
-core of
G_{1/2}
is
just barely
non-empty for any
t=k/4+o(k)
. In the light of this, it seems clear to us that improving on the easy lower bound of
\mathfrak{D}(k)\geq k/4+o(k)
sketched above is likely to require some interesting ideas.

This paper is organised as follows. After covering some preliminaries in
Section

2
, we give the proof of
Theorem

1.2
in
Section

3
and the proofs of
Theorems

1.4
and
1.5
in
Section

4
. Finally, we conclude in
Section

5
with a discussion of open problems and directions for further work.

2.
Preliminaries

We start by establishing some notation and collecting together some tools that we will rely on in the sequel.

Our graph theoretic notation is for the most part standard; we refer the reader to
[
6
]
for terms not defined here. That said, we remind the reader of a few standard notions that come up frequently in this paper.

First, recall that the
chromatic number
\chi(G)
of a graph
G
is the smallest number of colours needed to properly colour the vertices of
G
, i.e., to colour the vertices in such a way that no two adjacent vertices share the same colour.

Next, following Erdős and Hajnal
[
11
]
, the
colouring number
\mathscr{C}(G)
of a graph
G
is the least number
k
for which there exists an ordering of the vertices of
G
in which each vertex has fewer than
k
neighbours preceding it in the ordering; this parameter — also (essentially) called the degeneracy or the core number — is the number of colours used by the natural greedy algorithm for properly colouring the vertices of
G
.

We shall also need two notions of graph boundaries: for a subset
S\subset V(G)
of the vertices of a graph
G
, its
vertex boundary
\partial S
consists of those vertices of
G
not in
S
that are adjacent to at least one vertex in
S
, and its
edge boundary
\nabla S
consists of those edges of
G
with one end in
S
and the other in
\overline{S}
. Overloading this notation slightly, for a subset
S\subset V(G)
of the vertices of a directed graph
G
, its vertex boundary
\partial S
consists of those out-neighbours of
S
that are not in
S
, and similarly, its edge boundary
\nabla S
consists of those edges of
G
directed from
S
to
\overline{S}
.

We need a standard bound for counting connected components in graphs; the following bound is a simple consequence of the fact that the infinite
\Delta
-branching rooted tree contains precisely

\frac{\binom{\Delta v}{v}}{(\Delta-1)v+1}\leq(e\Delta)^{v-1}

rooted subtrees with
v
vertices.

Lemma 2.1
.

For a graph
G
of maximum degree
\Delta
, the number of connected,
t
-edge subgraphs of
G
containing a given vertex is less than
(e\Delta)^{t}
. ∎

3.
Chromatic number

First, following
[
1
]
, we record (in slightly greater generality) a proof of the fact that for any
n
-vertex graph
G
with
\chi(G)=k
, we have
\chi(G_{1/2})=\Omega(k/\log n)
with high probability (as
n\to\infty
).

Proposition 3.1
.

For any
n
-vertex graph
G
with
\chi(G)=k
, we have

\chi(G_{p})\geq\frac{pk}{2\log n}

with high probability (as
n\to\infty
).

Proof.

The probability that there exists a set
V^{\prime}\subset V(G)
for which

(1)

the induced subgraph
G[V^{\prime}]
has minimum degree at least
{2\log n}{/p}
, and

(2)

V^{\prime}
becomes an independent set in
G_{p}
,

is at most

\sum_{m={2\log n}/{p}}^{n}\binom{n}{m}(1-p)^{(m\log n)/p}\leq\sum_{m={2\log n}/{p}}^{n}\left(\frac{en}{m}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\right)^{m}=o(1).

This implies that with high probability, any independent set
V^{\prime}
in
G_{p}
induces a subgraph of
G_{p}
of chromatic number at most
(2\log n)/p
in
G
.
As
G_{p}
can be partitioned into
\chi(G_{p})
independent sets (by definition), the result follows.
∎

To prove
Theorem

1.2
, we shall require a fact from extremal set theory that is well-known to experts; we state and prove this below for completeness.

We say that a family
\mathcal{G}
of graphs is
t
-chromatic intersecting
if the intersection of any two graphs from
\mathcal{G}
is not
(t-1)
-colourable. The following is an easy consequence of Shearer’s lemma
[
8
]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
\mathcal{G}
is a
t
-chromatic intersecting family of subgraphs of some graph
G
, then

\left\lvert\mathcal{G}\right\rvert\leq 2^{|E(G)|-t+1}.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary colouring
C
of the vertices of
G
with
t-1
colours, and let
F\subset E(G)
be the set of monochromatic edges in this colouring. The main point is that the family

\mathcal{G}_{F}=\{F\cap H:H\in\mathcal{G}\}

is an intersecting family, for if not,
C
would be a proper
(t-1)
-colouring of the intersection of some two graphs in
\mathcal{G}
. Hence, we have
|\mathcal{G}_{F}|\leq 2^{|F|-1}
.

Now, let
\mathcal{F}
be the multiset of graphs
F
that are obtainable in this way from some
(t-1)
-colouring of
G
; a single such graph
F
can potentially be obtained from several distinct
(t-1)
-colourings of
G
, and we consider each
F
with this multiplicity. Clearly, each edge of
G
occurs in precisely
\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert/(t-1)
many elements of
\mathcal{F}
. It then follows from Shearer’s lemma (see
[
8
]
) that

\log_{2}\left\lvert\mathcal{G}\right\rvert\leq\frac{(t-1}{\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\log_{2}\left\lvert\mathcal{G}_{F}\right\rvert\leq\frac{(t-1)}{\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}(|F|-1)=|E(G)|-t+1,

with the last equality following from the fact that each edge of
G
occurs in precisely
\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert/(t-1)
many elements of
\mathcal{F}
; the desired bound follows.
∎

We are now ready to prove
Theorem

1.2
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

Our goal is to estimate
\mathbb{P}(\chi(G_{1/2})\leq d)
for an arbitrary graph
G
with
\chi(G)=k
. The first of the three stated bounds in the
Theorem

1.2
that holds for
k^{1/2}/2\leq d\leq k^{1/2}
was established by Shinkar
[
18
]
by considering the natural Doob martingale using mostly standard techinques, so we do not elaborate on this further here.

Our contribution is the second bound in the statement of the theorem, and the proof of this bound utilises bounds on intersecting families, very much in the spirit of Shinkar’s proof of the third bound in the statement of the theorem. Consequently, we present the proofs of both these bounds together for the sake of completeness.

We start with the second bound in the statement of the theorem. Let
\mathcal{G}_{0}
be the family of subgraphs of
G
consisting of the monochromatic edges in some
d
-colouring of
G
; then writing
\mathcal{G}
for the increasing subfamily of
2^{E(G)}
generated by
\mathcal{G}_{0}
, it is clear that

\mathbb{P}\left(\chi(G_{1/2})\leq d\right)=\frac{\left\lvert\mathcal{G}\right\rvert}{2^{\left\lvert E(G)\right\rvert}}.

Now, the point is that
\mathcal{G}
is
\lfloor k/d^{2}\rfloor
-chromatic intersecting. Indeed, the intersection of any two graphs from
\mathcal{G}
consists of the monochromatic edges in some
d^{2}
-colouring of
G
, so the standard product colouring argument shows that this intersection cannot be properly
(\lfloor k/d^{2}\rfloor-1)
-coloured (since
\chi(G)=k
). It then follows from
Lemma

3.2
that

\mathbb{P}\left(\chi(G_{1/2})\leq d\right)\leq 2^{-\lfloor k/d^{2}\rfloor+1},

establishing the desired bound for all
1\leq d\leq k^{1/2}/2
.

Finally, we turn to the third bound in the statement of the theorem, due to Shinkar
[
18
]
, that only applies when
d\leq k^{1/3}
but gives better estimates in this range than our bound above. In this range, it turns out to be more efficient to consider the 3-wise intersections of the family
\mathcal{G}
defined above. Indeed, it is not hard to check (again by a simple product argument, and the fact that an
r
-chromatic graph must contain at least
\binom{r}{2}
edges) that for
d\leq k^{1/3}
, the intersection of any three graphs from
\mathcal{G}
must contain at least
k(k-d^{3})/(2d^{3})
edges. The desired bound then follows from a result of Frankl
[
12
]
on
3
-wise
t
-intersecting families asserting that such families have uniform measure at most
((\sqrt{5}-1)/2)^{t}
.
∎

4.
Colouring number

Our proofs of
Theorem

1.4
and
Theorem

1.5
rely on the existence of good expander graphs. Here, we make use of Ramanujan graphs, but note in passing that any family of sufficiently strong expanders should suffice.

We start with the proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

Since
\mathfrak{D}(k)
is a non-decreasing function of
k
, it suffices to only consider
k
that are divisible by
3
. Given any small
0<\alpha<1/100
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
3\mid k
, we shall construct a
k
-regular graph
G
(infinitely many, in fact) with the property that, for
t=k/3+\alpha k
, the
t
-core of
G_{1/2}
is empty with probability
1-o(1)
as
k\to\infty
; clearly, this suffices to prove the result.

Recall that there are infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
for which there exists a
3
-regular Ramanujan graph on
n
vertices; see
[
17
]
for example. Take a
3
-regular Ramanujan graph
H
on
n\geq(\alpha/3)^{-k^{3}}
vertices, and let
G
be a
(k/3)
-blow-up of
H
, i.e.,
G
is obtained from
H
by replacing each vertex of
H
by an independent set of size
k/3
— we call these sets (and interchangeably, the vertices of
H
)
super-vertices
— and by replacing each edge of
H
by a complete bipartite graph in
G
between the corresponding super-vertices.

We say that a vertex of
G

survives
or
lives
if it is present in the
t
-core of
G_{1/2}
, and that it
dies
otherwise; similarly, we say that a super-vertex of
H

dies
if none of its constituent vertices survive, and that it
lives
or
survives
otherwise.

We shall, for technical reasons, construct
G_{1/2}
by deleting the edges of
G
in two rounds: in the first round, each edge of
G
is independently sampled with probability
\alpha/3
, in the second round, each edge of
G
is independently sampled with probability
(1/2-\alpha/3)/(1-\alpha/3)\geq 1/2-\alpha/2
, and finally, all the sampled edges are deleted to form
G_{1/2}
.

Since
n\geq(\alpha/3)^{-k^{3}}
, there is, with high probability
over the random deletions in the first round
, some super-vertex for which all the edges incident to it in
G
are deleted in the first round. Therefore, let us condition on the event that all the edges incident to some super-vertex are deleted in the first round; let
r
be any such super-vertex. Clearly, such an
r
dies. Let
T_{r}
be the connected set of dead super-vertices containing
r
; we claim that
|T_{r}|=n
with high probability
over the random deletions in the second round
. Since the two rounds of deletions are independent, this claim clearly implies that the
t
-core of
G_{1/2}
is empty with high probability.

The rest of the proof is devoted to the proof of the claim above, namely that for any fixed super vertex
r
, conditional on
r
dying after the first round of deletions, the second round of deletions guarantee that
|T_{r}|=n
with high probability. In what follows, we fix an arbitrary super-vertex
r
, abbreviate
T_{r}
by
T
, and write
\mathbb{P}_{r}
for the probability over the random deletions in the second round, conditioned on
r
dying in the first round.

The proof proceeds by a union bound over the potential choices of
T
. Our task then is to bound, for all choices of
T_{0}
, the probability
\mathbb{P}_{r}(T=T_{0})
. Before we turn to this task, we observe that

(1)

if
|T|<n
, then since
H
is connected, the vertex boundary
\partial T
of
T
in
H
is both non-empty and necessarily contained in the set of surviving super-vertices, and

(2)

for each surviving super-vertex
v\in\partial T
, there is at least one vertex
v^{*}\in V(G)
contained in
v
that survives.

Consider any connected set
T_{0}
of
m
super-vertices containing
r
. We shall need to distinguish between the case where
m
is very large and the case where
m
is not so large.

First, we dispose of the easy case where
99n/100\leq m<n
. Here, we use an inequality of Kahale
[
15
,
13
]
for Ramanujan graphs that implies that the edge boundary
\nabla T_{0}
of
T_{0}
has size at least
14(n-m)/10
. Thus, there exists a super-vertex
v\in\overline{T_{0}}
that has at least
2
neighbours in
T_{0}
; no vertex of
G
contained in such a super-vertex
v
can survive, whence we have

(1)

\mathbb{P}_{r}\left(99n/100\leq|T|<n\right)=0.

Next, we turn to the case where
1\leq m<99n/100
; in this case, since
H
is a Ramanujan graph, there exists a universal
c_{1}>0
such that
|\partial T_{0}|>c_{1}m
. Let
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{\ell}
be a maximal independent set of surviving super-vertices in
H[\partial T_{0}]
, and note that since each vertex in
H[\partial T_{0}]
has degree at most 2, we must have
\ell\geq|\partial T_{0}|/3\geq c_{1}m/3
. Next, note that if
T=T_{0}
, then there must exist vertices
v^{*}_{1}\in v_{1},v^{*}_{2}\in v_{2},\dots,v^{*}_{\ell}\in v_{\ell}
of
G
that also survive. For any such choice of vertices
v^{*}_{1},v^{*}_{2},\dots,v^{*}_{\ell}
, we shall now estimate the probability, over the second round of deletions, that these vertices survive. By virtue of how
G
is constructed from
H
, it is clear that
v^{*}_{i}
is not adjacent to any of the vertices
v^{*}_{1},v^{*}_{2},\dots,v^{*}_{i-1}
for all
1\leq i\leq\ell
. This allows us to bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{r}\left(v^{*}_{1},\dotsc,v^{*}_{\ell}\text{ survive}\mid T_{0}\textrm{ dies}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{P}_{r}\begin{pmatrix}\,\text{at least }k/3+\alpha k\text{ edges from }v^{*}_{i}\,\,\\
\text{to }\overline{T_{0}}\text{ survive the second round}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{P}\left(\operatorname{Binom}(2k/3,1/2+\alpha/2)\geq k/3+\alpha k\right)

\displaystyle\leq(1+c_{2})^{-k\ell},

where
c_{2}>0
is a constant depending on
\alpha
alone. Using the fact that
\ell\geq c_{1}m/3
, a union bound over all potential choices of
v^{*}_{1},v^{*}_{2},\dots,v^{*}_{\ell}
— of which there are at most
(k/3)^{2m}
since
|\partial T_{0}|\leq 2|T_{0}|=2m
— yields the estimate

\mathbb{P}_{r}\left(T=T_{0}\right)\leq(k/3)^{2m}(1+c_{2})^{-c_{1}km/3}.

Finally, the number of connected sets
T_{0}
of size
m
that contain the fixed root
r
is, by
Lemma

2.1
, at most
(3e)^{m}
. Thus, it follows again from the union bound that

(2)

\mathbb{P}_{r}\left(1\leq|T|<99n/100\right)\leq\sum_{m=1}^{99n/100}\left(ek^{2}/3\right)^{m}\left(1+c_{2}\right)^{-c_{1}km/3}=o(1),

with the last asymptotic estimate holding in the limit of
k\to\infty
.

The desired claim, namely that
\mathbb{P}_{r}(|T|<n)=o(1)
, follows from (
1
) and (
2
), and the proof is complete.
∎

Next, we give the proof of
Theorem

1.5
. The proof relies on a similar construction to the one used to prove
Theorem

1.4
: starting with a suitable expander, we replace each vertex with a gadget to form ‘super-vertices’ as before; the difference is that the starting graph is a
directed
expander, and the gadgets we now use to form the super-vertices are more complex than mere independent sets.

We call a directed graph

if the in-degree and out-degree of each vertex is
d

Lemma 4.1
.

For all sufficiently large

2
-regular directed graphs

n

of vertices, satisfy

\left\lvert\partial S\right\rvert\geq c_{3}\min(\left\lvert S\right\rvert,n-\left\lvert S\right\rvert)

where
c_{3}>0
is a universal constant.

Proof.

A uniformly random
2
-regular directed graph on
n
vertices has this property with high probability as
n\to\infty
. A proof of this fact is, at this point, a routine argument using the configuration model; see
[
13
]
for a similar argument in the context of undirected graphs (that extends to the directed case as well).
∎

5.
Conclusion

A large number of interesting open problems remain; below, we highlight a few that we find particularly appealing.

First, in the context of the chromatic number of problem, all the lower bounds on
\chi(G_{1/r})
in terms of
\chi(G)
that we currently have rely crucially on
r
being an integer. It would be very interesting to prove any lower bound for
\mathbb{E}[\chi(G_{0.499})]
that is (asymptotically) better than
\chi(G)^{1/3}
.

Second, in the context of the colouring number of problem, we have been unable to prove any interesting lower bounds for
\mathfrak{D}(k)
. We would not be surprised if the truth is that
\mathfrak{D}(k)=k/3+o(k)
as
k\to\infty
, but even showing that
\mathfrak{D}(k)\geq k/3.99+o(k)
appears to be a challenging problem, as evidenced by
Theorem

1.5
. In fact, we (somewhat embarrassingly) do not know if there exists a function
f(k)\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
such that
\mathfrak{D}(k)\geq k/4+f(k)
.
