Tubed embeddings
Anton Petrunin

Abstract
We consider the following question: When does a Riemannian manifold admit an embedding with a uniformly thick tubular neighborhood in another Riemannian manifold of large dimension?



Introduction

1.

Let
M
and
N
be Riemannian manifolds; we will always assume that Riemannian manifolds are complete connected, and
C^{\infty}
-smooth.

A smooth embedding
f\colon M\hookrightarrow N
will be called
tubed
if the image
f(M)\subset\penalty N
is a closed subset and it admits a tubular
\varepsilon
-neighborhood for some
\varepsilon>0
;
that is, the closest-point projection from the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of
f(M)
to
f(M)
is uniquely defined.
Equivalently,
f(M)
is a set with positive reach

[
6
,
2
]
.

We say that
M
has
uniformly polynomial growth
if there is a polynomial
p
such that

\operatorname{\rm vol}B(x,R)_{M}\leqslant p(R)

for any point
x\in M
and any radius
R\geqslant 0
;
here
B(x,R)_{M}
denotes open ball in
M
of radius
R
centered at
x
, and
\operatorname{\rm vol}
stands for volume.
In this case, we say that
p
is a
growth polynomial
of
M
.

Observation.

Suppose a complete Riemannian manifold
M
admits an isometric tubed embedding in a Euclidean space.
Then
M
has

1.

bounded sectional curvature,

2.

positive injectivity radius, and

3.

uniformly polynomial growth.

For example,
the hyperbolic plane has exponential growth;
therefore, it does not admit an isometric tubed embedding into
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for any integer
d
.

2.

Further, the sectional curvature of
M
will be denoted by
\mathrm{sec}_{M}
;
its injectivity radius at point
p\in M
will be denoted by
\operatorname{\rm inj}_{p}M
, and
\operatorname{\rm inj}M
will denote the injectivity radius of
M
; that is,

\operatorname{\rm inj}M\mathrel{{:}{=}}\inf\left\{\,\left.{\operatorname{\rm inj}_{p}M}\vphantom{p\in M}\,\right|\,{p\in M}\,\right\}.

We say that
M
has
bounded geometry
if
\operatorname{\rm inj}M>0
and for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
there is a constant
C_{k}>0
such that
|\nabla^{k}\operatorname{\rm Rm}|\leqslant C_{k}
;
here
\nabla
denotes the Levi-Civita connection, and
\operatorname{\rm Rm}
is the curvature tensor on
M
.
Note that manifolds with bounded geometry have bounded sectional curvature.
(Some authors define bounded geometry as bounded sectional curvature and injectivity radius — we do not follow this convention.)

Main theorem.

Suppose
M
is a Riemannian manifold with bounded geometry.
Then,
M
has uniformly polynomial growth
if and only if there is an isometric tubed embedding
M\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some integer
d
.

Moreover, the dimension
d
can be found in terms of the dimension and a growth polynomial of
M
.

3.Other ambient spaces.

Loosely speaking, the following statement says that there is no universal space — no space can serve as a target of an isometric tubed embedding for
any

n
-manifolds with bounded geometry.

Proposition.

Let
N
be a Riemannian manifold with bounded geometry and
n\geqslant 2
.
Then there is an
n
-dimensional Riemannian manifold
M
with bounded geometry that does not admit an isometric tubed embedding in
N
.

5.Lemma.

Suppose

-2\leqslant\mathrm{sec}_{M}(\sigma)\leqslant 1

Proof.

Denote by

By the assumptions,

We need to show that curvature of

Passing to a 2-dimensional subspace of

Let

Since

Note that

Note that normal curvatures of

Graph embedding

7.

Let
\Gamma

Recall that
\Gamma

.

Uniform Nash’s construction

12.Uniformly regular metrics.

Let
M
be a smooth
n
-dimensional manifold with a chosen locally finite atlas
\mathcal{A}
.
We say that
\mathcal{A}
is
appropriate
if it meets the following conditions:

\diamond

Each chart
\sigma\in\mathcal{A}
is given by a map from an open set
U_{\sigma}\subset M
to the unit ball
\mathbb{D}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

\diamond

The covering
\{U_{\sigma}\}
of
M
has bounded multiplicity.

\diamond

There exists
r\in(0,1)
such that
\{\sigma^{-1}(r{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}\mathbb{D}^{n})\}_{\sigma\in\mathcal{A}}
is a cover for
M
.

\diamond

All the transition maps of
\mathcal{A}
are uniformly bi-Lipschitz and for any integer
k\geqslant 0
they have uniformly bounded
C^{k}
-norms.

A metric tensor
g
on
M
will be called
uniformly regular
with respect to an appropriate atlas
\mathcal{A}

if for every integer
k
, all components of the metric tensor in all charts of
\mathcal{A}
have bounded
C^{k}
-norms,
and all the charts are uniformly bi-Lipschitz with respect to the metric induced by
g
on
M
.

Analogously, we can define a
uniformly regular sequence of metrics
on a fixed manifold with a chosen appropriate atlas.
(Note that a smooth metric on a fixed compact manifold, say
\mathbb{S}^{n}
, is uniformly smooth for some appropriate atlas,
but a sequence of smooth metrics does not have to be uniformly smooth.)

Let
M
be a Riemannian manifold with bounded geometry.
Applying rescaling if necessary, we may assume
|\mathrm{sec}_{M}|\leqslant\tfrac{1}{100}
and
\operatorname{\rm inj}M\geqslant 10
.

Let us apply the construction in §
9
for
r=\tfrac{1}{2}
.
Given
v\in V
, consider the normal chart
\sigma_{v}\colon 2{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}B_{v}\to\mathbb{D}^{n}
with the center at
v
;
we will call such an atlas
standard
.
It is straightforward to check that this atlas is appropriate and
the metric tensor is uniformly regular with respect to this atlas.
In particular,
any manifold of bounded geometry has a uniformly regular metric in an appropriate atlas
.
The following statement from
[
5
]
implies that the converse holds as well.

Proposition.

A Riemannian manifold
M
has bounded geometry if and only if its metric is uniformly regular for some appropriate atlas.

Also note that
if

comes with a uniformly regular metric for some appropriate atlas

,
then a map

is uniformly smooth if and only if for every integer

,
the functions
f\circ\sigma-f\circ\sigma(0)\colon\mathbb{D}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
for all charts
\sigma\in\mathcal{A}

have uniformly bounded
C^{k}
-norms.

\mathbb{S}^{n}
.
Then the construction in
[
16
, Part C]
with the same choices of functions produces uniformly smooth embeddings
s_{i}
.
Namely, the functions
\psi^{r}
in
[

and
\alpha_{r}
in
[

are uniformly smooth and we can assume that
\lambda
in
[

is the same for all
g_{i}
.

Further, the so-called
Nash’s reduction

[
16
, Part D]
reduces the existence of an isometric immersion of
M
to the existence of isometric immersions
(\mathbb{S}^{n},g_{i})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
for a sequence of Riemannian metrics
g_{i}
.
Nash starts with an atlas with bounded multiplicity, and for each chart, he produces a sequence of metrics
g_{i}
on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
with a sequence of smooth maps
\varphi_{i}\colon M\to(\mathbb{S}^{n},g_{i})

such that the sum of metrics induced by
\varphi_{i}
on
M
coincides with
g
.
Then an isometric immersion constructed from embeddings
s_{i}
as above.

Applying rescaling, we may assume that
|\mathrm{sec}_{M}|\leqslant\tfrac{1}{100}
and
\operatorname{\rm inj}M\geqslant 10
.
Let us choose a standard atlas on

as in §

.
Then Nash’s construction produces a uniformly smooth sequence of maps

and a sequence of metrics satisfying the above condition.
As a result, we get a uniformly smooth isometric immersion
(M,g)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where

Proof assembling

14.Proof of the main theorem (§
2
).

Let
f_{1}\colon M\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
f_{2}\colon M\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
be the maps constructed in §
10
and §
11
.
Consider the map
f_{1}\oplus f_{2}\colon M\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}}
defined by
p\mapsto(f_{1}(p),f_{2}(p))\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}=\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}}
.

Let
h
be the metric induced by
f_{1}\oplus f_{2}

and
g
be the original metric on
M
.

Choose
\varepsilon>0
such that
\varepsilon^{2}{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}h<\tfrac{1}{2}{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}g
.
Note that
g-\varepsilon^{2}{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}h
is a uniformly regular and smooth metric on
M
for a standard atlas; see §
12
.
By §
12
,
(M,g-\varepsilon^{2}{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}h)
has bounded geometry.
By §
13
, there is a uniformly smooth map
f_{3}\colon M\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{3}}
that induces metric
g-\varepsilon^{2}{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}h
on
M
.

Therefore, the map
f=(\varepsilon{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}f_{1})\oplus(\varepsilon{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}f_{2})\oplus f_{3}\colon M\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}+d_{3}}
is a uniformly smooth isometric immersion.
In particular, the image
f(M)
has bounded normal curvatures.
By §
10
and §
11
,
f_{1}\oplus f_{2}
is an embedding;
therefore so is
f
.
It remains to show that
f
is tubed.

Assume the contrary;
that is, for any
\varepsilon>0
there is a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that lies on distance smaller than
\varepsilon
to
f(M)
such that the distance function from
x
has at least two minimum points on
f(M)
,
say
f(p)
and
f(q)
.
Denote by
\gamma
a minimizing geodesic from
p
to
q
in
M
.

Suppose
\varepsilon<\tfrac{1}{2}
.
The properties in §
10
and §
11
imply that

\mathop{\rm length}\nolimits\gamma<L{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}\varepsilon

for some fixed
L\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let
y\in f(\gamma)
be a point that maximizes the distance from
x
, and
r=|x-y|
.
The triangle inequality implies that
r\leqslant\varepsilon{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}(1+L)
.
It follows that the curvature of
f(\gamma)
at
y
is at least
\tfrac{1}{r}
.
The latter is impossible if
\varepsilon
is small.
∎

Universal space

15.Theorem.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph of bounded degree.
Then there is a connected graph
\Delta
of degree
\leqslant 3
such that there is no map
f\colon\mathop{\rm Vert}\Delta\to\mathop{\rm Vert}\Gamma
such that

1.

|f^{-1}(h)|\leqslant K
for any vertex
h
in
\Gamma
, and

2.

|f(v)-f(x)|_{\Gamma}\leqslant K
for any two adjacent vertices
v,w
in
\Delta

for some constant
K
.

A slightly weaker statement was proved by Florian Lehner
[
14
, 1.2]
and rediscovered by Fedor Petrov
[
17
]
;
we will modify their proof straightforwardly.
The conditions are closely related to the so-called
regular maps
;
see
[
8
]
and the references therein.

Proof.

Suppose
\Gamma
is an infinite connected graph with bounded degree.
Note that
\Gamma
has a countable set of vertices; so we can identify
\mathop{\rm Vert}\Gamma
with the set of natural numbers
\mathbb{N}
.

Choose a sequence
n_{1},n_{2},\dots
of natural numbers such that each
n\in\mathbb{N}
appears infinitely many times in it;
for example, we may take the following sequence:
1,2,1,3,1,2,1,4,1,\dots

Let us construct a graph
\Delta
with degree at most
3
such that no map
f
meets the condition in the theorem for any
K
.
In the following construction, the set of vertices of
\Delta
will be
\mathbb{N}
, and it will contain an infinite path
P=1\sim 2\sim\dots
.
There will be a fast growing sequence
S_{1}<S_{2}<\dots
such that
if
i\sim j
for some
i<j
, then
j-i=1
or
S_{k}<i<j\leqslant S_{k+1}
for some
k
.

The subgraphs induced by
\{1,\dots,S_{k}\}
will be denoted by
\Delta_{k}
;
let
P_{k}
be the corresponding path
1\sim 2\sim\dots\sim S_{k}
.
The graphs
\Delta_{k}
will be constructed recursively so that they meet the following condition:

➌

There is no map
f_{k}\colon\mathop{\rm Vert}\Delta_{k}\to\mathop{\rm Vert}\Gamma
that meets the conditions for
K=k
and such that
f_{k}(1)=\penalty n_{k}
.

Once it is done, the theorem follows.

We assume that
\Delta_{0}
is the empty graph, and
S_{0}=0
.
Assume
\Delta_{k-1}
is constructed, let us construct
\Delta_{k}
.
To do so, we need to choose large
S_{k}\gg\penalty S_{k-1}
and add diagonals to the path
S_{k-1}+1\sim\dots\sim S_{k}
.

Suppose
L<(S_{k}-S_{k-1})/2
.
Let
A
(
B
) be the first (respectively, the last)
L
elements in the integer interval from
S_{k-1}+1
to
S_{k}
.
We may choose an arbitrary bijection
A\leftrightarrow B
and connect corresponding vertices by edges.
This way we get
L!
different graphs; in particular, the total number of graphs
\Delta_{k}
has superexponential growth with respect to
S_{k}
.
More precisely, we count labeled graphs; that is, graphs
\Delta_{k}
with vertices labeled by
\{1,\dots,S_{k}\}
and a path
P_{k}=1\sim 2\sim\dots\sim S_{k}
.

How much of these graphs have the required maps
f\colon\mathop{\rm Vert}\Delta_{k}\to\mathop{\rm Vert}\Gamma
?
Suppose that the degree of
\Gamma
does not exceed
d-1
.
Note that each
k
-ball in
\Gamma
has at most
d^{k}
vertices.
Therefore we have at most
d^{k{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}S_{k+1}}
different maps of the path
P_{k}
.
Given one such map, there are at most
k{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}d^{k}
ways to add a diagonal at each vertex.
Therefore the number of labeled graphs that admit a required map
f\colon\mathop{\rm Vert}\Delta_{k}\to\mathop{\rm Vert}\Gamma
for
K=k
grows exponentially with respect to
S_{k}
.
It follows that we may choose a sufficiently large
S_{k}
,
so that there is a graph
\Delta_{k}
that meets
15
.
∎

16.Proof of the proposition in §
3
.

Let
N
be a manifold with bounded geometry and
n\geqslant 2
;
we need to construct an
n
-dimensional manifold
M
with bounded geometry that does not admit an isometric tubed embedding into
N
.

Let
\Gamma_{2}
be the intersection graph obtained by applying the construction in §
9
to
N
.
Recall that
\Gamma_{2}
has bounded degree.
Let
\Delta
be the graph provided by §
15
for
\Gamma_{2}
;
it is a connected graph with degree at most 3.

Let us choose a
tube
; that is, the cylinder
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\times[0,1]
with smooth Riemannian metric such that a neighborhood of each boundary component is isometric to a small annulus in the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n}
, so it can be used to connect spheres.
Prepare a unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n}
for every vertex in
\Delta
.
Connect two spheres by a tube if the corresponding vertices are adjacent.
Note that the obtained manifold
M
has bounded geometry.

Assume there is an isometric tubed embedding
\iota\colon M\hookrightarrow N
.
We can assume that
\Delta
is embedded in
M
so that each vertex lies on the corresponding sphere.
Consider the map
\Delta\to\Gamma_{2}
that sends a vertex
v\in\mathop{\rm Vert}\Delta
to a vertex of
\Gamma_{2}
that lies at minimal distance from
\iota(v)
.
Note that for some
K
, the obtained map satisfies the conditions in the theorem in §

— a contradiction.
∎

Final remarks

17.

Let us say that a Riemannian manifold
M
has
k
-bounded geometry
if there is a constant
C_{k}>0
such that
|\nabla^{i}\operatorname{\rm Rm}|\leqslant C_{k}
for any nonnegative integer
i\leqslant k
.
For example,
0
-bounded geometry means positive injectivity radius and bounded curvature.

In the proof of the main theorem, one can assume that
M
has only
3
-bounded geometry;
the proof requires only minor changes.
Below we discuss
1
- and
0
-bounded geometry.

Let us generalize the definitions of appropriate atlas and uniformly regular metric (see §
12
).
Choose an integer
m\geqslant 1
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Suppose that in the definition of appropriate atlas,
instead of bounded
C^{k}
norm of the transition maps for all
k

we only require bounded
C^{m+1,\alpha}
norm.
Let us call such atlas
C^{m+1,\alpha}
-appropriate
.
Furthermore, in the definition of uniformly regular metric let us assume that the atlas is only
C^{m+1,\alpha}
-appropriate, and instead of bounded
C^{k}
norm for all
k
,
we only require that metric has bounded
C^{m,\alpha}
norm in every chart.
Let us call such metrics
uniformly
C^{m,\alpha}
-regular
.
Note that a
C^{\infty}
-smooth metric might fail to be uniformly
C^{m,\alpha}
-regular even if injectivity radius is positive.

Recall that if metric is
C^{m,\alpha}
, then it remains to be
C^{m,\alpha}
in the harmonic charts.
This fundamental result was obtained by Idzhad Sabitov and Samuil Shefel
[
19
]
and rediscoved by Dennis DeTurck and Jerry Kazdan
[
4
]
.
Applying this technique, one could get that
manifolds with
1
-bounded geometry have uniformly
C^{2,\alpha}
-regular metric for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Using Günther’s version of the embedding theorem
[
7
]
in §
13
,
we obtain the following:
for any
m\geqslant 2
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, any manifold with uniformly
C^{m,\alpha}
-regular metric admits a uniformly
C^{m,\alpha}
-smooth isometric immersion into Euclidean space;

in particular, such an immersion has bounded normal curvatures.
Furthermore, following §
16
, we get that

for any
m\geqslant 2
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, any manifold with uniformly
C^{m,\alpha}
-regular metric and uniformly polynomial growth admits a uniformly
C^{m,\alpha}
-smooth tubed isometric embedding into a Euclidean space of large dimension.

Note that uniformly
C^{1,1}
-regular metric has bounded sectional curvature and positive injectivity radius.
It is unknown if the converse holds.
Also it is unknown if a manifold with
C^{1,1}
-regular metric admits a
C^{1,1}
-smooth isometric embedding into Euclidean space.
These are two parts of the question posed by Vitali Kapovitch and Alexander Lytchak
[
11
, 1.10]
.
If both questions have affirmative answers, then the presented argument should produce a converse to the observation in §
1
.

If the smoothness is below
C^{1,1}
, then there is no hope for tubed isometric embedding.
However, it is easy to see that an embedding
f\colon M\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with bounded normal curvature is tubed if and only if it is
uniformly graphical
;
that is, there is
r>0
such that for any
p\in M
the set
B(f(p),r)\cap f(M)
is a graph of a function
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d-n}
for a suitable choice of coordinate system in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Applying the result of Anders Källén
[
10
]
, the argument in §
16
produces that

for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, any manifold with
C^{1,\alpha}
-regular metric and uniformly polynomial growth admits a uniformly
C^{1,\alpha}
-smooth uniformly graphical embedding into a Euclidean space of large dimension.

In other words, for arbitrary small positive
\varepsilon
, the
C^{2,\varepsilon}
and
C^{1,1-\varepsilon}
versions of the uniformly graphical question have affirmative answers, but
C^{1,1}
—the most important case—remains open.

Question.

Suppose
M
is an
n
-dimensional Riemannian manifold of bounded geometry.
What is necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of tubed isometric embeddings of
M

into (1) hyperbolic space of sufficiently large dimension or (2) product of sufficiently many hyperbolic planes?

Note that polynomial growth is a sufficient condition for both questions.
It follows from the main theorem; the second case is trivial and the first case follows
since a horosphere defines a tubed isometric embedding of a Euclidean space into a hyperbolic space.
Evidently this condition is not necessary.

The argument presented by David Hume and Alessandro Sisto
[
9
, 1.1]
can be used to find some necessary conditions; for example,
the product of the hyperbolic plane and the real line does not admit a tubed embedding into hyperbolic space of arbitrary dimension
.
Also, a manifold with infinite asymptotic dimension does not admit a tubed embedding into a product of hyperbolic planes.
The latter assertion relies on the monotonicity of asymptotic dimension with respect to maps between graphs satisfying the two conditions in §
15
.
Namely,
if
f\colon\Delta\to\Gamma
is such a map between graphs with bounded degrees, then
\mathrm{asdim}\,\Delta\leqslant\mathrm{asdim}\,\Gamma
.
(The proof is left to the reader.)

Finally, it would be interesting to understand what happens if we do not require
N
to have bounded geometry in §
3
.
