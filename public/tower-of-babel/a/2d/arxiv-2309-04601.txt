Dyadic linear programming and extensions
Ahmad Abdi, Gérard Cornuéjols, Bertrand Guenin, Levent Tunçel

Abstract
A rational number is dyadic if it has a finite binary representation p / 2 k p/2^{k} , where p p is an integer and k k is a nonnegative integer. Dyadic rationals are important for numerical computations because they have an exact representation in floating-point arithmetic on a computer. A vector is dyadic if all its entries are dyadic rationals. We study the problem of finding a dyadic optimal solution to a linear program, if one exists. We show how to solve dyadic linear programs in polynomial time. We give bounds on the size of the support of a solution as well as on the size of the denominators. We identify properties that make the solution of dyadic linear programs possible: closure under addition and negation, and density, and we extend the algorithmic framework beyond the dyadic case. Keywords. Linear programming, integer programming, dyadic rational, floating-point arithmetic, polynomial algorithm, dense abelian subgroup.

Introduction

A rational number is
dyadic
if it is an integer multiple of
\frac{1}{2^{k}}
for some nonnegative integer
k
. Dyadic numbers are important
for numerical computations because they have a finite binary representation, and therefore they
can be represented exactly on a computer in floating-point arithmetic.
When real or rational numbers are approximated by dyadic numbers on a computer, approximation errors may propagate and accumulate throughout the computations. So it is natural to ask when linear programs have a dyadic optimal solution.
More generally, the exact solution of linear programs with rational input data has lead to interesting work; we just mention here the excellent dissertation of Espinoza
[
15
]
. In this paper we investigate a different direction.

A vector
x
is
dyadic
if all its entries are dyadic numbers.
A
dyadic linear program
is an optimization problem of the form

\sup\left\{w^{\top}x:Ax\leq b,x\text{ dyadic}\right\}

where
A,b,w
have integral entries.

Note that we do not restrict
ourselves to fixed precision; we just require a finite number of bits in the binary representation. This is an important point as we will see that it makes the problem tractable. On the other hand, if the vector
x
in the dyadic linear program were restricted to be of the form
\frac{y}{2^{k}}
for an integral vector
y
and a nonnegative integer
k
bounded above by a given value
K
, then the problem would be a classical integer linear program. Indeed the problem can then be written as
\frac{1}{2^{K}}\cdot\max\left\{w^{\top}y:Ay\leq 2^{K}b,y\text{ integral}\right\}
.

Some natural questions about dyadic linear programs are: When is the problem feasible? Can we check feasibility in polynomial time? If the problem is infeasible, can we provide a certificate of infeasibility?
When does a dyadic linear program have an optimal solution?
Note that a dyadic linear program may be feasible and bounded, but not have an optimal solution (in dimension one,
\sup\left\{x:3x\leq 1,x\text{ dyadic}\right\}
is such an example).
Can
dyadic linear programs be solved in polynomial time? What is the size of the dyadic numbers in a solution when one exists? What is the support size of a solution? This paper addresses these questions. In particular, we show that dyadic linear programs can be solved in polynomial time.

The interest in dyadic linear programming stems not only from the computer science perspective mentioned above, but also from mathematics and from optimization. Take a mathematical point of view: Given a prime integer
p\geq 2
, we say that a rational number is
finitely
p
-adic
if it is of the form
\frac{r}{p^{k}}
for some integer

. This concept is closely related to the notion of the
p
-adic numbers
introduced by Hensel, formally defined as the set of “finite-tailed” infinite series
\sum_{i=N}^{+\infty}a_{i}p^{i}
where
N\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
0\leq a_{i}<p
for each
i\geq N
.

. This conjecture is still open but is known to hold in a few important special cases. For example, when

We will show in this paper that dyadic linear programming shares aspects of classical linear programming as well as certain aspects of integer programming. In particular, we will show that, just like linear programs, dyadic linear programs can be solved in polynomial time. However, when it comes to the support size of a solution, the situation is more akin to that in integer programming. Indeed, in classical linear programming, for a problem in standard equality form
x\geq 0,Ax=b
, if it has an optimal solution, there is a basic optimal solution with at most
m
nonzero entries where
m
is the row rank of the constraint matrix
A
. For integer programs, the support size may be superlinear in
m
, and a similar situation occurs for dyadic linear programs.
Next, we present an outline of the paper.

In
Section

2
, we present two key ideas that make the polynomial solution of dyadic linear programs possible. The first ingredient is the density of the dyadic numbers in the real line. This property enables us to convert the feasibility question for a dyadic linear program to that of the existence of a dyadic point in an affine space. Specifically, we show that, if
\mathbb{L}
is a dense subset of the real line closed under addition and negation, and
P
is a nonempty convex set whose affine hull is rational, then
P
contains a point in
\mathbb{L}^{n}
if and only if its affine hull
\aff(P)
does. This equivalence begs the question: When does the affine space
\aff(P)
contain a point in
\mathbb{L}^{n}
?

The second key ingredient is a theorem of the alternatives. This theorem allows us to answer the above question. Specifically, consider a matrix
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
and a vector
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
. Then exactly one of the following holds: (a)
Ax=b
has a solution in
\mathbb{L}^{n}
, or (b) there exists
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
A^{\top}u\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
b^{\top}u\not\in\mathbb{L}
.
This theorem of the alternative is reminiscent of the so-called “integer Farkas lemma” and it can be proved in a similar way, using the Hermite normal form of
A
. Because the Hermite normal form of an integral matrix can be found in polynomial time (Kannan and Bachem
[
21
]
), one can obtain a polynomial certificate for statements (a) or (b), whichever holds.

These two are the basic ingredients we need to check feasibility of dyadic linear programs in polynomial time.

In
Section

3
, we present an algorithm to solve dyadic linear programs. There are four possible outcomes for this optimization problem: (i) the problem is infeasible, (ii) the problem is unbounded, (iii) the problem has an optimal solution, (iv) the problem is feasible and bounded but has no optimal solution. We show how to decide in polynomial time which of these outcomes is the correct one and, in each case, we give a concise (polynomial size) certificate. The complexity of our algorithm is (up to a constant factor) the same as that of solving an ordinary linear program. Our results in this section are extended beyond the dyadic numbers to any subset
\mathbb{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
that is closed under addition and negation, contains all the
p
-adic numbers for some prime
p
, and is equipped with a membership oracle.

In
Section

4
, we focus on the size of the denominators of a solution to a feasible dyadic linear program. In particular, we show that if
Ax\leq b,x\text{ dyadic}
is feasible, where
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
and
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, then there exists a
\frac{1}{2^{k}}
-integral solution, where
k\leq\left\lceil\log_{2}n+(2n+1)\log_{2}(\|A\|_{\infty}\sqrt{n+1})\right\rceil
. Here
\|A\|_{\infty}
denotes the largest absolute value of an entry in
A
.

In
Section

5
, we study the size of the support of a solution to a dyadic linear program. That is, we consider the smallest number of nonzero components in a dyadic solution. Surprisingly, the lower and upper bounds that we obtain on the smallest support size resemble results for integer programming and are very different from the value of the support size given by Carathéodory’s theorem for classical linear programming. Specifically, let
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n},b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
\min\{w^{\top}x:Ax=b,x\geq\mathbf{0},x\text{ dyadic}\}
has an optimal solution, then we show that it has one with support size at most
m(1+0.84\ln{m}+1.68\ln\|A\|_{\infty})
. We give lower bounds by constructing examples and show that they are extremal in some sense.

Section

6
provides conclusions and possible directions for future research.

2
Foundational results

In this section, we identify two results that underpin much of the work in the paper, and are key to solving dyadic linear programs. The first result reduces
dyadic feasibility
of a rational polyhedron to that of its affine hull, while the second result provides a
theorem of the alternatives
for dyadic feasibility of a linear system of equations. We then combine the two results to obtain a
certificate of dyadic infeasibility
of a rational polyhedron. The results in this section are presented not only for the dyadic numbers, but more generally for any subset
\mathbb{L}
of
\mathbb{R}
closed under addition and negation, where sometimes we require
\mathbb{L}
to be dense in
\mathbb{R}
, and at other times
\mathbb{L}\neq\mathbb{R}
. Let us lay the groundwork.

2.1
Abelian subgroups,
p
-adic and
[p]
-adic numbers

Let
\mathbb{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. We say that
\mathbb{L}
is
closed under addition
if
x+y\in\mathbb{L}
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{L}
, and that
\mathbb{L}
is
closed under negation
, or equivalently
symmetric around the origin
, if
-x\in\mathbb{L}
for all
x\in\mathbb{L}
. Clearly, if
\mathbb{L}
is closed under addition and negation, then
ax\in\mathbb{L}
for all
a\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x\in\mathbb{L}
. In particular, if
\mathbb{L}\neq\emptyset
then
0\in\mathbb{L}
.

The condition that
\mathbb{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is closed under addition and negation is equivalent to requiring that
(\mathbb{L},+)
forms an abelian subgroup of
(\mathbb{R},+)
. It can be readily checked that for any such nonempty set, either
\mathbb{L}
is dense in
\mathbb{R}
, or
\mathbb{L}
is of the form
\{ax:a\in\mathbb{Z}\}
for some
x\in\mathbb{R}
. In the latter case,
\mathbb{L}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}
, in which case checking if a rational polyhedron in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
contains a point in
\mathbb{L}^{n}
is equivalent to integer programming feasibility in dimension
n
.

The discussion above motivates us to focus on dense sets in
\mathbb{R}
closed under addition and negation. We present a construction for such sets, but we first require a definition. A subset
\mathcal{S}
of positive integers is
closed under multiplication
if
pq\in\mathcal{S}
for all
p,q\in\mathcal{S}
. Given such a subset, note that
\mathcal{S}
is finite if and only if
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\{1\}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be an infinite set of positive integers that is closed under multiplication. Let
\mathbb{L}
be the set of all numbers
\frac{r}{s}
where
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
r\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
\mathbb{L}
is dense in
\mathbb{R}
and closed under addition and negation.

Proof.

Pick
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
\epsilon>0
.
Since
\mathcal{S}
is an infinite set of positive integers, there exists
s\in\mathcal{S}
with
s\geq\frac{1}{\epsilon}
.
Let
r=\lfloor sx\rfloor
, then
|x-\frac{r}{s}|<\epsilon
.
Since
\frac{r}{s}\in\mathbb{L}
, it follows that
\mathbb{L}
is a dense subset of
\mathbb{R}
.
Since
\mathcal{S}
is closed under multiplication, if
\frac{r}{s},\frac{r^{\prime}}{s^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{L}
, then their sum
\frac{rs^{\prime}+r^{\prime}s}{ss^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{L}
, so
\mathbb{L}
is closed under addition.
Finally, if
\frac{r}{s}\in\mathbb{L}
then
\frac{-r}{s}\in\mathbb{L}
, so
\mathbb{L}
is closed under negation.
∎

We can use this result to provide two important examples of dense sets in
\mathbb{R}
closed under addition and negation.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
p\geq 2
be a prime number. We say that a rational number is
p
-adic if it is of the form
\frac{r}{s}
where
s
is a power of
p
and
r\in\mathbb{Z}
. We say that a rational number is
[p]
-adic if it is of the form
\frac{r}{s}
where
s
is a product of powers of primes between
2
and
p
, and
r\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Observe that the set of
p
-adic numbers can be obtained from the construction in
2.1
by choosing
\mathcal{S}=\{p^{n}:n\geq\mathbf{0},n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, while the set of
[p]
-adic numbers is obtained by choosing
\mathcal{S}
to consist of all positive integers with prime factors less than or equal to
p
. Thus, both sets are dense in
\mathbb{R}
and closed under addition and negation, by
2.1
. Observe further that when
p=2
, the
p
-adic,
[p]
-adic, and dyadic numbers coincide. More generally, we have the following relation between
p
-adic and
[p]
-adic sets.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
p\geq 2
be an integer. Then the following are equivalent for a subset
\mathbb{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
:

(1)

\mathbb{L}
is the set of
[p]
-adic numbers,

(2)

\mathbb{L}
is an inclusion-wise minimal set closed under addition and negation that contains all
q
-adic numbers for primes
q\leq p
.

Proof.

Denote by
\mathbb{L}_{1}
the set
\mathbb{L}
as defined by (1) and by
\mathbb{L}_{2}
the set
\mathbb{L}
as defined by (2). We need to show
\mathbb{L}_{1}=\mathbb{L}_{2}
.
Note that
\mathbb{L}_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{L}_{1}
since
(i) every
q
-adic number for a prime
q\leq p
is
[p]
-adic by definition, and since
(ii)
\mathbb{L}_{1}
is closed under addition and negation by
2.1
.
We need to show that every point in
\mathbb{L}_{1}
is in
\mathbb{L}_{2}
.
Since
\mathbb{L}_{2}
is closed under addition and negation, it suffices to consider points of the form
\frac{1}{Q}\in\mathbb{L}_{1}
where
Q\in\mathbb{Z}
,
Q\geq 1
.
As
1\in\mathbb{L}_{2}
we may assume
Q\geq 2
.
Then
Q=\prod_{i=1}^{r}q_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
where
q_{1},\ldots,q_{r}\leq p
are distinct primes and
\alpha_{i}\geq 1
,
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
i\in[r]
.
For all
i\in[r]
define,
Q_{i}:=Qq_{i}^{-\alpha_{i}}
.
Then
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{r}
are relatively prime and so it follows from Bézout’s lemma that there exist
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\rho_{i}Q_{i}=1
.
Observe that,

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\rho_{i}q_{i}^{-\alpha_{i}}=\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\rho_{i}Q_{i}=\frac{1}{Q}.

Each of the terms on the left hand side of the previous expression is in
\mathbb{L}_{2}
.
Since
\mathbb{L}_{2}
is closed under addition, it follows that
\frac{1}{Q}\in\mathbb{L}_{2}
, as required.
∎

2.2
Density and affine hulls

In
[
3
,
4
]
we proved that a rational polyhedron contains a dyadic point if and only if its affine hull does. This is a special case of the following more general result.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\mathbb{L}
be a dense subset of
\mathbb{R}
that is closed under addition and negation.
Consider
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
where (i) the relative interior of
P
is non-empty, and (ii) the affine hull
\aff(P)
of
P
is a translate of a rational subspace.
Then
P\cap\mathbb{L}^{n}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
\aff(P)\cap\mathbb{L}^{n}\neq\emptyset
.

To prove this theorem, we need a technical lemma. To state it we need some notations. For a vector
v
and
1\leq q\leq+\infty
,
\|v\|_{q}
denotes the
q
-norm of
v
. Given
\epsilon>0
and a vector
\bar{x}
, we write
B_{q}(\bar{x},\epsilon)
for the closed
q
-norm ball
\left\{x:\|x-\bar{x}\|_{q}\leq\epsilon\right\}
centred at
\bar{x}
with radius
\epsilon
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\mathbb{L}
be a dense subset of
\mathbb{R}
that is closed under addition and negation, and let
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Suppose that
\aff(P)=z+\spn\{d^{1},\ldots,d^{\ell}\}
for some
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
d^{1},\ldots,d^{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, in particular,
\aff(P)
is a translate of a rational subspace. Consider
z^{\prime}\in\aff(P)
and
\epsilon>0
.
Then the following statements hold for any
1\leq q\leq+\infty
:

(a)

There exists
\bar{\rho}\in\mathbb{L}^{n}
such that
z+\bar{\rho}\in\aff(P)\cap B_{q}(z^{\prime},\epsilon)
.

(b)

If for some prime
p
,
\mathbb{L}
contains all the
p
-adic numbers, we can find in (a) an explicit
\bar{\rho}
that is
p
-adic.
Namely, pick a nonnegative integer
r
for which

p^{r}\geq\frac{\ell\max\{\|d^{1}\|_{q},\ldots,\|d^{\ell}\|_{q}\}}{\epsilon}.

(1)

Pick
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
satisfying

z+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}d^{i}=z^{\prime}.

(2)

Then we can choose

\bar{\rho}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{\lfloor p^{r}\alpha_{i}\rfloor}{p^{r}}d^{i}.

(3)

Furthermore, for this choice of
\bar{\rho}
we have
\|p^{r}\bar{\rho}\|_{q}\leq p^{r}(\|z^{\prime}-z\|_{q}+\epsilon)
.

Proof.

Given
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
denote
\rho(\beta):=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\beta_{i}d^{i}.
Then, since
z^{\prime}\in\aff(P)
, we have
z+\rho(\alpha)=z^{\prime}
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
. We need the claim below.

Claim
.

z+\rho(\beta)\in B_{q}(z^{\prime},\epsilon)
if

|\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\ell\max\{\|d^{1}\|_{q},\ldots,\|d^{\ell}\|_{q}\}}\qquad\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,\ell.

(4)

Proof of Claim.

We need to show that
\|z+\rho(\beta)-z^{\prime}\|_{q}\leq\epsilon
. We have,

\displaystyle\|z+\rho(\beta)-z^{\prime}\|_{q}=

\displaystyle\|\rho(\beta)-\rho(\alpha)\|_{q}=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}(\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i})d^{i}\right\|_{q}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\left\|(\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i})d^{i}\right\|_{q}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}|\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}|\;\|d^{i}\|_{q}\leq\epsilon,

where the first inequality arises from the triangle inequality and the second inequality from (
4
).

\Diamond

(a)

Since
\mathbb{L}
is dense in
\mathbb{R}
, we can pick
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{L},i\in[\ell]
such that (
4
) holds. Let
\bar{\rho}:=\rho(\beta)
.
Since
\mathbb{L}
is closed under addition and negation, and since
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{L}
and
d^{i}
is integral for each
i\in[\ell]
, we have
\bar{\rho}\in\mathbb{L}^{n}
.
By the claim,
z+\bar{\rho}\in B_{q}(z^{\prime},\epsilon)
.
Moreover, since
\aff(P)=z+\spn\{d^{1},\ldots,d^{\ell}\}
, we have
z+\bar{\rho}\in\aff(P)
.
Thus,
z+\bar{\rho}\in\aff(P)\cap B_{q}(z^{\prime},\epsilon)
, so (a) holds.

(b)

Suppose now
\mathbb{L}
contains all the
p
-adic numbers.
Pick
r
so that (
1
) holds and for each
i=1,\ldots,\ell
let
\beta_{i}:=\frac{\lfloor p^{r}\alpha_{i}\rfloor}{p^{r}}
.
Then
|\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}|\leq\frac{1}{p^{r}}
.
Equation (
1
) then implies that (
4
) holds.
It follows from the claim that for
\bar{\rho}:=\rho(\beta)
, we have
z+\bar{\rho}\in\aff(P)\cap B_{q}(z^{\prime},\epsilon)
.

Moreover, as
\beta_{i}
is
p
-adic and
d^{i}
is integral for each

,
\bar{\rho}
is
p
-adic.
By the triangle inequality,
\|\bar{\rho}\|_{q}\leq\|z^{\prime}-z\|_{q}+\|\bar{\rho}+z-z^{\prime}\|_{q}
.
Multiplying both sides by

yields
\|p^{r}\bar{\rho}\|_{q}\leq p^{r}\|z^{\prime}-z\|_{q}+p^{r}\|\bar{\rho}+z-z^{\prime}\|_{q}
.
As
\bar{\rho}+z\in B_{q}(z^{\prime},\epsilon)
, we have
\|\bar{\rho}+z-z^{\prime}\|_{q}\leq\epsilon
, so
\|p^{r}\bar{\rho}\|_{q}\leq p^{r}(\|z^{\prime}-z\|_{q}+\epsilon)

Let us present an important corollary of this result. Since every non-empty convex set has a non-empty relative interior,

2.4

Corollary 2.6

Let
\mathbb{L}
be a dense subset of
\mathbb{R}

P
, where
\aff(P)
is a translate of a rational subspace.
Then
P\cap\mathbb{L}^{n}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
\aff(P)\cap\mathbb{L}^{n}\neq\emptyset
.

When
\mathbb{L}
is the set of the dyadic numbers, and

is a polyhedron, we retrieve the fact that a rational polyhedron contains a dyadic point if and only if its affine hull does.

2.3
Theorem of the alternatives and consequences

In light of

we are interested in characterizing when a rational affine space contains a point in
\mathbb{L}^{n}
.
We addressed the case when
\mathbb{L}
is the set of dyadic points in
[
4
]
.
Density is irrelevant in this case however, and the following general theorem holds; its proof is a careful adaptation of the well-known result for the case of integers (e.g., see Theorem 1.17 in
[
10
]
). For the proof, we need a definition. A square matrix is
unimodular
if it has integral entries and its determinant is
\pm 1
. Observe that if
U
is unimodular then so is
U^{-1}
, by Cramer’s rule for instance.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
\mathbb{L}
be a proper subset of
\mathbb{R}
that is closed under addition and negation.
Consider a matrix
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
and a vector
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
.
Then exactly one of the following holds:

(a)

Ax=b
has a solution in
\mathbb{L}^{n}
,

(b)

there exists
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
A^{\top}u\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
b^{\top}u\notin\mathbb{L}
.

Moreover, if (a) holds, then
Ax=b
has a solution in
\mathbb{L}^{n}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{n}
.

Proof.

Suppose (a) and (b) both hold. Then we have
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{L}^{n}
with
A\bar{x}=b
and therefore
u^{\top}A\bar{x}=u^{\top}b
.
As
u^{\top}A
is integral and since
\mathbb{L}
is closed under addition and negation,
\mathbb{L}\ni u^{\top}A\bar{x}=u^{\top}b
, a contradiction.
Assume now that (a) does not hold. We prove that (b) holds.
If
Ax=b
does not have a solution in
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
, then there exists
u\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}
for which
u^{\top}A=\mathbf{0}^{\top}
and
u^{\top}b\neq 0
.
Since
\mathbb{L}
is a proper subset of
\mathbb{R}
, there exists
p\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{L}
. Then after updating
u:=p\cdot u
, we have
u^{\top}A=\mathbf{0}^{\top}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
u^{\top}b=p\notin\mathbb{L}
, and (b) holds.
Thus let us assume that
Ax=b
has a solution in
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
.
We may further assume that the rows of
A
are linearly independent for otherwise we can eliminate redundant constraints,
prove (b) for the smaller system and derive the result for the original system.
We can now find a unimodular matrix
U\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
for which
AU=(B\;\mathbf{0})
where
B
is a square non-singular matrix (for example one can choose
U
that converts
A
into Hermite normal form, see §
A
for more).
We claim that
\bar{z}:=B^{-1}b\notin\mathbb{L}^{m}
.
For otherwise,

\bar{x}:=U\begin{pmatrix}\bar{z}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix},

(5)

is a solution to
Ax=b
.
Since
\mathbb{L}
is closed under addition and negation and since
U
is integral, this would imply
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{L}^{n}
, a contradiction as (a) does not hold by assumption.
Thus for some
i\in[m]
we have
\bar{z}_{i}\notin\mathbb{L}
.
Let
u=B^{-\top}e_{i}
. Then

u^{\top}b=e_{i}^{\top}B^{-1}b=\bar{z}_{i}\notin\mathbb{L}
.
Moreover,
u^{\top}A=e_{i}^{\top}B^{-1}(B\;\mathbf{0})U^{-1}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
since
U^{-1}
is integral as
U
is unimodular.
Hence, (b) holds.

Thus, exactly one of (a) and (b) holds. For the final part of the theorem, suppose that (a) holds.
Then
\bar{z}=B^{-1}b\in\mathbb{L}^{m}
, for otherwise the above argument shows that (b) holds, which is not the case. Furthermore,
\bar{z}\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}
, since
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, and
B
is integral so

is rational.
Subsequently,
\bar{x}
as defined in (

in
\mathbb{L}^{n}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{n}
, as required.
∎

There are two caveats with

2.7
. The first one is in the condition that
\mathbb{L}\neq\mathbb{R}
; the only place in the proof where this condition is necessary is for finding the certificate
u
in (b) in case (a) does not hold. The second caveat is that if (a) does not hold, then we cannot always find a certificate

in (b) that is rational.
Consider for instance the case where

contains all rationals.
Then we require

in (b), in particular,

is not rational. However, in this example, if (a) does not hold, then

has no solution in
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
either. By eliminating this possibility we can guarantee that the certificate

in (b) is rational.

Remark 2.8
.

In

, if

has a solution in

and has no solution in

Indeed, suppose that

.

Given
2.6
and the second part of
Theorem

2.7
, we may ask the following question. Given a dense subset
\mathbb{L}
of
\mathbb{R}
closed under addition and negation, and given a rational polyhedron

necessarily contain a point in

Alas, this need not be the case. Consider for instance

, and let

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
\mathbb{L}
be a subset of
\mathbb{R}
that is closed under addition and negation and suppose that

Proof.

The result is well-known when
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{R}
. Otherwise,
\mathbb{L}\neq\mathbb{R}
.

(\Leftarrow)
is clear. For
(\Rightarrow)
, suppose we have a rational polyhedron

with

.
Then
\aff(P)\cap\mathbb{L}^{n}\neq\emptyset
, so it follows from
Theorem

2.7
that
\aff(P)\cap\mathbb{L}^{n}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{n}\neq\emptyset
. Since

is a dense subset of
\mathbb{R}
that is closed under addition and negation, we may apply

to conclude that

, as required.
∎

(D)

(a)
\Rightarrow
(b)

.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
\mathbb{L}

3
Algorithms: feasibility and optimization

In this section, we present algorithms to check whether a rational polyhedron contains a point in
\mathbb{L}^{n}
and to optimize a linear function over a rational polyhedron restricted to
\mathbb{L}^{n}

where
\mathbb{L}
satisfies the following three properties:

(group)
\mathbb{L}
is closed under addition and negation,

(density)
\mathbb{L}
contains all
p
-adic numbers for some prime
p
, and

(membership)   we have a membership oracle for
\mathbb{L}
.

Assuming the (group) property we also need to require
\mathbb{L}
to be a dense subset of the reals for otherwise we are operating within the context of integer programming as discussed in
Section

2.1
.
We want to be able to carry all computations over
\mathbb{Q}
, in particular, we insist that if a rational polyhedron contains a point in
\mathbb{L}^{n}
then it contains one in
\mathbb{L}^{n}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{n}
.
Because of
Theorem

2.9
this can be achieved by choosing
\mathbb{L}
with the property that
\mathbb{L}\cap\mathbb{Q}
is dense.
A natural choice is to include all
p
-adic numbers in
\mathbb{L}
, i.e. imposing the (density) condition above.
Note, that we will assume that we are also given the prime value
p
explicitly.
A
membership oracle
for
\mathbb{L}
is a function that takes as input
x\in\mathbb{Q}
and returns
yes
if
x\in\mathbb{L}
and
no
otherwise.
We will present algorithms that run in oracle polynomial time for both feasibility and optimization over polyhedra restricted to
\mathbb{L}^{n}
.
When
\mathbb{L}
is the set of
p
-adic, or
[p]
-adic numbers, our three properties (group), (density), and (membership) hold, and we have trivial polynomial oracles.
Thus, we will be able to solve the feasibility and optimization problems for
p
-adic and
[p]
-adic numbers in polynomial time.
In particular, we can solve the problem for dyadic numbers.

Our algorithms will rely on the existence of a number of subroutines that we present next.

Algorithm A.

Takes as input a matrix
A\in\mathbb{Q}^{m\times n}
and a vector
b\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}
.
Returns one of the following:
(i)
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
satisfying
Ax=b
, or
(ii)

such that
A^{\top}u=\mathbf{0}
and
b^{\top}u\neq 0
certifying
Ax=b
has no solution.

Algorithm B.

Takes as input a matrix

and a vector
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
.
We are also given a membership oracle that describes a set
\mathbb{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
that is closed under addition and negation.
Returns one of the following:
(i)
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{L}^{n}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{n}
satisfying
Ax=b
, or
(ii)
u\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}
such that
A^{\top}u=\mathbf{0}
and
b^{\top}u\neq 0
certifying
Ax=b
has no solution, or
(iii)
u\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}
such that
A^{\top}u
is integral and

certifying

has no solution in
\mathbb{L}^{n}
.

Algorithm C.

Takes as input a matrix

Returns linearly independent vectors

Takes as input a matrix

.
Then solves the linear program

(P)

Namely, it returns one of the following:

Consider a polyhedron

3.1.3
Output size and running time analysis

We refer to the algorithm described in
Section

3.1.1
as
the LFP algorithm
, and the algorithm described in
Section

3.1.2
as the
alternate
LFP algorithm.
Informally, the runtime of our LFP algorithm is dominated by the runtime of our linear program solver,
which we call twice, once for checking feasibility of
Ax\leq b
and once for solving (
7
).
Note, that both linear programs have essentially the same size (with respect to many measures), moreover, they are both using the original data
A,b
.
Thus, checking for
\mathbb{L}
-feasibility is about at most twice as time consuming as checking for real-feasibility.
The same conclusion applies to the alternate LFP algorithm.

We will analyze the running time of the LFP algorithm and the output size of the alternate LFP algorithm (the analysis is somewhat cleaner for that version because of the use of
\infty
-norms).
The

of an integer

is defined as

Observe that for rationals

we have
\mathrm{size}\!\left(\alpha\right)=\mathrm{size}\!\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)

\mathrm{size}\!\left(\alpha\beta\right)\in\mathit{O}\bigl(\mathrm{size}\!\left(\alpha\right)+\mathrm{size}\!\left(\beta\right)\bigr)

We consider

which appears in the (density) property of the set

to be an absolute constant.
For each of Algorithms A-D we can view the input as an

-by-

matrix where the largest encoding size of any entry is given by

Then the runtime is bounded by a function

,

,

,

for each of algorithms A, B, C, D where

,

,
f_{c}
,

are functions of
m^{\prime},n^{\prime},\sigma
.
In addition, for the output, the largest entry size is bounded by a function
g_{b}
,
g_{c}
,
g_{d}
for each of algorithms B, C, D where
g_{b}
,
g_{c}
,
g_{d}
are also functions of
m^{\prime},n^{\prime},\sigma
.
There exist implementations for each algorithm where each of

is a polynomial in

.
Note, for Algorithm B the runtime is in terms of the number of calls to the

-oracle.

Let us now analyze the runtime.

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider

and a vector

and let

Proof.

The runtime of the algorithm is longest when it finds a point in

so we assume that this is the case in the analysis.

Step 1.

The runtime is dominated by Algorithm D with
A,b
.
It is in
\mathit{O}\bigl(f_{d}(m,n+1,\sigma)\bigr)
since the data

can be represented as an

-by-
(n+1)
matrix.

and

which are used to find the implicit equalities

of

We run Algorithm B for the system

.

and

∎

We close this discussion by showing that functions

3.2
\mathbb{L}
-Linear Programming

Suppose that
\mathbb{L}
is dense and closed under addition and negation, and consider the following optimization problem,

\sup\left\{c^{\top}x:Ax\leq b,\;x\in\mathbb{L}^{n}\right\},

(16)

where
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
,
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
and
c\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
We say that (
16
) is an
\mathbb{L}
-linear program
.
Trivially, the status of this optimization problem has to fall within exactly one of the following outcomes:

(o1)

it is infeasible,

(o2)

it is unbounded,

(o3)

it has an optimal solution,

(o4)

it is feasible, bounded, but has no optimal solution.

Note that in contrast to linear programming, (o4) can occur, such as for
\sup\{x:3x\leq 1,\;\mbox{$x$ dyadic}\}
.

3.2.1
Certificates

Here we certify each of the outcomes (o1)-(o4).
We certified (o1) with a
certificate of
\mathbb{L}
-infeasibility
in
Theorem

2.11
.
(o2) is similar to linear programming.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\mathbb{L}\supset\mathbb{Z}
be a dense subset of
\mathbb{R}
that is closed under addition and negation.
Suppose that (
16
) is feasible.
Then the following are equivalent:

(a)

(
16
) is unbounded,

(b)

\max\{c^{\top}x:Ax\leq b\}
is unbounded,

(c)

there exists
r\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with

and

.

A

is a pair
(\bar{x},r)
, where
\bar{x}
is a feasible solution to (

), and

is from (b).

Proof.

Denote by (P) the linear program
\max\{c^{\top}x:Ax\leq b\}
.
Since (
16
) is feasible, so is (P).
For the equivalence between (b) and (c), see Proposition 3.9 in
[
10
]
.
Note, that for (c) the condition that

is rational is equivalent to the condition that

is integral because of scaling.
We show that (a) and (b) are equivalent.
First if (
16
) is unbounded, so is (P) since it is a relaxation of (

).
Thus assume that (P) is unbounded.
Then there exists
r
as described in (c).
Let
\bar{x}
be a feasible solution to (
16
) and for any

.
Consider
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}
where
\lambda\geq 0
. Then
\lambda r\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
By the hypothesis
\mathbb{L}\supset\mathbb{Z}
, it follows that

.
Since

is closed under addition and negation,

and is therefore feasible for (

).
For

.
Hence, (

∎

We next distinguish between outcomes (o3) and (o4).

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\mathbb{L}
be a dense subset of
\mathbb{R}
that is closed under addition and negation.
Suppose that (
16
) is feasible and bounded.
Then the following are equivalent,

(a)

(

) has no optimal solution,

(b)

there exist
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

(ii)

Condition (ii) implies that
\bar{x}
and
\bar{y}
form a pair of primal dual solutions with the same value, hence, that

is optimal for the dual of (P).
Conversely, if

\mathbb{L}\supset\mathbb{Z}

3.2.4
Solving linear programs versus solving

-linear programs

-linear program required at most two calls of the LFP procedure for outcomes (o1)-(o3) and three calls in case of outcome (o4). Thus the running time of our

-linear program solver is at most three times that of LFP.

to solve linear feasibility problems over rationals, i.e., finding a rational point in a polyhedron (which in turn is sufficient for solving linear programs).

4
Bounding the fractionality of dyadic solutions

5
Bounding the support size of dyadic solutions

Take

For the case of real solutions to

m

For the case of integral solutions to

, for any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for which there is a finite optimum.

A natural first step for studying both extremes, as well as dyadic linear program, is to obtain guarantees for the system of linear equations

5.1
Examples where every solution has full support

Let us present instances of (

) and (

Example 5.1
.

Let
q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}
be distinct primes such that
q_{i}\geq p_{k+1}
for all
i\in[n]
. Let
Q:=q_{1}q_{2}\cdots q_{n}
and

is feasible, and every solution has full support.

One can extend this example to one with an arbitrary number
m
of rows, by replacing

by
(A\otimes I)y=\mathbf{1}
, where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product,

the
m
-by-

identity matrix, and
\mathbf{1}
the all-ones

Proof.

We only prove the first statement, and leave the easy verification of the second statement to the reader. Since the entries of
A
have GCD
1
, it follows from Bézout’s Lemma that
Ax=1
has an integral, thus
[p_{k}]
-adic solution. This proves feasibility of the system

. Now let
\bar{x}
be a feasible solution. Suppose for a contradiction that

does not have full support, say
\bar{x}_{n}=0
. For each

where

and

is a product of primes in

. Let

be the largest common multiple of

, which is also a product of primes in
[p_{k}]
. Then we have the identity

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}A_{1i}\frac{a_{i}B}{b_{i}}=B.

By construction, the GCD of

is
q_{n}
.
Since each
\frac{a_{i}B}{b_{i}},i\in[n-1]
is an integer, it therefore follows from the identity above that

, a contradiction since

is a prime outside

while

is a product of primes in
[p_{k}]
.
∎

This example can naively be extended to the inequality case.

Example 5.2
.

For

Example 5.3

Let

Then

is the unique optimal solution, which in particular has full support.
As before, this example can readily be extended to one with an arbitrary number

\min\left\{\mathbf{1}^{\top}y:(A\otimes I)y=(2^{n}-1)\cdot\mathbf{1},y\geq\mathbf{0}\text{ and integral}\right\}.

We only verify the first statement, and leave the proof of the second statement to the reader. Clearly,

5.2
From full support solutions to large prime factors

In this section, we pave the way for obtaining an upper bound on the support size for (
23
), and an even better bound for (
24
). The gap between these two bounds is due to the following lemma.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n},b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m},w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and take an integer
k\geq 0
. Then the following statements hold:

1.

Suppose
Ax=b,x\text{ $[p_{k}]$-adic}
, is feasible, and every solution has full support. Then for every integral solution
\bar{x}
to
Ax=\mathbf{0}
, every nonzero entry has a prime factor greater than or equal to
p_{k+1}
.

2.

Suppose
\min\{w^{\top}x:Ax=b,x\geq\mathbf{0},x\text{ $[p_{k}]$-adic}\}
has an optimal solution, and every optimal solution has full support. Then for every nonzero integral solution
\bar{x}
to
Ax=\mathbf{0}
, there exists some nonzero entry with a prime factor greater than or equal to
p_{k+1}
.

Proof.

(1)

Let
\bar{x}
be a nonzero integral vector such that
A\bar{x}=\mathbf{0}
.
Suppose for a contradiction that for some index
i
,
\bar{x}_{i}\neq 0
and every prime factor of
\bar{x}_{i}
is at most
p_{k}
. Let
x^{1}
be a
[p_{k}]
-adic solution to
Ax=b
. By the hypothesis,
x^{1}
has full support, so
x^{1}_{i}\neq 0
. Let
x^{2}:=x^{1}-\frac{x^{1}_{i}}{\bar{x}_{i}}\bar{x}
, which is another solution to
Ax=b
. Since every prime factor of
\bar{x}_{i}
belongs to
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\}
, it follows that
x^{2}
is another
[p_{k}]
-adic solution to
Ax=b
, one whose support excludes
i
, a contradiction to our hypothesis.

(2)
is similar to (1), except that in order to ensure
x^{2}
remains nonnegative given the nonnegativity of
x^{1}
, we would need the prime factors of every nonzero entry of
\bar{x}
to be less than
p_{k+1}
. Let us elaborate. Let
\bar{x}
be a nonzero integral vector such that
A\bar{x}=\mathbf{0}
.

First, we prove that
w^{\top}\bar{x}=0
. Suppose otherwise. Let
x^{\star}
be an optimal solution to
\min\{w^{\top}x:Ax=b,x\geq\mathbf{0},x\text{ is $[p_{k}]$-adic}\}
. By assumption,
x^{\star}
has full support, implying in turn that for a sufficiently small and
[p_{k}]
-adic
\epsilon>0
, both
x^{\star}\pm\epsilon\bar{x}
are feasible solutions to the
[p_{k}]
-adic linear program. However, since
w^{\top}\bar{x}\neq 0
, one of
x^{\star}\pm\epsilon\bar{x}
would have a strictly smaller objective value than
x^{\star}
, thereby contradicting the optimality of
x^{\star}
. Thus,
w^{\top}\bar{x}=0
.

Secondly, we prove that some nonzero entry of
\bar{x}
has a prime factor greater than or equal to
p_{k+1}
. Suppose otherwise. We shall use
\bar{x}
to construct an optimal solution to
\min\{w^{\top}x:Ax=b,x\geq\mathbf{0},x\text{ is $[p_{k}]$-adic}\}
without full support, thereby contradicting the hypothesis. To this end, let
x^{1}
be an optimal solution to the
[p_{k}]
-adic linear program. By the hypothesis,
x^{1}
has full support. Choose

i\in\arg\min_{j\in[n]}\left\{\frac{x^{1}_{j}}{\bar{x}_{j}}:\bar{x}_{j}\neq 0\right\}.

Let
x^{2}:=x^{1}-\frac{x^{1}_{i}}{\bar{x}_{i}}\bar{x}
. Our choice of
i
ensures that
x^{2}
is defined and nonnegative. Since every prime factor of
\bar{x}_{i}
belongs to
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\}
by the contrary assumption,
x^{2}
is
[p_{k}]
-adic. Thus, since
w^{\top}\bar{x}=0
, it follows that
x^{2}
is another optimal solution to
\min\{w^{\top}x:Ax=b,x\geq\mathbf{0},x\text{ is $[p_{k}]$-adic}\}
, one whose support excludes
i
, a contradiction to our hypothesis.
∎

5.3
Large prime factors: examples and analysis

It may not be clear how to find examples that satisfies the conclusions of
5.4
parts (1) and (2). Let us present two examples, both of which we will prove to be extremal in a sense. Throughout the subsection we assume that
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

Example 5.5
.

Let
q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}
be distinct primes such that
q_{i}\geq p_{k+1}
for all
i\in[n]
. Let
Q:=q_{1}q_{2}\cdots q_{n}
and

. It can be readily checked that for any integral solution
\bar{x}
to

, and for each

, we have

then

First, notice that the example above comes from
5.1
, indicating that its essence is captured by
5.4
(1). Secondly, note that in the example above, the entries of the row vector

does not change its kernel. More precisely, the size of every entry divided by the GCD of the entries, is at least

Lemma 5.6
.

Let

be an

-by-
n

to

\frac{|\det(A_{[m]\times J})|}{\gcd(A)}\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}p_{k+2}^{n-m}&\text{ if $n<2m$}\\

Given a prime number

Claim 1
.

Every row of

Proof of Claim.

Let

, as claimed.

\Diamond

Claim 2
.

Every prime number

.

Proof of Claim.

Suppose otherwise, that is, for some prime number

\Diamond

Claim 3

For every subset

|\det(U_{\overline{I}\times I_{2}})|\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}p_{k+1}^{n-m}&\text{ if $n<2m$}\\
p_{k+1}^{m}p_{k+2}^{m}\cdots p_{k+\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\rfloor-1}^{m}p_{k+\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\rfloor}^{n-m\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\rfloor}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Moreover, there is a subset
J\subseteq[n]
of size
m
such that
\det(U_{\overline{J}\times I_{2}})\neq 0
, and

|\det(U_{\overline{J}\times I_{2}})|\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}p_{k+2}^{n-m}&\text{ if $n<2m$}\\
p_{k+2}^{m}p_{k+3}^{m}\cdots p_{k+\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\rfloor}^{m}p_{k+\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\rfloor+1}^{n-m\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\rfloor}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Proof of Claim.

Take a subset
I\subseteq[n]
of size
m
such that
\det(U_{\overline{I}\times I_{2}})\neq 0

By Claim 1, every row of

divisible by
p_{k+1}
(of which there are at most
m
by Claim 2), and
\det(U_{\overline{J}\times I_{2}})\neq 0
. This can be done by using the fact that
\gcd(U_{2})=1
, so there exists a minor of
U_{2}
of order
n-m
that is not divisible by
p_{k+1}
; this will be precisely
\det(U_{\overline{J}\times I_{2}})
.

\Diamond

Claim 4
.

For every subset
I\subseteq[n]
of size
m
, we have
|\det(A_{[m]\times I})|=\gcd(A)\cdot|\det(U_{\overline{I}\times I_{2}})|
.

Proof of Claim.

It follows from
A=(B~\mathbf{0})U^{-1}
that
A_{[m]\times I}=(B~\mathbf{0})(U^{-1})_{[n]\times I}=B\cdot(U^{-1})_{I_{1}\times I}
. Subsequently,

|\det(A_{[m]\times I})|=|\det(B)|\cdot|\det((U^{-1})_{I_{1}\times I})|=\gcd(A)\cdot|\det((U^{-1})_{I_{1}\times I})|.

The right-hand side term can be rewritten in terms of the adjugate
\mathrm{adj}(U)
of
U
. Observe that
\mathrm{adj}(U)=\det(U)\cdot U^{-1}
, so
U^{-1}=\pm\mathrm{adj}(U)
because
U
is unimodular. Thus,

|\det((U^{-1})_{I_{1}\times I})|=|\det(\mathrm{adj}(U)_{I_{1}\times I})|=|\det(U_{\bar{I}\times I_{2}})|\cdot|\det(U)|^{m-1}=|\det(U_{\bar{I}\times I_{2}})|

where the second equality follows from Jacobi’s Theorem on the adjugate matrix (see
Appendix

A
for more), and the last equality from the unimodularity of
U
. Combining the two lines of equalities above yields the claim.

\Diamond

The lemma readily follows from Claims 3 and 4.
∎

Next, let us present an example that satisfies the conclusion of
5.4
(2).

Example 5.7
.

Let
A:=\left(1~p_{k+1}~p_{k+1}^{2}~\cdots~p_{k+1}^{n-1}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}
. It can be readily checked that for any nonzero integral solution
\bar{x}
to
Ax=\mathbf{0}
, the smallest index
i\in[n]
such that
\bar{x}_{i}\neq 0
satisfies
p_{k+1}\mid\bar{x}_{i}
. Thus the
1
-by-
n
matrix
A
satisfies the conclusion of
5.4
(2).

First, notice that this example is the same as the feasible region of
5.3
for
k=0
, indicating that in this case the essence of the example is captured by
5.4
(2). Secondly, in the example above, the row vector
A
has small entries as well as large, however its
2
-norm is “large” relative to the GCD of its entries. More precisely, the
2
-norm of the row vector divided by the GCD of the entries, is equal to
\sqrt{\frac{p_{k+1}^{2n}-1}{p^{2}_{k+1}-1}}
; the latter is sandwiched between
p_{k+1}^{n-1}
and
\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{p^{2}_{k+1}-1}}\cdot p_{k+1}^{n-1}
. In
5.8
below, known as
Siegel’s Lemma
, we prove the lower bound more generally for a full-row-rank matrix with
m
rows, where the “
2
-norm of the row vector” is replaced by “the
m
-dimensional volume of the parallelepiped generated by the rows of
A
”, and as before, the “GCD of the entries” is replaced by the “GCD of order-
m
minors”.

Lemma 5.8

(Siegel’s Lemma, see
[
9
]
)
.

Consider a linear system
Ax=\mathbf{0}
, where
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
has full row rank, and let
\ell\geq 1
. Suppose for every nonzero integral solution
\bar{x}
to
Ax=\mathbf{0}
,
\|\bar{x}\|_{\infty}\geq\ell
. Then
\sqrt{\det(AA^{\top})}/\gcd(A)\geq\ell^{n-m}
.

Proof.

Consider the lattice
L:=\{A^{\top}y:y\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\}
and its orthogonal complement lattice
L^{\perp}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:Ax=\mathbf{0}\right\}
. By assumption, the convex set
Q:=\{x:\|x\|_{\infty}<\ell\}\cap\{x:Ax=\mathbf{0}\}
, which is symmetric about the origin, contains no nonzero vector of the lattice
L^{\perp}
. We may therefore apply Minkowski’s First Theorem to upper bound the
(n-m)
-dimensional volume of
Q
(see
Appendix

A
for more). The theorem implies that
\vol_{n-m}(Q)\leq 2^{n-m}\det(L^{\perp})
.
On the one hand,
\frac{1}{2\ell}\cdot Q
is an
(n-m)
-dimensional affine slice of the unit hypercube
\{x:\|x\|_{\infty}<\frac{1}{2}\}
going through the origin, so by Vaaler
[
32
]
,
\vol_{n-m}(\frac{1}{2\ell}Q)\geq 1
, implying in turn that
\vol_{n-m}(Q)\geq(2\ell)^{n-m}
.
On the other hand, given that
\overline{L}:=\{A^{\top}y:y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}\supseteq L
, we have

\det(L^{\perp})=\det(\overline{L})=\frac{\det(L)}{[\overline{L}:L]}=\frac{\det(L)}{\gcd(A^{\top})}=\frac{\sqrt{\det(AA^{\top})}}{\gcd(A)}

(see
Appendix

A
for more). Putting everything together we obtain the desired inequality.
∎

5.4
Proxy and direct upper bounds on the support size

We obtain the following exact upper bound on the support size of solutions to
[p_{k}]
-adic linear systems and
[p_{k}]
-adic linear programs. Note that the upper bounds are provided indirectly by suitable proxy functions of the input size.

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
,
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then the following statements hold for every integer
k\geq 0
:

1.

If
Ax=b,x\text{ $[p_{k}]$-adic}
, is feasible, then it has a solution with support size at most
n^{\prime}
, where
for some full-row-rank
m^{\prime}
-by-
n^{\prime}
submatrix
A^{\prime}
of
A
, we have

\Delta_{m^{\prime}}(A^{\prime})\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}p_{k+2}^{\frac{n^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}-1}&\text{ if $n^{\prime}<2m^{\prime}$}\\
p_{k+2}p_{k+3}\cdots p_{k+\lfloor\frac{n^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}\rfloor}p_{k+\lfloor\frac{n^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}\rfloor+1}^{\frac{n^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}-\lfloor\frac{n^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}\rfloor}&\text{ otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

where
\Delta_{m^{\prime}}(A^{\prime})
is the
m^{\prime}\textsuperscript{th}
root of the maximum absolute value of an order-
m^{\prime}
minor of
A^{\prime}
.

2.

If
\min\{w^{\top}x:Ax=b,x\geq\mathbf{0},x\text{ $[p_{k}]$-adic}\}
has an optimal solution, then it has an optimal solution with support size at most
n^{\prime}
, where for some full-row-rank submatrix
A^{\prime}
of
A
with
m^{\prime}
rows,
n^{\prime}
satisfies

\left(\frac{\sqrt{\det(A^{\prime}A^{\prime\top})}}{\gcd(A^{\prime})}\right)^{\frac{1}{m^{\prime}}}\geq p_{k+1}^{\frac{n^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}-1}.

Proof.

(1)
Let
x^{\star}
be a solution to
Ax=b,x\text{ $[p_{k}]$-adic}
, with minimum support size. After moving to a submatrix of
A
, and the corresponding subvector of
b
, if necessary, we may assume that
x^{\star}
has full support, and
A^{\prime}=A
has full row rank; thus
m^{\prime}=m\leq n=n^{\prime}
.
By
5.4
(1), for every integral solution
\bar{x}
to
Ax=\mathbf{0}
, every nonzero entry of
\bar{x}
has a prime factor greater than or equal to
p_{k+1}
. Thus, by
5.6
,
A
has an
m
-by-
m
submatrix
B
such that

|\det(B)|\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}p_{k+2}^{n-m}&\text{ if $n<2m$}\\
p_{k+2}^{m}p_{k+3}^{m}\cdots p_{k+\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\rfloor}^{m}p_{k+\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\rfloor+1}^{n-m\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\rfloor}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Taking the
m\textsuperscript{th}
of both sides, and taking advantage of the inequality
\Delta_{m}(A)\geq|\det(B)|^{1/m}
by definition, we obtain (1).

(2)

Let
x^{\star}
be an optimal solution to
\min\{w^{\top}x:Ax=b,x\geq\mathbf{0},x\text{ $[p_{k}]$-adic}\}
with minimum support size. After moving to a submatrix of
A
, and the corresponding subvectors of
w
and
b
, if necessary, we may assume that
x^{\star}
has full support, and
A^{\prime}=A
has full row rank; thus
m^{\prime}=m\leq n=n^{\prime}
. By
5.4
(2), for every integral solution
\bar{x}
to
Ax=\mathbf{0}
, there exists a nonzero entry with a prime factor greater than or equal to
p_{k+1}
, so in particular
\|\bar{x}\|_{\infty}\geq p_{k+1}
.
It therefore follows from Siegel’s Lemma that

\frac{\sqrt{\det(AA^{\top})}}{\gcd(A)}\geq p_{k+1}^{n-m},

proving (2).
∎

Observe that for fixed
A
, both bounds above guarantee that
\frac{n^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}\to 1
as
p_{k}\to\infty
, thereby matching Carathéodory’s bounds for the support size of solutions to linear systems, and optimal solutions to linear programs.

We can use the theorem above, along with Hadamard’s inequality, to provide loose but direct upper bounds on the support size of solutions.

Theorem 5.10
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
,
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then the following statements hold for every integer
k\geq 0
:

1.

If
Ax=b,x\text{ $[p_{k}]$-adic}
, is feasible, then it has a solution with support size at most
n^{\prime}
, where for
r=\lfloor\frac{n^{\prime}}{m}\rfloor
, the following holds: if
r\geq 1+2e
and
\ln(\sqrt{m}\|A\|_{\infty})\geq e
, then

r\leq 1+\frac{2(1+e)}{e}\frac{\ln(\sqrt{m}\|A\|_{\infty})}{\ln\ln(\sqrt{m}\|A\|_{\infty})}.

2.

If
\min\{w^{\top}x:Ax=b,x\geq\mathbf{0},x\text{ $[p_{k}]$-adic}\}
has an optimal solution, then it has an optimal solution with support size at most
n^{\prime}
, where for
r=\frac{n^{\prime}}{m}
, we have

r\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}1+\ln{(m\|A\|^{2}_{\infty})}/(2\ln{p_{k+1}}-1)&\text{for all $k\geq 0$,}\\
\\
1+\log_{2}\|A\|_{\infty}+\log_{2}(m\|A\|_{\infty})\cdot\frac{1+\log_{2}\|A\|_{\infty}}{1+2\log_{2}\|A\|_{\infty}}&\text{ if $k=0$ and $n^{\prime}\geq 4\|A\|^{2}_{\infty}$.}\end{array}\right.

Proof.

(1)
For some full-row-rank
m^{\prime}
-by-
n^{\prime}
submatrix
A^{\prime}
of
A
,
the inequality of
Theorem

5.9
(1) holds. We may assume that
A^{\prime}=A
,
m^{\prime}=m
and
n^{\prime}=n
. Let
r:=\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\rfloor
.
If
r=1
then there is nothing to prove. Otherwise,
r\geq 2
. Then by
Theorem

5.9
(1) we have

\Delta_{m}(A)\geq\prod_{i=1}^{r-1}(p_{k+2}+i-1)\geq\left(p_{k+2}+\left\lceil\frac{r-2}{2}\right\rceil\right)^{\left\lceil\frac{r-1}{2}\right\rceil}.

On the other hand, by Hadamard’s inequality,
\sqrt{m}\|A\|_{\infty}\geq\Delta_{m}(A).

Combining this inequality with the one above, and then taking the natural logarithm, we obtain

\left\lceil\frac{r-1}{2}\right\rceil\ln\left(p_{k+2}+\left\lceil\frac{r-2}{2}\right\rceil\right)\leq\ln(\sqrt{m}\|A\|_{\infty}).

Let
x:=\frac{r-1}{2}
and
y:=\ln(\sqrt{m}\|A\|_{\infty})
. Then the inequality above implies that
x\ln(x)\leq y
.

Claim 1
.

For
x,y\geq e
, we have

x\leq\frac{1+e}{e}\cdot\frac{y}{\ln(y)}
.

Proof of Claim.

Let
z:=x\ln(x)
. Then for
x\geq 1
,

\frac{z}{\ln(z)}=\frac{x\ln(x)}{\ln(x)+\ln\ln(x)}\geq\frac{x\ln(x)}{(1+1/e)\ln(x)}=\frac{e}{1+e}x

where the middle inequality holds because
\ln\ln(x)\leq\frac{1}{e}\ln(x)
for all
x\geq e
. Since the function
\frac{2t}{\ln(t)}
is increasing on
t\in[e,\infty)
, and
y\geq z\geq e
, it follows that
\frac{1+e}{e}\cdot\frac{y}{\ln(y)}\geq\frac{1+e}{e}\cdot\frac{z}{\ln(z)}
, so the claim follows.
\Diamond

Claim 1 proves the inequality of (1).

(2)
For some full-row-rank
m^{\prime}
-by-
n^{\prime}
submatrix
A^{\prime}
of
A
,
the inequality of
Theorem

5.9
(2) holds.
We may assume that
A^{\prime}=A
,
m^{\prime}=m
and
n^{\prime}=n
. Thus, we have that

\sqrt{\det(AA^{\top})}\geq p_{k+1}^{n-m}.

Suppose
a^{1},\ldots,a^{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
are the rows of
A
. By Hadamard’s inequality,

\sqrt{\det(AA^{\top})}\leq\sqrt{\prod_{i=1}^{m}\|Aa^{i}\|_{2}}\leq\sqrt{\prod_{i=1}^{m}(\sqrt{m}\sqrt{n}\|A\|^{2}_{\infty})}\leq\left(\sqrt{n}\|A\|_{\infty}\right)^{m}.

Combining the inequalities above, and then taking logarithms base
p_{k+1}
, we obtain

n\leq m+m\log_{p_{k+1}}\left(\sqrt{n}\|A\|_{\infty}\right).

(25)

Let
f,g:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 1}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

be the functions defined as
g(x)=x+x\log_{p_{k+1}}\|A\|_{\infty}
and
f(x):=\log_{p_{k+1}}x
.
Then (
25
) may be rewritten as
n\leq g(m)+\frac{m}{2}f(n)
. Observe that
f
is an increasing function.

Claim 2
.

The following inequalities hold:

a.

f(g(m)+\frac{m}{2}f(n))\leq f(g(m))+\frac{m}{2\ln(p_{k+1})g(m)}f(n)
,

b.

for
k=0
, if
n\geq 4\|A\|^{2}_{\infty}
, then
f(g(m)+\frac{m}{2}f(n))\leq f(g(m))+\frac{m}{2g(m)}f(n)
.

Proof of Claim.

To see the inequalities, note that
\log_{p_{k+1}}(x+y)=\log_{p_{k+1}}(x)+\log_{p_{k+1}}(1+\frac{y}{x})\leq\log_{p_{k+1}}(x)+\frac{1}{\ln(p_{k+1})}\frac{y}{x}
for all
x,y
over which the LHS and RHS are defined.
Thus,

f\left(g(m)+\frac{m}{2}f(n)\right)=\log_{p_{k+1}}\left(g(m)+\frac{m}{2}f(n)\right)\leq\log_{p_{k+1}}g(m)+\frac{m\cdot f(n)}{2\ln(p_{k+1})g(m)}

thereby proving (a). When
k=0
(i.e.
p_{k+1}=2
), we can replace
\log_{p_{k+1}}(1+\frac{y}{x})\leq\frac{1}{\ln(p_{k+1})}\frac{y}{x}
by the improved inequality
\log_{p_{k+1}}(1+\frac{y}{x})\leq\frac{y}{x}
as long as
\frac{y}{x}\geq 1
. Thus, after repeating the above argument with this improved inequality, we obtain (b).

\Diamond

Subsequently,

\displaystyle n\leq g(m)+\frac{m}{2}f(n)

\displaystyle\leq g(m)+\frac{m}{2}f\left(g(m)+\frac{m}{2}f(n)\right)\quad\text{since $f$ is increasing}

\displaystyle\leq g(m)+\frac{m}{2}f(g(m))+\frac{m}{2}\frac{m}{2\ln(p_{k+1})g(m)}f(n)\quad\text{by part (a) of Claim~2}

\displaystyle\quad\vdots

\displaystyle\leq g(m)+\frac{m}{2}f(g(m))\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{m}{2\ln(p_{k+1})g(m)}\right)^{t}

\displaystyle=g(m)+\frac{m}{2}f(g(m))\cdot\frac{2\ln(p_{k+1})g(m)}{2\ln(p_{k+1})g(m)-m}.

Substituting for
g(m)
and
f(g(m))
, and dividing both sides by
m
, we get the following inequality:

\displaystyle\frac{n}{m}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\log_{p_{k+1}}\|A\|_{\infty}+\frac{1}{2}\log_{p_{k+1}}(m+m\log_{p_{k+1}}\|A\|_{\infty})\cdot\frac{1+\log_{p_{k+1}}\|A\|_{\infty}}{1+\log_{p_{k+1}}\|A\|_{\infty}-\frac{1}{2\ln(p_{k+1})}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\log_{p_{k+1}}\|A\|_{\infty}+\left(\log_{p_{k+1}}m+\frac{\log_{p_{k+1}}\|A\|_{\infty}}{\ln(p_{k+1})}\right)\cdot\frac{1+\log_{p_{k+1}}\|A\|_{\infty}}{2+2\log_{p_{k+1}}\|A\|_{\infty}-\frac{1}{\ln(p_{k+1})}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\log_{p_{k+1}}\|A\|_{\infty}+\left(\log_{p_{k+1}}m+\frac{\log_{p_{k+1}}\|A\|_{\infty}}{\ln(p_{k+1})}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{2-\frac{1}{\ln(p_{k+1})}}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\ln{m}+2\ln{\|A\|_{\infty}}}{2\ln{p_{k+1}}-1}.

If
k=0
and
n\geq 4\|A\|^{2}_{\infty}
, then we can use part (b) instead of part (a) of Claim 2 in the inequalities above, and obtain

n\leq g(m)+\frac{m}{2}f(g(m))\cdot\frac{2g(m)}{2g(m)-m}.

Substituting for
g(m)
and
f(g(m))
, and dividing both sides by
m
, we get the following inequality:

\displaystyle\frac{n}{m}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\log_{2}\|A\|_{\infty}+\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(m+m\log_{2}\|A\|_{\infty})\cdot\frac{1+\log_{2}\|A\|_{\infty}}{1+2\log_{2}\|A\|_{\infty}-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\log_{2}\|A\|_{\infty}+\left(\log_{2}m+\log_{2}\|A\|_{\infty}\right)\cdot\frac{1+\log_{2}\|A\|_{\infty}}{1+2\log_{2}\|A\|_{\infty}},

as required.
∎

.

6
Concluding remarks and future research

In this paper, we studied dyadic linear programming and its extension to
\mathbb{L}
-linear programming, where
\mathbb{L}
is a dense subset of
\mathbb{R}
closed under addition and negation. Two important extensions were
p
-adic and
[p]
-adic linear programs, for a prime
p
.

We laid the foundation for
\mathbb{L}
-linear programming by characterizing feasibility, stating optimality conditions, classifying all the possible outcomes and providing concise certificates in each case. A distinguishing feature was that unlike linear programs, an
\mathbb{L}
-linear program may have an optimal value that is converged to but never attained within the feasible region.

We proved that under mild assumptions on
\mathbb{L}
, namely that
\mathbb{L}
comes with a membership oracle and contains all
p
-adic numbers for some explicitly given prime
p
, an
\mathbb{L}
-linear program can be solved in polynomial time. In fact, we established a constant factor equivalence between the running times of solving an
\mathbb{L}
-linear program and a linear program, with blackbox reductions going in either direction.

Going beyond the blackbox reductions, if we are given deeper access to an algorithm for solving LPs which is guaranteed to find strictly complementary solutions (when the instance has an optimal solution), we can modify such an algorithm in part by inserting our subroutines in suitable places so that the LP algorithm is run only once (with these inserted subroutines) to solve the corresponding
\mathbb{L}
-linear program. Such modifications can be particularly straightforward for the two-phase algorithms for LPs (as one can follow the analysis in
Section

3
to see how to modify the LP algorithm).

Our blackbox approach can also be useful in solving
\mathbb{L}
-convex programs
. For a special class of
convex optimization problems at hand, if we are able to compute affine hulls (of the feasible region and the optimal face) and
obtain rational representations for them (when possible) then, we can solve the
\mathbb{L}
-convex program using the approach in
Section

3
. We do not need the convex programming instance to satisfy strict complementarity, but we would require the convex optimization algorithm to compute rational vectors (when they exist) in the relative interiors of the corresponding sets. Limits of sequences of solutions generated by many interior-point algorithms for convex optimization lie in the relative interiors of the corresponding sets.

An irony of our polynomial algorithm, and even some of the foundational results, is that even though we set to solve an

Given

Appendix A
Primer on lattices and determinants

Generally we follow
[
23
,
8
,
26
]
as reference texts on lattices, inequalities, and equalities in linear algebra, respectively. More specifically, we use the following specific results in the paper.

Hermite normal form.

Let
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
be a matrix of full row rank.
Then
A
can be brought into
Hermite normal form
by means of
elementary unimodular column operations
. In particular, there exists an
n
-by-
n
unimodular matrix
U
such that
AU=(B~\mathbf{0})
, where
B
is a non-singular
m
-by-
m
matrix, and
\mathbf{0}
is an
m
-by-
(n-m)
matrix with zero entries. See (
[
10
]
, Section 1.5.2) or (
[
27
]
, Chapter 4) for more details.

Matrix GCD.

Denote by
\gcd(A)
the greatest common divisor (GCD) of all order-
m
minors of
A
. This quantity can be found as follows. First, bring
A
into Hermite normal form after applying elementary unimodular column operations, say we obtain
(B~\mathbf{0})
where
B
is a square matrix. Then
\gcd(A)=\gcd(B)=|\det(B)|
, where the first equality can be readily checked by the reader, and the second one follows immediately from definition.

Lattice “closure” and index.

Let
L
be an
m
-dimensional lattice contained in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, and let
\overline{L}:=\aff(L)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
Clearly,
\overline{L}
is a lattice containing
L
. Observe that within the affine hull of
L
, and confined to the integers,
\overline{L}
is the maximal lattice containing
L
. It is known that
\overline{L}
can be partitioned into a number of integral linear translations of
L
; this number is denoted as the
index

[\overline{L}:L]\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
. Thus,
L=\overline{L}
if and only if
[\overline{L}:L]=1
.

Lattice determinant and orthogonal complement.

The
determinant of
L
, denoted
\det(L)
, is the
m
-dimensional volume of its fundamental parallelepiped. The inverse of
\det(L)
may be used to measure the “density” of
L
: the smaller the determinant, the larger the density. It is known that
\det(L)=[\overline{L}:L]\det(\overline{L})
(see
[
23
]
, Proposition 1.1.5 (4)). The
orthogonal complement
of
L
is the lattice
L^{\perp}:=\left\{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:y^{\top}x=0~~\forall~x\in L\right\}
. It can be readily checked that
L^{\perp}
has index
1
, that is,
L^{\perp}=\overline{L^{\perp}}
. It is known that
\det(L^{\perp})=\det(\overline{L})
(see
[
23
]
, Proposition 1.9.8).

Lattice formulas.

Let
C\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times m}
be a matrix with
m
linearly independent columns, and consider the lattice
L:=\left\{Cy:y\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Let us find
\det(L),\overline{L},[\overline{L}:L]
and
L^{\perp}
in terms of
C
. First, the fundamental parallelepiped of
L
is
\left\{C\lambda:\mathbf{0}\leq\lambda<\mathbf{1}\right\}
, whose
m
-dimensional volume is
\det(L)
. Subsequently, it can be readily checked that
\det(L)^{2}
is the Gram determinant of the columns of
C
, that is,
\det(L)=\sqrt{\det(C^{\top}C)}
.
It can be readily seen that
\overline{L}=\{Cy:y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. By turning
C^{\top}
into Hermite normal form after applying elementary unimodular column operations, it can be shown that
[\overline{L}:L]=\gcd(C)
(see
[
23
]
, Proposition 1.1.5 (4)). Finally, observe that
L^{\perp}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:C^{\top}x=\mathbf{0}\right\}
. By combining the equalities provided thus far, we see that
\det(L^{\perp})=\sqrt{\det(C^{\top}C)}/\gcd(C)
.

Lattice-free sets.

Observe that
P:=\{C\lambda:-\mathbf{1}<\lambda<\mathbf{1}\}
is
lattice-free
, that is,
P
contains no nonzero vector from the lattice
L
.
The set
P
is the union of
2^{m}
reflections of the fundamental parallelepiped of
L
, whose relative interiors are pairwise disjoint, so
\vol_{m}(P)=2^{m}\det(L)
. Observe further that
P
is a convex set that is symmetric about the origin. An important result of Minkowski from the Geometry of Numbers states that among all such sets which are lattice-free,
P
reaches the maximum
m
-dimensional volume.

Minkowski’s First Theorem

(see
[
23
]
, Theorem 2.7.1)
.

Let
L\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
be an
m
-dimensional lattice. Let
C
be a convex set that is symmetric about the origin. If
C\cap L=\{\mathbf{0}\}
, then
\vol_{m}(C)\leq 2^{m}\det(L)
.

Hadamard’s inequality.

Let
B\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times m}
be a matrix with columns
b^{1},\ldots,b^{m}
. Observe that
|\det(B)|
is the volume of the parallelepiped with sides
b^{1},\ldots,b^{m}
, implying in turn the well-known
Hadamard inequality
, stating that
|\det(B)|\leq\prod_{i=1}^{m}\|b^{i}\|_{2}
(
[
18
]
, see
[
8
]
, Chapter 2, §11). Subsequently, since
\|b^{i}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{m}\|b^{i}\|_{\infty}
for each
i\in[m]
, it follows that
|\det(B)|^{1/m}\leq\sqrt{m}\|B\|_{\infty}.

Minors of the adjugate matrix.

Let
U
be an
n
-by-
n
matrix. The
adjugate of
U
, denoted
\mathrm{adj}(U)
, is the
n
-by-
n
matrix such that
U\mathrm{adj}(U)=\det(U)I
. It is well-known that for all
i,j\in[n]
,
\mathrm{adj}(U)_{ji}
is a cofactor of
U
, namely, it is
(-1)^{i+j}
times the determinant of the submatrix of
U
obtained after removing row
i
and column
j
. What is less known is an extension of this known attributed to Jacobi, which expresses every minor of
\mathrm{adj}(U)
as a cofactor of the original matrix
U
.

Jacobi’s Theorem

(see
[
26
]
, Theorem 2.5.2)
.

Let
U
be an
n
-by-
n
matrix, and
m\in[n-1]
. For row labels
I\subseteq[n]
and column labels
J\subseteq[n]
of
U
such that
|I|=|J|=m
, we have

\det(\mathrm{adj}(U)_{J\times I})=\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\det(U_{\overline{I}\times\overline{J}})\det(U)^{m-1}.

where
\sigma:[n]\to[n]
is the permutation defined as
\sigma(i_{k})=j_{k}
for all
k\in[n]
, for the following labelling of the rows and columns of
U
: the rows
/
columns of
U_{I\times J}
are labelled
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\}/\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}\}
from top to bottom
/
left to right, and the rows
/
columns of
U_{\overline{I}\times\overline{J}}
are labelled
\{i_{m+1},\ldots,i_{n}\}/\{j_{m+1},\ldots,j_{n}\}
from top to bottom
/
left to right.
