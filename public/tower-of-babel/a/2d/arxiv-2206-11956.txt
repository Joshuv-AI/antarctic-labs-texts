Word maps with constants on symmetric groups
Jakob Schneider, Andreas Thom

Abstract
. We study word maps with constants on symmetric groups. Even though there are non-trivial mixed identities of bounded length that are valid for all symmetric groups, we show that no such identities can hold in the limit in a metric sense. Moreover, we prove that word maps with constants and non-trivial content, that are short enough, have an image of positive diameter, measured in the normalized Hamming metric, which is bounded from below in terms of the word length. Finally, we also show that every self-map G → G G\to G on a finite non-abelian simple group is actually a word map with constants from G G .

on every group

and we set

to be the image of

is called an

for all choices of the

In this article we study word maps with constants. A word with constants in
G
is an element of the free product
\mathbf{F}_{r}\ast G
. We get an associated map
G^{n}\to G
in a similar way as before by replacing the variables with elements from
G
. The word is called a

or a

w(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})=1_{G}
for all choices of the

i=1,\ldots,r

Following
[
16

27

, for

\varepsilon(w)\in\mathbf{F}_{r}
, which replaces all constants by the neutral element. We call
\varepsilon(w)\in\mathbf{F}_{r}

of

. It has been observed in different circumstances
[
16
,
27
]
that word maps with constants which have a non-trivial content, tend to have a large image. We prove a corresponding result for symmetric groups with respect to the normalized Hamming metric. For
\sigma\in S_{n}
, we write

\fix(\sigma)\coloneqq\{x\,|\,x.\sigma=x\}

for the set of
fixed points
of
\sigma
,

\supp(\sigma)\coloneqq\{x\,|\,x.\sigma\neq x\}

for its
support
, and

\lvert\sigma\rvert\coloneqq\left\lvert\supp(\sigma)\right\rvert

for its (unnormalized)

. The

on

is then defined as
\lvert\sigma\rvert_{\rm H}\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\lvert\sigma\rvert
. Also, for
\sigma,\tau\in S_{n}
let
d(\sigma,\tau)\coloneqq\lvert\sigma^{-1}\tau\rvert
be their

and
d_{\rm H}(\sigma,\tau)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\lvert\sigma^{-1}\tau\rvert
be their
normalized Hamming distance
. For
S\subseteq S_{n}
write

\diam(S)\coloneqq\max_{\sigma,\tau\in S}{d(\sigma,\tau)}

for the
diameter
of the set
S
.

We say that
w\in\mathbf{F}_{r}\ast G
is
strong
if, when written in normal form, removing the constants does not lead to any cancellation among the variables – in particular, if
w
is strong, then either the content is non-trivial or the word was a constant from
G
.

There are some obvious mixed identities for
S_{n}
. E.g. take
\tau
a transposition, then
[x,\tau]^{6}
is such an identity, since
[g,\tau]=\tau^{-g}\tau
is the product of two transpositions and so either a
3
-cycle, the product of two disjoint transpositions, or trivial, where
g\in S_{n}
is arbitrary. We notice here that the word with constants
[x,\tau]^{6}
has a trivial content. It is one consequence of our main result, Theorem
1
, that this must be the case for any mixed identity on
S_{n}
of
small
length. However, there are long words with non-trivial content that are an identity for
S_{n}
, e.g. take
x^{n!}
. We show that a non-trivial word map with constants coming from a short word can have a small image (i.e. with small diameter) only if there are constants involved with small support.

Fix a group
G
of possible constants. Every word
w\in\mathbf{F}_{r}\ast G
can be written uniquely in the form

w=c_{0}x_{i(1)}^{\varepsilon(1)}c_{1}\cdots c_{l-1}x_{i(l)}^{\varepsilon(l)}c_{l}\in\mathbf{F}_{r}\ast G,

where
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
are the free generators of
\mathbf{F}_{r}
,
\varepsilon(j)=\pm 1
,
i(j)\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
(
j=1,\ldots,l
), and
c_{j}\in G
(
j=0,\ldots,l
) are such that there is no cancellation, i.e.
x_{i(j)}^{\varepsilon(j)}=x_{i(j+1)}^{-\varepsilon(j+1)}
implies that
c_{j}\neq 1_{G}
for
j=1,\ldots,l-1
. Then, we set
\ell(w)=l
and call it the
length
of
w
. Note that
\ell
is just the word length where we give elements from
G
length zero.

Our main result is the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
w\in\mathbf{F}_{r}\ast S_{n}
and consider the associated word map with constants
w\colon S_{n}^{r}\to S_{n}
. Then the following holds:

(i)

If
w
has non-trivial content, then

\frac{\diam(w(S_{n}^{r}))+1}{n}\geq\frac{1}{2}\exp(-\log(5\ell(w))\ell(w)/2).

(ii)

If
w
is strong and
w\notin S_{n}
, then

\frac{\diam(w(S_{n}^{r}))+1}{n}\geq\frac{1}{2\ell(w)}.

More precisely, if
w\notin S_{n}
is arbitrary and the inequality in
(ii)
is violated, then a constant of size at most
2(\diam(w(S_{n}^{r}))+1)\ell(w)
can be found, so that the removal of that constant leads to cancellation of variables.

Corollary 1
.

Assume that
w\in\mathbf{F}_{r}\ast S_{n}
(
n\geq 2
) is strong and induces a constant map
S_{n}^{r}\to S_{n}
. Then either
w\in S_{n}
or
\ell(w)\geq\frac{n}{2}
.

The shortest known strong mixed identities are the laws constructed in
[
17
]
, they are of length
\exp(C\log(n)^{4}\log\log(n)).
It is an interesting open problem to close this gap.

The second part of Theorem
1
implies the following corollary which is of independent interest.

Corollary 2
.

A metric ultraproduct of symmetric groups
S_{n}
equipped with the normalized Hamming metric, with
n
tending to infinity along the chosen ultrafilter, does not satisfy any non-trivial mixed identity.

For completeness, let us briefly recall the notion of the
metric ultraproduct
of a family of metric groups
(G_{n},d_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with respect to an
ultrafilter

\mathcal{U}
on
\mathbb{N}
. Here
d_{n}
is a bi-invariant metric on
G_{n}
(
n\in\mathbb{N}
).
It is defined as the quotient
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}{G_{n}}\big/N_{\mathcal{U}}
, where
N_{\mathcal{U}}\coloneqq\{(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\,|\,d_{n}(g_{n},1_{G_{n}})\to_{\mathcal{U}}0\}
is the normal subgroup of null sequences, and is itself a metric group when equipped with the limit metric.

For more on metric ultraproducts of symmetric groups, see
[
5
,
28
,
26
]
. Note that the previous corollary is in contrast to the usual ultraproduct of symmetric groups, which satisfies a mixed identity
[x,\tau]^{6}
, where
\tau
is an ultraproduct of transpositions as discussed above.

Despite the restrictions on images of word maps with constants given by the length of the words and the structure of the constants involved, we prove in the Appendix that every self-map of a non-abelian simple group is a word map with constants.

1.
Basic definitions

In this short section, we make some basic definitions, which are needed later. Let
G
be a group, the group of possible constants. As above, write the fixed word
w\in\mathbf{F}_{r}\ast G
uniquely in the form

w=c_{0}x_{i(1)}^{\varepsilon(1)}c_{1}\cdots c_{l-1}x_{i(l)}^{\varepsilon(l)}c_{l}\in\mathbf{F}_{r}\ast G,

where
\varepsilon(j)=\pm 1
,
i(j)\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
(
j=1,\ldots,l
), and
c_{j}\in G
(
j=0,\ldots,l
) are such that there is no cancellation.

We define the sets of indices
J_{0}(w),J_{+}(w),J_{-}(w)\subseteq\{1,\ldots,l-1\}
by

J_{0}(w)\coloneqq\{j\,|\,i(j)\neq i(j+1)\},

J_{+}(w)\coloneqq\{j\,|\,i(j)=i(j+1)\mbox{ and }\varepsilon(j)=\varepsilon(j+1)\},

and

J_{-}(w)\coloneqq\{j\,|\,i(j)=i(j+1)\mbox{ and }\varepsilon(j)=-\varepsilon(j+1)\}.

Note that
J_{0}(w),J_{+}(w),J_{-}(w)
partition the set
\{1,\ldots,l-1\}
.
We call
J_{-}(w)
the set of
critical indices
of
w
since the removal of a constant
c_{j}
for
j\in J_{-}(w)
leads to cancellation among the variables. The constant
c_{j}
is then called a
critical constant
. We call
v
an
elementary reduction
of
w\in\mathbf{F}_{r}\ast G
, if it is obtained from
w
by deleting a critical constant and reducing the outcome.

Now, we focus on word maps with constants in symmetric groups. Keep the notation from above and set
G\coloneqq S_{n}
. For simplicity, we assume that
c_{0}=1_{G}=\id
. Keep the definition of the sets
J_{-}(w)
,
J_{+}(w)
, and
J_{0}(w)
. Define

w_{j}\coloneqq x_{i(1)}^{\varepsilon(1)}c_{1}\cdots x_{i(j)}^{\varepsilon(j)}c_{j}

to be the
j
th prefix of
w
(
j=0,\ldots,l
). Note that
w_{l}=w
by construction. For a reduced word
v\in\mathbf{F}_{r}\ast S_{n}
, write
\lvert v\rvert_{i}
for its
i
-length
, i.e. the number of occurrences of
x_{i}
and
x_{i}^{-1}
in its reduced representation (
i=1,\ldots,r
). Let
w(S_{n}^{r})\subseteq S_{n}
denote the
image
of the
word map

w\colon S_{n}^{r}\to S_{n}
induced by
w
.

2.
Proof of the main result

We are now ready to state the main technical lemma:

Lemma 1
.

In this setting, assume that
l>0
and let
d\geq 1
be an integer such that the following hold:

(i)

n\geq(d-1)(\lvert w\rvert_{i(j)}+\lvert w\rvert_{i(j+1)})+\lvert w_{j}\rvert_{i(j)}+\lvert w_{j+1}\rvert_{i(j+1)}-1
for all
j\in J_{0}(w)
;

(ii)

n\geq 2((d-1)\lvert w\rvert_{i(j)}+\lvert w_{j+1}\rvert_{i(j)}-1)
for

;

(iii)

\lvert c_{j}\rvert\geq 2((d-1)\lvert w\rvert_{i(j)}+\lvert w_{j+1}\rvert_{i(j)})-3

j\in J_{-}(w)
;

(iv)

n\geq d\lvert w\rvert_{i(l)}+1
.

Then, we have that
\diam(w(S_{n}^{r}))\geq d
.

\alpha_{+1},\alpha_{-1}\colon E(G)\to V(G)

\alpha_{+1}(e)

and
\alpha_{-1}(e)

target
; also for

\alpha_{\varepsilon}(e)
the
\varepsilon
-source
and
\alpha_{-\varepsilon}(e)
the
\varepsilon
-target
of

. Let
S
be a set of

. By an edge-labeling of

by

we mean a mapping
\lambda\colon E(G)\to S
. If
G
is equipped with such a labeling, we call it a
directed
S
-edge-labeled graph
. In this case, an edge
e
of
G
which is labeled by

is called an

-arrow
. In this situation, if we exhibit a particular vertex

of
G
, we call

a

S
-edge-labeled graph
.

We will also make use of the following notation for partially defined maps: If

and

are such maps, then their
composition

is the partially defined map given by

\alpha_{-\varepsilon(j)}(e_{k}^{j}).c_{j}\neq\alpha_{-\varepsilon(j)}(e_{k}^{j})
rules out the

fixed points of

. In the worst case, the condition in (b) is satisfied, and (b) rules out

further vertices. Hence we have at least

\displaystyle n-(2((k-1)\lvert w\rvert_{i(j)}+\lvert w_{j+1}\rvert_{i(j)}-2)+n-\lvert c_{j}\rvert+d-k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lvert c_{j}\rvert-((k-1)(2\lvert w\rvert_{i(j)}-1)+2\lvert w_{j+1}\rvert_{i(j)}-5+d)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\lvert c_{j}\rvert-((d-1)(2\lvert w\rvert_{i(j)}-1)+2\lvert w_{j+1}\rvert_{i(j)}-5+d)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lvert c_{j}\rvert-(2((d-1)\lvert w\rvert_{i(j)}+\lvert w_{j+1}\rvert_{i(j)})-4)

choices for
\alpha_{-\varepsilon_{j}}(e_{k}^{j})
, which is positive by assumption (iii) in the lemma.

Case (iv):
j=l
.
Then (c) is irrelevant, (b) can rule out at most

\alpha_{-\varepsilon_{l}}(e_{k}^{l})
if (
\ast

vertices remaining. This is a positive number by assumption (iv) of the lemma. Hence the proof is complete.
∎

Remark 1
.

The condition on
\diam(w(S_{n}^{r}))
is almost sharp: Take

(1\cdots c)
and set

. Then
\diam(w(S_{n}^{r}))=\min\{2c,n\}
and the bound in the lemma gives
\diam(w(S_{n}^{r}))\geq\left\lfloor c/4\right\rfloor
.

We set

\lvert w\rvert_{\infty}\coloneqq\max_{i\in\{1,\ldots r\}}{\lvert w\rvert_{i}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\lvert w\rvert_{\rm crit}\coloneqq\min\left\{n,\min_{j\in J_{-}(w)}\lvert c_{j}\rvert\right\}.

The four assumptions in Lemma
1
can obviously by strengthened to the single inequality
\lvert w\rvert_{\rm crit}\geq 2d\lvert w\rvert_{\infty}
. It follows that

\diam(w(S_{n}^{r}))\geq\left\lfloor\frac{\lvert w\rvert_{\rm crit}}{2\lvert w\rvert_{\infty}}\right\rfloor>\frac{\lvert w\rvert_{\rm crit}}{2\lvert w\rvert_{\infty}}-1

for
n\geq 2
.
Note that
\lvert w\rvert_{\infty}
is obviously bounded by the length
\ell(w)
. We will use the above inequality in the form

\lvert w\rvert_{\rm crit}\leq 2\cdot(\diam(w(S_{n}^{r}))+1)\cdot\ell(w).

We interpret this inequality as follows: If the diameter of the word image is small, then either there is a small critical constant or the word length is large.

Remark 2
.

We suppose that a similar lemma can be proved for families like
\PSL_{n}(p)
for a fixed prime
p
and
n
growing, leading to results comparable to our main theorem. However, we note that for example for the family
\PSL_{2}(q)
for

growing, the length of the shortest mixed identity grows linearly in

. Note that in this case, the fact that the length of the shortest mixed identity must tend to infinity is already a consequence of the results in
[

]
. This will be the subject of further work.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

The bound in (ii) directly follows from the inequality
\lvert w\rvert_{\rm crit}\leq 2(\diam(w(S_{n}^{r}))+1)\ell(w)
, since when
w
is strong we have
\lvert w\rvert_{\rm crit}=n
as there is no critical constant. The assertion about existence of small critical constants is also an immediate consequence of the inequality above.

In order to prove (i) we study the effect of removal of critical constants in more detail.
Let
v
be an elementary reduction of
w\in\mathbf{F}_{r}\ast G
, i.e. it is obtained from
w
by deleting a critical constant and reducing the outcome.
Note that removing the smallest critical constant
c
does not change the diameter of the word image by more than
2\lvert c\rvert=2\lvert w\rvert_{\rm crit}
, so that given a small diameter, we can try to iterate elementary reductions. Let
w
have non-trivial content and let
w=w_{0},\ldots,w_{m}
be a chain of words, where
w_{i}
is an elementary reduction of
w_{i-1}
(
i\geq 1
) by a smallest critical constant, so that there is no such reduction for
w_{m}
, i.e.
w_{m}
is strong. Note that
w_{i}
no longer denotes the
i
th prefix of
w
. Then
\ell(w_{i})\leq\ell(w_{i-1})-2\leq\ell(w)-2i
. We have

\lvert w_{i-1}\rvert_{\rm crit}\leq 2\ell(w_{i-1})(\diam(w_{i-1}(S_{n}^{r}))+1)

and so

\displaystyle\diam(w_{i}(S_{n}^{r}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\diam(w_{i-1}(S_{n}^{r}))+2\lvert w_{i-1}\rvert_{\rm crit}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\diam(w_{i-1}(S_{n}^{r}))+4\ell(w_{i-1})(\diam(w_{i-1}(S_{n}^{r}))+1).

It follows that

\diam(w_{i}(S_{n}^{r}))+1\leq(1+4\ell(w_{i-1}))(\diam(w_{i-1}(S_{n}^{r}))+1).

Hence, we obtain

\diam(w_{i}(S_{n}^{r}))+1\leq(1+4\ell(w))^{i}(\diam(w(S_{n}^{r}))+1)

by induction on

, since
\ell(w_{i})\leq\ell(w)
. By assumption,
w_{m}
is not a constant from
G=S_{n}
. Then by the above inequality

\displaystyle n=\lvert w_{m}\rvert_{\rm crit}

\displaystyle\leq 2(\diam(w_{m}(S_{n}^{r}))+1)\ell(w_{m})

\displaystyle\leq 2(1+4\ell(w))^{m}(\diam(w(S_{n}^{r}))+1)\ell(w)

\displaystyle\leq 2(1+4\ell(w))^{\left\lfloor\ell(w)/2\right\rfloor}\ell(w)(\diam(w(S_{n}^{r}))+1)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp(\log(5\ell(w))\ell(w)/2)(\diam(w(S_{n}^{r}))+1)

as

and

such that

as

, i.e.

induces a constant map on the metric ultraproduct of the

Proof of Corollary

.

By the above
2\lvert w\rvert_{\infty}(\diam(w_{n}(S_{n}^{r}))+1)\geq\lvert w\rvert_{\mathrm{c}}
, so that

Appendix

It is well-known that every map
\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
is realized by a polynomial from
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
of degree less than
q
– in fact this is a characterization of fields among finite rings, see
[
15
]
. We prove the analogous result for non-abelian simple groups. We denote by the
covering number

\cn(G)
of
G
the minimal
m
, such that
C^{\ast m}=G
for all non-trivial conjugacy classes
C
. Various bounds on
\cn(G)
for finite simple groups
G
can be found in the literature, see for example the seminal work of Liebeck-Shalev
[
23
]
and the references therein.

Theorem 2
.

A non-abelian finite group is simple if and only if every map
G\to G
is a word map with constants. Every such map can be represented by a word of length at most
O(\left\lvert G\right\rvert^{3}\cn(G))
.

Remark 3
.

An abelian group
G
has the above property (i.e. each map
G\to G
comes from a word with constants) if and only if
\left\lvert G\right\rvert\leq 2
.

Remark 4
.

In the theorem, the term
\cn(G)
can also be replaced by the
covering diameter

\cd(G)
of
G
, which is the minimal number
m
such that
(\{1_{G}\}\cup C\cup C^{-1})^{\ast m}=G
for all non-trivial conjugacy classes
C
of
G
. Clearly,
\cd(G)\leq\cn(G)
.

Proof.

The evaluation map
\ev\colon\mathbf{F}_{1}\ast G=\langle x\rangle\ast G\to G^{G}
is a homomorpism. Also, if
\pi_{\{g,h\}}\colon G^{G}\to G^{\{g,h\}}
is the projection onto
g
and
h
, then
\pi_{\{g,h\}}\circ\ev
is surjective: Indeed, by Goursat’s lemma and since
G
is assumed to be simple, it is enough to show that its image is not the graph of an automorphism. However, this cannot be the case, since the constants
G
map to the diagonal subgroup, and
x
maps to a non-diagonal subgroup. This implies that
\ev
must be surjective too.

By using iterated commutators, one can give a more explicit construction: We prove that for any subset
S\subseteq G
of size
m\leq 2^{e}
and an element
g\in G\setminus S
, there is a commutator word
w_{g,S}\in\mathbf{F}_{1}\ast G
of length
4^{e}
such that the induced map
w_{g,S}\colon G\to G
satisfies
w_{g,S}(s)=1_{G}
for all
s\in S
and
w_{g,S}(g)\neq 1_{G}
.

Indeed, this is true when
e=0
and
S=\{s\}
: Simply take
w_{g,\{s\}}=xs^{-1}
. For
e\geq 1
, write
S=S_{1}\cup S_{2}
as a union of the subsets
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
such that
\left\lvert S_{1}\right\rvert,\left\lvert S_{2}\right\rvert\leq 2^{e-1}
. Then set
w_{g,S}\coloneqq[w_{g,S_{1}}^{a},w_{g,S_{2}}^{b}]
where
a,b\in G
are chosen such that
[w_{g,S_{1}}(g)^{a},w_{g,S_{2}}(g)^{b}]\neq 1_{G}
. This is possible since
(w_{g,S_{2}}(g))^{G}
generates the whole
G
by simplicity as
w_{g,S_{2}}(g)\neq 1_{G}
, whereas
\mathbf{C}_{G}(w_{g,S_{1}}(g))
is a proper subgroup. Hence

w_{g,S_{2}}^{G}(g)\not\subseteq\mathbf{C}_{G}(w_{g,S_{1}}(g)).

We have that
\ell(w_{g,S})\leq 2(\ell(w_{g,S_{1}})+\ell(w_{g,S_{1}}))\leq 4^{e}
.

Then setting
e\coloneqq\left\lceil\log_{2}(\left\lvert G\right\rvert-1)\right\rceil
, one can take
S=G\setminus\{g\}
, so that
w_{g,S}(s)=1_{G}
for
s\in S
and
w_{g,S}(g)\neq 1_{G}
as desired. Now we can multiply together conjugates of the map
w_{g,S}
to get a map
\delta_{g,h}\in\mathbf{F}_{1}\ast G
such that
\delta_{g,h}(s)=1_{G}
for
s\in S=G\setminus\{g\}
and
\delta_{g,h}(g)=h
for a chosen
h\in G
. Then
w=\prod_{g\in G}{\delta_{g,f(g)}}=f
as maps
w,f\colon G\to G
where
f
is arbitrary. So
f
is a word map. Here

\ell(w)\leq\left\lvert G\right\rvert 4^{\left\lceil\log_{2}(\left\lvert G\right\rvert-1)\right\rceil}\cn(G)\leq 4\left\lvert G\right\rvert(\left\lvert G\right\rvert-1)^{2}\cn(G)<4\left\lvert G\right\rvert^{3}\cn(G).

Conversely, if any map
f\colon G\to G
is a word map, then for
g\neq 1_{G}
, the map
\delta_{g,h}\colon G\to G
given by

\delta_{g,h}(x)=\begin{cases}1_{G}&\text{if $x\neq g$}\\
h&\text{if $x=g$}\end{cases}

is induced by some word
w\in\mathbf{F}_{1}\ast G
. Since
w(1_{G})=\delta_{g,h}(1_{G})=1_{G}
, we have that
w(g)=\delta_{g,h}(g)=h\in\langle\!\langle g\rangle\!\rangle
. But since
h
was arbitrary, we have
\langle\!\langle g\rangle\!\rangle=G
, so that
G
is simple, since
g\neq 1_{G}
was arbitrary as well.
∎

\cn(A_{n})=\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor
for

Corollary 3
.

For
n\geq 5
, every map
A_{n}\to A_{n}
can be represented by a word map with constants of length
O((n!)^{3}n)
.

Remark 5
.

There are at most
3^{l}\left\lvert G\right\rvert^{l+1}
words with constants of length at most
l
in
\mathbf{F}_{1}\ast G=\langle x\rangle\ast G
. So in order that all self-maps
G\to G
can be represented as word maps coming from words of length at most
l
, we must have

\left\lvert G\right\rvert^{\left\lvert G\right\rvert}\leq 3^{l}\left\lvert G\right\rvert^{l+1}.

This implies a linear lower bound on the minimal length of words with constants that can represent all possible maps.

Remark 6
.

It was pointed out to us by Ben Steinberg and Anton Klyachko, that the qualitative aspect of Theorem
2
is a well-known result
[
25
]
. Similar but weaker bounds have been obtained in
[
13
]
. Using methods similar to the ones in the proof of Theorem
2
one can show that every map
G^{r}\to G
arises as a word map with constants of length
O(\left\lvert G\right\rvert^{r+2}r^{2}\cn(G))
. Indeed, one can use the maps of length at most

to get maps that see just one coordinate of
G^{r}
. Taking
\left\lceil\log_{2}(r)\right\rceil
commutators of those maps, one gets a map that sees just one element of
G^{r}
. This then has length
O(r^{2}\left\lvert G\right\rvert^{2})
. Hence, we get a bound

O(\left\lvert G\right\rvert^{r+2}r^{2}\cn(G)).

This is exactly the bound in
[
13
]
for
A_{n}
, but the authors do not write it out for other non-abelian simple groups.
