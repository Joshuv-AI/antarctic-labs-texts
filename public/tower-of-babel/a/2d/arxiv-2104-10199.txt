Abstract
. A quandle is an algebraic structure satisfying three axioms: idempotency, right-invertibility and right self-distributivity. In quandles, right translations are permutations. The profile of a quandle is the list of cycle structures, one per right translation in the quandle. In this note we prove that if, for each cycle structure in the profile of a quandle, no two cycle lengths are equal, then the quandle is latin—this is the sufficient condition mentioned in the title.

1.
Introduction

A quandle is an algebraic structure satisfying three axioms: idempotency, right-invertibility, and right self-distributivity (
[
5
]
,
[
10
]
). These three axioms constitute an algebraic counterpart to the Reidemeister moves of knot theory (
[
7
]
) and, thus, quandles give rise to efficient ways of distinguishing knots (e.g., via counting colorings,
[
2
]
,
[
1
]
,
[
3
]
). The algebraic and combinatorial theory of quandles is of interest in its own right.

The second quandle axiom implies that right translations of quandles are permutations. The profile of a quandle (
[
9
]
) is the list of cycle structures, one cycle structure per right translation in the quandle. In a connected quandle, any two right translations have the same cycle structure and we thus identify its profile with this cycle structure.

In this note we prove that if no cycle structure in the profile of a finite quandle has repeats i.e., if, for each cycle structure in the profile, no two cycle lengths are equal, then the quandle is latin (Theorem
3.4
)—this is the sufficient condition mentioned in the title. More generally, if a finite quandle contains a right translation,
R_{i_{0}}
, with cycles of distinct lengths and if all right translations have a unique fixed point, then the corresponding left translation,
L_{i_{0}}
, is also a permutation and its cycles refine the cycles of
R_{i_{0}}
(Proposition
3.3
).

The organization of this article is as follows. In Section
2
the background material is introduced, including definitions and facts from the literature. The main results are stated and proved in Section
3
. In Section
4
we collect a few questions for further research. All connected quandles of orders up to
47
are included in the
GAP

[
4
]
package
RIG

[
12
]
. Let us call such connected quandles
small connected quandles
. The appendix contains:

•

the list of small connected quandles whose profiles do not contain repeats (these are among the quandles covered by Theorem
3.4
),

•

the list of profiles with repeats for small latin quandles (these are among the profiles not covered by Theorem
3.4
for which the conclusion of the theorem holds),

•

the list of profiles with a single fixed point for small non-latin connected quandles (these are among the profiles that are not immediately detected as not belonging to latin quandles, cf. Lemma
2.6
).

2.
Background material and results

The algebraic structure known as
quandle
, introduced independently in
[
5
]
and
[
10
]
, is defined as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X
be a set equipped with a binary operation denoted by
*
. The pair
(X,*)
is said to be a
\emph{quandle}
if, for each
i,j,k\in X
,

(i)

i*i=i
(idempotency);

(ii)

\exists!x\in X:x*j=i
(right-invertibility);

(iii)

(i*j)*k=(i*k)*(j*k)
(right self-distributivity).

To each finite quandle
(X,\ast)
of order
n
we associate a
quandle table
, which is the
n\times n
table whose element in row
i
and column
j
is
i\ast j
, for all
i,j\in X
. We add an extra
0
-th column (where we display the
i
’s) and an extra
0
-th row (where we display the
j
’s) to quandle tables to improve legibility.

Q_{6,2}

For an algebraic structure
(X,*)
and for
i\in X
, we let
R_{i}:X\rightarrow X,j\mapsto j*i
be the
right translation
by
i
in
(X,*)
and
L_{i}:X\rightarrow X,j\mapsto i*j
be the
left translation
by
i
in
(X,*)
. For example, the right translation by
1
in
Q_{6,2}
is
R_{1}=(1)(3)(2\,\,6\,\,4\,\,5)
. Note that axioms (ii) and (iii) in Definition
2.1
guarantee that each right translation in a quandle is an automorphism of that quandle.

The group
\langle R_{i}:i\in X\rangle
generated by the right translations of a quandle
(X,*)
is said to be the
right multiplication group
. A quandle
(X,*)
is
connected
if its right multiplication group acts transitively on
X
. A quandle
(X,*)
is
latin
if all its left translations are permutations of
X
. For instance,
Q_{6,2}
is connected but not latin.

For a permutation
f
on a finite set
X
, the
cycle structure
of
f
is the formal sequence
(1^{c_{1}},2^{c_{2}},3^{c_{3}},\dots)
such that
c_{m}
is the number of
m
-cycles in a decomposition of
f
into disjoint cycles. For convenience, we omit entries with
c_{i}=0
and we drop
c_{i}
whenever
c_{i}=1
. For example, the right translation by
1
in
Q_{6,2}
has cycle structure
(1^{2},4)
.

The
profile
of a quandle is the list of the cycle structures of its right translations.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(X,*)
be an algebraic structure and
f
an automorphism of
(X,*)
. Then
fR_{i}f^{-1}=R_{f(i)}
, for every
i\in X
.

Proof.

Indeed,
fR_{i}f^{-1}(j)=f(f^{-1}(j)*i)=f(f^{-1}(j))*f(i)=j*f(i)=R_{f(i)}(j)
, for every
i,j\in X
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

In a finite connected quandle
(X,*)
, all right translations have the same cycle structure.

Proof.

Since each right translation in a quandle is an automorphism, this is a straightforward consequence of Lemma
2.3
.
∎

We thus define the
profile

\mathrm{p}(X,*)
of a finite connected quandle
(X,*)
to be the cycle structure of any of its right translations. For instance,
Q_{6,2}
has profile
\mathrm{p}(Q_{6,2})=(1^{2},4)
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
(X,*)
be a latin quandle. Then
(X,*)
is connected.

Proof.

Indeed,
R_{L_{i}^{-1}(j)}(i)=i*L_{i}^{-1}(j)=L_{i}(L_{i}^{-1}(j))=j
, for every
i,j\in X
.
∎

Though each latin quandle is connected, not every connected quandle is latin. We would like to know under which assumptions on
\mathrm{p}(X,*)
a connected quandle

(X,*)
is latin. We start with an important observation.

Lemma 2.6
.

If
(X,*)
is a latin quandle, then each of its right translations has a unique fixed point.

Proof.

Let
i\in X
and notice that
R_{i}(i)=i
. Suppose that there is
i\neq j\in X
such that
R_{i}(j)=j
. Then
L_{j}(i)=j*i=R_{i}(j)=j
and
L_{j}(j)=j*j=j
show that
L_{j}
is not a permutation. Hence
(X,*)
is not latin.
∎

The simple necessary condition of Lemma
2.6
goes a long way towards identifying small connected quandles that are latin. Among the
791
small connected quandles, there are
547
quandles that are latin and only
4
quandles that are not latin and whose right translations have a unique fixed point. These
4
quandles all happen to be of order
28
and their profile is either
(1,3^{9})
or
(1,3,6^{4})
. Importantly, there are latin quandles of order
28
with profiles
(1,3^{9})
and
(1,3,6^{4})
. Hence, the profile alone cannot serve as both a sufficient and a necessary condition for identifying latin quandles among connected quandles.

Theorem
3.4
below is a sufficient condition on the profile for a quandle to be latin.

3.
Main results

We start with the following results.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f
be a permutation with a cycle
C=(k,k+1,\dots,k+\ell)
. If
i,j\in C
then
f^{j-i}(i)=j
.

Proof.

This is clear when
j\geq i
. If
j<i
then
f^{i-j}(j)=i
and therefore
j=f^{-(i-j)}(i)
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(X,*)
be an algebraic structure and
f
an automorphism of
(X,*)
. The cycle decomposition of
f
gives rise to a partition
{\mathcal{P}}
of
X
(whose parts are the disjoint cycles of f). Suppose
x,y,z\in X
are such that
x*y=z
,
u\in C_{u}\in{\mathcal{P}}
, where
u\in\{x,y,z\}
. Then
\ell_{z}
divides
\mathrm{lcm}(\ell_{x},\ell_{y})
, where
\ell_{u}=\lvert C_{u}\rvert
.

Proof.

If
x*y=z
then
f(x)*f(y)=f(x*y)=f(z)
and, by induction,
f^{n}(x)*f^{n}(y)=f^{n}(z)
, for every
n\geq 0
. Let
m=\mathrm{lcm}(\ell_{x},\ell_{y})
. Then
f^{m}(x)=x
and
f^{m}(y)=y
, so
f^{m}(z)=f^{m}(x)*f^{m}(y)=x*y=z
.
∎

See
[
11
, Lemma 3.6]
for a version of Lemma
3.2
for autotopisms of latin squares.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
(X,*)
be a finite quandle with
1\in X
. Suppose that the cycles of the right translation
R_{1}
have distinct lengths and that every right translation has a unique fixed point. Then the left translation
L_{1}
is a permutation of
X
and the elements of every cycle of
L_{1}
are contained in some cycle of
R_{1}
.

Proof.

Let
X=\{1,\dots,n\}
. Suppose that
R_{1}
has
c
cycles of lengths
1=\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}<\cdots<\ell_{c}
. Let
a_{0}=0
and
a_{i}=a_{i-1}+\ell_{i}
. By renaming the elements of
X
, we can assume without loss of generality that
R_{1}=(a_{0}+1,\dots,a_{1})(a_{1}+1,\dots,a_{2})\cdots(a_{c-1}+1,\dots,a_{c})
. For
1\leq t\leq c
, let
C_{t}=\{a_{t-1}+1,\dots,a_{t}\}
be the underlying set of the
t
-th cycle of
R_{1}
.

We will first prove that
1*C_{t}=\{1*x:x\in C_{t}\}\subseteq C_{t}
, for every
t
. For
t=1
, we have that
C_{1}=\{1\}
and therefore
1*C_{1}=\{1*1\}=\{1\}\subseteq C_{1}
. Now, let
t>1
and suppose that
1*C_{s}\subseteq C_{s}
holds for all
s<t\leq c
. We will prove that
1*C_{t}\subseteq C_{t}
. Let
x\in C_{t}
and
y\in C_{u}
be such that
1*x=y
. By Lemma
3.2
,
\ell_{u}
divides
\mathrm{lcm}(\ell_{1},\ell_{t})=\mathrm{lcm}(1,\ell_{t})=\ell_{t}
, so
u\in\{1,\dots,t\}
. If
u=t
, we are done. Hence, suppose that
u\in\{1,\dots,t-1\}
. We will show that
z=R_{1}^{\ell_{u}}(x)
is a fixed point of
R_{x}
. Noting that
z\neq x
(as
x\in C_{t}
and
\lvert C_{t}\rvert=\ell_{t}>\ell_{u}
), this will conflict with
x
being the unique fixed point of
R_{x}
.

Note that
R_{y}(1)\in C_{u}
by the induction assumption. Let
k=R_{y}(1)-y
and consider the automorphism

f=R_{y}^{-1}R_{1}^{k}R_{x}.

Since
R_{y}(1)\in C_{u}
and
y\in C_{u}
, Lemma
3.1
yields
R_{1}^{k}(y)=R_{y}(1)
and therefore

f(1)=R_{y}^{-1}R_{1}^{k}R_{x}(1)=R_{y}^{-1}R_{1}^{k}(y)=R_{y}^{-1}(R_{y}(1))=1.

Also note, using Lemma
2.3
, that
R_{1}^{\ell_{u}}R_{y}R_{1}^{-\ell_{u}}=R_{R_{1}^{\ell_{u}}(y)}=R_{y}
(since
y\in C_{u}
and
\lvert C_{u}\rvert=\ell_{u}
), so
R_{1}^{\ell_{u}}=R_{y}R_{1}^{\ell_{u}}R_{y}^{-1}
. Then we have

\displaystyle R_{x}^{-1}(z)

\displaystyle=R_{x}^{-1}R_{1}^{\ell_{u}}(x)=R_{x}^{-1}R_{1}^{\ell_{u}}R_{x}(x)

\displaystyle=R_{x}^{-1}R_{1}^{-k}R_{1}^{\ell_{u}}R_{1}^{k}R_{x}(x)

\displaystyle=R_{x}^{-1}R_{1}^{-k}R_{y}R_{1}^{\ell_{u}}R_{y}^{-1}R_{1}^{k}R_{x}(x)

\displaystyle=f^{-1}R_{1}^{\ell_{u}}f(x)=(f^{-1}R_{1}f)^{\ell_{u}}(x)=R_{f^{-1}(1)}^{\ell_{u}}(x)=R_{1}^{\ell_{u}}(x)=z.

We are now ready to prove that
L_{1}
is surjective, hence bijective. Let
x\in X
, say
x\in C_{t}=\{a_{t-1}+1,\dots,a_{t}\}
. By the previous paragraph, there is
y\in C_{t}
such that
1*a_{t}=y
. Since both
x
and
y
belong to
C_{t}
, there is
1\leq m\leq\ell_{t}
such that
x=R_{1}^{m}(y)
. Then we have
L_{1}(a_{t-1}+m)=1*(a_{t-1}+m)=R_{1}^{m}(1)*R_{1}^{m}(a_{t})=R_{1}^{m}(1*a_{t})=R_{1}^{m}(y)=x
.

Finally, since
1*C_{t}\subseteq C_{t}
for every underlying set
C_{t}
of a cycle of
R_{1}
, it follows that the elements of every cycle of
L_{1}
are contained in some cycle of
R_{1}
.
∎

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
(X,*)
be a finite quandle such that each right translation of
(X,*)
has cycles of distinct lengths. Then
(X,*)
is a latin quandle.

Proof.

Since each right translation has cycles of distinct lengths and it has a fixed point, it has precisely one fixed point. Given any right translation
R_{i}
of
(X,*)
, Proposition
3.3
then shows that the corresponding left translation
L_{i}
is a permutation of
X
. Hence
(X,*)
is a latin quandle.
∎

As an immediate corollary of Theorem
3.4
, we obtain:

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
(X,*)
be a finite connected quandle whose profile
\mathrm{p}(X,*)
has no repeats. Then
(X,*)
is a latin quandle.

4.
Further research

The connected quandles of order 28 demonstrate that we cannot identify latin quandles among connected quandles by their profile alone. Among the
791
small connected quandles, there are
547
latin quandles, of which
183
have cycles of distinct lengths and
364
do not. Can the sufficient condition of Theorem
3.4
be generalized to capture more latin quandles among connected quandles? Is there a criterion for characterizing latin quandles among connected quandles based on the profile and other combinatorial properties?

A conjecture of Hayashi
[
6
]
states that every right translation of a finite connected quandle has a regular cycle, that is, it is a permutation whose order is equal to the length of its longest cycle. Is there a connection between Hayashi’s conjecture and Theorem
3.4
? In particular, can Theorem
3.4
be generalized under the assumption that Hayashi’s conjecture is true?
