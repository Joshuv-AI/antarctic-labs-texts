Zihan Huang, Gaoming Wang, Zhao Yu

Abstract
We generalize the non-Gaussian parameter, which is utilized to characterize the distinction of dynamics between realistic and Gaussian Brownian diffusions, in k k -dimensional Euclidean space.

I
I. INTRODUCTION

It is commonly presumed that Fickian diffusion satisfies the Gaussian statistics. Namely, the stochastic displacements of the particles induced by the thermal jiggling follow a Gaussain (normal) distribution
Wang et al. 2012
. However, in recent years, numerous experimental and simulation results indicate that non-Gaussian diffusion is more prevalent than expected, in which mean-square displacement (MSD) is still proportional to time (Fickian), such as the diffusion of colloids on phospholipid fluid tubules
Wang et al. 2009
, and the heterogeneous dynamics of two-dimensional colloids in hexatic and solid phases
Kim et al. 2013
. The non-Gaussian features of these Fickian diffusions call for a general perspective and corresponding physical descriptions.

A straightforward but effective way to quantify the heterogeneity of the non-Gaussian diffusion, is to examine the ratio defined as
\langle r^{2n}(t)\rangle/{\langle r^{2}(t)\rangle}^{n}
, where
r(t)
represents the displacement at time
t
and
\langle\cdot\rangle
is the ensemble average. Through comparing the ratio of the realistic diffusion with that of the Gaussian one, the deviation from the Gaussian form that diffusion shows can be quantitatively identified. A non-Gaussian parameter
\alpha_{n,k}(t)
is thereby defined for this purpose, where
k
is the space dimensionality. In 3-dimensional (3D) space, this parameter is written as

\alpha_{n,3}(t)=c_{n,3}\frac{\langle r^{2n}(t)\rangle}{{\langle r^{2}(t)\rangle}^{n}}-1

(1)

where
c_{n,3}=3^{n}/(2n+1)!!
is the non-Gaussian coefficient in 3D space
Rahman 1964
. A larger
|\alpha_{n}(t)|
indicates a more heterogeneous dynamics of diffusion, and
\alpha_{n}(t)=0
when diffusion follows the Gaussian statistics. However, the general form of this parameter (i.e., in
k
-dimensional Euclidean space) has not yet been obtained, which is the critical and urgent challenge we address in the present letter.

II
II. ONE DIMENSION

Firstly, it is essential to summarize the diffusion of Brownian particles in one dimension as the basis of the further generalization. We assume that the space is homogeneous, thereby the diffusivity
D
remains constant. Afterwards, the self-part of the van Hove function [
G_{s}(x,t)
]
van Hove 1954
can be obtained by solving the one-dimensional diffusion equation as follow
Fick 1855
,

\frac{\partial G_{s}(x,t\mid x_{0})}{\partial t}=D\frac{\partial^{2}G_{s}(x,t\mid x_{0})}{\partial x^{2}},

(2)

with the initial condition given by

G_{s}(x,t=0\mid x_{0})=\delta(x-x_{0}),

(3)

where
x(t)
and
x_{0}
are the positions of the particle at time
t
and 0 respectively.
G_{s}(x,t)
thereby follows the form

G_{s}(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-x_{0})^{2}}{4Dt}\right).

(4)

Considering the Galilean invariance, it is feasible to fix the initial position of the particle at the origin of the Cartesian coordinates. Therefore,
G_{s}(x,t)
can be equivalently rewritten as

G_{s}(r,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{4Dt}\right),

(5)

where
r(t)
is the displacement of the particle as mentioned in section I. Hence,
G_{s}(x,t)
represents the probability distribution function of the displacement. Then we have

\displaystyle{\langle r^{2}(t)\rangle}

\displaystyle={\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{r^{2}G_{s}(r,t)dr}}=2Dt,

(6)

\displaystyle{\langle r^{2n}(t)\rangle}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{r^{2n}G_{s}(r,t)dr}=(2n-1)!!(2Dt)^{n}.

(7)

Therefore,
c_{n,1}
can be set as
1/(2n-1)!!
in one dimensional space.

III
III. GENERALIZATION IN
{\bm{k}}
-DIMENSION

Under the assumption that the
k
-dimensional Euclidean space is homogeneous, the diffusions in all directions are independent and share the same diffusivity
D
. Let
{\bm{r}}=(r_{1},r_{2},\cdots,r_{k})
be the displacement vector. Then
{\bm{r}}
follows a multivariate Gaussian distribution, and the joint van Hove function of
{\bm{r}}
can be given by

G_{s}({\bm{r}},t)=\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi})^{k}\sqrt{|{\bm{\Sigma}}|}}{\rm exp}\left(-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{r}}^{\rm T}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{r}}\right),

(8)

where
{\bm{\Sigma}}
is the covariance matrix and expressed as

{\bm{\Sigma}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}2Dt&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&2Dt&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&2Dt&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&2Dt\\
\end{array}\right]_{k\times k}.

(9)

Then
G_{s}({\bm{r}},t)
can be explicitly written as

\displaystyle G_{s}({\bm{r}},t)=\frac{1}{(\sqrt{4\pi Dt})^{k}}{\rm exp}\left(-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}{r_{i}^{2}}}{4Dt}\right)=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}{G_{s}(r_{i},t)}.

(10)

As a consequence, the MSD
{\langle r^{2}(t)\rangle}
can be obtained by

\displaystyle{\langle r^{2}(t)\rangle}

\displaystyle={\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{(r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2}+\cdots+r_{k}^{2})G_{s}({\bm{r}},t)dr_{1}dr_{2}\cdots dr_{k}}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}{\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{r_{i}^{2}}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}{G_{s}(r_{j},t)dr_{j}}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\left({\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{r_{i}^{2}}G_{s}(r_{i},t)dr_{i}}\cdot\prod\limits_{j=1\hfill\atop j\neq i\hfill}^{k}{\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{G_{s}(r_{j},t)dr_{j}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}{\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{r_{i}^{2}}G_{s}(r_{i},t)dr_{i}}=2kDt.

(11)

However, the derivation of
{\langle r^{2n}(t)\rangle}
is more complex. Using the
multinomial theorem
, we have

\displaystyle{\langle r^{2n}(t)\rangle}

\displaystyle={\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{(r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2}+\cdots+r_{k}^{2})^{n}G_{s}({\bm{r}},t)dr_{1}dr_{2}\cdots dr_{k}}}

\displaystyle={\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\sum{\frac{n!}{l_{1}!l_{2}!\cdots l_{k}!}r_{1}^{2l_{1}}r_{2}^{2l_{2}}\cdots r_{k}^{2l_{k}}}G_{s}({\bm{r}},t)dr_{1}dr_{2}\cdots dr_{k}}}

\displaystyle=\sum{\frac{n!}{l_{1}!l_{2}!\cdots l_{k}!}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}{r_{i}^{2l_{i}}G_{s}(r_{i},t)dr_{i}}}}

\displaystyle=\sum{\frac{n!}{l_{1}!l_{2}!\cdots l_{k}!}\prod\limits_{i=1\atop l_{i}\neq 0}^{k}{(2l_{i}-1)!!(2Dt)^{l_{i}}}},

(12)

where
l_{1}+l_{2}+\cdots+l_{k}=n
, and the sum is taken over all combinations of nonnegative integer indices
l_{1}
through
l_{k}
such that the sum of all
l_{i}
is
n
.

Eq. (12) can thereby be simplified as follow (See section
i
in Appendix for the proof),

\displaystyle{\langle r^{2n}(t)\rangle}

\displaystyle=(2Dt)^{n}\sum{n!\prod\limits_{i=1\atop l_{i}\neq 0}^{k}{\frac{(2l_{i}-1)!!}{l_{i}!}}}

\displaystyle=(2Dt)^{n}\frac{(2n+k-2)!!}{(k-2)!!}.

(13)

Thus,
c_{n,k}
is defined as
c_{n,k}=(k-2)!!k^{n}/(2n+k-2)!!
, and the non-Gaussian parameter in
k
-dimensional Euclidean space is given by

\alpha_{n,k}(t)=\frac{(k-2)!!k^{n}\langle r^{2n}(t)\rangle}{(2n+k-2)!!{\langle r^{2}(t)\rangle}^{n}}-1.

(14)

IV
IV. Appendix

IV.1
i. Proof for Eq. (13)

We use the mathematical induction to prove that Eq. (13) is exact. The equation that should be examined can be written as

\sum{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}{\frac{(2l_{i}-1)!!}{l_{i}!}}}=\frac{(2n+k-2)!!}{n!(k-2)!!}

(15)

for
k,n=1,2,\cdots
, where the meaning of the sum is same with that in Eq. (12).

Basis
: Eq. (7) has demonstrated that Eq. (15) holds for
k=1
and arbitrary
n
.

Inductive step
: We assume that Eq.(15) holds for
k\leq m
and arbitrary
n
. When
k=m+1
, we have
l_{1}+l_{2}+\cdots+l_{m+1}=n
. The following relation can thereby be obtained by using the induction hypothesis when
l_{m+1}
is fixed,

\sum\limits_{l_{m+1}\atop{\rm is~fixed}}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m}{\frac{(2l_{i}-1)!!}{l_{i}!}}}=\frac{(2n+m-2-2l_{m+1})!!}{(n-l_{m+1})!(m-2)!!},

(16)

where
l_{m+1}=0,1,\cdots,n
. Meanwhile, we have

\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m}{\frac{(2l_{i}-1)!!}{l_{i}!}}=\frac{l_{m+1}!}{(2l_{m+1}-1)!!}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m+1}{\frac{(2l_{i}-1)!!}{l_{i}!}}.

(17)

This leads to

\sum\limits_{l_{m+1}\atop{\rm is~fixed}}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m}{\frac{(2l_{i}-1)!!}{l_{i}!}}}=\frac{l_{m+1}!}{(2l_{m+1}-1)!!}\sum\limits_{l_{m+1}\atop{\rm is~fixed}}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m+1}{\frac{(2l_{i}-1)!!}{l_{i}!}}}.

(18)

Rearranging terms and using Eq. (16), we have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{l_{m+1}\atop{\rm is~fixed}}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m+1}{\frac{(2l_{i}-1)!!}{l_{i}!}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2l_{m+1}-1)!!}{l_{m+1}!}\cdot\frac{(2n+m-2-2l_{m+1})!!}{(n-l_{m+1})!(m-2)!!}.

(19)

Hence,

\displaystyle\sum{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m+1}{\frac{(2l_{i}-1)!!}{l_{i}!}}}=\sum\limits_{l_{m+1}=0}^{n}{\sum\limits_{l_{m+1}\atop{\rm is~fixed}}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m+1}{\frac{(2l_{i}-1)!!}{l_{i}!}}}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{l_{m+1}=0}^{n}{\frac{(2l_{m+1}-1)!!}{l_{m+1}!}\cdot\frac{(2n+m-2-2l_{m+1})!!}{(n-l_{m+1})!(m-2)!!}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!(m-1)!!}\times\frac{(m-1)!!}{(m-2)!!}\times

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{l_{m+1}=0}^{n}{{n\choose l_{m+1}}(2l_{m+1}-1)!!(2n+m-2-2l_{m+1})!!},

(20)

where
{n\choose l_{m+1}}=n!/[l_{m+1}!\cdot(n-l_{m+1})!]
.

Now the goal is to prove

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}{{n\choose k}(2i-1)!!(2n+m-2-2i)!!}

\displaystyle=(2n+m-1)!!\times\frac{(m-2)!!}{(m-1)!!}.

(21)

Based on Eq. (21), it is easy to find that Eq. (15) also holds for
k=m+1
. See section
ii
in Appendix for the proof of Eq. (21).

IV.2
ii. Proof for Eq. (21)

First we have

(1+x)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1+x)^{-\frac{m}{2}}=(1+x)^{-\frac{1+m}{2}}.

(22)

Note that

(1+x)^{\alpha}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{{\alpha\choose k}x^{k}},

(23)

where
|x|<1
. Thereby Eq. (22) can be transformed into

\left[\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}{{-\frac{1}{2}\choose i}x^{i}}\right]\left[\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}{{-\frac{m}{2}\choose j}x^{j}}\right]=\left[\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{{-\frac{1+m}{2}\choose n}x^{n}}\right].

(24)

Since

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}{{-\frac{1}{2}\choose i}x^{i}}\right]\left[\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}{{-\frac{m}{2}\choose j}x^{j}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}{\left[\sum_{i=0}^{n}{{-\frac{1}{2}\choose i}{-\frac{m}{2}\choose n-i}}\right]x^{n}},

(25)

we obtain

\sum_{i=0}^{n}{{-\frac{1}{2}\choose i}{-\frac{m}{2}\choose n-i}}={-\frac{1+m}{2}\choose n}

(26)

by comparing the right sides of Eqs. (24) and (25). Particularly,

\displaystyle{-\frac{1}{2}\choose i}{-\frac{m}{2}\choose n-i}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\frac{(2i-1)!!}{2^{i}i!}\cdot\frac{(2n+m-2-2i)!!}{2^{n-i}(n-i)!(m-2)!!},

(27)

\displaystyle{-\frac{1+m}{2}\choose n}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\frac{(2n+m-1)!!}{2^{n}n!(m-1)!!}.

(28)

Then we have

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}{\frac{(2i-1)!!(2n+m-2-2i)!!}{i!(n-i)!(m-2)!!}}=\frac{(2n+m-1)!!}{n!(m-1)!!}.

(29)

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}{\frac{n!}{i!(n-i)!}(2i-1)!!(2n+m-2-2i)!!}

\displaystyle=(2n+m-1)!!\times\frac{(m-2)!!}{(m-1)!!},

(30)

which proves Eq. (21).
