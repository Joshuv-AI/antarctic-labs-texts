Generating proof systems for three-valued propositional logics
Vitor Greati, Giuseppe Greco, Sérgio Marcelino, Alessandra Palmigiano, Umberto Rivieccio

Abstract
In general, providing an axiomatization for an arbitrary logic is a task that may require some ingenuity. In the case of logics defined by a finite logical matrix (three-valued logics being a particularly simple example), the generation of suitable finite axiomatizations can be completely automatized, essentially by expressing the matrix tables via inference rules. In this chapter we illustrate how two formalisms, the 3-labelled calculi of Baaz, Fermüller and Zach and the multiple-conclusion (or Set-Set ) Hilbert-style calculi of Shoesmith and Smiley, may be uniformly employed to axiomatize logics defined by a three-valued logical matrix. We discuss their main properties (related to completeness, decidability and proof search) and make a systematic comparison between both formalisms. We observe that either of the following strategies are pursued: (i) expanding the metalanguage of the formalism (via labels or types) or (ii) generalizing the usual notion of subformula property (as done in recent work by C. Caleiro and S. Marcelino) while remaining as close as possible to the original language of the logic. In both cases, desirable requirements are to guarantee the decidability of the associated symbolic procedure, as well as the possibility of an effective proof search. The generating procedure common to both formalisms can be described as follows: first (i) convert the matrix semantics into rule form (we refer to this step as the generating subprocedure ) and then (ii) simplify the set of rules thus obtained, essentially relying on the defining properties of any Tarskian consequence relation (we refer to this step as the streamlining subprocedure ). We illustrate through some examples that, if a minimal expressiveness assumption is met (namely, if the matrix defining the logic is monadic ), then it is straightforward to define effective translations guaranteeing the equivalence between the 3-labelled and the Set-Set approach.

Introduction

Different types of logical calculi are better suited for different purposes.
As is well known, so-called
Gentzen-style
(or
sequent
)
calculi
are commonly employed whenever we are interested in automatizing proof search. On the other hand, so-called
natural deduction calculi
are often taken as benchmarks for deciding when two derivations amount to the same argument, while
Hilbert-style calculi
are a most useful tool
in characterizing the sets of formulas that are
closed with respect to the derivability relation of
a given logic. In this chapter, we shall be concerned with automatizing proof system generation, that is, with defining a
procedure for generating decidable calculi
for a large class of logics, namely all logics that can be presented via a single finite-valued matrix and so, in particular, for three-valued logics.

Before getting into further technical details, let us spend a few words on what we mean by a
three-valued logic
, as well as on the choice of the particular logics considered in the present chapter. In the literature on (non-classical) logics, a
finite-valued
(propositional) logic is usually taken to be a logic determined or defined by some finite structure, typically a set whose elements
are interpreted as truth values in some intuitive sense. In turn, a
propositional logic
is usually defined as
a binary relation on the set of formulas – inductively constructed from propositional variables by means of a given set of propositional connectives – a relation
that satisfies the postulates laid out by A. Tarski
[
44
, p. 31]
; this is nowadays a standard notion
(though not the only existing one)
in formal logic.

It has been shown that every propositional logic
satisfying Tarski’s postulates can be defined by means of a (possibly infinite) family of (possibly infinite)
logical matrices

[
46
]
. It is thus fair to
affirm that
logical matrices constitute a most general method
(i.e. a standard
semantics
) for introducing arbitrary
(Tarskian) logics.
From this standpoint, finite-valued logics
may be taken to be those logics that can be defined
by means of a
finite
set of
finite
logical matrices – one cannot, without loss of generality, replace “finite set of finite matrices” by “a single finite matrix” (see
[
16
]
for further discussion and counter-examples).
In this broad sense,

three-valued logics
are thus those definable by some finite set of three-element logical matrices (also in this case we cannot replace “finite set” by
“a single (three-element) matrix”).
For technical reasons, in the present chapter
we shall narrow our focus to logics determined by
a single matrix
.
It is to these logics, as we shall illustrate through plenty of examples, that
the proof formalisms here considered apply more straightforwardly

By the above considerations, we shall fix a three-element set,
say
\{\mathbf{0},\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}},\mathbf{1}\}
, as our standard space of truth values. A particular three-valued logic is then determined by a choice of propositional connectives (each determined by the corresponding truth table) together with a choice of one or more sets of
designated
truth values.
All the non-trivial three-valued logics
arise in this way when combining the logics obtained by designating either a singleton (say
\{\mathbf{1}\}
) or a two-element set (say
\{\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}},\mathbf{1}\}
).

Over an arbitrary propositional language, the number of three-valued truth tables (and therefore of possible logics) obtainable in the above-described way is obviously unbounded: in the present chapter
we shall consider a few examples chosen according to the criteria detailed below (see Section
3
).
We shall introduce 3-labelled calculi
[
7
]
and
Set-Set
Hilbert-style calculi
[
42
]
for a number of logics determined by a single three-valued matrix,
as well as what we call their
streamlined
versions. We shall discuss their main properties (completeness, decidability, effective proof search) and draw a first systematic comparison of both formalisms.

Set-Set
Hilbert-style calculi are a generalization of traditional Hilbert-style calculi (introduced by Frege and popularized by Hilbert) in the sense that their metalanguage is reduced to a minimum, and inference rules just manipulate
sets of formulas
that constitute consecutions of (that is, belong to) the underlying logic.
3-labelled calculi, on the other hand, are not Hilbert-style, for their language includes labels and rules manipulate
sets of labelled formulas
or, equivalently,

3-sequents
.
The 3-labelled calculi generated for three-valued logics, as we will see, can actually be seen as generalizations of “multiple-conclusion sequent calculi” (see
[
33
, Chapter 3]
), insofar as
the distinction between structural and logical rules is in place, with zeroary rules being a generalized form of identity rules,
cut rules being a generalized form of (multi)cuts,
and each connective being associated with
its own set of introduction rules.

We observe, however, that the core of the corresponding generating procedures is essentially the same, and amounts to first (i) converting the matrix semantics in rule form (we refer to this step as the
generating subprocedure
) and then (ii) simplifying the set of rules thus obtained, essentially relying on the defining properties of any Tarskian consequence relation (we refer to this step as the
streamlining subprocedure
). The key point where both generating subprocedures depart from each other has to do with the specific properties of the output. In the case of 3-labelled calculi, we can rely on labels corresponding to truth values, so turning matrices into rule form is a quite straightforward process. In the case of
Set-Set
Hilbert-style calculi, this additional information has to be conveyed in the logical language, hence the need of
separators
, which are logical formulas capable of telling apart truth values pairwise in a sense that will be formally specified in Section
5.1
.

The chapter is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce basic
notions and terminology about algebras, languages, logics and logical matrices.
In Section
3
,
we provide an overview of the selected
three-valued logics that will be discussed
in subsequent sections. We devote special
attention to the traditional Hilbert-style calculi
available in the literature for
such logics, highlighting the fact
that for some of them no finite calculus
is currently known (whilst
Set-Set
finite calculi are guaranteed to exist by
[
42
]
).
In Section
4
,
we introduce the formalism of 3-labelled calculi and associated procedures for generating and streamlining analytic calculi
for three-valued logics.
We do the same in Section
5
for the formalism of
Set-Set
Hilbert-style calculi. Despite
the fact that the generating subprocedure
in the latter case demands an
expressiveness condition to be
satisfied by the
logical matrix at hand, we will
provide analytic finite axiomatizations for some non-sufficiently expressive
matrices in Subsection
5.2

(note that the result of
[
42
]

just referred to does not mention analyticity).
In Section
6
we compare
the generating procedures for 3-labelled
calculi and
Set-Set
Hilbert-style
calculi, showing possibilities of
translations from
Set-Set

to 3-labelled rules and proofs.
Finally, Section
7

closes the chapter with some concluding remarks
and suggestions for further research.

Łukasiewicz (Ł

The logic

Łukasiewicz’s

introducing a third truth value meant to represent propositions that are neither false nor true but only

Although there is nowadays consensus that

Various inter-definabilities hold among the

definable by the classical formula

Strong Kleene (

In his book
[

,
the prominent mathematician
S.C. Kleene introduced two
three-valued logics that nowadays bear his name.
The first we consider
(

Ł
3

as far as the connectives
\land,\lor
and
\neg

\varphi\to\psi:=\neg\varphi\lor\psi
.
Thus, formally,
K
is
easily shown to coincide with the
\{\land,\lor,\neg\}
-fragment of
Ł
3
. The meaning
Kleene had in mind for the third value is, however, different from the Łukasiewicz case, for
\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}}
is now interpreted as
undefined

The notion of partial function generalizes that of ordinary function in that the former may be undefined for some arguments, that is, we admit that no output may be produced
for certain inputs. Thus, for a given function

or

or

is undefined; within

\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}}

is true. This reasoning also makes perfect sense from a computational point of view: a program that computes the value of

may output
the value
\mathbf{1}
as soon as it successfully manages to establish that (e.g.)

has value
\mathbf{1}

p

Logic of Paradox (
LP
).

This logic
was introduced
(under the name
calculus of antinomies
) in the 1960s by F. G. Asenjo
[
3
]
,
but was studied in greater depth and made popular a decade later by G. Priest, who also proposed the name we are adopting here:
Logic of Paradox
.
The intention of both authors was to develop a logical framework that might allow one to handle logical paradoxes
(also known as
antinomies
) without trivializing the whole system, as happens in the classical setting.
Antinomies are propositions that, like the famous Russell’s paradox, appear to be
both true and false
at the same time. From the point of view of a particular logic,
we can say that an antinomy is a proposition
p
such that both
p
and
\neg p
assume a designated value. The logic devised by Asenjo-Priest is therefore
(a most prominent example of) a

paraconsistent logic
,
i.e. one that rejects the following
ex contradictione quodlibet
rule:

\varphi\wedge\neg\varphi

(
\mathbf{ECQ}
)

\psi

Semantically, achieving such a behavior is easy: one may just adopt the
same truth tables as Kleene’s

K
while having
\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}}

designated together with

\mathbf{1}
. This simple strategy, while necessarily renouncing some derivations that hold classically, allows
LP
to actually
retain all the tautologies of
classical logic (see below).

As mentioned earlier, the Belnap-Dunn logic
E
is, formally, a weakening common to

LP
and
K

It may be interesting to mention that,
in fact, the four-valued matrix which defines
E
is obtained by simultaneously enlarging
the set
\{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1}\}
with
two non-classical truth values: the former (usually called
n
, for
neither
true nor false) behaves as
\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}}
does in
K

(
n
is a fixpoint of the negation operator, but is not designated) and the latter
(usually called
b
, for
both
true and false)
behaves as
\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}}
does in
LP

(
b
is also a fixpoint of the negation operator but it is designated).

As shown in
[
1

(\textbf{{LP}}1)

As mentioned earlier, the tautologies of

are precisely the ones of classical logic
[

, so classical logic and
LP
only differ as far as proper inferences (that is, those with nonempty sets of premises) are concerned.

Paraconsistent Weak Kleene (
PK
).

This logic seems to have been considered already by S. Halldén
in his 1949 monograph
[
29
]
, and two decades later by A. Prior
[
40
]
, but has only recently been studied in depth (see e.g.
[
12
]
,
and also
[
19
]
which
explores applications to the theory of truth).

The connectives and truth tables of
PK
coincide with those of
BK
(see the next subsection), and so does the interpretation of the middle value. Logical consequence, however, is defined in
PK

in terms of preservation of non-falsity rather than (as in
BK
) of preservation of truth. This choice leads to designating
\{\mathbf{1},\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}}\}
, which entails (as we have seen with
LP
) that
PK
is indeed a paraconsistent logic.
In fact the valid formulas of

PK
coincide with those of

LP
which, as observed earlier, are precisely the classical tautologies
(see e.g.
[
12
, p. 257]
).
However,

PK
and
LP
differ in their derivations. To see this,
let us recall that

PK
can be axiomatized
as follows.
Take any complete
Set-Fmla
H-calculus for classical logic (having
modus ponens
as its only non-nullary rule)
and, while keeping all the axioms,
replace
modus ponens

by the following restricted version (see
[
12
, Prop. 4]
):

\varphi\quad\varphi\rightarrow\psi

provided
\mathsf{props}(\varphi)\subseteq\mathsf{props}(\psi)

This is in keeping with the observation that

left variable inclusion companion

of classical logic (in the sense of
[
11
, pp. 49-50]
, to which we refer for the relevant definitions and further details); likewise,
the logic
BK
dealt with in the sequel is the right variable inclusion companion of classical logic.

Using the preceding characterization,
one sees that
PK

validates, for instance, the inference

\varphi\land\neg\varphi\vdash\varphi\land\psi

which is easily refutable
in
LP
. On the other hand,

LP
satisfies all rules of
the Belnap-Dunn logic

while
it is easy to see that
PK
does not satisfy, for instance, the rule
(
\textbf{{K}}1

are, as consequence relations, mutually incomparable.

As shown in
[

similar to the one of

and also in this case it is an open
question whether

can be axiomatized by a finite H-calculus.
And just like in that case the divide between

and

after many decades we still do not know if these logics are finitely axiomatizable in
Set-Fmla
but a finite
Set-Set

5

see
[
17

.

[

The third value is intended to

5
Multiple-conclusion Hilbert-style calculi

In this section, we are interested in providing finite and analytic multiple-conclusion (or
Set-Set
)
Hilbert-style axiomatizations for three-valued logical matrices.
As we shall detail in a moment, this proof formalism generalizes
traditional Hilbert-style calculi in allowing sets of
formulas in the conclusion of the rules of inference.
Putting aside the trivial cases, we will focus on
two tasks: first, on specifying a general method
for axiomatizing any three-valued matrix that satisfies a
criterion of sufficient expressiveness called
monadicity
; and, second,
on axiomatizing some specific three-valued matrices that
do not meet such criterion.
The result proved in
[
42
, Theorem 19.12]

guarantees that every finite logical matrix (in particular, three-valued matrices)
has an adequate (sound and complete) finite
Set-Set
axiomatization, but finding one for the non-monadic matrices
can be a rather
involved task, as we will see.
In
[
31
]
, the recipe for the monadic case was generalized to deal with non-determinism and the resulting finite calculi were shown to satisfy a generalized subformula property, yielding what we call
\Theta
-analiticity
, a fact that had remained unnoticed for many decades.

We begin by giving the formal definitions related to the
proof formalism of
Set-Set
calculi together with some examples.

only the subformulas of the statement are enough to produce
a proof of that very statement in case such proof exists.

As explained in

and consequently to a decision procedure for the corresponding

Briefly put, if we want to know whether

,
where
\mathsf{R}
is a finite and finitary

we may attempt to build a derivation in
the following way: start from
a single node labelled with
\Phi

of
\mathsf{R}
not used in the same branch
with formulas in the set

\mathsf{subf}^{\Theta}(\Phi\cup\Psi)

whose premises are in
\Phi

expand that node by creating a
child node labelled with
\Phi\cup\{\psi\}
for each formula

in the succedent of the chosen rule instance
and repeat this step for each new node.
In case it fails in finding a rule instance for applying to some node,
we may conclude that no proof exists,
and from each non-

In case every branch eventually gets

This procedure is exponential-time in the size of the statement of interest in general
and, when

is

(all inference rules have a single formula
in the succedent), the running-time is

It is worth emphasizing the modularity of the
proposed approach: each connective is axiomatized by its own set of rule schemas,
thus axiomatizing a matrix expanded with new connectives is a matter
of adding new rule schemas corresponding to the new connectives.
The examples to be presented in a moment will show that
Definition
5.16
may deliver redundant and
complex rule schemas. This fact motivates the next result,
which introduces some streamlining steps that highly simplify
the generated axiomatization.
The proof uses, in essence, the properties
of
Set-Set
logics.
In what follows, we say that a rule schema is a
dilution
of another when the former can be obtained from the latter by adding formulas in the antecedent or in the succedent (recall property (D) in Definition
2.2
). In that case, we may also say that the latter is a
subschema
of the former.
In what follows, denote by

\mathsf{fmlas}(\mathsf{t})
the set of formulas
appearing in the
\mathsf{R}
-derivation
\mathsf{t}
.

Theorem 5.20
.

Let
\mathsf{R}
be a
\Theta
-analytical calculus
for a matrix
\mathbb{M}

and consider the following streamlining subprocedure:

(S1)

Remove all instances of overlap.

(S2)

Remove all rule schemas that are dilutions of other rule schemas in
\mathsf{R}
.

(S3)

Replace occurrences of pairs of rule schemas

\frac{\Phi,\varphi}{\Psi}

and
\frac{\Phi}{\Psi,\varphi}

by
\frac{\Phi}{\Psi}
.

(S4)

Replace a rule of inference by one of its subrules that is
derivable
using the rules of the calculus.

(S5)

Remove a rule of inference
\mathsf{r}:=\frac{\Phi}{\Psi}
that has a proof
\mathsf{t}

using the others rules of
\mathsf{R}
,
such that,
for all
\Gamma\subseteq L_{\Sigma}(P)

and all

\sigma:P\to\mathsf{subf}(\Gamma)
,
if
\sigma(\Phi),\sigma(\Psi)\subseteq\mathsf{subf}^{\Theta}(\Gamma)
, then

\sigma(\mathsf{fmlas}(\mathsf{t}))\subseteq\mathsf{subf}^{\Theta}(\Gamma)
.

Then
(S1)
,
(S2)
,
(S3)
,
(S4)

and
(S5)

preserve adequacy
for
\mathbb{M}

and
\Theta
-analyticity.

In order to use a terminology
that is closer to that
used in 3-labelled calculi,
if
\mathbf{L}
is determined
by
\mathbb{M}
, then
we will sometimes denote
\mathsf{R}_{\mathbb{M}}

by
mc.{\mathbf{L}}

and the streamlined version
of this calculus by

smc.{\mathbf{L}}
.

5.2
Axiomatization of non-monadic matrices

As we have been insisting on, non-monadic deterministic logical matrices can always be axiomatized in
Set-Set

[
42
, Theorem 19.12]
using the original language, although
not via our recipe.
There is no known algorithm for this general case.
By employing separators with contexts
[
42
, Section 19.5]

in an
ad hoc
manner, however, we managed to show
Set-Set

\Theta
-analytic axiomatizations — now allowing
\Theta

to contain formulas of arities other than 1, for instance
p\lor q

and
p\to q
— for fragments of some of the
logics presented in Section
3
.
We present them in the sequel.

Remark 5.28
.

We should mention that our recipe for monadic matrices can always be applied to non-monadic matrices
provided that the object language is extended with connectives guaranteeing the separation of all truth values. The resulting matrix is then monadic
and the logic
is a conservative extension of the original logic
[
31
]
.

7
Final considerations

When it comes to the
automatic generation of
proof systems for propositional
three-valued logics,
the formalism of 3-labelled calculi
is powerful enough to deliver finite and analytic
systems for any of such logics.
The mechanism behind this general applicability is fairly simple: each truth value has its own
place in the manipulated sequents, in such a
way that the meta-conjunctions and meta-disjunctions
that characterize each truth table
have a direct correspondence in terms of
3-labelled logical rules (see
[
7
]

for more details on this matter).
Enriching the structure of the sequents, however, may bring some disadvantages, namely
a greater distancing between the rules of the proof system and the logic being
captured, as well as the extra effort in dealing
with the new metalinguistic resources.
One of the research lines in which
these aspects may become practical problems is
in the combination of logics: putting two
sequent systems together may cause unwanted
interactions in the combined system
[
15
]
.

An alternative to increasing the metalanguage resides in investing on Hilbert-style
systems, which keep the metalanguage at a minimum,
the rules of inference being just a collection
of consecutions of the underlying logic.
When it comes to system generation, proof search and decidability,
these systems have been put aside due to the
lack of modularity and analyticity results. After
the works of C. Caleiro and S. Marcelino
[
31
,
14
]
, we learned that the
simple move to
Set-Set
Hilbert-style systems
[
42
]

allows us to get modular and analytic
systems for a wide class of many-valued logics.
The price for abandoning the metalanguage
present in 3-sequents is the sufficient
expressiveness requirement (monadicity),
which is demanded for the automatic
proof system generation in the

Set-Set
setting.
Summing up, as we have seen in Section
6
,
the choice of proof formalism for a
given application depends
essentially on a trade-off between
the acceptable usage of extra metalanguage
(or the proximity of the rules to the
logic of interest)
and the complexity of the formulas
present in the rules of inference
(cf. Example
6.2
).

It is worth emphasizing that
every three-valued logic (in the sense of being
determined by a single deterministic three-valued matrix)
has a finite
Set-Set
axiomatization
[
42
]
, even though
there is no general procedure for producing it
in the case non-monadic matrices (provided we want to use the same language — if one is allowed to expand the language, the problem is solved
[
31
, Section 4]
). The best we can
do, for now, is to proceed in an
ad hoc

manner, as we did for some fragments of
well-known three-valued logics in Subsection
5.2
.
A promising line of investigation, therefore, consists in
pursuing a general procedure covering non-monadic cases and delivering
analytic
Set-Set
calculi over the original language.

Finally, despite the fact that the three-valued
logics studied in this chapter are
all determined by single deterministic logical
matrices and the generation procedures were presented for these cases only, such procedures have all been generalized
to (partial) non-deterministic matrices
[
14
,
5
]
.
These are structures that allow for
(possibly empty) sets of truth values as
outputs of the interpretations of the
connectives. With partial matrices, for example,
one may capture by single monadic matrices
three-valued logics determined by
a family of deterministic three-valued matrices
[
31
, Example 3]
.
In this way, taking the study of three-valued logics
to partial non-deterministic semantics
is a natural step towards the discovery
of new logics and applications.
