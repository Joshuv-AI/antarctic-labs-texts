Scattering Amplitudes and The Cotton Double Copy
William T. Emond, and Nathan Moynihan

Abstract
We construct classical curvature spinors in topologically massive gauge theory and topologically massive gravity, expressed in terms of massive three-particle amplitudes. We show that when the amplitudes double copy, the curvature spinors satisfy the Cotton double copy , the three-dimensional cousin of the Weyl double copy. Furthermore, we show that under certain circumstances the Cotton double copy can be derived via a dimensional reduction of the Weyl double copy.

2
Motivation: Topological Mass via Dimensional Reduction

Equations of motion for free fields can be greatly simplified by using the relationship between tensors with
s
indices and spinors with
2s
indices. In four dimensions, this corresponds to representing every vector index with a pair of
SL(2,\mathbb{C})
indices, one in the
(\frac{1}{2},0)
representation and the other in
(0,\frac{1}{2})
, typically achieved by contracting the vector index with an Infeld–Van der Waerden symbol
\sigma^{N}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}
, where
N=0,1,2,3
is a
D=4
Lorentz index and
\alpha
and
\dot{\beta}
are spinor indices, manipulated using
\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}
and
\epsilon_{\dot{\alpha}\dot{\beta}}
respectively. Inner products between Lorentz tensors can easily be exchanged for spinor contractions, and vice versa, using the relationships

\eta^{MN}=-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{M}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}\sigma^{N\alpha\dot{\beta}},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \sigma^{M}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}\sigma_{M\gamma\dot{\delta}}=-2\epsilon_{\alpha\gamma}\epsilon_{\dot{\beta}\dot{\delta}}.

(2)

Using this, we can recast the Maxwell equations and Bianchi identities into the form

\displaystyle\partial_{N}F^{MN}=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \partial_{\dot{\beta}}^{\alpha}\varphi_{\alpha\beta}+\partial_{\beta}^{\dot{\alpha}}\bar{\varphi}_{\dot{\alpha}\dot{\beta}}=0,

(3)

\displaystyle\partial_{[M}F_{NR]}=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \partial_{\dot{\beta}}^{\alpha}\varphi_{\alpha\beta}-\partial_{\beta}^{\dot{\alpha}}\bar{\varphi}_{\dot{\alpha}\dot{\beta}}=0,

(4)

where
\varphi_{\alpha\beta}
is the self-dual Maxwell spinor (and
\bar{\varphi}_{\dot{\alpha}\dot{\beta}}
its complex conjugate, the anti-self-dual spinor) given by

\varphi_{\alpha\beta}=\varphi_{(\alpha\beta)}=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\dot{\alpha}\dot{\beta}}F_{MN}\sigma^{M}_{\alpha\dot{\alpha}}\sigma^{N}_{\beta\dot{\beta}}\penalty\ .

(5)

By combining both equations for
\varphi_{\alpha\beta}
, we can derive a single equation of motion for the self-dual sector

\partial_{\dot{\beta}}^{\alpha}\varphi_{\alpha\beta}=0.

(6)

In
2+1
dimensions the situation is a bit different, and the Lorentz group has several interesting isomorphisms

SO(1,2)\sim SL(2,\mathbb{R})\sim SU(1,1)\sim SP(2,\mathbb{R}).

We will choose to work in the
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
representation (with no distinction between left and right indices), meaning the components of a momentum bi-spinor
p_{\alpha\beta}
are all real assuming the momentum vector has real components. We can then use the (2+1)-dimensional relationship between Lorentz and
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
inner products

\eta^{\mu\nu}=-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}\sigma^{\nu\alpha\beta},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \sigma^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}\sigma_{\mu\gamma\delta}=-2\epsilon_{\alpha\gamma}\epsilon_{\beta\delta},

(7)

where
\mu=0,1,2
and the Infeld–Van der Waerden symbols
\sigma^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}
are completely real in the
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
basis given in the appendix.

The equations of motion for topologically massive gauge theory also have an
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
representation, as does the 3D Bianchi identity

\partial_{\mu}\tilde{F}^{\mu}\propto\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}\partial_{\mu}F_{\nu\rho}\propto\partial^{\alpha\beta}\varphi_{\alpha\beta}=0,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \implies\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \epsilon^{\alpha\beta}\left(\partial_{\beta}^{\gamma}\varphi_{\gamma\alpha}-\partial_{\alpha}^{\gamma}\varphi_{\gamma\beta}\right)=0.

(8)

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}F^{\mu\nu}+m\tilde{F}^{\mu}=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \partial_{\beta}^{\gamma}\Phi_{\gamma\alpha}+\partial_{\alpha}^{\gamma}\Phi_{\gamma\beta}-2m\Phi_{\alpha\beta}=0,

(9)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\tilde{F}^{\mu}=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \partial_{\beta}^{\gamma}\Phi_{\gamma\alpha}-\partial_{\alpha}^{\gamma}\Phi_{\gamma\beta}=0,

(10)

where
\Phi_{\alpha\beta}
is completely real and the dual to the Faraday tensor is given by

\tilde{F}^{\mu}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\rho\sigma}F_{\rho\sigma}.

(11)

F^{\mu\nu}
is real, the helicity is determined explicitly by the sign of the mass.

2.1
Spinor Dimensional Reduction

The convenience of working in the
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
representation is that it can be considered as the real slice of four dimensional spinor space, at least for vectors and antisymmetric two-tensors with real components. For a generic spacetime vector
\ell^{\mu}
(with real components), we can perform a dimensional reduction along a given spacetime direction by taking a real slice via
2
2

2

A simple example is the position vector
x^{M}=(t,x,y,z)

\operatorname{Re}\begin{pmatrix}-t+z&x+iy\\
x-iy&-t-z\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-t+z&x\\
x&-t-z\end{pmatrix}.

(13)

, i.e.

\operatorname{Re}\left(\ell_{4D}^{M}\sigma_{M}^{\alpha\dot{\beta}}\right)=\ell_{3D}^{\alpha\dot{\beta}}.

(14)

However, we still have the chiral ‘dotted’ index to deal with, which ought not to be present in a 3D representation. We can introduce an operator

\chi^{\penalty\ \dot{\alpha}}_{\alpha}=\chi^{M}(\gamma_{M})^{\penalty\ \dot{\alpha}}_{\alpha}=(\gamma_{2})^{\penalty\ \dot{\alpha}}_{\alpha},

(15)

which projects out the specified imaginary direction such that a symmetrization automatically performs the dimensional reduction, e.g.

\ell_{\alpha\dot{\alpha}}\chi^{\dot{\alpha}}_{\beta}=\ell_{\alpha\beta}+i\ell_{2}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \implies\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \ell_{(\alpha\dot{\alpha}}\chi^{\dot{\alpha}}_{\beta)}=\ell_{\alpha\beta}.

(16)

If we want to do a dimensional reduction from a massless vector to a massive, we take the direction of spacetime to be constant rather than zero, which gives

\det{p_{\alpha\dot{\alpha}}}=-p_{0}^{2}+p_{1}^{2}+p_{2}^{2}+p_{3}^{2}=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \longleftrightarrow\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ -p_{0}^{2}+p_{1}^{2}+p_{2}^{2}=-m^{2}.

(17)

In position space, this is simply

\chi^{\penalty\ \dot{\alpha}}_{\beta}\partial_{\dot{\alpha}\alpha}=\partial_{\alpha\beta}\pm m\epsilon_{\alpha\beta},

(18)

where the sign of
m
corresponds to either an outgoing or incoming plane-wave (and again determines the helicity).

We can use this to derive the spinor form of the topologically massive equations of motion directly from four dimensional self-dual Maxwell theory, since we can write

\operatorname{Re}\left(\chi^{\penalty\ \dot{\beta}}_{\alpha}\partial_{\dot{\beta}}^{\gamma}\varphi_{\gamma\beta}\right)=\partial_{\alpha}^{\gamma}\Phi_{\gamma\beta}-m\Phi_{\alpha\beta}=0.

(19)

This is only straightforwardly possible due to the fact that we are examining the spinor form of a completely antisymmetric tensor with real components. This tells us that all of the imaginary components of the resulting spinor will lie along one direction of spacetime - if it had been a symmetric tensor, or a tensor with imaginary components, this would not have necessarily been the case. This makes examining the gravitational case more complicated, since the Weyl spinor comes from a tensor which is symmetric in some of its indices.

Symmetric curvature spinors can, in general, always be decomposed in terms of rank-1 spinors in an effort to classify exact solutions of the field equations
Penrose:1960eq
;
Witten:1959zza
, including in topologically massive gravity
Barrow_1986
;
Milson:2012ry
;
Chow:2009km
. The Weyl curvature spinor can be decomposed uniquely into a symmetrized product of one-index spinors
Penrose:1960eq

\Psi_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}=\alpha_{(\alpha}\beta_{\beta}\gamma_{\gamma}\delta_{\delta)}.

(20)

The character of the rank one spinors, namely how they specify principal null directions, determines the possible Petrov class of the Weyl tensor. Since these spinors point out directions in spacetime, they ought to have a natural dimensional reduction of the form discussed. However, as discussed in the introduction the Weyl tensor is identically zero in less than four dimensions making this question less trivial. In three dimensions, the role of the Weyl tensor is typically taken up by the Cotton tensor
Garcia:2003bw
;
Sousa:2007ax
, which shares many of the desirable properties (e.g. its vanishing indicates conformal flatness), so it is natural to expect that solutions in topologically massive gravity might be classified using the Cotton spinor. To examine this problem, we will express the curvature spinors explicitly in terms of scattering amplitudes and momentum spinors, which are well behaved under dimensional reduction
Moynihan:2020ejh
;
Burger:2021wss
.

3
Curvature Spinors from Three-Particle Amplitudes

Curvature spinors can decomposed into rank one spinors, each of which is associated to some null vector in spacetime. Null vectors in four dimensions give rise to bi-spinors with vanishing determinant and can therefore always be decomposed into rank one spinors. It is useful to construct scattering amplitudes using spinor-helicity variables – the spinors that correspond to a decomposition of a null momentum in four dimensions. In three dimensions, we can decompose massive momentum bispinors via

\displaystyle p_{\alpha\beta}=N\lambda_{(\alpha}\bar{\lambda}_{\beta)},

(21)

where
N
is a dimensionless normalisation to be determined.
The on-shell condition, in the signature
(-,+,+)
, demands that

\displaystyle-2p^{\mu}p_{\mu}=p^{\alpha\beta}p_{\alpha\beta}=2m^{2}=-2N^{2}\braket{\lambda\bar{\lambda}}^{2}.

(22)

If we demand that the momentum bispinor is real and that it satisfies on the on-shell condition, then a suitible choice that gives rise to the momentum in the basis (
98
) is

\displaystyle\lambda_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{1}-p_{0}}}\begin{pmatrix}p_{2}-im\\
p_{1}-p_{0}\end{pmatrix},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \bar{\lambda}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{1}-p_{0}}}\begin{pmatrix}p_{2}+im\\
p_{1}-p_{0}\end{pmatrix}.

(23)

With
\braket{\lambda\bar{\lambda}}=2im
, the normalisation
N=\frac{1}{2}
satisfies the on-shell condition. We find a momentum bispinor of the form

\displaystyle p_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{(\alpha}\bar{\lambda}_{\beta)}=\begin{pmatrix}-p_{0}-p_{1}&p_{2}\\
p_{2}&-p_{0}+p_{1}\end{pmatrix}=p_{\mu}\sigma^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}.

(24)

The polarization vectors for these spinors are given by
Moynihan:2020ejh

\epsilon_{-}^{\mu}(q)=\frac{\braket{q|\gamma^{\mu}|q}}{2m},\quad\epsilon_{+}^{\mu}(q)=\frac{\braket{\bar{q}|\gamma^{\mu}|\bar{q}}}{2m}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\epsilon_{-\alpha\beta}(q)=\frac{|q\rangle_{\alpha}|q\rangle_{\beta}}{m},\quad\epsilon_{+\alpha\beta}(q)=\frac{|\bar{q}\rangle_{\alpha}|\bar{q}\rangle_{\beta}}{m}\;.

(25)

They complex conjugates of one another, with conjugation exchanging incoming for outgoing.

We could of course choose to ensure that our spinors are entirely real by analytically continuing the mass to
m\rightarrow-im
. This either causes the momentum bispinor to become pure imaginary (since we now require
N=i/2
) or we keep
N=1/2
and the bispinor real and the on-shell condition is violated, giving
p^{2}=m^{2}\neq-m^{2}
. In the following subsections we will opt for the spinors to be complex – including their momentum components – and comment on the other cases later.

3.1
Electromagnetic Curvature Spinor

Matter fields coupled to topologically massive gauge bosons are anyons due to the presence of the Chern-Simons term
Jackiw:1990ka
. We can do the usual mode expansion of a topologically massive gauge field into incoming and outgoing modes,

A_{\mu}(x)=\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(q)\left[a(q)\epsilon^{\mu}(q)e^{-iq\cdot x}+a^{\dagger}(q)\epsilon^{*\mu}(q)e^{iq\cdot x}\right],

(26)

where the measure is

\mathrm{d}\Phi(q)=\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{3}q\hat{\delta}(q^{2}+m^{2})\Theta(q^{0}),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \hat{\mathrm{d}}^{n}q\equiv\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}q}{(2\pi)^{n}},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \hat{\delta}^{n}(q)\equiv(2\pi)^{n}\delta^{n}(q).

(27)

We note that this expansion is the same for either value of the spin of the gauge field (which corresponds to the sign of the mass in the Lagrangian - no sum over helicities), with the difference encapsulated by the polarization vector
\epsilon^{\mu}

In 2+1 dimensions, the dual field strength vector is equivalent to the field strength tensor, and so we will use this to derive the corresponding Maxwell spinor. Thankfully, the dual field strength can be expanded into modes just as the gauge field can, and in fact by using the equations of motion

\displaystyle\tilde{F}^{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(q)\frac{q^{2}}{m}\left[a(q)\epsilon^{\mu}(q)e^{-iq\cdot x}+a^{\dagger}(q)\epsilon^{*\mu}(q)e^{iq\cdot x}\right]

(29)

In four dimensions, the expectation value of

\displaystyle\left\langle F^{\mu\nu}(x)\right\rangle=\frac{1}{M}\operatorname{Re}\sum_{\eta}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(\bar{k})\hat{\delta}(u\cdot\bar{k})\mathcal{A}_{-\eta}^{(3)}(\bar{k})\bar{k}^{[\mu}\varepsilon_{\eta}^{\nu]}e^{-i\bar{k}\cdot x},

(30)

where

(31)

where we have explicitly labelled the intrinsic helicity

For the remainder of the paper we will take

Acting with

3.2
Gravitational Curvature Spinor

The linearized graviton field operator for a single helicity is given by

h^{\mu\nu}=2\,\text{Re}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(q)\,a(q)\epsilon^{\mu}(q)\epsilon^{\nu}(q)\,e^{-iq\cdot x}\;,

(38)

where we have expressed the graviton polarisation tensor as an outer product of polarisation vectors
\epsilon^{\mu}(q)
.

The linearized Cotton tensor
C_{\mu\nu}
is given by

C^{\mu\nu}=\frac{\kappa}{2}\,\partial_{\lambda}\big[\partial^{\rho}\varepsilon^{\lambda\sigma(\mu}\partial^{\nu)}h_{\rho\sigma}-\partial^{2}\varepsilon^{\lambda\sigma(\mu}h^{\nu)}_{\;\;\sigma}\big]\;,

(39)

which in the de Donder gauge (
\partial_{\nu}h^{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}\partial^{\mu}h
), reduces to

C^{\mu\nu}=-\frac{\kappa}{4}\,\partial_{\lambda}\partial^{2}\varepsilon^{\lambda\sigma(\mu}h^{\nu)}_{\;\;\;\sigma}\;.

(40)

Inserting (
38
) into this expression gives us the Cotton tensor operator for a given fixed helicity
k

\displaystyle C^{\mu\nu}_{k}=

\displaystyle-\frac{i\kappa}{2}\,\text{Re}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(q)\,a_{j}(q)\,q^{2}q_{\lambda}\,\varepsilon^{\lambda\sigma(\mu}\epsilon_{k}^{\nu)}(q)\epsilon_{k\sigma}(q)\,e^{-iq\cdot x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{i\kappa m^{2}}{2}\,\text{Re}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(q)\,a_{k}(q)\,q_{\lambda}\,\varepsilon^{\lambda\sigma(\mu}\epsilon_{k}^{\nu)}(q)\epsilon_{k\sigma}(q)\,e^{-iq\cdot x}\;,

(41)

where we have used that
q^{2}=-m^{2}
, with
m
is the topological mass of the graviton.
To compute the corresponding expectation value of the Cotton tensor, we can do so in direct analogy to the gauge theory case for
C^{\mu\nu}(x)
. In doing so, one arrives at the following expression for the expectation value of the Cotton tensor

\langle C^{\mu\nu}\rangle_{k}=\,-\frac{\kappa m^{2}}{4M}\,\text{Re}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(q)\,\hat{\delta}(u\cdot q)\,\mathcal{M}_{-k}^{(3)}(q)\,q_{\lambda}\,\varepsilon^{\lambda\sigma(\mu}\epsilon_{k}^{\nu)}(q)\epsilon_{k\sigma}(q)\,e^{-iq\cdot x}\;.

(42)

such that for

3.2.1

4
Radiative Solutions and Curvature Spinors

Having established the relationship between scattering amplitudes in topologically massive gauge theories and curvature spinors, we now need to check from the field theory side that these do indeed give rise to the expected results. Bear in mind that we did not, in fact, use information from any
specific
theory to construct the curvature spinors above: we constructed the three-particle amplitudes for an arbitrary parity-violating massive spin-1 (and spin-2) theory coupled to scalars using little group constraints and dimensional analysis. We will now construct the same quantities for specific solutions from the field theory side.

4.1
Electromagnetic Curvature Spinor

Let us consider the (Lorenz gauge) field equations for the topologically massive dual Maxwell field
\tilde{F}^{\mu}(x)
in the presence of a static, electrically charged point source
J^{\mu}(x)

(\partial^{2}-m^{2})\tilde{F}^{\nu}(x)=eJ^{\mu}(x)\;,

(63)

where
J^{\mu}(x)=(m\eta^{\mu\nu}-\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}\partial_{\rho})u_{\nu}\,\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{x})
.
We are interested in field solutions that extend to the distant future but not the distant past, so we consider solutions involving the retarded Greens function
G_{\text{ret}}(x-y)
with support only inside the future lightcone
x_{0}>y_{0}
, giving a solution

\tilde{F}^{\mu}(x)=-e\int\mathrm{d}^{3}y\,G_{\text{ret}}(x-y)\,J^{\mu}(y)\;.

(64)

The retarded propagator satisfies the equation
(\partial^{2}-m^{2})G_{\text{ret}}(x-y)=-\delta^{(3)}(x-y)
(with boundary condition
G_{\text{ret}}(x-y)=0
for
x^{0}<y^{0}
), and can therefore be expressed as

G_{\text{ret}}(x-y)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int\hat{d}^{3}q\frac{e^{iq\cdot(x-y)}}{-(q^{0}+i\epsilon)^{2}+\mathbf{q}^{2}+m^{2}}\;.

(65)

One can also consider the advanced propagator
G_{\text{adv}}(x-y)
, which satisfies the same equation, but with the boundary condition
G_{\text{adv}}(x-y)=0
for
x^{0}>y^{0}
:

G_{\text{adv}}(x-y)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int\hat{d}^{3}q\frac{e^{iq\cdot(x-y)}}{-(q^{0}-i\epsilon)^{2}+\mathbf{q}^{2}+m^{2}}\;.

(66)

The radiative propagator is defined as the difference between these two propagators, i.e.,

G_{\text{rad}}(x-y)=G_{\text{ret}}(x-y)-G_{\text{adv}}(x-y)=i\int\hat{d}^{3}q\,\text{sgn}(q^{0})\hat{\delta}(q^{2}+m^{2})\,e^{iq\cdot(x-y)}\;.

(67)

Note that
G_{\text{rad}}(x-y)
only has support for on-shell (
q^{2}=-m^{2}
) Fourier modes, and as such solves the wave equation
(\partial^{2}-m^{2})G_{\text{rad}}(x-y)=0
.

Given this, we can express eq. (
64
) as

\tilde{F}^{\mu}(x)=-e\int\mathrm{d}^{3}y\,\big[G_{\text{rad}}(x-y)+G_{\text{adv}}(x-y)\big]\,J^{\mu}(y)\;.

(68)

Consequently, the radiative contribution to the solution is

\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{\text{rad}}^{\mu}(x)=

\displaystyle\ -e\int\mathrm{d}^{3}y\,G_{\text{rad}}(x-y)\,J^{\mu}(y)=-ie\int\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{3}q\,\text{sgn}(q^{0})\hat{\delta}(q^{2}+m^{2})\,J^{\mu}(q)\,e^{iq\cdot x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ -ie\int\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{3}q\,\left(\theta(q^{0})-\theta(-q^{0})\right)\hat{\delta}(q^{2}+m^{2})\,J^{\mu}(q)\,e^{iq\cdot x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ -ie\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(q)\,\big[J^{\mu}(q)\,e^{iq\cdot x}-J^{\mu}(-q)\,e^{-iq\cdot x}\big]\;.

(69)

In the case we are considering, the momentum space source term is given by

J^{\mu}(q)=\delta(q\cdot u)(mu^{\mu}+i\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}u_{\nu}q_{\rho})\;,

(70)

where
u^{\mu}=(1,0,0)
. Observe that
(J^{\mu}(q))^{\ast}=J^{\mu}(-q)
, and so eq. (
4.1
) can be expressed as

\tilde{F}_{\text{rad}}^{\mu}(x)=-2e\,\text{Re}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(q)\,iJ^{\mu}(q)\,e^{iq\cdot x}\;.

(71)

We can express
J^{\mu}(q)
in spinor form as

\displaystyle J_{\alpha\beta}=

\displaystyle\ J^{\mu}(q)\sigma_{\mu(\alpha\beta)}=\delta(q\cdot u)\big[mu_{(\alpha\beta)}+i\,u_{(\alpha\gamma}q^{\gamma}_{\;\;\beta)}\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{2m}\delta(q\cdot u)\big[\langle q|u|q\rangle|\bar{q}\rangle_{(\alpha}|\bar{q}\rangle_{\beta)}-2(q\cdot u)|q\rangle_{(\alpha}|\bar{q}\rangle_{\beta)}\big]=\frac{1}{4m}\delta(q\cdot u)\langle q|u|q\rangle|\bar{q}\rangle_{(\alpha}|\bar{q}\rangle_{\beta)}\;,

(72)

where we have made use of the following Schouten identities:

\displaystyle u_{(\alpha\beta)}=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{8m^{2}}\big[\langle\bar{q}|u|\bar{q}\rangle|q\rangle_{(\alpha}|q\rangle_{\beta)}+\langle q|u|q\rangle|\bar{q}\rangle_{(\alpha}|\bar{q}\rangle_{\beta)}-2(q\cdot u)|q\rangle_{(\alpha}|\bar{q}\rangle_{\beta)}\big]\;,

(73)

\displaystyle u_{(\alpha\gamma}q^{\gamma}_{\;\;\beta)}=

\displaystyle\ -\frac{i}{8m}\big[\langle\bar{q}|u|\bar{q}\rangle|q\rangle_{(\alpha}|q\rangle_{\beta)}-\langle q|u|q\rangle|\bar{q}\rangle_{(\alpha}|\bar{q}\rangle_{\beta)}+2(q\cdot u)|q\rangle_{(\alpha}|\bar{q}\rangle_{\beta)}\big]\;.

(74)

And as such, the corresponding Maxwell spinor is

\displaystyle\varphi_{\alpha\beta}(x)=

\displaystyle\ \tilde{F}_{\text{rad}}^{\mu}(x)\sigma_{\mu(\alpha\beta)}=-\frac{e}{m}\,\text{Re}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(q)\,i\delta(q\cdot u)\langle q|u|q\rangle|\bar{q}\rangle_{\alpha}|\bar{q}\rangle_{\beta}\,e^{iq\cdot x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{e}{m}\,\text{Re}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(q)\,i\delta(q\cdot u)\langle\bar{q}|u|\bar{q}\rangle|q\rangle_{\alpha}|q\rangle_{\beta}\,e^{-iq\cdot x}\;.

(75)

This is related to the amplitude formulation by noting that the static anyon amplitude is related via
\braket{\bar{q}|u|\bar{q}}=\frac{m}{eM}\mathcal{A}^{(3)}_{+}
.

4.2
Gravitational Curvature Spinor

For the gravity case, we can follow in direct analogy to the gauge theory construction. In this case, we consider the (de Donder gauge) field equation for the topologically massive graviton field
h_{\mu\nu}(x)
in the presence of a static gravitational source
T_{\mu\nu}=Mu_{\mu}u_{\nu}\,\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{x})
:

\left(\partial^{2}-m^{2}\right)\partial^{2}h_{\mu\nu}(x)=-\mathcal{T}_{\mu\nu}(x)\;,

(76)

where the effective energy-momentum tensor
\mathcal{T}_{\mu\nu}(x)
is given by

\mathcal{T}_{\mu\nu}(x)=2\kappa m^{2}\Big[T_{\mu\nu}(x)-\eta_{\mu\nu}T(x)-\frac{1}{2m}\partial_{\rho}\varepsilon^{\rho\lambda}_{\;\;\;(\mu}T_{\nu)\lambda}(x)+\frac{1}{2m^{2}}\left(\eta_{\mu\nu}\partial^{2}+\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}\right)T(x)\Big]\;.

(77)

We can recast (
76
) into a field equation for the Cotton tensor:

(\partial^{2}-m^{2})C_{\mu\nu}(x)=-\frac{\kappa}{4}\partial_{\rho}\varepsilon^{\rho\sigma}_{\;\;\;(\mu}\mathcal{T}_{\nu)\sigma}(x)\;,

(78)

where
C_{\mu\nu}=-\frac{\kappa}{4}\,\partial_{\lambda}\varepsilon^{\lambda\sigma}_{\;\;\;(\mu}\partial^{2}h_{\nu)\sigma}
in the de Donder gauge.

Given this, we can then define the radiative part of the Cotton tensor as

\displaystyle C^{\text{rad}}_{\mu\nu}(x)=

\displaystyle\ -\frac{\kappa}{4}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}y\,G_{\text{rad}}(x-y)\,\partial_{\rho}\varepsilon^{\rho\sigma}_{\;\;\;(\mu}\mathcal{T}_{\nu)\sigma}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ -\frac{\kappa}{4}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(q)\,q_{\rho}\varepsilon^{\rho\sigma}_{\;\;\;(\mu}\big[\mathcal{T}_{\nu)\sigma}(q)\,e^{iq\cdot x}+\mathcal{T}_{\nu)\sigma}(-q)\,e^{-iq\cdot x}\big]\;.

(79)

In momentum space, we have that
T_{\mu\nu}(q)=M\,\delta(q\cdot u)u_{\mu}u_{\nu}
, and so the effective energy-momentum tensor is

\mathcal{T}_{\mu\nu}(q)=2\kappa m^{2}M\,\delta(q\cdot u)\Big[u_{\mu}u_{\nu}+\eta_{\mu\nu}+\frac{i}{2m}\varepsilon(q,u)_{(\mu}u_{\nu)}+\frac{1}{2m^{2}}\left(q^{2}\eta_{\mu\nu}+q_{\mu}q_{\nu}\right)\Big]\;,

(80)

such that

\displaystyle q_{\rho}\varepsilon^{\rho\sigma}_{\;\;\;(\mu}\mathcal{T}_{\nu)\sigma}(q)=

\displaystyle\ 2\kappa m^{2}M\,\delta(q\cdot u)\,q_{\rho}\varepsilon^{\rho\sigma}_{\;\;\;(\mu}\Big[u_{\nu)}u_{\sigma}+\frac{i}{2m}\left(\varepsilon(q,u)_{\nu)}u_{\sigma}+u_{\nu)}\varepsilon(q,u)_{\sigma}\right)\Big]\;.

(81)

As is evident from this equation, we can neglect any terms proportional to
\eta_{\mu\nu}
or
q_{\mu}q_{\nu}
since they will vanish in the Cotton tensor. Thus, retaining only the terms that survive upon contraction with
q_{\rho}\varepsilon^{\rho\mu\nu}
, the spinorial form of
\mathcal{T}_{\mu\nu}(q)
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(q)=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{4}\mathcal{T}_{\mu\nu}(q)\sigma^{\mu}_{(\alpha\beta)}\sigma^{\nu}_{(\gamma\delta)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ -i\kappa mM\,\delta(q\cdot u)\big[\big(imu_{(\alpha\beta)}-q_{(\alpha\epsilon}u^{\epsilon}_{\;\;\beta)}\big)u_{(\gamma\delta)}+\big(imu_{(\gamma\delta)}-q_{(\gamma\epsilon}u^{\epsilon}_{\;\;\delta)}\big)u_{(\alpha\beta)}\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ -\frac{i\kappa M}{4}\,\delta(q\cdot u)\,\langle\bar{q}|u|\bar{q}\rangle\big[|q\rangle_{(\alpha}|q\rangle_{\beta)}u_{(\gamma\delta)}+|q\rangle_{(\gamma}|q\rangle_{\delta)}u_{(\alpha\beta)}\big]\;.

(82)

Given this, we can construct the spinorial form of (
81
), the momentum space Cotton spinor

\displaystyle\Phi_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(q)=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{4}q_{\rho}\varepsilon^{\rho\sigma}_{\;\;\;(\mu}\mathcal{T}_{\nu)\sigma}(q)\sigma^{\mu}_{(\alpha\beta)}\sigma^{\nu}_{(\gamma\delta)}+(\alpha\leftrightarrow\gamma,\beta\leftrightarrow\delta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{2}\big[q_{(\alpha\epsilon}\mathcal{T}^{\epsilon}_{\;\;\beta)\gamma\delta}+q_{(\gamma\epsilon}\mathcal{T}^{\epsilon}_{\;\;\delta)\alpha\beta}\big]+(\alpha\leftrightarrow\gamma,\beta\leftrightarrow\delta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ -\frac{\kappa M}{4m}\,\delta(q\cdot u)\big[\langle\bar{q}|u|\bar{q}\rangle^{2}|q\rangle_{\alpha}|q\rangle_{\beta}|q\rangle_{\gamma}|q\rangle_{\delta}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\langle\bar{q}|u|\bar{q}\rangle\langle q|u|q\rangle\big(|q\rangle_{\alpha}|q\rangle_{\beta}|\bar{q}\rangle_{\gamma}|\bar{q}\rangle_{\delta}-|q\rangle_{\gamma}|q\rangle_{\delta}|\bar{q}\rangle_{\alpha}|\bar{q}\rangle_{\beta}\big)\big]+(\alpha\leftrightarrow\gamma,\beta\leftrightarrow\delta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ -\frac{\kappa M}{2m}\,\delta(q\cdot u)\,\langle\bar{q}|u|\bar{q}\rangle^{2}|q\rangle_{\alpha}|q\rangle_{\beta}|q\rangle_{\gamma}|q\rangle_{\delta}\;.

(83)

It follows that the position space Cotton spinor
\Phi_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(x)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Phi_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(x)=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{4}C^{\text{rad}}_{\mu\nu}(x)\sigma^{\mu}_{(\alpha\beta)}\sigma^{\nu}_{(\gamma\delta)}=-\frac{\kappa}{2}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(q)\,\big[\Phi_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(q)\,e^{iq\cdot x}+(\Phi_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(q))^{\ast}\,e^{-iq\cdot x}\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ -\kappa\,\text{Re}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(q)\,\Phi_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(q)\,e^{iq\cdot x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{\kappa^{2}M}{4m}\,\text{Re}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(q)\,\delta(q\cdot u)\,\langle\bar{q}|u|\bar{q}\rangle^{2}|q\rangle_{\alpha}|q\rangle_{\beta}|q\rangle_{\gamma}|q\rangle_{\delta}\,e^{iq\cdot x}\;,

(84)

which precisely matches the amplitude calculation in eq. (
47
).

4.3
Reality and Dimensional Reduction Revisited

In section
2
we alluded to the fact that dimensional reduction can be more complicated for symmetric spinors with many indices, such as the Weyl spinor. However, scattering amplitudes expressed in spinor helicity notation are functions of momentum spinors only, which can be simply reduced using the operator
\chi^{\alpha\dot{\alpha}}
defined in (
15
). The general prescription for converting massless spinor helicity variables in 4D to their massive equivalents in 3D is simply

\lambda^{\text{3D}}_{\alpha}=\lambda^{\text{4D}}_{\alpha}\bigg|_{p_{2}=m},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \bar{\lambda}^{\text{3D}}_{\alpha}=\tilde{\lambda}^{\text{4D}}_{\dot{\alpha}}\chi^{\dot{\alpha}}_{\penalty\ \alpha}\bigg|_{p_{2}=m},

(85)

or, in terms of the spinor products,

\braket{ij}_{3D}=\braket{ij}_{4D}\bigg|_{p_{2}\rightarrow m},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \braket{\bar{i}\bar{j}}_{3D}=[ij]_{4D}\bigg|_{p_{2}\rightarrow m}.

(86)

However, we note that there is a significant problem with either
\Phi_{\alpha\beta}
or
\Psi_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}
expressed in their current forms above: they vanish for real momentum. This is precisely the same issue as in four dimensional Minkowski space, and the reason is the same - there is no way to solve the on-shell condition if the momentum is real. If we consider
u^{\mu}=(1,0,0,0)
in four dimensional Minkowski space, then the on-shell condition together with the energy-conserving delta function gives

\displaystyle\delta(q\cdot u)\delta(q^{2})\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \implies\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ q_{1}^{2}+q_{2}^{2}+q_{3}^{2}=0.

(87)

For real momentum, the only solution is
q_{1}^{2}=q_{2}^{2}=q_{3}^{2}=0
. The solution to this problem in four dimensions was to go to split signature, where the on-shell condition can be solved for real momentum as
q_{2}^{2}=q_{1}^{2}+q_{3}^{2}
. In three dimensions, there is no concept of split signature, however for massive particles there is another trick we can use: we can analytically continue the mass to be imaginary. In three dimensions, the on-shell condition is

\displaystyle\delta(q_{0})\delta(q^{2}+m^{2})\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \implies\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ q_{1}^{2}+q_{2}^{2}=-m^{2}.

(88)

This can be solved either for complex (or imaginary) momentum
or
for
m\in i\mathbb{R}
. Recall that we can dimensionally reduce along the imaginary direction in spin-space, the same direction that we might choose to analytically continue to arrive at
(2,2)
. The interpretation for this imaginary mass is then clear: it is the result of a dimensional reduction from
(2,2)
signature rather than
(1,3)
. In spinor form, this simply means taking

\displaystyle\braket{ij}_{3D}=\braket{ij}_{4D}\bigg|_{p_{2}\rightarrow-im},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \braket{\bar{i}\bar{j}}_{3D}=[ij]_{4D}\bigg|_{p_{2}\rightarrow-im},

(89)

where the four-dimensional spinors are massless Minkowski space spinors. If we are already working with
(2,2)
spinors, then we can simply taking
p_{2}=-m
to get the same result. Overall this has a similar effect as in four dimensional split signature: the spinors and barred spinors (and polarization vectors) are completely real, now related by
m\rightarrow-m
rather than complex conjugation. For real spinors, the polarization vectors are

\epsilon_{-}^{\mu}(q)=-\frac{\braket{q|\gamma^{\mu}|q}}{2m},\quad\epsilon_{+}^{\mu}(q)=\frac{\braket{\bar{q}|\gamma^{\mu}|\bar{q}}}{2m}\quad.

(90)

With this form of the dimensional reduction in mind, we can now relate our findings to four dimensions easily. In four dimensions with split signature, the negative helicity momentum space Maxwell spinor is given by
Monteiro:2020plf

\displaystyle\braket{\phi_{\alpha\beta}(q)}=-\sqrt{2}|q\rangle_{\alpha}|q\rangle_{\beta}\mathcal{A}_{+}^{(3)}(q).

(91)

Let’s consider the amplitude for a charged scalar, which is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{+}^{(3)}(q)=-2ep\cdot\epsilon_{+}(q)=-\sqrt{2}e\frac{[q|p\ket{\eta}}{\braket{q\eta}}.

(92)

Under a dimensional reduction using
\chi^{\alpha\dot{\alpha}}\chi^{\dot{\beta}}_{\alpha}=\epsilon^{\dot{\alpha}\dot{\beta}}
along with
\chi|q]=\ket{\bar{q}}
and
\chi^{\dot{\alpha}}_{(\beta}p_{\alpha)\dot{\alpha}}=2p_{\alpha\beta}
, this becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{+}^{(3)}(q)=-2ep\cdot\epsilon_{+}(q)=-\sqrt{2}e\frac{\braket{\bar{q}|p|\eta}}{\braket{q\eta}}.

(93)

Choosing

5
Discussion

We have established the idea of the Cotton double copy, which relates curvature spinors in both topologically massive gauge theory and topologically massive gravity. Rather pleasingly, we have shown that the vacuum expectation value of the curvature spinors in both theories can be expressed in terms of on-shell three-particle amplitudes involving a charged (massive) particle emitting a topologically massive gauge boson or graviton, where the gauge boson has imaginary mass. This follows in direct analogy to the four-dimensional double copy, as explored in
Monteiro:2020plf
, and indeed, we have shown that these quantities can easily be derived via a dimensional reduction from four dimensions, specifically by taking the spinor-helicity form of Coulomb or Schwarzchild three-particle amplitudes and setting a spacetime direction to be constant with imaginary mass.

Since the three-particle amplitudes double copy, the momentum space curvature spinors inherit this double copy, and the (dual) Maxwell spinor double copies to the Cotton spinor. We also provided an example of this in position space – many other examples of the position space Cotton double copy, including in curved spacetimes, can be found in a paper to be released concurrently by M. Carrillo-González, A. Momeni and J. Rumbutis
cottondcwaves
. While we have only worked perturbatively in this paper, it is highly likely that a non-perturbative coherent state construction is plausible, as was found in
Monteiro:2020plf
.

It is clear that like its four-dimensional counterpart, the Cotton double copy presents a compelling avenue of research. In future studies, it would be interesting to further explore its implications in position space — especially for type D spacetimes, which ought to be captured by the amplitudes above. One interesting idea is to formulate three-dimensional electromagnetic and gravitational shockwave solutions in terms of amplitudes, in analogy to the four-dimensional set-up recently explored in
Cristofoli:2020hnk
, as these have also been shown to obey the Cotton double copy
cottondcwaves
.
