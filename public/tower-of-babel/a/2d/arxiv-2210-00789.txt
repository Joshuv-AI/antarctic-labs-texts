Nested Sequents for First-Order Modal Logics via Reachability Rules
Tim S. Lyon

Abstract
We introduce the first cut-free nested sequent systems for first-order modal logics that admit increasing, decreasing, constant, and empty domains along with so-called general path conditions and seriality. We obtain such systems by means of two devices: reachability rules and structural refinement . Regarding the former device, we introduce reachability rules as special logical rules parameterized with formal grammars (viz. types of semi-Thue systems) that operate by propagating formulae and/or checking if data exists along certain paths within a nested sequent, where paths are encoded as strings generated by a parameterizing grammar. Regarding the latter device, structural refinement is a relatively new methodology used to extract nested sequent systems from labeled systems (which are ultimately obtained from a semantics) by means of eliminating structural/relational rules, introducing reachability rules, and then carrying out a notational translation. We therefore demonstrate how this method can be extended to the setting of first-order modal logics, and expose how reachability rules naturally arise from applying this method.

Introduction

Analytic proof systems consist of sets of inference rules that stepwise (de)compose logical theorems. Such proof systems not only facilitate reasoning within a given logic, but have proven useful for establishing (meta-)logical properties of logics as well as for developing automated reasoning tools. One of the most prominent formalisms for constructing analytic proof systems is Gentzen’s sequent calculus
[
15
,
16
]
, which utilizes (pairs of) multisets of formulae, called
sequents
, to carry out proofs of logical theorems. Since its introduction, Gentzen’s formalism has been generalized to supply ever more expressive logics with analytic proof systems. Such extensions include
display calculi

[
2
]
,
hypersequent calculi

[
1
]
,
labeled calculi

[
36
,
38
]
, and
nested calculi

[
5
,
22
]
, all of which have been exploited to establish meaningful results such as decidability
[
4
,
37
]
, interpolation
[
11
,
26
]
, complexity hardness
[
29
]
, and automated counter-model extraction
[
27
,
37
]
. In this paper, we will be concerned with the labeled and nested formalisms.

Labeled sequent calculi generalize Gentzen’s formalism by integrating semantic elements of a logic into the the syntax of sequents (cf.
[
21
]
). As such, labeled sequents can typically be viewed as
graphs
of Gentzen-style sequents, that is, graphs of (pairs of) multisets of logical formulae. This idea of defining inference rules over graphs of sequents has proven fruitful for the attainment and definition of sequent-style systems for diverse and sizable classes of logics
[
13
,
31
,
36
,
38
]
. Even more, sequent systems built within the labeled formalism exhibit a high degree of modularity, typically possess favorable proof-theoretic properties (e.g. invertibility of rules and cut-elimination), and algorithms exist that build labeled calculi for certain classes of logics on demand
[
8
]
. In spite of these desirable characteristics, the labeled formalism has certain drawbacks; in particular, labeled proof systems usually involve an unnecessarily complicated syntax (which incorporates redundant structures), include superfluous inference rules, and it is often cumbersome to establish the termination of associated proof search algorithms.

By contrast, the nested sequent formalism only employs
trees
of Gentzen-style sequents. Having originally been introduced by Bull
[
5
]
and Kashima
[
22
]
, and further expanded upon by Brünnler
[
4
]
and Poggiolesi
[
34
]
, the formalism of nested sequents avoids the pitfalls of labeled sequents, often yielding proof calculi that require less bureaucracy, are more compact, and where termination of proof search is more easily obtained. Moreover, nested sequent systems have a range of applications, being used in knowledge integration algorithms
[
29
]
, serving as a basis for constructive interpolation and decidability techniques
[
11
,
26
,
25
,
37
]
, and even being used to solve open questions about axiomatizability
[
20
]
.

More recently, it was discovered that nested sequent calculi could be extracted from labeled sequent calculi with the properties of the former transferred to the latter
[
7
,
19
,
33
]
. This discovery led to the formulation of a methodology referred to as
structural refinement

[
24
,
25
,
27
,
28
]
, which consists of two parts: first, one leverages general results regarding the (automated) construction of labeled sequent calculi (in possession of favorable properties) for a class of logics based on their semantics. Second, one transforms the labeled calculi into nested calculi via the introduction of
propagation
or
reachability rules
(cf.
[
6
,
9
,
25
]
) and the elimination of structural rules, followed by a notational translation. This entire process yields a method of transforming the semantics of a logic into a (cut-free) nested sequent calculus for the logic, being used to provide new and previously unknown nested sequent calculi
[
28
]
. Although this method has been investigated and put to use for a variety of different
propositional
modal logics, it has yet to be applied to
first-order
modal logics. Thus, the first contribution of this paper is the application and study of structural refinement in the setting of
first-order
modal logics, being used to obtain (to the best of the author’s knowledge) the first (cut-free) nested sequent systems for such logics. Interestingly, this method ‘naturally’ produces a generalized version of nested sequents, which include collections of terms (called
signatures
) in their syntax, used to process quantificational data.

As in the propositional setting, first-order modal logics admit a Kripke-style semantics, but unlike in the propositional setting, each world is associated with a domain of elements used to interpret quantificational formulae
[
23
]
. One may then characterize distinct first-order modal logics by either imposing frame conditions on the accessibility relation of the model (as with propositional modal logics), or by imposing conditions on the domains associated with worlds
[
3
,
12
]
. Regarding the latter, one obtains distinct first-order modal logics depending on if domains are permitted to be empty, if domains are permitted to increase or decrease along the accessibility relation, or if domains are required to be constant. In this paper, we consider a class of first-order modal logics that consists of extensions of a base logic
\mathsf{QK}

[
3
]
with seriality, general path conditions
[
7
]
, and the aforementioned domain conditions.

In order to give this class of logics a uniform nested sequent presentation, we make use of so-called
reachability rules
, which possess two kinds of functionality: such rules may (i) propagate formulae along certain paths, and/or (ii) search for data along certain paths, within a labeled or nested sequent. The first kind of functionality is exhibited by the well-known class of
propagation rules

[
6
,
9
,
18
]
, which have proven vital for the provision of nested sequent systems for propositional modal and constructive logics
[
7
,
17
,
18
,
27
]
. Rules exhibiting the second kind of functionality have only been defined more recently
[
10
,
24
,
25
]
. Thus, reachability rules subsume the class of propagation rules, allowing for either or both kinds of functionality
[
25
]
.

Our second contribution in this paper is the formulation of reachability rules for managing the introduction of quantificational formulae in labeled and nested sequent proofs. Motivated by the work in
[
18
,
27
]
, we parameterize such rules with (restricted versions of) semi-Thue systems
[
35
]
, which encode along what paths formulae may be propagated and along what paths a rule should search for data. Such rules endow our nested sequent systems with a high degree of modularity as one nested calculus for a first-order modal logic may be straightforwardly converted into a nested calculus for another first-order modal logic by simply parameterizing the reachability rules with alternative semi-Thue systems.

The paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
, we define the considered class of first-order modal logics, as well as provide the grammar theoretical foundations for the formulation of our reachability rules. In Section
3
, we present labeled sequent systems for first-order modal logics based on those of
[
32
, Chapter 12.1]
and
[
38
, Chapter 6]
. In the subsequent section (Section
4
), we show how to define reachability rules in the context of labeled sequents, and apply the structural refinement methodology to derive nested sequent calculi from the given labeled calculi. In Section
5
, we present the first (cut-free) nested sequent calculi for first-order modal logics, and in the final section (Section
6
) we conclude.

2.1
First-order Modal Logics

We let
\mathrm{Var}:=\{x,y,z,\ldots\}
be a denumerable set of

\Upphi:=\{p,q,r,\ldots\}
be a set of denumerably many
predicates
of each arity

with

predicates of arity zero.
Atomic formulae
are of form

\varphi::=p(\vec{x})\ |\ \neg\varphi\ |\ \varphi\lor\varphi\ |\ \Diamond\varphi\ |\ \exists x\varphi

where

\Upphi
, and the variables
\vec{x}=x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
and

. We refer to a formula in

as an
\mathcal{L}
-formula
, and define

,

, and

. As usual, we say that the occurrence of a variable

in

given that

does not occur within the scope of a quantifier. Also, we let

denote the substitution of the variable

in
\varphi
.

2.2
Grammar-theoretic Preliminaries

As will be seen later on, a significant aspect of our nested calculi is the incorporation of inference (viz. reachability) rules whose applicability depends upon certain strings generated by a formal grammar. Therefore, the current section introduces the grammar-theoretic notions required to properly define such rules.

We define our
alphabet

\Upsigma:=\{\Diamond,\blacklozenge\}
to be the set of
characters

\Diamond
and
\blacklozenge
, and we let
\langle?\rangle\in\Upsigma
. We make use of the symbols
\Diamond
and
\blacklozenge
to encode information in inference rules about worlds in the
future
and
past
(resp.) of an accessibility relation
R
in a model. We note that these symbols were adapted from the work in
[
18
]
, and their usage mirrors their analogous meaning in the context of tense logics
[
18
,
22
]
. Additionally, we say that
\Diamond
and
\blacklozenge
are
converses
, and define the
converse operation

{\cdot}^{-1}
(on characters) as
{\Diamond}^{-1}:=\blacklozenge
and
{\blacklozenge}^{-1}:=\Diamond
.

We let
\cdot
denote the typical
concatenation operation
with
\varepsilon
the
empty string
. The set
\Upsigma^{*}
of
strings over
\Upsigma
is defined to be the smallest set satisfying the following conditions: (i)
\Upsigma\cup\{\varepsilon\}\subseteq\Upsigma^{*}
, and (ii)
\text{If }s\in\Upsigma^{*}\text{ and }\langle?\rangle\in\Upsigma\text{, then }s\cdot\langle?\rangle\in\Upsigma^{*}
. We use
s
,
t
,
r
,
\ldots
(potentially annotated) to denote strings from
\Upsigma^{*}
. Also, we will often write the concatenation of two strings
s
and
t
as
st
as opposed to
s\cdot t
, and for the empty string
\varepsilon
, we have
s\varepsilon=\varepsilon s=s
. The converse operation on strings (adapted from
[
37
]
) is defined on strings accordingly: (i)
{\varepsilon}^{-1}:=\varepsilon
, and (ii)
\text{If }s=\langle?\rangle_{1}\cdots\langle?\rangle_{n}\text{, then }{s}^{-1}:={\langle?\rangle}^{-1}_{n}\cdots{\langle?\rangle}^{-1}_{1}
.

We now define
\Upsigma
-systems
, which are special kinds of
semi-Thue systems

[
35
]
that encode information about the conditions imposed on frames and models, and which will parameterize inference rules in our proof systems later on.

Definition 3

(
\Upsigma
-System)
.

We define a
\Upsigma
-system
to be a set
\mathrm{S}
of

of the form
\langle?\rangle\longrightarrow s
, where
\langle?\rangle\in\Upsigma
and

.

Specific
\Upsigma
-systems will be of use to us in this paper. In particular, for a set
\mathcal{G}
of generalized path conditions, we define the
\Upsigma
-system

as follows:

iff

.
We also define the
\Upsigma
-systems
\mathrm{S4}:=\{\Diamond\longrightarrow\varepsilon,\blacklozenge\longrightarrow\varepsilon,\Diamond\longrightarrow\Diamond\Diamond,\blacklozenge\longrightarrow\blacklozenge\blacklozenge\}
and
\mathrm{S5}:=\{\Diamond\longrightarrow\varepsilon,\blacklozenge\longrightarrow\varepsilon,\Diamond\longrightarrow\blacklozenge\Diamond,\blacklozenge\longrightarrow\blacklozenge\Diamond\}

Definition 4

(Derivation, Language)
.

Let
\mathrm{S}
be a
\Upsigma
-system. We write

and say that the string

may be derived from the string

in
\Upsigma^{*}

and
\langle?\rangle\longrightarrow r\in S

and

. We define the

\longrightarrow^{*}_{\mathrm{S}}
to be the reflexive and transitive closure of
\longrightarrow
. For

, we call

a

from
s
, and define the

of a derivation to be the minimal number of one-step derivations required to derive

from
s
in
\mathrm{S}
. Last, we define the

L_{\mathrm{S}}(s):=\{t\ |\ s\longrightarrow^{*}_{\mathrm{S}}t\}
, where

.

As noted in
[
25
, p. 143]
,
\mathrm{S4}
encodes a notion of

reachability and
\mathrm{S5}
encodes a notion of

reachability. Given a frame, recognizing what worlds are reachable from one another along paths of the accessibility relation
R
is relevant in our context as frame conditions such as

connect initial and terminal worlds, and
I_{d}
and

require domains to contain certain elements along

\mathrm{S4}
and
\mathrm{S5}
encode notions of (un)directed reachability, let us think of
\Diamond
as representing a forward move along the accessibility relation of a model, and
\blacklozenge
as representing a backward move along the accessibility relation. Then,
\mathrm{S4}
defines the languages
L_{\mathrm{S4}}(\Diamond)=\{\Diamond^{n}\ |\ n\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
L_{\mathrm{S4}}(\blacklozenge)=\{\blacklozenge^{n}\ |\ n\in\mathbb{N}\}
, where each string
\Diamond^{n}
and
\blacklozenge^{n}
(denoting a sequence of

’s and

’s, respectively, with
\Diamond^{0}=\blacklozenge^{0}=\varepsilon
) ‘connects’ a world

and

if

can be reached in

. Similarly,
\mathrm{S5}
defines the language
L_{\mathrm{S5}}(\Diamond)=L_{\mathrm{S5}}(\blacklozenge)=\Upsigma^{*}
where each string
\langle?\rangle_{1}\cdots\langle?\rangle_{n}
‘connects’ a world

to

can be reached in

.

3
Labeled Sequent Systems

We present labeled sequent systems within our logical signature, based on those of
[
38
, Chapter 6]
and
[
32
, Chapter 12.1]
, for first-order modal logics. These systems utilize a denumerable set
\mathrm{Lab}:=\{w,u,v,\ldots\}

Let
\Gamma

\mathcal{L}
-formulae,

be a multiset of variables called a

be a set of labels. A

\Phi
is recursively defined as:

1.

Each

of the form
\mathrm{X};\Gamma\vdash\Delta
is a nested sequent;

2.

Any expression of the form

In the context of nested sequents, we use

\mathcal{L}
-formulae,

-formulae. We use

Observe that every nested sequent of the above form encodes a

whose nodes are flat sequents, i.e. each nested sequent

-component

6
Conclusion

In this work, we unified first-order modal logics in a single nested sequent framework by means of reachability rules. These rules, which may propagate formulae and/or check for data along paths within a nested sequent, permit one to toggle between the various logics by parameterizing rules with distinct semi-Thue systems. We used such rules to provide the first cut-free nested sequent systems for first-order modal logics.

In future work, we aim to investigate how reachability rules can be leveraged to provide nested systems for broader classes of logics. This can be accomplished in multiple ways. For instance, we could investigate the effect of parameterizing reachability rules with alternative semi-Thue systems, or through the introduction of new logical connectives (e.g. converse modalities). Second, we aim to explore the formulation of reachability rules for alternative quantifiers; e.g. Miller and Tiu’s nabla quantifier
\nabla

[
30
]
or the Gabbay-Pitts

\mathsf{N}

quantifier
[
14
]
. Moreover, we aim to investigate the properties of our nested systems such as syntactic cut-elimination, invertibility of rules, and the admissibility of structural rules (e.g. contraction and weakening), which are conjectured to hold.
Last, such systems may prove well-suited for identifying decidable fragments of first-order modal (and related) logics by means of proof-search algorithms.
