Abstract
. The present paper introduces a modified version of cyclic-monotone independence which originally arose in the context of random matrices, and also introduces its natural analogy called cyclic-Boolean independence. We investigate formulas for convolutions, limit theorems for sums of independent random variables, and also classify infinitely divisible distributions with respect to cyclic-Boolean convolution. Finally, we provide applications to the eigenvalues of the adjacency matrices of iterated star products of graphs and also iterated comb products of graphs.

1.
Introduction

The present paper takes its origin in the concept of
cyclic-monotone
independence
which appeared in the study of random matrices

[
5
,
20
]
and which deserves separate treatment; see
[
2
]
for further work.
The term “cyclic-monotone independence” was coined in
[
5
]
because of its apparent similarity with monotone independence except that it involves two linear functionals: a state and a tracial linear functional. It abstracts an asymptotic formula for the mixed moments, with respect to the non-normalized trace, of a random rotation of two sets
\mathbf{A}^{N}
and
\mathbf{B}^{N}
consisting of
N\times N
deterministic matrices such that all mixed moments of
\mathbf{A}^{N}
have finite limits with respect to the
non-normalized trace
as
N
tends to infinity, and all mixed moments of
\mathbf{B}^{N}
have finite limits with respect to the
normalized trace
. More precisely, suppose that
\{A^{N}_{i}:1\leq i\leq k\}
and
\{B^{N}_{i}:1\leq i\leq k\},N=1,2,3,...
are families of
N\times N
deterministic matrices that satisfy the following conditions: for any
\ast
-polynomial
P(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k})
in non-commuting variables
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k}
over the field
\mathbb{C}
without a constant term (e.g.
P(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}^{2}x_{2}x_{1}^{*}
), the limits

\lim_{N\to\infty}{\rm Tr}_{N}[P(A_{1}^{N},A_{2}^{N},\dots,A_{k}^{N})]\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}{\rm Tr}_{N}[P(B_{1}^{N},B_{2}^{N},\dots,B_{k}^{N})]

exist in
\mathbb{C}
. According to
[
5
, Theorem 4.3]
, for an
N\times N
Haar unitary matrix
U^{N},N=1,2,3,\dots
, we have the almost sure convergence

(1.1)

\begin{split}&\lim_{N\to\infty}{\rm Tr}_{N}[A_{1}^{N}(U^{N})^{*}B_{1}^{N}U^{N}A_{2}^{N}(U^{N})^{*}B_{2}^{N}U^{N}\cdots A_{k}^{N}(U^{N})^{*}B_{k}^{N}U^{N}]\\
&\qquad=\lim_{N\to\infty}{\rm Tr}_{N}[A_{1}^{N}A_{2}^{N}\cdots A_{k}^{N}]\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left[\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}{\rm Tr}_{N}[B_{j}^{N}]\right].\end{split}

The formula (
1.1
) shows some similarity with monotone independence, but
they are not the same because the formula involves both normalized trace and
non-normalized trace.

The present paper offers a simple operator model for cyclic-monotone
independence realized on the tensor product of Hilbert spaces. This
construction also uncovers the associativity of cyclic-monotone independence
with respect to a state and a trace. In order to ensure associativity, we modify the definition of cyclic-monotone independence. The new definition consists of two conditions: one is basically the condition in
[
5
, Definition 3.2]
referring to both the state and the tracial linear functional, and the other is monotone independence with respect to the state (see Definition
7.2
).
The modified definition of cyclic-monotone independence shares the same spirit with c-monotone independence
[
10
]
(and c-freeness
[
3
]
) because they are all associative notions of independence referring to two linear functionals.

However, the relationship between the random matrix model and the operator
model is not perfectly understood. Curiously monotone independence does
not appear in the random matrix model above, although it appears very
naturally in the operator model. This is related to the fact that the random
matrix model above is limited to two families of random matrices
\mathbf{A}^{N}

and
\mathbf{B}^{N}
and hence the question of associativity is not relevant.

Our operator realization of (modified) cyclic-monotone independence also
indicates that a similar construction works for Boolean independence, which
therefore leads to a notion of
cyclic-Boolean independence
. We develop a
general theory of these two independences: computing generating functions for
the sum of independent random variables, limit theorems, cyclic-Boolean
cumulants which are governed by cyclic-interval partitions and infinitely
divisible distributions with respect to cyclic-Boolean convolution. We do not
know how to define cyclic-monotone cumulants and therefore this question is not
addressed in the present paper.

Moreover, the operator models for cyclic-Boolean and cyclic-monotone independences are directly connected to the star product of (rooted) graphs (see Section
2.6
)
and the comb product of (rooted) graphs (see Section
2.7
).
Specifically, the eigenvalues of their adjacency matrices can be analyzed by means of cyclic-Boolean independence and cyclic-monotone independence, respectively.

The techniques are motivated by the relations between the adjacency matrix, the spectrum, the characteristic polynomial and walk generating functions
of a graph. These form the core subject of
algebraic graph theory, which deals with various matrices, polynomials and
generating functions and other invariants carrying information about graphs.

It was shown by Schwenk
[
19
]
(later generalized by
Godsil and McKay
[
9
]
)
that the characteristic polynomial
of the star product (or coalescence)
and the comb product (or rooted product)
of graphs
only depends on the characteristic polynomials of the factors and the walk
generating function at the roots of the factors and he gave an explicit formula.
Similar simple formulas for the generating function of closed walks
starting at the root hold.
While Schwenk’s proofs are combinatorial, we will give algebraic proofs based
on the Schur complement which can be generalized to arbitrary matrices and operators.
Accardi, Ben Ghorbal and Obata
[
1
]
and Obata
[
15
]

initiated the application of monotone independence and Boolean independence to the asymptotic spectral analysis of adjacency matrices of iterated comb products and star products, respectively. The operator models for cyclic-monotone and cyclic-Boolean independences extend their work in the sense that the new framework also enables one to analyze refined properties of eigenvalues of the adjacency matrices.
These generalizations are the subject of the present paper and illustrate
the emergence of new notions of noncommutative independence, i.e., cyclic-monotone and cyclic-Boolean ones.

To summarize, the main contributions of the present paper are:

(1)

the new notion of cyclic-Boolean independence (Sections
3
) and a
modification of the definition of cyclic-monotone independence given in
[
5
]
(Section
7
);

(2)

operator models for cyclic-Boolean independence (Sections
3
) and for cyclic-monotone independence (Section
7
);

(3)

convolution formulas for the sum of independent random variables (Sections
4
,
7
) and their relationships to algebraic graph theory (Section
2
);

(4)

limit theorems for sums of independent random variables (Sections
3
,
7
);

(5)

cyclic-Boolean cumulants and the relevant partition structure with cyclic-interval partitions (Section
5
);

(6)

classification of infinitely divisible distributions for cyclic-Boolean convolution (Section
6
);

(7)

analysis of the asymptotics of the eigenvalues of the adjacency matrices of iterated star product of graphs and iterated comb product of graphs (Sections
4
,
7
).

Recently Collins, Leid and Sakuma found a different matrix model for monotone independence and cyclic monotone independence
[
6
]
. So far a connection between their model and ours is not clear.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Adjacency matrix

Let
\mathbf{\Gamma}=(V,E)
be a graph on a vertex set
V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{d}\}

with edge set
E
.
We always consider finite undirected graphs without loops
or multiple edges. An edge between two vertices
u
and
v
is denoted by
uv
. The
adjacency matrix
of
\mathbf{\Gamma}
is the matrix
A=\{a_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{d}

with entries

a_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,&v_{i}v_{j}\in E,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The
spectrum of the graph
\mathbf{\Gamma}
is the spectrum of its adjacency matrix. It consists of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
of
A
which are the roots
of the
characteristic polynomial

\phi_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(x)=\det(xI-A)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\lambda_{i}).

Alternatively, the eigenvalues of
A
are the poles of the (tracial)

Cauchy transform

(2.1)

\Cauchy_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(z)={\rm Tr}((zI-A)^{-1}).

The Cauchy transform and the characteristic polynomial
are mutually related by the logarithmic derivative

(2.2)

\Cauchy_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{z-\lambda_{i}}=\frac{d}{dz}\log\phi_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(z)=\frac{\phi_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\prime}(z)}{\phi_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(z)}.

For the generalization of this identity to trace class operators
it will be convenient to remove the moment of order zero and work with the “renormalized” Cauchy transform

(2.3)

\RC_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(z)=\Cauchy_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(z)-\frac{d}{z}={\rm Tr}((zI-A)^{-1}-z^{-1}I)

instead.

2.2.
Walk generating functions

Let
(\mathbf{\Gamma},o)
be a finite rooted graph, i.e.,
a graph on vertices
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{d}
where we single
out the vertex
o=v_{1}
as the root of the graph.
The number
m_{n}
of closed walks of length
n
starting at the root
o
is equal to
\langle A^{n}e_{1},e_{1}\rangle

where
A
is the adjacency matrix of
\mathbf{\Gamma}
and
e_{1}
is the vector
(1,0,0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
Denote by

(2.4)

M_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}m_{n}z^{n}=\langle(I-zA)^{-1}e_{1},e_{1}\rangle

the
walk generating function
.
One caution is in place here.
To keep notation simple here and below we do not explicitly
write the root in subscripts,
although the generating functions
depend on the choice of the root.

It will be more convenient to rather work with the resolvent of
A
and with the
Green function
(evaluated at the root)

(2.5)

G_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(z)=\langle(zI-A)^{-1}e_{1},e_{1}\rangle=\frac{1}{z}M_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)

and its reciprocal

(2.6)

F_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(z)=\frac{1}{G_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(z)}.

We can obtain a relation between the Green function
(
2.5
) and the Cauchy transform (
2.1
)
from the
Schur complement
.

2.3.
Schur complement

Let

M=\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix}

be a block matrix and assume
D
is invertible.
Then the
Schur complement

[
24
]

is defined as

(2.7)

M/D=A-BD^{-1}C.

It appears in
Aitken’s factorization

(2.8)

M=\begin{bmatrix}I&BD^{-1}\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}A-BD^{-1}C&0\\
0&D\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
D^{-1}C&I\end{bmatrix}.

which is obtained by Gaussian elimination on the original Matrix
M
.
From this factorization we infer the following assertions:

(1)

M
is invertible if and only
if
M/D
is invertible.
If this is the case, then the
Banachiewicz inversion formula

(2.9)

M^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}(A-BD^{-1}C)^{-1}&-(A-BD^{-1}C)^{-1}BD^{-1}\\
-D^{-1}C(A-BD^{-1}C)^{-1}&D^{-1}+D^{-1}C(A-BD^{-1}C)^{-1}BD^{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

holds.

(2)

The determinant factorizes and
Jacobi’s identity

(2.10)

\det M=\det(M/D)\det D

holds.

2.4.
Relation between the Green function and the characteristic polynomial

Let
(\mathbf{\Gamma},o)
be a rooted graph on vertices
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{d}
.
If we decompose its adjacency matrix

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&b^{*}\\
b&D\end{bmatrix}

into block form with
D=A_{\mathbf{\Gamma}\setminus o}
,
then
the Green function (
2.5
) is the upper left entry
of the inverse of the matrix

M=zI-A=\begin{bmatrix}z&-b^{*}\\
-b&zI-D\end{bmatrix}

and coincides with the inverse of its Schur complement,
which results in

G_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(z)=\langle(zI-A)^{-1}e_{1},e_{1}\rangle=(z-b^{*}(zI-D)^{-1}b)^{-1}.

Consequently the Schur complement
(
2.7
)
equals
G_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(z)^{-1}=F_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(z)
,
cf. (
2.9
).
Being a matrix of dimension 1 it equals its determinant
and identity (
2.10
) yields

(2.11)

\phi_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(x)=F_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(x)\,\phi_{\mathbf{\Gamma}\setminus o}(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}.

2.5.
Relation between the Green function and the Cauchy transform of a general matrix

Let
A
be a
d\times d
matrix. We want to understand the relation between
the functions

G_{A}(z)=\langle(z-A)^{-1}e_{1},e_{1}\rangle\quad\text{ and }\quad\Cauchy_{A}(z)={\rm Tr}((z-A)^{-1}).

To this end we partition the matrix into blocks of dimension
1
and
d-1

(2.12)

A=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&a_{1}^{*}\\
a_{2}&\mathring{A}\end{bmatrix}.

Now the corresponding Schur complement (
2.7
) of
z-A

is a scalar

S=z-\alpha-a_{1}^{*}(z-\mathring{A})^{-1}a_{2}

and we conclude from the Banachiewicz inversion formula (
2.9
)
that

(2.13)

G_{A}(z)=S^{-1}=\frac{1}{z-\alpha-a_{1}^{*}(z-\mathring{A})^{-1}a_{2}}

and

\displaystyle\Cauchy_{A}(z)

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\begin{bmatrix}S^{-1}&*\\
*&(z-\mathring{A})^{-1}+(z-\mathring{A})^{-1}a_{2}S^{-1}a_{1}^{*}(z-\mathring{A})^{-1}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=G_{A}(z)+\Cauchy_{\mathring{A}}(z)+G_{A}(z){\rm Tr}((z-\mathring{A})^{-1}a_{2}a_{1}^{*}(z-\mathring{A})^{-1})

\displaystyle=G_{A}(z)(1+{\rm Tr}(a_{1}^{*}(z-\mathring{A})^{-2}a_{2}))+\Cauchy_{\mathring{A}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{F_{A}^{\prime}(z)}{F_{A}(z)}+\Cauchy_{\mathring{A}}(z).

After subtracting the unit matrix according to
(
2.3
)
we obtain the identity

(2.14)

\RC_{\mathring{A}}(z)=\RC_{A}(z)+\frac{d}{dz}\log(zG_{A}(z))=\RC_{A}(z)+\frac{1}{z}+\frac{G_{A}^{\prime}(z)}{G_{A}(z)},

which can be extended to trace class operators.

and
(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2},o_{2})=(V_{2},E_{2},o_{2})

(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1},o_{1})\circledast{}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2},o_{2})
,
is defined by conglutinating the graphs
(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1},o_{1}),(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2},o_{2})
at their roots; see Fig.

Formally the vertex set of
\mathbf{\Gamma}
can be realized as a subset of
V_{1}\times V_{2}
,

V=\{(x_{1},o_{2}):x_{1}\in V_{1}\}\cup\{(o_{1},x_{2}):x_{2}\in V_{2}\}.

Two vertices
(x_{1},x_{2})
and
(y_{1},y_{2})
of

A=A_{1}\otimes P_{2}+P_{1}\otimes A_{2}

P_{i}

The star product is associative and hence one may define by iteration

(\mathbf{\Gamma},o)=(V,E,o)
of rooted graphs
(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{i},o_{i})=(V_{i},E_{i},o_{i}),i=1,2,\dots,N
. Suppose further that those graphs are finite and simple. Then
the
vertex set
V
of
\mathbf{\Gamma}
can be regarded as a subset of
V_{1}\times\cdots\times V_{N}
and hence the adjacency matrix
A_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
can be regarded as
an operator on
\ell^{2}(V_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes\ell^{2}(V_{N})
. Under this
identification one has

(2.15)

A_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_{1}\otimes P_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{i-1}\otimes A_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{i}}\otimes P_{i+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{N},

Let
\varphi
be the vacuum state on
B(\ell^{2}(V))
:
\varphi(X)=\langle X\delta_{o},\delta_{o}\rangle_{\ell^{2}(V)}
.

2.7.
The comb product and its adjacency matrix

Given a graph
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1})
and a rooted graph
(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2},o_{2})=(V_{2},E_{2},o_{2})
,
a new graph
\mathbf{\Gamma}=\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}\rhd{}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2},o_{2})

is defined by gluing a copy of
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2}

to every vertex of
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}
at the root
o_{2}
:
The vertex set
V
of
\mathbf{\Gamma}
is
V_{1}\times V_{2}
and two vertices
(x_{1},x_{2})
and
(y_{1},y_{2})
are connected by an edge if and only if either
x_{1}y_{1}\in E_{1}
and
x_{2}=y_{2}=o_{2}
, or
x_{1}=y_{1}
and
x_{2}y_{2}\in E_{2}
.
If we further specify a root of
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}

(o_{1},o_{2})
, which makes the comb product associative (but non-commutative) in the category of rooted graphs; see Fig.

In the present paper we study the

2.8.
Identities for the star product

For the sake of notational convenience we denote by
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}\circledast{}\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2}
the star product of two rooted graphs
(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1},o_{1})
and
(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2},o_{2})
. The Green function of the star product satisfies
the following relation, which follows from the decomposition (
2.15
) of the adjacency matrix into Boolean independent operators and the linearization formula for Boolean convolution in
[
23

[
17
]

Proposition 2.1
.

For rooted graphs
(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1},o_{1})
and
(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2},o_{2})
the following formula holds.

(2.17)

\displaystyle F_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}\circledast{}\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2}}(z)=F_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}}(z)+F_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2}}(z)-z.

The Cauchy transform of the star product is computed by the formula below.

Proposition 2.2
.

For rooted graphs
(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1},o_{1})
and
(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2},o_{2})
the following formula holds.

(2.18)

\displaystyle\Cauchy_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}\circledast{}\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2}}(z)+\frac{G_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}\circledast{}\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2}}^{\prime}(z)}{G_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}\circledast{}\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2}}(z)}

\displaystyle=\Cauchy_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}}(z)+\Cauchy_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2}}(z)+\frac{G_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}}^{\prime}(z)}{G_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}}(z)}+\frac{G_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2}}^{\prime}(z)}{G_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2}}(z)}.

Remark 2.3
.

Later we will give two alternative proofs in a more general setting; see Theorem
4.2
.

Proof.

The key is the simple identity

(2.19)

\phi_{(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}\circledast{}\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2})\setminus o}(x)=\phi_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}\setminus o_{1}}(x)\,\phi_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2}\setminus o_{2}}(x),

for the star product,
which follows from the fact that the removal of the root splits the graph into two disjoint connected components

(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}\circledast{}\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2})\setminus o=(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}\setminus o_{1})\cup(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2}\setminus o_{2}).

Using the Schur identity (
2.11
)
we can rewrite (
2.19
) as

(2.20)

\phi_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}\circledast{}\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2}}(z)\,G_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}\circledast{}\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2}}(z)=\phi_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}}(z)\,G_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}}(z)\,\phi_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2}}(z)\,G_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2}}(z)

and taking the logarithmic derivative of (
2.20
) together with (
2.2
) yields
(
2.18
).
∎

Finally, the characteristic polynomial of the star product satisfies the following
identity proved by Schwenk, for which we give an alternative proof.

Theorem 2.4

(

For rooted graphs

2.9.
Identities for the comb product

In this section, the comb product is denoted simply by
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}\rhd{}\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2}
, the root being omitted for simplicity, for a graph
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}
and a rooted graph
(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2},o_{2})
. The relation between the Green functions is
simple and follows from the decomposition (
2.16
) of the adjacency matrix together with Muraki’s formula
[
14
, Theorem 3.1]
; see also
[
16
, Theorem 3.2]
for another proof of Muraki’s formula.

Proposition 2.5
.

For rooted graphs
(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1},o_{1})
and
(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2},o_{2})
the following formula holds:

(2.22)

F_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}\rhd{}\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2}}(z)=F_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}}(F_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2}}(z)).

For the characteristic polynomial
Schwenk proved the following relation by combinatorial arguments;
we give an algebraic proof based on the simpler relation
(
2.22
).

Theorem 2.6

(
[
19
, Theorem 5]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{\Gamma}
be a graph on
d
vertices and
(\mathbf{H},o)
be a rooted graph. Then

(2.23)

\phi_{\mathbf{\Gamma}\rhd{}\mathbf{H}}(x)=\phi_{\mathbf{H}\setminus o}(x)^{d}\phi_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(\phi_{\mathbf{H}}(x)/\phi_{\mathbf{H}\setminus o}(x))=\phi_{\mathbf{H}\setminus o}(x)^{d}\phi_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(F_{\mathbf{H}}(x)).

Proof.

We proceed by induction.
Fix an arbitrary vertex
o^{\prime}
of
\mathbf{\Gamma}
as a root.
Then removing the root
o^{\prime\prime}
from
\mathbf{\Gamma}\rhd{}\mathbf{H}
splits off an extra copy of
\mathbf{H}\setminus o
(cf. Fig.
2
)

(\mathbf{\Gamma}\rhd{}\mathbf{H})\setminus o^{\prime\prime}=((\mathbf{\Gamma}\setminus o^{\prime})\rhd{}\mathbf{H})\cup(\mathbf{H}\setminus o)

and therefore

\phi_{(\mathbf{\Gamma}\rhd{}\mathbf{H})\setminus o^{\prime\prime}}(x)=\phi_{(\mathbf{\Gamma}\setminus o^{\prime})\rhd{}\mathbf{H}}(x)\,\phi_{\mathbf{H}\setminus o}(x).

We proceed with identities (
2.11
) and
(
2.22
) to conclude by induction

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathbf{\Gamma}\rhd{}\mathbf{H}}(x)

\displaystyle=\phi_{(\mathbf{\Gamma}\rhd{}\mathbf{H})\setminus o^{\prime}}(x)F_{\mathbf{\Gamma}\rhd{}\mathbf{H}}(x)

\displaystyle=\phi_{(\mathbf{\Gamma}\setminus o^{\prime})\rhd{}\mathbf{H}}(x)\,\phi_{\mathbf{H}\setminus o}(x)\,F_{\mathbf{\Gamma}\rhd{}\mathbf{H}}(x)

\displaystyle=\phi_{\mathbf{H}\setminus o}(x)^{d-1}\,\phi_{\mathbf{\Gamma}\setminus o^{\prime}}(F_{\mathbf{H}}(x))\,\phi_{\mathbf{H}\setminus o}(x)\,F_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(F_{\mathbf{H}}(x))

\displaystyle=\phi_{\mathbf{H}\setminus o}(x)^{d}\,\phi_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(F_{\mathbf{H}}(x)).

∎

Finally, formula (
2.23
) gives rise to an equivalent formula for the renormalized Cauchy transform.

Proposition 2.7
.

In the setting of Theorem
2.6
, one has

\RC_{\mathbf{\Gamma}\rhd{}\mathbf{H}}(z)=d\RC_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(z)+F_{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}(z)\,\RC_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(F_{\mathbf{H}}(z)).

Remark 2.8
.

Later we will give two more proofs in a more general setting; see Theorem
7.6
.

Proof.

Combining (
2.2
), (
2.23
) and (
2.11
) yields

\displaystyle\Cauchy_{\mathbf{\Gamma}\rhd{}\mathbf{H}}(z)

\displaystyle=d\frac{d}{dz}\log\frac{\phi_{\mathbf{H}}(z)}{F_{\mathbf{H}}(z)}+F_{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}(z)\,\Cauchy_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(F_{\mathbf{H}}(z))

\displaystyle=d\left(\Cauchy_{\mathbf{H}}(z)-\frac{F_{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}(z)}{F_{\mathbf{H}}(z)}\right)+F_{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}(z)\,\Cauchy_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(F_{\mathbf{H}}(z)),

which can be rewritten as

\RC_{\mathbf{\Gamma}\rhd{}\mathbf{H}}(z)+\frac{dd^{\prime}}{z}=d\left(\RC_{\mathbf{H}}(z)+\frac{d^{\prime}}{z}-\frac{F_{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}(z)}{F_{\mathbf{H}}(z)}\right)+F_{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}(z)\left(\RC_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}(F_{\mathbf{H}}(z))+\frac{d}{F_{\mathbf{H}}(z)}\right),

where
d^{\prime}
is the number of vertices of
\mathbf{H}
.
∎

3.
Cyclic-Boolean independence

In order to study the eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix of star product graphs, we will compute traces of powers of the adjacency matrix. These computations can be abstracted and formulated as a new notion of independence, which we call cyclic-Boolean independence.

3.1.
Definition and example

The definition of cyclic-Boolean independence is motivated by the star product
from Section
2.6
, which can be extended to the general setting of Hilbert spaces as follows.

Example 3.1
.

Let
H_{i},i\in\mathbb{N},
be Hilbert spaces with distinguished unit vectors
\xi_{i}\in H_{i}
,
P_{i}\colon H_{i}\to H_{i}
the orthogonal projection onto
\mathbb{C}\xi_{i}
,
T(H_{i})
the
*
-algebra of trace-class operators on
H_{i}
and
\varphi_{i}
the vector state on
B(H_{i})
defined by
\varphi_{i}(A)=\langle A\xi_{i},\xi_{i}\rangle
.

Let
H=H_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes H_{N}
,
\xi=\xi_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\xi_{N}
and
\varphi
the vacuum state on
B(H)
defined by
\xi
.
Let
\pi_{i}\colon B(H_{i})\to B(H)
be the
*
-homomorphism defined by

(3.1)

\pi_{i}(A)=P_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{i-1}\otimes A\otimes P_{i+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{N}.

The family of
\ast
-subalgebras
\{\pi_{i}(B(H_{i}))\}_{i=1}^{N}
is Boolean independent in
(B(H),\varphi)
; e.g. see
[
12
, Theorem 8.8]
.
Furthermore, we compute the mixed moments with respect to the trace. A key formula is

(3.2)

P_{i}AP_{i}=\varphi_{i}(A)P_{i},\qquad A\in B(H_{i}).

For any cyclically alternating tuple
(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, namely those
satisfying
k_{1}\neq k_{2}\neq\cdots\neq k_{n}\neq k_{1}
, and for any
A_{i}\in T(H_{k_{i}})
a direct computation using formula (
3.2
) yields

{\rm Tr}_{H}(\pi_{k_{1}}(A_{1})\cdots\pi_{k_{n}}(A_{n}))=\begin{cases}{\rm Tr}_{H_{k_{1}}}(A_{1}),&n=1,\\
\varphi_{k_{1}}(A_{1})\,\varphi_{k_{2}}(A_{2})\cdots\varphi_{k_{n}}(A_{n}),&n\geq 2.\end{cases}

Let us raise this identity to an abstract concept.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a
\ast
-algebra over
\mathbb{C}
,
\varphi
a positive linear functional on
\mathcal{A}
and
\omega
a positive tracial linear functional on
\mathcal{A}
. The triplet
(\mathcal{A},\varphi,\omega)
is called a
cyclic non-commutative probability space
(cncps).
The
distribution
of a self-adjoint element
a\in\mathcal{A}
is the data
\{(\varphi(a^{n}),\omega(a^{n})):n\geq 1\}
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\varphi,\omega)
be a cncps. A family of

\ast
-subalgebras
\{\mathcal{A}_{k}\}_{k\in K}
is said to be

cyclic-Boolean independent
if

(i)

it is Boolean independent with respect to
\varphi
, that is, for any
n\geq 2
, alternating tuple
(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})\in K^{n}
(namely, with
k_{1}\neq k_{2}\neq\cdots\neq k_{n})
and
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{k_{i}},i=1,2,\dots,n
, one has the factorization

\varphi(a_{1}\cdots a_{n})=\varphi(a_{1})\varphi(a_{2})\cdots\varphi(a_{n});

(ii)

for any
n\geq 1
, any cyclically alternating tuple
(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})\in K^{n}
and any choice of
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{k_{i}},i=1,2,\dots,n
, one has

\omega(a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{n})=\begin{cases}\omega(a_{1}),&n=1,\\
\varphi(a_{1})\varphi(a_{2})\cdots\varphi(a_{n}),&n\geq 2.\end{cases}

A family of
elements
\{a_{k}\}_{k\in K}
of
\mathcal{A}
is said to be cyclic-Boolean
independent if this is the case for
\{\mathcal{A}_{k}\}_{k\in K}
, where
\mathcal{A}_{k}
is the
\ast
-subalgebra generated by
a_{k}
without unit.

Example 3.4
.

Suppose that
\{a,b,c\}
is cyclic-Boolean independent in
(\mathcal{A},\varphi,\omega)
. Then

\varphi(ba^{2}bc^{2}b)=\varphi(b)\varphi(a^{2})\varphi(b)\varphi(c^{2})\varphi(b)

and

\omega(ba^{2}bc^{2}b)=\varphi(b^{2})\varphi(a^{2})\varphi(b)\varphi(c^{2}).

Another operator model occurs on star products of Hilbert spaces.

Example 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{i}
be separable Hilbert spaces with distinguished unit vectors

\xi_{i}
as above and
\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{i}=(\mathbb{C}\xi_{i})^{\perp}
.
The

star product
of the Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{i}
is the direct sum

\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}\xi\oplus\bigoplus_{i}\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{i}.

Then each
\mathcal{H}_{i}
can be identified with the subspace

\mathbb{C}\xi\oplus\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{H}

and there is a canonical representation
of
B(\mathcal{H}_{i})
on
\mathcal{H}
which acts by simply annihilating the
complement of
H_{i}
.
More precisely we decompose
\mathcal{H}
as a direct sum

\mathcal{H}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{i}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{i}^{\perp}

where
\mathcal{H}^{\perp}_{i}=\bigoplus_{j\neq i}\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{j}

and define the representation

\pi_{i}(X)=X\oplus 0
.
Then the algebras
\mathcal{A}_{i}=\pi_{i}(B(\mathcal{H}_{i}))
are Boolean independent
with respect to the vacuum expectation
\varphi=\langle\ldotp\xi,\xi\rangle

and
moreover, the algebras
\mathcal{A}_{i}
are cyclic-Boolean independent
with respect to the trace.
Indeed, let
P_{0}\in B(\mathcal{H})
be the projection onto
\mathbb{C}\xi

and
P_{i}
the projections onto
\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
;
then
P_{0},P_{1},P_{2},\dots
form a partition of unity
and by definition we have
X=(P_{0}+P_{i})X(P_{0}+P_{i})
for all
X\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
.
Let
X_{1}X_{2}\dots X_{n}
be a cyclically alternating product
of trace class operators with
X_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{i_{k}}
, then

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}(X_{1}X_{2}\dotsm X_{n})

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}((P_{0}+P_{i_{1}})X_{1}(P_{0}+P_{i_{1}})(P_{0}+P_{i_{2}})X_{2}(P_{0}+P_{i_{2}})\dotsm(P_{0}+P_{i_{n}})X_{n}(P_{0}+P_{i_{n}}))

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}(P_{0}X_{1}P_{0}X_{2}P_{0}\dotsm P_{0}X_{n}P_{0})

\displaystyle=\varphi(X_{1})\,\varphi(X_{2})\dotsm\varphi(X_{n}).

Next we show that any Boolean independent family
can be represented on a star product space.

3.2.
Construction of a cyclic-Boolean trace

Let
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
be a noncommutative probability space,
where
\mathcal{A}
is a
*
-algebra
and
\mathcal{A}_{i}
are Boolean independent subalgebras.
In the following assume that
\mathcal{A}
is faithfully represented on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}

and that the state
\varphi
is realized as a vector state

\varphi(X)=\langle X\xi,\xi\rangle
. One way to achieve this under
certain conditions is the
GNS-construction
.

Recall that the GNS-representation consists of the Hilbert
space
\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}
obtained by completing the quotient space

\mathcal{A}/N_{\varphi}
, where
N_{\varphi}=\{x\in\mathcal{A}\mid\varphi(x^{*}x)=0\}
,
with respect to the scalar product

\langle[x]_{\varphi},[y]_{\varphi}\rangle=\varphi(y^{*}x).

The action of the GNS representation is
\pi_{\varphi}(x)[y]_{\varphi}=[xy]_{\varphi}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

The GNS representation is faithful if and only if the state
\varphi
is
nondegenerate in the sense that
if
\varphi(axb)=0
for all
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
, then
x=0
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathcal{A}
, then

\pi_{\varphi}(x)=0

\iff

\pi_{\varphi}(x)[y]_{\varphi}=0
for all
y\in\mathcal{A}

\iff

\langle[xy]_{\varphi},[z]_{\varphi}\rangle_{\varphi}=0
for all
y,z\in\mathcal{A}

\iff

\varphi(z^{*}xy)=0
for all
y,z\in\mathcal{A}
.
∎

If
\mathcal{A}
is unital, then the state vector
\xi=[1]_{\varphi}
comes for free,
otherwise the state must satisfy the Cauchy-Schwarz condition

\left\lvert\varphi(x)\right\rvert^{2}\leq C\varphi(x^{*}x)

for some fixed constant
C
in order to allow a positive extension
to the unitization of
\mathcal{A}
,
see
[
18
, Theorem 4.5.11]
.

Assuming that
\mathcal{A}
and the state
\varphi
are faithfully
represented on some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}

we identify
\mathcal{A}
with a subalgebra of
B(\mathcal{H})
and
we are now going to reconstruct the star product space from this data.
Let
\mathcal{H}_{0}=[\xi]=\mathbb{C}\xi
be the subspace spanned
by
\xi
and
P_{0}
the orthogonal projection onto it.
Adjoining this projection to the algebra
\mathcal{A}

and to each subalgebra
\mathcal{A}_{i}
, Boolean independence is preserved
and wlog we may assume that
P_{0}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
for every
i
.
Let now
\mathring{\mathcal{A}}_{i}=\ker\varphi\cap\mathcal{A}_{i}
, then
we can construct the components of the star product space as follows.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{0}=[\xi]
and
\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{i}=[\mathring{\mathcal{A}}_{i}\xi]

the closed invariant subspace generated by
\xi
.
Then

(i)

\mathcal{H}_{0}\perp\mathcal{H}_{i}
for all
i
.

(ii)

\mathcal{H}_{i}\perp\mathcal{H}_{j}
for all
i\neq j
.

Proof.

It suffices to verify orthogonality on the dense subspaces
\mathring{\mathcal{A}}_{i}\xi
.

(i)

Let
X\in\mathring{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
, then

\langle X\xi,\xi\rangle=\varphi(X)=0.

(ii)

Let
X\in\mathring{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
and
Y\in\mathring{\mathcal{A}}_{j}
with
i\neq j
,
then

\langle X\xi,Y\xi\rangle=\varphi(Y^{*}X)=\varphi(Y^{*})\,\varphi(X)=0.

∎

We now construct the decomposition.
Under the assumption that
P_{0}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
we have

\mathcal{H}_{i}:=\mathcal{H}_{0}\oplus\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{i}=[\mathcal{A}_{i}\xi]
.
Denote by
P_{i}
the projection onto
\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
,
by
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
the subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
generated
by
(\mathcal{A}_{i})_{i\in I}

and let
\hat{\mathcal{H}}=[\hat{\mathcal{A}}\xi]\subseteq\mathcal{H}

the closed invariant subspace generated by
\xi
.
Let further
\hat{P}
and
\hat{P}^{\perp}
be the respective projections
onto the space
\hat{\mathcal{H}}
and its orthogonal complement
\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{\perp}
.

Proposition 3.8
.

(i)

For each
i
the space
\mathcal{H}_{i}

is invariant under
\mathcal{A}_{i}
, i.e., for
X\in\mathcal{A}_{i}

(3.3)

X(P_{0}+P_{i})=(P_{0}+P_{i})X(P_{0}+P_{i}).

(ii)

For
i\neq j
the subspace
\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{j}
is annihilated
by
\mathcal{A}_{i}
,
i.e., for
X\in\mathcal{A}_{i}

(3.4)

XP_{j}=P_{j}X=0.

(iii)

The space
\hat{\mathcal{H}}
is the closed linear span of the subspaces
\mathcal{A}_{i}\xi
,
i.e.,

(3.5)

\hat{\mathcal{H}}=\mathcal{H}_{0}\oplus\bigoplus_{i}\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{i}.

Proof.

(i)

This is an immediate consequence of the definition.

(ii)

It suffices to show that
XP_{j}=0
, i.e.,
X
vanishes on

\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{j}
. We verify this on the dense subspace
\mathring{A}_{j}\xi
.
Indeed, let
Y\in\mathring{\mathcal{A}}_{j}
, then

\lVert XY\xi\rVert^{2}=\langle XY\xi,XY\xi\rangle=\varphi(Y^{*}X^{*}XY)=\varphi(Y^{*})\,\varphi(X^{*}X)\,\varphi(Y)=0;

finally
P_{j}X=(X^{*}P_{j})^{*}=0
because
\mathcal{A}_{i}
is a
*
-algebra.

(iii)

The space
\hat{\mathcal{H}}
is the closure of the span of the alternating words

X_{1}X_{2}\dots X_{n}\xi
with
X_{j}\in\mathcal{A}_{i_{j}}
and
i_{j}\neq j_{j+1}
.
We claim that such a word satisfies

X_{1}X_{2}\dotsm X_{n}\xi\in\mathcal{H}_{i_{1}}
.
We proceed by induction.
The claim is obviously true for
n=1
.
Let now
\eta=X_{1}X_{2}\dotsm X_{n}\xi
be a given word.
Then
\eta=X_{1}\eta^{\prime}
with
\eta^{\prime}=X_{2}\dotsm X_{n}\xi

and by induction hypothesis
\eta^{\prime}\in H_{i_{2}}
,
say
\eta^{\prime}=\alpha\xi+\eta^{\prime\prime}
with

\eta^{\prime\prime}\in\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{i_{2}}
.
But then from item
(
ii
) we infer that

X_{1}\eta^{\prime}=\alpha X_{1}\xi+0\in\mathcal{H}_{i_{1}}
.

∎

Corollary 3.9
.

Every
X\in\hat{\mathcal{A}}
has block decomposition

(3.6)

X=\hat{P}X\hat{P}+\hat{P}^{\perp}X\hat{P}^{\perp}

and more precisely every
X\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
has the block decomposition

(3.7)

X=(P_{0}+P_{i})X(P_{0}+P_{i})+\hat{P}^{\perp}X\hat{P}^{\perp}.

Theorem 3.10
.

The functional
\omega(X)={\rm Tr}(\hat{P}X\hat{P})

is a semifinite trace on the algebra
\hat{\mathcal{A}}

and
the subalgebras
\mathcal{A}_{i}\cap T(\mathcal{H})
are cyclic-Boolean independent with respect
to
\omega
.

Proof.

\omega
is a trace on
\hat{\mathcal{A}}

because
\hat{P}
is in the commutant of
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
.
Now
let
X_{1}X_{2}\dotsm X_{n}

be a cyclically alternating product with
X_{j}\in\mathcal{A}_{i_{j}}
for
j=1,2,\dots,n
,
then we have

\displaystyle\omega(X_{1}X_{2}\dotsm X_{n})

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}{\rm Tr}\bigl(\hat{P}((P_{0}+P_{i_{1}})X_{1}(P_{0}+P_{i_{1}})+\hat{P}^{\perp}X_{1}\hat{P}^{\perp})((P_{0}+P_{i_{2}})X_{2}(P_{0}+P_{i_{2}})+\hat{P}^{\perp}X_{2}\hat{P}^{\perp})\\
\dotsm((P_{0}+P_{i_{n}})X_{n}(P_{0}+P_{i_{n}})+\hat{P}^{\perp}X_{n}\hat{P}^{\perp})\hat{P}\bigr)\end{multlined}

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\bigl((P_{0}+P_{i_{1}})X_{1}(P_{0}+P_{i_{1}})((P_{0}+P_{i_{2}})X_{2}(P_{0}+P_{i_{2}})\dotsm((P_{0}+P_{i_{n}})X_{n}(P_{0}+P_{i_{n}})\bigr)

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\bigl((P_{0}+P_{i_{1}})X_{1}P_{0}X_{2}P_{0}\dotsm P_{0}X_{n}(P_{0}+P_{i_{n}})\bigr)

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}(P_{0}X_{1}P_{0}X_{2}P_{0}\dotsm P_{0}X_{n}P_{0})

and it follows that
X_{1}X_{2}\dotsm X_{n}\in L^{1}(\omega)
.
∎

Remark 3.11
.

Conversely, assume that subalgebras
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}

and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}
are cyclic-Boolean independent in a cnps

(\mathcal{A},\varphi,\omega)
.
Assume further that
\mathcal{A}
is generated by
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
and

\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}

and that there is a projection
p\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}

such that
pap=\varphi(a)p
for
a\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
and
\omega(p)=\varphi(p)=1
.
Then
\varphi(x)=\omega(px)
for all
x\in\mathcal{A}
.

4.
Convolution and central limit theorem

4.1.
Cyclic-Boolean convolution

Let
(\mathcal{A},\varphi,\omega)
be a cncps.
For
a\in\mathcal{A}
the renormalized (tracial)
Cauchy transform is the formal Laurent series

\RC_{a}(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\omega(a^{n})}{z^{n+1}}.

By slight
abuse of terminology, we call
G_{a}
the Green function (evaluated
at the state
\varphi
) of
a
. It has formal Laurent expansion

G_{a}(z)=\frac{1}{z}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\varphi(a^{n})}{z^{n+1}},

and we denote the reciprocal Green function by
F_{a}(z)=1/G_{a}(z)
.

If
a
is a trace class operator on a Hilbert space and
\omega
is the trace
then
|{\rm Tr}(a^{n})|\leq\lVert|a|^{n-1}\rVert\,{\rm Tr}(|a|)\leq\|a\|^{n-1}{\rm Tr}(|a|)
,
and hence
\RC_{a}(z)
is absolutely convergent in
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|>\|a\|\}
. Moreover, if
a
is selfadjoint then
\RC_{a}
has analytic
extension to
\mathbb{C}\setminus\spec(a)
by Lidskii’s theorem

(4.1)

\RC_{a}(z)={\rm Tr}((z-a)^{-1}-z^{-1})=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{z(z-\lambda_{i})},

where
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
is the multiset of eigenvalues of
a
. In particular, the non-zero eigenvalues of
a
can be detected from
\RC_{a}
as poles. If the Hilbert space is finite-dimensional, then we also have the formula

(4.2)

\RC_{a}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{{\rm dim}(H)}\frac{1}{z-\lambda_{i}}-\frac{{\rm dim}(H)}{z},

and hence

\lim_{z\to 0}z\RC_{a}(z)=\text{the multiplicity of the eigenvalue zero}-{\rm dim}(H).

Remark 4.1
.

By
[
5
, Corollary 2.2]
, the tracial moments
{\rm Tr}(a^{n})
for all but finitely many natural numbers
n
determine the eigenvalues of
a
.
So, for any
p\in\mathbb{N}
, we can generalize the above setting to the Schatten class

S_{p}
by using the truncated generating function

\RC_{p}(z)=\sum_{n=p}^{\infty}\frac{\omega(a^{n})}{z^{n+1}}.

Let
a
and
b
be cyclic-Boolean independent in
(\mathcal{A},\varphi,\omega)
.
It is known
[
23
]
(and will be shown in Remark
4.4
below)
that the Green function of
a+b
can be computed via the formula

(4.3)

\frac{1}{G_{a}(z)}+\frac{1}{G_{b}(z)}-z=\frac{1}{G_{a+b}(z)};

i.e.,

B_{a+b}(z)=B_{a}(z)+B_{b}(z),

where

(4.4)

B_{a}(z)=\frac{z}{G_{a}(1/z)}-1

is the
Boolean cumulant transform
.
The next theorem generalizes this identity to an analogous formula
for the generating function
\RC_{a+b}(z)

which gives information on the eigenvalues of
a+b
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
a
and
b
be cyclic-Boolean
independent elements. Then the renormalized Cauchy transform of their sum is

\RC_{a+b}(z)=\RC_{a}(z)+\RC_{b}(z)+\frac{G_{a}^{\prime}(z)}{G_{a}(z)}+\frac{G_{b}^{\prime}(z)}{G_{b}(z)}-\frac{G_{a+b}^{\prime}(z)}{G_{a+b}(z)}+\frac{1}{z};

i.e., if we define
(cf. (
2.14
))

\HH_{a}(z)=\RC_{a}(z)+\frac{d}{dz}\log zG_{a}(z)

then

(4.5)

\HH_{a+b}(z)=\HH_{a}(z)+\HH_{b}(z).

Remark 4.3
.

While
\HH
linearizes independent sums and is useful for analyzing convolutions, we will later introduce a modification which deserves to be called the cyclic-Boolean cumulant transform; see Section
5
.

Algebraic proof.

We expand the power
(a+b)^{n}
and regroup the resulting monomials into
those ending in
a
and those ending in
b
:

\displaystyle(a+b)^{n}

\displaystyle=a^{n}+\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq 1\\
p_{0}\geq 0,\,p_{1},q_{1},\dots,p_{k},q_{k}\geq 1\\
p_{0}+p_{1}+q_{1}+\cdots+p_{k}+q_{k}=n\end{subarray}}}a^{p_{0}}b^{q_{1}}a^{p_{1}}\cdots b^{q_{k}}a^{p_{k}}+b^{n}+\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq 1\\
q_{0}\geq 0,\,p_{1},q_{1},\dots,p_{k},q_{k}\geq 1\\
q_{0}+q_{1}+p_{1}+\cdots+p_{k}+q_{k}=n\end{subarray}}}b^{q_{0}}a^{p_{1}}b^{q_{1}}\cdots a^{p_{k}}b^{q_{k}},

and applying
\omega
yields

\displaystyle\omega((a+b)^{n})

\displaystyle=\omega(a^{n})+\omega(b^{n})+\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq 1\\
p_{0}\geq 0,\,p_{1},q_{1},\dots,p_{k},q_{k}\geq 1\\
p_{0}+p_{1}+q_{1}+\cdots+p_{k}+q_{k}=n\end{subarray}}}\omega(a^{{p_{0}}+p_{k}}b^{q_{1}}a^{p_{1}}\cdots b^{q_{k}})+\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq 1\\
q_{0}\geq 0,\,p_{1},q_{1},\dots,p_{k},q_{k}\geq 1\\
q_{0}+p_{1}+q_{1}+\cdots+p_{k}+q_{k}=n\end{subarray}}}\omega(b^{q_{0}+q_{k}}a^{p_{1}}b^{q_{1}}\cdots a^{p_{k}})

\displaystyle=\omega(a^{n})+\omega(b^{n})+\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq 1\\
p_{0}\geq 0,\,p_{1},q_{1},\dots,p_{k},q_{k}\geq 1\\
p_{0}+p_{1}+q_{1}+\cdots+p_{k}+q_{k}=n\end{subarray}}}\varphi(a^{{p_{0}}+p_{k}})\,\varphi(b^{q_{1}})\,\varphi(a^{p_{1}})\cdots\varphi(b^{q_{k}})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq 1\\
q_{0}\geq 0,\,p_{1},q_{1},\dots,p_{k},q_{k}\geq 1\\
q_{0}+p_{1}+q_{1}+\cdots+p_{k}+q_{k}=n\end{subarray}}}\varphi(b^{q_{0}+q_{k}})\,\varphi(a^{p_{1}})\,\varphi(b^{q_{1}})\cdots\varphi(a^{p_{k}}).

Multiplying the above identity by
z^{-n-1}
and taking the summation over
n
yields

\displaystyle\RC_{a+b}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\omega((a+b)^{n})}{z^{n+1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\omega(a^{n})}{z^{n+1}}+\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\omega(b^{n})}{z^{n+1}}+\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq 1\\
p_{0}\geq 0,\,p_{1},q_{1},\dots,p_{k},q_{k}\geq 1\end{subarray}}}\frac{\varphi(a^{{p_{0}}+p_{k}})}{z^{p_{0}+p_{k}+1}}\,\frac{\varphi(b^{q_{1}})}{z^{q_{1}}}\,\frac{\varphi(a^{p_{1}})}{z^{p_{1}}}\cdots\frac{\varphi(b^{q_{k}})}{z^{q_{k}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq 1\\
q_{0}\geq 0,\,p_{1},q_{1},\dots,p_{k},q_{k}\geq 1\end{subarray}}}\frac{\varphi(b^{q_{0}+q_{k}})}{z^{q_{0}+q_{k}+1}}\,\frac{\varphi(a^{p_{1}})}{z^{p_{1}}}\,\frac{\varphi(b^{q_{1}})}{z^{q_{1}}}\cdots\frac{\varphi(a^{p_{k}})}{z^{p_{k}}}.

Now

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{0}\geq 0\\
p\geq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{\varphi(a^{p_{0}+p_{1}})}{z^{p_{0}+p+1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{0}\geq 0\\
p\geq 1\\
p_{0}+p=m\end{subarray}}\frac{\varphi(a^{m})}{z^{m+1}}=\sum_{m\geq 1}m\frac{\varphi(a^{m})}{z^{m+1}}=-(zG_{a}(z))^{\prime}

and thus

\displaystyle\RC_{a+b}(z)

\displaystyle=\RC_{a}(z)+\RC_{b}(z)-\sum_{k\geq 1}(zG_{a}(z))^{\prime}(zG_{a}(z)-1)^{k-1}(zG_{b}(z)-1)^{k}

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{k\geq 1}(zG_{b}(z))^{\prime}(zG_{a}(z)-1)^{k}(zG_{b}(z)-1)^{k-1}

\displaystyle=\RC_{a}(z)+\RC_{b}(z)-\frac{(zG_{a}(z))^{\prime}(zG_{b}(z)-1)+(zG_{a}(z)-1)(zG_{b}(z))^{\prime}}{1-(zG_{a}(z)-1)(zG_{b}(z)-1)}

\displaystyle=\RC_{a}(z)+\RC_{b}(z)-\frac{(zG_{a}(z))^{\prime}}{G_{a}(z)}\frac{z-F_{b}(z)}{F_{a}(z)F_{b}(z)-(z-F_{a}(z))(z-F_{b}(z))}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{(zG_{b}(z))^{\prime}}{G_{b}(z)}\frac{z-F_{a}(z)}{F_{a}(z)F_{b}(z)-(z-F_{a}(z))(z-F_{b}(z))}

\displaystyle=\RC_{a}(z)+\RC_{b}(z)-\left(\frac{1}{z}+\frac{G_{a}^{\prime}}{G_{a}}\right)\frac{z-F_{b}}{F_{a}+F_{b}-z}-\left(\frac{1}{z}+\frac{G_{b}^{\prime}}{G_{b}}\right)\frac{z-F_{a}}{F_{a}+F_{b}-z}

\displaystyle=\RC_{a}(z)+\RC_{b}(z)+\left(\frac{F_{a}^{\prime}}{F_{a}}(z-F_{b})+\frac{F_{b}^{\prime}}{F_{b}}(z-F_{a})-1\right)\frac{1}{F_{a}+F_{b}-z}+\frac{1}{z}

\displaystyle=\RC_{a}(z)+\RC_{b}(z)+\left(\frac{F_{a}^{\prime}}{F_{a}}(z-F_{a}-F_{b})+\frac{F_{b}^{\prime}}{F_{b}}(z-F_{a}-F_{b})-1+F_{a}^{\prime}+F_{b}^{\prime}\right)\frac{1}{F_{a}+F_{b}-z}+\frac{1}{z}

\displaystyle=\RC_{a}(z)+\RC_{b}(z)-\frac{F_{a}^{\prime}}{F_{a}}-\frac{F_{b}^{\prime}}{F_{b}}+\frac{F_{a+b}^{\prime}}{F_{a+b}}+\frac{1}{z}

\displaystyle=\RC_{a}(z)+\RC_{b}(z)+\frac{G_{a}^{\prime}}{G_{a}}+\frac{G_{b}^{\prime}}{G_{b}}-\frac{G_{a+b}^{\prime}}{G_{a+b}}+\frac{1}{z}.

∎

Analytic proof in the setting of Example
3.5
.

Under the assumption that our
\ast
-algebras are represented as trace class operators
on the star product Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}\xi\oplus\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\oplus\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{2}

equipped with a vacuum state
\varphi=\langle.\xi,\xi\rangle
and the trace
\omega={\rm Tr}
we can use this decomposition and represent the involved operators
as block operator matrices

(4.6)

A=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&a^{\prime}&0\\
a&\mathring{A}&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{bmatrix}\beta&0&b^{\prime}\\
0&0&0\\
b&0&\mathring{B}\end{bmatrix},\qquad A+B=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+\beta&a^{\prime}&b^{\prime}\\

In other words,

and we conclude with the identity
(
2.14
).
∎

Remark 4.4
.

4.3.
Cyclic-Boolean central limit theorem

Since we have an appropriate linearization (
4.5
) for
cyclic-Boolean convolution, we are able to determine the central limit law.

Theorem 4.7
.

For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\{a_{i}^{(N)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
be self-adjoint cyclic-Boolean independent random variables in a cncps
(\mathcal{A}_{N},\varphi_{N},\omega_{N})
. Assume that, for each fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the moments
\varphi_{N}((a_{i}^{(N)})^{k})
and
\omega_{N}((a_{i}^{(N)})^{k})
do not depend on
i
or
N
, and also
\omega_{N}(a_{i}^{(N)})=\varphi_{N}(a_{i}^{(N)})=0
,
\varphi_{N}((a_{i}^{(N)})^{2})=1
for all
i
and
N
. Then, for the normalized sum

s_{N}=\frac{a_{1}^{(N)}+a_{2}^{(N)}+\cdots+a_{N}^{(N)}}{\sqrt{N}},

it holds that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\varphi_{N}(s_{N}^{k})=\begin{cases}1,&k\in 2\mathbb{N},\\
0,&k\in 2\mathbb{N}-1,\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{N\to\infty}\omega_{N}(s_{N}^{k})=\begin{cases}2,&k\in 2\mathbb{N}+2,\\
0,&k\in 2\mathbb{N}+1.\end{cases}

Proof.

The Boolean central limit theorem
[
23
]
asserts that

G_{s_{N}}(z)\to z/(z^{2}-1)
. Let
\alpha=\omega_{N}((a_{i}^{(N)})^{2})
, then Theorem
4.2
yields

\HH_{s_{N}}(z)=N^{3/2}\HH_{a_{1}^{(N)}}(\sqrt{N}z)=N^{3/2}\left(\frac{\alpha-2}{(\sqrt{N}z)^{3}}+O(N^{-2})\right)\to\frac{\alpha-2}{z^{3}}.

Therefore

\RC_{s_{N}}(z)\to\frac{2z}{z^{2}-1}-\frac{2}{z}+\frac{\alpha-2}{z^{3}}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\RC_{n}}{z^{n+1}},

where

\RC_{n}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{$n$ is odd},\\
2,&\text{$n$ is even and $n\geq 4$},\\
\alpha,&n=2.\end{cases}

∎

The limit law exhibits a large spectral gap:

Corollary 4.8
.

In addition to the setting of Theorem
4.7
, suppose that
\mathcal{A}_{N}=T(H_{N})
for some Hilbert space
H_{N}

and
\omega_{N}={\rm Tr}_{H_{N}}
. Let
\lambda_{N}
and
\mu_{N}
be the largest and smallest eigenvalues of
s_{N}
, respectively. The following assertions hold:

(i)

the multiplicities of
\lambda_{N}
and
\mu_{N}
are both one for sufficiently large
N
;

(ii)

\lambda_{N}
converges to
1
and
\mu_{N}
converges to
-1
as
N\to\infty
;

(iii)

the remaining eigenvalues accumulate around 0:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\dist(\spec(s_{N})\setminus\{\lambda_{N},\mu_{N}\},0)=0

Proof.

Let
s
be a self-adjoint operator of rank two on a Hilbert space
K
having
eigenvalues
-1,1,0
. Then
{\rm Tr}_{K}(s^{n})=2
for even
n\geq 2
and
{\rm Tr}_{K}(s^{n})=0
for odd
n\geq 1
. The convergence of
{\rm Tr}_{H}(s_{N}^{k})
in Theorem

4.7
and
[
5
, Proposition 2.8]
imply that
s_{N}\to s
in
eigenvalues and this concludes the argument.
∎

5.
Cyclic-Boolean cumulants

5.1.
Univariate cumulants

Let
(\mathcal{A},\varphi,\omega)
be a cncps and
a\in\mathcal{A}
. The generating function
\HH_{a}
defined in (
4.5
) has the series expansion

\HH_{a}(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{h_{n}(a)}{z^{n+1}},

where the first two coefficients are
h_{1}(a)=\omega(a)-\varphi(a)
and
h_{2}(a)=\omega(a^{2})+\varphi(a)^{2}-2\varphi(a^{2})
. In general,
h_{n}(a)
is of the form
\omega(a^{n})-n\varphi(a^{n})+(\text{polynomial on $\varphi(a),\dots,\varphi(a^{n-1})$})
.

We can modify
\HH_{a}(z)
by adding the Boolean cumulants to delete
-n\varphi(a^{n})
from
h_{n}(a)
. We switch from
\RC_{a}
and
G_{a}
to the moment generating functions

\MG_{a}(z)=\frac{1}{z}\RC_{a}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\omega(a^{n})z^{n},\qquad M_{a}(z)=\frac{1}{z}\left(G_{a}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)-z\right)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\varphi(a^{n})z^{n}.

The Boolean cumulant transform (
4.4
) is then expressed by

B_{a}(z)=\frac{M_{a}(z)}{1+M_{a}(z)}=\sum_{n\geq 1}b_{n}(a)z^{n}.

We introduce the new generating function

\displaystyle\CB_{a}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z}\HH_{a}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)+zB_{a}^{\prime}(z)=\MG_{a}(z)-\frac{zM_{a}(z)M_{a}^{\prime}(z)}{(1+M_{a}(z))^{2}}

(5.1)

\displaystyle=\MG_{a}(z)-zM_{a}(z)B_{a}^{\prime}(z),

which linearizes the convolution

\CB_{a+b}(z)=\CB_{a}(z)+\CB_{b}(z).

The function
\CB_{a}
will be called the
cyclic-Boolean cumulant transform of
a
and the coefficients
c_{n}(a)
appearing as

(5.2)

\CB_{a}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}c_{n}(a)z^{n}

are called the (univariate)
cyclic-boolean cumulants
of
a
. The first two cumulants are

c_{1}(a)=\omega(a)\qquad{\rm and}\qquad c_{2}(a)=\omega(a^{2})-\varphi(a)^{2}.

For general
n\geq 2
, there exists a universal polynomial
P_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})
depending only on
n
such that

c_{n}(a)=\omega(a^{n})+P_{n}(\varphi(a),\dots,\varphi(a^{n-1})).

5.2.
Cyclic-interval partitions

Cyclic-Boolean independence gives rise to an exchangeability system and
we can define and compute the (multivariate) cyclic-Boolean cumulants using the methods of

[
13
,
11
]
.
The relevant partition structure turns out to be cyclic-interval partitions, which were already discussed
in
[
7
]
in their search for notions of independence, similar to Boolean and monotone ones, but such that the algebra of scalars,
\mathbb{C}
, is independent from any other algebra.

Before embarking on we recall some basic concepts on set partitions.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We often use the notation
[k]=\{1,2,\dots,k\}
.

(i)

A
set partition
of
[k]
is a set
\pi=\{B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{p}\}
of
nonempty and disjoint subsets
B_{1},\dots,B_{p}
of
[k]
, called

blocks
, such that their
union is
[k]
. The length
|\pi|
of a partition

is
the number of blocks. The set of the partitions of the set
[k]
is denoted
by
\SP(k)
. Set partitions are in one-to-one
correspondence with equivalence relations:
Any set partition

\pi\in\SP(k)
determines an equivalence relation
i\sim_{\pi}j
on
[k]
by
requiring that
i,j
belong to the same block of
\pi
; conversely, for an equivalence relation
\sim
on
[k]
its equivalence classes determine disjoint subsets of
[k]
and hence a set partition.

(ii)

A subset of
[k]
of form
\{i,i+1,\dots,j\}
is called an
interval

and a set partition of
[k]
is called an
interval partition
if all its blocks are intervals. The set of the interval partitions is denoted by
\Int(k).

(iii)

For set partitions
\sigma,\pi\in\SP(k)
we write
\sigma\leq\pi
if every block of
\sigma
is a subset of a block of
\pi
. This makes
\SP(k)
a poset. The trivial set partition
\{[k]\}
is the maximum of
\SP(k)
, which is denoted by
\hat{1}_{k}
.

(iv)

A tuple
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
induces a unique equivalence
relation
\sim
on
[k]
by the requirement that
p\sim q
holds if and only
if
i_{p}=i_{q}
. The corresponding set partition is called the

kernel set partition
, denoted by
\kappa(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
.

.

.

5.3.
Multivariate cumulants

In order to avoid the discussion of positivity (see Remark
5.4

below) we notice that one can
easily extend the definition of independence to a purely algebraic setting without
positivity. Thus in this section we will
focus on an algebraic cyclic probability space

(\mathcal{A},\varphi,\omega)
without positivity structure, that is,
\mathcal{A}
is
an algebra over
\mathbb{C}
,
\varphi
is a linear functional and
\omega
is a
tracial linear functional.

Take copies
\mathcal{A}_{k}
of
\mathcal{A}
and define the nonunital algebraic free product

\mathcal{U}=\mathop{\ast}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{A}_{k}=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\\
k_{1}\neq\cdots\neq k_{n}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{A}_{k_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{A}_{k_{n}}.

Let
\pi^{(i)}\colon a\mapsto a^{(i)}
denote the embedding of
\mathcal{A}
into
\mathcal{U}
as the

i
-th copy
\mathcal{A}_{i}
. By the universality of tensor products we can define

(\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\omega})
on
\mathcal{U}
as follows: for
n\geq 1
, an alternating tuple
(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
and
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{k_{i}},i=1,2,\dots,n
, set

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}(a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{n})

\displaystyle:=\varphi(a_{1})\varphi(a_{2})\cdots\varphi(a_{n}),

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}(a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{n})

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\omega(a_{1}),&n=1,\\
\varphi(a_{1})\varphi(a_{2})\cdots\varphi(a_{n}),&n\geq 2,k_{1}\neq k_{n},\\
\varphi(a_{n}a_{1})\varphi(a_{2})\cdots\varphi(a_{n-1}),&n\geq 2,k_{1}=k_{n}.\end{cases}

One can check that
\tilde{\omega}
is a trace on
\mathcal{U}
,
\varphi=\tilde{\varphi}\circ\pi^{(i)}
,
\omega=\tilde{\omega}\circ\pi^{(i)}
and the
family of subalgebras
\{\pi^{(i)}(\mathcal{A})\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
is cyclic-Boolean
independent in
(\mathcal{U},\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\omega})
.

Remark 5.4

(Positivity)
.

It is not clear under what conditions the product trace

\tilde{\omega}
preserves positivity.
First observe that the trivial example
\omega=0
shows
that some conditions are necessary.
Indeed,
if
\omega=0
and
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
are self-adjoint then

\tilde{\omega}((a^{(1)}+b^{(2)})^{2})=2\varphi(a)\varphi(b)
,
which can be negative, although both
\varphi
and
\omega

are positive.

This example suggests that in order to expect positivity
of
\tilde{\omega}
one should at least require

\omega(x^{*}x)\geq\left\lvert\varphi(x)\right\rvert^{2}

or

\omega(x^{*}x)\geq\varphi(x^{*}x)
.
Both conditions however are not promoted
to the cyclic free product.
Although the proof of

[
3
, Theorem 2.2]

adapts well to show

\tilde{\omega}(x^{*}x)\geq\left\lvert\tilde{\varphi}(x)\right\rvert^{2}

for
x\in\mathcal{U}_{11}
or
x\in\mathcal{U}_{22}
,
where

\mathcal{U}_{11}=\bigoplus_{n\geq 3}\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\\
k_{1}\neq\cdots\neq k_{n}\\
k_{1}=k_{n}=1\end{subarray}}\mathcal{A}_{k_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{A}_{k_{n}}

etc.,
the following example shows that positivity fails on elements mixing
these subspaces.
Choose
x,y,z\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
w\in\mathcal{U}_{22}

and put
a=x+ywz
. Note that
ywz
is alternating and we compute

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}(a^{*}a)

\displaystyle=\omega(x^{*}x)+\tilde{\omega}(x^{*}ywz)+\tilde{\omega}(z^{*}w^{*}y^{*}x)+\tilde{\omega}(z^{*}w^{*}y^{*}ywz)

\displaystyle=\omega(x^{*}x)+\varphi(zx^{*}y)\,\varphi(w)+\varphi(w^{*})\,\varphi(y^{*}xz^{*})+\varphi(zz^{*})\,\varphi(w^{*})\,\varphi(y^{*}y)\,\varphi(w)

and

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}(a^{*}a)

\displaystyle=\varphi(x^{*}x)+\tilde{\varphi}(x^{*}ywz)+\tilde{\varphi}(z^{*}w^{*}y^{*}x)+\tilde{\varphi}(z^{*}w^{*}y^{*}ywz)

\displaystyle=\varphi(x^{*}x)+\varphi(x^{*}y)\,\varphi(w)\,\varphi(z)+\varphi(z^{*})\,\varphi(w^{*})\,\varphi(y^{*}x)+\varphi(z^{*})\,\varphi(w^{*})\,\varphi(y^{*}y)\,\varphi(w)\,\varphi(z)

Now choose
x,y,z,w
such that

\omega(x^{*}x)=\varphi(x^{*}x),\qquad\varphi(z)=0,\qquad\varphi(zx^{*}y),\varphi(w)\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}.

Such a choice is possible, e.g., when
\omega=\varphi
is a tracial state:
first choose a unitary
u
such that
\varphi(u)=0

and set
z=u
,
y=u^{*}
,
then for selfadjoint
w
and
x

we obtain

\tilde{\omega}(a^{*}a)-\tilde{\varphi}(a^{*}a)=2\varphi(x)\varphi(w)+\left\lvert\varphi(w)\right\rvert^{2}

which can be made negative by an appropriate rescaling of
x
.

The positivity condition
\omega(x^{*}x)\geq\left\lvert\varphi(x)\right\rvert^{2}
proves to be inappropriate as well. In the last specification, we further choose
x
so that
|\varphi(x)|^{2}<\varphi(x^{2})<2|\varphi(x)|^{2}
. Then

\tilde{\omega}(a^{*}a)-|\tilde{\varphi}(a)|^{2}=\varphi(x^{2})-|\varphi(x)|^{2}+2\varphi(x)\varphi(w)+\left\lvert\varphi(w)\right\rvert^{2}.

Replacing
x
with
\lambda x,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, will change this value into

\left[\varphi(x^{2})-|\varphi(x)|^{2}\right]\left[\lambda+\frac{\varphi(x)\varphi(w)}{\varphi(x^{2})-|\varphi(x)|^{2}}\right]^{2}+\frac{|\varphi(w)|^{2}[\varphi(x^{2})-2|\varphi(x)|^{2}]}{\varphi(x^{2})-|\varphi(x)|^{2}}.

Taking
\lambda=-\frac{\varphi(x)\varphi(w)}{\varphi(x^{2})-|\varphi(x)|^{2}}
will make this value negative.

Both pairs
(\mathcal{U},\tilde{\varphi})
and
(\mathcal{U},\tilde{\omega})
are
exchangeability systems in the sense of
[
13
, Definition
1.8]
except that we are not assuming unitality, which
however is not essential for the theory of cumulants. For the first pair we will get Boolean cumulants
B_{\pi}
which are well known; therefore we will focus on
(\mathcal{U},\tilde{\omega})
from now on.
The exchangeability of
(\mathcal{U},\tilde{\omega})
means that, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathcal{A}
, the value of the function

\mathbb{N}^{n}\ni(i_{1},\dots,i_{n})\mapsto\tilde{\omega}(a_{1}^{(i_{1})}a_{2}^{(i_{2})}\cdots a_{n}^{(i_{n})})\in\mathbb{C}

is determined by the kernel set partition
\pi=\kappa(i_{1},\dots,i_{n})
. This value is denoted by
\omega_{\pi}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
, which then gives an
n
-linear functional
\omega_{\pi}\colon\mathcal{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
. For each
\pi\in\SP(n)
a partitioned cumulant
\CBC_{\pi}\colon\mathcal{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
is then defined by

(5.3)

\displaystyle\CBC_{\pi}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho\in\SP(n)\\
\rho\leq\pi\end{subarray}}\omega_{\rho}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})\,\mu(\rho,\pi),

where
\mu
is the Möbius function for the poset
\SP(n)
. By
[
11
, Lemma 4.18 (ii)]
or imitating the proof of
[
13
, Proposition 4.11]
we can prove that
\CBC_{\pi}=0
if
\pi\in\SP(n)\setminus\CI(n)
. This vanishing property and the Möbius inversion of (
5.3
) imply that

(5.4)

\omega(a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{n})=\sum_{\pi\in\CI(n)}\CBC_{\pi}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}).

To compute the non-vanishing cumulants we distinguish three cases.

(i)

Let
\pi\in\CI(n)
and first assume that
\pi\in\Int(n)
. Now if
\pi<\hat{1}_{n}
then
1\not\sim_{\pi}n
and
moreover
1\not\sim_{\rho}n
for any
\rho
satisfying
\rho\leq\pi
.
This allows us to
replace
\tilde{\omega}
by
\tilde{\varphi}
to obtain

\displaystyle\CBC_{\pi}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\rho\leq\pi}\varphi_{\rho}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})\,\mu(\rho,\pi)=B_{\pi}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n});

see
[
13
, Proposition 4.11]
for the last equality.

(ii)

Let us assume next that
\pi\in\CI(n)\setminus\Int(n)
.
This means that
\pi<\hat{1}_{n}
and that
1\sim_{\pi}n
.
In this case we cannot immediately replace
\omega
by
\varphi
,
but first must use the traciality of
\omega
and rotate the partition

\pi
into an element of
\Int(n)
.
Indeed fix a cyclic permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that

\sigma\cdot\pi\in\Int(n)
. Then
1\not\sim_{\pi}n
and
also
1\not\sim_{\rho}n
for any
\rho\leq\pi
and we have

\displaystyle\CBC_{\pi}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})

\displaystyle=\CBC_{\sigma\cdot\pi}(a_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},a_{\sigma^{-1}(2)},\dots,a_{\sigma^{-1}(n)})

\displaystyle=B_{\sigma\cdot\pi}(a_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},a_{\sigma^{-1}(2)},\dots,a_{\sigma^{-1}(n)});

notice that the rotation cannot be reversed now because the Boolean
cumulants are nontracial.

(iii)

Finally if
\pi=\hat{1}_{n}
there is no direct formula but we infer from
the moment-cumulant formula (
5.4
) that

\CBC_{n}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})=\omega(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})-\sum_{\pi\in\CI(n),\pi<\hat{1}_{n}}B_{\sigma\cdot\pi}(a_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},a_{\sigma^{-1}(2)},\dots,a_{\sigma^{-1}(n)}),

where for each
\pi
an appropriate cyclic permutation
\sigma
is chosen.

Remark 5.5
.

Note that for univariate cumulants the rotation does not change the value
of the cumulant and we can write

(5.5)

\omega(a^{n})=c_{n}(a)+\sum_{\pi\in\CI(n),\pi<\hat{1}_{n}}b_{\pi}(a),

where
b_{\pi}(a)=B_{\pi}(a,a,\dots,a)
and
c_{n}(a)=\CBC_{\hat{1}_{n}}(a,a,\dots,a)
.

We can see that the definition of
c_{n}(a)
in Remark
5.5
coincides with that in (
5.2
). This can be confirmed from the definition and uniqueness of cumulants, but here we directly prove the formula (
5.1
) for
c_{n}(a)=\CBC_{\hat{1}_{n}}(a,a,\dots,a)
using the recurrence relation
(
5.5
).
Decomposing
\CI(n)
into
\Int(n)
and
\CI(n)\setminus\Int(n)
we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\CI(n),\pi\neq\hat{1}_{n}}b_{\pi}(a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\Int(n),\pi\neq\hat{1}_{n}}b_{\pi}(a)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell\geq 1\\
k+\ell\leq n-1\end{subarray}}b_{k+\ell}(a)\sum_{\sigma\in\Int(n-k-\ell)}b_{\sigma}(a)

\displaystyle=\varphi(a^{n})-b_{n}(a)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell\geq 1\\
k+\ell\leq n-1\end{subarray}}b_{k+\ell}(a)\,\varphi(a^{n-k-\ell}).

Multiplying the above by
z^{n}
and taking the sum over
n
in (
5.5
) yields

\displaystyle\MG_{a}(z)

\displaystyle=\CB_{a}(z)+M_{a}(z)-B_{a}(z)+\sum_{k,\ell\geq 1}b_{k+\ell}(a)z^{k+\ell}\sum_{n\geq k+\ell+1}\varphi(a^{n-k-\ell})z^{n-k-\ell}

\displaystyle=\CB_{a}(z)+M_{a}(z)-B_{a}(z)+(zB_{a}^{\prime}(z)-B_{a}(z))M_{a}(z)=\CB_{a}(z)+zM_{a}(z)B_{a}^{\prime}(z).

6.
Cyclic-Boolean infinite divisibility

This section is devoted to the definition and classification of infinite divisibility. Due to the lack of a precise notion of positivity (see Remark
5.4
), we are not able to treat general
\ast
-algebras with a state and a tracial linear functional. Hence, we give the definition of infinitely divisible distributions in the special setting of operators on Hilbert spaces where the linear functional
\omega
is chosen to be the trace.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space and
\varphi
a state on
B(H)
. An element
a\in T(H)_{\rm sa}
is said to be cyclic-Boolean infinitely divisible if for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exist a Hilbert space
H_{n}
and a state
\varphi_{n}
on
B(H_{n})
and cyclic-Boolean i.i.d. elements
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in T(H_{n})_{\rm sa}
such that
a
with respect to
(\varphi,{\rm Tr}_{H})
has the same distribution as
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}
with respect to
(\varphi_{n},{\rm Tr}_{H_{n}})
.

Suppose that
a
is a trace class selfadjoint operator and cyclic-Boolean ID. For each
n\geq 2
,
a
equals the sum of certain cyclic-Boolean iid random variables
a_{n,1},\dots,a_{n,n}
in distribution, and let
t=1/n
and denote
\RC_{t}=\RC_{a_{n,i}}
and
G_{t}=G_{a_{n,i}}
.
Let
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be the set of mutually distinct eigenvalues of
a
and
m_{i}
be the multiplicity of
\lambda_{i}
. Setting
I_{0}=\{i\in I:\lambda_{i}\neq 0\}
we have

\RC_{a}(z)=\sum_{i\in I_{0}}\frac{m_{i}\lambda_{i}}{z(z-\lambda_{i})}.

Moreover, let
E_{a}
be the spectral decomposition of
a
and
p_{i}=\varphi(E_{a}(\{\lambda_{i}\}))\geq 0
; then we have

G_{a}(z)=\sum_{i\in I^{\prime}}\frac{p_{i}}{z-\lambda_{i}},\qquad I^{\prime}=\{i\in I:p_{i}>0\}.

For later use we also set

I^{\prime}_{0}=I^{\prime}\cap I_{0}.

By calculus, we see that
G_{a}
has a unique zero in each interval between neighboring poles and has no other zeros off the real line. Hence the set
\{\mu_{j}\}_{j\in J}
of zeros of
G_{a}
is contained in
\mathbb{R}
and is interlacing with
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in I^{\prime}}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

The factorization

G_{a}(z)=\frac{1}{z}\prod_{j\in J}\left(1-\frac{\mu_{j}}{z}\right)\prod_{i\in I^{\prime}}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{i}}{z}\right)^{-1}

holds for every
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(\{0\}\cup\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in I^{\prime}})
.

Proof.

When the set
I^{\prime}
is finite, the conclusion is easily proved since
G_{a}
is a rational function. We may then assume that
I^{\prime}
and hence
I_{0}^{\prime}
is an infinite set. We decompose the set
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in I_{0}^{\prime}}
into the positive part
\{\lambda_{k}^{+}\}_{k=1}^{n_{+}}
and the negative part
\{\lambda_{k}^{-}\}_{k=1}^{n_{-}}
arranged in the way

\lambda_{1}^{-}<\lambda_{2}^{-}<\cdots<0<\cdots<\lambda_{2}^{+}<\lambda_{1}^{+},

where
n_{\pm}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0,\infty\}
. By our assumption,
n_{-}
or
n_{+}
is infinity. We rewrite the function
G_{a}
into the form

G_{a}(z)=\frac{p}{z}+\sum_{k=1}^{n_{+}}\frac{p_{k}^{+}}{z-\lambda_{k}^{+}}+\sum_{k=1}^{n_{-}}\frac{p_{k}^{-}}{z-\lambda_{k}^{-}},\qquad p=\sum_{i\in I^{\prime}:\lambda_{i}=0}p_{i}\in[0,1],

where
p_{k}^{\pm}
is weight of
\lambda_{k}^{\pm}
with respect to
\varphi
.
Now introduce
n_{\pm}(\epsilon)=\max\{k\geq 1:|\lambda_{k}^{\pm}|>\epsilon\}<\infty

and then the truncated function

G_{a}^{\epsilon}(z)=\frac{p(\epsilon)}{z}+\sum_{k=1}^{n_{+}(\epsilon)}\frac{p_{k}^{+}}{z-\lambda_{k}^{+}}+\sum_{k=1}^{n_{-}(\epsilon)}\frac{p_{k}^{-}}{z-\lambda_{k}^{-}},\qquad p(\epsilon)=p+\sum_{k:|\lambda_{k}^{+}|\leq\epsilon}p_{k}^{+}+\sum_{k:|\lambda_{k}^{-}|\leq\epsilon}p_{k}^{-}.

The fact that
n_{-}
or
n_{+}
is infinity implies that
p(\epsilon)>0
for every
\epsilon>0
. Since
G_{a}^{\epsilon}
is a rational function, we have

(6.1)

G_{a}^{\epsilon}(z)=\frac{1}{z}\prod_{k=1}^{n_{-}(\epsilon)}\left(\frac{z-\mu_{k}^{-}(\epsilon)}{z-\lambda_{k}^{-}}\right)\prod_{k=1}^{n_{+}(\epsilon)}\left(\frac{z-\mu_{k}^{+}(\epsilon)}{z-\lambda_{k}^{+}}\right),

where
\mu_{k}^{\pm}(\epsilon)
is the unique zero of
G_{a}^{\epsilon}
on the interval between
\lambda_{k}^{\pm}
and
\lambda_{k+1}^{\pm}
for
k=1,2,\dots,n_{\pm}(\epsilon)-1
and
\mu_{n_{\pm}(\epsilon)}^{\pm}(\epsilon)
is the unique zero of
G_{a}
on the interval between
0
and
\lambda_{n_{\pm}(\epsilon)}^{\pm}
.

In order to pass to the limit in (
6.1
), first note that
n_{\pm}(\epsilon)\to n_{\pm}
as
\epsilon\to 0
. Since
G_{a}^{\epsilon}
converges locally uniformly to
G_{a}
on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,\lambda_{k}^{+},\lambda_{k}^{-}:k\geq 1\}
, we conclude that
\mu_{k}^{\pm}(\epsilon)
converges to
\mu_{k}^{\pm}
as
\epsilon\to 0
for each
k
. From (the product version of) Weierstrass’ M-test (recall that
|\mu_{k}^{\pm}(\epsilon)|\leq|\lambda_{k}^{\pm}|
) we obtain

G_{a}(z)=\frac{1}{z}\prod_{k=1}^{n_{-}}\left(\frac{z-\mu_{k}^{-}}{z-\lambda_{k}^{-}}\right)\prod_{k=1}^{n_{+}}\left(\frac{z-\mu_{k}^{+}}{z-\lambda_{k}^{+}}\right),

the desired formula.
∎

Now we are able to characterize infinitely divisible measures with respect to cyclic independence.

First, notice that taking the logarithmic derivative in Lemma
6.2
yields that

\frac{G_{a}^{\prime}(z)}{G_{a}(z)}=-\frac{1}{z}-\sum_{i\in I^{\prime}_{0}}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{z(z-\lambda_{i})}+\sum_{j\in J}\frac{\mu_{j}}{z(z-\mu_{j})}.

As before, let
t=1/n
and denote
\RC_{t}=\RC_{a_{n,i}}
and
G_{t}=G_{a_{n,i}}
. Let
\{\lambda_{i}(t)\}_{i\in I^{\prime}_{0}(t)}
be the set of (mutually distinct) non-zero poles of
G_{t}
. Since

G_{t}(z)=\frac{G_{a}(z)}{(1-t)zG_{a}(z)+t},

the set
\{\lambda_{i}(t)\}_{i\in I^{\prime}_{0}(t)}
is exactly the set of the zeros of
(1-t)zG_{a}(z)+t
. On the other hand, the set of zeros of
G_{t}
is exactly the set of the zeros of
G_{a}
, and hence

\frac{G_{t}^{\prime}(z)}{G_{t}(z)}=-\frac{1}{z}-\sum_{i\in I^{\prime}_{0}(t)}\frac{\lambda_{i}(t)}{z(z-\lambda_{i}(t))}+\sum_{j\in J}\frac{\mu_{j}}{z(z-\mu_{j})}.

By Theorem
4.2
we have

\RC_{a}+\frac{G_{a}^{\prime}}{G_{a}}+\frac{1}{z}=n\left(\RC_{t}+\frac{G_{t}^{\prime}}{G_{t}}+\frac{1}{z}\right)

and hence

\displaystyle\RC_{t}

\displaystyle=t\RC_{a}+t\frac{G_{a}^{\prime}}{G_{a}}-\frac{G_{t}^{\prime}}{G_{t}}+\frac{t-1}{z}

\displaystyle=t\sum_{i\in I_{0}}\frac{m_{i}\lambda_{i}}{z(z-\lambda_{i})}+t\left(-\frac{1}{z}-\sum_{i\in I^{\prime}_{0}}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{z(z-\lambda_{i})}+\sum_{j\in J}\frac{\mu_{j}}{z(z-\mu_{j})}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\left(-\frac{1}{z}-\sum_{i\in I^{\prime}_{0}(t)}\frac{\lambda_{i}(t)}{z(z-\lambda_{i}(t))}+\sum_{j\in J}\frac{\mu_{j}}{z(z-\mu_{j})}\right)+\frac{t-1}{z}

\displaystyle=t\sum_{i\in I^{\prime}_{0}}\frac{(m_{i}-1)\lambda_{i}}{z(z-\lambda_{i})}+t\sum_{i\in I_{0}\setminus I^{\prime}_{0}}\frac{m_{i}\lambda_{i}}{z(z-\lambda_{i})}-(1-t)\sum_{j\in J}\frac{\mu_{j}}{z(z-\mu_{j})}+\sum_{i\in I^{\prime}_{0}(t)}\frac{\lambda_{i}(t)}{z(z-\lambda_{i}(t))}.

Now, for each non-zero real
\alpha
the number
\lim_{z\to\alpha}(z-\alpha)\RC_{t}(z)
is non-negative, as it is a positive integer if
\alpha
is a non-zero eigenvalue of
a_{n,1}
and zero otherwise.

Therefore, to cancel the negative coefficient
-(1-t)
above, the only possibility is that each non-zero
\mu_{j}
must be a member of
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in I_{0}}\cup\{\lambda_{i}(t)\}_{i\in I_{0}^{\prime}(t)}
and because of interlacing of the zeros and poles of
G_{a}
, one sees that
\mu_{j}
can not be included in
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in I_{0}^{\prime}}\cup\{\lambda_{i}(t)\}_{i\in I_{0}^{\prime}(t)}
as it is a zero of
G_{a}
.

It is possible that
\mu_{j}=\lambda_{i}
for some
i\in I_{0}\setminus I_{0}^{\prime}
. In this case, however, for
t>0
sufficiently small (namely,
n
sufficiently large) the coefficient
tm_{i}-(1-t)
is negative; therefore, we conclude that
\mu_{j}=0
for all
j\in J
or
J=\emptyset
, and hence
\#J=0
or
1
. This happens only if
\#I^{\prime}=0,1
or
2
.

On the other hand, for
i\in I_{0}^{\prime}
we have
\lim_{z\to\lambda_{i}}(z-\lambda_{i})\RC_{t}(z)=t(m_{i}-1)
which must be a non-negative integer for any
t=1/n
. Therefore, we conclude that
m_{i}=1
for all
i\in I_{0}^{\prime}
. For
i\in I_{0}\setminus I_{0}^{\prime}
we have
\lim_{z\to\lambda_{i}}(z-\lambda_{i})\RC_{t}(z)=tm_{i}
, which cannot be an integer for sufficiently small
t
, and hence
I_{0}^{\prime}=I_{0}
.

Now it remains to study the possible cases for
I_{0}^{\prime}=I_{0}=0,1
or
2
.

Case 1:
\#I_{0}^{\prime}=\#I_{0}=0
or 1. Then
G_{a}(z)=1/(z-\alpha)
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
J=\emptyset
. The function
\RC_{a}(z)=\frac{\alpha}{z(z-\alpha)}
and
\RC_{t}(z)=t\alpha/(z-t\alpha)
.

Case 2:
\#I_{0}^{\prime}=\#I_{0}=2
. Note that
G_{a}
cannot have a pole at 0 because it would create a non-zero
\mu_{j}
. Hence
G_{a}(z)=p/(z-\alpha)+(1-p)/(z-\beta)
for some
\alpha,\beta\neq 0,\alpha<\beta,0<p<1
, and
G_{a}(0)=-p/\alpha-(1-p)/\beta=0
. The last condition yields the restriction that
\alpha<0<\beta
and
G_{a}(z)=z/[(z-\alpha)(z-\beta)]
. Solving the equation
(1-t)zG_{a}(z)+t=0
we obtain two solutions

\lambda_{\pm}(t)=\frac{t(\alpha+\beta)\pm\sqrt{t^{2}(\alpha+\beta)^{2}-4t\alpha\beta}}{2}.

The eigenvalues can be retrieved from the formula

\RC_{t}(z)=\frac{\lambda_{+}(t)}{z(z-\lambda_{+}(t))}+\frac{\lambda_{-}(t)}{z(z-\lambda_{-}(t))}.

It is easy to see that the above cases are actually cyclic Boolean ID. Thus we arrive to the following.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space and
\varphi
be a state on
T(H)
. An element
a\in T(H)_{\rm sa}
is cyclic-Boolean ID with respect to
(\varphi,{\rm Tr}_{H})
if and only if
a
has either

(i)

only zero eigenvalues (that is,
a=0
),

(ii)

only one non-zero eigenvalue and its multiplicity is one, or

(iii)

exactly two non-zero eigenvalues
\alpha,\beta
, their multiplicities are one,
\alpha\beta<0
and the distribution of
a
with respect to
\varphi
is

\frac{-\alpha}{\beta-\alpha}\delta_{\alpha}+\frac{\beta}{\beta-\alpha}\delta_{\beta}.

In the last case, for every
n\geq 2
an
n
-th root of
a
has two non-zero eigenvalues
\alpha_{n},\beta_{n}
given as the solutions to the equation

x^{2}-\frac{\alpha+\beta}{n}x+\frac{\alpha\beta}{n}=0,

and the distribution with respect to the state is

\frac{-\alpha_{n}}{\beta_{n}-\alpha_{n}}\delta_{\alpha_{n}}+\frac{\beta_{n}}{\beta_{n}-\alpha_{n}}\delta_{\beta_{n}}.

Example 6.4
.

The matrix

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\\
\end{pmatrix}

has eigenvalues
\{[1]^{1},[-1]^{1}\}
and its distribution with respect to the unit vector
e_{1}={}^{t}(1,0)
is

\frac{1}{2}\delta_{-1}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{1}.

By Theorem
6.3
,
A
is cyclic-Boolean infinitely divisible with respect to
(\langle\,\cdot\,e_{1},e_{1}\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{2}},{\rm Tr}_{\mathbb{C}^{2}})
.

7.
Cyclic-monotone independence

7.1.
Definition and example

We perform an investigation for monotone independence in a spirit similar to
cyclic-Boolean independence. To this end we start from a specific operator
model inspired by the comb product of rooted graphs in Section
2.7
.

Example 7.1
.

Let
H_{i},i\in\mathbb{N}
, be
finite-dimensional
Hilbert spaces with
distinguished unit vectors
\xi_{i}\in H_{i}
respectively. Let
P_{i}\colon H_{i}\to H_{i}
be the orthogonal projection onto
\mathbb{C}\xi_{i}
and
\varphi_{i}

be the vector state on
B(H_{i})
defined by
\xi_{i}
. Let
H=H_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes H_{N}
,
\xi=\xi_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\xi_{N}
and
\varphi

be the vacuum state on
B(H)
defined by
\xi
. This is the same setting
as in Example
3.1
with the additional requirement of finite
dimensionality.
Analogously to the embedding (
3.1
)
we introduce another embedding of
B(H_{i})
into
B(H)
:

(7.1)

\sigma_{i}(A)=I_{H_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes I_{H_{i-1}}\otimes A\otimes P_{i+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{N}.

Note that this embedding does not preserve trace class and therefore
the construction is restricted to finite dimensional spaces.
It is known that the family
\{\sigma_{i}(B(H_{i}))\}_{i=1}^{N}
is monotonically independent with respect to
\varphi
; see
[
12
, Theorem 8.9]
.

In addition, we can compute moments with respect to the trace. Again formula
(
3.2
) is crucial: for a cyclically alternating tuple
(i_{1},\dots,i_{n})\in[N]^{n}
and
A_{k}\in B(H_{i_{k}})
, if
p\in[n]
is such that
i_{p-1}<i_{p}>i_{p+1}
(with the conventions
i_{0}=i_{n}
and
i_{n+1}=i_{1}
) then direct computations entail

{\rm Tr}_{H}(\sigma_{i_{1}}(A_{1})\cdots\sigma_{i_{n}}(A_{n}))=\varphi_{i_{p}}(A_{p}){\rm Tr}_{H}\left[\sigma_{i_{1}}(A_{1})\cdots\sigma_{i_{p-1}}(A_{p-1})\sigma_{i_{p+1}}(A_{p+1})\cdots\sigma_{i_{n}}(A_{n})\right],\quad n\geq 2.

This example can be abstracted in the following way.

Definition 7.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\varphi,\omega)
be a cncps,
I
be a toset, and
\hat{I}:=\{-\infty\}\cup I
be an enlargement of
I
, where
-\infty
is the minimum of
\hat{I}
. An ordered family of
*
-subalgebras
\{\mathcal{A}_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
\mathcal{A}
is said to be cyclic-monotone independent if

(i)

it is monotonically independent with respect to
\varphi
, that is, for any
n\geq 2
, any alternating tuple
(i_{1},\dots,i_{n})\in I^{n}
(namely
i_{1}\neq\cdots\neq i_{n}
) and
a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{i_{k}},k=1,2,\dots,n
, if
p\in[n]
is such that
i_{p-1}<i_{p}>i_{p+1}
(with conventions
i_{0}=i_{n+1}=-\infty
) then

\varphi(a_{1}\cdots a_{n})=\varphi(a_{p})\varphi(a_{1}\cdots a_{p-1}a_{p+1}\cdots a_{n});

(ii)

for any
n\geq 2
, cyclically alternating tuple
(i_{1},\dots,i_{n})\in I^{n}
(namely
i_{1}\neq\cdots\neq i_{n}\neq i_{1}
) and
a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{i_{k}},k=1,2,\dots,n
, if
p\in[n]
is such that
i_{p-1}<i_{p}>i_{p+1}
(with different conventions
i_{0}=i_{n}
and
i_{n+1}=i_{1}
) then

\omega(a_{1}\cdots a_{n})=\varphi(a_{p})\omega(a_{1}\cdots a_{p-1}a_{p+1}\cdots a_{n}).

Definition 7.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\varphi,\omega)
be a cncps and
I
be a toset. An ordered family of elements
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
\mathcal{A}
is said to be cyclic-monotone independent if so is
\{\mathcal{A}_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, where
\mathcal{A}_{i}
is the
\ast
-algebra generated by
a_{i}
without unit.

Example 7.4
.

Suppose that
(a,b,c)
is cyclic-monotone independent in
(\mathcal{A},\varphi,\omega)
. Then

\varphi(ba^{2}bac^{2}b)=\varphi(c^{2})\varphi(b)^{3}\varphi(a^{3})

and

\omega(ba^{2}bac^{2}b)=\varphi(c^{2})\varphi(b)\varphi(b^{2})\omega(a^{3}).

Remark 7.5
.

Cyclic-monotone independence already appeared in the random matrix model in

[
5
]
(see also
[
20
,
2
]
and Section
1
),
where independence was defined for a pair of
\ast
-subalgebras and only for

\omega
. For a random matrix model for monotone independence see
[
4
]
.

In
[
5
]
the trace functional
\omega
is unbounded,
because it can diverge in the large dimensional limit, and therefore a domain
for
\omega
was specified.
To avoid this problem
in the present paper we focus on finite dimensional Hilbert spaces and
\omega={\rm Tr}
.

It should be noticed that Example
7.1
does not provide an i.i.d. operator model even when
H_{i}=K
does not depend on
i
; for
A\in B(K)
the operators
\{\sigma_{i}(A)\}_{i=1}^{N}
are identically distributed with respect to
\varphi
, but not with respect to
\omega
, because

\omega(\sigma_{i}(A))=d^{i-1}{\rm Tr}_{K}(A),

where
d={\rm dim}(K)
. In fact we do not know of any non-trivial operator model
for cyclic monotone i.i.d. random variables and for this reason
we do not see any meaningful notions of cumulants and of infinitely divisible distributions.

7.2.
Cyclic-monotone convolution

The convolution formula can be verified in ways.

Theorem 7.6
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\varphi,\omega)
be a cncps and
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
. Suppose that
(a,b)
is cyclic-monotone independent. We then have

\RC_{a+b}(z)=\RC_{b}(z)+F_{b}^{\prime}(z)\RC_{a}(F_{b}(z)).

Algebraic proof.

Expand
(a+b)^{n}
into

\displaystyle(a+b)^{n}

\displaystyle=b^{n}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq 1\\
q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{k+1}\geq 0\\
q_{1}+\cdots+q_{k+1}+k=n\end{subarray}}b^{q_{1}}ab^{q_{2}}a\cdots ab^{q_{k+1}},

and applying
\omega
yields

\displaystyle\omega((a+b)^{n})=\omega(b^{n})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq 1\\
q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{k+1}\geq 0\\
q_{1}+\cdots+q_{k+1}+k=n\end{subarray}}\varphi(b^{q_{1}+q_{k+1}})\varphi(b^{q_{2}})\cdots\varphi(b^{q_{k}})\omega(a^{k}).

Multiplying the above identity by
z^{-n-1}
and taking the summation over
n
yields

\displaystyle\RC_{a+b}(z)

\displaystyle=\RC_{b}(z)+\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{q_{1},\dots,q_{k+1}\geq 0}\frac{\varphi(b^{q_{1}+q_{k+1}})}{z^{q_{1}+q_{k+1}}}\frac{\varphi(b^{q_{2}})}{z^{q_{2}}}\cdots\frac{\varphi(b^{q_{k}})}{z^{q_{k}}}\frac{\omega(a^{k})}{z^{k+1}}

\displaystyle=\RC_{b}(z)-z^{2}G_{b}^{\prime}(z)\sum_{k\geq 1}[zG_{b}(z)]^{k-1}\frac{\omega(a^{k})}{z^{k+1}}

\displaystyle=\RC_{b}(z)-\frac{G_{b}^{\prime}(z)}{G_{b}(z)^{2}}\sum_{k\geq 1}G_{b}(z)^{k+1}\omega(a^{k})

\displaystyle=\RC_{b}(z)+F_{b}^{\prime}(z)\RC_{a}(F_{b}(z)).

Note here that the identity

\sum_{m\geq 0,n\geq 0}^{\infty}\frac{\varphi(b^{m+n})}{z^{m+n}}=-z^{2}G_{b}^{\prime}(z)

is used above.
∎

Analytic proof in the setting of Example
7.1
: Schur complement approach.

Let
A\in B(\mathcal{H}_{1})
and
B\in B(\mathcal{H}_{2})
be operators with
block decompositions

A=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&a^{\prime}\\
a&\mathring{A}\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad B=\begin{bmatrix}\beta&b^{\prime}\\
b&\mathring{B}\end{bmatrix}

according to the decomposition
\mathcal{H}_{i}=\mathbb{C}\xi_{i}\oplus\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
as in (
2.12
)
and
let
\sigma_{1}(A)=A\otimes P_{2}
and
\sigma_{2}(B)=I_{1}\otimes B
act
on
\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}\simeq\mathbb{C}\xi\oplus\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\oplus(\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{2})

according to Example
7.1
, i.e., if we denote by
\eta_{1}:\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{1}
the embedding
and
\eta_{1}^{*}:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
the projection, then

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(A)\xi

\displaystyle=\alpha\xi\oplus a\oplus 0

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}(B)\xi

\displaystyle=\beta\xi\oplus 0\oplus(\xi_{1}\otimes b)

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(A)\mathring{h}_{1}

\displaystyle=(a^{\prime}\mathring{h}_{1})\xi\oplus\mathring{A}\mathring{h}_{1}\oplus 0

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}(B)\mathring{h}_{1}

\displaystyle=0\oplus\beta\mathring{h}_{1}\oplus(\eta_{1}(\mathring{h}_{1})\otimes b)

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(A)(h_{1}\otimes\mathring{h}_{2})

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}(B)(h_{1}\otimes\mathring{h}_{2})

\displaystyle=(b^{\prime}\mathring{h}_{2})(\xi_{1}^{*}h_{1})\xi\oplus(b^{\prime}\mathring{h}_{2})\eta_{1}^{*}(h_{1})\oplus(h_{1}\otimes\mathring{B}\mathring{h}_{2})

and we obtain the block decompositions

a&\mathring{A}&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{2}(B)=\begin{bmatrix}\beta&0&\xi_{1}^{*}\otimes b^{\prime}\\

\xi_{1}\otimes b&\eta_{1}\otimes b&I_{1}\otimes\mathring{B}\end{bmatrix}

and together

\sigma_{1}(A)+\sigma_{2}(B)=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+\beta&a^{\prime}&\xi_{1}^{*}\otimes b^{\prime}\\
a&\mathring{A}+\beta\mathring{I}_{1}&\eta_{1}^{*}\otimes b^{\prime}\\

We compute the resolvent

(z-\sigma_{1}(A)-\sigma_{2}(B))^{-1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|cc}z-\alpha-\beta&-a^{\prime}&-\xi_{1}^{*}\otimes b^{\prime}\\
\hline\cr-a&(z-\beta)\mathring{I}_{1}-\mathring{A}&-\eta_{1}^{*}\otimes b^{\prime}\\
-\xi_{1}\otimes b&-\eta_{1}\otimes b&I_{1}\otimes(z\mathring{I}_{2}-\mathring{B})\end{array}\right]^{-1}

via the Schur complement.
To this end we first compute the lower resolvent

on
\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\oplus(\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathring{\mathcal{H}}_{2})
.
The corresponding Schur complement of

is

\displaystyle S_{L}

\displaystyle=(z-\beta)\mathring{I}_{1}-\mathring{A}-(\eta_{1}^{*}\otimes b^{\prime})(I_{1}\otimes(z\mathring{I}_{2}-\mathring{B})^{-1})(\eta_{1}\otimes b)

\displaystyle=(z-\beta)\mathring{I}_{1}-\mathring{A}-\eta_{1}^{*}I_{1}\eta_{1}\otimes b^{\prime}(z\mathring{I}_{2}-\mathring{B})^{-1}b

If we denote by

2.9
)

) can be written as

\displaystyle L^{-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}S_{L}^{-1}&S_{L}^{-1}(\eta_{1}^{*}\otimes b^{\prime})(I_{1}\otimes\resolv_{\mathring{B}}(z))\\

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\resolv_{\mathring{A}}(F_{B}(z))&\resolv_{\mathring{A}}(F_{B}(z))\eta_{1}^{*}\otimes b^{\prime}\resolv_{\mathring{B}}(z)\\

Now we plug

into Banachiewicz’ formula (
2.9

(z-\sigma_{1}(A)-\sigma_{2}(B))^{-1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}S^{-1}&S^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}a^{\prime}&\xi_{1}^{*}\otimes b^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}L^{-1}\\
\hline\cr L^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}a\\
\xi_{1}\otimes b\end{bmatrix}S^{-1}&L^{-1}+L^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}a\\
\xi_{1}\otimes b\end{bmatrix}S^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}a^{\prime}&\xi_{1}^{*}\otimes b^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}L^{-1}\end{array}\right].

After some cancellations the Schur complement evaluates to

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=F_{\sigma_{1}(A)+\sigma_{2}(B)}(z)

\displaystyle=z-\alpha-\beta-\begin{bmatrix}-a^{\prime}&-\xi_{1}^{*}\otimes b^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}L^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}-a\\
-\xi_{1}\otimes b\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=z-\alpha-\beta-a^{\prime}(F_{B}(z)\mathring{I}_{1}-\mathring{A})^{-1}a-\xi_{1}^{*}\xi_{1}b^{\prime}(z\mathring{I}_{2}-\mathring{B})^{-1}b

\displaystyle=F_{B}(z)-\alpha-a^{\prime}(F_{B}(z)\mathring{I}_{1}-\mathring{A})^{-1}a

\displaystyle=F_{A}(F_{B}(z))

where we used the Schur complement representation
(

).
Finally the resolvent is

(z-\sigma_{1}(A)-\sigma_{2}(B))^{-1}\\
=G_{A}(F_{B}(z))\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}1&a^{\prime}\resolv_{\mathring{A}}(F_{B}(z))\qquad[a^{\prime}\resolv_{\mathring{A}}(F_{B}(z))\eta_{1}^{*}+\xi_{1}^{*}]\otimes b^{\prime}\resolv_{\mathring{B}}(z)\\
\hline\cr\begin{matrix}\resolv_{\mathring{A}}(F_{B}(z))a\\

with

Finally the trace of the resolvent evaluates to

\displaystyle\Cauchy_{\sigma_{1}(A)+\sigma_{2}(B)}(z)

\displaystyle=G_{A}(F_{B}(z))+{\rm Tr}(L^{-1})+G_{A}(F_{B}(z)){\rm Tr}(L_{2})

\displaystyle=G_{A}(F_{B}(z))+\Cauchy_{\mathring{A}}(F_{B}(z))+{\rm Tr}(I_{1})\Cauchy_{\mathring{B}}(z)+{\rm Tr}[\eta_{1}(F_{B}(z)-\mathring{A})^{-1}\eta_{1}^{*}]

\displaystyle=G_{A}(F_{B}(z))+\Cauchy_{\mathring{A}}(F_{B}(z))+d_{1}\Cauchy_{\mathring{B}}(z)+\Cauchy_{\mathring{A}}(F_{B}(z))(F_{B}^{\prime}(z)-1)

\displaystyle\phantom{==}+G_{A}(F_{B}(z))[F_{A}^{\prime}(F_{B}(z))-1+F_{A}^{\prime}(F_{B}(z))(F_{B}^{\prime}(z)-1)]

\displaystyle=d_{1}\Cauchy_{\mathring{B}}(z)+\Cauchy_{A}(F_{B}(z))F_{B}^{\prime}(z).

∎

We note here that another equivalent convolution formula can be given in terms of

\KK_{a}(z)=-\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\omega(a^{n})}{nz^{n}}.

7.3.
Limit theorem

In the setting of Example
7.1
, let
H_{i}
be the same Hilbert space
K
with

and with a distinguished unit vector
\xi
and let
a^{(i)}=\sigma_{i}(a)
for some

. Let
\omega
be the trace on
H
,
{\rm Tr}
be the trace on
K
and
\psi
be the vector state on
K

.

Our main object in this section is the sum

(7.4)

b_{N}=a^{(1)}+\dots+a^{(N)}.

In order to see the convergence of trace moments of

we start from some examples.
Since
{\rm Tr}(I_{K})=d
, we obtain

\omega(b_{N})={\rm Tr}(a)+d{\rm Tr}(a)+\cdots+d^{N-1}{\rm Tr}(a)=[N]_{d}{\rm Tr}(a),

where
[N]_{d}=1+d+d^{2}+\dots+d^{N-1}
, and

\displaystyle\omega(b_{N}^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}\omega(a^{(i)}a^{(j)})+\sum_{i>j}\omega(a^{(i)}a^{(j)})+\sum_{i}\omega((a^{(i)})^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}\omega(a^{(i)})\varphi(a^{(j)})+\sum_{i>j}\varphi(a^{(i)})\omega(a^{(j)})+[N]_{d}{\rm Tr}(a^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{N}\frac{d^{j-1}-1}{d-1}{\rm Tr}(a)\psi(a)+\sum_{i=2}^{N}\frac{d^{i-1}-1}{d-1}{\rm Tr}(a)\psi(a)+[N]_{d}{\rm Tr}(a^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{d-1}\left[\frac{d(d^{N-1}-1)}{d-1}-(N-1)\right]{\rm Tr}(a)\psi(a)+[N]_{d}{\rm Tr}(a^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{d-1}([N]_{d}-N){\rm Tr}(a)\psi(a)+[N]_{d}{\rm Tr}(a^{2}).

A similar computation yields that

\displaystyle\omega(b_{N}^{3})

\displaystyle=6\left[\frac{[N]_{d}-N}{(d-1)^{2}}-\frac{N(N-1)}{2(d-1)}\right]{\rm Tr}(a)\psi(a)^{2}+\frac{3}{d-1}([N]_{d}-N){\rm Tr}(a^{2})\psi(a)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{3}{d-1}([N]_{d}-N){\rm Tr}(a)\psi(a^{2})+[N]_{d}{\rm Tr}(a^{3}).

Therefore, the normalized traces converge as
N\to\infty
without rescaling of
b_{N}
:

\displaystyle d^{-N}\omega(b_{N})

\displaystyle\to\frac{{\rm Tr}(a)}{d-1},

\displaystyle d^{-N}\omega(b_{N}^{2})

\displaystyle\to\frac{2}{(d-1)^{2}}{\rm Tr}(a)\psi(a)+\frac{1}{d-1}{\rm Tr}(a^{2}),

\displaystyle d^{-N}\omega(b_{N}^{3})

\displaystyle\to\frac{6}{(d-1)^{3}}{\rm Tr}(a)\psi(a)^{2}+\frac{3}{(d-1)^{2}}{\rm Tr}(a^{2})\psi(a)+\frac{3}{(d-1)^{2}}{\rm Tr}(a)\psi(a^{2})+\frac{1}{d-1}{\rm Tr}(a^{3}).

In order to describe the general situation, we need some concepts on ordered set partitions.

Definition 7.7
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

(i)

An ordered set partition of
[k]
is a tuple
\pi=(B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{p})
of subsets of
[k]
such that
\{B_{1},\dots,B_{p}\}
is a set partition of
[k]
; that is,
B_{1},\dots,B_{p}
are non-empty and mutually disjoint subsets of
[k]
, and their union is
[k]
. The length
p
of
\pi
is denoted by
|\pi|
. The set of the ordered set partitions of
[k]
is denoted by
\OP(k)
.

(ii)

For a tuple
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
, the ordered kernel set partition
\ker(\mathbf{i})\in\OP(k)
is defined as follows: first, pick the smallest value
p_{1}
among
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}
and then define the subset
B_{1}=\{j\in[k]:i_{j}=p_{1}\}
;
secondly, pick the second smallest value
p_{2}
among
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}
and define the subset
B_{2}=\{j\in[k]:i_{j}=p_{2}\}
; continuing this procedure until the end we arrive at an ordered set partition
(B_{1},B_{2},\dots)
, which is denoted by
\ker(\mathbf{i})
.

Example 7.8
.

\displaystyle\ker(6,3,2,3,6)

\displaystyle=(\{3\},\{2,4\},\{1,5\}),

\displaystyle\ker(2,7,4,7,4,2,4)

\displaystyle=(\{1,6\},\{3,5,7\},\{2,4\}).

For further information on ordered (kernel) set partitions the reader is referred to
[
11
]
.

For an ordered set partition
\pi
of
[k]
there exists a unique
packed
word
, i.e., a tuple
\mathbf{i}(\pi)=(i_{1}(\pi),\dots,i_{k}(\pi))\in[|\pi|]^{k}
such that
\pi=\ker(\mathbf{i}(\pi))
. Using this tuple we define
\omega(\pi)
to be
\omega(a^{(i_{1}(\pi))}\cdots a^{(i_{k}(\pi))})
.

Example 7.9
.

If
\pi=(\{1,3\},\{2\})
then
\mathbf{i}(\pi)=(1,2,1)
and

\omega(\pi)=\omega(a^{(1)}a^{(2)}a^{(1)})=\varphi(a){\rm Tr}(a^{2}).

If
\pi=(\{3\},\{2,4,6\},\{1,5\})
then
\mathbf{i}(\pi)=(3,2,1,2,3,2)
and

\omega(\pi)=\omega(a^{(3)}a^{(2)}a^{(1)}a^{(2)}a^{(3)}a^{(2)})=\varphi(a)\varphi(a)\varphi(a^{3}){\rm Tr}(a).

With those notions, we have

\displaystyle\omega(b_{N}^{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\OP(k)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{i}=(i_{1}\dots,i_{k})\in[N]^{k}\\
\ker(\mathbf{i})=\pi\end{subarray}}\omega(a^{(i_{1})}\cdots a^{(i_{k})})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\OP(k)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{i}=(i_{1}\dots,i_{k})\in[N]^{k}\\
\ker(\mathbf{i})=\pi\end{subarray}}d^{\min\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}-1}\omega(\pi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\OP(k)}\alpha_{|\pi|}(d,N)\omega(\pi),

where

\alpha_{p}(d,N):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(j_{1},\dots,j_{p})\in[N]^{p}\\
j_{1}<\cdots<j_{p}\end{subarray}}d^{j_{1}-1},\quad N\geq p;\qquad\alpha_{p}(d,N):=0,\quad 0\leq N<p.

In order to investigate the asymptotics of
\omega(b_{N}^{k})
it suffices to understand the function
\alpha_{k}(d,N)
.

Lemma 7.10
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a polynomial
P_{k}
in two variables such that

\alpha_{k}(d,N)=\frac{d^{N}}{(d-1)^{k}}+P_{k}((d-1)^{-1},N),\qquad d\geq 2,N\geq 0.

Proof.

The proof goes by induction on
k
. For
k=1
,
\alpha_{1}(d,N)=(d^{N}-1)/(d-1)
, and hence
P_{1}(x,y)=-x
.
For general
k\geq 2
we proceed as

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}(d,N)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(j_{1},\dots,j_{k-1})\in[j-1]^{k-1}\\
j_{1}<\cdots<j_{k-1}\end{subarray}}d^{j_{1}-1}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{k-1}(d,j-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[\frac{d^{j-1}}{(d-1)^{k-1}}+P_{k-1}((d-1)^{-1},j-1)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{N}}{(d-1)^{k}}-\frac{1}{(d-1)^{k}}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}P_{k-1}((d-1)^{-1},j-1),\qquad\qquad N\geq 1.

By Faulhaber’s formula and induction hypothesis, there is a polynomial
Q_{k}(x,y)
such that
Q_{k}(x,0)=0
and

Q_{k}(x,N)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}P_{k-1}(x,j-1),\qquad N\geq 1,

which implies the desired formula for
N\geq 1
by taking
P_{k}(x,y)=-x^{k}+Q_{k}(x,y)
. Since
Q_{k}(x,0)=0
the formula holds for
N=0
as well.
∎

By Lemma
7.10
we obtain the limit

\lim_{N\to\infty}d^{-N}\alpha_{k}(d,N)=\frac{1}{(d-1)^{k}}

and conclude the following.

Theorem 7.11
.

In the setting above we have

(7.5)

\lim_{N\to\infty}d^{-N}\omega(b_{N}^{k})=\sum_{\pi\in\OP(k)}\frac{\omega(\pi)}{(d-1)^{|\pi|}}.

Thus the empirical eigenvalue distributions of
b_{N}
converge (in the sense of
moments) to a probability measure whose
k
-th moment is the above limit. Of
course the empirical eigenvalue distributions of the rescaled sum
N^{-1/2}b_{N}

converge weakly to
\delta_{0}
, which means that the number of eigenvalues of

N^{-1/2}b_{N}
outside a fixed neighborhood of
0
is of the order

o(d^{N})
. Combining this with the monotone CLT, which asserts that vacuum spectral distribution of
N^{-1/2}b_{N}
weakly converges an arcsine distribution, it turns out that the vacuum vector captures a relatively small number of eigenvalues of
N^{-1/2}b_{N}
that lie outside the neighborhood of 0.

The limit moments (
7.5
) depend on a lot of information about trace
and vacuum moments of the original matrix
a
. This is in sharp contrast with the fact that if
\psi(a)=0
and
\psi(a^{2})=1
then the distribution of the rescaled sum
N^{-1/2}b_{N}
with respect to the vacuum state

converges weakly to the same arcsine law.

Lemma 7.13
.

Let
\{\beta_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the sequence defined by (
7.9

\beta_{0}=1
. There exists
C>0
such that
the inequality
\beta_{n}\leq(Cn)^{2n}
holds for all
n\geq 1
.

Remark 7.14
.

The proof below shows that
C=11
suffices. Moreover, according to OEIS A048286, a more precise asymptotics
\beta_{n}\sim c(2/(e\log 2))^{n}n^{n+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\log 2}{4}}
holds, where
c=1.6463...
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction using the recursion formula (
7.9
) and Stirling’s formula

\sqrt{2\pi}n^{n+1/2}e^{-n}\leq n!\leq en^{n+1/2}e^{-n},\qquad n\geq 1.

Let
n\geq 2
. We adopt the notation
0^{0}=1
. Assuming the desired inequality holds until
n-1
for some constant
C>1
, one has

\displaystyle\beta_{n}

\displaystyle=2+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-1}\binom{n+\ell}{n-\ell}\frac{2n}{n+\ell}\beta_{\ell}\leq 2+2\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-1}\binom{n+\ell}{n-\ell}(C\ell)^{2\ell}

\displaystyle\leq 2+\frac{e}{\pi\sqrt{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-1}\frac{(n+\ell)^{n+\ell+1/2}}{\sqrt{\ell}2^{2\ell}(n-\ell)^{n-\ell+1/2}}C^{2\ell}\leq 2+\frac{e}{\pi\sqrt{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-1}\frac{(n+\ell)^{n+\ell+1/2}}{2^{2\ell}(n-\ell)^{n-\ell+1/2}}C^{2\ell}.

We split the sum into the two parts
1\leq\ell\leq n-4
and
n-3\leq\ell\leq n-1
(the arguments below are valid even for
2\leq n\leq 4
by setting the irrelevant terms to be 0). The first part is estimated as

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-4}\frac{(n+\ell)^{n+\ell+1/2}}{2^{2\ell}(n-\ell)^{n-\ell+1/2}}C^{2\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-4}\frac{(2n)^{n+n-4+1/2}}{2^{2\ell}4^{n-\ell}}C^{2\ell}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-4}n^{2n}C^{2\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n-4}{nC^{8}}n^{2n}C^{2n}\leq\frac{1}{C^{2}}n^{2n}C^{2n},

and the second part is estimated as

\sum_{\ell=n-3}^{n-1}\frac{(n+\ell)^{n+\ell+1/2}}{2^{2\ell}(n-\ell)^{n-\ell+1/2}}C^{2\ell}\leq\frac{3}{C^{2}}\frac{(2n)^{2n}C^{2n}}{2^{2n-6}}=\frac{3\cdot 2^{6}}{C^{2}}n^{2n}C^{2n}.

Obviously,
2\leq\frac{1}{C^{2}}\frac{e}{\pi\sqrt{2}}n^{2n}C^{2n}
.
Putting everything together, we obtain

\beta_{n}\leq\frac{194e}{C^{2}\pi\sqrt{2}}(Cn)^{2n}.

By taking
C>1
such that
C^{2}\geq 194\frac{e}{\pi\sqrt{2}}
(
C=11

\beta_{n}\leq(Cn)^{2n}
.
∎
