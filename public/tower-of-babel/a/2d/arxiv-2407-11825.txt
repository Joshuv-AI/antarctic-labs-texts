Optimization under rare events: scaling laws for linear chance-constrained programs
Jose Blanchet 1, Joost Jorritsma 2, Bert Zwart 2,3

Abstract
. We consider a class of chance-constrained programs in which profit needs to be maximized while enforcing that a given adverse event remains rare. Using techniques from large deviations and extreme value theory, we show how the optimal value scales as the prescribed bound on the violation probability becomes small and how convex programs emerge in the limit. We use our results to analyze the performance of existing popular approaches in the rare-event regime. We show that the popular CVaR and sample approximations have optimality properties under light-tailed assumptions on the randomness, while they behave sub-optimal in a heavy-tailed setting. Our results are derived using large deviations theory, extreme value theory, process techniques, and random set theory.



Keywords: chance-constrained optimization, rare events, extreme values, regular variation.

MSC Class: 90C15 (primary), 60F10, 60G70 (secondary).

1.
Introduction

We analyze a class of optimization problems
where the goal is to maximize profit, subject to a reliability constraint that requires the probability of an undesirable event below a small level
\delta
. These formulations, known as chance-constrained (CC) optimization problems, arise in a wide range of applications, including:

–

Financial planning.
Banks and insurance companies need to optimize their resources such that the probability of insolvency is kept small.
For example, in fixed income portfolio optimization, an investment grade portfolio has a default rate of about
\delta=10^{-4}
, see
Frank (2008)
.

–

Network design.
In ultra-reliable communication system design for 5G wireless networks, the failure probability should be no bigger than
\delta=10^{-5}
, as is explained in
Alsenwi et al. (2019)
.

–

Power grid operations.
The European Union has set a goal that the amount of reserves available to mitigate possible frequency disturbance should be sufficient, up to once every 20 years. As these reserves are typically procured on an hourly basis, this amounts to a safety constraint of
\delta
smaller than
10^{-5}
,
see
Mosca (2024)
.

Additional examples originate in the
design of bridges
and
control of aircraft
, cf.
Shi and Holzapfel (2021)
, where the probability of a rare event is sometimes required to be below
\delta=10^{-9}
. For more examples, see
Subramanyam (2020)
,
Tong et al. (2022)
.

Because of their direct interpretation and versatility to be applied to various important areas,
chance-constrained optimization has been a subject of interest in Operations Research for several decades. It has become one of the standard formulations in risk-constrained optimization and is covered as such in several textbooks cf.
Prekopa (1970)
,
Prékopa (2003)
,
Klein Haneveld et al. (2020)
,
Shapiro et al. (2021)
.
Unfortunately, chance-constrained problems are typically not very tractable: the set of feasible solutions may be non-convex, and evaluating the chance constraint can be a computational challenge. In general, CC problems are provably NP-hard, see
Luedtke et al. (2010)
. As a result, researchers often resort to various approaches that provide bounds or relaxations to approximate the solution to CC optimization problems.

Our goal in this paper is to leverage the fact that in many applications, such as those described earlier,
\delta
is very close to zero. We use this feature, together with large-deviations principles and extreme-value theory to gain insights into the asymptotic behavior (as
\delta\downarrow 0
) of a stylized family of CC problems and their relaxations. In order to provide a more detailed description of our results and their implications, let us provide a brief overview of various methods in CC optimization.

One method, proposed in
Nemirovski and Shapiro (2006a)
, constructs a convex restriction of the feasible region by upper bounding the reliability constraint by the so-called conditional value at risk (abbreviated with CVaR; henceforth we refer to this as the CVaR approach, see also
Rockafellar and Uryasev (2002)
). Another option, popular because of its ease of implementation, is the so-called scenario approach, introduced by
Calafiore and Campi (2006)
. The scenario approach consists of replacing the probability constraint by several Monte Carlo-based scenarios,
all of which
need to satisfy the reliability constraint. If the undesirable event needs to be kept rare, the number of required samples is large, which can be prohibitive, though
it is sometimes possible to reduce the number of samples using importance sampling techniques, see
Nemirovski and Shapiro (2006b)
,
Barrera et al. (2016)
,
Blanchet et al. (2024)
.

Recently, several authors (see e.g.
Subramanyam (2020)
,
Nesti et al. (2019)
,
Tong et al. (2022)
) have suggested a different method, namely to approximate the probability constraint using ideas from large-deviations theory. The approach can be described as follows. Suppose that
{\bf P}(G(x))\leq\delta
is a safety constraint,
x
is the optimization decision variable, and
G(x)
is an undesirable event. A (heuristic) large-deviations approximation of
{\bf P}(G(x))
can be obtained by replacing the integral of the density of an underlying probabilistic model (say
f
) over the set
G(x)
by a supremum, i.e.:

(1)

{\bf P}(G(x))=\int_{G(x)}f(u)\mathrm{d}u\approx\sup_{u\in G(x)}f(u)=\exp\Big(-\inf_{u\in G(x)}\big\{\log 1/f(u)\big\}\Big).

The previous approximation can often be made rigorous by introducing a suitable asymptotic regime. Using (
1
), the chance-constrained program can be approximated by a bi-level (or nested) optimization problem (involving
x
at the top level and
u
at the bottom level).

The papers
Subramanyam (2020)
,
Nesti et al. (2019)
, and
Tong et al. (2022)
focus on various computational aspects of this bi-level procedure. The analysis in those papers requires sufficient tractability for the underlying density
f
, and this is carried out in situations where (for example) the randomness is a mixture of Gaussian distributions or where the randomness arises from another specific light-tailed distribution. Further, this analysis focuses on developing approximations as in (
1
) that result in computationally tractable procedures.

Moving on to a more precise description of our contributions, this paper takes a different perspective:
we
derive
rigorous
approximations like (
1
)
allowing both light and heavy-tailed distributions. In the latter case, the analogues of (
1
) can be harder to formulate. In turn, we consider a sequence of CC problems indexed by the safety parameter
\delta
, and complete the following technical tasks:

(a)

we show how large-deviations approximations for the solution of CC problems
emerge
in the limit and analyze the scaling of the chance-constrained program’s optimal value
v_{\delta}
as
\delta\downarrow 0
;

(b)

we compare
v_{\delta}
both to the performance of the CVaR relaxation and the scenario approach;

(c)

we identify conditions for which the latter approaches are asymptotically feasible and optimal or not.

An attractive feature of our analysis is that it is rooted in multivariate extreme-value theory,
allowing us to consider classes of distributions that are either light-tailed or heavy-tailed with a rich dependence structure. We restrict our analysis to a class of linear programs to keep it crisp. Specifically, the insights that we obtained based on our technical development can be summarized as follows:

(1)

If the underlying random variables are light-tailed, then we show (Theorem
1
below) that the basic formulation of the chance-constrained program, given in (

CCP
δ

) below,
converges to a convex optimization problem as
\delta\downarrow 0
, and that an appropriate version of (
1
) emerges. While this leads to a characterization of the growth rate of
v_{\delta}
, it is nontrivial to construct a sequence of asymptotically optimal solutions from the limiting optimization problem: essentially, the limit of the logarithmic estimate (
1
) is rather rough to guarantee feasibility for fixed
\delta>0
. However, the asymptotics are sharp enough to derive solutions with any prescribed (non-negative) asymptotic optimality gap.

(2)

Also in the context of light-tailed distributions, we show that both the CC problem and its CVaR relaxation converge to the same convex program in the regime
\delta\downarrow 0
. Thus, CVaR provides a sequence of convex approximations that are both conservative and asymptotically optimal: their optimal values scale in the same way as the actual profit. For a precise formulation, see Theorem
2
. In short, for light-tailed settings, the CVaR relaxation is asymptotically exact.

(3)

A similar, but more nuanced conclusion, once again in the light-tailed environment, holds for the scenario approach: in this case, we show that the sequence of random linear programs, indexed by the number of scenarios, converges after appropriate normalization to a deterministic optimization problem as the number of scenarios becomes large. To match the behavior of (

CCP
δ

), the scenario approach requires a careful choice in the number of scenarios
k
as a function of
\delta
. The standard prescription provided by
Calafiore and Campi (2006)
, see (
6
) below, allows for such a choice, providing a feasible and asymptotically optimal solution with probability tending to one.

(4)

If the underlying random variables are heavy-tailed (more precisely, if their tails are of regular variation), we show (Theorem
4
) that our basic chance-constrained problem converges to a convex optimization problem after appropriate renormalization. The approximation (
1
) is not adequate here: essentially logarithmic asymptotics are not enough and a more precise asymptotic estimate is needed. We provide such an estimate using multivariate regular variation.

(5)

A normalized sequence of CVaR constraints converges to a similar limit as the chance constraints. However, CVaR yields a value of the objective function which is a constant factor away from the actual optimum as
\delta\downarrow 0
.
This makes CVaR asymptotically suboptimal in the heavy-tailed case.
A detailed statement is Theorem
5
below.

(6)

The scenario approach behaves poorly in the heavy-tailed case: the appropriately normalized sequence of random optimization problems converges to a limit, but this limit is still random and feasibility to the original problem cannot be guaranteed, cf. Theorem
6
below. This is in contrast to methods such as sample-average approximation (also known) as empirical optimization in which, as the Monte Carlo sample size increases, the sample-based approximation converges to the desired solution. Our results suggest that in the heavy-tailed case the scenario approach must be modified to obtain asymptotically both optimal and feasible solutions. We utilize concepts from point-process theory by
Resnick (2007)
and random set theory, see
Molchanov (2017)
, to establish these results.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
We define our model and state our assumptions in Section
2
. In Section
3
, we derive our results for light-tailed distributions.
Our results for heavy-tailed distributions can be found in Section
4
. In Section
5
we consider an example showing how it is possible to model dependence between random variables in our framework for light-tailed distributions, and how using approximations like (
1
) impacts feasibility. This example allows for explicit calculations. Afterwards, we verify our theoretical results with a numerical example on portfolio optimization in Section
6
.
We give a short reflection in Section
7
, and present our proofs in Section
8
, which also develops a general-purpose result that circumvents the use of epi-convergence.

2.
Problem description

Notational conventions.

Given vectors
x,y
of identical dimension, the inequalities
x\geq y
and
x>y
are defined component wise, i.e. if
x>y
if
x_{i}>y_{i}
for all elements
i
. For an
m
-dimensional vector
x
we denote
|x|=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|x_{i}|
throughout the paper.

Basic formulation.
Let
X
be a compact convex subset of
[0,\infty)^{m}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and assume
X
contains the set
[0,h]^{m}
for some
h>0
. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\phi:X\times[0,\infty)^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function. Consider the problem

(
\rm{CCP}_{\delta}
)

\displaystyle\mbox{maximize }c^{T}x\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{ subject to }x\in X,\mbox{ and }{\bf P}(\phi(x,L)>1)\leq\delta,

where
L\geq 0

is an
n
-dimensional random vector. The elements
of
L
are often referred to as risk factors and may be dependent.
We assume
\phi
can be written in terms of
m\times n
matrices
A^{(i)},i=1,...,d
with non-negative entries: we take

(2)

\phi(x,L):=\max_{i=1,...,d}x^{T}A^{(i)}L.

This formulation is general enough to model linear programs with random non-negative coefficients in the constraints.
To see this, let
C
be a random matrix with column vectors
C_{j}
, and
b
be a random vector. We can write the matrix inequality
x^{T}C\leq b
as

\sup_{i}x^{T}C_{i}/b_{i}\leq 1
.
Next, define
L
as the row vector consisting of all the
C_{i}/b_{i}
and define the matrix
A^{(i)}
consisting of only
0
’s and
1
’s (in particular, the entries are nonnegative) in such a way that
A^{(i)}L=C_{i}/b_{i}
. We see also that the elements of
L
are non-negative, if the elements of
C_{i}/b_{i}
are nonnegative.

Our choice of
\phi
implies that
0
is always a feasible solution, regardless of the value of
\delta
.
In the context of linear random stochastic inequalities of the form
x^{T}AL\leq b
, if there exists an
x_{0}
such that
{\bf P}(x_{0}^{T}AL\leq b)=1
, one can always make a change of variables by setting
z=x_{0}+x
and considering
z^{T}AL\leq b-x_{0}^{T}AL
. In this case,
z=0
is always feasible and our results would examine the speed of convergence of the objective function towards
c^{T}z_{0}
as
\delta\downarrow 0
.
In this note, we focus on the specific form (
2
) of
\phi
to allow for short proofs. For a more general setting, we refer to the more recent paper by
Deo and Murthy (2024)
.

As mentioned earlier, the parameter
\delta>0
denotes the risk tolerance.
We focus on the case where the reliability constraint is a
rare event
, i.e.
\delta
is close to
0
.
We aim to understand how the optimal profit
scales as
\delta\downarrow 0
.

Definition 1

(Asymptotic optimality)
.

Let
v_{\delta}
be the optimal value of
c^{T}x
in (

CCP
δ

). Let
(x_{\delta})
be a sequence of
m
-dimensional vectors such that
x_{\delta}\in X
that is also asymptotically feasible, i.e.,

(3)

\limsup_{\delta\downarrow 0}{\bf P}\big(\phi(x_{\delta},L)>1\big)/\delta\leq 1.

We call
(x_{\delta})_{\delta>0}
asymptotically optimal if it is asymptotically feasible and

(4)

c^{T}x_{\delta}\,\big/\,v_{\delta}\rightarrow 1,\qquad\text{as }\delta\downarrow 0.

We now introduce two formulations that are
popular alternatives to (

CCP
δ

); we are interested in whether their solutions are feasible and asymptotically optimal. The determination of asymptotic optimality is facilitated by
Theorems
1
and
4
below, as they establish the asymptotic behavior of the optimal value
v_{\delta}
of (

CCP
δ

) for light-tail and heavy-tail distributions, respectively.

CVaR formulation.
The problem (

CCP
δ

) is generally not convex.
For this reason,

Nemirovski and Shapiro (2006a)

introduce a convex relaxation which typically (in particular in the case of non-atomic distributions) coincides with the
conditional value-at-risk

(5)

{\bf CVaR}(\phi(x,L))=\inf_{\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}}\Big\{\tau+\frac{1}{\delta}{\bf E}\big[(\phi(x,L)-\tau)^{+}\big]\Big\},

where
x^{+}:=\max(0,x)
, see also
Rockafellar and Uryasev (2002)
.

In particular,
Nemirovski and Shapiro (2006a)
propose to solve the optimization problem

(
\rm{CVaR}_{\delta}
)

\displaystyle\mbox{maximize }c^{T}x\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{ subject to }x\in X,\mbox{ and }{\bf CVaR}(\phi(x,L))\leq 1.

Let
v_{{\bf CVaR},\delta}
be the optimal solution of the CVaR version of (

CCP
δ

).
The CVaR constraint in (

CVaR
δ

) is more conservative than the chance constraint in (

CCP
δ

), so

v_{{\bf CVaR},\delta}\leq v_{\delta}
.
However, as this optimization problem is convex, it may be more tractable than (

CCP
δ

).
Theorems
2
and
5
below show how

v_{{\bf CVaR},\delta}

compares to
v_{\delta}
as
\delta\downarrow 0
.

Scenario formulation.

Another approach towards solving chance-constrained programs is to resort to Monte-Carlo methods, where the chance constraint is
replaced by constraints of the form
\phi(x,L^{({i})})\leq 1
, with
L^{(1)},...,L^{(k)}
a number of samples called scenarios.
This results in the optimization problem

(
\mathrm{SCP}_{k}
)

\displaystyle\mbox{maximize }c^{T}x\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{subject to }x\in X\mbox{ and }\phi(x,L^{(i)})\leq 1,\quad\forall i\in[k],

where
[k]:=\{1,\ldots,k\}
.
To ensure feasibility of (

CCP
δ

) with high enough probability,
k
needs to be chosen appropriately as
\delta\downarrow 0
. In
Calafiore and Campi (2006)
it is shown that the choice

(6)

k(\delta,\varepsilon)=\frac{2}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+2m+\frac{2m}{\delta}\log\frac{2}{\delta}

gives a solution that is feasible with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
. This bound is valid for any function
\phi(x,L)
that is convex in
x
.
We show in Theorem
3
below that the choice
k=k(\delta,\varepsilon)
matches the behavior of the optimal value in the light-tailed case if
\varepsilon
does not tend to 0 with
\delta
too quickly.

Plan.
We analyze the above three families of optimization problems in the next two sections by making appropriate assumptions on
L
. We will apply concepts and techniques from multivariate extreme-value theory and large-deviations theory. We begin with the case in which the elements of
L
are light-tailed.

3.
Light tails

In this section, we analyze (

CCP
δ

), (

CVaR
δ

), and (

SCP
k

) for a large class of distributions guaranteeing that all moments of
L
are finite. In particular, we assume that
Q(x):=-\log{\bf P}(L>x)

is
multivariate regularly varying
: there exists a regularly varying function
q(r)=h(r)r^{\beta}
for some index
\beta>0
and slowly-varying function
h
(
h(ar)/h(r)\rightarrow 1
as
r\rightarrow\infty
for any
a>0
), and a non-negative continuous function
\lambda:[0,\infty)^{m}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
such that
\inf_{x\geq 0:|x|=1}\lambda(x)>0
, and

(7)

Q(rx)/q(r)\rightarrow\lambda(x),\quad\text{as }r\rightarrow\infty.

The convergence as stated above is pointwise. However, it also holds uniformly on compact sets because of regular variation, see
Resnick (2007)
.
If we divide by
|x|
and use that
h(r|x|)/h(r)\rightarrow 1
as
r\rightarrow\infty
for
|x|>0
, we obtain

(8)

\lambda(x)=\lambda(x/|x|)\times|x|^{\beta}.

For technical simplicity, we assume that
q(r)
is continuous and increasing so that its inverse is well-defined. We refer to
Deo and Murthy (2024)
for a more general setting.

This family of distributions includes multi-variate normal distributions (
\beta=2
), exponential and gamma distributions (
\beta=1
) and Weibull distributions with a sub-exponential tail (
\beta<1
). The dependence structure can be rather arbitrary: the main form of dependence which is relevant is to which extent large values in the sample are correlated, which is encoded in
\lambda
. We give an example in Section
5
.

We apply large-deviations theory to analyze the optimal value
v_{\delta}
of (

CCP
δ

) for small
\delta
. Set

(9)

I(b):=\inf_{x\in[0,\infty)^{m}}\big\{\lambda(x):b^{T}x\geq 1\big\}=|b|^{-\beta}I(b/|b|),\qquad b\in[0,\infty)^{m},

Here,
b^{T}
will represent
y^{T}A^{(i)}
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.
We state a proposition on the role of
I
that we prove in Section
8
. We make the change of variable
x=y/r
, and write
f\sim g
if
f(r)/g(r)\to 1
as
r\rightarrow\infty
, or if
f(\delta)/g(\delta)\to 1
as
\delta\downarrow 0
.

Proposition 1
.

The set
\{b:I(b)\geq 1\}
is convex, compact, and possesses a non-empty interior. The following asymptotics hold as

(10)

\displaystyle-\log{\bf P}\big(b^{T}L>r\big)

\displaystyle\sim I(b)q(r),

(11)

\displaystyle-\log{\bf P}\big(\max_{i}y^{T}A^{(i)}L>r\big)

\displaystyle\sim\min_{i}I\big(y^{T}A^{(i)}\big)q(r)=:J(y)q(r).

Now,
consider the following problem, closely related to (

CCP
δ

),

(
\rm{LT}
)

\mbox{maximize }c^{T}y\hskip 28.45274pt{\mbox{ subject to }}y\geq 0\mbox{ and }J(y)\geq 1.

Formulation (

LT

) is motivated by making the change of variable
x=y/r
in (

CCP
δ

) with
r=q^{-1}(\log 1/\delta)
, replacing the probability in (

CCP
δ

) by the approximation (

). This ensures that the chance constraint, after taking logarithms, has the desired behavior.
The constraint

is no longer relevant in (

LT

) as

, since we assumed

for some

, and

.

The problem (

, strong duality holds, and since
\{J(y)\geq 1\}
has non-empty interior, (

In Section

, we give examples for
\lambda
for which

itself has an explicit expression.

Our first result is that (

LT

) determines the correct growth rate of

:

Theorem 1
.

Let
y^{*}
solve (

).
Then

as

.

To prove this result, a verification of the pointwise convergence property (
11
) is not sufficient, as it does not imply convergence of the optima to the optimum of the pointwise limiting function. To circumvent this, we prove a general statement (Proposition
2
below) that we also invoke in the proofs of the subsequent theorems.

Based on Theorem
1
, it is tempting to conjecture that
y^{*}/q^{-1}(\log 1/\delta)
is asymptotically optimal, but this may not be the case, as the asymptotic estimate (

) is not sharp enough to guarantee the asymptotic feasibility condition (

), as is exemplified in Section

(this follows from the proof of the next theorem in Section

), and accept an optimality gap

.

In contrast, (

δ

) produces an asymptotically optimal sequence of solutions. It is more conservative than (

), and thus feasible. Moreover, it is optimal:

Theorem 2
.

v_{{\bf CVaR},\delta}\sim v_{\delta}

as

.

We informally describe why Theorem

\{\phi\big(y^{*}/q^{-1}(\log 1/\delta),L\big)>\tau\}
, the overshoot vanishes, i.e.,
\phi\big(y^{*}/q^{-1}(\log 1/\delta),L\big)-\tau\to 0

Secondly, we show in Section

that we can approximate the optimum in (

) from below by setting

in (

) behaves asymptotically the same as the probability in (

CCP

). This makes the CVaR formulation appealing when optimizing under rare event constraints in a light-tail setting.

To assess the scenario formulation (

SCP

) in the region

,

Motivated by the insights from Theorem

(
{\rm{SCP}\textnormal{-}\rm{LT}}_{k}
)

\displaystyle\mbox{maximize }c^{T}y\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{subject to }\phi(y,L^{(i)})\leq q^{-1}(\log k),\quad\forall i\in[k].

.

Let

solve (

⁢
SCP

Then, as

In particular, when

), and

Summarizing our findings in the light-tail case, we see that the behavior of the optimal value

can be characterized using the rare-event approximation (

LT

). Because this approximation is based on logarithmic asymptotics (

), the rescaled optimum of

may not be feasible, even for small

Both the sampling and the CVAR approaches provide solutions which scale like the optimal value

, making both approaches viable in the light-tailed case.
The standard version of the sampling approach requires a large number of scenarios

. In recent work, it has been shown how to adapt the sampling approach and reduce

by

.

The CVaR approach, while not as tractable as (

), is asymptotically optimal in a light-tail regime, reinforcing the fact that CVaR is one of the main approaches for solving chance constraint programs.
In the next section, we will examine to what extent these findings hold if the underlying random variables are heavy-tailed.

4.
Heavy tails

Throughout this section, we suppose that
L
has a distribution that is of multivariate regular variation, which covers the case in which the elements
of
L
have a power law.
To describe this assumption in a convenient form, we use a representation which (apart from not taking logarithms) is different from the one in Section 3, building on an equivalent characterization of regular variation developed in
Basrak et al. (2002)
). Recall that
|\cdot|
denotes the
\ell_{1}
norm.
Our characterization guarantees that for some
\alpha>1
, and some slowly-varying function
h
,

\bar{F}(r)={\bf P}(|L|>r)=h(r)r^{-\alpha}.

We assume that
\bar{F}(t)
is continuous and strictly decreasing (it has no atoms) to reduce technicalities. As a result, the inverse
\bar{F}^{-1}
is well-defined.
Moreover, our assumption on multivariate regular variation implies that for some random vector

This formulation allows dependence between extreme values of risk factors. For background on multivariate regular variation we refer to

Letting

We proceed to the scenario-based approach. In the previous section on the light-tailed case, we observed that the approximation (

This scalar case provides the main insights into the sample approximation method in the heavy-tailed case. For completeness, we

Example for light-tailed distributions

While the heavy-tail case directly leads to the convex program (

HT

), the light-tail case leads to a bi-level optimization problem involving the function
I
in (
9
), making it less straightforward to parse.
The example here gives insights into how the tail behavior of
L
and its dependence structure may impact the optimal solution of (

CCP
δ

) as
\delta\downarrow 0
.

Throughout this section, we assume
d=1,m=n
and consider the problem

(16)

\displaystyle\mbox{maximize }c^{T}x\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{ subject to }x\geq 0,\mbox{ and }{\bf P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}a_{i}L_{i}\geq 1\Big)\leq\delta,

for some constants
a_{i}>0,i=1,...,n
. Suppose that
L
satisfies
(
7
), with
\lambda

given by

(17)

\lambda(x):=\ell(x^{\beta}):=\ell(x_{1}^{\beta},...,x_{n}^{\beta}):=\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\beta\theta}\bigg)^{1/\theta},\hskip 28.45274pt\theta\geq 1.

Here,
\ell(x)
corresponds to the so-called
Gumbel-Hougaard copula (see e.g.
Segers (2012)
).
For
\theta=1
, large coordinates in
L
occur independently; this is no longer true if
\theta>1
. For
\theta=\infty
, we take
\ell(x)=\max_{i}x_{i}
, so that large values are perfectly correlated.

If
\gamma=\beta\theta\leq 1
, then
I(b)=\min\{(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\gamma})^{1/\theta}:b^{T}x\geq 1\}
has as optimal solution
x_{i^{*}}=1/b_{i^{*}}
with
i^{*}=\arg\max b_{i}
(with arbitrary tie-breaking rule) and
x_{i}=0
for

. Consequently,

(18)

I(b)=|b|_{\infty}^{-\beta}.

Thus, if
\gamma\leq 1
, the limiting approximation (

LT

) of (

CCP
δ

) simplifies to

(19)

\displaystyle\mbox{maximize }c^{T}y\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{ subject to }y\geq 0,\mbox{ and }\max_{i}a_{i}y_{i}\leq 1,

which has solution
y_{i}^{\ast}=1/a_{i}
.

If
\gamma=\beta\theta>1
, then we find using the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions that the optimal
x_{i}
in the definition of

equals
b_{i}^{1/(\gamma-1)}/\sum_{j}b_{j}^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}
, and after some simplifications we obtain

(20)

I(b)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}\right)^{-(\gamma-1)/\theta}.

Thus, if
\gamma>1
,
the limiting approximation (

LT

) of (

CCP
δ

) simplifies to

(21)

\displaystyle\mbox{maximize }c^{T}y\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{ subject to }y\geq 0,\mbox{ and }\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}y_{i})^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}\leq 1.

Using the KKT conditions we obtain the solution

(22)

y_{i}^{\ast}=(c_{i}/a_{i})^{\gamma-1}/\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c_{j}/a_{j})^{\gamma}\Big).

If the coordinates of

are independent in the tail (
\theta=1)
, then the nature of

changes at the point
\beta=1
which is precisely the point
where the moment-generating function of

becomes finite. For
\beta<1
, the tails of

are heavy-tailed Weibull, for which it is known that
large values of sums are caused by a single big jump, leading to the simple form of the optimal solution.

Interestingly, the impact of the dependence structure, summarized by
\theta
, is nontrivial, in the sense that the single-big jump intuition is not relevant in our decision-making context if the tail of

is not very heavy, and the dependence is sufficiently strong, i.e. if
\beta>1/\theta
.

We mentioned before that asymptotic optimality in the light-tailed case is not guaranteed as the solution of (

LT

) after normalization may not be feasible for the problem (

CCP
δ

). To illustrate this in more detail in the context of our example, consider the case where
\theta=1

and
\beta<1
. Take also

. In this case, the chance
constraint in (
16

{\bf P}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}>q^{-1}(\log 1/\delta)\big)
.
The distribution of
L
is sub-exponential and we have the estimate, as
\delta\downarrow 0
,

(23)

{\bf P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}>q^{-1}(\log 1/\delta)\Big)\sim n{\bf P}\big(L_{1}>q^{-1}(\log 1/\delta)\big)=n\delta,

cf. Chapter 3 in
Nair et al. (2022)
. Thus, our choice leads to a violation probability of a factor

larger than it should be to satisfy (

For
\beta=1
we obtain a sum of i.i.d. exponential random variables, for which

(24)

\displaystyle{\bf P}\Big(\sum_{i\in[n]}L_{i}>q^{-1}(\log 1/\delta)\Big)

\displaystyle\sim{\bf P}\big(\mathrm{Gamma}(n,1)>(\log 1/\delta)\big)

(25)

\displaystyle=\int_{u=(\log 1/\delta)}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\Gamma(n)}x^{n-1}{\mathrm{e}}^{-x}\mathrm{d}u

(26)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\mathrm{e}^{-(\log 1/\delta)}(\log 1/\delta)^{n}=\delta\frac{(\log 1/\delta)^{n}}{n!}.

This behavior is even worse compared to

To complement our theoretical findings, we now perform numerical experiments for light- and heavy-tailed settings, demonstrating how the asymptotic predictions manifest in a practical example.

Formulation.
We consider an investment portfolio optimization problem in which an investor is interested in shorting stocks that are considered overvalued. There are several websites that can be accessed to list the top 10 overvalued stocks in various industries (e.g. see tradingview, seekingalpha, etc). We do not promote any particular set of stocks to short-sell, and mention readily available sources to stress the applicability of this problem formulation.

\displaystyle\mbox{maximize }c^{T}x\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{ subject to }x\geq 0,\mbox{ and }{\bf P}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}L_{i}\geq 1\bigg)\leq\delta,

In order to test the quality of our approximations, we will use a class of models for

7.
Concluding comments

Supported by the numerical experiments, the theoretical results in this paper provide a consistent picture of how chance-constrained programs behave in the rare-event regime. We summarize our findings before proceeding to the proofs.

This paper provides the first systematic large-deviations analysis of chance-constrained optimization problems. To facilitate the development of our algorithmic and structural insights, we focus on a linear objective and a safety constraint controlling (i.e. upper bounding by a fixed constant) the
1-\delta
quantile of the maxima of bi-linear functions (bi-linear both in the multivariate risk factor and the decision parameter).
We distinguish two regimes according to the tails of the risk factor, namely, light and heavy tailed regimes.

Under the light-tail assumption,
the main takeaway from Section
3
is that the CVaR and scenario formulations —with carefully chosen number of scenarios— are good approaches to chance-constrained optimization in a rare-event regime. Their solutions are asymptotically feasible (with probability tending to one), and are asymptotically optimal. The large-deviations approximation (suitably modified as explained in Section
3
) can be a good approach to chance-constrained optimization and leads to an asymptotically accurate estimate of the optimal value, though feasibility of its solution cannot be guaranteed.

In the heavy-tailed regime, we explicitly show that the CVaR relaxation is no longer asymptotically optimal: its solution remains a factor
1-1/\alpha
away from the optimum. The scenario approach behaves even worse, as explained in Section
4
. In contrast, the large-deviations approximation is asymptotically optimal in this case, as it is based on a sharper version of the large-deviations approximation (
1
).

There are several natural research questions that may be considered: (i) we expect that large-deviations approximations, for both light-tails and heavy-tails may still yield convex programming problems for functions
\phi
which are not be bilinear; (ii) computationally, it will be of interest to consider scaling regimes where also the dimension of the decision variable
x
and/or the dimension of
L
may grow —it is kept fixed in this study; (iii) the large-deviations insights we developed could be used to develop new algorithms for chance-constrained optimization which
are asymptotically optimal in the rare event regime, and also produce close to optimal solutions for fixed values of
\delta
, just like the notion of asymptotic efficiency has led to novel algorithms in the area of rare-event simulation.

For

Proposition 2
.

Let

be functions, such that

is non-decreasing for each
\ell
, and
\eta\mapsto g(\eta y)
is strictly increasing for each
y
as a function of the scalar
\eta\in[0,\infty)
. Let

, and define

and
v_{\ast}:=\sup\{c^{T}y:g(y)\leq 1,y\geq 0\}
. Then,
v_{\ell}\to v_{\ast}
.

Proof.

Assume first that
v_{\ast}=\infty
. For each fixed
a>0
, there exists
y^{(a)}\geq 0
such that
c^{T}y^{(a)}\geq a
and
g(y^{(a)})\leq 1
.
Fix
\eta\in(0,1)
. By the pointwise convergence
g_{\ell}(\eta y^{(a)})\to g(\eta y^{(a)})<1
, where the strict inequality follows from the fact that

is strictly increasing in
\eta
. In other words,
\eta y^{(a)}
is feasible for

for all sufficiently large
\ell
. Moreover, for such

. Since
a
is arbitrary, we obtain that
\liminf_{\ell\to\infty}v_{\ell}=\infty=v_{\ast}
.
Assume now that
v_{\ast}<\infty
. Analogously to above,
v_{\ell}\geq\eta v_{\ast}
for each
\eta\in(0,1)
and all
\ell
sufficiently large depending on
\eta
. As a result,
\liminf_{\ell\to\infty}v_{\ell}\geq v_{\ast}
.
We turn to the upper bound.

Let us partition
c=(\bar{c},0)
, where the part
\bar{c}
has strictly positive elements. We then note that
v_{*}=\sup\{\bar{c}^{T}\bar{y}:g(\bar{y},0)\leq 1,\bar{y}\geq 0\}
. Because
g_{\ell}
is non-decreasing
we have that

v_{\ell}=\sup\{\bar{c}^{T}\bar{y}:g_{\ell}(\bar{y},0)\leq 1,\bar{y}\geq 0\}.

So, we may assume without loss of generality that
c
has strictly positive elements.

Assume for contradiction that there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
(v_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 1}
contains a subsequence such that
v_{\ell_{i}}\geq v_{\ast}+3\varepsilon
for all

. Since
v_{\ell_{i}}=\sup\{c^{T}y:g_{\ell_{i}}(y)\leq 1,y\geq 0\}
, there must exist

such that

and
c^{T}y_{\ell_{i}}\geq v_{\ast}+2\varepsilon
. For each
i
, there exists
\eta_{i}\leq 1
and
y^{\prime}_{\ell_{i}}\in[0,\infty)^{m}
such that
y_{\ell_{i}}^{\prime}=\eta_{i}y_{\ell_{i}},g(y^{\prime}_{\ell_{i}})=g(\eta_{i}y_{\ell_{i}})\leq g(y_{\ell_{i}})\leq 1
and
c^{T}y^{\prime}_{\ell_{i}}=v_{\ast}+2\varepsilon
.

Let
A:=\{y\in[0,\infty)^{n}:c^{T}y=v_{\ast}+2\varepsilon\}
. As all elements of
c
are strictly positive,
A
is a compact set.
Define
f(y):=(\lfloor y_{1}\cdot|c|/\varepsilon\rfloor,\ldots,\lfloor y_{n}\cdot|c|/\varepsilon\rfloor)\cdot\varepsilon/|c|
, i.e., each coordinate is rounded down to the nearest multiple of
\varepsilon/|c|
. Define
\hat{A}:=\{f(y):y\in A\}\ni f(y^{\prime}_{\ell_{i}})=:\hat{y}_{\ell_{i}}
. Since
A
is compact,
\hat{A}
is a finite set, and thus we can extract a further subsequence such that
\hat{y}_{\ell_{i_{j}}}
is identical to some
\hat{y}\in\hat{A}
along the subsequence. By the rounding procedure,
c^{T}\hat{y}\geq v_{\ast}+\varepsilon
, and since
g_{\ell_{i_{j}}}
is non-decreasing,
g_{\ell_{i_{j}}}(\hat{y})\leq 1
.
By the pointwise convergence,
g(\hat{y})=\lim_{j\to\infty}g_{\ell_{i_{j}}}(\hat{y})\leq 1
, and thus
\hat{y}
is a feasible solution for

. This contradicts that

is the supremum of
\{c^{T}y:g(y)\leq 1,y\geq 0\}
. As a result,
\limsup_{\ell\to\infty}v_{\ell}\leq v_{\ast}+3\varepsilon
for any
\varepsilon>0
. Thus,
\limsup_{\ell\to\infty}v_{\ell}\leq v_{\ast}
.

\square
