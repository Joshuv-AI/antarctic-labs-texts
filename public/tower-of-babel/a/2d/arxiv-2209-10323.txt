Avoid One’s Doom: Finding Cliff-Edge Configurations in Petri Nets
Giann Karlo Aguirre-Samboní 1 \;\;{}^{1} Stefan Haar 1 \;\;{}^{1} Loïc Paulevé 2 \;\;{}^{2} Stefan Schwoon 1 \;\;{}^{1} Nick Würdemann 3 \;\;{}^{3}

Abstract
A crucial question in analyzing a concurrent system is to determine its long-run behaviour, and in particular, whether there are irreversible choices in its evolution, leading into parts of the reachability space from which there is no return to other parts. Casting this problem in the unifying framework of safe Petri nets, our previous work [ 4 ] has provided techniques for identifying attractors , i.e. terminal strongly connected components of the reachability space, whose attraction basins we wish to determine. Here, we provide a solution for the case of safe Petri nets. Our algorithm uses net unfoldings and provides a map of all of the system’s configurations (concurrent executions) that act as cliff-edges , i.e. any maximal extension for those configurations lies in some basin that is considered fatal. The computation turns out to require only a relatively small prefix of the unfolding, just twice the depth of Esparza’s complete prefix.

Introduction

Unfoldings
of Petri nets
[
7
]
, which are essentially event structures in the sense of Winskel et al.
[
19
]
with additional information about
states
, are an acyclic representation of the possible sequences of transitions, akin to Mazurkiewicz traces but enriched with branching information.

Many reachability-related verification problems for concurrent systems have been successfully addressed by Petri-net unfolding methods over the past decades, see
[
16
,
8
,
7
]
.
However, questions of long-term behaviour and stabilization have received relatively little attention. With the growing interest in formal methods for biology, the key feature of
multistability
of systems
[
31
,
25
,
21
,
24
]
comes into focus. It has been studied in other
qualitative models such as Boolean and multivalued networks
[
30
,
29
,
27
]
.
Multistability characterizes many fundamental biological processes, such as cellular differentiation, cellular reprogramming, and cell-fate decision; in fact, stabilization of a cell regulatory network corresponds to reaching one of the - possibly many - phenotypes of the cell, thus explaining the important role of multistability in cell biology. However, multistability emerges also in many other branches of the life sciences; our own motivation is the qualitative analysis of the fate of
ecosystems
, see
[
26
]
.

Multistability can be succinctly described as the presence of several
attractors
in the system under study.
Attractors characterize the stable behaviours, given as the smallest subsets of states from which the system cannot escape; in other words, they are terminal strongly connected components of the associated transition system. In the long run, the system will enter one of its attractors and remain inside; multi-stability arises when there is more than one such attractor.
The
basin
of attractor
\mathbf{A}
consists of the states from which the system inevitably reaches
\mathbf{A}
.

The basin includes the attractor itself, and possibly one or several transient states
[
15
]
.

We aim at finding the tipping points in which the system switches from an undetermined or
free
state into some basin; while interesting beyond that domain, this is a recurrent question in the analysis of
signalling and gene regulatory networks
[
6
,
17
]
.
In
[
9
]
, the authors provide a method for identifying, in a boolean network model, the states in which one transition
leads to losing the reachability of a given attractor (called
bifurcation transitions
there; we prefer to speak of
tipping points
instead).
However, enumerating the states in which the identified transitions make the system branch away from the attractor can be highly combinatorial and hinders a fine understanding of the branching.
Thus, the challenge resides in identifying the specific contexts and sequences of transitions leading to a strong basin.

Using a bounded unfolding prefix, all reachable attractors
[
4
]
can be extracted. Also, we have exhibited (
[
12
]
) the particular shape of basins that are visible in a concurrent model.

In the present paper, we build on this previous analyses;
the point of view taken here is that all attractors correspond to the
end
of the system’s free behaviour, in other words to its
doom
. We will give characterizations of basin boundaries (called
cliff-edges
below), and of those behaviours that remain
free
, in terms of properties of the unfolding, reporting also on practical experiments with an implementation of the algorithms derived. We finally introduce a novel type of quantitative measure, called
protectedness
,
to indicate how far away (or close) a system is from doom, in a state that is still free per se. General discussions and outlook will conclude this paper.

We begin now by recalling the basic definitions needed below.
A
Petri net

In this paper, we consider only

Safe (or 1-bounded) Petri nets

[

and the appendix of
[
4
]
for discussion). We are thus entitled to move between these models without loss of expressiveness; however, Petri nets provide more convenient ways to develop and present the theory and the algorithms here.

Unfoldings.

Roughly speaking, the unfolding of
a Petri net
\mathcal{N}
is an acyclic Petri net (with particular structural properties),
\mathcal{U}
, that reproduces exactly the
same behaviours as
\mathcal{N}
.

We now give some technical definitions to introduce unfoldings formally. A more extensive treatment can be found, e.g., in
[
8
,
7
]
.

Definition 2
(Causality, conflict, concurrency)

Let
{\mathit{N}}=\langle{P,T,F}\rangle
be a net and

two nodes
of
{\mathit{N}}
.
We say that

x
is a
causal predecessor
of
y
, noted
x<y
, if there exists a
non-empty path of arcs from
x
to
y
. We note
x\leq y
if
x<y
or
x=y
.
If
x\leq y
or
y\leq x
, then
x
and
y
are said to be

causally related
.
Transitions
u
and
v
are in
direct conflict
, noted
u\mathrel{\#}_{\delta}v
, iff
{{}^{\bullet}}{u}\cap{{}^{\bullet}}{v}\neq\emptyset
; nodes

x
and
y
are
in conflict
, noted
x\mathrel{\#}y
, if there exist

u,v\in T
such that
u\neq v
,
u\leq x
,
v\leq y
, and
u\mathrel{\#}_{\delta}v
. We call

x
and
y

concurrent
, noted
x~\mathbf{co}~y
, if they are neither
causally related nor in conflict.
A set of concurrent places is called a
co-set
.

Definition 3
(Occurrence net)

Let
\mathcal{O}=\langle{\mathit{B},\mathit{E},\mathit{G},\mathbf{c}_{0}}\rangle
be a Petri net. We say that
\mathcal{O}

is an
occurrence net
if it satisfies the following properties:

1.

The causality relation
<
is acyclic;

2.

|{{}^{\bullet}}{\mathit{b}}|\leq 1
for all places
\mathit{b}\in\mathit{B}
,
and
\mathit{b}\in\mathbf{c}_{0}
iff
|{{}^{\bullet}}{\mathit{b}}|=0
;

3.

For every transition
\mathit{e}\in\mathit{E}
,
\mathit{e}\mathrel{\#}\mathit{e}
does not hold, and
\{x\mid x\leq\mathit{e}\}
is finite.

Following the convention in the unfolding literature, we refer to the
places of an occurrence net as
conditions
and to its transitions
as
events
. Due to the structural constraints, the firing
sequences of occurrence nets have special properties: if some condition
\mathit{b}
is
marked during a run, then the token on
\mathit{b}
was either present initially
or produced by one particular event (the single event in
{{}^{\bullet}}{\mathit{b}}
);
moreover, once the token on
\mathit{b}
is consumed, it can never be replaced by
another token, due to acyclicity of
<
.

Definition 4
(Configurations, cuts)

Let
\mathcal{O}=\langle{\mathit{B},\mathit{E},\mathit{G},\mathbf{c}_{0}}\rangle
be an occurrence net.
A set
\mathit{C}\subseteq\mathit{E}

Definition of Unfoldings.
Let
\mathcal{N}=\langle{\mathit{P},{\mathit{T}},\mathit{F},\mathit{M}_{0}}\rangle

be a safe Petri net. The unfolding
\mathcal{U}=\langle{\mathit{B},\mathit{E},\mathit{G},\mathbf{c}_{0}}\rangle
of

\mathcal{N}
is an
occurrence net (equipped with a mapping
\pi
)
such that
the firing sequences and reachable markings of
\mathcal{U}
are exactly
the firing sequences and reachable markings of
\mathcal{N}
(modulo
\pi
), see below.

\mathcal{U}
may be infinite; it can be inductively constructed as follows:

1.

The condition set
\mathit{B}
is a subset of
(\mathit{E}\cup\{\bot\})\times\mathit{P}
.
For a condition
\mathit{b}=\langle{\mathit{e},\mathit{p}}\rangle
, we will have
\mathit{e}=\bot
iff
\mathit{b}\in\mathbf{c}_{0}
;
otherwise
\mathit{e}
is the singleton event in
{{}^{\bullet}}{\mathit{b}}
. Moreover,
\pi(\mathit{b})=\mathit{p}
.
The initial cut
\mathbf{c}_{0}
contains exactly one condition
\langle{\bot,\mathit{p}}\rangle

for each initially marked place
\mathit{p}\in\mathit{M}_{0}
of
\mathcal{N}
.

2.

The events of
\mathit{E}
are a subset of
2^{\mathit{B}}\times{\mathit{T}}
.
More precisely, for every co-set
\mathit{B}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathit{B}
such that

\pi(\mathit{B}^{\prime})={{}^{\bullet}}{\mathit{t}}
,
we have an event
\mathit{e}=\langle{\mathit{B}^{\prime},t}\rangle
.
In this case, we add edges
\langle{\mathit{b},\mathit{e}}\rangle

for each
\mathit{b}\in\mathit{B}^{\prime}
(i.e.
{{}^{\bullet}}{\mathit{e}}=\mathit{B}^{\prime}
), we set
\pi(\mathit{e})=\mathit{t}
, and for each

\mathit{p}\in{{\mathit{t}}^{\bullet}}
, we add to
\mathit{B}
a condition
\mathit{b}=\langle{\mathit{e},\mathit{p}}\rangle
connected by
an edge
\langle{\mathit{e},\mathit{b}}\rangle
.

Intuitively, a condition
\langle{\mathit{e},\mathit{p}}\rangle
represents the possibility of putting
a token onto place
\mathit{p}
through a particular set of events, while an event

\langle{\mathit{B}^{\prime},\mathit{e}}\rangle
represents a possibility of firing transition
\mathit{e}
in a particular
context.

Configurations and Markings.

The following fact from the literature will be used below:

Lemma 1
(see e.g.
[
8
]
)

Fix a safe Petri net
\mathcal{N}=\langle{\mathit{P},{\mathit{T}},\mathit{F},\mathit{M}_{0}}\rangle
and its unfolding
\mathcal{U}=\langle{\mathit{B},\mathit{E},\mathit{G},\mathbf{c}_{0},\pi}\rangle
.
Then for any two conditions (events)
\mathit{b},\mathit{b}^{\prime}
(
\mathit{e},\mathit{e}^{\prime}
) such that
\mathit{b}~\mathbf{co}~\mathit{b}^{\prime}
(
\mathit{e}~\mathbf{co}~\mathit{e}^{\prime}
), one has
\pi(\mathit{b})\neq\pi(\mathit{b}^{\prime})
(
\pi(\mathit{e})\neq\pi(\mathit{e}^{\prime})
).
Moreover, every
finite configuration
\mathit{C}
of
\mathcal{U}
represents a possible firing
sequence whose resulting marking corresponds, due to the construction of

\mathcal{U}
, to a reachable marking of
\mathcal{N}
. This marking is defined as

\mathit{Mark}(\mathit{C})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\{\,\pi(\mathit{b})\mid\mathit{b}\in\mathbf{cut}(\mathit{C})\,\}
.

This means, informally speaking, that any configuration of the system can be split into consecutive parts in such a way that each part is itself a configuration obtained by unfolding the Petri net ‘renewed’ with the marking reached by the previous configuration. The following definition formalizes this.

Definition 5

Let
\mathcal{O}=\langle{\mathit{B},\mathit{E},\mathit{G},\mathbf{c}_{0}}\rangle
be an occurrence net.
For any finite configuration
\mathit{C}\in\mathcal{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathbf{f}}(\mathcal{O})
, denote by
\mathcal{O}_{\mathit{C}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\mathcal{U}(\langle{{\mathit{N}},\mathit{Mark}(\mathit{C})}\rangle)
the
shift
of
\mathcal{O}
by
\mathit{C}
.

\mathit{C}
is the
concatenation
of
\mathit{C}_{1}
and
\mathit{C}_{2}
, written
\mathit{C}=\mathit{C}_{1}\oplus\mathit{C}_{2}
, iff one has

1.

\mathit{C}_{1}\in\mathcal{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathbf{f}}(\mathcal{O})
and

\mathit{C}_{1}\subseteq\mathit{C}
,

2.

\mathit{C}_{2}\in\mathcal{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathbf{f}}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathit{C}_{1}})
and

\mathit{C}_{2}=\mathit{C}\backslash\mathit{C}_{1}
.

Clearly, the empty configuration
\emptyset
satisfies
\mathit{C}\oplus\emptyset=\emptyset\oplus\mathit{C}=\mathit{C}
. If
\mathit{C}=\mathit{C}_{1}\oplus\mathit{C}_{2}
, write
\mathit{C}_{1}=\mathit{C}\ominus\mathit{C}_{2}

and
\mathit{C}_{2}=\mathit{C}\oslash\mathit{C}_{1}
. Moreover, write

\displaystyle\mathit{C}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathit{C}_{i}

iff

\displaystyle\mathit{C}=\mathit{C}_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathit{C}_{n}.

In figure
2

\mathit{C}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\{\beta_{1},\gamma_{1}\}
,
\mathit{C}_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\{\zeta_{1},\kappa_{1}\}
and
\mathit{C}_{3}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\{\beta_{1},\gamma_{1},\zeta_{1},\kappa_{1}\}
, one has
\mathit{C}_{3}=\mathit{C}_{1}\oplus\mathit{C}_{2}
and consequently
\mathit{C}_{1}=\mathit{C}_{3}\ominus\mathit{C}_{2}
and
\mathit{C}_{2}=\mathit{C}_{3}\oslash\mathit{C}_{1}
.

Complete Prefix.

In general,
\mathcal{U}

is an infinite net, but if
\mathcal{N}
is safe, then it is possible
to compute a finite prefix
\Pi

of
\mathcal{U}
that is “complete”
in the sense that every reachable marking of
\mathcal{N}
has a reachable
counterpart in
\Pi
, and vice
versa.

Definition 6
(complete prefix, see
[
16
,
8
,
7
]
)

Let
\mathcal{N}=\langle{{\mathit{N}},\mathit{M}_{0}}\rangle
be a safe Petri net and

\mathcal{U}=\langle{\mathit{B},\mathit{E},\mathit{G},\mathbf{c}_{0}}\rangle

{\Pi}=\langle{\mathit{B}^{\prime},\mathit{E}^{\prime},\mathit{G}^{\prime},\mathbf{c}_{0}}\rangle

is said to be a
prefix
of
\mathcal{U}
if
\mathit{E}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathit{E}
is
causally closed,
\mathit{B}^{\prime}=\mathbf{c}_{0}\cup{{\mathit{E}^{\prime}}^{\bullet}}
, and
\mathit{G}^{\prime}
is the restriction
of
\mathit{G}
to
\mathit{B}^{\prime}
and
\mathit{E}^{\prime}
. A prefix
\Pi
is said to be
complete

if for every reachable marking
\mathit{M}
of
\mathcal{N}
there exists a
configuration
\mathit{C}
of
\Pi
such that (i)
\mathit{Mark}(\mathit{C})=\mathit{M}
,
and (ii) for each transition

Complete prefix scheme.
The unfolding is stopped on each branch when some

The nested family

Doomed configurations, and how to avoid them

3.1
Bad, Free and Doomed Configurations and Markings.

In this section, we present an algorithm that identifies precisely those configurations of a Petri net unfolding from which one can no longer avoid reaching a certain long-term behaviour, its theoretical foundations, and some experimental results.
The formal setting here contains and extends the one established in
[
11
]
, specialized to the 1-safe case. We assume that we are given a set of
bad markings

\mathcal{Z}\subseteq 2^{\mathit{P}}
. Since we are interested in long-term behaviours, we assume that
\mathcal{Z}
is reachability-closed, i.e.
\mathit{M}\in\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathit{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathit{M}^{\prime}
imply
\mathit{M}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}
.

Define
\mathcal{B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\{\mathit{C}\in\mathcal{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathbf{f}}:\mathit{Mark}(\mathit{C})\in\mathcal{Z}\}
as the set of
bad configurations
, and let
\mathcal{B}_{0}
be the set of configurations in
\mathcal{B}
that are contained in
\Pi_{0}
.

\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
is
absorbing
or
upward closed
, that is,
for all
\mathit{C}_{1}\in\mathcal{B}
and
\mathit{C}_{2}\in\mathcal{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathbf{f}}
such that
\mathit{C}_{1}\subseteq\mathit{C}_{2}
, one must have
\mathit{C}_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
.

For any
\mathit{C}\in\mathcal{C}
, let
\Omega_{\mathit{C}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\left\{\omega\in\Omega:~\mathit{C}\subseteq\omega\right\}
denote the maximal runs into which
\mathit{C}
can evolve.
We are interested in
those finite configurations all of whose maximal extensions are ‘bad’, where we consider infinite configurations as bad if they contain a bad finite configuration. We will call such configurations
doomed
, since from them, the system cannot avoid entering a bad marking sooner or later (and from then on, all reachable markings are bad).

Definition 7

Configuration
\mathit{C}\in\mathcal{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathbf{f}}
is
doomed
iff

\displaystyle\forall~\omega\in\Omega_{\mathit{C}}:~\exists~\mathit{C}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathbf{f}}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathit{C}\subseteq\mathit{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\omega\\
\land~\mathit{Mark}(\mathit{C}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Z}\end{array}\right.

The set of doomed configurations is denoted
\mathcal{D}
; denote the set of minimal elements in
\mathcal{D}
by
\check{\mathcal{D}}
.
If
\mathit{C}
is not doomed, it has at least one maximal extension that never reaches bad markings. We call configurations that are not doomed
free
, and denote the set of free configurations by
\mathcal{F}
.

All reachable markings are represented by at least one configuration.
Moreover, since the future evolution of
\mathcal{N}
depends only on the current marking,

\mathit{Mark}(\mathit{C}_{1})=\mathit{Mark}(\mathit{C}_{2})
for two configurations
\mathit{C}_{1}
and
\mathit{C}_{2}
implies that either both
\mathit{C}_{1}
and
\mathit{C}_{2}
are free, or both are doomed.
Therefore, by extension, we call
\mathit{Mark}(\mathit{C})
free or doomed whenever
\mathit{C}
is.

4.1
Cliff-Edges and Ridges.

From the minimal doomed configurations, we derive the critical ‘points’ at which a run becomes doomed:

Definition 11

An event set
\gamma\subseteq\mathit{E}
is called a
cliff-edge
iff there exists a minimally doomed configuration
\mathit{C}\in\check{\mathcal{D}}
such that
\gamma=\mathbf{crest}(\mathit{C})
. The set of cliff-edges is denoted
\Gamma
. The folding
\chi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\pi(\gamma)\subseteq{\mathit{T}}
of a cliff-edge
\gamma
is called a
ridge
.

To complete the map of the evolutional landscape for
\mathcal{N}
, it is important to find, in a bounded prefix of the unfolding, all ridges that determine the viability of a trajectory. Notice that the completeness of prefix
\Pi_{0}
only guarantees that all reachable
markings
of
\mathcal{N}
are represented by at least one configuration of
\Pi_{0}
; this does not extend to a guarantee that all concurrent steps that lead into a doomed marking can be found in
\Pi_{0}
as well. Fortunately, one has:

Lemma 4

For every ridge
\chi
of
\mathcal{N}
there is a witness in
\Pi_{0}
, i.e. there exists a minimally doomed configuration
\mathit{C}
in
\Pi_{1}
such that
\pi(\mathbf{crest}(\mathit{C}))=\chi
.

Proof:
Fix
\chi
, and let
\mathit{C}_{\chi}
be any configuration such that
\pi(\mathbf{crest}(\mathit{C}_{\chi}))=\chi
; set
\mathit{M}^{\mathit{C}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\mathit{Mark}(\mathit{C}_{\chi})
, and let
\mathit{M}_{\chi}^{\mathit{C}}
the unique reachable marking such that
\mathit{M}_{\chi}^{\mathit{C}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\chi}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathit{M}^{\mathit{C}}
. Then any such
\mathit{M}_{\chi}^{\mathit{C}}
is represented by some
\mathit{C}^{\chi}
in
\Pi_{0}
. By construction, there exists a cliff-edge
\gamma
such that
\mathit{C}^{\chi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\gamma}}{{\leadsto}}
and
\pi(\gamma)=\chi
. Then
\mathit{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\mathit{C}^{\chi}\cup\gamma
is a minimally doomed configuration that lies within
\Pi_{1}
.

\Box

4.2
Measuring the Distance from Doom

With the above, we have the tools to draw a map of the ‘landscape’ in which the system evolves, with doomed zones and cliff-edges highlighted. What we wish to add now is to assist
navigation
in this landscape: we intend to give a meaningful measure of how well, or badly, a current system state is protected against falling from a cliff-edge. We chose to measure this distance not in terms of the
length
of paths, or similar notions, but rather in terms of the
choices
that are made by the system in following a particular path.

Consider a configuration
\mathit{C}
and the nonsequential process that it represents. Some of the events in
\mathit{C}
can be seen as representing a
decision
, in the sense that their occurrence took place in conflict with some event that was enabled by some prefix of
\mathit{C}
. The number of such events gives a measure of the information contained in
\mathit{C}
, in terms of the decisions necessary to obtain
\mathit{C}
:

Definition 12

Let
\mathit{C}\in\mathcal{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathbf{f}}
, and define

\displaystyle\mathbf{dech}(\mathit{C})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\left|\left\{\mathit{e}\in\mathit{C}:~\exists~\mathit{e}^{\prime}\in\mathit{E}:~\mathit{e}\mathrel{\#}_{\sigma}^{\mathit{C}}\mathit{e}^{\prime}\right\}\right|,

\mathrel{\#}_{\sigma}
is the
strict
\mathit{C}
-conflict
relation defined, for all
\mathit{e}\in\mathit{C}

\displaystyle\mathit{e}\mathrel{\#}_{\sigma}^{\mathit{C}}\mathit{e}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}

\displaystyle\mathit{e}\mathrel{\#}_{\delta}\mathit{e}^{\prime}~\land~\langle\mathit{e}^{\prime}\rangle\subseteq\mathit{C}.

\mathbf{dech}(\mathit{C})
is called the
decisional height
of
\mathit{C}
.

4.3
Computing Protectedness is Feasible

Computation of
\mathbf{prot}(\bullet)
does not require any larger data structure than those already required for computing
\check{\mathcal{D}}
according to Lemma
2
:

Lemma 5

There is a complete prefix scheme producing a complete prefix
\Pi_{0}
whose size is bounded by the number of reachable markings, and such that for every finite configuration
\mathit{C},

\mathbf{prot}(\mathit{C})
can be computed on
\Pi_{0}(\mathit{Mark}(\mathit{C}))
.

Proof:
If
\check{\mathcal{D}}\cap\mathcal{C}(\Pi_{0})=\emptyset
, then all extensions of
\mathit{C}
are free, and we are done. Otherwise,
the crucial step is to find an adequate
total
order
\prec
on finite configurations, that ensures that
\Pi_{0}
contains at least one minimally doomed configuration that minimizes
\mathbf{dech}(\bullet)
over all minimally doomed configurations in
\mathcal{U}(\mathit{Mark}(\mathit{C}))
.
The following order
\prec
is obtained by modifying the total order
\prec_{F}
introduced in
[
8
]
, Def. 6.2.:
For
\mathit{C}_{1},\mathit{C}_{2}\in\mathcal{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathbf{f}}
, write
\mathit{C}_{1}\prec\mathit{C}_{2}
iff either

•

\mathbf{dech}(\mathit{C}_{1})<\mathbf{dech}(\mathit{C}_{2})
, or

•

\mathbf{dech}(\mathit{C}_{1})=\mathbf{dech}(\mathit{C}_{2})
and
\mathit{C}_{1}\ll\mathit{C}_{2}
, or

•

\mathbf{dech}(\mathit{C}_{1})=\mathbf{dech}(\mathit{C}_{2})
and
\mathit{C}_{1}\equiv\mathit{C}_{2}
, and
FC(\mathit{C}_{1})\ll FC(\mathit{C}_{2})
,

where
\ll
(
\equiv
) denote lexicographic ordering (lexicographic equivalence) wrt some total ordering of the transition set
{\mathit{T}}
, and
FC
denotes Cartier-Foata normal form. The proof of Theorem 6.4. of
[
8
]
extends immediately, proving that
\prec
is an adequate total order; therefore, Lemma 5.3. of
[
8
]
applies, hence any complete prefix
\Pi_{0}^{\prec}
obtained via the scheme using
\prec
is bounded in size by the reachability graph. Now, let
\mathcal{C}^{*}
be the set of configurations from
\check{\mathcal{D}}(\mathit{Mark}(\mathit{C}))
that minimize
\mathbf{dech}(\bullet)
; by construction of
\prec
, one has
\mathcal{C}^{*}\cap\mathcal{C}(\Pi_{0}^{\prec})\neq\emptyset
.
\Box

5
Discussion

The results presented here give a toolkit for the analysis of tipping situations in a safe Petri net, i.e. when and how a basin boundary is crossed; an algorithmic method for finding minimally doomed configuration has been developed, implemented and tested.

Moreover, we have introduced a measure of
protectedness
that indicates the number of
decisions
that separate a free state from doom. It uses an intrinsic notion of decisional height that allows to warn about impending dangerous scenarios; at the same time, this height is also ’natural’ for unfoldings, in the sense that it induces an adequate linear order that allows to compute complete prefixes of bounded size.

On a more general level, the results here are part of a broader effort to provide a discrete, Petri-net based framework for dynamical systems analysis in the life sciences. The applications that we target lie in systems biology and ecology.

Future work will investigate
possibilities for Doom Avoidance Control, i.e. devising strategies that allow to steer away from doom; we expect to complement the existing approaches via structural methods of e.g. Antsaklis et al
[
14
]
, and also the unfolding construction of Giua and Xie
[
11
]
. A crucial question is the knowledge that any control player can be assumed to have, as a basis for chosing control actions. We believe the protectedness measure is a valid candidate for coding this information, so that a controller may take action when the system is too close to doom (wrt some thresholds to be calibrated) but there still remain decisns that can be taken to avoid it.
Evaluating this option, along with other approaches, must, however, be left to future work.
