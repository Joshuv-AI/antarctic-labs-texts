Vortical currents and reciprocal relations for transport coefficients in the electron hydrodynamic regime
Nisarg Chadha, Subroto Mukerjee

Abstract
We investigate the hydrodynamic regime in metals with momentum-conserving electron-electron scattering. The conservation of momentum results in well-defined dynamics whose effects we investigate via the relevant continuity equations. Studying semiclassical thermoelectric transport in the presence of a Berry curvature, we find anomalous contributions to the charge and heat transport currents arising from gradients of the velocity field. These contributions are non-vanishing for systems lacking centro-symmetry, and the corresponding transport coefficients do not obey the standard Onsager reciprocity relations. Instead, we show that the response coefficients relating the currents to the stress tensor obey independent reciprocity relations with the stress tensor and thus exhibit cross-tensor effects of the charge density and the entropy density with the momentum density. The requirement of the lack of centrosymmetry is shown to arise as a consequence of the Curie symmetry principle, which prevents cross-tensor effects in isotropic systems.



I
Introduction

The dynamics of electrons in typical metals are dominated by phonon and impurity scattering.
The synthesis of high-purity 2D crystals
Moll et al. 2016
;
Crossno et al. 2016
has generated interest in the hydrodynamic regime of electron interactions dominated by momentum-conserving electron-electron scattering.
Electron transport in the hydrodynamic regime has been studied extensively
Gurzhi 1963
;
Gurzhi 1968
;
Crossno et al. 2016
;
Levitov and Falkovich 2016
;
Scaffidi et al. 2017
;
Narozhny et al. 2017
;
Lucas and Das Sarma 2018
;
Glazov 2021
;
Fritz and Scaffidi 2023
to determine experimentally observable signatures of the hydrodynamic regime.

In the hydrodynamic regime, momentum-conserving scattering manifests as a viscosity for the electron fluid.
Consequently, the electron hydrodynamic regime displays interesting properties such as a higher-than-ballistic conductance
Gurzhi 1963
;
Guo et al. 2017
, negative non-local resistance
Crossno et al. 2016
;
Levitov and Falkovich 2016
, Poiseuille flow
de Jong and Molenkamp 1995
;
Sulpizio et al. 2019
;
Narozhny et al. 2021
, and a breakdown of the Wiedemann-Franz law
Moll et al. 2016
;
Principi and Vignale 2015
;
Lucas and Das Sarma 2018
.
Turbulent flow in the hydrodynamic regime leads to the formation of vortices, which have been used to visualize the hydrodynamic flow of electrons
Levitov and Falkovich 2016
;
Aharon-Steinberg et al. 2022
.
These signatures can be understood as collective phenomena emerging from a classical hydrodynamic treatment on large length and time scales compared to characteristic microscopic scales.

On the other hand, quantum geometric effects from the Berry curvature
Berry 1984
have been shown to contribute to the macroscopic properties of the system.
Transport effects such as the quantum anomalous Hall effect
Haldane 1988
,
quantum spin Hall effect
Kane and Mele 2005
;
Xiao et al. 2006
, valley Hall effect
Mak et al. 2014
, anomalous Nernst effect
Yu et al. 2015
, Magnus Hall effect
Papaj and Fu 2019
, along with their non-linear analogues
Sodemann and Fu 2015
;
Kang et al. 2019
;
Du et al. 2021
;
Das et al. 2023
arise from the effects of non-trivial Berry curvature and symmetries.

The dynamical response of a system to spontaneous fluctuations is described by the fluctuation-dissipation theorem
Kubo 1966
.
The fluctuation-dissipation theorem can also be used to describe transport for external forces, and leads to conditions for the transport coefficients.
Einstein’s relations
Einstein et al. 1905
;
Einstein 1956
for describing Brownian motion are the first application of the fluctuation-dissipation theorem, and have been generalized to describe transport contributions from gradients in the chemical potential.
Onsager derived his reciprocity relations
Onsager 1931a
;
Onsager 1931b
on the basis of the time-reversal invariance of the microscopic equations of motion.
Applying Onsager’s principle of microscopic reversibility to the linear response functions provides the phenomenological relations between the transport coefficients using the Kubo formulae
Kubo 1957
;
Kubo 1966
;
Kubo et al. 2012

Any discussion for transport usually refers to the transport currents and not the total current density.
Transport currents are obtained by removing contributions from the internal circulating currents, called the magnetization current
Cooper et al. 1997
.
Anomalous transport for mechanical forces such as electric and gravitational potential gradients is caused by the anomalous velocity
Chang and Niu 1996
;
Sundaram and Niu 1999
, leading to transport transverse to the applied force.
Statistical forces such as gradients in temperature and chemical potential contribute to anomalous transport from the subtraction of Berry curvature corrections to the magnetization currents
Xiao et al. 2006
.

The Berry curvature has been shown to cause an asymmetric Poiseuille flow profile
Toshio et al. 2020
;
Hasdeo et al. 2021
and an anomalous vortex-induced charge current
Toshio et al. 2020
.
It has long been known that rotating chiral fluids can have current contributions from vortices
Son and Surówka 2009
;
Shitade et al. 2020
.
Since the curl of the velocity field(vorticity) for a rotating fluid is simply the angular velocity
Nanda and Hosur 2023
, the vortical contributions for a non-uniform velocity field are related to the chiral vortical effects.

In this work, we formulate a semiclassical theory for thermoelectric transport in the hydrodynamic regime.
By deriving the expressions for the charge and heat currents, we show the contribution from vortices to the heat current.
An analysis of the vortical charge current and the vortical heat current shows that the usual Onsager relations
Onsager 1931a
;
Onsager 1931b
;
Casimir 1945
do not hold.
The dominance of momentum-conserving scattering in the hydrodynamic regime makes momentum density a well-defined variable whose dynamics are controlled by the stress tensor.
Motivated by the appearance of gradients in the velocity field, we formulate reciprocal relations between the currents and the stress tensor based on the principle of microscopic reversibility
Onsager 1931a
;
Casimir 1945
;
De Groot 1951
;
de Groot and Mazur 1954
.
We show that the vortical charge current obeys the reciprocity relation with the stress tensor and motivate the expression for the transport stress tensor.
The vortical currents vanish in an isotropic system, in accordance with the Curie symmetry principle
De Groot and Mazur 2013
, which prohibits cross-transport effects between quantities with different tensor ranks.

The rest of this work is organized as follows.
In Sec.
II
, we use the theory of semiclassical transport to derive the continuity equations for our system.
The momentum continuity equation gives the Navier-Stokes equation for the velocity field driven by the external mechanical and statistical forces.
We derive the expressions for the transport and magnetization currents in Sec.
III
to obtain the usual longitudinal and anomalous currents along with the vortical currents.
In Sec.
IV
, we derive the reciprocal relations between the transport coefficients expected as a consequence of Onsager’s principle of microscopic reversibility.
We conclude in Sec.
V
.

II
Semiclassical Theory of Transport

The theory of semiclassical evolution describes the dynamics of a wave packet constructed by the superposition of Bloch states
Chang and Niu 1996
;
Sundaram and Niu 1999
.
The external fields are assumed to vary on large length scales compared to the wave packet size
Girvin and Yang 2019
.
To first order in an applied electric field, the semiclassical equations of motion for the wave packet in an electric field without an external magnetic field are:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\dot{p}_{c}}=-e\mathbf{E}

\displaystyle\mathbf{\dot{r}_{c}}=\frac{\partial\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}}{\partial\mathbf{p}}-\frac{1}{\hbar}\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{c}\times\mathbf{\Omega_{p_{c}}}

(1)

Here,
\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}
is the band dispersion,
\Omega_{\mathbf{p}}
is the Berry curvature, -e is the electronic charge, and
(\mathbf{r_{c}},\mathbf{p_{c}})
denotes the position of the wave packet centre in phase space.
Hereafter, we shall drop the subscripts on the wave packet coordinates.

The second term in the expression for the velocity is the anomalous velocity.
It arises from the contributions to the velocity from the perturbations in the wave functions due to the electric field.
The anomalous velocity gives transport currents transverse to the electric field and is responsible for the quantum anomalous Hall effect.

Our semiclassical treatment of the wave packets in phase space allows us to use the Boltzmann transport equation to study the dynamics of a system perturbed from equilibrium by slowly varying fields.
The Boltzmann equation describes the phase-space evolution of the distribution function
f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p},t)
in the presence of collision scattering.

\displaystyle\frac{df}{dt}=\Bigl(\frac{df}{dt}\Bigr)_{coll}

(2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+\mathbf{\dot{r}}.\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}f+\mathbf{\dot{p}}.\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}f=\mathcal{C}[f]

The left side of Eq. (
2
) is a linear order Taylor expansion of the distribution function governed by the equations of motion Eq. (
1
).
The right side, called the collision integral, gives the change in phase space density arising from scattering processes.

Furthermore, we assume the external fields vary on long scales compared to the characteristic scattering scales set by the relaxation time and mean free path.
This is the hydrodynamic assumption
Chaikin et al. 1995
, which assumes that at every point, the system can be assumed to be close to equilibrium maintained by the rapid scattering processes.
Therefore, only the conserved quantities are allowed to have well-defined dynamics governed by continuity equations.

We define a local drift velocity field
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})
defined as the average velocity in a small volume around
\mathbf{r}
.
Due to our assumption of local equilibrium, in a frame moving with a velocity
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})
, the volume element will appear as a stationary subsystem at equilibrium.
In this frame, the dispersion is given by
\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}=\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}-\mathbf{u}.\mathbf{p}
.
Therefore, the distribution function for the whole system will be given by:

f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p},t)=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\mathbf{r},t)(\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}-\mu(\mathbf{r},t)-\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r}).\mathbf{p})}+1}

(3)

Such a distribution function has been assumed in Ref.
Lucas and Fong 2018
;
Toshio et al. 2020
;
Hasdeo et al. 2021
to get a convenient expression of current densities in terms of the drift velocity field, which can be solved self-consistently using the conservation laws.

We can get the continuity equations for a space-averaged conserved variable(
\bar{\chi}
) from Eq. (
2
) by multiplying both sides with
\chi
, and integrating over
d\mathbf{p}
.
By definition, the conserved variables are not relaxed by the collision integral.
Therefore, the integral of the conserved densities with the collision integral on the right-side vanishes.
Assuming the presence of a weak Drude-like momentum relaxation with scattering rate
\gamma
by impurities, the linear order continuity equations for the average particle density(
\bar{n}
) and average momentum density(
\bar{\mathbf{p}}
) are:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bar{n}+\nabla.\mathbf{J}^{N}=0

(4a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bar{p}_{i}+\nabla_{j}\Pi_{ij}=e\bar{n}E_{i}-\gamma\bar{p}_{i}

(4b)

Here
\mathbf{J}^{N}
, and
\mathbf{\Pi}
are the particle flux, and momentum-flux tensor(or stress tensor), respectively.

We shall assume summation over repeated indices in the second equation and throughout the rest of this manuscript.
The equations represent number conservation and momentum continuity, respectively.
The non-zero terms on the right side for the momentum continuity are forces due to the electric field, and the drag from impurity collisions.

The fluxes can be obtained using the wave packet velocity in Eq. (
1
).

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}^{N}=\mathbf{J}^{N,g}+\mathbf{J}^{N,a}=\int{[d\mathbf{p}]f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p},t)\left[\frac{\partial\epsilon}{\partial\mathbf{p}}+\frac{e}{\hbar}(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{\Omega_{p}})\right]}

(5a)

\displaystyle\Pi_{ij}=\Pi_{ij}^{g}+\Pi_{ij}^{a}=\int{[d\mathbf{p}]p_{i}f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p},t)\left[\frac{\partial\epsilon}{\partial\mathbf{p}}+\frac{e}{\hbar}(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{\Omega_{p}})\right]_{j}}

(5b)

The superscripts denote the contributions from the dispersion(
\frac{\partial\epsilon}{\partial\mathbf{p}}
) and anomalous velocity(
\frac{e}{\hbar}(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{\Omega_{p}})
) respectively.
For a linear order calculation of the fluxes, Eq. (
3
) can be expanded to give:

f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p},t)=f^{0}+\delta f=f^{0}(\mathbf{p})+\frac{\partial f^{0}}{\partial\epsilon}\left[\frac{\epsilon-\mu}{\beta}\delta\beta-\mathbf{p}.\mathbf{u}\right]

(6)

f^{0}
represents the equilibrium Fermi-Dirac distribution function with
\mathbf{u}=0
.
This allows us to evaluate the fluxes in terms of the drift velocity.
Particularly, this gives:

\mathbf{J}^{g}=-e\bar{n}\mathbf{u}

(7)

\bar{p}_{i}=\kappa_{i}u_{i},\kappa=\int{[d\mathbf{p}]p_{i}^{2}\left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial\epsilon}\right)}

(8)

\Pi_{ij}^{g}=P\delta_{ij}+\bar{p_{i}}u_{j},

(9)

Where P is the hydrostatic pressure
De Groot and Mazur 2013
,
P:=k_{B}T\int{[d\mathbf{p}]\ln(1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu-\mathbf{u}.\mathbf{p})})}
.

[d\mathbf{p}]=\frac{d^{d}\mathbf{p}}{(2\pi)^{d}}
is the d-dimensional volume element in
\mathbf{p}
-space.
We have used the fact that the currents are zero in the equilibrium state described by
f_{0}
.
Including the contributions from the anomalous velocity, we get the total stress tensor:

\Pi_{ij}=P\delta_{ij}+\kappa_{i}u_{i}u_{j}+\frac{e}{\hbar}\int{[d\mathbf{p}]p_{i}(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{\Omega_{\mathbf{p}}})_{j}(f^{0}+\delta f)}

(10)

Using these expressions, we can write the momentum continuity equation to get the Navier-Stokes equation for the electron drift as:

\kappa_{i}\partial_{t}u_{i}+\nabla_{i}P+\kappa_{i}[\partial_{j}(u_{i}u_{j})]+\frac{e}{\hbar}\int{[d\mathbf{p}]p_{i}(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{\Omega_{\mathbf{p}}})\frac{\partial f}{\partial\epsilon}\left[\frac{\epsilon-\mu}{\beta}\nabla_{j}(\beta-\mathbf{p}.\mathbf{u})\right]}=e\bar{n}E_{i}-\gamma\kappa_{i}u_{i}

(11)

In the weakly interacting limit, the electron-electron scattering can be assumed to give rise to a relaxation of higher moments of momentum
Lucas and Das Sarma 2018
;
Glazov 2021
.
We include such a second-harmonic relaxation through a viscous contribution to the stress tensor
Chaikin et al. 1995
;
De Groot and Mazur 2013
;
Levitov and Falkovich 2016
;
Hasdeo et al. 2021
from a shear viscosity proportional to the electron-electron scattering rate
\eta\propto\gamma_{e}
.

\kappa_{i}\partial_{t}u_{i}+\nabla_{i}P+\kappa_{i}[\partial_{j}(u_{i}u_{j})]+\frac{e}{\hbar}\int{[d\mathbf{p}]p_{i}(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{\Omega_{\mathbf{p}}})\frac{\partial f}{\partial\epsilon}\left[\frac{\epsilon-\mu}{\beta}\nabla_{j}(\beta-\mathbf{p}.\mathbf{u})\right]}-\eta\nabla^{2}u_{i}=-e\bar{n}E_{i}-\gamma\kappa_{i}u_{i}

(12)

Working in the static limit(
\partial_{t}u_{i}=0
), we get the linearized Euler equation for electron drift as:

(\gamma\kappa_{i}-\eta\nabla^{2})u_{i}=-e\bar{n}E_{i}-\frac{\nabla_{i}T}{T}\int{[d\mathbf{p}]\left[k_{B}T\ln(1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)})+(\epsilon-\mu)f^{0}\right]}

(13)

To linear-order, the pressure gradient gives the thermal driving force in Eq. (
13
).
Terms involving velocity gradients of the form
\partial_{j}u_{i}
vanish since the integrand becomes an odd function of the momentum.
The coefficient of the temperature gradient can be identified as the entropy density
\bar{s}=\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}
to simplify the Euler equation as:

(\gamma\kappa_{i}-\eta\nabla^{2})u_{i}=e\bar{n}E_{i}-\bar{s}\nabla_{i}T

(14)

Eq. (
14
) allows us to self-consistently solve for the drift velocity field for a given choice of boundary conditions.
The impurity scattering provides the solution to the inhomogenous source on the right, and the viscous term generates a spatially varying velocity profile depending on the boundary conditions.
Note that to linear order in the gradients, the velocity field is not modified by the presence of a Berry curvature.

III
Transport and Magnetization Current Densities

The fluxes in Eq. (
4
) are defined up to the addition of divergenceless quantities.
Ref.
Cooper et al. 1997
showed that the total current density can be divided into an experimentally measurable transport current and a magnetization current stemming from internal circulating currents with no net charge flow.
The Onsager and Einstein relations are shown to hold for the transport currents instead of the total current density.
In this section, we derive the expressions for the transport electric and heat currents.

Although Eq. (
5
) presents a direct inclusion for a coupling of the Berry curvature to mechanical forces in the currents, couplings of the Berry curvature with statistical forces are missing.
Such couplings are expected to exist on the basis of the Einstein relations and Onsager reciprocity.
Ref.
Xiao et al. 2006
showed that the Berry curvature leads to corrections to the magnetization currents, which give the appropriate couplings required by Einstein relations and Onsager reciprocity when subtracted from the total current density.
These contributions to the transport current explain the anomalous Nernst effect
Yu et al. 2015
.
In the wave packet picture, the Berry curvature gives an angular momentum to the electron about the wave packet centre
Xiao et al. 2006
.
Gradients in statistical forces thus produce net current flow arising from differential contributions from electron orbits about the wave packet centres.
Using the expressions for total current density and magnetization currents given in Ref.
Cooper et al. 1997
;
Xiao et al. 2006
;
Panigrahi and Mukerjee 2023
, we shall obtain the transport currents for the distribution function in Eq. (
3
).

The Berry curvature gives an orbital magnetic moment
\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{p}):=-\frac{e}{2}\bra{\psi}(\mathbf{\hat{r}}-\mathbf{r}_{c})\times\hat{\mathbf{v}}\ket{\psi}
to the wave packet.
Ref.
Culcer et al. 2004
gives the total electric current density including the contribution from the orbital magnetic moment as:

\mathbf{J}=-e\int{[d\mathbf{p}]f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p},t)\dot{\mathbf{r}}}+\nabla\times\int{[d\mathbf{p}]f\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{p})}

(15)

At zero magnetic field, the magnetization charge current(
\mathbf{j}_{M}
) is given by the curl of the magnetization density(
\mathbf{M}
)
Cooper et al. 1997
;
Xiao et al. 2006
;
Panigrahi and Mukerjee 2023

\mathbf{M}=\int{[d\mathbf{p}]\left[f\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{p})+k_{B}T\frac{e\mathbf{\Omega_{\mathbf{p}}}}{\hbar}\log{(1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu-\mathbf{u}.\mathbf{p})})}\right]}

(16)

\mathbf{j}_{M}=\nabla\times\mathbf{M}

(17)

We have made the replacement
\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}\rightarrow\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}-\mathbf{u}.\mathbf{p}
in Eq.(
16
) consistent with our formalism of equilibrium in a moving frame.
We get the charge transport current to linear order by subtracting the magnetization current from the total current density.

\mathbf{j}=-e\int{[d\mathbf{p}]f\left[\frac{\partial\epsilon}{\partial\mathbf{p}}+\frac{e}{\hbar}(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{\Omega_{\mathbf{p}}})\right]}-\nabla T\times\int{[d\mathbf{p}]\frac{e}{\hbar}\mathbf{\Omega_{\mathbf{p}}}k_{B}\left[\log(1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)})+\frac{\epsilon-\mu}{k_{B}T}f^{0})\right]}-\frac{e}{\hbar}\int{[d\mathbf{p}]\nabla(\mathbf{p}.\mathbf{u})\times\mathbf{\Omega_{p}}f^{0}}

(18)

Rearranging the terms gives us the charge transport current:

\mathbf{j}=-e\bar{n}\mathbf{u}-\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\int{[d\mathbf{p}]f^{0}(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{\Omega_{\mathbf{p}}})}-\nabla T\times\int{[d\mathbf{p}]\frac{e}{\hbar}\mathbf{\Omega_{\mathbf{p}}}k_{B}\left[\log(1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)})+\frac{\epsilon-\mu}{k_{B}T}f^{0}\right]}-\frac{e}{\hbar}\int{[d\mathbf{p}]\nabla(\mathbf{p}.\mathbf{u})\times\mathbf{\Omega_{p}}f^{0}}

(19)

The first term, obtained using Eq. (
7
) is the usual contribution from the drift of charge carriers.
The second term is a transverse current arising from the anomalous velocity, responsible for the quantum anomalous Hall effect.
The third term is the current arising from electrons rotating about the wave packet centre, manifested in the anomalous Nernst effect.
Interestingly, the fourth term represents a current contribution arising due to gradients in the drift velocity.
This current is the non-relativistic generalization of the chiral vortical effect
Son and Surówka 2009
, where the an axial current is generated from the rotation of a relativistic chiral fluid.
Since the vorticity for a rotating fluid is simply the angular velocity, the gradient-dependent vortical currents are the generalization of the chiral vortical effect.
This vortical charge current recently discussed in Ref.
Toshio et al. 2020
;
Nanda and Hosur 2023
.

The energy current to linear order at zero magnetic field is given by
Cooper et al. 1997
;
Xiao et al. 2006
;
Panigrahi and Mukerjee 2023
:

\mathbf{J^{E}}=\int{[d\mathbf{p}](\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}-\mathbf{u}.\mathbf{p})f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p},t)\dot{\mathbf{r}}}-\mathbf{E}\times\int{[d\mathbf{p}]}f^{0}\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{p})+\nabla\times\int{[d\mathbf{p}]f(\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}-\mathbf{u}.\mathbf{p})\frac{\mathbf{m}}{-e}}

(20)

Here again, we have substituted
\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}\rightarrow\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}-\mathbf{u}.\mathbf{p}
to get the dependence on the drift velocity.
The energy magnetization current is given as:

\mathbf{j}_{M}^{E}=\nabla\times\mathbf{M}^{E}-\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{M}

(21)

where the formula for the energy magnetization is derived in Ref.
Panigrahi and Mukerjee 2023
to be:

\mathbf{M}^{E}=\int[d\mathbf{p}](\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}-\mathbf{u}.\mathbf{p})f(\frac{\mathbf{m}}{-e})-\int[d\mathbf{p}]\frac{\mathbf{\Omega_{p}}}{\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{\mu}d\tilde{\mu}\left[(\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}-\mathbf{u}.\mathbf{p})f(\tilde{\mu})+k_{B}T\log(1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}-\tilde{\mu}-\mathbf{u}.\mathbf{p})})\right]

(22)

From these two equations, we can obtain the energy transport current.

\displaystyle\mathbf{j}^{E}=\int{[d\mathbf{p}](\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}-\mathbf{u}.\mathbf{p})f\frac{\partial\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}}{\partial\mathbf{p}}}+\int{[d\mathbf{p}]\frac{e}{\hbar}(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{\Omega_{p}})\left[\epsilon+k_{B}T\log(1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}-\mu)})\right]}+

(23)

\displaystyle\int{[d\mathbf{p}](\nabla T\times\mathbf{\Omega_{p}})\left[(\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}-\mu)k_{B}\log(1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)})+\int_{-\infty}^{\mu}d\tilde{\mu}\Bigl(\frac{\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}(\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}-\tilde{\mu})}{T}\frac{\partial f^{0}}{\partial\tilde{\mu}}+k_{B}\log(1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\tilde{\mu})})\Bigr)\right]}+

\displaystyle\int{d[\mathbf{p}]\frac{\nabla(\mathbf{u}.\mathbf{p})}{\hbar}\times\mathbf{\Omega_{p}}\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}f^{0}}

Using Eq. (
19
) and Eq.(
23
), we get the heat current:

\mathbf{J}^{Q}=\mathbf{J}^{E}-\frac{\mu}{-e}\mathbf{J}

(24)

The heat transport current can be obtained from the corresponding expressions for the charge transport and energy transport currents:

\displaystyle\mathbf{j}^{Q}=\bar{s}T\mathbf{u}+\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\int{[d\mathbf{p}]\left[(\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}-\mu)f^{0}+k_{B}T\log(1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}-\mu)})\right](\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{\Omega_{p}})f^{0}}+

(25)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hbar}\int{[d\mathbf{p}]\frac{(\nabla T\times\mathbf{\Omega_{p}})}{T}\int{d\tilde{\mu}(\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}-\tilde{\mu})^{2}\frac{\partial f^{0}}{\partial\tilde{\mu}}}}+\int{[d\mathbf{p}]\frac{(\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}-\mu)}{\hbar}\nabla(\mathbf{p}.\mathbf{u})\times\mathbf{\Omega_{p}}f^{0}}

The first term represents the drift current for the entropy density.
The second and third terms represent transverse heat currents due to an electric field and a temperature gradient, respectively.
The second term can be considered as the reciprocal response to the anomalous Nernst current in Eq. (
19
).
Importantly, the last term represents the heat current arising from gradients in the drift field and is the thermal analogue of the generalized vortical effect in Eq. (
19
).
Note that both the vortical currents vanish if the system has parity symmetry since the integrands become odd functions of
\mathbf{p}
.
Therefore, these vortical currents can be present only in non-centrosymmetric systems.
Since both the transport currents obey the Einstein relations
Xiao et al. 2006
;
Panigrahi and Mukerjee 2023
, we can easily incorporate a chemical potential gradient by making the replacement
\mathbf{E}\rightarrow\mathbf{E}+\frac{1}{-e}\nabla\mu
.

Onsager reciprocity relates the transport coefficients for electric current in a temperature gradient to those of a heat current in an electric field.
The reciprocity of the longitudinal drift currents in Eq. (
19
) and Eq. (
25
) can be worked out using the linearity of Eq. (
14
).
If we take the drift velocity to vanish at the boundaries, the solution of Eq. (
14
) for an electric field(
u_{i,E}
) and a temperature gradient(
u_{i,T}
) will have the same drift profile with an overall magnitude proportional to the driving terms on the right-hand side of the equation.
Therefore,
\frac{u_{i,E}(\mathbf{r})}{u_{i,T}(\mathbf{r})}=\frac{-e\bar{n}E_{i}}{\bar{s}\nabla_{i}T}
.
As a result, we see that the ratio of the magnitudes of the cross-currents will be:

\frac{j_{drift}}{j^{Q}_{drift}}=\frac{-e\bar{n}u_{T}}{\bar{s}Tu_{E}}=\frac{|\nabla T|}{T|E|}

(26)

Thus, the ratio of the transport coefficients is 1/T, as expected from Onsager reciprocity.
The reciprocity between the anomalous cross-currents(third term in Eq. (
19
) and the second term in Eq. (
25
)) is obvious from the expressions for the transport currents.

We are now left with the fourth term, the contribution from gradients in the drift field.
We cannot repeat our use of the linearity of the Euler equation to equate these terms since the integral coefficients of the drift gradients are different.
In fact, we see that the two current contributions are not reciprocal responses.

Motivated by the appearance of the strain tensor
\partial_{j}u_{i}
in the expressions for the currents, we show that these arise independently as reciprocal responses to the stress tensor.

IV
Reciprocity Relations and the Principle of Microscopic Reversibility

Irreversible phenomena(fluxes) such as heat conduction, electrical conduction, and diffusion occur due to driving
forces
such as gradients in temperature, electrostatic potential, and chemical potential.
Onsager reciprocity
Onsager 1931a
;
Onsager 1931b
provides relations between cross fluxes arising from different forces.
These relations were derived using the reversibility of the microscopic equations of motion, i.e. particles retrace their paths when their velocities are reversed and
t\rightarrow-t
.
In the presence of time-reversal odd variables like the magnetic field also need to be reversed.

We shall use the formalism in Ref.
de Groot and Mazur 1954
;
De Groot and Mazur 2013
to derive various reciprocity relations.
We divide the thermodynamic variables into even functions of the particle velocities(
A_{i}
) and odd functions of the particle velocities(
B_{k}
).
Under the influence of a force, we denote the fluctuations of these variables from their equilibrium values by
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{k}
, respectively.
To lowest order in the fluctuations, we can write the entropy change as a quadratic combination of the form:

\Delta S=-\frac{1}{2}\int{dV(g_{ij}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}+h_{kl}\beta_{k}\beta_{l})}

(27)

\Delta S\leq 0
is required by the second law of thermodynamics, which implies that
[g]
and
[h]
are positive semi-definite.

The variables
X_{i},Y_{k}
conjugate to
\alpha_{i},\beta_{k}
respectively are defined as:

\displaystyle X_{i}=\frac{\partial\Delta S}{\partial\alpha_{i}}=-\int{dVg_{ij}\alpha_{j}}

\displaystyle Y_{k}=\frac{\partial\Delta S}{\partial\beta_{k}}=-\int{dVg_{kl}\beta_{l}}

(30b)

\langle\alpha_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\beta_{k}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}},t+\tau)\rangle=-\langle\beta_{k}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}},t)\alpha_{i}(\mathbf{r},t+\tau)\rangle

(30c)

(31a)

\langle\beta_{k}(\mathbf{r},t)\Omega(\mathbf{r^{\prime}})Y_{l}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}},t)\rangle=-k_{B}\delta_{kl}\Omega(\mathbf{r^{\prime}})\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r^{\prime}})

V
Conclusion

In this work, we use a semiclassical theory to study thermoelectric transport for a viscous electron fluid.
In the presence of Berry curvature, we obtain current contributions that have been related to the chiral vortical effect in rotating relativistic chiral fluids.
We show that the charge and thermal vortical currents are not reciprocal cross-effects.
Instead, we use the contribution to the stress tensor from the anomalous velocity to demonstrate its reciprocity to the charge vortical effect.
A similar reciprocity relation for the heat current necessitates a formulation for the magnetization contribution to the stress tensor to get the coupling between the Berry curvature and statistical forces for the transport stress tensor.

The vortical currents can make pronounced contributions for non-centrosymmetric systems in the hydrodynamic regime, and can serve as experimental probes for the hydrodynamic regime.
The suppression of impurity scattering gives a well-defined momentum continuity equation with implications for measuring momentum transport in such systems.
