Introduction

1.1
Related Research and Our Main Results

The convexity of level sets of the solution to a partial differential equation in a ring-shaped domain has been studied for a long time. In general, the following Dirichlet problem is considered:

Problem 1.1

Suppose

\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\mathcal{R},

\displaystyle\Phi=0\ \ \ \ \ \

(1.2)

\displaystyle\Phi=1\ \ \ \ \ \

1.2
Main Ideas of the Proof

First, we observe that on
\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
, a quadratic function

\displaystyle f(z)=A_{\alpha{\overline{\beta}}}z^{\alpha}z^{\overline{\beta}}+\text{Re}\left(B_{\alpha\beta}z^{\alpha}z^{\beta}\right),

(1.21)

with
\alpha,\beta\in\{1,\ ...\ ,n-1\}
,
is strongly convex if and only if
A>0
and

\displaystyle\text{ the maximum eigenvalue of }B\overline{A^{-1}}\ \overline{B}A^{-1}<1.

(1.22)

Here, for strongly convex we mean the real Hessian of
f
is strictly positive definite.
This is the main idea of
[
H22N
]
and
[
H22A
]
, where we proved that in a product manifold, providing that the boundary value satisfies a convexity condition, the solution to the homogenous complex Monge-Ampère equation has Hessian of the maximum rank. In this paper, we apply this idea to the study of the convexity of level sets of solutions to the homogenous complex Monge-Ampère equation in a ring-shaped domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Suppose
\nabla\Phi\neq 0
at a point
{\bf p}
. We choose a set of coordinates
\left\{z^{\alpha},\tau\right\}
, where
\alpha,\beta\in\{1,\ ...\ ,n-1\}
and
\tau
is the
n
-th coordinate, so that
\Phi_{\alpha}=0
and
\Phi_{\tau}\neq 0
at
{\bf p}
. Then, at
{\bf p}
, we let

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha{\overline{\beta}}}=\Phi_{\alpha{\overline{\beta}}},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ {\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha\beta}=\Phi_{\alpha\beta}.

(1.23)

When the level sets of
\Phi
are strongly pseudoconvex, we have
{\mathcal{A}}>0
, and we let

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}_{\alpha}^{\theta}={\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha\mu}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mu{\overline{\eta}}}\ \overline{{\mathcal{B}}_{\eta\zeta}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\theta{\overline{\zeta}}}.

(1.24)

Using matrix notation, denoting
{\mathcal{A}}=({\mathcal{A}}_{{\alpha\overline{\beta}}}),\ {\mathcal{B}}=({\mathcal{B}}_{{\alpha\beta}})
and
{\mathcal{K}}=({\mathcal{K}}_{\alpha}^{\beta})
, the above is equivalent to

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}={\mathcal{B}}\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}}\ \overline{{\mathcal{B}}}{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}.

(1.25)

When the maximum eigenvalues of
{\mathcal{K}}
is smaller than 1, we let

\displaystyle\sigma_{\Phi}=\text{tr}(I-{\mathcal{K}})^{-1}.

(1.26)

We note that
{\mathcal{A}}
,
{\mathcal{B}}
and
{\mathcal{K}}
all depend on the choice of coordinate, but the eigenvalues of
{\mathcal{K}}
are invariant under a complex linear change of coordinates; therefore,
\sigma_{\Phi}

is invariant under a complex linear change of coordinates. So
\sigma_{\Phi}
is a well-defined function in
\overline{\mathcal{R}}
. The upper bound of
\sigma_{\Phi}
provides an estimate on the
\mathbb{C}
-convexity of the level sets of
\Phi
. How to quantitatively gauge the
\mathbb{C}
-convexity will be discussed in Section
2
.

If we assume the solution to Problem
1.6

\displaystyle\sigma_{\Phi}\leq\max_{\partial\mathcal{R}}\sigma_{\Phi}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{ in }\mathcal{R}.

We can consider the above estimate as a weak version of the constant rank theorem: providing the level sets of
\Phi
are strongly
\mathbb{C}
-convex, we have an estimate for the
\mathbb{C}
-convexity.

However, the solution
\Phi
to Problem

is only

in general. So we need to consider a perturbed Dirichlet problem, which has a smooth solution.

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathcal{R},\ \

\displaystyle>0\ \ \ \ \ \

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathcal{R},\ \

(1.30)

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle=0\ \ \ \ \ \

2.1
Quantitative Characterizations of the
\mathbb{C}
-Convexity

First, we introduce the convexity characterization of quadratic polynomials on
\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Suppose that
g_{\alpha{\overline{\beta}}}dz^{\alpha}\otimes dz^{\overline{\beta}}
is a constant coefficient Hermitian metric on
\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
. Then we say the
modulus of convexity
of a quadratic polynomial

\displaystyle P(z)=A_{{\alpha{\overline{\beta}}}}z^{\alpha}z^{{\overline{\beta}}}+\text{Re}\left(B_{\alpha\beta}z^{\alpha}z^{\beta}\right)+L

(2.1)

is greater than
\mu
, for
\mu\geq 0
, if

\displaystyle P(z)-L>\mu\left(g_{\alpha{\overline{\beta}}}z^{\alpha}z^{\overline{\beta}}\right)\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{ on }\mathbb{C}^{n-1}\backslash\left\{0\right\}.

(2.2)

Here
A
is a Hermitian matrix,
B
is a complex valued symmetric matrix, and
L
is a linear function. We say that
P
is strongly convex if and only if the modulus of convexity of
P
is greater than
0
.

We say the
degree of convexity
of
P

is greater than
\delta
, for
\delta\geq 0
, if for any

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(z)=\text{Re}\left(W_{{\alpha\beta}}z^{\alpha}z^{\beta}\right),

(2.3)

with

\displaystyle\left(W_{\alpha\beta}g^{\beta{\overline{\gamma}}}\ \overline{W_{\gamma\nu}}g^{\theta{\overline{\nu}}}\right)\leq\delta^{2},

(2.4)

P-\mathcal{W}
is strongly convex.

Remark 2.2
.

The degree and the modulus of convexity are equivalent; this is proved by Lemma A.2 of
[
H22N
]
. In Appendix
A
, we will provide an independent proof: Lemma
A.1
.

In literature, a function with convex level sets is often called quasi-convex. Therefore, we introduce the following concept of strongly quasi-
\mathbb{C}
-convexity:

Definition 2.3
.

Suppose that
\Omega
is a bounded domain with smooth boundaries and that
\Phi
is a smooth function defined on
\overline{\Omega}
. For a point
{\bf p}\in\overline{\Omega}
, we say
\Phi
is
strongly quasi-
\mathbb{C}
-convex
at
{\bf p}
if

\displaystyle\nabla{\Phi}({\bf p})\neq 0

(2.5)

and

\displaystyle\text{the second order Taylor expansion of }\Phi|_{T^{\mathbb{C}}_{\Phi,{\bf p}}}\text{ at ${\bf p}$ is strongly convex.}

(2.6)

Here,

{T^{\mathbb{C}}_{\Phi,{\bf p}}}
is the complex tangent plane of
\left\{\Phi=\Phi({\bf p})\right\}
at
{\bf p}
. We say
\Phi
is strongly quasi-
\mathbb{C}
-convex on
\Gamma\subset\overline{\Omega}
if it’s strongly quasi-
\mathbb{C}
-convex at any
{\bf p}\in\Gamma
.

Remark 2.4
.

The main goal of this paper is to prove the solution
\Phi
to Problem
1.12
is strongly quasi-
\mathbb{C}
-convex.
In the following, for convenience, we will denote quasi-
\mathbb{C}
-convexity by
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convexity
.

To gauge the
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convexity, we introduce the following concepts:

Definition 2.5

(Modulus of
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convexity)
.

Suppose
g_{i\overline{j}}dz^{i}\otimes\overline{dz^{j}}
is a constant coefficient metric on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
We say the
modulus of
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convexity
of
\Phi
is greater than
\delta\geq 0
if

\displaystyle|\nabla\Phi|_{g}>\delta

(2.7)

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{the modulus of convexity of the second order Taylor expansion}&\\
\text{ of }\Phi|_{T^{\mathbb{C}}_{\Phi,{\bf p}}}&\text{ at ${\bf p}$ is greater than $\delta$.}\end{split}

(2.8)

Definition 2.6

(Robustness of
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convexity)
.

Suppose
g_{i\overline{j}}dz^{i}\otimes\overline{dz^{j}}
is a constant coefficient Hermitian metric on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Let

{\mathscr{Q}}_{\epsilon}=\left\{q\ |\ \text{pluriharmonic quadratic polynomials with }|\nabla q|_{g}\leq\epsilon\right\}.

(2.9)

We say the
robustness of
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convexity
of
\Phi
is greater than
\epsilon\geq 0
if
for any
q\in{\mathscr{Q}}_{\epsilon}
,
\Phi-q
is strongly
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convex. In this paper, when we say quadratic functions we include linear functions.

Remark 2.7
.

An easy and useful observation is

\displaystyle{\mathscr{Q}}_{{\varepsilon}_{1}}+{\mathscr{Q}}_{{\varepsilon}_{2}}={\mathscr{Q}}_{{\varepsilon}_{1}+{\varepsilon}_{2}}.

(2.10)

So if the robustness of
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convexity of
\Phi
is greater than
{\varepsilon}
and
q\in{\mathscr{Q}}_{\delta}
with
\delta<{\varepsilon}
, then the robustness of
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convexity of
\Phi-q
is greater than
{\varepsilon}-\delta
.

Remark 2.8
.

The name robustness means how robust the
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convexity is under perturbations.
If the modulus of
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convexity of
\Phi
is greater than
0
, than the robustness of
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convexity is also greater than
0
, and vise versa; the two measurements are actually comparable in the sense of Lemma
A.3
and Lemma
A.5
.

For the quantitative characterization of the
\mathbb{C}
-convexity of a domain, we have the following concept of the modulus of
\mathbb{C}
-convexity:

Definition 2.9

(Modulus of
\mathbb{C}
-convexity of a Domain)
.

Suppose
\Omega
is a bounded domain in

with a

Then, at any point

after a unitary change of coordinates, which makes

and a

\displaystyle\partial E\cap\left\{|\text{Re}(z^{n})|<\delta,|\text{Im}(z^{n})|^{2}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n-1}|z^{\alpha}|^{2}<\delta^{2}\right\}

(2.11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{(z^{\alpha},\rho_{\bf p}(z^{\alpha},s)+\sqrt{-1}s)\big|\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n-1}|z^{\alpha}|^{2}+s^{2}<\delta^{2}\right\},

with

\mathbb{C}
-convexity of

Remark 2.10
.

A domain is strongly

-convex if and only if its modulus of

-convexity is greater than

. If the inequality (

then

Lemma
A.1
shows that for quadratic polynomials defined on
\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
the degree and the modulus of convexity are equivalent.

Lemma A.1
.

The modulus of convexity of a quadratic polynomial on
\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
is greater than
\mu\geq 0
if and only if its degree of convexity is greater than
\mu
.

Proof.

We note that any quadratic polynomial
P
on
\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
has the following form

\displaystyle P(z)=A_{{\alpha\overline{\beta}}}z^{\alpha}z^{\overline{\beta}}+\text{Re}(B_{\alpha\beta}z^{\alpha}z^{\beta})+L,

(A.1)

where

A
is hermitian,
B
is symmetric and
L
is a linear function.
Suppose
G_{\alpha{\overline{\beta}}}dz^{\alpha}\otimes\overline{dz^{\beta}}
is the metric on
\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
. We only need to show

\displaystyle\sup_{B\overline{G^{-1}}\ \overline{B}G^{-1}\leq 1}\text{Re}(B_{\alpha\beta}{z^{\alpha}z^{\beta}})=G_{\alpha{\overline{\beta}}}z^{\alpha}z^{\overline{\beta}}.

(A.2)

To simplify the computation, we change coordinates, so that
G_{{\alpha\overline{\beta}}}=\delta_{{\alpha\beta}}
. Then we need to show

\displaystyle\sup_{B\overline{B}\leq 1}\text{Re}(B_{\alpha\beta}{z^{\alpha}z^{\beta}})=|z|^{2}.

(A.3)

First, we fix
(z^{\alpha})
and choose
B_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{\overline{z^{\alpha}z^{\beta}}}{|z|^{2}}
, then

\displaystyle\text{Re}(B_{\alpha\beta}z^{\alpha}z^{\beta})=|z|^{2}.

(A.4)

So

\displaystyle\sup_{B\overline{B}\leq 1}\text{Re}(B_{\alpha\beta}{z^{\alpha}z^{\beta}})\geq|z|^{2}.

(A.5)

Then for any symmetric
B
, with
B\overline{B}\leq 1
, after a unitary change of coordinates, we can make
B=\text{diag}(\sigma_{1},\ ...\ ,\sigma_{n-1})
, with
\sigma_{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
and
\sigma_{\gamma}\leq 1
. This is the Autonne-Takagi factorization, which is Corollary 4.4.4(c) of
[
HJ13
]
(or see Lemma A.3 of
[
H22N
]
).
Therefore

\displaystyle\text{Re}(B_{\alpha\beta}z^{\alpha}z^{\beta})=\sum\sigma_{\gamma}\text{Re}({z^{\gamma}})^{2}\leq|z|^{2};

(A.6)

as a result,

\displaystyle\sup_{B\overline{B}\leq 1}\text{Re}(B_{\alpha\beta}{z^{\alpha}z^{\beta}})\leq|z|^{2}.

(A.7)

(
A.5
) and (
A.7
) together gives the equality.
∎

The following Lemmas show the relation between the
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convexity of a function and the
\mathbb{C}
-convexity of its level sets.

Lemma A.11
.

Let
\Omega
be a strongly
\mathbb{C}
-convex domain with a smooth boundary and with the modulus of
\mathbb{C}
-convexity greater than
\mu
. Suppose that
F\in C^{\infty}({\overline{\Omega}})
satisfies

\displaystyle F=0\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{ on }\Gamma,

(A.75)

\displaystyle F_{{\mathbb{n}}}>\sigma\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{ on }\Gamma,

(A.76)

where
\Gamma
is an open set of
\partial\Omega
, and
{\mathbb{n}}
is the exterior unit normal vector of
\partial\Omega
. Then the modulus of
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convexity of
F
is greater than
\min\{1,\sqrt{2}\mu\}\sigma

on
\Gamma
.

Proof of Lemma
A.11
.

The proof is straightforward, we only need to use the implicit function theorem.

Let
{\bf p}
be a point on
\Gamma
. We choose coordinates
(z^{\alpha},\tau)
, with
\tau=t+\sqrt{-1}s,
so that
{\bf p}=0
and
\{t=0\}
is the tangential plane of
\partial\Omega
at
{\bf p}
. Suppose that around
{\bf p}

\displaystyle\Omega=\left\{t>\rho(z^{\alpha},s)\right\}.

(A.77)

Then we have
\rho(0)=0
,
\nabla\rho=0
and
\partial_{t}=-\sqrt{2}{{\mathbb{n}}}
, at
{\bf p}
.
F=0
on
\Gamma
implies

\displaystyle F(z^{\alpha},\rho(z^{\alpha},s)+\sqrt{-1}s)=0.

(A.78)

The implicit function theorem gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\text{the second order Taylor expansion of }F|_{\{\tau=0\}}\text{ at ${\bf p}$}\\
=&\sqrt{2}|F_{\mathbb{n}}|\cdot\text{the second order Taylor expansion of }\rho|_{\{s=0\}}\text{ at $0$}.\end{split}

(A.79)

Therefore, the modulus of
\mathbb{C}
-convexity of
\Omega
is greater than
\mu
implies the modulus of convexity of the second order Taylor expansion of

is greater than
\sqrt{2}|F_{\mathbb{n}}|\cdot\mu.
Then the result follows.

[

Since

\displaystyle\left\{G<\log c\right\}=\mu\left\{G^{\ast}<\log c\right\},

(D.7)

we only need to study the level sets of
G^{\ast}
. We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c}\{G^{\ast}<\log(c)\}=\frac{1}{c}\left\{h(z,\zeta)+\log(|z|)<\log(c)\right\}=\left\{h(cz,\zeta)+\log(|z|)<0\right\}.

(D.8)

Here
\frac{1}{c}
means rescaling in
z
direction. So
\frac{1}{c}\left\{G^{\ast}<\log c\right\}
converges to

\displaystyle\left\{h(0,\zeta)+\log(|z|)<0\right\}=\left\{|z|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{H(0,\zeta)}\right\}.

(D.9)

Let
f_{\zeta}
be an extremal disc so that

\displaystyle f_{\zeta}(0)=0\text{ \ \ \ \ and \ \ \ \ }\pi f_{\zeta}^{\prime}(0)=\zeta,

(D.10)

where
\pi
is the projection from
\mathbb{C}^{n}\backslash\{0\}
to
\mathbb{C}P^{n-1}
;
then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{H(0,\zeta)}=\lim_{z\rightarrow 0}\frac{|z|^{2}}{e^{2G^{\ast}}(z,\zeta)}=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\frac{|f_{\zeta}(\tau)|^{2}}{|\tau|^{2}}=|f_{\zeta}^{\prime}(0)|^{2}.

(D.11)

So

\mu\left\{|z|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{H(0,\zeta)}\right\}
is the indicatrix.

By slightly generalizing the argument on Page 47 of
[
L88
]
, we know the indicatrix for a strongly
\mathbb{C}
-convex domain is strongly convex.
So when
\theta
is large enough,
\{G\leq-\theta\}
is a strongly convex domain. We choose
B_{r_{0}}\subset\{G\leq-\theta\}
, then

\{G\leq-\theta\}
is connected with
B_{r_{0}}
by
the following family of strongly convex domains

\displaystyle s\{G\leq-\theta\}+(1-s)B_{r_{0}}.

(D.12)

Then the family

\displaystyle E^{t}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\{G\leq-2\theta(1-t)\}&\text{ for }t\in[\frac{1}{2},1],\\
2t\{G\leq-\theta\}+(1-2t)B_{r_{0}}&\text{ for }t\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]\end{array}\right.

satisfies the condition of Lemma
D.1
.
∎

Appendix E
Simplified Proof in Dimension
2

In this appendix, we provide an estimate for the robustness of
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convexity in the case of
n=2
, under an unrealistic assumption that solutions to Problem
1.6
are smooth. But the computations and arguments in this case are much simpler and straightforward, and they demonstrates the main idea of this paper. That is the purpose of this appendix.
We will prove the following apriori estimate:

Proposition E.1

(Apriori Estimate in
2
-dimension with the Smoothness Assumption)
.

Suppose
\Phi
is a smooth solution to Problem
1.6
in the case of
n=2
, and
\Phi
is strongly
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convex. Then the robustness of the
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convexity of
\Phi
is greater than
\epsilon
, where
\epsilon
is a constant depending on the geometry of
\mathcal{R}
.

In Section
E.1
, we explain the foliation structure associated to a strongly
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convex solution of Problem
1.6
, which is not available when we consider the perturbed problem, Problem
1.12
.
In the proof of the above Proposition, we need to first estimate the gradient; this is the content of Section
E.2
.
We then introduce the auxiliary function
{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi}
in Section
E.3
, which is a simplification of the quantity
{\mathcal{K}}
we constructed in general dimensions. In Section
E.4
and
E.5
, we show
\frac{1}{1-{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi}}
is subharmonic along each leaf, which is essential for the convexity estimate. In Section
E.6
we proved Proposition
E.1
using a continuity estimate.

In this appendix, we let
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{{i\overline{j}}}dz^{i}\otimes dz^{\overline{j}}
be a constant coefficient Hermitian metric on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
and

\displaystyle{\mathscr{Q}}_{\epsilon}=\left\{q|\text{quadratic pluriharmonic polynomials, }|\nabla q|_{\mathcal{G}}\leq\epsilon\right\}.

(E.1)

E.1
Foliation Structure

Given a solution
\Phi
to the homogenous complex Monge-Ampère equation in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with the rank of
(\Phi_{{i\overline{j}}})
being
n-1
, there is a foliation structure, formed by integrating kernels of
(\Phi_{{i\overline{j}}})
. This has been discussed in many works including
[
L81
]

[
D02
]

[
CFH20
]
. In our case (n=2), according to the apriori assumption, given any interior point
{\bf p}
, we can choose a coordinate
(\tau,z)
on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
so that
\Phi_{{z\overline{z}}}({\bf p})\neq 0
. Then we let

\displaystyle Z=\partial_{\tau}-\frac{\Phi_{\tau\overline{z}}}{\Phi_{z\overline{z}}}\partial_{z},

(E.2)

and it satisfies

\displaystyle[Z,\overline{Z}]=0.

(E.3)

According to the complex Frobienius Theorem, we can find a holomorphic map
\varphi(\zeta)=(\zeta,h(\zeta)),
from a disc in
\mathbb{C}
to
\mathbb{C}^{2}
such that

\displaystyle\varphi(0)={\bf p},\text{ and }\varphi_{\ast}(\partial_{\zeta})=\partial_{\tau}-\frac{\Phi_{\tau\overline{z}}}{\Phi_{z\overline{z}}}\partial_{z}.

(E.4)

At a boundary point the map is from a half disc to
\mathbb{C}^{2}.
The image of
\varphi
is called a local leaf (or, for simplicity, a leaf), and since through any point we can find a leaf, these leaves form a foliation in
\mathcal{R}
. In the following, we use
\zeta
to denote the complex coordinate on a leaf.

With
\Phi_{z\overline{z}}\neq 0
, the homogenous complex Monge-Ampère equation for
\Phi
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\Phi_{\tau\overline{\tau}}-\frac{\Phi_{\tau\overline{z}}\Phi_{z\overline{\tau}}}{\Phi_{z\overline{z}}}=0

(E.5)

and also

\displaystyle\Phi_{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}=0.

(E.6)

This says
\Phi
is a harmonic function when restricted to a leaf.

We apply
\partial_{z}
to (
E.5
) and get

\displaystyle\Phi_{z{\tau\overline{\tau}}}-\frac{\Phi_{z\tau{\overline{z}}}\Phi_{z\overline{\tau}}}{\Phi_{z\overline{z}}}-\frac{\Phi_{\tau\overline{z}}\Phi_{zz{\overline{\tau}}}}{\Phi_{z\overline{z}}}+\frac{\Phi_{\tau\overline{z}}\Phi_{z\overline{\tau}}\Phi_{z{z\overline{z}}}}{(\Phi_{z\overline{z}})^{2}}=0.

(E.7)

This is equivalent to

\displaystyle\Phi_{z\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}=0,

(E.8)

which says
\Phi_{z}
is harmonic when restricted to a leaf.

Remark E.2
.

Let
G
be the pluricomplex Green’s function of a strongly
\mathbb{C}
-convex domain
\Omega
with a smooth boundary; then, the rank of
\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}G
is
n-1
and the kernels of
\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}G
form an extremal disc (see
[
L81
]
and
[
L84
]
). For any point
{\bf z}
in
\Omega\backslash\{0\}
, where
0
is the singular point of
G
, we can find a holomorphic map
\varphi
from the unit disc
D
in
\mathbb{C}
to
\Omega
, with

\displaystyle\varphi_{\ast}\partial_{\zeta}\in\text{ kernel of }\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}G,\ \ \ \ \ \text{ in }\Omega\backslash\{0\},

(E.9)

and

\displaystyle\varphi(D)\ni 0,{\bf z}\ \ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \ \varphi(\partial D)\subset\partial\Omega.

(E.10)

However, in our situation, we would not have an “extremal cylinder”. An entire leaf may be an infinite strap. So we only consider local leaves. This is the main reason why Problem
1.6
does not have a smooth solution in general. For geodesic problem in the space of Kähler potentials, we discussed a similar singular phenomenon in
[
H21
]
.

E.2
Positive Lower Bound Estimate for Norms of Gradients

In this section, we show the norms of the gradients of
\Phi
have a positive lower bound. We consider the following quantity:

\displaystyle S=\frac{\Phi_{\zeta}\Phi_{\overline{\zeta}}}{\mathcal{G}(\partial_{\zeta},\partial_{\overline{\zeta}})},

(E.11)

where
\zeta
is the complex coordinate on a leaf and
\mathcal{G}
is the Hermitian metric on
\mathbb{C}
. We know that
S
is independent of the choice of coordinate on the leaf. Actually, it’s the square of the norm of the gradient of the restriction of
\Phi
to a leaf; as a consequence,

\displaystyle|\nabla\Phi|_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}>S.

(E.12)

We will show
S
has a positive lower bound in
\mathcal{R}
. First, we estimate
S
on the boundary; then, we show
\log(S)
is a superharmonic function on a leaf. The desired estimate then follows from the maximum principle.

When estimating
S
at a boundary point
{\bf p}
, we choose a set of coordinates
(\tau,z)
on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, so that
\mathcal{G}_{{i\overline{j}}}=\delta_{ij}
and
\Phi_{z}({\bf p})=0
. Then, at
{\bf p}
,

\displaystyle S=\frac{\left|(\partial_{\tau}-\frac{\Phi_{\tau\overline{z}}}{\Phi_{z\overline{z}}}\partial_{z})\Phi\right|^{2}}{\left|\partial_{\tau}-\frac{\Phi_{\tau\overline{z}}}{\Phi_{z\overline{z}}}\partial_{z}\right|^{2}_{\mathcal{G}}}=\frac{|\Phi_{\tau}|^{2}}{1+\left|\frac{\Phi_{\tau\overline{z}}}{\Phi_{z\overline{z}}}\right|^{2}}.

(E.13)

Therefore, for a constant
C
depending on the
C^{2}
norm of
\Phi
and the lower bound of
\Phi_{z\overline{z}}
,

\displaystyle C|\Phi_{\mathbb{n}}|^{2}\leq S\leq|\Phi_{\mathbb{n}}|^{2},

(E.14)

where
{\mathbb{n}}
is the unit normal vector to
\partial_{\mathcal{R}}
at
{\bf p}
. According to Lemma
A.11
the lower bound of
\Phi_{z\overline{z}}
depends on the
\mathbb{C}
-convexity of the boundary and the gradient of
\Phi
on the boundary. The lower bound estimate for
|\Phi_{\mathbb{n}}|
can be find in Appendix
B
. Thus the lower bound for

S
on the boundary is known.

To get the interior estimate for
S
, we need to show
\log(S)
is superharmonic on each leaf. We have

\displaystyle(\log(S))_{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}=\left[\log(\Phi_{\zeta}\Phi_{\overline{\zeta}})-\log\mathcal{G}(\partial_{\zeta},\partial_{{\overline{\zeta}}})\right]_{{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}}.

(E.15)

In the following, we compute
\left[\log(\Phi_{\zeta}\Phi_{\overline{\zeta}})\right]_{{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}}
and
-\left[\log\mathcal{G}(\partial_{\zeta},\partial_{{\overline{\zeta}}})\right]_{{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}}
seperately and show that they are both non-positive.

Using
\Phi_{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}=0
, which is (
E.6
), and
\Phi_{\zeta{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}}=0
, which follows from the previous one, we have

\displaystyle\left[\log(\Phi_{\zeta}\Phi_{\overline{\zeta}})\right]_{{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}}=\left[\frac{\Phi_{\zeta\zeta}\Phi_{\overline{\zeta}}+\Phi_{\zeta}\Phi_{{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}}}{\Phi_{\zeta}\Phi_{\overline{\zeta}}}\right]_{{\overline{\zeta}}}=\frac{|\Phi_{\zeta\zeta}|^{2}}{|\Phi_{\zeta}|^{2}}-\frac{|\Phi_{\zeta\zeta}\Phi_{\overline{\zeta}}|^{2}}{|\Phi_{\zeta}|^{4}}=0.

(E.16)

For the computation of
\left[\log\mathcal{G}(\partial_{\zeta},\partial_{{\overline{\zeta}}})\right]_{{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}}
, we choose a coordinate
(\tau,z)
, so that
g_{{i\overline{j}}}=\delta_{ij}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\partial_{\zeta},\partial_{\overline{\zeta}})=1+\left|\frac{\Phi_{\tau{\overline{z}}}}{\Phi_{{z\overline{z}}}}\right|^{2}.

(E.17)

As a locally defined function
\frac{\Phi_{\tau{\overline{z}}}}{\Phi_{{z\overline{z}}}}
is holomorphic on each leaf; actually it is
-h^{\prime}
, where
h
is the
\tau
-component of
\varphi
in (
E.4
).
This can be verified by applying
\partial_{\overline{\zeta}}
to
\frac{\Phi_{\tau{\overline{z}}}}{\Phi_{{z\overline{z}}}}
. In the following, we denote
\frac{\Phi_{\tau{\overline{z}}}}{\Phi_{{z\overline{z}}}}
by
f
, and then

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\partial_{\zeta},\partial_{\overline{\zeta}})=1+|f|^{2};

(E.18)

we have

\displaystyle[\log(1+|f|^{2})]_{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}=\left(\frac{f_{\zeta}\overline{f}}{1+|f|^{2}}\right)_{\overline{\zeta}}=\frac{\overline{f}_{\overline{\zeta}}f_{\zeta}}{(1+|f|^{2})^{2}}\geq 0.

(E.19)

To sum up, we have

\displaystyle(\log S)_{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}\leq 0,

(E.20)

and combining with the boundary estimate, we have a positive lower bound for
S
.
Therefore,
|\nabla\Phi|_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}
is also bounded from below by a positive constant since it’s greater or equal to
S
. We denote

\displaystyle|\nabla\Phi|_{\mathcal{G}}\geq C_{\mathcal{R}}^{g}.

(E.21)

Remark E.3
.

A positive lower bound estimate for
|\nabla\Phi|_{\mathcal{G}}
is crucial in the study of level sets of
\Phi
; without it, we don’t even know the level sets of
\Phi
are submanifolds. It’s interesting to note the following results. In
[
MOZ10
]
, Proposition 4.1, they proved that for the solution
u
of the
p
-Laplace equation
|\nabla u|
strictly increases in the direction of
\nabla u
. In
[
L81
]
, for an extremal map
f:U\rightarrow D
, where
U
is the unit disc in
\mathbb{C}
and
D
is a strongly convex domain with a smooth boundary, it was proved that
f^{\prime}\neq 0
anywhere; as a consequence, for the pluricomplex Green’s function
\Phi
,
\nabla\Phi\neq 0
.

E.3
The Construction of
{\mathcal{Q}}
and Its Relation to
\mathbb{C}
-Convexity

Under the apriori assumption that
\Phi
is strongly
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convex, the gradients of
\Phi
never vanish; then, for any point
{\bf p}
, we can choose a coordinate
(\tau,z)
so that
\Phi_{\tau}\neq 0
,
\Phi_{z}=0
and
{\bf p}=(0,0)
. That’s to say, the plane
\{\tau=0\}
is the complex tangent plane of the level set of
\Phi
at the point
{\bf p}
. In such a coordinate chart, we define

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{b}_{\Phi}}=\Phi_{zz}-2\frac{\Phi_{z}}{\Phi_{\tau}}\Phi_{\tau z}+\left(\frac{\Phi_{z}}{\Phi_{\tau}}\right)^{2}\Phi_{\tau\tau},

(E.22)

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{a}_{\Phi}}=\Phi_{z\overline{z}}-\frac{\Phi_{z}}{\Phi_{\tau}}\Phi_{\tau\overline{z}}-\frac{\Phi_{\overline{z}}}{\Phi_{\overline{\tau}}}\Phi_{z\overline{\tau}}+\left|\frac{\Phi_{z}}{\Phi_{\tau}}\right|^{2}\Phi_{\tau\overline{\tau}}.

(E.23)

Because of the apriori assumption that
\Phi
is strongly
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convex, when this neighborhood is small enough,
{\mathfrak{a}_{\Phi}}\neq 0
and we can define

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}_{\Phi}}=\frac{|{\mathfrak{b}_{\Phi}}|^{2}}{{\mathfrak{a}_{\Phi}}^{2}}.

(E.24)

Actually,
{\mathcal{Q}_{\Phi}}
is invariant under a complex linear change of coordinate, even
{\mathfrak{a}_{\Phi}}
and
{\mathfrak{b}_{\Phi}}
depend on the choice of coordinates.
\Phi
is strongly
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convex is equivalent to that
{\mathfrak{a}}_{\Phi}>0
and
{\mathcal{Q}_{\Phi}}<1
. In Section
E.5
, we will show
\frac{1}{1-{\mathcal{Q}_{\Phi}}}
is subharmonic along each leaf.

We also need to perturb
\Phi
by pluriharmonic quadratic polynomials, which does not change the equation but will alter
{\mathcal{Q}_{\Phi}}
. This gives us the estimate of the robustness of
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convexity of
\Phi
. For a quadratic polynomial
q
, assuming that
\Phi-q
is strongly
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convex and
\partial_{\tau}(\Phi-q)\neq 0
, we replace
\Phi
by
\Phi-q
in (
E.22
) and (
E.23
) and let

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{b}}_{\Phi-q}=(\Phi-q)_{zz}-2\frac{(\Phi-q)_{z}}{(\Phi-q)_{\tau}}(\Phi-q)_{\tau z}+\left(\frac{(\Phi-q)_{z}}{(\Phi-q)_{\tau}}\right)^{2}(\Phi-q)_{\tau\tau},

(E.25)

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{a}}_{\Phi-q}=(\Phi-q)_{{z\overline{z}}}-\frac{(\Phi-q)_{z}}{(\Phi-q)_{\tau}}(\Phi-q)_{\tau{\overline{z}}}-\frac{(\Phi-q)_{\overline{z}}}{(\Phi-q)_{\overline{\tau}}}(\Phi-q)_{z{\overline{\tau}}}+\left|\frac{(\Phi-q)_{z}}{(\Phi-q)_{\tau}}\right|^{2}(\Phi-q)_{{\tau\overline{\tau}}}.

(E.26)

Then we let

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi-q}=\frac{|{\mathfrak{b}}_{\Phi-q}|^{2}}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{\Phi-q}^{2}}.

(E.27)

If
q
is a pluriharmonic function,
\Phi-q
is still a solution to the homogenous complex Monge-Ampère equation, and the subharmonicity of
{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi}
implies the subharmonicity of
{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi-q}
.

E.4
Equations for
{\mathfrak{a}}
and
{\mathfrak{b}}
with a Good Coordinate Choice

At any point
{\bf p}
, because of the assumption that
\Phi
is strongly
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convex, we have

\displaystyle\ker\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}\Phi\oplus\ker\partial\Phi=\mathbb{C}^{2},

(E.28)

so we can choose a coordinate
(\tau,z)
so that
\Phi_{z}=0
and
\Phi_{{\tau\overline{\tau}}}=0
. Then the homogenous complex Monge-Ampère equation implies
\Phi_{z\overline{\tau}}=0
.
We note that with this coordinate the coefficients of
\mathcal{G}
may be very large.

Then, the computations at
{\bf p}
become simple:
(
E.5
) becomes
\Phi_{\tau{\overline{\tau}}}=0
, and (
E.7
) becomes
\Phi_{z{\tau\overline{\tau}}}=0.

For any function
f
defined around
{\bf p}
, we have
f_{\zeta}=f_{\tau}
, and

\displaystyle f_{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}=\left(f_{\tau}-\frac{\Phi_{\tau\overline{z}}}{\Phi_{z\overline{z}}}f_{z}\right)_{\overline{\tau}}=f_{{\tau\overline{\tau}}}-\frac{\Phi_{\tau{\overline{z}}{\overline{\tau}}}}{\Phi_{z\overline{z}}}f_{z}=f_{{\tau\overline{\tau}}}.

(E.29)

In the last equality, we used
\Phi_{z{\tau\overline{\tau}}}=\Phi_{{\overline{z}}{\tau\overline{\tau}}}=0
.

In the following, we discuss the equations satisfied by
{\mathfrak{a}}_{\Phi}
and
{\mathfrak{b}}_{\Phi}
at
{\bf p}
with the coordinate chosen above. We will drop the subindex
\Phi
and just denote them by
{\mathfrak{a}}
and
{\mathfrak{b}}
. We compute
{\mathfrak{a}}_{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}
,
{\mathfrak{b}}_{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}
,
{\mathfrak{a}}_{\zeta}
and
{\mathfrak{b}}_{{\overline{\zeta}}}
and show that they satisfy the following equations:

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{b}}_{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}

\displaystyle=2\frac{{\mathfrak{b}}_{\overline{\zeta}}{\mathfrak{a}}_{\zeta}}{{\mathfrak{a}}},

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{a}}_{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathfrak{b}}_{\overline{\zeta}}{\overline{{\mathfrak{b}}}}_{\zeta}}{{\mathfrak{a}}}+\frac{{\mathfrak{a}}_{\zeta}{\mathfrak{a}}_{\overline{\zeta}}}{{\mathfrak{a}}}.

(E.31)

Using these equations, we show, in Section
E.5
,
\frac{1}{1-{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi}}
is subharmonic along any leaves.

To simplify the computation, let

and
a=\Phi_{{z\overline{z}}}
. By applying
\partial_{z\overline{z}}
and
\partial_{zz}
to (

), we have

\displaystyle b_{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}

\displaystyle=2\frac{b_{\overline{\zeta}}a_{\zeta}}{a},

(E.32)

\displaystyle a_{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{b_{\overline{\zeta}}{\overline{b}}_{\zeta}}{a}+\frac{a_{\overline{\zeta}}a_{\zeta}}{a}.

(E.33)

The computation is the same as that of Section 2 of
[

]
and Appendix A of
[

]
. In the following, we will show, at the point
{\bf p}
,

,

,
{\mathfrak{a}}_{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}=a_{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}
. Thus, (

) and (
E.31
) follow from (
E.32
) and (
E.33
).

Subtracting

from (

), we get

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{a}}_{\zeta}-a_{\zeta}=\left(-\frac{\Phi_{z}}{\Phi_{\tau}}\Phi_{\tau\overline{z}}-\frac{\Phi_{\overline{z}}}{\Phi_{\overline{\tau}}}\Phi_{z\overline{\tau}}+\left|\frac{\Phi_{z}}{\Phi_{\tau}}\right|^{2}\Phi_{\tau\overline{\tau}}\right)_{\zeta}=0,\ \ \ \ \ \ \text{ at }p.

It’s zero because

,

and

.

E.5
Subharmonicity of

In this section, we will show

{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi}

at

We directly apply

\frac{1}{1-{\mathcal{Q}}}
and get

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{1-{\mathcal{Q}}}\right)_{\tau{\overline{\tau}}}=\left[\frac{1}{(1-{\mathcal{Q}})^{2}}{\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}\right]_{{\overline{\tau}}}=\frac{1}{(1-{\mathcal{Q}})^{3}}\left[2{\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}{\mathcal{Q}}_{\overline{\tau}}+{\mathcal{Q}}_{{\tau\overline{\tau}}}(1-{\mathcal{Q}})\right].

(E.40)

To simplify the computation, we introduce a quantity:

\displaystyle\beta={\mathfrak{b}}_{\tau}-2\frac{{\mathfrak{b}}{\mathfrak{a}}_{\tau}}{{\mathfrak{a}}}.

(E.41)

With
\beta
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{{\mathfrak{b}}}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}\right)_{\tau}=\frac{\beta}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}

(E.42)

and

\displaystyle\beta_{\overline{\tau}}=\underbrace{{\mathfrak{b}}_{\tau\overline{\tau}}-2\frac{{\mathfrak{b}}_{\overline{\tau}}{\mathfrak{a}}_{\tau}}{{\mathfrak{a}}}}_{=0,\ \text{ by (\ref{equation:A_cb_Phi})}}\underbrace{-2\frac{{\mathfrak{a}}_{\tau\overline{\tau}}{\mathfrak{b}}}{{\mathfrak{a}}}+2\frac{{\mathfrak{a}}_{\tau}{\mathfrak{a}}_{\overline{\tau}}{\mathfrak{b}}}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}=-2\frac{\overline{{\mathfrak{b}}}_{\tau}{\mathfrak{b}}_{\overline{\tau}}{\mathfrak{b}}}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}}_{\text{by (\ref{equation:A_ca_Phi})}}.

(E.43)

In addition, we observe that (
E.30
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\left(\frac{{\mathfrak{b}}_{\overline{\tau}}}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}\right)_{\tau}=0.

(E.44)

Then

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}=\left(\frac{{\mathfrak{b}}}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}\cdot\overline{{\mathfrak{b}}}\right)_{\tau}=\left(\frac{{\mathfrak{b}}}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}\right)_{\tau}\overline{{\mathfrak{b}}}+\frac{{\mathfrak{b}}}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}\cdot\overline{{\mathfrak{b}}}_{\tau}=\frac{\beta}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}\overline{{\mathfrak{b}}}+\frac{{\mathfrak{b}}}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}\cdot\overline{{\mathfrak{b}}}_{\tau},

(E.45)

and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}{\mathcal{Q}}_{\overline{\tau}}=\left(\frac{\beta}{{\mathfrak{a}}},\frac{\overline{{\mathfrak{b}}}_{\tau}}{{\mathfrak{a}}}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\mathcal{Q}}&\frac{\overline{{\mathfrak{b}}}^{2}}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}\\
&\\
\frac{{\mathfrak{b}}^{2}}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}&{\mathcal{Q}}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\overline{\beta}}{{\mathfrak{a}}}\\
\\
\frac{{\mathfrak{b}}_{\overline{\tau}}}{{\mathfrak{a}}}\end{array}\right).

For
{\mathcal{Q}}_{{\tau\overline{\tau}}}
, we also make it into a quadratic form of
\frac{\beta}{{\mathfrak{a}}}
and
\frac{\overline{{\mathfrak{b}}}_{\tau}}{{\mathfrak{a}}}
.
Applying
\partial_{\overline{\tau}}
to (
E.45
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}_{{\tau\overline{\tau}}}=\left(\beta\cdot\frac{\overline{{\mathfrak{b}}}}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}+{\mathfrak{b}}\cdot\frac{\overline{{\mathfrak{b}}}_{\tau}}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}\right)_{\overline{\tau}}=\beta_{\overline{\tau}}\cdot\frac{\overline{{\mathfrak{b}}}}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}+\beta\cdot\left(\frac{\overline{{\mathfrak{b}}}}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}\right)_{\overline{\tau}}+{\mathfrak{b}}_{\overline{\tau}}\cdot\frac{\overline{{\mathfrak{b}}}_{\tau}}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}+{\mathfrak{b}}\cdot\left(\frac{\overline{{\mathfrak{b}}}_{\tau}}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}\right)_{\overline{\tau}}.

(E.52)

Using the conjugate of (
E.44
), the last term of (
E.52
) is zero; for
\left(\frac{\overline{{\mathfrak{b}}}}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}\right)_{\overline{\tau}}
, we use the conjugate of (
E.42
); for
\beta_{\overline{\tau}}
, we use the equation (
E.43
). Then we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}_{{\tau\overline{\tau}}}=\left(\frac{\beta}{{\mathfrak{a}}},\frac{\overline{{\mathfrak{b}}}_{\tau}}{{\mathfrak{a}}}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\\
&1-2{\mathcal{Q}}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\overline{\beta}}{{\mathfrak{a}}}\\
\\
\frac{{\mathfrak{b}}_{\overline{\tau}}}{{\mathfrak{a}}}\end{array}\right).

Combining (
E.5
) (
E.5
) with (
E.40
), we know

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{1-{\mathcal{Q}}}\right)_{\tau\overline{\tau}}=\frac{1}{(1-{\mathcal{Q}})^{3}}\left(\frac{\beta}{{\mathfrak{a}}},\frac{\overline{{\mathfrak{b}}}_{\tau}}{{\mathfrak{a}}}\right)M\left(\frac{\overline{\beta}}{{\mathfrak{a}}},\frac{{\mathfrak{b}}_{\overline{\tau}}}{{\mathfrak{a}}}\right)^{\prime},

(E.58)

where
M
is a
2\times 2
matrix:

\displaystyle M=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1+{\mathcal{Q}}&2\frac{\overline{{\mathfrak{b}}}^{2}}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}\\
\\
2\frac{{\mathfrak{b}}^{2}}{{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}&1-{\mathcal{Q}}+2{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}\end{array}\right)

Direct computation gives

\displaystyle\det(M)

\displaystyle=(1-{\mathcal{Q}})^{2}(1+2{\mathcal{Q}}),

(E.62)

\displaystyle\text{tr}(M)

\displaystyle=2+2{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}.

(E.63)

By the definition of
{\mathcal{Q}}
, it’s always nonnegative, so
1+2{\mathcal{Q}}>0
.
Therefore,
\left(\frac{1}{1-{\mathcal{Q}}}\right)_{\tau\overline{\tau}}\geq 0
, at
{\bf p}
, providing
{\mathcal{Q}}<1
.
We can choose
{\bf p}
to be any point in
\mathcal{R}
, so
{\mathcal{Q}}
, as a globally defined function in
\mathcal{R}
, satisfies

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{1-{\mathcal{Q}}}\right)_{{\zeta{\overline{\zeta}}}}\geq 0,

(E.64)

along a leaf, providing
{\mathcal{Q}}<1
.
Then,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-{\mathcal{Q}}}\leq\max_{\partial_{\mathcal{R}}}\frac{1}{1-{\mathcal{Q}}}\ \ \ \ \ \ \text{ in }\mathcal{R}\text{, \ providing }{\mathcal{Q}}<1\text{ in }\overline{\mathcal{R}}.

(E.65)

This is because the maximum of
\frac{1}{1-{\mathcal{Q}}}
cannot be achieved at any interior point in
\mathcal{R}
. Suppose
\frac{1}{1-{\mathcal{Q}}}
achieves its maximum at
{\bf p}\in\mathcal{R}
.
Let
\mathcal{L}
be a local leaf passing
{\bf p}
. Then the restriction of
\frac{1}{1-{\mathcal{Q}}}
on
\mathcal{L}
also achieves a local maximum at
{\bf p}
, but this contradicts with the strong maximum principle. It follows that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}\leq\max_{\partial_{\mathcal{R}}}{\mathcal{Q}}\ \ \ \ \ \ \text{ in }\mathcal{R}\text{, \ providing }{\mathcal{Q}}<1\text{ in }\overline{\mathcal{R}}.

(E.66)

E.6
Convexity Estimate

In this section we will show for a small constant
\epsilon_{0}
,
\Phi-q
is strongly
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convex for any
q\in{\mathscr{Q}}_{\epsilon_{0}}
. The constant
\epsilon_{0}
will depend on the geometry of
\mathcal{R}
. In this section, we only do unitary change of coordinate, so we assume the metric is
\sum_{i}dz^{i}\otimes\overline{dz^{i}}
.

Firstly, we need to choose
\epsilon_{0}
small enough, so that it’s smaller than the lower bound estimate for
|\nabla\Phi|_{\mathcal{G}}
in Section
E.2
: we choose

\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}<C_{\mathcal{R}}^{g}.

(E.67)

This implies
\nabla(\Phi-q)
is non-zero for
q\in{\mathscr{Q}}_{\epsilon_{0}}
and the level sets of
\Phi
are smooth. Then we make
\epsilon_{0}
smaller so that

\displaystyle\Phi-q\text{ is strongly $\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}$-convex},\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{R}\text{ for any }q\in{\mathscr{Q}}_{\epsilon_{0}}.

(E.68)

Then using a continuity argument, we will prove

\Phi-q
is actually strongly
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convex in
\mathcal{R}
for any
q\in{\mathscr{Q}}_{\epsilon_{0}}
.
Let

\displaystyle I_{\epsilon_{0}}=\left\{\epsilon\in[0,\epsilon_{0}]|\text{ for any }q\in{\mathscr{Q}}_{\epsilon},\Phi-q\text{ is strongly $\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}$-convex}\right\}.

(E.69)

We will show
I_{\epsilon_{0}}
is non-empty, open and right-closed; thus,
I_{\epsilon_{0}}=[0,\epsilon_{0}]
.

It’s obvious that
I_{\epsilon_{0}}
is non-empty since it contains
0
; this is because of the apriori assumption. The openness can also be proved easily: if
\Phi-q_{1}
is strongly
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convex, then we have

{\mathfrak{a}}_{\Phi-q_{1}}>0
; so,
{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi-q_{1}-q_{2}}
is close to
{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi-q_{1}}
for
q_{2}\in{\mathscr{Q}}_{\epsilon_{2}}
with small enough
\epsilon_{2}
.

The right-closeness is more complicated, and we need to use the subharmonicity of
\frac{1}{1-{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi-q}}
. Here, we notice that to derive (
E.66
) we only need that
\Phi
is a solution to the homogenous complex Monge-Ampère equation and that
\Phi
is strongly
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convex. For a pluriharmonic function
q
,
\Phi-q
is still the solution to the homogenous complex Monge-Ampère equation; so, if
\Phi-q
is also strongly
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convex, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi-q}\leq\max_{\partial_{\mathcal{R}}}{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi-q}\ \ \ \ \ \ \text{ in }\mathcal{R}.

(E.70)

Suppose
[0,\epsilon_{1})\subset I_{\epsilon_{0}}
and
\epsilon_{1}\notin I_{\epsilon_{0}}
. Then we will derive a contradiction. According to the definition of
I_{\epsilon_{0}}
(
E.69
), there is a
k\in{\mathscr{Q}}_{\epsilon_{1}}
so that
\Phi-k
is not strongly
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convex. This means we can find a point
{\bf p}\in\mathcal{R}
so that if we choose a coordinate
(\tau,z)
around
{\bf p}
such that
(\Phi-k)_{z}({\bf p})=0
, then either

\displaystyle(\Phi-k)_{{z\overline{z}}}({\bf p})=\Phi_{{z\overline{z}}}({\bf p})=0

(E.71)

or

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi-k}({\bf p})=1.

(E.72)

In the following we will argue none of these can happen.

Let
h
be an element of
{\mathscr{Q}}_{\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{2}}
. For
\theta\in(0,1]
we consider the following polynomials

\displaystyle k^{\theta}=(1-\theta)k-\theta h\ \ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \ k_{\theta}=(1-\theta)k+\theta h.

(E.73)

They all belong to the interior of
{\mathscr{Q}}_{\epsilon_{1}}
. So

\displaystyle\Phi^{\theta}=\Phi-k^{\theta}\ \ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \ \Phi_{\theta}=\Phi-k_{\theta}

(E.74)

are both strongly
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convex.
Then using a continuity argument, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi^{\theta}},{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi_{\theta}}\leq\max_{q\in{\mathscr{Q}}_{\epsilon_{0}}}\max_{\partial\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi-q}<1\ \ \ \ \ \ \text{for }\theta\in(0,1].

(E.75)

Because of (
E.67
), we know the gradient of
\Phi-k
does not vanish anywhere. We denote
(\Phi-k)_{\tau}({\bf p})=\nu
; then
\nu>0
. The gradients of
\Phi^{\theta}
and
\Phi_{\theta}
are both close to the gradient of
\Phi-k
, providing
\theta
very small. We choose
h
so that
\nabla h({\bf p})=0
; then, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\Phi^{\theta}=\partial_{\tau}\Phi_{\theta}=(\Phi-k)_{\tau}+\theta(k+h)_{\tau}=\nu+\theta k_{\tau},

(E.76)

\displaystyle\partial_{z}\Phi^{\theta}=\partial_{z}\Phi_{\theta}=(\Phi-k)_{z}+\theta(k+h)_{z}=\theta k_{z},

(E.77)

at the point
{\bf p}
.
Let

\displaystyle\mathfrak{v}_{\theta}=\frac{\theta k_{z}({\bf p})}{\nu+\theta k_{\tau}({\bf p})};

(E.78)

then
\partial_{z}-\mathfrak{v}_{\theta}\partial_{\tau}
is a complex tangential vector to the level sets of
\Phi-k
, with
\mathfrak{v}_{\theta}=O(\theta)
. We compute
{\mathcal{Q}}
in the complex tangential direction and get

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi^{\theta}}({\bf p})=\left|\frac{K_{\theta}+\theta(h_{zz}+2\mathfrak{v}_{\theta}h_{z\tau}+(\mathfrak{v}_{\theta})^{2}h_{\tau\tau})}{\Phi_{{z\overline{z}}}-\mathfrak{v}_{\theta}\Phi_{\tau{\overline{z}}}-\overline{\mathfrak{v}_{\theta}}\Phi_{z{\overline{\tau}}}+|\mathfrak{v}_{\theta}|^{2}\Phi_{{\tau\overline{\tau}}}}\right|^{2},

(E.79)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi^{\theta}}({\bf p})=\left|\frac{K_{\theta}-\theta(h_{zz}+2\mathfrak{v}_{\theta}h_{z\tau}+(\mathfrak{v}_{\theta})^{2}h_{\tau\tau})}{\Phi_{{z\overline{z}}}-\mathfrak{v}_{\theta}\Phi_{\tau{\overline{z}}}-\overline{\mathfrak{v}_{\theta}}\Phi_{z{\overline{\tau}}}+|\mathfrak{v}_{\theta}|^{2}\Phi_{{\tau\overline{\tau}}}}\right|^{2},

(E.80)

where we denote

\displaystyle(\Phi-(1-\theta)k)_{zz}-2(\Phi-(1-\theta)k)_{z\tau}\mathfrak{v}_{\theta}+(\Phi-(1-\theta)k)_{\tau\tau}(\mathfrak{v}_{\theta})^{2}=K_{\theta}.

(E.81)

Here
k
and
h
don’t appear in the denominator because they are both pluriharmonic. Equations above imply

\displaystyle\lim_{\theta\rightarrow 0}{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi^{\theta}}=\lim_{\theta\rightarrow 0}{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi^{\theta}}={\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi-k};

(E.82)

therefore, (
E.72
) is impossible.

(
E.75
) implies

\displaystyle\sqrt{{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi^{\theta}}({\bf p})}+\sqrt{{\mathcal{Q}}_{\Phi_{\theta}}({\bf p})}\leq 2,\ \ \ \ \ \ \text{ for }\theta\in(0,1].

(E.83)

The triangle inequality implies

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\theta(h_{zz}+2\mathfrak{v}_{\theta}h_{z\tau}+(\mathfrak{v}_{\theta})^{2}h_{\tau\tau})}{\Phi_{{z\overline{z}}}-\mathfrak{v}_{\theta}\Phi_{\tau{\overline{z}}}-\overline{\mathfrak{v}_{\theta}}\Phi_{z{\overline{\tau}}}+|\mathfrak{v}_{\theta}|^{2}\Phi_{{\tau\overline{\tau}}}}\right|\leq 1\text{\ \ \ \ \ at ${\bf p}$}.

(E.84)

If (
E.71
) is true, then the subharmonicity of
\Phi
implies

\displaystyle\Phi_{z{\overline{\tau}}}=\Phi_{\tau{\overline{z}}}=0,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{ at }{\bf p}.

(E.85)

So we get

\displaystyle\frac{\theta|h_{zz}({\bf p})|+O(\theta^{2})}{O(\theta^{2})}\leq 2

(E.86)

when
\theta
is small enough. This is a contradiction since we can choose
h
so that
h_{zz}({\bf p})\neq 0

Here we used
\Phi_{{z\overline{z}}}=\Phi_{z{\overline{\tau}}}=\Phi_{\tau{\overline{z}}}=0
at
{\bf p}
and
\mathfrak{v}_{\theta}=O(\theta)
.

So we have proved that
\Phi-k
is strongly
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convex, and as a consequence,
I_{\epsilon_{0}}
is right-closed. Therefore,
I_{\epsilon_{0}}=[0,\epsilon_{0}]
. This says the robustness of
\mathfrak{q}\mathbb{C}
-convexity of
\Phi
is greater than
\epsilon_{0}
.
