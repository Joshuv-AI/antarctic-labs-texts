Quadratic embedding constants of path graphs
Wojciech Młotkowski

Abstract
. We characterize positive definiteness for some family of matrices. As an application, we derive the explicit value of the quadratic embedding constants of the path graphs.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 05C50; Secondary 05C12, 15A15

1.
Introduction

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph, with
V
as the set of vertices and
E
as the set of edges, i.e. two-element subsets of
V
.
We assume that
G
is
connected
, which means that for every
x,y\in V
there exists a
walk
connecting
x
and
y
:
a finite sequence
x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in V
such that
n\geq 0
,
x_{0}=x,x_{n}=y
and
\{x_{i-1},x_{i}\}\in E
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
The distance
d(x,y)
is defined as the smallest possible length
n
of such a walk.
For some connected graphs the
distance matrix

(d(x,y))_{x,y\in V}
is conditionally negative definite,
equivalently, there exists a function
\phi
which maps
V
into a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}

and satisfies

d(x,y)=\|\phi(x)-\phi(y)\|^{2}

for all
x,y\in V
. This motivated the authors of
[
5
]
to introduce and study the
quadratic embedding constant
,
defined as

(1)

\mathrm{QEC}(G):=\sup\left\{\sum_{x,y\in V}d(x,y)f(x)f(y):f\in\mathcal{F}_{0,1}(V)\right\},

where
\mathcal{F}_{0,1}(V)
denotes the set of all finitely supported functions
f:V\to\mathbb{R}

satisfying
\sum_{x\in V}f(x)=0
and
\sum_{x\in V}f(x)^{2}=1
.
In particular, the distance matrix
(d(x,y))_{x,y\in V}
is conditionally negative definite if and only if
\mathrm{QEC}(G)\leq 0
.
Several examples and properties were furnished in
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
.
Applying the min-max theorem and the Perron-Frobenius theorem, one can observe
that if
V
is finite and
\lambda_{1}(G)\geq\lambda_{2}(G)\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{|V|}(G)

are the eigenvalues of the distance matrix of
G
, then

\lambda_{2}(G)\leq\mathrm{QEC}(G)<\lambda_{1}(G).

In this paper we will study finite
path graphs
, i.e. graphs of the form
P_{n}:=(V,E)
,
where

V:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\},\qquad E:=\big\{\{1,2\},\{2,3\},\ldots,\{n-1,n\}\big\}.

The eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}(P_{n})
of the distance matrix of
P_{n}
were found in
[
7
]
.
In particular,

\lambda_{2}(P_{n})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{-1}{1+\cos(\pi/n)}&\hbox{if $n$ is even,}\\
\displaystyle\frac{-1}{1-\cos\theta^{*}}&\hbox{if $n$ is odd,}\end{array}\right.

where
\theta^{*}
is the maximal solution of the equation:

\tan(\theta/2)\tan(n\theta/2)=-1/n,\qquad\theta\in(0,\pi).

It was observed in
[
3
, Proposition 5.4]
that
\mathrm{QEC}(P_{n})
is equal to the minimal
t

such that the matrix

(2)

\left[2\min\{i,j\}+t+t\cdot\delta_{i,j}\right]_{i,j=1}^{n-1}

is positive definite, i.e. all the eigenvalues of this matrix are nonnegative.
This led to inequality:
\mathrm{QEC}(P_{n})\leq-1/2
, see
[
3
, Theorem 5.6]
.
Our aim is to provide the exact value:

Theorem 1.1
.

For
n\geq 2
we have

(3)

\mathrm{QEC}(P_{n})=\frac{-1}{1+\cos(\pi/n)}.

Consequently, if
n
is even then
\lambda_{2}(P_{n})=\mathrm{QEC}(P_{n})

and if
n
is odd then
\lambda_{2}(P_{n})<\mathrm{QEC}(P_{n})
.

As an immediate consequence we obtain the quadratic embedding constant for
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{Z}

regarded as infinite path graphs, with edges
\{i,i+1\}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
or
i\in\mathbb{Z}
,
c.f.
[
3
, Theorem 5.7]
.

Corollary 1.2
.

\mathrm{QEC}(\mathbb{N})=\mathrm{QEC}(\mathbb{Z})=-1/2
.

The paper is organized as follows.
First we examine a family of auxiliary polynomials
S_{n}(a,b;t)
,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,
n\geq 0
.
Section
3
is devoted to the study of a family
A_{n}(s,t)
of matrices,
a two-parameter version of (
2
).
We provide formula for
\det A_{n}(s,t)
,
n<\infty
, in terms of polynomials
S_{n}(a,b;t)
,
and characterize these
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
for which
A_{n}(s,t)
,
1\leq n\leq\infty
, is positive definite.
Finally we prove Theorem
1.1
.

2.
A family of polynomials

Now we are going to study a family of polynomials defined
by the following recurrence:
S_{0}(a,b;t):=1
,
S_{1}(a,b;t):=at+b
and

(4)

S_{n}(a,b;t)=(1+2t)S_{n-1}(a,b;t)-t^{2}S_{n-2}(a,b;t)

for
n\geq 2
,
a,b,t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For
n\geq 0
we have
\deg S_{n}(a,b;t)\leq n
and the coefficient at
t^{n}

in
S_{n}(a,b;t)
is equal to
an-n+1
. Moreover,

(5)

S_{n}(a,b;t)=(at+b)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{2n-k-1}{k}t^{k}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\binom{2n-k-3}{k}t^{k+2}\\
=\frac{1}{2^{n+1}\sqrt{1+4t}}\left[\left(2b-1+2(a-1)t+\sqrt{1+4t}\right)\left(1+2t+\sqrt{1+4t}\right)^{n}\right.\\
\left.-\left(2b-1+2(a-1)t-\sqrt{1+4t}\right)\left(1+2t-\sqrt{1+4t}\right)^{n}\right].

The former formula holds for
n\geq 1
, while the latter for
n\geq 0
and
t\neq-1/4
.
For
t=-1/4
,
n\geq 0
we have

(6)

S_{n}(a,b;-1/4)=\frac{1}{4^{n}}\left(4nb-na-n+1\right).

Proof.

The first statement can be easily proved by induction.
Now define:

(7)

W_{n}(t):=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{2n-k+1}{k}t^{k}

(see
A172431
in OEIS
[
6
]
).
Then
W_{0}(t)=1
,
W_{1}(t)=2t+1
, and one can check that

W_{n}(t)=(1+2t)W_{n-1}(t)-t^{2}W_{n-2}(t)

for
n\geq 2
, so that
W_{n}(t)=S_{n}(2,1;t)
. Putting
Q_{0}(t):=1
and

Q_{n}(t):=(at+b)W_{n-1}(t)-t^{2}W_{n-2}(t)

for
n\geq 1
(
W_{-1}(t):=0
) we have
Q_{1}(t)=at+b
and
Q_{n}(t)=(1+2t)Q_{n-1}(t)-t^{2}Q_{n-2}(t)

for
n\geq 1
, so
Q_{n}(t)
coincides with
S_{n}(a,b;t)
and the first formula in (
5
) holds.
Moreover, it is easy to verify by induction that
W_{n}(-1/4)=(n+1)/4^{n}
, which leads to (
6
).

For
n\geq 0
,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,
t\neq-1/4
put

\phantom{u}T_{n}(t):=\frac{1}{2^{n+1}\sqrt{1+4t}}\left[\left(2b-1+2(a-1)t+\sqrt{1+4t}\right)\left(1+2t+\sqrt{1+4t}\right)^{n}\right.\\
\left.-\left(2b-1+2(a-1)t-\sqrt{1+4t}\right)\left(1+2t-\sqrt{1+4t}\right)^{n}\right].\phantom{u}

Then
T_{0}(t)=1
,
T_{1}(t)=at+b
and from the identity

\left(1+2t\pm\sqrt{1+4t}\right)^{2}=2(1+2t)\left(1+2t\pm\sqrt{1+4t}\right)-4t^{2}

we have

T_{n}(t)=(1+2t)T_{n-1}(t)-t^{2}T_{n-2}(t)

for
n\geq 2
, which implies that
T_{n}(t)=S_{n}(a,b;t)
for
n\geq 0
,
t\neq-1/4
.
∎

For later use, we record the following identities:

Proposition 2.2
.

For
W_{n}(t)
defined in (
7
) we have:

(8)

W_{n}(t)=S_{n}(2,1;t)=S_{n}(1,t+1;t).

Moreover,

(9)

W_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{2^{n+1}\sqrt{1+4t}}\left[\left(1+2t+\sqrt{1+4t}\right)^{n+1}-\left(1+2t-\sqrt{1+4t}\right)^{n+1}\right].

Proof.

The former equality in (
8
) was noted in the previous proof,
the latter, as well as (
9
), is a consequence of (
5
).
∎

In some particular cases we are able to find the roots of
S_{n}(a,b;t)
.

Proposition 2.3
.

For
n\geq 1
we have

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}S_{n}(2,1;t)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\left(t+\frac{1}{2+2\cos\left(\frac{k\pi}{n+1}\right)}\right),

(11)

\displaystyle S_{n}(1,1/2;t)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\left(t+\frac{1}{2+2\cos\big(\frac{(2k-1)\pi}{2n}\big)}\right),

(12)

\displaystyle S_{n}(1,1;t)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\left(t+\frac{1}{2+2\cos\left(\frac{2k\pi}{2n+1}\right)}\right),

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n+1}S_{n}(3,1;t)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\left(t+\frac{1}{2+2\cos\big(\frac{(2k-1)\pi}{2n+1}\big)}\right).

Proof.

First we note that in view of the first part of Proposition
2.1
,
if
a\geq 1
then
\deg S_{n}(a,b;t)=n
and the leading term is
(an-n+1)t^{n}
.

Fix
a,b,t\in\mathbb{R}
, with
1+4t<0
, and put

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle:=2b-1+2(a-1)t+\sqrt{1+4t},

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle:=1+2t+\sqrt{1+4t},

with
\mathrm{Im}(w)>0
,
\mathrm{Im}(z)>0
,
\alpha:=\mathrm{arg}(w)
,
\beta:=\mathrm{arg}(z)
,
0<\alpha,\beta<\pi
.
Then

\cos\beta=\frac{1+2t}{-2t}\qquad\hbox{and hence}\qquad t=\frac{-1}{2+2\cos\beta}.

In view of (
5
), we have
S_{n}(a,b;t)=0
if and only if
w\cdot z^{n}=\overline{w}\cdot\overline{z}^{n}
,
equivalently,
\alpha+n\beta=k\pi
for some
k=1,2,\ldots,n
.
Now we consider four cases.

1. If
a=2
,
b=1
then
w=z
,
\alpha=\beta
and therefore
\beta=k\pi/(n+1)
.

2. If
a=1
,
b=1/2
then
\alpha=\pi/2
and then
\beta=(2k-1)\pi/(2n)
.

3. If
a=b=1
then

\cos\alpha=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-4t}}=\sqrt{\frac{1+\cos\beta}{2}}=\cos(\beta/2),

which implies
\alpha=\beta/2
and
\beta=2k\pi/(2n+1)
.

4. Finally, if
a=3
,
b=1
then

\phantom{aaa}\cos\alpha=\frac{1+4t}{\sqrt{4t(1+4t)}}=-\sqrt{\frac{1+4t}{4t}}=-\sqrt{\frac{1-\cos\beta}{2}}\\
=-\sin(\beta/2)=\cos(\pi/2+\beta/2),\phantom{aaa}

therefore
\alpha=\pi/2+\beta/2
and, consequently,
\beta=(2k-1)\pi/(2n+1)
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

For
n\geq 1
,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
we have

(14)

S_{n}(2,1;t)\cdot S_{n}(a,b;t)-S_{n-1}(2,1;t)\cdot S_{n+1}(a,b;t)=t^{2n}.

Proof.

Putting

u_{\pm}:=2b-1+2(a-1)t\pm\sqrt{1+4t},\qquad v_{\pm}:=1+2t\pm\sqrt{1+4t}

we have

\phantom{uu}\left(v_{+}^{n+1}-v_{-}^{n+1}\right)\left(u_{+}v_{+}^{n}-u_{-}v_{-}^{n}\right)-\left(v_{+}^{n}-v_{-}^{n}\right)\left(u_{+}v_{+}^{n+1}-u_{-}v_{-}^{n+1}\right)\\
=(u_{+}-u_{-})(v_{+}-v_{-})(v_{+}v_{-})^{n}=4(1+4t)\left(4t^{2}\right)^{n},\phantom{uu}

and, by (
5
), the formula follows.
∎

Define

(15)

t_{n}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\infty&\hbox{if $n=1$,}\\
\displaystyle\frac{-1}{2+2\cos(\pi/n)}&\hbox{if $n\geq 2$.}\end{array}\right.

Lemma 2.5
.

If
t_{n}<t<t_{n+1}
then
S_{n-1}(2,1;t)>0
,
S_{n}(2,1;t)<0

and if
t>t_{n+1}
then
S_{n-1}(2,1;t)>0
,
S_{n}(2,1;t)>0
.

Proof.

First note that the function
\alpha\mapsto-1/(2+2\cos\alpha)
is decreasing with
\alpha\in(0,\pi)
.
Therefore the statement is a consequence of (
10
), because for
n\geq 2
we have

\frac{-1}{2+2\cos\big(2\pi/(n+1)\big)}<\frac{-1}{2+2\cos\big(\pi/n\big)}<\frac{-1}{2+2\cos\big(\pi/(n+1)\big)}.

∎

Now we collect properties of polynomials of the form
S_{n}(1,s+1;t)
, which will be applied in the next section.

Proposition 2.6
.

For
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
n\geq 1
, we have

(16)

\phantom{uu}S_{n}(1,s+1;t)=S_{n}(1,1;t)+s\cdot S_{n-1}(2,1;t)\\
=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{2n-k}{k}t^{k}+s\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{2n-1-k}{k}t^{k}\\
=\frac{1}{2^{n+1}\sqrt{1+4t}}\left[\left(1+2s+\sqrt{1+4t}\right)\left(1+2t+\sqrt{1+4t}\right)^{n}\right.\\
\left.-\left(1+2s-\sqrt{1+4t}\right)\left(1+2t-\sqrt{1+4t}\right)^{n}\right].\phantom{uu}

The latter formula is valid for
t\neq-1/4
, while

(17)

S_{n}(1,s+1;-1/4)=\frac{4ns+2n+1}{4^{n}}.

Proof.

The first equality in (
16
) can be verified by induction: putting

P_{n}(t):=S_{n}(1,1;t)+s\cdot S_{n-1}(2,1;t)
we have
P_{0}(t)=1
,
P_{1}(t)=t+1+s

and
P_{n}(t)=(1+2t)P_{n-1}(t)-t^{2}P_{n-2}(t)
for
n\geq 2
, consequently

P_{n}(t)=S_{n}(1,s+1;t)
for all
n\geq 0
. Now (
16
) and (
17
)
are consequences of (
5
) and (
6
).
∎

3.
A family of matrices

Define a family of matrices

(18)

A_{n}(s,t):=\left[\min\{i,j\}+s+t\delta_{i,j}\right]_{i,j=1}^{n},

where
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
,
1\leq n\leq\infty
.
These matrices seem interesting on their own, for the sake of Theorem
1.1

we are particularly interested in the case
s=t
.
We are going to study their determinants and positive definiteness.
Note that if
s_{1}\leq s_{2}
,
t_{1}\leq t_{2}
,
n_{1}\geq n_{2}
and
A_{n_{1}}(s_{1},t_{1})
is positive definite then so is
A_{n_{2}}(s_{2},t_{2})
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(19)

\det A_{n}(s,t)=S_{n}(1,s+1;t)=S_{n}(1,1;t)+s\cdot S_{n-1}(2,1;t).

With the notation of (
15
), the matrix
A_{n}(s,t)
is positive definite if and only if

(20)

t>t_{n}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad S_{n}(1,s+1;t)\geq 0.

Proof.

It is easy to verify (
19
) for
n=1,2
.
Now assume that
n\geq 3
and let
\mathbf{k}_{j}
denote the
j
th column of
A_{n}(s,t)
. Then

\det A_{n}(s,t)=\det(\mathbf{k}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{k}_{n})=\det(\mathbf{k}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{k}_{n-1},\mathbf{k}_{n}-\mathbf{k}_{n-1})

and, denoting the transposition by “
\mathrm{T}
”, we have

\mathbf{k}_{n}-\mathbf{k}_{n-1}=(0,\ldots,0,-t,1+t)^{\mathrm{T}}.

Expanding the determinant along the last column
\mathbf{k}_{n}-\mathbf{k}_{n-1}
,
we get

\det A_{n}(s,t)=(1+t)\det A_{n-1}(s,t)+t\det B,

where

B=\left(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{n-2},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{n-1}+\eta\right),

\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{j}
is the
j
th column of
A_{n-1}(s,t)
and
\eta:=(0,\ldots,0,-t)^{\mathrm{T}}
.
This yields

\det B=\det A_{n-1}(s,t)-t\det A_{n-2}(s,t),

and, consequently,

\det A_{n}(s,t)=(1+2t)\det A_{n-1}(s,t)-t^{2}\det A_{n-2}(s,t),

which completes the proof of (
19
).

Fix
t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
and put

\ell_{k}(s):=\det A_{k}(s,t^{\prime}):=b_{k}+s\cdot a_{k}.

Then, by Lemma
2.4
, we have

(21)

a_{k}b_{k-1}-a_{k-1}b_{k}=(t^{\prime})^{2k-2}.

With the notation of (
15
), if
t_{k-1}<t^{\prime}\leq t_{k}
then
a_{k-1}>0
,
a_{k}\leq 0
.
Consequently, the lines
\ell_{k-1},\ell_{k}
cross at some point
(s^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, i.e.

\ell_{k-1}(s^{\prime})=\ell_{k}(s^{\prime})=y^{\prime}
, and, by (
21
),

y^{\prime}=\frac{a_{k}b_{k-1}-a_{k-1}b_{k}}{a_{k}-a_{k-1}}<0.

This implies, that for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
we have either
\ell_{k-1}(s)<0
or
\ell_{k}(s)<0
,
therefore the matrix
A_{n}(s,t^{\prime})
is not positive definite for
n\geq k
.

Now, if we assume that
t^{\prime}>t_{n}
then
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}>0
.
Defining
s_{k}
by
\ell_{k}(s_{k})=0
, we have
s_{1}\leq s_{2}\leq\ldots\leq s_{n}
, by (
21
).
Therefore, if
\det A_{n}(s,t^{\prime})>0
then
s>s_{n}
,
\det A_{k}(s,t^{\prime})>0
for all
k\leq n
,
and hence
A_{n}(s,t^{\prime})
is positive definite. If
\det A_{n}(s,t^{\prime})=0

then
A_{n}(s,t^{\prime})
is positive definite as pointwise limit of positive definite matrices.
The proof is complete.
∎

Note, that in particular,

(22)

\det A_{n}(t,t)=S_{n}(1,t+1;t)=S_{n}(2,1)=W_{n}(t).

Since
S_{n}(1,s_{0}+1;t)
is the characteristic polynomial of a real symmetric matrix, we have

Corollary 3.2
.

For every fixed
s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
n\geq 1
, the polynomial
S_{n}(1,s_{0}+1;t)
has only real roots.

Now we consider some particular cases.

Theorem 3.3
.

We have the following:

•

The matrix
A_{n}(t,t)
is positive definite if and only if

t\geq\frac{-1}{2+2\cos\big(\pi/(n+1)\big)}.

•

The matrix
A_{n}(-1/2,t)
is positive definite if and only if

t\geq\frac{-1}{2+2\cos\big(\pi/(2n)\big)}.

•

The matrix
A_{n}(0,t)
is positive definite if and only if

t\geq\frac{-1}{2+2\cos\big(2\pi/(2n+1)\big)}.

•

The matrix
A_{n}(2t,t)
is positive definite if and only if

t\geq\frac{-1}{2+2\cos\big(\pi/(2n+1)\big)}.

Proof.

First note that by (
5
) we have
S_{n}(1,2t+1;t)=S_{n}(3,1;t)
.
It is easy to check the statements for
n=1
.
For
0<k<m
put

t^{k}_{m}:=\frac{-1}{2+2\cos(k\pi/m)}.

Then, for
n>1
, we have

t^{2}_{n+1}<t^{1}_{n}<t^{1}_{n+1}
,

t^{3}_{2n}<t^{1}_{n}<t^{1}_{2n}
,

t^{4}_{2n+1}<t^{1}_{n}<t^{2}_{2n+1}
,

t^{3}_{2n+1}<t^{1}_{n}<t^{1}_{2n+1}
, and
the statements follow from Theorem
3.1
and
Proposition
2.3
.
∎

Now we are able to describe the case
n=\infty
.

Theorem 3.4
.

The infinite matrix
A_{\infty}(s,t)
is positive definite if and only if

1+4t\geq 0\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad 1+2s+\sqrt{1+4t}\geq 0.

Proof.

If
1+4t\geq 0
and
1+2s+\sqrt{1+4t}\geq 0
then all the matrices
A_{n}(s,t)
,
1\leq n<\infty
, are positive definite
by (
19
), (
16
)
and (
17
), hence so is
A_{\infty}(s,t)
.

Now assume that
A_{\infty}(s,t)
is positive definite.
Then
t>-1/\big(2+2\cos(\pi/n)\big)
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, which implies
t\geq-1/4
.
If
t=-1/4
then
s\geq-1/2
in view of (
17
).
If
1+4t>0
then, putting

q:=\frac{1+2t-\sqrt{1+4t}}{1+2t+\sqrt{1+4t}},

we have
0<q<1
and, in view of the last formula in (
16
), the inequality

1+2s+\sqrt{1+4t}\geq\left(1-2s+\sqrt{1+4t}\right)q^{n}

holds for every
n\geq 1
. This implies that
1+2s+\sqrt{1+4t}\geq 0
.
∎

4.
Path graphs

Now we are ready to prove the main result of this paper.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

In view of

Proposition 4.1

For

\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{n,i}=0,\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{n,i}^{2}=\frac{n}{2}

(25)

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|i-j|x_{n,i}\cdot x_{n,j}=\frac{-1}{1+\cos(\pi/n)}\cdot\frac{n}{2}.

Proof.

Denoting by “
\mathbf{i}
” the imaginary unit, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}\exp\left(\frac{2i-1}{2n}\pi\mathbf{i}\right)=-\exp\left(\pi\mathbf{i}/(2n)\right)\frac{1+(-1)^{n}}{1+\exp\left(\pi\mathbf{i}/n\right)}=\frac{-1-(-1)^{n}}{2\cos\big(\pi/(2n)\big)}.

Taking the imaginary part we get the first equation in (
24

Similarly, since

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(\frac{(2i-1)\pi}{n}\mathbf{i}\right)=0,

we get

2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{(2i-1)\pi}{2n}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[1-\cos\left(\frac{(2i-1)\pi}{n}\right)\right]=n,

which completes the proof of (
24

Now we will prove (
25
). For
1\leq k<n
we have

\phantom{uuu}2\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}\sin\left(\frac{2i-1}{2n}\pi\right)\sin\left(\frac{2i+2k-1}{2n}\pi\right)\\
=\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}\left[\cos(k\pi/n)-\cos\big((2i+k-1)\pi/n\big)\right]\\
=(n-k)\cos(k\pi/n)+\frac{\sin(k\pi/n)}{\sin(\pi/n)}.\phantom{uuu}

Therefore

(26)

\phantom{uuu}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|i-j|x_{n,i}\cdot x_{n,j}=2\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}k\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}x_{n,i}\cdot x_{n,i+k}\\
=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}k(n-k)\cos(k\pi/n)+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}\frac{k\sin(k\pi/n)}{\sin(\pi/n)}.\phantom{uuu}

Now applying elementary formulas:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}kq^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{q(1-q^{n})-nq^{n}(1-q)}{(1-q)^{2}},

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}k(n-k)q^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{nq(1-q)(1+q^{n})-q(1+q)(1-q^{n})}{(1-q)^{3}}

to
q:=-\exp(\pi\mathbf{i}/n)

q^{n}=-(-1)^{n}

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}k(-1)^{k}\exp(k\pi\mathbf{i}/n)=\frac{-1-(-1)^{n}+(-1)^{n}n\big(\exp(-\pi\mathbf{i}/n)+1\big)}{4\cos^{2}\big(\pi/(2n)\big)},

\phantom{uuuu}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}k(n-k)(-1)^{k}\exp(k\pi\mathbf{i}/n)\\
=\frac{2n\cos\big(\pi/(2n)\big)\big((-1)^{n}-1\big)+2\sin\big(\pi/(2n)\big)\big(1+(-1)^{n}\big)\mathbf{i}}{8\cos^{3}\big(\pi/(2n)\big)}.\phantom{uuuu}

Consequently,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}k\sin(k\pi/n)}{\sin(\pi/n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{-(-1)^{n}n}{4\cos^{2}(\pi/(2n))},

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}k(n-k)\cos(k\pi/n)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left((-1)^{n}-1\right)n}{4\cos^{2}(\pi/(2n))},

which, together with (
26
