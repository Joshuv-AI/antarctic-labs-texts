Theory of quasiparticle interference in Kitaev quantum spin liquids
Ammar Jahin, Hao Zhang, Gábor B. Halász, Shi-Zeng Lin

Abstract
We study quasiparticle interference (QPI) in the Kitaev quantum spin liquid (QSL) for electrons tunneling into the QSL. The local tunneling conductance around a spin vacancy or localized vison reveals unique features associated with fractionalized Majorana fermions, chargons, and visons. In certain parameter regimes, the single-spinon density of states and momentum dispersion can both be directly extracted from the tunneling conductance. Our results suggest that QPI is a promising tool for identifying the Kitaev QSL and its fractionalized excitations.

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{r},E)=\int_{0}^{E}dE^{\prime}\rho_{C}(\bm{r},E-E^{\prime})\rho_{S}(\bm{r},E^{\prime}),

(4)

Results for fast hopping.
—We first discuss the conductance spectrum at fast hopping,
t\gg K
, which is the most relevant regime for real materials. In a clean system, the differential conductance is identical for all sites, and we can thus drop the site index
\bm{r}
from each density of states (DOS) in Eqs. (
4
)-(
6

4

{\rho}(E)
is then a convolution of a graphene-like chargon DOS
{\rho}_{C}(E)
and a spinon DOS
\rho_{S}(E)
with two components: a graphene-like contribution coming from matter fermions, and a sharp peak at energy
E_{V}\approx 0.26K
corresponding to vison pairs. The full range of
{\rho}(E)
is plotted in Fig.

follows
{\rho}_{C}(E)
with bandwidth

and a graphene-like structure including a linear node in the center and two Van Hove singularities around it. To understand this result, we first notice that
\Lambda_{C}\gg\Lambda_{S}

\displaystyle{\rho}(\bm{r},E)\approx{\rho}_{C}(\bm{r},E)\int_{0}^{\Lambda_{S}}dE^{\prime}\rho_{S}(\bm{r},E^{\prime})=4{\rho}_{C}(\bm{r},E),

(7)

where we use a sum rule for the spinon LDOS in the last step. Therefore, we find

at

. For

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\rho}(\bm{r},E)}{\partial E}=

\displaystyle\,{\rho}_{C}(\bm{r},E=0)\rho_{S}(\bm{r},E)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{E}dE^{\prime}\frac{\partial{\rho}_{C}(\bm{r},E-E^{\prime})}{\partial E}\rho_{S}(\bm{r},E^{\prime}),

Interestingly, the QPI is similar for a vacancy and a vison; thus, in the absence of any structural defect, the QPI can serve as evidence of a localized vison, which in turn binds a Majorana zero mode under a magnetic field. It is also worth noting that the effect of a vison is felt by the chargons through renormalized hopping amplitudes of the chargons around the vison. The vison is spatially localized in a hexagon and, therefore, produces a QPI similar to a vacancy.

Results for slow hopping.
—In the regime of slow hopping,
t\lesssim K
, the chargon bandwidth is much smaller than the spinon bandwidth,
\Lambda_{C}\ll\Lambda_{S}
, because
\Lambda_{C}\approx 0.356t
and
\Lambda_{S}=6K
. Thus, for any bias voltage
E\gg\Lambda_{C}
, one may use an approximation analogous to Eq. (
7
), but with interchanged roles of the spinons and the chargons, to argue that the electron LDOS
{\rho}(\bm{r},E)
largely follows the spinon LDOS
\rho_{S}(\bm{r},E)
. The only caveat is that
\rho_{S}(\bm{r},E)
is not smooth at the vison energy,
E_{V}\approx 0.26K
, which restricts this approximation to
E\gg\max(\Lambda_{C},E_{V})
. Indeed, while the electron DOS of a clean system shown in Fig.
4
(a) resembles the spinon DOS [see Fig.
1
(d)] for
E\gtrsim K
, with a broad peak at
E\approx 2K
due to the matter-fermion Van Hove singularity, the sharp peak at
E\approx E_{V}
coming from vison pairs is doubled as it is convolved with the bimodal chargon DOS [see Fig.
1
(b)]. In the presence of vacancy, for
E
near
E_{V}
, the contribution to the electron LDOS near a vacancy is dominated by the flat vison bands, which yields well localized three-lobe structure as shown in Fig.
4
(b). When
E\gg\max(\Lambda_{C},E_{V})
, electron LDOS [Fig.
4
(c)] shows clear oscillations following those of the spinon LDOS [Fig.
4
(e)]. This result suggests an even more direct correspondence between the QPI and the matter-fermion dispersion than in the previous regime of fast hopping. For
E\sim\max(\Lambda_{C},E_{V})
, there is no clear separation of energy scale between chargon and spinon, because the bias voltage is not enough to access the full spinon band. Nevertheless, clear spatial oscillation of electron LDOS occurs [Fig.
4
(d)] although a direct correspondence to the directly calculated spinon LDOS [Fig.
4
(f)] is less obvious.

Conclusions
— We study QPI in a Kitaev QSL next to an impurity, as directly measureable in an STM experiment.
Employing a parton mean-field theory, we obtain the electron LDOS as a convolution of the chargon and spinon LDOS, and highlight several distinctive features that can help identify the Kitaev QSL.
First, we point out that the electron DOS of a clean system exhibits signatures of both vison pairs at
E\approx E_{V}\approx 0.26K
and a matter-fermion (i.e., spinon) Van Hove singularity at
E\approx 2K
.
Second, in the presence of an impurity, we describe a simple protocol to extract the spinon LDOS and, hence, the spinon dispersion from the measured electron LDOS, thus providing direct evidence of a spinon Dirac point with linear dispersion at low energy.
This protocol is remarkable because it allows us to access key spinon properties that are otherwise hard to measure due to the fractionalized nature of the spinons.
We also emphasize that the QPI exhibits similar behavior near structural defects like spin vacancies and localized vison excitations that do not alter the structure but act like impurities for spinons.
Our results indicate that QPI is a powerful technique for identifying both the Kitaev QSL and its fractionalized excitations.
Moreover, our approach readily generalizes to other types of QSLs, such as U(1) QSLs with spinon Dirac points or Fermi surfaces.

We finally discuss the role of additional magnetic interactions commonly present in Kitaev magnets, such as Heisenberg and Gamma interactions. In the presence of such non-Kitaev interactions, the Kitaev QSL remains stable within a finite region of the parameter space
[
60
]
, and a straightforward generalization of our parton approach (see the SM
[
56
]
) can still be used to calculate the QPI inside the Kitaev QSL phase. Through a renormalization of the parton band structure, non-Kitaev interactions lead to quantitative (but not qualitative) changes in the QPI. Another notable effect of non-Kitaev interactions is the enhancement of
Z_{2}
gauge fluctuations. Deep inside the Kitaev QSL phase, the visons are gapped, resulting in weak gauge fluctuations. However, as the system approaches a phase transition out of the Kitaev QSL, gauge fluctuations become more pronounced. Analogous to electrons interacting with fluctuating gauge fields
[
61
]
, these enhanced fluctuations are expected to dampen the QPI signal and may eventually smear it out, particularly at higher energies where gauge fluctuations are stronger. Consequently, QPI serves as a powerful tool for probing not only the Kitaev QSL itself but also the phase transitions that separate it from nearby phases.

Appendix A
Parton decomposition and evaluation of the mean-field parameters

In this section, we give the details for the parton construction and the mean-field theory Hamiltonian we use in the main text.
The Hamiltonian governing the electron hopping is,

\displaystyle H_{t}=-t\sum_{\langle\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}\rangle}\sum_{\sigma}\left[\mathcal{P}d^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\sigma}d_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\sigma}\mathcal{P}+\text{h.c.}\right].

(1)

where
\mathcal{P}
projects out the local empty states.
We express the local electronic degrees of freedom in terms of two flavors of chargons
a_{\bm{r},\mu}
and two flavors of spinons
f_{\bm{r},\sigma}

\displaystyle d^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\uparrow}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},1}f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\uparrow}-a^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},2}f_{\bm{r},\downarrow})

\displaystyle d^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\downarrow}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},1}f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\downarrow}+a^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},2}f_{\bm{r},\uparrow}),

(2)

Such construction introduces an SU
(2)
gauge freedom, which is best seen rewriting the above equations as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\bm{r}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathcal{F}_{\bm{r}}\mathcal{A}_{\bm{r}}

(3)

with,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\bm{r}}=\begin{bmatrix}d^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\uparrow}&-d_{\bm{r},\downarrow}\\
d^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\downarrow}&d_{\bm{r},\uparrow}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\bm{r}}=\begin{bmatrix}f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\uparrow}&-f_{\bm{r},\downarrow}\\
f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\downarrow}&f_{\bm{r},\uparrow}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\bm{r}}=\begin{bmatrix}a^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},1}&-a_{\bm{r},2}\\
a^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},2}&a_{\bm{r},1}\end{bmatrix}.

(4)

In this form the spin SU
(2)
rotations are implemented by
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{r}}\rightarrow U_{s}\mathcal{D}_{\bm{r}}
.
In addition, this expansion of the Hilbert space introduces a gauge freedom in that the physical degrees of freedom
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{r}}
do not change under
\mathcal{F}_{\bm{r}}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{\bm{r}}U_{g}
and,
\mathcal{A}_{\bm{r}}\rightarrow U^{\dagger}_{g}\mathcal{A}_{\bm{r}}
.
The generators of this SU
(2)
gauge symmetry are,

\displaystyle K^{\alpha}_{\bm{r}}=\frac{1}{4}\text{Tr}\ \mathcal{F}_{\bm{r}}\sigma^{\alpha}F^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}}-\frac{1}{4}\text{Tr}\ \sigma^{z}\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}}\sigma^{\alpha}A_{\bm{r}}

(5)

The physical Hilbert space is invariant under such gauge transformation, i.e.
K^{\alpha}_{\bm{r}}=0
.
It can be shown that only three states satisfy this condition,

\displaystyle d^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\uparrow}|0\rangle=f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\uparrow}|\tilde{0}\rangle,

\displaystyle d^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\downarrow}|0\rangle=f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\downarrow}|\tilde{0}\rangle,

\displaystyle d^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\downarrow}d^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\uparrow}|0\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(a^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},2}+a^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},1}f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\downarrow}f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\uparrow}\right)|\tilde{0}\rangle.

(6)

where
d_{\bm{r},\sigma}|0\rangle=0
and
f_{\bm{r},\sigma}|\tilde{0}\rangle=a_{\bm{r},\mu}|\tilde{0}\rangle=0
.
We choose the decomposition of the electronic degrees of freedom into spinons and chargon so that the physical space coincides with the low energy subspace formed by the single, and doubly occupied local states in the presence of electron doping.

Substituting Eq. (
2
) in
H_{t}
we get,

\displaystyle H_{t}=-t\sum_{\langle\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}\rangle}\left[a^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},1}a_{\bm{r}^{\prime},1}\bigg(f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\uparrow}f_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\uparrow}+f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\downarrow}f_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\downarrow}\bigg)+a^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},2}a_{\bm{r}^{\prime},2}\bigg(f_{\bm{r},\uparrow}f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\uparrow}+f_{\bm{r},\downarrow}f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\downarrow}\bigg)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+a^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},1}a_{\bm{r}^{\prime},2}\bigg(f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\downarrow}f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\uparrow}-f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\uparrow}f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\downarrow}\bigg)+a^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},2}a_{\bm{r}^{\prime},1}\bigg(f_{\bm{r},\uparrow}f_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\downarrow}-f_{\bm{r},\downarrow}f_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\uparrow}\bigg)+\text{h.c.}\right].

(7)

We use a mean-field theory to study this Hamiltonian. We consider the fractionalized regime, where the mean-field order parameters for the
f
bilinears are those of the Kitaev quantum spin liquid.
The ansatz takes the following form,

\displaystyle H^{\text{MF}}_{t}=\frac{t}{8}\sum_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}\sum_{\mu}iW_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}a^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\mu}a_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\mu}

(8)

where

\displaystyle\langle a^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\mu}a_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\mu^{\prime}}\rangle=\langle f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\uparrow}f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\downarrow^{\prime}}\rangle=0

(9)

and

\displaystyle W_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}=\langle ic_{\bm{r}}c_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}+\sum_{\alpha}ib^{\alpha}_{\bm{r}}b^{\alpha}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}\rangle,

(10)

where we have defined the Majorana operators,

\displaystyle f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\uparrow}=\frac{1}{{2}}(c_{\bm{r}}+ib^{z}_{\bm{r}}),

\displaystyle f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\downarrow}=\frac{1}{{2}}(ib^{x}_{\bm{r}}+b^{y}_{\bm{r}}).

(11)

The expectation values
W_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}
are evaluated for the ground state of the Kitaev model with a defect introduced. This requires solving the quadratic Hamiltonian of the Kitaev model in the presence of a defect which can be done numerically.

The terms
\langle ib^{\alpha}_{\bm{r}}b^{\alpha}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}\rangle
are easy to compute. They are nonzero only when the product
b^{\alpha}_{\bm{r}}b^{\alpha}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}
does not create any visons.
This happens when
\bm{r}
and
\bm{r}^{\prime}
are nearest neighbors and make an
\alpha
-edge,

\displaystyle\langle ib^{\alpha}_{\bm{r}}b^{\alpha}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}\rangle=\begin{cases}u^{std}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}&\bm{r}\text{ and }\bm{r}^{\prime}\text{ make an }\alpha\text{-edge}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(12)

where
u^{std}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}
is the standard gauge field defining the vison-free subspace away from a defect and zero for edges connected to a vacancy.

The evaluation of
\langle ic_{\bm{r}}c_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}\rangle
requires the diagonalization of the Kitaev Hamiltonian which takes the general form

\displaystyle H_{K}=\frac{K}{2}\sum_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}A_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}\ ic_{\bm{r}}c_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}

(13)

Since
A_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}
is an antisymmetric matrix, there exists a matrix
Q\in O(N)
, with
N
being the number of sites of the system, such that

\displaystyle A=Q\mathcal{E}Q^{T}

(14)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=\bigoplus^{N/2}_{m=1}\begin{bmatrix}0&\varepsilon_{m}\\
-\varepsilon_{m}&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\varepsilon_{m}>0.

(15)

Using this to rewrite the Kitaev Hamiltonian, we have

\displaystyle H_{K}=\sum_{m=1}^{N/2}\varepsilon_{m}\ ib_{2m}b_{2m+1}=\sum_{m}\varepsilon_{m}\left[2\psi_{m}^{\dagger}\psi_{m}-1\right]

(16)

where we define
b_{i}=c_{\bm{r}}Q_{\bm{r},i}
, and
\psi_{m}=(b_{2m}+ib_{2m+1})/2
.
We obtain the matrix
Q
numerically which has a computational cost of
O(N^{3})
, the same as the exact diagonalization.
The ground state of the Kitaev model is defined such that
\psi_{m}|\Psi_{0}^{S}\rangle=0
for all
m
.
In the new basis, we can easily evaluate,

\displaystyle\langle ib_{2m}b_{2n}\rangle=\langle ib_{2m+1}b_{2n+1}\rangle=i\delta_{m,n},

\displaystyle\langle ib_{2m}b_{2n+1}\rangle=-\delta_{m,n}

(17)

Using this we can write

\displaystyle\langle ic_{i}c_{j}\rangle=Q_{j,j^{\prime}}Q_{i,i^{\prime}}\langle ib_{i^{\prime}}b_{j^{\prime}}\rangle

(18)

which we evaluate numerically.

Appendix B
Parton construction of generic Hamiltonians

A Hamiltonian for a family of honeycomb lattice spin liquid candidate materials can be written as

\mathcal{H}=\sum_{\langle\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}\rangle\in\alpha\beta(\gamma)}\big[J\bm{S}_{\bm{r}}\cdot\bm{S}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}+KS_{\bm{r}}^{\gamma}S_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}^{\gamma}+\Gamma(S_{\bm{r}}^{\alpha}S_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}^{\beta}+S_{\bm{r}}^{\beta}S_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}^{\alpha})\big]-\sum_{\bm{r}}\bm{B}\cdot\bm{S}_{\bm{r}},

(19)

where
J
is the Heisenberg exchange,
K
is the Kitaev exchange, and
\Gamma
denotes the symmetric off-diagonal exchange, and
\bm{B}
represents an external Zeeman field. We introduce the parton (complex) fermions to represent the local moment
\bm{S}_{\bm{r}}=\frac{1}{4}\text{Tr}\mathcal{F}_{\bm{r}}^{\dagger}\bm{\sigma}\mathcal{F}_{\bm{r}}
, where the matrix
\mathcal{F}_{\bm{r}}
is as defined in Eq. (
4
), and we impose the constraint
f_{\bm{r}\alpha}^{\dagger}f_{\bm{r}\alpha}=1
, where, for here and the rest of this section, repeated Greek indices are summed over.
The Zeeman term takes the following quadratic form in fermionic representation,

\displaystyle\bm{B}\cdot\bm{S}_{\bm{r}}=\frac{1}{2}B^{a}\sigma^{a}_{\alpha,\beta}f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\alpha}f_{\bm{r},\beta}

(20)

The magnetic interactions
J
,
K
, and
\Gamma
are all quartic in the fermionic language and take the following form

\displaystyle S^{a}_{\bm{r}}S^{a^{\prime}}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{4}\left(f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}\alpha}\sigma^{a}_{\alpha,\beta}f_{\bm{r},\beta}\right)\left(f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}\sigma^{a^{\prime}}_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}f_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}\right),\qquad a,a^{\prime}\in\{0,x,y,z\}

(21)

We can treat such quartic interactions using a mean-field theory. To facilitate a mean-field treatment, we further write this interaction in the following way,

\displaystyle S^{a}_{\bm{r}}S^{a^{\prime}}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{8}\left[(f_{\bm{r}\alpha}f_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}})^{\dagger}\sigma^{a}_{\alpha,\beta}\sigma^{a^{\prime}}_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}f_{\bm{r},\beta}f_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}\right]-\frac{1}{8}\left[(f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\beta}f_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}})^{\dagger}\sigma^{a}_{\alpha,\beta}\sigma^{a^{\prime}}_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}\alpha}f_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}\right].

(22)

We introduce the following fermion bilinears,

\hat{\rho}^{a}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}=f_{\bm{r}\alpha}^{\dagger}(\sigma^{a})_{\alpha\beta}f_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\beta},\ \ {\hat{\Delta}^{a}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}=f_{\bm{r}\alpha}(i\sigma^{y}\sigma^{a})_{\alpha\beta}f_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\beta},}\ \ \qquad a\in\{0,x,y,z\}

(23)

where
\hat{\rho}^{s}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}
and
\hat{{\Delta}}^{s}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}
are SU(2) spin singlet and
\hat{\rho}^{a}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}
and
\hat{\Delta}^{a}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}
are SU(2) spin triplet with
a=x,y,z
.
We can write bilinears
f_{\bm{r}\alpha}f_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\beta}
in terms of the
\hat{\rho}
and
\hat{\Delta}
using

\displaystyle f_{\bm{r},\uparrow}f_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\uparrow}=\frac{-1}{2}(\hat{\Delta}^{x}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}+i\hat{\Delta}^{y}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}),

\displaystyle f_{\bm{r},\downarrow}f_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\downarrow}=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\Delta}^{x}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}-i\hat{\Delta}^{y}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}),

\displaystyle f_{\bm{r},\uparrow}f_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\downarrow}=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\Delta}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}+\hat{\Delta}^{z}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}),

\displaystyle f_{\bm{r},\downarrow}f_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\uparrow}=\frac{1}{2}(-\hat{\Delta}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}+\hat{\Delta}^{z}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}})

(24)

and

\displaystyle f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\uparrow}f_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\uparrow}=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\rho}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}+\hat{\rho}^{z}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\downarrow}f_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\downarrow}=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\rho}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}-\hat{\rho}^{z}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\uparrow}f_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\downarrow}=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\rho}^{x}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}-i\hat{\rho}^{y}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle f^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\downarrow}f_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\uparrow}=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\rho}^{x}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}+i\hat{\rho}^{y}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}).

(25)

Using these relationships we can work out the expressions for the Kitaev interactions,

\displaystyle KS^{x}_{\bm{r}}S^{x}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}=\frac{-K}{16}\left[\hat{\Delta}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}^{0\dagger}\hat{\Delta}^{0}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}+\hat{\Delta}^{x^{\dagger}}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}\hat{\Delta}^{x}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}-\hat{\Delta}^{y^{\dagger}}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}\hat{\Delta}^{y}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}-\hat{\Delta}^{z^{\dagger}}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}\hat{\Delta}^{z}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}+\hat{\rho}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}^{0\dagger}\hat{\rho}^{0}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}+\hat{\rho}^{x^{\dagger}}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}\hat{\rho}^{x}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}-\hat{\rho}^{y^{\dagger}}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}\hat{\rho}^{y}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}-\hat{\rho}^{z^{\dagger}}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}\hat{\rho}^{z}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}\right].

(26)

Expressions for
S^{y}_{\bm{r}}S^{y}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}
and
S^{z}_{\bm{r}}S^{z}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}
can be worked out by permitting
x,y,z
in a cyclic manner,
x,y,z\rightarrow y,z,x
.

By adding
S^{x}_{\bm{r}}S^{x}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}+S^{y}_{\bm{r}}S^{y}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}+S^{z}_{\bm{r}}S^{z}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}
we obtain the expression for the

Heisenberg exchange term

J\bm{S}_{\bm{r}}\cdot\bm{S}_{\bm{r}}=-\frac{3J}{16}\left[\left(\hat{\rho}^{0}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}\right)^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}^{0}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}+\left(\hat{\Delta}^{0}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}\right)^{\dagger}\hat{\Delta}^{0}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}\right]+\frac{J}{16}\sum_{a=x,y,z}\left[\left(\hat{\rho}^{a}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}\right)^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}^{a}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}+\left(\hat{\Delta}^{a}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}\right)^{\dagger}\hat{\Delta}^{a}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}\right].

(27)

Lastly, we write the symmetric off-diagonal exchange interaction in terms of these fermion bilinears. For the
z
-bond, the interaction is given by:

-\frac{\Gamma}{8}\left[\left(\hat{\Delta}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}^{x}\right)^{\dagger}\hat{\Delta}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}^{y}+\left(\hat{\rho}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}^{x}\right)^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}^{y}+h.c.\right].

(28)

As before, the
x
and
y
-bond terms can be obtained by the permutation
x,y,z\rightarrow y,z,x
.

For any pair of the above fermion bilinears
\hat{A}\hat{B}
, the mean-field decoupling

\hat{A}\hat{B}\sim\hat{A}B+A\hat{B}-AB,

(29)

where

\displaystyle A=\langle\hat{A}\rangle

(30)

is to be determined self-consistently.

In the main text, we analyzed the mean-field solution for the case with
J=\Gamma=B=0
.
Including these terms will renormalize the parton band structure. This normalization will modify the quasiparticle interference signal, analogous to the behavior in metals.

Appendix C
Evaluation of the LDOS

C.1
Chargon LDOS

In the main text, we arrive at the following expression for chargon LDOS,

\displaystyle\rho_{C}(\bm{r},E)=\sum_{m}\sum_{\mu}|\langle\Psi^{C}_{m}|a^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},\mu}|\Psi^{C}_{0}\rangle|^{2}\delta(E_{m}^{C}-E).

(31)

The excited states
|\Psi^{C}_{m}\rangle
are those of
H_{t}
, and are written as,

\displaystyle|\Psi^{C}_{m}\rangle=a^{\dagger}_{m}|\Psi^{C}_{0}\rangle,

(32)

where

\displaystyle a^{\dagger}_{m}=U_{\bm{r},m}a^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}}.

(33)

We drop the
\mu
index for now, as different species of chargons do not mix.
With the matrix elements
W_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}
evaluated, we can numerically calculate
U_{\bm{r},m}
by exactly diagonalizing the Hamiltonian.
After obtaining
U_{\bm{r},m}
the chargon LDOS takes the following expression,

\displaystyle\rho_{C}(\bm{r},E)=2\sum_{m}|U_{\bm{r},m}|^{2}\delta(E_{m}^{C}-E),

(34)

with the factor of
2
from the sum over
\mu
.
In numerically evaluating this formula on a finite system size of linear size
L
the energy level spacing goes to zero as
1/L
.

In all of our numerical computations, we use the Gaussian broadening

\displaystyle\delta(E_{m}^{C}-E)\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left[-\frac{(E_{m}^{C}-E)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right].

(35)

This is similar to adding a non-zero inverse lifetime to the quasiparticles.
The standard deviation
\sigma
is chosen to be of the order of the level spacing and such that the DOS is smooth.

C.2
Spinon LDOS

In the main text, we arrive at the following expression for spinon LDOS,

\displaystyle\rho_{S}(\bm{r},E)=\sum_{n}\bigg[|\langle\Psi_{n}^{S}|c_{\bm{r}}|\Psi_{0}^{S}\rangle|^{2}+\sum_{\alpha}|\langle\Psi_{n}^{S}|b^{\alpha}_{\bm{r}}|\Psi_{0}^{S}\rangle|^{2}\bigg]\delta(E_{m}^{S}-E).

(36)

The operator
c_{\bm{r}}
does not change the vison configuration of
|\Psi_{0}^{S}\rangle
, and the evaluation of these terms requires only knowing the matrix
Q
in Eq. (
14
).
From Eq. (
16
) we see that the excited states contributing to a nonzero overlap of
\langle\Psi_{n}^{S}|c_{\bm{r}}|\Psi_{0}^{S}\rangle
take the form

\displaystyle|\Psi^{S}_{n}\rangle=\psi_{n}^{\dagger}|\Psi^{S}_{0}\rangle,

(37)

where
\Psi_{0}^{S}
is defined as the state belonging to the vison-free subspace and the vacuum of
\psi_{m}
.
We write
c_{\bm{r}}=Q_{\bm{r},i}b_{i}=Q_{\bm{r},2n}b_{2n}+Q_{\bm{r},2n+1}b_{2n+1}
, and thus,

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{n}^{S}|c_{\bm{r}}|\Psi_{0}^{S}\rangle=Q_{\bm{r},2n}-\frac{1}{i}Q_{\bm{r},2n+1}\rightarrow|\langle\Psi_{n}^{S}|c_{\bm{r}}|\Psi_{0}^{S}\rangle|^{2}=Q_{\bm{r},2n}^{2}+Q_{\bm{r},2n+1}^{2}.

(38)

The evaluation of the overlap
\langle\Psi_{n}^{S}|b^{\alpha}_{\bm{r}}|\Psi_{0}^{S}\rangle
is more involved since the operator
b^{\alpha}_{\bm{r}}
excites two neighboring visons at the plaquettes sharing the
\alpha
-edge connected to the site
\bm{r}
.
The full ground state of the Kitaev model takes a tensor product form,

\displaystyle|\Psi^{S}_{0}\rangle=|W_{0}\rangle\otimes|\Phi_{0}\rangle

(39)

where
|W_{0}\rangle
describes the vison configuration, and
|\Phi_{0}\rangle
describes the matter fermion
c_{\bm{r}}
as discussed above.
The
b^{\alpha}_{\bm{r}}
operator acts on the vison subspace,

\displaystyle b^{\alpha}_{\bm{r}}|\Psi^{S}_{0}\rangle=|W^{\prime}\rangle\otimes|\Phi_{0}\rangle

(40)

with
|W^{\prime}\rangle
describing the state with two visons excited.
To obtain a nonzero overlap
\langle\Psi_{n}^{S}|b^{\alpha}_{\bm{r}}|\Psi_{0}^{S}\rangle
, the state
|\Psi_{n}^{S}\rangle
must have the same vison configuration as
|b^{\alpha}_{\bm{r}}|\Psi_{0}^{S}\rangle
. We can always choose an excited state with a matching vison configuration, so we write

\displaystyle|\Psi^{S}_{n}\rangle=|W^{\prime}\rangle\otimes|\Phi^{\prime}_{n}\rangle.

(41)

and obtain

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{n}^{S}|b^{\alpha}_{\bm{r}}|\Psi_{0}^{S}\rangle=\langle\Phi^{\prime}_{n}|\Phi_{0}\rangle.

(42)

There are multiple possibilities for the state
|\Phi^{\prime}_{n}\rangle
, the one with the lowest energy being the ground state in the subspace of the excited visons.
We label this state as
|\Phi^{\prime}_{0}\rangle
and focus now on the evaluation of
\langle\Phi^{\prime}_{0}|\Phi_{0}\rangle
.
Similar to how the state
|\Phi_{0}\rangle
is defined as the state annihilated by
\psi_{m}
, the state
|\Phi^{\prime}_{0}\rangle
is defined as the state annihilated by a different set of operators
\psi^{\prime}_{m}
.
Thus, we are asking about the overlap between two different Bogoliubov vacua.
This is the same as asking about the overlap between two superconducting states.
This overlap is given using what is known in the nuclear physics literature as the “Onishi” formula.
We use a more practical formula developed in Ref.
[
57
]
which gives the overlap in terms of the Pfaffian,

\displaystyle\langle\Phi^{\prime}_{0}|\Phi_{0}\rangle=\frac{e^{i\theta}}{\prod_{n}v^{\prime}_{n}\prod_{m}v_{m}}\ \text{Pf}\begin{bmatrix}V^{T}U&V^{T}V^{\prime*}\\
-V^{\prime\dagger}V&U^{\prime T}V^{\prime*}\end{bmatrix}

(43)

where

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{g}\\
\bm{g}^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}U&V^{*}\\
V&U^{*}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\psi}\\
\bm{\psi}^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix},\qquad g_{m}=\frac{1}{2}(c_{2i}+c_{2i+1})

(44)

and
v_{m}
are the singular values of
V
. The matrices
U^{\prime}
, and
V^{\prime}
are defined similarly for
\psi^{\prime}_{m}
.

The Pfaffian formula above is numerically challenging to compute when either
V
or
V^{\prime}
has zero singular values.
To circumvent this issue, we use the development in Ref.
[
58
]
in which we remove the zero singular values using a Bloch-Messiah decomposition of the matrices
U
and
V
, and
U^{\prime}
and
V^{\prime}
.
We do not spend effort extracting the exact phase
e^{i\theta}
since for our problem we are only interested in the magnitude of the overlap.

In principle, the computation might produce
|\Phi_{0}\rangle
and
|\Phi^{\prime}_{0}\rangle
that have different fermion parity, leading to a zero overlap.
This can always be remedied for in the case of a vacancy where the model contains dangling
b^{\alpha}
Majorana operators around the vacancy.
These can be combined into a complex fermion that is completely decoupled from the system, and we are free to choose its filling to make the parity of
|\Phi_{0}\rangle
and
|\Phi^{\prime}_{0}\rangle
match.

Every evaluation of the Pfaffian formula incurs a computational cost that is
O(N^{3})
as we solve for
U^{\prime}
and
V^{\prime}
.
In our problem, we need to evaluate this Pfaffian
O(N)
times corresponding to different locations of the vison excitations on the lattice.
In total, the computational cost of evaluating the overlaps
\langle\Phi^{\prime}_{0}|\Phi_{0}\rangle
for all possible excitations is
O(N^{4})
.
Our calculations show that
|\langle\Phi^{\prime}_{0}|\Phi_{0}\rangle|^{2}\approx 0.77
for the two neighboring visons excited by
b^{\alpha}_{\bm{r}}
.

The next task is to compute the overlap for
|\Phi^{\prime}_{n}\rangle
which represents Bogoliubov quasiparticles excited on top of the vacuum
|\Phi^{\prime}_{0}\rangle
.
These Bogoliubov excitations must come in pairs to preserve the fermion parity to have a nonzero overlap.
In general, this can be done by writing
|\Phi^{\prime}_{n}\rangle
to be the vacuum of

Results for QPI at

In Fig.

Remember that
t

Using our mean-field treatment, we find that the chargon hopping is strongly renormalized and has a much smaller strength at

This means that even when
t=K

This makes the situation similar to the case where

E\gg K

This is indeed the case as shown in Figs.

3

This agreement is expected to get better the slower the chargon is.
For
E\sim K

This is because the bias energy is not sufficient to sample the whole spinon band, and there is no clear separation between spinon and chargon energy.
