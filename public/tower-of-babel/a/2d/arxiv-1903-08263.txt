Introduction

Let
R
and
B
be two point sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with

|R|+|B|=n
, where
d
is a constant,
and let
\lambda:R\cup B\to\mathbb{N}
be a function satisfying

\sum_{r\in R}\lambda(r)=\sum_{b\in B}\lambda(b)
.
We denote
U:=\max_{p\in R\cup B}\lambda(p)
.
We call a function
\tau:R\times B\to\mathbb{N}
,
a
transportation map
between
R
and
B
if

\sum_{b\in B}\tau(r,b)=\lambda(r)
for all
r\in R

and
\sum_{r\in R}\tau(r,b)=\lambda(b)
for all
b\in B
.
Informally, for a point
r\in R
, the value of
\lambda(r)

represents the
supply
at
r
, while for a point
b\in B
,
the value of
\lambda(b)
represents the
demand
at
b
. A transportation
map represents a plan for moving the supplies at points in

R
to meet the demands at points in
B
.

The cost of a transportation map
\tau
is defined as

\tfcost(\tau)=\sum_{(r,b)\in R\times B}\tau(r,b)\mathrm{d}(r,b)
,
where
\mathrm{d}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a suitable distance function
such as the
L_{p}
distance.
The
Hitchcock-Koopmans transportation problem

(or simply
transportation problem
)
on
\Sigma=(R,B,\lambda)
is to find the
minimum-cost transportation map for
\Sigma
,
denoted
\tau^{*}:=\tau^{*}(\Sigma)
.
The optimal cost
\tfcost(\tau^{*})
is often referred to as the

transportation distance
or
earth mover’s distance
.

The transportation problem is a discrete version of
the so-called
optimal transport
, or
Monge-Kantarovich
, problem,
originally proposed by the French mathematician Gaspard Monge in 1781.
This latter problem has been extensively studied in mathematics since the
early 20th century. See the book by Villani
[
29
]
.
In addition to this connection,
the (discrete) transportation problem has a wide range of applications, including
similarity computation between a pair of images, shapes, and distributions,
computing the barycenter of a family of distributions,
finding common structures in a set of shapes, fluid mechanics,
and partial differential equations. Motivated by these applications,
this problem has been studied extensively in many fields
including computer vision, computer graphics, machine learning,
optimization, and mathematics.
See e.g.
[
22
,
15
,
12
,
25
,
14
]

and references therein for a few examples.

The transportation problem can be formulated as an
instance of the uncapacitated minimum cost flow problem
in a complete bipartite graph
R\times B
with uncapacitated edges.
The minimum cost flow problem has been widely studied;
see
[
20
]
for a detailed review of known results.
The uncapacitated minimum cost flow problem in a graph with
n
vertices and
m

edges can be solved in
O((m+n\log n)n\log n)
time using
Orlin’s algorithm
[
21
]

or
\tilde{O}(m\sqrt{n}\polylog U)

time

.

using the algorithm by Lee and Sidford
[
20
]
.

For transportation in geometric settings,
Atkinson and Vaidya
[

]
adapted the Edmonds-Karp
algorithm to exploit geometric properties, and
obtained an
\tilde{O}(n^{2.5}\log U)
time algorithm for any
L_{p}
-metric,
and
\tilde{O}(n^{2})
for
L_{1}
,
L_{\infty}
-metrics.
The Atkinson-Vaidya algorithm was improved using faster
data structures for dynamic nearest-neighbor searching,
first in
[

]
and most recently in

[

,
for a running time of
\tilde{O}(n^{2}\log U)

Sharathkumar and Agarwal
[

designed a

\tilde{O}((n\sqrt{nU}+U\log U)\log(n/\varepsilon))

More efficient algorithms are known for estimating the
the optimal cost (earth mover’s distance) without computing
the map itself, provided that
U=n^{O(1)}
.
Indyk
[
18
]
gave an algorithm to find an
O(1)
-approximate estimate
in

time with probability at least
1/2
.
Cabello
et al.

[

reduced the problem to minimum cost flow
on a geometric spanner, obtaining a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate estimate in

\tilde{O}(n^{2})
time.
Andoni
et al.

[
4
]
gave a streaming algorithm
that finds a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate estimate in
O(n^{1+o_{\varepsilon}(1)})
time.
However, in many applications, one is interested in computing the map itself
and not just the transportation distance

14
,

]
. This is the problem
that we address in this paper.

The special case of the transportation problem where every point has unit
demand/supply is a minimum-cost bipartite matching problem (assignment problem).
After sequence of papers in the geometric setting,

[

,
28
,

,

]
,
a near-linear
\tilde{O}(n)
time
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation
was found by Agarwal and Sharathkumar
[
24
]

for geometric bipartite matching.
On the other hand, before our work, no constant-factor
approximation in subquadratic time was known for the transportation problem
with arbitrary demands and supplies, even for the special case of
U=O(n^{2})
.

Our results.

We present three new results for the geometric transportation problem,
for any
L_{p}
-metric.

Our first result (Section
2
) is a randomized algorithm that
for any
\varepsilon>0
, computes in
O(n^{1+\varepsilon})
expected time a
transportation map whose expected cost is

O(\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon))\tfcost(\tau^{*})
.
The expected cost improves to
O(\log(1/\varepsilon))\tfcost(\tau^{*})
if the
spread of
R\cup B
is
n^{O(1)}
,
where the
spread

\Phi
is the ratio of the
maximum and the minimum distance between a pair of points.
The overall structure of our algorithm is a simpler version of the
geometric bipartite matching algorithm by Agarwal and Varadarajan
[
3
]
,
but several new ideas are needed to handle arbitrary supplies and demands.

This algorithm can be extended to spaces with
bounded doubling dimension when the spread is polynomially bounded:
if
R,B
lie in a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with constant doubling dimension and
\Phi=n^{O(1)}
,
then a modified version of the algorithm finds,
in
O(n^{1+\varepsilon})
expected time,
a transportation map whose expected cost is
O(\log(1/\varepsilon))\tfcost(\tau^{*})
.

Our second result (Section
3
) is a

(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate,
\tilde{O}(n^{3/2}\varepsilon^{-d}\polylog(U))
time
algorthim, by reduction to minimum cost flow.
Using a quad-tree based well-separated pair decomposition (WSPD)
[
11
]

of a point set, we construct a graph
H
with
O(n)
vertices
and
O(n/\varepsilon^{d})
edges, and reduce the problem of computing
a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate transportation map to computing
the minimum cost flow in
H
.
We find a minimum cost flow
f^{*}
using the Lee-Sidford
[
20
]
algorithm,
and finally recover a transportation map
whose cost is close to
\tfcost(f^{*})
(in turn, close to
\tfcost(\tau^{*})
).
This algorithm extends to spaces with bounded doubling dimension
using the appropriate speedy WSPD construction for such spaces
[
17
]
.
Specifically, if the doubling dimension is
D
and the spread of

R\cup B
is
n^{O(1)}
, then a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate
transportation map can be computed in time

\tilde{O}(n^{3/2}\varepsilon^{-O(D)}\polylog(U))
.

Our third result (Section
4
) is an exact,

\tilde{O}(n^{2})
time algorithm for
d=2
,
thereby matching (up to poly-logarithmic factors) the best exact algorithm
for geometric bipartite matching
[
19
]
.
This is an implementation of Orlin’s strongly polynomial
minimum cost flow algorithm
[
21
]
,
an augmenting-paths algorithm with edge contractions.
A naive application of Orlin’s algorithm has a running time of
\tilde{O}(n^{3})
,
but by exploiting the geometry of the underlying graph,
we improve this to
\tilde{O}(n^{2})
time in the plane.

2
A Near-Linear Approximation

Let
\Sigma=(R,B,\lambda)
be an instance of the
transportation problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We say that
\Sigma
has
bounded spread
if the spread of

R\cup B
is bounded above by
n^{a}
, for some constant
a>0
.
We present a randomized recursive algorithm that, given
\Sigma
and a parameter

\varepsilon>0
, returns a transportation map in
O(n^{1+\varepsilon})
expected time whose
expected cost is
O(\log(1/\varepsilon))\tfcost(\tau^{*})
if
\Sigma
has
bounded spread, and
O(\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon))\tfcost(\tau^{*})
otherwise
(recall that
\tau^{*}
is the optimal map).
We assume that
n
is sufficiently large so that
n^{\varepsilon}
is
at least a suitably large constant.

We first give a high-level description of the algorithm without describing
how each step is implemented efficiently.
Next, we analyze the cost of the transportation map computed by the algorithm.
We then discuss an efficient implementation of the algorithm.
For simplicity, we describe the algorithm and its analysis for dimension

d=2
; the algorithm extends to
d>2
in a straightforward manner.

We need the notion of
randomly shifted grids
,
as in
[
5
,
3
]
.
Formally, let
\Box=[a-\ell,a]\times[b-\ell,b]

be a square of side length
\ell
with
(a,b)
as its top right corner.
For a parameter
\Delta>0
(grid cell sidelength),
set
l=\lceil\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\ell}{\Delta}\right)\rceil
,
and
L=2^{l+1}\Delta
.
Let
\Box_{L}=[a-L,a]\times[b-L,b]
be the square of
side length
L
with
(a,b)
as its top-right corner.
We choose uniformly at random a point
\xi\in[0,\Delta)^{2}
and set

\Box_{shifted}:=\Box_{L}+\xi
.
Note that
\Box\subseteq\Box_{shifted}
.
Let
\mathbb{G}(\Box,\Delta)
be the partition of
\Box_{shifted}

into the uniform grid of side length
\Delta
;

\mathbb{G}(\Box,\Delta)
has
2^{l+1}\times 2^{l+1}
grid cells.

\mathbb{G}(\Box,\Delta)
is called the randomly shifted grid on
\Box
.

2.1
A high-level description

A recursive subproblem
\Sigma^{\prime}=({R^{\prime}},{B^{\prime}},{\lambda^{\prime}})

consists of point sets
R^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
,
and a demand function
\lambda^{\prime}:R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}\to\mathbb{N}

such that
\lambda^{\prime}(R^{\prime})=\lambda^{\prime}(B^{\prime})
.
We denote
|R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}|=m
.
If
m\leq n^{\varepsilon/4}
, we call
\Sigma^{\prime}
a
base

subproblem and compute an optimal transportation using Orlin’s algorithm.
Thus, assume that
m>n^{\varepsilon/4}
.

:

Let
\delta=1/(3d)=1/6
, a constant.
We use
\Box
to denote the smallest axis-aligned square containing
R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}
,
and its sidelength
\ell
, and set
\Delta=\ell/m^{\delta}
.

Observation 1
.

A randomly shifted grid
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{G}(\Box,\Delta)
has the following properties.

1.

The number of cells is
O([n^{\delta}]^{d})=O(n^{1/3})
.

2.

The diameter of each
\mathbb{G}
cell is
\sqrt{d}\cdot\Delta=O(\Delta)
.

3.

For any two points
r\in R^{\prime}
and
b\in B^{\prime}
,
the probability that they lie in different cells of
\mathbb{G}
is
O(\mathrm{d}(r,b)/\Delta)
.

The first step of the algorithm is to choose a randomly shifted grid

\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{G}(\Box,\Delta)
that has the following additional property:
any two points in
R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}
that are within a distance of

\ell/m^{3}
lie in the same grid cell.
We call a grid satisfying this property
safe
.
Algorithmically, we place an axis-parallel square of
side length
2\ell/m^{3}
around every
p\in R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}
,
called the
moat
of
p
;

\mathbb{G}
is safe if none of its grid lines cross any moat
(see Figure
1
).

Observation 2
.

A randomly shifted grid
\mathbb{G}
is safe with probability
\Omega(1-1/m^{2})
.

Proof.

We can interpret the random shift on
\mathbb{G}
as a composition of 1-dimensional shifts in each dimension.
These 1-dimensional shifts are chosen uniformly at random from
[0,\ell]
.
In any single dimension
e_{i}
, the union of moats has length at most
m\cdot 2\ell/m^{3}=2\ell/m^{2}
.
Dividing, the probability that the shift in
e_{i}
crossed a grid line with a moat is
O(1/m^{2})
.
There are a constant number of dimensions, so the total failure probability is
O(1/m^{2})
.
∎

If
\mathbb{G}
is not safe, we re-sample random shift until we find one that is safe.

Let
\Pi\subseteq\mathbb{G}
be the set of nonempty grid cells,
i.e., ones that contain at least one point of
R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}
.
For each cell
\pi\in\Pi
, we create a recursive instance
\Sigma^{\prime}_{\pi}
,
which we refer to as an

.
Each
\Sigma^{\prime}_{\pi}

Whatever we are unable to transport locally within cells of

we transport globally with a single

\Sigma^{\prime}_{\Box}

We now describe these subproblems in more detail.

For each cell
\pi\in\Pi
, we define the

\pi

to be the absolute difference between the supply and demand in
\pi
,
denoted by
\chi_{\pi}
.
Without loss of generality, assume

\lambda^{\prime}(R^{\prime}\cap\pi)\geq\lambda^{\prime}(B^{\prime}\cap\pi)
,
i.e. that the extra supply of
\pi
is red.
We will use the entirety of
B^{\prime}\cap\pi
for the internal
subproblem, and arbitrarily partition
R^{\prime}\cap\pi
such that

\lambda^{\prime}(B^{\prime}\cap\pi)
supply is used for the internal subproblem,
and the remainder (of total supply

)
is used for the external subproblem.
We pick an arbitrary maximal subset of points

(R^{\prime}_{ex})_{\pi}\subseteq R^{\prime}\cap\pi

Let

For the external subproblem, we first convert the transportation map

For each red point

2.2
Cost analysis

There are two sources of error in our algorithm:
the distortion between
\Sigma^{\prime}_{\Box}
and
\Sigma^{\prime}_{ex}
,
and the error from restricting the solution to the
internal/external partitioning of demand.

\delta
-closeness.

We first formalize the way that
\Sigma^{\prime}_{\Box}
approximates
\Sigma^{\prime}_{ex}

when it shifts demand to cell centers.
We introduce a notion called
\delta
-closeness between transportation
instances: informally, two instances are
\delta
-close if we can shift the demands
of one to form the other, without moving any demand more than
\delta
.

For a given transportation map
\tau
, we define
\tfcost_{\infty}(\tau)=\max_{(r,b):\tau(r,b)>0}{\mathrm{d}(r,b)}
as the maximum distance used in
\tau
.
Now, let
\Sigma=({R},{B},{\lambda})
be an instance of the transportation problem and
\tau
a
transportation map for
\Sigma
.
Let
\Sigma^{\prime}=({R}^{\prime},{B}^{\prime},{\lambda}^{\prime})
be another instance of the transportation problem
with
\lambda(R)=\lambda^{\prime}(R^{\prime})
.
Consider the transportation instances
\Sigma_{R}=(R,R^{\prime},\lambda_{R})

and
\Sigma_{B}=(B,B^{\prime},\lambda_{B})

where
\lambda_{R}
(resp.,
\lambda_{B}
) is the supply (resp., demand) of points in

R
and
R^{\prime}
(resp.,
B
and
B^{\prime}
) in
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}

respectively.
We call
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}

\delta
-close
if there exist
transportation maps
\tau_{R}
and
\tau_{B}
of

\Sigma_{R}
and
\Sigma_{B}
such that

\tfcost_{\infty}(\tau_{R}),\tfcost_{\infty}(\tau_{B})\leq\delta
.
Observe that this condition holds between the extra supply points and the
contracted points.

Observation 3
.

Any point in a grid cell of side length
\Delta
is within
(\Delta/\sqrt{2})

of the cell center, so
\Sigma_{\Box}
and
\Sigma_{ex}
are
(\Delta/\sqrt{2})
-close.

The definition of
\delta
-closeness states that the supplies of

R
(resp., demands of
B
) (and therefore the units of
\tau
)
can be mapped to supplies at
R^{\prime}
(resp.,
B^{\prime}
) within distance
\delta
.
Let
\tau^{\prime}
be the result of passing
\tau
through these maps,
then we say that
\tau^{\prime}
solving
\Sigma^{\prime}
is
derived

from
\tau
solving
\Sigma
.
The following lemma relates the transportation map for
\Sigma_{ex}
to the
solution for
\Sigma_{\Box}
produced by the external subproblem.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
be two
\delta
-close instances of the
transportation problem with
\mathsf{X}^{\prime}
being the total demand of each.
Let
\tau
be a transportation map of
\Sigma
and let
\tau^{\prime}
be
a transportation map of
\Sigma^{\prime}
derived from
\tau
.
Then,
|\tfcost(\tau^{\prime})-\tfcost(\tau)|\leq 2\delta\mathsf{X}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Consider a unit of transportation in
\tau
, between points
r,b
.
In
\tau^{\prime}
, this unit is mapped to two new points
r^{\prime},b^{\prime}
,
which by
\delta
-closeness must satisfy

\mathrm{d}(r,r^{\prime})\leq\delta
and
\mathrm{d}(b,b^{\prime})\leq\delta
.
By triangle inequality,
\mathrm{d}(r^{\prime},b^{\prime})\leq\mathrm{d}(r,b)+2\delta
.
Summing over every unit transported in
\tau
, the lemma follows.
∎

Partitioning of demand.

Now that we have quantified the error between
\Sigma_{\Box}
and
\Sigma_{ex}
,
we analyze the second source of error.
First, we bound the error in a single subproblem (i.e. one subdividing grid),
and then the error for a single pair
(r,b)\in R\times B
across
all subproblems.
We combine these two arguments to bound the expected error
due to the partitioning across all subproblems and all pairs of points.

Fix a recursive problem
\Sigma^{\prime}=({R^{\prime}},{B^{\prime}},{\lambda^{\prime}})
, with cell side length
\Delta
.
Let
\chi_{\pi}
,
R^{\prime}_{\pi}
,
B^{\prime}_{\pi}
,
(R^{\prime}_{ex})_{\pi}
,
(B^{\prime}_{ex})_{\pi}

for
\pi\in\Pi
and
R_{\Box}
,
B_{\Box}
,

,

,

, be as defined in Section

Let
\mathcal{I}=\bigcup_{\pi\in\Pi}R^{\prime}_{\pi}\times B^{\prime}_{\pi}

be the set of “local” point pairs, solved by the algorithm within
internal subproblems.
We refer to a pair

The next lemma outlines a method for deforming an arbitrary transportation

Lemma 2
.

Let
\Sigma=({R},{B},{\lambda})
be a recursive subproblem with cell side length
\Delta
,
and let
\hat{\tau}
be an arbitrary transportation map for
\Sigma
.
Let
\hat{\mathsf{X}}=\sum_{(r,b)\notin\mathcal{I}}\hat{\tau}(r,b)

be the total non-local transport in
\hat{\tau}
,
and
\mathsf{X}=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi}\chi_{\pi}

be the total extra supply of
\Sigma^{\prime}
.
Then, there exists a transportation map
\tilde{\tau}

comprising local solutions
\tilde{\tau}_{\pi}
for each
\Sigma^{\prime}_{\pi}

and a non-local solution
\tilde{\tau}_{ex}
for
\Sigma^{\prime}_{ex}
,
such that the following properties hold:

1.

The cost of the transportation map
\tilde{\tau}
is bounded above

\tfcost(\tilde{\tau})=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi}\tfcost(\tilde{\tau}_{\pi})+\tfcost(\tilde{\tau}_{ex})\leq\tfcost(\hat{\tau})+8\sqrt{2}\Delta\hat{\mathsf{X}}.

2.

The local transport in
\tilde{\tau}
satisfies

\tilde{\tau}(r,b)\geq\hat{\tau}(r,b)

,

3.

and the sum of local difference

\sum_{(r,b)\in\mathcal{I}}\left(\tilde{\tau}(r,b)-\hat{\tau}(r,b)\right)\leq 3\hat{\mathsf{X}}

.

\hat{\tau}
to create a transportation
\tilde{\tau}

consistent with
\tau

is between

, and the non-local transport between
different cells is through pairs of
R^{\prime}_{ex}\times B^{\prime}_{ex}
.
Furthermore, the non-local transport from
\pi
is exactly

.
Initially, let

.

Stage 1.

The first stage ensures that the transport within each cell of

\Pi
is maximal.
Suppose there is a cell

with two points

\tilde{\tau}(r_{1},b_{1}),\tilde{\tau}(r_{2},b_{2})>0

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}(r_{1},b_{1})=\tilde{\tau}(r_{1},b_{1})-x,\quad\tilde{\tau}(r_{2},b_{2})=\tilde{\tau}(r_{2},b_{2})-x,

(1)

,
(r_{2},b_{2})

\tilde{\tau}(r_{1},b_{1}),\tilde{\tau}(r_{2},b_{2})>0
,
where
b_{1}\not\in B_{\pi}
and
b_{2}\in B_{\pi}
.
We perform the same redistribution as (
1
)
between this new choice of
r_{1},b_{1},r_{2},b_{2}
.

(b)

When Stage 2 terminates, non-local transport

local transport in

is between local pairs (

Let
\tilde{\tau}_{\pi}
be the restriction of
\tilde{\tau}
to
the pairs in

for
\pi\in\Pi
,
and let
\tilde{\tau}_{ex}
be the restriction of

Then
\tilde{\tau}_{\pi}

and

.

We note that

Error parameter
\eta
.

In the previous lemma, we bounded the error due to a single subproblem.
We now bound the error due to a single pair of points

(r,b)\in R\times B
, using a random variable
\eta(r,b)
,
defined as the cell side length of the first recursive grid to split

(r,b)
into different cells.

Formally, recall that a recursive subproblem may split a point

p\in R\cup B
into two copies
p^{\prime}
and
p^{\prime\prime}
with

\lambda(p^{\prime})+\lambda(p^{\prime\prime})=\lambda(p)
;
one of them passed to the external subproblem,
and the other passed down to an internal subproblem.
Abusing notation slightly, we use
R
and
B
to denote the
multisets that contain all copies of points that are split along
with the updated demands.

For any base subproblem
({R^{\prime}}_{\mathrm{base}},{B^{\prime}}_{\mathrm{base}},{\lambda^{\prime}}_{\mathrm{base}})
,
every point
p\in R^{\prime}_{\mathrm{base}}\cup B^{\prime}_{\mathrm{base}}
can be identified with a point

p\in R\cup B
such that
\lambda^{\prime}_{\mathrm{base}}(p)=\lambda(p)
.
With this interpretation, we define a function

\eta:R\times B\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
as follows:
If there is a base subproblem
({R^{\prime}}_{\mathrm{base}},{B^{\prime}}_{\mathrm{base}},{\lambda^{\prime}}_{\mathrm{base}})
such that

(r,b)\in R^{\prime}_{\mathrm{base}}\times B^{\prime}_{\mathrm{base}}
, we set
\eta(r,b)=0
.
Otherwise, there is a recursive subproblem
\Sigma^{\prime}
where

(r,b)\in R^{\prime}\times B^{\prime}
, but
r
and
b
are split into
different cells of the randomly shifted grid.
In this case,
\eta(r,b)
denotes the side length of the grid cells,
i.e.
\eta(r,b)=\ell/m^{\delta}
where
\ell
is the length of the
smallest square containing
R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}
,
and
m=|R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}|
.

The next lemma bounds the expected value of the error parameter

\eta(r,b)
in terms of the distance
\mathrm{d}(r,b)

for any pair
(r,b)\in R\times B
.
Its proof uses our choice of safe grids to argue that,
though the recursion depth can be large,
the number of recursive subproblems that can potentially split
(r,b)
is small.

Lemma 3
.

There exists a constant
c_{1}>0
such that for any

(r,b)\in R\times B
, the expectation

\mathsf{E}\left[\eta(r,b)\right]\leq c_{1}\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)\mathrm{d}(r,b)
.

Proof.

We say a recursive subproblem
\Sigma=({R},{B},{\lambda})
is
relevant
if contains
r
and
b

and there is some safe random shift that splits
(r,b)
.
Suppose the algorithm creates some relevant recursive subproblem
\Sigma
over
m
points, and
let
\ell
be the side length of the smallest orthogonal bounding square of that subproblem.
The measure of each (safe) horizontal and vertical random shift
is at least

\frac{\ell}{m^{\delta}}-\frac{2\ell}{m^{3}}\cdot m=\left(1-\frac{2}{m^{2-\delta}}\right)\cdot\frac{\ell}{m^{\delta}}\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\cdot\frac{\ell}{m^{\delta}},

whereas the measure of horizontal/vertical shifts
that split
(r,b)
is at most
\mathrm{d}(r,b)
.
Hence, the probability that a safe random shift of this
grid splits
(r,b)
is at most

\frac{2\mathrm{d}(r,b)}{(1-1/m_{i})(\ell/m^{\delta})}\leq 3\mathrm{d}(r,b)\cdot\frac{m^{\delta}}{\ell}.

We say the
expected contribution
of
\Sigma
to
\eta(r,b)
is

\displaystyle\Pr[\text{grid for $\Sigma$ splits $(r,b)$}]\cdot\frac{\ell}{m^{\delta}}

\displaystyle\leq 3\mathrm{d}(r,b)\cdot\frac{m^{\delta}}{\ell}\cdot\frac{\ell}{m^{\delta}}

\displaystyle=3\mathrm{d}(r,b).

Now, let
q_{i}
be the probability that the algorithm creates at least
i
relevant recursive
subproblems.
We have

\mathsf{E}\left[\eta(r,b)\right]\leq\sum_{i\geq 1}q_{i}\cdot 3\mathrm{d}(r,b)\leq\sum_{i\geq 1:q_{i}>0}3\mathrm{d}(r,b)

Therefore, we may prove the lemma by bounding the number of relevant recursive subproblems that
arise during a single run of the algorithm by
O(\log(1/\varepsilon))
.
To do so, we will categorize these subproblems in such a way that there are

O(\log(1/\varepsilon))
categories and each category can contain at most one relevant subproblem
during a single run of the algorithm.

Recall,
n
is the number of points in the input instance.
Again, suppose the algorithm creates some relevant recursive subproblem
\Sigma
over
m
points,
and let
\ell
be the side length of the smallest orthogonal bounding square of that subproblem.
We may assume that
m>n^{\varepsilon/4}
, since otherwise the subproblem is a base case and
therefore not relevant.

Define
\underline{\ell}:=\mathrm{d}(r,b)/\sqrt{2}
;
clearly,
\ell\geq\underline{\ell}
.
We partition the interval
[n^{\varepsilon/4},n]
into

u=\lceil\log_{2}(4/\varepsilon)\rceil
intervals of the form

[n_{j},n_{j}^{2}]
, where
n_{j}=n^{2^{-j}}
, for
1\leq j\leq u
.
There exists an index
j^{*}
where
m\in[n_{j^{*}},n_{j^{*}}^{2}]
,
and
\ell\leq\overline{\ell}:=(n_{j^{*}}^{2})^{3}\mathrm{d}(r,b)

because the grid is safe.
Thus,
\ell\in[\underline{\ell},\overline{\ell}]
,
where
\overline{\ell}/\underline{\ell}=\sqrt{2}n_{j^{*}}^{6}
.

The interval
[\underline{\ell},\overline{\ell}]
can be
covered by
7/\delta>(1/\delta)(6+\log_{2}(\sqrt{2})/\log_{2}(n_{j^{*}}))

intervals of the form

J_{i}=[n^{i\delta}_{j^{*}}\underline{\ell},n^{(i+1)\delta}_{j^{*}}\underline{\ell}]

for
0\leq i\leq 7/\delta=O(1)
.
For each value of
i,j
, the algorithm produces at most one subproblem containing a number of
points in
[n_{j},n_{j}^{2}]
and bounding square side length in
J_{i}
.
The total number of intervals is no more than

\lceil\log_{2}(4/\varepsilon)\rceil\cdot 7/\delta=O(\log(1/\varepsilon))
.
∎

Expected cost of algorithm.

We are now ready to analyze the expected cost of
\tau
,
the algorithm’s transportation map.
First, we analyze the cost if
\Sigma
has bounded spread.
Recall that, in this case, our algorithm computes an optimal solution

Lemma 4
.

If
\Sigma

and

Corollary 1
.

If

\mathsf{E}[\tfcost(\tau)]=O(\log(1/\varepsilon))\tfcost(\tau^{*})

is an optimal transport

The general case.

We now analyze the cost of the transportation map for the general case,
when the spread of
R\cup B
is arbitrary.

Recall the categorization of recursive subproblems as primary, secondary,
or tertiary based on the number of external subproblem invocations on
its path in the recursion tree (see Figure
2
).
We now introduce two functions

\eta_{1},\eta_{2}:R\times B\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

corresponding to the errors introduced in the primary and secondary recursions.
(Note that tertiary subproblems are solved exactly; hence, there is
no error introduced in solving a tertiary subproblem.)

The function
\eta_{1}
corresponds to the primary recursion and is
the same as the
\eta
defined before, i.e.,
\eta_{1}(r,b)=0

if
(r,b)
belongs to a primary base subproblem,
otherwise it is the length of the grid cell at the subproblem which
splits
r
and
b
.
The function
\eta_{2}(r,b)
corresponds to the secondary recursion for

(r,b)
.
If
(r,b)
belongs to a primary base problem
(i.e. does not appear in any secondary recursion),
then we set
\eta_{2}(r,b)=0
.
Otherwise, let
\bar{r}\in R_{\Box}
and
\bar{b}\in B_{\Box}

be the centers of the grid cells of the primary subproblem where

r
and
b
were split. Then,
\eta_{2}(r,b)
is defined
to be
\eta(\bar{r},\bar{b})
for
the secondary recursion on
(R_{\Box},B_{\Box})
.
From this definition, we observe that:

Lemma 5
.

1.

\eta_{2}(r,b)=\eta(r_{\pi_{1}},b_{\pi_{2}})
, for
(r,b)\in(R_{ex})_{\pi_{1}}\times(B_{ex})_{\pi_{2}}

2.

\eta_{1}(r,b)=\Delta
for
(r,b)\in R_{ex}\times B_{ex}

3.

\eta_{1}(r,b),\eta_{2}(r,b)\leq\Delta/(n^{\varepsilon/4})^{\delta}\leq\Delta/12
for
(r,b)\in R_{\pi}\times B_{\pi}

We now state two lemmas that are counterparts of Lemmas

3
and
4

for the general case.

Lemma 6
.

For any pair
(r,b)\in R\times B
,

\mathsf{E}\left[\eta_{2}(r,b)\right]=O(\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon))\mathrm{d}(r,b)
.

Proof.

Let
\bar{r},\bar{b}
be the grid centers as defined earlier; then

\mathrm{d}(\bar{r},\bar{b})\leq\mathrm{d}(r,b)+2\sqrt{2}\eta_{1}(r,b)/2
.
We first prove the expectation of
\eta_{2}(r,b)

conditioned on the value of
\eta_{1}(r,b)
.
By Lemma
3
,

\mathsf{E}\left[\eta_{2}(r,b)\mid\eta_{1}(r,b)\right]=O(\log(1/\varepsilon))\mathrm{d}(\bar{r},\bar{b})\leq O(\log(1/\varepsilon))\left(\mathrm{d}(r,b)+\sqrt{2}\eta_{1}(r,b)\right).

By applying Lemma
3
again, we obtain

\mathsf{E}\left[\eta_{2}(r,b)\right]\leq O(\log(1/\varepsilon))\left[\mathrm{d}(r,b)+\mathsf{E}\left[\eta_{1}(r,b)\right]\right]\leq O(\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon))\mathrm{d}(r,b).\qed

Lemma 7
.

There exists a constant
c_{3}>0

2.3
An efficient implementation

We now explain how the various steps of the algorithm are implemented
to run in
O(n^{1+\varepsilon})
time.
There are three main steps in the algorithm:

1.

partitioning a recursive subproblem
\Sigma^{\prime}=({R^{\prime}},{B^{\prime}},{\lambda^{\prime}})
into
internal subproblems and an external subproblem;

2.

solving subproblems recursively;

3.

recovering the transportation map
\tau
of
\Sigma^{\prime}

from the internal and external solutions
\tau_{\pi}

(
\pi\in\Pi
) and
\tau_{\Box}
.

A recursive subproblem partitions its points into internal subproblems,
but generates an additional set of points (at cell centers) for its
external subproblem; let us call these
external points
.
We bound the number of external points generated in the next lemma.

Lemma 8
.

The total number of external points over all recursive subproblems is
O(n)
.

Proof.

We first prove the lemma in the case of bounded spread
(external subproblems are solved exactly).
Each external point
p
is the center of a cell
\pi
that is either
bichromatic (contains points of both colors)
or monochromatic (only one color).
If
\pi
is monochromatic,
we charge
p
to any of the
R_{ex}\cup B_{ex}\subseteq R\cup B

points in
\pi
— by construction, these points are omitted from the internal
subproblems and do not appear in any later recursive subproblems.
If
\pi
is bichromatic,
we charge
p
to the internal subproblem created on
\pi
.
Thus, the number of external points is at most

(n+[\text{\# internal subproblems}])
.

When spread is bounded,
all internal subproblems are in the primary recursion.
Consider a problem instance
\Sigma
with
m
points, and let
I(m)

denote the maximum number of internal subproblems
that can be recursively generated by it.
We will inductively prove that
I(m)\leq 2m-1
.
If the subdividing grid for
\Sigma
generates a single
internal subproblem, then that subproblem contains at most

m-1
points. By the inductive hypothesis, this subproblem
generates at most
2(m-1)-1
points. Adding
\Sigma
itself
gives a total of
2m-2<2m-1
internal subproblems.
On the other hand, if the subdividing grid for
\Sigma

generates
k>1
internal subproblems with

m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{k}
points respectively, then

\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}\leq m
since each point can be
inherited into at most one internal subproblem.
Using the inductive hypothesis on these subproblems
gives a bound of
\sum_{i=1}^{k}(2m_{i}-1)\leq 2m-k

on the number of internal subproblems generated recursively.
Adding
\Sigma
to this sum
gives a total of
2m-k+1\leq 2m-1
internal subproblems.
It follows that for an input instance of bounded spread with

n
points, the number of internal subproblems is at most
2n
,
and the number of external points is at most
3n
.

In the general case, we can apply a similar analysis
for the subproblems of the primary recursion,
although there are now external points and internal subproblems
in the secondary recursion.
However, we can apply the bounded spread analysis to each
external subproblem that starts a secondary recursion.
We have

\displaystyle[\text{\# secondary internal subproblems}]

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot[\text{\# primary external points}]

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot(n+[\text{\# primary internal subproblems}])

\displaystyle\leq 2(n+(2n))\leq 6n.

Hence, the number of secondary external points is at most
(n+6n)=7n
,
and the total number of external points is at most
3n+7n=10n=O(n)
.
∎

A base subproblem of size
n_{i}\leq n^{\varepsilon/4}
is solved in

O(n_{i}^{3}\log n_{i})
time using Orlin’s algorithm.
We distribute this on the
n_{i}
points by charging

O(n_{i}^{2}\log n_{i})=O(n^{\varepsilon/2}\log n)
to each point.
Note every point in
R\cup B
, as well as every external point,
belongs to at most one base subproblem.
Since, by Lemma
8
, the
number of external points is
O(n)
, it follows that the total time spent
solving base subproblems is

O(n)\cdot O(n^{\varepsilon/2}\log n)=O(n^{1+\varepsilon})
.

The time spent in recovering the transportation map
\tau
for
\Sigma^{\prime}

from its internal and external subproblems is proportional to the number of
external points in
\Sigma^{\prime}
.
Hence, by Lemma
8
,
step
3
takes
O(n)
time.

Finally, the implementation of step
1
depends on whether
the instance
\Sigma=({R},{B},{\lambda})
has bounded spread.

The bounded spread case.

In this case, step
1
is implemented naively.
We choose a random shift, distribute the points of
R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}

among the grid cells in
O(m\log m)
time, where
|R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}|=m
,
and check in additional
O(m)
time whether the shift is safe.
So step
1
can be implemented in
O(m\log m)
expected time.
We charge
O(\log m)
time to each point of
R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}
.
Since the spread is
n^{O(1)}
, the depth of recursion is
O(1/\varepsilon)
.
Therefore, each input point is charged
O(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log n)
units of time,
implying that steps
1
over all subproblems
take
O(n\log n)
expected time total (recall that
\varepsilon
is a constant).
As the size of the external subproblem at
R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}
is
O(m^{2\delta})
,
and
\delta=1/6
, the time for solving it exactly is
O(m\log m)
.
Again, over all levels of recursion, the total time spent solving external subproblems is
O(n\log n)
.

We can neither spend linear time at a recursive subproblem, nor can we afford

To avoid spending

We build a 2D dynamic orthogonal range searching data structure that

•

Using a range-tree based data structure, each operation except for

[

.

We maintain two copies

The first one is initialized with

We use

With each recursive subproblem

For the root problem,

We call

\mathcal{R}\cap\rho_{\pi}=R^{\prime}_{\pi}

\mathcal{B}\cap\rho_{\pi}=B^{\prime}_{\pi}
,
and their weights are consistent with
\lambda^{\prime}_{\pi}
.

\mathbb{D}_{R}
and
\mathbb{D}_{B}
can also be used to test whether
the random shift is safe:
For each
\pi\in\Pi
, we check whether the moat of any point in

(R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime})\cap\pi
intersects an edge of
\pi
.
This is equivalent to checking whether

\Box_{\pi}\cap(R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime})=\emptyset
,
where
\Box_{\pi}
is the set of points that are within distance
\ell/m^{3}

from the boundary of
\pi
.
This test can be done in
O(\log^{2}n)
time using the
Empty
procedure.
The total expected time spent in generating internal subproblems
\Sigma^{\prime}_{\pi}

and external subproblems
\Sigma^{\prime}_{\Box}
of
\Sigma
is

O(|\Pi|\log^{3}n+m_{\Box}\log^{2}n)
,
where
m_{\Box}
is the total number of external points.

By Lemma
8
,
the total number of nonempty cells over all subproblems is
O(n)
,
and the number of external points is
O(n)
.
Thus, the expected time spent in step
1
overall is
O(n\log^{3}n)
.
Putting everything together, we obtain the main result of this section.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\Sigma
be an instance of the transportation problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d
is a constant.
Let
\Sigma
have size
n
, and let
\varepsilon>0
be a constant.
A transportation map of
\Sigma
can be computed in
O(n^{1+\varepsilon})

expected time whose expected cost is
O(\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon))\tfcost(\tau^{*})
,
where
\tau^{*}
is an optimal transport of
\Sigma
.

2.4
Extension to bounded doubling dimension

The
doubling dimension
of a metric space is the minimum
D

such that every radius
2r
ball can be covered by at most
2^{D}

balls of radius
r
.
When
D
is a constant, we say the metric space is
doubling
.
We now describe the changes to the algorithm when we are
given oracle access to a doubling metric
\mathrm{d}(\cdot,\cdot)
.
This result has a logarithmic dependence on the spread
\Phi
,
and therefore gives the same asymptotic running times only when

\Phi=n^{O(1)}
.
The dependence on spread exists because we lack the
“efficient datastructures” of Section
2.3

for doubling metrics.

A regular grid is not well-defined in this setting,
but we can replace the partitioning from a randomly shifted grid
with a partitioning generated by the
randomized split-tree
of Talwar
[
26
]
.
In the rest of this section, we will describe that this new partitioning
has similar bounds on “cell” count, “cell” diameter, and point separation probability.
Afterwards, we state the split-tree construction in-depth,
which allows us to define and efficiently implement a notion of “safety” for the new subdivision.

2.4.1
Replacing subdividing grid

A split-tree is a hierarchical decomposition of the metric,
and Talwar’s construction has properties reminiscent of a
randomly shifted quadtree in Euclidean space.
Each node of the split-tree corresponds to a cluster of points,
whose children are a partitioning of itself, and so forth.
The root contains all points, and each leaf contains a single point.

Formally, we number the
levels
of the tree,
with the root at level
L=O(\log\ell)
,
where
\ell
is the diameter of the input point set.
The children of level
i
nodes at level
(i-1)
.
Similar to the quadtree, the hierarchy contracts cluster diameters by
1/2
in successive levels.
The root of the tree is a cluster of all points,
whereas leaves are singleton clusters for each point.
Talwar’s construction (which we describe next) has the following properties:

1.

A level
i
cluster has diameter at most
2^{i+1}
.

2.

A level
i
cluster is comprised of at most

2^{O(D)}
level
(i+1)
clusters.

3.

For any two points
r,b
,
the probability that
r
and
b
lie in different
level
i
clusters is at most

O(D)\cdot\mathrm{d}(r,b)/2^{i}
.

Recall that we use
m
to denote the number of points in a recursive instance on
R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}
.
For a point partitioning analagous to an
[m^{\delta}]^{D}
grid,
it suffices to return the
(L-\delta\log_{2}m)
-level of the randomized split-tree.
Denote the partitioning induced by this level as
\mathbb{G}_{D}
.

Even in the Euclidean case,
\delta
is a constant parameter that depended on the dimension.
For
\mathbb{G}_{D}
, let the fanout bound in (
2
) be
2^{c^{\prime}D}
, for some constant
c^{\prime}>0
.
For the doubling metric, we choose
\delta=(3c^{\prime}D)^{-1}
, to achieve
O(m^{c^{\prime}D\delta})=O(m^{1/3})
.
With this choice of
\delta
, the external subproblem at
\mathbb{G}_{D}
can be solved in
O(m)
time.

Lemma 9
.

Let
R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}
be a set of
m
points in a doubling metric, and
\ell
be their diameter.
For a parameter
\delta>0
, let
\mathbb{G}_{D}
be the partitioning of
R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}
given by the

(L-\delta\log_{2}m)
-level of the randomized split tree.
Then for
\Delta=\ell/m^{\delta}
,

1.

\mathbb{G}_{D}
consists of
O(m^{c^{\prime}D\delta})=O(m^{1/3})
clusters.

2.

Each cluster in
\mathbb{G}_{D}
has diameter
2^{L}/m^{\delta}=c\Delta
,
for some constant
c>0
.

3.

For any
(r,b)\in R^{\prime}\times B^{\prime}
,
the probability that
r
and
b
lie in different
clusters of
\mathbb{G}_{D}
is at most
O(\mathrm{d}(r,b)/\Delta)
.

Proof.

Use level
(L-\delta\log_{2}m)
in the randomized split-tree properties.
∎

\mathbb{G}_{D}
can be made “safe” in a similar way as the Euclidean case.
Formally, we say
\mathbb{G}_{D}
is
safe
if,
for all
(r,b)\in R^{\prime}\times B^{\prime}
where
\mathrm{d}(r,b)\leq c\Delta/m^{3}
,

r,b
lie in the same cluster of
\mathbb{G}_{D}
.
Here,
c
is the constant that appears in the diameter bound for a
\mathbb{G}_{D}
cluster.
To describe the changes and demonstrate that they can be implemented efficiently,
we first detail Talwar’s construction algorithm.

2.4.2
Talwar’s construction

Suppose a set
S
of
m
points in the doubling metric.
Talwar’s construction builds the split-tree from a
net hierarchy
.
An
r
-net
of a point set
S
is a subset
N\subset S
which satisfies:

•

Covering
: for all
p\in S
, there exists some
q\in N
with
\mathrm{d}(p,q)\leq r
.

•

Packing
: for all
p,q\in N
,
\mathrm{d}(p,q)\geq r
.

Now, suppose that we build a nested series of nets
S=N_{0},N_{1},\ldots,N_{L}

such that
N_{i}
is a
2^{i-2}
-net of
N_{i-1}
.
Note that
N_{L}
consists of a single point.
Such a net hierarchy can be constructed in
2^{O(D)}m\log m
expected time using
the algorithm of Har-Peled and Mendel
[
17
]
.

Given a net hierarchy on
S
, Talwar’s algorithm is as follows.
We will use
\mathcal{C}_{i}
to denote the set of clusters at level
i
.

1.

Pick
\rho
uniformly at random from
[1/2,1)
,
a random permutation
\pi
of
R\cup B
,
and initialize
\mathcal{C}_{L}=\{R\cup B\}
.

2.

Repeat for each level
i
, from
L
to 0.
Let
r_{i}=2^{i}\rho
.
For each cluster
C\in\mathcal{C}_{i+1}
, we generate its children clusters
(into
\mathcal{C}_{i}
) by iterating through the net points
N_{i}
in order of
\pi
.
The points of
N_{i}
will form the cluster centers at
\mathcal{C}_{i}
.
For each
p\in N_{i}\cap C
in order of
\pi
, create a new cluster from the yet-unassigned points of
C
in the
r_{i}
-ball about
p
.
Formally, we create the child cluster
C^{\prime}(p)
defined as

C^{\prime}(p):=\{q\in C\mid\text{$\mathrm{d}(p,q)\leq r_{i}$, and $\forall q^{\prime}\in N_{i}$ where $\pi(q^{\prime})<\pi(q)$, $\mathrm{d}(p,q^{\prime})>r_{i}$}\}.

Talwar’s construction can be implemented in
O(2^{O(D)}m)
time per level.
The doubling property ensures that
|N_{i}\cap C|=2^{O(D)}
,
so we can compare each point naively against all the points of
N_{i}\cap C
.
The total time to construct the split-tree is
O(m\log m)+O(mL)=O(m\log m+m\log\Phi)
time.
To find
\mathbb{G}_{D}
, we stop the construction after
\delta\log_{2}m
levels,
in time
O(m\log m+m\delta\log m)=O(m\log m)
(recall
\delta=1/6
).

2.4.3
\mathbb{G}_{D}
safety

To determine if
\mathbb{G}_{D}
is safe, it suffices to check a local condition
at every split-tree cluster from level
L
to
(L-\delta\log_{2}m)
.
First, we define two predicates for a separation property between a cluster and points outside it.
Let
C
be a split-tree cluster with center
q
, construction radius
r
, and points
S\supseteq C
.

•

P(C,S)
: for all
p\in S\setminus C
and
p^{\prime}\in C
,
\mathrm{d}(p,p^{\prime})>c\Delta/m^{3}
.

•

Q(C,S)
: for all
p\in S\setminus C
,
\mathrm{d}(p,q)>r+c\Delta/m^{3}
.

By definition,
\mathbb{G}_{D}
is safe if
P(C,R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime})
holds for all
C\in\mathbb{G}_{D}
.

Lemma 10
.

For a split-tree cluster
C
, let
\operatorname{par}(C)
be its parent cluster.
If
Q(C,\operatorname{par}(C))
holds for all
C
in levels
L
to
(L-\delta\log_{2}m)
,
then
P(C^{\prime},R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime})
holds for all
C^{\prime}\in\mathbb{G}_{D}
.

Proof.

We prove, by induction on levels, that
P(C,R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime})
for clusters at every level in the range.
The root cluster
C_{root}
(at level
L
) excludes no vertices, so
P(C_{root},R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime})
holds vacuously.

Suppose the level
i+1
clusters have
P(C,R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime})
.
Consider a level
i
cluster
C
with center
q
and radius
r
.
For any
p\not\in C
, either

1.

p\not\in\operatorname{par}(C)
as well, or

2.

p\in\operatorname{par}(C)
, and was first separated from the points of
C
at level
i
.

If
p\not\in\operatorname{par}(C)
, then for any
p^{\prime}\in C
we have

P(\operatorname{par}(C),R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime})\implies\mathrm{d}(p,p^{\prime})>c\Delta/m^{3}
,
as
C
is a subset of
\operatorname{par}(C)
.
Otherwise
p\in\operatorname{par}(C)
, and we utilize the assumption that
Q(C,\operatorname{par}(C))
holds.
For any
p^{\prime}\in C
, triangle inequality implies that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}(p,p^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{d}(p,q)-\mathrm{d}(p^{\prime},q)

\displaystyle>r+c\Delta/m^{3}-r

\displaystyle=c\Delta/m^{3}

Since the separation property holds for all points of
\overline{C}
,
we have established
P(C,R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime})
for all level
i
clusters.
The lemma follows by induction down to the
(L-\delta\log_{2}m)
-level,
\mathbb{G}_{D}
.
∎

This suggests an efficient procedure for checking if
\mathbb{G}_{D}
is safe.
While constructing
\mathbb{G}_{D}
through the randomized split-tree,
we verify
Q(C,\operatorname{par}(C))
point-by-point as we add them to clusters.
Since we already do these naive distance comparisons to cluster the points,
the asymptotic running time of the construction stays the same.
Like in the Euclidean case, we restart the construction with new random parameters
if
Q(C,\operatorname{par}(C))
fails on any cluster.
We finish this section by showing that a random
\mathbb{G}_{D}
is safe with high probability,

Lemma 11
.

The randomized split-tree construction produces a safe
\mathbb{G}_{D}
with probability
\Omega(1-1/m^{5/3})
.

Proof.

We prove that
\mathbb{G}_{D}
satisfies
Q(C,\operatorname{par}(C))
at every cluster
C

with probability
\Omega(1-1/m^{5/3})
.
By Lemma
10
,
\mathbb{G}_{D}
meeting this condition is safe.

Consider the random radius parameter,
\rho\in[1/2,1)
, fixed at the start of the construction.
At level
i
, the split-tree construction choosese cluster radii
r_{i}=2^{i}\rho
.
For a cluster
C
with center
q
,
Q(C,\operatorname{par}(C))
fails if the
circle of radius
r_{i}
centered at
q
intersects the
(c\Delta/m^{3})
-ball
of any point outside of it.
The radial measure of these balls is no more than

m\cdot\frac{2c\Delta}{m^{3}}\leq\frac{4r_{i}}{m^{2}}\leq\frac{4\cdot 2^{i}}{m^{2}}

as
\rho c\Delta
is the smallest cluster radius and
\rho\geq 1/2
.
Then, the probability that
rho
causes
C
to fail
Q(C,\operatorname{par}(C))
is no more than

\frac{4\cdot 2^{i}/m^{2}}{2^{i}-2^{i-1}}=\frac{2^{i+2}}{2^{i-1}m^{2}}=\frac{8}{m^{2}}.

By Lemma
9
the number of
\mathbb{G}_{D}
clusters is
O(m^{1/3})
.
Since the split-tree has constant degree, the total number of clusters is also
O(m^{1/3})
.
By union bound, the probability that any
C
fails is no more than

O(m^{1/3})\cdot\frac{8}{m^{2}}=O(m^{-5/3})

and the probability that no
C
fails is
\Omega(1-1/m^{5/3})
.
∎

3
A
(1+\epsilon)
-Approximate Algorithm

In this section, we describe a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm based
on a reduction to minimum cost flow.
We begin by defining the minimum cost flow problem.
Our use of minimum cost flow in this section is relatively black box,
so we postpone some algorithmic definitions until Section
4
,
like the residual graph and the dual problem.

3.1
Minimum cost flow definitions

Let
G_{0}=(V,E)
be a directed graph with
n
vertices and
m
arcs,
with
costs

c:E\to\mathbb{R}
,
capacities

u:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
and signed supplies/demands
\phi:V\to\mathbb{Z}
where

\sum_{v\in V}\phi(v)=0
.
We call
\phi(\cdot)

flow-supply
(when
\phi(v)>0
) or

flow-demand
(when
\phi(v)<0
) to distinguish from the
nonnegative supply-demand function
\lambda(\cdot)
used for transportation.
We use
C=\max_{e\in E}c(e)
and
U=\max_{v\in V}|\phi(v)|
;
it is assumed that the costs are scaled such that the minimum arc cost is 1.

A
pseudoflow
is an arc function
f:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfying the

capacity constraints

f(v,w)\leq u(v,w)\qquad\forall(v,w)\in E.

If an arc
(v,w)\in E
has
f(v,w)>0
, we say it is
active
; otherwise it
is said to be
idle
.
We call the active arcs of a pseudoflow its
support
.
The cost of a pseudoflow is

\cost(f):=\sum_{e\in E}c(v,w)f(v,w).

With respect to a pseudoflow
f
, the
imbalance
of a vertex
v\in V
is

e_{f}(v):=\phi(v)+\sum_{(w,v)\in E}f(w,v)-\sum_{(v,w)\in E}f(v,w).

We refer to vertices with
e_{f}(v)>0
as
excess vertices
, and those
with
e_{f}(v)<0
as
deficit vertices
.

If imbalance is zero on all vertices,
f
is a
flow
.
The
minimum cost flow
problem (MCF) is to find a flow
f^{*}
of minimum
cost.
Transportation can be formulated as a special case of MCF on the complete
bipartite graph, using
V=R\cup B
,
E=R\times B

directed
R
to
B
,
c(r,b)=\|r-b\|
,
setting flow-supplies
\phi(r)=\lambda(r)
for
r\in R

and flow-demands
\phi(b)=-\lambda(b)
for
b\in B
.
The transportation map can be recovered from a flow solution as

\tau(a,b)=f(a,b)\forall a\in A,b\in B
.

3.2
Algorithm outline

As in Section
2
, we hierarchically cluster points,
but this time for the purpose of approximately representing
all
\Theta(n^{2})
pairwise distances between
R
and
B
compactly.
At a high level, our algorithm is as follows:

1.

Compute a hierarchical clustering of
R\cup B

using a quadtree with a single input point in each leaf.

2.

Construct a sparse directed acyclic graph

H=(V,E)

over the clusters with
R
and
B
inheriting their supplies
and demands respectively from the transportation instance.

H
has the following property:
for every pair
(r,b)\in R\times B
, there is a unique path
in
H
from
r
to
b
, with cost roughly
\mathrm{d}(r,b)
.
That is, the per-unit cost of flow from
r
to
b
in
H

approximates the per-unit cost of transporting from
r
to
b
in the
original metric.

3.

Compute an optimal flow
f^{*}
in

using a minimum
cost flow algorithm.
To achieve our advertized running time, we use the algorithm by
Lee and Sidford
[
20
]
.

4.

Recover a transportation map
\tau
from

.

The hierarchical structure is the foundation for the compact representation
– a well-separated pair decomposition
[
11
]
–
and further enables a near-linear time procedure for
recovering the transportation map (step

).
This fast recovery step is what distinguishes our algorithm from the similar reduction in
Cabello
et al.

[
9
]
, which uses

spanners

[
10
,
7
,
6
]
instead.
As black boxes, geometric spanners seem to be insufficient
for efficient recovery of the transportation map.

We construct a

A compressed quadtree prunes certain interior nodes of a standard quadtree,

[

]
.

Each node

\Box_{v}

For each node

If

Well-separated pair decomposition.

Originally proposed by Callahan and Kosaraju
[
11
]
,
the notion of a
well-separated pair decomposition
(WSPD) of a
point set
S
is widely used to represent the pairwise distances of

S
approximately in a compact manner.
The well-separated pair decomposition of a point set
P
is a set of
subset pairs
\mathcal{D}=\{(A_{1},B_{1}),\ldots,(A_{s},B_{s})\}
with
A_{i},B_{i}\subseteq S
where:

•

For every
p,q\in S
,
there is a unique pair
(A_{i},B_{i})\in\mathcal{D}

such that
p\in A_{i}
and
q\in B_{i}
(or vice versa).

•

Let
c_{\min}(A_{i},B_{i})
be the minimum distance between a
p\in A_{i}
and
q\in B_{i}
.
Every pair
(A_{i},B_{i})\in\mathcal{D}
is
well-separated
:
For a parameter
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\max\left\{\diam(A_{i}),\diam(B_{i})\right\}\leq\delta\cdot c_{\min}(u,v)
.

A simple WSPD construction using compressed quadtrees is described
in Chapter 3 of
[
16
]
.
It produces a WSPD of size
O(n/\delta^{d})
in
O(n/\delta^{d})
time, provided a compressed quadtree on
S
.

We set
\delta=\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}
.
For a pair of quadtree nodes
u,v\in T^{\uparrow}\times T
, we define

c_{\min}(u,v):=\min\{\mathrm{d}(x,y)\mid x\in\Box_{u},y\in\Box_{v}\}
.
Using the algorithm in
[
16
]
, we compute a pair decomposition

\mathcal{D}\subseteq V^{\uparrow}\times V^{\downarrow}
with the following properties:

(W1)

For every
(r,b)\in R\times B
,
there is a unique pair
(u,v)\in\mathcal{D}
such that

r\in R_{u},b\in B_{v}
.

(W2)

For every
(u,v)\in\mathcal{D}
,

\displaystyle\max\left\{\diam(\Box_{u}),\diam(\Box_{v})\right\}\leq\delta\cdot c_{\min}(u,v)
.

(W3)

|\mathcal{D}|=O(n/\varepsilon^{d})
.

Given
T
, this algorithm constructs
\mathcal{D}
in
O(n/\varepsilon^{d})
time.
We set
\overrightarrow{E}=\mathcal{D}
with each arc oriented from
T^{\uparrow}
to
T^{\downarrow}
.
The cost of each arc in

c(u,v)=\begin{cases}\diam(\Box_{u}\cup\Box_{v})&\text{if $(u,v)\in\overrightarrow{E}$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

By triangle inequality and
(W2)
, this ensures that

\mathrm{d}(r,b)\leq c(u,v)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mathrm{d}(r,b)

(7)

for any

,

, with
(u,v)\in\mathcal{D}
the unique pair separating them.

To complete the description of the min-cost flow instance,
we specify the flow-supply/flow-demand
\phi^{\prime}
at vertices of

.

\phi^{\prime}(v)=\begin{cases}\lambda(\ell_{R}(v))&\text{if $v$ is a red leaf in $T^{\uparrow}$}\\
-\lambda(\ell_{B}(v))&\text{if $v$ is a blue leaf in $T^{\downarrow}$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\\
\end{cases}

Let
(H,c,\phi^{\prime})
be the resulting minimum cost flow instance.
The total time spent in constructing
H
is
O(n\log n+n/\varepsilon^{d})
.

3.4
Cost analysis

Flow moves up from the leaves of
T^{\uparrow}
,
through the cross edges into
T^{\downarrow}
,
and finally descends to the sinks at leaves of
T^{\downarrow}
.
By construction and
(W1)
,
any pair
(r,b)\in R\times B
has a unique path

p(r,b)
from
r
to
b
in
H
which uses a single cross edge.
We can map any transport
\tau
(injectively) to a feasible pseudoflow

f_{\tau}
on
G
, by placing a pseudoflow of
\tau(r,b)
on
p(r,b)
.
Similarly, any pseudoflow
f
can be mapped to a feasible
transportation
\tau_{f}
by
decomposing

f
into pseudoflow on paths from supply vertices to demand vertices:
by the classical flow decomposition theorem,

f
can be decomposed
into a set
\{f(p(r,b)\}
of pseudoflows on the paths
p(r,b)

(since
G
is a directed acyclic graph,
any decomposition has no flow cycles, only paths).
Then, setting
\tau_{f}(r,b)=f(p(r,b))
for all

(r,b)\in R\times B
is a feasible transportation.

Applying Equation
7
to each path flow,

\tfcost(f_{\tau})\leq\tfcost(\tau)
and

\tfcost(\tau_{f})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\tfcost(f)
.
We can apply these transformations to bound the approximation quality
of a transportation recovered by decomposing the
optimal
pseudoflow

f^{*}
of
H
.

\tfcost(\tau^{*})\leq\tfcost(\tau_{f^{*}})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\tfcost(f^{*})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\tfcost(f_{\tau^{*}})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\tfcost(\tau^{*})

Then, by computing the optimal pseudoflow
f^{*}
on
H
,
a transportation solution
\tau_{f^{*}}
constructed by a flow decomposition of

f^{*}
meets the claimed approximation quality.
In the next subsection, we explain how to compute such a decomposition efficiently.
This cost analysis applies regardless of the specific flow decomposition of
f^{*}
.
Our recovery procedure in
4
is a greedy decomposition.

3.5
Recovering a transportation map

Let
f^{*}
be the optimal pseudoflow from
R
to
B
in
H
.
We use a two-part greedy algorithm to decompose
f^{*}
:
assigning the flow from
R
to the cross edges through the up-tree,
then claiming the assigned flow using the down-tree.
Both steps amount to performing a flow decomposition on
the portion of the flow lying in each tree,
treating the cross edge endpoints as sinks (resp., sources)
with demand equal to the sum of outgoing (resp., incoming) flow.
Both are arborescences, so flow decomposition can be done with a
postorder traversal.
After solving both trees, we combine the paths assigned into
and out of each cross edge to find end-to-end path pseudoflows.

We only describe the decomposition for
T^{\uparrow}
in detail;
it is nearly identical for
T^{\downarrow}
.
For each cross edge
(u,v)\in\overrightarrow{E}
, this produces lists

A_{R}(u,v)
and
A_{B}(u,v)
which hold pairs
(p,F)

indicating
point
p\in R\cup B

contributes
F
units of the flow through
(u,v)
.
The tree is processed in postorder:
a node is visited only after all its children.
Denote the children of node
u
by
\mathrm{children}(u)
.
Each node
u\in T^{\uparrow}
maintains a list
L(u)
of the
positive-demand red points in its subtree,
and a list
N(u)
of the positive-flow cross edges leaving
u
.

L(u)
is initialized by joining lists
L(w)
from each
w\in\mathrm{children}(u)

(at the leaves, we initialize
L(r)=\{r\}
for
r\in R
).
While
N(u)
is not empty, let
(u,v)\in N(u)
with flow
f^{*}(u,v)>0
.
Take any point
r\in L(u)
and add to
A_{R}(u,v)
a pair

(r,F)
, with
F=\min\{\phi(r),f^{*}(u,v)\}
,
also updating
\phi(r)\leftarrow\phi(r)-F
and

f^{*}(u,v)\leftarrow f^{*}(u,v)-F
.
This has the effect of removing either
r
from
L(u)
,

(u,v)
from
N(u)
, or both.
Once
N(u)=\emptyset
, all cross edges leaving
u
have
their flow assigned.

Finally, we complete the decomposition using
A_{R}(u,v)

and
A_{B}(u,v)
, for each cross edge
(u,v)
.
While both
A_{R}(u,v)
and
A_{B}(u,v)
are nonempty,
let
(r,F_{r})\in A_{R}(u,v)
and
(b,F_{b})\in A_{B}(u,v)
.
Output
\tau(r,b):=\min\{F_{r},F_{b}\}
, update

F_{r}\leftarrow F_{r}-\tau(r,b)
and
F_{b}\leftarrow F_{b}-\tau(r,b)
,
and remove from the lists any pair
(p,F)
for which
F=0
.

We describe a charging scheme to prove this recovery routine
takes
O(n/\varepsilon^{d})
time.
We charge the list union which constructs
L(u)
to the children of
u
.
Each iteration performs a constant number of list operations and either
removes a node from
L(u)
, or a cross edge from
N(u)
.
Each removal occurs exactly once for every
r\in R

and
(u,v)\in\overrightarrow{E}
,
so we charge iterations to the
r\in R
or
(u,v)\in\overrightarrow{E}

removed that iteration.
The processing of
A_{R}(u,v)
and
A_{B}(u,v)
can also
be charged per iteration to the pair
(p,F)
removed in that iteration,
which is then charged back to the
p\in R\cup B
or

(u,v)\in\overrightarrow{E}
whose removal introduced
(p,F)
.
Thus, the total recovery time is

O(|V|+|\overrightarrow{E}|)=O(n/\varepsilon^{d})
.

We computed an optimal pseudoflow
f^{*}:E\to\mathbb{N}
using
the algorithm by Lee and Sidford
[
20
]
.
Since
|E|=O(n/\varepsilon^{d})
, the Lee-Sidford algorithm
takes
\tilde{O}(n^{3/2}\varepsilon^{-d}\polylog U)
time;
where
U=\max_{p\in R\cup B}\phi(p)
is the maximum demand,
solving the minimum cost flow problem dominates the running time
over the construction of
H
and recovery steps.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\Sigma
be an instance of the transportation problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
where

,

3.6
Extension to doubling metric

As in Section

,
we use an analagous structure for the grid-based partitioning.
Here, we seek to replace the WSPD built from a compressed quadtree.

Since we use the WSPD in a relatively black-box fashion,

[

This construction is a randomized algorithm which builds a WSPD for doubling metrics

To elaborate, their main result is a fast construction for an

In this section, we present an

To this end, we make modifications to an uncapacitated minimum cost flow

Given an instance

4.1
Preliminaries: improving flows and optimality conditions

For the rest of this section, we work with an uncapacitated input network,
where
u(v,w)=\infty
for every
(v,w)\in E
.
Given a pseudoflow
f
, we define the capacitated
residual network
as
follows.
For each arc
e=(v,w)\in E
, create an arc in the reverse direction

e^{R}=(w,v)
and call the set of reverse arcs
E^{R}
.
We extend the costs to reverse arcs with
c(e^{R}):=-c(e)
for
e^{R}\in E^{R}
.
Let the
residual capacity
of each arcs in
E\cup E^{R}
be

u_{f}(v,w):=\begin{cases}\infty&(v,w)\in E\\
f(w,v)&(v,w)\in E^{R}\end{cases}

Let the
residual arcs
be

E_{f}:=\{(v,w)\in E\cup E^{R}\mid u_{f}(v,w)>0\}
,
and the
residual graph
be
G_{f}=(V,E_{f})
.
The residual network is
N_{f}=(G_{f},c,u_{f},\phi)
.
Pseudoflows
f^{\prime}
in
G_{f}
can
augment

f
to produce a new pseudoflow:
the arc-wise addition
f+f^{\prime}
results in a valid pseudoflow for
G
.
If
f^{\prime}
is a residual pseudoflow with (i)
e_{f^{\prime}}(v)=0
on vertices with

e_{f}(v)=0
, (ii)
e_{f^{\prime}}(v)\leq 0
on vertices with
e_{f}(v)>0
, and (iii)

e_{f^{\prime}}(v)\geq 0
on vertices with
e_{f}(v)<0
, and
(iv)
\sum_{v\in V}|e_{f^{\prime}}(v)|<\sum_{v\in V}|e_{f}(v)|
,
we call it an
improving flow
.
Intuitively, an improving flow routes some amount of excess to deficits,
reducing the total amount of excess/deficit.
If an improving flow
f^{\prime}
is a flow on a simple path from an excess vertex to a
deficit vertex, we call it an
augmenting path
.
Generally, an improving flow
f^{\prime}
may be any residual pseudoflow (e.g. a
blocking flow), but the improving flows of this algorithm will be augmenting
paths.

Next, we introduce some criteria for proving a flow is mininum-cost.
The MCF problem can be expressed as a linear program with variables
f
.

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\sum_{(v,w)\in E}{c(v,w)f(v,w)}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\sum_{(v,w)\in E}f(v,w)-\sum_{(w,v)\in E}f(w,v)=\phi(v)

\displaystyle\forall v\in V

\displaystyle f(v,w)\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall(v,w)\in E

The dual program has a variable for each vertex, that we call

potentials

\pi:V\to\mathbb{R}
.

\displaystyle\max

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}{\phi(v)\pi(v)}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\pi(v)-\pi(w)\leq c(v,w)

\displaystyle\forall(v,w)\in E

The dual feasibility conditions are often restated in terms of the

reduced cost

c_{\pi}(v,w):=c(v,w)-\pi(v)+\pi(w).

That is, a feasible
\pi
has
c_{\pi}(e)\geq 0
for all arcs.
The complementary slackness conditions of this LP can also be written using
reduced cost.

\displaystyle c_{\pi}(v,w)>0

\displaystyle\implies f(v,w)=0

(8)

\displaystyle f(v,w)>0

\displaystyle\implies c_{\pi}(v,w)=0

The complementary slackness conditions are
optimality conditions
—

f^{*}
is the minimum-cost flow if and only if there exists potentials
\pi^{*}

that satisfy (
8
).
We say that pseudoflow
f
and potentials
\pi
“satisfy the optimality
conditions” if together they meet (
8
).
An equivalent statement of (
8
), in terms of the residual
graph, is that
c_{\pi^{*}}(v,w)\geq 0
on all arcs of
G_{f^{*}}
.

4.2
Least-cost augmentation algorithms

A number of primal-dual algorithms for min-cost flow (including

[
21
]
) augment flow along shortest paths in
G_{f}
with respect
to reduced costs.
We will refer to such algorithms as
least-cost augmentation
algorithms.

Least-cost augmentation algorithms typically start with
f=0,\pi=0
, a
pseudoflow-potential pair which trivially meets the optimality conditions,
but where
f
will generally have excess and not be a flow.
The excess/deficit is gradually reduced to 0 by augmenting with improving flows
along shortest paths in
G_{f}
w.r.t.
c_{\pi}
.
Using improving flows along shortest paths guarantees that the new pseudoflow
meets the optimality conditions with some set of feasible potentials.

Lemma 12

(
[
21
]
Lemma 4)
.

Let
f
be a pseudoflow satisfying the optimality condition with
feasible potentials
\pi
, and
f^{\prime}
be derived from
f
by augmenting
along a shortest path in
G_{f}
with respect to costs
c_{\pi}
.
Then
f^{\prime}
also satisfies the optimality conditions with respect to
another set of feasible potentials
\pi^{\prime}
.

Thus, when
f
has 0 imbalance everywhere, it must be an optimal flow.
The proof of Lemma
12
includes a construction for the new
potentials
\pi^{\prime}
in linear time, after computing the single-source
shortest path (SSSP) distances
d_{s}(\cdot)
from one excess vertex
s
.
Specifically,
\pi^{\prime}(v)=\pi(v)-d_{s}(v)
.

4.3
Excess Scaling and Orlin’s Algorithm

Least-cost augmentation algorithms (with augmenting paths) can be tuned by
choosing which excess/deficit vertices to augment between, and the amount of
flow sent per augmentation.
Orlin’s algorithm is based on the
excess-scaling
paradigm due to Edmonds
and Karp
[
13
]
, which augments flow in units of

\Delta
(initially
\Delta=U
) from excess vertices with

e_{f}(v)\geq\Delta
to deficit vertices with
e_{f}(v)\leq-\Delta
.
Excess/deficit vertices which meet these thresholds are called
active
.
Augmentations are performed from any active excess vertex to any active
deficit vertex.
When there are either no more active excesses or no active deficits,
\Delta

is halved.
Each sequence of augmentations with a fixed
\Delta
is called a

scaling phase
, and
\Delta
is called the
scale
.
As an invariant, there is at most
2n\Delta
excess at the beginning of each scale,
and therefore
O(n)
augmentating paths in a scaling phase before the active
excess vertices or active deficit vertices are depleted and the phase ends.
There are
O(\log U)
scales before
\Delta<1
.
At this point, the remaining (integer) excess must be 0.

The algorithm in
[
21
]
extends excess-scaling
to obtain a strongly polynomial
O(n\log n)
bound on the number of
augmentations and scaling phases.
At a high level, this is done by contracting edges, adjusting the set of active
vertices, and reducing
\Delta
aggressively when possible.

We use
\hat{G}=(\hat{V},\hat{E})
to refer to the contracted multigraph,
where each
\hat{v}\in\hat{V}
is a contracted subset of
V
and

\hat{E}=\{(v,w)\in E\mid v\in\hat{v},w\in\hat{w},\hat{v}\neq\hat{w},\hat{v}\in\hat{V},\hat{w}\in\hat{V}\}
.
Initially,
\hat{G}
is identical to
G
and each
\hat{v}
is a singleton.
We refer to the elements of
\hat{V}
as
supervertices
.
The imbalance of
\hat{v}\in\hat{V}
is defined to be

e_{f}(\hat{v})=\sum_{v\in\hat{v}}e_{f}(v)
.
The algorithm maintains potentials for every
v\in V
, but only flow values
for the edges of
\hat{E}
.
Namely, flow values across of arcs within
\hat{v}\in\hat{V}
are unknown.

The algorithm executes a single scaling phase as follows:

1.

Using any
1/2<\alpha<1
, excess supervertices (resp. deficit
supervertices) are active if
e_{f}(v)\geq\alpha\Delta
(resp.

e_{f}(v)\leq-\alpha\Delta
).

2.

If there are no active supervertices and zero flow on all

\hat{E}
arcs,
\Delta
is set to

\max_{\hat{v}\in\hat{V}}e_{f}(\hat{v})
.

3.

For any
(v,w)\in\hat{E}
has
f(v,w)\geq 3n\Delta
, it is
contracted, merging
\hat{u}\leftarrow\hat{v}\cup\hat{w}
,
where
v\in\hat{v}
and
w\in\hat{w}
.
Set the flow of all arcs in
\hat{v}\times\hat{w}
beside

(v,w)
to 0.

4.

Otherwise, repeatedly perform least-cost augmentation from any
active excess supervertex to any active deficit supervertex,
in units of
\Delta
, until there are no more active excess
supervertices or no more active deficit supervertices.
Each augmentation involves the following steps:

(a)

Compute the SSSP tree from any
s\in\hat{v}
of an
active excess supervertex
\hat{v}
.
Let the SSSP distances from
s
be
d_{s}(\cdot)
.

(b)

Update potentials for all
v\in V
as

\pi(v)\leftarrow\pi(v)-d_{s}(v)
.

(c)

Augment
\Delta
flow from
s
to any
t\in\hat{w}
of
an active deficit supervertex
\hat{w}
, along the
shortest path from
s
to
t
.

5.

\Delta\leftarrow\Delta/2

Intuitively, arcs are contracted when they have flow so high that no set of
future augmentations can bring the flow back to 0 — these arcs are members
of the optimal flow’s support.
As a result, the vertices within a supervertex are connected by a set of
positive flow edges, although the exact flow value is unknown.
In the uncapacitated setting,
f(v,w)>0
induces
u_{f}(v,w)>0
and

u_{f}(w,v)>0
, so the members of a supervertex are strongly connected by
positive capacity residual edges.

During a contraction of
\hat{v}
and
\hat{w}
((3) above), we also set flow
to 0 on all arcs between
\hat{v}\times\hat{w}
besides the contracted arc.
Note that this does not change the imbalance of the new supervertex,
but the only flow support arcs within supervertices are contracted
arcs.
As a result, our augmenting paths will only use contracted
arcs to traverse the interior of supervertices.
Thus, augmenting paths which pass through the interior of supervertices
will not change the flow support status of arcs internal to the supervertex.
We can track the flow support within supervertices by marking the
contracted arcs as having positive flow and ignore the exact value, and this
status will not change throughout the augmentation process.

The output flow is one on the contracted graph, not
G
.
In contrast, the resulting optimal potentials are on
V
.
An extra step is taken to recover an optimal flow on
G
from the optimal
potentials, which we describe in Section
4.5
.
Our solution to this is new and extracts the optimal flow in near linear time.
Excepting this recovery step, Orlin bounds the running time of the other steps
in terms of the time to solve the SSSP problem.

Theorem 5

(
[
21
]
Theorem 4)
.

The algorithm determines the minimum cost flow in the contracted
network in
O((n\log n)S(n,m))
time, where
S(n,m)
is the time
to compute the single-source shortest path tree/distances in a graph of

n
vertices and
m
edges with nonnegative costs.

In Section
4.4
, we provide a geometric implementation of
Dijkstra’s algorithm by using a dynamic data structure for bichromatic closest
pair.
This approach is not new to geometric transportation/matching algorithms,
see
[
27
,
8
,
23
]
.
In the end,
S(n,m)=\widetilde{O}(n)
, completing the proof of
Theorem
4
.
From this implementation, we can also prove the flow support has size
O(n)

throughout the algorithm, meaning steps which check properties of flow support
edges (e.g. (2) and (3) above) take only
O(n)
time.

4.4
Computing shortest path trees

Consider Dijkstra’s algorithm for computing shortest distances
d_{s}(v)
to all
vertices from a single source
s\in V
.
Dijkstra’s algorithm expands a set
S\subseteq V
of vertices for which it has
calculated
d_{s}
.
Initially
S=\{s\}
,
d_{s}(s)=0
, and
d_{s}(v)
is unknown for all
v\neq s
.
In each iteration, Dijkstra’s algorithm grows
S
by
relaxing

the edge to the minimum-distance neighbor of
S

(v,w)=\argmin_{(v,w)\in S\times(V\setminus S)}d_{s}(v)+c_{\pi}(v,w),

fixing
d_{s}(w)\leftarrow d_{s}(v)+c_{\pi}(v,w)
and adding
w
to
S
.
The shortest path tree from
s
is composed of the relaxed edges
and can therefore be computed alongside the distances.

Geometrically, we can find this
\argmin
efficiently, i.e. without examining

\Theta(n^{2})
edges over the course of Dijkstra’s.
Our solution is to use a dynamic bichromatic closest pair data structure.
Let
P,Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be point sets with weights

W:P\cup Q\to\mathbb{R}
.
The
bichromatic closest pair
(BCP) between
P
and
Q
is the pair

(p,q)\in P\times Q
minimizing the additively weighted distance

\mathrm{d}(p,q)+\omega(p)+\omega(q)
.
The dynamic BCP data structure of Kaplan
et al.

[
19
]

maintains the BCP under insertion and deletion of points and implements each
update/query operation in
\widetilde{O}(1)
time.

Lemma 13
.

Using a dynamic bichromatic closest pair data structure,
we can compute the shortest path tree and distances under reduced costs
in
\widetilde{O}(n)
time.

We emphasize that the BCP query requires points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, so will act on
the original points in
V
rather than supervertices directly.
To choose the starting vertex
s
, we use any
s=v\in V
such that
v
is a
member of an active excess supervertex
\hat{v}\in\hat{V}
.
The vertices within a supervertex are strongly connected in
G_{f}
by flow
support arcs, which must have reduced cost 0, by the optimality conditions.
Thus, all members of a supervertex have the same
d_{s}
distance.

Dijkstra’s algorithm implementation

For the BCP, we maintain
P=(S\cap R)
,

Q=((V\setminus S)\cap B)
, and use weights

\omega(v)=d_{s}(v)+\pi(v)

for
v\in P
and
\omega(w)=-\pi(w)
for
w\in Q
.
Consider an iteration of Dijkstra’s algorithm, and let

v\in S,w\in V\setminus S
.
If
(v,w)\in E
(i.e., directed from
R
to
B
), then

d_{s}(v)+c_{\pi}(v,w)=\mathrm{d}(v,w)+\omega(v)+\omega(w)
and the BCP on

P
and
Q
above will accurately find the
\argmin
for these edges.

However, this BCP will not correctly report the minimum edge if it it is

(v,w)\in E_{f}\setminus E
(directed from
B\to R
).
These are residual edges which “undo” existing flow support.
On the other hand, when
(v,w)
is in the flow support, we must have

d_{s}(v)=d_{s}(w)
.
Dijkstra’s algorithm maintains that
d_{s}(v)\leq d_{s}(w)
for

v\in S,w\in(V\setminus S)
, so such a support edge is always a minimizer.
We prioritize existing support edges by relaxing any new support edges leaving

S
before querying the BCP for
R\to B
edges.
This can be done in total time proportional to the number of support edges, by
storing the support edges adjoining each vertex of
B
in a list.

Finally, we note that the search may become “stuck” and unable to reach
certain vertices of
R
if they are inactive and have no adjoining flow
support.
We call these vertices
dead
.
For these, we relax the high-cost dummy edges mentioned in the introduction.
Since the dummy edges have uniform cost, the minimum dummy edge to reach some

r\in R
is exactly
(b,r)
for

b=\argmax_{b\in S\cap B}\pi(b)
.
Since this is independent of the arc cost, the same
b
is the minimizer for
reaching every dead vertex.
Thus, we can compute
d_{s}
for all dead vertices in a single step at the end
of Dijkstra’s, by relaxing the dummy edges leaving the
b
vertex above.

Each relaxation, we add
w
to
S
and update
d_{s}(w)
,
P
,
Q
, and

\omega(w)
.
As before, we stop once
S=V
.
In summary, our implementation of Dijkstra’s algorithm is as follows.

1.

Initialize
S=\{s\}
and
d_{s}(s)=0
, and
d_{s}(v)=\infty

for all other vertices.
Initialize an empty queue reached support edges.

2.

Try to relax the next support edge
(v,w)
in the queue.
If
S
has grown so that
w
is no longer in
V\setminus S
,
then discard the edge and try again.

3.

Otherwise, if there are no support edges, query the BCP to relax
the minimum
R\to B
edge.
Repeat from step
2
until
S=V
or

(V\setminus S)\cap B=\emptyset
.

4.

If there are any dead vertices
r\in R
, relax every

(b,r)
dummy edge for

b=\argmax_{b\in S\cap B}\pi(b)
.

We now analyze the time spent in computing shortest paths.
Because we relax support edges before using the BCP data structure, we have the
following lemma.

Lemma 14
.

The flow support is acylic, in the undirected sense, throughout our
implementation of Orlin’s algorithm.
That is, there is no sequence of edges

(v_{1},v_{2}),(v_{2},v_{3})\cdots(v_{k},v_{k+1}=v_{1})

for which
\forall 1\leq i\leq k
either
f(v_{i},v_{i+1})>0
or

f(v_{i+1},v_{i})>0
.

Proof.

Let
f_{i}
be the pseudoflow after the
i
-th augmentation, and

F_{i}\subseteq E
be the flow support of
f_{i}
.
Let
T_{i}
be the shortest path tree generated for augmenting
f_{i}
.
Namely, the
i
-th augmenting path is an excess-deficit path in
T_{i}
, and all
arcs of
T_{i}
are admissible by the time the augmentation is performed.
Let
E(T_{i})
be the undirected edges corresponding to arcs of
T_{i}
.
Notice that,
E(F_{i+1})\subseteq E(F_{i})\cup E(T_{i})
.
We prove that
E(F_{i})\cup E(T_{i})
is acyclic by induction on
i
;
as
E(F_{i+1})
is a subset of these edges, it must also be acyclic.
At the beginning with
f_{0}=0
,
E(F_{0})
is vacuously acyclic.

Let
E(F_{i})
be acyclic by induction hypothesis.
Since
T_{i}
is a forest (thus, acyclic), any hypothetical cycle
\Gamma
that
forms in
E(F_{i})\cup E(T_{i})
must contain edges from both

E(F_{i})
and
E(T_{i})
.
To give a visual analogy, we will color
e\in\Gamma

purple
if
e\in E(F_{i})\cap E(T_{i})
,

red
if
e\in E(F_{i})
but
e\not\in E(T_{i})
,
and
blue
if
e\in E(T_{i})
but
e\not\in E(F_{i})
.
Then,
\Gamma
is neither entirely red nor entirely blue.
We say that red and purple edges are
red-tinted
, and similarly blue and
purple edges are
blue-tinted
.
Roughly speaking, our implementation of the Hungarian search prioritizes
relaxing red-tinted admissible arcs over pure blue arcs.

We can sort the blue-tinted edges of
\Gamma
by the order they were relaxed
into
S
during the Hungarian search forming
T_{i}
.
Let
(v,w)\in\Gamma
be the last pure blue edge relaxed, of all the
blue-tinted edges in
\Gamma
— after
(v,w)
is relaxed, the remaining
unrelaxed, blue-tinted edges of
\Gamma
are purple.

Let us pause the Hungarian search the moment before
(v,w)
is relaxed.
At this point,
v\in S
and
w\not\in S
, and the Hungarian search must have
finished relaxing all frontier support arcs.
By our choice of
(v,w)
,
\Gamma\setminus(v,w)
is a path of relaxed blue
edges and red-tinted edges which connect
v
and
w
.
Walking around
\Gamma\setminus(v,w)
from
v
to
w
, we see that every
vertex of the cycle must be in
S
already:
v\in S
, relaxed blue edges have
both endpoints in
S
, and any unrelaxed red-tinted edge must have both
endpoints in
S
, since the Hungarian search would have prioritized relaxing
the red-tinted edges to grow
S
before relaxing
(v,w)
(a blue edge).
It follows that
w\in S
already, a contradiction.

No such cycle
\Gamma
can exist, thus
E(F_{i})\cup E(T_{i})
is acyclic
and
E(F_{i+1})\subseteq E(F_{i})\cup E(T_{i})
is acyclic.
By induction,
E(F_{i})
is acyclic for all
i
.
∎

Lemma
14
implies there are at most
O(n)
edges in the
flow support at any point.
Each support edge enters/exits the Dijsktra search queue at most once, so the
time spent attempting to relax support edges is
O(n)
.
There are
O(n)
additional iterations where we query the BCP data structure,
and we spend
O(n)
time relaxing dummy edges at the end.
The total time to compute each shortest paths tree is
\widetilde{O}(n)
, so the main
scaling/augmentation procedure can be implemented in
\widetilde{O}(n^{2})
time.

4.5
Optimal flow from optimal duals

Let
T^{*}
be the shortest path tree under
c_{\pi^{*}}
rooted at an arbitrary

s\in R
, computed using the modified Dijkstra’s algorithm in the previous
section.
The following lemma completes the proof of the
Theorem
4
.

Lemma 15
.

T^{*}
does not use any dummy edges, and there exists a flow in
T^{*}

which satisifies all supplies and demands.

Proof.

Note that
T^{*}
is constructed based on the final flow support in
addition to the optimal dual
\pi^{*}
.
Recall that dummy edges cannot be flow support edges, and that dummy
edges are essentially only relaxed for
R
points without adjoining
flow support.
Assume that all vertices of
R
have nonzero supply (indeed,
discarding zero-supply red vertices results in an equivalent instance).
If
r\in R
was not contracted, then it has an adjoining
uncontracted flow edge to offload its supply.
Alternatively, if it is part of a contracted component, then there is a
support edge which resulted in
r
’s contraction, although we may not
know the flow value on the edge.

For the second part, we will start from the empty flow and consider the
linear program description of min-cost flow.
There are dual variables for each vertex and dual constraints for each
edge.
First, we argue that
T^{*}
forms a maximal set of tight, linearly
independent dual constraints.
We observe that a set of edges
S\subset E
correspond to a set of
linearly independent dual constraints exactly when
S
is acyclic
(in the undirected sense). Since
T^{*}
spans all vertices, its edges
have linearly independent constraints and the set is maximal.

Next, we show that this set of edges must contain a feasible primal
solution, by a perturbation argument.
Suppose we raise the cost of all edges in
E\setminus T^{*}
to
\infty

(or any quantity
\geq U\tfcost(\pi^{*})
, for example).
This does not change the optimal dual solution;
\pi^{*}
is still
“stuck” due to the maximal set of independent, tight constraints in

T^{*}
.
However, this forces all other dual constraints to be slack on

\pi^{*}
.

Strong duality implies that an equal-cost solution exists in the
primal, since
\tfcost(\pi^{*})
is finite.
However, the perturbation has caused any solution which uses edges
outside of
T^{*}
to be much more than
\tfcost(\pi^{*})
,
so the remaining optimal primal solution must be within the edges of

T^{*}
.
∎
