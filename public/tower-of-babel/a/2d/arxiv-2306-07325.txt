Homogeneous linear intrinsic constraints in the stationary manifold of a G G -invariant potential
R. Krishnan

Abstract
Given a G G -invariant potential 𝒱 \mathcal{V} of a scalar multiplet φ \varphi , there may exist a set of homogenous linear equations that constrain the components of a stationary point of 𝒱 \mathcal{V} independently of the coefficients of the terms in 𝒱 \mathcal{V} . We call them homogeneous linear intrinsic constraints (HLICs). HLICs in a stationary point manifest as HLICs in the corresponding vacuum alignment of φ \varphi , which plays a central role in predictive phenomenological models. We discover that a group H ~ \tilde{H} generates HLICs if the terms in 𝒱 \mathcal{V} satisfy a condition, which we call the compatibility condition. In this paper, we also develop a procedure, which involves splitting 𝒱 \mathcal{V} into smaller parts, to establish the existence of specific stationary points using arguments based on symmetries without the need for explicitly extremizing the potential. Using this procedure, we obtain H ~ \tilde{H} as a direct product of the symmetry groups associated with the various irreducible multiplets (irreps) in φ \varphi . This results from considering the potentials of the irreps separately and verifying if the cross terms are compatible with H ~ \tilde{H} . 1 1 1 A video presentation of this paper is available here .

.1
Stationary manifolds of the
G
-invariant renormalizable potential

Consider a scalar field
\varphi
transforming as a real, complex or quaternionic representation of a compact group
\mathbb{G}
. Compact groups consist of compact Lie groups as well as finite groups. We denote the number of real degrees of freedom (the real dimension) of
\varphi
by
\text{dim}(\varphi)
. The real components of
\varphi
can be denoted by
\varphi_{i}
with
i=1,..,\text{dim}(\varphi)
. The invariants of the group action constructed using
\varphi
constitute the terms in the potential. The invariant terms of mass dimension up to four are renormalizable. Let
{\mathfrak{n}}
be the set of integers
\{1,..,|{\mathfrak{n}}|\}
where
|{\mathfrak{n}}|
denotes the cardinality of
{\mathfrak{n}}
. Let the number of linearly independent real invariants up to order four be
|{\mathfrak{n}}|
. The general renormalizable potential is given by

\mathcal{V}=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{n}}}c_{\alpha}\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(\varphi),

(1)

where
\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(\varphi)
are the real invariants constructed using
\varphi
, and
c_{\alpha}
are the corresponding real coefficients, with
\alpha
ranging over the set of integers
{\mathfrak{n}}
. Let us consider the largest compact group under which
\varphi
transforms as a real orthogonal matrix representation such that the potential
\mathcal{V}
remains invariant. We call this group
G
,

G=\{g:g\in O(\text{dim}(\varphi)),\,\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(g\varphi)=\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(\varphi)\,\forall\,\alpha\in{\mathfrak{n}}\}.

(2)

In general, this group can be larger than
\mathbb{G}
. The larger set of group transformations present in
G
compared to
\mathbb{G}
are sometimes referred to as accidental symmetries. For examples with accidental symmetries, see Examples
B.7
,
B.8
.

In the space of
\varphi
, i.e., in
\mathbbm{R}^{\text{dim}(\varphi)}
, let us choose a specific point, say,
\varphi=\boldsymbol{\varphi}
. The action of the group
G
on
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
generates a set of points which forms the orbit of
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
under
G
, denoted by
G\boldsymbol{\varphi}
. This
G
-orbit, in general, is a union of one or more connected manifolds. One of the connected components of
G\boldsymbol{\varphi}
contains the original point
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
. Let
S\subseteq G
be the setwise stabilizer of this connected component. The action of elements in
S
moves a point in this connected manifold within the manifold transitively, while the action of elements in
G
that are not in
S
moves the point to another connected manifold. The connected manifold that contains
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
is nothing but the
S
-orbit of
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
, denoted by
S\boldsymbol{\varphi}
. Let the real dimension of
S\boldsymbol{\varphi}
be
\text{dim}(S\boldsymbol{\varphi})
. We may represent the continuous transformations under
S
with the parameters
\theta={\theta}_{1},..,{\theta}_{\text{dim}(S\boldsymbol{\varphi})}
. These parameters can uniquely label the points in the connected manifold
S\boldsymbol{\varphi}
. We note that
S
and
\text{dim}(S\boldsymbol{\varphi})
are dependent on the choice of the point
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
.

Let us use
c
to denote the coefficients in the potential (
1
), i.e.,
c=c_{1},..,c_{|{\mathfrak{n}}|}
. A stationary point is a point where the first derivatives of the potential vanish. Corresponding to a set of specific values for the coefficients given by
c_{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{c}_{\alpha}\,\forall\alpha\in{\mathfrak{n}}
, i.e.,
c=\boldsymbol{c}
, let us assume
3

\boldsymbol{\varphi}

\varphi=\boldsymbol{\varphi}
, i.e.,

\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{n}}}\boldsymbol{c}_{\alpha}\,\partial_{i}\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(\varphi)\big|_{\varphi=\boldsymbol{\varphi}}=0,

(3)

where
\partial_{i}
is the derivative with respect to the component
\varphi_{i}
. Since

\boldsymbol{\varphi}

G\boldsymbol{\varphi}

\boldsymbol{\varphi}

S\boldsymbol{\varphi}
‘the stationary manifold corresponding to the stationary point
\boldsymbol{\varphi}

Consider the Hessian of the potential at
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
,

\mathcal{H}_{ij}=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{n}}}c_{\alpha}\,\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(\varphi)\big|_{\varphi=\boldsymbol{\varphi}}.

(4)

We may block-diagonalize
\mathcal{H}
using an orthogonal change of basis
\varphi^{\prime}=R\varphi

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=R\,\mathcal{H}\,R^{\scriptscriptstyle T}=\left(\begin{matrix}0&0\\
0&\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\perp}\end{matrix}\right),

(5)

\text{dim}(S\boldsymbol{\varphi})
, and it involves the directions tangential to the stationary manifold
S\boldsymbol{\varphi}
. It vanishes because the value of the potential remains unchanged along the tangential directions.
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\perp}
is a square matrix of dimension
(\text{dim}(\varphi)-\text{dim}(S\boldsymbol{\varphi}))
. We assume that
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\perp}
is non-singular; in other words,
\mathcal{V}
is a Morse-Bott function

;
Austin and Braam 1995
at

We want to study how the stationary manifold changes away from
S\boldsymbol{\varphi}
when the coefficients are changed away from
\boldsymbol{c}
. A change in the stationary manifold can happen in two ways. One is a smooth change that does not involve a change in its nature. The other involves a change in its nature. A typical example of the second case is the electroweak phase transition resulting from the change in the coefficients in the Higgs potential. In the space of the coefficients, the region where the Higgs potential has the well-known mexican-hat-like structure is shown in FIG
1a
. At the boundary of this region, the nature of the stationary manifold changes. The theory of bifurcations
Golubitsky and Schaeffer 1985
;
Golubitsky et al. 1988
describes such changes. A change in the nature of the stationary manifold is associated with a change in the sign of the eigenvalues of the Hessian. Therefore, as long as the number of vanishing eigenvalues remains the same, the change in the stationary manifold will be smooth. Currently, we are interested only in such a smooth change since our objective is to express the stationary manifold as a smooth function of
c
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the largest connected open neighbourhood of
\boldsymbol{c}
throughout which the Hessian has exactly
\text{dim}(S\boldsymbol{\varphi})
vanishing eigenvalues. A brief description of the boundary of
\mathcal{C}
is provided in FIG
1b
. To ensure the existence of
\mathcal{C}
in relation to
\boldsymbol{c}
, i.e., to ensure that
\boldsymbol{c}
is not a point in the boundary, we need to assume that
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\perp}
(
5
) is non-singular, i.e., the potential is a Morse-Bott function
Bott 1982
;
Austin and Braam 1995
, at
\boldsymbol{c}
. We use
\mathcal{C}
as the domain to define the map
\varphi^{\circ}
,

\displaystyle\varphi^{\circ}\!:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow S\mathbb{R}^{\text{dim}(\varphi)}\!:\,\,\begin{split}&\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{n}}}c_{\alpha}\,\partial_{i}\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(\varphi)\big|_{\varphi=\varphi^{\circ}(c)(\theta)}=0\,\,\forall\theta\\
&\varphi^{\circ}(\boldsymbol{c})=S\boldsymbol{\varphi}.\end{split}

(6)

This map defines
\varphi^{\circ}(c)
as the stationary manifold of
\mathcal{V}
expressed as a function of the coefficients
c
. It is defined in relation to the chosen stationary point
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
obtained at
\boldsymbol{c}
. In (
6
),
\varphi^{\circ}(c)(\theta)
with
\theta=\theta_{1},..,\theta_{\text{dim}(S\boldsymbol{\varphi})}
identifies individual stationary points in the manifold
\varphi^{\circ}(c)
.

We may denote any point
c\in\mathcal{C}
as a variation from
\boldsymbol{c}
, i.e.,
c=\boldsymbol{c}+\delta c
. A change in the coefficients
\boldsymbol{c}\rightarrow(\boldsymbol{c}+\delta c)
results in a change in the stationary manifold
\varphi^{\circ}(\boldsymbol{c})\rightarrow\varphi^{\circ}(\boldsymbol{c}+\delta c)
which satisfies

\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{n}}}(\boldsymbol{c}_{\alpha}+\delta c_{\alpha})\,\partial_{i}\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(\varphi)\big|_{\varphi=\varphi^{\circ}(\boldsymbol{c}+\delta c)(\theta)}=0\,\,\,\forall\theta.

(7)

Here, to parameterize the points in the new stationary manifold
\varphi^{\circ}(\boldsymbol{c}+\delta c)
, we have used the same variables
\theta
as we used for the case of
\varphi^{\circ}(\boldsymbol{c})
. Such a unique way of assignment between
\varphi^{\circ}(\boldsymbol{c})
and
\varphi^{\circ}(\boldsymbol{c}+\delta c)
can be ensured by moving in the directions normal to the manifolds,

\big(\varphi^{\circ}\!(\boldsymbol{c})(\theta\!+\!\delta\theta)-\varphi^{\circ}\!(\boldsymbol{c})(\theta)\big)\!\perp\!\big(\varphi^{\circ}\!(\boldsymbol{c}\!+\!\delta c)(\theta)-\varphi^{\circ}\!(\boldsymbol{c})(\theta)\big)\,\,\forall\,\delta\theta,\theta.

(8)

In some cases, the stationary orbit
G\boldsymbol{\varphi}
will be a union of disconnected stationary points rather than a union of higher-dimensional stationary manifolds. Throughout this paper, we may use the term stationary manifold to refer to such a disconnected stationary point as well, since a point is nothing but a zero-dimensional manifold. If
\varphi^{\circ}(c)
is a disconnected stationary point (instead of a higher-dimesional manifold), we do not require the parameters
\theta
. In that case, the whole of the Hessian (instead of only its part normal to the higher-dimensional manifold) is assumed to be non-singular. The Morse-Bott theory, which studies stationary manifolds, is a generalisation of the Morse theory, which was formulated earlier
Bott 1982
to study disconnected stationary points.

.2
Coefficient-independent symmetries of the stationary manifold

Given the potential
\mathcal{V}
(
1
) containing
|{\mathfrak{n}}|
terms, we define

\tilde{\mathcal{V}}=\sum_{\alpha\in{\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}}}c_{\alpha}\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(\varphi),

(9)

where
{\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}}
is a subset of
{\mathfrak{n}}
. We express
\mathcal{V}
as the sum of two parts: the first part being
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
and the second part being the rest of the terms, i.e.,

\mathcal{V}=\tilde{\mathcal{V}}+\sum_{\alpha\in({\mathfrak{n}}-{\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}})}c_{\alpha}\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(\varphi).

(10)

The domain of the map
\varphi^{\circ}
(
6
) contains the point
c=\boldsymbol{c}
. Let us assume that the second part of
\mathcal{V}
(
10
) vanishes at
c=\boldsymbol{c}
, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{c}_{\alpha}=0\quad\forall\,\alpha\in({\mathfrak{n}}-{\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}}).

(11)

Let
\tilde{G}
be the symmetry group of
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
,

\tilde{G}=\{g:g\in O(\text{dim}(\varphi)),\,\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(g\varphi)=\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(\varphi)\,\forall\,\alpha\in{\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}}\}.\!\!\!

(12)

We have
G\subseteq\tilde{G}
. Let
\tilde{H}\subseteq\tilde{G}
be the pointwise stabilizer of
\varphi^{\circ}(\boldsymbol{c})
under
\tilde{G}
,

\tilde{H}=\{h:h\in\tilde{G},\,h\,\varphi^{\circ}(\boldsymbol{c})(\theta)=\varphi^{\circ}(\boldsymbol{c})(\theta)\,\forall\,\theta\}.\!\!

(13)

Let
\text{fix}(\tilde{H})
be the fixed-point subspace
Golubitsky et al. 1988
;
Gaeta 1994
of
\tilde{H}
,

\text{fix}(\tilde{H})=\{\varphi:h\varphi=\varphi\,\,\forall\,h\in\tilde{H}\}.

(14)

Suppose the terms that we added to
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
to obtain
\mathcal{V}
(
10
), i.e.,
\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(\varphi)\,\forall\alpha\in({\mathfrak{n}}-{\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}})
, satisfy the condition:

\boxed{\partial_{i}\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(\varphi)\in\text{fix}(\tilde{H})\quad\forall\,\,\varphi\in\text{fix}(\tilde{H})}\,,

(15)

i.e., the gradient of every newly added term calculated anywhere in the fixed-point subspace of
\tilde{H}
also lies in the fixed-point subspace. In Appendix
A.1
, we prove that the realization of this condition leads to

\varphi^{\circ}(c)(\theta)\in\text{fix}(\tilde{H})\quad\forall\,\theta,\,\,\forall\,c\in\mathcal{C}.

(16)

The above equation can be rephrased as

h\varphi^{\circ}(c)(\theta)=\varphi^{\circ}(c)(\theta)\quad\forall\,\theta,\,\,\forall\,\,c\in\mathcal{C},\,\,h\in\tilde{H}.

(17)

In other words,
\tilde{H}
encapsulates the symmetries of the stationary manifold
\varphi^{\circ}(c)
that are independent of the coefficients in the potential. The terms that we added to
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
to obtain
\mathcal{V}
do not spoil these symmetries even though they are not, in general, invariant under
\tilde{H}
. They have to satisfy condition (
15
) only. If a term satisfies (
15
), we say that it is
compatible with

\tilde{H}
. We call (
15
) the
compatibility condition
. The discovery of the compatibility condition is the main result of this paper. Compatibility is a weaker condition than invariance, i.e., if a term is invariant under
\tilde{H}
, it will be compatible with
\tilde{H}
. Please see Appendix
A.2
for proof.

Let us discuss a couple of special cases (corollaries) where the compatibility condition is satisfied by a term
\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(\varphi)
. These corollaries are of practical importance in the construction of potentials in model building. Suppose we can write the term
\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(\varphi)
in the form,

\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(\varphi)=\mathcal{X}(\varphi)^{{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\,\mathcal{Y}(\varphi),

(18)

where
\mathcal{X}(\varphi)
and
\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)
are multiplets that are constructed with
\varphi
and that transform under
\tilde{G}
. Let
h_{\mathcal{X}}
and
h_{\mathcal{Y}}
be the representations of
h\in\tilde{H}
acting on
\mathcal{X}(\varphi)
and
\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)
, respectively, i.e.,

h_{\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{X}(\varphi)=\mathcal{X}(h\varphi),\quad h_{\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)=\mathcal{Y}(h\varphi).

(19)

Corollary A: If

h_{\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)=\mathcal{Y}(\varphi),\,\,\,h_{\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{X}(\varphi)=\mathcal{X}(\varphi)\quad\forall\,\varphi\in\text{fix}(\tilde{H}),

(20)

then the compatibility condition (
15
) is satisfied. Please see Appendix
A.3
for proof.

Let
\tilde{H}_{\mathcal{X}}=\{h_{\mathcal{X}}:h\in\tilde{H}\}
and
\tilde{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}=\{h_{\mathcal{Y}}:h\in\tilde{H}\}
. In other words, let
\tilde{H}_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\tilde{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
be the set of representation matrices of the group
\tilde{H}
corresponding to the multiplets
\mathcal{X}(\varphi)
and
\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)
, respectively. If
\tilde{H}_{\mathcal{X}}=\tilde{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
, condition (
20
) will be satisfied.
\tilde{H}_{\mathcal{X}}=\tilde{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
does not imply that
h_{\mathcal{X}}=h_{\mathcal{Y}}
; the latter situation is rather trivial since
h_{\mathcal{X}}=h_{\mathcal{Y}}
corresponds to the term (
18
) being invariant under
\tilde{H}
.

Corollary B: If

\mathcal{X}(\varphi)=0,\quad\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)=0\quad\forall\,\varphi\in\text{fix}(\tilde{H}),

(21)

then the compatibility condition is satisfied. We can see that (
21
) implies (
20
), which in turn implies (
15
). Corollary B is the main result of
Krishnan 2021
. A number of examples that utilize corollaries A and B are given in Appendix

Homogenous linear intrinsic constraints

We define a homogenous linear intrinsic constraint (HLIC) in the stationary manifold
\varphi^{\circ}(c)
as a coefficient-independent constraint that can be expressed as a linear combination of the components of
\varphi^{\circ}(c)(\theta)
being equal to zero, i.e.,

\kappa^{\scriptscriptstyle T}\varphi^{\circ}(c)(\theta)=0\quad\forall\,c\in\mathcal{C},\,\forall\theta,

(22)

where
\kappa
is a constant vector. We have shown that
\varphi^{\circ}(c)(\theta)
belongs to
\text{fix}(\tilde{H})
independently of
c
and
\theta
. Therefore, the above equation is satisfied for all
\kappa
orthogonal to
\text{fix}(\tilde{H})
, i.e.,
\forall\,\kappa\in\text{fix}(\tilde{H})^{\perp}
where
\text{fix}(\tilde{H})^{\perp}
denotes the orthogonal complement of
\text{fix}(\tilde{H})
. We can show that
\text{fix}(\tilde{H})^{\perp}
is nothing but the space spanned by the rows of the matrices
(h-I)
with
h\in\tilde{H}
.

Let
H
be the pointwise stabilizer of
\varphi^{\circ}(\boldsymbol{c})
under
G

Obtaining the stationary manifold of

In this section, we propose to split the potential

. For a flavour model where this procedure is adopted, please see the companion paper
Krishnan 2023
.

.4
Summary

We consider a multiplet
\varphi
transforming under a compact group
\mathbb{G}
. Its general renormalizable potential
\mathcal{V}
consists of all independent invariant terms
\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(\varphi)
up to the fourth order and the corresponding coefficients
c_{\alpha}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{V}=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(\varphi)
. Since we do not include higher-order terms in
\mathcal{V}
, its symmetry group,
G
, can be larger than
\mathbb{G}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{G}\subseteq G
. We express a stationary manifold of
\mathcal{V}
as a function of the coefficients, namely,
\varphi^{\circ}(c)
, with a domain
\mathcal{C}
. Even though a change in
c
causes a change in
\varphi^{\circ}(c)
, its pointwise stabilizer,
H\subseteq G
, remains independent of
c
, i.e.,
h\varphi^{\circ}(c)=\varphi^{\circ}(c)\,\,\forall\,h\in H,c\in\mathcal{C}
. This implies that there exists a set of homogenous linear equations that constrain the components of
\varphi^{\circ}(c)
in a coefficient-independent way. We call them homogeneous linear intrinsic constraints (HLICs). These constraints can be given as
\kappa^{\scriptscriptstyle T}\varphi^{\circ}(c)=0\,\forall\,c\in\mathcal{C}
, where
\kappa
belongs to the orthogonal complement of the fixed-point subspace of
H
, i.e.,
\kappa\in\text{fix}(H)^{\perp}
. In modeling a physical system with discrete symetries, we often assign one of the stationary points (minima) of
\mathcal{V}
as the expectation value
\langle\varphi\rangle
via spontaneous symmetry breaking. The HLICs in
\varphi^{\circ}(c)
leads to constraints on the components of
\langle\varphi\rangle
that manifest as coefficient-independent predictions of the model.

In
Krishnan 2021
, we showed that
\varphi^{\circ}(c)
can contain HLICs beyond those generated by
H
, i.e., we can have
\kappa^{\scriptscriptstyle T}\varphi^{\circ}(c)=0\,\,\forall\,\,c\in\mathcal{C}
even for
\kappa\not\in\text{fix}(H)^{\perp}
. The current work formalizes and generalizes the results from
Krishnan 2021
. We analyze the potential
\mathcal{V}
by splitting it, resulting in the part
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
having the symmetry group
\tilde{G}
with
G\subseteq\tilde{G}
. We define
\tilde{H}
as the pointwise stabilizer of
\varphi^{\circ}(c)
under
\tilde{G}
. We discover that
\kappa^{\scriptscriptstyle T}\varphi^{\circ}(c)=0\,\,\forall\,\,c\in\mathcal{C},\,\kappa\in\text{fix}(\tilde{H})^{\perp}
if the terms in
\mathcal{V}
satisfy the
compatibility condition
:
\partial_{i}\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(\varphi)\in\text{fix}(\tilde{H})\,\forall\,\varphi\in\text{fix}(\tilde{H})^{\perp}
. The compatibility condition is more general than the requirement of invariance, i.e., the terms
\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(\varphi)
that are compatible with
\tilde{H}
need not be invariant under it. To easily verify the compatibility condition, we provide two corollaries which are its special cases.

We develop a step-by-step procedure to identify
\varphi^{\circ}(c)
by splitting
\mathcal{V}
into several parts. First, we obtain the minima of the parts of
\mathcal{V}
constructed with the irreps in
\varphi
as
n
-spheres. More terms are added to these parts leading to bifurcation and emergence of new stationary manifolds. We utilize the theorems by Michel, Golubitsky and Stewart to identify these stationary manifolds. We may further add terms that satisfy the compatibility condition resulting in the non-bifurcation scenario. We combine the stationary manifolds of the various irreps and add the cross terms, which again falls under the bifurcation or the non-bifurcation scenarios. The bifurcation scenario can create new HLICs or destroy pre-existing ones, while the non-bifurcation scenario preserves HLICs. By including all renormalizable terms, we obtain the whole potential
\mathcal{V}
and its stationary manifold
\varphi^{\circ}(c)
. Following this procedure, we construct
\tilde{H}
as the direct product of the stabilizers corresponding to each irrep, with all cross terms being compatible with
\tilde{H}
. In situations where
\varphi^{\circ}(c)
contains more HLICs than those generated by
H
, i.e., where
\text{fix}(H)^{\perp}
forms a subspace of
\text{fix}(\tilde{H})^{\perp}
, our procedure helps to determine
\tilde{H}
. More importantly, in general, it serves as a way to handle the stabilizers corresponding to each irrep separately so that we do not have to determine
G
and
H
.

To demonstrate this procedure, we provide a number of examples in Appendix
B
. In the flavour physics literature, authors resort to explicit extremization to obtain the minima. By contrast, our procedure utilizes theorems on symmetries well-established in the mathematics literature. One of the aims of this paper is to make these theorems more accessible and usable to physicists. Our examples provide a comprehensive study of these theorems. They also demonstrate the various ways driving fields can be utilized in our procedure. Finally, we provide an example where we construct an
effective
irrep by taking the tensor product of several
elementary

irreps
. Under a set of auxiliary generators, the elementary
irreps
transform nontrivially while the effective irrep remains invariant. We show that the auxiliary symmetries, though
hidden
from the effective irrep, can play a role in determining its HLICs. A realistic flavour model that invokes the framework of the auxiliary group and utilizes the mathematical procedure developed in this paper is constructed in the companion paper
Krishnan 2023
.

The non-renormalizable terms, which are of order higher than four, are suppressed. The truncation of the potential at a given order may increase symmetries in two ways: it produces accidental symmetries, i.e., enlarges
\mathbb{G}
to
G
, and it leads to
\tilde{H}
being larger than
H\subseteq G
. The higher-order terms that are not compatible with
\tilde{H}
will perturb the HLICs generated by
\tilde{H}
. In this paper, we have ignored the higher-order terms since their effect is small. On the other hand, we may utilize the non-renormalizable terms, as an alternative to driving fields, to break the accidental continuous symmetries in the potential. In that case, we need to consider the non-renormalizable terms hierarchically, e.g., we utilize the fifth order terms and ignore the sixth, and verify that the terms we utilize, e.g., the fifth order terms, are compatible with
\tilde{H}
. Such scenarios can also be analysed using our mathematical precedure even though we have not done so in this paper.

Let us discuss some mathematical definitions and general facts related to strata. The stabilizers of all points in an orbit are equal up to conjugation, i.e., they form a conjugacy class of stabilizers. All orbits with the same conjugacy class of stabilizers form a stratum. The manifold on which the group acts stratifies under the group action, i.e., the manifold can be expressed as a union of strata which are disjoint sets. Two strata
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are neighbouring strata if points in one of them, say,
S_{2}
, are the limit points of the other, say,
S_{1}
. In that case, the stabilizer of every point in
S_{1}
will be a subgroup of the stabilizer of every point in
S_{2}
up to conjugation. In other words, a stratum constituted by the limit points of another stratum will have a higher class of symmetries than the other. The union of
S_{1}
and all its neighbouring strata with higher symmetries than
S_{1}
(such as
S_{2}
) form the closure of
S_{1}
. The stratum with the lowest class of symmetries is called the generic stratum. The generic stratum is open and dense, so the whole manifold forms its closure. It is the only stratum whose dimension is equal to that of the manifold. If none of the neighbouring strata has higher symmetries than a given stratum, it is called a maximal symmetry stratum. We may have more than one maximal symmetry stratum in a stratified manifold. If and only if a stratum is closed, it is a maximal symmetry stratum. In FIG
4a

\mathbb{R}^{2}

It is possible to establish the existence of stationary points (or manifolds) of a potential invariant under a group by simply studying the stratification of the space under the group action. An equivalent statement is:
If an orbit is isolated in its stratum, it is stationary.
Case b)
If the maximal symmetry stratum contains an infinite (continuous) set of orbits, at least two stationary orbits are guaranteed to be present in every connected component of the stratum.

This example shows that a system that exhibits a set of discrete symmetries externally can also possess auxiliary symmetries, which relate to how the constituents of the system interact internally. Auxiliary symmetries are hidden in the sense that the external interactions do not possess these symmetries. However, the lowest energy states of the system, as observed externally, are determined not only by the external symmetries but also by the auxiliary symmetries. A rough analogy will be like observing hexagonal snowflakes that consistently possess features as shown in the lower panel of FIG.
7
. The author is not aware of the existence of such physical systems, but this paper shows that we can construct them mathematically. In the companion paper
Krishnan 2023
, we argue that this mathematical framework could be underlying the flavour symmetries in particle physics. The fermions are assumed to transform under a set of discrete symmetries. We introduce several elementary flavons (irreps) which transform under them as well as under auxiliary symmetries. We construct effective flavons (irreps) that are invariants of the auxiliary symmetries and hence that couple with the fermions. The vacuum alignments of these effective flavons, which correspond to a minimum of the potential, are determined not only by the symmetries under which they interact with the fermions but also by the auxiliary symmetries. We utilize this framework in
Krishnan 2023
to construct the fermion mass matrices and obtain neutrino oscillation parameters consistent with the current experimental data.
