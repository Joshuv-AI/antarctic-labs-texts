Abstract
. Dispatching systems, where arriving jobs are immediately assigned to one of multiple queues, are ubiquitous in computer systems and service systems. A natural and practically relevant model is one in which each queue serves jobs in FCFS (First-Come First-Served) order. We consider the case where the dispatcher is size-aware , meaning it learns the size (i.e. service time) of each job as it arrives; and state-aware , meaning it always knows the amount of work (i.e. total remaining service time) at each queue. While size- and state-aware dispatching to FCFS queues has been extensively studied, little is known about optimal dispatching for the objective of minimizing mean delay. A major obstacle is that no nontrivial lower bound on mean delay is known, even in heavy traffic (i.e. the limit as load approaches capacity). This makes it difficult to prove that any given policy is optimal, or even heavy-traffic optimal. In this work, we propose the first size- and state-aware dispatching policy that provably minimizes mean delay in heavy traffic. Our policy, called CARD (Controlled Asymmetry Reduces Delay) , keeps all but one of the queues short, then routes as few jobs as possible to the one long queue. We prove an upper bound on CARD’s mean delay, and we prove the first nontrivial lower bound on the mean delay of any size- and state-aware dispatching policy. Both results apply to any number of servers. Our bounds match in heavy traffic, implying CARD’s heavy-traffic optimality. In particular, CARD’s heavy-traffic performance improves upon that of LWL (Least Work Left), SITA (Size Interval Task Assignment), and other policies from the literature whose heavy-traffic performance is known.



1.
Introduction

Dispatching, or load balancing, is at the heart of many computer systems, service systems, transportation systems, and systems in other domains. In such systems, jobs arrive over time, and each job must be irrevocably sent to one of multiple queues as soon as it arrives. It is common for each queue to be served in First-Come First-Served (FCFS) order.

Motivated by the ubiquity of dispatching, we study a classical problem in dispatching theory:

How should one dispatch to FCFS queues to minimize jobs’ mean response time?

Despite the extensive literature on dispatching in queueing theory (see

. But the numerical approach has two drawbacks. First, the curse of dimensionality makes computation impractical for large numbers of queues. Second, the solution is specific to a particular instance (meaning a given number of queues, job size distribution, and load) and one has to solve the MDP again for a different instance.

Below, we describe the intuition behind CARD’s design in a two-server system. See
Fig.

1.2
for an illustration.

To minimize mean response time, one generally wants to avoid situations where small jobs need to wait behind large jobs. One way to do this is to dedicate one server to small jobs and the other server to large jobs, where the size cutoff between “small” and “large” is defined such that half the load is due to each size class. This is the approach taken by the SITA (Size Interval Task Assignment) policy
[
Harchol-Balter et al. 1999
;
Harchol-Balter et al. 2009
]
. Under SITA, due to Poisson splitting, the dispatching system reduces to two independent M/G/1 systems. As shown by
Harchol-Balter et al. 2009
, SITA can sometimes perform very well, but it can sometimes be much worse than simple LWL (Least Work Left) dispatching, under which the system behaves like a central-queue M/G/2.

As each of LWL and SITA can sometimes be worse than the other in heavy traffic, one might expect that they can be strictly improved upon. Indeed, in
Section

B.1
, we show that in the two-server case, both LWL and SITA are strictly suboptimal in heavy traffic. But the question remains: where in LWL or SITA’s design is there a specific opportunity for improvement?

Our key observation is that the main reason SITA performs poorly is that its “short server”, namely the queue to which it sends small jobs, can accumulate lots of work. CARD avoids this issue by actively regulating the amount of work at the short server. To do so, CARD creates a third class of “medium” jobs, which are on the border between small and large, and sets a threshold which serves as a target amount of work at the short server. Whenever a medium job arrives, CARD dispatches it to the short server if and only if the short server has less work than the threshold. This prevents too much work accumulating in the short server, and it also prevents the short server from unduly idling.

CARD’s performance beyond heavy traffic

Of course, practical systems rarely operate at loads very near capacity, but our theoretical bounds on CARD’s performance are admittedly not tight outside the heavy-traffic regime. As such, we also study CARD in simulation across a wider range of loads. We find empirically that CARD has good performance outside of heavy traffic, but slightly modifying CARD can significantly improves performance. Both the original and modified versions of CARD improve upon traditional heuristics like LWL and SITA, sometimes by an order of magnitude. The modified version is competitive with the Dice policy of
Hyytiä and Righter 2022
, the best known heuristic for the size- and state-aware setting. See
Fig.

1.2
for an example where at high load, CARD achieves reductions of over 75% relative to LWL and over 50% compared to SITA.

1.2.
Related Work

FCFS dispatching with incomplete information

Whether a dispatching policy is optimal depends critically on the information available to the dispatcher. When the size of the arriving job is unknown, but server states (e.g. number of jobs at each server, work at each server, etc.) are known (state-aware), depending on the server-state information, Round-Robin (RR)
[
Ephremides et al. 1980
;
Liu and Towsley 1994
;
Liu and Righter 1998
]
, Join-Shortest-Queue (JSQ)
[
Weber 1978
;
Winston 1977
]
, and LWL
[
Daley 1987
;
Foss 1980
;
Koole 1992
;
Akgun et al. 2013
]
are shown to be optimal. The common key idea of these policies is to join the queue with least (or least expected) amount of work.

When only the sizes and the distribution of the arriving jobs are known, SITA is known to be optimal
[
Feng et al. 2005
]
. But this result assumes that the dispatching policy must be entirely static. Recently, it was shown that combining SITA with RR can improve performance
[
Anselmi 2019
;
Hyytiä and Righter 2020
]
, which combines SITA with just a little bit of memory, namely which servers most recently received a job.

Perhaps the closest the SITA line of work gets to size- and state-aware dispatching is the SITA-JSQ policy proposed in
Wang and Down 2014
, in which the dispatcher uses the size of the arriving job and number of jobs at each server to make dispatching decisions. CARD is in some ways similar to SITA-JSQ, particularly the “multi-band” variant of CARD introduced in our simulation study (
Section

7
). But SITA-JSQ does not actively control the amount of work in each queue, and in particular does not maintain a large imbalance between queues. Our lower bound (
Section

4
) shows this imbalance is necessary for heavy-traffic optimality.

FCFS size- and state-aware dispatching

For size- and state-aware FCFS dispatching, various heuristics have been proposed and studied in simulations. Many of them are based on approximate dynamic programming e.g.
[
Hyytiä et al. 2012
;
Hyytiä 2013
;
Hyytiä and Righter 2023
]
. Another class of policies, called sequential dispatching policies, are introduced in
[
Hyytiä et al. 2022
]
. Among the sequential dispatching policies, Dice
[
Hyytiä et al. 2022
]
shows superior performance in simulations and is among the best heuristics that has been developed. In our simulations (
Section

7
), Dice often slightly outperforms CARD. However, there is no theoretical analysis so far on the performance of Dice, even in heavy traffic.

Heavy-traffic Optimality Results

The aforementioned optimality results are strong in the sense that they either show stochastic ordering optimality on sample paths, or show optimality for any load of jobs. For more complicated policies and systems, characterizing the mean response time for an arbitrary load is a difficult task. Therefore, a large number of works focus on analyzing the heavy-traffic regime and establish optimality therein. One approach is to prove optimality via process limits e.g.
[
Kelly and Laws 1993
;
Teh and Ward 2002
]
. Such approach focuses on the transient regime and interchange of limits are usually not established for analysis in steady state. Another approach is to work directly in the stationary regime and establish heavy-traffic optimality results on mean response times in steady state e.g.
[
Eryilmaz and Srikant 2012
;
Zhou et al. 2017
;
Zhou et al. 2018b
]
. However, these optimality results focus on settings where job sizes are unknown, so they do not address our goal of optimal size-aware dispatching.

Tools and Methodology

Recently,
Eryilmaz and Srikant 2012
introduced and popularized a Lyapunov drift-based approach that is applied to study the steady-state performance of
queueing systems in heavy traffic. The approach has been adopted in studying various switches (e.g.
[
Maguluri and Srikant 2016
;
Wang et al. 2017
;
Maguluri et al. 2018
;
Lange and Maguluri 2019
;
Jhunjhunwala and Maguluri 2021
;
Hurtado-Lange et al. 2022
;
Jhunjhunwala and Theja Maguluri 2023
]
), load-balancing algorithms (e.g.
[
Zhou et al. 2017
;
Zhou et al. 2018b
;
Zhou et al. 2018a
;
Weng et al. 2020
;
Hurtado-Lange and Maguluri 2022
;
Liu et al. 2022
]
), and other stochastic models (e.g. wireless scheduling, Stein’s method, mean-field models). In some sense, our paper applies drift method to continuous-time continuous state Markov processes. Our use of the Rate Conservation Law
[
Miyazawa 1994
]
parallels the use of “zero drift” condition in drift analysis. An important step in drift analysis is establishing state-space collapse. We prove a result of this type in
Lemma

5.2
.

Other Relevant Work

When scheduling is allowed at the servers, optimal dispatching policies can be very different. When there are multiple parallel SRPT servers,
Down and Wu 2006
study a multi-layer dispatching policy and show optimality using a diffusion limit argument.
Grosof et al. 2019
develop a dispatching policy, called
Guardrails
, that achieves optimal mean response time in heavy traffic.
2
2

2

Guardrails is optimal in the sense that the mean response time under Guardrails matches that of a resource-pooled SRPT in heavy-traffic. As
Theorem

3.1
suggests, dispatching to FCFS servers cannot in general match the performance of a resource-pooled SRPT in heavy-traffic. Therefore, in heavy-traffic, an optimal dispatching policy to SRPT servers generally outperforms an optimal policy to FCFS servers in terms of mean response time.
Both of these prior dispatching policies involve, roughly speaking, balancing work evenly across the multiple SRPT servers. This is in contrast to CARD, which maintains a large imbalance between the multiple FCFS servers. One interpretation is that while SRPT prioritizes jobs at each individual server, CARD prioritizes jobs at the dispatching stage, namely by sending shorter jobs to servers with less work.

In recent years, learning-based dispatching policies have also been studied in literature
[
Samúelsson and Hyytiä 2018
;
Fu and Modiano 2022
]
. CARD involves tuning some parameters that depend on the job size distribution, and we thus assume knowledge of the job size distribution. An interesting question for future work is whether CARD’s parameters could be learned online in settings where the job size distribution is unknown.

In the context of scheduling jobs on a single server, when SRPT (Shortest Remaining Processing Time) is shown to be optimal
[
Schrage 1968
]
,
Chen and Dong 2021
show that having two priority classes is sufficient for a good performance in heavy traffic. The heavy-traffic performance of CARD ends up roughly equivalent to the performance of a single-server system with two priority classes. However, we cannot match the performance demonstrated by
Chen and Dong 2021
: they decrease the fraction of load in the lower-priority class to zero in heavy traffic, whereas CARD’s “lower-priority jobs”, namely those sent to the long server, must constitute a roughly
\frac{1}{n}
fraction of the load.

System Model and the CARD Policy

2.1.
Model Description

We consider a system of
n\geqslant 2
identical FCFS (First-Come, First-Served) servers, each of which has its own queue. The system has one central dispatcher, which immediately dispatches jobs to a server when they arrive. We consider M/G job arrivals with (Poisson) arrival rate
\lambda
and job size distribution
S
. We assume
\mathbb{E}[S^{2}]<\infty
. The system load, namely the average rate at which work arrives, is
\rho=\lambda\mathbb{E}[S]
. We assume a server never idles unless there are no jobs present in its queue.

We use the convention that each server completes work at rate
\frac{1}{n}
, so a job of size
s
requires
ns
time in service. This convention means the largest possible stability region is
\rho\in[0,1)
, regardless of the number of servers
n
. The convention is also convenient when comparing our system’s performance to that of a “resource-pooled” M/G/1 with the same arrival process and server speed
1
. We write
\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathrm{M/G/1}}]
for the mean amount of work in such a resource-pooled M/G/1.

We consider
size- and state-aware
dispatching policies. That is, when a job arrives, the dispatcher may use both the job’s size and the system state to decide where to dispatch it to. For our purposes, the most important aspect of the system state is the amount of
work
remaining at each server. We write
W_{i}
for the amount of work at server
i
(but see also
Section

2.2
),
\mathbf{W}=(W_{1},\dots,W_{n})
for the vector of work amounts, and
W_{\mathsf{all}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}
for the total work. We write
W_{i}(t)
or
\mathbf{W}(t)
when discussing work at a specific time
t
.

The main metric we consider is
mean response time
. A job’s response time is the amount of time between its arrival and completion. Due to our
\frac{1}{n}
service rate convention, if a job of size
s
is dispatched to a server with
w
work, the job’s response time is
n(w+s)
. We write
\mathbb{E}[T_{\pi}]
for the mean response time over all jobs (in the usual limiting long-run average sense) under policy
\pi
.

Purely for simplicity of notation, we assume the job size distribution
S
has no atoms. This is to ensure that expressions like
\mathbb{E}[S\mathcal{I}(S<m)]
are continuous functions of
m
. One can generalize all of our definitions and results to distributions with atoms using a lexicographic ordering trick.
3
3

3

Have the system assign each job an i.i.d. uniform
U\in[0,1]
independent of its size
S
, and replace comparisons
S<m
with comparisons
(S,U)\prec(m,v)
for some
v\in[0,1]
, where
\prec
is the lexicographic order. If
\mathbb{E}[S\mathcal{I}((S,U)\prec(m,v))]
has a jump discontinuity at
m
, varying
v
interpolates continuously between the left and right limits.

2.2.
Defining the CARD Policy

We now introduce our policy,
CARD
, which stands for
Controlled Asymmetry Reduces Delay
. We first present it in the context of
n=2
servers, then generalize to
n\geqslant 2
servers.

CARD for two servers

In the
n=2
case, CARD designates server 1 as the
short server
and server 2 as the
long server
. To emphasize this, when discussing CARD, we write
W_{s}=W_{1}
and
W_{\ell}=W_{2}
for the work at the short and long servers, respectively.

CARD has three threshold parameters to set:

•

The two
size thresholds

m_{-}
and
m_{+}
,
0\leqslant m_{-}\leqslant m_{+}
, divide jobs into small, medium, and large (see below).

•

The
work threshold

c
,
c\geqslant m_{+}
is, roughly speaking, a target work level for the short server.

Based on these parameters, CARD dispatches jobs as follows (see also
Fig.

1.2
):

•

A
small job
, namely one with size in
[0,m_{-})
, is always dispatched to the short server.

•

A
medium job
, namely one with size in
[m_{-},m_{+})
, is dispatched depending on
W_{s}
at time of arrival. If
W_{s}\leqslant c
, it is sent to the short server, and if
W_{s}>c
, it is sent to the long server.

•

A
large job
, namely one with size in
[m_{+},\infty)
, is always dispatched to the long server.

Setting CARD’s parameters

There are a range of ways to set
m_{-}
,
m_{+}
, and
c
that yield stability and heavy-traffic optimality. We specify these formally in the statements of
Theorems

3.2
and
3.3
, but we highlight the key points here (see also
Section

2.3
).

The size thresholds
m_{-}
and
m_{+}
should be chosen such that small jobs and large jobs are each less than half the load. Formally, we require

\mathbb{E}[S\mathcal{I}(S<m_{-})]<\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[S]<\mathbb{E}[S\mathcal{I}(S<m_{+})].

In particular, we have
m_{-}<m<m_{+}
, where
m
is the solution to
\mathbb{E}[S\mathcal{I}(S<m)]=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[S]
. As we show in our lower bound (
Theorem

3.1
), this value
m
is in some sense the ideal cutoff between small and large jobs. As such, it is important that in heavy traffic, either
m_{-}\to m
or
m_{+}\to m
(or both). We do the former in our upper bound (
Theorem

3.3
).

The work threshold
c
must balance a tradeoff between two concerns. On one hand, we want there to be little work at the short server so that small jobs have low response times. On the other hand, we do not want the short server to run out of work, as excessive idling could increase response times or even cause instability. Roughly speaking, this means setting
c=\Theta\big(\big(\frac{1}{1-\rho}\big)^{p}\big)
for a suitable choice of
p\in(0,1)
.

It is convenient in our proofs to ensure
c\geqslant m_{+}
, so we assume this throughout. It also makes intuitive sense that a single medium job should not bring the short server from empty to above the work threshold. However, this assumption can be easily relaxed at the cost of a little more computation in the proofs.

Generalizing CARD to any number of servers

We now generalize the above policy to
n\geqslant 2
servers. Here we focus on an extension that prioritizes simplicity of analysis while still achieving optimal heavy-traffic performance. In our simulation study (
Section

7
), we consider a more complex variant which has better performance at practical loads.

The basic idea of
n
-server CARD is to reduce to the two-server case. We use the same three parameters
m_{-}
,
m_{+}
, and
c
, and we define small, medium, and large jobs in the same way. The only difference is that instead of one short and one long server, we use
n-1
short servers
1,\dots,n-1
and a single long server
n
. We thus write
W_{s_{i}}=W_{i}
and
W_{\ell}=W_{n}
when discussing
n
-server CARD. Abusing notation slightly, we write simply
W_{s}
when discussing a generic short server whose index is not important.
Jobs are dispatched as follows:

•

A small job is always dispatched to a uniformly random short server.

•

A medium job is dispatched as follows. The dispatcher selects a uniformly random short server
N\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
and inspects its amount of work
W_{s_{N}}
. If
W_{s_{N}}\leqslant c
, the job is dispatched to the chosen short server
N
, and if
W_{s_{N}}>c
, it is dispatched to the long server.

•

A large job is always dispatched to the long server.

Another way to view
n
-server CARD is in the following distributed manner. Suppose that instead of one dispatcher, we have
n-1
independent “subdispatchers”, each associated with a short server, and suppose that all jobs arrive at a uniformly random dispatcher. Then
n
-server CARD is the result of each of the subdispatchers using two-server CARD, except they all share the same long server.

The way we set the parameters of
n
-server CARD is essentially the same as how we set the parameters of two-server CARD. The only difference is that instead of wanting small and large jobs to both have less than half the load, we want small jobs to be less than a
1-\frac{1}{n}
fraction of the load, and we want large jobs to be less than a
\frac{1}{n}
fraction of the load. We therefore set

\mathbb{E}[S\mathcal{I}(S<m_{-})]<\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\mathbb{E}[S]<\mathbb{E}[S\mathcal{I}(S<m_{+})].

This means
m_{-}<m<m_{+}
, where now
m
is the solution to
\mathbb{E}[S\mathcal{I}(S<m)]=\big(1-\frac{1}{n}\big)\mathbb{E}[S]
.

2.3.
Key Definitions for Main Results and Analysis

We state our main results and perform our analysis in terms of the following quantities.

Drift-related quantities

The following quantities are related to characterizing
drifts
, which are the average rates at which work increases or decreases in various situations.

•

Let
\varepsilon=1-\rho
. If both servers are busy, then
W_{\mathsf{all}}
has drift
-\varepsilon
.

•

Let
\rho_{s}
,
\rho_{m}
, and
\rho_{\ell}
be the loads due to small, medium, and large jobs, respectively:

\rho_{s}=\lambda\mathbb{E}[S\mathcal{I}(S<m_{-})],\qquad\rho_{m}=\lambda\mathbb{E}[S\mathcal{I}(m_{-}\leqslant S<m_{+})],\qquad\rho_{\ell}=\lambda\mathbb{E}[S\mathcal{I}(S\geqslant m_{+})].

•

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be the following quantities related to the drift of
W_{s}
:

\alpha=\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n-1}\rho_{s},\qquad\beta=\frac{1}{n-1}(\rho_{s}+\rho_{m})-\frac{1}{n}.

If
W_{s}>c
, then
W_{s}
has drift
-\alpha
, and if
0<W_{s}\leqslant c
, then
W_{s}
has drift
+\beta
.

•

Let
\delta\in(0,\varepsilon]
be a bound on the probability the short server is idle, i.e.
\mathbb{P}[W_{s}=0]\leqslant\delta
. We show how to set CARD’s parameters to achieve this bound in
Theorem

3.2
(a).

To specify CARD’s
m_{-}
and
m_{+}
parameters, it suffices to specify
\alpha
and
\beta
: these determine
\rho_{s}
and
\rho_{m}
, which in turn determine
m_{-}
and
m_{+}
. Moreover, for any given
\beta
, we show in
Theorem

3.2
how to set CARD’s
c
parameter to achieve
\mathbb{P}[W_{s}=0]\leqslant\delta
. As such:

Instead of specifying
m_{-}
,
m_{+}
, and
c
directly, we specify
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\delta
.

In particular,
Theorem

3.3
specifies how
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\delta
should scale as functions of
\varepsilon
.

Heavy traffic

Our main results consider the
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
limit, which we call the
heavy-traffic
regime. This is equivalent to
\lambda\uparrow 1/\mathbb{E}[S]
. In particular, we leave the number of servers fixed.

Underlying our results are explicit bounds that hold even outside the limiting regime (see e.g.
Theorem

6.11
). Because of our focus on heavy traffic, we assume for convenience that
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{n}
. In particular, this ensures we can set
\beta>0
, which ensures that
W_{s}
always drifts towards
c
. The case where
\varepsilon>\frac{1}{n}
and
\beta<0
is less interesting, as then both
W_{s}
and
W_{\ell}
always drift towards

.

3.1.
Intuition for Lower Bound on All Policies

We now give some intuition for
Theorem

3.1
. We focus on the heavy-traffic regime, where our aim is to show that the best possible mean response time is roughly
\mathbb{E}[T]\approx K_{\mathrm{CARD}}\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathrm{M/G/1}}]
.

To begin, recall
\mathbb{E}[T]=\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbb{E}[N]
, where
\mathbb{E}[N]
is the mean number of jobs in the system. The key idea is to relate
\mathbb{E}[N]
to the mean amount of work
\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]
. This is helpful because one can easily show
\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]\geqslant\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathrm{M/G/1}}]
(see e.g.
Theorem

6.1
).

How can we relate
\mathbb{E}[N]
to
\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]
? In heavy traffic, most jobs in the system are waiting in a queue and have yet to enter service. We thus approximate
\mathbb{E}[N]\approx\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]/\mathbb{E}[S_{\mathsf{queue}}]
, where
\mathbb{E}[S_{\mathsf{queue}}]
is the mean size of jobs waiting in a queue. This means minimizing
\mathbb{E}[T]
amounts to maximizing the mean size of jobs waiting in the queue. This makes sense in light of the fact that when studying scheduling policies beyond FCFS, serving small jobs ahead of large jobs reduces mean response time
[
Schrage 1968
]
.

What is the largest that
\mathbb{E}[S_{\mathsf{queue}}]
can be? Because we are restricted to FCFS service, the only mechanism by which we can affect the sizes of jobs in the system is dispatching. In particular, we can dispatch jobs of different sizes to different servers. Suppose, for example, that servers
1,\dots,n-1
have a negligible amount of work, meaning nearly all of the work is at server
n
. Then
\mathbb{E}[S_{\mathsf{queue}}]
would be the average size of jobs dispatched to server
n
, which could be much greater than
\mathbb{E}[S]
. The best we could hope to do is
\mathbb{E}[S_{\mathsf{queue}}]=\mathbb{E}[S\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}S\geqslant m]
for as high a threshold
m
as possible. But in heavy traffic, we need server
n
to handle a
\frac{1}{n}
fraction of the load, so the largest value of
m
possible solves
\mathbb{E}[S\mathcal{I}(S\geqslant m)]=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[S]
. This is equivalent to the characterization of
m
from
Section

2.3
, so it leads to
\mathbb{E}[T]/\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]\approx\mathbb{E}[S]/\mathbb{E}[S\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}S\geqslant m]=K_{\mathrm{CARD}}
. Observing
\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]\geqslant\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathrm{M/G/1}}]
completes the bound.

To make this reasoning rigorous, it turns out that reasoning directly in terms of
\mathbb{E}[S_{\mathsf{queue}}]
is difficult. We instead prove
Theorem

3.1
using a potential-function approach. However, the potential function and manipulations we perform on it were directly inspired by the intuition:

The best-case scenario is to dedicate one server to the jobs of size at least
m
, and to ensure that all other servers have a negligible amount of work.

3.2.
Intuition for Upper Bound on CARD

We now give some intuition for
Theorem

3.3
. By the lower bound intuition above, CARD is already well on its way to achieving the best-case scenario: it attempts to keep the amount of work at the
n-1
short servers near
c
, and the long server only serves medium and large jobs. To show CARD matches the lower bound in heavy traffic, it would suffice to show the following.

•

CARD does not have much more work than a resource-pooled M/G/1:
\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]\approx\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathrm{M/G/1}}]
.

–

Roughly speaking, this amounts to showing that we avoid situations where one server is idle while another server has lots of work (see
Theorem

6.1
).

•

CARD’s short servers do not exceed
c
work by too much:
\mathbb{E}[W_{s}]\approx c
.

–

We also need to set
c
such that it is negligible in heavy traffic.

•

CARD rarely dispatches medium jobs to the long server:
\mathbb{P}[W_{s}\leqslant c]\approx 1
.

Our main tool for showing these and related properties is examining what we call
below-above cycles
. Consider a particular short server. It alternates between
below periods
, during which
W_{s}\leqslant c
, and
above periods
, during which
W_{s}>c
. It turns out that much of our analysis rests on below-above cycles not being too long. One reason for this is that when enough short servers are in above periods, the long server is temporarily overloaded. Long periods of transient overload could cause
\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]
to be significantly greater than
\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathrm{M/G/1}}]
. Short below-above cycles prevent this possibility. See
Section

6.1
for more details about how we use below-above cycles.

4.
Universal Lower Bound

See
3.1

Before diving into the proof, we give the high-level idea for
n=2
servers.

Suppose an arrival occurs while
W_{1}<W_{2}
. For that individual arrival, its response time if it were sent to queue
i
would be
2W_{i}
because each server processes work at rate
1/2
, so the “benefit” of sending it to queue
1
instead of queue
2
is
2(W_{2}-W_{1})
. Reasoning symmetrically if
W_{1}<W_{2}
, we conclude that the benefit of dispatching jobs to the shorter queue is proportional to
|W_{2}-W_{1}|
.

The main challenge is therefore to show that no dispatching policy can both frequently dispatch to the shorter queue, and also maintain large difference
|W_{2}-W_{1}|
between the queues. The key observation is that if we dispatch the job to the shorter queue, then
|W_{2}-W_{1}|
decreases, so the next arrival would see less benefit. That is, we can view
|W_{2}-W_{1}|
as a type of resource: dispatching jobs to the shorter queue depletes it, while dispatching jobs to the longer queue replenishes it. It is thus best to dispatch shorter jobs to the shorter queue, which slowly depletes
|W_{2}-W_{1}|
, and dispatch longer jobs to the longer queue, which quickly replenishes
|W_{2}-W_{1}|
. To formalize the idea of viewing
|W_{2}-W_{1}|
as a resource, we use the potential function
\tfrac{1}{2}(W_{2}-W_{1})^{2}
.

The proof below handles any number of servers
n
. The idea is essentially the same as the
n=2
case, except we look at the work differences
|W_{i}-W_{j}|
for every pair of servers
i\neq j
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

Consider an arbitrary stationary dispatching policy
\pi
. We first introduce notation for
\pi
’s dispatching decisions. Suppose a job of random size
S
arrives and observes work vector
\mathbf{W}=(W_{1},\dots,W_{n})
. We denote by
W_{\mathsf{choice}}
the work at the queue the arrival is dispatched to. Note that while
S
is independent of
\mathbf{W}
, it is
not
independent of
W_{\mathsf{choice}}
. We also write
W_{\mathsf{all}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}
for the total work at all queues. Because each server does work at rate
1/n
, we can write
\mathbb{E}[T_{\pi}]
as

\mathbb{E}[T_{\pi}]=n\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{choice}}+S]=\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]+\mathbb{E}[nW_{\mathsf{choice}}-W_{\mathsf{all}}]+n\mathbb{E}[S].

The main task is to give a lower bound on
\mathbb{E}[nW_{\mathsf{choice}}-W_{\mathsf{all}}]
. To do so, we apply the rate conservation law of
Miyazawa 1994
to
V(\mathbf{W})
, where

V(\mathbf{w})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}(w_{i}-w_{j})^{2}.

The value of
V(\mathbf{W})
can change in two ways.

•

Work is done continuously at each nonempty queue. We denote this average continuous change by
\mathbb{E}[D_{t}V(\mathbf{W})]
.

•

Arrivals add work to whichever queue the dispatcher chooses. By PASTA (Poisson Arrivals See Time Averages)
[
Wolff 1982
]
, this yields average change
\lambda\mathbb{E}[V(\mathbf{W}+S\mathbf{e}_{\mathsf{choice}})-V(\mathbf{W})]
, where
\mathbf{e}_{\mathsf{choice}}
is the standard basis vector with a
1
indicating the queue the job is dispatched to.

The rate conservation law
[
Miyazawa 1994
]
states that the average rate of change of
V(\mathbf{W})
is zero, so

\mathbb{E}[D_{t}V(\mathbf{W})]+\lambda\mathbb{E}[V(\mathbf{W}+S\mathbf{e}_{\mathsf{choice}})-V(\mathbf{W})]=0.

We now investigate each of the two terms in
Section

4
. We first observe that
\mathbb{E}[D_{t}V(\mathbf{W})]\leqslant 0
, because in the absence of arrivals, for any two queues
i
and
j
, the absolute difference
|W_{i}-W_{j}|
either decreases (if exactly one server is idle) or stays constant (otherwise). Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[V(\mathbf{W}+S\mathbf{e}_{\mathsf{choice}})-V(\mathbf{W})]\geqslant 0.

Expanding the definition of
V(\mathbf{w})
and writing
\sum_{i\neq\mathsf{choice}}
for sums over all queues other than the one the job is dispatched to, we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\neq\mathsf{choice}}\big((W_{\mathsf{choice}}+S-W_{i})^{2}-(W_{\mathsf{choice}}-W_{i})^{2}\big)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{n-1}{2}\mathbb{E}[S^{2}]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\neq\mathsf{choice}}S(W_{\mathsf{choice}}-W_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{n-1}{2}\mathbb{E}[S^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[S(nW_{\mathsf{choice}}-W_{\mathsf{all}})].

Subtracting both sides from
m\mathbb{E}[nW_{\mathsf{choice}}-W_{\mathsf{all}}]
and using the fact that

-W_{\mathsf{all}}\leqslant nW_{\mathsf{choice}}-W_{\mathsf{all}}\leqslant(n-1)W_{\mathsf{all}},

we obtain

\displaystyle m\mathbb{E}[nW_{\mathsf{choice}}-W_{\mathsf{all}}]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbb{E}[(m-S)(nW_{\mathsf{choice}}-W_{\mathsf{all}})]-\frac{n-1}{2}\mathbb{E}[S^{2}]

\displaystyle\geqslant-\mathbb{E}\big[(S-m)^{+}(n-1)W_{\mathsf{all}}-(m-S)^{+}W_{\mathsf{all}}\big]-\frac{n-1}{2}\mathbb{E}[S^{2}]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\textrm{(a)}}}}{{=}}-\big(\mathbb{E}\big[(n-1)(S-m)^{+}+(m-S)^{+}\big]\,\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]+\frac{n-1}{2}\mathbb{E}[S^{2}]\big)

\displaystyle=-\big((m-\mathbb{E}[S]+n\mathbb{E}[(S-m)^{+}])\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]+\frac{n-1}{2}\mathbb{E}[S^{2}]\big),

where (a) follows from the fact that an arriving job’s size
S
is independent of the work vector
\mathbf{W}
it observes upon arrival.

We now substitute the bound from
Section

4
into
Section

4
, obtaining

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{\pi}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}[S]-n\mathbb{E}[(S-m)^{+}]}{m}\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]-\frac{(n-1)\mathbb{E}[S^{2}]}{2m}+n\mathbb{E}[S].

The bound follows from
\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]\geqslant\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathrm{M/G/1}}]
(see e.g.
Theorem

6.1
) and
Section

2.3
, which implies

\mathbb{E}[S]-n\mathbb{E}[(S-m)^{+}]=\mathbb{E}[S]-n\mathbb{E}[S\mathcal{I}(S>m)]+mn\mathbb{P}[S>m]=mn\mathbb{P}[S>m]=mK_{\mathrm{CARD}}.\qed

5.
CARD Stability Analysis

Proving CARD’s stability is more than a straightforward application of the Foster-Lyapunov theorem, which is widely used to establish stability of queueing systems. The main obstacle here is that the long server alternates between being underloaded and overloaded. It is thus difficult to find a Lyapunov function that is negative outside a compact set.

To overcome this obstacle, we use a result of
Foss et al. 2012
. Notice that, under CARD,
W_{s}
is itself a Markov process because the decision of where to dispatch a job depends only on the work at the shorter server. Roughly,
[
Foss et al. 2012
, Theorem 1]
says that since
W_{s}
is a Markov process of its own, if it is ergodic, then it suffices to do a drift analysis of
W_{\ell}
,
averaged over
the stationary distribution of
W_{s}
. Of course, we first need to show that
W_{s}
is ergodic. Our proof for CARD’s stability therefore proceeds in three steps.

•

We show that the short server’s work
W_{s}(t)
, as a Markov process of its own, is Harris ergodic (
Lemma

5.1
).

•

With the stability of
W_{s}(t)
in hand, we bound the idleness probability of the short server in steady state (
Lemmas

5.2
and
5.3
).

•

We apply the result of
Foss et al. 2012
(
Theorem

3.2
) to show stability whenever the long server is
on average
not overloaded. Our bound on the short server’s idleness probability from the previous step thus gives a sufficient condition for stability.

Armed with these key ideas, the proofs themselves are relatively straightforward, with the bulk of the work being computation. As such, we defer most of these computation details to
Section

B.2
.

Lemma 5.1.

W_{s}
is Harris ergodic for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof sketch.

The proof uses a Foster-Lyapunov theorem for continuous-time Markov processes
[
Meyn and Tweedie 1993b
, Theorem 4.2]
. The key step is to verify that the Lyapunov function
V(w_{s})=w_{s}
has bounded drift when
w_{s}\leqslant c
and negative drift when
w_{s}>c
. This is true because when
w_{s}>c
, we only send small jobs to the short server. We defer the details to
Section

B.2
.

We establish our short server idleness bound by first proving a general bound on the probability that
W_{s}
is lower than
c
by a general amount
x
. The idleness bound follows by plugging in
x=c
.

Lemma 5.2.

Suppose
\theta>0
satisfies
\widetilde{(S_{s,m})_{e}}(\theta)>\frac{1}{n(n-1)\beta+n-1}
, where
\widetilde{(S_{s,m})_{e}}(\cdot)
is the Laplace transform of the equilibium distribution of the size of small and medium jobs,
S_{s,m}=(S\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}S<m_{+})
. Then for all
x\in[0,c]
,

\mathbb{P}[W_{s}<c-x]\leqslant\frac{\left(n(n-1)\beta+n-1\right)\widetilde{(S_{s,m})_{e}}(\theta)}{\left(n(n-1)\beta+n-1\right)\widetilde{(S_{s,m})_{e}}(\theta)-1}e^{-\theta x}.

Proof sketch.

This result is a Chernoff-type bound on
(c-W_{s})^{+}
, so the main task is to bound
\mathbb{E}[\exp(\theta(c-W_{s})^{+})]
. We do this by applying the rate conservation law
[
Miyazawa 1994
]
to
\exp(\theta(c-W_{s})^{+})
. We defer the details to
Section

B.2
.

Lemma 5.3.

We have the following bound on the idleness of the short server,

\mathbb{P}[W_{s}=0]\leqslant\frac{n+1}{n\beta}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta c}{n(n-1)m_{+}}\right).

Proof.

Let
\theta=\frac{\beta}{n(n-1)}\frac{1}{m_{+}}
. Since
\beta<\tfrac{1}{n(n-1)}
and all small and medium jobs have length at most
m_{+}
, we have
\widetilde{(S_{s,m})_{e}}(\theta)\geqslant 1-\theta\mathbb{E}[(S_{s,m})_{e}]\geqslant 1-\frac{\beta}{n(n-1)}
. We can therefore apply
Lemma

5.2
, from which the bound follows by the computation below and setting
x=c
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[W_{s}<c-x]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\left(n(n-1)\beta+n-1\right)\left(1-\frac{\beta}{n(n-1)}\right)}{\left(n(n-1)\beta+n-1\right)\left(1-\frac{\beta}{n(n-1)}\right)-1}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta x}{n(n-1)m_{+}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{n+1}{n\beta}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta x}{n(n-1)m_{+}}\right).\qed

We defer the proof of
Theorem

3.2
to
Section

B.2
.

6.
CARD Mean Response Time Analysis

With the lower bound from
Theorem

3.1
in mind, our next step is to establish an upper bound on the mean response time under CARD. We focus here on the two-server case. The general case uses the same ideas but has more complicated computations, so we defer its proof to
Section

B.4
.

Let
\mathbb{E}[T_{{\mathrm{CARD}},s}]
,
\mathbb{E}[T_{{\mathrm{CARD}},m}]
, and
\mathbb{E}[T_{{\mathrm{CARD}},\ell}]
be the mean response times of small, medium, and large jobs under CARD, respectively. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{{\mathrm{CARD}}}]

\displaystyle=p_{s}\mathbb{E}[T_{{\mathrm{CARD}},s}]+p_{m}\mathbb{E}[T_{{\mathrm{CARD}},m}]+p_{\ell}\mathbb{E}[T_{{\mathrm{CARD}},\ell}]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\mathbb{E}[S]+2p_{s}\mathbb{E}[W_{s}]+2p_{m}c\mathbb{P}[W_{s}\leqslant c]+2p_{m}\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}>c)]+2p_{\ell}\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}].

where the inequality follows from how CARD dispatches jobs, the PASTA property
[
Wolff 1982
]
, and the fact that the servers complete work at rate
1/2
. The main difficulty of analyzing (
6
) lies in bounding
\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}]
and
\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}>c)]
. We now give a high-level overview of the obstacles and our approach.

6.1.
Key Ingredients: Work Decomposition, Below-Above Cycles, and Palm Inversion

To bound
\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}]
, it suffices to bound
\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]
. The following theorem, called the
work decomposition law

[
Scully et al. 2020
;
Scully 2022
]
, provides a way to bound
\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]
. We state it below in a way that is specialized to our system.

Theorem 6.1.

Denote by
I
the fraction of servers that are idle in steady state, namely

I=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{I}(W_{i}=0).

The steady-state mean total work
\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]=\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathrm{M/G/1}}]+\frac{\mathbb{E}[IW_{\mathsf{all}}]}{\varepsilon}=\frac{\frac{\lambda}{2}\mathbb{E}[S^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[IW_{\mathsf{all}}]}{\varepsilon},

where
\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathrm{M/G/1}}]
is the work in an M/G/1 with arrival rate
\lambda
and job size distribution
S
.

The key component we need to bound from
Theorem

6.1
is
\mathbb{E}[IW_{\mathsf{all}}]
. We would like to study

\displaystyle IW_{\mathsf{all}}

\displaystyle=(W_{s}+W_{\ell})\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}=0)+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{I}(W_{\ell}=0)\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}W_{\ell}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}=0)+\tfrac{1}{2}W_{s}\mathcal{I}(W_{\ell}=0)

The main difficulty here is to bound
\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}=0)]
. Since CARD dispatches differently to the long server based on the state of the short server,
W_{\ell}
depends on the state of
W_{s}
. Such a dependency also poses challenges in analyzing
\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}W_{s}>c]
, when even knowing
\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}]
is not sufficient.

Under CARD,
W_{s}
alternates between being above and below the threshold
c
. Such a behavior naturally leads to renewal intervals consists of the “above” periods and “below” periods.

Definition 6.2.

We partition time into alternating intervals, called
below periods
and
above periods
, as follows:

•

A time
t
is in a
below period
if
W_{s}(t)\leqslant c
.

•

A time
t
is in an
above period
if
W_{s}(t)>c
.

A
below-above cycle
is then a complete below period followed by a complete above period. Below-above cycles start at times
t
for which
W_{s}(t)=c
. We can partition time into below-above cycles.

We introduce the following notation for working with below periods, above periods, and below-above cycles:

•

We write
\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}[\cdot]
for the Palm expectation
[
Baccelli and Brémaud 2002
]
taken at the start of a below-above cycle. Roughly speaking,
\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}[\cdot]=\text{``}\mathbb{E}[\cdot\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}\text{a below period starts at time~$0$}]\text{''}
, but the formal definition avoids conditioning on a measure-zero event.

•

In the context of a below-above cycle starting at time
0
, meaning
W_{s}(0)=c
, we denote the lengths of the below and above period by
B
and
A
, respectively:

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\inf\{t>0:W_{s}(t)>c\},

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\inf\{t>B:W_{s}(t)=c\}.

Abusing notation slightly, we also use
B
and
A
to denote the lengths of the below and above period in a generic below-above cycle, not necessarily one that starts at time
0
.

Why are above and below periods helpful for analyzing CARD? Within an above or below period, CARD does not change how it dispatches jobs, making it easier to analyze
W_{\ell}
within
one
below-above cycle. The Palm inversion formula
[
Baccelli and Brémaud 2002
]
, which is a generalization of the celebrated renewal-reward theorem, allows us to connect the average behavior of
W_{\ell}
within one below and above cycle to a steady-state average. For example, it implies

\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}]=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[A+B]}\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}\left[\int_{0}^{A+B}W_{\ell}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}\right],\qquad\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}>c)]=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[A+B]}\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}\left[\int_{B}^{A+B}W_{\ell}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}\right].

Our high-level idea is to relate both of these quantities to
\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}[W_{\ell}(0)]
, the mean work at the long server at the start of a below-above cycle. We show in
Lemmas

6.6
and
6.7
that, roughly speaking,

\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}]\approx\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}[W_{\ell}(0)],\qquad\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}>c)]\approx\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}[W_{\ell}(0)]\,\mathbb{P}[W_{s}>c].

The rest of this section is organized as follows.

•

Section

6.2
analyzes the behavior of the short server. In particular, we show that above and below cycles are not too long.

•

Section

6.3
analyzes the behavior of the long server. Using the fact that above and below cycles are not too long, we show
Section

6.1
. As part of this, we bound
\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]
.

•

Section

6.4
assembles the pieces to prove
Theorem

3.3
.

6.2.
Analyzing the Short Server and Below-Above Cycles

In this section, we bound various quantities relating to work at the short server and the below-above cycles. Of particular importance are the mean
excesses
of the above and below periods
\mathbb{E}[A_{e}]
and
\mathbb{E}[B_{e}]
, as they are used to better understand the relations between
\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}]
and
\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}[W_{\ell}(0)]
.

The techniques we use to obtain bounds on
\mathbb{E}[A_{e}]
and
\mathbb{E}[B_{e}]
also immediately yield bounds on
\mathbb{E}[A]
and
\mathbb{E}[B]
. Despite not using these bounds, given that they help complete the picture of how the system behaves, we state them, too.

As a reminder, the
excess
or
equilibrium distribution
of a random variable
V
is the distribution
V_{e}
whose probability density function is
f(t)=\mathbb{P}[V>t]/\mathbb{E}[V]
. The excess arises naturally in renewal theory
[
Baccelli and Brémaud 2002
;
Harchol-Balter 2013
;
Ross 1995
]
. Most important for our purposes is the fact that

\mathbb{E}[V_{e}]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[V^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[V]}.

Lemma 6.3.

\mathbb{E}[B]\leqslant\frac{m_{+}}{\beta},\qquad\mathbb{E}[B_{e}]\leqslant\frac{c+m_{+}}{\beta}\leqslant\frac{2c}{\beta},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{E}[(B_{e})_{e}]\leqslant\frac{c+m_{+}}{\beta}\leqslant\frac{2c}{\beta}.

Proof.

Suppose that at time
0
, the short server has
W_{s}(0)=v\leqslant c
work, so time
0
is in a below period. Let
\tau(v)
be the time until the end of the below period. We will show

\mathbb{E}[\tau(v)]\leqslant\frac{c+m_{+}-v}{\beta}\leqslant\frac{c+m_{+}}{\beta}\leqslant\frac{2c}{\beta},

where the last step follows because
c\geqslant m_{+}
(
Section

2.2
). This implies all three of the bounds.

•

A below period starts with
c
work at the short server, so
\mathbb{E}[B]=\mathbb{E}[\tau(c)]\leqslant\frac{m_{+}}{\beta}
.

•

The excesses
B_{e}
and
(B_{e})_{e}
can both be interpreted as the distribution of the amount of time until the below period ends, starting from some random amount of work at the short server, so their means can each be written as
\mathbb{E}[\tau(V)]
for an appropriate variable
V
.

It remains only to show
Section

6.2
, which we do using a supermartingale argument. Suppose
W_{s}(0)=v
as above, and define
X(t)=c-W_{s}(t)+\beta t
. We now show that
X(t)
is a supermartingale with respect to the Markov process
W_{s}(t)
. Let

•

\Delta_{s}(u,t)
be the amount of work completed by the short server during
(u,t]
and

•

\Sigma_{s}(u,t)
be the amount of work that arrives to the short server during
(u,t]
.

For any
0\leqslant u\leqslant t
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X(t)\mid W_{s}(u)]-X(u)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[W_{s}(u)-W_{s}(t)\mid W_{s}(u)]+\beta(t-u)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{s}(u,t)-\Sigma_{s}(u,t)\mid W_{s}(u)]+\beta(t-u)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}(t-u)-\Sigma_{s}(u,t)\mid W_{s}(u)\right]+\beta(t-u)

\displaystyle=(t-u)\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\rho_{s}-\rho_{\ell}+\beta\right)=0,

so
X(t)
is indeed a supermartingale. Applying the optional stopping theorem to
X(t)
and
\tau(v)
, which we justify below, yields

c-v=\mathbb{E}[X(0)]\geqslant\mathbb{E}[X(\tau(v))]=c-W_{s}(\tau(v))+\beta\tau(v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\textrm{(a)}}}}{{\geqslant}}-m_{+}+\beta\tau(v),

from which
Section

6.2
follows. Above, (a) uses the fact that all medium jobs have size at most
m_{+}
, so at the moment the below period ends, the short server’s work can jump to at most
c+m_{+}
.

All that remains is to verify that we can indeed apply the optional stopping theorem.

•

We have
\mathbb{E}[\tau(v)]<\infty
by positive recurrence of
W_{s}(t)
.

•

We have uniform integrability, namely
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[X(t)\,\mathcal{I}(\tau(v)>t)]=0
, thanks to the following two observations. First,
\mathbb{E}[W_{s}(t)\,\mathcal{I}(\tau(v)>t)]\to 0
because
c-W_{s}(t)\in[0,c]
when
t
is in a below period. Second,
\mathbb{E}[\beta t\,\mathcal{I}(\tau(v)>t)]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[\beta\tau(v)\,\mathcal{I}(\tau(v)>t)]\to 0
because
\mathbb{E}[\tau(v)]<\infty
.
∎

Lemma 6.4.

\mathbb{E}[W_{s}-c\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}W_{s}>c]\leqslant\frac{m_{+}}{4\alpha}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{E}[(W_{s}-c)^{2}\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}W_{s}>c]\leqslant\frac{m_{+}^{2}}{8\alpha^{2}}

Proof sketch.

Each above period starts with
W_{s}-c\in[0,m_{+}]
. Until the end of the above period,
W_{s}-c
evolves like the amount of work in an M/G/1 queue with server speed
1/2
, job size distribution
S_{s}
, and work arrival rate
\rho_{s}<1/2
. This means
(W_{s}-c\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}W_{s}>c)
has the same distribution as an M/G/1 with vacations, where the vacation length distribution is that of
W_{s}-c
at the start of an above period. The desired bounds follow from the work decomposition formula for the M/G/1 with vacations
[
Fuhrmann and Cooper 1985
]
and the observation that both job sizes and vacation lengths are bounded by
m_{+}
. We defer the details to
Section

B.3
.
∎

Lemma 6.5.

\mathbb{E}[A]\leqslant\frac{m_{+}}{\alpha}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{E}[A_{e}]\leqslant\frac{m_{+}}{4\alpha^{2}}.

Proof.

As in the proof sketch of
Lemma

6.4
, we view the short server during an above period as an M/G/1 with server speed
1/2
and work arrival rate
\rho_{s}
, so the mean drift of
W_{s}
is
-(1/2-\rho_{s})=-\alpha
. By standard results for M/G/1 busy periods
[
Harchol-Balter 2013
]
, starting from
W_{s}-c=v
, it takes
v/\alpha
time in expectation for the above period to end.

•

The
\mathbb{E}[A]
bound follows from the fact that at the start of an above period,
W_{s}-c\leqslant m_{+}
, implying
\mathbb{E}[A]\leqslant m_{+}/\alpha
.

•

The
\mathbb{E}[A_{e}]
bound follows from the fact that the residual time of an above period is distributed as
A_{e}
. But the residual time of an above period is the same as the amount of time until an above period ends starting from the stationary distribution of
W_{s}-c
conditional on being in an above period. This means
\mathbb{E}[A_{e}]=\mathbb{E}[W_{s}-c\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}W_{s}>c]/\alpha
, so the result follows from
Lemma

6.4
.
∎

6.3.
Analyzing the Long Server

In this section, we bound differences between
\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}[W_{\ell}(0)]
and
\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}]
,
\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}>c)]
, and
\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}=c)]
, separately. These bounds will help us upper bound
\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]
, thereby obtaining a bound on
\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}]
.

Let
q_{A}
and
q_{B}
be the probabilities of being in an above or below period, respectively. That is,

q_{A}=\mathbb{P}[W_{s}>c]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[A]}{\mathbb{E}[A+B]}\qquad\text{and}\qquad q_{B}=\mathbb{P}[W_{s}\leqslant c]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[B]}{\mathbb{E}[A+B]},

where the expressions in terms of expectations of
A
and
B
follow from renewal-reward theorem.

Lemma 6.6.

\big\lvert\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}]-\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}[W_{\ell}(0)]\big\rvert\leqslant\left(\sqrt{q_{A}\mathbb{E}[A_{e}]}+\sqrt{q_{B}\mathbb{E}[B_{e}]}\right)^{2}\leqslant\frac{q_{A}m_{+}}{2\alpha^{2}}+\frac{4q_{B}c}{\beta}.

Proof.

The long server workload process can be described as

W_{\ell}(t)=\left(W_{\ell}(0)-\Delta_{\ell}(0,t)+\Sigma_{\ell}^{m}(0,t)+\Sigma_{\ell}^{\ell}(0,t)\right)^{+},

where

•

\Delta_{l}(0,t)
is the total work processed by the long server in
(0,t]
,

•

\Sigma_{\ell}^{m}(0,t)
is the total work added to the long server from medium job arrivals in
(0,t]
, and

•

\Sigma_{\ell}^{\ell}(0,t)
is the total work added to the long server due to large job arrivals in
(0,t]
.

Applying the Palm inversion formula
[
Baccelli and Brémaud 2002
]
to
W_{\ell}
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[A+B]}\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}\left[\int_{0}^{A+B}\left(W_{\ell}(0)-\Delta_{\ell}(0,t)+\Sigma_{\ell}^{m}(0,t)+\Sigma_{\ell}^{\ell}(0,t)\right)^{+}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\textrm{(a)}}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}[W_{\ell}(0)]+\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[A+B]}\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}\left[\int_{0}^{A+B}\max\left\{-\Delta_{\ell}(0,t)+\Sigma_{\ell}^{m}(0,t)+\Sigma_{\ell}^{\ell}(0,t),-W_{\ell}(0)\right\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}\right],

where (a) holds since
W_{\ell}(0)
, the amount of long server work at time
0
, is independent of
A+B
, the length of the below-above cycle starting at time
0
.

We now bound
\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}]-\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}[W_{\ell}(0)]
separately from above and below. To obtain a lower bound, we bound the integrand below by
-\Delta(t)
, obtaining

\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}]-\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}[W_{\ell}(0)]\geqslant-\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[A+B]}\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}\left[\int_{0}^{A+B}\Delta_{\ell}(0,t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\textrm{(b)}}}}{{\geqslant}}-\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[A+B]}\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}\left[\int_{0}^{A+B}\frac{t}{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}\right]=-\frac{\mathbb{E}[(A+B)^{2}]}{4\mathbb{E}[A+B]},

where (b) holds because the server completes work at rate
\tfrac{1}{2}
while it is busy. To obtain an upper bound, we bound the integrand above by
\Sigma^{m}_{\ell}(0,t)+\Sigma^{\ell}_{\ell}(0,t)
. We first bound its conditional expectation given
A
and
B
. Notice that
\Sigma^{m}_{\ell}(0,t)+\Sigma^{\ell}_{\ell}(0,t)
consists of arrivals of large jobs during
(0,t]
and medium jobs during
(B,t]
. Neither of these types of arrivals impacts the lengths of the above and below periods, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}[\Sigma^{m}_{\ell}(0,t)+\Sigma^{\ell}_{\ell}(0,t)\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}A,B]

\displaystyle=\rho_{m}(t-B)^{+}+\rho_{\ell}t\leqslant t.

From
Section

6.3
and a computation similar to the lower bound, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}]-\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}[W_{\ell}(0)]\leqslant\frac{\mathbb{E}[(A+B)^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[A+B]}.

Combining this with the lower bound, the result follows from
Section

6.2
,
Section

6.3
, and Cauchy-Schwarz:

\frac{\mathbb{E}[(A+B)^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[A+B]}\leqslant\frac{\mathbb{E}[A^{2}]+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[A^{2}]\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}+\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[A+B]}=q_{A}\mathbb{E}[A_{e}]+2\sqrt{q_{A}\mathbb{E}[A_{e}]\,q_{B}\mathbb{E}[B_{e}]}+q_{B}\mathbb{E}[B_{e}].

To complete the proof, we use the AM-GM inequality (arithmetic mean
\geqslant
geometric mean) on
\sqrt{q_{A}\mathbb{E}[A_{e}]\,q_{B}\mathbb{E}[B_{e}]}
, then apply our bounds on
\mathbb{E}[A_{e}]
and
\mathbb{E}[B_{e}]
from
Lemmas

6.3
and
6.5
.
∎

Lemma 6.7.

\big\lvert\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}>c)]-q_{A}\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}[W_{\ell}(0)]\big\rvert\leqslant q_{A}\mathbb{E}[A_{e}]+2\sqrt{q_{A}\mathbb{E}[A_{e}]\,q_{B}\mathbb{E}[B_{e}]}\leqslant\frac{q_{A}m_{+}}{4\alpha^{2}}+\frac{\sqrt{2q_{A}q_{B}m_{+}c}}{\alpha\sqrt{\beta}}.

Proof.

Similar to that of
Lemma

6.6
. See
Section

B.3
.

Lemma 6.8.

\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}=0)]\leqslant\delta\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}[W_{\ell}(0)]+\sqrt{\delta\mathbb{E}[B_{e}^{2}]}\leqslant\delta\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}[W_{\ell}(0)]+\frac{2c\sqrt{2\delta}}{\beta}.

Proof.

Applying Palm inversion formula
[
Baccelli and Brémaud 2002
]
to
W_{\ell}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}=0)
yields

\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}=0)]=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[A+B]}\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}\left[\int_{0}^{B}W_{\ell}(t)\,\mathcal{I}(W_{s}(t)=0)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}\right],

where we can end the integral at
B
because we only have
W_{s}(t)=0
during below periods, which corresponds to
t\in[0,B)
. We further expand the right-hand side using
Section

6.3
. No medium jobs are dispatched to the short server during below periods, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}=0)]\leqslant\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[A+B]}\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}\left[\int_{0}^{B}(W_{\ell}(0)+\Sigma_{\ell}^{\ell}(0,t))\,\mathcal{I}(W_{s}(t)=0)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\textrm{(a)}}}}{{=}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}[W_{\ell}(0)]}{\mathbb{E}[A+B]}\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}\left[\int_{0}^{B}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}(t)=0)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}\right]+\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[A+B]}\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}\left[\int_{0}^{B}\Sigma_{\ell}^{\ell}(0,t)\,\mathcal{I}(W_{s}(t)=0)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}\right].

where (a) follows from the independence of
W_{\ell}(0)
and
\int_{0}^{B}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}(t)=0)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}
. To analyze the first term, we observe that by the Palm inversion formula
[
Baccelli and Brémaud 2002
]
and
Theorem

3.2
,

\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[A+B]}\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}\left[\int_{0}^{B}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}(t)=0)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{I}(W_{s}=0)]=\mathbb{P}[W_{s}=0]\leqslant\delta.

To analyze the second term, we apply
Section

6.3
, yielding

\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}\left[\int_{0}^{B}\Sigma_{\ell}^{\ell}(0,t)\,\mathcal{I}(W_{s}(t)=0)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}\right]\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}\left[\int_{0}^{B}t\mathcal{I}(W_{s}(t)=0)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}\right].

The right-hand side is difficult to compute directly due to the dependency of
B
and
W_{s}
. To resolve this, we apply the Palm inversion formula
[
Baccelli and Brémaud 2002
]
to
B_{a}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}=0)
, where
B_{a}(t)
is the age process of the below-above cycle, namely the amount of time since the current cycle began. This yields

\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[A+B]}\mathbb{E}_{c}^{0}\left[\int_{0}^{B}t\mathcal{I}(W_{s}(t)=0)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}[B_{a}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}=0)]

Thus, to bound
\mathcal{T}_{3}
, it suffices to bound
\mathbb{E}[B_{a}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}=0)]
. By Cauchy-Schwarz,

\mathbb{E}[B_{a}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}=0)]\leqslant\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[B_{a}^{2}]\mathbb{P}[W_{s}=0]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\textrm{(b)}}}}{{=}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[B_{e}^{2}]\mathbb{P}[W_{s}=0]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\textrm{(c)}}}}{{\leqslant}}\sqrt{\delta\mathbb{E}[B_{e}^{2}]},

where (b) follows because
B_{a}
has distribution
B_{e}
, and (c) follows from
Theorem

3.2
. The result then follows from bounding
\mathbb{E}[B_{e}^{2}]
using
Sections

6.2
and
6.3
.
∎

Lemma 6.9.

\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]\leqslant\bigg(1+\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathrm{M/G/1}}]+2c+\frac{m_{+}\sqrt{q_{A}}}{2\alpha\sqrt{\varepsilon}}+\frac{4c\sqrt{\delta}}{\alpha^{2}\beta\varepsilon}.

Proof.

We use
Theorem

6.1
to bound
\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]
, which amounts to analyzing
\mathbb{E}[IW_{\mathsf{all}}]
. We have

IW_{\mathsf{all}}=(W_{s}+W_{\ell})\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}=0)+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{I}(W_{\ell}=0)\right)=\tfrac{1}{2}W_{\ell}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}=0)+\tfrac{1}{2}W_{s}\mathcal{I}(W_{\ell}=0).

Combining
Lemmas

6.6
and
6.8
and noting
\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]
yields a bound on
\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}=0)]
:

\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}=0)]\leqslant\delta\left(\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]+\frac{q_{A}m_{+}}{4\alpha^{2}}+\frac{4q_{B}c}{\beta}\right)+\frac{2c\sqrt{2\delta}}{\beta}.

To bound
\mathbb{E}[W_{s}\mathcal{I}(W_{\ell}=0)]
, we compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{s}\mathcal{I}(W_{\ell}=0)]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\textrm{(a)}}}}{{\leqslant}}\mathbb{E}[(c+(W_{s}-c)^{+})\,\mathcal{I}(W_{\ell}=0)]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\textrm{(b)}}}}{{\leqslant}}c\mathbb{P}[W_{\ell}=0]+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[((W_{s}-c)^{+})^{2}\right]\,\mathbb{P}[W_{\ell}=0]}

\displaystyle=c\mathbb{P}[W_{\ell}=0]+\sqrt{q_{A}\mathbb{E}\left[(W_{s}-c)^{2}\nonscript\;\middle|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}W_{s}>c\right]\,\mathbb{P}[W_{\ell}=0]}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\textrm{(c)}}}}{{\leqslant}}2\varepsilon c+\frac{m_{+}\sqrt{q_{A}\varepsilon}}{2\alpha},

where (a) follows from
W_{s}\leqslant c+(W_{s}-c)^{+}
, (b) follows from Cauchy-Schwarz, and (c) follows from
Lemma

6.4
and the fact that
\varepsilon=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}[W_{s}=0]+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}[W_{\ell}=0]\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}[W_{\ell}=0]
. Combining the bounds on
\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}=0)]
and
\mathbb{E}[W_{s}\mathcal{I}(W_{\ell}=0)]
with
Theorem

6.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathrm{M/G/1}}]+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}=0)]+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[W_{s}\mathcal{I}(W_{\ell}=0)]\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathrm{M/G/1}}]+c+\frac{m_{+}\sqrt{q_{A}}}{4\alpha\sqrt{\varepsilon}}+\frac{\delta}{2\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathsf{all}}]+\frac{q_{A}m_{+}\delta}{8\alpha^{2}\varepsilon}+\frac{2q_{B}c\delta}{\beta\varepsilon}+\frac{c\sqrt{2\delta}}{\beta\varepsilon}.

The result follows after rearranging and simplifying. We use the fact that we have defined the parameters such that
c\geqslant m_{+}
and
\delta\leqslant\varepsilon
(
Sections

2.2
and
2.3
), which means
1/\big(1-\frac{\delta}{2\varepsilon}\big)\leqslant 1+\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon}\leqslant 2
. And, using the fact that
\alpha,\beta\leqslant\tfrac{1}{2}
, we loosely bound the terms with a
\sqrt{\delta}
factor by

\frac{q_{A}m_{+}\delta}{8\alpha^{2}\varepsilon}+\frac{2q_{B}c\delta}{\beta\varepsilon}+\frac{c\sqrt{2\delta}}{\beta\varepsilon}\leqslant(q_{A}+q_{B})\frac{c\delta}{2\alpha^{2}\beta\varepsilon}+\frac{c\sqrt{2\delta}}{\beta\varepsilon}\leqslant\frac{2c\sqrt{\delta}}{\alpha^{2}\beta\varepsilon}.\qed

6.4.
Bounding Mean Response Time

We now prove
Theorem

3.3
, our main upper bound result. It follows as a corollary of a more explicit bound, which we state in

6.11
. To simplify the computations, we assume that
\beta\geqslant 2\delta
, but we could remove this assumption at the cost of slightly complicating the expressions.

Lemma 6.10.

If
\beta\geqslant 2\delta
, then
q_{A}\leqslant\frac{2\beta}{\alpha+\beta}
and
q_{B}\leqslant\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}
.

Proof.

The short server is stable, so the load of jobs arriving to it equals the average rate it completes work. This means
\rho_{s}+\rho_{m}\mathbb{P}[W_{s}\leqslant c]=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}[W_{s}>0]
.
Theorem

3.2
implies
\mathbb{P}[W_{s}>0]\in[1-\delta,1]
, so the bound follows from the definitions of
\alpha
and
\beta
and the
\beta\geqslant 2\delta
assumption.
∎

Theorem 6.11.

In a system with

servers, if

and

, then by setting

according to

\displaystyle\quad+44m_{+}\max\Bigg\{\frac{\beta}{\alpha^{2}(\alpha+\beta)},\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{\alpha^{2}\varepsilon(\alpha+\beta)}},\frac{\log\frac{3}{2\beta\delta}}{\beta(\alpha+\beta)},\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{3}{2\beta\delta}}{\beta}},\frac{\sqrt{\delta}\log\frac{3}{2\beta\delta}}{\alpha^{2}\beta^{2}\varepsilon}\Bigg\}.

Proof sketch.

It suffices to bound the work quantities on the right-hand side of
Section

6
.

•

Lemma

implies
\mathbb{E}[W_{s}]\leqslant c+q_{A}\mathbb{E}[W_{s}-c\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}W_{s}>c]\leqslant c+\frac{q_{A}m_{+}}{\alpha}
.

•

Lemmas

6.6
and

imply, after some simplification,

\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}\mathcal{I}(W_{s}>c)]\leqslant q_{A}\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}]+\frac{q_{A}m_{+}}{\alpha^{2}}+\frac{4q_{A}q_{B}c}{\beta}+\frac{\sqrt{2q_{A}q_{B}m_{+}c}}{\alpha\sqrt{\beta}}.

to express the right-hand side in terms of

,

, and

, then simplify. We defer the details to

.

Proof of
Theorem

3.3
for
n=2
servers.

The bound follows directly from plugging the parameter choices into
Theorem

6.11
, and comparing with the lower bound in

8.
Conclusion

In this paper, we prove the first mean response time lower bound for FCFS servers. We design a new dispatching policy, called
CARD (Controlled Asymmetry Reduces Delay)
, and show that it is heavy-traffic optimal, thus making CARD the first proven heavy-traffic optimal size- and state-aware dispatching policy. CARD can thus serve as a new benchmark policy for future work in dispatching or load-balancing for FCFS servers. Methodologically, our method of analyzing CARD using below-above cycles could be of independent interest, as it can be adapted to study other threshold-based policies.

Underlying our results is the insight that in the
size-aware
dispatching setting, it is helpful to have a significant imbalance between the amounts of work at each server. This insight has been made multiple times throughout the size-aware dispatching literature (e.g.
Hyytiä et al. 2012
;
Harchol-Balter et al. 1999
). It is in contrast to the natural idea of always balancing the queues, which is helpful in size-oblivious dispatching
[
Zhou et al. 2018c
]
.

In addition to minimizing mean response time, researchers today are also interested in tail performance. We conjecture that for job sizes with exponential tails and mean
1/\mu
, work under CARD asymptotically decays exponentially with rate
\frac{\mu-\lambda}{n}
in an
n
-server system, which is worse (i.e. smaller) than the
\mu-\lambda
decay rate of an M/M/1 under FCFS. An interesting follow-up question is how to balance the tradeoff between mean response time and response time decay rate, perhaps starting with the heavy-traffic regime. We leave this to future work.

Appendix A
Dice

In this section, we give a brief introduction of Dice based on
Hyytiä and Righter 2022
and answer some questions readers may be curious about.

What is Dice?

Let
\mathbf{W}=(W_{1},\ldots,W_{n})
be the workload vector. Dice has
n-1
threshold parameters
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{n-1}
to set. When a job of size
s
arrives, Dice dispatches the job as follows:

•

Sort the workload vector in increasing order. We assume that
\tau_{i}
is the threshold on
W_{i}
.

•

The dispatcher goes through
\mathbf{W}
in increasing order and dispatches the job to the first server
i
that satisfies
\tau_{i}-W_{i}>s
. If none of the first
n-1
servers satisfy this condition, the job is dispatched to the last server.

How complicated is Dice compared with CARD?

Dice is easier to implement than CARD. To implement Dice, one only needs to find decent threshold parameters
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{n-1}
. Successful implementation of CARD, however, requires tuning thresholds on job sizes (i.e.
m_{-}
and
m_{+}
for two-server CARD and
m_{i}
’s for multi-band CARD) in addition to the threshold parameters.

Why does Dice perform so well in simulations?

Dice has the same spirit as CARD: it keeps one long queue populated mostly with large jobs and maintains short queues for small jobs to get through quickly, thereby increasing
\mathbb{E}[S_{\text{queue}}]
.

Is Dice heavy-traffic optimal?

This is an open problem. We conjecture that Dice may
not
be heavy-traffic optimal. Consider a two-server system. Under Dice, only jobs that fit into the gap between current work at the shorter server and the threshold get dispatched to the shorter server. However, this will not guarantee that as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, all jobs with size less than
m
gets dispatched to the shorter server, as there is always some probability that the gap is too small for a job with size less than
m
.

Why is it hard to analyze Dice?

Even with the tools we developed in this paper, Dice is more difficult to analyze than CARD. The main reason is that the dispatching policy under Dice changes continuously with the state of the shorter queue, which makes it challenging to directly apply our below-above cycle analysis. We expect that a refinement of our approach could work for Dice, but substantial extra work is needed.

, as well as the expression of
c
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{{\mathrm{CARD}}}]-n\mathbb{E}[S]

\displaystyle\leqslant n\left(p_{\ell}+\frac{2\beta}{\beta+\alpha}\right)\left(1+(n-1)\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon}\right)\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathrm{M/G/1}}]+\frac{4n\beta m_{+}}{\alpha^{2}(\alpha+\beta)}+n(n-1)\frac{m_{+}\sqrt{n\beta}}{2\alpha\sqrt{\varepsilon(\alpha+\beta)}}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\frac{n(n-1)(n^{2}(n-1)+n)m_{+}}{\beta}+\frac{8n^{2}(n-1)m_{+}\alpha}{\beta(\alpha+\beta)^{2}}\right)\log\frac{n+1}{n\beta\delta}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2n\sqrt{n(n-1)}m_{+}}{\alpha+\beta}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\log\frac{n+1}{n\beta\delta}}+\frac{8n(n-1)m_{+}\sqrt{\delta}}{\alpha^{2}\beta^{2}\varepsilon}\log\frac{n+1}{n\beta\delta}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{n^{2}(n-1)\left(\frac{n(n-1)m_{+}}{\beta}\log\frac{n+1}{n\beta\delta}+\frac{2m_{+}\beta}{\alpha(\alpha+\beta)}\right)\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon}}_{\mathcal{T}}

At this point, we note that the upper bound for
\mathbb{E}[T_{{\mathrm{CARD}}}]-n\mathbb{E}[S]
is, after letting
n=2
, the same as that in
Theorem

6.11
, except for
\mathcal{T}
. Thus, setting

\alpha=\Theta(1),\qquad\beta=\Theta\bigg(\varepsilon^{1/3}\bigg(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\bigg)^{2/3}\bigg),\qquad\text{and}\qquad c=\frac{n(n-1)m_{+}}{\beta}\log\frac{n+1}{n\beta\delta},

and noting that
\mathcal{T}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, we conclude that the bound yields the same heavy-traffic scaling as that in
Theorem

6.11
. Finally, we note that
K_{\mathrm{CARD}}
emerges because

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}np_{\ell}=n\mathbb{P}[S>m]=K_{\mathrm{CARD}}.\qed
