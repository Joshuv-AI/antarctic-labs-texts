Finite Sample Complexity of Sequential Monte Carlo Estimators on Multimodal Target Distributions
Joseph Mathews, Scott C. Schmidler

Abstract
We prove finite sample complexities for sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) algorithms which require only local mixing times of the associated Markov kernels. Our bounds are particularly useful when the target distribution is multimodal and global mixing of the Markov kernel is slow; in such cases our approach establishes benefits of SMC over the corresponding Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) estimator. The lack of global mixing is addressed by sequentially controlling the bias introduced by SMC resampling procedures. We apply these results to obtain complexity bounds for approximating expectations under mixtures of log-concave distributions, and show that SMC provides a fully polynomial time randomized approximation scheme for some difficult multimodal problems where the corresponding Markov chain sampler is exponentially slow. Finally, we compare the bounds obtained by our approach to existing bounds for tempered Markov chains on the same problems.

Introduction

Approximating integrals with respect to a complicated, high-dimensional probability distribution
\pi
is an important problem spanning multiple disciplines, such as Bayesian statistical inference, machine learning, statistical physics, and theoretical computer science
[
13
,
17
]
. Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) methods are a large class of stochastic approximation algorithms designed to solve these problems by combining Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods and resampling strategies to sequentially sample from a series of probability distributions. Some examples of SMC algorithms include population Monte Carlo methods
[
2
]
, annealed importance sampling
[
27
]
, sequential particle filters
[
3
]
, and population annealing
[
40
]
, among many others
[
15
]
. Closely related - but purely MCMC - methods include parallel tempering (PT)
[
14
]
and simulated tempering (ST)
[
23
]
, which have been referred to as population-based MCMC methods
[
15
]
.

An SMC sampler is generally constructed as follows. The target distribution
\pi
is embedded in a sequence of distributions
\mu_{0},\ldots,\mu_{V-1},\mu_{V}=\pi
chosen such that
\mu_{0}
is easy to sample from and
\mu_{v-1}
is ‘close’ to
\mu_{v}
for
v=1,\ldots,V
. Random variables (called
particles
) are initially sampled from
\mu_{0}
and are propagated through the sequence of distributions sequentially via a combination of time-inhomogeneous MCMC and resampling moves until step
V
, where the resulting particles can be used to approximate expectations under
\pi
. For a comparison of various resampling strategies, see
[
6
]
. For a discussion of different Markov kernels used for the MCMC (
mutation
) step, see
[
5
]
. For adaptive strategies, see
[
1
]
and
[
12
]
. An explicit statement of the algorithm considered in this paper is given in Section
2.1
.

Two properties of SMC samplers make them particularly attractive to practitioners. First, they can be parallelized easily, improving their scalability to high-dimensional problems
[
8
]
. Second, SMC samplers are believed to perform well when
\pi
has multiple, well-separated modes. In these situations, standard MCMC methods such as random walk Metropolis
[
26
]
, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
[
28
]
and Gibbs samplers, often exhibit metastable behavior, leading to slow mixing and poor approximation performance (see e.g.
[
22
]
). SMC samplers are frequently applied to such multimodal targets across different disciplines, see e.g.
[
8
,
32
,
37
]
for applications to Bayesian statistics and
[
30
,
39
,
40
]
for applications to spin models in statistical physics. The performance of SMC on multimodal problems
has also been tested via simulation studies on various model problems in
[
15
,
31
,
35
]
. See also
[
38
]
for simulation studies comparing the performance of SMC methods to PT and ST algorithms on the Potts model.

However, theoretical results in support of these purported benefits of SMC over MCMC are limited. For one thing, such comparisons require quantitative analysis of the number of particles or steps required by each algorithm on a given problem.
But while the asymptotic properties of SMC samplers have been explored extensively in the literature (for example,
[
4
]
establishes a central limit theorem under various resampling strategies,
[
29
]
proves a central limit theorem for SMC methods using adaptive schemes,
[
7
]
proves a law of large numbers, and
[
30
]
bound the asymptotic variance of SMC for multimodal problems), rigorous results on finite-sample performance are much more limited. Results for finite particle sets have been obtained by use of Feynman-Kac formulations
[
41
,
33
,
34
,
10
,
11
]
; however, these bounds typically involve expectations of the corresponding Feynman-Kac propagators, which are generally not available explicitly, and assume mixing conditions
on the associated MCMC kernels which are difficult to verify on general state spaces. These factors make it difficult to determine the dependence of SMC algorithms on key quantities of interest such as the problem dimension. More recently,
[
24
]
established finite sample bounds
using a coupling construction. However, their results depend on the global mixing rates of the mutation kernels, and problems where these are controllable (e.g when polynomial rather than exponential in problem size) are likely to be precisely those cases where MCMC performs well without SMC.

In this paper we establish rigorous conditions guaranteeing the performance of SMC samplers on multimodal target distributions by proving upper bounds on the computational complexity. These results are obtained by establishing upper bounds on the number of particles and MCMC steps needed to closely approximate expectations under the target distribution with high probability. Our approach builds on that of
[
24
]
, but here we emphasize settings where global mixing may be slow, as in multimodal situations, but where the kernel can be assumed to mix
locally
. In particular, our approach assumes particles are only mutated through restricted Markov kernels, corresponding to an arbitrary partition of the state space. This ‘local mixing’ approach is similar to
[
34
,
30
]
, but here we use this approach to obtain finite sample bounds rather than asymptotic ones.
We show that when SMC accurately approximates ‘local’ expectations (i.e. expectations restricted to each partition element), global expectations can also be approximated accurately. This approach also calls to mind
the previous mixing time analyses of the PT and ST algorithms in
[
42
]
(see also
[
16
]
), who used the state space decomposition approach of
[
20
]
.
We apply our bounds to mixtures of log-concave distributions and the mean field Ising model. In addition, we use our bounds to provide a rigorous comparison of the SMC, PT, and ST algorithms.

While our approach builds on the coupling argument of
[
24
]
, the inability to rely on global mixing substantially complicates the analysis. Unlike in
[
24
]
, particles can no longer be coupled to a set of exact
i.i.d.
random variates, as the bias introduced by resampling is no longer ‘corrected’ automatically by a globally mixing kernel, and instead must be controlled directly. Consequently our analysis highlights the benefits obtained from the resampling step, which indeed is crucial to the success of the algorithm when the mutation kernel cannot be assumed to mix globally at each step.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
establishes notation, formalizes the SMC algorithm we are considering, and outlines our proof approach. Section
4
contains our main theorem and supporting results. Section
5
, applies these results to obtain bounds for mixtures of log-concave distributions and the mean field Ising model. Section
6
compares our bounds to those obtained by
[
42
,
16
]

for the parallel and simulated tempering algorithms. Section
7
, concludes with a discussion of future directions.

2
Problem Setup

Let
\pi
be a target distribution defined on a state space
\mathcal{X}
with corresponding
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{B}
and dominating measure
\rho
. In a slight abuse of notation we use the same letter to denote both the distribution and its corresponding density (e.g. the density of
\pi
is
\pi(x)
). Let
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{j}:j=1,\ldots,p\}
be a partition of
\mathcal{X}
. Typically
\mathcal{A}
will represent a partition of
\mathcal{X}
into local modes of
\pi
, although our results apply for any partition. While
\mathcal{A}
is usually not specified in practice, the algorithm given in Section
2.1
serves as a useful theoretical model for understanding the behavior of SMC algorithms when
\pi
is multimodal.

2.1
Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC)

Let
\mu_{0},\ldots,\mu_{V}
be a sequence of distributions defined on
\mathcal{X}
with dominating measure
\rho
and
\mu_{V}=\pi
. We write
\mu_{v}(x)=q_{v}(x)/z_{v}
with
q_{v}(x)
denoting the unnormalized density and
z_{v}
the (often unknown) normalizing constant. Define the
importance weight

w_{v}(x):=q_{v}(x)/q_{v-1}(x)
. We assume that the density ratios are uniformly bounded.

Assumption A1
.

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{v}(x)}{\mu_{v-1}(x)}=\frac{z_{v-1}}{z_{v}}\cdot\frac{q_{v}(x)}{q_{v-1}(x)}=\frac{z_{v-1}}{z_{v}}\cdot w_{v}(x)\leq ZW,

where
Z=\max_{v\in\{0,\ldots,V\}}\frac{z_{v-1}}{z_{v}}
and
W=\max_{v\in\{0,\ldots,V\}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}w_{v}(x)
.

Denote the minimum probability of all partition elements across all steps as
\mu^{\star}:=\min_{v=0,\ldots,V}\min_{j=1,\ldots,p}\mu_{v}(A_{j})
. The algorithm we analyze is defined by the following procedure:

1.

For
v=0
, draw
X^{1:N}_{0}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mu_{0}

2.

For
v=1,\ldots,V
,

a)

Set
\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}=X^{k}_{v-1}
with probability
w_{v}(X^{k}_{v-1})/\sum^{N}_{m=1}w_{v}(X^{m}_{v-1})
independently for
i=1,\ldots,N
.

b)

Draw
X^{i}_{v}\sim K_{v|A_{\xi^{i}_{v}}}^{t}(\tilde{X}^{i}_{v},\cdot)
for
i=1,\ldots,N
and
t\geq 1
,

where
\xi^{i}_{v}=\sum^{p}_{j=1}j\mathbbm{1}_{A_{j}}(\tilde{X}_{v}^{i})
is the (random) partition element index containing
\tilde{X}_{v}^{i}
, and

\displaystyle K_{v|A}(x,B)=K_{v}(x,B)+K_{v}(x,A^{c})\mathbbm{1}_{x\in B}\text{ for }x\in A,B\subset A,

(1)

where
K_{v}
is a
\mu_{v}
-invariant Markov kernel and
K_{v\mid A}
is the restriction of
K
to
A\subset\mathcal{X}
. Note that
K_{v\mid A}
is
\mu_{v\mid A}
-invariant by construction and is assumed to be ergodic for any
A\subset\mathcal{X}
, where

\displaystyle\mu_{v|A}(B)=\frac{\mu_{v}(A\cap B)}{\mu_{v}(A)},\text{ for }B\subset\mathcal{X}

The use of
K_{v\mid A_{j}}
instead of
K_{v}
implies that the mutation kernel cannot cross between modes/partition elements. Consequently, we have that
X^{i}_{v}\in A_{j}
if and only if
\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in A_{j}
.
Each iteration of the algorithm above generates a set of
resampled
particles
\tilde{X}^{1:N}_{v}:=(\tilde{X}^{1}_{v},\ldots,\tilde{X}^{N}_{v})
and
mutated
particles
X^{1:N}_{v}:=(X^{1}_{v},\ldots,X^{N}_{v})
. The full algorithm then produces a total of
N(2V+1)
particles defined on
\mathcal{X}^{2N(V+1)}
that evolve according to the following joint probability laws,

\displaystyle\Prob_{0}(X^{1:N}_{0}\in B_{1}\times\ldots\times B_{N})

\displaystyle=\prod^{N}_{i=1}\mu_{0}(B_{i})

(2)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Prob}_{v}(\tilde{X}^{1:N}_{v}\in B_{1}\times\ldots\times B_{N}\mid X^{1:N}_{v-1})

\displaystyle=\prod^{N}_{i=1}\sum^{N}_{k=1}\frac{w_{v}(X^{k}_{v-1})\mathbbm{1}_{B_{i}}(X^{k}_{v-1})}{N\sum^{p}_{r=1}\hat{w}^{r}_{v}}

(3)

\displaystyle\Prob_{v}(X^{1:N}_{v}\in B_{1}\times\ldots\times B_{N}\mid\tilde{X}^{1:N}_{v})

\displaystyle=\prod^{N}_{i=1}K_{v|A_{\xi^{i}_{v}}}^{t}(\tilde{X}^{i}_{v},B_{i}),

(4)

where
\hat{w}^{j}_{v}=N^{-1}\sum^{N}_{i=1}w_{v}(X^{i}_{v-1})\mathbbm{1}_{A_{j}}(X_{v-1}^{j})
and
B_{i}\subset\mathcal{X}
for
i=1,\ldots,N
.

2.2
Mixing times

A distribution
\nu
is said to be
M
-warm
with respect to a distribution
\mu
if
\sup_{B\subset\mathcal{X}}\nu(B)/\mu(B)\leq M
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{M}(\mu)
denote the set of all such distributions. Define the
M
-warm mixing time for
j=1,\ldots,p
by:

\displaystyle\tau_{v,j}(\epsilon,M):=\min\left\{t:\sup_{\eta\in\mathcal{P}_{M}(\mu_{v|A_{j}})}\left\lVert\int_{A_{j}}\eta_{|A_{j}}(dx)K_{v|A_{j}}^{t}(x,\cdot)-\mu_{v|A_{j}}(\cdot)\right\rVert_{\text{TV}}\leq\epsilon\right\}

(5)

Assuming that the initial distribution is
M
warm is common among MCMC mixing time results (see e.g.
[
9
,
18
,
44
]
); however, choosing such an initial distribution can be difficult in practice.

2.2.1
Locally Warm Distributions

In later sections we will require a stronger notion of warmness than the standard definition above. We call this stronger version
local warmness
:

Definition 1
.

A distribution
\nu
is
locally
M
-warm
with respect to a distribution
\mu
and partition
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{j}:j=1,\ldots,p\}
if,

\displaystyle\sup_{B\subset A_{j}}\frac{\nu_{|A_{j}}(B)}{\mu_{|A_{j}}(B)}\leq M

Note that this is a stronger notion of warmness since it requires that
\tilde{\mu}_{v}
assign sufficiently large probability to each partition element. Local warmness is useful when considering potentially multimodal target distributions, because warmness of an initial distribution by the standard definition is insufficient to guarantee rapid mixing on multimodal distributions. For example, consider the Gaussian mixture
\mu(x)=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{N}(x;-\mathbf{1}_{d},I_{d})+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{N}(x;\mathbf{1}_{d},\sigma^{2}I_{d})
studied by
[
43
]
. The initial distribution
\nu(x)=\mathcal{N}(x;-\mathbf{1}_{d},I_{d})
provides a
2
-warm start but, as the authors show, a random-walk Metropolis-Hastings algorithm still produces a torpidly mixing chain.
A key step in our analysis will be to show that the resampling step (2a) of the SMC algorithm produces a distribution
\tilde{\mu}
that is locally warm for arbitrary partitions.

2.3
Coupling Constructions

The proof of Lemma
5
involves the construction of coupled random variables. We briefly introduce some notation for this here but defer the detailed construction to Appendix
A
. Our analysis constructs coupled pairs of random variables
(X^{i}_{v},\bar{X}^{i}_{v})

\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}
and satisfy for

(a)

(b)

\Prob(\bar{X}^{i}_{v}\in B\mid\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in A_{j})=\mu_{v|A_{j}}(B)

(c)

\Prob(X^{i}_{v}\neq\bar{X}^{i}_{v}\mid\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in A_{j})=||\hat{\mu}_{v|A_{j}}(\cdot)-\mu_{v|A_{j}}(\cdot)||_{\text{TV}}

While the
X_{v}^{i}
’s represent the particles produced by the SMC algorithm as described above, the coupled random variables
\bar{X}^{1:N}_{v}:=(\bar{X}^{1}_{v},\ldots,\bar{X}^{N}_{v})
are simply a theoretical tool for our analysis and are not constructed in practice. Note that the construction preserves
\mathcal{L}(X^{i}_{v})
and so the behavior of the algorithm given in Section
2.1
is unchanged (see Section
A
).

2.4
Probability space and Marginal Distributions

Going forward, it should be understood that expectations are taken with respect to the joint probability measure defined by (
2
)-(
4
) and the coupling constructions up to time
V
. We simply write a generic
\Prob
(and similarly for
\E
) to denote this joint probability measure. The corresponding state space is
\mathcal{X}^{N(3V+1)}
with product
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{B}^{N(3V+1)}
. Our analysis will focus on the marginal distribution of individual resampled, mutated, or coupled particles. We denote these marginal distributions as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}_{v}(B)

\displaystyle:=\Prob(\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in B)

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{v}(B)

\displaystyle:=\Prob(X^{i}_{v}\in B)

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}_{v}(B)

\displaystyle:=\Prob(\bar{X}^{i}_{v}\in B),

which are identical for
i=1,\ldots,N
by symmetry. Similarly, conditional distributions are denoted by
\tilde{\mu}(B\mid H)=\Prob(\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in B\mid H)
for
B\subset\mathcal{X}
and
H\in\mathcal{B}^{N(3V+1)}
. In addition, we will use the convenient shorthand
\tilde{\mu}_{v|A_{j}}
,
\hat{\mu}_{v|A_{j}}
, and
\bar{\mu}_{v|A_{j}}
to denote the corresponding restricted distributions, e.g.
\tilde{\mu}_{v|A_{j}}(B)=\tilde{\mu}_{v}(B\mid\tilde{X}_{v}^{i}\in A_{j})
, and the mixed notation
\tilde{\mu}_{v|A_{j}}(B\mid H)=\Prob(\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in B\mid\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in A_{j},H)
for restrictions of conditional distributions. We note that
\bar{\mu}_{v}\neq\mu_{v}
in general (see Appendix
A
for details). Finally, we define the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{v}:=\sigma(\{X^{1:N}_{k},\tilde{X}^{1:N}_{k}\}^{v}_{k=1})
with
\mathcal{F}_{0}=\sigma(X^{1:N}_{0})
and denote the probability of resampling into
A_{j}
as

\displaystyle\hat{P}^{j}_{v}:=\tilde{\mu}_{v}(A_{j}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-1})=\frac{\hat{w}^{j}_{v}}{\sum^{p}_{r=1}\hat{w}^{r}_{v}},

where
\hat{w}^{j}_{v}
is defined in Section
2.1
. That is,
\{\hat{P}^{j}_{v}\}
is a stochastic processes adapted to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{v}\}
.

3
Approach

Given some
\Prob
-measurable function
\left|f\right|\leq 1
, our goal is to show that the SMC estimator
\hat{f}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(X_{V}^{i})

approximates
E_{\pi}[f]
to high accuracy with high probability. The challenge is to do so without requiring
X_{V}^{i}\sim\pi
. Observe that

\displaystyle\E_{\pi}[f]=\sum^{p}_{j=1}\E_{\pi_{\mid A_{j}}}[f]\pi(A_{j})\quad\text{ while }\quad\E[\hat{f}]=\sum^{p}_{j=1}\E_{\hat{\mu}_{V\mid A_{j}}}[\hat{f}_{j}]\,\hat{\mu}_{V}(A_{j})

where
\hat{f}_{j}=\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}f(X^{i}_{V})\mathbbm{1}_{A_{j}}(X^{i}_{V})
.
Consequently, we must control two sources of error: the deviation of
\hat{f}

from its expectation
|\hat{f}-\E[\hat{f}]|
, and the error in the expectation itself (bias)
|\E[\hat{f}]-\E_{\pi}[f]|
.
Controlling the latter source of error is the more delicate of the two. To do so, we will define a sequence of events conditional upon which
\hat{\mu}_{v\mid A_{j}}\approx\mu_{v\mid A_{j}}

and
\hat{\mu}_{v}(A_{j})\approx\mu_{v}(A_{j})
, and then show that this sequence of events occurs with sufficiently high probability. This requires choosing
N
sufficiently large to sequentially control the bias introduced by resampling. Previously,
Marion, Mathews and Schmidler 2021
control this by constructing a coupling to
i.i.d.
particles and invoking concentration properties thereof. The main difficulty here is that, in the absence of assumptions of global mixing, the coupling construction provides particles which are neither independent nor have the correct distribution (
\mathcal{L}(\bar{X}^{i}_{v})\neq\mu_{v}
). As a result, establishing concentration properties involving
\bar{X}^{1:N}_{v}
(and consequently
X^{1:N}_{v}
) is non-trivial.

For
j=1,\ldots,p
and
v=1,\ldots,V
, define the following events on the probability space
(\mathcal{X}^{N(3V+1)},\mathcal{B}^{N(3V+1)},\Prob)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{j}_{1}(i)

\displaystyle=\left\{\left|\bar{w}^{j}_{1}-\frac{z_{1}}{z_{0}}\mu_{1}(A_{j})\right|\leq\lambda\cdot\frac{z_{1}}{z_{0}}\mu_{1}(A_{j})\right\}

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{j}_{v}(i)

\displaystyle=\left\{\left|\bar{w}^{j}_{v}-\E[\bar{w}^{j}_{v}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-2}]\right|\leq\lambda\cdot\E[\bar{w}^{j}_{v}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-2}]\right\}\text{ for }v\geq 2

(7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{v}(ii)

\displaystyle=\left\{\bar{X}^{1:N}_{v}=X^{1:N}_{v}\right\},

(8)

where
\bar{w}^{j}_{v}=N^{-1}\sum^{N}_{i=1}w_{v}(\bar{X}^{i}_{v-1})\mathbbm{1}_{A_{j}}(\bar{X}_{v-1}^{i})
, and
\lambda=\frac{\epsilon}{24V}
where
\epsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
is the error tolerance chosen by the user in the application of Theorem
1
. The event
\mathcal{C}_{v}^{j}(i)
represents the successful approximation by
\bar{w}^{j}_{k}
of its conditional mean to within desired relative error bound
\lambda
. Note that
\E[\bar{w}^{j}_{v}|\mathcal{F}_{v-2}]
is a random variable; the event
\mathcal{C}^{j}_{v}(i)

corresponds to
\bar{w}^{j}_{v}
being ‘sandwiched’ between two other random variables.
\mathcal{C}_{v}(ii)
is the simultaneous coupling event for all particles at step
k
.
Our approach is to show that
all
of the events
\mathcal{C}_{v}^{j}(i)
,
\mathcal{C}_{v}^{j}(ii)
,
j=1,\ldots,p
,
k=1,\ldots,V
,
occur simultaneously with high probability, and that conditional on their simultaneous occurrence we have
\hat{\mu}_{v\mid A_{j}}\approx\mu_{v\mid A_{j}}
and
\hat{\mu}_{v}(A_{j})\approx\mu_{v}(A_{j})
for all
v=1,\ldots,V
.

In Section
4.1
, we show that simultaneous occurrence of these events ensures
\hat{\mu}_{v\mid A_{j}}\approx\mu_{v\mid A_{j}}
and
\hat{\mu}_{v}(A_{j})\approx\mu_{v}(A_{j})
, and hence that
\sum^{p}_{r=1}\E[\hat{w}^{r}_{v}]
closely approximates
z_{v+1}/z_{v}
.
In Section
4.2
we show that all of the events
\mathcal{C}_{v}^{j}(i)
,
\mathcal{C}_{v}^{j}(ii)
,
j=1,\ldots,p
,
k=1,\ldots,V
,
occur simultaneously with high probability. We will do so by induction on
k
. In particular,
let
\mathcal{C}^{1:p}_{v}(i)=\cap^{p}_{r=1}\mathcal{C}^{r}_{v}(i)
. For each
V
and for a chosen
\lambda
we will show that the probability of the following event is large:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{v}:=\cap^{v}_{k=1}\{\mathcal{C}^{1:p}_{k}(i)\cap\mathcal{C}_{k}(ii)\}

\mathcal{E}_{v}
is the joint event that
all
(
6
), (
7
), and (
8
) occur for steps
1,\ldots,v
. It will also be convenient to define the events
\mathcal{G}_{v}:=\mathcal{E}_{v-1}\cap\mathcal{C}^{1:p}_{v}(i)
for

and
\mathcal{G}_{1}=\mathcal{C}^{1:p}_{1}(i)
so that
\mathcal{E}_{v}=\mathcal{G}_{v}\cap\mathcal{C}_{v}(ii)
.

4
Results

Our main result for the algorithm in Section

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
|f|\leq 1
and (
A1
) holds. Let
\epsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and choose

1.

N>\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\max\left\{3456\cdot\left(\frac{VWZ}{\mu^{\star}}\right)^{2}\cdot\log(\frac{64Vp}{\mu^{\star}}),\ p^{2}\cdot\log(1024p^{2})\right\}

2.

Then with probability at least

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}f(X^{i}_{V})-\frac{(1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{16})}{(1-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{16})}\E_{\pi}[f]\right|\leq\epsilon.

Note that Theorem

4.1
Results Over Restricted Space

We establish three key lemmas. The first shows that
\hat{P}^{j}_{v}
closely approximates
\mu_{v}(A_{j})
over
\mathcal{G}_{v}\subset\mathcal{E}_{v}
. The second shows that
\sum^{p}_{r=1}\hat{w}^{r}_{v}
closely approximates
z_{v+1}/z_{v}
conditional on
\mathcal{G}_{v}
.
The third shows that the distribution
\tilde{\mu}_{v}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})
is locally 7-warm (see Definition
1
) with respect to
\mathcal{A}
. Note that all of these results describe what happens
conditional
on
\mathcal{G}_{v}
holding. In Section
4.2
, we use the results developed here to show also that
\mathcal{G}_{v}
occurs with high probability. The results in these two sections are then combined to obtain the proof of Theorem
1
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\phi(\lambda)=\phi=\frac{1+\lambda}{1-\lambda}
. For
v=1,\ldots,V
,

\displaystyle\Prob\left(\phi^{-v}\mu_{v}(A_{j})\leq\hat{P}_{v}^{j}\leq\phi^{v}\mu_{v}(A_{j})\mid\mathcal{G}_{v}\right)=1.

In particular, since
\tilde{\mu}(A_{j}\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})=\Prob(\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in A_{j}\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})=\E\left[\hat{P}^{j}_{v}\mid\mathcal{G}_{v}\right]
we have:

\displaystyle\phi^{-v}\mu_{v}(A_{j})\leq\tilde{\mu}_{v}(A_{j}\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})\leq\phi^{v}\mu_{v}(A_{j}).

(Lemma
1
)
First notice that given
\mathcal{G}_{v}
we have

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{v}^{j}=\frac{\hat{w}^{j}_{v}}{\sum^{p}_{r=1}\hat{w}^{r}_{v}}=\frac{\bar{w}^{j}_{v}}{\sum^{p}_{r=1}\bar{w}^{r}_{v}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1+\lambda)\E[\bar{w}^{j}_{v}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-2}]}{(1-\lambda)\sum^{p}_{r=1}\E[\bar{w}^{r}_{v}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-2}]}

\displaystyle=\phi\frac{\E[\bar{w}^{j}_{v}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-2}]}{\sum^{p}_{r=1}\E[\bar{w}^{r}_{v}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-2}]}

(9)

where the second equality follows by recalling that
\mathcal{C}_{v-1}(ii)=\{X_{v-1}^{1:N}=\bar{X}^{1:N}_{v-1}\}
and
\mathcal{C}_{v-1}(ii)\subset\mathcal{G}_{v}
, and the first inequality by recalling that
\mathcal{C}^{1:p}_{v}(i)\subset\mathcal{G}_{v}

and
\mathcal{C}^{1:p}_{v}(i)=\{(1-\lambda)\E[\bar{w}^{j}_{v}|\mathcal{F}_{v-2}]\leq\bar{w}^{j}_{v}\leq(1+\lambda)\E[\bar{w}^{j}_{v}|\mathcal{F}_{v-2}]\}
. Lemma
10
in Appendix
A
shows that

\displaystyle\E[\bar{w}^{j}_{v}|\mathcal{F}_{v-2}]=\frac{z_{v}}{z_{v-1}}\cdot\frac{\mu_{v}(A_{j})}{\mu_{v-1}(A_{j})}\cdot\hat{P}^{j}_{v-1}

(10)

Intuitively, this means that
\E[\bar{w}^{j}_{v}|\mathcal{F}_{v-2}]
is the ratio of the normalizing constants of the restricted distributions
\mu_{v|A_{j}}
and
\mu_{v-1|A_{j}}

but weighted by the probability that a particle is resampled into
A_{j}
. We can think of the filtration as being summarized solely through
\hat{P}^{j}_{v-1}
due to the conditional independence properties of the constructed particles (see Lemma
9
in Appendix
A
).
Combining (
9
) and (
10
) and canceling, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{w}^{j}_{v}}{\sum^{p}_{r=1}\hat{w}^{r}_{v}}\leq\phi\frac{\frac{\mu_{v}(A_{j})}{\mu_{v-1}(A_{j})}\hat{P}^{j}_{v-1}}{\sum^{p}_{r=1}\frac{\mu_{v}(A_{r})}{\mu_{v-1}(A_{r})}\hat{P}^{r}_{v-1}}=\phi\frac{\frac{\mu_{v}(A_{j})}{\mu_{v-1}(A_{j})}\hat{w}^{j}_{v-1}}{\sum^{p}_{r=1}\frac{\mu_{v}(A_{r})}{\mu_{v-1}(A_{r})}\hat{w}^{r}_{v-1}}

(11)

Barring the constant terms, the right hand side of (
11
) is the same as the left hand side except the index has been decremented by one.
Recall
that
\mathcal{G}_{v}

ensures that the events
\mathcal{C}_{v}^{1:p}(i)
and
\mathcal{C}_{v-1}(ii)
which we appealed to for step
v
also hold for all previous steps
0,\ldots,v-1
. Thus given
\mathcal{G}_{v}
the argument above can be repeated recursively to give

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{w}^{j}_{v}}{\sum^{p}_{r=1}\hat{w}^{r}_{v}}\leq\phi\frac{\frac{\mu_{v}(A_{j})}{\mu_{v-1}(A_{j})}\hat{w}^{j}_{v-1}}{\sum^{p}_{r=1}\frac{\mu_{v}(A_{r})}{\mu_{v-1}(A_{r})}\hat{w}^{r}_{v-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\phi^{2}\frac{\frac{\mu_{v}(A_{j})}{\mu_{v-2}(A_{j})}\hat{w}^{j}_{v-2}}{\sum^{p}_{r=1}\frac{\mu_{v}(A_{r})}{\mu_{v-2}(A_{r})}\hat{w}^{r}_{v-2}}

\displaystyle\ldots

\displaystyle\leq\phi^{v-1}\frac{\frac{\mu_{v}(A_{j})}{\mu_{1}(A_{j})}\hat{w}^{j}_{1}}{\sum^{p}_{r=1}\frac{\mu_{v}(A_{r})}{\mu_{1}(A_{r})}\hat{w}^{r}_{1}}

Recall that
\mathcal{C}^{1:p}_{0}(i)\subset\mathcal{G}_{v}
is the event that
\hat{w}^{j}_{1}

closely approximates the normalizing constant of the restricted distribution
\mu_{1|A_{j}}
; plugging this in gives

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{v}^{j}=\frac{\hat{w}^{j}_{v}}{\sum^{p}_{r=1}\hat{w}^{r}_{v}}\leq\phi^{v}\frac{\frac{\mu_{v}(A_{j})}{\mu_{1}(A_{j})}\frac{z_{1}\mu_{1}(A_{j})}{z_{0}}}{\sum^{p}_{r=1}\frac{\mu_{v}(A_{r})}{\mu_{1}(A_{r})}\frac{z_{1}\mu_{1}(A_{r})}{z_{0}}}=\phi^{v}\mu_{v}(A_{j})

Repeating the argument in the other direction (with
\phi^{-1}
in place of
\phi
) yields the result.

∎

Remark 1
.

Note that the same argument implies

\displaystyle\Prob\left(\phi^{-v}\mu_{v}(A_{j})\leq\hat{P}_{v}^{j}\leq\phi^{v}\mu_{v}(A_{j})\mid\mathcal{G}_{v^{\prime}}\right)=1

for any
v^{\prime}\geq v
. To see this, notice that
\mathcal{G}_{v}\subset\mathcal{G}_{v^{\prime}}
and the above argument holds for any set containing
\mathcal{G}_{v}
.

To establish local warmness of
\tilde{\mu}_{v}
, we will use the following lemma which shows that
\sum_{j}\hat{w}^{j}_{v}
closely approximates
z_{v}/z_{v-1}
over
\mathcal{G}_{v}
. This establishes that, within the set

, the algorithm given in Section

can be used to approximate
z_{v}
for any
v=1,\ldots,V
when the initial

is known.

Lemma 2
.

For
v=1,\ldots,V
we have

\displaystyle\Prob\left(\frac{1}{\phi^{v}}\frac{z_{v}}{z_{v-1}}\leq\sum^{p}_{r=1}\hat{w}^{r}_{v}\leq\phi^{v}\frac{z_{v}}{z_{v-1}}\Bigm|\mathcal{G}_{v}\right)=1

Note that
\mathcal{C}_{v-1}(ii)\subset\mathcal{G}_{v}
and so
\hat{w}^{j}_{v}=\bar{w}^{j}_{v}
for

given
\mathcal{G}_{v}
. We then have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bar{w}_{v}^{j}\geq\frac{z_{v}}{z_{v-1}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(1-\lambda)\frac{\mu_{v}(A_{j})}{\mu_{v-1}(A_{j})}\hat{P}_{v-1}^{j}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(1-\lambda)}{\phi^{v-1}}\frac{z_{v}}{z_{v-1}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mu_{v}(A_{j})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\phi^{v}}\cdot\frac{z_{v}}{z_{v-1}}

The first inequality follows since
\mathcal{C}^{1:p}_{v}(i)\subset\mathcal{G}_{v}
and by Lemma

(see Appendix

). The second inequality follows by Lemma
1
. An identical argument can be repeated in the other direction showing
\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bar{w}_{v}^{j}\leq\phi^{v}\frac{z_{v}}{z_{v-1}}
.
∎We now show that
\tilde{\mu}_{v}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})
is a locally 7-warm distribution with respect to
\mu_{v}
and
\mathcal{A}
. To do this, we first state an inequality regarding
\phi^{v}
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\phi(\lambda)=\phi=\frac{1+\lambda}{1-\lambda}
and recall
\lambda\leq\frac{\epsilon}{6V}

. Then
\phi^{v}<\phi^{2v}<\frac{1+\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}
for
v=1,\ldots,V
,

\displaystyle\phi^{2v}=\left(\frac{1+\frac{\epsilon}{6V}}{1-\frac{\epsilon}{6V}}\right)^{2v}\leq\frac{e^{\frac{\epsilon}{3}}}{1-\frac{\epsilon}{3}}\leq\frac{1+\frac{\epsilon}{3}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{9}}{1-\frac{\epsilon}{3}}\leq\frac{1+\epsilon}{1-\epsilon},

where we used the elementary inequalities
1+x\leq e^{x}
,
(1+x)^{p}\geq 1+xp
for
x>-1
and
p>1
, and
e^{x}\leq 1+x+x^{2}
for
x\in(0,1)
.
∎Proposition
1
shows that taking
\lambda
to be
\mathcal{O}(V^{-1})
suffices to ensure the bound in Lemma
1
is uniform over
v=1,\ldots,V
.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose
\Prob(\mathcal{G}^{c}_{v})\leq\frac{\mu^{\star}}{4}
and recall
\lambda=\frac{\epsilon}{24V}\leq\frac{1}{6V}
. Then
\tilde{\mu}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})
is locally 7-warm with respect to
\mu_{v}
for partition
\mathcal{A}
; that is,

\displaystyle\sup_{B\subset A_{j}}\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{v\mid A_{j}}(B\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})}{\mu_{v\mid A_{j}}(B)}<7,\text{ for }j=1,\ldots,p.

Expand

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{v\mid A_{j}}(B\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})}{\mu_{v\mid A_{j}}(B)}=\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{v}(B\cap A_{j}\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})\mu_{v}(A_{j})}{\tilde{\mu}_{v}(A_{j}\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})\mu_{v}(B\cap A_{j})}

(12)

and consider first the term
\tilde{\mu}_{v}(B\cap A_{j}\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})=\Prob(\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in B\cap A_{j}\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})
. We have

\displaystyle\Prob(\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in B\cap A_{j}\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})

\displaystyle=\E\left[\frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{n=1}w_{v}(\bar{X}^{n}_{v-1})\mathbbm{1}_{A_{j}\cap B}(\bar{X}^{n}_{v-1})}{\sum^{p}_{r=1}\bar{w}^{r}_{v}}\Bigm|\mathcal{G}_{v}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\phi^{v}\E\left[\frac{\mu_{v}(\bar{X}^{i}_{v-1})}{\mu_{v-1}(\bar{X}^{i}_{v-1})}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{j}\cap B}(\bar{X}^{i}_{v-1})\Bigm|\mathcal{G}_{v}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi^{v}}{\Prob(\mathcal{G}_{v})}\E\left[\frac{\mu_{v}(\bar{X}^{i}_{v-1})}{\mu_{v-1}(\bar{X}^{i}_{v-1})}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{j}\cap B}(\bar{X}^{i}_{v-1})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{G}_{v}}(X_{1:v-1}^{1:N},\bar{X}_{1:v-1}^{1:N})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\phi^{v}}{\Prob(\mathcal{G}_{v})}\E\left[\frac{\mu_{v}(\bar{X}^{i}_{v-1})\mathbbm{1}_{A_{j}\cap B}(\bar{X}^{i}_{v-1})}{\mu_{v-1}(\bar{X}^{i}_{v-1})}\Bigm|\bar{X}^{i}_{v-1}\in A_{j}\right]\bar{\mu}_{v-1}(A_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi^{v}}{\Prob(\mathcal{G}_{v})}\mu_{v}(A_{j}\cap B)\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{v-1}(A_{j})}{\mu_{v-1}(A_{j})}

The first inequality follows by Lemma
2
. The second inequality follows from non-negativity of the integrand and from the law of total probability. The latter is required because
\bar{\mu}_{v-1}\neq\mu_{v-1}
but

\Prob(\bar{X}^{i}_{v-1}\in B^{\prime}\mid\bar{X}^{i}_{v-1}\in A_{j})=\mu_{v-1|A_{j}}(B{{}^{\prime}})
for
B{{}^{\prime}}\subset\mathcal{X}
(see Appendix
A
). This fact is also used in the final line, along with the fact that
\bar{\mu}_{v}(A_{j})=\tilde{\mu}_{v}(A_{j})
holds for all
i,j,v
by construction (see Appendix
A
). As a result, from (
12
) we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{v\mid A_{j}}(B\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})}{\mu_{v\mid A_{j}}(B)}\leq\frac{\phi^{v}}{\Prob(\mathcal{G}_{v})}\frac{\Prob(\tilde{X}^{i}_{v-1}\in A_{j})}{\mu_{v-1}(A_{j})}\cdot\frac{\mu_{v}(A_{j})}{\tilde{\mu}_{v}(A_{j}\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})}.

Now by Lemma
1
,
\tilde{\mu}_{v}(A_{j}\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})\geq\phi^{-v}\mu_{v}(A_{j})

and therefore

\frac{\mu_{v}(A_{j})}{\tilde{\mu}_{v}(A_{j}\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})}\leq\phi^{v}
provides a lower bound on the relative marginal probability of undersampling partition elements. From Lemma
1
we also have

\displaystyle\Prob(\tilde{X}^{i}_{v-1}\in A_{j})\,=\tilde{\mu}_{v-1}(A_{j})\leq\phi^{v-1}\mu_{v-1}(A_{j})\Prob(\mathcal{G}_{v})+\Prob(\mathcal{G}^{c}_{v}).

Our assumptions on
\lambda
and
\Prob(\mathcal{G}^{c}_{v})
then yield

\displaystyle\sup_{B\subset A_{j}}\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{v\mid A_{j}}(B\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})}{\mu_{v\mid A_{j}}(B)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\phi^{2v}}{\Prob(\mathcal{G}_{v})\mu_{v-1}(A_{j})}\Prob(\tilde{X}^{i}_{v-1}\in A_{j})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\phi^{2v}}{\Prob(\mathcal{G}_{v})}\left[\frac{\phi^{v-1}\mu_{v-1}(A_{j})\Prob(\mathcal{G}_{v})+\Prob(\mathcal{G}_{v}^{c})}{\mu_{v-1}(A_{j})}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\phi^{3v-1}+\frac{\phi^{2v}}{\Prob(\mathcal{G}_{v})}

\displaystyle\leq 4\,e^{\frac{1}{2}}\,<\,7

The third inequality follows since
\mu^{\star}\leq\mu_{v-1}(A_{j})
. The final inequality is obtained by repeating the argument of Proposition
1

for the choice of
\lambda
to get
\phi^{jv}<\frac{6e^{\frac{j}{6}}}{6-j}
, and using
\Prob(\mathcal{G}_{v})>3/4
.
∎

4.2
Induction Argument

The remainder of the analysis is to show that
\mathcal{G}_{v}
holds with high probability. We will do so inductively by first establishing that
\Prob(\mathcal{E}_{v}\mid\mathcal{E}_{v-1})
is sufficiently large for all
v
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
0<\delta<1
and suppose
N>\frac{2W^{2}Z^{2}\phi^{2(v-1)}\log(\frac{4p}{\delta})}{\lambda^{2}(\mu^{\star})^{2}}
. Then

\displaystyle\Prob(\mathcal{C}^{1:p}_{v}(i)\mid\mathcal{E}_{v-1})\geq 1-\frac{\delta}{\Prob(\mathcal{E}_{v-1})}

Note that
\mathcal{G}_{v-1}\subset\mathcal{E}_{v-1}
. By combining Lemma
10
(Appendix
A
) and Lemma
1
, we have over
\mathcal{G}_{v-1}
,

\displaystyle\E[\bar{w}^{j}_{v}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-2}]=\frac{z_{v}}{z_{v-1}}\cdot\frac{\mu_{v}(A_{j})}{\mu_{v-1}(A_{j})}\cdot\hat{P}^{j}_{v-1}\geq\frac{z_{v}}{z_{v-1}}\cdot\phi^{-(v-1)}\cdot\mu_{v}(A_{j})

Then

\displaystyle\Prob((\mathcal{C}^{j}_{v}(i))^{c}\mid\mathcal{E}_{v-1})=

\displaystyle\Prob\left(\left|\bar{w}^{j}_{v}-\E[\bar{w}^{j}_{v}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-2}]\right|>\lambda\E[\bar{w}^{j}_{v}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-2}]\mid\mathcal{E}_{v-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Prob\left(\left|\bar{w}^{j}_{v}-\E[\bar{w}^{j}_{v}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-2}]\right|>\lambda\frac{z_{v}}{z_{v-1}}\phi^{-(v-1)}\mu_{v}(A_{j})\mid\mathcal{E}_{v-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Prob(\mathcal{E}_{v-1})}\Prob\left(\left|\bar{w}^{j}_{v}-\E[\bar{w}^{j}_{v}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-2}]\right|>\lambda\frac{z_{v}}{z_{v-1}}\phi^{-(v-1)}\mu_{v}(A_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{p\Prob(\mathcal{E}_{v-1})}

where the last line
in Appendix
A
with
N>\frac{2W^{2}Z^{2}\phi^{2(v-1)}\log(\frac{4p}{\delta})}{\lambda^{2}(\mu^{\star})^{2}}
. By the union bound,

\displaystyle\Prob(\cup^{p}_{j=1}(\mathcal{C}^{j}_{v}(i))^{c}\mid\mathcal{E}_{v-1})\leq\frac{\delta}{\Prob(\mathcal{E}_{v-1})}

DeMorgan’s law gives the result.
∎To complete the inductive step, it remains to show that

P(\mathcal{C}_{v}(ii)\mid\mathcal{C}_{v}^{1:p},\mathcal{E}_{v-1})
is also large. To do so, we use the local warmness result established in Lemma
3
.

Lemma 5
.

Suppose
\Prob(\mathcal{G}^{c}_{v})\leq\frac{\mu^{\star}}{4}
and recall
\lambda\leq\frac{1}{6V}
. Let

\displaystyle t>\max_{v=1,\ldots,V}\max_{j=1,\ldots,p}\tau_{v,j}\left(\frac{\delta}{N},7\right)

Then
\Prob(\mathcal{C}_{v}(ii)\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})\geq 1-\delta
.

Since
\hat{\mu}_{v}(A_{j})=\tilde{\mu}_{v}(A_{j})
for all
v,j
(see Appendix
A
), we have for
j=1,\ldots,p
and
B\subset\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{v|A_{j}}(B\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{\Prob(X_{v}^{i}\in B,\tilde{X}_{v}^{i}\in A_{j}\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})}{\Prob(\tilde{X}_{v}^{i}\in A_{j}\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tilde{\mu}_{v}(A_{j}\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})}\int_{A_{j}}\tilde{\mu}_{v}(dx\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})K_{v|A_{j}}^{t}(x,B)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\tilde{\mu}_{v|A_{j}}(dx\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})K_{v|A_{j}}^{t}(x,B)

By Lemma
3
,
\tilde{\mu}_{v|A_{j}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})\in\mathcal{P}_{7}(\mu_{v|A_{j}})
. Hence, by our choice of

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{\mu}_{v|A_{j}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})-\mu_{v|A_{j}}(\cdot)\right\rVert_{\text{TV}}\leq\frac{\delta}{N}\text{ for }j=1,\ldots,p

It follows that we can construct
(X^{i}_{v},\bar{X}^{i}_{v})
conditional on
\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}
independently for

such that

\displaystyle\Prob(X^{i}_{v}\neq\bar{X}^{i}_{v}\mid\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in A_{j},\mathcal{G}_{v})\leq\frac{\delta}{N},\text{ for }j=1,\ldots,p

(see Appendix

for details). It follows that
\Prob(X^{i}_{v}\neq\bar{X}^{i}_{v}\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})\leq\frac{\delta}{N}
by the law of total probability. Finally, taking a union bound gives us the stated result:

\displaystyle\Prob(\mathcal{C}_{v}(ii)\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})=\Prob(X^{1:N}_{v}\neq\bar{X}^{1:N}_{v}\mid\mathcal{G}_{v})

\displaystyle\leq\delta

∎Lemma
4
and Lemma

are enough to establish that
\Prob(\mathcal{E}_{v}\mid\mathcal{E}_{v-1})
can be made sufficiently large, which gives us the following result.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
and
\epsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. Choose

1.

N>3456\cdot\left(\frac{VWZ}{\epsilon\mu^{\star}}\right)^{2}\cdot\log\left(\frac{8Vp}{\delta\mu^{\star}}\right)

2.

\displaystyle\Prob(\mathcal{E}_{V})\geq 1-\delta\mu^{\star}

\Prob(\mathcal{G}_{k}^{c})\leq\frac{\mu^{\star}}{4}

5
for steps

. Let
\delta^{\prime}=\frac{\delta\mu^{\star}}{2V}

N
and

\displaystyle\Prob(\mathcal{E}_{v})

\displaystyle=\Prob(\mathcal{C}^{1:p}_{v}(i)\,\cap\,\mathcal{C}_{v}(ii)\,\cap\,\mathcal{E}_{v-1})

\displaystyle=\Prob(\mathcal{C}_{v}(ii)\mid\mathcal{C}^{1:p}_{v}(i)\,\cap\,\mathcal{E}_{v-1})\Prob(\mathcal{C}^{1:p}_{v}(i)\mid\mathcal{E}_{v-1})\Prob(\mathcal{E}_{v-1})

\displaystyle\geq(1-\delta^{\prime})\Big(1-\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{\Prob(\mathcal{E}_{v-1})}\Big)\Prob(\mathcal{E}_{v-1})

\displaystyle\geq\Prob(\mathcal{E}_{v-1})-2\delta^{\prime}

The first inequality follows by Lemmas

Since we have assumed

\displaystyle\Prob(\mathcal{E}_{v})\geq\Prob(\mathcal{E}_{1})-2(v-1)\delta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\Prob(\mathcal{C}_{1}(ii)\mid\mathcal{G}_{1})\Prob(\mathcal{G}_{1})-2(v-1)\delta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\Prob(\mathcal{G}_{1})=\Prob(\mathcal{C}^{1:p}_{1}(i))>1-\frac{\delta\mu^{\star}}{2V}

\displaystyle=\Prob(\mathcal{C}^{1:p}_{v}(i)|\mathcal{E}_{v-1})\Prob(\mathcal{E}_{v-1})

4.3
Proof of Theorem
1

The proof of Theorem

uses the following additional lemma regarding the target random variables
X^{1:N}_{V}

Lemma 7
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and recall
\lambda=\frac{\epsilon}{24V}
. For any
f
such that
|f|\leq 1

\displaystyle\left|\sum^{p}_{j=1}\E\left[f(\bar{X}^{i}_{V})\mathbbm{1}_{A_{j}}(\bar{X}^{i}_{V})\mid\mathcal{F}_{V-1}\right]-\frac{(1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{16})}{(1-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{16})}\E_{\pi}[f]\right|\leq\frac{3}{4}\epsilon

f^{+}(x)=\max\{f(x),0\}
and
f^{-}(x)=\max\{-f(x),0\}

1

\lambda

\frac{\tilde{\epsilon}}{6V}

\tilde{\epsilon}=\epsilon/4
. For

\displaystyle\E\left[f(\bar{X}^{i}_{V})\mathbbm{1}_{A_{j}}(\bar{X}^{i}_{V})\mid\mathcal{F}_{V-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{\pi_{|A_{j}}}[f^{+}]\hat{P}^{j}_{V}-\E_{\pi_{|A_{j}}}[f^{-}]\hat{P}^{j}_{V}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1+\tilde{\epsilon})}{(1-\tilde{\epsilon})}\E_{\pi}[f^{+}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{j}}(\bar{X}^{i}_{V})]-\frac{(1-\tilde{\epsilon})}{(1+\tilde{\epsilon})}\E_{\pi}[f^{-}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{j}}(\bar{X}^{i}_{V})]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1+\tilde{\epsilon}^{2})}{(1-\tilde{\epsilon}^{2})}\E_{\pi}[f\mathbbm{1}_{A_{j}}(\bar{X}^{i}_{V})]+\frac{2\tilde{\epsilon}}{(1-\tilde{\epsilon}^{2})}\pi(A_{j}),

The first line follows by Lemma
10
, the second by application of Lemma
1
followed by Proposition
1
with our choice of
\lambda
, and the last since
|f|\leq 1
. Summing over
j
gives

\displaystyle\sum^{p}_{j=1}\E\left[f(\bar{X}^{i}_{V})\mathbbm{1}_{A_{j}}(\bar{X}^{i}_{V})\mid\mathcal{F}_{V-1}\right]\leq\frac{(1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{16})}{(1-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{16})}\E_{\pi}[f]+\frac{3}{4}\epsilon.

The argument for the lower bound is identical.
∎

We are now ready to give the complete proof of Theorem
1
.

(Theorem
1
)
Define the concentration event,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{j}=\left\{\left|\bar{f}^{j}-\E[f(\bar{X}^{i}_{V})\mathbbm{1}_{A_{j}}(\bar{X}^{i}_{V})|\mathcal{F}_{V-1}]\right|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{p}\right\},

where
\bar{f}^{j}=\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}f(\bar{X}^{i}_{V})\mathbbm{1}_{A_{j}}(\bar{X}^{i}_{V})
.
Note that over
\mathcal{E}_{v}
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}f(X^{i}_{V})=\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}\sum^{p}_{j=1}f(\bar{X}^{i}_{V})\mathbbm{1}_{A_{j}}(\bar{X}^{i}_{V})=\sum^{p}_{j=1}\bar{f}^{j}

By Lemma
7
, it follows that over
\mathcal{E}_{V}\cap\mathcal{D}^{1}\cap\ldots\cap\mathcal{D}^{p}
,

\displaystyle\frac{(1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{16})}{(1-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{16})}\E_{\pi}[f]-\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}f(X^{i}_{V})\leq\frac{(1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{16})}{(1-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{16})}\E_{\pi}[f]+\epsilon

It remains to show
\Prob(\mathcal{E}_{V}\cap\mathcal{D}^{1}\cap\ldots\cap\mathcal{D}^{p})\geq\frac{3}{4}
. First, by Lemma
11
,

\displaystyle\Prob(\cup^{p}_{j=1}(\mathcal{D}^{j})^{c})\leq\sum^{p}_{j=1}\Prob\left(\left|\bar{f}^{j}-\E[\bar{f}^{j}|\mathcal{F}_{V-1}]\right|>\frac{\epsilon}{p}\right)\leq\frac{1}{8},

for
N>\frac{2p^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\log(32p)
. Note that
\Prob(\mathcal{E}_{V})\geq\frac{7}{8}
by our choice of
N
and
t
after applying Lemma
6
with
\delta=\frac{1}{8}
. By the union bound,

\displaystyle\Prob((\mathcal{D}^{1})^{c}\cup\ldots\cup(\mathcal{D}^{p})^{c}\cup\mathcal{E}^{c}_{V})\leq\frac{1}{8}+\frac{1}{8}=\frac{1}{4}.

∎

5
Applications

In this section, we apply the results in the previous section to obtain bounds on the complexity of using sequential Monte Carlo to approximate expectations for two specific classes of multimodal distributions: mixtures of log-concave distributions and the mean-field Ising model. Each problem defines a nested sequence of distributions indexed by a dimension parameter
d
, and we consider the computational complexity, i.e. the growth of the computational cost with increasing
d
. We characterize the computational cost of applying the SMC algorithm given in Section
2.1
by the total number of Markov transition steps
NVt
required to apply Theorem
1
, since the Markov transition steps are typically the most computationally expensive step of the algorithm. Throughout we will assume the corresponding partition sequence
\mathcal{A}(d)
satisfies
\pi^{*}=\min_{j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}}\pi(A_{j})
decays at most polynomially in
d
. (Otherwise, the contribution of
\E_{\pi}[f\mathbbm{1}_{A_{j^{*}}}]
to
\E_{\pi}[f]
for the corresponding
j^{*}
is negligible for
d
large since
\left|f\right|\leq 1
.)

5.1
Mixtures of Log-Concave Distributions

Consider the mixture distribution

\displaystyle\pi(x)\propto w\pi_{1}(x)\mathbbm{1}_{H}(x)+(1-w)\pi_{2}(x)\mathbbm{1}_{H^{c}}(x)\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(15)

where
w\in(0,1)
,
\pi_{j}(x)\propto e^{-f_{j}(x)}
,
f_{j}(x)>0
is a convex function for
j\in\{1,2\}
, and
H
is a half-space. Throughout, we assume without loss of generality that
H
and
H^{c}
contain
\E_{\pi_{1}}[X]
and
\E_{\pi_{2}}[X]
, respectively.
Note that
\pi
is a sum of log-concave functions, since
H
and
H^{c}
being half-spaces means
\mathbbm{1}_{H}
and
\mathbbm{1}_{H^{c}}
are log-concave functions and log-concavity is closed under multiplication.

Suppose we are interested in sampling from
\pi(x)
using SMC, and we set
\mu_{v}(x)\propto\pi^{\beta_{v}}(x)
for
\beta_{v}\in(0,1]
and
\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{V}
a strictly increasing sequence with
\beta_{V}=1
. Since
f_{j}(x)>0
, it follows that
w_{v}(x)\leq 1
uniformly in
v
. We will assume that the following equality holds:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{d}}e^{-\beta_{v}f_{1}(x)}dx=\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{d}}e^{-\beta_{v}f_{2}(x)}dx\text{ for }\beta_{v}\in(0,1]

(16)

For example, if
f_{j}(x)=2^{-1}\sigma_{j}^{-2}\left\lVert x-\mu_{j}\right\rVert^{2}
, as below in Section
5.2
, then (
16
) requires that
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}
. More generally, (
16
) holds when
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are log-concave product distributions that belong to the same location-scale family and have equal scales. Similar assumptions have been made in previous analyses of these types of problems (see
[
42
]
and
[
16
]
), and it is unclear whether the assumption (
16
) can be dropped. For example, if
\sigma_{1}>\sigma_{2}
in the problem considered in Section
5.2
, then
\mu^{\star}
decays exponentially in
d

[
43
]
.

Note that
\mu_{v}(x)
can also be written as a sum of log-concave functions and assumption (
16
) gives

\displaystyle\mu_{v}(H)

\displaystyle=\frac{w\int_{H}\pi_{1}^{\beta_{v}}(x)dx}{w\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\pi_{1}^{\beta_{v}}(x)\mathbbm{1}_{H}(x)dx+(1-w)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\pi_{2}^{\beta_{v}}(x)\mathbbm{1}_{H^{c}}(x)dx}

\displaystyle=\frac{w\pi_{1,v}(H)}{w\pi_{1,v}(H)+(1-w)\pi_{2,v}(H^{c})}

where
\pi_{v,j}(x)=e^{-\beta_{v}f_{j}(x)}/\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{d}}e^{-\beta_{v}f_{j}(x)}dx

is an (untruncated) log-concave density function. By
[
19
]
, if
h(x)
is a log-concave density and
H
is a half-space containing the centroid of
h(x)
, then
\int_{H}h(x)dx\geq e^{-1}
. Hence,
\mu^{\star}\geq\max\{w,1-w\}/e=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

Since
H
and
H^{c}
are convex sets and
\pi_{v,j}(x)
is a log-concave density function,
\mu_{v|H}(x)
and
\mu_{v|H^{c}}(x)
are log-concave density functions.
Upper bounds on the mixing time of various Markov kernels have been obtained in the literature for log-concave target distributions, assuming a warm start. For example,
[
19
]
proved that both the ball walk and hit-and-run walk are rapidly mixing when the target distribution is a
C
-isotropic log-concave density function, and show that any
d
-dimensional log-concave density function can be brought into a
C
-isotropic position in
\mathcal{O}^{*}(d^{5})
time (see
[
36
]
for a survey of these results). Recall that both
\mu^{\star}=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
W=\mathcal{O}(1)
. It follows that by plugging into the bound on
N
in Theorem
1
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{NV}{\mu^{\star}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{V^{3}Z^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\log(V)\right)=\mathcal{O}(\text{poly}(V,\log(V),Z,\epsilon^{-1}))),

(17)

Therefore, by choosing
K_{v|A_{j}}
to be either the ball walk or hit-and-run walk and applying the results of
[
19
]
, we conclude

\displaystyle\max\limits_{j=1,\ldots,p}\max\limits_{v=1,\ldots,V}\tau_{v,j}\left(\frac{\mu^{\star}}{16NV},7\right)=\mathcal{O}(\text{poly}(d,V,\log(V),Z,\epsilon^{-1}))

(18)

Combining (
17
) and (
18
) gives that the computational cost of applying SMC to this problem is
\mathcal{O}(\text{poly}(d,V,\log(V),Z,\epsilon^{-1}))
.

5.2
Gaussian mixtures

We can provide more explicit bounds when
f_{j}(x)
is known. Consider the Gaussian mixture distribution
f_{j}(x)=(2\sigma^{2})^{-1}\left\lVert x-(-1)^{j+1}\mathbf{1}_{d}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}
for
j\in\{1,2\}
and
H=\{x:x^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{d}>0\}
studied by
Woodard, Schmidler and
Huber 2009a
. Here we assume
w=\frac{1}{2}
; similar results apply if
w\neq\frac{1}{2}
. Since the components share a common scale
\sigma>0
, it is straightforward to check that (
16
) holds and clearly both
\mathbf{1}_{d}\in H
and
-\mathbf{1}_{d}\in H^{c}
. Define the sequence of inverse-temperatures

\displaystyle\beta_{v}=\frac{1}{d}\left(1+\frac{1}{d}\right)^{v}\text{ for }v=0,\ldots,\lceil d\log(d)\rceil-1\text{ and }\beta_{V}=1.

Note
\beta_{0}=d^{-1}
, which ensures rapid mixing for initialization (see
[
42
]
for details). To bound the normalizing constants, notice

\displaystyle\frac{z_{v-1}}{z_{v}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{d}}e^{-\beta_{v-1}f_{1}(x)}\mathbbm{1}_{H}(x)dx+\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{d}}e^{-\beta_{v-1}f_{2}(x)}\mathbbm{1}_{H^{c}}(x)dx}{\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{d}}e^{-\beta_{v}f_{1}(x)}\mathbbm{1}_{H}(x)dx+\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{d}}e^{-\beta_{v}f_{2}(x)}\mathbbm{1}_{H^{c}}(x)dx}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi(\frac{\sqrt{d\beta_{v-1}}}{\sigma})\left(\frac{2\pi\sigma^{2}}{\beta_{v-1}}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\Phi(\frac{\sqrt{d\beta_{v}}}{\sigma})\left(\frac{2\pi\sigma^{2}}{\beta_{v}}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\leq 2\left(\frac{\beta_{v}}{\beta_{v-1}}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}

We see that
Z\leq 2e=\mathcal{O}(1)
. Next, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{v}(H)=\frac{\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{d}}e^{-\beta_{v}f_{1}(x)}\mathbbm{1}_{H}(x)dx}{\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{d}}e^{-\beta_{v}f_{1}(x)}\mathbbm{1}_{H}(x)dx+\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{d}}e^{-\beta_{v}f_{2}(x)}\mathbbm{1}_{H^{c}}(x)dx}\geq\frac{1/2}{2}=\frac{1}{4}

It follows that
\mu^{\star}\geq\frac{1}{4}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. Since
V=\mathcal{O}(d\log(d))
, combining with (
17
) and (
18
) implies that a computational complexity of

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\text{poly}(d,\log(d),\log(d\log(d)),\epsilon^{-1}))=\mathcal{O}^{*}(\text{poly}(d))

for SMC on this problem, where
\mathcal{O}^{*}
indicates that the dependence on logarithmic and error terms is suppressed.

5.3
Mean Field Ising Model

Let
\mathcal{X}=\{-1,1\}^{d}
for
d\in\mathbbm{N}
. The mean field Ising model is defined by the distribution

\displaystyle\pi(x)=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\left\{\frac{\alpha}{2d}\left(\sum^{d}_{i=1}x_{i}\right)^{2}\right\},

with normalizing constant
Z=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\exp\{\frac{\alpha}{2d}(\sum^{d}_{i=1}x_{i})^{2}\}
and interaction parameter
\alpha\in\mathbbm{R}
. We will assume
d
is odd as in
[
42
]
.
Let
K_{v}
be a Metropolis-Hastings chain with limiting distribution
\mu_{v}(x)\propto\pi^{\beta_{v}}(x)
. We assume
K_{v}
uses a single site proposal distribution in which a coordinate
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
is chosen uniformly and the sign of
x_{i}
is flipped. Letting
V=d
and
\beta_{v}=v/d
, it follows that both the parallel tempering and simulated tempering algorithms are rapidly mixing
[
21
,
42
]
. We will show that SMC also provides a polynomial time approximation of
\E_{\pi}[f]
for
|f|\leq 1

using the same sequence of distributions, in that the bounds on
N,t
required to apply Theorem
1
grow at most polynomially in
d
.

Our result is essentially a direct consequence of those from
Madras and Zheng 2003
and
Woodard, Schmidler and
Huber 2009a
. Let
A_{1}=\{x:\sum^{d}_{i=1}x_{i}\geq 0\}
and

A_{2}=\{x:\sum^{d}_{i=1}x_{i}<0\}
. First, note that sampling from
\mu_{0}(x)\propto 1
is trivial. Now, notice that
\pi(x)
is symmetric with respect to this partition for
\mu_{0},\ldots,\mu_{V}
if
d
is odd.
It follows that
\mu_{v}(A_{1})=\mu_{v}(A_{2})=1/2
for
v=0,\ldots,V
and so
\mu^{\star}=1/2
.
Woodard, Schmidler and
Huber 2009a
show that

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{v}(x)}{\mu_{v-1}(x)}\in\left[\exp\left\{-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right\},\exp\left\{\frac{\alpha}{2}\right\}\right]

therefore
ZW\leq\exp\{\frac{\alpha}{2}\}
. Since
V=d
,
p=2
,
\mu^{\star}=\mathcal{O}(1)
, and
ZW=\mathcal{O}(1)
, we have that Theorem
1
requires

\displaystyle N>\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\max\left\{c_{1}\cdot\left(\frac{VWZ}{\mu^{\star}}\right)^{2}\log\left(\frac{c_{2}Vp}{\mu^{\star}}\right),\ p^{2}\cdot\log\left(c_{3}p^{2}\right)\right\}=\mathcal{O}^{*}(d^{2}),

where
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
, and
c_{3}
are positive constants. To obtain the required bound for
t
, note also that
NV/\mu^{\star}=\mathcal{O}(d^{3}\log(d))
.
A mixing time bound can be obtained
[
20
,
42
]
by defining the restricted kernel as in (
1
) with corresponding invariant distributions
\mu_{v|A_{j}}
.
Madras and Zheng 2003
show that
\min_{v,j}\text{Gap}(K_{v|A_{j}})
decays at most polynomially in
d
. Since for
j\in\{1,2\}

we have (see
[
24
]
)

\displaystyle\tau_{v,j}(\epsilon,M)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\text{Gap}(K_{v\mid A_{j}})}\left[\log(2\epsilon^{-1})+\log(M-1)\right]

(19)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v^{\prime}=1,\ldots,V\\
j^{\prime}=1,\ldots,p\end{subarray}}\text{Gap}(K_{v^{\prime}\mid A_{j^{\prime}}})}\left[\log(2\epsilon^{-1})+\log(M-1)\right],

(20)

we obtain
\tau_{v,j}(\epsilon,M)=\mathcal{O}(\text{poly}(d,\log(d)))
. Hence,
NVt=\mathcal{O}^{*}(\text{poly}(d))
. That is, the values of
N
and
t
required to apply Theorem
1
grow at most polynomially in
d
.

6
Comparing SMC and Tempered MCMC

Throughout this section, we assume that the sequence of interpolating distributions is a tempered sequence, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mu_{v}(x)\propto\pi^{\beta_{v}}(x)\quad\text{ for }\;0<\beta_{0}<\ldots<\beta_{V}=1,

(21)

and that
\mu_{v}(x)/\mu_{v-1}(x)\leq ZW
. We let
\pi^{\star}=\min_{j=1,\ldots,p}\pi(A_{j})
and
K_{v|A_{j}}
a restricted kernel of the form (
1
).

Although population MC algorithms
[
15
]
such as SMC have been argued to perform better on multimodal target distributions than existing MCMC algorithms, it is reasonable to wonder whether the sequential, parallel nature of SMC plays a role, or whether any such benefits are simply attributable to the use of the distribution sequence (
21
). If the latter, then an MCMC algorithm that also takes advantage of the same distribution sequence may be expected to succeed (or fail) on the same problems where SMC succeeds (or fails).
The following results support this conjecture under fairly relaxed assumptions.

Ideally, we would like to show that the bounds on
N
and
t
in Theorem
1
grow at most polynomially in the problem size if and only if the parallel and simulated tempering algorithms are rapidly mixing on the same problem. The results below allow us to partially resolve the ‘if’ direction by relating the lower bounds of
[
42
]
on the spectral gap of swapping chains to our results. To formalize this, we first relate certain quantities defined in
[
42
]
to those appearing in Theorem
1
. Two key quantities appearing in the mixing time bounds of
Woodard, Schmidler and
Huber 2009a
are the
persistence
and the
overlap
. The following two propositions relate these quantities to the
\mu^{*}
and
ZW
quantities appearing in Theorem
1
.

Proposition 2
.

Define the
persistence

[
43
]
of partition
\mathcal{A}
under distribution sequence
\mu_{0},\ldots,\mu_{V}
by

\displaystyle\gamma(\mathcal{A})=\min_{j=1,\ldots,p}\prod^{V}_{v=1}\min\left\{1,\frac{\mu_{v-1}(A_{j})}{\mu_{v}(A_{j})}\right\}

(22)

Then
\mu^{\star}>\gamma(\mathcal{A})\cdot\pi^{\star}

For any
v=0,\ldots,V
and
j=1,\ldots,p
, we have that

\displaystyle\mu_{v}(A_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{v}(A_{j})}{\mu_{v+1}(A_{j})}\cdot\frac{\mu_{v+1}(A_{j})}{\mu_{v+2}(A_{j})}\ldots\frac{\mu_{V-1}(A_{j})}{\mu_{V}(A_{j})}\cdot\pi(A_{j})\geq\gamma(\mathcal{A})\cdot\pi^{\star}

Since this holds for any
v
and
j
, it must hold for
\mu^{\star}
as well.
∎

Controlling
\gamma(\mathcal{A})
is critical to the success of the PT and ST algorithms (see
[
43
]
). The quantity
\gamma(\mathcal{A})
describes how the measure of the partition elements of
\mathcal{A}
‘persists’ under tempering of the target distribution
\pi
. There is a close relationship between
\mu^{\star}
and
\gamma(\mathcal{A})
. Recall that
\pi^{\star}
decays at most polynomially in
d
by assumption. It follows that polynomial decay in
\gamma(\mathcal{A})
is enough to guarantee polynomial decay in
\mu^{\star}
by Proposition
2
.

Proposition 3
.

Define the
overlap

[
42
]
for distribution sequence
\mu_{0},\ldots,\mu_{V}
and partition
\mathcal{A}
by

\displaystyle\delta(\mathcal{A})=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v=0,\ldots,V-1\\
j=1,\ldots,p\end{subarray}}\frac{\int_{A_{j}}\min\{\mu_{v}(x),\mu_{v+1}(x)\}dx}{\max\{\mu_{v}(A_{j}),\mu_{v+1}(A_{j})\}}

(23)

Then,

\displaystyle\delta(\mathcal{A})\geq\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{ZW}\right\}\cdot\gamma(\mathcal{A})\cdot\pi^{\star}

Since
\mu_{v}(x)/\mu_{v+1}(x)\geq 1/ZW
, it follows that for any
v=0,\ldots,V-1
and
j=1,\ldots,p
,

\displaystyle\int_{A_{j}}\min\left\{\mu_{v}(x),\mu_{v+1}(x)\right\}dx

\displaystyle\geq\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{ZW}\right\}\cdot\mu_{v+1}(A_{j})\geq\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{ZW}\right\}\cdot\mu^{\star}

The result follows by Proposition
2
.
∎The overlap
\delta(\mathcal{A})
controls the rate at which temperature jumps occur in the simulated tempering algorithm and lower bounds the probability of swapping states at adjacent temperatures in the parallel tempering algorithm (see
[
42
]
). By Proposition
3
, if both
\gamma(\mathcal{A})
and
(ZW)^{-1}
decay at most polynomially in
d
, then the overlap decays at most polynomially.

Both
\delta(\mathcal{A})
and
ZW
can often be controlled in practice by decreasing the inverse temperature spacings.
Conversely,
\gamma(\mathcal{A})
and
\mu^{\star}
are highly dependent on the chosen
form
of the interpolating densities
and are potentially problematic. For example,
Woodard, Schmidler and
Huber 2009b
showed that
\gamma(\mathcal{A})
decays exponentially for the truncated
Gaussian mixture model of Section
5.2

and for the mean field Pott’s model, leading to torpid mixing of PT and ST in both cases.

Applying Proposition
2
and (
19
) allows us to restate Theorem
1
with
\gamma(\mathcal{A})\cdot\pi^{\star}
in place of
\mu^{\star}
and the mixing times bounded by
\text{Gap}(K_{v\mid A_{j}})^{-1}
:

Corollary 1
.

Suppose
|f|\leq 1
and (
A1
) holds. Let
\epsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and choose

1.

N>\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\max\left\{3456\cdot\left(\frac{VWZ}{\pi^{\star}\gamma(\mathcal{A})}\right)^{2}\cdot\log(\frac{64Vp}{\pi^{\star}\gamma(\mathcal{A})}),\ p^{2}\cdot\log(1024p^{2})\right\}

2.

t>\log\left(\frac{288NV}{\gamma(\mathcal{A})\pi^{\star}}\right)\cdot\left[\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v=1,\ldots,V\\
j=1,\ldots,p\end{subarray}}\textnormal{Gap}(K_{v|A_{j}})\right]^{-1}

Then with probability at least
3/4
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}f(X^{i}_{V})-\frac{(1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{16})}{(1-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{16})}\E_{\pi}[f]\right|\leq\epsilon

Comparing the complexity results for swapping chain algorithms (see Theorem 3.1 of
[
42
]
) to the bounds in Corollary
1
, we make the following observations. The dependence of
N
on
\gamma(\mathcal{A})
is quadratic (up to logarithmic terms) in Corollary

rather than exponential in
\left|\mathcal{A}\right|
as in
[
42
]
. (This is similar to the results stated in
[
16
]
, who proved a lower bound on the spectral gap of a simulated tempering chain with quadratic dependence on the minimum measure of a given partition (similar to
\mu^{\star}
)). In addition, the dependence in Corollary
1
on the number of temperatures

is quadratic rather than quartic, and dependence on
|\mathcal{A}|
quadratic rather than cubic, compared to
[
42
]
(omitting logarithmic terms).
(Note that
\text{Gap}(K_{0})
appears in the bound of
Woodard, Schmidler and
Huber 2009a
but not in ours, but our assumption in Section
2.1
that
\mu_{0}
can be sampled efficiently is equivalent to assuming
\text{Gap}(K_{0})
decays at most polynomially.)

Unfortunately, we are currently unable to replace
\delta(\mathcal{A})
in Corollary

with
ZW

3
suggests that (at most) polynomial growth of

may be a stronger requirement than polynomial decay of
\delta(\mathcal{A})
.
This prevents us from concluding the ‘if’ direction of our conjecture precisely. Nevertheless, we suspect that polynomial decay in
(ZW)^{-1}
and
\delta(\mathcal{A})
can be guaranteed on many problems of interest by decreasing the temperature spacings. We can summarize this discussion with the following corollary.

Corollary 2
.

Suppose
|f|\leq 1
, (
A1
) holds, and
\textnormal{Gap}(K_{v|A_{j}})
,
\textnormal{Gap}(K_{0})
,

,

,

. Then

1.

The parallel and simulated tempering algorithms provide a polynomial time
(\epsilon,\delta
)-randomized approximation for
\E_{\pi}[f]
.

2.

The SMC algorithm given in Section
2.1
provides a polynomial time
(\epsilon,\delta
)-randomized approximation for
\E_{\pi}[f]
.

This is a direct consequence of Corollary
1
and the results from
[
42
]
, along with the remarks regarding
\text{Gap}(K_{0})
above. ∎Corollary
2
says that if the parallel and simulated tempering algorithms provide an efficient Monte Carlo approximation, then the SMC algorithm in Section

will as well, up to the approximate equivalence of
(ZW)^{-1}
and
\delta(\mathcal{A})
discussed above. (It is unclear at this time if the latter distinction represents a meaningful difference in the algorithms, or simply an artifact of different proof approaches.)

Concluding the ‘only if’ direction of our conjecture necessitates development of lower bounds on SMC which are comparable to those in
Woodard, Schmidler and
Huber 2009b
. This is of interest but beyond the scope of the current paper.

7
Conclusion

The upper bounds established in Theorem
1
demonstrate that SMC algorithms can approximate global expectations using a polynomial number of Markov transition steps
without
assuming globally mixing mutation kernels. These results can be viewed as a generalization of those given in
[
24
]
: consider the special case of
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{X}
so that
\mu^{\star}=1
and
p=1
, then the bounds stated in Theorem
1
differ from those in
[
24
]
only by a
\mathcal{O}(V^{2})
term appearing in the lower bound on
N
. To see why this occurs, note that when the mutation kernels globally mix,
X^{1:N}_{v}
couples to an
i.i.d.
sample from
\mu_{v}
(see
[
24
]
). In this case, controlling
\hat{P}^{j}_{v}
is unnecessary, since
N^{-1}\sum^{N}_{i=1}w_{v}(X^{i}_{v-1})
always approximates
z_{v}/z_{v-1}
for sufficiently large
N
(conditional on the coupling event), regardless of whether
\tilde{\mu}_{v-1}(A_{j})
approximates
\mu_{v-1}(A_{j})
well. That is, globally mixing mutation kernels ‘correct’ for the bias of
N^{-1}\sum^{N}_{i=1}w_{v}(X^{i}_{v-1})
even if the resampling at step
v-1
goes terribly wrong. When global mixing cannot be assumed, it is necessary to control the trajectory of the resampling probability
\hat{P}^{j}_{v}
across steps
v=1,\ldots,V
in order to obtain such a concentration result; the
\mathcal{O}(V^{2})
term represents the cost of doing so.

In Section
5
we applied our results to two well known example multimodal problems: sampling from mixtures of log-concave distributions and the mean field Ising model. We found that the total number of mutation steps
NVt
required to apply Theorem
1
is comparable to bounds on the spectral gap given in the literature for seemingly similar algorithms. We explored this relationship further in Section
6
by comparing our results to those obtained in
[
42
]
for the parallel and simulated tempering algorithms. Corollary
2
almost suggests that if the complexity results of
[
42
]
grow at a polynomial rate in the problem size, then so do the lower bounds on
N
and
t
given in Theorem
1
. However, this conclusion cannot be made directly since we require uniform bounds on the density ratios. Establishing lower bounds on
Nt
to obtain results comparable to
Woodard, Schmidler and
Huber 2009b
is also of interest.

Finally, our results suggests that designing sequences of interpolating distributions for multimodal target distributions is an important problem. Indeed, Theorem
1
describes all of the properties such sequences should possess. Namely, adjacent densities should be ‘close’ to control
ZW
while still ensuring
V
polynomial in
d
, and modes should not be ‘lost’ to control
\mu^{\star}
.
Naturally
\mu_{0}
must also be easy to sample from. Unfortunately, finding such sequences is not straightforward. For example, annealed sequences break down on seemingly simple problems such as Gaussian mixtures with unequal scales (see
[
43
]
and
[
16
]
). The effect of distribution sequence choice on the computational complexity of SMC is considered further elsewhere
[
25
]
.

In this section, we provide the construction of the coupled random variables
\bar{X}^{1:N}_{v}
. Let
j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
and define

\displaystyle\frac{dK^{t}_{v\mid A_{j}}(x,\cdot)}{d\rho_{x}}:=f^{j}_{x}\qquad\qquad\frac{d\mu_{v\mid A_{j}}}{d\rho_{x}}:=g_{x}^{j},

where
\rho_{x}
is some dominating measure. The subscript denotes the implicit dependence on
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and the superscript denotes the partition element
A_{j}
. Now, let
h^{j}_{x}=\min\{g_{x}^{j},f^{j}_{x}\}
and set

\displaystyle a^{j}_{x}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}h^{j}_{x}d\rho_{x},\ \ b^{j}_{x}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}(f^{j}_{x}-h^{j}_{x})d\rho_{x},\ \ c^{j}_{x}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}(g_{x}^{j}-h^{j}_{x})d\rho_{x}

Each
\bar{X}^{i}_{v}
is constructed independently according to the following ‘coupling map’
C:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
. Let
Z^{i}_{v\mid j}
,
U_{v\mid j}^{i}
, and
V^{i}_{v\mid j}
denote random variables whose distributions have densities
h^{j}_{x}/a^{j}_{x}
,
(f^{j}_{x}-h^{j}_{x})/b_{x}
, and
(g_{x}^{j}-h^{j}_{x})/c^{j}_{x}
, respectively, for
j\in 1,\ldots,p
. The map
C
transitions
\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}
to
(X^{i}_{v},\bar{X}^{i}_{v})
as follows

1.

Independently draw
Z^{i}_{v\mid\xi^{i}_{v}}
,
U^{i}_{v\mid\xi^{i}_{v}}
, and
V^{i}_{v\mid\xi^{i}_{v}}
.

2.

Set
(X^{i}_{v},\bar{X}^{i}_{v})=(Z^{i}_{v|\xi^{i}_{v}},Z^{i}_{v\mid\xi^{i}_{v}})
with probability
a^{\xi^{i}_{v}}_{\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}}
. Else, set
(X^{i}_{v},\bar{X}^{i}_{v})=(U^{i}_{v|\xi^{i}_{v}},V^{i}_{v\mid\xi^{i}_{v}})
.

Then for
x\in A_{j}
we have

\displaystyle\Prob(C(x)\in B\times\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle=\Prob(U^{i}_{v\mid j}\in B\,\cap\,V^{i}_{v\mid j}\in\mathcal{X})(1-a^{j}_{x})+\Prob(Z^{i}_{v\mid j}\in B\,\cap\,\mathcal{X})a^{j}_{x},

\displaystyle=\Prob(U^{i}_{v\mid j}\in B)\Prob(V^{i}_{v\mid j}\in\mathcal{X})(1-a^{j}_{x})+\Prob(Z^{i}_{v\mid j}\in B)a^{j}_{x}

\displaystyle=K^{t}_{v\mid A_{j}}(x,B)

and similarly, for
x\in A_{j}
we have that
\Prob(C(x)\in\mathcal{X}\times B)=\mu_{v\mid A_{j}}(B)
.

We see then have that the new state
(X^{i}_{v},\bar{X}^{i}_{v})
satisfies

\displaystyle\Prob(X^{i}_{v}\in B\mid\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in A_{j})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\tilde{\mu}_{v|A_{j}}(dx)K_{v|A_{j}}^{t}(x,B)=\hat{\mu}_{v\mid A_{j}}(B)

and
\bar{\mu}_{v}(B\mid\tilde{X}_{v}^{i}\in A_{j})=\Prob(\bar{X}^{i}_{v}\in B\mid\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in A_{j})=\mu_{v\mid A_{j}}(B)
. By the law of total probability, it follows that
\mathcal{L}(X^{i}_{v})=\hat{\mu}_{v}
. That is, even though the constructions are performed conditionally on
\{\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in A_{j}\}
, the marginal law of
X^{i}_{v}
is preserved. However, note that this does not apply to coupled variable
\bar{X}_{v}^{i}
; that is, marginally
\mathcal{L}(\bar{X}^{i}_{v})\neq\mu_{v}
since in general
\Prob(\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in A_{j})\neq\mu_{v}(A_{j})
. Instead we have

\mathcal{L}(\bar{X}^{i}_{v})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mu_{v\mid A_{j}}\Prob(\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in A_{j})\neq\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mu_{v\mid A_{j}}\mu_{v}(A_{j})=\mu_{v}.

(24)

Note also that by construction,
\Prob(X^{i}_{v}=\bar{X}^{i}_{v}\mid\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in A_{j})=\|\hat{\mu}_{v\mid A_{j}}(\cdot)-\mu_{v\mid A_{j}}(\cdot)\|_{\text{TV}}
.

The next lemma shows that the random variables produced by
C
satisfy certain useful conditional independencies.

Lemma 8
.

Let
[-i]=\{1,\ldots,i-1,i+1,\ldots,N\}
. For
i=1,\ldots,N
we have:

(a)

\bar{X}^{i}_{v}\ind\tilde{X}^{[-i]}_{v}\mid\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}

(b)

\bar{X}^{i}_{v}\ind\bar{X}^{[-i]}_{v}\mid\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}

(c)

\bar{X}^{i}_{v}\ind(X^{1:N}_{k},\tilde{X}^{1:N}_{k})^{v-1}_{k=1}\mid\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}

Let
W^{i}_{v}=(Z^{i}_{v\mid\xi^{i}_{v}},\,U^{i}_{v\mid\xi^{i}_{v}},\,V^{i}_{v\mid\xi^{i}_{v}})
. All three statements follow immediately from the fact that

\displaystyle W^{i}_{v}\,\ind\,W^{[-i]}_{v}\mid\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}

for
i=1,\ldots,N
. That is,
Z^{i}_{v\mid\xi^{i}_{v}}
,
U^{i}_{v\mid\xi^{i}_{v}}
, and
V^{i}_{v\mid\xi^{i}_{v}}
are, by construction, drawn independently of
((W^{1:N}_{k},\tilde{X}^{1:N}_{k})^{v-1}_{k=1},W^{[-i]}_{v},\tilde{X}^{[-i]}_{v})

conditional on
\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}
for each
i=1,\ldots,N
.
∎Since the constructions are performed conditional on the event
\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in A_{j}
, the
\bar{X}^{1:N}_{v}
random variables are conditionally independent but not marginally independent; this is because the
\tilde{X}^{1:N}_{v}
random variables are dependent. Nonetheless, we can still refine Lemma

(c) slightly as follows:

Lemma 9
.

For
B\subset\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle\Prob(\bar{X}^{i}_{v}\in B\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-1},\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in A_{j})=\Prob(\bar{X}^{i}_{v}\in B\mid\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in A_{j})

\displaystyle\Prob(\bar{X}^{i}_{v}\in B\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-1},\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in A_{j})

\displaystyle=\E[\Prob(\bar{X}^{i}_{v}\in B\mid\tilde{X}^{i}_{v})\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-1},\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in A_{j}]

\displaystyle=\E[\mu_{v\mid A_{\xi^{i}_{v}}}(B)\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-1},\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in A_{j}]

\displaystyle=\mu_{v\mid A_{j}}(B)

\displaystyle=\Prob(\bar{X}^{i}_{v}\in B\mid\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}\in A_{j})

Lemma 10

Let

\displaystyle\E[f(\bar{X}^{i}_{v})\mathbbm{1}_{A_{j}}(\bar{X}^{i}_{v})\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-1}]=\E_{\mu_{v|A_{j}}}[f]\cdot\hat{P}^{j}_{v}

In particular,

\displaystyle\E[\bar{w}^{j}_{v+1}|\mathcal{F}_{v-1}]=\frac{z_{v+1}}{z_{v}}\cdot\frac{\mu_{v+1}(A_{j})}{\mu_{v}(A_{j})}\cdot\hat{P}^{j}_{v}

Lemma 11
.

Let
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow(a,b)
be a
\Prob
-measurable bounded function
and
\bar{f}=N^{-1}\sum^{N}_{i=1}f(\bar{X}^{i}_{v})
. Then for
\lambda>0
,

\Prob\left(\left|\bar{f}-\E[\bar{f}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-1}]\right|>\lambda\right)\leq 4e^{-\frac{N\lambda^{2}}{2(b-a)^{2}}}

Note that
\E[\bar{f}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-1}]=\E[\E[\bar{f}\mid\tilde{X}^{1:N}_{v}]\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-1}]
by Lemma
8
. Applying the triangle inequality and the union bound gives

\displaystyle\Prob\left(\left|\bar{f}-\E[\bar{f}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-1}]\right|>\lambda\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Prob\left(\left|\bar{f}-\E[\bar{f}\mid\tilde{X}^{1:N}_{v}]\right|>\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)+\Prob\left(\left|\E[\bar{f}\mid\tilde{X}^{1:N}_{v}]-\E[\E[\bar{f}\mid\tilde{X}^{1:N}_{v}]\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-1}]\right|>\frac{\lambda}{2}\right),

Notice that

\displaystyle\E[\bar{f}\mid\tilde{X}^{1:N}_{v}]=\E\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}f(\bar{X}^{i}_{v})\Bigm|\tilde{X}^{1:N}_{v}\right]=\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}\E[f(\bar{X}^{i}_{v})\mid\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}]

(27)

where the second equality follows by Lemma
8
. Since
\bar{X}^{1}_{v}\ind\ldots\ind\bar{X}^{N}_{v}\mid\tilde{X}^{1:N}_{v}
by Lemma
8
, the first term can be controlled by the conditional form of Hoeffding’s inequality:

\displaystyle\Prob\left(\left|\bar{f}-\E[\bar{f}\mid\tilde{X}^{1:N}_{v}]\right|>\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\E\left[\Prob\left(\left|\bar{f}-\E[\bar{f}\mid\tilde{X}^{1:N}_{v}]\right|>\frac{\lambda}{2}\Bigm|\tilde{X}^{1:N}_{v}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\Prob\left(\left|\sum^{N}_{i=1}\left(f(\bar{X}^{i}_{v})-\E[f(\bar{X}^{i}_{v})\mid\tilde{X}^{1:N}_{v}]\right)\right|>\frac{N\lambda}{2}\Bigm|\tilde{X}^{1:N}_{v}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{-\frac{N\lambda^{2}}{2(b-a)^{2}}}

Next, note that the random variable

\E[f(\bar{X}^{i}_{v})\mid\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}]
is also a bounded function of
\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}
. But we always have that
\tilde{X}^{1}_{v}\ind\ldots\ind\tilde{X}^{N}_{v}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-1}
, so we can reapply an identical argument using the conditional version of Hoeffding’s inequality:

\displaystyle\Prob\left(\left|\sum^{N}_{i=1}\left(\E[f(\bar{X}^{i}_{v})\mid\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}]-\E[\,\E[f(\bar{X}^{i}_{v})\mid\tilde{X}^{i}_{v}]\mid\mathcal{F}_{v-1}]\right)\right|>\frac{N\lambda}{2}\right)\leq 2e^{-\frac{N\lambda^{2}}{2(b-a)^{2}}}

It follows that

\displaystyle\Prob\left(\left|\bar{f}-\E[\bar{f}|\mathcal{F}_{v-1}]\right|>\lambda\right)\leq 4e^{-\frac{N\lambda^{2}}{2(b-a)^{2}}}

∎
