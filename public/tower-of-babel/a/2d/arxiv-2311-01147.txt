Variational Inference for Sparse Poisson Regression
Mitra Kharabati, Morteza Amini, Mohammad Arashi

Abstract
We have utilized the non-conjugate Variational Bayesian (VB) method for the problem of sparse Poisson regression model. To provide an approximate conjugacy in the model, the likelihood is approximated by a quadratic function, which provides the conjugacy of the approximation component with the Gaussian prior on the regression coefficient. Three sparsity-enforcing priors are used for this problem. The proposed models are compared with each other and two frequentist sparse Poisson methods (LASSO and SCAD) to evaluate the estimation, prediction, and sparsity performance of the proposed methods. Throughout a simulated data example, the accuracy of the VB methods is computed compared to the corresponding benchmark MCMC methods. It can be observed that the proposed VB methods have provided a good approximation to the posterior distribution of the parameters, while the VB methods are much faster than the MCMC ones. Using several benchmark count response data sets, the prediction performance of the proposed methods is evaluated in real-world applications.

Chen et al. 2010

The method used in
Luts and Wand 2015
was based on the non-conjugate VB technique. In this method, an assumed density filtering technique is used along with the mean-field VB for approximation of the posterior density. Precisely speaking, since the approximated component for the coefficients of the regression model is not conjugate with its Gaussian prior, we assume a Gaussian component and optimize the mean and the covariance function by minimizing the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the unnormalized posterior and its approximation. Another method in non-conjugate VB is the approximation method proposed by
Jaakkola and Jordan 2000

In this paper, we have used the VB method for the problem of the sparse Poisson regression model. A similar approximation method to that of
Jaakkola and Jordan 2000
is used to provide conjugacy in the model approximation. Three sparsity-enforcing priors are used for this problem, and the VB approximated formulas are calculated for all three models. The proposed models are compared with each other and two frequentist sparse Poisson methods (LASSO and SCAD) to evaluate the estimation, prediction, and the sparsity performance of the proposed methods. Throughout a simulated data example, the accuracy of the VB methods is computed compared to the corresponding benchmark MCMC methods. It can be observed that the proposed VB methods have provided a good approximation to the posterior distribution of the parameters, while the VB methods are much faster than the MCMC ones. Using several benchmark count response data sets, the prediction performance of the proposed methods is evaluated in real-world applications.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, the mean-field variational Bayes method is introduced. Three variational Bayesian sparse Poisson regression models are then proposed in Section 3. The derivation of the components as well as the evidence lower bound (ELBO) for these methods is given in Appendix A. In Section 4, the posterior predictive density function is approximated for the prediction of the response variable using new samples of the covariates. Section 5 presents a simulation study for evaluating the estimation, prediction, and sparsity performance of the three proposed methods and comparing them with two frequentist sparse Poisson regression methods (LASSO and SCAD Poisson regression models). A single replication of the simulation study is also used for computation of the accuracy of each approximation component compared to its corresponding benchmark MCMC method, and also a visual comparison of the MCMC empirical posteriors with their approximated counterparts. Some of these plots are given in Appendix B for the sake of brevity. Finally, in Section 6, the test set prediction performance of the proposed methods is compared with those of the frequentist alternatives, using 6 benchmark count response data sets. Some concluding remarks are given in Section 7.

2
Mean field variational Bayes

Let
x
be a vector of observed data, and
\theta
be a parameter vector with joint distribution
p(x,\theta)
.
In the Bayesian inference framework, the inference about
\theta
is done based on the posterior distribution

p(\theta|x)=p(x,\theta)/p(x)

where

p(x)=\int p(x,\theta)d\theta
.

Variational Bayes (VB) is a method for finding an approximate distribution
q(\theta)
of the posterior distribution
p(\theta|x)
, by minimizing the Kullback-Leibler divergence
{\rm KL}\left[q(\theta)||p(\theta|x)\right]
as a measure of closeness
(
Tran et al. 2023
)
. In the mean-field VB, we assume that the parameter vector
\theta
is divided into
M
partitions

\{\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{M}\}
, and we want to approximate

p(\theta|x)

by

q(\theta)=\prod_{j=1}^{M}q(\theta_{j}).

The best VB approximation

q^{\ast}(\cdot)

is then obtained as

q^{\ast}=\arg\min_{q}{\rm KL}\left[q(\theta)||p(\theta|x)\right],

where

{\rm KL}\left[q(\theta)||p(\theta|x)\right]=\int q(\theta)\log\frac{q(\theta)}{p(\theta|x)}d\theta=\log p(x)-\int q(\theta)\log\frac{p(x,\theta)}{q(\theta)}d\theta,

(1)

which results in

\log q(\theta_{j})=E_{(-\theta_{j})}\left[\log{p}(x,\theta)\right]{+{\rm Const.}},j=1,\ldots,M,

(2)

in which the expectation
E_{(-\theta_{j})}
is the expectation with respect to

q(-\theta_{j})=\prod_{i(\neq j)=1}^{M}q(\theta_{i}).

It is clear from (
1
) that minimizing KL is equivalent to maximizing the evidence lower bound (ELBO), defined as

{\rm ELBO}=\int q(\theta)\log\frac{p(x,\theta)}{q(\theta)}d\theta

ELBO is equal to
\log p(x)
when the KL divergence is zero, which means a perfect fit.
When the fit is not perfect,
{\rm ELBO}[q(\theta)]<\log p(x)
.

Variational Bayesian sparse Poisson regression models

In the following subsections, we consider three different sparsity-enforcing priors for the coefficients of the Poisson regression model. Then, we apply an approximation method similar to that of
Jaakkola and Jordan 2000
to the likelihood function, to provide approximated conjugacy for the regression coefficient vector. Finally, we propose the VB components for the parameters of each model. The computation process of these components is given in Appendix A.

3.1
Laplace prior

Suppose that
y_{i}\mathop{\sim}\limits^{\mathrm{ind}}\text{Poiss}(\lambda_{i}=\exp(\beta_{0}+X_{i}\beta)),\quad i=1,\ldots,n

X_{i}=(X_{i1},\ldots,X_{ip})

\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p})^{\top}

\beta_{j}\mathop{\sim}\limits^{\mathrm{ind}}\;\text{Laplace}(0,\lambda),\;j=1,\ldots,p

\beta_{j}|\tau_{j}\mathop{\sim}\limits^{\mathrm{ind}}\;\mathcal{N}(0,\tau_{j}),\quad\tau_{j}\mathop{\sim}\limits^{\mathrm{iid}}\;\text{exp}\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\right),\quad j=1,\ldots,p.

Adding the intercept priors and the hyper-priors, the Bayesian sparse Poisson regression model considered here is as follows

\displaystyle y_{i}|\beta_{0},\beta\mathop{\sim}\limits^{\mathrm{ind}}

\displaystyle\;\text{Poiss}(\lambda_{i}=\exp(\beta_{0}+X_{i}\beta)),\quad i=1,\ldots,n

\displaystyle\;\mathcal{N}(0,\tau_{j}),\quad\tau_{j}|\lambda\mathop{\sim}\limits^{\mathrm{iid}}\;\text{exp}\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\right),\quad j=1,\ldots,p,

\displaystyle\;\mathcal{N}(0,\tau_{0}),\;\;\;\;\lambda\sim\text{Gamma}(\nu,\delta)

\displaystyle\text{Inv-Gamma}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{a}\right),\;\;a\sim\text{Inv-Gamma}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{A}\right)

3.2
Continuous spike and slab prior

One of the most famous sparsity-enforcing priors is the spike and slab prior
(
Mitchell and Beauchamp 1988
;
Geweke, J. 1996
;
George and McCulloch 1997
)
, which is a mixture of a point mass
\delta(\cdot)
and a continuous prior, which is usually considered to be Gaussian. Let
Z=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{p})^{\top}
be a vector of the latent binary variables. Then, the spike and slab prior for the vector of regression coefficient
\beta
is defined as

p(\beta)=\prod_{j=1}^{p}\left[Z_{j}\mathcal{N}(\beta_{j};0,\sigma^{2})+(1-Z_{j})\delta(\beta_{j})\right],

(8)

where
\delta(\beta_{j})=1,
if
\beta_{j}=0
and
=0
, otherwise.

Continuous relaxations of (
9
), with
\delta(\cdot)
replaced by a peaked
continuous density, are considered by many authors
(see e.g.,
George and McCulloch 1993
;
Ishwaran and Rao 2003
;
Ishwaran and Rao 2005
;
Ročková 2018
, among others)
. One of the most famous variations of the continuous spike and slab prior is as follows

p(\beta)=\prod_{j=1}^{p}\left[Z_{j}\mathcal{N}(\beta_{j};0,\sigma^{2})+(1-Z_{j})\mathcal{N}(\beta_{j};0,c\sigma^{2})\right],

(9)

in which the constant
c
is small enough to enforce sparsity to
\beta_{j}
. Here, we take
c=0.001
, by examination of various small values during cross-validation.

Thus, the model is assumed to be as follows

\displaystyle y_{i}|\beta\mathop{\sim}\limits^{\mathrm{ind}}

\displaystyle\;\text{Poiss}(\lambda_{i}=\exp(X_{i}\beta)),\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

\displaystyle X_{i}=

\displaystyle\;(1,X_{i1},\ldots,X_{ip}),\;\beta=(\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p})^{\top}

\displaystyle p(\beta|Z,\sigma^{2})=

\displaystyle\;\prod_{j=0}^{p}\left[Z_{j}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})+(1-Z_{j})\mathcal{N}(0,c\sigma^{2})\right],

Bernoulli sparsity-enforcing prior

To enforce sparsity in the regression coefficient vector
\beta

Zhang et al. 2019
used the product of a diagonal matrix
\Gamma
and the vector
\beta
, in which the diagonal elements of
\Gamma
are Bernoulli distributed. When the
j
th diagonal element of
\Gamma

\beta_{j}
is removed from the linear regression function.
Zhang et al. 2019
used this prior for the logistic regression model.

Thus, the Bayesian sparse Poisson model with a Bernoulli prior is as follows

\displaystyle y_{i}|\beta\mathop{\sim}\limits^{\mathrm{ind}}

\displaystyle\text{Poiss}(\lambda_{i}=\exp(X_{i}\Gamma\beta)),\quad i=1,\ldots,n

\displaystyle X_{i}=

\displaystyle(1,X_{i1},\ldots,X_{ip}),\quad\Gamma=\text{diag}(1,\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{p}),\;\beta=(\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p})^{\top}

\displaystyle\beta|A\sim

\displaystyle\;\mathcal{N}_{p+1}(0,A^{-1})

\displaystyle A=

\displaystyle\;\text{diag}(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{p}),\;\;\alpha_{j}\mathop{\sim}\limits^{\mathrm{ind}}\text{Gamma}(a_{j},b_{j}),\;j=0,\ldots,p

\displaystyle\gamma_{j}|\pi_{j}\mathop{\sim}\limits^{\mathrm{ind}}

\displaystyle\text{Ber}(\pi_{j}),\;\;\pi_{j}\mathop{\sim}\limits^{\mathrm{iid}}\text{Beta}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\quad j=1,\ldots,p,

4
Sparsity-enforcing thresholds

Due to the structure of the Laplace-VB and CS-VB methods, the estimators of the coefficients (here
\mu_{\beta}
) are not sparse estimators. To obtain a sparse estimator, we propose sparsity-enforcing thresholds.

For the Bernoulli-VB method, the values of
P_{j}
s are almost always obtained to be equal to 0 or 1. If this does not happen, the
j
th variable is selected (i.e.
\widehat{\beta}_{j}^{\rm Bernoulli-VB}\neq 0
) if
P_{j}>0.5
.

For the Laplace-VB method, we mimic the strategy for the LASSO estimator proposed by
Tibshirani 1996
, and we let

\widehat{\beta}_{j}^{\rm Laplace-VB}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\mu_{\beta,j},&|\mu_{\beta,j}|>\hat{\gamma},\\
0,&{\rm o.w.}\end{array}\right.

where

\hat{\gamma}=\arg\min_{\gamma\geq 0}[\hat{R}(\gamma)].

in which the risk estimator
\hat{R}(\gamma)
is

\hat{R}(\gamma)=\hat{\tau}^{2}\left\{p-2\#\{j;|\mu_{\beta,j}|/\sqrt{\Sigma_{\beta,j,j}}<\gamma\}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\max(|\mu_{\beta,j}|/\sqrt{\Sigma_{\beta,j,j}},\,\gamma)^{2}\right\}

and

\hat{\tau}^{2}={\rm tr}(\Sigma_{\beta})/p.

For the Laplace-VB, we mimic the strategy proposed by
Ročková 2018
, as follows. Note that the Laplace model can be viewed as a penalized likelihood with a penalty

\sum_{j=1}^{p}\log p(\beta_{j}|\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}{\rm Pen}(\beta_{j})

where

{\rm Pen}(\beta_{j})=\log\left[\frac{\theta_{1}}{(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})}{\cal N}(\beta_{j},0,\sigma^{2}))+\frac{\theta_{2}}{(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})}{\cal N}(\beta_{j},0,c_{0}\sigma^{2}))\right]

The derivative of
{\rm Pen}(\beta_{j})
is

\frac{\partial{\rm Pen}(\beta_{j})}{\partial\beta_{j}}=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}P^{*}(\beta_{j})-\frac{1}{2c_{0}\sigma^{2}}(1-P^{*}(\beta_{j}))

where

\displaystyle P^{*}(\beta_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta_{1}{\cal N}(\beta_{j},0,\sigma^{2}))}{\theta_{1}{\cal N}(\beta_{j},0,\sigma^{2}))+\theta_{2}{\cal N}(\beta_{j},0,c_{0}\sigma^{2}))}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\frac{\theta_{2}}{\theta_{1}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{0}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{{c_{0}}}-1\right)\right\}}

which can be viewed as the conditional probability of inclusion given
\beta_{j}
, that is

P(Z_{j}=1|\beta_{j},\theta_{1},\theta_{2}).

The intersection point between spike and slab densities occurs when
P^{*}(\beta_{j})=0.5,
which occurs at points

\beta_{j}^{2}=\delta(c_{0},\eta)
, where

\displaystyle\delta(c_{0},\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{-2c_{0}}{1+c_{0}}\sigma^{2}\log\left(\frac{\theta_{1}}{\theta_{2}}\sqrt{c_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{-2c_{0}}{1+c_{0}}\sigma^{2}\log\left(\frac{P^{*}(0)}{1-P^{*}(0)}\right),

for
\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}/\sqrt{c_{0}}
.
Thus, by mimicking the strategy proposed by
Ročková 2018
, we let

\widehat{\beta}_{j}^{\rm CS-VB}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\mu_{\beta,j},&\mu_{\beta,j}^{2}>\delta^{*}(c_{0},\theta),\\
0,&{\rm o.w.}\end{array}\right.,

where

\delta^{*}(c_{0},\theta)=\frac{-2c_{0}}{1+c_{0}}E_{q(\sigma^{2})}(\sigma^{2})\log\left(\frac{\theta_{1}}{\theta_{2}}\sqrt{c_{0}}\right).

5
Posterior predictive mass function

The posterior predictive mass function (ppmf) of the response variable associated with the covariates of a new sample
x_{0}
given the training data set
(X,y)
is obtained as follows

\displaystyle p(y_{0}|x_{0},X,y)

\displaystyle=\int p(y_{0}|x_{0},\theta)p(\theta|X,y)d\theta

\displaystyle\approx\int{p}(y_{0}|x_{0},\theta)q(\theta)d\theta,

where
\theta=\beta
for Laplace-VB and CS-VB methods, and
\theta=(\gamma,\beta)^{\top}
for Bernoulli-VB method.

For Laplace-VB and CS-VB methods, we have

p(y_{0}|x_{0},X,y)\approx\frac{1}{y_{0}!}\int\exp\{-\exp\{x_{0}\beta\}\}\exp\{y_{0}x_{0}\beta\}{\cal N}(\beta;\mu_{\beta},\Sigma_{\beta})\;d\beta

Using re-paramerization
\lambda_{0}=\exp\{x_{0}\beta\}
, we see that

\displaystyle p(y_{0}|x_{0},X,y)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{y_{0}!}\int e^{-\lambda_{0}}\lambda_{0}^{y_{0}}{\cal LN}(\lambda_{0};x_{0}\mu_{\beta},x_{0}\Sigma_{\beta}x_{0}^{\top})\;d\lambda_{0}.

(18)

The predictive probability mass function in (
18
) can be obtained using a univariate numerical integration, and might be used for obtaining a point predictor (e.g., most likely predictor) and a prediction interval (e.g., the most likely prediction interval) for
y_{0}
given
x_{0}
and
(X,y)
.

For Bernoulli-VB method, we have

p(y_{0}|x_{0},X,y)\approx\prod_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{\gamma_{j}=0}^{1}{\rm Ber}(\gamma_{j};P_{j})\frac{1}{y_{0}!}\int\exp\{-\exp\{x_{0}\Gamma\beta\}\}\exp\{y_{0}x_{0}\Gamma\beta\}{\cal N}(\beta;\mu_{\beta},\Sigma_{\beta})\;d\beta

Using re-paramerization
\lambda_{0\gamma}=\exp\{x_{0}\Gamma\beta\}
, we see that

\displaystyle p(y_{0}|x_{0},X,y)

\displaystyle\approx\prod_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{\gamma_{j}=0}^{1}{\rm Ber}(\gamma_{j};P_{j})\frac{1}{y_{0}!}\int e^{-\lambda_{0\gamma}}\lambda_{0\gamma}^{y_{0}}{\cal LN}(\lambda_{0\gamma};x_{0}\Gamma\mu_{\beta},x_{0}\Gamma\Sigma_{\beta}\Gamma x_{0}^{\top})\;d\lambda_{0\gamma}.

(19)

Since, for the Bernoulli-VB method, after using sparsity-enforcing thresholds, the parameters
P_{j}
take only 0 and 1 values, the formula (
19
) can be simply written as

\displaystyle p(y_{0}|x_{0},X,y)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{y_{0}!}\int e^{-\lambda_{0}}\lambda_{0}^{y_{0}}{\cal LN}(\lambda_{0};x_{0}P\mu_{\beta},x_{0}P\Sigma_{\beta}Px_{0}^{\top})\;d\lambda_{0}.

(20)

6
Simulation study

In order to examine the performance of the proposed models, we have conducted a simulation study as follows. In each of
N=1000
replications, we have generated samples of size
n
, from a sparse Poisson regression model, whose vector of coefficients
\beta_{p\times 1}
, with
p
, is first generated from
N(1,1)
and then we generate a Bernoulli vector of length
p
, with success probability
\pi_{0}
, and multiply it by
\beta
, to set some randomly chosen elements of
\beta
to zero. The rows of the covariate matrix
X
are generated independently from a multivariate normal distribution with zero mean and a variance-covariance matrix with elements
\Sigma_{ij}=0.3^{|i-j|}
, to model a slight multicollinearity. The values of
n
,
p
, and
\pi_{0}
are determined in two low- and high-dimensional scenarios, in the following subsections.

Three proposed VB models (Laplace, CS, Bernoulli) are fitted to each generated dataset. Furthermore, two frequentist alternatives, LASSO and SCAD-penalized Poisson regression models, are considered for comparison. The sparsity parameters of both LASSO and SCAD models are optimized based on the Corrected Akaike Information Criterion

{\rm AICc}=-\log\widehat{L}(\beta|x)+2{\rm df}+2{\rm df}({\rm df}+1)/(n-{\rm df}-1),

where
\widehat{L}(\beta|x)
is the estimated likelihood of the model and
{\rm df}
is the degrees of freedom of the model, that is the number of nonzero coefficients in the regression model.

To compare the performance of the 3 proposed models with each other and with LASSO and SCAD-penalized Poisson regression models, the regression coefficient relative error is computed as follows

{\rm CRE}=\frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta)^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta)}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\beta^{\top}\beta}.

Furthermore, the train-set and test-set relative errors are obtained, respectively, as

{\rm TRRE}=\frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}(\widehat{y}_{\rm train}-{y}_{\rm train})^{\top}(\widehat{y}_{\rm train}-{y}_{\rm train})}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}{y}_{\rm train}^{\top}{y}_{\rm train}},

and

{\rm TSRE}=\frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}(\widehat{y}_{\rm test}-{y}_{\rm test})^{\top}(\widehat{y}_{\rm test}-{y}_{\rm test})}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}{y}_{\rm test}^{\top}{y}_{\rm test}},

where
{y}_{\rm train}=({y}_{1},\ldots,{y}_{n})^{\top}
and
{y}_{\rm test}=({y}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,{y}^{\prime}_{n^{\prime}})^{\top}
, are the train-set (80% of the full sample) and test-set (20 % of the full sample) response samples, respectively.

The false negative rate and the false positive rate are also defined as follows

{\rm FNR}=\frac{\#\{j;\;1\leq j\leq p,\;\beta_{j}\neq 0,\;\hat{\beta}_{j}=0\}}{\#\{j;\;1\leq j\leq p,\;\beta_{j}\neq 0\}},

{\rm FPR}=\frac{\#\{j;\;1\leq j\leq p\;\beta_{j}=0,\;\hat{\beta}_{j}\neq 0\}}{\#\{j;\;1\leq j\leq p,\;\beta_{j}=0\}},

where
\#A
stands for the cardinality of the set
A
. These criteria are used to examine the sparsity performance of the competitive methods.

The computation time of 3 proposed models, as well as the LASSO and SCAD-penalized Poisson regression models, is also computed in each iteration.

For the Laplace-VB method, we set the hyper-priors
\nu=0.0001
, and
\delta=0.01
, to obtain a mean of 0.01 and variance of 1 for the parameter
\lambda
, which controls the sparsity of the coefficients. The hyper-parameters
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
for the CS-VB and Bernoulli-VB methods are set to 1 and
\frac{1-p_{0}}{p_{0}}
to obtain a mean
p_{0}
for the parameters
\pi_{j}
, where
p_{0}
is predetermined (or estimated) value of the percentage of non-sparse coefficients. Here, we estimate
p_{0}
using the optimal LASSO method. Also, all
a_{j}
and
b_{j}
, hyperparameters for the Bernoulli-VB method, are set to 0.01. The hyperparameter
A
is set to 0.01 for the Laplace-VB and CS-VB methods.

We consider two scenarios for the simulation study. In the low dimensional scenario, we let
p=5
,
n=100
and
\pi_{0}=0.3
and we consider
p=200
,
n=30
and
\pi_{0}=0.1
for the high dimensional scenario.

9
Concluding remarks

The VB methods proposed in this paper provide a fast Bayesian inference about the parameters of the sparse Poisson regression model. These methods are significantly faster than the MCMC methods. The proposed VB methods are used for simultaneous estimation and variable selection in the Poisson regression model. It is observed that the proposed Bayesian inference performs better than the two well-known frequentist methods, LASSO and SCAD penalized Poisson regression models, in general, while there are some advantages of the Bayesian inference, compared to the frequentist ones, including the assessment of the highest posterior density intervals, posterior distributions of the parameters, and the predictive probability mass function of the response, given observations. It is observed that the proposed VB methods provide a good approximation of the marginal posterior densities. A limitation of the proposed models is their sensitivity to the assumption of the Poisson distribution of the response, which fails when there is over-dispersion and under-dispersion in the response observations. This issue is considered by the authors in their future research. A guideline for choosing the prior is given in Appendix B, based on the results of the simulation study. The codes and data for this paper are available online on GitHub at
https://github.com/mortamini/VBSparsePoisson
.

Here, we propose the details of the derivation of the VB components, as well as, the computation of the ELBO, for the three proposed VB models.

Laplace prior

For model (
3
), and using (
2
), we have

\displaystyle\log q(\beta)=

\displaystyle E_{-\beta}\left[\log\tilde{p}(y|\beta,\xi)+\log p(\beta|\tau)\right]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{-\beta}\left[-\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{\xi_{i}}X_{i}\beta+\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}X_{i}\beta\right.

\displaystyle-\left.\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\xi_{i}}\beta^{T}X_{i}X_{i}^{T}\beta-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{T}\text{diag}(\tau_{j}^{-1})\beta\right]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(y-M_{\xi})^{T}X\beta-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{T}\left[S_{X}^{\xi}+\text{diag}(E(\tau^{-1}))\right]\beta+\text{Const.,}

which is the kernel of the Gaussian distribution. Also, we have

\displaystyle\log q(\lambda)=

\displaystyle E_{-\lambda}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{p}\log p(\tau_{j}|\lambda)+\log p(\lambda)\right]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{-\lambda}\left[p\log\lambda-\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\tau_{j}+(\nu-1)\log\lambda-\lambda\delta\right]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(p+\nu-1)\log\lambda-\lambda(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}E(\tau_{j})+\delta)+\text{Const.},

which is the kernel of the gamma distribution. For the variance parameters
\tau_{j}
, for
j=1,\ldots,p
, we can see that

\displaystyle\log q(\tau_{j})=

\displaystyle E_{-\tau_{j}}\left[\log p(\beta_{j}|\tau_{j})+\log p(\tau_{j}|\lambda)\right]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{-\tau_{j}}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\log(\tau_{j})-\frac{1}{2}\dfrac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{\tau_{j}}-\frac{\lambda}{2}\tau_{j}\right]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\log(\tau_{j})-\frac{1}{2}\dfrac{E[\beta_{j}^{2}]}{\tau_{j}}-\frac{E[\lambda]}{2}\tau_{j}+\text{Const.},

which is the logarithm of the generalized inverse Gaussian density. For
\tau_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\log q(\tau_{0})=

\displaystyle E_{-\tau_{0}}\left[\log p(\beta_{0}|\tau_{0})+\log p(\tau_{0}|a)\right]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{-\tau_{0}}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\log\tau_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\dfrac{\beta_{0}^{2}}{\tau_{0}}-\frac{3}{2}\log\tau_{0}-\dfrac{1}{a\tau_{0}}\right]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\log\tau_{0}-\frac{1}{\tau_{0}}\left[\frac{1}{2}E(\beta_{0}^{2})+E(a^{-1})\right]+\text{Const.},

Finally, we can see that

\displaystyle\log q(a)=

\displaystyle E_{-a}\left[\log p(\tau_{0}|a)+\log p(a)\right]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{-a}\left[-\dfrac{1}{2}\log a-\dfrac{1}{a\tau_{0}}-\dfrac{3}{2}\log a-\dfrac{1}{Aa}\right]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\log a-\dfrac{1}{a}(E(\tau_{0}^{-1})+\dfrac{1}{A})+\text{Const.},

which is the kernel of the inverse gamma distribution.

To obtain the ELBO, we compute the following terms

\displaystyle E_{q}(\log\tilde{p}(y|\beta,\xi))=

\displaystyle-M_{\xi}^{T}(1+X\mu_{\beta})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\xi_{i}^{2}}e^{\xi_{i}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}{\rm tr}(S_{X}^{\xi}D_{\beta})+y^{T}X\mu_{\beta}+\text{Const.},

where
D_{\beta}=E(\beta\beta^{T})=\Sigma_{\beta}+\mu_{\beta}\mu_{\beta}^{T},

\displaystyle E_{q}(\log p(\beta|\tau))=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}E(\log\tau_{0})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}E(\log\tau_{j})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}E(\tau_{0}^{-1})E(\beta_{0}^{2})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}E(\tau_{j}^{-1})E(\beta_{j}^{2})+\text{Const.},

\displaystyle E_{q}(\log p(\lambda))=

\displaystyle(\nu-1)E(\log\lambda)-\delta E(\lambda)+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\nu-1)\left[\psi(p+\nu)-\log(\delta+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}E(\tau_{j}))\right]

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\dfrac{\delta(p+\nu)}{\delta+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}E(\tau_{j})}+\text{Const.},

\displaystyle E_{q}(\log p(\tau_{0}|a))=

\displaystyle E(-\frac{1}{2}\log a-\frac{3}{2}\log\tau_{0}-\frac{1}{a\tau_{0}})+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\log(\frac{1}{A}+E(\tau_{0}^{-1}))-\frac{3}{2}\log(\frac{1}{2}d_{00}+E(a^{-1}))

\displaystyle-E(a^{-1})E(\tau_{0}^{-1})+\text{Const.},

\displaystyle E_{q}(\log p(\tau|\lambda))=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}E\left[\log\lambda-\frac{1}{2}\lambda\tau_{j}\right]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-p\log(\delta+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}E(\tau_{j}))-\frac{1}{2}E(\lambda)\sum_{j=1}^{p}E(\tau_{j})+\text{Const.},

\displaystyle E_{q}(\log p(a))=

\displaystyle E_{q}(-\frac{3}{2}\log a-\dfrac{1}{Aa})+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{2}\log\big(\frac{1}{A}+E(\tau_{0}^{-1})\big)-\dfrac{\frac{1}{A}}{\frac{1}{A}+E(\tau_{0}^{-1})}+\text{Const.},

\displaystyle-E_{q}(\log q(\beta))=\frac{1}{2}\log|\Sigma_{\beta}|+\text{Const.},

\displaystyle-E_{q}(\log q(\lambda))=

\displaystyle(p+\nu-1)\log(\delta+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}E(\tau_{j}))+\text{Const.},

\displaystyle-E_{q}(\log q(\tau_{0}))=

\displaystyle\;2E(\log(\tau_{0}))+E(\tau_{0}^{-1})\big(\frac{1}{2}d_{00}+E(a^{-1})\big)

\displaystyle-\log(\frac{1}{2}d_{00}+E(a^{-1}))+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log(\frac{1}{2}d_{00}+E(a^{-1}))+\text{Const.},

\displaystyle-E_{q}(\log q(\tau))=

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}E(\log\tau_{j})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}E(\tau_{j}^{-1})d_{jj}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}E(\lambda)E(\tau_{j})-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{p}E(\lambda)/d_{jj}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\log K_{1/2}(\sqrt{E(\lambda)d_{jj}})+\text{Const.},

and

\displaystyle-E_{q}(\log q(a))=

\displaystyle\;2E(\log a)+E(a^{-1})\big(E(\tau_{0}^{-1})+\frac{1}{A}\big)+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;2\log\big(E(\tau_{0}^{-1})+\frac{1}{A}\big)-\psi(1)+E(a^{-1})\big(E(\tau_{0}^{-1})+\frac{1}{A}\big)

\displaystyle-\log(1/A+E(\tau_{0}^{-1}))+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\big(E(\tau_{0}^{-1})+A^{-1}\big)+\text{Const.}

Adding the above terms, we reach in (

For model (
10
), we can see that

\displaystyle\log q(\beta)=

\displaystyle E_{-\beta}\left[\log\tilde{p}(y|\beta,\xi)+\log p(\beta|Z,\sigma^{2})\right]+\text{Const.}

where

which is the kernel of a Bernoulli probability mass function,

\displaystyle\log q(\pi_{j})=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\Big[Z_{j}\log\pi_{j}+(1-Z_{j})(1-\pi_{j})+(\theta_{1}-1)\log\pi_{j}

which is the beta distribution.

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}P_{j}\big(E(\log\sigma^{2})+E(\sigma^{-2})d_{jj}\big)

For model (

\displaystyle E_{-\gamma_{j}}\left[\log\tilde{p}(y;\beta,\gamma,\xi)+\log p(\gamma_{j}|\pi_{j})\right]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{-\gamma_{j}}\Big[(y-M_{\xi})^{T}X\Gamma\beta-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{T}\Gamma S_{X}^{\xi}\Gamma\beta

\displaystyle+\gamma_{j}\log\big(\dfrac{\pi_{j}}{1-\pi_{j}}\big)\Big]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{-\gamma_{j}}\Big[(y-M_{\xi})^{T}X_{j}\gamma_{j}\beta_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{j}S_{X_{jj}}^{\xi}\beta_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}S_{X_{ij}}^{\xi}\beta_{i}\beta_{j}+\gamma_{j}\log\big(\dfrac{\pi_{j}}{1-\pi_{j}}\big)\Big]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{j}\Big[(y-M_{\xi})^{T}X_{j}\mu_{\beta j}-\frac{1}{2}S_{X_{jj}}^{\xi}d_{jj}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}P_{i}S_{X_{ij}}^{\xi}d_{ij}+E\big(\log\big(\dfrac{\pi_{j}}{1-\pi_{j}}\big)\big)\big]+\text{Const.},

which is the kernel of a Bernoulli distribution. Furthermore, for
j=0,1,\ldots,p
,

\displaystyle\log q(\alpha_{j})=

\displaystyle E_{-\alpha_{j}}\left[\log p(\beta|A)+\log p(\alpha_{j})\right]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a_{j}-1/2)\log\alpha_{j}-(b_{j}+\frac{d_{jj}}{2})\alpha_{j}+\text{Const.},

which corresponds with a gamma density.

\displaystyle\log q(\pi_{j})=

\displaystyle E_{-\pi_{j}}\left[\log p(\gamma_{j}|\pi_{j})+\log p(\pi_{j})\right]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(P_{j}+\theta_{1}-1)\log\pi_{j}+(\theta_{2}-P_{j})\log(1-\pi_{j})+\text{Const.},

which is the logarithm of a beta density.

The ELBO in (
17
) is computed by computation of the following terms:

\displaystyle E_{q}(\log\tilde{p}(y|\beta,\gamma,\xi))=

\displaystyle E_{q}\Big[-M_{\xi}^{T}X\Gamma\beta-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{T}\Gamma S_{X}^{\xi}\Gamma\beta

\displaystyle+y^{T}X\Gamma\beta-\sum_{i=1}^{n}(e^{\xi_{i}}+\xi_{i}-\frac{\xi_{i}^{2}}{2})\Big]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-M_{\xi}XP\mu_{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}[(\Sigma_{\beta}+\mu_{\beta}\mu_{\beta}^{T})(S_{X}^{\xi}\odot\Omega)]

\displaystyle+y^{T}XP\mu_{\beta}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(e^{\xi_{i}}+\xi_{i}-\frac{\xi_{i}^{2}}{2}\big)+\text{Const.},

\displaystyle E_{q}(\log p(\beta|A))=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}E_{q}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{p}\log\alpha_{j}-\beta^{T}A\beta\Big]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}E(\log\alpha_{j})-\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\big[(\Sigma_{\beta}+\mu_{\beta}\mu_{\beta}^{T})\text{diag}(E(\alpha))\big]\Big]+\text{Const.},

\displaystyle E_{q}(\log p(\alpha))=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}E_{q}\left[(a_{j}-1)\log\alpha_{j}-b_{j}\alpha_{j}\right]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}\big[(a_{j}-1)E(\log\alpha_{j})-b_{j}\frac{a_{j}+1/2}{b_{j}+\frac{d_{jj}}{2}}\big]+\text{Const.},

\displaystyle E_{q}(\log p(\gamma|\pi))=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}E_{q}\left[\gamma_{j}\log\pi_{j}+(1-\gamma_{j})\log(1-\pi_{j})\right]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left[P_{j}E(\log\pi_{j})+(1-P_{j})E(\log(1-\pi_{j}))\right]+\text{Const.},

\displaystyle E_{q}(\log p(\pi))=

\displaystyle(\theta_{1}-1)\sum_{j=1}^{p}E(\log\pi_{j})+(\theta_{2}-1)\sum_{j=1}^{p}E(\log(1-\pi_{j}))+\text{Const.},

Also,

\displaystyle-E_{q}(\log q(\beta))=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log|\Sigma_{\beta}|+\text{Const.},

\displaystyle-E_{q}(\log q(\gamma))=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{p}E_{q}\left[\gamma_{j}\log P_{j}+(1-\gamma_{j})\log(1-P_{j})\right]+\text{Const.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{p}[P_{j}\log P_{j}+(1-P_{j})\log(1-P_{j})]+\text{Const.},

\displaystyle-E_{q}(\log q(\pi))=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}[\log\Gamma(P_{j}+\theta_{1})+\log\Gamma(\theta_{2}-P_{j}+1)]

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{p}(P_{j}+\theta_{1}-1)E(\log\pi_{j})

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{p}(\theta_{2}-P_{j})E(\log(1-\pi_{j}))+\text{Const.},

and

\displaystyle-E_{q}(\log q(\alpha))=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{p}(a_{j}-1/2)E(\log\alpha_{j})-\sum_{j=1}^{p}(a_{j}+1/2)\log(b_{j}+d_{jj}/2)+\text{Const.}

Summing up, we obtain (
17
).
