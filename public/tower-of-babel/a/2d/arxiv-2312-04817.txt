MoVQA: A Benchmark of Versatile Question-Answering for Long-Form Movie Understanding
Hongjie Zhang Yi Liu Lu Dong Yifei Huang, Zhenhua Ling Yali Wang Limin Wang Yu Qiao, OpenGVLab, Shanghai AI Laboratory, Shenzhen Institutes of Advanced Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences

Abstract
While several long-form VideoQA datasets have been introduced, the length of both videos used to curate questions and sub-clips of clues leveraged to answer those questions have not yet reached the criteria for genuine long-form video understanding. Moreover, their QAs are unduly narrow and modality-biased, lacking a wider view of understanding long-term video content with rich dynamics and complex narratives. To remedy this, we introduce MoVQA, a long-form movie question-answering dataset, and benchmark to assess the diverse cognitive capabilities of multimodal systems rely on multi-level temporal lengths, with considering both video length and clue length. Additionally, to take a step towards human-level understanding in long-form video, versatile and multimodal question-answering is designed from the moviegoer-perspective to assess the model capabilities on various perceptual and cognitive axes. Analysis involving various baselines reveals a consistent trend: the performance of all methods significantly deteriorate with increasing video and clue length. Meanwhile, our established baseline method has shown some improvements, but there is still ample scope for enhancement on our challenging MoVQA dataset. We expect our MoVQA to provide a new perspective and encourage inspiring works on long-form video understanding research.

It is worth noting that, in what aspects does long-form VideoQA differ from general VideoQA in terms of “long”?
We argue that there are two indicators: clue length and video length.
The clue length, called as temporal certificate length in
Mangalam et al. 2023
, represents the total length of the minimum set of sub-clips of the video that are both necessary and sufficient to convince a human verifier that the annotated information for that data is indeed correct, without having to watch the rest of sub-clips outside of the clue set.
Undeniably, this indicator plays a crucial role in measuring the inherent temporal difficulty of long-form VideoQA task.
However, assuming two VideoQA tasks with same clue length, and without considering other factors, intuitively, answering the question within a longer video length is more challenging.
This is because a longer video length implies a longer contextual temporal relationship, resulting in more information redundancy and increasing the difficulty of capturing relevant clues.

In this work, considering both indicators, we introduce MoVQA, a new dataset and benchmark, contains 21,953 manually annotated QA pairs sourced from 100 movies with diverse genres, to assess the model capabilities on long-form video understanding with multi-level temporal lengths. As shown in Fig.
2
, QAs in our MoVQA come from three-level lengths: single-scene, multi-scene and full-scene.
Annotators are asked to manually segment the movie into multiple consecutive, non-overlapping single-scene intervals of varying length based on the storyline and visual presentation, with clear start and end timestamps.
A median clue length and video length of these single-scenes are about 200 seconds and 7.5 minutes.
For longer video understanding, adjacent and closely related single-scenes are merged to form multi-scenes with longer durations, with clue length and video length of about 320 seconds and 20 minutes, respectively.
Our MoVQA also includes specifically designed QAs for the full-scene, which have a clue length of about 540 seconds and a video length of about 120 minutes.
Compared to
Mangalam et al. 2023
, which has a median clue length of about 100 seconds and a video length of 3 minutes, our MoVQA exceeds it on both two indicators.
Additionally, the types of QAs in
Mangalam et al. 2023
are relatively narrow, primarily focusing on summarizing the overarching behavior of the video.

To take a step towards human-level long-video understanding, QAs in our MoVQA are designed from the perspective of the interconnected abilities required by moviegoers to understand movie content. They are grouped into six types: information synopsis, temporal perception, spatial perception, causal reasoning, hypothetical reasoning and external knowledge.
Compared to VideoQA dataset that also target movies
Tapaswi et al. 2016
, where QAs are solely collected based on text summaries, making them very plot-focused and less relevant for visual information. In contrast, ours are relatively more versatile and require integration of multimodal information for answering.
The primary objective of our MoVQA is to incentivize the development of multimodal systems that can tackle versatile QAs presented by moviegoers, spanning long periods of time and relying on a variety of modal information.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
(1) We focus on long-form with multi-level QAs by considering both clue length and video length, to support the research on video understanding across different temporal lengths.
(2) Versatile and multimodal question-answering is designed from the moviegoer-perspective to assess the model capabilities on various perceptual and cognitive axes.
(3) We benchmark various state-of-the-art multimodal systems on MoVQA and find that even the most advanced current systems achieve unsatisfactory results. Moreover, our established baseline with keyframe selection and contextual modelling achieves the state-of-the-art performance, which we hope can guide future directions in establishing long-form understanding methods.

The collected movies are publicly available from the internet, and are allowed to be crawled according to the service contract of the website.

Furthermore, our data source does not contain any information that names or uniquely identifiable individuals or offensive content.

Scene Partition.
To evaluate model capabilities on long-form video understanding across different temporal lengths, each movie was first segmented into clips of varying durations.
Our annotators were asked to first select a movie from the list that they were familiar with, and then manually segment the movie into multiple consecutive, non-overlapping single-scene intervals of varying length based on the storyline and visual presentation, with clear start and end timestamps. The average duration of single-scene is about
7.8 minutes.
For longer video understanding, adjacent and closely related single-scenes are merged to form multi-scenes with longer durations, typically spanning an average length of 22 minutes. Our MoVQA also specifically designed QAs with a super long-form for the full-scene (average duration of 125.7 minutes).

QA Annotation.
For each scene, the annotator had the freedom of forming 9 to 10 questions based on the 6 types, and these questions require both vision and language understanding to answer. The correct answer to each question is given by the annotator.
Another annotator was presented with a clip and a question at a time, but not provided with the correct answer, and asked to answer the question correctly. Finally, the two annotators jointly discussed and reorganized or corrected the QA pairs based on their answers.
The reason for inviting another annotator to answer the question is that assuming that the QA pairs are completed by the same annotator, biases in understanding from the annotator inevitably arise, leading to incorrect answers.

QA Types.
To assess model capabilities on various perceptual and cognitive axes in movie understanding, QAs in our MoVQA are designed from the perspective of the interconnected abilities required by moviegoers to understand the movie content. They are grouped into six types:

\bullet

Information Synopsis.
Information synopsis questions require the model to extract key information related to the question from the movie and summarize them concisely in language. This involve aspects such as the movie theme, plot synopsis, character relationships, and motivations.

\bullet

Temporal Perception.
Temporal perception questions require the model to
understand the timeline of events and the plot in the movie, and accurately locate the temporal location of the answer on the timeline.
Compared with the temporal action localization task
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Cheng and Bertasius 2022
, which requires the model to recognize and locate specific actions in a video, our temporal perception task places more emphasis on understanding the complex narrative sequence in the movie.

\bullet

Spatial Perception.
Spatial perception questions require the model to recognize and understand the visual elements in movies, which often have multiple characters, objects, and scenes. It may require combining the context of the story to infer the visual information related to the question.

\bullet

Causal Reasoning.
Causal reasoning questions require the model to understand and infer cause-and-effect relationships between events in the storyline, and causality is the key driving force in the development of the story.

\bullet

Hypothetical Reasoning.
Hypothetical reasoning questions require the model to understand the preconditions and post-effects of storylines. Based on causal tracing of storylines, we designed hypothetical reasoning questions with the assumption that certain events in the story are not happened, and how the storyline will develop under this condition.

\bullet

External Knowledge.
External knowledge questions require the model to understand additional knowledge that could not be found in movies but has relevance to the movie content. These external knowledge may involve cultural background, actor information, history, and social response.

We showcase examples of the various types mentioned above in our supplementary materials.

3.2
Distractor Options Generation

We evaluate long-form video understanding as a multiple-choice question-answering task, where each question has five candidate answers, only one of which is correct.
The role of distractor options in multiple-choice question-answering is crucial because they determine the difficulty of QA.
Well-designed distractor options should closely resemble the correct answer in terms of plausibility, making it challenging for participants to select the correct option.

We initially attempted to collect distractor options using ChatGPT, so as to save labor costs. However, we found that the generated candidates were unsatisfactory and had significant shortcuts. More details are shown in Sec
5.4
.
Therefore, we opted to engage human annotators in the process of writing distractor options within our dataset.
We established three guiding principles to avoid the bias from the distractor options generated by ChatGPT.
Firstly, we aimed to ensure that the length of the distractor options closely matched that of the correct options, to avoid overly long or short distractor bias.
Secondly, we ensured that the semantic content of the distractor options were different from that of the correct answers. To achieve this, we employed SentenceTransformer
Reimers and Gurevych 2019
to calculate the semantic differences and instructed annotators to verify and correct options with a semantic similarity exceeding 0.9.
Finally, for longer answers, we mandated that annotators modify at least two distinct parts of the response to create a more robust set of distractor options.
We show in Table
10
that human-corrected distractor options following these three principles are more challenging than those directly generated by the ChatGPT.

Figure 3

(a) The distribution of QA types based on multi-level scenes. (b) The distribution of clue length and video length in different VideoQA datasets.

One addition point is that we did not ask human workers to generate distractors for our temporal perception QA, because the answer format of temporal perception QA is a time interval that can be generated according to rules. To generate difficult distractors for temporal perception QA, we randomly chose four equal-length, non-overlap intervals that are before or after the time interval of the correct answer to generate the distractors. The equal-length design can avoid bias in the length distribution of time intervals.

Quality Control.

To construct a high-quality dataset which offers accurate annotations at all scenes and types, we adopt a series of mechanisms, including pretesting the annotators rigorously before formal annotation, preparing informative reference slides as well as demonstrations, and cross-validating across annotators.

Proposed Method

Figure 4
:
An overview of our DPM framework.

From the moviegoer-perspective, when answering a question about a movie, it is essential to consider both the key clues about the question and the contextual elements of the movie.
Based on this perspective, we propose a multimodal Dual Perceiver Modeling (DPM) framework, which consists of two branches: one is the keyframe branch for selecting the most important clues, and the other is the context branch for modeling contextual information of long videos.
An overview of our proposed method is shown in Fig.
4
.
The inputs are a long video clip
V
, a subtitle
S
, a question
Q
and five candidate answers
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{5}
.

4.1
Keyframe Branch

To capture clues relevant to the question from the long video, we refer to the keyframe selection module in
Yu et al. 2023
, which serves as a localizer to select the top-k keyframes related to the question as clues from long video frames.
Specifically, this localizer generates the probability of each frame by asking BLIP-2
Li et al. 2023b
with a localization question and selects the top-k frames as keyframes.
For video, the keyframe-aware perceiver module consists of the image encoder and the Q-Former used in BLIP-2
Li et al. 2023b
, while for text modality, it is the word embedding used in the LLM.
Considering that our movie data contains both videos and subtitles of dialogues, relying solely on visual information to locate keyframes can be suboptimal, we choose to combine visual and subtitle information in our keyframe generator module to locate keyframes relevant to the question.
Then, we adopt the selected top-k keyframes along with the corresponding top-k subtitles for the next operation.

4.2
Context Branch

To model the contextual information on long videos,
we first extract features from the video and subtitles using our feature extractor module, which has a similar structure to the keyframe perceiver module in the keyframe branch.
Then, we proceed to model the context of this long sequence features using a context-aware perceiver module, which is inspired by the design of Q-Former used in
Li et al. 2023b
. Specifically, the context-aware perceiver module is a transformer decoder, where the query is a set of learnable embeddings, and both the key and value are the long video token.

5.1
Implementation Details

Following
Yu et al. 2023
, we build our DPM upon BLIP-2
Li et al. 2023b
, using its flan-t5 xl LLM variant.
In the keyframe branch, we choose 32 keyframes along with their respective subtitles, from 512 uniformly sampled frames and their corresponding subtitles.
In the context branch, we uniformly sample 512 frames and truncated concatenated subtitles to a length of 512.
Then, we use 32 learnable query embeddings to model the context of long videos and long subtitles, respectively.
All experiments are conducted on 8 A100 GPUs. Due to the large computation of the BLIP-2, we can only use a batch size of 1.
Regarding other hyperparameters, we refer to the settings in
Yu et al. 2023
.

Choice Extraction.

During inference, although the problems are presented as clear single-choice questions with well-formatted options, many LLMs still provide answers in free-form text.
To address this issue, we first extract choices from the predictions of LLMs by employing exact matching techniques. For instance, when trying to match the choice “a0”, we check if the output of LLMs contains any occurrences of “a0”, “a0.”, “a0)”, “a0:”, and so on.
Once a match is found, we consider it a successful extraction of the model’s choice.
If the match strategy fails, we then try to extract the choice by semantic similarity.
We enhance semantic similarity by calculating the semantic similarity between the output answer and multiple options. We choose the option with the highest similarity as the final selection.

Generating reliable multiple choice QA based on ChatGPT may be a good avenue for future work.

6
Conclusions

We present our MoVQA, a long-form movie question-answering dataset, and benchmark it to assess the versatile cognitive capabilities of multimodal systems. The assessment relies on multi-level temporal lengths (single-scene, multi-scene and full-scene), considering both clue length and video length.
Compared to the second longest dataset, which has about a median clue length of 100 seconds and a video length of 3 minutes, our MoVQA surpasses it in both clue length (230 seconds) and video length (8 minutes).
Additionally, to take a step towards human-level understanding, QAs in our MoVQA are designed from the perspective of the interconnected abilities required by moviegoers to understand movie content.
We also benchmark various methods on MoVQA and find that even the most advanced methods achieve unsatisfactory results. Our established method has shown some improvements, but there is still ample scope for enhancement on our challenging MoVQA dataset.
We believe that our MoVQA will have a significant impact on the advancement and assessment of forthcoming long-form video understanding models.

Limitations.

There are a thousand Hamlets in a thousand people’s eyes. Despite conducting multiple rounds of cross-validation, the understanding of the essence of a movie may vary among individuals. As a result, for some challenging abstract questions, the answers may not be entirely accurate.
Further, there are inevitable distribution biases of distractor options in our MoVQA.
Finally, it is important to acknowledge that human curation is an imperfect process. Despite implementing multiple rounds of checks to minimize false positives, it is inevitable that the collected MoVQA may include some mislabeled or improperly formatted QA pairs.
In our future work, we will further expand the scale of MoVQA by including more movies in multiple languages with detailed QA annotations. We will also explore long-form video modeling techniques for enhanced movie understanding.
