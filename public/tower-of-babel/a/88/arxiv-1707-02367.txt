Monotonicity of saddle maps
Anton Petrunin and Stephan Stadler

Abstract
We prove an analog of the Schoen–Yau univalentness theorem for saddle maps between discs.

1
Introduction

A map from a closed unit disc
\mathbb{D}
to a Euclidean space is called
saddle
if for any hyperplane
\Pi
each connected component of the
complement
\mathbb{D}\setminus f^{-1}\Pi
intersects the boundary
\partial\mathbb{D}
.
A connected component that does not meet the boundary will be called a
hat
,
so saddle maps can be defined as
maps with no hats
.

Ruled surfaces and harmonic maps provide examples of saddle maps.

We prove a synthetic analog of the univalentness theorem for saddle maps from disc to disc.
The original theorem is formulated for harmonic maps and surfaces with nonpositive curvature.
It was proved by Richard Schoen and Shing Tung Yau
[
15
]
;
an interesting generalization was obtained by Jürgen Jost
[
7
]
.
An extensive study of general saddle maps was given by Samuil Shefel
[
13
,
14
]
;
his work was inspired by a note of Alexandr Alexandrov
[
1
]
on the intrinsic metric of a general ruled surface.
Part of Shefel’s work is written in a more popular way
[
2
]
.

A continuous map
f\colon X\to Y
is called
light
if the inverse image of any point
y\in Y
is totally disconnected.

1.1. Baby theorem.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
be a light saddle map.
Assume that
the restriction
f|_{\partial\mathbb{D}}
is the identity map.
Then
f
is a homeomorphism.

Let
\Delta
be a surface with possibly nonempty boundary and geodesic metric.
A map
f
from a closed disc
\mathbb{D}
to
\Delta
is called
saddle

if for any geodesic
[x,y]
in
\Delta
, each connected component of the complement
\mathbb{D}\setminus f^{-1}[x,y]
meets the boundary
\partial\mathbb{D}
.
It is easy to see that if
\Delta
is the plane then this new definition of saddle map agrees with the one given above.

A continuous map
f\colon X\to Y
is called
monotone
if the inverse image of any point
y\in Y
is connected.
Since a connected space is nonempty by definition,
any monotone map is onto
.

1.2. Monotonicity theorem.

Let
\Delta=(\mathbb{D},|{*}-{*}|)
be a closed disc equipped with a
\mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(\kappa)
metric
such that any two points are joined by a unique geodesic, and the geodesic depends continuously on the endpoints.
Assume
f\colon\mathbb{D}\to\Delta
is a saddle map and the restriction of
f
to the boundary
\partial\Delta
is a monotone map

\partial\mathbb{D}\to\partial\Delta
.
Then
f
is monotone.

The theorem essentially states that for saddle maps, monotonicity is an appropriate generalization of univalentness.

Note that the class of spaces satisfying the assumption for
\Delta
contains any disc with a
\mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0)
metric.
Moreover, this class includes nonpositively curved surfaces considered in the original univalentness theorem
[
15
]

as well as the surfaces described in
[
7
]
.

For the saddle map
f\colon\mathbb{D}\to\Delta
in the theorem,
one can use the so-called
monotone-light factorization

[
4
]

which is
f=g\circ h
,
where
g
is monotone and
h
is light.
Moore’s quotient theorem
[
10
]
implies that the target of the map
g
is homeomorphic to the disc
\mathbb{D}
, so we may think that
g\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
and
h\colon\mathbb{D}\to\Delta
.
The light map
h
is saddle, and according to the theorem, it is monotone.
The latter implies that
h
is a homeomorphism.

The following theorem is a generalization of the problem “Saddle surface” in
[
11
]
.
Together with Shefel’s theorem (see
[
14
]
and
[
2
, 4.5.5]
) it implies that the induced
intrinsic metric on
\Sigma
satisfies the
\mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0)
comparison.
(It is not known whether any saddle surface in the Euclidean space has locally
\mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0)
induced intrinsic metric.)

1.3. Saddle graph theorem.

Let
\Sigma
be a saddle surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
homeomorphic to a disc.
Assume that the orthogonal projection to the
(x,y)
-plane
maps the boundary of
\Sigma

injectively to a convex closed curve.
Then the orthogonal projection to the
(x,y)
-plane is injective on all of
\Sigma
.

In particular,
\Sigma
is a graph
z=f(x,y)
for a function
f
defined on a convex figure in the
(x,y)
-plane.

2
Energy-minimizing maps are saddle

Recall that harmonic maps
f\colon M\to N
between Riemannian manifolds can be defined as local energy minimizers
among maps with fixed values on the boundary.
Here the energy is defined as

E(f)=\int_{M}|df|^{2},

where
df\colon\mathrm{T}M\to\mathrm{T}N
is the differential of
f
.

2.1. Proposition.

Assume
\Delta=(\mathbb{D},|{*}-{*}|)
is a disc with Riemannian metric such that any two points
x,y\in\Delta
are joined by a minimizing geodesic
[x,y]
.
Then any energy-minimizing harmonic map
f\colon\mathbb{D}\to\Delta
with fixed values on the boundary is saddle.

Proof.

Assume the contrary; that is, for some geodesic
[x,y]
in
\Delta
the complement
\mathbb{D}\setminus f^{-1}[x,y]
has a component

\Omega
which does not meet the boundary
\partial\mathbb{D}
.

Let
\gamma(t)
be the unit speed parametrization of the geodesic
[x,y]
from
x
to
y
.
Let us redefine the map
f
in
\Omega
by setting
t(z)=\min\{\,|x-f(z)|,|x-y|\}
and

\hat{f}(z)=\left[\begin{aligned} &\gamma\circ t(z)&&\text{if}&&z\in\Omega,\\
&f(z)&&\text{if}&&z\notin\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Note that
f|_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\equiv\hat{f}|_{\partial\mathbb{D}}
and

|\hat{f}(z)-\hat{f}(z^{\prime})|\leqslant|f(z)-f(z^{\prime})|

for any
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{D}
.
It follows that
E(f)>E(\hat{f})
— a contradiction.
∎

Remark.

The above proof remains valid if the metric on the target disc is not Riemannian, but has an upper curvature bound in the sense of Alexandrov;
the harmonic maps into such spaces can also be defined as local energy minimizers, see
[
6
,
8
]
.

3
Monotonicity

3.1. Claim.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{D}\to\Delta
be as in the monotonicity theorem (
1
).
Then

(i)

for any closed convex set
K\subset\Delta
each connected component of
\mathbb{D}\setminus f^{-1}K
intersects
\partial\mathbb{D}
.

(ii)

for any open convex set
\Phi\subset\Delta
each connected component of
f^{-1}\Phi
is simply connected.

Proof; (i).

Let
\gamma
be a geodesic in
\Delta
, disjoint from
K
and with endpoints on
\partial\Delta
.
Let
\Sigma_{K}
be the set of all such geodesics.
For each
\gamma\in\Sigma_{K}
denote by

H_{\gamma}
the component of
\Delta\setminus\gamma
which contains
K
.
Since
K
is closed and convex and
\Delta
is homeomorphic to a disc, we have

K=\bigcap_{\gamma\in\Sigma_{K}}H_{\gamma}.

In other words, if
x\notin K
, then some geodesic separates
K
from
x
.

By definition of saddle maps, each connected component of
\mathbb{D}\setminus f^{-1}H_{\gamma}
meets the boundary
\partial\mathbb{D}
.
Therefore the same holds for the union

\mathbb{D}\setminus f^{-1}K=\bigcup_{\gamma\in\Sigma_{K}}(\mathbb{D}\setminus f^{-1}H_{\gamma}).

(ii)

Set
\Psi=f^{-1}\Phi\subset\mathbb{D}
.
Choose a simple closed curve
\gamma\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\Psi
;
denote by
\Gamma
the disc bounded by
\gamma
.
By
(i)
,
f(\Gamma)
lies in the convex hull of
f(\gamma)
which will be denoted by
K
.
Since
\Phi
is convex,
K\subset\Phi
.
It follows that
\Gamma\subset\Psi
and therefore
\gamma
is contractible in
\Psi
.

Since
\gamma
is arbitrary,
\Psi
is simply connected.
∎

Proof of the monotonicity theorem.

Since
f|_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\colon\partial\mathbb{D}\to\partial\Delta
is monotone, it has degree
\pm 1
.
We can assume that the orientations on
\mathbb{D}
and
\Delta
are chosen so that
\deg f|_{\partial\mathbb{D}}=1

and therefore
\deg f=1
;
in particular
f
is onto.

Assume
f
is not monotone;
that is, there is a point
x\in\Delta
such that the inverse image
f^{-1}\{x\}
is not connected.

Given
s\in\partial\Delta
, consider the open set

\Phi_{s}=\left\{\,\left.{y\in\Delta}\vphantom{\measuredangle[x\,^{y}_{s}]<\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\,\right|\,{\measuredangle[x\,^{y}_{s}]<\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\,\right\}.

Note that
\Phi_{s}
and its complement are convex in
\Delta
.
(Here we use that
\Delta
is homeomorphic to a disc; the analogous statement in higher dimensions does not hold.)
In particular, the relative boundary
\partial_{\Delta}\Phi_{s}
is a geodesic.

Consider the two open subsets
\Theta\subset\mathbb{D}
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{D}\times\partial\Delta
defined as

\displaystyle\Theta

\displaystyle=\mathbb{D}\setminus f^{-1}\{x\},

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle=\left\{\,\left.{(z,s)\in\mathbb{D}\times\partial\Delta}\vphantom{f(z)\in\Phi_{s}}\,\right|\,{f(z)\in\Phi_{s}}\,\right\}.

The projection
\mathbb{D}\times\partial\Delta\to\mathbb{D}
sends
\Omega
to
\Theta
.
Note that the induced homomorphism
\pi_{1}\Omega\to\pi_{1}\Theta
is onto
.
Indeed, for any point
z\in\Theta
there is
s\in\partial\Delta
such that
f(z)\in\Phi_{s}
or equivalently
\measuredangle[x\,^{f(z)}_{s}]<\tfrac{\pi}{2}
.
Moreover, since
f|_{\partial\mathbb{D}}
is monotone, the set of points
s
satisfying the above condition is an open arc in
\partial\Delta
.
By
[
16
, Theorem 2]
, one can fix a continuous map
z\mapsto s_{z}
such that
f(z)\in\Phi_{s_{z}}
for any
z\in\Theta
.
Then for any loop
\alpha
in
\Theta
, the loop
\tilde{\alpha}(t)=(\alpha(t),s_{\alpha(t)})
is an
f
-lift of
\alpha
in
\Omega\subset\mathbb{D}\times\partial\Delta
.

Note that
\Theta\cap\partial\mathbb{D}
is connected.
Indeed,

\diamond

If
x\notin\partial\mathbb{D}
, then
\Theta\cap\partial\mathbb{D}=\partial\mathbb{D}
.

\diamond

If
x\in\partial\mathbb{D}
, then since
f|_{\partial\mathbb{D}}
is monotone,
\Theta\cap\partial\mathbb{D}
is an open arc.

Since
\{x\}
is convex, by Claim
3
(
i
)
, every connected component of
\Theta
has to intersect
\partial\mathbb{D}
.
It follows that
\Theta
is connected as well.

Consider the restriction of the projection
\mathbb{D}\times\partial\Delta\to\partial\Delta
to
\Omega
;
it has fiber
\Psi_{s}=f^{-1}\Phi_{s}
at
s\in\partial\Delta
.
By
3
, the set
\Psi_{s}
is either empty or simply connected.

Indeed, fix
s\in\partial\Delta
and assume
\Psi_{s}\neq\varnothing
.
Since
\Phi_{s}
is a complement of a closed convex set,
each connected component of
\Psi_{s}
must meet
\sigma_{s}=\Psi_{s}\cap\mathbb{S}^{1}
.
Since
f
is monotone,
\sigma_{s}
is an open arc in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.
In particular,
\sigma_{s}
is connected and so is
\Psi_{s}
.
Since
\Phi_{s}
is a convex open set, by Claim
3
(
ii
)
,
\Psi_{s}
is simply connected.

Note that
\Phi_{s}=\varnothing\iff s=x
.
Applying
[
16
, Theorem 2]
again, we get the following.

\diamond

If
x\notin\partial\mathbb{D}
, then from above
\Psi_{s}
is simply connected for any
s
.
Therefore the projection
\Omega\to\partial\Delta
induces an isomorphism of fundamental groups; that is
\pi_{1}\Omega=\mathbb{Z}
.

\diamond

If
x\in\partial\mathbb{D}
, then by a similar reason, we have
\pi_{1}\Omega=0
.

Since
f^{-1}\{x\}
is not connected, it can be divided into two subsets by a curve in
\Theta
.

\diamond

If
x\notin\partial\mathbb{D}
, it follows that
\pi_{1}\Theta
and therefore
\pi_{1}\Omega
contains a free group with two generators — a contradiction.

\diamond

If
x\in\partial\mathbb{D}
, it follows that
\pi_{1}\Theta
and therefore
\pi_{1}\Omega
contains
\mathbb{Z}
as a subgroup — a contradiction again.∎

Saddle graph

The following proof reminds proof of Sergei Bernstein in
[
3
]
.

Proof of the saddle graph theorem (
1
).

Denote by
F
the convex figure in the
(x,y)
-plane bounded by the projection of boundary of
\Sigma
.
Since
\Sigma
is saddle, it lies in the convex hull of its boundary;
in particular, the projection of
\Sigma
lies in
F
.
Therefore the monotonicity theorem can be applied to the composition of the embedding
\mathbb{D}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
which describes
\Sigma
and the projection
to the
(x,y)
-plane. The composition sends
\mathbb{D}
to
F
.

It follows that any vertical line in
F\times\mathbb{R}
intersects
\Sigma
along a closed interval or a point.
In other words, there are two functions
\alpha,\beta\colon F\to\mathbb{R}

such that

(x,y,z)\in\Sigma\quad\iff\quad\alpha(x,y)\leqslant z\leqslant\beta(x,y).

Therefore, to prove the saddle graph theorem it is sufficient to show the following:

4.1. Claim.

\alpha\equiv\beta
.

Before proving the claim let us list properties of the functions
\alpha
and
\beta
.

Note that the function
\alpha\colon F\to\mathbb{R}
is lower semicontinuous
and
\beta\colon F\to\mathbb{R}
is upper semicontinuous.
Further,
\alpha
and
\beta
agree on
\partial F
, and the
restriction
\alpha|_{\partial F}=\beta|_{\partial F}
is continuous.
In particular, if an arc
\theta\subset\partial F
is sufficiently short, then
the change of values of
\alpha
in
\theta
is small.

Since
\Sigma
is saddle, for any linear function
\lambda\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
and any closed domain
D\subset F
,
the restriction
(\alpha-\lambda)|_{D}
admits a maximum point on the boundary
\partial D
— if not, then a plane
z=\lambda(x,y)+c
cuts a hat in
D
.

Now let us start to prove the claim.

Assume the contrary; that is,
\alpha(s)<\beta(s)
for some
s\in F
.
Note that
s
lies in the interior of
F
and we can find a pair of points
p
and
q
arbitrarily close to
s

such that

\alpha(q)>\beta(p)+\varepsilon

for some fixed
\varepsilon>0
.

Draw the chord
[a,b]
of
F
containing
p
and
q
;
we assume that the points
a,p,q,b
appear on the chord in this order.

Consider the linear function
\lambda\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\lambda(p)=\beta(p)+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{3},\quad\lambda(q)=\alpha(p)-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{3}.

Let
\ell
be the graph of
\lambda
over
[a,b]
; it is a line segment in
F\times\mathbb{R}
.

Consider a one-parameter family of planes
\Pi_{t}\supset\ell
,
t\in(0,1)
which rotates by angle
\pi
approaching the vertical plane as
t
goes to
0
or
1
.

Note that the plane
\Pi_{t}
is a graph of a linear function
\lambda_{t}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}

and the restriction
\lambda_{t}|_{[a,b]}
agrees with
\lambda
for any
t
.
Set

\Theta_{t}=\left\{\,\left.{u\in F}\vphantom{\alpha(u)>\lambda_{t}(u)}\,\right|\,{\alpha(u)>\lambda_{t}(u)}\,\right\};

note that
\Theta_{t}
is open in
F
and
\Theta_{t}\ni a,q
and
\Theta_{t}\not\ni p,b
for any
t
.

We say that the value
t
is left (right) if
a
can be connected to
q
by a curve in
\Theta_{t}
which goes on the left (correspondingly right) from
p
.

Since
\Sigma
is saddle, a value
t
can not be left and right at the same time — if it is the case, then
\Pi_{t}
cuts a hat from
\Sigma
.

The sets of left (right) values are open in
[0,1]
.
We can assume that all values
t\approx 0
are left and all values
t\approx 1
are right.
Therefore some value, say
t_{0}
, is neither left nor right.

Note that the connected component
\Theta_{t_{0}}^{\prime}\ni q
in
\Theta_{t_{0}}
does not contain
a
.

Since
\Sigma
is saddle, the restriction
(\alpha-\lambda_{t_{0}})|_{\Theta_{t_{0}}^{\prime}}
admits its maximum on
\partial F\cap\penalty\Theta_{t_{0}}^{\prime}
.
By construction
\alpha(q)-\lambda_{t_{0}}(q)>\tfrac{\varepsilon}{3}
;
therefore

\alpha(r)-\lambda_{t_{0}}(r)>\tfrac{\varepsilon}{3}

➊

for some
r\in\partial F\cap\Theta_{t_{0}}^{\prime}
.

Denote by
\theta
the connected component of
r
in
\partial F\cap\Theta_{t_{0}}^{\prime}
.
Without loss of generality, we can assume that the
z
-coordinate of points on
\Sigma
are between 0 and 1.
It follows that
\theta
lies in a thin strip
S
described by the inequalities

0\leqslant\lambda_{t_{0}}\leqslant 1.

Since
p
and
q
are close to
s
,
the slope of
\lambda
and therefore the slope of
\lambda_{t_{0}}
can be made arbitrarily large;
therefore the strip
S
can be assumed to be arbitrarily thin.
It follows that the arc
\theta
can be assumed to be arbitrarily short.
(Here we use that
s
lies in the interior of
F
and
S
passes arbitrarily close to
s
.)
Therefore the change of values of
\alpha
in
\theta
can be assumed to be arbitrarily small.

Note that
\lambda_{t_{0}}
is monotonic on
\theta
and
the function
\alpha-\lambda_{t_{0}}
vanishes on the ends of
\theta
.
It follows that

\sup\left\{\,\left.{\alpha(u)-\lambda_{t_{0}}(u)}\vphantom{u\in\theta}\,\right|\,{u\in\theta}\,\right\}

can be assumed to be arbitrarily small.
The latter contradicts
➊
.
∎

On the baby theorem.

The presented proof is a tricky fix of the following
fake
proof of the baby theorem (
1
).
We say where we cheat in the footnote.

Fake proof.

Note that
\deg f=1
;
in particular
f
is onto.
It remains to show that
f
is injective.

Assume
w=f(x)=f(y)
for distinct points
x,y\in\mathbb{D}
.
Note that
w
lies in the interior of
\mathbb{D}
.
Choose a chord
\gamma
that contains
w
and goes
from boundary to boundary of
\mathbb{D}
.
The inverse image
p^{-1}(\gamma)
is a contractible set with two ends at
\partial\mathbb{D}
, say
a
and
b
.
We can assume that the points
a,x,y,b
appear in the same order on
p^{-1}(\gamma)
.

1

1

This is where we are cheating: the inverse image
p^{-1}(\gamma)
might be as terrible as a pseudoarc, where the order of points has no sense.

There is a continuous one-parameter family of chords

with the ends at

A correct proof of the baby theorem can be built on the deep theorem of Shefel
[
13
]
, but this argument does not seem to be generalizable.

Proof.

Let us extend the map of the disc by the identity map outside the disc.
According to Shefel’s theorem, the induced length metric on the plane is
\mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0)
.
This metric coincides with the Euclidean metric outside a compact set
therefore the induced intrinsic metric is flat and the map preserves this metric;
in particular, it is a homeomorphism.∎

A generalization of the monotonicity theorem.

In the proof the condition that
\Delta
is a disc can be relaxed to the following:
the mapping cylinder of the target space over
f|_{\partial\mathbb{D}}
is homeomorphic to a closed disc.
For example, the target space
\Delta
might look like the solid figure eight in the picture.

Under the name
disc retracts
, these spaces are used in our paper
[
12
]
on a closely related subject.

On univalentness of harmonic maps.

If
f\colon\mathbb{D}\to\Sigma
is a harmonic map from a closed disc to a surface with a Riemannian metric,
then one can show that for any
y\notin f(\partial\mathbb{D})
the inverse image
f^{-1}\{y\}
is a discrete set of points.
A proof of this statement was suggested by Alexandre Eremenko
[
5
]
.
In particular,
f
is light if
f^{-1}\{f(x)\}=\{x\}
for any
x\in\partial\mathbb{D}
.
It reduces the univalentness theorems
[
15
,
7
]
to our monotonicity theorem.

As shown by Ernst Kuwert
[
9
, Theorem 3]
the corresponding statement for surfaces with
\mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0)
metrics is wrong.
Namely, there are (1) a disc
\Delta
with a flat metric everywhere except for one cone point
q
where it has negative curvature and (2) a harmonic map
f\colon\mathbb{D}\to\Delta
such that
f
restricts to a homeomorphism between boundaries but
f^{-1}\{q\}
is a nontrivial tree.

Therefore, the monotonicity theorem is optimal even for harmonic maps.

On saddle graph theorem.

The property
3
(
i
)
can be used to define saddle discs in arbitrary
\mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0)
space.
With this definition, the saddle graph theorem does not hold in the product space
C\times\mathbb{R}
, where
C
is a cone with large total angle — if
o
is the tip of the cone then the vertical line
\{o\}\times\mathbb{R}
might intersect the saddle disc along a nontrivial segment.
An example can be built from the quoted construction of Ernst Kuwert, but actually simpler.
