Many-Body Localization: Transitions in Spin Models
John Schliemann 1 , Jo a ~ \tilde{\rm a} o Vitor I. Costa 2 , Paul Wenk 1 , and J. Carlos Egues 2

Abstract
We study the transitions between ergodic and many-body localized phases in spin systems, subject to quenched disorder, including the Heisenberg chain and the central spin model. In both cases systems with common spin lengths 1 / 2 1/2 and 1 1 are investigated via exact numerical diagonalization and random matrix techniques. Particular attention is paid to the sample-to-sample variance ( Δ s ​ r ) 2 (\Delta_{s}r)^{2} of the averaged consecutive-gap ratio ⟨ r ⟩ \langle r\rangle for different disorder realizations. For both types of systems and spin lengths we find a maximum in Δ s ​ r \Delta_{s}r as a function of disorder strength, accompanied by an inflection point of ⟨ r ⟩ \langle r\rangle , signaling the transition from ergodicity to many-body localization. The critical disorder strength is found to be somewhat smaller than the values reported in the recent literature. Further information about the transitions can be gained from the probability distribution of expectation values within a given disorder realization.

It denotes the absence of thermalization in an

II.2
Random Matrix Theory

An important method to distinguish a many-body localized phase from an ergodic
phase is random matrix theory, which is a theory for statistical
fluctuations of energy levels of a given quantum system

Guhr98
. A modern tool to analyze the energy level statistics is
the consecutive gap ratio
Oganesyan07
defined as

r_{n}=\frac{\min\{s_{n},s_{n-1}\}}{\max\{s_{n},s_{n-1}\}}=\min\left\{\bar{r}_{n},\frac{1}{\bar{r}_{n}}\right\}\quad,\quad\bar{r}_{n}=\frac{s_{n}}{s_{n-1}}\,,

(2)

where
s_{n}=e_{n+1}-e_{n}
is the difference between two neighboring energy levels

e_{n+1}
,
e_{n}
. In the strictly many-body localized (or integrable)
phase, characterized by an extensive number of independent conserved quantities, the differences
s_{n}
obey Poisson
statistics, and the probability distribution for the random variable
r=r_{n}

can easily be determined to be
Oganesyan07

p(r)=\frac{2}{(1+r)^{2}}\,.

(3)

On the other hand, in the fully ergodic phase, a system of the type
(
1
) is generally assumed to be described by the
Gaussian orthogonal ensemble (GOE) of random matrices
Guhr98
.
Here the analysis of small random matrices mimicking the Hamiltonian predicts
the pertaining probability distribution to be
Atas13

p(r)=\frac{27}{4}\frac{r+r^{2}}{(1+r+r^{2})^{5/2}}\,,

(4)

which can be seen as an analog of the classic Wigner surmises for
probability distributions governing the traditional random variable
s=s_{n}

Guhr98
.

As a consequence, the lowest moments of the probability distribution
(
3
) in the integrable case are given by

\displaystyle\langle r\rangle_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\ln 2-1\approx 0.3863\,,

(5)

\displaystyle\langle r^{2}\rangle_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3-4\ln 2\approx 0.2274\,,

(6)

\displaystyle\Delta_{p}r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\langle r^{2}\rangle_{p}-\langle r\rangle^{2}_{p}}\approx 0.2796\,,

(7)

with

\langle\cdot\rangle_{p}=\int_{0}^{1}dr(p(r)\cdot)\,,

(8)

whereas in the ergodic situation (
4
) we have

\displaystyle\langle r\rangle_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4-2\sqrt{3}\approx 0.5359\,,

(9)

\displaystyle\langle r^{2}\rangle_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{27}{4}\ln\left(1+\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{5}{2}\sqrt{3}\approx 0.3515\,,

(10)

\displaystyle\Delta_{p}r

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 0.2536\,,

(11)

II.3
Statistical Data Analysis

II.3.1
Disorder ensemble

Consider an ensemble of
Q
realizations of the local disorder field

h_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\dots,K\}
.
Each disorder realization, or sample, labeled by
\alpha\in\{1\dots,Q\}

leads to a probability
distribution
p_{\alpha}(r)
for the consecutive gap ratio
r\in[0,1]
.
Given an arbitrary function
f(r)
, these distributions determine the
realization-dependent averages
(or expectation values)

\langle f\rangle_{\alpha}=\int_{0}^{1}drp_{\alpha}(r)f(r)

(12)

with variances

(\Delta_{\alpha}f)^{2}=\langle f^{2}\rangle_{\alpha}-\langle f\rangle_{\alpha}^{2}\,.

(13)

The disorder-averaged probability distribution
p(r)

at given disorder
strength
(and other system parameters) is

p(r)=\lim_{Q\to\infty}\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}p_{\alpha}(r)\,,

(14)

and a good numerical estimate for this quantity is

p(r)\approx\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}p_{\alpha}(r)\quad,\quad Q\gg 1\,,

(15)

for sufficiently large
Q
.
The disorder-averaged expectation values of
f(r)
reads

\langle f\rangle_{p}=\int_{0}^{1}drp(r)f(r)=\lim_{Q\to\infty}\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}\langle f\rangle_{\alpha}\,.

(16)

II.3.2
Probability distribution for average within sample

On the other hand, we can view the numbers
x=\langle f\rangle_{\alpha}

as random variables according to the distribution

\displaystyle s(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2h)^{K}}\int_{-h}^{h}dh_{1}\cdots\int_{-h}^{h}dh_{K}

(17)

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\delta\left(x-\int_{0}^{1}drp(r;h_{1},\dots,h_{K})f(r)\right)

where
p(r;h_{1},\dots,h_{K})=p_{\alpha}
is the probability distribution
within a system with local disorder fields
h_{1},\dots,h_{K}
forming the
disorder realization
\alpha
.
Thus, the disorder-averaged expectation
value of
f(r)
can be formulated as

\langle f\rangle_{s}=\int dxs(x)x=\lim_{Q\to\infty}\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}\langle f\rangle_{\alpha}=\langle f\rangle_{p}\,,

(18)

where the integration goes over all values of
x=f(r)
for
r\in[0,1]
.
The distribution
s(x)
does in general not coincide with
p(r)
even
for
x=f(r)=r
. Moreover
s(x)
will of course have a dependence on the function

f
, which we, however, shall suppress in the notation.

The finite average

\bar{f}=\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}\langle f\rangle_{\alpha}\approx\langle f\rangle_{s}\,,

(19)

is, again for appropriately large
Q
, an approximation to the
expression (
18
). On the other hand, it is a sum of stochastically
independent (and therefore uncorrelated) random variables with identical
probability distribution, so that the resulting joint distribution is

\pi(x_{1},\dots,x_{Q})=\prod_{\alpha=1}^{Q}s(x_{\alpha})\,.

(20)

Thus, the expectation value of
\bar{f}
is

\langle\bar{f}\rangle_{\pi}=\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}\langle f\rangle_{s}=\langle f\rangle_{s}\,.

(21)

II.3.3
Sample-to-sample variance

For the variance pertaining to the expectation value (
21
) one finds

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(\bar{f}-\langle f\rangle_{s}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\pi}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{Q}\frac{\left\langle\left(\langle f\rangle_{\alpha}-\langle f\rangle_{s}\right)\left(\langle f\rangle_{\beta}-\langle f\rangle_{s}\right)\right\rangle_{\pi}}{Q^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{Q^{2}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}\left\langle\left(\langle f\rangle_{\alpha}-\langle f\rangle_{s}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{s}=\frac{(\Delta_{s}f)^{2}}{Q}

(22)

with

\displaystyle(\Delta_{s}f)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(f-\langle f\rangle_{s})^{2}\rangle_{s}

(23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{Q\to\infty}\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}(\langle f\rangle_{\alpha}-\langle f\rangle_{s})^{2}\,.

Therefore, the fluctuations of the finite average (
18
) around
its expectation value (
21
) are characterized by the standard
deviation

\Delta_{\pi}\bar{f}=\sqrt{\left\langle\left(\bar{f}-\langle f\rangle_{s}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\pi}}=\frac{\Delta_{s}f}{\sqrt{Q}}

(24)

and shows the familiar decay
\propto 1/\sqrt{Q}
.
This result follows of course also from the Lindeberg-Levy central limit
theorem applied to the sum (
19
) of random variables.
An approximate expression for the variance (
23
) is

(\Delta_{s}f)^{2}\approx\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}(\langle f\rangle_{\alpha}-\bar{f})^{2}\quad,\quad Q\gg 1\,.

(25)

Note that the variance (
23
) also occurs as a contribution to
the variance of
f(r)
calculated from the disorder-averaged probability
distribution (
14
),

\displaystyle(\Delta_{p}f)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}drp(r)(f(r)-\langle f\rangle_{p})^{2}

(26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{Q\to\infty}\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}\int_{0}^{1}drp_{\alpha}(r)(f(r)-\langle f\rangle_{p})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{Q\to\infty}\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}(\langle f^{2}\rangle_{\alpha}-\langle f\rangle_{\alpha}^{2})

\displaystyle\qquad+\lim_{Q\to\infty}\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}(\langle f\rangle_{\alpha}^{2}-\langle f\rangle_{p}^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{Q\to\infty}\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}(\Delta_{\alpha}f)^{2}+(\Delta_{s}f)^{2}\,.

(27)

Hence, the variance (
26
) with respect to the averaged
probability distribution (
14
) is the average of all variances
within the disorder realizations around their individual expectation value
of
f
, plus the variance (
23
) describing the fluctuations
of these expectation values around their mean. An approximate
expression for the above result is

\displaystyle(\Delta_{p}f)^{2}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}\int_{0}^{1}drp_{\alpha}(r)(f(r)-\bar{f})^{2}

(28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}(\Delta_{\alpha}f)^{2}+\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}(\langle f\rangle_{\alpha}-\bar{f})^{2}

(29)

where again
Q\gg 1
.

II.3.4
Variance of the variance

Let us now analyze the statistical fluctuations of the r.h.s. of the
approximate quantity (
25
) for finite
Q
and define

\bar{g}:=\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}(\langle f\rangle_{\alpha}-\bar{f})^{2}=\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}\left(\langle f\rangle^{2}_{\alpha}-\bar{f}^{2}\right)\,.

(30)

An important difference between the above expression and the quantity
(
19
) is that above the summands depend, again via Eq. (
19
),
on all expectation values
\langle f\rangle_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\{1,\dots,Q\}
,
and are therefore distributed according to the joint
probability distribution (
20
).
The expectation value of the random variable (
30
) with respect to
the latter distribution is

\displaystyle\langle\bar{g}\rangle_{\pi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Q}\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}\left\langle\langle f\rangle^{2}_{\alpha}\right\rangle_{\pi}-\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{Q}\left\langle\langle f\rangle_{\alpha}\langle f\rangle_{\beta}\right\rangle_{\pi}\right)

(31)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Q-1}{Q}\left(\langle f^{2}\rangle_{s}-\langle f\rangle^{2}_{s}\right)=\frac{Q-1}{Q}(\Delta_{s}f)^{2}

which approaches the variance (
23
) for large
Q
.
Note also that, in contrast to
Eq. (
18
), it holds

\langle f^{2}\rangle_{s}=\int dxs(x)x^{2}=\lim_{Q\to\infty}\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}\langle f\rangle^{2}_{\alpha}\neq\langle f^{2}\rangle_{p}\,.

(32)

It is now straightforward to establish that

\displaystyle\left\langle(\langle f\rangle^{2}_{\alpha}-\bar{f}^{2}-\langle\bar{g}\rangle_{\pi})(\langle f\rangle^{2}_{\beta}-\bar{f}^{2}-\langle\bar{g}\rangle_{\pi})\right\rangle_{\pi}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\left(\langle f^{4}\rangle_{s}-\langle f^{2}\rangle^{2}_{s}\right)+{\cal O}\left(\frac{1}{Q}\right)\,,

(33)

and therefore, analogously as in Eq. (
22
),

\displaystyle\left(\Delta_{\pi}\bar{g}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{Q}\frac{\left\langle(\langle f\rangle^{2}_{\alpha}-\bar{f}^{2}-\langle\bar{g}\rangle_{\pi})(\langle f\rangle^{2}_{\beta}-\bar{f}^{2}-\langle\bar{g}\rangle_{\pi})\right\rangle_{\pi}}{Q^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{(\Delta_{s}f^{2})^{2}}{Q}+{\cal O}\left(\frac{1}{Q^{2}}\right)

(34)

where

\displaystyle(\Delta_{s}f^{2})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle f^{4}\rangle_{s}-\langle f^{2}\rangle^{2}_{s}=\left\langle\left(f^{2}-\langle f^{2}\rangle_{s}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{s}

(35)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\Delta_{s}\left((\Delta_{s}f)^{2}\right)\right)^{2}\,.

II.3.5
Consecutive gap ratio

In what follows we will be mainly concerned with the function
f(r)=r
where we
have

\displaystyle(\Delta_{s}r)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{Q\to\infty}\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}(\langle r\rangle_{\alpha}-\langle r\rangle_{s})^{2}

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{Q\to\infty}\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}\left(\langle r\rangle^{2}_{\alpha}-\langle r\rangle^{2}_{s}\right)

with

\langle r\rangle_{s}=\int_{0}^{1}dxs(x)x=\lim_{Q\to\infty}\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{Q}\langle r\rangle_{\alpha}=\langle r\rangle_{p}\,,

(37)

where
s(x)
is, in accordance with Eq. (
17
), the probability
distribution for the random variable
x=\langle r\rangle_{\alpha}
.
Finally, as seen in Eq. (
35
), the variance of the
variance (
36
) is determined by

\displaystyle(\Delta_{s}r^{2})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle r^{4}\rangle_{s}-\langle r^{2}\rangle^{2}_{s}=\left\langle\left(r^{2}-\langle r^{2}\rangle_{s}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{s}

(38)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\Delta_{s}\left((\Delta_{s}r)^{2}\right)\right)^{2}\,.

we estimate
the transition point to

h_{\rm max}\approx h_{\rm inf}\approx 2.6J\dots 3.0J
for spin length

I=1/2

h_{\rm max}\approx h_{\rm inf}\approx 4.0J\dots 4.5J
for
I=1
.
These values for the critical disorder strength for the transition from
the ergodic to the many-body lpcalized phase are somewhat smaller than the ones
reported in other works
Pal10
;
Luitz15
;
Devakul15
;
Doggen18
;
Chanda20a
,
which favor, for
I=1/2
, values of
h/J\approx 4
or larger. However, some
of these works
Pal10
;
Luitz15
;
Doggen18
concentrate on chains with an
even number of spins and the subspace with total spin
J^{z}=0
,
whereas here we also take into account odd numbers
of spins and all subspaces except for those with
|J^{z}|\in\{IK,IK-1\}
.
Moreover, we introduce a new and different criterion to locate the transition
given by the position of
(\Delta_{s}r)_{\rm max}
.

IV
Summary and Outlook

We have compared the transitions between ergodic and many-body localized phases
in disodered Heisenberg chains as well as central spin models
composed of spins of length
1/2
and
1
.
A useful new tool we introduce is the sample-to-sample standard
deviation
\Delta_{s}r

of the expectation value
\langle r\rangle_{\alpha}
of the consecutive-gap
ratio in an individual disorder realization (sample)
\alpha
.
This quantity assumes, for both types of systems and spin lengths, a maximum
as a function of disorder strength, accompanied by an inflection point of

\langle r\rangle
. These are typical features of a phase transition
where the latter quantity play the role of an order parameter.
The critical disorder strength deduced from these observations turn out to be
smaller than those reported in the recent literature.

Further information about the transitions is contained in the
probability distribution of the expectation values within a
given disorder realization. We expect the study of this probability
distribution and its moments to be a useful tool in the investgation
of phenomena related to many-body localization also in other systems.
