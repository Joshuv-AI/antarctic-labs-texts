Completely positive master equation for arbitrary driving and small level spacing
Evgeny Mozgunov, Daniel Lidar

Abstract
Markovian master equations are a ubiquitous tool in the study of open quantum systems, but deriving them from first principles involves a series of compromises. On the one hand, the Redfield equation is valid for fast environments (whose correlation function decays much faster than the system relaxation time) regardless of the relative strength of the coupling to the system Hamiltonian, but is notoriously non-completely-positive. On the other hand, the Davies equation preserves complete positivity but is valid only in the ultra-weak coupling limit and for systems with a finite level spacing, which makes it incompatible with arbitrarily fast time-dependent driving. Here we show that a recently derived Markovian coarse-grained master equation (CGME), already known to be completely positive, has a much expanded range of applicability compared to the Davies equation, and moreover, is locally generated and can be generalized to accommodate arbitrarily fast driving. This generalization, which we refer to as the time-dependent CGME, is thus suitable for the analysis of fast operations in gate-model quantum computing, such as quantum error correction and dynamical decoupling. Our derivation proceeds directly from the Redfield equation and allows us to place rigorous error bounds on all three equations: Redfield, Davies, and coarse-grained. Our main result is thus a completely positive Markovian master equation that is a controlled approximation to the true evolution for any time-dependence of the system Hamiltonian, and works for systems with arbitrarily small level spacing. We illustrate this with an analysis showing that dynamical decoupling can extend coherence times even in a strictly Markovian setting.

Introduction

Modeling experiments requires taking into account that physical systems are open, i.e., not ideally isolated from their environments. Usually an environment contains many more degrees of freedom than the system itself, but is not in any interesting phase of matter where the detailed modeling of each of those degrees of freedom is required. This makes the problem of modeling open systems tractable. More precisely, the problem is tractable under a list of assumptions on the parameters of the bath (environment) and its interaction with the system

One natural approach is to consider the case when this interaction is weak compared to all other energy scales in the problem. In this limit, Davies showed
[

This quantity, in general, becomes exponentially large in the system size due its denominator, so that the
coupling strength or the bath correlation time need to be sufficiently small for the use of Davies generators to be justified, up to some upper system size limit.

2
Three master equations

This section provides a summary of our main results. We first present a brief background to define basic concepts and notation, followed by the definition and properties of the two main timescales we use later to provide bounds and ranges of applicability. We then summarize the three master equations we focus on in this work, followed by upper bounds on the distance of their solutions from the true state.

2.1
Background

Consider a system interacting with its environment as described by the total Hamiltonian

H_{\textrm{tot}}=H\otimes I_{\text{b}}+A\otimes B+I\otimes H_{\text{b}}\ .

(3)

Here
H
is the time-independent system Hamiltonian (we treat the time-dependent case in Sec.
4
),
H_{\text{b}}
is the bath Hamiltonian, and
A
and
B
are, respectively, Hermitian system and bath operators. The more general situation, with
V=A\otimes B
replaced by
\sum_{i}A_{i}\otimes B_{i}
, is easily treated as well (see Sec.
3.7
), but we avoid it here to simplify the notation. We choose
A
to be dimensionless with
\|A\|=1
[the operator norm is defined in Eq. (
36
)] and
B
to have energy units, but we deliberately do
not
set
\|B\|=1
, since we wish to account for baths (such as harmonic oscillator baths) for which
\|B\|
can be infinite. We also set
\hbar\equiv 1
throughout, so that energy and frequency have identical units.

Let the eigenstates of
H
be
\{|n\rangle\}
, and the corresponding eigenvalues
\{E_{n}\}
. Equivalently,
H=\sum_{n}E_{n}\Pi_{n}
, where
\Pi_{n}
is a projector onto the eigenspace with eigenvalue
E_{n}
, and
\Pi_{m}\Pi_{n}=\delta_{mn}\Pi_{n}
.
Note that in the interaction picture
A(t)=U^{\dagger}(t)AU(t)=\sum_{nm}A_{nm}e^{-iE_{mn}t}|n\rangle\langle m|
where
E_{mn}=E_{m}-E_{n}
,
A_{nm}=\bra{n}A\ket{m}
, and
U
is the solution of
\dot{U}=-iHU
.
Let

A_{\omega}=\sum_{mn:E_{mn}=\omega}A_{nm}|n\rangle\langle m|=\sum_{mn:E_{mn}=\omega}\Pi_{n}A\Pi_{m}=A_{-\omega}^{\dagger}\ ,

(4)

so that
3
3

3

The choice of the sign for this notation, as well as other notation choices, follow the textbook
[
2
]
, p.133-134.

A(t)=\sum_{\omega}A_{\omega}e^{-i\omega t}\ .

(5)

Here
\omega
runs over all the energy differences (Bohr frequencies) between the eigenvalues of
H
.

We assume henceforth that the bath is stationary —
[\rho_{\text{b}},H_{\text{b}}]=0
where
\rho_{\text{b}}
is the bath state — and introduce the bath correlation function
\mathcal{C}(t)
and its Fourier transform

\gamma(\omega)
, known as the bath spectral density:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{\text{b}}B(t)B]=\mathcal{C}^{*}(-t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\omega t}\gamma(\omega)d\omega

(6a)

\displaystyle\gamma(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{C}(t)e^{i\omega t}dt=f(\omega)+f^{*}(\omega)\ ,

(6b)

where
B(t)=e^{iH_{\text{b}}t}Be^{-iH_{\text{b}}t}
and

f(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}C(t)e^{i\omega t}dt=\frac{1}{2}\gamma(\omega)+iS(\omega)\ ,\quad S(\omega)=\frac{1}{2i}\left[f(\omega)-f^{*}(\omega)\right]=S^{*}(\omega)\ .

(7)

Note that the real-valued functions
S(\omega)
and
\gamma(\omega)
are related via a Kramers-Kronig transform

S(\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}\left(\frac{1}{\omega-\omega^{\prime}}\right)[\gamma(\omega^{\prime})]d\omega^{\prime}\ ,

(8)

where
\mathcal{P}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)[f]=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}\frac{f(x)}{x}dx
is the Cauchy principal value.
4
4

4

This follows from the identity
\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau e^{ix\tau}=\pi\delta(x)+i\mathcal{P}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)
.

When
A\otimes B
is replaced by
\sum_{i}A_{i}\otimes B_{i}
, we note the positivity of
\gamma_{ij}(\omega)=\int dte^{i\omega t}\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{\text{b}}B_{i}(t)B_{j}]
as a matrix in the indices
i,j
(of course this reduces to
\gamma(\omega)\geq 0
in the scalar case). This is a non-trivial fact that is usually associated with Bochner’s theorem, as e.g., in the textbook
[
2
]
. In Appendix
A
we give two different proofs, one using Bochner’s theorem and another that is direct. If we assume not only that the bath state is stationary, but that it is also in thermal equilibrium at inverse temperature
\beta
, i.e.,
\rho_{\text{b}}=e^{-\beta H_{\text{b}}}/\mathcal{Z}
,
\mathcal{Z}=\mathrm{Tr}e^{-\beta H_{\text{b}}}
, then it follows that the correlation function satisfies the time-domain Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) condition
[
2
,
44
]
:

C(t)=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{\text{b}}B(0)B(t+i\beta)]\ .

(9)

If in addition the correlation function is analytic in the strip between
t=-i\beta
and
t=0
, then it follows that the Fourier transform of the bath correlation function satisfies the frequency domain KMS condition
[
2
,
44
]
:

\gamma(-\omega)=e^{-\beta\omega}\gamma(\omega)\ \forall\omega\geq 0\ .

(10)

We note that the thermal equilibrium assumption is not necessary for the results we derive in this work, and we never use it in our proofs. We mention it here for completeness, and also since we use it in one of our dynamical decoupling examples later on. Finally, note that
\mathcal{C}(t)
has units of frequency squared.

2.2
The bath correlation time
\tau_{B}
and the fastest system decoherence timescale
\tau_{SB}

We define the two key quantities

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau_{SB}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}|\mathcal{C}(t)|dt

(11a)

\displaystyle\tau_{B}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{0}^{T}t|\mathcal{C}(t)|dt}{\int_{0}^{\infty}|\mathcal{C}(t)|dt}

(11b)

Here
T
is the total evolution time, used as a cutoff which can often be taken as
\infty
. We show later that in the interaction picture
\|\dot{\rho}\|_{1}\leq 4\|\rho\|_{1}/\tau_{SB}
, where
\rho
is the system density matrix and the trace norm defined in Eq. (
35
), so that we can interpret
\tau_{SB}
as the fastest system decoherence timescale, or timescale over which
\rho
changes due to the coupling to the bath, in the interaction picture (i.e., every other system decay timescale, including the standard
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
relaxation and dephasing times, must be longer). The quantity
\tau_{B}
is the characteristic timescale of the decay of
\mathcal{C}(t)
. We return in Sec.
6
to why we define these timescales in this manner, but note that Eq. (
11b
) becomes an identity if we choose
|\mathcal{C}(t)|\propto e^{-t/\tau_{B}}
and take the limit
T\to\infty
.

We further note that
\tau_{SB}
and
\tau_{B}
are the only two parameters relevant for determining the range of applicability of the various master equations; in particular, nothing about the norm or time-dependence of
H_{\text{tot}}
affects the accuracy of our time-dependent CGME, given below. In particular,
H_{\text{tot}}
can be arbitrarily large or small in the operator norm (strong coupling, unbounded bath), or have an arbitrarily large derivative (non-adiabatic regime). This remark is important in light of previous approaches, so we expand on it some more.

In previous work it was common to extract a dimensionful coupling parameter
g
, i.e., to replace
B
by
g\tilde{B}
, where
\|\tilde{B}\|=1
. One then defines
\tau_{B}=\int_{0}^{\infty}|\tilde{C}(t)|dt
in terms of a dimensionless correlation function
\tilde{C}(t)=C(t)/g^{2}

[
31
]
. One problem with this approach is the arbitrariness of distributing the numerical factors between
g
and
\tilde{B}
. Another is that it precludes unbounded baths, such as oscillator baths, for which
\|B\|=\infty
. In contrast, the formalism we present here is applicable even when
\|B\|
diverges. Furthermore,

\|B\|
contains an extra scale that does not carry any new information about the range of applicability of the master equations we discuss and derive. By not introducing this extra scale we highlight that there are only two free parameters in our analysis of the error:
\tau_{B}
and
\tau_{SB}
, and no other information about the bath is needed. I.e., even though different baths will lead to different equations, our results are universal for any bath with the same values of these two parameters (in fact only their dimensionless ratio matters).

Note that we can relate
\tau_{B}
and
\tau_{SB}
to the spectral density. First, using
\gamma(\omega)>0
and
C(t)=C^{*}(-t)
:

\gamma(\omega)=|\gamma(\omega)|\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|C(t)|dt=2\int_{0}^{\infty}|C(t)|dt=\frac{2}{\tau_{SB}}\ .

(12)

The dephasing time
T_{2}
and relaxation time
T_{1}
for a single qubit are usually defined as
\propto 1/\gamma(0)
and
\propto 1/\gamma(\omega)
respectively, where
\omega
is the qubit operating frequency (e.g., see Ref.
[
45
]
), so we see that
\tau_{SB}\lesssim T_{1},T_{2}
, which illustrates our earlier comment that
\tau_{SB}
is the fastest system decoherence time-scale.
Second,
\gamma^{\prime}(\omega)=i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}tC(t)e^{i\omega t}dt
, so that

|\gamma^{\prime}(\omega)|\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|t||C(t)|dt=2\int_{0}^{\infty}t|C(t)|dt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tiny{T\to\infty}}}{{=}}2\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\ ,

(13)

where the limit is taken in Eq. (
11b
).
And lastly,

\frac{d\gamma(-\omega)}{d\omega}=-\frac{d\gamma(\omega)}{d\omega}|_{-\omega}
, while differentiating the KMS condition yields
\frac{d\gamma(-\omega)}{d\omega}=-\beta e^{-\beta\omega}\gamma(\omega)+e^{-\beta\omega}\frac{d\gamma(\omega)}{d\omega}
, so that:

\gamma^{\prime}(0)=\frac{1}{2}\beta\gamma(0)\ .

(14)

This implies that
\gamma^{\prime}(0)>0
, so that under the KMS condition (
10
) we can replace Eq. (
13
) by

\beta\gamma(0)\leq 4\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\ .

(15)

The upper bounds on the approximation errors of all the master equations we present below involves the ratio
\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}
[see, e.g., Eqs. (
26
)-(
28
)]. The smallness of this ratio is sufficient for the CGME to be useful, while additional assumptions are required for the Davies-Lindblad equation and some versions of the Redfield equation.
It follows from Eq. (
15
) that for our bound to be small it is necessary that the temperature
\beta^{-1}
is not too low and/or the spectral density at
\omega=0
(roughly the same as the dephasing rate
T_{2}^{-1}
) is not too large.
The simple condition
\beta\gamma(0)\ll 1
already rules out diverging spectral densities such as the case of
1/f
noise, though this can be ameliorated by introducing a low-frequency cutoff.

We now present the three master equations, in the Schrödinger picture.

2.3
Redfield master equation

For the derivation see Sec.
3.2
.
This equation was known earlier than the Davies-Lindblad equation
[
19
]
, and is often used in quantum chemistry. It is not CP and hence cannot be put in Lindblad form:

{\dot{\rho}}_{R}(t)=-i[H,\rho_{R}(t)]+(A\rho_{R}A_{f}-\rho_{R}A_{f}A+\textrm{h.c.})\ ,

(16)

where we defined the “filtered" operator:

A_{f}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{C}(-t)A(-t)dt=\sum_{\omega}A_{\omega}\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathcal{C}(-t)e^{i\omega t}dt=\sum_{\omega}f^{*}(-\omega)A_{\omega}\ ,

(17)

where
f(\omega)
is defined in Eq. (
7
), and we used the subscript
R
to denote that the density matrix satisfies the Redfield equation (we use a similar subscript notation below to distinguish the solutions of different master equations). There is no natural way to separate the Lamb shift. One of the benefits of the Redfield equation is that there is only one generator
A_{f}
per interaction with the environment
A\otimes B
. We will show that as long as it preserves positivity, the Redfield equation is more accurate than the Davies-Lindblad master equation.

2.4
Davies-Lindblad master equation

For the derivation see Sec.
3.3
.
This is the familiar result
[
4
]

\displaystyle{\dot{\rho}}_{D}(t)

\displaystyle=-i[H+H_{\mathrm{LS}},\rho_{D}]+\sum_{\omega}\gamma(\omega)(A_{\omega}\rho_{D}A^{\dagger}_{\omega}-\frac{1}{2}\{\rho_{D},A^{\dagger}_{\omega}A_{\omega}\})\ ,

(18a)

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{LS}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\omega}S(\omega)A^{\dagger}_{\omega}A_{\omega}\ ,\quad S(\omega)=\frac{1}{2i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sgn(t)\mathcal{C}(t)e^{i\omega t}dt\ ,

(18b)

where
H_{\mathrm{LS}}
is the Lamb shift. We note that ad hoc forms of the Lindblad equation are often written down and used without justification, e.g., with just one Lindblad term per qubit (e.g.,
\sigma_{-}
or
I-\sigma_{z}

[
46
,
47
]
). In reality the Davies generators are derived from first principles, i.e., from the description of the total Hamiltonian of the system and bath. The number of Davies generators is unfortunately exponential in the number of qubits

:
\sum_{\omega}
is over all
4^{n}
energy differences.

Coarse-grained master equation (CGME) for time-independent or time-dependent system Hamiltonians

This is our main new set of results, generalizing Ref.

2.5.1
The time-independent case

For the derivation see Sec.
3.4
.
First, considering time-independent system Hamiltonians, we obtain:

{\dot{\rho}}_{C}(t)=-i[H+H_{\mathrm{LS}},\rho_{C}(t)]+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\epsilon\left(A_{\epsilon}\rho_{C}(t)A_{\epsilon}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\rho_{C}(t),A_{\epsilon}^{\dagger}A_{\epsilon}\right\}\right)\ ,

(19)

where the Lindblad operators are

\displaystyle A_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma(\epsilon)}{2\pi T_{a}}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}e^{i\epsilon t}A(t)dt

(20a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega}f(\epsilon,\omega)A_{\omega}\ ,\quad f(\epsilon,\omega)=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma(\epsilon)T_{a}}{2\pi}}\textrm{sinc}[T_{a}(\epsilon-\omega)/2]\ ,

(20b)

with

The same applies for the Lamb shift, which is

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{LS}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}F_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}A_{\omega^{\prime}}A_{\omega}

\displaystyle F_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2T_{a}\omega_{+}}\textrm{Re}\int_{0}^{T_{a}}d\theta\left(e^{i(\omega\theta-T_{a}\omega_{+})}-e^{-i(\omega^{\prime}\theta-T_{a}\omega_{+})}\right)\mathcal{C}(\theta)=F_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}^{*}=F_{-\omega^{\prime},-\omega}\ ,

(24)

2.6
Error bounds and range of applicability

The error bound of the Redfield master equation is

\|\rho_{\text{true}}(t)-\rho_{R}(t)\|_{1}\leq O\left(\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}e^{12t/\tau_{SB}}\right)\text{ln}\left(\frac{\tau_{SB}}{\tau_{B}}\right)\ ,

(26)

where
\rho_{\text{true}}(t)
denotes the true (approximation-free) state.

The error bound of the Davies-Lindblad master equation is

\|\rho_{\text{true}}(t)-\rho_{D}(t)\|_{1}\leq O\left(\left(\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau_{SB}\delta E}}\right)e^{12t/\tau_{SB}}\right)\ ,

(27)

where
\delta E=
min
{}_{i\neq j}|E_{i}-E_{j}|
is the level spacing, with
E_{i}
the eigenenergies of the system Hamiltonian
H
.

The error bound of both the time-independent and time-dependent
coarse-grained master equation is:
5
5

5

Strictly, our proof is only for the time-independent case, but there do not seem to be any obstacles for its generalization to the time-dependent case.

\displaystyle\|\rho_{\text{true}}(t)-\rho_{C}(t)\|_{1}\leq O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}}e^{6t/\tau_{SB}}\right)\ .

(28)

In the above expressions,
O(X)
is understood for
X\to 0
, specifically for CGME
X=\sqrt{\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}}e^{6t/\tau_{SB}}
. There is a subtlety in the definition of big-
O
notation that we would like to emphasize. By definition, “
f(X)=O(X)
at
X\to 0
” means there exist
X_{0},M
s.t. for any
X\leq X_{0}
we have
f(X)\leq MX
.
Applying this to Eq. (
28
), we have the following unpacking of the above statement:
there exist universal constants
x_{0},M
such that if
\sqrt{\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}}\leq X_{0}
and
t\leq(\tau_{SB}/6)\text{ln}(X_{0}/\sqrt{\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}})
, then
\|\rho_{\text{true}}(t)-\rho_{C}(t)\|_{1}\leq M\sqrt{\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}}e^{6t/\tau_{SB}}
holds. Note that for sufficiently small
\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}
, a dimensionless quantity
t/\tau_{SB}
can have an arbitrarily large constant value in this bound. The error can be made arbitrarily small at any fixed value of
t/\tau_{SB}
just by choosing a sufficiently small
\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}
. This is what is commonly referred to as the weak coupling limit.
We emphasize that the constants
X_{0},M
involved in this statement are universal, i.e. independent of the parameters of the equation such as the Hamiltonian and the initial state. In that sense, our bound is uniform. It does depend on time
t
as explicitly stated, so it is not uniform in time.
6
6

6

Note that in both the Davies-Lindblad and CGME cases the l.h.s. is bounded above by
2
due to the positivity of the density matrices; this is not necessarily true in the Redfield case.
Thus, comparing these bounds, we observe that the Redfield bound is the tightest, which is natural given that it involves the fewest approximations. However, recall that the Redfield master equation is not CP. When comparing the bounds on the solutions of the two CP master equations, we observe that unlike the Davies-Lindblad equation the CGME does not involve the energy gap, which means that the CGME in principle has a much larger range of applicability, in particular for systems whose gap shrinks with growing system size.
7
7

7

By range of applicability we mean the range of parameters over which the approximation is accurate. This range can be defined formally in terms of an upper bound on the right-hand side (r.h.s.), e.g.,
\sqrt{{\tau_{B}}/{\tau_{SB}}}e^{6t/\tau_{SB}}<\epsilon
for some fixed
\epsilon<1
.

While the equations and inequalities can be derived for any bath, we made some extra assumptions to prove the error bounds as presented above. Specifically, we assumed a Gaussian bath and the convergence of our series expansion for the Born error, as defined and discussed in detail in Sec.
6.2
. For a non-Gaussian bath, extra time scales relating to higher correlation functions generally appear, and the error bound can be derived by a straightforward generalization of our approach. It will contain, besides
\tau_{B}
and
\tau_{SB}
, additional terms involving those time scales.

3
(Re-)Derivation of the coarse-grained master equation

Our goal in this section is to present a compact and simplified derivation of the CGME found in Ref.
[
40
]
. This master equation is in Lindblad form but avoids making use of the RWA, which contributes the largest approximation error. Instead, the key idea is to introduce a coarse-graining (averaging) timescale, first exploited in this context in Refs.
[
20
,
22
]
. In this section we consider the case of a time-independent system Hamiltonian, and later generalize our derivation to the time-dependent case, which includes adiabatic evolution as a special case.

Along the way we also derive the Redfield and Davies-Lindblad master equations.

3.1
Setting up

We start from the total Hamiltonian given in Eq. (
3
), and let
V=A\otimes B
. Recall that
A
is dimensionless and
B
has units of energy.
The first few steps in our derivation are standard
[
2
]
. In the double system-bath interaction picture
V(t)=U_{\textrm{0}}^{\dagger}(t)VU_{\textrm{0}}(t)
[where
U_{\textrm{0}}

\dot{U}_{\textrm{0}}=-i(H\otimes I_{\text{b}}+I\otimes H_{\text{b}})U_{\textrm{0}},\penalty\ U_{0}(0)=1

\displaystyle{\dot{\rho}}_{\text{tot}}(t)

\displaystyle=-i[V(t),\rho_{\text{tot}}(t)]

(29a)

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{tot}}(t)

\displaystyle=\rho_{\text{tot}}(0)-i\int_{0}^{t}[V(\tau),\rho_{\text{tot}}(\tau)]d\tau.

(29b)

Substituting this back into the original equation yields:

{\dot{\rho}}_{\text{tot}}(t)=-i[V(t),\rho_{\text{tot}}(0)]-[V(t),\int_{0}^{t}[V(\tau),\rho_{\text{tot}}(\tau)]]d\tau.

(30)

The reduced system state in the interaction picture is defined as
\rho_{\textrm{true},I}(t)=\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{b}}[\rho_{\text{tot}}(t)]

\rho_{\textrm{true}}=U_{0}(t)\rho_{\textrm{true},I}U^{\dagger}_{0}(t)

\rho_{\text{tot}}(t)=\rho_{\textrm{true,I}}(t)\otimes\rho_{\text{b}}+\delta\rho_{\text{tot}},

together with the shift of

{\dot{\rho}}_{\textrm{true},I}(t)=-\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{b}}[A(t)\otimes B(t),\int_{0}^{t}[A(\tau)\otimes B(\tau),\rho_{\textrm{true,I}}(\tau)\otimes\rho_{\text{b}}]]d\tau+\mathcal{E}_{B}\ ,

(32)

which can be understood as the definition of the Born approximation error

\mathcal{E}_{B}\equiv{\dot{\rho}}_{\textrm{true},I}(t)+\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{b}}[A(t)\otimes B(t),\int_{0}^{t}[A(\tau)\otimes B(\tau),\rho_{\textrm{true,I}}(\tau)\otimes\rho_{\text{b}}]]d\tau,\quad\|\mathcal{E}_{B}\|_{1}=O\left(\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}^{2}}\right)\ .

(33)

Henceforth we assume that the bath correlation function decays rapidly, namely that
\tau_{B}\ll\tau_{SB}

\tau_{SB}
and
\tau_{B}
were defined in Eq. (
11

Now, let
\rho_{B,I}
denote the solution of the master equation after the Born approximation:

{\dot{\rho}}_{B,I}(t)=-\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{b}}[A(t)\otimes B(t),\int_{0}^{t}[A(\tau)\otimes B(\tau),\rho_{B}(\tau)\otimes\rho_{\text{b}}]]d\tau\ .

(34)

Later we collect other errors as letters next to

[Eq. (

We digress briefly to carefully explain the meaning of the norms and big-

notation used here, since this is important for the remainder of this work.
\mathcal{E}_{B}
is an operator acting on the system Hilbert space.
The trace norm
\|A\|_{1}
is:

\|A\|_{1}\equiv\mathrm{Tr}\sqrt{A^{\dagger}A}

(35)

The operator norm
\|X\|
is defined as follows:

\|X\|=\max_{i}\lambda_{X,i}

(36)

where
\lambda_{X,i}
are the eigenvalues of
|X|\equiv\sqrt{X^{\dagger}X}
(singular values of
X
) indexed by
i
.
The big-
O
notation
\mathcal{E}=O(x)
is taken for times such that
t/\tau_{SB}\leq M
and
x\to 0
, i.e., there exist an
M

\epsilon_{M}
and an
M
-dependent constant
C_{M}
such that for any
x<\epsilon_{M}
the error
\|\mathcal{E}\|_{1}\leq C_{M}x
.

It is natural to use the trace norm for density matrices. Indeed, if we want to find the deviation in an observable
O
relative to the difference between two states,
\delta\rho=\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}
, then

|\mathrm{Tr}O\delta\rho|\leq 2^{n}\|O\|\|\delta\rho\|,\quad|\mathrm{Tr}O\delta\rho|\leq\|O\|\|\delta\rho\|_{1}\ .

(37)

The second expression gives a tighter bound, if we manage to have the same bound on

as on
\|\delta\rho\|
. Fortunately, this will turn out to be the case in this work, and was already observed for the Markov error in Ref.
[
31
]
. The key property used to prove the inequality above is submultiplicativity, valid for any unitarily invariant norm
[
48
]
:

\|AB\|_{1}\leq\|A\|\|B\|_{1}\ .

(38)

For simplicity, we assume the bath state to be stationary, such that
\mathcal{C}(t,t^{\prime})=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{B}B(t)B(t^{\prime})]
is invariant with respect to shifts in time of both arguments.
8
8

8

The derivation can potentially be extended to the general case of a two-time correlation function, i.e., without assuming translational invariance; indeed the derivation in
[
40

does not.
We can then replace
\mathcal{C}(t,t^{\prime})
by
\mathcal{C}(t)=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{B}B(t)B]

\mathcal{C}^{*}(t)=\mathcal{C}(-t)
. After expanding the commutators, one arrives at

{\dot{\rho}}_{B,I}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{C}(\tau-t)[A(t)\rho_{B,I}(\tau)A(\tau)-\rho_{B,I}(\tau)A(\tau)A(t)]d\tau+\textrm{h.c.}\equiv\int_{0}^{t}K^{B,2}_{t-\tau}(\rho_{B,I}(\tau))d\tau\ ,

where

Taking the trace norm, we note that:

\|\dot{\rho}_{B,I}\|_{1}\leq 4c_{B}\int_{0}^{\infty}|\mathcal{C}(t)|dt=4c_{B}/\tau_{SB},

where we used our earlier choice of setting

(41)

We next introduce the Markov approximation

\displaystyle{\dot{\rho}}_{B,I}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{C}(\tau-t)[A(t)\rho_{B,I}(t)A(\tau)-\rho_{B,I}(t)A(\tau)A(t)]d\tau+\textrm{h.c.}+\mathcal{E}_{M},\quad\|\mathcal{E}_{M}\|_{1}=O\left(\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}^{2}}\right)

(42a)

\displaystyle{\dot{\rho}}_{BM,I}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{C}(\tau-t)[A(t)\rho_{BM,I}(t)A(\tau)-\rho_{BM,I}(t)A(\tau)A(t)]d\tau+\textrm{h.c.}=\mathcal{L}^{BM,I}_{t}(\rho_{BM,I}(t))

(42b)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\forall t,\penalty\ \|\rho_{\text{test}}\|_{1}=1:\penalty\ \|\mathcal{L}^{BM,I}_{t}(\rho_{\text{test}})\|_{1}\leq 4/\tau_{SB}

Equation (

\displaystyle{\dot{\rho}}_{\text{true},I}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{C}(\tau-t)[A(t)\rho_{\text{true},I}(t)A(\tau)-\rho_{\text{true},I}(t)A(\tau)A(t)]d\tau+\textrm{h.c.}+\mathcal{E}_{B}+\mathcal{E}_{M}(\rho_{\text{true}}),

(43a)

In this particular case, using methods from Section

3.2

Let us digress briefly in order to establish the form of the Redfield equation presented in Sec.

3.3
Davies-Lindblad master equation

As a second digression let us establish the form of the Davies-Lindblad equation presented in Sec.
2

We start from the form given in Eq. (
44

\omega t+\omega^{\prime}\tau=\omega(t-\tau)+(\omega+\omega^{\prime})\tau

Next we apply the RWA, in which one considers the term

to be rapidly oscillating and hence self-cancelling, so the non-negligible contribution is only due to the terms with
\omega^{\prime}=-\omega

(53)

where we replaced
\rho_{BM,I}
by
\rho_{D,I}
and
\omega^{\prime}
by

The error
\mathcal{E}\sim O(1/\tau_{SB})
between the right-hand sides of Eqs. (

\rho_{BM,I}(t)
and
\rho_{D,I}(t)
remain close (see Sec.

We now transform back to the Schrödinger picture (recall that this requires multiplying each

\displaystyle=-i[H,\rho_{D}]+\sum_{\omega}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left(\mathcal{C}(t^{\prime})e^{i\omega t^{\prime}}(A_{\omega}\rho_{D}A_{\omega}^{\dagger}-\rho_{D}A_{\omega}^{\dagger}A_{\omega})+\mathcal{C}^{*}(t^{\prime})e^{-i\omega t^{\prime}}(A_{\omega}\rho_{D}A_{\omega}^{\dagger}-A_{\omega}^{\dagger}A_{\omega}\rho_{D})\right)dt^{\prime}.

(54)

\mathcal{C}^{*}(t)=\mathcal{C}(-t)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}dt^{\prime}\mathcal{C}(t^{\prime})e^{i\omega t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\gamma(\omega)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\sgn(t^{\prime})\mathcal{C}(t^{\prime})e^{i\omega t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}dt^{\prime}\mathcal{C}^{*}(t^{\prime})e^{-i\omega t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\gamma(\omega)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\sgn(t^{\prime})\mathcal{C}(t^{\prime})e^{i\omega t^{\prime}}\ .

(55b)

Using the decomposition given by Eq. (

]

3.4
Coarse-grained master equation

Let us now return to our main goal in this section, the derivation of the CGME.
At this point we deviate from the standard derivations and instead follow the coarse-graining approach
[
20
,
22
,
40
]
. There are two main steps: (i) Time-averaging of the state, which is done in lieu of the RWA (and reduces to the RWA in the limit of large time-averaging scale; see Appendix
C
), (ii) removal of a small part of the integration domain, which restores complete positivity (new in this work). We describe each in turn, along with the associated error.

3.4.1
Time-averaging of the state

Before we leave the interaction picture, we coarse-grain, or time-average over
T_{a}
such that
\tau_{B}\ll T_{a}\ll\tau_{SB}
. Specifically, we consider another equation satisfied by a new state
\rho_{BMT,I}(t)
, where the time-averaging operation
\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{t-T_{a}/2}^{t+T_{a}/2}dt^{\prime}
is applied to the r.h.s. of Eq. (
44
) taken at time
t^{\prime}
, except that the state is still at time
t
on the l.h.s.:

{\dot{\rho}}_{BMT,I}(t)=\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{t-T_{a}/2}^{t+T_{a}/2}dt^{\prime}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}d\tau\mathcal{C}(\tau-t^{\prime})e^{-i(\omega t^{\prime}+\omega^{\prime}\tau)}(A_{\omega}\rho_{BMT,I}(t)A_{\omega^{\prime}}-\rho_{BMT,I}(t)A_{\omega^{\prime}}A_{\omega})+\textrm{h.c.}

(56)

Alas, the associated error
\mathcal{E}_{T}
has
\|\mathcal{E}_{T}\|_{1}=O(1/\tau_{SB})
, which is not small. The reason is that the fast-rotating terms may have a large derivative. What can be proven is that the solutions of Eqs. (
44
) and (
56
) remain close:

\|\rho_{BM,I}(t)-\rho_{BMT,I}(t)\|_{1}=O(T_{a}/\tau_{SB})\ ,

(57)

where
t\leq c\tau_{SB}
, and the r.h.s.
O(T_{a}/\tau_{SB})
does not depend on
t
, only on
c
, which is considered a constant for the purposes of big-
O
notation.
The proof is given in Sec.
6.4
, in Lemma
2
(this analysis first appeared in Ref.
[
32
]
).
The error we track is now the error of the solution, not its derivative.

We use the following fact about time-local (Markovian) and time-nonlocal (non-Markovian) differential equations:

Lemma 1
.

Assume that

\dot{x}(t)=\mathcal{L}(x(t))+\mathcal{E},\quad\dot{y}(t)=\mathcal{L}(y(t)),\quad x(0)=y(0)\ ,

(58)

where
\mathcal{L}
is a linear superoperator and

\Lambda
is a positive constant such that
\sup_{\tau,x:\|x\|_{1}=1}\|\mathcal{L}_{\tau}(x)\|_{1}\leq\Lambda
.
Or, assume that

\dot{x}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}K_{t-\tau}(x(\tau))d\tau+\mathcal{E},\quad\dot{y}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}K_{t-\tau}(y(\tau))d\tau,\quad x(0)=y(0)\ ,

(59)

where
K_{t-\tau}(x)
is a linear superoperator and
\Lambda
is a positive constant such that
\sup_{t,x(\tau):\|x(\tau)\|_{1}=1}\int_{0}^{t}\|K_{t-\tau}(x(\tau))\|_{1}d\tau\leq\Lambda
.
Then:

\forall t\leq\frac{c}{\Lambda}:\penalty\ \|x(t)-y(t)\|_{1}\leq(e^{c}-1)\frac{\|\mathcal{E}\|_{1}}{\Lambda}\ .

(60)

For the proof see Sec.
6.1
.
We can take
\Lambda=4/\tau_{SB}
because of Eq. (
41
,
42c
). Using this, we rewrite the result of the Lemma as follows, for brevity: if
t\leq c/\Lambda=c\tau_{SB}/4
, then

3.5
Ranges of applicability of the three master equations

We now discuss the ranges of applicability of the master equations derived above in more rigor than in Sec.
2.6
, while postponing the derivations to Sec.
6
. Recall that the ranges are dependent on the timescales
\tau_{SB}
and
\tau_{B}
defined in Eq. (
11

Errors are incurred from different approximations made along the way. The very first approximation made in the derivation of all three master equations is the Born approximation (see Sec.
3.1
). Unlike the other approximations, we do not prove a rigorous error bound for the Born approximation error; we just provide a bound on the first order contribution to this error. However, under the assumption that the error converges in the first place, the Born error will turn out to be subleading. Therefore we settle for an estimate of this error for the sake of simplicity, but we note that a rigorous bound can be obtained
[
4
]
.

We first collect all the errors introduced in the derivation of the CGME: the difference in solutions
\|\rho_{\text{true},I}(t)-\rho_{BMT,I}(t)\|_{1}
[Eq. (
61b
), which includes the error due to time-averaging], and the error due to enforcing complete positivity,
\|\mathcal{E}_{p}\|_{1}=O(\tau_{B}/(T_{a}\tau_{SB}))
[Eq. (
66
)]. The difference between the solution of the CGME
\rho_{C}(t)
and the true time evolved state
\rho_{\text{true}}(t)
thus satisfies (note that the norm is unitarily invariant and hence unaffected by transformation to or out of the interaction picture):

\displaystyle\|\rho_{\text{true}}(t)-\rho_{C}(t)\|_{1}\leq\|\rho_{\text{true},I}(t)-\rho_{BMT,I}(t)\|_{1}+\|\rho_{BMT}(t)-\rho_{C}(t)\|_{1}

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{T_{a}+\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\right)+O(\tau_{SB}\|\mathcal{E}_{p}\|_{1})

(74a)

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{\tau_{B}}{T_{a}}+\frac{T_{a}+\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\right)

(74b)

Here we used Lemma
1
to multiply
\|\mathcal{E}_{p}\|_{1}
by
\tau_{SB}
.
Optimizing

to minimize this error, we obtain

, and:

\|\rho_{\text{true}}(t)-\rho_{C}(t)\|_{1}=O\left(2\sqrt{\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}}+\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\right)=O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}}\right)\quad\text{for}\ t>T_{a}/2\ .

(75)

Note that if we define the coupling

(recall the discussion in Sec.

We can in fact improve on the result presented in Eq. (

The time-averaging window leading to Eq. (

Let us now discuss the time-dependence of the error. From the above expression it follows that for any

[recall Eq. (

and the Davies-Lindblad equation:

\|\rho_{\text{true}}(t)-\rho_{D}(t)\|_{1}=O\left(\left(\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{\tau_{SB}\delta E}}\right)e^{12t/\tau_{SB}}\right)

We note that for Eq. (

It is important to emphasize that the solutions of open system master equations do not actually grow exponentially. In fact, for time-independent system Hamiltonians all of the equations presented have steady states to which they converge in the operator and trace norm at some rate. If that rate is sufficiently fast, one can prove a much stronger result
[
41

than our error bounds: a stability of the open system dynamics to small perturbations. The stability can be expressed in terms of a time-independent error bound on the difference in solutions.

We remark that the coefficients we presented and the time-dependence of the error are explicit, while other work hides them in big-
O
notation. Our results allow one to put error bars on the numerical solution of the above equations.

3.6
Discretization, spatial locality, and the Lieb-Robinson bound

Different forms of the CGME are preferred depending on the application. The form given in Eq. (
71

\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}

5

n
to store the density matrix, e.g., matrix product states (MPS)
[
50
,
51
]
. For a recent implementation of these methods see
[
46
,
47
]

In Eq. (

\epsilon

We now proceed to show that storing the Lindblad operators

\mathrm{Tr}_{\overline{i,j}}H-\mathrm{Tr}_{\overline{i}}H-\mathrm{Tr}_{\overline{j}}H

The computation of any observable in
|\psi(t,r)\rangle
requires a contraction of such a tensor network, which takes
O(n)e^{O(t)}
computational time, and
e^{O(t)}
memory. In practice, MPS calculations employ a truncation scheme for the bond dimension after every time step, which fixes the bond dimension to a constant and leads to
O(n,t)
computational time,
O(n)
memory. Such a method is generally not a controlled approximation, but it may be possible to develop a special version with an error estimate. Averaging over randomness adds an extra computational cost to either method. We leave the development of further details to a future publication.

The simultaneous locality and positivity of Eq. (
81
) is a new result: previously, the Davies generators were only local for commuting Hamiltonians, and the Redfield master equation leads to completely positive evolution only for flat spectral densities. Thus, this is the first method for
ab initio
completely positive open system evolution of MPSs.

3.7
Scaling with size

Here we discuss the system-size dependence. So far we assumed that there is only one term
V=A\otimes B
describing the interaction with the bath. In reality, there are
n
terms like this, one for each qubit. If qubits are coupled to the same bath
V=\sum_{i}^{n}A_{i}\otimes B
, it will lead to correlated noise (collective decoherence
[
55
]
). If the qubits are coupled to different baths
V=\sum_{i}^{n}A_{i}\otimes B_{i}
, it can lead to uncorrelated noise if
\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{B}B_{i}(t)B_{j}]=0
. We will consider the latter case since it contains the former case if one sets
B_{i}=B
(though distinctly different phenomena such as decoherence-free subspaces appear in this special case
[
56
,
57
]
). The form of the Davies-Lindblad and Redfield equations can be found elsewhere; here we present only the form of the CGME. The sum is carried through to the frequency form of the equation — Eq. (
71
):

\displaystyle{\dot{\rho}_{C}}(t)

\displaystyle=-i[H+H_{\mathrm{LS}},\rho_{C}]+\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime},i,j}\gamma^{ij}_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}(A_{\omega}^{j}\rho_{C}A_{\omega^{\prime}}^{i}-\frac{1}{2}\{\rho_{C},A_{\omega^{\prime}}^{i}A_{\omega}^{j}\})\ ,

(86a)

\displaystyle\gamma^{ij}_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt^{\prime}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}d\tau^{\prime}\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\tau^{\prime}-t^{\prime})e^{-i(\omega t^{\prime}+\omega^{\prime}\tau^{\prime})}\ ,

(86b)

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{LS}}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2T_{a}}\sum_{ij}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{1}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{2}\ \sgn(t_{1}-t_{2})\mathcal{C}_{ij}(t_{2}-t_{1})A^{i}(t_{2})A^{j}(t_{1})\ ,

(86c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{ij}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{B}B^{i}(t)B^{j}]\ .

(86d)

The theoretically optimal
T_{a}=\sqrt{\tau_{SB}\tau_{B}/5}
is given by the same expression in terms of time scales
\tau_{B}
and
\tau_{SB}
, which are now defined using
\int|\mathcal{C}|\mapsto\text{max}_{ij}\int|\mathcal{C}_{ij}|
. Such a definition keeps
\tau_{B}
and
\tau_{SB}
consistent with the single qubit quantities. The locality of the equation is now also dependent on the decay of
\mathcal{C}_{ij}
with the distance between
i
and
j
. If it can be approximated by zero outside some radius (“strong locality”), the equation remains locally generated in the sense that every term on the r.h.s. contains local terms around some qubit
i
conjugating the density matrix, and there are polynomially many of them. If we use a weaker sense of locality, when the operators on the right and left of the density matrix are allowed to be local around different
i,j
, and neglect the Lamb shift, then no assumption on
\mathcal{C}_{ij}
is needed.

Let us present an explicitly Lindblad form. Let
\mu
index the positive (see Appendix
A
) eigenvalues of
\gamma_{ij}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega s}\mathcal{C}_{ij}(s)ds
:

\gamma_{ij}(\omega)=\sum_{\mu}U_{\mu i}^{*}(\omega)D_{\mu}(\omega)U_{\mu j}(\omega)\ .

(87)

This allows one to write:

\displaystyle\gamma^{ij}_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\int d\epsilon\sum_{\mu}U_{\mu i}^{*}(\epsilon){\tilde{\gamma}}_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}(D_{\mu}(\epsilon),\epsilon)U_{\mu j}(\epsilon)

(88a)

\displaystyle{\tilde{\gamma}}_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}(D_{\mu}(\epsilon),\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt^{\prime}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}d\tau^{\prime}D_{\mu}(\epsilon)e^{-i(\epsilon(\tau^{\prime}-t^{\prime})+\omega t^{\prime}+\omega^{\prime}\tau^{\prime})}\ .

(88b)

Next, note that if we let
\tilde{A}=\sum_{j}U_{\mu j}(\epsilon)A_{j}
, the previous derivation goes through.

{\dot{\rho}}_{C}(t)=-i[H+H_{\mathrm{LS}},\rho_{C}(t)]+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\epsilon\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\left(A_{\epsilon,\mu}\rho_{C}(t)A_{\epsilon,\mu}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\rho_{C}(t),A_{\epsilon,\mu}^{\dagger}A_{\epsilon,\mu}\right\}\right)\ ,

(89)

where the Lindblad operators are

\displaystyle A_{\epsilon,\mu}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma(\epsilon)}{2\pi T_{a}}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}\sum_{j}e^{i\epsilon t}U_{\mu j}(\epsilon)A^{j}(t)dt

(90a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega,j}f(\epsilon,\mu,j,\omega)A^{j}_{\omega}\ ,\quad f(\epsilon,\mu,j,\omega)=\sqrt{\frac{D_{\mu}(\epsilon)T_{a}}{2\pi}}\textrm{sinc}[T_{a}(\epsilon-\omega)/2]U_{\mu j}(\epsilon)\ .

(90b)

These results generalize Eqs. (
19
)-(
20
) to the
n
-qubit setting. Now the locality is more directly defined: if the operators
A_{\epsilon,\mu}
can be approximately truncated to a ball around some point for each
\mu
, then the CGME for
n
qubits is local in the strong sense described above; otherwise it is local in a weak sense. In any case, it has
n
times more generator terms than the single-qubit CGME. The latter has a constant number of terms after the discretization described in Appendix
D
.

Let us now discuss the range of applicability of the
n
-qubit CGME. In the case of correlated noise, the norm of each
A
effectively grows by a factor of
n
. Thus the bound on the relaxation time becomes
\Lambda\sim n^{2}/\tau_{SB}
with
\tau_{SB}
taken from the single qubit case. For uncorrelated noise, the bound is
\Lambda\sim n/\tau_{SB}
. In both cases, the range of applicability can be derived by directly repeating the derivation given in Sec.
6
:

\|\rho_{C}(t)-\rho_{\text{true}}(t)\|_{1}\leq O(\sqrt{\Lambda\tau_{B}}e^{1.5\Lambda t})\ ,

(91)

where
\tau_{B}
is system-independent, and thus is the same as the single qubit quantity. The apparently shrinking range with
n
should not discourage us, since it can be mitigated under the assumption of locality. Namely, one can prove that the error in local density matrices obeys a stronger bound consistent with the isolated qubit result. Let us note that in an
n
-qubit system there are in fact nonlocal modes that relax with the rate
n/\tau_{SB}
, or
n^{2}/\tau_{SB}
for correlated noise (e.g., superradiance), as well as modes whose relaxation is completely suppressed (e.g., subradiance, or decoherence-free subspaces, as mentioned above). These phenomena are well known
[
58
]
and experimentally documented
[
59
,
60
,
61
,
62
]
.

The benefit expressed by Eq. (
91
) is that the requirement on the single qubit
\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}
value
switches from being exponential in the system size for the Davies-Lindblad case to polynomial for the CGME and the Redfield master equation. In terms of the coupling strength, we found these ranges of applicability at the relevant times of
\Lambda^{-1}
to be between
\sqrt{n}g\tau_{B}\ll 1
and
ng\tau_{B}\ll 1
depending on the correlations in the noise. For local observables the earlier range
g\tau_{B}\ll 1
for times
\sim\tau_{SB}
is expected to hold. Here the inequality notation (
\ll
) guarantees that the error
\|\delta\rho\|_{1}
or
\|\delta\rho_{\text{loc}}\|_{1}
is small.

4
The CGME with a time-dependent system Hamiltonian

We start again from the total Hamiltonian (
3
), but this time we let the system Hamiltonian be time-dependent:
H(t)
.
We denote the interaction term
A\otimes B
by
V
. To go to the interaction picture, we now need to specify both the start time
t_{i}
and the final time
t\leq t_{f}
. The unitary propagator
U_{tt_{i}}
satisfies

\frac{dU_{tt_{i}}}{dt}=-iH(t)U_{tt_{i}},\quad U_{t_{i}t_{i}}=I\ .

(92)

The formal solution is

U_{t_{f}t_{i}}=\mathcal{T}\textrm{exp}\left(-i\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}H(t)dt\right)\ ,

(93)

where
\mathcal{T}
represents forward time-ordering.
Some basic additional facts are:

U_{t_{3}t_{1}}=U_{t_{3}t_{2}}U_{t_{2}t_{1}}\ ,\quad\frac{dU_{tt_{i}}}{dt_{i}}=iU_{tt_{i}}H(t)\ ,\quad U_{t_{1}t_{2}}^{\dagger}=U_{t_{2}t_{1}}\ .

(94)

The relation between the Schrödinger picture and the interaction picture is:

\rho(t)=U_{t0}\rho_{I}(t)U_{0t},\quad V_{I}=V(t)=A(t,0)\otimes B(t)=U_{0t}AU_{t0}\otimes B(t)\ .

(95)

Here
B(t)
is given by the bath Hamiltonian in the same way as in the previous derivation. We again assume the bath state to be stationary for simplicity, although the derivation will go through without it. At this point we repeat the steps involved in Eqs. (
29
)-(
42b
), with the only change being that the
A(t)
operators now acquire a second time variable. Namely, Eq. (
42b
) — the Redfield equation in the interaction picture — is replaced by

\displaystyle{\dot{\rho}}_{B,I}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{C}(\tau-t)(A(t,0)\rho_{B,I}(t)A(\tau,0)-\rho_{B,I}(t)A(\tau,0)A(t,0))d\tau+\textrm{h.c.}+\mathcal{E}_{M}

(96a)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\left[\mathcal{C}(\tau-t)(A(t,0)\rho_{B,I}(t)A(\tau,0)-\rho_{B,I}(t)A(\tau,0)A(t,0))\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\qquad+\mathcal{C}(t-\tau)(A(\tau,0)\rho_{B,I}(t)A(t,0)-A(t,0)A(\tau,0)\rho_{B,I}(t))\right]+\mathcal{E}_{M}\ ,

(96b)

where we again introduced the Markov approximation
\rho_{B,I}(\tau)\mapsto\rho_{B,I}(t)
.

Let us digress briefly to derive the time-dependent Redfield equation. To do so, we transform Eq. (
96
) out of the interaction picture:

{\dot{\rho}}_{BM}(t)=-i[H(t),\rho_{BM}]+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{C}(\tau-t)(A\rho_{B}(t)A(\tau,t)-\rho_{B}(t)A(\tau,t)A+\textrm{h.c.})d\tau\ .

(97)

Changing variables
t-\tau=t^{\prime}
and switching the
dt^{\prime}
integration limit to
t\to\infty
we obtain:

{\dot{\rho}}_{R}(t)=-i[H(t),\rho_{R}]+(A\rho_{B}(t)A_{f}(t)-\rho_{B}(t)A_{f}(t)Ad\tau+\textrm{h.c.}),\quad A_{f}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{C}(-t^{\prime})A(t-t^{\prime},t)dt^{\prime}\ ,

(98)

which is the time-dependent Redfield equation.

We now proceed with essentially the same series of transformations we used in the derivation for the time-independent Hamiltonian in Sec.
3.1
. The main difference is that there are now additional time variables.
Similarly as in the transition to Eq. (
56
), we time-average the r.h.s. by shifting
t\mapsto t+t_{1}
and applying

\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{1}
.
The new equation is:

\displaystyle{\dot{\rho}}_{BMT,I}(t)=\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t+t_{1}}d\tau\left[\mathcal{C}(\tau-t-t_{1})(A(t+t_{1},0)\rho_{BMT,I}(t)A(\tau,0)-\rho_{BMT,I}(t)A(\tau,0)A(t+t_{1},0))\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\mathcal{C}(t+t_{1}-\tau)(A(\tau,0)\rho_{BMT,I}(t)A(t+t_{1},0)-A(t+t_{1},0)A(\tau,0)\rho_{BMT,I}(t))\right]\ ,

(99)

and as long as
T_{a}
is small compared to the fastest timescale over which
\rho_{I}(t)
changes, the associated error
\|\rho_{BM}(t)-\rho_{BMT}(t)\|_{1}
is small (see Sec.
6.4
for a detailed analysis of the time-independent case).
We now rotate out of the interaction picture using Eq. (
95
):

\displaystyle{\dot{\rho}}_{BMT}(t)

\displaystyle=-i[H(t),\rho_{BMT}(t)]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t+t_{1}}d\tau\left[\mathcal{C}(\tau-t-t_{1})(A(t+t_{1},t)\rho_{BMT}(t)A(\tau,t)-\rho_{BMT}(t)A(\tau,t)A(t+t_{1},t))\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\mathcal{C}(t+t_{1}-\tau)(A(\tau,t)\rho_{BMT}(t)A(t+t_{1},t)-A(t+t_{1},t)A(\tau,t)\rho_{BMT}(t))\right]\ .

(100)

Next, we change variables to
t_{2}=\tau-t
:

\displaystyle{\dot{\rho}}_{BMT}(t)

\displaystyle=-i[H(t),\rho(t)]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{1}\int_{-t}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\left[\mathcal{C}(t_{2}-t_{1})(A(t+t_{1},t)\rho_{BMT}(t)A(t+t_{2},t)-\rho_{BMT}(t)A(t+t_{2},t)A(t+t_{1},t))\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\mathcal{C}(t_{1}-t_{2})(A(t+t_{2},t)\rho_{BMT}(t)A(t+t_{1},t)-A(t+t_{1},t)A(t+t_{2},t)\rho_{BMT}(t))\right]\ .

(101)

The transformations we performed after coarse graining of the Redfield equation (
96
) were exact. We know what to do to recover complete positivity: as in the transition to Eq. (
65a
) in the time-independent case, we need to neglect a part of the integral by changing the lower limit from
-t
to
-T_{a}/2
:

\displaystyle{\dot{\rho}}_{BMT}(t)=-i[H(t),\rho_{BMT}(t)]+P_{1}+P_{2}-\rho_{BMT}(t)Q_{1}-Q_{2}\rho_{BMT}(t)+\mathcal{E}_{p},

(102)

where we defined

\displaystyle P_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{1}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\mathcal{C}(t_{2}-t_{1})A(t+t_{1},t)\rho_{BMT}(t)A(t+t_{2},t)

(103a)

\displaystyle P_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{1}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\mathcal{C}(t_{1}-t_{2})A(t+t_{2},t)\rho_{BMT}(t)A(t+t_{1},t)

(103b)

\displaystyle Q_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{1}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\mathcal{C}(t_{2}-t_{1})A(t+t_{2},t)A(t+t_{1},t)

(103c)

\displaystyle Q_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{1}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\mathcal{C}(t_{1}-t_{2})A(t+t_{1},t)A(t+t_{2},t)\ .

(103d)

The same estimates on the error incurred by doing this apply, i.e., this introduces an error of order
\|\mathcal{E}_{p}(\text{const}\cdot\tau_{SB})\|=O(\tau_{B}/T_{a})
. Complete positivity yet remains to be demonstrated.
To do so, we first note that we may interchange the order of integration since
\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{1}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}=\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{2}\int_{t_{2}}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{1}
. We then swap the integration variables
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
and obtain:

\displaystyle P_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{1}\int_{t_{1}}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{2}\mathcal{C}(t_{2}-t_{1})A(t+t_{1},t)\rho_{BMT}(t)A(t+t_{2},t)

(104a)

\displaystyle Q_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{1}\int_{t_{1}}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{2}\mathcal{C}(t_{2}-t_{1})A(t+t_{2},t)\rho_{BMT}(t)A(t+t_{1},t)\ ,

(104b)

so that:

\displaystyle P_{1}+P_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{1}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{2}\mathcal{C}(t_{2}-t_{1})A(t+t_{1},t)\rho_{BMT}(t)A(t+t_{2},t)

(105a)

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\equiv Q_{1}+Q_{2}=\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{1}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{2}\mathcal{C}(t_{2}-t_{1})A(t+t_{2},t)A(t+t_{1},t)

(105b)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\equiv-i(Q_{1}-Q_{2})=\frac{1}{iT_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{1}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{2}\sgn(t_{1}-t_{2})\mathcal{C}(t_{2}-t_{1})A(t+t_{2},t)A(t+t_{1},t)\ ,

(105c)

and note that
X
and
Y
are both Hermitian.
Also,
Q_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(X+iY)
and
Q_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(X-iY)
, so that

\rho_{BMT}Q_{1}+Q_{2}\rho_{BMT}=-\frac{i}{2}[Y,\rho_{BMT}]+\frac{1}{2}\{X,\rho_{BMT}\}\ .

(106)

Combining Eq. (
102
) with the expressions in Eqs. (
105
)-(
106
), we thus find

\displaystyle{\dot{\rho}}_{C}(t)=-i[H(t)+H_{\mathrm{LS}}(t),\rho_{C}(t)]+\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{1}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{2}C(t_{2}-t_{1})[A(t+t_{1},t)\rho_{C}(t)A(t+t_{2},t)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\{A(t+t_{2},t)A(t+t_{1},t),\rho_{C}(t)\}]\ ,

(107)

where

H_{\mathrm{LS}}(t)\equiv-\frac{1}{2}Y=\frac{i}{2T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{1}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt_{2}\sgn(t_{1}-t_{2})\mathcal{C}(t_{2}-t_{1})A(t+t_{2},t)A(t+t_{1},t)\ .

(108)

Finally, we establish complete positivity by again introducing the Fourier transform
C(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\epsilon t}\gamma(\epsilon)d\epsilon
[Eq. (
6a
)], where

\gamma(\epsilon)>0
(Appendix
A
). In analogy with Eq. (
20
) for the time-independent case, let us also define

A_{\epsilon}(t)\equiv\sqrt{\frac{\gamma(\epsilon)}{2\pi T_{a}}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}e^{i\epsilon t_{1}}A(t+t_{1},t)dt_{1}\ .

(109)

We then obtain from Eq. (
107
) a master equation in exactly the same form as the time-independent CGME [Eq. (
19
)], except that all the operators now depend on time:

{\dot{\rho}}_{C}(t)=-i[H(t)+H_{\mathrm{LS}}(t),\rho_{C}]+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\epsilon\left(A_{\epsilon}(t)\rho_{C}A_{\epsilon}^{\dagger}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\rho_{C},A_{\epsilon}^{\dagger}(t)A_{\epsilon}(t)\right\}\right)\ .

(110)

This result, which is in a manifestly completely positive form, establishes that the CGME is valid also for arbitrary time-dependent system Hamiltonians. The time-dependent CGME, Eq. (
110
) [also stated earlier as Eq. (
22
)], is one of the main new results of this work.

5
Applications and Examples

5.1
Application: dynamical decoupling

One interesting application of our time-dependent CGME is to study methods to reduce decoherence. One such method is dynamical decoupling (DD)
[
63
,
64
]
— the use of pulse sequences to cancel out the system-bath interaction (for reviews see
[
65
,
66
]
). Even though the master equation has a Markovian appearance, the underlying bath can have a nonzero correlation time, and can therefore describe the effects of DD. At one level this might appear to be a counterintuitive result since it is widely accepted that in the limit
\mathcal{C}(t)=\delta(t)
DD is useless, and hence one might naively expect that one needs to use non-Markovian methods (e.g., the Magnus expansion to second order or higher
[
67
]
) to describe the benefits of DD. However, it has recently been recognized that DD can in fact work in the Markovian setting as well, specifically the Davies-Lindblad master equation
[
68
]
, and a more abstract semigroup framework
[
69
]
(see also Refs.
[
70
,
71
]
). Our contribution here is to establish this in the framework of the Redfield master equation and, more importantly, the CGME. This ensures a wider range of applicability than the Davies-Lindblad master equation result. Since our result is obtained in a strictly Markovian setting, we differ with the conclusion reported in Ref.
[
72
]
, that “success, however limited, of DD is a meaningful concept of non-Markovianity".

We consider two examples, both for a qubit with
H=0
coupled to a purely dephasing environment:

H_{\textrm{tot}}=Z\otimes B+I\otimes H_{\text{b}}\ ,

(111)

where
Z\equiv\sigma^{z}
is the Pauli
z
-matrix.
The noise model (
111
) can be decoupled with a sequence of
X
pulses spaced by
\Delta t
. Here by a pulse we mean an instantaneous unitary being applied to the qubit, through the action of an externally controlled, time-dependent qubit Hamiltonian: instead of
H=0
we have a time-dependent system Hamiltonian
H(t)=\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{j}\delta(t-j\Delta t)X
, where the prefactor of
\pi/2
is chosen so that
X
-pulses are generated.

5.1.1
Example 1: time-dependent Redfield master equation with a rectangle bath correlation function

Consider the rectangle bath correlation function

\mathcal{C}(t)=g^{2}\theta(\tau_{c}-|t|)\ ,

(112)

where we have explicitly introduced the coupling strength
g
.
To analyze this problem it will suffice to use the time-dependent version of the Redfield equation, Eq. (
98
).
We thus need to compute

A_{f}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{C}(t^{\prime})A(t-t^{\prime},t)dt^{\prime}\ ,

(113)

and note that the time-evolution of the operator
A=Z
under
X
pulses results in
A(t-t^{\prime},t)=\pm Z
with the sign alternating every
\Delta t
:

A(t-t^{\prime},t)=(-1)^{\lceil t/\Delta t\rceil-\lceil(t-t^{\prime})/\Delta t\rceil}Z\ ,

(114)

Taking the integral, we find:

A_{f}(t)=g^{2}Z\int_{0}^{\tau_{c}}(-1)^{\lceil t/\Delta t\rceil-\lceil(t-t^{\prime})/\Delta t\rceil}dt^{\prime}=g^{2}c(t)Z,\quad|c(t)|\leq\Delta t\ ,

(115)

where
c(t)
is the remainder after the cancellation of different signs. To show why
|c(t)|\leq\Delta t
, note that there is no way to accumulate
>\Delta t
by integrating any interval of the function
(-1)^{\lceil x/\Delta t\rceil}

\forall\penalty\ a,b:\penalty\ \left|\int_{a}^{b}(-1)^{\lceil x/\Delta t\rceil}dx\right|\leq\Delta t

(116)

To see this, we note that a translation
a,b\to a+\Delta t,b+\Delta t
changes the sign of the integral. We then split the integration interval
[a,b]
into three subsets:
\{[a+2n\Delta t,a+(2n+1)\Delta t]\},\penalty\ \{[a+(2n+1)\Delta t,a+2(n+1)\Delta t]\},\penalty\ [a+2\Delta t(n_{\text{max}}+1),b]
where
n_{\text{max}}
has been chosen closest to
b
:
n_{\text{max}}=\lceil(b-a)/2\Delta t\rceil-2
. The integrals over the first two subsets cancel due to the translation property, and the remaining subset
[a+2\Delta t(n_{\text{max}}+1),b]
has length
L<2\Delta t
. Note that for
L=0
and
L=2\Delta t
the integral vanishes. Also note that the derivative of the integral with respect to
b
is
(-1)^{\lceil b/\Delta t\rceil}
, and its absolute value is
1
. Any function with such a derivative, up to two inflection points and zeros at the end of the interval
[0,2L]
stays within
[-\Delta t,+\Delta t]
, and the bound is saturated when
b=m\Delta t
.

We compare this with the case without pulses:

A_{f}(t)=g^{2}\tau_{c}Z\ ,

(117)

Here
\tau_{c}
enters the correlation function [see Eq. (
112
)].
Thus the decoherence rate is reduced by a factor of at least

5.1.2
Example 2: time-dependent CGME with an Ohmic spectral density

Now consider a physically permissible environment with a spectral density that satisfies the KMS condition (
10

We specify
\gamma(\omega)

We wish to use the time-dependent CGME [Eq. (

A(t+t_{1},t)=U^{\dagger}(t+t_{1},t)ZU(t+t_{1},t)

where
m_{i}
and

respectively count the number of pulses in the intervals
[0,t]
and
[0,t+t_{1}]

A(t+t_{1},t)=(-1)^{\Delta m}Z\ ,\quad\Delta m=m_{f}-m_{i}\ .

(119)

This yields the time-dependent Lindblad operators
A_{\epsilon}(t)
via Eq. (
23

A^{\text{DD}}_{\epsilon}(t)=f^{\text{DD}}_{\epsilon}(t)Z\ ,\quad f^{\text{DD}}_{\epsilon}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma(\epsilon)T_{a}}{2\pi}}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}e^{i\epsilon\zeta T_{a}}(-1)^{\lceil(t+\zeta T_{a})/\Delta t\rceil-\lceil t/\Delta t\rceil}d\zeta\ .

(120)

Note that the Lamb shift [Eq. (
24
)] is proportional to
Z^{2}=\mathbbm{1}
, so it drops out. The Lindblad operators without DD are simply

A^{\text{noDD}}_{\epsilon}(t)=f^{\text{noDD}}_{\epsilon}(t)Z\ ,\quad f^{\text{noDD}}_{\epsilon}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma(\epsilon)}{2\pi T_{a}}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}e^{i\epsilon t_{1}}dt_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma(\epsilon)T_{a}}{2\pi}}\sinc(\epsilon T_{a}/2)\ .

(121)

{\dot{\rho}}_{C}(t)=-i[H(t),\rho_{C}]+r^{x}(t)\left(Z\rho_{C}Z-\rho_{C}\right)\ ,

(122)

where

r^{x}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\epsilon\left|f^{x}_{\epsilon}\right|^{2}\ ,

(123)

with
x=\text{DD}
or
x=\text{noDD}
. The decoherence rate is thus suppressed by the factor

\xi(t)\equiv\frac{r^{\text{DD}}(t)}{r^{\text{noDD}}(t)}=\frac{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\epsilon{\gamma(\epsilon)}\left|\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}e^{i\epsilon\zeta T_{a}}(-1)^{\lceil(t+\zeta T_{a})/\Delta t\rceil-\lceil t/\Delta t\rceil}d\zeta\right|^{2}}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\epsilon{\gamma(\epsilon)}\left|\sinc\left(\frac{\epsilon T_{a}}{2}\right)\right|^{2}}\ .

(124)

The inner integral can be evaluated under certain simplifying assumptions. For example, let us assume that

is an even integer multiple of
\Delta t

T_{a}=2k\Delta t

which are integer multiples of
\Delta t

\displaystyle\left|\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}e^{i\epsilon\zeta T_{a}}(-1)^{\lceil(t+\zeta T_{a})/\Delta t\rceil-\lceil t/\Delta t\rceil}d\zeta\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{j}\int_{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{j}{k}}^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{j+1}{k}}e^{i\epsilon\zeta T_{a}}d\zeta\right|^{2}\quad(T_{a}=2k\Delta t,\ t=\ell\Delta t)

(125a)

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{j}\frac{e^{i\epsilon T_{a}[2(j-k)+1]/(2k)}}{k}\sinc\left(\frac{\epsilon T_{a}}{2k}\right)\right|^{2}

(125b)

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{2}{\epsilon T_{a}}\sin\left(\frac{k\pi}{2}+\frac{\epsilon T_{a}}{2}\right)\tan\left(\frac{\epsilon T_{a}}{2k}\right)\right|^{2}

(125c)

\displaystyle=\left|\sinc\left(\frac{\epsilon T_{a}}{2}\right)\tan\left(\frac{\epsilon T_{a}}{2k}\right)\right|^{2}\quad(k\text{ even})\ ,

(125d)

where in the last line we assumed for further simplicity assume that

T_{a}={4k^{\prime}}\Delta t

\xi(\ell\Delta t)=\frac{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega{\gamma(\omega)}\left|\sinc\left({2k^{\prime}}\omega\Delta t\right)\tan\left(\omega\Delta t\right)\right|^{2}}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega{\gamma(\omega)}\left|\sinc\left({2k^{\prime}}\omega\Delta t\right)\right|^{2}}\quad({k^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{N})\ .

(126)

5.2
Lambless master equations

It turns out that the Lamb shift terms containing
\sgn(t)
, such as Eq. (
18b
), are more demanding to compute numerically, but at the same time the interesting dynamics is often given by the other, relaxation terms. Therefore it is convenient to have a version of the equations in the “Lambless" regime. Even though these equations do not have any correctness guarantees, one can still use them as toy models, and their numerical solution is often significantly faster than their complete counterparts. Let us list the resulting equations:

•

Lambless Davies-Lindblad [replacing Eq. (
18

{\dot{\rho}}_{LD}(t)=-i[H,\rho]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\omega}\gamma(\omega)(A_{\omega}\rho_{LD}A_{-\omega}-\rho_{LD}A_{-\omega}A_{\omega})+\textrm{h.c.}

(129)

•

Lambless Redfield [replacing Eq. (
16

{\dot{\rho}}_{LR}(t)=-i[H,\rho_{LR}(t)]+(A\rho_{LR}A_{f}-\rho_{LR}A_{f}A+\textrm{h.c.})\ ,

(130)

where

A_{f}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\omega}\gamma(-\omega)A_{\omega}\ .

(131)

This simplified form of

follows from Eqs. (
7
) and (
17

(vanishing Lamb shift). When the KMS condition holds we may write

•

(132)

where

A_{\epsilon}=\sum_{\omega}A_{\omega}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma(\epsilon)T_{a}}{2\pi}}\sinc\left[T_{a}(\epsilon-\omega)/2\right]\ ,

(133)

\Delta\epsilon
, and

Toy example of a slow bath at the boundary of the range of applicability, with explicit correlation function and spectral density

6
Error bounds and estimates

In this section we give the details of the various error bounds and estimates mentioned without proof in our derivation of the CGME in Sec.
3

6.2
, followed by Markov in Sec.
6.3

6.4

T_{a}
. But we start with a proof of Lemma
1
, which plays a central role in our analysis.

6.1
Bound on the difference of solutions of differential equations differing by a bounded operator

We first present a proof of Lemma
1
.

Proof.

The first part of the Lemma is concerned with the pair of differential equations
\dot{x}=\mathcal{L}_{t}x+\mathcal{E}
and
\dot{y}=\mathcal{L}_{t}y
.
Their formal solution is:

x(t)=x(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}_{\tau}(x(\tau))d\tau+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{E}d\tau,\quad y(t)=y(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}_{\tau}(y(\tau))d\tau\ .

(142)

Let
\Lambda>0
be a constant defined as follows:
\sup_{\tau,x}\|\mathcal{L}_{\tau}(x)\|_{1}\leq\Lambda
for all
x
such that
\|x\|_{1}=1
. Since
\mathcal{L}
is linear, and writing
x=\delta/\|\delta\|_{1}
, it follows that
\forall\delta
:
\|\mathcal{L}_{\tau}(\delta)\|_{1}=\|\delta\|_{1}\|\mathcal{L}_{\tau}(x)\|_{1}\leq\|\delta\|_{1}\Lambda
. Then,
assuming
x(0)=y(0)
, and taking the operator norm of the difference yields, for
\delta=x(t)-y(t)
:

\|x(t)-y(t)\|_{1}\leq\Lambda\int_{0}^{t}\|x(\tau)-y(\tau)\|_{1}d\tau+t\|\mathcal{E}\|_{1}\ ,

(143)

Many different functions
f(t)\equiv\|x(t)-y(t)\|_{1}
satisfy this inequality for all
t
, but they are all upper-bounded by the function
b_{1}(t)=\max\|x(t)-y(t)\|_{1}
that saturates the inequality:

\dot{b}_{1}=\Lambda b_{1}+\|\mathcal{E}\|_{1},\quad b_{1}(0)=0\ .

(144)

The solution is

\displaystyle b_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=(e^{\Lambda t}-1)\frac{\|\mathcal{E}\|_{1}}{\Lambda}

(145a)

\displaystyle\leq(e^{c}-1)\frac{\|\mathcal{E}\|_{1}}{\Lambda}\quad\forall t\leq\frac{c}{\Lambda}\ .

(145b)

This proves the desired bound on
\|x(t)-y(t)\|_{1}\leq b_{1}(t)
.

The second part of the Lemma is concerned with the more general, non-Markovian pair of differential equations
\dot{x}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}K_{t-\tau}(x(\tau))d\tau+\mathcal{E}
and
\dot{y}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}K_{t-\tau}(y(\tau))d\tau
.
The first part of the Lemma in fact follows from this case as a corollary, but we presented it for clarity.
We again subtract the formal solutions:

x(t)-y(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\tau}K_{\tau-\theta}(x(\theta)-y(\theta))d\tau d\theta+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{E}d\tau\ .

(146)

Define
\Lambda_{t}=\sup_{x}\|K_{t}(x)\|_{1}
for all
x
such that
\|x\|_{1}=1
. By linearity of
K_{t}
it follows as above that
\forall\delta
:
\|K_{t}(\delta)\|_{1}\leq\Lambda_{t}\|\delta\|_{1}
. Therefore:

\|x(t)-y(t)\|_{1}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\tau}\Lambda_{\tau-\theta}\|x(\theta)-y(\theta)\|_{1}d\tau d\theta+\int_{0}^{t}\|\mathcal{E}\|_{1}d\tau\ .

(147)

The upper bound
b_{K}(t)
on
\|x(t)-y(t)\|_{1}
satisfies the equation:

b_{K}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\tau}\Lambda_{\tau-\theta}b_{K}(\theta)d\tau d\theta+\|\mathcal{E}\|_{1}t\ .

(148)

Taking the derivative once, we obtain:

\dot{b}_{K}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\Lambda_{t-\tau}b_{K}(\tau)d\tau+\|\mathcal{E}\|_{1}\ .

(149)

Since the r.h.s. is positive,
b_{K}(t)
is a monotonic function. So
b_{K}(\tau)\leq b_{K}(t)
, and it follows that:

\dot{b}_{K}(t)\leq\int_{0}^{t}\Lambda_{t-\tau}b_{K}(t)d\tau+\|\mathcal{E}\|_{1}\ .

(150)

In other words, we can again upper-bound
b_{K}(t)\leq b_{K}^{\prime}(t)
, such that

\dot{b}_{K}^{\prime}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\Lambda_{t-\tau}d\tau b_{K}^{\prime}(t)+\|\mathcal{E}\|_{1}\ .

(151)

Now let
\Lambda
be a positive constant such that
\int_{0}^{t}\Lambda_{\tau}d\tau\leq\Lambda
for all
t
.
We thus arrive at

\dot{b_{K}}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\Lambda b_{K}^{\prime\prime}(t)+\|\mathcal{E}\|_{1}\ ,

(152)

for a new upper-bound
b_{K}^{\prime\prime}(t)\geq b_{K}^{\prime}(t)
. This is exactly the same as Eq. (
144
), and hence the Lemma still holds for non-Markovian equations.
∎

Note that for our purposes
\Lambda=4/\tau_{SB}
is usually taken, thanks to Eq. (
41
,
42c
), and that the non-Markovian bound will be generalized in Sec.
6.2
.
The exponential on the r.h.s. of Eq. (
145b
) may be troublesome. Under additional assumptions we can prove a much tighter linear in
t
bound presented in Appendix
E
, yet we did not find a way to recast all of our results in that tighter form.

6.2
Born approximation error

We start with an estimate of the error associated with making the Born approximation, which we presented as the estimate
\|\mathcal{E}_{B}\|_{1}=O\left(\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}^{2}}\right)
in Eq. (
33
). I.e., we would like to bound the error
\mathcal{E}_{B}=\dot{\rho}_{\text{true},I}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}K_{t-\tau}^{2,B}(\rho_{\text{true},I}(\tau))d\tau
. It is difficult to do so directly since
\rho_{\text{true},I}(t)
is unknown, so instead we will first use a proxy for
\rho_{\text{true},I}(t)
, which we call
\rho_{B4,I}(t)
. The latter is the solution of the master equation obtained by iterating the substitution of
\rho_{\text{tot}}=\rho_{\text{tot}}(0)-i\int_{0}^{t}[V(\tau),\rho_{\text{tot}}(\tau)]d\tau
[Eq. (
29b
)]
into

{\dot{\rho}}_{\text{tot}}(t)=-i[V(t),\rho_{\text{tot}}(t)]
[Eq. (
29a
)] to 4th order, and only then applying the Born approximation. After developing an understanding of the 4th order we proceed to the infinite series. We make certain assumptions about its convergence, and comment below on their compatibility with the known convergence radius of the Dyson series.
Let us first define the type of bath for which we can carry out this procedure in order to arrive at a bound.

We refer to a bath as exponential if its correlation function
\mathcal{C}(t)
decays exponentially. This type of assumption is both common and convenient. For example, as we will see in more detail below, for a Gaussian bath (one which satisfies Wick’s theorem) the times
t_{1}<t_{2}<t_{3}<t_{4}
appear in the estimate of the Born error [they enter terms such as
\mathcal{C}(t_{1}-t_{3})\mathcal{C}(t_{2}-t_{4})
]. If
\mathcal{C}(t)
is exponentially decaying with a characteristic time
\tau_{B}
, that immediately lets us conclude that only
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}
that are all within
\sim\tau_{B}
of each other contribute to the estimate, and the bound on the Born error acquires a small factor of
\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}
. However, an exponentially decaying
\mathcal{C}(t)
may be an unrealistically strong assumption (e.g., it is hard to satisfy at low temperatures).
Therefore it is desirable to introduce a condition weaker than exponential decay, which we refer to as a non-exponential bath. Specifically, we consider the convergence of integrals such as

\int_{0}^{\infty}|\mathcal{C}(t)|t^{n}dt

for some integer
n\geq 0
. Such a condition was used in Ref.
[
31
]
to control the error of the subsequent Markov approximation. Here we show how these integrals for
n=0,1
control our estimate of the Born error for a Gaussian bath. The physical meaning of these integrals was defined in Eq. (
11
). A more general requirement on a non-Gaussian bath can be derived in the same way. However, that requirement will involve a higher-order correlation function that does not have a straightforward and concise interpretation.

Recall the steps of the derivation where we make the Born approximation, leading to Eq. (
39
).
For brevity, we drop the interaction picture subscript, replace the time argument by a subscript, and place the approximation level
B
in the superscript:
\rho_{B,I}(t)=\rho_{t}^{B}
. Written out explicitly, Eq. (
39
) in terms of
\rho_{t}^{B}
is:

{\dot{\rho}}^{B}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}K_{t-\tau}^{2,B}(\rho_{\tau}^{B})d\tau=\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{C}(\tau-t)(A_{t}\rho^{B}_{\tau}A_{\tau}-\rho^{B}_{\tau}A_{\tau}A_{t})+\mathcal{C}(t-\tau)(A_{\tau}\rho^{B}_{\tau}A_{t}-A_{t}A_{\tau}\rho^{B}_{\tau})d\tau\ .

(153)

The formal solution is:

{\rho}^{B}_{\tau}=\rho_{0}+\int_{0}^{\tau}d\theta\int_{0}^{\theta}dm\mathcal{C}(m-\theta)(A_{\theta}\rho^{B}_{m}A_{m}-\rho^{B}_{m}A_{m}A_{\theta})+\mathcal{C}(\theta-m)(A_{m}\rho^{B}_{m}A_{\theta}-A_{\theta}A_{m}\rho^{B}_{m}).

(154)

We substitute this solution back into Eq. (
153
), a necessary step because our estimate of the Born error will come from the 4th order terms, so we need to subtract the original solution. The full expression to be subtracted is:

\displaystyle{\dot{\rho}}^{B}_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}K_{t-\tau}^{2,B}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{0}^{\theta}K_{\theta-m}^{2,B}(\rho_{m}^{B})dmd\theta\right)d\tau=\int_{0}^{t}K_{t-m}^{4,B}(\rho_{m}^{B})d\tau

(155a)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{C}(\tau-t)(A_{t}\rho_{0}A_{\tau}-\rho_{0}A_{\tau}A_{t})+\mathcal{C}(t-\tau)(A_{\tau}\rho_{0}A_{t}-A_{t}A_{\tau}\rho_{0})d\tau

(155b)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}d\theta\int_{0}^{\theta}dm

(155c)

\displaystyle[

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\tau-t)\mathcal{C}(m-\theta)A_{t}(A_{\theta}\rho^{B}_{m}A_{m}-\rho^{B}_{m}A_{m}A_{\theta})A_{\tau}+\mathcal{C}(\tau-t)\mathcal{C}(\theta-m)A_{t}(A_{m}\rho^{B}_{m}A_{\theta}-A_{\theta}A_{m}\rho^{B}_{m})A_{\tau}

(155d)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\tau-t)\mathcal{C}(m-\theta)(A_{\theta}\rho^{B}_{m}A_{m}-\rho^{B}_{m}A_{m}A_{\theta})A_{\tau}A_{t}-\mathcal{C}(\tau-t)\mathcal{C}(\theta-m)(A_{m}\rho^{B}_{m}A_{\theta}-A_{\theta}A_{m}\rho^{B}_{m})A_{\tau}A_{t}

(155e)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(t-\tau)\mathcal{C}(m-\theta)A_{\tau}(A_{\theta}\rho^{B}_{m}A_{m}-\rho^{B}_{m}A_{m}A_{\theta})A_{t}+\mathcal{C}(t-\tau)\mathcal{C}(\theta-m)A_{\tau}(A_{m}\rho^{B}_{m}A_{\theta}-A_{\theta}A_{m}\rho^{B}_{m})A_{t}

(155f)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(t-\tau)\mathcal{C}(m-\theta)A_{t}A_{\tau}(A_{\theta}\rho^{B}_{m}A_{m}-\rho^{B}_{m}A_{m}A_{\theta})-\mathcal{C}(t-\tau)\mathcal{C}(\theta-m)A_{t}A_{\tau}(A_{m}\rho^{B}_{m}A_{\theta}-A_{\theta}A_{m}\rho^{B}_{m})]\ .

(155g)

Now, these terms constitute only a part of the expression for the derivative of the true density matrix
{\dot{\rho}}^{\text{true}}_{t}
to the
4
th order in the system-bath interaction. The full
4
th order expression can be obtained, as mentioned above, by substituting the solution of the total evolution,

\rho_{\text{tot}}=\rho_{\text{tot}}(0)-i\int_{0}^{t}[V(\tau),\rho_{\text{tot}}(\tau)]d\tau

into

{\dot{\rho}}_{\text{tot}}(t)=-i[V(t),\rho_{\text{tot}}(t)]

three consecutive times (to generate a 4th order commutator), and only then applying the Born approximation. We call this
\rho_{t}^{B4}
since it will differ from
\rho_{t}^{B}
in the 4th order in the interaction
V(t)
. Doing so, we obtain:

\displaystyle{\dot{\rho}}^{B4}_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{C}(\tau-t)(A_{t}\rho_{0}A_{\tau}-\rho_{0}A_{\tau}A_{t})+\mathcal{C}(t-\tau)(A_{\tau}\rho_{0}A_{t}-A_{t}A_{\tau}\rho_{0})d\tau

(156a)

\displaystyle+\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{b}}[A_{t}\otimes B_{t},\int_{0}^{t}d\tau[A_{\tau}\otimes B_{\tau},\int_{0}^{\tau}d\theta[A_{\theta}\otimes B_{\theta},\int_{0}^{\theta}dm[A_{m}\otimes B_{m},\rho^{B4}_{m}\otimes\rho_{\text{b}}]]]]\ .

(156b)

Defined in this way,
{\rho}^{B4}_{t}
is close to the
4
th order of the true state
{\rho}^{\text{true}}_{t}
up to terms of
6
th order and higher, so we use
\rho^{B4}_{t}-\rho^{B}_{t}
to estimate the actual leading order error
\rho^{\text{true}}_{t}-\rho^{B}_{t}
. We proceed to expand all the commutators:

\displaystyle{\dot{\rho}}^{B4}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}K_{t-\tau}^{4,B4}(\rho_{\tau}^{B4})d\tau=\int_{0}^{t}K_{t-\tau}^{2,B}(\rho_{0})d\tau

(157a)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}d\theta\int_{0}^{\theta}dm\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{b}}B_{t}B_{\tau}B_{\theta}B_{m}\rho_{\text{b}}A_{t}A_{\tau}A_{\theta}A_{m}\rho^{B4}_{m}-\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{b}}B_{t}B_{\tau}B_{\theta}\rho_{\text{b}}B_{m}A_{t}A_{\tau}A_{\theta}\rho^{B4}_{m}A_{m}-\dots

(157b)

There are 16 terms total in the last line, and the order of
B_{t},B_{\tau},B_{\theta},B_{m}
and
\rho_{\text{b}}
is exactly the same as the order of
A_{t},A_{\tau},A_{\theta},A_{m}
and
\rho^{B}_{m}
.

Let us now assume that the bath is Gaussian. By definition, a Gaussian bath obeys Wick’s theorem (or Isserlis’ theorem
[
75
]
), which states that at all orders the higher order correlation functions decouple into products of two-point correlation functions. For example, for the 4-point correlation function:

\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{b}}B_{t}B_{\tau}B_{\theta}B_{m}\rho_{\text{b}}=\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{b}}B_{t}B_{\tau}\rho_{\text{b}}\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{b}}B_{\theta}B_{m}\rho_{\text{b}}+\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{b}}B_{t}B_{\theta}\rho_{\text{b}}\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{b}}B_{\tau}B_{m}\rho_{\text{b}}+\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{b}}B_{t}B_{m}\rho_{\text{b}}\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{b}}B_{\tau}B_{\theta}\rho_{\text{b}}\ .

(158)

By definition,
\mathcal{C}(t-\tau)=\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{b}}B_{t}B_{\tau}\rho_{\text{b}}
. Thus:

\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{b}}B_{t}B_{\tau}B_{\theta}B_{m}\rho_{\text{b}}=\mathcal{C}(t-\tau)\mathcal{C}(\theta-m)+\mathcal{C}(t-\theta)\mathcal{C}(\tau-m)+\mathcal{C}(t-m)\mathcal{C}(\tau-\theta)

(159)

We see that in Eq. (
155
) only the first term was present. One can check that the order of
A
is exactly the same, so upon subtracting Eq. (
155
) from Eq. (
158
) only the two terms
\mathcal{C}(t-\theta)\mathcal{C}(\tau-m)+\mathcal{C}(t-m)\mathcal{C}(\tau-\theta)
remain.
These extra terms can be interpreted in terms of the corresponding diagrammatic technique. Indeed, note that
t>\tau>\theta>m
. If in Wick’s theorem we have a pairing
t\leftrightarrow\tau,\penalty\ \theta\leftrightarrow m
and the arrows are drawn above the time axis, they do not relate to each other, and each arc can be anywhere along the time axis. But if the pairing is
t\leftrightarrow\theta,\penalty\ \tau\leftrightarrow m
or
t\leftrightarrow m,\penalty\ \tau\leftrightarrow\theta
, then the two arcs are stuck together due to the condition
t>\tau>\theta>m
; see Fig.
8
.

\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{b}}B_{t}B_{\tau}B_{\theta}B_{m}\rho_{\text{b}}
in Wick’s theorem. Note that the first and second order are in
\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}
, not in the coupling strength itself, in which both diagrams are 4th order.

At the moment we have two equations: the Born equation in two forms Eq. (
153
) and Eq. (
155
), as well as the 4th order equation Eq. (

):

{\dot{\rho}}^{B}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}K_{t-\tau}^{2,B}(\rho_{\tau}^{B})d\tau=\int_{0}^{t}K_{t-\tau}^{4,B}(\rho_{\tau}^{B})d\tau,\quad{\dot{\rho}}^{B4}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}K_{t-\tau}^{4,B4}(\rho_{\tau}^{B4})d\tau,

(160)

where
K(\rho)
are corresponding linear superoperators.
K^{2,B}
is the 2nd order (in the bath interaction) operator from Eq. (
153
),
K^{4,B}
and
K^{4,B4}
have terms up to the 4th order defined in Eq. (
155
) and Eq. (
157a
). Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{B4}(\rho^{\text{any}})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}(K_{t-\tau}^{4,B4}(\rho_{\tau}^{\text{any}})-K_{t-\tau}^{4,B}(\rho_{\tau}^{\text{any}}))d\tau\ ,

(161)

for instance:

\mathcal{E}_{B4}(\rho^{\text{B4}})={\dot{\rho}}^{B4}_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}K_{t-\tau}^{4,B}(\rho_{\tau}^{B4})d\tau\ .

(162)

Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{B}

\displaystyle={\dot{\rho}}^{\text{true}}_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}K_{t-\tau}^{2,B}(\rho_{\tau}^{\text{true}})d\tau\ .

(163)

The quantity that appeared in the derivation is
\mathcal{E}_{B}
. We will now bound
\|\mathcal{E}_{B4}(\rho^{\text{any}})\|_{1}
and discuss its generalization
\|\mathcal{E}_{Bk}(\rho^{\text{any}})\|_{1}
. We postpone the estimate for
\|\mathcal{E}_{B}\|_{1}
till we develop more advanced tools.

The difference
K_{t-\tau}^{4,B4}(\rho_{\tau}^{4,B4})-K_{t-\tau}^{4,B}(\rho_{\tau}^{B4})
is given by the two second order pairings in Fig.
8
, times the
16
possible orders of
A_{t},A_{\tau},A_{\theta},A_{m}
and
\rho_{m}
:

\mathcal{E}_{B4}=\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}d\theta\int_{0}^{\theta}dm(\mathcal{C}(t-\theta)\mathcal{C}(\tau-m)+\mathcal{C}(t-m)\mathcal{C}(\tau-\theta))A_{t}A_{\tau}A_{\theta}A_{m}\rho^{\text{any}}_{m}-\dots

(164)

We take the trace norm of both sides:

\|\mathcal{E}_{B4}(\rho^{\text{any}})\|_{1}\leq\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}d\theta\int_{0}^{\theta}dm\|(\mathcal{C}(t-\theta)\mathcal{C}(\tau-m)+\mathcal{C}(t-m)\mathcal{C}(\tau-\theta))A_{t}A_{\tau}A_{\theta}A_{m}\rho^{\text{any}}_{m}\|_{1}+\dots

(165)

where we used the triangle inequality

\|A+B\|_{1}\leq\|A\|_{1}+\|B\|_{1}
.
We now use submultiplicativity [Eq. (
38
)].
Noting that the first norm in the submultiplicativity inequality is the operator norm, we obtain:

\|\mathcal{E}_{B4}(\rho^{\text{any}})\|_{1}\leq\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}d\theta\int_{0}^{\theta}dm\|(\mathcal{C}(t-\theta)\mathcal{C}(\tau-m)+\mathcal{C}(t-m)\mathcal{C}(\tau-\theta))A_{t}A_{\tau}A_{\theta}A_{m}\|\|\rho^{\text{any}}_{m}\|_{1}+\dots

(166)

The operator norm
\|A\|=1
and we will set
c_{a}=\text{max}_{t}\|\rho^{\text{any}}_{t}\|_{1}
such that
\|\rho^{\text{any}}\|_{1}\leq c_{a}
:

\|\mathcal{E}_{B4}(\rho^{\text{any}})\|_{1}\leq c_{a}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}d\theta\int_{0}^{\theta}dm|(\mathcal{C}(t-\theta)\mathcal{C}(\tau-m)+\mathcal{C}(t-m)\mathcal{C}(\tau-\theta))|+\dots

(167)

In particular,
\mathcal{E}_{B4}(\rho^{B})

c_{B}
and
\mathcal{E}_{B4}(\rho^{B4})

. What is left is to bound the integrals in Eq. (

\int_{0}^{\infty}|\mathcal{C}(t)|t^{n}dt\leq\frac{\tau_{B}^{n}}{\tau_{SB}}\ ,\quad\text{for $n=0,1$}\ ,

which is automatically satisfied for
\tau_{SB}
and

as defined in Eq. (

The first term in Eq. (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}d\theta\left[\int_{0}^{\theta}dm|\mathcal{C}(\tau-m)|\right]|\mathcal{C}(t-\theta)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\tau_{SB}}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}d\theta|\mathcal{C}(t-\theta)|=\frac{1}{\tau_{SB}}\int_{0}^{t}d\theta\int_{\theta}^{t}d\tau|\mathcal{C}(t-\theta)|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau_{SB}}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\theta)d\theta|\mathcal{C}(t-\theta)|\leq\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}^{2}},

(171b)

while the second term in Eq. (

) is bounded as:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}d\theta\int_{0}^{\theta}dm|\mathcal{C}(t-m)||\mathcal{C}(\tau-\theta)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\tau_{SB}}\int_{0}^{t}dm\int_{m}^{t}d\theta|\mathcal{C}(t-m)|=\frac{1}{\tau_{SB}}\int_{0}^{t}(t-m)dm|\mathcal{C}(t-m)|

(172b)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}^{2}}\ .

(172c)

Together, this yields:

\|\mathcal{E}_{B4}(\rho^{\text{any}})\|_{1}\leq 32c_{a}\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}^{2}}\ ,

(173)

\mathcal{E}_{B}
as defined in Eq. (

\|\mathcal{E}_{Bk}(\rho^{\text{any}})\|_{1}\leq 32c_{a}e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}^{2}}(1+4t/\tau_{SB}+O(e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}))

(177)

We do not know how to prove such a statement as of yet.
The proof would rely on the diagrammatic calculation of subleading terms. It is straightforward, but cumbersome, and we do not attempt it here. Instead we make two
assumptions
:

1.

In the
k\to\infty
limit, there is a finite radius of convergence in

e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}
:

\|\mathcal{E}_{B\infty}(\rho^{\text{any}})\|_{1}\leq 32c_{a}e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}^{2}}(1+4t/\tau_{SB}+O(e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}))\ .

(178)

2.

The true solution is the limit of the perturbative series:

{\dot{\rho}}^{\text{true}}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}K_{t-\tau}^{\infty,B\infty}(\rho_{\tau}^{\text{true}})d\tau\ .

(179)

To prove convergence, one needs to control the factorial number of terms appearing in Wick’s theorem. Such a proof was already presented by Davies
[
4
]
. The slight adjustments needed to include an integral condition such as Eq. (
169
) are a somewhat tedious problem left for a future study.

Our definition of
\mathcal{E}_{B}
under this assumption contains
\mathcal{E}_{B\infty}
discussed above, as well as an extra term:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{B}

\displaystyle={\dot{\rho}}^{\text{true}}_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}K_{t-\tau}^{2,B}(\rho_{\tau}^{\text{true}})d\tau=\int_{0}^{t}(K_{t-\tau}^{\infty,B\infty}(\rho_{\tau}^{\text{true}})-K_{t-\tau}^{2,B}(\rho_{\tau}^{\text{true}}))d\tau

(180a)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{E}_{B}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\int_{0}^{t}(K_{t-\tau}^{\infty,B\infty}(\rho_{\tau}^{\text{true}})-K_{t-\tau}^{\infty,B}(\rho_{\tau}^{\text{true}}))d\tau\right\|_{1}+\left\|\int_{0}^{t}(K_{t-\tau}^{\infty,B}(\rho_{\tau}^{\text{true}})-K_{t-\tau}^{2,B}(\rho_{\tau}^{\text{true}}))d\tau\right\|_{1}

(180b)

\displaystyle\leq 32\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}^{2}}(1+O(\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}))+\left\|\int_{0}^{t}(K_{t-\tau}^{\infty,B}(\rho_{\tau}^{\text{true}})-K_{t-\tau}^{2,B}(\rho_{\tau}^{\text{true}}))d\tau\right\|_{1}\ ,

(180c)

where
c_{a}=1
since the true evolution is CP. The second term will turn out to be time-dependent, and quite tricky to estimate. We will return to this problem after we develop some extra machinery.

Let us first note the form of
K^{k,B}
, starting with
k=4
:

\int_{0}^{t}K^{4,B}_{t-\tau}(\rho^{\text{any}}_{\tau})d\tau=\int_{0}^{t}K^{2,B}_{t-\tau}\left(\rho_{0}+\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{0}^{m}K^{2,B}_{m-\theta}(\rho^{\text{any}}_{m})dmd\theta\right)d\tau\ .

(181)

The solution of

\dot{\rho}^{B4}=\int_{0}^{t}K^{4,B4}_{t-\tau}(\rho_{\tau}^{B4})d\tau=\int_{0}^{t}K^{4,B}_{t-\tau}(\rho^{B4}_{\tau})d\tau+\mathcal{E}_{B4}

(182)

is equivalent to solving a system of
4
th order equations that are our proxy for the true solution (which is
\infty
order):

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\dot{\rho}^{B4}=\int_{0}^{t}K^{2,B}_{t-\tau}(\rho^{(1)}_{\tau})d\tau+\mathcal{E}_{B4},&\rho^{B4}(0)=\rho_{0}\\
\dot{\rho}^{(1)}=\int_{0}^{t}K^{2,B}_{t-\tau}(\rho^{B4}_{\tau})d\tau,&\rho^{(1)}(0)=\rho_{0}\ .\end{array}\right.

(183)

We will prove that the solution of the above system remains close to
\rho^{B}
, the solution of the equation after the Born approximation:

\dot{\rho}^{B}=\int_{0}^{t}K^{2,B}_{t-\tau}(\rho^{B}_{\tau})d\tau\ .

(184)

We will also present a straightforward generalization to all
k
which will allow one to bound
\|\mathcal{E}_{B}\|_{1}
.

First write the equation in the compact form:

\dot{\bf{p}}=\bf{Lp}+\bf{E},\hskip 11.49994pt\bf{p}=\left(\penalty\ {}^{\rho^{B4}}_{\rho^{(1)}}\right),\hskip 11.49994pt\bf{L}=\left(\penalty\ {}^{0\penalty\ \hskip 9.06108pt\hskip 9.06108pt\penalty\ \int K_{t-\tau}d\tau}_{\int K_{t-\tau}d\tau\penalty\ \hskip 9.06108pt\hskip 9.06108pt\penalty\ 0}\right),\hskip 11.49994pt\bf{E}=\left(\penalty\ {}^{\mathcal{E}_{B4}(\rho^{B4})}_{0}\right)\ .

(185)

Define a custom norm that is an infinity norm on the vector of operators, but for each operator the trace norm is taken:

\|{\bf{v}}\|_{c}=\left\|\penalty\ {}^{v^{1}}_{v^{2}}\right\|_{c}=\text{max}(\|v^{1}\|_{1},\|v^{2}\|_{1})

(186)

The triangle inequality is obeyed as required:

\displaystyle\|{\bf{v}+\bf{w}}\|_{c}

\displaystyle=\text{max}(\|v^{1}+w^{1}\|_{1},\|v^{2}+w^{2}\|_{1})\leq\text{max}(\|v^{1}\|_{1}+\|w^{1}\|_{1},\|v^{2}\|_{1}+\|w^{2}\|_{1})

(187a)

\displaystyle\leq\text{max}(\|v^{1}\|_{1},\|v^{2}\|_{1})+\text{max}(\|w^{1}\|_{1},\|w^{2}\|_{1})=\|{\bf{v}}\|_{c}+\|{\bf{w}}\|_{c}\ .

(187b)

The maximum of the custom norm of
{\bf{L}}
over inputs of norm 1 is the same as before:

\text{max}_{{\bf{p}}:\|{\bf{p}}\|_{c}=1}\|{\bf{Lp}}\|_{c}=\Lambda=\frac{4}{\tau_{SB}}\ ,

(188)

so all the properties needed to prove Lemma
1
hold. We establish:

\|\delta{\bf{p}}_{t}\|_{c}\leq\frac{e^{\Lambda t}-1}{\Lambda}\|{\bf{E}}\|_{c}

(189)

in the original variables:

\text{max}(\|\delta\rho^{(1)}_{t}\|_{1},\|\delta\rho^{B4}_{t}\|_{1})\leq\frac{e^{\Lambda t}-1}{\Lambda}\|\mathcal{E}_{B4}\|_{1}\ .

(190)

Note that the error we are trying to bound is

\|\mathcal{E}_{B}\|_{1}=\left\|\dot{\rho}^{\text{true}}-\int_{0}^{t}K^{2,B}_{t-\tau}(\rho^{\text{true}}_{\tau})d\tau\right\|_{1}=\left\|\int_{0}^{t}\left(K^{\infty,B\infty}_{t-\tau}(\rho^{\text{true}}_{\tau})-K^{2,B}_{t-\tau}(\rho^{\text{true}}_{\tau}\right)d\tau\right\|_{1}\ .

(191)

Our approximation to it is bounded as:

\left\|\dot{\rho}^{B4}-\int_{0}^{t}K^{2,B}_{t-\tau}(\rho^{B4}_{\tau})d\tau\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|\int_{0}^{t}K^{2,B}_{t-\tau}(\rho^{(1)}_{\tau}-\rho^{B4}_{\tau})d\tau\right\|_{1}+\|\mathcal{E}_{B4}\|_{1}\leq(2e^{\Lambda t}-1)\|\mathcal{E}_{B4}\|_{1}\ ,

(192)

where we have used the triangle inequality:
\|\rho^{(1)}_{\tau}-\rho^{B4}_{\tau}\|_{1}\leq\|\delta\rho^{(1)}_{\tau}\|_{1}+\|\delta\rho^{B4}_{\tau}\|_{1}
. Now we can repeat the above proof for arbitrary
k
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\dot{\rho}^{Bk}=\int_{0}^{t}K^{2,B}_{t-\tau}(\rho^{(1)}_{\tau})d\tau+\mathcal{E}_{Bk}(\rho^{Bk})&\rho^{Bk}(0)=\rho_{0}\\
\dot{\rho}^{(1)}=\int_{0}^{t}K^{2,B}_{t-\tau}(\rho^{(2)}_{\tau})d\tau&\rho^{(1)}(0)=\rho_{0}\\
\dots&\dots\\
\dot{\rho}^{(k/2-1)}=\int_{0}^{t}K^{2,B}_{t-\tau}(\rho^{B4}_{\tau})d\tau&\rho^{(k/2-1)}(0)=\rho_{0}\end{array}\right.

(193)

For appropriately defined
{\bf{L}}
,
\|\cdot\|_{c}
the r.h.s. is still bounded by the same constant:

\text{max}_{{\bf{p}}:\|{\bf{p}}\|_{c}=1}\|{\bf{Lp}}\|_{c}=\Lambda=\frac{4}{\tau_{SB}}\ ,

(194)

which leads to

\text{max}(\|\delta\rho^{(1)}_{t}\|_{1},\dots,\|\delta\rho^{Bk}_{t}\|_{1})\leq\frac{e^{\Lambda t}-1}{\Lambda}\|\mathcal{E}_{Bk}\|_{1}\ ,

(195)

and the error in the r.h.s. is bounded without change:

\left\|\dot{\rho}^{Bk}-\int_{0}^{t}K^{2,B}_{t-\tau}(\rho^{Bk}_{\tau})d\tau\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|\int_{0}^{t}K^{2,B}_{t-\tau}(\rho^{(1)}_{\tau}-\rho^{Bk}_{\tau})d\tau\right\|_{1}+\|\mathcal{E}_{Bk}\|_{1}\leq(2e^{\Lambda t}-1)\|\mathcal{E}_{Bk}\|_{1}\ .

(196)

Taking the
k\to\infty
limit, we obtain:

\|\rho^{\text{true}}_{t}-\rho^{B}_{t}\|_{1}\leq\frac{e^{\Lambda t}-1}{\Lambda}\|\mathcal{E}_{B\infty}(\rho^{\text{true}})\|_{1}\leq 8(e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}-1)e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}(1+4t/\tau_{SB}+O(e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}))\ ,

(197)

and, finally:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{E}_{B}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(2e^{\Lambda t}-1)\|\mathcal{E}_{B\infty}(\rho^{\text{true}})\|_{1}

(198a)

\displaystyle\leq 32(2e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}-1)e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}^{2}}(1+4t/\tau_{SB}+O(e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}))\ ,

(198b)

where we have used that
c_{a}=1
for
\rho^{\text{true}}
. This result is the sought-after justification of the estimate
\|\mathcal{E}_{B}\|_{1}=O\left(\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}^{2}}\right)
given in Eq. (
33
).

An immediate consequence of Eq. (
197
) is that we can use it to obtain a bound on
c_{B}
, which we defined after Eq. (
40
) as an upper bound on
\|\rho_{B,I}\|_{1}
. Since

\|\rho^{B}_{t}\|_{1}\leq\|\rho^{\text{true}}_{t}\|_{1}+\|\rho^{B}_{t}-\rho^{\text{true}}_{t}\|_{1}=1+\|\rho^{\text{true}}_{t}-\rho^{B}_{t}\|_{1}\ ,

(199)

we obtain:

c_{B}\leq 1+8(e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}-1)e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}(1+4t/\tau_{SB}+O(e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}))\ .

(200)

6.3
Markov approximation error

This subsection follows Ref.
[
76
]
initially. Before the Markov approximation but after the Born approximation, the master equation had the form given in Eq. (
39
).
The error due the Markov approximation
\rho_{B,I}(\tau)\mapsto\rho_{B,I}(t)
, in transitioning to Eq. (
42b
), is:

\mathcal{E}_{M}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{C}(\tau-t)[A(t)(\rho_{B,I}(\tau)-\rho_{B,I}(t))A(\tau)-(\rho_{B,I}(\tau)-\rho_{B,I}(t))A(\tau)A(t)]d\tau+\textrm{h.c.}

(201)

Since
\rho_{B,I}(t)-\rho_{B,I}(\tau)=\int_{\tau}^{t}{\dot{\rho}}_{B,I}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}
, we have

\|\rho_{B,I}(t)-\rho_{B,I}(\tau)\|_{1}\leq\|{\dot{\rho}}_{B,I}\|_{1}(t-\tau)\leq\frac{4(t-\tau)c_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\ ,

(202)

where we used Eq. (
40
) in the second inequality.
Now we can estimate the norm of the error:

\|\mathcal{E}_{M}\|_{1}\leq 4\int_{0}^{t}|\mathcal{C}(\tau-t)|\frac{4(t-\tau)}{\tau_{SB}}d\tau\leq 16\frac{\tau_{B}c_{B}}{\tau_{SB}^{2}}\ .

(203)

We found
c_{B}
and saw that it is
O(1)
in Eq. (
200
).

Let us now use the Markovian Lemma
1
with Eq. (
203
):

\|\rho^{BM}_{t}-\rho^{B}_{t}\|_{1}\leq 4\frac{\tau_{B}c_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\left(e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}-1\right)\ .

(204)

A total expression for the Born and Markov error is [adding Eq. (
197
)]:

\|\rho^{\text{true}}_{t}-\rho^{BM}_{t}\|_{1}\leq 4\frac{\tau_{B}c_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\left(e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}-1\right)+8(e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}-1)e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}(1+4t/\tau_{SB}+O(e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}))\ ,

(205)

which simplifies to:

\|\rho^{\text{true}}_{t}-\rho^{BM}_{t}\|_{1}\leq 4(e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}-1)\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\left(c_{B}+2e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}(1+4t/\tau_{SB}+O(e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}))\right)\ ,

(206)

or substituting in
c_{B}
:

\|\rho^{\text{true}}_{t}-\rho^{BM}_{t}\|_{1}\leq\frac{\left(e^{\frac{4t}{\tau_{SB}}}-1\right)\tau_{B}\left(4+8\left(1+\frac{4\left(e^{\frac{4t}{\tau_{SB}}}-1\right)\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\right)e^{\frac{4t}{\tau_{SB}}}\left(1+\frac{4t}{\tau_{SB}}+O\left(e^{\frac{4t}{\tau_{SB}}}\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\right)\right)\right)}{\tau_{SB}}\ .

(207)

The extra factor from
c_{B}
can be absorbed into the big-
O
notation:

\|\rho^{\text{true}}_{t}-\rho^{BM}_{t}\|_{1}\leq\frac{\left(e^{\frac{4t}{\tau_{SB}}}-1\right)\tau_{B}\left(4+8e^{\frac{4t}{\tau_{SB}}}\left(1+\frac{4t}{\tau_{SB}}\right)\left(1+O\left(e^{\frac{4t}{\tau_{SB}}}\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\right)\right)\right)}{\tau_{SB}}\ .

(208)

A loose bound follows from the above:

\|\rho^{\text{true}}_{t}-\rho^{BM}_{t}\|_{1}\leq\frac{12\left(e^{\frac{4t}{\tau_{SB}}}-1\right)e^{\frac{8t}{\tau_{SB}}}\tau_{B}\left(1+O\left(e^{\frac{4t}{\tau_{SB}}}\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\right)\right)}{\tau_{SB}}\ .

(209)

Sometimes we wish to report the bound briefly as:

\|\rho^{\text{true}}_{t}-\rho^{BM}_{t}\|_{1}=\left(e^{\frac{4t}{\tau_{SB}}}-1\right)O\left(e^{\frac{8t}{\tau_{SB}}}\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\right)

(210)

Proof.

The original big-O notation states that for
e^{\frac{4t}{\tau_{SB}}}\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\leq\epsilon
the following inequality holds:

\|\rho^{\text{true}}_{t}-\rho^{BM}_{t}\|_{1}\leq\frac{12\left(e^{\frac{4t}{\tau_{SB}}}-1\right)e^{\frac{8t}{\tau_{SB}}}\tau_{B}\left(1+Ce^{\frac{4t}{\tau_{SB}}}\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\right)}{\tau_{SB}}\leq\frac{12\left(e^{\frac{4t}{\tau_{SB}}}-1\right)e^{\frac{8t}{\tau_{SB}}}\tau_{B}\left(1+C\epsilon\right)}{\tau_{SB}}\ .

(211)

We first note that
e^{\frac{4t}{\tau_{SB}}}\leq e^{\frac{8t}{\tau_{SB}}}
, so if
e^{\frac{8t}{\tau_{SB}}}\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\leq\epsilon
, then
e^{\frac{4t}{\tau_{SB}}}\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\leq\epsilon
as well, and we are within the range of the original big-O notation. By taking
12(1+C\epsilon)
as the new constant, we prove Eq. (
210
).
∎

6.4
Time averaging error and its optimization

6.4.1
General analysis of time-averaging

We consider the time-averaging operation, which leads to a more complicated bound on the difference of the solutions. We first prove a general result:

Lemma 2
.

For any first order linear differential equation
\dot{\rho}(t)=\mathcal{L}_{t}\rho(t)
there is a time-averaged version

\dot{\pi}(t)=\overline{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\pi(t),\quad\overline{\mathcal{L}_{t}}=\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}\mathcal{L}_{t+\tau}d\tau\ .

(212)

Note that we use a different symbol
\pi(t)
for the solution of the time-averaged equation. Assume the same initial condition:
\rho(0)=\pi(0)
.
Then the deviation between the two,
\delta\rho(t)\equiv\pi(t)-\rho(t)
, is bounded as:

\displaystyle\|\delta\rho(t)\|_{1}\leq[b_{2}(\min(t,T_{a}/2))+c_{\rho}\Lambda T_{a}/4]\max(e^{\Lambda(t-T_{a}/2)},1)-c_{\rho}\Lambda T_{a}/4\ ,

(213)

where
\Lambda,c_{\rho}
are constants such that
\forall t,\rho_{0},\|\rho_{0}\|_{1}\leq 1:\penalty\ \|\mathcal{L}_{t}(\rho_{0})\|_{1}\leq\Lambda
and the solution
\rho(t)
with the initial condition
\rho(0)=\rho_{0}
is bounded as
\|\rho(t)\|_{1}\leq c_{\rho}
. The function
b_{2}(t)
is:

b_{2}(t)\equiv 4c_{\rho}\frac{t}{T_{a}}+c_{\rho}\frac{4-2\Lambda T_{a}-(\Lambda T_{a}/2)^{2}}{\Lambda T_{a}}(1-e^{\Lambda t})\ .

(214)

An immediate corollary is:

\|\delta\rho(t)\|_{1}=O(\Lambda T_{a})\text{ for }t=O(\Lambda^{-1})\ .

(215)

The proof can be found in Appendix
F
. The
O(\Lambda T_{a})
corollary enters the estimate for
\|\rho_{BM,I}(t)-\rho_{BMT,I}(t)\|_{1}
we presented in Eq. (
61b
) in the derivation (using
\Lambda=4/\tau_{SB}
).
We will also need a compact bound on:

\frac{b_{2}(T_{a}/2)}{c_{\rho}}=e^{\Lambda T_{a}/2}\frac{(\Lambda T_{a}/2)^{2}+4(e^{-\Lambda T_{a}/2}-1+\Lambda T_{a}/2)}{\Lambda T_{a}}-\frac{\Lambda T_{a}}{4}\ .

(216)

One can prove that the expression in the brackets is upper-bounded by

e^{-\Lambda T_{a}/2}-1+\Lambda T_{a}/2\leq(\Lambda T_{a})^{2}/8\ .

(217)

This allows us to bound

b_{2}(T_{a}/2)\leq c_{\rho}\left(e^{\Lambda T_{a}/2}\frac{3\Lambda T_{a}}{4}-\frac{\Lambda T_{a}}{4}\right)\ .

(218)

Note that the function
b_{2}(t)
is monotonic:
b_{2}(t)\leq b_{2}(T_{a}/2)
. One can then loosen the bound in Lemma
2
:

\|\delta\rho(t)\|_{1}\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\penalty\ c_{\rho}\left(e^{\Lambda T_{a}/2}\frac{3\Lambda T_{a}}{4}-\frac{\Lambda T_{a}}{4}\right),&t\leq T_{a}/2\\
\penalty\ c_{\rho}\left(e^{\Lambda t}\frac{3\Lambda T_{a}}{4}-\frac{\Lambda T_{a}}{4}\right),&t>T_{a}/2\end{array}\right.=c_{\rho}\left(e^{\Lambda\max(t,T_{a}/2)}\frac{3\Lambda T_{a}}{4}-\frac{\Lambda T_{a}}{4}\right)\ .

(219)

6.4.2
Time averaging of the Redfield ME

We now focus on the specific equations of interest to us.
We already established in Eq. (
42c
) that we can choose
\Lambda=4/\tau_{SB}
(recall that
\Lambda
needs to upper bound the r.h.s.:
\max_{t,\rho_{\text{test}},\|\rho_{\text{test}}\|_{1}=1}\|\mathcal{L}^{BM,I}_{t}(\rho_{\text{test}})\|_{1}\leq\Lambda

The total time-averaging error (

\displaystyle\|\rho_{BM}-\rho_{BMT}\|_{1}=\|\delta\rho(t)\|_{1}\leq c_{BM}\left(e^{\Lambda\max(t,T_{a}/2)}\frac{3T_{a}}{\tau_{SB}}-\frac{T_{a}}{\tau_{SB}}\right)\ ,

(220)

We will use both the compact bound of Eq. (

), and the tightest bound available which is a repetition of Lemma

\displaystyle\|\delta\rho(t)\|_{1}\leq b_{2}(t)=[b_{2}(\min(t,T_{a}/2))+c_{BM}\Lambda T_{a}/4]\max(e^{\Lambda(t-T_{a}/2)},1)-c_{BM}\Lambda T_{a}/4\ ,

(221)

\displaystyle b_{2}(t)=4c_{BM}\frac{t}{T_{a}}+c_{BM}\frac{4-2\Lambda T_{a}-(\Lambda T_{a}/2)^{2}}{\Lambda T_{a}}(1-e^{\Lambda t}),\quad t<T_{a}/2\ .

(222)

6.4.3
Bound on the error due to enforcing complete positivity

Recall that after the time-averaged equation is obtained, we need to drop an extra portion of the integral to obtain complete positivity [the transition to Eq. (

)]. Before that, Eq. (

) was:

{\dot{\rho}}_{BMT}(t)=-i[H,\rho_{BMT}]+\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt^{\prime}\int_{-t}^{t^{\prime}}d\tau^{\prime}\mathcal{C}(\tau^{\prime}-t^{\prime})e^{-i(\omega t^{\prime}+\omega^{\prime}\tau^{\prime})}(A_{\omega}\rho_{BMT}A_{\omega^{\prime}}-\rho_{BMT}A_{\omega^{\prime}}A_{\omega})+\textrm{h.c.}\ ,

(223)

which changes into Eq. (

) (same terms, but the portion of the integral is subtracted):

\displaystyle{\dot{\rho}}_{C}(t)

\displaystyle=-i[H,\rho_{C}]+\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt^{\prime}\int_{-t}^{t^{\prime}}d\tau^{\prime}\ \mathcal{C}(\tau^{\prime}-t^{\prime})e^{-i(\omega t^{\prime}+\omega^{\prime}\tau^{\prime})}(A_{\omega}\rho_{C}A_{\omega^{\prime}}-\rho_{C}A_{\omega^{\prime}}A_{\omega})+\textrm{h.c.}-\mathcal{E}_{p}

(224a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt^{\prime}\int_{-t}^{-T_{a}/2}d\tau^{\prime}\ \mathcal{C}(\tau^{\prime}-t^{\prime})e^{-i(\omega t^{\prime}+\omega^{\prime}\tau^{\prime})}(A_{\omega}\rho_{C}A_{\omega^{\prime}}-\rho_{C}A_{\omega^{\prime}}A_{\omega})+\textrm{h.c.}

(224b)

Collecting
\sum_{\omega}
into
A(t)
we obtain:

\mathcal{E}_{p}=\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt^{\prime}\int_{-t}^{-T_{a}/2}d\tau^{\prime}\ \mathcal{C}(\tau^{\prime}-t^{\prime})(A(t^{\prime})\rho_{C}A(\tau^{\prime})-\rho_{C}A(\tau^{\prime})A(t^{\prime}))+\textrm{h.c.}\ ,

(225)

so that:

\|\mathcal{E}_{p}\|_{1}\leq\frac{4}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}dt^{\prime}\left|\int_{-t}^{-T_{a}/2}d\tau^{\prime}|\mathcal{C}(\tau^{\prime}-t^{\prime})|\right|\ .

(226)

\mathcal{E}_{p}
for (a)

Since the resulting equation is CP, we used
\|\rho_{C}\|_{1}=1
. We would like to bound this norm by splitting the integration domain into different times. The corresponding domains are illustrated in Fig.

For

\|\mathcal{E}_{p}\|_{1}\leq\frac{8}{\tau_{SB}}\frac{T_{a}/2-t}{T_{a}}\leq\frac{4}{\tau_{SB}}\ .

\tau^{\prime}-t^{\prime}=-\tau,\penalty\ t^{\prime}+T_{a}/2=\theta

\|\mathcal{E}_{p}\|_{1}\leq\frac{4}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{-T_{a}/2}dt^{\prime}\int_{-t}^{-T_{a}/2}d\tau^{\prime}|\mathcal{C}(\tau^{\prime}-t^{\prime})|=\frac{4}{T_{a}}\int_{0}^{T_{a}}d\theta\int_{\theta}^{t+\theta-T_{a}/2}d\tau|\mathcal{C}(\tau)|\ .

We now change the order of integration, noting that

\|\mathcal{E}_{p}\|_{1}\leq\frac{4}{T_{a}}\int_{0}^{T_{a}}d\theta\int_{\theta}^{t+\theta-T_{a}/2}d\tau|\mathcal{C}(\tau)|\leq\frac{4}{T_{a}}\int_{0}^{t+T_{a}/2}\tau|\mathcal{C}(\tau)|d\tau\ .

(229)

Recall that the total evolution time
T
entered the definition
\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}=\int_{0}^{T}t|\mathcal{C}(t)|dt
[Eq. (

)]. We can usually set

\|\mathcal{E}_{p}\|_{1}\leq\frac{4}{T_{a}}\int_{0}^{T}\tau|\mathcal{C}(\tau)|d\tau=\frac{4\tau_{B}}{T_{a}\tau_{SB}}\ .

Collecting everything, we obtain:

\|\mathcal{E}_{p}\|_{1}(t)\leq\frac{4}{\tau_{SB}}\left(\theta(t-T_{a}/2)\frac{\tau_{B}}{T_{a}}+\theta(T_{a}/2-t)\right)\ ,

(231)

where
\theta(x)=1,\penalty\ x>0
and zero otherwise.

Recall that
\mathcal{E}_{p}
is defined in Eq. (
65a
) as the extra term appearing if the CGME is written with the same expression as Eq. (

6.5
The Davies-Lindblad error

We write the CGME with an explicit dependence on
T_{a}

{\dot{\rho}_{C}}(t)=-i[H+H_{\mathrm{LS}},\rho_{C}]+\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\gamma_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}^{T_{a}}(A_{\omega}\rho_{C}A_{\omega^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\rho_{C},A_{\omega^{\prime}}A_{\omega}\right\})\ .

(258)

The error bound is known for arbitrary

to the limit

{\dot{\rho}_{C}}(t)=-i[H+H_{\mathrm{LS}},\rho_{C}]+\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\gamma_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}^{T_{a}=\infty}(A_{\omega}\rho_{C}A_{\omega^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\rho_{C},A_{\omega^{\prime}}A_{\omega}\right\})+\mathcal{E}_{R}\ .

(259)

In Appendix

{\dot{\rho}_{D}}(t)=-i[H+H_{\mathrm{LS}},\rho_{D}]+\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\gamma_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}^{T_{a}=\infty}(A_{\omega}\rho_{D}A_{\omega^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\rho_{D},A_{\omega^{\prime}}A_{\omega}\right\})\ .

(260)

We also show in Appendix

\|\mathcal{E}_{R}\|_{1}\leq 4\sum_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}|\gamma_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}^{T_{a}}-\gamma_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}^{T_{a}=\infty}|\leq\frac{4^{n}}{\tau_{SB}}O\left(\frac{1}{T_{a}\delta E}\right)\ .

(261)

Here the system Hilbert space dimension
2^{n}
appeared, and we note that
1/\delta E
could contain an additional exponential dependence on
n
. The accumulated error in the solution is bounded as:

\|\rho_{\text{true}}(t)-\rho_{\text{D}}(t)\|_{1}\leq(e^{\Lambda t}-1)\frac{\|\mathcal{E}_{R}\|_{1}}{\Lambda}+\|\rho_{\text{true}}-\rho_{\text{C}}\|_{1}\ .

(262)

In the long time limit the leading terms are:

\|\rho_{\text{true}}(t)-\rho_{\text{D}}(t)\|_{1}\leq e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}4^{n}O\left(\frac{1}{T_{a}\delta E}\right)+\frac{5T_{a}}{\tau_{SB}}e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}+e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}\frac{\tau_{B}}{T_{a}}+\|\rho_{\text{true}}-\rho_{\text{BM}}\|_{1}+o(e^{4t/\tau_{SB}})\ .

(263)

Assuming that

\|\rho_{\text{true}}(t)-\rho_{\text{D}}(t)\|_{1}\leq 2^{n}O\left(\frac{e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}}{\sqrt{\tau_{SB}\delta E}}\left(1+\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\right)\right)+\|\rho_{\text{true}}-\rho_{\text{BM}}\|_{1}\leq 2^{n}O\left(e^{12t/\tau_{SB}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau_{SB}\delta E}}+\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\right)\right)\ .

(264)

Assuming a constant system size

Then, assuming

2.2
:
g=1/\sqrt{\tau_{B}\tau_{SB}}
, where

is the coupling strength
g

7
Summary and Conclusions

Our primary goal in this work was to derive a Markovian master equation with a complete positivity guarantee that is in addition capable of incorporating arbitrary time-dependent driving, and that has a larger range of applicability than the Davies-Lindblad Markovian master equation. The master equation that achieves these desiderata is based on the coarse-graining approach introduced in Ref.
[
40
]
, which only considered the time-independent (no driving) case. We rederived this result by showing that it results from the non-CP Redfield master equation after neglecting part of the time-integration domain, which restored complete positivity. We then included arbitrary time-dependent driving and obtained a new CGME that is still CP, and retains essentially the same form as its time-independent counterpart. This is our central new result, given in Eq. (
22
).

This time-dependent CGME depends on one free parameter, the coarse graining time
T_{a}
. We showed that it is optimal to choose it as proportional to the geometric mean of two key timescales, the bath correlation time
\tau_{B}
and the fastest system decoherence timescale
\tau_{SB}
, given in Eq. (
11
). These timescales are related to the bath spectral density
\gamma(\omega)
via the inequalities
2/\tau_{SB}\geq\gamma(\omega)
and
2\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}\geq|\gamma^{\prime}(\omega)|
. The
1
-parameter family of CGME equations involves a continuous integral, or equivalently, a sum over exponentially many terms in the frequency domain. Alongside this
1
-parameter family, we also derived an approximation in terms of
3
-parameter family (
T_{a}
,
\Delta\epsilon
,
k^{*}
), given in Eq. (
78
). We showed that if
[H,A]=O(1)
, where
H
is the system Hamiltonian and
A\otimes B
is the interaction with the bath [Eq. (
3
)], then there is no system-size dependence in
\Delta\epsilon,k^{*}
, where
k^{*}
is the number of terms replacing the continuous integral.

Along the way we provided rigorous error bounds on three different master equations: Redfield, Davies-Lindblad, and the CGME. These bounds [Eqs. (
26
)-(
28
)] are expressed in terms of the dimensionless ratio
\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}
, and in the Davies-Lindblad case also in terms of the quantity
1/\sqrt{\tau_{SB}\delta E}
, where
\delta E
is the minimum level spacing. These results are valid even when
\|B\|
diverges, so they apply in particular to, e.g., oscillator baths. When the system-bath coupling strength
g
is finite we can write
\tau_{B}/\tau_{SB}=(g\tau_{B})^{2}
and
1/\sqrt{\tau_{SB}\delta E}=g\sqrt{\tau_{B}/\delta E}
.
These bounds provide a new perspective on the range of applicability of Markovian master equations: the Redfield and CG master equations apply in the regime where
g\tau_{B}\ll 1
, but the Davies-Lindblad master equation applies when in addition the minimum level spacing is large relative to
g^{2}\tau_{B}
. While these are all sufficient and not necessary conditions, if we informally view a large level spacing as a requirement, then it is a very onerous one indeed, since in many cases of interest the spacing shrinks rapidly (polynomially or even exponentially) with problem size, e.g., in adiabatic quantum computing
[
77
]
. Thus the guaranteed range of applicability of the Davies-Lindblad master equation is severely limited, while the CGME achieves complete positivity without imposing this cost.

To summarize the latter in the simplest possible terms, what we have shown is that the CGME and Redfield master equations are valid for fast baths, in the sense of a very simple integral condition on the bath correlation function. Ohmic and super-Ohmic baths satisfy this condition, provided the system-bath coupling strength is weak but constant in system size (for a local system). For the Davies-Lindblad master equation the coupling strength needs to be exponentially small in the system size. However, the Redfield equation and the CGME are not on equal footing: the Redfield equation provides no guarantee of complete positivity, while the CGME does.

We list a few potential applications of the CGME:

•

The CGME opens the door to studies of open system dynamics with arbitrary time-dependent drives
H(t)
. In particular, the drive need not be adiabatic. In fact, the time-dependent CGME even correctly describes a system driven by
\delta
-function pulses, as demonstrated in Sec.
5.1
for dynamical decoupling. Moreover, the time-dependent CGME overcomes an inherent problem in applying the Davies-Lindblad ME to circuit model quantum computing, in particular in the context of fault-tolerant quantum error correction. The latter ME is incompatible with the assumption of arbitrarily fast gates needed there
[
43
]
, but the time-dependent CGME can be consistently applied in this context. This opens the door to a rigorous analysis of fault-tolerance in a first-principles Markovian setting.

•

Earlier studies of the lifetime of the toric code at finite temperature took advantage of the fact that the Davies generators are local for commuting models
[
78
]
. The CGME provides local generators of open system finite temperature dynamics for non-commuting models (see Sec.
3.6
). This opens the door to studying the finite-temperature lifetime of, e.g., topological subsystem codes
[
79
]
in the thermodynamic limit.

•

Many-body localization effects in quantum annealing — previously studied only in the closed system setting
[
80
]
— can now be extended to finite temperature open system quantum annealing.

We hope that this work, including the applications above, will stimulate further research into the uses of the CGME, the only master equation we know of that provides a complete positivity guarantee, is locally generated, and applies for arbitrarily driven open quantum systems.

Appendix A
Proof that
\gamma(\omega)>0

Trivial case.

First consider
V=A\otimes B
. The function
\gamma(\omega)
is the Fourier transform of the correlation function:

\gamma(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}\mathcal{C}(t)dt=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{B}B(t)B]dt

(275)

Our derivations in this paper are for a stationary bath
\rho_{B}(t)=\rho_{B}
, or
[\rho_{B},H_{B}]=0
. Note that
[\sqrt{\rho_{B}},H_{B}]=0
follows.

\gamma(\omega)=\sum_{kl}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}\mathrm{Tr}[\sqrt{\rho_{B}}e^{iH_{B}t}\sqrt{\rho_{B}}Be^{-iH_{B}t}B]dt

(276)

Inserting the sum over bath eigenstates:

\gamma(\omega)=\sum_{kl}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i(\omega+E_{kl})t}\langle k|\sqrt{\rho_{B}}B|l\rangle\langle l|B\sqrt{\rho_{B}}|k\rangle dt

(277)

Integration results in delta-functions:

\gamma(\omega)=2\pi\sum_{kl}\delta(\omega-E_{lk})|\langle l|B\sqrt{\rho_{B}}|k\rangle|^{2}\geq 0

(278)

This is enough for all the results in this paper. A more complicated interaction with environment, however, requires a different proof.

General case.

We consider the case
V=\sum_{i}A_{i}\otimes B_{i}
, so that
\gamma(\omega)
becomes a matrix.
\rho_{B}
will still be assumed stationary:
[\rho_{B},H_{B}]=0
. Repeating the same steps, we arrive at

\gamma_{ij}(\omega)=2\pi\sum_{kl}\delta(\omega-E_{lk})\langle k|\sqrt{\rho_{B}}B_{i}|l\rangle\langle l|B_{j}\sqrt{\rho_{B}}|k\rangle

(279)

Thus we need to prove that the matrix

M_{ij}=\langle k|\sqrt{\rho_{B}}B_{i}|l\rangle\langle l|B_{j}\sqrt{\rho_{B}}|k\rangle

(280)

is positive. Let
v_{i}=\langle k|\sqrt{\rho_{B}}B_{i}|l\rangle
, then

M_{ij}=v_{i}v_{j}^{*}\ .

(281)

For positivity, we need to prove that for any vector
\vec{w}
with elements
w_{i}
it holds that
\sum_{ij}w_{i}M_{ij}w_{j}^{*}\geq 0
. Indeed:

\forall\vec{w},\quad\sum_{ij}w_{i}M_{ij}w_{j}^{*}=\sum_{ij}w_{i}v_{i}v_{j}^{*}w_{j}=\left|\sum_{i}v_{i}w_{i}\right|^{2}\geq 0\ .

(282)

Thus

\gamma(\omega)\geq 0

(283)

as a matrix.

Bochner’s theorem

Our proof above used the eigendecomposition of the bath, and
\gamma(\omega)
was a sum of Dirac
\delta
functions. In practice, a smooth
\gamma(\omega)
is used, corresponding to, e.g., a bath with a continuous spectrum.
In this sense it is desirable to avoid using bath eigenstates and
\delta
-functions altogether. Here we give another proof that uses Bochner’s theorem, as suggested, e.g., in the textbook
[
2
]
, and which avoids the issue mentioned above.

Lemma 3

(Bochner)
.

If a function
\phi(t)
is of positive type, i.e.
\forall n\in\mathbb{Z},\penalty\ t_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\penalty\ \xi_{i}\in\mathbb{C}

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\phi(t_{i}-t_{j})\xi_{i}\xi_{j}^{*}\geq 0,

(284)

and also
\phi(0)
is finite and
\phi(t)
is continuous at
t=0
, then its Fourier transform (if it exists) is non-negative:

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(t)e^{i\omega t}dt\geq 0

(285)

We again consider many interaction terms
V=\sum_{i}A_{i}\otimes B_{i}
(as in Sec.
3.7
) and a stationary
\rho_{B}
s.t.
[\rho_{B},H_{B}]=0
.
Since
\gamma(\omega)
is Hermitian we can diagonalize it using a unitary transformation:

D\equiv U\gamma U^{\dagger}\Rightarrow D_{\alpha\beta}=\sum_{i,j}U_{\alpha i}\gamma_{ij}U_{\beta j}^{*}\ .

(286)

D
is diagonal so we only need to consider the diagonal elements (i.e., the eigenvalues of
\gamma
). Substituting
\gamma_{ij}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega s}\mathcal{C}_{ij}(s)ds
, where
\mathcal{C}_{ij}(s)=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{B}B_{i}(s)B_{j}]
, gives

D_{\alpha}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega s}\left(\sum_{i,j}U_{\alpha i}\mathcal{C}_{ij}(s)U_{\alpha j}^{*}\right)ds\ .

(287)

We wish to show that
D_{\alpha}
is non-negative for each
\alpha
. To do this we must consider the function in parenthesis.
D_{\alpha}
is the Fourier transform of this function so if we can show that it is of
positive type
then
D_{\alpha}
must be positive by Bochner’s theorem
[
81
]
. Define the following function with
\left\{t_{i}\right\}
an arbitrary time partition:

f_{mn}^{\alpha}\equiv\sum_{i,j}U_{\alpha i}\mathcal{C}_{ij}(t_{m}-t_{n})U_{\alpha j}^{*}\ .

(288)

Note that

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\alpha\beta}(t,t-\tau)

\displaystyle\equiv\langle B_{\alpha}(t)B_{\beta}(t-\tau)\rangle_{B}=\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(e^{iH_{B}t}B_{\alpha}e^{-iH_{B}t}e^{iH_{B}(t-\tau)}B_{\beta}e^{-iH_{B}(t-\tau)}\rho_{B}\bigr)

(289a)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(e^{-iH_{B}(t-\tau)}e^{iH_{B}t}B_{\alpha}e^{-iH_{B}t}e^{iH_{B}(t-\tau)}B_{\beta}\rho_{B}\bigr)=\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(e^{iH_{B}\tau}B_{\alpha}e^{-iH_{B}\tau}B_{\beta}\rho_{B}\bigr)=\langle B_{\alpha}(\tau)B_{\beta}\,\rangle_{B}

(289b)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{\alpha\beta}(\tau,0)\equiv\mathcal{B}_{\alpha\beta}(\tau)\ ,

(289c)

Now use the property
\langle B_{\alpha}(s)B_{\beta}(0)\rangle=\langle B_{\alpha}(t)B_{\beta}(t-s)\rangle
[Eq. (
289c
)] to write
f_{mn}^{\alpha}
as

f_{mn}^{\alpha}=\sum_{i,j}U_{\alpha i}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho_{B}B_{i}(t_{m})B_{j}(t_{n})\right]U_{\alpha j}^{*}=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\rho_{B}\sum_{i}U_{\alpha i}B_{i}(t_{m})\sum_{j}U_{\alpha j}^{*}B_{j}(t_{n})\right)\ .

(290)

We need to show that
f^{\alpha}
is a positive matrix. For arbitrary
\ket{v}
we have

\displaystyle\bra{v}f^{\alpha}\ket{v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}v_{m}^{*}v_{n}f_{mn}^{\alpha}=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\left(\sum_{i,m}v_{m}^{*}U_{\alpha i}\sqrt{\rho_{B}}B_{i}(t_{m})\right)\left(\sum_{j,n}v_{n}U_{\alpha j}^{*}B_{j}(t_{n})\sqrt{\rho_{B}}\right)\right]

(291a)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left(M_{\alpha}^{\dagger}M_{\alpha}\right)\geq 0\ ,

(291b)

where
M_{\alpha}\equiv\sum_{i,m}v_{m}^{*}U_{\alpha i}\sqrt{\rho_{B}}B_{i}(t_{m})
.

We have established that
\bra{v}f^{\alpha}\ket{v}\geq 0
for any time partition
\left\{t_{i}\right\}
. Therefore
D_{\alpha}
is non-negative by Bochner’s theorem. Consequently,
\gamma\geq 0
as a matrix since all its eigenvalues are non-negative.

The Davies-Lindblad equation is the infinite coarse-graining time limit of the CGME

The discretization of an integral introduces errors as follows:

Proof.

By the triangle inequality:

(313a)

\displaystyle+\left(\int_{-\infty}^{-\Delta\epsilon(k^{*}-0.5)}+\int_{\Delta\epsilon(k^{*}-0.5)}^{\infty}\right)d\epsilon\|f(\epsilon)\|_{1}\ .

(313b)

We apply the triangle inequality to pull the sum out:

\left\|\int_{-\Delta\epsilon(k^{*}-0.5)}^{\Delta\epsilon(k^{*}-0.5)}d\epsilon f(\epsilon)-\Delta\epsilon\sum_{k,\epsilon=\Delta\epsilon k,\penalty\ |k|<k^{*}}f(\epsilon)\right\|_{1}\leq\sum_{k,\epsilon=\Delta\epsilon k,\penalty\ |k|<k^{*}}\left\|\int_{\epsilon-\Delta\epsilon/2}^{\epsilon+\Delta\epsilon/2}d\mu f(\mu)-\Delta\epsilon f(\epsilon)\right\|_{1}\ .

To bound this, we use

which applied to the interval

(316)

Appendix E
An error bound that is linear in
t

Lemma 5
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a linear superoperator and consider the pair of equations

\dot{x}(t)=\mathcal{L}x(t)+\mathcal{E},\quad\dot{y}(t)=\mathcal{L}y(t)\ .

(331)

If any solution
y(t)
possesses the property that
\forall t,y(0)
such that
\|y(0)\|_{p}\leq 1:\|y(t)\|_{p}\leq c_{y}
, then for any finite
t
:

\|x(t)-y(t)\|_{p}\leq c_{y}t\|\mathcal{E}\|_{p}\ .

(332)

The result holds in the same form for the operator (
p=\infty
, omitted in our notation) and trace (
p=1
) norms.

Proof.

The linear differential equation for
y
has a unique solution in a neighborhood of any initial condition. A trivial corollary is that the evolution operator is reversible for any finite
t
:

e^{\mathcal{L}t}e^{-\mathcal{L}t}=\mathbbm{1}\ .

(333)

This is an equality between superoperators. Now we can express the difference as:

x(t)-y(t)=e^{\mathcal{L}t}(e^{-\mathcal{L}t}x(t)-y(0))\ .

(334)

The explicit form of the solution
x(t)
is:

x(t)=e^{\mathcal{L}t}x(0)+e^{\mathcal{L}t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\mathcal{L}\tau}\mathcal{E}d\tau\ ,

(335)

which yields, using Eq. (
334
):

x(t)-y(t)=e^{\mathcal{L}t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\mathcal{L}\tau}\mathcal{E}d\tau=\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mathcal{L}(t-\tau)}\mathcal{E}d\tau\ .

(336)

We now take the norm of both sides:

\|x(t)-y(t)\|_{p}\leq t\|e^{\mathcal{L}(t-\tau)}\mathcal{E}\|_{p}\ .

(337)

By linearity of the time evolution and the norm:

\|e^{\mathcal{L}(t-\tau)}\mathcal{E}\|_{p}=\|\mathcal{E}\|_{p}\|e^{\mathcal{L}(t-\tau)}y\|_{p},\quad y=\frac{\mathcal{E}}{\|\mathcal{E}\|_{p}}

(338)

Note that
\|y\|_{p}=1
. Since we can take
y
as the initial condition of the equation
\dot{y}(t)=\mathcal{L}y(t)
, and since
e^{\mathcal{L}(t-\tau)}y
will then be the solution at time
t-\tau
, we have, by assumption of the lemma:

\|e^{\mathcal{L}(t-\tau)}y\|_{p}\leq c_{y}\ .

(339)

Note that we had to make these assumptions for any
y
of bounded norm, not just density operators. It now follows from Eq. (
337
) that:

\|x(t)-y(t)\|_{p}\leq c_{y}t\|\mathcal{E}\|_{p}\ .

(340)

∎

In particular, if
\mathcal{L}
is a positive trace-preserving map then
c_{y}=1
.

The only error for which we can apply Lemma
5
in this work is the Markov error, which we present here:

\|\rho^{BM}_{t}-\rho^{B}_{t}\|_{1}\leq 16\frac{\tau_{B}c_{B}c_{BM}t}{\tau_{SB}^{2}}\ ,

(341)

where
c_{y}=c_{BM}
and we used Eq. (
203

c_{B}
. We instead used a loose bound from Eq. (
204
) since it results in a more compact expression.
Apart from this we have no occasion to use Lemma
5
in this work, since not all the equations appearing in our derivation are invertible. This is why we instead use Lemma

\|\rho_{BM,I}(t)-{\rho}_{C\sqrt{5},I}(t)\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\tau_{B}}{5\tau_{SB}}}\left(\left(3e^{\Lambda t}-1\right)c_{BM}+\left(7e^{\Lambda t}-5\right)\right)\ .

(365)

Now we find
\epsilon^{*}
:

1+c_{BM}=\frac{\epsilon^{*}}{\sqrt{5}}\left(\left(3e^{2\epsilon^{*}/\sqrt{5}}-1\right)c_{BM}+\left(7e^{2\epsilon^{*}/\sqrt{5}}-5\right)\right)\geq\frac{\epsilon^{*}}{\sqrt{5}}(2c_{BM}+2),\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{2\epsilon^{*}}{\sqrt{5}}\leq 1\ .

(366)

This allows one to bound:

\|\rho_{BM,I}(t)-{\rho}_{C\sqrt{5},I}(t)\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\tau_{B}}{5\tau_{SB}}}\left(\left(3e^{\Lambda t+1}-1\right)c_{BM}+\left(7e^{\Lambda t+1}-5\right)\right)

(367)

for all
t
.

Appendix H
Optimal time

We begin with Eq. (
357
), and use it to write down the optimum
t
-dependent coarse-graining time:

T_{a}(t)=\sqrt{c_{\Lambda}\tau_{B}\tau_{SB}\frac{(e^{\Lambda t}-1)}{(3c_{BM}+2)e^{\Lambda t}-c_{BM}}}\ .

(368)

In principle we could consider a family of equations with different
T_{a}(t)
and solve each one of them just to obtain
\rho(t)
at one point. This is not a significant overhead. We proceed with the goal of obtaining a single equation since it is conceptually simpler. To this end we now define the bound relevance time
t^{*}
, which is the largest time for which our bound is still better than the trivial bound
\|\rho_{BM,I}(t)-{\rho}_{C,I}(t)\|\leq 1+c_{BM}
:

1+c_{BM}(t^{*})=((3c_{BM}(t^{*})+2)e^{\Lambda t^{*}}-c_{BM}(t^{*}))\frac{T_{a}}{\tau_{SB}}+(e^{\Lambda t^{*}}-1)\frac{c_{\Lambda}\tau_{B}}{T_{a}}\ .

(369)

We would like to optimize
T_{a}
at
t=t^{*}
, which would mean that
t^{*}
is maximized. The corresponding equation for
t^{*}
is:

1+c_{BM}(t^{*})=2\sqrt{c_{\Lambda}\frac{\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}(e^{\Lambda t^{*}}-1)((3c_{BM}(t^{*})+2)e^{\Lambda t^{*}}-c_{BM}(t^{*}))}\ ,

(370)

and solving the resulting quadratic equation yields:

e^{\Lambda t^{*}}=\frac{4c_{BM}(t^{*})+2+Y}{2(3c_{BM}(t^{*})+2)},\quad Y=\sqrt{4(c_{BM}(t^{*})+1)^{2}+2(1+c_{BM}(t^{*}))^{2}(3c_{BM}(t^{*})+2)(\tau_{SB}/\tau_{B}c_{\Lambda})}\ .

(371)

Thus
T_{a}
is:

T_{a}=\sqrt{c_{\Lambda}\tau_{B}\tau_{SB}\frac{-2c_{BM}(t^{*})-2+Y}{(3c_{BM}(t^{*})+2)(2c_{BM}(t^{*})+2+Y)}}\ .

(372)

And the error is:

\displaystyle\|\rho_{BM}(t)-{\rho}_{C*}(t)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq((3c_{BM}(t^{*})+2)e^{\Lambda\text{max}(t,T_{a}/2)}-c_{BM}(t^{*}))\sqrt{\frac{c_{\Lambda}\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\frac{-2c_{BM}(t^{*})-2+Y}{(3c_{BM}(t^{*})+2)(2c_{BM}(t^{*})+2+Y)}}

(373a)

\displaystyle+(e^{\Lambda\text{max}(t,T_{a}/2)}-1)\sqrt{\frac{c_{\Lambda}\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\frac{(3c_{BM}(t^{*})+2)(2c_{BM}(t^{*})+2+Y)}{-2c_{BM}(t^{*})-2+Y}}

(373b)

We should only use these expressions if
t^{*}>T_{a}/2
.
For
\tau_{SB}\ll\tau_{B}
(when
t^{*}
is not likely to be
>T_{a}/2
), this changes the asymptotic form of
T_{a}
to
T_{a}\sim\tau_{SB}
. In the regime
\tau_{SB}\gg\tau_{B}
that we normally address with the CGME, we expect these formulas to be a minor improvement on the loose bounds presented in the main text. Their main advantage is a tighter bound on the error for
t>T_{a}/2
,
t\sim\tau_{SB}
.

Appendix I
Proof of the bound (
269b
) for the Redfield limits of integration error

Starting from Eq. (
268
), we first send the integration limit to infinity:

\int_{0}^{4t/\tau_{SB}}\min(1,\frac{4\tau_{B}}{x\tau_{SB}}+\epsilon_{T})e^{-x}dx\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\min(1,\frac{4\tau_{B}}{x\tau_{SB}}+\epsilon_{T})e^{-x}dx=\epsilon_{T}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\min(1-\epsilon_{T},\frac{4\tau_{B}}{x\tau_{SB}})e^{-x}dx\ .

(374)

The
\min
function evaluates to
1-\epsilon_{T}
when
x\leq x^{*}
and to
\frac{4\tau_{B}}{x\tau_{SB}}
when
x>x^{*}
, where

x^{*}=\frac{4\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}(1-\epsilon_{T})}\ .

(375)

Therefore:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\min(1-\epsilon_{T},\frac{4\tau_{B}}{x\tau_{SB}})e^{-x}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x^{*}}(1-\epsilon_{T})e^{-x}dx+\int_{x^{*}}^{\infty}\frac{4\tau_{B}}{x\tau_{SB}}e^{-x}dx

(376a)

\displaystyle=(1-\epsilon_{T})(1-e^{-x^{*}})+\frac{4\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}E_{1}(x^{*})\ ,

(376b)

where
E_{1}(x)
denotes the exponential integral function:
E_{1}(x)\equiv\int_{x}^{\infty}t^{-1}e^{-t}dt
. A tight bound in terms of elementary functions is given by
[
82
]
[Thm. 2]:

E_{1}(x)\leq-\ln(1-e^{-x})\quad(0<x<\infty)\ .

(377)

Thus

\displaystyle\|\rho_{BM}(t)-\rho_{R}(t)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq c_{BM}e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}\left[\epsilon_{T}+(1-\epsilon_{T})(1-e^{-x^{*}})-\frac{4\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\ln(1-e^{-x^{*}})\right]

(378a)

\displaystyle\leq c_{BM}e^{4t/\tau_{SB}}\left[\epsilon_{T}+\frac{4\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\left(1-\ln\left(1-e^{-\frac{4\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}(1-\epsilon_{T})}}\right)\right)\right]\ ,

(378b)

where in the second line we used that
\forall x
:
1-e^{-x}<x
.

Alternatively, and using more elementary methods, assuming
x^{*}<1
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\min(1,\frac{4\tau_{B}}{x\tau_{SB}}+\epsilon_{T})e^{-x}dx

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{T}+(1-\epsilon_{T})(1-e^{-x^{*}})+\int_{x^{*}}^{1}\frac{4\tau_{B}}{x\tau_{SB}}e^{-x}dx+\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{4\tau_{B}}{x\tau_{SB}}e^{-x}dx

(379a)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{T}+(1-\epsilon_{T})x^{*}+\int_{x^{*}}^{1}\frac{4\tau_{B}}{x\tau_{SB}}dx+\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{4\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}e^{-x}dx

(379b)

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{T}+(1-\epsilon_{T})x^{*}-\frac{4\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\text{ln}x^{*}+\frac{4\tau_{B}}{e\tau_{SB}}

(379c)

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{T}+\frac{4\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}+\frac{4\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}\text{ln}\frac{\tau_{SB}(1-\epsilon_{T})}{4\tau_{B}}+\frac{4\tau_{B}}{e\tau_{SB}}\quad(x^{*}<1)\ ,

(379d)

while when
x^{*}\geq 1
the term with the logarithm is absent:

\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{\infty}\min(1,\frac{4\tau_{B}}{x\tau_{SB}}+\epsilon_{T})e^{-x}dx\leq\epsilon_{T}+\frac{4\tau_{B}}{\tau_{SB}}+\frac{4\tau_{B}}{e\tau_{SB}}\quad(x^{*}\geq 1)}\ .

(380)

Combining these two cases directly yields Eq. (
269b
), which is an upper bound on the tighter bound presented in Eq. (
378b
).
