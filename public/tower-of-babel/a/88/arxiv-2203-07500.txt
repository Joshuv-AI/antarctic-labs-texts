Abstract
District cooling energy plants (DCEPs) consisting of chillers, cooling towers, and thermal energy storage (TES) systems consume a considerable amount of electricity. Optimizing the scheduling of the TES and chillers to take advantage of time-varying electricity price is a challenging optimal control problem. The classical method, model predictive control (MPC), requires solving a high dimensional mixed-integer nonlinear program (MINLP) because of the on/off actuation of the chillers and charging/discharging of TES, which are computationally challenging. RL is an attractive alternative to MPC: the real time control computation is a low-dimensional optimization problem that can be easily solved. However, the performance of an RL controller depends on many design choices. In this paper, we propose a Q-learning based reinforcement learning (RL) controller for this problem. Numerical simulation results show that the proposed RL controller is able to reduce energy cost over a rule-based baseline controller by approximately 8%, comparable to savings reported in the literature with MPC for similar DCEPs. We describe the design choices in the RL controller, including basis functions, reward function shaping, and learning algorithm parameters. Compared to existing work on RL for DCEPs, the proposed controller is designed for continuous state and actions spaces.



I
Introduction

In the U.S., 75% of the electricity is consumed by buildings, and a large part of that is due to heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) systems
[
1
]
. In cities and campuses, a large part of the HVAC’s share of electricity is consumed in District Cooling Energy Plants (DCEPs), also called central plants. A DCEP produces and supplies chilled water to a group of buildings it serves (hence the moniker “district”), and the air handling units in those buildings use the chilled water to cool and dehumidify air before supplying to building interiors. Figure
1
shows a schematic of such a plant, which consists of multiple chillers that produce chilled water, a cooling tower that rejects the heat extracted from chillers to the environment, and a thermal energy storage system (TES) for storing chilled water. Chillers - the most electricity intensive equipment in the DCEP - produces more chilled water than buildings’ need when the electricity price is low and stored the extra chilled water in the TES. Then the chilled water in the TES can be used during periods of high electricity price to reduce the total electricity cost.

At present, DCEPs are typically operated with rule based control algorithms that use heuristics to reduce cost while meeting the load. But making the best use of the chillers and the TES to keep electricity cost at the minimum requires non-trivial decision making. Because of time coupling, especially due to the TES, the problem is best cast as an optimal control problem, of which the objective is the total electricity cost while meeting the thermal load from the buildings and equipment limitations are the constraints.

A growing body of work has proposed algorithms for optimal real-time control of DCEPs. This includes Model Predictive Control (MPC), such as
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
. A twin challenge here is the discrete nature of certain decision variables, such as chiller on/off and TES charge/discharge commands, and the highly nonlinear dynamics of the equipment in DCEPs. This is a computationally challenging problem, which is typically addressed by avoiding it, such as by a MILP approximation. Solving large MILPs is also challenging, though.

An alternative to MPC is Reinforcement Learning (RL): an umbrella term for a set of tools used to approximate an optimal policy from data collected from a physical system, or more frequently, its simulation. Despite a computationally burdensome learning phase, real-time control is simpler since control computation is an evaluation of a state-feedback policy. This advantage is particularly strong for problems involving discrete decision variables. RL is thus an attractive candidate for optimal control of DCEPs.

In this paper we propose an RL controller for a DCEP with multiple chillers, a cooling tower and a TES, with a goal of reducing energy cost (over a rule-based control logic) while meeting the cooling load from buildings. The proposed controller uses a batch RL algorithm similar to the “convex Q-learning” proposed in recent work
[
7
]
and the classical least squares policy iteration (LSPI) algorithm
[
8
]
. Closed loop simulations with a model calibrated using data from a campus in Singapore shows a cost saving around 8%. This value is comparable to that of MPC controllers with mixed-integer formulation reported in the literature
[
2
,
6
]
. Compared to MPC, the real time computation burden of the RL controller is trivial.

The baseline controller, that the proposed RL controller is compared to, has been designed to utilize the TES and time varying electricity prices (to the extent possible with heuristics) to reduce energy costs. The RL controller and baseline controller have the same information about the price to learn the trends in its time-variation: the current price and a backward moving average.

Design of RL controllers for practically relevant applications with non-trivial dynamics is quite challenging. Firstly, a disadvantage of RL is that its performance depends on myriad design choices, not only on the stage cost/reward, function approximation architecture and bases, learning algorithm and method of exploration, but also on the choice of the state space itself. Secondly, training a RL controller is computationally intensive. Thirdly, and perhaps the most important for applications, if a particular set of design choices lead to a policy that does not perform well, there is no principled method to look for improvement. Although RL is being extensively studied in the control community, most works demonstrate their algorithms on plants with simple dynamics with a small number of states and inputs
[
9
,
10
]
. The model for a DCEP used in this paper, arguably still simple compared to available simulation models (e.g.
[
11
]
), is quite complex: it has 8-states, 5 control inputs, 3 disturbance inputs, and requires solving an optimization problem to compute the next state given the current state, control and disturbance.

II
System description and control problem

The DCEP contains multiple chillers and chilled water pumps, a cooling tower and cooling water pumps, and finally a collection of buildings that uses the chilled water to provide air conditioning; see Figure
2
.

Fig. 2:
Detailed description of District Cooling Energy Plant.

The heat load from the buildings is absorbed by the cold chilled water supplied by the DCEP, and thus the return chilled water temperature is warmer. This part of the water is called
load water
, and the related variables are denoted by superscript
lw
for “load water”; see Figure
2
. The chiller loop (subscript
ch
) removes this heat and transmits it to the cooling water loop (subscript
cw
). The cooling water loop absorbs this heat and sends it to the cooling tower, where this heat is then rejected to the ambient. The cooling tower will cool down the cooling water returned from the chiller by passing both the sprayed cooling water and ambient air through a fill. During this process, a small amount of water spray will evaporate into the air, removing heat from the cooling water. The cooling water loss due to its evaporation is replenished by fresh water, thus we assume the supply water flow rate equals to the return water flow rate at the cooling tower. A fan or a set of fans is used to maintain the ambient airflow at the cooling tower.

Connected to the chilled water loop is a TES tank that stores water (subscript
\text{tw})
. The total volume of the water in the TES tank is constant, but a thermocline separates two volumes: cold water that is supplied by the chiller (subscript
twc
for “tank water, cold”) and warm water returned from the load (subscript
tww
for “tank water, warm”).

DCEP dynamics

t_{s}

\displaystyle u_{k}=[n^{\text{ch}}_{k},\ \dot{m}^{\text{lw}}_{k},\ \dot{m}_{k}^{\text{tw}},\ \dot{m}^{\text{cw}}_{k},\dot{m}_{k}^{\text{oa}}]^{T}\in{\sf{U}},

\displaystyle{\sf{U}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\{0,\dots,n_{\text{max}}^{\text{ch}}\}\times[\dot{m}_{\text{min}}^{\text{lw}},\dot{m}_{\text{max}}^{\text{lw}}]\times[\dot{m}_{\text{min}}^{\text{tw}},\dot{m}_{\text{max}}^{\text{tw}}]\dots

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle[\dot{m}_{\text{min}}^{\text{cw}},\dot{m}_{\text{max}}^{\text{cw}}]\times[\dot{m}_{\text{min}}^{\text{oa}},\dot{m}_{\text{max}}^{\text{oa}}]\subset\mathbb{R}^{5}.

(2)

The state of the DCEP is
x^{p}
(superscript

\displaystyle x^{p}_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}[T_{k}^{\text{lw,r}},s_{k}^{\text{tww}},s_{k}^{\text{twc}},T_{k}^{\text{twc}},T_{k}^{\text{tww}},T_{k}^{\text{chw,s}},T_{k}^{\text{cw,r}},T_{k}^{\text{cw,s}}]^{T},

(3)

where

,s

2
. The plant state

is affected by exogenous disturbances

The control command and disturbances affect the state through a highly nonlinear dynamic model:

\displaystyle x^{p}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=f(x^{p}_{k},u_{k},w^{p}_{k}),

(4)

that is described in the Appendix.
The dynamics (

II-D

The control problem

The control goal is to operate the DCEP in a way so that the cost is minimized while the cooling load
q^{L,\text{ref}}_{k}
is met. The total electric power consumption of the DCEP is:

\displaystyle P^{\text{tot}}_{k}=P^{\text{ch}}_{k}+P^{\text{ct}}_{k}+P^{\text{chw},\text{pump}}_{k}+P^{\text{cw},\text{pump}}_{k},

(9)

so that the electricity cost at time
k
is

\displaystyle c^{\text{E}}_{k}=t_{s}\rho_{k}P^{\text{tot}}_{k},

(10)

where
\rho_{k}

(\frac{\text{USD}}{\text{kWh}})
is the electricity price. The control problem for a scheduling horizon
T
is then:

\displaystyle\min_{\{u_{k}\}_{k=0}^{T-1}}\

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}c_{k}^{\text{E}}

(11)

s.t.

\displaystyle x^{p}_{k+1}=f(x^{p}_{k},u_{k},w^{p}_{k}),\ x^{p}_{0}=x,

\displaystyle q^{L}_{k}(x^{p}_{k},u_{k})=q^{L,\text{ref}}_{k}\ \text{and}\ u_{k}\in{\sf{U}}(x^{p}_{k},w_{k}).

where
q^{L}_{k}(x^{p}_{k},u_{k})
is the actual cooling load met by the DCEP, which is a complicated function of the states and inputs, and

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\sf{U}}(x^{p}_{k},w_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\big\{&u_{k}\in{\sf{U}}:\ \dot{m}^{\text{lw}}_{k}\geq\dot{m}^{\text{lw}}_{\text{min}},\ \dot{q}^{\text{ct},\text{rej}}_{k}\geq\dot{q}^{\text{evap}}_{k},\\
&\dot{m}^{\text{lw}}_{k}+\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k}\leq\dot{m}^{\text{chw}}_{k},\\
&T^{\text{cw,s},-}_{k+1}\leq T^{\text{cw,s}}_{k+1}\leq T^{\text{cw,s},+}_{k+1},\\
&S^{\text{tw}}_{\min}\leq S^{\text{twc}}_{k}+t_{s}\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k}\leq S^{\text{tw}}_{\max}\big\},\end{split}

(12)

where
T^{\text{cw,s},-}_{k+1}=\max\big(25,T^{\text{oawb}}_{k+1}+0.5\big)
and
T^{\text{cw,s},+}_{k+1}=\min\big(T^{\text{cw,r}}_{k},40\big)
.

The optimization problem (
11
) is an MINLP due to
n^{\text{ch}}_{k}
being an integer and the nonlinear dynamics (
4
). The solution of (
11
) provides the best achievable control performance over the horizon
T
: only an oracle equipped with a perfect prediction of disturbance over the whole planning horizon will be able to compute it. With imperfect forecasts, one could use MPC to bring robustness to forecast uncertainty. Still, the challenge of computing the MINLP is a severe one. The ref.
[
2
]
used piece-wise linearization leading to an MILP. In the following we propose a RL controller that does not need to solve even MILPs.

All the plant state variables
x^{p}
can be measured with sensors. The same for the disturbances, except there is no sensor for the cooling load
q^{L,\text{ref}}_{k}
. There is a large literature on estimating and/or forecasting loads for buildings; see
[
23
,
24
]
and references therein. We therefore assume
q^{L,\text{ref}}_{k}
is known to the controller at
k
.

III
RL basics and proposed controller

III-A

RL basics

For the following construction, let
x
represent the state with state space
{\sf{X}}
and
u
the input with input space
{\sf{U}}(x)
. Now consider the following infinite horizon discounted optimal control problem

\displaystyle J^{*}(\bar{x})=

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{U}}\quad\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}c(x_{k},u_{k}),\quad x_{0}=\bar{x},

(13)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad x_{k+1}=F(x_{k},u_{k}),\ u_{k}\in{\sf{U}}(x_{k}),

where
\mathbf{U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\{u_{0},\dots,\}
,
c:{\sf{X}}\times{\sf{U}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
is the stage cost,
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the discount factor,
F(\cdot,\cdot)
defines the dynamics, and
J^{*}:{\sf{X}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the optimal value function. The goal of the RL framework is to learn an approximate optimal policy
\phi:{\sf{X}}\rightarrow{\sf{U}}
for the problem (
13
) without requiring explicit knowledge of the model
F(\cdot,\cdot)
. The learning process is based on the
Q
function. Given a policy
\phi
for the problem (
13
), the
Q
function associated with this policy is defined as

\displaystyle Q_{\phi}(x,u)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}c(x_{k},u_{k}),\quad x_{0}=x,\quad u_{0}=u,

(14)

where for
k\geq 0
we have
x_{k+1}=F(x_{k},u_{k})
and for
k\geq 1
we have
u_{k}=\phi(x_{k})
. A well known fact is that the optimal policy satisfies
[
25
]

\displaystyle\phi^{*}(x)=\arg\min_{u\in{\sf{U}}(x)}\ Q^{*}(x,u),\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in{\sf{X}},

(15)

where
Q^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}Q_{\phi^{*}}
is the
Q
function for the optimal policy. Further, for any policy
\phi
the
Q
function satisfies the following fixed point relation

\displaystyle Q_{\phi}(x,u)=c(x,u)+\gamma Q_{\phi}\big(x^{+},\phi(x^{+})\big),

(16)

for all
u\in{\sf{U}}(x)
and
x\in{\sf{X}}
and
x^{+}=F(x,u)
. The above relation is termed here as the fixed-policy Bellman equation.

III-B

RL algorithm: data driven policy iteration

The learning algorithm has two parts: policy evaluation and policy improvement. First, in policy evaluation, a parametric approximation to the fixed policy
Q
function is learned by constructing a residual term from (
16
) as an error to minimized. Second, in policy improvement, the learned approximation is used to define a new policy based on (
15
). For policy evaluation, suppose for a policy
\phi
the
Q
function is approximated as

\displaystyle Q^{\theta}_{\phi}(x,u)\approx\hat{q}_{\theta}(x,u)

(17)

where
\hat{q}_{\theta}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the function approximator (e.g., a neural network) and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the parameter vector (e.g., weights of the network). To fit the approximator, suppose that the system is simulated for
{\sf{T}}_{\text{sim}}
time so that
{\sf{T}}_{\text{sim}}
tuples of
(x_{k},u_{k},x_{k+1})
are collected to produce
{\sf{T}}_{\text{sim}}
values of

\displaystyle d_{k}(\theta)=c(x_{k},u_{k})+\gamma\hat{q}_{\theta}(x_{k+1},\phi(x_{k+1}))-\hat{q}_{\theta}(x_{k},u_{k}),

(18)

which is the temporal difference error for the approximator.
We then obtain
\theta^{*}
by solving the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\begin{split}\theta^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\arg\min_{\theta}\ &\|D(\theta)\|_{2}+\alpha\|\theta-\bar{\theta}\|_{2},\end{split}

(19)

where
D(\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}[d_{0}(\theta),\dots,d_{{\sf{T}}_{\text{sim}}-1}(\theta)]
. The term
\|\theta-\bar{\theta}\|_{2}
is a regularizer and
\alpha
is a gain. The values of
\bar{\theta}
and
\alpha
are specified in step 3) of Algorithm
1
. The solution to (
19
) results in
Q^{\theta^{*}}_{\phi}
, which is an approximation to
Q_{\phi}
. The quantity
Q^{\theta^{*}}_{\phi}
can be used to obtain an improved policy, denoted
\phi^{+}
, through

\displaystyle\phi^{+}(x)=\arg\min_{u\in{\sf{U}}(x)}\ Q^{\theta^{*}}_{\phi}(x,u),\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in{\sf{X}}.

(20)

This process of policy evaluation (
19
) and policy improvement (
20
) can be repeated. This iterative procedure is described formally in Algorithm
1
.

III-C

Proposed RL controller for DCEP

We now specify the ingredients required to apply Algorithm

Namely, (1) the state description, (2) the cost function design, (3) the approximation architecture, and (4) the exploration strategy. III-C

In RL, the construction of the state space is an important feature, and the state is not necessarily the same as the plant state. To define the state space for RL, we first denote

The expanded state for RL is:

\displaystyle x_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}[x_{k}^{p},w_{k}]^{T}\in{\sf{X}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{12}.

(22)

Notice that the set (
12

\displaystyle{\sf{U}}(x_{k})={\sf{U}}(x_{k}^{p},w_{k}).

(23)

Note that
a state feedback policy is implementable

III-C
2
Design of stage cost

The design of the stage cost is an important aspect of RL. We wish to obtain a policy that tracks the load

\displaystyle c(x_{k},u_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}c_{k}^{\text{E}}+\kappa\left(q^{L}_{k}-q^{L,\text{ref}}_{k}\right)^{2},

where

III-C

Approximation architecture

We choose the following linear-in-the-parameter approximation of the

\displaystyle Q^{\theta}_{\phi}(x,u)\approx\hat{q}_{\theta}(x,u)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\psi_{\ell}(x,u)\theta_{\ell},

(25)

where
\psi_{\ell}(x,u)
are basis functions and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the parameter vector. We elect a quadratic basis, so that each
\psi_{\ell}(x,u)
is of the form
xu
,
x^{2}
, or
u^{2}
. A subset of all possible combinations are included in the basis. We can equivalently express the approximation (
25
) as

\displaystyle Q^{\theta}_{\phi}(x,u)=[x,u]P_{\theta}[x,u]^{T},

(26)

for appropriately chosen
P_{\theta}
. In this form it is straightforward to ensure the Q function non-negative by enforcing the convex constraint
P_{\theta}\geq 0
.

III-C
4
Exploration strategy

Exploration refers to how the state/input sequences appearing in step 1) of Algorithm
1
are simulated. We utilize a modified
\epsilon-
greedy exploration scheme. At time step
k
of iteration
j
, we obtain the input
u_{k}^{j}
from one of three methods: (i) by using the policy in step 2) of Algorithm
1
, (ii) electing uniformly random feasible inputs, and (iii) using a rule-based baseline controller (described in Section
IV-A
). The states are obtained sequentially through simulation, starting from state
x^{j}_{0}
for each
j
. The choice to use either of the three controllers is determined by the probability mass function
\nu^{j}_{\text{exp}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which depends on the iteration index of the policy iteration loop:

\displaystyle\nu^{j}_{\text{exp}}=\begin{cases}[0,0.1,0.9]&\text{for}\ j\leq 5.\\
[0.5,0.25,0.25]&\text{for}\ j>5.\end{cases}

(27)

The entries correspond to the probability of using the corresponding control strategy, which appear in the (i)-(iii) order as just introduced. The rational for this choice is that the BL controller provides “reasonable” state input examples for the RL algorithm in the early learning iterations so to steer the parameter values in the correct direction. After this early learning phase, weight is shifted towards the current working policy so to force the learning algorithm to update the parameter vector in response to its actions.

III-D

Real time implementation

Once the RL controller is trained, it computes the control command in real-time as

\displaystyle u_{k}:=\phi^{*}(x_{k})=\arg\min_{u\in{\sf{U}}(x_{k})}\ Q^{\hat{\theta}}(x_{k},u),

(28)

where
\hat{\theta}
is the parameter vector learned at the end of the learning phase - after
{\sf{N}}_{\text{pol}}
policy improvement steps - that is described above. The value of
d
used in the numerical results reported later is
d=35
. Due to non-convexity of the set
{\sf{U}}(x_{k})
and
n^{\text{ch}}_{k}
being an integer variable, the problem (
28
) is non-convex. To solve it, for each possible value of
n^{\text{ch}}_{k}
, we solve the corresponding continuous variable non-linear program using CasADi/IPOPT
[
26
,
27
]
, and then choose the minimum out of
n^{\text{ch}}_{\text{max}}
solutions.

IV-A

Rule-based Baseline Controller

In order to evaluate the performance of the RL controller, we will compare it to a rule-based baseline controller (BL). The baseline controller determines the setpoints

and

If the current electricity price is cheaper than its four-hour backward average, the TES is charged at maximum flow rate. IV-D

Lessons learned

Training of the RL controller is an iterative task that required trying many various configurations of the parameters appearing in Table
I
. In particular,

1.

If the value of
\kappa
is too small, the controller will not learn to track the load
q^{L,\text{ref}}_{k}
. On the hand, if
\kappa
is too large the controller will not save energy cost.

2.

The choice of basis defines the approximate Q-function, and consequently the policy (
28
). Redundant basis functions can lead to overfitting, which causes poor out-of sample performance of the policy. We avoid this effect by selecting a reduced quadratic basis, which are the 35 unique non-zero entries in Figure
9
. These choices were made based on physical intuition, e.g., the term
\dot{m}^{\text{oa}}\times\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}
is not included as
\dot{m}^{\text{oa}}
and
\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}
have minimal dependency.

3.

The condition number of (
19
) significantly affects the performance of Algorithm
1
. The relative magnitudes of state and input values fundamentally determines the condition number. With appropriate scaling of the states/inputs, we reduce the condition number from
1\times 10^{20}
to
1\times 10^{3}
.

V
Conclusion

The proposed RL controller is able to reduce energy cost by 8% over the baseline for a district cooling plant. The savings are comparable to that by MPC reported in other works while only using a fraction of MPC’s real-time computational cost.

In future work, we would like to further test the robustness of our controller under different disturbance trajectories. We would also like to explore non-linear bases, such as neural networks, and examine convergence of the learning algorithm both numerically and through analysis.

-A

Simulation model of a DCEP

Consider a multi-chiller system with a primary-secondary pumping, a water thermal energy storage tank, a set of cooling water pumps, and a cooling tower, as is shown in Figure
2
. We now describe the plant states and their dynamics, along with the outputs. The DCEP is described in two parts: (i) the chilled water loop and (ii) the cooling water loop.

-A
1
Chilled water loop

The inputs to the chilled water loop are:
\dot{m}^{\text{lw}}
,
\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}
, and
n^{\text{ch}}
. Starting from the load, the load water supply is a mixture of chilled waters from both chillers and the TES. Temporarily using continuous time, and time
t_{k}
corresponding to the index
k
, the rate of enthalpy change of load water supply follows: (dropping
C_{\text{pw}}
from both sides)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Big(s^{\text{lw}}(t)T^{\text{lw,s}}(t)\Big)

\displaystyle\Bigg|_{t=t_{k}}

(29)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\dot{m}^{\text{sw}}_{k}T^{\text{sw}}_{k}-\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k}T^{\text{sw}}_{k},&\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k}>0\\
\dot{m}^{\text{sw}}_{k}T^{\text{sw}}_{k}-\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k}T^{\text{twc}}_{k},&\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k}<0\end{cases}.

Using the chain rule to expand the above differential, and supposing
T^{\text{lw,s}}
reaches steady state instantly (which yields
\dot{T}^{\text{lw,s}}=0
), we have from (
29
) that:

\displaystyle T^{\text{lw},s}_{k}=T^{\text{sw}}_{k}+\frac{\text{min}(\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k},0)}{\dot{m}^{\text{lw}}_{k}}\Big(T^{\text{sw}}_{k}-T^{\text{twc}}_{k}\Big).

(30)

The load water return temperature
T^{\text{lw},r}
, by modeling through the heat balance, evolves as:

\displaystyle{T}^{\text{lw,r}}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{\text{pw}}\dot{m}^{\text{lw}}_{k}}(q_{k}^{L}+C_{\text{pw}}\dot{m}_{k}^{\text{lw}}T_{k}^{\text{lw,s}}),

(31)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq q^{L}_{k}\leq q^{L,\text{ref}}_{k},5\leq{T}^{\text{lw,r}}_{k}\leq 15.

(32)

The unit delay between the left hand side and the right hand side is to model the non-instantaneous nature of heat exchange between water and air streams, and the transport delay in the chilled water distribution pipes.

The water thermal energy storage tank is modeled by two sub-tanks: warm water tank and cold water tank. This approach of modeling water TES is natural since a thermocline separates the warm water and cold water in the TES, which is the bedrock for TES to function properly
[
30
]
. Associated with each tank are two variables: the temperature of the water,
T^{\text{tww}}_{k}
and
T^{\text{twc}}_{k}
, and the mass of water,
s^{\text{tww}}_{k}
and
s^{\text{twc}}_{k}
. The input for both tanks is the mass flow rate
\dot{m}_{k}^{\text{tw}}
, which represents the water flow rate of two tanks. When
\dot{m}_{k}^{\text{tw}}>0
this means that at time
k
,
\dot{m}_{k}^{\text{tw}}t_{s}
(kg) of water is charged into the cold water tank and
\dot{m}_{k}^{\text{tw}}t_{s}
(kg) has been discharged from warm water tank. The dynamics for the storage variables are:

\displaystyle s^{\text{twc}}_{k+1}=s^{\text{twc}}_{k}+t_{s}\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad s^{\text{tww}}_{k+1}=s^{\text{tww}}_{k}-t_{s}\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k}.

(33)

Both quantities are constrained as
s^{\text{tww}}_{k},s^{\text{twc}}_{k}\in[s^{\text{min}},s^{\text{max}}]
with
s^{\text{min}}>0
. The TES is considered well insulated, so that the temperature of the tank is only effected by the temperature of water coming into and out of the tank. For the warm water tank, heat balance reads:

\displaystyle\big(T^{\text{tww}}_{k+1}s^{\text{tww}}_{k+1}-T^{\text{tww}}_{k}s^{\text{tww}}_{k}\big)=\begin{cases}-t_{s}\dot{m}_{k}^{\text{tw}}T^{\text{lw},r}_{k}&\dot{m}_{k}^{\text{tw}}<0\\
-t_{s}\dot{m}_{k}^{\text{tw}}T^{\text{tww}}_{k}&\dot{m}_{k}^{\text{tw}}>0\end{cases}

which can be combined into one equation as:

\displaystyle T^{\text{tww}}_{k+1}=T^{\text{tww}}_{k}+t_{s}\frac{\text{min}(\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k},0)}{s^{\text{tww}}_{k}-t_{s}\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k}}\Big(T^{\text{tww}}_{k}-T^{\text{lw},r}_{k}\Big).

(34)

The cold water tank derivation is symmetric, and the final result is

\displaystyle T^{\text{twc}}_{k+1}=T^{\text{twc}}_{k}+t_{s}\frac{\text{max}(\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k},0)}{s^{\text{twc}}_{k}+t_{s}\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k}}\Big(T^{\text{sw}}_{k}-T^{\text{twc}}_{k}\Big).

(35)

The supply water flow rates are obtained once inputs
\dot{m}^{\text{lw}}_{k}
and
\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k}
are chosen. The supply water temperature
T^{\text{sw}}_{k}
, return water temperature
T^{\text{rw}}_{k}
, and flow rate
\dot{m}^{\text{sw}}
are then:

\displaystyle T^{\text{sw}}_{k}

\displaystyle=T^{\text{chw,s}}_{k},\quad{\dot{m}}^{\text{sw}}_{k}=\dot{m}^{\text{lw}}_{k}+\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k},\ \text{and}

(36)

\displaystyle T^{\text{rw}}_{k}

\displaystyle=T^{\text{lw},r}_{k}+\frac{\text{max}(\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k},0)}{\dot{m}^{\text{sw}}_{k}}\Big(T^{\text{tww}}_{k}-T^{\text{lw},r}_{k}\Big).

(37)

In a primary-secondary pumping system, the water goes through each active (“on”) chiller is a constant
\dot{m}^{\text{indv}}
. The total chilled water produced,
\dot{m}^{\text{chw}}_{k}
, may be more than what is required by loads and TES; a one-way bypass valve sends the redundant chilled water,
\dot{m}^{\text{bp}}_{k}
, from chiller outlet to chiller inlet. Chilled water return
T^{\text{chw,r}}_{k}
is a mixture of return water and bypass water. The above relationships are summarized as:

\displaystyle\dot{m}^{\text{chw}}_{k}

\displaystyle=n^{\text{ch}}_{k}\dot{m}^{\text{indv}},\ \dot{m}^{\text{bp}}_{k}=\dot{m}^{\text{chw}}_{k}-\dot{m}^{\text{sw}}_{k},\ \dot{m}^{\text{bp}}_{k}\geq 0,

(38)

\displaystyle T^{\text{chw,r}}_{k}

\displaystyle=T^{rw}_{k}+\frac{\dot{m}^{\text{bp}}_{k}}{\dot{m}^{\text{chw}}_{k}}(T^{\text{chw,s}}_{k}-T^{\text{rw}}_{k}).

(39)

Assume all chillers are identical and each chiller has a nominal cooling capacity of
q^{\text{indv}}
, then the chilled water supply temperature
T^{\text{chw,s}}_{k+1}
coming out of the chiller evaporator is:

\displaystyle{T}^{\text{chw,s}}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=T^{\text{chw,r}}_{k}-\frac{\dot{q}^{\text{ch}}_{k}}{C_{\text{pw}}\dot{m}^{\text{chw}}_{k}},

(40)

\displaystyle\dot{q}^{\text{ch}}_{k}

\displaystyle\leq n^{\text{ch}}_{k}\dot{q}^{\text{ch},\text{indv}}_{\mathrm{rated}},\quad T^{\text{chw,s}}_{k}\geq T^{\text{chw,s}}_{\text{min}}.

(41)

-A
2
Cooling water loop

The inputs to the cooling water loop are:
\dot{m}^{\text{cw}}
and
\dot{m}^{\text{oa}}
. Simply speaking, without elaborating on the refrigerant loop in chillers, part (or all) of the heat absorbed by the chilled water at the buildings is removed at the chiller and then injected into the cooling tower (supply) water, which reduces the chilled water’s temperature from
T_{k}^{\text{chw,r}}
to
T_{k}^{\text{chw,s}}
and increases cooling water’s temperature from
T_{k}^{\text{cw,s}}
to
T_{k}^{\text{cw,r}}
. The above heat exchange occurs at the chiller condenser. The rate of this heat exchange is denoted by
\dot{q}_{k}^{\text{cond}}
, with the superscript denoting condenser, and is modeled via heat balance:

\displaystyle\dot{q}_{k}^{\text{cond}}=\dot{q}^{\text{ch}}_{k}+\eta_{1}P_{k}^{\text{ch}},

(42)

where
\eta_{1}P_{k}^{\text{ch}}
is the waste heat from the chiller compressor motors, and
P_{k}^{\text{ch}}
is described in (
5
). Following the heat balance,
\dot{q}_{k}^{\text{cond}}
results in an increase of the cooling water:

\displaystyle T_{k+1}^{\text{cw,r}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{q}_{k}^{\text{cond}}}{C_{\text{pw}}\dot{m}_{k}^{\text{cw}}}+T_{k}^{\text{cw,s}},\quad T_{k}^{\text{cw,r}}\leq T^{\text{cw,r}}_{\text{max}}.

(43)

The cooling capacity of a cooling tower, denoted
\dot{q}_{k}^{\text{ct},\text{rej}}
, is modeled as
[
31
]
:

\displaystyle\dot{q}_{k}^{\text{ct},\text{rej}}=\frac{c_{1}(\dot{m}_{k}^{\text{cw}})^{c_{3}}}{1+c_{2}\left(\frac{\dot{m}_{k}^{\text{cw}}}{\dot{m}_{k}^{\text{oa}}}\right)^{c_{3}}}(T_{k}^{\text{cw,r}}-T_{k}^{\text{oawb}}),

(44)

where
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
are empirical constants. The heat rejected through evaporation at the cooling tower is denoted
{\dot{q}^{\text{evap}}}_{k}
, and is modeled from the heat exchange at the condenser by a unit delay:

\displaystyle T^{\text{cw,s}}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=T^{\text{cw,r}}_{k}-\frac{\dot{q}^{\text{evap}}_{k}}{C_{\text{pw}}\dot{m}^{\text{cw}}_{k}},\quad T^{\text{cw,s}}_{k+1}\geq T^{\text{oawb}}_{k}+1,

(45)

\displaystyle 0\leq

\displaystyle\dot{q}^{\text{evap}}_{k}\leq\dot{q}_{k}^{\text{ct},\text{rej}}.

(46)

In order to simulate the dynamics of the DCEP, the capacity of all heat exchange devices, specified through the constraints in (
32
), (
41
), and (
46
), need to be satisfied. An efficient way to ensure this is through a constrained optimization with a “projection objective function.” The decision variable
Z_{k}
for the optimization problem consists of the next system states and the current outputs:

\displaystyle Z_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\big[T^{chw,s}_{k},T^{cw,s}_{k},\ \dot{q}^{L}_{k}\big]^{T}.

(47)

This variable
Z_{k}
is solved for by projecting it onto the dynamics and heat exchange capacities of the system. The system then evolves as:

\displaystyle Z^{*}_{k}=\arg

\displaystyle\min_{Z_{k}\in\Omega(x^{p}_{k},u_{k})}\|Z_{k}-\bar{Z}_{k}\|_{A},

(48)

where
\bar{Z}_{k}
is a vector of user-specified setpoints to reflect system nominal working status. Specifically,
\bar{\dot{q}}^{L}_{k}
=
\dot{q}^{L,\text{ref}}_{k}
. Diagonal matrix
A
is to set a trade-off between maintaining
x_{k+1}^{p}
at nominal working status and ensuring cooling capacities are respected; set
\Omega(x_{k}^{p},u_{k})
is defined by the dynamics and constraints of DCEP system:

\displaystyle\Omega(x_{k}^{p},u_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\big\{\mathbf{Z}:\mathbf{Z}\text{ satisfies}\eqref{eq:P_CH}-\eqref{eq:P_tot}\text{ and}~\eqref{eq:T_lws}-\eqref{eq:CTHeatBalance}\big\}.
