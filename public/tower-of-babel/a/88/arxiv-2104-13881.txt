Large Scale Prediction with Decision Trees
Jason M. Klusowski, Peter M. Tian

Abstract
This paper shows that decision trees constructed with Classification and Regression Trees (CART) and C4.5 methodology are consistent for regression and classification tasks, even when the number of predictor variables grows sub-exponentially with the sample size, under natural 0-norm and 1-norm sparsity constraints. The theory applies to a wide range of models, including (ordinary or logistic) additive regression models with component functions that are continuous, of bounded variation, or, more generally, Borel measurable. Consistency holds for arbitrary joint distributions of the predictor variables, thereby accommodating continuous, discrete, and/or dependent data. Finally, we show that these qualitative properties of individual trees are inherited by Breiman’s random forests. A key step in the analysis is the establishment of an oracle inequality, which allows for a precise characterization of the goodness-of-fit and complexity tradeoff for a mis-specified model.



Keywords:
CART; C4.5; random forests; interpretable machine learning; oracle inequality

1
Introduction

Decision trees are one of the most elemental methods for predictive modeling. Accordingly, they are the cornerstone of many celebrated algorithms in statistical learning. For example, decision trees are often employed in ensemble learning, i.e., bagging
(
Breiman 1996
)
, random forests
(
Breiman 2001
)
, and gradient tree boosting
(
Friedman 2001
)
.
From an applied perspective, decision trees scale well to large datasets, and are intuitive and interpretable, the latter of which makes them easy to explain to statistical non-experts, particularly in the context of rule-based decision-making. They are also supplemented by a rich set of analytic and visual diagnostic tools for exploratory data analysis. These qualities have led to the prominence of decision trees in disciplines---such as medicine and business---which place high importance on the ability to understand and interpret the output from
the training algorithm, even at the expense of predictive accuracy.
1
1

1

The oft-touted interpretability of CART and C4.5 is sometimes compromised by the instability of the splits, particularly among deeper nodes where less data is available. Therefore one should be cautious when attaching any meaning or interpretation to such splits.

Though our primary focus is theoretical, to make this paper likewise relevant to the applied user of decision trees, we focus on Classification And Regression Trees (CART)
(
Breiman et al. 1984
)
and C4.5
(
Quinlan 1993
)
methodology—undoubtedly the most popular varieties for regression and classification problems. On the theoretical side, these approaches raise a number of technical challenges which stem from the top down greedy recursive splitting and line search needed to find the best split points, thereby making CART and C4.5 notoriously difficult to study. These subtle mechanisms are of course desirable from a statistical standpoint, as they endow the decision tree with the ability to adapt to structural and qualitative properties of the underlying statistical
model (such as sparsity and smoothness).
Notwithstanding these major challenges, we take a significant step forward in advancing the theory of decision trees and prove the following (informal) statement in this paper:

Decision trees constructed with CART and C4.5 methodology are consistent for large scale predictive models, where the number of predictor variables is allowed to grow sub-exponentially with the sample size, under
\ell_{0}
or
\ell_{1}
sparsity constraints.

The consistency (with respect to mean squared error risk for regression and excess mis-classification risk for classification) is shown under essentially no assumptions on the predictor variables, thereby improving upon most past work which requires the them to be continuous and either independent or near-independent (e.g., uniformly distributed or with joint densities which are bounded above and below by fixed positive constants).

Expectedly, our results for individual trees also carry over to ensembles, namely, Breiman’s random forests
(
Breiman 2001
)
, which among other things, use CART methodology for the constituent trees.

1.1
Prior art

We now review some of the past theoretical work on decision trees, starting with CART in the regression setting, and then for C4.5 in the classification setting.

Regression trees.

The first consistency result for CART was provided in the original book that proposed the methodology
(
Breiman et al. 1984
)
, albeit under very strong assumptions on the tree construction, such as a minimum node size condition and shrinking cell condition.
Thirty years later,
Scornet et al. 2015
showed asymptotic consistency of CART for (fixed dimensional) additive regression models with continuous component functions, en route to establishing asymptotic consistency of Breiman’s random forests. This paper was an important technical breakthrough because it did not require any of the strong assumptions on the tree made in
Breiman et al. 1984
. Subsequent work by
Chi et al. 2020
;
Klusowski 2020
;
Syrgkanis and Zampetakis 2020
;
Wager and Walther 2015
provide finite sample consistency rates in a high dimensional setting with exact sparsity, though again, like
Breiman et al. 1984
, they operate under a set of conditions that may or may not hold in practice. Another notable paper by
Gey and Nedelec 2005
provides oracle-type inequalities for pruned CART, but the theory does not extend to out-of-sample prediction.

Motivated by Stone’s conditions for consistency in non-parametric regression
(
Stone 1977
)
, most existing convergence results for decision trees follow an approach in which the approximation error is bounded by the mesh of the induced partition of the input space. Conditions are then imposed, either explicitly or implicitly, to ensure that the mesh approaches zero as the depth of the tree increases. This is then combined with a standard empirical process argument to show vanishing estimation error, which in turn, implies that the prediction risk vanishes also
(
Denil et al. 2014
;
Breiman et al. 1984
;
Wager and Walther 2015
;
Wager and Athey 2018
)
.
In contrast, the aforementioned paper
(
Scornet et al. 2015
)
controls the variation of the regression function inside the cells of the partition, without explicitly controlling the mesh, though the theoretical consequences are similar. While these techniques can be useful to prove consistency statements, they are not generally delicate enough to capture the adaptive properties of the tree or handle high dimensional situations.

More recently,
Chi et al. 2020
;
Klusowski 2020
;
Syrgkanis and Zampetakis 2020
developed techniques to directly analyze the approximation error (instead of using the granularity of the partition as a proxy) by exploiting the greedy optimization inherent in CART methodology. These papers provide consistency rates for models with exact sparsity in a high dimensional regime (i.e., when the ambient dimensionality grows with the sample size); however, they make a number of assumptions that lead to an unsatisfactory theory. For example, the results of
Klusowski 2020
apply only to the noise free setting,
Chi et al. 2020
require the ambient dimensionality to grow at most polynomially with the sample size,
and
Syrgkanis and Zampetakis 2020
work with binary valued predictor variables. In addition, a local accuracy gain condition (akin to an edge or progress condition in boosting literature) is required in these works
(
Chi et al. 2020
;
Klusowski 2020
;
Syrgkanis and Zampetakis 2020
)
to ensure the approximation error decreases by a constant factor after splitting at each level. In
Chi et al. 2020
, this local accuracy gain condition is verified to hold for some simple classes of additive regression models, such as those with isotonic or piece-wise linear component functions and independent predictor variables.
Syrgkanis and Zampetakis 2020
do not provide any concrete examples of models that satisfy what they call a
sub-modularity
property, and the reader is required to accept its validity. In summary, it is difficult to verify which models satisfy these technical conditions and, therefore, the general applicability of the theory remains unclear.

Classification trees.

The story for the classification setting is far less complete. Theory for regression trees is far easier to assemble—and has therefore been overwhelmingly the focus of past literature—since one does not have to deal with the discrete nature of the model. This lack of attention is somewhat unfortunate as decision trees are more often successfully deployed in problems with discrete outputs, i.e., clinical decision support systems. As an exception, one stand-alone paper to tackle the classification problem is
Kearns and Mansour 1999
, where the authors show that classification trees constructed with CART
and C4.5 methodology have small mis-classification risk under a weak hypothesis assumption, i.e., the decision tree output in each node performs slightly better than random guessing as approximations to the target function. Their results, however, do not account for the effect of dimensionality, nor do they accommodate standard statistical models for classification, such as logistic regression.

1.2
Related work

In closing, we mention a few papers that study other tree based procedures, but with different aims and from different perspectives. Mondrian random forests
(
Mourtada et al. 2020
;
Mourtada et al. 2021
)
, unlike some of the aforementioned variants of Breiman’s random forests, provably attain near-optimal minimax rates for various levels of smoothness regularity. The dyadic CART procedure of
Donoho 1997
, obtained by optimal (non-greedy), dyadic recursive partitioning, was also shown to achieve near-optimal rates (and adaptation to unknown smoothness regularity) for the case of two predictor variables. (Dyadic CART unfortunately scales poorly with the dimensionality
p
, since finding the optimal dyadic partition may require up to
{\mathcal{O}}(2^{p}Np)
operations
(
Chatterjee and Goswami 2021
)
.)
An interesting line of work from a Bayesian perspective explores Bayesian trees and forests. For example,
Ročková and van der Pas 2020
;
Jeong and Ročková 2020
obtain near-optimal posterior concentration rates for non-parametric regression, similar to the optimal minimax rates discussed in Section
4.5
below, that adapt to both exact sparsity and smoothness regularity (for Bayesian CART and BART) and additive structures (for BART).

Organization

We review basic terminology associated with decision trees and describe CART and C4.5 methodology in Section

We then show that C4.5 achieves a faster consistency rate for separable, large margin binary data in Section

. Our main consistency results in Section
4

6

7
. We conclude with a discussion in Section
8
.
Finally, all proofs and technical lemmas are contained in the supplementary material.

2
Statistical framework

Throughout this paper, we operate under a standard predictive framework for regression and classification; i.e., from training data, we desire to predict a response value
y
for a new set of
p
predictor variables
\mathbf{x}
. More formally, we observe training data
{\mathcal{D}}\coloneqq\{(\mathbf{x}_{1},y_{1}),(\mathbf{x}_{2},y_{2}),\dots,(\mathbf{x}_{N},y_{N})\}
drawn i.i.d. from the statistical model
\mathbb{P}_{(\mathbf{x},y)}=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbb{P}_{y\mid\mathbf{x}}
, where
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}}
is a probability measure on the
\sigma
-algebra of Borel subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
To understand the predictive properties of decision trees in an (ultra) high dimensional setting,
the dimensionality
p=p_{N}
is permitted to grow sub-exponentially with the sample size
N
.

In order to unify
both the regression and classification settings, we consider a discriminative statistical model in which the conditional mean of
y
given
\mathbf{x}
is modeled indirectly via a (possibly non-linear) link function.
To this end, we assume
that there is a fixed and known link function
h:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

g^{*}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq h(\mathbb{E}(y\mid\mathbf{x}))\in{\mathcal{G}},

(1)

where
{\mathcal{G}}
is some class of
\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
functions to be chosen ahead.
A loss function
{\mathcal{L}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is chosen so that

g^{*}(\cdot)\in{\rm argmin}_{g(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{G}}}\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)}{\mathcal{L}}(y,g(\mathbf{x})).

(2)

The
empirical and population risk
corresponding to the loss function
{\mathcal{L}}(\cdot,\cdot)
are denoted by

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(g)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{L}}(y_{i},g(\mathbf{x}_{i})),\qquad{\mathcal{R}}(g)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)}{\mathcal{L}}(y,g(\mathbf{x})),

(3)

respectively.
In light of (
2
), we aim to estimate the true model
g^{*}(\cdot)
by finding a
{\mathcal{D}}
-dependent fit
g(\cdot)
, not necessarily in
{\mathcal{G}}
, which
comes close to minimizing the empirical risk
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(g)
.

2.1
Regression setting

We assume a real-valued response variable
y\in\mathbb{R}
and consider the identity link function

h(\mu)=\mu

so that

g^{*}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}(y\mid\mathbf{x})

is the conditional mean response.
We choose the loss function to be the squared error

{\mathcal{L}}(y,g(\mathbf{x}))=(y-g(\mathbf{x}))^{2},

(4)

which satisfies (
2
).
The out-of-sample performance is measured
with the squared
\mathscr{L}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}})
norm

\|g-g^{*}\|^{2}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}((g(\mathbf{x})-g^{*}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}),

(5)

which also equals the excess squared error risk
{\mathcal{R}}(g)-{\mathcal{R}}(g^{*})
under these model specifications.

2.2
Classification setting

We assume a binary response variable
y\in\{-1,1\}

and consider the logit link function

h(\mu)=\log(\frac{1+\mu}{1-\mu}),

so that

g^{*}(\mathbf{x})=h(2\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})-1)={\rm log}(\frac{\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})}{1-\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})})

is the log-odds of the conditional class probability
\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(y=1\mid\mathbf{x})=1/(1+\exp(-g^{*}(\mathbf{x})))
. Note that if
g^{*}(\cdot)
is a linear function, then this model is linear logistic regression.
We choose the loss function to be the logistic loss

{\mathcal{L}}(y,g(\mathbf{x}))=\log(1+\exp(-yg(\mathbf{x}))),

(6)

which satisfies (
2
) and, appealingly, corresponds to maximum likelihood estimation of conditionally Bernoulli distributed data with probability model
\mathbb{P}(y\mid\mathbf{x})=1/(1+\exp(-yg(\mathbf{x})))
.
Instead of assessing performance with the logistic risk, we more familiarly consider the mis-classification risk

{\rm Err}(g)\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{(\mathbf{x},y)}(c(\mathbf{x})\neq y)

(7)

of the plug-in classifier

c(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\begin{cases}+1&\text{if}\;g(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\\
-1&\text{if}\;g(\mathbf{x})<0\end{cases}.

The out-of-sample performance
is then measured by the excess mis-classification risk

{\rm Err}(g)-{\rm Err}(g^{*}),

(8)

where
{\rm Err}(g^{*})
is the Bayes error rate.

Definition 2.1
.

When no context is provided,
prediction risk
will refer to excess squared error risk (
5
) in the regression setting (Section
2.1
) or excess mis-classification risk (
8
) in the classification setting (Section
2.2
).

3
CART and C4.5 methodology

As mentioned earlier, regression trees are commonly constructed with Classification and Regression Trees (CART) methodology. In the context of classification, one can use either CART or its contemporary counterpart, C4.5.
For technical reasons that will be explained at the end of this section,
we will focus on the latter methodology.
While CART and C4.5 are algorithmically similar, they differ in important ways. The notable distinction lies in the criterion used to determine the split points, which turns out to be the key to their success for the predictive models we consider in Section
2.1
and Section
2.2
.
In a nutshell, the objective of decision tree learning is to find partitions of the predictor variables that produce minimal empirical risk of the constant (average response) values over the partition.
Because of the computational infeasibility of choosing the best overall partition, CART and C4.5 operate in a greedy, top down fashion (with a mere
{\mathcal{O}}(pN\log^{2}(N))
average-case complexity
(
Louppe 2014
, Section 5)
) using a procedure in which a sequence of locally optimal splits recursively partition the input space.

In Section
3.1
, we first discuss the splitting rule, stopping criterion, and tree output for a generic (top down, greedy) tree construction algorithm.
We then specialize our treatment of decision trees to CART and C4.5 in Section
3.2
and Section
3.3
, respectively.

3.1
Greedy tree construction

Consider splitting a decision tree
T
at a node
\mathrm{t}
(a hyperrectangular region in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
). Let
s
be a candidate split point for a variable
x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
that divides the parent node
\mathrm{t}
into left and right daughter nodes
\mathrm{t}_{L}
and
\mathrm{t}_{R}
according to whether
x_{j}\leq s
or
x_{j}>s
, respectively. These two nodes will be denoted by
\mathrm{t}_{L}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{t}:x_{j}\leq s\}
and
\mathrm{t}_{R}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{t}:x_{j}>s\}
.

An effective split divides the data from the parent node into two daughter nodes so that the heterogeneity in each of the daughter nodes, as measured through the
impurity
,
is maximally reduced from that of the parent node. The impurity is determined by the within-node empirical risk

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(g)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{t}}{\mathcal{L}}(y_{i},g(\mathbf{x}_{i})),\quad N_{\mathrm{t}}\coloneqq\#\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{t}\}.

(9)

In accordance with the directive of minimizing the empirical risk, it is equal to the smallest within-node empirical risk over all constant predictors in the node, i.e.,

{\mathcal{I}}(\mathrm{t})=\min_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}}\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\beta)=\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(h(\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}})),\quad\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{t}}y_{i}.

(10)

The parent node
\mathrm{t}
is split into two daughter nodes using a variable
x_{j_{\mathrm{t}}}
and split point
s_{\mathrm{t}}
which produce the largest impurity gain

(
Breiman et al. 1984
, Definition 8.13)
,

(
Quinlan 1993
, Page 22)

\mathcal{IG}(j,s,\mathrm{t})\coloneqq{\mathcal{I}}(\mathrm{t})-P_{\mathrm{t}_{L}}{\mathcal{I}}(\mathrm{t}_{L})-P_{\mathrm{t}_{R}}{\mathcal{I}}(\mathrm{t}_{R}),\quad\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})\coloneqq\max_{(j,s)}\mathcal{IG}(j,s,\mathrm{t}),

(11)

breaking ties arbitrarily, where
P_{\mathrm{t}_{L}}\coloneqq N_{\mathrm{t}_{L}}/N_{\mathrm{t}}
and
P_{\mathrm{t}_{R}}\coloneqq N_{\mathrm{t}_{R}}/N_{\mathrm{t}}
are the proportions of data points
within
\mathrm{t}
that are contained in
\mathrm{t}_{L}
and
\mathrm{t}_{R}
, respectively.
Equivalently, the variable and split point, chosen to maximize (
11
), also minimize the within-node empirical risk (
9
) over all
decision stumps
, since

P_{\mathrm{t}_{L}}{\mathcal{I}}(\mathrm{t}_{L})+P_{\mathrm{t}_{R}}{\mathcal{I}}(\mathrm{t}_{R})=\min_{\beta_{0},\beta_{1}\in\mathbb{R}}\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\mathbf{1}(x_{j}>s)).

(12)

We can thus view the maximum impurity gain
\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})
as the amount by which the optimal decision stump decreases the empirical risk in the node.

The daughter nodes
\mathrm{t}_{L}
and
\mathrm{t}_{R}
of
\mathrm{t}
become new parent nodes at the next level of the tree and are themselves further divided according to the previous scheme, and so on and so forth, until a desired depth
K
is reached.
There are many criteria that can be used to determine when to stop splitting, each one giving rise to a different tree structure. In this paper, we use the following stopping rule.

Definition 3.1

(Stopping rule)
.

We stop splitting a node if (i) the node contains a single data point, (ii) all input values and/or all response values within the node are the same, or (iii) a depth of
K
is reached, whichever occurs sooner.

Finally, the output of the tree
T
at a terminal node
\mathrm{t}
is the best constant predictor in the node:

\hat{g}(T)(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq h(\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}})\approx g^{*}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{t}.

(13)

When we wish to emphasize the dependence of the tree
T
on the depth
K
, we will write
T_{K}
.

3.2
CART algorithm

Recall the regression setting in Section
2.1
,
where
h(\cdot)
is the identity link and
{\mathcal{L}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the squared error loss (
4
). In this case, the impurity (
10
) becomes the within-node sample variance of the response variable, i.e.,

{\mathcal{I}}(\mathrm{t})=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{t}}(y_{i}-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}})^{2}.

(14)

According to these model specifications, the tree output (
13
) makes a prediction by returning the within-node sample mean of the response variable, i.e.,

\hat{g}(T^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})(\mathbf{x})=h(\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}})=\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{t}.

(15)

3.3
C4.5 algorithm

Recall the classification setting in Section
2.2
,
where
h(\cdot)
is the logit link and
{\mathcal{L}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the logistic loss (
6
). In this case,
the impurity (
10
) becomes the binary entropy of the within-node empirical class probability, i.e.,

{\mathcal{I}}(\mathrm{t})=\eta_{\mathrm{t}}\log(1/\eta_{\mathrm{t}})+(1-\eta_{\mathrm{t}})\log(1/(1-\eta_{\mathrm{t}})),\quad\eta_{\mathrm{t}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{t}}\mathbf{1}(y_{i}=1).

(16)

As entropy quantifies the information content of a random variable, the impurity gain (
11
) is sometimes called the
information gain
or the
mutual information
.
According to these model specifications, the tree output (
13
) is the log-odds of the within-node empirical class probability

\hat{g}(T^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})(\mathbf{x})=h(\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}})=\log\Big(\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{t}}}{1-\eta_{\mathrm{t}}}\Big),\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{t}.

(17)

The tree makes a class prediction by returning the majority vote of the classes in the node, i.e.,

\hat{c}(T^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}+1&\text{if}\;\hat{g}(T^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\\
-1&\text{if}\;\hat{g}(T^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})(\mathbf{x})<0\end{cases}.

.

.
Similarly,
a decision tree
T

) is denoted by

.

Remark 1
.

The curious reader may wonder why we do not analyze
classification trees constructed with CART methodology—which use the so-called
Gini
splitting criterion—and instead focus on C4.5 methodology. Gini impurity for classification trees is equivalent to squared error impurity (
14
) for regression trees
(
Louppe 2014
, Section 3)
, and thus both types of trees produce identical estimates of the conditional mean response, namely,
\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}}\approx\mathbb{E}(y\mid\mathbf{x})
for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{t}
. However, our forthcoming results for regression trees are relegated to additive
\mathbb{E}(y\mid\mathbf{x})
, which are appropriate for regression, but awkward for classification.
For this reason, C4.5 methodology allows us to work with more common models for discrete responses
y\in\{-1,1\}
, such as the logistic regression model in Section
2.2
.

4
Main results

In this section, we first describe
a class of large scale predictive models.
For this class of models, we establish an adaptive prediction risk bound, which in turn, leads to consistency of CART and C4.5.

4.1
Large scale predictive models

We illustrate the high dimensional properties of CART and C4.5 in the context of
generalized additive models
, whereby an
additive function
is related to the conditional mean of the response variable by a link function (
1
). While there are many link functions that could be used, the canonical choices for regression and classification tasks are the aforementioned identity and logit functions from Section
2.1
and Section
2.2
, respectively.

In particular, note that additive logistic regression models are, importantly, different from ordinary additive regression in the sense that

\mathbb{E}(y\mid\mathbf{x})=2\mathbb{P}(y=1\mid\mathbf{x})-1
is not equal to an additive function of the predictor variables. This means that one cannot deduce consistency
from existing results for regression trees
(
Scornet et al. 2015
)
, even in the fixed dimensional setting, since they are limited to additive
\mathbb{E}(y\mid\mathbf{x})
.

In practice, additive logistic regression models are typically fit with backfitting or boosting algorithms
(
Hastie and Tibshirani 1990
;
Tutz and Binder 2006
)
. Despite a rich literature on theoretical guarantees in the fixed dimensional setting
(
Horowitz and Mammen 2004
)
, consistency results in the (ultra) high dimensional setting (i.e.,
\log(p)\asymp N^{1-\xi}
,
\xi\in(0,1)
) with either
\ell_{0}
or
\ell_{1}
sparsity constraints do not appear to be available, unless
the logistic regression model is linear and the sparsity pattern is the number of relevant predictor variables
(
Abramovich and Grinshtein 2019
)
.
Therefore, sparse logistic regression models render a situation in which decision trees are unrivaled as a scalable, theoretically grounded method.

Generalized additive models.

We now describe the generalized additive modeling framework with additional precision. Consider the additive function class

{\mathcal{G}}^{1}\coloneqq\big\{g(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq g_{1}(x_{1})+g_{2}(x_{2})+\cdots+g_{p}(x_{p})\big\},

where
g_{1}(x_{1}),g_{2}(x_{2}),\dots,g_{p}(x_{p})
is a collection of
p
univariate (Borel measurable) functions.
The generalized additive modeling framework involves finding a
g(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{G}}^{1}
for which

g(\mathbf{x})=g_{1}(x_{1})+g_{2}(x_{2})+\cdots+g_{p}(x_{p})

(18)

approximates the true model (
1
).

Generalized additive models are often used in high dimensional settings, in part because notions of exact
\ell_{0}
or approximate
\ell_{1}
sparsity are easy to define and interpret. We now describe these two types of sparsity patterns in detail.

Approximate
\ell_{1}
sparsity.

As we have already mentioned, we would like to consider models with
approximate
sparsity. To this end, for
g(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{G}}^{1}
, we define the
total variation
\ell_{1}
norm

\|g\|_{{\rm TV}}
as the infimum of

{\rm TV}(g_{1})+{\rm TV}(g_{2})+\cdots+{\rm TV}(g_{p})

(19)

over all representations of
g(\cdot)
as (
18
), i.e.,
\|g\|_{{\rm TV}}
is the aggregated total variation of the individual component functions (see
Tan and Zhang 2019
and the references therein).
To simplify the arguments, we henceforth assume
g(\cdot)
has a canonical representation (
18
) such that (
19
) achieves this infimum.
One can think of
\|g\|_{{\rm TV}}
as a measure of the capacity of
g(\cdot)
and, as we shall see, it will play a central role in the paper. The total variation
\ell_{1}
norm is a desirable quantification of sparsity because it allows for some predictor variables to make very small yet meaningful contributions to the model.

In the case that all the component functions
g_{j}(\cdot)
are smooth over a domain
{\mathcal{X}}
with Lebesgue measure one, the total variation
\ell_{1}
norm can be expressed as the multiple Riemann integral

\|g\|_{{\rm TV}}=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}^{p}}\|\nabla g(\mathbf{x})\|_{\ell_{1}}d\mathbf{x},

where
\nabla(\cdot)
is the gradient operator and
\|\cdot\|_{\ell_{1}}
is the usual
\ell_{1}
norm of a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
In particular, if
g(\mathbf{x})=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T}\mathbf{x}
is linear over
{\mathcal{X}}^{p}
, then
\|g\|_{{\rm TV}}=\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\ell_{1}}
, the
\ell_{1}
norm of the coefficient vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

Exact
\ell_{0}
sparsity.

To account for
exact
sparsity, we also define the
\ell_{0}
norm
\|g\|_{\ell_{0}}
of
g(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{G}}^{1}
as the infimum of

\#\{j:g_{j}(\cdot)\;\text{is non-constant}\}

over all representations of
g(\cdot)
as (
18
).
In other words, the
\ell_{0}
norm counts the number of relevant variables that affect
g(\cdot)
. For
g(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{G}}^{1}
,
we have the relation

\|g\|_{{\rm TV}}\leq\|g\|_{\ell_{0}}\cdot\max_{j}{\rm TV}(g_{j}),

provided
\max_{j}{\rm TV}(g_{j})<\infty
.
Thus, a small total variation
\ell_{1}
norm captures
either
exact or approximate sparsity, whichever is present.

Finally, our results will require us to have uniform control on the magnitude of a function
g:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
, which we do through the supremum norm
\|g\|_{\infty}\coloneqq\sup_{\mathbf{x}}|g(\mathbf{x})|
.

4.2
Empirical risk bound

The empirical risk bound in this section is the key to all forthcoming results.
We prove a purely algorithmic guarantee, namely, that the (excess) empirical risk of a depth
K
regression tree constructed with CART or C4.5 methodology is of order
1/K
. To the best of our knowledge, this result is the first of its kind for any decision tree algorithm. The math behind it is surprisingly simple; in particular, unlike most past work, we do not need to directly analyze the partition of the input space that is induced by recursively splitting the variables. Nor do we need to rely on concentration of measure to show that certain local (i.e., within-node) empirical quantities concentrate around their population level versions. Because we are able to circumvent these technical aspects with a new method of analysis, the astute reader will notice and appreciate that we make no assumptions on the decision tree itself (such as a minimum node size condition or shrinking cell condition that typifies extant literature). In contrast with the recent work of
Chi et al. 2020
;
Syrgkanis and Zampetakis 2020
, we also do not need to assume a local accuracy gain condition so that the approximation error decreases by a constant factor after splitting at each level.

We now describe the empirical risk bound in detail. We aim to upper bound the excess empirical risk
of the decision tree
T_{K}
. In view of (
12
),
as we grow deeper trees, the empirical risk reduces by the impurity gain, as can be seen from the recursion (which holds generically)

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K}))=\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}))-\sum_{\mathrm{t}\in T_{K-1}}\frac{N_{\mathrm{t}}}{N}\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t}),

(20)

where the notation “
\mathrm{t}\in T
” means that
\mathrm{t}
is a terminal node of of a tree
T
.
In view of (
20
), to obtain an inductive upper bound on
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K}))
, we further aim to the lower bound the impurity gain
\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})
for
\mathrm{t}\in T_{K-1}
in terms of the within-node excess risk
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}))-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(g)
for a candidate model
g(\cdot)
. Lemma
4.1
below accomplishes this goal.

Lemma 4.1

(Impurity gain for CART and C4.5)
.

Let
g(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{G}}^{1}
be any additive function and
K\geq 1
be any depth.
Then for any terminal node
\mathrm{t}
of the tree
T_{K-1}
such that
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}))>\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(g)
,
we have

\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})\geq\frac{(\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}))-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(g))^{2}}{V^{2}(g)},

where

V(g)=\|g\|_{\rm TV}
for CART and
V(g)=\|g\|_{\rm TV}+\|g\|_{\infty}+3
for C4.5.

Plugging Lemma
4.1
into (
20
) and subtracting
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(g)
from both sides, we see that

{\mathcal{E}}_{K}\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}-\frac{1}{V^{2}(g)}\sum_{\mathrm{t}\in T_{K-1}:{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}(\mathrm{t})>0}\frac{N_{\mathrm{t}}}{N}{\mathcal{E}}^{2}_{K-1}(\mathrm{t}),

(21)

where

{\mathcal{E}}_{K}\coloneqq\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K}))-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(g),\quad{\mathcal{E}}_{K}(\mathrm{t})\coloneqq\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K}))-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(g)

(22)

are the global and within-node excess empirical risks, respectively.
Next, using Jensen’s inequality on (
21
) and the fact that
{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}=\sum_{\mathrm{t}\in T_{K-1}}\frac{N_{\mathrm{t}}}{N}{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}(\mathrm{t})
,
it can be shown that (see Lemma
D.1
in Supplement
D
)

{\mathcal{E}}_{K}\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}\Big(1-\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}}{V^{2}(g)}\Big),\quad{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}\geq 0,\quad K\geq 1.

(23)

Iterating the recursion (
23
),
we establish the following upper bound on the excess empirical risk
{\mathcal{E}}_{K}
.

Theorem 4.2

(Empirical risk bound for CART and C4.5)
.

Let
T_{K}
be a depth
K\geq 1
decision tree.
Then we have

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K}))\leq\inf_{g(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{G}}^{1}}\Big\{\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(g)+\frac{V^{2}(g)}{K+3}\Big\},

where
V(g)
is the constant specified in Lemma
4.1
.

The above theorem says that a decision tree of depth
K
minimizes the empirical risk (for squared error loss and logistic loss) over all additive functions, up to a slackness term of order
1/K
.

4.3
Oracle inequality

Our main theorem
establishes an adaptive prediction risk bound (also known as an
oracle inequality
) for decision trees under model mis-specification; that is, when the true model (
1
) may not belong to
{\mathcal{G}}^{1}
. Essentially, the result says that CART and C4.5 adapt to the class of (ordinary or logistic) additive regression models, performing as if they were finding the best additive approximation of the true model (
1
),
while accounting for the capacity (the total variation
\ell_{1}
norm
\|\cdot\|_{{\rm TV}}
) of the approximation.

In the regression setting, for simplicity and ease of exposition, we assume that the error
\varepsilon=y-g^{*}(\mathbf{x})=y-\mathbb{E}(y\mid\mathbf{x})
is sub-Gaussian, i.e., there exists
\sigma^{2}>0
such that for all
u\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}(|\varepsilon|\geq u)\leq 2\exp(-u^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})).

(24)

Before we state our main theorem, we remind the reader that
\|\cdot\|
denotes the
\mathscr{L}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}})
norm
(
5
) and
{\rm Err}(\cdot)
denotes the mis-classification risk (
7
). Using these risk measures, we evaluate the performance of CART for the regression model in Section
2.1
and the performance of C4.5 for the logistic regression model in Section
2.2
.

Theorem 4.3

(Oracle inequalities for CART and C4.5)
.

Let
K\geq 1
be any depth. Granting the noise condition
(
24
), we have

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}(\|\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})-g^{*}\|^{2})\leq 2\inf_{g(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{G}}^{1}}\Bigg\{\|g-g^{*}\|^{2}+\frac{\|g\|^{2}_{{\rm TV}}}{K+3}+C_{1}\frac{2^{K}\log^{2}(N)\log(Np)}{N}\Bigg\},

(25)

where
C_{1}
is a positive constant that depends only on
\|g^{*}\|_{\infty}
and
\sigma^{2}
.
Furthermore,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}({\rm Err}(\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))-{\rm Err}(g^{*})

(26)

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{g(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{G}}^{1}}\Bigg\{\|g-g^{*}\|+2\frac{\|g\|_{{\rm TV}}+\|g\|_{\infty}+3}{\sqrt{K+3}}+C_{2}\Big(\frac{2^{K}\log^{2}(N)\log(Np)}{N}\Big)^{1/4}\Bigg\},

where

C_{2}
is a positive universal constant.

Remark 2
.

Throughout this paper, we measure the accuracy of a predictor via the
expected
prediction risk over the data
{\mathcal{D}}
, as in Theorem
4.3
. However, at the expense of more complicated expressions, one can also obtain statements that hold with high probability.

Theorem
4.3
reveals the tradeoff between the goodness-of-fit and complexity relative to sample size. The goodness-of-fit terms involving
\|g-g^{*}\|
,
\|g\|_{{\rm TV}}
, and
\|g\|_{\infty}
stem from the excess empirical risk bound in Theorem
4.2
, and the descriptive complexity term
2^{K}\log(Np)
comes from the fact that the empirical
\epsilon
-metric entropy for depth
K
decision trees with
p
predictor variables is of order
2^{K}\log(Np/\epsilon)
.

4.4
Consistency

We now explore the case where the true model
g^{*}(\cdot)
has the freedom to change with the sample size, which is common in other literature on high dimensional consistency

(
Bühlmann 2006
)
.
To this end, Theorem
4.3
immediately implies
consistency when the model is well-specified (i.e.,
g^{*}(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{G}}^{1}
) and has a controlled sparsity pattern.
More specifically, choosing
g(\cdot)=g^{*}(\cdot)
in Theorem
4.3
and stipulating that
the total variation
\ell_{1}
norm of
g^{*}(\cdot)

does not grow too fast, we find that CART and C4.5 are consistent, even when the dimensionality grows sub-exponentially with the sample size. We note that this type of result is impossible with non-adaptive procedures that do not automatically adjust the amount of smoothing along a particular dimension according to how much the predictor variable affects the response variable. Such procedures perform local estimation at a query point using data that are close in every single dimension, making them prone to the curse of dimensionality even if the true model is sparse. This is the case with conventional multivariate (Nadaraya-Watson or local polynomial) kernel regression in which the bandwidth is the same for all directions, or
k
-nearest neighbors with Euclidean distance. Indeed, one can compute asymptotic expansions of their bias and variance
(
Ruppert and Wand 1994
;
Mack 1981
)
, which evidently do not exploit low dimensional structure in the regression model.

Corollary 4.4

(Consistency of CART and C4.5)
.

Consider a sequence of prediction problems (
1
) with true models
\{g^{*}_{N}(\cdot)\}_{N=1}^{\infty}
.
Assume that
g^{*}_{N}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{p_{N}}g_{j}(x_{j})\in{\mathcal{G}}^{1}
and
\sup_{N}\|g^{*}_{N}\|_{\infty}<\infty
. Suppose that
K_{N}\rightarrow\infty
,
\|g_{N}^{*}\|_{{\rm TV}}=o(\sqrt{K_{N}})
, and
\frac{2^{K_{N}}\log^{2}(N)\log(Np_{N})}{N}\rightarrow 0
as
N\rightarrow\infty
.
Granting the noise condition (
24
), regression trees are consistent, that is,

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}(\|\hat{g}(T_{K_{N}}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})-g^{*}_{N}\|^{2})=0.

Furthermore, classification trees are consistent, that is,

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}(\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}({\rm Err}(\hat{g}(T_{K_{N}}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))-{\rm Err}(g^{*}_{N}))=0.

Remark 3
.

Note that because
\|g^{*}_{N}\|_{{\rm TV}}\leq\|g^{*}_{N}\|_{\ell_{0}}\cdot\max_{j\leq p_{N}}{\rm TV}(g^{*}_{j})
, the consistency statement in Corollary
4.4
also applies to models with sparsity patterns defined by the number of relevant variables. Thus, the condition
\|g_{N}^{*}\|_{{\rm TV}}=o(\sqrt{K_{N}})
can be replaced with
\|g_{N}^{*}\|_{\ell_{0}}=o(\sqrt{K_{N}})
, provided
\max_{j\leq p_{N}}{\rm TV}(g^{*}_{j})
is independent of
N
. The same reasoning applies to all forthcoming results that use
\|\cdot\|_{{\rm TV}}
to measure sparsity.

4.4.1
Consistency rates

We now describe the effect of specific choices of the depth and regimes of the ambient dimension on the consistency rate for CART and C4.5. The hypotheses of Corollary
4.4
are satisfied if, for example,
K_{N}=\lfloor(\xi/2)\log_{2}(N)\rfloor
,
\log(p_{N})\asymp N^{1-\xi}
for
\xi\in(0,1)
,
\sup_{N}\|g_{N}^{*}\|_{\infty}<\infty
, and
\sup_{N}\|g_{N}^{*}\|_{\rm TV}<\infty
.
In this case, from (
25
) in Theorem
4.3
, the consistency rate of the CART algorithm is

\frac{4\sup_{N}\|g_{N}^{*}\|^{2}_{{\rm TV}}}{\xi\log_{2}(N)+6}+2C_{1}\frac{\log^{3}(N)}{N^{1-\xi/2}}+2C_{1}\frac{\log^{2}(N)}{N^{\xi/2}}={\mathcal{O}}(1/\log(N))

(27)

and from (
26
) in Theorem
4.3
,
the consistency rate of the C4.5 algorithm is

4\frac{\sup_{N}\|g_{N}^{*}\|_{{\rm TV}}+\sup_{N}\|g_{N}^{*}\|_{{\infty}}+3}{\sqrt{2\xi\log_{2}(N)+12}}+C_{2}\Big(\frac{\log^{3}(N)}{N^{1-\xi/2}}+\frac{\log^{2}(N)}{N^{\xi/2}}\Big)^{1/4}={\mathcal{O}}(1/\sqrt{\log(N)}).

(28)

The dependence on the total variation
\ell_{1}
norm
\|g_{N}^{*}\|_{{\rm TV}}
in the consistency rates (
27
) and (
28
) shows that CART and C4.5 can tolerate an approximate sparsity level that grows as fast as
o(\sqrt{\log(N)})
. As per Remark
3
, a similar growth is tolerated for the
\ell_{0}
norm
\|g_{N}^{*}\|_{\ell_{0}}
.

4.4.2
Consistency for unbounded variation component functions

Corollary
4.4
implicitly considers (ordinary or logistic) additive regression models whose component functions
have bounded variation,
per the finiteness of
\|g_{N}^{*}\|_{\text{TV}}
.
In fact,
consistency holds when the component functions
g_{j}(\cdot)
are merely Borel measurable,
as
Corollary
4.5
reveals.

Corollary 4.5

(Consistency of CART and C4.5 for unbounded variation components)
.

Consider a sequence of prediction problems with true models
\{g^{*}_{N}(\cdot)\}_{N=1}^{\infty}
.
Assume
g_{N}^{*}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{p_{N}}g_{j}(x_{j})\in{\mathcal{G}}^{1}
,
\sup_{N}\|g^{*}_{N}\|_{\infty}<\infty
, and
\sup_{N}\|g^{*}_{N}\|_{\ell_{0}}<\infty
.
Suppose that
K_{N}\rightarrow\infty
and
\frac{2^{K_{N}}\log^{2}(N)\log(Np_{N})}{N}\rightarrow 0
as
N\rightarrow\infty
.
Granting the noise condition (
24
), regression trees are consistent, that is,

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}(\|\hat{g}(T_{K_{N}}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})-g^{*}_{N}\|^{2})=0.

Furthermore, classification trees are consistent, that is,

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}(\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}({\rm Err}(\hat{g}(T_{K_{N}}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))-{\rm Err}(g^{*}_{N}))=0.

While Corollary
4.5
allows the component functions of the model to have unbounded variation, it requires the number of relevant variables
\|g^{*}_{N}\|_{\ell_{0}}
to be uniformly bounded in
N
, in contrast to the
o(\sqrt{K_{N}})
growth of
\|g^{*}_{N}\|_{\text{TV}}
tolerated in Corollary
4.4
.

Remark 4
.

Corollary
4.4
and Corollary
4.5
do not offer guidance on how to choose the depth
K_{N}
. In practice, it is best to let the data decide and therefore cost complexity pruning (i.e., weakest link pruning
(
Breiman et al. 1984
)
) is recommended. This would have one first grow a full tree
T_{max}
(to maximum depth) and then minimize

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T))+\lambda|T|

over all trees
T
that can be obtained from
T_{max}
by iteratively merging its internal nodes, where
\lambda
is a positive constant and
|T|
is the number of terminal nodes of
T
. Working with the resulting pruned tree enables one to obtain oracle inequalities of the form (
25
),
but with the advantage of having the infimum over both the depth
K\geq 1
and additive functions
g(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{G}}^{1}
.

4.5
Related consistency results and optimality

Here we compare our consistency results for CART and C4.5 to those of other prediction methods and the optimal minimax rates.

The reader might be somewhat surprised by the consistency statements in Corollary
4.4
and Corollary
4.5
, especially since they are qualitatively similar to existing performance guarantees for predictors based on very different principles, like boosting or neural networks.
For example,
(
Bühlmann 2006
, Theorem 1)
states that boosting with linear learners is also consistent for a sequence of
\ell_{1}
constrained linear models
g_{N}^{*}(\mathbf{x})=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T}_{N}\mathbf{x}
on
[0,1]^{p}
in the high dimensional regime, i.e., when
\log(p_{N})\asymp N^{1-\xi}
for
\xi\in(0,1)
and
\sup_{N}\|g_{N}^{*}\|_{{\rm TV}}=\sup_{N}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{N}\|_{\ell_{1}}<\infty
.

To provide another frame of reference, we also compare the consistency rates of CART (
27
) and C4.5 (
28
) to the corresponding minimax rates for the model classes we consider.
For regression, the optimal minimax rate (with respect to excess squared error risk) for
s
-sparse additive regression with continuously differentiable component functions is
\max\{(s/N)\log(p/s),sN^{-2/3}\}

(
Raskutti et al. 2012
)
, while the optimal rate for additive regression models with bounded total variation
\ell_{1}
norm (when
p\gg N
) is
\sqrt{(\log(p))/N}

(
Tan and Zhang 2019
)
. Both of these settings, corresponding to
\ell_{0}
and
\ell_{1}
sparsity, respectively, are covered by our theory for regression trees, and so our
1/\log(N)
rate (
27
) is evidently sub-optimal. For classification, the literature is less developed, though there are results for linear logistic regression. For example,
Abramovich and Grinshtein 2019
show that the optimal minimax rate (with respect to excess mis-classification risk) for
s
-sparse linear logistic regression is
\sqrt{(s/N)\log(p/s)}
, again much faster than our
1/\sqrt{\log(N)}
rate (
28
).

The sub-optimality of our rates is due to a combination of two factors. First, the form of the decision tree predictions—averaging the response data in the terminal nodes—introduces an inductive bias, which, in the aforementioned
s
-sparse additive regression setting, leads to best-case (yet still sub-optimal) rates of order
N^{-2/(2+s)}

(
Tan et al. 2021
)
.
The second source of sub-optimality stems from our method of analysis. Recall from Lemma
4.1

that a key step in our proofs is to lower bound the impurity gain at each node by a constant multiple of the
squared
excess risk, viz.,

\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})\gtrsim(\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}))-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(g^{*}))^{2}

whenever
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}))>\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(g^{*})
.
Had we been able to show (or rather presumed in the form of an assumption) a lower bound

\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})\gtrsim\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}))-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(g^{*})

whenever
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}))>\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(g^{*})
,
then it would have been possible for us to obtain polynomial convergence rates,
instead of logarithmic. In the next section, we obtain a similar lower bound on the impurity gain (Lemma
5.1
) for separable, large margin data, which does indeed lead to a faster consistency rate.

5
Beyond discriminative models

In Section
4
, we
operated under a
discriminative statistical model of the data; that is, we study decision trees under an explicit form of the conditional distribution
\mathbb{P}_{y\mid\mathbf{x}}
.
In lieu of the logistic regression model from Section
2.2
, here we consider
another ubiquitous classification setting in which the data can be perfectly separated into two classes by an additive decision boundary, with margin
\gamma>0
. As we shall see, this setting will allow us to obtain consistency rates which are exponentially faster than those from Section
4.4
.
Separable data assumptions, such as the one formalized in Assumption
1
below,
are prevalent in statistical learning literature, especially in the context of (kernel) support vector machines
(
Boser et al. 1992
)
and boosting
(
Bartlett et al. 1998
)
.
Note that such an assumption is particularly appropriate for our high dimensional setting—when
p
is large, there is more freedom for the data to be separated by an additive decision boundary.

Assumption 1

(Additively separable, large margin)
.

There exists
\gamma\in(0,1]
and
f^{*}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}f_{j}(x_{j})\in{\mathcal{G}}^{1}
with
\max\{\|f^{*}\|_{{\rm TV}},\|f^{*}\|_{\infty}\}\leq 1
such that for almost all
pairs
(\mathbf{x},y)
drawn from the joint distribution
\mathbb{P}_{(\mathbf{x},y)}
,

yf^{*}(\mathbf{x})\geq\gamma.

Remark 5
.

If the additive function
f^{*}(\mathbf{x})=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T}\mathbf{x}
is linear over
[0,1]^{p}
, then
\gamma
corresponds to the maximum

\ell_{1}
-margin subject to
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\ell_{1}}\leq 1
, matching the standard margin framework for linearly separable data.

Note that any separable, large margin distributional assumption implies that the Bayes risk is zero; therefore, we aim to show that a tree constructed with C4.5 methodology will have mis-classification risk converging to zero.
While there are some similarities with the proof of Corollary
4.4
, the key difference
is an improved lower bound on the impurity gain that establishes nearly linear, rather than quadratic (see Lemma
4.1
), dependence on the within-node empirical risk.

Lemma 5.1

(Information gain for C4.5 with separable, large margin data)
.

Grant Assumption
1
.
Let
K\geq 1
be any depth and let
\mathrm{t}
be any terminal node of
T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}
.
We have that

\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})\geq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{30}\cdot\frac{\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))}{\log(1/\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))}.

(29)

Using Lemma
5.1
in conjunction with (
20
)
shows that the empirical risk decays sub-exponentially fast in the depth
K
,
much faster than the polynomial
rate of decay for a logistic regression model (Theorem
4.2
).

Theorem 5.2

(Empirical risk for C4.5 with separable, large margin data)
.

Granting Assumption
1
, for all depths
K\geq 1
,

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))\leq\exp\Big(-\Big(\frac{\gamma^{2}K}{30}\Big)^{1/2}\Big).

Employing the above empirical risk bound, we can establish the following mis-classification risk bound.

Theorem 5.3

(Mis-classification risk for C4.5 with separable, large margin data)
.

Granting Assumption
1
, for all depths
K\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}({\rm Err}(\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))\leq 2\exp\Big(-\Big(\frac{\gamma^{2}K}{120}\Big)^{1/2}\Big)+C_{2}\Big(\frac{2^{K}\log^{2}(N)\log(Np)}{N}\Big)^{1/4},

where

C_{2}
is the same constant in the statement of Theorem
4.3
.

Theorem
5.3
immediately implies the following consistency result.

Corollary 5.4

(Consistency of C4.5 with separable, large margin data)
.

Consider a sequence of decision boundaries
\{f^{*}_{N}(\cdot)\}_{N=1}^{\infty}
with respective margins
\{\gamma_{N}(\cdot)\}_{N=1}^{\infty}

such that Assumption
1
holds.
Suppose
f^{*}_{N}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{p_{N}}f_{j}(x_{j})\in{\mathcal{G}}^{1}
and that
K_{N}\rightarrow\infty
,
\gamma_{N}=\omega(1/\sqrt{K_{N}})
,
and
\frac{2^{K_{N}}\log^{2}(N)\log(Np_{N})}{N}\rightarrow 0

as
N\rightarrow\infty
. Then, classification trees are consistent, that is,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}({\rm Err}(\hat{g}(T_{K_{N}}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))=0.

The hypotheses of Corollary
5.4
are satisfied if, for example,
K_{N}=\lfloor(\xi/2)\log_{2}(N)\rfloor
,
\log(p_{N})\asymp N^{1-\xi}
for
\xi\in(0,1)
, and
\gamma_{N}=\gamma

for some
\gamma\in(0,1]
.
In this case, from Theorem
5.3
, the consistency rate of C4.5 is

{\mathcal{O}}\big(\exp\big(-\sqrt{\gamma^{2}\xi\log(N)/240}\big)\big),

(30)

which is sub-polynomial in
N
,
and exponentially faster than the
1/\sqrt{\log(N)}
rate (
28
) for a logistic regression model.

6
Models with interactions

Our main results in Section
4
focused on (ordinary or logistic) additive regression models primarily because notions of approximate and exact sparsity are easier to define and more interpretable in high dimensional settings. However, the interpretation of CART and C4.5 in an (ordinary or logistic) additive regression setting will be muddled by the fact that all interactions it finds will be spurious. It is therefore desirable to have a more comprehensive theory, particularly for data settings where decision trees could be useful.

As we now explain, it is possible to motivate reasonable assumptions
so that our main results for (ordinary or logistic) additive regression models
can be extended to models with interactions.
To this end, recall the proof outline in Section
4
. We started with the recursion (
20
) and then used a lower bound on the impurity gain (Lemma
4.1
), tailored for (ordinary or logistic) additive regression models, to bound the empirical risk (Theorem
4.2
) and ultimately show consistency (Corollary
4.4
). In fact, under suitable assumptions, many of these same ideas work when model class
{\mathcal{G}}
has interaction terms.

To illustrate the broad strokes in obtaining these extensions, we will consider the class of
d
-way interaction models

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}^{d}\coloneqq\Big\{g(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\sum_{j_{1}}g_{j_{1}}(x_{j_{1}})+\sum_{j_{1}<j_{2}}g_{j_{1},j_{2}}(x_{j_{1}},x_{j_{2}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\cdots+\sum_{j_{1}<j_{2}<\cdots<j_{d}}g_{j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{d}}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d})\Big\},

which encompasses the additive function class
{\mathcal{G}}^{1}
. Thus, models belonging to
{\mathcal{G}}^{d}
have interactions involving up to
d
predictor variables. Conversely, any square-integrable function with interactions involving at most
d
variables admits a functional ANOVA decomposition in the form above, where the functional components in the expansion have zero mean and are orthogonal to each other
(
Hooker 2007
)
. We note in passing that, while decision trees can discover interaction effects through the way they are constructed, they do not directly leverage additive structure in the model and so are unlikely to achieve the optimal rates of convergence on
{\mathcal{G}}^{d}
(this is certainly the case for
{\mathcal{G}}^{1}
; see Section
4.5
).

By recursing (
20
), we can write the empirical risk of the tree as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K}))

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-d}))-\sum_{\mathrm{t}\in T_{K-d}}\frac{N_{\mathrm{t}}}{N}\mathcal{IG}_{d}(\mathrm{t}),

(31)

where
\mathcal{IG}_{d}(\mathrm{t})\coloneqq\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})+\sum_{\mathrm{t}^{\prime}}\frac{N_{\mathrm{t}^{\prime}}}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t}^{\prime})
is the
d
-level impurity gain
of a node
\mathrm{t}\in T_{K-d}
. Here in the definition of
\mathcal{IG}_{d}(\mathrm{t})
, the sum extends over all descendent nodes
\mathrm{t}^{\prime}
of
\mathrm{t}
up to depth
K-1
. Another way of thinking about
\mathcal{IG}_{d}(\mathrm{t})
is that it measures the decrease in risk from greedily splitting
d
times in
\mathrm{t}
and thus captures interactions involving up to
d
predictor variables.
For example, according to the representations given by (
11
) and (
12
),

\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})=\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(h(\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}}))-\min_{\beta_{0},\beta_{1}}\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\mathbf{1}(x_{j_{\mathrm{t}}}>s_{\mathrm{t}})),

(32)

which captures only main effects from splitting
once
in
\mathrm{t}
. This explains why Lemma
4.1
relates the impurity gain to the empirical risk of an (ordinary or logistic) additive regression model. On the other hand,
\mathcal{IG}_{2}(\mathrm{t})=\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})+P_{\mathrm{t}_{L}}\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t}_{L})+P_{\mathrm{t}_{R}}\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t}_{R})
equals

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(h(\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}}))-\min_{\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3},j_{1},s_{1},j_{2},s_{2}}\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\mathbf{1}(x_{j_{\mathrm{t}}}>s_{\mathrm{t}})+

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\beta_{2}\mathbf{1}(x_{j_{1}}>s_{1},\;x_{j_{\mathrm{t}}}\leq s_{\mathrm{t}})+\beta_{3}\mathbf{1}(x_{j_{2}}>s_{2},\;x_{j_{\mathrm{t}}}>s_{\mathrm{t}})),

and thus captures both main effects and second order effects from greedily splitting
twice
in
\mathrm{t}
. It is then reasonable to postulate that one could relate
\mathcal{IG}_{2}(\mathrm{t})
to the empirical risk of a function in
{\mathcal{G}}^{2}
. Consequently, a natural generalization of the impurity gain inequality in Lemma
4.1
to functions in
{\mathcal{G}}^{d}
would be the following condition.

Assumption 2
.

Let
g^{*}(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{G}}^{d}
and
K\geq d
. For any terminal node
\mathrm{t}
of the tree
T_{K-d}
such that
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-d}))>\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(g^{*})
, we have

\mathcal{IG}_{d}(\mathrm{t})\geq\frac{(\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-d}))-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(g^{*}))^{2}}{V^{2}(g^{*})},

(33)

for some complexity constant
V(g^{*})
that depends only on
g^{*}(\cdot)
.

Following a similar argument to the one outlined in Section
4
, we can substitute the purported lower bound (
33
) into (
31
) and use Jensen’s inequality to produce the recursion

{\mathcal{E}}_{K}\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}\Big(1-\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}}{V^{2}(g^{*})}\Big),\quad{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}\geq 0,\quad K\geq d.

Thus, granting the impurity gain condition (
33
), by Lemma
D.1
in Supplement
D
,
we have that

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K}))\leq\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(g^{*})+\frac{V^{2}(g^{*})d}{K+2d+1},\quad K\geq d,

(34)

a direct analogue to Theorem
4.2
. Thus, the excess empirical risk for a
d
-way interaction model is of order
d/K
, which means that the depth
K
should exceed
d
for it to be small. This is to be expected since the depth

K
controls the interaction order of the tree.
Using (
34
) and
the same steps as the proof of Theorem
4.3
and Corollary
4.4
, we can easily deduce that
if
\{g^{*}_{N}(\cdot)\}_{N=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence of true models in
{\mathcal{G}}^{d}
with
\sup_{N}\|g^{*}_{N}\|_{\infty}<\infty
and
K_{N}\rightarrow\infty
,
V(g_{N}^{*})=o(\sqrt{K_{N}})
, and
\frac{2^{K_{N}}\log^{2}(N)\log(Np_{N})}{N}\rightarrow 0
as

, then both regression trees and classification trees are consistent, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}(\|\hat{g}(T_{K_{N}}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})-g^{*}_{N}\|^{2})\to 0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}({\rm Err}(\hat{g}(T_{K_{N}}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))-{\rm Err}(g^{*}_{N})\to 0,\quad\text{as}\quad N\to\infty.

(35)

7
Random forests

The predictive abilities of individual decision trees should intuitively be inherited by random forests due to the ensemble principle and convexity of squared error (see
(
Denil et al. 2014
, Proposition 3 and Proposition 4)
,
(
Breiman 2001
, Section 11)
, or
(
Breiman 1996
, Section 4.1)
). Indeed, our main results for individual trees in Section
4

also carry over to Breiman’s random forests
(
Breiman 2001
)
with relative ease, as we now explain.
To keep redundancy to a minimum, we will restrict ourselves to the regression setting of Section
2.1
.

7.1
Growing the forest

Consider a sub-sample
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
of size
a_{N}

from the original dataset
{\mathcal{D}}
, whereby each sample point is drawn uniformly at random without replacement.
2
2

2

We deviate slightly from Breiman’s original random forests
(
Breiman 2001
)
, as we do not grow the constituent trees to maximum depth with bootstrapped data. Note, however, that sampling with and without replacement produce similar results when
a_{N}=\lfloor N/2\rfloor

(
Friedman and Hall 2007
)
.

From this sub-sample, we train a depth
K
tree
T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}}
with CART methodology in the usual way, except that, at each internal node, we select
m
(also known as
mtry
) of the
p
variables uniformly at random without replacement, as candidates for splitting. That is, for each internal node
\mathrm{t}
of
T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}}
, we generate a random subset
{\mathcal{S}}\subset\{1,2,\dots,p\}
of size
m
and split along a variable
x_{\hat{\jmath}_{\mathrm{t}}}
with split point
\hat{s}_{\mathrm{t}}
, where
(\hat{\jmath}_{\mathrm{t}},\hat{s}_{\mathrm{t}})\in\argmax_{(j\in{\mathcal{S}},\;s\in\mathbb{R})}\mathcal{IG}(j,s,\mathrm{t})
.

We grow
B
of these depth
K
regression trees separately using, respectively,
B
independent realizations
\boldsymbol{\Theta}=(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2},\dots,\Theta_{B})^{T}
of a random variable
\Theta
. Here
\Theta
is distributed according to the law that generates the sub-sampled training data
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
and candidate variables
{\mathcal{S}}
for splitting at each of the nodes. The output of the
b^{{\rm th}}
regression tree is denoted by
\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}}(\Theta_{b}))
.

With this notation in place, the random forest output is then simply the empirical average of the
B
regression tree outputs, namely,

\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq B^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}}(\Theta_{b}))(\mathbf{x}).

(36)

7.2
Oracle inequality for random forests

By a modification of the proofs of Theorem
4.2
and Theorem
4.3
, it is possible to show the following oracle inequality for random forests.

Theorem 7.1

(Oracle inequality for CART random forests)
.

Granting the noise condition (
24
), for all depths
K\geq 1
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\Theta},{\mathcal{D}}}(\|\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})-g^{*}\|^{2})\leq 2\inf_{g(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{G}}^{1}}\Bigg\{\|g-g^{*}\|^{2}+\frac{p}{m}\frac{\|g\|^{2}_{{\rm TV}}}{K+3}+C_{1}\frac{2^{K}\log^{2}(a_{N})\log(a_{N}p)}{a_{N}}\Bigg\},

where
C_{1}
is the same constant in the statement of Theorem
4.3
.

Remark 6
.

As the number of trees
B
in the forest approaches infinity,
by
(
Breiman 2001
, Theorem 11.1)
,

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\Theta},{\mathcal{D}}}(\|\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})-g^{*}\|^{2})\rightarrow\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(\|\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mid{\mathcal{D}}}(\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}))-g^{*}\|^{2}),\quad\text{almost surely.}

Thus, when
B
is large, Theorem
7.1
resembles results such as (
25
) in Theorem
4.3

which measure the accuracy of a predictor via the
expected
prediction risk over the data
{\mathcal{D}}
.

To the best of our knowledge, Theorem
7.1
is one of the first results in the literature that shows explicitly the impact on the prediction risk from randomly choosing subsets of variables as candidates for splitting at the nodes, without any restrictive assumptions on the tree or data generating process. Even though it is not captured by Theorem
7.1
, empirically, the random variable selection mechanism of forests has the effect of de-correlating and encouraging diversity among the constituent trees, which can greatly improve the performance. It also reduces the computational time of constructing each tree, since the optimal split points do not need to be calculated for every variable at each node. What Theorem
7.1
does reveal, however, is that this mechanism cannot hurt the prediction risk beyond a benign factor of
p/m
. In fact, standard implementations of regression forests use a default value of
m
equal to
\lfloor p/3\rfloor
.
With this choice, we see by comparing Theorem
7.1
and Corollary
4.4
that there is essentially no loss in performance (at most a factor of
p/m=3
) over individual trees, despite not optimizing over the full set of variables at the internal nodes. It is also interesting to note that we recover the bound (
25
) for individual regression trees when
m=p
.

7.3
Consistency of random forests

Theorem
7.1
immediately implies
a consistency result for regression forests, analogous to Corollary
4.4
for individual regression trees.

Corollary 7.2

(Consistency of random forests)
.

Consider a sequence of prediction problems (
1
) with true models
\{g^{*}_{N}(\cdot)\}_{N=1}^{\infty}
.
Assume that
g^{*}_{N}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{p_{N}}g_{j}(x_{j})\in{\mathcal{G}}^{1}
and
\sup_{N}\|g^{*}_{N}\|_{\infty}<\infty
.
Suppose that
K_{N}\rightarrow\infty
,

\|g_{N}^{*}\|_{{\rm TV}}=o(\sqrt{(m_{N}/p_{N})K_{N}})
, and
\frac{2^{K_{N}}\log^{2}(a_{N})\log(a_{N}p_{N})}{a_{N}}\rightarrow 0
as
N\rightarrow\infty
.
Then, granting the noise condition (
24
), regression forests
are consistent, that is,

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\Theta},{\mathcal{D}}}(\|\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})-g^{*}\|^{2})=0.

Like the consistency statement for CART in Corollary
4.4
,
the hypotheses of Corollary
7.2
are satisfied if, for example,
K_{N}=\lfloor(\xi/2)\log_{2}(a_{N})\rfloor
,
\log(p_{N})\asymp a_{N}^{1-\xi}
, and
m_{N}=\lfloor p_{N}/3\rfloor
, for some constant
\xi\in(0,1)
, yielding the same
{\mathcal{O}}(1/\log(N))
rate as a single tree (see (
27
)).
It is also interesting to note that consistency is still possible even if only a vanishing fraction of variables are randomly selected at each node, i.e.,
m_{N}=o(p_{N})
.
At the extreme end, consistency holds even when
m_{N}\equiv 1
; that is, only a single coordinate is selected at random at each node, provided appropriate restrictions are placed on
p_{N}
and
\|g^{*}_{N}\|_{\text{TV}}
.

Corollary
7.2
provides a partial answer to a problem posed by
Scornet et al. 2015
:

It remains that a substantial research effort is still needed to understand the properties of forests in a high-dimensional setting, when
p=p_{N}
may be substantially larger than the sample size.

More specifically, Corollary
7.2
strengthens
(
Scornet et al. 2015
, Theorem 1)
, which shows that random forests are consistent for additive regression when
p
is fixed, the component functions are continuous, and
K_{N}\rightarrow\infty
and
\frac{2^{K_{N}}(\log^{9}(a_{N}))}{a_{N}}\rightarrow 0
as
N\rightarrow\infty
.
In contrast, here we allow the dimensionality
p_{N}
to grow sub-exponentially with the sample size
N
(under
\ell_{1}
sparsity constraints) and also for the component functions to be possibly discontinuous. In fact, the proof of Corollary
4.5
can be modified to establish consistency of random forests for additive regression with growing dimensionality
p_{N}
(under
\ell_{0}
sparsity constraints) and Borel measurable component functions—without assuming continuity or finite total variation.
More specifically, suppose that
\sup_{N}\|g^{*}_{N}\|_{\infty}<\infty
,
\sup_{N}\|g^{*}_{N}\|_{\ell_{0}}<\infty
, and
m_{N}\asymp p_{N}
.
If
K_{N}\rightarrow\infty

and
\frac{2^{K_{N}}\log^{2}(a_{N})\log(a_{N}p_{N})}{a_{N}}\rightarrow 0
as
N\rightarrow\infty
, then granting the noise condition (
24
), we have

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\Theta},{\mathcal{D}}}(\|\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})-g^{*}_{N}\|^{2})=0.

We close this section by saying that it is still largely a mystery (at least theoretically) why bagging and the random feature selection mechanism are so effective at reducing the prediction risk. Our bounds in Theorem
7.1
only show that these apparatuses do not degrade the performance beyond small factors. Certainly more work needs to be done to answer these questions.

7.4
Empirical studies

Because random forests are now a classic topic in machine learning and data science, they have been subject to thorough empirical scrutiny and investigation under a variety of model specifications. This includes the high dimensional regime when
p\gg N
. As the literature is vast, we only mention a few experimental papers below.

Regarding the
m
parameter,
Genuer et al. 2008
provide a detailed empirical analysis of random forests in the high dimensional setting. They consider both synthetic and real-world data for regression and classification tasks. Among the simulated examples for regression are Friedman’s benchmark models
(
Breiman 1996
)
, which, in our notation, belong to the classes
{\mathcal{G}}^{2}
and
{\mathcal{G}}^{4}
and involve either
4
or
5
relevant predictor variables. For a sample size of
N=100
, the simulation results (see Figures 3-5) reveal that forests are quite robust to the inclusion of noisy predictor variables (with ambient dimensionality ranging from
p=100
to
p=1000
). The author finds that forests achieve best performance when
m
is set to be equal to the ambient dimension
p
, corresponding to bagging. Similar observations are made in
(
Segal 2004
, Table I)
for one of Friedman’s models with
N=200
and
p=510
.

An investigation of the tree depth in random forests can be found in
(
Zhou and Mentch 2021
, Figures 10 & 11)
, where they sample
N=50
and
N=100
data points from a sparse linear model with
5
and
10
relevant variables, and ambient dimensionality
p=1000
. Shallow trees are found to be advantageous when the model has a low signal-to-noise ratio. In
(
Duroux and Scornet 2018
)
, Figures A.1 & A.3 reveal that forests with smaller trees achieve similar performance to forests with fully grown trees if the number of terminal nodes is properly tuned. One of their examples (Model 8) consists of a sparse additive model with
4
relevant variables,
p=1000
, and
N=500
.

Other references on the impact of hyperparameter tuning in random forests can be found in the review article
(
Probst et al. 2019
)
.

It is still largely a mystery (at least theoretically) why bagging and the random feature selection mechanism are so effective at reducing the prediction risk. Our bounds in Theorem
7.1
only show that these apparatuses do not degrade the performance beyond small factors. Fascinating recent work by
Mentch and Zhou 2020
shows that
m
plays a similar role as the shrinkage penalty in explicitly regularized procedures. More specifically, when
p>N
, they show that if an ensemble predictor is formed by averaging over many linear regression models with orthogonal designs and randomly selected subsets of variables, then asymptotically as the number of models goes to infinity, the coefficient vector of the ensemble is shrunk by a factor of
m/p
. It is possible that a similar form of implicit regularization is occurring in our high dimensional additive setting, which may lead to even better performance over individual trees. Certainly more work needs to be done to answer these questions.

8
Conclusion

In this paper, we showed that decision trees and random forests adapt to
\ell_{0}
or
\ell_{1}
forms of sparsity and can accommodate (essentially) arbitrary types of predictor variables, such as continuous, discrete, and/or dependent.
Our work is primarily of theoretical value, since we study existing decision tree methodology, namely, CART and C4.5. Nevertheless, given their widespread popularity in many applied disciplines, we believe our results can be used as a theoretical justification for practical application on high dimensional regression and classification problems. Specifically, Theorem
4.3
and Theorem
7.1
give an explicit characterization of how the various quantities (e.g., tree depth, mtry, sample size, ambient dimension, sparsity level) interact with each other and determine the performance, thereby motivating specific choices of the parameters (e.g., tree depth, mtry). Furthermore, consistency in Corollary
4.4
and Corollary
7.2
serves as a
stress-test
and shows how practical implementations of decision trees and random forests can be accurate even with a very high predictor variable count.

Though we do not measure the out of sample performance with the population (also known as the logistic) risk for C4.5, it will be a useful tool in our proofs. Recall that the population risk (
3
) for C4.5 is

{\mathcal{R}}(g)=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)}\log(1+\exp(-yg(\mathbf{x})))=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)}q(yg(\mathbf{x})),

(A.8)

where we define
q(z)\coloneqq\log(1+\exp(-z))
for convenience. We will frequently use the first and second derivatives

q^{\prime}(z)=\frac{-e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}},\quad q^{\prime\prime}(c)=\frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^{2}}=\frac{e^{-|z|}}{(1+e^{-|z|})^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{4}

(A.9)

to approximate
q(\cdot)
with a second order Taylor expansion:

(b-a)q^{\prime}(a)\leq q(b)-q(a)\leq(b-a)q^{\prime}(a)+q^{\prime\prime}(c)(b-a)^{2},

(A.10)

where
c
is some number between
\min\{a,b\}
and
\max\{a,b\}
.

B.1.1
Impurity gain for CART

Before we prove Lemma
4.1
for CART, we first introduce some key ideas.

We use recent tools for the analysis of decision trees from
Klusowski 2020
, namely, Lemma A.4 in the supplement therein. Let
g(\cdot)
be any additive function in
{\mathcal{G}}^{1}
.
Define an empirical measure
\Pi(j,s)
on split points
s
and variables
x_{j}
, having Radon-Nikodym derivative (with respect to Lebesgue measure and counting measure)

\frac{d\Pi(j,s)}{d(j,s)}\coloneqq\frac{|Dg_{j}(s)|\sqrt{P_{\mathrm{t}_{L}}P_{\mathrm{t}_{R}}}}{\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{p}\int|Dg_{j^{\prime}}(s^{\prime})|\sqrt{P_{\mathrm{t}^{\prime}_{L}}P_{\mathrm{t}^{\prime}_{R}}}ds^{\prime}},

(B.1)

where
(\mathrm{t}_{L},\mathrm{t}_{R})
and
(\mathrm{t}^{\prime}_{L},\mathrm{t}^{\prime}_{R})
are the daughter nodes corresponding to the splits
(j,s)
and
(j^{\prime},s^{\prime})
, respectively. We use the shorthand
Dg_{j}(\cdot)
to denote the divided difference of
g_{j}(\cdot)
for the successive ordered data points along the
j^{\text{th}}
direction within
\mathrm{t}
.
That is, if
x_{(1)j}\leq x_{(2)j}\leq\cdots\leq x_{(N_{\mathrm{t}})j}
denotes the ordered data along the
j^{\text{th}}
direction within
\mathrm{t}
, then

Dg_{j}(s)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{g_{j}(x_{(i+1)j})-g_{j}(x_{(i)j})}{x_{(i+1)j}-x_{(i)j}}&\text{if}\;x_{(i)j}\leq s<x_{(i+1)j}\\
0&\text{if}\;s=x_{(i)j}=x_{(i+1)j},\;s<x_{(1)j},\;\text{or}\;s>x_{(N_{\mathrm{t}})j}\\
\end{cases}.

(B.2)

Observe that
Dg_{j}(\cdot)
coincides with the derivative of the function that linearly interpolates the points
\{(x_{(1)j},g_{j}(x_{(1)j})),(x_{(2)j},g_{j}(x_{(2)j})),\dots,(x_{(N_{\mathrm{t}})j},g_{j}(x_{(N_{\mathrm{t}})j}))\}
.

We are now ready to prove Lemma
4.1
for CART.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
for CART.

Since
\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})
is by definition the maximum of
\mathcal{IG}(j,s,\mathrm{t})
over
j
and
s
, we have from the fact that a maximum is larger than average

\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})\geq\int\mathcal{IG}(j,s,\mathrm{t})d\Pi(j,s)=\int|\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},\Psi_{\mathrm{t}}\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}|^{2}d\Pi(j,s),

(B.3)

where the last identity follows from
Lemma
A.1
.
Next, by Jensen’s inequality for the square function, (
B.3
) is further lower bounded by

\int|\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},\Psi_{\mathrm{t}}\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}|^{2}d\Pi(j,s)\geq\Big(\int|\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},\Psi_{\mathrm{t}}\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}|d\Pi(j,s)\Big)^{2}.

(B.4)

We now evaluate the expression in the right hand side of (
B.4
).
Rewriting (
A.4
) as

\sqrt{P_{\mathrm{t}_{L}}P_{\mathrm{t}_{R}}}\Psi_{\mathrm{t}}\mathbf{1}(\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{t})=\mathbf{1}(\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{t}_{L})P_{\mathrm{t}_{R}}-\mathbf{1}(\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{t}_{R})P_{\mathrm{t}_{L}}=-(\mathbf{1}(x_{j}>s)-P_{\mathrm{t}_{R}})\mathbf{1}(\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{t}),

we see that
\sqrt{P_{\mathrm{t}_{L}}P_{\mathrm{t}_{R}}}\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},\Psi_{\mathrm{t}}\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}=-\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},\mathbf{1}(x_{j}>s)\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}
, which implies that

\int|\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},\Psi_{\mathrm{t}}\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}|d\Pi(j,s)=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{p}\int|Dg_{j}(s)||\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},\mathbf{1}(x_{j}>s)\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}|ds}{\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{p}\int|Dg_{j^{\prime}}(s^{\prime})|\sqrt{P_{\mathrm{t}^{\prime}_{L}}P_{\mathrm{t}^{\prime}_{R}}}ds^{\prime}}.

(B.5)

Continuing on the numerator in (
B.5
), we use the fact that the integral of the absolute value is at least the absolute value of the integral, yielding

\sum_{j=1}^{p}\int|Dg_{j}(s)||\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},\mathbf{1}(x_{j}>s)\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}|ds\geq\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{p}\int Dg_{j}(s)\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},\mathbf{1}(x_{j}>s)\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}ds\Big|.

(B.6)

Using linearity of the inner product and integration and the fundamental theorem of calculus, the expression in the right hand side of (
B.6
) can be simplified as follows

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}\int Dg_{j}(s)\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},\mathbf{1}(x_{j}>s)\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}ds

\displaystyle=\Big\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},\sum_{j=1}^{p}\int Dg_{j}(s)\mathbf{1}(x_{j}>s)ds\Big\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}

(B.7)

\displaystyle=\Big\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},\sum_{j=1}^{p}g_{j}\Big\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}

\displaystyle=\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},g\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}.

Combining
(
B.3
), (
B.4
), (
B.5
), (
B.6
), and
(
B.7
)
proves that

\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})\geq\frac{|\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},g\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}|^{2}}{\big(\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{p}\int|Dg_{j^{\prime}}(s^{\prime})|\sqrt{P_{\mathrm{t}^{\prime}_{L}}P_{\mathrm{t}^{\prime}_{R}}}ds^{\prime}\big)^{2}}.

(B.8)

Our next goal is to provide respective lower and upper bounds on the numerator and denominator of (
B.8
).
For the denominator of (
B.8
), note that for each
j^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\int|Dg_{j^{\prime}}(s^{\prime})|\sqrt{P_{\mathrm{t}^{\prime}_{L}}P_{\mathrm{t}^{\prime}_{R}}}ds^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}-1}\int_{x_{(i)j^{\prime}}}^{x_{(i+1)j^{\prime}}}|Dg_{j^{\prime}}(s^{\prime})|ds^{\prime}\sqrt{(i/{N_{\mathrm{t}}})(1-i/{N_{\mathrm{t}}})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}-1}|g_{j^{\prime}}(x_{(i+1)j^{\prime}})-g_{j^{\prime}}(x_{(i)j^{\prime}})|\sqrt{(i/{N_{\mathrm{t}}})(1-i/{N_{\mathrm{t}}})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{{\rm TV}(g_{j^{\prime}})}{2},

and hence, summing over
j^{\prime}=1,2,\dots,p
, we obtain

\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{p}\int|Dg_{j^{\prime}}(s^{\prime})|\sqrt{P_{\mathrm{t}^{\prime}_{L}}P_{\mathrm{t}^{\prime}_{R}}}ds^{\prime}\leq\frac{\|g\|_{\rm TV}}{2}.

(B.9)

For the numerator of (
B.8
), we use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to lower bound
\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},g\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}
by

\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},g\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}=\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},y\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}+\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},g-y\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}\geq\|y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{t}}-\|y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}}\|_{\mathrm{t}}\|y-g\|_{\mathrm{t}},

where we also used
\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},y\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}=\|y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{t}}
.
Now, by the AM–GM inequality,
\|y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}}\|_{\mathrm{t}}\|y-g\|_{\mathrm{t}}\leq(\|y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{t}}+\|y-g\|^{2}_{\mathrm{t}})/2
, and hence
\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},g\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}\geq(\|y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{t}}-\|y-g\|^{2}_{\mathrm{t}})/2
.
Squaring both sides and using the assumption
\|y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{t}}-\|y-g\|^{2}_{\mathrm{t}}\geq 0
, we have

|\langle y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}},g\rangle_{\mathrm{t}}|^{2}\geq\frac{(\|y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{t}}-\|y-g\|^{2}_{\mathrm{t}})^{2}}{4}.

(B.10)

Applying inequalities (
B.9
) and (
B.10
) to (
B.8
), we therefore have shown that

\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})\geq\frac{(\|y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{t}}-\|y-g\|^{2}_{\mathrm{t}})^{2}}{\|g\|^{2}_{\rm TV}}=\frac{(\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}))-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(g))^{2}}{\|g\|_{\rm TV}^{2}},

which completes the proof.
∎

B.1.2
Information gain for C4.5

for C4.5
is similar to the structure of the proof of Lemma

Let

(B.11)

(B.13)

Before proving Lemma
4.1

Lemma B.1
.

For any
v\in[0,1]
and any additive

\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})\geq v(\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(g))-v^{2}\Big(\frac{(\|g\|_{\rm TV}+\|g\|_{\infty})^{2}}{4}+\frac{\exp(v(\|g\|_{\rm TV}+\|g\|_{\infty}))}{(1-v)^{2}}\Big).

Proof.

Recalling (
A.5
),
we define the decision stump

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathrm{t}}(i,j,s)=\beta_{\mathrm{t}}(1-v)+\beta(g)\mathbf{1}(x_{ij}>s),

(B.15)

\displaystyle\beta(g)\coloneqq v\cdot\Big(\text{sgn}(Dg_{j}(s))\cdot\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{p}\int|Dg_{j^{\prime}}(s^{\prime})|ds^{\prime}+g(\mathbf{x}_{(1)})\Big).

By (
B.13
)
we have that

|\beta(g)|\leq v\Bigg(\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{p}\int|Dg_{j^{\prime}}(s^{\prime})|ds^{\prime}+|g(\mathbf{x}_{(1)})|\Bigg)\leq v(\|g\|_{\rm TV}+\|g\|_{\infty}).

(B.16)

From (
32
)
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\beta_{\mathrm{t}})-\min_{\beta_{0},\beta_{1},j,s}\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\mathbf{1}(x_{j}>s))

(B.17)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{t}}\int(q(y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}})-q(y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}(i,j,s)))d\Pi(j,s).

Applying (
A.10
) to (
B.17
), we see that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{t}}\int(q(y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}})-q(y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}(i,j,s)))d\Pi(j,s)

(B.18)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{t}}\Big(\int(y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}-y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}(i,j,s))q^{\prime}(y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}})d\Pi(j,s)-\frac{v^{2}(|\beta_{\mathrm{t}}|+\|g\|_{\rm TV}+\|g\|_{\infty})^{2}}{2}q^{\prime\prime}(c_{i})\Big),

where
c_{i}
is between
y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}
and
y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}(i,j,s)
and we used the fact that
|y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}-y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}(i,j,s)|\leq v(|\beta_{\mathrm{t}}|+\|g\|_{\rm TV}+\|g\|_{\infty})
from (
B.16
).

q^{\prime\prime}(c_{i})\leq\exp(-|c_{i}|)\leq\exp(-(1-v)|\beta_{\mathrm{t}}|+v(\|g\|_{\rm TV}+\|g\|_{\infty}))

\beta_{\mathrm{t}}^{2}q^{\prime\prime}(c_{i})\leq\beta^{2}_{\mathrm{t}}\exp(-(1-v)|\beta_{\mathrm{t}}|+v(\|g\|_{\rm TV}+\|g\|_{\infty}))\leq\frac{\exp(v(\|g\|_{\rm TV}+\|g\|_{\infty}))}{(1-v)^{2}},

(B.19)

where we used
x^{2}\exp(-(1-v)x)\leq 1/(1-v)^{2}
.
Combining (
B.19
) with
q^{\prime\prime}(c_{i})\leq 1/4
from (

\displaystyle\frac{v^{2}(|\beta_{\mathrm{t}}|+\|g\|_{\rm TV}+\|g\|_{\infty})^{2}}{2}q^{\prime\prime}(c_{i})

\displaystyle\leq v^{2}\Big(\frac{(\|g\|_{\rm TV}+\|g\|_{\infty})^{2}}{4}+\frac{\exp(v(\|g\|_{\rm TV}+\|g\|_{\infty}))}{(1-v)^{2}}\Big).

Now we aim to bound the first term in (

Substituting (
B.15
) and using the identity (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{t}}\int(y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}-y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}(i,j,s))q^{\prime}(y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}})d\Pi(j,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{v}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{t}}\int y_{i}(\beta_{\mathrm{t}}+\beta(g)\mathbf{1}(x_{ij}>s))q^{\prime}(y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}})d\Pi(j,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{v}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{t}}(y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}-y_{i}g(\mathbf{x}_{i}))q^{\prime}(y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}})

\displaystyle\frac{v}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{t}}(y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}-y_{i}g(\mathbf{x}_{i}))q^{\prime}(y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}})

\displaystyle=v(\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(g)).

Proof of Lemma
4.1
for C4.5.

In Lemma
B.1
, choose

v=\frac{2(\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(g))}{(\|g\|_{\rm TV}+\|g\|_{\infty})^{2}+8},

which is non-negative by the assumption that

B.3.1
Oracle inequality for CART

Proof of Theorem
4.3
for CART.

We first assume that
|y_{i}|\leq U
,
i=1,2,\dots,N
, for some
U\geq 0
.
Write
\|\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})-g^{*}\|^{2}=E_{1}+E_{2}
, where

E_{1}\coloneqq\|\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})-g^{*}\|^{2}-2(\|y-\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})\|_{{\mathcal{D}}}^{2}-\|y-g^{*}\|_{{\mathcal{D}}}^{2})-\alpha-\beta

(B.27)

and

E_{2}\coloneqq 2(\|y-\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{D}}}-\|y-g^{*}\|_{{\mathcal{D}}}^{2})+\alpha+\beta,

and
\alpha
and
\beta
are positive constants to be chosen later.
Notice that by Theorem
4.2
, we have

E_{2}\leq 2(\|y-g\|_{{\mathcal{D}}}^{2}-\|y-g^{*}\|_{{\mathcal{D}}}^{2})+\frac{2\|g\|^{2}_{\rm TV}}{K+3}+\alpha+\beta,

(B.28)

for any
g(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{G}}^{1}
.
Taking expectations on both sides of (
B.28
) and using
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(\|y-g\|_{{\mathcal{D}}}^{2}-\|y-g^{*}\|_{{\mathcal{D}}}^{2})=\|g-g^{*}\|^{2}

yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(E_{2})

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(\|y-g\|_{{\mathcal{D}}}^{2}-\|y-g^{*}\|_{{\mathcal{D}}}^{2})+\frac{2\|g\|^{2}_{\rm TV}}{K+3}+\alpha+\beta

(B.29)

\displaystyle=2\|g-g^{*}\|^{2}+\frac{2\|g\|^{2}_{\rm TV}}{K+3}+\alpha+\beta.

To bound
E_{1}
, we first introduce a few useful concepts and definitions due to
Nobel 1996
for studying data-dependent partitions.
Let

\Pi_{N}\coloneqq\{{\mathcal{P}}(\{(\mathbf{x}_{1},y_{1}),(\mathbf{x}_{2},y_{2}),\dots,(\mathbf{x}_{N},y_{N})\}):(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}\}

(B.30)

be the family of all achievable partitions
{\mathcal{P}}
by growing a depth
K
binary tree on
N
points (in particular, note that
\Pi_{N}
contains all data-dependent partitions).
Define

M(\Pi_{N})\coloneqq\max\{\#{\mathcal{P}}:{\mathcal{P}}\in\Pi_{N}\}

to be the maximum number of terminal nodes among all partitions in
\Pi_{N}
. Note that
M(\Pi_{N})\leq 2^{K}
.
Given a set
\mathbf{z}^{N}=\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{N}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we also define
\Delta(\mathbf{z}^{N},\Pi_{N})
to be the number of distinct partitions of
\mathbf{z}^{N}
induced by elements of
\Pi_{N}
, that is, the number of different partitions
\{\mathbf{z}^{N}\cap A:A\in{\mathcal{P}}\}
, for
{\mathcal{P}}\in\Pi_{N}
. The partitioning number
\Delta_{N}(\Pi_{N})
is defined by

\Delta_{N}(\Pi_{N})\coloneqq\max\{\Delta(\mathbf{z}^{N},\Pi_{N}):\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\},

i.e., the maximum number of different partitions of any
N
point set that can be induced by members of
\Pi_{N}
.
Finally, let
{\mathcal{G}}_{N}
denote the collection of all piece-wise constant functions (bounded by
U
) on partitions
{\mathcal{P}}\in\Pi_{N}
.

Now, by
(
Györfi et al. 2002
, Theorem 11.4)
(choosing, in their notation,
\epsilon=1/2
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(E_{1}\geq 0)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{{\mathcal{D}}}\big(\exists\;g(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{G}}_{N}:\|g-g^{*}\|^{2}\geq 2(\|y-g\|_{{\mathcal{D}}}^{2}-\|y-g^{*}\|_{{\mathcal{D}}}^{2})+\alpha+\beta\big)

(B.31)

\displaystyle\leq 14\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{N}}{\mathcal{N}}\Big(\frac{\beta}{40U},{\mathcal{G}}_{N},\mathscr{L}_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}^{N}})\Big)\exp\Big(-\frac{\alpha N}{2568U^{4}}\Big),

where
\mathbf{x}^{N}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{N}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
{\mathcal{N}}(r,{\mathcal{G}}_{N},\mathscr{L}_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}^{N}}))
is the covering number for
{\mathcal{G}}_{N}
by balls of radius
r>0
in
\mathscr{L}_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}^{N}})
with respect to the empirical discrete measure
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}^{N}}
on
\mathbf{x}^{N}
.

Next, we use
(
Györfi et al. 2002
, Lemma 13.1 and Theorem 9.4)
as in the proof of
(
Györfi et al. 2002
, Theorem 13.1)
with
\epsilon=(32/40)\beta

to bound the empirical covering number by

{\mathcal{N}}\Big(\frac{\beta}{40U},{\mathcal{G}}_{N},\mathscr{L}_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}^{N}})\Big)\leq\Delta_{N}(\Pi_{N})\Big(\frac{40}{32}\cdot\frac{333eU^{2}}{\beta}\Big)^{2M(\Pi_{N})}\leq\Delta_{N}(\Pi_{N})\Big(\frac{417eU^{2}}{\beta}\Big)^{2^{K+1}}.

(B.32)

We further bound (
B.32
) by noting that

\Delta_{N}(\Pi_{N})\leq((N-1)p)^{2^{K}-1}\leq(Np)^{2^{K}}.

(B.33)

To see this,
let
\delta_{K}\coloneqq\Delta_{N}(\Pi_{N})
to emphasize the dependence on the depth
K
. Now, for each depth
K
tree constructed from a set of
N
points in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, there are at most
(N-1)p
possible split points at the root node. The left and right (sub)trees resulting from this split have depth
K-1
, and hence they each independently contribute to the count in
\delta_{K-1}
. Thus,
\delta_{K}
satisfies the recursion
\delta_{K}\leq((N-1)p)\cdot\delta_{K-1}\cdot\delta_{K-1}=((N-1)p)\delta^{2}_{K-1}
with
\delta_{1}\leq(N-1)p
. It can easily be shown that the solution is
\delta_{K}\leq((N-1)p)^{2^{K}-1}
for
K\geq 1
. (The authors were inspired by
Scornet et al. 2015
for this estimate of
\Delta_{N}(\Pi_{N})
.)
We therefore can bound the covering number by

{\mathcal{N}}\Big(\frac{\beta}{40U},{\mathcal{G}}_{N},\mathscr{L}_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}^{N}})\Big)\leq(Np)^{2^{K}}\Big(\frac{417eU^{2}}{\beta}\Big)^{2^{K+1}}.

(B.34)

Returning to (
B.31
) and applying (
B.34
)
to bound the covering number, since
\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{G}}_{N}
, we thus have

\mathbb{P}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(E_{1}\geq 0)\leq 14(Np)^{2^{K}}\Big(\frac{417eU^{2}}{\beta}\Big)^{2^{K+1}}\exp\Big(-\frac{\alpha N}{2568U^{4}}\Big).

We choose
\alpha\asymp\frac{U^{4}2^{K}\log(Np)}{N}
and
\beta\asymp\frac{U^{2}}{N}

so that
\mathbb{P}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(E_{1}\geq 0)\leq C_{1}^{\prime}/N
for come universal constant
C_{1}^{\prime}
. Furthermore, since
E_{1}\leq\|\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})-g^{*}\|^{2}+2\|y-g^{*}\|_{{\mathcal{D}}}^{2}\leq 12U^{2}
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(E_{1})\leq 12U^{2}\mathbb{P}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(E_{1}\geq 0)\leq 12C_{1}^{\prime}U^{2}/N
.

Adding this bound on
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(E_{1})
to the bound on
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(E_{2})
from (
B.29
) and plugging in the choices of
\alpha
and
\beta
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(\|\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})-g^{*}\|^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(E_{1})+\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(E_{2})

(B.35)

\displaystyle\leq 2\|g-g^{*}\|^{2}+\frac{2\|g\|^{2}_{\rm TV}}{K+3}+C_{1}^{\prime\prime}\Big(\frac{U^{4}2^{K}\log(Np)}{N}+\frac{U^{2}}{N}\Big),

where
C_{1}^{\prime\prime}
is a positive universal constant.

Next, we consider the case when the response data is not bounded.
Let
E=\bigcap_{i}\{|y_{i}|\leq U\}
, where
U\geq\|g^{*}\|_{\infty}
.
Using (
B.35
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(\|\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})-g^{*}\|^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(\|\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})-g^{*}\|^{2}\mathbf{1}(E))+\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(\|\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})-g^{*}\|^{2}\mathbf{1}(E^{c}))

(B.36)

\displaystyle\leq 2\|g-g^{*}\|^{2}+\frac{2\|g\|^{2}_{\rm TV}}{K+3}+C_{1}^{\prime\prime}\Big(\frac{U^{4}2^{K}\log(Np)}{N}+\frac{U^{2}}{N}\Big)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(\|\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})-g^{*}\|^{2}\mathbf{1}(E^{c})).

By a union bound, we have

\mathbb{P}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(E^{c})\leq N\mathbb{P}(|y|>U)\leq N\mathbb{P}(|\varepsilon|>U-\|g^{*}\|_{\infty})\leq 2N\exp\Big(-\frac{(U-\|g^{*}\|_{\infty})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big),

(B.37)

per the noise condition (
24
).
Next, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and (
B.37
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(\|\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})-g^{*}\|^{2}\mathbf{1}(E^{c}))

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(\|\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})-g^{*}\|^{4})\mathbb{P}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(E^{c})}

(B.38)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{32N(\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(\|\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})\|^{4})+\|g^{*}\|^{4}_{\infty})}\exp\Big(-\frac{(U-\|g^{*}\|_{\infty})^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}\Big).

Notice that
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(\|\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})\|^{4})\leq\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(\max_{i}|y_{i}|^{4})\leq N\mathbb{E}(|y|^{4})\leq 8N(\|g^{*}\|^{4}_{\infty}+\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon^{4}))
.
Choosing
U=\|g^{*}\|_{\infty}+2\sigma\sqrt{2\log(N)}
yields that
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(\|\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})-g^{*}\|^{2}\mathbf{1}(E^{c}))\leq C^{\prime\prime\prime}_{1}/N
, for some positive constant
C^{\prime\prime\prime}_{1}
that depends only on
\|g^{*}\|_{\infty}
and
\sigma^{2}
. Plugging this choice of
U
into (
B.38
) and then using the risk bound (
B.36
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(\|\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})-g^{*}\|^{2})\leq 2\|g-g^{*}\|^{2}+\frac{2\|g\|^{2}_{\rm TV}}{K+3}+C_{1}\frac{2^{K}\log^{2}(N)\log(Np)}{N},

for some constant
C_{1}>0
that depends only on
\|g^{*}\|_{\infty}
and
\sigma^{2}
.
∎

B.3.2
Oracle inequality for C4.5

We prove Theorem
4.3
for C4.5 in two steps: we first relate the mis-classification risk to the logistic risk, and then use an empirical process argument to relate the logistic risk to the empirical risk.

Recalling (
17
),
the tree output
\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})(\cdot)
will be
\pm\infty
for homogenous nodes
\mathrm{t}
, i.e., where
\eta_{\mathrm{t}}=0
or
\eta_{\mathrm{t}}=1
, which means the logistic risk
{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))
could be infinite.
To circumvent this problem, we
define the
truncated
output of a
C4.5 classification tree as

\tilde{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\log\big(\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{t}}}{1-\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{t}}}\big),\quad\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{t}}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\eta_{\mathrm{t}},&\text{if}\;\eta_{\mathrm{t}}\neq 0,1\\
\frac{1}{N+2},&\text{if}\;\eta_{\mathrm{t}}=0\\
\frac{N+1}{N+2},&\text{if}\;\eta_{\mathrm{t}}=1\end{cases},\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{t}.

(B.39)

The following lemma relates the excess mis-classification risk to the excess logistic risk for the truncated output of the tree.

Lemma B.2
.

We have

\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}({\rm Err}(\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))-{\rm Err}(g^{*})\leq\sqrt{2(\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}({\mathcal{R}}(\tilde{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))-{\mathcal{R}}(g^{*}))}.

(B.40)

Proof.

First notice that the truncated output of the tree makes the same class prediction as the original tree, so we have

{\rm Err}(\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))={\rm Err}(\tilde{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})).

Define the estimated conditional class probability of the truncated output of the tree as

\tilde{\eta}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\tilde{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})(\mathbf{x}))}.

(B.41)

Now we first use
(
Györfi et al. 2002
, Theorem 1.1)
for plug-in classifiers,
to see that

{\rm Err}(\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))-{\rm Err}(g^{*})={\rm Err}(\tilde{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))-{\rm Err}(g^{*})\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}|\tilde{\eta}(\mathbf{x})-\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})|

(B.42)

We then use Pinsker’s inequality
(
Tsybakov 2009
, Lemma 2.5)

to show that, for each
\mathbf{x}
,

|\tilde{\eta}(\mathbf{x})-\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})|\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}D(\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})\;\|\;\tilde{\eta}(\mathbf{x}))},

where
D(p\;\|\;q)\coloneqq p\log(p/q)+(1-p)\log((1-p)/(1-q))
is the
binary relative entropy
.
Taking expectations and using Jensen’s inequality, we get

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}|\tilde{\eta}(\mathbf{x})-\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})|\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}D(\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})\;\|\;\tilde{\eta}(\mathbf{x}))}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}D(\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})\;\|\;\tilde{\eta}(\mathbf{x}))}.

(B.43)

Using
\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{P}(y=1\mid\mathbf{x})=1/(1+\exp(-g^{*}(\mathbf{x})))
in conjunction with (
B.41
) we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}D(\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})\;\|\;\tilde{\eta}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\Big(\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})\log\frac{1}{\tilde{\eta}(\mathbf{x})}+(1-\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x}))\log\frac{1}{1-\tilde{\eta}(\mathbf{x})}\Big)

(B.44)

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\Big(\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})\log\frac{1}{\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})}+(1-\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x}))\log\frac{1}{1-\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)}\log(1+\exp(-y\tilde{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))-\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)}\log(1+\exp(-yg^{*}))

\displaystyle={\mathcal{R}}(\tilde{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))-{\mathcal{R}}(g^{*}).

Combining (
B.42
), (
B.43
), and (
B.44
) shows that

{\rm Err}(\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))-{\rm Err}(g^{*})\leq\sqrt{2({\mathcal{R}}(\tilde{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))-{\mathcal{R}}(g^{*}))}.

(B.45)

Taking expectations of both sides of (
B.45
)
and using Jensen’s inequality
on the right hand side completes the proof. ∎

For the second step, we use an empirical process argument to argue that the empirical risk of the truncated output of the tree concentrates near the logistic risk of the truncated output of the tree.

Lemma B.3
.

For any
\delta>0
,
we have

\mathbb{P}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(|\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\tilde{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))-{\mathcal{R}}(\tilde{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))|>\delta)\geq 1-8(N^{3}p)^{2^{K}}\exp(-N\delta^{2}/(128\log^{2}(N+2))).

Proof.

We use concepts that were introduced in the proof of Theorem
4.3
for CART in Supplement
B.3.1
.
Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{N}
denote the collection of all piece-wise constant functions on a partition
{\mathcal{P}}\in\Pi_{N}
(
\Pi_{N}
is defined in (
B.30
)), where each of the constants has the form
\log(a/(b-a))
, with
b>a
and
a,b\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}
.
Let
{\mathcal{H}}
denote the set of all mappings
h(\mathbf{z})\coloneqq\log(1+\exp(-y\tilde{g}(\mathbf{x}))
, where
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{x},y)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\{-1,1\}
and
\tilde{g}(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{G}}_{N}
.
We have

\mathbb{P}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(|\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\tilde{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))-{\mathcal{R}}(\tilde{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))|>\delta)\leq\mathbb{P}_{{\mathcal{D}}}\Big(\sup_{h(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{H}}}\Big|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}h(\mathbf{z}_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}h(\mathbf{z})\Big|>\delta\Big).

Since
h(\mathbf{z})\in[0,\log(N+2)]
for all
\mathbf{z}
, by
(
Györfi et al. 2002
, Theorem 9.1)
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\mathcal{D}}}\Big(\sup_{h(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{H}}}\Big|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}h(\mathbf{z}_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}h(\mathbf{z})\Big|>\delta\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 8\sup_{\mathbf{z}^{N}}{\mathcal{N}}(\delta/8,{\mathcal{H}},\mathscr{L}_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{z}^{N}}))\exp\Big(-\frac{N\delta^{2}}{128\log^{2}(N+2)}\Big).

Next, we show that, for all
\delta\geq 0
,

{\mathcal{N}}(\delta,{\mathcal{H}},\mathscr{L}_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{z}^{N}}))\leq(N^{3}p)^{2^{K}}.

(B.46)

Fix
\mathbf{z}^{N}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{1},y_{1}),(\mathbf{x}_{2},y_{2}),\dots,(\mathbf{x}_{N},y_{N})\}=\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{N}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\{-1,1\}
.
We aim to bound the cardinality
of the set

\{h(\mathbf{z}):\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{z}^{N},\;h(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{H}}\}=\{\log(1+\exp(-y\tilde{g}(\mathbf{x}_{i}))):(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbf{z}^{N},\;\tilde{g}(\cdot)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{N}\}.

(B.47)

To this end, note that a given
{\mathcal{P}}\in\Pi_{N}
induces a partition
of
\mathbf{x}^{N}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{N}\}
consisting of sets
B=\mathbf{x}^{N}\cap A
, where
A
is an element of
{\mathcal{P}}
.
For all
\mathbf{x}\in B
, there are at most
N^{2}
possible outputs
\log(a/(b-a))
for a piece-wise constant function
\tilde{g}(\cdot)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{N}
on
{\mathcal{P}}
.
Since there are at most
M(\Pi_{N})\leq 2^{K}
sets
B
, the number of possible outputs at the points
\mathbf{x}^{N}

is at most
(N^{2})^{2^{K}}
.
Any other
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}\in\Pi_{N}
that induces the same partition of
\mathbf{x}^{N}
as
{\mathcal{P}}
will result in the same set of possible outputs for
\tilde{g}(\cdot)
at
\mathbf{x}^{N}
.
Since are at most
\Delta_{N}(\Pi_{N})\leq(p(N-1))^{2^{K}-1}
induced partitions
\{\mathbf{x}^{N}\cap A:A\in{\mathcal{P}}\}
(see (
B.33
)), the cardinality of (
B.47
)
is at most
(p(N-1))^{2^{K}-1}(N^{2})^{2^{K}}\leq(N^{3}p)^{2^{K}}
.
Since the cardinality of the set (
B.47
) upper bounds
{\mathcal{N}}(\delta,{\mathcal{H}},\mathscr{L}_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{z}^{N}}))
for all
\delta\geq 0
, we have established (
B.46
) and hence Lemma
B.3
as well.
∎

Now we are ready to complete the proof of Theorem
4.3
.

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

In light of Lemma
B.2
, it suffices to upper bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}({\mathcal{R}}(\tilde{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))-{\mathcal{R}}(g^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}({\mathcal{R}}(\tilde{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\tilde{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))}_{\text{(I)}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\tilde{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))}_{\text{(II)}}

(B.48)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))-{\mathcal{R}}(g)}_{\text{(III)}}+\underbrace{{\mathcal{R}}(g)-{\mathcal{R}}(g^{*})}_{\text{(IV)}}.

We now separately bound the four terms in (
B.48
).
By Lemma
B.3
,
for any
\delta>0
we have

\text{(I)}\leq\delta+8(N^{3}p)^{2^{K}}\exp\Big(-\frac{N\delta^{2}}{128\log^{2}(N+2)}\Big)\log(N+2),

so setting

\delta^{2}\asymp\frac{2^{K}\log^{2}(N)\log(Np)}{N}

shows that

\text{(I)}\leq C_{2}^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{2^{K}\log^{2}(N)\log(Np)}{N}},

(B.49)

for some positive universal constant
C_{2}^{\prime}
.
For the second term, notice that (
B.39
) says that
\tilde{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})
only differs from
\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})
when
\eta_{\mathrm{t}}=0
or
\eta_{\mathrm{t}}=1
, so we have

\text{(II)}\leq\log(1+\exp(-\log(N+1)))\leq\frac{1}{N+1}.

(B.50)

Theorem
4.2
allows us to bound the third term by

\text{(III)}=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(g))\leq\frac{(\|g\|_{\rm TV}+\|g\|_{\infty}+3)^{2}}{K+3}.

(B.51)

Finally for the last term, we use a second order Taylor expansion of the logistic function (
A.10
) to see that

\displaystyle\text{(IV)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)}(q(yg(\mathbf{x}))-q(yg^{*}(\mathbf{x})))

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)}\Big(y(g(\mathbf{x})-g^{*}(\mathbf{x}))q^{\prime}(yg^{*}(\mathbf{x}))+\frac{(g(\mathbf{x})-g^{*}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}}{8}\Big),

where we used a uniform bound on the second derivative (
A.9
). But notice that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)}(y(g(\mathbf{x})-g^{*}(\mathbf{x}))q^{\prime}(yg^{*}(\mathbf{x})))

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\Big(-\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})(g(\mathbf{x})-g^{*}(\mathbf{x}))\frac{e^{-g^{*}(\mathbf{x})}}{1+e^{-g^{*}(\mathbf{x})}}+(1-\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x}))(g(\mathbf{x})-g^{*}(\mathbf{x}))\frac{e^{g^{*}(\mathbf{x})}}{1+e^{g^{*}(\mathbf{x})}}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad=0,

where in the last line we used
\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})=1/(1+\exp(-g^{*}(\mathbf{x})))
.
Thus we have shown that

\text{(IV)}\leq\frac{\|g-g^{*}\|^{2}}{8}.

(B.52)

Combining (
B.48
), (
B.49
), (
B.50
), (
B.51
), (
B.52
) and Lemma
B.2
shows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}({\rm Err}(\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))-{\rm Err}(g^{*})

(B.53)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{\|g-g^{*}\|^{2}}{4}+\frac{2(\|g\|_{{\rm TV}}+\|g\|_{\infty}+3)^{2}}{K+3}+\frac{2}{N+1}+2C_{2}^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{2^{K}\log^{2}(N)\log(Np)}{N}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|g-g^{*}\|+2\frac{\|g\|_{{\rm TV}}+\|g\|_{\infty}+3}{\sqrt{K+3}}+C_{2}\Big(\frac{2^{K}\log^{2}(N)\log(Np)}{N}\Big)^{1/4},

where
C_{2}
is a positive universal constant.
∎

B.4
Consistency for unbounded variation component functions

Proof of Corollary
4.5
.

By
(
Stein and Shakarchi 2005
, Theorem 4.3)
, there exists a sequence of integrable step functions
(s_{jN}(\cdot))_{N}
that converges pointwise almost surely to
g_{j}(\cdot)
. (The proof of
(
Stein and Shakarchi 2005
, Theorem 4.3)
extends to any Borel probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.) By expanding the sequence to include successive duplicate elements, we can assume without loss of generality that
\|s_{jN}\|_{\rm TV}=o(\sqrt{K_{N}})
.
Next, define
s_{N}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{p_{N}}s_{jN}(x_{j})
so that by the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem and the fact that
\sup_{N}\|g_{N}^{*}\|_{\ell_{0}}<\infty
, we have
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\|s_{N}-g^{*}_{N}\|=0
. Furthermore, since each component
s_{jN}(\cdot)
is a step function with total variation
o(\sqrt{K_{N}})
, we likewise have
\|s_{N}\|_{\rm TV}=o(\sqrt{K_{N}})
, where we again used the assumption that
\sup_{N}\|g_{N}^{*}\|_{\ell_{0}}<\infty
. Thus, by Theorem
4.3
and the hypotheses of Corollary
4.4
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}(\|\hat{g}(T_{K_{N}}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})-g^{*}_{N}\|^{2})\leq 2\|s_{N}-g_{N}^{*}\|^{2}+\frac{2\|s_{N}\|^{2}_{\rm TV}}{K_{N}+3}+2C_{1}\frac{2^{K_{N}}\log^{2}(N)\log(Np_{N})}{N}\rightarrow 0,

as
N\rightarrow\infty
. Similarly, it can be shown that

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}(\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}}({\rm Err}(\hat{g}(T_{K_{N}}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))-{\rm Err}(g^{*}_{N}))\to 0,\quad\text{as}\quad N\rightarrow\infty.\qed

Information gain for C4.5 with large margin

\int\text{sgn}(Df_{j}(s))\mathbf{1}(x_{ij}>s)d\Pi(j,s)=\frac{f^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{i})-f^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{(1)})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{p}\int|Df_{j^{\prime}}(s^{\prime})|ds^{\prime}}

(C.1)

and

.
Before proving Lemma

.

Lemma C.1
.

For any
v\in[0,1]
and any terminal node
\mathrm{t}
of
T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})\geq 2v(\gamma-ve^{2v})\eta_{\mathrm{t}}(1-\eta_{\mathrm{t}}).

Proof.

We slightly modify the definition of the decision stump in (
B.15
).
Let

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathrm{t}}(i,j,s)=\beta_{\mathrm{t}}+\beta(f^{*})\mathbf{1}(x_{ij}>s),

\displaystyle\beta(f^{*})=v\Big(\text{sgn}(Df_{j}(s))\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{p}\int|Df_{j^{\prime}}(s^{\prime})|ds^{\prime}+f^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{(1)})\Big).

By (
C.2
) and Assumption
1
, we have

|\beta(f^{*})|\leq v\Big(\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{p}\int|Df_{j^{\prime}}(s^{\prime})|ds^{\prime}+|f^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{(1)})|\Big)\leq v(1+\|f^{*}\|_{\infty})\leq 2v.

(C.3)

Similar to (
B.17
) and (
B.18
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{t}}\int(q(y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}})-q(y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}(i,j,s)))d\Pi(j,s)

(C.4)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{t}}\Big(\int y_{i}\beta(f^{*})\mathbf{1}(x_{ij}>s)q^{\prime}(y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}})d\Pi(j,s)+2v^{2}q^{\prime\prime}(c_{i})\Big),

where
c_{i}
is between
y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}
and
y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}(i,j,s)

and we used the fact that
|y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}-y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}(i,j,s)|=|\beta(f^{*})|\leq 2v
from (
C.3
).
Using our expression (
A.5
) for
\beta_{\mathrm{t}}
, we can simplify both the first and second derivatives.
For the second derivative, we have the uniform bound

q^{\prime\prime}(c_{i})=\frac{e^{-c_{i}}}{(1+e^{-c_{i}})^{2}}\leq e^{2v}\frac{e^{-y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}}}{(1+e^{-y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}})^{2}}=e^{2v}\eta_{\mathrm{t}}(1-\eta_{\mathrm{t}}).

(C.5)

The first derivative also simplifies nicely:

-q^{\prime}(y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}})=\frac{e^{-y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}}}{1+e^{-y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}}}}=\begin{cases}1-\eta_{\mathrm{t}}&\text{if }y_{i}=1\\
\eta_{\mathrm{t}}&\text{if }y_{i}=-1.\\
\end{cases}

Thus, we split the summation into two cases depending on whether
y_{i}=1
or
y_{i}=-1
:

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\sum_{x_{i}\in\mathrm{t}\atop y_{i}=1}\int y_{i}\beta(f^{*})\mathbf{1}(x_{ij}>s)q^{\prime}(y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}})d\Pi(j,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\eta_{\mathrm{t}}}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{t}\atop y_{i}=1}vy_{i}f^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{i})

(C.6)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1-\eta_{\mathrm{t}}}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{t}\atop y_{i}=1}v\gamma

\displaystyle=v\gamma(1-\eta_{\mathrm{t}})\eta_{\mathrm{t}},

and similarly

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\sum_{x_{i}\in\mathrm{t}\atop y_{i}=-1}\int y_{i}\beta(f^{*})\mathbf{1}(x_{ij}>s)q^{\prime}(y_{i}\beta_{\mathrm{t}})d\Pi(j,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{t}}}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{t}\atop y_{i}=-1}vy_{i}f^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{i})

(C.7)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{t}}}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{t}\atop y_{i}=-1}v\gamma

\displaystyle=v\gamma(1-\eta_{\mathrm{t}})\eta_{\mathrm{t}}.

Substituting (
C.5
), (
C.6
), and (
C.7
) into (
C.4
) completes the proof.
∎

Now we are ready to complete the proof of Lemma
5.1
.

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

Choose
v=\gamma/4
in Lemma
C.1
to see that

\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})\geq\Big(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}-\frac{\gamma^{2}\exp({\frac{\gamma}{2}})}{8}\Big)\eta_{\mathrm{t}}(1-\eta_{\mathrm{t}})\geq\frac{\gamma^{2}(4-\sqrt{e})}{8}\eta_{\mathrm{t}}(1-\eta_{\mathrm{t}}),

(C.8)

since
\gamma\leq 1
.
Recalling (
A.6
),
we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{t}}(1-\eta_{\mathrm{t}})\log(1/\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))}{\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{t}}(1-\eta_{\mathrm{t}})\log(\eta_{\mathrm{t}}\log(\frac{1}{\eta_{\mathrm{t}}})+(1-\eta_{\mathrm{t}})\log(\frac{1}{1-\eta_{\mathrm{t}}}))}{\eta_{\mathrm{t}}\log(\eta_{\mathrm{t}})+(1-\eta_{\mathrm{t}})\log(1-\eta_{\mathrm{t}})},

which is a convex function
with minimum at
\eta_{\mathrm{t}}=1/2
; hence

\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{t}}(1-\eta_{\mathrm{t}})\log(1/\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))}{\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))}\geq\frac{\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot\log(\log(2))}{\log(1/2)}\geq\frac{1}{8}.

(C.9)

Combining (
C.8
) and (
C.9
)
yields

\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})\geq\frac{\gamma^{2}(4-\sqrt{e})}{64}\cdot\frac{\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))}{\log(1/\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))}\geq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{30}\cdot\frac{\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))}{\log(1/\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))}.\qed

C.2
Empirical risk for C4.5 with large margin

As with the proof of Lemma
D.1
, we prove Theorem
5.2
using Lemma
5.1
.

Proof of Theorem
5.2
.

Let
A\coloneqq\gamma^{2}/30
be the constant in Lemma
5.1
.
Plugging Lemma
5.1

into (
20
) we see that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))-A\sum_{\mathrm{t}\in T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}}\frac{N_{\mathrm{t}}}{N}\frac{\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))}{\log(1/\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))}

(C.10)

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))-A\frac{\sum_{\mathrm{t}\in T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}}\frac{N_{\mathrm{t}}}{N}\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))}{\log(1/\sum_{\mathrm{t}\in T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}}\frac{N_{\mathrm{t}}}{N}\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))\Big(1-\frac{A}{\log(1/\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}})))}\Big),

where
the second inequality comes from Jensen’s inequality and the fact that
x\mapsto x/\log(x)
is convex for
x\in[0,1)
.
Letting
{\mathcal{E}}_{K}=\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))
, we end up with the recursion

{\mathcal{E}}_{K}\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}\Big(1-\frac{A}{\log(1/{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1})}\Big),\quad K\geq 1.

(C.11)

We prove that the recursion (
C.11
) has solution

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{C4.5}}}))={\mathcal{E}}_{K}\leq\exp(-(AK)^{1/2}).

The base case follows since
R_{0}\leq\log(2)\leq 1
.
Next assume that for some
K\geq 1
that

{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}\leq\exp(-(A(K-1))^{1/2}).

(C.12)

First notice that if
{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}\leq\exp(-(AK)^{1/2})
, then by (

) we have that

{\mathcal{E}}_{K}\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}\leq\exp(-(AK)^{1/2}),

so our induction step is complete. Thus we may assume that

{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}\geq\exp(-(AK)^{1/2}).

(C.13)

By (
C.13
) and (
C.12
), we have that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{K}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}\Big(1-\frac{A}{\log(1/{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1})}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-(A(K-1))^{1/2})\Big(1-\frac{A}{(AK)^{1/2}}\Big)

is upper bounded by

which completes the induction step.

Appendix D
Models with interactions

We prove a generalization of the empirical risk bound in Theorem
4.2
to the setting in Section
6
where
{\mathcal{G}}
may have interaction terms.

Lemma D.1
.

Let
g(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{G}}^{d}
and
K\geq d
. Assume that for any terminal node
\mathrm{t}
of the tree
T_{K-d}
such that
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-d}))>\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(g)
, we have

\mathcal{IG}_{d}(\mathrm{t})\geq\frac{(\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K-d}))-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(g))^{2}}{V^{2}(g)},

(D.1)

for some complexity constant
V(g)
that depends only on
g(\cdot)
. Then

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K}))\leq\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(g)+\frac{V(g)d}{K+2d+1}.

Proof of Lemma
D.1
.

Recall the notation

{\mathcal{E}}_{K}\coloneqq\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K}))-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(g),\quad{\mathcal{E}}_{K}(\mathrm{t})\coloneqq\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{g}(T_{K}))-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(g)

(D.2)

defined in (
22
) for the global and within-node excess empirical risks, respectively. It can be seen from (
20
) that the empirical risk decreases with the depth so that
{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\geq{\mathcal{E}}_{2}\geq\cdots\geq{\mathcal{E}}_{K}
. If
{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}<0
, then there is nothing to prove because
{\mathcal{E}}_{K}\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}<0
and hence
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{g}(T_{K}))<\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(g)
, as desired.
Therefore, we assume throughout that
{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}\geq 0
.

Substituting (
D.1
) into (
31
) and subtracting
\widehat{{\mathcal{R}}}(g)
from both sides, we see that

{\mathcal{E}}_{K}\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}-\frac{1}{V(g)}\sum_{\mathrm{t}\in T_{K-d}:{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}(\mathrm{t})>0}\frac{N_{\mathrm{t}}}{N}{\mathcal{E}}^{2}_{K-d}(\mathrm{t}).

(D.3)

We note that if a node
\mathrm{t}\in T_{K-d}
cannot be refined any further, corresponding to a case where the node contains a single data point or where all input values and/or all response values within the node are the same, then
{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}(\mathrm{t})\leq 0
.

By convexity of the square function and the fact that
\sum_{\mathrm{t}\in T_{K-d}:{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}(\mathrm{t})>0}\frac{N_{\mathrm{t}}}{N}\leq 1
, we have from Jensen’s inequality that

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathrm{t}\in T_{K-d}:{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}(\mathrm{t})>0}\frac{N_{\mathrm{t}}}{N}{\mathcal{E}}^{2}_{K-d}(\mathrm{t})

\displaystyle\geq\Big(\sum_{\mathrm{t}\in T_{K-d}:{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}(\mathrm{t})>0}\frac{N_{\mathrm{t}}}{N}{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}(\mathrm{t})\Big)^{2}

(D.4)

\displaystyle\geq\Big(\sum_{\mathrm{t}\in T_{K-d}}\frac{N_{\mathrm{t}}}{N}{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}(\mathrm{t})\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{E}}^{2}_{K-d},

where the last inequality comes from the fact that
{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}
is non-negative by assumption.
Applying (
D.4
) to (
D.3
), we obtain

{\mathcal{E}}_{K}\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}\Big(1-\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}}{V(g)}\Big),\quad K\geq d.

(D.5)

Now we prove that
{\mathcal{E}}_{K}\leq\frac{V(g)d}{K+2d+1}
for all
K\geq d
by induction.
The base case
K<2d
is established by noticing that

{\mathcal{E}}_{K}\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}\Big(1-\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}}{V(g)}\Big)\leq\frac{V(g)}{4}\leq\frac{V(g)d}{K+2d+1}.

For
K\geq 2d
, assume that
{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}\leq\frac{V(g)d}{K+d+1}
. Then either
{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}\leq\frac{V(g)d}{K+2d+1}
, in which case we are done since
{\mathcal{E}}_{K}\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}
, or
{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}>\frac{V(g)d}{K+2d+1}
, in which case

{\mathcal{E}}_{K}\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}\Big(1-\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{K-d}}{V(g)}\Big)\leq\frac{V(g)d}{K+d+1}\Big(1-\frac{d}{K+2d+1}\Big)=\frac{V(g)d}{K+2d+1}.\qed

Appendix E
Random forests

We prove the oracle inequality for random forests (Theorem
7.1
) given in Section
7
.

Proof of Theorem
7.1
.

The proof follows similar lines as Theorem
4.2
and Theorem
4.3
, but with some interesting twists due to the additional randomness in the trees.

Throughout the proof, we focus on a single random base regression tree
T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}}=T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}}(\Theta)
in the forest.
We let
{\mathcal{I}}^{\prime}\subset\{1,\ldots,N\}
denote the random indices corresponding to the subsampled data
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
from the original dataset
{\mathcal{D}}
.
Furthermore, we let
\Xi_{K}
denote the random variable whose law generates the subsets
{\mathcal{S}}
of candidate splitting variables at all internal nodes in
T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}}
, conditional on the subsampled training data
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
.
Notice that the pair of random variables
({\mathcal{I}}^{\prime},\Xi_{K})
has the same law as
\Theta
.

In order to prove Theorem
7.1
, it is necessary to first establish an empirical risk bound akin to Theorem
4.2
.
Let
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{t}}
denote the analogues of the empirical norms in (
A.1
) and (
A.2
), respectively, but based on the subsampled data
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
.
Following the notation in the proof of Theorem
4.2
, we let
{\mathcal{E}}_{K}
denote the excess empirical risk
\|y-\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})\|_{{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}}^{2}-\|y-g\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}}
.
Similarly, for a terminal node
\mathrm{t}
of
T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}}
and additive function
g(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{G}}^{1}
, we define
{\mathcal{E}}_{K}(\mathrm{t})
to be the within-node excess empirical risk
\|y-\overline{y}_{\mathrm{t}}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{t}}-\|y-g\|^{2}_{\mathrm{t}}
, but now based on the subsample
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
.

Because of the additional randomness injected into the trees, we proceed by bounding the empirical risk averaged with respect to
\Xi_{K}
; that is, we aim to bound
\mathbb{E}_{\Xi_{K}}(\|y-\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})\|_{{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}}^{2})
. Note that due to independence of the subsets
{\mathcal{S}}
across nodes, any terminal node
\mathrm{t}
of
T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}}

\Xi_{K}
given
\Xi_{K-1}
. Thus, we study the
\Xi_{K}
randomness of the tree at depth
K
conditional on the randomness in the previous
K-1
levels of the tree.
As with the analysis for Theorem
4.2
, we begin with the identity

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Xi_{K}\mid\Xi_{K-1}}(\|y-\hat{g}(T_{K}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}})

(E.1)

\displaystyle=\|y-\hat{g}(T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}})\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}}-\sum_{\mathrm{t}\in T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}}}\frac{N_{\mathrm{t}}}{a_{N}}\mathbb{E}_{\Xi_{K}\mid\Xi_{K-1}}\big(\max_{(j\in{\mathcal{S}},\;s\in\mathbb{R})}\mathcal{IG}(j,s,\mathrm{t})\big),

which results from taking the expected value of (
20
) with respect to the conditional distribution of
\Xi_{K}
given
\Xi_{K-1}
(again, recognizing that the terminal nodes of
T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}}
are conditionally independent of
\Xi_{K}
given
\Xi_{K-1}
).

We follow a similar argument as the proof of Theorem
4.2
,
except now in lieu of Lemma
4.1
, one shows that, for each terminal node
\mathrm{t}
of
T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\Xi_{K}|\Xi_{K-1}}\big(\max_{(j\in{\mathcal{S}},\;s\in\mathbb{R})}\mathcal{IG}(j,s,\mathrm{t})\big)\geq\frac{m}{p}\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}^{2}(\mathrm{t})}{\|g\|^{2}_{\text{TV}}},

(E.2)

provided
{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}(\mathrm{t})>0
.
To see this, notice that we have the lower bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Xi_{K}\mid\Xi_{K-1}}\big(\max_{(j\in{\mathcal{S}},\;s\in\mathbb{R})}\mathcal{IG}(j,s,\mathrm{t})\big)\geq\mathbb{E}_{\Xi_{K}\mid\Xi_{K-1}}\big(\mathbf{1}(j_{\mathrm{t}}\in{\mathcal{S}})\mathcal{IG}(j_{\mathrm{t}},s_{\mathrm{t}},\mathrm{t})\big)\geq\frac{m}{p}\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t}),

where the last inequality follows from the fact that the probability that a specific coordinate index belongs to
{\mathcal{S}}
is
\binom{p-1}{m-1}/\binom{p}{m}=m/p
. The conclusion then follows directly from Lemma
4.1
since
\mathcal{IG}(\mathrm{t})\geq{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}^{2}(\mathrm{t})/\|g\|^{2}_{\text{TV}}
whenever
{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}(\mathrm{t})>0
.

Proceeding in the same way as before with the proof of Lemma
D.1
that produced (
D.5
), and applying (
E.2
) to (
E.1

\mathbb{E}_{\Xi_{K}|\Xi_{K-1}}({\mathcal{E}}_{K})\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}-\frac{m/p}{\|g\|^{2}_{\text{TV}}}({\mathcal{E}}^{+}_{K-1})^{2},

(E.4)

where
{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}^{+}=\sum_{\mathrm{t}\in T_{K-1}^{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{CART}}}:{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}(\mathrm{t})>0}\frac{N_{\mathrm{t}}}{a_{N}}{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}(\mathrm{t})\geq{\mathcal{E}}_{K-1}
.

\Xi_{K-1}

\mathbb{E}_{\Xi_{K-1}}(\mathbb{E}_{\Xi_{K}|\Xi_{K-1}}({\mathcal{E}}_{K}))=\mathbb{E}_{\Xi_{K}}({\mathcal{E}}_{K})
, and Jensen’s inequality for the square function, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{\Xi_{K-1}}(({\mathcal{E}}^{+}_{K-1})^{2})\geq(\mathbb{E}_{\Xi_{K-1}}({\mathcal{E}}^{+}_{K-1}))^{2}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Xi_{K}}({\mathcal{E}}_{K})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\Xi_{K-1}}({\mathcal{E}}_{K-1})-\frac{m/p}{\|g\|^{2}_{\text{TV}}}\mathbb{E}_{\Xi_{K-1}}(({\mathcal{E}}^{+}_{K-1})^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\Xi_{K-1}}({\mathcal{E}}_{K-1})-\frac{m/p}{\|g\|^{2}_{\text{TV}}}(\mathbb{E}_{\Xi_{K-1}}({\mathcal{E}}^{+}_{K-1}))^{2}.

Let us first assume that
\mathbb{E}_{\Xi_{K-1}}({\mathcal{E}}_{K-1})\geq 0
. Since
\mathbb{E}_{\Xi_{K-1}}({\mathcal{E}}^{+}_{K-1})\geq\mathbb{E}_{\Xi_{K-1}}({\mathcal{E}}_{K-1})\geq 0
(which follows from
