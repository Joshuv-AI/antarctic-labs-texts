A New Algorithm for Whitney Stratification of Varieties
Martin Helmer, Rafael Mohr

Abstract
. We describe a new algorithm to compute Whitney stratifications of real and complex algebraic varieties. This algorithm is a modification of the algorithm of Helmer and Nanda (HN) [ 7 , 9 ] , but is made more efficient by using techniques for equidimensional decomposition rather than computing the set of associated primes of a polynomial ideal at a key step in the HN algorithm. We note that this modified algorithm may fail to produce a minimal Whitney stratification even when the HN algorithm would produce a minimal stratification. We, additionally, present an algorithm to coarsen any Whitney stratification of a complex variety to a minimal Whitney stratification; the theoretical basis for our approach is a classical result of Teissier.

2.1.
Gröbner Bases

Let us briefly state the necessary definitions and properties of
Gröbner bases needed for our algorithm. Just for this section, let

\mathbb{K}
be any field and let
R:=\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{x}]
be the polynomial ring
over
\mathbb{K}
in a finite set of variables
\mathbf{x}
. Denote by

\mon(\mathbf{x})
the set of monomials in
\mathbf{x}
.

Definition 2.1

(Monomial Order)
.

A
monomial order

\prec
on
\mathbf{x}
is a total
order on
\mon(\mathbf{x})
which:

(1)

extends the partial order on
\mon(\mathbf{x})
given by divisibility, and

(2)

is compatible with multiplication i.e. we have,

\displaystyle u\prec v\;\Rightarrow\;wu\prec wv\quad\forall u,v,w\in\mon(\mathbf{x}).

A monomial order on
\mathbf{x}
yields a notion of
leading monomial

and
leading coefficient
in
R
.

Definition 2.2

(Leading Monomial)
.

Let
\prec
be a monomial order on
\mon(\mathbf{x})
. For a
nonzero element
f\in R
the
leading monomial
of
f
with respect to

\prec
, denoted
\lm_{\prec}(f)
, is the
\prec
-largest monomial
in the support of
f
. The
leading coefficient
, denoted

\lc_{\prec}(f)
, is the corresponding coefficient of

\lm_{\prec}(f)
in
f
. For a finite set
F
in
R
we define

\lm_{\prec}(F):=\{\lm_{\prec}(f)\;|\;f\in F\}
. For an ideal

I
in
R
we define the
leading monomial ideal
of
I
as

\lm_{\prec}(I):=\langle\lm_{\prec}(f)\;|\;f\in I\rangle
.

We finally define the notion of Gröbner bases.

Definition 2.3

(Gröbner Basis)
.

A
Gröbner basis
of an ideal
I\subset R
with respect to a
monomial order
\prec
is a finite set
G\subset I
such that

\langle\lm_{\prec}(G)\rangle=\lm_{\prec}(I)
. It is called
minimal
if
for any
g\in G
,
\lm_{\prec}(g)
is not divisible by any element in

\lm_{\prec}(G-\{g\})

Any Gröbner basis can be turned into a minimal one by
removing all elements whose leading monomials are divisible by another
leading monomial occuring in the Gröbner basis.

For our algorithm, we will need to compute Gröbner bases for

block orders
.

Definition 2.4

(Block Order)
.

Let
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
be two finite sets of variables. Write each
monomial
u\in\mon(\mathbf{x}\cup\mathbf{y})
uniquely as a product

u=u_{\mathbf{x}}u_{\mathbf{y}}
with
u_{\mathbf{x}}\in\mon(\mathbf{x})
and

u_{\mathbf{y}}\in\mon(\mathbf{y})
. Fix a monomial order
\prec_{1}
on

\mon(\mathbf{x})
and a monomial order
\prec_{2}
on
\mon(\mathbf{y})
. The
corresponding
block order eliminating
\mathbf{x}
is defined as
follows:
u\prec v
if and only if
u_{\mathbf{x}}\prec_{1}v_{\mathbf{x}}
or
u_{\mathbf{x}}=v_{\mathbf{x}}

and
u_{\mathbf{y}}\prec_{2}v_{\mathbf{y}}
for
u,v\in\mon(\mathbf{x}\cup\mathbf{y})
.

The key property of Gröbner bases with respect to block orders that we need is
the following (Lemmas 8.91 and 8.93 in
[
1
]
):

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
be any two finite sets of variables and let

\prec
be any block order eliminating
\mathbf{x}
. Let
G
be a Gröbner basis
of some ideal
I
with respect to
\prec
. Then

(1)

G
is also a Gröbner basis of the ideal
I\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{y})[\mathbf{x}]
.

(2)

Let
H
be the corresponding minimal Gröbner basis of
G
. Let

h\in\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{y}]
be the least common multiple of the leading
coefficients of
H
, regarded as a subset of

\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{y})[\mathbf{x}]
. Then

I\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{y})[\mathbf{x}]\cap\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}]=(I:h^{\infty}).

We will want to compute Gröbner bases of ideals of the form

I\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{y})[\mathbf{x}]
when
\mathbf{y}
is chosen such that the map

\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{y}]\rightarrow\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}]/I
is injective. To this end, define:

Definition 2.6

(Maximally Independent Subset)
.

A
maximally independent subset

\mathbf{u}\subset\mathbf{x}
of an
ideal
I\subset\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{x}]
is a set such that the map

\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{y}]\rightarrow\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}]/I
is injective and such that the
cardinality of
\mathbf{u}
is equal to
\dim(I)
.

Computationally, we will determine maximally independent subsets from
any Gröbner basis of
I
(Theorem 9.27 in
[
1
]
):

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
G
be any Gröbner basis of an ideal

I\subset\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{x}]
. Let
\mathbf{u}\subset\mathbf{x}
be maximal such that no leading
monomial of any
g\in G
depends only on
\mathbf{u}
. Then
\mathbf{u}
is a
maximally independent subset.

2.2.
Whitney Stratifications

Throughout the paper, let
\mathbb{C}^{n}
be the
n
-dimensional affine space
over the complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
,
\mathbb{P}^{n}
be the
n
-dimensional
projective space over
\mathbb{C}
with dual projective space

(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{*}
, and let
\mathbf{Gr}(k,n)
denote the projective variety of

k
-dimensional vector spaces in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We assume throughout the
rest of the paper that all varieties in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
are defined over

\mathbb{Q}
, i.e. cut out by
n
-variate polynomials with coefficients in

\mathbb{Q}
. For any variety
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
we will denote by
I(X)
the
radical ideal of all polynomials vanishing on
X
, whereas
I_{X}
will denote
any ideal with
\mathbf{V}(I_{X})=X
, and in particular if
X
is non-reduced (i.e. a scheme)
I_{X}
will denote the ideal defining the scheme
X
.

Our goal is to compute a Whitney stratification of a singular variety

X\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
, which is a certain flag on
X
where the
successive differences between varieties in the flag are smooth and where these smooth pieces satisfy an additional regularity condition. This additional regularity condition, called
Whitney’s Condition (B)
is designed to enforce that the local structure of each connected component of each smooth piece of the stratification is geometrically identical in a precise way.

Definition 2.8

(Whitney’s Condition (B))
.

Let
Y\subset X
be algebraic varieties in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with
\dim(Y)<\dim(X)
and with
X-Y
smooth. Let
Y_{\rm reg}
denote the smooth part of
Y
. The pair
(X,Y)

satisfies
Whitney’s condition (B)
if for any
p\in Y_{\rm reg}

•

and any sequences
(x_{i})\subset X
and
(y_{i})\subset Y_{\rm reg}
converging to
p
,

•

if the secant lines
\ell_{i}=[x_{i},y_{i}]\in\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
converge to some
\ell\in\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
,

•

and if the tangent spaces
T_{x_{i}}(X-Y)\in\mathbf{Gr}(\dim(X),n)
converge
to some
T\in\mathbf{Gr}(\dim(X),n)
,

then
\ell\subset T
.

This now allows to formally define
a Whitney stratification of a variety
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
, see e.g.
[
14
,
15
]
:

Definition 2.9

(Whitney Stratification)
.

A
Whitney stratification
of
X
is a flag

\emptyset=W_{-1}\subset W_{0}\subset\dots\subset W_{k-1}\subset W_{k}=X

where each difference
M_{i}:=W_{i}-W_{i-1}
is a smooth variety. The
connected components of each
M_{i}
are called
strata
and each
pair of strata must satisfy Whitney’s condition (B). Note that the condition that
M_{i}
is smooth in particular means that for each
W_{i}
we must have that the singular locus of
W_{i}
is contained in
W_{i-1}
, i.e.
(W_{i})_{\rm Sing}\subset W_{i-1}
.

A
minimal
Whitney stratification of
X
is a Whitney
stratification such that, after removing any stratum, the resulting
stratification fails to be a Whitney stratification. The existence of a unique minimal stratification follows from results of
[
13
]
, see also
[
4
]
.

In
[
7
]
an algebraic criterion based on primary decomposition
of polynomial ideals is given that allows to check computationally if
a given pair
X,Y\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
satisfies condition (B), and as such, to
compute a Whitney stratification of a given variety by succesively
computing singular loci and then checking condition (B) for all pairs
of resuling varieties. The central contribution of this present work
is to still apply the same algebraic criterion but to give a simple
way in which having to explicitly compute primary decompositions can
nonetheless be avoided, see
Section

3.1
.

Let us now fix an
equidimensional
, affine variety
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
, where
equidimensional means that all irreducible components of
X
have the
same dimension. We now introduce the aforementioned algebraic
criterion, which will use the notion of a
conormal space
.

Definition 2.10

(Conormal Space)
.

Denote by
X_{\text{reg}}
the set of regular points of
X
. The
conormal space

\mathbf{Con}(X)\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}\times(\mathbb{P}^{n-1})^{*}
of

X
is the Zariski closure of the set

\{(p,\zeta)\;|\;p\in X_{\text{reg}}\text{ and }T_{p}X_{\text{reg}}\subset\zeta\}.

The canonical projection
\kappa_{X}:\mathbf{Con}(X)\rightarrow X
is called the
conormal map
.

This notion was used in
[
7
]
to give the aforementioned
algebraic criterion for condition (B), which we restate below.

Theorem 2.11

(Theorem 4.3 in
[
7
]
)
.

Let
\emptyset\neq Y\subset X_{\text{sing}}
be equidimensional and let

I_{Y}
be any ideal with
\mathbf{V}(I_{Y})=Y
. Let

I_{Y}+I(\mathbf{Con}(X))=\bigcap_{i\in I}Q_{i}

be a primary decomposition. Let

J:=\{i\in I\;|\;\dim(\kappa_{X}(\mathbf{V}(Q_{i})))<\dim(Y)\}.

Further, let

A:=\left[\bigcup_{j\in J}\kappa_{X}(\mathbf{V}(Q_{i}))\right]\cup Y_{\text{sing}}.

Then the pair

(X_{\text{reg}},Y-A)
satisfies condition (B).

2.3.
Local Polar Varieties and Minimal Whitney Stratifications

While our new algebraic criterion for Whitney’s condition (B) avoids
the computation of primary decompositions, and thus speeds up the
stratification process, it may fail to produce a minimal Whitney
stratification even when the algorithm in
[
7
]
does. In order
to rectify this, we will use the concept of
local polar
varieties
which we introduce here.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a variety of dimension
d
and consider the
conormal space
\mathbf{Con}(X)\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
with associated conormal
map
\kappa_{X}:\mathbf{Con}(X)\to X
. For fixed
i
with
1\leq i\leq d-1
, consider a
dimension
d+i-1
linear space in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with dual

L_{i}\subset(\mathbb{P}^{n-1})^{*}
. We use the convention
L_{0}=\mathbb{C}^{n}\times(\mathbb{P}^{n-1})^{*}
.

Definition 2.12

(Polar Variety)
.

A
polar variety
is a variety of the form

\delta_{i}(X):=\kappa_{X}(\mathbf{Con}(X)\cap L_{i}).

Consider now a point
y\in X
. Picking our linear spaces as general
linear spaces through
y
with dual
\tilde{L}_{i}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
we define

Definition 2.13

(Local Polar Variety)
.

A
codimension
i
local polar variety through
y
is a variety
of the form

\delta_{i}(X,y):=\kappa_{X}(\mathbf{Con}(X)\cap\tilde{L}_{i}).

Since the conormal space is reduced (as a scheme), i.e. is a variety
[
4
, Proposition 2.9]
, and since the
L_{i}
(and the
\tilde{L}_{i}
) are general it follows that the local polar varieties are also reduced
[
4
, Remark 3.10 (b)]
and are hence are each defined by a radical ideal.

If our linear space
\tilde{L}_{i}
are chosen sufficiently generic,
then, using a dimension count, some of the local polar varities will
contain
y
and some will not (see
[
4
, Remark 3.1]
). We
will want to compute the
multiplicity
of points
y
in local
polar varieties
\delta_{i}(X,y)
. Let us define the notion of multiplicity
used here:

Definition 2.14

(Multiplicity)
.

Let
Z\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a subvariety and let
z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and write

\mathfrak{m}_{z}:=I(z)
. If
z\in Z
, the
multiplicity
of
Z
at

z
, denoted
m_{z}(Z)
, is defined as the Hilbert-Samuel multiplicity
(see e.g. Chapter 12 in
[
3
]
) of the local ring

(\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x}]/I(Z))_{\mathfrak{m}_{z}}
at
\mathfrak{m}_{z}
. If
z\notin Z
we define
m_{z}(Z):=0
.

For a subvariety
Y\subset X
and a given point

y\in Y
, computing multiplicities of the form
m_{y}(\delta_{i}(X,y))
can be
used to check if Whitney’s condition (B) holds for
X
and
Y
at
y
.
More precisely (see p. 69 and Proposition 3.6 in
[
4
]
):

Theorem 2.15
.

Let
Y\subset X
be a subvariety and
y\in Y
. Then the sequence

m_{\bullet}(X,x):=(m_{x}(X),m_{x}(\delta_{1}(X,x)),\dots,m_{x}(\delta_{\dim(X)-1}(X,x)))

is
independent of the linear subspaces chosen to construct the local
polar varieties if they are sufficiently general. In addition,
Whitney’s condition (B) is satisfied at
y
for
X
and
Y
if the
sequence takes the same value for every
x
in a euclidean
neighborhood of
y
in
Y
.

Making this theorem computationally profitable will require us to
compute sequences
m_{\bullet}(X,y)
where
y
lies in a subvariety

Y\subset X
. We will show how to do this in
Section

3.3
.

3.
The New Whitney Stratification Algorithm

In this section we present the main results of this work, namely a new algebraic condition to identify subvarieties where condition (B) fails, new algorithms to compute a Whitney stratification of an algebraic variety and an algorithm to produce the unique minimal Whitney stratification for complex varieties along with theoretical results which guarantee the correctness of our algorithms. Our new formulation of Condition B is given in
Section

3.1
, the resulting stratification is given in
Section

3.2
, and finally an algorithm to coarsen a given Whitney stratification to the unique minimal stratification is given in
Section

3.3
.

3.1.
A New Algebraic Criterion for Checking Whitney’s Condition

Throughout this section we denote by
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x}]
the underlying
polynomial ring of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We will study the problem of effectively computing a Whitney stratification of a complex affine algebraic variety
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We first show how to, in the setting of
Theorem

2.11
, compute a
superset of
A
without having to compute a primary decomposition of

I_{Y}+I(\mathbf{Con}(X))
. For this we denote by

the underlying

, where

First recall the following elementary proposition from commutative
algebra:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let

be a prime ideal and let

\mathbf{u}\subset\mathbf{x}
be of cardinality

\dim(P)\geq d
.

Proof.

This follows from the definition of Krull dimension of an ideal: Any
maximal chain of prime ideals in
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{u}]
extends to a chain of
prime ideals in
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x}]/P
since
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{u}]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x}]/P
is
injective.
∎

Using
Theorem

alternative algebraic criterion which will also allow us to construct
a Whitney stratification. When reading the result below keep in mind that
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u},
and
\mathbf{y}
(and hence
\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u}
) denote sets of variables.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let

be an algebraic variety and let

of

.

of

{J\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})[\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u},\mathbf{y}]}

.

Throughout this proof, we denote by
\ass(I)
the set of associated
primes of any ideal

.

(
Proof of
1
) By

2.5
, we have

(3)

(J:h^{\infty})=J\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})[\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u},\mathbf{y}]\cap\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}].

The ring
\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})[\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u},\mathbf{y}]
is the localization of

\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}]
at the multiplicative

\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{u}]-\{0\}

\ass((J:h^{\infty}))=\{P\in\ass(J)\;|\;P\cap\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{u}]=0\}.

Further, clearly,

and finally

.

(

{P\cap\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{u}]\neq 0}

and therefore also

{P\notin\ass((J:h^{\infty}))}

3.2.
The Algorithm

To start, let us briefly recall how to compute an ideal defining the
conormal space of a given
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
(see also Section 4.1 in

[
7
]
). Suppose that
X=\mathbf{V}(f_{1},\dots,f_{r})
for certain

augmented Jacobian matrix

\text{\bf J}_{\mathbf{y}}(F):=\begin{bmatrix}y_{0}&y_{1}&\dots&y_{n-1}\\
\partial_{1}f_{1}&\partial_{2}f_{1}&\dots&\partial_{n}f_{n}\\
\vdots&&&\vdots\\
\partial_{1}f_{r}&\partial_{2}f_{r}&\dots&\partial_{n}f_{r}\\
\end{bmatrix}

with new variables
\mathbf{y}:=\{y_{0},\dots,y_{n-1}\}
. Here,
\partial_{i}
denotes
the partial derivative by the

th variable in
\mathbf{x}
. Denote by

\text{\bf J}(F)
the usual Jacobian matrix of

. Now (see again

[
7
]
) we have the following fact.

Proposition 3.3
.

With the notation above let

, let

be the set of

-minors of
\text{\bf J}(F)
,
and let

be the set of

-minors of

\text{\bf J}_{\mathbf{y}}(F)
. Then

\mathbf{Con}(X)=\mathbf{V}((\langle F\rangle+\langle M_{\mathbf{y}}\rangle):M^{\infty}).

Remark 3.4
.

Recall that the necessary saturation to compute conormal spaces can
be performed using Gröbner basis computations with block orders and

Rabinowitsch’s trick
, see e.g. Exercise 15.41 in

[
3
]
.

Remark 3.9
.

While we have focused on the case of varieties over

in this note, applying the results of

, it follows that the algorithm

3.3.
Minimizing a Given Whitney Stratification

Our first goal here is to show how to compute, for a given

and a random point

.
Combining this with

.

Suppose for the moment that
Y=\mathbf{V}(f_{1},\dots,f_{c})
where

and

Using the notation above, for any

Since

∎

Remark 3.16
.

Note that we may also combine
WhitneyMinimize
directly
with
WhitPoints
: For given
X
and irreducible
Y
let
h

be the output of
WhitPoints
(
X,Y
) and let

. We know that the set of points in
Y
where
Whitney’s condition (B) fails to hold with respect to
X
and
Y
is
contained in
A
. We may then compute the irreducible components

A_{1},\dots,A_{r}
of
A
and compare for each
A_{i}

(X,Y)

(X,A_{i})

with
X
and
A_{i}

more optimal then minimizing after the stratification is finished
because it prevents a build up of unneeded varieties.
