Liftable self-similar groups and scale groups
Rostislav Grigorchuk, Dmytro Savchuk

Abstract
We canonically identify the groups of isometries and dilations of local fields and their rings of integers with subgroups of the automorphism group of the ( d + 1 ) (d+1) -regular tree T ~ d + 1 \widetilde{T}_{d+1} , where d d is the residual degree. Then we introduce the class of liftable self-similar groups acting on a d d -regular rooted tree whose ascending HNN extensions act faithfully and vertex transitively on T ~ d + 1 \widetilde{T}_{d+1} fixing one of the ends. The closures of these extensions in Aut ( T ~ d + 1 ) \mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{d+1}) are totally disconnected locally compact group that belong to the class of scale groups as defined in [ 84 ] . We give numerous examples of liftable groups coming from self-similar groups acting essentially freely or groups admitting finite L L -presentations. In particular, we show that the finitely presented group constructed by the first author in [ 38 ] and the finitely presented HNN extension of the Basilica group constructed in [ 56 ] embed into the group 𝒟 ⁡ ( ℚ 2 ) \mathcal{D}(\mathbb{Q}_{2}) of dilations of the field ℚ 2 \mathbb{Q}_{2} of 2 2 -adic numbers. These actions, translated to T ~ 3 \widetilde{T}_{3} , are 2-transitive on the punctured boundary of T ~ 3 \widetilde{T}_{3} . Also we explore scale-invariant groups studied in [ 72 ] with the purpose of getting new examples of scale groups.



1
Introduction

The main goal of this article is to shed more light on the question about what kind of groups can act by automorphisms and cocompactly on a locally finite infinite tree. As a byproduct of our considerations we gain some information about subgroups of groups of isometries (and more generally of dilations) of local fields, and about the class of scale groups, an important subclass of totally disconnected locally compact (TDLC) groups. Our focus is on subgroups of automorphisms of a
(d+1)
-regular tree (
d\geq 2
) which we will denote by
\widetilde{T}_{d+1}
(keeping the notation
T_{d}
for a
d
-regular rooted tree that also will play an important role in the paper). Such trees are the most symmetric objects among all trees and deserve special attention. The group
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{d+1})_{\omega}
of automorphisms of
\widetilde{T}_{d+1}
preserving an end
\omega
, is known for a while, in particular in relation with amenability (of topological groups), random walks, and harmonic analysis
[
25
,
9
,
86
]
.

The study of groups acting on trees (and more generally metric trees, including
\mathbb{R}
-trees) was initiated by J.-P. Serre and J. Tits in 1960’s and the basics of the theory are known as the
Bass-Serre theory

[
78
,
12
,
30
]
. One of the key questions is which (discrete or more generally topoplogical) groups act on a tree faithfully and vertex transitively. There are many articles on this topic (see, for example,
[
21
,
13
,
22
]
) but the question still is far from getting the satisfactory answer. A special case considered by Culler and Morgan in
[
28
, Theorem 2.7]
(see also a more detailed exposition of this result in
[
74
]
, similar result in connection to closed amenable groups acting on trees by Nebbia
[
69
]
, and a short exposition with a simple proof in an unpublished note by Wilton
[
85
]
) is when the group
G
does not contain a free group
F_{2}
on two generators as a subgroup. In this case any action of
G
on a tree must preserve a vertex, an edge, an end or a pair of ends (as a set). Discrete amenable groups do not contain
F_{2}
as a subgroup, hence when acting on a tree, they must fix something. The case when there is a global fixed vertex is orthogonal to co-compactness of the action and is a matter of study of groups acting on rooted trees
[
44
,
71
]
. This case is quite understood and the groups in this class are residually finite
[
44
,
22
]
. The case when the edge is fixed is not far from the previous one, and the case when a group fixes a set of two ends is also well understood. For instance, when it fixes both ends then the involved group is a semidirect product of
\mathbb{Z}
and a group acting level transitively on a rooted tree.

Thus we are left with the case when
G
fixes an end of the tree (i.e., a point of the boundary of the tree) and we focus our attention to this case.

If a field is supplied with a natural metric then one could be interested in the group of its isometries (or more generally dilations). A natural class of fields to be considered is the class of non-Archimedian local fields which, by the famous characterization (see, for example, Theorem 22 in
[
76
]
or Remark 7.49 in
[
64
]
, or an earlier work
[
73
]
), are either finite extensions of the field
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
of
p
-adic numbers or are isomorphic to the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}((x))
of Laurent powers series over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Our result (in fact observation) given by Theorem
A
and numerous examples coming from theory of groups acting on rooted trees show that the groups of isometries and dilations of local fields contain a plethora of exotic subgroups satisfying various conditions of transitivity, including those that do not satisfy the famous Tits alternative that states that a finitely generated linear group (i.e., a group generated by matrices over a field) either contains a free subgroup
F_{2}
or is virtually solvable, and hence amenable. This includes groups of intermediate growth, finitely presented amenable, but not elementary and not subexponentially amenable groups, non-elementary just-infinite groups, groups of branch type, and finitely constrained groups.
Thus, the situation here is very different from the situations with subgroups of linear groups.

While the theory of connected locally compact topological groups is well developed, totally disconnected locally compact groups are very far from being well understood. A mysterious connection of them to self-similar groups was discovered recently in
[
24
,
84
]
via the notion of
scale
group, that is a closed vertex transitive subgroup of
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{d+1})
fixing an end. By the result of Nebbia
[
69
, Theorem 2]
this is exactly the class of closed amenable (as topological groups) vertex transitive subgroups of
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{d+1})
.

Our main result, Theorem
B
, gives a way to construct new examples of scale groups realizing them as closures of the ascending HNN extensions of the so-called liftable groups introduced in the article (see Definition
4.1
).

The alternative approach of getting scale groups is via not finitely co-hopfian groups, i.e., groups containing proper subgroups of finite index isomorphic to them. Getting interesting examples of finitely generated groups of this sort is a challenging problem which is closely related to the problem of constructing
scale-invariant
groups (see Definition
5.3
) initiated in
[
72
]
. The level of transitivity of the scale group is a matter of a special interest and is discussed for instance in
[
84
]
. We give some insight on it as well in Theorem
C
.

Scale groups are amenable groups in the category of topological groups and in most of our examples they are closures of (non-elementary) amenable abstract groups. Moreover, in some cases these amenable groups are finitely presented and non-elementary amenable.

We give also examples of scale groups that are closures of non-amenable groups (even not satisfying the Haagerup property). Observe that closed subgroups of
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{d+1})
that act transitively on
\partial\widetilde{T}_{d+1}
and hence are not scale groups belong to the class of Kunze-Stein groups
[
69
]
.

Now let us pass more closely to the context of the article. We start from establishing a connection between groups of isometries and dilations of local fields and groups of automorphisms of regular trees. The following theorem could be viewed as a folklore type statement, but it seems that it was never stated in this form before. By dilation of a metric space we mean a homeomorphism that uniformly expands distances by some constant
\lambda>0
(see Definition
3.1
).

Theorem A
.

Let
F
be a (non-Archimedean) local field with the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}
and the residue field of size
q
, endowed with the metric induced by its valuation. Then

(i)

the group
\mathop{\rm Isom}\nolimits(\mathcal{O})
of isometries of
\mathcal{O}
is isomorphic to the group
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(T_{q})
of automorphisms of
T_{q}
.

(ii)

the group
\mathop{\rm Isom}\nolimits(F)
of isometries of
F
is isomorphic to the group
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits_{0}(\widetilde{T}_{q+1})_{\omega}
consisting of automorphisms of
\widetilde{T}_{q+1}
that fix a distinguished end
\omega
pointwise (eventually fixing the vertices in the ray corresponding to
\omega
).

(iii)

the group
\mathcal{D}(F)
of dilations of

is isomorphic to the group
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{q+1})_{\omega}
consisting of automorphisms of
\widetilde{T}_{q+1}
that fix an end
\omega
as a point of the boundary of
\widetilde{T}_{q+1}
(but possibly moving the vertices in rays corresponding to
\omega

\omega
as a point of the boundary).

The automorphism groups involved in the theorem were discussed before (see, for example,
[

,
9

As was already mentioned one of the goal of the paper is to construct examples of amenable totally disconnected locally compact (TDLC) groups coming from the actions of self-similar groups on

The idea of lifting (the notion that will appear in the next Theorem

to embed the group
\mathcal{G}
of intermediate growth constructed by the first author

into a finitely presented group. The presentation of
\mathcal{G}

\mathcal{G}=\langle a,b,c,d\mid a^{2},b^{2},c^{2},d^{2},bcd,\sigma^{i}((ad)^{4}),\sigma^{i}((adacac)^{4}),i\geq 0\rangle,

(1.1)

where the substitution
\sigma
is defined as

\sigma\colon\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}a\mapsto aca\\
b\mapsto d\\
c\mapsto b\\
d\mapsto c\end{array}\right.

(1.2)

discovered by I. Lysionok in
[
61
]
, leads to the embedding of
\mathcal{G}
into the ascending HNN extension
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}=\langle a,b,c,d,t\mid a^{2},b^{2},c^{2},d^{2},bcd,(ad)^{4},(adacac)^{4},tst^{-1}\sigma(s)^{-1},s\in\{a,b,c,d\}\rangle.

(1.3)

The latter is a finitely presented amenable, but not elementary amenable group containing a subgroup of intermediate growth. The presentation (
1.3
) was later simplified by L. Bartholdi (given in
[
29
]
) to the one with only 2 generators and 4 relators of the combined length of 109:

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\cong\left\langle a,t\mid a^{2}=TaTatataTatataTataT=(Tata)^{8}=(T^{2}ataTat^{2}aTata)^{4}=1\right\rangle,

(1.4)

where

denotes

The further observation made by the first author a long time ago and privately reported to A. Valette is that

embeds into the group of automorphisms of
\widetilde{T}_{3}
preserving one end of this tree. Hence we have a good example illustrating the theorem of Nebbia
[

]
mentioned earlier. Moreover, as recently shown by C. Bodart this group does not have rational cross-sections

The presentation found by Lysionok attracted a lot of attention and led to the definition (in different forms) of (finite)

The question of interest is which of self-replicating groups lead to examples of scale groups. This question is studied from one angle in

As one of the main applications of Theorem
B
we establish the following embedding result.

Theorem C
.

The groups
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
embed into
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{Q}_{2})
. Moreover, when
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{Q}_{2})
is identified with
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{3})_{\omega}
, the images of these embeddings act transitively on
\widetilde{T}_{3}
and their closures are scale groups acting 2-transitively on the punctured boundary
\partial_{\omega}\widetilde{T}_{3}

It is shown in
[

that every scale group acts transitively on the corresponding punctured boundary
\partial_{\omega}\widetilde{T}_{d+1}
. The 2-transitivity condition in the above theorem is a stronger statement.

Finally, at the end of the article we discuss one more method of constructing scale groups. It is based on the use of some facts from the Bass-Serre theory of groups acting on trees and not finitely co-Hopfian groups, i.e., groups that contain a subgroup of finite index isomorphic to the whole group. The class of scale-invariant groups studied in
[
72
]
is suitable for our purposes and allows us to construct more examples of scale groups. One such example is obtained as a closure of a group without Haagerup property. Observe that the closure of a group with Kazhdan’s property (T) cannot be a scale group since this property implies Serre’s property (FA)
[
83
]
, which means that every action of a group on a tree has a global fixed point. Nevertheless we use property (T) in Section
7
to construct groups without Haagerup property whose closures are scale groups. Thus (and we emphasize it), the examples of scale groups given in this article are of two kinds: obtained as the closures of (elementary and non-elementary) amenable groups and as the closures of non-amenable groups.

The paper is organized as follows. We begin with preliminaries in Section
2
. In Section
3
we describe the identification of the groups of isometries and dilations of local fields and their rings of integers with groups of automorphisms of regular rooted and unrooted trees. Section
4
contains the definition of liftable groups and the main embedding result. In Section
5
we provide many examples of liftable groups and analyze the actions of their HNN extensions on corresponding regular trees. Section
6
offers an alternative method of constructing of actions of ascending HNN extentions on regular trees via the Bass-Serre theory (see Theorem
6.1
). Finally, in Section
7
we use some scale-invariant groups to provide new examples of a scale groups. Note that the paper contains a rather extended bibliography as we tried to unite various notation and approaches used in the literature.

Preliminaries

We start this section by introducing the notions and terminology of automorphisms of regular rooted trees, self-similar groups, and transformations generated by Mealy automata. For more details, the reader is referred to
[
44
,
71
]
.

2.1
Rooted trees and self-similar groups

Let
X=\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
be a finite alphabet with
d\geq 2
elements (called letters) and let
X^{*}
denote the set of all finite words over
X
. The set
X^{*}
can be equipped with the structure of a rooted
d
-ary tree
T(X)
by declaring that
v
is adjacent to
vx
for every
v\in X^{*}
and
x\in X
. As a graph the tree
T(X)
is isomorphic to
T_{d}
, but the vertices of
T(X)
are labelled by finite words over
X
. We will denote the length of a word
w\in X^{*}
by
|w|
. The empty word corresponds to the root of the tree and for each positive integer
n
the set
X^{n}
corresponds to the
n\,
th level of
T(X)
. Also the set
X^{\infty}
of infinite (to the right) words over
X
can be identified with the
boundary

\partial T(X)
of the tree
T(X)
, which consists of all infinite paths in the tree, without backtracking, initiating at the root. We consider automorphisms of the tree
T(X)
(that is, the bijections of
T(X)
that preserve the root and the adjacency of vertices). We sometimes denote the tree
T(X)
as
T_{|X|}
when only the cardinality of
X
is important. The group of all automorphisms of
T(X)
is denoted by
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(T(X))
. To operate with such objects, we use alternatively the languages of sections and wreath recursions, and of Mealy automata.

Let
g\in\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(T(X))
and
x\in X
. For any
v\in X^{*}
we can write

g(xv)=g(x)v^{\prime}

for some
v^{\prime}\in X^{*}
. Then the map
g|_{x}\colon X^{*}\to X^{*}
given by

g|_{x}(v)=v^{\prime}

defines an automorphism of
T(X)
which we call the
section
(or
state
) of
g
at vertex
x
. We can inductively extend the definition of a section at a letter
x\in X
to a section at any vertex
x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{n}\in X^{*}
as follows:

g|_{x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{n}}=g|_{x_{1}}|_{x_{2}}\cdots|_{x_{n}}.

With this notation for any
g\in\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(T(X))
, and any words
v,w\in X^{*}
we have

g(vw)=g(v)g|_{v}(w).

(2.1)

Definition 2.1
.

A subgroup of
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(T(X))
is called
self-similar
if it is closed under taking sections at the vertices of
T(X)
.

We adopt the following convention throughout the paper.

Convention 2.2
.

If
g
and
h
are elements of some group acting on a set
Y
and
y\in Y
, then

gh(y)=h(g(y)).

We work with the right actions partially because it corresponds to the standard order of multiplication of permutations in
GAP
, the computer algebra platform containing the package
AutomGrp

[
66
]
dealing with self-similar groups and semigroups and used in this paper for many routine calculations. However, we due convenience in notation, we still write the arguments of functions on the right. Note that knowledge of GAP is not required for reading this paper or for verification of its results.

With the above convention, the state
g|_{v}
at
v\in X^{*}
of any product
g=g_{1}g_{2}\cdots g_{n}
, where
g_{i}\in\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(T(X))
for
1\leq i\leq n
, can be computed as follows:

g|_{v}=g_{1}|_{v}g_{2}|_{g_{1}(v)}\cdots g_{n}|_{g_{1}g_{2}\cdots g_{n-1}(v)}.

Also we use the language of wreath recursions. For each self-similar group
G
there is a natural embedding

G\hookrightarrow G\wr\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits(X),

where
\wr
is a symbol for the permutational wreath product, and
\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits(X)
denotes the symmetric group on
X
. This embedding is given by

G\ni g\mapsto(g_{0},g_{1},\ldots,g_{d-1})\sigma_{g}\in G\wr\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits(X),

(2.2)

where
g_{0},g_{1},\ldots,g_{d-1}
are the states of
g
at the vertices of the first level, and
\sigma_{g}
is the transformation of
X
induced by the action of
g
on the first level of the tree. If
\sigma_{g}
is the trivial permutation, it is customary to omit it in (
2.2
). We call
(g_{0},g_{1},\ldots,g_{d-1})\sigma_{g}
the

2.3
The boundary of a rooted tree as a topological and measure space

The boundary
\partial T(X)
of the rooted tree is endowed with a natural topology in which two infinite words are close if they have a large common prefix. Each automorphism of
T(X)
naturally induces a continuous transformation (respectively, homeomorphism) of
\partial T(X)
.

The boundary
\partial T(X)
can be viewed as an (ultra)metric space in which the distance between two points is equal to
\lambda_{k}
, where
\{\lambda_{n}\}
is any monotonically decreasing sequence converging to 0, and
k
is the length of the longest common beginning of these two points.
This metric defines a topology on
\partial T(X)
that coincides with the Tychonoff product topology on
X^{\mathbb{N}}
(when using the discrete topologies on each copy of
X
. In this topology
\partial T(X)
is homeomorphic to the Cantor set. Let
\mu
be the uniform Bernoulli measure defined by

\mu(C_{v})=\frac{1}{|X|^{n}},

where for a vertex
v\in X^{n}
the cylindrical set
C_{v}\subset\partial T(X)
consists of all infinite paths in
\partial T(X)
that go through
v
. Such sets generate a
\sigma
-algebra of Borel sets and hence measure
\mu
is defined by its values on them. This measure is invariant with respect to the action of
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(T(X))
. A action of a countable group
G<\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(T(X))
on
(\partial T(X),\mu)
by measure preserving transformations is called essentially free if the measure of a set of points in
X
that have non-trivial stabilizers is equal to 0. The examples of such groups will be given in Section
5
.

It is a well known fact (see, for example,
[
44
]
) that if a group
G<\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(T(X))
acts transitively on levels of
T(X)
, then its action on
\partial T(X)
is ergodic and its closure in
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(T(X))
acts transitively on
\partial T(X)
. More generally, the orbit structure of the action of
G
on the levels of
T(X)
gives rise to the decomposition into ergodic components of the action of
G
on
\partial T(X)

[
49
]
.

2.4
Historical remarks

The reader should be aware that during the period 1999–2003, when there was an explosion of the activity on self-similar and branch groups, the terminology associated with the groups acting on rooted trees and, in particular, with self-similar groups was changing. Now it is more or less settled, but still there are nuances. In the articles
[
39
,
40
,
43
,
44
]
the terms “self-similar”, “self-replicating”, “branch”, “regular branch”, etc. already appear, but sometimes with different meaning. For instance, the term “self-similar” action of a group was used in the past to denote what is now called “self-replicating” (or “recurrent” in
[
71
]
).

Currently most of the authors use the definition of a self-similar group presented in
[
4
,
71
]
as used in this article, which we will call
standard
. However, an early version of
[
84
]
by G. Willis used a slightly different definition, calling a group self-similar if the projection of the stabilizer of every vertex in the tree on the subtree rooted at that vertex remains in the original group. This condition corresponds to the definition of a
fractal
group in
[
2
, Definition 2.1]
. The following remarks aim to clarify the distinction between these two definitions. Any automorphism
g
acting on the binary rooted tree with the wreath recursion
g=(h,h^{-1})\varepsilon
for arbitrary
h\in\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(T_{2})
would generate a fractal group isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
as the stabilizer of every vertex is trivial. Clearly, this group would not be self-similar (in the standard sense) if
h
is not a power of
g
.

Another example constructed by A. Garrido shows that there are self-replicating groups that are fractal, but not self-similar (in the standard sense). Consider the infinite dihedral group
D_{\infty}
acting on a binary rooted tree and generated by the following wreath recursion:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}a&=&(1,1)\varepsilon,\\
b&=&(a,b).\end{array}

Let
H=\langle ab\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}
be the index 2 subgroup in
D_{\infty}
. Then, since
ab=(b,a)\varepsilon
we get

(ab)^{2^{n}}=\bigl((ba)^{2^{n-1}},(ab)^{2^{n-1}}\bigr)

for
n\geq 1
and the stabilizer
\mathop{\rm St}\nolimits_{H}(v)
in
H
of any vertex
v
of level
n
is generated by
(ab)^{2^{n}}
. Therefore,
\pi_{v}\colon\mathop{\rm St}\nolimits_{H}(v)\to H
projects
\mathop{\rm St}\nolimits_{H}(v)
onto
H
and
H
is self-replicating and fractal. However, clearly
H
is not self-similar in the standard sense because
(ab)|_{0}=b\notin H
.

On the other hand, the following proposition shows that every fractal group is conjugate to a self-similar group (in the standard sense), so no difference between the who classes can be detected by analyzing the algebraic structure of these groups. Note, that this small difference does not usually play a significant role, but it is important to understand it.

Proposition 2.6

(V. Nekrashevych)
.

Let
G
be a group acting on the tree
X^{*}
so that for every
g\in G
stabilizing
a vertex
v
we have
g|_{v}\in G
. Then the action of
G
is conjugate to a self-similar
action.

Proof.

Choose a representative in each orbit of the first level, and choose for every

x\in X
an element
r_{x}\in G
moving the representative
x^{\prime}
of the orbit of
x
to
x
. Denote

h_{x}=r_{x}|_{x^{\prime}}
. Conjugate then the action of
G
by the automorphism
a
of
X^{*}

defined by the recursion:

a(xv)=x(h_{x}a)(v).

Then
xv=a^{-1}(x(h_{x}a)(v))
, so, replacing
(h_{x}a)(v)=w
, we get
a^{-1}(xw)=xa^{-1}h^{-1}_{x}(w)
and for every
g\in G
we have

a^{-1}ga(x)=a^{-1}g(x(h_{x}a)(v))=a^{-1}g(x)(g|_{x}h_{x}a)(v)=g(x)(a^{-1}h^{-1}_{g(x)}g|_{x}h_{x}a)(v).

The element
h^{-1}_{g(x)}g|_{x}h_{x}
is equal to the section of
r^{-1}_{x}gr_{x}
at the representative
x^{\prime}
of the orbit of x, since we have
r_{x}(x^{\prime})=x
,
r_{x}|_{x^{\prime}}=h_{x}
,
r_{g(x)}=r_{x}
. The element
r^{-1}_{x}gr_{x}
stabilizes
x^{\prime}
, so its section at
x^{\prime}
belongs to
G
. In the conjugated action, the section of
g
at
x
is equal to
(r^{-1}_{x}gr_{x})|_{x^{\prime}}
, hence belongs to
G
.
∎

Additionally, other terms have been used to denote the class of self-similar groups, like “automaton groups” and “state-closed groups” (mostly in the school of S. Sidki). In some papers, a group is called self-similar if it is generated by all the states of a finite automaton, which is again different from the standard sense since in the latter one there is no condition of finiteness. In such papers sometimes standard self-similar groups are called “weakly self-similar”. Also, the terms of “fractal” and “semifractal” were used in the past to denote self-similar or self-replicating groups. Finally, the terms “self-similar” and self-replicating were also used in earlier papers to denote regular branch groups and branching subgroups
[
43
]
. The reader should be aware of these differences and nuances.

In

4
Liftable groups and actions of their HNN extensions on regular trees

In this section we embed certain ascending HNN extensions of liftable self-similar groups into the group
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{d+1})_{\omega}
.

4.1
Definition and main embedding result

Definition 4.1
.

A self-similar group
G
acting on a tree
T_{d}=T(X)
is called
liftable
if there exists some
i\in X
and a homomorphism
\sigma\colon G\to\mathop{\rm St}\nolimits_{G}(i)
, called the
lifting
, that is the right inverse of the projection map
\pi_{i}
defined by (
2.4
) (i.e., such that
\pi_{i}\circ\sigma
is the identity on
G
).

Note that it follows immediately from the definition that the lifting
\sigma
must be a monomorphism.

An equivalent definition of a liftability of a group is that for some
i\in X=\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
the sequence

1\to\ker\pi_{i}\hookrightarrow\mathop{\rm St}\nolimits_{G}(i)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{i}}}{{\twoheadrightarrow}}G\to 1

is a short exact sequence defining a split extension, and hence the stabilizer
\mathop{\rm St}\nolimits_{G}(i)
of vertex
i
decomposes as a semidirect product:

\mathop{\rm St}\nolimits_{G}(i)=\ker\pi_{i}\rtimes G.

It follows from the definition of a liftable group that the projection map
\pi_{i}\colon\mathop{\rm St}\nolimits_{G}(i)\to G
must be surjective. This is always the case when
G
is self-replicating. However, the fact that
\pi_{i}
is onto for some
i
does not automatically imply the existence of a lifting. On the other hand we do not know of any example of a self-replicating non-liftable group.

Question 4.2
.

Is there a self-replicating non-liftable group?

\pi_{\Lambda}(\mathop{\rm St}\nolimits_{\theta(\widetilde{G})}(\Lambda))<G
.

To justify the semidirect product structure of
\mathop{\rm St}\nolimits_{\theta(\widetilde{G})}(\Lambda)
we note that since the action of
\theta(G)
on the subtree
T_{\Lambda}
is faithful (because it coincides with the action of
G
on
T_{d}
),

\theta(G)\cap\bigl(\ker\pi_{\Lambda}\cap\theta(\widetilde{G})\bigr)=\{1\},

(4.1)

On the other hand,

\mathop{\rm St}\nolimits_{\theta(\widetilde{G})}(\Lambda)=\bigl(\ker\pi_{\Lambda}\cap\theta(\widetilde{G})\bigr)\cdot\theta(G).

(4.2)

Indeed, since all sections of the shift
\theta(t)
are trivial, for each
s\in\mathop{\rm St}\nolimits_{\theta(\widetilde{G})}(\Lambda)
we have
g:=s|_{\Lambda}\in G
, so
h=s\cdot\theta(g)^{-1}\in\ker\pi_{\Lambda}\cap\theta(\widetilde{G})
. Therefore,
s=h\cdot\theta(g)\in\bigl(\ker\pi_{\Lambda}\cap\theta(\widetilde{G})\bigr)\cdot\theta(G)
. Equations (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) yield that
\mathop{\rm St}\nolimits_{\theta(\widetilde{G})}(\Lambda)=\ker\pi_{\Lambda}\rtimes\theta(G)
.

Item (iv) is proved analogously to item (iii).

∎

4.2
Connection to the results of Willis on scale groups

Here we briefly discuss the relation between Theorem
B
and recent results of George Willis, who noticed a connection between the classes of self-replicating and scale groups
[
84
]
. Willis showed in Proposition 3.8 that, given a scale group acting on
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{d+1})_{\omega}
, the stabilizers of vertices of
\widetilde{T}_{d+1}
in this group project to the same subgroup of
T_{d}
that is self-replicating. Conversely, in Proposition 3.10 he proved that given a closed self-replicating group
G
acting on
T_{d}
, there is a scale group
\widetilde{G}<\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{d+1})_{\omega}
such that
\pi_{\Lambda}(\mathop{\rm St}\nolimits_{\widetilde{G}}(\Lambda))=G
. The group
\widetilde{G}
is constructed as a subgroup of
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{d+1})_{\omega}
consisting of all automorphisms of
\widetilde{T}_{d+1}
whose sections at all vertices of that tree belong to
G
. This part of the article does not address the question of when one can embed a closed self-replicating group into a scale groups, but realizes the former as a section of the latter. Theorem
B
, on the other hand, provides explicit way to embed many self-replicating groups (not necessarily closed) and their HNN extensions into scale groups under condition of liftability. Moreover, it yields many examples of countable discrete transitive subgroups of
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{d+1})_{\omega}
, whose closures are scale groups, as described in Section
5
below.

Proposition 5.1
.

If a group
G<\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(T_{d})
acts on
\partial T_{d}
essentially freely and is self-replicating, then it is liftable.

Proof.

We identify

with

for an alphabet

acts essentially freely, the projection homomorphism

, we can uniquely define the right inverse
\sigma
of the projection

∎

By Proposition 10 in
[
55
]
the map
\sigma_{\mathcal{B}}
extends to a monomorphism
\sigma_{\mathcal{B}}\colon\mathcal{B}\to\mathop{\rm St}\nolimits_{\mathcal{B}}(0)
and since
b=(a,1),a^{2}=(b,b)
, this monomorphism is a lifting for
\mathcal{B}
. Thus, by Theorem
B
the HNN extension
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
of
\mathcal{B}
via the homomorphism
\sigma
embeds into
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{Q}_{2})
. The group

is an example of a finitely presented amenable but not subexponentially amenable group of exponential growth.

5.3
Transitivity of the scale group coming from liftable groups

We now study the closures of
\theta(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}})
and
\theta(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}})
from Examples
5.8
and
5.12
in
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{3})_{\omega}
. The next result completes the proof of Theorem
C
from the Introduction. In this section
\sigma
and
\sigma_{\mathcal{B}}
denote the substitutions defined in (

) and (

), respectively.

Theorem 5.13
.

The groups
\overline{\theta(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}})}
and
\overline{\theta(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}})}
are scale groups that act 2-transitively on the punctured boundary
\partial_{\omega}\widetilde{T}_{3}
of
\widetilde{T}_{3}
.

The proof of this theorem is based on the
2
-transitivity property of the actions of
\mathcal{G}
on the levels of
T_{2}
that was discovered in
[
5
]
(see also Appendix by Bekka, Grigorchuk, de la Harpe in
[
17
]
), and a similar result for the Basilica group
\mathcal{B}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{B}
satisfy the conditions of Theorem
B
and act transitively on the first level, we immediately obtain
\theta(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}})
and
\theta(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}})
act transitively on the set of vertices of
T_{3}
and that their closures
\overline{\theta(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}})}
and
\overline{\theta(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}})}
are scale groups acting transitively on the punctured boundary
\partial_{\omega}\widetilde{T}_{3}
of
\widetilde{T}_{3}
by Proposition 2.4 in
[
84
]
.

We start from proving
2
-transitivity of the action of
\overline{\theta(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}})}
on
\partial_{\omega}\widetilde{T}_{3}
. Suppose
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
and
(\xi^{\prime}_{1},\xi^{\prime}_{2})
are two pairs of distinct points of
\partial_{\omega}\widetilde{T}_{3}
. Let
n
(resp.,
n^{\prime}
) be the largest level at which
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}
(resp.,
\xi^{\prime}_{1},\xi^{\prime}_{2}
) agree. Then
elements
\theta(t)^{-n}
and
\theta(t)^{-n^{\prime}}
of
\theta(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}})
will move them to pairs
(\theta(t)^{-n}(\xi_{1}),\theta(t)^{-n}(\xi_{2}))
and
(\theta(t)^{-n^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime}_{1}),\theta(t)^{-n^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime}_{2}))
such that all of the 4 involved elements of
\partial_{\omega}\widetilde{T}_{3}
pass through the root
\Lambda
of
T_{2}^{(0)}
and such that
\theta(t)^{-n}(\xi_{1})
and
\theta(t)^{-n}(\xi_{2})
(resp.,
\theta(t)^{-n^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime}_{1})
and
\theta(t)^{-n^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime}_{2})
) do not agree on level 1.

Since
\mathcal{G}
(and thus
\theta(\mathcal{G})<\theta(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}})
) acts transitively on the levels of
T^{(0)}_{2}
, for each level
l>0
there are elements
g_{l},g^{\prime}_{l}\in\mathcal{G}
such that

[\theta(g_{l})\bigl(\theta(t)^{-n}(\xi_{1})\bigr)]_{l}=[1^{-\infty}.1^{\infty}]_{l}

(5.12)

and

[\theta(g^{\prime}_{l})\bigl(\theta(t)^{-n^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime}_{1})\bigr)]_{l}=[1^{-\infty}.1^{\infty}]_{l}

(5.13)

Automorphisms
\theta(g_{l})
and
\theta(g_{l}^{\prime})
preserve levels of
\widetilde{T}_{3}
. Since
\theta(t)^{-n}(\xi_{1})
and
\theta(t)^{-n}(\xi_{2})
(resp.,
\theta(t)^{-n^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime}_{1})
and
\theta(t)^{-n^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime}_{2})
) do not agree on level 1, their images under
\theta(g_{l})
and
\theta(g_{l}^{\prime})
also must not agree on level 1. Therefore, by (
5.12
) and (

) we have

[\theta(g_{l})\bigl(\theta(t)^{-n}(\xi_{2})\bigr)]_{l}=[1^{-\infty}.0w]_{l}

and

[\theta(g^{\prime}_{l})\bigl(\theta(t)^{-n^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime}_{2})\bigr)]_{l}=[1^{-\infty}.0w^{\prime}]_{l}

for some infinite words
w,w^{\prime}
.

Now we use the second part of Lemma 9.10 in
[
5
]
, which states, in particular, that the stabilizer
P_{l}
of the vertex

in
\mathcal{G}
has
0X^{l-1}
as one of the orbits. It implies that there is

such that

[\theta(p_{l})\left(\theta(g_{l})\bigl(\theta(t)^{-n}(\xi_{2})\bigr)\right)]_{l}=[\theta(g^{\prime}_{l})\bigl(\theta(t)^{-n^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime}_{2})\bigr)]_{l}.

We can rewrite the last equality as

[\theta((g^{\prime}_{l})^{-1}p_{l}g_{l})\bigl(\theta(t)^{-n}(\xi_{2})\bigr)]_{l}=[\bigl(\theta(t)^{-n^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime}_{2})\bigr)]_{l}.

Since the sequence of elements

belongs to the compact stabilizer of the root

of

in
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits_{0}(\widetilde{T}_{3})_{\omega}
, it must contain a convergent subsequence
\left(\theta((g^{\prime}_{l_{j}})^{-1}p_{l_{j}}g_{l_{j}})\right)_{j\geq 1}
such that

\lim_{j\to\infty}\theta((g^{\prime}_{l_{j}})^{-1}p_{l_{j}}g_{l_{j}})=g\in\overline{\theta(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}})}.

By construction we have

g(\theta(t)^{-n}(\xi_{1}))=\theta(t)^{-n^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime}_{1})

and

g(\theta(t)^{-n}(\xi_{2}))=\theta(t)^{-n^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime}_{2}).

The last two equalities imply that the element
\theta(t)^{n^{\prime}}g\theta(t)^{-n}\in\overline{\theta(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}})}
moves
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
to
(\xi^{\prime}_{1},\xi^{\prime}_{2})
.

The proof that
\overline{\theta(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}})}
acts 2-transitively on
\partial_{\omega}\widetilde{T}_{3}
is similar to the above proof for
\overline{\theta(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}})}
. We only need to establish the analog of Lemma 9.10 from
[
5
]
for Basilica group. Namely, we will prove that

acts transitively on

for each

. It is proved in
[
55
]
that the group
\mathcal{B}
is weakly regular branch over its commutator subgroup

, i.e.,

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\succ\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{B}^{\prime}.

(5.14)

We show by induction on

that
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
acts transitively on
0X^{l-1}
and
1X^{l-1}
. Induction base is trivial for
l=1
. Assume that the statement is true for a fixed value of

. Then equation (
5.14
) immediately implies that
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
acts transitively on
00X^{l-1}
,
01X^{l-1}
,
10X^{l-1}
, and

. Since

is such that both

act transitively on the first level, we obtain that
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
acts transitively on

and

.

To complete the proof we observe that since

for each

\mathop{\rm St}\nolimits_{\mathcal{B}}(1^{l})
acts transitively on

and

∎

5.4
GGS-groups

Important situation where one can prove liftability is when the projections of
\sigma(G)
on the vertices of the first level, other than the distinguished vertex
i\in X
, happen to be in a finite subgroup of
G
. This is somewhat intermediate between the examples of groups acting essentially freely on the boundary of the tree (where the mentioned subgroup is trivial) and the examples of groups with finite
L
-presentation (where one needs to find the corresponding
L
-presentation). This situation is different from the case of groups acting essentially freely on the boundary of the tree that was discussed above. While the class of such groups is larger, it requires more work to check that the substitution
\sigma
defined on the generators of
G
extends to an endomorphism of
G
. In this section we demonstrate this situation on a large subclass of GGS-groups
[
6
,
32
]
. The class of GGS-groups (Grigorchuk-Gupta-Sidki groups, the term coined to G. Baumslag) includes the group
\mathcal{G}
(a second group from
[
35
]
) and all Gupta-Sidki
p
-groups constructed in
[
57
]
. It was shown in
[
32
]
that all GGS
p
-groups are regular branch, so they act totally non-freely on
\partial T_{p}
, which makes them very different from groups acting essentially freely on the boundaries of trees considered above.

For a fixed
d\geq 2
every GGS-group is defined by a non-zero vector
\mathrm{e}=(e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{d-1})\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{d-1}
as the group acting on the regular rooted tree
T_{d}=T(X)
for
X=\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
with the following wreath recursion:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}a&=&(1,1,\ldots,1,1)\varepsilon,\\
b&=&(a^{e_{1}},a^{e_{2}},\ldots,a^{e_{d-1}},b),\end{array}

where
\varepsilon=(0,1,\ldots,d-1)
denotes a long cycle in
\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits(X)
. We will denote the corresponding group as
G_{\mathrm{e}}=\langle a,b\rangle
.

Theorem 5.14
.

Let
p\geq 3
be a prime and
G_{\mathrm{e}}
be a GGS-group defined by a non-symmetric vector
\mathrm{e}=(e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{p-1})\in(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{p-1}
with the property that there is
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,p-1\}
such that
e_{j}\neq 0
and
e_{p-j}=0
. Then
G_{\mathrm{e}}
is a liftable group.

Proof.

We first observe that both generators
a
and
b
of
G_{\mathrm{e}}
have order
p
. Moreover, it is shown in
[
32
]
(Theorem 2.1) that the abelianization
G_{\mathrm{e}}/G_{\mathrm{e}}^{\prime}
is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{2}
.

Let
f=e_{j}^{-1}
, so that
(a^{e_{j}})^{f}=a
. Then we have

\begin{array}[]{rcllllllllll}a^{-1}ba&=&(b,&a^{e_{1}},&\ldots,&a^{e_{p-j-1}},&1,&a^{e_{p-j+1}},&\ldots,&a^{e_{p-2}},&a^{e_{p-1}}&),\\
(a^{j-1}b{a^{1-j}})^{f}&=&(a,&a^{e_{j+1}f},&\ldots,&a^{e_{p-1}f},&b^{f},&a^{e_{1}f},&\ldots,&a^{e_{j-2}f},&a^{e_{j-1}f}&),\\[2.84526pt]
\text{positions}&&\phantom{(}0&1&\ldots&p-j-1&p-j&p-j+1&\ldots&p-2&p-1&\\
\end{array}

where the last row indicates the positions of corresponding coordinates of the vectors in the first two rows. Define a substitution
\sigma
as

\sigma\colon\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}a\mapsto(a^{j-1}b{a^{1-j}})^{f}\\
b\mapsto a^{-1}ba\\
\end{array}\right.

(5.15)

Then
\sigma
extends to an injective endomorphism of
G_{\mathrm{e}}
. Indeed, suppose
w=w(a,b)
is a word in the free group
F(a,b)
that is a relator in
G_{\mathrm{e}}
. Then

\pi_{i}\circ\sigma(w)=\pi_{i}\bigl(w((a^{j-1}b{a^{1-j}})^{f},a^{-1}ba)\bigr)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}w(a,b)=_{G_{\mathrm{e}}}1,&\text{if }\ i=0,\\
w(1,b^{f}),&\text{if }\ i=p-j+1,\\
w(a^{e_{i}},a^{j+i}),&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Since
G_{\mathrm{e}}/G_{\mathrm{e}}^{\prime}\cong(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{2}
and
w=_{G_{\mathrm{e}}}1
, we must have that the total exponents of
a
and
b
in
w
are both equal to 0 modulo
p
. This implies that both
w(1,b^{f})
and
w(a^{e_{i}},a^{j+i})
are also trivial in
G_{\mathrm{e}}
since both
a
and
b
have order
p
. Here we use the fact that
\pi_{i}\circ\sigma(G)
is a subgroup of either
\langle b\rangle
or
\langle a\rangle
for
i\neq 0
, which are both finite subgroups of
G
, which makes the verification that
\sigma
is an endomorphism possible.

Finally, by construction we have
\pi_{0}\circ\sigma
, so
\sigma
is a lifting of
G_{\mathrm{e}}
and thus
G_{\mathrm{e}}
is liftable. In particular, Theorem
B
applies for
G_{\mathrm{e}}
.
∎

Now we consider the group

studied by A. Erschler in

from Example

is determined by a periodic sequence
(01)^{\infty}

\begin{array}[]{rcl}a&=&(1,1)\varepsilon\\
b&=&(a,c)\\
c&=&(1,b)\\
d&=&(a,d)\\
\end{array},

(5.16)

Similarly to

and the GGS-groups studied in Subsection

Proposition 5.16
.

The group
\mathcal{G}_{(01)^{\infty}}
is liftable.

Observe that it is proved by Bartholdi and Nekrashevych in
[
8
]
that
\mathcal{G}_{(01)^{\infty}}
is an iterated monodromy group of a quadratic polynomial and is finitely
L
-presented. One can check that the presentation that comes from this result is based on a lifting and Theorem
B
can be applied. Instead we demonstrate another (practical) approach based on the use of the GAP package working when no presentation of a group is known.

We introduce some groups related to
\mathcal{G}_{(01)^{\infty}}
and use
AutomGrp
GAP package
[
66
]
to perform our calculations. This is why we separated this example from others considered in other sections. All of our calculations can be verified by hands if needed. First, we observe that the following relations in
\mathcal{G}_{(01)^{\infty}}

a^{2}=b^{2}=c^{2}=d^{2}=bcd=(ac)^{4}=1.

Therefore, the group

is a quotient of the dihedral group
D_{4}
. On the other hand, the quotient of

by its stabilizer of the third level has size 8:

Therefore,

. Further, let

in
\mathcal{G}_{(01)^{\infty}}
. Then,
\mathcal{G}_{(01)^{\infty}}/B\cong C\cong D_{4}
and

.

Let

be the stabilizer of the first level in

. Then
[\mathcal{G}_{(01)^{\infty}}:H]=2
and there is a homomorphism

\begin{array}[]{llll}\psi\colon&H&\to&\mathcal{G}_{(01)^{\infty}}\times\mathcal{G}_{(01)^{\infty}}\\

(5.17)

The wreath recursion (
5.16
) implies that

, so we define the substitution
\sigma
on the generators of

\sigma\colon\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}a&\mapsto&aba&=(c,a)\\

(5.18)

We will show below that

extends to an injective endomorphism of
\mathcal{G}_{(01)^{\infty}}
(whose image is obviously a subgroup of

Define two subgroups

.

The subgroup

. The subgroup

is complementary to

, so that

First, we observe that the stabilizer

is generated by

, so

To confirm that

, which follows from the fact that

and that the projection of

Finally, since

6
Scale groups coming from Bass-Serre theory

One more way to construct an action of a group on
\widetilde{T}_{d+1}
is via using Bass-Serre Theory. Recall, that if for a finitely generated group
G
there is an injective endomorphism
\sigma\colon G\to H
onto a subgroup
H
of
G
of finite index
d
, then the corresponding ascending HNN extension
\widetilde{G}=G*_{\sigma}
, whose presentation is given by equation (
1.5
), acts on its Bass-Serre tree
T\cong\widetilde{T}_{d+1}
by automorphisms preserving one of its ends. The set of vertices of
T
is the set
\{xG\colon x\in\widetilde{G}\}
of left cosets of
G
in
\widetilde{G}
, where vertices
xG
and
yG
are connected by an oriented edge (going from
xG
to
yG
) if and only if
yG=xt^{-1}G
. In this tree a vertex
xG
has one outgoing edge connecting it to
xt^{-1}G
and
d
incoming edges starting at vertices
xg_{s}tG
,
i=1,\ldots,d
, where
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}\}
is a left transversal of
H
in
G
(for our purposes we do not need to talk about the inversions of edges). The group
\widetilde{G}
acts on
T
by multiplication on left, so this action is vertex transitive. It also preserves the end
\omega
of the tree
T
corresponding to the sequence of vertices of the form
t^{-n}G
,
n\geq 0
as follows from the normal form for the elements of ascending HNN extensions. This situation is discussed by Minasyan and Osin in
[
65
, Proposition 4.13]
.

The stabilizers of vertices in
T
are conjugates of
G
by elements of
\widetilde{G}
, with
G
itself being the stabilizer of the vertex
\Lambda=G\in V(T)
. Therefore, the action of
\widetilde{G}
on
T
is faithful (so that
\widetilde{G}
embeds into
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{d+1})_{\omega}
) if and only if the core of
G
in
\widetilde{G}
(i.e., the maximal normal subgroup of
\widetilde{G}
in
G
) is trivial:

\mathrm{Core}(G)=\bigcap_{x\in\widetilde{G}}xGx^{-1}=\{1\}.

(6.1)

In practice it is easier to check an equivalent to (
6.1
) condition:
\mathrm{Core}\left(\cap_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma^{n}(G)\right)=\{1\}
(it is equivalent since
\sigma^{n}(G)
for each
n
is a conjugate of
G
by a power of
t
). By construction the stabilizer of the vertex
\Lambda
corresponding to the coset
1\cdot G=G
is equal to
G
. The above facts are summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
G
be a group with a subgroup
H
isomorphic to
G
and of finite index
d
and let
\sigma\colon G\rightarrow H
be an isomorphism. Let
\widetilde{G}=G*_{\sigma}
be the ascending HNN-extension of
G
with respect to
\sigma
, whose presentation is given in equation (
1.5
). Then

(i)

\widetilde{G}
acts on its Bass-Serre tree
T
, which is isomorphic to
\widetilde{T}_{d+1}
, by automorphisms fixing one of its ends
\omega
.

(ii)

The action of
\widetilde{G}
on
T
is vertex transitive with the stabilizer of the vertex
\Lambda
of
T
corresponding to the coset
1\cdot G
equal to
G
.

(iii)

The action of
\widetilde{G}
on
T
is faithful if and only if
\mathrm{Core}(G)=\{1\}
(equivalently, if
\mathrm{Core}\left(\cap_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma^{n}(G)\right)=\{1\}
). In this case
\widetilde{G}
embeds into
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{d+1})_{\omega}
as a group acting vertex transitively on
\widetilde{T}_{d+1}
and its closure in
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{d+1})_{\omega}
is a scale group. Moreover, the image of
G
under this embedding coincides with the stabilizer of the distinguished vertex
\Lambda=1\cdot G
of
T
.

Remark 6.2
.

The above theorem works also in the case
d=[G:\sigma(G)]=\infty
, but in this case we get an action of
\widetilde{G}=G*_{\sigma}
on a tree
\widetilde{T}_{\infty}
that is not locally finite.

To use the above theorem we need to deal with groups that are not finitely co-Hopfian, i.e. that have finite index subgroups isomorphic to the whole group. The obvious examples of such groups are
\mathbb{Z}^{d},d\geq 1
(of course, we are interested in finitely generated examples). Less obvious examples come from scale-invariant groups that were defined at the beginning of Section
5
. To get embeddings of such groups into
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{d+1})_{\omega}
the last condition of Theorem
6.1
(on faithfulness of the action) needs to be satisfied.

Example 6.3
.

Our first example illustrating the use of Theorem
6.1
is the lamplighter group
\mathcal{L}=(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\wr\mathbb{Z}
, studied in Example
5.4
. Recall that the standard presentation of
\mathcal{L}
coming from the “lamplighter” interpretation of the action, is given with respect to a generating set
\{x=a^{-1}b,s=b\}
, where
\{a,b\}
is the generating set from Example
5.4
:

\mathcal{L}\cong\langle x,s\mid x^{2}=[x,x^{s^{n}}]=1,n\geq 1\rangle.

It was shown in
[
53
]
that the substitution
\alpha\colon x\mapsto xx^{s},s\mapsto s
extends to an injective endomorphism
\alpha\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{L}
, whose image has index 2 in
\mathcal{L}
. The homomorphism
\alpha
written in terms of generating set
\{a,b\}
coincides with the lifting
\sigma_{1}
defined in (
5.1
). Thus, the image of
\alpha
is equal to
\mathop{\rm St}\nolimits_{\mathcal{L}}(1)
and has index
2
in
\mathcal{L}
. It is also shown in
[
53
]
that HNN extension of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to
\alpha
is a metabelian group given by finite presentation

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\alpha}=\langle x,s,t\mid x^{2}=[t,s]=[x,x^{s}]=1,x^{t}=xx^{s}\rangle.

By Corollary
5.2
this group embeds into
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{3})_{\omega}
. However, Theorem
6.1
can also be applied here by verifying that the core of
\mathcal{L}
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is trivial. We start with the following lemma.

Lemma 6.4
.

For each
n\geq 0
we have

\alpha^{2^{n}}(x)=x\cdot x^{s^{2^{n}}},

\alpha^{2^{n}}(s)=s.

Proof.

Since
\alpha(s)=s
, the second claim of the lemma is trivial. To prove the first claim we use induction on
n
. The base of induction is trivial and to prove the step we assume that the statement is correct for some
n\geq 0
. Then

\alpha^{2^{n+1}}(x)=\alpha^{2^{n}}\left(\alpha^{2^{n}}(x)\right)=\alpha^{2^{n}}\left(x\cdot x^{s^{2^{n}}}\right)=x\cdot x^{s^{2^{n}}}\cdot\left(x\cdot x^{s^{2^{n}}}\right)^{s^{2^{n}}}\\
=x\cdot x^{s^{2^{n}}}\cdot x^{s^{2^{n}}}\cdot x^{s^{2^{n+1}}}=x\cdot x^{s^{2^{n+1}}}.

∎

Lemma 6.5
.

\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha^{n}(\mathcal{L})=\langle s\rangle
.

Proof.

It is enough to show that
\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha^{2^{n}}(\mathcal{L})=\langle s\rangle
. Since
s\in\alpha^{2^{n}}(\mathcal{L})
for all
n\geq 1
, one inclusion is obvious. We will show below that
\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha^{2^{n}}(\mathcal{L})\subset\langle s\rangle
.

Every element
g
of
\mathcal{L}
can be uniquely written as

g=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}x^{s^{n_{i}}}\right)\cdot s^{l},

(6.2)

where
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
n_{1}<n_{2}<\cdots<n_{k}
are distinct integers. In the lamplighter interpretation such an element corresponds to turning on lamps at positions
n_{i}
and moving the pointer to position
l
.

By Lemma
6.4

\alpha^{2^{n}}(\mathcal{L})
is generated by
\{x\cdot x^{s^{2^{n}}},s\}
. Define commuting elements

x_{2^{n},i}=\left(x\cdot x^{s^{2^{n}}}\right)^{s^{i}}=x^{s^{i}}\cdot x^{s^{2^{n}}+i},i\in\mathbb{Z}

Then every element
h
of
\alpha^{2^{n}}(\mathcal{L})
can be written uniquely as

h=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}x_{2^{n},m_{i}}\right)\cdot s^{l},

where
l\in\mathbb{Z}
and
m_{1}<m_{2}<\cdots<m_{k}
are distinct integers. In the case
k\geq 1
(i.e., when
h\notin\langle s\rangle
), the above decomposition rewritten in the form (
6.2
), will satisfy
n_{1}=m_{1}
and
n_{k}=m_{k}+2^{n}\geq n_{1}+2^{n}
. Therefore, every element in
\alpha^{2^{n}}(\mathcal{L})\setminus\langle s\rangle
will turn on 2 lamps that are at least
2^{n}
units apart. This shows that there is no element in
\mathcal{L}\setminus\langle s\rangle
that belongs to
\alpha^{2^{n}}(\mathcal{L})
for all
n\geq 1
.
∎

Lemma 6.6
.

\mathrm{Core}\bigl(\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha^{n}(\mathcal{L})\bigr)=\{1\}
.

Proof.

According to Lemma
6.5
we only need to show that
\langle s\rangle
does not contain a non-trivial normal subgroup in
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\alpha}
. This is the case because the conjugate
(s^{i})^{x}
of any non-trivial element
s^{i}
of
\langle s\rangle
is not a power of a shift
s
as it changes the states of 2 lamps, so
(s^{i})^{x}\notin\langle s\rangle
and thus
\langle s\rangle
contains no normal subgroup of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\alpha}
.
∎

Now, since
\mathrm{Core}(\mathcal{L})<\mathrm{Core}\bigl(\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha^{n}(\mathcal{L})\bigr)
, the group
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\alpha}
embeds into
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{3})_{\omega}
by Theorem
6.1
.

The above example is interesting in particular because the group
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\alpha}
is a quotient by the relation
x^{2}=1
of Baumslag’s finitely presented metabelian group with the commutator isomorphic to a free abelian group of infinite rank considered in
[
14
]
. Hence,
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\alpha}
is a finitely presented metabelian group with the commutator subgroup isomorphic to an elementary 2-group of infinite rank. Moreover,
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\alpha}
is isomorphic to the fundamental group of a closed Riemannian manifold with a non-integer
L^{2}
-Betti number, constructed in
[
53
]
(as the first example answering the original question of M. Atiyah and a counterexample to the Strong Atiyah conjecture on
L^{2}
-Betti numbers).

Formally, the two constructions of the actions of HNN extensions on
\widetilde{T}_{d+1}
given by Theorem
B

and Theorem
6.1
are quite different and it is quite unlikely that they lead to conjugate action of the same group (although in some cases perhaps conjugacy of action is possible). Observe that in the construction used in the proof of Theorem
6.1
we do not have a requirement that the index
[G:\sigma(G)]
is related to the degree of the tree as we have in the case of Theorem
6.1
. Moreover, this index in most examples presented in Section
5
is infinite. On the other hand, for each liftable self-similar group
G
that acts transitively on the first level and is defined by a lifting
\sigma
, the core of
G
in its HNN extension
\widetilde{G}
from Theorem
B
is trivial. Indeed, since
G
stabilizes the vertex
\Lambda
in
\widetilde{T}_{d+1}
, its conjugates by elements of
\widetilde{G}
are subgroups of the stabilizers of vertices of
\widetilde{T}_{d+1}
in
\widetilde{G}
. Moreover, since
\widetilde{G}
acts transitively on
\widetilde{T}_{d+1}
, one can conjugate
G
into a stabilizer of any vertex. Now, as the action of
\widetilde{G}
on
\widetilde{T}_{d+1}
is faithful, the intersection of all these stabilizers, which includes
\mathrm{Core}(G)
in
\widetilde{G}
, is trivial. Therefore, by Theorem
6.1
,
\widetilde{G}
acts on the corresponding Bass-Serre tree
\widetilde{T}_{d^{\prime}+1}
, where
d^{\prime}=[G:\sigma(G)]
and in the case
d^{\prime}<\infty
this leads to the constructions of scale groups via Theorem
6.1
(like in the last example).

7
Scale-invariant groups and Haagerup property

We are going to explore scale-invariant groups to get more examples of scale groups using Theorem
6.1
. The main result of Nekrashevych and Pete in
[
72
]
and its corollary state:

Theorem 7.1

(Theorem 1.1 in
[
72
]
)
.

Let
H
be a countable scale-invariant group, with a descending sequence

H=H_{0}>H_{1}>\cdots
of finite index subgroups, each isomorphic to
H
,
with trivial intersection. Let
A
be a countable automorphism group of
H
leaving all subgroups
H_{n}
invariant. Assume that the action of
A
is faithful on each
H_{n}
, and that the semidirect products
H_{n}\rtimes A
are isomorphic to
H\rtimes A
. Then
G:=H\rtimes A
is scale-invariant; in fact, there is a required subgroup chain with
\cap_{n\geq 0}G_{n}=\{1\}
.

Corollary 7.2

(Corollary 1.2 in
[
72
]
)
.

The following groups are scale-invariant.

(1)

The lamplighter groups
G=F\wr\mathbb{Z}
, where
F
is any finite Abelian group.

(2)

The solvable Baumslag–Solitar groups
BS(1,m)=\langle a,b\mid bab^{-1}=a^{m}\rangle
with
m>1
.

(3)

The affine group
\mathbb{Z}^{d}\rtimes\mathrm{GL}(\mathbb{Z},d)
, and its subgroups
\mathbb{Z}^{d}\rtimes A
for any
A\leq\mathrm{GL}(\mathbb{Z},d),d>1
.

The proof of the theorem uses (rooted) coset tree
\mathcal{T}
associated with
\{H_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and the natural level transitive action of
H\rtimes A
on it by affine automorphisms. The sequence
\{G_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is obtained as the sequence of stabilizers of vertices belonging to the path in
\mathcal{T}
joining the root vertex with infinity (i.e., with an end of the tree). It is proved that the stabilizers are isomorphic to
G
. The fact that they have finite index in
G
is obvious from the construction, and the main nontrivial point is to show that there are points in the boundary of
\mathcal{T}
with trivial stabilizers. The arguments provided in
[
72
]
show that in fact the set of such points is a dense
G_{\delta}
set, so is non-empty (and comeager).

For our purposes we will need Proposition 2.2(i) from
[
72
]
and its proof. It states that if, under conditions of Theorem
7.1
, the sequence
\{H_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
satisfies
H_{n}=\varphi^{n}(H)
for some injective endomorphism
\varphi\colon H\to H
, then there is an injective endomorphism
\tilde{\varphi}\colon G\to G
defined by (see equation (2.3) in
[
72
]
)

\tilde{\varphi}(h,\alpha)=\bigl(\varphi(h),\alpha\bigr)

(7.1)

for
(h,\alpha)\in G
such that
[G:\tilde{\varphi}(G)]=[H:\varphi(H)]<\infty
, and such that
\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(G)
is the stabilizer in
G
of a vertex of
\mathcal{T}
of level
n
. Let

\widetilde{G}=G*_{\tilde{\varphi}}=\langle G,t\mid\text{relations in}\ G,tgt^{-1}=\tilde{\varphi}(g)\ \text{for all}\ g\in G\rangle

be the ascending HNN extension of
G
with respect to
\tilde{\varphi}
.

Proposition 7.3
.

The core of
G
in
\widetilde{G}
is trivial.

Proof.

By the proof of Proposition 2.2(i) in
[
72
]
,
t^{n}Gt^{-n}=\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(G)
is the stabilizer in
G
of a vertex of
\mathcal{T}
of level
n
. Since the action of
G
on
\mathcal{T}
is level transitive, one can use conjugate
\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(G)
by elements of
G
into a stabilizer of any vertex of that (arbitrary) level. Since the action of
G
on
\mathcal{T}
is faithful, the intersection of all vertex stabilizers is trivial, which implies that
\mathrm{Core}(G)
, that is the intersection of all conjugates of
G
in
\widetilde{G}
is also trivial.
∎

As a direct corollary of the above proposition and Theorem
6.1
we obtain:

Corollary 7.4
.

The group
\widetilde{G}
embeds into
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{d+1})_{\omega}
for
d=[G:\tilde{\varphi}(G)]
.

We apply Corollary
7.4
to get examples of scale groups obtained as a closures of groups without Haagerup property. Recall that a (discrete) group
G
has a
Haagerup property
(or is
a-T-menable
) if it admits a proper affine action on a Hilbert space. There are several equivalent definitions of this property (see, for instance, Section 1.1.1 and Theorem 2.1.1 in
[
26
]
). Also, there are many definitions of
Kazhdan’s property (T)
and its relative version for a pair consisting of a group and its subgroup (see, for example,
[
26
, Definition 1.4.1]
). Property (T) groups also have Serre’s property (FA) meaning that every action on a tree has a fixed vertex. The class of Haagerup groups includes amenable groups, free groups, groups acting properly on trees,
\mathbb{R}
-trees, and
\mathrm{CAT}(0)
cube complexes. Examples of groups with property (T) mostly come from lattices in higher rank Lie groups. A nontrivial example of a pair with relative property (T) is
(\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{Z}^{2})
(see, for example,
[
16
, Theorem 4.2.2]
), which, in particular, implies that
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
does not have Haagerup property.

Theorem 7.5
.

(a)

There is a subgroup of
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{9})_{\omega}
without the Haagerup property that acts vertex transitively on
\widetilde{T}_{9}
and whose closure is a scale group.

(b)

There is a subgroup of
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{5})_{\omega}
without the Haagerup property that acts vertex transitively on
\widetilde{T}_{5}
and whose closure is a scale group.

Proof.

To prove item (a), consider a scale-invariant group
H=\mathbb{Z}^{3}
with an injective endomorphism
\varphi\colon H\to H
defined by
\varphi(v)=2v
. This defines a nested sequence
H_{n}=\varphi^{n}(H)
of subgroups of
H
with trivial intersection such that
[H_{n}:H_{n+1}]=8
for all
n\geq 0
. Then according to the proof of Theorem
7.1
the group
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{3}\rtimes\mathrm{SL}(\mathbb{Z},3)
consisting of affine transformations of
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
, which is a group without Haagerup property (as the class of groups with Haagerup property is closed under taking subgroups), acts faithfully by automorphisms on the coset tree
\mathcal{T}
, that is a regular rooted 8-ary tree. By Corollary
7.4
its HNN extension
\widetilde{\Gamma}*_{\tilde{\varphi}}
, where
\tilde{\varphi}
is defined by equation (
7.1
), embeds into
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{9})_{\omega}
. Its closure in
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{9})_{\omega}
is a scale group by Theorem
6.1
(iii).

The proof of item (b) is very similar with the difference that we take
H=\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes\mathrm{SL}(\mathbb{Z},2)
as the corresponding scale-invariant group. Since now
[H:\varphi(H)]=4
, the HNN extension
\widetilde{\Gamma}*_{\tilde{\varphi}}
, where
\tilde{\varphi}
is defined by equation (
7.1
), embeds into
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{5})_{\omega}
. Its closure in
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\widetilde{T}_{5})_{\omega}
is again a scale group by Theorem
6.1
(iii).

∎
