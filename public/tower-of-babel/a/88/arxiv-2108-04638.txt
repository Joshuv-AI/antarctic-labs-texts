Abstract
. Let ψ \psi be a continuous decreasing function defined on all large positive real numbers. We say that a real m × n m\times n matrix A A is ψ \psi -Dirichlet if for every sufficiently large real number t t one can find 𝒑 ∈ ℤ m \bm{p}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m} , 𝒒 ∈ ℤ n ∖ { 𝟎 } \bm{q}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\smallsetminus\{\bm{0}\} satisfying ‖ A ​ 𝒒 − 𝒑 ‖ m < ψ ⁡ ( t ) \|A\bm{q}-\bm{p}\|^{m}<\psi({t}) and ‖ 𝒒 ‖ n < t \|\bm{q}\|^{n}<{t} . This property was introduced by Kleinbock and Wadleigh in 2018, generalizing the property of A A being Dirichlet improvable which dates back to Davenport and Schmidt (1969). In the present paper, we give sufficient conditions on ψ \psi to ensure that the set of ψ \psi -Dirichlet matrices has zero or full Lebesgue measure. Our proof is dynamical and relies on the effective equidistribution and doubly mixing of certain expanding horospheres in the space of lattices. Another main ingredient of our proof is an asymptotic measure estimate for certain compact neighborhoods of the critical locus (with respect to the supremum norm) in the space of lattices. Our method also works for the analogous weighted problem where the relevant supremum norms are replaced by certain weighted quasi-norms.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

Let
m,n
be two positive integers and let
M_{m,n}({\mathbb{R}})
be the space of
m
by
n
real matrices. The starting point of our work is the following higher dimensional generalization of the classical Dirichlet’s Diophantine approximation theorem, see e.g.
[
3
, §1.5]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
A\in M_{m,n}({\mathbb{R}})
and
t>1
, there exists
(\bm{p},\bm{q})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}\times({\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\smallsetminus\{\bm{0}\})
satisfying the following system of inequalities:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\|A\bm{q}-\bm{p}\|^{m}\leq\frac{1}{t}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\|\bm{q}\|^{n}<t.

Here
\|\cdot\|
denotes the supremum norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
respectively.

A natural question to ask is whether one can improve (
1.1
) by replacing
1/t
by a smaller function, that is, consider the following system of inequalities:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\|A\bm{q}-\bm{p}\|^{m}<\psi(t)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\|\bm{q}\|^{n}<t

where
\psi
is a positive, continuous, decreasing function which decays to zero at infinity.
Historically there have been two directions to pursue in this regard: looking for solvability of (
1.2
) for an unbounded set of
t>0
vs. for all large enough
t
. The former is sometimes referred to as
asymptotic approximation
, and has culminated in definitive results such as the Khintchine-Groshev theorem. In this paper we are interested in the latter, less studied set-up of
uniform approximation
.
Following the definition in
Kleinbock and Wadleigh
[
26
]
,
we say that an
m
by
n
real matrix
A
is
\psi
-Dirichlet
if the system of inequalities (
1.2
)
has solutions in
(\bm{p},\bm{q})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}\times({\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\smallsetminus\{\bm{0}\})
for all sufficiently large
t
. It is clear that
A\in M_{m,n}({\mathbb{R}})
is
\psi
-Dirichlet if and only if
A+A^{\prime}
is
\psi
-Dirichlet for any
A^{\prime}\in M_{m,n}({\mathbb{Z}})
. Thus with slight abuse of notation, we denote by
\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{m,n}(\psi)\subset M_{m,n}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
the set of
\psi
-Dirichlet matrices.

Let
\psi_{1}(t)=1/t
. The problem of improving Dirichlet’s theorem was initiated by Davenport and Schmidt
[
11
,
10
]
where they showed that the set

(1.3)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{m,n}:=\bigcup_{0<c<1}\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{m,n}(c\psi_{1})

of
Dirichlet improvable matrices

is of Lebesgue measure zero, while having full Hausdorff dimension
mn
. More recently, Kleinbock and Mirzadeh
[
23
, Theorem 1.5]
showed that for any fixed
0<c<1
, the Hausdorff dimension of
\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{m,n}(c\psi_{1})
is strictly smaller than
mn
.
There have also been extensive studies on the Hausdorff dimensions of the (even smaller) set of the
singular matrices
,

\displaystyle\textbf{Sing}_{m,n}:=\bigcap_{0<c<1}\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{m,n}(c\psi_{1}).

After a series of breakthrough work, it is now known that the Hausdorff dimension of
\textbf{Sing}_{m,n}
is
mn-\tfrac{mn}{m+n}
whenever
\max\{m,n\}>1
; see
[
4
,
5
,
14
,
8
,
9
]
.

On the other hand, for a general decreasing function
\psi

with
t\mapsto t\psi(t)
increasing,
Kleinbock and Wadleigh proved a zero-one law for the Lebesgue measure of
\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{1,1}(\psi)
depending on the divergence or convergence of a certain series involving
\psi

[
26
, Theorem 1.8]
. See also
[
13
]
for the relevant dimension theory of
\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{1,1}(\psi)
,
[
24
]
for a similar zero-one law with the supremum norm replaced by the Euclidean norm and
[
27
,
16
]
for analogous results in the
inhomogeneous
setting.

The arguments in
[
26
]
rely on the theory of continued fractions and are not applicable for higher dimensions. Nevertheless, for general dimensions, building on ideas from
[
7
,
20
]
,
a dynamical approach was proposed in
[
26
, §4]
, reformulating the problem as a
shrinking target problem
, which asks whether a generic orbit in a dynamical system hits a given sequence of shrinking targets infinitely often.
To describe this dynamical interpretation, let us first fix some notation. Let
d=m+n
and let
X_{d}:=\text{SL}_{d}({\mathbb{R}})/\text{SL}_{d}({\mathbb{Z}})

be the homogeneous space which parameterizes the space of unimodular lattices in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
via
g\text{SL}_{d}({\mathbb{Z}})\leftrightarrow g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. We note that
\text{SL}_{d}({\mathbb{R}})
acts on
X_{d}
naturally via the regular action:
g\Lambda=gh{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
for any
g\in\text{SL}_{d}({\mathbb{R}})
and
\Lambda=h{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\in X_{d}
.
For any
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
, let
a_{s}
be the diagonal matrix

\displaystyle a_{s}:=\left(\begin{matrix}e^{s/m}I_{m}&0\\
0&e^{-s/n}I_{n}\end{matrix}\right)\in\text{SL}_{d}({\mathbb{R}}).

Let
\Delta:X_{d}\to[0,\infty)
be the function defined by

(1.4)

\displaystyle\Delta(\Lambda):=\sup_{\bm{v}\in\Lambda\smallsetminus\{\bm{0}\}}\log\Bigl(\frac{1}{\|\bm{v}\|}\Bigr).

Finally, let us denote

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}:=\left\{\Lambda_{A}:=\left(\begin{matrix}I_{m}&A\\
0&I_{n}\end{matrix}\right){\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\in X_{d}\>:\>A\in M_{m,n}({\mathbb{R}})\right\}.

The submanifold
\mathcal{Y}\subset X_{d}
can be naturally identified with the
mn
-dimensional torus
M_{m,n}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
via
\Lambda_{A}\leftrightarrow A\in M_{m,n}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
. Throughout the paper, we denote by
Leb
the probability Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{Y}\cong M_{m,n}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
; for simplicity of notation, for any function
f
on
{\mathcal{Y}}
we will abbreviate the space average
\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}f(\Lambda_{A})\operatorname{d}\!{\,}\textrm{Leb}(A)
by either
\textrm{Leb}(f)
or
\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}f(\Lambda_{A})\,\operatorname{d}\!{A}
.

It was shown in
[
26
, Proposition 4.5]
that for any given
\psi
as above, there exists a unique continuous function
r=r_{\psi}:[s_{0},\infty)\to(0,\infty)
such that

(1.6)

A\in M_{m,n}({\mathbb{R}})
is
not

\psi
-Dirichlet
\Leftrightarrow

a_{s}\Lambda_{A}\in\Delta^{-1}[0,r(s)]
for an unbounded set of
s>s_{0}
.

This equivalence is usually called the
Dani Correspondence
.
In view of this interpretation, our task is to find conditions which ensure that for almost every (or almost no)
A\in M_{m,n}({\mathbb{R}})
, the orbit
\{a_{s}\Lambda_{A}\}_{s>s_{0}}
hits the shrinking target
\Delta^{-1}[0,r(s)]
for an unbounded set of
s
-values. We note that this was also the strategy used in
[
20
]
giving a dynamical proof of the classical Khintchine-Groshev Theorem, where the relevant shrinking targets are certain cusp neighborhoods in
X_{d}
. For our case, by Mahler’s compactness criterion, the shrinking targets
\Delta^{-1}[0,r(s)]
are compact neighborhoods of the
critical locus
\Delta^{-1}\{0\}
,
whose explicit description is given by
Hajós’s Theorem
[
12
]
(cf. Theorem
2.3
below). The fact that these shrinking targets are compact sets causes new difficulties compared to the situation in
[
20
]
, see the discussion in Section
1.3
.

1.2.
Main results

In the present paper, based on the dynamical interpretation described above, we give sufficient conditions on
\psi
implying that
\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{m,n}(\psi)
is of zero or full Lebesgue measure. In fact, with some modifications, our arguments also work for the analogous
weighted
problem where the supremum norms in (
1.2
) are replaced by certain
weighted quasi-norms
,
as introduced in
[
17
]
.
We thus prove our main result in that generality.
We first introduce the relevant notation.

Let
\bm{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
\bm{\beta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be two
weight vectors
, that is

\displaystyle\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})\in({\mathbb{R}}_{>0})^{m}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})\in({\mathbb{R}}_{>0})^{n}

with
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=\sum_{j}\beta_{j}=1
. We say that
A\in M_{m,n}({\mathbb{R}})
is
\psi_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}
-Dirichlet
if the system of inequalities

(1.7)

\displaystyle\|A\bm{q}-\bm{p}\|_{\bm{\alpha}}<\psi(t)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\|\bm{q}\|_{\bm{\beta}}<t

has solutions in
(\bm{p},\bm{q})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}\times({\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\smallsetminus\{\bm{0}\})
for all sufficiently large
t
. Here

\displaystyle\|\bm{x}\|_{\bm{\alpha}}:=\max\left\{|x_{i}|^{1/\alpha_{i}}\>:\>1\leq i\leq m\right\}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\|\bm{y}\|_{\bm{\beta}}:=\max\left\{|y_{j}|^{1/\beta_{j}}\>:\>1\leq j\leq n\right\}

are the two quasi-norms associated with
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
respectively. Again it is easy to see that
A\in M_{m,n}({\mathbb{R}})
is
\psi_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}
-Dirichlet if and only if
A+A^{\prime}
is
\psi_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}
-Dirichlet for any
A^{\prime}\in M_{m,n}({\mathbb{Z}})
, and we denote by
\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\psi)\subset M_{m,n}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
the set of
\psi_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}
-Dirichlet matrices.
We note that when
\bm{\alpha}=(\tfrac{1}{m},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{m})
and
\bm{\beta}=(\tfrac{1}{n},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{n})
, then
\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\psi)=\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{m,n}(\psi)
.

We now state our main result which gives sufficient conditions on
\psi
determining when
\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\psi)
is of full or zero Lebesgue measure.

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix
m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and two weight vectors
\bm{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
\bm{\beta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Let
d=m+n
, and let

\displaystyle\varkappa_{d}=\frac{d^{2}+d-4}{2}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\lambda_{d}=\frac{d(d-1)}{2}.

Let
t_{0}>0
and let
\psi:[t_{0},\infty)\to{(0,\infty)}
be a continuous, decreasing function such that

(1.8)

the function
t\mapsto t\psi(t)
is increasing

and

(1.9)

\displaystyle\psi(t)<\psi_{1}(t)=1/t\quad\textrm{for all $t\geq t_{0}$}.

Let
F_{\psi}(t):=1-t\psi(t)
. If the series

(1.10)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq t_{0}}k^{-1}F_{\psi}(k)^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\left(\frac{1}{F_{\psi}(k)}\right)

converges, then
\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\psi)
is of full Lebesgue measure. Conversely, if the series (
1.10
) diverges, and

(1.11)

\displaystyle\liminf_{t_{1}\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{t_{0}\leq k\leq t_{1}}k^{-1}F_{\psi}(k)^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\lambda_{d}+1}\left(\frac{1}{F_{\psi}(k)}\right)}{\left(\sum_{t_{0}\leq k\leq t_{1}}k^{-1}F_{\psi}(k)^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\left(\frac{1}{F_{\psi}(k)}\right)\right)^{2}}=0,

then
\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\psi)
is of zero Lebesgue measure.

Remark 1
.

When
m=n=1
, Theorem
1.2
is not new; in fact
[
26
, Theorem 1.8]
is stronger in the sense that it gives a tight zero-one law without the extra assumption (
1.11
).
We believe that an analogous tight zero-one law should also hold for general dimensions
m,n
,
i.e. that Theorem
1.2
should hold
with the assumption (
1.11
) removed.
See Remark
8
for a discussion of why assumption (
1.11
) is needed in our proof.

Remark 2
.

The function
F_{\psi}(t)=1-t\psi(t)
encodes
\psi
via
\psi(t)=\frac{1-F_{\psi}(t)}{t}
. In view of the assumptions (
1.8
) and (
1.9
),
F_{\psi}
is a decreasing function and takes values in
(0,1)
. In particular, the limit
\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{\psi}(t)
exists and lies in
[0,1)
.

If
\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{\psi}(t)>0
,
then the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
follows from
the work of Kleinbock and Weiss
[
28
]
.
Indeed, in this case
the series (
1.10
) diverges,
and for any fixed
\beta
we have

\sum_{t_{0}\leq k\leq t_{1}}k^{-1}F_{\psi}(k)^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\beta}\bigl(\frac{1}{F_{\psi}(k)}\bigr)\asymp\log t_{1}

as
t_{1}\to\infty
, so that also the assumption (
1.11
) holds;
moreover,
\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{\psi}(t)>0
implies that there exists some
c\in(0,1)

such that
\psi(t)<c/t
, implying that
\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\psi)\subset\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(c\psi_{1})
; and by

[
28
, Theorem 1.4]
,
\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(c\psi_{1})
is a null set.

Remark 3
.

Let us give some explicit examples to illustrate our results.
We note that each function
\psi
appearing below is
strictly decreasing on
[t_{0},\infty)
for
t_{0}
sufficiently large.

(1)

Let
\psi(t)=\frac{1-c(\log t)^{-\tau}}{t}
(
\Leftrightarrow F_{\psi}(t)=c(\log t)^{-\tau}
) for some
c>0
and
\tau\geq 0
. In this case the series (
1.10
) diverges if and only if
\tau\leq\frac{1}{\varkappa_{d}}
. It is also easy to check that condition (
1.11
) is satisfied whenever
\tau\leq\frac{1}{\varkappa_{d}}
. Hence Theorem
1.2
implies that for such
\psi
,
\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\psi)

is of full measure if
\tau>\frac{1}{\varkappa_{d}}
,
and of zero measure if
\tau\leq\frac{1}{\varkappa_{d}}
.

(2)

Let
\psi(t)=\frac{1-c(\log t)^{-1/\varkappa_{d}}(\log\log t)^{-\tau}}{t}
(
\Leftrightarrow F_{\psi}(t)=c(\log t)^{-1/\varkappa_{d}}(\log\log t)^{-\tau}
) for some
c>0
and
\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}
. In this case the series (
1.10
) diverges if and only if
\tau\leq\frac{\lambda_{d}+1}{\varkappa_{d}}
,
while the condition (
1.11
) is satisfied if and only if
\tau<\frac{\lambda_{d}}{\varkappa_{d}}
.
Hence Theorem
1.2
implies that for such
\psi
,
\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\psi)

is of full measure if
\tau>\frac{\lambda_{d}+1}{\varkappa_{d}}
,
and of zero measure if
\tau<\frac{\lambda_{d}}{\varkappa_{d}}
.
However, for
\tau
in the range

\frac{\lambda_{d}}{\varkappa_{d}}\leq\tau\leq\frac{\lambda_{d}+1}{\varkappa_{d}}
,
Theorem
1.2
gives
no information

(although we believe that
\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\psi)

is of zero measure also for these
\tau
; cf. Remark
1
).
We point out that in the special case
\tau=\frac{\lambda_{d}}{\varkappa_{d}}
,
the quotient in (
1.11
) remains
bounded

as
t_{1}\to\infty
; in this case our method of proof allows us to conclude that
at least the set
\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\psi)
is not of full
Lebesgue measure;
see Remark
16
.

Remark 4
.

Let us also point out that the assumption (
1.9
) is imposed only to avoid making the statement of Theorem
1.2
unnecessarily complicated (since otherwise
F_{\psi}(t)
could be negative and then the series (
1.10
) is not well-defined). Indeed, if (
1.9
) fails but
\psi
satisfies the other assumptions in Theorem
1.2
, then
\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\psi)
is certainly of full Lebesgue measure. This is true since in this case after possibly enlarging
t_{0}
, we have
\psi(t)\geq 1/t\geq\frac{1-(\log t)^{-\tau}}{t}
for all
t\geq t_{0}
and
\tau\geq 0
, and therefore
\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\psi)\supset\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}\left({t\mapsto}\frac{1-(\log t)^{-\tau}}{t}\right)
, where the last set is of full Lebesgue measure whenever
\tau>\frac{1}{\varkappa_{d}}
by Remark
3
(1).

One of the main ingredients in our proof of Theorem
1.2

is a measure estimate in geometry of numbers, which we believe is of independent interest.
Let
\mu_{d}
be the unique left
\text{SL}_{d}({\mathbb{R}})
-invariant probability measure on
X_{d}=\text{SL}_{d}({\mathbb{R}})/\text{SL}_{d}({\mathbb{Z}})
.
We are interested in the sets

\Delta^{-1}[0,r]
in
X_{d}
, as
r\to 0^{+}
.
As we have discussed, these sets shrink toward the critical locus
\Delta^{-1}\{0\}

as
r\to 0^{+}
,
and by Hajós’s Theorem
[
12
]
(cf. Theorem
2.3
below),
the set
\Delta^{-1}\{0\}
has a simple explicit description as a finite union of
compact submanifolds of positive codimension
\frac{d^{2}+d}{2}-1=\varkappa_{d}+1
in
X_{d}
.
In particular this implies that
\mu_{d}\bigl(\Delta^{-1}\{0\}\bigr)=0

and
\mu_{d}\left(\Delta^{-1}[0,r]\right)\to 0
as
r\to 0^{+}
.
The following theorem gives an asymptotic estimate
on the exact rate of convergence in the limit just mentioned.

Theorem 1.3
.

We have

(1.12)

\displaystyle\mu_{d}\left(\Delta^{-1}[0,r]\right)\asymp_{d}r^{\frac{(d-1)(d+2)}{2}}\log^{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{r}\Bigr)=r^{\varkappa_{d}+1}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{r}\Bigr),\qquad\textrm{as $r\to 0^{+}$}.

Our proof of Theorem
1.3
proceeds
by bounding the sets
\Delta^{-1}[0,r]
from above and below by
more explicit sets whose Haar measure we can estimate directly.
In the proof of the upper bound we make crucial use of Hajós’s Theorem.
We remark that
Hajós’s proof (from 1941) of the theorem,
which settled a conjecture
of Minkowski from 1896,
is surprisingly complicated, with the first step being a translation of the question
into an algebraic statement about factorizations of finite abelian groups
(see also
[
36
]
for a nice presentation).
It seems difficult to extend this proof in any direct way
from the case of

\Delta^{-1}\{0\}

to deduce restrictions on the sets

\Delta^{-1}[0,r]
which are sufficiently strong to imply the desired upper bound on

\mu_{d}\left(\Delta^{-1}[0,r]\right)
.
Instead we apply Hajós’s Theorem, in
combination with a compactness argument,
to obtain a convenient containment relation for

\Delta^{-1}[0,r]
valid for
all
sufficiently small
r

(see Lemma
4.5
and Remark
11
).
This initial restriction
serves as the starting point for our analysis where we use direct, geometric arguments to derive further,
r
-dependent restrictions
on
\Delta^{-1}[0,r]
for
r
small,
strong enough to finally imply the desired upper bound
on
\mu_{d}\left(\Delta^{-1}[0,r]\right)
.

Remark 5
.

In the case
d=2
, the following explicit formula holds

[
37
, p. 74]
:

\displaystyle\mu_{2}\left(\Delta^{-1}[0,r]\right)=\begin{cases}\frac{12}{\pi^{2}}\bigl(e^{-2r}+2r-1+\Psi(e^{-2r})\bigr)&\text{if }\>0\leq r\leq\frac{1}{2}\log 2,\\[3.0pt]
1-\frac{12}{\pi^{2}}e^{-2r}&\text{if }\>r\geq\frac{1}{2}\log 2,\end{cases}

where the function
\Psi:(0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
is defined by
\Psi(1)=0

and
\Psi^{\prime}(x)=(x^{-1}-1)\log(x^{-1}-1)
.
It follows that in this case we have an explicit asymptotic expansion sharpening (
1.12
):

\displaystyle\mu_{2}\left(\Delta^{-1}[0,r]\right)\sim\frac{24}{\pi^{2}}r^{2}\log\Bigl(\frac{1}{r}\Bigr)+\frac{12(3-2\log 2)}{\pi^{2}}r^{2}-\frac{16}{\pi^{2}}r^{3}\log\Bigl(\frac{1}{r}\Bigr)+\cdots\qquad\text{as }\>r\to 0^{+}.

The explicit formula for

\mu_{2}\left(\Delta^{-1}[0,r]\right)
,
stated in a different notation, was independently obtained in
[
29
]
using a different method.

Remark 6
.

Theorem
1.3
is also relevant for the study of the Hausdorff dimension of the set
\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{m,n}(c\psi_{1})
.
As we have mentioned,
Kleinbock and Mirzadeh
recently proved that the Hausdorff dimension of
\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{m,n}(c\psi_{1})
is less than
mn

for every
0<c<1

[
23
, Theorem 1.5]
.
They derived this as an application of their main result,

[
23
, Theorem 1.2]
, which gives
an explicit upper bound on the Hausdorff dimension of a certain kind of dynamically defined subsets in the space
X_{d}
.
It seems that by using
Theorem
1.3
(cf. also Theorem
5.1
below),
together with a further analysis of the quantities appearing in

[
23
, Theorem 1.2]
,
it should be possible to sharpen the conclusion of
[
23
, Theorem 1.5]

into a bound of the form

\displaystyle\dim_{H}\bigl(\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}_{m,n}(c\psi_{1})\bigr)<mn-\delta(1-c)^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\lambda_{d}-1}\bigl((1-c)^{-1}\bigr)

for all
c<1
sufficiently near
1
, where

d=m+n
and
\delta>0
is a constant which only depends on
m,n
.

1.3.
Discussion of the proof of Theorem
1.2

We next give a more detailed outline of our proof of Theorem
1.2
.
For simplicity of presentation, we will only focus on the special case when
\bm{\alpha}=(\tfrac{1}{m},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{m})
and
\bm{\beta}=(\tfrac{1}{n},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{n})
;
we comment in Remark
9
below on the modifications needed to treat general weights.

We start from the Dani Correspondence, (
1.6
), and discretize the
shrinking target problem which appears there by introducing the following thickened targets:

\displaystyle B_{k}:=\bigcup_{0\leq s<1}a_{-s}\Delta^{-1}[0,r(k+s)],\qquad\textrm{for any integer}\ k>s_{0}.

It follows from this definition that for any
\Lambda\in X_{d}
,
a_{k}\Lambda\in B_{k}
if and only if
a_{s}\Lambda\in\Delta^{-1}[0,r(s)]
for some
k\leq s<k+1
. In particular, by (
1.6
),

A\in M_{m,n}({\mathbb{R}})
is not
\psi
-Dirichlet
if and only if
a_{k}\Lambda_{A}\in B_{k}
for infinitely many integers
k
. For any
k>s_{0}
let us define

\displaystyle E_{k}:=\left\{\Lambda_{A}\in{\mathcal{Y}}\>:\>a_{k}\Lambda_{A}\in B_{k}\right\}.

Then, in view of the previous discussion and the identification

\mathcal{Y}\cong M_{m,n}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
,
we have

\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}^{c}_{m,n}(\psi)=\limsup_{k\to\infty}E_{k}.

Hence, by the Borel-Cantelli lemma,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{k}\textrm{Leb}(E_{k})<\infty&\Longrightarrow\textrm{Leb}\left(\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}^{c}_{m,n}(\psi)\right)=0;\\
\sum_{k}\textrm{Leb}(E_{k})=\infty\ \&\ \textrm{``quasi-independence conditions''}&\Longrightarrow\textrm{Leb}\left(\operatorname{\mathbf{DI}}^{c}_{m,n}(\psi)\right)=1.\end{array}\right.

We thus need to understand when the sum
\sum_{k}\textrm{Leb}(E_{k})
diverges or converges, respectively.
It follows from our definitions that

\textrm{Leb}(E_{k})=\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\chi_{B_{k}}(a_{k}\Lambda_{A})\,\operatorname{d}\!{A}.

It is well known that the
a_{s}
-translates
a_{s}{\mathcal{Y}}
equidistribute in
X_{d}
as
s\to\infty
;
since our shrinking target
B_{k}
varies in the parameter
k
, we require an
effective
version of
this fact. Such a result was first proved by Kleinbock and Margulis
[
19
, Proposition 2.4.8]
building on Margulis’s original thickening argument
[
31
]
and the exponential mixing of the diagonal flow
\{a_{s}\}_{s\in{\mathbb{R}}}
.
Here we use the following explicit version (see Corollary
6.4
below):
there exists
\delta>0

such that for any
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X_{d})
and any
s>0
,

(1.13)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}f(a_{s}\Lambda_{A})\,\operatorname{d}\!{A}=\mu_{d}(f)+O\big(e^{-\delta s}{\mathcal{N}}(f)\big),

where the norm
{\mathcal{N}}(\cdot)
is the maximum of a Lipschitz norm and a Sobolev
L^{2}
-norm (see Section
6.2
).
By approximating
\{\chi_{B_{k}}\}_{k>s_{0}}
from above and below by smooth functions
and applying (
1.13
) together with an
explicit bound on the norm
{\mathcal{N}}(\cdot)
(see Lemma
6.5
),
it follows that (see Lemma
7.1
)

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\textrm{Leb}(E_{k})=\infty\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\sum_{k}\mu_{d}\left(B_{k}\right)=\infty.

Furthermore, it is not difficult to see from Theorem
1.3
that the series

\sum_{k}\mu_{d}\left(B_{k}\right)
diverges
if and only if the series in (
1.10
) diverges (see Theorem
5.1
and Lemma
6.1
).
This in particular settles the convergence case of Theorem
1.2
.

For the divergence case, in addition to the assumption that the series in (
1.10
) diverges (which implies that
\sum_{k}\textrm{Leb}(E_{k})=\infty
), one also needs to establish a certain
quasi-independence
condition, see (
7.8
). Roughly speaking, we need to show that the quantities

\left|\textrm{Leb}\left(E_{i}\cap E_{j}\right)-\textrm{Leb}(E_{i})\textrm{Leb}(E_{j})\right|,\qquad i\neq j>s_{0}

are small on average. Here note that

\displaystyle\textrm{Leb}\left(E_{i}\cap E_{j}\right)=\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\chi_{B_{i}}(a_{i}\Lambda_{A})\chi_{B_{j}}(a_{j}\Lambda_{A})\,\operatorname{d}\!{A}.

We now apply the
effective doubly mixing
for the
a_{s}
-translates
\{a_{s}{\mathcal{Y}}\}_{s>0}
.
This result is due to Kleinbock-Shi-Weiss
[
25
, Theorem 1.2]
; we use a more explicit version due to
Björklund-Gorodnik
[
2
, Corollary 2.4]
which
states that for any
f_{1},f_{2}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X_{d})
and any
s_{1},s_{2}>0
,

(1.14)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}f_{1}(a_{s_{1}}\Lambda_{A})f_{2}(a_{s_{2}}\Lambda_{A})\,\operatorname{d}\!{A}=\mu_{d}(f_{1})\mu_{d}(f_{2})+O\left(e^{-\delta D(s_{1},s_{2})}\mathcal{N}(f_{1}){\mathcal{N}}(f_{2})\right),

where
D(s_{1},s_{2}):=\min\{s_{1},s_{2},|s_{1}-s_{2}|\}
.
Combining this result with (
1.13
) we get

\displaystyle\left|\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}f_{1}(a_{s_{1}}\Lambda_{A})f_{2}(a_{s_{2}}\Lambda_{A})\,\operatorname{d}\!{A}-\textrm{Leb}(f_{1})\textrm{Leb}(f_{2})\right|\ll e^{-\delta D(s_{1},s_{2})}\prod_{i=1}^{2}\max\left\{\mathcal{N}(f_{i}),\mu_{d}(f_{i})\right\}.

Finally, by approximating
\{\chi_{B_{k}}\}_{k>s_{0}}
from below by smooth functions, applying the above estimate (together with a trivial estimate when
D(s_{1},s_{2})
is small, see (
7.13
)) and the bounds on the norm
\mathcal{N}(\cdot)
(see Lemma
6.5
),
we show that the divergence of the series in (
1.10
)
together with the additional technical assumption (
1.11
), implies that
the required quasi-independence condition (
7.8
) is satisfied,
thus concluding the proof of
the divergence case of Theorem
1.2
.

We end our discussion with a few remarks.

Remark 7
.

Our argument should be compared to that of Kleinbock and Margulis
[
20
]
,
where the shrinking targets are certain cusp neighborhoods:
In
[
20
]
the relevant shrinking target problem
is first solved for the case of
a_{s}
-orbits starting at
\mu_{d}
-generic
points in the ambient space
X_{d}
;
for this task it suffices to use,
in place of (
1.13
) and (
1.14
)
respectively, the invariance of the measure
\mu_{d}

and the exponential mixing of the
a_{s}
-flow.
Then by an approximation argument
[
20
, §8.7]
,
the shrinking target property for
\mu_{d}
-generic points in
X_{d}

is shown to imply the same property for generic points in the submanifold
{\mathcal{Y}}
.
A key observation in this approximation step
is that
all
shrinking targets, by virtue of being cusp neighborhoods,
remain essentially unaffected by perturbations
from a
fixed
neighborhood of the identity in the
neutral leaf of the
a_{s}
-flow, i.e. the centralizer of the
a_{s}
-flow in
\text{SL}_{d}({\mathbb{R}})
.
This, however, is no longer
the case in our setting, with the shrinking targets being compact sets. This is why we take the more direct approach using effective equidistribution and doubly mixing of the
a_{s}
-translates
of
{\mathcal{Y}}
, that is, (
1.13
) and (
1.14
).

One potential advantage of this more direct approach is that
if (
1.13
) could be refined by
replacing the measure
Leb
by
a natural measure on some
submanifold
of
{\mathcal{Y}}
,
then by mimicking our analysis,
one could establish the
\psi
-Dirichlet property for generic points in that submanifold,
for any
\psi
such that (
1.10
) converges.
See Remark
14
below for a discussion
of the application along these lines
of a recent effective equidistribution result obtained by Chow and Yang
[
6
]
.

We note that the use of equidistribution of
a_{s}
-translates of
{\mathcal{Y}}

in the study of the Dirichlet improvability problem is not new;
it has been applied several times in the more well-studied setting of
Dirichlet improvable vectors and matrices.
For
\min\{m,n\}=1
and
{\mathcal{I}}\subset{\mathcal{Y}}
being an analytic curve in
{\mathcal{Y}}
satisfying certain explicit conditions,
Shah
[
33
, Theorem 1.2]
proved that the
a_{s}
-translates of
{\mathcal{I}}
equidistribute in
X_{d}
with respect to
\mu_{d}

as
s\to\infty
.
Shah’s proof relies on Ratner’s classification of measures invariant under unipotent flows

[
32
]
,
and his equidistribution theorem is not effective;
still it suffices for the deduction of the fact that generic points on the curve
{\mathcal{I}}

are Dirichlet non-improvable, that is, lie outside of the set (
1.3
).
(This is so since in this case, the relevant “shrinking” target is in fact a
fixed
set of positive measure.)
Shah’s results have been generalized and strengthened in various directions
[
34
,
35
,
38
,
18
]
. In a recent breakthrough of Khalil and Luethi
[
15
]
, the authors refined (
1.13
) (for the case when
n=1
) by replacing
Leb
with a certain fractal measure, from which they deduced a complete analogue of Khintchine’s theorem with respect to this fractal measure.

Remark 8
.

Another difficulty, which also stems from the fact that our targets are shrinking compact sets,
is the fact that the norm
{\mathcal{N}}(\cdot)

unavoidably grows (polynomially) for the smooth functions
approximating the shrinking targets from above and below
(see Lemma
6.5
).
While the impact of this polynomial growth of the norm can be eliminated in the convergence case due to the exponential decay in the parameter
s
(i.e. the factor
e^{-\delta s}
in the error term in (
1.13
)), it causes serious problems in the divergence case,
and this is exactly why we need to impose the extra assumption (
1.11
).
Let us here also note that this assumption (
1.11
) can be rephrased in terms of the measure of the shrinking targets
as follows:

\displaystyle\liminf_{s_{1}\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{s_{0}<k\leq s_{1}}\mu_{d}(B_{k})\log\left(\tfrac{1}{\mu_{d}(B_{k})}\right)}{\left(\sum_{s_{0}<k\leq s_{1}}\mu_{d}(B_{k})\right)^{2}}=0.

Remark 9
.

In order to extend the argument outlined above to the case of
general weight vectors
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
, we have to consider a more general one-parameter flow

\{g_{s}\}_{s>0}\subset\text{SL}_{d}({\mathbb{R}})
associated to
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
(see (
5.1
)),
and use a dynamical interpretation of
\psi_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}
-Dirichlet matrices
which involves this
g_{s}
-flow
and generalizes (
1.6
);
see Proposition
6.2
and Remark
13
.
We therefore need analogous effective equidistribution and doubly mixing results for the
g_{s}
-translates of
{\mathcal{Y}}
.
Fortunately, such more general (and considerably more difficult) effective results are known to hold, thanks to the work of Kleinbock-Margulis
[
21
, Theorem 1.3]
and, again, Kleinbock-Shi-Weiss
[
25
, Theorem 1.2]
and Björklund-Gorodnik
[
2
, Corollary 2.4]

(see Theorem
6.3
below).
In fact in
[
2
]
a uniform treatment was given proving effective mixing of arbitrary order for
the
g_{s}
-translates of
{\mathcal{Y}}
;
however we will not make use of this.

Notation and conventions

Throughout the paper, the notation
\|\cdot\|
denotes the supremum norm on various Euclidean spaces or matrix spaces (which can be viewed as Euclidean spaces on the matrix entries). Let
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be an interval (not necessarily bounded). A function
f:I\to{\mathbb{R}}
is called
increasing
(resp.
decreasing
) if
f(t_{1})\leq f(t_{2})
(resp.
f(t_{1})\geq f(t_{2})
) whenever
t_{1}<t_{2}
. All the vectors in this paper are column vectors. For two positive quantities
A
and
B
, we will use the notation
A\ll B
or
A=O(B)
to mean that there is a constant
c>0
such that
A\leq cB
, and we will use subscripts to indicate the dependence of the constant on parameters. We will write
A\asymp B
for
A\ll B\ll A
.

2.
Some preliminaries for Theorem
1.3

Fix an integer
d\geq 2
. In what follows we always denote
G=\text{SL}_{d}({\mathbb{R}})
,
\Gamma=\text{SL}_{d}({\mathbb{Z}})
and
X_{d}=G/\Gamma
the space of unimodular lattices in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Let
\mu_{d}
be the unique
G
-invariant probability measure on
X_{d}
. Let
\Delta:X_{d}\to[0,\infty)
be the function on
X_{d}
defined as in (
1.4
). In this section, we collect some preliminary results for our proof of Theorem
1.3
. In fact, for simplicity of presentation we will prove an equivalent measure estimate result. For any
r\in[0,1)
let
\mathcal{C}_{r}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be the open “
{(1-r)}
-cube”, i.e.

\mathcal{C}_{r}:=(r-1,1-r)^{d}.

Let
K_{r}\subset X_{d}
be the set of unimodular lattices having no nonzero points in
\mathcal{C}_{r}
, i.e.

K_{r}:=\big\{\Lambda\in X_{d}\>:\>\Lambda\cap\mathcal{C}_{r}=\{\bm{0}\}\big\}.

We note that by definition of
\Delta
,
K_{r}=\Delta^{-1}[0,-\log\left(1-r\right)]
, or equivalently,
\Delta^{-1}[0,r]=K_{1-e^{-r}}
. Since
1-e^{-r}=r+O(r^{2})\asymp r
for all
r\in(0,1)
, Theorem
1.3

can be equivalently restated as follows.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\varkappa_{d}=\frac{d^{2}+d-4}{2}
and
\lambda_{d}=\frac{d(d-1)}{2}
be as in Theorem
1.2
. Then

\displaystyle\mu_{d}\left(K_{r}\right)\asymp_{d}r^{\varkappa_{d}+1}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{r}\Bigr),\qquad\textrm{as $r\to 0^{+}$}.

We will prove Theorem
2.1
by proving a lower bound and an upper bound separately.

2.1.
Haar measure and coordinates

Let
P<G
be the maximal parabolic subgroup fixing the line spanned by
\bm{e}_{d}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and let
N<G
be the transpose of the unipotent radical of
P
. Here and hereafter,
\{{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}:1\leq i\leq d\}
denotes the standard orthonormal basis of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Explicitly,

\displaystyle P=\{p\in G\>:\>p\bm{e}_{d}=t\bm{e}_{d}\ \textrm{for some $t\neq 0$}\},

and

\displaystyle N=\left\{u_{\bm{x}}:=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}I_{d-1}&\bm{x}\\
\bm{0}^{t}&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\>:\>\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}\right\}.

For any
p\in P
, let
{\text{\boldmath$b$}}_{1}:=p\bm{e}_{1},\ldots,\bm{b}_{d-1}:=p\bm{e}_{d-1}
be the first
d-1
column vectors of
p
. We note that
p
is uniquely determined by
\bm{b}_{1},\ldots,\bm{b}_{d-1}
; we will sometimes denote
p\in P
by
p_{\bm{b}_{1},\ldots,\bm{b}_{d-1}}
to indicate this dependence.
For any
g\in G
, let us denote by
\tilde{g}\in M_{d-1,d-1}({\mathbb{R}})
the top left
(d-1)\times(d-1)
block of
g
. If
\det\tilde{g}\neq 0
, then
g
can be written uniquely as a product

(2.1)

\displaystyle g=p_{\bm{b}_{1},\ldots,\bm{b}_{d-1}}u_{\bm{x}}\quad{\text{for some $p_{\bm{b}_{1},\ldots,\bm{b}_{d-1}}\in P$ and }u_{\bm{x}}\in N}.

Let
\nu
be the (left and right) Haar measure on
G
, normalized so that it agrees locally with
\mu_{d}
.
In terms of the coordinates in (
2.1
),
\nu
is given by

(2.2)

\operatorname{d}\!{\nu}(g)=\frac{1}{\zeta(2)\cdots\zeta(d)}\,\operatorname{d}\!{{\text{\boldmath$x$}}}\prod_{1\leq i\leq d-1}\operatorname{d}\!{{\text{\boldmath$b$}}}_{i},

where
\zeta(\cdot)
is the Riemann zeta function,
and where
\operatorname{d}\!{{\text{\boldmath$x$}}}
and
\operatorname{d}\!{{\text{\boldmath$b$}}}_{i}
denote Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
respectively.
For later purpose, we also note that the lattice
\Lambda
represented by
p_{\bm{b}_{1},\ldots,\bm{b}_{d-1}}u_{\bm{x}}
, i.e.
\Lambda=p_{\bm{b}_{1},\ldots,\bm{b}_{d-1}}u_{\bm{x}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, has a basis

\Lambda={\mathbb{Z}}\bm{b}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}\bm{b}_{d-1}\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}\bm{b}_{d},

where
\bm{b}_{d}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}x_{j}\bm{b}_{j}+(\det\tilde{p})^{-1}\bm{e}_{d}
is the
d
-th column vector of the matrix
p_{\bm{b}_{1},\ldots,\bm{b}_{d-1}}u_{\bm{x}}
. Here
\tilde{p}
is the top left
(d-1)\times(d-1)
block of
p_{\bm{b}_{1},\ldots,\bm{b}_{d-1}}
.

For our computation of the upper bounds, it will be more convenient to use another set of coordinates: For any
g=(g_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\in G
with
\det\tilde{g}\neq 0
, as mentioned above, we can write
g
uniquely as in (
2.1
).
It is clear from this relation that
g
and
p_{\bm{b}_{1},\ldots,\bm{b}_{d-1}}
share the same first
d-1
column vectors, i.e.
g\bm{e}_{j}=\bm{b}_{j}
for all
1\leq j\leq d-1
. Moreover, as noted above, for the
d
-th column vector we have

\displaystyle g\bm{e}_{d}=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}x_{j}\bm{b}_{j}+(\det\tilde{p})^{-1}\bm{e}_{d}=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}x_{j}\left(g\bm{e}_{j}\right)+(\det\tilde{g})^{-1}\bm{e}_{d}.

In particular, we have
(g_{1d},\ldots,g_{d-1,d})^{t}=\tilde{g}\bm{x}
, which further implies

\operatorname{d}\!{{\text{\boldmath$x$}}}=(\det\tilde{g})^{-1}\prod_{1\leq i\leq d-1}\operatorname{d}\!{g}_{id}.

This relation, together with the relations
g\bm{e}_{j}=\bm{b}_{j},1\leq j\leq d-1
and the Haar measure description (
2.2
),
immediately implies the following:

Lemma 2.2
.

For any (Borel) subset
\mathcal{K}
of
\bigl\{g\in G\>:\>|\det\tilde{g}-1|<\frac{1}{2}\bigr\}
,
we have

(2.3)

\displaystyle\nu(\mathcal{K})\asymp_{d}\int_{\mathcal{K}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i,j\leq d\\
(i,j)\neq(d,d)\end{subarray}}\operatorname{d}\!{g}_{ij}.

2.2.
A small parameter for the lower bound

To prove the lower bound, we will construct a subset of
K_{r}
whose measure is of the same magnitude as
K_{r}
. For a lattice
\Lambda=g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\in X_{d}
, to show
\Lambda\in K_{r}
, by definition one needs to show
g\bm{m}\notin\mathcal{C}_{r}
for all nonzero
\bm{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. If
g\in G
is sufficiently close to the identity element
I_{d}\in G
, so that
\Lambda
has a basis close to the standard basis
\{\bm{e}_{i}:1\leq i\leq d\}
, then one only needs to consider vectors
\bm{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
with small supremum norms. For this reason, we will only focus on lattices that are close to
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.
Recall that the set
K_{r}
certainly does not get concentrated near the lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
as
r\to 0^{+}
;
indeed, we have
\cap_{r>0}K_{r}=K_{0}=\Delta^{-1}\{0\}
,
which as we have mentioned is a finite union of compact submanifolds of positive codimension
\varkappa_{d}+1
in
X_{d}

(see also Section
2.3
).
The fact that it still suffices to consider a small neighborhood of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
when proving the
lower
bound
in Theorem
2.1

is related to the fact that the
mass

of
K_{r}
(with respect to
\mu_{d}
)
becomes concentrated near the lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
as
r\to 0^{+}
;
see Remark
10
.

Explicitly, we fix a small norm ball in
X_{d}
around
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
as follows:
For any
c>0
, let

(2.4)

\displaystyle G_{c}:=\left\{g\in G\>:\>\|g-I_{d}\|<c\right\}

be the open ball in
G
of radius
c
, centered at
I_{d}
with respect to the supremum norm on
M_{d,d}({\mathbb{R}})
. Let
\pi:G\to X_{d}
be the natural projection from
G
to
X_{d}
.
We fix a parameter
c_{0}\in(0,1)
(which only depends on
d
)
so small that
\pi|_{G_{c_{0}}}
is injective
and, for any vectors
{\text{\boldmath$b$}}_{1},\ldots,{\text{\boldmath$b$}}_{d}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
satisfying

\|{\text{\boldmath$b$}}_{i}-{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}\|<c_{0}
for all
1\leq i\leq d
,
every hyperplane of the form

\displaystyle m{\text{\boldmath$b$}}_{j}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell\leq d\\
\ell\neq j\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{R}}{\text{\boldmath$b$}}_{\ell}

with
m\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
|m|\geq 2
and
1\leq j\leq d
,
is disjoint from the cube
[-1,1]^{d}
.
In particular, if
\Lambda=g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
for some
g\in G_{c_{0}}
, then
\Lambda
has a basis
\{g\bm{e}_{i}:1\leq i\leq d\}
satisfying
\|g\bm{e}_{i}-\bm{e}_{i}\|\leq c_{0}
for all
1\leq i\leq d
.
It follows that in order to prove that
\Lambda\in K_{r}
for a given
r\in(0,1)
,
it suffices to verify that
\sum_{1\leq i\leq d}m_{i}\left(g\bm{e}_{i}\right)\notin\mathcal{C}_{r}
for all

\bm{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{d})\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d}\smallsetminus\{\bm{0}\}
.

2.3.
Hajós’s Theorem and its consequences

Recall that

K_{0}=\left\{\Lambda\in X_{d}\>:\>\Lambda\cap(-1,1)^{d}=\{\bm{0}\}\right\}.

As we mentioned in the introduction, the explicit description of
K_{0}
was conjectured
(and proved in two and three dimensions) by Minkowski,
and proved in full generality by Hajós in 1941
[
12
]
:

Theorem 2.3

(Hajós)
.

Let
U
be the subgroup of upper triangular unipotent matrices in
G
. Let
W
be the subgroup of permutation matrices in
\text{GL}_{d}({\mathbb{Z}})
. Then

\displaystyle K_{0}=\bigcup_{w\in W}\left(wUw^{-1}\right){\mathbb{Z}}^{d}.

If we set

(2.5)

\displaystyle U_{0}=\left\{(u_{ij})\in U\>:\>-\tfrac{1}{2}<u_{ij}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\text{ for all }1\leq i<j\leq d\right\}

so that
U_{0}
is a fundamental domain for
U/(\Gamma\cap U)
,
then we have the following immediate corollary of Hajós’s Theorem.

Corollary 2.4
.

Given any
\Lambda\in K_{0}
, there exist
w\in W
and
u\in U_{0}
such that
\Lambda=wu{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}=wuw^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.

Proof.

Since
\Lambda\in K_{0}
, by Theorem
2.3
we can find
u^{\prime}\in U
and
w\in W
such that

\Lambda=wu^{\prime}w^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
; but since
w^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}={\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, we have

\Lambda=wu^{\prime}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. Now using the fact that
U_{0}
is a
fundamental domain for

U/(\Gamma\cap U)
,
we can then find
u\in U_{0}
such that

u{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}=u^{\prime}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. Thus
\Lambda=wu^{\prime}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}=wu{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}=wuw^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, finishing the proof.
∎

There is a geometric interpretation of
K_{0}
in terms of
lattice tilings by unit cubes

[
36
, Ch. 1.4]
:
Let us write
\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{C}_{0}=(-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})^{d}

for the unit cube obtained by dilating
\mathcal{C}_{0}
by a factor
\tfrac{1}{2}
.
Then for any
\Lambda\in X_{d}
,
the family of cubes

\bm{v}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{C}_{0}
, with
\bm{v}
running through the lattice
\Lambda
,
forms a tiling of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(modulo a null set) if and only if
\Lambda\in K_{0}
.
More generally, for any
r\in[0,1)
we write
\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}=\bigl(\frac{1}{2}(r-1),\frac{1}{2}(1-r)\bigr)^{d}
.
Then for any
\Lambda\in X_{d}
,
the condition
\Lambda\in K_{r}
, i.e.
\Lambda\cap\mathcal{C}_{r}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
,
is equivalent to the condition that the cubes

{\text{\boldmath$v$}}+\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
(
\bm{v}\in\Lambda
)
are
pairwise disjoint
.
When this holds, we write

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{C}}_{\Lambda,r}:=\Lambda+\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}

for the union of these disjoint cubes.
This set is used in the statement of the following simple bound,
which is of crucial importance in our proof of the upper bound
in Theorem
2.1
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\Lambda\in X_{d}
and
r\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
be such that
\Lambda\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{r}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
,
and let
{\mathcal{U}}
be a Borel subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

which is disjoint from
{\mathfrak{C}}_{\Lambda,r}
and
which is contained in some translate of the cube
(0,\frac{3}{4})^{d}
.
Then
\operatorname{vol}({\mathcal{U}})<dr
.

Proof.

The set
{\mathfrak{C}}_{\Lambda,r}
is invariant under translation by any vector in
\Lambda
,
and if
{\mathcal{F}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is any fundamental domain for
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/\Lambda
,
then

\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}({\mathcal{F}}\cap{\mathfrak{C}}_{\Lambda,r})=\sum_{{\text{\boldmath$v$}}\in\Lambda}\operatorname{vol}\left({\mathcal{F}}\cap({\text{\boldmath$v$}}+\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r})\right)=\sum_{{\text{\boldmath$v$}}\in\Lambda}\operatorname{vol}\left(({\mathcal{F}}-{\text{\boldmath$v$}})+\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}\right)=\operatorname{vol}(\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r})=(1-r)^{d},

where the first equality holds since the cubes
{\text{\boldmath$v$}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
(
{\text{\boldmath$v$}}\in\Lambda
) are pairwise disjoint,
and the third equality holds since the sets
{\mathcal{F}}-{\text{\boldmath$v$}}
(
{\text{\boldmath$v$}}\in\Lambda
)
form a partition of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Hence

\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}({\mathcal{F}}\smallsetminus{\mathfrak{C}}_{\Lambda,r})=1-(1-r)^{d}<dr.

Next, since
{\mathcal{U}}
is contained in a translate of
(0,\frac{3}{4})^{d}
,
the difference between any two vectors in
{\mathcal{U}}
lies in
(-\frac{3}{4},\frac{3}{4})^{d}={\mathcal{C}}_{1/4}\subset{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
,
and since
\Lambda\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{r}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
,
this implies that the points in
{\mathcal{U}}
are pairwise inequivalent modulo
\Lambda
.
Hence the set
\big({\mathcal{F}}\smallsetminus(\Lambda+{\mathcal{U}})\big)\cup{\mathcal{U}}
is another fundamental domain for
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/\Lambda
, and it contains
{\mathcal{U}}
. After replacing
{\mathcal{F}}
by this set, we have
{\mathcal{U}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
; thus
{\mathcal{U}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}\smallsetminus{\mathfrak{C}}_{\Lambda,r}
,
and hence
\operatorname{vol}({\mathcal{U}})\leq\operatorname{vol}({\mathcal{F}}\smallsetminus{\mathfrak{C}}_{\Lambda,r})<dr
.
∎

3.
Proof of the lower bound

We keep the notation introduced in Section
2
.
In this section we prove the lower bound in Theorem
2.1
.
We will do this by constructing, for every sufficiently small
r
,
an explicit subset
\underline{K}_{r}\subset X_{d}
which we will show satisfies

\displaystyle\underline{K}_{r}\subset K_{r}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\mu_{d}(\underline{K}_{r})\gg_{d}r^{\varkappa_{d}+1}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{r}\Bigr).

We start by giving
a family of conditions which ensures that a given lattice is contained in
K_{r}
.
Recall from Section
2.2
that

c_{0}\in(0,1)
is a small fixed parameter
with the property that for any

g\in G_{c_{0}}
and
0<r<1
, we have
g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\in K_{r}
if and only if

\sum_{1\leq j\leq d}m_{j}\left(g\bm{e}_{j}\right)\notin\mathcal{C}_{r}
for all
\bm{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{d})^{t}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d}\smallsetminus\{\bm{0}\}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
c_{0}\in(0,1)
be as above,
let
r\in(0,c_{0}/d)
and let
\Lambda=p_{\bm{b}_{1},\dots,\bm{b}_{d-1}}u_{\bm{x}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\in X_{d}
with

\bm{b}_{j}=(b_{1j},\ldots,b_{dj})^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(
j=1,\ldots,d-1
)
and
\bm{x}\in(0,c_{0}/d)^{d-1}
.
Let
\tilde{p}=(b_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq d-1}
be the top left
(d-1)\times(d-1)
block of
p_{\bm{b}_{1},\dots,\bm{b}_{d-1}}
as before. Suppose
\bm{b}_{1},\ldots,\bm{b}_{d-1},\bm{x}
further satisfies

(3.1)

\displaystyle b_{ij},-b_{ji}\in(-c_{0},0),\ \forall\ 1\leq j<i\leq d-1;\ b_{d\ell}\in(-c_{0},0),b_{\ell\ell}\in(1-r,1),\ \forall\ 1\leq\ell\leq d-1,

(3.2)

\displaystyle b_{ij}<\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}b_{ik},\qquad\forall\ 2\leq j<i\leq d,

(3.3)

\displaystyle 1-r<\det\tilde{p}<(1-r)^{-1}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}|b_{dj}|x_{j}<(\det\tilde{p})^{-1}-(1-r),

and

(3.4)

\displaystyle b_{ii}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}b_{ij}x_{j}>1-r,\qquad\forall\ 1\leq i\leq d-1.

Then
\Lambda\in K_{r}
.

Proof.

Let us set
g:=p_{\bm{b}_{1},\dots,\bm{b}_{d-1}}u_{\bm{x}}

and
{\text{\boldmath$b$}}_{d}=(b_{1d},\ldots,b_{dd})^{t}:=g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{d}
;
then
{\text{\boldmath$b$}}_{j}=g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{j}
for all
1\leq j\leq d
,
and
\bm{b}_{d}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}x_{j}\bm{b}_{j}+(\det\tilde{p})^{-1}\bm{e}_{d}
;
in particular
b_{id}=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}b_{ij}x_{j}
for
1\leq i\leq d-1
.
We will start by proving that
g\in G_{c_{0}}
, i.e.
\|\bm{b}_{j}-\bm{e}_{j}\|<c_{0}
for all
1\leq j\leq d
.
In fact, if
1\leq j\leq d-1
then
\|\bm{b}_{j}-\bm{e}_{j}\|<c_{0}

is an immediate consequence of
(
3.1
) and
0<r<c_{0}/d<c_{0}
;
thus it remains to show
\|\bm{b}_{d}-\bm{e}_{d}\|<c_{0}
.
For each
1\leq i\leq d-1
we have

\displaystyle\left|b_{id}\right|=\Biggl|\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}b_{ij}x_{j}\Biggr|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\left|b_{ij}\right|\left|x_{j}\right|<\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\frac{c_{0}}{d}<c_{0},

where for the second inequality we used
the assumption that
\bm{x}\in(0,c_{0}/d)^{d-1}

and the fact that

|b_{ij}|<1
for all
1\leq i,j\leq d-1
, which is immediate from (
3.1
).
It remains to prove that
1-c_{0}<b_{dd}<1+c_{0}
.
In fact, we have the following stronger bound:

(3.5)

\displaystyle 1-r<b_{dd}<1+c_{0}.

Indeed, using
b_{dj}<0
and
x_{j}>0
(
1\leq j\leq d-1
)
in combination with (
3.3
), we get

\displaystyle b_{dd}=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}b_{dj}x_{j}+(\det\tilde{p})^{-1}=(\det\tilde{p})^{-1}-\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\left|b_{dj}\right|x_{j}>1-r

as well as

\displaystyle b_{dd}=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}b_{dj}x_{j}+(\det\tilde{p})^{-1}<(\det\tilde{p})^{-1}<(1-r)^{-1}<1+2r\leq 1+c_{0}.

(For the second to last inequality
we used the fact that
0<r<c_{0}/d<1/2
.)
This finishes the proof of (
3.5
), and hence
g\in G_{c_{0}}
.

Because of
g\in G_{c_{0}}
,
in order to show
\Lambda\in K_{r}
,
it suffices to prove that
g\bm{m}=\sum_{1\leq j\leq d}m_{j}\bm{b}_{j}\notin\mathcal{C}_{r}
for all
\bm{m}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d}\smallsetminus\{\bm{0}\}
.
Thus we now let the vector
\bm{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{d})^{t}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d}\smallsetminus\{\bm{0}\}
be given,
and show that
g\bm{m}\notin\mathcal{C}_{r}
.

First assume that all the nonzero entries of
\bm{m}
are of the same sign.
After replacing
\bm{m}
by
-\bm{m}
if necessary, we may assume
m_{j}\geq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq d
.
Let
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
be the smallest integer such that
m_{k}=1
.
If
k=d
then

\bm{m}=\bm{e}_{d}
, and thus
g\bm{m}=\bm{b}_{d}\notin\mathcal{C}_{r}
by (
3.5
).
In the remaining case when
k<d
,
the
k
-th coordinate of
g\bm{m}
satisfies

(3.6)

\displaystyle m_{k}b_{kk}+\sum_{j=k+1}^{d}m_{j}b_{kj}\geq b_{kk}+m_{d}b_{kd}\geq\min\{b_{kk},b_{kk}+b_{kd}\},

where we used the fact that, by (
3.1
),
b_{kj}>0
for each
k<j\leq d-1
.
Furthermore,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\min\{b_{kk},b_{kk}+b_{kd}\}>1-r,

since
b_{kk}>1-r
by (
3.1
)
and
b_{kk}+b_{kd}=b_{kk}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}b_{kj}x_{j}>1-r
by (
3.4
).
It follows from (
3.6
) and (
3.7
)
that the
k
-th coordinate of
g\bm{m}
is larger than
1-r
,
and so
g\bm{m}\notin{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
.
This completes the proof in the case when
all the nonzero entries of
\bm{m}
are of the same sign.

It remains to treat the case when
\{-1,1\}\subset\{m_{j}:1\leq j\leq d\}
.
Then let
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\leq d
be the indices which record the latest instance
when the signs of the entries of
\bm{m}
change,
i.e. the unique indices such that

m_{i_{1}}m_{i_{2}}=-1
,
m_{j}=0
for
i_{1}<j<i_{2}
and
m_{j}\in\{m_{i_{2}},0\}
for
i_{2}<j\leq d

(the last two conditions are void if
i_{1}+1=i_{2}
or
i_{2}=d
, respectively).
Again after replacing
\bm{m}
by
-\bm{m}
if necessary, we may assume

m_{i_{1}}=-1
,
m_{i_{2}}=1
, and thus
m_{j}\geq 0
for all
i_{2}<j\leq d
.
Now consider the
i_{2}
-th coordinate of
g\bm{m}
which is

\sum_{1\leq j<i_{1}}m_{j}b_{i_{2},j}-b_{i_{2},i_{1}}+b_{i_{2},i_{2}}+\sum_{i_{2}<j\leq d}m_{j}b_{i_{2},j}.

Here we have

(3.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq j<i_{1}}m_{j}b_{i_{2},j}-b_{i_{2},i_{1}}\geq\sum_{1\leq j<i_{1}}b_{i_{2},j}-b_{i_{2},i_{1}}>0,

where the first inequality holds since
|m_{j}|\leq 1
and
b_{i_{2},j}<0
for all
j<i_{1}
,
and the second inequality holds by (
3.2
)
(except if
i_{1}=1
; in that case
(
3.8
) simply says that
b_{i_{2},i_{1}}<0
,
which holds by (
3.1
)).
Furthermore,

(3.9)

\displaystyle b_{i_{2},i_{2}}+\sum_{i_{2}<j\leq d}m_{j}b_{i_{2},j}\quad\begin{cases}=b_{dd}>1-r&\text{if }\>i_{2}=d,\\[2.0pt]
\geq b_{i_{2},i_{2}}+m_{d}b_{i_{2},d}>1-r&\text{if }\>i_{2}<d,\end{cases}

where we used (
3.5
) in the case
i_{2}=d
,
and in the case
i_{2}<d
we used the fact that

m_{j}\geq 0
and
b_{i_{2},j}>0
for all
i_{2}<j\leq d-1
(if any),
and then applied (
3.7
) with
k=i_{2}
.
Adding the inequalities in (
3.8
) and (
3.9
),
we conclude that the
i_{2}
-th coordinate of
g\bm{m}
is larger than
1-r
; hence
g\bm{m}\notin\mathcal{C}_{r}
.
This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We next give another family of conditions,
which implies the conditions in Lemma
3.1
,
and which is more suitable for the measure computations which
we are going to carry out.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
c_{0}\in(0,1)
be as above,
let
r\in(0,c_{0}/d)
and let
\Lambda=p_{\bm{b}_{1},\dots,\bm{b}_{d-1}}u_{\bm{x}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\in X_{d}
with

\bm{b}_{j}=(b_{1j},\ldots,b_{dj})^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(
j=1,\ldots,d-1
)
and
\bm{x}\in(0,c_{0}/d)^{d-1}
.
Assume that

(3.10)

\displaystyle b_{ij},-b_{ji}\in(-c_{0},0),\ \forall\ 1\leq j<i\leq d-1;\ b_{d\ell}\in(-c_{0},0),b_{\ell\ell}\in(1-\tfrac{r}{2d},1),\ \forall\ 1\leq\ell\leq d-1,

(3.11)

\displaystyle b_{ij}<db_{i,j-1}\ (\Leftrightarrow|b_{ij}|>d|b_{i,j-1}|),\qquad\forall\ 2\leq j<i\leq d,

(3.12)

\displaystyle|b_{ij}b_{ji}|<\frac{r}{d!},\quad\forall 1\leq j<i\leq d-1,\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}|b_{dj}|x_{j}<\frac{r}{2},

and

(3.13)

\displaystyle b_{kj}>b_{ij}\ (\Leftrightarrow|b_{kj}|<|b_{ij}|),\qquad\forall 1\leq j<k<i\leq d.

Then
\Lambda\in K_{r}
.

Proof.

In view of Lemma
3.1
, it suffices to show that
the conditions
(
3.1
), (
3.2
), (
3.3
) and (
3.4
)
are fulfilled.
Among these, (
3.1
) is an immediate consequence of (
3.10
).
Furthermore, (
3.2
) follows from (
3.10
) and (
3.11
)
by the following computation,
valid for any
2\leq j<i\leq d
:

\displaystyle b_{ij}<db_{i,j-1}<(j-1)b_{i,j-1}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}b_{ik}\quad(<0),

where the last relation is an equality when
j=2
,
while for
j\geq 3
it is a strict inequality which
holds since (
3.11
) forces

b_{i,j-1}<b_{i,j-2}<\cdots<b_{i,1}
.
Also (
3.4
) is easily proved:
Let
1\leq i\leq d-1
.
Then for every
1\leq j\leq d-1
we have
x_{j}>0

and
b_{ij}>b_{dj}

(the latter holds by (
3.13
) if
j<i
and by (
3.10
) if
j\geq i
).
Hence

b_{ii}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}b_{ij}x_{j}>b_{ii}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}b_{dj}x_{j}>1-\frac{r}{2d}-\frac{r}{2}>1-r,

where for the second last inequality we used
(
3.10
) and the second part of (
3.12
).

It remains to prove (
3.3
).
We first note that the ordering assumptions in (
3.11
) and (
3.13
) imply

(3.14)

\displaystyle|b_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}|\leq|b_{ij}|\qquad\textrm{whenever
$1\leq j^{\prime}<i^{\prime}\leq d$, $1\leq j<i\leq d$
and
$i^{\prime}\leq i$ and $j^{\prime}\leq j$.}

Now let
\tilde{p}=(b_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq d-1}
be as in Lemma
3.1
. Define

\varphi_{\tilde{p}}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{d-1}\\
\sigma\neq\operatorname{id}\end{subarray}}\prod_{1\leq i\leq d-1}\left|b_{\sigma(i)i}\right|,

where
S_{d-1}
is the symmetric group over the finite set
\{1,2,\ldots,d-1\}

and
\operatorname{id}\in S_{d-1}
is its identity element.
Then

(3.15)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}b_{ii}-\varphi_{\tilde{p}}\leq\det\tilde{p}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}b_{ii}+\varphi_{\tilde{p}}.

If
d=2
then
S_{d-1}=\{\operatorname{id}\}
and
\varphi_{\tilde{p}}=0
.
Now assume
d\geq 3
, and consider an arbitrary permutation

\sigma\in S_{d-1}\smallsetminus\{\operatorname{id}\}
.
Let
(i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell})
be a cycle of
\sigma
of length
\ell\geq 2
, meaning that

\sigma(i_{j})=i_{j+1}
for all
1\leq j\leq\ell-1
and
\sigma(i_{\ell})=i_{1}
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume
i_{\ell}=\max\{i_{j}:1\leq j\leq\ell\}
.
Let
i_{j_{0}}:=\min\left\{i_{j}:1\leq j\leq\ell\right\}
.
Then (
3.14
) applies for the pairs

(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})=(\sigma(i_{j_{0}}),i_{j_{0}})
and
(i,j)=(i_{\ell},i_{1})
,
so that
\bigl|b_{\sigma(i_{j_{0}}),i_{j_{0}}}\bigr|\leq\bigl|b_{i_{\ell},i_{1}}\bigr|
.
We also have
|b_{ij}|<1
for all
1\leq i,j\leq d-1
,
by (
3.10
).
Hence

\prod_{1\leq i\leq d-1}\left|b_{\sigma(i)i}\right|\leq\left|b_{\sigma(i_{j_{0}}),i_{j_{0}}}b_{i_{1},i_{\ell}}\right|\leq\left|b_{i_{\ell},i_{1}}b_{i_{1},i_{\ell}}\right|<\frac{r}{d!},

where the last inequality holds by (
3.12
).
The above holds for every
\sigma\in S_{d-1}\smallsetminus\{\operatorname{id}\}
; hence

(3.16)

\displaystyle\varphi_{\tilde{p}}<(d-1)!\frac{r}{d!}=\frac{r}{d}.

Note that (
3.16
) also holds when
d=2
, trivially.

Using (
3.15
), (
3.16
),
and the fact that
1-\frac{r}{2d}<b_{ii}<1
for all
1\leq i\leq d-1
(cf. (
3.10
)), we get

(3.17)

\displaystyle\det\tilde{p}<\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}b_{ii}+\frac{r}{d}<1+\frac{r}{d}<(1-r)^{-1},

and

\displaystyle\det\tilde{p}>\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}b_{ii}-\frac{r}{d}>\left(1-\frac{r}{2d}\right)^{d-1}-\frac{r}{d}>1-\frac{r}{2}-\frac{r}{d}\geq 1-r.

Hence we have proved the first condition in (
3.3
).
For the second condition in (
3.3
), in view of the second condition in (
3.12
) it suffices to show
\tfrac{r}{2}<(\det\tilde{p})^{-1}-(1-r)
, or equivalently, that
\det\tilde{p}
is smaller than
(1-\tfrac{r}{2})^{-1}
. But this is true since by (
3.17
),
\det\tilde{p}<1+\tfrac{r}{d}<(1-\tfrac{r}{2})^{-1}
. This finishes the proof.
∎

We can now give the

Proof of the lower bound in Theorem
2.1
.

Keep
0<r<c_{0}/d
, and define

\displaystyle\underline{K}_{r}:=\left\{p_{\bm{b}_{1},\dots,\bm{b}_{d-1}}u_{\bm{x}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\in X_{d}\>:\>\begin{array}[]{ll}(\bm{b}_{1},\ldots,\bm{b}_{d-1},\bm{x})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{d-1}\times(0,c_{0}/d)^{d-1}\\
\textrm{\ \ satisfies}\ \eqref{equ:con1},\eqref{equ:con2},\eqref{equ:con3},\eqref{equ:con4}\end{array}\right\}.

By Proposition
3.2
we have
\underline{K}_{r}\subset K_{r}
.
It remains to bound
\mu_{d}\left(\underline{K}_{r}\right)

from below.
Set

\displaystyle{\underline{\mathcal{K}}_{r}}:=\left\{p_{\bm{b}_{1},\dots,\bm{b}_{d-1}}u_{\bm{x}}\in G\>:\>\begin{array}[]{ll}(\bm{b}_{1},\ldots,\bm{b}_{d-1},\bm{x})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{d-1}\times(0,c_{0}/d)^{d-1}\\
\textrm{\ \ satisfies}\ \eqref{equ:con1},\eqref{equ:con2},\eqref{equ:con3},\eqref{equ:con4}\end{array}\right\},

so that
\pi({\underline{\mathcal{K}}_{r}})=\underline{K}_{r}
.
Here
\pi:G\to X_{d}
is the natural projection as before.
By immediate inspection of the proofs of Proposition
3.2

and Lemma
3.1
,
we have
{\underline{\mathcal{K}}_{r}}\subset G_{c_{0}}
,
and by our choice of
c_{0}
,
\pi|_{G_{c_{0}}}
is injective
(see Section
2.2
).
Hence
\mu_{d}\left(\underline{K}_{r}\right)=\nu\left({\underline{\mathcal{K}}_{r}}\right)
,
and by (
2.2
) we have

\displaystyle\nu\left({\underline{\mathcal{K}}_{r}}\right)

\displaystyle\asymp_{d}\prod_{1\leq k\leq d-1}\left(\int_{1-\frac{r}{2d}}^{1}\operatorname{d}\!{b}_{kk}\right)\int_{\mathcal{R}}\delta\bigl(\,(b_{ij})_{1\leq j<i\leq d}\,\bigr)\prod_{1\leq j<i\leq d}\operatorname{d}\!{b}_{ij},

where

\mathcal{R}:=\left\{(b_{ij})_{1\leq j<i\leq d}\in(-c_{0},0)^{d(d-1)/2}\>:\>\textrm{$(b_{ij})$ satisfies \eqref{equ:con2} and \eqref{equ:con4}}\right\},

and

\displaystyle\delta\bigl(\,(b_{ij})_{1\leq j<i\leq d}\,\bigr):

\displaystyle=\prod_{1\leq j<i\leq d-1}\int_{0}^{\min\left\{c_{0},\frac{r}{d!|b_{ij}|}\right\}}\operatorname{d}\!{b}_{ji}\times\int_{\left\{\bm{x}\in\left(0,\tfrac{c_{0}}{d}\right)^{d-1}\>:\>\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}|b_{dj}|x_{j}<\frac{r}{2}\right\}}\prod_{1\leq j\leq d-1}\operatorname{d}\!{x}_{j}

\displaystyle\asymp_{d,c_{0}}\prod_{1\leq j<i\leq d-1}\min\left\{1,\frac{r}{|b_{ij}|}\right\}\times\prod_{1\leq j\leq d-1}\min\left\{1,\frac{r}{|b_{dj}|}\right\}

\displaystyle=\prod_{1\leq j<i\leq d}\min\left\{1,\frac{r}{|b_{ij}|}\right\}.

Hence

(3.20)

\displaystyle\mu_{d}\left(\underline{K}_{r}\right)=\nu\left({\underline{\mathcal{K}}_{r}}\right)\asymp_{d,c_{0}}r^{d-1}\int_{\mathcal{R}}\left(\prod_{1\leq j<i\leq d}\min\left\{1,\frac{r}{|b_{ij}|}\right\}\operatorname{d}\!{b}_{ij}\right).

Now for each
1\leq j<i\leq d
, we make a change of variable,
b_{ij}=-d^{j-1}z_{ij}
,
to simplify the ordering condition (
3.11
).
Then all the
z_{ij}
’s are positive, and the conditions (
3.11
) and (
3.13
) become

(3.21)

\displaystyle z_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}<z_{ij}\quad\textrm{whenever
$1\leq j^{\prime}<i^{\prime}\leq d$, $\>1\leq j<i\leq d$,
$\>i^{\prime}\leq i$, $\>j^{\prime}\leq j$ and $(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\neq(i,j)$}.

Moreover, for any
j<i
, the condition
b_{ij}\in(-c_{0},0)
corresponds to
z_{ij}\in(0,c_{0}/d^{j-1})
,
and we note that each of these intervals contains the fixed interval

(0,c_{0}/d^{d-1})
.
In fact, let us restrict each
z_{ij}
to the even smaller interval

(r,c_{0}/d^{d-1})
,
and assume
r<c_{0}/d^{d-1}
so that this interval is non-empty;
then
r/|b_{ij}|=rd^{1-j}/z_{ij}<d^{1-j}\leq 1
,
so that
\min\left\{1,r/|b_{ij}|\right\}=rd^{1-j}/z_{ij}\asymp_{d}r/z_{ij}
.
Note also that
|\operatorname{d}\!{b}_{ij}|=d^{j-1}\,\operatorname{d}\!{z}_{ij}\asymp_{d}\operatorname{d}\!{z}_{ij}
;
hence we get from (
3.20
):

\displaystyle\mu_{d}(\underline{K}_{r})

\displaystyle\gg_{d,c_{0}}r^{d-1+\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}\int_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}\prod_{1\leq j<i\leq d}\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{z}_{ij}}{z_{ij}}=r^{\frac{(d-1)(d+2)}{2}}\int_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}\prod_{1\leq j<i\leq d}\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{z}_{ij}}{z_{ij}},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\prime}:=\left\{(z_{ij})_{1\leq j<i\leq d}\in(r,c_{0}/d^{d-1})^{d(d-1)/2}\>:\>(z_{ij})\ \textrm{satisfies \eqref{equ:ordering}}\right\}.

The last integrand is invariant under every permutation of the variables
(z_{ij})_{1\leq j<i\leq d}
,
and the integration regions
\sigma(\mathcal{R}^{\prime})
with
\sigma
running through all these permutations cover (modulo a null set) the cube
(r,c_{0}/d^{d-1})^{d(d-1)/2}
;
hence we obtain

(3.22)

\displaystyle\mu_{d}\left(\underline{K}_{r}\right)

\displaystyle\gg_{d,c_{0}}r^{\frac{(d-1)(d+2)}{2}}\prod_{1\leq j<i\leq d}\left(\int_{r}^{c_{0}/d^{d-1}}\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{z}_{ij}}{z_{ij}}\right)\asymp_{d,c_{0}}r^{\frac{(d-1)(d+2)}{2}}\log^{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{r}\Bigr),

where the last estimate is valid e.g. for all
0<r<c_{0}/(2d^{d-1})
.
Recalling that
c_{0}
only depends on
d
,
we see that
the lower bound in Theorem
2.1
is now proved.
∎

4.
Proof of the upper bound

We keep the notation introduced in Section
2
.
In this section we prove the upper bound in Theorem
2.1
.
The main step in the proof is to show that for
r
small,

K_{r}
is contained in a certain set of more explicit nature; see Proposition
4.2
below.
To prepare for the statement of this result, we start by introducing the following set,
for any
r\in(0,1)
and
C>0
:

\displaystyle{\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{r,C}}:=\left\{g=(g_{ij})\in G\>:\>\begin{array}[]{ll}1-r\leq g_{ii}\leq 1+Cr,\ \forall\ 1\leq i\leq d\\
|g_{ij}|<1,\;|g_{ij}g_{ji}|\leq Cr,\ \forall\ 1\leq i\neq j\leq d\\
|\det\tilde{g}-1|<\tfrac{1}{2}\end{array}\right\}.

Here
\tilde{g}
denotes the top left
(d-1)\times(d-1)
block of
g
as before. In view of the Haar measure description (
2.3
), it is not difficult to compute the measure of
{\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{r,C}}
:

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
r\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and
C>0
we have

\displaystyle\nu\bigl({\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{r,C}}\bigr)\ll_{d,C}r^{\varkappa_{d}+1}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{r}\Bigr).

Proof.

Let us define

\displaystyle{\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{\prime}_{r,C}}:=\left\{g=(g_{ij})\in G\>:\>\begin{array}[]{ll}1-r\leq g_{ii}\leq 1+Cr,\ \forall\ 1\leq i\leq d-1\\
|g_{ij}|<1,\;|g_{ij}g_{ji}|\leq Cr,\ \forall\ 1\leq i\neq j\leq d\\
|\det\tilde{g}-1|<\tfrac{1}{2}\end{array}\right\}

by disregarding the restriction on
g_{dd}
. Then clearly
{\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{r,C}}\subset{\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{\prime}_{r,C}}
.
Moreover, in view of the Haar measure description (
2.3
) we have (noting also that
\varkappa_{d}+1=\lambda_{d}+d-1
)

\displaystyle\nu\left({\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{\prime}_{r,C}}\right)

\displaystyle\ll_{d}\prod_{1\leq i\leq d-1}\int_{1-r}^{1+Cr}\operatorname{d}\!{g}_{ii}\prod_{1\leq j<i\leq d}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}{\chi_{\left\{(g_{ij},g_{ji})\>:\>|g_{ij}g_{ji}|<Cr\right\}}}\,\operatorname{d}\!{g}_{ij}\operatorname{d}\!{g}_{ji}

\displaystyle\asymp_{C}r^{d-1}\left(r\log\Bigl(\frac{1}{r}\Bigr)\right)^{\lambda_{d}}=r^{\varkappa_{d}+1}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{r}\Bigr).

Thus

\displaystyle\nu\left({\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{r,C}}\right)\leq\nu\left({\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{\prime}_{r,C}}\right)\ll_{d,C}r^{\varkappa_{d}+1}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{r}\Bigr),

finishing the proof.
∎

Recall that

\pi:G\to X_{d}
is the natural projection from
G
to
X_{d}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

There exist
r_{0}>0
and
C>0
(depending only on
d
) such that for all
r\in(0,r_{0})

(4.1)

\displaystyle K_{r}\subset\bigcup_{w\in W}\pi\bigl(w\,{\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{r,C}}\,w^{-1}\bigr).

Let us first give a quick

Proof of the upper bound in Theorem
2.1
assuming Proposition
4.2
.

The Haar measure
\nu
on
G
is preserved by conjugation by any element
w\in W

(even though
w
may be outside
G
).
Hence it follows from (
4.1
) that, for any
r\in(0,r_{0})
,

\displaystyle\mu_{d}(K_{r})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{w\in W}\mu_{d}\bigl(\pi(w\,{\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{r,C}}\,w^{-1})\bigr)\leq\sum_{w\in W}\nu(w\,{\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{r,C}}\,w^{-1})=d!\,\nu({\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{r,C}}).

Using this inequality,
the upper bound in Theorem
2.1
now follows from Lemma
4.1
.
∎

Remark 10
.

Proposition
4.2
,
in combination with the lower bound
in Theorem
2.1
,
also implies that
as
r\to 0^{+}
, the mass of
K_{r}
with respect to
\mu_{d}

becomes concentrated near the lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.
In precise terms, if

{\mathcal{O}}
is any fixed neighborhood of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
in
X_{d}
,
then

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mu_{d}\left(K_{r}\smallsetminus{\mathcal{O}}\right)\ll_{d}r^{\varkappa_{d}+1}\log^{\lambda_{d}-1}\Bigl(\frac{1}{r}\Bigr),\quad\text{and thus }\quad\frac{\mu_{d}(K_{r}\cap{\mathcal{O}})}{\mu_{d}(K_{r})}\to 1\qquad\textrm{as $r\to 0^{+}$}.

Indeed, we can fix
\varepsilon>0
so that
{\mathcal{O}}
contains the set

\pi(G_{\varepsilon})
with
G_{\varepsilon}\subset G
the norm ball defined as in (
2.4
);
then by arguing along the same lines as above,
the first relation in (
4.2
)
will follow from the following bound:

\displaystyle\nu\left({\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{r,C}}\smallsetminus G_{\varepsilon}\right)\ll_{d,C}r^{\varkappa_{d}+1}\log^{\lambda_{d}-1}\Bigl(\frac{1}{r}\Bigr),

for
C>0
and
r
small.
However, for
r<\varepsilon/C
,
g=(g_{ij})\in{\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{r,C}}
forces
|g_{ii}-1|<\varepsilon
for all
i
,
and so the set

{\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{r,C}}\smallsetminus G_{\varepsilon}
is contained in the union

\cup_{i^{\prime}\neq j^{\prime}}\bigl\{g=(g_{ij})\in{\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{r,C}}\>:\>|g_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}|\geq\varepsilon\bigr\}
.
Therefore, it suffices to prove that for any given

1\leq i^{\prime}\neq j^{\prime}\leq d
we have

\displaystyle\nu\bigl(\bigl\{g=(g_{ij})\in{\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{r,C}}\>:\>|g_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}|\geq\varepsilon\bigr\}\bigr)\ll_{d,C}r^{\varkappa_{d}+1}\log^{\lambda_{d}-1}\Bigl(\frac{1}{r}\Bigr).

This is shown by following the proof of Lemma
4.1

and using

\int_{\varepsilon\leq|x|\leq 1}\int_{-1}^{1}{\chi_{\{(x,y)\>:\>|xy|<Cr\}}}\,\operatorname{d}\!{y}\,\operatorname{d}\!{x}\ll_{\varepsilon,C}r.

Finally, the second relation in (
4.2
)
follows from the first relation
combined with the lower bound
in Theorem
2.1
.

The remainder of this section is devoted to the proof of Proposition
4.2
.

4.1.
Bounds on diagonal entries

Recall that
U_{0}
is the fixed fundamental domain for
{U/(\Gamma\cap U)}
given in (
2.5
). The next lemma shows that if a lattice
\Lambda\in K_{r}
has a representative sufficiently close to some element in
U_{0}
, then the diagonal entries of such a representative satisfy the desired bounds.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
g=(g_{ij})\in G
and
r\in(0,\frac{1}{8})
,
and assume that
g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{r}=\{\mathbf{0}\}

and
\|g-u\|<\frac{1}{8}
for some
u\in U_{0}
.
Then

(4.3)

\displaystyle 0\leq g_{ii}-(1-r)\ll_{d}r,\qquad\forall\>1\leq i\leq d.

Proof.

Note that it follows from

\|g-u\|<\frac{1}{8}
and
u\in U_{0}

that
|g_{ij}|<\frac{5}{8}
for all
1\leq i<j\leq d
,

|g_{ii}-1|<\frac{1}{8}
for all
1\leq i\leq d
,
and
|g_{ij}|<\frac{1}{8}
for all
1\leq j<i\leq d
.
For each
i
we have
g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}\notin{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
,
since
g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{r}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
.
Combining this with the fact that

|g_{ji}|<\frac{5}{8}<1-r
for all
j\neq i
,
we conclude that
|g_{ii}|\geq 1-r
.
Since also
|g_{ii}-1|<\frac{1}{8}
,
we must in fact have
g_{ii}\geq 1-r
,
i.e. we have proved the left inequality in (
4.3
).

Next, let
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
be given,
and let
{\mathcal{U}}
be the open box

\displaystyle{\mathcal{U}}={\mathcal{I}}_{1}\times\cdots\times{\mathcal{I}}_{d},\quad\text{with }\>\left\{\hskip-4.0pt\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mathcal{I}}_{i}=\bigl(\frac{1}{2}(1-r),g_{ii}-\frac{1}{2}(1-r)\bigr),\\[5.0pt]
{\mathcal{I}}_{j}=\bigl(g_{ji}^{+}-\frac{3}{8},g_{ji}^{-}+\frac{3}{8}\bigr)\ (j\neq i),\end{array}\right.

where
g_{ji}^{+}:=\max\{g_{ji},0\}
and
g_{ji}^{-}:=\min\{g_{ji},0\}
.
Note that each interval
{\mathcal{I}}_{j}
(
j\neq i
) has length
|{\mathcal{I}}_{j}|>\frac{1}{8}
, since
|g_{ji}|<\frac{5}{8}
;
furthermore
|{\mathcal{I}}_{i}|=g_{ii}-(1-r)\geq 0

(thus
{\mathcal{I}}_{i}
and
{\mathcal{U}}
are empty if
g_{ii}=1-r
, but otherwise non-empty).

We claim that
{\mathcal{U}}
is disjoint from
{\mathfrak{C}}_{g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d},r}:=g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}+\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
.
Indeed, assume the opposite; then there is some
{\text{\boldmath$v$}}\in g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}

such that
{\mathcal{U}}\cap({\text{\boldmath$v$}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r})\neq\varnothing
.
We must have
{\text{\boldmath$v$}}\neq\mathbf{0}
and
{\text{\boldmath$v$}}\neq g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}
,
since
{\mathcal{U}}
is, by construction, disjoint both from
\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
and from
g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
.
Pick a point

{\text{\boldmath$x$}}\in{\mathcal{U}}\cap({\text{\boldmath$v$}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r})
.
It follows from

{\text{\boldmath$x$}}\in{\text{\boldmath$v$}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}

and
\frac{1}{2}(1-r)>\frac{1}{4}

that at least one of the points
{\text{\boldmath$x$}}-\frac{1}{4}{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}
or
{\text{\boldmath$x$}}+\frac{1}{4}{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}

also
lies in

{\text{\boldmath$v$}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
.
But we have

\displaystyle{\text{\boldmath$x$}}-\tfrac{1}{4}{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}\in\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r},

since
x_{j}\in{\mathcal{I}}_{j}\subset\big(-\frac{1}{2}(1-r),\frac{1}{2}(1-r)\big)
for all
j\neq i

and
x_{i}-\frac{1}{4}<g_{ii}-\frac{1}{2}(1-r)-\frac{1}{4}<\frac{1}{2}(1-r)
.
We also have

\displaystyle{\text{\boldmath$x$}}+\tfrac{1}{4}{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}\in g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r},

since
x_{j}\in{\mathcal{I}}_{j}\subset g_{ji}+\big(-\frac{1}{2}(1-r),\frac{1}{2}(1-r)\big)
for all
j\neq i

and

x_{i}+\frac{1}{4}>\frac{1}{2}(1-r)+\frac{1}{4}>g_{ii}-\frac{1}{2}(1-r)
. Hence we have arrived at a contradiction against the fact that

{\text{\boldmath$v$}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
is disjoint from both

\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
and
g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
.
This completes the proof of the fact that

{\mathcal{U}}
is disjoint from
{\mathfrak{C}}_{g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d},r}
.

Note also that
{\mathcal{U}}
is contained in a translate of the cube
(0,\frac{3}{4})^{d}
,
since each interval
{\mathcal{I}}_{j}
has length at most
\frac{3}{4}
.
Hence Lemma
2.5
applies,
and yields that
\operatorname{vol}({\mathcal{U}})<dr
.
But we have noted that
|{\mathcal{I}}_{j}|>\frac{1}{8}

for each
j\neq i
, and
|{\mathcal{I}}_{i}|=g_{ii}-(1-r)
;
hence
\operatorname{vol}({\mathcal{U}})\geq 8^{1-d}(g_{ii}-(1-r))
.
Combining these facts, we obtain the right bound in (
4.3
)
with the implied constant
8^{d-1}d
.
∎

4.2.
A technical choice of lattice representatives

For any
\varepsilon>0
let us write

\displaystyle U_{\varepsilon}=\bigl\{(u_{ij})\in U\>:\>-\tfrac{1}{2}+4\varepsilon<u_{ij}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\text{ for all }1\leq i<j\leq d\bigr\}.

Lemma 4.4
.

There exist constants
0<a<1
and
A>1
, which only depend on
d
,
such that the following holds:
given any
u\in U
and
\varepsilon\in(0,a)
, there
exist
\gamma\in\Gamma\cap U
and

B\in[1,A]
such that
u\gamma\in U_{B\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

We will show that the statement of this lemma holds with

A:=4^{2^{d}}
and
a:=2^{-(3+2^{d+1})}
.
Let us set

\displaystyle U[t]:=\bigl\{(u_{ij})\in U\>:\>-\tfrac{1}{2}+t<u_{ij}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}+t\text{ for all }1\leq i<j\leq d\bigr\}\qquad(t\in{\mathbb{R}}).

Note that
U_{0}=U[0]
,
and for each
t
,
U[t]
is a fundamental domain for
U/(\Gamma\cap U)
.

For the given
u\in U
and
\varepsilon\in(0,a)
,
and for each
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
,
we let
u^{(k)}
be the unique element in

u\Gamma\cap U[4^{k}\varepsilon]
,
and set

\displaystyle P^{(k)}=\sum_{j=2}^{d}P^{(k)}_{j},\quad\text{where}\quad P^{(k)}_{j}:=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}2^{i-1}\cdot{\chi_{(0,\infty)}(u_{ij}^{(k)})}.

We now claim:

(4.4)

\displaystyle\forall\,k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}:\quad k<\log_{4}(\tfrac{1}{8\varepsilon})\text{ and }u^{(k)}\notin U_{4^{k}\varepsilon}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P^{(k)}<P^{(k+1)}.

To prove (
4.4
),
assume
0\leq k<\log_{4}(\frac{1}{8\varepsilon})
and
u^{(k)}\notin U_{4^{k}\varepsilon}
.
Then
u^{(k+1)}\neq u^{(k)}
,
since otherwise
u^{(k)}

would lie in the intersection
U[4^{k}\varepsilon]\cap U[4^{k+1}\varepsilon]=U_{4^{k}\varepsilon}
.
Let us denote by
{\text{\boldmath$u$}}_{j}^{(k)}
the
j
th column vector of
u^{(k)}
.
It follows that
{\text{\boldmath$u$}}_{j}^{(k+1)}\neq{\text{\boldmath$u$}}_{j}^{(k)}
for at least one
j\in\{2,\ldots,d\}
,
and for each such index
j
we can argue as follows:
Since
u^{(k)},u^{(k+1)}\in u\Gamma\cap U=u(\Gamma\cap U)
, we have

\bm{u}_{j}^{(k+1)}=\bm{u}_{j}^{(k)}+\sum_{1\leq i<j}m_{i}\bm{u}_{i}^{(k)}
for some
m_{1},\ldots,m_{j-1}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Because of
{\text{\boldmath$u$}}_{j}^{(k+1)}\neq{\text{\boldmath$u$}}_{j}^{(k)}
,
there exists
i\in\{1,\ldots,j\}
such that

m_{i}\neq 0
;
let us fix
i
to be the
largest
such index.
Thus
u_{ij}^{(k+1)}=u_{ij}^{(k)}+m_{i}
; in particular
\left|u_{ij}^{(k+1)}-u_{ij}^{(k)}\right|\geq 1
.
On the other hand, note that
u_{ij}^{(k)}\in\left(-\frac{1}{2}+4^{k}\varepsilon,\frac{1}{2}+4^{k}\varepsilon\right]
,
u_{ij}^{(k+1)}\in\left(-\frac{1}{2}+4^{k+1}\varepsilon,\frac{1}{2}+4^{k+1}\varepsilon\right]
and
\left(\frac{1}{2}+4^{k+1}\varepsilon)-(-\frac{1}{2}+4^{k}\varepsilon\right)=1+3\cdot 4^{k}\varepsilon<2
(since
k<\log_{4}(\frac{1}{8\varepsilon})
).
Hence we must have
m_{i}=1
, implying that

{\text{\boldmath$u$}}_{j}^{(k+1)}\in{\text{\boldmath$u$}}_{j}^{(k)}+{\text{\boldmath$u$}}_{i}^{(k)}+{\mathbb{Z}}{\text{\boldmath$u$}}_{i-1}^{(k)}+\cdots+{\mathbb{Z}}{\text{\boldmath$u$}}_{1}^{(k)}.

Thus

u_{i^{\prime}j}^{(k+1)}=u_{i^{\prime}j}^{(k)}
for all
i^{\prime}>i
,
and
u_{ij}^{(k+1)}=u_{ij}^{(k)}+1>0

while
u_{ij}^{(k)}\leq-\frac{1}{2}+4^{k+1}\varepsilon<0

(again since
k<\log_{4}(\frac{1}{8\varepsilon})
).
It follows that
P_{j}^{(k+1)}-P_{j}^{(k)}\geq 2^{i-1}-\sum_{i^{\prime}<i}2^{i^{\prime}-1}=1
.
On the other hand we clearly have
P_{j}^{(k+1)}=P_{j}^{(k)}

for each
j\in\{2,\ldots,d\}
such that

{\text{\boldmath$u$}}_{j}^{(k+1)}={\text{\boldmath$u$}}_{j}^{(k)}
.
Hence
\sum_{j=2}^{d}P^{(k+1)}_{j}>\sum_{j=2}^{d}P^{(k)}_{j}
,
i.e.
P^{(k+1)}>P^{(k)}
. This finishes the proof of (
4.4
).

Next note that by definition,
for each
k
,

P^{(k)}
is a non-negative integer
satisfying

P^{(k)}\leq\sum_{j=2}^{d}\sum_{i=0}^{j-2}2^{i}<2^{d}.

This implies that we cannot have
P^{(k)}<P^{(k+1)}
for all

k\in\{0,1,\ldots,2^{d}-1\}
.
But our assumption on
\varepsilon
implies that

\log_{4}(\frac{1}{8\varepsilon})>2^{d}

(recall that
0<\varepsilon<a=2^{-(3+2^{d+1})}
);
hence it now follows from (
4.4
) that

u^{(k)}\in U_{4^{k}\varepsilon}
for at least one
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,2^{d}-1\}
.
Furthermore, for such
k
we have
4^{k}<4^{2^{d}}=A

and
u^{(k)}\in u(\Gamma\cap U)
by construction.
Hence the lemma is proved.
∎

Using Lemma
4.4
, Corollary
2.4
and a compactness argument we have the following technical lemma, which gives us a good choice of lattice representatives for lattices in
K_{r_{0}}
for some small
r_{0}>0
.

Lemma 4.5
.

There exist constants
0<a<1
and
A>1
, which only depend on
d
,
such that the following holds:
for any
{\varepsilon_{0}}\in(0,a)
there exists
r_{0}>0
such that
for every
\Lambda\in X_{d}
satisfying

\Lambda\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{r_{0}}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
,
there exist
g\in G
,

w\in W
,
B\in[1,A]
and
u\in U_{B{\varepsilon_{0}}}
such that

\Lambda=g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and
\|g-wuw^{-1}\|<{\varepsilon_{0}}
.

Proof.

Let
a
and
A
be as in Lemma
4.4
;
we will prove that the statement of the lemma holds with these
a,A
.
The proof is by contradiction;
thus we assume that the statement of the lemma is false,
i.e. we assume that
there exist some
{\varepsilon_{0}}\in(0,a)
and a sequence

r_{1}>r_{2}>\cdots
in
(0,1)
with
r_{j}\to 0
,
and a corresponding sequence
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},\ldots
in
X_{d}
,
such that
\Lambda_{j}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{r_{j}}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
for each
j
,
and furthermore, for each
j
we have that there do

not
exist any

g\in G
,

w\in W
,
B\in[1,A]
and
u\in U_{B{\varepsilon_{0}}}
satisfying

\Lambda_{j}=g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and
\|g-wuw^{-1}\|<{\varepsilon_{0}}
.
Now for every
j
we have

{\mathcal{C}}_{r_{1}}\subset{\mathcal{C}}_{r_{j}}
, and thus
\Lambda_{j}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{r_{1}}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
.
Hence by Mahler’s Compactness Theorem,
after passing to a subsequence we may assume that

\Lambda_{j}
tends to a limit point in
X_{d}
.
Let us call this limit point
\Lambda_{0}
;
thus
\Lambda_{j}\to\Lambda_{0}
in
X_{d}
as
j\to\infty
.
Let us also fix a representative
g_{0}\in G
such that
\Lambda_{0}=g_{0}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.

Recall that the standard topology on
X_{d}=G/\Gamma

is given by the metric

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(g\Gamma,g^{\prime}\Gamma):=\inf\{d(g\gamma,g^{\prime})\>:\>\gamma\in\Gamma\}\qquad(g,g^{\prime}\in G),

where
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is any fixed right
G
-invariant Riemannian metric on
G
.
Hence the fact that
\Lambda_{j}
converges to
\Lambda_{0}=g_{0}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}

implies that there exist
g_{1},g_{2},\ldots\in G
such that

\Lambda_{j}=g_{j}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
for each
j
and
d(g_{j},g_{0})\to 0
as
j\to\infty
.

Using
\Lambda_{j}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{r_{j}}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
for each
j
, and
r_{j}\to 0
,
we claim that

(4.5)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{0}\cap(-1,1)^{d}=\{\mathbf{0}\}.

Indeed, assume the opposite;
this means that there exists some
{\text{\boldmath$m$}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\smallsetminus\{\bm{0}\}

such that
g_{0}{\text{\boldmath$m$}}
belongs to
(-1,1)^{d}
,
i.e.
\|g_{0}{\text{\boldmath$m$}}\|<1
.
Set
r:=\frac{1}{2}(1-\|g_{0}{\text{\boldmath$m$}}\|)
; then
g_{0}{\text{\boldmath$m$}}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
.
We have
g_{j}{\text{\boldmath$m$}}\to g_{0}{\text{\boldmath$m$}}
as
j\to\infty
,
since
d(g_{j},g_{0})\to 0
;
hence for all sufficiently large
j
we have
g_{j}{\text{\boldmath$m$}}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
(since
{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
is open).
Also for all sufficiently large
j
we have
r_{j}<r
.
Hence there exists some
j
for which
r_{j}<r
and

g_{j}{\text{\boldmath$m$}}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{r}\subset{\mathcal{C}}_{r_{j}}
.
This contradicts the fact that
\Lambda_{j}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{r_{j}}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
for all
j
.
Hence (
4.5
) is proved.

It follows from (
4.5
) and Corollary
2.4

that
\Lambda_{0}=wu^{\prime}w^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
for some

w\in W
and
u^{\prime}\in U_{0}
. Next, by Lemma
4.4

(and since
{\varepsilon_{0}}<a
),
there exist
\gamma\in\Gamma\cap U
and
B\in[1,A]

such that
u:=u^{\prime}\gamma\in U_{B{\varepsilon_{0}}}
.
Using
w^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}={\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and
\gamma^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}={\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
,
we then have
\Lambda_{0}=wuw^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.
Hence
wuw^{-1}=g_{0}\gamma_{0}
for some
\gamma_{0}\in\Gamma
.
Now
d(g_{j}\gamma_{0},wuw^{-1})=d(g_{j}\gamma_{0},g_{0}\gamma_{0})=d(g_{j},g_{0})\to 0
as
j\to\infty
.
This implies that every matrix entry of
g_{j}\gamma_{0}
tends to the corresponding entry of
wuw^{-1}
,
i.e. we have
\|g_{j}\gamma_{0}-wuw^{-1}\|\to 0
as
j\to\infty
.
In particular there exists some
j
such that

\|g_{j}\gamma_{0}-wuw^{-1}\|<{\varepsilon_{0}}
.
Now we have a contradiction against our previous assumption;
namely for our chosen
j
,
if we set
g:=g_{j}\gamma_{0}
then
\Lambda_{j}=g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}

and
\|g-wuw^{-1}\|<{\varepsilon_{0}}
.
This completes the proof of Lemma
4.5
.
∎

Remark 11
.

By a similar compactness argument as in
the proof of Lemma
4.5
,
one can also prove a more basic statement:
for any
\varepsilon_{0}>0
there exists
r_{0}>0
such that every
\Lambda\in K_{r_{0}}
has a representative

g\in G
(i.e.
\Lambda=g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
) satisfying
\|g-wuw^{-1}\|<\varepsilon_{0}
for some
w\in W
and
u\in U_{0}
.
The purpose of the choice of the more technical lattice representatives in Lemma
4.5

(with
U_{B\varepsilon_{0}}
in place of
U_{0}
)
is to ensure the following
property, which is a key
ingredient in the proof of the important Lemma
4.6
below:
for any
g=(g_{ij})\in G
satisfying
\|g-u\|<\varepsilon_{0}
for some
u\in U_{B\varepsilon_{0}}
,
and any
1\leq i<j\leq d
,
we have (cf. (
4.8
))

\displaystyle|g_{ki}-g_{kj}|<1-B\varepsilon_{0}\quad\textrm{for all $k\notin\{i,j\}$}.

A crucial consequence of this is that if
g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}-g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{j}\notin\mathcal{C}_{r}
for some
r<B\varepsilon_{0}
,
then either

|g_{ii}-g_{ij}|>1-r
or
|g_{ji}-g_{jj}|>1-r
must hold.

4.3.
Bounds on off-diagonal symmetric pairs

The next lemma shows that if a lattice
\Lambda
in
K_{r}
has a representative as in Lemma
4.5
, then its entries satisfy the desired bounds for proving Proposition
4.2
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
A>1
,

0<\varepsilon_{0}<(16A)^{-1}
and
0<r_{0}<\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{0}
.
Let
g=(g_{ij})\in G
and
r\in(0,r_{0})
,
and assume that

g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{r}=\{\mathbf{0}\}

and that there exist
B\in[1,A]
and
u\in U_{B\varepsilon_{0}}
such that

\|g-u\|<\varepsilon_{0}
.
Then

(4.6)

\displaystyle 0\leq g_{ii}-(1-r)\ll_{d}r\quad\text{for all }\>1\leq i\leq d,

and

(4.7)

\displaystyle|g_{ij}g_{ji}|\ll_{d,\varepsilon_{0}}r\quad\text{for all }\>1\leq i<j\leq d.

Proof.

Let us write
\varepsilon=B\varepsilon_{0}
.
Note that
\|g-u\|<\varepsilon_{0}\leq\varepsilon
and
u\in U_{\varepsilon}
together imply that

(4.8)

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}+3\varepsilon<g_{ij}<\tfrac{1}{2}+2\varepsilon\quad\text{ and }\quad|g_{ji}|<\varepsilon,\quad\forall 1\leq i<j\leq d.

It also follows that

|g_{ii}-1|<\varepsilon
for all
i
;
however these inequalities may be
sharpened using Lemma
4.3
.
Indeed, we have
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{16}
since

A\varepsilon_{0}<\frac{1}{16}
;
hence the above inequalities imply that
\|g-u^{\prime}\|<\frac{1}{8}
for some

u^{\prime}\in U_{0}
. Hence Lemma
4.3
applies, yielding that
(
4.6
) holds.

Now let
1\leq i<j\leq d
be given.
We separate the proof of (
4.7
)
into three cases.
(Note that (
4.7
) holds trivially if

g_{ij}=0
or
g_{ji}=0
;
hence we may without loss of generality assume

g_{ij}g_{ji}\neq 0
.)

Case I:

g_{ij}>0
and
g_{ji}>0
.
In this case we will build the proof on the fact that

g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}-g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{j}\notin{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
,
which holds since

g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{r}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
.
Using (
4.8
) and
r<r_{0}<2r_{0}\leq\varepsilon<\frac{1}{16}
,
it follows that

|g_{ki}-g_{kj}|<1-\varepsilon<1-r

for all
k\notin\{i,j\}
.
Hence we must have
either

|g_{ii}-g_{ij}|\geq 1-r

or
|g_{ji}-g_{jj}|\geq 1-r
.
If
|g_{ii}-g_{ij}|\geq 1-r
, then because of
g_{ii}\geq 1-r
and
0<g_{ij}<\frac{1}{2}+2\varepsilon

it follows that
g_{ii}-g_{ij}\geq 1-r
,
and so by (
4.6
),

0<g_{ij}\ll r
.
Similarly if
|g_{ji}-g_{jj}|\geq 1-r

then
0<g_{ji}\ll r
.
In both cases, it follows that
(
4.7
) holds for our
i,j
.

Case II:

g_{ij}<0
and
g_{ji}<0
.
In this case we will prove the desired bound by proving the stronger assertion that either

|g_{ij}|\leq Cr
or
|g_{ji}|\leq Cr
, with
C:=12d(\varepsilon_{0}/8)^{2-d}
. Assume the opposite, i.e. assume that

(4.9)

\displaystyle g_{ij}<-Cr\qquad\text{and}\qquad g_{ji}<-Cr\qquad(C:=12d(\varepsilon_{0}/8)^{2-d}).

We will prove that this leads to a contradiction.

Set
{\mathcal{J}}_{r}:=\bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2}(1-r),\tfrac{1}{2}(1-r)\bigr)
,
so that
\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}={\mathcal{J}}_{r}^{d}
.
For each
k\notin\{i,j\}
we introduce the following open interval:

(4.10)

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathcal{I}}}_{k}:={\mathcal{J}}_{r}\cap(g_{ki}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r})\cap(g_{kj}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r}).

Using
|g_{ki}|,|g_{kj}|,|g_{ki}-g_{kj}|<1-\varepsilon

(see (
4.8
))
it follows that
\widetilde{{\mathcal{I}}}_{k}
has length

|\widetilde{{\mathcal{I}}}_{k}|>(1-r)-(1-\varepsilon)>\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{0}
.
Define
{\mathcal{I}}_{k}\subset\widetilde{{\mathcal{I}}}_{k}
to be the open interval of length
\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon_{0}
with the same center as
\widetilde{{\mathcal{I}}}_{k}
.
Let us also set
{\mathcal{I}}_{i}=\bigl(\frac{1}{2}(1-r)+g_{ij},\frac{1}{2}(1-r)\bigr)

and
{\mathcal{I}}_{j}=\bigl(g_{jj}-\frac{1}{2}(1-r),\frac{3}{4}\bigr)
.
Then by construction,

(4.11)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{i}\subset{\mathcal{J}}_{r},\qquad{\mathcal{I}}_{i}\cap(g_{ii}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r})=\varnothing,\qquad{\mathcal{I}}_{i}\cap(g_{ij}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r})=\varnothing,

and

(4.12)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{j}\subset g_{jj}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r},\qquad{\mathcal{I}}_{j}\cap{\mathcal{J}}_{r}=\varnothing.

Furthermore,

|{\mathcal{I}}_{i}|=|g_{ij}|
and
\frac{1}{8}<|{\mathcal{I}}_{j}|<\frac{1}{2}
.
Now let
{\mathcal{U}}
be the open box

{\mathcal{U}}={\mathcal{I}}_{1}\times\cdots\times{\mathcal{I}}_{d}
.
Then
\operatorname{vol}({\mathcal{U}})>\frac{1}{8}|g_{ij}|\cdot(\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon_{0})^{d-2}>dr
,
where we used the first part of our assumption (
4.9
).
Note also that
|{\mathcal{I}}_{k}|<\frac{3}{4}
for all
k
.
Hence by Lemma
2.5
,

{\mathcal{U}}\cap{\mathfrak{C}}_{g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d},r}\neq\varnothing
,
i.e. there exists some
{\text{\boldmath$v$}}\in g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}

such that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{U}}\cap({\text{\boldmath$v$}}+\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r})\neq\varnothing.

It follows from the disjointness relations in (
4.11
)
and (
4.12
)
that
{\mathcal{U}}
is disjoint from the three cubes

g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
,
g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{j}+\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}

and
\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
;
hence
{\text{\boldmath$v$}}\notin\{\mathbf{0},g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{j},g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}\}
.
Let us fix a point
{\text{\boldmath$y$}}\in{\mathcal{U}}\cap({\text{\boldmath$v$}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r})
.
Then the line
{\text{\boldmath$y$}}+{\mathbb{R}}{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}
goes through both the cubes

{\text{\boldmath$v$}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}

and
g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}

(the latter holds since
{\mathcal{I}}_{k}\subset g_{kj}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r}
for all
k\neq i
;
see (
4.10
) and (
4.12
));
hence since these two cubes are disjoint,
we must have
v_{i}\geq g_{ij}+1-r
.
It also follows from
y_{i}\in v_{i}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r}
and
y_{i}\in{\mathcal{I}}_{i}

that
v_{i}<y_{i}+\frac{1}{2}(1-r)<1-r
.
In summary:

(4.13)

\displaystyle g_{ij}+1-r\leq v_{i}<1-r.

Similarly, using the fact that the line
{\text{\boldmath$y$}}+{\mathbb{R}}{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{j}

goes through the two disjoint cubes

{\text{\boldmath$v$}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}

and
\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
,
and also using
y_{j}\in v_{j}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r}
and
y_{j}\in{\mathcal{I}}_{j}
,
it follows that

(4.14)

\displaystyle 1-r\leq v_{j}<y_{j}+\tfrac{1}{2}(1-r)<\tfrac{5}{4}.

Next, for each
k\notin\{i,j\}
,
since
y_{k}\in{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
and
y_{k}\in v_{k}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r}
, we have that both of the intervals

(y_{k}-\tfrac{1}{8}\varepsilon_{0},y_{k}]
and
[y_{k},y_{k}+\tfrac{1}{8}\varepsilon_{0})

are contained in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{I}}}_{k}
,
and at least one of them is contained in
v_{k}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r}
;
hence there exists an open subinterval
{\mathcal{I}}_{k}^{\prime}
of

\widetilde{{\mathcal{I}}}_{k}\cap(v_{k}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r})
of length
\frac{1}{8}\varepsilon_{0}
.
Let us also set
{\mathcal{I}}_{i}^{\prime}=(\tfrac{2}{3},\tfrac{3}{4})
and
{\mathcal{I}}_{j}^{\prime}=\bigl(g_{ji}+\tfrac{1}{2}(1-r),v_{j}-\tfrac{1}{2}(1-r)\bigr)
,
and then let
{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}
be the open box

{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{I}}_{1}^{\prime}\times\cdots\times{\mathcal{I}}_{d}^{\prime}
.
Using (
4.14
)
we have

|{\mathcal{I}}_{j}^{\prime}|\geq-g_{ji}
;
hence

\operatorname{vol}({\mathcal{U}}^{\prime})\geq(\frac{1}{8}\varepsilon_{0})^{d-2}\cdot\frac{1}{12}\cdot|g_{ji}|>dr
,
where we used the second part of our assumption
(
4.9
).
Note also that
|{\mathcal{I}}_{k}^{\prime}|<\frac{3}{4}
for all
k
;
for
k=j
this uses
v_{j}<\frac{5}{4}

(see (
4.14
))
and
g_{ji}>-\varepsilon
(see (
4.8
)).
Hence, by Lemma
2.5
,

{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}\cap{\mathfrak{C}}_{g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d},r}\neq\varnothing
,
i.e. there exists some
{\text{\boldmath$v$}}^{\prime}\in g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}

such that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}\cap({\text{\boldmath$v$}}^{\prime}+\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r})\neq\varnothing.

Choose a point
{\text{\boldmath$z$}}\in{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}\cap({\text{\boldmath$v$}}^{\prime}+\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r})
.
Note that for every
k\notin\{i,j\}

we have
{\mathcal{I}}_{k}^{\prime}\subset(g_{ki}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r})\cap(v_{k}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r})
by construction.
Furthermore, using
1-r\leq g_{ii}<1+\varepsilon

we have
{\mathcal{I}}_{i}^{\prime}\subset g_{ii}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r}
,
and using (
4.13
)
we have
{\mathcal{I}}_{i}^{\prime}\subset v_{i}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r}
.
Hence the line
{\text{\boldmath$z$}}+{\mathbb{R}}{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{j}
goes through of
the cubes
g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}

and
{\text{\boldmath$v$}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
.
Of course this line also goes through the cube

{\text{\boldmath$v$}}^{\prime}+\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
.
Note also that
z_{j}\in{\mathcal{I}}_{j}^{\prime}
,
and by construction,

{\mathcal{I}}_{j}^{\prime}
is disjoint from and lies between the two intervals

g_{ji}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r}
and
v_{j}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r}
.
Hence we must have
{\text{\boldmath$v$}}^{\prime}\notin\{g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i},{\text{\boldmath$v$}}\}
;
thus the three cubes

g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
,

{\text{\boldmath$v$}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}

and
{\text{\boldmath$v$}}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}

are pairwise disjoint,
and the two intervals

g_{ji}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r}
and
v_{j}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r}
must lie at a distance
\geq 1-r
from each other.
However, this is impossible,
since
v_{j}-g_{ji}<\frac{5}{4}+\varepsilon<2(1-r)
.
This completes the proof in Case II.

Case III:

g_{ij}g_{ji}<0
.
If
g_{ji}<0
then let us swap the values of
i
and
j
;
thus from now on we have
g_{ji}>0
and
g_{ij}<0
,
but either
i<j
or
i>j
.
If
g_{ji}\leq g_{jj}-(1-r)
, then

g_{ji}\ll r
by (
4.6
),
and so (
4.7
) holds for our
i,j
.
Hence from now on we may assume

g_{ji}>g_{jj}-(1-r)
.
Now set:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{i}=\bigl(g_{ij}+\tfrac{1}{2}(1-r),g_{ii}-\tfrac{1}{2}(1-r)\bigr);

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{j}=\bigl(g_{jj}-\tfrac{1}{2}(1-r),g_{ji}+\tfrac{1}{2}(1-r)\bigr);

\displaystyle\text{and }\>\widetilde{{\mathcal{I}}}_{k}=(g_{ki}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r})\cap(g_{kj}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r})\quad\text{for $k\notin\{i,j\}$}.

These are non-empty intervals.
Indeed,

{\mathcal{I}}_{i}
is non-empty since
g_{ii}\geq 1-r
and
g_{ij}<0
;

{\mathcal{I}}_{j}
is non-empty because of our assumption

g_{ji}>g_{jj}-(1-r)
,
and for each
k\notin\{i,j\}
,
it follows from
|g_{ki}-g_{kj}|<1-\varepsilon

(see (
4.8
))
that
\widetilde{{\mathcal{I}}}_{k}
is non-empty with
|\widetilde{{\mathcal{I}}}_{k}|>(1-r)-(1-\varepsilon)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{0}
.
Now for each
k\notin\{i,j\}

we choose an open subinterval
{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
of
\widetilde{{\mathcal{I}}}_{k}

of length
\min\big\{\frac{3}{4},|\widetilde{{\mathcal{I}}}_{k}|\big\}
,
and then define
{\mathcal{U}}
to be the open box

{\mathcal{U}}={\mathcal{I}}_{1}\times\cdots\times{\mathcal{I}}_{d}
.
We claim that

(4.15)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{U}}\cap{\mathfrak{C}}_{g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d},r}=\varnothing.

Indeed, assume the opposite;
then there is some

{\text{\boldmath$v$}}\in g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
with

{\mathcal{U}}\cap\left({\text{\boldmath$v$}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}\right)\neq\varnothing
.
By construction,

{\mathcal{I}}_{i}
is disjoint from both the intervals

g_{ij}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r}
and
g_{ii}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r}
;
hence
{\mathcal{U}}
is disjoint from the two cubes

g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
,

g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
,
and thus
{\text{\boldmath$v$}}\notin\{g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{j},g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}\}
.
Let
x
be a point in

{\mathcal{U}}\cap\left({\text{\boldmath$v$}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}\right)
.
Then
x_{i}\in{\mathcal{I}}_{i}\cap(v_{i}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r})
.
Note also that the three intervals
g_{ij}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r}
,
{\mathcal{I}}_{i}
,
g_{ii}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r}

are adjacent to each other in this order
along the real line,
with the length of
{\mathcal{I}}_{i}
being

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{I}}_{i}|=g_{ii}-g_{ij}-(1-r)<1+\varepsilon+(\tfrac{1}{2}-3\varepsilon)-(1-r)=\tfrac{1}{2}-2\varepsilon+r<\tfrac{1}{2}<1-r.

But
v_{i}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r}
has length
1-r
;
hence there exists a number
x_{i}^{\prime}\in v_{i}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r}

lying either in
g_{ii}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r}
or
g_{ij}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r}
.
Noticing also that
x_{k}\in{\mathcal{I}}_{k}\subset(g_{ki}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r})\cap(g_{kj}+{\mathcal{J}}_{r})

for all
k\neq i
,
it now follows that the point

{\text{\boldmath$x$}}+(x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i}){\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}
lies in the cube
{\text{\boldmath$v$}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}

and also in one of the two cubes

g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}

or
g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
.
This is a contradiction against the fact that

{\text{\boldmath$v$}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}

is disjoint from both

g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}

and
g{\text{\boldmath$e$}}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
;
hence we have completed the proof of (
4.15
).

We have
|{\mathcal{I}}_{k}|<\frac{3}{4}
for all
1\leq k\leq d
;
hence Lemma
2.5
applies,
giving
\operatorname{vol}({\mathcal{U}})<dr
.
But
|{\mathcal{I}}_{k}|\geq\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{0}
for all
k\notin\{i,j\}
,
and
|{\mathcal{I}}_{i}|\geq|g_{ij}|
;
thus

\displaystyle|g_{ij}|\bigl(g_{ji}-g_{jj}+1-r\bigr)=|g_{ij}||{\mathcal{I}}_{j}|\ll\operatorname{vol}({\mathcal{U}})\ll r.

Furthermore,
|g_{ij}|\big(g_{jj}-(1-r)\big)\ll r
by (
4.6
).
Adding the last two bounds,
we conclude that
(
4.7
) holds for our
i,j
.
∎

4.4.
Proof of Proposition
4.2

Finally, we can give the

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

Choose
0<a<1
and
A>1
as in Lemma
4.5
.
Fix a number
0<\beta<\frac{1}{16}

so small that for every matrix
g\in G
which has distance
(w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|
) less than
\beta
to a matrix in
U_{0}
,
we have
|\det\tilde{g}-1|<\frac{1}{2}
,
where
\tilde{g}
is the top left
(d-1)\times(d-1)
block of
g
.
Fix a number
0<\varepsilon_{0}<\min\bigl\{a,\beta/(2A)\bigr\}
,
and for this
\varepsilon_{0}
, take
r_{0}>0
as in Lemma
4.5
.
Note that the defining property of
r_{0}

trivially remains valid if we decrease
r_{0}
;
hence we may assume that
0<r_{0}<\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{0}
.
Let
C>0
be the maximum of the implied constants in the two
“
\ll
” bounds in Lemma
4.6
,
for our fixed
d
and
\varepsilon_{0}
.

Now let
r\in(0,r_{0})
and
\Lambda\in K_{r}
be given.
This means that
\Lambda\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{r}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
,
and a fortiori,

\Lambda\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{r_{0}}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
.
Hence by our choice of

A,a,\varepsilon_{0},r_{0}
(see the statement of Lemma
4.5
),
there exist
g^{\prime}\in G
,

w\in W
,
B\in[1,A]
and
u\in U_{B{\varepsilon_{0}}}
such that

\Lambda=g^{\prime}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and
\|g^{\prime}-wuw^{-1}\|<{\varepsilon_{0}}
.
Note that the norm
\|\cdot\|
is preserved by left and right multiplication by elements from
W
;
hence letting
g=(g_{ij}):=w^{-1}g^{\prime}w
we have
\|g-u\|<\varepsilon_{0}
, and also
g{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{r}=w^{-1}(g^{\prime}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{r})=\{\mathbf{0}\}

(this is true since
w{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}={\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{r}=w^{-1}{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
).
Hence by Lemma
4.6
,
and by our choice of
C
,
we have
0\leq g_{ii}-(1-r)\leq Cr
for all
i
and
|g_{ij}g_{ji}|\leq Cr
for all
i\neq j
. Furthermore, it follows from
u\in U_{B{\varepsilon_{0}}}
and
\|g-u\|<\varepsilon_{0}

that
\|g-u^{\prime}\|<(B+1)\varepsilon_{0}
for some
u^{\prime}\in U_{0}
;
hence a fortiori

\|g-u^{\prime}\|<2A\varepsilon_{0}<\beta
,
which implies that
|\det\tilde{g}-1|<\frac{1}{2}

by our choice of
\beta
.
It also follows that

|g_{ij}|<\frac{1}{2}+2A\varepsilon_{0}<1
for all

. Hence
g\in{\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{r,C}}
,
and thus
g^{\prime}=wgw^{-1}\in w\,{\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{r,C}}\,w^{-1}

and
\Lambda=g^{\prime}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\in\pi(w\,{\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{r,C}}\,w^{-1})
, finishing the proof.
∎

5.
Measure estimates of the thickenings

Fix
m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and let
d=m+n
. Let
\bm{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
\bm{\beta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be two fixed weight vectors as in Theorem
1.2
.
As mentioned in Remark
9
, in order to incorporate the case of general weights,
we need to consider a more general one-parameter subgroup of
G
associated to
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
.
Explicitly, for any
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
let us define

(5.1)

\displaystyle g_{s}=g_{s}^{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}:=\operatorname{diag}(e^{\alpha_{1}s},\ldots,e^{\alpha_{m}s},e^{-\beta_{1}s},\ldots,e^{-\beta_{n}s})\in G.

Let
\Delta:X_{d}\to[0,\infty)
be the function defined in (
1.4
).
The main result of this section is an asymptotic estimate for the measure of the thickened set

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}_{r}:=\bigcup_{0\leq s<1}g_{-s}\,\Delta^{-1}[0,r],

when
r>0
is small.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\varkappa_{d}=\frac{d^{2}+d-4}{2}
and
\lambda_{d}=\frac{d(d-1)}{2}
be as in Theorem
1.2
. Then

\displaystyle\mu_{d}\bigl(\widetilde{\Delta}_{r}\bigr)\asymp_{d}r^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{r}\Bigr),\qquad\textrm{as $r\to 0^{+}$},

where the implicit constant is independent of
r
and the two weight vectors
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
.

Just as for Theorem
2.1
, we prove Theorem
5.1
by proving the upper and lower bounds separately.

5.1.
Proof of the upper bound

Note that for any
(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

g_{-s}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\bm{x}\\
\bm{y}\end{smallmatrix}\right)=(e^{-\alpha_{1}s}x_{1},\ldots,e^{-\alpha_{m}s}x_{m},e^{\beta_{1}s}y_{1},\ldots,e^{\beta_{n}s}y_{n})^{t}.

This implies that

\displaystyle|\Delta(g_{-s}\Lambda)-\Delta(\Lambda)|\leq\max\bigl\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}\bigr\}|s|<|s|,\qquad\forall\ s\in{\mathbb{R}},\ \Lambda\in X_{d}.

Hence

(5.2)

\displaystyle g_{-s}\Delta^{-1}\big[0,r]\subset\Delta^{-1}[0,r+|s|\big],\qquad\forall\ s\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Given any
r\in(0,1)
, let
q=\left\lceil{1/r}\right\rceil
. Using (
5.2
) and the fact that
1/q\leq r
, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}_{r}=\bigcup_{0\leq s<1}g_{-s}\Delta^{-1}[0,r]=\bigcup_{k=0}^{q-1}\,\bigcup_{0\leq s<1/q}g_{-k/q}\,g_{-s}\,\Delta^{-1}[0,r]\subset\bigcup_{k=0}^{q-1}g_{-k/q}\,\Delta^{-1}[0,2r],

implying that (using the
G
-invariance of
\mu_{d}
and
q\asymp r^{-1}
)

\displaystyle\mu_{d}\bigl(\widetilde{\Delta}_{r}\bigr)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\mu_{d}\bigl(g_{-k/q}\,\Delta^{-1}[0,2r]\bigr)\asymp r^{-1}\mu_{d}\big(\Delta^{-1}[0,2r]\big).

Finally, by Theorem
1.3
we get

\displaystyle\mu_{d}\bigl(\widetilde{\Delta}_{r}\bigr)\ll_{d}r^{-1}r^{\varkappa_{d}+1}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{r}\Bigr)=r^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{r}\Bigr),\qquad\textrm{as $r\to 0^{+}$}.

This finishes the proof of the upper bound in Theorem
5.1
.

5.2.
Proof of the lower bound

In this subsection we prove the lower bound in Theorem
5.1
.
By the discussion in the beginning of Section
2
,
we may replace the set
\Delta^{-1}[0,r]
by
K_{r}
, that is, it suffices to prove the following lower bound

(5.3)

\displaystyle\mu_{d}\Biggl(\,\bigcup_{0\leq s<1}g_{-s}\,K_{r}\Biggr)\gg_{d}r^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{r}\Bigr),\qquad\text{as $r\to 0^{+}$}.

The following lemma is the crucial ingredient in our proof of (
5.3
).
Let
c_{0}
be the small parameter which we fixed in Section
2.2
;
after possibly shrinking
c_{0}
, we may without loss of generality assume that
0<c_{0}<(3e)^{-1}
.
For
r\in(0,c_{0}/d)
, let
\underline{K}_{r}\subset K_{r}
be as in (
3
).

Lemma 5.2
.

For any
r\in(0,c_{0}/d)
and
s\in[r,1)
,
the two sets

\underline{K}_{r}
and
g_{-s}\,\underline{K}_{r}
are disjoint.

Proof.

Assume the opposite; then there exist

r\in(0,c_{0}/d)
,
s\in[r,1)

such that
\underline{K}_{r}\cap g_{-s}\underline{K}_{r}\neq\varnothing
,
or equivalently
g_{s}\underline{K}_{r}\cap\underline{K}_{r}\neq\varnothing
.
Pick a lattice
\Lambda\in g_{s}\underline{K}_{r}\cap\underline{K}_{r}
;
thus
\Lambda\in\underline{K}_{r}
and
g_{-s}\Lambda\in\underline{K}_{r}
.

By the definition of
\underline{K}_{r}
in (
3
),
we now have
\Lambda=p_{\bm{b}_{1},\dots,\bm{b}_{d-1}}u_{\bm{x}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
for some
vectors
\bm{b}_{j}=(b_{1j},\ldots,b_{dj})^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(
j=1,\ldots,d-1
)
and
\bm{x}\in(0,c_{0}/d)^{d-1}

satisfying
(
3.10
), (
3.11
), (
3.12
) and (
3.13
);
moreover, we also have

g_{-s}\Lambda=p_{\bm{b}^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\bm{b}^{\prime}_{d-1}}u_{\bm{x}^{\prime}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
for some
vectors
\bm{b}^{\prime}_{j}=(b_{1j}^{\prime},\ldots,b_{dj}^{\prime})^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(
j=1,\ldots,d-1
)
and
\bm{x}^{\prime}\in(0,c_{0}/d)^{d-1}

which again satisfy
(
3.10
), (
3.11
), (
3.12
) and (
3.13
).

Because of
{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\in({\mathbb{R}}_{>0})^{m}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}=1
,
there exists an index
1\leq i\leq m
such that
\alpha_{i}\geq\tfrac{1}{m}
.
Fixing such an
i
, we consider the vector

\displaystyle{\text{\boldmath$y$}}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})^{t}:=g_{-s}{\text{\boldmath$b$}}_{i}=\bigl(e^{-\alpha_{1}s}b_{1i},\ldots,e^{-\alpha_{m}s}b_{mi},e^{\beta_{1}s}b_{m+1,i},\ldots,e^{\beta_{n}s}b_{d,i}\bigr)^{t}.

By (
3.10
) we have

|b_{ji}|<c_{0}
for all
j\neq i
, and

1-\frac{r}{2d}<b_{ii}<1
.
Hence
|y_{j}|<ec_{0}
for all
j\neq i
(since
0<s<1
and
\beta_{\ell}\leq 1
for all
1\leq\ell\leq n
),
and
0<y_{i}<e^{-\alpha_{i}s}<e^{-s/m}<1-\frac{s}{2m}<1-\frac{r}{2d}
.
We have
{\text{\boldmath$b$}}_{i}\in\Lambda\smallsetminus\{\mathbf{0}\}

and thus
{\text{\boldmath$y$}}=g_{-s}{\text{\boldmath$b$}}_{i}\in g_{-s}\Lambda\smallsetminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
;
also
g_{-s}\Lambda\in\underline{K}_{r}\subset K_{r}
,
and hence
{\text{\boldmath$y$}}\notin{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
.
But for all
j\neq i
we have
|y_{j}|<ec_{0}<\frac{1}{3}<1-r

(indeed, recall that
0<c_{0}<(3e)^{-1}
);
also
y_{i}>0
; hence
{\text{\boldmath$y$}}\notin{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
implies
y_{i}\geq 1-r
.
In summary:

\displaystyle|y_{j}|<ec_{0}\quad(\forall\ j\neq i)\qquad\text{and}\quad 1-r\leq y_{i}<1-\tfrac{r}{2d}.

Furthermore,
{\text{\boldmath$b$}}_{i}^{\prime}\in g_{-s}\Lambda
,
since
g_{-s}\Lambda=p_{\bm{b}^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\bm{b}^{\prime}_{d-1}}u_{\bm{x}^{\prime}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
;
and
by (
3.10
) we have

|b^{\prime}_{ji}|<c_{0}
for all
j\neq i
and

1-\frac{r}{2d}<b^{\prime}_{ii}<1
.
It follows that
|b^{\prime}_{ji}-y_{j}|<(e+1)c_{0}<(e+1)(3e)^{-1}<\frac{1}{2}<1-r
for all
j\neq i
,
and
|b^{\prime}_{ii}-y_{i}|<r<1-r
;
hence
{\text{\boldmath$b$}}^{\prime}_{i}-{\text{\boldmath$y$}}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
.
Note also that
{\text{\boldmath$b$}}_{i}^{\prime}-{\text{\boldmath$y$}}\in g_{-s}\Lambda
,
and
{\text{\boldmath$b$}}_{i}^{\prime}-{\text{\boldmath$y$}}\neq\mathbf{0}
, since
y_{i}<1-\frac{r}{2d}<b^{\prime}_{ii}
;
hence we have obtained a contradiction against
g_{-s}\Lambda\in\underline{K}_{r}\subset K_{r}
.
This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

It follows from Lemma
5.2
that
for any
r\in(0,c_{0}/d)
,
the sets
g_{-kr}\,\underline{K}_{r}
,
for
k
running through the integers in the interval

0\leq k<1/r
,
are pairwise disjoint.
(Indeed, if
g_{-kr}\,\underline{K}_{r}\cap g_{-k^{\prime}r}\,\underline{K}_{r}\neq\varnothing

for some
0\leq k<k^{\prime}<1/r
then
\underline{K}_{r}\cap g_{(k-k^{\prime})r}\,\underline{K}_{r}\neq\varnothing
,
contradicting Lemma
5.2
.)
Hence, using also
K_{r}\supset\underline{K}_{r}
,
we have

\displaystyle\mu_{d}\Biggl(\,\bigcup_{0\leq s<1}g_{-s}\,K_{r}\Biggr)\geq\mu_{d}\Biggl(\,\bigcup_{0\leq k<1/r}g_{-kr}\,\underline{K}_{r}\Biggr)=\sum_{0\leq k<1/r}\mu_{d}\bigl(g_{-kr}\,\underline{K}_{r}\bigr)=\#\bigl({\mathbb{Z}}\cap[0,1/r)\bigr)\cdot\mu_{d}\bigl(\underline{K}_{r}\bigr),

Here
\#\big({\mathbb{Z}}\cap[0,1/r)\big)\gg r^{-1}
,
and for
r
sufficiently small we have

\mu_{d}\bigl(\underline{K}_{r}\bigr)\gg r^{\varkappa_{d}+1}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\bigl(\frac{1}{r}\bigr)

by (
3.22
).
Hence we obtain the lower bound (
5.3
),
and the proof of Theorem
5.1
is complete.

\square

6.
Some preliminaries for Theorem
1.2

In this section we collect some preliminary results for our proof of Theorem
1.2
.

6.1.
Dynamical interpretation of weighted
\psi
-Dirichlet matrices

Let
m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and let
\bm{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
\bm{\beta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be two fixed weight vectors as before.
Let
t_{0}>0
and let
\psi:[t_{0},\infty)\to(0,\infty)
be a continuous decreasing function which tends to zero at infinity.
In this subsection we give a dynamical interpretation of
\psi_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}
-Dirichlet matrices which generalizes
[
26
, Proposition 4.5]
; see Proposition
6.2
. Let us first introduce the following modified Dani Correspondence which is a special case of
[
20
, Lemma 8.3]
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Fix
m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and let
d=m+n
. Let
t_{0}>0
, and let
\psi:[t_{0},\infty)\to(0,\infty)
be a continuous, decreasing function satisfying (
1.8
) and (
1.9
). Then there exists a unique continuous, decreasing function

r=r_{\psi}:[s_{0},\infty)\to(0,\infty),\quad\textrm{where $s_{0}=\frac{m}{d}\log t_{0}-\frac{n}{d}\log\psi(t_{0})$},

such that

(6.1)

the function
s\mapsto s+mr(s)
is increasing,

and

(6.2)

\psi\left(e^{s-nr(s)}\right)=e^{-s-mr(s)}\quad\textrm{for all $s\geq s_{0}$}.

Conversely, given
s_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and a continuous, decreasing function
r:[s_{0},\infty)\to(0,\infty)
satisfying (
6.1
), there exists a unique continuous, decreasing function
\psi=\psi_{r}:[t_{0},\infty)\to(0,\infty)
with
t_{0}=e^{s_{0}-nr(s_{0})}
satisfying (
1.8
), (
1.9
) and (
6.2
). Furthermore, for any fixed
\alpha,\beta>0
the series

(6.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq t_{0}}\frac{\big(1-k\psi(k)\big)^{\alpha}\big(-\log\left(1-k\psi(k)\right)\big)^{\beta}}{k}

diverges if and only if the series

(6.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq s_{0}}r(k)^{\alpha}\log^{\beta}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{r(k)}\Bigr)

diverges.

Proof.

The
\psi
and
r
-functions determine each other uniquely via the relation

(6.5)

\displaystyle\psi(t)^{1/m}e^{s/m}=t^{1/n}e^{-s/n}=e^{-r(s)},

which captures the moment when the
a_{s}
-flow transforms the long and thin ‘rectangle’

\big\{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\>:\>\|\bm{x}\|^{m}<\psi(t),\ \|\bm{y}\|^{n}<t\big\}

determined by (
1.2
) into a cube (with side length
2e^{-r(s)}
). Here
a_{s}=\operatorname{diag}(e^{s/m}I_{m},e^{-s/n}I_{n})
, as defined in the introduction.
This correspondence between
\psi(\cdot)
and
r(\cdot)
is a special case of

[
20
, Lemma 8.3]
,
as here we assume that
\psi(\cdot)
additionally satisfies (
1.8
) and (
1.9
), which on the
r
-function side corresponds respectively to the assumptions that
r(\cdot)
is decreasing and
r(s)>0
for all
s\geq s_{0}
.
The equivalence of these additional assumptions is easily checked using the following three relations,
which follow from (
6.5
):

(6.6)

\displaystyle e^{-dr(s)}=t\psi(t),\qquad s=\frac{m}{d}\log t-\frac{n}{d}\log\psi(t),\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad t=e^{s-nr(s)}.

Finally we prove the equivalence of the divergence of the two series.
If
\lim_{t\to\infty}t\psi(t)<1
then both the functions
1-t\psi(t)
and
r(s)
are bounded away from zero
(and positive), which implies that the two series in
(
6.3
) and (
6.4
) are divergent.
Hence from now on we may assume that

\lim_{t\to\infty}t\psi(t)=1
.
Then
\lim_{s\to\infty}r(s)=0

(by (
6.6
)),
and
F_{\psi}(t):=1-t\psi(t)

is a decreasing function taking values in the interval
(0,1)
and satisfying
\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{\psi}(t)=0
.
After enlarging
s_{0}
(thus also enlarging
t_{0}
)
we may assume that
0<r(s)<1/d
for all
s\geq s_{0}
.
Then by (
6.6
) we have,
with
t=t(s)=e^{s-nr(s)}
:

(6.7)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{2}r(s)<F_{\psi}(t)<d\,r(s)\quad\text{and }\quad e^{s-1}<t<e^{s},\qquad\forall\ s\geq s_{0}.

It follows that

r(s)^{\alpha}\log^{\beta}\bigl(1+\frac{1}{r(s)}\bigr)\asymp_{d,\alpha,\beta}F_{\psi}(t)^{\alpha}\log^{\beta}\bigl(\frac{1}{F_{\psi}(t)}\bigr)

for all
s\geq s_{0}
.
Hence, using also
e^{k-1}<t(k)<e^{k}
(see (
6.7
)),
the fact that
F_{\psi}(t)
is decreasing,
and
\sum_{e^{k}\leq j<e^{k+1}}\frac{1}{j}\asymp 1
(
\forall k\geq 1
),
we have for all sufficiently large integers
k
:

\displaystyle r(k)^{\alpha}\log^{\beta}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{r(k)}\Bigr)\ll_{d,\alpha,\beta}F_{\psi}(e^{k-1})^{\alpha}\log^{\beta}\Bigl(\frac{1}{F_{\psi}(e^{k-1})}\Bigr)\ll_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{e^{k-2}\leq j<e^{k-1}}\frac{1}{j}F_{\psi}(j)^{\alpha}\log^{\beta}\Bigl(\frac{1}{F_{\psi}(j)}\Bigr),

and similarly

\displaystyle r(k)^{\alpha}\log^{\beta}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{r(k)}\Bigr)\gg_{d,\alpha,\beta}F_{\psi}(e^{k})^{\alpha}\log^{\beta}\Bigl(\frac{1}{F_{\psi}(e^{k})}\Bigr)\gg_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{e^{k}\leq j<e^{k+1}}\frac{1}{j}F_{\psi}(j)^{\alpha}\log^{\beta}\Bigl(\frac{1}{F_{\psi}(j)}\Bigr).

It follows that the series in (
6.4
) diverges if and only if
\sum_{j}j^{-1}F_{\psi}(j)^{\alpha}\log^{\beta}\bigl(\frac{1}{F_{\psi}(j)}\bigr)

diverges,
that is, if and only if the series in (
6.3
) diverges.
∎

Remark 12
.

Let
\psi
and

with

. Let

)

))

.

Remark 13
.

When
\bm{\alpha}=(\tfrac{1}{m},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{m})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
\bm{\beta}=(\tfrac{1}{n},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

recovers
[

, Proposition 4.5]
.

Proof of Proposition
6.2
.

For any
t>\max\{t_{0},1\}
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{t}=\mathcal{R}^{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}_{t}:=\big\{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\>:\>\|\bm{x}\|_{\bm{\alpha}}<\psi(t),\|\bm{y}\|_{\bm{\beta}}<t\big\},

so that
(\bm{p},\bm{q})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}\times({\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\smallsetminus\{\bm{0}\})
is a solution to (

) if and only if

\left(A\bm{q}-\bm{p},\bm{q}\right)\in\mathcal{R}_{t}
.
On the other hand, the lattice

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}I_{m}&A\\
0&I_{n}\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}-\bm{p}\\
\bm{q}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}A\bm{q}-\bm{p}\\
\bm{q}\end{matrix}\right)\qquad\textrm{for}\ (\bm{p},\bm{q})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}.

It follows that

-Dirichlet if and only if

for all sufficiently large

6.2.
Effective equidistribution and doubly mixing for certain
g_{s}
-translates

Let
m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
d=m+n
be as before.
Let

\mathcal{Y}=\left\{\Lambda_{A}:A\in M_{m,n}({\mathbb{R}})\right\}\cong M_{m,n}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})

be defined as in (
1.5
), and recall that
{\mathcal{Y}}
is equipped with the probability Lebesgue measure,
Leb
.

As mentioned in Remark
9
, we will need an effective equidistribution and doubly mixing result for the
g_{s}
-translates
\{g_{s}{\mathcal{Y}}\}_{s>0}
which is analogous to (
1.13
) and (
1.14
) respectively. In fact, we will state a corollary of a more general effective mixing result of arbitrary order proved by Björklund and Gorodnik
[
2
, Theorem 2.2]
. To state their result, let us first fix some notation.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{d}({\mathbb{R}})
be the Lie algebra of
G
. For each
Y\in\mathfrak{g}
, let us denote by
\mathcal{D}_{Y}
the corresponding Lie derivative (a first order differential operator) on
C^{\infty}(G)
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{Y}(f)(g):=\frac{\text{d}}{\operatorname{d}\!{t}}f(\exp(tY)g)\big|_{t=0},\qquad f\in C^{\infty}(G).

Here
\exp:\mathfrak{g}\to G
denotes the usual exponential map from
\mathfrak{g}
to
G
. Note that this definition naturally extends to the function space
C_{c}^{\infty}(X_{d})
since we can view elements in
C_{c}^{\infty}(X_{d})
as right
\Gamma
-invariant smooth functions on
G
.
Fix an ordered basis
\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{a}\}
of
\mathfrak{g}
.
Then every monomial
Z=Y_{1}^{\ell_{1}}\cdots Y_{a}^{\ell_{a}}
defines a
differential operator of degree
\deg(Z):=\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{a}
, via

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{Z}:=\mathcal{D}_{Y_{1}}^{\ell_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{D}_{Y_{a}}^{\ell_{a}}.

Now for each
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
we define the “
L^{2}
, degree
\ell
” Sobolev norm on
C_{c}^{\infty}(X_{d})
by

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{H^{\ell}}}:=\biggl(\sum_{\deg(Z)\leq\ell}\int_{X_{d}}|\mathcal{D}_{Z}(f)|^{2}\,\operatorname{d}\!{\mu}_{d}\biggr)^{1/2},

where the summation is over all the monomials
Z
in
\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{a}\}
with degree no greater than
\ell
.
Fix a metric
\textrm{dist}(\cdot,\cdot)
on
X_{d}=G/\Gamma
which is induced from a right
G
-invariant
Riemannian metric on
G
. We also define the following Lipschitz (semi-)norm on
C_{c}^{\infty}(X_{d})
with respect to this metric:

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\operatorname{Lip}}:=\sup\left\{\frac{|f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})|}{\textrm{dist}(x_{1},x_{2})}\>:\>x_{1},x_{2}\in X_{d},\ x_{1}\neq x_{2}\right\},\qquad f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X_{d}).

Let us also write
\|\cdot\|_{C^{0}}
for the uniform norm on
C_{c}(X_{d})
.
Finally, for any
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X_{d})
we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(f):=\max\left\{\|f\|_{C^{0}},\|f\|_{\operatorname{Lip}},\|f\|_{{H^{\ell}}}\right\}.

We can now state the result which we need from
[
2
]
.

Theorem 6.3

(
[
2
, Corollary 2.4]
)
.

There exist
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\delta>0
such that for every
b\in{\mathbb{N}}
and any

f_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{Y})
,
f_{1},\ldots,f_{b}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X_{d})
and
s_{1},\ldots,s_{b}>0
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{Y}}f_{0}(\Lambda_{A})\left(\prod_{i=1}^{b}f_{i}(g_{s_{i}}\Lambda_{A})\right)\,\operatorname{d}\!{A}

\displaystyle=\textrm{Leb}(f_{0})\prod_{i=1}^{b}\mu_{d}(f_{i})+O_{b,f_{0}}\left(e^{-\delta D(s_{1},\ldots,s_{b})}\prod_{i=1}^{b}\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(f_{i})\right),

where
D(s_{1},\ldots,s_{b}):=\min\left\{s_{i},|s_{i}-s_{j}|\>:\>1\leq i\neq j\leq b\right\}
.

(In fact, in
[
2
, Corollary 2.4]
, the error term is also explicit in terms of
f_{0}
.)

Taking
f_{0}\equiv 1
on
\mathcal{Y}
and
b=1,2
, we get the following effective equidistribution and doubly mixing of the family of
g_{s}
-translates
\{g_{s}\mathcal{Y}\}_{s>0}
in
X_{d}
.

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\delta>0
be as in Theorem
6.3
.
Then for any
f,f_{1},f_{2}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(X_{d})
and
s,s_{1},s_{2}>0
,

(6.9)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{Y}}f(g_{s}\Lambda_{A})\,\operatorname{d}\!{A}=\mu_{d}(f)+O\left(e^{-\delta s}\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(f)\right),

and

(6.10)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{Y}}f_{1}(g_{s_{1}}\Lambda_{A})f_{2}(g_{s_{2}}\Lambda_{A})\,\operatorname{d}\!{A}=\mu_{d}(f_{1})\mu_{d}(f_{2})+O\left(e^{-\delta\min\{s_{1},s_{2},|s_{1}-s_{2}|\}}\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(f_{1})\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(f_{2})\right).

6.3.
Smooth approximations and estimates on norms

In this subsection we prove the existence of smooth functions

bounding our shrinking targets from above and below in an appropriate sense,

Recall that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}_{r}:=\bigcup_{0\leq s<1}g_{-s}\Delta^{-1}[0,r]\qquad{(0<r<1)}.

Let

Let

We will need the following auxiliary lemma.

(The implied constant in the bound on

We may assume that the support of

For each

\displaystyle\eta_{t}({\text{\boldmath$x$}})=t^{-a}\eta(t^{-1}{\text{\boldmath$x$}})\qquad({\text{\boldmath$x$}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{a}),

Let us choose the constant

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\phi_{r}(g_{s}\Lambda_{A})\,\operatorname{d}\!{A}\>\>\begin{cases}{\displaystyle\asymp_{d}r^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{r}\bigr)}&\textrm{if $r>e^{-\eta s}$},\\[8.0pt]
{\displaystyle\ll_{d}e^{-\varkappa_{d}\eta s/2}}&\textrm{if $r\leq e^{-\eta s}$.}\end{cases}

In particular, for any
sequence
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset(0,\frac{1}{2})

with
\lim_{k\to\infty}\rho_{k}=0
, we have

(7.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\phi_{\rho_{k}}(g_{k}\Lambda_{A})\,\operatorname{d}\!{A}=\infty\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sum_{k}\rho_{k}^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{k}}\right)=\infty.

Proof.

First we note that by Theorem
5.1
and the relation
\chi_{\widetilde{\Delta}_{r}}\leq\phi_{r}\leq\chi_{\widetilde{\Delta}_{2r}}
(see Lemma
6.5
),
we have

\mu_{d}(\phi_{r})\asymp_{d}r^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)
.
Furthermore, if

r>e^{-\eta s}
, then
the ratio of the main term and the error term in
(
7.1
) satisfies:

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{d}(\phi_{r})}{e^{-\delta s}r^{-L}}\asymp_{d}\frac{r^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{r}\bigr)}{e^{-\delta s}r^{-L}}>\log^{\lambda_{d}}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{r}\bigr),

which we can force to be as large as we like by taking the constant
r_{0}
sufficiently small
(in a way which only depends on
d
).
Hence it follows from (
7.1
)
that (
7.2
) holds
in the case
r>e^{-\eta s}
.

Next assume
r\leq e^{-\eta s}
.
Set

\rho:=2e^{-\eta s}
.
If
\rho<r_{0}
, then by what we proved in the previous paragraph,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\phi_{\rho}(g_{s}\Lambda_{A})\,\operatorname{d}\!{A}\asymp_{d}\rho^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\rho}\bigr)\ll_{d}e^{-\varkappa_{d}\eta s/2},

and hence the bound in (
7.2
) follows,
since

\phi_{r}\leq\chi_{\widetilde{\Delta}_{2r}}\leq\chi_{\widetilde{\Delta}_{\rho}}\leq\phi_{\rho}

(again see Lemma
6.5
).
In the remaining case when
\rho\geq r_{0}
,
we have
s\ll_{d}1
and
e^{-\varkappa_{d}\eta s/2}\gg_{d}1
,
and hence the bound in (
7.2
) holds simply because of
\phi_{r}\leq 1
.
This completes the proof of (
7.2
).

For the last part of the lemma, since
\lim_{k\to\infty}\rho_{k}=0
, after possibly deleting finitely many terms from
the two sums in (
7.3
),
we may assume
\rho_{k}<r_{0}
for all appearing terms.
Next, using the second bound in (
7.2
)
and the fact that both of the series

and

converge,
it follows that the two divergence statements in
(
7.3
) remain unaffected if all the terms
for which
\rho_{k}\leq e^{-\eta k}
are removed from the respective series. After this operation, the equivalence in
(

relation in (
7.2
).
∎

7.3.
The divergence case

In this subsection we prove the divergence case of Theorem
1.2
. We first record from

[
30
]

the following divergence Borel-Cantelli lemma which we will use.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\mu)
be a probability space. Let
\{h_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of measurable functions on
\mathcal{X}
taking values in
[0,1]
. Let
b_{k}:=\mu(h_{k})
. Suppose
\sum_{k}b_{k}=\infty
and

(7.8)

\displaystyle\liminf_{k_{2}\to\infty}\frac{\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left(\sum_{i=k_{1}}^{k_{2}}h_{i}(x)-\sum_{i=k_{1}}^{k_{2}}b_{i}\right)^{2}\operatorname{d}\!{\mu}(x)}{\left(\sum_{i=k_{1}}^{k_{2}}b_{i}\right)^{2}}=0\quad\textrm{for some $k_{1}\in{\mathbb{N}}$}.

Then for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
h_{k}(x)>0
infinitely often.

Proof.

Let
Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots
be the sequence of random variables defined by

Y_{k}(x)=\sum_{i=k_{1}}^{k+k_{1}-1}h_{i}(x)
(
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
).
Note that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}\biggl(\sum_{i=k_{1}}^{k_{2}}h_{i}(x)-\sum_{i=k_{1}}^{k_{2}}b_{i}\biggr)^{2}\,\operatorname{d}\!{\mu}(x)=\mu\bigl(Y_{k_{2}-k_{1}+1}^{2}\bigr)-\mu\bigl(Y_{k_{2}-k_{1}+1}\bigr)^{2};

hence (
7.8
) implies that

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mu(Y_{k})^{2}/\mu(Y_{k}^{2})=1
.
Therefore by part (iii) of the main theorem in

[
30
]
,
for
\mu
-a.e.
x
we have

\limsup_{k\to\infty}Y_{k}(x)/\mu(Y_{k})>0
.
Also
\mu(Y_{k})=\sum_{i=k_{1}}^{k+k_{1}-1}b_{i}\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
.
Hence it follows that
for
\mu
-a.e.
x
we have

\lim_{k\to\infty}Y_{k}(x)=+\infty
,
and in particular
h_{i}(x)>0
for infinitely many
i
.
∎

Remark 15
.

) by the weaker assumption that

:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=k_{1}}^{k_{2}}b_{j}\gg_{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}\leq j\leq k_{2}\\
(\rho_{j}>e^{-\eta j})\end{subarray}}\rho_{j}^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\rho_{j}}\Bigr)\gg\sum_{j=k_{1}}^{k_{2}}\rho_{j}^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\rho_{j}}\Bigr)\gg_{\omega_{2}}\sum_{j=k_{1}+1}^{k_{2}+1}r(j)^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{r(j)}\Bigr),

where the second relation holds since
the series
\sum_{j=k_{1}}^{\infty}\rho_{j}^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\bigl(\frac{1}{\rho_{j}}\bigr)
diverges (this follows from the relation
\rho_{j}=\omega_{2}r(j+1)/2
and the fact that the series
\sum_{j}r(j)^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{r(j)}\bigr)
diverges), while the same sum restricted to those
j
for which

\rho_{j}\leq e^{-\eta j}
is convergent.

Combining the last two bounds, we conclude that for any
k_{1}>C
, and for
k_{2}
sufficiently large,

(7.17)

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{k_{1},k_{2}}}{\left(\sum_{j=k_{1}}^{k_{2}}b_{j}\right)^{2}}\ll_{d,\omega_{2}}\frac{1+\sum_{j=k_{1}+1}^{k_{2}+1}r(j)^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\lambda_{d}+1}\bigl(1+\frac{1}{r(j)}\bigr)}{\Bigl(\sum_{j=k_{1}+1}^{k_{2}+1}r(j)^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\bigl(1+\frac{1}{r(j)}\bigr)\Bigr)^{2}}.

Since the series
\sum_{j}r(j)^{\varkappa_{d}}\log^{\lambda_{d}}\bigl(1+\frac{1}{r(j)}\bigr)
diverges,
condition (
7.9
) implies that
the limit inferior of the expression in the right hand side of (
7.17
)
tends to zero as
k_{2}\to\infty
.
Hence (
7.8
) holds.
We have also noted that
\sum_{k}b_{k}=\infty
.
Hence by Lemma
7.2
,
for
Leb
-a.e.
A\in M_{m,n}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
we have
h_{k}(\Lambda_{A})=f_{k}(g_{k}\Lambda_{A})>0
infinitely often. Together with (
7.10
), this implies that for
Leb
-a.e.
A\in M_{m,n}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
, the lattice
g_{k}\Lambda_{A}
belongs to
\operatorname{supp}(f_{k})\subset\underline{B}_{k}
for infinitely many
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. This finishes the proof.
∎
