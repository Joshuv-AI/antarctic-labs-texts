Stationary Descendents and the Discriminant Modular Form
Adam Afandi

Abstract
The generating functions of stationary descendent Gromov-Witten invariants of an elliptic curve are known to be Fourier expansions of quasimodular forms. When one restricts to the subspace of forms of a fixed weight k k , there is an abundance of linear relations among these generating functions. This naturally leads one to study the resulting linear matroid, which we refer to as the descendent matroid of weight k k . In the case of weight 12, we use this matroid to compute and organize all of the ways to express the discriminant modular form in terms of stationary descendents. As a consequence, we find a closed-form expression of Ramanujan tau values in terms of Gromov-Witten invariants of an elliptic curve. All computations were aided with the use of Sage, and the classes and functions written in Sage are discussed in the appendix.

Introduction

Let
z\in\mathcal{H}
be a coordinate in the upper half plane. Recall the
discriminant modular form

\Delta
, given by

\Delta(z):=q\prod_{n\geq 1}(1-q^{n})^{24}:=\sum_{n\geq 1}\tau(n)q^{n}

where
q=e^{2\pi iz}
. The function
\tau:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
is often referred to as the
Ramanujan tau function
, in honor of Ramanujan’s work on
\tau(n)

[
Ram16
]
. What makes
\tau(n)
an intriguing function are its remarkable arithmetic properties. For example, we have the following properties, once conjectured by Ramanujan himself, and then proven by Mordell
[
MM17
]
and Deligne
[
Del74
]
:

1.

(m,n)=1\implies\tau(mn)=\tau(m)\tau(n)

2.

p\ \text{prime},\ r>0\implies\tau(p^{r+1})=\tau(p)\tau(p^{r})-p^{11}\tau(p^{r-1})

3.

p\ \text{prime}\implies|\tau(p)|\leq 2p^{\frac{11}{2}}

One of the longstanding problems concerning the Ramanujan tau function is proving that
\tau(n)\not=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This problem is often referred to as
Lehmer’s conjecture
, as Lehmer was the first to posit its validity
[
Leh47
]
. The computational evidence corroborating Lehmer’s conjecture is substantial, see for example (
[
Ser12
]
, pg. 98).

In order to gain more insight as to why Lehmer’s conjecture might be true, it’s worthwhile to find new ways to interpret what the Ramanujan tau function is actually computing. In
[
Nie75
]
, Niebur found a closed form expression for
\tau(n)
in terms of the sum-of-divisors function,

\tau(n)=n^{4}\sigma(n)-24\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}i^{2}(35i^{2}-52in+18n^{2})\sigma(i)\sigma(n-i)

In
[
GS18
]
, Garvan and Schlosser found a combinatorial expression for
\tau(n)
in terms of vector partition functions. In the same spirit of trying to find different interpretations of
\tau(n)
, in this paper, we systematically compute all of the ways one can express
\tau(n)
in terms of disconnected Gromov-Witten invariants of an elliptic curve. The motivation of our investigation is to provide more tools and perspectives to attack Lehmer’s conjecture, and to gain a better understanding of how Gromov-Witten invariants generate the ring of quasimodular forms.

First, let’s recall why such a computation is even possible. The function
\Delta
is an important example of a
modular form
, that is, an element in the ring
\text{M}:=\mathbb{Q}[E_{4},E_{6}]
, where
E_{k}
is the (non-normalized) Eisenstein series of weight
k\in 2\mathbb{Z}
, defined by

E_{k}:=-\frac{B_{k}}{2k}+\sum_{n\geq 1}\sigma_{k-1}(n)q^{n}

(1)

where
B_{k}
is the
k^{th}
Bernoulli number, and
\sigma_{k-1}(n):=\sum_{d|n}d^{k-1}
. It’s a nice exercise to show that

\Delta=8000E_{4}^{3}-147E_{6}^{2}

In the literature, one often sees
\Delta
expressed in the normalized Eisenstein series, in which the Eisenstein series has constant term
1
. In this case, the expression for
\Delta
reads
(E_{4}^{3}-E_{6}^{2})/1728
. In this paper, we choose to use the non-normalized Eisenstein series, as it aligns better with the results that are cited.

We say that the modular form
E_{4}^{i}E_{6}^{j}\in\text{M}
, has
weight

4i+6j
. This weight function induces a homogeneous grading on
\text{M}:=\bigoplus M_{k}
, where
M_{k}:=\{\text{forms of weight $k$}\}
. We can use Equation
1
to define the Eisenstein series
E_{2}
. However,
E_{2}
is not a modular form, but is rather an example of a
quasimodular form
. The ring of quasimodular forms
\text{QM}:=\mathbb{Q}[E_{2},E_{4},E_{6}]
was first introduced and studied by Zagier and Kaneko in
[
KZ95
]
, and the theory was further elaborated in
[
BvdGH+08
]
. Similar to the case of modular forms, we say a monomial
E_{2}^{a}E_{4}^{b}E_{6}^{c}\in\text{QM}
has weight
2a+4b+6c
, which induces the grading
\text{QM}=\bigoplus\text{QM}_{k}
, where
\text{QM}_{k}:=\{\text{forms of weight $k$}\}
.

Let
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\bullet}_{g,n}(E,d)
be the moduli stack of possibly disconnected stable maps of degree
d
from
n
-pointed genus
g
curves to a smooth elliptic curve
E
. This space has virtual dimension
2g-2+n
. It comes equipped with
n
evaluation maps
\text{ev}_{i}:\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\bullet}_{g,n}(E,d)\rightarrow E
. Furthermore, we let
\psi_{i}:=c_{1}(\mathbb{L}_{i})\in H^{2}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\bullet}_{g,n}(E,d))
, where
\mathbb{L}_{i}
is the
i^{th}
universal cotangent line bundle. We call the
\psi
-classes
descendent classes
. The
disconnected stationary descendent Gromov-Witten invariants
of
E
are intersection numbers of the form

\left<\tau_{k_{1}}\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}\right>^{\bullet E}_{d}:=\int_{\left[.\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}^{\bullet}(E,d)\right]^{\text{vir}}}\psi_{1}^{k_{1}}\text{ev}_{1}^{*}([pt.])\ldots\psi_{n}^{k_{n}}\text{ev}_{n}^{*}([pt.])

The starting point of our investigation begins with a result of Okounkov and Pandharipande:

Theorem 1
.

[
[
OP06
]
]
Let
k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}\geq 0
be integers. Then the generating function

\left<\tau_{k_{1}}\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}\right>:=\prod_{n\geq 1}(1-q^{n})\sum_{d\geq 0}\left<\tau_{k_{1}}\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}\right>^{\bullet E}_{d}q^{d}

is a quasimodular form of weight
k=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(k_{i}+2)
.

Throughout this paper, we refer to the generating functions
\left<\tau_{k_{1}}\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}\right>
simply as
stationary descendents of weight
k=\sum(k_{i}+2)
.

Theorem
1
tells us that we have a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear map

\text{GW}_{k}:\bigoplus_{\sum(k_{i}+2)=k}\mathbb{Q}\cdot\left<\tau_{k_{1}}\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}\right>\rightarrow\text{QM}_{k}

In order to compute
\Delta
in terms of stationary descendents of weight 12, we need to understand the linear map
\text{GW}_{12}
. However, it turns out that trying to understand
\text{GW}_{k}
for all weights
k
leads to some interesting combinatorics. The reason is as follows. If we want to understand
\text{GW}_{k}
, we need to compute its kernel and image. But as
k
grows larger, the amount of linear-algebraic data that determines
\text{GW}_{k}
becomes formidable. Fortunately, there already exists combinatorial machinery that helps one think clearly about this data, namely, the theory of
matroids
.

Matroids are combinatorial objects that abstract the notion of linear independence. They were first introduced by Whitney
[
Whi92
]
, and form a pillar of modern research in combinatorics
[
Oxl11
]
. For a nice survey on how matroids have steered mathematical progress up to the present time, we recommend the ICM article of Ardila
[
Ard21
]
.

Each linear map
\text{GW}_{k}
is completely determined by the matroid
\mathcal{M}_{k}
, whose ground set is the set of stationary descendents of weight
k
, and whose bases are the collections of stationary descendents that form a basis of
\text{QM}_{k}
. We refer to this matroid as the
descendent matroid of weight
k
. In this paper, we initiate the study of the matroids
\mathcal{M}_{k}
.

Stationary Descendents and Quasimodular Forms

In this section, we introduce the ring of quasimodular forms
QM
, and recall the relevant results in
[
OP06
]
that allow us to go back and forth between Gromov-Witten invariants of an elliptic curve
E
and quasimodular forms.

2.1
The Ring of Quasimodular Forms

Let’s begin with the classical theory of modular forms on the full modular group. See
[
DS05
]
for a standard reference.

Definition 1
.

Let
f:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a holomorphic function on the upper half plane
\mathcal{H}
that is holomorphic at
\infty
. For any even integer
k
, we say
f
is a
modular form
of weight
k
if, for all
\gamma=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}\in\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and for all
z\in\mathcal{H}
,

f(\gamma(z))=(cz+d)^{k}f(z)

Every modular form
f
can be expanded into its Fourier coefficients
a_{n}(f)
,

f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}(f)e^{2\pi izn}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}(f)q^{n},\hskip 14.22636ptq:=2\pi iz

When we write
f(q)
, we are referring to the Fourier expansion of
f
, and will otherwise write
f(z)
. The
Eisenstein series
of weight
k
, denoted
E_{k}
, is defined to be

E_{k}:=-\frac{B_{k}}{2k}+\sum_{n\geq 1}\sigma_{k-1}(n)q^{n}

It turns out that
E_{4}
and
E_{6}
are modular forms of weight
4
and
6
, respectively, and that the ring of modular forms
M
is freely generated by
E_{4}
and
E_{6}
i.e.
\text{M}=\mathbb{Q}[E_{4},E_{6}]
. The weight of the modular form
E_{4}^{i}E_{6}^{j}
is
4i+6j
, and this induces a grading on
\text{M}=\bigoplus M_{k}
, where
M_{k}:=\{\text{forms of weight $k$}\}
. In this paper, we are interested in finding different ways to express the
discriminant modular form
.

Definition 2
.

The
discriminant modular form

\Delta
is the unique
cusp form
(i.e. modular form with vanishing constant term) of weight
12
with Fourier coefficient
a_{1}=1
. Specifically,

\Delta:=q\prod_{n\geq 1}(1-q^{n})^{2}4=\sum_{n\geq 1}\tau(n)q^{n}

One would hope that
E_{2}
is a ‘modular form of weight 2’, but this is not the case, since

E_{2}(\gamma z)=(cz+d)^{2}E_{2}(z)+\frac{ic}{4\pi}(cz+d)

(2)

for all
\gamma\in\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
z\in\mathcal{H}
(see
[
BvdGH+08
]
, pg. 19). To allow
E_{2}
to mingle with
M
in a consistent way, we relax the condition of modularity.

Definition 3

(
[
Roy12
]
, Definition 3.2)
.

Let
f:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a holomorphic function. We say
f
is a
quasimodular form
of weight
k
and depth
s\geq 0
if there exists holomorphic functions
f_{0},\ldots,f_{s}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
with
f_{s}\not=0
, such that, for all
\gamma=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}\in\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
z\in\mathcal{H}
,

(cz+d)^{-k}f(\gamma z)=\sum_{j=0}^{s}f_{j}(z)\left(\frac{c}{cz+d}\right)^{j}

The Eisenstein series
E_{2}
is a quasimodular form of weight
2
and depth
1
. Indeed, if we let
f_{0}=E_{2}
and
f_{1}=\frac{i}{4\pi}
, we get back the transformation of Equation
2
. Furthermore, every modular form of weight
k
is also a quasimodular form of weight
k
and depth
0
.

The ring of quasimodular forms
QM
is freely generated by
E_{2},E_{4}
, and
E_{6}
, so that
\text{QM}=\mathbb{Q}[E_{2},E_{4},E_{6}]
. The monomial
E_{2}^{a}E_{4}^{b}E_{6}^{c}
is a quasimodular form of weight
2a+4b+6c
, and depth
a
. As in the case of
M
,
\text{QM}=\bigoplus\text{QM}_{k}
has a natural grading by weight. Intuitively, one should think of the depth of a quasimodular form
f
as a measure of how far
f
is from being modular. Indeed, any quasimodular form
f\in\text{QM}
of weight
k
and depth
s
can be uniquely written as

f=\sum_{j=0}^{s}m_{j}E_{2}^{j}

where
m_{j}
is a modular form of weight
k-2j
. The higher the depth, the more nonmodular terms appear in the sum above.

2.2
Stationary Descendents of Weight
k

Let
g
and
n
be nonnegative integers satisfying
2g-2+n>0
. Let
X
be a smooth projective variety, and let
d\in H_{2}(X)
be a curve class. If
(C,p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
is a nodal curve of genus
g
with
n
smooth maked points, a morphism
f:C\rightarrow X
is a
stable map
of degree
d
if
f_{*}([C])=d
, and, whenever
f
contracts a rational component
C_{0}\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
,
C_{0}
is incident to at least
3
‘special’ points (marked points or nodes). We denote by
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)
the moduli stack of stable maps to
X
of degree
d
. We denote by
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}^{\bullet}(X,d)
the stack of possibly disconnected stable maps to
X
. The space of stable maps (both connected and possibly disconnected) has virtual dimension

\text{vdim}\left(\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\bullet}_{g,n}(X,d)\right)=\int_{d}c_{1}(T_{X})+(\text{dim}(X)-3)(1-g)+n

We can compute intersection numbers on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}^{\bullet}(X,d)
by integrating cohomology classes against the virtual fundamental class
\left[\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}^{\bullet}(E,d)\right]^{\text{vir}}\in H_{e}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}^{\bullet}(X,d))
, where
e
is the virtual dimension.

This paper is concerned with the special case when
X=E
is a smooth elliptic curve. In this case, the homology class
d
is assumed to be a nonnegative integer. The space
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}^{\bullet}(E,d)
comes equipped with
n
evaluation maps
\text{ev}_{i}:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}^{\bullet}(E,d)\rightarrow E,\ 1\leq i\leq n
. The map
\text{ev}_{i}
sends a stable map
\left[f:(C,p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})\rightarrow E\right]
to
f(p_{i})\in E
. Furthermore, we consider the cohomology classes
\psi_{i}:=c_{1}(\mathbb{L}_{i})\in H^{2}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}^{\bullet}(E,d))
, where
\mathbb{L}_{i}
is the line bundle whose fiber over a point
[f:(C,p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})\rightarrow E]
is the cotangent space to the
i^{th}
marked point
p_{i}\in C
. These
\psi
-classes are called
descendent classes
. We are concerned with intersection numbers of the form

\left<\tau_{k_{1}}\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}\right>_{d}^{\bullet E}:=\int_{\left[\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}^{\bullet}(E,d)\right]^{\text{vir}}}\psi_{1}^{k_{1}}\text{ev}_{1}^{*}\left(\left[pt.\right]\right)\ldots\psi_{n}^{k_{n}}\text{ev}_{n}^{*}\left(\left[pt.\right]\right)

These intersection numbers are referred to as
stationary descendent Gromov-Witten invariants
of
E
. The word ‘stationary’ is used because the classes
\text{ev}_{i}^{*}\left(\left[pt.\right]\right)
restrain the stable map to send the marked points to a fixed set of points. Our first goal in this section is to answer:
how do we compute stationary descendent GW invariants of E?
Fortunately, there is a nice closed formula for these intersection numbers.

In order to write down this closed formula, we need to recall some basic facts from the representation theory of the symmetric group (see, for example,
[
Sag13
]
). Let
S_{d}
be the symmetric group on
d
letters. The
irreducible characters
of
S_{d}
are in bijection with the set of partitions
\left\{\lambda\vdash d\right\}
. We denote the irreducible character corresponding to
\lambda\vdash d
by
\chi^{\lambda}
. The conjugacy classes of
S_{d}
are also indexed by partitions
\mu\vdash d
. This is due to the fact that
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in S_{d}
are conjugate to each other if and only if they have the same cycle type
\mu
. Let
\pi_{\lambda}
be any permutation of cycle type
\lambda
. The order of the
centralizer
of
\pi_{\lambda}
, that is, the order of the subgroup of elements in
S_{d}
that commute with
\pi_{\lambda}
only depends on
\lambda
, and is computed by
z_{\lambda}=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}m_{i}!\right)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}\lambda_{i}\right)
, where
m_{i}
is the number of times the number
i
appears in the partition
\lambda
. Since the irreducible character
\chi^{\lambda}
is constant on conjugacy classes of
S_{d}
, we can unambiguously define the integer
\chi^{\lambda}_{\mu}
as the value of
\chi^{\lambda}
evaluated at any element in the conjugacy class corresponding to
\mu
. There are two standard ways to compute the numbers
\chi^{\lambda}_{\mu}
, either via
Frobenius’ formula
(
[
FH13
]
, Frobenius Formula 4.10), or the
Murnaghan-Nakayama rule
(
[
FH13
]
Problem 4.45).

Next, we need the notion of a
shifted symmetric power sum
. Shifted symmetric power sums are examples of
shifted symmetric functions
. The ring
\Lambda^{*}
of shifted symmetric functions is a deformation of the standard ring of symmetric functions
\Lambda
. They were introduced and studied by Okounkov and Olshanksi
[
OO97
]
, and form a crucial ingredient in the celebrated Bloch-Okounkov Theorem
[
BO00
]
. For the purposes of this paper, we only need the following definition.

Definition 4
.

Let
k\geq 0
be an integer, and let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m})\vdash d
be a partition. The
shifted symmetric power sum of order
k
evaluated at
\lambda
is

p_{k}(\lambda):=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[\left(\lambda_{i}-i+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{k}-\left(-i+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{k}\right]-(1-2^{-k})\frac{B_{k}}{2k}

We now have all of the ingredients to compute the intersection number
\left<\tau_{k_{1}}\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}\right>^{\bullet E}_{d}
.

Theorem 5

(Gromov-Witten/Hurwitz Correspondence,
[
OP06
]
)
.

The intersection numbers
\left<\tau_{k_{1}}\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}\right>^{\bullet E}_{d}
have the following closed form expression:

\left<\tau_{k_{1}}\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}\right>^{\bullet E}_{d}=\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(k_{i}+1)!}\sum_{\lambda,\mu\vdash d}\frac{1}{z_{\lambda}}\left(.\chi^{\lambda}_{\mu}\right)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{k_{i}+1}(\lambda)

A formal power series that will come up often in our calculations is the so-called
Euler function
, defined by

(q)_{\infty}:=\prod_{n\geq 1}(1-q^{n})=\sum_{d=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{d}q^{\frac{3d^{2}-d}{2}}

(3)

The second equality in Equation
3
is often referred to as the
Pentagonal Number Theorem
. It will help us write down closed-form expressions for Ramanujan tau values in terms of Gromov-Witten invariants. The individual intersection numbers
\left<\tau_{k_{1}}\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}\right>^{\bullet E}_{d}
are not our main focus, but rather, we want to stitch them together into the following generating functions:

Definition 5
.

Let
k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}
be nonnegative integers. We call

\left<\tau_{k_{1}}\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}\right>:=(q)_{\infty}\sum_{d\geq 0}\left<\tau_{k_{1}}\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}\right>^{\bullet E}_{d}q^{d}

a
stationary descendent of weight
k=\sum(k_{i}+2)

.

[
[
OP06
]
]
Stationary descendents of weight
k
are quasimodular forms of weight

, that is, if
\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}=k
,
\left<\tau_{k_{1}}\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}\right>\in\text{QM}_{k}

Example 1
.

It’s a straightforward calculation to show that

\left<\tau_{0}\right>=E_{2}

Here is a less trivial example in weight

\left<\tau_{2}^{2}\right>=\frac{1}{12}E_{6}E_{2}+\frac{73}{112}E_{4}^{2}-\frac{3}{4}E_{4}E_{2}^{2}-\frac{15}{4}E_{2}^{3}

Theorem

allows us to go from GW invariants to quasimodular forms. But it turns out that we can also go the other way around.

Proposition 1
.

Let
(\left<\tau_{\text{wt.}=2}\right>,\left<\tau_{\text{wt.}=4}\right>,\left<\tau_{\text{wt.}=6}\right>)
be a triple of stationary descendents of weight
2,4
, and
6
, respectively. Then

\text{QM}=\mathbb{Q}\left[\left<\tau_{\text{wt.}=2}\right>,\left<\tau_{\text{wt.}=4}\right>,\left<\tau_{\text{wt.}=6}\right>\right]

To motivate the introduction of matroids, let’s first examine the simple cases of weights

Let us now introduce matroids properly. As was said in the Introduction, matroids are combinatorial objects that abstract the notion of linear independence/dependence.

Definition 7

(
[
Oxl11
]
, Section 1.1)
.

A
matroid

\mathcal{M}
is an ordered pair
(E,\mathcal{I})
, where
E
is a finite set, and
\mathcal{I}
is a collection of subsets of
E
satisfying the following properties:

1.

\varnothing\in\mathcal{I}

2.

I\in\mathcal{I},I^{\prime}\subseteq I\implies I^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}

3.

I_{1},I_{2}\in\mathcal{I},|I_{1}|<|I_{2}|\implies\text{there exists}\ e\in I_{2}\setminus I_{1}\ \text{such that}\ I_{1}\cup e\in\mathcal{I}

The set
E
is called the
ground set
of
\mathcal{M}
, and the elements of
\mathcal{I}
are called the
independent sets
of
\mathcal{M}
.

A standard way to construct a matroid is to start with an
(m\times n)
-matrix
A
with entries from a field
\bf{k}

\bf{k}^{m}
. The matroid constructed this way is denoted
M[A]
, and is called the
vector matroid
associated to
A
. The matroid
M[A]
is completely determined by its maximal independent sets i.e. the
bases
of
M[A]
. The
rank
of a matroid is the length of any of its maximal independent sets. When describing a matroid by its bases, we use the ordered pair
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{B})
, where
\mathcal{B}
is the set of bases of
\mathcal{M}
. The theory of matroids, along with what we have established in weights
k=2,4,6
, motivates the following definition.

Corollary 3

Let
\tau(d)
be the Ramanujan tau function evaluated at the integer

. For any basis
B\in\mathcal{B}^{12}|S^{12}

\tau(d)=\sum_{b\in B\atop 3d_{1}^{2}-d_{1}+2d_{2}=2d}(-1)^{d_{1}}a_{b}\left<\tau_{b}\right>^{\bullet E}_{d_{2}}

(7)

Proof.

Let

such that

\Delta=\sum_{b\in B}a_{b}b=(q)_{\infty}\sum_{b\in B}a_{b}\left(\sum_{d\geq 0}\left<\tau_{b}\right>^{\bullet E}_{d}q^{d}\right)

The Pentagonal Number Theorem says that

(q)_{\infty}=\sum_{d=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{d}q^{\frac{3d^{2}-d}{2}}

The desired result follows from matching up the degree-

\tau(m)\tau(n)=\tau(mn)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{b\in B\atop 3d_{1}^{2}-d_{1}+2d_{2}=2m}(-1)^{d_{1}}a_{b}\left<\tau_{b}\right>^{\bullet E}_{d_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{b\in B\atop 3d_{1}^{2}-d_{1}+2d_{2}=2n}(-1)^{d_{1}}a_{b}\left<\tau_{b}\right>^{\bullet E}_{d_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in B\atop 3d_{1}^{2}-d_{1}+2d_{2}=2mn}(-1)^{d_{1}}a_{b}\left<\tau_{b}\right>^{\bullet E}_{d_{2}}

which, as far as we know, is a new relation of Gromov-Witten invariants for the elliptic curve. Similar relations can be derived by choosing different properties of
\tau(d)
and seeing what it says on the Gromov-Witten side.

3.1
Outlook

It would be worthwhile to further develop the theory of descendent matroids, and to see which modular forms have nice representations in terms of stationary descendents. As was mentioned after Definition
8
, it’s not clear whether
\mathcal{B}^{k}
is always nonempty, but we conjecture that this is indeed the case.

Conjecture 1
.

The rank of the descendent matroid
\mathcal{M}_{k}
is always equal to
\text{dim}(\text{QM}_{k})
. Equivalently,

\mathbb{Q}\left[\left\{\left<\tau_{k_{1}}\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}\right>\right\}_{\sum(k_{i}+2)=k}\right]=\text{QM}_{k}

To check that Conjecture
1
is at least plausible, we should make sure the ground set
E^{k}
is at least as large as
\text{dim}(\text{QM}_{k})
. By the following lemma, this is indeed true:

Lemma 1
.

The number of elements in the ground set
E^{k}
is

\left|E^{k}\right|=\#\{\text{partitions of $k$ with no part equal to $1$}\}

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{k}^{>1}
be the set of partitions of
k
with no part equal to 1. The map
\varphi:\mathcal{P}_{k}^{>1}\rightarrow E^{k}
given by
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\mapsto\left<\tau_{\lambda_{1}-2}\ldots\tau_{\lambda_{n}-2}\right>
is a bijection.
∎

In particular,
\left|E^{k}\right|\gg\text{dim}(\text{QM}_{k})=\#\{\text{partitions of $\frac{k}{2}$ with no parts greater than 3}\}
for large
k
.

The calculations we have made in this paper suggest that the ground set
E^{k}
contains many redundant generators of
\text{QM}_{k}
, but that a good portion of this redundancy can be killed by restricting to
S^{k}
. However, in order to find minors that are nontrivial uniform matroids, further restriction seems to be the key. For example,

\displaystyle=U_{8,10}

4.1
The Stationary_Descendent( ) Class

When instantiating Stationary_Descendent( ), one specifies the descendent insertions via a list
[k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{n}]
. Several methods are available for this object. The .evaluate() method returns the Gromov-Witten invariant
\left<\tau_{k_{1}}\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}\right>^{\bullet E}_{d}
. For example, suppose we wanted to compute
\left<\tau_{2}^{2}\right>^{\bullet E}_{3}
. We would run the following commands

sage: desc = Stationary_Descendent([2, 2])
sage: desc.evaluate(d=3)
sage: 166577809/11059200

which shows that
\left<\tau_{2}^{2}\right>^{\bullet E}_{3}=\frac{166577809}{11059200}
. The method .expand() returns the expansion of the stationary descendent
\left<\tau_{k_{1}}\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}\right>
up to a prescribed order. For example, we have

sage: desc.expand(order = 3)
sage: 248437/17280*q^3 + 15703/23040*q^2 + 127/69120*q + 49/33177600

which means that

\left<\tau_{2}^{2}\right>:=(q)_{\infty}\sum_{d\geq 0}\left<\tau_{2}^{2}\right>^{\bullet E}_{d}=\frac{49}{33177600}+\frac{127}{69120}q+\frac{15703}{23040}q^{2}+\frac{248437}{17280}q^{3}+O(q^{4})

The .to_eisenstein_basis( ) method returns the expansion of
\left<\tau_{k_{1}}\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}\right>
in the Eisenstein basis. Specifically, the output is a dictionary whose keys are tuples corresponding to Eisenstein monomials, and whose values are the corresponding coefficients. For example,

sage: desc.to_eisenstein_basis()
sage: {(6, 2): 1/12, (4, 4): 73/112, (4, 2, 2): -3/4, (2, 2, 2, 2): -15/4}

means that

\left<\tau_{2}^{2}\right>=\frac{1}{12}E_{6}E_{2}+\frac{73}{112}E_{4}^{2}-\frac{3}{4}E_{4}E_{2}^{2}-\frac{15}{4}E_{2}^{4}
