David B. Leep, Rachel L. Petrik

Abstract
. We study lower bound estimates for the number of solutions of systems of equations over finite fields. Heath-Brown improved the lower bounds given by the classical Chevalley-Warning Theorems by excluding systems of equations whose solutions form an affine space. We improve each of Heath-Brown’s results and demonstrate sharpness in several cases.

11E76, 11T06

1.
Introduction

The goal of this paper is to study lower bounds on the number of solutions to systems of polynomial equations over finite fields. Given a general system of polynomials, a classical question has been to determine the existence of a solution. For systems over finite fields, the key results on the existence of solutions are the
Chevalley–Warning theorems

[
2
,
4
]
(see
Theorem

1.1
below). In particular, Warning provided a lower bound in
[
4
]
for the number of solutions. Easy examples exist to show that Warning’s result is best possible. Avoiding these easy cases, Heath-Brown
[
3
]
(see
Theorem

1.3
) improved the lower bound given in
[
4
]
. In this paper, we improve each of Heath-Brown’s lower bounds (see
Theorem

1.4
) by separating out the various cases more clearly and computing the estimates more precisely. In addition, we simplify the proof of
Theorem

1.3
(2) for
q\geq 4
and extend it to
q=3
. In
Section

4
, we provide examples showing many of our bounds are optimal.

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
denote the finite field of order
q=p^{k}
, where
p
is a prime and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. We write
\textit{{f}}=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{r}\}
to denote a system of polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
. Let
d_{i}=\deg(f_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq r
. We define the degree of
f
to be
d\coloneqq d_{1}+\dots+d_{r}
. Let
Z(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
be the set of zeros of
f
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and let
N(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})=|Z(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})|
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{r}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
,
\deg(f_{i})=d_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq r
. Assume
n>d_{1}+d_{2}+\cdots+d_{r}
. Then the following statements hold.

(1)

(Chevalley,
[
2
]
)
If
Z(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
is nonempty, then
N(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})\geq 2
.

(2)

(Warning,
[
4
]
)

N(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})\equiv 0\bmod{p}
.

(3)

(Warning,
[
4
]
)
If
Z(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
is nonempty, then
N(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})\geq q^{n-d}
.

(4)

(Ax,
[
1
]
)

N(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})\equiv 0\bmod{q}
.

A polynomial
f
in
n
variables is defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
if
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
. A polynomial
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is a
(homogeneous) form of degree

d
, for some
d\geq 0
, if
f\neq 0
and each monomial in
f
has degree
d
. We say that
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is a zero of
f
if
f_{i}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=0
,
1\leq i\leq r
. We say
f
is a
homogeneous system
if each
f_{i}
is a homogeneous form. If
f
is a homogeneous system with
\deg(f_{i})\geq 1
for each
i
, then
f_{i}(0,\dots,0)=0
for each
i
. A zero
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
of a homogeneous system
f
is a
nontrivial zero
if
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\neq(0,\dots,0)
.

If
f_{1},\dots,f_{r}
are homogeneous forms and
n>d
, then the system has a nontrivial zero by Theorem
1.1
(1) and, therefore, there are at least
q^{n-d}\geq 2
zeros by Theorem
1.1
(3). If
P
is the number of projective zeros defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, then
N(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})=P(q-1)+1
. This implies

(1)

N(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})\equiv 1\bmod q-1.

Further improvements require additional hypotheses, as examples exist (e.g. norm forms) that show the bound in Theorem
1.1
(3) is best possible. In these examples the set of zeros forms a subspace of dimension
n-d
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. In fact, Heath-Brown showed (see Theorem
1.3
below) that all examples where Theorem
1.1
(3) is sharp have the property that the set of zeros forms an affine space of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.

Definition 1.2
.

An affine space is a coset of a subspace. We say that two affine spaces are parallel if they are cosets of the same subspace. The dimension of an affine space is the dimension of the corresponding subspace. We let
\mathbb{A}^{t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
denote any affine space of dimension
t
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Since we work entirely in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, it follows that
\mathbb{A}^{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. When
Z(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
is not an affine space, Heath-Brown (
[
3
]
) provided the following improvement to Theorem
1.1
. If
d=1
, then
Z(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
is always an affine space, which is excluded. Thus, without loss of generality, we can always assume
d\geq 2
.

Theorem 1.3

(Heath–Brown,
[
3
]
, Theorem 2)
.

Suppose that
n>d
,
Z(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
is non-empty, and
Z(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
is not an affine space of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. Then the following statements hold.

(1)

For any
q
, we have
N(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})>q^{n-d}
.

(2)

If
q\geq 4
, we have
N(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})\geq 2q^{n-d}
.

(3)

For any
q
, we have
N(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})\geq\dfrac{q^{n+1-d}}{(n+2-d)}
provided that the polynomials
f_{1},\dots,f_{r}
are homogeneous.

The main result of this paper, Theorem
1.4
, is to show that each part of Theorem
1.3
can be improved as follows.

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose that
n>d
,
Z(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
is non-empty, and
Z(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
is not an affine space of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. Then the following statements hold.

(1)

For
q=2
,
N(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n})\geq 2^{n-d}+2
.

(2)

For
q\geq 3
,
N(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})\geq 2q^{n-d}
.

(3)

For
q\geq 2
,
N(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})>\dfrac{q^{n+1-d}-1}{q-1}\cdot\dfrac{q}{n+2-d}
provided that the polynomials
f_{1},\dots,f_{r}
are homogeneous.

(4)

For
q\geq 3
,
N(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})\geq 2q^{n-d}+(q-2)q
provided that the polynomials
f_{1},\dots,f_{r}
are homogeneous.

Moreover, the bounds in (1), (2), and (4) are sharp under these hypotheses.

In Section
4
, we state more precisely our results on the sharpness of these bounds. When
n>d
, the bound given in Theorem
1.4
(3) is better than the result given in Theorem
1.3
(3) because

\dfrac{q^{n+1-d}-1}{q-1}\cdot\dfrac{q}{n+2-d}>q^{n-d}\cdot\dfrac{q}{n+2-d}=\dfrac{q^{n+1-d}}{n+2-d}.

Assumed Results from Heath-Brown’s Paper

We need the following results, which are proven in
[

Theorem 2.1

(Heath–Brown,
[
3
]
, Theorem 1)
.

For any two parallel affine spaces
L_{1},L_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
of dimension at least d, we have

N(\textit{{f}},L_{1})\equiv N(\textit{{f}},L_{2})\bmod{q}.

Lemma 2.2

(Heath–Brown,
[
3
]
, Lemma 1)
.

Let
L_{0}\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
be an affine space. Choose an affine space
L
of maximal dimension
k
such that
L\supseteq L_{0}
and
N(\textit{{f}},L)=N(\textit{{f}},L_{0})
. Suppose
L^{\prime}\supset L
is an affine space of dimension
k+1
such that
N(\textit{{f}},L^{\prime})
is minimal.
Then
N(\textit{{f}},L^{\prime})>N(\textit{{f}},L)
and

N(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{A}^{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))\geq N(\textit{{f}},L)+\dfrac{q^{n-k}-1}{q-1}(N(\textit{{f}},L^{\prime})-N(\textit{{f}},L)).

For the next lemma, we require the following definition.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field. A set of points in
\mathbb{K}^{t}
is in general position if no
n
of them lie in an
(n-2)
-dimensional affine space for
2\leq n\leq t+1
.

Lemma 2.4

(Heath–Brown,
[
3
]
, Lemma 2)
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
be a set containing
t+1
points in general position. Then the following statements hold.

(1)

If
q=2
, and if there is no 2-dimensional affine space
L\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
meeting
S
in exactly 3 points, then
S=\mathbb{A}^{t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

(2)

If
q\geq 3
, and if
l\subseteq S
for every line
l
meeting
S
in at least two points, then
S=\mathbb{A}^{t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

(3)

If
q\geq 4
, and if
|S\cap l|

\geq q-1
for every line
l
meeting
S
in at least two points, then
\mathbb{A}^{t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\setminus S
is contained in a hyperplane.

(4)

If
m\geq 2
is an integer, and if
|S\cap l|

\geq m+1
for every line
l
meeting
S
in at least two points, then
|S|

\geq\dfrac{m^{t+1}-1}{m-1}
.

Our proof of Theorem
1.4
does not depend on Lemma
2.4
(3), the most difficult part of Lemma
2.4
, whereas the proof provided by Heath-Brown requires this statement.

Heath-Brown did not explicitly state the following lemmas, however, the proofs of these lemmas appear within the proof of his main result in
[
3
]
. We include the proofs for the convenience of the reader.

Lemma 2.5

(Heath–Brown,
[
3
]
, page 431)
.

Let
L
be an affine space of dimension
d
. If

and
N(\textit{{f}},L)=v
where

,
then
N(\textit{{f}},L^{*})\geq v

.
As a result,

.

By

,
N(\textit{{f}},L^{*})\equiv N(\textit{{f}},L)=v\bmod q
.
Since

N(\textit{{f}},L^{*})\geq v

Since there are

.
∎

Lemma 2.6

(Heath–Brown,
[
3
]
, page 432)
.

Let
L\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
be an affine space. Suppose
2\leq N(\textit{{f}},L)\leq q-1
and
\deg(\textit{{f}})=d
.

(1)

Then
\dim(L)\leq d
.

(2)

If
f
is a homogeneous system, then
\dim(L)\leq d-1
.

Proof.

(1)
If
\dim(L)>d
, then

by Theorem
1.1
(4) and Theorem

, which is a contradiction.

(2)
By (1), assume

. First suppose
\boldsymbol{0}\in L
and thus,
L
is a subspace. Since
f
is a homogeneous system, we have
q-1\mid N(\textit{{f}},L)-1
by Equation (
1
).
Thus, either
N(\textit{{f}},L)=1
or
N(\textit{{f}},L)\geq q
, which is a contradiction.

Now suppose
\boldsymbol{0}\notin L
. Let
L^{\prime}
be the

-dimensional subspace containing

. Since

is a homogeneous system, distinct zeros of

correspond bijectively to distinct projective zeros of

. Therefore,

by Equation (

(4) implies that

.
Thus,

, which is a contradiction.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

The following inequality is stated without proof in a slightly weaker form (for
q\geq 2v+3
) in
[
3
]
. Recall that
\lfloor x\rfloor
denotes the greatest integer less than or equal to
x
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose
v\in\mathbb{Z}
. Let
v\geq 2
and
q\geq 2v+1
, then
\Big\lfloor\dfrac{qv}{v+1}\Big\rfloor^{v}>\dfrac{q^{v}}{v+1}
with the exception of
v=2
,
q=5,7
.

Proof.

First we demonstrate that it is sufficient to show

v+1>\Big(1+\dfrac{1}{q-1}\Big)^{v}\Big(1+\dfrac{1}{v}\Big)^{v}.

Suppose

v+1>\Big(1+\dfrac{1}{q-1}\Big)^{v}\Big(1+\dfrac{1}{v}\Big)^{v}=\Big(\dfrac{q}{q-1}\Big)^{v}\Big(\dfrac{v+1}{v}\Big)^{v}.

Taking the
v^{\text{th}}
root of both sides yields

(v+1)^{\frac{1}{v}}>\Big(\dfrac{q}{q-1}\Big)\Big(\dfrac{v+1}{v}\Big).

Rearranging these terms through multiplication, we find

\dfrac{q}{(v+1)^{\frac{1}{v}}}<\dfrac{(q-1)v}{v+1}=\dfrac{qv}{v+1}-\dfrac{v}{v+1}\leq\Big\lfloor\dfrac{qv}{v+1}\Big\rfloor,

which gives the result of the lemma after raising both sides to the
v^{\text{th}}
power.

Assume
q\geq 2v+1
. Note that since
e^{y}>1+y
for
y>0
, it follows that

(2)

e^{x}>\Big(1+\frac{x}{v}\Big)^{v},

for all
x>0
,
v>0
. Then

1+\dfrac{1}{q-1}\leq 1+\dfrac{1}{2v}=1+\dfrac{\frac{1}{2}}{v}

\Big(1+\dfrac{1}{q-1}\Big)^{v}\leq\Big(1+\dfrac{\frac{1}{2}}{v}\Big)^{v}<e^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Applying (
2
)

\displaystyle\Big(1+\dfrac{1}{q-1}\Big)^{v}\Big(1+\dfrac{1}{v}\Big)^{v}<e^{\frac{1}{2}}e=e^{\frac{3}{2}}<5\leq v+1\text{ for }v\geq 4.

Now let
v=3
. Then

\Big(1+\dfrac{1}{q-1}\Big)^{v}\Big(1+\dfrac{1}{v}\Big)^{v}<e^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\dfrac{4}{3}\Big)^{3}=e^{\frac{1}{2}}\dfrac{64}{27}<3.91<4=v+1.

Now let
v=2
. Notice that

\displaystyle\Big(\dfrac{q}{q-1}\Big)^{v}\Big(1+\dfrac{1}{v}\Big)^{v}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{9}{4}\Big(\dfrac{q}{q-1}\Big)^{2}.

We want the values of
q
satisfying the inequality

\dfrac{9}{4}\Big(\dfrac{q}{q-1}\Big)^{2}<3.
This inequality is satisfied if and only if
\Big(\dfrac{q}{q-1}\Big)^{2}<\dfrac{4}{3}
, which is true if and only if
q\geq 8
.

∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume
n>d
and
Z(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
is nonempty. If
Z(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
is not an affine space of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, then
Z(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
contains at least
n+2-d
points in general position.

Proof.

By Theorem
1.1
(3),
N(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})\geq q^{n-d}
. Suppose that a maximal set of points of
Z(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
in general position has at most
n+1-d
points. Then this maximal set of points lies in some affine space
\mathbb{A}^{n-d}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. Then
Z(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{n-d}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, for otherwise we would have a larger set of points in general position. Since
N(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})\geq q^{n-d}
and
|\mathbb{A}^{n-d}(\mathbb{F}_{q})|=q^{n-d}
, it follows that
N(\textit{{f}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})=q^{n-d}
and
Z(\textit{{f}};\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
forms an affine space, which is a contradiction.
∎

4.
Optimality of Theorem
1.4

Sharpness of Theorem
1.4
(1)
We will show that Theorem
1.4
(1) is optimal for every
d\geq 2
and
n\in\{d+1,d+2\}
.

For each
d\in\mathbb{Z}
,
d\geq 1
, let
g_{d}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\dots,x_{d}]
be a homogeneous form of degree
d
having no nontrivial zero over
\mathbb{F}_{2}.
For example,
g_{d}
could be chosen to be a norm form.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{Z}
,
d\geq 2
, and let
n=d+1
. Then there exists a homogeneous form
f_{d}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
of degree
d
such that
|Z(f_{d},\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n})|=4=2^{n-d}+2
and
Z(f_{d},\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n})
is not an affine space in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.

Proof.

For
d=2,
let
f_{2}=x_{1}x_{2}-x_{3}^{2}
. Then
|Z(f_{2},\mathbb{F}_{2}^{3})|=4
because if
x_{1}=0
, then the zeros over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
are
(0,0,0)
and
(0,1,0)
, while if
x_{1}=1
, then the zeros over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
are
(1,0,0)
and
(1,1,1)
. These four zeros do not form a subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{3}
and thus do not form an affine space in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{3}
.

Let
d\geq 3
and suppose by induction that
f_{d-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\dots,x_{d}]
is a homogeneous form of degree
d-1
such that
|Z(f_{d-1},\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d})|=4
and
Z(f_{d-1},\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d})
is not an affine space in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}
. Let

f_{d}=x_{d+1}^{d}-f_{d-1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})x_{d+1}+g_{d}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}).

Then
f_{d}
is a homogeneous form of degree
d
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\dots,x_{d+1}]
. Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
. First suppose
f_{d-1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\neq 0
. Then
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\neq 0
and so
g_{d}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\neq 0
. Then for each
a_{d+1}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
,

f_{d}(a_{1}\dots,a_{d+1})=a_{d+1}^{d}-a_{d+1}+g_{d}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})=g_{d}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\neq 0.

Now suppose
f_{d-1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})=0.
Then there exists a unique
a_{d+1}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
such that
a_{d+1}^{d}+g_{d}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})=0.
Thus,
|Z(f_{d},\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n})|=|Z(f_{d},\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+1})|=|Z(f_{d-1},\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d})|=4.

Let
\pi:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}
be the projection map defined by
\pi(a_{1},\dots,a_{d+1})=(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})
. Then
\pi(Z(f_{d},\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+1}))=Z(f_{d-1},\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}).
Since
Z(f_{d-1},\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d})
is not an affine space in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}
, it follows that
Z(f_{d},\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+1})
is not an affine space in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+1}=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}.

∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{Z}
,
d\geq 2
, and let
n=d+2
. Then there exists a homogeneous form
f_{d}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
of degree
d
such that
|Z(f_{d},\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n})|=6=2^{n-d}+2
and
Z(f_{d},\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n})
is not an affine space in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.

Proof.

Any subset of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with cardinality
6
is not an affine space in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
because
6
is not a
2
-power.

For
d=2,
let
f_{2}=x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}x_{4}.
Then
|Z(f_{2},\mathbb{F}_{2}^{4})|=6
because there are
4
zeros in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{4}
if
x_{3}=1
and there are
2
zeros in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{4}
if
x_{3}=0
.

Let
d\geq 3
and suppose by induction that
f_{d-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\dots,x_{d+1}]
is a homogeneous form of degree
d-1
with
|Z(f_{d-1},\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+1})|=6.
By an invertible linear change of variables, we can assume that
f_{d-1}(0,0,\dots,0,1)=0
.

Let
f_{d}=x_{d+2}^{d}-f_{d-1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d+1})x_{d+2}+g_{d}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}).
Then
f_{d}
is a homogeneous form of degree
d
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\dots,x_{d+2}].
Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{d+1}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
. First suppose
f_{d-1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d+1})\neq 0
. If
a_{1}=\dots=a_{d}=0,
then
f_{d-1}(0,0,\dots,0,a_{d+1})=0
for
a_{d+1}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
because
f_{d-1}(0,\dots,0,1)=0
by assumption. Thus
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\neq 0
and so
g_{d}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\neq 0
. Then

f_{d}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d+1},a_{d+2})=a_{d+2}^{d}-a_{d+2}+g_{d}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})=g_{d}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\neq 0.

Now suppose
f_{d-1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d+1})=0
. Then there exists a unique
a_{d+2}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
with
a_{d+2}^{d}+g_{d}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})=0
. Thus,
|Z(f_{d},\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n})|=|Z(f_{d},\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+2})|=|Z(f_{d-1},\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+1})|=6
.

∎

Sharpness of Theorem
1.4
(2)
Consider the polynomial

g(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=x_{1}x_{2}+x_{3}^{2}+1

over
\mathbb{F}_{3}
. Since
|Z(g,\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3})|=6
and

is not a power of 3,
Z(g,\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3})
does not form an affine space in

. The lower bound given in Theorem
1.4
(2) is

.

Sharpness of Theorem
1.4
(4)

An example with
d=2
,
n=3
is given by the homogeneous form

h(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=x_{1}x_{2}-x_{3}^{2}

\mathbb{F}_{q}

.

(q-1)q+q=q^{2}

2q^{n-d}+(q-2)q=2q+(q-2)q=q^{2}
.
If

is a subspace since

\boldsymbol{0}\in Z(h,\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3})
.
This is not the case because
(1,0,0),(0,1,0)\in Z(h,\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3})
, but, their sum,

.

A second example with
d=3
,

.

,

(4).

.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
q\geq 3
,
n-d\geq 2
, and
v=n+1-d
.

(1)

If
q\geq 3v+2
, then the hypothesis in Proposition
5.1
(1) holds.

(2)

If
q\leq 2v
, then the hypothesis in Proposition
5.1
(2) holds.

Proof.

(1) Since
n-d\geq 2
and
q\geq 3
, we have
0<\frac{q^{2}-2q}{q^{n-d}}<1
,
and so

2<\Big(2+\dfrac{q^{2}-2q}{q^{n-d}}\Big)<3.

This gives

3v+2>\Big(2+\dfrac{q^{2}-2q}{q^{n-d}}\Big)v+1+\dfrac{q^{2}-2q}{q^{n-d}}-\Big(\dfrac{1}{q}+\dfrac{1}{q^{2}}+\dots+\dfrac{1}{q^{n-d-1}}\Big).

(2)
We have
\Big(\dfrac{1}{q}+\dfrac{1}{q^{2}}+\dots+\dfrac{1}{q^{n-d-1}}\Big)<\frac{1}{q-1}\leq 1
. Thus

1-\Big(\dfrac{1}{q}+\dfrac{1}{q^{2}}+\dots+\dfrac{1}{q^{n-d-1}}\Big)>0.

This gives

2v<\Big(2+\dfrac{q^{2}-2q}{q^{n-d}}\Big)v+1+\dfrac{q^{2}-2q}{q^{n-d}}-\Big(\dfrac{1}{q}+\dfrac{1}{q^{2}}+\dots+\dfrac{1}{q^{n-d-1}}\Big).

∎
