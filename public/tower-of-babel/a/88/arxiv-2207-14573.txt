Growth-induced instabilities for transversely isotropic hyperelastic materials Publication type: Original research article
Cem Altun, Ercan Gürses, Hüsnü Dal*

Abstract
This work focuses on planar growth-induced instabilities in three-dimensional bilayer structures, i.e., thick stiff film on a compliant substrate. Growth-induced instabilities are examined for a different range of fiber stiffness with a five-field Hu-Washizu type mixed variational formulation. The quasi-incompressible and quasi-inextensible limits of transversely isotropic materials were considered. A numerical example was solved by implementing the T2P0F0 element on an automated differential equation solver platform, FEniCS . It was shown that both the wavelength and critical growth parameter g g decrease by increasing the fiber stiffness for the first instability, which is obtained along the stiff fiber direction. The effect of the fiber stiffness is minor on the secondary buckling, which was observed perpendicular to the fiber direction. For a range of fiber stiffnesses, bifurcation points of instabilities were also determined by monitoring displacements and energies. The energy contributions of layers with different ranges of fiber stiffnesses were examined. It is concluded that the energy release mechanism at the initiation of the primary buckling is mainly due to isotropic and anisotropic contributions of the stiff film layer. For high fiber stiffnesses, the effect of the anisotropic energy on the first buckling becomes more dominant over other types. However, in the secondary instability, the isotropic energy of the film layer becomes the dominant one. Numerical outcomes of this study will help to understand the fiber stiffness effect on the buckling and post-buckling behavior of bilayer systems.

Introduction

Growth-induced deformations are common phenomena that confront living systems and engineering applications. In a certain level of growth, called critical growth, structural instabilities may rise as an indicator of pathological conditions of biological tissues, and it plays a key role in the development of materials for biomedical applications. These materials are mostly in the form of multi-layer structures, which may have different mechanical properties and fiber reinforcement layouts. The main mechanism of the buckling in a multi-layered structure is the compression type of loading that causes different levels of stresses in the thin stiff film and the compliant substrate; thus, buckling leads to a release of the energy. Fiber stiffness is a critical parameter that designates the critical growth in primary buckling as a wrinkle form. It is significant to understand the mechanism behind the growth-induced instabilities for transversely isotropic materials and to observe the effect of fiber stiffness over primary and secondary instabilities on bilayer systems. Growth-induced deformations may lead to instabilities as they turn into different patterns on advanced engineering materials and soft biological tissues. Mechanism of growth is also used to achieve to desired geometrical form change in biomedical applications. 2
Governing equations of motion

This section presents the kinematics and governing field equations of growth phenomena in the transversely isotropic hyperelastic solids. The kinematics based on the multiplicative decomposition of the deformation gradient into elastic and growth tensors in the sense of Rodriguez
[
3
]
are introduced. A simple neo-Hookean hyperelastic response in the intermediate state is considered. This study presents a quasi-incompressible and quasi-inextensible transversely isotropic model with transversely isotropic or planar growth. Finally, a five-field mixed variational formulation in the sense of Dal
[
62
]
and respective Euler-Lagrange equations are demonstrated. The governing equations are presented both in Lagrangian and Eulerian configurations. It is concluded that pursuing with the Eulerian setting leads to a more straightforward and compact form in terms of stress and moduli expressions.

2.2
Governing equations for finite growth

The second law of thermodynamics states a positive dissipation by stress power and the objective rate of free energy. The dissipation inequality can be applied to form thermodynamically consistent stress relations as

{\Symb D}={\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}:\frac{1}{2}\dot{}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}-\dot{\psi}=\hat{}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{P}}}:\dot{}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}-\dot{\psi}={\boldsymbol{\tau}}:\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\pounds}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{v}}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}-\dot{\psi}\geq 0

(9)

It is significant to notice that
\hat{}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{P}}}
implies the mixed-variant Piola tensor, which is represented by
\hat{}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{P}}}:={\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{P}}}

[
8

\boldsymbol{\pounds}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{v}}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}=({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{l}}}+{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{l}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}})

{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{l}}}=\dot{}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{-1}
is the spatial velocity gradient. By the evaluation of the dissipation inequality, stress derivations can be performed. In finite growth, hyperelasticity constitutive equations are assumed to be held on the elastic state, and growth represents an intermediate stress-free state. Depending on the microstructure of the material or the tissue, the form of the growth tensor
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g}
can be either isotropic or anisotropic. Stress arises in the solid domain due to the elastic part of the deformation gradient
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{e}
. The growth does not have any energy contribution to the free energy function, meaning that purely growth tensor does not cause any stress in the body. The growth tensor can directly either be dependent on a scalar strain-driven variable or the microstructure that leads the growth evolution. It can be defined by evolution equations based on an internal variable. This type of growth depends on the growth criteria as it seen in the flow rule in plasticity formulation, and growth initiates when the mechanical driving force exceeds the threshold level. For the sake of convenience, in this study, it is assumed that every continuum point exceeds the threshold level in the solid body; hence growth tensor
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g}
can be driven by certain scalar variables.

In general, fiber-reinforced rubber polymers, fibrous soft biological tissues, and reinforced composite elastomers exhibit nearly incompressible responses and, in the reinforcement direction, nearly inextensible behavior. It is common to observe that the growth of multi-layered materials (stiff bilayer film, hydrogels, etc.) or tissues (skin, artery, plant, etc.) can cause instabilities in the form of wrinkles, stripes, and secondary buckling shapes under residual stresses. The multi-layered materials can be composed of layers of different isotropic materials with different stiffnesses (e.g., stiff film on a compliant soft substrate). They can be formed by combinations of isotropic and fiber-reinforced anisotropic materials that we usually observe in nature, see Figure
4
. Depending on the physical conditions, these solids can be subjected to growth or shrinkage, resulting in compression or tension in the matrix and fibers, respectively. In line with the macro-level continuum approach, we introduce the elastic Helmholtz free energy function for one fiber family elastomers or soft tissues that is additively split into volumetric, isotropic, and anisotropic parts.

\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{e},{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}_{0}):=\psi_{vol}(J^{e})+\psi_{iso}({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{e})+\psi_{ani}({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{e},{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}_{0})

(10)

In general, stored free energy for hyperelastic solid is governed by three invariants of the right Cauchy Green tensor. These invariants can be used in combination with any kind of isotropic hyperelastic material model. In growth-induced hyperelasticity free energy formulation, elastic invariants are determined as

I_{1}^{e}:=\mathop{\operator@font tr}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e},\quad I_{2}^{e}:=\frac{1}{2}\left[I_{1}^{e2}-\mathop{\operator@font tr}({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e2})\right],\quad\textrm{and}\quad I_{3}^{e}:=\det{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e}=J^{e2}

(11)

In the present study, an incompressible Neo-Hookean model, which considers only the first invariant for the isotropic response, is used to keep the formulation simple. The elastic right Cauchy Green tensor
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e}={\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g-T}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g-1}
is defined in the intermediate configuration. In addition to the isotropic response, the anisotropic response that is generated by fibers requires additional invariants
I_{4}
and
I_{5}
. These additional invariants are introduced in terms of
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}_{0}
unit vector of fibers in the undeformed configuration

I_{4}^{e}:={\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}_{0}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}_{0}\,\qquad I_{5}^{e}={\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}_{0}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e2}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}_{0}\,,

(12)

that relates the energy storage due to fiber reinforcement in the material. The volumetric part of the free energy function in equation (
10
) is defined as

\psi_{vol}^{e}(J)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(J^{e}-1\right)^{2}

(13)

imposing the quasi-incompressibility, where
{\lambda}
is Lame constant of the material. The second term of the free energy function is the isotropic part and represented by the Neo Hookean model as

\psi_{iso}^{e}({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{e})=\frac{\mu_{0}}{2}(I_{1}^{e}-{2}\mbox{ln}\left(J^{e}\right)-3)\,,

(14)

where
\mu_{0}
is the shear modulus. The last term of the free energy function stands for the anisotropic part

\psi_{ani}^{e}({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{e},{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}_{0})=\mu_{f}(I_{4}^{e}-1)^{2}

(15)

which is considered as a function of the fourth invariant. The volumetric and anisotropic parts of the free energy function will enter the formulation as constraints that lead the quasi-incompressible and quasi-inextensible behavior. In this study, fibers are assumed to carry both tension and compression. In some applications, such as some soft biological tissues, it is assumed that fibers contribute only in tension but do not take any load in compression. For such applications, the anisotropic part of the free energy can be defined by Macaulay brackets
\langle\bullet\rangle
that represent the tension-only behavior. Then, the parenthesis term in equation (
15
) can be replaced by
\langle I_{4}^{e}-1\rangle^{2}
for tension-only actions. By evaluating the dissipation inequality (
9
), the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}
, the Piola stress
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{P}}}
and the Kirchhoff stress
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
, can be obtained thermodynamically conjugate to the right Cauchy Green deformation tensor
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}
, and deformation gradient
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}
, and the current metric
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}
, respectively
[
8
,
42
]
. Then Kirchhoff stress
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
can be determined by the push forward of the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}
as

\begin{array}[]{l@{}l}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}=2\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}}{\psi}=2\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e}}{\psi}:\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}}{{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e}}={\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g-1}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}^{e}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g-T}\,,\\[10.76385pt]
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{P}}}=\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}}{\psi}=\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{e}}{\psi}:\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}}{{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{e}}={\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{P}}}^{e}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g-T}\,,\\[10.76385pt]
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}=2\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}}{\psi}={\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{e}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}^{e}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{eT}\end{array}

(16)

Corresponding tangent moduli
{\Ibb C}
,
{\Ibb A}
, and
{\IIbb c}
can be derived by push forward and pull back operations from elastic moduli
{\Ibb C}^{e}
,
{\Ibb A}^{e}
, and
{{\IIbb c}^{e}}
, which can be determined by taking the second derivative with respect to conjugate tensors
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}
,
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}
, respectively
[
8
,
42
]
.

\begin{array}[]{l@{}l}{\Ibb C}=2\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}}{{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}}=2\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g-1}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}^{e}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g-T})=\left[{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g-1}\bar{\otimes}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g-1}\right]:{\Ibb C}^{e}:\left[{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g-T}\bar{\otimes}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g-T}\right]\,,\\[10.76385pt]
{\Ibb A}=\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}}{{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{P}}}}=\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{P}}}^{e}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g-T})=\left[{\boldsymbol{1}}\bar{\otimes}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g-T}\right]:{\Ibb A}^{e}:\left[{\boldsymbol{1}}\bar{\otimes}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g-T}\right]\,,\\[10.76385pt]
{\IIbb c}=2\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}}{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}=\left[{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}\bar{\otimes}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}\right]:{\Ibb C}:\left[{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{T}\bar{\otimes}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{T}\right]\end{array}

(17)

where
\bar{\otimes}
implies non-standard tensor product and can be defined as
\left[\bullet\bar{\otimes}\circ\right]_{ijkl}=\left[\bullet\right]_{ik}\left[\circ\right]_{jl}
. After defining Lagrangian, Eulerian, and two-point stress relations with elastic components in equation (
16
), it is required to derive corresponding elastic second Piola-Kirchhoff stress
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}^{e}
and the Kirchhoff stress
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
. It should be noted that, elastic Kirchhoff stress can be determined by the push forward of the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress. However, as the outcome of the Eulerian configuration is more compact, we prefer to give both deformed and undeformed configurations in this study.

2.3
Definition of the growth tensor

Depending on the nature of the material or the tissue, the growth tensor
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g}
can be isotropic, transversely isotropic, orthotropic or anisotropic
[
38
]
. The simplest approach is to express the growth tensor as isotropic; by this approach, the growth amount is equal in all directions. It can be a function of one scalar growth parameter
g
, and then the isotropic growth tensor is defined as

{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g}=\left[1+\textit{g}\right]{\boldsymbol{1}}

(18)

where
{\boldsymbol{1}}
is the identity tensor. Note that,
g
is the scalar growth parameter here and should not be confused by metric tensor
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}
. If the growth parameter g is zero, the growth tensor becomes equal to identity (
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g}={\boldsymbol{1}}
) which means there is no growth. Parameter
g
can be positive or negative, corresponding to growth or shrinkage in the solid, respectively.
In this study, we consider a special case of growth, namely transversely isotropic or planar growth. It exhibits uneven growth for different orthogonal axes. Here, we focus on growth in the membrane plane while there is no growth in the thickness direction. In this way, the growth tensor
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g}
is defined as

{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g}=\left[1+\textit{g}\right]{\boldsymbol{1}}-\textit{g}\left[{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{m}}}_{0}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{m}}}_{0}\right]

(19)

where
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{m}}}_{0}
is the unit normal of membrane in the reference configuration. Equation (
19
) describes an in plane growth within membrane and there is no growth along
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{m}}}_{0}
direction. For the transversely isotropic growth, the inverse of the growth tensor
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g-1}
can be defined as below
[
79
]

{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{g-1}=\left[\frac{1}{1+\textit{g}}\right]{\boldsymbol{1}}-\frac{1}{\textit{g}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{m}}}_{0}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{m}}}_{0}\right]

(20)

2.4
Stresses and moduli expressions in Lagrangian configuration

Similar to the free energy decomposition in (
10
), the elastic second Piola-Kirchhoff stress
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}^{e}
is defined as additively decoupled terms, namely volumetric, isochoric, and anisotropic such as

{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}^{e}=2\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e}}{\psi^{e}}={\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}^{e}_{vol}+{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}^{e}_{iso}+{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}^{e}_{ani}

(21)

In line with the (
21
), the elastic Lagrangian moduli
{\Ibb C}^{e}
, can be additively split into volumetric, isotropic, and anisotropic parts respectively.

{\Ibb C}^{e}=2\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e}}{{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}^{e}}=4\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e}}{\psi^{e}}={\Ibb C}^{e}_{vol}+{\Ibb C}^{e}_{iso}+{\Ibb C}^{e}_{ani}

(22)

The volumetric part of the elastic second Piola-Kirchhoff stress
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}^{e\textit{ }vol}
reads

{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}^{e}_{vol}:=2\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e}}\psi^{e}_{vol}(J^{e})=p^{e}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e-1}\quad\text{with}\quad p^{e}:=J^{e}\psi^{\prime e\textit{ }vol}(J^{e})=J^{e}{\lambda}(J^{e}-1)\,

(23)

The Lagrangian moduli expression for the volumetric part is determined as

\begin{array}[]{l@{}l}{\Ibb C}^{e}_{vol}:=2\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e}_{vol}}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}^{e}_{vol}(J^{e})=(p^{e}+\hat{\kappa}_{L}){\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e-1}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e-1}-{2}p^{e}{\Ibb I}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e-1}}\,,\\[10.76385pt]
\text{with}\quad\hat{\kappa}_{L}=J^{e\textit{ }2}\psi^{\prime\prime e}_{vol}(J^{e})={\lambda}J^{e\textit{ }2}\end{array}

(24)

where
{\Ibb I}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e-1}}^{ABCD}=\frac{1}{2}\left[{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}_{AC}^{e-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}_{BD}^{e-1}+{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}_{AD}^{e-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}_{BC}^{e-1}\right]
. The isotropic elastic second Piola-Kirchhoff stress is obtained as

\begin{array}[]{l@{}l}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}^{e}_{iso}:=2\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e}}\psi^{e}_{iso}({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e})=\mu_{0}({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{G}}}^{-1}-{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e-1})\end{array}

(25)

Then, the Lagrangian elastic moduli for the isotropic response can be derived as

\begin{array}[]{l@{}l}{\Ibb C}^{e}_{iso}:=2\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e}}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}^{e}_{iso}({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e})=2\mu_{0}{\Ibb I}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e-1}}\end{array}

(26)

The last term of the elastic second Piola-Kirchhoff stress is the anisotropic response due to fiber reinforcement that can be derived as

{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}^{e}_{ani}:=2\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e}}\psi^{e}_{ani}(I_{4})=4\mu_{f}\left(I_{4}^{e}-1\right){\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}_{0}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}_{0}

(27)

Finally, the corresponding anisotropic elastic Lagrangian moduli read

{\Ibb C}^{e}_{ani}:=2\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{C}}}^{e}}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}^{e}_{ani}(I_{4})=8\mu_{f}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}_{0}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}_{0}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}_{0}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}_{0}

(28)

2.5
Stresses and moduli expressions in Eulerian configuration

Similar to Lagrangian configuration, the Kirchhoff stress can be defined by additively decomposition of volumetric, isotropic, and anisotropic terms

{\boldsymbol{\tau}}:=2\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}}{\psi}={\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{vol}+{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{iso}+{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{ani}

(29)

and the corresponding Eulerian tangent moduli read

{\IIbb c}:=4\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}}^{2}\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{f}}}_{0})={\IIbb c}_{vol}+{\IIbb c}_{iso}+{\IIbb c}_{ani}

(30)

The Kirchoff stress for the volumetric response can be derived as

{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{vol}:=2\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}}\psi_{vol}(J)=p{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}^{-1}\quad\text{with}\quad p:=J\psi^{\prime}_{vol}(J)=J{\lambda}(J-1)\,.

(31)

The Eulerian tangent moduli expression for the volumetric part is defined as

{\IIbb c}_{vol}:=4\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}}^{2}U(J)=(p+\hat{\kappa}){\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}^{-1}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}^{-1}-2p{\Ibb I}\,,\quad\text{with}\quad\hat{\kappa}=J^{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}_{vol}(J)={\lambda}(J^{2}+1)\,.

(32)

The elastic Kirchhoff stress for the isotropic response reads

{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{iso}:=2\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}}{\psi_{iso}({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}})}=\mu_{0}({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{b}}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}^{-1})

(33)

Then the corresponding elastic Eulerian moduli for the isotropic response can be derived as

{\IIbb c}_{iso}:=4\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}}^{2}\psi_{iso}({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}})=2\mu_{0}{\Ibb I}

(34)

Anisotropic part of the Kirchhoff stress representing the fiber reinforcement is defined as

{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{ani}=2\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}}\psi_{ani}({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}_{0})=4\mu_{f}(I_{4}-1){\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}\,.

(35)

Moreover, the corresponding anisotropic part of Eulerian moduli reads

{\IIbb c}_{ani}=4\partial_{\mkern-1.0mu{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}}^{2}\psi_{ani}({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}_{0})=8\mu_{f}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}\,.

(36)

Finally, by using the relations of pull back and push forward operations defined in (
16
) and (
17
), one can end up with the overall stress and moduli in terms of the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{S}}}
, the Piola stress
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{P}}}
, the Kirchhoff stress
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
and the corresponding moduli
{\Ibb C}
,
{\Ibb A}
and
{{\IIbb c}}
, respectively.

3
Variational formulation for anisotropic and incompressible continuum

3.1
Variational formulation for finite elasticity

The potential functional can be defined as below for the
finite elasticity

\hat{\Pi}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},t):=\hat{\Pi}^{int}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},t)-\hat{\Pi}^{ext}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},t)\,,

(37)

where

\hat{\Pi}^{int}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},t):=\displaystyle\int\nolimits_{{\Symb B}}\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{e})\mkern 4.0mu plus 2.0mu minus 4.0mudV\quad\mathchoice{\hbox{{and}}}{\hbox{{and}}}{\hbox{{and}}}{\hbox{{and}}}\quad\hat{\Pi}^{ext}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}}):=\displaystyle\int\nolimits_{{\Symb B}}{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\cdot\rho_{0}\bar{}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mkern 4.0mu plus 2.0mu minus 4.0mudV+\displaystyle\int\nolimits_{\partial{\Symb B}_{t}}{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\cdot\bar{}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{T}}}\mkern 4.0mu plus 2.0mu minus 4.0mudA\,.

(38)

For elastic loading, the stored energy in the body can be defined by
\hat{\Pi}^{int}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}})
, and
\hat{\Pi}^{ext}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}})
refers to the work done by external forces. In equation (
37
),
\rho_{0}
,
\bar{}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, and
\bar{}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{T}}}
are the density, body force, and the surface traction, respectively.
\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{e})
is the volume-specific elastic Helmholtz free energy. The boundary value problem governing finite elasticity is obtained from the elastic potential by the
principle of minimum potential energy
in the variational form

{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{t}=\mathop{\operator@font Arg}\{\inf_{{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{t}\in{\Symb W}}\hat{\Pi}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},t)\}

(39)

subject to Dirichlet-type boundary condition

{\Symb W}=\{{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{t}~|~{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{t}\in{\Symb B}\quad\land\quad{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{t}=\bar{}{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\quad\textrm{on}\quad\partial{\Symb B}_{u}\}\,.

(40)

Due to the stationary condition of the elastic potential
\hat{\Pi}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},t)
, the variation of (
39
) along with localization theorem yields the
Euler–Lagrange equation

J^{e}\mathop{\operator@font div}[J^{e-1}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}]+\rho_{0}\bar{}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=0

(41)

yielding to the balance of linear momentum for static problems in the domain
{\Symb B}
along with Neumann-type boundary condition

{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{P}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{N}}}={\boldsymbol{\tau}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}=\bar{}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{T}}}\qquad\textrm{on}\qquad\partial{\Symb B}_{t}

(42)

where we have used the identity of
Nanson’s formula
as
J{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{-T}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{N}}}~dA={\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}~da
. The element formulation can be derived by the consistent linearization of the weak form obtained as the first variation of (
39
).

3.2
A mixed variational formulation for quasi-incompressible and quasi-inextensible continuum

The quasi-incompressible and quasi-inextensible behavior can be enforced by two additional penalty terms in the decomposed representation (
10
) of the free energy functional as

\hat{\Pi}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},p^{e},\theta,s^{e},\lambda):=\displaystyle\int\nolimits_{{\Symb B}}\pi^{\ast}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},p^{e},\theta,s^{e},\lambda)\mkern 4.0mu plus 2.0mu minus 4.0mudV-\hat{\Pi}^{ext}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},t)\,.

(43)

The mixed potential density in equation (
43
) is defined as

\pi_{int}^{\ast}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},p^{e},\theta,s^{e},\lambda)=\psi_{iso}({\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{F}}}^{e})+\underbrace{p^{e}(J^{e}-\theta)+\psi_{vol}(\theta)}_{\textrm{volumetric constraint}}+\underbrace{s^{e}(I_{4}^{e}-\lambda)+\psi_{ani}(\lambda)}_{\textrm{inextensibility constraint}}\,.

(44)

Here,
p^{e},\,s^{e}
are penalty parameters dual to the kinematic quantities
\theta,\,\lambda
. The deformation of the body enforced by incompressible and inextensible constraints is governed by the
mixed saddle point principle

\{{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{t},\theta,p^{e},\lambda,s^{e}\}=\mathop{\operator@font Arg}\{\inf_{{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{t}\in{\Symb W}}\inf_{\theta}\inf_{\lambda}\sup_{p^{e}}\sup_{s^{e}}\hat{\Pi}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},t)\}\,.

(45)

subject to the boundary conditions
{\Symb W}=\{{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{t}~|~{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{t}\in{\Symb B}\quad\land\quad{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{t}=\bar{}{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\quad\textrm{on}\quad\partial{\Symb B}_{u}\}
.
Taking the first variation of (
43
) with respect to
{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
,
p^{e}
,
\theta
,
s^{e}
and
\lambda
provides the weak form

\begin{array}[]{l@{~=~}l}\delta_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\hat{\Pi}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},p^{e},\theta,s^{e},\lambda)&\displaystyle\int\nolimits_{{\Symb B}}\{\left({\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{iso}+p^{e}J^{e}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}^{-1}+2s^{e}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}\right):\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\pounds}_{\delta{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}\}\mkern 4.0mu plus 2.0mu minus 4.0mudV-\delta\hat{\Pi}^{ext}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}})=0\,,\\[10.76385pt]
\delta_{p^{e}}\hat{\Pi}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},p^{e},\theta,s^{e},\lambda)&\displaystyle\int\nolimits_{{\Symb B}}\delta p^{e}~(J^{e}-\theta)\mkern 4.0mu plus 2.0mu minus 4.0mudV=0\,,\\[10.76385pt]
\delta_{\theta}\hat{\Pi}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},p^{e},\theta,s^{e},\lambda)&\displaystyle\int\nolimits_{{\Symb B}}\delta\theta~(\psi_{vol}^{\prime e}(\theta)-p^{e})\mkern 4.0mu plus 2.0mu minus 4.0mudV=0\,,\\[10.76385pt]
\delta_{s^{e}}\hat{\Pi}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},p^{e},\theta,s^{e},\lambda)&\displaystyle\int\nolimits_{{\Symb B}}\delta s^{e}~(I_{4}^{e}-\lambda)\mkern 4.0mu plus 2.0mu minus 4.0mudV=0\,,\\[10.76385pt]
\delta_{\lambda}\hat{\Pi}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},p^{e},\theta,s^{e},\lambda)&\displaystyle\int\nolimits_{{\Symb B}}\delta\lambda~(\psi_{ani}^{\prime e}(\lambda)-s^{e})\mkern 4.0mu plus 2.0mu minus 4.0mudV=0\,,\end{array}

(46)

from which the mixed finite element method can be formulated. Therein,
\boldsymbol{\pounds}_{\delta{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{g}}}
is the Lie derivative of the current metric along the variation
\delta{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
.
Taking the variation of the potential density (
44
),
Euler–Lagrange equations
of the mixed variational principle gives

\begin{array}[]{ll@{~=~}l}1.&J\mathop{\operator@font div}[J^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}]+\rho_{0}\bar{}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}&0\\[4.30554pt]
2.&J-\theta&0\\[4.30554pt]
3.&\psi_{vol}^{\prime}(\theta)-p^{e}&0\\[4.30554pt]
4.&I_{4}-\lambda&0\\[4.30554pt]
5.&\psi_{ani}^{\prime}(\lambda)-s^{e}&0\\[4.30554pt]
\end{array}

(47)

along with the Neumann-type boundary conditions

{\Symb W}_{t}=\{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}={\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{t}}}\quad\textrm{on}\quad\partial{\Symb B}_{t}\}
. The consistent linearization of the mixed potential has been implemented and discretized in Python-based open-source finite element platform
FEniCS

[
65
]
. The linear Newton iterations are solved through MUltifrontal Massively Parallel sparse direct Solver (MUMPS)
[

4
Representative numerical examples

A numerical example is demonstrated in this section through a boundary value problem. As a mathematical representative case, effects of fiber’s stiffness to the buckling mode under planar growth are studied using
T2P0F0
element in the open-source automated finite element program
FEniCS
for a 3D stiff film on a compliant substrate.

4.1
Planar growth of fiber reinforced 3D-bilayer stiff film on a compliant substrate

Figure 5
:
Schematic representation of the 3D bilayer plate (stiff transversely isotropic film on isotropic compliant substrate) with its geometric dimensions. L is the edge length of the square in-plane section, H is the total height including the film and the substrate,
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}_{0}
is the stiff fiber direction along y axis, L
cr1
and L

are the critical lowest dimensions needed to be taken into account to reflect periodic behaviour of RVE (Representative Volume Element)

This example demonstrates the effect of the stiffness of the fibers, which exist in the stiff film, on the critical growth parameter of instability state under the planar growth. Since the analyzed domain is just a small part/patch of the real structure like a representative volume element, it is necessary to define boundary conditions properly to represent the overall behavior accurately. The schematic illustration of the 3D bilayer plate model is shown in Figure
5
. In order to reflect the infinitely long plate analogy along x and y-axes, we assume that buckling the behavior of the structure repeats itself periodically with a critical wavelength (
\lambda_{cr}
). Thus, we define periodic boundary conditions on sidewalls (blue and purple faces and opposite faces in
5
). To define periodic boundary conditions, we relate these face pairs by displacement relations on the left face at
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{x}}}=0
and on the right face at
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{x}}}=L_{cr1}
as shown below:

\begin{array}[]{l@{}l}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{x}}}^{L}-{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{x}}}^{R}=L_{cr1}\\[10.76385pt]
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{y}}}^{L}={\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{y}}}^{R}\qquad\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{u}}}^{L}-{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{u}}}^{R}=0\\[10.76385pt]
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{z}}}^{L}={\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{z}}}^{R}\end{array}

(48)

Similary, we define periodic boundary relations on the front face at
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{y}}}=0
and on the rear face at
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{y}}}=L_{cr2}
as show below:

\begin{array}[]{l@{}l}{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{x}}}^{F}={\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{x}}}^{B}\\[10.76385pt]
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{y}}}^{F}-{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{y}}}^{B}=L_{cr2}\quad\quad\text{and}\qquad{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{u}}}^{F}-{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{u}}}^{B}=0\\[10.76385pt]
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{z}}}^{F}={\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{z}}}^{B}\end{array}

(49)

where superscripts

,
L
,
F
, and

Since an infinitely long and wide plate is modeled as a finite-sized computational domain with periodic boundary conditions, the fiber direction is no longer significant for the one family fiber configuration. In other words, for single-family fiber models with periodic boundary conditions, the same buckling modes are obtained for different angles. Therefore, the initial fiber angle
{\boldsymbol{\mathnormal{n}}}_{0}
is aligned to the y-direction as

5
Remarks and conclusion

In this work, planar growth-induced instabilities on bilayer 3D thick stiff film on the compliant substrate are examined in a various range of fiber stiffness with a five field Hu-Washizu type mixed variational formulation on Eulerian configuration for the quasi-incompressible and quasi-inextensible limits of transversely isotropic materials. We have studied a numerical example of mixed variational formulation by the implementation of
T2P0F0
element, in order to understand the effect of fiber stiffness on a planar growth-induced primary and secondary instabilities on 3D representative bilayer plate. As a preliminary study for the post-buckling analysis, it was worked on a long but thin bilayer plate for the determination of exact wavelengths which plays key role in the periodic boundary condition in 3D plate having rectangular in-plane section. It is shown that, the wavelength decreases by increasing the fiber stiffness. According to results of second phase of the study, while higher fiber stiffness on the film layer causes the first instability in the direction of fibers with a lower growth parameter
g
, the effect of the fiber stiffness is in the minor level on the secondary buckling mode where it is observed perpendicular to fiber direction. Another outcome is the energy release mechanism at the initiation of buckling is mainly composed of isotropic and anisotropic contributions of stiff film layer. For a higher fiber stiffness, the effect of the anisotropic energy on the first buckling becomes more dominant than other type of energy components. However in the secondary instability, isotropic energy of the film layer plays key role for energy release mechanism. Numerical outcomes of this study will help to comprehend the role of the fiber stiffness on the the buckling and post-buckling behavior of multi-layer structures. As a future work, the investigation of the growth-induced buckling on 3D biological tissues will be studied.
