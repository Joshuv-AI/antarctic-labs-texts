Recursion-free solution for two-loop vacuum integrals with “collinear” masses
Andrei I. Davydychev, and York Schröder

Abstract
We investigate the structure of a particular class of massive vacuum Feynman integrals at two loops. This class enjoys the linear relation m 1 + m 2 = m 3 m_{1}+m_{2}=m_{3} between its three propagator masses, corresponding to zeros of the associated Källén function. Apart from having applications in thermal field theory, the integrals can be mapped onto one-loop three-point functions with collinear external momenta, suggesting the term “collinear” masses. We present a closed-form solution for these integrals, proving that they can always be factorized into products of one-loop cases, for all integer-valued propagator powers.

Introduction

Loop integrals with internal propagator masses play a prominent role in contemporary precision collider phenomenology.
They are also interesting objects from a mathematical point of view, motivating the study of classes of special functions,
see
Smirnov:2012gma
;
Weinzierl:2022eaz
;
Abreu:2022mfk
;
Blumlein:2022zkr
for recent reviews of techniques and results.
For example, tremendous efforts have been invested into studying massive two-point functions at two loops, the so-called
sunrise (aka sunset aka banana) class of Feynman integrals
Lairez:2022zkj
,
giving rise to elliptic structures that go beyond the polylogarithmic results
Bourjaily:2022bwx
known
to arise from a number of simpler two-loop examples.

One is typically interested in resolving integrals with higher propagator powers
as well, as those arise e.g. from diagrammatic insertions, from taking derivatives when evaluating moments of observables
such as for example when expanding two-point
Davydychev:1992mt
;
Fleischer:1994ef

or three-point functions
Davydychev:1995nq
in external momenta,
or from gauge-field propagators. A standard method to reduce such higher propagator powers to lower values is the
systematic exploitation
Laporta:2000dsw
of integration-by-parts (IBP) identities
Chetyrkin:1981qh
;
Tkachov:1981wb
,
which provide for a recursive mapping of large sets of Feynman integrals
to a small linearly independent basis of so-called master integrals.

Oftentimes, however, the IBP reduction has turned out
to be a bottleneck (note however new developments, connected to intersection
theory
Mizera:2019ose
;
Frellesvig:2020qot
;
Caron-Huot:2021iev
, or methods exploiting relations that involve
also changes in the space-time dimension
Tarasov:1996br
;
Lee:2009dh
).
It is therefore extremely valuable to solve specific classes of Feynman integrals analytically, in non-recursive form.
Such general all-order solutions are rather scarce, exceptions being some massless cases such as two-point functions
with massless propagators, which are completely known at four loops
Baikov:2010hf
;
Lee:2011jt
,
or some specific single-scale problems (see e.g.
Vladimirov:1979zm
for a two-loop example relevant for the present work).
In the present paper, we add one massive two-loop example to the pool of general solutions,
be it as ingredient for optimizing higher-order calculations, or as a benchmark for alternative evaluation methods.

To motivate the specific choice of masses that we study here, let us briefly recall the Källén function, perhaps mainly known
from its role in kinematics.
The completely symmetric Källén function

of 3 arguments

\displaystyle\lambda(x^{2},y^{2},z^{2})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle x^{4}+y^{4}+z^{4}-2(x^{2}y^{2}+y^{2}z^{2}+z^{2}x^{2})

(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x+y+z)(x-y-z)(y-z-x)(z-x-y)

(2)

Byckling:1971vca
.
It is sometimes also called triangle function, since in geometry Heron’s formula asserts that

measures (four times) the area of a triangle with side lengths
x

Here, we will encounter this function multiple times, in quite a different – but in view of section

We note that in IBP reductions of Feynman integrals to their respective sets of master integrals the Källén function appears in a number of explicit integral reduction relations, as a function of squared external four-momenta or Mandelstam variables and squared masses.
For concrete examples, we refer to
Berends:1996gs
as well as the seminal paper

It might therefore be natural to ask whether the integral reduction problem is affected by zeros of the Källén function.

In order to get a first insight by studying the cleanest possible setting, let us focus on Feynman integrals where all external momenta vanish, so-called vacuum integrals. This leaves only the internal particles’ masses as energy scales. 5

Returning to the discussion of section
1

p_{1}

p_{2}
and hence
p_{3}=-p_{1}-p_{2}
for momentum conservation), the Källén function

vanishes in the collinear limit (i.e. for parallel external momenta:

It is therefore natural to ask whether one can also expect a significant simplification of three-point functions in the collinear limit, analogous to the factorization property of “mass-collinear” vacuum integrals proven above.
In the one-loop case with massless internal lines the answer turns out to be affirmative, as we will demonstrate in this section.

The key ingredient is an astonishing exact relation between 1-loop massless triangles

5.1
Re-deriving the “magic” connection

The Feynman parameter representations for our two types of three-propagator integrals are both three-fold and read

\displaystyle\frac{T^{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\nu_{3}}(d;p_{1}^{2},p_{2}^{2},p_{3}^{2})}{L^{d}\,\Gamma({\Sigma\nu_{i}}-\frac{d}{2})}

\displaystyle\int_{x_{i}\geq 0}\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{3}\frac{{\rm d}x_{j}\,x_{j}^{\nu_{j}}}{x_{j}\,\Gamma(\nu_{j})}\Big)\,\frac{\delta(x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}-1)}{(x_{1}x_{2}p_{3}^{2}+x_{1}x_{3}p_{2}^{2}+x_{2}x_{3}p_{1}^{2})^{{\Sigma\nu_{i}}-\frac{d}{2}}}\;,

Considering the effort that went into establishing the partial results

We have gone full circle, guided by the Källén function of eq. (

First, its zeros provided us with a linear propagator mass relation.
Then, at these Källén zeros, IBP reduction relations for our massive two-loop vacuum integral simplified to a degree that allowed to

Next, and perhaps surprisingly, the Källén function resurfaced in operator form at the core of the powerful two-step IBP reduction relation eq. (

Finally, it not only governed the generating function for the combinatorics at the core of the coefficients

Looking back, we note that in the case of

while for deriving the coefficients in the full solution for

Both of these solutions of specific multidimensional recurrences seem currently out of reach for

A first application of our factorization formula has been to massless collinear three-point functions in section

This is in close analogy to the well-known triangle rule

These closed forms for general-index cases are solutions in the sense that they express the result as a non-recursive linear
combinations over simpler (lower-loop or lower-point) structures.
The advantage of such explicit solutions is that fewer terms are generated (than in IBP recursions),
and that spurious poles at intermediate steps of a recursion can be avoided.

Another straightforward, albeit rather mathematical, application would be to extract explicit results for
certain types of Bessel moments. In

Setting

triple-Bessel moments with general indices.

One might be tempted to further simplify the above result and solve one of the two remaining finite sums, perhaps after commuting them.
We have not been able to do this ourselves, but regard our final result as highly useful in the present form, as for most practical
cases the indices
\nu_{i}
have rather small integer values, such that only very few terms contribute to the double sum.
Other interesting generalizations would be to derive compact results for other special-index cases other than
B^{\tau,\tau,\nu}(d)

such as e.g. for

relevant kinematic constraints such as the linear mass-relations exploited here) of higher-loop IBP relations and attempt progress

To show how eq. (

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\nu}p_{\nu,j}\,\frac{[(d-j-1)\,x^{j-1}+(2j-d)\,x^{j}]}{(1-x)^{\nu+1-j}}\;,

(135)

with

Replace

\displaystyle=

Rewrite the double sum by adding and subtracting a number of
(j,k)
-points (see figure

\displaystyle 0\;,

(143)

Appendix B
Exposing and solving the recurrence at the core of eq. (
38
)

Recurrence relations (or difference equations) are discrete equations that determine the elements of a sequence over the integers,
given some initial conditions. Solving a recurrence relation amounts to deriving an explicit solution for this sequence in
non-recursive
form.

In the case of one-dimensional linear recurrences for example, a sequence
f_{n}
is determined for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
from an

r
-th order recurrence relation
\sum_{k=0}^{r}a^{(k)}f_{n+k}=b
, given
r
initial conditions
f_{n_{i}}
.
In analogy to differential equations, the relation is called homogeneous when
b=0
, and the coefficients can be constant or variable
(i.e. depend on the index
n
). There are solution strategies for various such types of recurrences, typically involving generating functions
and the roots of the characteristic polynomial.
A prominent example is the second-order homogeneous recurrence with constant coefficients
f_{n}=a\,f_{n-1}+b\,f_{n-2}
,
with boundary conditions
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
, whose special case
\{f_{0},f_{1},a,b\}=\{0,1,1,1\}
generates the Fibonacci sequence.
The general linear first-order non-homogeneous recurrence relation with variable coefficients

\displaystyle f_{n+1}\;=\;a_{n}\,f_{n}+b_{n}

(146)

can be solved in terms of an initial condition
f_{n_{0}}
as

\displaystyle f_{n}\;=\;\Big(\prod_{k=n_{0}}^{n-1}a_{k}\Big)\,f_{n_{0}}+\sum_{j=n_{0}}^{n-1}b_{j}\,\Big(\prod_{k=j+1}^{n-1}a_{k}\Big)\;.

(147)

If the coefficient
a_{n}
vanishes for some integers
n=n_{i}
, the above form of the solution is useful for determining
f_{n}
for all
n\leq{\rm min}\{n_{i}\}
, while the term proportional to the initial condition
f_{n_{0}}
is annihilated by its prefactor when determining
f_{n}
for
n>{\rm min}\{n_{i}\}
. In the latter case, the relation itself enforces initial values as
f_{n_{i}+1}=b_{n_{i}}
, such that it is sufficient to set
f_{n}=\sum_{j=n_{i}}^{n-1}b_{j}\,\Big(\prod_{k=j+1}^{n-1}a_{k}\Big)
for
n>n_{i}
.

For multi-dimensional linear recurrences, which define multi-dimensional arrays of values, much less is known.
A well-known example are the binomial coefficients, defined by the recurrence (two-dimensional, linear, homogeneous, constant coefficients)

\displaystyle b_{n,k}=b_{n-1,k}+b_{n-1,k-1}\;,\quad b_{0,k}=\delta_{k}\;,

(148)

with solution
b_{n,k}={n\choose k}
and bivariate generating function
g(x,y)\equiv\sum_{i,j}b_{i,j}x^{i}y^{j}=\frac{1}{1-x-xy}
.
Another simple example are the Stirling numbers of second kind, defined by the recurrence
(two-dimensional, linear, homogeneous, variable coefficients)

\displaystyle S_{n,k}=k\,S_{n-1,k}+S_{n-1,k-1}\;,\quad S_{0,k}=\delta_{k}\;,

(149)

that is solved by the one-dimensional sum
S_{n,k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{k-j}\,j^{n-1}}{(j-1)!\,(k-j)!}
, while no closed form of the generating function is known.

Looking now at the structure of the recurrence eq. (
38
), it is clear that integrals
B^{\tau,\tau,\nu}

can be reduced to a linear combination of similar integrals at
a=1
, but different values of
\nu
, as

\displaystyle B^{1+N,1+N,\nu}(d)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N}{u}_{N,k}(d,\nu)\,(m_{3}^{2}\,\mathbb{3}^{+})^{k}(\mathbb{d}^{-})^{N}B^{1,1,\nu}(d)\;,

(150)

where the
{u}_{N,k}(d,\nu)
are coefficient functions that need to be determined.

At
N=0
, this Ansatz reduces to
B^{1,1,\nu}(d)={u}_{0,0}(d,\nu)\,B^{1,1,\nu}(d)
, fixing
{u}_{0,0}(d,\nu)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}1
.

Applying the recurrence eq. (
38
) at
\nu_{1}=\nu_{2}=N
, using the Ansatz eq. (
150
) on both sides,
and comparing coefficients of
(m_{3}^{2}\,\mathbb{3}^{+})^{j}(\mathbb{d}^{-})^{n}B^{1,1,\nu}(d)
leads to a recurrence for
{u}

\displaystyle(d-2)n^{2}\,{u}_{n,j}(d,\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\delta_{j,n})(d-2-2\nu)\,{u}_{n-1,j}(d-2,\nu)+(1-\delta_{j,0})\,2\nu\,{u}_{n-1,j-1}(d-2,\nu+1)\;.

This looks impenetrable, as it involves values of the unknown coefficient functions
{u}
with all four parameters changing.
Note however that pairs of values are related: the recurrence leaves
d-2n\equiv 2c_{1}
and
\nu+j\equiv c_{2}
invariant.
We hence set
d=2c_{1}+2n
and
\nu=c_{2}-j
, which exposes the much simpler, but completely equivalent, two-parameter recurrence

\displaystyle(c_{1}-1+n)n^{2}\,{u}_{n,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\delta_{j,n})(c_{1}-1-c_{2}+j+n)\,{u}_{n-1,j}+(1-\delta_{j,0})(c_{2}-j)\,{u}_{n-1,j-1}

(151)

where we have dropped the common function arguments
(c_{1},c_{2})
.
The values of
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are then substituted back only
after
solving the equation.
To clean up the equation further, we can remove the factor
a_{n}\equiv(c_{1}-1+n)n^{2}

from the left-hand side
by defining

\displaystyle\bar{{u}}_{n,j}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle(-1)^{n+j}\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\Big)\,{u}_{n,j}\;=\;(-1)^{n+j}\big(c_{1}\big)_{n}\,[n!]^{2}\,{u}_{n,j}\;,

(152)

where the sign has been introduced for convenience. The recurrence is now (with
c_{3}=1+c_{2}-c_{1}
)

\displaystyle\bar{{u}}_{n,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\delta_{j,n})(c_{3}-j-n)\bar{{u}}_{n-1,j}+(1-\delta_{j,0})(c_{2}-j)\bar{{u}}_{n-1,j-1}\;,\quad\bar{{u}}_{0,0}=1\;.

(153)

The function
\bar{{u}}_{n,j}
takes non-zero values only in the wedge
0\leq j\leq n
.
One can drop the
(1-\delta)
factors by using a generalized boundary condition at
n=0
for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle\bar{{u}}_{n,j}=(c_{3}-j-n)\,\bar{{u}}_{n-1,j}+(c_{2}-j)\,\bar{{u}}_{n-1,j-1}\;,\quad\bar{{u}}_{0,j}=\delta_{j}\;.

(154)

Undeterred by the fact that eq. (
154
) looks very similar to – but is slightly more complicated than – the Stirling
recurrence eq. (
149
) (for which no closed-form solution exists), we also note its structural similarity to the
binomial recurrence eq. (
148
) which motivates us to try the Ansatz
\bar{{u}}_{n,j}={n\choose j}\,\tilde{{u}}_{n,j}
.
This takes care of the generalized boundary conditions, since
{0\choose j}=\delta_{j}
, resulting in the new recurrence
(after dividing by
\frac{1}{n}{n\choose j}=\frac{1}{n-j}{n-1\choose j}=\frac{1}{j}{n-1\choose j-1}
)

\displaystyle n\,\tilde{{u}}_{n,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(c_{3}-n-j)\,(n-j)\,\tilde{{u}}_{n-1,j}+(c_{2}-j)\,j\,\tilde{{u}}_{n-1,j-1}\;,\quad\tilde{{u}}_{0,0}=1\;.

(155)

We can rearrange this equation into groups that involve
n
- or
j
-changes only

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\big[(c_{3}-n)\,\tilde{{u}}_{n-1,j}-\tilde{{u}}_{n,j}\big]+j\big[(c_{2}-j)\,\tilde{{u}}_{n-1,j-1}-(c_{3}-j)\,\tilde{{u}}_{n-1,j}\big]\;,

(156)

which suggests to solve by a factorization Ansatz
\tilde{{u}}_{n,j}=f_{n}\,g_{j}
, leading to two one-parameter recurrences that correspond to nullifying the two square brackets. These can be solved easily as

\displaystyle f_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(c_{3}-n)\,f_{n-1}=\Big(\prod_{k=1}^{n}(c_{3}-k)\Big)\,f_{0}=\frac{\Gamma(c_{3})}{\Gamma(c_{3}-n)}\,f_{0}\;,

(157)

\displaystyle g_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(c_{2}-j)}{(c_{3}-j)}\,g_{j-1}=\Big(\prod_{k=1}^{j}\frac{c_{2}-k}{c_{3}-k}\Big)\,g_{0}=\frac{\Gamma(c_{2})}{\Gamma(c_{2}-j)}\,\frac{\Gamma(c_{3}-j)}{\Gamma(c_{3})}\,g_{0}\;.

(158)

Equation (
154
) is therefore solved by

\displaystyle\bar{{u}}_{n,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{n\choose j}\,\frac{\Gamma(c_{3})}{\Gamma(c_{3}-n)}\,\frac{\Gamma(c_{3}-j)}{\Gamma(c_{3})}\,\frac{\Gamma(c_{2})}{\Gamma(c_{2}-j)}\,\bar{{u}}_{0,0}\;.

(159)

Reversing the definitions, replacing the
c_{i}
, and simplifying, we finally obtain the coefficients of eq. (
150
) in closed form as

\displaystyle{u}_{n,j}(d,\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{j}\,\big(\nu\big)_{j}\,\big(1+n-\frac{d}{2}\big)_{\nu}}{n!\,j!\,(n-j)!\,\big(1-\frac{d}{2}\big)_{j+\nu}}\;.

(160)

With eqs. (
160
) and (
150
) established as solution to eq. (
38
),
we can as well simply postulate this solution right away as in eq. (
40
), and prove it via induction over
N
,
see the comments in the main text.
We think, however, that it is instructive to see the derivation as exposed here, given that a non-trivial two-dimensional
recurrence with variable coefficients has been solved explicitly.

In summary, at the core of the IBP relation eq. (
38
) lies the recurrence eq. (
154
), with closed-form solution eq. (
159
).

Here we would like to offer an alternative derivation of the main result of section

As we have seen, the analytically known special-mass case

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1-\nu_{2}}^{\nu_{3}-1}\sum_{k={\rm max}(1+j,1)}^{{\rm min}(\nu_{2}+j,\nu_{3})}\frac{\big(1-\frac{d}{2}\big)_{n_{j}-1}\,\big(1-\frac{d}{2}\big)_{n_{j}-j-1}}{\big(1\!-\!\frac{d}{2}\big)_{n_{j}-k}\,\big(\frac{d+3}{2}\!-\!{\Sigma\nu_{i}}\big)_{n_{j}-k}}\,\frac{(-1)^{{\Sigma\nu_{i}}+j}\,G_{\nu_{2}+j-k,\nu_{3}-k,\nu_{1}-1}}{(-4)^{n_{j}-k}\,\Gamma(k)\,\Gamma(k-j)}\;,\qquad
