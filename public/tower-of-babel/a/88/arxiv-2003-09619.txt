Optimal Control of Perfect Plasticity Part II: Displacement Tracking Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Christian Meyer, Stephan Walther

Abstract
The paper is concerned with an optimal control problem governed by the rate-independent system of quasi-static perfect elasto-plasticity. The objective is optimize the displacement field in the domain occupied by the body by means of prescribed Dirichlet boundary data, which serve as control variables. The arising optimization problem is nonsmooth for several reasons, in particular, since the control-to-state mapping is not single-valued. We therefore apply a Yosida regularization to obtain a single-valued control-to-state operator. Beside the existence of optimal solutions, their approximation by means of this regularization approach is the main subject of this work. It turns out that a so-called reverse approximation guaranteeing the existence of a suitable recovery sequence can only be shown under an additional smoothness assumption on at least one optimal solution.

Optimal control of variational inequalities, perfect plasticity, rate-independent systems,
Yosida regularization, reverse approximation

Introduction

In this paper, we investigate the following optimal control problem governed by the equations of

quasi-static perfect plasticity
at small strain:

\left\{\quad\begin{aligned} &\min\text{ \ \ }J(u,u_{D}):=\Psi(u)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|u_{D}\|_{H^{1}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}))}^{2}\\
&\;\begin{aligned} &\text{s.t.}\quad&-\operatorname{div}\sigma&=0&&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
&&\sigma&=\mathbb{C}(\nabla^{s}u-z)&&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
&&\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{z}&\in\partial I_{\mathcal{K}(\Omega)}(\sigma)&&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
&&u&=u_{D}&&\text{ on }\Gamma_{D},\\
&&\sigma\nu&=0&&\text{ on }\Gamma_{N},\\
&&u(0)&=u_{0},\quad\sigma(0)=\sigma_{0}&&\text{ in }\Omega,\\[4.30554pt]
&\text{and}\quad&u_{D}(0)&=u_{0}&&\text{ on }\Gamma_{D}.\end{aligned}\end{aligned}\right.

(P)

Herein,
u:(0,T)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n=2,3
, is the displacement field, while

\sigma,z:(0,T)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are the stress tensor and the plastic strain.
The boundary of
\Omega
is split in two disjoint parts
\Gamma_{D}
and
\Gamma_{N}

with outward unit normal
\nu
. Moreover,
\mathbb{C}
is the elasticity tensor
and
\mathcal{K}(\Omega)
denotes the set of feasible stresses. The initial data
u_{0}
and
\sigma_{0}

are given and fixed. The Dirichlet data
u_{D}
represent the control variable and

\alpha>0
a fixed Tikhonov regularization parameter.
The objective
\Psi
only contains the displacement field. Objectives involving the stress are considered in
a companion paper
[
21
]
.
This is the reason for calling (
P
)
displacement tracking problem
.
A mathematically rigorous version of (
P
) involving the function spaces
and a rigorous notion of solutions for the state equation will be formulated in

section

3
and
4
below.
The precise assumptions on the data are given in
section

2
.
Regarding to a more detailed description of the plasticity model, we refer to
[
25
]

and the references therein.

Some words concerning our choice of the control variable are in order:
In general, Dirichlet control problems provide particular difficulties due to regularity issues, when control functions
in
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
are considered, see e.g.
[
18
]
.
Nonetheless, we consider the Dirichlet displacement as control variables instead of distributed loads
or forces on the Neumann boundary due to the
safe load condition
.
It is well known that the existence of solutions for the perfect plasticity system can only be shown under this additional condition
(see e.g.
[
30
,
8
]
),
which would lead to rather complex control constraints and it is a completely open question how to
incorporate these constraints in the analysis of (
P
). For this reason,
we focus on the Dirichlet control problem. A possible realization of these controls
by means of an additional linear elasticity equation avoiding the
H^{2}
-norm in the objective
is elaborated in the companion paper
[
21
]
.

Beside the safe-load condition,
problem (
P
) exhibits several additional particular challenges. First of all, it is obviously nonsmooth
due to the convex subdifferential appearing in the state equation.
Moreover, the state equation is in general not uniquely solvable and its solutions significantly lack regularity,
see
[
30
,
8
]
.
Therefore, there is no single-valued control-to-state mapping and (
P
) should rather be regarded as
an optimization problem in Banach space rather than an optimal control problem.
Beside the existence of optimal solutions, our main goal is to approximate (
P
) via replacing

\partial I_{\mathcal{K}(\Omega)}
by its Yosida regularization.
This is of course a classical procedure and, in order to show that the approximation works, i.e., that optimal solutions
of the regularized problems converge to solutions of (
P
) (in a certain topology),
the following steps have to be performed:

1.

The existence of (weak) accumulation points of sequences of optimal solutions of the regularized
problems have to be verified.

2.

Weak limits have to be feasible for the original problem (
P
).

3.

In order to show the optimality of the weak limit, one has to construct a
recovery sequence

for at least one optimal solution of the original problem.

The last item is also known as
reverse approximation
and might become a challenging task
in the context of optimization of rate-independent systems, see
[
22
]
.
This also happens to be the case here:
In contrast to the perfect plasticity system, its regularized counterpart admits a unique solution with full regularity.
It is therefore very unlikely that one can approximate
every
solution of the perfect plasticity system
by means of regularization and indeed, as classical examples demonstrate, this is in fact not true, see e.g.
[
30
]

and
example

13
below.
However, in the context of optimal control and optimization, respectively, we have the control as an additional variable at hand
and, in order to construct a recovery sequence, we have to find a sequence of
tuples of state and control
feasible for
the regularized problems so that the associated objective function values converge to the optimal value of (
P
). This leads to much more flexibility in the construction of recovery sequences, provided
that the set of controls is sufficiently rich. Unfortunately, this is not the case for our Dirichlet control and we need an
additional control variable in terms of
distributed loads
for the construction of a recovery sequence.
The idea is thus to introduce an additional load in the balance of momentum of the regularized problems and to drive
this load to zero for vanishing regularization parameter.
Our regularization procedure therefore does not only replace the convex subdifferential by its Yosida regularization,
but also introduces a new additional control variable. To the best of our knowledge, this is a completely new idea.

Nevertheless, even with this additional control variable, we are only able to construct a recovery sequence under a fairly restrictive assumption. This assumption is caused by additional smoothness constraints as part of the regularized optimal control problems,
which in turn are needed to pass to the limit in the regularized plasticity system,
when the regularization parameter is driven to zero. If we assume that at least one optimal solution of the original
(i.e., unregularized) optimization problem admits an admittedly high regularity, then we are able to construct
a recovery sequence for this particular solution, which meets the smoothness constraints and is therefore
feasible for the regularized optimal control problems.
We thus obtain the desired approximation result under the assumption that there exists at least one “smooth”
solution of (
P
).

Let us put our work into perspective:
Quasi-static perfect plasticity is a rate-independent system. Optimization and optimal control of such systems
have been considered by various authors and we only refer to
[
4
,
5
,
1
,
6
,
7
,
29
,
24
,
2
,
14
]
and the references therein.
Albeit still nonsmooth, optimization problems of this type substantially simplify, if the energy underlying the rate-independent system
is uniformly convex. In quasi-static plasticity, this is the case, if hardening is present.
In this case, the plasticity system admits a unique solution in the energy space, which makes the construction of
recovery sequences almost trivial.
Nevertheless, the derivation of optimality conditions is still an intricate issue, see
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
.
While all contributions mentioned so far deal with uniformly convex energies,
the literature becomes rather scarce, when it comes to energies that lack strict convexity.
In
[
26
,
28
,
11
,
10
]
the existence of optimal solutions for problems with non-convex energies are shown.
To the best of our knowledge, the approximation of such problems has only been investigated in
[
22
,
27
]
,
where a time-discretization instead of a regularization is considered.
The approximation via discretization can however be hardly compared to our situation, since the discrete
rate-independent systems are still not uniquely solvable so that there is still no (discrete) control-to-state map
in contrast to the regularized setting. Therefore, the discrete optimization problems are still all but straight forward
to solve, whereas the regularized optimal control problems are amenable for standard adjoint-based optimization methods.

The paper is organized as follows:
After introducing our notation and standard assumptions in
section

, we introduce a rigorous notion of solution to the perfect plasticity
system and recall the known results concerning the existence of solutions and the lack of uniqueness in
section

3
.
Then,
section

4
is devoted to the existence of at least one (globally) optimal solution of (
P
).
In
section

5
, we lay the foundations for our reverse approximation argument for the construction of a recovery sequence,
which is a basic ingredient for our main result in
theorem

32
. The last
section

6
covers this result and shows that
solutions of (
P
) can indeed be approximated via Yosida regularization provided the mentioned
regularity assumption is fulfilled.

Coefficients

3
State Equation

We start our investigations with the analysis of the state system and recall some known results concerning
quasi-static perfect plasticity. Already since the pioneering work of Suquet
[
30
]
,
it is well known that a precise definition of a solution to
the system of perfect plasticity is all but straight forward, since a solution of the system in its “natural” form
(below termed strong solution) does in general not exist due to a lack of regularity of the displacement and the
plastic strain, respectively.
We start with the definition of the function spaces already indicating this lack of regularity:

Definition 4
(State spaces).

1.

Stress space:

\Sigma(\Omega):=\{\tau\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega):\operatorname{div}\tau\in\mathbf{L}^{n}(\Omega),\;\tau^{D}\in\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)\}

2.

Displacement space:

\mathcal{U}:=\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbf{L}^{\frac{n}{n-1}}(\Omega)):\nabla^{s}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}\in L^{2}_{w}(\mathfrak{M}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\textup{sym}}))\}.

Herein,
L^{2}_{w}(\mathfrak{M}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\textup{sym}}))
is the space of weakly measurable functions with values in
\mathfrak{M}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\textup{sym}})
,
for which
t\mapsto\|\mu(t)\|_{\mathfrak{M}}
is an element of
L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R})
.
For the definition of weak measurability, we refer to
[
9
, Section 8]
.

We say that a sequence
\{u_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{U}
converges weakly in
\mathcal{U}
to

and write
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u
in
\mathcal{U}
, iff

u_{n}\rightharpoonup u\;\text{ in }H^{1}(\mathbf{L}^{\frac{n}{n-1}}(\Omega)),\quad\nabla^{s}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{n}\rightharpoonup^{*}\nabla^{s}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}\;\text{ in }L^{2}_{w}(\mathfrak{M}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\textup{sym}})).

(5)

Note that, by
[
9
, Theorem 8.20.3]
,
L^{2}_{w}(\mathfrak{M}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\textup{sym}}))=L^{2}(C_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\textup{sym}}))^{*}
,
which gives a meaning to the weak-
\ast
convergence in (
5
).

Remark 5
.

Unfortunately,
\mathrm{BD}(\Omega)
does not admit the Radon-Nikodým property and therefore weak measurability does not imply Bochner-measurability.

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\Omega):=\operatorname{ker}(\operatorname{div})=\{\tau\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega):\left(\tau,\nabla^{s}\varphi\right)_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=0\;\;\forall\,\varphi\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega)\}.

With the above definitions at hand, we can now define a hierarchy of three different solutions:

\bullet

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathbb{A}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}(t)-\nabla^{s}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{D}(t)\big):\big(\tau-\sigma(t)\big)\,\textup{d}x\geq 0\quad\forall\,\tau\in\mathcal{E}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{K}(\Omega),

(6c)

\bullet

\displaystyle\sigma(0)=\sigma_{0}.

(6d)

2.

A tuple

\displaystyle\sigma(t)\in\mathcal{E}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{K}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{\Omega}\mathbb{A}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}(t):\big(\tau-\sigma(t)\big)\,\textup{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}(t)\cdot\operatorname{div}\big(\tau-\sigma(t)\big)\,\textup{d}x\qquad\qquad&\\

\forall\,\tau\in\Sigma(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{K}(\Omega),&\end{aligned}

\bullet
Initial condition:

\displaystyle u(0)=u_{0},\quad\sigma(0)=\sigma_{0}.

(7d)

Strong solution:

if, for almost all
t\in(0,T)
, there holds

\bullet
Equilibrium and yield condition:

\displaystyle\sigma(t)\in\mathcal{E}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{K}(\Omega),

(8b)

\bullet
Strong flow rule inequality:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{\Omega}\mathbb{A}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}(t):\big(\tau-\sigma(t)\big)\,\textup{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\nabla^{s}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}(t):\big(\tau-\sigma(t)\big)\,\textup{d}x\geq 0\qquad&\\[-4.30554pt]
\forall\,\tau\in\mathcal{K}(\Omega),&\end{aligned}

(8c)

\bullet
Dirichlet boundary condition:

\displaystyle u(t)-u_{D}(t)\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega)

\bullet
Initial condition:

\displaystyle u(0)=u_{0},\quad\sigma(0)=\sigma_{0}.

(8e)

Some words concerning this definition are in order.
First, let us shortly investigate the relationship between the three different solution concepts.
By restricting the test functions in (
7c
) to functions in
\mathcal{E}(\Omega)
, one immediately
observes that every weak solution is also a reduced solution.
Moreover, by integration by parts,
it is evident that (
8c
) and (
8d
) imply (
7c
).
On the other hand, if a weak solution satisfies
u\in H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega))
and the Dirichlet boundary conditions in (
8d
),
then integration by parts yields (
8c
), provided that
\Sigma(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{K}(\Omega)

is dense in
\mathcal{K}(\Omega)
, which is a direct consequence of
lemma

36
proven in the appendix.
Thus, we have the following relations between the three different solution concepts:

Corollary 8
(Relations between the solution concepts).

1.

If
(u,\sigma)
is a weak solution, then
\sigma
is automatically a reduced solution.

2.

A weak solution
(u,\sigma)
is a strong solution,
if and only if
u\in H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega))
and
(u-u_{D})(t)\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega)
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

One may further ask why no Dirichlet boundary conditions appear in the definition of a weak solution.
In fact, from a mechanical point of view, it is reasonable that no boundary conditions are imposed,
since plastic slips may well develop on the Dirichlet part on the boundary, too,
in form of tangential jumps of the displacement perpendicular to the outward normal
\nu
.
This observation is implicitly contained in the above definition as demonstrated in
[
8
, Theorem 6.1]
.
For convenience of the reader, we shortly sketch the underlying arguments.
To this end, suppose that a weak solution is given and let us define
the
plastic strain

z\in L^{2}_{w}(\mathfrak{M}(\Omega\cup\Gamma_{D};\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\textup{sym}}))
by

z\lfloor_{\Omega}:=\nabla^{s}(u)-\mathbb{A}\sigma\,\textup{d}x,\quad z\lfloor_{\Gamma_{D}}:=(u-u_{D})\odot\nu\mathcal{H}^{n-1},

(9)

where
\odot
refers to the symmetrized dyadic product, i.e.,
a\odot b=1/2(a_{i}b_{j}+a_{j}b_{i})_{i,j=1}^{n}

for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Note that functions in
\mathrm{BD}(\Omega)
admit traces in
L^{1}(\partial\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
(see e.g.
[
31
, Chap. II, Thm. 2.1]
)
so that
z\lfloor_{\Gamma_{D}}
is well defined.
According to
[
8
, Lemma 5.5]
, these equations carry over to the time derivatives for almost all
t\in(0,T)
, i.e.,

\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{z}\lfloor_{\Omega}:=\nabla^{s}(\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u})-\mathbb{A}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}\,\textup{d}x,\quad\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{z}\lfloor_{\Gamma_{D}}:=(\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}-\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{D})\odot\nu\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

(10)

Let us prove that the trace of
p
vanishes. For this purpose, we need the following formula of integration by parts:

Lemma 9
(
[
31
, Chap. II, Thm. 2.1]
).

For every
v\in\mathrm{BD}(\Omega)
and every
\varphi\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
,
p>n
, there holds

\int_{\Omega}\tfrac{1}{2}(v_{i}\partial_{j}\varphi+v_{j}\partial_{i}\varphi)\,\textup{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,\textup{d}(\nabla^{s}v)_{ij}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi\,\tfrac{1}{2}(v_{i}\nu_{j}+v_{j}\nu_{i})\,\textup{d}s

(11)

for all
i,j=1,...,n
.

Remark 10
.

The result in
[
31
]
is only stated for test functions in
C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})
. However, the embeddings

\mathrm{BD}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathbf{L}^{\frac{n}{n-1}}(\Omega)
and
W^{1,p}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow C(\bar{\Omega})
,
p>n
,
along with the trace theorem for
\mathrm{BD}
-functions and the density of
C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})
in
W^{1,p}(\Omega)

imply that the integration by parts also holds for test functions in
W^{1,p}(\Omega)
.

Now, let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
be arbitrary.
Then, since
\mathcal{K}(\Omega)
just acts on the deviatoric part,

\varphi\,\delta_{ij}+\sigma_{ij}(t)\in\Sigma(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{K}(\Omega)
for
all
t\in[0,T]
and therefore (
7c
) and the above formula of integration by parts give

\sum_{i}\Big(\int_{\Omega}(\mathbb{A}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma})_{ii}\,\varphi\,\textup{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,\textup{d}(\nabla^{s}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u})_{ii}\Big)=0\quad\forall\,\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)

and therefore
\operatorname{tr}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{z}\lfloor_{\Omega}=0
f.a.a.
t\in(0,T)
.
Since
\operatorname{tr}(\nabla^{s}(u_{0})-\mathbb{A}\sigma_{0})=0
,

[
8
, Theorem 7.1]
yields
\operatorname{tr}z\lfloor_{\Omega}=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
.
Similarly, we choose an arbitrary test function
\psi\in C^{\infty}(\Gamma)
with
\operatorname{supp}(\psi)\subset\Gamma_{D}
and
test (
7c
) with
\mathfrak{E}\psi\,\delta_{ij}+\sigma_{ij}(t)\in\Sigma(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{K}(\Omega)
.
Note that
\mathfrak{E}\psi\,\delta_{ij}\in\Sigma(\Omega)
, since the harmonic extension maps into
W^{1,p}(\Omega)

with
p>n
.
Applying then again the formula of integration by parts implies, in view of

\operatorname{tr}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{z}\lfloor_{\Omega}=0
, that

(\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}-\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{D})\cdot\nu=0\quad\text{a.e.~on }\Gamma_{D}.

(12)

As
u_{0}=u_{D}(0)
a.e. on
\Gamma_{D}
, this yields
(u-u_{D})\cdot\nu=0
a.e. on
\Gamma_{D}
, giving in turn

\operatorname{tr}z\lfloor_{\Gamma_{D}}=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
.
Now that we know that
z
is deviatoric, the formula of integration by parts from
[
8
, Proposition 2.2]

is applicable, which yields

\langle\tau^{D},\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{z}(t)\rangle+\int_{\Omega}\tau:\big(\mathbb{A}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}(t)-\nabla^{s}(\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{D}(t))\big)\,\textup{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{div}\tau\cdot(\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}(t)-\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{D}(t))\,\textup{d}x

(13)

for almost all
t\in(0,T)
and all
\tau\in\Sigma(\Omega)
.
It is to be noted that the duality product
\langle\tau^{D},\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{z}\rangle
has to be treated with care, since, in general,

\tau^{D}\notin C(\bar{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\textup{sym}})
, but
\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{z}
is only a measure. For a detailed and rigorous discussion of this issue,
we refer to
[
8
, Section 2.3]
.
Inserting (
13
) in the flow rule inequality (
7c
) then results in

\langle\tau^{D}-\sigma^{D}(t),\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{z}(t)\rangle\geq 0\quad\forall\,\tau\in\Sigma(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{K}(\Omega),

(14)

which is just the maximum plastic work inequality illustrating that
z
as defined in (
9
) is indeed
the correct object for the plastic strain.
As a byproduct, we obtain the second equation in (
9
) as boundary condition on
\Gamma_{D}

indicating that the Dirichlet boundary condition in (
8d
) as part of the definition of a strong solution
is in general too restrictive as already mentioned above.
Accordingly, a strong solution does in general not exist, while we have the following result for a weak solution:

Proposition 11
(Existence of weak solutions,
[
30
, Résultat 2]
).

For all
u_{D}\in H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega))
, there exists a weak solution in the sense of
definition

7
.

Proof.

Using the Yosida regularization, Suquet showed in
[
30
]
the existence of a functions

\sigma\in H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
and
v\in L^{2}_{w}(\mathrm{BD}(\Omega))
so that, for almost all
t\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle-\operatorname{div}\sigma(t)

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{E}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{K}(\Omega),

(15)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{A}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}(t):

\displaystyle\big(\tau-\sigma(t)\big)\,\textup{d}x+\int_{\Omega}v(t)\cdot\operatorname{div}\big(\tau-\sigma(t)\big)\,\textup{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\langle\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{D}(t),(\tau-\sigma(t))\nu\rangle_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{D}),H^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{D})}\quad\forall\,\tau\in\Sigma(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{K}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\sigma(0)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0}.

Due to the continuous embedding
\mathrm{BD}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathbf{L}^{\frac{n}{n-1}}(\Omega)

(see e.g.
[
31
, Chap. II, Theorem 2.2]
) and the Radon-Nikodym property of
\mathbf{L}^{\frac{n}{n-1}}(\Omega)
, we have that

v\in L^{2}(\mathbf{L}^{\frac{n}{n-1}}(\Omega))
. Therefore,

u(t):=u_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}v(r)\,\textup{d}r

is an element of
H^{1}(\mathbf{L}^{\frac{n}{n-1}}(\Omega))
and satisfies the initial condition in (

).
Inserting this in (
15
) and integrating the right hand side by parts gives the desired flow
rule inequality (

). The claimed regularity of

directly follows from the regularity of
v=\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}
.

Remark 12
(Other equivalent notions of solutions).

Beside the reformulation of the flow rule in terms of the maximum plastic work inequality (
14
),
there are other solutions concepts, which are equivalent to the definition of a weak solution,
such as the notion of a
quasi-static evolution
, which in essence corresponds to a global energetic solution
in the sense of
[
23
]
.
For an overview over the various notions of solutions and a rigorous proof of their equivalence, we refer to

[
8
, Section 6]
. A slightly sloppy, but very illustrating derivation of the flow rule
out of the quasi-static evolution can also be found in
[
13
]
.

Unfortunately, the weak solution is not unique, as the following example shows:

Example 13
(
[
30
, Section 2.1]
).

We choose
\Omega=(0,1)
,
\Gamma_{D}=\partial\Omega=\{0,1\}
,
T=1
,

K=[-1,1]
,
\mathbb{C}=1
,
(\sigma_{0},u_{0})=0
, and
u_{D}(t,x):=2tx
.
One easily verifies that the stress does only depend on the time with

\sigma(t)=2t
for
t\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and
\sigma(t)=1
for
t\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
.
For the displacement one obtains
u(t,x)=2tx
for
(t,x)\in(0,\frac{1}{2})\times(0,1)

so that it is unique for
t\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. For
t\in(\frac{1}{2},0)
there are more than one solution, for example

\displaystyle u(t,x)

\displaystyle=2tx,\text{\, if \,}(t,x)\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)\times(0,1),

\displaystyle u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}\frac{2tx}{\beta}+x-\frac{x}{\beta},&\text{\, if \,}(t,x)\in(\frac{1}{2},1)\times[0,\beta],\\[2.15277pt]
2t+x-1,&\text{\, if \,}(t,x)\in(\frac{1}{2},1)\times[\beta,1],\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}x,&\text{\, if \,}(t,x)\in(\frac{1}{2},1)\times[0,\beta],\\[2.15277pt]
\alpha t+x-\frac{\alpha}{2},&\text{\, if \,}(t,x)\in(\frac{1}{2},1)\times[\beta,1],\end{array}\right.

where
\alpha\in[0,2]
and
\beta\in[0,1]
can be freely chosen.
Note that the last solution just provides the minimal regularity, i.e.,
\partial_{x}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}(t)\in\mathfrak{M}(\Omega)
.

The uniqueness of the stress field observed in the above example is a general result:

Lemma 14
(Uniqueness of the stress,
[
17
, Theorem 1]
,
[
21
, Lemma 3.5]
).

Assume that
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
are two reduced solutions. Then
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}
.

Remark 15
(Optimal control vs. optimization).

Since the displacement field as part of a weak solution is not unique in general, there is no
(single-valued) control-to-state operator mapping
u_{D}
to
u
.
Therefore, one might argue that (
P
) is actually no real optimal control problem.
Strictly speaking, one should rather regard it as an
optimization problem
with the triple
(u,\sigma,u_{D})

as optimization variables.

4
Existence of Optimal Solutions

Before we come to the main point of our analysis, which concerns the approximation of (
P
)
by means of regularized optimal control problems, let us address the existence of optimal solutions.
The proof in principle follows the classical direct method, for which we need the following boundedness and
continuity results:

Lemma 16
(
[
21
, Lemma 3.6]
).

Let
u_{D}\in H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega))
be given and
\sigma
be the associated reduced solution. Then there holds

\|\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}\leq\gamma_{\mathbb{A}}^{-1}\,\|u_{D}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega))},

(16)

where
\gamma_{\mathbb{A}}
is the coercivity constant of
\mathbb{A}
.
Consequently, there is a constant
C>0
such that

\|\sigma\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C(\|\sigma_{0}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u_{D}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega))})
.

Lemma 17
(Continuity of reduced solutions,
[
21
, Proposition 3.10]
).

Let
\{u_{D,n}\}\subset H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega))
be a sequence such that

\begin{gathered}u_{D,n}\rightharpoonup u_{D}\;\text{ in }H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)),\quad u_{D,n}\rightarrow u_{D}\;\text{ in }L^{2}(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)),\\
u_{D,n}(T)\rightarrow u_{D}(T)\;\text{ in }\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)\end{gathered}

(17)

and denote the (unique) reduced solution associated with
u_{D,n}
by
\sigma_{n}
.
Then
\sigma_{n}\rightharpoonup\sigma
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
, where
\sigma
is the reduced solution w.r.t.
u_{D}
.

Lemma 18
.

There is a constant
C>0
, independent of
u_{D}
, such that every weak solution
w.r.t.
u_{D}
fulfills

\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\|\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}(t)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{BD}(\Omega)}\,\textup{d}t\Big)^{1/2}\leq C\,\|u_{D}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega))}\big(1+\|u_{D}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega))}\big).

Proof.

Let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
with
\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq 1
and
i,j\in\{1,...,n\}
be arbitrary.
According to (
3
), the test function

(\tau_{\varphi})_{ij}=(\tau_{\varphi})_{ji}:=-\frac{\varrho}{\sqrt{2}}\,\varphi,\quad(\tau_{\varphi})_{kl}=0\quad\forall\,(k,l)\notin\{(i,j),(j,i)\}

is admissible for (
7c
). Using
\operatorname{div}\sigma=0
, we deduce

\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,\textup{d}(\nabla^{s}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u})_{ij}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\varrho}\Big(\int_{\Omega}\nabla^{s}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{D}:\sigma\,\textup{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{A}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}:(\tau_{\varphi}-\sigma)\,\textup{d}x\Big)

and consequently, since
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
with
\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq 1

was arbitrary,

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{s}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}\|_{L^{2}_{w}(\mathfrak{M}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\textup{sym}}))}

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\begin{aligned} &\|u_{D}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega))}\,\|\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}\\
&+\|\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}\|\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}\big)\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\leq C\,\|u_{D}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega))}\big(1+\|u_{D}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega))}\big),

where we used
lemma

16
.

Since
\Gamma_{D}
is assumed to have a nonempty relative interior, there is a set
\Lambda\subset\Gamma_{D}

and a constant
\delta>0
such that
\Lambda
has positive boundary measure and
\operatorname{dist}(\Lambda,\partial\Gamma_{D})\geq\delta
.
By
[
31
, Chap. II, Theorem 2.1]
,
\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}(t)
admits a trace in
\mathbf{L}^{1}(\Gamma)
for almost all
t\in(0,T)
.
In the following, we neglect the variable
t
for the sake of readability.
The restriction of this trace to
\Lambda
is denoted by
\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}|_{\Lambda}
.
We extend
\operatorname{sign}(\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}|_{\Lambda})
(where the sign is to be understood componentwise) to the whole boundary
\Gamma

by zero and apply convolution with a smoothing kernel to obtain a sequence of functions
\{\varphi_{n}\}\subset C^{\infty}(\Gamma;\mathbb{R}^{n})

with
\operatorname{supp}(\varphi_{n})\subset\Gamma_{D}
(thanks to
\operatorname{dist}(\Lambda,\partial\Gamma_{D})\geq\delta
)
and
\|\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq 1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Given these functions, let us define

(\tau_{n})_{ij}=\frac{\varrho}{\sqrt{2}}\,\mathfrak{E}(\varphi_{n,i}\,\nu_{j}+\varphi_{n,j}\,\nu_{i}),

where
\mathfrak{E}
denoted the harmonic extension and
\nu
is the outward normal.
Then, (
1
) implies
\|\tau_{n}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\varrho
and, since in addition

\tau_{n}
vanishes on
\Gamma_{N}
by construction, we have
\tau_{n}\in\Sigma(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{K}(\Omega)
.
Note that, by the mapping properties of
\mathfrak{E}
,
\tau_{n}\in\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\Sigma(\Omega)
.
If we insert this as test function in (
7c
) and apply again the integration by parts from

9
,
then
\operatorname{div}\sigma=0
and (
12
) imply

\int_{\Gamma_{D}}\varphi_{n}\cdot\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}\,\textup{d}s\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\varrho}\Big(\int_{\Omega}\tau_{n}:\textup{d}\nabla^{s}(\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u})-\int_{\Omega}\nabla^{s}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{D}:\sigma\,\textup{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{A}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}:(\tau_{n}-\sigma)\,\textup{d}x\Big).

Now, since
\varphi_{n}\to\operatorname{sign}(\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u})
a.e. in
\Lambda

, Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem along
with our previous estimate gives

\displaystyle\|\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbf{L}^{1}(\Lambda))}\leq C\,\|u_{D}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega))}\big(1+\|u_{D}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega))}\big).

Thanks to
[
31
, Chap. II, Proposition 2.4]

Remark 19
.

A priori estimates for quasistatic evolutions (which is an equivalent notion of solution as mentioned above)
are already proven in
[

in a slightly different setting.

Lemma 20
.

Let
\{u_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{U}
be a sequence such that, for all

,

u_{n}(0)=u_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{0}^{T}\|\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{n}(t)\|_{\mathrm{BD}(\Omega)}^{2}\,\textup{d}t\leq C

with a constant

as defined in (

Owing to (

, is the dual of

Thus, there exists a subsequence such that

(19)

Due to
\mathrm{BD}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathbf{L}^{\frac{n}{n-1}}(\Omega)

\{\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{n_{k}}\}

and, since all

\{u_{n_{k}}\}
is bounded in

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\langle\nabla^{s}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{n_{k}}(t),\tau\rangle\varphi(t)\,\textup{d}t

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{n_{k}}(t)\cdot\operatorname{div}\tau\,\textup{d}x\,\varphi(t)\,\textup{d}t

a.e. in
(0,T)

Then, there is a subsequence of weak solutions

\{u_{D,n_{k}}\}

and the weak limit
(u,\sigma)
is a weak solution associated with the limit

Proof.

Since we already know that the stress component of every weak solution is also a reduced one and the latter is unique

Owing to

\{\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{n}\}

It remains to prove that

t\in[0,T]
.
Then, the flow rule inequality for
(u_{n_{k}},\sigma_{n_{k}})
along with
\operatorname{div}\sigma_{n_{k}}=0

and the (weak) convergences of
u_{D,n_{k}}
,
u_{n_{k}}
, and
\sigma_{n_{k}}
yields

\displaystyle\liminf_{k\to\infty}\left(\mathbb{A}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}_{n_{k}},\sigma_{n_{k}}\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}

(21)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{k\to\infty}\begin{aligned} \Big[&\left(\mathbb{A}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}_{n_{k}}-\nabla^{s}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{D,n_{k}},\tau\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}\\
&+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{n_{k}}-\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{D,n_{k}})\operatorname{div}\tau\,\textup{d}x\textup{d}t-\left(\nabla^{s}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{D,n_{k}},\sigma_{n_{k}}\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}\Big]\end{aligned}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbb{A}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}-\nabla^{s}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{D},\tau\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}-\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{D})\operatorname{div}\tau\,\textup{d}x\textup{d}t-\left(\nabla^{s}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}_{D},\sigma\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}

where we used Lemma 3.9 in our companion paper
[
21
]
for the convergence of the last term.
On the other hand, the weak lower semicontinuity of
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)_{\mathbb{A}}}
together with

H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))\hookrightarrow C(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
gives

\displaystyle\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}\left(\mathbb{A}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}_{n_{k}},\sigma_{n_{k}}\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}

(22)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\sigma_{n_{k}}(T)\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)_{\mathbb{A}}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|\sigma_{0}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)_{\mathbb{A}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|\sigma(T)\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)_{\mathbb{A}}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|\sigma_{0}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)_{\mathbb{A}}}^{2}=\left(\mathbb{A}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma},\sigma\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}.

Together with (
21
) and
\operatorname{div}\sigma=0
, this implies the flow rule inequality for the weak limit.

Given these boundedness and continuity results, we can now establish the existence of at least one optimal solution.
Before we do so, let us recall our optimization problem and state it in a rigorous manner:

\left\{\,\begin{aligned} \min\text{ \ \ }&J(u,u_{D}):=\Psi(u)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\|u_{D}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\\
\text{s.t.}\text{ \ \ }&u_{D}\in H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega)),\quad(u,\sigma)\in\mathcal{U}\times H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)),\\
&(u,\sigma)\text{ is a weak solution w.r.t.\ }u_{D},\quad\text{and}\quad u_{D}(0)-u_{0}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega),\end{aligned}\right.

(P)

where
\Psi:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
is bounded from below and
lower semicontinous w.r.t. weak convergence in
\mathcal{U}
as defined in (
5
), i.e.,

u_{n}\rightharpoonup u\text{ in }\mathcal{U}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Psi(u_{n})\geq\Psi(u).

(23)

An example for such a functional
\Psi
will be given in
section

6
below.

Theorem 22
(Existence of optimal solutions).

There exists a globally optimal solution of
eq.

P
.

Proof.

Based on our above findings,
the assertion immediately follows from the standard direct method of calculus of variations.
Nevertheless, let us shortly sketch the arguments.
First, we observe that the triple
(u,\sigma,u_{D})\equiv(u_{0},\sigma_{0},u_{0})
(constant in time)
satisfies the constraints in (
P
)
so that the feasible set is nonempty. (At this point, we need the additional regularity
u_{0}\in\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega)
.)
Let
(u_{n},\sigma_{n},u_{D,n})
be a minimizing sequence.
Then either
(u_{0},u_{0})
is already optimal or
J(u_{n},u_{D,n})\leq J(u_{0},u_{0})<\infty
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large.
Thus, since
\Psi
is bounded from below,
\{u_{D,n}\}
is bounded in
H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega))
.
Via continuous and compact embedding, there is thus a subsequence satisfying (
17
).
Clearly, the associated limit satisfies the conditions on the initial value in (
P
).
Moreover, according to
proposition

21
, a subsequence of weak solutions converges weakly in

\mathcal{U}\times H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
to a weak solution. Thus the weak limit is feasible
and the weak lower semicontinuity of norms and of
\Psi
implies its optimality.

Remark 23
(More general objectives).

The proof of existence readily transfers to slightly more general objectives than the one in (
P
).
For instance, one could add a term of the form
\Phi(\sigma)
with a function
\Phi:H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))\to\mathbb{R}
, which
weakly lower semicontinuous and bounded from below. Since objectives of this form have already been discussed in
the companion paper, we restrict ourselves to objectives just depending on
u
in order to keep the discussion concise.
Moreover, one could use other Tikhonov terms different from the
H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega))
-norm to ensure
the convergence properties in (
17
) required for
proposition

21
.
For example, thanks to the Aubin-Lions lemma, a Tikhonov term of the form

\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\Big(\|u_{D}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}+\|u_{D}\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\Big)

with any Banach space
X
embedding compactly in
\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)
(such as e.g.
\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega)
)
is sufficient to guarantee (
17
) for (a subsequence of) a minimizing sequence.
However, in order to shorten presentation, we just consider the
H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega))
-norm.

5
Yosida Regularization and Reverse Approximation

As already mentioned above, the ultimate goal of our analysis is to establish conditions that guarantee
that optimal solutions to the optimization problem (
P
) governed by perfect plasticity
can be approximated via Yosida regularization.
The most crucial point in this respect is the so-called
reverse approximation
, which
essentially means to construct a
recovery sequence
for a given perfect plastic solution.
This is a rather challenging task, as
example

13
illustrates: one easily verifies that every sequence of
regularized solutions tends to the linear solution
u(t,x)=2\,t\,x
for regularization parameter tending to zero,
although there are infinitely many other solutions.
There is thus no hope that every perfect plastic
solution can be approximated via Yosida regularization!
However, when it comes to optimization, there is not only the state (i.e., the solution of the perfect plasticity system),
but also the
control
variables, which can be used to construct a recovery sequence.
Unfortunately, the Dirichlet data
u_{D}
, which serve as control variables in our case, are not sufficient for this purpose.
Instead we need a set of control variables that is rich enough to generate a sufficiently large set of regularized solutions.
For this purpose, we introduce an
additional control variable in form of distributed loads
and end up with
the following regularized version of the state equation:

\displaystyle-\operatorname{div}\sigma_{\lambda}(t)

\displaystyle=\ell(t)

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathbf{H}^{-1}_{D}(\Omega),

(24a)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\lambda}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}(\nabla^{s}u_{\lambda}(t)-z_{\lambda}(t))

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega),

(24b)

\displaystyle\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{z}_{\lambda}(t)

\displaystyle=\partial I_{\lambda}(\sigma_{\lambda}(t))

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega),

(24c)

\displaystyle u_{\lambda}(t)-u_{D}(t)

\displaystyle\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega),

(24d)

\displaystyle(u_{\lambda},\sigma_{\lambda})(0)

\displaystyle=(u_{0},\sigma_{0})

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)\times\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega).

(24e)

where
\lambda>0
is the regularization parameter,
I_{\lambda}
is the Yosida regularization of the
indicator functional, see (
4
), and
\ell\in H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{-1}_{D}(\Omega))
is the mentioned load.
Existence and uniqueness of a solution to the regularized state equation
eq.

24

follows from Banach’s fixed point theorem and can be proven by a reduction of the system to an equation in the variable
z
only,
cf. e.g.
[
21
, Proposition 3.15]
. This gives rise to the following

Lemma 24
(Existence of solutions to the regularized state system,
[
21
, Corollary 3.16]
).

For every
\lambda>0
,
\ell\in H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{-1}_{D}(\Omega))
, and
u_{D}\in H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega))

with
\ell(0)=0
and
u_{D}(0)|_{\Gamma_{D}}=u_{0}|_{\Gamma_{D}}
,
there exists a unique solution

(u_{\lambda},\sigma_{\lambda},z_{\lambda})\in H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega))\times H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))\times H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))

of
eq.

24
.

The associated solution operator is globally Lipschitz continuous with a Lipschitz constant proportional to
\lambda^{-1}
.

The proof of existence is a direct consequence of the Lipschitz continuity of
\partial I_{\lambda}
and Banach’s
contraction principle.
In
[
21
]
, the external loads are set to zero, but it is straightforward to incorporate them into the
existence theory.
The Lipschitz continuity of the solution mapping
directly follows from the Lipschitz estimate for the Yosida approximation, see e.g.
[
3
, Proposition 55.2(b)]
.

Before we address the approximation properties of this regularization approach
and its convergence behavior for
\lambda
tending to zero in
section

6
below,
see
proposition

31
, we first lay the foundations for the construction of a recovery sequence
in the upcoming three lemmas.
Unfortunately, as already indicated in the introduction, the passage to the limit in the regularized state equation
in
proposition

31
below requires a rather high regularity of the stress field, and the recovery sequence
has to fulfill this regularity, too, as it is a constraint in the regularized optimal control problem (
Pλ
).
The key issue for our reverse approximation argument is therefore to
improve the regularity of the stress field provided a displacement field with higher regularity is given.
To this end, we first need an auxiliary result on the derivative of the Yosida regularization.
Since the set of admissible stresses admits a pointwise representation by the set
K
,
the Fréchet-derivative of the Yosida regularization does the same, i.e., given an arbitrary
\tau\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, it holds

\partial I_{\lambda}(\tau)(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\big[\tau(x)-\pi_{K}(\tau(x))\big]\quad\text{f.a.a.\ }x\in\Omega,

(25)

where
\pi_{K}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\textup{sym}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\textup{sym}}
is the projection on
K
.
This pointwise representation allows to derive the following

Lemma 25
.

Let
\lambda>0
,
p>2
, and
\tau\in\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)
be arbitrary.
Then
\partial I_{\lambda}(\tau)\in\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)
and there holds

\displaystyle\|\partial I_{\lambda}(\tau)\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\,\|\tau\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)}

(26)

and

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{i}(\partial I_{\lambda}(\tau))\colon\partial_{i}\tau\big)(x)

\displaystyle\geq 0\quad\text{a.e.\ in }\Omega,\quad\forall\,i=1,...,n.

(27)

Proof.

As a projection,
\pi_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\textup{sym}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\textup{sym}}
is globally Lipschitz continuous.
Thus, the chain rule for Sobolev functions (see e.g.
[
36
, Thm 2.1.11]
) implies that

\partial I_{\lambda}(\tau)\in\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)
with

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{m}}[\partial I_{\lambda}(\tau)]_{ij}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\Big(\frac{\partial\tau_{ij}}{\partial x_{m}}-\sum_{kl}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau_{kl}}[\pi_{K}(\tau)]_{ij}\frac{\partial\tau_{kl}}{\partial x_{m}}\Big).

(28)

Since the Lipschitz constant of the projection equals one, its directional derivative clearly satisfies

|\pi_{K}^{\prime}(A;B)|_{F}\leq|B|_{F}
for all
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\textup{sym}}
and, consequently,

\displaystyle\partial_{m}(\partial I_{\lambda}(\tau))\colon\partial_{m}\tau

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\big(|\partial_{m}\tau|_{F}^{2}-\pi_{K}^{\prime}(\tau;\partial_{m}\tau):\partial_{m}\tau\big)\geq 0,

which is (
27
). It is moreover easily seen that
\operatorname{Id}-\pi_{K}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\textup{sym}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\textup{sym}}
is
globally Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant 1, too. Thus, for every
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\textup{sym}}
, there holds
|(\operatorname{Id}-\pi_{K})^{\prime}(A;B)|_{F}\leq|B|_{F}
.
Since
(\operatorname{Id}-\pi_{k})(0)=0
, the Lipschitz continuity moreover entails
|(\operatorname{Id}-\pi_{k})(A)|_{F}\leq|A|_{F}
for all
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\textup{sym}}
.
In view of (
28
), this yields (
26
).

The next lemma addresses the crucial regularity result for the stress field
\sigma_{\lambda}
as solution of

w-\mathbb{A}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}_{\lambda}=\partial I_{\lambda}(\sigma_{\lambda}),\text{ \ \ \ \ }\sigma_{\lambda}(0)=\sigma_{0}.

(29)

In the proof of our main result in
theorem

32
, an optimal strain rate will play the role of
w
and the
following regularity result will be essential for the construction of a recovery sequence associated with that strain rate.
The required regularity of
w
will carry over to this optimal strain rate and represents the most restrictive
assumption of our reverse approximation approach.

Lemma 26
(Higher regularity of the stress field).

Let
\lambda>0
be arbitrary and
w\in L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))
with
p\geq 2
be given.
Then (
29
)
admits a unique solution
\sigma_{\lambda}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))
and there holds

\|\sigma_{\lambda}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))}\leq C_{p}\Big(\|w\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))}+\|\sigma_{0}\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)}^{p}\Big)

(30)

with
C_{p}:=p\,\|\mathbb{A}\|^{p/2-1}
.

Proof.

Step 1. Existence of solutions in
H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
:

First we note that (
29
) is just an ODE in
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
and
\partial I_{\lambda}
is globally Lipschitz in
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
.
Thus, the existence and uniqueness of solutions in
H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
follows from the generalized Picard-Lindelöf theorem in Banach spaces.
However, a pointwise projection is in general not Lipschitz continuous in Sobolev spaces.
Therefore, we cannot apply this simple argument to show that the solution is an element of
W^{1,1}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))
.

Step 2. Higher regularity in case of smooth data:

To prove this, we apply a time discretization scheme, namely the explicit Euler method.
At first we consider the case
w\in C(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))
.
For
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
n\in\{0,...,N\}
, we set
d_{t}^{N}:=\frac{T}{N}
and

t_{n}^{N}:=nd_{t}^{N}
such that
0=t_{0}^{N}<t_{1}^{N}<...<t_{N}^{N}=T
.
Now define
\sigma_{0}^{N}:=\sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)
and

\sigma^{N}_{n}:=\sigma^{N}_{n-1}+d_{t}^{N}\mathbb{C}\big(w(t_{n-1}^{N})-\partial I_{\lambda}(\sigma^{N}_{n-1})\big)\in\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)\quad\text{(by \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{lem:yosida})}

such that

\mathbb{A}\frac{\sigma^{N}_{n}-\sigma^{N}_{n-1}}{d_{t}^{N}}+\partial I_{\lambda}(\sigma^{N}_{n-1})=w(t_{n-1}^{N})

(31)

for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
n\in\{1,...,N\}
.
We define the piecewise linear approximation
\sigma^{N}\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))
by

\sigma^{N}(t):=\sigma_{n-1}^{N}+\frac{t-t_{n-1}^{N}}{d_{t}^{N}}(\sigma_{n}^{N}-\sigma_{n-1}^{N})

and the piecewise constant approximation

\tilde{\sigma}^{N}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))
by

\tilde{\sigma}^{N}(t):=\sigma_{n-1}^{N}
for
t\in[t_{n-1}^{N},t_{n}^{N})
.
Using (
26
), we deduce from (
31
) that

\|\sigma^{N}_{n}\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\leq\|\sigma_{0}\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)}+d_{t}^{N}C\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\|\sigma^{N}_{i}\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\Big)+C\|w\|_{C(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)},

which, together with the discrete Gronwall lemma (cf.
[
16
, Lemma 5.1 and the following remark]
), shows that

\sigma^{N}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))
by a constant independent of
d_{t}^{N}
.
Thus, again owing to
eq.

31
and (
26
),

\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}^{N}(t)=\frac{\sigma_{n}^{N}-\sigma_{n-1}^{N}}{d_{t}^{N}}
,

t\in(t_{n-1}^{N},t_{n}^{N})
is also bounded in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))
. Therefore,
\sigma^{N}
is bounded in
H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
and
consequently, there is a weakly converging subsequence, for simplicity also denoted by
\sigma^{N}
, such that
\sigma^{N}\rightharpoonup\sigma
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))

and
\sigma^{N}\rightharpoonup^{*}\sigma
in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))
as
N\to\infty
.
Note that, due to the reflexivity of
\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)
,
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))
can be identified
with the dual of
L^{1}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega))
so there is a weakly-
\ast
converging subsequence.
It remains to show that
\sigma
solves (
29
).
Since
\sigma^{N}
is bounded in
W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))
as seen above,
we have by compact embeddings that
\sigma^{N}\to\sigma
in
C(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
.
Thus, we find for the piecewise constant interpolation that, for every
t\in[t_{n-1}^{N},t_{n}^{N})
,

\|\tilde{\sigma}^{N}(t)-\sigma(t)\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|\sigma^{N}(t_{n-1}^{N})-\sigma(t)\|_{\mathbb{L}}^{2}(\Omega)\to 0\quad\text{as }N\to\infty.

Therefore, (
31
) and the Lipschitz continuity of
\partial I_{\lambda}
in
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
give

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{A}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}^{N}+\partial I_{\lambda}(\sigma^{N})-w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbb{A}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}^{N}+\partial I_{\lambda}(\tilde{\sigma}^{N})-\tilde{w}^{N}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\partial I_{\lambda}(\sigma^{N})-\partial I_{\lambda}(\tilde{\sigma}^{N})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\tilde{w}^{N}-w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\|\sigma^{N}-\tilde{\sigma}^{N}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\tilde{w}^{N}-w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as }N\to\infty,

where
\tilde{w}^{N}
denotes the piecewise constant interpolation of
w
, which converges strongly in
C(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
to
w

thanks to the assumed regularity of
w
. Therefore, by the weak lower semicontinuity of the
L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
-norm, we see
that the limit satisfies (
29
).

Step 3. Higher regularity for nonsmooth data:

Let now
w\in L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))
be arbitrary and take a sequence
\{w_{n}\}\subset C(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))

such that
w_{n}\rightarrow w
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))
.
Let
\sigma_{\lambda}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
be the solution of (
29
) and denote by

\sigma_{\lambda,n}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))
the solution of

\displaystyle w_{n}-\mathbb{A}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}_{\lambda,n}=\partial I_{\lambda}(\sigma_{\lambda,n}),\text{ \ \ \ \ }\sigma_{\lambda,n}(0)=\sigma_{0}.

(32)

Since
\partial I_{\lambda}:\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
is
monotone, one obtains
\sigma_{\lambda,n}\rightarrow\sigma_{\lambda}
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
by standard arguments.
Moreover, (
32
) holds almost everywhere in time and space and so that, f.a.a.
t\in[0,T]
,

\partial_{j}w_{n}(t)-\mathbb{A}\partial_{j}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}_{\lambda,n}(t)=\partial_{j}\partial I_{\lambda}(\sigma_{\lambda,n})(t)\quad\text{a.e.\ in }\Omega.

follows. Testing this equation with

((\mathbb{A}\partial_{j}\sigma_{\lambda,n}:\partial_{j}\sigma_{\lambda,n})^{p/2-1}\partial_{j}\sigma_{\lambda,n})(t)\in\mathbb{W}^{1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega)

and using (
27
) leads to

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}(\mathbb{A}\partial_{j}\sigma_{\lambda,n}:\partial_{j}\sigma_{\lambda,n})^{p/2}\textup{d}x

(33)

\displaystyle\leq p\int_{\Omega}(\mathbb{A}\partial_{j}\sigma_{\lambda,n}:\partial_{j}\sigma_{\lambda,n})^{p/2-1}\big(\mathbb{A}\partial_{j}\sigma_{\lambda,n}:\partial_{j}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}_{\lambda,n}+\partial_{j}\partial I_{\lambda}(\sigma_{\lambda,n}):\partial_{j}\sigma_{\lambda,n}\big)\textup{d}x

\displaystyle=p\int_{\Omega}(\mathbb{A}\partial_{j}\sigma_{\lambda,n}:\partial_{j}\sigma_{\lambda,n})^{p/2-1}\,\partial_{j}w_{n}:\partial\sigma_{n}\,\textup{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\,\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{j}w|_{F}\,|\partial_{j}\sigma_{\lambda,n}|_{F}^{p-1}\,\textup{d}x\leq C_{p}\,\|w\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\,\|\sigma_{\lambda,n}\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)}^{p-1},

with
C_{p}
as defined in the statement of the lemma.
Integrating this inequality in time and taking the coercivity of
\mathbb{A}
into account gives

\|\sigma_{\lambda,n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))}\leq C_{p}\Big(\|w_{n}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))}+\|\sigma_{0}\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)}^{p}\Big).

Therefore,
\sigma_{\lambda,n}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))
and we can select a weakly-
\ast
converging
subsequence. The uniqueness of the weak limit then gives
\sigma\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))
as claimed.
The estimate in (
30
) finally follows from the above inequality and the lower semicontinuity
of the norm w.r.t. weak-
\ast
convergence.

Remark 27
.

We observe that (
26
), (
29
), and the proven regularity of
\sigma_{\lambda}

even imply that
\sigma_{\lambda}\in W^{1,1}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))
. However, we do not obtain an estimate independent
of
\lambda
in this norm (in contrast to (
30
)) and therefore, this additional regularity is not useful for us.

Lemma 28
(
[
20
, Section 3]
).

Let
w\in L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
be given and
\lambda\searrow 0
. Then
\sigma_{\lambda}\rightarrow\sigma
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
,
where
\sigma
is the solution of

w-\mathbb{A}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}\in\partial I_{\mathcal{K}(\Omega)}(\sigma),\text{ \ \ \ \ }\sigma(0)=\sigma_{0}.

(34)

Moreover, there holds

\|\sigma_{\lambda}-\sigma\|_{C(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\leq\lambda\,\frac{\|\mathbb{C}\|^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathbb{C}}}\,\|w-\mathbb{A}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\sigma}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}^{2},

(35)

where
\gamma_{\mathbb{C}}>0
is the coercivity constant of
\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

The assertion is proven in
[
20
]
, but, for convenience of the reader, we shortly sketch the arguments.
First, observe that
\sigma_{\lambda}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
and
\sigma\in H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
solve
(
29
) and (
34
), respectively, if and only if

z_{\lambda}:=W-\mathbb{A}\sigma_{\lambda}
and
z:=W-\mathbb{A}\sigma

with
W(t):=\int_{0}^{t}w(s)\textup{d}s
solve

6
Convergence of Minimizers

We are now in the position to state the regularized optimal control problems.
Beside the additional control variable

they differ from (

is needed to improve the regularity of the stress in order to pass to the limit in the regularized state equation,

This additional regularity of the stresses is unfortunately not enough to pass to the limit in the state system.
We additionally need to bound the displacement in

itself, unless the loads fulfill a safe load condition. This however cannot be ensured for the loads arising
in the construction of the recovery sequence in the proof of our main

(at least, we were not able to verify it). Therefore, we directly enforce this boundedness by a special choice of the
objective functional as a tracking type objective of the following form:

One could even allow for less regular desired strain rates (in the space of measures), but for convenience,
we restrict to functions in

The regularized counterpart of (

\left\{\;\begin{aligned} \min\quad&J_{\lambda}(u,u_{D},\ell):=\|\nabla^{s}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}-\mu\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}+\|\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}-v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbf{L}^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}\\[-2.15277pt]

Remark 33
(Crucial regularity assumption).

The assumption of existence of a global minimizer
(\overline{u},\overline{\sigma},\overline{u}_{D})

with the properties listed in
theorem

32
is admittedly very restrictive. Notice in particular that the regularity assumptions
on
\overline{u}
imply that
(\overline{u},\overline{\sigma})
is a
strong solution
w.r.t.
\overline{u}_{D}
,
whose existence can in general not be guaranteed.
The regularity assumption however seems to be indispensable, as the above proof demonstrates:
In order to pass to the limit in the flow rule inequality to show the feasibility of an accumulation point in step 2 of the proof,
we need the additional regularity of the stress ensured by the inequality constraint in (
Pλ
).
The generic regularity of the stress, which is
H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
(see
lemma

16
),
is by far not sufficient for this passage to the limit. It therefore appears to be unavoidable to enforce the required regularity
by additional inequality constraints in (
Pλ
). The elements of the recovery sequence however have to be feasible
for (
Pλ
) and thus have to fulfill this inequality constraint, too.
As the generic regularity of the stress is
H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
, it is not possible to guarantee this constraint to be fulfilled
without further hypotheses on the recovery sequence and its limit, respectively. At the end, this leads to the regularity assumption
on
\nabla^{s}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{\overline{u}}
.

We however emphasize that we do not require the existence of a strong
solution with the addition regularity of the strain rate
for every Dirichlet displacement
u_{D}\in H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega))
(which would really be unrealistic),
but only for one optimal
\overline{u}_{D}
.
(Of course, there might be many optimal solutions, since (
P
) is a non-convex problem).
Whether an optimal solution fulfilling these regularity assumptions exists or not, clearly depends on the data,
especially on the smoothness of the desired strain rate
\mu
in (
38
).

Remark 34
(Extensions and modifications of the approximation result).

(i)

One essential drawback of the approximation result is that the bound
R
given in (
43
)
depends on the unknown solution
(\overline{u},\overline{u}_{D})
and is therefore in general unknown, too.
One could replace the inequality constraints on the stress involving this bound in (
Pλ
)
by an additional tracking term in the objective of the form

\|\sigma-\sigma_{d}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\|\sigma-\sigma_{d}\|_{X}
with a given
desired stress distribution
\sigma_{d}
and a reflexive Banach space
X
with the following properties:
On the one hand,
H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))\cap X
should compactly embed in
L^{2}(C(\bar{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\textup{sym}}))
.
On the other hand,
H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{W}^{1,p}(\Omega))
should compactly be embedded in
X
.
Provided these embeddings hold, the steps 2 and 3 of the previous proof can easily be adapted.
At this point, one benefits from the strong convergence of the recovery sequence in
H^{1}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
by
lemma

28
.

(ii)

The above analysis is restricted to objectives of the type (
38
) or other types
of objectives ensuring the boundedness of
\{\overline{u}_{\lambda}\}
in
\mathcal{U}
. This bound cannot be deduced
from the regularized plasticity system in (
24
) unless the loads fulfill a

safe load condition
, see
[
30
]
. One could thus allow for more general objectives, if
a safe load condition would be included in the set of constraints in (
Pλ
).
We were however not able to find a safe load condition that is satisfied by the loads associated with the recovery sequence.
This is due to several reasons, among these a lack of regularity of the recovery sequence. This issue is subject to future research.

(iii)

By contrast, it is well possible to consider objectives, which give the boundedness of
the displacement in more regular spaces such as
H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega))
. In this case, the inequality constraints on the stress in
(
Pλ
) can be weakened or even be completely left out, since the higher regularity of the displacement
enables the passage to the limit at the end of the proof of
lemma

28
. Such a setting is
treated in
[
35
]
.

(iv)

We have chosen the space
H^{1}(\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega))
as the
control space for the Dirichlet displacement in order to guarantee the compact embeddings in step 2 of the above proof
and in the proof of
theorem

22
.
Of course, one might want to avoid the
\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega)
-norm in the objective, which
could be achieved by an additional (pseudo-)force-to-Dirichlet-map, for example by solving an additional
linear elasticity system. This strategy was employed in
[
21
, Subsection 6.1]
.

Remark 35
(Numerical treatment of (
Pλ
)).

Although they are still nonsmooth optimization problems,
the regularized problems in (
Pλ
) offer ample possibilities for a numerical treatment.
A popular strategy is to further regularize the problem by smoothing the Yosida approximation
\partial I_{\lambda}
.
This has been used for the numerical computations in the companion paper
[
21
]
. Moreover,
the non-smooth objective in (
Pλ
) calls for an additional regularization of the
L^{1}
-norms
for instance in terms of a Huber-regularization. In this way, one ends up with a smooth optimal control problem, which
can be treated by the classical adjoint approach. Our convergence result in
theorem

32
implies that,
under the certainly restrictive assumptions of this theorem, there is an
optimal solution of the original optimization problem governed by the perfect plasticity system that
can be approximated by this procedure.

Appendix A
Auxiliary results

Lemma 36
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain and

X
be a Banach space and
A\subset X
be a convex and closed set with
0\in A^{\circ}
.
Set
\mathcal{A}(\Omega):=\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega;X):v\in A\text{ a.e.~in }\Omega\}
. Then

C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega;X)\cap\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
is dense in
\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Let
v\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be arbitrary. By assumption there exists
\delta>0
such that

\overline{B_{X}(0,\delta)}\subset A
. We set
\overline{v}:=(1-\varepsilon)v

and select a sequence
\{v_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega;X)
such that

\|v_{n}-\overline{v}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;X)}^{2}\leq\frac{\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{3}}{4n}\quad\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N}.

(49)

We moreover define

\displaystyle S_{n}^{c}:=\{x\in\Omega:v_{n}(x)\in X\setminus A^{\circ}\},\quad S_{n}^{o}:=\{x\in\Omega:v_{n}(x)\in X\setminus(1-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2})A\}.

Hence,
S_{n}^{c}\subset S_{n}^{o}
and, by continuity and compact support of
v_{n}
,

S_{n}^{c}
is compact, while
S_{n}^{o}
is open. Thus, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
there is a function
\varphi_{n}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};[0,1])
with
\varphi_{n}\equiv 1
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus S_{n}^{o}
and

\varphi_{n}\equiv 0
in
S_{n}^{c}
. Furthermore, if

\|v_{n}(x)-\overline{v}(x)\|_{X}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\delta
, then the convexity of
A
and

\overline{B_{X}(0,\delta)}\subset A
imply

\displaystyle\frac{v_{n}(x)}{1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\,v(x)+\Big(1-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\Big)\,\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\,\big(v_{n}(x)-\overline{v}(x)\big)\in A

Therefore, we obtain by contraposition that

\displaystyle\|v_{n}-\overline{v}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;X)}^{2}\geq\int_{S_{n}^{o}}\|v_{n}-\overline{v}\|_{X}^{2}\textup{d}x\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\,\delta^{2}\,|S_{n}^{o}|

so that (
49
) yields
|S_{n}^{c}|\leq|S_{n}^{o}|\leq\varepsilon/n
. Thus,
due to Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem, there exists

N=N(\varepsilon)\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\|\overline{v}\|_{L^{2}(S_{N}^{o};X)}\leq\|v\|_{L^{2}(S_{N}^{o};X)}\leq\varepsilon.

(50)

Now we define
v_{s}:=\varphi_{N}\,v_{N}
. Then, by construction

v_{s}\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)\cap C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega;X)
and, in addition,
(
49
) and (
50
) imply

\displaystyle\|v-v_{s}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;X)}

\displaystyle\leq\|v-\overline{v}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;X)}+\|\overline{v}-v_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;X)}+\|v_{N}-v_{s}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;X)}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;X)}+\|\overline{v}-v_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;X)}+\|v_{N}\|_{L^{2}(S_{N}^{o};X)}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;X)}+2\,\|\overline{v}-v_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;X)}+\|\overline{v}\|_{L^{2}(S_{N}^{o};X)}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\Big(\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;X)}+\frac{\delta\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{N}}+1\Big).

Since
\varepsilon
was arbitrary, this finishes the proof.
