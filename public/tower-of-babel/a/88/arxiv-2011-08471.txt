Abstract
. It is well known that two elliptic curves are isogenous if and only if they have same number of rational points. In fact, isogenous curves can even have isomorphic groups of rational points in certain cases. In this paper, we consolidate all the current literature on this relationship and give a extensive classification of the conditions in which this relationship arises. First we prove two ordinary isogenous elliptic curves have isomorphic groups of rational points when they have the same j j -invariant. Then, we extend this result to certain isogenous supersingular elliptic curves, namely those with equal j j -invariant of either 0 or 1728, using Vlăduţ’s characterization of the group structure of rational points. We finally introduce and use a method by Heuberger and Mazzoli to establish a general case of this relationship within isogenous elliptic curves not necessarily having equal j j -invariant, and discuss the required parameters for their method.

1.
Introduction

Elliptic curves have long been a popular subject of interest in number theory due to their rich algebraic structure and their unique morphisms known as isogenies. In 1966, Sato and Tate proved that two elliptic curves defined over the same field are isogenous if and only if they have the same number of rational points in
[
13
]
. This theorem offers a helpful tool to relate the study of multiple elliptic curves’ algebraic structures to that of the isogenies between them.

The group structure of the rational points of an elliptic curve over a finite field is a central area of study of the algebraic structure of these objects as abelian varieties. In 1999, Vlăduţ in
[
15
]
describes the situations in which these elliptic curves have a cyclic group of rational points based on its trace of Frobenius and the finite field over which the elliptic curve is defined. In 2001, Wittmann determines the possible group structures of
E(\mathbb{F}_{q^{r}})
for all elliptic curves defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with isomorphic group of rational points. We will combine these two results to characterize the differences in the group structure of rational points of an elliptic curve based on the field that elliptic curve is defined over. Specifically, for an elliptic curve defined over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}
, we will show which different structures
E(\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}})
can have depending on whether
r
is odd or even. This allows us to link the group structures of
E(\mathbb{F}_{q^{r}})
and
E(\mathbb{F}_{q^{s}})
when
r
and
s
have different parity. In this context, we also condense Vlăduţ’s work to analyze the occurrence of both cyclic and non-cyclic groups of rational points.

With this classification of these groups of rational points at hand, we then study the situations in which two isogenous curves have isomorphic groups of rational points. We first give a proof that two elliptic curves with the same
j
-invariant are isogenous if and only if they have isomorphic groups of rational points in the ordinary case. We then extend this statement to isogenous supersingular elliptic curves when their
j
-invariants are either 0 or 1728. Finally, we give a brief introduction to the tool introduced by Heuberger and Mazzoli in
[
6
]
to decide whether two elliptic curves have isomorphic groups of rational points if their cardinalities are same. This method does not require two elliptic curves to have same
j
-invariant, and thus it can be used to classify groups of rational points in the more general case. However, their method requires using additional parameters. Here we analyze the utility of certain parameters and their consequences.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Elliptic Curves

We introduce some elementary features of elliptic curves. Let
K
be a field and
\overline{K}
be its algebraic closure. An elliptic curve
E
is an algebraic curve defined by a minimal Weierstrass equation

\displaystyle y^{2}+a_{1}xy+a_{3}y=x^{3}+a_{2}x^{2}+a_{4}x+a_{6}

where
a_{i}\in K
. In this paper, fields will be assumed to have characteristic not 2 or 3, and in this case we can use the Weierstrass normal form given by

\displaystyle E:y^{2}=x^{3}+Ax+B,

with
A,B\in K
. An elliptic curve is a nonsingular algebraic curve, and a curve is nonsingular if the discriminant
\Delta=-16(4a^{3}+27B^{2})
is nonzero.

The
j
-invariant of an elliptic curve is an important invariant of curves. For a curve
E:y^{2}=x^{3}+Ax+B
in Weierstrass normal form, the
j
-invariant is given by

j(E)=1728\cdot\frac{4A^{3}}{4A^{3}+4B^{2}}.

For an elliptic curve
E
defined over a field
K
, we will define two important sets. The first is

E/K=E(\overline{K})=\{(x,y)\in\overline{K}^{2}:y^{2}=x^{3}+Ax+B\}\cup\{\infty\}

where the element
\infty
is called the
point at infinity
, and will serve as an identity element. Note that this set is infinite in general. The second set is the
K
-rational points, denoted

E(K)=\{(x,y)\in K^{2}:y^{2}=x^{3}+Ax+B\}\cup\{\infty\}.

These sets are both Abelian groups when equipped with the following group operation:

2.1.1.
Group Law

For two points
P
and
Q
on an elliptic curve, the point
P+Q
is computed by first calculating the line intersecting both
P
and
Q
. This line will intersect the elliptic curve at a third point, call it
R
. Finally, draw a vertical line through
R
and denote the point at which this line intersects the curve as
P+Q
. Points that share a vertical line are inverses, and when adding a point to itself, we use the tangent line. See Figure 1 for some examples of this for an elliptic curve defined over
\mathbb{R}
.

Note that
E(K)\subseteq E(\overline{K})
, in particular,
E(K)
is a subgroup of
E(\overline{K})
. Let
q=p^{r}
where
p
is prime and
r\in\mathbb{N}
. When
K
is the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
E(K)
is a finite abelian group and is classified by the following theorems:

Theorem 2.1

(Hasse)
.

Let
E
be an elliptic curve over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then,

\#E(\mathbb{F}_{q})=q+1-t

where
|t|\leq 2\sqrt{q}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
E
be an elliptic curve over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then,

E(\mathbb{F}_{q})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/n_{1}\mathbb{Z})\oplus(\mathbb{Z}/n_{2}\mathbb{Z})

where
n_{1}n_{2}=\#E(\mathbb{F}_{q})
and
n_{1}|n_{2}
.

The proof of this theorem will be left later after we introduce the notion of torsion points in section
2.3
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
E
be an elliptic curve over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}
. We call
E

supersingular
if
\#E(\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}})\equiv 1\mod p
. Elliptic curves that are not supersingular are called
ordinary
elliptic curves.

2.2.
Maps Between Elliptic Curves

The maps between elliptic curves will be crucial in understanding their structure. Let
E
and
E^{\prime}
be elliptic curves defined over a field
K
. Then, a rational function
f:E(\overline{K})\to E^{\prime}(\overline{K})
over
K
is given by

f(x,y)=\left(\frac{f_{0}(x,y)}{f_{1}(x,y)},\frac{f_{2}(x,y)}{f_{3}(x,y)}\right)\quad f_{i}\in K[x,y]

Alternatively, a rational function defined over
\overline{K}
would consist of polynomials from
\overline{K}[x,y]
.

For notation purposes, we will denote rational functions as
f:E\to E^{\prime}
, even though they are technically maps between
E(\overline{K})
and
E^{\prime}(\overline{K})
. This is to reinforce that these are maps of
curves
rather than of groups.
A
morphism

f:E\to E^{\prime}
over
K
is a rational function over
K
that is defined everywhere. The following lemma gives a canonical form for all these morphisms.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
f:E\to E^{\prime}
be a morphism over
K
. Then, there exist
r_{1},r_{2},s_{1},s_{2}\in K[x]
such that

f(x,y)=\left(\frac{r_{1}(x)}{s_{1}(x)},\frac{r_{2}(x)}{s_{2}(x)}y\right).

Additionally, all morphisms are either constant or surjective.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
E,E^{\prime}
be elliptic curves over
K
. A morphism
\alpha:E\to E^{\prime}
is an isogeny if is surjective and satisfies
\alpha(\infty_{E})=\infty_{E^{\prime}}
.

Isogenies play the role of homomorphisms on curves. In fact, an isogeny
\alpha:E\to E^{\prime}
defined over
K
restricts to a group homomorphism
\alpha:E(K)\to E^{\prime}(K)
. Hence, we will call two curves
E
and
E^{\prime}
isomorphic over
K
if there exists a pair of isogenies defined over
K

\phi:E\to E^{\prime}
and
\psi:E^{\prime}\to E
such that
\psi\circ\phi=[1]_{E}
and
\phi\circ\psi=[1]_{E^{\prime}}
where
[1]_{E}
is the identity map on
E
. Similarly, elliptic curves are isomorphic over
\overline{K}
if the pair of isogenies are defined over
\overline{K}
. The following theorem is an example of an explicit isogeny over
\overline{K}
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
E_{1}:y_{1}^{2}=x_{1}^{3}+A_{1}x_{1}+B_{1}
and
E_{2}:y_{2}^{2}=x_{2}^{3}+A_{2}x_{2}+B_{2}
be elliptic curves defined over
K
. If
j(E_{1})=j(E_{2})
there exists a
\mu\in\overline{K}
with

A_{2}=\mu^{4}A_{1},\quad B_{2}=\mu^{6}B_{1}

such that the transformation

x_{2}=\mu^{2}x_{1},\quad y_{2}=\mu^{3}y_{1}

is an isomorphism over
\overline{K}
.

Proof.

See Theorem 2.19 in
[
16
]
.
∎

It is also the case that the converse is true. If two elliptic curves are isomorphic over
\overline{K}
, then they must have the same
j
-invariant.

Next, we introduce the notion of the dual and show that isogenies actually form equivalence relations. From this, a theorem of Sato and Tate shows that isogeny classes of curves are in one-to-one correspondence with the orders of the curves’
K
-rational points.

Theorem 2.7
.

For every isogeny
\phi:E\to E^{\prime}
, there exists a unique isogeny
\hat{\phi}:E^{\prime}\to E
, called the dual that is an isogeny itself.

Corollary 2.8
.

Isogenies form an equivalence relation on elliptic curves.

Proof.

Isogenies satisfy the required properties:

(1)

Reflexive: Every elliptic curve is isogenous to itself via the identity map.

(2)

Symmetric: The existence of dual isogenies imply that if
E
is isogenous to
E^{\prime}
, then
E^{\prime}
is isogenous to
E
.

(3)

Transitive: Since isogenies are surjective, they can be composed to create new isogenies.

∎

Theorem 2.9

(Sato-Tate’s Isogeny Theorem (1966))
.

Two elliptic curves
E_{1}/K
and
E_{2}/K
are isogeneous if and only if

\#E_{1}(K)=\#E_{2}(K).

As mentioned previously, isogenies defined over
K
induce group homomorphisms between the elliptic curves’
K
-rational points. The following theorem shows that invertible isogenies over
K
induce isomorphisms of
K
-rational points.

Theorem 2.10
.

If
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are elliptic curves isomorphic over
K
, then
E_{1}(K)\cong E_{2}(K)
.

Proof.

Let
\phi:E\to E^{\prime}
be an isomorphism over
K
. Then, since
E(K)\leq E(\overline{K})
,
\phi[E(\overline{K})]\leq E^{\prime}(\overline{K})
. In particular, since
\phi
takes
K
-rational points to
K
-rational points, as it is defined over
K
, we have

\phi[E(K)]\leq E^{\prime}(K)

(2.1)

This same argument holds for the inverse,
\phi^{-1}:E^{\prime}\to E
. So,

\phi^{-1}[E^{\prime}(K)]\leq E(K).

Now, applying
\phi
to the inequality above, we have

E^{\prime}(K)\leq\phi[E(K)].

(2.2)

Thus, by (
2.1
) and (
2.2
),
\phi[E(K)]
and
E^{\prime}(K)
are subgroups of each other, and thus

\phi[E(K)]\cong E^{\prime}(K).

Finally, by the First Isomorphism Theorem,
\phi
is an isomorphism between
E(K)
and
E^{\prime}(K)
.
∎

We will now give two tools to detect if isogenies are defined over
K
.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
\sigma\in\Gal(\overline{K}/K)
and
\phi:E\to E^{\prime}
be an isogeny. Then
\phi
is defined over
K
if and only if
\phi^{\sigma}=\phi

for all
\sigma
.

Lemma 2.12
.

An isogeny
\phi:E\to E^{\prime}
is an isogeny is defined over
K
if the image of
E(K)
under
\phi
is contained in
E^{\prime}(K)
.

2.3.
Endomorphisms

An endomorphism of an elliptic curve
E
is an isogeny from
E
to itself. The set
\operatorname{End}_{K}(E)
of endomorphisms over
K
has a ring structure with the following operations. Let
E/K
be an elliptic curve and let
\alpha,\beta\in\Endo_{K}(E)
. Then,
\alpha+\beta
will be the pointwise addition of functions. So for
P\in E/K
,
(\alpha+\beta)(P)=\alpha(P)+\beta(P)
. Multiplication will be given by function composition, so
\alpha\cdot\beta=\alpha\circ\beta
. The structure is exactly the same for the ring
\operatorname{End}_{\overline{K}}(E)
of endomorphisms defined over
\overline{K}
.

An example of an endomorphism is the
multiplication by
n
map
, denoted by
[n]
where
n
is a nonzero integer. For an elliptic curve
E/K
and a point
P\in E/K
, the map is defined via

[n]P=nP=\underbrace{P+\cdots+P}_{\text{$n$ times}}

Note that
[0]
maps everything to zero, so it is constant, and not an isogeny by definition. This map has some noteworthy properties:

•

[n+m]=[n]+[m]
as
(n+m)P=nP+mP

•

[nm]=[n]\circ[m]
as
(nm)P=n(mP)
.

With the multiplication by
n
map defined, we will also introduce the definition of torsion points here. Given an elliptic curve
E/K
,
E[n]:=\{P\in\overline{K}\mid nP=\infty\}
, that is
E[n]
is the set of point that goes to infinity under the multiplication by
n
map. We also have the following theorem concerning the group structure of
E[n]
:

Theorem 2.13

(Theorem 3.2 in
[
16
]
)
.

Let
E
be an elliptic curve defined over
K
and
n
be a positive integer. If the characteristic of
K
does not divide
n
or is 0, then

E[n]\cong\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}.

If the char(
K
)=
p>0
and
p|n
, then we can write
n=p^{r}n^{\prime}
with
p\nmid n^{\prime}
. Then

E[n]\cong\mathbb{Z}/n^{\prime}\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/n^{\prime}\mathbb{Z}\text{ or }\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/n^{\prime}\mathbb{Z}.

Now we are ready to give the proof of theorem
2.2
.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

From group theory, we know that a finite abelian group can be written into a direct sum of cyclic groups of the form

\mathbb{Z}/n_{1}\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/n_{2}\mathbb{Z}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}/n_{r}\mathbb{Z}

with
n_{i}|n_{i+1}
for
i\geq 1
. For each
n_{i}
,
\mathbb{Z}/n_{i}\mathbb{Z}
contains a one copy
\mathbb{Z}/n_{1}\mathbb{Z}
and thus have
n_{1}
elements of order dividing
n_{1}
. So in total we get
n_{1}^{r}
elements in
E(\mathbb{F}_{q})
with order dividing
n_{1}
. But by Theorem
2.13
, there can be at most
n_{1}^{2}
such elements, so we get
r=2
, which proves the theorem.
∎

Another important endomorphism is the
Frobenius endomorphism
. Let
E/\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then, the Frobenius endomorphism is defined by

\displaystyle\pi_{q}:

\displaystyle E/\mathbb{F}_{q}\to E/\mathbb{F}_{q}

\displaystyle(x,y)\mapsto(x^{q},y^{q})

Lemma 2.14

(Lemma 4.5 in
[
16
]
)
.

Let
E/\mathbb{F}_{q}
be an elliptic curve, and let
(x,y)\in E(\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}})
. Then,

(1)

\pi_{q}(x,y)\in E(\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}})
and

(2)

(x,y)\in E(\mathbb{F}_{q})
if and only if
\pi_{q}(x,y)=(x,y)
.

Corollary 2.15
.

If an endomorphism
\phi\in\operatorname{End}_{\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}}(E)
commutes with the Frobenius endomorphism
\pi_{q}
, then
\phi\in\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(E)
.

Proof.

Suppose
\phi
commutes with
\pi_{q}
. Let
P
be a point in
E(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. Then,

\pi_{q}\circ\phi(P)=\phi\circ\pi_{q}(P).

Since
P\in E(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, then
\pi(P)=P
by Lemma 2.12. Hence,

(\phi(P))^{q}=\phi(P^{q})=\phi(P).

Again, by Lemma 2.12, this means that
\phi(P)\in E(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. Therefore,
\phi
restricted to
E(\mathbb{F}_{q})
maps into
E(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, and is thus defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
by Lemma 2.11.
∎

3.
Ordinary Elliptic Curves with Equal J-Invariant

In this section we will discuss the relationship between isogenous elliptic curves’
F_{q}
-rational points with equal
j
-invariant.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
12
]
)
.

If
E/\mathbb{F}_{q}
is an ordinary elliptic curve, then
\operatorname{End}_{\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}}(E)
is an order in an imaginary quadratic field, and hence commutative.

Corollary 3.2
.

If
E/\mathbb{F}_{q}
is ordinary, then
\operatorname{End}_{\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}}(E)=\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(E)
.

Proof.

Let
\phi\in\operatorname{End}_{\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}}(E)
. Since
\operatorname{End}_{\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}}(E)
is commutative, then
\phi
commutes with
\pi_{q}
, hence
\phi\in\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(E)
. Thus,
\operatorname{End}_{\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}}(E)\subseteq\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(E)
. Trivially,
\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(E)\subseteq\operatorname{End}_{\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}}(E)
since all isogenies defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
are also defined over
\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
. Thus,

\operatorname{End}_{\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}}(E)=\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(E)

∎

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are ordinary elliptic curves defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
j(E_{1})=j(E_{2})
. Then,

\#E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\#E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\iff E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\cong E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

Proof.

We proceed as Proposition 14.19 in
[
2
]
. Suppose
E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\cong E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, then
\#E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\#E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, as isomorphisms of finite groups preserve order. Conversely, suppose
\#E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\#E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. By Sato-Tate, these curves are isogenous over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, so there exists some isogeny
\lambda:E_{1}\to E_{2}
defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Next, since
j(E_{1})=j(E_{2})
, there exists an isomorphism
\phi:E_{2}\to E_{1}
defined over
\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
. Consider
\phi\circ\lambda
which is in
\operatorname{End}_{\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}}(E_{1})
. Since
E_{1}
is ordinary, this endomorphism can be defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, so
\phi\circ\lambda\in\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(E_{1})
. Let
\sigma\in\Gal(\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}/\mathbb{F}_{q})
. Then,

\phi^{\sigma}\circ\lambda=\phi^{\sigma}\circ\lambda^{\sigma}=(\phi\circ\lambda)^{\sigma}=\phi\circ\lambda

Since isogenies are surjective, then
\phi^{\sigma}=\phi
. Since this holds for all such
\sigma
, then
\phi
is defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Finally, since
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are isomorphic over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, then
E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\cong E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
via Theorem 2.9.
∎

4.
J-Invariant Zero Case

We prove that when the
j
-invariant is zero, two curves are in the same isogeny class if and only if they have isomorphic
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-rational points. Since saying two curves are in the same isogeny class is equivalent to saying they have the same number of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
rational points, we will prove that two curves have the same number of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-rational points if and only if the group of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-rational points are isomorphic.

We show the conditions for which an elliptic curve with
j
-invariant zero is supersingular.

Theorem 4.1
.

For
p\equiv 2\mod 3
, if
E
is an elliptic curve with
j
-invariant 0 defined over
F_{q}
, then:

(1)

If
q
is an odd power of
p
, then
\#E(\mathbb{F}_{q})=q+1
;

(2)

If
q
is an even power of
p
, then
\#E(\mathbb{F}_{q})=q+1+\{\pm 2\sqrt{q},\pm\sqrt{q}\}
.

Theorem 4.2

(Gauss)
.

When
p\equiv 1\mod 3
, if
E:y^{2}=x^{3}+r
is an elliptic curve defined over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, then

\#E(\mathbb{F}_{p})=\begin{cases}p+1+2a&\text{if }r\text{ is sextic residue modulo }p\\
p+1-2a&\text{if }r\text{ is cubic residue but not quadratic residue modulo }p\\
p+1-a\pm 3b&\text{if }r\text{ is quadratic residue but not cubic residue modulo }p\\
p+1+a\pm 3b&\text{if }r\text{ is neither quadratic nor cubic residue modulo }p,\end{cases}

where
a,b
satisfy the conditions
a^{2}+3b^{2}=p
,
b>0
, and
a\equiv 2\mod 3
.

Definition 4.3
.

The
trace of Frobenius
of an elliptic curve
E/\mathbb{F}_{q}
, usually denoted as
m
, is defined as
m=q+1-\#E(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

Lemma 4.4
.

An elliptic curve
E:y^{2}=x^{3}+r
defined over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
is ordinary if
p\equiv 1\mod 3
.

Proof.

Here we consider the relationship between
p
and the trace of Frobenius,
m=p+1-\#E(\mathbb{F}_{p})
. By Theorem
4.2
, when
p\equiv 1\mod 3
, we have
m\in\{\pm 2a,-a\pm 3b,a\pm 3b\}
where
a\equiv 2\mod 3
and
b>0
. These conditions give us the following inequalities:

\displaystyle|2a|

\displaystyle\leq a^{2}=p-3b^{2}<p,

\displaystyle|-a\pm 3b|

\displaystyle\leq|a|+|3b|<a^{2}+3b^{2}=p,

\displaystyle|a\pm 3b|

\displaystyle\leq|a|+|3b|<a^{2}+3b^{2}=p.

Thus,
p
does not divide any of the elements in
\{\pm 2a,-a\pm 3b,a\pm 3b\}
.
Hence,
p
does not divide the trace of Frobenius.
Therefore, when
p\equiv 1\mod 3
,
E
is ordinary.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

For an elliptic curve
E:y^{2}=x^{3}+r
defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
r\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
, if
E
is ordinary over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
,
E
is also ordinary over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Proof.

Since
E
is ordinary over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, we have
E[p]\cong\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
. Since
\mathbb{F}_{q}
also has characteristic
p
,
E[p]\cong\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
implies
E
is ordinary over
F_{q}
.
∎

Theorem 4.6
.

An elliptic curve
E
defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
j(E)=0
is supersingular if and only if
p\equiv 2\mod 3
.

Proof.

If
p\equiv 2\mod 3
, by theorem
4.1

m=q+1-\#E(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }q\text{ is an odd power of }p\\
\pm 2\sqrt{q}\text{ or }\pm\sqrt{q}&\text{if }q\text{ is an even power of }p.\end{cases}

Thus when
q
is odd power of
p
,
p\mid m=0
,
E
is supersingular. When
q
is even power of
p
,
\sqrt{q}
will still be a power of
p
, so
E
is also supersingular.

Now suppose
p\not\equiv 2\mod 3
,
i.e.

p\equiv 1\mod 3
. For any elliptic curve
E:y^{2}=x^{3}+r
, there must exist an
r^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
such that
r
and
r^{\prime}
are in the same sextic class over
F_{q}
. So we know
y^{2}=x^{3}+r
and
y^{2}=x^{3}+r^{\prime}
have the same number of rational points, and thus have the same trace of Frobenius,
i.e.

m_{r}=m_{r^{\prime}}
. According to lemma
4.4
and lemma
4.5
,
p\nmid m_{r^{\prime}}
since
r^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
, thus
p\nmid m_{r}
and
E
is ordinary over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
∎

Now since we know an elliptic curve with
j
-invariant 0 is supersingular if and only if
p\equiv 2\mod 3
(thus is ordinary if and only if
p\equiv 1\mod 3
), we will discuss the relationship between order and group structure of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-rational points with respect to
p
.

4.1.
Group Structure of Supersingular Curves

Theorem 4.7

(Theorem 2.1 in
[
15
]
)
.

A finite abelian group
G
of order
N=q+1-m
, with
m^{2}\leq 4q
, is isomorphic to
E(\mathbb{F}_{q})
for
E
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
if and only if one of the following conditions holds:

(1)

p
does not divide
m
, and
G\cong\mathbb{Z}/A\times\mathbb{Z}/B
, where
B|A
and
B|(m-2)
.

(2)

q
is an odd power of
p
and one of the following holds:

(a)

m=0
,
p\equiv 1,2\mod 4
, and
G
is cyclic.

(b)

m=0
,
p\equiv 3\mod 4
, and
G
is either cyclic or
G\cong\mathbb{Z}/(q+1)/2\times\mathbb{Z}/2
.

(c)

p=2
or 3,
m=\pm\sqrt{pq}
, and
G
is cyclic.

(3)

q
is an even power of
p
and one of the following holds:

(a)

m=\pm 2\sqrt{q}
and
G\cong(\mathbb{Z}/(\sqrt{q}\mp 1))^{2}
.

(b)

m=\pm\sqrt{q}
, and
p=3
or
p\equiv 2\mod 3
, and
G
is cyclic.

(c)

m=0
, and
p\equiv 2,3\mod 4
, and
G
is cyclic.

Theorem 4.8
.

If
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
p\equiv 2\mod 3
, then
\#E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\#E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
if and only if
E_{1}(F_{q})\cong E_{2}(F_{q})
.

Proof.

One direction of the theorem is very straightforward, so we will only show that same number of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
rational points implies same group structure of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
rational points.

Firstly, we consider the case when
q
is an odd power of
p
. By theorem
4.1
,

\#E(\mathbb{F}_{q})=q+1\text{ for all }E/\mathbb{F}_{q}.

So we have the trace of Frobenius, denoted by
m
, is given by

m=q+1-\#E(\mathbb{F}_{q})=0.

Since
p\equiv 2\mod 3
, we can either have
p\equiv 1\mod 4
or
p\equiv 3\mod 4
, which corresponds to (
2a
) and (
2b
) in Theorem
4.7
. In the first case,
E(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is cyclic. In the latter case,
E(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is either cyclic or isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/(q+1)/2\times Z/2
according to the theorem. However, since we know
E[2]\not\subseteq E(\mathbb{F}_{p})
, by Theorem 4.1 in
[
18
]
,
E(\mathbb{F}_{q})
can only be cyclic.
Therefore, since they all have the same order, we know

E_{1}(F_{q})\cong\mathbb{Z}/(q+1)\mathbb{Z}\cong E_{2}(F_{q}).

Now suppose instead that
q
is an even power of
p
. Then according to theorem
4.1
,

m=q+1-\#E(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\pm 2\sqrt{q}\text{ or }\pm\sqrt{q}.

By using Theorem
4.7
(3)(b), if
m=\pm\sqrt{q}
,
E(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is again cyclic,
i.e.

E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\cong A\cong E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}),

where

A\in\{\mathbb{Z}/(q+1+\sqrt{q})\mathbb{Z},\ \mathbb{Z}/(q+1-\sqrt{q})\mathbb{Z}\}.

Likewise, Theorem
4.7
(3)(c) says that if
m=\pm 2\sqrt{q}
, then

E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\cong B\cong E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}),

where

B\in\{\mathbb{Z}/(\sqrt{q}-1)\times\mathbb{Z}/(\sqrt{q}-1),\ \mathbb{Z}/(\sqrt{q}+1)\times\mathbb{Z}/(\sqrt{q}+1)\}.

Therefore, when two curves have the same number of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
rational points, their group of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
rational points are always isomorphic.
∎

4.2.
Group Structure of Ordinary Curves

According to Theorem
4.6
, we know that
E
is ordinary if
p\equiv 1\mod 3
. Therefore, our results for ordinary elliptic curves will hold here. We conclude that for elliptic curves
E_{1},E_{2}
defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, if their
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-rational points have the same cardinality, then
E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\cong E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

5.
J-invariant 1728 Case

Let
E
be elliptic curves over field
K
with j-invariant 1728. That means
E
is of the form

y^{2}=x^{3}+ax,

(5.1)

where
a\in K
. Its automorphism group
Aut(E)
has order 4, with one automorphism mapping
(x,y)
to
(-x,iy)
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
E
be an elliptic curve defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
p\equiv 3\mod 4
.

(1)

If
q
is an odd power of
p
, then
\#E(\mathbb{F}_{q})=q+1
.

(2)

If
q
is an even power of
p
, then
\#E(\mathbb{F}_{q})\in\{q+1\>,\>q+1\pm 2\sqrt{q}\}

Proof.

See Theorem 3.6 and 3.7 in
[
1
]
.
∎

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
E
be an elliptic curve defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
j
-invariant 1728.

E
is supersingular if and only if
p\equiv 3\mod 4
.

Proof.

Suppose
p\equiv 3\mod 4
. By Theorem
5.1
, we calculate the trace of Frobenius
m
,

m=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }q\text{ is an odd power of }p\\
0\text{ or }\pm 2\sqrt{q}&\text{ if }q\text{ is an even power of }p.\end{cases}

Since
p
divides
m
when
m=0
, and
p
divides
2\sqrt{q}
when
q
is an even power of
p
,
E
is supersingular.

Now suppose
p\equiv 1\mod 4
. Use a similar technique to the proof of Theorem
4.6
, by applying the Gauss’s Theorem for
E:y^{2}=x^{3}+ax
and extend the result from
\mathbb{F}_{p}
to
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. This shows
E
is ordinary.
∎

5.1.
Group Structure of Supersingular Curves

By Theorem
5.2
, saying a elliptic curve defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
j
-invariant 1728 is supersingular is equivalent to saying
p\equiv 3\mod 4
. Since the we considered the trace of Frobenius in two cases, namely when
q
is an odd power of
p
and when
q
is an even power of
p
, we may also discuss the group structure of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
rational points separately according to these two cases.

5.1.1.
When
q
is an odd power of
p

The group structure of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
rational points in this case corresponds to Theorem
4.7
(
2b
), from which we can see that the there are two possible group structures for
E(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. However, when
q
is an odd power of
p
, the order of
E(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is uniquely given by
\#E(\mathbb{F}_{q})=q+1
. So two elliptic curves having the same number of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-rational points does not imply their group of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-rational points are isomorphic. Example
5.3
shows an example of this statement.

Example 5.3
.

Over
\mathbb{F}_{7}
, there are six elliptic curves all of order 8. So, all the curves are isogeneous by Sato-Tate Theorem, forming one isogeny class. But, there are two isomorphism classes with distinct group structures. In this case these are
\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}
. See the cases when
j=1728
and
r
is odd in Appendix for more examples.

On the other hand, in the following example where
q
is an even power of
p
, same order of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
rational points does imply the isomorphic group structure. In fact, this is true whenever
q
is an even power of
p
and we will prove this statement in next section of this type.

Example 5.4
.

Over
\mathbb{F}_{7^{2}}
, there are 48 elliptic curves of three distinct orders, forming three isogeny classes, by Sato-Tate Theorem.
But, there are four isomorphism classes.
All the curves within the same isogeny class have the same group structure. However, two of the isomorphism classes contain curves of order 50. See the cases when
j=1728
and
r
is even in Appendix for more examples of this type.

5.1.2.
When
q
is an even power of
p

Theorem 5.5
.

If
E_{1},E_{2}
be supersingular elliptic curves over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
r
even and
j
-invariant 1728, then

\#E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\#E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\iff E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\cong E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}).

Proof.

E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\cong E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
implies
\#E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\#E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
by properties of isomorphic groups. So we only need to show that same order of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
rational points imply the same group structure.

In proof of Theorem
5.2
, we showed that when
p\equiv 3\mod 4
and
q
is an even power of
p
,
m\in\{0,2\sqrt{q},-2\sqrt{q}\}
.
When
m=0
,
\#E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\#E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=q+1
. By Theorem
4.7
(3c), we know both
E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
and
E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
are cyclic, that is

E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\cong Z/(q+1)\cong E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}).

When
m=2\sqrt{q}
,
\#E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\#E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=p^{r}+1-2\sqrt{q}
. By Theorem
4.7
(3a), we have

E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\cong A\cong E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}),

where
A
is given by

A=\mathbb{Z}/(\sqrt{q}-1)\times\mathbb{Z}/(\sqrt{q}-1).

When
m=-2\sqrt{q}
,
\#E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\#E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=p^{r}+1+2\sqrt{q}
. By (3)(a) in Theorem
4.7
, we have

E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\cong B\cong E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}),

where
B
is given by

B=\mathbb{Z}/(\sqrt{q}+1)\times\mathbb{Z}/(\sqrt{q}+1).

Therefore, we may conclude that when
q
is an even power of
p
and
p\equiv 3\mod 4
, two elliptic with
j
-invariant 1728 have the same number of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
rational points if and only if their group of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
rational points are isomorphic.
∎

5.2.
Group Structure of Ordinary Curves

According to Theorem
5.2
,
E(\mathbb{F}_{q})
with
j
-invariant 1728 is ordinary if and only if
p\equiv 1\mod 4
. Then by Theorem
3.3
, we have
\#E_{1}(K)=\#E_{2}(K)
if and only if
E_{1}(K)\cong E_{2}(K)
.

6.
General Case: Not Depending on J-invariant

In the preceding sections, we discussed when two elliptic curves with the same number of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-rational points have isomorphic group structures of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-rational points. Notice that all of the previous discussions require the two elliptic curves to have the same
j
-invariant. In this section, we provide a method to decide whether two elliptic curves
E_{1},E_{2}
defined over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
have isomorphic groups of
F_{q}
rational points, instead with the assumption that
\#E_{1}(F_{q})=\#E_{2}(F_{q})
. More details can be found in
[
6
]
.

Before we introduce this method, we first introduce some background knowledge about endomorphism rings and the Frobenius endomorphism. Let
\tau
be the Frobenius endomorphism for an ordinary elliptic curve
E/\mathbb{F}_{q}
. It is well known that the endomorphism algebra of
E
, denoted
\mathbb{Q}(\tau)
, must be an imaginary quadratic field, and more specifically it is of the form
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{m})
for some square free integer
m<0
. Thus, the endomorphism ring of
E
, being an order of the endomorphism algebra, can be written as

\text{End}(E)=\mathbb{Z}\oplus g\mathbb{Z}\delta,

where
g
is the conductor of this order, defined as the set
\{x\in\mathbb{Q}(\tau):x\mathcal{O}_{K}\subset\mathcal{O}\}
, and
\delta=\sqrt{m}
if
m\equiv 2,3\mod 4
and
\delta=\frac{1+\sqrt{m}}{2}
if
m\equiv 1\mod 4
. Therefore, the Frobenius endomorphism
\tau
can also be written as
\tau=a+b\delta
for some integers
a,b
.

Theorem 6.1

(
[
6
]
, Theorem 2.4)
.

Let
E_{1}/\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
E_{2}/\mathbb{F}_{q}
be ordinary elliptic curves with
\#E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\#E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. Let
\tau=a+b\delta
be their Frobenius endomorphism relative to
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and let
End(E_{1})=\mathcal{O}_{1}
and
End(E_{2})=\mathcal{O}_{2}
be the orders in
\mathbb{Q}(\tau)
of conductor
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
respectively. Let
\mathcal{P}:=\{p\text{ }|\text{ }v_{p}(g_{1})\neq v_{p}(g_{2})\text{ and }p\text{ is prime}\}
. For every
p\in\mathcal{P}
, define
s_{p}=\max\{v_{p}(g_{1}),v_{p}(g_{2})\}
.

Fix an integer
k\geq 1
, and let
\tau^{k}=a_{k}+b_{k}\delta
for some
a_{k},b_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then

E_{1}(F_{q})\cong E_{2}(F_{q})\Leftrightarrow v_{p}(a_{k}-1)\leq v_{p}(b_{k})-s_{p}\text{ for all }p\in\mathcal{P},

where
v_{p}(a)
of an integer
a
is the
p
-adic valuation of
a
.

Thus, one possible way to apply this theorem is by fixing
k
to be, say, 1. After, carrying out the calculation of the above
p
-adic valuations, we will be able to see if
E_{1}(F_{q})\cong E_{1}(F_{q})
given
\#E_{1}(F_{q})=\#E_{2}(F_{q})
, where
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are of possibly different
j
-invariant.
