Abstract
. We define two new families of polynomials that generalize permanents and prove upper and lower bounds on their determinantal complexities comparable to the known bounds for permanents. One of these families is obtained by replacing permutations by signed permutations, and the other by replacing permutations by surjective functions with preimages of prescribed sizes.



1.
Introduction

The determinantal complexity of a multivariate polynomial
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
defined over a field
k
is the minimum number
m
for which there exists an
m\times m
matrix of affine linear functions of
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
whose determinant is equal to
f
. A flagship problem in algebraic complexity is to prove or disprove
Valiant’s Conjecture
, which states that the determinantal complexity of the
permanent

\operatorname{perm}_{n}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,\sigma(i)}
of an
n\times n
matrix grows superpolynomially as a function of
n
. The best known upper and lower bounds for this complexity, as discussed below, are respectively exponential and quadratic.

We will prove similar bounds on two families of generalized permaments. The first family is obtained from permanents by replacing permutations by
signed permutations
: that is, permutations
\tau
of the set
\{1,\dots,n,-1,\dots,-n\}
such that
\tau(-i)=-\tau(i)
for
i=1,\dots,n
. In effect, we replace the symmetric group by the hyperoctahedral group of symmetries of the
n
-cube, and thus these polynomials can be seen as a
type B analogue

[
Rei93
]
of the permanents.

Definition 1.1
.

We define the
hyperoctahedral permanent

\operatorname{hoperm}_{n}
to be the polynomial on
2n^{2}
variables
x_{i,j}:1\leq i\leq n,1\leq\pm j\leq n
given by

\operatorname{hoperm}_{n}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\sum_{(\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n})\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,\epsilon_{i}\sigma(i)}

The second family is obtained from permanents by generalizing permutations to functions with preimages of prescribed sizes.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})
be a composition of
\gamma
. We define the
multipermament

\operatorname{mperm}_{\mathbf{m}}=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{\mathbf{m}}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,\sigma(i)}

where
\Sigma_{\mathbf{m}}
is the set of functions
\sigma:[\gamma]\to[n]
such that
|\sigma^{-1}(i)|=m_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
.

We recover the ordinary permanents as
\operatorname{perm}_{n}=\operatorname{mperm}_{(1,\dots,1)}
. Also, note that reordering the components of
\mathbf{m}
simply results in relabeling the variables in the multipermanent. We define multipermanents via compositions rather than partitions in order to facilitate proofs by induction.

It is intuitive from the definitions of hyperoctahedral permanents and multipermanents that these polynomials should be at least as hard to calculate as permanents. We could make this intution precise by using the notion of
VNP-completeness
, introduced by Valiant
[
Val79a
]
. Informally, (sequences of) permanents, hyperoctahedral permanents, or multipermanents are all in the class VNP because any given coefficient is easy to determine. Valiant showed that the family of permanents is in fact VNP-complete; that is, if there is a family of polynomial-size algebraic circuits to compute permanents, then VP=VNP, and in fact P=NP
[
Val79b
]
. Now
\operatorname{perm}_{n}
can be efficiently reduced either to
\operatorname{hoperm}_{n}
or to
\operatorname{mperm}_{\mathbf{m}}
where
\mathbf{m}
is any composition into
n
nonzero parts, so families of hyperoctahedral permanents or of multipermanents with
n
increasing are also VNP-complete. A recent article by Ikenmeyer and Landsberg
[
IL17
]
lays out precise relations between VNP-completeness, determinantal complexity, and various other measures of algebraic complexity.

Meaningful upper and lower bounds on determinantal complexity are typically difficult to obtain. The best known upper bound on
\operatorname{dc}(\operatorname{perm}_{n})
for
n\geq 3
, obtained by Grenet
[
Gre11
]
, is
2^{n}-1
. Our new polynomials are suggested by the observation that Grenet’s method of proof works for any
poset polynomial
(as defined in the next section). For permanents, the upper bound is obtained from a natural labeling of the cover relations of the Boolean lattice. By using similar labelings of the face lattice of the
n
-cube, and of the lattice of multisets contained in a given multiset (equivalently, of monomials dividing a given monomial), we obtain the following upper bounds.

Theorem 1.3
.

(1)

For every
n
, we have
\operatorname{dc}(\operatorname{hoperm}_{n})\leq 3^{n}
.

(2)

For every composition
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})
, we have

\operatorname{dc}(\operatorname{mperm}_{\mathbf{m}})\leq\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}(m_{i}+1)\right)-1
.

For
n\geq 5
, the best known lower bound on
\operatorname{dc}(\operatorname{perm}_{n})
in characteristic zero, obtained by Mignon and Ressayre
[
MR04
]
, is
\frac{n^{2}}{2}
. Their proof involves bounding the rank of the Hessian matrix at a given zero of the permanent. Cai, Chen, and Li
[
CCL08
]
later adapted this technique to give a quadratic lower bound for
\operatorname{dc}(\operatorname{perm}_{n})
in characteristic
p>2
. The technique can also be applied to other polynomials. For example, Chen, Kayal, and Wigderson suggest as an exercise
[
CKW11
, Exercise 13.6]
to show in this way that the determinantal complexity of the symmetric polynomial
\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}x_{i}x_{j}
is
\Omega(n)
. In a more involved application, Chillara and Mukhopadhyay
[
CM19
]
showed that the determinantal complexity of the
iterated matrix multiplication polynomial

IMN_{n,d}(X)=\sum\limits_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d-1}\in[n]}x_{1,i_{1}}^{(1)}x_{i_{1},i_{2}}^{(2)}\cdots x_{i_{d-2},i_{d-1}}^{(d-1)}x_{i_{d-1},1}^{(d)}

(that is, the top left entry of the product of
d
generic
n\times n
matrices) is at least
d(n-1)
.

Unlike the Grenet argument for upper bounds, each application of the Mignon-Ressayre method to a new family of polynomials seems to require significant work: first in identifying an appropriate zero of the polynomial (not all zeros yield a useful bound), then in analyzing the Hessian at this zero. In this way, we prove the following results.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
k
be a field of characteristic zero.

(1)

For all
n
we have
dc(\operatorname{hoperm}_{n})\geq n^{2}/2.

(2)

For all
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
we have
\operatorname{dc}(\operatorname{mperm}_{\mathbf{m}})\geq(m_{1}+\dots+m_{n})^{2}/2
.

We note that these upper and lower bounds are exponentially far apart, just as in the case of the permanent. It would be interesting to identify new families of polynomials based on combinatorially natural posets for which the approach described here not only succeeds in proving both lower and upper bounds, but for which the upper and lower bounds are significantly closer together.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we explain Grenet’s method as applied to general labeled graded posets and apply it to prove Theorem
1.3
.
In Section 3, we explain the Mignon-Ressayre method and apply it to prove Theorem
1.4
.

2.
Proofs of upper bounds and VNP-completeness

Let
P
be a graded poset of rank
d
with
p
elements, including a unique maximum
\hat{1}
and a unique minimum
\hat{0}
. We say that a polynomial
f=f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is
supported on
P
if there exists a labelling of the edges
e
of the Hasse diagram of
P
with linear forms
L_{e}
in the variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
such that

f=\sum_{C}\left(\prod_{e\in C}L_{e}\right)

where
C
ranges over all of the saturated chains
\hat{0}<i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{d-1}<\hat{1}
in
P
.

Proposition 2.1
.

If
f
is supported on a graded poset
P
with
p
elements and a unique minimum
\hat{0}
and maximum
\hat{1}
, then
f
has a determinantal representation of size
p-1
.

Proof.

The construction of polynomials supported on a poset directly generalizes Grenet’s representation of the permanent polynomial
[
Gre11
]
and the proof will do so as well. Recall that a
cycle cover
in a directed graph
G
is a union of vertex-disjoint cycles that passes through every vertex of
G
, and that the permanent of the directed adjacency matrix equals the number of cycle covers of
G
. If we replace the 1’s in the adjacency matrix by arbitrary weights, then the permanent computes the number of weighted cycle covers.

To obtain our graph
G
, we begin with the Hasse diagram of
P
. That is, each cover relation
x\lessdot y
(i.e. edge of the Hasse diagram) defines an edge
e=x\to y
in
G
which we label by
L_{e}
. Now identify the maximum of
P
with the minimum as a single vertex
v_{0}
. Finally, we add a loop at every vertex except
v_{0}
and label these loops by 1.

Now let
A
be the weighted adjacency matrix of
G
with weights given by the labeling
L_{e}
. Every cycle cover passes through
v_{0}
exactly once, so it includes one cycle of the form

(v_{0},v_{i_{1}},v_{i_{2}},\dots,v_{i_{d-1}},v_{0})
where
C=\hat{0}\lessdot i_{1}\lessdot i_{2}\lessdot\dots\lessdot i_{d-1}\lessdot\hat{1}
is a saturated chain in
P
. To avoid passing through
v_{0}
again, we must complete the cycle cover by taking the loop at each vertex outside
C
. Thus

\operatorname{perm}(A)=\sum_{C}\left(\prod_{e\in C}L_{e}\right)1^{p-1-d}=f.

But since
P
is graded, all of the cycles have the same length
d
, so all of the cycle covers have the same sign (positive if
d
is odd, or negative if
d
is even.) Thus

\operatorname{det}(A)=\pm\operatorname{perm}(A)=\pm f.

Finally, if this process yields
-f
instead of
f
, we obtain a determinantal representation of
f
by multiplying any single row of
A
by -1.
∎

Grenet’s determinantal representation of
perm_{n}
of size
2^{n}-1
is obtained by applying this process to the Boolean lattice on
n
elements. If
T
is a set of size
i
and
e
is the edge from the set
T
to the set
T\cup\{j\}
, then we take
L_{e}=x_{ij}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

For the multipermanent
\operatorname{mperm}_{\mathbf{m}}
, let
P_{\mathbf{m}}
be the poset of multisets contained in the multiset
S=\{1^{m_{1}},\dots,n^{m_{n}}\}
. Then
P_{\mathbf{m}}
is a graded poset of rank
\gamma=m_{1}+\dots+m_{n}
with
\prod_{i=1}^{n}(m_{i}+1)
elements, including a unique maximum
\emptyset
and a unique maximum
S
. Just as in the case of permanents, if
T
is a multiset of size
i
and if there is an edge from
T
to
T\cup\{j\}
, then we label this edge by
x_{ij}
. Each term of the multipermanent indexes a unique saturated chain for
\emptyset
to
S
, so the result follows from Proposition
2.1
.

For the hyperoctahedral permanent
\operatorname{hoperm}_{n}
, let
Q_{n}
be the face lattice of the
n
-cube. A face
F
of the cube determines a unique vector
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in\{0,1,-1\}^{n}
where
u_{i}=1
if
F
is contained in the hyperplane
y_{i}=1
,
u_{i}=-1
if
F
is contained in the hyperplane
y_{1}=-1
, and otherwise
u_{i}=0
. Conversely, every
u\in\{0,1,-1\}^{n}
determines a unique face of the cube, so
\tilde{Q}
is a graded poset of rank
n
with
3^{n}
elements including a unique minimum
\emptyset
and
2^{n}
maxima indexed by vectors
u\in\{1,-1\}^{n}
. The covering relation is given by
u\lessdot v
when:

(1)

there is a unique index
j
such that
u_{j}=0
and
v_{j}\neq 0
, and

(2)

u_{k}=v_{k}
for all
k\neq j
.

We label such a relation (i.e. edge in the Hasse diagram) by
x_{i,\pm j}
where
i
is the rank (= number of nonzero coordinates) of
u
and the sign depends on
v_{j}
. The result then follows from the definition of hyperoctahedral permanents and Proposition
2.1
.
∎

3.
Proofs of lower bounds

Let
k
be a field of characteristic zero. For a polynomial
f\in k[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, we consider the gradient function

\displaystyle Tf:k^{N}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow k^{N}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\longmapsto T_{x}f=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}(x),\ldots,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{N}}(x)\right)

and the Hessian

\displaystyle T^{2}f:k^{N}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow k^{N\times N}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\longmapsto T^{2}_{x}f=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(x)\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq N}.

Lemma 3.1
.

[
MR04
, Proposition 3.8]

For any singular
n\times n
matrix
A
, we have

\operatorname{rank}(T^{2}_{A}{\operatorname{det}}_{n})\leq 2n.

The following result is also based on
[
MR04
]
, but appears there only for permanents.

Proposition 3.2
.

Given
f\in k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
, let
F:k^{m}\rightarrow M_{n}(k)
be a determinantal repesentation of
f
; that is,
f
is an affine linear function such that
f=\operatorname{det}_{n}\circ F
. If
f(y)=0
then

\operatorname{rank}(T^{2}_{y}f)\leq 2n.

Proof.

Let
F(x)=(g_{ij}(x))_{i,j\in[n]}
. Since
F
is affine linear, all of its partial derivatives are constant so write
c_{ij,h}=\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial x_{h}}
. By two applications of the chain rule, we obtain

\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{h}}(y)=\sum\limits_{i,j\in[n]}c_{ij,h}\frac{\partial{\operatorname{det}}_{n}}{\partial z_{i,j}}(F(y)),

\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{h}\partial x_{e}}(y)=\sum\limits_{i,j\in[n]}\sum\limits_{k,l\in[n]}c_{ij,h}\frac{\partial^{2}{\operatorname{det}}_{n}}{\partial z_{k,l}\partial z_{i,j}}(F(y))c_{kl,e}.

Thus
T^{2}_{y}f=LT^{2}_{F(y)}{\operatorname{det}}_{n}L^{t}
where

L=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}c_{11,1}&c_{12,1}&\cdots&c_{nn,1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
c_{11,m}&c_{12,m}&\cdots&c_{nn,m}\end{array}\right)_{m\times n^{2}}.

Thus
\operatorname{rank}(T^{2}_{y}f)=\operatorname{rank}\left(LT^{2}_{F(y)}{\operatorname{det}}_{n}L^{t}\right)\leq\operatorname{rank}(T^{2}_{F(y)}{\operatorname{det}}_{n})\leq 2n
, where the last inequality follows from Lemma
3.1
and the fact that
{\operatorname{det}}_{n}(F(y))=f(y)=0.

∎

The following general bound on determinantal complexity is an immediate corollary of Proposition
3.2
.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
f\in k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
. For each
y\in k^{m}
such that
f(y)=0
,

\operatorname{rank}(T^{2}_{y}f)/2\leq\operatorname{dc}(f).

For our lower bounds, we will need the following fact from linear algebra.

Lemma 3.4
.

[
MR04
, Lemma 3.7]

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
and let
Q,R
be
a\times a
invertible matrices. Then the
(ab)\times(ab)
matrix

M=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&Q&Q&\cdots&Q\\
Q&0&R&\cdots&R\\
Q&R&0&\cdots&R\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
Q&R&R&\cdots&0\end{array}\right)

is invertible.

3.1.
Proof of the lower bound for hyperoctahedral permanents

Recall that
\operatorname{hoperm}_{n}
is a polynomial in
2n^{2}
variables
\{x_{i,j}:1\leq i\leq n,1\leq\pm j\leq n\}
. We take these variables to form an
(n\times 2n)
generic matrix

X=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1,1}&\cdots&x_{1,n}&x_{1,-1}&\cdots&x_{1,-n}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&&\vdots\\
x_{n,1}&\cdots&x_{n,n}&x_{n,-1}&\cdots&n_{1,-n}\end{bmatrix}.

We begin by proving a recursive formula for the hyperoctahedral permanent using row expansion.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
X=(x_{i,j})
be as above. Then

\operatorname{hoperm}_{n}(X)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(x_{i,j}+x_{i,-j}\right)\operatorname{hoperm}_{n-1}(X_{i,\pm j})

where
X_{i,\pm j}
is obtained from
X
by removing row
i
and the columns indexed by
j
and
-j
.

Proof.

\displaystyle\operatorname{hoperm}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\sum_{(\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n})\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}x_{k,\epsilon_{k}\sigma(k)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n}\\
\sigma(i)=j\end{subarray}}(x_{i,j}+x_{i,-j})\sum_{\epsilon_{1},\dots,\hat{\epsilon_{i}},\dots,\epsilon_{n}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n-1}}\prod_{k\neq i}x_{k,\epsilon_{k}\sigma(k)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(x_{i,j}+x_{i,-j}\right)\operatorname{hoperm}_{n-1}(X_{i,\pm j}).\qed

In order to identify an appropriate zero, we need the following observation. Let
U_{n,m}
be the
n\times m
matrix of all ones.

Lemma 3.6
.

For all
n
, we have
\operatorname{hoperm}_{n}(U_{n,2n})=2^{n}n!
.

Proof.

For
n=1
we have
U_{1,2}=(1,1)
so the hyperoctedral permanent is
1+1=2^{1}1!
. For
n\geq 2
, we use Lemma
3.5
and induction to obtain

\operatorname{hoperm}_{n+1}(U_{n+1,2n+2})=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n+1}2\cdot\operatorname{hoperm}_{n}(U_{n,2n})=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n+1}2\cdot 2^{n}n!=2^{n+1}(n+1)!.\qed

Proposition 3.7
.

Consider the
n\times 2n
matrix
B=(b_{i,j})
where

b_{i,j}=\begin{cases}-2n+1&\mbox{if }i=n\ \mbox{and}\ j=n\\
1&\mbox{otherwise. }\end{cases}

Then
\operatorname{hoperm}_{n}(B)=0
.

Proof.

The proof is again by induction on
n
. If
n=1
, then
B=(-1,1)
and so
\operatorname{hoperm}(B)=-1+1=0
. If
n\geq 2
, then from Lemma
3.5
and Lemma
3.6
we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{hoperm}_{n}(B)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}2\operatorname{hoperm}_{n-1}(U_{n-1,2n-2})+(-2n+1+1)\operatorname{hoperm}_{n-1}(U_{n-1,2n-2})

\displaystyle=2(n-1)2^{n-1}(n-1)!+(-2n+2)2^{n-1}(n-1)!

\displaystyle=0.\qed

We now analyze the Hessian matrix of
\operatorname{hoperm}_{n}
at the zero
B
. Let
W_{n}=U_{(n,n)}-I_{n}
.

Proposition 3.8
.

Order the variables of
\operatorname{hoperm}
as follows:

a_{1,1},\ldots,a_{1,n},\ldots,a_{n,1},\ldots,a_{n,n},a_{1,-1},\ldots,a_{1,-n},\ldots,a_{n,-1},\ldots,a_{n,-n}.

Then the Hessian matrix of
\operatorname{hoperm}
evaluated at
B
is

H(B)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}C&C\\
C&C\end{array}\right)

where
C
is the
n^{2}\times n^{2}
matrix given in
n\times n
blocks by

C=2^{n-2}(n-3)!\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&A&\cdots&A&(n-2)W\\
A&0&\cdots&A&(n-2)W\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
A&A&\cdots&0&(n-2)W\\
(n-2)W&(n-2)W&\cdots&(n-2)W&0\end{array}\right),

\textup{where }A=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&-2&\cdots&-2&n-2\\
-2&0&\cdots&-2&n-2\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
-2&-2&\cdots&0&n-2\\
n-2&n-2&\cdots&n-2&0\end{array}\right).

Proof.

By the symmetry between positive and negative indices in
\operatorname{hoperm}_{n}
, we have

H_{(i,\pm j),(k,\pm l)}=H_{(i,j),(k,l)}
. Thus
H
can be partitioned into four identical square blocks, each of which we label
C
. From now on we thus assume that
j
and
l
are positive.

We now consider the distinct cases for the indices
i
and
k
.

(1)

If
i=k
, then
H_{(i,j),(k,l)}=0
, regardless of
j
and
l
. This justifies the diagonal blocks of zeros in
C
.

(2)

Suppose
i\neq k
and
i,k<n
. We then consider different cases for
j
and
l
.

(a)

If
j=l
, then again
H_{(i,j),(k,l)}=0
.

(b)

If
j\neq l
and neither is equal to
n
, then

\displaystyle H_{(i,j),(k,l)}

\displaystyle=(-2n+2)\operatorname{hoperm}_{n-3}(U_{n-3,2n-6})+\sum\limits_{t\neq j,l,n}2\operatorname{hoperm}_{n-3}(U_{n-3,2n-6})

\displaystyle=(-2n+2)2^{n-3}(n-3)!+2(n-3)\cdot 2^{n-3}(n-3)!

\displaystyle=-2^{n-1}(n-3)!.

(c)

If
j\neq l
and
j
or
l
equals
n
, then

H_{(i,j),(k,l)}=\sum\limits_{t\neq j,l}2\operatorname{hoperm}_{n-3}(U_{n-3,2n-6})=2(n-2)2^{n-3}(n-3)!=2^{n-2}(n-2)!.

This justifies the blocks labelled
A
.

(3)

Finally, suppose
i\neq k
and either
i
or
k
is equal to
n
. If
j=l
then we still have
H_{(i,j),(k,l)}=0
. Otherwise, we have

H_{(i,j),(k,l)}=\operatorname{hoperm}_{n-2}(U_{n-2,2n-4})=2^{n-2}(n-2)!.

This justifies the blocks labelled
W
. ∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
(2).

By Lemma
3.4
we have that the matrices
A
and
(n-2)W
are nonsingular. Again using Lemma
3.4
, it now follows that the
n\times n
matrix
C
is also nonsingular, so the rank of
H
is
n
. The theorem now follows from Corollary
3.3
and Proposition
3.8
.
∎

3.2.
Proof of the lower bound for multipermanents

Let
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})
be a composition of
\gamma
. As
\operatorname{mperm}_{\mathbf{m}}
is a polynomial in the variables
(x_{ij})
for
1\leq i\leq\gamma,1\leq j\leq n
, we consider its input to be a
\gamma\times n
matrix.

Again, we begin with a recurrence for multipermanents and their values at matrices of all ones.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. Then

\operatorname{mperm}_{\mathbf{m}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{\gamma,j}\operatorname{mperm}_{\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{e}_{j}}.

where
\mathbf{e}_{j}
is the
j
th standard basis vector and we take
\operatorname{mperm}_{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}}
to be the zero polynomial if
\mathbf{m}^{\prime}_{j}
is negative for some
j
.

Proof.

By definition,
\operatorname{mperm}_{\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{e}_{j}}
is the number of chains in
P_{\mathbf{m}}
from
\emptyset
to the multiset
\{1^{m_{1}},\dots,i^{m_{i}-1},\dots,n^{m_{n}}\}
. The total number of chains from
\emptyset
to
S
is obtained by summing over the possible element
j
to be added at the last step.
∎

Lemma 3.10
.

For all
\gamma
and
n
, we have
\operatorname{mperm}_{\mathbf{m}}(U)=\binom{\gamma}{m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{n}}=\frac{\gamma!}{m_{1}!m_{2}!\dots m_{n}!}

Proof.

Note that
\operatorname{mperm}_{\mathbf{m}}(U)
is simply the number of terms of

, which is the number of saturated chains in the poset

. Such a chain is given by a sequence

in which the number

Again, we begin by identifying an appropriate zero of

Proposition 3.11

Let

(1)

(2)

(1)

Using Lemmas

and
3.9

\operatorname{mperm}_{\mathbf{m}}\left(B^{(1)}\right)

\displaystyle\sum_{j\notin C}1\cdot\operatorname{mperm}_{\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{e}_{j}}(U_{\gamma-1,n-1})+\sum_{j\in C}\left(1-\frac{\gamma}{c\cdot m_{k}}\right)\cdot\operatorname{mperm}_{\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{e}_{j}}(U_{\gamma-1,n-1})

(2)

3.10
and
3.9

\displaystyle\operatorname{mperm}_{\mathbf{m}}\left(B^{(2)}\right)
