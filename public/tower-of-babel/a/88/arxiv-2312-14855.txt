Generalised
T

Abstract




Deformations of many-body Hamiltonians by certain products of conserved currents, referred to as
T\bar{T}
-deformations, are known to preserve integrability. Generalised
T\bar{T}
-deformations, based on the complete space of pseudolocal currents, were suggested [B. Doyon, J, Durnin, T. Yoshimura, Scipost Physics 13, 072 (2022)] to give rise to integrable systems with arbitrary two-body scattering shifts, going beyond those from known models or standard CDD factors.
However, locality properties were not clear. We construct explicit generalised
T\bar{T}
-deformations of the system of classical free particles. We show rigorously that they are Liouville integrable Hamiltonian systems with finite-range interactions. We show elastic, factorised scattering, with a two-particle scattering shift that can be any continuously differentiable non-negative even function of momentum differences, fixed by the
T\bar{T}
-deformation function. We show that the scattering map (or wave operator) has a finite-range property allowing us to trace carriers of asymptotic momenta even at finite times — an important characteristics of many-body integrability. We evaluate the free energy and prove the thermodynamic Bethe ansatz with Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics, including with space-varying potentials and in finite and infinite volumes. We give equations for the particles’ trajectories where time appears explicitly, generalising the contraction map of hard rod systems: the effect of generalised
T\bar{T}
-deformations is to modify the local metric perceived by each particle, adding extra space in a way that depends on their neighbours. The systems generalise the gas of interacting Bethe ansatz wave packets recently introduced in the Lieb-Liniger model. They form a new class of models that, we believe, most clearly make manifest the structures of many-body integrability.

They fundamentally affect the dynamics, but, if the system is completely integrable, they preserve its integrability. They are so referred to, because one of the simplest example is a deformation, in conformal field theory, by a product of the holomorphic,

In order to understand the importance of
T\bar{T}
-deformations, let us recall how the structure of scattering plays a crucial role in integrable many-body systems. Take for instance a classical Hamiltonian system of particles in one dimension
[
18
]
, with interaction between particles that is translation-invariant and short-range, and without bound states. Naturally, it is a general property of such systems, integrable or not, that at large times, particles typically separate out and follow straight trajectories determined by “asymptotic coordinates”: constant asymptotic momenta (their slopes) and impact parameters (their spatial shifts). The scattering map, often called “wave operator” – the symplectic transformation from real to asymptotic coordinates, either “in” or “out” – is a canonical transformation that trivialises the dynamics. Thus, the asymptotic momenta form a set of conserved quantities in involution, much like in Liouville-Arnold integrability
[
19
]
. However, the existence of such a trivialising transformation certainly does not, in fact, imply integrability!

Two effects occur from integrability. First, in integrable models many-body scattering is very constrained: it is elastic and factorised. That is, the sets of asymptotic in- and out-momenta is the same (elastic scattering), and the full scattering shift incurred by each particle is the sum of successive two-body scattering shifts, as if many-particle scattering were a succession of well separated two-particle events (factorised scattering). Elastic scattering follows from factorised scattering by energy-momentum conservation at two-particle events; and under certain conditions on the interaction potential, factorised scattering can also be shown to follow from elastic scattering
[
18
]
. Second, the scattering map is expected to have special properties. In addition to asymptotic momenta being differentiable functions of phase-space coordinates, they satisfy a short-range condition, guaranteeing that it is somehow possible to “trace” the carrier of a given asymptotic momentum throughout time, from its in- to its out-trajectory. This is closely related to the crucial condition, for integrable systems with unbounded trajectories, that Liouville conserved quantities be
extensive
, essentially they must possess densities acting on short spatial ranges. Such “short-range Liouville integrability” implies elastic and factorised scattering by Parke’s argument, originally made in QFT
[
20
]
but more generally applicable
[
21
,
22
]
. The short-range property of asymptotic momenta is however in general difficult to establish, besides simple models such as hard rods
[
23
]
. Similar concepts hold in integrable PDEs, QFTs, quantum chains, cellular automata (e.g. Rule 54
[
24
]
and box-ball
[
25
]
systems offer good examples of the scattering theory), etc.

This scattering structure is important. For instance, it is well known that the thermodynamics of completely integrable many-body systems is determined solely by the two-body scattering shift function

(for incoming momenta

In this paper, we address these questions in a rigorous fashion. We construct the explicit generalised
T\bar{T}
-deformations of the system of classical free particles. We consider, in fact, a more general form than that used in the literature, based on a deformation function

-deformed systems; it is closely related to the form of the Bethe ansatz wave function. We show that the deformations are short-range Liouville integrable Hamiltonian systems, with interactions whose spatial range is in fact finite (in a specific way that we will explain). The interaction potential is differentiable and particle-symmetric, but depends in general on momenta – this allows us to elude the theorem according to which integrable interaction potentials cannot be finite-range
[

We show that the

We also show that the scattering map has the properties of many-body integrability. Not only the map to asymptotic momenta is a continuously differentiable function, but also we show that it satisfies a short-range property, allowing us to estimate the positions of carriers of asymptotic momenta even at finite times. That is, if a set of particles is separated enough from the rest, their positions and momenta fully determine their asymptotic momenta, and these asymptotic momenta are simply those one would obtained in a “time-of-flight” experiment, where the particles are taken out of the system and let to expand in the vacuum. The time-of-flight argument is one way of justifying the emergence of the GHD equations based on the scattering picture of the thermodynamics (see e.g.

2
Main results and discussion

In this section we present our setup and main results. The results are about certain trajectories in canonical phase space
{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}
(
N\in{\mathbb{N}}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
), which are defined by a certain transformation of coordinates. We state that they correspond to a Liouville integrable Hamiltonian evolution with finite-range interaction, which has the property of elastic, factorised scattering, which is a generalised
T\bar{T}
-deformation of the system of free particles, and whose thermodynamics is given by the classical thermodynamic Bethe ansatz (TBA), with Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics. All these notions, that we show hold for this Hamiltonian system, are notions that are well understood at least informally, and have been studied in various setups (such as classical mechanics, QFT, Bethe ansatz integrability, PDEs, soliton gases, etc.). In order express our results unambiguously, we provide precise definitions of these notions in the setup of many-particle classical systems in Section
3
.

Let
\psi\in C^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
and
\epsilon>0
. The following properties are assumed: for all
(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,

\psi_{x\theta}(x,\theta)\geq 0,\quad\psi(-x,-\theta)=\psi(x,\theta),\quad\psi_{x}(x,\theta)=0\ \mbox{whenever $|x|\geq\epsilon$}

(1)

where
\psi_{x}=\partial\psi/\partial x
,

\varphi(\theta)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\rm d}x\,\psi_{x\theta}(x,\theta)=\psi_{\theta}(X,\theta)-\psi_{\theta}(-X,\theta)\quad\mbox{(every $X>\epsilon$).}

(2)

Note that
\varphi(\theta)\geq 0
for all
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
, that
\varphi\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}})

\varphi(\theta)=\varphi(-\theta)

Below we use the boldface notation for vectors

The main object is the map
\omega:{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}\ni(\bm{y},\bm{\theta})\mapsto(\bm{x},\bm{p})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}
defined as the solution to the following system of nonlinear equations:

\displaystyle y_{i}

\displaystyle=x_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\psi_{\theta}(x_{i}-x_{j},\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})

(3)

\displaystyle p_{i}

\displaystyle=\theta_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\psi_{x}(x_{i}-x_{j},\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}).

2.1
Finite-range integrable models with arbitrary scattering shift

We will show that this dynamics has interesting properties: the trajectories indeed exist and are unique by the above definition, they are generated by a Hamiltonian that is Liouville integrable and that has finite-range interaction, and the many-body scattering of the particles is elastic, and factorises into two-body scattering events.

. In order to understand their origin, and the meaning of the Hamiltonian (
5
), one route is via
T\bar{T}
-deformations, discussed in Subsection
3.3
. A generalised
T\bar{T}
-deformation of a Hamiltonian
H_{0}
is a flow of Hamiltonians
\lambda\mapsto H_{\lambda}
, which takes the form

\frac{\partial H_{\lambda}(\bm{x},\bm{p})}{\partial\lambda}=-\sum_{ij}\big(\dot{x}^{\lambda}_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})-\dot{x}^{\lambda}_{j}(\bm{x},\bm{p})\big)\,w\big(x_{i}-x_{j},\theta^{\lambda}_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})-\theta^{\lambda}_{j}(\bm{x},\bm{p})\big)

(16)

for all (say)
\lambda\in[0,1]
and a.e.
(\bm{x},\bm{p})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}
, and for some continuously differentiable “deformation function”
w(x,\theta)
. On the right-hand side, the upper index
λ
indicates that the quantities are evaluated with respect to the Hamiltonian
H_{\lambda}
, in particular
\dot{x}_{i}^{\lambda}(\bm{x},\bm{p})=\{x_{i},H_{\lambda}(\bm{x},\bm{p})\}
and
\theta^{\lambda}_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})
are asymptotic momenta with respect to the dynamics induced by
H_{\lambda}(\bm{x},\bm{p})
. Then we say that
H_{\psi}
is a generalised
T\bar{T}
deformation of
H_{0}
if there is such a flow such that
H_{\psi}=H_{1}
.

The above is a generalisation of the deformation by products of local conserved densities and currents that is the hallmark of
T\bar{T}
-deformations
[
2
]
. Here, as originally proposed in
[
13
]
, it is generalised to densities and currents that are parametrised by a continuous parameter: an asymptotic momentum. This generalisation is possible, as, being tracer dynamics, our family of models admit well-defined extensive conserved quantities
Q_{\theta}
,
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
that measure the density of asymptotic particles of asymptotic momenta
\theta
, and that
possess local densities and associated currents
, phase-space functions
q_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)
and
j_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)
satisfying
\dot{q}_{\theta}(x)+\partial_{x}j_{\theta}(x)=0
(keeping the phase-space dependence implicit). In terms of the latter, a generalised
T\bar{T}
-deformation takes the form

\frac{\partial H_{\lambda}}{\partial\lambda}=\int{\rm d}\theta{\rm d}\alpha{\rm d}x{\rm d}x^{\prime}\,w(x-x^{\prime},\theta-\alpha)\big(q^{\lambda}_{\theta}(x)j^{\lambda}_{\alpha}(x^{\prime})-j^{\lambda}_{\theta}(x)q^{\lambda}_{\alpha}(x^{\prime})\big)

(17)

and the specific form (
16
) come from the explicit expressions of these local densities and currents.

The following result characterises
H_{\psi}
in this language.

Theorem 2.2

(generalised
T\bar{T}
-deformation)
.

H_{\lambda}:=H_{\lambda\psi}
satisfies (
16
), with deformation function

w(x,\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\psi_{x}(x,\theta).

(18)

Thus
H_{\psi}
is a generalised
T\bar{T}
-deformation (Definition
3.9
) of the system of free particles
H_{0}=\sum_{i}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2}
.

Thus, the semiclassical Bethe systems form a complete family of generalised
T\bar{T}
-deformations of the system of free particles on the line (we have not shown uniqueness of the solution to (
16
), but we expect so). Combining with our discussion in Subsection
2.1
, our result gives credence to the claim made in
[
13
]
that
arbitrary two-body scattering shifts can be achieved from generalised
T\bar{T}
-deformations.

Remark 2.2
.

The generalised
T\bar{T}
-deformations introduced in
[
13
]
in fact correspond to a special choice of the
x
dependence of the deformation function
w(x,\theta)
: it is
w(x,\theta)=\delta(x)w(\theta)
, involving the Dirac delta function
\delta(x)
(as this choice is not a differentiable function, it is formally outside of our framework). In this case, with our requirements,
w(\theta)
must be non-decreasing and anti-symmetric. Above, we have therefore extended the deformations to include more general spatial dependence. The further specialisation to deformations by products of the “conventional” family of local conserved densities and currents, the original
T\bar{T}
-deformations of
[
2
]
, corresponds to the choice of
w(\theta)
as a polynomial
w(\theta)=\sum_{c=1,3,4,\ldots}w_{c}\theta^{c}
(only odd polynomials arise because of our anti-symmetry condition), and is expressed in terms of conventional local densities and currents as

\frac{\partial H_{\lambda}}{\partial\lambda}=\sum_{a,b}\int{\rm d}x\,w_{ab}\big(q^{\lambda}_{a}(x)j^{\lambda}_{b}(x)-j^{\lambda}_{a}(x)q^{\lambda}_{b}(x)\big)

(19)

for weights
w_{ab}=w_{a+b}\left({a+b\atop a}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}
. In particular, the mass-momentum deformation, showed in
[
10
]
to transform point particles into rods of finite lengths
-\lambda
, is
w(\theta)=\theta/2
.

2.3
Thermodynamic Bethe ansatz and local density approximation

Finally, we look at the thermodynamics. We consider the specific free energy

f_{L}=-L^{-1}\log\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{N!}\int{\rm d}^{N}x{\rm d}^{N}p\,\exp\Big[-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta\big(x_{i}/L,\theta_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})\big)\Big].

(20)

Here
L
is the macroscopic scale, and
\beta(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
is a generalised chemical potential function. One may put sharp boundaries at
\pm L/2
, thus bounding a volume
L
where the system lies, by choosing
\beta(x,\theta)=\infty
for
|x|\geq 1/2
. A slowly-varying potential, which varies on large scales
L
, is obtained by choosing
\beta(x,\theta)
to be, say, smooth in
x
. The specific free energy in infinite volumes is

f=\lim_{L\to\infty}f_{L}.

(21)

The form (
20
) is the grand-canonical partition function for the Boltzmann weight

\exp\Big[-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta\big(x_{i}/L,\theta_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})\big)\Big]=\exp\Big[-\int{\rm d}x{\rm d}\theta\,\beta\big(x/L,\theta\big)q_{\theta}(x)\Big]

where the continuum of local densities
q_{\theta}(x)
is involved.

In integrable models, one expects
f
to be given by a combination of the local density approximation (LDA) and the TBA (if
\beta(x,\theta)
is constant in
x
except for a bounding volume, then no LDA is involved); and for
L
finite, one does not expect any simple expression for
f_{L}
. For short we refer to “local TBA” the combination of the LDA and TBA. As far as we are aware, the only rigorous results showing the local TBA for a large family of functions
\beta(x,\theta)
in an interacting integrable system is that of
[
49
,
50
]
for the hard rods; this also gives the only known result for a TBA-like equation at
L
finite.

In our family of models, we will show that
the specific free energy
f_{L}
satisfies equations of TBA type
; this is expressed in Theorem
4.4
. This is surprising, as it applies to the full generality of our model, hence of the two-body scattering shift. We believe this is a more general phenomenon in integrable systems.

Further, we will show that the local TBA indeed describes
f
. The requirements of the following theorem admit a wide range of possible generalised chemical potential functions, with or without sharp boundaries.

Theorem 2.3

(Thermodynamic Bethe ansatz)
.

Assume that
e^{-\beta(x,\theta)}
is
x,\theta
-uniformly continuous in
x
for a.e.
(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, and that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta\,\varphi(\theta)=\varphi_{\rm tot}<\infty,\quad\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}{\rm d}x{\rm d}\theta\,e^{-\beta(x,\theta)}=F<\infty,\quad\sup_{(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}e^{-\beta(x,\theta)}=e^{-\beta_{-}}<\infty

(22)

and

e^{-\beta_{-}}\varphi_{\rm tot}<\pi\big(\sqrt{1+4e^{-1}}-1\big)=1.797324....<2\pi e^{-1}.

(23)

Then the free energy at infinite volume,
f
, exists and is given by

f=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\frac{{\rm d}x{\rm d}\theta}{2\pi}\,e^{-\varepsilon(x,\theta)}

(24)

where
\varepsilon(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}
is the unique solution to

\varepsilon(x,\theta)=\beta(x,\theta)-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}}{2\pi}\,\varphi(\theta-\theta^{\prime})e^{-\varepsilon(x,\theta^{\prime})}\qquad\mbox{a.e. }x\in{\mathbb{R}},\quad\mbox{all }\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(25)

Thus, the free energy takes the local thermodynamic Bethe ansatz form, with Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics and two-body scattering shift
\varphi(\theta-\theta^{\prime})
(Definition
3.13
). Further, we have the bounds

-\frac{F}{2\pi}\frac{e^{-\beta_{-}}\varphi_{\rm tot}}{2\pi e^{-1}-e^{-\beta_{-}}\varphi_{\rm tot}}\leq f\leq 0,\qquad e^{-\varepsilon(x,\theta)}\varphi_{\rm tot}<2\pi\quad\forall\;x,\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(26)

(See the full statements in Theorem
4.5
).

Remark 2.3
.

Note that the Boltzmann weight in (
20
) is the most general form of the generalised Gibbs ensembles (GGEs)
[
51
,
52
,
35
]
. A sum of conventional local conserved densities in the exponent,
\sum_{a}\beta_{a}(x)q_{a}(x)
, as is usually taken for GGEs, is obtained again by taking
\beta(x,\theta)=\sum_{a}\beta_{a}(x)\theta^{a}
as a polynomial in
\theta
.

3
Properties of classical Hamiltonian many-particle systems

In this section, we gather the basic definitions of the important concepts about classical many-body dynamics that are referred to in Theorems
2.1
,
2.2
and
2.3
. These concepts – Liouville integrability and finite-range interactions, elastic and factorised scattering,
T\bar{T}
-deformations, and the thermodynamic Bethe ansatz – are well known in many-particle integrable systems. However it is convenient here to formalise them, in a way that is physically sensible and at least broadly in agreement with previous investigations. This will make our main statements entirely unambiguous.

We use the notation
\mathbb{I}_{n}=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, as well as
\bm{x}=(x_{i})_{i=1,\ldots,n}
and
\bm{x}_{I}=(x_{i})_{i\in I}
for subsets
I
(with the understanding that the ordering is in increasing order of the indices within
I
). We take the physical phase space to be
{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}
, with coordinates
(\bm{x},\bm{p})
and canonical Poisson structure

\{x_{i},x_{j}\}=0,\quad\{p_{i},p_{j}\}=0,\quad\{x_{i},p_{j}\}=\delta_{ij}\qquad(i,j\in\mathbb{I}_{N}).

(27)

A Hamiltonian
H\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2N})
generates the dynamics in the usual way,

\dot{x}_{i}=\{x_{i},H(\bm{x},\bm{p})\},\quad\dot{p}_{i}=\{p_{i},H(\bm{x},\bm{p})\}.

(28)

The trajectories are fully determined once the initial conditions
(\bm{x}(0),\bm{p}(0))
are fixed.

A
many-particle system
should be understood not as a single Hamiltonian
H
, but as a sequence of Hamiltonians, for
N=1,2,3,\ldots
. That is, the phase space is the direct sum of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}
for
N=1,2,3,\ldots
. This is important in order to be able to take the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
and consider aspects of locality, and will be implicitly understood below. For this sequence to represent a “unique” many-particle system, interaction potentials in the sequence should be related to each other in appropriate ways. We discuss such relations in the next subsection.

In order to keep the general definitions as targeted as possible to the models of interest,
we restrict ourselves to many-particle systems that are invariant under re-labelling of the particles
. That, is the Hamiltonian is assumed to have the symmetry

H(\bm{x},\bm{p})=H(\bm{x}_{\sigma},\bm{p}_{\sigma})

(29)

where
(\bm{x}_{\sigma})_{i}=x_{\sigma(i)}
, etc., and
\sigma
is any permutation of
N
elements. This means that the interaction only depends on the positions and momenta of the particles, not on their labels. Most definitions are easily extended to situations where the particle labels matter.

Remark 3.1
.

In general, the phase space may be a subset
\mathcal{S}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}
, invariant under the dynamics. A typical nontrivial example, where particle labels matter, is the “ordered” phase space,
\mathcal{S}=\{(\bm{x},\bm{p})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}:x_{i+1}-x_{i}>a\;\forall\;i\}
for some
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
, occurring with interactions that forbid particles to pass through each other. We keep
\mathcal{S}={\mathbb{R}}^{2N}
for simplicity, as this is the case needed here.

3.1
Liouville integrability and finite-range interactions

Recall that a
N
-particle Hamiltonian
H
is
Liouville integrable
(or Liouville-Arnold integrable) if there exists
N
functionally independent non-constant differentiable functions (one often assumes them to be smooth)
Q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p})
,
a=1,2,\ldots,N
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}
such that
\{Q_{a},Q_{b}\}=0
for all
a,b
, and
H=\sum_{a}c_{a}Q_{a}
for some constants
c_{a}
(not all zero). These are the conserved quantities.

For a many-particle system with unbounded trajectories, however, in order to have integrability one must impose additionally a short-range constraint on the interactions associated to the conserved quantities; here we restrict ourselves to a stronger finite-range constraint. In discussing the notion of interaction, there is an understanding that we separate the Hamiltonian into a non-interacting “kinetic part”, defined via a dispersion relation
E\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
(independent of
N
) as
\sum_{i}E(p_{i})
, plus a quasi-potential
V\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2N})
(dependent on
N
; recall that it is “quasi” as we admit dependence on momenta). The finite-range constraint we propose, with respect to a choice of dispersion relation, is a precise clustering condition for the quasi-potentials of each conserved quantity, that represents their finite-range interactions. We show how it implies good locality properties of conserved densities and currents. We will refer to this concept as
Liouville integrability with finite-range interaction
.

In order to express the clustering property of the quasi-potential, we need the following. For every
\epsilon>0
and
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{{\mathbb{R}}}^{N}
, we define the unique partition
P_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})=\{I_{1},I_{2},\ldots,I_{n}\}
of
\mathbb{I}_{N}
(i.e.
I_{r}\cap I_{s}=\emptyset\;\forall\;r\neq s,\ \cup_{r}I_{r}=\mathbb{I}_{N}
) into “clusters”: the partition with the smallest
\sum_{r}|I_{r}|
such that
|x_{i}-x_{j}|>\epsilon\;\forall\;i\in I_{r},\;j\in I_{s},\;r\neq s
. Jumping on the particles from the left-most to the right-most (say), one gathers them into clusters, a different cluster being made every time a distance larger than
\epsilon
is jumped. Thus
\bm{x}_{I}
, for every
I\in P_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})
, is a cluster of positions so that each nearest neighbours are a distance
\epsilon
or less from each other, and that any two different clusters are a distance larger than
\epsilon
from each other.

Definition 3.2

(Finite-range Liouville integrable system)
.

1.

A quasi-potential
V
has finite range
\epsilon>0
if it satisfies the following clustering condition:
For every
n\in\mathbb{I}_{N}
there exists
V^{(n)}\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, such that:

V(\bm{x},\bm{p})=\sum_{I\in P_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})}V^{(|I|)}(\bm{x}_{I},\bm{p}_{I})\quad\forall\ (\bm{x},\bm{p})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}.

(30)

Further,
V^{(1)}(x,p)
is a constant (which we set to
0
without loss of generality).

2.

A quantity
Q\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2N})
has interaction of finite-range
\epsilon>0
with respect to a dispersion relation
E(p)
if it can be written in the form

Q(\bm{x},\bm{p})=\sum_{i}E(p_{i})+V(\bm{x},\bm{p})

(31)

for some quasi-potential
V(\bm{x},\bm{p})
of finite range
\epsilon
.

3.

The many-particle system with Hamiltonian
H
(more precisely, with sequence of
H
’s for
N=1,2,\ldots
) is Liouville integrable with finite-range interaction if
H
is Liouville integrable for every
N
, and if there are
E_{a}\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}),\,a=1,2,\ldots
(all independent of
N
) such that
\sum_{i=1}^{N}E_{a}(p_{i}),\,a=1,2,\ldots,N
are functionally independent, and such that the conserved quantities

Q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p})=\sum_{i}E_{a}(p_{i})+V_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p}),\quad a=1,2,\ldots

(32)

have uniformly (over
N
) finite-range interactions with respect to the
E_{a}
’s. That is, all
V_{a}
’s satisfy (
30
) for a single
\epsilon>0
and where the
V^{(n)}_{a}
’s do not depend on
N
.

Note that, with
N
particles, the quantities
Q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p})
are functionally independent for
a=1,2,\ldots,N
, as when coordinates are well separated, by clustering of the quasi-potential the resulting functions are
\sum_{i}E_{a}(p_{i})
, which are functionally independent by assumption. Also, as we restrict to systems invariant under re-labelling, Eq. (
29
),
Q_{a}
’s, hence
V_{a}^{(n)}
’s, are invariant.

Our finite-range definition means that if particles’ positions cluster into separate groups, with different clusters separated by distances of more than
\epsilon
, then the interaction, for every conserved charge, separates into a sum of terms, one for each cluster. Thus, particles
i
and
j
do not interact with each other if they are a distance greater than
\epsilon
from each other, and there is no chain of particles, each a distance
\epsilon
or less to the next on the chain, that connects particles
i
with
j
. Definition
3.2
makes it clear how a sequence of
H
, for
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, consistently corresponds to a single many-particle system: the system is defined by a unique set of
E_{a}
and of
V_{a}^{(n)}
for
a,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and a unique
\epsilon
.

For simplicity, we concentrate from now on on kinetic parts with
polynomial form
: we assume that
E_{a}(p)=p^{a}
, that is

Q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p})=\sum_{i}p_{i}^{a}+V_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p}),\quad a=0,1,2,\ldots.

(33)

This is well adapted to the Galilean dispersion relation, on which we focus; it is a simple matter to adapt the discussion in this paper to other functions
E_{a}(p)
. Note that we added for convenience the index
a=0
, with
V_{0}=0
and
Q_{0}=N
. The Hamiltonian is taken as

H=\frac{1}{2}Q_{2}=\sum_{i}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2}+V(\bm{x},\bm{p})

(34)

where
V=\frac{1}{2}V_{2}
.

Remark 3.3
.

In fact, the condition (
30
) says a bit more than the interaction being finite-range: it says that the particle labelling is not important (as all restricted quasi-potentials are the same) and that there is no “external force” acting on single particles (as
V^{(1)}(x,p)
is a constant). These are for simplicity, and clearly not essential for the interaction between particles to be considered finite-range. One may also extend this definition to short- / long-range interactions, where corrections to quasi-potential clustering are exponentially / algebraically decaying with the distances between the clusters
I_{r}
. The above is sufficient for our purposes.

3.1.1
Conserved densities and currents

In order to understand the meaning of finite-range interactions, and physically justify our definition, it is helpful to see how it allows us to construct a
charge density and a current
with good locality properties, for a charge
Q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p})
. Charge densities and currents will play an important role in the description of
T\bar{T}
-deformations.

A charge density for the conserved quantity
Q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p})
is a distribution-valued phase-space function, which we denote by
q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)
, such that

\int{\rm d}x\,q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)=Q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p}).

(35)

It is required that
q_{a}
be a
C^{1}
distribution-valued function, by which we mean that for any Schwartz function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, the resulting
(\bm{x},\bm{p})\mapsto\int{\rm d}x\,q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)f(x)
is in
C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2N})
. A current associated to the charge density is also a distribution-valued phase-space function, such that the following conservation law

\{q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x),H\}+\partial_{x}j_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)=0

(36)

holds in the distributional sense – integrated against any Schwartz function.

If
Q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p})
has finite-range interaction, then one may define
q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)
and
j_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)
as follows. For every
n
, choose quasi-potentials
V^{(n,r)}\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n}),\;r=1,\ldots,n
such that
V^{(n)}=\sum_{r=1}^{n}V^{(n,r)}
. This should be interpreted as a separation of the quasi-potential
V^{(n)}(\bm{x}_{\mathbb{I}_{n}},\bm{p}_{\mathbb{I}_{n}})
into the individual contributions of the particles
r=1,2,\ldots,n
; but here it is just a general construction. With re-labelling invariance (
29
), one may additionally ask

V^{(n,\sigma(r))}(\bm{x}_{\mathbb{I}_{n}},\bm{p}_{\mathbb{I}_{n}})=V^{(n,r)}((\bm{x}_{\mathbb{I}_{n}})_{\sigma},(\bm{p}_{\mathbb{I}_{n}})_{\sigma})

(37)

for all
n
-element permutations
\sigma
, although this is not required for the general construction. In constructing conserved densities for tracer dynamics below, we will make a particular physically sensible choice which satisfies (
37
).

Then, given such separations of the quasi-potentials into individual particle contributions, we define

q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(x-x_{i})\Big(p_{i}^{a}+\sum_{I\in P_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})}\sum_{r=1}^{|I|}V^{(|I|,r)}(\bm{x}_{I},\bm{p}_{I})\delta_{i,I(r)}\Big)

(38)

where
I(r)
is the
r^{\rm th}
element of the index subset
I
(in increasing order of indices). This reproduces (
35
). Note that for every
\bm{x},\bm{p}
(and every
N
), the resulting
q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)
is a finite sum of Dirac delta functions supported on
x_{i}
’s. Further, writing

A_{a,i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})=p_{i}^{a}+\sum_{I\in P_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})}\sum_{r=1}^{|I|}V^{(|I|,r)}(\bm{x}_{I},\bm{p}_{I})\delta_{i,I(r)},

we have
q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(x-x_{i})A_{a,i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})
and the current can be defined as

j_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(\dot{x}_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})\delta(x-x_{i})A_{a,i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})-\Theta(x-x_{i})\dot{A}_{a,i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})\Big)

(39)

where
\Theta
is Heaviside’s step function and
\dot{A}_{a,i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})=\{A_{a,i}(\bm{x},\bm{p}),H\}
. This satisfies the continuity relation (
36
).

The important aspect of this (somewhat formal) construction is that both the densities and currents have “good” locality properties. In order to express these, we define

\ell_{\epsilon}:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\ :\ \ell_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})=\max(\epsilon|I|\,:\,I\in P_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})).

(40)

This is the largest distance that can separate two positions that pertain to the same cluster, over all available clusters. Thus
\ell_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})
is the
effective interaction range
of the configuration
\bm{x}
, given an interaction of finite range
\epsilon
: it is the maximal distance over which there can be interaction between two particles.

Lemma 3.1

(Finite-range properties of densities and currents)
.

In the distributional sense,

\{q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x),q_{b}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x^{\prime})\}=\{q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x),j_{b}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x^{\prime})\}=\{j_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x),j_{b}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x^{\prime})\}=0

(41)

for every
a,b
, and every
x,x^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
|x-x^{\prime}|>4\ell_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})
. For the equations involving currents to make sense, we require additionally that
j_{a}
be a
C^{1}
distribution-valued function; this holds in particular if all quasi-potentials are in
C^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2N})
.

Proof.

What we show is that the conserved densities
q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)
do not depend on
x_{i},p_{i}
for all
i,x
such that
|x_{i}-x|>\ell_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})
, and the conserved currents
j_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)
do not depend on
x_{i},p_{i}
for any
i
such that
|x_{i}-x|>2\ell_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})
. For the densities, the statement is clear from (
38
). For the currents, because of the finite-range form of
H
, the first term inside the sum in (
39
) depends only on particles (that is, on their positions and momenta) whose positions are within a distance
\ell_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})
of
x
. Likewise, for the second term, the quantity
\dot{A}_{a,i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})
depends only on particles whose positions are within a distance
2\ell_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})
from
x_{i}
. As
\sum_{i}\dot{A}_{a,i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})=\dot{Q}_{a}=0
, then
\sum_{i:x_{i}\leq x}\dot{A}_{a,i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})=-\sum_{i:x_{i}>x}\dot{A}_{a,i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})
. By using either the left or right-hand side of this equality, one concludes that
\sum_{i:x_{i}\leq x}\dot{A}_{a,i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})
must depend only on particles whose positions are within a distance
2\ell_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})
from
x
.

The above allows us to consider the system for infinitely-many particles. A configuration for infinitely-many particles is “good” if
|\{I\in P_{\epsilon}(\bm{x}):I\subset[-x,x]\}|
increases unboundedly as
x\to\infty
(that is, the number of pairs of neighbouring particles a distance larger than
\epsilon
from each other, lying within
[-x,x]
, increases unboundedly with
x
). For many-particle systems that are Liouville integrable with finite-range interactions, densities and currents of conserved quantities Poisson-commute at large enough distances in any good configuration, and time evolution – at least for small enough times – is well defined. One would expect good configurations to stay good in time, and to arise almost surely in thermodynamic states. This justifies our definition of finite-range interactions.

3.2
Elastic, factorised scattering and tracer dynamics

Another important property of many-particle integrable systems on the line is that scattering processes are elastic and factorised. We provide here a precise definition of this property, in the simplest cases. The elastic, factorised scattering condition below is a formalisation of a standard concept, but the property of the dynamics being a
tracer dynamics
is something which, to our knowledge, has not been fully appreciated before.

Recall that
{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}_{\neq}:=\{(\bm{y},\bm{\theta})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}:\theta_{i}\neq\theta_{j}\;\forall\;i\neq j\}
. Consider a function
\varphi\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
.

Definition 3.4
.

We say that the many-body system
H
satisfies the elastic, factorised scattering condition, with two-body scattering shift
\varphi(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
, if for every
N
, there exists a dense subspace
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}
invariant under time evolution, such that for every initial condition
(\bm{x}(0),\,\bm{p}(0))\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
: (1) the trajectories become linear with non-coinciding asymptotic momenta
\theta_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
\theta_{i}\neq\theta_{j}
for
i\neq j
and “impact parameters”
x_{i}^{\pm}\in{\mathbb{R}}
at large times,

p_{i}(t)=\theta_{\sigma^{\pm}(i)}+o(1),\qquad x_{i}(t)=\theta_{\sigma^{\pm}(i)}t+x_{\sigma^{\pm}(i)}^{\pm}+o(1)\qquad(t\to\pm\infty)

(42)

where
\sigma^{-}(i)=i
(this just defines the
\theta_{i}
’s) and
\sigma^{+}=:\sigma
is a permutation of
N
elements; (2) the impact parameters are related by a sum of individual two-body shifts,

x_{i}^{+}-x_{i}^{-}=\sum_{j\neq i}\sgn(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i})\varphi(\theta_{i},\theta_{j});

(43)

and (3) the map
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2N}_{\neq}:(\bm{x}(0),\,\bm{p}(0))\mapsto(\bm{x}^{-},\bm{\theta})
is a bijection. We say, in addition, that
H
is a tracer dynamics if for every
N
,
\sigma={\bf 1}
(the identity permutation) for all
(\bm{x}(0),\,\bm{p}(0))\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.

Here, elastic scattering is the statement that it is the same set of momenta
\{\bm{\theta}\}
that occurs both at negative and positive infinite times – thus a permutation
\sigma
exists. Factorised scattering is the statement (
43
): for the case
N=2
, this fixes
\varphi(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
as the shift incurred by particle 1 upon scattering with particle 2, and for
N>2
this indicates that the total shift of particle
i
is obtained by summing over the two-body shifts that would be incurred by independent two-body collisions with other particles. In the case of a tracer dynamics, each trajectory
x_{i}(t)
is associated with a unique asymptotic momentum
\theta_{i}
– thus the trajectory is a “tracer” of this asymptotic momentum, and effectively carries the information of the asymptotic momentum even within configurations where particles are not well separated and their momenta are very different from the asymptotic momenta.

Note that the bijection condition says in particular that the asymptotic parameters
(\bm{x}^{-},\bm{\theta})
can be chosen arbitrarily in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}_{\neq}
. These can be seen as alternative initial conditions, which fix the scattering problem by fixing the asymptotic trajectories as
t\to-\infty
. Then,
\sigma
and
\bm{x}^{+}
are determined by the dynamics.

The coordinates
\bm{x}^{-},\bm{\theta}
are canonical. Indeed, the canonical Poisson bracket is preserved by time evolution, thus we just have to look at the asymptotic expansion at large times of
\{x_{i}(t),x_{j}(t)\}=0,\ \{p_{i}(t),p_{j}(t)\}=0
and
\{x_{i}(t),p_{j}(t)\}=\delta_{i,j}
. Further, they evolve trivially under the dynamics:

\theta_{i}(t)=\theta_{i}(0),\quad x^{-}_{i}(t)=\theta_{i}t+x^{-}_{i}(0)

(44)

as is immediate from (
42
).

Note that if
\varphi(\theta,\theta^{\prime})>0
then the scattering shifts correspond to
positive time delays
at head-on collisions. In particular, in the geometric interpretation of scattering (see Subsection
2.1
and discussions in
[
43
,
45
]
), this corresponds to an addition of effective space by the amount
\varphi(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
, as perceived by a particle with asymptotic momentum
\theta
, in the presence of a particle with asymptotic momentum
\theta^{\prime}
.

Remark 3.5
.

Following Remark
3.1
, one may extend this definition to phase spaces
\mathcal{S}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}
, with
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{S}
a dense subset. Also, in certain problems it may be more appropriate to consider different asymptotic in-spaces
\mathcal{S}^{-}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}_{\neq}
, with
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{S}^{-}
being a bijection. In any case, the asymptotic in-space must satisfy
\mathcal{S}^{-}=\{(\bm{y}+t\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}):(\bm{y},\bm{\theta})\in\mathcal{S}^{-},\,t\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
.

Remark 3.6
.

It is of course simple to extend to many particle species. Further, the above defines an “asymptotically free” elastic, factorised scattering dynamics, as we do not allow bound states. In dynamics where bound states can form, the bijection condition would be broken, and one would have to extend the space of asymptotic particles to include bound states. Alternatively, an appropriate choice of phase space
\mathcal{S}
may be possible to avoid the formation of bound state. Naturally, scattering is asymptotically free if the interaction between particles is repulsive. For simplicity we concentrate on asymptotically free scattering.

3.2.1
Finite-range tracer dynamics: conserved densities for asymptotic momenta

One expects a close relation between elastic, factorised scattering and finite-range Liouville integrability. Let us discuss briefly three relations; the third is of most interest here.

From finite-range Liouville integrability to elastic, factorised scattering.
Finite-range Liouville integrability can be expected to guarantee elastic, factorised scattering. This is because of the argument
[
20
,
22
]
that conserved charges
Q_{a}
can be used to shift trajectories in such a way that particles only interact in well separated two-body processes. It should be straightforward to make this rigorous, assuming some basic description of asymptotic states; this is beyond the scope of this paper.

Finite-range Liouville integrability and elastic, factorised scattering: charges in terms of asymptotic momenta.
If
H
satisfies the elastic, factorised scattering condition, then the following distribution-valued functions of phase space (for distributions in
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
) are independent of time:

Q_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p})=\sum_{i}\delta(\theta-\theta_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})).

(45)

In particular, one may construct the Liouville conserved quantities as

Q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p})=\int{\rm d}\theta\,\theta^{a}Q_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p})=\sum_{i}\theta_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})^{a}.

(46)

If
H
is in addition Liouville integrable with finite-range interaction, then the
Q_{a}
’s in the form (
33
) agree with (
46
) at least on the dense subspace
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}
. Indeed, they are constant in time, so we may evaluate them on
(\bm{x},\bm{p})\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
evolved to any finite but large enough time; the scattering condition (
42
) and the finite-range property of the quasi-potentials (Definition
3.2
) imply that they take the form (
46
). This implies strong short-range constraints on
\sum_{i}\theta_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})^{a}
. Combining with the previous discussion, we see that short-range Liouville integrability along with some basic description of asymptotic states should give much structure to the scattering theory; again this is beyond the scope of this work.

From elastic, factorised scattering to finite-range Liouville integrability.
Can we go in the opposite direction? Assuming
H
satisfies the elastic, factorised scattering condition, and perhaps that it has a finite-range potential, can we deduce finite-range Liouville integrability? Do the
Q_{a}
’s defined in (
46
) have finite-range interactions? These are interesting questions that we cannot address here. However, with an additional condition on the scattering,
finite-range tracer dynamics
, we can indeed answer positively, as we now explain.

Definition 3.7
.

A many-particle system
H
that satisfies the elastic, factorised scattering condition and that is a tracer dynamics (Definition
3.4
) is a finite-range tracer dynamics with interaction range
\epsilon>0
, if for every
n\geq 0
and
r=0,1,\ldots,n
there is
\theta^{(n,r)}(\bm{x}_{\mathbb{I}_{n}},\bm{p}_{\mathbb{I}_{n}})
in
C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, such that for every
(\bm{x},\bm{p})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}
,
I\in P_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})
and
r=0,1,\ldots,|I|
, we have
\theta_{I(r)}(\bm{x},\bm{p})=\theta^{(|I|,r)}(\bm{x}_{I},\bm{p}_{I})
.

Recall that
I(r)
is the
r^{\rm th}
element of the index subset
I
(in increasing order of the indices), and that
P_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})
is the partition into clusters of interacting particles, defined in the paragraph above Definition
3.2
.

The definition means that the information of the asymptotic momentum for particle
i
is already included within the interacting cluster to which this particle belongs – there is no need for the information about other particles (with which, of course, particle
i
will nevertheless interact in passing from time 0 to asymptotic times). Further, the information of the label of the particle is also irrelevant. In fact, as we restrict to systems invariant under re-labelling, Eq. (
29
), we have the re-labelling invariance

\theta^{(n,\sigma(r))}(\bm{x}_{\mathbb{I}_{n}},\bm{p}_{\mathbb{I}_{n}})=\theta^{(n,r)}((\bm{x}_{\mathbb{I}_{n}})_{\sigma},(\bm{p}_{\mathbb{I}_{n}})_{\sigma}).

(47)

If
H
is a finite-range tracer dynamics, then it is easy to show that it is Liouville integrable with finite-range interaction. Indeed, one can choose, in Definition
3.2
,

V^{(|I|)}_{a}(\bm{x}_{I},\bm{p}_{I})=\sum_{i\in I}(\theta_{i}^{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p})-p_{i}^{a})

(48)

and all charges (
46
) now have the form (
33
), with finite-range interactions. One can also construct the conserved densities and currents (see (
38
), (
39
)). Making the choice
V_{a}^{(n,r)}(\bm{x}_{\mathbb{I}_{n}},\bm{p}_{\mathbb{I}_{n}})=\big(\theta^{(n,r)}(\bm{x}_{\mathbb{I}_{n}},\bm{p}_{\mathbb{I}_{n}})\big)^{a}-p_{r}^{a}
which satisfies (
37
), we have

q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(x-x_{i})\theta_{i}^{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p}),\quad j_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\dot{x}_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})\delta(x-x_{i})\theta_{i}^{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p});

(49)

these have the locality properties stated at the end of Subsection
3.1
. Of course, one can construct a “continuum” of conserved densities and currents: these are functions of phase space valued in distributions over
(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, defined as

q_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)=\sum_{i}\delta(x-x_{i})\delta(\theta-\theta_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})),\quad j_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)=\sum_{i}\dot{x}_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})\delta(x-x_{i})\delta(\theta-\theta_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})).

(50)

They satisfy
\{q_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x),H\}+\partial_{x}j_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)=0
in the distributional sense, and we recover
q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)=\int{\rm d}\theta\,\theta^{a}q_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)
, etc. Further, by Lemma
3.1
,

\{q_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x),q_{\theta^{\prime}}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x^{\prime})\}=\{q_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x),j_{\theta^{\prime}}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x^{\prime})\}=\{j_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x),j_{\theta^{\prime}}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x^{\prime})\}=0

(51)

for
|x-x^{\prime}|>4\ell_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})
, again in the distributional sense and with the additional differentiability properties for the currents (as in Lemma
3.1
).

3.3
Generalised
T\bar{T}
-deformations

We discussed
T\bar{T}
-deformations in Subsection
2.2
. Here we provide the formal definitions.

It is convenient to start with a formalisation of the standard
T\bar{T}
-deformations, for arbitrary local densities and currents. This only requires finite-range Liouville integrability (in fact, only at least one pair of local conserved density and current is required to exist):

Definition 3.8
.

For every
\lambda\in[0,1]
, let the many-body system
H_{\lambda}
be Liouville integrable with finite-range interaction, and assume that
H_{\lambda}(\bm{x},\bm{p})
is differentiable in
\lambda
for all
(\bm{x},\bm{p})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}
(for every
N
). We say that
H_{1}
is a
T\bar{T}
-deformation of
H_{0}
if there exists finitely many functions
w_{a,b}\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
such that for every
N
,
(\bm{x},\bm{p})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

\frac{\partial H_{\lambda}(\bm{x},\bm{p})}{\partial\lambda}=\sum_{a,b}\int{\rm d}y{\rm d}z\,w_{a,b}(y-z)\big(q^{\lambda}_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};y)j^{\lambda}_{b}(\bm{x},\bm{p};z)-j^{\lambda}_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};y)q^{\lambda}_{b}(\bm{x},\bm{p};z)\big).

(52)

Here and below, we use the upper index
\lambda
in order to represent phase-space functions that are constructed from the Hamiltonian
H_{\lambda}
. We note that the integrals on
y
and
z
can be evaluated as explicit phase space functions using the definitions (
38
), (
39
). Eq. (
52
) defines a flow of Hamiltonians
[0,1]\ni\lambda\mapsto H_{\lambda}
, the
T\bar{T}
-deformation of
H_{0}
to
H_{1}
. In particular, the right-hand side of (
52
) is in general a non-linear functional of
H_{\lambda}
.

More interesting is the generalised
T\bar{T}
deformation proposed in
[
13
]
. We now have the right framework in order to define it rigorously: that of finite-range tracer dynamics.

Definition 3.9
.

In the context of Definition
3.8
, let each many-body system
H_{\lambda}
satisfy the elastic, factorised scattering property, and be a finite-range tracer dynamics, Definitions
3.4
and
3.7
. Let
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}^{\prime}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}
be the dense subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}
involved in these definitions. Assume that
H_{\lambda}(\bm{x},\bm{p})
is differentiable in
\lambda
for all
(\bm{x},\bm{p})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}
(and every
N
). We say that
H_{1}
is a generalised
T\bar{T}
-deformation of
H_{0}
if there exists a function
w\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
such that for every
N
,
(\bm{x},\bm{p})\in\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}^{\prime}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H_{\lambda}(\bm{x},\bm{p})}{\partial\lambda}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}\theta{\rm d}\alpha{\rm d}y{\rm d}z\,w(y-z,\theta-\alpha)\big(q^{\lambda}_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p};y)j^{\lambda}_{\alpha}(\bm{x},\bm{p};z)-j^{\lambda}_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p};y)q^{\lambda}_{\alpha}(\bm{x},\bm{p};z)\big)

(53)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{ij}\big(\dot{x}^{\lambda}_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})-\dot{x}^{\lambda}_{j}(\bm{x},\bm{p})\big)\,w\big(x_{i}-x_{j},\theta^{\lambda}_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})-\theta^{\lambda}_{j}(\bm{x},\bm{p})\big).

Here we have explicitly evaluated the integrals in the generalised
T\bar{T}
-deformation as a function on phase space, as densities and currents take a simple form for finite-range tracer dynamics, Eq. (
50
). Note how, according to our definitions, the deformation equation (
53
) makes sense on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\lambda}
, but as this is a dense subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}
, this is a strong enough constraint on the family of Hamiltonians.

Remark 3.10
.

In relation to Remark
3.1
, in general we may need to consider a
\lambda
-dependent physical phase space
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}
. For simplicity we assume that the physical phase space is not affected by the
T\bar{T}
-deformation, which is sufficient for our models, but may not always be the case more generally.

3.4
Thermodynamic Bethe ansatz

In order to understand the thermodynamics of a system of particles, one may evaluate the specific free energy (per unit volume). In integrable systems, where one considers the generalised free energy, involving all conserved densities, this takes a very special form. The form goes by the name of thermodynamic Bethe ansatz (TBA) – despite the term “Bethe ansatz” in it, which reminds one of quantum models, this form is valid more generally (see e.g.
[
22
]
).

For a Liouville integrable system of with finite-range interactions, the generalised partition function in the finite volume
[-L/2,L/2]
is defined by

Z_{L}=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N}N!}\int_{[-L/2,L/2]^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{j}{\rm d}p_{j}\,e^{-\sum_{a=0}^{b}\beta_{a}Q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p})}.

(54)

Q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p})
,
a=0,1,2,\ldots
are defined in (
33
) and
\beta_{a}\in{\mathbb{R}}
are chemical potentials for the conserved charges
Q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p})
. Note how the factor
1/N!
is natural under our assumption that the charges are invariant under re-labelling. As the integrands and each term in the series are non-negative,
Z_{L}
always converges absolutely, but we will require that it converges to a finite value. The finite-volume specific free energy is

f_{L}=-L^{-1}\log Z_{L}

(55)

and the specific free energy is

f=\lim_{L\to\infty}f_{L}

(56)

if the limit exists.

We now formalise what it means for the specific free energy to take the TBA form.

Definition 3.11
.

Let a many-body system be Liouville integrable with finite-range interaction, Definition
3.2
, and satisfy the elastic, factorised scattering property, Definition
3.4
. The specific free energy associated to (
54
), i.e. Eq. (
56
) with (
55
), is said to take the TBA form for the free energy function
\mathsf{F}(\varepsilon)
and the two-body scattering shift
\varphi(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
, if the partition function (
54
) converges for all
L>0
, and the limit (
56
) exists and is given by

f=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{{\rm d}\theta}{2\pi}\,\mathsf{F}(\varepsilon(\theta)),

(57)

where the pseudo-energy
\varepsilon(\theta)
solves the integral equation

\varepsilon(\theta)=\sum_{a=0}^{b}\beta_{a}\theta^{a}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}}{2\pi}\,\varphi(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\mathsf{F}(\varepsilon(\theta^{\prime})),\qquad\mbox{a.e. }\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(58)

The free energy function may take different forms depending on the particle statistics of the system
[
22
]
. For classical particles, Eq. (
54
), it is natural to expect
\mathsf{F}(\varepsilon)=-e^{-\varepsilon}
, but this has to be proven. One may consider a more general definition where, in (
57
), the measure is
{\rm d}p(\theta)
for a given momentum function
p(\theta)
(for instance, in relativistic models one takes
p(\theta)=\sinh(\theta)
); here, in agreement with our setup, the momentum function is simply taken as
p(\theta)=\theta
.

The interpretation of (
58
) is clear: the specific free energy takes the form of that for free particles with contribution from particle
\theta
to the “energy” (negative logarithm of the Boltzmann weight) given by
\varepsilon(\theta)
. This contribution is the bare “energy”, modified because the presence of a particle
\theta
effectively changes, in the geometric interpretation of scattering, the volume perceived by all particles
\theta^{\prime}
; the free energy gained by this change of volume must be added.

In this definition, we do not specify if the solution to (
58
) is unique. Implied in the definition is that both
\mathsf{F}(\varepsilon(\theta))
and
\varphi(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\mathsf{F}(\varepsilon(\theta^{\prime}))
(for all
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}
) are Lebesgue measurable with finite integrals, and that the set of measure zero where (
58
) might not hold, does not contribute to the integrals.

Of course, in Eq. (
54
), one may in principle put infinitely many charges,
b=\infty
. This brings the problem of convergence of the sum, which we will not discuss here in the level of generality of Definition
3.11
. Instead, we extend Definition
3.11
in the following three natural directions.

Extension: tracer dynamics.
First, if the system is a finite-range tracer dynamics, Definition
3.7
, then one may consider more generally the partition function

Z_{L}=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N}N!}\int_{[-L/2,L/2]^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{j}{\rm d}p_{j}\,e^{-\int{\rm d}\theta\,\beta(\theta)Q_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p})}.

(59)

Here
Q_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p})
is defined in (
45
). As
\int{\rm d}\theta\,\beta(\theta)Q_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p})=\sum_{i}\beta(\theta_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{p}))
, it is sufficient for
e^{-\beta(\theta)}\in[0,\infty)
to be Lebesgue measurable on
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
for the integrals to make sense. Note that we admit positive infinite values for
\beta(\theta)
.

Definition 3.12
.

In the setup of Definition
3.11
, assume that the system is, in addition, a finite-range tracer dynamics, Definition
3.7
. Paralleling Definition
3.11
, the specific free energy associated to (
59
) is said to take the TBA form if

f=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{{\rm d}\theta}{2\pi}\,\mathsf{F}(\varepsilon(\theta)),

(60)

where the pseudo-energy
\varepsilon(\theta)
solves the integral equation

\varepsilon(\theta)=\beta(\theta)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}}{2\pi}\,\varphi(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\mathsf{F}(\varepsilon(\theta^{\prime})),\qquad\mbox{a.e. }\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(61)

For convergence to finite values, typically
\beta(\theta)
must increase in
|\theta|
sufficiently fast; although the precise constraints depend on the model. The case (
54
) is recovered by choosing
\beta(\theta)=\sum_{a=0}^{b}\beta_{a}\theta^{a}
. The partition function (
59
) is the more accurate definition of the case
b=\infty
: there is no need for
\beta(\theta)
to have a Taylor series expansion.

Extension: local density approximation.
Second, of much interest is to take the chemical potentials to be space-varying. We consider

Z_{L}=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N}N!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{j}{\rm d}p_{j}\,e^{-\sum_{a=0}^{b}\int{\rm d}x\,\beta_{a}(x/L)q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)}

(62)

or, when combined with the extension to finite-range tracer dynamics,

Z_{L}=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N}N!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{j}{\rm d}p_{j}\,e^{-\int{\rm d}x{\rm d}\theta\,\beta(x/L,\theta)q_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)}.

(63)

Here,
q_{a}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)
and
q_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)
are defined in (
38
) and (
50
), respectively, and we have

\int{\rm d}x{\rm d}\theta\,\beta(x/L,\theta)q_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)=\sum_{i}\beta(x_{i}/L,\theta_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{p})).

For the integrals to make sense, it is sufficient for
\beta_{a}(x)
’s to be measurable on
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, or
\beta(x,\theta)
to be measurable on
(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
; again these functions take values in
{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
. Note that it is possible to implement a sharp volume
L
where the system lies within this formulation, simply by setting
\beta_{a}(x)
for appropriate
a
(corresponding to a strictly positive
q_{a}
), or
\beta(x,\theta)
for all
\theta
, to be
+\infty
for all
|x|\geq 1/2
. Thus, this is the most general formulation.

Definition 3.13
.

Consider either the setup of Definition
3.11
with partition function (
62
), or of Definition
3.12
with partition function (
63
). For the former, set
\beta(x,\theta)=\sum_{a=0}^{b}\beta_{a}(x)\theta^{a}
. Paralleling these definitions, the associated specific free energy is said to take the local TBA form if

f=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{{\rm d}x{\rm d}\theta}{2\pi}\,\mathsf{F}(\varepsilon(x,\theta))

(64)

where the pseudo-energy
\varepsilon(x,\theta)
solves the integral equation

\varepsilon(x,\theta)=\beta(x,\theta)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}}{2\pi}\,\varphi(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\mathsf{F}(\varepsilon(x,\theta^{\prime})),\qquad\mbox{a.e. }(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}.

(65)

The local TBA form is often referred to as the
local-density approximation
(LDA). Clearly, the TBA form is recovered from the local TBA form by choosing
\beta(x,\theta)=\beta(\theta)
independent of
x
for
|x|<1/2
, and
\beta(x,\theta)=+\infty
for
|x|\geq 1/2
.

Extension: finite volumes.
It is not known in general if a TBA-like equation describes the thermodynamics of integrable systems in finite volumes. However, as we demonstrate later, it is possible to derive the finite-volume version of TBA for the semiclassical Bethe systems that we study here. We conjecture that the form of the finite-volume TBA equations that we obtain may also be valid in quantum integrable systems.

Definition 3.14
.

Consider the setup of Definition
3.13
. The finite-volume specific free energy, Eq. (
55
), is said to take the finite-volume TBA form if

f_{L}=\frac{1}{L}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{{\rm d}x{\rm d}\theta}{2\pi}\,\mathsf{F}(\varepsilon_{L}(x,\theta))

(66)

where the finite-volume pseudo-energy
\varepsilon(x,\theta)
solves

\varepsilon_{L}(x,\theta)=\beta(x/L,\theta)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{{\rm d}x^{\prime}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}}{2\pi}\kappa_{x}(x-x^{\prime},\theta,\theta^{\prime})\mathsf{F}(\varepsilon_{L}(x^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})),\qquad\mbox{a.e. }(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}.

(67)

Here
\kappa(x-x^{\prime},\theta,\theta^{\prime})
is some differentiable function that may depend on
L
, and that reproduces the scattering shift
\varphi(\theta^{\prime},\theta)
at infinite volumes,

\lim_{x\to\infty}\lim_{L\to\infty}(\kappa(x,\theta,\theta^{\prime})-\kappa(-x,\theta,\theta^{\prime}))=\varphi(\theta^{\prime},\theta).

(68)

We will show that for any semiclassical Bethe system, the finite-volume and infinite-volume specific free energies for chemical potential function
\beta(x,\theta)
take the finite-volume and local TBA forms, with
\kappa(x,\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\psi_{\theta}(x,\theta-\theta^{\prime})
, under appropriate constraints and for a large family of functions
\beta(x,\theta)
.

63
), with space-varying chemical potentials; as discussed, this is the most general formulation. Thus we consider

Z_{L}=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N}N!}\int\prod_{j=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{j}{\rm d}p_{j}\,e^{-\int{\rm d}x{\rm d}\theta\,\beta(x/L,\theta)q_{\theta}(\bm{x},\bm{p};x)}

(108)

for a semiclassical Bethe system, for some finite
L>0
and some Lebesgue measurable function
e^{-\beta}:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to[0,\infty)
(note that
\beta(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
). We are looking to evaluate

f_{L}=-\frac{1}{L}\log Z_{L}\quad\mbox{and}\quad f=\lim_{L\to\infty}f_{L}.

(109)

Theorem
2.3
is a corollary of the Theorems
4.4
and
4.5
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Consider a semiclassical Bethe system. Assume the following:

\int{\rm d}\theta\,\varphi(\theta)=\varphi_{\rm tot}<\infty,

(110)

\int{\rm d}x{\rm d}\theta\,e^{-\beta(x,\theta)}=F<\infty,

(111)

\sup_{(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}e^{-\beta(x,\theta)}=e^{-\beta_{-}}<\infty

(112)

(each of these quantities is non-negative) and

A:=\frac{e^{1-\beta_{-}}\varphi_{\rm tot}}{2\pi}<1.

(113)

For every
L>0
, we have
Z_{L}<\infty
and
f_{L}\leq 0
; and
\sup_{L>0}|f_{L}|<\infty
. In particular,

\sup_{L>0}|f_{L}|\leq\frac{F}{2\pi}\frac{A}{1-A}.

(114)

Further,
f_{L}
takes the finite-volume TBA form for Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics
\mathsf{F}(\varepsilon)=-e^{-\varepsilon}
and two-body scattering shift
\varphi(\theta-\theta^{\prime})
(with
\kappa(x,\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\psi_{\theta}(x,\theta-\theta^{\prime})
; Definition
3.14
).

Proof.

Note that the Jacobian produced as a result of switching from
(\bm{x},\bm{p})
coordinates to
(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})
is nothing other than the Gaudin determinant
|\Gamma(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})|
. We thus rewrite the partition function Eq. (
108
) as

Z_{L}=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N}N!}\int\prod_{j=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{j}{\rm d}\theta_{j}\,|\Gamma(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})|e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta(x_{i}/L,\theta_{i})}.

(115)

It is clear that sharp boundaries (giving a system supported on the finite volume
[-L/2,L/2]
) can be obtained by setting
\beta(x,\theta)=\infty
for all
|x|\geq 1/2
; this still satisfies conditions (
111
), (
112
), (
113
).

The integrand, and every term in the series, are non-negative. Hence in order to show absolute convergence, we only need to show convergence for some re-organisation of the terms. We will use a combinatoric argument to re-organise the terms appropriately, from which we will both show convergence and obtain the TBA form.

At first glance the integral does not seem particularly simple, but it turns out that a graph theoretic interpretation of the Gaudin determinant allows us to evaluate it exactly. The same observation was in fact made already for quantum integrable systems in
[
55
]
. Since the calculation is almost identical, we omit the detail of the computation, and outline the steps in Appendix
A
.

The calculation is based on expressing the Gaudin matrix as
\Gamma_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+L_{ij}
, where
L_{ij}
is a Laplacian matrix (a matrix with zero row sums)

L_{ij}=\delta_{ij}\sum_{k\neq i}\psi_{x\theta}(x_{ik},\theta_{ik})-(1-\delta_{ij})\psi_{x\theta}(x_{ij},\theta_{ij}),

(116)

where we use again
x_{ij}=x_{i}-x_{j}
and
\theta_{ij}=\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}
. We then have

|\Gamma|=\sum_{\alpha\subset\{1,\cdots,N\}}\mathcal{L}(\alpha|\alpha),

(117)

where
\mathcal{L}(\alpha|\alpha)
is the principal minor obtained by removing the rows and column
\alpha
from
L_{ij}
(and
\mathcal{L}(\alpha|\alpha)=1
for
\alpha=\{1,\ldots,N\}
). The crucial insight of
[
55
]
is to notice that the matrix tree theorem allows us to evaluate the principal minors by appropriate sums over graphs, providing a combinatorial interpretation of
|\Gamma|
.

The result (see Appendix
A
) is an expression for the term
N
in the series (
115
), as a sum over all distinct topological rooted forests with
N
vertices. A topological rooted forest with
N
vertices is the (unordered) juxtaposition of topological rooted trees
m
with
N_{m}
vertices and
\sum_{m}N_{m}=N
; and a topological rooted tree with
N^{\prime}
vertices is the equivalence class, under permutations of non-root vertices
\{2,3,\ldots,N^{\prime}\}
amongst themselves, of a rooted spanning tree for the complete graph with
N^{\prime}
vertices labelled
1,2,\ldots,N^{\prime}
, with root at vertex 1. In their evaluations, the forest and trees come with appropriate symmetry factors. Organising the (countable) set of all topological rooted trees with a number
m=1,2,\ldots
(say with non-decreasing number of vertices), and denoting
N_{m}\geq 1
the number of vertices for tree
m
, the result is

\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N}N!}\int\prod_{j=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{j}{\rm d}\theta_{j}\,|\Gamma(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})|e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta(x_{i}/L,\theta_{i})}=\sum_{n_{1},n_{2},\ldots=0\atop\sum_{m}n_{m}N_{m}=N}^{\infty}\prod_{m}\frac{1}{n_{m}!}V_{m}^{n_{m}}.

(118)

Here, each term is the value of a topological rooted forest with
\sum_{m}n_{m}
roots (trees); note that for finite
N
there is a finite number of terms. The number
n_{m}
is the multiplicity of topological rooted tree
m
in the forest, and the tree
m
has value

V_{m}=\frac{1}{S_{m}}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N_{m}}}\int\prod_{i=1}^{N_{m}}{\rm d}x_{i}{\rm d}\theta_{i}\,e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{m}}\beta(x_{i}/L,\theta_{i})}\prod_{j=1}^{N_{m}-1}\psi_{x\theta}(x_{{\rm e}_{j}},\theta_{{\rm e}_{j}}),

(119)

with
{\rm e}_{j}=\{i_{j,1},i_{j,2}\},\,j=1,\ldots,N_{m}-1
the set of edges (sets of two vertex labels) of one representative of the equivalence class, and
S_{m}
the tree’s symmetry factor. The last product in the above equation is 1 if
N_{m}=1
. In fact, because of the full symmetry under permutation of integration variables, it is not necessary at this stage to consider rooted trees (that is, we could sum over unrooted forests), but it will be convenient below in order to obtain the TBA form.

We note that the total number of spanning trees for the complete graph of
N
vertices is
N^{N-2}
according to Cayley’s formula
[
56
]
, and that the total number of trees in the equivalence class
m
is
(N_{m}-1)!/S_{m}
(this formula in fact defines the symmetry factor
S_{m}
; we remark that there are
(N_{m}-1)!
permutations of non-root vertices). Thus

\sum_{m:N_{m}=N}\frac{1}{S_{m}}=\frac{N^{N-1}}{N!}.

(120)

Expression (
118
) allows us to re-organise the series (
115
) as

Z_{L}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\Bigg(\sum_{m:N_{m}\leq N}V_{m}\Bigg)^{k}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\exp\Bigg[\sum_{m:N_{m}\leq N}V_{m}\Bigg].

(121)

We see that the specific free energy
f=L^{-1}\log Z
is indeed non-positive.

From the above, we may now analyse convergence of the partition function. It is convenient to bound the result of summing over all topological rooted trees with “fixed root”, that is writing

V_{m}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}x_{1}{\rm d}\theta_{1}\,e^{-\beta(x_{1}/L,\theta_{1})}V_{m}(x_{1},\theta_{1})

(122)

where

V_{m}(x_{1},\theta_{1})=\frac{1}{S_{m}}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N_{m}-1}}\int\prod_{i=2}^{N_{m}}{\rm d}x_{i}{\rm d}\theta_{i}\,e^{-\sum_{i=2}^{N_{m}}\beta(x_{i}/L,\theta_{i})}\prod_{j=1}^{N_{m}-1}\psi_{x\theta}(x_{{\rm e}_{j}},\theta_{{\rm e}_{j}}).

(123)

We sequentially bound the integral over
x_{j},\,\theta_{j}
for
j=N_{m}
,
j=N_{m}-1
, up to
j=2
, by using non-negativity of
\psi_{x\theta}(x,\theta)
from (
1
), the definition (
2
), finiteness of the total scattering shift (
110
), and the boundedness condition (
112
) on
e^{-\beta(x,\theta)}
:

0\leq\int{\rm d}x{\rm d}\theta\,e^{-\beta(x/L,\theta)}\psi_{x\theta}(x^{\prime}-x,\theta^{\prime}-\theta)\leq e^{-\beta_{-}}\varphi_{\rm tot}=2\pi C

(124)

where
C=Ae^{-1}
. Therefore

V_{m}(x,\theta)\leq\frac{C^{N_{m}-1}}{S_{m}}

(125)

for all
x,\theta
. Eq. (
120
) then gives

\sum_{m:N_{m}=N}V_{m}(x,\theta)\leq C^{N-1}\frac{N^{N-1}}{N!}=:a_{N}.

(126)

Note that
\frac{a_{N+1}}{a_{N}}=C\left(1+\frac{1}{N}\right)^{N-1}\leq C\left(1+\frac{1}{N}\right)^{N}\leq Ce=A
and therefore
a_{N}\leq A^{N}
. Since
A<1
by (
113
) we thus find that

Y(x,\theta):=e^{-\beta(x/L,\theta)}\sum_{m}V_{m}(x,\theta)

(127)

converges, with

0<Y(x,\theta)=e^{-\beta(x/L,\theta)}\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m:N_{m}=N}V_{m}(x,\theta)\leq e^{-\beta(x/L,\theta)}\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}A^{N}=e^{-\beta(x/L,\theta)}\frac{A}{1-A}.

(128)

Therefore, we may evaluate the limit in (
121
) from (
122
) by evaluating the limit inside the integral: as the summands are non-negative, the pointwise limit
N\to\infty
of the sum is a uniform upper bound and, by (
111
), it has finite integral, hence we may use the bounded convergence theorem. We find that

0\leq\log Z_{L}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}x{\rm d}\theta\,Y(x,\theta)\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{LFA}{1-A}

(129)

hence the limit (
121
) exists and we have absolute convergence of the partition function. On the right-hand side of (
129
), the full
L
dependence is explicit. Hence we find that
\log Z_{L}
grows at most like
L
, thus the finite-volume specific free energy is bounded on
L>0
:

\sup_{L>0}|f_{L}|\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{FA}{1-A}.

(130)

This proves the bound expressed in the theorem.

One can reconstruct all topological rooted trees with a given root, by adjoining to this root
n=1
edge, then
n=2
edges, then
n=3
edges, etc, and connecting to these edges the roots of all possible topological rooted trees, dividing by
n!
for the symmetry. Thus we get a Schwinger-Dyson equation
[
55
]
,

\displaystyle Y(x,\theta)

\displaystyle=e^{-\beta(x/L,\theta)}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi n!}\left(\int{\rm d}x^{\prime}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\,\psi_{x\theta}(x-x^{\prime},\theta-\theta^{\prime})Y(x^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=e^{-\beta(x/L,\theta)}\exp\left[\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}x^{\prime}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\,\psi_{x\theta}(x-x^{\prime},\theta-\theta^{\prime})Y(x^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})\right].

(131)

Introducing the pseudo-energy
\varepsilon_{L}(x,\theta)
defined by
Y(x,\theta)=:e^{-\varepsilon_{L}(x,\theta)}
, the last equation can be recast into the equation

\varepsilon_{L}(x,\theta)=\beta(x/L,\theta)-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}x^{\prime}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\,\psi_{x\theta}(x-x^{\prime},\theta-\theta^{\prime})e^{-\varepsilon_{L}(x^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})}.

(132)

Note that in the case of sharp boundaries, we have
\varepsilon_{L}(x,\theta)=\infty
for all
|x|\geq L/2
, thus the
x^{\prime}
integrand is supported
(-L/2,L/2)
.
Thus we have shown that the partition function takes the finite-volume local TBA form both with and without sharp boundaries, for Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics and two-body scattering shift
\varphi(\theta-\theta^{\prime})
, with
\kappa(x,\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\psi_{\theta}(x,\theta-\theta^{\prime})
, where Eq. (
68
) holds thanks to (
2
) and Theorem
2.1
c. This completes the proof of Theorem
4.4
.

Theorem 4.5
.

In the context of Theorem
4.4
, assume additionally the following. First,
e^{-\beta(x,\theta)}
is continuous in
x
, uniformly for a.e.
(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
; that is, there exists a set
S\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
of Lebesgue measure zero, and
R:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
with
\lim_{u\to 0}R(u)=0
, such that

\Big|e^{-\beta(x+u,\theta)}-e^{-\beta(x,\theta)}\Big|\leq R(u)\quad\forall\,(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus S,\;u\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(133)

Second,

A<\frac{e}{2}\big(\sqrt{1+4e^{-1}}-1\big)=0.77757288...

(134)

(The bound (
134
) implies (
113
).) Then the specific free energy
f
exists, and takes the local TBA form for Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics
\mathsf{F}(\varepsilon)=-e^{-\varepsilon}
and two-body scattering shift
\varphi(\theta-\theta^{\prime})
(Definition
3.13
). Further, the solution
\varepsilon(x,\theta)
to the integral equation (
65
) (with
r=\infty
) exists for a.e.
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
and all
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
, is unique, and satisfies

e^{-\varepsilon(x,\theta)}<\frac{2\pi}{\varphi_{\rm tot}}\qquad\forall\;x,\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(135)

Proof.

As (
134
) implies
A<1
, (
113
) indeed holds. Recalling (
130
), we define

\beta_{+}=\log\Big(\frac{A}{1-A}\Big)\in{\mathbb{R}},

(136)

and it will be convenient to define the following quantity, which evaluates as follows from the definition of
A
in (
113
) and is bounded as follows by (
134
),

D:=\frac{A^{2}e^{-1}}{1-A}=\frac{e^{\beta_{+}-\beta_{-}}\varphi_{\rm tot}}{2\pi}<1.

(137)

Consider the scaled pseudo-energy
\bar{\varepsilon}_{L}(x,\theta)=\varepsilon_{L}(Lx,\theta)=-\log Y(Lx,\theta)
. For lightness of notation, we keep the dependence on
L
of
\bar{\varepsilon}_{L}(x,\theta)
implicit, writing instead
\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)
. By (
128
) and strict positivity of
Y(x,\theta)
this is real and bounded as

\beta(x,\theta)-\beta_{+}\leq\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)<\infty.

(138)

Also that by the bound (
112
),
e^{-\varepsilon(x,\theta)}
is bounded from above:

\sup_{(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2},L>0}e^{-\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)}\leq e^{\beta_{+}-\beta_{-}}<\infty.

(139)

Defining
\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)=\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)-\beta(x,\theta)
, we also have the bound

\sup_{(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2},L>0}e^{-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)}\leq e^{\beta_{+}}<\infty.

(140)

By Theorem
4.4
(see (
132
) and (
129
)),

\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)=\beta(x,\theta)-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}x^{\prime}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\,L\psi_{x\theta}(Lx-Lx^{\prime},\theta-\theta^{\prime})e^{-\bar{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})}

(141)

and

f_{L}=-\int\frac{{\rm d}x{\rm d}\theta}{2\pi}e^{-\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)}.

(142)

We are looking to evaluate the limit as
L\to\infty
.

We show that
\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)
is continuous in
x
, uniformly on
x,\theta\in{\mathbb{R}},\,L>0
. We have, for all
x,u,\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle|\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x+u,\theta)-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)|

(143)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}x^{\prime}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\,L\psi_{x\theta}(Lx-Lx^{\prime},\theta-\theta^{\prime})\,\Big|e^{-\bar{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime}+u,\theta^{\prime})}-e^{-\bar{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})}\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}x^{\prime}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\,L\psi_{x\theta}(Lx-Lx^{\prime},\theta-\theta^{\prime})\,\Big|e^{-\beta(x^{\prime}+u,\theta^{\prime})}e^{-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime}+u,\theta^{\prime})}-e^{-\beta(x^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})}e^{-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}x^{\prime}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\,L\psi_{x\theta}(Lx-Lx^{\prime},\theta-\theta^{\prime})e^{-\beta(x^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})}\,\Big|e^{-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime}+u,\theta^{\prime})}-e^{-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})}\Big|+\frac{e^{\beta_{+}}\varphi_{\rm tot}R(u)}{2\pi}.

Here we used a.e. uniform continuity (
133
), the bound (
140
), finiteness of the total scattering shift (
110
) and continuity of
\psi_{x\theta}
(thus avoiding the possibly non-empty measure-zero set of discontinuities) to evaluate:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}x^{\prime}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\,L\psi_{x\theta}(Lx-Lx^{\prime},\theta-\theta^{\prime})e^{-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime}+u,\theta^{\prime})}\,\Big|e^{-\beta(x^{\prime}+u,\theta^{\prime})}-e^{-\beta(x^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})}\Big|

(144)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{R(u)}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}x^{\prime}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\,L\psi_{x\theta}(Lx-Lx^{\prime},\theta-\theta^{\prime})e^{-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime}+u,\theta^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{e^{\beta_{+}}R(u)}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}x^{\prime}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\,L\psi_{x\theta}(Lx-Lx^{\prime},\theta-\theta^{\prime})=\frac{e^{\beta_{+}}\varphi_{\rm tot}R(u)}{2\pi}.

Recall that
\lim_{u\to 0}R(u)=0
. Continuing, we make use of the fact that
1-e^{-u}<u
for every
u>0
. From the bounds (
112
), (
140
) and (
110
), as well as the definition (
137
), we have

\displaystyle|\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x+u,\theta)-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)|

(145)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}x^{\prime}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\,L\psi_{x\theta}(Lx-Lx^{\prime},\theta-\theta^{\prime})e^{-\beta(x^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})}\max\big(e^{-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime}+u,\theta^{\prime})},e^{-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})}\big)\,\times

\displaystyle\,\Big|1-e^{-|\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime}+u,\theta^{\prime})-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})|}\Big|+\frac{e^{\beta_{+}}\varphi_{\rm tot}R(u)}{2\pi}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle D\sup_{(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x+u,\theta)-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)|+\frac{e^{\beta_{+}}\varphi_{\rm tot}R(u)}{2\pi}.

By the first inequality of (
143
) and using (
139
),
\sup_{(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x+u,\theta)-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)|\leq 2e^{\beta_{+}-\beta_{-}}\varphi_{\rm tot}/(2\pi)
is finite. Since
D<1
(Eq. (
137
)), we find:

\sup_{(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x+u,\theta)-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)|\leq\frac{1}{1-D}\frac{e^{\beta_{+}}\varphi_{\rm tot}R(u)}{2\pi}

(146)

(note that the right hand-side does not depend on
L
). Therefore
\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)
is continuous in
x
, uniformly for
x,\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
L>0
.

By boundedness (
140
),
e^{-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)}
is also continuous in
x
, uniformly for
x,\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
L>0
: this is obtained by evaluating
|e^{-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x+u,\theta)}-e^{-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)}|\leq e^{\beta_{+}}(1-e^{-|\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x+u,\theta)-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)|})\leq e^{\beta_{+}}|\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x+u,\theta)-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)|
. Thanks to a.e. uniform continuity (
133
) and boundedness (
112
), then
e^{-\Delta\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)}e^{-\beta(x,\theta)}=e^{-\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)}
is continuous in
x
uniformly for a.e.
(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and all
L>0
.

Now define

\varepsilon^{\infty}(x,\theta)=\beta(x,\theta)-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}x^{\prime}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\,L\psi_{x\theta}(Lx-Lx^{\prime},\theta-\theta^{\prime})e^{-\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta^{\prime})}=\beta(x,\theta)-\int\frac{{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}}{2\pi}\,\varphi(\theta-\theta^{\prime})e^{-\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta^{\prime})}.

(147)

Using (
112
), (
110
) and (
139
) along with (
137
) we find that
e^{-\varepsilon^{\infty}(x,\theta)}
is uniformly bounded,

\sup_{(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2},L>0}e^{-\varepsilon^{\infty}(x,\theta)}\leq e^{-\beta_{-}+D}.

(148)

Then

\displaystyle|\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)-\varepsilon^{\infty}(x,\theta)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}x^{\prime}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\,L\psi_{x\theta}(Lx-Lx^{\prime},\theta-\theta^{\prime})\,|e^{-\bar{\epsilon}(x^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})}-e^{-\bar{\epsilon}(x,\theta^{\prime})}|

(149)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}x^{\prime}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\,L\psi_{x\theta}(Lx-Lx^{\prime},\theta-\theta^{\prime})\,\sup_{u\in[x-\epsilon/L,x+\epsilon/L]}|e^{-\bar{\epsilon}(u,\theta^{\prime})}-e^{-\bar{\epsilon}(x,\theta^{\prime})}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\,\varphi(\theta-\theta^{\prime})\,\sup_{u\in[x-\epsilon/L,x+\epsilon/L]}|e^{-\bar{\epsilon}(u,\theta^{\prime})}-e^{-\bar{\epsilon}(x,\theta^{\prime})}|

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle 0\quad\mbox{uniformly for a.e.~$x\in{\mathbb{R}}$ and all $\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}$}\qquad(L\to\infty)

where the last line holds thanks to
L,x,\theta
-uniform continuity of
e^{-\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)}
as well as the condition (
110
). Hence,

\lim_{L\to\infty}\Bigg|\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)-\Bigg(\beta(x,\theta)-\int\frac{{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}}{2\pi}\,\varphi(\theta-\theta^{\prime})e^{-\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta^{\prime})}\Bigg)\Bigg|=0\quad\mbox{uniformly for a.e.~$x\in{\mathbb{R}}$ and all $\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}$}.

(150)

The above shows that the equation

\varepsilon(x,\theta)=\beta(x,\theta)-\int\frac{{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}}{2\pi}\,\varphi(\theta,\theta^{\prime})e^{-\varepsilon(x,\theta^{\prime})}\qquad\mbox{a.e. }x\in{\mathbb{R}},\;\forall\,\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}

(151)

has at least one solution. It also shows that, as
L\to\infty
,
\bar{\varepsilon}(x,\theta)
approaches a.e. a set of solutions (in the sense that the additive correction term in the equation as shown tends to zero
x,\theta
-uniformly). We now show that the solution is in fact unique. Suppose
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
are two solutions (for some fixed value of the
x
argument), and that both satisfy the bound (
139
). Then

\displaystyle|\varepsilon_{1}(x,\theta)-\varepsilon_{2}(x,\theta)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int\frac{{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}}{2\pi}\varphi(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\Big|e^{-\varepsilon_{1}(x,\theta^{\prime})}-e^{-\varepsilon_{2}(x,\theta^{\prime})}\Big|

(152)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{\beta_{+}-\beta_{-}}\int\frac{{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}}{2\pi}\varphi(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\Big|1-e^{-|\varepsilon_{1}(x,\theta^{\prime})-\varepsilon_{2}(x,\theta^{\prime})|}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{\beta_{+}-\beta_{-}}\int\frac{{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}}{2\pi}\varphi(\theta,\theta^{\prime})|\varepsilon_{1}(x,\theta^{\prime})-\varepsilon_{2}(x,\theta^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle D\sup_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}}|\varepsilon_{1}(x,\theta)-\varepsilon_{2}(x,\theta)|

where
\sup_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}}|\varepsilon_{1}(x,\theta)-\varepsilon_{2}(x,\theta)|\leq 2D
by the bound implied by the first line, thus is finite. As
D<1
the last line implies

for all

Therefore, we conclude that the limits

and

and all

and correspond to the unique solution that satisfy (

also exists, thanks to (

5
Conclusion

In this paper we introduced a new class of classical integrable models. They are constructed from Galilean non-interacting particles using a canonical transformation determined by an explicit generating function. The resulting dynamics of the particles is a “tracer dynamics”: If it is sufficiently far away from other particles, a particle evolves with its asymptotic momentum
\theta
. If two particles scatter they both accumulate a Wigner time delay, which effectively displaces the trajectories of both particles according to their scattering phase-shift. This construction has a lot of freedom, with a freely chosen function
\psi(x,\theta)
(under some weak constraints) which describes the trajectories of the particles during scattering. Thus, this allows us to construct integrable models with any phase-shift and any interaction range. Here, the constraint we impose on
\psi(x,\theta)
mean that the phase shift is non-negative and that the interaction range is finite, but the idea is more general, as described in
[
45
]
.

We showed that the resulting models are Liouville integrable with finite-range interaction and that their scattering is elastic and factorised. We showed that they can be obtained as generalized
T\bar{T}
- deformations of the free particle model, thus solving the problem of their locality properties in this context, at least for deformations giving rise to positive phase shifts. Furthermore, we proved that their generalised Gibbs ensembles (GGEs) are described by the usual thermodynamic Bethe ansatz in infinite volume, and by a similar set of equations even in finite volumes, and this, for any space-varying generalised temperatures; in infinite volume we recover the local density approximation. We believe this new class of integrable models very clearly illustrates the structures of many-body integrability.

As a next natural step one could consider the time evolution from slowly-varying GGEs, which is expected to be given by generalized hydrodynamics (GHD); we provide arguments for this in
[
45
]
. Since GHD is a universal theory, these dynamical systems should reproduce all features of GHD, including diffusive and dispersive corrections and correlations. Proving the emergence of GHD rigorously is a complicated problem and has only been accomplished in very special models (most notably hard rods models
[
23
,
33
]
); the relatively simple nature of the present family of models, in particular the explicitly known
x\to y
map, which allows to trivialise the dynamics, offers the prospect of a rigorous proof of the emergence of GHD using a similar strategy as that used for hard rods, and of an understanding of the hydrodynamic scale for a wide range of two-body scattering shifts. Another question is that of the processes of relaxation from far-from-GGE states, which again should be accessible in our models.

For negative phase shifts, the present construction must be modified: as
\psi_{x\theta}(x,\theta)
is not a non-negative function, the transformation generated by
\Phi(x,\theta)
, Eqs. (
11
), (
12
), are not necessarily invertible. One may still make physical sense of this situation in various ways. One of them is by borrowing Feynman’s idea, and considering processes where pairs of particles and anti-particles are created and annihilated; this is quite novel in the context of classical particles. This is explained in
[
45
]
. We believe that this interpretation can be made rigorous, giving classical integrable models represented by particle non-conserving Hamiltonians on the classical ‘Fock space’
\bigotimes_{N}\mathbb{R}^{2N}
. We hope to come back to this in future works.

Finally, other interesting directions of research would be to try to find similar explicit implementations of
T\bar{T}
-deformed models in the quantum case; investigate how the present classical models can be useful for numerical implementations, either to simulate the GHD equation or as a tool for numerical preparation of soliton gases
[
57
]
(see the discussion in
[
45
]
); and study the inclusion of external force terms, expected to be of the form, for an energy function
E(x,\theta)
,

\displaystyle E_{\theta}(x_{i},\theta_{i})

\displaystyle=\dot{x}_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\psi_{x\theta}(x_{i}-x_{j},\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})(\dot{x}_{i}-\dot{x}_{j})

(153)

\displaystyle-E_{x}(x_{i},\theta_{i})

\displaystyle=\dot{\theta}_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\psi_{x\theta}(x_{i}-x_{j},\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})(\dot{\theta}_{i}-\dot{\theta}_{j})

(154)

(which conserves the total energy
\sum_{i}E(x_{i},\theta_{i})
).
