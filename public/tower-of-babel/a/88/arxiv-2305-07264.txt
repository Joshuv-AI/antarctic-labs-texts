Simple blow-up solutions of singular Liouville equations
Lina Wu

Abstract
. In a recent series of important works [ 25 , 26 , 27 ] , Wei-Zhang proved several vanishing theorems for non-simple blow-up solutions of singular Liouville equations. It is well known that a non-simple blow-up situation happens when the spherical Harnack inequality is violated near a quantized singular source. In this article, we further strengthen the conclusions of Wei-Zhang by proving that if the spherical Harnack inequality does hold, there exist blow-up solutions with non-vanishing coefficient functions.

1.
Introduction

It is well known that the following Liouville equation has a rich background in geometry and Physics.

(1.1)

\Delta u+h(x)e^{u(x)}=\sum_{t=1}^{L}4\pi\gamma_{t}\delta_{p_{t}}\quad\mbox{in}\quad\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},

where
\Omega
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
p_{1},..p_{L}
are
L
points in
\Omega
and
4\pi\gamma_{t}\delta_{p_{t}}
(
t=1,...,L
) are Dirac masses placed at
p_{t}
. Since applications require integrability of
e^{u}
we assume
\gamma_{t}>-1
for each
t
.

Equation (
1.1
) is one of the most extensively studied elliptic partial differential equations in recent years. In conformal geometry, (
1.1
) is related to the well-known Nirenberg problem when all
\gamma_{t}=0
. The recent progress on this project can be seen in Kazdan-Warner
[
15
]
, Chang-Gursky-Yang
[
5
]
, Chang-Yang
[
6
]
, Cheng-Lin
[
10
]
, and the references therein. If some
\gamma_{t}\neq 0
, (
1.1
) arises from the existence of conformal metric with conic singularities, seen in Fang-Lai
[
13
]
, Troyanov
[
22
]
, Wei-Zhang
[
23
]
. Also, it serves as
a model equation in the Chern-Simons-Higgs theory and in the Liouville system, the interested readers may browse Chanillo-Kiessling
[
7
]
, Spruck-Yang
[
19
]
, Tarantello
[
20
]
, Yang
[
28
]
, and the references therein.

It is well known that if there is no singularity in (
1.1
),
h\equiv 1
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{u}{\rm d}x<\infty
, a global solution belongs to a family described by three parameters (see
[
9
]
). Then Y. Y. Li
[
17
]
proved the first uniform approximation theorem, which confirms that around a regular blow-up point, the profile of a blow-up sequence is close to that of a sequence of global solutions. Later Chen-Lin
[
8
]
, Zhang
[
29
]
, Gluck
[
14
]
, Bartolucci, et,al
[
2
]

improved Li’s estimate by obtaining better pointwise estimates and some gradient estimates. It turns out that the blow-up point has to be a critical point of a function determined by the coefficient function. This plays a crucial role in applications. In the non-quantized case, the classification theorem was proved by Prajapat-Tarantello, the uniform estimate is obtained by Bartolucci-Chen-Lin-Tarantello
[
1
]
, Bartolucci-Tarantello
[
3
]
, Zhang
[
30
]
. The most difficult case is when the singular source is quantized. In this case, the first breakthrough was obtained by Kuo-Lin in
[
16
]
, then independently by Bartolucci-Tarantello in
[
3
]
. In this case, if the spherical Harnack inequality is violated near a quantized singular source, the profile of bubbling solutions appears to have multiple local maximums. Here a sequence of bubbling solutions satisfying
spherical Harnack inequality
means the oscillation of solutions on each fixed radius around the singular point is uniformly bounded. In the work of Kuo-Lin, they use
non-simple blow-up
to describe this phenomenon. In a recent series of works of Wei-Zhang
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
, they proved the first vanishing theorems for the non-simple blow-up case. Their two main results can be stated as follows:

Let
\{u_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of blow-up solutions of

(1.2)

\Delta u_{k}+|x|^{2\alpha}h_{k}(x)e^{u_{k}(x)}=0,\quad\mbox{in}\quad B_{1}

where
h_{k}
is a sequence of smooth, positive functions in
B_{1}
:

(1.3)

\frac{1}{c_{1}}\leq h_{k}(x)\leq c_{1},\quad\|\nabla^{\beta}h_{k}(x)\|_{B_{1}}\leq c_{1},\quad x\in B_{1},\quad|\beta|=1,2,3.

for some
c_{1}>0
. Let
0
be the only blow-up point of
u_{k}
in
B_{1}
,
and suppose
u_{k}
has a bounded oscillation on
\partial B_{1}
:

(1.4)

|u_{k}(x)-u_{k}(y)|\leq C,\quad\forall x,y\in\partial B_{1},

and a uniform bound on its integration:

(1.5)

\int_{B_{1}}|x|^{2\alpha}h_{k}(x)e^{u_{k}(x)}{\rm d}x<C

for some
C>0
independent of
k
. In their first vanishing theorem Wei-Zhang proved that

Theorem A: (Wei-Zhang). Let
u_{k}
be a sequence of non-simple blow-up solutions around the origin. Suppose
0
is the only blow-up point in
B_{1}
and

u_{k}
satisfies (
1.2
),(
1.4
) and (
1.5
). Then along a sub-sequence

\lim_{k\to\infty}\nabla(\log h_{k}+\psi_{k})(0)=0

where
\psi_{k}
is the harmonic function that eliminates the finite oscillation of
u_{k}
on
\partial B_{1}
:

(1.6)

\Delta\psi_{k}=0,\quad\mbox{in}\quad B_{1},\quad\psi_{k}(x)=u_{k}(x)-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial B_{1}}u_{k},\quad x\in\partial B_{1}.

In their recent work, Wei-Zhang further proved the following Laplacian vanishing theorem:

Theorem B: (Wei-Zhang). Let
u_{k}
be the same as in Theorem A. Then along a subsequence,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\Delta(\log h_{k})(0)=0.

It is important to point out that in both Theorem A and Theorem B, the blow-up sequence has to be
non-simple
, this assumption implies that
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
. Both Theorem A and Theorem B are powerful tools in application, since the equation (
1.2
) represents a number of situations in more general equations/systems. For example, in the author’s recent joint work with Wei and Zhang
[
24
]
, we proved that under certain conditions on the coefficient function and Gauss curvature, all blow-up points to Toda systems are simple.

The purpose of this article is twofold. First if
\alpha=0
in (
1.2
) and
0
is the only blow-up point, it is well known that (see
[
8
,
14
,
29
]
) along a sub-sequence

\lim_{k\to\infty}\nabla(\log h_{k}+\psi_{k})(0)=0
. Over the years it has long been suspected that this property does not hold if
\alpha
is not an integer. This is indeed verified in our first main theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

For any given
\alpha>-1
and
\alpha\not\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, there exist a sequence
h_{k}
satisfying (
1.3
) and

(1.7)

|\nabla\log h_{k}(0)+\nabla\psi_{k}(0)|\geq c_{1},\quad|\Delta\log h_{k}(0)|\geq c_{1}

for some
c_{1}>0
,
Corresponding to
h_{k}
there is a sequence of blow-up solutions
u_{k}
of (
1.2
) such that the origin is its only blow-up point, (
1.4
) (
1.5
) holds for
u_{k}
, which also satisfies the spherical Harnack inequality around the origin.

The second goal is to prove that
when
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
we can construct a sequence of
simple
blow-up solutions that
does not satisfy the Laplacian vanishing theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, there exist a sequence of blow-up solutions
\{u_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
of (
1.2
) having
0
as its only blow-up point in
B_{1}
. Moreover
\{u_{k}\}
satisfies (
1.4
) (
1.5
) and the coefficient
h_{k}
satisfies (
1.3
) and

|\Delta(\log h_{k})(0)|\geq c,\,\,\mbox{for a constant }\,\,c>0\,\,\mbox{independent of $k$}.

Theorem
1.1
settles the conjecture that around a non-quantized singular source, the vanishing theorems do not hold. Theorem
1.2
proves that it is essential to have a
non-simple
blow-up sequence in Theorem B. If this assumption is violated, the corresponding Laplacian vanishing property also fails. However, this article did not provide a similar example for the first-order vanishing theorem in Theorem A.

The paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
, we establish Theorem
1.1
. Our proof is based on the thorough comprehension of the corresponding linearized operator of a model equation. It is also essential that we analyze the Fourier series of some correction terms and prove its convergence. In Section
3
, we establish Theorem
1.2
, and the key point of the proof is to use a radial coefficient function and reduce all the iterations into radial cases. This method made us avoid kernel functions in the linearized equation corresponding to the quantized case.

2.
Non-quantized situation

In this section, we consider the non-quantized case. In other words, we set
\alpha>-1
and
\alpha\notin\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. It is known that the spherical Harnack holds around the origin when
\alpha
is not an integer (See
[
16
]
).

Denote
\lambda_{k}=u_{k}(0)
and
\epsilon_{k}=e^{-\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2(1+\alpha)}}
. Let
v_{k}
be the scaling of
u_{k}
:

v_{k}(y)=u_{k}(\epsilon_{k}y)+2(1+\alpha)\log\epsilon_{k},\quad y\in\Omega_{k}:=B(0,\epsilon_{k}^{-1}).

Clearly, we need to construct
v_{k}
to satisfy

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\Delta v_{k}(y)+|y|^{2\alpha}h_{k}(\epsilon_{k}y)e^{v_{k}(y)}=0,&&{\rm in}\ \,\Omega_{k},\\
v_{k}(0)=0,\\
|v_{k}(y_{1})-v_{k}(y_{2})|\leq C,&&{\rm for\ \ any}\ \,y_{1},y_{2}\in\partial\Omega_{k},\\
v_{k}(y)\to-2\log(1+|y|^{2+2\alpha}),&&{\rm in}\ \,C_{loc}^{\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\end{array}\right.

where
\beta\in(0,1)
.
It suffices to construct
\{v_{k}\}
satisfying (
2.1
). Since we can choose
h_{k}
we require
h_{k}(0)=8(1+\alpha)^{2}
for convenience. Let

U_{k}(y)=-2\log(1+|y|^{2+2\alpha})

be a standard bubble that satisfies

(2.2)

\Delta U_{k}(y)+8(1+\alpha)^{2}|y|^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}(y)}=0\quad{\rm in}\ \,\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Here we note that a uniform estimate of Bartolucci-Chen-Lin-Tarantello
[
1
]
assures that any blow-up solution
v_{k}
of (
2.1
) satisfies

|v_{k}(y)-U_{k}(y)|\leq C,\quad y\in\Omega_{k}.

We will construct our solutions based on the expansion of
v_{k}
established in
[
30
]
. Firstly, let us recall some notations and results in
[
30
]
. Denote

\begin{split}g_{k}(r)&=-\frac{1}{4\alpha(1+\alpha)}\frac{r}{1+r^{2+2\alpha}},\quad r=|y|,\\
c_{1}^{k}(y)&=g_{k}(r)\epsilon_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\partial_{j}h_{k}(0)\theta_{j},\quad\theta_{j}=\frac{y_{j}}{r}\ \ (j=1,2).\end{split}

Then
c_{1}^{k}
satisfies

(2.3)

\Delta c_{1}^{k}+8(1+\alpha)^{2}|y|^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}(y)}c_{1}^{k}=-\sum_{j=1}^{2}\epsilon_{k}\partial_{j}h_{k}(0)y_{j}|y|^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}(y)}\quad{\rm in}\ \,\Omega_{k}.

[
30
]
tells us that
c_{1}^{k}
is the second term in the expansion of
v_{k}
if
\alpha>0
is a non-integer. For the case
-1<\alpha<0
, Bartolucci-Yang-Zhang
[
4
]
have established the same result.
Here we point out that the radial part of
c_{1}^{k}
decays like
\epsilon_{k}r^{-1-2\alpha}
at infinity. In particular, for
r=\epsilon_{k}^{-1}
, the angular part of the function is comparable to
\epsilon_{k}^{2+2\alpha}e^{i\theta}
, which means this term contributes no oscillation on the boundary. So as long as
|\nabla\log h_{k}(0)|\geq 2c>0
, we have
|\nabla\log h_{k}(0)+\nabla\psi_{k}(0)|\geq c
.

For the convenience of the readers, we comment that the construction of
c_{1}^{k}
is essentially solving

(2.4)

\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}g+\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}g+\Big(8(1+\alpha)^{2}r^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}-\frac{l^{2}}{r^{2}}\Big)g=-r^{1+2\alpha}e^{U_{k}},\quad r>0,

with

. From the proof of Lemma 2.1 in
[
30
]
, we know two fundamental solutions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
of the homogeneous equation of (
2.4
) can be written explicitly as follows:

(2.5)

\begin{split}F_{1}(r)&=\frac{(\frac{l}{1+\alpha}+1)r^{l}+(\frac{l}{1+\alpha}-1)r^{l+2(1+\alpha)}}{1+r^{2(1+\alpha)}},\\
F_{2}(r)&=\frac{(\frac{l}{1+\alpha}+1)r^{-l+2(1+\alpha)}+(\frac{l}{1+\alpha}-1)r^{-l}}{1+r^{2(1+\alpha)}}.\end{split}

Therefore, we can verify that
g
can be explicitly written with two fundamental solutions above by the standard ODE methods.

For the motivation of adding more terms in the correction, we use the decay of
c_{1}^{k}
to
obtain

(2.6)

\displaystyle\Delta(U_{k}+c_{1}^{k})+8(1+\alpha)^{2}r^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}+c_{1}^{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta U_{k}+\Delta c_{1}^{k}+8(1+\alpha)^{2}r^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}\big(1+c_{1}^{k}+\frac{(c_{1}^{k})^{2}}{2}\big)+O(\epsilon_{k}^{3})(1+r)^{-7-8\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 8(1+\alpha)^{2}r^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}\frac{(c_{1}^{k})^{2}}{2}-\epsilon_{k}\sum_{j}\partial_{j}h_{k}(0)\theta_{j}r^{1+2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}+O(\epsilon_{k}^{3})(1+r)^{-7-8\alpha}.

At this moment we write the expansion of
h_{k}(\epsilon_{k}y)
:

(2.7)

\begin{split}h_{k}(\epsilon_{k}y)=&8(1+\alpha)^{2}+\epsilon_{k}\nabla h_{k}(0)\cdot y+\frac{\epsilon_{k}^{2}}{2}\partial_{11}h_{k}(0)(y_{1}^{2}-\frac{|y|^{2}}{2})+\frac{\epsilon_{k}^{2}}{2}\partial_{22}h_{k}(0)\cdot\\

=&8(1+\alpha)^{2}+\epsilon_{k}\nabla h_{k}(0)\cdot y+\epsilon_{k}^{2}r^{2}\Theta_{2}+\frac{1}{4}\epsilon_{k}^{2}r^{2}\Delta h_{k}(0)+O(\epsilon_{k}^{3})r^{3}.\end{split}

where

\begin{split}\Theta_{2}:=&\frac{1}{2}\partial_{11}h_{k}(0)(\theta_{1}^{2}-\frac{1}{2})+\partial_{12}h_{k}(0)\theta_{1}\theta_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{22}h_{k}(0)(\theta_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2})\\
=&\frac{1}{4}(\partial_{11}h_{k}(0)-\partial_{22}h_{k}(0))\cos 2\theta+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{12}h_{k}(0)\sin 2\theta.\end{split}

Based on (
2.3
), (
2.6
) and (

(2.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Delta(U_{k}+c_{1}^{k})+h_{k}(\epsilon_{k}y)|y|^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}+c_{1}^{k}}\\
=&r^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}\Big(8(1+\alpha)^{2}\frac{(c_{1}^{k})^{2}}{2}+\Theta_{2}\epsilon_{k}^{2}r^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\epsilon_{k}^{2}r^{2}\Delta h_{k}(0)+\epsilon_{k}\nabla h_{k}(0)\cdot yc_{1}^{k}\\
&+O(\epsilon_{k}^{3}(1+r)^{3})\Big).\end{split}

Now we compute
(c_{1}^{k})^{2}
:

\displaystyle(c_{1}^{k})^{2}=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}^{2}g_{k}^{2}(\partial_{1}h_{k}(0)\cos\theta+\partial_{2}h_{k}(0)\sin\theta)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}^{2}g_{k}^{2}\Big(\frac{|\nabla h_{k}(0)|^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}((\partial_{1}h_{k}(0))^{2}-(\partial_{2}h_{k}(0))^{2})\cos 2\theta

\displaystyle+\partial_{1}h_{k}(0)\partial_{2}h_{k}(0)\sin 2\theta\Big)

Also the remaining term of the order
O(\epsilon_{k}^{2})
is

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}\nabla h_{k}(0)\cdot yc_{1}^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}^{2}g_{k}r(\partial_{1}h_{k}(0)\cos\theta+\partial_{2}h_{k}(0)\sin\theta)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}^{2}g_{k}r\Big(\frac{|\nabla h_{k}(0)|^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{1}h_{k}(0)^{2}-\partial_{2}h_{k}(0)^{2})\cos 2\theta+\partial_{1}h_{k}(0)\partial_{2}h_{k}(0)\sin 2\theta\Big).

To get rid of the terms with
e^{2i\theta}
of the order
O(\epsilon_{k}^{2})
in (
2.8
) we let
c_{2}^{k}
be the solution of

\displaystyle\Delta c_{2}^{k}+8(1+\alpha)^{2}|y|^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}c_{2}^{k}=-r^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}\Big(\epsilon_{k}^{2}r^{2}\Theta_{2}+\mathcal{A}(\frac{(c_{1}^{k})^{2}}{2}+\epsilon_{k}\nabla h_{k}(0)\cdot yc_{1}^{k})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\epsilon_{k}^{2}|y|^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}\Big(r^{2}\Theta_{2}+\big(4(1+\alpha)^{2}g_{k}^{2}+g_{k}r\big)\cdot((\partial_{1}h_{k}(0))^{2}-(\partial_{2}h_{k}(0))^{2})\frac{\cos 2\theta}{2}

\displaystyle+\partial_{1}h_{k}(0)\partial_{2}h_{k}(0)\sin 2\theta)\Big).

Note that
\mathcal{A}(\cdot)
means the non-radial part of the term in the parenthesis.

Since each term in
c_{2}^{k}
is a product of a radial function and a spherical harmonic function, we set
w_{1}
to be a solution of

\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}w_{1}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}w_{1}+\Big(8(1+\alpha)^{2}r^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}-\frac{4}{r^{2}}\Big)w_{1}=r^{2+2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}

with the control of

for all

. Similarly, we set
w_{2}
to be a solution of

\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}w_{2}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}w_{2}+\Big(8(1+\alpha)^{2}r^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}-\frac{4}{r^{2}}\Big)w_{2}=r^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}\big(4(1+\alpha)^{2}g_{k}^{2}+g_{k}r\big)

with
|w_{2}(r)|\leq C
for all
r
. Two fundamental solutions of the corresponding homogeneous equation can be seen in (
2.5
) with

. Furthermore, we observe that the non-homogenous terms have good decay rates at infinity. Therefore, the construction of
w_{1}
and

is standard. At this point, it is easy to verify that
c_{2}^{k}
can be constructed as

\displaystyle c_{2}^{k}(y)=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}^{2}\Big(w_{1}(r)\Theta_{2}+w_{2}(r)\big((\partial_{1}h_{k}(0))^{2}-(\partial_{2}h_{k}(0))^{2})\frac{\cos 2\theta}{2}

\displaystyle+\partial_{1}h_{k}(0)\partial_{2}h_{k}(0)\sin 2\theta\big)\Big).

Finally we use
c_{0}^{k}
to handle the radial term of the order
O(\epsilon_{k}^{2})
:
We let
c_{0}^{k}
solve

\displaystyle\Delta c_{0}^{k}+8(1+\alpha)^{2}|y|^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}c_{0}^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\epsilon_{k}^{2}|y|^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}\Big(r^{2}\frac{\Delta h_{k}(0)}{4}+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla h_{k}(0)|^{2}\big(4(1+\alpha)^{2}g_{k}(r)^{2}+g_{k}(r)r\big)\Big).

Since both
U_{k}
and the right-hand side of the above are radial, we can construct
c_{0}^{k}
as a radial function
c_{0}^{k}(r)
that satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}c_{0}^{k}(r)+\frac{d}{dr}c_{0}^{k}(r)+8(1+\alpha)^{2}r^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}c_{0}^{k}(r)\\
\quad=-\epsilon_{k}^{2}r^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}\Big(\frac{r^{2}}{4}\Delta h_{k}(0)+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla h_{k}(0)|^{2}\big(4(1+\alpha)^{2}g_{k}(r)^{2}+g_{k}(r)r\big)\Big).\\

We only need to define
c_{0}^{k}
for

. It is easy to use the standard ODE method to obtain

(2.9)

|c_{0}^{k}(r)|\leq C\epsilon_{k}^{2}(1+r)^{-2\alpha}\log(2+r),\quad 0<r<\epsilon_{k}^{-1}.

Set
c_{k}=c_{0}^{k}+c_{1}^{k}+c_{2}^{k}
, we verify by direct computation that

(2.10)

\Delta(U_{k}+c_{k})+|y|^{2\alpha}h_{k}(\epsilon_{k}y)e^{U_{k}+c_{k}}=E_{k}

(2.11)

|E_{k}(y)|\leq c_{1}\epsilon_{k}^{3}(1+|y|)^{-1-2\alpha},\quad y\in\Omega_{k}.

So in order to find a solution with a non-vanishing coefficient, we need to find
d_{k}
to satisfy

(2.12)

\Delta(U_{k}+c_{k}+d_{k})+|y|^{2\alpha}h_{k}(\epsilon_{k}y)e^{U_{k}+c_{k}+d_{k}}=0,\quad\mbox{in}\quad\Omega_{k}.

The difference between (
2.10
) and (

(2.13)

\Delta d_{k}+8(1+\alpha)^{2}|y|^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}d_{k}=-E_{k}-f(d_{k}).

where

(2.14)

f(d_{k})=-|y|^{2\alpha}h_{k}(\epsilon_{k}y)e^{U_{k}+c_{k}}(e^{d_{k}}-1-d_{k})+|y|^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}(h_{k}(\epsilon_{k}y)e^{c_{k}}-h_{k}(0))d_{k}.

is of higher order.
Based on (
2.13
) we design an iteration scheme: Let
d_{k}^{(0)}\equiv 0
and

d_{k}^{(1)}
satisfy

\Delta d_{k}^{(1)}+8(1+\alpha)^{2}|y|^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}d_{k}^{(1)}=-E_{k}-f(d_{k}^{(0)}).

In general we shall construct
d_{k}^{(m+1)}
that satisfies

\Delta d_{k}^{(m+1)}+8(1+\alpha)^{2}|y|^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}d_{k}^{(m+1)}=-E_{k}-f(d_{k}^{(m)})

and

(2.15)

d_{k}^{(m+1)}(0)=|\nabla d_{k}^{(m+1)}(0)|=0.

Here we claim that there exists
c_{0}>0
independent of

and

such that

(2.16)

|d_{k}^{(m)}(y)|\leq c_{0}\epsilon_{k}^{3}(1+|y|)^{1-2\alpha}\log(2+|y|).

The constant
c_{0}
will be determined based on
c_{1}
later.
To prove this uniform bound, we assume that (
2.16
) holds for
d_{k}^{(m)}
, and we shall show that it also holds for
d_{k}^{(m+1)}
.

The projection to
1
is the following equation: Let
f_{0}
be the
projection of
d_{k}^{(m+1)}
onto
1
, then
f_{0}
solves

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f_{0}^{\prime\prime}(r)+\frac{1}{r}f_{0}^{\prime}(r)+8(1+\alpha)^{2}r^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}f_{0}(r)+E_{0}^{k}=0,\quad 0<r\leq\epsilon_{k}^{-1}\\
f_{0}(0)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}f_{0}(0)=0,\end{array}\right.

where
E_{0}^{k}
is the corresponding projection of
E_{k}+f(d_{k}^{(m)})
onto 1, and satisfies a similar bound of

:

(2.17)

|E_{0}^{k}(r)|\leq 2c_{1}\epsilon_{k}^{3}(1+|y|)^{-1-2\alpha}.

The reason that
E_{0}^{k}
has a worse coefficient
2c_{1}
is that the
d_{k}^{(m)}
terms are absorbed.

We denote the two fundamental solutions of the homogeneous equation of
f_{0}
as
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
, where

u_{1}(r)=\frac{1-r^{2+2\alpha}}{1+r^{2+2\alpha}},

and
u_{2}(r)
is comparable to
\log r
near
0
and infinity. Based on standard ODE theory,

f_{0}(r)=-u_{1}(r)\int_{0}^{r}tE_{1}^{k}(t)u_{2}(t)\mathrm{d}t-u_{2}(r)\int_{0}^{r}tE_{1}^{k}(t)u_{1}(t)\mathrm{d}t.

Integrating the identity above, we know that

f_{0}(r)=O(\epsilon_{k}^{3}(1+r)^{1-2\alpha}r|\log(r)|),\ \ \mathrm{at}\ \ 0,\quad f_{0}(r)=O(\epsilon_{k}^{3}(1+r)^{1-2\alpha}\log(r)),\ \ \mathrm{at}\ \ \infty.

In other words, we have the following estimate for
f_{0}

|f_{0}(r)|\leq c_{0}\epsilon_{k}^{3}(1+r)^{1-2\alpha}\log(2+r),

where
c_{0}
is a constant independent of
l
and only depends on
c_{1}
.

Next, we consider the projections on high frequencies. For
l\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, let
f_{l}
satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f_{l}^{\prime\prime}(r)+\frac{1}{r}f_{l}^{\prime}(r)+\Big(8(1+\alpha)^{2}r^{2\alpha}e^{U_{k}}-\frac{l^{2}}{r^{2}}\Big)f_{l}(r)+E_{2,k}^{l}=0,\quad 0<r\leq\epsilon_{k}^{-1}\\
f_{l}(0)=0.\end{array}\right.

Here
E_{2,k}^{l}
(
l\geq 1
) is the radial part of the projection of some error term on
\cos({l\theta}
):

E_{2,k}^{l}(r)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}E_{k}\cos{(l\theta)}\mathrm{d}\theta

The estimate of
E_{2,k}^{l}
is

(2.18)

|E_{2,k}^{l}(r)|\leq 2c_{1}\epsilon_{k}^{3}(1+r)^{-1-2\alpha}

In order to find
f_{l}
we use two fundamental solutions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
of the homogeneous equation, whose explicit expressions can be seen in (
2.5
).
As one can see that
F_{1}
is comparable to
r^{l}
at the origin and at infinity, and
F_{2}
is comparable to
r^{-l}
at the origin and infinity. At this point, we can construct
f_{l}
as follows

f_{l}(r)=-F_{1}(r)\int_{r}^{\infty}\frac{t}{2l}E_{2,k}^{l}(t)F_{2}(t)\mathrm{d}t-F_{2}(r)\int_{0}^{r}\frac{t}{2l}E_{2,k}^{l}(t)F_{1}(t)\mathrm{d}t.

Integrate the identity above, we know that

|f_{l}(r)|\leq\frac{c_{2}}{l^{2}}\epsilon_{k}^{3}(1+r)^{1-2\alpha},

where
c_{2}
is a constant independent of
l
. It is easy to see that
f_{l}(0)=0
. Furthermore the summation of projections on all
\cos{(l\theta})

(l\geq 1)
is convergent. That is

\big|\sum_{l\geq 1}f_{l}(r)\big|\leq c_{2}\epsilon_{k}^{3}(1+r)^{1-2\alpha}\sum_{l\geq 1}\frac{1}{l^{2}}\leq c_{2}\epsilon_{k}^{3}(1+r)^{1-2\alpha}

In the same way we can construct the projection on
\sin{(l\theta)}
for all
l\geq 1
, called
\tilde{f}_{l}
, and the summation of
\tilde{f}_{l}
is convergent as well.
d_{k}^{(m)}
is well-defined and satisfies the estimate (
2.16
).

Thus by Brower fixed point theorem, we obtain the existence of
d_{k}
. The construction is complete in this case.

The Laplacian term is also obviously true, which can be seen in the construction. The construction of a non-quantized case is complete.

3.
Quantized situation

Let
N
be a positive natural number, our goal is to construct a sequence of blow-up solutions
u_{k}
such that

\Delta u_{k}+|x|^{2N}h_{k}(x)e^{u_{k}(x)}=0,\quad\mbox{in}\quad B_{1}

such that the spherical Harnack holds around the origin, the only blow-up point in
B_{1}
and
\Delta\log h_{k}(0)
do not tend to zero. Here
\psi_{k}
is the set of harmonic functions that eliminate the oscillation of
u_{k}
on
\partial B_{1}
.

The main result of this section is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
h_{k}(x)
satisfying (
1.3
) and a sequence of blow-up solutions
u_{k}
of (
1.2
)(
1.4
)(
1.5
) such that
u_{k}
is simple and
|\Delta(\log h_{k})(0)|\geq c
for some
c>0
independent of
k
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
:
We set

h_{k}(x)=8(N+1)^{2}+|x|^{2},\quad x\in B_{1}.

Obviously

\nabla\log h_{k}(0)=0,\quad\Delta(\log h_{k})(0)=\frac{\Delta h_{k}(0)}{h_{k}(0)}=\frac{1}{4(1+N)^{2}}.

Let
v_{k}
be the scaling of
u_{k}
according to the maximum of
u_{k}
: Let

\epsilon_{k}=e^{-\frac{u_{k}(0)}{2(1+N)}}

and

v_{k}(y)=u_{k}(\epsilon_{k}y)+2(1+N)\log\epsilon_{k}.

The equation for
v_{k}
is

\Delta v_{k}+(1+\epsilon_{k}^{2}|y|^{2})|y|^{2N}e^{v_{k}}=0.

Our goal is to construct
v_{k}
satisfying the equation above based on the global solution
U_{k}
. The classification theorem of Prajapat-Tarantello
[
21
]
gives the standard bubble of
\Delta U+8(N+1)^{2}|y|^{2N}e^{U}=0
:

U(y)=\log\frac{\lambda}{\big(1+\lambda|y^{N+1}-\xi|^{2}\big)^{2}}

where parameters
\lambda>0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
.

Setting
\lambda=1
and
\xi=0
in
U
, we use the radial
U_{k}(y)
:

U_{k}(y)=\log\frac{1}{\big(1+|y|^{2N+2}\big)^{2}}.

Here we note that
\partial_{\lambda}|_{\lambda=0}U
,
\partial_{\xi}|_{\xi=0}U
and
\partial_{\bar{\xi}}|_{\xi=0}U
for a basis for the linearized space.

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}\big|_{\lambda=1}U

\displaystyle=\frac{1-r^{2N+2}}{1+r^{2N+2}},

\displaystyle\partial_{\xi}\big|_{\xi=0}U

\displaystyle=\frac{2r^{N+1}}{1+r^{2N+2}}e^{-i(N+1)\theta},

\displaystyle\partial_{\bar{\xi}}\big|_{\xi=0}U

\displaystyle=\frac{2r^{N+1}}{1+r^{2N+2}}e^{i(N+1)\theta}.

Because of this, we see that corresponding to
N
we have

\frac{2r^{N+1}}{1+r^{2N+2}}\sin((N+1)\theta),\quad\frac{2r^{N+1}}{1+r^{2N+2}}\cos((N+1)\theta)

in the kernel, this is the reason we only obtain the non-vanishing estimate for
\Delta(\log h_{k})(0)
. It would be interesting to construct a simple blowup sequence with non-vanishing first-order coefficients.

Based on the fact
h_{k}(\epsilon_{k}y)=8(N+1)^{2}+\epsilon_{k}^{2}|y|^{2}
and the equation of
U_{k}
, we have

\Delta U_{k}+h_{k}(\epsilon_{k}y)|y|^{2N}e^{U_{k}}=\epsilon_{k}^{2}|y|^{2N+2}e^{U_{k}}.

In order to deal with the right-hand side of the equation above, we let
c_{k}
solve

\Delta c_{k}+8(N+1)^{2}|y|^{2N}e^{U_{k}}c_{k}=-\epsilon_{k}^{2}|y|^{2N+2}e^{U_{k}}.

Similar with
c_{0}^{k}
in the non-quantized case, we can construct
c_{k}
as a radial function
c_{k}(r)
satisfying

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}c_{k}(r)+\frac{d}{dr}c_{k}(r)+8(N+1)^{2}r^{2N}e^{U_{k}}c_{k}(r)=-\epsilon_{k}^{2}r^{2N+2}e^{U_{k}},\quad 0<r<\epsilon_{k}^{-1}\\
c_{k}(0)=\frac{d}{dr}c_{k}(0)=0.\end{array}\right.

After the standard ODE method, we obtain the estimate as in (
2.9
):

(3.1)

|c_{k}(r)|\leq C\epsilon_{k}^{2}(1+r)^{-2N}\log(2+r),\quad 0<r<\epsilon_{k}^{-1}.

Note that
e^{U_{k}+c_{k}}=e^{U_{k}}(1+c_{k}+O(\epsilon_{k}^{4}))
. By direct computation, we obtain

(3.2)

\Delta(U_{k}+c_{k})+|y|^{2N}h_{k}(\epsilon_{k}y)e^{U_{k}+c_{k}}=E_{k}.

Here
E_{k}
is radial and satisfies

(3.3)

|E_{k}(y)|\leq c\epsilon_{k}^{3}(1+|y|)^{-1-2N},\quad y\in\Omega_{k},

where
c
is a positive constant independent of
k
.

Then we set
v_{k}=U_{k}+c_{k}+b_{k}
. Removing the equation for
U_{k}
and
c_{k}
we write the equation of
b_{k}
as

(3.4)

\Delta b_{k}+|y|^{2N}h_{k}(\epsilon_{k}y)e^{U_{k}+c_{k}+b_{k}}-|y|^{2N}h_{k}(\epsilon_{k}y)e^{U_{k}+c_{k}}=-E_{k},\quad|y|\leq\tau\epsilon_{k}^{-1}.

The equation can be further written as

(3.5)

\Delta b_{k}+8(N+1)^{2}|y|^{2N}e^{U_{k}}b_{k}=-E_{k}-\hat{f}(b_{k}),\quad|y|\leq\tau\epsilon_{k}^{-1}

where

\hat{f}(b_{k})=-|y|^{2N}h_{k}(\epsilon_{k}y)e^{U_{k}+c_{k}}(e^{b_{k}}-1-b_{k})+|y|^{2N}e^{U_{k}}(h_{k}(\epsilon_{k}y)e^{c_{k}}-h_{k}(0))b_{k}.

Similar to the non-quantized case, we construct
b_{k}
by iteration. Let
b_{k}^{(m)}\equiv 0
and
b_{k}^{(1)}
satisfy

\Delta b_{k}^{(1)}+8(N+1)^{2}r^{2N}e^{U_{k}}b_{k}^{(1)}=-E_{k}-\hat{f}(b_{k}^{(0)}).

In general we construct
b_{k}^{(m+1)}
satisfying

\Delta b_{k}^{(m+1)}+8(N+1)^{2}r^{2N}e^{U_{k}}b_{k}^{(m+1)}=-E_{k}-\hat{f}(b_{k}^{(m)})

and
b_{k}^{(m+1)}(0)=0
.
Denote
F_{k}^{m}(r)=-E_{k}(r)-\hat{f}(b_{k}^{(m)}(r))
. Then by the iteration method as before if we set

\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}b_{k}^{(m+1)}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}b_{k}^{(m+1)}+8(N+1)^{2}r^{2N}e^{U_{k}}b_{k}^{(m+1)}=F_{k}^{m},\quad 0<r<\tau\epsilon_{k}^{-1},

and

b_{k}^{(m+1)}(0)=0.

The homogeneous equation has two fundamental solutions, one is

u_{1}=\frac{1-r^{2N+2}}{1+r^{2N+2}}.

The second fundamental solution
u_{2}
satisfies
|u_{2}(r)|\leq C\log\frac{1}{r}
near
0
and
\infty
. We can construct
b_{k}^{(m+1)}
from
b_{k}^{(m)}
as

b_{k}^{(m+1)}(r)=u_{1}(r)\int_{0}^{r}tF_{k}^{m}(t)u_{2}(t){\rm d}t+u_{2}(r)\int_{0}^{r}tF_{k}^{m}(t)u_{1}(t){\rm d}t.

If
b_{k}^{(m)}(t)
satisfies

|b_{k}^{(m)}(t)|\leq C\epsilon_{k}^{2}\log(2+t),

one can verify by direct computation that
b_{k}^{(m+1)}
satisfies the same bound. Thus by standard Brower fixed point theorem, there is a
b_{k}
such that

\Delta b_{k}+8(N+1)^{2}r^{2N}e^{U_{k}}b_{k}=-E_{k}-\hat{f}(b_{k}).

Theorem
3.1
is established.
\Box
