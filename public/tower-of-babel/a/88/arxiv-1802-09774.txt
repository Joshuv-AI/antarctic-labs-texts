Abstract
We study the termination problem for probabilistic term rewrite systems. We prove that the interpretation method is sound and complete for a strengthening of positive almost sure termination, when abstract reduction systems and term rewrite systems are considered. Two instances of the interpretation method—polynomial and matrix interpretations—are analyzed and shown to capture interesting and nontrivial examples when automated. We capture probabilistic computation in a novel way by way of multidistribution reduction sequences, this way accounting for both the nondeterminism in the choice of the redex and the probabilism intrinsic in firing each rule.

Interactions between computer science and probability theory are

also suggests a new model

[

]
,
and the

[

are known from the early days
of theoretical computer science.

The simplest way probabilistic choice can be made available in
programming is endowing the language of programs with an
operator modeling sampling from (one or many) distributions. Fair,
binary, probabilistic choice is for example perfectly sufficient to
get universality if the underlying programming language is itself
universal (e.g., see
[
9

).

Term rewriting

[
28
]

is a well-studied model of computation when no probabilistic behavior is involved.
It provides
a faithful model of pure functional programming which is, up to a
certain extent, also adequate for modeling higher-order parameter
passing
[
11
]
. What is peculiar in term rewriting is
that, in principle, rule selection turns reduction into a potentially
nondeterministic process. The following question is then a natural
one: is there a way to generalize term rewriting to a
fully-fledged

model of computation?
Actually, not
much is known about probabilistic term rewriting:
the definitions we find
in the literature are
one by Agha et al.
[
1
]
and
one by Bournez and Garnier
[
4
]
.
We base our work on the latter,
where
probabilistic rewriting is captured as a Markov decision process;
rule selection remains a nondeterministic process, but each rule
can have one of many possible outcomes, each with its own probability
to happen. Rewriting thus becomes a process in which both
nondeterministic and probabilistic aspects are present and
intermingled. When firing a rule, the reduction process implicitly
samples from a distribution, much in the same way as when performing
binary probabilistic choice in one of the models mentioned above.

In this paper, we first define a new, simple framework for discrete
probabilistic reduction systems, which properly generalizes standard
abstract reduction systems
[
28
]
. In particular, what
plays the role of a reduction sequence, usually a (possibly
infinite) sequence

of

is a sequence

of
(multi)distributions

over the set of states. A multidistribution is not merely a
distribution, and this is crucial to appropriately account for both
the probabilistic behaviour of each rule and the nondeterminism in
rule selection. Such correspondence does not exist in Bournez and Garnier’s
framework, as
nondeterminism has to be resolved by a
strategy
, in order to define
reduction sequences. However, the two frameworks turn out to be
equiexpressive, at least as far as every rule has finitely many possible outcomes.
We then prove that the probabilistic ranking functions
[
4
]
are
sound and complete

Related Work

Termination is a crucial property of programs, and has been widely
studied in term rewriting. Tools checking and certifying
termination of term rewrite systems are nowadays capable of
implementing tens of different techniques, and can prove termination
of a wide class of term rewrite systems, although the underlying
verification problem is well-known to be undecidable
[
28
]
.

Termination remains an interesting and desirable property in a probabilistic
setting, e.g., in probabilistic programming
[
19
]
where
inference algorithms often rely on the underlying program to
terminate. But what does termination
mean
when systems become
probabilistic? If one wants to stick to a
qualitative

definition, almost-sure termination is a well-known answer: a
probabilistic computation is said to almost surely terminate iff
non-termination occurs with null probability. One could even
require
positive
almost-sure termination, which
asks the expected time to termination to be finite.
Recursion-theoretically,
checking (positive) almost-sure termination is harder than checking
termination of non-probabilistic programs, where termination is at
least recursively enumerable, although undecidable: in a universal
probabilistic imperative programming language, almost sure termination
is
\Pi^{0}_{2}
complete, while positive almost-sure termination is

\Sigma^{0}_{2}
complete
[
22
]
.

Many sound verification methodologies for probabilistic termination
have recently been introduced (see,
e.g.,
[
4
,
5
,
17
,
14
,
8
]
). In particular,
the use of ranking martingales has turned out to be quite successful
when the analyzed program is imperative, and thus does not
have an intricate recursive structure. When the latter
holds, techniques akin to sized types have been shown to be applicable
[
10
]
.
Finally, as already
mentioned,
the current work can be seen as stemming from
the work by Bournez et al.
[
6
,
4
,
5
]
.
The added value compared to
their work are first of all the notion of multidistribution as way
to give an instantaneous description of the state of the underlying
system which exhibits both nondeterministic and
probabilistic features. Moreover, an interpretation method inspired by
ranking functions is made more general here, this way
acommodating not only interpretations over the real numbers, but also
interpretations over vectors, in the sense of matrix interpretations.
Finally, we provide an automation of polynomial and matrix interpretation inference
here, whereas nothing about implementation were presented in
Bournez’s work.

We write

A
(probability) distribution

\sum_{a\in A}d(a)=1

We say a distribution

\mathsf{Supp}(d)\mathrel{:=}\{a\in A\mid d(a)>0\}

Definition 1

\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{1\mathbin{:}\mathtt{a}}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{N}}\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{{\mathchoice{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptscriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}}\mathbin{:}\mathtt{b_{1}},{\mathchoice{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptscriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}}:\mathtt{b_{2}}}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{N}}\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{{\mathchoice{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptscriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}}\mathbin{:}\mathtt{c},{\mathchoice{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptscriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}}:\mathtt{c}}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{N}}\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{{\mathchoice{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptscriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}}\mathbin{:}\mathtt{d_{1}},{\mathchoice{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptscriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}}:\mathtt{d_{2}}}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$.\hss}

The final step is possible
because
\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{{\mathchoice{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptscriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}}\mathbin{:}\mathtt{c},{\mathchoice{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptscriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}}:\mathtt{c}}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}
is not collapsed to
\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{1\mathbin{:}\mathtt{c}}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}
.

When every probabilistic reduction in
\mathcal{A}
is of form
a\to\{1\mathbin{:}b\}
for some
b
,
then
\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}
simulates the non-probabilistic ARS
via the relation
\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{1\mathbin{:}\cdot}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{1\mathbin{:}\cdot}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}
.
Only a little care is needed as normal forms are followed by
\varnothing
.

Proposition 1

Let
{\hookrightarrow}
be an ARS and define
\mathcal{A}
by
a\to\{1\mathbin{:}b\}\in\mathcal{A}
iff

a\hookrightarrow b
.
Then
\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{1\mathbin{:}a}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}\mu
iff
either
a\hookrightarrow b
and
\mu=\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{1\mathbin{:}b}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}
for some
b
,
or
\mu=\varnothing
and
a
is a normal form in
\hookrightarrow
.

Proof

For
\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{1\mathbin{:}a}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}\mu
only the first two rules of Definition
3

are effective. Then the claim directly follows.

4.1
Notions of Probabilistic Termination

A binary relation
\to
is called
terminating
if it does not give rise to an infinite
sequence
a_{1}\to a_{2}\to\dots
. In a probabilistic setting,
infinite sequences are problematic only if they occur with

non-null
probability.

Definition 4
(AST)

A PARS
\mathcal{A}
is
almost surely terminating
(
AST
)
if for any reduction sequence

\{\mu_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathcal{A}(\mu)
,
it holds that

\lim_{n\to\infty}|\mu_{n}|=0
.

Intuitively,

|\mu_{n}|
is the probability of having
n
-step reducts,
so its tendency towards zero indicates that infinite reductions occur with zero probability.

Example 3
(Example
1
Revisited)

The system
{\mathcal{W}_{p}}
is AST for
p\leq\frac{1}{2}
, whereas
it is not for
p>\frac{1}{2}
.
Note that although
{\mathcal{W}_{{\mathchoice{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptscriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}}}}
is AST, the expected number of reductions
needed to reach a terminal is infinite.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a PARS
and
\vec{\mu}=\{\mu_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathcal{A}(\mu)
.
Following terminology from rewriting, we define the

expected derivation length

\mathsf{edl}(\vec{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
of
\vec{\mu}
by

\mathsf{edl}(\vec{\mu})\mathrel{:=}\sum_{i\geq 1}|\mu_{i}|\ .

Intuitively, this definition is equivalent to taking mean length of
terminal paths in
\vec{\mu}
.
The notion of
positive almost sure termination (PAST)
,
introduced by Bournez and Garnier
[
4
]
,
constitutes a refinement of AST demanding that the expected derivation length is finite
for every initial state
a
and for
every strategy
, i.e.,
for every reduction sequence
\vec{\mu}
starting from
a
,

\mathsf{edl}(\vec{\mu})
is bounded.
Without fixing a strategy, however, this condition does not ensure
bounds on the derivation length.

Example 4

Consider the (non-probabilistic) ARS on
\mathbb{N}\cup\{\omega\}

with reductions

\omega\to n
and
n+1\to n
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
It is easy to see that every reduction sequence is of finite length,
and thus, this ARS is PAST.
There is, however, no global bound on the length of reduction sequences starting from
\omega
.

Hence we introduce a stronger notion, which actually plays a more
essential role than PAST. It is based on a natural extension of

derivation height
from complexity analysis of term rewriting.

Definition 5
(Strong AST)

A PARS
\mathcal{A}
is
strongly almost surely terminating
(
SAST
)
if the
expected derivation height

\mathsf{edh}_{\mathcal{A}}(a)

of every
a\in A
is finite,
where
\mathsf{edh}_{\mathcal{A}}(a)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is defined as

\sup_{\vec{\mu}\in\mathcal{A}(\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{1\mathbin{:}a}\}\hskip-3.0pt\})}\mathsf{edl}(\vec{\mu})
.

In Example
4
, we make essential use of
\omega
that
admits infinitely many one-step reducts. Thus the ARS is not finitely
branching, and does not contradict the claims in
[
4
]
.
Nevertheless PAST and SAST does not coincide on finitely branching PARSs.
The following example is found by an anonymous reviewer.

Example 5

Consider PARS
\mathcal{A}
over

\mathbb{N}\cup\{{a_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}}\}
, consisting of

\displaystyle a_{n}

\displaystyle\to\{{\mathchoice{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptscriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}}\mathbin{:}a_{n+1},\mathrel{}{\mathchoice{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptscriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}}\mathbin{:}0\}

\displaystyle a_{n}

\displaystyle\to\{1\mathbin{:}2^{n}\cdot n\}

\displaystyle n+1

\displaystyle\to\{1\mathbin{:}n\}\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$.\hss}

Then
P
is finitely branching and PAST, because every reduction sequence from
\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{1\mathbin{:}a_{0}}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}

is one of the following forms:

•

\vec{\mu}_{0}=\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{1\mathbin{:}a_{0}}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\rightsquigarrow\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{1\mathbin{:}0}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}

•

\vec{\mu}_{n}=\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{1\mathbin{:}a_{0}}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\rightsquigarrow^{n}\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{\frac{1}{2^{n}}\mathbin{:}a_{n},\mathrel{}\frac{1}{2^{n}}\mathbin{:}0}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\rightsquigarrow\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{\frac{1}{2^{n}}\mathbin{:}2^{n}\cdot n}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\rightsquigarrow^{2^{n}\cdot n}\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{\frac{1}{2^{n}}\mathbin{:}0}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}

with
n=1,2,\dots

•

\vec{\mu}_{\infty}=\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{1\mathbin{:}a_{0}}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\rightsquigarrow\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{\frac{1}{2}\mathbin{:}a_{1},\mathrel{}\frac{1}{2}\mathbin{:}0}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\rightsquigarrow\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{\frac{1}{4}\mathbin{:}a_{2},\mathrel{}\frac{1}{4}\mathbin{:}0}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\rightsquigarrow\cdots

and
\mathsf{edl}(\vec{\mu}_{\alpha})
is finite for each
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{{\infty}\}
.
However,
\mathsf{edh}_{\mathcal{A}}(a_{0})
is not bounded,
since

\mathsf{edl}(\vec{\mu}_{n})=\frac{1}{2^{0}}+\cdots+\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}+\frac{1}{2^{n}}+\frac{1}{2^{n}}(2^{n}\cdot n)\geq n
.

4.2
Probabilistic Ranking Functions

Bournez and Garnier
[
4
]
generalized
ranking
functions
, a popular and classical method for proving termination
of non-probabilistic systems, to PARS. We give here a simpler but
equivalent definition of probabilistic ranking function, taking
advantage of the notion of multidistribution.

For a (multi)distribution
\mu
over real numbers,
the
expected value
of
\mu
is denoted by

\mathbb{E}(\mu)\mathrel{:=}\sum_{p\mathbin{:}x\in\mu}p\cdot x
.
A function
f:A\to\mathbb{R}
is naturally generalized to

f:\mathsf{FMDist}(A)\to\mathsf{FMDist}(\mathbb{R})
,
so for
\mu\in\mathsf{FMDist}(A)
,
\mathbb{E}(f(\mu))=\sum_{p\mathbin{:}x\in\mu}p\cdot f(x)
.
For
\epsilon>0
we define the order
>_{\epsilon}
on
\mathbb{R}
by

x>_{\epsilon}y
iff
x\geq\epsilon+y
.

Definition 6

Given a PARS
\mathcal{A}
on
A
,
we say that a function
f:A\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a

(probabilistic) ranking function

(sometimes referred to as
Lyapunov
ranking function),
if there exists
\epsilon>0
such that

a\to d\in\mathcal{A}
implies

f(a)>_{\epsilon}\mathbb{E}(f(d))
.

The above definition slightly differs from the formulation in
[
4
]
:
the latter demands
the
drift

\mathbb{E}(f(d))-f(a)
to at least
-\epsilon
,
which is equivalent to
f(a)>_{\epsilon}\mathbb{E}(f(d))
;
and allows any lower bound
\inf_{a\in A}f(a)>-\infty
,
which can be easily turned into
0
by adding the lower bound to the ranking function.

We prove that a ranking function ensures
SAST and gives a bound on expected derivation length.
Essentially the same result can be found in
[
8
]
,
but we use only elementary mathematics not requiring notions from probability theory.
We moreover show that this method is complete for proving SAST.

Lemma 1

Let
f
be a ranking function for a PARS
\mathcal{A}
.
Then there exists
\epsilon>0
such that

\mathbb{E}(f(\mu))\geq\mathbb{E}(f(\nu))+\epsilon\cdot|\nu|

whenever
\mu\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}\nu
.

Proof

As
f
is a ranking function for
\mathcal{A}
,
we have
\epsilon>0
such that

a\to d\in\mathcal{A}
implies

f(a)>_{\epsilon}\mathbb{E}(f(d))
.
Consider
\mu\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}\nu
.
We prove the claim by induction on the derivation of

\mu\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}\nu
.

•

Suppose
\mu=\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{1\mathbin{:}a}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}
and
a\in\mathsf{TRM}(\mathcal{A})
.
Then
\nu=\varnothing
and

\mathbb{E}(f(\mu))\geq 0=\mathbb{E}(f(\nu))+\epsilon\cdot|\nu|
since

\mathbb{E}(f(\varnothing))=|\varnothing|=0
.

•

Suppose
\mu=\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{1\mathbin{:}a}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}
and
a\to\nu\in\mathcal{A}
.
From the assumption

\mathbb{E}(f(\mu))=f(a)>_{\epsilon}\mathbb{E}(f(\nu))
, and
as
|\nu|=1
we conclude

\mathbb{E}(f(\mu))\geq\mathbb{E}(f(\nu))+\epsilon\cdot|\nu|
.

•

Suppose
\mu=\biguplus_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\cdot\mu_{i}
,

\nu=\biguplus_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\cdot\nu_{i}
, and

\mu_{i}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}\nu_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.
Induction hypothesis gives

\mathbb{E}(f(\mu_{i}))\geq\mathbb{E}(f(\nu_{i}))+\epsilon\cdot|\nu_{i}|
.
Thus,

\mathbb{E}(f(\mu))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\cdot\mathbb{E}(f(\mu_{i}))\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\cdot(\mathbb{E}(f(\nu_{i}))+\epsilon\cdot|\nu_{i}|)\\
=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\cdot\mathbb{E}(f(\nu_{i}))+\epsilon\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\cdot|\nu_{i}|=\mathbb{E}(f(\nu))+\epsilon\cdot|\nu|\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$.\hss}

Lemma 2

Let
f
be a ranking function for PARS
\mathcal{A}
.
Then there is
\epsilon>0
such that

\mathbb{E}(f(\mu_{0}))\geq\epsilon\cdot\mathsf{edl}(\vec{\mu})

for every
\vec{\mu}=\{\mu_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathcal{A}(\mu_{0})
.

Proof

We first show

\mathbb{E}(f(\mu_{m}))\geq\sum_{i=m+1}^{n}|\mu_{i}|
for every
n\geq m
,
by induction on
m-n
.
Let
\epsilon
be given by Lemma
1
.
The base case is trivial, so let us consider the inductive step.
By Lemma
1
and induction hypothesis we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(f(\mu_{m}))

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}(f(\mu_{m+1}))+\epsilon\cdot|\mu_{m+1}|

\displaystyle\geq\epsilon\cdot\sum_{i=m+2}^{n}|\mu_{i}|+\epsilon\cdot|\mu_{m+1}|=\epsilon\cdot\sum_{i=m+1}^{n}|\mu_{i}|\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$.\hss}

By fixing
m=0
, we conclude that
the sequence
\bigl\{\epsilon\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\mu_{i}|\bigr\}_{n\geq 1}
is bounded by

\mathbb{E}(f(\mu_{0}))
,
and so is its limit
\epsilon\cdot\sum_{i\geq 1}|\mu_{i}|=\epsilon\cdot\mathsf{edl}(\vec{\mu})
.
∎

Theorem 4.1

Ranking functions are sound and complete for proving SAST.

Proof

For soundness,
let
f
be a ranking function for a PARS
\mathcal{A}
.
For every derivation

\vec{\mu}
starting from
\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{1\mathbin{:}a}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}
,
we have

\mathsf{edl}(\vec{\mu})\leq\frac{f(a)}{\epsilon}

by Lemma
2
.
Hence,

\mathsf{edh}_{\mathcal{A}}(a)\leq\frac{f(a)}{\epsilon}
, concluding that
\mathcal{A}
is SAST.

For completeness, suppose that
\mathcal{A}
is SAST, and let
a\to d\in\mathcal{A}
.
Then we have
\mathsf{edh}_{\mathcal{A}}(a)\in\mathbb{R}
, and

\displaystyle\mathsf{edh}_{\mathcal{A}}(a)\ =\sup_{\vec{\mu}\in\mathcal{A}(\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{1\mathbin{:}a}\}\hskip-3.0pt\})}\mathsf{edl}(\vec{\mu})\

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\vec{\mu}\in\mathcal{A}(d)}(1+\mathsf{edl}(\vec{\mu}))

\displaystyle=\ 1+\sup_{\vec{\mu}\in\mathcal{A}(d)}\mathsf{edl}(\vec{\mu})\ \ =\ 1+\mathbb{E}(\mathsf{edh}_{\mathcal{A}}(d))\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$,\hss}

concluding
\mathsf{edh}_{\mathcal{A}}(a)>_{1}\mathbb{E}(\mathsf{edh}_{\mathcal{A}}(d))
.
Thus, taking
\epsilon=1
,
\mathsf{edh}_{\mathcal{A}}
is a ranking function according to
Definition
6
.
∎

4.3
Relation to Formulation by Bournez and Garnier

As done by Bournez and Garnier
[
4
]
, the dynamics of probabilistic systems are commonly defined as stochastic sequences, i.e.,
infinite sequences of random variables whose
n
-th variable represents the
n
-th reduct.
A disadvantage of this approach is that
nondeterministic choices have to be
a priori

resolved by means of strategies,
making the rewriting-based understanding of nondeterminism inapplicable.
We now relate this formulation
to ours, and see that the corresponding notions of AST and PAST coincide.

We shortly recap central definitions from
[
4
]
.
We assume basic familiarity with stochastic processes, see e.g.
[
7
]
. Here we fix a PARS
\mathcal{A}
on
A
.
A
history
(of length
n+1
) is a finite sequence

\vec{a}=a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
of objects from
A
, and such a sequence is called

terminal
if
a_{n}
is.
A
strategy

\phi
is a function from nonterminal histories to distributions such that

a_{n}\to\phi(a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathcal{A}
.
A history
a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
is called

realizable under
\phi
iff for every
0\leq i<n
, it holds that

\phi(a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{i})(a_{i+1})>0
.

Definition 7
(Stochastic Reduction,
[
4
]
)

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a PARS on
A
and
\bot\notin A
a special symbol.
A sequence of random variables

\vec{X}=\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
over
A\cup\{\bot\}

is a
(stochastic) reduction
in
\mathcal{A}

(under strategy
\phi
)
if

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}=\bot\mid X_{n}=\bot)

\displaystyle=1;

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}=\bot\mid X_{n}=a)

\displaystyle=1

if
a
is terminal;

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}=\bot\mid X_{n}=a)

\displaystyle=0

if
a
is nonterminal;

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}=a\mid X_{n}=a_{n},\dots,X_{0}=a_{0})

\displaystyle=d(a)

if
\phi(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})=d
,

where
a_{0},\dots,a_{n}
is a realizable nonterminal history under
\phi
.

Notice that as an immediate consequence of the law of total probability we obtain

\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=a_{n})=\sum_{\mathclap{a_{0},\dots,a_{n}\in A}}\mathbb{P}(X_{0}=a_{0},\dots,X_{n}=a_{n})\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$.\hss}

(2)

Thus,
\vec{X}
is set up so that trajectories correspond to
reductions
a_{0}\to_{\mathcal{A}}a_{1}\to_{\mathcal{A}}\cdots
,
and

\bot
signals termination.
In correspondence, the derivation length is given by the
first hitting time
to
\bot
:

Definition 8
(AST/PAST of
[
4
]
)

For
\vec{X}=\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

define the random variable
T_{\vec{X}}\mathrel{:=}\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid X_{n}=\bot\}
, where
\min\varnothing=\infty
by convention.
A PARS
\mathcal{A}
is
stochastically AST
(resp. PAST) if
for every stochastic reduction
\vec{X}
in
\mathcal{A}
,

\mathbb{P}(T_{\vec{X}}=\infty)=0

(resp.
\mathbb{E}(T_{\vec{X}})<\infty
).

We will now see that stochastic AST/PAST coincides with AST/PAST.
To this end, we first clarify the correspondence of stochastic reductions and reductions over multidistributions.
The quintessence of this correspondence is that any stochastic derivation
\vec{X}=\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

translates to a reduction
\mu_{0}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}\mu_{1}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}\mu_{2}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}\dots

so that the probabilities
p=\mathbb{P}(X_{0}=a_{0},\dots,X_{n}=a_{n})>0
of realisable
histories
a_{0},\dots,a_{n}
in
\vec{X}
are recorded in
\mu_{n}
, i.e.,
(p:a_{n})\in\mu_{n}
.
Equation (
2
) then gives the correspondence between
X_{n}
and
\mu_{n}
.
With Lemmas
4
and
6
, we make this correspondence precise.
Guided by (
2
), we associate the multidistribution
\mu
over
A
with the distribution over
A\cup\{\bot\}

such that
\overline{\mu}(a)\mathrel{:=}\sum_{p\mathbin{:}a\in\mu}p
for all
a\in A
, and

\overline{\mu}(\bot)\mathrel{:=}1-|\mu|
.

Stochastic Reduction to Multidistribution Reduction.

Here we fix a stochastic reduction
\vec{X}
in a PARS
\mathcal{A}
. We show that
\vec{X}
corresponds to a multidistribution reduction,
assuming
X_{0}
is finitely supported:

\mathbb{P}(X_{0}=a)>0
for only finitely many
a
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the multidistribution
\nu_{n}
over realizable histories of length
n
by

\nu_{n}\mathrel{:=}\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{p:(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})\mid p=\mathbb{P}(X_{0}=a_{0},\dots,X_{n}=a_{n})>0}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$.\hss}

Note that
\nu_{0}
is well defined since
X_{0}
is finitely supported.
Then we can inductively show that
\nu_{n}
is well defined, using the fact that

\mathsf{Supp}(d)
is finite for every
a\to d\in\mathcal{A}
.

The following lemma clarifies then how the multidistributions
\nu_{n}
evolve.

Lemma 3

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\nu_{n+1}=\biguplus_{p\mathbin{:}(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})\in\nu_{n},a_{n}\notin\mathsf{TRM}(\mathcal{A})}\begin{array}[]{l}\{\hskip-3.0pt\{p\cdot\phi(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})(a_{n+1}):(a_{0},\dots,a_{n},a_{n+1})\\
{}\mid\phi(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})(a_{n+1})>0\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\end{array}\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$.\hss}

Proof

Fix a realizable history
(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})
and let
d=\phi(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})
.
Notice that
(a_{0},\dots,a_{n},a_{n+1})
is realizable if
d(a_{n+1})>0
.
Recall
\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}=a_{n+1}\mid X_{0}=a_{0},\dots,X_{n}=a_{n})=d(a_{n+1})
.
By definition of conditional probability we thus have

\mathbb{P}(X_{0}=a_{0},\dots,X_{n+1}=a_{n+1})=d(a_{n+1})\cdot\mathbb{P}(X_{0}=a_{0},\dots,X_{n}=a_{n})\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$,\hss}

as desired.
∎

Lemma 4

There exist multidistributions
\mu_{0},\mu_{1},\dots
such that
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\overline{\mu_{n}}(a)=\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=a)
and
\mu_{n}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}\mu_{n+1}
.

Proof

From
\nu\in\mathsf{FMDist}(A^{n})
define multidistribution

\mathsf{last}(\nu)\mathrel{:=}\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{p:{a_{n}}\mid p:(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})\in\nu}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$.\hss}

We show
\mu_{n}\mathrel{:=}\mathsf{last}(\nu_{n})

satisfies the desired properties.
It is easy to see that
\overline{\mu_{n}}(a)=\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=a)
.
We show
\mathsf{last}(\nu_{n})\rightsquigarrow\mathsf{last}(\nu_{n+1})
.
Consider arbitrary
p\mathbin{:}(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})\in\nu_{n}
.
If
a_{n}
is terminal,
we have

\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{1\mathbin{:}a_{n}}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}\varnothing\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$,\hss}

and otherwise
we have
a_{n}\to\phi(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathcal{A}
,
so

\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{1\mathbin{:}a_{n}}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}\phi(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$.\hss}

Combining them we get

\displaystyle\mathsf{last}(\nu_{n})

\displaystyle=\biguplus_{p:(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})\in\nu_{n}}p\cdot\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{1:a_{n}}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}

\displaystyle\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}\biguplus_{p:(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})\in\nu_{n},a_{n}\notin\mathsf{TRM}(\mathcal{A})}p\cdot\phi(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{last}(\nu_{n+1})\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$.\hss}

The last equation follows from Lemma

.
∎

Multidistribution Reduction to Stochastic Reduction.

For the inverse translation, let us fix a PARS
\mathcal{A}

and
M=\{\mu_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\mu_{0}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}\mu_{1}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}\dots
and
\mu_{0}
is a distribution.
We now map
M
to a stochastic sequence by fixing a strategy
\phi_{M}
according to
M
.
As a first step, let us construct a sequence of multidistributions
\xi_{n}
over realizable histories from
M

in such a way that the multidistribution
\xi_{n}
assigns the
probability
p
to the history
a_{0},\dots,a_{n}
precisely when
the occurrence
p:a_{n}\in\mu_{n}
was developed through a sequence of steps

a_{0}\to_{\mathcal{A}}\dots\to_{\mathcal{A}}a_{n}
in
\mu_{0}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}\cdots\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}\mu_{n}
.

Definition 9

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that there is a step
\mu_{n-1}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}\mu_{n}
in
M
,
we define the multidistribution
\xi_{n}
over realizable histories of length

, with
\mathsf{last}(\xi_{n})=\mu_{n}
, inductively as follows.
Here,
\mathsf{last}(\xi_{n})
is defined as in Lemma
4
.
First, we set
\xi_{1}\mathrel{:=}\mu_{0}
.
In the inductive case, observe that for each nonterminal history
(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})
occurring in
\xi_{n}

there exists a transition
a_{n}\to d_{a_{0},\dots,a_{n}}\in\mathcal{A}

so that

\displaystyle\mu_{n}

\displaystyle=\biguplus_{p:(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})\in\xi_{n}}\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{p\mathbin{:}a_{n}}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}

\displaystyle\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{A}}\biguplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}p:(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})\in\xi_{n}\\
a_{n}\notin\mathsf{TRM}(\mathcal{A})\\
a_{n+1}\in\mathsf{Supp}(d_{a_{0},\dots,a_{n}})\end{subarray}}\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{p\cdot d_{a_{0},\dots,a_{n}}(a_{n+1})\mathbin{:}a_{n+1}}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}=\mu_{n+1}\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$.\hss}

(3)

We set

\xi_{n+1}\mathrel{:=}\biguplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}p:(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})\in\xi_{n}\\
a_{n}\notin\mathsf{TRM}(\mathcal{A})\\
a_{n+1}\in\mathsf{Supp}(d_{a_{0},\dots,a_{n}})\end{subarray}}\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{p\cdot d_{a_{0},\dots,a_{n}}(a_{n+1}):(a_{0},\dots,a_{n+1})}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$.\hss}

Note that when
\mu_{n}=\varnothing
we have
\xi_{n+1}=\varnothing
.

Crucially, even if two objects occurring in
\mu_{n}
are equal, they are separated by their history in
\xi_{n}
.
In other words:

Lemma 5

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
\xi_{n}
defined, we have that
\mathsf{Supp}(\xi_{n})
is a set.

Proof

The proof is by induction on
n
.
The base case is trivial, as
\mu_{0}
and thus
\xi_{0}
is a distribution.
The inductive step follows directly from the induction hypothesis and the fact that the supports
\mathsf{Supp}(d)
,
for the distributions
d
mentioned in (

), are sets.
∎

This then justifies the following definition of the strategy
\phi_{M}
, which we use in the simulation of
M
below.

Definition 10

We define the strategy
\phi_{M}
so that

\phi_{M}(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})=d_{a_{0},\dots,a_{n}}
,
such that,
if
(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})
is a nonterminal histories,
then
a_{n}\to d_{a_{0},\dots,a_{n}}\in\mathcal{A}
is
used in (
3
) to reduce the occurrence of

in
\mu_{n}
,
and otherwise

is arbitrary.

Lemma 6
(Reduction to Stochastic Sequences)

Let
\vec{X}=\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the stochastic derivation under strategy
\phi_{M}

with
\mathbb{P}(X_{0}=a)=\mu_{0}(a)
.
Then

\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=a)=\overline{\mu_{n}}(a)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof

We show that
p:(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})\in\xi_{n}
iff
\mathbb{P}(X_{0}=a_{0},\dots,X_{n}=a_{n})=p>0
.
Using that
\mathsf{last}(\xi_{n})=\mu_{n}
, the lemma follows then from (
2
).
The proof is by induction on
n
.

The base case is trivial, as
\xi_{0}=\mu_{0}
corresponds to the starting distribution
d
of
\vec{X}
.
Concerning the inductive step, it suffices to realise that

p:(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})\in\xi_{n}
if and only if

p\cdot q:(a_{0},\dots,a_{n+1})\in\xi_{n+1}
,
a_{n}
is nonterminal
and
q\mathrel{:=}\phi_{M}(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})(a_{n+1})>0
.
As
\mathbb{P}(X_{0}=a_{0},\dots,X_{n}=a_{n},X_{n+1}=a_{n+1})=q\cdot\mathbb{P}(X_{0}=a_{0},\dots,X_{n}=a_{n})
by definition,
the lemma follows then from induction hypothesis.
∎

Relating AST and PAST to its stochastic versions.

We have established a one-to-one correspondence between infinite multidistribution reductions
M
and stochastic reductions
\vec{X}
.
It is then not difficult to establish a correspondence between the expected derivation length of
M

and the time of termination of
\vec{X}
, relying on the following auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 7

Let
\vec{X}=\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a
stochastic derivation in
\mathcal{A}

with finitely supported
X_{0}
,
and
M=\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
a sequence of multidistributions
satisfying
\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=a)=\overline{\mu_{n}}(a)
.
The following two properties hold.

1.

\mathbb{P}(T_{\vec{X}}\geq n)=|\mu_{n}|
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

2.

\mathbb{P}(T_{\vec{X}}=\infty)=\lim_{n\to\infty}|\mu_{n}|
.

Proof

Concerning the first property, we have

\mathbb{P}(T_{\vec{X}}\geq n)=\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\in A)=\sum_{a\in A}\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=a)=\sum_{a\in A}\overline{\mu_{n}}(a)=|\mu_{n}|\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$,\hss}

for all
n\in N
, where the penultimate equation follows from the assumption, and the last from the definition of
\overline{\mu_{n}}
.
As we have

\mathbb{P}(T_{\vec{X}}=\infty)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(T_{\vec{X}}\geq n)\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$,\hss}

the second property thus follows from the first.
∎

Theorem 4.2

A PARS
\mathcal{A}
is (P)AST if and only if it is stochastically (P)AST.

Proof

We consider the “if” direction first.
Suppose
\mathcal{A}
is AST.
Lemma
4
translates an
arbitrary stochastic derivation
\vec{X}=\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in

to a reduction

,
for which we have
\mathbb{P}(T_{\vec{X}}=\infty)=\lim_{n\to\infty}|\mu_{n}|=0

by Lemma
7
(

). Hence,
\mathcal{A}
is stochastically AST.
If
\mathcal{A}
is moreover PAST, using Lemma
7
(
1
)
and (
1
) we
get

\mathbb{N}\ni\mathsf{edl}(M)=\sum_{n\geq 1}|\mu_{n}|=\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(T_{\vec{X}}\geq n)=\mathbb{E}(T_{\vec{X}})\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$,\hss}

where we tacitly employ

.

The “only if” direction is proven dual, using Lemma
6
.
∎

5
Probabilistic Term Rewrite Systems

Now we formulate probabilistic term rewriting following
[
4
]
, and then lift the
interpretation method for term rewriting to the probabilistic case.

We briefly recap notions from rewriting; see
[
3
]
for an
introduction to rewriting. A
signature

F
is a set of

function symbols

\mathtt{f}
associated with their
arity

\ARITY(\mathtt{f})\in\mathbb{N}
. The set
T(F,V)
of
terms
over a signature

F
and a set
V
of variables (disjoint with
F
) is the least set
such that
\mathit{x}\in T(F,V)
if
\mathit{x}\in V
and

\mathtt{f}(t_{1},\dots,t_{\ARITY(\mathtt{f})})\in T(F,V)
whenever
\mathtt{f}\in F
and
t_{i}\in T(F,V)
for all
1\leq i\leq\ARITY(\mathtt{f})
.
A
substitution
is a mapping
\sigma:V\to T(F,V)
,
which is extended homomorphically to terms.
We write
t\sigma
instead of
\sigma(t)
.
A
context
is a term
C\in T(F,V\cup\{\Box\})

containing exactly one occurrence of a special variable
\Box
.
With
C[t]
we denote the term obtained by replacing
\Box
in
C
with
t
.

To define probabilistic rewriting,
we first extend substitutions and contexts to multidistributions as before:

\mu\sigma\mathrel{:=}\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{p_{1}\mathbin{:}t_{1}\sigma,\dots,\mathrel{}p_{n}\mathbin{:}t_{n}\sigma}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}

and
C[\mu]\mathrel{:=}\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{p_{1}\mathbin{:}C[t_{1}],\dots,\mathrel{}p_{n}\mathbin{:}C[t_{n}]}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}

for
\mu=\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{p_{1}\mathbin{:}t_{1},\dots,\mathrel{}p_{n}\mathbin{:}t_{n}}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}
.
Given a multidistribution
\mu
over
A
,
we define a mapping
\overline{\mu}:A\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by

\overline{\mu}(a)\mathrel{:=}\sum_{p:a\in\mu}p
,
which forms a distribution if
|\mu|=1
.

Definition 11
(Probabilistic Term Rewriting)

A
probabilistic rewrite rule
is a pair of
l\in T(F,V)
and

d\in\mathsf{FDist}(T(F,V))
, written
l\to d
. A

probabilistic term rewrite system (PTRS)
is a (typically
finite) set of probabilistic rewrite rules. We write

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}
for the PARS consisting of a probabilistic reduction

C[l\sigma]\to C[\overline{d\sigma}]
for every probabilistic rewrite rule

l\to d\in\mathcal{R}
, context
C
, and substitution
\sigma
.
We
say a PTRS
\mathcal{R}
is AST/SAST if
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}
is.

Note that,
for a distribution
d
over terms,
d\sigma
is in general a multidistribution;
e.g., consider
\{{\mathchoice{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptscriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}}\mathbin{:}x,\mathrel{}{\mathchoice{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\scriptscriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}}\mathbin{:}y\}\sigma
with
x\sigma=y\sigma
.
This is why we need
C[\overline{d\sigma}]
, which is a distribution,
to obtain a probabilistic reduction above.

Example 6

The random walk of Example
1
can be modeled by a PTRS
consisting of a single rule
\mathtt{s}(x)\to\{p\mathbin{:}x,\mathrel{}1-p\mathbin{:}\mathtt{s}(\mathtt{s}(x))\}
. To
rewrite a term, typically there are multiple choices of a subterm to
reduce (
redex
), which is up to strategy. For instance,

\mathtt{s}(\mathtt{f}(\mathtt{s}(\mathtt{0})))
has two possible reducts:
\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{p\mathbin{:}\mathtt{f}(\mathtt{s}(\mathtt{0})),\mathrel{}1-p\mathbin{:}\mathtt{s}(\mathtt{s}(\mathtt{f}(\mathtt{s}(\mathtt{0}))))}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}
and
\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{p\mathbin{:}\mathtt{s}(\mathtt{f}(\mathtt{0})),\mathrel{}1-p\mathbin{:}\mathtt{s}(\mathtt{f}(\mathtt{s}(\mathtt{s}(\mathtt{0}))))}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}
.

5.1
Interpretation Methods for Proving SAST

We now generalise the
interpretation method
for term rewrite systems to the probabilistic setting. The following notion is standard.

Definition 12
(
F
-Algebra)

An
F
-algebra

\mathcal{X}
on a non-empty carrier set
X

specifies the
interpretation

{\mathtt{f}}_{\mathcal{X}}:X^{\ARITY(\mathtt{f})}\to X
of
each function symbol
\mathtt{f}\in F
.
We say
\mathcal{X}
is
monotone
with respect to a binary relation

{\mathrel{\succ}}\subseteq X\times X

if
x\mathrel{\succ}y
implies

{\mathtt{f}}_{\mathcal{X}}(\dots,x,\dots)\mathrel{\succ}{\mathtt{f}}_{\mathcal{X}}(\dots,y,\dots)

for every
\mathtt{f}\in F
.
Given an
assignment

\alpha\mathrel{\colon}V\to X
,
the interpretation of a term is
defined as follows:

\left\llbracket t\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}\mathrel{:=}\begin{cases}\alpha(t)&\text{if $t\in V$,}\\
{\mathtt{f}}_{\mathcal{X}}(\left\llbracket t_{1}\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha},\dots,\left\llbracket t_{n}\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha})&\text{if $t=\mathtt{f}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})$.}\end{cases}

We write
s\mathrel{\succ}_{\mathcal{X}}t
iff
\left\llbracket s\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}\mathrel{\succ}\left\llbracket t\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}
for every assignment
\alpha
.

In the non-probabilistic case, the interpretation method refers to a class of termination
methods that use monotone
F
-algebras to embed reduction sequences into a well-founded order
\succ
.
This method is also complete:

Theorem 5.1
(cf.
[
28
]
)

A TRS
\mathcal{R}
is terminating iff
there exists an
F
-algebra
\mathcal{X}

which is monotone with respect to a well-founded order
\mathrel{\succ}

and satisfies
\mathcal{R}\subseteq{\mathrel{\succ}_{\mathcal{X}}}
.

For a completeness proof, the
term algebra

\mathcal{T}
, an

F
-algebra on
T(F,V)
such that
{\mathtt{f}}_{\mathcal{T}}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})=\mathtt{f}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
, plays a crucial role. In this term algebra,
assignments are substitutions, and

\llbracket t\rrbracket_{\mathcal{T}}^{\sigma}=t\sigma
.

Now we introduce a probabilistic version of interpretation methods,
which is sound and complete for proving SAST.
To achieve completeness, we first keep the technique as general as possible.
For an
F
-algebra
\mathcal{X}
,
we lift the interpretation of terms to multidistributions as before, i.e.,

\left\llbracket\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{p_{1}\mathbin{:}t_{1},\dots,\mathrel{}p_{n}\mathbin{:}t_{n}}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}\mathrel{:=}\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{p_{1}\mathbin{:}\left\llbracket t_{1}\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha},\dots,\mathrel{}p\mathbin{:}\left\llbracket t_{n}\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$.\hss}

Definition 13
(Probabilistic
F
-Algebra)

A
probabilistic monotone
F
-algebra

(\mathcal{X},{\sqsupset})

is an
F
-algebra
\mathcal{X}
equipped with
a relation
{\sqsupset}\subseteq X\times\mathsf{FDist}(X)
, such that
for every
\mathtt{f}\in F
,
{\mathtt{f}}_{\mathcal{X}}
is monotone
with respect to
\sqsupset
, i.e.,
x\sqsupset d
implies

{\mathtt{f}}_{\mathcal{X}}(\dots,x,\dots)\sqsupset\overline{{\mathtt{f}}_{\mathcal{X}}(\dots,d,\dots)}

where
{\mathtt{f}}_{\mathcal{X}}(\dots,\cdot,\dots)

is extended to (multi-)distributions.

The following is an extension of a notion from
[
20
]
.

Definition 14
(Collapsibility)

We say a relation
{\sqsupset}\subseteq X\times\mathsf{FDist}(X)

is
collapsible
if
there exist a function
\mathsf{G}:X\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\epsilon>0
such that

x\sqsupset d
implies

\mathsf{G}(x)>_{\epsilon}\mathbb{E}(\mathsf{G}(d))
.
We say a monotone
F
-algebra
(\mathcal{X},\sqsupset)
is collapsible if
\sqsupset
is.

For a relation
{\sqsupset}\subseteq X\times\mathsf{FDist}(X)
,
we define the relation
{\sqsupset_{\mathcal{X}}}\subseteq T(F,V)\times\mathsf{FDist}(T(F,V))

by

t\sqsupset_{\mathcal{X}}d

iff
\left\llbracket t\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}\sqsupset\overline{\left\llbracket d\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}}
for every assignment
\alpha:V\to X
.
The following property is easily proven by induction.

Lemma 8

Let
(\mathcal{X},\sqsupset)
be a probabilistic monotone
F
-algebra.
If
s\sqsupset_{\mathcal{X}}d
then

\left\llbracket s\sigma\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}\sqsupset\overline{\left\llbracket d\sigma\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}}

and

\left\llbracket C[s]\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}\sqsupset\overline{\left\llbracket C[d]\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}}

for arbitrary
\alpha
,

\sigma
, and

C
.

Proof

Let
d=\{p_{1}\mathbin{:}t_{1},\dots,\mathrel{}p_{n}\mathbin{:}t_{n}\}
.
Concerning the first property, define the assignment
\beta
by

\beta(x)=\left\llbracket x\sigma\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}
for every
x\in V
.
By structural induction on
t
, one can verify
\left\llbracket t\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\beta}=\left\llbracket t\sigma\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}
for all terms
t
.
Thus, from the assumption we get

\displaystyle\left\llbracket s\sigma\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}=\left\llbracket s\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\beta}\sqsupset\overline{\left\llbracket d\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\overline{\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{p_{1}\mathbin{:}\left\llbracket t_{1}\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\beta},\dots,\mathrel{}p_{n}\mathbin{:}\left\llbracket t_{n}\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\beta}}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}}

\displaystyle=\overline{\{\hskip-3.0pt\{{p_{1}\mathbin{:}\left\llbracket t_{1}\sigma\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha},\dots,\mathrel{}p_{n}\mathbin{:}\left\llbracket t_{n}\sigma\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}}\}\hskip-3.0pt\}}=\overline{\left\llbracket d\sigma\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}}\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$.\hss}

The second property is proven by induction on
C
, where
the base case follows directly from the assumption, and the inductive step
from monotonicity.
∎

Theorem 5.2
(Soundness and Completeness)

A PTRS
\mathcal{R}
is SAST iff
there exists a collapsible monotone
F
-algebra
(\mathcal{X},\sqsupset)

such that
\mathcal{R}\subseteq{\sqsupset_{\mathcal{X}}}
.

Proof

For the “if” direction, we show that the PARS
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}
is SAST using Theorem
4.1
.
Let
\alpha:V\to X
be an arbitrary assignment,
which exists as
X
is non-empty.
Consider
s\to d\in\widehat{\mathcal{R}}
.
Then we have

s=C[l\sigma]
and
d=C[\overline{d^{\prime}\sigma}]
for some
\sigma
,
C
, and
l\to d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
.
By assumption we have
l\sqsupset_{\mathcal{X}}d^{\prime}
, and
thus
\left\llbracket s\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}\sqsupset\overline{\left\llbracket d\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}}
by Lemma
8
.
The collapsibility of
\sqsupset
gives a function

\mathsf{G}:X\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\epsilon>0

such that

\mathsf{G}(\left\llbracket s\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha})>_{\epsilon}\mathbb{E}(\mathsf{G}(\overline{\left\llbracket d\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}}))
,
and by extending definitions
we easily see
\mathbb{E}(\mathsf{G}(\overline{\left\llbracket d\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}}))=\mathbb{E}(\mathsf{G}(\left\llbracket d\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}))
.
Thus
\mathsf{G}(\left\llbracket\cdot\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha})
is a ranking function.

For the “only if” direction, suppose that
\mathcal{R}
is SAST.
We show
(\mathcal{T},\widehat{\mathcal{R}})
forms a collapsible probabilistic monotone

F
-algebra orienting
\mathcal{R}
.

•

Since
\mathcal{R}
is SAST, Theorem
4.1
gives
a ranking function
f\mathrel{\colon}T(F,V)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\epsilon>0

for the underlying PARS
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}
.
Taking
\mathsf{G}=f
,
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}
is
collapsible.

•

Suppose
s\mathrel{\widehat{\mathcal{R}}}d
.
Then we have
s=C[l\sigma]
and
d=C[\,\overline{d^{\prime}\sigma}\,]
for some
C
,
\sigma
,
and
l\to d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
.
As

f(\dots,C,\dots)
is also a context,

f(\dots,s,\dots)\mathrel{\widehat{\mathcal{R}}}f(\dots,d,\dots)
,
concluding monotonicity.

•

For every probabilistic rewrite rule
l\to d\in\mathcal{R}
and
every assignment (i.e., substitution)
\sigma:V\to T(F,V)
,
we have

\llbracket l\rrbracket_{\mathcal{T}}^{\sigma}=l\sigma\mathrel{\widehat{\mathcal{R}}}\overline{d\sigma}=\overline{\llbracket d\rrbracket_{\mathcal{T}}^{\sigma}}
,
and hence
l\mathrel{\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathcal{T}}}d
.
This concludes
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathcal{T}}
. ∎

5.2
Barycentric Algebras

As probabilistic
F
-algebras are defined so generally, it is not
yet clear how to search them for ones that prove the termination of a
given PTRS. Now we make one step towards finding probabilistic
algebras, by imposing some conditions to (non-probabilistic)

F
-algebras, so that the relation
\sqsupset
can be defined from
orderings which we are more familiar with.

Let us introduce some auxiliary notions.

Definition 15
(Barycentric Domain)

A
barycentric domain
is a set
X
equipped with
the
barycentric operation

\mathbb{E}_{X}:\mathsf{FDist}(X)\to X
.

Of particular interest in this work will be the barycentric domains
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}

with barycentric operations

\mathbb{E}(\{p_{1}\mathbin{:}a_{1},\dots,\mathrel{}p_{n}\mathbin{:}a_{n}\})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\cdot a_{i}
.

We naturally generalize the following notions from standard mathematics.

Definition 16
(Concavity, Affinity)

Let
f:X\to Y
be a function from and to barycentric domains.
We say
f
is
concave
with respect to an order
{\mathrel{\succ}}
on
Y

if

f(\mathbb{E}_{X}(d))\mathrel{\succcurlyeq}\mathbb{E}_{Y}(\overline{f(d)})

where
\mathrel{\succcurlyeq}
is the reflexive closure of
\mathrel{\succ}
.
We say
f
is
affine
if it satisfies

f(\mathbb{E}_{X}(d))=\mathbb{E}_{Y}(\overline{f(d)})
.

Clearly, every affine function is concave.

Now we arrive at the main definition and theorem of this section.

Definition 17
(Barycentric
F
-Algebra)

A
barycentric
F
-algebra
is a pair
(\mathcal{X},{\mathrel{\succ}})
of
an
F
-algebra
\mathcal{X}
on a barycentric domain
X

and an order
\mathrel{\succ}
on
X
,
such that for every
\mathtt{f}\in F
,

{\mathtt{f}}_{\mathcal{X}}
is monotone and concave with respect to
\mathrel{\succ}
.
We say it is
collapsible
if there exist a concave function

\mathsf{G}\colon X\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
(with respect to
>
) and
\epsilon>0
such
that
\mathsf{G}(x)>_{\epsilon}\mathsf{G}(y)
whenever
x\mathrel{\succ}y
.

We define the relation
{\mathrel{\succ}^{\mathbb{E}}}\subseteq X\times\mathsf{FDist}(X)
by

x\mathrel{\succ}^{\mathbb{E}}d
iff
x\mathrel{\succ}\mathbb{E}_{X}(d)
.

Note that
the following theorem claims soundness but not completeness,
in contrast to Theorem
5.2
.

Theorem 5.3

A PTRS
\mathcal{R}
is SAST if
\mathcal{R}\subseteq{\mathrel{\succ}^{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{X}}}

for a collapsible barycentric
F
-algebra
(\mathcal{X},{\mathrel{\succ}})
.

Proof

Due to Theorem
5.2
,
it suffices to show that

(\mathcal{X},{\mathrel{\succ}^{\mathbb{E}}})
is a collapsible probabilistic monotone
F
-algebra.
Concerning monotonicity, suppose
x\mathrel{\succ}^{\mathbb{E}}d
, i.e.,

x\mathrel{\succ}\mathbb{E}_{X}(d)
,
and let
\mathtt{f}\in F
.
Since
{\mathtt{f}}_{\mathcal{X}}
is monotone and concave with respect to
\mathrel{\succ}
in every argument, we have

\displaystyle{\mathtt{f}}_{\mathcal{X}}(\dots,x,\dots)\mathrel{\succ}{\mathtt{f}}_{\mathcal{X}}(\dots,\mathbb{E}_{X}(d),\dots)\mathrel{\succcurlyeq}\mathbb{E}_{X}(\overline{{\mathtt{f}}_{\mathcal{X}}(\dots,d,\dots)})\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$.\hss}

Concerning collapsibility, whenever
x\mathrel{\succ}\mathbb{E}_{X}(d)

we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}(x)

\displaystyle>_{\epsilon}\mathsf{G}(\mathbb{E}_{X}(d))

by assumption on
\mathsf{G}
,

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}(\overline{\mathsf{G}(d)})

as
\mathsf{G}:X\to\mathbb{R}
is concave with respect to
>
,

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(\mathsf{G}(d))

by the definition of
\mathbb{E}
on multidistributions.

∎

Polynomial interpretations

were introduced
(on natural numbers
[

]

and real numbers

24

)

Various techniques for synthesizing polynomial interpretations (e.g.,

Definition 18
(Polynomial Interpretation)

A
polynomial interpretation
is an
F
-algebra
\mathcal{X}
on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

such that
\mathtt{f}_{\mathcal{X}}
is a polynomial for every
\mathtt{f}\in F
.
We say
\mathcal{X}
is
multilinear
if
every
{\mathtt{f}}_{\mathcal{X}}
is of the following form
with
\mathsf{c}_{V}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
:

{\mathtt{f}}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{V\subseteq\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}}\mathsf{c}_{V}\cdot\prod_{x_{i}\in V}x_{i}\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$.\hss}

In order to use polynomial interpretations for probabilistic termination,
multilinearity is necessary for satisfying the concavity condition.

Proposition 2

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a monotone multilinear polynomial interpretation and
\epsilon>0
.
If
\left\llbracket l\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}>_{\epsilon}\mathbb{E}(\left\llbracket d\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha})
for every
l\to d\in\mathcal{R}
and
\alpha
,
then the PTRS
\mathcal{R}
is SAST.

Proof

The order
>_{\epsilon}
is trivially collapsible with
\mathsf{G}(x)=x
.
Further,
every multilinear polynomial is affine and thus concave in all variables.
Hence
(\mathcal{X},>_{\epsilon})
forms a barycentric
F
-algebra,
and thus Theorem
5.3
shows that
\mathcal{R}
is SAST.
∎

An observation by Lucas
[
24
]
also holds in probabilistic case:
To prove a finite PTRS
\mathcal{R}
SAST with polynomial interpretations,
we do not have to find
\epsilon
, but
it is sufficient to check

l>^{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{X}}d
for all rules
l\to d\in\mathcal{R}
.
Define
\epsilon_{l\to d}\mathrel{:=}\mathbb{E}(\left\llbracket d\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha})-\left\llbracket l\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}
for
such
\alpha
that
\alpha(x)=0
.
Then for any other
\alpha
,
we can show
\mathbb{E}(\left\llbracket d\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha})-\left\llbracket l\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}\geq\epsilon_{l\to d}>0
.
As
\mathcal{R}
is finite,
we can take

\epsilon\mathrel{:=}\min\{\epsilon_{l\to d}\mid l\to d\in\mathcal{R}\}>0
.

Corollary 1

A PTRS
\mathcal{R}
is SAST if

\mathcal{R}\subseteq{>^{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{X}}}
for a monotone multilinear polynomial interpretation
\mathcal{X}
.

Matrix interpretations

are
introduced for the termination analysis of term rewriting

Now we extend them for probabilistic term rewriting.

Definition 19
(Matrix Interpretation)

A
(real) matrix interpretation
is
an
F
-algebra
\mathcal{X}
on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
such that
for every
f\in F
,
{\mathtt{f}}_{\mathcal{X}}
is of the form

{\mathtt{f}}_{\mathcal{X}}(\vec{x}_{1},\dots,\vec{x}_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_{i}\cdot\vec{x}_{i}+\vec{c}\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$,\hss}

(4)

where
\vec{c}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
,
and
C_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m\times m}
.
The order
{\gg_{\epsilon}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
is
defined by

(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})^{T}\gg_{\epsilon}(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})^{T}\mathrel{{:}\!\!{\iff}}x_{1}>_{\epsilon}y_{1}\text{ and }x_{i}\geq y_{i}\text{ for all $i=2,\dots,m$.}

It is easy to derive the following from Theorem
5.3
:

Proposition 3

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a monotone matrix interpretation and
\epsilon>0
.
If
\left\llbracket l\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha}\gg_{\epsilon}\mathbb{E}(\left\llbracket d\right\rrbracket_{\mathcal{X}}^{\alpha})
for every
l\to d\in\mathcal{R}
and
\alpha
,

\mathcal{R}
is SAST.

Proof

The order
\gg_{\epsilon}
is collapsible with
\mathsf{G}((x_{1},\dots,x_{m})^{T})=x_{1}
.
It is well known that (
4
) is affine and thus concave.
∎

As in polynomial interpretations, for finite systems we do not have to find
\epsilon

Monotonicity can be ensured

Let
\mathcal{R}

Example 8

Consider the PTRS consisting of the single probabilistic rule

\mathtt{a}(\mathtt{a}(x))\to\{p\mathbin{:}\mathtt{a}(\mathtt{a}(\mathtt{a}(x))),\mathrel{}1-p\mathbin{:}\mathtt{a}(\mathtt{b}(\mathtt{a}(x)))\}\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$.\hss}

Consider the two-dimensional matrix interpretation

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathtt{a}\rrbracket(\vec{x})=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\cdot\vec{x}+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix}\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$,\hss}

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathtt{b}\rrbracket(\vec{x})=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\cdot\vec{x}\hbox to0.0pt{$\;$.\hss}

Then we have

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathtt{a}(\mathtt{a}(x))\rrbracket^{\alpha}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}+x_{2}+1\\
1\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\gg_{1-2p}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}+x_{2}+2p\\
1\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=p\cdot\llbracket\mathtt{a}(\mathtt{a}(\mathtt{a}(x)))\rrbracket^{\alpha}+(1-p)\cdot\llbracket\mathtt{a}(\mathtt{b}(\mathtt{a}(x)))\rrbracket^{\alpha}

where
\alpha(x)=(x_{1},x_{2})^{T}
.
Hence this PARS is SAST if
p<\frac{1}{2}
, by Proposition
3
.

It is worthy of note that the above example cannot be handled with polynomial interpretations, intuitively
because monotonicity enforces the interpretation of the probable reducts
\mathtt{a}(\mathtt{a}(\mathtt{a}(x)))
and
\mathtt{a}(\mathtt{b}(\mathtt{a}(x)))
to be greater than that of the left-hand side
\mathtt{a}(\mathtt{a}(x))
.
Generally, polynomial and matrix interpretations are incomparable in strength.

7
Conclusion

This is a study on how much of the classic interpretation-based
techniques well known in term rewriting can be extended to

probabilistic
term rewriting, and to what extent they remain
automatable. The obtained results are quite encouraging, although
finding ways to combine techniques is crucial if one wants to capture
a reasonably large class of systems, similarly to what happens in
ordinary term rewriting
[
2
]
.
Another hopeful future work includes extending our result for proving AST,
not only SAST.
