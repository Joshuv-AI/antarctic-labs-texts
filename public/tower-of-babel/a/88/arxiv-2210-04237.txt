DMRG study of the higher-charge Schwinger model and its ’t Hooft anomaly Preprint: YITP-22-100, RIKEN-iTHEMS-Report-22
Masazumi Honda, Etsuko Itou, Yuya Tanizaki

Abstract
The charge- q q Schwinger model is the ( 1 + 1 ) (1+1) -dimensional quantum electrodynamics (QED) with a charge- q q Dirac fermion. It has the ℤ q \mathbb{Z}_{q} 1 1 -form symmetry and also enjoys the ℤ q \mathbb{Z}_{q} chiral symmetry in the chiral limit, and there is a mixed ’t Hooft anomaly between those symmetries. We numerically study the charge- q q Schwinger model in the lattice Hamiltonian formulation using the density-matrix renormalization group (DMRG). When applying DMRG, we map the Schwinger model to a spin chain with nonlocal interaction via Jordan-Wigner transformation, and we take the open boundary condition instead of the periodic one to make the Hilbert space finite-dimensional. When computing the energy density or chiral condensate, we find that using local operators significantly reduces the boundary effect compared with the computation of corresponding extensive quantities divided by the volume. To discuss the consequence of the ’t Hooft anomaly, we carefully treat the renormalization of the chiral condensates, and then we confirm that Wilson loops generate the discrete chiral transformations in the continuum limit.

2.1
Charge-
q
Schwinger model and its
\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{[1]}

The Euclidean action of the charge-

2

In this section we use the differential form notation since it is convenient to discuss ’t Hooft anomalies and topological aspects. The Lagrangian density for the gauge field in the component notation is

\frac{1}{4g^{2}}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}-\frac{\mathrm{i}\theta}{4\pi}\epsilon_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}
.

S=\int_{M_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2g^{2}}F\wedge\star F-\frac{\mathrm{i}\>\!\theta}{2\pi}F\right)+\int_{M_{2}}\overline{\psi}\left(\gamma^{\mu}_{\mathrm{E}}(\partial_{\mu}+\mathrm{i}\>\!qA_{\mu})+m\right)\psi\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x,

where
A
is the
U(1)

F=\mathrm{d}A
is the gauge-field strength,
\psi
is the fermion field, and
\gamma^{\mu}_{\mathrm{E}}
is the Euclidean gamma matrix satisfying
\{\gamma^{\mu}_{\mathrm{E}},\gamma^{\nu}_{\mathrm{E}}\}=2\delta^{\mu\nu}

The
U(1)

.
This quantization comes from the fact that the (large)

A\mapsto A+\mathrm{d}\lambda,\quad\psi\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\>\!q\lambda}\psi,

(2)

with
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda}:M_{2}\to U(1)

Accordingly, the

satisfies the Dirac quantization condition,
\int_{M_{2}}\mathrm{d}A\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
, on any oriented closed
2
-manifolds,
and thus the
\theta
angle becomes the
2\pi

When

, the theory enjoys the
\mathbb{Z}_{q}

1
-form symmetry, which we denote as
\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{[1]}
.
This is a remnant of the
U(1)
1-form symmetry in the pure Maxwell theory

q

\mathbb{Z}_{q}

The presence of the
\mathbb{Z}_{q}

1
-form symmetry implies that
the theory is completely decomposed into
q

Pantev:2005rh
;
Pantev:2005wj
;
Pantev:2005zs
;
Hellerman:2006zs
;
Hellerman:2010fv

A convenient way to observe this fact is to turn on a background gauge field
for the
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
1-form symmetry that is given by a
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
-valued 2-form gauge field.
For this purpose, we realize it as a pair of the
U(1)

2
-form gauge field
\mathcal{B}
and the
U(1)

1
-form gauge field
\mathcal{C}
, with the constraint

q\>\!\mathcal{B}=\mathrm{d}\mathcal{C}.

(3)

Due to this constraint, the holonomy of
\mathcal{B}
is quantized in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}\subset U(1)

\mathbb{Z}_{q}
gauge field.
The
2
-form gauge field has the
U(1)

1
-form gauge transformation,

\mathcal{B}\mapsto\mathcal{B}+\mathrm{d}\Lambda,\quad\mathcal{C}\mapsto\mathcal{C}+q\Lambda,

(4)

where the gauge parameter
\Lambda
itself is the
U(1)

1
-form gauge field.
Under this background gauge transformation, we impose that

A\mapsto A-\Lambda.

(5)

An action invariant under the above transformation with a minimal coupling is

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{gauged}}[\mathcal{B}]

\displaystyle=\int_{M_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2g^{2}}(F+\mathcal{B})\wedge\star(F+\mathcal{B})-\frac{\mathrm{i}\>\!\theta}{2\pi}(F+\mathcal{B})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{M_{2}}\overline{\psi}\left(\gamma^{\mu}_{\mathrm{E}}(\partial_{\mu}+\mathrm{i}(qA_{\mu}+\mathcal{C}_{\mu}))+m\right)\psi\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x.

(6)

By performing the path integral for dynamical fields,
we obtain the partition function with the background gauge field
\mathcal{B}

Z_{q}[g,\theta;\mathcal{B}]:=\int{\mathcal{D}}A{\mathcal{D}}\overline{\psi}{\mathcal{D}}\psi\exp\left(-S_{\mathrm{gauged}}[\mathcal{B}]\right).

(7)

While we can go back to the original partition function by simply taking
\mathcal{B}=0
,

q
Schwinger model to the usual
q=1

This is done by performing the path integral over
\mathcal{B}

In this process, we consider the following topological action

\mathrm{i}k\int_{M_{2}}\mathcal{B}.

(8)

Under the

1
-form gauge transformation, this is gauge invariant modulo

This quantity is quantized in

Let us perform the path integral over
\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle\int{\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{B}Z_{q}[\theta;\mathcal{B}]\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k\int\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle=\int{\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{B}{\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{C}\int{\mathcal{D}}A{\mathcal{D}}\overline{\psi}{\mathcal{D}}\psi\exp\left(-S_{\mathrm{gauged}}[\mathcal{B}]+\mathrm{i}k\int\mathcal{B}\right)

\displaystyle=\int{\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{C}{\mathcal{D}}\overline{\psi}{\mathcal{D}}\psi\exp\left(-S^{\prime}\right),

where

S^{\prime}=\int_{M_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2(qg)^{2}}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{C}\wedge\star\mathrm{d}\mathcal{C}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\theta+2\pi k}{2\pi q}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{C}\right)+\int_{M_{2}}\overline{\psi}\left(\gamma^{\mu}_{\mathrm{E}}(\partial_{\mu}+\mathrm{i}\mathcal{C}_{\mu})+m\right)\psi\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x.

(10)

This is nothing but the charge-

Therefore we find

\int{\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{B}Z_{q}[g,\theta;\mathcal{B}]\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k\int\mathcal{B}}=Z_{1}\left[qg,\frac{\theta+2\pi k}{q};\mathcal{B}=0\right].

(11)

We can undo the

Z_{q}[g,\theta;\mathcal{B}=0]=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\int{\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{B}Z_{q}[g,\theta;\mathcal{B}]\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k\int\mathcal{B}}=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}Z_{1}\left[qg,\frac{\theta+2\pi k}{q};\mathcal{B}=0\right].

The above relation (

2.2
Chiral symmetry and ’t Hooft anomaly

When we set the fermion mass to be zero,
m=0
, the classical theory enjoys the
U(1)
axial symmetry,

\overline{\psi}\mapsto\overline{\psi}^{\prime}=\overline{\psi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\alpha\overline{\gamma}_{\mathrm{E}}},\quad\psi\mapsto\psi^{\prime}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\alpha\overline{\gamma}_{\mathrm{E}}}\psi,

(13)

where
\overline{\gamma}_{\mathrm{E}}=\mathrm{i}\gamma^{1}_{\mathrm{E}}\gamma^{2}_{\mathrm{E}}
gives the chirality.
Quantum mechanically, we have the following change of the path integral measure
Fujikawa:1979ay

{\mathcal{D}}\overline{\psi}^{\prime}{\mathcal{D}}\psi^{\prime}={\mathcal{D}}\overline{\psi}{\mathcal{D}}\psi\exp\left(\frac{2\mathrm{i}q\alpha}{2\pi}\int\mathrm{d}A\right),

(14)

and thus the continuous axial symmetry is explicitly broken.
This is called the Adler-Bell-Jackiw (ABJ) anomaly
Adler:1969gk
;
Bell:1969ts
.
When
q>1
, however, there still exists a nontrivial subgroup,
\mathbb{Z}_{2q}
, of the axial transformation, which gives the genuine symmetry,

\overline{\psi}\mapsto\overline{\psi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{2\pi}{2q}\overline{\gamma}_{\mathrm{E}}},\quad\psi^{\prime}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{2\pi}{2q}\overline{\gamma}_{\mathrm{E}}}\psi.

(15)

We note that
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{2q}
gives the fermion parity, which is a part of the
U(1)
gauge redundancy, and thus the axial symmetry group is given by
(\mathbb{Z}_{q})_{\chi}
.

Under the presence of the background gauge field for the
1
-form symmetry, the discrete chiral symmetry is broken:

(\mathbb{Z}_{q})_{\chi}:Z_{q}[g,\theta;\mathcal{B}]\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\int\mathcal{B}}Z_{q}[g,\theta;\mathcal{B}].

(16)

This is the mixed ’t Hooft anomaly between the
1
-form symmetry and the discrete chiral symmetry
Anber:2018jdf
;
Anber:2018xek
;
Armoni:2018bga
;
Misumi:2019dwq
.
The anomaly matching condition claims that there must be
q
vacua associated with the spontaneous chiral symmetry breaking.

It is convenient to rephrase this fact from the viewpoint of (extended) operators.
In
2
spacetime dimensions, the
0
-form (ordinary) symmetries are generated by the topological
1
-dimensional objects,
and the
1
-form symmetries are generated by the topological
0
-dimensional objects,
i.e. local operators with the scaling dimension
=0
.
In the charge-
q
massless Schwinger model, there has to be a line operator
U_{\chi}(L)
defined on the loop
L
, which generates the
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
chiral symmetry, and there is also a local operator
V(x)
, which generates the
1
-form symmetry.
These operators are topological in the sense that infinitesimal deformations do not affect the correlation functions, which generalizes the Ward-Takahashi identity.

The fact that
U_{\chi}(L)
generates the
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
chiral symmetry can be seen from its commutation relation with the chiral condensate operator,

O_{\pm}(x):=\overline{\psi}(1\pm\overline{\gamma}_{\mathrm{E}})\psi(x).

(17)

Its real and imaginary parts are called the scalar and pseudo-scalar condensates, respectively:

O_{\pm}(x)=S(x)\pm\mathrm{i}\,P(x).

(18)

Let us consider the situation, where
x
is inside the loop
L
and we deform
L
to
L^{\prime}
so that
x
is outside the loop
L^{\prime}
, and then

U_{\chi}(L)O_{\pm}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{\pm\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}}{q}}U_{\chi}(L^{\prime})O_{\pm}(x).

(19)

This equality is understood as the operator identity, so we assume that we can deform the loop
L
to
L^{\prime}
without crossing any operators except
O_{\pm}(x)
.

Similarly, the presence of the
\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{[1]}
symmetry implies the presence of the topological point-like operator
V(x)
, which has the nontrivial commutation relation with the Wilson loop
W(C)=\exp(\mathrm{i}\oint_{C}A)
.
Let us move the location of
V
from a point
x
of the inside of
C
to another point
x^{\prime}
of the outside of
C
, then

V(x)W(C)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}}{q}}V(x^{\prime})W(C).

(20)

So far, everything we mentioned about
U_{\chi}(L)
and
V(x)
is the defining property of symmetry generators, and there is nothing specific to the charge-
q
Schwinger model.
The interesting feature of the charge-
q
Schwinger model is the mixed ’t Hooft anomaly (
16
).
In the language of the symmetry generators, the ’t Hooft anomaly implies the nontrivial commutation relation between the symmetry generators,

U_{\chi}(L)V(x)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}}{q}}U_{\chi}(L^{\prime})V(x^{\prime}),

(21)

where
x
is inside of
L
and
x^{\prime}
is the outside of
L^{\prime}
.

Let us compare (

) with (

).
This suggests that the chiral condensate operator
O_{+}(x)
becomes with the

-form symmetry generator

by performing the renormalization group (RG) transformation.

3

To be precise, we assume the existence of the mass gap for this statement, and we know that the massless Schwinger model is gapped due to the ABJ anomaly.

Similarly, the comparison between (

) and (

is identified with

Therefore, we can confirm the existence of ’t Hooft anomaly by checking the following equality,

\langle W(C)O_{+}(x)\rangle=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}}{q}}\langle W(C)O_{+}(x^{\prime})\rangle,

2.3
Analytical results

So far, we have discussed the kinematical aspects of the charge-
q
Schwinger model.
As the massless Schwinger model is exactly solvable
Schwinger:1962tp
;
Lowenstein:1971fc
;
Coleman:1975pw
;
Coleman:1976uz
;
Manton:1985jm
;
Faddeev:1986pc
;
Jayewardena:1988td
;
Hetrick:1988yg
;
Iso:1988zi
;
Smilga:1992hx
;

;

It is known that
there is a correspondence between Dirac fermion and compact boson in two dimensions.
This is called Abelian bosonization.
Specifically,
the kinetic term
\overline{\psi}\not{\partial}\psi
for the Dirac fermion
is mapped to
\frac{1}{8\pi}|\mathrm{d}\phi|^{2}

with a
2\pi
-periodic scalar field
\phi
.
The vector and axial currents correspond to

\overline{\psi}\gamma_{\mathrm{E}}^{\mu}\psi\leftrightarrow\frac{1}{2\pi}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\nu}\phi,\quad\overline{\psi}\,\overline{\gamma}_{\mathrm{E}}\gamma_{\mathrm{E}}^{\mu}\psi\leftrightarrow\frac{1}{2\pi}\partial_{\mu}\phi.

(23)

The chiral condensate operators are related as

O_{\pm}(x)\leftrightarrow C(M)\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{i}\phi},

(24)

where
C(M)
is a multiplicative renormalization constant proportional to the renormalization scale
M
.

The bosonized action of the charge-
q
Schwinger model is given by

S=\int_{M_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2g^{2}}\mathrm{d}A\wedge\star\mathrm{d}A+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi}(q\>\!\phi-\theta)\mathrm{d}A+\frac{1}{8\pi}d\phi\wedge\star\mathrm{d}\phi\right).

(25)

In the bosonized description, the discrete chiral symmetry is realized as the shift symmetry,
\phi\mapsto\phi+\frac{2\pi}{q}
.
By completing the square in terms of
\mathrm{d}A
, we find that
\phi
becomes the massive boson,

S=\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{M_{2}}\left((\partial_{\mu}\phi)^{2}+\frac{g^{2}}{\pi}(q\>\!\phi-\theta)^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}^{2}x+\frac{1}{2g^{2}}\int_{M_{2}}\left(F_{01}+\frac{\mathrm{i}g^{2}}{2\pi}(q\>\!\phi-\theta)\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}^{2}x.

(26)

Although this expression does not respect the
2\pi
periodicity of the compact boson
\phi
, it is useful to identify the mass gap
\mu^{2}
,

\mu^{2}=\frac{q^{2}g^{2}}{\pi}.

(27)

Since
\phi
and
A
are dual to each other via the topological coupling, this is often called as the photon mass of the massless Schwinger model.

In order to understand the consequence of ’t Hooft anomaly, we are interested in the phase of the chiral condensate operator.
For this purpose, we can take the massive limit,
g\to\infty
, where the mass gap becomes infinite.
In this case, the path integral for
A
becomes

\int{\mathcal{D}}A\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi}\int_{M_{2}}(\theta-q\>\!\phi)\mathrm{d}A\right)\propto\delta(\mathrm{d}\phi)\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n(\theta-q\phi)}.

(28)

The first factor on the right-hand-side is the consequence of the equation of motion,
\mathrm{d}\phi=0
, and thus
\phi
should be constant to obtain the nontrivial values of this path integral.
The second factor comes from the summation over the instanton sectors, and this becomes nonzero only when
\theta-q\>\!\phi\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
.
Therefore, because of the
2\pi
periodicity of
\phi
,
possible values of
\phi
are
\phi=(\theta+2\pi k)/q
with
k=0,1,\ldots,q-1
.
This implies that
the theory has
q
distinct vacua characterized by

\langle\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi}\rangle_{k}\sim\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\theta+2\pi k}{q}}.

(29)

This is exactly what we expect
for the spontaneous breaking of the
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
chiral symmetry
as a consequence of the ’t Hooft anomaly matching.

In order to confirm (
22
), we should perform the path integral under the presence of the Wilson loop
W(C)
.
Since we can write
W(C)^{p}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}p\int_{D}\mathrm{d}A}
with
C=\partial D
, the Wilson loop can be represented as the spacetime-dependent
\theta
angle, where

\theta(x)=\begin{cases}\theta&{\rm for}\ x\not\in D\\
\theta+2\pi p&{\rm for}\ x\in D\end{cases}.

(30)

We can perform the path integral of
A
exactly in the same way, and we find

\langle\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi(x)}\rangle_{k}\sim\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\theta(x)+2\pi k}{q}}.

(31)

Since the value of the
\theta
angle jumps by
2\pi
across the Wilson loop, we obtain the relation (
22
) as required by the ’t Hooft anomaly combined with the RG argument.

While the above discussion gives an immediate confirmation of the relation (
22
),
we have taken the limit
g\rightarrow\infty
in which the mass gap is infinite.
Let us also confirm the relation (
22
) in a more elementary fashion keeping
g
finite.
In this case, we set
M_{2}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
and take the rectangular Wilson loop
W(C_{T\times L})^{p}
, where
C_{T\times L}=\partial([-T/2,T/2]\times[-L/2,L/2])
.
By taking the limit

, the classical configuration becomes constant along the imaginary-time direction, and thus the classical equation of motion becomes

-\partial_{x}^{2}\phi(x)+\mu^{2}\left(\phi(x)-\frac{\theta(x)}{q}\right)=0,

(32)

where

\theta(x)=\begin{cases}\theta&{\rm for}\ |x|>\frac{L}{2}\\
\theta+2\pi p&{\rm for}\ |x|<\frac{L}{2}\end{cases}.

(33)

We solve this equation with the boundary condition
\phi(x)\to\frac{\theta}{q}
for
x\to\pm\infty

\phi(x)=\frac{\theta}{q}+\begin{cases}\frac{2\pi p}{q}\sinh\left(\frac{\mu L}{2}\right)\mathrm{e}^{\mu x}&{\rm for}\ x<-\frac{L}{2}\\
\frac{2\pi p}{q}\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mu L}{2}}\cosh\left(\mu x\right)\right)&{\rm for}\ -\frac{L}{2}<x<\frac{L}{2}\\
\frac{2\pi p}{q}\sinh\left(\frac{\mu L}{2}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\mu x}&{\rm for}\ x>\frac{L}{2}\end{cases}.

(34)

This tells us that

\phi(x)
is almost constant away from the Wilson loop at
x=\pm L/2
:

\phi(x)\simeq\begin{cases}\frac{\theta}{q}&{\rm for}\ |x|\gg\frac{L}{2}\\
\frac{\theta+2\pi p}{q}&{\rm for}\ |x|\ll\frac{L}{2}\end{cases}.

(35)

In particular, when
\mu L
is large,

\phi(x)
around the Wilson loop quickly changes from
\theta/q
to
(\theta+2\pi p)/q

if we go from outside to inside.
In terms of
\phi(x)
, the chiral condensate operator under the presence of the Wilson loop is then given by

O_{+}(x)=S(x)+\mathrm{i}\>\!P(x)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\gamma}qg}{2\pi^{3/2}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi(x)},

(36)

where the overall coefficient is determined from the knowledge on the charge-
1
Schwinger model
Jayewardena:1988td
;
Hetrick:1988yg
;
Iso:1988zi
;
Smilga:1992hx
;
Adam:1993fc
;
Adam:1997wt
.
Therefore, this is also almost constant away from the Wilson loop:

S(x)+\mathrm{i}\>\!P(x)\simeq\begin{cases}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\gamma}qg}{2\pi^{3/2}}\ \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\theta}{q}}&{\rm for}\ |x|\gg\frac{L}{2}\\
\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\gamma}qg}{2\pi^{3/2}}\ \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\theta+2\pi p}{q}}&{\rm for}\ |x|\ll\frac{L}{2}\end{cases},

(37)

where the
{\rm mod}(q)
structure of the

\mathbb{Z}_{q}
1-form symmetry.
When we cross the Wilson loop from outside to inside,
it quickly rotates along the circular arc from the angle
\theta/q
to
(\theta+2\pi p)/q
,
where rotating direction is counterclockwise for
p>0
and clockwise for
p<0
.

Lattice Hamiltonian formulation and numerical setup

In this section, we describe the lattice Hamiltonian formulation of the charge-
q
Schwinger model and explain our setup for the numerical computations.
In Sec.
3.1
, we introduce the lattice Hamiltonian
U(1)
gauge theory with the staggered fermion
Banks:1975gq
;
Carroll:1975gb
with a special emphasis on the charge-
q
model
Honda:2021ovk
.
We here adopt the recent proposal
Dempsey:2022nys
for the correspondence between the lattice and continuum parameters.
In Sec.
3.2
,

and their UV renormalization shall be discussed in detail.
In Sec.

3.1
Lattice Hamiltonian for charge-
q
Schwinger model with
\theta
term

The continuum Lagrangian in the Minkowski metric
\eta_{\mu\nu}=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1)
is given by

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2g^{2}}F_{01}^{2}+\frac{\theta}{2\pi}F_{01}+\mathrm{i}\,\overline{\psi}\gamma^{\mu}(\partial_{\mu}+\mathrm{i}\>\!q\>\!A_{\mu})\psi-m\,\overline{\psi}\psi,

(38)

where we take the gamma matrices as
\gamma^{0}=\sigma^{3}
,
\gamma^{1}=\mathrm{i}\sigma^{2}
and
\overline{\gamma}=\gamma^{0}\gamma^{1}=\sigma^{1}
.
We take the temporal gauge
A_{0}=0
for the canonical quantization.
The Gauss law is obtained by the equation of motion for
A_{0}
,

\partial_{1}\left(\frac{1}{g^{2}}F_{01}+\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\right)=q\,\psi^{\dagger}\psi(x),

(39)

where the dynamical charge
q
appears explicitly on the right-hand-side.
Introducing the canonical momentum
\Pi(x)=\frac{\delta L}{\delta\dot{A_{1}}(x)}
,
the Hamiltonian is given by

H(x)=\frac{g^{2}}{2}\left(\Pi-\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\right)^{2}-\mathrm{i}\,\bar{\psi}\gamma^{1}(\partial_{1}+\mathrm{i}\>\!q\>\!A_{1})\psi+m\bar{\psi}\psi.

(40)

The Gauss law constraint (
39
) is rewritten as

\partial_{1}\Pi(x)=q\,\psi^{\dagger}\psi(x).

(41)

The lattice regularization of the model can be found as follows.
Introducing a lattice with
N
sites and lattice spacing
a
, we define the staggered fermion
\chi_{n}
for the two-component Dirac fermion
\psi(x)
, where
n
labels the lattice site (
x=na
).
Here,
\chi_{n}
is a single component complex fermionic operator, and the Dirac fermion at
x
extends over two sites on the lattice:

\psi(x)\leftrightarrow\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{2\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\cr\chi_{2\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1}\end{pmatrix}.

(42)

The gauge field and its canonical momentum are represented by the link variables,

U_{n}\leftrightarrow\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}aA_{1}(x)},\quad L_{n}\leftrightarrow-\Pi(x),

(43)

which are defined on the link between the sites
n
and
n+1
.
The canonical commutation relations for the lattice fields are given

[L_{n},U_{m}]=U_{m}\delta_{nm},\quad\{\chi_{n},\chi_{m}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{nm}.

(44)

Then the lattice Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle H=J\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\left(L_{n}+\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\right)^{2}-\mathrm{i}w\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\Bigl[\chi_{n}^{\dagger}(U_{n})^{q}\chi_{n+1}-{\rm h.c.}\Bigr]-m_{\mathrm{lat}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(-1)^{n}\chi_{n}^{\dagger}\chi_{n},

(45)

where

J=\frac{g^{2}a}{2},\quad w=\frac{1}{2a}.

(46)

We relate the lattice fermion mass
m_{\mathrm{lat}}
with the continuum fermion mass
m
as

m_{\mathrm{lat}}=m-\frac{q^{2}g^{2}a}{8},

(47)

following the recent proposal
Dempsey:2022nys
, which is reviewed in Appendix
B
.

As in the continuum theory,
physical states are constrained by the lattice version of the Gauss law

L_{n}-L_{n-1}=q\Biggl[\chi_{n}^{\dagger}\chi_{n}-\frac{1-(-1)^{n}}{2}\Biggr].

(48)

We remove all degree of freedom for the variables
(U_{n},L_{n})
by taking open boundary condition
L_{0}=0
, solving the Gauss law and fixing the gauge so that
U_{n}=1
for all
n
.
Then the Hamiltonian is written pure in terms of the fermion operators as

\displaystyle H=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}w\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\Bigl[\chi_{n}^{\dagger}\chi_{n+1}-{\rm h.c.}\Bigr]-m_{\mathrm{lat}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(-1)^{n}\chi_{n}^{\dagger}\chi_{n}

\displaystyle+J\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\Biggl[\frac{\theta}{2\pi}+q\sum_{j=0}^{n}\left(\chi_{j}^{\dagger}\chi_{j}-\frac{1-(-1)^{j}}{2}\right)\Biggr]^{2},

(49)

which acts on a finite dimensional Hilbert space.
We note that the periodicity of
\theta
is lost by taking the open boundary condition in this formulation.

To see the ’t Hooft anomaly, we introduce the Wilson loop, which size is
\hat{\ell}
, on the lattice.
It corresponds to putting the two probe charges
+q_{p}
and
-q_{p}
with a distance
\hat{\ell}
.
It is realized by
introducing the position-dependent
\theta
-angle,

\displaystyle\vartheta_{n}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\theta+2\pi q_{p}&&\ {\rm for}\ \hat{\ell}_{0}\leq n<\hat{\ell}_{0}+\hat{\ell},\\
\theta&&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Under the open boundary condition, it would be appropriate to take

\hat{\ell}_{0}=\frac{N-\hat{\ell}-1}{2},

(52)

with odd
\hat{\ell}
for even
N
and even
\hat{\ell}
for odd
N
.
The lattice Hamiltonian in the presence of the probes is

\displaystyle H=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}w\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\Bigl[\chi_{n}^{\dagger}\chi_{n+1}-{\rm h.c.}\Bigr]-m_{\mathrm{lat}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(-1)^{n}\chi_{n}^{\dagger}\chi_{n}

\displaystyle+J\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\Biggl[\frac{\vartheta_{n}}{2\pi}+q\sum_{j=0}^{n}\left(\chi_{j}^{\dagger}\chi_{j}-\frac{1-(-1)^{j}}{2}\right)\Biggr]^{2},

(53)

where
\theta
in (
49
) is replaced by the position dependent one,
\vartheta_{n}
in (
3.1
).

At the end of this subsection, let us give a relation between
q=1
and general charge-
q
Schwinger model:

H\left(q,J,\vartheta_{n}\right)=H\left(1,q^{2}J,\frac{\vartheta_{n}}{q}\right),

(54)

and this realizes the decomposition (
12
) in the case of the open boundary condition.
This translation to
q=1
is possible because we take the open boundary condition.
If we took the periodic boundary condition, then we could not eliminate the spatial link variables completely by gauge fixing.
The spatial hopping term,
\chi^{\dagger}_{n}(U_{n})^{q}\chi_{n+1}
, genuinely depends on the choice of
q\geq 1
, and we cannot relate them by simple replacements of coupling constants.

3.2
Local observables and UV renormalization

In this subsection, we define energy density and chiral condensates as local observables in the lattice Hamiltonian formalism and discuss the treatment of their UV divergence.
Local operators play the central role in quantum field theories.
Moreover, when we use the open boundary condition, use of local operators has a huge advantage to reduce the boundary effects
Honda:2021ovk
.

Let us first define the local behavior of the energy
E(n)
at each site
n
.
As it is defined by the Hamiltonian density, we can simply obtain it by extracting the summand of (
53
) with one caution.
Since the staggered fermion is used, a pair of even and odd sites forms the actual spatial point and thus we have to take an average between the neighboring sites to define the physical local quantities.
As a result, we define the site-dependent energy density by
4
4

4

In this paper, we take a specific averaging around the site
n
. We may also consider more smooth one, such as
\sum_{n^{\prime}}\mathcal{N}\exp\left(-\frac{(n-n^{\prime})^{2}}{2n_{0}^{2}}\right)h^{w,M,J}_{n^{\prime}}
, and then the energy density is averaged over the region
\Delta x=an_{0}
. We can regard it as a local operator as long as it satisfies
a\ll an_{0}\ll\xi=\mu^{-1}
.

E_{\mathrm{bare}}(n)=\underbrace{\left(\frac{h_{n-1}^{w}}{4}+\frac{h_{n}^{w}}{2}+\frac{h_{n+1}^{w}}{4}\right)}_{\mbox{fermion kinetic term}}+\underbrace{\left(\frac{h_{n-1}^{M}}{4}+\frac{h_{n}^{M}}{2}+\frac{h^{M}_{n+1}}{4}\right)}_{\mbox{fermion mass term}}+\underbrace{\left(\frac{h_{n-1}^{J}}{4}+\frac{h_{n}^{J}}{2}+\frac{h_{n+1}^{J}}{4}\right)}_{\mbox{gauge kinetic term}},

(55)

where

\displaystyle h_{n}^{w}=-\mathrm{i}w(\chi_{n}^{\dagger}\chi_{n+1}-\chi_{n+1}^{\dagger}\chi_{n}),\quad h_{n}^{M}=-(-1)^{n}m_{\rm lat}\>\!\chi_{n}^{\dagger}\chi_{n},

\displaystyle h_{n}^{J}=J\Biggl[\frac{\vartheta_{n}}{2\pi}+q\sum_{j=0}^{n}\left(\chi_{j}^{\dagger}\chi_{j}-\frac{1-(-1)^{j}}{2}\right)\Biggr]^{2}.

(56)

This is a bare quantity, and its expectation value
\langle E_{\mathrm{bare}}(n)\rangle
is UV divergent when we take
a\to 0
.
We note that the Schwinger model is super-renormalizable as it only has the dimensionful couplings, so its UV divergence can be subtracted by the normal-ordering procedure.
Furthermore, since the UV divergence does not depend on the
\theta
parameter in our formulation, it can be simply achieved by subtracting the ground-state expectation value at
\theta=0
:

E(n)=E_{\mathrm{bare}}(n)-\left.\left\langle E_{\mathrm{bare}}\left([N/2]\right)\right\rangle\right|_{\theta=0}.

(57)

Next, we define the local scalar condensate,
\overline{\psi}\psi\rightarrow S(n)
, and local pseudo-scalar condensate,
-\mathrm{i}\overline{\psi}\overline{\gamma}\psi\rightarrow P(n)
, at site
n
by using the correspondence (
42
):

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{bare}}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{s_{n-1}}{4}+\frac{s_{n}}{2}+\frac{s_{n+1}}{4},\quad P_{\mathrm{bare}}(n)=\frac{p_{n-1}}{2}+\frac{p_{n}}{2},

(58)

where

\displaystyle s_{n}=(-1)^{n}w\,\chi_{n}^{\dagger}\chi_{n},\quad p_{n}=(-1)^{n+1}w\>\!(\chi_{n}^{\dagger}\chi_{n+1}-\chi_{n+1}^{\dagger}\chi_{n}).

(59)

Here, we take suitable average over neighboring sites to take into account the even-odd inequality of staggered fermion.
As we have done for the energy density, one can eliminate UV divergences of these quantities by subtracting their expectation values at
\theta=0
.
However, as we are going to confirm the anomaly relation (
37
), we would like to define the origin of the chiral condensate in the limit
m\to 0
.

As the UV divergence of chiral condensates comes only from the fermion one-loop diagram due to the super-renormalizability, we can evaluate the UV-divergent piece using free fermion:

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{div}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{m}{\pi\sqrt{1+(\mu a)^{2}}}\,K\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(\mu a)^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{m_{\mathrm{lat}}+\frac{q^{2}g^{2}a}{8}}{\pi\sqrt{1+(\mu a)^{2}}}\,K\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(\mu a)^{2}}}\right),

(60)

where
K(k)
denotes the complete elliptic integral of the first kind,

\displaystyle K(k)=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-k^{2}\sin^{2}t}}\mathrm{d}t.

(61)

We have replaced the fermion mass parameter in the loop integral by the mass gap
\mu
as it does not change the UV structure and it circumvents the IR singularity in the chiral limit.
The renormalized condensates are given by

S(n)=S_{\mathrm{bare}}(n)-S_{\mathrm{div}},\quad P(n)=P_{\mathrm{bare}}(n).

(62)

When we subtract the UV divergence
S_{\mathrm{div}}
, the
\mathcal{O}(a)
correction (
47
) for one-dimensional staggered fermion proposed in Ref.
Dempsey:2022nys
is taken into account.
As continuum fermion mass and lattice fermion mass are different, we have a nonzero subtraction even for
m_{\mathrm{lat}}=0
.
As
a\to 0
,
S_{\mathrm{div}}
behaves as

S_{\mathrm{div}}=\left(\frac{m_{\mathrm{lat}}}{\pi}+\frac{q^{2}g^{2}a}{8\pi}\right)\left(\ln\frac{\mu a}{4}+\mathcal{O}((\mu a)^{2}\ln(\mu a))\right).

(63)

We note that there is no UV divergence if we set
m_{\mathrm{lat}}=0
and this is consistent with the super-renormalizability. However, the convergence in the limit
a\to 0
behaves as
\mathcal{O}(a\ln a)
and this is slower than polynomials.
5
5

5

The presence of
\mathcal{O}(a\ln a)
in the continuum limit itself has been recognized, for example, in Ref.
Banuls:2016lkq
.
However, to our best knowledge, its physical origin has not been known so its coefficient is treated as one of fitting parameters. We here show that it comes from the
\mathcal{O}(a)
shift for the lattice mass parameter, and its coefficient is fixed by the requirement of the discrete axial anomaly relation.

Subtraction of
S_{\mathrm{div}}
is practically useful to achieve the continuum limit of the scalar condensate
6
6

6

Another option is to set
m_{\mathrm{lat}}=-\frac{q^{2}g^{2}a}{8}
to achieve the chiral limit,
m=0
, and then we need no subtractions as
S_{\mathrm{div}}=0
. This is the procedure suggested by Ref.
Dempsey:2022nys
and it is indeed more convenient than setting
m_{\mathrm{lat}}=0
.
In this paper, however, we do not take this option and we simply set
m_{\mathrm{lat}}=0
to study the chiral limit, since this is the numerical setup in almost all previous literature and it would be easier for readers to compare our results with them.

because we can take the polynomial ansatz for the continuum extrapolation after the subtraction of
S_{\mathrm{div}}
.

3.3
Simulation method

In our previous papers
Honda:2021aum
;
Honda:2021ovk
, we employed the adiabatic state preparation formulated for digital quantum simulation to study the ground state of the same model.
We carried out the numerical simulations up to the lattice size
N=25
with the IBM qiskit simulator.
In the present paper, to investigate the model more quantitatively,
we scale up the simulation up to the lattice size
N=801
by applying the density-matrix renormalization group (DMRG) with the ITensor Library
itensor
.

Here, we first convert the fermionic degrees of freedom into the spin degrees of freedom via the Jordan-Wigner transformation
Jordan1928
,

\displaystyle\chi_{n}=\frac{X_{n}-\mathrm{i}\>\!Y_{n}}{2}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}-\mathrm{i}Z_{i}\right),~~\chi_{n}^{\dagger}=\frac{X_{n}+\mathrm{i}\>\!Y_{n}}{2}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathrm{i}Z_{i}\right),

(64)

where (
X_{n},Y_{n},Z_{n}
) stands for the Pauli matrices
(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})
located at site
n
.
Accordingly, the Hamiltonian takes the form,

\displaystyle H=

\displaystyle\,J\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\left[q\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{Z_{i}+(-1)^{i}}{2}+\frac{\vartheta_{n}}{2\pi}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{w}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\big[X_{n}X_{n+1}+Y_{n}Y_{n+1}\big]-\frac{m_{\rm lat}}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(-1)^{n}Z_{n},

(65)

and it acts on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\simeq\otimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
We represent the wave function
|\Psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
in the form of the matrix-product state (MPS):

\displaystyle\lvert\Psi\rangle=\sum_{i_{1},\dots i_{N}=1}^{d}\mbox{Tr}\left(A_{1}^{(i_{1})}A_{2}^{(i_{2})}\cdots A_{N}^{(i_{N})}\right)\lvert i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{N-1}i_{N}\rangle.

(66)

where
d
denotes the dimension of local Hilbert space corresponding to a local spin degrees of freedom i.e.
d=2
here, and
A_{n}^{(i_{n})}
is a
D\times D
complex matrix.

D
is called the bond dimension, and MPS can represent any elements of the Hilbert space when
D\geq d^{N/2}
.
When applying the DMRG, we assume that the ground-state wave function can be approximated by the MPS with a fixed bond dimension, and the ground state is searched using variational methods within the ansatz.
The number of variational parameters is roughly given by
NdD^{2}
, and thus the numerical cost only grows linearly in terms of the system size
N
when
D
is treated as a constant.
Once the ground state is obtained in the form of MPS, the expectation values of local observables can be also efficiently calculated.

This prescription is thought to give a good approximation for searching the ground state of
(1+1)
d quantum many-body systems with local and gapped Hamiltonian.
The bond dimension gives the upper bound for the entanglement entropy as
S_{\mathrm{EE}}\leq\ln D
, and thus DMRG is useful if the entanglement entropy stays constant in the infinite-volume limit.
As
S_{\mathrm{EE}}
generically obeys the area law for gapped systems, the above criterion is satisfied for such systems and the DMRG becomes useful for numerical computations.
The Schwinger model in the chiral limit
m\to 0
is a gapped system, and thus we can expect DMRG is applicable to study the ’t Hooft anomaly of the charge-
q
Schwinger model.
7
7

7

Strictly speaking, the area law of the entanglement entropy for the
(1+1)
d quantum many-body systems is shown only for systems with finite-dimensional local Hilbert space for the gapped Hamiltonian with finite-range interactions
Eisert:2008ur
.
We should note that the Schwinger model does not belong to this class: Before solving the Gauss law, the local Hilbert space for the gauge field is infinite-dimensional, and after solving the Gauss law, the range of interaction becomes infinite.
Still, electric fields of the ground state for the Schwinger model cannot be too large practically; thus, it is reasonable to believe in the validity of the area law.
Let us give another remark about the bond dimension. The correlation length in the lattice unit is given by
\xi=\frac{1}{\mu a}
, and we can expect that the entanglement entropy behaves as
S_{\mathrm{EE}}=\mathcal{O}(\ln\xi)
. As we approach the continuum limit, the bond dimension should be also increased.

4.1
Boundary effects and periodicity of theta angle

Since we are working in the finite size lattice with the open boundary condition,

In particular, not only values of observables deviate from that of the infinite volume limit but also periodicity of theta is broken due to the presence of the boundaries.
Here we study the boundary effects
Honda:2021ovk
.
8
8

8

See also a very recent study

We will see that it strongly depends on

5
Summary and discussion

In this paper, we numerically studied the charge-
q
Schwinger model by DMRG with a special emphasis on the mixed ’t Hooft anomaly between the
1
-form symmetry and discrete chiral symmetry in the chiral limit.
Upon applying DMRG, we mapped the Schwinger model to the spin chain with the non-local interaction via the Jordan-Wigner transformation,
and we took the open boundary condition instead of the periodic one to make the Hilbert space finite-dimensional.
When computing the local energy density or chiral condensate,
we found that using the local operators significantly reduces the boundary effect compared with the computation of corresponding extensive quantities divided by the volume.
In particular the local observables exhibited the right periodicity of
\theta

with a good accuracy in our simulation.
This point should be useful also in simulating more realistic theories such as QCD in future when we take open boundary conditions.
We confirmed that the Wilson loops generate discrete chiral transformations by carefully analyzing the continuum limit.
When renormalizing the chiral condensate, we find it helpful to relate the lattice fermion mass and the continuum one with the
\mathcal{O}(a)
correction,
m_{\mathrm{lat}}=m-\frac{q^{2}g^{2}a}{8}
, suggested by Ref.
Dempsey:2022nys
.
Lastly, we also studied how the above property of the chiral condensate operator is changed when we turn on the fermion mass, which is outside of the analytically calculable regime.

For the charge-
q
Schwinger model, the deconfinement of Wilson loops is the consequence of the discrete chiral symmetry.
A similar phenomenon is known to occur also for
(1+1)
d adjoint QCD in the chiral limit
Gross:1995bp
;
Dalley:1992yy
;
Bhanot:1993xp
, but it cannot be understood solely from the ordinary chiral symmetry
Cherman:2019hbq
and requires the presence of noninvertible topological lines
Komargodski:2020mxz
(For noninvertible symmetries in
(1+1)
d QFTs, see Refs.
Bhardwaj:2017xup
;
Chang:2018iay
;
Thorngren:2019iar
).
It would be an interesting future study to develop the lattice Hamiltonian formulation for those models and to study them using DMRG.

Appendix B
The discrete ’t Hooft anomaly on the lattice

In a recent paper
Dempsey:2022nys
, it has been found that a part of the discrete ’t Hooft anomaly can be preserved under the lattice regularized Hamiltonian formulation when we take a suitable choice of the lattice mass parameter.
As in the case of the Lieb-Schultz-Mattis theorem
Lieb:1961fr
;
Affleck:1986pq
;
PhysRevLett.84.1535
;
Hastings:2003zx
, we can prove rigorously that the ground states should be doubly degenerate when we take the periodic lattice with the even number of sites for the case of even charge
q
, and the one-unit lattice translation plays the essential role there.
Here, we give a quick review about the lattice realization of the discrete ’t Hooft anomaly for the charge-
q
Schwinger model.

B.1
Discrete
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
chiral symmetry

Let us remind the case of the free fermion.
We consider the periodic lattice with the even number of sites
N\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, then the one-flavor naive fermion,

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}w\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(\chi^{\dagger}_{n}\chi_{n+1}-\chi^{\dagger}_{n+1}\chi_{n}),

(73)

has the dispersion,
\varepsilon(k)=2w\sin\frac{2\pi k}{N}
, and the zero modes locate at the lattice momenta
\frac{2\pi k}{N}=0,\pi
.
Moreover, they have the opposite chirality and can be regarded as the
(1+1)
d Dirac fermion
\psi(x)
as we have identified in (
42
).
This clarifies that the one-unit lattice translation,

\displaystyle\chi_{n}\mapsto\chi_{n+1},\quad\chi^{\dagger}_{n}\mapsto\chi^{\dagger}_{n+1},

(74)

acts as the discrete chiral transformation,

\displaystyle\psi(x)\to\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2}(-1+\overline{\gamma})}\psi=\overline{\gamma}\psi(x).

(75)

In the continuum formulation, the massless Dirac fermion has the
U(1)_{L}\times U(1)_{R}
chiral symmetry, and the lattice regularization explicitly breaks it down to
U(1)_{V}\times(\mathbb{Z}_{2})_{L}
.

The discrete chiral symmetry
(\mathbb{Z}_{2})_{L}
has a mixed ’t Hooft anomaly with
U(1)_{V}
classified by
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and it also has the ’t Hooft anomaly
\Omega_{3}^{\mathrm{spin}}(B\mathbb{Z}_{2})\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{8}
.
Although the on-site chiral symmetry is prohibited due to the presence of ’t Hooft anomaly, a part of it can be realized as the local but non-ultralocal symmetry that is related to the lattice symmetry.

To obtain the Schwinger model, we gauge the
U(1)_{V}
symmetry by introducing the dynamical gauge field.
Due to the ABJ anomaly, the continuous axial symmetry is explicitly broken even in the continuum theory.
As we have seen in Sec.
2
, however, the charge-
q
Schwinger model still enjoys the discrete chiral symmetry, given by
(\mathbb{Z}_{q})_{L}
.
When
q
is even,

\displaystyle(\mathbb{Z}_{2})_{L}\subset(\mathbb{Z}_{q})_{L},

(76)

and thus it is a reasonable question to ask if the lattice translation generates the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
subgroup of the discrete chiral symmetry even with the charge-
q
gauge interaction.
Ref.
Dempsey:2022nys
gives the positive answer to this problem.

Although the Hamiltonian (
45
) with
m_{\mathrm{lat}}=0
may look to have the one-unit translation invariance, we should note that the symmetry operation should be also consistent with the canonical commutation relations (
44
) and with the Gauss law constraint (
48
):

L_{n}-L_{n-1}=q\Biggl[\chi_{n}^{\dagger}\chi_{n}-\frac{1-(-1)^{n}}{2}\Biggr].

In the case of the Schwinger model, the naive one-unit lattice translation is inconsistent with the Gauss law due to the presence of the staggering constant,
\frac{1-(-1)^{n}}{2}
, on the right-hand-side of (
48
).
To keep the Gauss law (
48
) intact, we define the one-unit lattice translation of
L_{n}
as

\displaystyle L_{n}\mapsto L_{n+1}+q\frac{1-(-1)^{n+1}}{2}.

(77)

Then, the left-hand-side of (
48
) transforms as
L_{n}-L_{n-1}\mapsto L_{n+1}-L_{n}+q(-1)^{n}
, while the right-hand-side becomes
q[\chi^{\dagger}_{n}\chi_{n}-\frac{1-(-1)^{n}}{2}]\mapsto q[\chi^{\dagger}_{n+1}\chi_{n+1}-\frac{1-(-1)^{n+1}}{2}]+q(-1)^{n}
.
We note that the additive constant
q\frac{1-(-1)^{n+1}}{2}
is an integer, so it does not change the spectrum of
L_{n}
and thus this is a well-defined operation.
We can also readily confirm that it does not change the canonical commutation relation.

Thus, the remaining task is to establish the invariance of the Hamiltonian. The gauge kientic term transforms as

\displaystyle J\sum_{n}\left(L_{n}+\frac{\theta_{0}}{2\pi}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\mapsto J\sum_{n}\left(L_{n+1}+q\frac{1-(-1)^{n+1}}{2}+\frac{\theta_{0}}{2\pi}\right)

\displaystyle=J\sum_{n}\left(L_{n}+\frac{\theta_{0}+q\pi}{2\pi}-\frac{(-1)^{n}q}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=J\sum_{n}\left(L_{n}+\frac{\theta_{0}+q\pi}{2\pi}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{qJ}{2}\sum_{n}(-1)^{n}\left\{(L_{n}-L_{n-1})+q\frac{1-(-1)^{n}}{2}\right\}.

(78)

Using the Gauss law (
48
), the last term takes the same form
as the mass term on the physical Hilbert space.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\qquad J\sum_{n}\left(L_{n}+\frac{\theta_{0}}{2\pi}\right)^{2}+m_{\mathrm{lat}}\sum_{n}(-1)^{n}\chi^{\dagger}_{n}\chi_{n}

\displaystyle\mapsto J\sum_{n}\left(L_{n}+\frac{\theta_{0}+q\pi}{2\pi}\right)^{2}-\left(m_{\mathrm{lat}}+\frac{q^{2}J}{2}\right)\sum_{n}(-1)^{n}\chi^{\dagger}_{n}\chi_{n}.

(79)

In particular, when we choose the lattice mass parameter as
Dempsey:2022nys

\displaystyle m_{\mathrm{lat}}=-\frac{q^{2}J}{4}=-\frac{q^{2}g^{2}a}{8},

(80)

the one-unit lattice translation relates the Hamiltonian at
\theta_{0}
and
\theta_{0}+q\pi
:

\displaystyle H_{\theta_{0}}\mapsto H_{\theta_{0}+q\pi}=\mathcal{T}H_{\theta_{0}}\mathcal{T}^{-1},

(81)

where
\mathcal{T}
is the one-unit lattice translation defined above.
When
q
is even, we can use the unitary operator,
W^{q/2}(S^{1})=(\prod_{n}U_{n})^{q/2}
, to obtain the original Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle H_{\theta_{0}}

\displaystyle=W^{q/2}(S^{1})H_{\theta_{0}+q\pi}(W^{q/2}(S^{1}))^{-1}

\displaystyle=(W^{q/2}(S^{1})\mathcal{T})\,H_{\theta_{0}}\,(W^{q/2}(S^{1})\mathcal{T})^{-1},

(82)

and thus the one-unit lattice translation
\mathcal{T}
becomes the good symmetry operation by associating it with the above unitary transformation.

B.2
Exact
2
-fold degeneracy on the periodic even lattice with even
q

For the even-charge lattice Schwinger model on the periodic even lattice, we have the
(\mathbb{Z}_{2})_{L}
chiral symmetry with the suitable choice of the mass parameter,
m=-\frac{q^{2}g^{2}a}{8}
.
In the continuum theory, there is the mixed anomaly between
\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{[1]}
and
(\mathbb{Z}_{q})_{L}
, and it still exists even if we break the chiral symmetry to
(\mathbb{Z}_{2})_{L}\subset(\mathbb{Z}_{q})_{L}
when
q
is even.
As a result of the anomaly matching, the chiral symmetry should be spontaneously broken.

Let us prove the lattice counterpart of this statement to conclude the double degeneracy of the ground states.
For this purpose, we note that the
\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{[1]}
symmetry is generated by

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}}{q}L_{n}}.

(83)

Due to the Gauss law, this operator does not depend on the spatial sites
n
, and we can easily check that it commutes with the Hamiltonian.
Moreover,
(\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}}{q}L_{n}})^{q}=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}L_{n}}
gives the large gauge transformation, and thus it should be the identity,
1
, on the physical Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
As a result, the physical Hilbert space decomposes into the
q
distinct sectors,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{q}\mathcal{H}_{k},

(84)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{k}=\{\psi\in\mathcal{H}\,|\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}}{q}L_{n}}\psi=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}k}{q}}\psi\}.

(85)

As the discrete chiral symmetry is generated by
W^{q/2}(S^{1})\mathcal{T}
, let us compute its commutation relation with the
1
-form symmetry generator:

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}}{q}L_{n}}(W^{q/2}(S^{1})\mathcal{T})

\displaystyle=-(W^{q/2}(S^{1})\mathcal{T})\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}}{q}L_{n-1}}

\displaystyle=-(W^{q/2}(S^{1})\mathcal{T})\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}}{q}L_{n}}.

(86)

Therefore, the Hamiltonian has the same energy spectrum on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
and
\mathcal{H}_{k+q/2}
.
This shows that the whole energy spectrum must be two-fold degenerate, and, in particular, so is the ground state.
Assuming the presence of the mass gap, we can conclude the spontaneous breaking of
(\mathbb{Z}_{2})_{L}
chiral symmetry in the thermodynamic limit.
