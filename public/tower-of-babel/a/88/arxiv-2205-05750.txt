Combining nonperturbative transverse momentum dependence with TMD evolution Preprint: JLAB-THY-22-3615
J. O. Gonzalez-Hernandez, T. C. Rogers, N. Sato

Abstract
Central to understanding the nonpertubative, intrinsic partonic nature of hadron structure are the concepts of transverse momentum dependent (TMD) parton distribution and fragmentation functions. A TMD factorization approach to the phenomenology of semi-inclusive processes that includes evolution, higher orders, and matching to larger transverse momentum, is ultimately necessary for reliably connecting with phenomenologically extracted nonperturbative structures, especially when widely different scales are involved. In this paper, we will address some of the difficulties that arise when phenomenological techniques that were originally designed for very high energy applications are extended to studies of hadron structures, and we will solidify the connection between standard high energy TMD implementations and the more intuitive, parton model based approaches to phenomenology that emphasize nonperturbative hadron structure. In the process, we will elaborate on differences between forward and backward TMD evolution, which in the context of this paper we call “bottom-up” and “top-down” approaches, and we will explain the advantages of a bottom-up strategy. We will also emphasize and clarify the role of the integral relations that connect TMD and collinear correlation functions. We will show explicitly how they constrain the nonperturbative “ g g -functions” of standard Collins-Soper-Sterman (CSS) implementations of TMD factorization. This paper is especially targeted toward phenomenologists and model builders who are interested in merging specific nonperturbative models and calculations (including lattice QCD) with TMD factorization at large Q Q . Our main result is a recipe for incorporating nonperturbative models into TMD factorization, and for constraining their parameters in a way that matches to perturbative QCD and evolution.

There are, nevertheless, some remaining open issues related to the interpretation of intrinsic nonperturbative transverse momentum dependence that is extracted pheomenologically and its role in cross section calculations, and these can have practical consequences. For now, we will prepare the reader by noting how some of the remaining complications originate in a clash between the natural phenomenological strategies that are often implicit in Type I and type II situations.

Starting from a typical Type II perspective, the main issue is that large transverse momentum perturbation theory calculations (with very large

In a more general sense, within typical Type II oriented approaches, transverse momentum dependence is seen as primarily perturbative and requiring corrections from non-perturbative behavior, while in typical Type I oriented approaches it is seen as a primarily non-perturbative phenomenon that needs to be improved with perturbative corrections from large transverse momentum.
In the former, divergences are seen as a low
q_{\text{T}}

q_{\text{T}}

There is no formal difference between the bottom-up and top-down viewpoints just discussed, and they are generally described by identical sets of factorization and evolution equations. The logarithms like Eq. (
2

1

However, they suggest different phenomenological strategies and this can have practical consequences, as we will see.
The focus of this paper will be to give a full account of a bottom-up approach, which to our knowledge has never been made entirely explicit. We will also formally link it back to top-down approaches.

As we will see, adopting a bottom-up strategy brings with it some significant practical advantages in situations where extracting nonperturbative transverse momentum dependence is the primary goal. It also raises several important aspects of nonperturbative parametrizations that are often hidden to the foreground. All of this will be made clearer in the main body of the text.

One advantage is that, at the input scale
Q_{0}

space.

It will also turn out to be easier to match these models to perturbative transverse momentum calculations at large

in transverse momentum space than in coordinate space. Generally, fixed order perturbation theory calculations in collinear factorization for large transverse momentum (

When we categorize behavior as perturbative or nonperturbative in TMD factorization according to momentum space regions, we will find that we are better able to ensure a consistent transition to the large-

Another issue that will become clearer from a bottom-up perspective is the question of how to describe the transition between perturbative and nonperturbative transverse
coordinate
space dependence. Working in coordinate space has significant advantages for both setting up TMD factorization theoretically and implementing it. For example, most versions of the CSS formalism
Collins and
Soper 1982a
;
Collins and Soper 1981
;
Collins et al. 1982
;
Collins et al. 1985
are in coordinate space. In this paper, we will continue to use coordinate space for implementing evolution.
But in most top-down approaches to phenomenology, which are almost always formulated in coordinate (
b_{\text{T}}
) space,
it is only when
b_{\text{T}}
is very large, usually above some specified transition point
b_{\text{max}}
, that there is an explicit allowance for a nonperturbative transverse coordinate dependence.
The strategy is often to push reliance on perturbation theory, with its well-understood collinear pdfs, normally considered valid only at small
b_{\text{T}}
, as far into the large
b_{\text{T}}
region as possible and thereby maximize the predictive power supplied by collinear factorization.
However, unless the functions that parametrize the nonperturbative
b_{\text{T}}>b_{\text{max}}
region are constructed very carefully, there can be undesired side effects, particularly if the nonperturbative
b_{\text{T}}
-dependent contribution becomes non-negligible.
In the original setup of TMD factorization,
b_{\text{max}}
marks a totally arbitrary line of demarcation between what are labeled “large/nonperturbative” and “small/perturbative” transverse coordinate space regions. Formally,
b_{\text{max}}
-dependence vanishes exactly from any physical observables. (See, for example, our discussion in section 13.10.4 of
Collins 2011

However, in practical implementations there is an unsettling tendency for
b_{\text{max}}
-dependence to propagate into collinear perturbative calculations

and affect final results. Thus, purely perturbative calculations can
appear to get contaminated by nonperturbative modeling.
A significant
b_{\text{max}}
-dependence in a calculation is a symptom that something is likely non-optimal about the underlying approximations being used.
Part of the difficulty is that a valid description over the whole range of
b_{\text{T}}
needs to interpolate between the perturbative and nonperturbative regions, and varying
b_{\text{max}}
amounts to simply reshuffling contributions between factors.
In many practical applications, however, there is a rather abrupt switch between a purely collinear perturbation-theory calculation and one that involves a nonperturbative ansatz.
We will show how switching from a top-down to a bottom-up perspective clarifies the steps that are needed to minimize
b_{\text{max}}
-dependence in the parametrizations used in phenomenological applications.
Indeed, we will find that we are able to avoid the usual
b_{*}
-prescription entirely, along with a

Q_{0}
to demarcate any transition between large and small transverse momentum.

Part of the work that is necessary in setting up reasonable phenomenological TMD parametrizations involves ensuring that they retain at least an approximate hadron structure interpretation.
For example, the probability density interpretation of an unpolarized quark TMD pdf
f_{i/p}(x,k_{\text{T}})
gives

\int\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu^{2}\bm{k}_{\text{T}}}\nolimits f_{i/p}(x,\bm{k}_{\text{T}})\approx f_{i/p}(x)\,

(3)

where
i
is the flavor of a quark in hadron
p
and
f_{i/p}(x)
is the corresponding collinear pdf. We use the “
\approx
” symbol here because the actual transverse momentum integral in Eq. (
3
) is ultraviolet (UV) divergent in QCD and needs to be regulated. After accounting for the UV divergence (by renormalizing the operator definition of the pdf, for example, and introducing regulators), the equality only holds up to order
\alpha_{s}
and power-suppressed corrections.

The role that identities like Eq. (
3
) play in constraining nonperturbative transverse momentum dependence is often unclear in implementations of the CSS and related formalisms. The main reason is that the relation seems to be satisfied automatically in the way that final CSS cross section formulas normally are usually presented. To see how this happens, first consider the TMD pdf expressed in terms of its transverse coordinate space version,
\tilde{f}_{i/p}(x,\bm{b}_{\text{T}})

f_{i/p}(x,\bm{k}_{\text{T}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu^{2}\bm{b}_{\text{T}}}\nolimits{}e^{i\bm{k}_{\text{T}}\bm{b}_{\text{T}}}\tilde{f}_{i/p}(x,\bm{b}_{\text{T}})\,.

(4)

The UV divergence in Eq. (
3
) manifests itself in Eq. (
4
) as a divergence in
\tilde{f}_{i/p}(x,\bm{b}_{\text{T}})
as
b_{\text{T}}\to 0
, so
it may be regulated by freezing the
\bm{b}_{\text{T}}
argument in
\tilde{f}_{i/p}(x,\bm{b}_{\text{T}})
below some very small

3

4

\int\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu^{2}\bm{k}_{\text{T}}}\nolimits f_{i/p}(x,\bm{k}_{\text{T}})=\tilde{f}_{i/p}(x,{\bm{b}}_{\text{min}})\,.

(5)

In the next step, one observes that an operator product expansion (OPE) applies to

\tilde{f}_{i/p}(x,{\bm{b}}_{\text{min}})
in the limit of small
{\bm{b}}_{\text{min}}
and relates it to the collinear pdf

\tilde{f}_{i/p}(x,{\bm{b}}_{\text{min}})=\sum_{j}\mathcal{C}_{i/j}\otimes f_{j/p}=f_{i}(x)+O\left(\alpha_{s}\right)+\text{p.s.}\,.

(6)

The
C_{i/j}
is a perturbative hard coefficient,
\mathcal{C}_{i/j}\otimes f_{j/p}
is the usual convolution integral of collinear factorization, the sum is over parton flavors, and “

” refers to power suppressed corrections. The perturbation theory expansion on the right hand side is optimized by choosing renormalization scales appropriately in relation to
{\bm{b}}_{\text{min}}
.
So, dropping small corrections from the right side of Eq. (
5
)/Eq. (

3

At first sight, the steps leading from Eq. (
5
) to Eq. (
6
) can appear to constitute a
derivation
of Eq. (
3
).
The normal presentations (for example, Eq. (22) of
Collins and Rogers 2015

6
) at small
\bm{b}_{\text{T}}
automatically in the final cross section expressions.
From Eq. (
6
) above, it then appears that any nonperturbative modeling of large
\bm{b}_{\text{T}}
behavior can only impact the right hand side of Eq. (
3
) through power suppressed terms that are mostly irrelevant.

However, Eq. (
6
) is itself a restatement of Eq. (
3
), not a derivation of it. That is, a derivation of Eq. (
6
) requires that one identify and factorize integrals over the small, nonperturbative region of parton transverse momentum (i.e., the core contribution to the TMD pdf) and identify them with contributions to a collinear pdf, just as in Eq. (
3
). Therefore, a derivation of Eq. (
3
) that refers back to Eq. (
6

6

3
) already holds nonperturbatively.

There are practical consequences of this for setting up nonperturbative parametrizations of TMD correlations functions. One discards an important and useful constraint from the hadron structure interpretation of the nonperturbative
f_{i/p}(x,\bm{k}_{\text{T}})
if one assumes that Eq. (
3
) holds automatically on the basis of the OPE alone.
By doing so, one is effectively assuming that, from the outset, all important regions of the integrand on the left side of Eq. (
3
) are at such large
k_{\text{T}}
that they are expressible entirely in terms of collinear pdfs, and that the intrinsic transverse momentum is completely irrelevant insofar as the integral is concerned. But there are many practical situations in TMD physics where that is obviously not the case. For example, at moderate scales where
f_{i/p}(x,\bm{k}_{\text{T}})
can be successfully modeled phenomenologically by a Gaussian distribution, with no collinear perturbative tail present at all, the entire range of the integration is described by a nonperturbative model.
Thus, it is a requirement that Eq. (
3
) be imposed explicitly as a constraint from the outset if the goal is to maintain a connection between the nonperturbative TMD parametrizations of hadron structure interpretations and the corresponding collinear functions.

Many past TMD parton model approaches to Type I phenomenology do of course impose a version of Eq. (
3
) on TMD pdfs, but they typically do not include the transition to perturbative transverse momentum at large transverse momentum (or small
\bm{b}_{\text{T}}
as in Eq. (
6
)). Gaussian fits in momentum space are the most prominent examples.
The reverse limitation is true of many CSS and similar approaches; these approaches account appropriately for large transverse momentum behavior, but they rarely (if ever) impose conditions analogous to Eq. (
3
) directly on the models of nonpeturbative transverse momentum dependence.
We will show in this paper how adopting a bottom-up perspective makes it clearer how to do both simultaneously, and we will give special attention to integral relations like Eq. (
3
) and expand on the discussions above.

It is worth noting that similar issues also appear in other formalisms that deal with parton transverse momentum, such as the small-

The issues that are clarified by a bottom-up perspective are interrelated, and the two just discussed will turn out to be examples of that.
On page 2, we mentioned how failing to interpolate explicitly between nonperturbative and perturbative transverse momentum dependence can lead to undesired
b_{\text{max}}
-dependence in perturbative calculations. But, as we will see, setting up that interpolation involves the use of relations like Eq. (
3
). Conversely, imposing a version of Eq. (
3
) on small transverse momentum descriptions, in a way that accounts for a cutoff around
k_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}
, naturally requires a careful treatment of the large
k_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}
region, and thus the interpolation to perturbatively large
k_{\text{T}}
becomes relevant.

Very broadly speaking, switching to a bottom-up approach helps with the inverse problem that arises from transforming between transverse momentum and coordinate space (see also
Ebert et al. 2022a
).
Performing exact Fourier transformations to momentum space requires exact knowledge about the full range of the transverse coordinate space correlation functions. But actual measurements only probe specific limited ranges of
b_{\text{T}}
. Very large
Q
measurements are only sensitive to small transverse sizes and so they poorly constrain the nonpertrubative transverse coordinate dependence at very large
b_{\text{T}}
. As a consequence, fits evolved from large
Q
down to moderate
Q
can be unstable. For instance,

Sun and Yuan 2013a
;
Sun and Yuan 2013b
pointed out that the nonperturbative evolution extracted from the earlier CSS fits, which were frequently dominated by very large
Q
data, is too rapid when it is used to evolve down to the
Q
of only a few GeVs that are relevant for hadron structure studies. Conversely, measurements done at a more moderate
Q\approx Q_{0}
might be dominated by nonperturbatively large
b_{\text{T}}
(or small
k_{\text{T}}
), but they are usually insensitive to the TMD correlation functions’ very small
b_{\text{T}}\ll 1/Q_{0}
(or large
k_{\text{T}}\gg Q_{0}
) behavior. The latter, however, do not generally need to be extracted but are instead calculable in terms of collinear correlation functions.
So, a more stable phenomenological strategy is to first emphasize the role of more moderate
Q
measurements for constraining input nonperturbative transverse momentum and then rely on perturbation theory to evolve to larger
Q
.

Another aspect of TMD phenomenology that will be easier to control in a bottom-up approach is the matching to a momentum space, large transverse momentum asymptotic term. That step is important for relating the small
q_{\text{T}}\ll Q
treatment supplied by TMD factorization to the standard momentum space
q_{\text{T}}\approx Q
treatments in collinear factorization. Agreement between the two types of calculations at large
q_{\text{T}}
provides an important consistency test.
In practice, however, ensuring that the asymptotic term and the TMD factorization calculations match can be difficult at moderate
Q
. That is especially the case near the lowest scales, around
Q_{0}\approx 1-2
GeV, that are conventionally accepted as reasonable input scales.
The difficulties can be largely traced back to those that we have discussed so far in this introduction. Errors that grow as
Q
evolves downward propagate, at least to some degree, to all values of transverse momentum, including the large transverse momentum. The range of accessible
q_{\text{T}}
shrinks, so there is a less well-defined separation between different transverse momentum regions.

We have so far emphasized a dichotomy between bottom-up/top-down approaches to phenomenology to highlight steps that continue to be a challenge when merging nonperturbative TMD parton model descriptions with TMD factorization and evolution in phenomenology, and to provide readers a basic foundation from which to read the rest of this paper. We reassure readers who find the discussion to be rather vague so far that it likely will become much clearer in the main body of this paper; some of the points noted above require explicit examples in order to be made entirely clear. We will provide some such examples within the main text.

•

When we discuss phenomenology at an input scale
Q_{0}
, we will work mainly in transverse momentum space rather than in coordinate space.

•

We will nevertheless account for the perturbative behavior at
q_{\text{T}}\gg Q_{0}
in TMD correlation functions. We will do this even for
Q\approx Q_{0}
where such contributions are often phenomenologically irrelevant.

•

For the region where
Q\approx Q_{0}
,
q_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}
, all renormalization scales will be fixed at order
Q_{0}
and not
1/b_{\text{T}}
since there is no need for resummation of logarithms of the type
\ln(q_{\text{T}}/Q_{0})
or
\ln(b_{\text{T}}Q_{0})
.

•

We will nevertheless perform evolution to
Q\gg Q_{0}
in coordinate space, and switch to a
\sim 1/b_{\text{T}}
scale, but only at a later step.

•

Also for
Q\approx Q_{0}
, we will explicitly impose approximate matching to the fixed order asymptotic behavior at
q_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}
.

•

In nonperturbative parametrizations of TMD correlation functions at
Q=Q_{0}
, we will explicitly impose a version of Eq. (
3
). We will do this in momentum space with a large transverse momentum cutoff to regulate UV divergences and to match with what is typically done in phenomenological models.

•

Our approach will include an explicit interpolation between purely nonperturbative and purely perturbative descriptions of transverse momentum dependence in the TMD correlation functions.

Along the way, we will highlight the advantages of these choices by using explicit examples. At the end, we will translate the expressions into forms that are more familiar from standard TMD factorization implementations in the CSS formalism.

We will start by reminding the reader of the basic setup of TMD factorization and evolution in Sec.

Sections

and in momentum space.
The steps for using input parametrizations to evolve to higher scales are summarized in a practical phenomenological recipe in Sec.

In Sec.

VIII

II
TMD Factorization and Evolution

We begin by reviewing some of the basic setup of TMD factorization to establish context and introduce
notation for later sections. While all of what we discuss is meant to apply to any of the basic processes for which there are TMD factorization theorems, it will be instructive to work within a specific example. For this we will
use semi-inclusive annihilation (SIA) of a lepton-antilepton pair (usually electron-positron) into a pair
of nearly back-to-back hadrons with a sum over all other final state particles X,

\displaystyle e^{-}(l)+e^{+}(\bar{l})\to H_{A}(p_{A})+H_{B}(p_{B})+X\,.

(7)

A quark-antiquark pair is produced in the hard vertex, and hadrons
H_{A}
and
H_{B}
are measured in the final state.
This is among the simplest processes to work with theoretically, and it is ideal for illustrating the basics of TMD factorization.
(See, for example, the discussion in chapter 13 of Ref.
Collins 2011
.)

The process is illustrated graphically in Figure
1
: An electron (
l
) and a positron (
\bar{l}
) annihilate to create a virtual photon of momenta
q
, which creates a quark-antiquark pair. The two hadrons measured in the final state with momenta
p_{A}
and
p_{B}
are then produced when partons A and B fragment. The momentum of the virtual photon sets the hard scale of the process
Q
, with

. See also Refs.
Boer 2009
;
Boer et al. 1997
;
Moffat et al. 2019
for more details about the general kinematical setup.

In a reference frame where the hadrons are back-to-back, the transverse momentum of the photon
\bm{q}_{\text{T}}
is the relevant observed final state transverse momentum. When it is small relative to the hard scale,
q_{\text{T}}\ll Q
, it is sensitive to intrinsic transverse momenta of the hadronizing quark and antiquark respectively.

Schematic of the semi-inclusive

The usual Lorentz invariant kinematic variables related to collinear momentum fractions are

\displaystyle z_{A}=\dfrac{p_{A}\cdot p_{B}}{q\cdot p_{B}}\approx\dfrac{p_{A,h}^{+}}{q_{h}^{+}},\hskip 14.22636ptz_{B}=\dfrac{p_{A}\cdot p_{B}}{q\cdot p_{A}}\approx\dfrac{p_{B,h}^{-}}{q_{h}^{-}}\,

In TMD factorization, the unpolarized cross section differential in

\displaystyle Q^{2}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\sigma^{A,B}}\nolimits}{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999muz_{A}}\nolimits\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999muz_{B}}\nolimits\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999muq^{2}_{\text{T}}}\nolimits}

\displaystyle Q^{2}

\displaystyle{}=H(\mu_{Q};C_{2})\int\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu^{2}\bm{k}_{A\text{T}}}\nolimits{}\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu^{2}\bm{k}_{B\text{T}}}\nolimits{}D_{A}\left(z_{A},z_{A}\bm{k}_{A\text{T}};\mu_{Q},Q^{2}\right)D_{B}\left(z_{B},z_{B}\bm{k}_{B\text{T}};\mu_{Q},Q^{2}\right)\delta^{(2)}\left(\bm{q}_{\text{T}}-\bm{k}_{A\text{T}}-\bm{k}_{B\text{T}}\right)

\displaystyle{}\qquad+Y^{\text{NS}}(q_{\text{T}},Q;\mu_{Q})+O\left(m/Q\right)\,.

-term from here forward.

The second line in Eq. (

It can be convenient to write the
W

\displaystyle W(q_{\text{T}},Q)

\displaystyle{}=H(\mu_{Q};C_{2})\int\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mskip-0.59999mu\bm{b}_{\text{T}}}\nolimits}{(2\pi)^{2}}~e^{-i\bm{q}_{\text{T}}\cdot\bm{b}_{\text{T}}}~\tilde{D}_{A}(z_{A},\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q},Q^{2})~\tilde{D}_{B}(z_{B},\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q},Q^{2})\,,

(15)

where the transverse coordinate and momentum space TMD ffs are related to one another via

D\left(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu,\zeta\right)=\int\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu^{2}\bm{b}_{\text{T}}}\nolimits}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{-i\bm{k}_{\text{T}}\cdot\bm{b}_{\text{T}}}\tilde{D}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu,\zeta)\,.

(16)

The TMD ffs
D_{A}
and
D_{B}
are hadron-vacuum correlation functions defined in a very particular way in terms of quark field operators. An explanation of these definitions and their origins in factorization theorems would involve topics like Wilson lines, rapidity divergences, soft factors, and other related issues that are beyond the scope of this article. In recent years, they have been reviewed in many places, so to avoid repetition we refer the reader to
Rogers 2016
which includes an overview and a list references associated with TMD definitions derived in the style relevant for this article, and also to
Diehl 2016
which includes additional relevant references.

With regard to the TMD definitions, what is important for our purposes is only that they satisfy an exact set of evolution equations for the auxiliary variables
\mu
and
\zeta
. For a TMD ff in coordinate space, the evolution equations are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ln\tilde{D}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu,\zeta)}{\partial\ln\sqrt{\zeta}}

\displaystyle{}=\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu)\,,

(17)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu)}\nolimits}{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\ln\mu}\nolimits{}}

\displaystyle{}=-\gamma_{K}(\alpha_{s}(\mu))\,,

(18)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\ln\tilde{D}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu,\zeta)}\nolimits}{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\ln\mu}\nolimits}

\displaystyle{}=\gamma(\alpha_{s}(\mu);\zeta/\mu^{2})

\displaystyle=\gamma(\alpha_{s}(\mu);1)

\displaystyle-\gamma_{K}(\alpha_{s}(\mu))\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{\zeta}}{\mu}\right)\,.

(19)

The evolution kernels,
\gamma
,
\gamma_{K}
and
\tilde{K}
are known by many
different names in the literature. In keeping with our earlier work, we will refer to
\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu)
as the Collins-Soper (CS) kernel.

For large enough
\mu
, the anomalous dimensions
\gamma_{K}
and
\gamma
are both calculable in perturbation theory and they are independent of
b_{\text{T}}
.
\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu)
is also perturbatively calculable at small
b_{\text{T}}\sim 1/\mu
, but it becomes nonperturbative over large distances,
b_{\text{T}}\to\infty
. However, since it is independent of the identity of external hadrons, it has very strong universality properties related to the QCD vacuum.

The evolution equations are first order linear differential equations that relate the TMD ffs in Eq. (
15
) at an input scale
Q_{0}
to a different scale
Q
, so they can be easily solved exactly and analytically. The general solutions to the evolution equations for
D_{A}
and
D_{B}
, substituted into Eq. (

as

\displaystyle W(q_{\text{T}},Q)

\displaystyle{}=H(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q});C_{2})\int\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mskip-0.59999mu\bm{b}_{\text{T}}}\nolimits}{(2\pi)^{2}}~e^{-i\bm{q}_{\text{T}}\cdot\bm{b}_{\text{T}}}~\tilde{D}_{A}(z_{A},\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})~\tilde{D}_{B}(z_{B},\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle\,\times\exp\left\{\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})\ln\left(\frac{Q^{2}}{Q_{0}^{2}}\right)+\int_{\mu_{Q_{0}}}^{\mu_{Q}}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\mu^{\prime}}\nolimits}{\mu^{\prime}}\biggl[2\gamma(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime});1)-\ln\frac{Q^{2}}{{\mu^{\prime}}^{2}}\gamma_{K}(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime}))\biggr]\right\}\,.

(20)

We could express the same evolved
W
with TMD ffs in transverse momentum space, and in some cases that might help with intuition since it more closely matches the TMD parton model.
But then the Fourier transforms of simple factors become transverse momentum convolution integrals of several functions. For the purposes of implementing evolution, we will continue to work with Eq. (
20
) in coordinate space, as is normally done.

When
Q=Q_{0}
, Eq. (
20
) exactly reproduces the TMD parton model form of the factorization,

\displaystyle W(q_{\text{T}},Q_{0})

\displaystyle{}=H(\mu_{Q_{0}};C_{2})\int\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mskip-0.59999mu\bm{b}_{\text{T}}}\nolimits}{(2\pi)^{2}}~e^{-i\bm{q}_{\text{T}}\cdot\bm{b}_{\text{T}}}~\tilde{D}_{A}(z_{A},\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})~\tilde{D}_{B}(z_{B},\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})\,

\displaystyle{}=H(\mu_{Q_{0}};C_{2})\int\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu^{2}\bm{k}_{A\text{T}}}\nolimits{}\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu^{2}\bm{k}_{B\text{T}}}\nolimits{}D_{A}\left(z_{A},z_{A}\bm{k}_{A\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2}\right)D_{B}\left(z_{B},z_{B}\bm{k}_{B\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2}\right)\delta^{(2)}\left(\bm{q}_{\text{T}}-\bm{k}_{A\text{T}}-\bm{k}_{B\text{T}}\right)\,.

(21)

So far, Eq. (
20
) and Eq. (
21
) still involve no approximations at all on the
W
-term. However, approximations are ultimately necessary, of course, for getting practical results.

Rather different kinds of approximations to Eq. (
20
) can enter in a number of different ways, so the language can become confusing. We will be as precise as possible with our wording here. There are at least four different types of approximation that generally take place simultaneously, including

1.

The choices of models, assumptions, or approximations used to describe nonperturbative contributions,

2.

The neglect of power suppressed corrections to factorization, like the last term in Eq. (
14
),

3.

Truncation of high powers of
\alpha_{s}
in perturbative parts of the calculation and,

4.

In phenomenological applications involving fits, whatever assumptions and approximations are used at the level of fit extractions.

When we discuss an “
n^{\text{th}}
-order” perturbative treatment, we will mean by this that all parts that are calculable in fixed order perturbative QCD have been included through order
n
, and they are optimized using the renormalization group and Collins-Soper evolution (Eq. (
17
)). These “perturbative parts” include the right sides of Eqs. (
17
)–(
II
), which in Eq. (
20
) appear as
\gamma_{K}(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime}))
,
\gamma(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime});1)

and
\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
when
b_{\text{T}}
(or
k_{\text{T}}
) is small (large) enough that its
b_{\text{T}}
-dependence is perturbative. In Eq. (
20
) it also includes
H(\mu_{Q};C_{2})
and the
\tilde{D}_{A}
,
\tilde{D}_{B}
functions in regions of small
b_{\text{T}}
(or large
k_{\text{T}}
).
An “
(n)
” superscript on a function means that it has been replaced by its truncated, fixed order perturbation theory calculation through order
n
.
When we discuss nonperturbative parts in later sections, it should be understood to be in the context of phenomenological extractions. We will
assume
for the sake of our discussion here that all nonperturbative parametrizations have been made flexible enough that the significant errors come only from the limitations of factorization and truncated perturbation theory, and not from a poor choice of nonperturbative parametrizations or artifacts of the fitting procedure.
Thus, a function like
\tilde{D}_{A}^{(n)}
should be read as “a
\tilde{D}_{A}
parametrization including nonperturbative parts extracted from measurements and using an
n^{\text{th}}
-order perturbation theory treatment for its perturbative ingredients.”

Also, when we use the phrase “nonperturbative part,” (e.g., the
g

It only refers to contributions that we choose to exclude from those factors that we explicitly identify as perturbative.

Now consider how one might use Eq. (
20
) to do phenomenology in a Type I scenario and from a bottom-up perspective. Near the input scale,
Q\approx Q_{0}
, the evolution factor on the second line is nearly unity and we can, to a good approximation, just work with Eq. (

). If
Q_{0}
is only of order
\sim 1-2

is only sensitive to the region of nonperturbatively large

parts of
\tilde{D}_{A}
and
\tilde{D}_{B}
whereas the

contributions is strongly suppressed in cross sections. Therefore, measurements of the
Q\approx Q_{0}
region place rigid constraints on the nonperturbative transverse momentum dependence while remaining mostly insensitive to perturbative tails from large
k_{A,B\text{T}}
(or small
b_{\text{T}}
). Indeed, past phenomenology with Eq. (
21
) has confirmed that the basic TMD parton model is quite successful at describing moderate

k_{\text{T}}

parametrizations like Gaussians
Anselmino et al. 2003
;
Collins et al. 2006
;
Schweitzer et al. 2010
;

.
Work that is done in this style effectively replaces the exact Eq. (

\displaystyle W^{(n)}(q_{\text{T}},Q_{0})

\displaystyle{}=H^{(n)}(\mu_{Q_{0}};C_{2})\int\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mskip-0.59999mu\bm{b}_{\text{T}}}\nolimits}{(2\pi)^{2}}~e^{-i\bm{q}_{\text{T}}\cdot\bm{b}_{\text{T}}}~\tilde{D}_{A,\text{np}}(z_{A},\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})~\tilde{D}_{B,\text{np}}(z_{B},\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})\,,

(22)

where the “

and

(e.g., Gaussians, etc), with no matching to perturbative large-

tails.
If most of the

region of transverse momentum dependence is nonperturbative at

Notice that, if we have perfectly constrained the input TMD ffs for all

might appear at first sight to be all the input that is necessary, aside from a treatment of the evolution kernels, for evolution to any other scale.

However, this will fail to work if applied directly to traditional Type I oriented styles of phenomenological extractions that neglect large

tails. The reason is that
D_{A}\left(z_{A},z_{A}\bm{k}_{A\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2}\right)
and
D_{B}\left(z_{B},z_{B}\bm{k}_{B\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2}\right)
need to be well-described over their
entire

k_{\text{T}}
ranges, not just over the
0\lesssim k_{\text{T}}\lesssim Q_{0}
regions that moderate
Q_{0}
measurements effectively probe, in order for the evolution to larger
Q
to be accurate. Fortunately, the large-
k_{\text{T}}
behavior of TMD ffs is calculable in collinear factorization, so nothing prevents us from simply interpolating between phenomenological fits of the small
k_{\text{T}}
region and a large-
k_{\text{T}}
perturbative description of the input TMD correlation functions at
k_{\text{T}}>Q_{0}
. We will discuss how to make this adjustment to standard Type II oriented approaches in later sections.

If
Q_{0}
is, as we intend, a lower bound on what is considered a reasonable hard scale, near the boundary between large and small coupling, then there is a very rapid scale dependence when a choice of
\mu\propto Q_{0}
is varied downward.
Therefore, it is preferable to keep renormalization and rapidity scales fixed around
\mu_{Q_{0}}
and
Q_{0}
in the moderate
k_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}
or
b_{\text{T}}\approx 1/Q_{0}
regions.
(We elaborate on this slightly subtle point in Appendix
B
to avoid breaking the flow of our main discussion here.)

But in the
k_{\text{T}}\gg Q_{0}
region, large logarithms of the form
\ln(k_{\text{T}}/Q_{0})
start to degrade the accuracy of such a treatment. Extending calculations to the very large
k_{\text{T}}\gg Q_{0}
region requires another iteration of the evolution equations to switch scales.
(Actually, when we do this in later sections, it will be in coordinate space as is more common, and we will switch to the usual
\sim 1/b_{\text{T}}
scale.) We will explain the details of both the interpolation and the scale transformation in Sec.
V
.
The discussion of Eq. (
2
) in the introduction was in reference to these types of logarithmic errors.

For readers accustomed to more standard presentations of the CSS formalism, the above discussion might seem like a slightly odd starting point because normally one does not stop with Eq. (
20
). Rather, one immediately performs the step of evolving
\tilde{D}_{A}(z_{A},\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
and
\tilde{D}_{B}(z_{B},\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
to scales of order
1/b_{\text{T}}
to optimize collinear factorization calculations with the OPE in the region of
b_{\text{T}}\sim 1/Q
.
The resulting expressions (see for example Eq.(22) of
Collins and Rogers 2015
) highlight the role of the
b_{\text{T}}\sim 1/Q
calculation. Ultimately, we will perform an equivalent step, but we choose to delay it for now for reasons that we hope will become clear later. The connection between Eq. (
20
) and the usual CSS presentation will be made in Sec.
IX
.

III
Integral Relations

As we will see in later sections, consistently interpolating between nonperturbatively small
k_{\text{T}}
and perturbatively large
k_{\text{T}}
will require integral relations like Eq. (
3
). In this section we will define and discuss a collinear ff that obeys an analogous equation with a cutoff on large transverse momentum. This section also introduces additional notation that will be necessary in later sections. We remind the reader of our notation glossary, Appendix
A
.

We will use a superscript “
(n,d_{r})
” on a TMD ff to indicate that it is calculated in a large (
k_{\text{T}}\approx Q
) transverse momentum approximation.
Thus,

\displaystyle{}D\left(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}}\approx Q;\mu_{Q},Q^{2}\right)\equiv

\displaystyle{}D^{(n,d_{r})}\left(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q},Q^{2}\right)+O\left(\frac{m}{k_{\text{T}}},\alpha_{s}(k_{\text{T}})^{n+1}\right)\,.

(23)

As usual, “
n
” is the order of collinear perturbation theory. Thus,
D^{(n,d_{r})}\left(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q},Q^{2}\right)
is calculated
through
order
\alpha_{s}^{n}
, with powers of
\alpha_{s}^{n+1}
and
\sim m/k_{\text{T}}
errors neglected. However, now we have also included a “
d_{r}
” in the superscript. This is to indicate that the collinear factorization calculation uses a renormalized collinear ff
d_{r}(z;\mu_{Q})
. The subscript “
r
” on “
d_{r}
” in turn labels the UV renormalization or regularization scheme (such as, for example,
r=\overline{\rm MS}{}
renormalization).
We also define

D^{(n,d_{r})}\left(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q},Q^{2}\right)\equiv 0\;\;\forall\;\;n<1\,.

(24)

The
m/k_{\text{T}}
in the error term of Eq. (
23
)
symbolizes contributions that are power suppressed when
k_{\text{T}}\approx Q
. Throughout this paper, an “
m
” will always represent any generic mass scale that is of order a small hadronic size like
\Lambda_{\text{QCD}}
or an intrinsic transverse momentum. Also, to simplify notation, any power-suppressed contributions of the form
(m/Q)^{\beta}
or

(m/k_{\text{T}})^{\beta}
, with
\beta>0
will always simply be written as
O\left(m/Q\right)
or
O\left(m/k_{\text{T}}\right)
, regardless of the power
\beta
.

To summarize, the symbol
D^{(n,d_{r})}\left(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q},Q^{2}\right)
is the approximation to an individual TMD ff wherein it is calculated in fixed order collinear perturbation theory, optimized to the region
k_{\text{T}}\approx Q
and
Q\to\infty
, and using
d_{r}
collinear fragmentation functions.
The fixed order perturbative expression for
D^{(n,d_{r})}\left(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q},Q^{2}\right)
in collinear factorization has the form

D^{(n,d_{r})}\left(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q},Q^{2}\right)=\left[\mathcal{C}^{(n)}_{D}(zk_{\text{T}})\otimes d_{r}\right](z;\mu_{Q})\,.

(25)

The “
\otimes
” here symbolizes the usual collinear convolution integral,

(f\otimes{g})(z;\mu)\equiv\int_{z}^{1}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\xi}\nolimits}{\xi}f(z/\xi)g(\xi;\mu)\,.

(26)

In Eq. (
25
),
\mathcal{C}^{(n)}_{D}(zk_{\text{T}})
is a hard coefficient. We have written its
zk_{\text{T}}
argument explicitly as a reminder that this particular hard factor has
k_{\text{T}}
-dependence.
Approximations to
D\left(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q},Q^{2}\right)
appropriate to regions other than
k_{\text{T}}\approx Q
will be left unaddressed for now. They will be discussed in Sec.
V
.

The TMD ff is related to a collinear ff by an integral over transverse momentum,

\displaystyle{}2\pi z^{2}\int_{0}^{\mu_{Q}}\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999muk_{\text{T}}}\nolimits{}k_{\text{T}}D(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q},Q^{2})=d_{r}(z;\mu_{Q})

\displaystyle{}+\Delta^{(n,d_{r})}(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q}))+O\left(\frac{m}{Q},\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q})^{n+1}\right)\,,

(27)

with the details of the notation to be explained below.
In a literal probability density interpretation,
\mu_{Q}
would be set equal to infinity and the second two terms on the right-hand side would be zero.
In a renormalizable theory like QCD, the integral needs to be regulated, and corrections are necessary to relate the cutoff integral to collinear ffs defined in standard schemes. The
\Delta^{(n,d_{r})}(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q}))
term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
27
) is our notation for the perturbative correction through
n^{\text{th}}
-order that relates the cutoff integral to the collinear ff
d_{r}(z;\mu_{Q})
in scheme
r
. There are also, in general, power-suppressed corrections, as indicated by the error term in Eq. (
27
). The correction term
\Delta^{(n,d_{r})}(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q}))
is related to collinear ffs via another factorization theorem,

\Delta^{(n,d_{r})}(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q}))=\left[\mathcal{C}^{(n)}_{\Delta}\otimes d_{r}\right](z;\mu_{Q})\,.

(28)

and
C_{\Delta}^{(n)}
is an order-
\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q})^{n}
hard coefficient, with a
\Delta
subscript included here to distinguish it from the
k_{\text{T}}
-dependent hard coefficient in Eq. (
25
). The “
(n,d_{r})
” superscript in
\Delta^{(n,d_{r})}(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q}))
is to symbolize that Eq. (
28
) is to be calculated through order
\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q})^{n}
, and that the collinear ff is defined in the
r
renormalization and/or regularization scheme. Note that
\Delta^{(n,d_{r})}(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q}))
is also a function of
z
and
Q
, but we have dropped explicit dependence on those variables to maintain as compact a notation as possible. We also define

\Delta^{(n,d_{r})}(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q}))\equiv 0\;\;\forall\;\;n<1\,.

(29)

To make the above more explicit, let us
define
a new collinear ff that is the transverse momentum integral of a TMD ff regulated with a cutoff on all
k_{\text{T}}>\mu_{Q}
:

d_{c}(z;\mu_{Q})\equiv 2\pi z^{2}\int_{0}^{\mu_{Q}}\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999muk_{\text{T}}}\nolimits{}k_{\text{T}}D(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q},Q^{2})\,.

(30)

The
r=c
subscript on the left indicates that this is an ff defined in the ‘‘cutoff’’ scheme.
2
2

2

For the renormalization of the TMD ff in the integrand of Eq. (
30
), it is to be understood that the scheme is a standard one like
\overline{\rm MS}
.

Equation (
30
) is just the left side of Eq. (
27
). Dropping the power-suppressed and order-
\alpha_{s}^{n+1}(\mu_{Q})
terms on the right side of Eq. (
27
) gives an equation that is satisfied only approximately.
To give this a notation, we also define

d^{(n,d_{r})}_{c}(z;\mu_{Q})\equiv d_{r}(z;\mu_{Q})+\Delta^{(n,d_{r})}(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q}))\,.

(31)

Then, Eq. (
27
) is

d^{(n,d_{r})}_{c}(z;\mu_{Q})-d_{c}(z;\mu_{Q})=O\left(\frac{m}{Q},\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q})^{n+1}\right)\,,

(32)

If the scheme for dealing with UV transverse momentum divergences is the cutoff scheme itself,
r=c
, then

\Delta^{(n,d_{c})}(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q}))=0

(33)

exactly by definition.
If the scheme is modified minimal subtraction,
r=\overline{\rm MS}
, then

\displaystyle{}\Delta^{(n,d_{\text{$\overline{\rm MS}$}})}(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q}))=\frac{\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q})}{2\pi}\times

\displaystyle{}\times\int_{z}^{1}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999muz^{\prime}}\nolimits{}}{z^{{}^{\prime}}}d_{\overline{\rm MS}}(z/z^{\prime};\mu_{Q})\left[2P_{qq}(z^{\prime})\ln z^{\prime}+C_{F}(1-z^{\prime})\right]\,

\displaystyle{}\qquad+O\left(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q})^{2}\right)\,,

(34)

where we have used the standard splitting function notation

P_{qq}(z)=P_{\bar{q}\bar{q}}(z)=C_{F}\left[\frac{1+z^{2}}{\left(1-z\right)_{+}}+\frac{3}{2}\delta\left(1-z\right)\right]\,.

(35)

The quantities in Eqs. (
23
)–(
32
) are so far intended to be exact. That is,
D(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q},Q^{2})
,
d_{r}(z;\mu_{Q})
, etc are the operator definitions.

Equation (
30
) is a version of Eq. (
3
) wherein the collinear ff
d_{c}(z;\mu_{Q})
is defined such that the integral relation is satisfied exactly when the UV divergent integral is regulated with a cutoff. A collinear pdf or ff defined in this way does have some practical advantages.
It is a natural definition when regions are categorized as perturbative or nonperturbative in transverse momentum space, and it is the preferred definition in some work associated with hadron structure (see, for example, the work of Brodsky and collaborators
Brodsky et al. 2001
). It is also a natural definition for matching to fixed order large-
q_{\text{T}}
calculations, which of course are almost always performed in momentum space.
However, it also comes with significant disadvantages as well; see, for example, reference
Collins 2003
for a discussion of the different ways of handling UV divergences in definitions of collinear pdfs and the pitfalls of each. An important point is that the correction terms relating cut-off scheme and renormalized pdfs are not just perturbative, but include also the power-suppressed errors in Eq. (
27
).

IV
The Collins-Soper Kernel near the Input Scale
Q_{0}

Up to now, our discussion has mainly focused on establishing context and defining notation. In the bulleted list of Sec.
I
, we explained that we will explicitly interpolate between nonperturbatively small
k_{\text{T}}
and perturbatively large
k_{\text{T}}
in our parametrizations of TMD functions at an input scale. With the language and notation of Sec.
II
and Sec.
III
, we are ready to step through the interpolation details.

Inspection of Eq. (
20
) shows that there are actually two separate types of functions in which we will need to interpolate between nonperturbative parametrizations and perturbative regions of transverse momentum when we do calculations for phenomenology. First, there are the TMD ffs themselves,
\tilde{D}_{A}(z_{A},\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
and
\tilde{D}_{B}(z_{B},\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
, at the input scale
Q_{0}
. Second, there is the CS kernel
\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
, also at the input scale
Q_{0}
, which will be necessary for evolving away from
Q=Q_{0}
.

Between these two, it is actually the CS kernel that is the simpler to handle, so we will begin by focusing on it. In fact, the steps for interpolating a full parametrization of an input TMD ff
D(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
between large and small
k_{\text{T}}
will turn out to be very analogous to what we do for the CS kernel.

It is straightforward to calculate
K(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
in fixed order perturbative theory if
k_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}
. Using notation analogous to that of Sec.
III
, we express this as

\displaystyle{}K(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})=

\displaystyle{}\qquad K^{(n)}\left(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}}\right)+O\left(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}})^{n+1},\frac{m}{k_{\text{T}}}\right)\,.

(36)

K^{(n)}\left(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}}\right)
is our notation for the fixed order perturbative calculation through order
n
and with all nonperturbative effects ignored. It takes the form

K^{(n)}\left(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}}\right)=\frac{1}{k^{2}_{\text{T}}}\kappa\left(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}}),\frac{k_{\text{T}}}{\mu_{Q_{0}}}\right)\,,

(37)

where
\kappa
is a function of
\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}})
and logarithms involving
k_{\text{T}}/\mu_{Q_{0}}
. For example, in
\overline{\rm MS}
renormalization it is

K^{(1)}(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})=\frac{\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}})C_{F}}{\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{k^{2}_{\text{T}}}\,.

(38)

Away from
k_{\text{T}}\approx\mu_{Q_{0}}
, the accuracy of
K^{(n)}\left(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}}\right)
as an approximation to
K\left(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}}\right)
degrades. The reason
for
k_{\text{T}}<\mu_{Q_{0}}
is simply that the
k_{\text{T}}
-dependence of the zero momentum soft radiation is nonperturbative. Conversely, the reason for the approximation failing at
k_{\text{T}}\gg Q_{0}
is that the error terms in Eq. (
IV
) include logarithms of
k_{\text{T}}/\mu_{Q_{0}}
that diverge in the large
k_{\text{T}}/\mu_{Q_{0}}
limit.

The
k_{\text{T}}\gg Q_{0}
contribution is numerically unimportant in Eq. (
21
) or Eq. (
22
) because the region of transverse momenta far above
Q_{0}
is suppressed at the input scale (for reasonable fixed values of
z_{A}
and
z_{B}
). With regard to
Q\approx Q_{0}
cross sections, therefore, a suitable parametrization of
K\left(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}}\right)
only requires that we deal with the region
0<k_{\text{T}}\lesssim Q_{0}
. That is to say, near the input scale we only need to extend the perturbative
k_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}
treatment in
K^{(n)}(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
downward into the nonperturbative
k_{\text{T}}<\mu_{Q_{0}}
region in order to have a parametrization of
K(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
that is useful for all
k_{\text{T}}
accessible at the input scale.
Let us therefore start by defining an “input” parametrization for
K^{(n)}\left(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}}\right)
that equals the fixed order calculation, Eq. (
38
), when
k_{\text{T}}\approx\mu_{Q_{0}}
or larger, but interpolates to a nonperturbative parametrization for
k_{\text{T}}<\mu_{Q_{0}}
.

{K}_{\rm input}^{(n)}\left(\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}}\right)\equiv\begin{cases}K^{(n)}\left(\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}}\right)\qquad\text{if}\;\;k_{\text{T}}\gtrsim\mu_{Q_{0}}\,,\\
\\
\text{nonperturbative parametrization otherwise}\end{cases}\,.

(39)

Now the “
(n)
” superscript on the left side of this equation refers to the perturbative order of the large
k_{\text{T}}
tail in this input nonperturbative parametrization.
When we work with Eq. (
20
), we will need its coordinate space version of the CS kernel,

{\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})\equiv\int\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu^{2}\bm{k}_{\text{T}}}\nolimits{}e^{i\bm{k}_{\text{T}}\bm{b}_{\text{T}}}{K}_{\rm input}^{(n)}(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})\,.

(40)

The scale-dependence of the exact
\tilde{K}
is exactly
b_{\text{T}}
-independent by the RG equation Eq. (
18
), so we will enforce the condition that an
n^{\text{th}}
-order parametrization satisfies Eq. (
18
) to order
\alpha_{s}(\mu)^{n}
, with only
O\left(\alpha_{s}(\mu)^{n+1}\right)
errors,

\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu{\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu)}\nolimits}{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\ln\mu}\nolimits{}}=-\gamma^{(n)}_{K}(\alpha_{s}(\mu))+O\left(\alpha_{s}(\mu)^{n+1}\right)\,.

(41)

In Sec.
VII.1
we will provide an example of a specific trial functional form for
Eq. (
39
). In general, however, any phenomenologically successful parametrization that satisfies Eq. (
39
) and Eq. (
41
) is allowed.
The parametrizations in Eq. (
39
) and Eq. (
40
) are appropriate specifically when
Q\approx Q_{0}
such that only the region of
0<k_{\text{T}}\lesssim Q_{0}
is important.

However, it is a poor approximation to the true

\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
in the
k_{\text{T}}\gg Q_{0}
region, and this matters if we evolve to large enough
Q
for contributions from
k_{\text{T}}\gg Q_{0}
to become significant. In Eq. (
40
), the large errors manifest themselves as higher order terms logarithmic in

b_{\text{T}}\mu_{Q_{0}}
, which diverge in the
b_{\text{T}}\to 0
limit. There needs to be a change in renormalization scale. Thus, in coordinate space the more common choice for the RG scale is
\mu=C_{1}/b_{\text{T}}
, with
C_{1}
being an order unity proportionality constant. The truncated RG improved perturbation theory then increases in accuracy as
b_{\text{T}}\to 0
.

To obtain a
K\left(\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}}\right)
parametrization that works well for all
Q
, we need steps that combine the stability of fixed scale calculations in the
Q\approx Q_{0}
,
k_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}
region with the RG-improved calculations that optimize for the
b_{\text{T}}\to 0
limit. Specifically, we need to perform a scale transformation on the above parametrization using the RG equation at a
b_{\text{T}}
somewhat below
1/Q_{0}
. If we implement this scale transformation at small enough
b_{\text{T}}
, it will have a negligible effect on phenomenology that uses the above parametrization near
Q\approx Q_{0}
where the
b_{\text{T}}\ll 1/Q_{0}
is strongly suppressed.
Therefore, fits that use Eq. (
39
) will be largely unaffected. And, if the transformation takes place in a range of
b_{\text{T}}
at least comparable to
\lesssim 1/Q_{0}
, then its overall effect will only appear at order
n+1
or higher, so the effect of the transformation will always be one order higher in perturbation theory than the working order. So, the transformation will ensure an accurate treatment of evolution to large
Q
in any subsequent steps. We will show how this works in detail below.

The first step in implementing the scale transformation is to define a
b_{\text{T}}
-dependent mass scale, which we will call
\overline{Q}_{0}(b_{\text{T}})
, that smoothly transitions between
Q_{0}
and a
1/b_{\text{T}}
-dependence in the region just below
b_{\text{T}}\approx 1/Q_{0}
. Specifically,

\overline{Q}_{0}(b_{\text{T}})=\begin{cases}C_{1}/b_{\text{T}}&b_{\text{T}}\ll C_{1}/Q_{0}\,,\\
Q_{0}&\text{otherwise}\,,\end{cases}

(42)

where
C_{1}
is an order unity numerical constant, typically taken to be
C_{1}=2e^{-\gamma_{E}}
. When
b_{\text{T}}
is comparable to
C_{1}/Q_{0}
, the scales
Q_{0}
and
C_{1}/b_{\text{T}}
are numerically similar, so any sensitivity to the difference between the two scale
is a higher order effect that can be reduced by
including higher orders in perturbation theory. Therefore, the exact form of
\overline{Q}_{0}(b_{\text{T}})
is arbitrary so long as it provides a reasonably smooth interpolation between the
Q_{0}
and
C_{1}/b_{\text{T}}
behavior at large and small
b_{\text{T}}
.
Some example suggestions for
\overline{Q}_{0}(b_{\text{T}})
, which we will call the transformation function, are shown in Appendix
C
.

Next, we need to combine this with the RG equation Eq. (
18
), whose exact solution is

\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu)=\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{i})-\int_{\mu_{i}}^{\mu}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\mu^{\prime}}\nolimits}{\mu^{\prime}}\gamma_{K}(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime}))\,.

(43)

Here,
\mu_{i}
is an arbitrary initial scale.
To make it useful in applications of Eq. (
20
), let us evolve from an initial scale
\mu_{i}=\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}
(where
\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}=C_{2}\overline{Q}_{0}
) so that the right side contains
\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}})
:

\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu)=\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}})-\int_{\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}}^{\mu}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\mu^{\prime}}\nolimits}{\mu^{\prime}}\gamma_{K}(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime}))\,.

(44)

For any
\mu\approx Q_{0}
, the second term in Eq. (
44
) is calculable in perturbation theory with
the
n^{\text{th}}
-order anomalous dimension,
\gamma_{K}(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime}))\to\gamma_{K}^{(n)}(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime}))
, and it vanishes for
b_{\text{T}}\approx C_{1}/Q_{0}
or larger.

The original parametrization in Eq. (
40
) was designed to provide an accurate perturbative description of
\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
in the region of
b_{\text{T}}\approx 1/Q_{0}
and larger.
Now if we replace the first term on the right side of Eq. (
44
) with
{\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}})
, it continues to describe the
b_{\text{T}}\gtrsim 1/Q_{0}
region, by our construction of
\overline{Q}_{0}(b_{\text{T}})
. However, now the RG-improved perturbative contribution to
\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}})
also remains accurate into the
b_{\text{T}}\ll 1/Q_{0}
region.

Therefore, we obtain an optimal parametrization
by replacing the exact
\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}})
on the right side of Eq. (
44
) by the approximate
{\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}})
and the exact
\gamma_{K}(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime}))
by
\gamma_{K}^{(n)}(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime}))
. We define this this as

\displaystyle{}\tilde{\underline{K}}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu)

\displaystyle{}\equiv{\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}})-\int_{\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}}^{\mu}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\mu^{\prime}}\nolimits}{\mu^{\prime}}\gamma_{K}^{(n)}(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime}))\,.

(45)

The underline on
\tilde{\underline{K}}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu)
is our notation for the final parametrization to be used with evolution.
The above applies to the cases where
\mu\approx Q_{0}
, so as a final step we set
\mu=\mu_{Q_{0}}
and write the underlined parametrization as

\displaystyle{}\tilde{\underline{K}}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})

\displaystyle{}\equiv{\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}})-\int_{\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}}^{\mu_{Q_{0}}}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\mu^{\prime}}\nolimits}{\mu^{\prime}}\gamma_{K}^{(n)}(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime}))\,.

(46)

This is the form of the parametrization for the CS kernel that we will ultimately use in Eq. (
20
).
The errors in
\tilde{\underline{K}}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
, as an approximation to
\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
, are suppressed by at least
\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}})^{n+1}
point-by-point for all
b_{\text{T}}
.

A final constraint on parametrizations of
{K}_{\rm input}^{(n)}\left(\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}}\right)
is
obtained by recalling that soft gluon effects cancel in collinear factorization when we integrate over all transverse momentum. Thus, after an integration of
{K}_{\rm input}^{(n)}\left(\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}}\right)
over
\bm{k}_{\text{T}}
up to a cutoff
k_{\rm max}
of order
\mu_{Q_{0}}
, sensitivity to any nonperturbative mass parameters must vanish as
m/\mu_{Q_{0}}\to 0
. We may express this by demanding that

\displaystyle{}\pi\int_{0}^{k_{\rm{max}}^{2}}\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999muk^{2}_{\text{T}}}\nolimits{}{K}_{\rm input}^{(n)}\left(\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle{}\qquad=\chi^{(n)}(k_{\rm{max}}/\mu_{Q_{0}},\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}}))+O\left(\frac{m}{\mu_{Q_{0}}},\frac{m}{k_{\rm{max}}}\right)\,,

(47)

where
\chi^{(n)}(k_{\rm{max}}/\mu,\alpha_{s}(\mu))
is either zero or a perturbatively calculable function, independent of any nonperturbative mass parameters in
{K}_{\rm input}^{(n)}\left(\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}}\right)
.

Before continuing, let us summarize the basic properties of the parametrization,
\tilde{\underline{K}}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
:

•

For
b_{\text{T}}\approx 1/Q_{0}
or larger, it differs negligibly
from
{\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
, by construction. Therefore, both
\tilde{\underline{K}}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
and
{\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
are equally appropriate for describing the
Q\approx Q_{0}
region phenomenologically.

•

For small
b_{\text{T}}
, the RG scale transitions to the usual
\mu\sim 1/b_{\text{T}}
RG-improved form, but only when
b_{\text{T}}
is very small relative to the input scale,
b_{\text{T}}\ll 1/Q_{0}
.

•

The parametrization in Eq. (
IV
) obeys an exact RG equation,

\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\tilde{\underline{K}}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu)}\nolimits}{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\ln\mu}\nolimits{}}=-\gamma^{(n)}_{K}(\alpha_{s}(\mu))\,,

(48)

with no error terms present.

•

By contrast, the “input” parametrization defined in Eqs. (
39
)–(
41
) obeys the approximate RG equation in Eq. (
41
) with possible perturbative error terms, as shown in the equation.

•

Both Eq. (
41
) and Eq. (
48
) are satisfied for all
b_{\text{T}}
.

The resulting
\tilde{\underline{K}}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
is an accurate representation of the exact
\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
up to at most (non-logarithmic) order
\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}})^{n+1}
errors.

There is an ambiguity in the exact choice of functional form for
\overline{Q}_{0}(b_{\text{T}})
in Eq. (
42
) in the region of
b_{\text{T}}\approx 1/Q_{0}
, but this is just the usual scale uncertainty that appears in any truncated perturbation theory, akin to the dependence on the exact numerical choices for
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
. Since
Q_{0}
and
C_{1}/b_{\text{T}}
are of similar size when
b_{\text{T}}\approx 1/Q_{0}
the effect of the transformation is under perturbative control and the ambiguity diminishes as one incorporates higher orders.

To state this more explicitly, consider a family of different choices for
\overline{Q}_{0}(b_{\text{T}})
smoothly connected by an extra parameter
a
:

\overline{Q}_{0}(b_{\text{T}})\to\overline{Q}_{0}(b_{\text{T}},a)\,.

(49)

The only requirement is that Eq. (
49
) satisfies Eq. (
42
) for all the
a
one might consider.
Then,

\displaystyle{}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu}\nolimits}{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mua}\nolimits}\tilde{\underline{K}}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu)=\frac{1}{\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}}\nolimits}{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mua}\nolimits}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu}\nolimits}{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\ln\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}}\nolimits}\tilde{\underline{K}}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu)

\displaystyle{}=\frac{1}{\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}}\nolimits}{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mua}\nolimits}\left[\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu}\nolimits}{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\ln\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}}\nolimits}{\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}})+\gamma_{K}(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}))\right]

\displaystyle{}\sim\frac{1}{\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\mu_{\bar{Q}_{0}}}\nolimits}{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mua}\nolimits}\alpha_{s}(\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}})^{n+1}\ln^{(n+1)}\left(b_{\text{T}}\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}\right)\,.

(50)

In the second line, we have substituted Eq. (
IV
) and in the last
line we have used Eq. (
41
) while noting
that at small
b_{\text{T}}
the suppressed errors are enhanced by terms logarithmic in
b_{\text{T}}\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}
. However, by construction
\frac{1}{\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}}\nolimits}{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mua}\nolimits}
is only allowed to be nonzero in a region of
b_{\text{T}}
where
1/b_{\text{T}}
,
Q_{0}
, and
\overline{Q}_{0}
are all of
comparable size.
So Eq. (
50
) is just

\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu}\nolimits}{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mua}\nolimits}\tilde{\underline{K}}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu)=O\left(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}})^{n+1}\right)\,,

(51)

So effects from varying the precise choice of transition function
\overline{Q}_{0}(b_{\text{T}})

are always one order higher in
\alpha_{s}(Q_{0})
than the working order
n
.

We will illustrate the steps above more concretely with specific examples in Sec.
VII.1
.

V
Parametrization of the TMD ffs at
Q=Q_{0}

Now we turn to the parametrizations of the input TMD ffs themselves. Following the strategy outlined in the introduction, we will categorize regions as perturbative or nonperturbative for the input scale TMD ff in transverse momentum space. The steps will be very analogous to those just described in the previous section for
\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu)
.
As in that case, we will use an “
input
” subscript to label the TMD ff parametrization that applies phenomenologically at the input scale
Q=Q_{0}
, and which is to be used in Eq. (
22
).
For
k_{\text{T}}<Q_{0}
, the input parametrization will be defined to have a mainly nonperturbative transverse momentum dependence while for
k_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}
or larger it will transition into its
n^{\text{th}}
-order perturbative description, the first term in Eq. (
23
). Specifically, we define

{D}_{\rm input}^{(n,d_{r})}\left(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2}\right)\equiv\begin{cases}D^{(n,d_{r})}\left(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2}\right)\qquad\text{if}\;\;k_{\text{T}}\gtrsim Q_{0}\,\\
\\
\text{nonperturbative parametrization otherwise}\end{cases}\,.

(52)

The only condition on the intermediate region between
k_{\text{T}}\ll Q_{0}
and
k_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}
is that it should be reasonably smooth.
The input parametrization in Eq. (
52
) has the coordinate space representation,

\displaystyle{}{\tilde{D}}_{\rm input}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle{}\equiv\int\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu^{2}\bm{k}_{\text{T}}}\nolimits{}e^{i\bm{k}_{\text{T}}\cdot\bm{b}_{\text{T}}}{D}_{\rm input}^{(n,d_{r})}\left(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2}\right)\,.

(53)

We will require that the input coordinate space parametrizations satisfy the evolution equations through
n^{\text{th}}
-order in the evolution kernels, at least for the
b_{\text{T}}\approx 1/Q_{0}
region:

\displaystyle{}\frac{\partial\ln{\tilde{D}}_{\rm input}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu,Q^{2})}{\partial\ln Q}

\displaystyle{}\;={\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu)+O\left(\alpha_{s}(\mu)^{n+1}\right)+O\left(b_{\text{T}}m\right)\,,

(54)

\displaystyle{}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\ln{\tilde{D}}_{\rm input}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu,Q^{2})}\nolimits}{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\ln\mu}\nolimits}

\displaystyle{}\;=\gamma^{(n)}(\alpha_{s}(\mu);Q^{2}/\mu^{2})+O\left(\alpha_{s}(\mu)^{n+1}\right)+O\left(b_{\text{T}}m\right)\,.

(55)

Usually, these will be satisfied automatically if the parametrization follows Eq. (
52
).
Note the analogy between Eqs. (
52
)–(
V
) above and Eqs. (
39
)–(
41
) for the CS kernel.

Finally, for the integrated TMD ff to be consistent with the definition in Eq. (
30
) we must impose it directly on the parametrization,

\displaystyle{}2\pi z^{2}\int_{0}^{\mu_{Q_{0}}}\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999muk_{\text{T}}}\nolimits{}k_{\text{T}}{D}_{\rm input}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle{}\qquad\equiv\underline{d}^{(n,d_{r})}_{c}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})\,.

(56)

Here we have introduced new notation and another definition.
The underline on
\underline{d}^{(n,d_{r})}_{c}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})
is meant to indicate that
this is a specific
parametrization
(one determined by the
{D}_{\rm input}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
parametrization) of the
d_{c}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})
defined in Eq. (
30
). In accordance with Eq. (
32
), it is to have, by its definition, the property that

\underline{d}^{(n,d_{r})}_{c}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})=d^{(n,d_{r})}_{c}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})+O\left(\frac{m}{\mu_{Q_{0}}}\right)\,.

(57)

The parametrization
\underline{d}^{(n,d_{r})}_{c}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})
is a description of the definition
d_{c}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})
, with an
n^{\text{th}}
-order collinear treatment of the high transverse momentum region.
It is simply the
d^{(n,d_{r})}_{c}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})
from Eq. (
31
), but with account taken of the power-suppressed behavior in Eq. (
27
) that vanishes as
m/\mu_{Q_{0}}\to 0
.

It is worth pausing to review the different types of cutoff collinear ffs that we have introduced so far, given that there are now at least three. First, the version in Eq. (
30
) with no underlines or superscripts is the exact
d_{c}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})
that follows from the abstract operator definitions. Second,
the
\underline{d}^{(n,d_{r})}_{c}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})
above is a specific parametrization of that definition, with the only requirement being that in the limit of large
\mu_{Q_{0}}
it reduces to
n^{\text{th}}
-order collinear perturbation theory in terms of renormalized ffs with scheme
r
.
Finally, there is the
d^{(n,d_{r})}_{c}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})
defined in Eq. (
31
), which is just the limit of
\underline{d}^{(n,d_{r})}_{c}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})
where power-suppressed terms are dropped. An equally valid definition is

d^{(n,d_{r})}_{c}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})\equiv\lim_{\frac{m}{\mu_{Q_{0}}}\to 0}\underline{d}^{(n,d_{r})}_{c}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})\,.

(58)

Because Eq. (
56
) is just a definition, it contains no constraint by itself. The constraint is in Eq. (
57
).

So far, the steps for constructing the TMD ff parametrizations are very analogous to those of Sec.
IV
for
K(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
, but there are some differences. The most significant is that the “perturbative” large
k_{\text{T}}
part of Eq. (
52
) is not
entirely
perturbative because it involves non-perturbative collinear ffs as input in Eq. (
25
). The perturbative contribution to large transverse momentum dependence only enters in the coefficient function
C_{D}^{(n)}(zk_{\text{T}})
. By contrast, the only input to the perturbative calculation in Eq. (
37
) is the strong coupling
\alpha_{s}
.

The conditions in Eqs. (
52
)–(
56
) are all that we need for constructing phenomenologically useful parametrizations in the
Q\approx Q_{0}
region. Any model or parametrization that satisfies them is acceptable, but we will give some explicit examples in later sections.

However, the perturbative part of the parametrization in Eq. (
52
)
does not provide an accurate description in the
region of
k_{\text{T}}\gg Q_{0}
, where ratios of
k_{\text{T}}
and
Q_{0}
diverge. In coordinate space, the same issue arises at
b_{\text{T}}\ll 1/Q_{0}
in the form of large logarithms of
\mu b_{\text{T}}
. That does not create a problem for phenomenological applications near
Q\approx Q_{0}
where the
k_{\text{T}}\gg Q_{0}
contributions are suppressed in the integral of Eq. (
21
). However, it becomes important
as one evolves to
Q\gg Q_{0}
and the
k_{\text{T}}\gg Q_{0}
region starts to contribute more significantly.

Therefore, there needs to be a scale transformation from
\mu=\mu_{Q_{0}}
to
\mu=C_{1}/b_{\text{T}}
in the coordinate space TMD ff in the region of
b_{\text{T}}
just below
b_{\text{T}}\approx 1/Q_{0}
. This of course is exactly the same issue that we faced in the case of
{\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
in the previous section. For the TMD ff, it also implies that we have to evolve the CS scale
\sqrt{\zeta}
from
Q_{0}
to
C_{1}/b_{\text{T}}
.
For the scale change we can reuse the same scale transformation function from Eq. (
42
).

The exact solution to the TMD evolution equations for an individual TMD ff evolving from scales
\mu_{i}
,
Q_{i}
to
\mu_{Q_{0}}
,
Q_{0}
is

\displaystyle\tilde{D}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})=\tilde{D}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{i},Q_{i}^{2})\exp\left\{\int_{\mu_{i}}^{\mu_{Q_{0}}}\frac{d\mu^{\prime}}{\mu^{\prime}}\left[\gamma(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime});1)-\ln\frac{Q_{0}}{\mu^{\prime}}\gamma_{K}(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime}))\right]+\ln\frac{Q_{0}}{Q_{i}}\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{i})\right\}\,.

(59)

Or, if we use
\mu_{i}=\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}
,
Q_{i}=\overline{Q}_{0}
for the input scale,

\displaystyle\tilde{D}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})=\tilde{D}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}},\overline{Q}_{0}^{2})\exp\left\{\int_{\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}}^{\mu_{Q_{0}}}\frac{d\mu^{\prime}}{\mu^{\prime}}\left[\gamma(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime});1)-\ln\frac{Q_{0}}{\mu^{\prime}}\gamma_{K}(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime}))\right]+\ln\frac{Q_{0}}{\overline{Q}_{0}}\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}})\right\}\,.

(60)

As of yet, there are still no approximations on
\tilde{D}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
.
The left side has no dependence on
\overline{Q}_{0}
; any
\overline{Q}_{0}
-dependence in

\tilde{D}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}},\overline{Q}_{0}^{2})
is exactly canceled by an opposite
\overline{Q}_{0}
-dependence in
the exponential factor.

Now we can substitute approximations into the right side of Eq. (
60
) in a way that is again very analogous to the way we handled

\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu)
in the previous section by making substitutions on the right side of Eq. (
44
). We approximate

\tilde{D}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}},\overline{Q}_{0}^{2})

on the right side of Eq. (
60
) by replacing it with the
{D}_{\rm input}^{(n,d_{r})}\left(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}},\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}^{2}\right)
from Eq. (
52
). Because of the scale transformation, the result is a parametrization of
\tilde{D}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}},\overline{Q}_{0}^{2})
that is an accurate description not only for
b_{\text{T}}\approx 1/Q_{0}
and larger but also for all
b_{\text{T}}\ll 1/Q_{0}
. For the
\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}})
in the exponent on the right side of Eq. (
60
), we already have the analogous result from Eq. (
IV
) in Sec.
IV
, and we can reuse it here. All that remains then is to substitute

\gamma(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime});1)
and
\gamma_{K}(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime}))
by their truncated
n^{\text{th}}
-order perturbation theory approximations. Thus, our final parametrization of the input TMD ff is

\displaystyle\tilde{\underline{D}}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle={\tilde{D}}_{\rm input}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}},\overline{Q}_{0}^{2})\exp\left\{\int_{\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}}^{\mu_{Q_{0}}}\frac{d\mu^{\prime}}{\mu^{\prime}}\left[\gamma^{(n)}(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime});1)-\ln\frac{Q_{0}}{\mu^{\prime}}\gamma_{K}^{(n)}(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime}))\right]+\ln\frac{Q_{0}}{\overline{Q}_{0}}{\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}})\right\}\,.

(61)

The underline on
\tilde{\underline{D}}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
is our notation for the final parametrization of the
TMD ff at the input scale
\mu=\mu_{Q_{0}}
,
\zeta=Q_{0}^{2}
.

To summarize,
\tilde{\underline{D}}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
has the following properties:

•

Each factor in Eq. (

) is an
accurate approximation to the corresponding factor in the exact Eq. (

) point-by-point in
b_{\text{T}}
.

•

For
b_{\text{T}}\approx 1/Q_{0}
or larger, the exponential evolution factor deviates from unity by a negligible amount. This is by our construction of
\overline{Q}_{0}(b_{\text{T}})
.
\tilde{\underline{D}}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
therefore deviates negligibly from
{\tilde{D}}_{\rm input}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
when
Q\approx Q_{0}
. Both
\tilde{\underline{D}}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
and
{\tilde{D}}_{\rm input}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
with Eq. (
22
) are equally appropriate for applications to phenomenology when
Q\approx Q_{0}
. Also, recall that the

{\tilde{D}}_{\rm input}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
was originally formulated in transverse momentum space.

•

There is a smooth scale transformation to
\mu\sim\sqrt{\zeta}\sim 1/b_{\text{T}}
at small
b_{\text{T}}
, but only when
b_{\text{T}}\ll 1/Q_{0}
. Therefore,
\tilde{\underline{D}}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
continues to provide an accurate approximation to the exact TMD ff after
Q\gg Q_{0}
where the
b_{\text{T}}\ll 1/Q_{0}
region starts to be relevant.

•

Thus, at small
b_{\text{T}}
we may express the TMD ff in terms of collinear ffs
d_{r}
using the usual OPE methods.

•

\tilde{\underline{D}}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
satisfies the exact evolution equations

\displaystyle{}\frac{\partial\ln\tilde{\underline{D}}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})}{\partial\ln Q_{0}}

\displaystyle{}\qquad\qquad=\tilde{\underline{K}}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})\,,

(62)

\displaystyle{}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\ln\tilde{\underline{D}}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})}\nolimits}{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\ln\mu_{Q_{0}}}\nolimits}

\displaystyle{}\qquad\qquad=\gamma^{(n)}(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}});1)

\displaystyle{}\qquad\qquad\;-\gamma_{K}^{(n)}(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}}))\ln\left(\frac{Q_{0}}{\mu_{Q_{0}}}\right)\,.

(63)

There are no error terms in either of these equations, and
both Eq. (
•
‣
V
) and Eq. (
•
‣
V
) are valid for all
b_{\text{T}}
.

•

By contrast, the evolution equations for the “input” subscript TMD ffs in Eqs. (
V
)–(
V
) do come with explicit error terms.

As was the case for
\tilde{\underline{K}}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
, sensitivity to the choice of functional form for
\overline{Q}_{0}(b_{\text{T}})
is the standard scale uncertainty in truncated perturbation theory, and it vanishes in the limit that
Q_{0}
is large and/or high enough orders in
\alpha_{s}(Q_{0})
are included. To see this, we may repeat steps analogous to those after Eq. (
49
):

\displaystyle{}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu}\nolimits}{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mua}\nolimits}\ln\tilde{\underline{D}}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle{}\;=\frac{1}{\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}}\nolimits}{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mua}\nolimits}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu}\nolimits}{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\ln\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}}\nolimits}\ln\tilde{\underline{D}}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle{}\;+\frac{1}{\overline{Q}_{0}}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\overline{Q}_{0}}\nolimits}{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mua}\nolimits}\frac{\partial}{\partial\ln\overline{Q}_{0}}\ln\tilde{\underline{D}}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle{}\;=\frac{1}{\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}}}\nolimits}{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mua}\nolimits}\left[{\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}})+\gamma^{(n)}(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}});1)\right.

\displaystyle{}\;\left.-{\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}})-\gamma^{(n)}(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}});1)\right.

\displaystyle{}\;\left.+O\left(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}})^{n+1}\right)+O\left(b_{\text{T}}m\right)\right]\,.

\displaystyle{}\;=O\left(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}})^{n+1}\right)+O\left(\frac{m}{Q_{0}}\right)\,.

(64)

After the second equality, we have substituted the
expression for
\tilde{\underline{D}}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
from Eq. (
61
) and applied Eqs. (
V
)–(

) to
{\tilde{D}}_{\rm input}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{\overline{Q}_{0}},\overline{Q}_{0}^{2})

Thus, sensitivity to the choice of

VI
Summary of Steps

So far, we have focused on describing
\tilde{D}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
only at a fixed input scale
Q_{0}
. Now all that is necessary to calculate
W(q_{\text{T}},Q)
at any other scale using
\tilde{\underline{D}}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
and
\tilde{\underline{K}}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
is to substitute them into the right side of Eq. (

), along with the

-order perturbative expressions for
H(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q});C_{2})
,
\gamma(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime});1)
, and
\gamma_{K}(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime}))
. The result is an approximation for
W(q_{\text{T}},Q)
that includes evolution and is accurate for
Q\geq Q_{0}
,

\displaystyle W^{(n)}(q_{\text{T}},Q)

\displaystyle{}\equiv H^{(n)}(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q});C_{2})\int\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mskip-0.59999mu\bm{b}_{\text{T}}}\nolimits}{(2\pi)^{2}}~e^{-i\bm{q}_{\text{T}}\cdot\bm{b}_{\text{T}}}~\tilde{\underline{D}}_{A}^{(n,d_{r})}(z_{A},\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})~\tilde{\underline{D}}_{B}^{(n,d_{r})}(z_{B},\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle\,\times\exp\left\{\tilde{\underline{K}}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})\ln\left(\frac{Q^{2}}{Q_{0}^{2}}\right)+\int_{\mu_{Q_{0}}}^{\mu_{Q}}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu\mu^{\prime}}\nolimits}{\mu^{\prime}}\biggl[2\gamma^{(n)}(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime});1)-\ln\frac{Q^{2}}{{\mu^{\prime}}^{2}}\gamma_{K}^{(n)}(\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime}))\biggr]\right\}\,.

(65)

The approximation, notated by the “
(n)
” superscript on
W^{(n)}(q_{\text{T}},Q)
, is such that the scale dependence given by the evolution equations in Eqs. (
17
)–(
II
) is accurate point-by-point for
Q\geq Q_{0}
with errors at most of order
O\left(\alpha_{s}(Q_{0})^{n+1}\right)
– see Eqs. (
•
‣
V
)–(
•
‣
V
).
When
Q=Q_{0}
the
W^{(n)}(Q_{0},q_{\text{T}})
defined in Eq. (

) reduces to the TMD parton model up to the overall factor of
H^{(n)}(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q});C_{2})
and

\displaystyle{}W(q_{\text{T}},Q_{0})-W^{(n)}(q_{\text{T}},Q_{0})

\displaystyle{}\qquad=O\left(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}})^{n+1}\right)+O\left(\frac{m}{Q_{0}}\right)\,.

(66)

While it might appear that we have only succeeded at introducing an excessive amount of notation, the end result is a fairly simple recipe for combining any arbitrary model of nonperturbative transverse momentum dependence with full TMD factorization and evolution. After some basic initial decisions like choosing a value for
Q_{0}
and fixing renormalization schemes, the steps are as follows:

A) Model Building

A1:

Choose a nonperturbative model, or a nonperturbative technique more generally, to phenomenologically parametrize the small transverse momentum dependence in the TMD ff

and in

at the input scale. (See, for example, the list of models in the introduction. These can likely be used here.)

A2:

For step A
VI
, make any modifications to the models that are necessary to ensure that they satisfy Eq. (
39
), Eq. (
41
), Eq. (
47
), Eq. (
52
), and Eqs. (
V
)–(
57
). This step mostly amounts to extrapolating existing models to low order perturbative descriptions of

behavior. The result is a set of parametrizations for
{\tilde{D}^{(n,d_{r})}_{\text{input},A}}\left(z_{A},\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2}\right)
,
{\tilde{D}^{(n,d_{r})}_{\text{input},B}}\left(z_{B},\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2}\right)
, and
{\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
.

A3:

Choose a functional form for the
\overline{Q}_{0}(b_{\text{T}})
in Eq. (
42
) to implement the transition between scales. Use the “input” functions from step A
VI
to
construct
\tilde{\underline{K}}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
and
\tilde{\underline{D}}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
via
Eq. (
IV
) and Eq. (
61
).

B) Phenomenology at
\bm{Q\approx Q_{0}}

B1:

Apply factorization phenomenologically to
Q=Q_{0}
, Type I processes by taking

\displaystyle\tilde{\underline{D}}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle\to{\tilde{D}}_{\rm input}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})

in Eq. (
65
). This corresponds to the TMD parton model formula in Eq. (
22
) with the input function of step A
VI
. Fix any parameters in the nonperturbative model. This step is essentially no different from traditional TMD parton model motivated approaches to describing Type I processes. Thus, prior existing phenomenological results can likely be reused here.

B2:

Consider the phenomenological behavior of cross sections in a region of
Q
around
Q\approx Q_{0}
.
Take

\displaystyle\tilde{\underline{D}}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle\to{\tilde{D}}_{\rm input}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle\tilde{\underline{K}}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})

\displaystyle\to{\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})

in Eq. (
65
), and use the resulting formula in phenomenological fits to fix any nonperturbative parameters in
{\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
in the
Q\approx Q_{0}
region.

B3:

Verify that the effect of replacing
\tilde{\underline{K}}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
and
\tilde{\underline{D}}^{(n,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
by

{\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(n)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})

and
{\tilde{D}^{(n,d_{r})}_{\text{input}}}\left(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2}\right)
respectively is negligible for numerical calculations around
Q\approx Q_{0}
.

C) Phenomenology at large
\bm{Q}

C1:

Use Eq. (
65
) to evolve to significantly larger
Q
and make predictions for Type II observables.
Then refit and/or tune the nonperturbative parameters and improve the agreement with the higher
Q
observables. The adjustment of parameters should be expected to be minimal since the larger

.

C2:

Continue to repeat step C

with even higher

b_{\text{T}}
and more sensitive to small

The steps bridge standard approaches to Type I and Type II phenomenology.

VII
Low order examples

We mean for the steps in Sec.
VI
to apply to any model or nonperturbative method of parametrizing the intrinsic transverse momentum, so we have left the nonperturbative parts in Eq. (

We caution that the nonperturbative parametrizations that we will use below should be regarded only as toy examples at this stage, only to be used for illustration purposes. In future work, we hope to implement the steps in Sec.
VI
with input from at least some of the more sophisticated models or nonperturbative techniques referenced in the introduction.

Since this section makes much use of the notation we have introduced in earlier sections, we remind the reader once more of our notation glossary Appendix
A
. We will assume that
\overline{\rm MS}
renormalization is used everywhere, and we will always use
Q_{0}=2

VII.1
CS kernel example

To motivate a simple model for the nonperturbative part of the CS kernel, let us recall the physical effect that a nonzero
\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
has on
momentum-space cross sections at small transverse momentum. Consider the CS evolution
equation for
W(Q,q_{\text{T}})
in transverse momentum space. Fourier-Bessel transforms become transverse momentum convolutions so

\displaystyle{}\frac{\partial}{\partial\ln Q^{2}}W(Q,\bm{q}_{\text{T}})=

\displaystyle{}\int\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999mu^{2}\bm{k}_{\text{T}}}\nolimits K\left(\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu\right)W(Q,\bm{q}_{\text{T}}-\bm{k}_{\text{T}})+\text{Const.}\times W(Q,\bm{q}_{\text{T}})\,.

(68)

(See, for example, Eq. (25) of
Collins and Rogers 2015
.) The partial derivative here indicates that
z_{A}
and
z_{B}
should be fixed.
The “
Const.
” is independent of transverse momentum and is related to the anomalous dimension
\gamma
. It only contributes to a
Q
-dependent normalization.
Our task is to find a reasonable input
K\left(\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}}\right)
parametrization that satisfies Eq. (
39
) and also gives a good phenomenological description of cross sections in the region of
Q\approx Q_{0}
close to the input scale.
One way that
K\left(\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu\right)
can contribute to the evolution of
W(Q\approx Q_{0},\bm{q}_{\text{T}})
is simply through a normalization. To capture that behavior in Eq. (
68
), the model parametrization should include a term proportional to a
\delta
-function,

{K}_{\rm input}(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})\propto\delta^{(2)}(\bm{k}_{\text{T}})\,.

(69)

This is an example of step A
VI
applied to
K(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
.
But as step A
VI
prescribes,
{K}_{\rm input}(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
also needs to match the large
k_{\text{T}}
perturbative description,

{K}_{\rm input}(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}}}{{=}}K^{(1)}(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})\,,

(70)

where for now we work at order
n=1
.
We also know that

K^{(1)}(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})=\frac{\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}})C_{F}}{\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{k^{2}_{\text{T}}}\,.

(71)

(For a textbook derivation of Eq. (
71
), see section 13.10.2 of
Collins 2011
. Also see Ref.
Davies and Stirling 1984
for earlier calculations. For higher order
\tilde{K}
expressions, see also
Li and Zhu 2017
;
Li et al. 2020
;
Echevarria et al. 2016
;
Lübbert et al. 2016
, and see Ref.
Collins and Rogers 2017

for translating between different notations. See also
Vladimirov 2020
for more discussion of the operator definition.)

As
k_{\text{T}}
decreases below
Q_{0}
, Eq. (
70
) needs to transition into a nonperturbative eparametrization in a way that is still phenemonologically successful at describing
Q\approx Q_{0}
behavior. Existing evidence, both theoretical and phenomenological
Sun and Yuan 2013a
;
Sun and Yuan 2013b
;
Aidala et al. 2014
and from lattice calculations
Shanahan et al. 2020
, points toward a shape for TMD pdfs and ffs that varies only very weakly with scale in the
Q\approx Q_{0}
region. Our trial parametrization will reproduce this behavior if it is fairly sharply peaked around
k_{\text{T}}\ll Q_{0}
and then falls off rapidly for larger
k_{\text{T}}
. Equation (
68
) with
K^{(1)}(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
captures that general behavior if we make the replacement
k^{2}_{\text{T}}\to k^{2}_{\text{T}}+m_{K}^{2}
and keep the nonperturbative parameter
m_{K}
small relative to
Q_{0}
.
Thus, we obtain a reasonable candidate for a
{K}_{\rm input}^{(1)}(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
parametrization that satisfies Eq. (
39
) if we combine the
k^{2}_{\text{T}}\to k^{2}_{\text{T}}+m_{K}^{2}
modification of Eq. (
71
) with Eq. (
69
):

\displaystyle{}{K}_{\rm input}^{(1)}(k_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})=

\displaystyle{}\qquad\frac{\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}})C_{F}}{\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{k^{2}_{\text{T}}+m_{K}^{2}}+C_{K}\delta^{(2)}(\bm{k}_{\text{T}})\,.

(72)

The transformation into coordinate space is

{\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(1)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})=\frac{2\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}})C_{F}}{\pi}K_{0}(b_{\text{T}}m_{K})+C_{K}\,.

(73)

Satisfying both Eq. (
41
) and Eq. (
47
) with the
\overline{\rm MS}
expression for
\gamma_{K}^{(1)}
requires

C_{K}=\frac{2\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}})C_{F}}{\pi}\ln\left(\frac{m_{K}}{\mu_{Q_{0}}}\right)\

(74)

So the input CS kernel is just the single parameter function

\displaystyle{}{\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(1)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})=

\displaystyle{}\qquad\frac{2\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}})C_{F}}{\pi}\left[K_{0}(b_{\text{T}}m_{K})+\ln\left(\frac{m_{K}}{\mu_{Q_{0}}}\right)\right]\,.

(75)

Note that the same mass
m_{K}
appears in Eq. (
74
) and the first term of Eq. (

in

at small

\displaystyle{}\lim_{b_{\text{T}}\to 0}{\tilde{K}}_{\rm input}^{(1)}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})=

\displaystyle{}-\frac{2\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}})C_{F}}{\pi}\left[\ln\left(\frac{b_{\text{T}}\mu_{Q_{0}}e^{\gamma_{E}}}{2}\right)+\ln\left(\frac{m_{K}}{\mu_{Q_{0}}}\right)\right]+C_{K}\,

\displaystyle{}=-\frac{2\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}})C_{F}}{\pi}\ln\left(\frac{b_{\text{T}}\mu_{Q_{0}}e^{\gamma_{E}}}{2}\right)\,.

(76)

At large

(

(77)

VII.2
TMD ff example

Next we need to repeat steps A
VI
-A
VI
from Sec.
VI
for the TMD ffs themselves. To keep the discussion here simple, we will assume that the TMD ffs are the same for hadrons A and B, and we will continue to focus only on the
n=1
case. Fortunately, the steps are very analogous to the CS kernel, so much of the below will be repetition.

A typical parametrization, common in TMD parton-model descriptions of Type I processes, is a Gaussian,

{D}_{\rm input}^{(0,d_{r})}(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})=\frac{C}{\pi M^{2}}e^{-z^{2}k^{2}_{\text{T}}/M^{2}}\,.

(82)

This fails to satisfy Eq. (
52
) when we try to extend it directly to

n=1
because it does not have the right functional form to match to
D^{(n,d_{r})}\left(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2}\right)
when
k_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}
. In order to construct a TMD ff for
n=1
, we need to describe the transition from a nonperturbative peak like Eq. (
82
) to a perturbative large
k_{\text{T}}
power-law tail. The simplest way to do this
is to just append
D^{(n,d_{r})}\left(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2}\right)
to Eq. (
82
) as an additive term. Inside
D^{(n,d_{r})}\left(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2}\right)
, we can then make the replacement
k^{2}_{\text{T}}\to k^{2}_{\text{T}}+m_{D}^{2}
, where
m_{D}
is a nonperturbative parameter, to smooth the
k_{\text{T}}\to 0
behavior into a nonperturbative peak, analogous to what we did in Eq. (
72
). Thus, our trial input parametrization is

\displaystyle{}{D}_{\rm input}^{(1,d_{r})}(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle{}=\frac{1}{2\pi z^{2}}\frac{1}{k^{2}_{\text{T}}+m_{D}^{2}}\left[A^{(d_{r})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})\right.

\displaystyle{}\left.+B^{(d_{r})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})\ln\frac{Q_{0}^{2}}{k^{2}_{\text{T}}+m_{D}^{2}}\right]+\frac{C^{(d_{r})}}{\pi M^{2}}e^{-z^{2}k^{2}_{\text{T}}/M^{2}}\,,

(83)

where we have utilized the following abbreviations,

\displaystyle{}A^{(d_{r})}(z;\mu)\equiv\frac{\alpha_{s}(\mu)}{\pi}\left\{\left[(P_{qq}\otimes{d_{r}})(z;\mu)\right]\vphantom{\frac{3C_{F}}{2}d_{r}(z;\mu)}\right.

\displaystyle{}\qquad\left.-\frac{3C_{F}}{2}d_{r}(z;\mu)\right\}\,,

(84)

\displaystyle{}B^{(d_{r})}(z;\mu)\equiv\frac{\alpha_{s}(\mu)C_{F}}{\pi}d_{r}(z;\mu)\,.

(85)

For a textbook derivation of the large
k_{\text{T}}
perturbative behavior see, for example, Eq.(13.101) and Eq.(13.66) of Ref.
Collins 2011
. At the order-
\alpha_{s}
we are working in, the expressions are independent of the exact renormzalization scheme
r
used for the collinear ffs, so we have left it general in Eq. (
83
) for now. We will specialize to
r=\overline{\rm MS}
later.

The TMD ff should at least approximately
match its collinear perturbative expansion for
k_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}
, so
m_{D}
and
M
should be kept small relative to
Q_{0}
in any fits.

One of our requirements from step A
VI
is that parametrizations of the TMD ffs must satisfy the integral relation in
Eq. (
56
) with Eq. (
57
) satisfied. We will use this constraint to fix
C^{(d_{r})}
, which so far is just another nonperturbative parameter. Evaluating the transverse momentum integral of Eq. (
83
), expanding in small

m/\mu_{Q_{0}},
and solving for
C^{(d_{r})}
in terms of
\underline{d}_{c}^{(1,d_{r})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})
gives

\displaystyle C^{(d_{r})}

\displaystyle{}=\underline{d}_{c}^{(1,d_{r})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})-A^{(d_{r})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})\ln\left(\frac{\mu_{Q_{0}}}{m_{D}}\right)

\displaystyle{}-B^{(d_{r})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})\ln\left(\frac{\mu_{Q_{0}}}{m_{D}}\right)\ln\left(\frac{Q_{0}^{2}}{\mu_{Q_{0}}m_{D}}\right)

\displaystyle{}+O\left(\frac{m}{\mu_{Q_{0}}}\right)\,.

(86)

We still need to choose a form for
\underline{d}_{c}^{(1,d_{c})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})
, but our requirement is that it must satisfy Eq. (
57
), where we allow the
m/\mu_{Q_{0}}
-suppressed contributions to be chosen to give an optimal parametrization. Thus, let us define the power-suppressed terms in Eq. (
57
) (again, for
n=1
) to exactly equal those in Eq. (
86
).
Then,

\displaystyle C^{(d_{r})}

\displaystyle{}=d_{c}^{(1,d_{r})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})-A^{(d_{r})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})\ln\left(\frac{\mu_{Q_{0}}}{m_{D}}\right)

\displaystyle{}\qquad-B^{(d_{r})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})\ln\left(\frac{\mu_{Q_{0}}}{m_{D}}\right)\ln\left(\frac{Q_{0}^{2}}{\mu_{Q_{0}}m_{D}}\right)

\displaystyle{}=d_{r}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})-A^{(d_{r})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})\ln\left(\frac{\mu_{Q_{0}}}{m_{D}}\right)

\displaystyle{}\qquad-B^{(d_{r})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})\ln\left(\frac{\mu_{Q_{0}}}{m_{D}}\right)\ln\left(\frac{Q_{0}^{2}}{\mu_{Q_{0}}m_{D}}\right)

\displaystyle{}\qquad+\Delta^{(n,d_{r})}(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q}))\,,

(87)

where in the last line we have used Eq. (
31
). These last few steps are necessary if we wish to relate
C^{(d_{r})}
to known collinear ffs in standard schemes.

Equation (
83
), with Eq. (
87
) now for
C^{(d_{r})}
, is the parametrization of the TMD ff that is to be substituted into Eq. (
22
) and, in accordance with step B
VI
, used phenomenologically for describing Type I processes with standard TMD parton model techniques near the input scale
Q\approx Q_{0}
.

To get a general sense of what the Eq. (
83
) parametrization of the TMD ff looks like at
Q=Q_{0}
, we have plotted it in Fig.
5
using
\overline{\rm MS}
collinear ffs and
reasonable values of the mass parameters

m_{D}
, and
M
. (The nonperturbative mass parameters are kept small relative to
Q_{0}=2.0
GeV.) For this case,
\Delta^{(n,d_{\overline{\rm MS}})}(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q}))
is given by Eq. (
34
). The plot is for a sample value of
z=0.3
, but other values of
z
produce qualitatively similar curves, as can be easily checked. Since there is a general expectation from existing Type I TMD phenomenology that the small transverse momentum region in moderate
Q
processes is well-described by Gaussian TMDs, we have overlaid a Gaussian curve on top of Eq. (
83

k_{\text{T}}\ll Q_{0}
region retains a generally Gaussian shape.

As per step B
VI
, the small
k_{\text{T}}
region is to be described by fitting the
m_{D}
and

parameters to measurements. So long as these mass parameters are reasonably small compared to
Q_{0}

In principle
M
and

can both have

M\to M(z)\,,\qquad m_{D}\to m_{D}(z)\,,

(88)

but we will not include this in any of our example plots.

While we intend for the above parametrization to be only a toy example to illustrate broader procedural points, it is worth noting that at least some phenomenological support for an additive two-component model (like Eq. (
83
)) exists in the observation from
Aghasyan et al. 2018
that a sum of two peaked nonperturbative functions provides a good fit to data in the moderate
Q
region. In that case, the two peaked functions were both Gaussians, but the trend is nevertheless suggestive of a two component form more generally. Reference
Anselmino
et al. 2007b
has also confirmed that a combination of a Gaussian peak at small transverse momentum and a power-law tail at large transverse momentum provides a reasonable description of moderate

data.

The two model parameters in our construction have natural interpretations: The lower-case
m_{D}
describes exactly how the TMD ff transitions from the Gaussian-like behavior typically ascribed to nonperturbative dependence to the power-law behavior more typical of a perturbative tail, while the capital
M

k_{\text{T}}
.

\displaystyle{}z^{2}{\tilde{D}}_{\rm input}^{(1,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})=A^{(d_{r})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})K_{0}(b_{\text{T}}m_{D})

\displaystyle{}\qquad+B^{(d_{r})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})K_{0}(b_{\text{T}}m_{D})\ln\left(\frac{b_{\text{T}}}{2m_{D}}Q_{0}^{2}e^{\gamma_{E}}\right)

\displaystyle{}\qquad+d_{r}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})+\Delta^{(1,d_{r})}(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q}))+O\left(mb_{\text{T}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle z^{2}{\tilde{D}}_{\rm input}^{(1,d_{\overline{\rm MS}})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})=

\displaystyle{}\qquad-A^{(d_{\overline{\rm MS}})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})\ln\left(\frac{b_{\text{T}}\mu_{Q_{0}}e^{\gamma_{E}}}{2}\right)-B^{(d_{\overline{\rm MS}})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})\left[\ln^{2}\left(\frac{b_{\text{T}}\mu_{Q_{0}}e^{\gamma_{E}}}{2}\right)+\ln\left(\frac{b_{\text{T}}\mu_{Q_{0}}e^{\gamma_{E}}}{2}\right)\ln\left(\frac{Q_{0}^{2}}{\mu_{Q_{0}}^{2}}\right)\right]

VIII
Integral Relations II

It is worthwhile to return again to the integral
relations discussed in Sec.
III
in light of parametrizations like the one we constructed above.
Now recall how integral relations often appear
in phenomenological extractions of TMD functions near the input scale. For
Q\approx 1-2
GeV and
k_{\text{T}}\ll Q_{0}
, it is well-known that the shapes of transverse momentum distributions are generally well approximated by Gaussians, so one might reasonably adopt a parametrization of the form

D(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle??}}{{=}}\frac{d(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})}{\pi M^{2}}e^{-z^{2}k^{2}_{\text{T}}/M^{2}}\,,

(94)

where we have dropped renormalization subscripts etc to simplify expressions. The TMD and collinear ffs parametrized in this way automatically satisfy the parton model integral relation

\displaystyle 2\pi z^{2}\int\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999muk_{\text{T}}}\nolimits{}k_{\text{T}}D(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})=d(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})\,.

(95)

A parametrization like Eq. (
94
) imposes a strong suppression on large
k_{\text{T}}
, cutting off the large
k_{\text{T}}
tail. Because large
k_{\text{T}}
-dependence is generally regarded as perturbatively calculable, it is tempting to assume that it is possible to fix
Eq. (
94
) in later steps simply by appending a purely perturbative tail for the region of
k_{\text{T}}\gtrsim Q_{0}
while leaving the initial Gaussian of Eq. (
94
) completely unchanged. Taking that view literally would mean that, as regards the integral relation, one does not need to modify either Eq. (
94
) or Eq. (
95
) other than to explicitly indicate an upper cutoff on the integral on the right-hand side of Eq. (
95
) and to specify that higher orders in
\alpha_{s}
and/or
1/Q_{0}

are neglected:

\displaystyle{}2\pi z^{2}\int_{0}^{\mu_{Q_{0}}}\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999muk_{\text{T}}}\nolimits{}k_{\text{T}}D(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle{}\qquad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle??}}{{=}}d(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})+\text{small corrections}\,.

(96)

A problem with this approach can be seen, however, in our examples from the previous section, where Eq. (
96
) would amount to applying the transverse momentum integral in Eq. (
96
) to Eq. (
83
) with
C^{(d_{r})}=d_{r}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})
instead of Eq. (
87
) so that

\displaystyle{}z^{2}{\tilde{D}}_{\rm input}^{(1,d_{r})}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})=A^{(d_{r})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})K_{0}(b_{\text{T}}m_{D})

\displaystyle{}\qquad+B^{(d_{r})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})K_{0}(b_{\text{T}}m_{D})\ln\left(\frac{b_{\text{T}}}{2m_{D}}Q_{0}^{2}e^{\gamma_{E}}\right)

\displaystyle{}\qquad+d_{r}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})\exp\left(-\frac{b^{2}_{\text{T}}M^{2}}{4z^{2}}\right)\,.

(97)

But then the integral on the left side of Eq. (
96
) becomes

\displaystyle{}2\pi z^{2}\int_{0}^{\mu_{Q_{0}}}\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999muk_{\text{T}}}\nolimits{}k_{\text{T}}{D}_{\rm input}(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})=

\displaystyle{}\;d_{r}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})+A^{(d_{r})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})\ln\left(\frac{\mu_{Q_{0}}}{m_{D}}\right)

\displaystyle{}\;+B^{(d_{r})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})\ln^{2}\left(\frac{\mu_{Q_{0}}^{2}}{m_{D}^{2}}\right)\,.

(98)

The last two terms involving
A^{(d_{r})}
and
B^{(d_{r})}
would thus need to be identified with the “small corrections” of Eq. (
96
).
However, the factorization theorem applies to the limit that

m/\mu_{Q_{0}}\to 0
, so the last two terms are not the purely perturbative corrections implied by an expression like Eq. (
96
).
While
A^{(d_{r})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})
and
B^{(d_{r})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})
involve a coupling
\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}})
that vanishes asymptotically like

\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}})\sim\frac{1}{\ln(\mu_{Q_{0}}/\Lambda_{\text{QCD}})}\,,

at large
\mu_{Q_{0}}
,
the logarithms in Eq. (
98
) more than compensate for this in the limit of large
\mu_{Q_{0}}
. Indeed, the term in Eq. (
98
) involving
B^{(d_{r})}(z;\mu_{Q_{0}})
blows up as
\mu_{Q_{0}}/m\to\infty
. To keep a consistent integral relation that matches a parton model interpretation while also accounting for tails, evolution, etc, the coefficient of the Gaussian in Eq. (
94
) needs to be
an expression like Eq. (
87
) rather than a simple collinear ff.

To write a version of Eq. (
96
), that is consistent with the presence of a large
k_{\text{T}}
tail region, as with our example in Sec.
VII.2
with Eq. (
89
), it was necessary to interpolate between the nonperturbative and tail regions first.

q_{\text{T}}
cross section (or rather
W(q_{\text{T}},Q)
) in Eq. (
20
) into the form that involves Eq. (
99
)
g

Setting up the usual presentation of the cross section begins with a partition of the coordinate space
\tilde{W}(b_{\text{T}},Q)
into regions considered large and small
b_{\text{T}}
. One does this by defining an arbitrary function of
b_{\text{T}}
, traditionally called
\bm{b}_{*}(b_{\text{T}})
. The
function should smoothly interpolate between
\bm{b}_{\text{T}}
at small values of
b_{\text{T}}
and a maximum transverse size
\bm{b}_{\rm max}
as
b_{\text{T}}
grows to
b_{\text{T}}\gg b_{\text{max}}
. It is otherwise arbitrary. In other words,

\bm{b}_{*}(b_{\text{T}})=\begin{cases}\bm{b}_{\text{T}}&b_{T}\ll b_{\rm max}\\
\bm{b}_{\rm max}&b_{T}\gg b_{\rm max}\end{cases}\,.

(100)

The value of
b_{\text{max}}
is also arbitrary, but it is usually interpreted roughly as a value somewhere near the boundary between nonperturbatively large and perturbatively small regions of
b_{\text{T}}

The purpose of the “
\bm{b}_{*}
method”

Collins and
Soper 1982b
is to sequester a purely perturbative
calculation of transverse coordinate dependence away
from a part that involves nonperturbative
modeling or fitting.
While any reasonably well-behaved, smooth function of

that obeys the right side of Eq. (
100
) is a valid
\bm{b}_{*}(b_{\text{T}})
, the most often used choice is

\displaystyle\bm{b}_{*}(b_{\text{T}})=\frac{\bm{b}_{\text{T}}}{\sqrt{1+b^{2}_{\text{T}}/b_{\text{max}}^{2}}}\,.

(101)

Later on, we will also need to define another hard scale that approaches the RG improved value of
\mu=C_{1}/b_{\text{T}}

(102)

The behavior of
\mu_{b_{*}}
is similar to that of our

, but
\overline{Q}_{0}(b_{\text{T}})
approaches

at large

(103)

Exactly the same equation applies independently of the
transverse coordinate
\bm{b}_{\text{T}}
, so we also have

\displaystyle{}\tilde{D}_{A}(z,\bm{b}_{*};\mu,\zeta)

\displaystyle{}\qquad=\tilde{D}_{A}(z,\bm{b}_{*};\mu,Q_{0}^{2})\exp\left\{\tilde{K}(b_{*};\mu)\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{\zeta}}{Q_{0}}\right)\right\}\,.

(104)

Then the ratio of Eq. (
103
) and Eq. (

\displaystyle{}\frac{\tilde{D}_{A}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu,\zeta)}{\tilde{D}_{A}(z,\bm{b}_{*};\mu,\zeta)}=\frac{\tilde{D}_{A}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu,Q_{0}^{2})}{\tilde{D}_{A}(z,\bm{b}_{*};\mu,Q_{0}^{2})}\times

\displaystyle{}\qquad\times\exp\left\{-\left[\tilde{K}(b_{*},\mu)-\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}},\mu)\right]\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{\zeta}}{Q_{0}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle{}=\frac{\tilde{D}_{A}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu,Q_{0}^{2})}{\tilde{D}_{A}(z,\bm{b}_{*};\mu,Q_{0}^{2})}\exp\left\{-g_{K}(b_{\text{T}})\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{\zeta}}{Q_{0}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle{}=\frac{\tilde{D}_{A}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})}{\tilde{D}_{A}(z,\bm{b}_{*};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})}\exp\left\{-g_{K}(b_{\text{T}})\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{\zeta}}{Q_{0}}\right)\right\}\,,

(105)

where on the second line we have

(106)

\mu

of

is also

-independent,

is

\mu
-independent. That is,
\mu
-dependence cancels between the two terms, so

is
\mu
-independent by definition.

\mu

is a

-independent overall factor – recall the evolution equation in Eq. (

\displaystyle-g_{A}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}})

\displaystyle{}\equiv\ln\left(\frac{\tilde{D}_{A}(z,\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})}{\tilde{D}_{A}(z,\bm{b}_{*};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})}\right)\,,

But implementing this style of approach in practice is complicated by the fact that it leaves very little said about the details of the Eq. (

)

g_{A}^{(n,d_{\overline{\rm MS}})}(z_{A},b_{\text{T}})\longrightarrow g_{A}^{(n,d_{\overline{\rm MS}})}(z_{A},b_{\text{T}};\{c_{1},c_{2},\dots\})\,.

(123)

Here, the
\{c_{1},c_{2},\dots\}
is a list of ansatz parameters. But notice that the integral

\int_{0}^{\mu_{Q_{0}}}\mathop{\mathrm{d}\mskip-0.59999muk_{\text{T}}}\nolimits{}k_{\text{T}}e^{-g_{A}^{(n,d_{\overline{\rm MS}})}(z_{A},b_{\text{T}};\{c_{1},c_{2},\dots\})}

is constrained by Eqs. (
56
)–(
57

\{c_{1},c_{2},\dots\}
parameters are not independent if a separate set of collinear ffs is known and available. At least one of the parameters is fixed in terms of the other parameters and the collinear functions.
In the example from Sec.
VII
, this corresponds to the step where we replaced the
C^{(d_{c})}
in Eq. (
83
) by the right side of Eq. (
87
).
Without that step, the parameters of
g_{A}^{(n,d_{\overline{\rm MS}})}(z_{A},b_{\text{T}})
are underconstrained, and the model of the nonperturbative transverse momentum will generally be inconsistent with the collinear correlation functions.

To our knowledge, such a step is not explicitly performed in the top-down implementations of TMD factorization and evolution.

These constraints also mean that the nonperturbative

Conclusion

In this paper, we have explained the advantages of a bottom-up/forward-evolution style of treating TMD correlation functions modeled or calculated nonperturbatively at a moderate input scale. Before concluding, let us reemphasize that we have not made any fundamental modifications to standard TMD factorization and evolution – indeed, see Eq. (
121
). Rather, our purpose has been to make explicit the steps for combining any choice of nonperturbative model or parametrization of transverse momentum dependence with full TMD evolution in a reasonably automated way. This did lead to a significant amount of reorganization of previously known results, but we hope that the final prescription is fairly straightforward. Overall, our discussion in this paper might be summarized as a prescription for constructing the “
g
-functions” of typical CSS implementations in a way that conforms simultaneously with the TMD parton model viewpoint and TMD evolution.

The phenomenological strategy is to take TMDs generated from models, nonperturbative calculations, and/or fits near an input scale
Q_{0}
, and use these to make predictions for higher
Q
measurements. Based on how successful those predictions are, models and/or fit parameters can be updated. The expected trend is that the amount of parameter adjustment diminishes as larger
Q
measurements are included.

Future steps involve constructing nonperturbative parametrizations analogous to our example functions in
Sec.
VII
, but with more sophisticated input from nonperturbative QCD, including the models discussed in the introduction.
It is likely (as was our intent) that many existing “Type I” phenomenological results can be combined with our approach with minimal modifications. Using the steps from Sec.
VI
to implement evolution with specific models like the spectator or bag models is a natural next task. Of course, all expressions need to be extended to all flavors and channels and to TMD pdfs. Other minor tasks still to be completed include extending to higher
n
(for
\tilde{K}
this is simple), including the singlet gluon channel, and extending it to a treatment of gluon TMDs
Scarpa et al. 2020
.

Over the past several years, there has been significant progress also in lattice-based methods for calculating TMD functions
Chu et al. 2022
;
Ji et al. 2021
;
Constantinou et al. 2021
;
Cichy and Constantinou 2019
;
Ebert et al. 2022b
;
Ebert et al. 2020
;
Ebert et al. 2019a
;
Ebert et al. 2019b
. The techniques discussed in this paper will also be important for connecting those developments to experimental data and phenomenological extractions.

We have focused on discussing unpolarized cross sections, but the same steps carry over in a straightforward way to
polarized processes. A point of caution is that the integral relations analogous to Eq. (
30
) become more subtle in some spin dependent TMD functions, and the translation between correlation functions defined with cutoffs and in other schemes is not as straightforward
Qiu et al. 2020
;
Rogers 2020
.

Nonperturbative
g
-functions exactly analogous to Eq. (
99
) appear in the TMD factorization formula for
double
scattering with double parton TMDs – see Eq.(6.31) of

Buffing et al. 2018
. Therefore, techniques analogous to what we have described for single scattering should in principle apply there as well.

An application where our procedure also likely helps, but which we have not yet discussed in detail, is in the matching to large transverse momentum. The so-called “asymptotic” term discussed in, for example,
Collins et al. 2016
is the large
k_{\text{T}}
asymptote of Eq. (
21
), and it is an important ingredient for matching to the fixed order collinear calculation at large
q_{\text{T}}\approx Q
. In top-down/backward evolution approaches, errors that grow larger as
Q
decreases tend to spoil reasonable agreement between direct calculations of Eq. (
21
) and the asymptotic term
Boglione et al. 2015
. By contrast, in the bottom-up approach we have laid out in this paper, the matching to the asymptotic term at
Q\approx Q_{0}
is automatic by construction. A separate but related issue is the difficulty observed in some processes, especially at moderate
Q
, of explaining
q_{\text{T}}\approx Q
using standard fixed order collinear factorization and existing collinear pdfs and ffs
Daleo et al. 2005
;
Gonzalez-Hernandez
et al. 2018
;
Bacchetta
et al. 2019a
;
Wang et al. 2019
.

In large
Q
measurements, there is reason to expect that nonperturbative transverse momentum eventually becomes phenomenologically irrelevant.
It can remain important, however, when very high precision is a goal, such as measurements of vector boson masses
Banfi et al. 2011
;
Bacchetta
et al. 2019b
;
Guzzi et al. 2014
;
Nadolsky 2005
;
Aaltonen et al. 2022
.

We leave all of these considerations to future work.

Appendix B
Scale setting

The expansion in
m/Q
that gives the TMD factorization in Eq. (
14
) as its leading power must eventually break down as
Q
becomes small. In practice, this means one must choose a minimum
Q_{0}
below which one stops trusting factorization in phenomenological applications, and this is what we have called the input scale
Q_{0}
. Values of
\mu
near
Q_{0}
are understood to be close to the boundary where perturbative expansions in
\alpha_{s}{}(\mu)
and power expansions in
m/Q_{0}
cease to be valid even approximately. For the purposes of the present paper,
Q_{0}
may be treated as a phenomenologically determined number. In practice it is usually taken to be somewhere in the range of
1
-
3
GeV.

The value of
Q_{0}
fixes the overall RG (
\mu
) and rapidity (
\zeta
) scales in the input TMD ffs in Eq. (
21
).
There is nothing incorrect in principle with working only with TMD factorization in the form that is written in Eq. (
14
) and Eq. (
20
). One may regard
\tilde{D}_{A}(z_{A},\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
,
\tilde{D}_{B}(z_{B},\bm{b}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})
, and
\tilde{K}(b_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}})
as entirely unknown functions to be determined phenomenologically or by other nonperturbative means. This is essentially the TMD parton model and is a standard approach to much phenomenology applied to nucleon structure studies near
Q\approx Q_{0}
. However, it leaves the TMD ffs very badly constrained at large
k_{\text{T}}
where perturbative calculations should be possible. When
k_{A,B\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}
in Eq. (
14
), or equivalently when

b_{\text{T}}\approx 1/Q_{0}
in Eq. (
20
), it is possible to expand the TMD functions perturbatively in collinear factorization and thereby increase predictive power.
When
k_{A,B\text{T}}
is much larger than
Q_{0}
, one can further improve perturbative calculations by choosing
k_{A,B\text{T}}
itself to set the hard scale in the individual TMD ffs rather than
Q_{0}
. Of course, when
k_{A,B\text{T}}
is small, it is not appropriate as a hard scale and one should instead continue to use
Q_{0}
. Indeed, it is the nonperturbative
k_{\text{T}}
-dependence that is often of primary interest in TMD hadron structure studies.

In this appendix, we elaborate on the issue of the transition between the choices of
Q_{0}
and
k_{\text{T}}
as scales for the TMD ffs, keeping in mind that the ultimate goal is to smoothly transition between traditional Type I and type II styles of approach. The discussion will be somewhat schematic and is meant to further motivate the main body of the text. Also, we will work in transverse momentum space where our main points will be somewhat more intuitive, but the discussion applies equally in transverse coordinate space, which is more common.

To see the issues clearly,
recall that when
k_{\text{T}}
is large we may express the
k_{\text{T}}
-dependence in a single input TMD ff in the form

\displaystyle{}D(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k_{\text{T}}\gg m}}{{=}}

\displaystyle{}\qquad\frac{1}{k^{2}_{\text{T}}}\left[\delta\left(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}}),\frac{k_{\text{T}}}{\mu_{Q_{0}}},\frac{Q_{0}^{2}}{\mu_{Q_{0}}^{2}}\right)+O\left(\frac{m}{\mu_{Q_{0}}},\frac{m}{k_{\text{T}}}\right)\right]\,

(124)

where
\delta
is some function of the coupling and the ratios of the scales that generally appear in logarithms.

When
k_{\text{T}}\gg Q_{0}
, the
k_{\text{T}}/\mu_{Q_{0}}
ratio on the right side of Eq. (
124
) diverges so that perturbative calculations of
\delta
with fixed
\mu_{Q_{0}}
degrade in accuracy. Optimizing
\delta
in perturbation theory requires another application of evolution from
\mu_{Q_{0}}
to
\mu_{k_{\text{T}}}=C_{k}k_{\text{T}}
and
Q_{0}^{2}
to
\mu_{k_{\text{T}}}^{2}=C_{k}^{2}k^{2}_{\text{T}}
where
C_{k}
is an order unity numerical constant analogous to
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
in the main body of the text.
After evolution, the TMD ff that we work with instead has the general behavior

\displaystyle{}D(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{k_{\text{T}}},\mu_{k_{\text{T}}}^{2})=

\displaystyle{}\qquad\frac{1}{k^{2}_{\text{T}}}\left[\delta\left(\alpha_{s}(C_{k}k_{\text{T}}),O\left(1\right),O\left(1\right)\right)+O\left(\frac{m}{k_{\text{T}}}\right)\right]\,.

(125)

Now as
k_{\text{T}}/Q_{0}\to\infty
the convergence properties of a perturbative expansion of
\delta
only improves as the coupling and power suppressed terms vanish. Most of the standard Type II/top-down styles of TMD factorization implementations thus only use a scale choice analogous to Eq. (
125
) (or rather its coordinate space analog) and rarely the fixed scale version in Eq. (
124
).

However, small-
\alpha_{s}
calculations of
\delta
in Eq. (
125
) come with numerically unstable truncation errors in the region around
k_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}
simply because
Q_{0}
is at the border between perturbative and nonperturbative scales. Small variations in, for example, the value of
C_{k}
can have a big effect on calculations. The problem is exacerbated by the fact that the transformation from
\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2}
to
\mu_{k_{\text{T}}},\mu_{k_{\text{T}}}^{2}
effectively involves a resummation of many higher order logarithms in region of
k_{A,B\text{T}}
. That is, what appear in the factorization after the scale transformation is not Eq. (
125
) alone but

\displaystyle{}\sim D(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{k_{\text{T}}},\mu_{k_{\text{T}}}^{2})

\displaystyle\;\times\left[1+\sum_{\text{terms like}}\;\alpha_{s}(\mu_{k})^{m}\ln^{n}\frac{\mu_{k_{\text{T}}}}{\mu_{Q_{0}}}\right]\,.

(126)

However, when
k_{\text{T}}\approx\mu_{Q_{0}}
the logarithms on
the second line are anyway no larger than any other contributions that are higher order in
\alpha_{s}(k_{\text{T}})
.
5
5

5

It is also worth recalling here that QCD perturbation series are only asymptotic rather than convergent.
The advantage of the scale transformation is lost here. More problematically, because of the rapidly rising coupling
\alpha_{s}(\mu_{k})
just below
k_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}
, the higher order logarithmic terms in Eq. (
126
) are numerically unstable over the range of
k_{\text{T}}
extending from just below to just above
Q_{0}
.
Thus, calculations that use Eq. (
125
) are rather poorly behaved in the
k_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}
borderline region where we should expect a reasonably smooth transition to a nonperturbative region.

But calculations with a fixed scale can still be at least approximately valid around
k_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}
where Eq. (
124
) is

\displaystyle{}D(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q_{0}},Q_{0}^{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle{}\qquad\frac{1}{k^{2}_{\text{T}}}\left[\delta\left(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q_{0}}),O\left(1\right),O\left(1\right)\right)+O\left(\frac{m}{Q_{0}}\right)\right]\,.

(127)

As long as
Q_{0}
is not very small, one can use low order perturbative calculations to approximate
D
. And there are no large logarithms in Eq. (
124
) if
k_{\text{T}}\sim Q_{0}
, so the advantages of changing scales to Eq. (
125
) are absent.

Therefore, our preferred choice of scale for the transition region around
k_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}
is actually the fixed scale in Eq. (
124
), not the
k_{\text{T}}\gg Q_{0}
RG improved scale of Eq. (
125
). Another advantage of using the fixed scale is that it automatically gives the fixed order asymptotic term in momentum space at
Q=Q_{0}
and
q_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}
, which is also a fixed scale calculation in momentum space, when the TMD ff is substituted into Eq. (
14
).

The above suggests that it is best to categorize the
k_{\text{T}}
-dependence not into just two regions of small
k_{\text{T}}
and large
k_{\text{T}}
, but into three regions: small
k_{\text{T}}
(
k_{\text{T}}\ll Q_{0}
), large
k_{\text{T}}
(
k_{\text{T}}\approx Q_{0}
), and very large
k_{\text{T}}
(
k_{\text{T}}\gg Q_{0}
).
The large
k_{\text{T}}
region is where
k_{\text{T}}
is just barely large enough for small coupling descriptions of transverse momentum dependence to be reasonable.

In the discussion above we have worked in transverse momentum space because that better matches the intuition of models and phenomenology. However, the analogous observations apply straightforwardly to transverse coordinate space. In that case, there is a region of very large
b_{\text{T}}\gg 1/Q_{0}
that is entirely nonperturbative, a region of very small
b_{\text{T}}\ll 1/Q_{0}
that is purely perturbative as long as an RG improved
\mu\sim 1/b_{\text{T}}
is used, and an intermediate region of
b_{\text{T}}\sim 1/Q_{0}
where fixed order, fixed scale calculations are ideal.

One of our tasks in the main body of the paper is to interpolate between these three regions in our parameterizations. For the intermediate region of transverse momentum, transitioning between scales only introduces higher order errors.

Notice that our discussion of large logarithms above is a mirror image of how large logarithms are often introduced in explanations of top down approaches. There, one starts with Eq. (
124
) but using
\mu_{Q}
and
Q^{2}
instead of the input
\mu_{Q_{0}}
and
Q_{0}^{2}
and assuming
Q\gg Q_{0}
:

\displaystyle{}D(z,z\bm{k}_{\text{T}};\mu_{Q},Q^{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k_{\text{T}}\gg m}}{{=}}

\displaystyle{}\qquad\frac{1}{k^{2}_{\text{T}}}\left[\delta\left(\alpha_{s}(\mu_{Q}),\frac{k_{\text{T}}}{\mu_{Q}},\frac{Q^{2}}{\mu_{Q}^{2}}\right)+O\left(\frac{m}{\mu_{Q}},\frac{m}{k_{\text{T}}}\right)\right]\,

(128)

Then the task is to determine how to resum large logarithms of
\ln(k_{\text{T}}/\mu_{Q})
as
k_{\text{T}}
gets
small
relative to
Q
, rather than as
k_{\text{T}}
gets large relative to
Q_{0}

Scale transformation function

For the scale transition function in Eq. (
42
), we must arrange for the transition from
\sim 1/b_{\text{T}}
to
Q_{0}
to occur at
b_{\text{T}}
somewhat smaller than
1/Q_{0}
to avoid modifying the treatment of Eq. (
20
) in the
Q\approx Q_{0}
region.
One choice that satisfies this for a
Q_{0}=2
GeV is

\displaystyle{}\overline{Q}_{0}(b_{\text{T}})

\displaystyle{}=2.0~\text{GeV}\left[1-\left(1-\frac{C_{1}}{(2.0~\text{GeV})b_{\text{T}}}\right)e^{-(4~\text{GeV}^{2})b^{2}_{\text{T}}}\right]\,.

(129)

If we wish to adjust the exact shape in the
\approx 1/Q_{0}
transition region by adding a parameter as in Eq. (
49
), we may modify Eq. (
C
) by introducing a parameter
a
,

\displaystyle\overline{Q}_{0}(b_{\text{T}},a)

\displaystyle{}=2.0~\text{GeV}\left[1-\left(1-\frac{C_{1}}{(2.0~\text{GeV})b_{\text{T}}}\right)e^{-b^{2}_{\text{T}}a^{2}}\right]\,.

(130)

Here the transition between the two RG scales takes place around
b_{\text{T}}\sim 1/a
. We can use the Eq. (
C
) form to check approximate scale independence in the transition region by varying
a
slightly.
C_{1}
is the usual numerical constant,
C_{1}=2e^{-\gamma_{E}}\approx 1.123
.
