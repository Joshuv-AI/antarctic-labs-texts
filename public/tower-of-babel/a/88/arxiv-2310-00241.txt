The Complexity of Distance- r r Dominating Set Reconfiguration
Niranka Banerjee, Duc A. Hoang

Abstract
For a fixed integer r ≥ 1 r\geq 1 , a distance- r r dominating set of a graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) is a vertex subset D ⊆ V D\subseteq V such that every vertex in V V is within distance r r from some member of D D . Given two distance- r r dominating sets D s , D t D_{s},D_{t} of G G , the Distance- r r Dominating Set Reconfiguration (D r r DSR) problem asks if there is a sequence of distance- r r dominating sets that transforms D s D_{s} into D t D_{t} (or vice versa) such that each intermediate member is obtained from its predecessor by applying a given reconfiguration rule exactly once. The problem for r = 1 r=1 has been well-studied in the literature. We consider D r r DSR for r ≥ 2 r\geq 2 under two well-known reconfiguration rules: Token Jumping ( 𝖳𝖩 \mathsf{TJ} , which involves replacing a member of the current D r r DS by a non-member) and Token Sliding ( 𝖳𝖲 \mathsf{TS} , which involves replacing a member of the current D r r DS by an adjacent non-member). It is known that under any of 𝖳𝖲 \mathsf{TS} and 𝖳𝖩 \mathsf{TJ} , the problem on split graphs is 𝙿𝚂𝙿𝙰𝙲𝙴 \mathtt{PSPACE} -complete for r = 1 r=1 . We show that for r ≥ 2 r\geq 2 , the problem is in 𝙿 \mathtt{P} , resulting in an interesting complexity dichotomy. Along the way, we prove some non-trivial bounds on the length of a shortest reconfiguration sequence on split graphs when r = 2 r=2 which may be of independent interest. Additionally, we prove some observations that lead to the polynomial-time solvability of D r r DSR for r ≥ 2 r\geq 2 on dually chordal graphs under 𝖳𝖩 \mathsf{TJ} and on cographs under any of 𝖳𝖲 \mathsf{TS} and 𝖳𝖩 \mathsf{TJ} , and design a linear-time algorithm for solving the problem under 𝖳𝖩 \mathsf{TJ} on trees. On the negative side, we show that D r r DSR for r ≥ 1 r\geq 1 on planar graphs of maximum degree three and bounded bandwidth is 𝙿𝚂𝙿𝙰𝙲𝙴 \mathtt{PSPACE} -complete, improving the degree bound of previously known results. We also show that the known 𝙿𝚂𝙿𝙰𝙲𝙴 \mathtt{PSPACE} -completeness results under 𝖳𝖲 \mathsf{TS} and 𝖳𝖩 \mathsf{TJ} for r = 1 r=1 on bipartite graphs and chordal graphs can be extended for r ≥ 2 r\geq 2 .

Introduction

Reconfiguration Problems.

For the last few decades,
reconfiguration problems
have independently emerged in different areas of computer science, including recreational mathematics (e.g., games and puzzles), computational geometry (e.g., flip graphs of triangulations), constraint satisfaction (e.g., solution space of Boolean formulas), and even quantum complexity theory (e.g., ground state connectivity), among others
[
19
,
17
,
16
,
3
]
. In a
reconfiguration variant
of a computational problem (e.g.,
Satisfiability
,
Independent Set
,
Dominating Set
,
Vertex-Coloring
, etc.), two
feasible solutions
(e.g., satisfying truth assignments, independent sets, dominating sets, proper vertex-colorings, etc.)
S
and
T
are given along with a
reconfiguration rule
that describes how to slightly modify one feasible solution to obtain a new one. The question is whether one can transform/reconfigure
S
into
T
via a sequence of feasible solutions such that each intermediate member is obtained from its predecessor by applying the given rule exactly once. Such a sequence, if it exists, is called a
reconfiguration sequence
.

Distance Domination.

In 1975, Meir and Moon
[
14
]
combined the concepts of “distance” and “domination” in graphs and introduced
distance-
r
dominating set
of a graph (which they called a “
r
-covering”), where
r\geq 1
is some fixed integer.
For a fixed positive integer
r
and a graph
G
, a
distance-
r
dominating set
(D
r
DS) (also known as
r
-hop dominating set
or
r
-basis
) of
G
is a vertex subset
D
where each vertex of
G
is within distance
r
from some member of
D
. In particular, a D
1
DS is nothing but a
dominating set
of
G
.

Given a graph
G
and some positive integer
k
, the
Distance-
r
Dominating Set
problem asks if there is a D
r
DS of
G
of size at most
k
.

Distance-
r
Dominating Set
remains
{\mathtt{NP}}
-complete even on bipartite graphs or chordal graphs of diameter
2r+1

[
5
]
.
Since the work of Meir and Moon, this notion of distance domination in graphs has been extensively studied from different perspectives.
We refer readers to the survey of Henning
[
10
]
for more details.

Dominating Set Reconfiguration.

Reconfiguration of dominating sets has been well-studied in the literature from both algorithmic and graph-theoretic viewpoints.
Imagine that a token is placed on each vertex of a dominating set of a graph
G
and no vertex has more than one token.
In a reconfiguration setting for dominating sets, the following reconfiguration rules have been considered.

•

Token Sliding (
{\mathsf{TS}}
):
One can move a token to one of its unoccupied neighbors as long as the resulting token-set forms a dominating set.

•

Token Jumping (
{\mathsf{TJ}}
):
One can move a token to any unoccupied vertex as long as the resulting token-set forms a dominating set.

•

Token Addition/Removal (
{\mathsf{TAR}}(k)
):
One can either add or remove a token as long as the resulting token-set forms a dominating set of size at most some threshold
k\geq 0
.

Given a graph
G
, a fixed integer
r\geq 1
, two dominating sets
D_{s},D_{t}
of
G
, and a reconfiguration rule
{\mathsf{R}}\in\{{\mathsf{TS}},{\mathsf{TJ}},{\mathsf{TAR}}\}
,
the
Dominating Set Reconfiguration (DSR)
problem under
{\mathsf{R}}
asks if there is a sequence of dominating sets between
D_{s}
and
D_{t}
where each intermediate member is obtained from its predecessor by applying
{\mathsf{R}}
exactly once.

We briefly mention some well-known algorithmic results and refer readers to
[
16
]
for recent developments from the graph-theoretic perspective.
Haddadan et al.
[
8
]
first studied the computational complexity of
DSR
under
{\mathsf{TAR}}
and showed that the problem is
{\mathtt{PSPACE}}
-complete on planar graphs of maximum degree six, bounded bandwidth graphs, split graphs, and bipartite graphs.
On the positive side, they designed polynomial-time algorithms for solving
DSR
under
{\mathsf{TAR}}
on cographs, forests, and interval graphs.
Bonamy et al.
[
1
]
observed that the
{\mathsf{TJ}}
and
{\mathsf{TAR}}
rules are equivalent under some constraints, and therefore the above-mentioned results indeed hold for
DSR
under
{\mathsf{TJ}}
.
Bonamy et al.
[
1
]
first studied the computational complexity of
DSR
under
{\mathsf{TS}}
and showed that the hardness results of Haddadan et al.
[
8
]
hold even under
{\mathsf{TS}}
.
On the positive side, Bonamy et al.
[
1
]
designed polynomial-time algorithms for solving
DSR
under
{\mathsf{TS}}
on cographs and dually chordal graphs (which contain trees and interval graphs).
Bousquet and Joffard
[
2
]
later showed that
DSR
under
{\mathsf{TS}}
is
{\mathtt{PSPACE}}
-complete on circle graphs and can be solved in polynomial time on circular-arc graphs, answering some open questions previously asked by Bonamy et al.
[
1
]
.
Recently, Křišt’an and Svoboda
[
12
]
improved the positive results of Bonamy et al.
[
1
]
by showing polynomial-time algorithms to find a
shortest
reconfiguration sequence, if it exists, between two given dominating sets under
{\mathsf{TS}}
when the input graph is either a tree or an interval graph.
However, their techniques cannot be extended to dually chordal graphs.

A systematic study of the parameterized complexity of several reconfiguration problems, including
DSR
, was initiated by Mouawad et al.
[
15
]
.
There are two natural parameterizations: the number of tokens
k
and the length of a reconfiguration sequence
\ell
. In
[
15
]
, Mouawad et al. showed that
DSR
under
{\mathsf{TAR}}
on general graphs is
\mathtt{W[1]}
-hard parameterized by
k
and
\mathtt{W[2]}
-hard parameterized by
k+\ell
.
When parameterized by
k
on graphs excluding
K_{d,d}
as a subgraph for any constant
d
(including bounded degeneracy and nowhere dense graphs), Lokshtanov et al.
[
13
]
designed an
\mathtt{FPT}
algorithm for solving the problem.
When parameterized by
\ell
alone, it was mentioned in
[
3
]
that the problem is fixed-parameter tractable on any class where first-order model-checking is fixed-parameter tractable.
We refer readers to
[
13
,
3
]
and the references therein for more details.

Our Problem.

Similar to
DSR
, one can define
Distance-
r
Dominating Set Reconfiguration (D
r
DSR)
under
{\mathsf{R}}
for a fixed integer
r\geq 2
.
To the best of our knowledge, for
r\geq 2
, the computational complexity of
D
r
DSR
has not yet been studied.
On the other hand, from the parameterized complexity viewpoint, Siebertz
[
18
]
studied
D
r
DSR
under
{\mathsf{TAR}}
parameterized by
k
and proved that there exists a constant
r
such that the problem is
\mathtt{W[2]}
-hard on somewhere dense graphs, which are close under taking subgraphs.
On the positive side, Siebertz showed that the problem is in
\mathtt{FPT}
on nowhere dense graphs.
From the graph-theoretic viewpoint, DeVos et al.
[
6
]
introduced the
\gamma_{r}
-graph
of a graph
G
–a (reconfiguration) graph whose nodes are
minimum
distance-
r
dominating sets of
G
and edges are defined under
{\mathsf{TJ}}
–and proved several results on its realizability.
In this paper, to obtain a better understanding of the separating line between “hard” and “easy” instances of
D
r
DSR
(
r\geq 2
) on different graphs, we study the problem under
{\mathsf{TS}}
and
{\mathsf{TJ}}
from the computational complexity viewpoint.

Preliminaries

Graph Notation.

For concepts and notations not defined here, we refer readers to
[
7
]
.
Unless otherwise mentioned, throughout this paper, we consider simple, connected, undirected graphs
G
with vertex-set
V(G)
and edge-set
E(G)
.
For any pair of vertices

, the
distance
between

and

in

, denoted by
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(u,v)
, is the length of the shortest path between them.
The

of

, denoted by

, is the largest distance between any pair of vertices.
For two vertex subsets

, we use

and

to indicate

and

, respectively.
If

contains a single vertex

, we write

and

instead of

and
X+\{u\}
, respectively.
We denote by
X\Delta Y
their
symmetric difference
, i.e.,
X\Delta Y=(X-Y)+(Y-X)
.
For a subgraph
H
of
G
, we denote

as the graph obtained from
G
by deleting all vertices of
H
and their incident edges in
G
.
A
dominating set (DS)
of

is a vertex subset
D
such that for every
u\in V(G)
, there exists
v\in D
such that
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(u,v)\leq 1
.
For a fixed positive integer
r
, a
distance-
r
dominating set (D
r
DS)
of
G
is a vertex subset
D
such that for every
v\in V(G)
, there exists
v\in D
such that
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(u,v)\leq r
.
In particular, any D
1
DS is also a DS, and vice versa. Let

be the set of all vertices of distance at most
r
from

in
G
.
We say that a vertex

.
We say that a vertex subset

r
-dominated by some vertex in

.
A D

that

-dominates

. We denote by

the size of a minimum D

.

Graph Classes.

We mention some graph classes that will be considered in this paper.
A graph
G=(V,E)
is called a
split graph
if its vertex set
V
is partitioned into two subsets,
K
and
S
, which respectively induce a clique and an independent set of
G
.
For any split graph
G
, we assume that such a partition is always given and write
G=(K\uplus S,E)
.
A graph
G
is called a
chordal graph
if every cycle of
G
on four or more vertices has a chord—an edge joining two non-adjacent vertices in the cycle.
It is known that any split graph is also a chordal graph.
A
bipartite graph

G
is a graph where

can be partitioned into two disjoint vertex subsets
X
and
Y
where no edge of
G
joins two vertices in
X
or two vertices in
Y
.

G
is a
planar graph
if it can be drawn in the plane in such a way that no edges cross each other.
A
tree
is a graph having no cycles.
The
bandwidth
of a graph
G
is the minimum over all injective assignments
f:V(G)\to\mathbb{N}
of
\max_{uv\in E(G)}|f(u)-f(v)|
.

G
is called a
bounded bandwidth graph
if there exists a constant
b
such that the bandwidth of

is at most
b
.

Reconfiguration Notation.

Throughout this paper, we write “
(G,D_{s},D_{t})
under
{\mathsf{R}}
” to indicate an instance of
D
r
DSR

where
D_{s}
and
D_{t}
are two given D
r
DSs of a graph
G
, and the reconfiguration rule is
{\mathsf{R}}\in\{{\mathsf{TS}},{\mathsf{TJ}}\}
.
Imagine that a token is placed on each vertex in a D
r
DS of a graph
G
.
A
{\mathsf{TS}}
-sequence
in
G
between two D
r
DSs
D_{s}
and
D_{t}
is a sequence
\mathcal{S}=\langle D_{s}=D_{0},D_{1},\dots,D_{q}=D_{t}\rangle
such that for
i\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}
, the set
D_{i}
is a D
r
DS of
G
, and there exists a pair
x_{i},y_{i}\in V(G)
such that
D_{i}-D_{i+1}=\{x_{i}\}
,
D_{i+1}-D_{i}=\{y_{i}\}
, and
x_{i}y_{i}\in E(G)
.
A
{\mathsf{TJ}}
-sequence
in
G
is defined similarly, but without the restriction

.
For a given rule
{\mathsf{R}}\in\{{\mathsf{TS}},{\mathsf{TJ}}\}
, we say that
D_{i+1}
is obtained from

by
immediately sliding/jumping
a token from

to

under rule
{\mathsf{R}}
, and we write

.
This allows us to express
\mathcal{S}
as a sequence of token-moves:
\mathcal{S}=\langle x_{0}\overset{G}{\longrightarrow}_{{\mathsf{R}}}y_{0},\dots,x_{q-1}\overset{G}{\longrightarrow}_{{\mathsf{R}}}y_{q-1}\rangle
.
In short,
\mathcal{S}
can be viewed as an (ordered) sequence of either D

DSs or token-moves.
(Recall that we defined
\mathcal{S}
as a sequence between
D_{s}
and
D_{t}
. As a result, when regarding
\mathcal{S}
as a sequence of token-moves, we implicitly assume that the initial D

With respect to the latter viewpoint, we say that
\mathcal{S}

from

to

in

if

and finally on

\mathcal{S}
.
The

of a

-sequence is simply the number of times the rule

Polynomial-Time Algorithms

3.1
Observations

Graphs and Their Powers.

For a graph
G
and an integer
s\geq 1
, the
s^{th}
power of
G
is the graph
G^{s}
whose vertices are
V(G)
and two vertices
u,v
are adjacent in
G^{s}
if
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(u,v)\leq s
.
Observe that
D
is a D
r
DS of
G
if and only if
D
is a DS of
G^{r}
.
The following proposition is straightforward.

Proposition
0

(
\ast
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
and
{\mathcal{H}}
be two graph classes and suppose that for every
G\in{\mathcal{G}}
we have
G^{r}\in{\mathcal{H}}
for some fixed integer
r\geq 1
.
If
DSR
under
{\mathsf{TJ}}
on
{\mathcal{H}}
can be solved in polynomial time, so does
D
r
DSR
under
{\mathsf{TJ}}
on
{\mathcal{G}}
.

Dually Chordal Graphs.

We refer readers to
[
4
]
for a precise definition of this graph class.
It is known that
DSR
can be solved in quadratic time under
{\mathsf{TS}}
on connected dually chordal graphs
[
1
, Theorem 13]
.
Specifically, given an instance
(G,D_{s},D_{t})
of
DSR
under
{\mathsf{TS}}
, where
D_{s}
and
D_{t}
are two dominating sets of a connected dually chordal graph
G
, one can construct in quadratic time a
{\mathsf{TS}}
-sequence between
D_{s}
and
D_{t}
.
Since any
{\mathsf{TS}}
-sequence is also a
{\mathsf{TJ}}
-sequence, the result also applies for
{\mathsf{TJ}}
.
In other words,
DSR
under
{\mathsf{TJ}}
on connected dually chordal graphs can be solved in quadratic time.

One can construct in quadratic time a
{\mathsf{TJ}}
-sequence between
D_{s}
and
D_{t}
on disconnected dually chordal graphs
G
as follows.
Bonamy et al. also proved that one can construct in linear time a canonical minimum dominating set
C
and respectively reconfigure
D_{s}
and
D_{t}
under
{\mathsf{TS}}
into
D_{s}^{\prime}
and
D_{t}^{\prime}
such that
C\subseteq D_{s}^{\prime}\cap D_{t}^{\prime}

[
1
, Lemma 12]
.
Moreover, in a component, note that any token not in a canonical minimum dominating set can be arbitrarily moved/jumped to any other component.
As a result, one can indeed reconfigure
D_{s}
and
D_{t}
under
{\mathsf{TJ}}
such that, finally, their intersections with each component are of equal size.
Now, it remains to solve for each component independently, which can be done as described in
[
1
, Theorem 13]
.
In summary, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 0
.

DSR
under
{\mathsf{TJ}}
is quadratic-time solvable on dually chordal graphs.

It is also known that the power of any dually chordal graph is also a dually chordal graph

4
Hardness Results

We begin this section with the following remark.
It is well-known that for any computational problem in
{\mathtt{NP}}
, any of its reconfiguration variants is in
{\mathtt{PSPACE}}
(see the proof of
[
11
, Theorem 1]
).
Since
D
r
DS
is in
{\mathtt{NP}}

[
5
]
,
D
r
DSR
is in
{\mathtt{PSPACE}}
.
As a result, when proving the
{\mathtt{PSPACE}}
-completeness of
D
r
DSR
, it suffices to show a polynomial-time reduction.

4.1
Base Problems for Hardness Reduction

Nondeterministic Constraint Logic (NCL).

In an
NCL constraint graph
(or
NCL graph
for short), first introduced by Hearn and Demaine
[
9
]
, each edge has weight either
1
(red, thin) or
2
(blue, thick).
A
configuration
of an NCL graph is an orientation of its edges satisfying that the
in-weight
(i.e., the sum of all weights of edges that are directed inward) at any vertex is at least two.
Two NCL configurations of an NCL graph are
adjacent
if one can be obtained from the other by reversing the direction of exactly one edge.
Given an NCL graph and two configurations
C_{s},C_{t}
, the NCL
configuration-to-configuration (C2C)
variant asks whether there is a sequence of adjacent configurations between
C_{s}
and
C_{t}
, i.e., a sequence
\langle C_{s}=C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{p}=C_{t}\rangle
where all
C_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq p
) are NCL configurations and
C_{i}
and
C_{i+1}
(
1\leq i\leq p-1
) are adjacent.

An
NCL
And/Or
constraint graph
is an NCL graph where only two types of vertices, namely
And
vertices and
Or
vertices, are allowed.
(See
\cref
fig:NCL-def.)

•

In an NCL
And
vertex
u
(
\cref
fig:NCL-def(b)), there are three incident edges: two of which have weight
1
(red) and one has weight
2
(blue).
To direct the weight-
2
(blue) edge outward,
both
two weight-
1
(red) edges must be directed inward.
It is not required that when two weight-
1
(red) edges are directed inward, the weight-
2
(blue) edge must be directed outward.
This mimics the behavior of a logical
And
gate.

•

In an NCL
Or
vertex
v
(
\cref
fig:NCL-def(c)), there are three incident edges, each of which have weight
2
(blue).
To direct any weight-
2
(blue) edge outward,
at least one
of the other two weight-
2
(blue) edges must be directed inward.
This mimics the behavior of a logical
Or
gate.

Figure 4:
(a) A configuration of an NCL
And/Or
constraint graph, (b) NCL
And
vertex, (c) NCL
Or

For simplicity, we call the NCL C2C variant as the
NCL problem
and an NCL
And/Or
constraint graph as an
NCL graph
.
It is well-known that the NCL problem remains
{\mathtt{PSPACE}}
-complete even on NCL graphs that are both planar and bounded bandwidth
[
20
]
.
