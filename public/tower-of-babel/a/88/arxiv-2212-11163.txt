Differential forms on C ∞ C^{\infty} -ringed spaces.
Eugene Lerman

Abstract
. We construct a complex of sheaves of differential forms on a local C ∞ C^{\infty} -ringed space. The two main classes of spaces we have in mind are differential spaces in the sense of Sikorski and C ∞ C^{\infty} -schemes. Just as in the case of manifolds the construction is functorial. Consequently forms can be integrated over simplices and Stokes’ theorem holds.



1.
Introduction

This paper is one in a series of papers on differential geometry of

C^{\infty}
-ringed spaces. At the moment two other papers in the series are

[
KL
]
(which constructs flows of derivations on differential
spaces) and
[
Le
]
(which, based on the results of this paper,
constructs Cartan calculus on
C^{\infty}
-ringed spaces).

The goal of this paper is to construct a complex of sheaves of
differential forms on local
C^{\infty}
-ringed spaces. The category of
local
C^{\infty}
-ringed spaces includes manifolds, manifolds with corners,
differential spaces in the sense of Sikorski
[
Sn
]
,

C^{\infty}
-differentiable space of Navarro González and Sancho de
Salas
[
NGSS
]
, affine
C^{\infty}
-schemes of Dubuc
[
D
]
and
Kreck’s stratifolds
[
Kre
]
.

The sheaves of commutative differential graded algebras (CDGAs) that
we construct agree with ordinary sheaves of de Rham differential
forms on manifolds with corners. Our construction of differential forms is
functorial. Consequently our differential forms can be integrated over simplexes,
(
C^{\infty}
-)singular chains and compact manifolds. As a result a version of Stokes’s theorem
holds.

On the level of 1-forms our sheaves are the cotangent complexes of

C^{\infty}
-Kähler differentials studies by Joyce
[
J
]
. In the case of differential
spaces our differential forms appear to agree with the differential forms defined
by Mostow
[
Mos
]
. In the case of stratifolds our differential
forms include Kreck’s.

The construction that we carry out in the paper is fairly straightforward (the necessary background is reviewed in the next section).
Thanks to a theorem of Dubuc and Kock, given
a
C^{\infty}
-ring
\mathscr{C}
there exists a
\mathscr{C}
-module
\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}
and a universal derivation

d_{\mathscr{C}}:\mathscr{C}\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}
. This is the analogue of Kähler differentials in commutative algebra in the setting of
C^{\infty}
-rings.
Similarly to what happens in commutative algebra the differential
d_{\mathscr{C}}
extends to a derivation
d
of the exterior algebra
\Lambda^{\bullet}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}
, giving us a commutative differential graded algebra (a CDGA)
(\Lambda^{\bullet}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}},\wedge,d)
. This algebra is natural in the
C^{\infty}
-ring
\mathscr{C}
.
Consequently given a local
C^{\infty}
-ringed space
(M,\mathscr{A})
we get a presheaf
(\Lambda^{\bullet}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}},\wedge,d)
of commutative differential graded algebras, which assigns to each open set
U\subseteq M
the CDGA
(\Lambda^{\bullet}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}(U)},\wedge,d)
. That is,

(\Lambda^{\bullet}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}})(U):=\Lambda^{\bullet}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}(U)}

for all open subsets
U\subseteq M
. In general there is no reason for the presheaf
\Lambda^{\bullet}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}}
to be a sheaf. There is one notable exception: when
M
is a manifold with corners and
\mathscr{A}
is the sheaf of smooth functions
C^{\infty}_{M}
on
M
. In this case
\Lambda^{\bullet}\Omega^{1}_{C^{\infty}_{M}}
agrees with the sheaf
\bm{\Upomega}^{\bullet}_{dR,M}
of ordinary differential forms (see Lemma
5.3
).
More precisely the presheaves of CDGAs
(\Lambda^{\bullet}\Omega^{1}_{C^{\infty}_{M}},\wedge,d)
and
(\bm{\Upomega}^{\bullet}_{dR,M},\wedge,d)
agree.

For a general local
C^{\infty}
-ringed space
(M,\mathscr{A})
we sheafify the presheaf

\Lambda^{\bullet}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}}
and obtain a sheaf
\bm{\Upomega}^{\bullet}_{\mathscr{A}}
of CDGAs over
(M,\mathscr{A})
.

The standard geometric

-simplex

is a manifold with corners. It makes sense to define a

2.
Background

In this section we collect all the mathematical background we need for constructing sheaves of differential forms on
C^{\infty}
-ringed spaces.
Most, if not all, of the material is probably known to various experts. However, I don’t know of any source that has all the material in one
place.

Remark 2.1
.

In the paper a manifold is always
C^{\infty}
, Hausdorff and second
countable.

2.i.
C^{\infty}
-rings

Roughly speaking, a
C^{\infty}
-ring
is a (nonempty)
set
\mathscr{C}
together with
operations (i.e., functions)

g_{\mathscr{C}}:\mathscr{C}^{m}\to\mathscr{C}

for all
m
and all
g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{m})

such that for all

n,m\geq 0
, all
g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
and all

f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

(2.1)

({g\circ(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})})_{\mathscr{C}}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})=g_{\mathscr{C}}({(f_{1})}_{\mathscr{C}}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}),\ldots,{(f_{m})}_{\mathscr{C}}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}))

for all
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in\mathscr{C}^{n}
.

A formal definition of a
C^{\infty}
-ring is that of set-valued
product-preserving functor from the category
\mathsf{Euc}
of Euclidean (also
known as Cartesian) spaces. This definition requires a certain amount
of unpacking. We start by defining the category
\mathsf{Euc}
.

Definition 2.2

(The category
\mathsf{Euc}
of Euclidean spaces)
.

The objects of the category
\mathsf{Euc}
are the coordinate vector spaces

\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 0
, thought of as
C^{\infty}
manifolds. The morphisms are

C^{\infty}
maps.

We note that all objects of
\mathsf{Euc}
are finite powers of one object:

\mathbb{R}^{n}=(\mathbb{R}^{1})^{n}
for all
n\geq 0
.

Definition 2.3
.

A
C^{\infty}
-ring

{\mathcal{C}}
is a functor

{\mathcal{C}}:\mathsf{Euc}\to\mathsf{Set}

from the category
\mathsf{Euc}
of Euclidean spaces to the category
\mathsf{Set}
of
sets that
preserves finite products
.

A
morphism
of
C^{\infty}
-rings from a
C^{\infty}
-ring
{\mathcal{C}}
to a

C^{\infty}
-ring
{\mathcal{B}}
is a natural transformation
{\mathcal{C}}\Rightarrow{\mathcal{B}}
.

Remark 2.4
.

We unpack
Definition
2.3
.

(2.4.i)

For any
n\geq 0
, the set
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is an
n
-fold product of
the set
\mathscr{C}:={\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{1})
, i.e.,
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\mathscr{C}^{n}
. Moreover
the canonical projection
\pr_{i}:\mathscr{C}\,^{n}\to\mathscr{C}

on the
i
th factor is
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\xrightarrow{x_{i}}\mathbb{R})
, where
x_{i}

is the
i
th coordinate function.

(2.4.ii)

For any smooth function
g=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
we
have a map of sets

{\mathcal{C}}(g):{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\mathscr{C}^{n}\to\mathscr{C}^{m}={\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{m})

with

\pr_{j}\circ\,{\mathcal{C}}(g)={\mathcal{C}}(g_{j}).

(2.4.iii)

For any triple of smooth functions
\mathbb{R}^{n}\xrightarrow{g}\mathbb{R}^{m}\xrightarrow{f}\mathbb{R}
we have three maps of sets
{\mathcal{C}}(g)
,
{\mathcal{C}}(f)
,

{\mathcal{C}}(f\circ g)
with

{\mathcal{C}}(f)\circ{\mathcal{C}}(g)={\mathcal{C}}(f\circ g).

(2.4.iv)

{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{0})
is a single point set
*
. This is because by definition
product-preserving functors take terminal objects to terminal objects.

It follows that
\mathscr{C}={\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{1})
is a set together with, for every

n\geq 0
, a collection of
n
-ary operations indexed by the set of smooth
functions
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Conversely given a set
\mathscr{C}
with a family of operations indexed by
smooth functions there is a unique product-preserving functor
{\mathcal{C}}:\mathsf{Euc}\to\mathsf{Set}
with
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{1})=\mathscr{C}
.

Remark 2.5
.

Throughout the paper we will treat
C^{\infty}
-rings as sets with
operations and morphisms of
C^{\infty}
-rings as operation-preserving maps
(rather than as functors and natural transformations). This treatment
is standard, cf.
[
MR
]
or
[
J
]
.

From this point of view (
C^{\infty}
-rings are sets with operations) a
morphism
of
C^{\infty}
-rings
\varphi:\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{D}
is a map of sets

\varphi
which preserves all the operations: for any
n
, any
h\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and any
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\in\mathscr{C}

\varphi(h_{\mathscr{C}}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})=h_{\mathscr{D}}(\varphi(c_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(c_{n})).

Remark 2.6
.

Any
C^{\infty}
-ring has an underlying
\mathbb{R}
-algebra. This can be
seen as follows. The category
\mathsf{Euc}
contains the subcategory
\mathsf{Euc}_{poly}

with the same objects whose morphism are polynomial maps. A
product-preserving functor from
\mathsf{Euc}_{poly}
to
\mathsf{Set}
is an

\mathbb{R}
-algebra. Clearly any product preserving functor
{\mathcal{C}}:\mathsf{Euc}\to\mathsf{Set}
restricts to a product preserving functor from
\mathsf{Euc}_{poly}
to
\mathsf{Set}
.

The structure of an
\mathbb{R}
-algebra on a
C^{\infty}
-ring
\mathscr{C}
can also be
seen on the level of sets and operations.
Note that
\mathbb{R}^{0}=\{0\}
,
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{0})=\mathbb{R}
and that for any set
\mathscr{C}

the zeroth power
\mathscr{C}^{0}
is a 1-point set
*
. Consequently for
a
C^{\infty}
-ring
\mathscr{C}
,
n=0
, and
\lambda\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{0})=\mathbb{R}

the corresponding 0-ary operation is a function
\lambda_{\mathscr{C}}:*\to\mathscr{C}
, which we identify with an element of
\mathscr{C}
. By abuse of
notation we denote this element by
\lambda_{\mathscr{C}}
. This gives us a
map
\mathbb{R}\to\mathscr{C}
,
\lambda\mapsto\lambda_{\mathscr{C}}
. One can show that
unless
\mathscr{C}
consists of one element (i.e.,
\mathscr{C}
is the zero
C^{\infty}
-ring)
the map is injective. Together with the operations
+:\mathscr{C}^{2}\to\mathscr{C}
and
\cdot:\mathscr{C}^{2}\to\mathscr{C}
, which correspond to the functions

h(x,y)=x+y
and
g(x,y)=xy
, respectively, the 0-ary operations make any

C^{\infty}
-ring into a unital
\mathbb{R}
-algebra. We will not notationally
distinguished between a
C^{\infty}
-ring and the corresponding (underlying)
\mathbb{R}
-algebra.

Notation 2.7
.

C^{\infty}
-rings and their morphisms form a category that we denote by

C^{\infty}\mathsf{Ring}
.

Example 2.8
.

Let
M
be a
C^{\infty}
-manifold and
C^{\infty}(M)
the set of
smooth (real-valued) functions. Then

.

The real line

is a

-ring: the

for all

and all

are functions

Example 2.10
.

Let

There are several reasons why Definition
2.21
is
reasonable. We list three:

(i)
As we have seen above, if
M
is a manifold
and

C^{\infty}
-ring

(ii)
Given a
C^{\infty}
-ring
\mathscr{C}
and an
\mathscr{C}
-module
\mathscr{M}
, the product
\mathscr{C}\times\mathscr{M}
has the structure of a

C^{\infty}
-ring. Namely, for any
n>0
, any

and any

-algebra underlying a

-ring
comes from the function

\begin{split}(a_{1},m_{1})\cdot(a_{2},m_{2})&=f_{\mathscr{C}\times\mathscr{M}}((a_{1},m_{1}),(a_{2},m_{2}))\\
&=(f_{\mathscr{C}}(a_{1},a_{2}),\sum_{i=1}^{2}(\partial_{i}f)_{\mathscr{C}}(a_{1},a_{2})\cdot m_{i})=(a_{1}a_{2},a_{2}m_{1}+a_{1}m_{2}).\end{split}

In particular

(0,m_{1})\cdot(0,m_{2})=(0\cdot 0,0m_{1}+0m_{2})=(0,0).

Therefore, just as in the case of commutative rings and commutative

-algebras, modules over

(2.2)

Remark 2.25

2.ii.
Local

-ringed spaces

Our next step is to define local

-ringed spaces and (graded) modules over

-ringed spaces. We then discuss in more detail two

-ringed spaces: differential spaces in

Remark 2.27
.

There are two ways to view
sheaves on a space

with values in an algebraic category
\mathsf{C}
.
We can view them as functors
{\mathsf{Open}(M)}^{\mbox{\sf{\tiny{op}}}}\to\mathsf{C}
, where
\mathsf{Open}(M)

is the poset of open subsets of

ordered by inclusion. Or we can
view them as “
\mathsf{C}

sheaves on

maps of set-valued sheaves subject a number of commutative diagrams.
We will go back and forth between the two points of view.

Definition 2.28
.

An
\mathbb{R}
-point
of a
C^{\infty}
-ring
\mathscr{C}
is a nonzero homomorphism

p:\mathscr{C}\to\mathbb{R}
of
C^{\infty}
-rings.

Example 2.29
.

Let
M
be a manifold and
\mathscr{C}=C^{\infty}(M)
the

-ring of smooth

. Then for any point

is an
\mathbb{R}
-point of the
C^{\infty}
-ring
C^{\infty}(M)
.

Conversely, given an
\mathbb{R}
-point

there is a point

so that

. This fact is known as “Milnor’s exercise.” It is a
theorem of Pursell
[
Pu
]
.

Thus for a manifold

the set of

-points of

is (in bijection with) the set of ordinary

.

Remark 2.30
.

The zero

-ring (which corresponds to the functor

that sends every object to a 1-point set and whose

-points.

Remark 2.31
.

The kernel of an

-point

Remark 2.32
.

There are
C^{\infty}
-rings
\mathscr{C}
containing maximal ideals

\mathfrak{M}
with the property that the field
\mathscr{C}/\mathfrak{M}
is not
isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}
. Here is an example. Fix
n>0
. The set of
compactly supported functions
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is an ideal in the

C^{\infty}
-ring
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Let
\mathfrak{M}
be a maximal ideal
containing

(which exists by Zorn’s lemma). We will
argue that the quotient

has no
\mathbb{R}
-points

.

Consider the sequence of projections
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\xrightarrow{\pi}C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})/C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\xrightarrow{\pr}C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})/\mathfrak{M}

If
\varphi:C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})/\mathfrak{M}\to\mathbb{R}
is an

so is the composite
\varphi\circ\pr\circ\pi:C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathbb{R}

so that
\varphi\circ\pr\circ\pi
is the evaluation at
x
:

ev_{x}=\varphi\circ\pr\circ\pi.

But then every compactly supported function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
vanishes at

,
which is impossible.

Definition 2.33
.

A
C^{\infty}
-ring is

if it has exactly one

-point.

Remark 2.34
.

Our terminology in Definition

]

]
. Moerdijk and Reyes call our local

.

Let

Definition 2.36
.

A

-ringed space

.

Remark 2.38
.

A reader unfamiliar with ringed spaces of any kind may wonder why
anyone would single out local ringed spaces. There is a variety of
reasons. One of them is that if
(M,\mathscr{A})
is a local

C^{\infty}
-ringed space, then for any open set
U\subset M
a section

s\in\mathscr{A}(U)
gives rise to a real-valued function
f_{s}
as follows:
given
x\in U
, there is a unique
\mathbb{R}
-point
\hat{x}:\mathscr{A}_{x}\to\mathbb{R}
. We then define

f_{s}(x):=\hat{x}(s_{x}),

where, as before,
s_{x}
is the germ of
s
at
x
. For differential
spaces (which are defined below in Definition
2.48
), the function
f_{s}
is just
s
. But in general the sheaf
\mathscr{A}
need
not be a sheaf of
C^{\infty}
-rings of
functions
, and locality gives us a
map from local sections to (local) functions.
See the proof of Theorem
B.1
for an example of the use
of this fact.

If manifolds were the only examples of local
C^{\infty}
-ringed spaces,
this paper would not have much of a point. Fortunately there are two
broad classes of examples: affine
C^{\infty}
-schemes of Dubuc and
differential spaces in the sense of Sikorski. They are discussed
below, but not immediately.

When discussing derivations of
C^{\infty}
-rings we’ll need the
definition of a point-determined

-ring, which we presently
recall.

Definition 2.39
.

A
C^{\infty}
-ring

\mathbb{R}
-points separate
elements of
\mathscr{C}
. That is, if

and

Example 2.40
.

Let

be a topological space. Then the

-ring

of continuous functions on

.

Remark 2.41
.

In order to define maps (or, if you prefer, morphisms) between local
C^{\infty}
-ringed
spaces we need to recall that (pre)sheaves can be pushed forward by
continuous maps.
Namely, if
\mathscr{S}
is a presheaf on a space
X
and
f:X\to Y
is a
continuous map, then there is a presheaf
f_{*}\mathscr{S}
on
Y
which is
defined by

f_{*}\mathscr{S}(U):=\mathscr{S}(f^{-1}(U))

for every open set
U\subset Y
. It is not hard to check that if

\mathscr{S}
is a sheaf then the pushforward presheaf
f_{*}\mathscr{S}
is also a
sheaf.

The following example is meant to motivate the definition of a map
between two
C^{\infty}
-ringed spaces.

Example 2.42
.

Consider a smooth map
f:M\to N

between two manifolds. It induces a map
f_{\#}:C^{\infty}(N)\to C^{\infty}(M)

between
C^{\infty}
-rings;
f_{\#}
is given by

f_{\#}h:=h\circ f

for all
h\in C^{\infty}(M)
; i.e.,
f_{\#}
is just a pullback map. There
is also more: for every open set
U\subset N
we have a map of

C^{\infty}
-rings

f_{\#,U}:C^{\infty}(U)\to C^{\infty}(f^{-1}(U)),\qquad f_{\#}(h)=h\circ f.

More precisely
C^{\infty}(U)=C^{\infty}_{N}(U)
and
C^{\infty}(f^{-1}(U))=C^{\infty}_{M}(f^{-1}(U))=(f_{*}C^{\infty}_{M})(U)
. Moreover, the maps

\{f_{\#,U}\}_{U\subset N}
are compatible with restrictions, and therefore
pullback by
f
defines a map of sheaves

f_{\#}:C^{\infty}_{N}\to f_{*}C^{\infty}_{M}.

Definition 2.43
.

A
morphism
(or a
map
) of local
C^{\infty}
-ringed spaces from
(X,\mathscr{O}_{X})
to

(Y,\mathscr{O}_{Y})
is a pair
(f,f_{\#})
where
f:X\to Y
is continuous and

f_{\#}:\mathscr{O}_{Y}\to f_{*}\mathscr{O}_{X}
is a map of sheaves of
C^{\infty}
-rings on the space
Y
.

Remark 2.44
.

Note that the map
(f,f_{\#}):(X,\mathscr{O}_{X})\to(Y,\mathscr{O}_{Y})
of local

C^{\infty}
-ringed spaces induces, for every point
x\in X
, a map of
stalks
f_{x}:\mathscr{O}_{Y,f(x)}\to\mathscr{O}_{X,x}

In algebraic geometry one defines a morphism of locally ringed
spaces to be a pair
(f,f_{\#}):(X,\mathscr{O}_{X})\to(Y,\mathscr{O}_{Y})
so that
the induced maps on stalks

are all local: they are required to
take the unique maximal ideal to the unique maximal ideal. For
local
C^{\infty}
-ringed spaces this is unnecessary because any map of
local
C^{\infty}
-rings automatically preserves
\mathbb{R}
-points.

Remark 2.45
.

In order to define the composition of two maps of
C^{\infty}
-ringed spaces
we need to recall that pushing (pre)sheaves forward along a continuous
map
f:X\to Y
is actually a functor from the category
\mathsf{Psh}_{X}
of
presheaves on
X
to the category
\mathsf{Psh}_{Y}
of presheaves on
Y
: given
a map of presheaves
\varphi:\mathscr{O}\to\mathscr{O}^{\prime}
in
\mathsf{Psh}_{X}
we get a map
of presheaves
f_{*}(\varphi):f_{*}\mathscr{O}\to f_{*}\mathscr{O}
. Namely for an
open set
U\subset Y
the
U
-component
\left(f_{*}(\varphi)\right)_{U}:f_{*}\mathscr{O}(U)\to f_{*}\mathscr{O}^{\prime}(U)

of

is, by definition,

\varphi_{f^{-1}(U)}:\mathscr{O}(f^{-1}(U))\to\mathscr{O}^{\prime}(f^{-1}(U)).

Definition 2.46
.

Given two maps
(f,f_{\#}):(X,\mathscr{O}_{X})\to(Y,\mathscr{O}_{Y})
and
(g,g_{\#}):(Y,\mathscr{O}_{Y})\to(Z,\mathscr{O}_{Z})
of local
C^{\infty}
-ringed spaces we define their composite to be

(g\circ f,(g\circ f)_{\#}):(X,\mathscr{O}_{X})\to(Z,\mathscr{O}_{Z})
, where

(g\circ f)_{\#}:=(g_{*}(f_{*}\mathscr{O}_{x})\xleftarrow{g_{*}(f_{\#})}g_{*}\mathscr{O}_{Y})\circ(g_{*}\mathscr{O}_{Y}\xleftarrow{g_{\#}}\mathscr{O}_{Z}).

Here we used Remark
2.45
and the fact that
(g\circ f)_{*}=g_{*}\circ f_{*}

Strictly speaking the following definition is a proposition, which we won’t prove.

Definition 2.47

(The category
\mathsf{LC^{\infty}RS}
)
.

Local
C^{\infty}
-ringed spaces and their morphisms form a category which
we denote by
\mathsf{LC^{\infty}RS}
.

2.iii.
Differential spaces

We next review the definition and some properties of a class of spaces that give rise to local
C^{\infty}
-ringed spaces.
These are differential spaces in the sense of Sikorski
[
Si
]
. Traditionally the work on differential spaces avoids explicit mention of
C^{\infty}
-rings and of sheaves.
This has its advantages and its problems.

Definition 2.48

(Differential space in the sense of Sikorski)
.

A
differential space
is a pair
(M,\mathscr{F})
where
M
is a
topological space and
\mathscr{F}
is a (nonempty) set of real valued functions on
M
so that

(2.48.i)

The topology on
M
is the smallest topology making all
functions in
\mathscr{F}
continuous;

(2.48.ii)

For any natural number
k
, for any
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in\mathscr{F}
and any
smooth function
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
the composite
f\circ(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
is in
\mathscr{F}
;

(2.48.iii)

If
g:M\to\mathbb{R}
is a function with the property that for any
x\in M
there is an
open neighborhood
U
of
x
and
f\in\mathscr{F}
(
f
depends on
x
)
so that
g|_{U}=f|_{U}
then
g\in\mathscr{F}
.

The whole set
\mathscr{F}
is called a
differential structure
on
M
.

Remark 2.49
.

•

One thinks of the family of functions
\mathscr{F}
on a differential
space
(M,\mathscr{F})
as the set of all abstract smooth functions on

M
.

•

Condition (
2.48.ii
) of
Definition
2.48
says that the smooth structure
\mathscr{F}
is a

C^{\infty}
-ring: for any
n>0
,

a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathscr{F}
and
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the composite

f\circ(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}):M\to\mathbb{R}
is again in
\mathscr{F}
by
(
2.48.ii
). Thus setting

f_{\mathscr{F}}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}):=f\circ(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})

for
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathscr{F}
makes
\mathscr{F}
into a
C^{\infty}
-ring.

(If
n=0
, then
\lambda\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{0})=\mathbb{R}
is a constant and
\lambda_{\mathscr{F}}:M\to\mathbb{R}
is
defined to be the constant function taking value
\lambda
at all points of
M
.
Note that all constant functions have to be in
\mathscr{F}
. This is because
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1})
has constant functions,
and for any constant function
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and any
a\in\mathscr{F}
, the function
f_{\mathscr{F}}(a)=f\circ a
is constant.)

•

Condition (
2.48.iii
)
is a condition on the sheaf of
functions on
M
generated by
\mathscr{F}
. See Remark
2.56
.
It is often phrased as: “if a function
g:M\to\mathbb{R}
is locally
smooth then it is smooth.”

•

Condition (
2.48.i
) is equivalent to the existence of bump
functions. See Appendix
A
.

Definition 2.50
.

Given a differential space
(M,\mathscr{F})
we refer to the
C^{\infty}
-ring

\mathscr{F}
as a
differential structure
on the set
M
.

Note that by (
2.48.i
) the topology on
M
is uniquely
determined by the differential structure
\mathscr{F}
.

Example 2.51
.

Let
M
be a smooth manifold
Then the pair
(M,C^{\infty}(M))
, where
C^{\infty}(M)
is the set of

C^{\infty}
functions, is a differential space.

The only hard thing to check is that
the topology on
M
is the smallest among the topologies for which
smooth functions are continuous. But this follows from a theorem of
Whitney: any closed subset of
M
is the zero set of some smooth
function (all our manifolds are Hausdorff and second
countable). See, for example,
[
Lee
, Theorem 2.29]
. Hence if

U\subset M
is open then there is a smooth function
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
so
that
U=\{x\in M\mid f(x)\not=0\}
.

Example 2.52
.

Let
M
be a smooth manifold Then the pair
(M,C^{0}(M))
, where

C^{0}(M)
is the set of continuous functions, is a differential
space.

Remark 2.53
.

If
(M,\mathscr{F})
is a differential space then the
C^{\infty}
-ring
\mathscr{F}
is
point-determined. This is because a function on
M
is non-zero only if there is a
point at which it takes a non-zero value.

Every differential space is a local
C^{\infty}
-ringed space.
Here are the details.

Definition 2.54

(The structure sheaf
\mathscr{F}_{M}
of a differential space

(M,\mathscr{F})
)
.

Let
(M,\mathscr{F})
be a differential space. Define a presheaf
{\mathcal{P}}

of
C^{\infty}
-rings on
M
as follows: for an open set
U\subset M
let

{\mathcal{P}}(U):=\{f|_{U}\mid f\in\mathscr{F}\}

and let the structure maps of
{\mathcal{P}}
be the restrictions of functions.
Define the
structure sheaf

\mathscr{F}_{M}
of the differential space
(M,\mathscr{F})
to be the
sheafification of
{\mathcal{P}}
.

The structure sheaf
\mathscr{F}_{M}
has a concrete description:

Lemma 2.55
.

Let
(M,\mathscr{F})
be a differential
space,
{\mathcal{P}}
the presheaf on
M
defined by

{\mathcal{P}}(U):=\{f|_{U}\mid f\in\mathscr{F}\}

as in Definition
2.54
.
For an open subset

U\subseteq M
let

\begin{split}\mathscr{F}_{M}(U):=&\{f:U\to\mathbb{R}\mid\textrm{ for any }x\in U\textrm{ there is
an open neighborhood }V\\
&\textrm{ of }x\textrm{ in }U\textrm{ and }g\in C^{\infty}(M)\textrm{ with }g|_{V}=f|_{V}\}.\end{split}

Define the restriction maps
\rho^{U}_{V}:\mathscr{F}_{M}(U)\to\mathscr{F}_{M}(V)
to be the restrictions of functions:

\rho^{U}_{V}(h):=h|_{V}.

Then
\mathscr{F}_{M}:=\{\mathscr{F}_{M}(U)\}_{U\in\mathsf{Open}(M)}
is a sheaf. Moreover
\mathscr{F}_{M}
is a sheafification of the presheaf
{\mathcal{P}}
.

Proof of Lemma
2.55
.

It is easy to see that the restriction maps
\rho^{U}_{V}:\mathscr{F}_{M}(U)\to\mathscr{F}_{M}(V)
are well-defined. Consequently
\mathscr{F}_{M}
is a presheaf. Moreover
{\mathcal{P}}(U)\subset\mathscr{F}_{M}(U)
for all opens
U\in\mathsf{Open}(M)
. Furthermore the presheaves
\mathscr{F}_{M}
and
{\mathcal{P}}
have the same
stalks. So to prove that
\mathscr{F}_{M}
is a sheafification of
{\mathcal{P}}
it is enough to
check that
\mathscr{F}_{M}
is a sheaf. This is not hard.
To finish the proof one checks that
\mathscr{F}_{M}
is a sheaf of

C^{\infty}
-rings.
∎

Remark 2.56
.

We now see what condition (
2.48.iii
) of
Definition
2.48
is saying. It says that the set of functions

\mathscr{F}
on a differential space
(M,\mathscr{F})
is the
C^{\infty}
-ring of
global sections of the sheaf
\mathscr{F}_{M}
induced on
M
by
\mathscr{F}

and that we don’t get any more global sections.

Remark 2.57
.

Note that for an open subset
U
of
M
a function
f
is in

\mathscr{F}_{M}(U)
if and only if there is an open cover
\{U_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in A}
of
U
and a set

Lemma 2.58

Let

be a differential space and

the induced sheaf
of

-rings. Then the stalks of

-ringed space.

Proof.

Let

\mathscr{F}_{M}
at

Recall that
\mathscr{F}_{M,x}
is the set of equivalence classes of pairs

We denote an element of

that the function representing the germ is defined on a neighborhood

We have a well-defined evaluation map

Since the evaluation map is onto, its kernel

is a maximal ideal

Suppose

. Choose a smooth function

It follows that

2.iv.

-schemes

We next discuss

-schemes. The definition is
is due to Dubuc

There is an evident global sections functor

-ring

Then

[

Definition 2.73
.

An affine

-scheme

-ringed space isomorphic to

-ring

.

For any manifold

-ringed space

In particular the real and complex projective spaces

Unlike the spectrum functor in algebraic geometry

For example

Remark 2.76
.

The relation between
C^{\infty}
-schemes and differential
spaces is a bit messy. For a
C^{\infty}
-scheme to be a differential
space its structure sheaf must be a sheaf of functions. In
particular it can have no nilpotents. As as result
many (most?)
C^{\infty}
-schemes are not differential spaces.

On the other hand, given a differential space
(M,\mathscr{F})
any point

gives rise to the
\mathbb{R}
-point

evaluation at

. However there are differential spaces where the
set of
\mathbb{R}
-points is bigger than their set of (ordinary) points,
see
[
CS
]
.

Worse yet, a differential space may have too many
\mathbb{R}
-points. Here
is an extreme example. Take any set
M
. Give it the trivial topology.
The set
\mathscr{F}
of constant
\mathbb{R}
-valued functions on

is a
differential structure. As a

-ring
\mathscr{F}
is isomorphic to

and consequently has only one
\mathbb{R}
-point.

In other words, while the topology on the differential space
(M,\mathscr{F})
is uniquely determined by its differential structure

the set

itself is not uniquely determined by
\mathscr{F}
. Compare
this with affine
C^{\infty}
-schemes, where the set of
points of a local
C^{\infty}
-ringed space

is uniquely determined
by the
C^{\infty}
-ring
\mathscr{C}
.

2.v.
Graded objects

Remark 2.77
.

There are two conventions in the literature regarding (
\mathbb{N}
- or
\mathbb{Z}
-)
graded objects in an abelian category: they can be viewed either as
direct sums or as sequences of objects. Kamps and Porter

[
KP
, p. 207]
, for instance, use the second convention.

Depending on the category one is working in the choice of the
convention may or may not matter. For example, a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded real
Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
can be viewed as a direct sum

\mathfrak{g}=\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\,\mathfrak{g}_{n}
together with an
\mathbb{R}
-bilinear map
(the bracket)
\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
satisfying the appropriate
conditions. It can equivalently be viewed as a sequence of vector
spaces
\{\mathfrak{g}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
together with a family of linear maps

\{\mathfrak{g}_{n}\otimes\mathfrak{g}_{m}\to\mathfrak{g}_{n+m}\}_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}}
(again, subject to
conditions). So for graded
Lie algebras the two conventions are equivalent.

However, in the case of graded sheaves of modules, the two approaches are
not equivalent: see Remark
2.89
.
Since we will need the (pre-)sheaf versions of various graded objects,
we use the second convention throughout: a graded object is a sequence
of objects. Since graded derivations of non-negatively graded algebras may have negative degrees,
our graded objects are all graded by the integers
\mathbb{Z}
. Note that any algebra
graded by the natural numbers
\mathbb{N}
can be viewed as a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded
object by placing zeros in negative degrees.

The following definition is a variation on the conventional definition of
a commutative graded algebra.

Definition 2.78
.

A
commutative graded algebra
(CGA) over a

C^{\infty}
-ring
\mathscr{C}
is a sequence of
\mathscr{C}
-modules
\{B^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}

together with a sequence of
\mathscr{C}
-bilinear maps

\mathscr{C}
-linear maps

the two) so that

is associative and graded
commutative. That is for all
k,\ell,m\in\mathbb{Z}
and all

,

b\in B^{\ell}
and

(2.78.i)

(a\wedge b)\wedge c=a\wedge(b\wedge c)
and

(2.78.ii)

b\wedge a=(-1)^{k\ell}a\wedge b
(Koszul sign convention).

We denote such a CGA by
(B^{\bullet},\wedge)
. We write
b\in B^{\bullet}
if
b\in B^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.
We refer to elements of

as

Remark 2.89
.

Since the category of modules over a
C^{\infty}
-ring has coproducts so
does the category of presheaves of modules over a fixed local

C^{\infty}
-ringed space
(M,\mathscr{A})
. Moreover coproducts of presheaves
are computed object-wise. Consequently given a sequence

\{\mathscr{S}^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
of presheaves of
\mathscr{A}
-modules their coproduct

\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathscr{S}_{n}
is defined by

\left(\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathscr{S}^{n}\right)(U)=\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathscr{S}^{n}(U),

for all open sets
U\subset M
. Here on the right the direct sum is
taken in the category of
\mathscr{A}(U)
-modules. Since the sheafification
functor
\mathsf{sh}:\mathsf{Psh}_{M}\to\mathsf{Sh}_{M}
from presheaves to sheaves is left
adjoint to inclusion functor
i:\mathsf{Sh}_{M}\hookrightarrow\mathsf{Psh}_{M}
, the
functor
\mathsf{sh}
preserves colimits and, in particular, coproducts. Therefore if

\{\mathscr{S}^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a sequence of sheaves, then their coproduct (“direct sum”)
in
\mathsf{Sh}_{M}
exists and equals
\mathsf{sh}(\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathscr{S}^{n})
. In
particular for an open set
U\subset M
, the
\mathscr{A}(U)
-module

\mathsf{sh}(\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathscr{S}^{n})(U)
is, in general, different from

\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathscr{S}^{n}(U)
. See Example
2.90

below. Of course
\mathsf{sh}(\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathscr{S}^{n})
(with the
appropriate structure maps)
is
a coproduct in
\mathsf{Sh}_{M}
, so one
can view
\mathbb{Z}
-graded objects in
\mathsf{Sh}_{M}
as
\mathbb{Z}
-indexed coproducts.
One can even make sense of a map

f:\mathsf{sh}(\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathscr{S}^{n})\to\mathsf{sh}(\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathscr{S}^{\prime n})
as having a degree
k
: we require that there are maps

f^{m}:\mathscr{S}^{m}\to{\mathscr{S}^{\prime}}^{m+k}
of sheaves of modules making the diagrams

\textstyle{\mathsf{sh}(\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathscr{S}^{n})}
\textstyle{\mathsf{sh}(\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}{\mathscr{S}^{\prime}}^{n})}
\textstyle{\mathscr{S}^{m}}
\textstyle{{\mathscr{S}^{\prime}}^{m+k}}
\scriptstyle{f}
\scriptstyle{\imath^{m}}
\scriptstyle{{\imath^{\prime}}^{m+k}}
\scriptstyle{f^{m}}

commute for all
m
(here
\{\imath^{m}:\mathscr{S}^{m}\to\mathsf{sh}(\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathscr{S}^{n})\}_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
\{{\imath^{\prime}}^{m}:{\mathscr{S}^{\prime}}^{m}\to\mathsf{sh}(\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}{\mathscr{S}^{\prime}}^{n})\}_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}
are the structure maps of the coproducts).
However it is not clear what the value of
the sheaf
\mathsf{sh}(\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathscr{S}^{n})
on some open subset

U\subset M
actually is in concrete situations. This is why we use
Definition
2.88
.

Example 2.90
.

We construct an example
of a
\mathbb{Z}
-indexed collection of sheaves on a manifold so that their
direct sum (as a presheaf) is not a sheaf.

Let
B^{n}
denote the open
n
-ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of radius 1 centered at
0. Let
M=\bigsqcup_{n\geq 0}B^{n}
, the coproduct of the balls
B^{n}
’s. The
space
M
is a second countable Hausdorff manifold of unbounded
dimension. Let
\bm{\Upomega}^{k}_{dR,M}
denote the sheaf of ordinary
differential
k
-forms on
M
(with
\bm{\Upomega}^{k}_{dR,M}\equiv 0
for
k<0
).
Form the direct sum (in the category of presheaves)

\bm{\Upomega}^{\bullet}_{dR,M}:=\oplus_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\bm{\Upomega}^{k}_{dR,M}.

This presheaf
is not a sheaf
. Here is one way to see it: for
each
n
pick a volume form
\alpha_{n}\in\bm{\Upomega}_{dR}^{n}(B^{n})
. The
collection of open balls
\{B^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is an open cover of
M

and all the elements of the cover are mutually disjoint. If

\oplus_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\bm{\Upomega}^{k}_{dR,M}
were a sheaf, we would have an
element
\alpha\in\oplus_{k\geq 0}\bm{\Upomega}^{k}_{dR,M}
so that

\alpha|_{B^{n}}=\alpha_{n}
for all
n
. But any element of the direct
sum has a bounded degree, so no such
\alpha
can exist. Therefore
the direct sum
\oplus_{k\geq 0}\bm{\Upomega}^{k}_{dR,M}
of sheaves
(computed in the category
\mathsf{Psh}_{M}
of presheaves!)
is not a sheaf.

3.
Kähler differentials of
C^{\infty}
-rings

In
this section we recall the construction of the
C^{\infty}
-ring analogue of
Kähler differentials of commutative algebra.

Definition 3.1
.

A
module of
C^{\infty}
-Kähler
differentials
over a
C^{\infty}
-ring
\mathscr{C}
is an
\mathscr{C}
-module
\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}

together with a
C^{\infty}
-derivation
d_{\mathscr{C}}:\mathscr{C}\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}
, called the
universal derivation
, with the following property: for any
\mathscr{C}
-module
\mathscr{N}
and any derivation

X:\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{N}
there exists a unique map of modules
\varphi_{X}:\Omega^{1}_{A}\to\mathscr{N}
with

\varphi_{X}\circ d_{\mathscr{C}}=X.

Remark 3.2
.

The module of
C^{\infty}
-Kähler differentials is not be confused with
the module of ordinary algebraic Kähler differentials, since the
two modules are quite different. For example Gómez
[
G
]

shows that the cardinality of a set of generators of the module of algebraic Kähler
differentials
\Omega^{1}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),alg}
of
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is at
least the cardinality of the reals. Compare this with the fact that
the module of
C^{\infty}
-Kḧaler differentials
\Omega^{1}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

is isomorphic, as a
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
-module to the module of de Rham
1-forms
\bm{\Upomega}^{1}_{dR}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
— see Example
3.6
, which
has rank
n
. Note
that
\Omega^{1}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\simeq\bm{\Upomega}^{1}_{dR}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a free

C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
-module.

Remark 3.3
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a
C^{\infty}
-ring.
The universal property of the derivation
d:\mathscr{C}\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}
says
that for any
\mathscr{C}
-module
\mathscr{M}
, the map of modules

d^{*}:\Hom(\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}},\mathscr{M})\to\cDer(\mathscr{C},\mathscr{M}),\quad d^{*}(\varphi):=\varphi\circ d

is an isomorphism (of
\mathscr{C}
-modules).

Consider now the special case of
\mathscr{M}=\mathscr{C}
. Denote the inverse of

d^{*}
by
\imath
. In this notation, for any derivation
v\in\cDer(\mathscr{C})
there is a unique map of
\mathscr{C}
-module

\imath_{v}:\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}\to\mathscr{C}

so that

\imath_{v}(d_{\mathscr{C}}c)=v(c)

for all
c\in\mathscr{C}
. We think of
\imath_{v}\alpha
as the
contraction
of a derivation
v
and a differential
\alpha\in\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}
. We then have a canonical map

\psi:\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}\to\Hom(\cDer(\mathscr{C}),\mathscr{C})

with

\psi(\alpha)v=\imath_{v}\alpha

for all
v
and
\alpha
. In the case where
M
is a manifold and

\mathscr{C}=C^{\infty}(M)
, the space of derivations
\cDer(C^{\infty}(M)
is the
space of vector fields on
M
and can be identified with the space

\Gamma(TM)
of sections of the tangent bundle. On the other hand,
the space
\Omega^{1}_{C^{\infty}(M)}
of
C^{\infty}
-Kähler differentials is
the space of ordinary 1-forms
\bm{\Upomega}^{1}_{dR}(M)=\Gamma(T^{*}M)
(cf. Remark
5.11
below) and the map
\psi
is then the map

\psi:\Gamma(T^{*}M)\to\Hom(\Gamma(TM),C^{\infty}(M)),\quad\psi(\alpha)(v)=\imath_{v}\alpha,

which is an isomorphism of
C^{\infty}(M)
-modules. This is because
for a vector bundle
E\to M
there is a canonical isomorphism

\Gamma(E^{*})\to\Hom_{C^{\infty}(M)}(\Gamma(E),C^{\infty}(M))
of

C^{\infty}(M)
-modules (here as before
\Gamma(E^{*})
,
\Gamma(E)
are the modules of global
sections of
E^{*}
and
E
, respectively). See
[
Co
, Proposition 7.5.4]
.

In general the map
\psi
is not an isomorphism See
Example
3.17
of a differential space where
\psi
is not
injective. For this reason I think one should not define the space
of 1-forms on a
on differential space
(M,\mathscr{F})
to be
\Hom(\cDer(\mathscr{F}),\mathscr{F})
.

We’ll prove existence of Kähler differentials in
Theorem
3.8
below. The result is known — it was proved
by Dubuc and Kock
[
DK
]
over 30 years ago. The proof we will
present is essentially the one in
[
J
]
. Our excuses for
providing the proof are that we (1) are trying to keep the paper
self-contained and (2) we will need the details of the proof for the
construction of the
C^{\infty}
-algebraic de Rham complex of a
C^{\infty}
-ring. The
uniqueness of Kähler differentials follows easily from their
universal properties — see Remark
3.5
.

We will compute two examples before proving Theorem
3.8
. But
first, a few observations and remarks.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a
C^{\infty}
-ring,
w:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{A}
a derivation and
a,b\in\mathscr{A}
two elements with
ab=1
(i.e.,
a
is invertible and
b
is the
inverse of
a
). Then

(3.1)

w(b)=-b^{2}w(a).

Proof.

Since
w
is a derivation,
w(1)=0
. Hence
0=w(ab)=w(a)b+a\,w(b)

and the result follows.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

It follows from the definition of the module
\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}
of Kähler differentials
of a
C^{\infty}
-ring
\mathscr{C}
that the module has to be unique in the following
sense: if
\mathscr{N}
is another
\mathscr{C}
module and
d:\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{N}
is another derivation with the same
universal property as
d_{\mathscr{C}}:\mathscr{C}\to\Omega_{\mathscr{C}}^{1}
then there is a
unique isomorphism
\psi:\mathscr{N}\to\Omega_{\mathscr{C}}^{1}
of
\mathscr{C}
-modules so that

\psi\circ d=d_{\mathscr{C}}.

Following a common practice we may refer to
any

\mathscr{C}
-module

\mathscr{N}
with a derivation
d:\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{N}
that has the
appropriate universal property as “the” module of Kähler
differentials of the
C^{\infty}
-ring
\mathscr{C}
.

Example 3.6
.

The module
\bm{\Upomega}_{dR}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of ordinary differential 1-forms together with the exterior
derivative
d:C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\bm{\Upomega}_{dR}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a module of Kähler
differentials over the
C^{\infty}
-ring
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
:

\Omega^{1}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\bm{\Upomega}_{dR}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof.

We need to show that for any
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
-module

\mathscr{N}
and for any derivation
X:C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathscr{N}
there is a
unique map of modules
\varphi_{X}:\bm{\Upomega}_{dR}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathscr{N}
so that

\varphi_{X}(dg)=X(g)

for all
g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Since
\bm{\Upomega}_{dR}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is freely
generated, as a module, by the differentials
dx_{1},\ldots,dx_{n}

(where
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
are the coordinate functions),
the map
\varphi_{X}
given by

\varphi_{X}(\sum a_{i}dx_{i}):=\sum a_{i}X(x_{i})

is a well-defined map of
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
modules. And it is the unique map
from
\bm{\Upomega}_{dR}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
\mathscr{N}
satisfying
\varphi_{X}(dx_{i})=X(x_{i})
.

It is not hard to check that the exterior derivative
d:C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\bm{\Upomega}_{dR}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a
C^{\infty}
-ring derivation: any
k>0
, any

h\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
and any
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

\begin{split}d\left(h_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})\right)&=d(h\circ(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}))\\
&=\sum\partial_{i}(h(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}))\,dx_{i}=\sum_{i}\left(\sum_{j}(\partial_{j}h)(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})\cdot\partial_{i}f_{j}\right)\,dx_{i}\\
&=\sum_{j}\left((\partial_{j}h)(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})\cdot\sum_{i}\partial_{i}f_{j}\,dx_{i}\right)=\sum_{j}(\partial_{j}h)_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})\cdot df_{j}.\end{split}

It remains to check that
\varphi_{X}(dg)=X(g)
for all
g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
The
C^{\infty}
-ring
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a free ring on the generators

x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
(see Definition
2.11
and Lemma
2.12
).
In particular
g_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=g
for all

g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Since
X
is a derivation

\begin{split}X(g)=X(g_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))&=\sum(\partial_{i}g)_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\,X(x_{i})\\
&=\sum(\partial_{i}g)\,X(x_{i})=\varphi_{X}(\sum(\partial_{i}g)\,dx_{i})=\varphi_{X}(dg).\end{split}

Thus there is a unique map of
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
-modules

\varphi_{X}:\bm{\Upomega}_{dR}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathscr{N}
which satisfies

\varphi_{X}\circ d=X
. It is given by

\varphi_{X}(\sum a_{i}dx_{i})=\sum a_{i}X(x_{i})
.
∎

Example 3.7
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set. The module
\bm{\Upomega}_{dR}^{1}(U)
of
1-forms together with the exterior derivative
d:C^{\infty}(U)\to\bm{\Upomega}_{dR}^{1}(U)
is a module of Kähler differentials
over the
C^{\infty}
-ring
C^{\infty}(U)
. That is,
\Omega^{1}_{C^{\infty}(U)}=\bm{\Upomega}_{dR}^{1}(U)
.

Proof.

As in the case of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the differentials

\{d(x_{i}|_{U})=dx_{i}|_{U}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
of the standard coordinate
functions
\{x_{i}|_{U}:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
freely generate the
C^{\infty}(U)
-module

\bm{\Upomega}_{dR}^{1}(U)
of 1-forms. Therefore for any

C^{\infty}(U)
-module
\mathscr{N}
and any derivation
X:C^{\infty}(U)\to\mathscr{N}

there is a
C^{\infty}(U)
-module map

\varphi_{X}:\bm{\Upomega}_{dR}^{1}(U)\to\mathscr{N}
defined uniquely by

\varphi_{X}\left(\sum a_{i}\,dx_{i}|_{U}\right)=\sum a_{i}\,X(x_{i}|_{U}).

Using the fact that

df=\sum(\partial_{i}f)\,dx_{i}|_{U}

for all
f\in C^{\infty}(U)
it is not hard to show that the exterior
derivative
d:C^{\infty}(U)\to\bm{\Upomega}_{dR}^{1}(U)
is a
C^{\infty}
-derivation. It remains
to check that

\varphi_{X}\circ d=X.

Since for any
g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

g|_{U}=g\circ(x_{1}|_{U},\ldots,x_{n}|_{U})=g_{C^{\infty}(U)}(x_{1}|_{U},\ldots,x_{n}|_{U})

d(g|_{U})=d\left(g_{C^{\infty}(U)}(x_{1}|_{U},\ldots,x_{n}|_{U})\right)=\sum(\partial_{i}g)_{C^{\infty}(U)}(x_{1}|_{U},\ldots,x_{n}|_{U})dx_{i}|_{U}=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}g|_{U}dx_{i}|_{U}.

Therefore

\varphi_{X}(d(g|_{U}))=\sum\partial_{i}g|_{U}\,X(x_{i}|_{U})=X(g|_{U}),

where in the last equality we used the fact that
X
is a

C^{\infty}
-derivation.
However not all functions in
C^{\infty}(U)
are restrictions of smooth
functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For arbitrary functions in
C^{\infty}(U)
we argue
as follows.
By the Localization Theorem of Muñoz Díaz and Ortega

[
MO
]
(see also
[
NGSS
, p. 28]
)
given a function
f\in C^{\infty}(U)
there exist
g,h\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
so that

h|_{U}f=g|_{U}

and
h|_{U}
is invertible in
C^{\infty}(U)
. Then

df=d\frac{g|_{U}}{h|_{U}}=\frac{1}{h|_{U}}d(g|_{U})-\frac{g|_{U}}{(h|_{U})^{2}}d(h|_{U}).

We now write
g
and
h
for
g|_{U}
and
h|_{U}
to reduce the clutter.
We have

\varphi_{X}(df)=\varphi_{X}(\frac{1}{h}dg-\frac{g}{h^{2}}dh)=\frac{1}{h}X(g)-\frac{g}{h^{2}}X(h)\overset{\textrm{ Lemma~\ref{lem:B.2}}}{=}X(\frac{g}{h})=X(f).

And we are done.
∎

We now prove existence of
C^{\infty}
-Kähler differentials.

Theorem 3.8

(Dubuc and Kock
[
DK
]
)
.

For any
C^{\infty}
-ring
\mathscr{C}
there exists a module of Kähler
differentials
\Omega_{\mathscr{C}}^{1}
together with the
C^{\infty}
-ring derivation

d_{\mathscr{C}}:\mathscr{C}\to\Omega_{\mathscr{C}}^{1}
, which has the appropriate universal properties.

Before we proceed with a proof of this theorem, we note that for any
C^{\infty}
-ring
\mathscr{C}

and any set
S
there exists a free
\mathscr{C}
-module
F[S]
on the set

S
. The elements of
F[S]
are finite formal linear combination

a_{1}s_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}s_{n}
for
n>0
,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathscr{C}
,
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\in S
.

Proof of Theorem
3.8
.

In commutative algebra there are two equivalent constructions of
Kähler differentials of a commutative ring. Dubuc and Kock adapted
one of them to the
C^{\infty}
-ring setting (strictly speaking they adapted
it to the setting of Fermat theories, which include
C^{\infty}
-rings). Joyce
[
J
]
adapts the
other. It will be convenient for us to follow Joyce’s approach.

Consider the free
\mathscr{C}
-module
F[\hat{d}\mathscr{C}]

where
\hat{d}\mathscr{C}
is a
set of formal symbols
\hat{d}b
, one symbol for each
b\in\mathscr{C}
. Thus

F[\hat{d}\mathscr{C}]=\{a_{1}\hat{d}b_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}\hat{d}b_{n}\mid n>0,a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\in\mathscr{C}\}.

Let
I
denote the submodule of
F[\hat{d}\mathscr{C}]
generated by the set

\mathscr{R}:=\{\hat{d}(f_{\mathscr{C}}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}))-\sum(\partial_{i}f)_{\mathscr{C}}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\,\hat{d}a_{i}\mid n>0,f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathscr{C}\},

that is,

I=\langle\mathscr{R}\rangle.

Let

\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}:=F[\hat{d}\mathscr{C}]/I.

Then

\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}
is an
\mathscr{C}
-module. Define
d_{\mathscr{C}}:\mathscr{C}\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}
by

d_{\mathscr{C}}(a)=\hat{d}a+I.

It is easy to check that
d_{\mathscr{C}}
is a
C^{\infty}
-derivation. Note also
that the module
\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}
is generated by the set

\{d_{\mathscr{C}}(a)\}_{a\in\mathscr{C}}
.

It remain to check the universal property of
d_{\mathscr{C}}
. Let
\mathscr{N}
be
an
\mathscr{C}
-module and
X:\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{N}
a
C^{\infty}
-derivation. Since

F[\hat{d}\mathscr{C}]
is a free
\mathscr{C}
-module on the set
\{\hat{d}b\}_{b\in\mathscr{C}}
, there is a unique map of
\mathscr{C}
-modules
\hat{\varphi}:F[\hat{d}\mathscr{C}]\to\mathscr{N}
with

\hat{\varphi}(\hat{d}b)=X(b).

for all
b\in\mathscr{C}
.
Since
X
is a derivation,
\hat{\varphi}(\alpha)=0
for all
\alpha
in the
submodule
I
. Consequently
\hat{\varphi}
descends to a map of

\mathscr{C}
-modules

\varphi_{X}:\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}\to\mathscr{N}.

Then by construction of the map
\varphi_{X}
and of the derivation
d:\mathscr{C}\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}

\varphi_{X}(d_{\mathscr{C}}b)=X(b)

for all
b\in\mathscr{C}
. Since the set
\{d_{\mathscr{C}}b\}_{b\in\mathscr{C}}

generates the module
\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}
of Kähler differentials, the map
\varphi_{X}

is unique.
∎

The next lemma shows that just like in the case of

-Kähler

of a quotient

is a quotient of

\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}
by an appropriate submodule. It is useful for computations.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
f:\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{D}
be a surjective map of

C^{\infty}
-rings,
I=\ker f
and
\Lambda^{1}(f):\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{D}}
the induced map on Kähler differentials. Then

\Lambda^{1}(f)
is surjective and
\ker\Lambda^{1}(f)=J
, where

J=\mathscr{C}d_{\mathscr{C}}I+Id_{\mathscr{C}}\mathscr{C}=\mathscr{C}d_{\mathscr{C}}I.

Proof.

Since
\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}
,
\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{D}}
are generated by the sets

\{d_{\mathscr{C}}c\}_{c\in\mathscr{C}}
and
\{d_{\mathscr{D}}x\}_{x\in\mathscr{D}}
,
respectively, since
f
is surjective and since
\Lambda^{1}(f)(d_{\mathscr{C}}c)=d_{\mathscr{D}}(f(c))
for all
c\in\mathscr{C}
,
\Lambda^{1}(f)
is surjective.

We next drop the subscript
C
on
d_{\mathscr{C}}
and argue that
\mathscr{C}dI+Id\mathscr{C}=\mathscr{C}dI
. For any
c\in\mathscr{C}

and any
i\in I
,
d(ic)\in dI
. Hence
dI\ni d(ic)=cdi+idc
.
Consequently
i\,dc=d(ci)-cdI\in\mathscr{C}dI
. It follows that

Id\mathscr{C}\subseteq\mathscr{C}dI
. We conclude that
J=\mathscr{C}dI
.

Since (
3.2
) commutes and
\Lambda^{1}(f)
is a map of

\mathscr{C}
-modules,
J=\mathscr{C}dI\subseteq\ker\Lambda^{1}(f)
. We need to
show that
J=\ker\Lambda^{1}(f)
.

Note that for any
i\in I
and any
a\in J
, the product
ia
is in

J
. Therefore the quotient
\mathscr{C}
-module
\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}/J
is
naturally a
\mathscr{D}
-module with the action of
\mathscr{D}
given by

f(c)\cdot(\alpha+J)=(c\alpha)+J

for all
c\in\mathscr{C}
and all
\alpha+J\in\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}/J
.
The map

\bar{f}:\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}/J\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{D}},\qquad\bar{f}(\alpha+J):=\Lambda^{1}(\alpha)

is
\mathscr{D}
-linear. We will argue that
\bar{f}
is an isomorphism of
modules, which is enough to prove that
\ker\Lambda^{1}(f)=J
and
thereby finish the proof of the lemma.

Let
p:\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}/J
denote the canonical projection. The map
p

is a map of
\mathscr{C}
-modules. Then
p\circ d_{\mathscr{C}}:\mathscr{C}\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}/J
is a
C^{\infty}
-derivation of the
C^{\infty}
-ring
\mathscr{C}
. Since for any
i\in I
,

d_{\mathscr{C}}(i)\in J
, the composite
p\circ d_{\mathscr{C}}
induces a map

\bar{d}:\mathscr{D}\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}/J

making the diagram

\textstyle{\mathscr{C}}
\textstyle{\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}}
\textstyle{\mathscr{D}}
\textstyle{\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}/J}
\scriptstyle{d_{\mathscr{C}}}
\scriptstyle{f}
\scriptstyle{p}
\scriptstyle{\bar{d}}

commute, i.e.,

\bar{d}(f(c))=p(d_{\mathscr{C}}(c))=d_{\mathscr{C}}(c)+J

for all
c\in\mathscr{C}
. A computation shows that
\bar{d}
is a

C^{\infty}
-derivation of the
C^{\infty}
-ring
\mathscr{D}
. By the universal
property of
d_{\mathscr{D}}:\mathscr{D}\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{D}}
there exists a unique

\mathscr{D}
-linear map
\psi:\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{D}}\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}/J
such that

(3.3)

\bar{d}(x)=\psi(d_{\mathscr{D}}(x))

for all
x\in\mathscr{D}
. Since the set
\{d_{\mathscr{C}}(c)\}_{c\in\mathscr{C}}

generates the module
\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}
and since
p
is onto, the set

\{p(d_{\mathscr{C}}(c))\}_{c\in\mathscr{C}}=\{\bar{d}(c)\}_{c\in\mathscr{C}}=\{\bar{d}(x)\}_{x\in\mathscr{D}}

generates the module
\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}/J
(both over
\mathscr{C}
and over
\mathscr{D}
).
Equation (
3.3
) now implies that
\psi
is onto.

Since
J\subset\ker\Lambda^{1}(f)
, the map
\Lambda^{1}(f)
induces a
map
\bar{f}:\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}/J\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{D}}
so that

\bar{f}\circ p=\Lambda^{1}(f).

For any
c\in\mathscr{C}

(3.4)

\bar{f}(\bar{d}(f(c)))=\bar{f}(p(d_{\mathscr{C}}(c)))=\Lambda^{1}(f)(d_{\mathscr{C}}(c)=d_{\mathscr{C}}(f(c)).

Since
f
is onto (
3.4
) implies that

(3.5)

\bar{f}\circ\bar{d}=d_{\mathscr{D}}.

Since
\bar{d}=\psi\circ d_{\mathscr{D}}
equation (
3.5
) implies that

(\bar{f}\circ\psi)(d_{\mathscr{D}}(x))=d_{\mathscr{D}}(x)

for all
x\in\mathscr{D}
,
Since the set

\{d_{\mathscr{D}}(x)\}_{x\in\mathscr{D}}
generates
\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{D}}
we conclude that

\bar{f}\circ\psi=\id_{\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{D}}}.

Hence
\psi
is injective. Since we have already shown that
\psi

is onto, this implies that
\psi
is an isomorphism. But then

\bar{f}
has to be an isomorphism as well, and we are done.
∎

Lemma 3.15
.

Let

be a closed subset and
\mathscr{F}=C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})|_{M}
the

(3.8)

V(f)|_{M}=v(f|_{M})

3.13

Lemma 4.3

It is easy to see that if such map

\varphi:F(\hat{d}\mathscr{C})\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}\wedge\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}},\qquad\varphi(\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\hat{d}b_{i})=\sum_{i}d_{\mathscr{C}}a_{i}\wedge d_{\mathscr{C}}b_{i},

We check that

factors through the natural surjection

It will be useful to know that

is
functorial in the

-ring

Proposition 4.4
.

A map
f:\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{B}
of
C^{\infty}
-rings induces a unique map

\Lambda^{\bullet}(f):\Lambda^{\bullet}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}\to\Lambda^{\bullet}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{B}}
of
commutative differential graded algebras. Explicitly

\Lambda^{\bullet}(f)(a_{0}d_{\mathscr{C}}a_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge d_{\mathscr{C}}a_{k})=f(a_{0})d_{\mathscr{B}}f(a_{1})\wedge\cdots\wedge d_{\mathscr{B}}f(a_{k})

for all
k>0
and all
a_{0},\ldots,a_{k}\in\mathscr{C}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.10
the map
f:\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{B}
induces a map

\Lambda^{1}(f):\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{B}}
. By taking exterior
powers of
\Lambda^{1}(f)

we get
\Lambda^{k}(f):\Lambda^{k}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}\to\Lambda^{k}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{B}}
for each
k\geq 1
. Together these maps give us a map of commutative graded algebras

\Lambda^{\bullet}(f):=\Lambda^{\bullet}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}\to\Lambda^{\bullet}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{B}}

with the property that

\Lambda^{\bullet}(f)(a_{0}d_{\mathscr{C}}a_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge d_{\mathscr{C}}a_{k})=f(a_{0})d_{\mathscr{B}}f(a_{1})\wedge\cdots\wedge d_{\mathscr{B}}f(a_{k})

for all
k>0
and all
a_{0},\ldots,a_{k}\in\mathscr{C}
(since

for all

Now

\begin{split}d\left(\Lambda^{\bullet}(f)\,(a_{0}d_{\mathscr{C}}a_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge d_{\mathscr{C}}a_{k})\right)&=d\left(f(a_{0})\wedge d_{\mathscr{B}}f(a_{1})\wedge\cdots\wedge d_{\mathscr{B}}f(a_{k})\right)\\

&=\Lambda^{\bullet}(f)\left(d\left(a_{0}\,d_{\mathscr{C}}a_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge d_{\mathscr{C}}a_{k}\right)\right).\end{split}

Hence
\Lambda^{\bullet}(f)
also commutes with

and we are done.
∎

5.
Differential forms on
C^{\infty}
-ringed spaces

In this section we construct sheaves of commutative differential
graded algebras over local
C^{\infty}
-ringed spaces.
We think of sections of these sheaves as
differential forms, since for manifolds with corners they are sheaves
of ordinary
differential forms. We show that our differential forms pull back,
that pull back commutes with the exterior derivatives and exterior
multiplication and that our differential forms can be integrated over
simplices and, more generally, over smooth singular chains.
Consequently a version of Stokes’ theorem holds for these differential
forms. We start by constructing the appropriate presheaves.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
(M,\mathscr{A})
be a
C^{\infty}
-ringed
space. There exists a presheaf
(\Lambda^{\bullet}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}},\wedge,d)
of commutative differential graded algebras over
\mathscr{A}
so that for each
open subset
U\subset M
the CDGA
(\Lambda^{\bullet}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}}(U),\wedge_{U},dU)
is the
C^{\infty}
-algebraic de Rham complex of the

C^{\infty}
-ring
\mathscr{A}(U)
.

Proof.

For every open set
U\subset M
we have an
\mathscr{A}(U)
-module

\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}(U)}
of Kähler differentials, which comes with the
universal derivation
d:\mathscr{A}(U)\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}(U)}
. By
Lemma
3.10
the assignment
U\mapsto\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}(U)}
is
a presheaf of
\mathscr{A}
-modules. We denote this presheaf by

\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}}
. Consequently for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
U\mapsto\Lambda^{k}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}(U)}
is also a presheaf of
\mathscr{A}
-modules. We denote it
by
\Lambda^{k}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}}
. Proposition
4.4
implies for
all
k
and
\ell

that we have maps

\wedge:\Lambda^{k}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}}\times\Lambda^{\ell}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}}\to\Lambda^{k+\ell}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad d:\Lambda^{k}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}}\to\Lambda^{k+1}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}}

of presheaves of
\mathscr{A}
-modules and that these maps turn the sequence

\{\Lambda^{k}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
of presheaves into a presheaf
of commutative graded
\mathscr{A}
-algebras.
We thus get a presheaf of
CDGAs
(\{\Lambda^{k}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}},\wedge,d)
over the
sheaf
\mathscr{A}
.
∎

In general there is no reason to expect that the presheaf

\Lambda^{\bullet}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}}
of CDGAs associated to

C^{\infty}
-ringed space
(M,\mathscr{A})
is a sheaf. So we need
to sheafify. Recall that there is a sheafification functor

\mathsf{sh}:\mathsf{Psh}_{M}\to Sh_{M}
from the category of presheaves of

\mathscr{A}
-modules to the category of sheaves.
The functor is left-adjoint to the inclusion functor

i:\mathsf{Sh}_{M}\hookrightarrow\mathsf{Psh}_{M}
(more often than not we will
suppress the inclusion functor).

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
(\{{\mathcal{M}}^{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty},d,\wedge)
be a presheaf of CDGAs on
a
C^{\infty}
-ringed space
(M,\mathscr{A})
. Then

(\{\mathsf{sh}({\mathcal{M}}^{k})\}_{k=0}^{\infty},\mathsf{sh}(d),\mathsf{sh}(\wedge))

is a sheaf of CDGAs on
the
C^{\infty}
-ringed space
(M,\mathscr{A})
. Here as before
\mathsf{sh}:\mathsf{Psh}_{M}\to\mathsf{Sh}_{M}
is the sheafification functor.

Proof.

Note that since the sheafification of
the zero (pre)sheaf is zero, the sheafification of any zero morphism
is zero as well:
\mathsf{sh}(0)=0
. Then
0=\mathsf{sh}(0)=\mathsf{sh}(d^{2})=(\mathsf{sh}(d))^{2}
. Hence
(\{\mathsf{sh}({\mathcal{M}}^{k})\}_{k=0}^{\infty},\mathsf{sh}(d))
is a cochain complex of sheaves. Since sheafification
preserves finite products we get
collection of morphisms

\mathsf{sh}(\wedge):\mathsf{sh}({\mathcal{M}}^{k})\times\mathsf{sh}({\mathcal{M}}^{\ell})\to\mathsf{sh}({\mathcal{M}}^{k+\ell})

We can express the fact that
\wedge
is graded commutative and the
fact that
d
is a derivation of degree 1 with respect to
\wedge

in terms of commutative diagrams. Since
\mathsf{sh}
is a
functor, it follows that
(\{\mathsf{sh}({\mathcal{M}}^{k})\}_{k=0}^{\infty},\mathsf{sh}(d),\mathsf{sh}(\wedge))
is a sheaf
of CDGAs.
∎

\Lambda^{\bullet}\Omega^{1}_{C^{\infty}_{Q}}
does nothing and the possibly
mysterious sheaf

\bm{\Upomega}^{\bullet}_{C^{\infty}_{Q}}=\mathsf{sh}(\Lambda^{\bullet}\Omega^{1}_{C^{\infty}_{Q}})

turns out to be
the sheaf
(\bm{\Upomega}^{\bullet}_{dR,Q},d,\wedge)
of ordinary de Rham
differential forms. Next we recall a definition of the standard
(geometric)
k
-simplex.

Definition 6.1
.

The
standard
k
-simplex
\Delta^{k}
is the
space

\Delta^{k}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}\mid x_{i}\geq 0,x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}=1\}.

The
k
-simplex is a manifold with corners, hence a local

C^{\infty}
-ringed space
(\Delta^{k},C^{\infty}_{\Delta^{k}})

The
i
th face
of the
k
th simplex is the map
d_{i}:\Delta^{k-1}\to\Delta^{k}
given by

d_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1})=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},0,x_{i},\ldots,x_{k-1}).

We view
d_{i}
as a map of differential spaces (and of local

C^{\infty}
-ringed spaces, see Remark
6.2
).

Remark 6.2
.

(Cf. Remark
5.11
.)
Recall that there is a fully faithful functor from the category of
differential spaces to the category
\mathsf{LC^{\infty}RS}
of local
C^{\infty}
-ringed
spaces (Theorem
B.1
). For this reaon we will not
notationally distinguished between the map
d_{i}:\Delta^{k-1}\to\Delta^{k}
of differential spaces
and the corresponding map

d_{i}:(\Delta^{k-1},C^{\infty}_{\Delta^{k-1}})\to(\Delta^{k},C^{\infty}_{\Delta^{k}})
in
\mathsf{LC^{\infty}RS}
.

Definition 6.3
.

A
k
-simplex
of a local
C^{\infty}
-ringed space
(M,\mathscr{A})
is a map

\sigma:(\Delta^{k},C^{\infty}_{\Delta^{k}})\to(M,\mathscr{A})
of local

C^{\infty}
-ringed spaces.

We define the
vector space
C_{k}(M,\mathscr{A};\mathbb{R})
of real
k
-chains

of a local
C^{\infty}
-ringed space
(M,\mathscr{A})
to be the real vector space
generated by the set of
k
-simplexes of
(M,\mathscr{A})
. We will refer to
elements of
C_{k}(M,\mathscr{A};\mathbb{R})
a
k
-chains and write them as finite
sums
\sum n_{\sigma,}\sigma
.

Remark 6.4
.

A simplex
\sigma:(\Delta^{k},C^{\infty}_{\Delta^{k}})\to(M,\mathscr{A})
is really
a pair of maps: a continuous map
f:\Delta^{k}\to M
and a map of
sheaves
f_{\#}:\mathscr{A}\to f_{*}C^{\infty}_{\Delta_{k}}
. However, writing
(f,f_{\#})
for
\sigma
in this situation is notationally too heavy, so we
will avoid it.

Definition 6.5
.

The
boundary

\partial\sigma
of a
k
-simplex
\sigma:(\Delta^{k},C^{\infty}_{\Delta^{k}})\to(M,\mathscr{A})
is the chain
\partial\sigma\in C_{k-1}(M,\mathscr{A};\mathbb{R})
defined by

\partial\sigma=\sum(-1)^{i}\sigma\circ d_{i}

where
d_{i}:\Delta_{k-1}\to\Delta_{k}
,
i=1,\ldots,k
are the
standard face maps of Definition
6.1
.

Definition 6.6
.

The
boundary

\partial\Delta^{k}
of the standard

k
-simplex is the chain
\partial(\id)\in C_{k-1}(\Delta^{k},C^{\infty}_{\Delta_{k}};\mathbb{R})
where
\id:(\Delta^{k},C^{\infty}_{\Delta_{k}})\to(\Delta^{k},C^{\infty}_{\Delta_{k}})
is the identity
k
-simplex.

Definition 6.7

(Integral of a global
C^{\infty}
-algebraic differential
form)
.

Let
(M,\mathscr{A})
be a local
C^{\infty}
-ringed space,
\sigma:(\Delta^{k},C^{\infty}_{\Delta^{k}})\to(M,\mathscr{A})
a
k
-simplex and
\omega\in\bm{\Upomega}^{k}_{\mathscr{A}}(M)=\left(\mathsf{sh}(\Lambda^{k}\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{A}})\right)(M)
a global
C^{\infty}
-algebraic differential
form. Then, since by Lemma
5.3
,
\mathsf{sh}(\Lambda^{k}\Omega^{1}_{C^{\infty}_{\Delta^{k}}})=\bm{\Upomega}^{k}_{dR}(\Delta^{k})
, the pullback
\sigma^{*}\omega
is an
ordinary
k
-form on the manifold with corners
\Delta^{k}
.

We define

the integral of
\omega
over
\sigma
to be

\int_{\sigma}\omega:=\int_{\Delta^{k}}\sigma^{*}\omega.

We define
the integral of
\omega
over a
k
-chain

\sum n_{\sigma}\sigma\in C_{k}(M,\mathscr{A};\mathbb{R})
to be

\int_{\sum n_{\sigma}\sigma}\omega:=\sum n_{\sigma}\int_{\Delta^{k}}\sigma^{*}\omega.

Recall Stokes’s theorem for simplices (see, for example,
[
Co
, Theorem 8.2.9]
) :

Theorem 6.8
.

For any
k>0
and for any differential form

\gamma\in\bm{\Upomega}_{dR}(\Delta^{k})

\int_{\Delta_{k}}d\gamma=\int_{\partial\Delta_{k}}\gamma.

As an immediate consequence we get

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
(M,\mathscr{A})
be a local
C^{\infty}
-ringed space,
\gamma\in\bm{\Upomega}^{k-1}_{\mathscr{A}}(M)
a global
C^{\infty}
-algebraic de Rham form
and
\sigma:(\Delta^{k},C^{\infty}_{\Delta^{k}})\to(M,\mathscr{A})
a
k
-simplex.
Then

\int_{\sigma}d\gamma=\int_{\partial\sigma}\gamma.

Proof.

The proof is a computation:

\int_{\sigma}d\gamma=\int_{\Delta^{k}}\sigma^{*}(d\gamma)=\int_{\Delta^{k}}d(\sigma^{*}\gamma)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{Theorem~\ref{thm:Stokes_simp}}}}{{=}}\int_{\partial\Delta^{k}}\sigma^{*}\gamma=\sum(-1)^{i}\int_{\Delta^{k-1}}d_{i}^{*}(\sigma^{*}\gamma)=\int_{\partial\sigma}\gamma.

∎

Remark 6.10
.

Instead of integrating over singular chains we may just as easily
integrate over compact manifolds with boundary and then there is a
version of the Stokes’ theorem as well.

Appendix A
Differential spaces and bump functions

The goal of the appendix is to prove that condition
(
2.48.i
) in the definition of a differential space is
equivalent to existence of bump functions. The material is extracted from
[
KL
]
.

Definition A.1
.

Let
(M,\mathscr{T})
be a topological space,

C\subset M
a closed set and
x\in M\smallsetminus C
a point. A

bump function
(relative to
C
and
x
) is a continuous
function
\rho:M\to[0,1]
so that
(\supp\rho)\,\cap C=\varnothing

and
\rho
is identically 1 on a neighborhood of
x
.

Definition A.2
.

Let
(M,\mathscr{T})
be a topological
space and
\mathscr{F}\subseteq C^{0}(M,\mathbb{R})
a collection of continuous
real-valued functions on
M
. The topology
\mathscr{T}
on
M
is
\mathscr{F}
-regular
iff for any closed subset
C
of
M
and any point

x\in M\smallsetminus C
there is a bump function
\rho\in\mathscr{F}

with
\supp\rho\subset M\smallsetminus C
and
\rho
identically 1
on a neighborhood of
x
.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
(M,\mathscr{T})
be a topological space and
\mathscr{F}\subset C^{0}(M,\mathbb{R})
a
C^{\infty}
-subring. Then
\mathscr{T}
is the smallest topology making all
the functions in
\mathscr{F}
continuous if and only if the topology
\mathscr{T}
is
\mathscr{F}
-regular.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{T}_{\mathscr{F}}
denote the smallest topology making all the functions
in
\mathscr{F}
continuous. Note that the set

\{f^{-1}(I)\mid f\in\mathscr{F},\text{ $I$ is an open
interval }\}

is a sub-basis for
\mathscr{T}_{\mathscr{F}}
. Since all the functions in
\mathscr{F}
are
continuous with respect to
\mathscr{T}
,
\mathscr{T}_{\mathscr{F}}\subseteq\mathscr{T}
.
Therefore it is enough to argue that
\mathscr{T}\subseteq\mathscr{T}_{\mathscr{F}}
if and
only if
\mathscr{T}
is
\mathscr{F}
-regular.

(
\Rightarrow
) Suppose
\mathscr{T}\subseteq\mathscr{T}_{\mathscr{F}}
. Let

C\subset M
be
\mathscr{T}
-closed and
x
a point in
M
which is not in

C
. Then
M\setminus C
is
\mathscr{T}
-open. Since
\mathscr{T}\subseteq\mathscr{T}_{\mathscr{F}}
by assumption,
M\setminus C
is in
\mathscr{T}_{\mathscr{F}}
.
Then there exist functions
h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}\in\mathscr{F}

and open intervals
I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}
such that

x\in\cap_{i=1}^{k}h_{i}^{-1}(I_{i})\subset M\setminus C
. There is a

C^{\infty}
function
\rho:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to[0,1]

with
\supp\rho\subset I_{1}\times\ldots\times I_{k}
and the
property that

\rho=1
on a neighborhood of
(h_{1}(x),\ldots,h_{k}(x))
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
Then
\tau:=\rho\circ(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})
is in
\mathscr{F}
, since
\mathscr{F}
is a

C^{\infty}
-subring of
C^{0}(M)
. The function
\tau
is a desired bump
function.

(
\Leftarrow
) Suppose the topology
\mathscr{T}
is

\mathscr{F}
-regular. Let
U\in\mathscr{T}
be an open set. Then
C=M\setminus U
is closed. Since
\mathscr{T}
is
\mathscr{F}
-regular, for any point
x\in U
there
is a bump function
\rho_{x}\in\mathscr{F}
with
\supp\rho_{x}\subset U
and

\rho_{x}
is identically
1
in a neighborhood of
x
. Then
\rho_{x}^{-1}((0,\infty))\subset U

and
\rho_{x}^{-1}((0,\infty))\in\mathscr{T}_{\mathscr{F}}
. It follows that

U=\bigcup_{x\in U}\rho_{x}^{-1}((0,\infty))\in\mathscr{T}_{\mathscr{F}}.

Since
U
is an arbitrary element of
\mathscr{T}
,
\mathscr{T}\subseteq\mathscr{T}_{\mathscr{F}}
.
∎

Appendix B
Differential spaces as local
C^{\infty}
-ringed
spaces

The goal of the appendix is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem B.1
.

The category
\mathsf{DiffSpace}
of differential spaces is (isomorphic to) a
full subcategory of the category
\mathsf{LC^{\infty}RS}
of local
C^{\infty}
-ringed spaces.

Proof.

We construct a functor
I:\mathsf{DiffSpace}\to\mathsf{LC^{\infty}RS}
and check that it is
fully faithful. We define
I
on objects by sending a differential
space
(M,\mathscr{F})
to the corresponding local
C^{\infty}
-ringed space

(M,\mathscr{F}_{M})
:

I((M,\mathscr{F}))=(M,\mathscr{F}_{M}),

cf. Lemma
2.58
.

We need to define
I
on morphisms. Recall that a smooth map

f:(M,\mathscr{F})\to(N,\mathscr{G})
between two differential spaces, i.e., a
morphism in
\mathsf{DiffSpace}
, is a continuous map
f:M\to N
with the
property that for all
h\in\mathscr{G}
its pullback
f^{*}h
is in
\mathscr{F}
.
To define
I(f)
we need to construct a map of sheaves

f_{\#}:\mathscr{G}_{N}\to f_{*}\mathscr{F}_{M}
and set
I(f):=(f,f_{\#})
.

Recall that
\mathscr{G}_{N}
is the sheafification of the presheaf
{\mathcal{P}}_{N}

defined by

{\mathcal{P}}_{N}(U)=\mathscr{G}|_{U}

for all open sets
U\subseteq N
. Since pullback by
f
commutes
with restrictions we get a map of presheaves

f_{\#}:{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\to f_{*}({\mathcal{P}}_{M})\hookrightarrow f_{*}\mathscr{F}_{M},\qquad f_{\#}(h)=h\circ f.

The universal property of sheafification
{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\hookrightarrow\mathscr{G}_{N}

uniquely extends
f_{\#}
to a map

\mathscr{G}_{N}\to f_{*}\mathscr{F}_{M},

which is given by the same formula: for any open set
U\subset N
and
for any function
h\in C^{\infty}_{N}(U)
,

h\mapsto h\circ f\in\mathscr{F}_{M}(f^{-1}(U)).

We now abuse notation and define
f_{\#}:\mathscr{G}_{N}\to f_{*}\mathscr{F}_{M}
by

f_{\#}(h):=h\circ f
. The map
f_{\#}
is a map of sheaves.
We now define
I
on morphisms by

I\big((M,\mathscr{F})\xrightarrow{f}(N,\mathscr{G})\big)=(f,f_{\#}):(M,\mathscr{F}_{M})\to(N,\mathscr{G}_{N}),

where as above
f_{\#}h=h\circ f
.

It is not hard to check that
I
preserves identity maps and
composition. Therefore
I
is a functor.
If
I(f)=(f,f_{\#})=(g,g_{\#})=I(g)
for two maps
f,g:(M,\mathscr{F})\to(N,\mathscr{G})
then clearly
f=g
. Therefore
I
is
injective on
\Hom
sets, i.e, the functor
I
is faithful.

It remains to prove that
I
is surjective on
\Hom
sets. This is
where locality has to come in. Suppose we have a continuous map

f:M\to N
and a map of sheaves
\psi:\mathscr{G}_{N}\to f_{*}\mathscr{F}_{M}
. We want
to show that for any open set
U\subset N
and any
h\in\mathscr{G}_{N}(U)

\psi(h)=h\circ f,

for then
(f,\psi)=I(f)
.

Consider a point
p\in U
and a point
q\in f^{-1}(p)
. The map

\psi
induces a map on stalks

\psi_{p}:(\mathscr{G}_{N})_{p}\to(f_{*}\mathscr{F}_{M})_{p}.

Since
q\in f^{-1}(p)
, there is a canonical map of stalks

(f_{*}\mathscr{F}_{M})_{p}\to(\mathscr{F}_{M})_{q}
. Precomposing this canonical map with

\psi_{p}
we get a map

\varphi:(\mathscr{G}_{N})_{p}\to(\mathscr{F}_{M})_{q}.

We can give this map
\varphi
a concrete description: if
U
is a
neighborhood of
p
and
h\in\mathscr{G}_{N}(U)
then

\varphi([(U,h)]=[(f^{-1}(U),\psi(h))]\in(\mathscr{F}_{M})_{q}

The stalk
(\mathscr{F}_{M})_{q}
is a local
C^{\infty}
-ring, hence has a unique

\mathbb{R}
-point
\hat{q}:(\mathscr{F}_{M})_{q}\to\mathbb{R}
:
\hat{q}(g_{q})=g(q)
for
all germs
g_{q}\in(\mathscr{F}_{M})_{q}
; it is the evaluation at
q
. The composite

\hat{q}\circ\varphi:(\mathscr{G}_{N})_{p}\to\mathbb{R}
is also an
\mathbb{R}
-point.
Since the
C^{\infty}
-ring
(\mathscr{G}_{N})_{p}
is local, it has only one

\mathbb{R}
-point, namely
\hat{p}
, the evaluation at
p
. Therefore

\hat{q}\circ\varphi=\hat{p}.

We are almost done.
Recall that any nonzero
C^{\infty}
-ring
\mathscr{C}
contains a copy of
\mathbb{R}
. This
copy correspond to operations of arity
0
, i.e., to constants, and any
map of
C^{\infty}
-rings preserves these constants.
Now for any germ
h_{p}\in(\mathscr{F}_{M})_{p}

0=\hat{p}(h_{p}-h(p))=\hat{q}(\varphi(h_{p}-h(p)))=\hat{q}(\varphi(h_{p}))-h(p)

since
h(p)
is a constant. Since
h(p)=h(f(q))
and since
\hat{q}(\varphi(h_{p}))=\psi(h)(q)
we have

\psi(h)(q)=h(f(q))

for all
q\in f^{-1}(U)
, and we are done.
∎
