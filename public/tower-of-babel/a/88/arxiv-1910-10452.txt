Introduction

The purpose of this paper is to derive the aforementioned equality in a more general functional analytic setting:

•

Preliminaries

2.1
General Notation

All vector spaces used in this paper will be complex.
For linear operators, we will write

AB:=A\circ B

for composition and

Ax:=A(x)

for evaluation.
I
denotes the identity (it will be clear from the context on which space). For a linear operator
A
and a scalar
\lambda
, write

A+\lambda:=A+\lambda I.

The adjoint of
A
will be denoted by
A^{*}
.

B(X,Y)
denotes the space of bounded operators between normed spaces
X
and
Y
and

B(X):=B(X,X).

A projection will always mean an orthogonal projection, i.e. a self-adjoint idempotent operator.

An unbounded operator on a Hilbert space will be a linear map from a dense subspace to the whole space.

For integrals, the convention

\int\limits_{s}^{t}f(r)dr:=-\int\limits_{t}^{s}f(r)dr

for
s>t
will be used.

For any function
f
, the restriction of
f
to some subset
X
of its domain will be denoted by
f|_{X}
. In cases where the codomain is important, i.e. for Fredholm properties,
[f:X\rightarrow Y]
will denote
f
with domain restricted to
X
and codomain restricted to
Y
.

2.2
Notation of Norms

Various norms will be used in this paper. If only one norm is applicable, this will simply be denoted by
||\cdot||
. Otherwise,
||\cdot||
denotes the norm of the Hilbert space
H
(to be introduced later) when applied to an element of
H
and the operator norm with respect to the norm of
H
when applied to an operator.
Similarly,
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
will denote the scalar product on
H
if more than one scalar product is applicable.

||\cdot||_{X}
will be used to denote the norm of some normed space

. For an unbounded operator
U
on a Hilbert space,
||\cdot||_{U}
denotes the graph norm on the domain

\left<x,y\right>_{U}=\left<x,y\right>+\left<Ux,Uy\right>.

This is chosen such that the map

becomes an isometry from

If the operator norm is to be taken with respect to non-canonical norms

||\cdot||_{||\cdot||_{1}\rightarrow||\cdot||_{2}}

For

2.3
Functional Calculus

Throughout this paper, it is assumed that the reader is familiar with the Spectral Theorem for unbounded self-adjoint operators, which allows to define to
f(A)
, for a self-adjoint operator
A
and a Borel measurable function

on its spectrum. One version of it can be found, for example, in [PE] (Theorem 5.3.8 and surroundings). This functional calculus is compatible with addition, multiplication and composition in the ways the notation suggests. Note that
\chi_{a}(A)

f(A)

2.4
Integration of Banach Space Valued Functions

Let
J\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be a compact interval and
X
be a Banach space (Integration works for general measure spaces instead of
J
, but we will only need a compact interval).

A function
f:J\rightarrow X
is called strongly measurable, if it is an almost everywhere uniform limit of countably-valued measurable functions, i.e. functions of the form

\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\chi_{E_{n}}x_{n},

where the
E_{n}
are pairwise disjoint measurable sets, the
x_{n}
are elements of
X
and the sum is taken pointwise (at every point, only one summand is non-zero). Since we will not need any other notion of measurability, we will write measurable for strongly measurable.

Continuous functions are measurable. For measurable functions whose norm is integrable, there is a notion of integration called the Bochner integral, which has many of the properties of the usual Lebesgue integral (see Chapter 3 of [HiPh] for further details). In particular, it coincides with the Riemann integral for continuous functions and the fundamental theorem of calculus holds. Integration can be interchanged with bounded operators, i.e.

\int\limits_{J}Sf(t)dt=S\int\limits_{J}f(t)dt

for a bounded operator
S
. Taking
S
to be the inclusion shows that an integral with respect to a stricter norm on a subspace agrees with the integral with respect to the weaker norm of the ambient space.

The inequality

\left|\left|\int\limits_{J}f(t)dt\right|\right|\leq\int\limits_{J}\left|\left|f(t)\right|\right|dt

holds, as well as Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
f:J\times J\rightarrow X
is continuously differentiable, we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int\limits_{s}^{t}f(t,r)dr=f(t,t)+\int\limits_{s}^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f(t,r)dr.

Proof.

For
h\in{\mathbb{R}}\backslash\{0\}
such that
t+h\in J
, we have

\displaystyle h^{-1}\left(\int\limits_{s}^{t+h}f(t+h,r)dr-\int\limits_{s}^{t}f(t,r)dr\right)

\displaystyle=h^{-1}\int\limits_{s}^{t}f(t+h,r)-f(t,r)dr+h^{-1}\int\limits_{t}^{t+h}f(t+h,r)-f(t,r)dr+h^{-1}\int\limits_{t}^{t+h}f(t,r)dr.

Letting
h
tend to
0
, the first term converges to

\int\limits_{s}^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f(t,r)dr

by dominated convergence, as

||f(t+h,r)-f(t,r)||\leq h\max\limits_{x,y\in J}\left|\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f(x,y)\right|\right|.

The second term converges to 0 by the same inequality.
The last term converges to
f(t,t)
by the fundamental theorem of calculus.
∎

One can define
L^{p}(J,X)
in the usual way, as the space of measurable functions
f
such that

||f||_{L^{p}(J,X)}:=\left|\left|(||f(\cdot)||)\right|\right|_{L^{p}(J,{\mathbb{R}})}

is finite, modulo functions vanishing outside a set of measure zero. This is a Banach space. For
p<q
, we have
L^{q}(J,X)\subseteq L^{p}(J,X)
with bounded inclusion, as an immediate consequence of the corresponding result for real valued functions (using that
J
is a compact interval). With the usual amount of sloppiness, functions will be identified with their
L^{p}
-classes and if a class has a continuous representative, it will be identified with that representative. It is sometimes convenient to consider a dense subspace of ”nice” functions, like the one provided in the following lemma:

Lemma 2.2
.

For
p\in[1,\infty)
, the set

S(J,X):=\left\{\left.\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\phi_{n}x_{n}\right|N\in{\mathbb{N}},\phi_{n}\in C^{\infty}(J,{\mathbb{R}}),x_{n}\in X\right\}

is dense in
L^{p}(J,X)
.

Proof.

As any characteristic function can be approximated by
C^{\infty}
-functions in
L^{p}(J,{\mathbb{R}})
, all finitely valued functions in
L^{p}(J,X)
can be approximated by elements in
S(J,X)
. Finitely valued functions are dense in the countably-valued functions, as
\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\chi_{E_{n}}x_{n}
converges in
L^{p}
to
\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\chi_{E_{n}}x_{n}
by dominated convergence. As uniform convergence almost everywhere implies
L^{p}
-convergence on a compact interval, countably valued functions are dense in
L^{p}(J,X)
by definition of measurability. Putting everything together, we obtain that
S(J,X)
is dense in
L^{p}(J,X)
.
∎

2.5
Compact Operators and Fredholm Operators

Let
X
and
Y
be Hilbert spaces. An operator is called compact if it maps the unit ball into some compact set. The set
K(X,Y)
of compact operators from
X
to
Y
is a closed ideal in
B(X,Y)
. Operators of finite rank (dimension of the range) are dense in
K(X,Y)
.

An operator
F:X\rightarrow Y
is called Fredholm if its kernel
\Ker(F)
and its (vector space) cokernel

\Coker(F)=Y/\Ran(F)

are finite dimensional. In this case, its Fredholm index is defined by

\ind(F)=\Dim(\Ker(F))-\Dim(\Coker(F)).

If
F
is Fredholm,
F
has closed range. If
F
has closed range,

\Coker(F)\cong\Ran(F)^{\bot}=\Ker(F^{*}).

If
F
is a Fredholm operator and
K
is a compact operator,
F+K
is Fredholm and

\ind(F+K)=\ind(F).

If any of the two operators
F
,
G
and
FG
are Fredholm, then so is the third and we have

\ind(FG)=\ind(F)+\ind(G).

The adjoint of a compact operator is compact, the adjoint of a Fredholm operator is Fredholm.
An operator
F\in B(X,Y)
is Fredholm if and only if it is ”invertible up to compact operators”, i.e. there is
G\in B(Y,X)
, such that
FG-I
and
GF-I
are compact. In this case,
G
is called a parametrix for
F
.
An unbounded operator
F
on
X
is called Fredholm, if it is Fredholm in
B(\Dom(F),X)
.

2.6
Fredholm Pairs

Let
P
and
Q
be projections in a Hilbert space (recall that ”projection” means ”orthogonal projection”). The pair
(P,Q)
is called a Fredholm pair, if
[Q:{\Ran(P)}\rightarrow{\Ran(Q)}]
is Fredholm. In this case the index of
(P,Q)
is defined to be the Fredholm index of
[Q:{\Ran(P)}\rightarrow{\Ran(Q)}]
. We shall only need three theorems about Fredholm pairs. More information on them can be found in [AvSeSi].

Lemma 2.3
.

For two projections
P
and
Q
, the following are equivalent

•

(P,Q)
is a Fredholm pair

•

(Q,P)
is a Fredholm pair

•

(I-P,I-Q)
is a Fredholm pair

Proof.

Proposition 3.1 in [AvSeSi] states the
(P,Q)
is Fredholm if and only if 1 and -1 are not in the essential spectrum of
P-Q
. As this is true if and only if 1 and -1 are not in the essential spectrum of

(I-P)-(I-Q)=Q-P,

the claim follows.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

If
P-Q
is compact, then
(P,Q)
is a Fredholm pair.

Proof.

[P:{\Ran(Q)}\rightarrow\Ran(P)]
is a parametrix for
[Q:{\Ran(P)}\rightarrow\Ran(Q)]
, as

(QP-I)|_{\Ran(Q)}=Q(P-Q)|_{\Ran(Q)}

and

(PQ-I)|_{\Ran(P)}=P(Q-P)|_{\Ran(P)}

are compact.
∎

The last theorem is shown in [LE] (lemma 3.2). It states that continuous families of Fredholm pairs have constant index:

Theorem 2.5
.

If
P,Q:[0,1]\rightarrow B(X)
are continuous paths of projections in some Hilbert space
X
, such that
(P(t),Q(t))
is a Fredholm pair for all
t\in[0,1]
. Then

\ind(P(0),Q(0))=\ind(P(1),Q(1)).

2.7
Some Considerations on Families of Operators

This subsection contains some theorems on strongly continuously differentiable families of operators between Banach spaces that are presented here so as to make the presentation of the main argument later on more straightforward. None of the results presented here are particularly surprising or new, the point is to check that things work as expected.

For this whole section, let
X
,
Y
and
Z
be Banach spaces, let
J
be a compact interval and let
S:J\rightarrow B(X,Y)
and
R:J\rightarrow B(Y,Z)
be families of operators.

A family of operators
J\rightarrow B(X,Y)
is called strongly continuous, if it is continuous with respect to the strong operator topology on
B(X,Y)
. It is called strongly continuously differentiable, if it is differentiable with respect to the strong operator topology and the derivative is strongly continuous.

We want to show that (strong) continuity and continuous differentiability of
R
and
S
imply the same for
RS
. Note that this implies the analogous results for evaluation instead of composition: If
f:[0,T]\rightarrow Y
is a function, we can set
S:[0,T]\rightarrow B({\mathbb{C}},Y)
,
S(t)(1)=f(t)
. Then
R(t)f(t)
has the same regularity properties that
R(t)S(t)
has in the strong topology.

Lemma 2.6
.

If
S
is strongly continuous, it is uniformly bounded.

Proof.

As
S(t)x
is continuous in
t
for every
x
,
S
is a pointwise bounded family in
B(X,Y)
. Thus, by the Banach-Steinhaus uniform boundedness principle, it is uniformly bounded.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

If
S
and
T
are strongly continuous, the map
t\mapsto R(t)S(t)
is strongly continuous on
J
.

Proof.

Multiplication of operators is continuous when restricted to subsets on which the operator norm is bounded. As
R
and
S
have bounded range by the previous lemma,
t\mapsto R(t)S(t)
can be viewed as a composition of continuous functions and hence is continuous again (with respect to the strong topology).
∎

The following lemma asserts that the product rule holds for strong differentiation.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
Y_{0}\subseteq Y
be a subspace of
Y
with a stronger norm that turns it into a Banach space. Assume S is a strongly continuous family in
B(X,Y_{0})
, strongly continuously differentiable in
B(X,Y)
. Assume
R
is a strongly continuous family in
B(Y,Z)
that restricts to a strongly differentiable family in
B(Y_{0},Z)
. Then
t\mapsto R(t)S(t)
is strongly continuously differentiable in
B(X,Z)
, with derivative
R^{\prime}(t)S(t)+R(t)S^{\prime}(t)
.

Remark:
Note that this includes the special case
Y_{0}=Y
, in which the theorem asserts that compositions of strongly continuously differentiable functions are strongly continuously differentiable.

Proof.

The calculation is the same as for any continuous bilinear map:

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{h\rightarrow 0}h^{-1}(R(t+h)S(t+h)-R(t)S(t))

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{h\rightarrow 0}h^{-1}(R(t+h)-R(t))S(t+h)+R(t)h^{-1}(S(t+h)-S(t))

Note that multiplication is not continuous in the strong topology, but it is continuous when restricted to bounded subsets. As the Banach-Steinhaus theorem guarantees uniform boundedness of the differential quotients (evaluated at some vector, the differential quotients converge, thus they are bounded) , this is sufficient to pull the limit into the products, viewing the first summand as a composition
X\rightarrow Y_{0}\rightarrow Z
and the second as a composition
X\rightarrow Y\rightarrow Z
. We get that the above is equal to

\displaystyle(\lim\limits_{h\rightarrow 0}h^{-1}(R(t+h)-R(t)))(\lim\limits_{h\rightarrow 0}S(t+h))+R(t)(\lim\limits_{h\rightarrow 0}h^{-1}(S(t+h)-S(t)))

\displaystyle=R^{\prime}(t)S(t)+R(t)S^{\prime}(t)

∎

Lemma 2.9
.

Assume
X_{0}\subseteq X
is a dense subspace. If
S:[0,T]\rightarrow B(X,Y)
is a uniformly bounded family of operators such that its restriction to
X_{0}
is strongly continuous in
B(X_{0},Y)
, then
S
is strongly continuous.

Proof.

This is a standard
\frac{\epsilon}{3}
-argument.
Let
C
be an upper bound for
S
. Given
x\in X
,
s\in[0,T]
and
\epsilon>0
, choose
x^{\prime}\in X_{0}
such that

||x-x^{\prime}||\leq\frac{\epsilon}{3C}.

Choose
\delta>0
such that
|s-t|<\delta
implies

||S(t)x^{\prime}-S(s)x^{\prime}||\leq\frac{\epsilon}{3}.

Then for
|s-t|\leq\epsilon
we have

||S(t)x-S(s)x||\leq||S(t)||||x-x^{\prime}||+||S(s)||||x-x^{\prime}||+||S(t)x^{\prime}-S(s)x^{\prime}||\leq\epsilon,

so
S(t)x
is continuous around
t=s
.
∎

Lemma 2.10
.

If
S
is strongly differentiable, it is norm continuous.

Proof.

Let
t\in J
and let

F:=\left\{\left.\frac{1}{s-t}(S(s)-S(t))\right|s\in J\backslash\{t\}\right\}.

Since S is strongly differentiable,
(\frac{1}{s-t}(S(s)-S(t))x)
is bounded for every
x
. By the Banach-Steinhaus uniform boundedness principle,
F
is uniformly bounded by some constant
C
.
Thus

||S(s)-S(t)||\leq C(s-t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle s\rightarrow t}}{{\rightarrow}}0.

∎

Lemma 2.11
.

If
S
is strongly continuously differentiable and
S(t)
is invertible for all
t\in J
, then the family

\displaystyle S^{-1}:J

\displaystyle\rightarrow B(X,Y)

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle\mapsto S(t)^{-1}

is strongly continuously differentiable in B(Y,X) with derivative
-S^{-1}S^{\prime}S^{-1}
.

Proof.

By the previous theorem,
S
is norm-continuous. As the inversion map is norm-continuous as well,
S^{-1}
is norm-continuous (and in particular uniformly bounded).

Let
t\in J
and
h\in{\mathbb{R}}
small enough such that
t+h\in J
. Then for
y\in Y

\displaystyle(S(t+h)^{-1}-S(t)^{-1})y

\displaystyle=S(t+h)^{-1}(S(t)-S(t+h))S(t)^{-1}y

\displaystyle=-S(t+h)^{-1}(hS^{\prime}(t)S^{-1}(t)y+o(h))

\displaystyle=-hS(t)^{-1}S^{\prime}(t)S(t)^{-1}y+o(h),

which implies the claim.
∎

The point of the next theorem is to show that strong differentiability is preserved when changing domain and range, as long as the derivative permits it.

Theorem 2.12
.

Assume
X_{0}\subseteq X
is a dense subspace and
Y_{0}\subseteq Y
is a subspace with a stricter norm, with respect to which it is complete. Assume additionally that
S(t)\in B(X,Y_{0})
for all
t\in J
and that
t\mapsto S(t)|_{X_{0}}
is strongly differentiable in
B(X_{0},Y)
with a derivative extending to a continuous family
S^{\prime}
in
B(X,Y_{0})
. Then
S
is differentiable with derivative
S^{\prime}
in the strong topology of
B(X,Y_{0})
.

Proof.

For
x\in X_{0}
, we have

S(t)x=S(t_{0})x+\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{t}S^{\prime}(r)xdr

with the integral being taken in
Y
. By the assumptions on
S^{\prime}
, the integral also exists in
Y_{0}
and, as the norm of
Y_{0}
is stricter than that of
Y
, takes the same value. Thus we have the same equality in
Y_{0}
. Moreover, both sides are bounded with respect to the norm of
X
, so the equality holds for all
x\in X
. Thus
S^{\prime}(t)x
is the derivative of
S(t)x
in
Y_{0}
for all
x\in X
.
∎

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
X
,
Y
be separable Hilbert spaces. For
K:X\rightarrow Y
, the following are equivalent:

1.

K
is compact.

2.

For any Hilbert space Z and any sequence of
R_{n}\in B(Y,Z)
converging to a limit
R\in B(Y,Z)
in the strong topology,
R_{n}K
converges to
RK
in the operator norm.

3.

If B is an (ordered) orthonormal Basis of
Y
and
P_{n}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto the span of the first
n
basis vectors (or all, if
\Dim(Y)<n
),
P_{n}K
converges to
K
in norm.

4.

If B is an (ordered) orthonormal Basis of
X
and
P_{n}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto the span of the first
n
basis vectors (or all, if
\Dim(X)<n
),
KP_{n}
converges to
K
in norm.

Remark:
Separabity of Y can be enforced by replacing
Y
with
K(X)
, which is separable if
X
is separable. Thus all items apart from
3.
are still equivalent, if
Y
is not separable.

Proof.

"1\Rightarrow 2":
By Banach-Steinhaus, the family
R_{n}
is uniformly bounded by some constant
C
. If
K
is compact, there is a sequence of finite rank operators converging to
K
in the norm topology. For
\epsilon>0
, choose a finite rank operator
K^{\prime}
such that

||K-K^{\prime}||\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2(C+||R||)}.

As
\Ran(K^{\prime})
is finite dimensional,
R_{n}|_{\Ran(K^{\prime})}
converges to
R|_{\Ran(K^{\prime})}
in norm. Thus we can choose
n_{0}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
, we have

||(R-R_{n})|_{\Ran(K^{\prime})}||\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2||K^{\prime}||}.

Then we get for all
n\geq n_{0}
:

||RK-R_{n}K||\leq||R(K-K^{\prime})||+||R_{n}(K-K^{\prime})||+||(R-R_{n})K^{\prime}||\leq\epsilon.

"2\Rightarrow 3":
This follows immediately, as
P_{n}
converges strongly to the identity.

"3\Rightarrow 1":

P_{n}K
is of finite rank and hence compact. As the space of compact operators is norm closed, the limit
K
is also compact.

"1\Leftrightarrow 4":
We have the following equivalences (using what is already shown):

K
is compact

\Leftrightarrow

K^{*}
is compact

\Leftrightarrow

P_{n}K^{*}
converges to
K^{*}

\Leftrightarrow

KP_{n}=(P_{n}K^{*})^{*}
converges to
K
.
∎

With this we can now show the following:

Theorem 2.14
.

If
S
is strongly continuously differentiable and
S^{\prime}(r)
is compact for all
r\in J
, then
S(t)-S(s)
is compact for all
s,t\in J
.

Proof.

Claim:
S^{\prime}
vanishes on the complement of a separable subspace.

Assume not. Then there is an uncountable orthonormal set
B
with

\sup\limits_{t\in J}||S^{\prime}(t)b||>0

for all
b\in B
. As
(0,\infty)
is the union of conutably many intervals of the form
[2^{k},2^{k+1})
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, the pigeon hole principle implies that there is
\epsilon>0
such that

\sup\limits_{t\in J}||S^{\prime}(t)b||>2\epsilon

for uncountably many
b
. By strong continuity of
S^{\prime}
, there is a rational interval
I_{b}
for each such
b
such that
||S^{\prime}(t)b||>\epsilon
for
t\in I_{b}
. As there are only countably many rational intervals, we can apply the pigeon hole principle again to obtain an interval
I
on which
||S^{\prime}(t)b||>\epsilon
holds for uncountably many
b
. Thus for
t\in I
, the operator
S^{\prime}(t)
is not the norm limit of finite rank operators (which vanish on almost all of the aforementioned
b
), contradicting compactness of
S^{\prime}(t)
.

This proves the claim. Thus we can assume without loss of generality that
X
is separable, otherwise restrict to a separable subspace on whose complement
S^{\prime}(t)
vanishes.

Fix an orthonormal Basis
B
of
X
.
By the previous lemma,
K\in B(X,Y)
is compact if and only if
F_{n}(K):=||K(I-P_{n})||
converges to 0.
As

||(S(t)-S(s))(I-P_{n})(x)||=\left|\left|\int\limits_{s}^{t}S^{\prime}(r)(I-P_{n})xdr\right|\right|\leq\left|\int\limits_{s}^{t}F_{n}(S^{\prime}(r))dr\right|\left|\left|x\right|\right|,

(with absolute values in case
t<s
) we have

F_{n}(S(t)-S(s))\leq\left|\int\limits_{s}^{t}F_{n}(S^{\prime}(r))dr\right|.

By Banach Steinhaus,
S^{\prime}(r)
is uniformly bounded. By Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem, we thus have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\limits_{s}^{t}F_{n}(S^{\prime}(r))dr=\int\limits_{s}^{t}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}(S^{\prime}(r))dr=0.

Thus
F_{n}(S(t)-S(s))
converges to 0, whence
S(t)-S(s)
is compact.
∎

3
Setting and Spectral Flow

3.1
Setting

The objects defined in this section will be used throughout the paper. In particular, the Operator
A
is always assumed to satisfy the following assumptions.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space. Let
W\subseteq H
be a dense subset. Let
A(t)_{t\in[0,T]}
be a family of unbounded self-adjoint Fredholm operators in
H
with domain
W
. Use the graph norm of
A(0)
as the norm of
W
. As
A(0)
is self-adjoint and hence closed,
W
is complete with respect to this norm.

Lemma 3.1
.

All
A(t)
are in
B(W,H)

Proof.

For
t\in[0,T]
, the graph of
A(t)
is closed in
H\times H
. Thus it is also closed with respect to the stricter norm of
W\times H
. By the closed graph theorem,
A(t)
is in
B(W,H)
.
∎

Additionally, assume that
t\mapsto A(t)
is continuously differentiable with respect to the strong operator topology on
B(W,H)
(”strongly continuously differentiable”).

The choice of norm for
W
is somewhat arbitrary, but it doesn’t matter, as the following lemma shows:

Lemma 3.2
.

All
A(t)
have uniformly equivalent graph norms.

Proof.

For any
s\in[0,T]
,
A(s)
is self-adjoint, hence
A(s)+i
is invertible. We have for
x\in W

||x||_{A(t)}=||(A(t)+i)x||\leq||(A(t)+i)(A(s)+i)^{-1}||||x||_{A(s)}

By lemmas
2.6
and
2.11
,
(A(t)+i)(A(s)+i)^{-1}
is uniformly bounded, hence

||x||_{A(t)}\leq C||x||_{A(s)},

with a constant
C
independent of
s
and
t
.
∎

For
s<t\in[0,T]
, let
D_{0}|_{[s,t]}
denote the operator

D_{0}|_{[s,t]}:C^{1}([s,t],W)\rightarrow L^{2}([s,t],H)

(D_{0}|_{[s,t]}f)(r):=f^{\prime}(r)-iA(r)f(r).

Lemma 3.3
.

D_{0}|_{[s,t]}
is closable in
L^{2}([s,t],H)
.

Proof.

For
f\in C^{1}([s,t],W)
and
g\in C^{1}([s,t],W)
with
g(s)=g(t)=0
, we have

\left<g,D_{0}|_{[s,t]}f\right>_{L^{2}}=\left<-D_{0}|_{[s,t]}g,f\right>_{L^{2}}

by partial integration and self-adjointness of
A
. As the space of all such
g
is dense, the adjoint of
D_{0}|_{[s,t]}
is densely defined. This implies that
D_{0}|_{[s,t]}
is closable.
∎

Let
D|_{[s,t]}
denote the closure of
D_{0}|_{[s,t]}
, let
D:=D|_{[0,T]}
.

3.2
The Spectral Flow

Heuristically, the spectral flow of
A
gives the number of eigenvalues of
A(t)
(counted with multiplicities) crossing 0 as
t
varies from
0
to
T
, i.e. the number of negative eigenvalues becoming positive minus that of positive eigenvalues becoming negative. The approach and definition used are from [PH]. The results of this section are formulated for
A
, but everything works for any family of self-adjoint Fredholm operators that is norm continuous in
B(W,H)
. Note that
A
is norm continuous by Lemma
2.10
.

Definition 3.4
.

For an Interval
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
and
t\in[0,T]
, let

P_{I}(t):=\chi_{I}(A(t))

be the corresponding spectral Projection for A(t). Here
\chi_{I}
denotes the characteristic function of
I
.
Let

H_{I}(t):=\Ran(P_{I})

be the corresponding spectral subspace.
For
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
, let

P_{<a}:=P_{(-\infty,a)}

We will need a theorem about how the spectrum and the spectral projections behave under continuous changes of the the operator. The following is an immediate consequence of Theorem 5.12 in chapter 6 of [KA]:

Theorem 3.5
.

For
t\in[0,T]
and
a\in{\mathbb{R}}\backslash\spec(A(t))
, there is a neighborhood
U
of
t
, such that for all
s\in U
we have
a\notin\spec(A(s))
and the spectral projection
P_{<a}(s)
is norm continuous on
U
(as a function of
s
).

To define the spectral flow, we will need finite dimensional spectral subspaces of the form
H_{[0,a)}
. To get those, we need to show that the spectrum of
A(t)
is discrete in a neighborhood of
0
. We will show this first at every point
t
and then for an open cover.

Lemma 3.6
.

For every
t\in[0,T]
, there is
a>0
such that
\spec(A(t))\cap[-a,a]\subseteq\{0\}
. In particular
P_{[-a,a]}(t)
has finite rank.

Proof.

Some
x\in H
is in
\Ran(A(t))^{\bot}
if and only if for all
y\in W
,

\left<x,A(t)y\right>=0.

By definition of the adjoint, this is true if and only if
x
is in the domain of
A(t)^{*}
and

A(t)^{*}x=0.

Thus

\Ran(A(t))^{\bot}=\Ker(A(t)^{*})=\Ker(A(t)).

As
A(t)
is Fredholm, it has closed range, so we get that the restriction of
A(t)
to

A(t)_{r}:=[A(t):W\cap\Ran(A(t))\rightarrow\Ran(A(t))]

produces a surjective map with kernel 0, i.e. an invertible map. As the spectrum of
A(t)_{r}
is closed and does not contain 0, there is
a>0
, such that

\spec(A(t)_{r})\cap[-a,a]=\emptyset

Using the splitting
H=\Ran(A)\oplus\Ker(A)
, we obtain that for non-zero
|\lambda|\leq a
, the operator

A(t)+\lambda=(A(t)_{r}+\lambda)\oplus\lambda id_{\Ker(A(t))}

is invertible, so
\lambda\notin\spec(A(t))
, showing the first claim. For the second claim, note that

P_{[-a,a]}(t)=P_{\{0\}}(t)

is the projection onto
\Ker(A(T))
, which is finite dimensional since
A(t)
is Fredholm.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

If
J\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
is an interval and
P:J\rightarrow B(H)
is a continuous family of projections, the rank of
P
is constant as a map from
J
into
{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
.

Proof.

Let
s,t\in J
be close enough such that
||P(s)-P(t)||<1
.
Assume for contradiction that
P(s)
and
P(t)
do not have the same rank. Without loss of generality, assume that
P(s)
has the smaller rank.
Then
P(t)(\Ran(P(s)))
is a proper subspace of
(\Ran(P(t))
. It is finite dimensional and hence closed, so its complement is non-zero.
Thus there is a non-zero
x\in\Ran(P(t))
such that for all
y\in\Ran(P(s))
, we have

0=\left<x,P(t)y\right>=\left<P(t)x,y\right>=\left<x,y\right>.

This means

x\in\Ran(P(s))^{\bot}=\Ker(P(s)),

whence

||P(s)x-P(t)x||=||x||,

contradicting
||P(s)-P(t)||<1
.
Thus
P(s)
and
P(t)
have the same rank for all
s
in some neighborhood of any
t\in[0,T]
, i.e the rank of
P
is locally constant. As
J
is connected, the rank of
P
is constant.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

For every
t\in[0,T]
, there is a neighborhood
U
of
t
and
a>0
such that
P_{[-a,a]}(s)
has finite rank and
\pm a\notin\spec(A(s))
for all
s\in U
.

Proof.

Choose
a
as in Lemma
3.6
. Then
P_{[-a,a]}(t)
has finite rank and
\pm a\notin\spec(A(t))
. By Theorem
3.5
, there is a neighborhood
U
of
t
such that
\pm a\notin\spec(A(s))
for
s\in U
and

P_{[-a,a]}=P_{<a}-P_{<-a}

is continuous on
U
. By Lemma
3.7
, this implies that
P_{[-a,a]}
has constant, and thus finite, rank on
U
.
∎

For defining the spectral flow, we will need suitable partitions.

Definition 3.9
.

A partition

0=t_{0}<t_{1}<...<t_{N}=T

with numbers
a_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
for
1\leq n\leq N
will be called a flow partition (for
A
), if

for all
n\leq N
and
t\in[t_{n-1},t_{n}]
we have
a_{n}\notin\spec(A(t))
and
H_{[0,a_{n})}(t)
is finite dimensional.

Theorem 3.10
.

There is a flow partition for
A
. This can be chosen in such a way that it is also a flow partition for
-A
.

Proof.

For every
t\in[0,T]
, choose a neighborhood
U_{t}
with
b_{t}>0
, such that
P_{[-b_{t},b_{t}]}(s)
has finite range and
\pm b_{t}\notin\spec(A(s))
for all
s\in U_{t}
(using Lemma
3.8
).
By Lebesgue’s number lemma, there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that every interval of length less that
\frac{1}{N}
is contained in one of the
U_{t}
. Let
t_{n}:=\frac{nT}{N}
.
Let
a_{n}:=b_{t}
for some
t
such that
[t_{n-1},t_{n}]\subseteq U_{t}
. Then the
t_{n}
and
a_{n}
form a flow partition. As we also have
-a_{n}\notin\spec(A(t))
for
t\in[t_{n-1},t_{n}]
, the
t_{n}
and
a_{n}
also form a flow partition for
-A
.
∎

Definition 3.11
.

If
(t_{n})_{0\leq n\leq N},(a_{n})_{1\leq n\leq N}
is a flow partition for
A(t)
, the spectral flow is defined as

\sfl(A)=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\Dim(H_{[0,a_{n})}(t_{n}))-\Dim(H_{[0,a_{n})}(t_{n-1}))

The intuition behind this is that, since
a_{n}
is a spectral gap, all eigenvalues entering or leaving
[0,a_{n})
must do so through 0. The following theorem shows that the spectral flow is well-defined.

Theorem 3.12
.

The spectral flow is independent of the choice of flow partition.

Proof.

For a flow partition
(t_{n}),(a_{n})
, define
\alpha(t):=a_{n}
for
t\in[t_{n-1},t_{n}]
. As

\displaystyle\Dim(H_{[0,a_{n})}(t_{n}))-\Dim(H_{[0,a_{n})}(t_{n-1}))

\displaystyle=\Dim(H_{[0,a_{n})}(t_{n}))-\Dim(H_{[0,a_{n})}(\tau))+\Dim(H_{[0,a_{n})}(\tau))-\Dim(H_{[0,a_{n})}(t_{n-1})),

inserting an additional point
\tau
in the flow partition while choosing the
a_{n}
such that
\alpha
remains unchanged does not change the spectral flow. Thus any two flow partitions can be refined to have the same
(t_{n})
without changing their spectral flow.

Assume
(t_{n}),(a^{\prime}_{n})
is another partition with the same
(t_{n})
as the first. Let
n\leq N
and assume w.l.o.g.
a^{\prime}_{n}\geq a_{n}
(otherwise interchange
a_{n}
and
a^{\prime}_{n}
in the following argument). The projection

P_{[a_{n},a^{\prime}_{n})}=P_{<a^{\prime}_{n}}-P_{<a_{n}}

has constant rank on
[t_{n-1},t_{n}]
by Theorem
3.5
and Lemma
3.7
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\Dim(H_{[0,a^{\prime}_{n})}(t_{n}))-\Dim(H_{[0,a^{\prime}_{n})}(t_{n-1}))

\displaystyle=\Dim(H_{[0,a_{n})}(t_{n}))+\Dim(H_{[a_{n},a^{\prime}_{n})}(t_{n}))-\Dim(H_{[0,a_{n})}(t_{n-1}))-\Dim(H_{[a_{n},a^{\prime}_{n})}(t_{n-1}))

\displaystyle=\Dim(H_{[0,a_{n})}(t_{n}))-\Dim(H_{[0,a_{n})}(t_{n-1})).

As this equality holds for all
n
, changing the
a_{n}
does not change the spectral flow either, so it is the same for all flow partitions.
∎

From the definition it is obvious that the spectral flow is additive in the sense that for
r<s<t\in[0,T]
, we have

\sfl(A|_{[r,t]})=\sfl(A|_{[r,s]})+\sfl(A|_{[s,t]}).

The spectral flow is antisymmetric under sign change, up to counting the kernel twice:

Lemma 3.13
.

If
(t_{n})
is a partition with
a_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
such that
-a_{n}\notin\spec(A(t))
for all
t\in[t_{n-1},t_{n}]
(in other words,
(t_{n})
,
(a_{n})
is a flow partition for
-A
), then the spectral flow of
A
is

\sfl(A)=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\Dim(H_{[-a_{n},0)}(t_{n-1}))-\Dim(H_{[-a_{n},0)}(t_{n})),

or equivalently

\sfl(A)=-\sfl(-A)+\Dim\Ker(A(T))-\Dim\Ker(A(0)).

Proof.

Choose a flow partition
(t_{n}),(a_{n})
for
A
that is also a flow partition for
-A
(using Theorem
3.10
). The projection

P_{[-a_{n},a_{n})}

has constant rank on
[t_{n-1},t_{n}]
by Theorem
3.5
and Lemma
3.7
(as above).
Thus

\displaystyle\sfl(A)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\Dim(H_{[0,a_{n})}(t_{n}))-\Dim(H_{[0,a_{n})}(t_{n-1}))

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\left(\Dim(H_{[-a_{n},a_{n})}(t_{n}))-\Dim(H_{[-a_{n},0)}(t_{n}))\right.

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \left.-\Dim(H_{[-a_{n},a_{n})}(t_{n-1}))+\Dim(H_{[-a_{n},0)}(t_{n-1}))\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}-(\Dim(H_{[-a_{n},0)}(t_{n}))-\Dim(H_{[-a_{n},0)}(t_{n-1}))).

As the spectral subspace of
A
corresponding to an interval
I
is that of
-A
corresponding to
-I
, we have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}-(\Dim(H_{[-a_{n},0)}(t_{n}))-\Dim(H_{[-a_{n},0)}(t_{n-1})))

\displaystyle=-\sfl(-A)+\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\Dim(H_{\{0\}}(t_{n}))-\Dim(H_{\{0\}}(t_{n-1}))

\displaystyle=-\sfl(-A)+\Dim\Ker(A(T))-\Dim\Ker(A(0)).

As the last line is independent of the choice of flow partition and the second set of equalities holds for any flow partition of
-A
, the theorem holds for other flow partitions of
-A
as well.
∎

The spectral flow is unchanged by conjugating with unitaries:

Theorem 3.14
.

Let
(U(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
be a family of unitary operators such that spectral flow is defined for
t\mapsto U(t)^{*}A(t)U(t)
. Then

\sfl(U^{*}AU)=\sfl(A)

Proof.

\chi_{[0,a)}(U^{*}(t)A(t)U(t))=U^{*}(t)P_{[0,a)}(t)U(t)

is the projection onto
U^{*}(t)H_{[0,a)}(t)
, which has the same dimension as
H_{[0,a)}(t)
. Thus all summands in the definition of the spectral flow are unchanged by the conjugation, so the spectral flow is the same.
∎

Remark:
The condition that
\sfl(U^{*}AU)
is defined is purely formal. The defining formula for the spectral flow will always be well defined, because it takes the same values as for
A
.

The following theorem will be the first step toward proving the main theorem. Its proof uses arguments from section 4.2 of [BäSt] reformulated in terms of spectral projections and combines them with theorems
3.5
and
2.5
.

Theorem 3.15
.

If
(P_{<0}(0),P_{<0}(t))
is a Fredholm pair for all
t\in[0,T]
, we have

\sfl(A)=\ind(P_{<0}(0),P_{<0}(T)).

Proof.

Let
(t_{n})
,
(a_{n})
be a flow partition for
A
.

The idea of the proof is to consider the pair
(P_{<0}(0),P_{<a_{n}}(t))
for
t\in[t_{n-1},t_{n}]
, and show that it is Fredholm with constant index in
t
on
[t_{n-1},t_{n}]
. Then we will compare its index to that of the pair
(P_{<0}(0),P_{<0}(t))
. As the index of the former pair is constant on
[t_{n-1},t_{n}]
, changes in the latter index, as
t
varies over
[t_{n-1},t_{n}]
, are the same as changes in the difference of the two indices. These will correspond exactly to the spectral flow over that interval.

For any projection
P
let
P_{r}
denote the restriction

P_{r}:=[P:{\Ran(P_{<0}(0))}\rightarrow{\Ran(P)}]\ .

By the definition of Fredholm pairs and their index, the pair
(P_{<0}(0),P)
is Fredholm with index k if and only if
P_{r}
is Fredholm with index k.

Fix
n\leq N
. For
t\in[t_{n-1},t_{n}]
, define

P_{t}:=[P_{<0}(t):{\Ran(P_{<a_{n}}(t))}\rightarrow{\Ran(P_{<0}(t))}]=[P_{<0}(t):{H_{(-\infty,a_{n})}(t)}\rightarrow{H_{(-\infty,0)}(t)}].

As
H_{(-\infty,0)}(t)
is a subspace of
H_{(-\infty,a_{n})}(t)
with complement
H_{[0,a_{n})}(t)
, this is Fredholm with index

\ind(P_{t})=\Dim(\Ker(P_{t}))=\Dim(H_{[0,a_{n})}(t)).

As projecting onto
H_{(-\infty,a_{n})}(t)
and then onto
H_{(-\infty,0)}(t)
is the same as projecting onto
H_{(-\infty,0)}(t)
immediately, we have

P_{<0}(t)_{r}=P_{t}P_{<a_{n}}(t)_{r}.

As the first two projections in the equation are Fredholm,

P_{<a_{n}}(t)_{r}
is Fredholm as well, and we have

\ind(P_{<0}(t)_{r})=\ind(P_{t})+\ind(P_{<a_{n}}(t)_{r})=\Dim(H_{[0,a_{n})}(t))+\ind(P_{<a_{n}}(t)_{r}).

By Theorem
3.5
,
P_{<a_{n}}(t)_{r}
is continuous in
t
on
[t_{n-1},t_{n}]
. By Theorem
2.5
,
(P_{<0}(0),P_{<a_{n}}(t))
has constant index for
t\in[t_{n-1},t_{n}]
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\ind(P_{<a_{n}}(t_{n})_{r})=\ind(P_{<0}(0),P_{<a_{n}}(t_{n}))=\ind(P_{<0}(0),P_{<a_{n}}(t_{n-1}))=\ind(P_{<a_{n}}(t_{n-1})_{r})

Moreover, as
P_{<0}(0)_{r}
is the identity on
H_{(-\infty,0)}(0)
, it has index 0.

Combining everything, we get:

\displaystyle\ind(P_{<0}(0),P_{<0}(T))

\displaystyle=\ind(P_{<0}(T)_{r})

\displaystyle=\ind(P_{<0}(T)_{r})-\ind(P_{<0}(0)_{r})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\ind(P_{<0}(t_{n})_{r})-\ind(P_{<0}(t_{n-1})_{r})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\Dim(H_{[0,a_{n})}(t_{n}))+\ind(P_{<a_{n}}(t_{n})_{r})-\Dim(H_{[0,a_{n})}(t_{n-1}))-\ind(P_{<a_{n}}(t_{n-1})_{r})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\Dim(H_{[0,a_{n})}(t_{n}))-\Dim(H_{[0,a_{n})}(t_{n-1}))

\displaystyle=\sfl(A).

∎

4
The Evolution Operator and the Cauchy Property

Recall the setting from section
3.1
.
The purpose of this section is to show that for given
g\in L^{2}([0,T],H)
,
s\in[0,T]
and
x\in H
, the equations

Df=g

f(s)=x\

have a unique solution
f
. This will be called the Cauchy property. It basically follows from chapter 5 in [PA], but since the results needed here are slightly different from those derived in [PA] and some things are easier in this setting than in the one of [PA], a slightly adapted version of the arguments in [PA] will be presented below.

4.1
The Evolution Operator

The family
Q
constructed in the following theorem will be called the evolution operator. It will be used throughout the rest of the paper. Its construction will occupy this subsection.

Theorem 4.1
.

There is a family of operators
Q(t,s):H\rightarrow H
for
s,t\in[0,T]
, satisfying the following conditions (for all
s,t,r\in[0,T]
):

1.

Q(t,t)=I

2.

Q(t,s)Q(s,r)=Q(t,r)

3.

Q(t,s)
is an isometry (of
H
).

4.

Q(t,s)(W)\subseteq W
and
Q(t,s):W\rightarrow W
is bounded.

5.

Q
is strongly continuously differentiable in
B(W,H)
with derivatives

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}Q(t,s)=iA(t)Q(t,s)

and

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}Q(t,s)=-Q(t,s)iA(s).

6.

Q(t,s)x
(as a function of
s
and
t
) is continuous in
H
for
x\in H
and continuous in W for
x\in W
.

Remark:
The function

. Applying

can thus be thought of evolving from

or

.

Proof.

For a constant family

, the family

has all the above properties. The idea for constructing

Lemma 4.2
.

The family
Q(t,s)=exp((t-s)iA_{0})
has the properties required in Theorem
4.1
for the constant family
A(t)=A_{0}
. Moreover, it is an isometry of
W
with respect to
||\cdot||_{A_{0}}
.

Proof.

By a version of the Spectral Theorem, there is a finite measure space
(M,\mu)
with a measurable function
\alpha:M\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
and a unitary map
U:L^{2}(M)\rightarrow H
such that

U^{*}A_{0}Uf=\alpha f,

for

f\in U^{*}(\Dom(A))=\Dom(U^{*}AU)=\{f\in L^{2}(M)|\alpha f\in L^{2}(M)\}

i.e.
A_{0}
is unitarily equivalent to a multiplication operator. The exponential function then takes the form

U^{*}exp(i(t-s)A_{0})Uf=exp(i(t-s)\alpha)f

for
f\in L^{2}(M)
.

As the properties of Theorem
4.1
are preserved under conjugating with
U
, when replacing
A(t)
with
U^{*}A_{0}U
,
W
with
\Dom(U^{*}A_{0}U)
and
H
with
L^{2}(M)
, it suffices to show the properties for multiplication with
exp(i(t-s)\alpha)
with those replacements.

As multiplication with
exp(i(t-s)\alpha)
has properties 1. and 2., so does
exp(i(t-s)A_{0})
. As the
L^{2}
-norm of a function only depends on its absolute value and we have for any
r\in M

|exp(i(t-s)\alpha(r))f(r)|=|f(r)|

and

|\alpha(r)exp(i(t-s)\alpha(r))f(r)|=|\alpha(r)f(r)|,

we know that
U^{*}exp(i(t-s)A_{0})U
is an isometry of
L^{2}(M)
and
\Dom(U^{*}AU)
. We can conclude that
exp(i(t-s)A_{0})
is an isometry of
H
and
W
.

This leaves us with strong continuity and differentiability to show. Note that it suffices to show these for
t
, as changes in
s
have the same effect as changes in
t
with opposite sign. We’ll start with continuity.

Fix
f\in L^{2}(M)
and
\epsilon>0
. Let
b>0
be large enough such that

\int\limits_{|\alpha|^{-1}((b,\infty))}|2f|^{2}d\mu<\epsilon.

For
r\in\alpha^{-1}([-b,b])
,

|exp(i(t+h-s)\alpha(r))-exp(i(t-s)\alpha(r))|=\left|\int\limits_{0}^{h}i\alpha(r)exp(i(t+u-s)\alpha(r))du\right|\leq hb

converges to 0 uniformly for
h\rightarrow 0
. Thus

\displaystyle\limsup\limits_{h\rightarrow 0}||(exp(i(t+h-s)\alpha)-exp(i(t-s)\alpha))f||_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup\limits_{h\rightarrow 0}\sup\limits_{r\in\alpha^{-1}([-b,b])}|exp(i(t+h-s)\alpha(r))-exp(i(t-s)\alpha(r))|^{2}\int\limits_{\alpha^{-1}([-b,b])}|f|^{2}d\mu

\displaystyle+\int\limits_{|\alpha|^{-1}((b,\infty))}|(exp(i(t+h-s)\alpha)-exp(i(t-s)\alpha))f|^{2}d\mu

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{|\alpha|^{-1}((b,\infty))}|2f|^{2}d\mu

\displaystyle<\epsilon.

As
\epsilon
was arbitrary,
exp(i(t+h-s)\alpha)f
converges to
exp(i(t-s)\alpha)f
in
L^{2}(M)
. If
\alpha f
is square integrable (i.e.
f\in\Dom(U^{*}A_{0}U)
), the same holds for
\alpha f
instead of
f
. Thus multiplication with
exp(i(t-s)\alpha)
is strongly continuous on
L^{2}(M)
and
\Dom(U^{*}A_{0}U)
. We can conclude that
exp(i(t-s)A_{0})
is strongly continuous on
H
and
W
.

For strong differentiability, pick
f
such that
\alpha f\in L^{2}(M)
. For
\epsilon>0
, choose
\delta>0
such that for
|u|<\delta
, we have

||(exp(i(t+u-s)\alpha)-exp(i(t-s)\alpha))\alpha f||_{L^{2}(M)}<\epsilon.

This is possible by the strong continuity shown in the previous paragraph. For
|h|<\delta
, we have

\displaystyle||exp(i(t+h-s)\alpha)f-exp(i(t-s)\alpha)f-hi\alpha exp(i(t-s)\alpha)f||_{L^{2}(M)}

\displaystyle=\left(\int\limits_{M}\left|\int\limits_{0}^{h}i\alpha(r)(exp(i(t+u-s)\alpha(r))-exp(i(t-s)\alpha(r)))f(r)du\right|^{2}d\mu(r)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Using Minkowski’s integral inequality, we can estimate this by

\displaystyle\left|\int\limits_{0}^{h}||i\alpha(exp(i(t+u-s)\alpha)-exp(i(t-s)\alpha))f||_{L^{2}(M)}du\right|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon|h|.

Thus
exp(i(t-s)\alpha)f
is differentiable in
L^{2}(H)
for all
f\in\Dom(U^{*}A_{0}U)
with derivatives

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}exp(i(t-s)\alpha)f=-\frac{\partial}{\partial s}exp(i(t-s)\alpha)f=i\alpha exp(i(t-s)\alpha)f=exp(i(t-s)\alpha)i\alpha f.

We can conclude that
exp(i(t-s)A_{0})
is strongly differentiable in
B(W,H)
with the desired derivatives (which are strongly continuous).
Thus we have all the desired properties.
∎

For
k\leq n\in{\mathbb{N}}

For

For

The product is to be ordered right to left, i.e.

As the definitions of

with

is strongly continuous in
t
. The same is true for
s
. Moreover, if
t
and

Lemma 4.3
.

||Q_{n}(s,t)||_{W\rightarrow W}
is bounded by a constant independent of
s
,
t
and
n
.

Proof.

For
x\in W
with
||x||=1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{||x||_{A(t_{k}^{n})}^{2}}{||x||_{A(t_{k-1}^{n})}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+||A(t_{k}^{n})x||^{2}}{1+||A(t_{k-1}^{n})x||^{2}}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{||A(t_{k}^{n})x||^{2}-||A(t_{k-1}^{n})x||^{2}}{1+||A(t_{k-1}^{n})x||^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+|||A(t_{k}^{n})x||^{2}-||A(t_{k-1}^{n})x||^{2}|

\displaystyle\leq 1+\left|\ \int\limits_{t_{k-1}^{n}}^{t_{k}^{n}}\frac{d}{dr}||A(r)x||^{2}dr\right|

\displaystyle\leq 1+\int\limits_{t_{k-1}^{n}}^{t_{k}^{n}}2||A(r)||_{W\rightarrow H}||A^{\prime}(r)||_{W\rightarrow H}dr

\displaystyle=:1+\Delta_{k}^{n},

where
\Delta_{k}^{n}
is defined by the last equality.
For
s,t\in[t_{k-1}^{n},t_{k}^{n}]
,
Q_{n}(t,s)
is an isometry with respect to
||\cdot||_{A(t_{k}^{n})}
.
From this we obtain for
s\in[t_{k-1}^{n},t_{k}^{n}]
,
t\in[t_{l-1}^{n},t_{l}^{n}]
,
k<l
(the cases
k=l
and
k>l
work in the same way):

\displaystyle||Q_{n}(t,s)||_{||\cdot||_{A(t_{k}^{n})}\rightarrow||\cdot||_{A(t_{l}^{n})}}

\displaystyle\leq||Q_{n}(t,t_{l}^{n})||_{||\cdot||_{A(t_{l}^{n})}\rightarrow||\cdot||_{A(t_{l}^{n})}}\left(\prod\limits_{j=k+1}^{l}||Q_{n}(t_{j}^{n},t_{j-1}^{n})||_{||\cdot||_{A(t_{j}^{n})}}||id||_{||\cdot||_{A(t_{j-1}^{n})}\rightarrow||\cdot||_{A(t_{j}^{n})}}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot||Q_{n}(t_{k}^{n},s)||_{||\cdot||_{A(t_{k}^{n})}\rightarrow||\cdot||_{A(t_{k}^{n})}}

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{j=k+1}^{l-1}||id||_{||\cdot||_{A(t_{j-1}^{n})}\rightarrow||\cdot||_{A(t_{j}^{n})}}

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{j=k+1}^{l-1}(1+\Delta_{j}^{n})^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Write

The inequality of arithmetic and geometric mean gives

\prod\limits_{j=k+1}^{l-1}(1+\Delta_{j}^{n})^{\frac{1}{l-k-1}}\leq\frac{1}{l-k-1}\sum\limits_{j=k+1}^{l-1}(1+\Delta_{j}^{n}).

Thus we can estimate

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{j=k+1}^{l-1}(1+\Delta_{j}^{n})^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{l-k-1}\sum\limits_{j=k+1}^{l-1}(1+\Delta_{j}^{n})\right)^{\frac{l-k-1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{\Delta}{l-k-1}\right)^{\frac{l-k-1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{\Delta}{l-k-1}\right)^{\frac{l-k-1}{\Delta}\frac{\Delta}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sup\limits_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(\left(1+\frac{\Delta}{m}\right)^{\frac{m}{\Delta}}\right)\right)^{\frac{\Delta}{2}}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{\Delta}{2}},

using a suitable characterization of Euler’s number

.
As all Graph norms of

are uniformly equivalent by Lemma

and

,

and

We are interested in taking the limit for

\displaystyle=\left|\left|Q_{n}(t,s)Q_{m}(s,s)x-Q_{n}(t,t)Q_{m}(t,s)x\right|\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left|\int\limits_{s}^{t}Q_{n}(t,r)(iA_{m}(r)-iA_{n}(r))Q_{m}(r,s)xdr\right|\right|

Here we used Lemma

for existence and form of the derivative. Arguments like this one will be used several times in his section to estimate differences.

By the above estimate, we can conclude that

To show condition 4. and the second part of condition 6., we need the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
E,F:[0,T]\times[0,T]\rightarrow B(H)
are strongly continuous families of operators, there is a unique, strongly continuous family
V:[0,T]\times[0,T]\rightarrow B(H)
such that for all
x\in H
, we have

V(t,s)x=E(t,s)x+\int\limits_{s}^{t}V(t,r)F(r,s)xdr

Proof.

Let

V_{0}:=E

and define inductively

V_{m}(t,s)x:=\int\limits_{s}^{t}{V_{m-1}}(t,r)F(r,s)xdr.

Claim:

||V_{m}(t,s)||\leq CK^{m}\frac{(t-s)^{m}}{m!},

for all
s,t\in[0,T]
, with

C:=sup_{s,t\in[0,T]}||E(t,s)||

and

K:=sup_{s,t\in[0,T]}||F(t,s)||.

Note that
K
and
C
are finite by Banach-Steinhaus. We will prove the claim by induction.
||V_{0}(t,s)||\leq C
holds by definition. Assume the claim holds for all
m<n
. Then we have

\displaystyle||V_{n}(t,s)x||\leq\int\limits_{s}^{t}\left|\left|V_{n-1}(t,r)\right|\right|\left|\left|F(r,s)\right|\right|||x||dr

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{s}^{t}CK^{n-1}\frac{(t-r)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}K||x||dr

\displaystyle=CK^{n}\frac{(t-s)^{n}}{n!}||x||,

concluding the induction and proving the claim. The claim implies that the series

V(t,s):=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}V_{m}(t,s)x,

converges uniformly in
t
,
s
and
x
for fixed
||x||
. The operators
V(t,s)
defined this way are thus a strongly continuous family of bounded operators. Moreover, uniform convergence allows us to interchange the infinite sum with an integral over a compact interval. Thus we get

\displaystyle E(t,s)+\int\limits_{s}^{t}V(t,r)F(r,s)xdr

\displaystyle=V_{0}(t,s)x+\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{s}^{t}V_{m}(t,r)F(r,s)xdr

\displaystyle=V_{0}(t,s)x+\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}V_{m+1}(t,s)x

\displaystyle=V(t,s)x,

concluding the proof of existence. For uniqueness, assume
U
was another solution to the equation.
Then we have

U(t,s)x-V(t,s)x=\int\limits_{s}^{t}(U(t,r)-V(t,r))F(r,s)xdr.

The norm of a strongly continuous family is lower semi-continuous and hence measurable. Since
U(t,r)
and
V(t,r)
are uniformly bounded by Banach-Steinhaus, the norm of their difference is bounded and hence integrable.
Thus we can estimate

||U(t,s)-V(t,s)||\leq K\int\limits_{s}^{t}||V(t,r)-U(t,r)||dr.

By Gronwalls inequality, this implies
U(t,s)-V(t,s)=0
, proving uniqueness.
∎

With the help of this, we shall now show the following lemma.

Lemma 4.5
.

Q(t,s)
maps
W
to
W
boundedly and
(t,s)\mapsto Q(t,s)|_{W}
is a strongly continuous family in
B(W)
.

Proof.

Let
R(t):=(A(t)+i)^{-1}
. As
R
is strongly differentiable as a map

by

, the composition
Q_{n}(t,r)R(r)Q_{n}(r,s)
is strongly differentiable in
B(W,H)
as a function of
r
, except at

, where it is still strongly continuous. We can calculate for

\displaystyle R(t)Q(t,s)x-Q(t,s)R(s)x

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}R(t)Q_{n}(t,s)x-Q_{n}(t,s)R(s)x

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\limits_{s}^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(Q_{n}(t,r)R(r)Q_{n}(r,s)x)dr

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\limits_{s}^{t}Q_{n}(t,r)(-iA(r)R(r)+R^{\prime}(r)+R(r)iA(r))Q_{n}(r,s)xdr

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\limits_{s}^{t}Q_{n}(t,r)R^{\prime}(r)Q_{n}(r,s)xdr

As the
Q_{n}
are unitary in
B(H)
and
R^{\prime}(r)
is strongly continuous and hence uniformly bounded, the integrand is bounded, so we can apply dominated convergence to pull the limit into the integral. We get

\displaystyle R(t)Q(t,s)x-Q(t,s)R(s)x

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{s}^{t}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}Q_{n}(t,r)R^{\prime}(r)Q_{n}(r,s)xdr

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{s}^{t}Q(t,r)R^{\prime}(r)Q(r,s)xdr

\displaystyle=-\int\limits_{s}^{t}Q(t,r)R(r)A^{\prime}(r)R(r)Q(r,s)xdr

which we can reformulate as

Q(t,s)R(s)x=R(t)Q(t,s)x+\int\limits_{s}^{t}Q(t,r)R(r)A^{\prime}(r)R(r)Q(r,s)xdr.

Let
V(t,s)
be the unique family satisfying

V(t,s)x=Q(t,s)x+\int\limits_{s}^{t}V(t,r)A^{\prime}(r)R(r)Q(r,s)xdr

for all
x\in H
, whose existence is guaranteed by Lemma
4.4
with
E:=Q
and
F(t,s):=A^{\prime}(t)R(t)Q(t,s)
.
Then both
H(t,s)=Q(t,s)R(s)
and
H(t,s)=R(t)V(t,s)
are solutions to

H(t,s)=R(t)Q(t,s)+\int\limits_{s}^{t}H(t,r)A^{\prime}(r)R(r)Q(r,s)xdr.

By the uniqueness in Lemma
4.4
(this time with
E(t,s)=R(t)Q(t,s)
), this means

Q(t,s)R(s)=R(t)V(t,s).

Reformulating this as

Q(t,s)|_{W}=R(t)V(t,s)R(s)^{-1}

yields the desired result, as
R
,
V
and
R^{-1}
are strongly continuous in
B(H,W)
,
B(H)
and
B(W,H)
respectively.
∎

The last remaining condition to check is that

has the desired strong derivatives.

Lemma 4.6
.

Q(t,s)|_{W}
is strongly continuously differentiable in both
s
and
t
as a function into
B(W,H)
, with derivatives

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}Q(t,s)=iA(t)Q(t,s)

and

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}Q(t,s)=-Q(t,s)iA(s).

Proof.

The goal is to show that at
t=s
,
Q(t,s)
has the same derivatives as

exp((t-s)iA(t))
. For this we need to estimate the difference around
s=t
. We have for
x\in W
:

\displaystyle exp((t-s)iA(t))x-Q_{n}(t,s)x

\displaystyle=-\int\limits_{s}^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(exp((t-r)iA(t))Q_{n}(r,s)x)dr

\displaystyle=-\int\limits_{s}^{t}exp((t-r)iA(t))i(-A(t)+A_{n}(r))Q_{n}(r,s)xdr

Using that
exp((t-s)iA(t))
is an isometry on
H
and
||Q_{n}(t,s)||_{W\rightarrow W}
has a uniform bound C by Lemma
4.3
, we have

||exp((t-s)iA(t))x-Q_{n}(t,s)x||\leq C||x||\int\limits_{s}^{t}||A_{n}(r)-A(t)||_{W\rightarrow H}dr.

Taking the limit and using dominated convergence (as
A(t)
and hence also
A_{n}(t)
are uniformly bounded in
B(W,H)
), we obtain

\displaystyle||exp((t-s)iA(t))x-Q(t,s)x||

\displaystyle\leq C||x||\int\limits_{s}^{t}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}||A_{n}(r)-A(t)||_{W\rightarrow H}dr

\displaystyle=C||x||\int\limits_{s}^{t}||A(r)-A(t)||_{W\rightarrow H}dr.

As
A
is norm continuous in
B(W,H)
, we know that

I_{max}(s,t):=\sup\limits_{r\in[s,t]}||A(r)-A(t)||_{W\rightarrow H}

converges to 0 for
|t-s|\rightarrow 0
, both for fixed
t
and for fixed

. Thus

|t-s|^{-1}||exp((t-s)iA(t))x-Q(t,s)x||\leq C||x||I_{max}(s,t)

converges to 0 as well, so we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(exp((t-s)iA(t))x-Q(t,s)x)|_{t=s}=\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow s}\ (t-s)^{-1}(exp((t-s)iA(t))x-Q(t,s)x)=0

and

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}(exp((t-s)iA(t))x-Q(t,s)x)|_{s=t}=\lim\limits_{s\rightarrow t}\ (s-t)^{-1}(exp((t-s)iA(t))x-Q(t,s)x)=0.

We can conclude that
Q(t,s)
is strongly differentiable both in
s
and in
t
at
t=s
with

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}Q(t,s)|_{t=s}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}exp((t-s)iA(t))|_{t=s}=iA(s)

and

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}Q(t,s)|_{s=t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}exp((t-s)iA(t))|_{s=t}=-iA(t).

The result for
s\neq t
now follows, as

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}Q(t,s)=\frac{\partial}{\partial r}Q(r,t)Q(t,s)|_{r=t}=iA(t)Q(t,s)

and

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}Q(t,s)=\frac{\partial}{\partial r}Q(t,s)Q(s,r)|_{r=s}=-Q(t,s)iA(s).

∎

This concludes the proof of Theorem
4.1
.
∎

Theorem 4.7
.

For every
s\in[0,T]
and
Q
as constructed above,
U(t)=Q(t,s)|_{W}
is the only strongly continuous family of operators in
B(W)
that is strongly differentiable in
B(W,H)
and satisfies

U(s)=I

and

\frac{d}{dt}U(t)=iA(t)U(t).

In particular,
Q
is the unique family satisfying the conditions of Theorem
4.1
.

Proof.

Assume
U
is a family satisfying the conditions given above. Then we can calculate for any
x\in H
:

\displaystyle U(t)x-Q(t,s)x

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{s}^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}Q(t,r)U(r)xdr

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{s}^{t}Q(t,r)(-iA(r)+iA(r))U(r)xdr

\displaystyle=0.

∎

4.2
The Cauchy Property

Q(t,s)x
can be thought of as taking the function
f:[0,T]\rightarrow H
satisfying
f(s)=x
and
Df=0
and evaluating it at
t
, where in this case
f=Q(\cdot,s)
. This can be generalized in the following definition

Definition 4.8
.

Denote by
ev_{t}
the evaluation map at
t
, i.e.

ev_{t}(f):=f(t).

We say that a closed unbounded operator
R
in
L^{2}([0,T],H)
has the Cauchy property, if
\Dom(R)
is a subset of
C([0,T],H)
(with maximum norm) with bounded inclusion and for all
t\in[0,T]
the map

R\oplus ev_{t}:\Dom(R)\rightarrow L^{2}([0,T],H)\oplus H

is an isomorphism.

Remark:
Another way of thinking about the Cauchy property is to say that for each
s\in[0,T]
,
x\in H
and
g\in L^{2}([0,T],H)
, the initial value problem

Rf=g

f(s)=x

has a unique solution (in
\Dom(R)
).
For any
R
with the Cauchy property, the evolution operator
Q(t,s):H\rightarrow H
for
t,s\in[0,T]
can be defined by

Q(t,s)x=ev_{t}(R\oplus ev_{s})^{-1}(0,x).

If
R=D
has the Cauchy property, this agrees with the previous definition of
Q
, as

(D\oplus ev_{s})Q(\cdot,s)x=(0,x)

and hence

ev_{t}(R\oplus ev_{s})^{-1}(0,x)=ev_{t}(Q(\cdot,s)x)=Q(t,s)x.

We will now show that
D
has the Cauchy property, starting with the following lemma:

Lemma 4.9
.

\Dom(D)
(with graph norm) is a subspace of
C^{0}([0,T],H)
and the inclusion is continuous.

Remark:
This implies that evaluation at some point is well-defined and bounded on
\Dom(D)
.

Proof.

For
f\in C^{1}([0,T],W)
and
t\in[0,T]
, we want to estimate
f(t)
in terms of
||f||_{L^{2}}
and
||Df||_{L^{2}}
. The idea is, that a bound on the
L^{2}
norm prevents the function from being large everywhere and
Df
gives us a bound on the derivative of
||f||
, so
f
cannot be large on a small region only. The first observation is that
D
is as good as the derivative when estimating the change in the norm. As
A(t)
is self-adjoint,
\left<x,A(t)x\right>
is real for all
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in W
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}||f(t)||^{2}

\displaystyle=2Re(\left<f(t),f^{\prime}(t)\right>)

\displaystyle=2Re(\left<f(t),f^{\prime}(t)\right>)-2Re(i\left<f(t),A(t)f(t)\right>)

\displaystyle=2Re(\left<f(t),f^{\prime}(t)-iA(t)f(t)\right>)

\displaystyle=2Re(\left<f(t),Df(t)\right>).

We can now estimate for any
t\in[0,T]
:

\displaystyle T\left|\left|f(t)\right|\right|^{2}-\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{T}\left|\left|f(t)\right|\right|^{2}-\left|\left|f(s)\right|\right|^{2}ds

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{T}\int\limits_{s}^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left|\left|f(r)\right|\right|^{2}drds

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{T}\int\limits_{s}^{t}2Re(\left<f(r),Df(r)\right>)drds

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{0}^{T}2|Re\left<\chi_{[s,t]}f,Df\right>_{L^{2}}|ds

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{0}^{T}2||\chi_{[s,t]}f||_{L^{2}}\cdot||Df||_{L^{2}}ds

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{0}^{T}2||f||_{L^{2}}||Df||_{L^{2}}ds

\displaystyle\leq T(\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left|\left|Df\right|\right|_{L^{2}}^{2})

\displaystyle\leq T\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{D}^{2},

We get

\left|\left|f(t)\right|\right|^{2}\leq T^{-1}\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{D}^{2}\leq(1+T^{-1})\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{D}^{2}.

Since this is true for all
T
, we have

||f||_{C^{0}}:=\max\limits_{t\in[0,T]}||f(t)||\leq(1+T^{-1})^{\frac{1}{2}}\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{D}.

As
C^{1}([0,T],W)
is dense in
\Dom(D)
, its inclusion into
C^{0}([0,T],H)
extends uniquely to a continuous map on
\Dom(D)
. As both convergence in
\Dom(D)
and in
C^{0}([0,T],H)
imply convergence in
L^{2}([0,T],H)
, the limits in both spaces agree, so
\Dom(D)\subseteq C^{0}([0,T],H)
and the continuous extension is the inclusion of
\Dom(D)
into
C^{0}([0,T],H)
.
∎

Theorem 4.10
.

D
has the Cauchy property. More specifically

F_{s}(g,x)(t):=Q(t,s)x+\int\limits_{s}^{t}Q(t,r)g(r)dr

defines an inverse for
D\oplus ev_{s}
.

Proof.

For
g\in C^{1}([0,T],W)
and
x\in H
, we get (using Lemma
2.1
for differentiating the integral)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}F_{s}(g,x)(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}Q(t,s)x+\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int\limits_{s}^{t}Q(t,r)g(r)dr

\displaystyle=iA(t)Q(t,s)x+\int\limits_{s}^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}Q(t,r)g(r)dr+Q(t,t)g(t)

\displaystyle=iA(t)Q(t,s)x+iA(t)\int\limits_{s}^{t}Q(t,r)g(r)dr+g(t)

\displaystyle=iA(t)F_{s}(g,x)(t)+g(t),

where
A
can be pulled out of the integral, as the integral also exists in
W
.
Thus
DF_{s}(g,x)=g
. As

F_{s}(g,x)(s)=Q(s,s)x=x,

we have

(D\oplus ev_{s})\circ F_{s}|_{C^{1}([0,T],W)}=id.

Conversely, for
f\in C^{1}([0,T],W)
, we have

\displaystyle F_{s}(Df,f(s))(t)

\displaystyle=Q(t,s)f(s)+\int\limits_{s}^{t}Q(t,r)\left(\frac{d}{dr}f(r)-iAf(r)\right)dr

\displaystyle=Q(t,s)f(s)+\int\limits_{s}^{t}\left(Q(t,r)\frac{d}{dr}f(r)+\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial r}Q(t,r)\right)f(r)\right)dr

\displaystyle=Q(t,s)f(s)+\int\limits_{s}^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(Q(t,r)f(r)\right)dr

\displaystyle=Q(t,s)f(s)+Q(t,t)f(t)-Q(t,s)f(s)

\displaystyle=f(t),

whence

F_{s}\circ(D\oplus ev_{s})|_{C^{1}([0,T],W)}=id.

Now it remains to extend this to the whole spaces. If
x_{n}
is a sequence in
W
converging to a limit
x
in
H
and
\phi
is in
C^{\infty}([0,T],{\mathbb{C}})
,
\phi x_{n}
converges to
\phi x
in
L^{2}([0,T],H)
. Thus
S([0,T],W)
(as defined in Lemma
2.2
) is dense in
S([0,T],H)
, which is dense in
L^{2}([0,T],H)
by Lemma
2.2
. We can conclude that
S([0,T],W)
is dense in
L^{2}([0,T],H)
. As
C^{1}([0,T],W)
contains
S([0,T],W)
, it is also dense in
L^{2}([0,T],H)
. Moreover,
C^{1}([0,T],W)
is dense in
\Dom(D)
by definition.
Using that
Q
is an isometry, we obtain for
(f,x)\in C^{1}([0,T],W)\oplus H
:

\displaystyle||F_{s}(f,x)||_{D}^{2}

\displaystyle=||F(f,x)||_{L^{2}}^{2}+||DF(f,x)||_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left|Q(\cdot,s)x\right|\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left|\left|\int\limits_{s}^{\cdot}Q(\cdot,r)f(r)dr\right|\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left|x\right|\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left|\left|\int\limits_{t}^{\cdot}\left|\left|f(r)\right|\right|dr\right|\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq T||x||^{2}+\left|\left|\int\limits_{0}^{T}\left|\left|f(r)\right|\right|dr\right|\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq T||x||^{2}+T||f||_{L_{1}}^{2}+||f||_{L_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq T||x||^{2}+CT||f||_{L_{2}}^{2}+||f||_{L_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}||(f,x)||_{L^{2}([0,T],H)\oplus H}^{2},

for some constants
C
and
C^{\prime}
(as
L^{2}([0,T],H)
is boundedly included in
L^{1}([0,T],H)
).
Thus
F_{s}|_{C^{1}([0,T],W)\oplus H}
extends uniquely to a bounded map
L^{2}([0,T],H)\oplus H\rightarrow\Dom(D)
. As both this extension and
F_{s}
are bounded as maps into
L^{2}([0,T],H)
, they coincide, so
F_{s}
maps boundedly into
\Dom(D)
.
D\oplus ev_{s}
is bounded by definition of the operator norm and by Lemma
4.9
.

Thus
F_{s}(D\oplus ev_{t})
and
(D\oplus ev_{t})F_{s}
are bounded maps that are the identity on a dense subset, so they must be the respective identities.
∎

5
The Index and the Spectral Flow

In the following, we will use the splitting of
H
in positive and negative spectral subspaces of
A(t)
:

Definition 5.1
.

For
t\in[0,T]
, let

P_{\geq 0}(t):=P_{[0,\infty)}(t)=I-P_{<0}(t).

Let

H_{<0}(t):=\Ran(P_{<0}(t))

and

H_{\geq 0}(t):=\Ran(P_{\geq 0}(t))

We define APS boundary conditions:

Definition 5.2
.

For
s<t\in[0,T]
, let
(D|_{[s,t]})_{\APS}
be the restriction of
D|_{[s,t]}
to

\Dom((D|_{[s,t]})_{\APS}):=\{f\in\Dom(D)|f(s)\in H_{<0}(s),f(t)\in H_{\geq 0}(t)\}.

Let

D_{\APS}:=(D|_{[0,T]})_{\APS}

The following evolved spectral projection will be crucial for proving the main theorem.

Definition 5.3
.

Let

\hat{P}_{<a}(t):=Q(0,t)P_{<a}(t)Q(t,0).

Let
\hat{P}_{<a}(t)_{r}
be the restriction

\hat{P}_{<a}(t)_{r}:=[\hat{P}_{<a}(t):{H_{<0}(0)}\rightarrow{Q(0,t)H_{(-\infty,a)}(t)}].

Note that
\hat{P}_{<a}(t)
is the projection onto
Q(0,t)H_{(-\infty,a)}(t)
. Thus
\hat{P}_{<a}(t)_{r}
is defined so that, by definition of the index of pairs, its index is that of the pair
(P_{<0}(0),\hat{P}_{<a}(t))
. Also note that
\hat{P}_{<0}(0)=P_{<0}(0)
.

In this section, the main theorem will be shown, stating that under a certain condition we have

\ind(D_{\APS})=\sfl(A).

For this purpose, we will first show that the index of
D_{\APS}
and that of the pair
(\hat{P}_{<0}(0),\hat{P}_{<0}(T))
coincide (if either one is defined). Then we will show that those projections are spectral projections of a strongly differentiable family
\hat{A}
of operators that has the same spectral flow as
A
. We can then use Theorem
3.15
for
\hat{A}
to conclude the proof of the equality above.

5.1
The Index of
D_{\APS}

The first step towards the index formula is to investigate the Fredholm index of
D_{\APS}
. This corresponds to section 3 in [BäSt].

Theorem 5.4
.

Write
Q
for
Q(T,0)
.
The kernel of
D_{\APS}
is:

\Ker(D_{\APS})\cong H_{<0}(0)\cap Q^{-1}H_{\geq 0}(T)=\Ker(\hat{P}_{<0}(T)_{r})

D_{\APS}
has closed range if and only if
P_{<0}(t)_{r}
has closed range and in this case

\Coker(D_{\APS})\cong H_{\geq 0}(0)\cap Q^{-1}H_{<0}(T)\cong\Coker(\hat{P}_{<0}(T)_{r}).

In particular,
D_{\APS}
is Fredholm with index
k
if and only if
(P_{<0}(0),\hat{P}_{<0}(T))
is a Fredholm pair with index
k
.

Remark:
By replacing
A
by
A|_{[0,t]}
, we obtain for any
t\in[0,T]
that
(D|_{[0,t]})_{\APS}
is Fredholm with index
k
if and only if
\hat{P}_{<0}(t)_{r}
is.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\Ker(D_{\APS})

\displaystyle=\{f\in\Dom(D)|Df=0,f(0)\in H_{<0}(0),f(T)\in H_{\geq 0}(T)\}

\displaystyle\cong\{f(0)\in H_{<0}(0)|Qf(0)\in H_{\geq 0}(T)\}

\displaystyle=H_{<0}(0)\cap Q^{-1}H_{\geq 0}(T)

\displaystyle=\Ker(\hat{P}_{<0}(t)_{r}),

where in the second line, we use that
f\mapsto f(0)
is an isomorphism due to the Cauchy property and that
Df=0
implies
f(t)=Q(t,0)f(0)
. In the last line, we used that
Q^{-1}H_{\geq 0}(T)
is the orthogonal complement of
Q^{-1}H_{<0}(T)
, onto which
\hat{P}_{<0}(t)
projects.

For
g\in L^{2}([0,T],H)
define

E(g):=ev_{T}(D\oplus ev_{0})^{-1}(g,0).

This is in some sense the counterpart to
Q
: Rather than starting with
f(0)=x
for some
x\in H
and evolving subject to
Df=0
, one starts with
f(0)=0
and evolves subject to
Df=g
. By linearity, we have

ev_{T}(D\oplus ev_{0})^{-1}(g,x)=E(g)+Qx

To see when
D_{\APS}
has closed range and to determine its cokernel, we need to characterize its range.

For
g\in L^{2}([0,T],H)
, we have the following chain of equivalences:

\displaystyle g\in\Ran(D_{\APS})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\exists f\in\Dom(D):f(0)\in H_{<0}(0)\wedge f(T)\in H_{\geq 0}(T)\wedge Df=g

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\exists f(0)\in H_{<0}(0):ev_{T}(D\oplus ev_{0})^{-1}(g,f(0))\in H_{\geq 0}(T)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\exists f(0)\in H_{<0}(0):\exists z\in H_{\geq 0}(T):E(g)+Q(f(0))=z

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\exists x\in QH_{<0}(0):\exists z\in H_{\geq 0}(T):E(g)=z-x

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow E(g)\in QH_{<0}(0)+H_{\geq 0}(T)

Defining

V:=QH_{<0}(0)+H_{\geq 0}(T),

we get

\Ran(D_{\APS})=\{g\in L^{2}([0,T],H)|E(g)\in V\}=E^{-1}(V).

Claim
:
E:L^{2}([0,T],H)\rightarrow H
is surjective.

We need to show that functions in
\Dom(D)
that vanish at 0 can take any value at
T
. For
z\in H
choose
f\in\Dom(D)
with
f(T)=z
(a possible choice is
f(t)=Q(t,T)z
) and let

\phi(t):=\frac{t}{T}
. Multiplication with
\phi
preserves
\Dom(D)
as it is bounded on
\Dom(D_{0})
with respect to
||\cdot||_{D}
. Thus,
\phi f
is in
\Dom(D)
, with
\phi(0)f(0)=0
and
\phi(T)f(T)=z
. We get

E(D(\phi f))=ev_{T}\circ(D\oplus ev_{0})^{-1}(D(\phi f),0)=ev_{T}(\phi f)=z.

As
z
was arbitrary,
E
is surjective.

As
E|_{\Ker(E)^{\bot}}
is an isomorphism, we can define

\iota:=(E|_{\Ker(E)^{\bot}})^{-1}.

By the bounded inverse theorem,
\iota
is continuous.
We can conclude that

\Ran(D_{\APS})=E^{-1}(V)=\iota(V)\oplus\Ker(E)

is closed if and only if
V
is closed.

Next we relate closedness of
V
to that of
\Ran(\hat{P}_{<0}(T)_{r})
.

For
x\in QH_{<0}(0)
and
y\in H_{\geq 0}(T)
, we have

x+y=P_{<0}(T)x+(P_{\geq 0}(T)x+y)\in P_{<0}(T)QH_{<0}(0)+H_{\geq 0}(T)

and conversely

P_{<0}(T)x+y=x+(y-P_{\geq 0}(T)x)\in V.

Thus

V=P_{<0}(T)QH_{<0}(0)+H_{\geq 0}(T).

As
H_{\geq 0}(T)
is closed and the summands are orthogonal,
V
is closed if and only if
P_{<0}(T)QH_{<0}(0)
is closed. This is closed if and only if

Q^{-1}P_{<0}(T)QH_{<0}(0)=\Ran(\hat{P}_{<0}(T)_{r})

is closed. Putting things together, we get that
D_{\APS}
has closed range if and only if
\hat{P}_{<0}(T)_{r}
has closed range.

Now assume that
\Ran(D_{\APS})
and hence
V
is closed.
Let
P
be the orthogonal projection onto
V^{\bot}=QH_{\geq 0}(0)\cap H_{<0}(T)
.

P\circ E:L^{2}([0,T],H)\rightarrow QH_{\geq 0}(0)\cap H_{<0}(T)

is a surjective map with kernel

\Ker(P\circ E)=E^{-1}(\Ker(P))=E^{-1}(V)=\Ran(D_{\APS}).

This means

QH_{\geq 0}(0)\cap H_{<0}(T)=\Ran(P\circ E)\cong\Dom(P\circ E)/\Ker(P\circ E)=L^{2}([0,T],H)/\Ran(D_{\APS}).

We can conclude:

\Coker(D_{\APS})\cong QH_{\geq 0}(0)\cap H_{<0}(T)\cong H_{\geq 0}(0)\cap Q^{-1}H_{<0}(T).

Moreover, for every
x\in Q^{-1}H_{<0}(T)
,
x
is in
H_{\geq 0}(0)=H_{<0}(0)^{\bot}
if and only if for all
y\in H_{<0}(0)

0=\left<x,y\right>=\left<\hat{P}_{<0}(T)x,y\right>=\left<x,\hat{P}_{<0}(T)y\right>.

Thus we can conclude

\displaystyle H_{\geq 0}(0)\cap Q^{-1}H_{<0}(T)

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{P}_{<0}(T)H_{<0}(0)\right)^{\bot}\cap Q^{-1}H_{<0}(T)

\displaystyle=\Ran(\hat{P}_{<0}(T)_{r})^{\bot}\ \ \ \ \ (in\ Q^{-1}H_{<0}(0))

\displaystyle\cong\Coker(\hat{P}_{<0}(T)_{r}).

∎

In [BäSt], the operator
Q_{--}
defined below is used instead of the pair
(P_{<0}(0),\hat{P}_{<0}(t))
. When considering indices, the two are equivalent:

Theorem 5.5
.

For every
t\in[0,T]
, the operator

Q_{--}(t,0):=[P_{<0}(t)Q(t,0):{H_{<0}(0)}\rightarrow{H_{<0}(t)}]

is Fredholm with index k if and only if
(P_{<0}(0),\hat{P}_{<0}(t))
is a Fredholm pair with index k.

Proof.

\hat{P}_{<0}(t)
is the projection onto
Q(0,t)H_{<0}(t)
.
We have

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{<0}(t)_{r}

\displaystyle=[(Q(0,t)P_{<0}(t)Q(t,0)):{H_{<0}(0)}\rightarrow{Q(0,t)H_{<0}(t)}]

\displaystyle=[Q(0,t):{H_{<0}(t)}\rightarrow{Q(0,t)H_{<0}(t)}]\circ Q_{--}(t,0)

As
[Q(0,t):{H_{<0}(t)}\rightarrow{Q(0,t)H_{<0}(t)}]
is an isomorphism,

\hat{P}_{<0}(t)_{r}

is Fredholm with index k if and only if
Q_{--}(t,0)
is.
∎

In some special cases , this can be immediately used to show
\ind(D_{\APS})=\sfl(A)
:

Theorem 5.6
.

If
A
is uniformly bounded from below and
\spec(A)
is discrete with only finite multiplicities,
D_{\APS}
is Fredholm and

\ind(D_{\APS})=\sfl(A)

Remark:
This covers the special case that
H
is finite dimensional.

Proof.

Let
a
be a lower bound for all
A(t)
. Then

H_{<0}(t)=H_{[-a,0)}(t)

is finite dimensional all
t
. In particular,
\hat{P}_{<a}(T)_{r}
is an operator between finite dimensional spaces, so it is Fredholm, with its index being the difference of the dimensions of domain and range. Thus we have

\displaystyle\ind(\hat{P}_{<a}(T)_{r})

\displaystyle=\Dim(H_{[-a,0)}(0))-\Dim(Q(0,T)H_{[-a,0)}(T))

\displaystyle=\Dim(H_{[-a,0)}(0))-\Dim(H_{[-a,0)}(T))

\displaystyle=\sfl(A),

by Lemma
3.13
.
By Theorem
5.4
, this implies the claim.
∎

Remark:
Note that the specific form of
D
was not used anywhere in this section yet. The proof works for any operator
D
that has the Cauchy property and such that
ev_{0}\oplus ev_{T}:\Dom(D)\rightarrow H\oplus H
is surjective, with
Q
being the associated evolution operator. The dependence of the index on
\sfl(A)
can thus be seen to come mainly (in the special case above, only) from the APS boundary conditions with respect to
A
, while the appearance of
A
in the definition of
D
is only relevant for the evolution operator, which has to be ”nice enough” to not destroy the equality. In particular, we could look at
D=\overline{\frac{d}{dt}-iA_{1}}
and take APS boundary conditions with respect to the spectral decomposition of
A_{2}
, where
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
satisfy the conditions for
A
. Everything we have done so far would work, with the evolution operator being defined with respect to
A_{1}
and all spectral projections (and the spectral flow) with respect to
A_{2}
. In fact, for the conclusion of the proof of the main theorem, we would only need that
A_{1}(t)
and
A_{2}(t)
commute. As a special case of this, we could consider
A_{1}=A
and
A_{2}=-A
. This would give the same operator
D
, but with ”anti-APS” boundary conditions, i.e.
f(0)\in H_{(0,\infty)}(0)
and
f(T)\in H_{(-\infty,0]}(T)
. We would then get the equality

\ind(D_{\operatorname{anti-APS}})=\ind(P_{>0}(0),\hat{P}_{>0}(t))=sf(-A).

5.2
The Main Theorem

Let

\hat{A}(t):=Q(0,t)A(t)Q(t,0).

As functional calculus is equivariant under conjugation with isometries,
we have

\chi_{(-\infty,a)}(\hat{A}(t))=Q(0,t)\chi_{(-\infty,a)}(A(t))Q(t,0)=\hat{P}_{<a}(t).

For every
t\in[0,T]
,
\hat{A}(t)
is self-adjoint and Fredholm, with domain
W
(as
Q(t,0)^{-1}(W)=W
). In order to show that
\hat{A}
has all the properties required of
A
, the only thing we need to prove is that
\hat{A}
is strongly differentiable in
B(W,H)
.

Lemma 5.7
.

\hat{A}:J\rightarrow B(W,H)
is strongly continuously differentiable with derivative

\hat{A}^{\prime}(t)=Q(0,t)A^{\prime}(t)Q(t,0)

Remark:
An informal calculation ignoring domains would read

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(Q(0,t)A(t)Q(t,0))

\displaystyle=-Q(0,t)iA(t)A(t)Q(t,0)+Q(0,t)A^{\prime}(t)Q(t,0)+Q(0,t)A(t)iA(t)Q(t,0)

\displaystyle=Q(0,t)A^{\prime}(t)Q(t,0).

However,
Q
is not differentiable as a map
H\rightarrow H
or
W\rightarrow W
and the terms that cancel out contain second powers of
A
, which are not defined on
W
. One would like to restrict to a dense subspace where everything exists, but it is not clear if such a subspace exists. To remedy this, we will consider the inverse. This maps
H
to
W
, allowing us to restrict the domain and extend the range in such a way that the calculations work rigorously.

Proof.

Let
R(t):=(A(t)-i)^{-1}
for
t\in[0,T]
.
\hat{A}(t)
is differentiable at
t
if and only if

\hat{A}(t)-i=Q(0,t)(A(t)-i)Q(t,0)

is. As
Q(t,0)
and
Q(0,t)
are strongly continuously differentiable in
B(W,H)
and
R(t)
is strongly continuously differentiable in
B(H,W)
, we get from Lemmas
2.8
and
2.11
that

(\hat{A}(t)-i)^{-1}=Q(0,t)R(t)Q(t,0)

is strongly differentiable in
B(W,H)
. Its derivative is

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(\hat{A}(t)-i)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}Q(0,t)R(t)Q(t,0)

\displaystyle=Q(0,t)R(t)iA(t)Q(t,0)-Q(0,t)R(t)A^{\prime}(t)R(t)Q(t,0)-Q(0,t)iA(t)R(t)Q(t,0)

\displaystyle=-Q(0,t)R(t)A^{\prime}(t)R(t)Q(t,0).

As this is strongly continuous in
B(H,W)
, Lemma
2.12
implies that
(\hat{A}(t)-i)^{-1}
is strongly continuously differentiable in
B(H,W)
. By Lemma
2.11
,
\hat{A}(t)-i
and hence
\hat{A}(t)
are strongly continuously differentiable, with derivative

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}((\hat{A}(t)-i)^{-1})^{-1}

\displaystyle=-(\hat{A}(t)-i)\left(\frac{d}{dt}(\hat{A}(t)-i)^{-1}\right)(\hat{A}(t)-i)

\displaystyle=Q(0,t)(A(t)-i)Q(t,0)\circ Q(0,t)R(t)A^{\prime}(t)R(t)Q(t,0)\circ Q(0,t)(A(t)-i)Q(t,0)

\displaystyle=Q(0,t)A^{\prime}(t)Q(t,0)

∎

With this, we have all the pieces in place now to prove the main theorem.

Theorem 5.8
.

(Main Theorem)

If
(D|_{[0,t]})_{\APS}
is Fredholm for all
t\in[0,T]
, we have

\ind(D_{\APS})=\sfl(A).

In light of Theorem
5.4
, this is equivalent to the following:

Theorem 5.9
.

(Main Theorem II)

If
(P_{<0}(0),\hat{P}_{<0}(t))
is Fredholm for all
t\in[0,T]
, we have

\ind(D_{\APS})=\sfl(A).

Proof.

\hat{A}
has all properties required of
A
. We can thus apply Theorem
3.15
to it. The spectral projections of
\hat{A}
are given by

\chi_{(-\infty,0)}(\hat{A}(t))=\hat{P}_{<0}(t).

Using theorems
5.4
and
3.14
, we can now conclude

\displaystyle\ind(D_{\APS})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{inD}}}{{=}}\ind(P_{<0}(0),\hat{P}_{<0}(T))

\displaystyle=\ind(\hat{P}_{<0}(0),\hat{P}_{<0}(T))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{flowind}}}{{=}}\sfl(\hat{A})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{conflow}}}{{=}}\sfl(A).

∎

Remark:
By Theorem
2.6
,
(P_{<0}(0),\hat{P}_{<0}(t))
is Fredholm if and only if
(P_{\geq 0}(0),Q(0,t)P_{\geq 0}(t)Q(t,0))
is Fredholm. Thus one can just as well consider positive instead of negative spectral projections.

As an immediate consequence of the main theorem, we obtain a gluing formula for the index of
D_{\APS}
:

Theorem 5.10
.

If
(D|_{[s,t]})_{\APS}
is Fredholm for all
s,t\in[0,T]
, we have for all
r<s<t\in[0,T]
:

\ind((D|_{[r,t]})_{\APS})=\ind((D|_{[r,s]})_{\APS})+\ind((D|_{[s,t]})_{\APS})

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\ind((D|_{[r,t]})_{\APS})

\displaystyle=\sfl(A|_{[r,t]})

\displaystyle=\sfl(A|_{[r,s]})+\sfl(A|_{[s,t]})

\displaystyle=\ind((D|_{[r,s]})_{\APS})+\ind((D|_{[s,t]})_{\APS}).

∎

5.3
Sufficient Conditions for the Main Theorem

It may not be easy to determine a priori whether
D_{\APS}
is Fredholm. The evolution operator and thus
\hat{P}_{<0}(t)
might be difficult to compute as well. The aim of this section is to give other sufficient criteria for the use of the main theorem that will be much less general, but sometimes easier to check.

Theorem 5.11
.

For
t\in[0,T]
, the following are equivalent:

1.

Q_{+-}(t,0):=P_{\geq 0}(t)Q(t,0)P_{<0}(0)
and
Q_{-+}(t,0):=P_{<0}(t)Q(t,0)P_{\geq 0}(0)
are compact.

2.

(P_{<0}(0),\hat{P}_{<0}(t))
is a compact pair, i.e. the difference of the two is compact.

Each of the above conditions imply that
D_{\APS}
is Fredholm and

\ind(D_{\APS})=\sfl(A).

Remark:
The former condition is shown in [BäSt] in the setting considered there (Lemma 2.6), so the main theorem is indeed applicable to that special case.

Proof.

The second condition implies that
(P_{<0}(0),\hat{P}_{<0}(t))
is Fredholm for all
t\in[0,T]
, by
2.4
. Thus we can apply the second main theorem (
5.9
) to obtain the second part of the claim.

What is left to show is that the two conditions are equivalent.

For
t\in[0,T]
, let

S_{0}:=P_{<0}(0)-\hat{P}_{<0}(t).

and

S:=Q(t,0)S_{0}=Q(t,0)P_{<0}(0)-P_{<0}(t)Q(t,0).

Condition 2. holds if and only if
S_{0}
is compact, which is true if and only if
S
is compact.
Let
S_{\geq 0}:=P_{\geq 0}(t)S
and
S_{<0}:=P_{<0}(t)S
.
As compact operators are an ideal,
S_{\geq 0}
and
S_{<0}
are compact if
S
is compact. Conversely,

S=S_{\geq 0}+S_{<0}

is compact if
S_{\geq 0}
and
S_{<0}
are.

Since

S_{\geq 0}=P_{\geq 0}(t)Q(t,0)P_{<0}(0)=Q_{+-}(t,0)

and

S_{<0}=P_{<0}(t)Q(t,0)P_{<0}(0)-P_{<0}(t)Q(t,0)=-P_{<0}(t)Q(t,0)(I-P_{<0}(0))=-Q_{-+}(t,0),

the theorem follows.
∎

Theorem 5.12
.

If
A^{\prime}(t)
is compact in
B(W,H)
for all
t\in[0,T]
, then
D_{\APS}
is Fredholm and

\ind(D_{\APS})=\sfl(A).

Proof.

By Lemma
5.7
and Theorem
2.14
,
\hat{A}(t)-A(0)
is compact in
B(W,H)
for every
t\in[0,T]
.
Corollary 3.5 in [LE] states that in this case
\chi_{[0,\infty)}(\hat{A}(t))-\chi_{[0,\infty)}(A(0))
is compact in
B(H)
.
Thus

\hat{P}_{<0}(t)-P_{<0}(0)=(I-\chi_{[0,\infty)}(\hat{A}(t)))-(I-\chi_{[0,\infty)}(A(0)))=-(\chi_{[0,\infty)}(\hat{A}(t))-\chi_{[0,\infty)}(A(0)))

is compact, so
(P_{<0}(0),\hat{P}_{<0}(t))
is a compact pair and hence a Fredholm pair by Lemma
2.4
for all
t\in[0,T]
.
By Theorem
5.11
, we get the desired result.
∎

Remark:
The counterexample in the next chapter shows that it is not sufficient to ask for relative compactness of
A(t)-A(0)
.

For the next theorem, we will need the following lemma:

Lemma 5.13
.

If
J
is an interval and
P:J\rightarrow B(H)
is a continuous family of commuting projections,
P
is constant.

Proof.

For
s\in J
, there is a neighborhood
U
of
s
such that for
t\in U
, we have

||P(t)-P(s)||<1.

For
t\in U
, we have

P(t)P(s)P(t)P(s)=P(t)P(t)P(s)P(s)=P(t)P(s)

and

(P(t)P(s))^{*}=P(s)P(t)=P(t)P(s).

Thus
P(t)P(s)
is a (orthogonal) projection as well. The range of
P(t)P(s)
is a subspace of the range of
P(t)
. It is closed, as (orthogonal) projections have closed range.

Assume for contradiction that it is a proper subspace, i.e.
P(t)\neq P(t)P(s)
. Then there must be a non-zero
x\in\Ran(P(t))
such that
x\bot\Ran(P(t)P(s))
, i.e.
x\in\Ker(P(t)P(s))
.
Then

P(s)x=P(s)P(t)x=P(t)P(s)x=0.

But this implies

||P(t)x-P(s)x||=||x||,

contradicting
||P(t)-P(s)||<1
.
We can thus conclude the assumption was wrong and

P(t)=P(t)P(s).

The same reasoning with
s
and
t
interchanged yields

P(s)=P(s)P(t)=P(t)P(s)=P(t).

As this holds for all
t\in U
, for a neighborhood
U
of every
s\in J
,
P
is locally constant. As the interval is connected,
P
is constant.
∎

Theorem 5.14
.

Let
R(t):=(A(t)-i)^{-1}
. If
R(s)
and
R(t)
commute for all
s,t\in[0,T]
,
D_{\APS}
is Fredholm with index
\sfl(A)
.

Remark:
Basically, the assumption is that all
A(t)
commute with each other. Considering the resolvents is necessary, as
A(t)A(s)
is not defined on all of
W
.

Proof.

Assume that
\rho:H\rightarrow H
is a bounded operator such that

\rho R(s)=R(s)\rho

for
s,\in[0,T]
. Note that this implies

\rho(W)=\Ran(\rho R(s))\subseteq\Ran(R(s))=W.

We have

\displaystyle\rho A(s)

\displaystyle=\rho(A(s)+i)-i\rho

\displaystyle=(A(s)+i)R(s)\rho(A(s)+i)-i\rho

\displaystyle=(A(s)+i)\rho R(s)(A(s)+i)-i\rho

\displaystyle=(A(s)+i)\rho-i\rho

\displaystyle=A(s)\rho.

For
\rho=R(t)
, we obtain

R(t)A(s)=A(s)R(t).

Thus we have for
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in W

\displaystyle R(t)Q(t,0)-Q(t,0)R(t)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}(Q(t,s)R(t)Q(s,0))xds

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{t}Q(t,s)\left(-iA(s)R(t)+R(t)iA(s)\right)Q(s,0)xds

\displaystyle=0.

Setting
\rho=Q(t,0)
in the above, we obtain

Q(t,0)A(t)=A(t)Q(t,0).

We can conclude

\hat{A}(t)=Q(0,t)Q(t,0)A(t)=A(t)

and thus we have

\hat{P}_{<a}(t)=P_{<a}(t).

Let
f(\lambda):=\frac{1}{\lambda-i}
.
As for
t\in[0,T]

P_{<a}(t)=\chi_{(-\infty,a)}(f^{-1}(f(A(t))))=(\chi_{(-\infty,a)}\circ f^{-1})(R(t))

the projection
P_{<a}(t)
commutes with every operator that commutes with
R(t)
. In particular, it commutes with
R(s)
for
s\in[0,T]
. As
P_{<a}(s)
commutes with all operators commuting with
R(s)
, we can conclude that
P_{<a}(t)
and
P_{<a}(s)
commute.

For
t\in[0,T]
choose
a\in{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
and a neighborhood
U
of
t
such that for all
s\in U

H_{[0,a)}(s)
is finite dimensional and
a\notin\spec(A(s))
(possible by Lemma
3.8
). The latter allows us to use Theorem
3.5
to conclude that
P_{<a}
is continuous on
U
, so by Lemma
5.13
it is constant.

[P_{<0}(s):H_{<0}(0)\rightarrow H_{<0}(s)]

is the composition of

[P_{<a}(s):H_{<0}(0)\rightarrow H_{(-\infty,a)}(s)]

with

[P_{<0}(s):H_{(-\infty,a)}(s)\rightarrow H_{<0}(s)]

and the latter is Fredholm, as
H_{[0,a)}(s)
is finite dimensional. Thus the first of the three is Fredholm if and only if the second is. We can conclude that
(P_{<0}(0),P_{<0}(s))
is a Fredholm pair if and only if
(P_{<0}(0),P_{<a}(s))
is. As the latter is the same for all
s\in U
, we can conclude that if
(P_{<0}(0),P_{<0}(s))
is Fredholm for some
s\in U
it is Fredholm for all
s\in U
. As this implication holds for neighborhoods of every point in
[0,T]
, the set of
s\in[0,T]
such that
(P_{<0}(0),P_{<0}(s))
is Fredholm is open and closed. As the set contains
0
and
[0,T]
is connected, we can conclude that
(P_{<0}(0),P_{<0}(s))=(P_{<0}(0),\hat{P}_{<0}(s))
is Fredholm for all
s\in[0,T]
. By the second main theorem (
5.9
), this implies the claim.
∎

6
A Counterexample with Bounded Perturbation

In this section an example is given to illustrate that
D_{\APS}
will not always be Fredholm. There might be ”infinite exchange” between the positive and the negative spectral subspace. This is possible, even if
A(t)
has only discrete spectrum and its difference from
A(0)
is bounded. The idea is to choose a perturbation of
A(0)
such that the eigenspaces undergo a 90-degree-rotation ”up to evolution with A(0)” under the evolution operator. The first step is to show that such an exchange works in a two dimensional subspace, with some bounds on the derivative of the perturbation. These bounds will then allow us to pass to an infinite direct sum, in which all eigenspaces are interchanged. This means that
\Ker(D_{\APS})=H_{<0}(0)\cap Q^{-1}H_{\geq 0}(T)
will be infinite dimensional, whence
D_{\APS}
is not Fredholm.

6.1
Interchanging two Eigenspaces

Theorem 6.1
.

There is
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
, such that the following is true: For any

a={\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&0\\
0&\lambda_{2}\end{pmatrix}},

with
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, there is a
C^{2}
-family
(b(t))_{t\in[0,1]}
of self-adjoint operators on
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
such that for
\lambda:=|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}|+1
we have

•

||b(t)||\leq 2

•

||b^{\prime}(t)||\leq c\lambda

•

b(0)=b(1)=0

•

q(1,0)e_{1}\in\spann(e_{2})
,

where q is the evolution operator associated with
a+b(t)
and
e_{i}
denotes the
i-th
standard unit vector.

Remark:

The idea of the proof is to choose
b
in such a way, that

ib(t)q(t,0)e_{1}=\gamma^{\prime}(t)

”up to evolution with a”, where
q
is the evolution operator associated to the family
a+b(t)
and
\gamma
is a path from
e_{1}
to
e_{2}
. Then
q(t,0)e_{1}=\gamma(t)
, ”up to evolution with a”, which preserves
\spann(e_{2})
. Heuristically, this works well, because
q
only depends on earlier values of
b
. In case
\lambda_{1}=-\lambda_{2}
(which would be sufficient for a counterexample), this argument can be made rigorous by quotienting out the orbits of
exp(ita)
and applying the Banach fixed point theorem to show that the above equation has a solution on the quotient. However, this proof is rather long and yields a less general result, so a less conceptual proof will be used below.

Proof.

Let
\phi:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
be a smooth function (chosen independently of the
\lambda_{i}
) with

\displaystyle|\phi^{\prime}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq 2

\displaystyle\phi(0)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\phi(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2}

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=\phi^{\prime}(1)=0.

Let

b(t):={\begin{pmatrix}0&i\phi^{\prime}(t)exp(i(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})t)\\
-i\phi^{\prime}(t)exp(i(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})t)&0\end{pmatrix}}.

b(t)
is self-adjoint and the evolution operator of
a+b(t)
is given by

q(t,0):={\begin{pmatrix}exp(i\lambda_{1}t)cos(\phi(t))&-exp(i\lambda_{1}t)sin(\phi(t))\\
exp(i\lambda_{2}t)sin(\phi(t))&exp(i\lambda_{2}t)cos(\phi(t))\end{pmatrix}},

as it can easily be seen that

q(0,0)=id

and straightforward calculation of both sides shows

\frac{d}{dt}q(t,0)=i(a+b(t))q(t,0).

The estimates follow, as
\phi^{\prime}
is bounded by 2, and
\phi^{\prime\prime}
is bounded as well, as the interval is compact. Thus

||b(t)||=|\phi^{\prime}(t)||exp(\pm i(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})t)|\leq 2

and there is a constant
c
such that

\left|\left|b^{\prime}(t)\right|\right|\leq|\phi^{\prime\prime}(t)|+|\phi^{\prime}(t)(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})|\leq c\lambda.

b(0)=b(1)=0
follows from
\phi^{\prime}(0)=\phi^{\prime}(1)=0
.
Finally, note that

q(1,0)e_{1}=exp(i\lambda_{2})e_{2}\in\spann(e_{2}).

∎

6.2
Interchanging all Eigenspaces

Now that we have seen how to interchange two eigenspaces, it remains to show that we can do it with infinitely many simultaneously.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
H:=\bigoplus\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
and let
(\lambda_{i})_{i\geq 0}
be an unbounded increasing sequence of positive real numbers.
Let

A_{0}:=\bigoplus\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}

with

a_{i}={\begin{pmatrix}-\lambda_{i}&0\\
0&\lambda_{i}\end{pmatrix}}.

There is a bounded family
B:[0,T]\rightarrow B(H)
such that
A(t):=A_{0}+B(t)
satisfies the requirements from the setting and such that
D_{\APS}
is not Fredholm.

Remark:
The form of
A_{0}
was chosen for simplicity. The construction works with any self-adjoint operator that has pairs of arbitrarily large positive and negative eigenvalues in the same order of magnitude.

Proof.

For
i\geq 0
, let
b_{i}
and
q_{i}
be chosen as
b
and
q
in theorem
6.1
with
\lambda_{1}=-\lambda_{i}
and
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{i}
. Let

B:=\bigoplus\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}b_{i}

Q:=\bigoplus\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}q_{i},

where the direct sum is meant to include taking the closure. Define

A(t):=A_{0}+B(t).

As every
b_{i}(t)
is bounded by 2,
B(t)=A(t)-A(0)
is bounded by 2. Thus

\Dom(A(t))=\Dom(A_{0})

with equivalent norms for all
t
. By the Kato-Rellich theorem,
A_{0}+B(t)
is self-adjoint.

For
|\lambda_{i}|>4
,
a_{i}+b_{i}(t)
is invertible with inverse bounded by the inequality

\left|\left|(a_{i}+b_{i}(t))^{-1}\right|\right|\leq\frac{1}{\left|\left|a_{i}^{-1}\right|\right|^{-1}-\left|\left|b_{i}(t)\right|\right|}\leq\frac{2}{\lambda_{i}}

for every
t\in[0,1]
.
Thus

\bigoplus\limits_{|\lambda_{i}|>4}(a_{i}+b_{i}(t))^{-1}

is a well defined inverse of

\bigoplus\limits_{|\lambda_{i}|>4}a_{i}+b_{i}(t).

As only finitely many
\lambda_{i}
are smaller than 4 and operators between finite dimensional spaces are Fredholm, we can conclude that

A(t)=\bigoplus\limits_{|\lambda_{i}|\leq 4}(a_{i}+b_{i}(t))\oplus\bigoplus\limits_{|\lambda_{i}|>4}(a_{i}+b_{i}(t))

is a direct sum of two Fredholm operators and thus Fredholm.

Let
\iota_{i}
denote the inclusion of the
i
-th summand.
For

x=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\iota_{i}(x_{i})\in\Dom(A_{0}),

we know that

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{d}{dt}(A(t)\iota_{i}(x_{i}))=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\iota_{i}(b_{i}^{\prime}(t)x_{i})

converges absolutely, as
||b_{i}^{\prime}||\leq 2c(\lambda_{i}+1)
(with
c
as above). Thus
A(t)
is strongly differentiable with strong derivative

A^{\prime}=\bigoplus\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}b_{i}^{\prime},

which is uniformly bounded by
2c
in
B(W,H)
.
As every
b_{i}^{\prime}
is continuous,
A^{\prime}
is strongly continuous on the linear span of all
\iota_{i}({\mathbb{C}}^{2})
. As this is a dense subspace of
W
, Lemma
2.9
implies that
A^{\prime}
is strongly continuous in
B(W,H)
, hence
A
is strongly continuously differentiable.

Let
\tilde{q}_{i}
be the component of the evolution operator of
A
on the i-th direct summand. As this satisfies
\tilde{q}_{i}^{\prime}(t,0)=i(a_{i}+b_{i}(t))\tilde{q}_{i}(t,0)
and
\tilde{q}_{i}(0,0)=id
, we have
\tilde{q}_{i}=q_{i}
by
4.7
. Thus
Q
agrees with the evolution operator associated with
A
on every summand, so they are the same.

For all
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\iota_{i}(e_{1})
is a negative eigenvector of
A(0)=A_{0}
, but

Q(1,0)\iota_{i}(e_{1})=\iota_{i}(q_{i}(1,0)e_{1})\in\spann(\iota_{i}(e_{2}))

is a positive eigenvector of
A(1)=A_{0}
by construction. Thus

\Ker(D_{\APS})\cong\Ker(\hat{P}_{<0}(T)_{r})=H_{<0}(0)\cap Q(0,1)H_{\geq 0}(1)=\overline{\{\spann\{\iota_{i}(e_{1})|i\in{\mathbb{N}}\}}

is infinite dimensional and hence
D_{\APS}
is not Fredholm.

∎

Remark:
Note that requiring only
D_{\APS}
to be Fredholm is not sufficient either. To see this extend the family constructed above with another one constructed similarly, such that there is still no spectral flow,
A(2)=A(0)
and
Q(2,1)
swaps
\iota_{i}(e_{2})
and
\iota_{i+1}(e_{1})
