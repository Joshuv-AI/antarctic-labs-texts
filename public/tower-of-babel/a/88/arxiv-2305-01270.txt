Classification of real and complex 3-qutrit states
Sabino Di Trani, Willem A. de Graaf, Alessio Marrani

Abstract
. In this paper we classify the orbits of the group SL ​ ( 3 , F ) 3 \mathrm{SL}(3,F)^{3} on the space F 3 ⊗ F 3 ⊗ F 3 F^{3}\otimes F^{3}\otimes F^{3} for F = ℂ F=\mathbb{C} and F = ℝ F=\mathbb{R} . 2.
Qutrits

2.1.
Complex qutrits

A quantum trit (
qutrit
) is the superposition of three orthogonal
basis states
\left\{\left|0\right\rangle,\left|1\right\rangle,\left|2\right\rangle\right\}

[
NJDH+13
]
, rather than the two
which characterize a quantum bit (
qubit
), namely :

(2.1)

\left|\psi\right\rangle=\alpha\left|0\right\rangle+\beta\left|1\right\rangle+\gamma\left|2\right\rangle,\penalty\ \text{with\penalty\ }\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{C}:\left|\alpha\right|^{2}+\left|\beta\right|^{2}+\left|\gamma\right|^{2}=1.

Therefore, a qutrit is an element of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
and the
corresponding SLOCC group acting on it is
GL_{3}(\mathbb{C})
.
The entanglement measures for qutrit systems are provided by relative
invariants under the action of the multipartite local SLOCC group. For
example, as the simplest relative SLOCC invariant for a two-qutrit state of
the form (
A,B=0,1,2
)

(2.2)

\left|\Psi\right\rangle=\Psi_{AB}\left|A\right\rangle\otimes\left|B\right\rangle,

one can take the determinant det
(\Psi)
of the
3\times 3
complex matrix
\Psi_{AB}
, which is invariant under
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{C})
. The classification of two-qutrit states is thus very simple :
different SLOCC classes are labelled by the rank of
\Psi_{AB}
. An
operator representation of the qutrit density matrix has been developed, and
qutrit entanglement has been studied; see e.g. in
[
CM00a
]
. Furthermore, the
generalized concurrence formula as a measure of two qutrits entanglement has
also been introduced
[
HFL05
]
.

Nowadays, there is ongoing work to develop quantum computers using not only
qubits, but also qutrits with multiple states
[
Cho
]
. Indeed, qutrits
exhibit several peculiar and interesting features when used for storing
quantum information; for example, they are more robust to decoherence under
certain environmental interactions
[
MDDR+04
]
; however, in reality their
direct manipulation might be tricky, and it is thus often easier to use an
entanglement with a qubit
[
LWL+08
]
. In
[
PV08
]
, the maximum
quantum violation of over 100 tight bipartite Bell inequalities on systems
with up to four-dimensional Hilbert spaces was numerically determined, and
it was found that there exist Bell inequalities that can be violated more
with real qutrits than with complex qubits.

The physical implementation of a qutrit quantum computer in the context of
trapped ions has been studied
[
KGRS03
]
, and quantum computer simulation
packages for qutrits have been implemented
[
TJD11
]
. New studies claim that
qutrits offer a promising path towards extending the frontier of quantum
computers
[
GBD+20
,
LYF13
]
. In fact, accessing the third state in current
quantum processors is primarily useful for researchers exploring the cutting
edge of quantum computing, quantum physics
[
BRS+21
]
and those
interested in traditional, qubit-based algorithms alike. Promisingly,
qutrits cannot only increase the amount of information encoded in a single
element, but they can also enable techniques that can dramatically decrease
readout errors
[
MOPL+09
]
. Recent progress in world-leading,
cutting-edge quantum computer laboratories has shown how qutrit-qutrit gates
can reduce the cost of decomposing 3-qubit gates into basic 2-qubit
components
[
HHDR21
]
. This is in part due to the much larger state
space accessible using qutrits : for instance, single qutrit operations
pertain to a 3-dimensional Hilbert space, while 2-qutrit
operations live in a 9-dimensional Hilbert space, which is more than twofold
larger than the four-dimensional Hilbert space of the 2-qubit case.

2.1.1.
Neutrinos as qutrits

Recently, qutrits found an intriguing application in high energy particle
physics : indeed, neutrino flavour states, which are a superposition of
three states, have been characterized in terms of qutrits. This can be done
by mapping the density matrix for neutrinos to a
generalized Poincaré sphere

[
CM00a
,
Gam16
]
.

The Poincaré sphere has its origin in optics and is a way of visualizing
different types of polarized light using the mapping from
SU_{2}
to
S^{3}
. A qubit represents a point on the Poincaré sphere of
SU_{2}
defined
as the complex projective line

(2.3)

P^{1}\mathbb{C}=\frac{SU_{2}}{U_{1}}.

A generalization of the Poincaré sphere to
SU_{3}
can be constructed

[
AMM97
,
MM97
,
KMSM97
]
, yielding to the characterization of qutrits as points on
the complex projective plane
[
BD06
,
Byr98
]

(2.4)

P^{2}\mathbb{C}=\frac{SU_{3}}{U_{2}}.

An
n
-qutrit register can represent
3^{n}
different states
simultaneously, i.e., a superposition state vector in
3^{n}
-dimensional
Hilbert space
[
CM00b
]
. Entanglement of neutrinos realized in terms of
Poincaré sphere representation for two resp. three-flavour neutrino
states using
SU_{2}
Pauli matrices resp.
SU_{3}
Gell-Mann matrices has
been investigated in
[
JCB22
]
.

2.2.
Real qutrits: retrits

Real
quantum mechanics (that is, quantum mechanics defined over
real vector spaces) dates back to Stückelberg
[
Stu60
]
, and
provides an interesting theory, whose study may shed some light to
discriminate among the aspects of
quantum entanglement
which are
unique to standard quantum theory and those which are more generic over
other physical theories endowed with this phenomenon
[
CFR01
]
. Besides

rebits
(real qubits), in real quantum mechanics there has been an
interest in introducing
retrits
, i.e. qutrits with
real

coefficients for probability amplitudes of a three-level system, namely a
three-level quantum state that may be expressed as a
real
linear
combination of the orthonormal basis states
\left|0\right\rangle
,
\left|1\right\rangle
and
\left|2\right\rangle
; such a system
may be in a superposition of three mutually orthonormal quantum states

[
NJDH+13
]
, which span the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{3}

[
Byr98
]
,
with dim
=3
(over
\mathbb{C}
for qutrits, or over
\mathbb{R}
for
retrits). As for rebits, also for retrits the density matrix of the
processed quantum state
\rho
is real; i.e., at each point in the quantum
computation, it holds that
\left\langle x|\rho|y\right\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
, for all
\left|x\right\rangle
,
\left|y\right\rangle
in
the computational basis.

2.2.1.
Black hole/string charges as retrits

In recent years, the relevance of rebits in high-energy theoretical physics

1
1

1

In supergravity, the approximation of

disregarding the charge quantization.
.

m
. Indeed,
if a grading as above is given then by defining
\theta(x)=\zeta^{i}x
for

x\in\mathfrak{g}_{i}
defines an automorphism of order
m
. Conversely, if
\theta

is an automorphism of order
m
then letting
\mathfrak{g}_{i}
be the eigenspace of

\theta
with eigenvalue
\omega^{i}
yields a
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
-grading. We also
remark that given a grading as above, the subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
is reductive
(
[
Kac90
, Lemma 8.1(c)]
), and that
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
is naturally a
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
-module.

The finite order automorphisms have been classified, up to conjugacy in

\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{g})
, by several authors. One classification is due to Kac; we refer
to
[
Hel78
, Chapter X, §5]
for a thorough treatment. The inner
automorphisms in this
classification correspond to labelings by non-negative integers of the affine
Dynkin diagram. Here we just consider the case where all labels
are 0 or 1. Let
\Phi
be the root system of
\mathfrak{g}
with simple system
\Delta
.
Let
\tilde{\alpha}
be the highest root of
\Phi
with respect to the dominance
order induced by
\Delta
. Then the nodes of the affine Dynkin diagram
correspond to the elements of
\widetilde{\Delta}=\Delta\cup\{-\tilde{\alpha}\}
.
The weights of
the nodes
-\tilde{\alpha}
is 1 whereas the weights of the other nodes
are the coefficients of
\tilde{\alpha}
with respect to
\Delta
.
Let
\Pi_{0},\Pi_{1}\subset\widetilde{\Delta}
be the subsets consisting of the
roots with label 0, 1 respectively. Then we have the following (see

[
GOV94
, Chapter 3, §3.7]
):

•

the automorphism
\theta
associated to the labeling has order equal to
the sum of the weights of the nodes corresponding to
\Pi_{0}
,

•

\Pi_{0}
generates the root system of the semisimple part of
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
,

•

the module
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
has
|\Pi_{1}|
irreducible components.

Now let
G=\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{g})^{\circ}
be the identity component of the automorphism
group of
\mathfrak{g}
. The Lie algebra of
G
is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{g}
(to be precise,
it is equal to
\mathrm{ad}\mathfrak{g}\subset\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})
). Let
G_{0}
be the connected subgroup
of
G
whose
Lie algebra is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
(or, equal to
\mathrm{ad}\mathfrak{g}_{0}
). Then
G_{0}

naturally acts on
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
. The representation of
G_{0}
in

\mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak{g}_{1})
is called
a
\theta
-representation. They were introduced and studied in detail by
Vinberg,
[
Vin76a
,
Vin79
]
. Among other things he developed methods
for classifying the orbits of
G_{0}
in
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
.

A first observation here is that
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
is closed under Jordan decomposition.
Indeed, if
x=x_{s}+x_{n}
is the Jordan decomposition of
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{1}
, then

\theta(x)=\theta(x_{s})+\theta(x_{n})
is the Jordan decomposition of

\theta(x)
. But also
\theta(x)=\zeta x=\zeta x_{s}+\zeta x_{n}
is the
Jordan decomposition of
\theta(x)
. Hence
x_{s},x_{n}\in\mathfrak{g}_{1}
. This immediately
divides the orbits into three groups:
nilpotent, semisimple and mixed orbits (that consists, respectively, of
nilpotent, semisimple, and mixed (that is, neither nilpotent nor semisimple)
elements). We will briefly indicate the methods for classifying these
orbits in the relevant sections of this paper.

\hat{\mathfrak{g}}=\mathfrak{a}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{a}_{2}\oplus\mathfrak{a}_{3}

4.1.
Permuting the tensor factors

Consider the vector space
\mathcal{V}
. Let
\pi\in S_{3}
be a permutation of

\{1,2,3\}
.
To
\pi
we associate a linear map (denoted by the same symbol)

\pi:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}

\pi(v_{1}\otimes v_{2}\otimes v_{3})=v_{\pi(1)}\otimes v_{\pi(2)}\otimes v_{\pi(3)}

\pi:\widehat{G}\to\widehat{G}
,
\pi(g_{1}\times g_{2}\times g_{3})=g_{\pi(1)}\times g_{\pi(2)}\times g_{\pi(3)}
. Then obviously we have

\pi(g)\cdot\pi(v)=\pi(g\cdot v)\text{ for $g\in\widehat{G}$, $v\in V$.}

It follows that
\widehat{G}\cdot\pi(v)=\pi(\widehat{G}\cdot v)
. Furthermore,
denoting the stabilizer of

in
\widehat{G}
by

we have

Z_{\widehat{G}}(\pi(v))=\pi(Z_{\widehat{G}}(v))

As seen in Section

it is of interest to classify the orbits of

in

up to permutation of
the tensor factors, or up to permutation of two of the tensor factors.
In other words, instead of the group
\widehat{G}
we consider the groups

\mathrm{Sym}_{3}\ltimes\widehat{G}
and
\mathrm{Sym}_{2}\ltimes\widehat{G}
(where

\mathrm{Sym}_{2}
permutes the second and third factors only). For the
nilpotent elements the permutation action is given in detail in Table

. For the semisimple and mixed elements see Remarks

7.5
and
8.2
respectively.

4.2.

We can of course also consider the action of

on the module

(where the last factor is the dual module

Let

be defined
by
\sigma_{3}(g_{1},g_{2},g_{3})=(g_{1},g_{2},g_{3}^{-T})

on

with respect to the

5.
Galois cohomology and real orbits

One of the main goals of this paper is to achieve a classification of the
orbits of
\widehat{G}(\mathbb{R})=\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})\times\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})\times\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})

on the real space spanned by the elementary tensors
|j_{1}\,j_{2}\,j_{3}\rangle

in
\mathcal{V}
.
Our methods are based on Galois cohomology, to which we here give a
brief introduction.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
, stable under complex conjugation

g\mapsto\bar{g}
(given by the complex conjugation of the matrix entries).
By
\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{R})
we denote the group of real points,

\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{R})=\{g\in\mathcal{G}\mid\bar{g}=g\}.

We have the following definitions:

•

a
g\in\mathcal{G}
is a
cocycle
if
\bar{g}g=1
,

•

two cocycles
g_{1},g_{2}
are
equivalent
if there is an
h\in\mathcal{G}
such
that
h^{-1}g_{1}\bar{h}=g_{2}
,

•

the set of equivalence classes of cocycles is the
first Galois
cohomology set

\mathrm{H}^{1}\mathcal{G}
of
\mathcal{G}
.

We note that these definitions are an
ad hoc
version of the classical
definition of Galois cohomology of a group, to the special case where the
Galois group has order 2. We refer to the book by Serre
[
Ser97
]

for a more general account.

The crucial result for our computations is the following theorem; see

[
Ser97
, Section I.5.4]
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that
\mathrm{H}^{1}\mathcal{G}=1
. Let
\mathcal{G}
act on the set
U
. Suppose that

U
has an involution
u\mapsto\bar{u}
such that
\overline{g\cdot u}=\bar{g}\cdot\bar{u}
for
g\in\mathcal{G}
,
u\in U
. Let
u_{0}\in U
be real (that is,

\bar{u}_{0}=u_{0}
) and consider the set of real points of the orbit of
u_{0}
,

\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{R})=\{v\in\mathcal{G}\cdot u_{0}\mid\bar{v}=v\}.

Let
Z_{\mathcal{G}}(u_{0})=\{g\in\mathcal{G}\mid g\cdot u_{0}=u_{0}\}
be the stabilizer of
u_{0}

in
\mathcal{G}
. Then there exists a bijection between
\mathrm{H}^{1}Z_{\mathcal{G}}(u_{0})
and the set of

\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{R})
-orbits in
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{R})
. This bijection is described explicitly
as follows. Let
[g]\in\mathrm{H}^{1}Z_{\mathcal{G}}(u_{0})
, then there exists
h\in\mathcal{G}
such
that
h^{-1}\bar{h}=g
(as
\mathrm{H}^{1}\mathcal{G}=1
). Then the class
[g]
corresponds
to the
\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{R})
-orbit of
(h\cdot u_{0})

In order to use this we need to be able to compute the first Galois cohomology
set of matrix groups. This can be done by computer. However, we also use
some criteria to derive the cohomology sets directly.

Criterion 5.2
.

We have that
\mathrm{H}^{1}\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
and
\mathrm{H}^{1}\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{C})
are both trivial.
So in particular
\mathrm{H}^{1}T
is trivial, where
T\subset\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
is a torus.

The next criterion is
[
BdGL21
, Corollary 3.2.2]
.

Criterion 5.3
.

If
\mathcal{G}
is finite, abelian and of odd order then
\mathrm{H}^{1}\mathcal{G}
is trivial.

Criterion 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a group of order
p^{m}
, where
p
is an odd prime. Then

\mathrm{H}^{1}\mathcal{G}
is trivial.

This follows from
[
BdGL21
, Lemma 3.2.3]
.
The next criterion is
[
BdGL21
, Lemma 3.2.6]
.

Criterion 5.5
.

If
\mathcal{G}
is finite of order
2p^{m}
where
p
is an odd prime,
then
\mathrm{H}^{1}\mathcal{G}=\{1,[c]\}
, where
c
is a real element of order 2.

The next criterion is
[
BdGL21
, Proposition 3.3.16]
.

Criterion 5.6
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a linear algebraic group with identity component
\mathcal{G}^{\circ}
.
Suppose that
\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{G}^{\circ}
is of order
p^{n}
for some odd prime
p
and

n\geq 0
and
|\mathrm{H}^{1}\mathcal{G}^{\circ}|<p
. Then the canonical map

\mathrm{H}^{1}\mathcal{G}^{\circ}\to\mathrm{H}^{1}\mathcal{G}
is bijective.

6.
Nilpotent orbits

In this section we comment on the methods for classifying complex and
real nilpotent orbits. We present the complex classification of Nurmiev,

[
Nur00b
]
to which we add some data (see below). We also classify the
real nilpotent orbits.

From
[
BdGL21
, §4.3]
we recall some general results.
Let
\mathfrak{a}=\oplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}}\mathfrak{a}_{i}
be a
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
-graded semisimple Lie algebra
over a field of characteristic 0. Let

be a group of automorpisms of

such that each element preserves the spaces
\mathfrak{a}_{i}
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
, and such
that

contains
\exp\mathrm{ad}x
for all nilpotent

. Then

•

A nilpotent

lies in a
homogeneous

\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triple

(h,e,f)
. This means that
h\in\mathfrak{a}_{0}

and

[h,e]=2e,\,[h,f]=-2f,\,[e,f]=h.

•

Two nilpotent elements

\mathfrak{sl}_{2}

there is a

with
\sigma(h)=h^{\prime}
,
\sigma(e)=e^{\prime}
,
\sigma(f)=f^{\prime}
.

7.
Semisimple Elements

In this section we consider the orbits of semisimple elements of
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
.
We first describe the complex classification where we follow Nurmiev

[
Nur00b
]
adding many details on the proof of the correctness of the
classification. These are then also needed for the classification in the
real case.

7.1.
Semisimple orbits: the complex case

Much of the theory needed to classify the semisimple orbits in
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
is due
to Vinberg
[
Vin76a
]
. Here we give a short overview of some of the main
concepts and results.

A Cartan subspace
\mathfrak{C}\subset\mathcal{V}
is by definition a maximal subspace
consisting of commuting semisimple elements. Vinberg
[
Vin76a
]
showed
that any two Cartan subspaces are conjugated with respect to
G_{0}
.
As a consequence, every semisimple orbit has a representative in any given
Cartan subspace.

Definition 7.1
.

The Weyl group of a Cartan subspace
\mathfrak{C}
is
W_{\mathfrak{C}}=N_{G_{0}}(\mathfrak{C})/Z_{G_{0}}(\mathfrak{C})
.

The group
W_{\mathfrak{C}}
is a finite complex reflection group,

[
Vin76a
, Theorem 8]
. Moreover, two
elements from
\mathfrak{C}
are
G_{0}
-conjugate if and only if they are

W_{\mathfrak{C}}
-conjugate,
[
Vin76a
, Theorem 2]
. So the classification of
of the semisimple orbits reduces to the classification of the
W_{\mathfrak{C}}
-orbits
in
\mathfrak{C}
where the latter is any fixed Cartan subspace. Those orbits
are infinitely many, and it does not seem to be possible to present them
in an irredundant list. However, it is possible to define several special
subsets of
\mathfrak{C}
such that each semisimple orbit has a point in exactly one
of them. Secondly, two elements of the same subset are
G_{0}
-conjugate
if and only if they are conjugate under an explicitly given finite group.
Here we show how that is done in our case.

Consider the following elements

\displaystyle u_{1}

\displaystyle=\text{\small$|000\rangle$}+\text{\small$|111\rangle$}+\text{\small$|222\rangle$}

\displaystyle u_{2}

\displaystyle=\text{\small$|012\rangle$}+\text{\small$|120\rangle$}+\text{\small$|201\rangle$}

\displaystyle u_{3}

\displaystyle=\text{\small$|021\rangle$}+\text{\small$|210\rangle$}+\text{\small$|102\rangle$}.

By
[
Nur00b
]
, see also
[
Wal17
, §5.4.4, Exercise 2]
, these
span a Cartan subspace
\mathfrak{C}
of
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
. We also checked this by computer.
In the sequel we fix this space.

Now we briefly review the construction of complex reflections in the Weyl
group given in
[
VE88
, §3.3]
, adapted to our situation. Let
\mathfrak{h}
be
the centralizer of
\mathfrak{C}
in the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. Then
\mathfrak{h}
is
a Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
. Let
\Phi
be the root system of

with respect to
\mathfrak{h}

\theta
of
order 3 (see Section
4
). This automorphism stabilizes
\mathfrak{h}
.
It induces a map
\theta_{*}
of the dual space
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
by
\theta_{*}(\mu)(h)=\mu(\theta(h))
for

. For a root

\pm\alpha

\pm\theta_{*}(\alpha)

. By

. Let
\mathfrak{C}(\alpha)=\mathfrak{C}\cap U(\alpha)

\mathfrak{C}_{0}(\alpha)=\{p\in\mathfrak{C}\mid\alpha(p)=0\}

\mathfrak{C}=\mathfrak{C}_{0}(\alpha)\oplus\mathfrak{C}(\alpha)

defining the linear map

Remark 7.2
.

The set
\mathfrak{C}_{p}^{\circ}

w\,q\neq q

w\,q\neq q

\psi_{\alpha}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\neq 0

taking the product of all
\psi_{\alpha}
where
\alpha

\mathfrak{C}_{p}^{\circ}

Now for

v\cdot\mathfrak{C}_{p}^{\circ}=\mathfrak{C}_{q}^{\circ}\text{ if and only if }W_{q}=vW_{p}v^{-1}.

Theorem 7.3

Two elements of

-conjugate if and only if they are

-conjugate.

We also have the following lemma, which is of fundamental importance for
the complex and for the real classification.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
p\in\mathfrak{C}
and
q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathfrak{C}_{p}^{\circ}
. Let
Z_{G_{0}}(q_{i})=\{g\in G_{0}\mid g\cdot q_{i}=q_{i}\}
be their stabilizers in
G_{0}
. Then
Z_{G_{0}}(q_{1})=Z_{G_{0}}(q_{2})
.

Proof.

Let
w\in W_{\mathfrak{C}}
be a complex reflection. Then in the above notation we have

w=w_{\alpha}
or
w=w_{\alpha}^{2}
for an
\alpha\in\Phi
. By the construction
of the
w_{\alpha}
it follows that
w_{\alpha}(q)=q
if and only if
\alpha(q)=0
.
Set
\Phi_{i}=\{\alpha\in\Phi\mid\alpha(q_{i})=0\}
. Then as
W_{q_{1}}=W_{q_{2}}
it follows that
\Phi_{1}=\Phi_{2}
. Let
\mathfrak{z}_{i}=\{x\in\mathfrak{g}\mid[x,q_{i}]=0\}
. Then

\mathfrak{z}_{i}=\mathfrak{h}\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi_{i}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},

where
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
denotes the root space in
\mathfrak{g}
corresponding to the
root
\alpha
. It follows that
\mathfrak{z}_{1}=\mathfrak{z}_{2}
. By

[
Ste75
, Corollary 3.11]

the stablizers
Z_{G}(q_{i})
are connected. The Lie algebra of
Z_{G}(q_{i})
is

\mathfrak{z}_{i}
. So
Z_{G}(q_{1})=Z_{G}(q_{2})
. But
Z_{G_{0}}(q_{i})=G_{0}\cap Z_{G}(q_{i})
.
∎

Here we comment on the classification up to permutation of the tensor factors

Let

\displaystyle=\{g\in\widehat{G}\mid g\cdot q\in\mathcal{F}\text{ for all }q\in\mathcal{F}\}

\displaystyle Z_{\widehat{G}}(\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle=\{g\in\widehat{G}\mid g\cdot q=q\text{ for all }q\in\mathcal{F}\}.

Define a map

in the following way. Let

We have the following theorem. For a proof see

[

Theorem 7.6

Let

.
Write

.
Suppose that for each

there is

with

.
Then
\mathcal{O}
has a real point if and only if
there exist

and

with

then

An

x=s+n

where
s
and
n

respectively such that

8.1.

Each mixed element is conjugate under
G_{0}
to an element whose

Section

where
\mathfrak{a}_{1}=\mathfrak{a}\cap\mathfrak{g}_{i}

s

\mathfrak{a}_{1}

\mathfrak{a}_{0}

Z_{G_{0}}(s)

\mathfrak{a}_{1}

for the classification of the nilpotent orbits of a

have the same

\mathfrak{g}

8.2.
Mixed elements: the real case

The real semisimple elements are divided in two types. There are the real
semisimple elements in one of the families described in Section
7
;
we say that those are canonical semisimple elements. Secondly we have the
non-canonical semisimple elements: they are
\widehat{G}
-conjugate, but not

\widehat{G}(\mathbb{R})
-conjugate to elements of one of the families. From Section

7.2
we see that they occur relative to the second and third
families. Correspondingly we have canonical and non-canonical mixed elements,
whose semisimple parts are canonical and non-canonical respectively.

Each complex orbit of a canonical mixed element has a real representative given
in the tables of the previous section. Let
u=s+e
be such an element. Then

e\in\mathfrak{a}_{1}
, where
\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{z}_{\mathfrak{g}}(s)

(h,e,f)

\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triple in
\mathfrak{a}
containing
e

Z_{\widehat{G}}(s,h,e,f)=\{g\in\widehat{G}\mid g\cdot s=s,\,g\cdot h=h,\,g\cdot e=e,\,g\cdot f=f\}.

Then the
\widehat{G}(\mathbb{R})
-orbits contained in the complex orbit
\widehat{G}\cdot u
correspond
bijectively to
\mathrm{H}^{1}Z_{\widehat{G}}(s,h,e,f)

of Section
8.1
we see that this cohomology set is mostly
trivial. In those cases the complex orbit
\widehat{G}\cdot(s+e)
contains one real

\mu(y_{0})\in Y
