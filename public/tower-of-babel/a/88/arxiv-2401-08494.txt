Noncommutative Geometry of Hensel-Steinitz Algebras
Shelley Hebert, Slawomir Klimek, Matt McBride, J. Wilson Peoples

Abstract
. We discuss various aspects of noncommutative geometry of smooth subalgebras of Hensel-Steinitz algebras. In particular we study the structure of derivations and K K -Theory of those smooth subalgebras.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we study natural C
∗
-algebras which are related to multiplication maps of the ring of
s
-adic integers
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
. We name those algebras Hensel-Steinitz algebras. They are defined as crossed products by endomorphisms (
2.2
) of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
induced by
s
-adic multiplications on the space of
s
-adic integers. We provide a short appendix summarizing relevant properties of crossed products by monomorphisms with hereditary ranges. Hensel-Steinitz algebras are one of many interesting examples of C
∗
-algebras with connections to number theory, a subject of increasing interest, see for example
[
8
]
.

Alternative C
∗
-algebras associated to endomorphisms of groups in general and to
s
-adic multiplication in particular have been considered before, see in particular
[
7
]
,
[
14
]
and
[
21
]
However, in those papers, the endomorphisms of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
are not the same as ours and they come from the endomorphisms
x\mapsto(x-x\text{ mod }s)/s
of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
. Consequently, the entire analysis and the properties of the corresponding C
∗
-algebras differ significantly.

In this paper we concentrate mostly on smooth subalgebras of Hensel-Steinitz algebras, which in noncommutative geometry capture a smooth structure on such “quantum spaces”. Smooth algebras play an important role in cyclic cohomology theory
[
22
]
and other noncommutative geometry applications
[
6
]
. Further motivation for studying smooth subalgebras and general frameworks can be found in
[
2
]
and
[
3
]
.

This work is a continuation of previous papers on the subject of noncommutative geometry of various examples of quantum spaces, in particular
[
12
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
18
]
. A companion paper
[
11
]
discusses further generalizations to other multiplication maps in the ring of
p
-adic integers.

We verify that the smooth subalgebras studied in this paper are dense
*
-subalgebras closed under the holomorphic functional calculus and complete in their own stronger locally convex topology. We show also that they are closed under the smooth functional calculus of self-adjoint elements.

Next, we look at derivations on smooth subalgebras of Hensel-Steinitz algebras. Derivations naturally arise in differential geometry as vector fields and play an analogous role in noncommutative geometry. It was observed in
[
4
]
that derivations on smooth subalgebras, modulo inner derivations, often have geometric significance. Using “generalized” Toeplitz operators we provide detailed structure of the space of continuous derivations on smooth subalgebras of Hensel-Steinitz algebras in Theorem
4.10
, a geometrically interesting and unexpected result.

Finally, we discuss
K
-Theory of Hensel-Steinitz algebras.
K
-Theory is a key invariant in the noncommutative topology of quantum spaces. We relate Hensel-Steinitz algebras with some familiar C
∗
-algebras and compute their
K
-Theory. By stability under the holomorphic functional calculus, smooth subalgebras have the same
K
-Theory as the Hensel-Steinitz algebras.

The paper is organized as follows. In section 2 we define Hensel-Steinitz algebras and study their structure. In section 3 we discuss smooth subalgebras of Hensel-Steinitz algebras and their properties. In section 4 we classify continuous derivations on the smooth subalgebras from section 3. In section 5 we discuss the
K
-Theory of Hensel-Steinitz algebras. Finally, in section 6, the appendix, we review crossed products by monomorphisms with hereditary ranges.

2.
The Hensel-Steinitz Algebras

For
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
s\geq 2
, the space of
s
-adic integers
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
is defined as consisting of infinite sums:

{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}=\left\{x=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}x_{j}s^{j}:0\leq x_{j}\leq s-1,j=0,1,2,\ldots\right\}\,.

We call the above expansion of
x
its
s
-adic expansion. Note that
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
is isomorphic with the countable product
\prod{\mathbb{Z}}/s{\mathbb{Z}}
and so, when the product is equipped with the Tychonoff topology, it is a Cantor set. It is also a metric space with the usual norm,
|\cdot|_{s}
, which is defined as follows:

|0|_{s}=0\textrm{ and, if }x\neq 0,|x|_{s}=s^{-n},

(2.1)

where
x_{n}
is the first nonzero term in the above
s
-adic expansion.

Moreover,
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
is an abelian ring with unity with respect to addition and multiplication with a carry and we have:

{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}\cong\lim_{\longleftarrow}{\mathbb{Z}}/s^{n}{\mathbb{Z}}\,,

for more details, see
[
20
]
.

The main objects of interest in this paper are the maps,
\alpha:C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\to C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
defined by

(\alpha f)(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &f\left(\frac{x}{s}\right)&&\textrm{ if }s|x\\
&0&&\textrm{else}\end{aligned}\right.

(2.2)

and
\beta:C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\to C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
given by

(\beta f)(x)=f(sx).

Notice that both maps are endomorphisms. It is clear that
\alpha
is an injection and we have the following relation:

((\beta\alpha)f)(x)=\beta(\alpha f)(x)=(\alpha f)(sx)=f(x)

for any
f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
. Additionally, we have

\beta(1)=1\textrm{ and }(\alpha\beta)f=\alpha(1)f.

Notice that

\alpha(1)(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &1&&\textrm{ if }s|x\\
&0&&\textrm{else}\end{aligned}\right.

is the characteristic function of the subset of the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
consisting of those elements that are divisible by
s
. It follows from the formula for
\alpha
that the range of
\alpha
is equal to
\alpha(1)C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
. Thus
\alpha
is a monomorphism with hereditary range and in particular
\beta
satisfies equation (
6.1
) from the appendix.

We define the Hensel-Steinitz algebra
HS(s)
to be the following crossed product:

HS(s):=C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}\,,

see the appendix for details on crossed products by monomorphisms with hereditary range.

It is useful to realize
HS(s)
as a concrete C
∗
-algebra. Let
H=\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
and
\{E_{l}\}_{l\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
be its canonical basis, then since
{\mathbb{Z}}
is a dense subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
, the mapping
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\to B(H)
,
f\mapsto M_{f}
, given by

M_{f}E_{l}=f(l)E_{l}

is a faithful representation of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
.
Let
V
be the
s
-adic shift operator defined on
H
via

VE_{l}=E_{sl}\,.

A simple calculation verifies that

V^{*}E_{l}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &E_{l/s}&&\textrm{ if }s|l\\
&0&&\textrm{else.}\end{aligned}\right.

Given
l\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, write
l=s^{m}l^{\prime}
and define the following diagonal operator in
H
:

\mathbb{P}E_{s^{m}l^{\prime}}=mE_{s^{m}l^{\prime}},\ \ \ \mathbb{P}E_{0}=0.

It is clear that
\mathbb{P}
is an unbounded self-adjoint operator on
H
. We need this operator for the proof of the next theorem, however it is used substantially more in the upcoming sections.

Theorem 2.1
.

There is an isomorphism between
C^{*}
-algebras:

HS(s)=C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}\cong C^{*}(V,M_{f}:f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}))\,.

Proof.

To prove this we must first verify the relations between
V
and
M_{f}
. From the definition of
V
, an immediate calculation shows that
V^{*}V=1
.
Similarly we have that

VM_{f}V^{*}E_{l}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &VM_{f}\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)E_{l/s}&&\textrm{ if }s|l\\
&0&&\textrm{ else}\end{aligned}\right.=\left\{\begin{aligned} &f\left(\frac{l}{s}\right)E_{l}&&\textrm{ if }s|l\\
&0&&\textrm{ else}\end{aligned}\right.=M_{\alpha f}E_{l}\,.

Thus
V
and
M_{f}
satisfy the relations of the crossed product of Stacey, see the appendix. Also, it follows from a direct calculation (and from general considerations in the appendix) that we have:

\displaystyle M_{f}V=VM_{\beta f}.

(2.3)

The algebra
C^{*}(V,M_{f}:f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}))
carries a natural circle action defined by maps
\rho_{\theta}
given by

\rho_{\theta}(a)=e^{2\pi i\theta\mathbb{P}}ae^{-2\pi i\theta\mathbb{P}}\,.

(2.4)

A calculation on basis elements of
H
shows that
\rho_{\theta}(V)=e^{2\pi i\theta}V
and that
\rho_{\theta}(M_{f})=M_{f}
. It follows that
\rho_{\theta}
is a continuous 1-parameter family of automorphisms of
C^{*}(V,M_{f})
with
f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
. Additionally, we infer from the relation
VV^{*}=\alpha(1)
that the
\rho_{\theta}
invariant elements of
C^{*}(V,M_{f})
are precisely the multiplication operators
M_{f}
.

Next we consider an expectation in
C^{*}(V,M_{f})
onto the invariant subalgebra
C^{*}(M_{f})\subseteq C^{*}(V,M_{f})
. Define the map
E:C^{*}(V,M_{f})\to C^{*}(V,M_{f})
by

E(a)=\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{\theta}(a)\,d\theta\,.

Clearly,
E
is an expectation in
C^{*}(V,M_{f})
onto
C^{*}(M_{f})\cong C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
.

For any
a\in C^{*}(V,M_{f})
given by a finite sum

a=\sum_{n\geq 0}V^{n}M_{f_{n}}+\sum_{n<0}M_{f_{n}}(V^{*})^{-n}\,,

we get

M_{f_{n}}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &E((V^{*})^{-n}a)&&\textrm{ if }n\geq 0\\
&E(aV^{n})&&\textrm{ if }n<0\,.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.5)

In, particular, we have that

\|M_{f_{0}}\|\leq\|a\|,

which is the O’Donovan condition from the appendix. Finally, as mentioned above, the density of
{\mathbb{Z}}
in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
implies that the representation is faithful. Thus we have
HS(s)\cong C^{*}(V,M_{f})
.
∎

Below we consider the Hensel-Steinitz algebra
HS(s)
as the concrete C
∗
-algebra from the above proposition. To understand the structure of
HS(s)
we first look at an ideal, denoted by
I_{s}
, which can be defined in the following way. Consider the map
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\to B(\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}))
defined by
f\mapsto m_{f}
, where
m_{f}
is given by

m_{f}E_{l}=f(0)E_{l}.

Additionally, let
v\in B(\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}))
denote the standard bilateral shift

vE_{l}=E_{l+1}.

It is easy to check that sending
V\mapsto v
and
M_{f}\mapsto m_{f}
gives rise to a representation of
HS(s)
onto
C^{*}(v,m_{f})\cong C({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
. Denote this surjective representation by

\pi_{0}:HS(s)\to C({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}).

We define
I_{s}
to be the ideal which is the kernel of this representation:

I_{s}:=\textup{Ker }\pi_{0}.

The ideal
I_{s}\subseteq HS(s)
can also be described as the subalgebra generated by the operators
V^{m}M_{f}
,
m\geq 0

and
M_{f}(V^{*})^{-m}
,
m<0
such that
f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
with
f(0)=0
.

Let
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times}
be the unit ball in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
. Notice that if
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
has the
s
-adic expansion:

x=x_{0}+x_{1}s+x_{2}s^{2}+\cdots

then,
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times}
if and only if
x_{0}\neq 0
.
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times}
is both a closed and open subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
. Let
\mathcal{K}
be the ideal of compact operators in
B(H)
.

Theorem 2.2
.

There is the following isomorphism of C
∗
-algebras:

I_{s}\cong C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times})\otimes\mathcal{K}\,.

Proof.

As noticed above, we have:

I_{s}\cong C^{*}(V^{m}M_{f}:f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}),f(0)=0).

Suppose
f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
with
f(0)=0
. For
x\neq 0
we can uniquely write
f(x)=f(s^{m}\tilde{x})
where
x=s^{m}\tilde{x}
and
|\tilde{x}|_{s}=1
, so
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times}
. Consequently, we have an isomorphism of C
∗
-algebras

\{f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}):f(0)=0\}\cong\left\{F\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}):\lim_{m\to\infty}F(m,\cdot)=0\right\}:=C_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})

where
f\mapsto F
is given by
f(s^{m}\tilde{x})=F(m,\tilde{x})
.
Additionally, we also have the following identification:

C_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})\cong c_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})\otimes C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times})\,.

Given nonzero
l
, decompose
l=s^{m}l^{\prime}
, with
l^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
,
|l^{\prime}|_{s}=1
. For
\lambda\in C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
, define the multiplication operator
M_{\lambda}:H\to H
by the following

M_{\lambda}E_{l}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\lambda(l^{\prime})E_{l}&&\textrm{if }l\neq 0\\
&0&&\textrm{if }l=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.6)

Similarly, given a sequence
\chi=\{\chi(l)\}\in c_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})
and the decomposition above, define
M_{\chi}:H\to H
by

M_{\chi}E_{l}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\chi(m)E_{l}&&\textrm{if }l\neq 0\\
&0&&\textrm{if }l=0.\end{aligned}\right.

Then, we have the following isomorphism of
C^{*}
-algebras:

C^{*}(M_{f}:f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}),f(0)=0)\cong C^{*}(M_{\lambda}M_{\chi}:\lambda\in C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}),\chi\in c_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}))\cong C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})\otimes c_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})\,.

Notice we have the following commutation relations:

M_{\lambda}M_{\chi}=M_{\chi}M_{\lambda}\text{ and }M_{\lambda}V=VM_{\lambda}.

(2.7)

Using the relations in equation (
2.7
) the following two C
∗
-algebras are isomorphic:

\displaystyle C^{*}(V^{n}M_{f}:f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}),f(0)=0,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})

\displaystyle C^{*}(M_{\lambda}:\lambda\in C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}))\otimes C^{*}(V^{n}M_{\chi}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},\chi\in c_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})).

We claim the following isomorphism:

C^{*}(V^{n}M_{\chi}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},\chi\in c_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}))\cong\mathcal{K}\,.

For
j\geq 0
let
\chi_{j}\in c_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})
be a sequence given by
\chi_{j}=\{\delta_{ij}\}_{i\geq 0}
and define the operators
P_{ij}
by

P_{ij}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &V^{i-j}M_{\chi_{j}}&&\textrm{if }i\geq j\\
&M_{\chi_{i}}(V^{*})^{j-i}&&\textrm{if }j\geq i\,.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.8)

Clearly,
\{P_{ij}\}
form the system of units of
\mathcal{K}
, thus we have

C^{*}(P_{ij})\cong\mathcal{K}\,.

Finally,
C^{*}(P_{ij})
and
C^{*}(V^{n}M_{\chi}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},\chi\in c_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}))
clearly coincide and thus our claim holds, finishing the proof.
∎

This theorem leads to the short exact sequence describing the structure of
HS(s)
:

0\to C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times})\otimes\mathcal{K}\to HS(s)\to C({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})\to 0,

which is instrumental in further analysis of the Hensel-Steinitz algebra. The quotient map in the above short exact sequence will be denoted by

q:HS(s)\to C({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}).

For completeness, we briefly describe two more structural results regarding
HS(s)
. The first observation realizes
HS(s)
as a full corner of a regular crossed product by an automorphism C
∗
-algebra, see also
[
28
]
and
[
19
]
for general constructions of this type. The second statement identifies
HS(s)
with a subalgebra of the tensor product
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times})\otimes\mathcal{T}
, where
\mathcal{T}
is the Toeplitz algebra. This identification motivates the concept of “generalized” Toeplitz operators introduced in the next section.

Consider the space

{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}:=\left\{x=\sum_{j=N}^{\infty}x_{j}s^{j}:N\in{\mathbb{Z}},0\leq x_{j}\leq s-1,x_{N}\neq 0\right\}\,.

It is an abelian ring with unity with respect to the usual addition and multiplication with a carry to the right.
Equipped with the
s
-adic norm given by equation (
2.1
),
{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}
is a locally compact space containing
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
. The key property of the ring
{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}
is that
s
is now invertible. The analog of the endomorphism
\alpha
is an automorphism
\tilde{\alpha}:C_{0}({\mathbb{Q}}_{s})\to C_{0}({\mathbb{Q}}_{s})
given by:

(\tilde{\alpha}f)(x)=f\left(\frac{x}{s}\right).

Consider the crossed product
C_{0}({\mathbb{Q}}_{s})\rtimes_{\tilde{\alpha}}{\mathbb{Z}}

of
C_{0}({\mathbb{Q}}_{s})
by the automorphism
\tilde{\alpha}
. Notice that the characteristic function
1_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}}\in C_{0}({\mathbb{Q}}_{s})\subseteq C_{0}({\mathbb{Q}}_{s})\rtimes_{\tilde{\alpha}}{\mathbb{Z}}
is a projection in
C_{0}({\mathbb{Q}}_{s})\rtimes_{\tilde{\alpha}}{\mathbb{Z}}
and we can identify
HS(s)
with the corner:

HS(s)\cong 1_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}}\cdot\left(C_{0}({\mathbb{Q}}_{s})\rtimes_{\tilde{\alpha}}{\mathbb{Z}}\right)\cdot 1_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}}.

(2.9)

Indeed, we have inclusion
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\ni f\mapsto f\cdot 1_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}}\in 1_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}}\cdot\left(C_{0}({\mathbb{Q}}_{s})\rtimes_{\tilde{\alpha}}{\mathbb{Z}}\right)\cdot 1_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}}
. Additionally, if
U
is the unitary generating
{\mathbb{Z}}
in the crossed product
C_{0}({\mathbb{Q}}_{s})\rtimes_{\tilde{\alpha}}{\mathbb{Z}}
then
V:=1_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}}\cdot U\cdot 1_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}}
is an isometry in the corner
1_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}}\cdot\left(C_{0}({\mathbb{Q}}_{s})\rtimes_{\tilde{\alpha}}{\mathbb{Z}}\right)\cdot 1_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}}
. It is easy to verify that the defining relations for the crossed product
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}
are satisfied and indeed we have an isomorphism in equation (
2.9
).

Moreover, since under the map
x\mapsto x/s
in
{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}
, the smallest invariant set containing
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
is
{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}
itself, it is easy to deduce that the smallest closed two-sided ideal containing the corner
1_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}}\cdot\left(C_{0}({\mathbb{Q}}_{s})\rtimes_{\tilde{\alpha}}{\mathbb{Z}}\right)\cdot 1_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}}
is the full algebra
C_{0}({\mathbb{Q}}_{s})\rtimes_{\tilde{\alpha}}{\mathbb{Z}}
and thus the corner is full.

Our second observation relates Hensel-Steinitz algebra with the Toeplitz algebra. Notice that for the tensor product
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times})\otimes\mathcal{T}
, we have
the following short exact sequence:

0\to C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times})\otimes\mathcal{K}\to C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times})\otimes\mathcal{T}\to C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times}\times{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})\to 0.

Observe that the left-most term in the above sequence is isomorphic to
I_{s}
and that
C({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
is the subalgebra of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times}\times{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
of functions independent of the first variable.
Denote the quotient map in the above short exact sequence by

\sigma:C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times})\otimes\mathcal{T}\to C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times}\times{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}).

Then, using formulas (
2.7
), it is not difficult to see that we have:

HS(s)\cong\sigma^{-1}(C({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}))=\{a\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times})\otimes\mathcal{T}:\sigma(a)\subseteq C({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})\}.

Notice in particular that
I_{s}\cong C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times})\otimes\mathcal{K}=\sigma^{-1}(0)\subseteq\sigma^{-1}(C({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}))\cong HS(s)
.

3.
Smooth Subalgebras

In this section we introduce natural dense
*
-subalgebras of Hensel-Steinitz algebras
HS(s)
and we verify that they satisfy the properties of smooth subalgebras of
[
2
]
. Namely, we show that they are closed under the holomorphic functional calculus, they are complete in their own stronger locally convex topology and that they are closed under the smooth functional calculus of self-adjoint elements. It should be noted that in our case, the definition of smooth subalgebras doesn’t fit the entire framework of
[
2
]
, nor
[
3
]
, and thus requires verifications of all the properties. Studying these smooth subalgebras is a necessity to classifying derivations in the next section.

3.1.
Smooth Ideal
I_{s}^{\infty}
.

Similarly to the approach to smooth subalgebras in
[
16
]
and
[
17
]
, we first define a smooth subalgebra of the ideal
I_{s}
. This is done by using Fourier coefficients of elements of
HS(s)
with respect to the circle action given by equation (
2.4
).

For
a\in HS(s)
and
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
let
f_{n}\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
be defined by the equation (
2.5
).
If
a\in I_{s}
we again use the decomposition of nonzero elements of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
as
s^{m}x
,
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
,
|x|_{s}=1
and we put

F_{n}(m,x)=f_{n}(s^{m}x).

Functions
F_{n}\in C_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
are called the Fourier coefficients of
a\in I_{s}
. By the usual Fourier series theory
[
15
]
, the coefficients
F_{n}
fully determine
a
.

The smooth subalgebra
I_{s}^{\infty}
of
I_{s}
is defined as

I_{s}^{\infty}:=\left\{a\in I_{s}:\{\underset{x}{\textrm{sup}}|F_{n}(m,x)|\}_{m,n}\textrm{ is RD}\right\}\,.

Here RD means the coefficients are rapid decay. In particular, the Fourier coefficients of
a\in I_{s}^{\infty}

are in the space

C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}):=\left\{F\in C_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}):\{\underset{x}{\textrm{sup }}|F(m,x)|\}_{m}\textrm{ is RD}\right\}\,.

Appealing again to general Fourier series theory we have that if
a\in I_{s}^{\infty}
then
a
has a norm convergent series expansion:

a=\sum_{n\geq 0}V^{n}M_{F_{n}}+\sum_{n<0}M_{F_{n}}(V^{*})^{-n}

where
F_{n}\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
.

In what follows, we rephrase the definition of
I_{s}^{\infty}
in terms of a family of norms and endow
I_{s}^{\infty}
with a locally convex topology. Given
l=s^{m}l^{\prime}
as above, recall the following diagonal operator in
H=\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
:

\mathbb{P}E_{s^{m}l^{\prime}}=mE_{s^{m}l^{\prime}},\ \ \ \mathbb{P}E_{0}=0.

Given an
a\in HS(s)
, define the following commutator operator (a derivation)
\delta_{\mathbb{P}}
on
HS(s)
by:

\delta_{\mathbb{P}}(a)=[\mathbb{P},a]\,.

Clearly this operator is only densely defined on
HS(s)
.

For non-negative integer
N
, define
\|\cdot\|_{N}:B(H)\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
by
\|a\|_{N}=\|a(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}\|
where
\|\cdot\|
is the norm on
B(H)
. As
\|\cdot\|
is a norm on
B(H)
, so too is
\|\cdot\|_{N}
. Note that
\|\cdot\|_{0}=\|\cdot\|
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
a
and
b\in B(H)
. The following hold:

(1)

\|a\|_{N}
is increasing in
N
.

(2)

\|ab\|_{N}\leq\|a\|\|b\|_{N}\leq\|a\|_{N}\|b\|_{N}
.

(3)

\|a^{*}\|_{N}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{N}\binom{N}{j}\|\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}^{j}(a)\|_{N}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{D}\subseteq H
be a dense subspace in the Hilbert space
H
given by

\mathscr{D}=\left\{x\in H:x=\sum x_{m,l}E_{s^{m}l}\textrm{ such that }m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},l\in{\mathbb{Z}},s\nmid l,\{x_{m,l}\}\textrm{ is }\mathrm{\ RD}\right\}\,.

Note that
\mathscr{D}
is in the domain of
(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}
for every
N
. Moreover

\|a\|_{N}=\|a(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}\|=\underset{x\in\mathscr{D},\|x\|\leq 1}{\textrm{sup }}\|a(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}x\|\,.

Since
{\mathbb{P}}
is a positive operator, notice that
\|(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}x\|\geq\|x\|
. Therefore it follows that we have
\|(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N+1}x\|\geq\|(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}x\|
. By continuity of the norm and density of
\mathscr{D}
, we have
\|a\|_{N+1}\geq\|a\|_{N}
.

Item 2 follows directly from the submultiplicativity of
\|\cdot\|
and Item 1. The last Item follows from an induction argument similar to Proposition 2.2 in
[
17
]
.
∎

We define the (possibly infinite valued)
M,N
-norm on
B(H)
by

\|a\|_{M,N}=\sum_{j=0}^{M}\binom{M}{j}\|\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}^{j}(a)\|_{N}\,.

It is clear from the definition that those expressions satisfy the norm properties for each
M,N\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
. We can immediately see that
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{0,N}=\left\|\cdot\right\|_{N}
.

Our previous papers
[
16
,
17
]
discussed similarly defined norms. Our next result describes properties of these norms, analogous to those demonstrated in those previous papers. While the proofs are almost identical to the proofs of those other papers, we comment on some of the details below.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
a
and
b
be in
B(H)
. The following hold:

(1)

a\in I_{s}^{\infty}
if and only if
\|a\|_{M,N}<\infty
for all nonnegative integers
M
and
N
.

(2)

\|a\|_{M+1,N}=\|a\|_{M,N}+\|\delta_{\mathbb{P}}(a)\|_{M,N}
.

(3)

\|a\|_{M,N}\leq\|a\|_{M,N+1}
.

(4)

\|ab\|_{M,N}\leq\|a\|_{M,0}\|b\|_{M,N}\leq\|a\|_{M,N}\|b\|_{M,N}
.

(5)

\|\delta_{\mathbb{P}}(a)\|_{M,N}\leq\|a\|_{M+1,N}
.

(6)

\|a^{*}\|_{M,N}\leq\|a\|_{M+N,N}
.

(7)

I_{s}^{\infty}
is a complete topological vector space.

Proof.

Suppose
a\in I_{s}^{\infty}
. Then
a
has a norm convergent series expansion. We write
a
as

a=\sum_{n\geq 0}V^{n}M_{F_{n}}+\sum_{n<0}M_{F_{n}}(V^{*})^{-n}

where
F_{n}\in C_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
. Applying
\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}
to
V
and
M_{F}
for some
F\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
, and noting that
M_{F}
is a diagonal operator, we have

\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(V)=V,\quad\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(V^{*})=-V^{*},\quad\text{and}\quad\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(M_{F})=0\,.

It follows that

\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}^{j}(a)=\sum_{n\geq 0}n^{j}V^{n}M_{F_{n}}+\sum_{n<0}n^{j}M_{F_{n}}(V^{*})^{-n}\,.

(3.1)

Notice on a basis element we have that

M_{F_{n}}(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}E_{s^{m}j}=F_{n}(m,j)(1+m)^{N}E_{s^{m}j}\,.

Since
F_{n}(m,j)
is RD in both
m
and
n
and bounded in
j
, it follows that
\|a\|_{M,N}<\infty
.

Now assume
\left\|a\right\|_{M,N}<\infty
. We want to prove that
\{\sup_{x}|F_{n}(m,x)|\}_{m,n}
is RD in
m
and
n
. If
n\geq 0
, from the expectation formula, we have

M_{F_{n}}=E((V^{*})^{n}a)\,.

(3.2)

It follows from the definition of the expectation that

E(a(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N})=E(a)(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}.

Consequently,

M_{F_{n}}(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}=E((V^{*})^{n}a(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}),

and so, using
\|E(a)\|\leq\|a\|
, we have

\|M_{F_{n}}(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}\|\leq\|V^{*}\|^{n}\left\|a\right\|_{N}=\left\|a\right\|_{N}<\infty\,.

Since
M_{F_{n}}(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}
is a diagonal operator we can compute its norm and, using the above inequality, we get

\sup_{m}\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times}}(1+m)^{N}|F_{n}(m,x)|=\sup_{m,l}(1+m)^{N}|F_{n}(m,l)|=\|M_{F_{n}}(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}\|<\infty\,.

A similar argument works for
n<0
. Hence, we have the rapid decay in
m
.

To get the rapid decay in both
m
and
n
, first consider
n\geq 0
. It now follows from the expectation formula (
3.2
) and equation (
3.1
) that

\sup_{n\geq 0}\|n^{j}M_{F_{n}}(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}\|=\sup_{n\geq 0}\sup_{m}\sup_{x}n^{j}(1+m)^{N}|F_{n}(m,x)|<\infty

for all
j,N
. There is a similar argument for
n<0
. Therefore,
a\in I_{s}^{\infty}
.

Items (2)-(6) follow from either an induction argument, Proposition
3.1
or a combination of them. The last item follows from standard completeness arguments.
∎

Recall from Proposition 2.1 in
[
17
]
, that the space of smooth compact operators
\mathcal{K}^{\infty}
can be described as

\mathcal{K}^{\infty}=\left\{\sum_{n\geq 0}V^{n}x_{n}+\sum_{n<0}x_{n}(V^{*})^{-n}:x_{n}\in c_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}),\{x_{n}(m)\}_{m,n}\text{ is RD}\right\}\,.

Notice that it is nuclear Fréchet space. Thus we have the following isomorphism of algebras:

I_{s}^{\infty}\cong C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})\otimes\mathcal{K}^{\infty}.

3.2.
Holomorphic Stability of
I_{s}^{\infty}

Next we turn to stability properties of the smooth subalgebra
I_{s}^{\infty}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

If
c\in I_{s}^{\infty}
and
I+c
is invertible in
HS(s)
, then

(I+c)^{-1}-I=-(I+c)^{-1}c\in I_{s}^{\infty}\,.

Proof.

Since
I+c
is invertible in
HS(s)
we obtain

(I+c)^{-1}-I=-(I+c)^{-1}c.

Next, consider the following calculation:

\displaystyle\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}((I+c)^{-1}c)

\displaystyle=\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}((I+c)^{-1})c+(I+c)^{-1}\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(c)

\displaystyle=-(I+c)^{-1}\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(c)(I+c)^{-1}c+(I+c)^{-1}\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(c)

Notice that both terms in that equation are of the form
ab
where
a
is a bounded operator and
b
is in
I_{s}^{\infty}
. In fact, by induction we have for any
j

\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}^{j}((I+c)^{-1}c)=\sum_{i}a_{i}b_{i},\text{ a finite sum,}

where
a_{i}
are bounded operators and
b_{i}\in I_{s}^{\infty}
.
Therefore, from Proposition
3.1
, part
(2)
, we have that for every
N

\|\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}^{j}((I+c)^{-1}c)\|_{0,N}<\infty

and so for every
M
and
N
we get

\|(I+c)^{-1}-I\|_{M,N}<\infty

as desired.
∎

Theorem 3.4
.

I_{s}^{\infty}
is closed under the holomorphic functional calculus: for any
a\in I_{s}^{\infty}
and
f
holomorphic on an open set containing the spectrum of
a
with
f(0)=0
, we have
f(a)\in I_{s}^{\infty}
.

Proof.

Let
a\in I_{s}^{\infty}
,
f
a holomorphic function on open set that contains the spectrum of
c
. If
\gamma
is a curve around the spectrum of
a
contained in the domain of
f
, define
f(a)
via the Cauchy Integral Formula:

f(a)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}f(z)(z-a)^{-1}\,dz\,.

Completeness of
I_{s}^{\infty}
and the Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem yield convergence of the integral;
f(0)=0
and Proposition
3.3
implies that
f(a)
is in
I_{s}^{\infty}
, see
[
3
]
for a more general discussion.
∎

3.3.
Smooth Stability of
I_{s}^{\infty}
.

We now consider stability of
I_{s}^{\infty}
under smooth functional calculus of self-adjoint elements. We again are following
[
17
]
.

The next proposition will require an additional derivation, related to
\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}
. Define the continuous derivation
\partial_{j}
on
I_{s}^{\infty}
by

\partial_{j}(a)=[(I+{\mathbb{P}})^{j},a].

(3.3)

It is clear that
\partial_{1}=\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}
. A straightforward induction argument establishes the following relations between
\partial_{j}
and powers of
\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}
:

\partial_{j}(a)=\sum_{k=1}^{j}\binom{j}{k}\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}^{k}(a)(I+{\mathbb{P}})^{j-k}

(3.4)

and

\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}^{j}(a)=\sum_{k=1}^{j}(-1)^{j-k}\binom{j}{k}\partial_{k}(a)(I+{\mathbb{P}})^{j-k}

(3.5)

using the convention that
(I+{\mathbb{P}})^{0}=I
. These formulas lead to the following observation.

Proposition 3.5
.

The norms
\|a\|_{M,N}
,
M,N=0,1,2\ldots
are equivalent to the seminorms
\|\partial_{j}(a)\|_{0,N}
,
j,N=0,1,2,\ldots
.

Proof.

This follows directly from the definition of the
\|\cdot\|_{M,N}
norm and equations (
3.4
) and (
3.5
).
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

If
a\in I_{s}^{\infty}
then
e^{ia}-I\in I_{s}^{\infty}
and we have the following estimates:

\|e^{ia}-I\|_{0,N}\leq\|a\|_{0,N}.

and for
j\geq 1
,

\|\partial_{j}(e^{ia}-I)\|_{0,N}\leq\|\partial_{j}(a)\|_{0,N}+\|\partial_{j}(a)\|\|a\|_{0,N}\,.

Proof.

First we estimate the
0,N
-norm of
e^{ia}-I
. To do this we write:

e^{ia}-I=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\left(e^{ita}\right)\,dt=i\int_{0}^{1}e^{ita}a\,dt\,.

It follows from Proposition
3.1
that we have:

\|e^{ia}-I\|_{0,N}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|e^{ita}\|\|a\|_{0,N}\,dt=\|a\|_{0,N}\,.

(3.6)

For
j\geq 1
we write:

\displaystyle\partial_{j}(e^{ia})

\displaystyle=(I+{\mathbb{P}})^{j}e^{ia}-e^{ia}(I+{\mathbb{P}})^{j}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\left(e^{i(1-t)a}(I+{\mathbb{P}})^{j}e^{ita}\right)\,dt=

(3.7)

\displaystyle=i\int_{0}^{1}e^{i(1-t)a}\partial_{j}(a)e^{ita}\,dt=i\int_{0}^{1}e^{i(1-t)a}\partial_{j}(a)\,dt+i\int_{0}^{1}e^{i(1-t)a}\partial_{j}(a)(e^{ita}-I)\,dt\,.

Consequently, we estimate, using equation (
3.6
):

\displaystyle\|\partial_{j}(e^{ia}-I)\|_{0,N}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|e^{i(1-t)a}\partial_{j}(a)\|_{0,N}\,dt+\int_{0}^{1}\|e^{i(1-t)a}\partial_{j}(a)(e^{ita}-I)\|_{0,N}\,dt\leq

\displaystyle\leq\|\partial_{j}(a)\|_{0,N}+\|\partial_{j}(a)\|\|a\|_{0,N}\,,

by Proposition
3.1
.

∎

Theorem 3.7
.

I_{s}^{\infty}
is closed under the smooth functional calculus of self-adjoint elements: for any self-adjoint
c\in I_{s}^{\infty}
and smooth
f
on a neighborhood of the spectrum of
c
with
f(0)=0
, we have
f(c)\in I_{s}^{\infty}
.

Proof.

The proof below is inspired by ideas in
[
2
]
. Pick a number
L
bigger than
2\|c\|
and extend
f
to the whole of
{\mathbb{R}}
in such a way that it is not only still smooth, but also it is
L
-periodic:

f(\theta+L)=f(\theta)

for every
\theta
. Then
f(\theta)
admits a Fourier series representation:

f(\theta)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}f_{n}e^{2\pi in\theta/L}

with rapid decay coefficients
\{f_{n}\}
. Taking into account the assumption
f(0)=0
, we have:

f(c)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}f_{n}\left(e^{2\pi inc/L}-I\right).

Using the previous proposition we see that the seminorms

\|\partial_{j}(e^{2\pi inc}-I)\|_{0,N}

(3.8)

grow at most quadratically in
n
, so that
\|\partial_{j}(f(c))\|_{M,N}<\infty
, by RD of
\{f_{n}\}
, implying that
f(c)
is in
I_{s}^{\infty}
.
∎

3.4.
Smooth Hensel-Steinitz Algebra

For
\phi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
, a Toeplitz operator,
T(\phi)
, acting on
H
is given by

T(\phi)=\sum_{m\geq 0}\phi_{m}V^{m}+\sum_{m<0}\phi_{m}(V^{*})^{-m}

where
\{\phi_{m}\}
are the
m^{\textrm{th}}
Fourier coefficients of
\phi
. Since
\phi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
, it follows that the sequence
\{\phi_{m}\}
is RD and the above series is norm convergent.

We define the smooth Hensel-Steinitz algebra,
HS^{\infty}(s)
by:

HS^{\infty}(s)=\{a\in HS(s):a=T(\phi)+c\text{ with }\phi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}),c\in I_{s}^{\infty}\}\,.

We use the following function norms on
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
:

\left\|\phi\right\|_{C^{k}}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\left\|\left(\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{d}{d\theta}\right)^{j}\phi\right\|_{\infty}

It is straightforward to verify that they are submultiplicative and that

\left\|\phi\right\|_{C^{k+1}}=\left\|\phi\right\|_{C^{k}}+\left\|\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{d}{d\theta}\phi\right\|_{C^{k}}.

(3.9)

The basic properties of
HS^{\infty}(s)
are established in the following statement.

Proposition 3.8
.

If
\phi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
and
c\in I_{s}^{\infty}
, then both
T(\phi)c
and
cT(\phi)
are in
I_{s}^{\infty}
. If
\phi
and
\psi
are in
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
, then

T(\phi)T(\psi)-T(\phi\psi)\in I_{s}^{\infty}\,.

Consequently,
HS^{\infty}(s)
is a dense
*
-subalgebra of
HS(s)
with
a^{*}=T({\overline{f}})+c^{*}
for a given
a\in HS^{\infty}(s)
. Moreover,
I_{s}^{\infty}
is a two-sided ideal of
HS^{\infty}(s)
with

HS^{\infty}(s)/I_{s}^{\infty}\cong C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})\,,

and the quotient map
q:HS^{\infty}(s)\to C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
acts as

{q}(T(\phi)+c)=\phi

with
\phi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
.

Proof.

From Proposition
3.1
we have that
\left\|T(\phi)c\right\|_{N}\leq\|T(\phi)\|\left\|c\right\|_{N}
. In a similar fashion to equation (3.5) in
[
16
]
we have that

\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(T(\phi)c)=T\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{d}{d\theta}\phi\right)c+T(\phi)\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(c).

Using this, it follows that

\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}^{j}(T(\phi)c)=\sum_{k}a_{k}b_{k}\quad(\textrm{finite sums})

with
a_{k}
bounded and
b_{k}\in I_{s}^{\infty}
. So
\left\|T(\phi)c\right\|_{M,N}<\infty
and hence
T(\phi)c\in I_{s}^{\infty}
. A similar argument shows
cT(\phi)\in I_{s}^{\infty}
.

We further get estimates on
\left\|T(\phi)c\right\|_{M,N}
and
\left\|cT(\phi)\right\|_{M,N}
, namely:

\left\|T(\phi)c\right\|_{M,N}\leq\left\|\phi\right\|_{C^{M}}\left\|c\right\|_{M,N}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\left\|cT(\phi)\right\|_{M,N}\leq\left\|\phi\right\|_{C^{M+N}}\left\|c\right\|_{M,N}\,.

(3.10)

These are simple for
M=0
. Inductively, we have

\displaystyle\left\|T(\phi)c\right\|_{M+1,N}

\displaystyle=\left\|T(\phi)c\right\|_{M,N}+\left\|\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(T(\phi)c)\right\|_{M,N}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\phi\right\|_{C^{M}}\left\|c\right\|_{M,N}+\left\|\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(T(\phi))c\right\|_{M,N}+\left\|T(\phi)\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(c)\right\|_{M,N}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\left\|\phi\right\|_{C^{M}}+\left\|\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{d}{d\theta}\phi\right\|_{C^{M}}\right)(\left\|c\right\|_{M,N}+\left\|\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(c)\right\|_{M,N})=\left\|\phi\right\|_{C^{M+1}}\left\|c\right\|_{M+1,N}.

For the second estimate, we use the derivation from equation (
3.3
):

\partial_{N}(a)=[(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N},a]=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\binom{N}{j}\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}^{j}(a)(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N-j}.

For the
M=0
case of our estimate, we get

\displaystyle\left\|cT(\phi)\right\|_{0,N}

\displaystyle=\|cT(\phi)(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}\|=\|(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}T(\overline{\phi})c^{*}\|=\left\|\partial_{N}(T(\overline{\phi})c^{*})+T(\overline{\phi})c^{*}(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\partial_{N}(T(\overline{\phi})c^{*})\|+\|T(\overline{\phi})c^{*}(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}\|=\|\partial_{N}(T(\overline{\phi})c^{*})\|+\|T(\overline{\phi})(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}c^{*}\|

Using the formula for the commutator
\partial_{N}(a)
we get:

\displaystyle\left\|cT(\phi)\right\|_{0,N}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\binom{N}{j}\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}^{j}(T(\overline{\phi})c^{*})(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N-j}\right\|+\|T(\overline{\phi})(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}c^{*}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{N}\binom{N}{j}\|\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}^{j}(T(\overline{\phi}))(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N-j}c^{*}\|=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\binom{N}{j}\left\|T\left(\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{d}{d\theta}\right)^{j}\phi\right)\right\|\|c\|_{N-j}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\phi\right\|_{C^{N}}\left\|c\right\|_{N}=\left\|\phi\right\|_{C^{N}}\left\|c\right\|_{0,N}

Then for the inductive step, we see that

\displaystyle\left\|cT(\phi)\right\|_{M+1,N}

\displaystyle=\left\|cT(\phi)\right\|_{M,N}+\left\|\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(cT(\phi))\right\|_{M,N}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|c\right\|_{M,N}\left\|\phi\right\|_{C^{M+N}}+\left\|\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(c)\right\|_{M,N}\left\|\phi\right\|_{C^{M+N}}+\left\|c\right\|_{M,N}\left\|\left(\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{d}{d\theta}\phi\right)\right\|_{C^{M+N}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\phi\right\|_{C^{M+1+N}}\left\|c\right\|_{M+1,N}

Since all norms
\left\|cT(\phi)\right\|_{M,N}
and
\left\|T(\phi)c\right\|_{M,N}
are finite, the corresponding products are in
I_{s}^{\infty}
.

Let
\phi,\psi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
with Fourier series

\phi(\theta)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\phi_{n}e^{2\pi in\theta}\mbox{\ and\ }\psi(\theta)=\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\psi_{m}e^{2\pi im\theta}

with
\{\phi_{n}\},\{\psi_{m}\}
RD. Separate these into positive and negative frequency components:

\phi_{+}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\phi_{n}e^{2\pi in\theta}\mbox{\ and\ }\phi_{-}=\sum_{n<0}\phi_{n}e^{2\pi in\theta}

(3.11)

and similarly for
\psi
. Clearly
\phi=\phi_{+}+\phi_{-}
and so
T(\phi)=T(\phi_{+})+T(\phi_{-})
; likewise for
\psi
. A straightforward calculation shows that
T(\phi_{+})T(\psi_{+})=T(\phi_{+}\psi_{+})
,
T(\phi_{-})T(\psi_{-})=T(\phi_{-}\psi_{-})
, and
T(\psi_{-})T(\phi_{+})=T(\psi_{-}\phi_{+})
. It follows that

T(\phi)T(\psi)-T(\phi\psi)=T(\phi_{+})T(\psi_{-})-T(\phi_{+}\psi_{-})=T(\phi_{+})T(\psi_{-})-T(\psi_{-})T(\phi_{+})\,.

For
n<0
, let
P_{<-n}
be the orthogonal projection onto the subspace
\{E_{s^{m}l^{\prime}}\}_{m<-n}
. For
n<0
and expanding
T(\psi_{-})
as

T(\psi_{-})=\sum_{n<0}\psi_{n}(V^{*})^{-n},

it follows that

T(\phi)T(\psi)-T(\phi\psi)=-\sum_{n<0}\psi_{n}(V^{*})^{-n}T(\phi_{+})P_{<-n}\,.

It is straightforward to see that
\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}((V^{*})^{-n})=n(V^{*})^{-n}
and that
\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(P_{<-n})=0
. Thus, applying
\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}
to
T(\phi)T(\psi)-T(\phi\psi)
we obtain that:

\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}\left(T(\phi)T(\psi)-T(\phi\psi)\right)=\sum_{n<0}\psi_{n}(V^{*})^{-n}\left(nT(\phi_{+})+T\left(\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{d}{d\theta}\right)\phi_{+}\right)\right)P_{<-n}\,.

Repeating this process
l
-times we get

\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}^{l}(T(\phi)T(\psi)-T(\phi\psi))=-\sum_{n<0}\sum_{j=0}^{l}\binom{l}{j}\psi_{n}n^{l-j}(V^{*})^{-n}T\left(\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{d}{d\theta}\right)^{j}\phi_{+}\right)P_{<-n}\,.

(3.12)

To estimate
M,N
-norm of the above, we first estimate the
N
-norm. Using the triangle inequality, Proposition
3.1
, and the fact that
\left\|P_{<-n}\right\|_{N}=|n|^{N}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\left\|\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}^{l}(T(\phi)T(\psi)-T(\phi\psi))\right\|_{N}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n<0}\sum_{j=0}^{l}\binom{l}{j}|\psi_{n}||n|^{l-j+N}\left\|T\left(\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{d}{d\theta}\right)^{j}\phi_{+}\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n<0}|\psi_{n}|(1+|n|)^{l+N}\|\phi\|_{C^{l}}\,.

Using equation (3.7) in Section 3.3 from
[
17
]
, we get

\left\|\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}^{l}(T(\phi)T(\psi)-T(\phi\psi))\right\|_{N}\leq\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}-1\right)\|\psi\|_{C^{l+N+2}}\|\phi\|_{C^{l}}\,.

Consequently, by the definition of the
M,N
-norm along with the following combinatorial estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{M}\sum_{j=0}^{l}\binom{M}{l}\binom{l}{j}|n|^{l-j}\left\|\left(\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\right)^{j}\phi\right\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{l=0}^{M}\sum_{j=0}^{l}\binom{M}{l-j}\binom{M}{j}|n|^{l-j}\left\|\left(\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\right)^{j}\phi\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{M}\sum_{k=0}^{M}|n|^{k}\binom{M}{k}\binom{M}{j}\left\|\left(\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\right)^{j}\phi\right\|_{\infty}=(1+|n|)^{M}\|\phi\|_{C^{M}}

we get

\|T(\phi)T(\psi)-T(\phi\psi))\|_{M,N}\leq\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}-1\right)\|\psi\|_{C^{M+N+2}}\|\phi\|_{C^{M}}<\infty

for all
M,N
.
∎

Following Proposition 3.2 from
[
17
]
, we can define a countable family of submultiplicative norms defining the topology on
HS^{\infty}(s)
as follows:

\|T(\phi)+c\|_{M,N}:=S\|\phi\|_{C^{M+N+2}}+\|c\|_{M,N}\,,

where
S
is a sufficiently large constant.

3.5.
Generalized Toeplitz Operators

Recall that for
\lambda\in C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
, we defined a multiplication operator
M_{\lambda}:H\to H
in equation (
2.6
). The key feature of those operators is that, while not in
HS(s)
, they commute with all elements of
HS(s)
and if
c\in I_{s}
then
M_{\lambda}c
and
cM_{\lambda}
are also in
I_{s}
.
This lets us introduce the concept of a “generalized” Toeplitz operator. Consider the space

C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})=\left\{\Lambda(\theta,x)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{2\pi in\theta}\lambda_{n}(x):\lambda_{n}\in C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})\text{ and }\{\underset{x}{\textrm{sup }}|\lambda_{n}(x)|\}_{n}\textrm{ is RD}\right\}\,.

There are natural norms on
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
given by:

\left\|\Lambda\right\|_{C^{k}}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\left\|\left(\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{d}{d\theta}\right)^{j}\Lambda\right\|_{\infty},

where, for simplicity, we abuse the regular
C^{k}
norm notation. These norms are submultiplicative and satisfy recurrence:

\left\|\Lambda\right\|_{C^{k+1}}=\left\|\Lambda\right\|_{C^{k}}+\left\|\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{d}{d\theta}\Lambda\right\|_{C^{k}}

Given
\Lambda\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
we can define a “generalized” Toeplitz operator by

\mathcal{T}(\Lambda)=\sum_{n\geq 0}V^{n}M_{\lambda_{n}}+\sum_{n<0}M_{\lambda_{n}}(V^{*})^{-n}\,.

If
\Lambda
does not depend on
x
then
\mathcal{T}(\Lambda)
is an actual Toeplitz operator, that is
\mathcal{T}(\Lambda)=T(\Lambda)
. Conversely, if
\mathcal{T}(\Lambda)
is an actual Toeplitz operator then
\Lambda
does not depend on
x
. Because of the properties of
M_{\lambda}
for
\lambda\in C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
mentioned above, Proposition
3.8
immediately generalizes to the following result, with the same proof.

Proposition 3.9
.

If
\Lambda\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
and
c\in I_{s}^{\infty}
, then

\left\|\mathcal{T}(\Lambda)c\right\|_{M,N}\leq\left\|\Lambda\right\|_{C^{M}}\left\|c\right\|_{M,N}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\left\|c\mathcal{T}(\Lambda)\right\|_{M,N}\leq\left\|\Lambda\right\|_{C^{M+N}}\left\|c\right\|_{M,N}\,.

If
\Lambda
and
\Omega
are in
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
, then

\|\mathcal{T}(\Lambda)\mathcal{T}(\Omega)-\mathcal{T}(\Lambda\Omega))\|_{M,N}\leq\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}-1\right)\|\Omega\|_{C^{M+N+2}}\|\Lambda\|_{C^{M}}.

3.6.
Holomorphic Stability of
HS^{\infty}(s)

With extra manipulations of Toeplitz operators, the stability of
HS^{\infty}(s)
under holomorphic functional calculus follows from the corresponding stability of
I_{s}^{\infty}
.

Proposition 3.10
.

If
a\in HS^{\infty}(s)
and
a
is invertible in
HS(s)
, then
a^{-1}\in HS^{\infty}(s)
.

Proof.

Since
a\in HS^{\infty}(s)
,
a=T(\phi)+b
for some
\phi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
and
b\in I_{s}^{\infty}
. Since the quotient map
q(a)=\phi
is a homomorphism,
q(a^{-1})=1/\phi
. Since
\phi\neq 0
,
1/\phi
is in
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
. Write
a^{-1}=T(1/\phi)+\tilde{b}
for some
\tilde{b}
. We will show that
\tilde{b}\in I_{s}^{\infty}
.

Rearranging terms we get

\displaystyle\tilde{b}

\displaystyle=a^{-1}-T(1/\phi)=a^{-1}(I-aT(1/\phi))

\displaystyle=a^{-1}(I-(T(\phi)+b)T(1/\phi))

\displaystyle=a^{-1}(I-T(\phi)T(1/\phi)+bT(1/\phi))

By Proposition
3.8
,
I-T(\phi)T(1/\phi)\in I_{s}^{\infty}
and
bT(1/\phi)\in I_{s}^{\infty}
, so consequently, there is a
\tilde{c}\in I_{s}^{\infty}
such that
\tilde{b}=a^{-1}\tilde{c}
. It follows from Proposition
3.1
that

\|\tilde{b}\|_{0,N}\leq\|a^{-1}\|\|\tilde{c}\|_{0,N}<\infty\,.

(3.13)

Computing
\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}
on
\tilde{b}
we have

\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(\tilde{b})=\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(a^{-1})\tilde{c}+a^{-1}\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(\tilde{c})=-a^{-1}\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(a)a^{-1}\tilde{c}+a^{-1}\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(\tilde{c})\,.

So, as in the proofs of Proposition
3.3
or Proposition
3.8
, we have, inductively, for any
j
that

\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}^{j}(\tilde{b})=\sum_{i}a_{i}b_{i}\quad\textrm{finite sum,}

with
a_{i}
bounded and
b_{i}
are in
I_{s}^{\infty}
. Using this and the estimate in equation (
3.13
), we see that
\|\tilde{b}\|_{M,N}
is finite for all
M
and
N
. Thus
\tilde{b}\in I_{s}^{\infty}
, completing the proof.

∎

Theorem 3.11
.

The smooth Hensel-Steinitz algebra
HS^{\infty}(s)
is closed under the holomorphic functional calculus. In other words, for any
a\in HS^{\infty}(s)
and a function
g
that is holomorphic on an open neighborhood containing the spectrum of
a
we have
g(a)\in HS^{\infty}(s)
.

Proof.

If
C
is a contour around an open set containing the spectrum of
a
then the Cauchy integral formula gives us

g(z)=\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C}\dfrac{g(\zeta)}{\zeta-z}\,d\zeta

Then we define
g(a)
by

g(a)=\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C}g(\zeta)(\zeta-a)^{-1}\,d\zeta

It is thus enough to show that if
a
is invertible in
HS(s)
and
a\in HS^{\infty}(s)
then
a^{-1}\in HS^{\infty}(s)
. However, this was established in the previous proposition.
∎

3.7.
Smooth Stability of
HS^{\infty}(s)

To establish stability of
HS^{\infty}(s)
under smooth calculus of self-adjoint elements we repeat similar ideas from the study of stability of
I_{s}^{\infty}
and study exponentials
e^{ia}
of self-adjoint elements
a\in HS^{\infty}(s)
. We first identify the Toeplitz operator part of the exponentials and then estimate the difference using essentially Duhamel’s principle.

The key inequality to estimate the
M,N
-norms of products, Proposition
3.2
part
(4)
, requires a better understanding of the
M,0
-norms of exponentials, which is done in the next statement. We follow reference
[
17
]
, Proposition 3.5.

Proposition 3.12
.

Suppose
c\in I_{s}^{\infty}
is a self-adjoint element. Then we have the following inequality:

\left\|e^{ic}\right\|_{M,0}\leq\prod_{j=1}^{M}(1+\left\|c\right\|_{j,0})^{2^{M-j}}\,.

Proof.

We prove this via induction. For
M=0
it is clear that
\left\|e^{ic}\right\|_{0,0}=1
. From Proposition
3.2
part (2) we have that

\left\|e^{ic}\right\|_{M+1,0}=\left\|e^{ic}\right\|_{M,0}+\left\|\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(e^{ic})\right\|_{M,0}

Notice that we have

\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(e^{ic})=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\left(e^{i(1-t)c}(1+{\mathbb{P}})e^{itc}\right)\,dt=i\int_{0}^{1}e^{i(1-t)c}\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(c)e^{itc}\,dt\,.

Assume the inequality is true for
M
and consider the following:

\displaystyle\left\|e^{ic}\right\|_{M+1,0}

\displaystyle=\left\|e^{ic}\right\|_{M,0}+\left\|\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(e^{ic})\right\|_{M,0}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{j=1}^{M}(1+\left\|c\right\|_{j,0})^{2^{M-j}}+\left\|\int_{0}^{1}e^{i(1-t)c}\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(c)e^{itc}\,dt\right\|_{M,0}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{j=1}^{M}(1+\left\|c\right\|_{j,0})^{2^{M-j}}+\int_{0}^{1}\|e^{i(1-t)c}\|_{M,0}\|\delta_{\mathbb{P}}(c)\|_{M,0}\|e^{itc}\|_{M,0}\,dt.

Using the inductive assumption again we get

\displaystyle\left\|e^{ic}\right\|_{M+1,0}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{j=1}^{M}(1+\left\|c\right\|_{j,0})^{2^{M-j}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left\|\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(c)\right\|_{M,0}\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{j=1}^{M}(1+(1-t)\left\|c\right\|_{j,0})^{2^{M-j}}\prod_{j=1}^{M}(1+t\left\|c\right\|_{j,0})^{2^{M-j}}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{j=1}^{M}(1+\left\|c\right\|_{j,0})^{2^{M-j}}+\left[\prod_{j=1}^{M}(1+\left\|c\right\|_{j,0})^{2^{M-j}}\right]^{2}\left\|\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(c)\right\|_{M,0}\,.

By Proposition
3.2
, we have the inequality

\left\|\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}(c)\right\|_{M,0}\leq\left\|c\right\|_{M+1,0},

and thus we obtain the desired result:

\displaystyle\left\|e^{ic}\right\|_{M+1,0}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{j=1}^{M}(1+\left\|c\right\|_{j,0})^{2^{M-j}}\left(1+\prod_{j=1}^{M}(1+\left\|c\right\|_{j,0})^{2^{M-j}}\left\|c\right\|_{M+1,0}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{j=1}^{M}(1+\left\|c\right\|_{j,0})^{2^{M-j}}\prod_{j=1}^{M}(1+\left\|c\right\|_{j,0})^{2^{M-j}}\left(1+\left\|c\right\|_{M+1,0}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{M+1}(1+\left\|c\right\|_{j,0})^{2^{M+1-j}}\,.

∎

In order to prove the next theorem we need the following inequality.
If
\phi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
is real-valued, then we have the following estimate

\left\|e^{i\phi}\right\|_{C^{M}}\leq\prod_{j=1}^{M}(1+\|\phi\|_{C^{j}}).

(3.14)

This was proved in Proposition 3.4 in
[
17
]
.

Theorem 3.13
.

The algebra
HS^{\infty}(s)
is closed under the smooth functional calculus for self-adjoint elements. That is, if
a\in HS^{\infty}(s)
is self-adjoint and
g
is smooth on an open neighborhood of the spectrum of
a
then
g(a)\in HS^{\infty}(s)
.

Proof.

Extend
g
to be smooth,
L
-periodic in a neighborhood of the spectrum of
a
; then
g(\theta)=g(\theta+L)
.

Then for
a=T(\phi)+c\in HS^{\infty}(s)
, we have
g(a)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}g_{n}e^{2\pi ina/L}
. Since
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{M,N}
is submultiplicative, we get
e^{2\pi ina/L}\in HS^{\infty}(s)
for each
n
. The sum will be in
HS^{\infty}(s)
if there is a polynomial bound in
n
for
\left\|e^{2\pi ina/L}\right\|_{M,N}
.

Since for the quotient map
q:HS(s)\to C({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
we have
q(a)=\phi
and since
q
is a homomorphism it follows that
q(e^{2\pi ina/L})=e^{2\pi in\phi/L}
. Note that norms of
e^{2\pi in\phi}
grow at most polynomially in
n
from equation (
3.14
). Thus we need to study the
M,N
-norms of the difference
e^{2\pi ina/L}-T(e^{2\pi in\phi/L})\in I_{s}^{\infty}
and show that they grow at most polynomially in
n
. To do this, we again use essentially Duhamel’s principle.

\displaystyle e^{i(T(\phi)+c)}-T(e^{i\phi})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\left(e^{it(T(\phi)+c)}T(e^{i(1-t)\phi})\right)\,dt

\displaystyle=i\int_{0}^{1}e^{it(T(\phi)+c)}cT(e^{i(1-t)\phi})\,dt

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{1}e^{it(T(\phi)+c)}\left(T(\phi)T(e^{i(1-t)\phi})-T(fe^{i(1-t)\phi})\right)\,dt\,.

Applying the
M,N
-norm to this and using Proposition
3.2
yields:

\displaystyle\left\|e^{i(T(\phi)+c)}-T(e^{i\phi})\right\|_{M,N}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left\|e^{it(T(\phi)+c)}\right\|_{M,0}\left\|cT(e^{i(1-t)\phi})\right\|_{M,N}\,dt

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{1}\left\|e^{it(T(\phi)+c)}\right\|_{M,0}\left\|T(\phi)T(e^{i(1-t)\phi})-T(fe^{i(1-t)\phi})\right\|_{M,N}\,dt\,.

Using equation (
3.14
) and Proposition
3.12
we have

\displaystyle\left\|e^{i(T(\phi)+c)}-T(e^{i\phi})\right\|_{M,N}\leq\left(\prod_{j=1}^{M}(1+\left\|\phi\right\|_{C^{j}}+\left\|c\right\|_{j,0})^{2^{M-j}}\right)\left\|c\right\|_{M,N}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{M}(1+\left\|\phi\right\|_{C^{j}})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\prod_{j=1}^{M}(1+\left\|\phi\right\|_{C^{j}}+\left\|c\right\|_{j,0})^{2^{M-j}}\right)\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}-1\right)\left\|\phi\right\|_{C^{M}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{M+N+2}(1+\left\|\phi\right\|_{C^{j}})\right)\,.

This yields the desired growth estimate in
n
.
∎

4.
Derivations

The purpose of this section is to describe continuous derivations on
HS^{\infty}(s)
. The main result, Theorem
4.10
, exhibits a geometric structure of the space of derivations modulo inner derivations. This is achieved through a series of steps. We first observe that continuous derivations preserve the ideal
I_{s}^{\infty}
and thus define derivations on the factor algebra
HS^{\infty}(s)/I_{s}^{\infty}\cong C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
. Conversely, we show that any derivation on
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
can be lifted to a derivation on
HS^{\infty}(s)
. This observation reduces the study of derivations to those with range in
I_{s}^{\infty}
. We analyze continuous derivations
HS^{\infty}(s)\to I_{s}^{\infty}
, the key part of classification of derivations, by using Fourier components with respect to the natural circle action given by
\rho_{\theta}
defined in equation (
2.4
).

4.1.
Invariance of
I_{s}^{\infty}
under Derivations

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\delta:HS^{\infty}(s)\to HS^{\infty}(s)
be a continuous derivation. Then we have the following inclusion:
\delta(I_{s}^{\infty})\subseteq I_{s}^{\infty}
.

Proof.

We first need to show that
\delta(P_{ij}M_{\lambda})\in I_{s}^{\infty}
for
\delta
a continuous derivation, where
P_{ij}
are given by equation (
2.8
), and
M_{\lambda}
is given by equation (
2.6
).

Using Leibniz rule we have that

\delta(P_{ik})=\delta(P_{ij}P_{rk})=P_{ij}\delta(P_{rk})+\delta(P_{ij})P_{rk}\in I_{s}^{\infty}

since
I_{s}^{\infty}
is an ideal.
Next notice that

\delta(P_{ij}M_{\lambda})=\delta(P_{ij})M_{\lambda}+P_{ij}\delta(M_{\lambda})\in I_{s}^{\infty}

because of the above calculation and the fact that
I_{s}^{\infty}
is an ideal.
Moreover, finite linear combinations of
P_{ij}M_{\lambda}
are elements of
I_{s}^{\infty}
and since such combinations are dense in
\mathcal{K}\otimes C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
and
\delta
is continuous, it follows that
\delta(I_{s}^{\infty})\subseteq I_{s}^{\infty}
.
∎

Any continuous derivation
\delta:HS^{\infty}(s)\to HS^{\infty}(s)
defines a derivation
[\delta]
in the factor algebra
[\delta]:C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})\to C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
given by

[\delta](a+I_{s}^{\infty})=\delta(a)+I_{s}^{\infty}\,.

This is well defined, as if
a
and
a^{\prime}
are such that
[a]=[a^{\prime}]
then
a-a^{\prime}\in I_{s}^{\infty}
and Proposition
4.1
implies that
\delta(a-a^{\prime})\in I_{s}^{\infty}
, so
[\delta(a)]=[\delta(a^{\prime})]
. Since
\delta
is a derivation on
HS^{\infty}(s)
, it follows that
[\delta]
is a derivation on
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
. General theory, see
[
24
]
, implies that derivations on smooth functions on manifolds are smooth vector fields, so there is an
\varphi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
such that

[\delta]=\varphi(\theta)\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{d}{d\theta}\,.

(4.1)

Below we show that any derivation on
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
can be lifted to a derivation on
HS^{\infty}(s)
.

For an
\varphi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
, decompose it as
\varphi=\varphi_{+}+\varphi_{-}
, where

\varphi_{+}(\theta)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\varphi_{n}e^{2\pi in\theta}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\varphi_{-}(\theta)=\sum_{n<0}\varphi_{n}e^{2\pi in\theta}\,.

Proposition 4.2
.

The formula

\delta_{\varphi}(a)=[T(\varphi_{+})\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{P}T(\varphi_{-}),a]

defines a continuous derivation on
HS^{\infty}(s)
so that the following hold for the quotient map:

{q}(\delta_{\varphi}(V))=\varphi(\theta)e^{2\pi i\theta}\quad\textrm{and}\quad{q}(\delta_{\varphi}(V^{*}))=-\varphi(\theta)e^{-2\pi i\theta}\,.

Proof.

That
\delta_{\varphi}
is continuous is straightforward: multiplication by a Toeplitz operator
T(\varphi)
or
{\mathbb{P}}
is continuous, as are sums of such.

For a given
\varphi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
and writing
\varphi=\varphi_{+}+\varphi_{-}
, we have the following:

\delta_{\varphi}(V)=T(\varphi_{+}){\mathbb{P}}V-VT(\varphi_{+}){\mathbb{P}}+{\mathbb{P}}T(\varphi_{-})V-V{\mathbb{P}}T(\varphi_{-})\,.

Using the identities
{\mathbb{P}}VV^{*}={\mathbb{P}}
and
[{\mathbb{P}},V]=V
and the fact that
V
commutes with
T(\varphi_{+})
, we have

\delta_{\varphi}(V)=VT(\varphi_{+})+{\mathbb{P}}T(\varphi_{-})V-V{\mathbb{P}}T(\varphi_{-})\,.

Using the Fourier series expansion of
\varphi
, we have the following calculation:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}T(\varphi_{-})V-V{\mathbb{P}}T(\varphi_{-})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n<0}\varphi_{n}{\mathbb{P}}(V^{*})^{-n}V-V{\mathbb{P}}\sum_{n<0}\phi_{n}(V^{*})^{-n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n<0}\varphi_{n}{\mathbb{P}}(V^{*})^{-n-1}-V{\mathbb{P}}\sum_{n<0}\phi_{n}(V^{*})^{-n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n<0}\varphi_{n}{\mathbb{P}}V(V^{*})^{-n}-V{\mathbb{P}}\sum_{n<0}\phi_{n}(V^{*})^{-n}

where we used the fact that
{\mathbb{P}}VV^{*}={\mathbb{P}}
again. It now follows that

{\mathbb{P}}T(\varphi_{-})V-V{\mathbb{P}}T(\varphi_{-})=VT(\varphi_{-})\,.

Combining this with the above calculation we get that

\delta_{\varphi}(V)=VT(\varphi_{+})+VT(\varphi_{-})=VT(\varphi)\,.

Under the mapping
q
we have

q(\delta_{\varphi}(V))=q(VT(\varphi))=e^{2\pi i\theta}\varphi(\theta)=\varphi(\theta)\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\dfrac{d}{d\theta}e^{2\pi i\theta}=[\delta_{\varphi}](q(V)).

∎

It follows by continuity that for the derivation
\delta_{\varphi}
defined in the above proposition, given an
a\in HS^{\infty}(s)
, we have for the following formula for the quotient

{q}(\delta_{\varphi}(a))=\varphi(\theta)\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{d}{d\theta}{q}(a)(\theta)\,.

Hence,
\delta_{\varphi}
is a lift of the derivation

\varphi(\theta)\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{d}{d\theta}

on
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
.

4.2.
Fourier Components of Derivations

The main tool to study derivations in
HS^{\infty}(s)
is a version of Fourier transform. This is because the corresponding Fourier coefficients are derivations with additional covariance properties that makes it possible to fully classify them.

Given
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, a derivation
\delta:HS^{\infty}(s)\to HS^{\infty}(s)
is said to be a
n
-covariant derivation
if the relation

\rho_{\theta}^{-1}\delta\rho_{\theta}(a)=e^{-2\pi in\theta}\delta(a)

holds for all
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}
, where
\rho_{\theta}
was defined in equation (
2.4
). When
n=0
we say that the derivation is invariant. With this definition, we point out that
\delta_{\mathbb{P}}:HS^{\infty}(s)\to HS^{\infty}(s)
is an invariant continuous derivation.

Definition:

If
\delta
is a continuous derivation in
HS^{\infty}(s)
, the
n
-th Fourier component
of
\delta
is defined as:

\delta_{n}(a)=\int_{0}^{1}e^{2\pi in\theta}\rho_{\theta}^{-1}\delta\rho_{\theta}(a)\,d\theta\,.

Below we describe properties of Fourier components of derivations and of
n
-covariant derivations in general.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\delta:HS^{\infty}(s)\to HS^{\infty}(s)
be a continuous derivation. Then
\delta_{n}:HS^{\infty}(s)\to HS^{\infty}(s)
is a continuous
n
-covariant derivation, where
\delta_{n}
are the
n
-th Fourier components of
\delta
. Moreover,
\delta=0
if and only if
\delta_{n}=0
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Proof.

Since
\delta:HS^{\infty}(s)\to HS^{\infty}(s)
,
\rho_{\theta}:HS^{\infty}(s)\to HS^{\infty}(s)
and
HS^{\infty}(s)
is complete, it follows that
\delta_{n}(a)\in HS^{\infty}(s)
for all
a\in HS^{\infty}(s)
. Since
\delta
is a continuous derivation and the automorphism
\rho_{\theta}
is continuous, it follows that
\delta_{n}
is also continuous, see also the proof of the lemma below. It is straightforward to see that
\delta_{n}
is a derivation.

The following computation verifies that
\delta_{n}
is
n
-covariant:

\rho_{\theta}^{-1}\delta_{n}\rho_{\theta}(a)=\int_{0}^{1}e^{2\pi in\varphi}\rho_{\theta}^{-1}\rho_{\varphi}^{-1}\delta\rho_{\varphi}\rho_{\theta}(a)\,d\varphi=\int_{0}^{1}e^{2\pi in\varphi}\rho_{\theta+\varphi}^{-1}\delta\rho_{\theta+\varphi}(a)\,d\varphi\,.

Changing to new variable
\theta+\varphi
, and using the translation invariance of the measure, it now follows that
\rho_{\theta}^{-1}\delta_{n}\rho_{\theta}(a)=e^{-2\pi in\theta}\delta_{n}(a)
.

The usual Cesàro mean convergence result for Fourier components in harmonic analysis
[
15
]
implies that if
\delta
is a continuous derivation on
HS^{\infty}(s)
then

\delta(a)=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{L+1}\sum_{j=0}^{L}\left(\sum_{n=-j}^{j}\delta_{n}(a)\right)\,,

(4.2)

for every
a\in HS^{\infty}(s)
.
In particular,
\delta
is completely determined by its Fourier components
\delta_{n}
.
This shows that
\delta=0
if and only if
\delta_{n}=0
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

∎

We will also need the following growth estimate on Fourier components of derivations.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\delta:HS^{\infty}(s)\to HS^{\infty}(s)
be a continuous derivation. Then, for every
k\geq 0
,
M\geq 0
and
N\geq 0
, there exist
M^{\prime}\geq 0
and
N^{\prime}\geq 0
and a constant
C_{k}=C_{k}(M,N)
such that for every
a\in I_{s}^{\infty}
we have:

n^{k}\|\delta_{n}(a)\|_{M,N}\leq C_{k}\|a\|_{M^{\prime},N^{\prime}}\,,

where
\delta_{n}
are the
n
-th Fourier components of
\delta
.

Proof.

Since
\delta
is a continuous linear map on the Frechét space
HS^{\infty}(s)
for every
M
and
N
there exist constants
C=C(M,N)
,
M^{\prime},N^{\prime}\geq 0
such that for every
a\in I_{s}^{\infty}
,

\|\delta(a)\|_{M,N}\leq C\|a\|_{M^{\prime},N^{\prime}}.

Using this and the fact that
\rho_{\theta}
is continuous, we have

\|\delta_{n}(a)\|_{M,N}=\left\|\int_{0}^{1}e^{2\pi in\theta}\rho_{\theta}^{-1}\delta\rho_{\theta}(a)\,d\theta\right\|_{M,N}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|\rho_{\theta}^{-1}\delta\rho_{\theta}(a)\|_{M,N}\,d\theta\leq C\|a\|_{M^{\prime},N^{\prime}}.

(4.3)

Consider the following calculation, using integration by parts, the continuity of
[\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}},\delta]
, and the continuous differentiability of
\theta\to\rho_{\theta}^{-1}\delta\rho_{\theta}
:

\displaystyle 2\pi in\delta_{n}(a)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}2\pi ine^{2\pi in\theta}\rho_{\theta}^{-1}\delta\rho_{\theta}(a)\,d\theta=\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{d}{d\theta}(e^{2\pi in\theta})\rho_{\theta}^{-1}\delta\rho_{\theta}(a)\,d\theta

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}e^{2\pi in\theta}\dfrac{d}{d\theta}(\rho_{\theta}^{-1}\delta\rho_{\theta}(a))\,d\theta=-2\pi i\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{\theta}^{-1}[\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}},\delta]\rho_{\theta}(a)\,d\theta

\displaystyle=-2\pi i([\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}},\delta])_{n}(a),

where
([\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}},\delta])_{n}
is the
n
-th Fourier component of the derivation
[\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}},\delta]
. Consequently, using estimate (
4.3
) for the commutator
[\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}},\delta]
we obtain

n\|\delta_{n}(a)\|_{M,N}\leq C\|a\|_{M^{\prime},N^{\prime}},

for some
M^{\prime},N^{\prime}
and constant
C
. The result now follows easily by induction on
k
.
∎

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\delta:HS^{\infty}(s)\to HS^{\infty}(s)
be a continuous derivation, then

\delta(a)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\delta_{n}(a)

(4.4)

for every
a\in I_{s}^{\infty}
where
\delta_{n}
are the
n
-th Fourier components of
\delta
. Here the convergence is with respect to the
M,N
-norms. In particular, this implies norm convergence in
HS(s)
.

Proof.

Lemma
4.4
implies that
\{\|\delta_{n}(a)\|_{M,N}\}
is a RD sequence for every
a\in I_{s}^{\infty}
and thus the series given in equation (
4.4
) is norm convergent. Also, the right-hand side of equation (
4.4
) is a continuous derivation on
HS^{\infty}(s)
with the same Fourier components
\delta_{n}
as
\delta
so they must be equal.
∎

4.3.
Derivations with Range in
I_{s}^{\infty}

We now turn to study of derivations with range in
I_{s}^{\infty}
.
Recall that the Fourier coefficients of
a\in I_{s}^{\infty}

are in the space

C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})=\left\{F\in C_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}):\{\underset{x}{\textrm{sup }}|F(m,x)|\}_{m}\textrm{ is RD}\right\}\,.

Using the following identification

\{f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}):f(0)=0\}\cong C_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})

we can extend the endomorphisms
\alpha
and
\beta
to
C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
by

(\beta F)(m,x)=F(m+1,x)\quad\textrm{and}\quad(\alpha F)(m,x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &F(m-1,x)&&\textrm{if }m>0\\
&0&&\textrm{else,}\end{aligned}\right.

(4.5)

where we abused notation for readability purposes.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\delta:HS^{\infty}(s)\to I_{s}^{\infty}
be a continuous
n
-covariant derivation. Then there exists
R\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
and
r\in C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
such that

\delta(a)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &[V^{n}(M_{R}+M_{r}),a]&\text{ if }n\geq 0\\
&[(M_{R}+M_{r})(V^{*})^{-n},a]&\text{ if }n<0&.\end{aligned}\right.

Proof.

We first consider the case when
n=0
or
\delta
is invariant. Since
\delta
is invariant, we must have

\rho_{\theta}(\delta(M_{f}))=\delta(\rho_{\theta}(M_{f}))=\delta(M_{f})

for every
f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
. Thus
\delta(M_{f})
is
\rho_{\theta}
-invariant for every
\theta
.
Note the following observation:

\{a\in HS(s):\forall\theta,\rho_{\theta}(a)=a\}=C^{*}(M_{g}:g\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})).

Consequently, we have

\delta(M_{f})\in C^{*}(M_{g}:g\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})).

Therefore
\delta
defines a derivation on
C^{*}(M_{g}:g\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}))
, however
C^{*}(M_{g}:g\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}))
is abelian and there are no non-trivial derivations on abelian C
∗
-algebras, see
[
27
]
. Therefore
\delta(M_{f})=0
.

Consider the following calculation:

\displaystyle\rho_{\theta}(\delta(V)V^{*})

\displaystyle=\rho_{\theta}(\delta(V))\rho_{\theta}(V^{*})=\delta(e^{2\pi i\theta}V)e^{-2\pi i\theta}V^{*}=\delta(V)V^{*}.

From the above observation,
\delta(V)V^{*}=M_{f}
for some
f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
. It follows from the commutation relation that

\delta(V)=M_{f}V=VM_{\beta f}:=VM_{F}

with
F\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
. Since
V\in HS^{\infty}(s)
we have that
\delta(V)\in I_{s}^{\infty}
and so
F\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times})
.

By a similar argument,
\delta(V^{*})=M_{G}V^{*}
with
G\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times})
. Consider the following calculation:

0=\delta(I)=\delta(V^{*}V)=\delta(V^{*})V+V^{*}\delta(V)=M_{G}V^{*}V+V^{*}VM_{F}.

It follows that
G=-F
. By continuity,
\delta
is determined by
F
.

We are looking for an
R\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times})
so that

\delta(a)=[M_{R},a]\,.

Computing directly we have

VM_{F}=\delta(V)=[M_{R},V]=M_{R}V-VM_{R}=V(M_{\beta R}-M_{R})

where
\beta
is defined in equation (
4.5
). This yields the equation:

F(m,x)=R(m+1,x)-R(m,x)\,.

Solving recursively gets

R(m,x)=-\sum_{j=m+1}^{\infty}F(j,x)\,.

This sum converges since the sequence
\left\{\sup_{x}|F(j,x)|\right\}_{j}
is rapid decay. For any
N
, we have the following estimate

m^{N}|R(m,x)|\leq\sum_{j=m}^{\infty}m^{N}|F(j,x)|\leq\sum_{j=m}^{\infty}j^{N}|F(j,x)|\,.

The right hand side of the above inequality goes to zero as
m\to\infty
since
\{j^{N}|F(j,x)|\}
is still a RD sequence. It follows that
R(m,x)
is RD in
m
and hence
\delta
has the desired form.

Next suppose that
n>0
, and since
\delta
is assumed to be
n
-covariant we have that

\rho_{\theta}(\delta(a))=e^{2\pi in\theta}\delta(\rho_{\theta}(a))

for all
a\in HS^{\infty}(s)
. Argued similarly to the invariant case above we have that

\delta(V)=V^{n+1}M_{F}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\delta(V^{*})=V^{n-1}M_{G}

for some
F,G\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{*})
. Moreover, we have

0=\delta(V^{*}V)=V^{n-1}M_{G}V+V^{*}V^{n+1}M_{F}=V^{n}(M_{\beta G+F})\,.

So we have that
F=-\beta G
.

Given
f\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
, since
M_{f}
is invariant under
\rho_{\theta}
it follows that
\delta(M_{f})=V^{n}M_{\tilde{\delta}(f)}
where
\tilde{\delta}:C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})\to C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
is a linear map. Since
a\in HS^{\infty}(s)
we can write
a=T(\varphi)+c
with
\varphi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
and
c\in I_{s}^{\infty}
, and so if
M_{f}\in HS^{\infty}(s)
, then
M_{f}=f_{0}+M_{F}
with
f_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
F\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
. Since
\delta(f_{0})=0
we only need to study

\delta(M_{F})=V^{n}M_{\tilde{\delta}(F)}

with
\tilde{\delta}:C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})\to C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
.

From the definition of
\tilde{\delta}
and the Leibniz rule we have

V^{n}M_{\tilde{\delta}(F)}M_{G}+M_{F}V^{n}M_{\tilde{\delta}(G)}=\delta(M_{F}M_{G})=\delta(M_{FG})

which yields

\delta(M_{FG})=V^{n}(M_{\tilde{\delta}(F)G+\beta^{n}(F)\tilde{\delta}(G)})\,.

This gives a “twisted” derivation
\tilde{\delta}
with the following product rule:

\tilde{\delta}(FG)=\tilde{\delta}(F)G+\beta^{n}(F)\tilde{\delta}(G)\,.

Since
C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
is a commutative algebra, we get

\tilde{\delta}(F)G+\beta^{n}(F)\tilde{\delta}(G)=\tilde{\delta}(G)F+\beta^{n}(G)\tilde{\delta}(F)

which gives

\tilde{\delta}(F)(G-\beta^{n}(G))=\tilde{\delta}(G)(F-\beta^{n}(F))

for all
F,G\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
. Now pick any
G_{0}
so that
G_{0}(m,x)\neq G_{0}(m+n,x)
for all
m
and all
x
, note that this is always possible. Thus we get

\tilde{\delta}(F)=\frac{\tilde{\delta}(G_{0})}{G_{0}-\beta^{n}(G_{0})}(F-\beta^{n}(F)):=H(F-\beta^{n}(F))

where
H
is some complex-valued function defined on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}
. Applying
\delta
to the relation
VM_{F}V^{*}=M_{\alpha F}
gives

\delta(M_{\alpha F})=\delta(V)M_{F}V^{*}+V\delta(M_{F})V^{*}+VM_{F}\delta(V^{*})\,.

Using this and the definition of
\tilde{\delta}
yields

-V^{n}M_{\alpha\beta G}M_{\alpha F}+V^{n}M_{\alpha H(\alpha F-\alpha\beta^{n}F)}+V^{n}M_{\beta^{n-1}F}M_{G}=V^{n}M_{H(\alpha F-\beta^{n}\alpha F)}\,.

This implies that

(-\alpha\beta(G)+\alpha(H)-H)\alpha(F)+(G+H)\beta^{n-1}(F)-\alpha(H)\alpha\beta^{n}(F)=0\,.

Since
\alpha(H)\alpha\beta^{n}(F)=\alpha(H)\beta^{n-1}(F)
for all
x
, we get

(-G+\alpha(H)-H)\alpha(F)=(\alpha(H)-G-H)\alpha\beta^{n-1}(F)

for every
F
.
Choose
F
such that
\alpha(F)
and
\alpha\beta^{n-1}(F)
are nonzero, then it follows that

G=\alpha(H)-H.

(4.6)

Explicitly, equation (
4.6
) is the following recurrence equation:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &G(k,x)=H(k-1,x)-H(k,x)&&\textrm{ if }k\geq 1\\
&G(0,x)=-H(0,x)&&\textrm{ if }k=0\,.\end{aligned}\right.

This system has a formal solution, namely:

H(k,x)=-\sum_{j=0}^{k}G(j,x)=\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty}G(j,x)-\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}G(j,x):=R+r\,.

Since
\delta(V^{*})\in I_{s}^{\infty}
, we have that
G\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
. Consequently the above sums converge absolutely and are RD. Thus
R\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
and
r\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{*})
.

Thus, so far we have

\delta(V)=-V^{n+1}M_{\beta(G)}\,,\quad\delta(V^{*})=V^{n-1}M_{G}\,,\quad\textrm{and}\quad\delta(M_{F})=V^{n}M_{H(F-\beta^{n}F)}

where
G=\alpha(H)-H
. We wish to show
\delta
has the following form:

\delta(a)=[V^{n}M_{H},a]

for
n>0
. A direct calculation verifies the above formula works for
a=V
,
a=V^{*}
, and
a=M_{F}
.
Thus, by continuity, the formula must be true for all
a\in HS^{\infty}(s)
.
A similar proof works for when
n<0
.
∎

Notice that in the above statement a continuous
n
-covariant derivation
\delta:HS^{\infty}(s)\to I_{s}^{\infty}
is inner if and only if the corresponding
r\in C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
is a constant.

Recall the space

C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})=\left\{\Lambda(\theta,x)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{2\pi in\theta}\lambda_{n}(x):\lambda_{n}\in C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})\text{ and }\{\underset{x}{\textrm{sup }}|\lambda_{n}(x)|\}_{n}\textrm{ is RD}\right\}\,.

Given
\Lambda\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
recall that we can associate a “generalized” Toeplitz operator

\mathcal{T}(\Lambda)=\sum_{n\geq 0}V^{n}M_{\lambda_{n}}+\sum_{n<0}M_{\lambda_{n}}(V^{*})^{-n}\,.

The next proposition classifies all continuous derivations on
HS^{\infty}(s)
whose range is in
I_{s}^{\infty}
. Proposition
3.9
implies that
[\mathcal{T}(\Lambda),a]
is in
I_{s}^{\infty}
for any
a\in HS^{\infty}(s)
.

Proposition 4.7
.

If
\delta:HS^{\infty}(s)\to I_{s}^{\infty}
is a continuous derivation, then there exists an element
\Lambda\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
and an element
b\in I_{s}^{\infty}
such that

\delta(a)=[\mathcal{T}(\Lambda)+b,a]\,.

Moreover,
\delta
is inner if and only if
\Lambda
does not depend on
x
.

Proof.

First write
\delta
as its Fourier decomposition:

\delta(a)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\delta_{n}(a)

By Proposition
4.5
, this sum is convergent with respect to the
M,N
-norms. Further, Proposition
4.3
implies that
\delta_{n}
is
n
-covariant. By Proposition
4.6
,
\delta_{n}
may be represented by a special commutator. We only consider the case
n\geq 0
as the case
n<0
is similar.

For
n\geq 0
, we get

\delta_{n}(a)=[V^{n}(M_{r_{n}}+M_{R_{n}}),a]

where
r_{n}\in C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
and
R_{n}\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
for each
n
.

Recall from the proof of Proposition
4.6
,

\delta_{n}(V^{*})=V^{n-1}M_{G_{n}}

where
G_{n}\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
and has the following relation with
R_{n}
and
r_{n}
:

\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty}G_{n}(j,x)-\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}G_{n}(j,x)=R_{n}(k,x)+r_{n}(x)\,.

The goal is to establish rapid decay properties of
R_{n}
and
r_{n}
.

Since
\delta(V^{*})\in I_{s}^{\infty}
, we have that for all
N
and
j
there exists a constant such that

\|\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}^{j}(\delta(V^{*}))(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}\|\leq const(N,j).

However by the definition of
\delta_{n}
we get

\delta_{n}(V^{*})=\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2\pi in\theta}\rho_{\theta}\delta\rho_{\theta}^{-1}(V^{*})\,d\theta=\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2\pi i(n+1)\theta}\rho_{\theta}\delta(V^{*})\,d\theta.

Note that

\displaystyle\|\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}^{j}(\delta_{n}(V^{*}))(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2\pi i(n+1)\theta}\rho_{\theta}\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}^{j}(\delta(V^{*}))(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}\,d\theta\right\|\leq const(N,j).

On the other hand, computing the norm explicitly in the above inequality we have

\displaystyle\|\delta_{{\mathbb{P}}}^{j}(V^{n-1}M_{G_{n}})(1+{\mathbb{P}})^{N}\|=(n-1)^{j}\sup_{m,x}|G_{n}(m,x)|(1+m)^{N}\leq const(N,j).

So
\left\{\sup_{x}|G_{n}(m,x)|\right\}_{m,n}
is RD. It follows that
\{\sup_{x}|R_{n}(m,x)|\}_{m,n}
is an RD sequence and
\{\sup_{x}|r_{n}(x)|\}_{n}
is an RD sequence. A similar argument shows the same RD results for
n<0
.

Define
\Lambda
by

\Lambda(\theta,x):=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{2\pi in\theta}r_{n}(x).

The above argument shows that
\Lambda\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
. Also define
b
by

b:=\sum_{n\geq 0}V^{n}M_{R_{n}}+\sum_{n<0}M_{R_{n}}(V^{*})^{-n}.

The above argument also shows that
b\in I_{s}^{\infty}
.

We now claim that

\delta(a)=[\mathcal{T}(\Lambda)+b,a]

for all
a\in HS^{\infty}(s)
. Indeed, since
\Lambda\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
and
b\in I_{s}^{\infty}
both sides of the equation are continuous derivations
HS^{\infty}(s)\to I_{s}^{\infty}
with the same Fourier components and thus must be equal.

Notice again that
\mathcal{T}(\Lambda)
is a Toeplitz operator if and only if
\Lambda
does not depend on
x
, thus
\delta
is inner if and only if
\Lambda
does not depend on
x
.

∎

4.4.
Classification of Derivations

For
\Lambda\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
define the following map

a\mapsto\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda}(a):=[\mathcal{T}(\Lambda),a]

(4.7)

and notice by Proposition
3.9
that
\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda}
is a continuous derivation from
HS^{\infty}(s)
to
I_{s}^{\infty}
, appearing in Proposition
4.7
.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
\Lambda\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
and let
\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda}:HS^{\infty}(s)\to I_{s}^{\infty}
be as above. Then we have the following properties.

(1)

\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda}
is inner if and only if
\Lambda
does not depend on
x
.

(2)

\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda}=0
if and only if
\Lambda
does not depend on
\theta
.

Proof.

The first item has already been established, thus we only need to prove the second. Consider
\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda}
acting on
V
:

\displaystyle 0=\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda}(V)

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{n\geq 0}V^{n}M_{\lambda_{n}}+\sum_{n<0}M_{\lambda_{n}}(V^{*})^{-n},V\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{n\geq 0}V^{n}M_{\lambda_{n}},V\right]+\left[\sum_{n<0}M_{\lambda_{n}}(V^{*})^{-n},V\right]

and notice that the first commutator is 0. The second commutator is the sum of projections
P_{<-n}
with coefficients
M_{\lambda_{n}}
. Since the sum must be 0 and the projections
P_{<-n}
are not zero,
M_{\lambda_{n}}
must be
0
for
n<0
.

Similarly, applying
\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda}
to
V^{*}
we have
M_{\lambda_{n}}=0
for
n>0
. So
\Lambda(\theta,x)=\Lambda(0,x)
for all
x
, and thus
\Lambda
does not depend on
\theta
.
∎

To obtain uniqueness in the classification of derivations we need to make further choices of
\Lambda
’s.
One convenient way to do it is to consider the following expectations in
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
:
E_{1}:C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})\to C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
and
E_{2}:C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})\to C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
given by

E_{1}(\Lambda)=\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}}\Lambda(\theta,x)\,dx\text{\quad and\quad}E_{2}(\Lambda)=\int_{0}^{1}\Lambda(\theta,x)\,d\theta.

(4.8)

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
\Lambda\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
. Then we have the following:

(1)

There is a
\Lambda^{\prime}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
so that
E_{2}(\Lambda^{\prime})=0
and
\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda}=\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}
.

(2)

If
E_{2}(\Lambda)=0
then there is a
\Lambda^{\prime}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
so that
E_{1}(\Lambda^{\prime})=E_{2}(\Lambda^{\prime})=0
and
\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda}=\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}+\tilde{\delta}
where
\tilde{\delta}
is an inner derivation on
HS^{\infty}(s)
.

(3)

If
E_{1}(\Lambda)=E_{2}(\Lambda)=0
then
\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda}
is inner if and only if
\Lambda=0
.

Proof.

For the first item, note that
E_{2}(\Lambda)
does not depend on
\theta
. Define
\Lambda^{\prime}=\Lambda-E_{2}(\Lambda)
; then

\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda}=\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda-E_{2}(\Lambda)}.

Moreover, since
E_{2}
is an expectation we have

E_{2}(\Lambda^{\prime})=E_{2}(\Lambda-E_{2}(\Lambda))=0.

For the second item, put
\Lambda^{\prime}=\Lambda-E_{1}(\Lambda)
. Since
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are expectations, we have

E_{1}(\Lambda^{\prime})=E_{1}(\Lambda-E_{1}(\Lambda))=0

and

E_{2}(\Lambda^{\prime})=E_{2}(\Lambda)-E_{2}(E_{1}(\Lambda))=0-E_{1}(E_{2}(\Lambda))=0

where Fubini’s Theorem is used to interchange the order of the expectations.

Thus we have that

\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}=\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda}-\mathfrak{d}_{E_{1}(\Lambda)}.

Note that
\mathfrak{d}_{E_{1}(\Lambda)}
does not depend on
x
and hence is inner, proving part 2. Therefore we may always choose
\Lambda
such that
E_{1}(\Lambda)=E_{2}(\Lambda)=0
.

Now suppose
E_{1}(\Lambda)=E_{2}(\Lambda)=0
and
\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda}
is inner. Since
\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda}
is inner,
\Lambda
does not depend on
x
. Thus we have that

0=E_{1}(\Lambda)=\Lambda.

So
\Lambda=0
. It is clear that if
\Lambda=0
then
\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda}
is inner.
∎

The following theorem describes a decomposition of any continuous derivation on
HS^{\infty}(s)
into special derivations and is the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.10
.

If
\delta:HS^{\infty}(s)\to HS^{\infty}(s)
is a continuous derivation, then there exist unique
\varphi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
and
\Lambda\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
with
E_{1}(\Lambda)=E_{2}(\Lambda)=0
such that

\delta=\delta_{\varphi}+\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda}+\tilde{\delta}\,,

where
\delta_{\varphi}
was defined in Proposition
4.2
,
\mathfrak{d}_{\Lambda}
defined in equation (
4.7
), and
\tilde{\delta}
is an inner derivation on
HS^{\infty}(s)
.

Proof.

Since
\delta
is a continuous derivation, equation (
4.1
) defines a
\varphi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
. Consider the following calculation:

q(\delta(a)-\delta_{\varphi}(a))=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\varphi(\theta)\frac{d}{d\theta}q(a)-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\varphi(\theta)\frac{d}{d\theta}q(a)=0

for all
a\in HS^{\infty}(s)
. Thus the difference
\delta-\delta_{\varphi}
is a continuous derivation from
HS^{\infty}(s)
to
I_{s}^{\infty}
. Observe that the range of
\delta_{\varphi}
is contained in
I_{s}^{\infty}
if and only if
\varphi=0
. This gives a unique

in this decomposition. By Proposition

there exists a

and

such that

(\delta-\delta_{\varphi})(a)=\mathfrak{d}_{\Psi}(a)+[b,a]

for any

choose
\Psi

. Define
\Lambda

\Lambda=\Psi-E_{1}(\Psi)
and again by Proposition

for every

4.9

∎

Appendix

The main objective of this appendix is to review some generalities about
the concept of crossed products by endomorphisms under special hypotheses first considered in
[
23
]
.

Let
A
be a unital C
∗
-algebra and
\alpha:A\to A
be a monomorphism with hereditary range.
Clearly
\alpha(1)
is a projection and notice that
\textrm{Ran}(\alpha)
, the range of
\alpha
, is contained in
\alpha(1)A\alpha(1)
. It easily follows, see
[
10
]
Proposition 4.1, that if
\textrm{Ran}(\alpha)
is a hereditary subalgebra of
A
then we have

\textrm{Ran}(\alpha)=\alpha(1)A\alpha(1).

Thus we can define a left inverse
\beta
of
\alpha
by:

\beta(a):=\alpha^{-1}(\alpha(1)a\alpha(1)).

(6.1)

It is easy to see that we have the following properties:

\beta\alpha(a)=a,\,\alpha\beta(a)=\alpha(1)a\alpha(1),\textrm{ and }\beta(1)=1.

Moreover, the map
\beta:A\to A
is linear, continuous, positive and has the “transfer” property:

\beta(\alpha(a)b)=a\beta(b),

and thus is an example of Exel’s transfer operator
[
10
]
. Because we have
\alpha\beta(a)=\alpha(1)a\alpha(1)
it is a complete transfer operator of
[
1
]
and in particular a non-degenerate transfer operator of
[
10
]
, i.e. it satisfies
\alpha(\beta(1))=\alpha(1)
. Also notice that if the algebra
A
is commutative, then
\beta
is also an endomorphism.

Stacey in
[
28
]
(and Murphy in
[
23
]
) defines the crossed product
A\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}
as the universal unital C
∗
-algebra with generators
M_{a}
for
a\in A
such that the map
a\mapsto M_{a}
is a
*
-homomorphism, and an isometry
V
, subject to the relation:

M_{\alpha(a)}=VM_{a}V^{*},\ a\in A.

This implies that
M_{\alpha(1)}=VV^{*}
. We also have

V^{*}M_{a}V=M_{\beta(a)},

which follows from the following calculation:

V^{*}M_{a}V=V^{*}VV^{*}M_{a}VV^{*}V=V^{*}M_{\alpha(1)}M_{a}M_{\alpha(1)}V=V^{*}M_{\alpha\beta(a)}V=V^{*}VM_{\beta(a)}V^{*}V=M_{\beta(a)}.

If the algebra
A
is commutative we get a stronger relation:

M_{a}V=VM_{\beta(a)}.

(6.2)

Indeed, we can manipulate as follows:

VM_{\beta(a)}=VV^{*}M_{a}V=M_{\alpha(1)}M_{a}V=M_{a}M_{\alpha(1)}V=M_{a}VV^{*}V=M_{a}V.

Similarly to crossed products by automorphisms the map
a\mapsto M_{a}
is injective by Corollary 1.3 in
[
5
]
. For the remainder of the appendix we identify
a
with
M_{a}
and drop the
M_{a}
notation.

Given a unital C
∗
-algebra
A
, an endomorphism
\alpha:A\to A
, and a transfer operator
\beta
, the Exel’s crossed product is the universal C
∗
-algebra generated by a copy of
A
and element
V
subject to the relations:

(1)

Va=\alpha(a)V

(2)

V^{*}aV=\beta(a)

(3)

(Redundancy Condition) If for
a\in\overline{A\alpha(A)A}
and
k\in\overline{AVV^{*}A}
we have
abV=kbV
for all
b\in A
then
a=k
.

Notice that, compared to Stacey’s definition,
V
does not have to be an isometry, unless
\beta(1)=1
. If
\beta(1)=1
and so
V
is an isometry then the first condition of Exel’s crossed product is weaker than Stacey’s condition
VaV^{*}=\alpha(a)
. On the other hand, there is no analog of condition 2 above in Stacey’s scheme, where in general
V^{*}AV
is not contained in
A
. Notice also that the less intuitive redundancy condition is empty if the two ideals
\overline{A\alpha(A)A}
and
\overline{AVV^{*}A}
are equal. This is because if
abV=0
for all
b\in A
then
abVV^{*}c=0
for all
b,c\in A
, so
ax=0
for all
x\in\overline{AVV^{*}A}
and in particular for
x=a^{*}
, which implies
a=0
.

Interestingly, if
\alpha
is a monomorphism with hereditary range then both seemingly quite different concepts of crossed products by endomorphisms coincide if we take
\beta
to be the transfer operator given by equation (
6.1
). Then, as we have seen above, Stacey’s conditions imply the first two of Exel’s conditions. Also, since
VV^{*}=\alpha(1)
, we have

\overline{AVV^{*}A}=\overline{A\alpha(1)A}\subseteq\overline{A\alpha(A)A}=\overline{A\alpha(1)A\alpha(1)A}\subseteq\overline{A\alpha(1)A}.

Consequently, the two ideals
\overline{A\alpha(A)A}
and
\overline{AVV^{*}A}
are equal and the redundancy condition is trivially satisfied.

Conversely, starting with Exel’s conditions for a monomorphism
\alpha
with hereditary range and the transfer operator
\beta
given by equation (
6.1
) we see that
\beta(1)=1
and so
V
is an isometry. The other Stacey condition follows from the redundancy condition since
\alpha(a)\in\overline{A\alpha(A)A}
,
VaV^{*}=aVV^{*}\in\overline{AVV^{*}A}
and we have:

VaV^{*}bV=Va\beta(b)=\alpha(a)\alpha\beta(b)V=\alpha(a)\alpha(1)b\alpha(1)V=\alpha(a)bV,

where we used
\alpha(1)V=V\cdot 1=V
.

To understand the structure of the crossed product C
∗
-algebra
A\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}
, the following Fourier type series expansions are frequently useful.
Using the crossed product commutation relations one can arrange the polynomials in
V
,
V^{*}
and
a
so that
a
’s are in front of powers of
V
and powers of
V^{*}
are in front of
a
’s. This leads to the following observation:
the vector space
\mathcal{F}(A,V)
consisting of finite sums

x=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}V^{n}+\sum_{n<0}(V^{*})^{-n}a_{n}\,,

(6.3)

where
a_{n}\in A
, is a dense
*
-subalgebra of
A\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}
.
Additionally, using
V^{n}=V^{n}(V^{*})^{n}V^{n}
and its adjoint
one can always choose coefficients
a_{n}
such that

a_{n}=a_{n}V^{n}(V^{*})^{n},\ n\geq 0\textrm{ and }a_{n}=V^{-n}(V^{*})^{-n}a_{n},\ n<0.

Moreover such coefficients are unique. This follows from the existence of an expectation in
A\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}
.
It is defined using a one-parameter group of automorphisms given on generators by formulas
\rho_{\theta}(V)=e^{2\pi i\theta}V
and
\rho_{\theta}(a)=a
and extending by universality to the whole crossed product
A\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}
.
Define the map
E:A\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}\to A\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}
by

E(a)=\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{\theta}(a)\,d\theta\,.

Clearly,
E
is an expectation in
A\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}
onto
A\subseteq A\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}
.
For any
x\in\mathcal{F}(A,V)
given by a finite sum as in equation (
6.3
)
we compute directly to get

a_{n}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &E(x(V^{*})^{n})&&\textrm{ if }n\geq 0\\
&E(V^{-n}x)&&\textrm{ if }n<0\,.\end{aligned}\right.

Alternatively, if the algebra
A
is commutative and we have the stronger condition, equation (
6.2
), then we can arrange the polynomials in
V
,
V^{*}
and
a
in the opposite order to equation (
6.3
). Then the
*
-subalgebra
\mathcal{F}(A,V)
can be described as consisting of finite sums

x=\sum_{n\geq 0}V^{n}b_{n}+\sum_{n<0}b_{n}(V^{*})^{-n}\,

(6.4)

with
b_{n}\in A
. The advantage of equation (
6.4
) over equation (
6.3
) is that the coefficients
b_{n}
are uniquely determined by
x
and are given by the formulas:

b_{n}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &E((V^{*})^{n}x)&&\textrm{ if }n\geq 0\\
&E(xV^{-n})&&\textrm{ if }n<0\,.\end{aligned}\right.

Notice that by the properties of expectations we have the following norm estimates for coefficients in equations (
6.3
) and (
6.4
):

||a_{n}||\leq||x||\textrm{ and }||b_{n}||\leq||x||

for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Suppose
\pi:A\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}\to B(H)
is a representation of
A\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}
in a Hilbert space

. We say that
\pi
satisfies the O’Donovan conditions if

(1)

\pi
restricted to
A
is a faithful representation of the C
∗
-algebra
A
.

(2)

for every

we have:

||\pi(a_{0})||=||\pi(b_{0})||\leq||\pi(x)||.

A key result from
[
5

says that if a representation
\pi:A\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}\to B(H)
satisfies the O’Donovan conditions then it is a faithful representation of the crossed product C
∗
-algebra
A\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}
.
