Analysis of a Computational Framework for Bayesian Inverse Problems: Ensemble Kalman Updates and MAP Estimators Under Mesh Refinement
Daniel Sanz-Alonso), Nathan Waniorek)

Abstract
This paper analyzes a popular computational framework to solve infinite-dimensional Bayesian inverse problems, discretizing the prior and the forward model in a finite-dimensional weighted inner product space. We demonstrate the benefit of working on a weighted space by establishing operator-norm bounds for finite element and graph-based discretizations of Matérn-type priors and deconvolution forward models. For linear-Gaussian inverse problems, we develop a general theory to characterize the error in the approximation to the posterior. We also embed the computational framework into ensemble Kalman methods and MAP estimators for nonlinear inverse problems. Our operator-norm bounds for prior discretizations guarantee the scalability and accuracy of these algorithms under mesh refinement.

Introduction

Bayesian inverse problems on infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces arise in numerous applications, including medical imaging, seismology, climate science, reservoir modeling, and mechanical engineering
[
13
,
11
,
31
,
7
,
16

. In these and other applications, it is important to reconstruct function input parameters of partial differential equations (PDEs) based on noisy measurement of the PDE solution. This paper analyzes a framework to numerically solve infinite-dimensional Bayesian inverse problems, where the discretization is carried out in a weighted inner product space. The framework, along with a compelling demonstration of its computational benefits, was introduced in
[
11
]
to solve PDE-constrained Bayesian inverse problems using finite element discretizations. We develop a rigorous analysis that explains the advantage of working on a weighted space. Our theory accommodates not only finite element discretizations, but also graph-based methods from machine learning. For linear-Gaussian inverse problems, we bound the error in the approximation to the posterior. More broadly, we embed the discretization framework into ensemble Kalman methods and
maximum a posteriori
(MAP) estimators for nonlinear inverse problems, and study the accuracy and scalability of these algorithms under mesh refinement.

An overarching theme of this paper is that,
by suitably choosing the weighted inner product, one can ensure accurate approximation of the infinite-dimensional Hilbert inner product as the discretization mesh is refined. Following this unifying principle, we analyze the finite element discretizations considered in
[
11
]
together with graph-based discretizations. Finite elements are predominant in scientific computing solution of PDE-constrained inverse problems, see e.g.
[

[

27
]
. For both types of discretization, we obtain operator-norm bounds for important classes of prior and forward models. Specifically, we will use Matérn-type Gaussian priors and deconvolution forward models as guiding examples.

Matérn priors play a central role in Bayesian inverse problems

Deconvolution forward models arise in image deblurring and heat inversion, among other important applications. Since the seminal paper by Franklin that introduced the formulation of Bayesian inversion in function space

For linear-Gaussian inverse problems, operator-norm error bounds for prior and forward model discretizations translate into error bounds in the approximation of the posterior. We formalize this claim under a general assumption, which we verify for our guiding examples of Matérn priors and deconvolution. Additionally, we apply the computational framework in

The well-posedness of the posterior measure under perturbations is one of the hallmarks of the Bayesian formulation of inverse problems
[
54
,
40
,
41
,
49
]
. For a fixed prior measure, the error in the posterior measure caused by discretization of the forward model can be bounded in Hellinger distance
[
17
]
and in Kullback-Leibler divergence
[
42
]
.
Posterior stability under perturbations to the prior measure in addition to perturbations to the likelihood have been recently investigated using Wasserstein distance
[
51
]
and more general integral probability metrics
[
24

[
51

yield a stability theory similar to ours; however, bounding the Wasserstein distance between the prior measures requires trace bounds on the covariance operators, whereas our results only necessitate operator norm bounds.

1.1
Outline and Main Contributions

•

Section
2
reviews the formulation of a Bayesian inverse problem in a Hilbert space and presents our general discretization framework. We introduce the heat inversion problem with Matérn Gaussian process prior as a model guiding example, and then illustrate how finite element and graph-based methods can be interpreted within our discretization framework.

•

Section
3
presents a novel analysis of the error incurred in our discretization framework for linear-Gaussian Bayesian inverse problems. Theorems
10
and
11
quantify the errors in the discretized posterior mean and covariance operators to their continuum counterparts, up to universal constants. We then verify the assumptions of this general theory for the finite element and graph-based discretizations of the heat inversion problem, thus deriving error bounds for these popular discretization schemes.

•

Section
4
formulates the ensemble Kalman update within our general discretization framework. The results in this section give non-asymptotic bounds on the ensemble estimation of the posterior mean and covariance in terms of a notion of effective dimension based on spectral decay of the prior covariance operator. We then show that the effective dimension of the discretized prior covariance can be controlled by the effective dimension of the continuum covariance, which is necessarily finite. Consequently, the ensemble approximation will not deteriorate under mesh refinement.

•

Section
5

considers posterior measures resulting from nonlinear Bayesian inverse problems and their MAP estimators. We show that with a suitable discretization of the forward model, the MAP estimators of the computationally tractable discretized posterior measures converge to the MAP estimators of the continuum posterior. Finally, we apply the theory to the inverse problem of recovering the initial condition of the Navier-Stokes equations from pointwise observations.

•

Section
6
closes with conclusions and directions for future work.

Problem Setting and Computational Framework

This section contains necessary background. Subsection
2.1
overviews the formulation of Bayesian inverse problems in Hilbert space. Subsection
2.2
describes the computational framework analyzed in this paper. Finally, Subsection

2.1
Inverse Problem in Hilbert Space

Let
\mathcal{H}
be an infinite-dimensional separable Hilbert space with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.
Consider the inverse problem of recovering an unknown
u\in\mathcal{H}
from data
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
related by

\displaystyle y=\mathcal{F}u+\eta,\quad\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Gamma),

(1)

where
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
is a linear and bounded forward model, and
\eta
represents Gaussian observation noise with known covariance matrix
\Gamma\in S_{++}^{d_{y}}
. Nonlinear forward models will be considered in Section
5
. The observation model (
1
) implies a Gaussian likelihood function

\displaystyle\pi_{\text{like}}(y|u)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|y-\mathcal{F}u\|_{\Gamma^{-1}}^{2}\right).

(2)

We adopt a Bayesian approach
[
56
,
34
,
49
]
and let
\mu_{0}=\mathcal{N}(m_{0},\mathcal{C}_{0})
be a Gaussian prior measure on
\mathcal{H},
where
\mathcal{C}_{0}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is a trace-class covariance operator defined by the requirement that

\displaystyle\langle v,\mathcal{C}_{0}w\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\mathbb{E}^{u\sim\mu_{0}}\Bigl[\bigl\langle v,(u-m_{0})\bigr\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\bigl\langle(u-m_{0}),w\bigr\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\Bigr],\quad\quad\forall v,w\in\mathcal{H},

(3)

and
m_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
is assumed to belong to the Cameron-Martin space
E=\text{Im}(\mathcal{C}_{0}^{1/2})\subset\mathcal{H}.

The Bayesian solution to the inverse problem is the conditional law of
u
given
y,
called the posterior probability measure
\mu_{\text{post}}.

Application of Bayes’ rule in infinite dimension
[
54
]
characterizes the posterior as a change of measure with respect to the prior, with Radon-Nikodym derivative given by the likelihood:

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu_{\text{post}}}{d\mu_{0}}(u)=\frac{1}{Z}\pi_{\text{like}}(y|u),

(4)

where
Z=\int\pi_{\text{like}}(y|u)\,d\mu_{0}
is a normalizing constant. Since the prior is Gaussian and the forward model is linear and bounded, the posterior is also Gaussian,
\mu_{\text{post}}=\mathcal{N}(m_{\text{post}},\mathcal{C}_{\text{post}}),
where

\displaystyle m_{\text{post}}

\displaystyle=m_{0}+\mathcal{C}_{0}\mathcal{F}^{*}(\mathcal{F}\mathcal{C}_{0}\mathcal{F}^{*}+\Gamma)^{-1}(y-\mathcal{F}m_{0}),

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\text{post}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{0}-\mathcal{C}_{0}\mathcal{F}^{*}(\mathcal{F}\mathcal{C}_{0}\mathcal{F}^{*}+\Gamma)^{-1}\mathcal{F}\mathcal{C}_{0}.

(6)

Here,
\mathcal{F}^{*}
denotes the adjoint of
\mathcal{F},
which is the unique map from
\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
to
\mathcal{H}
that satisfies

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{F}u,y\rangle=\langle u,\mathcal{F}^{*}y\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\quad\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{H},y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}.

In Subsection
2.2
, we will review the computational framework in
[
11
]
to approximate the posterior. The idea is to replace the inverse problem (
1
) on the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with an inverse problem on a finite-dimensional weighted inner product space. Under general conditions on the discretization of the forward model and prior measure, the posterior mean and covariance of the finite and infinite-dimensional inverse problems are close together; this claim will be formalized and rigorously established in Section
3
.

2.2
Computational Framework

This subsection overviews the computational framework presented in
[
11
]
.
Let
\{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\ldots,\phi_{n}\}
be a basis for an
n
-dimensional space
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{H}.

For
u=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\phi_{i}\in\mathcal{V}
, we denote by
\vec{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})^{T}
the vector of coefficients of
u
in this basis. We will endow the space of coefficients with a weighted inner product that is naturally inherited from the inner product in
\mathcal{H}.
Specifically, for any
u,v\in\mathcal{V}
, we have

\langle u,v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\vec{u}^{T}M\vec{v}=\langle\vec{u},\vec{v}\rangle_{M},

where the matrix
M=(M_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}
is given by

M_{ij}=\langle\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\quad i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}.

This observation motivates us to introduce the inner product space
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}
defined by vector space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{M}.

We remark that for both the finite elements and graph-based discretizations introduced in Subsections
2.3.1
and
2.3.2
, the corresponding basis
\{\phi_{i}\}
will not be orthonormal; consequently
M\neq I
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}
will not agree with the standard Euclidean inner product space.
We now aim to replace the inverse problem (
1
) with an inverse problem on the weighted space
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}
.

To begin with, we define a discretization map
P:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}
that assigns to
u\in\mathcal{H}
the vector
\vec{u}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}
satisfying

Pu=\vec{u}^{\star}=(u_{1}^{\star},\ldots,u_{n}^{\star})^{T}=\arg\min_{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}}\frac{1}{2}\Bigl\|u-\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\phi_{i}\Bigr\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

(7)

In practice, this discretization can be computed by solving the linear system
M\vec{u}^{\star}=\vec{b}
, where
b_{i}=\langle u,\phi_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
. Conversely, our theoretical analysis will rely on an extension map
P^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}\to\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{H}
that assigns to a vector
\vec{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}
the element
P^{*}u\in\mathcal{V}
defined by

P^{*}\vec{u}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\phi_{i}.

(8)

One can verify that
\langle Pu,\vec{v}\rangle_{M}=\langle u,P^{*}\vec{v}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},
so that
P^{*}
is the adjoint of
P,
as our notation suggests. Similarly, one can verify that the map
P^{*}P:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{H}
is the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{V},
so that
PP^{*}:\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
is the identity map.

We are ready to introduce the inverse problem on the weighted inner product space
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}.
In analogy with (
1
), we seek to recover
\vec{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}
from data
y
related by

y=F\vec{u}+\eta,\quad\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Gamma),

(9)

where
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
is a discretized forward model, identified with a matrix
F\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}\times n}
. (Here and henceforth we will abuse notation and identify linear maps and matrices without further notice.) The map
F
should approximate
\mathcal{F}
in the sense that
\|\mathcal{F}-FP\|_{op}
is small; we refer to Subsections
3.2.2
and
3.3.2
for examples arising from discretization of PDE-constrained forward models in function space. In analogy with (
2
), the likelihood of observed data
y
given a coefficient vector
\vec{u}\in\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
is given by

\pi_{\text{like}}(y|\vec{u})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|y-F\vec{u}\|_{\Gamma^{-1}}^{2}\right).

Following the analogy with Subsection
2.1
, we choose a Gaussian prior
\mu_{0}^{n}=\mathcal{N}(\vec{m}_{0},C_{0})
on the space
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M},

with Lebesgue density

\displaystyle\pi_{0}(\vec{u})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bigl\langle\vec{u}-\vec{m}_{0},C_{0}^{-1}(\vec{u}-\vec{m}_{0})\bigr\rangle_{M}\right).

(10)

For the prior mean, we take
\vec{m}_{0}=Pm_{0};
for the prior covariance, we may take an invertible operator
C_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}
that approximates well
\mathcal{C}_{0},
in the sense that
\|\mathcal{C}_{0}-P^{*}C_{0}P\|_{op}
is small; we refer to Subsections
3.2.1
and
3.3.1
for examples arising from discretization of Matérn-type Gaussian processes.
Similar to (
3
), the prior covariance
C_{0}
satisfies, by definition, that

\langle\vec{v},C_{0}\vec{w}\rangle_{M}=\mathbb{E}^{\vec{u}\sim\mu_{0}^{n}}\Bigl[\langle\vec{v},(\vec{u}-\vec{m})\rangle_{M}\bigl\langle(\vec{u}-\vec{m}),\vec{w}\bigr\rangle_{M}\Bigr],\quad\quad\forall\vec{v},\vec{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}.

While we view
\mu_{0}^{n}
as a Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M},
it is helpful to notice that as a multivariate Gaussian distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with the standard inner product, we have that
\mu_{0}^{n}=\mathcal{N}(\vec{m_{0}},C_{0}^{E}),
with covariance
C_{0}^{E}=C_{0}M^{-1}.

Applying Bayes’ rule as in (
4
), we can express the posterior as a change of measure with respect to the prior:

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{\text{post}}^{n}}{\mu_{0}^{n}}=\frac{1}{Z_{h}}\pi_{\text{like}}(y|\vec{u}).

(11)

Since the space
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}
is finite dimensional, we recover the standard Bayes’ formula for the posterior distribution with Lebesgue density given by

\displaystyle\pi^{n}_{\text{post}}(\vec{u}|y)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|y-F\vec{u}\|_{\Gamma^{-1}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\langle\vec{u}-\vec{m}_{0},C_{0}^{-1}(\vec{u}-\vec{m}_{0})\rangle_{M}\right).

(12)

As in the infinite-dimensional setting, the posterior
\mu_{\text{post}}^{n}=\mathcal{N}(\vec{m}_{\text{post}},C_{\text{post}})
is Gaussian with analogous expressions for its mean
\vec{m}_{\text{post}}
and covariance
C_{\text{post}}
. A note of caution is that in the weighted space
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}
a careful distinction must be made between adjoint and matrix transpose. We present a detailed discussion of this distinction in Appendix
A
. In particular,
if the forward model is given by matrix
F\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}\times n},
then the adjoint map is given by
F^{\natural}=M^{-1}F^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{y}}.
Thus,

\displaystyle\vec{m}_{\text{post}}

\displaystyle=\vec{m}_{0}+C_{0}F^{\natural}(FC_{0}F^{\natural}+\Gamma)^{-1}(y-F\vec{m}_{0}),

(13)

\displaystyle C_{\text{post}}

\displaystyle=C_{0}-C_{0}F^{\natural}(FC_{0}F^{\natural}+\Gamma)^{-1}FC_{0}.

(14)

One can also view the posterior as a Gaussian measure in the standard Euclidean space, in which case the covariance operator is given by
C_{\text{post}}^{E}=C_{\text{post}}M^{-1}
. For completeness, we include the expressions for the posterior mean and covariance in Euclidean space written in terms of the Euclidean prior covariance:

\displaystyle\vec{m}_{\text{post}}

\displaystyle=\vec{m}_{0}+C_{0}^{E}F^{T}(FC_{0}^{E}F^{T}+\Gamma)^{-1}(y-F\vec{m}_{0}),

(15)

\displaystyle C_{\text{post}}^{E}

\displaystyle=C_{0}^{E}-C_{0}^{E}F^{T}(FC_{0}^{E}F^{T}+\Gamma)^{-1}FC_{0}^{E}.

(16)

Notice that both pairs of equations (
13
)–(
14
) and (
15
)–(
16
) are analogous to their infinite-dimensional counterparts in (
5
)–(
6
). However, our theory and examples in the next subsection will demonstrate the advantage of working on the weighted inner product space. Specifically, we will show that finite element and graph discretizations of important classes of prior covariance and forward model give discretized quantities
F^{\natural}
and
C_{0}
that approximate well in operator norm their infinite-dimensional counterparts
\mathcal{F}^{*}
and
\mathcal{C}_{0}
; the same would not be true for the Euclidean analogs
F^{T}
and
C_{0}^{E}.

Example 1
(Matérn-Type Prior).

We will consider Gaussian priors
\mu_{0}=\mathcal{N}(m_{0},\mathcal{C}_{0})
with Matérn covariance operator
\mathcal{C}_{0}
and mean

m_{0}
in the Cameron-Martin space
\emph{Im}(\mathcal{C}_{0}^{1/2})
. Specifically, for positive integer
\alpha,
we define
\mathcal{C}_{0}=\mathcal{A}^{-\alpha},
where
\mathcal{A}^{-1}:L^{2}(\Omega)\to H^{2}(\Omega)
is the solution operator for the following elliptic PDE in weak form:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Theta\nabla u\cdot\nabla p+\int_{\Omega}bup=\int_{\Omega}fp\,,\quad\quad\forall p\in H^{1}(\Omega).

(17)

That is, for
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
\mathcal{A}^{-1}f=u
, where
u
solves (
17
). Here and below, integrals are with respect to the Lebesgue measure if
\Omega=\mathcal{D}
and with respect to the Riemannian volume form if
\Omega=\mathcal{M}.

In the case
\Omega=\mathcal{D},
we assume for concreteness Dirichlet boundary conditions and take
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Omega)\equiv H_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{D}).
In the case
\Omega=\mathcal{M},
we take
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Omega)\equiv H^{1}(\mathcal{M}).

We emphasize that we view
\mathcal{A}^{-1}
as a map from the right-hand side of the elliptic PDE to the solution. To ensure that a solution to the PDE exists, is unique, and is sufficiently regular, the coefficients
\Theta
and
b
must be positive and sufficiently smooth; precise assumptions will be given in our theorem statements. Choosing these coefficients to be spatially varying allows one to encode additional prior information about the unknown, such as anisotropic correlations. The parameter
\alpha
in the covariance
\mathcal{C}_{0}=\mathcal{A}^{-\alpha}
controls the regularity of prior draws. For simplicity, we restrict our attention to positive integer-valued
\alpha
, but extensions to fractional values are possible
[
8
,
3
]
.
If the domain
\Omega
is sufficiently regular,
\alpha
is chosen to be large enough, and
\Theta,b
are positive and sufficiently smooth, then the covariance operator
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is trace-class on
L^{2}(\Omega)

[
54
]
.

Example 2
(Deconvolution Forward Model).

Consider the heat equation:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}v_{t}(x,t)=\Delta v(x,t),&x\in\Omega,\,t>0,\\
v(x,0)=u(x),&x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

(18)

In the case
\Omega=\mathcal{D},
we supplement (
18
) with Dirichlet boundary conditions
v(x,t)=0,\,x\in\partial\mathcal{D}.

We let
\mathcal{G}:L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
map an input function
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
to the solution to (
18
) at time
t=1.

It is well known that the heat equation admits a variational characterization, which will lend itself more naturally to the numerical approximations introduced in Subsections
2.3.1
and
2.3.2
.
We consider a linear observation model given by local averages of the solution over a Euclidean ball of radius
\delta>0
, which approximate pointwise observations:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}v=\left[v^{\delta}(x_{1}),\ldots,v^{\delta}(x_{d_{y}})\right]^{T},\quad v^{\delta}(x_{i}):=\int_{B_{\delta}(x_{i})\cap\Omega}v(x,1).

(19)

We then write our forward model as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}:=\mathcal{O}\circ\mathcal{G}.

(20)

The utility of considering the local average observation map is that it is a bounded linear map from
L^{2}(\Omega)
to
\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
, whereas pointwise observations are only bounded from subspaces of
L^{2}(\Omega)
with enough regularity to guarantee almost everywhere continuity. While the forward map given by the heat equation is sufficiently smoothing to guarantee the solution is continuous, the graph-based approximations to the forward map that we will consider may only converge in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. In those cases, it will be more convenient to consider the local average observations, as in
[
27
]
.

2.3.1
Finite Element Discretizations

The use of finite elements within the computational framework overviewed in Subsection
2.2
was proposed and numerically investigated in
[
11
]
. Here, the domain
\Omega
is an open, bounded, and sufficiently regular subset
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.
We let
\mathcal{V}
be a finite element discretization space with basis functions denoted by
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
. For concreteness, we restrict our attention to linear Lagrange polynomial basis vectors. These basis functions correspond to nodal points
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\phi_{i}(x_{j})=\delta_{ij}
for
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}.
The domain
\mathcal{D}
is partitioned by a mesh into elements
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots
with
h_{i}:=\text{diameter}(e_{i})
and
h:=\max h_{i}.
Two canonical families of Lagrange elements include
d
-simplexes and
d
-hypercubes. A
d
-simplex is the region
e\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
determined by
d+1
distinct points (for
d=1
this is an interval and for
d=2
a triangle). A
d
-hypercube is a product of
d
intervals on the real line,
e=[a_{1},b_{1}]\times\cdots\times[a_{d},b_{d}]\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. As the mesh is refined (that is, as we take
h\to 0
), the dimension of
\mathcal{V}
grows. To emphasize that we are considering a family of subspaces indexed by the mesh width parameter
h
, we adopt the notation
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}_{h}
, as is typical in the finite element literature.
The theory we will present in Subsection
3.2
holds for uniform (and more generally for quasi-uniform) mesh refinements.

Example 3
(Finite Element Approximation of Matérn Covariance).

For an operator
\mathcal{B}:L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
, one can consider the action of this operator restricted to the subspace
\mathcal{V}_{h}
, given by
\mathcal{B}_{h}=P^{*}P\mathcal{B}P^{*}P:\mathcal{V}_{h}\to\mathcal{V}_{h}
, which is a finite-dimensional linear map. The matrix representation
B
of this operator must satisfy

\int_{\mathcal{D}}\phi_{i}\mathcal{B}\phi_{j}\,dx=\langle\vec{e}_{i},B\vec{e}_{j}\rangle_{M},

where
\vec{e}_{i}
is the coordinate vector that corresponds to
\phi_{i}.
This implies that
B:\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
can be written explicitly as

\displaystyle B=M^{-1}K,

(21)

where

\displaystyle K_{ij}=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\phi_{i}\mathcal{B}\phi_{j}\,dx,\quad i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}.

(22)

In particular, when representing the differential operator
\mathcal{A}
defined in (
17
) we get that
K
is given by the stiffness matrix with entries

\displaystyle K_{ij}=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\Theta(x)\nabla\phi_{i}(x)\cdot\nabla\phi_{j}(x)+b(x)\phi_{i}(x)\phi_{j}(x)\,dx,\quad i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}.

(23)

One can verify that both
A=M^{-1}K
and
A^{-1}=K^{-1}M
are self-adjoint in the weighted inner product space. Recall that the coefficients of the Galerkin approximation to the elliptic PDE (
17
) are given by solving the linear system

\displaystyle K\vec{u}=\vec{b},

where
b_{i}=\int_{\mathcal{D}}f\phi_{i}\,dx
for
1\leq i\leq n,
see
[
44
]
. Since we have that
\vec{b}=M\vec{f}
(where
\vec{f}=Pf
), we see that
A^{-1}\vec{f}=K^{-1}M\vec{f}
is precisely the Galerkin approximation to
\mathcal{A}^{-1}f
. We will show in Subsection
3.2.1
that the matrix
C_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}
given by
C_{0}=A^{-\alpha}=(K^{-1}M)^{\alpha}
approximates well the continuum Matérn covariance operator
\mathcal{C}_{0}.

Example 4
(Finite Element Approximation of Heat Forward Model).

The variational formulation of the heat equation (
18
) naturally leads to a semidiscrete Galerkin approximation

\displaystyle v_{h}(x,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A^{i}(t)\phi_{i}(x),

(24)

which is required to satisfy that, for all
\phi\in\mathcal{V}_{h},

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{D}}\frac{\partial v_{h}}{\partial t}\phi(x)\,dx+\int_{\mathcal{D}}\nabla v_{h}\cdot\nabla\phi(x)\,dx=0,\quad\quad

\displaystyle t\in(0,1],

(25)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{D}}v_{h}(x,0)\phi(x)\,dx=\int_{D}u(x)\phi(x)\,dx,\quad

\displaystyle t=0.

(26)

This leads to a system of differential equations for the time-dependent coefficients
A^{i}(t):

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}M_{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}A^{j}(t)+K_{ij}A^{j}(t)=0,\quad\quad

\displaystyle i=1,\ldots,n,

(27)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}M_{ij}A^{j}(0)=g_{i},

\displaystyle i=1,\ldots,n,

(28)

where
M_{ij}=\langle\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}
,
K_{ij}=\langle\phi_{i},-\Delta\phi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}
, and
g_{i}=\langle u,\phi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}.
Note that
\vec{A}(t)=\left(A^{1}(t),\ldots,A^{n}(t)\right)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}.
Given the eigenpairs of
K
and
M
, this system of differential equations can be solved analytically. However, computing the eigenpairs may be prohibitively expensive in practice, so the solution is often approximated by a time-discretization of the semidiscrete problem. One such classical time discretization of (
25
) is the Crank-Nicolson method: find
v_{h}^{k}\in V_{h},
for
k=0,\ldots,1/\Delta t
such that, for every
\phi\in\mathcal{V}_{h},

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{D}}\frac{v_{h}^{k}(x)-v_{h}^{k-1}(x)}{\Delta t}\phi(x)\,dx+\int_{\mathcal{D}}\nabla\left(\frac{v_{h}^{k}(x)+v_{h}^{k-1}(x)}{2}\right)\cdot\nabla\phi(x)\,dx=0,\quad k=1,\ldots,1/\Delta t,

(29)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{D}}v_{h}^{0}(x)\phi(x)\,dx=\int_{\mathcal{D}}u(x)\phi(x)\,dx.

(30)

This leads to a system of linear equations analogous to (
27
) for the coefficients
A^{j}_{k}\approx A^{j}(k\Delta t)
for
k=0,\ldots,1/\Delta t-1
:

\displaystyle\frac{M+K\Delta t}{2}\vec{A}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{M-K\Delta t}{2}\vec{A}_{k},\quad\quad

\displaystyle k=1,\ldots,1/\Delta t-1,

\displaystyle M\vec{A}_{0}

\displaystyle=\vec{g},\quad

\displaystyle k=0.

The second equation is simply the requirement that
\vec{A}_{0}
be given by the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{V}_{h}
as
\vec{A}_{0}=Pu.
We write the mapping from the coefficients of the initial condition to the coefficients of the solution at
t=1
as
G:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}
, where

\displaystyle G=\left(\left(\frac{M+K\Delta t}{2}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{M-K\Delta t}{2}\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{\Delta t}}.

(31)

We denote by
O:\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
the map from coefficients to observations. Our discretized forward model
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
is then given by

\displaystyle F:=OG.

(32)

We remark that the observation map
\mathcal{O}
applied to the numerical solution to the heat equation is an integral of a piecewise linear function. Such an integral can be computed exactly via quadrature methods, which are typically tractable in low dimensions (e.g.
d=1,2,3
). Since finite element methods are seldom implemented in higher dimensions in practice, we make the simplifying assumption that the observation map can be evaluated exactly for functions in
\mathcal{V}_{h}
.

2.3.2
Graph-Based Discretizations

We will now see how the graph-based discretizations considered in
[
27
]
fit into the above framework. Here we let
\Omega=\mathcal{M}
be a
d
-dimensional smooth, connected, compact, Riemannian manifold embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{D}.
We assume further that
\mathcal{M}
has bounded sectional curvature and Riemannian metric inherited from
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Without loss of generality, we let
\mathcal{M}
be normalized such that
vol(\mathcal{M})=1.
We assume we have access to a point cloud
\mathcal{M}_{n}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{M}
of
n
samples from the uniform distribution
\gamma
on
\mathcal{M}
. We let
\gamma_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}
denote the empirical measure of
\mathcal{M}_{n}
. We will show that this setting naturally fits into our computational framework with basis vectors of the form
\phi_{i}(x)=\mathbb{1}_{U_{i}}(x)
, where the sets
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{M}
form a partition of
\mathcal{M}
. We recall the following result from
[
25
]
:

Proposition 5
(Existence of Transport Maps).

There is a constant
c
such that, with probability one, there exists a sequence of transport maps
T_{n}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}_{n}
such that
\gamma_{n}=T_{n\sharp}\gamma
and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup\frac{n^{1/d}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}d_{\mathcal{M}}(x,T_{n}(x))}{(\log n)^{c_{d}}}\leq c,

(33)

where
c_{d}=3/4
if
d=2
and
c_{d}=1/d
otherwise.

In the above,
\gamma_{n}=T_{n\sharp}\gamma
indicates that
\gamma(T_{n}^{-1}(U))=\gamma_{n}(U)
for all measurable
U
, and
d_{\mathcal{M}}
denotes the geodesic distance. We denote the pre-image of each singleton as
U_{i}=T_{n}^{-1}(\{x_{i}\})
. The sets
U_{i}
form a partition of
\mathcal{M}
and by the measure preserving property of
T_{n}
all have mass
\gamma(U_{i})=1/n.
It follows from Proposition
5
that we have
U_{i}\subset B_{\mathcal{M}}(x_{i},\varepsilon_{n})
, where

\varepsilon_{n}:=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}d_{\mathcal{M}}(x,T_{n}(x))\lesssim\frac{(\log n)^{c_{d}}}{n^{1/d}}.

These sets will be used to define our basis functions:
\phi_{i}(x)=\mathbb{1}_{U_{i}}(x)
, which correspond to locally constant interpolations of functions in
L^{2}(\mathcal{M}).
We can then interpret vectors of function values on the point cloud
\mathcal{M}_{n}
as coefficients vectors in
\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
, where the corresponding mass matrix is
M=\frac{1}{n}I_{n}.

Example 6
(Graph Approximation of Matérn Covariance).

In the manifold setting, we opt to approximate the elliptic differential operator via the graph Laplacian. We define a symmetric weight matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
that has entries
W_{ij}\geq 0
corresponding to closeness between points in
\mathcal{M}_{n}
by

\displaystyle W_{ij}=\frac{2(d+2)}{n\nu_{d}h_{n}^{d+2}}\mathbb{1}_{\{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|_{2}<h_{n}\}}.

(34)

Here
\nu_{d}
denotes the volume of the
d
-dimensional unit ball, and
h_{n}
denotes the graph connectivity. Defining the degree matrix
D=\emph{diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
where
d_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}W_{ij},
the unnormalized graph Laplacian is given by
\Delta_{n}^{un}=D-W.
This operator can be shown to converge pointwise and spectrally to
-\Delta
(the Laplace-Beltrami operator) in the manifold setting
[
6
,
12
]
. This construction can also be generalized to elliptic operators with spatially varying coefficients. To do so, we define a nonstationary weight matrix
\tilde{W}
with entries

\displaystyle\tilde{W}_{ij}=W_{ij}\sqrt{\Theta(x_{i})\Theta(x_{j})}=\frac{2(d+2)}{n\nu_{d}h_{n}^{d+2}}\mathbb{1}_{\{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|_{2}<h_{n}\}}\sqrt{\Theta(x_{i})\Theta(x_{j})}\,.

(35)

We then define
\Delta^{\Theta}_{n}=\tilde{D}-\tilde{W}
where
\tilde{D}_{ii}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tilde{W}_{ij}
. Denoting
B_{n}=\emph{diag}\bigl(b(x_{1}),\ldots,b(x_{n})\bigr)
, we approximate the differential operator
\mathcal{A}
as the matrix
A:\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
given by

\displaystyle A=\Delta^{\Theta}_{n}+B_{n}.

(36)

Both
A
and
A^{-1}
are symmetric and self-adjoint in the weighted inner product space. The matrix
C_{0}:\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
given by
C_{0}=A^{-\alpha}
will be shown to approximate well the continuum Matérn covariance operator
\mathcal{C}_{0}
in Subsection
3.3.1
.

Example 7
(Graph Approximation of Heat Forward Model).

For our graph-based approximation of the heat forward model, we consider the differential equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial t}=-\Delta_{n}^{un}v_{n},&t>0,\\
v_{n}(0)=u_{n}.\end{cases}

(37)

We let
G_{n}:\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
map
u_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
to the solution
v_{n}(1)
to (
37
). Given the eigenpairs of the graph Laplacian, denoted
\bigl\{(\lambda_{n}^{(i)},\psi_{n}^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we can explicitly write the solution to this ODE:

\displaystyle G_{n}(u_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\lambda_{n}^{(i)}\right)\langle u_{n},\psi_{n}^{(i)}\rangle_{M}\psi_{n}^{(i)}.

(38)

The computations presented above for both the prior covariance discretization and the heat forward model do not require the explicit computation of the sets
U_{i}
that partition
\mathcal{M}
and define our basis vectors. In order to exactly compute the observation map defined in (
19
), we would need to exactly know the sets
U_{i}
. To circumvent this challenge, we instead consider a surrogate observation map
\mathcal{O}_{n}:\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
that only requires knowledge of the point cloud
\mathcal{M}_{n}:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{n}v_{n}=\left[\bar{v}^{\delta}_{n,1},\ldots,\bar{v}^{\delta}_{n,1}\right]^{T},\quad\bar{v}^{\delta}_{n,j}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k:x_{k}\in B_{\delta}(x_{j})\cap\mathcal{M}_{n}}[v_{n}(1)]_{k},

(39)

for
1\leq j\leq d_{y},
where
[v_{n}(1)]_{k}
denotes the
k^{th}
entry of the vector
v_{n}
. For the purposes of our analysis, it will be necessary to make the assumption on the boundary of
\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(x_{k})\cap\mathcal{M}
. In particular, we assume that
\text{length}\left(\partial(\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(x_{k})\cap\mathcal{M})\right)=C_{\delta,j}
for
1\leq j\leq d_{y},
where
C_{\delta,j}
is a constant that depends on
\mathcal{M}
,
\delta
and the observation point
x_{j}
.

Our discretized forward model
F:\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
is then given by

\displaystyle F:=\mathcal{O}_{n}G_{n}.

(40)

3
Error Analysis

This section analyzes the computational framework introduced in Section
2
. We start in Subsection
3.1
by establishing error bounds on the infinite-dimensional posterior mean and covariance based on a general assumption. Next, we verify this assumption in the function space setting of Subsection
2.3
, studying finite element discretizations (Subsection
3.2
) and graph-based methods (Subsection
3.3
).

3.1
Error Analysis: General Computational Framework

The main results of this subsection, Theorems
10
and Theorem
11
below, quantify the errors in the mean and covariance approximations

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{m}

\displaystyle=\|m_{\text{post}}-P^{*}\vec{m}_{\text{post}}\|_{\mathcal{H}},

(41)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{C}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{C}_{\text{post}}-P^{*}C_{\text{post}}P\|_{op},

(42)

where we recall that
P:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
is the discretization map defined in (
7
) and
P^{*}:\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}\to\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{H}
is the extension map defined in (
8
). Our bounds on
\varepsilon_{m}
and
\varepsilon_{C}
will rely on a general assumption on (i) the closeness of the covariance operator
\mathcal{C}_{0}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
and its discrete approximation
C_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M},
encoded in an assumption on the operator norm
\|\mathcal{C}_{0}-P^{*}C_{0}P\|_{op};
(ii) the closeness of the forward model
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
and its approximation
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}},
encoded in an assumption on the operator norm
\|\mathcal{F}-FP\|_{op};
and (iii) the closeness of the finite-dimensional space
\mathcal{V}
and the infinite-dimensional space
\mathcal{H},
encoded in an assumption that the orthogonal projection operator
P^{*}P:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{V}
is close to the identity.

Assumption 8
.

The following holds:

(i)

(Error in Approximation of Prior Covariance.)
There is a constant
r_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
and a function
\psi_{1}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\psi_{1}(n)=0
such that
\|\mathcal{C}_{0}-P^{*}C_{0}P\|_{op}\leq r_{1}\psi_{1}(n).

(ii)

(Error in Approximation of Forward Model.)
There is a constant
r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and a function
\psi_{2}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\psi_{2}(n)=0
such that
\|\mathcal{F}-FP\|_{op}\leq r_{2}\psi_{2}(n).

(iii)

(Error in Orthogonal Projection.)

There is a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
continuously embedded in
\mathcal{H},
a constant
r_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
and a function
\psi_{3}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\psi_{3}(n)=0
such that, for every
u\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime},

\|(I-P^{*}P)u\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq r_{3}\psi_{3}(n)\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}.

In Subsections
3.2
and
3.3
, we will verify Assumption
8
for the important examples of finite element and graph discretization spaces, Matérn-type prior covariance operators, and deconvolution forward models. In these examples, we will take the space
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
in Assumption
8
(iii) to be an appropriate Sobolev space contained in the Cameron-Martin space
\text{Im}(\mathcal{C}_{0}^{1/2})
.

To establish upper bounds on the mean and covariance approximation errors (
41
) and (
42
), we will first prove a lemma similar to those in
[
2
]
and
[
37
]
. Following the approach in these papers, we introduce the Kalman gain operator

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}:S_{+}\times B(\mathcal{H},\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}})\to B(\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}},\mathcal{H}),\quad\quad\mathscr{K}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{F})=\mathcal{C}\mathcal{F}^{*}(\mathcal{F}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{F}^{*}+\Gamma)^{-1}.

(43)

Unlike
[
2
,
37
]
, we view the Kalman gain operator as a function of both the forward map and the covariance, not just the covariance.
The following lemma shows that the Kalman gain operator is pointwise continuous.

Lemma 9
(Continuity of Kalman Gain Update).

Let
\mathscr{K}
be the Kalman gain operator defined in (
43
). Let
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}\in S_{+}
, let
\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}\in B(\mathcal{H},\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}),
and let
\Gamma\in S_{++}^{d_{y}}.
The following holds:

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{K}(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{1})-\mathscr{K}(\mathcal{C}_{2},\mathcal{F}_{2})\|_{op}\leq c_{1}\|\mathcal{C}_{1}-\mathcal{C}_{2}\|_{op}+c_{2}\|\mathcal{F}_{1}-\mathcal{F}_{2}\|_{op},

(44)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}c_{1}&=\|\Gamma^{-1}\|_{op}\|\mathcal{F}_{2}\|_{op}+\|\Gamma^{-1}\|_{op}^{2}\|\mathcal{C}_{1}\|_{op}\|\mathcal{F}_{1}\|_{op}\|\mathcal{F}_{2}\|_{op}^{2},\\
c_{2}&=\|\Gamma^{-1}\|_{op}\|\mathcal{C}_{1}\|_{op}+\|\Gamma^{-1}\|_{op}^{2}\|\mathcal{C}_{1}\|_{op}^{2}\|\mathcal{F}_{1}\|_{op}^{2}+\|\Gamma^{-1}\|_{op}\|\mathcal{C}_{1}\|_{op}^{2}\|\mathcal{F}_{1}\|_{op}\|\mathcal{F}_{2}\|_{op}.\end{split}

(45)

Proof.

Applying Lemma A.8 from
[
2
]
(which was stated for matrices, but also holds in our infinite-dimensional setting), we get that

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{K}(\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{1})-\mathscr{K}(\mathcal{F}_{2},\mathcal{C}_{2})\|_{op}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Gamma^{-1}\|_{op}\|\mathcal{C}_{1}\mathcal{F}_{1}^{*}-\mathcal{C}_{2}\mathcal{F}_{2}^{*}\|_{op}

\displaystyle+\|\Gamma^{-1}\|_{op}^{2}\|\mathcal{C}_{1}\mathcal{F}_{1}^{*}\|_{op}\|\mathcal{F}_{1}\mathcal{C}_{1}\mathcal{F}_{1}^{*}-\mathcal{F}_{2}\mathcal{C}_{2}\mathcal{F}_{2}^{*}\|_{op}.

We can then bound each of these terms with the triangle inequality:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{C}_{1}\mathcal{F}_{1}^{*}-\mathcal{C}_{2}\mathcal{F}_{2}^{*}\|_{op}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{C}_{1}\mathcal{F}_{1}^{*}-\mathcal{C}_{1}\mathcal{F}_{2}^{*}+\mathcal{C}_{1}\mathcal{F}_{2}^{*}-\mathcal{C}_{2}\mathcal{F}_{2}^{*}\|_{op}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{C}_{1}\|_{op}\|\mathcal{F}_{1}-\mathcal{F}_{2}\|_{op}+\|\mathcal{F}_{2}\|_{op}\|\mathcal{C}_{1}-\mathcal{C}_{2}\|_{op},

and

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{F}_{1}\mathcal{C}_{1}\mathcal{F}_{1}^{*}-\mathcal{F}_{2}\mathcal{C}_{2}\mathcal{F}_{2}^{*}\|_{op}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{F}_{1}\mathcal{C}_{1}\mathcal{F}_{1}^{*}-\mathcal{F}_{2}\mathcal{C}_{1}\mathcal{F}_{1}^{*}+\mathcal{F}_{2}\mathcal{C}_{1}\mathcal{F}_{1}^{*}-\mathcal{F}_{2}\mathcal{C}_{2}\mathcal{F}_{2}^{*}\|_{op}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{C}_{1}\mathcal{F}_{1}^{*}\|_{op}\|\mathcal{F}_{1}-\mathcal{F}_{2}\|_{op}+\|\mathcal{F}_{2}\|_{op}\|\mathcal{C}_{1}\mathcal{F}_{1}^{*}-\mathcal{C}_{2}\mathcal{F}_{2}^{*}\|_{op}.

Combining the previous three displayed inequalities gives the desired result.

Theorem 10
(Mean Approximation Error).

Consider the error
\varepsilon_{m}
in (
41
) between the true posterior mean in (
5
) and its finite-dimensional posterior approximation in (
13
). Then, under Assumption
8
it holds that

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{m}\leq r_{1}^{\prime}\psi_{1}(n)+r_{2}^{\prime}\psi_{2}(n)+r_{3}^{\prime}\psi_{3}(n),

(46)

where

\displaystyle r_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=2c_{1}r_{1}\|y-\mathcal{F}m_{0}\|_{2},

\displaystyle r_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=2c_{2}r_{2}\|y-\mathcal{F}m_{0}\|_{2}+r_{2}\|m_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|\mathscr{K}(\mathcal{C}_{0},\mathcal{F})\|_{op},

\displaystyle r_{3}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=r_{3}\|m_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}},

and
c_{1},

c_{2}
are defined in (
45
).

Proof.

By the definition of
\varepsilon_{m}
in (
41
) and an application of the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varepsilon_{m}\leq\|m_{0}-P^{*}\vec{m}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}}&+\|\mathscr{K}(\mathcal{C}_{0},\mathcal{F})-\mathscr{K}(P^{*}C_{0}P,FP)\|_{op}\|y-F\vec{m}_{0}\|_{2}\\
&+\|\mathscr{K}(\mathcal{C}_{0},\mathcal{F})\|_{op}\|\mathcal{F}m_{0}-F\vec{m}_{0}\|_{2}.\end{split}

(47)

We next bound each of the terms on the right-hand side.
By Assumption
8
(iii) and the fact that
\vec{m}_{0}=Pm_{0}
, we have that

\displaystyle\|m_{0}-P^{*}\vec{m}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=\|m_{0}-P^{*}Pm_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq r_{3}\|m_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}\psi_{3}(n).

(48)

By Lemma
9
and Assumption
8
(i) and (ii), we have that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\mathscr{K}(\mathcal{C}_{0},\mathcal{F})-\mathscr{K}(P^{*}C_{0}P,FP)\|_{op}&\leq c_{1}\|\mathcal{C}_{0}-P^{*}C_{0}P\|_{op}+c_{2}\|\mathcal{F}-FP\|_{op}\\
&\leq c_{1}r_{1}\psi_{1}(n)+c_{2}r_{2}\psi_{2}(n).\end{split}

(49)

By Assumption
8
(ii) and (iii) we have that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|y-F\vec{m}_{0}\|&\leq\|y-\mathcal{F}m_{0}\|+\|\mathcal{F}\|_{op}\|m_{0}-Pm_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}}+\|\mathcal{F}-FP\|_{op}\|m_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\\

4
Sample Size Requirements for Ensemble Kalman Updates

In this section, we formulate the ensemble Kalman update in weighted inner product space and show that the effective dimension of the discretized problem is controlled by the effective dimension of the continuum problem, which is finite.

4.1
Ensemble Approximation to Finite-Dimensional Posterior

To implement an ensemble Kalman approximation to the finite-dimensional Gaussian posterior given by (
12
), we need to draw samples from the Gaussian prior distribution,
\mathcal{N}(\vec{m}_{0},C_{0})
given by (
10
). To do so, we draw
\xi^{(j)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),
and compute samples

\displaystyle u^{(j)}=\vec{m}_{0}+L\xi^{(j)},\quad u^{(j)}\sim\mathcal{N}(\vec{m}_{0},C_{0}),

(63)

for
1\leq j\leq J
,
where
L
is a linear map from
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}
such that
C_{0}=LL^{\sharp}=LL^{T}M.
Recall that given samples
u^{(1)},\ldots,u^{(J)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{C})
from a Gaussian measure in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, the sample covariance operator is defined as the operator
\widehat{\hskip 0.0pt\mathcal{C}}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}

\widehat{\hskip 0.0pt\mathcal{C}}u:=\frac{1}{J-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\langle u^{(j)},u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}u^{(j)},\quad u\in\mathcal{H}.

As such, the sample covariance operator on
\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{C}_{0}=\frac{1}{J-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}(u^{(j)}-\widehat{m}_{0})(u^{(j)}-\widehat{m}_{0})^{T}M,

where
\widehat{m}_{0}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}u^{(j)}
is the sample mean. The perturbed observation ensemble Kalman update transforms each sample from the prior ensemble via

\displaystyle v^{(j)}=u^{(j)}+\widehat{C}_{0}F^{\natural}\left(F\widehat{C}_{0}F^{\natural}+\Gamma\right)^{-1}\left(y-Fu^{(j)}+\eta^{(j)}\right),\quad\eta^{(j)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\Gamma).

(64)

We then output the sample mean and covariance of the transformed ensemble, given by

\displaystyle\widehat{m}_{\text{post}}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}v^{(j)},\text{ and }\widehat{C}_{\text{post}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{J-1}\sum_{J=1}^{J}(v^{(j)}-\widehat{m}_{\text{post}})(v^{(j)}-\widehat{m}_{\text{post}})^{T}M.

(65)

4.2
Ensemble Kalman Approximation: Error Analysis

We now want to derive non-asymptotic expectation bounds for the error in the ensemble approximation to the infinite-dimensional posterior. We consider the errors

\displaystyle\widehat{\varepsilon}_{m}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|m_{\text{post}}-P^{*}\widehat{m}_{\text{post}}\|_{\mathcal{H}},

(66)

\displaystyle\hat{\varepsilon}_{C}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\mathcal{C}_{\text{post}}-P^{*}\hat{C}_{\text{post}}P\|_{op}.

(67)

Controlling these errors will amount to controlling the error between the ensemble approximations and the finite-dimensional approximations using the theory from
[
2
]
and then applying our results from Section
3
. Since the dimension of the discretized covariance matrices increases as the mesh is refined, we will require bounds that do not have an explicit dependence on the dimension. To do so, we define the
effective dimension
of a mapping
C_{0}:\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
to be

\displaystyle r_{M}(C_{0}):=\frac{\text{Tr}(C_{0})}{\|C_{0}\|_{op}},

(68)

where
\|\cdot\|_{op}
is the operator norm from
\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}.
When the eigenvalues of
C_{0}
decay rapidly, the effective dimension
r_{M}(C_{0})
is a better measure of dimension than the nominal state dimension
n

[
59
]
. We will show in Subsection
4.3
that for the finite element discretization, the effective dimension of the discretized operator is controlled by the effective dimension of the continuum operator, defined to be
r(\mathcal{C}_{0})=\frac{\text{Tr}(\mathcal{C}_{0})}{\|\mathcal{C}_{0}\|_{op}}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{op}
denotes the operator norm from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{H}
. Since the prior covariance operator must be chosen to be bounded and trace-class, this quantity is necessarily finite. As such, the error bounds that we now derive for the ensemble approximation will not degenerate as the mesh is refined, despite the state dimension increasing arbitrarily.

Theorem 20
(Mean Ensemble Approximation Error).

Let
\widehat{\varepsilon}_{m}
be the expected error between the infinite-dimensional posterior mean and the ensemble posterior mean with an ensemble of size
J
, as defined in (
66
). Assume that the finite-dimensional approximations satisfy Assumption
8
, that for
n
sufficiently large
r_{M}(C_{0})\leq cr(\mathcal{C}_{0})
for some constant
c
independent of
n
, and that
J\geq r_{M}(C_{0})
. Then, it holds that

\displaystyle\widehat{\varepsilon}_{m}\leq r_{1}^{\prime}\psi_{1}(n)+r_{2}^{\prime}\psi_{2}(n)+r^{\prime}_{3}\psi_{3}(n)+r_{4}^{\prime}\frac{1}{\sqrt{J}},

(69)

where the constants
r_{i}^{\prime}
are independent of
n
and
J.

Proof.

By the triangle inequality, we decompose the expected error as

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|m_{\text{post}}(x)-P^{*}\widehat{m}_{\text{post}(x)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}\leq\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|m_{\text{post}}(x)-P^{*}\vec{m}_{\text{post}}(x)\|_{L^{2}}+\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\widehat{m}_{\text{post}}-\vec{m}_{\text{post}}\|_{M}.

The first term on the right-hand side is deterministic and is bounded by Theorem
10
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|m_{\text{post}}(x)-P^{*}\vec{m}_{\text{post}}(x)\|_{L^{2}}=\|m_{\text{post}}(x)-P^{*}\vec{m}_{\text{post}}(x)\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim r_{1}^{\prime}\psi_{1}(n)+r_{2}^{\prime}\psi_{2}(n)+r_{3}^{\prime}\psi_{3}(n).

For the second term on the right-hand side, we recall
[
2
, Theorem 3.3]
, which guarantees the following:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\widehat{m}_{\text{post}}-\vec{m}_{\text{post}}\|_{M}\lesssim c_{n}^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{r_{M}(C_{0})}{J}}+c_{n}^{\prime\prime}\sqrt{\frac{r_{2}(\Gamma)}{J}},

(70)

where

c_{n}^{\prime}=\left(\|C_{0}\|_{op}^{1/2}\vee\|C_{0}\|_{op}^{2}\right)\left(\|F\|_{op}\vee\|F\|_{op}^{4}\right)\left(\|\Gamma^{-1}\|_{op}\vee\|\Gamma^{-1}\|_{op}^{2}\right)\left(1\vee\|y-F\vec{m}_{0}\|_{2}\right)
, and
c_{n}^{\prime\prime}=\|F\|_{op}\|\Gamma^{-1}\|_{op}\|\Gamma\|_{op}^{1/2}\|C_{0}\|_{op}
. The quantity

is the effective dimension of
\Gamma
in the usual Euclidean inner product space. The quantities
c_{n}^{\prime}
and
c_{n}^{\prime\prime}
depend on
n;
however, under Assumption
8
each of the discretized operators appearing in the constants can be made arbitrarily close to their continuum counterparts when
n
is sufficiently large. In particular, we can take
n
to be large enough such that the following two bounds hold:

\displaystyle c_{n}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq c^{\prime}=2\left(\|\mathcal{C}_{0}\|_{op}^{1/2}\vee\|\mathcal{C}_{0}\|_{op}^{2}\right)\left(\|\mathcal{F}\|_{op}\vee\|\mathcal{F}\|_{op}^{4}\right)\left(\|\Gamma^{-1}\|_{op}\vee\|\Gamma^{-1}\|_{op}^{2}\right)\left(1\vee\|y-\mathcal{F}m_{0}\|_{2}\right),

\displaystyle c_{n}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\leq c^{\prime\prime}=2\|\mathcal{F}\|_{op}\|\Gamma^{-1}\|_{op}\|\Gamma\|_{op}^{1/2}\|\mathcal{C}_{0}\|_{op}.

From these bounds combined with our assumption that
r_{M}(C_{0})\leq cr(\mathcal{C}_{0})
, we take
r_{4}^{\prime}=\sqrt{c}c^{\prime}\sqrt{r(\mathcal{C}_{0})}+c^{\prime\prime}\sqrt{r(\Gamma)}
, and the result follows.

Theorem 21
(Covariance Ensemble Approximation Error).

Let
\widehat{\varepsilon}_{C}
be the expected error between the infinite-dimensional posterior covariance and the ensemble posterior covariance with an ensemble of size

, as defined in (

). Assume that the finite-dimensional approximations satisfy Assumption

(71)

where the constants

are independent of the discretization dimension

Proof.

The proof proceeds similarly to that of Theorem

. We decompose the error as

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\mathcal{C}_{\text{post}}-P^{*}\widehat{C}_{\text{post}}P\|_{op}\leq\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\mathcal{C}_{\text{post}}-P^{*}C_{\text{post}}P\|_{op}+\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|C_{\text{post}}-\widehat{C}_{\text{post}}\|_{op}.

(72)

The first term on the right-hand side is deterministic and is controlled by Theorem

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\mathcal{C}_{\text{post}}-P^{*}C_{\text{post}}P\|_{op}=\|\mathcal{C}_{\text{post}}-P^{*}C_{\text{post}}P\|_{op}\lesssim r_{1}^{\prime}\psi_{1}(n)+r_{2}^{\prime}\psi_{2}(n).

For the second term, we use
[

, which guarantees that

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\widehat{C}_{\text{post}}-C_{\text{post}}\|_{op}\lesssim c_{n}^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{r_{M}(C_{0})}{J}}+c_{n}^{\prime\prime}\left(\sqrt{\frac{r_{M}(C_{0})}{J}}\vee\sqrt{\frac{r_{2}(\Gamma)}{J}}\right),

where

\displaystyle c_{n}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left(\|C_{0}\|_{op}\vee\|C_{0}\|_{op}^{3}\right)\left(\|F\|_{op}^{2}\vee\|F\|_{op}^{4}\right)\left(\|\Gamma^{-1}\|_{op}\vee\|\Gamma^{-1}\|_{op}^{2}\right),

\displaystyle c_{n}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\left(\|F\|_{op}\vee\|F\|_{op}^{3}\right)\left(\Gamma^{-1}\|\vee\|\Gamma^{-1}\|^{2}\right)\left(\|C_{0}\|_{op}\vee\|\Gamma\|_{op}\right)\left(\|C_{0}\|_{op}\vee\|C_{0}\|^{2}\right).

Again, the quantities

and

depend on

, however under Assumption

is sufficiently large. In particular, we take

to be large enough such that the following two bounds hold:

\displaystyle c_{n}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq c^{\prime}=2\left(\|\mathcal{C}_{0}\|_{op}\vee\|\mathcal{C}_{0}\|_{op}^{3}\right)\left(\|\mathcal{F}\|_{op}^{2}\vee\|\mathcal{F}\|_{op}^{4}\right)\left(\|\Gamma^{-1}\|_{op}\vee\|\Gamma^{-1}\|_{op}^{2}\right),

\displaystyle c_{n}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\leq c^{\prime\prime}=2\left(\|\mathcal{F}\|_{op}\vee\|\mathcal{F}\|_{op}^{3}\right)\left(\Gamma^{-1}\|\vee\|\Gamma^{-1}\|^{2}\right)\left(\|\mathcal{C}_{0}\|_{op}\vee\|\Gamma\|_{op}\right)\left(\|\mathcal{C}_{0}\|_{op}\vee\|\mathcal{C}_{0}\|^{2}\right).

With these bounds and our assumption that
r_{M}(C_{0})\leq cr(\mathcal{C}_{0})
, we take
r_{3}=\sqrt{c}c^{\prime}\sqrt{r(\mathcal{C}_{0})}+c^{\prime\prime}\left(\sqrt{c}\sqrt{r(\mathcal{C}_{0})}\vee r_{2}(\Gamma)\right)
and we are done.

The results in
[
2
]
also prove high probability bounds for the error in the posterior means and covariances, which are stronger than the expectation bounds used here. High probability bounds can be just as easily obtained within our computational framework, but we elect to present the expectation bounds for simplicity of exposition.

4.3
Effective Dimension of Prior
Covariance

The results in the previous subsection relied on the assumption that the effective dimension of the discretized covariance operator in the weighted inner product space is bounded above independently of the discretization level for sufficiently large
n.
We will now show that this assumption does indeed hold for the finite element covariance discretization given in Subsection
2.3.1
. To get an upper bound for
68
, we need an upper bound for
\text{Tr}(C_{0})
and a lower bound for
\|C_{0}\|_{M}
. The following result uses classical eigenvalue approximation results and our operator-norm convergence result to derive such a bound.

Theorem 22
(Effective Dimension Upper Bound).

Let the assumptions of Theorem
13
hold. Then, for
h
sufficiently small, there exists a constant
\tau
independent of
h
such that

\displaystyle r_{M}(C_{0})\leq\frac{\emph{Tr}(\mathcal{C}_{0})}{\|\mathcal{C}_{0}\|_{op}-\tau h^{2}}.

(74)

Consequently, for any constant
c>1
taking
h\leq\sqrt{\tau(1-\frac{1}{c})\|\mathcal{C}_{0}\|_{op}}
guarantees that

\displaystyle r_{M}(C_{0})\leq cr(\mathcal{C}_{0}).

(75)

Proof.

By Theorem
13
we have that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{C}_{0}-P^{*}C_{0}P\|_{op}\leq\tau h^{2},

for some constant
\tau
independent of
h
. Applying the reverse triangle inequality to this inequality gives us that

\displaystyle\|P^{*}C_{0}P\|_{op}\geq\|\mathcal{C}_{0}\|_{op}-\tau h^{2}.

Then, using the fact that
\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
matrix operator norm coincides with the
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
operator norm of any mapping in the weighted inner product space, we get that

\displaystyle\|C_{0}\|_{op}\geq\|\mathcal{C}_{0}\|_{op}-\tau h^{2}.

(76)

To upper bound the trace of
C_{0}
we proceed to bound the eigenvalues of
C_{0}.
Recall that
C_{0}=A^{-\alpha}.
We let
\{\lambda^{(i)}_{h}\}_{i=1}^{n}
denote the eigenvalues of
A
, and
\{\lambda^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
denote the eigenvalues of the continuum differential operator
\mathcal{A}
, both in ascending order. To characterize these eigenvalues, we can use classical finite element eigenvalue estimates. In particular, we refer to
[
53
, Theorem 6.1]
, which proves that the eigenvalues of the finite element approximation overestimate the eigenvalues of the continuum differential operator. That is, for each
i=1,\ldots,n
we have that
\lambda^{(i)}\leq\lambda_{h}^{(i)}
. It then follows that

\displaystyle\text{Tr}(C_{0})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\lambda_{h}^{(i)})^{-\alpha}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\lambda^{(i)})^{-\alpha}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\lambda^{(i)})^{-\alpha}=\text{Tr}(\mathcal{C}_{0}).

(77)

Combining (
76
) and (
77
) gives (
74
), completing the proof.

Remark 23
.

In the graph-based approximation setting, one must work slightly harder to derive such an upper bound on the effective dimension as the eigenvalues of the graph Laplacian are not guaranteed to overestimate the eigenvalues of the Laplace-Beltrami operator. Further, the eigenvalue estimates in Theorem
31
only hold for eigenvalues that satisfy
h_{n}\sqrt{\lambda^{(k)}}\ll 1
, which typically only holds up to some
k<n
. As such, it may be most natural to consider a truncated prior covariance that retains only the portion of the spectrum that provably approximates the continuum operator, as is done in
[
26
]
.

5
Convergence of MAP Estimators Under Mesh Refinement

For many inverse problems of interest, the forward model is not linear. In this section, we will see how our computational framework and analysis can be leveraged to guarantee the convergence of the discretized maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimators to their continuum counterparts in nonlinear Bayesian inverse problems as the mesh is refined. In particular, our framework very naturally fits into the theory developed in
[
4
]
, from which we will show
\Gamma
-convergence of the relevant Onsager-Machlup functions and consequently convergence of the MAP estimators (up to subsequences). Under reasonable conditions on the nonlinear forward model (see Assumption 2.7 in
[
54
]
), the posterior can still be characterized as a change of measure with respect to the prior as in (
4
):

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu_{\text{post}}}{d\mu_{0}}(u)=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|y-\mathcal{F}(u)\|_{\Gamma^{-1}}^{2}\right),

(78)

where
Z=\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-\Phi(u)\right)d\mu_{0}(u).
For a general
\mathcal{F}
, this posterior measure will no longer be Gaussian. As such, it is difficult to fully characterize the posterior measure. One particularly useful point summary of the posterior measure is a maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimator. In infinite dimensions, the notion of a MAP estimator was introduced in
[
21
]
as the maximizer of a small ball probability. The theory of MAP estimation in function spaces has been further refined in
[
36
,
39
,
35
,
4
,
38
]
. For
u\in\mathcal{H},
we let
B^{\delta}(u)\subset\mathcal{H}
be the open ball centered at
u
with radius
\delta.
A
MAP estimator
(or
strong mode
) for
\mu_{\text{post}}
is any point
u^{\text{MAP}}\in\mathcal{H}
satisfying

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\mu_{\text{post}}\left(B^{\delta}(u^{\text{MAP}})\right)}{M_{\delta}}=1,\quad M_{\delta}=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{H}}\mu_{\text{post}}\bigl(B^{\delta}(u)\bigr).

(79)

It is shown in
[
38
]
that the MAP estimators of (
78
) with a Gaussian prior
\mu_{0}=\mathcal{N}(m_{0},\mathcal{C}_{0})
in a separable Hilbert space are precisely characterized by the minimizers of the Onsager-Machlup (OM) functional
I_{\text{post}}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\displaystyle I_{\text{post}}(u)=\begin{cases}\Phi(u)+I_{0}(u),\quad&\text{if }u-m_{0}\in\text{Im}(\mathcal{C}_{0}^{1/2}),\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(80)

where

\displaystyle\Phi(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|y-\mathcal{F}(u)\|_{\Gamma^{-1}}^{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad I_{0}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{C}_{0}^{-1/2}(u-m_{0})\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}.

In more general settings, the minimizers of (
80
) may not coincide with MAP estimators defined in (
79
), and a weaker notion of MAP estimator is required
[
30
]
. The optimization problem of minimizing (
80
) is in general difficult, if not impossible, to solve analytically. The computational framework put forth in Subsection
2.2
provides a tractable finite-dimensional optimization problem that provably approximates the infinite-dimensional problem. The discretized posterior measure in (
11
) gives the following OM functional:

\displaystyle I_{\text{post}}^{(n)}(u)=\begin{cases}\Phi^{(n)}(u)+I_{0}^{(n)}(u),\quad&\text{if }u-m\in\text{Im}((P^{*}C_{0}P)^{1/2}),\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(81)

where

\displaystyle\Phi^{(n)}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|y-F(Pu)\|_{\Gamma^{-1}}^{2},\quad\text{ and }\quad I_{0}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{2}\|(P^{*}C_{0}P)^{\dagger/2}(u-m_{0})\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}.

Here
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a discretized approximation to
\mathcal{F}
and the operator
(P^{*}C_{0}P)^{\dagger/2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\lambda_{n}^{(i)})^{-\alpha/2}P^{*}\psi_{n}^{(i)}\otimes P^{*}\psi_{n}^{(i)}
is the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of
(P^{*}C_{0}P)^{1/2}.
We remark that
u-m\in\text{Im}((P^{*}C_{0}P)^{1/2})
if and only if
u,m\in\mathcal{V}
, so we can equivalently write (
81
) as
I_{\text{post}}^{(n)}:\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},
with

\displaystyle I_{\text{post}}^{(n)}(\vec{u})=\frac{1}{2}\|y-F(\vec{u})\|_{\Gamma^{-1}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|C_{0}^{-1/2}(\vec{u}-\vec{m}_{0})\|_{M}^{2},

(82)

which can be minimized by methods from the breadth of literature on finite-dimensional optimization problems. To apply the results in
[
4
]
, we first review some preliminary definitions and results regarding
\Gamma
-convergence.

Definition 24
.

Let
I,I_{n}:\mathcal{H}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
. We say that
I_{n}

\Gamma
-converges to
I
, or
\Gamma
-
\lim_{n\to\infty}I_{n}=I
, if, for every
u\in\mathcal{H}
, the following two conditions hold:

(a)

(
\Gamma
-lim inf Inequality.)
For every sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
converging to
u
in
\mathcal{H}
,

\displaystyle I(u)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}I_{n}(u_{n});

(83)

(b)

(
\Gamma
-lim sup Inequality.)
There exists a sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
converging to
u
such that

\displaystyle I(u)\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}I_{n}(u_{n}).

(84)

Definition 25
.

A sequence of functionals
\{I_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is equicoercive if, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists a compact subset
K_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
such that, for all
n
,
I_{n}^{-1}([-\infty,t])\subseteq K_{t}.

Definition 26
.

Let
I,I_{n}:\mathcal{H}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
. We say that
I_{n}
converges continuously to
I
if, for every
u\in\mathcal{H}
and every neighborhood
V
of
I(u)
in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, there exist
N\in\mathbb{N}
and a neighborhood
U
of
u
such that
n\geq N
and
u^{\prime}\in U
imply that
I_{n}(u^{\prime})\in V.

Continuous convergence is a stronger notion of convergence than pointwise convergence and
\Gamma
-convergence, but is implied by uniform convergence of
I_{n}
to
I
in the case that
I
is continuous
[
20
]
. The following classical result can be found for instance in
[
9
]
.

Proposition 27
.

Let
I_{n},I:\mathcal{H}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
with
\Gamma
-
\lim_{n\to\infty}I_{n}=I
, and
\{I_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
equicoercive. Then,

\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{H}}I=\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{\mathcal{H}}I_{n},

(85)

and if
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a precompact sequence such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}I_{n}(u_{n})=\min_{\mathcal{H}}I,
then every limit of a convergent subsequence of
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a minimizer of
I.
In particular, if each
I_{n}
has a minimizer
u_{n}
, then any convergent subsequence of these minimizers is a minimizer of
I.

Essentially, this proposition states that
\Gamma
-convergence is the correct notion of convergence of functionals to guarantee that their minimizers also converge. We now use our results from Subsection
3.2
and the results in
[
4
]
to prove convergence of the finite element discretized MAP estimators as the mesh is refined:

Theorem 28
(Convergence of MAP Estimators).

Let the covariance operator
\mathcal{C}_{0}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}

and its discrete approximation
C_{0}:\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
satisfy Assumption
8
(i), and let the orthogonal projection operator
P
satisfy Assumption
8
(iii). Assume additionally that the approximate forward model is such that
\Phi^{(n)}
converges to
\Phi
continuously as
n\to\infty
. Then, the corresponding sequence
\{I_{\emph{post}}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of OM functionals given in (
82
) satisfies that

\displaystyle\Gamma\text{-}\lim_{n\to\infty}I_{\emph{post}}^{(n)}=I_{\emph{post}},

(86)

and the cluster points as
n\to\infty
of the MAP estimators of the posteriors
\mu_{\emph{post}}^{n}
are MAP estimators of the continuum posterior
\mu_{\emph{post}}.

Proof.

Assumptions
8
(i) and (iii) imply that, as
n\to\infty,

P^{*}C_{0}P
converges to
\mathcal{C}_{0}
in operator norm and
P^{*}Pm_{0}
converges to
m_{0}
in
\mathcal{H}
.
Therefore, we can apply Theorem 5.5 in
[
4
]
to conclude that
I_{0}=\Gamma\text{-}\lim_{n\to\infty}I_{0}^{(n)}
and that the sequence
\{I_{0}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is equicoercive. Then, under the assumption that
\Phi^{(n)}\to\Phi
continuously as
n\to\infty
, applying Theorem 6.1 in
[
4
]
gives us that
\Gamma\text{-}\lim_{n\to\infty}I_{\text{post}}^{(n)}=I_{\text{post}},
and the sequence
\{I^{(n)}_{\text{post}}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is also equicoercive since
\Phi^{(n)}\geq 0
in our setting. Thus, by Proposition
27
we conclude that
if
u_{n}
is a minimizer of
I_{\text{post}}^{(n)},

n\geq 1,
then any convergent subsequence of
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
converges to a minimizer of
I_{\text{post}}.

Since the minimizers of the OM functionals
I_{\text{post}}^{(n)}
and
I_{\text{post}}
coincide with the MAP estimators of
\mu_{\text{post}}^{(n)}
and
\mu_{\text{post}}
respectively, we have hence shown that any convergent subsequence of MAP estimators of the discretized posterior converges
to a MAP estimator of the continuum posterior.

We have demonstrated how Assumptions
8
(i) and (iii) can be verified for finite element and graph-based discretizations. For nonlinear forward maps, verifying the continuous convergence of the discretized potential may not be straightforward. Lemma B.9 in
[
4
]
shows that if the approximate forward map is of the form
\mathcal{F}(P^{*}P\cdot),
then continuous corresponding potentials converge continuously. The following example illustrates an important problem where the approximate forward model is not of this form; nonetheless, we will show in Theorem
30
that continuous convergence —as well as Assumptions
8
(i) and (iii)— can still be verified.

Example 29
(Eulerian Data Assimilation for the Navier-Stokes Equations).

Eulerian data assimilation is concerned with learning the initial condition of a dynamical system from pointwise observations of the state at fixed spatial locations. This example briefly overviews Eulerian data assimilation for the Navier-Stokes equations, an important model problem in numerical weather forecasting. We refer to
[
19
,
17
]
for a more detailed discussion.

As described in
[
47
,
58
]
, the Navier-Stokes equations on the two-dimensional torus
\mathcal{D}=\mathbb{T}^{2}=[0,1]^{2}
can be written as an infinite-dimensional dynamical system

\displaystyle\frac{dv}{dt}=\nu\mathsf{A}v+\mathsf{B}(v,v)=f,\quad v(0)=u,

(87)

on the Hilbert space

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\left\{v\in L^{2}_{\text{per}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right):\int_{\mathcal{D}}v\,dx=0,\nabla\cdot v=0\right\}

(88)

equipped with the standard
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
inner product. The Stokes operator
\mathsf{A}
in (
87
) is self-adjoint, positive, densely defined on
\mathcal{H}
, and has a complete set of eigenpairs,
\{(\lambda^{(i)},\psi^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{\infty},
with increasingly sorted eigenvalues. The energy-conserving quadratic nonlinearity
\mathsf{B}(v,v)
arises from projection under the Leray operator
[
57
]
. For
u\in\mathcal{H}
and
f
sufficiently regular
[
47
,
17
]
, there exists a unique solution to (
87
) such that
v\in L^{\infty}\bigl(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{1+s}\bigr)\subset L^{\infty}\bigl(0,T;L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})\bigr).

We wish to determine the initial condition
u
from noisy observations of the velocity field
v
at time
t>0
and fixed spatial locations
x_{1},\ldots x_{K}\in\mathcal{D},
given by

\displaystyle y_{k}=v(x_{k},t)+\eta_{k},\quad k=1,\ldots,K,

(89)

where
\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Gamma).

This Eulerian data assimilation task can be formulated as an inverse problem with nonlinear forward map

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(u)=\left(v(x_{1},t)^{T},\ldots,v(x_{K},t)^{T}\right)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}},

(90)

where
d_{y}=2K.

We consider a Gaussian prior measure
\mu_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{0},\mathcal{C}_{0})
, where
\mathcal{C}_{0}=\mathsf{A}^{-\alpha}
with
\alpha>1
and
m_{0}\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{D})
. Here, powers of
\mathsf{A}
are defined spectrally. The posterior measure can then be characterized as in (
78
).

We discretize the inverse problem on the space spanned by the first
n
eigenfunctions
\{\psi^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of the Stokes operator.
Note that since the eigenfunctions are orthonormal, the weighted inner product described in Subsection
2.2
coincides with the usual Euclidean inner product. The discretized prior covariance operator
C_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is then given by
C_{0}=\emph{diag}\left((\lambda^{(1)})^{-\alpha},\ldots,(\lambda^{(n)})^{-\alpha}\right)
. The discretized forward map is given by a Galerkin approximation to the PDE solution, defined via the projection
P:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
given in (
7
). We denote by
v^{N}
the solution to the (finite-dimensional) ordinary differential equation

\displaystyle\frac{dv^{N}}{dt}+\nu\mathsf{A}v^{N}+P\mathsf{B}(v^{N},v^{N})=Pf,\quad v^{N}(0)=Pu.

(91)

We then define

\displaystyle F(u)=\left(v^{N}(x_{1},t),\ldots,v^{N}(x_{d_{y}},t)\right)^{T}.

(92)

Given these approximations, we have a discretized OM functional exactly of the form (
82
).

For the Eulerian data assimilation problem summarized in Example
29
and further detailed in
[
17
]
,
we can verify the assumptions of Theorem
28
to obtain the following result. The proof can be found in Appendix
D
.

Theorem 30
(MAP Estimation for Eulerian Data Assimilation).

Let
f\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{s})
with
s>0
and consider the Eulerian data assimilation problem summarized in Example
29
.
Then, as
n\to\infty,
the sequence
\{I_{\emph{post}}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of discretized OM functionals satisfies that

\displaystyle\Gamma\text{-}\lim_{n\to\infty}I_{\emph{post}}^{(n)}=I_{\emph{post}},

(93)

and the cluster points of the MAP estimators of the posteriors
\mu_{\emph{post}}^{n}
are MAP estimators of the continuum posterior
\mu_{\emph{post}}.

6
Conclusion and Future Directions

This paper analyzed a computational framework to solve infinite-dimensional Bayesian inverse problems. Working on a weighted inner product space, we have studied finite element and graph-based discretizations in a unified framework, using Matérn-type priors and deconvolution forward models as guiding examples. We have established error guarantees for linear inverse problems, and analyzed ensemble Kalman algorithms and MAP estimators applicable in nonlinear inverse problems. In future work, our analysis may be extended to other types of discretizations, priors, and forward models. The generality of our presentation will facilitate the integration of numerical analysis for PDEs to obtain similar error guarantees for other priors and forward models. Additionally, our general approach to obtain error bounds for discretizations of covariance operators and forward maps will also facilitate the analysis under mesh refinement of other algorithms for nonlinear inverse problems.

Appendix A
Adjoints in Weighted Space

Let
X
and
Y
be two Hilbert spaces. Recall that
[
55
]
, given
A\in B(X,Y),
the adjoint of
A
is the unique map
A^{*}\in B(Y,X)
such that

\displaystyle\langle Ax,y\rangle_{Y}=\langle x,A^{*}y\rangle_{X},\quad\quad\forall x\in X,\forall y\in Y.

(94)

In
\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with the usual Euclidean inner product, the adjoint of a matrix coincides with the matrix transpose. However, for linear maps to and from the weighted inner product space
\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
, the structure of the adjoint specified by (
94
) no longer coincides with the matrix transpose. For the case where
A\in B(\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n},\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n})
, we have that
A^{*}
must satisfy

\displaystyle\vec{u}^{T}A^{T}M\vec{v}=\langle A\vec{u},\vec{v}\rangle_{M}=\langle\vec{u},A^{*}\vec{v}\rangle_{M}=\vec{u}^{T}MA^{*}\vec{v},\quad\quad\forall\vec{u},\vec{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M},

from which it follows that
A^{*}=M^{-1}A^{T}M.
Similarly, for
F\in B(\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}})
,
F^{\natural}
must satisfy

\displaystyle\vec{u}^{T}F^{T}y=\langle F\vec{u},y\rangle=\langle\vec{u},F^{\natural}y\rangle_{M}=\vec{u}^{T}MF^{\natural}y,\quad\quad\forall\vec{u}\in\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n},\,\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}},

from which it follows that
F^{\natural}=M^{-1}F^{T}.

Appendix B
Proofs Subsection
3.2

This appendix contains the proofs of Theorems
13
and
14
in Subsection

. The proofs rely on classical finite element method convergence results from
[

Proof of Theorem
13
.

We let
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
with
\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}\neq 0
. We proceed by induction on
\alpha
. For
\alpha=1
we have that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{A}^{-1}f-P^{*}A^{-1}Pf\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}\leq ch^{2}\|\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\|_{H^{2}(\mathcal{D})}\leq ch^{2}\|\mathcal{A}^{-1}\|_{op}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})},

by
[
10
, Theorem 14.3.3]
and boundedness of the PDE solution operator from
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
to
H^{2}(\mathcal{D})
(see
[
22
]
Chapter 6.3). Dividing both sides by
\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})},
we have that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{A}^{-1}-P^{*}A^{-1}P\|_{op}\leq ch^{2}

for a constant
c
independent of
h
, completing the base case. Now, we assume that for some integer
\alpha\geq 1
, we have that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{A}^{-\alpha}-P^{*}A^{-\alpha}P\|_{op}\leq ch^{2}.

We then consider the quantity
\|\mathcal{A}^{-(\alpha+1)}f-P^{*}A^{-\alpha-1}Pf\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}
. Then, by the triangle inequality we have that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{A}^{-\alpha-1}f-P^{*}(K^{-1}M)^{\alpha+1}Pf\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{A}^{-\alpha}(\mathcal{A}^{-1}-P^{*}A^{-1}P)f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}

\displaystyle+\|(\mathcal{A}^{-\alpha}-P^{*}A^{-\alpha}P)P^{*}A^{-1}Pf\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}.

The first of these terms can be controlled using the boundedness of
\mathcal{A}^{-1}
as in the proof of the base case:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{A}^{-\alpha}(\mathcal{A}^{-1}-P^{*}A^{-1}P)f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}\leq\|\mathcal{A}^{-1}\|_{op}^{\alpha}\|\mathcal{A}^{-1}f-P^{*}A^{-1}Pf\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}\leq c\|\mathcal{A}^{-1}\|_{op}^{\alpha}h^{2}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})},

and the second follows from submultiplicativity and the inductive hypothesis:

\displaystyle\|(\mathcal{A}^{-\alpha}-P^{*}A^{-\alpha}P)P^{*}A^{-1}Pf\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{A}^{-\alpha}-P^{*}A^{-\alpha}P\|_{op}\|P^{*}A^{-1}Pf\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}

\displaystyle\leq c\|\mathcal{A}^{-1}\|_{op}h^{2}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}.

We conclude that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{A}^{-\alpha-1}-P^{*}A^{-\alpha}P\|_{op}\lesssim h^{2},

completing the proof.

Proof of Theorem
14
.

Let
u\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
with
\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}\neq 0
. We denote
v=\mathcal{G}u
and
v_{h}=GPu.
We then have that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{F}u-FPu\|_{2}\leq\|\mathcal{O}\|_{op}\|v-v_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}\leq\|\mathcal{O}\|_{op}\left(c_{1}h^{2}\|v\|_{H^{2}(\mathcal{D})}+c_{2}\Delta t^{2}\|Pu\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}\right),

where the first inequality uses the fact that
\mathcal{O}
is a bounded linear operator from
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
and the second uses Theorem
[
44
, Theorem 9.6]
, which is a classical finite element error result for the Crank-Nicolson method. Then, since
