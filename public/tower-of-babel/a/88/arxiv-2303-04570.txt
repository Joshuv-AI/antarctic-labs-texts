Braids and linked periodic orbits of disc homeomorphisms Thanks: Supported by NSF of China (No. 12071309)
Xiang LIU

Abstract
. This paper concerns on linked periodic orbits of orientation-preserving homeomorphisms of the 2 2 -disc in the sense of Gambaudo. We interpret the linking of periodic orbits by using their induced braids. Then based on the forcing relation of braids, we present a method for finding periodic orbits which are linked with a given one. In particular, for 3 3 -periodic orbits of pseudo-Anosov braid types, we provide new examples of linked orbits of periods at most 4 4 .

1.
Introduction

The geometric structure of periodic orbits is a main subject in both continuous and discrete dynamical systems. For example, it is well studied for (oriented) links composed of closed orbits of
3
-dimensional flows
[
10
]
. A naive fact is that two closed orbits having non-zero linking number are linked.

For a self-map
f\colon X\to X
, a subset
A\subset X
is
invariant
if
f(A)\subset A
. The simplest invariant sets are orbits. The
orbit
of a point
x\in X
is the set

\mathcal{O}_{f}(x)=\{f^{k}(x)\big|k\in\mathbb{N}\}.

x
is a
periodic point
of
(least) period

n
if its orbit has exactly
n
elements. In particular, a
fixed point
is a
1
-periodic point. The sets of
n
-periodic points and fixed points of
f
are denoted by
\operatorname{P}_{n}(f)
and
\operatorname{Fix}(f)
, respectively.

This paper studies discrete systems in dimension
2
, especially the
linking
of periodic orbits of orientation-preserving homeomorphisms of the disc, in the sense introduced by Jean-Marc Gambaudo in 1990
[
11
]
. This notion in fact arose from the study of the structure of periodic orbits of orientation-preserving disc diffeomorphisms with topological entropy zero
[
12
]
, where the unlinked was called
disjoint
.

Let
M
be a surface and
h\colon M\to M
be a homeomorphism.

Definition 1
.

Two invariant sets
A_{1},A_{2}
of
h
are called
unlinked
if there are disjoint closed discs
D_{1},D_{2}
in
M
, such that
A_{i}\subset\operatorname{int}(D_{i})
, and
h(\partial D_{i})
is isotopic to
\partial D_{i}
in
M-A_{1}\cup A_{2}
, for
i=1,2
.

Otherwise, they are
linked
.

For example, any two fixed points are always unlinked; For a rotation of the disc, any periodic orbit is linked with the center. It is clear that the linking property is invariant under isotopy.

The known results of linked orbits are mainly about a periodic orbit linked with a fixed point, which we called the
simple
case. Gambaudo showed that any periodic orbit of an orientation-preserving
C^{1}
-embedding of
\mathbb{D}
to itself is linked with a fixed point
[
11
]
, while Kolev proved the same result for orientation-preserving homeomorphisms of
\mathbb{R}^{2}

[
26
]
. Then Bonino showed that any periodic orbit of period at least
3
of an orientation-reversing homeomorphism of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is linked with an orbit of period
2

[
4
]
. Boroński recently generalized Gambaudo-Kolev Theorem to the case of bounded orbits linked with fixed points of orientation-preserving homeomorphisms of
\mathbb{R}^{2}

[
5
]
.

The simple case is related to the
linking number problem
asked by John Franks, which asserts that for any orientation-preserving homeomorphism
f
of the plane, any
n
-periodic orbit always has non-zero linking number around a certain fixed point. Bonatti and Kolev confirmed it provided by
f-\mathrm{id}
contracting
[
3
]
, while Guaschi proved it for
n=3,4

[
16
]
. Finally, Le Calvez solved the problem in general
[
27
]
. We note that if the linking number of
p\in\operatorname{P}_{n}(f)
around
q\in\operatorname{Fix}(f)
is non-zero, then the orbit
\mathcal{O}_{f}(p)
is linked with
q
in the sense of Gambaudo.

Here are two natural problems in this subject. Firstly, are there more
non-simple
examples of linked periodic orbits,
i.e.

none
of the two orbits are fixed points? And more fundamentally, what is the mechanism of linking of two orbits?

This paper is motivated by the first one and is inspired by
[
16
]
. Focusing on the simplest case,
i.e.
for orientation-preserving homeomorphisms of
\mathbb{D}
, we reduce the linking of orbits to the linking of closed braids they induced in mapping tori, and solve the first problem based on the algorithm of braid forcing proposed in
[
24
]
.

The phenomenon that a given periodic orbit implies the existence of other orbits is usually called
forcing
by the given one. The closely related notions are the
braid type
and the
forcing relation
of braid types introduced by Boyland and Matsuoka
[
8
,
28
]
. For an orientation-preserving homeomorphism of
\mathbb{D}
, a periodic orbit induces a braid, whose conjugacy class in the braid group is called the braid type. The braid (type) forces its extensions, some of whom correspond to orbits linked with the given periodic orbit.

Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1
.

Given a set
P
of
n
points in
\operatorname{int}(\mathbb{D})
, a cyclic braid
\beta\in B_{n}
, and an integer
m>0
, there is an algorithm to decide a homeomorphism
f\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
satisfying that
f|_{\partial\mathbb{D}}=\mathrm{id}
,
P
as an
n
-periodic orbit of
f
induces the braid
\beta
, and
f
has an
m
-periodic orbit linked with
P
.

Therefore, for orientation-preserving disc homeomorphisms, the problem of finding periodic orbits linked with a given one is solved, at least in principle. In particular, to get new non-simple examples of linked periodic orbits, we consider the ”simplest” pseudo-Anosov braid type.

Proposition 2
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
be an orientation-preserving homeomorphism. If
f
has a
3
-periodic orbit
P
whose braid type is
[\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1}]
, then there are
m
-periodic orbits of
f
linked with
P
for
m\leq 4
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we discuss the relationships between periodic orbits, braids, and mapping classes. Then we give a method for finding linked periodic orbits of disc homeomorphisms based on the forcing relation of braids in Section 3. Next in Section 4 we present examples of linked periodic orbits for a pseudo-Anosov
3
-braid. Section 5 devotes further topics.

2.
Periodic orbits, braids, and mapping classes

In this section, we review the relationship between periodic orbits and braids to reduce the linked orbits problem, as well as the relationship between braids and mapping classes. We also recall the definition and computation of linking number of two closed braids, which is a part of Theorem
1
.

Let
\mathbb{D}
be the unit closed disc in the plane, and denote the group of orientation-preserving homeomorphisms of
\mathbb{D}
by
\operatorname{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{D})
. By the Alexander trick, any map
f\in\operatorname{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{D})
is isotopic to the identity of
\mathbb{D}
. We fix an isotopy
f_{t}\colon\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{D}}\sim f
.

For
n\geq 2
and an
n
-periodic orbit
P
of
f
, the subset

\beta(P,f_{t})=\{(f_{t}(P),t)\big|t\in I\}

of
\mathbb{D}\times I
is a (geometric)
braid
of
n
strands. We usually omit the isotopy
f_{t}
in braid notations. The isotopy classes of
n
-braids form the Artin braid group
B_{n}
, which has the standard presentation

\big<\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{n-1}\big|\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\sigma_{j}\sigma_{i}\;\mbox{for}\;\big|i-j\big|\geq 2,\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1}\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{i+1}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1}\;\mbox{for}\;1\leq i\leq n-2\big>.

There is a canonical group homomorphism
\pi\colon B_{n}\to S_{n}
, which maps
\sigma_{i}
to the transition
(i,i+1)
for
i=1,\cdots,n-1
. The braid induced by a periodic orbit is called the
braid
of the orbit, and is
cyclic
as
\pi
maps it to an
n
-cycle in
S_{n}
. More on braid groups see
[
2
]
.

Fig 1. The braid
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1}\in B_{3}

In general, an invariant set
X
of
N
points induces an
N
-braid
\beta(X)
, called the
braid
of
X
, and periodic orbits in
X
are bijective correspondence to cycles in
\pi(\beta(X))
. If
P\subset X
is a periodic orbit, then its braid
\beta(P)
is a
sub-braid
of
\beta(X)
, and we also call
\beta(X)
an
extension
of
\beta(P)
.

The mapping torus is useful in fixed point theory and in our subject. For
f\in\operatorname{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{D})
, its
mapping torus
is defined to be the quotient space

\operatorname{M}_{f}=\mathbb{D}\times I\big/(x,1)\sim(f(x),0).

Since
f
is isotopic to
\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{D}}
,
\operatorname{M}_{f}
is a solid torus, and moreover, it is a disc bundle over
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. We denote the corresponding point of
(x,t)
in
\operatorname{M}_{f}
by
[x,t]
. If
f
is of class
C^{1}
, there is a canonical flow
\phi_{t}
defined on
\operatorname{M}_{f}
whose vector field is descended from
\partial_{t}
on
\mathbb{D}\times I
. Then the Poincaré map of
\phi_{t}
at the slice
[\mathbb{D}\times 0]
can be identified with
f\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
, and
(\operatorname{M}_{f},\phi_{t})
is the suspension flow of
f
. We also use the similar construction for orientation-preserving homeomorphisms of punctured discs.

We always suppose that
\operatorname{M}_{f}
is embedded in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
trivially. For an
n
-periodic orbit
P
, the
closure

\hat{\beta}(P)
of the braid
\beta(P)
is obtained by identifying the initial points and end points of each of the strands. It is a simple closed curve contained in
\operatorname{M}_{f}
and hence embedded in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
. We orient it in the direction of increasing
t
, thus
\hat{\beta}(P)
is an oriented knot.

Here is the key observation.

Proposition 3
.

Two periodic orbits
P
and
Q
of
f\in\operatorname{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{D})
are linked if and only if the corresponding closed braids
\hat{\beta}(P)
and
\hat{\beta}(Q)
are linked in
\operatorname{M}_{f}(\subset\mathbb{S}^{3})
.

The only if part is similar to the Corollary in Gambaudo’s original paper
[
11
]
, and the if part comes from Guaschi’s paper
[
16
]
.

We determine whether two such closed braids are linked by using the linking number. Two closed braids are linked if they have non-zero linking number, but the converse is not true. Firstly, we can read the linking number geometrically from the diagram of the braids. In a diagram of two cyclic braids
\beta,\alpha
, for each crossing
C
where the arc of
\beta
crosses under the arc of
\alpha
, we assign the
sign

\varepsilon(C)
of
C
as in Fig 2.

Fig 2. The sign of a crossing

Then the linking number of the two closed braids satisfies

\operatorname{lk}(\hat{\beta},\hat{\alpha})=\sum_{C}\varepsilon(C).

Next we recall the homological definition of linking number of two periodic orbits and a computation method due to John Guaschi
[
16
]
by using representations of braid groups.

For
f\in\operatorname{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{D})
with an
n
-periodic orbit
P
, fix an isotopy
f_{t}\colon\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{D}}\sim f
. Let
\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\}
be the standard basis of

\mathrm{H}_{1}(\operatorname{M}_{f|_{\mathbb{D}-P}})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
where
\gamma_{1}
corresponds to an oriented meridian of the closed braids
\hat{\beta}(P)
, and
\gamma_{2}
corresponds to a longitude on

\partial(\operatorname{M}_{f|_{\mathbb{D}-P}})
oriented by increasing
t
. Then if
Q
is an
m
-periodic orbit of
f
, the loop
\{[Q\times t]\big|t\in I\}
is a simple closed curve in
\operatorname{M}_{f|_{\mathbb{D}-P}}
, which gives a homology class

l\gamma_{1}+m\gamma_{2}\in\mathrm{H}_{1}(\operatorname{M}_{f|_{\mathbb{D}-P}}).

We call
l
the
linking number
\operatorname{L}(Q,P)
of
Q
about
P
(relative to
f_{t}
)
. We note that for
m=1
,
\operatorname{L}(q,P)=\operatorname{L}_{f,p}(q)
the linking number of
P
around a fixed point
q
with
p\in P
, the linking number
\operatorname{L}
is symmetric as

\operatorname{L}(Q,P)=\operatorname{lk}(\hat{\beta}(Q),\hat{\beta}(P))=\operatorname{lk}(\hat{\beta}(P),\hat{\beta}(Q))=\operatorname{L}(P,Q),

and
P,Q
are linked if
\operatorname{L}(Q,P)\neq 0
. See more details in
[
16
]
.

Artin in 1925 showed that
B_{n}
acts faithfully on the free group
F_{n}=\big<x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\big>
from right by

x_{i}\cdot\sigma_{i}=x_{i}x_{i+1}x_{i}^{-1},\quad x_{i+1}\cdot\sigma_{i}=x_{i},\quad x_{j}\cdot\sigma_{i}=x_{j}

for
j\neq i,i+1
.
For a braid
\beta\in B_{n}
, suppose that its closure
\hat{\beta}
is a link with
\mu\geq 1
components
L_{1},\cdots,L_{\mu}
. Then the link group
G=\pi_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{3}-\hat{\beta})
has the presentation

\big<x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\big|R_{1},\cdots,R_{n}\big>,

where
R_{i}=A_{i}x_{\rho(i)}A_{i}^{-1}x_{i}^{-1}
for some
\rho\in S_{n}
consisting of disjoint cycles
\rho_{j}
corresponding to
L_{j}
and
A_{i}\in F_{n}
,
i=1,\cdots,n
.
There is a canonical homomorphism

\Psi\colon\mathbb{Z}F_{n}\to\mathbb{Z}G

of group rings induced by the quotient homomorphism
F_{n}\to G
.

We consider a free Abelian group
H=\big<t_{1}\big>\times\cdots\times\big<t_{\mu}\big>
, where
t_{j}
corresponds to the component
L_{j}
.
Then we define a homomorphism

\Phi\colon\mathbb{Z}G\to\mathbb{Z}H,\quad x_{i}\mapsto t_{j},

whenever
i
is in the cycle
\rho_{j}
. We note that the group ring
\mathbb{Z}H=\mathbb{Z}[t_{1}^{\pm 1},\cdots,t_{\mu}^{\pm 1}]
.
Now let
g_{i}=x_{1}\cdots x_{i}
for
i=1,\cdots,n
. Then
\{g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}\}
freely generates
F_{n}
as
x_{i}=g_{i-1}^{-1}g_{i}
, and
g_{n}\cdot\beta=g_{n}
for any
\beta\in B_{n}
.

The
Magnus representation

R\colon B_{n}\to\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z}H)
of
B_{n}
is defined by

R(\beta)=\Big(\Phi\circ\Psi\Big(\frac{\partial(g_{i}\cdot\beta)}{\partial g_{j}}\Big)\Big)_{1\leq i,j\leq n},

where
\partial\big/\partial g_{j}
are the
Fox’s free derivatives
given by

\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial g_{j}}=\delta_{ij},\quad\frac{\partial(vw)}{\partial g_{j}}=\frac{\partial v}{\partial g_{j}}+v\frac{\partial w}{\partial g_{j}},

for
1\leq i,j\leq n
and words
v,w
in
\big<g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}\big>
.
Then the
link representation

r\colon B_{n}\to\operatorname{GL}(n-1,\mathbb{Z}H)

is given by

R(\beta)=\begin{bmatrix}r(\beta)&*\\
0\cdots 0&1\end{bmatrix}.

Now we state Guaschi’s formula for computing the linking number of two closed braids. Here
\mathbb{Z}H=\mathbb{Z}[t_{1}^{\pm 1},t_{2}^{\pm 1}]
.

Theorem 2

(
[
16
]
, for
\mu=2
)
.

Let
\beta\in B_{n}
be a sub-braid of
\beta\cup\alpha\in B_{n+m}
, whose closure is a link with two components
\hat{\beta},\hat{\alpha}
. Then

\det(r(\beta\cup\alpha)-I)\big|_{t_{2}=1}=(-1)^{m-1}(t_{1}^{l}-1)\det(r(\beta)-I),

where
l=\operatorname{lk}(\hat{\beta},\hat{\alpha})
.

Remark 4
.

These representations of the braid group are also useful to compute the generalized Lefschetz number as

L_{H}(\beta)=1-\operatorname{Tr}(R(\beta))=-\operatorname{Tr}(r(\beta))\in\mathbb{Z}H.

Example 5
.

Let
\beta=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1}\in B_{3}
. Then
\det(r(\beta)-I)=1+t_{1}+t_{1}^{-1}
.

For
m=2
,
\beta
is a sub-braid of
\beta\cup\alpha_{1}=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{3}^{-2}\sigma_{4}^{-1}\in B_{5}
. Then we have
\operatorname{lk}(\hat{\beta},\hat{\alpha}_{1})=-1
, since

\det(r(\beta\cup\alpha_{1})-I)\big|_{t_{2}=1}=-(t_{1}^{-1}-1)(1+t_{1}+t_{1}^{-1}).

Similarly,
\beta
is also a sub-braid of
\beta\cup\alpha_{2}=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{4}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{3}^{-1}\in B_{5}
, and
\operatorname{lk}(\hat{\beta},\hat{\alpha}_{2})=1
.

For
m=3
,
\beta
is a sub-braid of

\beta\cup\gamma_{1}=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{4}\sigma_{5}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{4}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{4}^{-1}\sigma_{5}^{-1}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}^{-1}\sigma_{4}^{-1}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{3}^{-3}\sigma_{4}^{-1}\in B_{6},

For

\beta

\begin{array}[]{rl}\beta\cup\delta_{1}=&\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{4}\sigma_{5}\sigma_{6}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{4}\sigma_{5}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{4}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}\\

At the end of this section, we recall the Nielsen-Thurston classification of mapping classes and how braids connect with them. We begin with pseudo-Anosov maps, which are incisively introduced by William Thurston in 1970s.

A homeomorphism
\phi\colon M\to M
of a surface
M
is called
pseudo-Anosov
if there is a number
\lambda>1
and a pair of transverse measured foliations
(\mathcal{F}^{u},\mu^{u})
and
(\mathcal{F}^{s},\mu^{s})
on
M
,
s.t.

\phi(\mathcal{F}^{u},\mu^{u})=(\mathcal{F}^{u},\lambda\mu^{u}),\quad\phi(\mathcal{F}^{s},\mu^{s})=(\mathcal{F}^{s},\frac{1}{\lambda}\mu^{s}).

We only emphasize that pseudo-Anosov maps have useful dynamical properties to be the candidate for presenting new examples of linked periodic orbits.

Proposition 6
.

Let
\phi\colon M\to M
be pseudo-Anosov. Then

(1) the set of periods of
\phi
is cofinite in
\mathbb{N}
, and

(2) every periodic point of
\phi
is unremovable.

The first property due to Gambaudo and Llibre
[
13
]
guarantees that given a periodic orbit, there are plenty of other periodic orbits. The second one, noted by Asimov and Franks, means that periodic orbits are persistent under isotopy
[
1
]
.

Now we state the Nielsen-Thurston classification theorem
[
32
]
, which is fundamental in geometric topology.

Theorem 3
.

Let
f\colon M\to M
be a homeomorphism of a compact connected surface
M
. Then there is a homeomorphism
\phi
isotopic to
f
, called its Thurston form, satisfying that

•

either
\phi
is of finite type, i.e.
\phi^{m}=\mathrm{id}
for some
m\geq 1
; or

•

\phi
is pseudo-Anosov; or

•

\phi
is reducible, i.e. there is a system of disjoint simple closed curves
\Gamma=\{\gamma_{1},\cdots,\gamma_{k}\}
in
\operatorname{int}(M)
, called reducing curves, s.t.
\Gamma
is
\phi
-invariant.

Moreover, pseudo-Anosov maps are neither of finite type nor reducible.

The isotopy class of
f
is called its
mapping class
, thus we have the Nielsen-Thurston classification for mapping classes. Mapping classes form a group with multiplication induced by the composition of maps, which is called the
mapping class group
of
M
and denoted by
\operatorname{MCG}(M)
. If
M
is oriented, then
\operatorname{MCG}(M)=\pi_{0}(\operatorname{Homeo}_{+}(M))
.

Braids and mapping classes are naturally related as follows. Given punctures
X=\{p_{1},\cdots,p_{n}\}
in
\operatorname{int}(\mathbb{D})
and a braid
\beta\in B_{n}
, sliding
\mathbb{D}
down along
\beta
gives an orientation-preserving homeomorphism
h\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
satisfying
h(X)=X
. We denote the subgroup of such homeomorphisms by
\operatorname{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{D},X)
. If, in addition, we require that
\partial\mathbb{D}
is point-wise fixed, the corresponding mapping class group is denoted by
\operatorname{MCG}(\mathbb{D},X;\partial\mathbb{D})
. Furthermore, we have the following

Proposition 7

(
[
2
]
)
.

There is a canonical isomorphism

\lambda\colon B_{n}\to\operatorname{MCG}(\mathbb{D},X;\partial\mathbb{D})

mapping
\sigma_{i}
to the twist exchanging
p_{i},p_{i+1}
.

The above sliding down the disc along a braid exactly realizes the isomorphism
\lambda
, after perturbing the homeomorphism to fix the boundary point-wise.

Under
\lambda
, the conjugacy class of a braid in
B_{n}
corresponds to the conjugacy class of its image in
\operatorname{MCG}(\mathbb{D},X;\partial\mathbb{D})
, and the former is exactly the braid type we consider in the next section. On the other hand, by the Nielsen-Thurston classification, braids and their conjugacy classes naturally fall into three types induced by
\lambda
: finite type, pseudo-Anosov, and reducible.

3.
Forcing of braids and the algorithm of linked periodic orbits

In this section, we recall the notion of braid types, which focuses on the existence of periodic orbits implied by a given orbit. Then relying on the algorithm of forced extensions due to Jiang and Zheng
[
24
]
, we present our method for finding linked periodic orbits of disc homeomorphisms, which is the proof of Theorem
1
.

The notion of braid types was introduced by Philip Boyland and Takashi Matsuoka in 1980s for characterizing the forcing relation in dimension
2
, in analog to the role of periods in the Sharkovsky ordering for interval self-maps
[
28
,
8
]
.

Definition 8
.

For
f\in\operatorname{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{D})
with an
n
-periodic orbit
P
in
\operatorname{int}(\mathbb{D})
, the
braid type

\operatorname{bt}(P,f)
of
P
is defined to be the conjugacy class of the braid
\beta(P,f_{t})
in
B_{n}
for an isotopy
f_{t}\colon\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{D}}\sim f
.

It is easy to see that
\operatorname{bt}(P,f)
is independent of the choice of the isotopy
f_{t}
. Thus an
n
-braid type is in fact a conjugacy class in the braid group
B_{n}
. It is equivalent to define
n
-braid types to be the conjugacy classes in
\operatorname{MCG}(\mathbb{D},P;\partial\mathbb{D})
for any subset
P\subset\operatorname{int}(\mathbb{D})
of
n
points by the isomorphism
\lambda
, or to be the free loop classes in the unordered configuration space of
n
points in
\mathbb{D}

[
29
]
. Let

\mathrm{BT}_{n}=\{\mbox{braid types of}\;n\mbox{-periodic orbits for all homeomorphisms of}\;\mathbb{D}\},

and let
\mathrm{BT}=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{BT}_{n}
be the set of all braid types.

Definition 9
.

We say
f\in\operatorname{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{D})

exhibits
a braid type
[\beta]\in\mathrm{BT}_{n}
if
f
has an
n
-periodic orbit
P
whose braid type is
[\beta]
. Then we say a braid type
[\beta_{1}]\in\mathrm{BT}

forces

[\beta_{2}]\in\mathrm{BT}
, denoted by
[\beta_{1}]\geq[\beta_{2}]
, if once
f\in\operatorname{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{D})
exhibits
[\beta_{1}]
then
f
also exhibits
[\beta_{2}]
.

We also say the forcing of braids,
i.e.
a braid
\beta_{1}

forces
another braid
\beta_{2}
if
[\beta_{1}]\geq[\beta_{2}]
. It turns out that the forcing relation

is a partial order on
\mathrm{BT}

[
7
]
.

To reveal the extra information about how a forced braid winds around the given one, BoJu Jiang and Hao Zheng considered the forcing problem on extensions
\beta(P\cup Q)
of a given braid
\beta(P)
, in stead of just on
\beta(Q)
. We apply their criterion for forced extensions in the problem on linked periodic orbits, as the most significant step in our algorithm.

Here are their main notions and results. Suppose that
\beta^{\prime}\in B_{n+m}
is an extension of
\beta\in B_{n}
, and let
\phi
be the Thurston form of mapping class
\lambda(\beta^{\prime})
.

Definition 10
.

The extension
\beta^{\prime}
is
collapsible
(resp.
peripheral
) relative to
\beta
if there exists a system of reducing curves of
\phi
,
s.t.
one of them encloses none of (resp. precisely one of or all of) the punctures corresponding to
\beta
.

Proposition 11

(
[
24
]
)
.

An extension
\beta^{\prime}
of a pseudo-Anosov braid
\beta
is collapsible or peripheral relative to
\beta
if and only if
\beta^{\prime}
is reducible.

To give the conditions of forced extensions, Jiang and Zheng constructed a group representation

\zeta_{n,m}\colon B_{n}\to\operatorname{GL}(N,\mathbb{Z}B_{n+m})

for
N=\tbinom{m+n-2}{m}
, which is the dual of another matrix representation
\xi_{n,m}
relating to the Lawrence-Krammer-Bigelow representation
[
35
]
. Then they got the following trace formula by applying Nielsen fixed point theory on the
m
-th symmetric product space of
Y_{n}
, the disc with
n
open discs removed, which is the compactification of
\mathbb{D}-P
. For a nontrivial braid
\beta\in B_{n}
and
m\geq 1
,

L_{\Gamma_{\beta,m}}(\operatorname{Sym}^{m}(\bar{f}_{\beta}))=(-1)^{m}\operatorname{Tr}_{\Gamma_{\beta,m}}\zeta_{n,m}(\beta)-\mbox{collapsible terms},

where
\bar{f}_{\beta}\colon Y_{n}\to Y_{n}
is the blowing-up (introduced by Bowen
[
6
]
) of a representative
f_{\beta}
of
\lambda(\beta)
,
\operatorname{Sym}^{m}(\bar{f}_{\beta})
is the induced map of
\bar{f}_{\beta}
on the symmetric product space, and
\Gamma_{\beta,m}
is the fundamental group of the mapping torus of the deleted symmetric product map
\operatorname{DSym}^{m}(\bar{f}_{\beta})
being identified with a subgroup of
B_{n+m}
. Note that the generalized Lefschetz number
L_{\Gamma_{\beta,m}}(\operatorname{Sym}^{m}(\bar{f}_{\beta}))
takes value in the free Abelian group generated by conjugacy classes in
\Gamma_{\beta,m}
. Then the
(n+m)
-forced extensions of
\beta
are exactly the
non-peripheral
terms in
L_{\Gamma_{\beta,m}}(\operatorname{Sym}^{m}(\bar{f}_{\beta}))
. More details see
[
24
]
, we only state their criterion here.

Theorem 4

(
[
24
]
)
.

Let
\beta^{\prime}\in B_{n+m}
be an extension of
\beta\in B_{n}
. Then
\beta
forces
\beta^{\prime}
if and only if

•

\beta^{\prime}
is neither collapsible nor peripheral relative to
\beta
, and

•

the conjugacy class
[\beta^{\prime}]
has a non-zero coefficient in
\operatorname{Tr}_{B_{n+m}}\zeta_{n,m}(\beta)
.

Combining the theorems of Guaschi and Jiang-Zheng, we can now present our algorithm of linked periodic orbits for disc homeomorphisms.

Proof of Theorem
1
.
The inputs of the algorithm are the following: a set
P\subset\operatorname{int}(\mathbb{D})
of
n
points as the given
n
-periodic orbit; a cyclic
n
-braid
\beta
, which will be the braid of
P
; and an integer
m>0
as the period of orbits linked with

.

1. Slide the disc
\mathbb{D}
down along
\beta
to obtain a map
f\in\operatorname{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{D},P)
, which is a representative of
\lambda(f)
after perturbing as described in Proposition
7
.

2. Apply Jiang-Zheng’s Theorem
4
to determine forced extensions
\beta\cup\alpha\in B_{n+m}
of
\beta
. Some of them may have sub-braids
\alpha
that correspond to periodic orbits linked with
P
.

3. Identify sub-braids
\alpha
satisfying the condition
\operatorname{lk}(\hat{\beta},\hat{\alpha})\neq 0

2
algebraically or by reading diagrams geometrically.

4. Locate the periodic orbits
Q
linked with
P
to be the endpoints of
\alpha
obtained from the last step.

Then we get the output including an orientation-preserving homeomorphism
f\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
with a given
n
-periodic orbit
P
of braid
\beta
, and several
m
-periodic orbits

linked with
P
.
∎

We give some remarks on the algorithm.

If

forced by

Remark 13

In general, it is complicated to compute the trace

by hand. However, for

-extensions of a given

-braid is easier

We should note that in Step 3, the case of linked closed braids of linking number zero can be identified geometrically but not algebraically. For an example, see Remark

5.
Final remarks

We propose an algorithm of linked periodic orbits of orientation-preserving disc homeomorphisms, relying on Jiang-Zheng’s theorem on forced extensions and Guaschi’s formula on the linking number of braids. The former is obtained by analyzing fixed point classes of the induced map on the symmetric product space of the punctured disc, while the later comes from translating the linking information of the strands to coordinates of the fixed point classes. Both of them are essentially refinements of
Nielsen fixed point theory

[
21
]
.

It is natural to seek linked periodic orbits on other surfaces, such as the annulus, torus, and hyperbolic surfaces. However, the braid type theory of surface braids is almost an unknown world. Besides, the forcing relation on the annulus is related to rotation sets
[
8
,
14
]
.

Here are two facts coming from the
template theory
of
3
-dimensional flows. Philip Holmes and R. F. Williams in 1985 pointed out that suspensions of Smale horseshoe have closed orbits which are linked torus knots
[
19
]
. Later, Holmes showed that any two closed orbits of suspensions of the Josephson equation

\dot{\theta}=r,\quad\dot{r}=-\sin\theta+\nu-\delta r+\beta\cos t,\quad(\theta,r)\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\times I.

are linked
[
18
]
. Consequently, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 17
.

(1) Systems containing Smale horseshoe have non-simple linked periodic orbits.

(2) Any two periodic orbits of the Poincaré map of the Josephson equation are linked.

If one fixes an area form on a compact surface, then after perturbing one can choose a monotone symplectomorphism as the representative in each mapping class
[
31
]
. This leads the
symplectic Floer homology
, whose chain complex is generated by non-degenerate fixed points. The structure of the symplectic Floer homology of all the three Nielsen-Thurston types is clear
[
15
,
9
,
30
]
. More generally, we want to know what can the variants of Floer theory do about linked periodic orbits, including the periodic Floer homology
[
20
]
and the braid Floer homology
[
33
]
.
