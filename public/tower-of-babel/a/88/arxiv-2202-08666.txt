Scaling limits of random looptrees and bipartite plane maps with prescribed large faces
Cyril Marzouk

Abstract
We first rephrase and unify known bijections between bipartite plane maps and labelled trees with the formalism of looptrees, which we argue to be both more relevant and technically simpler since the geometry of a looptree is explicitly encoded by the depth-first walk (or Łukasiewicz path) of the tree, as opposed to the height or contour process for the tree. We then construct continuum analogues associated with any càdlàg path with no negative jump and derive several invariance principles. We especially focus on uniformly random looptrees and maps with prescribed face degrees and study their scaling limits in the presence of macroscopic faces, which complements a previous work in the case of no large faces. The limits (along subsequences for maps) form new families of random metric measured spaces related to processes with exchangeable increments with no negative jumps and our results generalise previous works which concerned the Brownian and stable Lévy bridges.

1.
Introduction

A rooted plane map is the embedding of a finite, connected multigraph with a distinguished oriented edge (hereafter the
root edge
) in the two-dimensional sphere and viewed up to orientation-preserving homeomorphisms.
We shall drop both adjectives and simply write “maps” in the rest of this paper.
Maps can be viewed as discrete surfaces and thus random maps offer simple models of random geometry and one can hope that by sampling large maps and letting the edge length tend to zero appropriately, one can obtain in the limit a nontrivial continuum random surface.
Due to embedding of the graph, it makes sense to define the
faces
of the map as the connected components of the complement of the graph on the sphere; the
degree
of a face is the number of edges incident to it, counted with multiplicity, i.e. an edge incident on both sides to the same face contributes twice to its degree.
The simplest model to study is that of quadrangulations with
n
faces, all of which with degree
4
, sampled uniformly at random. For this model Chassaing & Schaeffer
[
CS04
]
identified the growth rate as
n^{1/4}
and after a series of works, Le Gall
[
LG13
]
and Miermont
[
Mie13
]
independently proved that the metric space obtained by endowing the vertex set of such a quadrangulation with
(9/(8n))^{1/4}
times the graph distance converges in distribution towards a random space
(S,D^{\ast})
called the
Brownian sphere
(or
Brownian map
). The latter is almost surely homemorphic to the sphere
[
LGP08
,
Mie08
]
and with Hausdorff dimension
4

[
LG07
]
.

A natural question following this result is that of its universality. Already in
[
LG13
]
, Le Gall proves that for
p=3
or any
p\geqslant 6
even, random maps with
n
faces all of which with degree
p
also converge in distribution towards
(S,D^{\ast})
at the scale
n^{1/4}
up to a model-depending constant. The case of odd
p
’s has been treated more recently by Addario-Berry & Albenque
[
ABA21
]
.
In
[
Mar18b
,
Mar19
]
, a more general model was introduced, in which one chooses for every
n
a deterministic array of
n
positive (even) integers, which can differ from each others as well as vary with
n
, and one samples a plane map uniformly at random with
n
faces whose degrees are given by these numbers.
In
[
Mar19
]
, fully extending
[
Mar18b
]
, convergence to the Brownian sphere for this model is shown under an optimal assumption of “no macroscopic degree”.
The aim of this paper is to study this model in the regime when large faces are allowed.
Let us next discuss our method before stating our main results.
Let us already mention that independently, Blanc-Renaudie
[
BR
]
is currently working on the same model and same results but with completely different methods.

1.1.
A new formulation of the bijections with labelled trees

A key tool to study the behaviour of large random maps
is a tailored constructive bijection with
labelled trees
, i.e. plane trees in which each vertex carries an integer, positive or not, and not necessarily distinct. It started with the celebrated Cori–Vauquelin–Schaeffer bijection that applies to quadrangulations and was then generalised to any plane maps by Bouttier, Di Francesco, & Guitter
[
BDFG04
]
which was successfully used in numerous works such as
[
MM07
,
Mie06
,
LG07
,
Wei07
,
MW08
,
LG13
,
Abr16
,
BM17
,
ABA21
]
. This bijection takes a much simpler form in the case of
bipartite
plane maps (when all faces have even degree), to which we will stick. Nevertheless the labelled trees, called
mobiles
are still more complicated than in the case of quadrangulations. Later, Janson & Stefánsson
[
JS15
]
constructed a bijection between these mobiles and trees, thus providing a bijection between bipartite maps and simple labelled trees. This bijection has been used in the context of maps in e.g.
[
CK15
,
Ric18
]
without the labels, and in
[
Mar18b
,
Mar18a
,
Mar19
]
with the labels.

From left to right: A plane tree, its looptree version as defined in
[
CK14
]
, and the version we consider here obtained by merging each internal vertex of the tree with its right-most offspring.

As opposed to all other listed papers, in
[
Mar19
]
the limit theorems for maps are obtained without a strong control on the associated trees themselves (their maximal height are unknown for example).
This suggests that these trees are in some sense not the right object of interest. Instead, we propose here to code the maps by a variation of the so-called
looptree
version of a tree introduced in
[
CK14
]
, as depicted in Figure
2
. This encoding, described in Lemma
2.2
below, is a simple modification of that from
[
BDFG04
]
and the reader acquainted with the latter can directly look at Figure
4
below. An example of this bijection is provided in Figure
3
.
Let us already mention that it boils down to the CVS bijection in the particular case of quadrangulations, see Figure
5
.
Ultimately, we code these objects by a pair of discrete paths, which is the same as in
[
Mar19
]
but we believe that this viewpoint sheds some lights on the intermediate results of this reference and more generally on the construction of maps, their continuum analogues, and convergences. Furthermore, it allows to extend the results in
[
Mar19
]
without caring about the behaviour of the trees, which is still to be understood, although progress has been made very recently.
The point is that the geometry of the looptree is explicitly coded by the so-called
Łukasiewicz path
, whereas that of the tree is encoded in the
height
or
contour
process, and the latter is much more complicated to study than the former.

Let us next introduce formally our model of random looptrees and maps.

1.2.
Random models with prescribed degrees

Let us consider a triangular array of nonnegative integers:
\mathrm{f}_{n,1}\geqslant\dots\geqslant\mathrm{f}_{n,n+1}\geqslant 0
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\mathrm{f}_{n,i}=n
for every
n\geqslant 1
.
For every subset
A\subset\mathbb{Z}
, let
\mathrm{f}_{n}(A)=\#\{i\leqslant n+1:\mathrm{f}_{n,i}\in A\}
and simply write
\mathrm{f}_{n}(k)=\mathrm{f}_{n}(\{k\})
in the case of a singleton.
In order to lighten the notation, we shall only indicate the dependence in
n
in our objects, although they really depend on the whole sequence of
\mathrm{f}_{n,i}
’s.
Then sample a looptree
LT^{n}
uniformly at random amongst all those whose cycle lengths are the nonzero terms amongst
\mathrm{f}_{n,1},\dots,\mathrm{f}_{n,n+1}
.
We refer the reader to Section
2.1
for a formal definition of a looptree, and to Figure
2
for an example.
We will see that, once suitably randomly labelled, it encodes a bipartite map
M^{n}
chosen uniformly at random amongst all those whose face degrees are the nonzero terms amongst
2\mathrm{f}_{n,1},\dots,2\mathrm{f}_{n,n+1}
.
One can check that both
LT^{n}
and
M^{n}
necessarily have
n
edges and their number of vertices is respectively
\mathrm{f}_{n}(0)
and
\mathrm{f}_{n}(0)+1
.

This model is inspired by similar trees sampled uniformly amongst all plane trees
whose offspring numbers are the
\mathrm{f}_{n,i}
’s,
studied in
[
AB12
,
BM14
]
, also
[
Lei19
]
in case of forests, and more recently in
[
AHUB20b
,
ABHK21
,
ABDMM21
,
BR21
,
BOHT21
]
.
Their scaling limits are expected to be the so-called Inhomogeneous Continuum Random Trees studied especially in
[
AMP04
]
in a framework close to ours, and recently in
[
BR20
]
from another point of view. These trees are related to random processes with exchangeable increments which constitute the analogue of the Łukasiewicz path in the discrete models. The convergence of the random Łukasiewicz paths to such processes is well-understood, but this is not sufficient to control strongly the geometry of the trees and
[
BM14
,
Lei19
]
are restricted to the Brownian Continuum Random Tree, while the other papers are mostly restricted to a weak notion of convergence. What is especially missing is a tightness argument, however we shall prove in Theorem
7.1
the convergence of such trees in the weak sense of so-called subtrees spanned by finitely many independent and uniform random vertices.
The study of the geometry of the associated labelled looptrees is however simpler.

The fundamental quantity which appears in our statements is

\sigma_{n}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\mathrm{f}_{n,i}(\mathrm{f}_{n,i}-1)=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}k(k-1)\mathrm{f}_{n}(k).

Observe that
k-1\leqslant k(k-1)\leqslant 2(k-1)^{2}
for every
k\geqslant 0
, which shows that

\sigma_{n}^{2}
lies between
\mathrm{f}_{n}(0)-1
and
2(\mathrm{f}_{n}(0)-1)^{2}
. We shall therefore assume henceforth that
\sigma_{n}^{2}\to\infty
as otherwise our graphs have a bounded number of vertices.
We shall also assume that there exists a sequence
\boldsymbol{\theta}
of real numbers
\theta_{1}\geqslant\theta_{2}\geqslant\dots\geqslant 0
such that

\sigma_{n}^{-1}\mathrm{f}_{n,i}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to\infty}\enskip\theta_{i}\quad\text{for every}\enskip i\geqslant 1.

(1.1)

By Fatou’s lemma, we have
\sum_{i\geqslant 1}\theta_{i}^{2}\leqslant 1
; let us define
\theta_{0}\in[0,1]
by

\theta_{0}^{2}=1-\sum_{i\geqslant 1}\theta_{i}^{2}.

Let us refer to
[
AHUB20b
, Section 6.2]
for explicit examples of triangular arrays satisfying (
1.1
) in the case
\theta_{0}=0
and
\sum_{i}\theta_{i}=\infty
as well as in the case
\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\ldots>0
.

The limits in the theorems below will be constructed later in this paper. More precisely, Theorem
A
will be specified in Proposition
5.1
and
Theorem
7.4
.
The definition of the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov topology is recalled in Section
2.4
.

Theorem A
.

Suppose that
\sigma_{n}^{2}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

(i)

From every increasing sequence of integers, one can extract a subsequence along which
\sigma_{n}^{-1}LT^{n}
converges in distribution in the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov topology towards a limit with nonzero diameter.

(ii)

Suppose that (
1.1
) holds and that there exists
a\geqslant 0
such that

\sigma_{n}^{-2}\mathrm{f}_{n}(2\mathbb{Z}_{+})\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to\infty}\enskip a\theta_{0}^{2}.

Then the convergence in distribution

\sigma_{n}^{-1}LT^{n}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{(d)}_{n\to\infty}\enskip\mathrm{Loop}(\boldsymbol{\theta},a)

holds in the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov topology, where
\mathrm{Loop}(\boldsymbol{\theta},a)
is a random compact metric measured space whose law only depends on
a
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

Let us next decorate our random looptree
LT^{n}
by the analogue of a branching random walk (or random snake) on trees. Precisely, let
(\xi_{k})_{k\geqslant 0}
be i.i.d. copies of a random variable
\xi
supported by
\mathbb{Z}
, which is centred and has variance
\operatorname{Var}(\xi)>0
. Assume that for every
\ell\geqslant 1
, the event
\{\xi_{1}+\dots+\xi_{\ell}=0\}
has a nonzero probability.
Then conditionally given
LT^{n}
, equip each vertex with a random label, such that the root has label
0
, and the label increments when following any cycle in clockwise order has the law of
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{\ell})
under
\mathbb{P}(\,\cdot\mid\xi_{1}+\dots+\xi_{\ell}=0)
, where
\ell
is the length of the cycle, and finally the collection of these bridges for all cycles are independent.
We encode the labels into a process
Z^{n}=(Z^{n}_{i})_{0\leqslant i\leqslant n}
which gives successively the label of the
i
’th vertex when following the contour of the looptree and we extend it to
[0,n]
by linear interpolation. See the precise definition in Section
2.3
. Observe that
Z^{n}_{0}=Z^{n}_{n}=0
.
Theorem
B
will be specified in Corollary
5.2
and
Theorem
7.9
.

Theorem B
.

Suppose that
\sigma_{n}^{2}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
and that
\mathbb{E}[|\xi|^{4+\varepsilon}]<\infty
for some
\varepsilon>0
.

(i)

From every increasing sequence of integers, one can extract a subsequence along which the processes

(ii)

Suppose that (

) holds and that there exists
\Sigma\geqslant 0
such that

\sigma_{n}^{-2}\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\mathrm{f}_{n}(k)k(k+1)\operatorname{Var}(\xi_{1}\mid\xi_{1}+\dots+\xi_{k}=0)\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to\infty}\enskip\operatorname{Var}(\xi)(1+(\Sigma^{2}-1)\theta_{0}^{2}).

Then the convergence in distribution

\left((\operatorname{Var}(\xi)\sigma_{n})^{-1/2}Z^{n}_{nt}\right)_{t\in[0,1]}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{(d)}_{n\to\infty}\enskip Z^{\boldsymbol{\theta},\Sigma^{2}/3},

and

.

Random maps are associated via the bijection of Lemma

below (which is a mere reformulation of

Theorem C
.

Suppose that
\sigma_{n}^{2}\to\infty
as

(i)

From every increasing sequence of integers, one can extract a subsequence along which

(ii)

Suppose that (

and for every continuous and bounded function

,

\frac{1}{\mathrm{f}_{n}(0)}\sum_{v\in V(M^{n})}F\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\sigma_{n}}}d_{M^{n}}(u_{n},v)\right)\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{(d)}_{n\to\infty}\enskip\int_{0}^{1}F(\mathbf{Z}_{t}-\min\mathbf{Z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(iii)

Still under (
1.1
) all the subsequential limits

D_{\infty}
are
such that if
U
is sampled uniformly at random on
[0,1]
and independently of the rest, then

D_{\infty}(U,\cdot)\enskip\mathop{=}^{(d)}\enskip\mathbf{Z}-\min\mathbf{Z}.

In the particular case
\theta_{1}=0
, when the largest degree is
\mathrm{f}_{n,1}=o(\sigma_{n})
, the space
\mathrm{Loop}(\boldsymbol{\theta},a)
reduces to the Brownian tree, coded by

times the standard Brownian excursion, and the process

is known in the literature as the head of the Brownian snake driven by

times the standard Brownian excursion. In this case, Theorem

is completed in

by showing that the subsequential limits all agree with

and the rescaled maps thus converge without extraction towards the Brownian sphere

This theorem can thus be seen as an extension to this more general model of maps of the work

1.3.
Plan of the paper

In Section
2

We then describe these objects by a pair of discrete paths. Then in Section

2.
A new look at the bijections with labelled (loop)trees

2.1.
The main characters

Maps and looptrees.

Recall the notion of (rooted plane) maps from the introduction, which have a distinguished oriented root edge; we shall often also distinguish a vertex
v_{\star}
in the map, in which case we say that the map is
pointed
.
Then a (rooted plane)
tree
is a map with only one face.
We shall think of such an object as a genealogical tree: the origin of the root edge (hereafter the
root vertex
) is the ancestor of the family, the tip of the root edge is its left-most child, and then the neighbour of a vertex closer to the root is its parent whereas the other ones are its offspring, which are ordered from left to right. More generally, the vertices lying between the root and a given vertex are the ancestors of the latter. Finally an individual with no child is called a leaf, the other ones are called internal vertices.

Another way to view a tree is via its
looptree
version and we use here a variation of the modified looptrees defined in Section 4 of
[
CK14
]
. First, for every internal vertex, instead of linking it to each offspring, only keep the edges to the first and last one and then link two consecutive offspring to each other; if it has only one offspring, then create a double edge. The resulting graph inherits a root edge from that of the tree.
This graph is the looptree defined in
[
CK14
]
and we shall denote such objects by
\overline{LT}\vphantom{LT}
; here we further contract in each cycle the edge that links the parent to its right-most child and this looptree is denoted by
LT
.
Let us refer to Figure
2
for an example.
Note that each cycle of this looptree corresponds to an internal vertex of the tree and the length of the cycle equals the offspring number of the vertex; in particular an individual with only one child induces a loop in the looptree.
These looptrees already appeared in several works in order to study large boundaries of maps
[
CK15
,
Ric18
,
KR20
]
.

For a direct definition, the present looptrees are plane maps which satisfy the property that there is a distinguished “outer face”, to the left of the root edge, and each edge has exactly one side incident to this face. This implies that all other “inner faces” are simple cycles and are edge-disjoint; also no edge is pending inside the outer face.
The looptrees from
[
CK14
]
ironically forbid loops as well as vertices with degree more than
4
.

Contour sequence and labelled looptrees.

Given a looptree with say
n
edges one can orient these edges such that the outer face always lie to their left. In this way the edges are naturally ordered
e_{0},\dots,e_{n-1}
, where
e_{0}
is the root edge, by following the outer face in the direction prescribed by the oriented edges. We extend the sequence
(e_{i})_{i\geqslant 0}
by periodicity. Then for every
i\geqslant 1
, the angular sector in the outer face between
e_{i}
and
e_{i-1}
is called a
corner
and denoted by
c_{i}
; we also set
c_{0}=c_{n}
to be the root corner. It may be useful later to identify
e_{i}
and
c_{i}
in a one-to-one correspondence.
One can define a similar contour order on the
2n
corners of a tree with
n
edges by applying this construction to the looptree obtained by simply doubling each edge of the tree.

Note that each corner is incident to a vertex of the (loop)tree, and may thus be identified with it, but of course the list of vertices thus obtained contains redundancies. We can get rid of them by retaining each vertex either only when it first appears in this list, which corresponds to the so-called
depth-first search order
, or only when its last appears in this list, i.e. when we finish visiting all its corners.
The following result should be clear from a picture and the proof is left as an exercise to the reader.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
T
be a tree with
n
edges and let
LT
denote its associated looptree. Then
LT
has
n
edges as well and furthermore the following two properties hold:

(i)

The corners
(c_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}
of
LT
are in a one-to-one correspondence with the non root vertices of
T
, and the induced order is that of their first visit in the contour sequence of
T
;

(ii)

The vertices of
LT
are in a one-to-one correspondence with the leaves of
T
and the order of appearance of the latter in the contour sequence of
T
corresponds to the order of the vertices of
LT
by their last visit in the contour sequence of
LT
.

Note the shift in the first property: the root corner
c_{n}=c_{0}
is placed at the end of the sequence.
The first property comes from the fact that when following the contour of the tree, after the first visit of a vertex, each other visit corresponds to backtracking in the DFS order, while the second property comes from the fact that we close each loop when visiting the right-most leaf of the subtree of the descendants of the corresponding internal vertex.
This lemma will be used in Section
2.3
below.

We shall equip looptrees with labels that assign to each vertex a real number, not necessarily distinct, see the right of Figure
6
for an example.
To this end, we assign instead labels to the oriented edges (keeping the external face to the left) of the looptree, which satisfy the consistency relation that the sum of all labels along any cycle must equal
0
. Then given any two vertices and any path between them in the looptree, the sum of the labels of the edges on this path traversed in their canonical direction minus the sum of the labels of the edges traversed in opposite direction does not depend on the path. This labelling of the edges therefore defines a unique labelling of the vertices up to a global shift; we fix this shift by requiring that the label of the root is
0
.
We shall encode the labelling by considering the bridges when turning around each cycle. Precisely, for a given cycle with length, say
\ell\geqslant 1
, let
(e_{i_{1}},\dots,e_{i_{\ell}})
denote its oriented edges, with
0\leqslant i_{1}<\dots<i_{\ell}\leqslant n-1
, let then
(\xi_{i_{1}},\dots,\xi_{i_{\ell}})
denote the label increments along these oriented edges.
Note that the consistency relation reads
\xi_{i_{1}}+\dots+\xi_{i_{\ell}}=0
.
The collection of all these bridges associated with each cycle then entirely characterises the labels on the looptree.

We say that a labelling of a looptree is
good labelling
if the label increment
\xi
along each edge oriented in the natural direction lies in
\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant-1}=\{-1,0,1,2,\dots\}
. In other words, each vector
(\xi_{i_{1}},\dots,\xi_{i_{\ell}})
associated with each cycle lies in the set

\mathcal{B}_{\ell}^{\geqslant-1}\coloneqq\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant-1}^{\ell}:x_{1}+x_{2}+\dots+x_{\ell}=0\right\}.

2.2.
The bijection

Let us now review the bijection of
[
BDFG04
]
, in the particular case of bipartite maps, from the point of view of looptrees.
Let
M
denote a bipartite pointed map, i.e. which a distinguished vertex
v_{\star}
in addition to the root edge. Then label all the vertices of
M
by their graph distance to
v_{\star}
and observe that since
M
is bipartite, then the labels along each edge differ by exactly one. In particular the root edge may either be positively or negatively oriented.
We only consider negative maps, when the tip is closer to
v_{\star}
than the root vertex; if the map is positive, we therefore switch the orientation of the root edge.
The next result is really just a reformulation of
[
BDFG04
, Section 2]
.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
BDFG04
]
)
.

There is a one-to-one correspondence between looptrees equipped with a good labelling and pointed negative bipartite plane maps, which furthermore enjoys the following two properties:

(i)

The map and the looptree have the same amount of edges;

(ii)

The cycles of the looptree correspond to the faces of the map, and the length of a cycle is half the degree of the associated face;

(iii)

The vertices of the looptree correspond to the non-distinguished vertices of the map, and their label, minus the smallest label plus one, equals the graph distance in the map of the associated vertex to the distinguished one.

Let us construct the correspondence in both directions. Let us already mention that a similar construction yields a bijection between rooted maps without distinguished vertex and looptrees equipped with a
positive labelling
, in which instead all labels are positive and the root vertex has label
1
. However these objects are more complicated to study, which is why we consider pointed maps.

Right:

From maps to looptrees.

Let us start with the map; inside each face, the cyclic sequence of labels in clockwise order only has increments either
+1
or
-1
. Let us mark each corner if the next one in clockwise order in the face has a smaller label, so half of the corners in each face are marked. Note that the vertex
v_{\star}
has no marked corner.
In
[
BDFG04
]
one then adds a new vertex inside each face and link it to each marked corner of the face; the collection of these edges only and their endpoints is called a
mobile
. Instead, let us join these marked corners inside each face in a cycle, whose length is therefore half the degree of the face. See Figure
4
for an example where both constructions are depicted. Since the mobile is a tree
[
BDFG04
, Section 2.1]
, then what we construct here is a looptree; the mobile is simply a “vertex-dual” graph of the looptree, obtained by linking each vertex in each cycle to an extra vertex inside. Note that, since the map is negative, then the corner of the root vertex in the face to the right of the root edge is marked, then the edge we draw from this corner is chosen as the root of the looptree.

The distance labelling in the map induces a labelling of the looptree. In a sense, in each face, reading the labels in clockwise order, we have merged the chains of positive increments into single nonnegative jumps, therefore the label increment in the looptree along each edge oriented in the canonical direction lies in
\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant-1}
. Furthermore, the labels are all positive and the minimum equals
1
; by shifting them all so the root has label
0
we thus obtain a good labelling of the looptree.

From looptrees to maps.

For the converse construction, take a looptree equipped with a good labelling and shift all labels so the minimum is
1
. Then let us assign to each corner in the outer face the label of the incident vertex and link each corner of the looptree to the next one (in the infinite periodic sequence) with a smaller label, which in fact can only be smaller by exactly
1
. Note that this construction fails for the corners labelled
1
, instead we join them all to an extra vertex labelled
0
in the outer face.
By observing that the corner sequence of the looptree corresponds to the corner sequence defined in the mobile in
[
BDFG04
, Section 2.2]
, the arguments there show that the graph we just produced is a map, pointed at the extra vertex, and the labelling corresponds to the graph distance in this map to this vertex. The edge emanating from the root corner of the looptree is the root edge of the (necessarily negative) map.
Finally the two constructions are inverse of one another by
[
BDFG04
, Section 2.3]
and the claimed properties in Lemma
2.2
follow from each construction respectively.

Remark 2.3
.

When all the faces of the map are quadrangles, then all the cycles of the looptrees have length
2
, so a good labelling can actually only vary by either
-1
,
0
, or
+1
along each oriented edge. This construction in fact reduces exactly to Schaeffer’s bijection in which each edge of the tree has been doubled, see Figure
5
for an example.

Relation with one-type trees.

Let us now mention the relation with the bijection from

The mobile of

is a tree in which two types of vertices alternate: the vertices of the map different from

Then in

the authors modify the construction of the mobile as follow:
if a white vertex is linked to several black vertices, then remove these edges and instead link these black vertices in a chain, starting from the closest one to the root of the mobile and turning around the white vertex in clockwise order; finally link the last black vertex to the white vertex.
This modification of the mobile creates another tree, in which the white vertices turn into leaves and the black vertices into internal vertices. Let us mention that this bijection between mobiles and trees was already constructed by Deutsch

2.3.
Coding paths

Lemma
2.2
reduces the study of a pointed bipartite map to that of a simpler labelled looptree. We explain now how to code these objects by a pair of discrete paths, as illustrated in Figure
7
. In the next section we then adapt this construction with paths that evolve in continuous time in order to build the potential scaling limits of large looptrees and maps.

The (modified) Łukasiewicz path.

Fix a tree
T
with
n
edges and let
LT
be its associated looptree. Recall from Lemma
2.1
that the depth-first search order on the vertices of
T
corresponds to the contour order of the corners of
LT
. Also the cycles of
LT
correspond to the internal vertices of
T
, and the lengths of the former equal the offspring numbers of the latter. Therefore we may construct the so-called
Łukasiewicz path
of the tree directly from its looptree version as follows.
We shall modify the usual definition in such a way that applies similarly to the discrete and continuum models.

Recall the notation
(e_{0},\dots,e_{n-1})
for the sequence of (oriented) edges of the looptree, then extended by periodicity; for every
i\geqslant 0
, let
\ell(e_{i})
denote the length of the cycle adjacent to
e_{i}

if

e_{i}
is the first edge in
(e_{j})_{j\geqslant 0}
adjacent to this cycle, and let
\ell(e_{i})=0
otherwise. Note that
\ell(e_{i})=0
for every
i\geqslant n
.
Then define the Łukasiewicz path
X=(X_{t};t\in\mathbb{R})
associated with
LT

by
X_{t}=0
for every
t<0
, and for every
t\geqslant 0
,

X_{t}=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor t\rfloor}\ell(e_{i})-(t-\lfloor t\rfloor).

In words,
X_{0}=\ell(e_{0})
equals the length of the cycle incident to the root edge; then at every integer time, say
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the path
X
makes a nonnegative jump
\Delta X_{i}=X_{i}-X_{i-}=\ell(e_{i})
, and between integer times it decreases at unit speed. One easily checks that
X_{n}=0
, and further
X_{n+t}=-t
for every
t\geqslant 0
, whereas for every
0\leqslant t<n
, we have
X_{t}>0
(although it may occur that
X_{t-}=0
for some integer
t\in(0,n)
).
See Figure
7
for an example.

Remark 2.4
.

By Lemma
2.1
, the path
X
is a simple variation of the classical depth-first walk of the plane tree
T
associated with
LT
, which takes value
0
at time
0
and
X_{i-1}-1
at time
i\in\{1,\dots,n+1\}
, and it is usually extended to non integer values by adding flat steps, see e.g.
[
Pit06
, Chapter 6]
or
[
LG05
]
. Note that one path converges after suitable scaling if and only if the other one does, and with the same limit so this won’t change anything, except that the construction is now exactly the same in the continuum setting.

The path
X
entirely describes the geometry of the tree and its looptree and one can explicitly recover the graph distance between two vertices of the looptree from
X
.
Indeed let us call
ancestral cycles
of a vertex (and by abuse of notation, of a corner) the cycles which must be traversed by any path to the root. For
j\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, the ancestral cycles of
c_{j}
are in one-to-one correspondence with the integers
i<j
such that
X_{t}>X_{i-}
for every
t\in(i,j)
. Note that necessarily for such an
i
we have
\Delta X_{i}>0
and the precise value of
\Delta X_{i}
is the length of the cycle; also
X_{i}-\inf_{t\in[i,j]}X_{t}
and
\inf_{t\in[i,j]}X_{t}-X_{i-}
equal respectively the length of the left and right part of the cycle when going from the root to
c_{j}
. This allows to recover the graph distance of
c_{j}
to the root by summing the minimum of these two lengths over all ancestral cycles. To recover the graph distance between any to vertices, we add the preceding contribution over all cycles which are ancestral cycles of exactly one of them, whereas the cycle which is ancestral to both (if any) receives a special treatment. We refer to Equation (
3.5
) below which generalises this idea to any càdlàg path with no negative jump.

The label process.

Next, if the looptree is labelled, meaning that every vertex carries a number, then we define the
label process

Z=(Z_{i})_{0\leqslant i\leqslant n}
such that
Z_{i}
is the label of the origin of the edge
e_{i}
for every
0\leqslant i\leqslant n
. Since
e_{n}=e_{0}
, then
Z_{0}=Z_{n}
which shall always be
0
. We further extend
Z
to the whole interval
[0,n]
by linearly interpolating between integer times. See Figure
7
for an example.
Then a labelled looptree is entirely characterised by the pair of paths
(X,Z)
.

It will be useful to describe the label process in terms of the Łukasiewicz path.
Recall that the labels on the vertices are obtained by summing labels on the edges of the looptree on the path from the root which always follows each cycle on its left. Recall also that we assign to each corner the label of its incident vertex.
Recall finally the bridges of label increments along each cycle introduced at the end of Section
2.1
: each integer
i\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
such that
\Delta X_{i}>0
encodes a cycle of the looptree, with length
\ell_{i}=\Delta X_{i}
and we denote by
(\xi^{i}_{1},\dots,\xi^{i}_{\ell_{i}})
the label increments along the edges of the cycle, in the order induced by following the contour of the looptree.
Then the label process is given by the formula: for every
0\leqslant j\leqslant n
,

Z_{j}\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell_{i}}\left(\xi^{i}_{1}+\dots+\xi^{i}_{k}\right)\mathbf{1}_{\{\inf_{t\in[i,j]}X_{\lfloor t\rfloor}-X_{i-}=\ell_{i}-k\}}.

(2.2)

We point out that the terms with
k=0
and
k=\ell_{i}
actually give a null contribution.

Let us finally recall that the labelling is good when each bridge
(\xi^{i}_{1},\dots,\xi^{i}_{\ell_{i}})
belongs to
\mathcal{B}_{\ell_{i}}^{\geqslant-1}
defined in (
2.1
). In this case, the increments of
Z
all lie in
\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant-1}
.
If
(\xi_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}
are i.i.d. copies of a random variable
\xi
with the centred geometric distribution
\sum_{i\geqslant-1}2^{-i-2}\delta_{i}
, then for every
\ell\geqslant 1
, one can check that the sequence
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{\ell})
under the conditional law
\mathbb{P}(\,\cdot\mid\xi_{1}+\dots+\xi_{\ell}=0)
has the uniform distribution on the set
\mathcal{B}_{\ell}^{\geqslant-1}
.
This provides a way to sample conditionally given
X
a good labelling of the looptree uniformly at random.

The vertex-counting process.

Consider a looptree with
n
edges and its Łukasiewicz path
X^{n}
.
Recall that following the contour of the looptree induces an order on the external corners, say
c^{n}_{0},\dots,c^{n}_{n}
, where
c^{n}_{n}=c^{n}_{0}
. If we denote by
v^{n}_{i}
the vertex incident to the corner
c^{n}_{i}
, then
(v^{n}_{0},\dots,v^{n}_{n})
lists the vertices of looptree
with redundancies
since some vertices are visited more than once.
For every
0\leqslant t\leqslant n
, let us denote by
\Lambda^{n}(t)
the number of vertices amongst
v^{n}_{0},\dots,v^{n}_{\lfloor t\rfloor}
which have all their external corners in the list
(c^{n}_{0},\dots,c^{n}_{\lfloor t\rfloor})
and note that it equals
\#\{0\leqslant i\leqslant\lfloor t\rfloor:\Delta X^{n}_{i}=0\}
, the number of null jumps up to time
\lfloor t\rfloor
; then let
V_{n}=\Lambda^{n}(n)
denote the number of vertices of the looptree.
Conversely for
0\leqslant t\leqslant V_{n}
, let
\lambda^{n}(t)\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
be the index such that the vertex
v^{n}_{\lambda(\lceil t\rceil)}
is the
\lceil t\rceil
’th vertex fully visited in the contour of the looptree. Note that the sequence
(v^{n}_{\lambda^{n}(0)},\dots,v^{n}_{\lambda^{n}(V_{n}-1)})
now lists the vertices
without redundancies
. In our random model, the following convergence

\left(V_{n}^{-1}\lx@nobreakspace\Lambda^{n}(nt),n^{-1}\lambda^{n}(V_{n}t)\right)_{t\in[0,1]}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to\infty}\enskip\left(t,t\right)_{t\in[0,1]}

(2.3)

will hold for the uniform topology.
Observe that since
\Lambda^{n}
and
\lambda^{n}
are inverse of one another and nondecreasing, then
n^{-1}\lx@nobreakspace\lambda^{n}(V_{n}\cdot)
converges to the identity if and only if
V_{n}^{-1}\lx@nobreakspace\Lambda^{n}(n\cdot)
does. Under this assumption, sampling uniformly at random in the looptree a corner (which can be done simply by sampling an instant in
[0,n]
) or a vertex is asymptotically equivalent.

From labels to large plane maps

Let us end this section with invariance principles for bipartite pointed plane maps.
Precisely, we deduce partial results on the geometry of the maps from the convergence of the associated labels; this is very classical and we only recalled these results for the reader’s convenience.
Lemma

below on the

profile

Let
\mathsf{M}^{n}

(\mathsf{X}^{n},\mathsf{Z}^{n})

In this coding, the vertices of
\mathsf{M}^{n}

Then with the preceding notation, the sequence
(v^{n}_{\lambda^{n}(0)},\dots,v^{n}_{\lambda^{n}(\mathsf{V}_{n}-1)})

\mathsf{M}_{n}

The key property of the bijection from Lemma

that we shall need is that the labels on the looptree correspond to the graph distance to

d_{\mathsf{M}^{n}}(v^{n}_{i},v^{n}_{\star})=\mathsf{Z}^{n}_{i}-\min\mathsf{Z}^{n}+1.

(2.4)

The next result easily follows from the definitions and (

Recall from the introduction the notation

Lemma 2.5
.

Let

\left(a_{n}^{-1}\mathsf{Z}^{n}_{nt},n^{-1}\lambda^{n}(\mathsf{V}_{n}t)\right)_{t\in[0,1]}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to\infty}\enskip\left(\mathsf{Z}_{t},t\right)_{t\in[0,1]}

\left(a_{n}^{-1}d_{\mathrm{gr}}(v^{n}_{\star},v^{n}_{\lambda^{n}(\mathsf{V}_{n}t)})\right)_{t\in[0,1]}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to\infty}\enskip\left(\mathsf{Z}_{t}-\min\mathsf{Z}\right)_{t\in[0,1]},

We finally consider the map as a metric measured space by endowing the underlying graph with its graph distance and uniform probability measure. Lemma
2.6
below on the convergence of this metric space
after extraction of a subsequence
finds its root in the work of Le Gall
[

[

The major issue is then to prove that all the subsequential limits agree. In the case of random quadrangulations and the Brownian sphere, this was eventually solved simultaneously in
[
LG13

Mie13
]
and their arguments allow to extend this uniqueness to other discrete models that converge to the same limit and is a key input in the papers cited above. In the case of stable maps, this is under active investigation

In words, two compact metric spaces equipped with a Borel probability measure, say
(X,d_{X},p_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y},p_{Y})
, are close to each other in the
Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov topology
if one can find a subset of each which carries most of the mass and which are close to be isometric. Formally, a
correspondence
between
X
and
Y
is a subset
R\subset X\times Y
such that for every
x\in X
, there exists
y\in Y
such that
(x,y)\in R
and vice-versa. The
distortion
of
R
is defined as

\mathrm{dis}(R)=\sup\left\{\left|d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})\right|;(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in R\right\}.

Then the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov distance between these spaces is the infimum of all the values
\varepsilon>0
such that there exists a coupling
\nu
between
p_{X}
and
p_{Y}
and a compact correspondence
R
between
X
and
Y
such that

\nu(R)\geqslant 1-\varepsilon\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{dis}(R)\leqslant 2\varepsilon.

This is only a pseudo-distance, but after taking the quotient by measure-preserving isometries, one gets a genuine distance which is separable and complete, see Miermont
[
Mie09
, Proposition 6]
.

We keep the preceding notation:
\mathsf{M}^{n}
is a plane map with
n

\mathsf{V}_{n}+1

denotes a distinguished vertex. Let

in
\mathsf{M}^{n}

The distances to

(2.6)

and then extend

Recall the assumption (

Here and below, if

Lemma 2.6
.

Under the assumption (

\left(a_{n}^{-1}d_{n}(\mathsf{V}_{n}s,\mathsf{V}_{n}t)\right)_{s,t\in[0,1]}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to\infty}\enskip\left(d_{\infty}(s,t)\right)_{s,t\in[0,1]},

a_{n}^{-1}\mathsf{M}^{n}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to\infty}\enskip{}[0,1]/\{d_{\infty}=0\}

Proof.

Let us modify

and then extend it similarly to the whole square

From the convergence of

2.5
),
it is equivalent to replace
d_{n}(\mathsf{V}_{n}\cdot,\mathsf{V}_{n}\cdot)
in the statement by
\widetilde{d}_{n}(n\cdot,n\cdot)
.

For a continuous function
g:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
, let us set for every
0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant 1
,

D_{g}(s,t)=D_{g}(t,s)=g(s)+g(t)-2\max\left\{\min_{r\in[s,t]}g(r);\min_{r\in[0,s]\cup[t,1]}g(r)\right\}.

For
0\leqslant i<j\leqslant n
, let
[i,j]
denote the set of integers from
i
to
j
, and let
[j,i]
denote
[j,n]\cup[0,i]
. Recall that we construct our map from a labelled looptree, using a Schaeffer-type bijection; following the chain of edges drawn starting from two points of the forest to the next one with smaller label until they merge, one obtains the following upper bound on distances: for every
0\leqslant i,j\leqslant n
,

d_{\mathsf{M}^{n}}(v^{n}_{i},v^{n}_{j})\leqslant D_{L^{n}}(i,j)+2.

(2.7)

See Le Gall
[
LG07
, Lemma 3.1]
for a detailed proof in a different context.

By continuity, if
a_{n}^{-1}\mathsf{Z}^{n}
converges uniformly towards
\mathsf{Z}
, then
a_{n}^{-1}D_{\mathsf{Z}^{n}}
converges towards
D_{\mathsf{Z}}
. From the previous bound and (
2.5
), one can then extract a subsequence along which we have

a_{n}^{-1}\left(\mathsf{Z}^{n}_{nt},D_{\mathsf{Z}^{n}}(s,t),\widetilde{d}_{n}(ns,nt),d_{n}(\mathsf{V}_{n}s,\mathsf{V}_{n}t)\right)_{s,t\in[0,1]}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to\infty}\enskip(\mathsf{Z}_{t},D_{\mathsf{Z}}(s,t),d_{\infty}(s,t),d_{\infty}(s,t))_{s,t\in[0,1]},

(2.8)

where
d_{\infty}
is a continuous pseudo-distance which depends a priori on the subsequence and, by (
2.7
), satisfies
d_{\infty}\leqslant D_{\mathsf{Z}}
, see
[
LG07
, Proposition 3.2]
for a detailed proof in a similar context.

Finally, let
\mathsf{M}_{\infty}
be the quotient
[0,1]/\{d_{\infty}=0\}
equipped with the metric induced by
d_{\infty}
, which we still denote by
d_{\infty}
. We let
\Pi_{\infty}
be the canonical projection from
[0,1]
to
\mathsf{M}_{\infty}
which is continuous (since
d_{\infty}
is) so
(\mathsf{M}_{\infty},d_{\infty})
is a compact metric space, which finally we endow with the Borel probability measure
p_{\infty}
given by the push-forward by
\Pi_{\infty}
of the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
.
The set

R_{n}=\left\{\left(v^{n}_{\lambda^{n}(\mathsf{V}_{n}t)},\Pi_{\infty}(t)\right);t\in[0,1]\right\}.

is a correspondence between
\mathsf{M}^{n}\setminus\{v^{n}_{\star}\}
and
\mathsf{M}_{\infty}
. Let further
\nu
be the coupling between
p_{\mathsf{M}^{n}}
and
p_{\infty}
given by

\int_{(M_{n}\setminus\{v^{n}_{\star}\})\times\mathsf{M}_{\infty}}f(x,z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(x,z)=\int_{0}^{1}f\left(v^{n}_{\lambda^{n}(\mathsf{V}_{n}t)},\Pi_{\infty}(t)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

for every test function
f
. Then
\nu
is supported by
R_{n}
by construction. Finally, the distortion of
R_{n}
is given by

\sup_{s,t\in[0,1]}\left|a_{n}^{-1}d_{n}\big(\mathsf{V}_{n}s,\mathsf{V}_{n}t\big)-d_{\infty}(s,t)\right|,

which tends to
0
whenever the convergence (
2.8
) holds, which concludes the proof of the convergence of the maps.
∎

3.
Continuum labelled looptrees

In this section, we replace our Łukasiewicz paths by any càdlàg path with no negative jump and extend the construction of a looptree from this path in Section
3.3
. We then construct random Gaussian labels on such a deterministic looptree in Section
3.4
.
Let us first introduce some notation that we will use in the rest of this paper.

3.1.
Path decomposition

Let
\mathsf{X}
denote a càdlàg path in the space
\mathbb{D}([0,1],\mathbb{R})
, with
\mathsf{X}_{0}\geqslant 0
and with no negative jump:
\Delta\mathsf{X}_{s}\geqslant 0
for all
s\in[0,1]
. Although it will not be needed for this conversation, we note that all the paths we shall consider also satisfy
X_{1}=0
. By convention, we shall extend it to
[-1,1]
by setting
\mathsf{X}_{t}=0
for every
t\in[-1,0)
. We stress that
\mathsf{X}
may have a positive jump at
0
.
Define a partial order
\preceq
on
[0,1]
as follows. For every
s,t\in[0,1]
, set

s\prec t\qquad\text{when both}\qquad s<t\quad\text{and}\quad\mathsf{X}_{s-}\leqslant\inf_{[s,t]}\mathsf{X}.

We then set
s\preceq t
if
s\prec t
or
s=t
.
Then for every pair
s,t\in[0,1]
, set

\mathsf{R}^{t}_{s}\coloneqq\inf_{r\in[s,t]}\mathsf{X}_{r}-\mathsf{X}_{s-}\qquad\text{if}\enskip s\prec t,

(3.1)

and let
\mathsf{R}^{t}_{s}=0
otherwise. Note that
\mathsf{X}
must jump at time
s
for
\mathsf{R}^{t}_{s}
to be nonzero.
In analogy to the discrete setting, when
\mathsf{X}
is the Łukasiewicz path associated with a plane tree, we interpret
s=\sup\{r\in[0,1]:r\prec t\}
as the parent of
t
, then
\Delta\mathsf{X}_{s}
as the offspring number of
s
, and finally
\mathsf{R}^{t}_{s}
is the number of siblings of
t
that lie to its right; in the looptree, this corresponds to the right-length of the loop. Finally, for any
s,t\in[0,1]
, we let
s\wedge t\coloneq\sup\{r\in[0,1]:r\prec s\text{ and }r\prec t\}
denote their last common ancestor.

Fix
t\in[0,1]
and consider the dual path
\mathsf{X}^{t}_{s}=\mathsf{X}_{t-}-\mathsf{X}_{(t-s)-}
and its running supremum
\overline{\mathsf{X}}\vphantom{X}^{t}_{s}=\sup_{r\in[0,s]}\mathsf{X}^{t}_{r}
for every
s\in[0,t]
. Then
\mathsf{R}^{t}_{t-s}
is equal to the size of the (possibly null) jump
\Delta\overline{\mathsf{X}}\vphantom{X}^{t}_{s}
, and we may decompose this supremum path as the sum of its jump part and its continuous part, namely for
0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant 1
,

\mathsf{J}^{t}_{s}=\sum_{t-s\preceq r\prec t}\mathsf{R}^{t}_{r}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathsf{C}^{t}_{s}=\overline{\mathsf{X}}\vphantom{X}^{t}_{s}-\mathsf{J}^{t}_{s}=\mathrm{Leb}\big(\big\{\overline{\mathsf{X}}\vphantom{X}^{t}_{r};r\in[0,s]\big\}\big),

(3.2)

where here and below, a sum over an empty set is null, and
\mathrm{Leb}
denotes the Lebesgue measure. Finally set

\mathsf{J}_{t}=\mathsf{J}^{t}_{t}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathsf{C}_{t}=\mathsf{C}^{t}_{t},\qquad\text{so}\qquad\mathsf{J}_{t}+\mathsf{C}_{t}=\mathsf{X}_{t-}.

(3.3)

We stress that even if
\mathsf{X}
is continuous as a process on
[0,1]
, it may have a jump
\Delta\mathsf{X}_{0}=\mathsf{X}_{0}
at the origin, which then contributes to
\mathsf{J}
.
The path
\mathsf{C}
, thus given for every
t\in[0,1]
by

\mathsf{C}_{t}=\mathrm{Leb}(\inf_{r\in[s,t]}\mathsf{X}_{r};s\in[0,t]\})
, is continuous, and it may be null. In the latter case, constructions and invariance principles are simpler, so we shall emphasise the following “pure jump” assumption:

For every
t\in[0,1]
, the function
\overline{\mathsf{X}}\vphantom{X}^{t}
is pure jump, i.e.
\mathsf{C}_{t}=0
.

(PJ)

For example, in Section
8
we consider Lévy processes, for which (
PJ
) is equivalent to having no Gaussian component in their Lévy–Khintchine decomposition.

3.2.
Continuum trees

Let
g
denote a continuous function on
[0,1]
. It is classical, see e.g.
[
LG05
]
for a detailed discussion, that it encodes a continuum real tree as follows. For every
0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant 1
, set

d_{g}(s,t)=g_{s}+g_{t}-2\min_{r\in[s,t]}g_{r},

(3.4)

and let
d_{g}(t,s)=d_{g}(s,t)
, then
d_{g}
defines a continuous pseudo-distance.
The quotient space
T_{g}=[0,1]/\{d_{g}=0\}
is called the tree coded by
g
.
It is a real tree in the topological sense, it is compact metric space when equipped with the canonical projection of
d_{g}
(which we shall still denote by
d_{g}
), and finally the push-forward
p_{g}
of the Lebesgue measure defines a uniform probability measure on
T_{g}
.
This coding of a real tree from a continuous function is analogous to that of a discrete plane tree (actually a forest) from its associated contour function.

Given the path
\mathsf{X}
, one can then construct the tree
T_{\mathsf{C}}
from the continuous function
\mathsf{C}
in (
3.3
). We stress that it may not be the continuum tree for which
\mathsf{X}
would play the role of the Łukasiewicz path. Indeed, under the pure jump assumption (
PJ
) it is reduced to a single point, whereas a nontrivial tree might still be constructed from
\mathsf{X}
, see the example of Lévy trees
[
DLG02
]
recalled in Section
8
. However for Lévy processes which do not satisfy (
PJ
), the process
\mathsf{C}
corresponds up to a scaling factor to the so-called height process, so
T_{\mathsf{C}}
is indeed a multiple factor of the associated Lévy tree (or rather forest).

3.3.
Continuum looptrees

We aim at defining a looptree
\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})
from the path
\mathsf{X}
.
Recall the partial order
\preceq
on
[0,1]
previously defined from
\mathsf{X}
and the notation
s\wedge t\coloneq\sup\{r\in[0,1]:r\preceq s\text{ and }r\preceq t\}
for the last common ancestor of
s,t\in[0,1]
. Let
t\in[0,1]
be a jump time of
\mathsf{X}
, we associate with it a cycle
[0,\Delta\mathsf{X}_{t}]
with
\Delta\mathsf{X}_{t}
identified with
0
, equipped with the metric
\delta_{t}(a,b)=\min\{|a-b|,\Delta\mathsf{X}_{t}-|a-b|\}
for all
a,b\in[0,\Delta\mathsf{X}_{t}]
. For definiteness, if
\Delta\mathsf{X}_{t}=0
, then we set
\delta_{t}(0,0)=0
. For every
s,t\in[0,1]
, we let

d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}^{0}(s,t)\coloneqq\delta_{s\wedge t}(\mathsf{R}^{s}_{s\wedge t},\mathsf{R}^{t}_{s\wedge t})+\sum_{s\wedge t\prec r\prec s}\delta_{r}(0,\mathsf{R}^{s}_{r})+\sum_{s\wedge t\prec r\prec t}\delta_{r}(0,\mathsf{R}^{t}_{r}).

(3.5)

When
s\prec t
, the first two terms on the right are zero.
This function was defined in
[
CK14
]
with the property (
PJ
) in mind but it is not satisfactory otherwise; in the extreme case when
\mathsf{X}
is continuous, it is constant equal to
0
since only jump times
can contribute to (
3.5
). Let us therefore modify it using the continuous function
\mathsf{C}
from (
3.3
):
define the general looptree distance with parameter
a\geqslant 0
between any pair
s,t\in[0,1]
by

d^{a}_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}(s,t)\coloneqq d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}^{0}(s,t)+a\cdot d_{\mathsf{C}}(s,t),

(3.6)

and let simply
d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}=d^{1}_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}
.
Intuitively
\mathsf{X}
plays the role of the Łukasiewicz path, which counts for every vertex of the looptree the length of the path to the root by following each loop to their right; typically for random models, a geodesic from a vertex to the root follows a loop to its right about half of the time and follows the left part otherwise, so the parameter
a=1/2
shall play a prominent role. However in a “finite variance” regime an extra factor comes into play; Theorem
7.4
makes this discussion more precise.

Remark 3.1
.

Recall from Section
2.3
the coding of a discrete looptree
\mathsf{LT}^{n}
with
n
edges by its Łukasiewicz path
\mathsf{X}^{n}
and note that the latter satisfies (
PJ
). Then Formula (
3.5
) recovers the graph distance when
ns
and
nt
are both integers. In fact this formula describes the “cable looptree” obtained by replacing each edge of
\mathsf{LT}^{n}
by a line segment with length
1
.

The following result is given in
[
CK14
, Lemma 2.1 and Proposition 2.2]
under (
PJ
).

Proposition 3.2
.

For any
a\geqslant 0
the function
d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}^{a}
is a continuous pseudo-distance on
[0,1]
. Moreover the following upper bound holds: for every
0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant 1
,

d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}(s,t)\leqslant\mathsf{X}_{s}+\mathsf{X}_{t-}-2\inf_{r\in[s,t]}\mathsf{X}_{r}.

In words, in the looptree distances, one follows the loops between
s
and
t
by taking their shortest side, whereas in the upper bound we always take the right side of each loop.

Proof.

Let us first prove the upper bound for
d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}^{0}
.
First suppose that
s\prec t
and observe that
\delta_{r}(0,\mathsf{R}^{t}_{r})\leqslant\mathsf{R}^{t}_{r}
for any
s\prec r\prec t
, as well as
\delta_{s}(0,\mathsf{R}^{t}_{s})\leqslant\Delta\mathsf{X}_{s}-\mathsf{R}^{t}_{s}
, so the identity (
3.2
) yields

d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}^{0}(s,t)=\delta_{s}(0,\mathsf{R}^{t}_{s})+\sum_{s\prec r\prec t}\delta_{r}(0,\mathsf{R}^{t}_{r})\leqslant\Delta\mathsf{X}_{s}+\mathsf{J}^{t}_{t-s}-2\mathsf{R}^{t}_{s}.

Observe that

\mathsf{J}^{t}_{t-s}\leqslant\overline{\mathsf{X}}\vphantom{X}^{t}_{t-s}=\mathsf{X}_{t-}-\mathsf{X}_{s-}

so the bound follows in this case.
If
s
is not an ancestor of
t
, then
s\wedge t<s
and the minimum of
\mathsf{X}
between
s\wedge t
and
t
is actually achieved in the interval
[s,t]
. Moreover

\delta_{s\wedge t}(\mathsf{R}^{s}_{s\wedge t},\mathsf{R}^{t}_{s\wedge t})\leqslant\mathsf{R}^{s}_{s\wedge t}-\mathsf{R}^{t}_{s\wedge t}
.
We infer that

d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}^{0}(s,t)\leqslant\mathsf{J}^{s}_{s-s\wedge t}+\mathsf{J}^{t}_{t-s\wedge t}-2\mathsf{R}^{t}_{s\wedge t}.

As previously, we have

\mathsf{J}^{s}_{s-s\wedge t}+\mathsf{J}^{t}_{t-s\wedge t}\leqslant\mathsf{X}_{s-}+\mathsf{X}_{t-}-2\mathsf{X}_{(s\wedge t)-}
and the upper bound on
d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}^{0}(s,t)
follows.

Let us turn to
d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}
. In upper bounding
\mathsf{J}^{t}
by
\overline{\mathsf{X}}\vphantom{X}^{t}
, we actually added the continuous part
\mathsf{C}^{t}
. When
s\prec t
, we have
d_{\mathsf{C}}(s,t)=\mathsf{C}^{t}_{t-s}
so

d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}(s,t)\leqslant d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}^{0}(s,t)+\mathsf{C}^{t}_{t-s}\leqslant\mathsf{X}_{s}+\mathsf{X}_{t-}-2\inf_{[s,t]}\mathsf{X}.

Similarly, when
s\wedge t<s
,

d_{\mathsf{C}}(s,t)=\mathsf{C}^{s}_{s}+\mathsf{C}^{t}_{t}-2\inf_{r\in[s,t]}\mathsf{C}^{(r)}_{r}=\mathsf{C}^{s}_{s-s\wedge t}+\mathsf{C}^{t}_{s-s\wedge t},

and so

d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}(s,t)\leqslant\mathsf{J}^{s}_{s-s\wedge t}+\mathsf{J}^{t}_{t-s\wedge t}-2\mathsf{R}^{t}_{s\wedge t}+\mathsf{C}^{s}_{s-s\wedge t}+\mathsf{C}^{t}_{s-s\wedge t}\leqslant\mathsf{X}_{s-}+\mathsf{X}_{t-}-2\inf_{[s,t]}\mathsf{X}.

A similar reasoning shows that
d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}^{a}
satisfies the triangular inequality for any
a\geqslant 0
and is left to the reader. Moreover the upper bound shows that it is finite so it indeed defines a pseudo-distance. It remains to prove that it is continuous.
By the triangle inequality, for every
s,s^{\prime},t,t^{\prime}\in[0,1]
,

|d^{a}_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}(s,s^{\prime})-d^{a}_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}(t,t^{\prime})|\leqslant d^{a}_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}(s,t)+d^{a}_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}(s^{\prime},t^{\prime}),

and the right hand side is arbitrarily small when
s
is close to
t
and
s^{\prime}
close to
t^{\prime}
by the preceding upper bound.
∎

3.4.
Randomly labelled looptrees

Let
g
be a continuous function defined on
[0,1]
and recall from the beginning of Section
3.3
that it encodes a real tree
T_{g}
. One may define a random Gaussian field on this tree, known as the (head of the)
Brownian snake
driven by
g
; formally, given
g
, there exists a centred Gaussian process
Z^{g}=(Z^{g}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
with covariance function

\mathbb{E}[Z^{g}_{s}Z^{g}_{t}]=\min_{r\in[s,t]}g_{r},\qquad s,t\in[0,1].

Equivalently,
\mathbb{E}[(Z^{g}_{s}-Z^{g}_{t})^{2}]=d_{g}(s,t)
, where the latter is defined in (
3.4
).
Let us mention that the Brownian snake is actually a path-valued process which describes for every
t\in[0,1]
the path
Z^{g}_{s}
for all the ancestors
s
of
t
in
T_{g}
; see
[
DLG02
, Chapter 4]
for details on such processes in a broader setting.
On an intuitive level, after associating a time
t
with its projection in
T_{g}
, the values of
Z^{g}
evolve along the branches of the tree like a Brownian motion, and these Brownian motions separate at the branchpoints to continue to evolve independently.

In the case of a looptree, although without this formalism, Le Gall & Miermont
[
LGM11
]
constructed a process that describes a Gaussian field on
\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})
under the assumption (
PJ
). In this case, one places on each cycle of the looptree an independent Brownian bridge, with duration given by the length of the cycle, which describes the increments of the field along the cycle. The value of the field at a given point is then the sum of the increments along a geodesic path on
\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})
to the root, analogously to (
2.2
) in the discrete setting. In the case where
\mathsf{C}
defined in (
3.3
) is nontrivial, one then adds an independent Brownian snake driven by
\mathsf{C}
.

Formally, recall that the standard Brownian bridge
b=(b_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is centred Gaussian process with covariance

\mathbb{E}[b_{s}b_{t}]=\min(s,t)-st,\qquad s,t\in[0,1].

In particular, the variance is
\mathbb{E}[b_{t}^{2}]=t(1-t)
. One can consider bridges of arbitrary given duration using a diffusive scaling.
Now recall the notation
\mathsf{R}^{t}_{t_{i}}=\inf_{s\in[t_{i},t]}\mathsf{X}_{s}-\mathsf{X}_{t_{i}-}
from (
3.1
), where the
t_{i}
’s are the jump times of
\mathsf{X}
up to time
t
, and also the partial order
\prec
above. Let us finally recall the notation
\delta_{t}(a,b)
used in (
3.5
).
Let
Z^{\mathsf{C}}
be defined as above, and independently let
(b_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
denote a sequence of i.i.d. standard Brownian bridges,
and define for every
a\geqslant 0
and
t\in[0,1]
,

Z^{a}_{t}\coloneqq\sqrt{a}\,Z^{\mathsf{C}}_{t}+\sum_{t_{i}\prec t}\sqrt{\Delta\mathsf{X}_{t_{i}}}\,b_{i}\left((\Delta\mathsf{X}_{t_{i}})^{-1}\mathsf{R}^{t}_{t_{i}}\right),

(3.7)

and set
Z=Z^{1}
. Note that by scaling
\sqrt{a}\,Z^{\mathsf{C}}
has the same law as
Z^{a\mathsf{C}}
.

Let us prove that the series converges in
L^{2}
for any fixed
t
; note that only jump times
t_{i}\prec t
of
\mathsf{X}
contribute.
Since the
b_{i}
’s are independent Brownian bridges, then the nonzero summands in the formula are independent zero-mean Gaussian random variables with variance

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\left((\Delta\mathsf{X}_{t_{i}})^{1/2}b_{i}\left((\Delta\mathsf{X}_{t_{i}})^{-1}\mathsf{R}^{t}_{t_{i}}\right)\right)^{2}\bigg]=\frac{\mathsf{R}^{t}_{t_{i}}(\Delta\mathsf{X}_{t_{i}}-\mathsf{R}^{t}_{t_{i}})}{\Delta\mathsf{X}_{t_{i}}}\leqslant\delta_{t_{i}}(0,\mathsf{R}^{t}_{t_{i}}).

Then by definition of the looptree distance (
3.6
),

\mathbb{E}[|Z^{a}_{t}|^{2}]\leqslant a\mathsf{C}_{t}+\sum_{t_{i}\prec t}\delta_{t_{i}}(0,\mathsf{R}^{t}_{t_{i}})=d^{a}_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}(0,t).

This shows that the random variable
Z^{a}_{t}
is well-defined for any fixed
t
.
Note that by Proposition
3.2
we have
\mathbb{E}[|Z_{t}|^{2}]\leqslant X_{t-}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose that there exist
c>0
and
K>0
such that for every
s,t\in[0,1]
,

d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}(s,t)\leqslant K\cdot|t-s|^{c}.

(3.8)

Then the process
(Z_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
admits a continuous modification that is almost surely Hölder continuous with any exponent smaller than
c/2
.

Proof.

The preceding argument generalises to show that for any
q>0
, there exists
K_{q}>0
such that for every
s,t\in[0,1]
, it holds that

\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{t}-Z_{s}|^{q}\right]\leqslant K_{q}\cdot d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}(s,t)^{q/2}.

(3.9)

Indeed, note that
Z_{t}-Z_{s}
is the sum of independent Gaussian random variables, and by splitting at the last common ancestor of
s
and
t
, we see that the sum of the variances involved is given exactly by the same quantity as
d_{\mathrm{Loop}}(s,t)
, except that the function
\delta_{t}(x,y)=\min\{|x-y|,\Delta\mathsf{X}_{t}-|x-y|\}
used in (
3.5
) is replaced by the covariance function
\widehat{\delta}_{t}(x)=|x-y|(\Delta\mathsf{X}_{t}-|x-y|)/\Delta\mathsf{X}_{t}\leqslant\delta_{t}(x,y)
. We refer to the proof of Proposition 6 in
[
LGM11
]
for details. We infer that (
3.9
) holds, where
K_{q}
is the absolute
q
’th moment of a standard Gaussian random variable.
We conclude from the Kolmogorov criterion and the assumption (
3.8
).
∎

Remark 3.4
.

If
s
and
t
are such that
d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}(s,t)=0
, then
Z_{t}-Z_{s}=0
almost surely by (
3.9
) so one can view
Z
as a random process indexed by the looptree. The bound (
3.9
) further shows
that the process
(Z_{x};x\in\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X}))
has a modification that is almost surely Hölder continuous with any exponent smaller than
1/2
.

Let us mention that, even when
\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})
is a tree coded by a continuous function, it may be the case that the process
Z
is not continuous, see
[
DLG02
, Chapter 4.5]
.
On the other hand, in Sections
5
to
7
we consider random paths with exchangeable increments and they do satisfy (
3.8
) for some
c>0
.
In the particular case of a Brownian excursion, in which case the looptree reduces to the Brownian tree, from the Hölder continuity of the Brownian excursion,
c
can take any value in
(0,1/2)
, but cannot exceed
1/2
.
On the other hand, when
\mathsf{X}

is an excursion of a stable Lévy process with index
\alpha\in(1,2)
, although not explicitly written there, we can derive from
[
CK14
]
that (
3.8
) holds with any
c
smaller than
1/\alpha
, and again this is the largest possible value. For more general Lévy processes, we prove in the forthcoming paper
[
KM
]

4.
Invariance principles in the pure jump case

In this short section, we consider deterministic càdlàg paths
\mathsf{X}
and
(\mathsf{X}^{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
with
\mathsf{X}_{0},\mathsf{X}^{n}_{0}\geqslant 0
and no negative jump, all extended to
[-1,1]
by setting
\mathsf{X}_{t}=\mathsf{X}^{n}_{t}=0
for every
t\in[-1,0)
. We assume that
\mathsf{X}^{n}
converges to some
\mathsf{X}
for the Skorokhod topology and that
\mathsf{X}
satisfies the pure jump property (
PJ
) and we derive invariance principles for the associated labelled looptrees and maps.
Before that, let us start with a simple result that we shall use throughout Section
7
and whose proof’s ideas will be used in Proposition
4.3
just below.

4.1.
The continuous part as limit of small jumps

Recall from Section
3.1
that we denote by
\mathsf{C}
the continuous part of the supremum of the dual path of
\mathsf{X}
, and by
\mathsf{J}
the jump part, so that
\mathsf{C}_{t}+\mathsf{J}_{t}=\mathsf{X}_{t-}
for every
t\in[0,1]
. For
\delta>0
, let us define
\mathsf{C}_{t}^{\delta}
as the complement of the contribution to
\mathsf{X}_{t-}
of the jumps larger than
\delta
, namely for every
t\in[0,1]
,

\mathsf{C}^{\delta}_{t}\coloneqq\mathsf{C}_{t}+\sum_{0\preceq r\prec t}\mathsf{R}^{t}_{r}\mathbf{1}_{\{\Delta\mathsf{X}_{r}\leqslant\delta\}}.

(4.1)

We prove that for every
\delta>0
, the quantity
\mathsf{C}^{\delta}
depends continuously on
\mathsf{X}
, which is not the case of
\mathsf{C}
itself.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that
\mathsf{X}^{n}\to\mathsf{X}
for the Skorokhod topology as
n\to\infty
, then for every
\delta>0
the path
\mathsf{C}^{n,\delta}
converges uniformly to
\mathsf{C}^{\delta}
, which further converges uniformly to
\mathsf{C}
as
\delta\downarrow 0
.

Proof.

The second convergence follows from Dini’s theorem which shows that if
(r_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
is an enumeration of the set
\{r\prec t\}
, then the series
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathsf{R}^{t}_{r_{i}}
converges uniformly to
J_{t}
as
N\to\infty
. Let us focus on the first assertion.
Fix
\delta>0
and suppose by contradiction that
\mathsf{C}^{n,\delta}
does not converge uniformly to
\mathsf{C}^{\delta}
, then
there exists
\varepsilon>0
and a sequence
(t_{n})_{n}
such that
|\mathsf{C}^{n,\delta}_{t_{n}}-\mathsf{C}^{\delta}_{t_{n}}|\geqslant\varepsilon
for infinitely many indices
n
. Extracting a subsequence if necessary, let us assume that this holds for all
n
and that
t_{n}
converges to some
t\in[0,1]
.
Note that the set
\{0\preceq r\prec t:\Delta\mathsf{X}_{r}>\delta\}
is finite, and properties of the Skorokhod topology imply that the values
\mathsf{R}^{t}_{r}
for such
r
’s are the limits of analogous values
\mathsf{R}^{n,t_{n}}_{r_{n}}
for
0\preceq r_{n}\prec t_{n}
. Consequently the sum of the latter,
\mathsf{X}^{n}_{t_{n}-}-\mathsf{C}^{n,\delta}_{t_{n}}
, converges to that of the former,
\mathsf{X}_{t-}-\mathsf{C}^{\delta}_{t}
.
On the other hand, the convergence in the Skorokhod topology also implies that
\mathsf{X}_{t_{n}-}
converges to
\mathsf{X}_{t-}
and this yields a contradiction.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

We shall apply this lemma to (random) discrete Łukasiewicz paths
\mathsf{X}^{n}
, which satisfy
\mathsf{C}^{n}=0
for every
n
, and which converge once rescaled towards some limit process
\mathsf{X}
. Then the continuous part
\mathsf{C}
in the latter arises as the limit of the contribution of the jumps in
\mathsf{X}^{n}
which are smaller than
\delta
(after scaling) when first
n\to\infty
and then
\delta\downarrow 0
.

4.2.
Convergence of deterministic looptrees

For the rest of this section, we assume that
\mathsf{X}
satisfies the pure jump property (
PJ
). In this case, the geometry of the looptree
\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})
and the Gaussian labels on it are very constraint and the function
\mathrm{Loop}
is actually continuous at
\mathsf{X}
. The next result is very close to
[
CK14
, Theorem 4.1]
; the proof there is completely deterministic and only uses the key property (
PJ
), see
[
CK14
, Corollary 3.4]
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose that
\mathsf{X}^{n}\to\mathsf{X}
for the Skorokhod topology and that
\mathsf{X}
satisfies (
PJ
). Then

\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X}^{n})\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to\infty}\enskip\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})

in the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov topology.

Proof.

The argument follows the lines of the preceding proof.
We argue by contradiction and therefore suppose that there exist
\varepsilon>0
, a pair
0\leqslant s<t\leqslant 1
, and sequences
s_{n}\to s
and
t_{n}\to t
such that for every
n\geqslant 1
(limiting ourselves to a subsequence if necessary),

\big|d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X}^{n})}(s_{n},t_{n})-d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}(s,t)\big|>\varepsilon.

To ease notation, we assume that
s_{n}\preceq t_{n}
for every
n
, and so
s\preceq t
; the general case can be adapted in a straightforward way by cutting at the last common ancestor.
Recall that
\mathsf{X}_{t-}-\mathsf{X}_{s-}=\sum_{s\preceq r\prec t}\mathsf{R}^{t}_{r}
; let us fix
\eta>0
small enough, so that, by the property (
PJ
), it holds
\sum_{s\preceq r\preceq t}\mathsf{R}^{t}_{r}\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathsf{R}^{t}_{r}\leqslant\eta\}}\leqslant\varepsilon/2
. Note that the set
\{s\preceq r\prec t:\mathsf{R}^{t}_{r}>\eta\}
is finite, and properties of the Skorokhod topology imply that the values
\Delta\mathsf{X}_{r}
and
\mathsf{R}^{t}_{r}
for such
r
’s are approximated by similar quantities in
\mathsf{X}^{n}
. Therefore the total contribution of these large cycles to
d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X}^{n})}(s_{n},t_{n})
converges to that in
d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}(s,t)
.

Now we note that
\mathsf{X}_{t_{n}}-\mathsf{X}_{s_{n}-}
converges either to
\mathsf{X}_{t-}-\mathsf{X}_{s-}
or to
\mathsf{X}_{t}-\mathsf{X}_{s-}
. In any case we have that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{X}_{t_{n}}-\mathsf{X}_{s_{n}-}-\sum_{s_{n}\preceq r\prec t_{n}}\mathsf{R}^{t_{n}}_{r}\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathsf{R}^{t_{n}}_{r}>\eta\}}\leqslant\sum_{s\preceq r\preceq t}\mathsf{R}^{t}_{r}-\sum_{s\preceq r\prec t}\mathsf{R}^{t}_{r}\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathsf{R}^{t}_{r}>\eta\}}\leqslant\varepsilon/2.

Precisely, the term on the left converges to the one in the middle where
\mathsf{R}^{t}_{t}
may or may not be included.
The left hand side bounds above the contribution of the small cycles to
d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X}^{n})}(s_{n},t_{n})
so this yields a contradiction and
d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X}^{n})}
converges to
d_{\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})}
uniformly on
[0,1]^{2}
, which implies the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov convergence.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

For every
n\geqslant 1
, let
\mathsf{LT}^{n}
be a looptree with
n
edges and let
\mathsf{X}^{n}
be its Łukasiewicz path. Suppose that there exists
a_{n}\to\infty
such that
(a_{n}^{-1}\mathsf{X}^{n}_{nt})_{t\in[-1,1]}\to\mathsf{X}
for the Skorokhod topology and that
\mathsf{X}
satisfies (
PJ
). Suppose in addition that the convergence (
2.3
) holds. Then

a_{n}^{-1}\mathsf{LT}^{n}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to\infty}\enskip\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X})

in the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov topology.

Proof.

We already observed that
\mathrm{Loop}(\mathsf{X}^{n})
is the continuum looptree obtained by replacing each edge of
\mathsf{LT}^{n}
by a segment with unit length, then
\mathrm{Loop}(a_{n}^{-1}\mathsf{X}^{n}_{n\cdot})
describes the same looptree when the distances have been multiplied by
a_{n}^{-1}
, which therefore lies at Gromov–Hausdorff distance less than
a_{n}^{-1}
from
a_{n}^{-1}\mathsf{LT}^{n}
. Finally the convergence (
2.3
) shows that the uniform probability measure on the vertices is close to the one on the corners, so the rescaled discrete and continuum looptrees lie at Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov distance
O(a_{n}^{-1})
from each other.
The claim then immediately follows from Proposition
4.3
.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

The sole functional convergence fails to dictate the behaviour of the looptrees in the following two cases:

(i)

In the presence of a nontrivial continuous part.
Even in the extreme case where
\mathsf{X}
is continuous, so for every
t\in[0,1]
one has
\mathsf{J}_{t}=0
, one needs to look closer to deduce the convergence of looptrees towards the tree
T_{\mathsf{X}}
.
This is studied in Sections
5
and
7
in a probabilistic setting.

(ii)

Also, for looptrees as defined in
[
CK14
]
, i.e. in the middle of Figure
2
, without merging each right-most offspring in the tree with its parent. This is due to the fact that in this looptree, the graph distance between such a pair equals
1
, whereas the Łukasiewicz path at the time of visit of the last offspring is exactly at the same level as the left limit at the time of visit of the parent. In
[
CK14
, Theorem 4.1]
, the authors therefore also require that the maximal height of the tree is small compared to
a_{n}
. It can be reduced to requiring that the maximum over all vertices of the tree of the number of its ancestors which are the right-most offspring of their parent is small compared to
a_{n}
. One easily checks that this assumption is necessary, at least when
a_{n}=o(n)
, since in this case, one can add a chain of length
a_{n}
of cycles with length two (individuals with only one offspring in the tree) to the root with no effect on the convergence of the Łukasiewicz path. Again this is discussed in the probabilistic setting of Sections
5
and
7
.

Model and notations

Let us extend the models presented in the introduction by considering more generally looptrees and maps

for the uniform topology. We henceforth concentrate on the convergence of the processes
X^{n}
and

, and the spaces
LT^{n}
and
M^{n}
.

5.2.
Tightness results

Fix
n\geqslant 1
and recall that
LT^{n}
denotes a looptree sampled uniformly at random amongst those with cycle lengths
\varrho_{n}
and
\mathbf{f}_{n}
. Recall that its has
\mathrm{f}_{n}(0)
vertices and
E_{n}
edges in total.
Theorem 6 in
[
Mar19
]
states that the label process associated with a uniform random good labelling on
LT^{n}
is always tight; we explain below that behind this result hides tightness of the distances on
LT^{n}
.

Fix two corners, say
c_{i}
and
c_{j}
, of
LT^{n}
with
i<j
. Recall that their looptree distance
d_{LT^{n}}(i,j)
is obtained by considering all the cycles between them and summing the shortest between the left and right side. Let us modify this distance by defining
\widehat{d}_{LT^{n}}(i,j)\geqslant d_{LT^{n}}(i,j)
as the length of the path between
c_{i}
and
c_{j}
which always follows the right side of the ancestral cycles of
c_{i}
and, if
c_{j}
does not belong these ancestral cycles, always follows the left side of the ancestral cycles of
c_{j}
, and finally follows the top part of their common ancestral cycle.
We extend bo
d_{LT^{n}}
and
\widehat{d}_{LT^{n}}
to
[0,E_{n}]
by linear interpolation.

Proposition 5.1
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
K>0
such that for every
n
large enough,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s\neq t}\frac{\widehat{d}_{LT^{n}}(E_{n}s,E_{n}t)}{\sigma_{n}\cdot|t-s|^{1/2-\varepsilon}}\leqslant K\right)\geqslant 1-\varepsilon.

Consequently, the sequence of processes
(\sigma_{n}^{-1}d_{LT^{n}}(E_{n}s,E_{n}t))_{s,t\in[0,1]}
is tight for the uniform topology.

Proof.

This result in implicit in the proof of Theorem 6 in
[
Mar19
]
to which we refer for details and only sketch the argument here. Note that we may assume that the number
E_{n}-\mathrm{f}_{n}(1)
of edges which do not belong to single loops tends to infinity as otherwise, if it is uniformly bounded say then so is the number of vertices and the diameter of the looptree as well as
\sigma_{n}
.

Let us fix
s<t
and assume to ease notation that
E_{n}s
and
E_{n}t
are both integers. We shall abuse notation and for an integer
i\in\{0,\dots,E_{n}\}
, we shall talk about the corner
i
or the vertex
i
to mean the corner
c_{i}
or its incident vertex.
Then let
r\in[s,t]
be such that
E_{n}r
is also an integer and such that the vertex
E_{n}r
is the first one that belongs to an ancestral cycle of
E_{n}t
but not of
E_{n}s
. If
E_{n}t
itself belongs to an ancestral cycle of
E_{n}s
, then we let
r=t
. Let
X^{n}
denote the Łukasiewicz path associated with
LT^{n}
, then we have

\widehat{d}_{LT^{n}}(E_{n}s,E_{n}r)=X^{n}_{E_{n}s-1}-\inf_{q\in[s,r]}X^{n}_{E_{n}q-1}.

According to
[
Mar19
, Proposition 3]
, for
\varepsilon>0
fixed, there exists
C>0
such that the right hand side is smaller than
C\cdot\sigma_{n}\cdot|t-s|^{(1-\varepsilon)/2}
uniformly for
0\leqslant s<t\leqslant 1
with a probability at least
1-\varepsilon
.
It only remains to prove a similar bound for
\widehat{d}_{LT^{n}}(E_{n}r,E_{n}t)
.

The argument is provided by the proof of Theorem 6 in
[
Mar19
]
. One would like to proceed symmetrically by inverting the orientation of the edges of the looptree and thus following the contour from right to left, however there is a break of symmetry in the construction of the looptree version of a tree since we contract each edge from an inner vertex of the tree to its right-most offspring. This is consistent with the Łukasiewicz path in which the value at the instant of visit of the right-most offspring equals the value of the left limit at the instant of visit of its parent, but if we were to consider the Łukasiewicz path of the mirror tree, we would miss the left-most edge on each ancestral cycle of
E_{n}t
and only count the other ones.

Note that an ancestral cycle must have length at least two. There are two cases when following such a cycle on its left. Suppose first that we traverse
i\geqslant 2
edges, since
i\leqslant 2(i-1)
and the quantity
i-1
is counted by the mirror Łukasiewicz path, then the previous argument applies and this only replaces the constant
C
above by
2C
.
However if we traverse only the left-most edge of the cycle, then this case really is lost when considering the mirror Łukasiewicz path. It is shown in
[
Mar19
, Lemma 5]
that with high probability the proportion of such cycles is bounded away from
1
on all ancestral lines consisting of at least some constant times
\log(E_{n}-\mathrm{f}_{n}(1))
cycles.
Then on the one hand the argument for the left branch applies to these ancestral lines, and only the constant
C
is modified, and on the other hand the total contribution of the left-most edge of the ancestral lines with less than
\log(E_{n}-\mathrm{f}_{n}(1))
cycles is anyway small.

Finally, since
\widehat{d}_{LT^{n}}\geqslant d_{LT^{n}}
, then the latter satisfies the claim as well. We then infer from the triangle inequality that for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
K>0
such that for every
n
large enough,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{(s,t)\neq(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})}\frac{|d_{LT^{n}}(\lfloor E_{n}s\rfloor,\lfloor E_{n}t\rfloor)-d_{LT^{n}}(\lfloor E_{n}s^{\prime}\rfloor,\lfloor E_{n}t^{\prime}\rfloor)|}{\sigma_{n}\cdot\|(s,t)-(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})\|^{1/2-\varepsilon}}\leqslant K\right)\geqslant 1-\varepsilon.

Tightness of the rescaled looptree distance then follows.
∎

This bound combined with that from (
4.4
) shows a similar result for the label process which extends
[
Mar19
, Theorem 6]
which focused on the particular case of the labels associated with maps. Recall that
Z^{n}
is defined in (
4.2
).

Corollary 5.2
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{E}[|\xi|^{q_{0}}]<\infty
for some
q_{0}>4
.
For every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
K>0
such that for every
n
large enough,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s\neq t}\frac{|Z^{n}_{E_{n}s}-Z^{n}_{E_{n}t}|}{\sigma_{n}^{1/2}|t-s|^{1/4-\varepsilon}}\leqslant K\right)\geqslant 1-\varepsilon.

Consequently, the sequence of processes
(\sigma_{n}^{-1/2}Z^{n}_{E_{n}t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is tight for the uniform topology.

Recall the convergence (
5.1
); then jointly with this corollary (and say Skorokhod’s representation theorem), this allows us to apply Lemma
2.5
and Lemma
2.6
. We deduce that from every sequence of integers one can extract a subsequence along which
\sigma_{n}^{-1/2}Z^{n}
converges in distribution to a continuous limit
Z
. When
\xi
has the centred geometric distribution, one can extract a further subsequence along which the associated rescaled map
\sigma_{n}^{-1/2}M^{n}
converges in distribution.
In Section
7
we characterise the subsequential limits of
Z^{n}
and
LT^{n}
; the key technical input for this is Lemma
5.5
in the next subsection.

Before moving to this topic, let us consider looptrees
\overline{LT}\vphantom{LT}^{n}
as defined in
[
CK14
]
, i.e. without merging the right-most offspring of each individual with its parent (so the cycles are longer by
1
).
Typically, our random models satisfy
\limsup_{n\to\infty}E_{n}^{-1}\mathrm{f}_{n}(1)<1
so
\overline{\sigma}\vphantom{\sigma}_{n}
below can be replaced by
\sigma_{n}
.

Corollary 5.3
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
K>0
such that for every
n
large enough,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s\neq t}\frac{d_{\overline{LT}\vphantom{LT}^{n}}(E_{n}s,E_{n}t)}{\overline{\sigma}\vphantom{\sigma}_{n}\cdot|t-s|^{1/2-\varepsilon}}\leqslant K\right)\geqslant 1-\varepsilon\qquad\text{where}\qquad\overline{\sigma}\vphantom{\sigma}_{n}=\sigma_{n}\frac{E_{n}}{E_{n}-\mathrm{f}_{n}(1)}.

Consequently, the sequence of processes
(\overline{\sigma}\vphantom{\sigma}_{n}^{-1}d_{LT^{n}}(E_{n}s,E_{n}t))_{s,t\in[0,1]}
is tight for the uniform topology. Similarly, if one constructs random labels on
\overline{LT}\vphantom{LT}^{n}
using random
\xi
-bridges with
\mathbb{E}[|\xi|^{q_{0}}]<\infty
for some
q_{0}>4
, then the corresponding label process is tight as well, once rescaled by a factor
\overline{\sigma}\vphantom{\sigma}_{n}^{1/2}
.

Proof.

Let us briefly sketch the proof.
First, the difference with Proposition
5.1
is that the right-most edge along each cycle is not counted there; the argument in the previous proof allows to control this difference for cycles with length at least
3
in this looptree, but cycles with length exactly
2
are really lost when identifying the single child with its parent.
Therefore, if one first samples
\overline{LT}\vphantom{LT}^{n}
and then removes from it all the cycles with length
2
, then the resulting looptree satisfies the claim from Proposition
5.1
, with the scaling factor
\sigma_{n}
.
One then needs to plug back these cycles. This was done for the trees in
[
BM14
, Lemma 8]
to which we refer for a detailed argument. Without these
\mathrm{f}_{n}(1)
cycles, we have lost as many vertices, so it remains
E_{n}-\mathrm{f}_{n}(1)
of them and we distribute these cycles above these vertices, according to a multinomial law with parameters
\mathrm{f}_{n}(1)
and
(E_{n}-\mathrm{f}_{n}(1))^{-1},\dots,(E_{n}-\mathrm{f}_{n}(1))^{-1}
. Such a distribution is well concentrated around its mean (namely the Chernoff bound applies), which allows to show that the distances are very close to get multiplied by
1+\mathrm{f}_{n}(1)/(E_{n}-\mathrm{f}_{n}(1))
, see
[
BM14
, page 303]
for details.
∎

Note that for the looptrees
\overline{LT}\vphantom{LT}^{n}
, the looptree distance is lower bounded by the tree distance, which is not the case for the looptrees
LT^{n}
because of the identification of the right-most offspring of each individual with its parent. Then we infer from the preceding result tightness for trees. Precisely, consider the
height process
, defined for every integer
j\in\{0,\dots,E_{n}\}
by

H^{n}_{j}=\#\{i\prec j\}=\#\{0\leqslant i<j:X^{n}_{i-1}\leqslant\inf_{[i,j]}X^{n}\},

and then linearly interpolated.

Corollary 5.4
.

Suppose that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}E_{n}^{-1}\mathrm{f}_{n}(1)<1
.
Then for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
K>0
such that for every
n
large enough,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s\neq t}\frac{|H^{n}_{E_{n}s}-H^{n}_{E_{n}t}|}{\sigma_{n}\cdot|t-s|^{1/2-\varepsilon}}\leqslant K\right)\geqslant 1-\varepsilon.

Consequently, the sequence of processes
(\sigma_{n}^{-1}H^{n}_{E_{n}t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is tight for the uniform topology.

However, the reader should have in mind that the scaling factor
\sigma_{n}
may in general be too large, and the rescaled height process may converge to the null process. Indeed, as shown in
[
Mar19
, Proposition 5]
, for
U
independently sampled uniformly at random on
[0,1]
, the height
H^{n}_{E_{n}U}
grows at most like
n/\sigma_{n}
, and we expect the maximal height of the tree to grow at this rate in most cases. If this is the case, then Corollary
5.4
is only useful when
\sigma_{n}^{2}
behaves asymptotically like some constant, say
\sigma^{2}>0
times
n
, and in this regime it extends
[
BM14
, Lemma 8]
.
Let us point out that the maximal height of a leaf can be much larger than
n/\sigma_{n}
and trees are not always tight, as opposed to their looptree versions; see
[
Kor15
]
for examples with subcritical size-conditioned Bienaymé–Galton–Watson trees.
The very recent work
[
BR21
, Theorem 8]
provides a precise tail bound for the maximal height of such trees.

5.3.
A spinal decomposition and its consequences

Lemma 3 in
[
Mar19
]
describes the ancestral lines of finitely many random vertices in the forest
T^{n}
, from which we deduce here a key lemma which will allow us in Section
7
to determine the geometry of our random (loop)trees and their labels from the behaviour of the Łukasiewicz path.

Let us here abuse notation and identify the vertices of
T^{n}
with their instant of visit in depth-first search order, which belongs to
\{1,\dots,E_{n}\}
.
Fix
q\geqslant 1
and sample
q
i.i.d. random vertices independent of
T^{n}
; the set of these vertices and their ancestors inherits a forest structure from
T^{n}
, let us remove from it its leaves and branchpoints and let us denote by
A^{n}_{q}
the resulting set. The latter is made of a random number
N\in\{1,\dots,2q-1\}
of chains of vertices, which we denote respectively by
A^{n}_{q,1},\dots,A^{n}_{q,N}
.
Any individual
i\in A^{n}_{q}
has say,
k^{n}_{i}\geqslant 1
offspring
in the original forest
, and only one of them, the
j^{n}_{i}
’th say, is an ancestor of (or equal to) at least one of the random vertices. In the case
q=1
, with a single random vertex
U_{n}
say, the set
A^{n}_{1}
is simply given by
\{i\prec U_{n}\}=\{i<U_{n}:X^{n}_{i-}<X^{n}_{t}\text{ for every }t\in(i,U_{n})\}
and each pair
(k^{n}_{i},j^{n}_{i})
is given by
(\Delta X^{n}_{i},\Delta X^{n}_{i}-R^{n,U_{n}}_{i})
, where

R^{n,j}_{i}=\inf_{t\in[i,j]}X^{n}_{t}-X^{n}_{i-}.

We let
g
be a function from
\{(k,j)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}:k\geqslant j\geqslant 1\}
to
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and define

G_{n}^{2}=\sum_{k\geqslant j\geqslant 1}\mathrm{f}_{n}(k)g(k,j)=\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\mathrm{f}_{n}(k)\sum_{j=1}^{k}g(k,j).

Below, we shall use the notation for any
\delta>0
,

G_{n}^{2,\delta}=\sum_{k\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}}\mathrm{f}_{n}(k)\sum_{j=1}^{k}g(k,j)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{n}^{2,\delta}=\sum_{k\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}}k(k-1)\mathrm{f}_{n}(k).

Lemma 5.5
.

Suppose that
g
satisfies the following property: there exist
c,C>0
such that

c\min\{j,k-j\}\leqslant g(k,j)\leqslant C(k-1)\qquad\text{for every}\enskip k\geqslant j\geqslant 1.

For every
q\geqslant 1
and every
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{1\leqslant m\leqslant N}\bigg|\frac{\sigma^{2,\delta}_{n}}{G^{2,\delta}_{n}}\sum_{i\in A^{n}_{q,m}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\Delta X^{n}_{i}\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}\}}g(k^{n}_{i},j^{n}_{i})-\frac{\sigma^{2,\delta}_{n}}{n}\#A^{n}_{q,m}\bigg|>\varepsilon\sqrt{\sigma^{2,\delta}_{n}}\bigg)=0,

as soon as
\sigma_{n}\to\infty
and
\sigma_{n}^{-1}\varrho_{n}
is uniformly bounded above.

Note that the function
g(k,j)=k-j
satisfies the assumptions and moreover
G^{2,\delta}_{n}=\sigma^{2,\delta}_{n}/2
and further in the case
q=N=1
of a single random vertex
U_{n}
, the sum in the lemma equals

C^{n,\delta}_{U_{n}}\coloneqq\sum_{i\prec U_{n}}R^{n,U_{n}}_{i}\mathbf{1}_{\{\Delta X^{n}_{i}\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}\}}

where the notation is reminiscent of Lemma
4.1
. Finally
\#A^{n}_{1}=H^{n}_{U_{n}}
is the height of the random vertex. Therefore, applying the lemma twice, we infer that for every
\varepsilon>0
, we have both

\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigg|C^{n,\delta}_{U_{n}}-\frac{\sigma^{2,\delta}_{n}}{2n}H^{n}_{U_{n}}\bigg|>\varepsilon\sqrt{\sigma^{2,\delta}_{n}}\bigg)=0,

(5.2)

and then, for any function
g
as in the lemma,

\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigg|\frac{\sigma^{2,\delta}_{n}}{2G^{2,\delta}_{n}}\sum_{i\prec U_{n}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\Delta X^{n}_{i}\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}\}}g(\Delta X^{n}_{i},\Delta X^{n}_{i}-R^{n,U_{n}}_{i})-C^{n,\delta}_{U_{n}}\bigg|>\varepsilon\sqrt{\sigma^{2,\delta}_{n}}\bigg)=0.

(5.3)

According to Lemma
4.1
and Remark
4.2
, if the Łukasiewicz path, rescaled by a factor
\sigma_{n}^{-1}
, converges in distribution to some limit path, then
\sigma_{n}^{-1}C^{n,\delta}
converges in distribution towards the continuous part when first
n\to\infty
and then
\delta\downarrow 0
. Along these limits the ratio
\sigma^{2,\delta}_{n}/\sigma^{2}_{n}
will converge in our main results; therefore Lemma
5.5
will allow us to derive in Section
7
the convergence of a function averaged along the ancestral line of random vertices. For example, in Section
7.2
, we apply it to the function
g(k,j)=\min\{j,k-j\}
, in which case
G^{2,\delta}_{n}
equals the contribution to the looptree spanned by random vertices of the loops shorter than
\delta\sigma_{n}
.

For every
k\geqslant 1
, one easily calculates

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\min\{j,k-j\}=\frac{k^{2}-\mathbf{1}_{\{k\in 2\mathbb{Z}+1\}}}{2}\geqslant\frac{k(k-1)}{2}.

Then the bounds on
g
imply that

\frac{c}{2}\sigma_{n}^{2,\delta}\leqslant G_{n}^{2,\delta}\leqslant C\sigma_{n}^{2,\delta}.

(5.4)

We shall need the following straightforward calculations.
Let
(K^{n}_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}
denote a uniform random permutation of the finite sequence consisting of
k\mathrm{f}_{n}(k)
occurrences of the integer
k
for every
k\geqslant 1
, and conditionally on this vector, let
(J^{n}_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}
be independent random variables, such that each
J^{n}_{i}
has the uniform distribution in
\{1,\dots,K^{n}_{i}\}
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
g
be as in Lemma
5.5
, then for every
\delta>0
,
n\geqslant 1
, and
h\geqslant 1
, it holds,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\leqslant h}g(K^{n}_{i},J^{n}_{i})\mathbf{1}_{\{K^{n}_{i}\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}\}}\right]=h\frac{G_{n}^{2,\delta}}{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{i\leqslant h}g(K^{n}_{i},J^{n}_{i})\mathbf{1}_{\{K^{n}_{i}\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}\}}\right)\leqslant 2Ch\delta\sigma_{n}\frac{G_{n}^{2,\delta}}{n}.

Proof.

Let us first denote for every
k
by
J_{k}
a random variable with the uniform distribution on
\{1,\dots,k\}
and let us denote by
K
a random variable with the size-biased law
(k\mathrm{f}_{n}(k)/n)_{k\geqslant 1}
. Then by bounding the variance by the second moment, we have

\mathbb{E}[g(k,J_{k})]=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{g(k,j)}{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{Var}(g(k,J_{k}))\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{g(k,j)^{2}}{k}.

Further

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{g(K,j)}{K}\mathbf{1}_{\{K\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}\}}\right]=\sum_{k\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{g(k,j)}{k}\frac{k\mathrm{f}_{n}(k)}{n}=\frac{G_{n}^{2,\delta}}{n}.

Also, since
g(k,j)\leqslant Ck
for every pair
j\leqslant k
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{g(K,j)^{2}}{K}\mathbf{1}_{\{K\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}\}}\right]\leqslant C\delta\sigma_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{g(K,j)}{K}\mathbf{1}_{\{K\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}\}}\right]\leqslant C\delta\sigma_{n}\frac{G_{n}^{2,\delta}}{n},

and similarly, bounding again the variance by the second moment and then the ratios in one of the two sums by
C
,

\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{g(K,j)}{K}\mathbf{1}_{\{K\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}\}}\right)\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[CK\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{g(K,j)}{K}\mathbf{1}_{\{K\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}\}}\right]\leqslant C\delta\sigma_{n}\frac{G_{n}^{2,\delta}}{n}.

Let
K^{n,\delta}_{i}=K^{n}_{i}\mathbf{1}_{\{K^{n}_{i}\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}\}}
and similarly
J^{n,\delta}_{i}=J^{n}_{i}\mathbf{1}_{\{K^{n}_{i}\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}\}}
in order to lighten the notation; let us also set
g(0,0)=0
. Then we infer first that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\leqslant h}g(K^{n,\delta}_{i},J^{n,\delta}_{i})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\leqslant h}g(K^{n,\delta}_{i},J^{n,\delta}_{i})\;\middle|\;(K^{n}_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant h}\right]\right]=h\frac{G_{n}^{2,\delta}}{n}.

Furthermore, the
J^{n,\delta}_{i}
’s are conditionally independent and it is well known that the
K^{n}_{i}
’s are negatively correlated, and thus so are random variables of the form
f(K^{n}_{i})
with a given function
f
; in particular the variance of their sum is bounded above by the sum of their variances. Consequently,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{i\leqslant h}g(K^{n,\delta}_{i},J^{n,\delta}_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\leqslant h}g(K^{n,\delta}_{i},J^{n,\delta}_{i})\;\middle|\;(K^{n}_{i})_{i\leqslant h}\right]\right)+\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{i\leqslant h}g(K^{n,\delta}_{i},J^{n,\delta}_{i})\;\middle|\;(K^{n}_{i})_{i\leqslant h}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{i\leqslant h}\sum_{j=1}^{K^{n}_{i}}\frac{g(K^{n}_{i},j)}{K^{n}_{i}}\mathbf{1}_{\{K^{n}_{i}\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}\}}\right)+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\leqslant h}\sum_{j=1}^{K^{n}_{i}}\frac{g(K^{n}_{i},j)^{2}}{K^{n}_{i}}\mathbf{1}_{\{K^{n}_{i}\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant h\left(\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K^{n}_{1}}\frac{g(K^{n}_{1},j)}{K^{n}_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{K^{n}_{1}\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}\}}\right)+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{K^{n}_{1}}\frac{g(K^{n}_{1},j)^{2}}{K^{n}_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{K^{n}_{1}\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}\}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2Ch\delta\sigma_{n}\frac{G_{n}^{2,\delta}}{n},

and the proof is complete.
∎

,
and any integers
k_{m,i}\geqslant j_{m,i}\geqslant 1
for every
m\in\{1,\dots,q+b\}
and
i\in\{1,\dots,h_{m}\}
,
the probability that the forest reduced to the
q
random vertices and their ancestors possesses

branch-points and that for every

, we have
\#A^{n}_{q,m}=h_{m}
and
(k^{n}_{m,i},j^{n}_{m,i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant\#A^{n}_{q,m}}=(k_{m,i},j_{m,i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant h_{m}}
is upper bounded by

C^{\prime}\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}}{n}\right)^{q+b}\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{m=1}^{q+b}\bigcap_{i=h_{m-1}+1}^{h_{m}}\left\{(K^{n}_{i},J^{n}_{i})=(k_{m,i-h_{m-1}},j_{m,i-h_{m-1}})\right\}\right),

(5.5)

where

depends on the sequences
(\varrho_{n})_{n}
and
(\mathbf{f}_{n})_{n}
, as well as on

and

.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
,
x\geqslant 1
, and
h\geqslant 1
. We deduce from Lemma

and Chebychev’s inequality, and then the bound (

) that

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigg|\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2,\delta}}{G_{n}^{2,\delta}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant h\\
K^{n}_{i}\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}\end{subarray}}g(K^{n}_{i},J^{n}_{i})-\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2,\delta}}{n}h\bigg|>\varepsilon\sqrt{\sigma_{n}^{2,\delta}}\bigg)\leqslant\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2,\delta}}{G_{n}^{2,\delta}}\right)^{2}\frac{2Ch\delta\sigma_{n}G_{n}^{2,\delta}}{n\varepsilon^{2}\sigma_{n}^{2,\delta}}\leqslant\frac{4Ch\sigma_{n}\delta}{c\varepsilon^{2}n}.

We infer by first union bounds and then crude bounds that for any
\varepsilon>0
,
x\geqslant 1
, and
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{m\leqslant N}\bigg|\frac{\sigma^{2,\delta}_{n}}{G^{2,\delta}_{n}}\sum_{i\in A^{n}_{q,m}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\Delta X^{n}_{i}\leqslant\delta\sigma_{n}\}}g(k^{n}_{i},j^{n}_{i})-\frac{\sigma^{2,\delta}_{n}}{n}\#A^{n}_{q,m}\bigg|>\varepsilon\sqrt{\sigma^{2,\delta}_{n}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{1}\operatorname{e}^{-c_{2}x}+C^{\prime}\sum_{b=0}^{q-1}\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}}{n}\right)^{q+b}\sum_{h_{1}+\dots+h_{q+b}\leqslant xn/\sigma_{n}}\sum_{m\leqslant q+b}\frac{4Ch_{m}\delta\sigma_{n}}{c\varepsilon^{2}n}

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{1}\operatorname{e}^{-c_{2}x}+C^{\prime}qx^{2q+1}\frac{4C\delta}{c\varepsilon^{2}}.

Given

∎

6.
Random paths with exchangeable increments

Lemma
5.5
will allow us in the next section to relate geometric information about random trees, looptrees, and their labels to their Łukasiewicz paths. In this section, we provide more information on the latter and prove some invariance principles.
Let henceforth
X^{n}
be the Łukasiewicz path of a looptree sampled uniformly at random with cycle lengths
\varrho_{n}
and
\mathbf{f}_{n}
.
Then
X^{n}
is sampled uniformly at random in the set of càdlàg paths on
[0,E_{n}]
such that:

(i)

X^{n}_{0}=\varrho_{n}
and
\#\{1\leqslant i\leqslant E_{n}:\Delta X^{n}_{i}=k\}=\mathrm{f}_{n}(k)
for every
k\geqslant 0
,

(ii)

X^{n}_{t}=X^{n}_{\lfloor t\rfloor}-(t-\lfloor t\rfloor)
for every
t\in[0,E_{n}]
,

(iii)

X^{n}_{t}>0
for every
t\in[0,E_{n})
.

As previously, we shall extend
X^{n}
to
[-E_{n},E_{n}]
by setting
X^{n}_{t}=0
for every
t\in[-E_{n},0)
.
Note that

X^{n}_{E_{n}}=X^{n}_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{E_{n}}\Delta X^{n}_{i}-E_{n}=\varrho_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{f}_{n,i}-E_{n}=0.

A classical, simple way to sample such a random path is to first remove the positivity constraint and then make a conjugation operation also known as cyclic shift or Vervaat transform, as we next recall.

6.1.
The Vervaat transform

Let us first introduce a variation of the classical conjugation pf paths, also known as Vervaat transform. Our modification is chosen so that the shifted path may have a different value at the starting point compared to the original one, which will be needed in a finite variation regime.

Fix
T>0
, let
Y
be a càdlàg path on
[-T,T]
with no negative jump, with
Y_{t}=0
for every
t<0
, and let
r\in[0,T]
. Consider the shift operation
\vartheta_{r}
defined for every
t\in[0,T]
by

(\vartheta_{r}Y)_{t}=\begin{cases}Y_{t+r}-Y_{r-}+Y_{0}&\text{if }t+r\leqslant T,\\
Y_{(t+r-)-T}-Y_{r-}+Y_{T}&\text{if }t+r>T.\end{cases}

(6.1)

Set also
(\vartheta_{r}Y)_{t}=0
for
t\in[-T,0)
.
One checks that this path càdlàg; it is obtained by swapping the parts
(Y_{s};0\leqslant s\leqslant r)
and
(Y_{s};r\leqslant s\leqslant T)
while keeping the value at the right edge and adding the value of the jump at time
r
to the starting point:
(\vartheta_{r}Y)_{T}=Y_{T}
and
(\vartheta_{r}Y)_{0}=Y_{0}+\Delta Y_{r}
.
The properties of the Skorokhod’s topology immediately imply that for any fixed path
Y
, the function
r\mapsto\vartheta_{r}Y
is left-continuous; it is right-continuous at
r
as soon
Y
is continuous at
r
.
More generally, in this case, since
Y
does not vary much in a neighborhood of
r
, then neither does a function close to
Y
in the Skorokhod’s topology.
We leave the proof of the following lemma to the reader.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
Y
be a path as above and
r\in[0,T]
be such that
Y
is continuous at
r
, then the shift function
\vartheta
is continuous at the pair
(Y,r)
.

Now let
\varrho\geqslant 0
and suppose that
Y_{0}=\varrho
and
Y_{T}=0
.
Let
u\in[0,\varrho]
and define

I_{u}=\inf\left\{t\in[0,T]:Y_{t-}=u+\inf_{s\in[0,T]}Y_{s}\right\},

(6.2)

to which we shall refer by abuse of language as the first passage time of
Y
at level
u+\inf Y
, although if
Y
makes a positive jump at time
I_{u}
this is incorrect.
Then the
Vervaat transform
of
Y
at height
u\in[0,\varrho]
is the càdlàg path defined on
[-T,T]
by

V_{u}Y=\vartheta_{I_{u}}Y.

(6.3)

For every
u\in[0,\varrho]
the path
V_{u}Y
satisfies
(V_{u}Y)_{0}=\varrho+\Delta Y_{I_{u}}
and
(V_{u}Y)_{T}=0=Y_{T}
, and furthermore
(V_{u}Y)_{t}>0
for every
t\in(0,T)
.
We say that
Y
realises a weak local minimum at a time
r
if there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
Y_{s}\geqslant Y_{r-}
for every
s\in[r-\varepsilon,r+\varepsilon]\cap[0,T]
; if moreover the inequality is strict except maybe for
s=r
, we say that
Y
realises a strict local minimum at a time
r
. Note that this may include the case when
Y
has a positive jump at time
r
. Again the denomination is slightly incorrect (one should in fact replace
Y
by its left-continuous version).

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
Y
be a càdlàg path on
[-T,T]
, null on
[-T,0)
, with no negative jump, and with
Y_{0}=\varrho\geqslant 0
and
Y_{T}=0
.

(i)

If there exists a unique
I=I_{0}\in[0,T]
such that
Y_{I-}=\inf Y
, then the function
V_{0}
is continuous at
Y
.

(ii)

If
u\in(0,\varrho)
and
Y
does not realise a weak local minimum at time
I_{u}
, then the function
V:(Y,u)\mapsto V_{u}Y
is continuous at the pair
(Y,u)
. Moreover the shifted path satisfies
(V_{u}Y)_{0}=Y_{0}=\varrho
.

Proof.

The first part of the lemma is
[
Ber01
, Lemma 3]
when furthermore
Y
is continuous at
I_{0}
. It extends when
\Delta Y_{I_{0}}>0
thanks to our definition of the shift. Indeed, let
(Y^{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
be a sequence of paths satisfying the same properties as
Y
and which converges to it in the Skorokhod’s topology. Then by uniqueness of
I_{0}
, the time
I_{0}^{n}
defined as in (
6.2
) for
Y^{n}
converges to
I_{0}
and
Y^{n}_{I^{n}_{0}-}\to Y_{I_{0}-}
. Moreover there exists an instant
t^{n}\geqslant I^{n}_{0}
which also converges to
I_{0}
such that both
Y^{n}_{t^{n}-}\to Y_{I_{0}-}
and
Y^{n}_{t^{n}}\to Y_{I_{0}}
.
Consequently, the path
Y^{n}
shifted at time
I^{n}_{0}
is close when
n\to\infty
to the same path shifted at time
t^{n}
, which converges to the path
Y
shifted at time
I_{0}
.
For the second claim, since
Y
does not realise a weak local minimum at time
I_{u}
, then if
u_{n}
converges to
u
, then
I_{u_{n}}
converges to
I_{u}
. Note also that since
Y
makes no negative jump, then it is continuous at
I_{u}
. We conclude from Lemma
6.1
.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

Let us note that, as any real-valued function, the
times
at which
Y
realises a strict local minimum are at most countable, and so are the
values
of the weak local minima (since there is at most one such value in each open time interval with rational edges). Therefore, for any
Y
, the second claim holds true for every
u\in(0,\varrho)
outside a countable set.

6.2.
Bridges with exchangeable increments

Let
Y^{n}
be a uniformly random path satisfying:

(i)

Y^{n}_{0}=\varrho_{n}
and
\#\{1\leqslant i\leqslant E_{n}:\Delta Y^{n}_{i}=k\}=\mathrm{f}_{n}(k)
for every
k\geqslant 0
,

(ii)

Y^{n}_{t}=Y^{n}_{\lfloor t\rfloor}-(t-\lfloor t\rfloor)
for every
t\in[0,E_{n}]
.

Recall that our Łukasiewicz paths
X^{n}
further satisfy
X^{n}_{t}>0
for every
t\in[0,E_{n})
. The path
Y^{n}
can simply be obtained by taking
(\Delta Y^{n}_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant E_{n}}
to be a uniform random permutation of
(\mathrm{f}_{n,i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant E_{n}}
. By definition, these increments are exchangeable: their joint law is invariant under permutation. In continuous time, a random process
Y
on the interval
[0,1]
, is said to have
exchangeable increments
when for every
k\geqslant 1
, the law of the increments
(Y_{i/k}-Y_{(i-1)/k})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant k}
is exchangeable.
According to
[
Kal02
, Theorem 16.21]
, such random processes which start at
\varrho
and end at
0
admit the following representation: for every
t\in[0,1]
,

Y_{t}=\varrho(1-t)+\theta_{0}b_{t}+\sum_{i\geqslant 1}\theta_{i}(\mathbf{1}_{\{U_{i}\leqslant t\}}-t),

(6.4)

where
b
is a standard Brownian bridge, the
U_{i}
’s are i.i.d. uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
, and
\varrho
and the
\theta_{i}
’s are (possibly dependent) random real numbers with
\sum_{i}\theta_{i}^{2}<\infty
a.s. and the three groups
b
,
(U_{i})_{i}
and
(\varrho,(\theta_{i})_{i})
are independent.
In our case,
\varrho
and the
\theta_{i}
’s are deterministic and nonnegative.

Recall the (abusively called) first passage times
I_{u}
for
u\in[0,\varrho]
from (
6.2
).
In the case
\varrho=0
, by the work of Knight
[
Kni96
]
, the process
Y
admits a.s. a unique instant of infimum
I=I_{0}
.
Then by Lemma
6.2
the Vervaat transform
V_{0}
is a.s. continuous at
Y
and the
excursion
path

X=V_{0}Y

satisfies
X_{0}=\Delta Y_{I}\geqslant 0
,
X_{1}=0
, and
X_{t}>0
for every
t\in(0,1)
. According to Bertoin
[
Ber01
, Section 3.2]
, the path
Y
is continuous at
I
, and so
X_{0}=0
, as soon as

\text{either}\quad\theta_{0}>0,\qquad\text{or}\quad\sum_{i\geqslant 1}\theta_{i}=\infty,

(6.5)

which is known to be equivalent to requiring that
Y
has infinite variation paths. On the other hand in the case of finite variation paths,
\Delta Y_{I}>0
a.s. as explained by Miermont
[
Mie01
]
, see Corollary 1 and the discussion before there; precisely
\Delta Y_{I}
in this case has the law of a size biased pick from the sequence of jump values
(\theta_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
.

Let us next consider the case
\varrho>0
.
Let us mention Proposition 1 in
[
CUB15
]
for a less restrictive sufficient condition for
\Delta Y_{I}=0
and Theorem 2 in
[
AHUB20a
]
which relates continuity at the infimum and regularity at
0
. We shall not use these results since in this case, we instead sample
U
uniformly at random on
[0,\varrho]
and independently of
Y
and define the
first-passage bridge
version of
Y
as the random path

X=V_{U}Y=\vartheta_{I_{U}}Y.

(6.6)

It is classical that a.s. every local minimum of the Brownian motion is a strict local minimum, so there only are countably many times of local minimum. The proof can be adapted to the path
Y
whenever
\theta_{0}>0
. We shall not consider this question when
\theta_{0}=0
and only recall from Remark
6.3
that the
values
of the local minima are always countable.
Indeed, we infer by combining Lemma
6.2
and Remark
6.3
that since
U
has a diffuse law, then a.s. the Vervaat transform
V:(Y,u)\mapsto V_{u}Y
is continuous at the pair
(Y,U)
and moreover

X_{0}=\varrho
,
X_{1}=0
, and
X_{t}>0
for every
t\in(0,1)
.

A key feature is that in both cases, the path
X
and the random time
I_{0}
or
I_{U}
are independent, and the latter has the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
, see e.g.
[
Ber01
, Equation 3.14]
.

Let us mention that when
\varrho=0
Theorem 3 in
[
AHUB20a
]
provides a sufficient condition, weaker than finite variation paths, for
X=V_{0}Y
to really be the excursion version of
Y
, in the sense that it is the weak limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
of the process
Y
conditioned on the event
\{\inf Y>-\varepsilon\}
. When
\varrho>0
, it is argued under more technical assumptions in
[
BCP03
]
that
X
defined in (
6.6
) is similarly the first-passage bridge version of
Y
.
Again we do not consider this interesting question in general and only content ourselves with this construction of
X
.

Recall from the previous sections that the continuous part
C
of
X
as in (
3.3
) plays a role in the construction of the labelled looptrees and the pure jump case, when this path is constant null has been treated in Section
4
. An important question is then to characterise when
X
satisfies (
PJ
).

Lemma 6.4
.

Suppose that
\sum_{i}\theta_{i}<\infty
, then the following holds:

(i)

Either
\theta_{0}>0
and then almost surely, for Lebesgue almost every
t\in[0,1]
, we have
C_{t}>0
,

(ii)

Or
\theta_{0}=0
and then almost surely
C_{t}=0
for all
t\in[0,1]
.

Remark 6.5
.

We believe that this result can be strengthened into the following directions, although we were enable to prove it: regardless the convergence of the sum
\sum_{i}\theta_{i}
, almost surely, for all
t\in[0,1]
,

(i)

C_{t}=0
if and only if
X_{t}=\min_{s\leqslant t}X_{s}
when
\theta_{0}>0
;

(ii)

C_{t}=0
when
\theta_{0}=0
.

Both are known to hold in the case of Lévy processes, which are mixtures of such processes in which the
\theta_{i}
’s are random, see Proposition
8.1
.

Proof of Lemma
6.4
.

Since
C
has continuous paths, the claims are equivalent to showing that if
T
is an independent random time with the uniform distribution, then
C_{T}>0
a.s. or
C_{T}=0
a.s. according as wether
\theta_{0}>0
.
Recall that
1-T
can be taken as the time at which we cyclically shift
Y
to produce
X
, then by time-reversal of
Y
,
C_{T}
has the same law as the Lebesgue measure of the range of the supremum process of
Y
, namely
\mathrm{Leb}(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}Y_{s};t\in[0,1]\})
.

Now when
\sum_{i}\theta_{i}<\infty
, the process
Y
can be written in the following form

Y_{t}=\varrho+b_{t}^{\prime}+Y_{t}^{\prime}\qquad\text{where}\qquad b_{t}^{\prime}=\theta_{0}b_{t}-\bigg(\varrho+\sum_{i\geqslant 1}\theta_{i}\bigg)t\qquad\text{and}\qquad Y_{t}^{\prime}=\sum_{i\geqslant 1}\theta_{i}\mathbf{1}_{\{U_{i}\leqslant t\}},

so
b^{\prime}
is a Brownian bridge with a drift and
Y^{\prime}
is a nondecreasing process (and both are independent).
Suppose first that
\theta_{0}=0
, so
b^{\prime}
reduces to a negative linear drift. Arguing as in Section
4.1
, removing the (finitely many) jumps larger than a small
\delta>0
, the sum of the other jumps can be made arbitrarily small and this sum is certainly larger than or equal to the Lebesgue measure of the range of the supremum process. This proves the second claim.

As for the first claim, using e.g. the law of the iterated logarithm for the Brownian motion combined with standard absolute continuity arguments to transfer it to the bridge version, we have that
\limsup_{t\downarrow 0}(2t\ln\ln 1/t)^{-1/2}b_{t}=1
a.s. In particular the negative drift is negligible and
b^{\prime}
reaches positive values at arbitrarily small times and this shows that the Lebesgue measure of the range of the supremum process after removing the jumps larger than
\delta
, does not converge to
0
.
∎

Let us note that even when
\sum_{i}\theta_{i}=\infty
, it holds that
\limsup_{t\downarrow 0}(2t\ln\ln 1/t)^{-1/2}\sum_{i}\theta_{i}(\mathbf{1}_{\{U_{i}\leqslant t\}}-t)=0
a.s. so the Brownian bridge part dominates at small times, which may eventually lead to the first claim in this regime, but we loose here the monotonicity of
Y^{\prime}
which was used in the previous proof.

6.3.
Scaling limits of random paths

Let
Y^{n}
be as above: a càdlàg path on
[0,E_{n}]
started from
Y^{n}_{0}=\varrho_{n}
, which only jumps at integer times, as a uniform random permutation of the sequence
\mathbf{f}_{n}=(\mathrm{f}_{n,i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant E_{n}}
, and decreases at unit speed otherwise.
Independently, sample
U_{n}
uniformly at random in
[0,\varrho_{n}]
and define
X^{n}
as for the Vervaat transform (
6.6
) of
Y^{n}
, except that the first passage time
I_{U_{n}}
defined in (
6.2
) is replaced by its integer part
\lfloor I_{U_{n}}\rfloor
.
Necessarily, the path
Y^{n}
is continuous at time
\lfloor I_{U_{n}}\rfloor
and precisely
Y^{n}
is linear with slope
-1
in the open interval
(\lfloor I_{U_{n}}\rfloor-1,\lfloor I_{U_{n}}\rfloor+1)
so
X^{n}_{0}=\varrho_{n}
.
Then
X^{n}
has the same law as the Łukasiewicz path of a uniformly random looptree with root cycle with length
\varrho_{n}
and other cycle length prescribed by
\mathbf{f}_{n}
.

The convergence criterion from
[
Kal02
, Theorem 16.23]
can be written as follows. Fix any sequence
a_{n}\to\infty
; then the rescaled processes
(a_{n}^{-1}Y^{n}_{E_{n}t})_{t\in[-1,1]}
converge in distribution in the Skorokhod topology to some limit, say
Y
, if and only if there exist
\theta_{0}\geqslant 0
and
\theta_{1}\geqslant\theta_{2}\geqslant\dots\geqslant 0
with
\sum_{i\geqslant 1}\theta_{i}^{2}<\infty
such that, as
n\to\infty
,

a_{n}^{-1}\varrho_{n}\to\varrho,\qquad a_{n}^{-1}\mathrm{f}_{n,i}\to\theta_{i}\quad\text{for every}\enskip i\geqslant 1,\qquad\text{and}\qquad a_{n}^{-2}\sum_{k\geqslant 0}k(k-1)\mathrm{f}_{n}(k)\to\sum_{i\geqslant 0}\theta_{i}^{2}.

Furthermore, in this case, the limit
Y
takes the form (
6.4
) with also
Y_{t}=0
for every
t<0
.
To be precise and fit the framework of
[
Kal02
, Theorem 16.23]
we should replace
Y^{n}
by a path that at any integer time
i\in\{1,\dots,E_{n}+1\}
makes a jump with value
\Delta Y^{n}_{i-1}-1
and remains constant otherwise; then the quoted theorem stipulates that the above statement holds, with only the last condition being replaced by
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}^{-2}\sum_{k\geqslant 0}(k-1)^{2}\mathrm{f}_{n}(k)=\sum_{i\geqslant 0}\theta_{i}^{2}
. On the one hand, the difference between the two series equals
\sum_{k\geqslant 0}k(k-1)\mathrm{f}_{n}(k)-\sum_{k\geqslant 0}(k-1)^{2}\mathrm{f}_{n}(k)=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}(k-1)\mathrm{f}_{n}(k)=-\varrho_{n}
, which we assume is of order
a_{n}
and therefore small compared to
a_{n}^{2}
. On the other hand, the two paths lie at Skorokhod distance
1
from each other, hence one converges after scaling if and only if the other one does, and with the same limit.

Considering this convergence criterion, let us define

\sigma_{n}^{2}=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}k(k-1)\mathrm{f}_{n}(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{E_{n}}\mathrm{f}_{n,i}(\mathrm{f}_{n,i}-1).

(6.7)

We shall assume that
\sigma_{n}^{2}\to\infty
, as if it is bounded our objects are too degenerate.
We conclude from the preceding results that the following holds. The case
\varrho=0
can also be found in
[
AHUB20b
, Proposition 1]
.
Let
\varrho\geqslant 0
,
\theta_{0}\geqslant 0
, and
\theta_{1}\geqslant\theta_{2}\geqslant\dots\geqslant 0
be such that
\sum_{i\geqslant 0}\theta_{i}^{2}=1
and define
X=V_{U}Y
as in (
6.6
), where
Y
is as in (
6.4
) and
U
is independently sampled uniformly at random on
[0,\varrho]
.

Proposition 6.6
.

Suppose that, as
n\to\infty
, we have
\sigma_{n}^{2}\to\infty
and

\sigma_{n}^{-1}\varrho_{n}\to\varrho\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{n}^{-1}\mathrm{f}_{n,i}\to\theta_{i}\quad\text{for every}\enskip i\geqslant 1.

Then
(\sigma_{n}^{-1}X^{n}_{E_{n}t})_{t\in[-1,1]}
converges in distribution to
X
in the Skorokhod topology.

Proof.

Under the assumptions, the uniformly permuted path
\sigma_{n}^{-1}Y^{n}
converges in distribution to
Y
, and (jointly) the independent random height
\sigma_{n}^{-1}U_{n}
converges to
U
. The convergence in distribution of the first-passage bridges
\sigma_{n}^{-1}X^{n}
to
X
then follows from the almost sure continuity of the Vervaat transform at
(Y,U)
provided by combining Remark
6.3
and Lemma
6.2
.
∎

Remark 6.7
.

When the largest cycle length is
\mathrm{f}_{n,1}=o(\sigma_{n})
, then
\theta_{i}=0
for every
i\geqslant 1
, so
\theta_{0}=1
and then
X
reduces to the Brownian first passage bridge; in this case, the result was proved in
[

, Proposition 1]
and previously in

7.2.

Theorem 7.4

Suppose that, as

holds in the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov topology.

\Xi^{\ell}_{j}

under the conditional law

for every

as

Theorem 7.9
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{E}[|\xi|^{4+\varepsilon}]<\infty
for some
\varepsilon>0
and that

\sigma_{n}^{-1}\varrho_{n}\to\varrho,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{n}^{-1}\mathrm{f}_{n,i}\to\theta_{i}\quad\text{for every}\enskip i\geqslant 1.

Finally assume that there exists

such that

where

Then the convergence in distribution

\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}}X^{n}_{E_{n}t},\frac{1}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\xi)\sigma_{n}}}Z^{n}_{E_{n}t}\right)_{t\in[-1,1]}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{(d)}_{n\to\infty}\enskip(X,Z^{\Sigma^{2}/3}),

holds for the Skorokhod topology.

We shall see that the main point of the proof Theorem

is to show a conditional central limit theorem, given

, for a sum of independent random variables of the form
\Xi^{\ell}_{k}
, where each
\ell
, each

, and the number of terms itself are measurable with respect to

and an independent sample of i.i.d. uniform random times in
[0,1]
. A useful idea to prove such a convergence is to replace these random variables by Gaussian random variables with the same variance, for which the CLT holds as soon as the sum of the variances converges once suitably rescaled. Since these variances are measurable with respect to
X^{n}
(and the independent uniform random times), this removes one layer of randomness. The next lemma bounds the cost of such a replacement.

Lemma 7.10
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{E}[|\xi|^{3}]<\infty
. Then there exists a constant
K>0

such that the following holds:
Let
h\geqslant 1
and
k_{m}\geqslant j_{m}\geqslant 1
for every
1\leqslant m\leqslant h
and sample independently
\Xi^{1,k_{1}}_{j_{1}},\dots,\Xi^{h,k_{h}}_{j_{h}}
, where each
\Xi^{m,k_{m}}_{j_{m}}
has the law of
\xi_{1}+\dots+\xi_{j_{m}}
under the conditional law
\mathbb{P}(\,\cdot\mid\xi_{1}+\dots+\xi_{k_{m}}=0)
,
then for every
z\in\mathbb{R}
,

\bigg|\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\bigg(\operatorname{i}z\sum_{m=1}^{h}\Xi^{m,k_{m}}_{j_{m}}\bigg)\bigg]-\exp\bigg(-\frac{z^{2}}{2}\sum_{m=1}^{h}\operatorname{Var}(\Xi^{m,k_{m}}_{j_{m}})\bigg)\bigg|\leqslant K|z|^{3}\sqrt{\max_{1\leqslant m\leqslant h}k_{m}}\sum_{m=1}^{h}(k_{m}-j_{m}).

Proof.

We use the fact that for any random variable,
X
say, which is centred and has finite third moment, it holds that

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\operatorname{i}zX\right)\right]-\left(1-\frac{z^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[X^{2}\right]\right)\right|\leqslant\frac{|z|^{3}}{6}\mathbb{E}\left[|X|^{3}\right].

When
X
has the standard Gaussian law, so
\mathbb{E}[|X|^{3}]=\sqrt{8/\pi}\leqslant 2
, we obtain using also Jensen’s inequality that for any
k\geqslant j\geqslant 1
,

\left|\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{2}\operatorname{Var}(\Xi^{k}_{j})\right)-\left(1-\frac{z^{2}}{2}\operatorname{Var}(\Xi^{k}_{j})\right)\right|\leqslant\frac{|z|^{3}}{6}\mathbb{E}\big[|\Xi^{k}_{j}|^{2}\big]^{3/2}\sqrt{\frac{8}{\pi}}\leqslant\frac{|z|^{3}}{3}\mathbb{E}\big[|\Xi^{k}_{j}|^{3}\big].

The same bound applied to
\Xi^{k}_{j}
and the triangle inequality then yield

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\operatorname{i}z\,\Xi^{k}_{j})\right]-\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{2}\operatorname{Var}(\Xi^{k}_{j})\right)\right|\leqslant\frac{|z|^{3}}{2}\mathbb{E}\big[|\Xi^{k}_{j}|^{3}\big].

On the other hand, one can show by induction that for any complex numbers in the closed unit disk
(\alpha_{m})_{1\leqslant m\leqslant h}
and
(\beta_{m})_{1\leqslant m\leqslant h}
, it holds that
|\prod_{1\leqslant m\leqslant h}\alpha_{m}-\prod_{1\leqslant m\leqslant h}\beta_{m}|\leqslant\sum_{1\leqslant m\leqslant h}|\alpha_{m}-\beta_{m}|
. We infer that

\bigg|\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\bigg(\operatorname{i}z\sum_{m=1}^{h}\Xi^{m,k_{m}}_{j_{m}}\bigg)\bigg]-\exp\bigg(-\frac{z^{2}}{2}\sum_{m=1}^{h}\operatorname{Var}(\Xi^{k_{m}}_{j_{m}})\bigg)\bigg|\leqslant\frac{|z|^{3}}{2}\sum_{m=1}^{h}\mathbb{E}\big[|\Xi^{m,k_{m}}_{j_{m}}|^{3}\big].

Property
(i)
of the bridges, below Equation (
4.2
), shows that there exists a constant
K_{3}
such that for every
k\geqslant j\geqslant 1
, we have

\mathbb{E}[|\Xi^{k}_{j}|^{3}]\leqslant K_{3}|k-j|^{3/2}\leqslant K_{3}\sqrt{k}|k-j|.

Our claim then follows.
∎

Let us mention that a better bound in the first line of the proof allows to extend the claim assuming only that
\mathbb{E}[|\xi|^{2+\varepsilon}]<\infty
for some
\varepsilon>0
, with
|z|^{2+\varepsilon}\max_{1\leqslant m\leqslant h}k_{m}^{\varepsilon/2}

for some

\left(\operatorname{Var}(\xi)\sigma_{n}\right)^{-1/2}\left(Z^{n}_{E_{n}U_{1}},\dots,Z^{n}_{E_{n}U_{q}}\right)\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{(d)}_{n\to\infty}\enskip(Z_{U_{1}},\dots,Z_{U_{q}}),

(7.6)

and

Recall from the introduction that in this case Marckert & Miermont

Theorem 7.12
.

Suppose that

\sigma_{n}^{2}\to\infty
, and that

\sigma_{n}^{-1}\varrho_{n}\to\varrho,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{n}^{-1}\mathrm{f}_{n,i}\to\theta_{i}\quad\text{for every}\enskip i\geqslant 1.

(i)

The convergence in distribution

\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\sigma_{n}}}d_{\mathrm{gr}}(v^{n}_{\star},v^{n}_{\lfloor\mathrm{f}_{n}(0)t\rfloor})\right)_{t\in[0,1]}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{(d)}_{n\to\infty}\enskip\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}-\min\mathbf{Z}\right)_{t\in[0,1]},

holds for the uniform topology. Consequently,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\sigma_{n}}}d_{M^{n}}(v^{n}_{\star},\vec{e}_{n})\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{(d)}_{n\to\infty}\enskip-\min\mathbf{Z}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\sigma_{n}}}\max_{v\in V(M^{n})}d_{M^{n}}(v^{n}_{\star},v)\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{(d)}_{n\to\infty}\enskip\max\mathbf{Z}-\min\mathbf{Z},

and for every continuous and bounded function
F
, we have

\frac{1}{\mathrm{f}_{n}(0)}\sum_{v\in V(M^{n})}F\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\sigma_{n}}}d_{M^{n}}(v^{n}_{\star},v)\right)\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{(d)}_{n\to\infty}\enskip\int_{0}^{1}F(\mathbf{Z}_{t}-\min\mathbf{Z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(ii)

From every increasing sequence of integers, one can extract a subsequence along which the convergence in distribution

\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\sigma_{n}}}d_{n}(\mathrm{f}_{n}(0)s,\mathrm{f}_{n}(0)t)\right)_{s,t\in[0,1]}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{(d)}_{n\to\infty}\enskip\left(D_{\infty}(s,t)\right)_{s,t\in[0,1]},

holds for the uniform topology, where
D_{\infty}
is a random continuous pseudo-distance which satisfies

D_{\infty}(U,\cdot)\enskip\mathop{=}^{(d)}\enskip\mathbf{Z}-\min\mathbf{Z},

where
U
is sampled uniformly at random on
[0,1]
and independently of the rest.
Finally along this subsequence, we have

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\sigma_{n}}}M^{n}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{(d)}_{n\to\infty}\enskip{}[0,1]/\{D_{\infty}=0\}

in the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov topology.

Proof.

Combining Theorem
7.9
, the convergence (
5.1
), and Skorokhod’s representation theorem, we may assume the almost sure convergence

\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\sigma_{n}}}Z^{n}_{nt},\frac{1}{E_{n}}\lambda^{n}(\mathrm{f}_{n}(0)t)\right)_{t\in[0,1]}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{a.s.}_{n\to\infty}\enskip\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t},t\right)_{t\in[0,1]}.

The first claim then follows from Lemma
2.5
and the second one from Lemma
2.6
.
For the identity in law
D_{\infty}(U,\cdot)=\mathbf{Z}-\min\mathbf{Z}
, the left hand side is the limit in law of the rescaled distances between the vertices
v^{n}_{1},\dots,v^{n}_{\mathrm{f}_{n}(0)}
of

and a uniformly random vertex

in this set. As remarked in Section

, the distinguished vertex

has the uniform distribution on the set of all vertices of

and is independent of the latter. Therefore we can couple

and

in such a way that they differ with a probability at most

. Then replacing
u_{n}
by

, we conclude from the first claim.
∎

8.1.

Let

We shall work in the same setting as Duquesne & Le Gall

]
and consider such a process with no negative jump and whose law is determined by its Laplace transform given for all
t,\lambda\geqslant 0
, by

\mathbb{E}[\exp(-\lambda X_{t})]=\exp(t\psi(\lambda))
, where

\psi(\lambda)=d\lambda+\beta\lambda^{2}+\int_{(0,\infty)}(\operatorname{e}^{-\lambda r}-1+\lambda r)\pi(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r),

where
d\geqslant 0
is the drift coefficient,
\beta\geqslant 0
is the Gaussian coefficient, and finally
\pi
is the Lévy measure, which satisfies
\int_{(0,\infty)}\min\{r,r^{2}\}\pi(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r)<\infty
. This integrability condition, together with the nonnegativity of
d
, ensures that the path is either recurrent, when
d=0
, or drifts to
-\infty
otherwise (this is a kind of subcritical regime); this ensures that the path has no finite lower bound so one can easily consider excursions and first passage-bridges. Finally the path has finite variation if and only if
\beta=0
and
\int_{(0,1)}r\pi(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r)<\infty
.
In the case
\psi(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}
, the process
X
is
\sqrt{2}
times a standard Brownian motion; more generally the case
\psi(\lambda)=\lambda^{\alpha}
where
\alpha\in(1,2]
is known as that of stable Lévy processes with index
\alpha
.

From any such Lévy process,
Duquesne & Le Gall
[
DLG02
]
constructed the so-called
height process

H
and studied thoroughly its properties. If (an excursion of)
X
is the analogue of the Łukasiewicz path of a plane tree, then (an excursion of)
H
is the analogue of its height or contour process, and the continuum Lévy tree is
T_{H}=[0,1]/\{d_{H}=0\}
, where
d_{H}
is defined in (
3.4
).
Recall the notation at the very beginning of Section
3
, in particular that for every
t\in[0,1]
, we write the running supremum of the dual path
\overline{\mathsf{X}}\vphantom{X}^{t}
as the sum of a pure jump path
J^{t}
and a continuous path
C^{t}
; then
X_{t-}=J^{t}_{t}+C^{t}_{t}=J_{t}+C_{t}
.
As shown in the next proposition, Lévy processes satisfy the pure jump property (
PJ
) if and only if they have no Gaussian component, which in this case answers Remark
6.5
.

Proposition 8.1
.

For any such Lévy process, it holds:

(i)

Either
\beta=0
and then
C=0
almost surely,

(ii)

Or
\beta>0
and then
C_{t}=\beta H_{t}=\mathrm{Leb}(\{\min_{r\in[s,t]}X_{s};s\in[0,t]\})
, which vanishes only at times
t
such that
X_{t}=\min_{s\leqslant t}X_{s}
.

Proof.

Fix
t\in[0,1]
and first note that the dual process
X^{t}
has the same law as
X
.
According to
[
DLG02
, Lemma 1.1.2]
, its ladder height process is a subordinator with
drift coefficient
\beta
, and the range of the ladder process and the running supremum agree except on a set which is at most countable. Therefore if
\beta=0
, then the ladder process is a pure jump process and so
\overline{\mathsf{X}}\vphantom{X}^{t}=J^{t}
. On the other hand, if
\beta>0
, then
\overline{\mathsf{X}}\vphantom{X}^{t}
has a continuous part, given by
\beta
times the local time at the supremum of the dual process, which is exactly the height process
[
DLG02
, Definition 1.2.1]
.
The second claim is then
[
DLG02
, Equation 14]
.
∎

We next want to consider conditioned Lévy processes.
Under the two assumptions that
(i) the point
0
is regular for both
(0,\infty)
and
(-\infty,0)
, which holds since
X
has no negative jump and infinite variation
[
Ber96
, Chapter VII]
, and
(ii) that
\int|\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{e}^{iuX_{t}}]|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u<\infty
for every
t>0
,
it is shown in
[
UB14
]
that one can construct bridge and excursion versions of
X
, i.e. paths
X^{\rm br}
and
X^{\rm ex}
which are regular conditional distributions of
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
, conditioned on
X_{0}=X_{1}=0
for the former and moreover on
X_{t}>0
for all
t\in(0,1)
for the latter. Moreover, the bridge is locally absolutely continuous with respect to the unconditioned path
[
UB14
, Equation 2.1]
and then the excursion is obtained from the bridge via the Vervaat transform
[
UB14
, Theorem 4]
, therefore Proposition
8.1
remains valid for both processes. More generally one can construct first-passage bridges versions from
\varrho>0
to
0
, which satisfy
X_{0}=\varrho
,
X_{1}=0
, and
X_{t}>0
for every
t\in(0,1)
.

We may therefore construct as in Section
3
the looptree associated with such a process
X
, or with a bridge or excursion version, and then add random Gaussian labels on it.
We study more these Lévy labelled looptrees and associated maps in the forthcoming paper
[
KM
]
; in particular we prove the regularity assumption (
3.8
) on the looptree distance with the optimal exponent and we calculate the fractal (Hausdorff, Minkowski, packing) dimensions of the looptree and the corresponding map in terms of the so-called Blumenthal–Getoor exponents of
X
, which generalise the Brownian and stable cases
[
CK14
,
LGM11
]
.

8.2.
Boltzmann distributions and random degree sequences

Marckert & Miermont
[
MM07
]
defined laws on pointed bipartite plane maps which are designed so the maps have random face degrees, called
Boltzmann distributions
. Let us also refer to
[
BM17
]
for more details, in the context of maps with a boundary as well as to
[
Mar18b
]
for a presentation closer to the present work. The model is parameterised by a sequence
\mathbf{q}=(q_{k})_{k\geqslant 0}
of nonnegative real numbers which in a sense plays the role of the offspring distribution for Bienaymé–Galton–Watson random trees. More precisely, the
\mathbf{q}
-Boltzmann distribution on finite pointed maps with boundary length
2\varrho
is defined as

\mathbb{P}^{\varrho}(M,v_{\star})=\frac{1}{W^{\varrho}}\prod_{f\text{ inner face}}q_{\mathrm{deg}(f)/2},

where
W^{\varrho}
is a normalising constant. For
\mathbb{P}^{\varrho}
to be well-defined, this constant has to be finite, and this can be checked analytically on the sequence
\mathbf{q}
, see
[
MM07
, Proposition 1]
, recast in
[
Mar18b
, Proposition 9]
.

By the bijection from Lemma
2.2
, such a random pointed map is related to a labelled (loop)tree whose offspring distribution, say
\mu_{\mathbf{q}}
, is explicit
[
MM07
,
Mar18b
]
. One can then condition such a map to have “size”
S=N
, in the sense of having
N
vertices, or
N
edges, or
N
faces, or even having
N
faces whose degree belongs to a given set
A\in 2\mathbb{N}
, and possibly other faces, but with a degree in
2\mathbb{N}\setminus A
. By Lemma
2.2
, this corresponds to conditioning the looptree on its number of vertices, edges, or loops, i.e. conditioning the corresponding plane tree on its number of leaves, edges, or internal vertices respectively.
Such conditionings have been especially studied by Kortchemski
[
Kor12
]
, relying on the Łukasiewicz path, as well as Rizzolo
[
Riz15
]
using a different approach.

Note that one can consider the same law
\mathbb{P}^{\varrho}
on non-pointed maps, with a different normalising constant, and further distinguish a vertex uniformly at random. Since the number of vertices of the map is random — unless one condition on it — then sampling a pointed map induces a bias on the total number of vertices; however it is now classical that this bias vanishes in the large
N
limit in many models and a pointed Boltzmann is close to a non pointed one in which one further distinguish a vertex uniformly at random, see
[
Mar19
, Proposition 6.4]
, adapted from
[
BJM14
,
Abr16
,
BM17
,
Mar18b
]
.

Thanks the preceding remark, one can focus on pointed maps and thus rely on the bijection from Lemma
2.2
.
Let
\mathrm{f}_{n,1}\geqslant\mathrm{f}_{n,2}\geqslant\dots\geqslant 0
denote the ranked outdegrees of the associated conditioned random labelled forest, which, for the nonzero terms, are the cycle lengths of the looptree and the half-face degrees of the pointed map.
The key observation is that these
\mathrm{f}_{n,i}
’s and
n
itself (unless we condition on the number of edges) are random, but conditional on these numbers, the forests, looptrees, and pointed maps have the uniform distribution with these degrees. Therefore, using e.g. Skorokhod’s representation theorem, one can apply our previous general results conditionally given these degrees as soon as they satisfy the assumptions with convergences in distribution, where the
\theta_{i}
’s are a priori random. Note that the scaling factor
\sigma_{n}^{2}=\sum_{i}\mathrm{f}_{n,i}(\mathrm{f}_{n,i}-1)
is also random, however in several cases it behaves asymptotically deterministically, by a kind of law of large numbers.

8.2.1.
The Brownian regime

According to
[
Kor12
, Theorem 8.1]
when
\varrho=1
and the law
\mu_{\mathbf{q}}
is critical and has finite variance, the Łukasiewicz path rescaled by some constant times
N^{-1/2}
converges in distribution towards a Brownian excursion.
Moreover in this case, as shown in
[
Mar19
, Section 6]
the random scaling
\sigma_{n}^{2}
divided by
N
converges in probability to a constant. We deduce that our results apply, when
\sigma_{n}^{2}
is replaced by this constant times
N
, and where
\theta_{1}=0
so
\theta_{0}=1
. In particular in this case, the trees converge to the Brownian tree at a scaling of order
N^{1/2}
by Corollary
7.3
, so do the looptrees by Corollary
7.6
, the labels converge to the head of the Brownian snake at a scaling of order
N^{1/4}
by Theorem
7.9
, and finally the maps then converge to the Brownian sphere at the same scaling by Theorem
7.12
. This can be generalised to forests with
\varrho_{n}\sim\varrho N^{1/2}
trees with
\varrho\in[0,\infty)
.
More generally
[
Kor12
, Theorem 8.1]
treats the case when
\mu_{\mathbf{q}}
is critical and belongs to
the domain of attraction of a Gaussian law
, which means that
(j^{2}\sum_{k\geqslant j}\mu_{\mathbf{q}}(k))_{j\geqslant 1}
is a
slowly varying sequence
. The previous results extend, except tightness of trees, with all scaling factors multiplied by some slowly varying sequence.
We refer to
[
Mar19
, Section 6]
for details.

8.2.2.
The stable regimes

Theorem 8.1 in
[
Kor12
]
also treats the case when
\mu_{\mathbf{q}}
is critical and belongs to the domain of attraction of a stable law with index
\alpha\in(1,2)
, i.e. when
(j^{\alpha}\sum_{k\geqslant j}\mu_{\mathbf{q}}(k))_{j\geqslant 1}
is a slowly varying sequence. In this case the Łukasiewicz path, rescaled by some other slowly varying sequence times
N^{-1/\alpha}
converges in distribution towards the excursion of a stable Lévy process with index
\alpha
as defined in Section
8.1
. Les us mention that when the map is conditioned on having
N
faces with degree in a set
A
, then it is assumed in
[
Kor12
]
that either
A
or its complement is finite, however Thévenin
[
Thé20
]
lifted this restriction.
As opposed to the Brownian regime, the stable Lévy excursion admits positive jumps, so now the
\theta_{i}
’s are positive for
i\geqslant 1
and random, and satisfy
\sum_{i}\theta_{i}=\infty
a.s., and finally
\theta_{0}=0
. By Proposition
8.1
and the remark after, these processes and their conditioned versions satisfy (
PJ
), so we can apply our results from Section
4
. In this case the stable looptrees are defined in
[
CK14
]
, whereas the limit of the label process is the “continuous distance process” in
[
LGM11
]
. Finally, as in the Brownian regime, one can replace the random scaling factor of labels and maps
\sigma_{n}^{1/2}
by the deterministic one
N^{-1/(2\alpha)}
times a slowly varying sequence, see again
[
Mar19
, Section 6]
for details. We stress that this reference could only provide an abstract tightness argument whereas our present results now allow to recover (and extend to more general conditionings and boundaries) the results of
[
LGM11
,
Mar18a
]
.
Let us mention that as in these reference, the convergence of the maps holds only after extraction of a subsequence and proving the uniqueness of the limits in this case is under active investigation
[
CMR
]
.

8.2.3.
Condensation regimes

When
\mu_{\mathbf{q}}
has mean smaller than
1
, the situation is different and the Łukasiewicz paths of the trees conditioned to have
N
edges, now rescaled by a factor
N
, converge in distribution towards a linear slope by
[
Kor15
]
. This is also the case when
\mu_{\mathbf{q}}
is critical and belongs to the domain of attraction of a Cauchy distribution by
[
KR19
]
.
In both cases we have
\theta_{0}=1
and Corollary
7.8
applies: the rescaled looptrees converge towards circles. Then random labels on them converge towards Brownian bridges and the associated maps converge to the Brownian CRT. We refer again to
[
Mar19
, Section 6]
as well as
[
JS15
]
for the original result on subcritical random maps.

8.2.4.
Non stable Lévy processes

In the recent paper
[
KM21
]
we consider maps conditioned to have
N
edges
and
an arbitrary number
K_{N}+1
of vertices, and so
N-K_{N}+1
faces by Euler’s formula.
Under appropriate assumptions on both
\mathbf{q}
and
K_{N}
, the largest degree satisfies
\sigma_{n}^{-1}\mathrm{f}_{n,1}\to 0
in probability and we identify the asymptotic behaviour of
\sigma_{n}
, so the rescaled maps again converge in distribution to the Brownian sphere, see precisely
[
KM21
, Theorem 5.2 & 5.4]
. Again our results here on the one hand show the convergence in this case of the underlying looptree to the Brownian CRT, and on the other hand they also provide the convergence of the labels and thus more precise results on the geometry of these maps in the presence of macroscopic degrees, when
[
KM21
, Theorem 5.5]
, based on
[
Mar19
]
does not. Let us point out that in this particular framework the limits of the Łukasiewicz paths are simply excursions of stable Lévy processes with a drift (positive or negative, depending on
K_{N}
); a condensation phenomenon can also occur if
K_{N}
is large enough.
These natural models are the main motivation to study more these non stable Lévy objects in
[
KM
]
, which should also more generally arise as limits of size-conditioned Boltzmann maps sampled with a weight sequence which varies with the size.
