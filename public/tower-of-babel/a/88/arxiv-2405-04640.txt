Momentum non-conservation in a scalar quantum field theory with a planar θ \theta interface
Daniel G. Velázquez, R. Martínez von Dossow, Luis F. Urrutia

Abstract
Motivated by the recent interest aroused by non-dynamical axionic electrodynamics in the context of topological insulators and Weyl semimetals, we discuss a simple model of the magnetoelectric effect in terms of a θ \theta -scalar field that interacts through a delta-like potential located at a planar interface. Thus, in the bulk regions the field is constructed by standard free waves with the absence of evanescent components. These waves have to be combined into linear superposition to account for the boundary conditions at the interface in order to yield the corresponding normal modes. Our aim is twofold: first we quantize the θ \theta -scalar field using the normal modes in the canonical approach and then we look for applications emphasizing the effect of momentum non-conservation due to the presence of the interface. To this end we calculate the decay of a standard scalar particle into two θ \theta -scalar particles showing the opening of new decay channels. As a second application we deal with the two body scattering of standard charged scalar particles mediated by a θ \theta -scalar particle, focusing on the momentum non-conserving contribution of the scattering amplitude ℳ N ​ C {\cal M}^{NC} . We define a generalization of the usual cross section in order to quantify the emergence of these events. We also study the allowed kinematical region for momentum non-conservation as well as the position of the poles of the amplitude ℳ N ​ C {\cal M}^{NC} . Finally, the ratio of the magnitudes between ℳ N ​ C {\cal M}^{NC} and the momentum conserving amplitude is discussed in the appropriate region of momentum space.



I
Introduction

The study of quantum field theories in the presence of interfaces gained relevance in quantum electrodynamics, when Carniglia and Mandel
[
1
]
introduced a technique for quantizing evanescent waves (which result when a beam of light travels from a medium with high refractive index to a medium with low refractive index) in a system where each half-space, separated by the
z=0
plane, is filled with two different dielectric. The procedure is based in finding the normal modes of the equations of motion that included the contribution of the dielectric medium. Such normal modes, which now replace the standard plane wave basis, are wave triplets formed by an incident, a reflected and a transmitted wave and are called the ingoing modes. Moreover, these modes form a complete and orthogonal set which allow to quantize the electromagnetic field in terms of modified creation and annihilation operators.
Further progress was made in Ref.
[
2
]
who
showed that there are changes in the spontaneous emission rates for electric and magnetic dipole transitions of excited atoms within or near a dielectric media. They compared the vacuum quantization scheme with that of the system including the dielectric and proved that the coefficients connecting the creation and annihilation operators of both methods can be interpreted as scattering amplitudes. The wave triplet quantization scheme was successfully used in Ref.

[
3
]
, which studied the radiation of a harmonic oscillator in the presence of the same vacuum-dielectric planar interface of Refs.
[
1
,
2
]
and evaluated the radiative frequency shifts due to the interface. Later, it was observed in Ref.
[
4
]
that although the basis proposed in Ref.
[
1
]
includes interface effects and can describe sources, it was necessary to introduce a new basis (the outgoing modes) to be able to describe sinks. These new modes are obtained by making time-reversal and spatial-rotation transformations on the original wave triplet basis. Further use of the the mode triplet basis was investigated
to determine the energy-level shift of a ground-state atom in front of a nondispersive dielectric half-space

[
5
]
, and
the self-energy of a free electron in the presence of a flat dielectric surface at the level of one loop
[
6
]
. Recent progress in the study of the quantization of systems with interfaces
which include evanescent waves is reported in Ref.
[
7
]
and references therein.

Nevertheless, permittivity and permeability are not the only parameters that characterize a dielectric. A huge class of important materials, called magnetoelectrics, incorporate the magnetoelectric susceptibility (MES)
\Theta
that modifies the constitutive relations as

\bm{D}=\epsilon\bm{E}+\Theta\bm{B},\bm{H}=\frac{1}{\mu}\bm{B}-\Theta\bm{E},

giving rise to the so called magnetoelectric effect. This effect produces a coupling between the electrical and magnetic properties of a material allowing magnetic fields to generate polarization and electric fields to give rise to magnetization. The prediction of this property in antiferromagnetic materials is credited to Landau and Lifshitz
[
8
]
. The effect was also predicted in 1959 by Dzyaloshinskii
[
9
]
, and in 1960 it was confirmed experimentally in
{\rm Cr}_{2}{\rm O}_{3}
, which is indeed an antiferromagnetic material
[
10
]
. The first investigations on the magnetoelectric effect are condensed in Ref.
[
11
]
. A recent update of this study, including new methods for designing magnetoelectric materials, new experimental techniques, and theoretical concepts for understanding the magnetoelectric behavior is reported in Ref.
[
12
]
.

Unfortunately, the magnetoelectric response is very much suppressed with the respect to the normal effects due to
\epsilon
and
\mu
. This motivates the search for large magnetoelectric couplings which continues mainly in multiferroic compounds,
which are characterized by presenting spontaneous and simultaneous polarization and magnetization
[
13
]
. Other phases giving rise to the magnetoelectric effect are found in magnetically and/or electrically polarizable media
[
14
,
15
]
. This range of materials has being recently extended with the discovery of time reversal invariant topological insulators (TIs). Microscopically, these materials are insulators in the bulk but have conducting surface states stemming from their peculiar band structure
[
16
,
17
]
. Topological phenomena in condensed matter goes back to Ref.
[
18
]
, where the conductivity of the quantum Hall effect
[
19
]
was identified with the first Chern number of the Berry curvature in the reciprocal space. The existence of TIs in two-dimensional
{\rm HgTe}
quantum wells was predicted in Ref.
[
20
]
and some time later confirmed experimentally
[
21
]
. Subsequently, the phenomenon was generalized to three-dimensional systems with theoretical predictions in Refs.
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
, followed by an experimental confirmation in Ref.
[
28
]
.

Work searching for the generation of measurable magnetic fields via the magnetoelectric effect includes the study of point-like electrical sources
[
29
,
30
]
and metallic spheres of finite radius
[
31
]
placed in front of a planar magnetoelectric medium that occupies the half-space
z>0
. Also the effects of an electric dipolar source in the form of a semispherical capacitor surrounded by a spherical topologically insultating shell have been investigated
[
32
]
. The electric field manipulation of the magnetoelectric effect is an exciting new area of research with the potential to impact magnetic data storage, spintronics and high frequency magnetic devices, leading to smaller and more energy efficient devices.
Recent advances in the manufacture of electrically manipulable magnetoelectric materials
[
33
,
34
]
and new developments in coating techniques for conductors
[
35
]
, could give viability to new configurations that give rise to stronger and controllable magnetic fields.

The properties of a conventional insulator are determined by its permittivity
\epsilon
and its permeability
\mu
. The Maxwell equations that describe the behavior of such materials can be derived from the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{\rm em}=(1/8\pi)\left(\varepsilon\mathbf{E}^{2}-(1/\mu)\mathbf{B}^{2}\right)-\rho\Phi+(1/c)\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{A}
, once the fields are expressed in terms of the electromagnetic potentials
{\mathbf{A}},\Phi
, where
\rho
and
\mathbf{J}
are the charge and current densities and
c
is the speed of light. To include the magnetoelectric effect, one must add the term
\mathcal{L}_{\Theta}=\frac{\alpha}{4\pi^{2}}\Theta(x)\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}
,
where
\alpha
is the fine structure constant and
\Theta(t,\mathbf{x})
is the MES. The system defined by
{\cal L}_{\rm em}+{\cal L}_{\Theta}
is commonly referred to as axion electrodynamics
[
36
]
or Carroll-Field-Jackiw electrodynamics
[
37
]
. When
\Theta
is constant the term
\mathcal{L}_{\Theta}
is a total derivative which does not modifies Maxwell equations. Nevertheless,
\mathcal{L}_{\Theta}
has physical consequences in systems where
\Theta
is a function of the space-time coordinates
[
38
]
.
It is important to bear in mind that the effective equations emerging from the addition of the term
\mathcal{L}_{\Theta}
can describe diverse physical phenomena according to the different choices of
\Theta(t,\mathbf{x})
. For instance:
\Theta
real and arbitrary yields the electromagnetic response of general magnetoelectrics,
\Theta=0,\pi
describes TIs
[
25
]
,
\Theta\in\mathbb{C}
gives the electrodynamics of metamaterials
[
39
,
40
]
and
\Theta(\mathbf{x},t)=2\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{x}-2b_{0}t
provides the response of Weyl semi-metals
[
39
,
41
,
42
]
. It is interesting to observe that in high energy physics the term
\mathcal{L}_{\Theta}
describes the interaction with the electromagnetic field
of an hypothetical dynamical axionic field
\Theta(t,\mathbf{x})

[
43
,
27
]
, which remains good a candidate for the particle that constitute dark matter
[
44
]
.
The simplest way to observe the magnetoelectric effect is
to place adjacent media with different constant values values
\Theta_{1}
and
\Theta_{2}
, such that the interface between them
provides the necessary
\partial\Theta\neq 0
. Even more, in order to minimize the usually dominant electromagnetic effects one can choose the materials such that they are non-magnetic and have almost identical permittivities.

The electromagnetic phenomena described above can be viewed as particular cases of field theories where space in divided at least into two regions, characterized by a set of discontinuous parameters across the interface. The necessity of a quantum version in these cases has motivated us to review its construction in the simplest possible setting incorporating a tractable analogy of the magnetoelectric coupling.
Taking as inspiration the case of axion electrodynamics we consider a system which has only the analogous of a magnetoelectric interface, i.e. such that on each side of the interface there are media that have a different value of a constant parameter playing the role of the MES.

We look at the simplest case of a scalar field in the presence of a planar interface at
z=0
and study the consequences that this has on some physical processes.
To this end we start from the Lagrangian of a real massive scalar field
\Phi
to which we add a contribution proportional to
\theta^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\Phi^{2}
, that is a total derivative when
\theta^{\mu}
is constant everywhere. This constitutes a simple model that mimics the
{\cal L}_{\Theta}
coupling of axion electrodynamics. The vector
\theta^{\mu}
is chosen to describe a system with a planar interface at
z=0
. Along the work we pay attention to one important feature
of our system, which is the non-conservation of momentum along the
z
-axis due to the presence of the interface and highlight its physical consequences.

A large number of technical details are summarized in the Supplementary Materials
[
45
]
and only the results are included in the main text.

The work is organized as follows. In section
II.1
we define our model in terms of a scalar field
\Phi
, to be called the
\theta
-scalar field, obtaining the equations of motion together with the boundary condition at the interface. Section
II.2
discusses the normal modes of the system which result in triplets of plane waves due to the boundary conditions at the interface. Both ingoing and outgoing basis are considered, their orthogonality and completeness are established and the relation between them is also determined.
The canonical quantization of
\Phi
in both basis is done in section
II.3
, where we obtain the respective Feynman propagators.
In section
III
we deal with the first application of this system by discussing the decay of a standard scalar particle (one which does not see the interface) into a pair of
\theta
-scalar
\Phi
particles. We calculate the mean life of the initial particle showing the effects of momentum non-conservation.
In section
IV
we consider a second application
consisting on the scattering of two equally charged standard scalar particles mediated by the
\theta
-scalar particle. Since momentum is not conserved, we study the kinematics in the initial center of mass frame in detail identifying the undetermined variables of the final state. Next, we obtain the scattering amplitude for the process and focus in the momentum-non-conserving contribution. We calculate the transition probability per unit time for these events and define the analogous of a differential cross section which can be measurable. We identify the region in momentum space where momentum violation is allowed and also look at the poles of the scattering amplitude determining their location in the allowed zone. We present polar plots
showing this features for the simplifying case when the ingoing particles impinge perpendicularly to the interface, for some selected choices of the particle parameters. Finally we calculate the absolute value of the ratio between the non-conserving scattering amplitude and the standard one in the coexisting kinematic region, for some particular cases. We close in section
V
with a summary and conclusions.

II
The model

II.1
Lagrangian and equations of motion

In analogy with the Lagrangian density describing non dynamical axion electrodynamics
{\cal L}_{\rm em}+{\cal L}_{\Theta}
, let us consider the following addition to the standard massive scalar field

{\cal L}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\Phi\partial^{\mu}\Phi-\frac{m^{2}}{2}\Phi^{2}-\theta^{\alpha}(x)\Phi\partial_{\alpha}\Phi,

(1)

yielding the equation of motion

\Big(\partial^{2}+m^{2}-\partial_{\alpha}\theta^{\alpha}(x)\Big)\Phi=0,

(2)

of the
\theta
-scalar field
\Phi
.
For a constant
\theta^{\alpha}
the last term in the right hand side of Eq. (
1
) is a total derivative which does not modify the dynamics of the free particle, as can be seen in Eq. (
2
). An interesting and non-trivial situation arises when
\theta^{\alpha}
is a time independent piecewise constant function of the coordinates, which we take as

\theta^{\alpha}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\delta^{\alpha}_{3}\Big(\theta_{1}H(-z)+\theta_{2}H(z)\Big),

(3)

where
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
are constant real numbers and
H(z)
is the Heaviside function. This choice yields

\partial_{\alpha}\theta^{\alpha}(x)={\tilde{\theta}}\delta(z),\qquad{\tilde{\theta}}=\theta_{2}-\theta_{1},

(4)

together with

\Big(\partial^{2}+m^{2}-{\tilde{\theta}}\delta(z)\Big)\Phi=0.

(5)

In this way we obtain a simple model of a plane interface
\Sigma
located at
z=0
, which modifies the particle propagation by breaking translational invariance along the
z
-axis yielding momentum non-conservation in that direction. This setup is shown in the Fig.
1
. Let us observe that for
z\neq 0
we recover the standard free equations of motion for the massive spin zero particle.

Figure 1:
Planar interface
\Sigma
dividing two regions (
z<0
and
z>0
) with different values of the
\theta
parameter.

The presence of the interface is dealt with as usual by finding the solutions in the regions
z\neq 0
and subsequently matching them at
z=0
according to the boundary conditions at the interface. We observe that in the bulk regions we have a free particle meaning that the components of the momentum are always real. Thus our
\theta
-model does not provide the realization of evanescent waves at the interface.
Separation of variables yields the linearly independent solutions of Eq. (
5
) as

\phi(x)=e^{-iE_{\mathbf{k}}t}e^{i{\mathbf{k}}_{\perp}\cdot{\mathbf{x}}_{\perp}}\phi^{k}(z),

(6)

together with its complex conjugate. Here
{\mathbf{x}}_{\perp}=(x,y)
,
\,{\mathbf{k}}_{\perp}
denotes the momentum in the direction perpendicular to the
z
-axis (i.e. parallel to the interface) and
k
is a positive number labeling the normal modes. The function
\phi^{k}(z)
satisfies the equation

\Big(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}+E_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}-{\mathbf{k}}_{\perp}^{2}-m^{2}+{\tilde{\theta}}\delta(z)\Big)\phi^{k}(z)=0.

(7)

and will be constructed as a linear combination of
e^{\pm ikz}
with

k=+\sqrt{E^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}-{\mathbf{k}}_{\perp}^{2}-m^{2}}\geq 0
, as dictated by the solutions of Eq. (
7
) outside the interface. This linear combination arises from the boundary conditions at the interface
which require the continuity of
\phi^{k}(z)
together with the discontinuity in the derivative of
\phi^{k}(z)
provided by the integration of Eq.(
7
) around
z=0
. In other words we
should fulfill

\phi_{1}(z=0_{-})=\phi_{2}(z=0_{+})=\phi(z=0),\qquad\Big(\frac{\partial{\phi_{1}}}{\partial z}\Big)_{z=0_{-}}-\Big(\frac{\partial{\phi_{2}}}{\partial z}\Big)_{z=0_{+}}={\tilde{\theta}}\phi(z=0),

(8)

in standard notation. From now on the subindices 1 and 2 denote functions living on
z<0
and
z>0
, respectively.

II.2
Normal modes: ingoing (incident) and outgoing (detector) modes

The first step in the quantization of the proposed model is to find the normal modes associated to Eq. (
5
) in order to describe the propagation non-perturbatively in
{\tilde{\theta}}
. Due to the interface, a wave impinging on it will be accompanied by reflected and transmitted waves as depicted in Fig
2
. Since the coupling to the interface is independent of the coordinates perpendicular to the
z
-axis, the momentum
{\mathbf{k}}_{\perp}
parallel to the interface is conserved.

Figure 2:
Left panel: the ingoing left mode. Right panel: the ingoing right mode. Both modes include contributions of incident, reflected, and transmitted plane waves.

II.2.1
The ingoing (incident) modes

We choose to label the ingoing normal modes as left (L) and right (R) modes, according to the incident wave impinging on the interface approaches from the left or from the right, respectively. This is emphasized with the drawing in bold of the vector
{\mathbf{k}}=({\mathbf{k}}_{\perp},k_{z})
in the panels of Fig.
2
. To this end we restrict the label
k
of these wave functions to
k\geq 0
.

We take the ingoing normal modes to be the functions

\nu_{S}^{{\mathbf{K}}}({\mathbf{x}})=e^{i{\mathbf{k}}_{\perp}\cdot{\mathbf{x}}_{\perp}}\phi^{k}_{S}(z),

(9)

where
S\in\{L,R\}
and
\phi^{k}_{S}(z)
satisfies the Eq. (
7
) together with the boundary conditions (
8
). At this level
{\mathbf{K}}
is just the label
({\mathbf{k}}_{\perp},k)
.
The contribution to the normal modes of the plane waves in the perpendicular direction is trivial so that we emphasize the non-trivial dependence upon
\phi_{S}^{k}(z)
. In an abuse of language we refer to the normal modes when dealing only with the
z
-dependent contribution.

The construction of the functions
\phi^{k}_{S}(z)
start by taking the standard linear combinations of the free plane waves

e^{\pm ikz}
and writing

\displaystyle\phi^{k}_{1L}=e^{ikz}+P_{Lk}e^{-ikz},\qquad\phi^{k}_{2L}=Q_{Lk}e^{ikz}

\displaystyle\phi^{k}_{1R}=Q_{Rk}e^{-ikz},\qquad\phi^{k}_{2R}=e^{-ikz}+P_{Rk}e^{ikz},

(10)

where
P_{Rk},P_{Lk}
are the reflected amplitudes and
Q_{Rk},Q_{Lk}
are the transmitted amplitudes. The boundary conditions (
8
) yield

P_{Lk}=P_{Rk}=P_{k}=-\frac{{\tilde{\theta}}}{2ik+{\tilde{\theta}}},\qquad Q_{Lk}=Q_{Rk}=Q_{k}=\frac{2ik}{2ik+{\tilde{\theta}}},\qquad Q_{k}=1+P_{k}.

(11)

We can verify that
|P_{k}|^{2}+|Q_{k}|^{2}=1
, as expected for reflection and transmission coefficients. The relations

P_{k}^{*}=P_{-k},\qquad Q_{k}^{*}=Q_{-k},\qquad P_{-k}Q_{k}+P_{k}Q_{-k}=0,

(12)

will be useful in the following. From (
10
) and (
11
) the normal modes are

\displaystyle\phi_{L}^{k}(z)=H(-z)(e^{ikz}+P_{k}e^{-ikz})+H(z)Q_{k}e^{ikz}=e^{ikz}+P_{k}e^{ik|z|},

(13)

\displaystyle\phi_{R}^{k}(z)=H(-z)Q_{k}e^{-ikz}+H(z)(e^{-ikz}+P_{k}e^{ikz})=e^{-ikz}+P_{k}e^{ik|z|},

(14)

which satisfy
\phi^{k*}_{L,R}(z)=\phi_{L,R}^{-k}(z)
and
\phi^{k}_{L,R}(z)=\phi_{R,L}^{k}(-z)
.

Our next step is to obtain the scalar product of different normal modes. The scalar product is

\langle f|g\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d^{3}xf^{*}({\mathbf{x}})g({\mathbf{x}}),

(15)

which reduces to

\langle\nu_{S}^{{\mathbf{K}}}|\nu_{S^{\prime}}^{{\mathbf{K}^{\prime}}}\rangle=(2\pi)^{2}\delta^{2}({\mathbf{k}}_{\perp}-{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}_{\perp})\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dz(\phi^{k}_{S})^{*}(z)\phi^{k^{\prime}}_{S^{\prime}}(z)\equiv(2\pi)^{2}\delta^{2}({\mathbf{k}}_{\perp}-{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}_{\perp})\,\langle{\phi_{S}^{k}}|{\phi_{S^{\prime}}^{k^{\prime}}}\rangle.

(16)

The calculations to obtain the scalar product in the
z
-space
are rather involved and we illustrate one of them in the Supplementary Materials
[
45
]
. Here we only quote the final results for further use. We find

\displaystyle\langle{\phi_{S}^{q}}|{\phi_{S^{\prime}}^{k}}\rangle=(2\pi)\delta(k-q)\delta_{SS^{\prime}},

(17)

\displaystyle\langle{\phi_{S}^{q*}}|{\phi_{S^{\prime}}^{k}}\rangle=[\delta_{SS^{\prime}}P_{q}+(1-\delta_{SS^{\prime}})Q_{q}](2\pi)\delta(k-q)=[Q_{q}-\delta_{SS^{\prime}}](2\pi)\delta(k-q).

(18)

where
S,S^{\prime}\in\{L,R\}
, which show that the normal modes are orthogonal.

It is convenient to introduce the notation

U^{k}_{SS^{\prime}}=\delta_{SS^{\prime}}P_{k}+(1-\delta_{SS^{\prime}})Q_{k}=U^{k}_{S^{\prime}S},

(19)

which implies that the matrix
U^{k}
is symmetrical.
In terms of the complete wave functions (
9
) the orthogonality relations read

\displaystyle\langle{\nu_{S}^{\mathbf{K}}}|{\nu_{S^{\prime}}^{{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}}}\rangle=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{K}-\mathbf{K}^{\prime})\delta_{SS^{\prime}},

(20)

\displaystyle\langle{\nu_{S}^{{\mathbf{K}}*}}|{\nu_{S^{\prime}}^{{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}}}\rangle=U^{k}_{SS^{\prime}}(2\pi)^{3}\delta(k-k^{\prime})\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{\perp}).

(21)

After a long calculation summarized in the Supplementary Materials
[
45
]
we establish the completeness relation of the ingoing normal modes

\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\left(\Phi_{L}^{k}(z)\Phi_{L}^{k*}(z^{\prime})+\Phi_{R}^{k}(z)\Phi_{R}^{k*}(z^{\prime})\right)=2\pi\delta(z-z^{\prime}).

(22)

Let us emphasize that the above relation is fulfilled provided
{\tilde{\theta}}<0
, which determines the correct choice of the sign in the coupling term of the Lagrangian density (
1
). As we will discover in a subsequent section this condition is also required to have a positive definite Hamiltonian for arbitrary configuration of the fields.

II.2.2
The outgoing (detector) modes

So far, we have introduced a basis of ingoing modes denoted by
\nu_{S}^{{\mathbf{K}}}({\mathbf{x}})
parametrized by
\mathbf{K}=({\mathbf{k}}_{\perp},k)
. Such basis is adequate for describing particle sources (which impinge at the interface), but not particle sinks (e.g. detectors) associated with outgoing particles. Focusing on the non-trivial dependence of the normal modes in the
z
-component of the momentum, we choose to define the basis of outgoing (detector modes) by means of the substitution
{k}\rightarrow-{k}
in the functions
\phi_{S}^{{k}}(z)
which we already know. Given that
\phi_{S}^{{k}*}({z})=\phi_{S}^{-{k}}({z})
, the basis of outgoing modes is given by
\{\phi_{S}^{{k}*}({z})\}
with
k>0
.
Since the outgoing functions correspond to the complex conjugate of the ingoing functions in the
z
-space, the completeness relation of the former is automatically satisfied.
In the new basis, we have an outgoing, a pseudoreflected and a pseudotransmitted wave, as outlined in the Fig. (
3
).

Figure 3:
Left panel: the outgoing left mode. Right panel: the outgoing right mode. Both modes include contributions of outgoing, pseudoreflected and pseudotransmitted plane waves.

In analogy to Eq. (
9
) we define the outgoing normal modes as

{\tilde{\nu}}_{S}^{{\mathbf{K}}}({\mathbf{x}})=e^{i{\mathbf{k}}_{\perp}\cdot{\mathbf{x}}_{\perp}}\phi^{k*}_{S}(z).

(23)

We warn the reader that we are not taking the complex conjugate of the perpendicular contribution in the above definition.

II.2.3
The relation between the ingoing and outgoing basis

Next we inquire about the relationship between both bases. To find their connection, we start from the completeness relation:

\Phi_{S}^{k*}(z)=\int dz^{\prime}\delta(z-z^{\prime})\Phi_{S}^{k*}(z^{\prime})=\int dz^{\prime}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dk^{\prime}}{2\pi}\sum_{S^{\prime}\in\{L,R\}}\Phi_{S^{\prime}}^{k^{\prime}}(z)\Phi_{S^{\prime}}^{k^{\prime}*}(z^{\prime})\right)\Phi_{S}^{k*}(z^{\prime}).

(24)

This is rewritten in terms of the known scalar product as

\Phi_{S}^{k*}(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dk^{\prime}}{2\pi}\sum_{S^{\prime}\in\{L,R\}}\left(\int dz^{\prime}\Phi_{S^{\prime}}^{k^{\prime}*}(z^{\prime})\Phi_{S}^{k*}(z^{\prime})\right)\Phi_{S^{\prime}}^{k^{\prime}}(z),

(25)

where we know that

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dz^{\prime}\,\Phi_{S^{\prime}}^{k^{\prime}}(z^{\prime})\Phi_{S}^{k}(z^{\prime})=U^{k}_{SS^{\prime}}(2\pi)\delta(k-k^{\prime}),

(26)

from Eqs. (
18
) and (
19
).
Thus, taking the complex conjugate yields

\Phi_{S}^{k*}(z)=\sum_{S^{\prime}\in\{L,R\}}U^{*k}_{SS^{\prime}}\,\Phi_{S^{\prime}}^{k}(z),\qquad U^{*k}_{SS^{\prime}}\equiv(U^{k}_{SS^{\prime}})^{*},

(27)

which provides the searched relationship among the basis.
The inverse relation is found by conjugating Eq. (
27
) and yields

\Phi_{S}^{k}(z)=\sum_{S^{\prime}\in\{L,R\}}U^{k}_{SS^{\prime}}\,\Phi_{S^{\prime}}^{*k}(z).

(28)

Eqs. (
27
) and (
28
) imply the following condition on the symmetrical matrix
U^{k}_{SS^{\prime}}

\sum_{S^{\prime\prime}\in\{L,R\}}U^{k}_{SS^{\prime\prime}}\,U^{*k}_{S^{\prime\prime}S^{\prime}}=\delta_{SS^{\prime}},

(29)

which is recovered by explicit calculation in the Supplementary Materials
[
45
]
. Thus,
U^{k}_{SS^{\prime}}
is a unitary symmetric matrix.

The orthogonality of the outgoing modes

\langle{\Phi_{S}^{q*}}|{\Phi_{S^{\prime}}^{k*}}\rangle=2\pi\delta(k-q)\delta_{SS^{\prime}},\qquad\langle{\Phi_{S}^{q}}|{\Phi_{S^{\prime}}^{k*}}\rangle=U^{*q}_{SS^{\prime}}(2\pi)\delta(k-q).

(30)

is a direct consequence of the orthogonality of the ingoing modes.

Recalling Eq. (
9
), together with (
28
), we find the important relation between the ingoing and outgoing modes

{\nu}_{S}^{{\mathbf{K}}}({\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{S^{\prime}\in\{L,R\}}U^{k}_{SS^{\prime}}\,{\tilde{\nu}}_{S^{\prime}}^{{\mathbf{K}}}({\mathbf{x}}),\qquad{\tilde{\nu}}_{S}^{{\mathbf{K}}}({\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{S^{\prime}\in\{L,R\}}U^{*k}_{SS^{\prime}}\,{\nu}_{S^{\prime}}^{{\mathbf{K}}}({\mathbf{x}}),

(31)

which produces

\sum_{S\in\{L,R\}}{\nu}_{S}^{{\mathbf{K}}}({\mathbf{x}}){\nu}_{S}^{{\mathbf{K}}*}({\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{S\in\{L,R\}}{\tilde{\nu}}_{S}^{{\mathbf{K}}}({\mathbf{x}}){\tilde{\nu}}_{S}^{{\mathbf{K}}*}({\mathbf{x}}).

(32)

II.2.4
The momentum content of the modes

So far we have emphasized that
{\mathbf{K}}=({\mathbf{k}}_{\perp},k)
with
k>0

II.3
The quantization, the Hamiltonian and the propagator

The most general real solution to the modified Klein-Gordon equation (
2
) can be taken as a linear combination of either the ingoing normal modes or the outgoing normal modes. We start with the former and write

\displaystyle\Phi(t,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\{L,R\}}\int_{k>0}\frac{d^{3}{\mathbf{K}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf{K}}}}\left[a_{S}(\mathbf{K})\nu^{\mathbf{K}}_{S}(\mathbf{x})e^{-iE_{\mathbf{K}}t}+a_{S}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{K})\nu_{S}^{*\mathbf{K}}(\mathbf{x})e^{iE_{\mathbf{K}}t}\right],

(33)

with
E_{\mathbf{K}}=\sqrt{{\mathbf{k}}_{\perp}^{2}+k^{2}+m^{2}}
and
d^{3}{\mathbf{K}}=d^{2}{\mathbf{k}}_{\perp}\,dk
.

We quantize the field by imposing the following commutation relations

\displaystyle\left[a_{S}(\mathbf{K}),a^{\dagger}_{S^{\prime}}(\mathbf{K}^{\prime})\right]=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{K}-\mathbf{K}^{\prime})\delta_{SS^{\prime}},\qquad\left[a_{S}(\mathbf{K}),a_{S^{\prime}}(\mathbf{K}^{\prime})\right]=0=\left[a^{\dagger}_{S}(\mathbf{K}),a^{\dagger}_{S^{\prime}}(\mathbf{K}^{\prime})\right],

(34)

which reproduce the equal time canonical commutation relations among the field
\Phi(t,{\mathbf{x}})
and its canonically conjugated momentum

\Pi(t,{\mathbf{x}})=\dot{\Phi}(t,{\mathbf{x}})
, at different space points. The calculations, which involve the completeness relation (
22
), are completely analogous to the standard case so we write the expected final results

\left[\Phi(t,\mathbf{x}),{\Pi}(t,\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right]=i\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),\qquad\left[\Phi(t,\mathbf{x}),{\Phi}(t,\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right]=0,\qquad\left[\Pi(t,\mathbf{x}),{\Pi}(t,\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right]=0.

(35)

The Fock space of the system generated from the vacuum
|0\rangle
, such that
a_{S}({\mathbf{K}})|0\rangle=0
, is constructed in the usual way in terms of the operators
a_{S}^{\dagger}({\mathbf{K}})
acting on the vacuum.

To identify the Hamiltonian and the momentum operator it is convenient to start from the energy-momentum tensor. To obtain a symmetric energy-momentum tensor we use the Lagrangian density

{\cal L}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\Phi\partial^{\mu}\Phi-\frac{m^{2}}{2}\Phi^{2}+\frac{\tilde{\theta}}{2}\delta(z)\Phi^{2}

(36)

which differs from the original Eq. (
1
) by a total derivative. We obtain

T^{0}{}_{0}=\frac{1}{2}{\dot{\Phi}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\Phi)^{2}+\frac{m^{2}}{2}\Phi^{2}+\theta(z)\Phi\partial_{z}\Phi,\qquad T^{0}{}_{i}={\dot{\Phi}}\partial_{i}\Phi,

(37)

together with the non-conservation equation

\partial_{\mu}T^{\mu}{}_{\nu}=-\frac{{\tilde{\theta}}}{2}\Phi^{2}\partial_{\nu}\delta(z),

(38)

which is consistent with the violation of translational invariance in the direction
z
. In fact, here we have energy conservation and momentum conservation only in the directions perpendicular to de
z
-axis. Let us observe that the last term of
T^{0}{}_{0}
in Eq. (
37
) can be integrated by parts in the expression for the Hamiltonian, yielding the manifestly negative contribution
-\frac{{\tilde{\theta}}}{2}\delta(z)\Phi^{2}
to the Hamiltonian density. Thus, in order to have a positive definite Hamiltonian for all configurations of the field
\Phi
we demand
{\tilde{\theta}}<0
. This is consistent with the condition required for the completeness relation (
22
) obtained in the previous section. As expected, the calculation of the Hamiltonian in terms of the normal modes yields

H=\sum_{S\in\{L,R\}}\int_{k>0}\frac{d^{3}{\mathbf{K}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}E_{\mathbf{K}}\,a_{S}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{K})a_{S}(\mathbf{K}).

(39)

The details are summarized in the
Supplementary Materials
[
45
]
.

From the commutation relations (
34
) we realize that
a_{S}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{K})a_{S}(\mathbf{K})
can be interpreted as a number operator which eigenvalues label the number of
\{S,\mathbf{K}\}
modes. Then, the usual interpretation of
a^{\dagger}_{S}({\mathbf{K}})
(
a_{S}({\mathbf{K}})
) as creation (annihilation) operators for the ingoing normal modes

\{S,\mathbf{K}\}
follows. A similar calculation yields the conserved momentum in the perpendicular direction

{\mathbf{P}}_{\perp}=\int d^{3}x\,{\dot{\Phi}}\nabla_{\perp}\Phi=\sum_{S\in\{L,R\}}\int_{k>0}\frac{d^{3}{\mathbf{K}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,{\mathbf{k}}_{\perp}\,a_{S}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{K})a_{S}(\mathbf{K}).

(40)

The momentum
P_{z}
is not conserved and can not be written as in Eq. (
40
). This can be understood noticing that
\nu_{S}^{\mathbf{K}}({\mathbf{x}})
, being a linear combination of three plane waves, is not an eigenfunction of
i\partial_{z}
, as it is evident from Eqs. (
13
) and (
14
).

We can also write the field (
33
) in terms of the outgoing normal modes. To this end we start from

\displaystyle\Phi(t,{\mathbf{x}})=\int_{k>0}\frac{d^{3}{\mathbf{K}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf{K}}}}\left[\left(\sum_{S\in\{L,R\}}a_{S}(\mathbf{K})\phi_{S}^{k}(z)\right)e^{-iE_{\mathbf{K}}t+i\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\perp}}+\text{h.c.}\right].

(41)

and use the Eq. (
28
) to replace the functions

\phi_{S}^{k}
in terms of the outgoing modes
\phi_{S}^{k*}
. This yields

\displaystyle\hskip-42.67912pt\sum_{S\in\{L,R\}}a_{S}(\mathbf{K})\phi_{S}^{k}(z)=\sum_{S,S^{\prime}\in\{L,R\}}a_{S}(\mathbf{K})U^{k}_{SS^{\prime}}\phi_{S^{\prime}}^{*k}(z)=\sum_{S^{\prime}\in\{L,R\}}\Big(\sum_{S\in\{L,R\}}U^{k}_{S^{\prime}S}\,a_{S}(\mathbf{K})\Big)\phi_{S^{\prime}}^{*k}(z),

(42)

and suggests defining the annihilation operators for the outgoing modes as

{\tilde{a}}_{S^{\prime}}(\mathbf{K})\equiv\sum_{S\in\{L,R\}}U^{k}_{S^{\prime}S}\,a_{S}(\mathbf{K}),

(43)

with the corresponding Hermitian conjugates being the creation operators.
We note that in the limit
\tilde{\theta}\rightarrow 0
, we have

{\tilde{a}}_{L}(\mathbf{K})=a_{R}(\mathbf{K})
and
{\tilde{a}}_{R}(\mathbf{K})=a_{L}(\mathbf{K})
,
that is, an incoming
L
mode corresponds to an outgoing
R
mode, and viceversa. This is the expected behavior.

In the Supplementary Materials
[
45
]
we verify that the algebra of the tilde operators is the expected one. Since each of them is a linear combination of the corresponding untilded ones, we only need to check the commutator
[{\tilde{a}_{S}}({\mathbf{K}}),{\tilde{a}^{\dagger}_{{S}^{\prime}}}({\mathbf{K}}^{\prime})]
. Furthermore, we calculate the mixed commutators obtaining

\displaystyle\left[{\tilde{a}}(\mathbf{K}),a_{S^{\prime}}(\mathbf{K}^{\prime})\right]=0,

(44)

\displaystyle\left[{\tilde{a}}_{S}(\mathbf{K}),a_{S^{\prime}}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{K}^{\prime})\right]=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{K}-\mathbf{K}^{\prime})U^{k}_{SS^{\prime}}.

(45)

In terms of the outgoing (detector) modes the field is

\Phi(t,\mathbf{x})=\sum_{S\in\{L,R\}}\int_{k>0}\frac{d^{3}{\mathbf{K}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf{K}}}}\left[{\tilde{a}}_{S}(\mathbf{K}){\tilde{\nu}}^{\mathbf{K}}_{S}(\mathbf{x})e^{-iE_{\mathbf{K}}t}+\text{h.c.}\right].

(46)

As shown in the Supplementary Materials
[
45
]
the Feynmann propagator can adopt either of the following two forms

\displaystyle{\Delta}_{F}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\in\{L,R\}}\int_{k>0}\frac{d^{4}{k}}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{i}{k^{2}-m^{2}+i\epsilon}e^{-ik^{0}(x^{0}-{x^{\prime}}^{0})}{\tilde{\nu}}_{S}^{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x}){\tilde{\nu}}_{S}^{\mathbf{k}*}(\mathbf{x^{\prime}}),

(47)

\displaystyle{\Delta}_{F}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\in\{L,R\}}\int_{k>0}\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{i}{k^{2}-m^{2}+i\epsilon}e^{-ik^{0}(x^{0}-{x^{\prime}}^{0})}\nu_{S}^{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})\nu_{S}^{{\mathbf{k}}*}(\mathbf{x^{\prime}}),

(48)

when expressed in terms of the outgoing (ingoing) modes, respectively.
The notation is
k^{\mu}=(k^{0},\mathbf{k}_{\perp},k)
, with

d^{4}k=dk^{0}\,d^{3}{\mathbf{K}}=dk^{0}d{\mathbf{k}}_{\perp}dk,\qquad k^{2}=k_{0}^{2}-{\mathbf{K}}^{2}=k_{0}^{2}-{\mathbf{k}}_{\perp}^{2}-k^{2}.

(49)

In the Supplementary Materials
[
45
]
we show that the propagator (
48
) can also be written as

\Delta_{F}(x,x^{\prime})=i\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{e^{-ik^{0}(x^{0}-{x^{\prime}}^{0})+i\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}})_{\perp}}}{k^{2}-m^{2}+i\epsilon}\Big(e^{ik(z-z^{\prime})}+P_{k}e^{ik(|z|+|z^{\prime}|)}\Big).

(50)

The same result holds for the representation (
47
) in virtue the relation (
32
). This form has the advantage of exhibiting the momentum violating contribution as the term proportional to
P_{k}
inside the round brackets, as well as the limit

{\tilde{\theta}}=0,P_{k}=0
where the standard form is recovered.

III
Decay and mean life

In order to illustrate the consequences of the momentum non-conservation produced by the interface we consider the decay of a particle described by a spin cero field
\Psi
that is not affected by its presence into two
\theta
-scalar particles. The total Lagrangian density is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\Phi\partial^{\mu}\Phi-\frac{m^{2}}{2}\Phi^{2}-\theta(z)\Phi\partial_{z}\Phi+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\Psi\partial^{\mu}\Psi-\frac{M^{2}}{2}\Psi^{2}+\lambda\Psi\Phi^{2}.

(51)

Since we are interested in detecting the outgoing
\theta
-scalar particles, we will express this field in terms of the detector modes.
We write the expansion for the field
\Psi
as

\Psi(t,\mathbf{x})=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{p}}}\left[b(\mathbf{p})e^{-ip\cdot x}+\text{h.c.}\right],

(52)

and we quantize it in the standard way.
The commutation relations are the usual free-field ones,

\left[b(\mathbf{p}),b^{\dagger}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime})\right]=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{p}^{\prime}),\qquad\left[b^{\dagger}(\mathbf{p}),b^{\dagger}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime})\right]=\left[b(\mathbf{p}),b(\mathbf{p}^{\prime})\right]=0.

(53)

We add the commutation relations

\left[{\tilde{a}}_{S}(\mathbf{K}),b(\mathbf{p})\right]=\left[{\tilde{a}}_{S}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{K}),b(\mathbf{p})\right]=\left[{\tilde{a}}_{S}(\mathbf{K}),b^{\dagger}(\mathbf{p})\right]=\left[{\tilde{a}}_{S}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{K}),b^{\dagger}(\mathbf{p})\right]=0,

(54)

indicating that
\Phi
and
\Psi
are independent fields.

The initial state is merely a particle of the type
\Psi
with momentum
\mathbf{p}
, while the final state is composed of two modes with labels
\{\mathbf{K}_{S},\mathbf{K}^{\prime}_{S^{\prime}}\}
. In terms of creation operators acting on the vacuum of the Fock space this is

\displaystyle|{i}\rangle\equiv\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf{p}}}\,b^{\dagger}(\mathbf{p})\,|0\rangle=|{\mathbf{p}}\rangle

(55)

\displaystyle|{f}\rangle\equiv\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf{K}}}\,\sqrt{2E_{{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}}}\,{\tilde{a}}_{S}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{K})\,{\tilde{a}}_{S^{\prime}}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{K}^{\prime})\,|{0}\rangle=|{\mathbf{K}_{S},\mathbf{K}^{\prime}_{S^{\prime}}}\rangle=|{\mathbf{K}_{S}}\rangle\otimes|{\mathbf{K}^{\prime}_{S^{\prime}}}\rangle.

(56)

To first order, the invariant
amplitude that connects the initial state with the final state is

\mathfrak{T}=i\lambda\int d^{4}x\langle{f}|\mathcal{T}\left\{\Psi(x)\Phi^{2}(x)\right\}|{i}\rangle,

(57)

where
\mathcal{T}
is the time ordering operator. Decomposing the fields in positive and negative frequency parts we have

\displaystyle\Psi^{+}(x)|{\mathbf{p}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-iE_{{\mathbf{p}}}x^{0}}e^{+i{\mathbf{p}}\cdot{\mathbf{x}}}|{0}\rangle,\qquad\langle\mathbf{K}_{S}|\Phi^{-}(x)=\langle 0|\,e^{iE_{{\mathbf{K}}}x^{0}}\,{\tilde{\nu}}_{S}^{{\mathbf{K}}*}(\mathbf{x}),

(58)

which yield the following contribution for the decay amplitude in the center of mass reference frame (
\mathbf{p}=0
)

\mathfrak{T}=i2\lambda\left(\int dx^{0}e^{i(E_{\mathbf{K}}+E_{{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}}-M)x^{0}}\right)\left(\int d\mathbf{x}_{\perp}e^{-i(\mathbf{k}_{\perp}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{\perp})\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\perp}}\right)\left(\int dz\,\phi_{S}^{k}(z)\phi_{S^{\prime}}^{k^{\prime}}(z)\right),

(59)

where
\mathbf{k}_{\perp}=(k_{x},k_{y})
,
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{\perp}=(k^{\prime}_{x},k^{\prime}_{y})
and
\mathbf{x}_{\perp}=(x,y)
. Thus,

\mathfrak{T}=i2\lambda(2\pi)^{3}\delta(E_{\mathbf{K}}+E_{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}-M)\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{\perp})\langle{\Phi_{S}^{k*}}|{\Phi_{S^{\prime}}^{k^{\prime}}}\rangle.

(60)

From the result of Eq. (
18
) for the last integral we obtain

\mathfrak{T}=i2\lambda(2\pi)^{4}\bigg[\delta_{SS^{\prime}}P_{k}+(1-\delta_{SS^{\prime}})Q_{k}\bigg]\delta(M-E_{\mathbf{K}}-E_{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime})\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{\perp})\delta(k-k^{\prime}).

(61)

Note that the delta function
\delta(k-k^{\prime})
indicates that
k=k^{\prime}
. Nonetheless, this does not correspond to momentum conservation, since the labels
k
are always positive and hence do not specify a given direction. The relation of these labels with the physical momentum in the direction
z
of each decaying particle is determined according to Table
1
. Equation (
61
) indicates that
there are various channels for the decaying particle: (i) the decay can go into a left and a right outgoing modes (
S=L,S^{\prime}=R
for example), thus conserving momentum in the direction
z
, or (ii) the particle can decay into two left (right) outgoing modes (
S=S^{\prime}
), going from an initial state with zero momentum to a final state with momentum
-2k
(
+2k
), respectively, in the direction
z
. As already mentioned, this is a consequence of the lack of translation invariance this direction. As a check of consistency we observe from the definitions of
P_{k}
and
Q_{k}
that the case without interface (
\tilde{\theta}=0
) reduces to

\mathfrak{T}=i2\lambda(2\pi)^{4}\big[1-\delta_{SS^{\prime}}\big]\delta(M-E_{k}-E_{k}^{\prime})\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{\perp})\delta(k-k^{\prime}).

(62)

This means that the decay amplitude is different from zero only if the outgoing modes are different, so that the particles have opposite
z
-component of the momentum with the same absolute value, as expected.

Going back to the general case, from Eq. (
61
) we extract the amplitude

\mathcal{M}=i2\lambda\bigg[\delta_{SS^{\prime}}P_{k}+(1-\delta_{SS^{\prime}})Q_{k}\bigg].

(63)

Since
\delta_{SS^{\prime}}(1-\delta_{SS^{\prime}})=0,\,\,\forall\,\,S,S^{\prime}
and
\delta_{SS^{\prime}}^{2}=\delta_{SS^{\prime}}
,we have

|\mathcal{M}|^{2}=4\lambda^{2}\bigg[\delta_{SS^{\prime}}|P_{k}|^{2}+(1-\delta_{SS^{\prime}})|Q_{k}|^{2}\bigg],

(64)

where
|P_{k}|^{2}=\tilde{\theta}^{2}/[4k^{2}+\tilde{\theta}^{2}]
and
|Q_{k}|^{2}=4(k)^{2}/[4k^{2}+\tilde{\theta}^{2}]
.

Even though
\delta(k-k^{\prime})
is not related to momentum conservation, it provides the combination
\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{\perp})\delta(k-k^{\prime})
similar to
\delta^{3}({\mathbf{p}}_{\rm out}-{\mathbf{p}}_{\rm in})

which allows us to take the standard expression for the differential decay rate

\displaystyle d\Gamma_{SS^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{32\pi^{2}M}\frac{d^{3}{\mathbf{K}}}{E_{\mathbf{K}}}\frac{d^{3}{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}}{E_{{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}}}|\mathcal{M}|^{2}\delta(M-E_{\mathbf{K}}-E_{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime})\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{\perp})\delta(k-k^{\prime}).

(65)

The integration over the phase space yields

\Gamma_{SS^{\prime}}=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4\pi M^{2}}\left[\delta_{SS^{\prime}}\frac{|\tilde{\theta}|}{2}\sin^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{1+\frac{{\tilde{\theta}}^{2}}{4a}}}\right)+(1-\delta_{SS^{\prime}})\left\{\sqrt{a}-\frac{|{\tilde{\theta}}|}{2}\sin^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{1+\frac{{\tilde{\theta}}^{2}}{4a}}}\right)\right\}\right],

(66)

where the details are presented in the Supplementary Materials
[
45
]
. Here
a=M^{2}/4-m^{2}
and the presence of
\sqrt{a}
in Eq. (
66
) demands
a>0
. A consistency check of the above result for
\Gamma_{SS^{\prime}}
is that when
\tilde{\theta}=0
we recover the well known decay width when there is no interface

\Gamma_{0}=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8\pi M}\sqrt{1-4m^{2}/M^{2}}.

(67)

Summarizing, there are now three decay channels. One when the outgoing modes are different (
\Gamma_{LR}
), which in the limit
\tilde{\theta}\rightarrow 0
reduces to
\Gamma_{0}
, and two others when the modes are the same (
\Gamma_{RR}=\Gamma_{LL}
), which arise due to the non-conservation of momentum in the direction of the
z
-axis.

In the first case we have

\begin{split}\Gamma_{LR}=&\Gamma_{0}\,\left[1-R\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+R^{2}}}\right)\right],\qquad R=\frac{|\tilde{\theta}|}{\sqrt{M^{2}-4m^{2}}}>0.\end{split}

(68)

Notice that the condition
\Gamma_{LR}>0
sets a new threshold in
M
for the decay to occur. Since
1/\sqrt{1+R^{2}}<1
the resulting angle
\beta=\sin^{-1}(1/\sqrt{1+R^{2}})
is between
0
and
\pi/2
. Then the condition
(1-R\pi/2)>0
guarantees that the square bracket in (
68
) is positive, imposing the stronger constraint

M^{2}>4m^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}{\tilde{\theta}}^{2},

(69)

which is equivalent to write
0<R<2/\pi
.

The remaining cases yield

\Gamma_{LL}=\Gamma_{RR}=\Gamma_{0}\,\left[R\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+R^{2}}}\right)\right],

(70)

which only require the condition

M^{2}>4m^{2}.

(71)

Recapping, when
4m^{2}<M^{2}<4m^{2}+\pi^{2}{\tilde{\theta}}^{2}/4

only the channels
LL
and
RR
are open, with a total decay rate

\Gamma_{\tilde{\theta}}=\Gamma_{LL}+\Gamma_{RR}=2\Gamma_{0}\,\left[R\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+R^{2}}}\right)\right].

(72)

Let us emphasize that in this region of
M^{2}
the decay proceeds only due to the momentum violating channels.
On the other hand, when
4m^{2}+\pi^{2}{\tilde{\theta}}^{2}/4<M^{2}
the three channels participate with a total decay rate

\Gamma_{T}=\Gamma_{\tilde{\theta}}+\Gamma_{LR}=\Gamma_{0}\left[1+R\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+R^{2}}}\right)\right].

(73)

The function in square brackets in (
73
) is an increasing function of
R\geq 0
, with value
1
for
R=0
and value
1.64
when
R=2/\pi
. Thus we conclude that
\Gamma_{0}<\Gamma_{T}<1.64\,\Gamma_{0}
.

IV
Momentum non-conservation in a scattering process

As another signal of momentum non-conservation in our model we study a scattering process mediated by the
\theta
-scalar particle. To simplify the calculation we take its interaction with a complex scalar field
\chi
, describing standard particles with mass
{\tilde{M}}
and charges
\pm{\tilde{\lambda}}
which are not affected by the interface. The interaction term is
{\cal L}_{\rm int}={\tilde{\lambda}}\,\Phi\,\chi^{\dagger}\,\chi
and we consider the scattering of two incoming
\chi
particles with the same charge going into two outgoing
\chi
particles. In this way we only allow the
t
and
u
channels, suppressing the
s
channel due to charge conservation. Our aim is to give a quantitative description of the momentum
-
violating contribution. To set the stage, let us recall the most typical example of a process conserving energy but violating momentum conservation, which is the scattering of a charge by an external potential (Coulomb scattering by an external electromagnetic field, for example). In our case the interface plays the role of the external potential providing a
\delta(z)
-type interaction.

IV.1
The kinematics

The normal modes of the
\chi
-particles are standard plane waves and the label
{\mathbf{p}}=\{{\mathbf{p}}_{\perp},p\}
refers to the usual momentum with
p
being the
z
-component of
{\mathbf{p}}
. The
\theta
-particle mediates de interaction and only contributes through the propagator. Nevertheless, since the interaction does not conserve momentum along the
z
-axis, we need some care when specifying the kinematics of the participating
\chi
particles.
The four momenta of the incident particles are denoted as

\displaystyle p_{1}^{\mu}=(E_{1},{\mathbf{p}}_{1\perp},p_{1}),\qquad p_{2}^{\mu}=(E_{2},{\mathbf{p}}_{2\perp},p_{2}),\qquad E_{i}=\sqrt{{\mathbf{p}}^{2}_{i\perp}+p_{i}^{2}+{\tilde{M}}^{2}},\quad i=1,2,

(74)

while those of the outgoing particles follow the same notation with a superindex prime. We locate the incident particles in their center of mass, i.e.,
\mathbf{p}\equiv\mathbf{p}_{1}=-\mathbf{p}_{2}
, with
p=|{\mathbf{p}}|
. We must bear in mind that, due to the non-conservation of momentum, this frame does not coincide with the center of mass frame of the final momenta.

Now we choose a convenient coordinate system according to the following convention: the direction of the incident center of mass, determined by
{\mathbf{p}}_{1}
, forms an angle
\theta
with the normal
{\hat{\mathbf{k}}}
to the interface, which defines our
z
-axis. The projection of
{\mathbf{p}}_{1}
on the interface (
x-y
plane) defines the
x
-axis of the coordinate system, as shown in Fig.
4
.

Figure 4:

\chi
particles mediated by a
\Phi
particle. The

-axis is determined by the normal to the interface which is the (

)-plane. The incident direction
{\mathbf{p}}_{1}
of the center of mass forms the angle
\theta
with the normal to the interface. The projection of
{\mathbf{p}}_{1}
on the interface determines de

-axis.

In this way

\displaystyle{\mathbf{p}}_{1}=(p\sin\theta,0,p\cos\theta),\qquad{\mathbf{p}}_{2}=(-p\sin\theta,0,-p\cos\theta),\quad E_{1}=E_{2}=\sqrt{p^{2}+{\tilde{M}}^{2}}\equiv\frac{E}{2},

(75)

where
E
is the center of mass energy.
Since we have momentum conservation only in the perpendicular direction we expect that the projection on the interface of the direction of the incident center of mass will rotate by an angle
\phi
. Thus for the outgoing particles we parametrize the momenta as follows

\mathbf{p^{\prime}}_{1}=(p^{\prime}\cos\phi,\;p^{\prime}\sin\phi,\;p^{\prime}_{1}),\qquad\mathbf{p^{\prime}}_{2}=(-p^{\prime}\cos\phi,\;-p^{\prime}\sin\phi,\;p^{\prime}_{2}),\qquad|\mathbf{p^{\prime}}_{1\perp}|=p^{\prime},

(76)

which satisfies
\mathbf{p^{\prime}}_{1\perp}=-\;\mathbf{p^{\prime}}_{2\perp}
and
|\mathbf{p^{\prime}}_{1\perp}|=|\mathbf{p^{\prime}}_{2\perp}|=p^{\prime}
. Let us emphasize that
p_{1,2}
denote the third component of the momenta
\mathbf{p}_{1,2}
and should not be confused with
|\mathbf{p}_{1,2}|
, with analogous convention for the primed vectors. As we will show in the next section, the exchange of the
\theta
-particle will not provide the momentum conserving
\delta
function in the
z
-direction
\delta(p_{1}^{\prime}+p_{2}^{\prime})
so that
p_{1}^{\prime}
and
p_{2}^{\prime}
remain arbitrary within the restriction imposed by energy conservation. Our aim is to determine the probability of occurrence for each combination
(p_{1}^{\prime},\,p_{2}^{\prime})
, which will be provided by the transition amplitude.

The energies are

E^{\prime}_{1}=\sqrt{p^{\prime 2}+p^{\prime}_{1}{}^{2}+{\tilde{M}}^{2}},\qquad E^{\prime}_{2}=\sqrt{p^{\prime 2}+p^{\prime}_{2}{}^{2}+{\tilde{M}}^{2}}.

(77)

Energy conservation
E_{1}+E_{2}=E=E^{\prime}_{1}+E^{\prime}_{2}
allows to solve
p^{\prime}={\cal P}^{\prime}
in terms of
E,p^{\prime}_{1},p^{\prime}_{2}
with the result

The outgoing energies can be written in a convenient form as

and it is a direct matter to verify

.

In preparation for the definition of a modified cross section we observe that we have three delta functions that can be integrated, which enforce the conservation laws at our disposal: energy and perpendicular momentum conservation. This means that from the six original outgoing variables
{\mathbf{p}}_{1}^{\prime}
and
{\mathbf{p}}_{2}^{\prime}

p^{\prime}_{1},p^{\prime}_{2}
and the angle
\phi
measuring the rotation of the projection of the center of mass direction on the interface. The required integral in the outgoing phase space will be of the form

(80)

where the function
{\cal F}
, depending on all the variables, need not to be specified for the moment. This can naturally be split into

(81)

Integrating

yields

(82)

Next we set
d^{2}{\mathbf{p}^{\prime}}_{2\perp}=p^{\prime}\;dp^{\prime}\;d\phi
and rewrite

(83)

We carry out the integral with respect to
p^{\prime}
and we use

\delta\left(\sqrt{p^{\prime 2}+p^{\prime}_{1}{}^{2}+{\tilde{M}}^{2}}+\sqrt{p^{\prime 2}+p^{\prime}_{2}{}^{2}+{\tilde{M}}^{2}}-E\right)=\frac{E^{\prime}_{1}E^{\prime}_{2}}{p^{\prime}E}\delta(p^{\prime}-{\cal P}^{\prime})

(84)

where
{\cal P}^{\prime}
was already calculated in Eq. (
78

The final result can be presented as

(85)

with
E^{\prime}_{1}
and
E^{\prime}_{2}
given by Eq. (
79

{\cal F}
are evaluated in terms of the independent variables
p^{\prime}_{1},p^{\prime}_{2},\phi
according to the relations previously developed. Notice that (

z
of the outgoing momenta, together with the rotation angle of the projection of the center of mass direction on the interface.

IV.3
Regions where momentum is not conserved

In this section we determine the allowed region in the
p_{1}^{\prime}
-
p_{2}^{\prime}
plane where the violation of the
z
-component of the momentum occurs.
Also we locate the loci where the scattering amplitude diverge identifying the zeros in the denominators of Eq. (

The condition
{{\cal P}^{\prime}}^{2}>0
determines the existence of momentum-violating events in the (
p^{\prime}_{1}
-
p^{\prime}_{2}
) plane. To characterize the acceptable region we introduce a convenient parametrization defined by

\frac{p^{\prime}_{1}}{E}=\rho\cos\alpha\equiv X,\qquad\frac{p^{\prime}_{2}}{E}=\rho\sin\alpha\equiv Y,\qquad{\mathfrak{b}}=\frac{p}{E},\qquad{\mathfrak{c}}=\frac{{\tilde{M}}}{E},\qquad{\mathfrak{b}}^{2}+{\mathfrak{c}}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}.

(109)

As shown in the Supplementary Materials
[
45
]
, energy conservation provides the necessary condition
\rho<1
for the allowed region. The sufficient condition comes from Eq. (
78
). Using the quantities defined in (
109
) we can write
{{\cal P}^{\prime}}^{2}\geq 0
as

W(\rho,\alpha)\equiv 4\mathfrak{b}^{2}-2\rho^{2}+\rho^{4}\cos^{2}2\alpha\geq 0.

(110)

For a given
\alpha
, the value of
\rho
establishing the limit between the allowed and the forbidden region is given by the solutions of
W(\rho,\alpha)=0
, which are

\rho^{2}_{+}=\left|\frac{1}{\cos^{2}2\alpha}\left(1+\sqrt{1-4\mathfrak{b}^{2}\cos^{2}2\alpha}\right)\right|,\quad\rho^{2}_{-}=\left|\frac{1}{\cos^{2}2\alpha}\left(1-\sqrt{1-4\mathfrak{b}^{2}\cos^{2}2\alpha}\right)\right|.

(111)

We find that the relation
\rho_{-}<1<\rho_{+}
is satisfied, so that the allowed region is determined by
\rho_{-}
. To decide whether this region is either
\rho>\rho_{-}
or
\rho<\rho_{-}
we consider the behavior of the function
W(\rho,\alpha)
in Eq. (
110

W(\rho_{-},\alpha)=0

\frac{\partial W(\rho,\alpha)}{\partial\rho^{2}}=-2(1-\rho^{2}\cos^{2}2\alpha)<0

is a decreasing function of
\rho
in the range
\rho<1

W=4{\mathfrak{b}}^{2}>0

occurs at
\rho=0
and the minimum is
W=0
at
\rho=\rho_{-}
.
Thus the allowed region for momentum violation at a given angle

\alpha
is for
\rho<\rho_{-}(\alpha)

\cos 2\alpha\rightarrow 0

\rho_{-}\rightarrow\sqrt{2}\,\mathfrak{b}

{\cal M}^{NC}

IV.4
Comparison between the amplitudes
{\cal M}^{C}
and
{\cal M}^{NC}
to order
{\tilde{\theta}}

The purpose of this section is to compare both scattering amplitudes in a suitable common region. First we identify the kinematics of the momentum-conserving case in our notation.
In the center of mass frame we have

\displaystyle p_{1}^{\mu}=\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{p}^{2}+{\tilde{M}}^{2}},\mathbf{p}\right),\quad p_{2}^{\mu}=\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{p}^{2}\mathbf{+}{\tilde{M}}^{2}},-\mathbf{p}\right)\quad p_{1}^{\prime\mu}=\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{p}^{\prime 2}\mathbf{+}{\tilde{M}}^{2}},\mathbf{p}^{\prime}\right),\quad p_{2}^{\prime\mu}=\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{p}^{\prime 2}\mathbf{+}{\tilde{M}}^{2}},-\mathbf{p}^{\prime}\right)

(121)

with

|\mathbf{p}^{\prime}|=|\mathbf{p}|
and
E_{1}=E_{2}=E_{1}^{\prime}=E_{2}^{\prime}=E/2=\sqrt{|\mathbf{p}|^{2}+{\tilde{M}}^{2}}
.

In our notation we have

\displaystyle p_{1}^{\mu}=(E/2,\;p\sin\theta,\;0,\;p\cos\theta),\qquad p_{2}^{\mu}=(E/2,\;-p\sin\theta,\;0,\;-p\cos\theta),

\displaystyle p_{1}^{\prime\mu}=(E/2,\;p^{\prime}\cos\phi,\;p^{\prime}\sin\phi,\;p_{1}^{\prime}),\qquad p_{2}^{\prime\mu}=(E/2,\;-p^{\prime}\cos\phi,\;-p^{\prime}\sin\phi,p_{2}^{\prime})

(122)

Momentum conservation demands

p=|\mathbf{p}|,\quad p^{\prime}_{1}=-p^{\prime}_{2},\quad|\mathbf{p}_{1}^{\prime}|^{2}=p^{\prime 2}+p_{1}^{\prime 2},

(123)

which means that in our (
p^{\prime}_{1}
-
p^{\prime}_{2}
) plane, the line
X=-Y,\,(\alpha=3\pi/4)
is selected when momentum is conserved. We define the ratio

r=\left|\frac{{\cal M}^{NC}}{{\cal M}^{C}}\right|_{X=-Y}

(124)

and calculate it for normal incidence
(\theta=0)
. In the notation where the square bracket
[]
denotes the dimension of the quantity inside, we have that

[\tilde{\theta}]=[{\tilde{\lambda}}]={\rm eV}
. Consequently
[{\cal M}^{NC}]={(\rm eV)}^{-1}
and
[{\cal M}^{C}]={(\rm eV)}^{0}
such that
[r]={(\rm eV)}^{-1}
.

The momentum conserving amplitude is

{\cal M}^{C}={\tilde{\lambda}}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{(p_{1}^{\prime}-p_{1})^{2}-m^{2}}+\frac{1}{(p_{2}^{\prime}-p_{1})^{2}-m^{2}}\right),

(125)

with

\displaystyle(p_{1}^{\prime}-p_{1})^{2}=2{\tilde{M}}^{2}-\frac{E^{2}}{2}+2p\,p^{\prime}_{1},\qquad(p_{2}^{\prime}-p_{1})^{2}=2{\tilde{M}}^{2}-\frac{E^{2}}{2}-2p\,p^{\prime}_{1},

(126)

yielding

\displaystyle{\cal M}^{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{2\tilde{\lambda}^{2}}{E^{2}}\left(\frac{2\mathfrak{b}^{2}+\mathfrak{r}^{2}}{(2\mathfrak{b}^{2}+\mathfrak{r}^{2})^{2}-4\mathfrak{b}^{2}\rho^{2}\cos^{2}\alpha}\right),

(127)

where we use
p^{\prime}_{1}/E=\rho\cos\alpha
together with
p=\sqrt{E^{2}/4-{\tilde{M}}^{2}}
.

As an intermediate case between the ultra relativistic limit and the unrestricted value of the parameters, let us consider
{\tilde{M}}\neq 0,\,m=0=\mathfrak{r}
, which describes the exchange of a massless
\theta
-particle analogous to the photon. Here we have

\displaystyle\left|{\cal M}^{C}\right|_{X=-Y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{2\tilde{\lambda}^{2}}{E^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2\mathfrak{b}^{2}-\;{\rho^{2}}}\right).

(128)

Next we start from the general form of the non-conserving amplitude to order
{\tilde{\theta}}

\mathcal{M}^{NC}=\tilde{\lambda}^{2}\tilde{\theta}\left(\frac{1}{(q_{1}\cdot q_{1}-m^{2})(q_{2}\cdot q_{2}-m^{2})}+\frac{1}{(\tilde{q}_{1}\cdot\tilde{q}_{1}-m^{2})(\tilde{q}_{2}\cdot\tilde{q}_{2}-m^{2})}\right),

(129)

together with the denominators evaluated at
X=-Y
and
m=0

\displaystyle\left(q_{1}\cdot q_{1}-m^{2}\right)=\left(q_{2}\cdot q_{2}-m^{2}\right)=-4\mathfrak{b}(2\mathfrak{b}+\sqrt{2}\rho),

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{q}_{1}\cdot\tilde{q}_{1}-m^{2}\right)=\left(\tilde{q}_{2}\cdot\tilde{q}_{2}-m^{2}\right)=-4\mathfrak{b}(2\mathfrak{b}-\sqrt{2}\rho),

(130)

yielding

\left|{\cal M}^{NC}\right|_{X=-Y}=\frac{{\tilde{\lambda}}^{2}{\tilde{\theta}}}{E^{4}\mathfrak{b}^{2}}\left(\frac{2\mathfrak{b}^{2}+\rho^{2}}{(2\mathfrak{b}^{2}-\rho^{2})^{2}}\right).

(131)

The final result is

r=\left|\frac{{\cal M}^{NC}}{{\cal M}^{C}}\right|_{X=-Y}=\frac{\tilde{\theta}}{2E^{2}\mathfrak{b}^{2}}\left(\frac{2\mathfrak{b}^{2}+\rho^{2}}{2\mathfrak{b}^{2}-\rho^{2}}\right)

(132)

Though this ratio is suppressed by the factor
{\tilde{\theta}}/E^{2}
, the presence of singularities in the allowed region (shaded area in the figures) might serve as an enhancement factor when
\rho
is close the maximum allowed value of
\sqrt{2}\mathfrak{b}
along
X=-Y
. The ratio (
132
) further simplifies in the ultrarelativistic limit
E>>({\tilde{M}},m)
, when we have
\mathfrak{b}=1/2
.
The case
\mathfrak{r}\neq 0
is more elaborate and we only make a few comments. In the line
X=-Y
, the amplitude
{\cal M}^{C}
diverges for
\rho=(2\mathfrak{b}^{2}+\mathfrak{r}^{2})/(\sqrt{2}\mathfrak{b})>\sqrt{2}\mathfrak{b}
, that is to say, outside the allowed region for
\rho
. Then any enhancement factor can appear only due to the vicinity of a singularity
in the amplitude
{\cal M}^{NC}
. These do not arise from the contribution
\rho_{1}
, as seen in Fig
9
and Fig.
10
. On the contrary,
\rho_{3}
might produce singularities inside the shaded region. To find them we look for the value of
\rho_{3}
in the line
X=-Y
obtaining

\rho_{3}(\alpha=3\pi/4)=\left|\frac{\mathfrak{r}^{2}-2\mathfrak{b}^{2}}{\sqrt{2}\mathfrak{b}}\right|\equiv\rho_{30},

(133)

after taking the limit in the corresponding Eq.(
115
). We distinguish two cases recalling that the allowed region occurs for
\rho<\sqrt{2}\mathfrak{b}
along the line. When
\mathfrak{r}<\sqrt{2}\mathfrak{b}
we have
\rho_{30}=\sqrt{2}\mathfrak{b}-\mathfrak{r}^{2}/(\sqrt{2}\mathfrak{b})<\sqrt{2}\mathfrak{b}
so that the singularity falls inside the allowed region. This can be seen in the second plot of Fig.
7
, for example. In the complementary case

\mathfrak{r}>\sqrt{2}\mathfrak{b}
we have to demand

(\mathfrak{r}^{2}-2\mathfrak{b}^{2})/(\sqrt{2}\mathfrak{b})\leq\sqrt{2}\mathfrak{b}
for the singularity to be available. This yields
\mathfrak{r}\leq 2\mathfrak{b}
. The equality is illustrated in the second plot in Fig.
8
where the intersection of the upper curve of the graph with the boundary of the shaded area
must correspond to
\alpha=3\pi/4
. Notice that this is consistent with the property
\alpha_{c3}<\pi/4
. Clearly,
\rho_{30}
falls outside the allowed region when
2\mathfrak{b}\leq\mathfrak{r}<\infty
and no enhancement can take place.

V
Summary and Conclusions

Our results can be summarized in two topics: (i) the quantization of the
\theta
-scalar field
\Phi
in the presence of the interface
\Sigma
at
z=0
and (ii) the study of phenomena arising as a consequence of the non-conservation of the linear momentum in the direction perpendicular to the interface.

The quantization is simplified by using separation of variables so that the each component of the field is a product of a standard plane wave propagating parallel to the interface in the
x
-
y
directions, times a
z
-dependent field that carries the information of the interface via the boundary conditions. In an abuse of language w e refer to the latter as the normal modes of the system, which trivially combine with the contribution in the orthogonal directions. We label them by
\phi^{k}(z)
where
k
is always positive.
The normal modes are a triplet of plane waves in the
z
axis resulting from the application of the boundary conditions at the interface. They are labeled as ingoing modes when we have only an incident wave impinging on the interface. This means we can have either left (L) or right (R) ingoing modes as shown in Fig.
2
. These modes represent a particle entering the interface from either side. An explicit form of these modes is given in Eqs. (
13
) and (
14
). To deal with particles coming out from the interface we introduce the outgoing L-R modes composed only by one plane wave leaving the interface as depicted in Fig.
3
. Outgoing modes are defined as the complex conjugate of the ingoing modes and they are related also by a symmetric unitary transformation as shown in Eqs. (
28
)-(
29
). The component of the momentum
k_{z}
of a particle described by an ingoing or outgoing mode is related to the label
k
of the mode according to the Table
1
.

The non-trivial calculations showing the orthogonality and completeness of the normal modes are relegated to the Supplementary Materials
[
45
]
, presenting only the results in the main text.
The quantization follows the standard procedure of writing the field
\Phi
either as a superposition of
L
-
R
ingoing modes or
L
-
R
outgoing modes, supplemented by the plane waves in the orthogonal directions, and introducing the corresponding creation and annihilation operators according to Eqs. (
33
) and (
46
), respectively. In parallel we calculate the Hamiltonian which we impose to be positive definite for a general field configuration by demanding
{\tilde{\theta}}
to be negative. The same condition is necessary to have the completeness of the normal modes. We interpret this apparent restriction as a consequence of the liberty we have in choosing the sign of the coupling
\theta^{\alpha}\Phi\partial_{\alpha}\Phi

in our initial Lagrangian (
1
). In other words, given a configuration as in Fig.
1
, the sign of
{\tilde{\theta}}
is fixed. When
{\tilde{\theta}}
is positive (negative) we must choose the plus (minus) sign in from of the
\theta
-coupling to have a positive definite Hamiltonian. In terms of the creation and annihilation operators the Hamiltonian and the total momentum in the direction perpendicular to the interface
{\mathbf{P}}_{\perp}
reduce to the expected expressions (
39
) and (
40
), respectively. As a final step in the quantization we calculate the Feynman propagator of the field
\Phi
, which is written in the convenient form (
50
)
showing explicitly the momentum violation contribution as well as the correct limit when
{\tilde{\theta}}=0
.

The next applications we deal with focus upon the momentum non-conserving effects produced by the interface. First we consider a standard scalar particle
\Psi
with mass
M
, which does not feel the interface, and calculate its decay rate in the center of mass into two
\theta
-scalar particles. The interaction is given by the Lagrangian (
51
). Momentum non conservation of the outgoing particles open new channels in the decay such as the outgoing
L
-
L
and
R
-
R
modes whose widths are calculated in Eq. (
70
). The decay threshold is also modified and we have the following result: when
4m^{2}<M^{2}<4m^{2}+\pi^{2}{\tilde{\theta}}^{2}/4
only the
L
-
L
and
R
-
R
modes are open, while
4m^{2}+\pi^{2}{\tilde{\theta}}^{2}/4<M^{2}
allows the appearance of the outgoing
L
-
R
mode. For the total decay rate
\Gamma_{T}
we find
\Gamma_{0}<\Gamma_{T}<1.64\Gamma_{0}
where
\Gamma_{0}
is the decay rate with no interface.

In our second application we consider the scattering of standard charged scalar particles
\chi^{\pm}
mediated by a
\theta
-scalar particle, through the interaction
{\tilde{\lambda}}\Phi\chi^{\dagger}\chi
. We take two incoming equal charges in their center of mass going into a two-body final state, such that only the
t
and
u
channels contribute. Our main interest is in the momentum-violation contribution to the scattering amplitude whose final expression in Eq. (
88
) lacks the delta function corresponding to the momentum conservation in the
z
-direction perpendicular to the interface. The leaves three undetermined variables in the final state, instead of the usual two when we have full four momentum conservation.
The kinematics is described in section
IV.1
and the free final variables are chosen to be the
z
component of the momentum of each outgoing particle,
p^{\prime}_{1}
and
p^{\prime}_{2}
, together with the angle
\phi
measuring the rotation of the projection of the center of mass direction on the interface, where the perpendicular momentum is conserved. We obtain the scattering amplitude in section
IV.2
and select the momentum non-conserving contribution
{\cal M}^{NC}
in Eq. (
95
). Our aim is to determine a measure for the non-zero probability that allows these events to occur. We obtain the transition probability per unit time
d{\dot{P}}
in Eq. (
96
) and we introduce the concept of flux per unit length
\tilde{F}
in (
97
), which serves to define the following generalization of the standard cross section in the center of mass

d\,\Xi=\frac{d{\dot{P}}}{\tilde{F}}=\frac{1}{32\pi^{3}}\frac{1}{|{\mathbf{p}}|E^{2}}\,\left|{\cal M}^{NC}\right|^{2}\,dp^{\prime}_{1}dp^{\prime}_{2}\,d\phi.

The quantity
\Xi
, with dimensions of
{\rm cm}^{3}
measures the abundance of momentum-violating events in the scattering process.

Next we focus on the scattering amplitude
{\cal M}^{NC}
and determine the allowed kinematical region in the
p^{\prime}_{1}
-
p^{\prime}_{2}
plane, together with the location of its poles. At this stage we restrict ourselves to normal incidence (
\theta=0
) and introduce the convenient polar notation in (
109
) for the
p^{\prime}_{1}
-
p^{\prime}_{2}
plane. The allowed region is determined by the condition
0<\rho<\rho_{-}
, with
\rho_{-}
given in the second Eq. (
111
) and it is depicted as a shaded area in all the included polar plots. There we also indicate the loci of the poles of
{\cal M}^{NC}
determined by Eqs. (
115
) in different approximations. Symmetry considerations imply that it is sufficient to plot the lines
\rho_{1}(\alpha)=0=\rho_{3}(\alpha)
determining such poles, such that the full description is obtained after adding a rigid rotation of the graph by
\pi
radians. The simplest case is when we take
m=0\,(\mathfrak{r}=0)
which is plotted in Figs.
5
-
6
for different choices of
\mathfrak{b}=p/E
. In this case, the poles of the scattering amplitude fall outside of the allowed momentum-violating region, displaying touching points only at the angles
\alpha=(2n+1)\pi/4
, where
\rho=\sqrt{2}\mathfrak{b}
.
The case
\mathfrak{r}\neq 0
is more elaborate since the solutions giving the position of the poles acquire imaginary contributions in some angular ranges determined by the parameters in Eqs. (
119
) and (
120
) together with the indications just before these equations. We observe that the angles determining the forbidden angular regions range from zero to
\pi/4
over the whole definition interval.
The forbidden angular ranges are shown by dashed lines in the figures, and they behave as asymptotes where the loci displaying the poles collapse. One branch of the loci determined by
\rho_{3}=0
penetrates the shaded region while the other branch always recedes from it, as shown in Figs.
7
-
8
. In this case we have the critical angle
\alpha_{c3}
in Eq. (
119
), determined by
\mathfrak{r}>\sqrt{2}\,\mathfrak{b}
, beyond which an imaginary part develops. On the other hand, the loci determined by
\rho_{1}
always recedes from the shaded region and has an imaginary part for all values of
\mathfrak{r}
, according to Eq. (
120
). These features are shown in Figs.

9
-
10
.

As a final result in this work we compare the magnitudes of the momentum non-conserving scattering amplitude
{\cal M}^{NC}
with that of the momentum conserving contribution

{\cal M}^{C}
by defining its ratio
r
in Eq. (
124
). Since momentum conservation sets
p^{\prime}_{1}=-p^{\prime}_{2}
we choose the line
X=-Y
, in the notation of Eq. (
109
), to set the comparison. In the case
m=\mathfrak{r}=0
we obtain

r=\left|\frac{{\cal M}^{NC}}{{\cal M}^{C}}\right|_{X=-Y}=\frac{\tilde{\theta}}{2E^{2}\mathfrak{b}^{2}}\left(\frac{2\mathfrak{b}^{2}+\rho^{2}}{2\mathfrak{b}^{2}-\rho^{2}}\right),

(134)

showing that the ratio is suppressed by the factor
{\tilde{\theta}}/E^{2}
. Nevertheless, the presence of singularities in the allowed region
0<\rho<\sqrt{2}\mathfrak{b}
might serve as an enhancement factor when
\rho
is close the maximum allowed value of
\sqrt{2}\mathfrak{b}
along the line
X=-Y
. The ratio (
134
) further simplifies in the ultrarelativistic limit
E>>({\tilde{M}},m)
, when we have
\mathfrak{b}=1/2
.

The case
\mathfrak{r}\neq 0
is more involved and we only make a few comments. When
X=-Y
, the amplitude
{\cal M}^{C}
diverges for
\rho=(2\mathfrak{b}^{2}+\mathfrak{r}^{2})/(\sqrt{2}\mathfrak{b})>\sqrt{2}\mathfrak{b}
, that is to say, outside the shaded region for
\rho
. Then, any enhancement factor can appear only due to the vicinity of a singularity
in the amplitude
{\cal M}^{NC}
. These do not arise from the contribution
\rho_{1}
, as seen in Fig
9
and Fig.
10
. On the contrary,
\rho_{3}
produces singularities inside the shaded region only in the range
0<\mathfrak{r}<2\mathfrak{b}
, as discussed in detail at the end of section
IV.4
. In this way, momentum violating events may become important for some choices in the parameter region.
