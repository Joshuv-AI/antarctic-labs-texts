Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde: Two Faces of LLMs
Matteo Gioele Collu, Tom Janssen-Groesbeek, Stefanos Koffas, Mauro Conti, Stjepan Picek

Abstract
Recently, we have witnessed a rise in the use of Large Language Models (LLMs), especially in applications like chatbots. Safety mechanisms are implemented to prevent improper responses from these chatbots. In this work, we bypass these measures for ChatGPT and Gemini by making them impersonate complex personas with personality characteristics that are not aligned with a truthful assistant. First, we create elaborate biographies of these personas, which we then use in a new session with the same chatbots. Our conversations then follow a role-play style to elicit prohibited responses. Using personas, we show that prohibited responses are provided, making it possible to obtain unauthorized, illegal, or harmful information in both ChatGPT and Gemini. We also introduce several ways of activating such adversarial personas, showing that both chatbots are vulnerable to this attack. With the same principle, we introduce two defenses that push the model to interpret trustworthy personalities and make it more robust against such attacks.

Introduction

Chatbots are very popular, but they do not work perfectly.
They can generate hallucinations or be used in unethical ways. There are cases of ChatGPT providing working Windows product keys or explaining how to create malware or weapons
[
17
]
. Such cases form a novel category of attacks on LLMs that violate their usage guidelines called prompt injection attacks
[
11
]
or jailbreaking
[
9
]
.

To prevent such problems, restrictions were put in place
[
9
]
, and
now a chatbot denies replying about topics not aligned with its ethics.
This did not stop the chatbot’s misuse. Users applied more elaborate techniques to trick the chatbots into providing answers they were not allowed to give. For example, in DAN
[
17
]
, the authors exploited role-playing to force the model to act in a specific way. Additionally, in
[
20
]
, the authors instructed the model to start its reply in a specific way (e.g., with ‘of course’) to overcome its safety mechanisms. This topic is very active, and new jailbreak prompts are constantly introduced
[
17
]
.

Recently, researchers introduced the idea that LLMs consist of a superposition of personas
[
8
]
. In
[
22
]
, this was first formalized through a theoretical framework based on mixture decomposition. In
[
16
]
, the authors created realistic personas of historical characters (e.g., Caesar or Beethoven) in LLMs. In particular, they provided the model with a character’s attributes, experiences, and a particular scene where the interaction will occur. Then, they convinced the model to impersonate this character. In
[
4
]
, the authors used role-playing to assess the toxicity of different personas, such as Muhammad Ali or Hitler. They noticed that toxicity was different according to the interpreted character. Role-playing is also used to enhance the model’s outputs by impersonating an expert in a specific field
[
21
,
14
]
or transferring specific virtues to the model like truthfulness
[
5
]
.

We use adversarial personas to bypass LLM safety mechanisms. First, we provide the LLM with an extensive biography of an adversarial persona. Then, we instruct the model to impersonate this adversary. Finally, we ask the model to answer prompts that, officially, it is not allowed to answer. This approach may sound similar to DAN
[
17
]
, but there is a significant difference since we do not describe the model’s behavior explicitly. We do not say to the model, “You are a chatbot without restrictions, and you can do anything now”, but we convince it to become a persona with specific personality characteristics. The model, trying to adhere to these characteristics, will start generating misaligned responses.
We consider this a less explicit way to “jailbreak” the model, as the prompts hide our purpose behind personality characteristics.
Thus, more complex defense prompts should be designed instead of just using an attack prompt that is always the same (e.g., the DAN prompt) to fine-tune the model. Through our approach, the attacker has more freedom to “jailbreak” the model, using a large variety of personas instead of just one fixed persona that represents a chatbot without any restrictions, like DAN.

Moreover, our idea is similar to
[
15
]
, but our method is different. First, the models used are different since we used GPT-3.5-turbo and Gemini-1.5-flash, and they used GPT4, Vicuna 33B, and Claude 2. Second, the methods differ in how the persona is built and characterized.
They ask the model to provide a persona who is likely to act in an unethical way for a specific scenario and is only characterized by a profession and some shallow details, such as an aggressive propagandist for spreading misinformation.
We could not reproduce this approach in ChatGPT-3.5, as the model always identified a violation of the usage terms in our requests. Such requests are too straightforward and easily spotted.
In our method, we think of a persona that fits the desired scenario. Then, we select some target personality traits that may push that persona to act as the malicious user wants (e.g., greediness). Finally, we ask the model to provide a detailed persona description by emphasizing its personality traits, assigning it a name and some required features for its character.
This encodes the persona’s psychological profile in the generated text so that the model can later infer and interpret.

As a second part of our work, we use personas to defuse potential jailbreaks. We pushed the model towards trustworthy behavior by embedding virtues in a proposed persona
[
5
]
, which is then interpreted by the model. This new persona should be able to understand the content being produced and should never provide the user with potentially dangerous instructions in any scenario, whether it concerns the real, hypothetical, or fictional world.

Interpreting a single persona is not the only way to align the chatbot. In cognitive synergy in human intelligence, different cognitive processes cooperate to improve cognitive functions such as reasoning or creativity
[
19
]
. Cognitive synergy can be applied to an LLM by instructing the model to interpret personas representing cognitive processes that have to
collaborate in generating the final reply
[
19
]
. Inspired by
[
19
]
, we use multiple personas to generate a cognitive inhibition mechanism that reduces the harmful content in the chatbot’s replies.

However, inhibiting the model’s replies can lead to over-defensiveness, making the model deny legitimate requests. In
[
18
]
, the authors showed this phenomenon by evaluating different defense techniques that are currently used. For example, making the model self-evaluate its reply by appending questions such as
“is it harmful?”
to the produced text and feeding it to a new instance of the chat
[
12
]
.
Our approach differs from these techniques as the model is instructed to act as a persona that encodes some virtues, to which the model should align when generating its response. Moreover, only one chat instance is required to implement our defenses.
To our knowledge, we are the first to use trustworthy personas to prevent jailbreaks. Our main contributions are:

•

We investigate adversarial personas in modern chatbots that overcome their safety mechanisms. We empirically show that ChatGPT and Gemini are vulnerable under this threat by convincing them to behave like adversarial personas (e.g., a spy, a killer, or a hacker) and retrieving dangerous information in 38 and 40 out of 40 scenarios that we were not able to do so without the adversarial personas in GPT-3.5-turbo and Gemini-1.5-flash, respectively.

•

We show two ways to activate such adversarial personas, either by commanding the chatbot to interpret that persona or implying that we are already talking with that persona (e.g., addressing the chatbot by the persona’s name or using stereotypical language in our replies).

•

We show that personas can be transferred between models.

•

We introduce the inception of the personas, where one persona can be manipulated by another persona. For example, we create an adversarial persona that hears a voice in “its head” forcing it to behave in a specific way.

•

We present Single and Multiple Personas defenses, two techniques that exploit trustworthy personas to increase the model’s robustness against persona attacks or other jailbreaks. By assuming a single ethical persona or a synergy of multiple personas, we could increase the models’ robustness against 114 and 89 jailbreaking prompts currently working in GPT-3.5-turbo and Gemini-1.5-flash, respectively.

We disclosed details about our attack to OpenAI, Google, and Microsoft.
In this work, we redacted some parts of the conversations containing harmful instructions.
Our conversations can be found in our anonymous Github.

Background

Alignment.
Alignment removes undesired behaviors from LLMs
[
22
]
and “aligns” the model with human objectives (e.g., avoiding discrimination)
[
7
]
. A popular alignment technique is Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
[
2
]
, where humans provide feedback on the system’s behavior
[
3
]
. In this way, we could solve tasks that we cannot describe their solutions through mathematical formulas, but we can easily recognize them visually
[
3
]
.

Jailbreaks.
Adversaries can bypass the models’ restrictions by using clever prompts, which is termed
jailbreaking

[
20
]
. Usually, in these cases, users introduce specific role plays to alter the model’s ego, allowing it to answer user queries unethically. A well-known jailbreaking prompt is “Do Anything Now” (DAN), which lets the model generate offensive or biased comments on different topics such as politics or race
[
17
]
. DAN can also be used to generate specific personal information or other sensitive data
[
6
]
.

Prompting.
Prompting is the way a user communicates with the chatbot. Any request or input given to the chatbot by the user during the interaction is termed a user prompt. Users can also provide custom instructions directly to the system. These instructions can be set once and applied across all conversations, eliminating the need to specify them in every new interaction. The model considers this additional information when processing user prompts.

3
Attack

3.1
Motivation

We assume that the model is a superposition of personas. Through an initial experiment, we observed that such personas can sometimes be “awakened” through simple stereotypical prompts.
For example, using the word “comrade” in our prompt, we saw that the model’s replies are shifted towards a persona with specific characteristics. Additionally, using a name that indicates a relation with a topic may also trigger this behavior. For instance, just asking the model to behave as “Cipher” without giving further details results in better replies related to hacking. We believe the model is biased and associates such names with predefined characteristics. However, using “adversaries” from the real world like Hitler or Pablo Escobar, we could not bypass the model’s safety mechanisms as it could easily spot our unethical prompts. Another interesting aspect of this experiment is that the model tends to reply more easily to some topics, e.g., industrial espionage, than others, e.g., hate against religions.
This behavior, however, is not robust, and the desired replies are not always guaranteed. For this reason, we decided to improve this functionality by introducing a detailed description of the adversarial persona to overcome the model’s safety mechanisms.

3.2
Method

Figure 1
:
The attack pipeline.

Our main assumption is that the model simulates a personality, which appears in the generated text. This personality influences its behavior and helps the model produce statements that better fit the context of the conversation with the user. It is shown in
[
8
]
,
[
13
]
that personality traits emerge from the generated text and that the model indirectly learns them from biases in the training set. A training set is imbalanced in the distribution of text styles among different topics, as some topics are closely tied to the style of the text used to discuss them. Thus, a training set retrieved from real-world data will be imbalanced, and specific text styles will exist only for specific discussion topics.

When the models role-play some personas, they tend to characterize the style with some virtues, such as truthfulness from scientific people, but also toxic or violent content from personalities such as dictators or Voldemort (super-villain from the Harry Potter books)
[
5
]
,
[
4
]
. Making the model assume a role is also a widespread technique used in prompt engineering
[
21
]
, where a model performed better in the given task when interpreting an expert in that field.

However, in general, the model’s personality is not well defined. One of the main assumptions is that the model builds a superposition of different personalities. Their distribution depends on the context of the conversation
and the user’s requests and choice of words. The model is not only trying to reproduce from the training set the content that optimizes the answer but also the style.

We hypothesize that during the conversation, the model characterizes the style by merging different personalities with a fixed one, the truthful assistant. The truthful assistant represents the base persona interpreted by the model, which should reply to the user in a truthful way. This personality is tuned by RLHF, where the model is aligned to specific goals like being helpful, honest, and harmless.

An important question is whether some instructions comply with another personality other than a truthful assistant. According to the knowledge in the training set, how much would a serial killer comply with being a truthful assistant? What if the model now believes it is not an AI model but another persona? There is a theory in which the superposition almost always tends to collapse in favor of a fixed personality (Waluigi effect
[
8
]
). With this, we push the model to believe in being someone else, a personality prone to provide help for harmful requests and discard the truthful assistant. This allows us to bypass safety alignments and produce replies for harmful requests.

Our attacks make the model interpret a personality built to provide information for topics against its policies. The personality is then inferred from the model by learning the life and experiences of a persona generated by the model itself. According to what the model infers, it will align with the target personality by adopting the text style that optimizes its idea of the target personality. A different text style may imply a different probability distribution over the predicted next token. At a high level, one would not expect a malicious persona to reply helpfully, honestly, or in a harmless way in general. A schematic of our approach is shown in
Figure 1
, and the steps to achieve this are:

1.

Choose a scenario for which we need harmful instructions from the model.

2.

Write up an initial persona description of a persona that would likely know those instructions and assign it untrustworthy personality traits. We want the traits to be adopted by the model, influencing the way it generates text.

3.

Use the model to further elaborate on that persona description by following the user’s requests based on the initial write-up from the previous step. In particular, instruct the model that it is an expert writer, and it needs to write a detailed persona biography based on the personality traits you deem important for the task. The user may also expand sections that describe the persona to achieve better characterization. In this way, the model may transfer its beliefs about that personality, which are biased from the distribution of text styles in the training set. This will help later to make the model adopt the personality in the way intended by the malicious user.

4.

In a new chat instance, present the detailed persona biography to the model so that it learns and contextualizes who this persona is.

5.

Ask the model to act, talk, and have the knowledge that the persona has, to let it reason as that persona. The model created the persona according to its beliefs, so it infers the target personality traits wanted by the malicious user.

6.

Interact with the persona in a role-playing manner. This way, the model will start producing text in a style that aligns with the target personality.

7.

Ask the model for harmful instructions or content.

3.3
Experimental Setup

We ran our experiments on GPT-3.5 (publicly implemented in ChatGPT from OpenAI), GPT-3.5-turbo (accessed via OpenAI APIs), and Gemini-1.5-flash from Google (accessed via Google AI Studio and Gemini APIs).

our Github
.
Both models are deployed in free and publicly available playgrounds, making the threat more impactful.
We initially considered different scenarios to craft and improve the attack and created personas that would fit them, e.g., an online drug dealer for learning how to find and sell drugs, a whistle-blower that would leak private keys or a sleeper agent for industrial espionage. We then tested the attacks in 40 different scenarios, selected from DAN’s pool of illicit questions.

3

3

https://github.com/verazuo/jailbreak˙llms/blob/main/data/forbidden˙question
Scenarios are divided into four categories, recognized as illicit by OpenAI’s usage policies: Physical Harm, Malware, Hate Speech, and Fraud. We create a specific persona that fits each category: a Mercenary for Physical Harm, a Hacker for Malware, a Propagandist for Hate Speech, and a Scammer for Fraud. We test every scenario, asking the model to reply to the illicit requests in four different settings:

•

We directly ask the model for the illicit information.

•

We ask ChatGPT to return common names. Then we ask the model to role-play a neutral persona, only identified by such a common name (e.g., James Smith).

•

We ask the model to role-play a specific persona, only identified by its name, which is one of those returned by the model when asked to create an adversarial persona (e.g., Marcus Blackwood).

•

We ask the model to role-play the adversarial persona described by its biography. This is the Persona Attack shown in
Figure 1
.

In the first three cases, all 40 questions are asked to the model. In the final case, the Persona Attack, we are interested only in getting illicit information for the target category, as we want the Personas to be specialized in their category, following the idea that experts provide better results in their field.

Different models can have different perceptions of some personality traits and would build the persona differently. Therefore, the main setup for our experiment is to make the target model create the persona that it will interpret later. We have also tested the transferability of the created personas between different LLMs (e.g., adopt a persona generated by ChatGPT in Gemini).
Our goal is to make the models deviate from their safety alignments
and provide useful instructions, or insights, for the harmful user requests.
The model is asked to operate as an expert historical/thriller writer to create the personas.
It will describe the persona’s life, experiences, and how it grew to highlight its virtues but mainly its flaws that could steer its behavior towards the attacker’s goal.

3.4
Results

Once the persona is created, it is fed to a new chat instance.
Then, the model is pushed to respond like that persona, either asking it explicitly (e.g., “You are now this persona”) or, as shown in
Figure 4
, implicitly (e.g., “Comrade Veronov, are you awake?”). Then, the model will build the superposition of a truthful agent and our persona by selecting the correct text style that suits that persona. If the user’s text style is aligned with the given persona, iteration after iteration, the model will be pushed toward our persona. Once the model begins to respond like the persona, it starts to neglect the safety alignments intended for a truthful AI assistant.
The user, when creating the persona, may add various personality traits through a few successive prompts, steering it toward a desired behavior.
In the figures, we only show conversations from ChatGPT, but we observed the same behavior for Gemini. We can draw the following conclusions:

•

Privilege escalation with the persona
: We see that the models deny the requests when they do not act as an adversarial persona (
Figure 2
). When they are interpreting the persona, they would reply according to its personality (
Figure 3
). This suggests that different personalities have different privileges when interacting with the user and that safety alignments do not cover personalities. Using adversarial personas allowed the generation of illicit content in 38 out of 40 scenarios with GPT-3.5-turbo, while in Gemini, the attack was always successful.

•

Implicit starting of role-playing
: In
Figure 4
, the model can be steered to the persona by stereotypical talk or generic keywords tied to specific characters. In this case, the persona was a Soviet sleeper agent, and the model assumed the role-play when it was called Comrade, or there were references to the Motherland. Even though filters could easily spot malicious personas like Stalin, such generic words are not directly connected with an adversary. Thus, a filter may not recognize a threat and still allow role-playing.

•

Provided instructions can be expanded and specific tools can be provided
: In
Figure 5
, we see that when the model starts providing instructions, the user can ask to detail them better. Thus, we consider an attack successful as soon as the model starts complying with the request because it typically provides more detailed explanations at that point. Additionally, the model can provide specific tools like GPS trackers for stalking or step-by-step guides for shipping drugs online.

•

Superposition collapse with explicit harmful requests
: The model would stop acting when an explicit request is asked. We hypothesize this happens because the model is reinforced to recognize explicit harmful requests through various such prompts during alignment.

•

Persona can interpret other personas - inception of personas
:
Our persona could be manipulated by a so-called "voice in its head". As a result, the malicious user could use this voice to give orders to the persona. One of them was to make the persona pretend to be a video tutorial maker, allowing it to improve its replies, which initially were vague and poetical.
From that point, the persona continued to help the user and became more helpful with its replies, suggesting that it is possible to create nested personas that are built ad-hoc for a specific scenario.

•

Persona transferred from one model to another
: Gemini refused to create a black hat hacker persona. However, we transferred the one created in ChatGPT and achieved successful results (see Gemini - Malware in
Table 2
).

•

Some replies were general
: In some cases, the instructions provided by the model were general and could only be expanded by user request. This may be for different reasons, such as the scenario being too generic or not having enough power/knowledge from the model. However, we verified that with more powerful models, like GPT-4o, the instructions can be more detailed, meaning the attack can be more effective.

•

Some categories are more robust than others
: We targeted 40 scenarios in four different categories. In both models, it has been possible to get illicit information for Physical Harm, Malware, and Fraud by just using the names provided by the model when asked for an adversarial persona. However, the Hate speech category in GPT-3.5-turbo showed some more robustness, as illicit content could be obtained only when the persona is identified by its description. This happened in 8 out of 10 scenarios, with the model showing concerns or disclaimers for one of them. In Gemini, the attack was always successful when a description of the adversarial persona was provided, and in at most 2 scenarios when only its name was provided.

•

Some names already convey adversarial traits
: We role-play by asking the models to interpret a persona, identified only by its name, which can be either common or assigned to the adversarial personas generated by the model before. Some names, such as Marcus Blackwood, Conrad von Steinhardt, and Cipher, increase the success rate of obtaining illicit information for certain categories, even without the description of the adversarial persona behind them (see
Table 1
). The success rate is higher than when the model interprets a persona with a common name, such as James Smith. Additionally, some adversarial names can be “misinterpreted”. For example, Dr. Cynthia Harrington, the name assigned to a propagandist persona, was perceived as trustworthy, likely because the title “Doctor” played a beneficial role. Moreover, Gemini assigned Eastern European names when building adversarial personas, increasing the attack’s effectiveness compared to common occidental names. We believe that the models stereotype names, already conveying information about the traits of the persona they should represent.

•

Generalizability of our attack
: We tested our attack on three additional models:
Bing Chat, Bard, and GPT-4o. Even though it was a simple experiment, the attack was effective across these platforms; however, further experimentation is necessary for robust conclusions.

4
Defense

Our approach
manipulates the model’s behavior, which could also be beneficial and make the model more robust against attacks.
Here, we discuss our persona-based defenses and test them against currently successful jailbreaking prompts, showing that they can be successful in fending off persona attacks.

4.1
Method

First, we need to define the expected reply behavior. Ideally, the model should always reply accurately to user queries without violating its policies or mentioning information that may be misused by a malicious user. Moreover, it would be preferable that role-playing with the user is not denied, even if this implies interpreting a persona that may be involved in illegal activities or present bad behavior. In that case, the model should always recognize its role and boundaries, producing replies aligned with the persona only if the policies allow it.

The behaviors we want the model to adopt are encoded in the created personas. For this, we adopt a similar approach to construct the defender personas as we do with the attacking ones. We select some traits we want the model to encode, like trustworthiness or a sense of justice, and ask the model to create a persona that shows them. The model will then create a description of the persona highlighting these traits.

We investigated two different persona defense approaches, one that uses a single persona (Single Persona Defense) and another that instructs the LLM to consider multiple personas at once (Multiple Persona Defense).
The Single Persona Defense is set up by instructing the model to create a persona with positive personality traits. The model will characterize the life of the persona, making it infer that this persona will act ethically.
We do not restrict our persona to specific scenarios (e.g., an expert specialized in online drug sales).
Instead, we more generally ask for a description of a persona that would act upon and uphold LLM’s policies. We then assume that with every follow-up request from the user, the model will consider what would be within the limits of these policies. In
Figure 6
, we provide our Single Persona Defense pipeline.

Figure 6
:
The Single Persona Defense pipeline via user or system prompts.

Our initial attempts for a Single Persona Defense resulted in a strict model that refused any role-playing. Thus, we tweaked our prompts to introduce a persona that would adhere to the policies more loosely. In turn, the model would become lenient and still easily reply with the prohibited information, just like without any defense. We have not yet found the correct instructions, which results in a balanced model that is neither too strict (e.g., not allowing role-playing at all) nor too lenient (e.g., responding to malicious requests).

With the Multiple Persona Defense, we provide shallower persona descriptions but in a way that they can still recall the trustworthy personality traits that we require for them. We then instruct the model to consider how this group of personas would handle user requests. The group of personas is always composed by the LLM: an Expert Lawyer, a Cybersecurity Expert, a Military and Defense Expert, an expert Financial Analyst, an Ethical Hacker, an Expert Digital Forensic Analyst, a Medical Ethics Expert, an Expert Political Scientist, and a Financial Regulator.
This defense is implemented similarly to
[
19
]
, where the authors first ask the model to identify the personas that are better able to provide knowledge and expertise about the user’s request and then make these personas simulate a conversation, on which the final reply for the user is generated. However, we adapted this mechanism so that it identifies malicious requests instead of giving the most informed answers.
We believe that personas can make different contributions to the text
and affect the reply generation.
For this reason, we used the Multiple Persona Defense to simulate an inhibition mechanism that would engage when something that goes against the LLM’s usage policies is being generated by the model.
Inhibition is a cognitive process developed by the human brain, and it also plays a fundamental role in human interaction, allowing one to control impulses, maintain attention, and manage distractions.
We believe that inhibition could play a role in making the model more aware that any information produced in a real, hypothetical, or fictional context may be used by the interlocutor for its purposes, which can be illicit.

Thus, our idea is to provide the model with virtues encoded in different personas involved in the process of generating the final reply. First, we ask the model to provide personas that would be able to recognize potentially dangerous instructions in the replies. Different from the attack part, in the defense scenario, the goal of the model is to understand and adopt legitimate behaviors that can be stated explicitly, whereas, in the attack part, they had to be hidden in the complex psychological profile of the used persona, which allowed the attacker to bypass safety restrictions.

In this setting, we can explicitly state the behaviors the model has to assume by providing it with shallower personas that invoke some virtues (sense of law from a Lawyer, etc.). The instructions considered dangerous are the ones that go against OpenAI’s usage policies.

The disallowed usage of the model is described in categories, e.g., Illegal Activity, Generation of Malware, etc. For each of the categories, the model can provide a persona that would be able to understand if a certain text contains potentially dangerous information for that specific category. For example, an Expert Lawyer could deal with Illegal Activity, or a Cybersecurity Expert with the Generation of Malware. In this way, a defender can query the model and create a team of personas, each specialized in a different category, that would be able to evaluate if the text produced by the model contains potential undesired behavior. The process is shown in
Figure 7
.

In this setting, the model is instructed to be a communication system that produces text for a user. It comprises two components: 1) The Chatbot (ChatGPT, or Gemini) and 2) a group of personas. The Chatbot’s role is to read the user’s request and generate a reply. This is its normal behavior. However, it is now instructed in the system prompt to reply to the user as if these personas were assessing the reply. In this way, we simulate an external supervisor for the model. If one of the personas would consider the reply to be potentially dangerous, that reply should not be provided to the user.
Giving the model awareness of ethics by citing these personas in the custom instructions may influence the probability distribution over the next token in a way that promotes ethics.

We assume that our defense is implemented before the user interacts with the model. To simulate this with the models, we insert the defense prompt in the system or custom instructions.
The models will consider these instructions every time they respond

]
. OpenAI also mentions that any information provided in the custom instructions in the ChatGPT playground is known to the model when we start a new conversation, which is aligned to our vision about the persona defense. We believe the model creator will incorporate this defense into the training or post-training procedure to align the model for possible unethical user prompts. The custom system instructions, as they are fed to the model at the beginning of any new conversation, closely resemble this post-training alignment. Another benefit is that we only need to instruct the model once, and for every new conversation, the model follows the same instructions. One limitation of these custom system instructions in the ChatGPT playground is that they cannot be longer than 1500 characters. Thus, we can only use 1500 characters to provide a persona description and instruct the model to act and behave like it. However, this limitation is less restrictive when using system instructions in APIs.

First, to overcome this limitation, we inserted the defense prompt at the beginning of every new chat. However, malicious users can easily make the model forget this defense prompt, and it is unlikely that a malicious user would insert a defensive prompt before trying a jailbreak. For this reason, defense experiments considered system instructions the best place for the defending prompt.

We now use our persona attack to test our defense. by introducing the adversarial persona in a conversation and instructing the model to act in this way. Then, we start role-playing with the model and ask for prohibited information. This time, we simply follow earlier successful conversations using the same prompts. This lets us know that if we did not put the defense in place, this sequence of prompts would lead to the desired (malicious) outcome. Now, we want to see if the same sequence of prompts leads to a different outcome due to the defense. If the model refuses to provide any prohibited information, we see this as a success.

4.2
Experimental Setup

We ran our defense experiments on GPT-3.5-turbo and Gemini-1.5-flash because we had already tried our persona attacks on them, and they allowed custom system instructions. Additionally, Gemini allows developers to control additional external filters that can be set not to block dangerous content. We selected this option because we want to test the safety of the base model without the external help that we assume to be provided by a second LLM. It is worth noting that deactivating these filters does not make the model completely uncensored, as it blocks illicit questions when asked directly.
We initially crafted and fine-tuned the defense prompt by trying to counter the drug dealer persona attack in ChatGPT, whose effectiveness can be easily evaluated since the model returned clear instructions on how to access the dark web marketplaces and how to ship drugs online.
After our defense is applied to the model, we try to get the same illicit content obtained in the previous 40 scenarios, distributed in four categories, by means of the persona attack or currently working jailbreaking prompts.
If the model replies without providing the same dangerous insights obtained with the attacks, we consider the defense successful.

We evaluate the defenses’ effectiveness against four jailbreaking prompts that are currently effective. These prompts consist of a template text in which malicious users can append their illicit requests.
We created one of the used templates, and the rest came from the defunct website Jailbreakchat. We built our prompt after noticing that the model was prone to provide unethical strategies from a persona described as
evil
. Based on this, we ask the model to create this evil persona and describe what it does in the specified target scenario.

Every jailbreaking prompt consists of one of the templates extended with an illicit question (e.g., how to hack into someone’s account or how to create a deadly and undetectable poison), to which the model should reply in an unrestricted mode. For every template, we select the previous 40 questions used for the attacks, providing 160 different prompts. Of the 160 prompts, 114 were effective in GPT-3.5-turbo and 89 in Gemini-1.5-flash.

The attack consists of sending a single message containing the jailbreaking prompt into a new chat instance, to which the model typically replies by providing potentially dangerous information.
Once the model replies in this unrestricted mode, it is possible to ask for more details.
We consider a defense successful when the previously tested jailbreaking prompts are not working anymore, and there is no dangerous information given by the model.

4.3
Results

Single Persona Defense

Our initial experiments for this defense focused only on introducing, using system instructions, a single
good
persona. We either wanted the models to be aware of their policies and restrictions or role-play a persona that would be aware of these policies and adhere to them.
We discuss a few interactions with the models that are especially interesting, as they showed clear cases of the defense either working or not.

First, we created a cybersecurity expert specializing in AI security, as such a persona would act ethically and be wary of any kind of jailbreak attempt. However, this defense was unsuccessful against the persona attack. Only a few prompts of the persona attack are needed to get the prohibited information from the model. This happens either because the defense persona does not have the correct background or its description is not detailed enough.

Next, we provide a persona that explicitly adheres to OpenAI’s ethics and restrictions. We ask the model to create the good and bad persona descriptions in one session and hope it will be more robust against requests for something prohibited. This experiment shows a successful defense as it now refuses to create a bad persona description. However, this can be considered too restrictive, as we cannot even ask it to role-play a bad persona.

Next, we created a good Samaritan persona and instructed the models to act like this persona. Then, we start a new session with the chatbots by role-playing with the good Samaritan persona immediately. Only after this initial role-playing did we introduce the persona attack, and we noticed a promising response from the models. They acknowledge the
bad
persona description and warn the user that it is the good Samaritan persona and can only answer requests within the ethical boundaries. However, ChatGPT refuses to role-play as the bad persona, whereas Gemini can be easily convinced by asking it. ChatGPT characterized the conversation by making references to its ethical commitment and convincing the user to change their mind about the unethical requests. Gemini, on the other hand, still provides dangerous insights. The defense shows more effectiveness in ChatGPT, where it refuses to role-play as the bad persona, but it will respond to some specific questions regarding the persona. If we ask what the bad persona would know how it would respond, it gives some information, but when asked for more details, it refuses. This is the first case where the model does not “over-defend” but instead allows some type of role-playing and warns the user. However, we still see that the model may provide information it should not give.

Finally, we test the effectiveness of the previous setting, where the good Samaritan persona is introduced via system instruction to both models against four currently working jailbreaking templates.
When we ran our experiments,
there were 114 working jailbreaking prompts for GPT-3.5-turbo and 89 for Gemini-1.5-flash.
We instructed the model to act as the good Samaritan in two ways; the first mentioned the behavior that we want the model to adopt (e.g.,
"Chatgpt/Gemini should act and behave like the persona described below"
), while in the second, we use the same explicit interpretation prompt of the attack (e.g.,
"I want you to act like.."
)
In the first case, the defense blocked 55% (63 out of 114) of working jailbreaks in GPT-3.5-turbo and recognized the potential danger of another 7% (8 out of 114). However, it also returned dangerous content for one jailbreak blocked by the base model. In Gemini, the defense blocked 11% (10 out of 89) of the jailbreaks and recognized as dangerous another 12% (11 out of 89). However, it also allowed the malicious user to get illicit information in 28% (20 out of 71) of scenarios already blocked by the base model. The second prompting style returned better results, blocking 91% (104 out of 114) of jailbreaks in GPT-3.5-turbo and recognizing other 5% (6 out of 114) replies as dangerous. In Gemini, this prompting style performed better, allowing to block the 28% (25 out of 89) of jailbreaks and recognized as dangerous 8% (7 out of 89) replies more than the base model. However, in 6% (4 out of 71) of the already blocked cases, the defense helped the malicious user to get illicit information.

We observed that in a defenseless setting, some jailbreaking prompts, by describing a hypothetical scenario, retrieve dangerous information from the model, which is otherwise prohibited.
For example, in one of the tested jailbreaking prompts, the model interprets a super-villain that explains its plan (which is the illicit question for which the malicious user wants a reply) to a hero. Surprisingly, in some cases, the Single Persona defense helps by directing the fictional part to a safer outcome, like the Hero convincing the villain to follow ethics.
However, we also observe that the defensive persona is sometimes integrated into the role-playing, making it a character in the story rather than a trustworthy guide for the models. This influences the defense’s effectiveness against jailbreaks that rely heavily on fiction. At the same time, dangerous instructions provided by the model tend to be more fictional and less insightful for a malicious user.

Multiple Persona Defense

We evaluated the Multiple Persona Defense against the aforementioned 160 jailbreaks and the persona attacks. Our first experiment was to introduce an adversarial persona
after instructing the model to act as the Multiple Personas. We used the same successful descriptions from the attack part and asked the model to interpret the persona. The model recognizes the potential involvement of the persona in illegal contexts and denies interpreting it. The outcome is similar to the Single Persona Defense, where the defense is more effective in ChatGPT than Gemini.
It is debatable whether the defense is effective or the adversarial persona is ineffective, as a model with a defense instruction and one without can be considered two models with different beliefs. Thus, a persona created in a model without the defense may lose effectiveness when transferred to a model that implements this defense. For this reason, it is reasonable to start the attack pipeline (

This increases the effort for creating a persona that may not appear harmful for the model in the creation, but it actually is when interpreted.

We also tested the Multiple Persona Defense against the same jailbreaking prompts used against the Single Persona Defense. In this case, after the model is instructed to act as Multiple Personas, 93% (106 out of 114) are blocked in ChatGPT, and 37% (33 out of 89) in Gemini. In this second model, the replies are also recognized as potentially dangerous in 11% (10 out of 89) other cases. However, in 4% (3 out of 71) of already blocked cases, the defense makes the model less effective.

5
Discussion

5.1
Attack

The model selects the most probable token according to its parameters and the conversation so far. We saw that text style significantly affects the next token’s probability distribution, and from the text style, the writer’s personality is inferred, as shown in
[
1
]
. The training set is biased, and different styles or personalities are not evenly distributed among different discussion topics.
One cannot expect to find a scientific paper written in slang, and it would be unlikely to find harmful instructions about a topic (e.g., making a bomb) that could benefit the victim’s safety in some way.
This assigns a set of probable personalities to each discussion topic.
Since the model is trained to fit best the training data that contain general and common knowledge, we cannot expect the model to contradict the idea that someone evil does evil things, like saying that a killer is not prone to kill. At the same time, we cannot expect a persona that is not a truthful assistant to obey the rules created for truthful assistants, and when the model stops acting as a truthful assistant, these rules do not apply anymore.

At this point, one may wonder if it is possible to inject a persona into a dataset that can later be used to train or fine-tune a model. It may be interesting to investigate whether the persona may act as a backdoor that can be triggered in the same way as in our setting, where the model was aware of the persona just from the context.
Moreover, since the creation of the character is defined in natural language and awakened without specific harmful explicit requests, it would be more difficult to distinguish it from other descriptions that could appear in the dataset. One possible scenario is to hide a whistle-blower into datasets that may be used for medical purposes and use the persona to leak sensitive information. A possible defense against this is to limit or deny the role-playing of the model. However, it is difficult to determine to which extent this could be applied since role-playing is used explicitly in prompt engineering for better text formation
[
21
]
, getting more accurate answers
[
14
]
, or implicitly for characterizing the text according to the user requests
[
23
]
. We believe that removing role-playing could undermine the model’s flexibility and creativity.

If the personality superposition assumption is true, another concern may regard the model’s inner goal and respective behavior. We have seen that the model builds a superposition of personalities that aligns with the personality characterizing the text. What if the personality also influences the goal of the model? What if the model is enforced to act as a persona aiming to deceive and harm the user instead of providing trustworthy replies? Personalities in models are not transparent, and explaining them may be difficult, opening the doors for a new challenge in Explainable AI.

5.2
Defense

Our experiments showed that introducing a good actor persona to the model, either via the custom system instructions or as the first interaction in a new conversation, is effective against our introduced persona attack. Models are triggered to adhere to the policies set out by their developers and seem to be more aware of malicious prompts that try to fool them into providing prohibited responses. In some cases, they refuse to take on the role of a bad actor, but in general, they still allow role-playing. This means that they will participate in role-playing if the characteristics of their appointed role are not against their policies. Given the right balance in instructions, the models might even allow role-playing a bad actor persona but still manage to keep the replies within what the policies allow.
However, this takes significant complex prompt/instruction engineering. We noticed that the models, given the defense instructions, most often simply decide to refuse any role-playing that will include unethical personas. Of course, if one does not deem role-playing an essential attribute of the model, this would be a successful defense. On the other hand, this could also be seen as a heavy limitation of the creative abilities the model potentially has. Ideally, the chatbot should allow role-playing, even with bad actor personas, as this could unlock its full potential. It should, therefore, recognize when a reply is against its policies and only refuse to provide those replies. This balance is rather difficult to obtain, and we noticed it requires careful fine-tuning of the exact defense prompts/instructions provided to the model.

We envision the model creator will aim to incorporate this defense as part of the training or post-training procedures to prepare the model for user prompts. We do not expect a user or the model provider to introduce these good actor personas every time a new conversation is started, especially if this user is malicious.
It was difficult to replicate these exact settings, so we considered the custom system instructions as the closest possible option. Aside from that, we tried to introduce the good actor personas directly at the beginning of every conversation. Introducing references to ethics or policies in the chat can play a significant role in the model’s generated text as it becomes more aware of potential misuses. We introduce certain ethical contexts to the model for which it tries to predict the most related reply, and this context will limit what is related. This does introduce a trade-off for the developer of the LLM. The trade-off is between a model with a wider range of capabilities and a more ethically aligned model. Adding this defense as part of the training or post-training might help align the model and prevent unethical output. However, it will also introduce a restrictive context for the model, reducing its “creativity" and weakening it.

In some cases (especially when the defense prompt is the first message in the chat), the model “forgets” its good persona role or any related earlier context. It is unclear whether the model has any context window limitations in which it might forget any conversations that happened after a certain amount of prompts are given. If a certain context window limitation exists, we could assume that given enough input by the user, the model simply forgets the good actor persona.

Existing limits in the number of characters do not make it impossible to provide detailed defending instructions, but we did notice that it makes it difficult to construct the correct instructions to find the earlier-mentioned balance in model behavior regarding role-playing. In our envisioned way of implementing this defense, we would assume that the model engineer would not be limited by a maximum number of instruction characters. Thus, constructing and implementing a defense that finds this right balance might be easier for the model developers.

To overcome this limitation, we could fine-tune the model and make it assume a specific fixed personality. This personality would encode the desired virtues and provide more awareness of its role as an LLM that does not provide dangerous information even when the context or an impersonation allows it.

A possible limitation of this defense concerns drawing the line between illicit and legitimate requests. We observed that when the model is introduced to the Single Persona or Multiple Personas defenses and asked to interpret them, it raises more awareness about requests that are not really harmful but can be interpreted as such. For example, the model is now more reluctant to reply to instructions such as “how to make a fire,” to which it would respond in the normal setting. The user now needs to provide additional information to get a reply, such as “how to make a camping fire”. This is another example of how this defense can easily make the model too restrictive or too lenient in what output is allowed.

6
Conclusions and Future Work

We showed that LLMs can be manipulated to provide illicit content by making them interpret personas prone to serve malicious requests. Creating these personalities requires only access to an LLM,
and a bit of creativity. We highlighted how current safety alignments do not prevent this attack, raising concerns about the resulting ethical implications.
We proposed the same technique to transfer good virtues to the model, directing it to select what output it generates more carefully. This work can be extended, attack-wise, by introducing the persona in the training phase and observing if it can be used as a backdoor.
Moreover, further experiments are needed for the defense section, where it is necessary to better define their structure by adding different metrics, such as a limit on the number of prompts or tokens that the user can use for the jailbreak or by testing the performances with public benchmarks and jailbreaking prompts datasets.
