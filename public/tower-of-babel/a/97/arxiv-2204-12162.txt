Budgeted Out-tree Maximization with Submodular Prizes
Gianlorenzo D’Angelo, Esmaeil Delfaraz, Hugo Gilbert

Abstract
We consider a variant of the prize collecting Steiner tree problem in which we are given a directed graph D = ( V , A ) D=(V,A) , a monotone submodular prize function p : 2 V → ℝ + ∪ { 0 } p:2^{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\{0\} , a cost function c : V → ℤ + c:V\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{+} , a root vertex r ∈ V r\in V , and a budget B B . The aim is to find an out-subtree T T of D D rooted at r r that costs at most B B and maximizes the prize function. We call this problem Directed Rooted Submodular Tree ( DRSO ). For the case of undirected graphs and additive prize functions, Moss and Rabani [SIAM J. Comput. 2007] gave an algorithm that guarantees an O ⁡ ( log ⁡ | V | ) O(\log|V|) -approximation factor if a violation by a factor 2 of the budget constraint is allowed. Bateni et al. [SIAM J. Comput. 2018] improved the budget violation factor to 1 + ε 1+\varepsilon at the cost of an additional approximation factor of O ⁡ ( 1 / ε 2 ) O(1/\varepsilon^{2}) , for any ε ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] \varepsilon\in(0,1] . For directed graphs, Ghuge and Nagarajan [SODA 2020] gave an optimal quasi-polynomial-time O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ′ log ⁡ log ⁡ n ′ ) O\left(\frac{\log n^{\prime}}{\log\log n^{\prime}}\right) -approximation algorithm, where n ′ n^{\prime} is the number of vertices in an optimal solution, for the case in which the costs are associated to the edges. In this paper, we give a polynomial-time algorithm for DRSO that guarantees an approximation factor of O ⁡ ( B / ε 3 ) O(\sqrt{B}/\varepsilon^{3}) at the cost of a budget violation of a factor 1 + ε 1+\varepsilon , for any ε ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] \varepsilon\in(0,1] . The same result holds for the edge-cost case, to the best of our knowledge this is the first polynomial-time approximation algorithm for this case. We further show that the unrooted version of DRSO can be approximated to a factor of O ⁡ ( B ) O(\sqrt{B}) without budget violation, which is an improvement over the factor O ⁡ ( Δ ​ B ) O(\Delta\sqrt{B}) given in [Kuo et al. IEEE/ACM Trans. Netw. 2015] for the undirected and unrooted case, where Δ \Delta is the maximum degree of the graph. Finally, we provide some new/improved approximation bounds for several related problems, including the additive-prize version of DRSO , the maximum budgeted connected set cover problem, and the budgeted sensor cover problem.

Introduction

Prize collecting Steiner tree problems
(
PCSTP
) have been extensively studied due to their applications in designing computer and telecommunication networks, VLSI design, computational geometry, wireless mesh networks, and cancer genome studies
[
5
,
7
,
14
,
22
,
31
]
.
Very interesting polynomial-time constant/poly-logarithmic approximation algorithms have been proposed for many variants of
PCSTP
when the graph is undirected
[
1
,
2
,
9
,
11
,
17
,
20
,
28
]
. However, these problems are usually much harder on directed graphs. For instance, there is a simple polynomial-time
2
-approximation algorithm for the
undirected Steiner tree
problem, but no quasi-polynomial-time algorithm for the
directed Steiner tree
problem achieving an approximation ratio of
o\left(\frac{\log^{2}k}{\log\log k}\right)
exists, unless
NP\subseteq\bigcap_{0<\varepsilon<1}\text{ZPTIME}(2^{n^{\varepsilon}})
or the Projection Game Conjecture is false
[
12
]
, where
k
is the number of terminal nodes.

Some of the most relevant variants of
PCSTP
are represented by prize collecting problems with budget constraints. In such problems, we are usually given a graph with prizes and costs on the nodes and the goal is to find a tree that maximizes the sum of the prize of its nodes, while keeping the total cost bounded by a given budget. Guha et al.
[
13
]
introduced the case in which the graph is undirected and the goal is to find a tree that contains a distinguished vertex, called root, respects the budget constraint, and maximizes the prize, we call this problem
Undirected Rooted Additive Tree
(
URAT
). They gave an algorithm that achieves an
O(\log^{2}n)
-approximation factor, where
n
is the number of nodes in the graph, but the computed solution requires a factor-2 violation of the budget constraint. Moss and Rabani
[
26
]
and Bateni et al.
[
2
]
further investigated
URAT
and improved the results from the approximability point of view. The former paper improved the approximation factor to
O(\log n)
, with the same budget violation, and the latter one improved the budget violation factor to
1+\varepsilon
to obtain an approximation factor of
O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log n\right)
, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.
Kortsarz and Nutov
[
21
]
showed that the unrooted version of
URAT
, so does
URAT
, admits no
o(\log\log n)
-approximation algorithm, unless
NP\subseteq DTIME(n^{\text{polylog}(n)})
, even if the algorithm is allowed to violate the budget constraint by a factor equal to a universal constant.

In this paper, we consider a generalization of
URAT
on directed graphs. We are given a directed graph, where each node is associated with a cost, and the prize is defined by a monotone submodular function on the subsets of nodes, and the goal is to find an
out-tree
(a.k.a.
out-arborescence
) rooted at a specific vertex
r
with the maximum prize such that the total cost of all vertices in the out-tree is no more than a given budget. We term this problem
Directed Rooted Submodular Tree
(
DRSO
). A closely related problem, called
Submodular Tree Orienteering
(
STO
), has been recently introduced by Ghuge and Nagarajan
[
10
]
.
STO
is the same problem as
DRSO
except that edges and not nodes have costs. They provided a tight quasi-polynomial-time
O(\frac{\log n^{\prime}}{\log\log n^{\prime}})
-approximation algorithm that requires
(n\log B)^{O(\log^{1+\varepsilon}n^{\prime})}
time, where
n^{\prime}
is the number of vertices in an optimal solution and
B
is the budget constraint.

Notation and problem statement

For an integer
k
, let
[k]:=\{1,\ldots,k\}
. A directed
path
is a directed graph made of a sequence of distinct vertices
(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
and a sequence of directed edges
(v_{i},v_{i+1})
,
i\in[k-1]
.
An
out-tree
(a.k.a. out-arborescence) is a directed graph in which there is exactly one directed path from a specific vertex
r
, called
root
, to each other vertex. If a subgraph
T
of a directed graph
D
is an out-tree, then we say that
T
is an out-tree of
D
.

Let
D=(V,A)
be a directed graph with
n
nodes,
c:V\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{+}
be a cost function on nodes,
p:2^{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\{0\}
be a monotone submodular prize function on the subsets of nodes,
r\in V
be a root vertex, and
B
be an integer budget. For any subgraph
D^{\prime}
of
D
, we denote by
V(D^{\prime})
and
A(D^{\prime})
the set of nodes and edges in
D^{\prime}
, respectively. Given
S\subseteq V
, we denote the cost of
S
by
c(S)=\sum_{v\in S}c(v)
and we use shortcuts
c(D^{\prime})=c(V(D^{\prime}))
and
p(D^{\prime})=p(V(D^{\prime}))
for a subgraph
D^{\prime}
of
D
.
In the Directed Rooted Submodular Tree problem (
DRSO
), the goal is to find an out-tree
T
of
D
rooted at
r
such that
c(T)\leq B
and
p(T)
is maximum. Throughout the paper, we denote an optimal solution to
DRSO
by
T^{*}
.

Given two nodes
u
and
v
in
V
, a path in
D
from
u
to
v
with the minimum cost is called a
shortest path
and its cost, denoted by
dist(u,v)
, is called the
distance
from
u
to
v
in
D
.

An algorithm is a bicriteria
(\beta,\alpha)
-approximation algorithm for
DRSO
if, for any instance
I
of the problem, it returns a solution
Sol_{I}
such that
p(Sol_{I})\geq\frac{OPT_{I}}{\alpha}
and
c(Sol_{I})\leq\beta B
, where
OPT_{I}
is the optimum for
I
.

3
Results and Techniques

Our main result is given in the next theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

DRSO
admits a polynomial-time bicriteria
\left(1+\varepsilon,O\left(\frac{\sqrt{B}}{\varepsilon^{3}}\right)\right)
-approximation algorithm, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

Our approach combines and extends techniques given by Kuo et al.
[
22
]
and Bateni et al.
[
2
]
.
To illustrate our techniques, we now consider the case in which costs are unitary, i.e.
c(v)=1
, for each
v\in V
, and the prize function is additive, i.e.
p(S)=\sum_{v\in S}p(\{v\})
, for any
S\subseteq V
. In this case, the distance from a node
u
to a node
v
is equal to the minimum number of nodes in a path from
u
to
v
and the cost of a tree
T
is equal to its size,
c(T)=|V(T)|
. W.lo.g. we also assume that the distance from
r
to any node is at most
B
. We will give the proof for the general case in Section
4
.

The algorithm works as follows. For any vertex
u
, we denote as
V_{u}
the set of all nodes that are at a distance no more than
\lfloor\sqrt{B}\rfloor
from
u
,
V_{u}:=\{v~|~dist(u,v)\leq\lfloor\sqrt{B}\rfloor\}
. We first select a subset
S_{u}
of
V_{u}
of at most
\lfloor\sqrt{B}\rfloor
nodes with the maximum prize,
S_{u}:=\arg\max\{p(S)~:~S\subseteq V_{u},|S|\leq\lfloor\sqrt{B}\rfloor\}
.
1
1

1

This step can be done in polynomial time since function
p
is additive.
If
p
is monotone and submodular, this step consists in solving the submodular maximization problem. See Section
4
for more details.

We then compute a minimal inclusion-wise out-tree
T_{u}
rooted at
u
that spans all nodes in
S_{u}
. Note that
|V(T_{u})|\leq B
since the distance from
u
to any node in
S_{u}
is at most
\lfloor\sqrt{B}\rfloor
. Let
z
be a node such that
p(T_{z})
is maximum. If
z=r
, then we take
T_{z}
as our solution, otherwise we compute a solution by adding to
T_{z}
a shortest path
P
from
r
to
z
and removing the edges in
A(T_{z})\setminus A(P)
incoming the nodes in
V(T_{z})\cap V(P)
. Let
T
be our solution and
T^{*}
be an optimal solution.

We will prove (Lemma
4.3
) that any out-tree
\hat{T}
can be covered by at most
N=O(|\hat{T}|/m)
out-subtrees
\{\hat{T}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
with at most
m
nodes each, where
m
is any positive integer less than
|\hat{T}|
. By applying this claim to an optimal solution
T^{*}
and by setting
m=\lfloor\sqrt{B}\rfloor
, we obtain

p(T^{*})=p\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}V(T^{*}_{i})\right)\leq Np(T^{\prime}),

where
p(T^{\prime})=\max\{p(T^{*}_{i})~|~i\in[N]\}
,
|T^{\prime}|\leq\lfloor\sqrt{B}\rfloor
, and
N=O(|T^{*}|/m)=O(\sqrt{B})
. Let
w
be the root of
T^{\prime}
.
Recall that
S_{w}
is a set of at most
\lfloor\sqrt{B}\rfloor
nodes that are at a distance no more than
\lfloor\sqrt{B}\rfloor
from
w
and have the maximum prize and
T_{w}
contains all the nodes in
S_{w}
. Since
|T^{\prime}|\leq\lfloor\sqrt{B}\rfloor
, we have

p(T^{\prime})\leq p(S_{w})\leq p(T_{w})\leq p(T_{z})\leq p(T).

Since
N=O(\sqrt{B})
, we conclude that
p(T^{*})=O(\sqrt{B})p(T)
.

Note that the cost of
T
is upper-bounded by
2B
, as both the cost of
T_{z}
and that of a shortest path from
r
to
z
are at most
B
. We can use the trimming procedure introduced by Bateni et al.
[
2
]
to obtain an out-subtree of
T
with cost at most
(1+\varepsilon)B
by loosing an approximation factor of
O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
(see Lemma
B.1
). This shows Theorem
3.1
for the unit-cost, additive-prize case. In the case in which the prize is a general monotone submodular function, the trimming procedure by Bateni et al. cannot be applied. We show how to generalize this procedure to the case of any monotone submodular prize function by loosing an extra approximation factor of
O(1/\varepsilon)
.

We can use the same approach to obtain a polynomial-time bicriteria
\left(1+\varepsilon,O\left(\frac{\sqrt{B}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\right)
-approximation algorithm for the case of additive prize function and edge-cost.
More importantly, we can obtain a polynomial-time bicriteria
\left(1+\varepsilon,O\left(\frac{\sqrt{B}}{\varepsilon^{3}}\right)\right)
-approximation algorithm for
STO
, i.e. for the edge-cost case where the prize function is monotone submodular. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first polynomial-time approximation algorithm for
STO
.

Finally, for the unrooted version the same approach with some minor changes achieves an
O(\sqrt{B})
-approximation with no budget violation.

4
Approximation Algorithm for
DRSO

We now introduce our polynomial-time approximation algorithm for
DRSO
. We start by defining a procedure that takes as input an out-tree of a directed graph
D
and returns another out-tree of
D
which has a smaller cost but preserves the same prize-to-cost ratio (up to a bounded multiplicative factor).

Bateni et al.
[
2
]
introduced a similar procedure for the case of undirected graphs and additive prize function.
In their case, we are given an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, a distinguished vertex
r\in V
and a budget
B
, where each vertex
v\in V
is assigned with a prize
p^{\prime}(v)
and a cost
c^{\prime}(v)
. For a tree
T
, the prize and cost of
T
are the sum of the prizes and costs of the nodes of
T
and are denoted by
p^{\prime}(T)
and
c^{\prime}(T)
, respectively. A graph
G
is called
B
-proper
for the vertex
r
if the cost of reaching any vertex from
r
is at most
B
. Bateni et al. proposed a trimming process that leads to the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1

(Lemma 3 in
[
2
]
)
.

Let
T
be a tree rooted at
r
with the prize-to-cost ratio
\gamma=\frac{p^{\prime}(T)}{c^{\prime}(T)}
. Suppose the underlying graph is
B
-proper for
r
and for
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
the cost of the tree is at least
\frac{\varepsilon B}{2}
. One can find a tree
T^{\prime}
containing
r
with the prize-to-cost ratio at least
\frac{\varepsilon\gamma}{4}
such that
\varepsilon B/2\leq c^{\prime}(T^{\prime})\leq(1+\varepsilon)B
.

We now generalize this trimming process to the case in which the underlying graph is directed and the prize function is monotone and submodular by borrowing ideas from
[
2
]
.

We introduce some additional definitions. Let
T
be an out-tree rooted at
r
. A
full
out-subtree of
T
rooted at some node
v
is an out-subtree of
T
containing all the vertices that are reachable from
r
through
v
in
T
. The set of
strict
out-subtrees of
T
is the set of all full out-subtrees of
T
other than
T
itself. The set of
immediate
out-subtrees of
T
is the set of all full out-subtrees rooted at the children of
r
in
T
. A directed graph
D=(V,A)
is
B
-
appropriate
for a node
r
if
dist(r,v)\leq B
for any node
v\in V
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
D=(V,A)
be a
B
-appropriate graph for a node
r
. Let
T
be an out-tree of
D
rooted at
r
with the prize-to-cost ratio
\gamma=\frac{p(T)}{c(T)}
, where
p
is a monotone submodular function. Suppose that
\frac{\varepsilon B}{2}\leq c(T)\leq hB
, where
h\in(1,n]
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. One can find an out-subtree
\hat{T}
rooted at
r
with the prize-to-cost ratio at least
\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\gamma}{32h}
such that
\varepsilon B/2\leq c(\hat{T})\leq(1+\varepsilon)B
.

Proof.

We run the following initial trimming procedure. We iteratively remove a strict out-subtree
T^{\prime}
from
T
that satisfies two conditions: (i) the prize-to-cost ratio of
T\setminus T^{\prime}
is at least
\gamma
, and (ii)
c(T\setminus T^{\prime})\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}B
. We repeat this process until no such strict out-subtree exists. Let
T_{-}
be the remaining out-tree after applying this process on
T
.

Now if
c(T_{-})\leq(1+\varepsilon)B
, the desired out-subtree is obtained. Suppose it is not the case. A full out-subtree
T^{\prime}
is called
rich
if
c(T^{\prime})\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}B
and the prize-to-cost ratio of
T^{\prime}
and all its strict out-subtrees are at least
\gamma
. We claim that if there exists a rich out-subtree, then we can find the desired out-subtree
\hat{T}
.

Claim 4.1
.

Given a rich out-subtree
T^{\prime}
, the desired out-subtree
\hat{T}
can be found.

Proof.

We first find a rich out-subtree
T^{\prime\prime}
of
T^{\prime}
such that the strict out-subtrees of
T^{\prime\prime}
are not rich, i.e.,
c(T^{\prime\prime})\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}B
while the cost of any strict out-subtree of
T^{\prime\prime}
(if any exist) is less than
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}B
. Let
C
be the total cost of the immediate out-subtrees of
T^{\prime\prime}
. We distinguish between two cases:

1.

If
C<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}B
, then let
\hat{T}
be the union of
T^{\prime\prime}
and a shortest path
P
from
r
to the root
r^{\prime\prime}
of
T^{\prime\prime}
.
\hat{T}
has cost at most
C+B\leq(1+\varepsilon)B
and prize at least
\gamma(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}B)
. This implies that
\hat{T}
has ratio at least
\frac{\gamma\varepsilon}{2(1+\varepsilon)}\geq\frac{\gamma\varepsilon}{4}\geq\frac{\gamma\varepsilon^{2}}{32h}
.

2.

If
C\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}B
, we proceed as follows. Since each immediate out-subtree of
T^{\prime\prime}
has a cost strictly smaller than
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}B
, we can partition all the immediate out-subtrees of
T^{\prime\prime}
into
M
groups
S_{1},\dots,S_{M}
in such a way that for each
i\in[M-1]
the total cost of immediate out-subtrees in
S_{i}
is at least
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}B
, and for each
i\in[M]
it is at most
\varepsilon B
.
We can always group in this way since the cost of each immediate out-subtree of
T^{\prime\prime}
is less than
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}B
while
C\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}B
. Since the total cost of all the immediate out-subtrees of
T^{\prime\prime}
is upper bounded by
hB
, then the number of selected groups
M
is at most

M\leq\left\lceil\frac{hB}{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}B}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac{2h}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil\leq\left\lfloor\frac{2h}{\varepsilon}\right\rfloor+1\leq\left\lfloor\frac{4h}{\varepsilon}\right\rfloor\leq\frac{4h}{\varepsilon}.

We now add the root
r^{\prime\prime}
of
T^{\prime\prime}
to each group
S_{i}
and denote the new group by
S^{\prime}_{i}
, i.e.,
S^{\prime}_{i}=S_{i}\cup\{r^{\prime\prime}\}
, for any
i\in[M]
. By the monotonicity and submodularity of
p
, we have
\sum_{i=1}^{M}p(S^{\prime}_{i})\geq p(S^{\prime}_{1})+\sum_{i=2}^{M}p(S_{i})\geq p(S^{\prime}_{1}\cup\bigcup_{i=2}^{M}S_{i})=p(T^{\prime\prime})
. Now among
S^{\prime}_{1},\dots,S^{\prime}_{M}
, we select the group

Lemma 5.1

Let

Given a rich out-subtree

.

.

-approximation algorithm for the unrooted case improves over the factors

Q\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, and a vertex
r
, and the goal is to find a tree
T
such that
p(T)\geq Q
,
r\in V(T)
and
c(T)
is minimum. We prove that this problem admits a
2\Delta
-approximation algorithm (Theorem
B.3
).

Maximum Weighted Budgeted Connected Set Cover (MWBCSC)
. Let
X
be a set of elements,
\mathcal{S}\subseteq 2^{X}
be a collection of sets,
p:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a prize function,
c:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}^{+}
be a cost function,
G_{\mathcal{S}}
be a graph on vertex set
\mathcal{S}
, and
B
be a budget. In
MWBCSC
, the goal is to find a subcollection
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
such that
c(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}c(S)\leq B
, the subgraph induced by
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is connected and
p(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})=\sum_{x\in X_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}}p(x)
is maximum, where
X_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}=\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}S
.
We show that
MWBCSC
admits a polynomial-time
\alpha f
-approximation algorithm, where
f
is the maximum frequency of an element and
\alpha
is the performance ratio of an algorithm for the unrooted version of
MCSB
with additive prize function (Theorem
B.4
).
Moreover, one can have a polynomial-time
O(\log{n})
-approximation algorithm for
MWBCSC
under the assumption that if two sets have an element in common, then they are adjacent in
G_{\mathcal{S}}
(Corollary
B.2
). This last result is an improvement over the factor
2(\Delta+1)\alpha/(1-e^{-1})
by Ran et al.
[
29
]
.

Budgeted Sensor Cover Problem (BSCP)
. In
BSCP
, we are given a set
\mathcal{S}
of sensors, a set
\mathcal{P}
of target points in a metric space, a sensing range
R_{s}
, a communication range
R_{c}
, and a budget
B
. A target point is covered by a sensor if it is within distance
R_{s}
from it. Two sensors are connected if they are at a distance at most
R_{c}
.
The goal is to find a subset
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
such that
|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}|\leq B
, the number of covered target points by
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is maximized and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
induces a connected subgraph.
We give a
2f
-approximation algorithm for
BSCP
(Theorem
B.5
), where
f
is the maximum number of sensors that cover a target point. We also show that, under the assumption that
R_{s}\leq R_{c}/2
,
BSCP
admits a polynomial-time
8/(1-e^{-1})
-approximation algorithm (Theorem
B.8
), which improves the factors
8(\lceil 2\sqrt{2}C\rceil+1)^{2}/(1-1/e)

[
16
]
and
8(\lceil 4C/\sqrt{3}\rceil+1)^{2}/(1-1/e)

[
33
]
, where
C=R_{s}/R_{c}
.
Note that Huang et al.
[
16
]
do not assume that
R_{s}/R_{c}\leq R_{c}/2
, however, our technique improves over their result if
R_{s}\leq R_{c}/2
.

Appendix B
Some Variants of
DRSO

Additive prize function

We consider a the special case of
DRSO
where the prize function
p
is an additive function, i.e., for any
S\subseteq V
,
p(S)=\sum_{v\in S}p(\{v\})
, which we call
Directed Rooted Additive Tree
(
DRAT
).

We apply a variant of Algorithm
1
to
DRAT
where, in Line 12 of the algorithm, we use a different trimming process to achieve a better approximation factor.
In particular, we show that we can use the trimming process by Bateni et al.
[
2
]
(Lemma
4.1
) in directed graphs and achieve the same guarantee.

Let us first briefly explain the trimming process by Bateni et al.
[
2
]
. We are given an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, a distinguished vertex
r\in V
and a budget
B
, where each vertex
v\in V
is assigned with a prize
p^{\prime}(v)
and a cost
c^{\prime}(v)
. Let
T
be a tree, where
V(T)\subseteq V
and
E(T)\subseteq E
, let
c^{\prime}(T)=\sum_{v\in V(T)}c^{\prime}(v)
, and let
p^{\prime}(T)=\sum_{v\in V(T)}p^{\prime}(v)
. The trimming procedure by Bateni et al. takes as input a subtree
T
rooted at a node
r
of a
B
-proper graph
G
and first (i) computes a subtree
T^{\prime\prime}
of
T
, not necessarily rooted
r
, such that
\frac{\varepsilon B}{2}\leq c^{\prime}(T^{\prime\prime})\leq\varepsilon B
and
p^{\prime}(T^{\prime\prime})\geq\frac{\varepsilon B}{2}\gamma
, where
\gamma=\frac{p^{\prime}(T)}{c^{\prime}(T)}
. Then (ii) it connects node
r
to the root of
T^{\prime\prime}
with a minimum-cost path and obtains a tree
T^{\prime}
rooted at
r
. Since
G
is
B
-proper, this path exists and has length at most
B
, which implies that the cost of the resulting tree is between
\frac{\varepsilon B}{2}
and
(1+\varepsilon)B
, and its prize to cost ratio is at least
\frac{p^{\prime}(T^{\prime\prime})}{(1+\varepsilon)B}\geq\frac{\varepsilon\gamma}{4}
. This leads to Lemma
4.1
. For the case of directed graphs, we prove a lemma equivalent to Lemma
4.1
.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
D=(V,A)
be a
B
-appropriate graph for a node
r
. Let
T
be an out-tree of
D
rooted at
r
with the prize-to-cost ratio
\gamma=\frac{p(T)}{c(T)}
. Suppose that for
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
,
c(T)\geq\frac{\varepsilon B}{2}
. One can find an out-tree
\hat{T}
rooted at
r
with the prize-to-cost ratio at least
\frac{\varepsilon\gamma}{4}
such that
\varepsilon B/2\leq c^{\prime}(T^{\prime})\leq(1+\varepsilon)B
.

Proof.

We first define an undirected tree by removing the directions from
T
and apply to it the step (i) of the trimming procedure by Bateni et al. with
p^{\prime}(\cdot)=p(\cdot)
and
c^{\prime}(\cdot)=c(\cdot)
as prize and cost functions, respectively. We obtain a subtree
T^{\prime\prime}
such that
\frac{\varepsilon B}{2}\leq c^{\prime}(T^{\prime\prime})\leq\varepsilon B
and
p^{\prime}(T^{\prime\prime})\geq\frac{\varepsilon B}{2}\gamma
. We reintroduce in
T^{\prime\prime}
the same directions as in
T
to obtain an out-tree
T^{\prime\prime}_{out}
corresponding to
T^{\prime\prime}
. Then, we add a minimum cost path from
r
to the root of
T^{\prime\prime}_{out}
. Since
D
is
B
-appropriate, this path exists and has length at most
B
. The obtained out-tree
\hat{T}
has the desired properties because
p(T^{\prime\prime}_{out})=p^{\prime}(T^{\prime\prime})
and
c(T^{\prime\prime}_{out})=c^{\prime}(T^{\prime\prime})
and therefore
\frac{\varepsilon B}{2}\leq c(\hat{T})\leq(1+\varepsilon)B
and
\frac{p(\hat{T})}{c(\hat{T})}\geq\frac{p^{\prime}(T^{\prime\prime})}{(1+\varepsilon)B}\geq\frac{\varepsilon\gamma}{4}
.
∎

This results in the following theorem.

Theorem B.1
.

There exists a polynomial-time bicriteria
(1+\varepsilon,O(\frac{\sqrt{B}}{\varepsilon^{2}}))
-approximation algorithm for
DRAT
.

Sketch of proof.

The theorem follows by the same arguments used in the proof of Theorem
3.1
and by Lemma
B.1
.
∎

Undirected graphs

Here we consider the particular case of
DRSO
in which the graph is undirected. Since it is a special case of
DRSO
, all our results hold.

Kuo et al.
[
22
]
studied the unrooted version of
DRSO
in undirected graphs, which they call
Maximum Connected Submodular function with Budget constraint
(
MCSB
).
In
MCSB
, we are given an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, a monotone submodular function
p:2^{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
, a cost function
c:V\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and a budget
B
, and the goal is to find a tree
T
s.t.
c(T)=\sum_{v\in V(T)}c(v)\leq B
and
p(T)
is maximum.

Kuo et al. provided an
O((\Delta+1)\sqrt{B})
-approximation algorithm for
MCSB
, where
\Delta
is the maximum degree. Recently, Hochbaum and Rao
[
14
]
investigated
MCSB
in the case in which each vertex costs
1
for the detection of mutated driver pathways in cancer and provided an approximation algorithm with factor
\min\{\frac{1}{(1-1/e)(1/R-1/B)},B\}
, where
R
is the radius of the input graph
G
.

Our algorithm for
DUST
can be applied to undirected graphs and improves the above results
[
22
,
14
]
by providing an
O(\sqrt{B})
-approximation.

Additive prize function in undirected graphs

We consider the problem where we are given an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, a cost function
c:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
, a prize function
p:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
, a budget
B
and a vertex
r
, and the goal is to find a tree
T
such that
c(T)=\sum_{v\in V(T)}c(v)\leq B
,
r\in V(T)
and
p(T)=\sum_{v\in V(T)}p(v)
is maximum.
We call this problem
Undirected Rooted Additive Tree
(
URAT
) and we show that it admits a bicriteria polynomial-time approximation algorithm, which depends on the maximum degree in the graph.

Theorem B.2
.

URAT
admits a polynomial-time bicriteria
(1+\varepsilon,\frac{16\Delta}{\varepsilon^{2}})
-approximation algorithm, where
\Delta
is the maximum degree in the given graph.

To prove Theorem
B.2
, we need the following observations. Let
E-URAT
be the same problem as
URAT
except that the edges and not the nodes have costs. In particular, in
E-URAT
, we are given an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, a cost function
c:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
, a prize function
p:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
, a budget
B
and a vertex
r
, and the goal is to find a tree
T=(V(T),E(T))
such that
c(T)=\sum_{e\in E(T)}c(e)\leq B
,
r\in V(T)
and
p(T)=\sum_{v\in V(T)}p(v)
is maximum.
Paul et al.
[
28
]
gave a
2
-approximation algorithm for
E-URAT
.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for
E-URAT
. Using
\mathcal{A}
, a bicriteria
(\Delta,\alpha)
-approximation can be obtained for
URAT
, where
\Delta
is the maximum degree of a node.

Proof.

Consider an instance
I=\langle G=(V,E),p,c,r,B\rangle
of
URAT
. We create an instance
I^{\prime}=\langle G,p,c^{\prime},r,B^{\prime}\rangle
of
E-URAT
where
B^{\prime}=\Delta B
and, for any edge
e=\{u,v\}\in E^{\prime}
, we set
c^{\prime}(e)=c(u)+c(v)
. Let
OPT_{I}
and
OPT_{I^{\prime}}
be optimal solutions to
I
and
I^{\prime}
, respectively.

First, observe that
p(OPT_{I^{\prime}})\geq p(OPT_{I})
as
OPT_{I}
is a feasible solution for
I^{\prime}
. By running
\mathcal{A}
on instance
I^{\prime}
, we have a tree
T^{\prime}
with the cost
c(T^{\prime})=\sum_{e\in E(T^{\prime})}c(e)\leq B^{\prime}=\Delta B
and the prize
p^{\prime}(T^{\prime})\geq\alpha p(OPT_{I^{\prime}})\geq\alpha p(OPT_{I})
. Therefore
T^{\prime}
is a
(\Delta,\alpha)
-approximation for
I
.
∎

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
B.2
.

Proof of Theorem
B.2
.

Consider an instance
I=\langle G=(V,E),p,c,r,B\rangle
of
URAT
. From graph
G
, we first create a maximal inclusion-wise
B
-appropriate graph
G^{\prime}
for
r
. Let
OPT
be an optimal solution to
I
. By Lemma
B.2
and the
2
-approximation algorithm for
E-URAT
proposed by Paul et al.
[
28
]
, we create a subtree
T^{\prime}
of
G^{\prime}
with prize
p(T)\geq\frac{OPT}{2}
and cost
c(T)\leq\Delta B
. Now by using Lemma
4.1
, we obtain a subtree
\hat{T}
from
T
with the cost at most
c(\hat{T})\leq(1+\varepsilon)B
and the prize-to-cost ratio:

\frac{p(\hat{T})}{c(\hat{T})}\geq\frac{\varepsilon p(T)}{4c(T)}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{8\Delta B}OPT_{B}.

As
c(\hat{T})\geq\varepsilon B/2
, we have
p(\hat{T})\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{16\Delta}OPT_{B}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Quota Problem

We consider the quota version of
URAT
, called
Q-URAT
, where we are given an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, a cost function
c:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
, a prize function
p:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
, a quota
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, and a vertex
r
, and the goal is to find a tree
T
such that
p(T)=\sum_{v\in V(T)}p(v)\geq Q
,
r\in V(T)
and
c(T)=\sum_{v\in V(T)}c(v)
is minimum.

We provide a new polynomial-time approximation algorithm for
Q-URAT
that depends on the maximum degree of the given graph.

Theorem B.3
.

Q-URAT
admits a
2\Delta
-approximation algorithm, where
\Delta
is the maximum degree of a node in the given graph.

To prove Theorem
B.3
, we first prove the following lemma. Let
EQ-URAT
be the same problem as
Q-URAT
except that the edges and not the nodes have costs. In particular, in
EQ-URAT
, we are given an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, a cost function
c:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
, a prize function
p:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
, a quota
Q
and a vertex
r
, and the goal is to find a tree
T
such that
p(T)=\sum_{v\in V(T)}p(v)\geq Q
,
r\in V(T)
and
c(T)=\sum_{e\in E(T)}c(v)
is minimum.

Lemma B.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for
EQ-URAT
. Using
\mathcal{A}
, an
\alpha\Delta
-approximation can be obtained for
Q-URAT
.

Proof.

From an instance
I=\langle G=(V,E),p,c,r,Q\rangle
of
Q-URAT
, we create an instance
I^{\prime}=\langle G,p,c^{\prime},r,Q\rangle
of
EQ-URAT
, where, for any edge
e=\{u,v\}\in E^{\prime}
, we set
c^{\prime}(e)=c(u)+c(v)
.

By running
\mathcal{A}
on instance
I^{\prime}
, we have a tree
T
with prize
p(T)\geq Q
and cost
c^{\prime}(T)\leq\alpha c^{\prime}(OPT_{I^{\prime}})
, where
OPT_{I^{\prime}}
is an optimal solution to
I^{\prime}
.
Moreover, we have that
c(T)\leq c^{\prime}(T)
and
c^{\prime}(OPT_{I})\leq\Delta c(OPT_{I})
, where
OPT_{I}
is an optimal solution to
I
.
We obtain
c(T)\leq\alpha\Delta c(OPT_{I})
. Therefore
T
is an
\alpha\Delta
-approximation for
I
.
∎

Now we prove Theorem
B.3
.

Proof of Theorem
B.3
.

Johnson et al.
[
17
]
observed that an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for the
k
-MST problem guarantees an
\alpha
-approximation also for
EQ-URAT
. This observation, along with the
2
-approximation algorithm by Garg
[
9
]
for the
k
-MST problem result in a
2
-approximation algorithm for
EQ-URAT
. Therefore, by Lemma
B.3
, the theorem follows.
∎

Maximum Weighted Budgeted Connected Set Cover (
MWBCSC
).

Let
X
be a set of elements,
\mathcal{S}\subseteq 2^{X}
be a collection sets,
p:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a prize function,
c:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a cost function,
G_{\mathcal{S}}
be a graph on vertex set
\mathcal{S}
, and
B
be a budget. In
MWBCSC
, the goal is to find a subcollection
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
such that
c(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}c(S)\leq B
, the subgraph induced by
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is connected and
p(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})=\sum_{x\in X_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}}p(x)
is maximized, where
X_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}=\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}S
. Note that, as
MWBCSC
coincides with the unrooted version of
URAT
when no two sets share an element,
MWBCSC
admits no
o(\log\log n)
-approximation algorithm unless
NP\subseteq DTIME(n^{\text{polylog}(n)})
even if the budget constraint is violated by a factor of any universal constant
\rho

[
21
]
. Moreover, since the prize function of
MWBCSC
is submodular w.r.t. subsets of nodes, our algorithm for the undirected unrooted version of
DRSO
guarantees an
O(\sqrt{B})
-approximation for
MWBCSC
.

Consider the following assumption.

Assumption B.1
.

Two sets
S
and
S^{\prime}
having an element in common are adjacent in
G_{\mathcal{S}}
.

Denote by
UUAT
the unrooted version of
URAT
. Ran et al.
[
29
]
provided a
\frac{2(\Delta+1)\alpha}{1-e^{-1}}
-approximation algorithm for
MWBCSC
under Assumption
B.1
, where
\alpha
is the performance ratio for the algorithm used to approximate
UUAT
. Note that the best known algorithm for
UUAT
is an
O(\log{n})
-approximation
[
2
]
. Let
f
be the maximum frequency of an element in an instance of
MWBCSC
. Clearly, in the work by Ran et al.
[
29
]
,
\Delta\geq f
as the authors investigated
MWBCSC
under Assumption
B.1
. In the following, we aim to provide some approximation results for some variants of
MWBCSC
.

We first show that there exists a polynomial-time
\alpha f
-approximation algorithm for
MWBCSC
(without Assumption
B.1
), where
\alpha
is the performance ratio of an algorithm for
UUAT
.

Theorem B.4
.

MWBCSC
admits a polynomial-time
\alpha f
-approximation algorithm, where
f
is the maximum frequency of an element and
\alpha
is the performance ratio of an algorithm for
UUAT
.

Proof.

Let
I=(X,\mathcal{S},c,p,G_{\mathcal{S}}=(\mathcal{S},E_{S}),B)
be an instance
MWBCSC
. We create an instance
J=(G_{\mathcal{S}},c,p^{\prime},B)
of
UUAT
, where
p^{\prime}(S)=\sum_{x\in S}p(x)
for each
S\in\mathcal{S}
, that results in loosing an approximation factor
f
.

Let
T
be the solution returned by an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for
UUAT
applied to
J
,
X_{T}=\cup_{S\in V(T)}S
,
OPT_{I}
, and
OPT_{j}
be optimal solutions for
I
and
J
, respectively.

We have
p^{\prime}(T)\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}p^{\prime}(OPT_{J})
,
p(OPT_{I})\leq p^{\prime}(OPT_{I})\leq p^{\prime}(OPT_{J})
, and

\displaystyle p^{\prime}(T)=\sum_{S\in V(T)}\sum_{x\in S}p(x)=\sum_{x\in X_{T}}|\{S\in V(T):x\in S\}|p(x)\leq f\sum_{x\in X_{T}}p(x)=fp(V(T)).

Therefore,

\displaystyle p(V(T))\geq\frac{1}{f}p^{\prime}(T)\geq\frac{1}{\alpha f}p^{\prime}(OPT_{J})\geq\frac{1}{\alpha f}p^{\prime}(OPT_{I}).

This implies that
V(T)
is an
\alpha f
-approximation for
I
.
∎

Since the algorithm by Bateni et al. for
UUAT
guarantees an
O(\log n)
-approximation, where
n
is the number of nodes in the graph, then
MWBCSC
admits a polynomial-time
O(f\log{|\mathcal{S}|})
-approximation algorithm.

In the next step, we show that
MWBCSC
admits a
2f
-approximation algorithm when each set has cost
1
. Note that this variant of
MWBCSC
has some applications, e.g., the detection of mutated pathways in cancers
[
14
,
31
]
and wireless sensor networks
[
7
,
16
,
22
,
29
,
32
,
33
]
.

Theorem B.5
.

When each set costs
1
,
MWBCSC
admits a polynomial-time
2f
-approximation algorithm, where
f
is the maximum frequency of an element.

Proof.

We observe that
URAT
when each vertex costs
1
is equivalent to
E-URAT
when each edge costs
1
from an approximation point of view. Indeed, each tree with at most
B-1
edges includes at most
B
nodes and vice-versa. The same holds for the unrooted versions of
URAT
and
E-URAT
, which we call
UUAT
and
E-UUAT
, respectively. Moreover,
E-UUAT
admits a
2
-approximation algorithm that consists in guessing the root and applying the 2-approximation algorithm for
E-URAT
by Paul et al.
[
28
]
. Therefore, we have a
2
-approximation algorithm for
UUAT
when each vertex costs 1 and the theorem follows by Theorem
B.4
.
∎

In the next step, we will provide an approximation algorithm for
MWBCSC
which does not depend on
f
. Recall that in a node-weighted graph
G
, for any two vertices
u
and
v
,
dist(u,v)
is the minimum cost of a path
P
from
u
to
v
, where the cost of
P
is the sum of the costs of its nodes. Now we show the following.

Theorem B.6
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for
UUAT
.
MWBCSC
admits a polynomial-time bicriteria
(h+1,\alpha)
-approximation algorithm, where
h=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}\\
S\cap S^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}(dist(S,S^{\prime})-(c(S)+c(S^{\prime})))
such that
c(S)\geq 1,\forall{S\in\mathcal{S}}
.

To prove Theorem
B.6
, we need some observations and materials.
Let
I=(X,\mathcal{S},c,p,G_{\mathcal{S}},B)
be an instance
MWBCSC
. Now we show that one can transform an instance
I
of
MWBCSC
to an instance of
J=(G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime}),c^{\prime},p^{\prime},B)
of
UUAT
. From
G_{\mathcal{S}}=(V(G_{\mathcal{S}}),E(G_{\mathcal{S}}))
, we create graph a
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
as follows:
V^{\prime}=V_{\mathcal{S}}\cup V_{X}
, where
V_{\mathcal{S}}=\{v_{S}:S\in V(G_{\mathcal{S}})\}
and
V_{X}=\{v_{x}:x\in X\}
, and
E^{\prime}=E(G_{\mathcal{S}})\cup\{\{v_{S},v_{x}\}:x\in S\}
. We set for any
v_{S}\in V_{\mathcal{S}}
,
p^{\prime}(v_{S})=0
and
c^{\prime}(v_{S})=c(S)
. For any
v_{x}\in V_{X}
,
p^{\prime}(v_{x})=p(x)
and
c^{\prime}(v_{x})=0
.

Lemma B.4
.

Let
SOL_{J}
be a solution to
UUAT
on the instance
J
. From
SOL_{J}
, a solution
SOL_{I}
to
MWBCSC
on instance
I
can be obtained such that
p(SOL_{I})\geq p^{\prime}(SOL_{J})
and
c(SOL_{I})\leq(h+1)B
.

Proof.

Let
Sol^{\prime}=\emptyset
. We repeat the following process: if there exist two edges
\{v_{S},v_{x}\},\{v_{S^{\prime}},v_{x}\}\in E^{\prime}(SOL_{J})
, then
Sol^{\prime}=Sol^{\prime}\cup\{v_{S},v_{S^{\prime}}\}
, where
\{v_{S},v_{S^{\prime}}\}
is an edge. Let
G(Sol^{\prime})
be the graph with edges in
Sol^{\prime}
. Let
\bar{T}=(V(\bar{T}),E(\bar{T}))
be a spanning tree of
G(Sol^{\prime})
. Note that we have
|E(\bar{T})|\leq B-1
as
c(S)\geq 1,\forall{S\in\mathcal{S}}
.
Note that if there exist two edges
\{v_{S},v_{x}\},\{v_{S^{\prime}},v_{x}\}\in E^{\prime}(SOL_{J})
, where
v_{S}\neq v_{S^{\prime}}
, there is a path
P
of distance at most
h+c(v_{S})+c(v_{S^{\prime}})
between
v_{S},v_{S^{\prime}}\in G_{\mathcal{S}}
. Let
SP_{e}
be a shortest path on
G_{\mathcal{S}}
connecting
e=\{v_{S},v_{S^{\prime}}\}
. Let
Sol^{\prime\prime}=\{SP_{e}:e\in E(\bar{T})\}
and
G(Sol^{\prime\prime})
is a graph with edges in
Sol^{\prime\prime}
.
G(Sol^{\prime\prime})
is a connected subgraph of
G_{\mathcal{S}}
. Let
SOL_{I}
be a spanning tree of
G(Sol^{\prime\prime})
. This means that
c(SOL_{I})\leq B+(B-1)h\leq(h+1)B
as
c(\bar{T})\leq B
and
|E(\bar{T})|\leq B-1
and each edge
e
of
G(Sol^{\prime})
is replaced by a path
SP_{e}
of cost at most
h
in
SOL_{I}
. Also, note that
p(SOL_{J})=\sum_{v_{x}\in V_{X}\cap V^{\prime}(SOL_{J})}p(v_{x})
as vertices in
V_{\mathcal{S}}\cap V^{\prime}
has prize zero in
J
. So, we have
p^{\prime}(SOL_{I})\geq p(SOL_{J})
as
SOL_{I}
spans all vertices in
Sol^{\prime\prime}
and
Sol^{\prime\prime}
spans all vertices in
Sol^{\prime}
. Hence a feasible solution (with at least the same prize as
SOL_{J}
and cost no more than
(h+1)B
) can be obtained from
SOL_{J}
for
MWBCSC
. This concludes the lemma.
∎

Observation B.1
.

Let
J=(G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime}),c^{\prime},p^{\prime},B)
be an instance of
UUAT
resulting in our transformation from an instance
I=(X,\mathcal{S},c,p,G_{\mathcal{S}},B)
of
MWBCSC
. Let
OPT_{J}
and
OPT_{I}
be optimal solutions to
J
and
I
, respectively. Then
p(OPT_{J})\geq p(OPT_{I})
.

Proof.

From any solution
T
to
I
where
c(T)\leq B
, we can always create a solution
T^{\prime}
to
J
by our transformation, where
c(T^{\prime})=c(T)=B
and
p(T^{\prime})=p(T)
. This means that we never have the case where
p(OPT_{J})<p(OPT_{I})
, otherwise by transforming
OPT_{I}
to a solution for
J
, we can achieve a better solution for
J
, which contradicts the assumption that
OPT_{J}
is an optimal solution to
J
. This concludes the proof.∎

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
B.6
.

Proof of Theorem
B.6
.

Let
OPT_{J}
and
OPT_{I}
be the optimum solutions to
J
and
I
, respectively. By Lemma
B.4
and Observation
B.1
, any
\alpha
-approximation for
UUAT
results in a solution
SOL_{I}
for
MWBCSC
such that
p(SOL_{I})\geq\frac{OPT_{J}}{\alpha}\geq\frac{OPT_{I}}{\alpha}
and
c(SOL_{I})\leq(h+1)B
. ∎

Corollary B.1
.

One can have a polynomial-time
O(h\log{n})
-approximation algorithm for
MWBCSC
, when
c(S)\geq 1,\forall{S\in\mathcal{S}}
.

Proof.

Note that Bateni et al.
[
2
]
’s approach for the prize collecting problem and the black-box introduced by Moss and Rabbani
[
26
]
result in an
O(\log n)
-approximation algorithm for
UUAT
violating the budget constraint by a factor of
2
. We call this approach
BMAlg
. Let
T
be such a solution after applying
BMAlg
, where
c(T)\leq 2B
and
p(T)=O(p(OPT))
that
OPT
is an optimal solution to
UUAT
. Then Bateni et al.
[
2
]
provided a trimming process to achieve a solution
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
which has cost
c(T^{\prime})\leq B
by loosing some constant in the approximation factor.

Note that as in
MWBCSC
the prize function is monotone submodular, we need to use our trimming process (i.e., Lemma
5.1
). Now we follow the same argument as that of Theorem
5.1
. This means that we first restrict ourselves to flat trees by guessing the vertex
x
with the maximum cost, setting the budget
B^{\prime}=B-c(x)
and removing vertices with cost more than
B/2
. Next we transform the instance
I
of
MWBCSC
to the instance
J
of
UUAT
as above. Now by
BMAlg
, we can achieve a solution
SOL_{J}
to
J
such that
p(SOL_{J})=O(\log n)
and
c(SOL_{J})\leq 2B
. We then transform
SOL_{J}
to a solution for
I
, denoted by
SOL_{I}
as Lemma
B.4
. Hence
p(SOL_{I})=O(\log n)
and
c(SOL_{I})\leq 2B(h+1)
. Finally, we apply Lemma
5.1
to achieve a subtree
\hat{T}
of
SOL_{I}
with the cost
B/4\leq c(\hat{T})\leq B
and the prize-to-cost ratio
\frac{p(\hat{T})}{c(\hat{T})}\geq\frac{\gamma}{32(2B(h+1))+8}
. This implies that
p(\hat{T})=O(\frac{1}{h\log n})
.∎

Another important consequence of Theorem
B.6
is that an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for
UUAT
yields an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for
MWBCSC
under Assumption

. This is an improvement over
\frac{2(\Delta+1)\alpha}{1-e^{-1}}
-approximation algorithm of Ran et al.
[

]
, where

is the maximum degree of the graph. Note that in this case we do not need to assume that
c(S)\geq 1,\forall{S\in\mathcal{S}}

Corollary B.2
.

One can have a polynomial-time
O(\log{n})
-approximation algorithm for
MWBCSC
under Assumption
B.1
.

Proof.

Let
J=(G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime}),c^{\prime},p^{\prime},B)
be an instance of
UUAT
resulting in our transformation from an instance
I=(X,\mathcal{S},c,p,G_{\mathcal{S}},B)
of
MWBCSC
. Now we run the algorithm by Bateni et al. on

. Let
SOL_{J}
be the solution that returned by their approach, where

and

. We know that if there exist two edges

and

are neighbor in

for

form

∎

Last we show that there is a polynomial-time constant approximation algorithm for
MWBCSC
when each set costs one under Assumption
B.1
.

Theorem B.7
.

Under Assumption
B.1
,
MWBCSC
admits a polynomial-time
\frac{16}{1-e^{-1}}
-approximation algorithm, when each set has cost one.

We need one useful observation.

Lemma B.5

(Lemma 3 in Lamprou et al.
[
23
]
)
.

Let
k
be an integer. Given any tree
T
on
ak
vertices, where
a\in N
is a constant, and
k\geq 4a-2
, we can decompose it into
2a
subtrees each on at most
k
vertices.

We are ready to prove Theorem
B.7
.

Proof.

Let
I=(X,\mathcal{S},c,p,G_{\mathcal{S}},B)
be an instance
MWBCSC
. We first assign prizes to the nodes in
G_{\mathcal{S}}
as follows: we iteratively pick the node
v
in
G_{\mathcal{S}}
that covers the elements with the maximum prize
p_{max}
, assign
p_{max}
to
v
as its prize and remove
v
and the covered elements from further consideration. Ran et al.
[
29
]
showed that this assigning-prize process to the nodes ensures that there exists a connected subcollection of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
such that
p(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})\geq\frac{1-e^{-1}}{2}opt
and
c(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})\leq 2B
, where
opt
is the optimum solution to
MWBCSC
. As in our case each vertex in
G_{\mathcal{S}}
has cost
1
, by the same argument as that of Theorem
B.5
, we can set the budget to
2B-1
and run the polynomial-time
2
-approximation algorithm of Paul et al.
[
28
]
on this input. This provides us a tree
T=(V(T),E(T))
with
|E(T)|\leq 2B-1
and
p(T)\geq\frac{1-e^{-1}}{4}opt
. By Lemma
B.5
, we can decompose
T
into
4
subtrees each containing at most
B
vertices, and then select the subtree maximizing the prize. This concludes the proof.
∎

Budgeted Sensor Cover Problem (
BSCP
).

This problem is very well-known in wireless sensor networks. In
BSCP
, we are given a set
\mathcal{S}
of sensors, a set
\mathcal{P}
of target points in a metric space, a sensing range
R_{s}
, a communication range
R_{c}
, and a budget
B
. A target point is covered by a sensor
s
if it is within a distance
R_{s}
of
s
’center. Two sensors are connected if they are at a distance at most
R_{c}
.
The goal is to find a subset
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
such that
|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}|\leq B
, the number of covered target points by
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is maximized and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
induces a connected subgraph.

Note that
BSCP
is a special case of
MWBCSC
. Then, by Theorem
B.5
, we have a
2f
-approximation algorithm for
BSCP
, where
f
is the maximum number of sensors that cover a target point.

Next we investigate
BSCP
under the widely used assumption that
R_{s}\leq R_{c}/2
, where
R_{s}
and
R_{c}
are the sensing range and communication range of sensors, respectively. In the following, we show that using some materials from the literature leads to an improvement to
[
16
,
22
,
29
,
32
,
33
]
for
BSCP
under the assumption that
R_{s}\leq R_{c}/2
. Note that Huang et al.
[
16
]
studied
BSCP
under the assumption that
R_{s}/R_{c}=O(1)
, however, our result improves their result under the assumption that
R_{s}\leq R_{c}/2
.

Theorem B.8
.

If
R_{s}\leq R_{c}/2
, then
BSCP
admits a polynomial-time
\frac{8}{1-e^{-1}}
-approximation algorithm.

Proof.

We first construct a graph
G=(V,E)
as follows:
V=\mathcal{S}
and for any two sensors
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
,
e=(s,s^{\prime})\in E
if
d(s,s^{\prime})\leq R_{c}
, where
d
denotes the minimum distance between two senors
s
and
s^{\prime}
in the plane. Now we assign prizes to the nodes in
G
as follows: we iteratively pick the sensor
s
that covers the maximum number of the target points in the plane, assign the number of the covered target points to
s
as its prize and remove
s
and the covered target points from further consideration. It can be shown that assigning prizes to the nodes in such a way ensures that
G
contains a connected subgraph
G^{\prime}
,
G^{\prime}\subseteq G
, with
|V(G^{\prime})|\leq 2B
and the prize
p(G^{\prime})\geq(1-e^{-1})opt
, where
opt
is the optimum solution to
BSCP
(see e.g.
[
7
]
).

From the proof of Theorem
B.5
, we only need to consider the unrooted version of
E-URAT
where the input graph is
G
with cost
1
on the edges and budget
2B-1
. Now we apply the polynomial-time
2
-approximation algorithm of Paul et al.
[
28
]
to this input and achieve a tree
T
of cost at most
2B-1
and prize at least
p(T)\geq\frac{1-e^{-1}}{2}opt
. By Lemma
B.5
, we can decompose
T
into
4
subtrees each containing at most
B
vertices, and then select the one with the maximum prize. This concludes the proof.
∎
