Random Generator of Orthogonal Matrices in Finite Fields
Lasha Ephremidze and Ilya Spitkovsky

Abstract




1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
47A68

Abstract.
We propose a superfast method for constructing orthogonal matrices
M\in\mathcal{O}(n,q)
in finite fields
GF(q)
. It can be used to construct
n\times n
orthogonal matrices in
Z_{p}
with very high values of
n
and
p
, and also orthogonal matrices with certain circulant structure.
Equally well one can construct paraunitary filter banks or wavelet matrices over finite fields.
The construction mechanism is highly efficient, allowing for the complete screening and selection of an orthogonal matrix that meets specific constraints. For instance, one can generate a complete list of orthogonal matrices with given
n
and
q=p^{m}
provided that the order of
\mathcal{O}(n,q)
is not too large. Although the method is based on randomness, isolated cases of failure can be identified well in advance of the basic procedure’s start.

The proposed procedures are based on the Janashia-Lagvilava method which was developed for entirely different task, therefore, it may seem somewhat unexpected.

15B10, 20G40, 42C40, 94A60.

1.
Introduction.

Spectral factorization is a powerful mathematical tool with various applications in control engineering and communications. The Janashia-Lagvilava method
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
has been developed over the years to solve the challenging spectral factorization problem in the matrix case. This method has been successfully algorithmized
[
4
]
and offers several advantages over existing methods, as demonstrated in previous works
[
11
]
,
[
2
]
. One noteworthy advantage of this method, which can be achieved through a suitable modification, is that it unexpectedly has the ability to generate a multitude of orthogonal matrices over finite fields in mere milliseconds, potentially finding applications in coding theory.

It had been revealed in
[
10
]
,
[
3
]
, that the Janashia-Lagvilava method was closely related with wavelet theory.
The discovery of this connection paved the way for the accomplishments detailed in this paper. Furthermore, the method has been recently generalized for any field satisfying the minimal requirements
[
5
]
. However, these requirements necessitate the existence of a positive element in the field, precluding finite fields. In fact, the field of rational polynomials in several complex variables was the main focus of our paper
[
5
]
, and by taking this viewpoint, we were able to generalize the Janashia-Lagvilava method to matrix functions on the multidimensional torus
[
6
]
. The main result of
[
5
]
, Theorem 4.1, does not hold for finite fields and, accordingly, the procedure described therein cannot guarantee the construction of the required unitary matrix polynomial for finite fields. Nevertheless, as it was further realized and confirmed by numerical simulations, the isolated cases of failure do not exclude the existence of statistically robust method of constructing above mentioned unitary matrix polynomials for finite fields, while these matrix polynomials can be converted into orthogonal matrices. To be specific, this paper provides efficient computational procedures for proving the following

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a finite field and
G
be an
n\times n
matrix function of the form

(1)

G(t)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1&0\\
\zeta_{1}(t)&\zeta_{2}(t)&\zeta_{3}(t)&\cdots&\zeta_{n-1}(t)&1\end{pmatrix},

where
\zeta_{i}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\gamma_{ik}t^{-k}
,
i=1,2,\ldots,n-1
, are Laurent polynomials,
0,1,\gamma_{ik}\in\mathbb{F}
. Denote by
\Gamma_{i}
,
i=1,2,\ldots,n-1
, the Hankel matrices

(2)

\Gamma_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\gamma_{i1}&\gamma_{i2}&\cdots&\gamma_{i,N-1}&\gamma_{iN}\\
\gamma_{i1}&\gamma_{i2}&\gamma_{i3}&\cdots&\gamma_{iN}&0\\
\gamma_{i2}&\gamma_{i3}&\gamma_{i4}&\cdots&0&0\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\cdot\\
\gamma_{iN}&0&0&\cdots&0&0\end{pmatrix},

and by
\Delta
the following
(N+1)\times(N+1)
matrix

\Delta=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\Gamma_{i}^{2}+I_{N+1}.

If
\det\Delta\not=0
, then there exists a unique
n\times n
polynomial matrix
U
,

(3)

{\bf U}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}u_{11}(t)&u_{12}(t)&\cdots&u_{1,n-1}(t)&u_{1n}(t)\\
u_{21}(t)&u_{22}(t)&\cdots&u_{2,n-1}(t)&u_{2n}(t)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
{u_{n1}(t)}&{u_{n2}(t)}&\cdots&{u_{n,n-1}(t)}&{u_{nn}(t)}\\
\end{pmatrix},

u_{ij}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}a_{ij,k}t^{k}
,
a_{ij,k}\in\mathbb{F}
, such that the entries in the product matrix

G(t)\mathop{\rm diag}(1,1,\ldots,1,t^{-N}){\bf U}(t)

contain only the non-negative powers of
t

(
i.e. the coefficients of
t^{-k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots
, are
0
of
\mathbb{F}

)
and, in addition,
U(1)=I_{n}
.

Furthermore, the matrix polynomial (
3
) has the property that

(4)

{\bf U}(t)\widetilde{{\bf U}}(t)=I_{n}

where
\widetilde{{\bf U}}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}U_{k}^{T}t^{-k}
for
{\bf U}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}U_{k}t^{k}
.

The straightforward computations show that, due to the property (
4
), the
n\times n
matrix

W_{0}={\bf U}(-1)=U_{0}-U_{1}+U_{2}-\ldots(-1)^{N}U_{N},

and the block circulant
n(N+1)\times n(N+1)
matrix
W
with the first (block) row

(5)

W_{1}=(U_{0}\;U_{1}\;\ldots\;U_{N})

are orthogonal, i.e.

(6)

W_{0}W_{0}^{T}=I_{n}\;\;\text{ and }\;\;WW^{T}=I_{n(N+1)}.

Hence, relying on the proof of Theorem 1, after selecting positive integers
n
and
N
, one can randomly generate the coefficients
\gamma_{ik}
,
1\leq i<n
,
1\leq k\leq N
, of polynomials
\zeta_{i}
in (
1
) from the field
\mathbb{F}
and construct the orthogonal matrices satisfying (
6
). Concerning computer memory allocation, these
(n-1)N
integers,
\gamma_{ik}
(generators), contain full (lossless) information about
n^{2}(N+1)
integers of
W_{1}
in (
5
). Explicit formulas converting the matrix
W_{1}
back into the generators are also provided.

Remark 1
.

Note that the method generates only the trivial matrix
W_{0}=I_{n}
for
q=2
since
-1=1
in
GF(2)
and
{\bf U}(1)=I_{n}
by construction. However, the circulant matrix
W
still may be different from the trivial matrix
I_{n(N+1)}
.

We would like to emphasize that Janashia-Lagvilava method in fact contains the proof of the above theorem and it can be readily apparent from previous publications
[
9
]
,
[
5
]
. However, the primary theoretical improvement of the Janashia-Lagvilava method proposed in what follows is the modification of the existing computational procedures. This modification eliminates the need to invert any
n\times n
matrix, which was previously required (see
[
9
, Eq. (51)]
or reasoning after Eq. (4.23) in
[
5
]
), and makes the entire procedure superfast. Namely, constructing orthogonal matrices
W_{0}
and
W
of sizes
n\times n
and
n(N+1)\times n(N+1)
, respectively, requires
O(N^{3})+(N+1)^{2}n^{2}
(with the exact constant 1 instead of
O
in front of the second summand) multiplications (in the field
\mathbb{F}
). Furthermore, for arbitrarily large
n
, it is possible to select the second parameter
N
as small as
1
, admitting a moderate level of randomness.

2.
Notation

This section summarizes the notation used throughout the paper, although most symbols have already been introduced in the introduction. Let
\mathbb{F}\equiv GF(q)=GF(p^{m})
be the Galois field consisting of
q=p^{m}
elements, where
p
is a prime number, and
\mathbb{Z}_{p}\cong\mathbb{Z}

(\mod p)
. Wherever we encounter 0 or 1 they are presumed to belong to this field, while
-1
is the additive inverse of
1
in
\mathbb{F}
.

For a set
\mathcal{S}
, let
\mathcal{S}^{n\times k}
be the set of
n\times k
matrices with entries from
\mathcal{S}
, while
\mathcal{S}^{n}:=\mathcal{S}^{n\times 1}
.
I_{n}=\mathop{\rm diag}(1,1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
is the identity matrix and
0_{n\times k}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times k}
is the
n\times k
matrix consisting of zeros. A matrix
M\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times k}
with entries
M_{ij}\in\mathbb{F}
is denoted by
M:=[M_{ij}]_{i=1:n}^{j=1:k}
, or simply by
M:=[M_{ij}]
. A matrix
M=[M_{ij}]\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
is called orthogonal if

(7)

MM^{T}=I_{n},

where
M^{T}=[M_{ji}]
stands for the transpose of
M
. The above equation implies also that
M^{T}M=I_{n}
.
The set of orthogonal
n\times n
matrices in
GF(q)
is denoted by
\mathcal{O}(n,q)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}\equiv\mathcal{P}[\mathbb{F}]
be the set of Laurent polynomials with coefficients from
\mathbb{F}
:

(8)

\mathcal{P}:=\left\{\sum\nolimits_{k=k_{1}}^{k_{2}}c_{k}t^{k}:c_{k}\in\mathbb{F},\;k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z};\;k_{1}\leq k_{2}\right\}.

We also consider the following subsets of
\mathcal{P}
:
\mathcal{P}^{+}
,
\mathcal{P}^{-}
,
\mathcal{P}^{+}_{N}
and
\mathcal{P}^{-}_{N}
, where
N
is a non-negative integer, which corresponds to sets
k_{1}=0
,
k_{2}=0
,
0=k_{1}\leq k_{2}=N
, and
-N=k_{1}\leq k_{2}=0
in (
8
), respectively. So,
\mathcal{P}^{+}
is the set of usual polynomials, and
\mathcal{P}^{+}_{N}
is the set of polynomials of degree less than or equal to
N
. Of course, any polynomial
P\in\mathcal{P}[\mathbb{F}]
,

(9)

P(t)=\sum\nolimits_{k=k_{1}}^{k_{2}}c_{k}t^{k},

can be considered as the function
P:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
by plugging in it the values from
\mathbb{F}
instead of indeterminate variable
t
. The coefficient
c_{0}\in\mathbb{F}
is called the free term of
P
. It is also naturally assumed that
\mathbb{F}\subset\mathcal{P}[\mathbb{F}]
, i.e.
c\in\mathbb{F}
can be identified with the (constant) polynomial
ct^{0}
. Saying that a polynomial (
9
) is constant means that all coefficients, except for a possible term
c_{0}
, are zero. In this case, slightly abusing notation, we can write
P(t)=c_{0}
. In a similar manner,
P\in\mathcal{P}^{+}
means that all the coefficients of
P
corresponding to negative powers of
t
are equal to
0
.

A matrix (Laurent) polynomial
{\bf P}\in\mathcal{P}^{n\times k}
can also be viewed as a polynomial with matrix coefficients,

(10)

{\bf P}(t)=\sum\nolimits_{k=k_{1}}^{k_{2}}C_{k}t^{k},\;\;\;C_{k}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times k}.

For a polynomial (
9
), let

(11)

\widetilde{{\bf P}}(t)=\sum\nolimits_{k=k_{1}}^{k_{2}}c_{k}t^{-k},

and for a matrix polynomial (
10
), let

(12)

\widetilde{{\bf P}}(t)=\sum\nolimits_{k=k_{1}}^{k_{2}}C_{k}^{T}t^{-k},

i.e. if
{\bf P}(t)=[{\bf P}_{ij}(t)]_{i=1:n}^{j=1:k}
, then
\widetilde{{\bf P}}(t)=[\widetilde{{\bf P}}_{ji}(t)]_{j=1:k}^{i=1:n}
. A matrix polynomial
{\bf A}
is called paraunitary if

(13)

{\bf A}(t)\widetilde{{\bf A}}(t)=I_{n},

i.e., all matrix coefficients of
{\bf A}\widetilde{{\bf A}}
are
0_{n\times n}
except the free term, and the latter is equal to
I_{n}
. Thus, the matrix polynomial
{\bf U}
in (
4
) is paraunitary.

The symbol
\delta_{ij}
stands for the Kronecker delta, i.e.,
\delta_{ij}=1
if
i=j
and
\delta_{ij}=0
otherwise.

Note that basic linear algebra rules apply to linear spaces over finite fields, and therefore we can use them without further justification.

3.
Constructive proof of Theorem 1

We mainly follow the path developed in the Janashia-Lagvilava method described in publications
[
9
]
,
[
5
]
. Therefore, we do not repeat many details given therein and emphasize only the arising differences.

Construction of the paraunitary matrix (
3
), for a given matrix (
1
), starts by considering the following system:

(14)

\begin{cases}\zeta_{1}x_{n}-\widetilde{x_{1}}\in\mathcal{P}^{+},\\
\zeta_{2}x_{n}-\widetilde{x_{2}}\in\mathcal{P}^{+},\\
\vdots\\
\zeta_{n-1}x_{n}-\widetilde{x_{n-1}}\in\mathcal{P}^{+},\\
\zeta_{1}x_{1}+\zeta_{2}x_{2}+\ldots+\zeta_{n-1}x_{n-1}+\widetilde{x_{n}}\in\mathcal{P}^{+}.\end{cases}

We search for its solution in the class
\mathcal{P}^{+}_{N}
, i.e., for
(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in(\mathcal{P}^{+}_{N})^{1\times n}
satisfying the conditions of the system (
14
).

It is proved in
[
9
]
,
[
5
]
that if we find
n
different solutions

(15)

{\bf U}_{j}(t)=(x^{j}_{1}(t),x^{j}_{2}(t),\ldots,x^{j}_{n}(t))^{T}\in(\mathcal{P}^{+}_{N})^{n\times 1},\;j=1,2,\ldots,n,

of (
14
) satisfying also

(16)

{\bf U}_{j}(1)=(\delta_{1j},\delta_{2j},\ldots,\delta_{nj})^{T},

and assume

\hat{{\bf U}}_{j}(t)=(x^{j}_{1}(t),x^{j}_{2}(t),\ldots,x^{j}_{n-1}(t),\;t^{N}\widetilde{x^{j}_{n}}(t))^{T},\;\;j=1,2,\ldots,n

(the order of coefficients has been reversed in the last entry of
{\bf U}_{j}
in order to get
\hat{{\bf U}}_{j}
), then (
3
) can be represented as a concatenation of the columns

(17)

{\bf U}=(\hat{{\bf U}}_{1},\hat{{\bf U}}_{2},\ldots,\hat{{\bf U}}_{n}),

and thus constructed matrix (
17
) will satisfy the condition (
4
) as well.

To solve the system (
14
), we rewrite it in the equivalent form in terms of the coefficients of polynomial entries. In particular, to obtain the solution (
15
) satisfying (
16
), we reduce (
14
) to

(18)

\begin{cases}D\cdot{X_{1}}-\Gamma_{1}\cdot X_{n}={\bf 0},\\
D\cdot{X_{2}}-\Gamma_{2}\cdot X_{n}={\bf 0},\\
\cdot\;\;\;\;\;\cdot\;\;\;\;\;\cdot\\
D\cdot{X_{j}}-\Gamma_{j}\cdot X_{n}={\bf 1},\\
\cdot\;\;\;\;\;\cdot\;\;\;\;\;\cdot\\
D\cdot{X_{n-1}}-\Gamma_{n-1}\cdot X_{n}={\bf 0},\\
\Gamma_{1}\cdot X_{1}+\Gamma_{2}\cdot X_{2}+\ldots+\Gamma_{n-1}\cdot X_{n-1}+D\cdot{X_{n}}={\bf 0},\end{cases}

j=1,2,\ldots,n
, where

D=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&\cdots&1&1\\
0&1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0&0\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\cdot\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0&1\end{pmatrix},\;\;\Gamma_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
\gamma_{i1}&\gamma_{i2}&\gamma_{i3}&\cdots&\gamma_{iN}&0\\
\gamma_{i2}&\gamma_{i3}&\gamma_{i4}&\cdots&0&0\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\cdot\\
\gamma_{iN}&0&0&\cdots&0&0\end{pmatrix},

{\bf 0}=0_{(N+1)\times 1}=(0,0,\ldots,0)^{T}
,
{\bf 1}=(1,0,0,\ldots,0)^{T}\in\mathbb{F}^{N+1}
are given coefficients, and

X_{i}=(a_{i0},a_{i1},\ldots,a_{iN})^{T},\text{ where }x_{i}(t)=\sum\nolimits_{k=0}^{N}a_{ik}t^{k},

are unknown matrix coefficients. Note that matrices
D
and
\Gamma_{i}
differ from the corresponding matrices
[
9
, Eq. (26)]
,
[
5
, Eq. (4.10)]
in the first row. This replacement is justified by freedom of selection of constant terms of polynomials entering (
14
). On the other hand, this flexibility allows the condition
{\bf U}(1)=I_{n}
to be merged into the system (
14
). Therefore, we can avoid an expensive step of inverting
n\times n
matrix required in preceding papers. Note also that
\Gamma_{i}
differs from (
2
) in the same way, however, this difference is immaterial and we keep the notation for convinience.

We proceed solving the system (
18
) in a natural way: we have

(19)

X_{i}=D^{-1}\cdot\Gamma_{i}\cdot X_{n}+\delta_{ij}D^{-1}\cdot{\bf 1},\;\;i=1,2,\ldots,n-1.

Substituting these equations into the last equation of (
18
), we get

\Gamma_{1}D^{-1}\Gamma_{1}X_{n}+\Gamma_{2}D^{-1}\Gamma_{2}X_{n}+\ldots+\Gamma_{n-1}D^{-1}\Gamma_{n-1}X_{n}+D{X_{n}}=-\Gamma_{j}D^{-1}{\bf 1}

(where it is assumed that the right-hand side is equal to
{\bf 1}
for
j=n
), which leads to

(20)

\Delta X_{n}=-D^{-1}\Gamma_{j}D^{-1}{\bf 1},

where

\Delta=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n-1}(D^{-1}\Gamma_{i})^{2}+I_{N+1}.

The sufficient condition,
\det\Delta\not=0
, for the existence of the solution to (
20
) is reflected (in the slightly different but equivalent form) in the hypothesis of the theorem. Therefore, assuming that this condition holds, we determine
X_{n}
from (
20
) and then
X_{i}
,
i=1,2,\ldots,n-1
from (
19
).

4.
Paraunitary filter banks and wavelets over
\mathbb{F}

The theory of paraunitary filter banks (PFB) in real and complex fields was developed in
[
16
]
and it is closely related with the concept of wavelet matrices as it is presented in
[
15
]
. Indubitably, this theory has many applications in signal processing. A complete parameterization of such filter banks and wavelet matrices depending on Janashia-Lagvilava method was proposed in
[
3
]
.

In
[
13
]
, PFB were considered over finite fields. It was emphasized that filter banks over finite fields have the advantage that all the round-off error and the coefficient quantization error can be eliminated completely. In addition, its potential applicability in cryptography, in the theory of error-correcting codes, was mentioned. Accordingly, various theoretical properties of PFB were explored. In parallel, the theory of wavelet transforms over finite fields was proposed by several authors
[
17
]
,
[
1
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
7
]
and further applications envisioned. As one can observe, these two developments mostly differ from each other by terminology only.

As paraunitary filter banks are nothing but the coefficients of polynomial matrices (
3
) with property (
4
), the present paper offers a superfast method for constructing PFB and related wavelet matrices. Furthermore, a great deal of facts observed in
[
3
]
,
[
5
]
can be directly extended to finite fields setting as well. In particular, whenever paraunitary matrix

(21)

{\bf U}(t)=\sum\nolimits_{k=0}^{N}U_{k}t^{k}

is constructible by the method described in the paper, it is unique for a given set of generators, its determinant is always equal to
t^{N}
, i.e.

(22)

\det{\bf U}(t)=t^{N},

and the last row of the matrix
U_{N}
in (
21
) cannot be all zeros. Conversely, if we have a paraunitary matrix (
21
) such that
(n,j)
-th entry of
U_{N}
differs from zero,
(U_{N})_{nj}\not=0
, then the generators
\zeta_{i}
,
i=1,2,\ldots,n-1
, can be reconstructed by the formulas (cf.
[
3
, Eq. (25)]
)

(23)

\zeta_{i}(t)=\left[\widetilde{u_{ij}}(t)\big(t^{N}\widetilde{u_{nj}}(t)\big)^{-1}\right]^{-},\;\;\;i=1,2,\ldots,n-1,

where under
\big(t^{N}\widetilde{u_{nj}}(t)\big)^{-1}
we assume a formal inverse series of the polynomial
t^{N}\widetilde{u_{nj}}(t)
(it exists since its constant term differs from 0), and
\left[\sum_{k=-N}^{\infty}a_{k}t^{k}\right]^{-}:=\sum_{k=-N}^{-1}a_{k}t^{k}
. The formula (
23
) can be derived similarly to
[
3
, Eq. 56]
.

5.
Numerical simulations

We performed numerical simulations described in this section using a MATLAB code that was executed on a laptop equipped with an Intel(R) Core(TM) i7 8650U CPU (with a clock speed of 1.90 GHz) and 16.00 GB of RAM. Whenever the matrix parameters exceeded the available memory of our local computer, we utilized a compute node from the High Performance Computing resources at New York University, Abu Dhabi. The compute node had an Intel(R) Xeon(R) CPU E5-2680 v4 @ 2.40GHz processor, 28 CPUs, and 102 Gigabytes of usable memory. The results of these simulations are given in Tables 1 and 2, respectively.
They present the computational time (
t
in seconds) required to construct an
n\times n
orthogonal matrix
W_{0}
or an
n(N+1)\times n(N+1)
circulant orthogonal matrix
W
(see (
6
)) with elements from
Z_{p}
by the proposed method. The corresponding columns of the tables display the values of
p
,
n
,
N
, and
t
. (The gaps in the last row of Table 2 indicate that the available RAM was not sufficient to perform the corresponding computations.)
We also statistically estimate the probabilities of failure for the construction of the corresponding matrices. Namely, for each triple
(p,n,N)
we conducted 1000 random trials and recorded the number of failures
f
. The observed stability of results, as shown in the
f
column of the table, indicates that the probability of failure is contingent solely upon
p
.
At first glance, this appears particularly surprising, warranting further investigation into its underlying cause.

6.
Conclusion

In this paper, we have introduced a novel method for generating
n\times n
orthogonal matrices in the Galois field
GF(q)
that is highly efficient and can handle large values of
n
and
q
. Our method is based on random selection, but we have shown that it has a low probability of failure for large
q
, as verified through statistical testing. In addition, our method can be used to construct paraunitary filter banks over finite fields. We believe that this approach can be useful in constructing orthogonal matrices that satisfy specific constraints, making it potentially valuable for applications in coding theory and signal processing.
