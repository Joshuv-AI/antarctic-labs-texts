Quantum parameter estimation with optimal control
Jing Liu, Haidong Yuan

Abstract
A pivotal task in quantum metrology, and quantum parameter estimation in general, is to design schemes that achieve the highest precision with given resources. Standard models of quantum metrology usually assume the dynamics is fixed, the highest precision is achieved by preparing the optimal probe states and performing optimal measurements. However, in many practical experimental settings, additional controls are usually available to alter the dynamics. Here we propose to use optimal control methods for further improvement on the precision limit of quantum parameter estimation. We show that by exploring the additional degree of freedom offered by the controls higher precision limit can be achieved. In particular we show that the precision limit under the controlled schemes can go beyond the constraints put by the coherent time, which is in contrast to the standard scheme where the precision limit is always bounded by the coherent time.

II
Methodology

In this article we consider the system whose dynamics can be described by the master equation

\partial_{t}{\rho}(t)=\mathcal{L}[\rho(t)],

(1)

where
\mathcal{L}
is a super-operator. For unitary evolution

\mathcal{L}=-iH^{\times}
where
H^{\times}(\rho)=\left[H,\rho\right]
;
for noisy evolution
\mathcal{L}=-iH^{\times}+\Gamma
where
\Gamma
denotes the super-operator for the noisy process.
The Hamiltonian of a controlled system can be written as
Khaneja05
;
Ernst1987

H=H_{0}(x)+\sum_{k=1}^{p}V_{k}(t)H_{k},

(2)

where
H_{0}(x)
is the free evolution Hamiltonian,
x
is the interested parameter,

\sum_{k=1}^{p}V_{k}(t)H_{k}
are control Hamiltonians with
V_{k}(t)
representing the amplitude
of
k
th control field. Here we assume the correlation in the environment decays much faster than
the evolution of the system under Eq. (
1
), and the Markovian approximation is still valid at the presence
of controls
Breuer07
. For example, in Nuclear Magnetic Resonance, the correlation time of the environment is
around
10^{-6}
s and the coherent time is around
0.1\sim 1
s
Ernst1987
, if the time scale of the control is
around
10^{-3}
s, then the Markovian approximation is valid, and the controls are fast enough to generate the desired
operations. We also assume the controls do not change the noisy operators, this holds under some physical
settings
Sklarz2004
;
Tannor1999
;
note
but not in general. The situations that noisy operators are affected by
controls will be addressed in another work.

To implement the GRAPE we will divide the evolution time
T
into small time steps, and within each time
step
\Delta t
the controls will be approximated as constants. The final
state at time
T
can thus be written as
\rho(T)=\Pi_{i=1}^{m}\exp(\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{i})\rho(0),

here
m=T/\Delta t
is the number of time steps and
\mathcal{L}_{i}
is the super-operator
for the
i
th time step. The multiplication in
\rho(T)
is taken from right to left.

GRAPE can obtain controls that optimize a given objective function. In this article we focus on the local precision limit for the
measurement of small shifts around certain known values. Such local precision limit can be quantified by the QFI,
we will thus take the QFI as the objective function. The QFI is defined as

F(T)=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho(T)L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(T)\right],

(3)

where
L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)
denotes the symmetric logarithmic derivative (SLD) which is the solution to the equation

\partial_{x}\rho(T)=\left[\rho(T)L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)+L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\rho(T)\right]/2
. The flow of the algorithm
is shown in Fig.
2
(detailed description is in appendix
A
).
Some steps of the algorithm may require the knowledge of
x
,
which is a-priori unknown, in that case an estimated value
\hat{x}
will be used and the
controls will be updated adaptively. This, however, does not affect the precision limit asymptotically.

III.1
Transverse dephasing

The improvement of the controlled scheme with transverse noises is shown in Fig.
4
(a).
When the probe state is taken as
|+\rangle
, the obtained optimal controls are

V_{1}(t)=V_{2}(t)=0
and
V_{3}(t)=-0.5\omega_{0}
, shown in Fig.
4

Such controls essentially keep the probe state at
|+\rangle
, where it is not affected by the noises.
The QFI under such controls is given by (see appendix for detailed derivation)

F(T)=\frac{2}{\gamma^{2}}\left(e^{-\gamma T}+\gamma T-1\right).

(6)

It always increases with
T
as
F^{\prime}(T)>0

The precision limit thus is not constrained by the coherent time. In contrast, without
controls there is an optimal time

(which is determined by the decay rate,
for example when
\omega_{0}\gg\gamma
,
T_{\mathrm{opt}}\simeq 2/\gamma
),
at which the precision reaches the maximum, and beyond
T_{\mathrm{opt}}
the QFI starts to
decrease with time, which can be seen in Fig.
4
(a).

We note in this case the control
V_{3}(t)=-0.5\omega_{0}
depends on the true value which is a-priori unknown, in practice an estimated
value
\hat{\omega}_{0}
need to be used and the controls need to be updated adaptively according to the estimated value as

V_{3}(t)=-0.5\hat{\omega}_{0}
. In Fig.
4
(c) we plotted the improvement provided by the controls with different estimation error,
it can be seen that the improvement is quite robust. For example assume the true value
\omega_{0}=1
and
T=20
, then
as long as
\hat{\omega}_{0}\in[0.8,1.2]
the controlled scheme outperforms the uncontrolled scheme,
and when
\hat{\omega}_{0}\in[0.9,1.1]

10

the uncontrolled schemes.

If the measurement is fixed, for example the measurement is taken as
\{|+\rangle\langle+|,|-\rangle\langle-|\}
(here
|\pm\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
),
we can also use the optimal control to improve the precision. From Fig.
4
(a) we can see that with the
optimal controls the CFI can actually achieve the maximal QFI, indicating that the precision limit under the optimal controlled scheme
is insensitive to the measurement performed on the final state, as long as it is projective. This is because the optimal measurement(which is a projective
measurement) can always be rotated to the fixed measurement which corresponds to a counter rotation on the probe state that can be achieved via controls.
As a comparison the precision without controls is also plotted, in this case the CFI oscillate with time and can only reach the QFI for
some specific time points, indicating this measurement scheme is only optimal for some specific time points.
From Fig.
4
(c) it can be seen that the precision obtained is also very robust against the estimation error.

III.3
Spontaneous emission

We give some analysis for the controlled scheme at the presence of the spontaneous emission, which is another
major noise for many practical systems. We consider the general master equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i[H,\rho]+\gamma_{+}\left[\sigma_{+}\rho(t)\sigma_{-}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\sigma_{-}\sigma_{+},\rho(t)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle+\gamma_{-}\left[\sigma_{-}\rho(t)\sigma_{+}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\sigma_{+}\sigma_{-},\rho(t)\right\}\right],

\sigma_{\pm}=(\sigma_{1}\pm i\sigma_{2})/2

H=\frac{1}{2}\omega_{0}\sigma_{3}+\vec{V}(t)\cdot\vec{\sigma}

The effects of the controls are shown in Figure

controlled scheme shows significant improvement over the value without controls. And similar to the dephasing case,
under the measurement

normazlied CFI (dashed blue line) not only achieves the maximum value, but also ceases to oscillate.

provide some intuition on how controls helped improving the precision. For simplicity, we assume

\gamma_{-}=\gamma

\displaystyle r_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{1}{2}\gamma t}\left[\cos(\omega_{0}t)r_{1}(0)-\sin(\omega_{0}t)r_{2}(0)\right],

(16)

\displaystyle r_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{1}{2}\gamma t}\left[\cos(\omega_{0}t)r_{2}(0)+\sin(\omega_{0}t)r_{1}(0)\right],

(17)

\displaystyle r_{3}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-1+e^{-\gamma t}+e^{-\gamma t}r_{3}(0).

(18)

If the initial state is taken as
|+\rangle
, then the QFI at time
t
is
F=e^{-\gamma t}t^{2}
and
the maximum is achieved at
T_{\mathrm{opt}}=2/\gamma
.

T-t_{0}
.

IV
Summary

For many current experimental settings the controlled sequential scheme is more implementable than the parallel
scheme since high-fidelity controls can now be routinely done on many physical systems, such as
nuclear magnetic resonance
Rancan
;
Kocher
;
Maximov
;
Spindler
;
Ryan
, nitrogen-vacancy
centers
Scheuer
;
Dolde
;
Rong
and cold atoms
Bucker
. GRAPE provides a general method
to obtain optimal controls for the improvement of the precision limit, which
is expected to find wide applications for many practical quantum parameter estimation tasks.

As a demonstration we applied the method to the frequency estimation with different noises and showed
that GRAPE can improve the precision limit beyond the limit set by the coherent time, which is contrary
to the conventional belief that coherent time sets the limit on the achievable precision. This is particular
useful for those systems where the measurements are costly and one would like to extract more information for each
measurement. For the dephasing cases, we showed that the gain of the controlled scheme is most eminent when
the dephasing noise is orthogonal to the Hamiltonian, while when the noise is parallel to the Hamiltonian,
the controls do not increase the precision one can obtain per unit of time.
Future research includes characterizing the dynamics and noises for which the controls are (un)useful.

The optimal control method can also be used for non-Markovian dynamics
Reich
;
Rebentrost
;
Schmidt

and easily incorporate various practical constraints on pulse shape
Dridi
;
Machnes
;
Koch
;
Bartels
;
Li
,
thus provides a versatile tool for designing controlled schemes for various quantum parameter estimation tasks.

Appendix A
Algorithm description

GRAPE can obtain controls that optimize a given objective function. In this article we focus on the local precision limit for the
measurement of small shifts around certain known values. Such local precision limit can be quantified by the QFI,
we will thus take the QFI as the objective function. The QFI is given by
F(T)=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho(T)L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(T)\right]
where

L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)
denotes the symmetric logarithmic derivative (SLD) which is the solution to the equation

\partial_{x}\rho(T)=\left[\rho(T)L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)+L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\rho(T)\right]/2
. The flow of the algorithm is as following:

1.

guess initial values of
V_{k}(j)
(here
V_{k}(j)
denotes the
k
th control at the
j
th time step);

2.

evolve the dynamics and obtain a trajectory of the system;

3.

calculate the QFI at the target time;

4.

calculate the gradient
\frac{\delta F(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}
;

5.

update
V_{k}(j)
to
V_{k}(j)+\epsilon\frac{\delta F(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}
;

6.

restart from step 2 using the updated
V_{k}(j)
until the QFI converges.

The detailed calculation of
\frac{\delta F(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}
is in appendix
B
.
The gradient of the QFI can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta t\mathrm{Tr}\left[L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(T)\mathcal{M}_{j}^{(1)}\right]

(19)

\displaystyle-2\Delta^{2}t\mathrm{Tr}\left[L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\!\left(\mathcal{M}_{j}^{(2)}+\mathcal{M}_{j}^{(3)}\right)\right],

where
\mathcal{M}^{(1)}_{j}
,
\mathcal{M}^{(2)}_{j}
and
\mathcal{M}^{(3)}_{j}
are Hermitian operators and in the form below

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{j}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\mathcal{D}_{j+1}^{m}H_{k}^{\times}(\rho_{j}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{j}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{j}\mathcal{D}_{j+1}^{m}H_{k}^{\times}\mathcal{D}_{i+1}^{j}\dot{H}_{0}^{\times}(\rho_{i}),

(20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{j}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\delta_{jm})\!\!\!\sum_{i=j+1}^{m}\!\!\mathcal{D}_{i+1}^{m}\dot{H}_{0}^{\times}\mathcal{D}_{j+1}^{i}H_{k}^{\times}(\rho_{j}),

here
\delta_{jm}
is Kronecker delta function.
L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)
is the SLD of
\rho(T)
,
D_{j+1}^{m}\coloneqq\prod_{i=j+1}^{m}\exp(\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{i})

is the propagating superoperator from the
j
th time point to the target time,
j<m
(we will let
\mathcal{D}_{i}^{i^{\prime}}=\openone

when
i>i^{\prime}
).
\rho_{j}=D_{1}^{j}\rho(0)
is the state at the
j
th time point.
H^{\times}_{k}=[H_{k},\cdot]
and

\dot{H}^{\times}_{0}=[\partial_{x}H_{0},\cdot]
.

In practical experiments, the measurements that can be taken are restricted.
It is thus also of practical importance to find the optimal controls that can lead to the highest precision under a fixed measurement,
which is quantified by the CFI
F_{\mathrm{cl}}
under the particular measurement, instead of the QFI.
This can also be treated via GRAPE. Given a set of POVM measurement
\{E(y)\}
with
\sum_{y}E(y)=\openone
,
the probability of getting the measurement result
y
is given by
p_{y|x}=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho(T)E(y))
, and the CFI is given by

F_{\mathrm{cl}}(T)=\sum_{y}\frac{(\partial_{x}p_{y|x})^{2}}{p_{y|x}}.

The gradient of
F_{\mathrm{cl}}
can be similarly obtained as Eq. (
19
), which is

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F_{\mathrm{cl}}(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta t\mathrm{Tr}\left(\tilde{L}_{2}\mathcal{M}_{j}^{(1)}\right)

(21)

\displaystyle-2\Delta^{2}t\mathrm{Tr}\left[\tilde{L}_{1}\left(\!\mathcal{M}_{j}^{(2)}\!+\!\mathcal{M}_{j}^{(3)}\!\right)\right],

where

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{y}(\partial_{x}\ln p_{y})E(y),

(22)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{y}(\partial_{x}\ln p_{y})^{2}E(y).

(23)

Here
\mathcal{M}^{(1,2,3)}_{j}
takes the same form as in Eq. (
20
)
(see appendix
C
for detailed derivation).

Appendix B
Gradient for QFI

The dynamics for the density matrix of a system can be described by the following general master equation

\partial_{t}\rho(t)=\mathcal{L}[\rho(t)],

(24)

where
\mathcal{L}
is a super-operator and
\mathcal{L}=-iH^{\times}+\Gamma
.
H^{\times}=[H,\cdot]

and
\Gamma
is the super-operator for the noise part. The Hamiltonian here is

H=H_{0}(x)+\sum_{k=1}^{p}V_{k}(t)H_{k},

(25)

where
H_{0}(x)
is the free evolution Hamiltonian,
x
is the interested parameter,

\sum_{k=1}^{p}V_{k}(t)H_{k}
are control Hamiltonians with
V_{k}(t)
representing the amplitude
of
k
th control field.

Our objective function is the QFI and the goal is to find the optimal control to obtain the maximum QFI.
The QFI is defined as

F(T)=\mathrm{Tr}(L^{2}_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\rho(T)),

(26)

where
L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)
is the SLD operator at target time
T
and is determined by the equation

2\partial_{x}\rho(T)=\rho(T)L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)+L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\rho(T)
.

Before utilizing GRAPE to obtain the optimal control, it is necessary to know the corresponding gradient for QFI
on the control coefficients, i.e.,
\frac{\delta F(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}
based on the general master equation. In this
section we will show the detailed calculation for the general dynamics given by Eq. (
24
).

Since
F(T)=\mathrm{Tr}\left[L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(T)D_{j+1}^{m}\rho_{j}\right]=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\lambda_{j}\rho_{j}\right]
,
here
L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)
is the symmetric logarithm derivative of
\rho(T)
,
D_{j+1}^{m}\coloneqq\prod_{i=j+1}^{m}\exp(\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{i})

is the propagating superoperator from the
j
th time point to the target time,
j<m
(we will let
\mathcal{D}_{i}^{i^{\prime}}=\openone

when
i>i^{\prime}
),
\rho_{j}=D_{1}^{j}\rho(0)
is the
state at the
j
th time point and
\lambda_{j}=L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(T)D_{j+1}^{m}
.
The gradient of the QFI with respect to the controls at the
j
th time step
\frac{\delta F(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}
can then be computed

\frac{\delta F(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\frac{\delta\lambda_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\rho_{j}\right)+\mathrm{Tr}\left(\lambda_{j}\frac{\delta\rho_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\right).

(27)

we calculate both terms in the following.

1)
First we calculate
\delta\rho_{j}/\delta V_{k}(j)
.
Here the only term contains
V_{k}(j)
is the propagator at the
j
th time point,

e^{\Delta tL_{j}}
. Since
\rho_{j}=e^{\Delta tL_{j}}\rho_{j-1}
, we have

\frac{\delta\rho_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}=\frac{\delta e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{j}}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\rho_{j-1}.

(28)

It is known that the derivative of an exponential operator is

\partial_{x}e^{A(x)}=\int_{0}^{1}e^{sA}\left(\partial_{x}A\right)e^{(1-s)A}ds.

Thus,

\frac{\delta e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{j}}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}=\int_{0}^{1}e^{\tau\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{j}}\left(\Delta t\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\right)e^{-\tau\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{j}}d\tau e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{j}}.

(29)

Since
\mathcal{L}_{j}(\cdot)=-i[H_{0}+\sum_{k}V_{k}(j)H_{k},\cdot]+\Gamma(\cdot)
, we have

\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}=-iH_{k}^{\times},

where
H_{k}^{\times}
represents the commutation superoperator,
i.e.,
H_{k}^{\times}A=[H_{k},A]
. We thus have

\frac{\delta e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{j}}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}=-i\Delta t\int_{0}^{1}e^{\tau\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{j}}H_{k}^{\times}e^{-\tau\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{j}}d\tau e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{j}},

(30)

which can be rewritten as

\frac{\delta e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{j}}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}=-i\Delta t\int_{0}^{1}e^{\tau\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{j}^{\times}}H_{k}^{\times}d\tau e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{j}}.

(31)

Expand it with the Taylor series,

\displaystyle\frac{\delta e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{j}}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\Delta t\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\tau\Delta t\right)^{n}d\tau}{n!}\left(\mathcal{L}_{j}^{\times}\right)^{n}H_{k}^{\times}e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{j}}

(32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\Delta t\right)^{n+1}}{\left(n+1\right)!}\left(\mathcal{L}_{j}^{\times}\right)^{n}H_{k}^{\times}e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\Delta tH_{k}^{\times}e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{j}},

where the last equation we used the first order approximation. Thus

\frac{\delta\rho_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}=-i\Delta tH_{k}^{\times}\rho_{j}.

(33)

2)
Next we calculate
\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{j}\delta\lambda_{j}/\delta V_{k}(j)]
.
We first consider the cases when
j<m
.
Since
\lambda_{j}=L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(T)D_{j+1}^{m}
, and
D_{j+1}^{m}
does not contain
V_{k}(j)
, thus

\frac{\delta\lambda_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}=\frac{\delta L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}D_{j+1}^{m},

we then have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\frac{\delta\lambda_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\rho_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\frac{\delta L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\rho(T)\right)

(34)

\displaystyle+\mathrm{Tr}\left(L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\frac{\delta L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\rho(T)\right).

where we used the fact that
D_{j+1}^{m}\rho_{j}=\rho(T)
.

Now take the functional derivative at both sides of the equation
\partial_{x}\rho(T)=[\rho(T)L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)+L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\rho(T)]/2
, then multiply
L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)
and take the
trace, we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[\frac{\delta\left(\partial_{x}\rho(T)\right)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\right]

(35)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[\frac{\delta\rho(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(T)\right]+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho(T)\frac{\delta L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\frac{\delta L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\rho(T)L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\right].

Compare with Eq. (
34
), we then have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[\frac{\delta\left(\partial_{x}\rho(T)\right)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\right]

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[\frac{\delta\rho(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(T)\right]+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\frac{\delta\lambda_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\rho_{j}\right),

which means

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\frac{\delta\lambda_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\rho_{j}\right)

(37)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{Tr}\!\!\left[\frac{\delta\left(\partial_{x}\rho(T)\right)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\right]\!\!-\!\!2\mathrm{Tr}\!\!\left[\frac{\delta\rho(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\!L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(T)\right]\!\!.

Since

\mathrm{Tr}\left[\frac{\delta\rho(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(T)\right]=\mathrm{Tr}\left[L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(T)D_{j+1}^{m}\frac{\delta\rho_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\right]=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\lambda_{j}\frac{\delta\rho_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\right),

we thus have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\frac{\delta\lambda_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\rho_{j}\right)

(38)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{Tr}\left[\left(\frac{\delta(\partial_{x}\rho(T))}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\right)\!\!L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\right]\!\!-\!\!2\mathrm{Tr}\left(\lambda_{j}\frac{\delta\rho_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{Tr}\left[\partial_{x}\!\!\left(\frac{\delta\rho(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\right)\!\!L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\right]\!\!-\!\!2\mathrm{Tr}\left(\lambda_{j}\frac{\delta\rho_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\right)\!\!,

where the last equality we assume the functional derivative and partial differentiation can be exchanged.
Substitute Eq. (
38
) into Eq. (
27
), we can obtain the expression for the
gradient, which is

\frac{\delta F(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}=2\mathrm{Tr}\left[\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\delta\rho(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\right)L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\right]+i\Delta t\mathrm{Tr}\left(\lambda_{j}H_{k}^{\times}\rho_{j}\right).

(39)

We now derive
\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\delta\rho(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\right)
.
Since
\frac{\delta\rho(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}=\mathcal{D}_{j+1}^{m}\frac{\delta\rho_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)},

we have

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\delta\rho(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta t\!\!\sum_{i=j+1}^{m}\mathcal{D}_{i+1}^{m}\left(\partial_{x}\mathcal{L}_{i}\right)\mathcal{D}_{j+1}^{i}\frac{\delta\rho_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}

(40)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{D}_{j+1}^{m}\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\delta\rho_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\right),

where in the first term we used the fact that in the first order

\partial_{x}e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{i}}=\Delta t\left(\partial_{x}\mathcal{L}_{i}\right)e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{i}}

(41)

From Eq. (
33
),
we then have
\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\delta\rho_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\right)=\partial_{x}(-i\Delta tH_{k}^{\times}\rho_{j})=-i\Delta tH_{k}^{\times}\partial_{x}\rho_{j}
. Now as

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\rho_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{j}\mathcal{D}_{i+1}^{j}\left(\partial_{x}\mathcal{L}_{i}\right)\mathcal{D}_{1}^{i}\rho_{0}

(42)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{j}\mathcal{D}_{i+1}^{j}\left(\partial_{x}\mathcal{L}_{i}\right)\rho_{i},

one have

\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\delta\rho_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\right)=-i\Delta^{2}t\sum_{i=1}^{j}H_{k}^{\times}\mathcal{D}_{i+1}^{j}\left(\partial_{x}\mathcal{L}_{i}\right)\rho_{i}.

With this expression, we have

\mathcal{D}_{j+1}^{m}\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\delta\rho_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\right)=-i\Delta^{2}t\sum_{i=1}^{j}\mathcal{D}_{j+1}^{m}H_{k}^{\times}\mathcal{D}_{i+1}^{j}\left(\partial_{x}\mathcal{L}_{i}\right)\rho_{i}.

Thus

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\delta\rho(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\right)\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!-i\Delta^{2}t\!\sum_{i=j+1}^{m}\mathcal{D}_{i+1}^{m}\left(\partial_{x}\mathcal{L}_{i}\right)\mathcal{D}_{j+1}^{i}H_{k}^{\times}\rho_{j}

(43)

\displaystyle\!\!-i\Delta^{2}t\sum_{i=1}^{j}\mathcal{D}_{j+1}^{m}H_{k}^{\times}\mathcal{D}_{i+1}^{j}\!\!\left(\partial_{x}\mathcal{L}_{i}\right)\!\rho_{i}.

Note that here we cannot discard
\Delta^{2}t
as the second order, as we have a summation which can effectively
add up to cancel one order of
\Delta t
(for example
\Delta^{2}t\sum_{i=1}^{m}1=m\Delta^{2}t=T\Delta t
).

Furthermore, as

\mathcal{L}_{i}(\cdot)=-i\left[H_{0}+\sum_{k}V_{k}(i)H_{k},\cdot\right]+\Gamma(\cdot),

and only the free Hamiltonian
H_{0}
contains
x
, we have
\partial_{x}\mathcal{L}_{i}=-i\left[\partial_{x}H_{0},\cdot\right]=-i(\partial_{x}H_{0})^{\times}
.
Thus, Eq. (
43
) can be expressed by

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\delta\rho(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\right)\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!-\Delta^{2}t\!\!\sum_{i=j+1}^{m}\mathcal{D}_{i+1}^{m}\left(\partial_{x}H_{0}\right)^{\times}\mathcal{D}_{j+1}^{i}H_{k}^{\times}\rho_{j}

(44)

\displaystyle\!\!-\Delta^{2}t\sum_{i=1}^{j}\mathcal{D}_{j+1}^{m}H_{k}^{\times}\mathcal{D}_{i+1}^{j}\!\!\left(\partial_{x}H_{0}\right)^{\times}\!\!\rho_{i}\!.

Multiplying
L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)
on both sides of the equation above and taking the trace gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\delta\rho(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\right)L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\right]

(45)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Delta^{2}t\!\!\sum_{i=j+1}^{m}\mathrm{Tr}\left[L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\mathcal{D}_{i+1}^{m}\left(\partial_{x}H_{0}\right)^{\times}\mathcal{D}_{j+1}^{i}H_{k}^{\times}\rho_{j}\right]

\displaystyle-\Delta^{2}t\!\!\sum_{i=1}^{j}\mathrm{Tr}\left[L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\mathcal{D}_{j+1}^{m}H_{k}^{\times}\mathcal{D}_{i+1}^{j}\left(\partial_{x}H_{0}\right)^{\times}\!\!\rho_{i}\right]\!\!.

Utilizing above equation, one can obtain the final expression for the gradient, which is

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\Delta^{2}t\sum_{i=j+1}^{m}\mathrm{Tr}\left[L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\mathcal{D}_{i+1}^{m}\left(\partial_{x}H_{0}\right)^{\times}\mathcal{D}_{j+1}^{i}H_{k}^{\times}\rho_{j}\right]

(46)

\displaystyle-2\Delta^{2}t\sum_{i=1}^{j}\mathrm{Tr}\left[L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\mathcal{D}_{j+1}^{m}H_{k}^{\times}\mathcal{D}_{i+1}^{j}\left(\partial_{x}H_{0}\right)^{\times}\rho_{i}\right]

\displaystyle+i\Delta t\mathrm{Tr}\left[L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(T)\mathcal{D}_{j+1}^{m}H_{k}^{\times}\rho_{j}\right].

For the case when
j=m
, the gradient is

\frac{\delta F(T)}{\delta V_{k}(m)}=2\mathrm{Tr}\left[\left(\partial_{x}\frac{\delta\rho_{m}}{\delta V_{k}(m)}\right)L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\right]+i\mathrm{Tr}\left[L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(T)\Delta tH_{k}^{\times}\rho_{m}\right].

In this case,
\delta\rho_{m}/\delta V_{k}(m)=-i\Delta tH_{k}^{\times}\rho_{m}
, thus

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[\left(\partial_{x}\frac{\delta\rho_{m}}{\delta V_{k}(m)}\right)L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)\right]

(47)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Delta^{2}t\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{Tr}\left[L_{\mathrm{s}}(T)H_{k}^{\times}D_{i+1}^{m}\left(\partial_{x}H_{0}\right)^{\times}\rho_{i}\right].

the gradient is then

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F(T)}{\delta V_{k}(m)}

\displaystyle=

(48)

\displaystyle+i\Delta t\mathrm{Tr}\left[L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(T)H_{k}^{\times}\rho_{m}\right].

Combine this equation with Eq. (

Appendix C
Gradient for CFI

It is known that CFI is

F_{\mathrm{cl}}(T)=\sum_{y}\frac{\left(\partial_{\theta}p_{y}\right)^{2}}{p_{y}},

(49)

where
p_{y}=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho(x,T)E(y))
. Here
E(y)

is a POVM measurement which satisfying
\sum_{y}E(y)=\openone
. To
calculate the gradient, we need to know

\displaystyle\frac{\delta p_{y}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[\frac{\delta\rho(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}E(y)\right]

(50)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[D_{j+1}^{m}\frac{\delta\rho_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}E(y)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\Delta t\mathrm{Tr}\left[E(y)D_{j+1}^{m}H_{k}^{\times}\rho_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Delta t\mathrm{Tr}\left[E(y)\mathcal{M}_{j}^{(1)}\right].

Then we have

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\left(\partial_{x}p_{y}\right)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\Delta t\mathrm{Tr}\left[E(y)\partial_{x}\left(D_{j+1}^{m}H_{k}^{\times}\rho_{j}\right)\right]

(51)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\Delta t\mathrm{Tr}\Big\{E(y)\Big[\left(\partial_{x}D_{j+1}^{m}\right)H_{k}^{\times}\rho_{j}

\displaystyle+D_{j+1}^{m}H_{k}^{\times}\partial_{x}\rho_{j}\Big]\Big\}.

From previous calculations, we know

\displaystyle\partial_{x}D_{j+1}^{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta t\sum_{i=j+1}^{m}D_{i+1}^{m}\left(\partial_{x}\mathcal{L}_{i}\right)D_{j+1}^{i},

(52)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\rho_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{j}D_{i+1}^{j}\left(\partial_{x}\mathcal{L}_{i}\right)\rho_{i},

(53)

then for
j\neq m
,

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\left(\partial_{x}p_{y}\right)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Delta^{2}t\mathrm{Tr}\Big[\Big(E(y)\sum_{i=j+1}^{m}D_{i+1}^{m}\dot{H}_{0}^{\times}D_{j+1}^{i}H_{k}^{\times}\rho_{j}

(54)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{j}D_{j+1}^{m}H_{k}^{\times}D_{i+1}^{j}\dot{H}_{0}^{\times}\rho_{i}\Big)\Big].

for
j=m
, there is

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\left(\partial_{x}p_{y}\right)}{\delta V_{k}(m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\Delta t\mathrm{Tr}\left[E(y)H_{k}^{\times}\partial_{x}\rho_{m}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Delta^{2}t\mathrm{Tr}\left[E(y)H_{k}^{\times}\sum_{i=1}^{m}D_{i+1}^{j}\dot{H}_{0}^{\times}\rho_{i}\right].

Thus, combined above equations, we have

\frac{\delta\left(\partial_{x}p_{y}\right)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}=-\Delta^{2}t\mathrm{Tr}\left\{E(y)\left[\mathcal{M}_{j}^{(2)}+\mathcal{M}_{j}^{(3)}\right]\right\}.

Finally, the gradient is

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F_{\mathrm{cl}}(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{y}\frac{\delta}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\left(\frac{(\partial_{x}p_{y})^{2}}{p_{\mathrm{T}}(y|x)}\right)

(55)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{y}2\frac{\partial_{x}p_{y}}{p_{y}}\left[\frac{\delta\left(\partial_{x}p_{y}\right)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}\right]-\left(\frac{\partial_{x}p_{y}}{p_{y}}\right)^{2}\frac{\delta p_{y}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{y}-2\Delta^{2}t\frac{\partial_{x}p_{y}}{p_{y}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[E(y)\left(\mathcal{M}_{j}^{(2)}+\mathcal{M}_{j}^{(3)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\Delta t\left(\frac{\partial_{x}p_{y}}{p_{y}}\right)^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left(E(y)\mathcal{M}_{j}^{(1)}\right).

The gradient for CFI is then obtained.

Appendix D
Analytical solution for transverse dephasing noise

For the dynamics with transverse dephasing noises, the controls obtained from the
GRAPE are shown in Fig.
4
(b), which are
V_{x}=0
,
V_{y}=0
and

V_{z}=-\omega_{0}/2
. The QFI under such control is the same as the QFI under free
evolution for
\omega_{0}=0
, which can actually be computed analytically. In the following we give a detailed calculation.

Under the Bloch representation

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\left(\openone+\vec{r}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right),

the initial state
|+\rangle
can be expressed as
\vec{r}(0)=(1,0,0)
.
From the master equation we can obtain the differential equations for the Bloch vector as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}r_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{0}r_{2}(t),

(56)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}r_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\gamma r_{2}(t)-\omega_{0}r_{1}(t),

(57)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}r_{3}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\gamma r_{3}(t).

(58)

The solution of these equations are

\displaystyle r_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{1}{2}\gamma t}\left[\frac{\gamma}{a}\sinh\left(\frac{1}{2}at\right)+\cosh\left(\frac{1}{2}at\right)\right],

(59)

\displaystyle r_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{2\omega_{0}}{a}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\gamma t}\sinh\left(\frac{1}{2}at\right),

(60)

\displaystyle r_{3}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(61)

where
a=\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-4\omega^{2}_{0}}
. We will now compute
f(\rho_{\omega_{0}},\rho_{\omega_{0}+\delta\omega_{0}})

where
f(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=\mathrm{Tr}\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho_{1}}\rho_{2}\sqrt{\rho_{1}}}
denotes the fidelity, the QFI can
then be obtained from the second order expansion of
f(\rho_{\omega_{0}},\rho_{\omega_{0}+\delta\omega_{0}})
for
\omega_{0}=0
.
It is easy to see that
\vec{r}(t)|_{\omega_{0}=0}=(1,0,0)
, which is
|+\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)

and the fidelity between
|+\rangle
and an evolved state with a general
\omega_{0}
is

f=\sqrt{\langle+|\frac{1}{2}\left(\openone+\vec{r}(t)\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)|+\rangle}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}r_{1}(t)}.

(62)

For a small
\delta\omega_{0}
, up to the second order we have
a=\gamma-\frac{2}{\gamma}\delta^{2}\omega_{0}

and
\frac{\gamma}{a}=1+\frac{2}{\gamma^{2}}\delta^{2}\omega_{0}
, then

\displaystyle r_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{1}{2}\gamma t}\left[\frac{\gamma}{a}\sinh\left(\frac{1}{2}at\right)+\cosh\left(\frac{1}{2}at\right)\right]

(63)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{t}{\gamma}\delta^{2}\omega_{0}}+\frac{2}{\gamma^{2}}\delta^{2}\omega_{0}\frac{e^{-\frac{t}{\gamma}\delta^{2}\omega_{0}}-e^{-\gamma t}e^{\frac{t}{\gamma}\delta^{2}\omega_{0}}}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\left(e^{-\gamma t}+\gamma t-1\right)\delta^{2}\omega_{0}.

Thus the fidelity is

\displaystyle f(\rho_{0},\rho_{\delta\omega_{0}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\left(e^{-\gamma t}+\gamma t-1\right)\delta^{2}\omega_{0}}

(64)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\left(e^{-\gamma t}+\gamma t-1\right)\delta^{2}\omega_{0}.

The QFI can then be obtained from the second order term as

F(t)=\frac{2}{\gamma^{2}}\left(e^{-\gamma t}+\gamma t-1\right).

(65)

Now we consider the classical Fisher information under optimal controls. Taking the measurement as

\{|+\rangle\langle+|,|-\rangle\langle-|\}
, then the probabilities are
p_{+}(t)=(1+r_{1}(t))/2
and

p_{-}=(1-r_{1}(t))/2
. The corresponding classical Fisher information is

F_{\mathrm{class}}(t)=\frac{(\partial_{\omega_{0}}p_{+})^{2}}{p_{+}p_{-}}=\frac{(\partial_{\omega_{0}}r_{1})^{2}}{1-r^{2}_{1}(t)}.

(66)

For the the controls
\hat{\omega}_{0}\sigma_{3}/2
, where
\hat{\omega}_{0}
is very close to
\omega_{0}
, based on
Eq. (
63
), one can see
1-r^{2}_{1}(t)=F(t)\delta^{2}\omega_{0}
and
\partial_{\omega_{0}}r_{1}(t)=-F(t)\delta\omega_{0}
,
which indicates
F_{\mathrm{class}}(t)=F(t)
, i.e., the measurement
\{|+\rangle\langle+\rangle,|-\rangle\langle-|\}

is the optimal measurement to access the quantum Fisher information.
