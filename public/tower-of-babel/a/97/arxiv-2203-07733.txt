Implications of the cosmological 21-cm absorption profile for high-redshift star formation and deep JWST surveys 2022 Implications of the cosmological 21-cm absorption profile for high-redshift star formation and deep JWST surveys – References
Shikhar Mittal ID, Girish Kulkarni ID

Abstract
Apart from its anomalously large depth, the cosmological 21-cm absorption signal measured by the EDGES collaboration also has a shape that is distinctly different from theoretical predictions. Models with non-traditional components such as super-adiabatic baryonic cooling or an excess radio background explain the depth of the observed profile, but still conspicuously fail to explain its shape. In this paper, we quantify the requirements imposed by the EDGES measurement on sources of Ly α \alpha and X-ray photons in the presence of excess radio background at cosmic dawn. In extreme cases, the Ly α \alpha and X-ray emissivities require to be enhanced by up to an order of magnitude relative to traditional models. Furthermore, this enhancement needs to be active only for a short duration. We find that under conventional assumptions for the cosmic star formation rate density, standard stellar populations are incapable of meeting these conditions. Only highly unusual models of massive metal-free stars seem to provide a possible mechanism. Conversely, if the sources of Ly α \alpha and X-ray photons are compelled to have standard properties, the EDGES measurement puts strong demands on the cosmic star formation rate density. This provides interesting falsifiable predictions for high-redshift galaxy surveys enabled by James Webb Space Telescope ( JWST ). We derive predictions for galaxy UV luminosity functions and number densities, and show that a deep JWST survey with a limiting UV magnitude of m UV , lim = 32 m_{\mathrm{UV,lim}}=32 would potentially be able to rule out the predictions enforced by the EDGES measurement.

Introduction

The absorption signal in the low-frequency spectrum of the sky reported by the EDGES collaboration
(
Bowman et al. 2018
)
has been widely considered to potentially have a cosmological origin
(
Barkana 2018
;
Mirocha &
Furlanetto 2019
)
. But it is also famously known that the amplitude of this signal,
\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}=0.5^{+0.5}_{-0.2}\,
K at
z\approx 17.2
, is larger than the expected cosmological value by a factor of two. This anomalously large depth has received considerable attention in the literature. Many studies explain the depth either by postulating excess cooling of cosmic hydrogen due to novel physical mechanisms
(
Meiksin &
Madau 2020
;
Li
et al. 2021
;
Mathur
et al. 2022
, eg.)
, or by bringing into play a part of the excess radio background (ERB) observed at relevant frequencies
(
Mittal &
Kulkarni 2022
, eg.)
. Some studies invoke even more exotic scenarios, such as a modified Hubble parameter at cosmic dawn
(
Hill &
Baxter 2018
;
Costa
et al. 2018
;
Wang & Zhao 2018
;
Xiao
et al. 2019
)
, new coupling terms in the spin temperature
(
Widmark 2019
;
Lambiase &
Mohanty 2020
)
, or special classes of dark energy and dark matter models
(
Mukhopadhyay
et al. 2021
)
.

Still, apart from the large depth, which in this work we explain by incorporating excess radio background, the absorption profile measured by EDGES also has peculiarly sharp bounds, at
z\sim 20
and
z\sim 15
, and a surprisingly flat bottom
(
Bowman et al. 2018
)
. At the minimum, these unusual features in the shape of the signal seem to require that a cosmic Ly
\alpha
background must get established within 25 Myr from
z\sim 21
to 19 and that the cosmic gas content be heated above the background temperature within 50 Myr from
z\sim 16
to 14. This aspect of the EDGES measurement has received relatively less attention in the literature. For example,
Kaurov et al. 2018
argue that the rapid Ly
\alpha
production required by the falling edge of the signal suggests star formation was dominated by rapidly growing rare and massive haloes.
Vipp
et al. 2021
claimed that such a falling feature could be explained by warm dark matter models and put upper and lower bounds on the mass of their particles. Population III motivated models by
Mebane
et al. 2020
naturally achieved a reasonably flat trough, though the rise out of absorption was not too rapid. In this work we aim to interpret the overall shape of the EDGES signal, capturing not just its timing, width or depth but also the sharp drop and rise.

It is possible that the signal measured by EDGES is not of a cosmological origin. Indeed,
Hills
et al. 2018
argued against a cosmological origin of this signal by pointing out that the EDGES inference results in unphysical properties for the ionosphere and/or unexplained structure in the foreground spectrum. The inferred absorption profile is also sensitive to the order of the polynomial model used for the foregrounds, without a means to choose the optimum order. More recently,
Singh et al. 2022
used observations made with the SARAS 3 (Shaped Antenna measurement of the background RAdio Spectrum) experiment to rule out an astrophysical origin of the EDGES measurement at 93.5 per cent confidence. Still, in spite of these new developments, an investigation into the shape of the absorption profile measured by EDGES continues to be of interest. For instance, the new findings by
Singh et al. 2022
seem to remain in need of corroboration. First, the frequency band of SARAS 3 covers only part of the the EDGES absorption profile, with the rising part of the signal noticeably excluded. This makes it likely that any signal consistent with the EDGES measurement gets partly or wholly fitted out by the sixth-order polynomial foreground model used in the SARAS 3 analysis. [Although it is important to note that
Singh et al. 2022
have argued against this possibility by showing that even with a partial coverage of 55–85 MHz, the EDGES signal should be detected by SARAS 3 against a sixth-order polynomial at more than two-sigma confidence.] Second, the choice of a sixth-order polynomial foreground model by SARAS 3 is as problematic as the use of the fifth-order linearised foreground model in the EDGES analysis by
Bowman et al. 2018
. As
Hills
et al. 2018
pointed out, it is surprising that such higher-order models are necessary to describe the foreground for which third-order polynomials are usually sufficient
(
Bernardi
et al. 2015
)
. Third, it is remarkable that with a fifth-order polynomial foreground, both SARAS 3 and EDGES report residuals that are roughly identical in the rms amplitude. Until a reliable explanation is found for this residual, the possibility of its astrophysical origin remains open. It is also interesting to note that
Bowman et al. 2018
found the shape of the inferred absorption signal in the EDGES data to be robust against different hardware modifications, data processing pipelines, and calibration techniques
(
Mahesh et al. 2021
)
. Newer high-band measurements by the EDGES collaboration also seem to confirm this
(
Monsalve et al. 2019
)
. Further, it is important to note that more experiments are currently being set up to verify the EDGES and SARAS 3 observation. These include Large Aperture Experiment to Detect the Dark Ages
(
Bernardi
et al. 2015
;
Bernardi
et al. 2016
;
Price et al. 2018
, LEDA,)
, Probing Radio Intensity at high-Z from Marion
(
Philip et al. 2019
, PRIzM,)
, Radio Experiment for the Analysis of Cosmic Hydrogen
(
de Lera Acedo 2019
, REACH,)
, Sonda Cosmológica de las Islas para la Detección de Hidrógeno Neutro
(
Voytek et al. 2014
, SCI-HI,)
and the Cosmic Twilight Polarimeter
(
Nhan
et al. 2017
;
Nhan et al. 2019
, CTP,)
. If only conventional seven-parameter models are used for the cosmological signal in the analysis of these experiments, the analysis of data from these upcoming experiments runs the risk of missing cosmological information. Finally, the successful launch of
James Webb Space Telescope
(
JWST
) has opened a window of opportunity for a rest-frame UV detection of sources of radiation at cosmic dawn
(
Cowley
et al. 2017
;
Williams
et al. 2018
;
Kauffmann
et al. 2020
;
Mirocha &
Furlanetto 2019
)
. A quantification of the implications of 21-cm experiments for
JWST
surveys is therefore timely.

The 21-cm absorption profile

The 21-cm signal is a differential brightness temperature measured at low frequencies against the radio background. This can be written as
(
Furlanetto
et al. 2006
;
Pritchard &
Loeb 2012
;
Barkana 2016
;
Mesinger 2019
;
Liu & Shaw 2020
)

\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}=\frac{T_{\mathrm{s}}-T_{\mathrm{r}}}{1+z}\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tau_{21}}\right)\,,

(1)

where
\tau_{21}
is the optical depth for the 21-cm photon
(
Madau
et al. 1997
)
,
T_{\mathrm{s}}
is the hydrogen spin temperature,
T_{\mathrm{r}}
is the radio background temperature. For small 21-cm optical depth Equation (
1
) becomes

\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}=27\bar{x}_{\text{H\,{i}}}\left(\frac{1-Y_{\mathrm{p}}}{0.76}\right)\left(\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}}{0.023}\right)\sqrt{\frac{0.15}{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}h^{2}}\frac{1+z}{10}}\left(1-\frac{T_{\mathrm{r}}}{T_{\mathrm{s}}}\right)$\mathrm{mK}$\,,

(2)

where
x_{\text{H\,{i}}}\equiv n_{\text{H\,{i}}}/n_{\text{H}}
is the ratio of number densities of neutral hydrogen (
H
i
) and total hydrogen (H). The bar on
x_{\text{H\,{i}}}
represents a global average over the cosmic volume. At the high redshifts that correspond to the cosmic dawn,
H(z)=H_{0}\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}(1+z)^{3}}
, where
H_{0}
is the Hubble’s constant measured today. The signal is seen in absorption if
\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}<0
and in emission if
\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}>0
. The temperature of the radio background is
(
Feng &
Holder 2018
;
Fialkov &
Barkana 2019
)

T_{\mathrm{r}}(z)=2.725(1+z)\left[1+0.169\zeta_{\mathrm{ERB}}(1+z)^{2.6}\right]\,,

(3)

where we have assumed a power-law ERB over the CMB with
\zeta_{\mathrm{ERB}}=0.03
. Our ERB model is based on ARCADE2/LWA1 observations
(
Fixsen
et al. 2011
;
Dowell &
Taylor 2018
)
. The value of
\zeta_{\mathrm{ERB}}
quoted is required to explain the depth seen in EDGES observation
(
Mittal
et al. 2022
, hereafter
73
)
.

This problem persists even when we disregard the ‘non-standard’ ERB. In the absence of an ERB, the model is no longer able to achieve the observed amplitude, but the shape and the central redshift would remain relatively similar to what is seen in Figure

3
Direct consequences of a flat-bottomed 21-cm profile

Thanks to the simplicity of Equations (
1
) and (
4
), the most direct implication of the flat-bottomed shape of the 21-cm absorption profile is a stringent requirement on the evolution of the Ly
\alpha
coupling and the gas temperature. A non-trivial evolution in one or both of these quantities is necessary if we are to improve the model fit shown in Figure
1
. This non-standard evolution in
x_{\alpha}
and
T_{\mathrm{k}}
can in turn arise due to modified evolution of any of the several underlying processes, such as star formation, or Ly
\alpha
and X-ray emission. But before we investigate the implications for these more fundamental quantities, it is instructive to understand the primary implications for
x_{\alpha}
and
T_{\mathrm{k}}
.

3.1
Ly
\alpha
emission

To understand the requirements for the Ly
\alpha
emission, starting from the best-fitting fiducial model described above, we can give a time dependence to Ly
\alpha
specific intensity, keeping everything else fixed, to try and reproduce the observed profile. Let the fiducial Ly
\alpha
background be
J_{\alpha}(z)
and the required version be
J_{\alpha}^{\prime}(z)
. We derive
J_{\alpha}^{\prime}(z)
as follows: the observed signal
\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}^{\mathrm{obs}}
corresponds to the spin temperature

{T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{s}}}^{-1}=T_{\mathrm{r}}^{-1}\left[1-$5.29\text{\times}{10}^{-3}\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}$\frac{\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}^{\mathrm{obs}}}{h\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}(1-Y_{\mathrm{p}})\bar{x}_{\text{H\,{i}}}}\sqrt{\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{1+z}}\right]\,,

(6)

which is obtained by inverting Equation (
2
). Next, given Equation (
4
), we can obtain the required Ly
\alpha
coupling as

x_{\alpha}^{\prime}=\frac{T_{\mathrm{r}}^{-1}-{T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{s}}}^{-1}}{{T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{s}}}^{-1}-T_{\mathrm{k}}^{-1}}\,.

(7)

Since the Ly
\alpha
coupling is directly proportional to the Ly
\alpha
specific intensity we can write the required Ly
\alpha
specific intensity as

J^{\prime}_{\alpha}=\frac{x_{\alpha}^{\prime}}{x_{\alpha}}J_{\alpha}\,.

(8)

Although theoretically it is easy to calculate
J^{\prime}_{\alpha}
by the above method, we must investigate if it is physically possible to have such a background. Stated more concretely, how should the Ly
\alpha
emissivity be modified to get
J^{\prime}_{\alpha}
? We start by expressing the specific intensity in terms of emissivity as

\bar{J}_{\alpha}(z)=\frac{c}{4\pi}(1+z)^{2}\sum_{n=2}^{23}P_{n}\int_{z}^{z^{\mathrm{max}}_{n}}\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(z^{\prime})\frac{\epsilon_{\alpha}(E_{n}^{\prime},z^{\prime})}{H(z^{\prime})}\,\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}\,,

(9)

where
c
is the speed of light and
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
serves the purpose of providing
z
dependence to the normalization parameter
f_{\alpha}
, so that
f_{\alpha}\to\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(z)f_{\alpha}
. In the above equation
\epsilon_{\alpha}
is the comoving emissivity defined as the number of photons emitted per unit comoving volume per unit proper time per unit energy and
P_{n}
is the probability with which a photon in the upper Lyman lines will redshift to Ly
\alpha
wavelength.

Our task boils down to evaluating the modifying factor
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(z)
. Solving for
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
is not straightforward because the integration limits in Equation (
9
) are functions of
z
. As a result, we can find this function by an iterative procedure in which we update
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(z)
by a factor of
J_{\alpha}^{\prime}/\bar{J}_{\alpha}
after each iteration starting with
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(z)=1
.

We see that outside the absorption feature of EDGES, i.e. for
z>21
and
15>z
, the signal is almost 0, which should imply the same for
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(z)
. At the centre of the profile,
z\sim 17
, our current fitting already has the same depth as in the EDGES signal, which means we must have
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(z=17)\sim 1
. At the high-redshift bound of the profile, from
z\sim 18
to 20,
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
should be more than 1 and decrease to 0 towards higher redshifts. At the low-redshift bound of the profile, there is only a small amount of improvement that can be derived from the Ly
\alpha
emissivity. This involves increasing
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
until the Ly
\alpha
coupling has saturated. Once the Ly
\alpha
coupling has saturated,
T_{\mathrm{s}}=T_{\mathrm{k}}
, subsequent improvement can only be obtained by modifying the
T_{\mathrm{k}}
evolution.

The modifying factor
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(z)
obtained using the above iterative procedure is shown in Figure
2
(top left panel). It has the shape we anticipated above. The black dotted line,
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}=1
, represents the fiducial model. The top right panel in Figure
2
shows the corresponding specific intensity (blue curve), in comparison with the fiducial model (black dotted curve). Incorporating the resultant specific intensity into our model leads to a significantly improved fit to the EDGES measurement, as shown in the right panel of Figure
3
(dashed pink curve). The rms value of the residuals is
49\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}
, a dramatic improvement over the fiducial model.

In spite of this improvement, however, the model continues to perform poorly at low redshifts (
z\lesssim 15.5
). The fit at
z\lesssim 15.5
in the dashed pink line can only be improved by increasing the gas temperature, which in our model can be done by enhancing the X-ray heating rate. For this purpose we introduce the function
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}
, analogous to
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
, so that
f_{\mathrm{X}}\to\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}(z)f_{\mathrm{X}}
. Setting

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}(z)=1+\mathrm{e}^{-4(z-14.75)^{2}}\,,

(10)

improves the model to the solid blue line as seen in the right panel of Figure
3
. The evolution of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}
is shown in the bottom left panel of Figure
2
, and the corresponding X-ray intensity is shown in the bottom right panel of this figure. The model corresponding to Figure
2
has an rms value of the residuals
26\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}
, which is a dramatic improvement over the fiducial model. This model, which we label ‘M1’, represents the direct consequence of the flat-bottomed shape of the 21-cm absorption profile for the Ly
\alpha
coupling, with minimal modification to the X-ray heating relative to the standard model. In the next section, we will examine if this requirement on the Ly
\alpha
coupling is plausible.

It is worth stressing that in this model, the flat bottom of the absorption profile does not arise from a fine tuning of heating and cooling processes, as one might first guess. Instead, as we see in the left panel of Figure
3
, the flatness arises out of a finely tuned non-standard evolution of the Ly
\alpha
coupling.

3.2
X-ray emission

The above scenario with a maximum modification in Ly
\alpha
emissivity supplemented by a small change to X-ray emission for
z\lesssim 14
improves our model considerably. Here we consider an alternative scenario where we keep the modification to Ly
\alpha
background to a minimum, precisely for
z\gtrsim 20
, but consider significant evolution of X-ray emission. The evolutionary factor
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(z)
is now a monotonically increasing function for
z\gtrsim 20
(see upper left corner of Figure
4
) which partly takes care of the sharp drop in the 21-cm signal. We design an X-ray background for
z\lesssim 20
, which gives a flat bottom and a sharp rise in the 21-cm signal. We write the X-ray specific intensity in terms of X-ray emissivity modified by dimensionless function of
z
,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}
as

\bar{J}_{\mathrm{X}}(E,z)=\frac{c(1+z)^{2}}{4\pi}\int_{z}^{z_{\star}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}(z^{\prime})\frac{\epsilon_{\mathrm{X}}(E^{\prime},z^{\prime})}{H(z^{\prime})}\mathrm{e}^{-\tau_{\mathrm{X}}(E,z,z^{\prime})}\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}\,,

(11)

where
z_{\star}\sim 60
is the redshift when the star formation starts
(
Naoz & Barkana 2008
)
, and
\epsilon_{\mathrm{X}}
and
\tau_{\mathrm{X}}
are the X-ray emissivity and optical depth for X-ray photons, respectively. To find
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}(z)
we follow an iterative procedure as before: we update
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}(z)
by a factor of
J_{\mathrm{X}}^{\prime}/\bar{J}_{\mathrm{X}}
after each iteration starting with
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}(z)=1
. Note that we should in principle choose
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}
to be a function of both energy and redshift. However, we find that it suffices to keep
E
fixed at which
J_{\mathrm{X}}
peaks, i.e.,
E\sim$0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{keV}$
.

We first find the required form of X-ray specific intensity,
J_{\mathrm{X}}^{\prime}
, which modifies the gas temperature evolution to result in a rapid increasing 21-cm brightness that is consistent with the EDGES measurement. We can estimate this from Equation (
5
). Since
q_{\mathrm{X}}\sim J_{\mathrm{X}}
, we get

J_{\mathrm{X}}^{\prime}=\frac{(1+z)\mathrm{d}T_{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime}/\mathrm{d}z-2T_{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime}}{(1+z)\mathrm{d}T_{\mathrm{k}}/\mathrm{d}z-2T_{\mathrm{k}}}J_{\mathrm{X}}\,,

(12)

where
T_{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime}
is the required gas temperature without altering the Ly
\alpha
coupling, which can be computed from

{T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{k}}}^{-1}={T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{s}}}^{-1}-\frac{T_{\mathrm{r}}^{-1}-{T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{s}}}^{-1}}{x_{\alpha}}\,,

(13)

where
T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{s}}
is the required spin temperature given in Equation (
6
). The quantity
J_{\mathrm{X}}^{\prime}
so obtained may or may not be feasible. This assessment can be made by asking if there exists a function
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}
such that it gives
\bar{J}_{\mathrm{X}}=J_{\mathrm{X}}^{\prime}
, where
\bar{J}_{\mathrm{X}}
is defined in Equation (
11
). Our iterative procedure answers this question.

Figure
4
shows the resultant modification factors for the Ly
\alpha
and X-ray emissivities, alongside the corresponding specific intensities. Relative to Figure
2
,
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
now has small values, while
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}
has a relatively stronger evolution. The consequent thermal evolution and the 21-cm signal are shown in Figure
5
. Similar to model M1, this model too is a significant improvement over the fiducial model, with a residual rms value of
30\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}
. This scenario represents the direct empirical implication of the EDGES measurement for the X-ray heating, with minimal modification to the fiducial Ly
\alpha
coupling. We term this model ‘M2’. Both models, M1 and M2, are much better at reproducing the observed shape of the 21-cm absorption profile. Unlike M1, however, model M2 does invoke a balance between heating and cooling to explain the flat bottom. This is achieved by means of a non-standard evolution of the X-ray heating rate. We will discuss the plausibility of this scenario in the next section.

It can be noticed that models M1 and M2 represent two extreme scenarios. M1 allows the Ly
\alpha
physics to deviate away from the fiducial picture but admits only a minimal modification to the X-ray model. Conversely, M2 allows the X-ray model to freely deviate away from the fiducial model but restricts modifications to the Ly
\alpha
physics to a minimum. One could possibly investigate the degeneracies between these two models, thereby constructing a family of models with flat-bottomed absorption profiles, but M1 and M2 already allow us to study the plausibility of an EDGES-like flat-bottomed absorption profile and outline its implications for high-redshift galaxies.

4
Astrophysical plausibility

The factors
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
or
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}
derived in previous sections are purely empirical. We now discuss if the values of these factors required by the EDGES measurement are reasonable.

The non-trivial evolution of the Ly
\alpha
emissivity seen in Figure
2
can be obtained either by a similarly non-trivial evolution in the cosmic SFRD or a change in the SED of the sources of UV photons. We will discuss modifications to the SFRD below. For now, it is worth asking if a standard SFRD evolution model could possibly result in the Ly
\alpha
emissivity required in our models M1 and M2. Previous literature worked with a fixed number of Lyman series photons per baryon at all epochs
(
Barkana & Loeb 2005
)
.
The cumulative Lyman series photons per baryon from newer population synthesis models such as
bpass

(
Eldridge et al. 2017
;
Stanway &
Eldridge 2018
, Binary Population and Stellar Synthesis;)
give somewhat higher values. For example, consider a stellar population formed in an instantaneous starburst, metallicity
Z=10^{-3}
stars and a
Kroupa
et al. 1993
IMF (a broken power law with slope
-1.3
for masses
0.1
–
0.5\,\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
and
-2.35
for masses
0.5
–
300\,\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
). This gives
\sim 2\times 10^{4}
Lyman series photons per baryon implying
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\approx 2.06
.

Recently,
Gessey-Jones et al. 2022
showed that Pop III stars could have much brighter SEDs. A 2–
180\,\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
power-law Pop III IMF with slope
-0.5
has
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\sim 6
. As a comparison, our extreme value for
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
is
\sim 8
at about
z=16
, as we see in Figure
2
. It therefore appears that conventional Pop II IMFs are unlikely to be able to produce the level of Ly
\alpha
emissivity demanded by the 21-cm signal unless, as we discuss below, the SFRD is boosted significantly. Pop III IMFs, on the other hand, potentially can produce the required levels of Ly
\alpha
emission
(
Schauer
et al. 2019
, see also)
. For instance, the burst feature in
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
at
z=16
could be the result of the peak in SED due to Pop III stars of mass
\sim 5\,\mathrm{M}_{\odot}

(
Gessey-Jones et al. 2022
)
.

Possible sources of X-rays include X-ray binaries (XRBs)
(
Power
et al. 2013
;
Fragos et al. 2013b
)
, inverse Compton scattering in supernova remnants
(
Oh 2001
)
, mini-quasars
(
Madau
et al. 2004
)
and galactic winds
(
Meiksin et al. 2017
)
. Evidence suggests that quasars dominate at low redshifts while XRBs are dominant at high redshifts
(z\gtrsim 5)

(
Fabbiano 2006
;
Brorby
et al. 2016
;
Lehmer
et al. 2016
)
. Among the two types of XRBs, high-mass XRBs are dominant over low-mass XRBs for
z\gtrsim 2.5

(
Fragos
et al. 2013a
)
.

The X-ray SED
\phi_{\mathrm{X}}
(defined in units of number per unit energy per stellar baryon, which for us would be such that
\phi_{\mathrm{X}}\propto E^{-5/2}
) is normalized by specifying
C_{\mathrm{X}}=\int E\phi_{\mathrm{X}}\,\mathrm{d}E

(
Mesinger
et al. 2011
;
Mesinger
et al. 2013
)
. The quantity
C_{\mathrm{X}}\equiv L_{\mathrm{X}}/\mathrm{SFR}
is an observable that can be measured from the relationship between the X-ray luminosity
L_{\mathrm{X}}
and the star formation rate (SFR). Measurements from nearby star-forming galaxies suggest a linear relationship, i.e., a constant
C_{\mathrm{X}}

(
Grimm
et al. 2003
;
Gilfanov
et al. 2004
)
. In our previous work
(
73
)
we adopted the following
L_{\mathrm{X}}
–SFR relation for photon energies 0.5–
8\text{\,}\mathrm{keV}

C_{\mathrm{X,fid}}=\frac{L_{\mathrm{X}}}{\mathrm{SFR}}\approx$2.61\text{\times}{10}^{32}\text{\,}\mathrm{W}$\left(\mathrm{M}_{\odot}\mathrm{yr}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\,,

(14)

based on observation of HMXBs in star-forming galaxies out to distances of
\lesssim$40\text{\,}\mathrm{Mpc}$

(
Mineo
et al. 2012
)
. Observations of star-forming galaxies for
z\lesssim 1.3

(
Mineo et al. 2013
)
also suggest a scaling relation consistent with Equation (
14
). In
73
and here, we extend the relation in Equation (
14
) for photon energies 0.2–
30\text{\,}\mathrm{keV}

(
Mirocha &
Furlanetto 2019
)
. The X-ray emissivity (in units of number per unit time per unit energy per unit comoving volume) is then given by

\epsilon_{\mathrm{X}}(E,z)=f_{\mathrm{X}}\phi_{\mathrm{X}}(E)\frac{\dot{\rho}_{\star}(z)}{m_{\mathrm{b}}}\,,

(15)

where
m_{\mathrm{b}}
is the average baryon mass and
\dot{\rho}_{\star}
is the comoving SFRD. The above emissivity then gives us the specific intensity as in Equation (
11
).

Our fiducial value of
f_{\mathrm{X}}
, as mentioned in Table
1
, is
2.379
. In the language of Section
3.2
, this value of
f_{\mathrm{X}}
corresponds to
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}=1
, and Equation (
14
) corresponds to
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}=0.42
. At redshifts
z\gtrsim 1.3
, because of lack of direct observations,
f_{\mathrm{X}}
remains largely unconstrained. However, evidence suggests that it is higher at higher redshifts
(
Mirabel et al. 2011
)
. An evolving
f_{\mathrm{X}}
(power law in
1+z
) was constrained by
Dijkstra
et al. 2012
based on the unresolved cosmic X-ray background
(
Lehmer
et al. 2012
)
assuming that this background is produced by HMXBs. But this was applicable to much lower redshifts
(z\lesssim 2)
compared to the redshifts of our interests. At higher redshifts
(z\gtrsim 5)
the unresolved cosmic X-ray background puts only weak constraints on
f_{\mathrm{X}}

(
Fialkov
et al. 2016
, see also)
.

Not much is known about
f_{\mathrm{X}}
for
z>14
. The only information for these redshifts we have is by
Fragos et al. 2013b
which is based on population synthesis models. They gave fitting functions for HMXB luminosity density for
0\leqslant z\leqslant 20
. Converting this to
C_{\mathrm{X}}
we find that
C_{\mathrm{X}}
peaks at
z\approx 1.7
, decreases to about 0.025 at
z\approx 19
before increasing slightly to
\sim 0.03
for
z\sim 20
. Translating this to our language,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}(z)
varies from about 0.7 at
z<2
to
0.013
at
z\sim 20
, far smaller than what is required by the EDGES measurement (in either of our models M1 and M2, Figures
2
and
4
). It thus appears that obtaining rapidly increasing X-ray background of the sort required by data is not plausible in conservative models. One way of increasing
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}
is to again invoke Pop III HMXBs, which can increase
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}
to values as large as
10^{3}

(
Mirocha et al. 2018
)
.

It is worth pointing out that the low-redshift spikes in
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}
in Figures
2
and
4
are connected to the rapid disappearance of 21-cm signal around
z\sim 15
. The onset of reionization can also potentially explain this rapid disappearance. Although reionization at such high redshifts will violate CMB constraints, such a scenario could potentially lead to a picture in which
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}
level off or continue rising at
z\lesssim 15
.

5
Implications

5.1
Implications for high-redshift star formation

We see above that the EDGES measurement demands Ly
\alpha
and X-ray production mechanisms that are at the limits of what is reasonable. An alternative therefore is that the cosmic star formation evolution is modified relative to our fiducial model.

In
73
we worked with a constant value of SFE (defined as the fraction of gas in the dark matter haloes which gets converted into stars) of
f_{\star}=0.1
. However, in general
f_{\star}
depends on feedback mechanisms, metallicity of the collapsing gas, and the mass of the star forming haloes
(
Cohen
et al. 2017
;
Monsalve et al. 2019
)
. In order to obtain a flat-bottomed absorption profile, we follow a similar method as before and change
f_{\star}
so that it now has a time dependence:
f_{\star}\to\mathcal{M}_{\star}(z)f_{\star}
. (We continue to hold the SFE independent of the halo mass. Also, the values of other model parameters, including
f_{\alpha}
and
f_{\mathrm{X}}
are held fixed to the fiducial values listed in Table
1
.) Changing
f_{\star}
inevitably affects both Ly
\alpha
as well as X-ray backgrounds. As a result, the calculation of
\mathcal{M}_{\star}
is not as straightforward as that of
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{X}}
in Models M1 and M2. We use hit-and-trial method for this starting with
\mathcal{M}_{\star}=\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
. The result is shown in Figure
6
.

We see that the 21-cm profile requires the SFE to drop rapidly to zero at redshift higher than
\sim 20
. This is to prevent a Ly
\alpha
background from getting established too early. A rapid enhancement followed by a quick drop in
f_{\star}
relative to the fiducial model is then required at redshift of
z\sim 20
–
19
to reproduce the sharp falling edge of the profile. Finally, the model requires a significant enhancement in
f_{\star}
once more at
z\lesssim 17
to obtain the required X-ray heating. The resulting signal is a good fit, as shown in Figure
7
. This model has a residual rms value of
47\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}
.

This is a considerable improvement over the fiducial model. But this model is still significantly worse than the models M1 and M2 in which the properties of the Ly
\alpha
and X-ray sources were varied. Thus it is hard to fit the EDGES data for
17>z>15
by only modifying the SFRD. This can be understood to be because X-ray and Ly
\alpha
have contrasting effects on the 21-cm signal. Increasing SFRD for
17>z>15
to get more Ly
\alpha
and hence a better fitting (by lower the signal) also increases X-ray heating at the same time (in turn raising the signal). The effects are reversed if we consider lowering the SFRD. Thus, if we leave the intrinsic properties of Ly
\alpha
and X-ray sources untouched and modify only the SFRD as shown in Figure
6
then the signal shown in Figure
7
is the best that can be achieved.

It is also possible to get an equivalent model by changing the minimum mass of star-forming haloes. This minimum mass in our fiducial model corresponds to a virial temperature of
e4.25\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
, which is close to the limit set by atomic hydrogen cooling. The required reduction in the SFRD at
z\gtrsim 18
necessitates a very high value of the minimum halo mass. Conversely, the large SFRD required at the lower redshift end of the data can be obtained by reducing the minimum mass by a factor of
\lesssim 2
.

5.2
Predictions for galaxy surveys

Given the required SFRD evolution, and with our assumption that the SFE is independent of the halo mass, we can make predictions for the ultraviolet luminosity functions (UV LFs) of high-redshift galaxies. As a measure of the comoving number density of galaxies for a given luminosity, the UV LF has a one-to-one correspondence with the halo mass function. The LF is traditionally expressed as the comoving number density per unit absolute magnitude rather than number density per unit luminosity. We construct the intrinsic LF as

\mathrm{LF}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi(M_{\lambda})}{\mathrm{d}M_{\lambda}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}n(M_{\mathrm{h}})}{\mathrm{d}M_{\mathrm{h}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}M_{\mathrm{h}}}{\mathrm{d}L_{\lambda}}\frac{\mathrm{d}L_{\lambda}}{\mathrm{d}M_{\lambda}}\,,

(16)

where
\mathrm{d}n
is comoving number density of haloes with mass between
M_{\mathrm{h}}
and
M_{\mathrm{h}}+\mathrm{d}M_{\mathrm{h}}
,
L_{\lambda}
is the specific luminosity at a wavelength
\lambda
, and
M_{\lambda}
is the corresponding absolute magnitude. We consider the UV LF for
\lambda=$1500\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolAngstrom}$
.

Figure 8:
Left panel: galaxy UV luminosity function at
z\sim 17
for
M_{\mathrm{UV}}<-13
. Middle panel: number density of
z=17
sources on the sky. Right panel: predictions for an ultra-deep
JWST
survey with
m_{\mathrm{UV,lim}}=32
. In all panels, dotted curves show our fiducial models; solid blue curves show model M3.

The UV luminosity of a given star-forming halo can be assumed to track the instantaneous SFR
(
Kennicutt 1998
;
Madau &
Dickinson 2014
, e.g.,)
so that

L_{\mathrm{UV}}=\dot{M}_{\star}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{UV}}\,,

(17)

where
\dot{M}_{\star}
is the SFR and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{UV}}=$8.695\text{\times}{10}^{20}\text{\,}\mathrm{W}\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}$\big(\mathrm{M}_{\odot}\mathrm{yr}^{-1}\big)^{-1}
is the specific luminosity per unit SFR
(
Mirocha
et al. 2016
)
. We follow
Sun &
Furlanetto 2016
to model the SFR as

\dot{M}_{\star}=f_{\star}\dot{M}_{\mathrm{b,acc}}\,,

(18)

where
\dot{M}_{\mathrm{b,acc}}
is accretion of baryons onto dark matter haloes. A halo’s baryonic mass accretion rate can be described by the empirical relation
(
McBride
et al. 2009
;
Sun &
Furlanetto 2016
;
Mirocha
et al. 2016
)

\dot{M}_{\mathrm{b,acc}}=\dot{M}_{0}\left(\frac{M_{\mathrm{h}}}{10^{10}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}}\right)^{a}\left(\frac{1+z}{7}\right)^{b}\,,

(19)

where
\dot{M}_{0}=3\,\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
yr
-1
,
a=1.127
and
b=2.5
. Combining equations (
17
), (
18
) and (
19
) we get

L_{\mathrm{UV}}=f_{\star}\dot{M}_{0}\left(\frac{M_{\mathrm{h}}}{10^{10}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}}\right)^{a}\left(\frac{1+z}{7}\right)^{b}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{UV}}\,.

(20)

The corresponding apparent magnitude (in the AB system) is given by
(
Oke 1974
)

m_{\mathrm{UV}}=-2.5\log_{10}\left[\frac{1}{4\pi}\left(\frac{L_{\mathrm{UV}}}{$1\text{\,}\mathrm{W}\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}$}\right)\left(\frac{d_{\mathrm{L}}}{$1\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$}\right)^{-2}\right]-56.1\,,

(21)

where

d_{\mathrm{L}}(z)=c(1+z)\int_{0}^{z}\frac{\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}}{H(z^{\prime})}\,,

(22)

is the luminosity distance. The corresponding absolute magnitude is

M_{\mathrm{UV}}=-2.5\log_{10}\left[\frac{1}{4\pi}\left(\frac{L_{\mathrm{UV}}}{$1\text{\,}\mathrm{W}\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}$}\right)\left(\frac{d_{10}}{$1\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$}\right)^{-2}\right]-56.1\,,

(23)

where
d_{10}=$10\text{\,}\mathrm{pc}$
.

If we assume that
f_{\star}
is independent of
M_{\mathrm{h}}
, then using equations (
20
) and (
23
) we arrive at

\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln M_{\mathrm{h}}}{\mathrm{d}M_{\mathrm{UV}}}=-\frac{2\ln 10}{5a}\,.

(24)

Inserting the above into Equation (
16
) we get

\mathrm{LF}=-\frac{2\ln 10}{5a}\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}\ln M_{\mathrm{h}}}\,,

(25)

where we will use the
Press &
Schechter 1974
form for the mass function
\mathrm{d}n/\mathrm{d}\ln M_{\mathrm{h}}
. Because the above relation gives us the LF as a function of halo mass we again use equations (
20
) and (
23
) to eliminate
M_{\mathrm{h}}
to get LF as a function of
M_{\mathrm{UV}}
. As a consistency check, we used the above formalism to verify that our model results in a UV LF consistent with observations from
Bouwens
et al. 2015
.

For high-redshift galaxy surveys, such as those planned with the
JWST
or
Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope
(
NGRST
), a useful quantity that can be derived from the LF is the galaxy count brighter than a certain limiting luminosity,
L_{\mathrm{UV,lim}}
, per unit redshift per unit angular area on the sky. This can be defined as
(
Mo
et al. 2010
)

\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}z}=\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}z}\int_{L_{\mathrm{UV,lim}}}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathrm{d}L_{\mathrm{UV}}}\mathrm{d}L_{\mathrm{UV}}\,,

(26)

where the differential comoving volume element per square radian is given by comoving area on the sky per square radian times the comoving differential length, i.e.,
(
Hogg 1999
)

\mathrm{d}V=\left[(1+z)^{2}\,d^{2}_{\mathrm{A}}\right]\left[(1+z)\,c\,\mathrm{d}t\right]=\frac{c}{H(z)}\left(\frac{d_{\mathrm{L}}}{1+z}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}z\,,

(27)

where
d_{\mathrm{A}}
is the angular diameter distance. Equation (
26
) simplifies to

\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}z}(M_{\mathrm{h,lim}},z)=\frac{c}{H(z)}\left(\frac{d_{\mathrm{L}}}{1+z}\right)^{2}\int_{M_{\mathrm{h,lim}}}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}M_{\mathrm{h}}}\mathrm{d}M_{\mathrm{h}}\,,

(28)

where
M_{\mathrm{h,lim}}
is the limiting halo mass which gives luminosity
L_{\mathrm{UV,lim}}
. Finally, to get galaxy count brighter than a limiting luminosity per unit angular area on the sky we evaluate
N(M_{\mathrm{h,lim}})=\int(\mathrm{d}N/\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}z
.

Conclusion

The cosmological 21-cm absorption signal measured by the EDGES collaboration has a distinctly different shape than predictions from standard models. In this paper, we have developed models that provide far superior fits to the EDGES measurement than standard models. These new models either invoke exotic stellar populations or modulate the cosmic SFRD evolution in order to establish a cosmic Ly
\alpha
background within 25 Myr from
z\sim 21
to 19, and a cosmic X-ray background within 50 Myr from
z\sim 16
to 14.

Scenarios involving exotic stellar populations have the advantage of the cosmic SFRD evolution in these models being relatively standard. Our models M1 and M2 presented in Section
3
are two examples of such models. However, as discussed in Section
4
above, these models seem to require extreme Population III IMFs evolving over redshifts. It appears difficult to obtain the required level of both Ly
\alpha
as well as X-ray emission from models with standard stellar populations.

On the other hand, in scenarios involving modifications to the cosmic SFRD, more conservative assumptions about stellar populations are sufficient. The model M3 presented in Section
5
is an example. This models the cosmic star formation to rapidly drop to zero at redshifts higher than
z\sim 21
. This leads to robust predictions for upcoming deep galaxy surveys using
JWST
or
NGRST
. Combining data from such surveys with global 21-cm measurements should be a rich source of information about galaxy formation as well as radiative background at cosmic dawn.

Our three models represent extremes of their respective categories. Model M1 admits large modifications to the Ly
\alpha
source properties but attempts to keep the properties of X-ray sources to their conventional values. Conversely, model M2 tries to get the maximum contribution from the X-ray side. Model M3 retains standard assumptions for the Ly
\alpha
and X-ray sources and maximises the contribution from SFRD modifications.

There are obvious degeneracies between these models. These degeneracies could perhaps be captured by developing parametric phenomenological models of the radiative sources and the cosmic SFRD evolution. Indeed, without such extensions to the standard models, it would not be possible to fit the EDGES measurement to a satisfactory level at all. This would also prevent inferring constraints on exotic physics models affecting the thermal properties of the universe at cosmic dawn. One way to develop such extensions is to consider families of fitting functions to the Ly
\alpha
and X-ray emissivities discussed in Section
3
. Another possibility is to fold such possibilities in end-to-end forward models of the signal such as those employed by the REACH experiment
(
de Lera Acedo 2019
)
.
