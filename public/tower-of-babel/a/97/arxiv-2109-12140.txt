Vertebrate interval graphs
Rain Jiang, Kai Jiang, Minghui Jiang

Abstract
A vertebrate interval graph is an interval graph in which the maximum size of a set of independent vertices equals the number of maximal cliques. For any fixed v ≥ 1 v\geq 1 , there is a polynomial-time algorithm for deciding whether a vertebrate interval graph admits a vertex partition into two induced subgraphs with claw number at most v v . In particular, when v = 2 v=2 , whether a vertebrate interval graph can be partitioned into two proper interval graphs can be decided in polynomial time.



1
Introduction

In any graph, the maximum size of a set of independent vertices is at most
the number of maximal cliques.
It is natural to consider graphs with the property that these two numbers are equal.
Golumbic
[
7
]
noted that
“we cannot expect to discover much about
the structure” of graphs with this property alone,
and instead defined
trivially perfect graphs

as graphs with the stronger, hereditary property that
the maximum size of a set of independent vertices
equals the number of maximal cliques
in every induced subgraph.

Trivially perfect graphs are also known as comparability graphs of trees,
and as intersection graphs of nested intervals.
An
interval graph
is the intersection graph of a family of open
intervals.
A trivially perfect graph is an interval graph
with an interval representation in which no interval partially overlaps
another interval.

Figure 1:
Left: intervals representing a trivially perfect graph.
Middle: intervals representing a vertebrate interval graph.
Right: intervals representing an invertebrate interval graph.

In this paper, we introduce
vertebrate interval graphs

as a subclass of interval graphs
and a superclass of trivially perfect graphs.
Specifically,
an interval graph is

vertebrate

if the maximum size of a set of independent vertices equals the number of maximal cliques
in the graph,
and is
invertebrate
otherwise.
Here the equality of the two numbers for a vertebrate interval graph is not hereditary:
it holds for the graph as a whole,
but not necessarily for its induced subgraphs.
Refer to Figure
1
for some examples.

The
claw number

\psi(G)
of a graph
G
is the largest number

v\geq 0
such that
G
contains
K_{1,v}
as an induced subgraph
[
2
]
.
In particular, a graph with claw number
0
is an empty graph with no edges.
A graph with claw number at most
1
is a disjoint union of cliques,
also known as a
cluster graph
.
Every cluster graph is clearly also an interval graph,
with claw number at most
1
.
A
proper interval graph
is the intersection graph of a family of open
intervals in which no one properly contains another.
A
unit interval graph
is the intersection graph of a family of open
intervals of the same length.
It is well-known
[
4
]

that an interval graph is a proper interval graph if and only if it is a unit
interval graph, and if and only if it does not contain
K_{1,3}
as an induced subgraph.
Thus a proper / unit interval graph is an interval graph of claw number at most
2
.

A
k
-partition
of a graph refers to a partition of its vertices into
k
subsets,
and the resulting
k
vertex-disjoint induced subgraphs.
For
k\geq 2
and
v\geq 1
,

k
-Partition
(v)
is the problem of
deciding whether a given graph admits a vertex partition
into
k
induced subgraphs with claw number at most
v
.
Certain generic results on vertex-partitioning
[
1
,
6
]

imply that

k
-Partition
(v)
in general graphs
is NP-hard for all
k\geq 2
and
v\geq 1
.

The subchromatic number of a graph
is the smallest number
k
such that
the graph admits a
k
-partition into cluster graphs
[
3
]
.
For any fixed
k
,
whether an interval graph with
n
vertices has subchromatic number at most
k

can be decided in
O(k\cdot n^{2k+1})
time
[
5
, Theorem 5.4]
.
Thus for
k\geq 2
,

k
-Partition
(1)
in interval graphs
admits an algorithm running in
O(k\cdot n^{2k+1})
time.
It is unknown
[
9
]

whether there exist
k\geq 2
and
v\geq 2
such that

k
-Partition
(v)
in interval graphs
admits an polynomial-time algorithm.

In this paper,
we show that vertebrate interval graphs have sufficient “structure” so that

2
-Partition
(v)
can be solved in polynomial time for all
v\geq 1
:

Theorem 1
.

For
v\geq 1
,

2
-Partition
(v)
in vertebrate interval graphs
admits an algorithm running in
2^{O(v^{2})}n^{O(v)}
time.

In particular, for
v=2
,
whether a vertebrate interval graph
admits a vertex partition into two proper interval graphs
can be decided in polynomial time.

2
Preliminaries

Denote by
m(G)
the number of maximal cliques in a graph
G
.
Besides
m(G)
and the claw number
\psi(G)
mentioned earlier,
there are two other common graph parameters.
An
independent set
(respectively, a
clique
)
is a set of pairwise non-adjacent (respectively, adjacent)
vertices in a graph.
The
independence number

\alpha(G)
is the maximum number of vertices in an independent set
in
G
.
The
vertex-clique-partition number

\vartheta(G)
is the minimum number of parts
in a vertex partition of
G
into cliques.

It is easy to see that for any graph
G
,

\psi(G)\leq\alpha(G)\leq\vartheta(G)\leq m(G)
.
If
G
is an interval graph,
then

\alpha(G)=\vartheta(G)

[
8
]
.
By our definition,

\alpha(G)=\vartheta(G)=m(G)

for any vertebrate interval graph
G
.

The equality

\alpha(G)=\vartheta(G)
for every interval graph
G

has a simple constructive proof.
Recall that interval graphs can be recognized in linear time
[
8
]
.
Let
\mathcal{I}
be any family of open intervals whose intersection graph is
G
.
Assume without loss of generality that all endpoints of intervals in
\mathcal{I}
are integers.
We can find an independent set and a vertex clique partition at the same time
by a standard
sweepline algorithm
:

Initialize
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{I}
and
i\leftarrow 1
.
While
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
is not empty,
let
T_{i}=(l_{i},r_{i})
be an interval in
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
whose right endpoint
r_{i}
is
minimum,
let
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{i}
(respectively,
\mathcal{I}_{i}
) be the subfamily of intervals in

\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
(respectively,
\mathcal{I}
) that contain
the subinterval
S_{i}=(r_{i}-1,r_{i})
of
T_{i}
,
then update
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{i}
and
i\leftarrow i+1
.

Let
m
be the maximum round
i
such that
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
is not empty.
Then

S_{i}\subseteq T_{i}\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{i}

for
1\leq i\leq m
.
Note that the
m
intervals
T_{i}
correspond to an independent set in
G
,
and that the
m
subfamilies
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{i}
correspond to a partition of the vertices of
G

into cliques.
So we have
\alpha(G)=\vartheta(G)=m
.
Moreover,
if
G
is a vertebrate interval graph,
then
\alpha(G)=\vartheta(G)=m(G)=m
,
and the
m
subfamilies
\mathcal{I}_{i}

correspond to the
m
maximal cliques in
G
,
which cover all vertices and all edges.

After running the sweepline algorithm on
an arbitrary interval representation
\mathcal{I}
of a vertebrate interval graph
G
,
we can then obtain a possibly more compact interval representation
\mathcal{J}
of
G
,
by converting each interval
I\in\mathcal{I}
to an interval
J\in\mathcal{J}
as follows.
Suppose that
a
and
b
, respectively,
are the smallest and largest integers
i
,
1\leq i\leq m
,
such that
I\in\mathcal{I}_{i}
.
Then
I\in\mathcal{I}_{i}
if and only if
a\leq i\leq b
.
Let
J=(a-1,b)
.

Note that all intervals in
\mathcal{J}
have integer endpoints in
[0,m]
.
Moreover,
for each interval
I\in\mathcal{I}
,
the length of the corresponding interval
J\in\mathcal{J}

is equal to the number of maximal cliques
\mathcal{I}_{i}
such that
I\in\mathcal{I}_{i}
.
In particular,
each interval
T_{i}\in\mathcal{I}
corresponds to a unit interval
T^{\prime}_{i}=(i-1,i)
in
\mathcal{J}
,
and each interval
J\in\mathcal{J}
of length
\ell\geq 2
is the concatenation of
\ell

disjoint unit intervals
T^{\prime}_{i}
in
\mathcal{J}
.
It is easy to verify that
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
represent the same graph
G
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be the subfamily of
m
unit intervals
T^{\prime}_{i}
in
\mathcal{J}
.
We call
\mathcal{J}
a
vertebrate representation
of
G
,
and call
\mathcal{U}
the
backbone
of
\mathcal{J}
.
Because of the unit intervals composing the backbone,
the maximum length of an interval in
\mathcal{J}
is exactly
\psi(G)
.
In summary, we have the following proposition:

Proposition 1
.

Every vertebrate interval graph
G
has an interval representation
\mathcal{J}

with integer endpoints in
[0,m(G)]
and with maximum interval length
\psi(G)
,
which includes the
m(G)
unit intervals
(i-1,i)
for
1\leq i\leq m(G)
.

3
Algorithm for
2
-Partition
(v)
in vertebrate interval graphs

In this section we prove Theorem
1
.
Fix any
v\geq 1
.
We will present a polynomial-time algorithm for
2
-Partition
(v)

based on dynamic programming.

Let
G
be a vertebrate interval graph with
n
vertices.
Obtain a vertebrate representation
\mathcal{J}
of
G

with integer endpoints in
[0,m]
,
where
m=m(G)
is the number of maximal cliques in
G
.
Let
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{J}
be the subfamily of
m
disjoint unit intervals

(i-1,i)
,
1\leq i\leq m
, forming the backbone.

We say that a family of intervals is
good

if it represents an interval graph with claw number at most
v
,
and that a
2
-partition of a family of intervals is
good

if each part is good.
For any two subfamilies
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{S}
of
\mathcal{J}
,
we write

\mathcal{R}\mid\mathcal{S}
if each interval in
\mathcal{R}

intersects at most
v
disjoint intervals in
\mathcal{S}
other than itself.
Then a
2
-partition
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})
of
\mathcal{J}
is good if and only if

\mathcal{P}\mid\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}\mid\mathcal{Q}
.

For any good
2
-partition of
\mathcal{J}
,
if there are two duplicate intervals in different parts,
then we can always relocate them to the same part
while keeping the
2
-partition good.
Thus we can assume without loss of generality
that
\mathcal{J}
includes no duplicates.

3.1
Basic properties of good
2
-partitions

For any subfamily
\mathcal{S}
of
\mathcal{J}
,
denote by
\mathcal{S}_{\leq}
the subfamily of intervals in
\mathcal{S}
of length at most
v
,
and by
\mathcal{S}_{>}
the subfamily of intervals in
\mathcal{S}
of length greater than
v
.
For
0\leq l\leq r\leq m
,
denote by
\mathcal{S}(l,r)
the subfamily of intervals in
\mathcal{S}

that are contained in the interval
(l,r)
.
In particular, when
l=r
, both
(l,r)
and
\mathcal{S}(l,r)
are empty.
We write
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})\succ\mathcal{S}

if
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})
is a
2
-partition of
\mathcal{S}
.
We say that
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})\succ\mathcal{S}
is
simple

if either
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})=(\mathcal{S}_{\leq},\mathcal{S}_{>})
or

(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})=(\mathcal{S}_{>},\mathcal{S}_{\leq})
.

For any two subfamilies
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{S}
of
\mathcal{J}
,
we say that two
2
-partitions

(\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{R}_{2})\succ\mathcal{R}

and

(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})\succ\mathcal{S}

are
consistent

if
(\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{R}_{2}\cup\mathcal{S}_{2})\succ\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{S}
,
and write

(\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{R}_{2})\simeq(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})
.
For
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
,
and for any
2
-partition
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})\succ\mathcal{S}
,
the
2
-partition
(\mathcal{P}\cap\mathcal{R},\mathcal{Q}\cap\mathcal{R})\succ\mathcal{R}

is called the
derived

2
-partition of
\mathcal{R}
.
Clearly, if
(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})
is derived from
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})
,
then
(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})\simeq(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})
.

For
0\leq l<r\leq s\leq m
,
the interval
(l,r)
is a
vertebrate range

for some
2
-partition of
\mathcal{J}(0,s)

if the
r-l
unit intervals in
\mathcal{U}(l,r)

are all in the same part.
A
2
-partition of
\mathcal{J}(0,s)
is

if
for every maximal vertebrate range
(l,r)
,
the derived
2
-partition of
\mathcal{J}(l,r)

Lemma 1
.

\mathcal{J}
has a good
2
-partition if and only if
it has a good basic
2
-partition.

Proof.

Given any good
2
-partition of
\mathcal{J}
that is not basic,
we can transform it into a good basic
2
-partition as follows.
Let
(l,r)
be a maximal vertebrate range,
such that the
r-l
unit intervals in
\mathcal{J}(l,r)
are in the same part.
Then the intervals of length greater than
v
in
\mathcal{J}(l,r)
,
if any, must be in the other part.
For each interval
I
of length at most
v
in
\mathcal{J}(l,r)
,
we simply move it to the same part as the unit intervals
if it is not already there.
We claim that this move does not introduce any star
K_{1,v+1}
.
First,

I
cannot be the center of any star
K_{1,v+1}
,
since any interval of length at most
v
intersects at most
v
disjoint
intervals in
\mathcal{J}
.
Second, if there is a star
K_{1,v+1}
with
I
as a leaf after the move,
then there would have been a star
K_{1,v+1}
even before the move,
with
I
replaced by some unit interval contained in
I
.
∎

For any subfamily
\mathcal{S}
of
\mathcal{J}
,
and for
0\leq l\leq r\leq m
,
denote by
\alpha(\mathcal{S},l,r)

the maximum size of a subfamily of disjoint intervals in
\mathcal{S}

that intersect the interval
(l,r)
.
In particular, when
l=r
,
(l,r)
is empty,
and hence
\alpha(\mathcal{S},l,r)=0
.
Note that

\alpha(\mathcal{S},l,r)

is not necessarily equal to

\alpha(\mathcal{S}(l,r),l,r)
.

Lemma 2
.

For
0\leq a<s<b\leq m
,
if
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})
is a
2
-partition of
\mathcal{J}
with

(s-1,s)\in\mathcal{P}
and
(s,s+1)\in\mathcal{Q}
,
then
\alpha(\mathcal{P},a,b)=\alpha(\mathcal{P}(0,s),a,s)+\alpha(\mathcal{P}(s,m),s,b)

and
\alpha(\mathcal{Q},a,b)=\alpha(\mathcal{Q}(0,s),a,s)+\alpha(\mathcal{Q}(s,m),s,b)
.

Proof.

Consider any subfamily of disjoint intervals in
\mathcal{J}
that intersect
(a,b)
,
from the same part
\mathcal{P}
or
\mathcal{Q}
.
If this subfamily contains an interval
(c,d)
with
c<s<d
,
then we can replace it by
(s-1,s)
or
(s,s+1)
.
∎

For
0\leq s\leq m
,
a sequence

\boldsymbol{r}=\langle r_{0},r_{1},\ldots,r_{v},r_{v+1},r_{v+2}\rangle

of
v+3
integers is
s
-monotonic
if

-1=r_{v+2}\leq r_{v+1}\leq r_{v}\leq\ldots\leq r_{1}\leq r_{0}=s,

and moreover, either
-1=r_{u+1}=r_{u}
or
r_{u+1}<r_{u}
holds for each
u
,

0\leq u\leq v+1
.
Denote by
\boldsymbol{0}
the unique
0
-monotonic sequence

\langle 0,-1,\ldots,-1\rangle
.

For
0\leq u\leq v+2

and
0\leq s\leq m
,
and for a subfamily
\mathcal{R}
of
\mathcal{J}(0,s)
,
let
i(\mathcal{R},u,s)
be the maximum index

such that

,
or

if such an index

Clearly, we always have

and
i(\mathcal{R},v+2,s)=-1
.
Also,

for

.
For
1\leq u\leq\min\{\alpha(\mathcal{R},0,s),v+1\}
,
we have

and
\alpha(\mathcal{R},i(\mathcal{R},u,s),s)=u
.

For
0\leq s\leq m
,
we say that
an

-monotonic sequence

encodes

a subfamily
\mathcal{R}
of
\mathcal{J}(0,s)

if

r_{u}=i(\mathcal{R},u,s)
for

.
By definition,
every subfamily of
\mathcal{J}(0,s)
is encoded by

If

encodes
\mathcal{R}
,
that is, if

,
then from

we can recover the values of

relative to

\begin{array}[]{ll}\alpha(\mathcal{R},i,s)=u&\textrm{when }r_{u+1}<i\leq r_{u}\textrm{ for }0\leq u\leq v,\\
\alpha(\mathcal{R},i,s)\geq v+1&\textrm{when }r_{v+2}<i\leq r_{v+1}.\end{array}

For
0\leq i\leq s\leq m
,
and for an
s
-monotonic sequence
\boldsymbol{r}
,
denote by

\alpha(\boldsymbol{r},i,s)

the maximum index
u\in[0,v+1]
such that

.
Then we immediately have the following lemma:

Lemma 3
.

For
0\leq s\leq m
,
if an

-monotonic sequence
\boldsymbol{r}
encodes a subfamily
\mathcal{R}
of
\mathcal{J}(0,s)
,
then for

,

.

For
0\leq s\leq m
,
we say that a pair of
s
-monotonic sequences

encodes
a

-partition
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})\succ\mathcal{J}(0,s)

\mathcal{P}

and

.

For
0\leq s\leq m
,
denote by

in

with

.

is a pair of

encoding

(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})

is a pair of

-monotonic sequences

Then

(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{U}(s^{\prime},s)\subseteq\mathcal{J}_{\leq}(s^{\prime},s)=\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{P}

and moreover,

\begin{array}[]{ll}i(\mathcal{P},u,s)=s-u&\textrm{for }1\leq u\leq\min\{s-s^{\prime},v+1\},\\
i(\mathcal{P},u,s)<s^{\prime}&\textrm{for }s-s^{\prime}<u\leq v+1.\end{array}

Note that all intervals in

\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{D}

that intersect
(s^{\prime},s)
are in

\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{D}
.
Thus

\alpha(\mathcal{Q},i,s)=\alpha(\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{D},i,s)

for
s^{\prime}\leq i\leq s
.
Since
\alpha(\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{D},s^{\prime},s)=w^{\prime}
,
it follows that
for
u\leq\min\{w^{\prime},v+1\}
,

i(\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{D},u,s)\geq s^{\prime}
,
and hence

i(\mathcal{Q},u,s)=i(\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{D},u,s)
.
Thus,

\begin{array}[]{ll}i(\mathcal{Q},u,s)=i(\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{D},u,s)&\textrm{for }1\leq u\leq\min\{w^{\prime},v+1\},\\
i(\mathcal{Q},u,s)<s^{\prime}&\textrm{for }w^{\prime}<u\leq v+1.\end{array}

We next elaborate on the two inequalities,

i(\mathcal{P},u,s)<s^{\prime}

for
s-s^{\prime}<u\leq v+1
,
and

i(\mathcal{Q},u,s)<s^{\prime}

for
w^{\prime}<u\leq v+1
,
by considering two cases.

Case 1:

s^{\prime}=0
.
Then we must have

i(\mathcal{P},u,s)=-1
for
s-s^{\prime}<u\leq v+1
,
and
i(\mathcal{Q},u,s)=-1
for
w^{\prime}<u\leq v+1
.
On the other hand,
since

(0,s^{\prime})
is empty,
we have

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}=\emptyset
.
For
s-s^{\prime}<u\leq v+1
,
we have
u-s+s^{\prime}>0
,
and hence
i(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},u-s+s^{\prime},s^{\prime})=-1
.
Also,
for
w^{\prime}<u\leq v+1
,
we have
u-w^{\prime}>0
.
Since
w=\alpha(\mathcal{D},s^{\prime},s)\leq\alpha(\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{D},s^{\prime},s)=w^{\prime}
,
it follows that
u-w>0
,
and hence
i(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime},u-w,s^{\prime})=-1
.
Therefore,

\begin{array}[]{ll}i(\mathcal{P},u,s)=-1=i(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},u-s+s^{\prime},s^{\prime})&\textrm{for }s-s^{\prime}<u\leq v+1,\\
i(\mathcal{Q},u,s)=-1=i(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime},u-w,s^{\prime})&\textrm{for }w^{\prime}<u\leq v+1.\end{array}

Case 2:
s^{\prime}>0
.
Since
(s^{\prime},s)
is the rightmost maximal vertebrate range of

(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})\succ\mathcal{J}(0,s)

with
\mathcal{U}(s^{\prime},s)\subseteq\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
,
we have
(s^{\prime}-1,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Q}
and
(s^{\prime},s^{\prime}+1)\in\mathcal{P}
.
For
0\leq i<s^{\prime}<s\leq m
,
it then follows by Lemma
2
that

\displaystyle\alpha(\mathcal{P},i,s)

\displaystyle=\alpha(\mathcal{P}(0,s^{\prime}),i,s^{\prime})+\alpha(\mathcal{P}(s^{\prime},s),s^{\prime},s)=\alpha(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},i,s^{\prime})+\alpha(\mathcal{C},s^{\prime},s),

\displaystyle\alpha(\mathcal{Q},i,s)

\displaystyle=\alpha(\mathcal{Q}(0,s^{\prime}),i,s^{\prime})+\alpha(\mathcal{Q}(s^{\prime},s),s^{\prime},s)=\alpha(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime},i,s^{\prime})+\alpha(\mathcal{D},s^{\prime},s).

Since

\alpha(\mathcal{C},s^{\prime},s)=s-s^{\prime}

and

\alpha(\mathcal{D},s^{\prime},s)=w
,
we have

\displaystyle\alpha(\mathcal{P},i,s)

\displaystyle=\alpha(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},i,s^{\prime})+s-s^{\prime},

\displaystyle\alpha(\mathcal{Q},i,s)

\displaystyle=\alpha(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime},i,s^{\prime})+w,

which implies that

\begin{array}[]{ll}i(\mathcal{P},u,s)=i(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},u-s+s^{\prime},s^{\prime})&\textrm{for }s-s^{\prime}<u\leq v+1,\\
i(\mathcal{Q},u,s)=i(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime},u-w,s^{\prime})&\textrm{for }w<u\leq v+1.\end{array}

In particular, since
w\leq w^{\prime}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}i(\mathcal{P},u,s)=i(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},u-s+s^{\prime},s^{\prime})&\textrm{for }s-s^{\prime}<u\leq v+1,\\
i(\mathcal{Q},u,s)=i(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime},u-w,s^{\prime})&\textrm{for }w^{\prime}<u\leq v+1.\end{array}

Since
(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})
encodes
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})

and
(\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{q^{\prime}})
encodes
(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})
,
it follows that for
1\leq u\leq v+1
,

p_{u}=i(\mathcal{P},u,s),\quad q_{u}=i(\mathcal{Q},u,s),\quad p^{\prime}_{u}=i(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},u,s),\quad q^{\prime}_{u}=i(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime},u,s).

In summary, we have proved that

\displaystyle p_{u}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}s-u&\quad\textrm{for }1\leq u\leq\min\{s-s^{\prime},v+1\},\\
p^{\prime}_{u-s+s^{\prime}}&\quad\textrm{for }s-s^{\prime}<u\leq v+1,\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle q_{u}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}i(\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{D},u,s)&\quad\textrm{for }1\leq u\leq\min\{w^{\prime},v+1\},\\
q^{\prime}_{u-w}&\quad\textrm{for }w^{\prime}<u\leq v+1.\end{array}\right.

Thus

(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})

extends

(\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{q^{\prime}})

by
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
.
∎

2

For

s

2

.

•

(\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{q^{\prime}})
by

(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})=(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{B})\succ\mathcal{K}_{s^{\prime}}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{s}
.
Since
any pair
(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})

that
extends

(\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{q^{\prime}})

by
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})

is uniquely determined by

(\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{q^{\prime}})

and
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
,

(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})
must be encoded by
the pair
(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})

that extends

(\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{q^{\prime}})
by
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})

in condition 3.

We next show that

\mathcal{P}\mid\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{A}

and

\mathcal{Q}\mid\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{B}
.
Note that

\mathcal{J}(0,s)=\mathcal{J}(0,s^{\prime})\cup(\mathcal{K}_{s^{\prime}}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{s})\cup\mathcal{J}(s^{\prime},s)
.
The three subfamilies

\mathcal{J}(0,s^{\prime})
,

\mathcal{K}_{s^{\prime}}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{s}
,
and

\mathcal{J}(s^{\prime},s)

have neighborhoods

\mathcal{J}(0,s^{\prime})\cup\mathcal{K}_{s^{\prime}}
,

\mathcal{J}(0,s)\cup\mathcal{K}_{s}
,
and

\mathcal{K}_{s^{\prime}}\cup\mathcal{J}(s^{\prime},s)\cup\mathcal{K}_{s}
,
respectively.

For
\mathcal{J}(0,s^{\prime})
and its neighborhood

\mathcal{J}(0,s^{\prime})\cup\mathcal{K}_{s^{\prime}}
,
condition 1 guarantees that

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{A}^{\prime}

and

\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
.

For

\mathcal{J}(s^{\prime},s)

and its neighborhood

\mathcal{K}_{s^{\prime}}\cup\mathcal{J}(s^{\prime},s)\cup\mathcal{K}_{s}
,
condition 4 guarantees that

\mathcal{C}\mid\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{A}

and

\mathcal{D}\mid\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{B}
.

For

\mathcal{K}_{s^{\prime}}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{s}

and its neighborhood

\mathcal{J}(0,s)\cup\mathcal{K}_{s}
,
recall that by condition 1,
we have either
s^{\prime}=0
or
(s^{\prime}-1,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
.
If
s^{\prime}=0
, then
\mathcal{K}_{s^{\prime}}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{s}
is empty.
Otherwise, we have
(s^{\prime}-1,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{B}

and
(s^{\prime},s^{\prime}+1)\in\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{A}

for the
2
-partition
(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{A},\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{B})\succ\mathcal{J}(0,s)\cup\mathcal{K}_{s}
.

Consider any interval
(a,b)\in\mathcal{K}_{s^{\prime}}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{s}
,
where
0\leq a<s^{\prime}<b\leq s
.
If
(a,b)\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{A}
,
then by Lemma
2

we have

\alpha(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{A},a,b)=\alpha(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},a,s^{\prime})+\alpha(\mathcal{C}\cup(\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}^{\prime}),s^{\prime},b).

Condition 5 guarantees that

\alpha(\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}},a,s^{\prime})\leq\alpha(\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}},a,s^{\prime})+\alpha(\mathcal{C}\cup(\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}^{\prime}),s^{\prime},b)\leq v.

By Lemma
3
,

\alpha(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},a,s^{\prime})=\alpha(\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}},a,s^{\prime})

whenever
\alpha(\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}},a,s^{\prime})\leq v
.
Thus

\alpha(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{A},a,b)=\alpha(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},a,s^{\prime})+\alpha(\mathcal{C}\cup(\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}^{\prime}),s^{\prime},b)=\alpha(\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}},a,s^{\prime})+\alpha(\mathcal{C}\cup(\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}^{\prime}),s^{\prime},b)\leq v.

Similarly, if
(a,b)\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{B}
,
then

\alpha(\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{B},a,b)=\alpha(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime},a,s^{\prime})+\alpha(\mathcal{D}\cup(\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{B}^{\prime}),s^{\prime},b)=\alpha(\boldsymbol{q^{\prime}},a,s^{\prime})+\alpha(\mathcal{D}\cup(\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{B}^{\prime}),s^{\prime},b)\leq v.

Thus
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{A}\mid\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{A}

and

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{B}\mid\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{B}
.

We have shown that

\mathcal{P}\mid\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{A}

and

\mathcal{Q}\mid\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{B}
.
In particular,

\mathcal{P}\mid\mathcal{P}

and

\mathcal{Q}\mid\mathcal{Q}
,
and hence
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})
is good.
In summary, we have a good basic
2
-partition
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})
of
\mathcal{J}(0,s)

such that

(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})
encodes
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})
,

\mathcal{P}\mid\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{A}
,

\mathcal{Q}\mid\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{B}
,
and
(s-1,s)\in\mathcal{P}
.
Thus
Y(s,\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})=\mathrm{true}
,
as desired.
∎

3.3
Time complexity

We say that two intervals in
\mathcal{J}

overlap significantly

if the length of their intersection is at least
2v+1
.
Construct a graph
H
with vertex set
\mathcal{J}
such that two vertices in
H

are connected by an edge if and only if the corresponding two intervals in
\mathcal{J}

overlap significantly.
Compute connected components in
H
,
and partition
\mathcal{J}
into
groups
accordingly.
We say that a
2
-partition of
\mathcal{J}
is

group-conforming

if no two intervals from the same group are put in different parts.

Lemma 6
.

Any good
2
-partition of
\mathcal{J}
must be group-conforming.

Proof.

It suffices to show that any two intervals that overlap significantly
must be in the same part.
Let
A
and
B
be two intervals in
\mathcal{J}
such that the length
\ell

of their intersection
A\cap B
is at least
2v+1
.
Then
A\cap B
contains
\ell\geq 2v+1
disjoint unit intervals in
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{J}
.
If
A
and
B
are in two different parts of a
2
-partition of
\mathcal{J}
,
then either
A
or
B
would contain at least
\lceil(2v+1)/2\rceil=v+1

disjoint unit intervals in the same part,
forming a star
K_{1,v+1}
.
∎

Lemma 7
.

Any integer point of the line is contained in intervals from at most

2v^{2}+v
groups.

Proof.

Fix any point
x
.
For each interval of length at least
2v+1
that contains
x
,
let its
core
be any subinterval of length exactly
2v+1
that still contains
x
.
If the cores of two intervals coincide,
then they overlap significantly and hence belong to the same group.
Thus the number of groups with at least one interval of length at least
2v+1

containing
x

is at most the number of distinct intervals of length exactly
2v+1
containing
x
,
which is
2v
.

On the other hand, for each
\ell
,
1\leq\ell\leq 2v
,
there are
\ell-1
distinct intervals of length
\ell
containing
x
.
In total,
there are at most
(2v)(2v-1)/2=2v^{2}-v
such intervals,
and hence there are at most
2v^{2}-v
distinct groups including such intervals in
\mathcal{J}
.

In summary,
the point
x
is contained in intervals from at most

2v+2v^{2}-v=2v^{2}+v
groups.
∎

Note that the number
m
of maximal cliques is at most the number
n
of vertices
in vertebrate interval graphs.
For each
s
,
1\leq s\leq m\leq n
,
there are
n^{O(v)}

pairs of
s
-monotonic sequences
(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})
.
By Lemma
7
,
each
\mathcal{K}_{s}
includes intervals from
O(v^{2})
groups,
and hence has
2^{O(v^{2})}
group-conforming
2
-partitions
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.
For two tuples

(s,\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})

and
(s^{\prime},\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{q^{\prime}},\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{B}^{\prime})
,
all conditions in Lemma
5
can be checked in
n^{O(1)}
time.
Thus the running time of our dynamic programming algorithm
is
2^{O(v^{2})}n^{O(v)}
.
This completes the proof of Theorem
1
.
