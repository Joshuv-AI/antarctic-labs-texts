Abstract
. This article is concerned with the development of a theoretical framework of global measure-valued solutions for a class of hyperbolic–parabolic cross-diffusion systems, and its application to the convergence analysis of a fully discrete finite-volume scheme. After introducing an appropriate notion of dissipative measure-valued solutions to the PDE system, a numerical scheme is proposed which is shown to generate, in the continuum limit, a dissipative measure-valued solution. The “parabolic density part” of the limiting measure-valued solution is atomic and converges to its constant state for long times. Furthermore, it is proved that whenever the PDE system possesses a strong solution, the convergence of the approximation scheme holds in the strong sense. The results are based on Young measure theory and a weak–strong stability estimate combining Shannon and Rao entropies. The convergence of the numerical scheme is achieved by means of discrete entropy dissipation inequalities and an artificial diffusion, which vanishes in the continuum limit.

1.
Introduction

The segregation of multi-species populations can be modeled at a macroscopic level by cross-diffusion equations. Segregation typically requires the associated diffusion matrix to have a nontrivial kernel. In this situation, solutions may have spatial discontinuities; see, e.g.,
[
3
]
for a two-species model.
Diffusively regularized segregation models have been derived, for an arbitrary number of species, from interacting particle systems in a mean-field-type limit
[
11
]
.
The class considered here has recently been found to possess a symmetric hyperbolic–parabolic structure
[
19
]
.
In this paper, we establish the global existence of dissipative measure-valued solutions as a limit of finite-volume approximations,
the uniqueness of strong solutions among dissipative measure-valued solutions, and a result on the long-time asymptotic behavior.

1.1.
Equations

The segregation cross-diffusion equations for the vector
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})
of
the population densities
u_{i}
are systems of continuity equations,

(1)

\partial_{t}u_{i}+\operatorname{div}(u_{i}\mathsf{v}_{i})=0,\quad\mathsf{v}_{i}=-\nabla p_{i}(u),\quad\mbox{in }\Omega,\ t>0,\ i=1,\ldots,n,

where
p_{i}(u)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}u_{j}
and
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(
d\geq 1
) is a bounded Lipschitz domain, supplemented with the
no-flux boundary and initial conditions

(2)

u_{i}\nabla p_{i}(u)\cdot\nu=0\quad\mbox{on }\partial\Omega,\ t>0,\quad u_{i}(0)=u_{i}^{\rm in}\quad\mbox{in }\Omega,\ i=1,\ldots,n,

where
\nu
denotes the exterior unit normal vector to
\partial\Omega
.
The variables
(u_{i})
represent, for instance, densities of animal populations
[
3
]
,
healthy and tumor cell densities
[
41
]
, or heights of thin fluid layers

[
16
,
39
]
.

The parameters
a_{ij}\geq 0
are assumed to satisfy the following two conditions:
The matrix
A=(a_{ij})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
is semistable, i.e., the real parts of
all its eigenvalues are nonnegative, and it satisfies the detailed-balance condition,
i.e., there exist
\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n}>0
such that

(3)

\pi_{i}a_{ij}=\pi_{j}a_{ji}\quad\mbox{for all }i,j=1,\ldots,n,\ i\neq j.

These equations can be recognized as the detailed-balance condition for the Markov chain associated to
A
, and the vector
(\pi_{i})
is an invariant measure.
Under condition (
3
), the change of variables
u_{j}\mapsto\pi_{j}u_{j}=:\widetilde{u}_{j}
brings the equation in the form
\partial_{t}\widetilde{u}_{i}=\operatorname{div}(\widetilde{u}_{i}\nabla(B\widetilde{u})_{i})
, where the matrix
B=(a_{ij}\pi_{j}^{-1})_{ij}
is symmetric and positive semidefinite. Thus, from now on we consider, without loss of generality, the equations

(4)

\partial_{t}u_{i}=\operatorname{div}(u_{i}\nabla p_{i}(u)),\quad p_{i}(u)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{ij}u_{j}\quad\mbox{in }\Omega,\ t>0,\ i=1,\ldots,n,

where
B=(b_{ij})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
is symmetric positive semidefinite and
b_{ij}\geq 0
for all
i,j=1,\dots,n
.
We note that if
b_{ii}=0
for some
i
, then
b_{ji}=b_{ij}=0
for
j=1,\dots,n
due to the positive semidefiniteness of
B
. Thus, in this case,
the dynamics of
u_{i}
become trivial and the
i
th species can be removed from the system. We may therefore further assume that
b_{ii}>0
for all
i=1,\dots,n
.
If
\operatorname{rank}B=n
and
u_{i}>0
for all
i=1,\dots,n
, equation (
4
) is parabolic in the sense of Petrovskii,
which at the linear level is a minimal condition for the
generation of an analytic semigroup on
L^{p}(\Omega)

[
1
]
.
The existence of global weak solutions in the case
\operatorname{rank}B=n
was
investigated in
[
34
, Theorem 17]
.
If
B
has a nontrivial kernel, it is positive definite only on the subspace
(\operatorname{ker}B)^{\perp}
,
and we lose the parabolic structure.
This is the situation we are primarily concerned with in this paper.

1.2.
State of the art

Equations (
1
) with nontrivial kernel of
A
have been studied in the literature in special cases.
The first work is
[
3
]
, where the global existence of segregated solutions for two species in one space dimension with
a_{11}=a_{12}=1
and
a_{21}=a_{22}=k>0
was shown.
This result relies on a change to mass variables.
The analysis was generalized in
[
4
]
to several space dimensions if
k=1
.
The idea is to introduce new variables
w_{1}=u_{1}+u_{2}
and
w_{2}=u_{1}/(u_{1}+u_{2})
.
It turns out that
w_{1}
solves a porous-medium equation with quadratic nonlinearity
and
w_{2}
solves a transport equation, demonstrating the hyperbolic–parabolic nature of
the system. The same idea was used in
[
9
]
for a related system with general pressures
p_{i}
and, employing different techniques, in
[
29
]
with
p_{i}(u)=(u_{1}+u_{2})^{\gamma}
for
\gamma>1
. Notice that the choice
k=1
means that the corresponding velocity fields
\textsf{v}_{i}
in (
1
) are independent of
i
, so that the motion of the two species is governed by a single velocity field.

The existence of an infinite family of minimizers of the entropy
(or free energy) functional for different local and nonlocal variants was proved in
[
7
]
, showing that both segregation and mixing of species is possible.
If the pressure is the variational derivative of a certain functional,
one may formulate (
1
) for
n=2
as a formal gradient flow. This
property has been exploited in
[
7
,
17
]
to prove the convergence of a minimizing scheme.

The one-velocity two-species case was generalized to an arbitrary number of species
in
[
20
]
, proving the global existence
of classical and weak solutions by decomposing the system
into one decoupled porous-medium equation and
n-1
transport equations. This approach was generalized in
[
19
]
to the case of multiple velocity fields and with associated diffusion matrices of arbitrary rank
r\in\{1,\dots,n\}
to show the local existence of classical solutions. Segregating solutions for one-velocity multi-species reactive systems were constructed in
[
32
]
.

There exist related cross-diffusion models with rank-deficient diffusion matrices
in the literature, for instance the Maxwell–Stefan equations for fluid mixtures

[
5
]
, where the diffusion matrix has a one-dimensional kernel. In contrast to the present problem, the kernel can
be removed by taking into account the volume-filling assumption
\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}=1
, which allows one to reduce the system for the densities
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}

to a parabolic one for the variables
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n-1}
via

u_{n}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}u_{i}

[
35
]
.

The analysis and the convergence of approximation schemes to equations (
1
) for general rank-deficient matrices
A

is challenging, since the decomposition of the parabolic and hyperbolic parts is involved. Moreover, in view of the
results of
[
3
]
, we cannot expect weak solutions in
H^{1}(\Omega)
,
and the hyperbolic part makes it difficult to obtain (entropy) solutions
in the distributional sense.
In the present paper, we choose to enlarge the solution space by
considering dissipative measure-valued solutions, which allow us to encode information
about the oscillation properties of the approximate solutions.

DiPerna introduced the concept of (entropy)
measure-valued solutions
to
conservation laws
[
18
]
. In this framework, solutions are no longer integrable functions
but Young measures (parametrized probability measures), which are able to capture
the limiting behavior of sequences of oscillating functions. This concept is based
on an earlier work by Tartar
[
46
]
, who characterized weak limits of sequences of bounded functions.
Due to the lack of uniqueness results, the framework of measure-valued solutions does
not allow one to identify the physically relevant solutions, and further
structural conditions on the solutions are necessary.

One idea to resolve this issue is to require
an integrated form of the entropy or energy inequality, which leads to the
concept of
dissipative solutions
. It has been introduced by P.-L. Lions

[
40
, Sec. 4.4]
in the context of the incompressible Euler equations.
In
[
6
]
it is shown that dissipative measure-valued solutions to the incompressible Euler equations enjoy the weak–strong uniqueness property, i.e., the dissipative measure-valued solution is atomic and coincides with
the strong or classical solution of the same initial-value problem if the
latter exists.
This idea was further applied to models from polyconvex elastodynamics
[
13
]
, to the compressible Euler and Navier–Stokes equations
[
23
,
30
]
,
to hyperbolic–parabolic systems in thermoviscoelasticity
[
12
]
, and to various other, mainly fluid mechanical models.

In the present paper, we obtain dissipative measure-valued solutions to (
4
), (
2
) by passing to the limit from discrete
finite-volume solutions
.
We further show that they enjoy the weak–strong uniqueness property (in the sense of measure-valued–strong uniqueness),
which entails important consequences for the numerical approximation.
Indeed, one may expect that reasonable structure-preserving approximation schemes generate a dissipative measure-valued solution. If such a measure-valued solution turns out to be atomic, i.e. taking the form of a Dirac measure at each point in space-time,
Young measure theory implies that the underlying approximate solutions converge in the strong sense. This idea has, for instance, been exploited in the proof of the convergence of finite-volume-type schemes for the compressible Navier–Stokes and Euler equations
[
24
,
25
]
.
For a further discussion on the use of measure-valued solutions in the numerical context, we refer to
[
26
]
.

The novelty of this paper is the analysis of equations (
4
) with
general rank-deficient matrices
B
by combining the
measure-valued framework, entropy methods, and finite-volume schemes.

1.3.
Key tools, definitions, and overview

The analysis of (
4
) is based on the observation that the system possesses
two Lyapunov functionals, respectively, the Shannon and Rao entropies

(5)

\displaystyle H_{S}(u)=\int_{\Omega}h_{S}(u)\mathrm{d}x,\quad h_{S}(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(u_{i}(\log u_{i}-1)+1\big),

(6)

\displaystyle H_{R}(u)=\int_{\Omega}h_{R}(u)\mathrm{d}x,\quad h_{R}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}b_{ij}u_{i}u_{j}.

The Shannon (–Boltzmann) entropy is related to the thermodynamic entropy of the system,
while the Rao entropy measures the functional diversity of the species

[
45
]
.

The functionals have two important properties. First, a computation shows that,
along smooth solutions to (
4
), (
2
),

(7)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}H_{S}}{\mathrm{d}t}(u)+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}b_{ij}\nabla u_{i}\cdot\nabla u_{j}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=0,

(8)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}H_{R}}{\mathrm{d}t}(u)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}u_{i}|\nabla p_{i}(u)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=0.

Since the matrix
B
is positive semidefinite, the Shannon entropy dissipation
term (the integral term in (
7
)) is nonnegative and consequently,

t\mapsto H_{S}(u(t))
is nonincreasing.
The expression
p_{i}(u)
can be interpreted as the
i
th partial pressure and

-\nabla p_{i}(u)
as the
i
th partial velocity (by Darcy’s law). Thus, we may interpret
the Rao entropy dissipation integral as the total kinetic energy of the system,
and
t\mapsto H_{R}(u(t))
is also nonincreasing.

Second, the Shannon and Rao entropy densities
h_{S}
and
h_{R}
are convex, and their sum

h_{S}+h_{R}
is strictly convex and has quadratic growth as
|u|\to\infty
,
u\in(0,\infty)^{n}
, as soon as
b_{ij}\geq 0
and
b_{ii}>0
for all
i,j=1,\dots,n
.
These properties allow us to derive a weak–strong stability estimate based on the Bregman distance
h(u|v):=h(u)-h(v)-h^{\prime}(v)\cdot(u{-}v)
associated with
h=h_{S}+h_{R}
.

Identities (
7
)–(
8
)
provide estimates for
u_{i}
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))

and for
(Bu)_{i}
in
L^{2}(0,T;

H^{1}(\Omega))
,
T>0
.
If
B
is rank-deficient,
these bounds do not ensure gradient estimates for the whole vector
u
.
Notice that the weak convergence for
u_{m}
and
\nabla p_{i}(u_{m})=\nabla(Bu_{m})_{i}
in
L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T))
, which may be expected for suitable approximating sequences
u_{m}
, does not allow us to identify the weak limit of
u_{m,i}\nabla(Bu_{m})_{i}
. This
issue is overcome by a suitable concept of dissipative measure-valued solutions. Let us mention that the estimates coming from (
8
) lead to a control of
u_{m,i}\nabla(Bu_{m})_{i}
in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{4/3}(\Omega))
, thus ruling out potential concentrations in this term.

Before giving the definition of the measure-valued solutions, we introduce some notation. We rewrite equation (
4
) as

\partial_{t}u_{i}=\operatorname{div}(u_{i}\nabla(Bu)_{i}),\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

and set
L:=\operatorname{ker}B\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

L^{\perp}=\operatorname{ran}B
. Let
P_{L^{\perp}}
be the projection
onto
L^{\perp}
and set
\widehat{s}:=P_{L^{\perp}}s
for
s\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Any vector-valued function
u
is written as
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})
.
We define
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}=[0,\infty)
and let
\mathcal{P}(W)
be the space of
probability measures on

W:={\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{n}\times(L^{\perp})^{d}.

The space
L^{\infty}_{w}(\Omega\times[0,\infty);\mathcal{P}(W))
is the set of
weakly
∗
measurable, essentially bounded functions of
\Omega\times[0,\infty)

taking values in
\mathcal{P}(W)
.
We henceforth use the notation

\langle\nu,f(s,p)\rangle:=\int_{W}f(s,p)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(s,p)\quad\text{for }\nu\in\mathcal{P}(W),f\in C_{0}(W),

where
C_{0}
denotes the space of
continuous functions vanishing at infinity. Whenever the right-hand side is well defined, this notation will also be used for more general continuous functions
f
.
Finally, we let
\Omega_{T}:=\Omega\times(0,T)
for
T>0
.

Definition 1

(Dissipative measure-valued solution)
.

Suppose that
u^{\rm in}\in L^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{n})
.
We call a parametrized measure

\mu\in L^{\infty}_{w}(\Omega\times[0,\infty);\mathcal{P}(W))

with barycenters

,
y:=\langle\mu,p\rangle

a dissipative measure-valued solution to (
4
), (
2
)
if the following is satisfied for all
T>0
:

•

Regularity:

It holds that

\displaystyle\qquad{u}\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega;{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}})),\ \partial_{t}u\in L^{2}(0,\infty;W^{1,4}(\Omega;{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}})^{*}),\ y\in L^{2}(\Omega_{T};(L^{\perp})^{d}),\ y=\nabla\widehat{u}.

Moreover,
\mu
acts trivially on the
\widehat{s}
-component,

(9)

\langle\mu,f(s,p)\rangle=\langle\mu,f(\widehat{u}+P_{L}s,p)\rangle

for all
f\in C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{n}\times(L^{\perp})^{d})
.

•

Shannon and Rao entropy inequalities:
It holds for a.e.

that

(10)

\displaystyle H_{S}^{\rm mv}(u(t))+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\langle\mu_{x,\tau},|B^{1/2}p|^{2}\rangle\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq H_{S}(u^{\rm in}),

(11)

\displaystyle H_{R}(u(t))+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\big\langle\mu_{x,\tau},s_{i}|(Bp)_{i}|^{2}\big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq H_{R}(u^{\rm in}),

where
H_{S}
and
H_{R}
are defined in (
5
)–(
6
)
and
H_{S}^{\rm mv}(u(t)):=\int_{\Omega}\langle\mu_{x,t},h_{S}(s)\rangle\mathrm{d}x
.

•

Evolution equation:
It holds for all
i=1,\ldots,n
and

\phi\in C^{1}_{c}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T))
that

(12)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u_{i}\partial_{t}\phi\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\Omega}u_{i}^{\rm in}\phi(0)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\langle\mu_{x,t},s_{i}(Bp)_{i}\rangle\cdot\nabla\phi\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

It is easy to see that, under the hypotheses of Definition
1
,
the term
\langle\mu,s_{i}(Bp)_{i}\rangle\in L^{1}(\Omega_{T})
is well defined for all
T>0

(cf. Section

u_{i}=\langle\mu,s_{i}\rangle\geq 0
.
Property (
9
) can be extended to a larger class of continuous functions

.
In particular, it holds for all
f\in C(W)
with
f\geq 0
.
If
{\rm rank}\,B=n
, property (

) implies that

fulfills (
4

.

with the standard weak solution concept.

(Consistency of the definition)
.

The definition of dissipative measure-valued solutions is consistent with the

2.
Numerical scheme and main results

First, we introduce the notation necessary to formulate our numerical method. Then we state the numerical scheme and the main results.

2.1.
Spatial domain and mesh

Let
d\geq 2
and let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a bounded polygonal domain
(or polyhedral if
d\geq 3
).
We associate to this domain an admissible mesh, given by
(i) a family
{\mathcal{T}}
of open polygonal (or polyhedral) control volumes, which are
also called cells,
(ii) a family
{\mathcal{E}}
of edges (or faces if
d\geq 3
),
and (iii) a family of points
(x_{K})_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}
associated to the control volumes
and satisfying
[
22
, Definition 9.1]
. This definition implies that
the straight line
\overline{x_{K}x_{L}}
between two centers of neighboring cells
is orthogonal to the edge (or face)
\sigma=K|L
between two cells.
For instance, Voronoï meshes satisfy this condition
[
22
, Example 9.2]
.
The size of the mesh is given by
\Delta x=\max_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\operatorname{diam}(K)
.
The family of edges
{\mathcal{E}}
is assumed to consist of interior edges

{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}
satisfying
\sigma\subset\Omega
and boundary edges
\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm ext}

satisfying
\sigma\subset\partial\Omega
. For a given
K\in{\mathcal{T}}
,
{\mathcal{E}}_{K}
denotes the
set of edges of
K
, splitting into
{\mathcal{E}}_{K}={\mathcal{E}}_{{\rm int},K}\cup{\mathcal{E}}_{{\rm ext},K}
.
For any
\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}
, there exists at least one cell
K\in{\mathcal{T}}
such that

\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}
.

We need a regularity assumption on the families of meshes we admit.
For given
\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}
,
we define the distance

{\operatorname{d}}_{\sigma}=\begin{cases}{\operatorname{d}}(x_{K},x_{L})&\quad\mbox{if }\sigma=K|L\in{\mathcal{E}}_{{\rm int},K},\\
{\operatorname{d}}(x_{K},\sigma)&\quad\mbox{if }\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{{\rm ext},K},\end{cases}

where d is the Euclidean distance in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and the transmissibility coefficient

(13)

\tau_{\sigma}=\frac{\mathfrak{\widetilde{m}}(\sigma)}{{\operatorname{d}}_{\sigma}},

where
\mathfrak{\widetilde{m}}(\sigma)
denotes the
(d{-}1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of
\sigma
.
We suppose the following mesh regularity condition for any admissible family of meshes
\{{\mathcal{T}}\}
: There exists a fixed

\zeta>0
, independent of
{\mathcal{T}}
, such that for all
K\in{\mathcal{T}}
and
\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}
,

(14)

{\operatorname{d}}(x_{K},\sigma)\geq\zeta{\operatorname{d}}_{\sigma}.

This condition means that the family of meshes
\{{\mathcal{T}}\}
is (locally) quasi-uniform.
We also use the geometric property

(15)

\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{{\rm int},K}}\mathfrak{\widetilde{m}}(\sigma){\operatorname{d}}(x_{K},\sigma)\leq d\mathfrak{m}(K)\quad\mbox{for any }K\in{\mathcal{T}},

where
\mathfrak{m}
denotes the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure.
Inequalities (
14
) and (
15
) are needed, for instance,
to derive a uniform bound
for the discrete time derivative of the approximate solution;
see Lemma
13
.

2.2.
Function spaces

Let
T>0
,
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and introduce the time step size
\Delta t=T/N

and the time steps
t_{k}=k\Delta t
for
k=0,\ldots,N
.
We denote by

\mathcal{D}
the space-time discretization of

\Omega_{T}=\Omega\times(0,T)
determined by the mesh
{\mathcal{T}}
and by
the values
(\Delta t,N)
.

The space of piecewise constant functions is defined by

V_{\mathcal{T}}=\bigg\{v:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}:\exists(v_{K})_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\subset{\mathbb{R}},\ v(x)=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}v_{K}\mathbf{1}_{K}(x)\bigg\},

where
\mathbf{1}_{K}
is the characteristic function on
K
. To define a norm
on this space, we define for
v\in V_{\mathcal{T}}
,
K\in{\mathcal{T}}
, and
\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}
,

v_{K,\sigma}=\begin{cases}v_{L}&\quad\mbox{if }\sigma=K|L\in{\mathcal{E}}_{{\rm int},K},\\
v_{K}&\quad\mbox{if }\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{{\rm ext},K},\end{cases}\quad\textrm{D}_{K,\sigma}v:=v_{K,\sigma}-v_{K},\quad\textrm{D}_{\sigma}v:=|\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}v|.

Let
1\leq q<\infty
and
v\in V_{\mathcal{T}}
.
The discrete
W^{1,q}(\Omega)
norm on
V_{\mathcal{T}}
is given by

\displaystyle\|v\|_{1,q,{\mathcal{T}}}=\big(\|v\|_{0,q,{\mathcal{T}}}^{q}+|v|_{1,q,{\mathcal{T}}}^{q}\big)^{1/q},\quad\mbox{where}

\displaystyle\|v\|_{0,q,{\mathcal{T}}}^{q}=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\mathfrak{m}(K)|v_{K}|^{q},\quad|v|_{1,q,{\mathcal{T}}}^{q}=\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}}\mathfrak{\widetilde{m}}(\sigma){\operatorname{d}}_{\sigma}\bigg|\frac{\text{D}_{\sigma}v}{{\operatorname{d}}_{\sigma}}\bigg|^{q},

where
v\in V_{\mathcal{T}}
. If
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})\in V_{\mathcal{T}}^{n}
is a vector-valued function,
we write for notational convenience

\|v\|_{1,q,{\mathcal{T}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|v_{i}\|_{1,q,{\mathcal{T}}}.

We associate to the discrete
W^{1,q}
norm a dual norm with respect
to the
L^{2}
inner product:

\|v\|_{-1,q,{\mathcal{T}}}=\sup\bigg\{\int_{\Omega}vw\mathrm{d}x:w\in V_{\mathcal{T}},\ \|w\|_{1,q,{\mathcal{T}}}=1\bigg\}.

Then the following property holds:

\bigg|\int_{\Omega}vw\mathrm{d}x\bigg|\leq\|v\|_{-1,q,{\mathcal{T}}}\|w\|_{1,q,{\mathcal{T}}}\quad\mbox{for all }v,w\in V_{\mathcal{T}},\ 1<p<\infty.

Finally, we introduce the space
V_{{\mathcal{T}},\Delta t}
of piecewise constant
functions with values in
V_{\mathcal{T}}
,

V_{{\mathcal{T}},\Delta t}=\bigg\{v:\Omega\times[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}:\exists(v^{k})_{k=1,\ldots,N}\subset V_{\mathcal{T}},\ v(x,t)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}v^{k}(x)\mathbf{1}_{(t_{k-1},t_{k}]}(t)\bigg\},

equipped with the discrete
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
norm

\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Delta t\|v^{k}\|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\quad\mbox{for all }v\in V_{{\mathcal{T}},\Delta t}.

2.3.
Discrete gradient

The discrete gradient is defined on a dual mesh. For this, we
define the cell
T_{K,\sigma}
of the dual mesh for
K\in{\mathcal{T}}
and
\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}
:

•

Diamond: Let
\sigma=K|L\in{\mathcal{E}}_{{\rm int},K}
. Then
T_{K,\sigma}
is that cell
whose vertices are given by
x_{K}
,
x_{L}
, and the end points of the edge
\sigma
.

•

Triangle: Let
\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{{\rm ext},K}
. Then
T_{K,\sigma}
is that cell
whose vertices are given by
x_{K}
and the end points of the edge
\sigma
.

The union of all diamonds and triangles
T_{K,\sigma}
equals the domain
\Omega

(up to a set of measure zero).
The property that the straight line
\overline{x_{K}x_{L}}
between two neighboring centers
of cells is orthogonal to the edge
\sigma=K|L
implies that

\mathfrak{\widetilde{m}}(\sigma)\,{\operatorname{d}}(x_{K},x_{L})=d\,\mathfrak{m}(T_{K,\sigma})\quad\mbox{for all }\sigma=K|L\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}.

The approximate gradient of
v\in V_{{\mathcal{T}},\Delta t}
is then defined by

\nabla^{\mathcal{D}}v(x,t)=\frac{\mathfrak{\widetilde{m}}(\sigma)}{\mathfrak{m}(T_{K,\sigma})}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}(v^{k})\nu_{K,\sigma}\quad\mbox{for }x\in T_{K,\sigma},\ t\in{(t_{k-1},t_{k}]},

where
\nu_{K,\sigma}
is the unit vector that is normal to
\sigma
and
points outwards of
K
.

2.4.
Numerical scheme

The initial functions are approximated by
u^{0}\in V_{\mathcal{T}}^{n}
defined via

(16)

u_{i,K}^{0}=\frac{1}{\mathfrak{m}(K)}\int_{K}u_{i}^{\rm in}(x)dx\quad\mbox{for all }K\in{\mathcal{T}},\ i=1,\ldots,n.

Let
u^{k-1}=(u^{k-1}_{1},\dots,u^{k-1}_{n})\in V_{\mathcal{T}}^{n}
be given. Then the values
u_{i,K}^{k}
for all
K\in{\mathcal{T}}
and
i=1,\ldots,n
are determined
by the implicit Euler finite-volume scheme

(17)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}(K)\frac{u_{i,K}^{k}-u_{i,K}^{k-1}}{\Delta t}+\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\mathcal{F}_{i,K,\sigma}^{k}=0,

(18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{i,K,\sigma}^{k}=-\tau_{\sigma}u_{i,\sigma}^{k}\textrm{D}_{K,\sigma}p_{i}(u^{k})-\eta^{\alpha}\tau_{\sigma}\textrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u_{i}^{k},

where
\eta=\max\{\Delta x,\Delta t\}
,
0<\alpha<2
, and

\tau_{\sigma}
is given by (
13
).
The mobility
u_{i,\sigma}^{k}
is defined for
\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}
by the upwind scheme

(19)

u_{i,\sigma}^{k}=\begin{cases}u_{i,K,\sigma}^{k}&\quad\mbox{if }\textrm{D}_{K,\sigma}p_{i}(u^{k})\geq 0,\\
u_{i,K}^{k}&\quad\mbox{if }\textrm{D}_{K,\sigma}p_{i}(u^{k})<0.\end{cases}

The upwind approximation allows us to derive the discrete Shannon entropy inequality; see Remark
2
. We may also use a logarithmic mean function; see Remark
3
.

We have added some artificial diffusion in the numerical flux

\mathcal{F}_{i,K,\sigma}^{k}
, which vanishes in the limit
\eta\to 0
.
The term is needed to show the convergence of the scheme. In particular,
it provides an
\eta
-dependent bound for the full gradient,
compensating the incomplete gradient estimate.
Note that the artificial diffusion is
not
needed
to prove the existence of discrete solutions, and we may set
\eta=0
in this case. Artificial diffusion/viscosity is used in numerical approximations of the Euler equations to stabilize the scheme; see, e.g.,
[
25
, (3.8)]
.

The numerical fluxes
\mathcal{F}_{i,K,\sigma}^{k}
are consistent approximations of the exact fluxes through the edges, since
\mathcal{F}_{i,K,\sigma}+\mathcal{F}_{i,L,\sigma}=0
for all edges
\sigma=K|L
and
\mathcal{F}_{i,K,\sigma}=0
for all
{\mathcal{E}}_{{\rm ext},K}
.
The following discrete integration-by-parts formula holds for
v=(v_{K})\in V_{\mathcal{T}}
:

(20)

\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\mathcal{F}_{i,K,\sigma}v_{K}=-\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\mathcal{F}_{i,K,\sigma}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}v.

Notice that the terms
\mathcal{F}_{i,K,\sigma}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}v
on the right-hand side only depend on
\sigma
, but not on the specific control volume
K
satisfying
\sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{K}
. Hence, to evaluate the sum on the right, we may pick for each
\sigma
any
K
with
\sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{K}
as long as we keep
K
fixed.

Remark 2

(Discrete gradient-flow property for upwind scheme)
.

The upwind approximation implies a discrete gradient-flow property. Indeed, we first observe that the concavity of the logarithm gives

b(\log a-\log b)\leq a-b\leq a(\log a-\log b)\quad\mbox{for all }a,b>0.

Combined with definition (
19
) of
u_{i,\sigma}^{k}
, this leads for
u_{i,K}^{k}>0
and
u_{i,L}^{k}>0
to

(21)

u_{i,\sigma}^{k}(p_{i}(u_{L}^{k})-p_{i}(u_{K}^{k}))(\log u_{i,L}^{k}-\log u_{i,K}^{k})\geq(p_{i}(u_{L}^{k})-p_{i}(u_{K}^{k}))(u_{i,L}^{k}-u_{i,K}^{k})

and therefore, by discrete integration by parts (
20
),

(22)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\mathcal{F}_{i,K,\sigma}^{k}\log u_{i,K}^{k}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\tau_{\sigma}u_{i,\sigma}^{k}\textrm{D}_{K,\sigma}p_{i}(u^{k})\textrm{D}_{K,\sigma}\log u_{i}^{k}

\displaystyle{}-\eta^{\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\tau_{\sigma}\textrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u_{i}^{k}\textrm{D}_{K,\sigma}\log u_{i}^{k}\leq-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\tau_{\sigma}b_{ij}\textrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u_{j}^{k}\textrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u_{i}^{k},

where we used the monotonicity of the logarithm implying that

\textrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u_{i}^{k}\textrm{D}_{K,\sigma}\log u_{i}^{k}\geq 0
.
The right-hand side of (
22
) is nonpositive due to the positive semidefiniteness of
B=(b_{ij})
. We deduce from this inequality the discrete entropy inequality (
25
).

Remark 3

(Discrete gradient flow property for logarithmic mean)
.

We may alternatively define
u_{i,\sigma}^{k}
via the logarithmic mean

(23)

u_{i,\sigma}^{k}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{u_{i,L}^{k}-u_{i,K}^{k}}{\log u_{i,L}^{k}-\log u_{i,K}^{k}}&\quad\mbox{if }u_{i,K}^{k}\neq u_{i,L}^{k}\mbox{ and }u_{i,K}^{k}>0,\ u_{i,L}^{k}>0,\\
u_{i,K}^{k}&\quad\mbox{if }u_{i,K}^{k}=u_{i,L}^{k}>0,\\
0&\quad\mbox{else}.\end{cases}

We remark that the artificial diffusion in the numerical flux (
18
) allows us to show that
u_{i,K}^{k}
is positive for all
K\in{\mathcal{T}}
(see Section
3.5
) such that
u_{i,\sigma}^{k}
(for
\sigma=K|L
) is always defined by one of the first two cases.
Definition (
23
) also leads to a discrete gradient-flow property. Indeed, observing that
u_{i,\sigma}^{k}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}\log u_{i}^{k}=\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u_{i}^{k}
and
multiplying (
18
) by
\log u_{i,K}^{k}
and
summing over all
i=1,\ldots,n
,
K\in{\mathcal{T}}
, and
\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}
, we
see that (
22
) holds too.
Notice that (
21
) becomes an equality in this case.

Finally, we observe that the mobility satisfies in both cases the following properties:

(24)

u_{i,\sigma}^{k}\leq\max\{u_{i,K}^{k},u_{i,L}^{k}\},\quad|u_{i,\sigma}^{k}-u_{i,K}^{k}|\leq|u_{i,K}^{k}-u_{i,L}^{k}|\quad\mbox{for }\sigma=K|L.

2.5.
Main results

We impose the following hypotheses.

(H1)

Data:
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a bounded polygonal
(or polyhedral if
d\geq 3
) domain,
T>0
, and
u^{\rm in}\in L^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{n})
such that
\|u^{\rm in}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}>0
.
We set
\Omega_{T}=\Omega\times(0,T)
.

(H2)

Discretization:
\mathcal{D}
is an admissible discretization of

\Omega_{T}
satisfying (
14
).

(H3)

Diffusion coefficients:

B=(b_{ij})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{n\times n}
is symmetric positive semidefinite with

\operatorname{rank}B\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
b_{ii}>0
for
i=1,\dots,n
.

Note that since
B
is positive semidefinite,
its square root
B^{1/2}
exists and

z^{T}Bz=|B^{1/2}z|^{2}
for
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Moreover, with
\lambda>0
being the smallest positive eigenvalue of
B^{1/2}
, we have
|B^{1/2}z|\geq\lambda|\widehat{z}|
(cf. Lemma
16
).

Theorem 4
.

Let Hypotheses (H1)–(H3) hold,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\eta\geq 0
,
and let
u^{k-1}\in V_{\mathcal{T}}^{n}
be given.
Then there exists a solution
u^{k}=(u_{1}^{k},\ldots,u_{n}^{k})\in V_{\mathcal{T}}^{n}
to scheme (
16
)–(
18
) satisfying

u_{i,K}^{k}>0
for
i=1,\ldots,n
,
K\in{\mathcal{T}}
.
Inductively, let
u^{j}\in V_{\mathcal{T}}^{n},j=1,\dots,k
, be the solution to scheme (
16
)–(
18
) with
u^{k-1}
replaced by
u^{j-1}
. Then
\{u^{j}\}
obey the discrete
entropy inequalities

(25)

\displaystyle H_{S}(u^{k})+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Delta t|B^{1/2}u^{j}|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}^{2}+4\eta^{\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{n}|(u_{i}^{j})^{1/2}|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq H_{S}(u^{0}),

(26)

\displaystyle H_{R}(u^{k})+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}}\tau_{\sigma}u_{i,\sigma}^{j}|\mathrm{D}_{\sigma}(Bu^{j})_{i}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq H_{R}(u^{0}).

Moreover,
H_{R}(u^{k})\leq H_{R}(u^{k-1})
.

The existence of finite-volume solutions to (
16
)–(
18
)
was shown in
[
36
]
by using the Rao entropy only,
but the proof needs matrices
B
with full rank.
We can avoid this condition since we exploit the estimates coming from
the Shannon entropy. Theorem
4
is proved by adding a discrete
version of the regularizing term
\varepsilon(-\Delta w_{i}+w_{i})
, where

w_{i}=\log u_{i}
are the entropic variables
[
27
,
33
,
38
]
,
and a topological degree argument, similar as in
[
36
]
.
Uniform estimates from the Shannon entropy inequality (
25
) allow us to perform the de-regularizing limit
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Observe that the theorem is valid for
\eta=0
, i.e., no artificial diffusion is needed here.

Theorem
4
and the subsequent results also hold for
domains
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with curved (Lipschitz) boundary. Indeed, one may triangulate
\Omega
in such a way that the control volumes have a curved boundary
[
42
]
, or one may cover
\Omega
by additional cells and estimate the integral error; we refer to Remark
14
for details.

For the convergence result,
we introduce a family
(\mathcal{D}_{m})_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of admissible
space-time discretizations of
\Omega_{T}
indexed by the size

\eta_{m}=\max\{\Delta x_{m},\Delta t_{m}\}
of the mesh, where

\Delta x_{m}=\max_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{m}}\operatorname{diam}(K)
and
\Delta t_{m}
is the
time step size of the mesh
\mathcal{D}_{m}
, satisfying
\eta_{m}\to 0

as
m\to\infty
. We denote by
{\mathcal{T}}_{m}
the corresponding meshes of
\Omega

and set
\nabla^{m}:=\nabla^{\mathcal{D}_{m}}
.

Theorem 5

(Convergence of the scheme)
.

Let Hypotheses (H1)–(H3) hold, and let
(\mathcal{D}_{m})
be a family
of admissible meshes satisfying (
14
) uniformly in
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Let
(u_{m})
be a sequence of finite-volume solutions to
(
16
)–(
18
) with
\eta=\eta_{m}>0
,
constructed in Theorem
4
.
Then, up to a subsequence,
(u_{m},\nabla^{m}\widehat{u}_{m})
generates a Young measure
\mu
which is a dissipative measure-valued solution to (
4
), (
2
) in the sense of Definition
1
.
Moreover, the function

t\mapsto H_{R}(u(t))
is nonincreasing.

The strategy of the proof of Theorem
5
is as follows.
The estimates from the discrete entropy inequalities and a uniform bound
for the discrete time derivative of
u_{m}
allow us to apply the compactness result
of
[
28
]
to conclude the strong convergence of (a subsequence of)

\widehat{u}_{m}
in
L^{2}(\Omega_{T})
as
m\to\infty
. Moreover,
(u_{m})

and
\nabla^{m}(B\widehat{u}_{m})
are weakly converging in
L^{2}(\Omega_{T})
.
Clearly, these convergences are too weak to conclude the convergence
of the nonlinear flux (
18
).
However, the sequence
(u_{m},\nabla^{m}\widehat{u}_{m})

generates a parametrized measure
\mu

[
44
, Chap. 6]

such that
\langle\mu,s_{i}(Bp)_{i}\rangle

is the distributional limit of
u_{m,i,\sigma}\nabla^{m}(B\widehat{u}_{m})_{i}
.
Moreover, because of the strong convergence of
(\widehat{u}_{m})
,
we can separate this part, leading to (
9
).

Remark 6

(Full-rank approximation)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. An alternative to the finite-volume approach is to consider a suitable full-rank symmetric positive definite regularization
(B_{\rho})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
of
B
with
\lim_{\rho\to 0}B_{\rho}=B
,
and to approximate (
4
) by

(27)

\partial_{t}u_{i}=\operatorname{div}(u_{i}\nabla(B_{\rho}u)_{i}),\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

After an appropriate additional regularization, it is possible to apply the entropy method of
[
33
, Sec. 4.4]
(using the Rao entropy structure) and to establish the existence of a nonnegative weak solution to (
27
), (
2
) that satisfies both the Rao and Shannon entropy inequalities with
B
replaced by
B_{\rho}
. The dissipative measure-valued solution to (
4
), (
2
) is then obtained in the limit
\rho\to 0
.

The statement of Theorem
5
is rather weak, since the Young measure may not be unique. However, we can prove a weak–strong uniqueness result. According to Remark
14
, we can assume in the following that
\Omega
is a general bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary.

Theorem 7

(Weak–strong uniqueness)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain.
Let
v\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T];{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{n})

be a positive solution to (
4
), (
2
) (in the weak sense) with initial datum
v(0)=u^{\rm in}>0
,
and let
\mu
be a dissipative measure-valued solution to (
4
), (
2
).
Then

\mu_{x,t}=\delta_{v(x,t)}\otimes\delta_{\nabla\widehat{v}(x,t)}\quad\mbox{for a.e. }(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T).

The assertion is deduced from a stability estimate based on the
Bregman distance
h(u|v):=h(u)-h(v)-h^{\prime}(v)\cdot(u{-}v)

associated with the convex function
h:=h_{S}+h_{R}
, which has to be adapted to the measure-valued framework.
Loosely speaking, we consider the sum
H_{S}(u|v)+H_{R}(u|v)
, where

H_{k}(u|v)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}\big(h_{k}(u)-h_{k}(v)-h_{k}^{\prime}(v)\cdot(u-v)\big)\mathrm{d}x,\quad k=S,R,

and compute its time derivative along solutions to (
4
).
Certain error terms arising in this computation need to be estimated above by
C\int_{\Omega}h(u|v)\mathrm{d}x
. For this step and in the absence of
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
-bounds on the densities
u_{i}
, we take advantage of the better coercivity properties at infinity of the Rao entropy.

As a consequence of Theorem
7
, the finite-volume solution converges
strongly
to the classical solution if the latter exists.

Corollary 8
.

Let
u\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T];{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{n})

be a positive solution to (
4
), (
2
).
Let
(u_{m})
be a sequence of finite-volume solutions to (
16
), (
18
) with
\eta=\eta_{m}>0
. Then, as
m\to\infty
,

\displaystyle(u_{m},\nabla^{m}\widehat{u}_{m})\to(u,\nabla\widehat{u})\quad\text{strongly in $L^{p}(\Omega_{T})$}

for all
p\in[1,2)
and all
T>0
.

Indeed, the weak–strong uniqueness implies that the Young measure generated by
(u_{m},

\nabla^{m}\widehat{u}_{m})
coincides at each point
(x,t)
with the Dirac measure concentrated at the smooth solution. Since
|(u_{m},\nabla^{m}\widehat{u}_{m})|^{p}\subset L^{1}(\Omega_{T})
is equi-integrable for every
p\in[1,2)
, the assertion in Corollary
8
thus follows from classical Young measure theory (cf. e.g.
[
44
, Theorem 6.12]
).

It is shown in
[
19
, Theorem 2.6]
for
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{d}
(with periodic boundary conditions) that problem (
4
), (
2
) possesses a positive classical solution on a short time interval if the initial data are positive and smooth. The main results in the present paper should equally be valid in the periodic setting.

If
B
has a non-trivial kernel, steady states to (
4
), (
2
) are not necessarily constant in space
and for any fixed mass vector
\mathsf{m}\in(0,\infty)^{n}
, there exist infinitely many steady states. Given such
\mathsf{m}
, we define the space of steady states as

\mathfrak{S}_{\mathsf{m}}=\bigg\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{n}):\int_{\Omega}v\,\mathrm{d}x=\mathsf{m}\mbox{ and }\nabla(Bv)=0\mbox{ in }\Omega\bigg\}.

Theorem 9

(Long-time behavior)
.

Let
\mu
be a dissipative measure-valued solution to (
4
), (
2
).
Let
u=\langle\mu,s\rangle
and set
\mathsf{m}:=\int_{\Omega}u^{\rm in}dx
. Then

\mathfrak{S}_{\mathsf{m}}\subset L^{\infty}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{n})
and there
exists
u^{*}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathsf{m}}
such that, as
t\to\infty
,

\widehat{u}(t)\to\widehat{u}^{*}\quad\mbox{strongly in }L^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{n}),

where
\widehat{u}^{*}=P_{L^{\perp}}u^{*}
. We recall that
P_{L^{\perp}}
is
the projection onto
L^{\perp}=\operatorname{ran}B
.

For the proof of Theorem
9
, we argue as follows. The fact that
\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\nabla(B^{1/2}\widehat{u})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\mathrm{d}t
is finite implies the existence of a sequence
t_{k}\to\infty
such that

k\mapsto(B^{1/2}u)(t_{k})
converges strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
to

B^{1/2}u^{*}
as
k\to\infty
, where
u^{*}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathsf{m}}
. The monotonicity of
t\mapsto H_{R}(u(t)|u^{*})
then shows that
B^{1/2}\widehat{u}(t)
converges and consequently,
\widehat{u}(t)
converges to
\widehat{u}^{*}
for all
sequences
t\to\infty
. Such reasoning is classical in degenerate cases, where entropy–entropy dissipation estimates are not available;
see for instance
[
8
,
31
]
.

3.
Discrete problem

In this section, we prove Theorem
4
.
The existence proof uses a discrete analog of the entropy
method for cross-diffusion systems
[
33
]
.
We first introduce a regularized numerical scheme involving an approximation
parameter
\varepsilon>0
, prove the existence of a solution to this scheme and
suitable estimates coming from the Shannon entropy inequality, and apply
a topological degree argument. The uniform estimates allow us to perform
the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
.

3.1.
Definition and continuity of the fixed-point operator

Let
u^{k-1}\in V_{\mathcal{T}}^{n}
be given and let
R>0
,
\delta>0
. We set

Z_{R}=\big\{w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in V_{\mathcal{T}}^{n}:\|w\|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}<R\mbox{ for }i=1,\ldots,n\big\}

and define the mapping
F:Z_{R}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\theta n}
by

F(w)=w^{\varepsilon}
, where

\theta=\#{\mathcal{T}}
and
w^{\varepsilon}=(w_{1}^{\varepsilon},\ldots,w^{\varepsilon}_{n})
is the solution
to the linear regularized problem

(28)

\varepsilon\bigg(-\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\tau_{\sigma}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}w_{i}^{\varepsilon}+\mathfrak{m}(K)w_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}\bigg)=-\bigg(\frac{\mathfrak{m}(K)}{\Delta t}(u_{i,K}-u_{i,K}^{k-1})+\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\mathcal{F}_{i,K,\sigma}\bigg),

where
u_{i,K}:=\exp(w_{i,K})
and
\mathcal{F}_{i,K,\sigma}
is defined
as in (
18
) with
u_{i,K}^{k}
replaced by
u_{i,K}
.

To show that
F
is well defined,
we write (
28
) as

(29)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &Mw^{\varepsilon}=v,\quad\mbox{where }v=(v_{i,K})_{i=1,\ldots,n,\,K\in{\mathcal{T}}},\\
&v_{i,K}=\frac{\mathfrak{m}(K)}{\Delta t}(u_{i,K}-u_{i,K}^{k-1})+\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\mathcal{F}_{i,K,\sigma},\end{aligned}

and
M=\operatorname{diag}(M^{\prime},\ldots,M^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\theta n\times\theta n}

is a block diagonal matrix with
M^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\theta\times\theta}
, which has the entries

M^{\prime}_{K,K}=-\varepsilon\mathfrak{m}(K)-\varepsilon\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\tau_{\sigma},\quad M^{\prime}_{K,L}=\begin{cases}\varepsilon\tau_{\sigma}&\quad\mbox{if }K\cap L\neq\emptyset,\ \sigma=K|L,\\
0&\quad\mbox{if }K\cap L=\emptyset.\end{cases}

Therefore, the system
Mw^{\varepsilon}=v
can be decomposed into the independent subsystems

M^{\prime}w_{i}^{\varepsilon}=v_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Since
M^{\prime}
is strictly diagonally dominant,
these subsystems possess a unique solution
w_{i}^{\varepsilon}
. Then

w^{\varepsilon}=(w_{1}^{\varepsilon},\ldots,w_{n}^{\varepsilon})
is the unique solution to (
29
).
Thus, the mapping
F
is well defined.

Next, we prove that
F
is continuous. We multiply (
28
) for some
fixed
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
by
w_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}
and sum over all
i=1,\ldots,n

and
K\in{\mathcal{T}}
:

(30)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} -\varepsilon&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\tau_{\sigma}(\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}w_{i}^{\varepsilon})w_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\mathfrak{m}(K)(w_{i,K}^{\varepsilon})^{2}\\
&=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(K)}{\Delta t}(u_{i,K}-u_{i,K}^{k-1})w_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\mathcal{F}_{i,K,\sigma}w_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}.\end{aligned}

Using discrete integration by parts analogous to (
20
), we can rewrite the left-hand side as

\displaystyle-\varepsilon

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\tau_{\sigma}(\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}w_{i}^{\varepsilon})w_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\mathfrak{m}(K)(w_{i,K}^{\varepsilon})^{2}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\tau_{\sigma}(\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}w_{i}^{\varepsilon})^{2}+\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\mathfrak{m}(K)(w_{i,K}^{\varepsilon})^{2}=\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|w_{i}^{\varepsilon}\|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}^{2}.

We turn to the terms on the right-hand side of (
30
).
By definition, we have
\|w_{i}\|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}<R
and consequently

\|w_{i}\|_{0,\infty,{\mathcal{T}}}\leq C(R,{\mathcal{T}})
and
\|u_{i}\|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}\leq C(R,{\mathcal{T}})

(since the problem is finite-dimensional).
This shows that

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(K)}{\Delta t}(u_{i,K}-u_{i,K}^{k-1})w_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\Delta t}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|u_{i}-u_{i}^{k-1}\|_{0,2,{\mathcal{T}}}\|w_{i}^{\varepsilon}\|_{0,2,{\mathcal{T}}}

\displaystyle\leq C(R,{\mathcal{T}},\Delta t)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|w_{i}^{\varepsilon}\|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}.

Finally, using definition (
18
) of the flux and discrete integration
by parts,

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\mathcal{F}_{i,K,\sigma}w_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\tau_{\sigma}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{ij}u_{i,\sigma}(\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u_{j})+\eta^{\alpha}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u_{i}\bigg)w_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\tau_{\sigma}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{ij}u_{i,\sigma}(\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u_{j})(\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}w_{i}^{\varepsilon})+\eta^{\alpha}(\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u_{i})(\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}w_{i}^{\varepsilon})\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}}\|u_{i,\sigma}\|_{0,\infty,{\mathcal{T}}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}b_{ij}|u_{j}|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}|w_{i}^{\varepsilon}|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}+\eta^{\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|u_{i}|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}|w_{i}^{\varepsilon}|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}

\displaystyle\leq C(R,{\mathcal{T}})\|w_{i}^{\varepsilon}\|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}.

For the last inequality, we used the fact that
u_{i,\sigma}
depends on

u_{i,K}
and
u_{i,L}
for
\sigma=K|L
, and their discrete
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
norms can be
bounded by the discrete
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
norm of
w_{i}
, which in turn can be estimated by
C({\mathcal{T}})\|w_{i}\|_{0,\infty,{\mathcal{T}}}\leq C(R,{\mathcal{T}})
.

Inserting these estimates into (
30
) and dividing by
\|w_{i}^{\varepsilon}\|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}
if

\|w_{i}^{\varepsilon}\|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}>0
,
it follows that
\varepsilon\|w_{i}^{\varepsilon}\|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}\leq C(R,{\mathcal{T}},\Delta t)
. This bound allows us to
verify the continuity of
F
. Indeed, let
w^{\ell}\to w
as
\ell\to\infty
and
set
w^{\varepsilon,\ell}=F(w^{\ell})
. Then
(w^{\varepsilon,\ell})_{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is uniformly
bounded in the discrete
H^{1}(\Omega)
norm. Therefore, there exists a subsequence,
which is not relabeled, such that
w^{\varepsilon,\ell}\to w^{\varepsilon}
as
\ell\to\infty
.
Passing to the limit
\ell\to\infty
in scheme (
28
), we see that

w^{\varepsilon}
is a solution to the scheme and
w^{\varepsilon}=F(w)
.
Since the solution to the
linear scheme (
28
) is unique, the entire sequence
(w^{\varepsilon,\ell})_{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}}

converges to
w^{\varepsilon}
, which shows the continuity of
F
.

3.2.
Existence of a fixed point

We will now show that the map
F
admits a fixed point by using a topological degree
argument. We prove that
\operatorname{deg}(I-F,Z_{R},0)=1
, where deg is the
Brouwer topological degree
[
14
, Chap. 1]
.
Since deg is invariant by homotopy, it is sufficient to verify that any solution

(w^{\varepsilon},\rho)\in\overline{Z}_{R}\times[0,1]
to the fixed-point equation

w^{\varepsilon}=\rho F(w^{\varepsilon})
satisfies
(w^{\varepsilon},\rho)\not\in\partial Z_{R}\times[0,1]

for sufficiently large values of
R>0
. Let
(w^{\varepsilon},\rho)
be a fixed point.
The case
\rho=0
being clear, we assume that
\rho\neq 0
. Then
w_{i}^{\varepsilon}

solves

(31)

\varepsilon\bigg(-\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\tau_{\sigma}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}w_{i}^{\varepsilon}+\mathfrak{m}(K)w_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}\bigg)=-\rho\bigg(\frac{\mathfrak{m}(K)}{\Delta t}(u_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}-u_{i,K}^{k-1})+\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\mathcal{F}_{i,K,\sigma}^{\varepsilon}\bigg)

for
i=1,\ldots,n
and
K\in{\mathcal{T}}
, where
u_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}=\exp(w_{i,K}^{\varepsilon})
and

\mathcal{F}_{i,K,\sigma}^{\varepsilon}
is defined as in (
18
) with

u_{i,K}^{k}
replaced by
u_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}
.
The following inequality is the key argument.

Lemma 10

(Discrete Shannon entropy inequality)
.

Let
w^{\varepsilon}
be a solution to (
31
) and
u_{i}^{\varepsilon}:=\exp(w_{i}^{\varepsilon})
. Then

(32)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \rho H_{S}(u^{\varepsilon})&+\varepsilon\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|w_{i}^{\varepsilon}\|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}^{2}+\rho\Delta t\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\tau_{\sigma}b_{ij}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u^{\varepsilon}_{i}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u_{j}^{\varepsilon}\\
&{}+4\rho\eta^{\alpha}\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{n}|(u_{i}^{\varepsilon})^{1/2}|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}^{2}\leq\rho H_{S}(u^{k-1}).\end{aligned}

Proof.

We multiply (
31
) by
\Delta tw_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}
, sum over
i=1,\ldots,n

and
K\in{\mathcal{T}}
, and use discrete integration by parts (cf. (
20
)).
Then

\varepsilon\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|w_{i}^{\varepsilon}\|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}^{2}=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}
, where

\displaystyle I_{1}=-\rho\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\mathfrak{m}(K)(u_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}-u_{i,K}^{k-1})w_{i,K}^{\varepsilon},

\displaystyle I_{2}=-\rho\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\tau_{\sigma}u_{i,\sigma}^{\varepsilon}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}p_{i}(u^{\varepsilon})\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}w_{i,K}^{\varepsilon},

\displaystyle I_{3}=-\rho\eta^{\alpha}\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\tau_{\sigma}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u_{i}^{\varepsilon}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}w_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}.

The definition

u_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}=\exp(w_{i,K}^{\varepsilon})
and the convexity of the Shannon entropy imply that

I_{1}=-\rho\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\mathfrak{m}(K)(u_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}-u_{i,K}^{k-1})\log u_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}\leq-\rho\big(H_{S}(u^{\varepsilon})-H_{S}(u^{k-1})\big).

For
I_{2}
, we rely on inequality (
21
):

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=-\rho\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma=K|L\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\tau_{\sigma}u_{i,\sigma}^{\varepsilon}(p_{i}(u_{L}^{\varepsilon})-p_{i}(u_{K}^{\varepsilon}))(\log u_{i,L}^{\varepsilon}-\log u_{i,K}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq-\rho\Delta t\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma=K|L\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\tau_{\sigma}b_{ij}(u_{j,L}^{\varepsilon}-u_{j,K}^{\varepsilon})(u_{i,L}^{\varepsilon}-u_{i,K}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=-\rho\Delta t\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma=K|L\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\tau_{\sigma}b_{ij}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u_{i}^{\varepsilon}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u_{j}^{\varepsilon}.

Finally, using the elementary inequality

(a-b)(\log a-\log b)\geq 4(\sqrt{a}-\sqrt{b})^{2}
,

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=-\rho\eta^{\alpha}\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma=K|L\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\tau_{\sigma}(u_{i,L}^{\varepsilon}-u_{i,K}^{\varepsilon})(\log u_{i,L}^{\varepsilon}-\log u_{i,K}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq-4\rho\eta^{\alpha}\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma=K|L\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\tau_{\sigma}\big((u_{i,L}^{\varepsilon})^{1/2}-(u_{i,K}^{\varepsilon})^{1/2}\big)^{2}=-4\rho\eta^{\alpha}\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{n}|(u_{i}^{\varepsilon})^{1/2}|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}^{2}.

Combining these estimates finishes the proof of Lemma
10
.
∎

We now complete the topological degree argument. Lemma
10
implies that

\varepsilon\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|w_{i}^{\varepsilon}\|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}^{2}\leq\rho H_{S}(u^{k-1})\leq H_{S}(u^{k-1}).

With the choice
R:=(\varepsilon\Delta t)^{-1/2}H_{S}(u^{k-1})^{1/2}+1
we find that

w^{\varepsilon}\not\in\partial Z_{R}
and
\operatorname{deg}(I-F,Z_{R},0)=1
. We conclude that
F
possesses a fixed point.

3.3.
Limit
\varepsilon\to 0

By Lemma
10
, there exists
C>0
,
independent of
\varepsilon
, such that

C\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\mathfrak{m}(K)(u_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}-1)\leq H_{S}(u^{\varepsilon})\leq H_{S}(u^{k-1}).

This gives a uniform discrete
L^{1}(\Omega)
bound for
u_{i}^{\varepsilon}
.
There exists a subsequence
(not relabeled) such that
u_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}\to u_{i,K}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
for all

i=1,\ldots,n
and
K\in{\mathcal{T}}
. Moreover, the discrete
H^{1}(\Omega)
bound for

\sqrt{\varepsilon}w_{i}^{\varepsilon}
implies that
\varepsilon w_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}\to 0
for
i=1,\ldots,n

and
K\in{\mathcal{T}}
. Then the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
in (
31
) yields the existence
of a solution
u^{k}:=(u_{i,K})_{i=1,\ldots,n,\,K\in{\mathcal{T}}}
to (
17
).
Observing that

\displaystyle\sum_{{i,j=1}}^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}}\tau_{\sigma}b_{ij}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u_{i}^{\varepsilon}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u_{j}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}}\tau_{\sigma}(\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u^{\varepsilon})^{T}B(\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}}\tau_{\sigma}|B^{1/2}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u^{\varepsilon}|^{2}=|B^{1/2}u^{\varepsilon}|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}^{2},

the same limit in the regularized entropy inequality (
32
)
directly leads to the discrete entropy inequality (
25
).

3.4.
Discrete Rao entropy inequality

To verify (
26
), we multiply (
17
) by
\Delta tp_{i}(u^{k}_{K})
,
sum over
i=1,\ldots,n
and
K\in{\mathcal{T}}
, and use discrete integration by parts:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\mathfrak{m}(K)(u_{i,K}^{k}-u_{i,K}^{k-1})p_{i}(u^{k}_{K})

\displaystyle=\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\tau_{\sigma}\big(u_{i,\sigma}^{k}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}p_{i}(u^{k})+\eta^{\alpha}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u_{i}^{k}\big)p_{i}(u^{k}_{K})

\displaystyle=-\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\tau_{\sigma}u_{i,\sigma}^{k}(\mathrm{D}_{\sigma}p_{i}(u^{k}))^{2}-\eta^{\alpha}\Delta t\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\tau_{\sigma}b_{ij}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u_{i}^{k}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}u_{j}^{k}

\displaystyle=-\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\tau_{\sigma}u_{i,\sigma}^{k}(\mathrm{D}_{\sigma}p_{i}(u^{k}))^{2}-\eta^{\alpha}\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{n}|(B^{1/2}u^{k})_{i}|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}^{2}.

By the definition of
p_{i}(u^{k})
and the symmetry and positive semidefiniteness of

B
, the left-hand side becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\mathfrak{m}(K)(u_{i,K}^{k}-u_{i,K}^{k-1})p_{i}(u^{k}_{K})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\mathfrak{m}(K)b_{ij}(u_{i,K}^{k}-u_{i,K}^{k-1})u_{j,K}^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\mathfrak{m}(K)b_{ij}\big(u_{i,K}^{k}u_{j,K}^{k}-u_{i,K}^{k-1}u_{j,K}^{k-1}+(u_{i,K}^{k}-u_{i,K}^{k-1})(u_{j,K}^{k}-u_{j,K}^{k-1})\big)

\displaystyle\geq H_{R}(u^{k})-H_{R}(u^{k-1}).

We infer the monotonicity of
k\mapsto H_{R}(u^{k})
.
After summation over
k=1,\ldots,j
and a renaming of the indices
k
and
j
, this shows (
26
) and thus completes the proof of Theorem
4
.

3.5.
Positivity

Thanks to the artificial diffusion, the discrete solution
u_{i,K}^{k}
is positive for
i=1,\ldots,n
and
K\in{\mathcal{T}}
. Indeed, let
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
be fixed and assume that there exists
K\in{\mathcal{T}}
such that
u_{i,K}^{k}=0
. We infer from
I_{3}
in Section
3.2
that

\eta^{\alpha}(u_{i,L}^{\varepsilon}-u_{i,K}^{\varepsilon})(\log u_{i,L}^{\varepsilon}-\log u_{i,K}^{\varepsilon})\leq C(\Delta t,u^{0}),

where
L\in K
is a neighboring cell of
K
.
If
u_{i,L}^{k}>0
, the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
in the previous estimate leads to a contradiction since
\log u_{i,K}^{\varepsilon}
diverges. Therefore,
u_{i,L}^{k}=0
. Let
L^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{T}}
be a neighboring cell of
L
. Arguing in a similar way as before, it follows that
u_{i,L^{\prime}}^{k}=0
. Repeating this argument for all cells in
{\mathcal{T}}
, we find that
u_{i,K}^{k}=0
for all
K\in{\mathcal{T}}
. This implies that
\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\mathfrak{m}(K)u_{i,K}^{k}=0
and, by mass conservation,
\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\mathfrak{m}(K)u_{i,K}^{0}=0
, which contradicts the positivity of the
L^{1}(\Omega)
norm of
u^{0}
in Hypothesis (H1).

4.
Convergence

In this section, we prove Theorem
5
, that is, we show the asserted convergence of the numerical scheme.
Uniform estimates are derived from the entropy inequalities
(
25
) and (
26
). Lemma
16
in the appendix
shows that
|\widehat{u}^{k}|\leq\lambda^{-1}|B^{1/2}u^{k}|
, where we
recall that
\widehat{u}^{k}=P_{L^{\perp}}u^{k}
. Thus, we obtain a uniform estimate
for
\widehat{u}^{k}
in the seminorm
|\cdot|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}
.
Moreover, since
b_{ii}>0
and
b_{ij}\geq 0
for all
i,j
(cf. Hypothesis (H3)),
estimate (
26
) provides a uniform bound for
u^{k}
in the discrete

L^{2}(\Omega)
norm. Hence, there exists a constant
C>0

which is independent of
\eta=\max\{\Delta x,\Delta t\}
such that

(33)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N_{T}}\Delta t\big(\|\widehat{u}^{k}\|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}^{2}+\|B^{1/2}u^{k}\|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}^{2}\big)+\eta^{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{T}}\Delta t|(u^{k})^{1/2}|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C,

(34)

\displaystyle\max_{k=1,\ldots,N_{T}}\|u^{k}\|_{0,2,{\mathcal{T}}}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\tau_{\sigma}u_{i,\sigma}^{j}|\mathrm{D}_{\sigma}(Bu^{j})_{i}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C.

4.1.
Compactness properties

We first prove a full gradient bound with a negative power of
\eta
on the right-hand side.

Lemma 11
.

There exists
C=C(\zeta)>0
independent of
\eta
such that

\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Delta t|u_{i}^{k}|_{1,4/3,{\mathcal{T}}}^{2}\leq C\eta^{-\alpha},\quad\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Delta t|u_{i}^{k}|_{1,1,{\mathcal{T}}}^{2}\leq C\eta^{-\alpha}.

Proof.

By the mesh regularity (
14
) and property
(
15
),

(35)

\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\frac{\mathfrak{\widetilde{m}}(\sigma){\operatorname{d}}_{\sigma}}{\mathfrak{m}(K)}\leq\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\frac{\mathfrak{\widetilde{m}}(\sigma){\operatorname{d}}(x_{K},\sigma)}{\zeta\mathfrak{m}(K)}\leq\frac{d}{\zeta}.

This yields, using Hölder’s inequality and the

L^{2}(\Omega)
bound (
34
) for
u_{i}^{k}
,

\displaystyle|u_{i}^{k}|_{1,4/3,{\mathcal{T}}}^{4/3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\mathfrak{\widetilde{m}}(\sigma){\operatorname{d}}_{\sigma}\bigg|\frac{u_{i,L}^{k}-u_{i,K}^{k}}{{\operatorname{d}}_{\sigma}}\bigg|^{4/3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\mathfrak{\widetilde{m}}(\sigma){\operatorname{d}}_{\sigma}^{-1/3}\big|(u_{i,L}^{k})^{1/2}-(u_{i,K}^{k})^{1/2}\big|^{4/3}\big|(u_{i,L}^{k})^{1/2}+(u_{i,K}^{k})^{1/2}\big|^{4/3}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\mathfrak{\widetilde{m}}(\sigma){\operatorname{d}}_{\sigma}^{-1}\big((u_{i,L}^{k})^{1/2}-(u_{i,K}^{k})^{1/2}\big)^{2}\bigg)^{2/3}

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}\times\bigg(\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\mathfrak{\widetilde{m}}(\sigma){\operatorname{d}}_{\sigma}\big((u_{i,L}^{k})^{1/2}+(u_{i,K}^{k})^{1/2}\big)^{4}\bigg)^{1/3}

\displaystyle\leq C|(u_{i}^{k})^{1/2}|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}^{4/3}\bigg(\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\mathfrak{m}(K)(u_{i,K}^{k})^{2}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\frac{\mathfrak{\widetilde{m}}(\sigma){\operatorname{d}}_{\sigma}}{\mathfrak{m}(K)}\bigg)^{1/3}

\displaystyle\leq C(\zeta)|(u_{i}^{k})^{1/2}|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}}^{4/3}\|u_{i}^{k}\|_{0,2,{\mathcal{T}}}^{2/3}.

Taking the exponent
3/2
, multiplying by
\Delta t
, and summing
over
k=1,\ldots,N
proves the first inequality.
The second inequality follows along the same lines (or by Hölder’s inequality).
∎

Lemma 12
.

There exists
C=C(\zeta)>0
independent of
\eta
such that

\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Delta t\|u_{i,\sigma}^{k}(B\nabla^{\mathcal{D}}\widehat{u}^{k})_{i}\|_{0,4/3,{\mathcal{T}}}^{2}\leq C.

Proof.

We infer from the definition of the discrete gradient
and Hölder’s inequality that

(36)

\displaystyle\|

\displaystyle u_{i,\sigma}^{k}(B\nabla^{\mathcal{D}}\widehat{u}^{k})_{i}\|_{0,4/3,{\mathcal{T}}}^{4/3}=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{{\rm int},K}}\mathfrak{m}(T_{K,\sigma})(u_{i,\sigma}^{k})^{4/3}\bigg|\frac{\mathfrak{\widetilde{m}}(\sigma)}{\mathfrak{m}(T_{K,\sigma})}\mathrm{D}_{K,\sigma}(B\widehat{u}^{k})_{i}\bigg|^{4/3}

For the compactness argument, we need an estimate for the discrete time derivative, which is defined by

Lemma 13

(Discrete time derivative)
.

Proof.

\displaystyle\frac{\mathfrak{m}(K)}{\Delta t}(u_{i,K}^{k}-u_{i,K}^{k-1})\phi_{K}\bigg|

4.2.
Convergence of the scheme

We show that
\mu
is a dissipative measure-valued solution in the sense of Definition
1
satisfying (
9
). The proof adapts the strategy of
[
10
]
to the present situation, where only a weaker form of convergence is known to hold. Let
T\in(0,\infty)
, let

,

\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega\times[0,T))
, and let

\eta_{m}=\max\{\Delta x_{m},\Delta t_{m}\}
be small enough such that

\operatorname{supp}(\psi)\subset\{x\in\Omega:{\operatorname{d}}(x,\partial\Omega)>\eta_{m}\}\times[0,T)

\displaystyle F_{10}^{m}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u_{m,i}\partial_{t}\psi\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t-\int_{\Omega}u_{m,i}^{0}(x)\psi(x,0)\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle F_{20}^{m}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u_{m,i,\sigma}\nabla^{m}(B\widehat{u}_{m})_{i}\cdot\nabla\psi\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

The convergence results established above imply that, as

The limit in

is more involved.
First, Lemma
12
implies that the term

is weakly relatively compact in

and thus weakly convergent in
L^{1}(\Omega_{T})
along a subsequence. Second, we assert that

(39)

\displaystyle u_{m,\sigma}-u_{m}\to 0\quad\text{in }L^{1}(\Omega_{T})\text{ as }m\to\infty.

We proceed as in
[
43
, Section 4.2]
, but
since we cannot control the full
gradient, we need to rely on the artificial diffusion.
It follows from

that

\displaystyle\|u_{m,i,\sigma}^{k}-u_{m,i}^{k}\|_{0,1,{\mathcal{T}}_{m}}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{m}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{{\rm int},K}}\mathfrak{m}(T_{K,\sigma})|u_{m,i,\sigma}^{k}-u_{m,i,K}^{k}|

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{m}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{{\rm int},K}}\mathfrak{m}(T_{K,\sigma})|u_{m,i,L}^{k}-u_{m,i,K}^{k}|

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{\sigma=K|L\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}\mathfrak{m}(T_{K,\sigma})|u_{m,i,L}^{k}-u_{m,i,K}^{k}|

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{\sigma=K|L\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm int}}{\operatorname{d}}_{\sigma}^{2}\tau_{\sigma}|u_{m,i,L}^{k}-u_{m,i,K}^{k}|\leq C\eta_{m}|u_{m,i}^{k}|_{1,1,{\mathcal{T}}_{m}},

where the constant

may change from line to line.
We take the square, multiply by
\Delta t_{m}
, sum over

, and use Lemma

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N_{m}}\Delta t_{m}\|u_{m,i,\sigma}^{k}-u_{m,i}^{k}\|_{0,1,{\mathcal{T}}_{m}}^{2}\leq C\eta_{m}^{2-\alpha}.

As

. Hence,

strongly in

We note that, by interpolation, the strong convergence (
39

implies that

\displaystyle u_{m}-u_{m,\sigma}\to 0\quad\text{strongly in }L^{p}(\Omega_{T})\mbox{ for every }p<2.

We now assert that, as a consequence of (
39
), the sequence

\mu

are uniformly continuous.
Since
u_{m,i,\sigma}(B\nabla^{m}\widehat{u}_{m})_{i}

Remark 14

(Curved domains)
.

We claim that Theorems
4
and
5
also hold for curved Lipschitz domains
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
The triangulation then contains control volumes with curved segments that are part of
\partial\Omega
. The analysis of this section is still possible, since we consider no-flux boundary conditions and no boundary values need to be defined.
The analysis has to be adapted in two points. First, the convergence of the scheme is typically proved on polygonal meshes and the error between the curved cell and the polygonal cell (which is of order
(\Delta x)^{d+1}
) needs to be taken into account. Second, as the compactness of the approximate sequence has been established for polygonal domains
[
28
]
, the error between the approximate sequence and its extension by zero to the polygonal domain has to be estimated. In two space dimensions, it is of order
\Delta x
; see
[
42
, Prop. 4.14]
for details.
The drawback of this approach is that one has to perform numerical integrations over the curved elements, which may be cumbersome in particular in three space dimensions.

Figure 1.
Triangulation of a curved domain.

Here we report on the simple approach of
[
21
]
. The idea is to cover
\Omega
by additional control volumes and to estimate the integral error. To simplify the presentation, let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a sufficiently fine triangulation of
\Omega
into triangles.
To each cell with two vertices on
\partial\Omega
, we add the reflected triangle to the triangulation such that
\Omega\subset\cup_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}^{*}}K
, where
{\mathcal{T}}^{*}
consists of all cells
K\in{\mathcal{T}}
and the associated reflected cells
K_{r}
with nonempty intersection with
\Omega
; see Figure
1
. Denoting by
\omega_{r}=K_{r}\cap\Omega
if
K_{r}\cap\Omega\neq\emptyset
and
\omega_{c}=K\setminus\Omega
if
K_{r}\cap\Omega=\emptyset
, the domain splits into

\Omega=\Omega_{h}\cup\Omega_{r}\setminus\Omega_{c}:=\bigg(\bigcup_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}}K\bigg)\cup\bigg(\bigcup_{\omega_{r}}\omega_{r}\bigg)\setminus\bigg(\bigcup_{\omega_{c}}\omega_{c}\bigg).

We can perform the numerical analysis on
V_{{\mathcal{T}}^{*}}
as in Sections
3
and
4
. For the convergence of the scheme, we need to show that the difference of the integrals over
\Omega_{h}
and
\Omega
vanishes when
\eta_{m}\to 0
. The difference consists of two contributions: the integral over
\Omega_{r}
and the integral over
\Omega_{c}
. We illustrate the convergence for the integral

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\Omega_{r}}u_{m,i,\sigma}\nabla^{m}(B\widehat{u}_{m})_{i}\cdot\nabla\psi\mathrm{d}x\bigg|\leq C\sum_{\omega_{r}}\mathfrak{m}(\omega_{r})\|u_{m,i,\sigma}\|_{0,\infty,\omega_{r}}\|\nabla^{m}(B\widehat{u}_{m})_{i}\|_{0,\infty,\omega_{r}},

where
\psi
is a smooth test function.
By the inverse inequality
[
15
, Section 21.1]

\|v\|_{0,\infty,\omega_{r}}\leq\|v\|_{0,\infty,K_{r}}\leq C(\Delta x)^{-d/2}\|v\|_{0,2,K_{r}},

the bound
\mathfrak{m}(\omega_{r})\leq C(\Delta x)^{d+1}
(which is valid under certain regularity conditions on the mesh), and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\Omega_{r}}u_{m,i,\sigma}\nabla^{m}(B\widehat{u}_{m})_{i}\cdot\nabla\psi\mathrm{d}x\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta x\bigg(\sum_{K_{r}}\|u_{m,i,\sigma}\|_{0,2,K_{r}}^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\sum_{K_{r}}\|\nabla^{m}(B\widehat{u}_{m})_{i}\|_{0,2,K_{r}}^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta x\to 0\quad\mbox{as }\eta\to 0,

taking into account the uniform bounds from (
25
) and (
26
). In a similar way, the integral over
\Omega_{c}
tends to zero as
\eta\to 0
.

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\bigg\langle\mu_{x,\tau},\frac{1}{2}|B^{1/2}(p-\nabla\widehat{v}(x,\tau))|^{2}\bigg\rangle\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\tau\leq{\rm e}^{Ct}H_{\rm rel}(u^{\rm in}|v(0))=0,

where the last equality follows from
v(0)=u^{\rm in}
.
Hence,
\mu_{x,t}=\delta_{v(x,t)}\otimes\delta_{\nabla\widehat{v}(x,t)}
for a.e.
(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T)
, which finishes the proof of Theorem
7
.

6.
Long-time asymptotics

In this section, we prove Theorem
9
.
First, we verify that
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathsf{m}}\subset L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Indeed, if
v\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathsf{m}}
,
the vector
Bv
is constant and
\int_{\Omega}Bv\mathrm{d}x=B\mathsf{m}
,
which implies that
Bv=(B\mathsf{m})/|\Omega|
.
Since the entries of
B
and the components of
v
are nonnegative,

v_{i}\leq(B\mathsf{m})_{i}/(b_{ii}|\Omega|)
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
. This proves the
claim.

The entropy inequalities (
10
)–(
11
) and the bound

|\langle\mu,B^{1/2}p\rangle|^{2}\leq\langle\mu,|B^{1/2}p|^{2}\rangle
, which follows from Jensen’s inequality, show that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\nabla(B^{1/2}u)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\mathrm{d}t<\infty,\quad\sup_{0<t<\infty}\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}<\infty.

Thus, there exists a sequence
(t_{k})\subset(0,\infty)
with
t_{k}\to\infty

such that
u(t_{k})\rightharpoonup u^{*}
weakly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and

B^{1/2}u(t_{k})\to B^{1/2}u^{*}
strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
as
k\to\infty
.
Since
\int_{\Omega}u(t_{k})\mathrm{d}x=\mathsf{m}
and the sequence

(\nabla(B^{1/2}u(t_{k})))
converges to zero
in the
L^{2}(\Omega)
norm, we find that

\int_{\Omega}u^{*}\mathrm{d}x=\mathsf{m}
and
\nabla(B^{1/2}u^{*})=0
.
This implies that
u^{*}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathsf{m}}
.
Moreover, we deduce from the strong convergence that

\lim_{k\to\infty}H_{R}(u(t_{k})|u^{*})=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{k\to\infty}\|B^{1/2}(u(t_{k})-u^{*})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=0.

We assert that
t\mapsto H_{R}(u(t)|u^{*})
is nonincreasing for a.e.
t>0
. Indeed, we know from Section
4.3

that
t\mapsto H_{R}(u(t))
is nonincreasing. Furthermore, since

\int_{\Omega}u(t)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}u^{*}\mathrm{d}x
and
Bu^{*}
is a constant vector,
we have
\int_{\Omega}u(t)^{T}Bu^{*}\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}u(s)^{T}Bu^{*}\mathrm{d}x
for all
s,t\geq 0
. Hence, for
t\geq s
,

\displaystyle H_{R}(u(t)|u^{*})

\displaystyle=H_{R}(u(t))+H_{R}(u^{*})-\int_{\Omega}u(t)^{T}Bu^{*}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq H_{R}(u(s))+H_{R}(u^{*})-\int_{\Omega}u(s)^{T}Bu^{*}\mathrm{d}x=H_{R}(u(s)|u^{*}),

proving the claim.

We conclude that
H_{R}(u(t)|u^{*})\leq H_{R}(u(t_{k})|u^{*})\to 0
for
t\geq t_{k}\to\infty
.
It follows from the positive definiteness of
B^{1/2}
on
L^{\perp}
that

\|\widehat{u}(t)-\widehat{u}^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|B^{1/2}(\widehat{u}(t)-\widehat{u}^{*})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq 2H_{R}(u(t)|u^{*})\to 0

as
t\to\infty
. This finishes the proof of Theorem
9
.

Let the matrix

Lemma 16
.

Let
\lambda>0
be the smallest positive eigenvalue of

|P_{L^{\perp}}z|\leq\lambda^{-1}|B^{1/2}z|\quad\mbox{for }z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

∎

We introduce the relative entropy densities

\displaystyle h_{S}(u|v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\mathfrak{h}(u_{i})-\mathfrak{h}(v_{i})-\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}(v_{i})(u_{i}-v_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg(u_{i}\log\frac{u_{i}}{v_{i}}-(u_{i}-v_{i})\bigg),

\displaystyle h_{R}(u|v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(u-v)^{T}B(u-v)=\frac{1}{2}|B^{1/2}(u-v)|^{2},\quad u,v\in[0,\infty)^{n},

where
\mathfrak{h}(z)=z(\log z-1)+1
. We denote by
\|A\|_{2}
the norm of
A
induced by
the Euclidean norm
|\cdot|
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Lemma 17

(Coercivity)
.

Let
a_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\min_{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}b_{ii}>0
,

a_{1}=\|B\|_{2}
, and let
K\geq 1
. Then there exists a constant
c_{*}>0
, only depending on
a_{0}
,
a_{1}/a_{0}
,
and
M
, such that for all
u
,
v\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{n}
with
0<|v|\leq M
,

h_{S}(u|v)+h_{R}(u|v)\geq c_{*}|u-v|^{2}.

Proof.

By assumption, we have

\frac{1}{2}u^{T}Bu\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{ii}u_{i}^{2}\geq a_{0}|u|^{2}
for all

u\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{n}
. If
(a_{0}/2)|u|\geq a_{1}|v|
then

\displaystyle h_{R}(u|v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}u^{T}Bv-v^{T}Bu+\frac{1}{2}v^{T}Bv\geq a_{0}|u|^{2}-a_{1}|u||v|+a_{0}|v|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq a_{0}|u|^{2}-\frac{a_{0}}{2}|u|^{2}+a_{0}|v|^{2}=\frac{a_{0}}{2}|u|^{2}+a_{0}|v|^{2}\geq\frac{a_{0}}{3}|u-v|^{2}.

Next let
(a_{0}/2)|u|<a_{1}|v|
. We find for
f(z)=z\log z
that

\displaystyle u_{i}\log

\displaystyle\frac{u_{i}}{v_{i}}-(u_{i}-v_{i})=f(u_{i})-f(v_{i})-f^{\prime}(v_{i})(u_{i}-v_{i})

\displaystyle=(u_{i}-v_{i})\int_{0}^{1}\big(f^{\prime}(s(u_{i}-v_{i})+v_{i})-f^{\prime}(v_{i})\big)\big|_{s=0}^{\theta}\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=(u_{i}-v_{i})^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\theta}f^{\prime\prime}(s(u_{i}-v_{i})+v_{i})\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}\theta.

Then we infer from
|u_{i}/v_{i}|<2a_{1}/a_{0}

f^{\prime\prime}(s(u_{i}-v_{i})+v_{i})=\frac{1}{v_{i}(s(u_{i}/v_{i}-1)+1)}>\frac{1}{M(s(2a_{1}/a_{0}-1)+1)}

and consequently,

u_{i}\log\frac{u_{i}}{v_{i}}-(u_{i}-v_{i})\geq\frac{(u_{i}-v_{i})^{2}}{M}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\theta}\frac{\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}\theta}{s(2a_{1}/a_{0}-1)+1},

which shows that
h_{S}(u|v)\geq c_{1}|u-v|^{2}
, where

c_{1}=\frac{1}{M}\min_{i=1,\ldots,n}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\theta}\frac{\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}\theta}{s(2a_{1}/a_{0}-1)+1}.

Putting these estimates together and observing that
h_{S}(u|v)\geq 0
,
h_{R}(u|v)\geq 0
,
we conclude the proof with
c_{*}=\min\{a_{0}/3,c_{1}\}
.
∎

Appendix B
A discrete Aubin–Lions compactness result

We here summarize the compactness result
[
28
, Theorem 3.4]
, which is a basic ingredient in the proof of the convergence of our numerical approximation scheme (cf. Section
4
).
We focus on the specific functional setting that is needed for our purpose.
In this setting, the proof of
[
28
, Theorem 3.4]
relies on the following two key properties, whose validity has been verified in the proof of Proposition 9 of
[
36
, Section 6.1]
:

(P1)

Let
(v_{m})_{m}
be a sequence of functions with
v_{m}\in V_{{\mathcal{T}}_{m}}
for all
m
and such that
\sup_{m}\|v_{m}\|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}_{m}}<\infty
. Then there exists a function
v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that, along a subsequence,
v_{m}\to v
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

(P2)

If
v_{m}\to v
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\|v_{m}\|_{-1,4,{\mathcal{T}}_{m}}\to 0
, then
v\equiv 0
.

Recall that, for a given spatial mesh
{\mathcal{T}}
and a time step size
\Delta t
, the discrete function spaces
V_{\mathcal{T}}
and
V_{{\mathcal{T}},\Delta t}
were defined in Section
2.2
.
Thanks to (P1) and (P2), the specific version of Theorem 3.4 in
[
28
]
, which we rely upon, can be stated as follows.

Proposition 18

(Corollary of Theorem 3.4 in
[
28
]
)
.

Let
(\widehat{u}_{m})_{m}
be a sequence of functions such that
\widehat{u}_{m}\in V_{{\mathcal{T}}_{m},\Delta t_{m}}
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Suppose that there exists a finite constant
C>0
such that for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

\sum_{k=1}^{N_{m}}\Delta t_{m}\|\widehat{u}^{k}_{m}\|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}_{m}}^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{m}}\Delta t_{m}\|\partial_{t}^{\Delta t_{m}}\widehat{u}^{k}_{m}\|_{-1,4,{\mathcal{T}}_{m}}^{2}\leq C.

Then there exists
v\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
such that, after passing to a subsequence,

\widehat{u}_{m}\to v\text{ in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\;\;\text{as }m\to\infty.

The key point which this result addresses, as compared to more classical versions of the Aubin–Lions lemma, is its ability to handle a dependency of the spatial norms on the parameter
m
itself.
