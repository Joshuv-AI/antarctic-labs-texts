Jean-Daniel Boissonnat, Ramsay Dyer, Arijit Ghosh

Abstract
We present criteria for establishing a triangulation of a manifold. Given a manifold M M , a simplicial complex 𝒜 \mathcal{A} , and a map H H from the underlying space of 𝒜 \mathcal{A} to M M , our criteria are presented in local coordinate charts for M M , and ensure that H H is a homeomorphism. These criteria do not require a differentiable structure, or even an explicit metric on M M . No Delaunay property of 𝒜 \mathcal{A} is assumed. The result provides a triangulation guarantee for algorithms that construct a simplicial complex by working in local coordinate patches. Because the criteria are easily verified in such a setting, they are expected to be of general use.



1
Introduction

A
triangulation
of a manifold
M
is a homeomorphism

H\colon\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert\to M
, where
\mathcal{A}
is a simplicial
complex, and
\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert
is its
underlying topological space. If such a homeomorphism exists, we say
that
\mathcal{A}

triangulates

M
.

The purpose of this paper is to present criteria which ensure that a
candidate map
H
is indeed a homeomorphism. This work is motivated
by earlier investigations into the problem of algorithmically
constructing a complex that triangulates a given manifold

[
BG14
,
BDG17
]
. It complements and
is closely related to recent work that investigates a particular
natural example of such a map
[
DVW15
]
.

In the motivating algorithmic setting, we are given a compact manifold

M
, and a manifold simplicial complex
\mathcal{A}
is constructed by
working locally in Euclidean coordinate charts. Here we lay out criteria,
based on local properties that arise naturally in the
construction of
\mathcal{A}
, that guarantee that
H
is a homeomorphism.
These criteria, which are summarized in
Theorem
17
, are based on metric
properties of
H
within “compatible” coordinate charts
(Definition
4
). The Euclidean metric in the local
coordinate chart is central to the analysis, but no explicit metric on

\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert
or
M
is involved, and no explicit assumption
of differentiability is required of
H
or
M
. However, our only
examples that meet the required local criteria are in the
differentiable setting. We do not know whether or not our criteria for
homeomorphism implicitly imply that
M
admits a differentiable
structure. They do imply that
\mathcal{A}
is piecewise linear (admits an
atlas with piecewise linear transition functions).

Relation to other work

The first demonstrations that differentiable manifolds
can

always be triangulated were constructive. Cairns
[
Cai34
]

used coordinate charts to cover the manifold with embeddings of
patches of Euclidean triangulations. He showed that if the complexes
were sufficiently refined the embedding maps could be perturbed such
that they remain embeddings and the images of simplices coincide where
patches overlap. A global homeomorphic complex is obtained by
identifying simplices with the same image. The technique was later
refined and extended
[
Whi40
,
Mun68
]
, but it is not
easily adapted to provide triangulation guarantees for complexes
constructed by other algorithms.

An alternative approach was developed by Whitney
[
Whi57
]

using his result that a manifold can be embedded into Euclidean
space. A complex is constructed via a process involving the
intersection of the manifold with a fine Cartesian grid in the ambient
space, and it is shown that the
closest-point projection map
,
which takes a point in the complex to its unique closest point in the
manifold, is a homeomorphism. The argument is entwined with this
specific construction, and is not easily adapted to other settings.

More recently, Edelsbrunner and Shah
[
ES97
]

defined the restricted Delaunay complex of a subset
M
of Euclidean
space as the nerve of the Voronoi diagram on
M
when the ambient
Euclidean metric is used. They showed that if
M
is a compact
manifold, then the restricted Delaunay complex is homeomorphic to
M

when the Voronoi diagram satisfies the
closed ball property
(cbp)
: Voronoi faces are closed topological balls of the
appropriate dimension.

Using the cbp, Amenta and Bern
[
AB99
]

demonstrated a specific sampling density that is sufficient to
guarantee that the restricted Delaunay complex triangulates the
surface. However, since the complex constructed by their
reconstruction algorithm cannot be guaranteed to be exactly the
restricted Delaunay complex, a new argument establishing homeomorphism
was developed, together with a simplified version of the algorithm

[
ACDL02
]
.

Although it was established in the context of restricted Delaunay
triangulations, the cbp is an elegant topological result that
applies in more general contexts. For example, it has been used to
establish conditions for intrinsic Delaunay triangulations of surfaces

[
DZM08
]
, and Cheng et al.
[
CDR05
]
have indicated
how it can be applied for establishing weighted restricted Delaunay
triangulations of smooth submanifolds of arbitrary dimension in
Euclidean space.

However, the cbp is only applicable to Delaunay-like complexes that
can be realized as the nerve of some kind of Voronoi diagram on the
manifold. Thus, for example, it does not necessarily apply to the
tangential Delaunay complex constructed by Boissonnat and
Ghosh
[
BG14
]
. Secondly, even when a
Delaunay-like complex is being constructed, it can be
difficult to directly verify the properties of the associated Voronoi
structure; sampling criteria and conditions on the complex under
construction are desired, but may not be easy to obtain from the cbp.
A third deficiency of the cbp is that, although it can
establish that a complex
\mathcal{A}
triangulates the manifold
M
, it
does not provide a specific triangulation

H:\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert\to M
. Such a correspondence allows us to
compare
geometric
properties of
\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert
and
M
.

In
[
BG14
]
Whitney’s argument was adapted to
demonstrate that the closest-point projection maps the tangential
Delaunay complex homeomorphically onto the original manifold. The
argument is intricate, and like Whitney’s, is tailored to the specific
complex under consideration. In contrast, the result of

[
ACDL02
]
, especially in the formulation presented by
Boissonnat and Oudot
[
BO05
]
, guarantees a
triangulation of a surface by any complex which satisfies a few easily
verifiable properties. However, the argument relies heavily on the
the codimension being 1.

If a set of vertices is contained within a sufficiently small
neighbourhood on a Riemannian manifold, barycentric coordinates can be
defined. So there is a natural map from a Euclidean simplex of the
appropriate dimension to the manifold, assuming a correspondence
between the vertices of the simplex and those on the manifold. Thus
when a complex
\mathcal{A}
is appropriately defined with vertices on a
Riemannian manifold
M
, there is a natural
barycentric
coordinate map

\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert\to M
. In

[
DVW15
]
, conditions are presented which guarantee that
this map is a triangulation. Although this map is widely applicable,
the intrinsic criteria can be inconvenient, for example, in the
setting of Euclidean submanifold reconstruction, and furthermore the
closest-point projection map may be preferred for
triangulation in that setting.

The argument in
[
DVW15
]
is based on a general result

[
DVW15
, Proposition 16]
for establishing that a given
map is a triangulation of a differentiable manifold. However, the
criteria include a bound on the differential of the map, which is not
easy to obtain. The analysis required to show that the closest-point
projection map meets this bound is formidable, and this motivated the
current alternate approach. We have relaxed this constraint to a much
more easily verifiable bound on the metric distortion of the map when
viewed within a coordinate chart.

The sampling criteria for submanifolds imposed by our main result
applied to the closest-point projection map
(Theorem
39
) are the most relaxed that we are aware
of. The result could be applied to improve the sampling guarantees
of previous works, e.g.,
[
CDR05
,
BG14
]
.

In outline, the argument we develop here is the same as that of

[
ACDL02
]
, but extends the result to apply to abstract
manifolds of arbitrary dimension and submanifolds of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
of
arbitrary codimension. We first show that the map
H
is a local
homeomorphism, and thus a covering map, provided certain criteria are
met. Then injectivity is ensured when we can demonstrate that each
component of
M
contains a point
y
such that
H^{-1}(y)
is a
single point. A core technical lemma from Whitney
[
Whi57
, Appendix II
Lemma 15a]
still lies at the heart of our argument.

Outline

The demonstration is developed abstractly in Section
2
,
without explicitly defining the map
H
. We assume that it has already
been established that the restriction of
H
to any Euclidean simplex
in
\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert
is an embedding. This is a nontrivial step that
needs to be resolved from the specific properties of a particular
choice of
H
. The criteria for local homeomorphism apply in a common
coordinate chart (for
\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert
and
M
), and relate the
size and quality of the simplices with the metric distortion of
H
,
viewed in the coordinate domain. The requirement that leads to
injectivity is also expressed in a local coordinate chart; it
essentially demands that the images of vertices behave in a natural
and expected way.

In Section
4
, Theorem
17
is applied to the
specific case where
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and
H
is the projection to
the closest-point on
M
. This is a refinement of the argument
presented in
[
BG14
]
, also correcting an error.

In Appendix
C
the argument presented in

[
DVW15
]

is reviewed. This is very similar to the argument presented in
Section
2
, but it exploits a detailed analysis of
the differential of the map. Although it depends on differentiability,
and more analysis, an advantage of this approach is that the size of
the simplices is restricted by a bound that is linear with respect to
the simplex quality, instead of quadratic.
The main reason for reviewing this argument is that as a result of an
error in
[
DVW15
]
, the criteria in the announced
theorems there do not in fact ensure injectivity of the map. The
appendix illuminates the problem and corrected statements of those
results are presented in Section
C.1
.

2
The homeomorphism criteria

We assume that
\mathcal{A}
and
M
are both compact manifolds of
dimension
m
, without boundary, and we have a map

H\colon\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert\to M
that we wish to demonstrate is a
homeomorphism. We first show that
H
is a covering map, i.e., every

y\in M
admits an open neighbourhood
U_{y}
such that
H^{-1}(y)
is
a disjoint union of open sets each of which is mapped homeomorphically
onto
U_{y}
by
H
. In our setting it is sufficient to establish that

H
is a local homeomorphism whose image touches all components of

M
: Brouwer’s invariance of domain then ensures that
H
is
surjective, and, since
\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert
is compact, has the covering
map property.

Notation 1
(simplices and stars).

In this section, a simplex
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
will always be a
full
simplex
: a closed Euclidean simplex, specified by a set of
vertices together with all the points with nonnegative barycentric
coordinates. The
relative interior
of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is the
topological interior of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
considered as a subspace of its
affine hull, and is denoted by
\rinterior(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
. If
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is a
simplex of
\mathcal{A}
, the subcomplex consisting of all simplices that
have
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
as a face, together with the faces of these
simplices, is called the
star
of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, denoted
by
\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
; the star of a vertex
p
is
\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p)
.

We also sometimes use the
open star
of a simplex
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathcal{C}
. This is the union of the relative interiors of the
simplices in
\mathcal{C}
that have
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
as a face:

\mathrm{st}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\supseteq\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\rinterior(\boldsymbol{\tau})
. It is an open set in
\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert
,
and it is open in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
if
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\not\in\partial{\mathcal{C}}
.

Notation 2
(topology).

If
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, then the topological closure, interior, and
boundary of
A
are denoted respectively by
\overline{A}
,
\interior(A)
,
and
\partial{A}=\overline{A}\setminus\interior(A)
. We denote by

B_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(c,r)
the open ball in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
of radius
r
and centre
c
.

Notation 3
(linear algebra).

The Euclidean norm of
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is denoted by
\left|v\right|
, and

\left\|A\right\|=\sup_{\left|x\right|=1}\left|Ax\right|
denotes the operator norm of
the linear operator
A
.

We will work in local coordinate charts.
To any given map
G\colon\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
\mathcal{C}

is a simplicial complex, we associate a piecewise linear map
\widehat{G}

that agrees with
G
on the vertices of
\mathcal{C}
, and maps

x\in\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathcal{C}
to the point with the same barycentric
coordinates with respect to the images of the vertices. The map

\widehat{G}
is called the
secant map
of
G
with respect to

\mathcal{C}
.

The following definition provides the framework within which we will
work (see diagram (
2
)).

Definition 4
(compatible atlases).

We say that
\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert
and
M
have
compatible
atlases
for
H\colon\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert\to M
if:

(1)

There is a coordinate atlas
\{(U_{p},\phi_{p})\}_{p\in\mathcal{P}}
for

M
, where the index set
\mathcal{P}
is the set of vertices of

\mathcal{A}
and each set
U_{p}
is connected.

(2)

For each
p\in\mathcal{P}
, there is a subcomplex
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{p}
of

\mathcal{A}
that contains
\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p)
and

H(\lvert\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{p}\rvert)\subset U_{p}
. Also, the secant map of

\Phi_{p}:=\phi_{p}\circ H|_{\lvert\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{p}\rvert}
defines a
piecewise linear embedding of
\lvert\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{p}\rvert
into

\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We denote this secant map by
\widehat{\Phi}_{p}
. By definition,

\widehat{\Phi}_{p}
preserves the barycentric coordinates within each
simplex, and thus the collection

\{(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{p},\widehat{\Phi}_{p})\}_{p\in\mathcal{P}}
provides a piecewise linear
atlas for
\mathcal{A}
.

Remark 5
.

The requirement in Definition
4
that the local
patches
U_{p}
be connected implies that on each connected component

M^{\prime}
of
M
, there is a
p\in\mathcal{P}
such that
H(p)\in M^{\prime}
.

We let
\mathcal{C}_{p}=\widehat{\Phi}_{p}(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{p})
, and we will work within
the compatible local coordinate charts.
Thus we are studying a map of the form

F_{p}\colon\lvert\mathcal{C}_{p}\rvert\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m},

where
\mathcal{C}_{p}
is an
m
-manifold complex with boundary embedded in

\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and

F_{p}=\phi_{p}\circ H\circ\widehat{\Phi}_{p}^{-1},

(1)

as shown in the following diagram:

We
will focus on the map
F_{p}
, which can be considered as a local

H|_{\lvert\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{p}\rvert}
. By construction,
F_{p}

leaves the vertices of
\mathcal{C}_{p}
fixed: if
q\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a vertex
of
\mathcal{C}_{p}
, then
F_{p}(q)=q
, since
\widehat{\Phi}_{p}
coincides with

\phi_{p}\circ H
on vertices.

Remark 6
.

The setting described here conforms to the
paradigm laid out in the tangential complex work

[
BG14
]
. There one locally constructs a
(weighted) Delaunay triangulation in the tangent space

T_{p}M
. This gives us the local patch
\mathcal{C}_{p}
(the star of
p

in
T_{p}M
). The vertices of the constructed complex actually lie
on
M
, and we recognise
\mathcal{C}_{p}
as the orthogonal projection

\widehat{\Phi}_{p}
of the corresponding complex
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{p}
with
vertices on
M
.

In this context, the homeomorphism
H
that we are trying to
establish is given by the closest-point projection map onto
M
,
restricted to
\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert
. Now we are going to
work in local coordinate charts, given at each vertex
p\in\mathcal{P}

by the orthogonal projection
\phi_{p}
of some neighbourhood of
p
,

U_{p}\subset M
into
T_{p}M
. We recognise that
\widehat{\Phi}_{p}

really does coincide with the secant map of

\phi_{p}\circ H|_{|\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{p}|}
.

2.1
Local homeomorphism

Our goal is to ensure that there is some open

V_{p}\subset\lvert\mathcal{C}_{p}\rvert
such that
F_{p}|_{V_{p}}
is an
embedding and that the sets
\widetilde{V}_{p}=\widehat{\Phi}_{p}^{-1}(V_{p})
are
sufficiently large to cover
\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert
. This will imply that

H
is a local homeomorphism. Indeed, if
V_{p}
is embedded by
F_{p}
,
then
\widetilde{V}_{p}
is embedded by

H|_{\widetilde{V}_{p}}=\phi_{p}^{-1}\circ F_{p}\circ\widehat{\Phi}_{p}|_{\widetilde{V}_{p}}
, since
\phi_{p}
and
\widehat{\Phi}_{p}
are both embeddings.
Since
\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert
is compact, Brouwer’s invariance of domain,
together with
Remark
5
,
implies that
H
is surjective, and a covering map. It will only
remain to ensure that
H
is also injective.

We assume that we are given (i.e., we can establish by
context-dependent means) a couple of properties of
F_{p}
. We
assume that it is
simplexwise positive
, which means that
it is continuous, and its restriction to any

m
-simplex in
\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})
is an orientation preserving
topological embedding. As discussed in Appendix
A
, we
say that
F_{p}
preserves the orientation of an
m
-simplex

\boldsymbol{\sigma}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
if
F_{p}|_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
has degree 1 at any point
in the image of the interior of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, i.e.,

\deg(F_{p},\interior(\boldsymbol{\sigma}),y)=1
for
y\in F_{p}(\interior(\boldsymbol{\sigma}))
. (The
other assumption we make is that
F_{p}
, when restricted to an

m
-simplex does not distort distances very much, as discussed below.)

The local homeomorphism demonstration is based on
Lemma
7
below,
which is a particular case of an observation made by Whitney

[
Whi57
, Appendix II Lemma 15a]
. Whitney demonstrated a more
general result from elementary first principles. The proof we give
here is the same as Whitney’s, except that we exploit elementary
degree theory, as discussed in Appendix
A
, in order to
avoid the differentiability assumptions Whitney made.

In the statement of the lemma,
\mathcal{C}^{m-1}
refers to the

(m{-}1)
-skeleton
of
\mathcal{C}
: the subcomplex consisting of
simplices of dimension less than or equal to
m-1
. When

\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert
is a manifold with boundary, as in the lemma, then

\partial\mathcal{C}
is the subcomplex containing all
(m{-}1)
-simplices
that are the face of a single
m
-simplex, together with the faces of
these simplices.

Lemma 7
(simplexwise positive embedding).

Assume
\mathcal{C}
is an oriented
m
-manifold finite simplicial
complex with boundary embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Let

F\colon\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be simplexwise positive in

\mathcal{C}
. Suppose
V\subset\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert
is a connected open
set such that
F(V)\cap F(\lvert\partial{\mathcal{C}}\rvert)=\emptyset
. If
there is a
y\in F(V)\setminus F(\lvert\mathcal{C}^{m-1}\rvert)
such
that
F^{-1}(y)
is a single point, then the restriction of
F
to

V
is a topological embedding.

Proof.

Notice that the topological boundary of

\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is equal to the carrier of the
boundary complex (see, e.g.,
[
BDG13
, Lemmas 3.6,
3.7]
):

\partial{\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert}=\lvert\partial{\mathcal{C}}\rvert.

Let
\Omega=\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\setminus\lvert\partial{\mathcal{C}}\rvert
.
Since
F
is simplexwise positive, and
F(V)
lies within a
connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus F(\partial\Omega)
, the fact
that
F^{-1}(y)
is a single point implies that
F^{-1}(w)
is a
single point for any
w\in F(V)\setminus F(\lvert C^{m-1}\rvert)

(Lemma
49
).
We need to show that
F
is also injective on

V\cap\lvert\mathcal{C}^{m-1}\rvert
.

We now show that
F(\mathrm{st}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}))
is open for any

\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathcal{C}^{m-1}\setminus\partial{\mathcal{C}}
, where

\mathrm{st}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
is the open star of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, defined in
Notation
1
. Suppose
x\in\rinterior(\boldsymbol{\tau})
for
some
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\partial{\mathcal{C}}
. Since
F
is
injective when restricted to any simplex, we can find a sufficiently
small open (in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
) neighbourhood
U
of
F(x)
such that

U\cap F(\partial{\mathrm{st}(\boldsymbol{\tau})})=\emptyset
. Since the closure of
the open star is equal to the carrier of our usual star:

\overline{\mathrm{st}(\boldsymbol{\tau})}=\lvert\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\rvert,

Lemma
49
implies that every point in

U\setminus F(\lvert\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})^{m-1}\rvert)
has the same number
of points in its preimage. By the injectivity of
F
restricted to

m
-simplices, this number must be greater than zero for points near

F(x)
. It follows that
U\subseteq F(\mathrm{st}(\boldsymbol{\tau}))
.

If
x\in\mathrm{st}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
, then
x\in\rinterior(\boldsymbol{\tau})
for some

\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\partial{\mathcal{C}}
that has
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
as a
face. Since
\mathrm{st}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\subseteq\mathrm{st}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
, we have

U\subseteq F(\mathrm{st}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}))
, and we conclude that

F(\mathrm{st}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}))
is open.

Now, to see that
F
is injective on

\lvert\mathcal{C}^{m-1}\rvert\cap V
, suppose to the contrary that

w,z\in\lvert\mathcal{C}^{m-1}\rvert\cap V
are two distinct points such
that
F(w)=F(z)
. Since
F
is injective on each simplex, there are
distinct simplices
\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}
such that

w\in\rinterior(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
and
z\in\rinterior(\boldsymbol{\tau})
. So there is an
open neighbourhood
U
of
F(w)=F(z)
that is contained in

F(\mathrm{st}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}))\cap F(\mathrm{st}(\boldsymbol{\tau}))
.

We must have
\mathrm{st}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\cap\mathrm{st}(\boldsymbol{\tau})=\emptyset
,
because if
x\in\mathrm{st}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\cap\mathrm{st}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
, then

x\in\rinterior(\boldsymbol{\mu})
for some
\boldsymbol{\mu}
that has both
\boldsymbol{\sigma}

and
\boldsymbol{\tau}
as faces. But this means that both
w
and
z
belong
to
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, contradicting the injectivity of
F|_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
. It
follows that points in the nonempty set

U\setminus\lvert\mathcal{C}^{m-1}\rvert
have at least two points in
their preimage, a contradiction. Thus
F|_{V}
is injective, and
therefore, by Brouwer’s invariance of domain, it is an embedding.

Our strategy for employing
Lemma
7

is to demand that
the restriction of

F_{p}

to any
m
-simplex
has low metric distortion, and use this fact to ensure that the
image of
V_{p}\subset\lvert\mathcal{C}_{p}\rvert
is not intersected by the
image of the boundary of
\lvert\mathcal{C}_{p}\rvert
, i.e., we will establish
that
F_{p}(V_{p})\cap F_{p}(\lvert\partial{\mathcal{C}_{p}}\rvert)=\emptyset
. We need
to also establish that there is a point
y
in

F_{p}(V_{p})\setminus F_{p}(\lvert\mathcal{C}_{p}^{m-1}\rvert)

such that
F^{-1}(y)
is a single
point. The metric distortion bound will help us here as well.

Definition 8
(
\xi
-distortion map).

A map
F\colon U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a
\xi
-distortion
map
if for all
x,y\in U
we have

\bigl|\left|F(x)-F(y)\right|-\left|x-y\right|\bigr|\leq\xi\left|x-y\right|.

(3)

We are interested in
\xi
-distortion maps with small
\xi
.
Equation (
3
) can be equivalently written

(1-\xi)\left|x-y\right|\leq\left|F(x)-F(y)\right|\leq(1+\xi)\left|x-y\right|,

and it is clear that when
\xi<1
, a
\xi
-distortion map is a
bi-Lipschitz map. For our purposes the metric distortion constant

\xi
is more convenient than a bi-Lipschitz constant. It is easy to
show that if
F
is a
\xi
-distortion map, with
\xi<1
, then
F
is
a homeomorphism onto its image, and
F^{-1}
is a

\frac{\xi}{1-\xi}
-distortion map (see
Lemma
19
(1)).

Assuming that
F_{p}|_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
is a
\xi
-distortion map for each

m
-simplex
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathcal{C}_{p}
, we can bound how much it displaces
points. Specifically, for any point
x\in\lvert\mathcal{C}_{p}\rvert
, we
will bound
\left|x-F(x)\right|
. We exploit the fact that the
m+1

vertices of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
remain fixed, and use

trilateration
,
i.e., we use the estimates of the distances to the fixed vertices to
estimate the location of
F(x)
. Here, the quality of the simplex
comes into play.

Notation 9
(simplex quality).

If
p
is a vertex of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, the
altitude
of
p
is the
distance from
p
to the opposing facet of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
and is denoted

a_{p}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
.
The
thickness
of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, denoted

t(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
(or just
t
if there is no risk of confusion) is given
by
\frac{a}{mL}
, where
a=a(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
is the smallest altitude of

\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, and
L=L(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
is the length of the longest edge. We
set
t(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=1
if
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
has dimension
0
.

Lemma 10
(trilateration).

Suppose
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is an
m
-simplex, and

F\colon\boldsymbol{\sigma}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a
\xi
-distortion map that leaves the
vertices of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
fixed. If
\xi\leq 1
, then for any

x\in\boldsymbol{\sigma}
,

\displaystyle\left|x-F(x)\right|\leq\frac{3\xi L}{t},

where
L
is the length of the longest edge of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, and
t
is
its thickness.

Proof.

Let
\{p_{0},\ldots,p_{m}\}
be the vertices of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
.
For
x\in\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, let
\tilde{x}=F(x)
.

We choose
p_{0}
as the origin, and observe that

{p_{i}}^{\mathsf{T}}x=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left|x\right|^{2}+\left|p_{i}\right|^{2}-\left|x-p_{i}\right|^{2}\right),

(4)

which we write in matrix form as
{P}^{\mathsf{T}}x=b
, where
P
is the

m\times m
matrix whose
i
-th column is
p_{i}
, and
b
is the
vector whose
i
-th component is given by the right-hand side of
(
4
). Similarly, we have

{P}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{x}=\tilde{b}
with the obvious definition of

\tilde{b}
. Then

\tilde{x}-x=({P}^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}(\tilde{b}-b).

Since
F(p_{0})=p_{0}=0
, we have

\lvert{\left|\tilde{x}\right|-\left|x\right|}\rvert\leq\xi\left|x\right|
, and so

\lvert{\left|\tilde{x}\right|^{2}-\left|x\right|^{2}}\rvert\leq\xi(2+\xi)\left|x\right|^{2}\leq 3\xi L^{2}.

Similarly,

\lvert{\left|x-p_{i}\right|^{2}-\left|\tilde{x}-p_{i}\right|^{2}}\rvert<3\xi L^{2}
.
Thus
\lvert{\smash{\tilde{b}_{i}}-b_{i}}\rvert\leq 3\xi L^{2}
, and

\left|\smash{\tilde{b}}-b\right|\leq 3\sqrt{m}\xi L^{2}
.

By
[
BDG13
, Lemma 2.4]
we have
\left\|({P}^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}\right\|\leq(\sqrt{m}tL)^{-1}
, and
the stated bound follows.

2.1.1
Using
\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p)
as
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{p}

For the local complex
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{p}\subset\mathcal{A}
introduced in
Definition
4
, we now make a specific choice:

\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{p}=\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p)
. This is the smallest complex allowed by the
definition. For convenience, we define
\hat{p}=\widehat{\Phi}_{p}(p)
, so that

\mathcal{C}_{p}=\widehat{\Phi}_{p}(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{p})=\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})
.

Figure 1:
The open set
V_{p}
(shaded) is a homothetically shrunk copy
of the interior of
\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})
.

We define
V_{p}
to be the open set obtained by homothetically
“shrinking”
\lvert\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})\rvert
such that it is just large enough to
contain the barycentres of the simplices that have
\hat{p}
as a vertex
(see Figure
1
).
To be more specific we define
V_{p}
to be the open set consisting of
the points in
\lvert\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})\rvert
whose barycentric coordinate with
respect to
\hat{p}
is strictly larger than
\frac{1}{m+1}-\delta
, where

\delta>0
is arbitrarily small. Since the barycentric coordinates in
each
m
-simplex sum to 1, and the piecewise linear maps
\widehat{\Phi}_{p}

preserve barycentric coordinates, this ensures that the sets

\widehat{\Phi}_{p}^{-1}(V_{p})
cover
\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert
.

We assume that
F_{p}
is a
\xi
-distortion map on each simplex. The
idea is to show that
F_{p}
is an embedding on
V_{p}
. In order to
employ the simplexwise positive embedding lemma
(Lemma
7
), we need to establish that there is a
point in
V_{p}\setminus\lvert\mathcal{C}_{p}^{m-1}\rvert
that is not mapped
to the image of any other point in
\lvert\mathcal{C}_{p}\rvert
. We choose the
barycentre of a simplex for this purpose. We say that a simplicial
complex is a
pure

m
-dimensional simplicial complex if every
simplex is the face of an
m
-simplex.

Lemma 11
(a point covered once).

Suppose
\mathcal{C}
is a pure
m
-dimensional finite simplicial complex embedded
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and that for each
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathcal{C}
we have

t(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\geq t_{0}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathcal{C}
be an
m
-simplex with the largest diameter,
i.e.,
L(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\geq L(\boldsymbol{\tau})
for all
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{C}
, and let
b
be the barycentre of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
.
If
F\colon\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

leaves the vertices of
\mathcal{C}
fixed, and its restriction to any

m
-simplex in
\mathcal{C}
is a
\xi
-distortion map with

\xi\leq\frac{1}{6}\frac{m}{m+1}t_{0}^{2},

(5)

then
F^{-1}(F(b))=\{b\}
.

Proof.

Since
\xi<1
, the restriction of
F
to
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is
injective. Suppose
x\in\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert
is such that
F(x)=F(b)
, but

x\neq b
. Then
x

belongs to some
m
-simplex
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{C}
different from

\boldsymbol{\sigma}
. Since the distance from
b
to the boundary of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}

is

\frac{a(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}{m+1}=\frac{mt(\boldsymbol{\sigma})L(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}{m+1},

it follows that
\left|x-b\right|>mt(\boldsymbol{\sigma})L(\boldsymbol{\sigma})/(m+1)
.
But
using Lemma
10
and the constraint
(
5
) we arrive at a contradiction:

\left|x-b\right|\leq\left|b-F(b)\right|+\left|x-F(x)\right|\leq\frac{3\xi L(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}{t(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}+\frac{3\xi L(\boldsymbol{\tau})}{t(\boldsymbol{\tau})}\leq\frac{mt(\boldsymbol{\sigma})L(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}{m+1}.

Now we also need to ensure that

F_{p}(V_{p})\cap F_{p}(\lvert\partial{\mathcal{C}_{p}}\rvert)=\emptyset
. Here we
will explicitly use the assumption that
\mathcal{C}_{p}
is
\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})
. We
say that
\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})
is a
full star
if its carrier is an

m
-manifold with boundary and
\hat{p}
does not belong to

\partial{\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})}
.

Lemma 12
(barycentric boundary separation).

Suppose
\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})
is a full
m
-dimensional star embedded in

\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Let
a_{0}=\min_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})}a_{\hat{p}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
be the
smallest altitude of
\hat{p}
in the
m
-simplices in
\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})
. Suppose

x\in\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})
, where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is an
m
-simplex, and

\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\hat{p}}(x)
, the barycentric coordinate of
x
with
respect to
\hat{p}
in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, satisfies

\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\hat{p}}(x)\geq\alpha
. Then

d_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(x,\lvert\partial{\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})}\rvert)\geq\alpha a_{0}
.

If
t_{0}
is a lower bound on the thicknesses of the simplices in

\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})
, and
s_{0}
is a lower bound on their diameters, then

d_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(x,\lvert\partial{\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})}\rvert)\geq\alpha mt_{0}s_{0}
.

Proof.

Since we are interested in the distance to the boundary, consider a
point
y\in\lvert\partial{\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})}\rvert
such that the segment
[x,y]

lies in
\lvert\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})\rvert
. The segment passes through a sequence of

m
-simplices,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}
, that
partition it into subsegments
[x_{i},y_{i}]\subset\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}
with

x_{0}=x
,
y_{n}=y
and
x_{i}=y_{i-1}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

Observe that

\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i},\hat{p}}(x_{i})=\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i-1},\hat{p}}(y_{i-1})
, and
that

\left|x_{i}-y_{i}\right|\geq a_{\hat{p}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i})\lvert{\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i},\hat{p}}(x_{i})-\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i},\hat{p}}(y_{i})}\rvert.

Thus

\begin{split}\left|x-y\right|&=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left|x_{i}-y_{i}\right|\\
&\geq\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{\hat{p}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i})\lvert{\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i},\hat{p}}(x_{i})-\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i},\hat{p}}(y_{i})}\rvert\\
&\geq a_{0}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i},\hat{p}}(x_{i})-\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i},\hat{p}}(y_{i}))\\
&=a_{0}(\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\hat{p}}(x)-\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n},\hat{p}}(y))=a_{0}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\hat{p}}(x)\\
&\geq a_{0}\alpha.\end{split}

From the definition of thickness we find that
a_{0}\geq t_{0}ms_{0}
,
yielding the second statement of the lemma.

Lemma
12
allows us to quantify the
distortion bound that we need to ensure that the boundary of
\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})

does not get mapped by
F_{p}
into the image of the open set
V_{p}
. The
argument is the same as for Lemma
11
, but there we
were only concerned with the barycentre of the largest simplex, so the
relative sizes of the simplices were not relevant as they are here
(compare the bounds (
5
) and
(
6
)).

(boundary separation for

Suppose
\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})

\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and every

-simplex
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
in
\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})

. If the

-distortion map, with

\hat{p}
in a containing

-simplex strictly greater than

If

2.2
Injectivity

Having established that
H
is a covering map, to ensure that
H
is
injective it suffices to demonstrate that on each component of
M

there is a point with only a single point in its preimage. Injectivity
follows since the number of points in the preimage is locally constant
for covering maps.

Since each simplex is embedded by
H
, it is sufficient to show that
for each vertex
q\in\mathcal{P}
, if
H(q)\in H(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
, then
q
is a
vertex of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
. This ensures that
H^{-1}(H(q))=\{q\}
, and
by Remark
5
each component of
M
must
contain the image of a vertex.

In practice, we typically don’t obtain this condition directly. The
complex
\mathcal{A}
is
constructed
by means of the local patches

\mathcal{C}_{p}
, and it is with respect to these patches that the vertices
behave well.

Definition 15
(vertex sanity).

If
H\colon\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert\to M
has compatible atlases
(Definition
4
), then
H
exibits
vertex
sanity
if: for all vertices
p,q\in\mathcal{P}
, if

\phi_{p}\circ H(q)\in\lvert\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})\rvert=\widehat{\Phi}_{p}(\lvert\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p)\rvert)
, then
q
is a vertex of
\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p)
.

Together with the distortion bounds that are imposed on
F_{p}
,
Definition
15
ensures that the image of a vertex cannot
lie in the image of a simplex to which it does not belong:

Lemma 16
(injectivity).

If
H\colon\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert\to M
satisfies the hypotheses of
Lemma
14
as well as Definition
15
, then

H
is injective, and therefore a homeomorphism.

Proof.

Towards a contradiction, suppose that
H(q)\in H(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
and that

q
is not a vertex of the
m
-simplex
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
. This means there
is some
x\in\boldsymbol{\sigma}
such that
H(x)=H(q)
. Let
p
be a vertex
of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
. The vertex sanity hypothesis
(Definition
15
) implies that
\phi_{p}\circ H(q)
must
be either outside of
\lvert\underline{\mathrm{St}}(\hat{p})\rvert
, or belong to its boundary.
Thus Lemmas
12
and

10
, and the bound on
\xi
from

14
(3) imply that the barycentric coordinate of

with respect to
p
must be smaller than
\frac{1}{m+1}
: Let

, and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=\widehat{\Phi}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
.
Lemma

says that

\lvert{F_{p}(\hat{x})-\hat{x}}\rvert\leq\frac{3\xi L_{0}}{t_{0}}<\frac{ms_{0}t_{0}}{2(m+1)}\leq\frac{a_{0}}{2(m+1)},

where

\hat{p}
, as in

. Since

is
at least as far away from

as

,

implies that the barycentric

\hat{x}\in\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
with respect to
\hat{p}
must be
no larger than
\frac{1}{2(m+1)}
. Since
\widehat{\Phi}_{p}
preserves

of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, we conclude that all the barycentric coordinates of

in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
are strictly less than
\frac{1}{m+1}

3
Metric and differentiable distortion maps

In this short section we gather some useful lemmas on general
distortion maps, and on differentiable distortion maps.

3.1
Metric distortion maps

The definition of a distortion map (Definition
8
) makes
sense in a more general context: a
map
F:(X,d_{X})\to(Y,d_{Y})
between metric spaces is a

\xi
-distortion map
if

\lvert{d_{Y}(F(x),F(y))-d_{X}(x,y)}\rvert\leq\xi d_{X}(x,y)\quad\text{for
all }x,y\in X.

(7)

Lemma 19
(inverse and composition of distortion maps).

(1)

If
F:(X,d_{X})\to(Y,d_{Y})
is a

\xi
-distortion map with
\xi<1
, then
F^{-1}
is a

\frac{\xi}{1-\xi}
-distortion map.

(2)

Suppose
F_{i}:(X_{i},d_{X_{i}})\to(X_{i+1},d_{X_{i+1}})
,
1\leq i\leq k
, are
respectively
\xi_{i}
-distortion maps. Then

F_{k}\circ F_{k-1}\circ\cdots\circ F_{1}:X_{1}\to X_{k+1}

is a
\bigl(\sum_{W\in\wp(\{k\})}\prod_{i\in W}\xi_{i}\bigr)
-distortion map, where
\wp(\{k\})
is the set of nonempty
subsets of
\{1,\ldots,k\}
.

In particular,
F_{2}\circ F_{1}
is a

(\xi_{1}{+}\xi_{2}{+}\xi_{1}\xi_{2})
-distortion map.

Proof.

(1)  Let
u=F(x)
and
v=F(y)
. Then
(
7
) becomes

\lvert{d_{X}(F^{-1}(u),F^{-1}(v))-d_{Y}(u,v)}\rvert\leq\xi d_{X}(F^{-1}(u),F^{-1}(v)).

(8)

But it follows from (
8
) that

d_{X}(F^{-1}(u),F^{-1}(v))\leq\frac{1}{1-\xi}d_{Y}(u,v)
, and
plugging this back into (
8
) yields the result.

(2)  Using the observation that

d_{X_{2}}(F_{1}(x),F_{1}(y))\leq(1+\xi_{1})d_{X_{1}}(x,y),

we find

\begin{split}&\bigl\lvert{d_{X_{3}}(F_{2}\circ F_{1}(x),F_{2}\circ F_{1}(x))-d_{X_{1}}(x,y)}\bigr\rvert\\
&\phantom{d_{X_{3}}F_{2}\circ}\leq\bigl\lvert{d_{X_{3}}(F_{2}\circ F_{1}(x),F_{2}\circ F_{1}(x))-d_{X_{2}}(F_{1}(x),F_{1}(y))}\bigr\rvert\\
&\hskip 220.0pt+\bigl\lvert{d_{X_{2}}(F_{1}(x),F_{1}(y))-d_{X_{1}}(x,y)}\bigr\rvert\\
&\phantom{d_{X_{3}}F_{2}\circ}\leq\xi_{2}d_{X_{2}}(F_{1}(x),F_{1}(y))+\xi_{1}d_{X_{1}}(x,y)\\
&\phantom{d_{X_{3}}F_{2}\circ}\leq\xi_{2}(1+\xi_{1})d_{X_{1}}(x,y)+\xi_{1}d_{X_{1}}(x,y)\\
&\phantom{d_{X_{3}}F_{2}\circ}=(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}+\xi_{2}\xi_{1})d_{X_{1}}(x,y).\end{split}

This establishes the bound for the composition of two maps, but it
also serves as the inductive step for the general bound. If
G
is the

\eta
-distortion map defined by
G=F_{k-1}\circ\cdots\circ F_{1}
,
then
F_{k}\circ G
is a
(\xi_{k}{+}\eta{+}\xi_{k}\eta)
-distortion
map. Now notice that

\sum_{W\in\wp(\{k\})}\prod_{i\in W}\xi_{i}=\xi_{k}+\left(\sum_{W\in\wp(\{k-1\})}\prod_{i\in W}\xi_{i}\right)+\xi_{k}\left(\sum_{W\in\wp(\{k-1\})}\prod_{i\in W}\xi_{i}\right).

3.2
Differentiable distortion maps

Although the homeomorphism demonstration that yields
Theorem
17
makes no explicit requirement of
differentiability, it is convenient to exploit differentiability when
it is available. We collect here some observations relating metric
distortion and bounds on the differential of a map.

Recall that if
F\colon U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is differentiable,
then the
differential
of
F
at
x
is the linear map defined
by

dF_{x}(v)=\frac{d}{dt}F\circ\alpha(t)\big|_{t=0},

where
\alpha\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is any curve such that

\alpha(0)=x
and
\alpha^{\prime}(0)=v
, where
\alpha^{\prime}
is the derivative
with respect to
t
.

Bounds on the differential of
F
are closely related to the metric
distortion of
F
. If
A\colon U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a linear
map, we let
\left\|A\right\|
denote the operator norm:

\left\|A\right\|=\sup_{\left|v\right|=1}\left|Av\right|
. Associated with
A
are
m

nonnegative numbers called the
singular values
of
A
, denoted

s_{i}(A)
,
1\leq i\leq m
, ordered such that
s_{i}(A)\geq s_{j}(A)

if
i\leq j
. We only mention the singular values because they
provide a notational convenience. The largest singular value is
defined by
s_{1}(A)=\left\|A\right\|
, and the smallest singular value is

s_{m}(A)=\inf_{\left|v\right|=1}\left|Av\right|
.

Lemma 20
.

If
F\colon U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a differentiable

\xi
-distortion map, then

\lvert{s_{i}(dF_{x})-1}\rvert\leq\xi\quad\text{for all }x\in U\text{ and }1\leq i\leq m.

Proof.

For
x\in U
, and
v\in T_{x}\mathbb{R}^{m}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
\left|v\right|=1
, let

\alpha(t)=x+tv
. Since
F
is a
\xi
-distortion map, we have

(1-\xi)\left|(x+tv)-x\right|\leq\left|F(x+tv)-F(x)\right|\leq(1+\xi)\left|(x+tv)-x\right|,

so for all
t\neq 0
,

1-\xi\leq\frac{\left|F(x+tv)-F(x)\right|}{\lvert{t}\rvert}\leq 1+\xi.

Since
F
is differentiable,

\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\left|F(x+tv)-F(x)\right|}{\lvert{t}\rvert}=\left|\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{F(x+tv)-F(x)}{t}\right|=\left|dF_{x}(v)\right|,

so

1-\xi\leq\left|dF_{x}(v)\right|\leq 1+\xi,

which yields the claimed result.

So a bound on the metric distortion of a differentiable map directly
yields the same bound on the amount the singular values of the
differential can differ from
1
. We are interested in a converse
assertion: we want to bound the metric distortion of
F
, given a
bound on (the singular values of) its differential. This can only be
done with caveats.

Lemma 21
.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{\omega}
is a convex set,
\boldsymbol{\omega}\subseteq U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and

F\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a differentiable map such that

F(\boldsymbol{\omega})\subseteq V\subseteq F(U)
for some convex set
V
.
If
F
is injective, and

\lvert{s_{i}(dF_{x})-1}\rvert\leq\xi<1,\quad\text{for all }x\in U\text{ and }1\leq i\leq m,

then
F|_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
is a
\xi
-distortion map.

Proof.

Since
\boldsymbol{\omega}
is convex, the length of the image of the line segment
between
x
and
y
provides an upper bound on the distance between

F(x)
and
F(y)
, and this length can be bounded because of the
bound on
dF
:

\left|F(y)-F(x)\right|\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left|dF_{x+t(y-x)}(y-x)\right|\,dt\leq(1+\xi)\left|y-x\right|.

(9)

To get a lower bound we use the fact that injectivity and the bound
on the singular values imply that
F
is invertible. For any point

z=F(x)
we have

\left\|dF^{-1}_{z}\right\|=s_{m}(dF_{x})^{-1}\leq(1-\xi)^{-1}.

Since, for
x,y\in\boldsymbol{\omega}
the segment
[F(x),F(y)]
is contained in

F(U)
, we can use the same argument as in (
9
),
but using
F^{-1}
instead of
F
, so

\left|x-y\right|\leq(1-\xi)^{-1}\left|F(x)-F(y)\right|.

Therefore, combining with (
9
) we have

(1-\xi)\left|x-y\right|\leq\left|F(x)-F(y)\right|\leq(1+\xi)\left|x-y\right|.

4
Submanifolds of Euclidean space

As a specific application of Theorem
17
, we consider a
smooth (or at least
C^{2}
) compact
m
-dimensional submanifold of
Euclidean space:
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
. A simplicial complex
\mathcal{A}
is
built whose vertices are a finite set
\mathcal{P}
sampled from the manifold:

\mathcal{P}\subset M
. The motivating model for this setting is the
tangential Delaunay complex
[
BG14
]
. In that case

\mathcal{A}
is constructed as a subcomplex of a weighted Delaunay
triangulation of
\mathcal{P}
in the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, so it is necessarily
embedded. However, in general we do not need to assume
a priori

that
\mathcal{A}
is embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
(This does not force us to consider
\mathcal{A}
to be abstract in the
combinatorial sense. In particular, the simplices are Euclidean
simplices, not just sets of vertices.)
Instead, we assume only that the
embedding of the vertex set
\mathcal{P}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
defines an

immersion

\iota\colon\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
. By this we
mean that for any vertex
p\in\mathcal{P}
we have that the restriction of

\iota
to
\lvert\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p)\rvert
is an embedding.

At each point
x\in M
, the tangent space
T_{x}M\subset T_{x}\mathbb{R}^{N}

is naturally viewed as an
m
-dimensional affine flat in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, with
the vector-space structure defined by taking the distinguished point

x
as the origin. The maps involved in Theorem
17

will be defined by projection maps. The coordinate charts are defined
using the orthogonal projection
\mathrm{pr}_{\!T_{p}M}\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to T_{p}M.
As
discussed in Section
4.3
, for a sufficiently small
neighbourhood
U_{p}\subset M
, we obtain an embedding

\phi_{p}=\mathrm{pr}_{\!T_{p}M}|_{U_{p}}\colon U_{p}\subset M\to T_{p}M\cong\mathbb{R}^{m},

which will define our coordinate maps for
M
.

For the map
H\colon\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert\to M
, we will employ the
closest point projection map defined in Section
4.1
and
discussed further in
Section
4.4
.
There is an open
neighbourhood
U_{\!M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
of
M
on which each point
has a unique closest point on
M
, so the closest-point projection
map
\mathrm{pr}_{\!M}\colon U_{\!M}\to M
is well-defined. We define

H=\mathrm{pr}_{\!M}\circ\iota
.

As demanded by Definition
4
, for each
p\in\mathcal{P}

the coordinate map
\widehat{\Phi}_{p}
for
\mathcal{A}
is the secant map of

\phi_{p}\circ H
restricted to
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{p}=\lvert\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p)\rvert
, and
since
\mathrm{pr}_{\!T_{p}M}
is already a linear map, and
\mathrm{pr}_{\!M}
is the identity on the
vertices, this means

\widehat{\Phi}_{p}=\mathrm{pr}_{\!T_{p}M}\circ\iota|_{\lvert\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p)\rvert}
.

In Sections
4.1
and
4.2
we
review some of the geometric concepts and standard results that we
will use in the rest of the section.
In order to bound the metric
distortion of the maps
F_{p}=\phi_{p}\circ H\circ\widehat{\Phi}_{p}^{-1}
via
Lemma
19
, we are free to choose any convenient
metric on
M
. As is common in computational geometry, we employ
here the metric of the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, rather than the intrinsic
metric of geodesic distances.

4.1
Submanifold geometry

Since
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is compact, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
there is a
point
z\in M
that realizes the distance to
M
, i.e.,

\delta_{M}(x):=d_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(x,M):=\inf_{y\in M}d_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(x,y)=d_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(x,z).

The
medial axis
of
M
is the set of points

\mathop{\mathrm{ax}}\nolimits(M)\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
that have more than one such closest point on

M
. In other words, if
x\in\mathop{\mathrm{ax}}\nolimits(M)
, then an open ball

B_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(x,r)
, centred at
x
and of radius
r=\delta_{M}(x)
, will be
tangent to
M
at two or more distinct points. The
cut locus

of
M
is the closure of the medial axis, and is denoted

\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{ax}}\nolimits}(M)
.
The
reach
of
M
is defined by
\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(M):=d_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(M,\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{ax}}\nolimits}(M))
.
We observe below that
for compact
C^{2}
submanifolds,
\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(M)>0
.
Thus, by definition, every point
x
in the open
neighbourhood
U_{\!M}:=\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{ax}}\nolimits}(M)
of
M
has a unique
closest point
\check{x}\in M
. The
closest-point projection
map

\mathrm{pr}_{\!M}\colon U_{\!M}\to M,

takes
x
to this closest point:
\mathrm{pr}_{\!M}(x)=\check{x}
.

To each point
x\in M
, we associate a
normal space

N_{x}M=\{n\in T_{x}\mathbb{R}^{N}\mid n\boldsymbol{\cdot}v=0\ \forall v\in T_{x}M\}

of vectors orthogonal to
T_{x}M
. Thus

T_{x}\mathbb{R}^{N}=N_{x}M\oplus T_{x}M
. As with the tangent space, the
normal space at
x
is naturally identified with an affine flat in

\mathbb{R}^{N}
. It has dimension
k=N-m
and is orthogonal to
T_{x}M
.

The tubular neighbourhood theorem is a well known result in
differential topology. The statement presented here is adapted from

[
Fed59
, Theorem 4.8(13)]
, and the regularity assertions in
the second paragraph are demonstrated in
[
Foo84
]
.

Theorem 22
(tubular neighbourhood).

There is a natural structure on
U_{\!M}
given by partitioning it into
subsets of points that all project via
\mathrm{pr}_{\!M}
onto the same point
of
M
. This allows us to identify
U_{\!M}
as a portion of the

normal bundle
of
M
,

N\!M:=\{(x,n)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\mid x\in M,\ n\in N_{x}M\}.

The map
\psi:U_{\!M}\to N\!M
given by
x\mapsto(x,x-\mathrm{pr}_{\!M}(x))
is
a diffeomorphism onto its image, with inverse

\psi(U_{\!M})\subset N\!M\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
given by
(x,n)\mapsto x+n
.

If
M
is a
C^{j}
submanifold, then
\psi
is a
C^{j-1}

diffeomorphism onto its image
[
Foo84
]
, and the function

\delta_{M}
has is
C^{j}
on
U_{\!M}\setminus M
.

If
x\in U_{\!M}
, with
\check{x}=\mathrm{pr}_{\!M}(x)
, then the ball

B_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(x,r)
of radius
r=\left|x-\check{x}\right|
is tangent to
M
at

\check{x}
, and
(x-\check{x})\in N_{\check{x}}M
. For any point
y\in M
, the

local reach

[
AEM07
]
of
M
at
y
is

\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(y,M):=\sup\{r\in\mathbb{R}\mid(y+ru)\in U_{\!M}\ \forall u\in N_{y}M\text{ with }\left|u\right|=1\}.

By the tubular neighbourhood theorem,

\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(y,M)
can be equivalently defined as

\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(y,M)=\sup\{r\in\mathbb{R}\mid\mathrm{pr}_{\!M}(y+ru)=y\ \forall u\in N_{y}M\text{ with }\left|u\right|=1\}.

With this formulation, it is easy to see the following standard
observation:

Lemma 23
.

For any
y\in M
,
any open ball that is tangent to
M
at
y
and with radius

r\leq\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(y,M)
, does not intersect
M
.

This property is useful for bounding the extrinsic curvatures of

M
at
y
(see, e.g.,

[
BLW17a
, Lemma 3.3]
). However, it can be
awkward to work with the local reach since it is not known to be
continuous, even when
M
is smooth. The
local feature size

at
y\in M
is the distance from
y
to the medial axis;
equivalently, it is the supremum of the radii of balls centred at
y

and contained in
U_{\!M}

[
Fed59
, p. 432]
:

\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(y):=\sup\{r\mid B_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(y,r)\subset U_{\!M}\}.

Although it was introduced by Federer, the local feature size was
later rediscovered, and given its name, by Amenta and Bern

[
AB99
]
. Since the local reach at
y
is the distance to
the medial axis measured only in directions orthogonal to
M
at

y
, we have
\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(y,M)\geq\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(y)
. It is a short exercise to
show that the local feature size is 1-Lipschitz:

\lvert{\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(y)-\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(x)}\rvert\leq\left|x-y\right|,\quad\text{for all }x,y\in M.

The
reach

of
M
is the infimum of the local feature size, or equivalently,
the infimum of the local reach:

\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(M):=d_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(M,\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{ax}}\nolimits}(M))=\inf_{y\in M}\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(y)=\inf_{y\in M}\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(y,M).

The last equality comes from the observation that since
M
is
compact, there is some
y^{*}
for which
\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(y^{*})=\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(M)
, and we must
have
\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(y^{*},M)=\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(y^{*})
because
z-y^{*}
must lie in

N_{y^{*}}M
for all
z\in\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{ax}}\nolimits}(M)\cap\overline{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(y^{*},\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(y^{*}))
.

Observe also, that since, by the tubular neighbourhood theorem,

\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(x)>0
at any point
x\in M
, the continuity of the local
feature size implies that
\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(M)>0
for any compact
C^{2}
submanifold.

Remark 24
(locally bounding the local reach).

We will often need a lower bound
R_{\mathrm{rch}}
on
\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(x,M)
in a
neighbourhood of a point
p\in M
, and moreover, the
size

of this neighbourhood will depend on the size of the bound. For
example, we will have the following awkward self-referential
definition:
U_{p}=B_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(p,r)\cap M
, where
r\leq R_{\mathrm{rch}}

and
\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(x,M)\geq R_{\mathrm{rch}}
for all
x\in U_{p}
.

This can be easily resolved if we choose
R_{\mathrm{rch}}=\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(M)
, the global bound
on the local reach. However, the local reach could vary by orders of
magnitude over the manifold, making it inefficient to use a global
bound to govern the size of the simplices in the constructed
simplicial approximation.

The Lipschitz property of
\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits
makes it well-suited to bound the
local reach in a small neighbourhood of
p\in M
. For example,

\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(x,M)\geq\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(x)\geq(1-\epsilon)\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(p),\quad\text{for all }x\text{ with }\left|p-x\right|\leq\epsilon\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(p).

Thus we can choose
r=\epsilon\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(p)
and

R_{\mathrm{rch}}=(1-\epsilon)\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(p)
, and the criterion
r\leq R_{\mathrm{rch}}
is satisfied
provided
\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}
.

4.2
Affine flats and angles

The angle between two vectors
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}

is denoted
\angle(u,v)
(this angle is
\leq\pi
).
If
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a linear subspace,
\mathrm{pr}_{\!K}(u)
is the
orthogonal projection of into
K
. We define
\angle(u,K)
to be

\pi/2
if
\mathrm{pr}_{\!K}(u)=0
, and otherwise

\angle(u,K)=\angle(u,\mathrm{pr}_{\!K}(u))
(thus
\angle(u,K)\leq\pi/2
).
If
K
and
L
are two linear subspaces,
then

\angle(K,L)=\sup_{u\in K\setminus\{0\}}\angle(u,L).

The definition is only interesting when
\dim K\leq\dim L
.
Observe that if
\dim K=\dim L
, then
\angle(K,L)=\angle(L,K)
. If

K
and
L
are affine flats in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
then
\angle(K,L)
is the angle between the corresponding parallel
vector subspaces. If
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is a simplex with

\dim\boldsymbol{\sigma}\leq\dim L
, then

\angle(\boldsymbol{\sigma},L):=\angle(\mathrm{aff}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}),L)
, where
\mathrm{aff}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
is
the affine hull of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
. We denote the orthogonal complement of a
linear subspace
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
by
K^{\perp}
. A short exercise
yields the following observations:

Lemma 25
.

(1)

If
K,L
are subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, then

\angle(L^{\perp},K^{\perp})=\angle(K,L).

(2)

If
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a subspace of codimension 1, then

\angle(Q^{\perp},K)=\pi/2-\angle(K,Q).

The following standard observation
(cf.
[
Fed59
, Theorem 4.8(7)]
) follows
easily from Lemma
23
.

Lemma 26
.

Given any two points
x,y\in M\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we have

(1)

\sin\angle([x,y],T_{x}M)\leq\frac{\left|x-y\right|}{2\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(x,M)}
.

(2)

d_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(y,T_{x}M)\leq\frac{\left|x-y\right|^{2}}{2\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(x,M)}
.

The following lemma is a local adaptation of results presented in

[
BLW17a
]
:

Lemma 27
(tangent space variation).

Suppose
B_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(c,r)\subset U_{\!M}
,

x,y\in B=B_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(c,r)\cap M
and
\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(z,M)\geq R_{\mathrm{rch}}
for all
z
in
B
. If
r<R_{\mathrm{rch}}
, then

\sin\angle(T_{x}M,T_{y}M)\leq\frac{\left|y-x\right|}{R_{\mathrm{rch}}},

and

\angle(T_{x}M,T_{y}M)\leq\frac{\pi\left|y-x\right|}{2R_{\mathrm{rch}}}.

Proof.

See Appendix
B
for a sketch of how this result
follows from the arguments presented in

[
BLW17a
]
.

Remark 28
.

Since the bounds in Lemma
27
are vacuous if

\left|y-x\right|\geq R_{\mathrm{rch}}
, in practice we require that either

c=x
or
r<\frac{1}{2}R_{\mathrm{rch}}
.

We will need to bound the angle between a simplex with vertices on

M
and the nearby tangent spaces. To this end we employ a result
established by Whitney
[
Whi57
, p. 127]
in the formulation
presented in
[
BDG13
, Lemma 2.1]
:

Lemma 29
(Whitney’s angle bound).

Suppose
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is a
j
-simplex whose vertices all lie within a
distance
\eta
from a
k
-dimensional affine space,
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, with
k\geq j
. Then

\sin\angle(\boldsymbol{\sigma},K)\leq\frac{2\eta}{tL},

where
t
is the thickness of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
and
L
is the length of its
longest edge.

Lemma 30
(simplices lie close to
M
).

Let
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\subset U_{\!M}
be a simplex with vertices on
M
,
and
R_{\mathrm{rch}}
a constant such that
\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(\check{x},M)\geq R_{\mathrm{rch}}
for all

x\in\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, where
\check{x}=\mathrm{pr}_{\!M}(x)
. Then for all

x,y\in\boldsymbol{\sigma}
,

d_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(y,T_{\check{x}}M)<\frac{2L(\boldsymbol{\sigma})^{2}}{R_{\mathrm{rch}}},\quad\text{and in particular,}\quad\delta_{M}(x)<\frac{2L(\boldsymbol{\sigma})^{2}}{R_{\mathrm{rch}}}.

Proof.

Since
\check{x}
is the closest point on
M
to
x
, and every vertex
lies on
M
, we must have
\left|x-\check{x}\right|<L(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
,
and therefore, for any vertex
p
of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
,

\left|p-\check{x}\right|\leq\left|p-x\right|+\left|x-\check{x}\right|<2L(\boldsymbol{\sigma}).

(10)

By Lemma
26
(2) we have
d_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(p,T_{\check{x}}M)<2L^{2}/R_{\mathrm{rch}}
.
This is true for all vertices
p
of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, and since the
function
d_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(\,\cdot\,,T_{\check{x}}M)
is affine on
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
,
it is also true for any
y\in\boldsymbol{\sigma}
. The second inequality
follows by taking
y=x
, since

d_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(x,T_{\check{x}}M)=\left|x-\check{x}\right|.

Remark 31
.

If
p
is a vertex of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, then the constraint

\boldsymbol{\sigma}\subset U_{\!M}
of Lemma
30
is ensured
if
L(\boldsymbol{\sigma})<\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(p)
, since
\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits
is the distance to the
medial axis. In practice
R_{\mathrm{rch}}
is defined either in terms of

\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(M)
or in terms of
\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(p)
.

For example, since
\left|p-\check{x}\right|\leq 2L(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
, we have that

\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(\check{x})\geq\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(p)-2L
. So the requirements of
Lemma
30
are satisfied by demanding

L\leq\epsilon\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(p)
, with
\epsilon<{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}
, and setting

R_{\mathrm{rch}}=(1-2\epsilon)\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(p)
. Alternatively, we can simply demand

L<\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(M)
, and use
R_{\mathrm{rch}}=\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(M)
. Of course, other variations are
possible.

Lemma 32
(simplex-tangent space angle bounds).

Suppose
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a simplex of dimension
\leq m

with vertices on
M
. If
p
is a vertex of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
,
then

(1)

\displaystyle\sin\angle(\boldsymbol{\sigma},T_{p}M)\leq\frac{L}{t\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(p,M)}.

(2)

In addition, suppose
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\subset U_{\!M}
, and there is a
ball
B_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(c,r)\subset U_{\!M}
such that for any
x\in\boldsymbol{\sigma}
,

\check{x}=\mathrm{pr}_{\!M}(x)\in B=B_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(c,r)\cap M
and

\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(z,M)\geq R_{\mathrm{rch}}
for all
z\in B
. If
r<R_{\mathrm{rch}}
, then

\sin\angle(\boldsymbol{\sigma},T_{\check{x}}M)\leq\frac{3L}{tR_{\mathrm{rch}}}.

Proof.

(1)  By Lemma
26
(2), all the
vertices of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
are within a distance
\eta=L^{2}/(2\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(p,M))
from
T_{p}M
, and so
Lemma
29
ensures

\sin\angle(\boldsymbol{\sigma},T_{p}M)\leq\frac{2\eta}{tL}=\frac{L}{t\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(p,M)}.

(2)  By Lemma
27
and
(
10
),

\sin\angle(T_{p}M,T_{\check{x}}M)\leq\frac{\left|p-\check{x}\right|}{R_{\mathrm{rch}}}\leq\frac{2L}{R_{\mathrm{rch}}},

and the result follows using part 1.

4.3
Distortion of orthogonal projection:
\widehat{\Phi}_{p}
and
\phi_{p}

The coordinate maps
\phi_{p}
and
\widehat{\Phi}_{p}
are defined in terms of the
orthogonal projection to
T_{p}M
. The size of the neighbourhoods
used to define the coordinate charts are constrained by the
requirement that these maps be embeddings, which we establish by
ensuring that they are
\xi
-distortion maps with
\xi<1
.

Lemma 33
(definition and distortion of
\phi_{p}
).

Let
U_{p}=B_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(p,r)\cap M
, where
r=\rho R_{\mathrm{rch}}
, with

\rho<\frac{1}{2}
, and

\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(x,M)\geq R_{\mathrm{rch}}
for all
x\in U_{p}

(see
Remark
24
)
. Define
\phi_{p}:=\mathrm{pr}_{\!T_{p}M}|_{U_{p}}
. Then
\phi_{p}
is a
\xi
-distortion map with

\xi=4\rho^{2}.

Proof.

For any distinct
x,y\in U_{p}
, we have, from
Lemma
26
(1), that

\sin\angle([x,y],T_{x}M)\leq\rho,

and, from Lemma
27
, that

\sin\angle(T_{p}M,T_{x}M)\leq\rho.

Combining these bounds we have

\sin\angle([x,y],T_{p}M)\leq 2\rho.

Letting
\widetilde{x}=\phi_{p}(x)
,
\widetilde{y}=\phi_{p}(y)
, and
\theta=\angle([x,y],T_{p}M)
, we find

\begin{split}\left|x-y\right|-\left|\widetilde{x}-\widetilde{y}\right|&=(1-\cos\theta)\left|x-y\right|\\
&\leq(1-\sqrt{1-4\rho^{2}})\left|x-y\right|\\
&\leq 4\rho^{2}\left|x-y\right|.\end{split}

The result follows, since
\left|\widetilde{x}-\widetilde{y}\right|\leq\left|x-y\right|
.

Remark 34
(differential of
\phi_{p}
).

It straight forward to verify from the definitions that for any

x\in U_{p}
,

d(\mathrm{pr}_{\!T_{p}M}|_{M})_{x}=\mathrm{pr}_{\!T_{p}M}|_{T_{x}M}.

The domain of the map
\widehat{\Phi}_{p}
, i.e., an upper bound on the allowable
size of the simplices in
\iota(\lvert\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p)\rvert)
, is governed by
the following bound on the metric distortion of the projection from a
simplex.

Lemma 35
(simplexwise distortion of
\widehat{\Phi}_{p}
).

Suppose
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a simplex of dimension
\leq m

with vertices on
M
. If
p
is a vertex of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
,
and

L(\boldsymbol{\sigma})<t(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(p,M),

then the restriction of
\mathrm{pr}_{\!T_{p}M}
to
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is a

\xi
-distortion map with

\xi=\left(\frac{L(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}{t(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(p,M)}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in\boldsymbol{\sigma}
and set
\hat{x}=\mathrm{pr}_{\!T_{p}M}(x)
,

\hat{y}=\mathrm{pr}_{\!T_{p}M}(y)
.
By Lemma
32
(1),

\sin\angle(\boldsymbol{\sigma},T_{p}M)\leq\frac{L}{t\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(p,M)}.

So, putting

\theta=\angle([x,y],[\hat{x},\hat{y}])\leq\angle(\boldsymbol{\sigma},T_{p}M)
we find

\begin{split}\left|x-y\right|-\left|\hat{x}-\hat{y}\right|&=(1-\cos\theta)\left|x-y\right|\\
&\leq\left(1-\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{L}{t\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(p,M)}\right)^{2}}\right)\left|x-y\right|\\
&\leq\left(\frac{L}{t\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(p,M)}\right)^{2}\left|x-y\right|.\end{split}

The result follows since
\left|\hat{x}-\hat{y}\right|\leq\left|x-y\right|
.

Therefore, in order to use our framework, we need to ensure that for
each simplex
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathcal{A}
, the simplex

\iota(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfies
L<tR_{\mathrm{rch}}
, where

R_{\mathrm{rch}}\leq\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(p,M)
for each vertex
p
of
\iota(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
(we will
in fact need a stronger bound than this). Then,
in conformance with Section
2.1.1
, we set

\widehat{\Phi}_{p}=\mathrm{pr}_{\!T_{p}M}\circ\iota|_{\lvert\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p)\rvert}
, and we

require
that it be an embedding. Although we have established
that
\mathrm{pr}_{\!T_{p}M}
is an embedding on each simplex, and we have
assumed that
\iota|_{\lvert\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p)\rvert}
is an embedding, these
criteria do not imply that
\widehat{\Phi}_{p}
is an embedding. We leave this as
a requirement of the embedding theorem (requirement (a) of
Theorem
39
), i.e., something that needs to be
established in context. For the case of the tangential Delaunay
complex, the embedding follows naturally because

\phi_{p}(\lvert\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p)\rvert)
is seen as a weighted Delaunay
triangulation in
T_{p}M

[
BG14
]
.

4.4
Distortion of the closest-point projection map:
H

Recall that
H\colon\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert\to M
is the map that we
wish to show is a homeomorphism. In our current context
H
is based
on the closest-point projection map
\mathrm{pr}_{\!M}\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to M
. As
discussed at the beginning of this section, we define

H=\mathrm{pr}_{\!M}\circ\iota
, where
\iota\colon\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert\to\mathbb{R}^{N}

is the immersion of our simplicial complex into
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Once
H
is
shown to be a homeomorphism, it follows that
\iota
is in fact an
embedding, but we don’t assume this a priori. The metric on

\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert
(i.e., the edge lengths of the Euclidean simplices)
is
defined
by
\iota
, so
\iota
itself does not contribute to
the metric distortion of
H
.

An upper bound for the metric distortion of
\mathrm{pr}_{\!M}
was demonstrated
by
[
Fed59
, Theorem 4.8(8)]
; the proof we present here is
similar, but less general, since we require
M
to be a
differentiable submanifold:

Lemma 36
(upper bound for
\mathrm{pr}_{\!M}
distortion).

Let
x,y\in U_{\!M}
and
R_{\mathrm{rch}}=\min\{\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(\check{x},M),\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(\check{y},M)\}
,
where
\check{x}=\mathrm{pr}_{\!M}(x)
, and
\check{y}=\mathrm{pr}_{\!M}(y)
, as usual. If

a\geq\max\{\delta_{M}(x),\delta_{M}(y)\}
for some
a<R_{\mathrm{rch}}
, then

\left|\check{y}-\check{x}\right|\leq\left(1-\frac{a}{R_{\mathrm{rch}}}\right)^{-1}\left|y-x\right|.

(11)

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{x}
,
\widetilde{y}
be the orthogonal projection of
x
and
y
into
the line
\ell
generated by
\check{y}-\check{x}
. Then

\left|y-x\right|\geq\left|\widetilde{y}-\widetilde{x}\right|\geq\left|\check{y}-\check{x}\right|-\left|\check{y}-\widetilde{y}\right|-\left|\check{x}-\widetilde{x}\right|.

(12)

Let
Q_{x}
be the hyperplane through
\check{x}
and orthogonal to

[x,\check{x}]
. Then, using Lemma
25
(2),
we have

\left|\check{x}-\widetilde{x}\right|=\left|x-\check{x}\right|\cos\angle([x,\check{x}],\ell)=\left|x-\check{x}\right|\sin\angle(\ell,Q_{x}).

Since
T_{\check{x}}M\subseteq Q_{x}
, we have

\angle(\ell,Q_{x})\leq\angle(\ell,T_{\check{x}}M)
, and so by
Lemma
26
(1),

\left|\check{x}-\widetilde{x}\right|\leq\frac{\left|\check{y}-\check{x}\right|}{2\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(\check{x},M)}\left|x-\check{x}\right|\leq\frac{a}{2R_{\mathrm{rch}}}\left|\check{y}-\check{x}\right|.

Likewise,

\left|\check{y}-\widetilde{y}\right|\leq\frac{a}{2R_{\mathrm{rch}}}\left|\check{y}-\check{x}\right|.

Thus (
12
) yields

\left|y-x\right|\geq\left(1-\frac{a}{R_{\mathrm{rch}}}\right)\left|\check{y}-\check{x}\right|,

and hence the result.

Lemma 37
(simplexwise distortion of
H
).

Suppose
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\subset U_{\!M}
is a simplex of dimension
\leq m

whose vertices lie in
M
, and there is a ball
B_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(c,r)

such that for all
z\in\boldsymbol{\sigma}
,
\check{z}\in B=B_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(c,r)\cap M
, where
\check{z}=\mathrm{pr}_{\!M}(z)
. Let
R_{\mathrm{rch}}
be a lower bound on

\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(\widetilde{z},M)
for all
\widetilde{z}\in B
. If
r<R_{\mathrm{rch}}
, and

L(\boldsymbol{\sigma})<t(\boldsymbol{\sigma})R_{\mathrm{rch}}/3
, then
the restriction of
\mathrm{pr}_{\!M}
to
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is a
\xi
-distortion
map with

\xi=\frac{12L^{2}}{t^{2}R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}.

Proof.

By Lemma
30
,

\delta_{M}(x)<a=\frac{2L^{2}}{R_{\mathrm{rch}}}\quad\text{for any }x\in\boldsymbol{\sigma}.

(13)

Thus it follows from
Lemma
36
that

\left|\check{y}-\check{x}\right|\leq\left(1-\frac{2L^{2}}{R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}\right)^{-1}\left|y-x\right|\leq\left(1+\frac{4L^{2}}{R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}\right)\left|y-x\right|,

(14)

for any
x,y\in\boldsymbol{\sigma}
.

We now need to establish a lower bound on
\left|\check{y}-\check{x}\right|
. Let
Q_{x}

be the hyperplane through
\check{x}
and orthogonal to
[x,\check{x}]
, and let

\widehat{y}
and
\widehat{\check{y}}
be the orthogonal projection of
y
and
\check{y}
into

Q_{x}
. We have

\left|\check{y}-\check{x}\right|\geq\left|\widehat{\check{y}}-\check{x}\right|\geq\left|\widehat{y}-\check{x}\right|-\left|\widehat{\check{y}}-\widehat{y}\right|.

(15)

To get a lower bound on
\left|\widehat{y}-\check{x}\right|=\left|y-x\right|\cos\angle([x,y],Q_{x})
, notice that

\angle([x,y],Q_{x})\leq\angle(\mathrm{aff}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}),Q_{x})\leq\angle(\mathrm{aff}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}),T_{\check{x}}M),

and by Lemma
32
(2),

\sin\angle(\mathrm{aff}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}),T_{\check{x}}M)\leq\frac{3L}{tR_{\mathrm{rch}}}.

Thus,

\cos\angle([x,y],Q_{x})\geq\left(1-\left(\frac{3L}{tR_{\mathrm{rch}}}\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq\left(1-\frac{9L^{2}}{t^{2}R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}\right),

and so

\left|\widehat{y}-\check{x}\right|\geq\left(1-\frac{9L^{2}}{t^{2}R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}\right)\left|y-x\right|.

(16)

To get an upper bound on the second term on the right side of
(
15
), let
Q_{y}
be the hyperplane through
\check{y}

and orthogonal to
[\check{y},y]
. We have

\begin{split}\left|\widehat{\check{y}}-\widehat{y}\right|&=\left|\check{y}-y\right|\cos\angle([\check{y},y],Q_{x})\\
&=\left|\check{y}-y\right|\sin\angle(Q_{x},Q_{y})\quad\text{by Lemma~\ref{lem:angle.flat.complement}(2)}\\
&\leq\left|\check{y}-y\right|\sin\angle(T_{\check{x}},T_{\check{y}})\\
&\leq\left|\check{y}-y\right|\frac{\left|\check{y}-\check{x}\right|}{R_{\mathrm{rch}}}\quad\text{by Lemma~\ref{lem:tan.var}}\\
&\leq\frac{2L^{2}}{R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}\left|\check{y}-\check{x}\right|\quad\text{by \eqref{eq:splx.man.dist}}.\end{split}

Now, using (
14
), we have

\left|\widehat{\check{y}}-\widehat{y}\right|\leq\frac{2L^{2}}{R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}\left(1+\frac{4L^{2}}{R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}\right)\left|y-x\right|<\frac{3L^{2}}{R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}\left|y-x\right|,

(17)

since the hypothesis
3L/R_{\mathrm{rch}}<t
implies
4L^{2}/R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}<\frac{1}{2}
.

Finally, plugging (
16
) and
(
17
) back into (
15
), we get

\left|\check{y}-\check{x}\right|\geq\left(1-\frac{12L^{2}}{t^{2}R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}\right)\left|y-x\right|.

Comparing this lower bound with (
14
), we arrive
at the stated value for the metric distortion
\xi
.

4.5
Triangulation criteria for submanifolds

In order to employ Theorem
17
we first ensure that we
meet the compatible atlases criteria
(Definition
4
). In Lemma
33
we defined

U_{p}=B_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(p,r)\cap M,\quad\text{where }r=\rho R_{\mathrm{rch}}<{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}R_{\mathrm{rch}}.

We need to ensure that
H(\lvert\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p)\rvert)\subseteq U_{p}
. In our
context, this means that we require
\mathrm{pr}_{\!M}(\iota(\lvert\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p)\rvert))\subset U_{p}
, and
Lemma
30

ensures that if
L(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\leq L_{0}
for all
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\iota(\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p))
, then it is
sufficient to choose
r=L_{0}+2L_{0}^{2}/R_{\mathrm{rch}}
, or

\rho=\frac{L_{0}}{R_{\mathrm{rch}}}\left(1+\frac{2L_{0}}{R_{\mathrm{rch}}}\right).

(18)

Our bound on
L_{0}
itself will be much smaller than

R_{\mathrm{rch}}
, which in turn will be expressed in terms of
\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(p)
or

\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(M)
, so we will have
r<\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(p)
, and thus
B_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(p,r)\subset U_{\!M}
, ensuring also that each
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\iota(\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p))
also lies
in
U_{\!M}
.

We need to establish the metric distortion of

F_{p}=\phi_{p}\circ H\circ\widehat{\Phi}_{p}^{-1}
restricted to any

m
-simplex in
\widehat{\Phi}_{p}(\iota(\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p)))
, and ensure that it meets the
distortion control criterion of Theorem
17
.

Anticipating the bound we will need to meet the distortion-control
criterion of Theorem
17
, we impose the constraint

\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{t_{0}^{2}R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{16^{2}},

(19)

where
t_{0}
is a lower bound on the thickness:
t(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\geq t_{0}
for all
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\iota(\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p))
. We remark that
L_{0}

and
R_{\mathrm{rch}}
may be considered to be
local constants
, i.e., they
may depend on the vertex
p\in\mathcal{P}
, however
t_{0}
and the ratio

L_{0}/R_{\mathrm{rch}}
will be global constants.

Using (
19
) together with Lemmas

35
and
19
(1), we
can bound the metric distortion of
\widehat{\Phi}_{p}^{-1}
as

\xi_{1}=\frac{L^{2}}{t^{2}R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}\left(1-\frac{L^{2}}{t^{2}R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}\right)^{-1}\leq\left(\frac{16^{2}}{16^{2}-1}\right)\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{t_{0}^{2}R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}.

Lemma
37
gives us the distortion of
H
:

\xi_{2}=12\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{t_{0}^{2}R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}.

For
\phi_{p}
, using Lemma
33
and
(
18
) we get the distortion bound

\xi_{3}=4\rho^{2}=\frac{4L_{0}^{2}}{R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}\left(1+\frac{2L_{0}}{R_{\mathrm{rch}}}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{9}{2}\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{t_{0}^{2}R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}.

Lemma
19
(2) says that the distortion of
F_{p}
is
no more than

\xi=\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}+\xi_{3}+\xi_{1}\xi_{2}+\xi_{1}\xi_{3}+\xi_{2}\xi_{3}+\xi_{1}\xi_{2}\xi_{3}.

Using (
19
) we find that
F_{p}
is a
\xi
-distortion
map with

\xi=\frac{19L_{0}^{2}}{t_{0}^{2}R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}.

(20)

Observe that (
19
) implies that all the maps involved
have distortion less than 1.

Now we need to ensure that this bound meets the distortion-bound
requirement for Theorem
17
. We have chosen to use here
the properties of the Euclidean simplices in the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
but in Theorem
17
we are considering simplices in the
local coordinate space; for us these are the projected simplices, e.g.,

\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=\widehat{\Phi}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\mathrm{pr}_{\!T_{p}M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
. Using
Lemma
35
, the distortion properties of the
affine map
\mathrm{pr}_{\!T_{p}M}
imply

a(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\geq\left(1-\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{t_{0}^{2}R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}\right)a(\boldsymbol{\sigma}),\quad L(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\leq L(\boldsymbol{\sigma}),

and therefore

t(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\geq\left(1-\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{t_{0}^{2}R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}\right)t(\boldsymbol{\sigma}).

We can thus set

\hat{t}_{0}=\left(1-\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{t_{0}^{2}R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}\right)t_{0},\quad\hat{L}_{0}=L_{0},\quad\hat{s}_{0}=\left(1-\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{t_{0}^{2}R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}\right)s_{0},

where
s_{0}
is a lower bound for the diameters of the simplices
in
\iota(\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p))
.

Then, in order to meet the distortion control criterion of
Theorem
17
, we require

\frac{19L_{0}^{2}}{t_{0}^{2}R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}<\frac{\hat{s}_{0}\hat{t}_{0}^{2}}{12\hat{L}_{0}}=\left(1-\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{t_{0}^{2}R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}\right)^{3}\frac{s_{0}t_{0}^{2}}{12L_{0}}.

(21)

It is convenient to define
\mu_{0}=s_{0}/L_{0}
. Then, observing
that

\left(1-\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{t_{0}^{2}R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}\right)^{3}\geq\left(1-3\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{t_{0}^{2}R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}\right),

we see that (
21
) is satisfied if

L_{0}^{2}\leq\frac{\mu_{0}t_{0}^{4}R_{\mathrm{rch}}^{2}}{16^{2}}.

(22)

Now we consider
R_{\mathrm{rch}}
. For any
x\in U_{p}
, the Lipschitz
continuity of
\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits
ensures that
\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(x)>\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(p)-\rho R_{\mathrm{rch}}
,
where
\rho
is given by (
18
). Our
constraint (
22
) on
L_{0}
implies

\rho R_{\mathrm{rch}}\leq\frac{9}{8}L_{0}\leq\frac{9}{2^{7}}R_{\mathrm{rch}}.

(23)

Thus we need

R_{\mathrm{rch}}\leq\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(p)-\frac{9}{128}R_{\mathrm{rch}}
,
which is
satisfied by

R_{\mathrm{rch}}=\frac{128}{137}\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(p).

(24)

Of course, we can also choose
R_{\mathrm{rch}}=\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(M)
independent of

p
. Plugging these values back into (
22
) gives us
two alternatives for the bound on
L_{0}
:

L_{0}\leq\frac{\mu_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}t_{0}^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{lfs}}\nolimits(p)}{18}\quad\text{or}\quad L_{0}\leq\frac{\mu_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}t_{0}^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{rch}}\nolimits(M)}{16}.

(25)

We now only need to establish that
F_{p}
is simplexwise positive
to arrive at our triangulation theorem for submanifolds.

Lemma 38
.

F_{p}
is simplexwise positive on

\mathrm{pr}_{\!T_{p}M}(\iota(\lvert\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p)\rvert))
.

Proof.

Recall that
F_{p}=\phi_{p}\circ H\circ\widehat{\Phi}_{p}^{-1}
, and observe
that the restriction of
F_{p}
to an
m
-simplex

\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\in\mathrm{pr}_{\!T_{p}M}(\iota(\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p)))
is differentiable.
The bound (
22
) together with
(
20
) implies
F_{p}
is a
\xi
-distortion map with

\xi<1
, so
Lemma
20
ensures that for any

m
-simplex
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathrm{pr}_{\!T_{p}M}(\iota(\underline{\mathrm{St}}(p)))
, the
differential of
F_{p}
does not vanish on
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
.

We choose an orientation on
U_{p}
; thus we have an
orientation on each tangent space
T_{x}M
,
x\in U_{p}
. The
projection map
\mathrm{pr}_{\!T_{p}M}|_{T_{x}M}=d(\mathrm{pr}_{\!T_{p}M}|_{M})_{x}=d(\phi_{p})_{x}

(see Remark
34
) is then orientation preserving, because
of continuity: it is certainly true when
x=p
, and
Lemmas
33

and
20

imply that
the differential

Appendix A
Elementary degree theory

We recall here some basic ideas in degree theory. Our primary
motivation is to facilitate the statement and proof of
Lemma
7
, recovering what we need of a result of
Whitney
[
Whi57
, Appendix II Lemma 15a]
, without using
differentiability assumptions.

We are interested in the degree of continuous maps

F\colon\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is an open,
bounded, and nonempty domain in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, as can be found Chapter IV,
Sections 1 and 2 of
[
OR09
]
, for example.
As with most modern treatments of degree theory, Outerelo and Ruiz start by
defining the degree for a regular value of a differentiable map, where
the idea is transparent. For
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus F(\partial{\Omega})
,
the degree is defined as

\deg(F,\Omega,y):=\sum_{x\in F^{-1}(y)}\sgn_{x}(F),

where
\sgn_{x}(F)
denotes the sign (
\pm 1
) of the Jacobian determinant
(the determinant of the differential of
F
) at
x
. Thus the degree
at
y
counts the number of points in the preimage, accounting for the
local orientation of the map.

It is then shown that the degree is locally constant on the (open) set
of regular values, and after showing that it is also invariant under
homotopies that avoid conflicts between
y
and the image of the
boundary, the degree is defined for an arbitrary point in

\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus F(\partial{\Omega})
, and it is constant on each
connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus F(\partial{\Omega})
.

Then the definition of degree is extended to continuous maps
F\colon\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
by means of the Weierstrass approximation
theorem (here
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{\infty}
denotes the supremum norm):

Lemma 41
(
[
OR09
, Proposition and Definition IV.2.1]
).

Let
F\colon\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a continuous map, and let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus F(\partial{\Omega})
. Then there exists a smooth map

G:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
\left\|F-G\right\|_{\infty}<d_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(y,F(\partial{\Omega}))
. For all such
G
, the degree

\deg(G,\Omega,y)
is defined (
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus G(\partial{\Omega})
)
and is the same, and we define the degree of
F
by

\deg(F,\Omega,y)=\deg(G,\Omega,y).

Furthermore,
G
can be chosen such that
y
is a regular value of

G|_{\Omega}
, and then

\deg(F,\Omega,a)=\sum_{x\in G^{-1}(y)}\sgn_{x}(G).

The locally constant nature of the degree is the main property we wish
to exploit:

Lemma 42
(
[
OR09
, Proposition IV.2.3]
).

Let
F\colon\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a continuous map. Then the
degree
y\mapsto\deg(F,\Omega,y)
is constant on every connected
component of
\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus F(\partial{\Omega})
.

Notice that if
F
is a topological embedding, then

\deg(F,\Omega,y)=\pm 1
for any point
y\in F(\Omega)
. We will
also have occasion to use the following:

Lemma 43
(
[
OR09
, Corollary IV.2.5(3)]
).

Given a continuous mapping
F\colon\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, two
disjoint open subsets
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}\subset\Omega
, and a
point
y\not\in F(\overline{\Omega}\setminus(\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}))
,

\deg(F,\Omega,y)=\deg(F,\Omega_{1},y)+\deg(F,\Omega_{2},y).

Since no connectedness assumptions are made on the open sets in
question, a straightforward inductive argument allows us to strengthen
the statement of Lemma
43
:

Lemma 44
.

Suppose
\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{n}
are mutually disjoint open subsets of
the open domain

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
F\colon\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a
continuous map. If
y\not\in F(\overline{\Omega}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\Omega_{i})
, then

\deg(F,\Omega,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\deg(F,\Omega_{i},y).

m

Suppose

Suppose
