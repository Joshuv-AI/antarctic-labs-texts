Abstract
Given tensors 𝓐 , 𝓑 , 𝓒 \bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}} of size m × 1 × n m\times 1\times n , m × p × 1 m\times p\times 1 , and 1 × p × n 1\times p\times n , respectively, their Bhattacharya-Mesner (BM) product will result in a third-order tensor of dimension m × p × n m\times p\times n and BM-rank of 1 (Mesner and Bhattacharya, 1990). Thus, if an arbitrary m × p × n m\times p\times n third-order tensor can be written as a sum of a small number, relative to m , p , n m,p,n , of such BM-rank 1 terms, this BM-decomposition (BMD) offers an implicitly compressed representation of the tensor. In this paper, we first show that grayscale surveillance video can be accurately captured by a low BM-rank decomposition and give methods for efficiently computing this decomposition. To this end, we first give results that connect rank-revealing matrix factorizations to the BMD. Next, we present a generative model that illustrates that spatio-temporal video data can be expected to have low BM-rank. We combine these observations to derive a regularized alternating least squares (ALS) algorithm to compute an approximate BMD of the video tensor. The algorithm itself is highly parallelizable since the bulk of the computations break down into relatively small regularized least squares problems that can be solved independently. We study the ability of our algorithm to separate the video data into stationary and non-stationary components while simultaneously compressing the data. We then introduce a new type of BM-product suitable for color video and provide an algorithm that shows an impressive ability to extract important temporal information from color video while simultaneously compressing the data. Extensive numerical results compared against the state-of-the-art matrix-based DMD for surveillance video separation show our algorithms can consistently produce results with superior compression properties while simultaneously providing better separation of stationary and non-stationary features in the data.

In this paper, we offer the following contributions:

Overview of the BM-algebra

The BM-product on a third-order tensor triplet was first introduced by Mesner and Bhattacharya
[
34
,
35
]
and later generalized to tensors of arbitrary orders by Gnang and Filmus
[
12

13
]
. In this section, we will focus on the third-order case.

Definition 1
.

For a third-order conformable tensor triplet
\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times\ell\times n};\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times\ell}
and
\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times p\times n}
, the BM-product
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}={\mbox{\tt bmp}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times n}
is given entry-wise by

\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}_{i,j,k}=\sum_{1\leq t\leq\ell}\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}_{i,t,k}\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}_{i,j,t}\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}_{t,j,k}.

(1)

The BMP also conveniently expresses the notion of the BM-outer product. When
\ell=1

2

\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times 1\times n}

\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times 1}
, and a horizontal slice
\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times p\times n}
. Consequently, the BM-product
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}={\mbox{\tt bmp}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\right)
given in Eq. (

\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}=\sum_{1\leq t\leq\ell}{\mbox{\tt bmp}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}_{:,t,:},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}_{:,:,t},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}_{t,:,:}\right).

Remark 1
.

Every BM-product expresses a sum of BM-outer products and vice versa.

The BM-outer product in Eq.(
2
) induces a natural notion of the tensor BM-rank.

Definition 2
(BM-rank
[
13
]
).

The BM-rank of
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times n}
is the minimum number of the BM-outer products of conformable matrix slices that sum up to
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
.

Tensor transpose was introduced analogous to matrix transpose
[
12
,
13
]
.

Definition 3
.

Suppose
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
is a third-order tensor of size
m\times p\times n
, then
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
has the following transpose operations which are given by cyclic permutations of the indices of each entry:

\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\top}_{i,j,k}=\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}_{k,i,j},\quad\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n\times m};\qquad\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\top^{2}}_{i,j,k}=\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}_{j,k,i},\quad\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\top^{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m\times p}.

Note that in
Matlab
, we can conveniently perform the transpose by the command
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\top}={\mbox{\tt permute}}(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}},[2,3,1])
.
Then
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\top^{2}}={\mbox{\tt permute}}(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}},[3,1,2])
and

\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\top^{3}}={\mbox{\tt permute}}(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}},[1,2,3])=\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}.

When
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
is a BM-product of tensors
\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}
and
\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}
, then the transpose of the BM-product is a BM-product of transposes such that

\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\top}={\mbox{\tt bmp}}(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}})^{\top}={\mbox{\tt bmp}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}^{\top},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{\top},\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}^{\top}\right).

Moreover, the BM-rank of a third-order tensor is equal to the BM-rank of its transpose.

3
Matrix-to-BMD Connections

Two primary goals for this paper are to show that video can be accurately captured by a low BM-rank decomposition, and to give methods for efficiently computing this decomposition. A first step in this process is to give results that connect rank-revealing matrix factorizations to the BMD. We will then use these results both in analyzing our generative model for surveillance video and as starting guesses for our iterative methods.

3.1
Matrix based decomposition

Let
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times n}
. Construct a matrix
{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn\times p}
such that
{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{:,j}={\mbox{\tt vec}}(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}_{:,j,:})
.
In the context of video data, the
j

{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}
corresponds to the vectorized set of pixels at the
j

Our first result shows that we can rewrite this matrix decomposition as a BMD:

Theorem 4
.

Suppose
{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}
has the following decomposition
{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}={\bm{\mathbf{{U}}}}{\bm{\mathbf{{V}}}}^{\top}
, where
{\bm{\mathbf{{U}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn\times\ell}
and
{\bm{\mathbf{{V}}}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times p}
. The matrix decomposition of
{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}
can be represented by a tensor BMD of
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times n}

Proof.

Define
\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}_{:,t,:}={\mbox{\tt reshape}}({\bm{\mathbf{{U}}}}_{:,t},[m,n])
for all
1\leq t\leq\ell
, and
\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}_{:,:,k}={\bm{\mathbf{{V}}}}^{\top}
for all
1\leq k\leq n
. Let
\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}={\mbox{\tt ones}}([m,p,\ell])
. Then
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}={\mbox{\tt bmp}}(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}})

Note we can bound the BM rank of
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
in terms of ranks of the data arranged as one of three matrices, where the three matrices come from unfolding each of
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}},\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\top},\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\top^{2}}.

Corollary 5
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
4
, the BM-rank of
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
is bounded above by the minimum rank of the unfoldings of
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}},\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\top},\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\top^{2}}

So the matrix decomposition can be viewed as a BMD with maximum rank of
\ell

However, the converse is generally not true: given a rank-

not just a rearrangement of a low-rank matrix decomposition of the data set. If the data can be approximated by a tensor of small BM rank relative to the dimensions, it offers the opportunity for a fundamentally new way of compressing the data.

4
Background on Video Surveillance Separation and Modeling

The task of video background and foreground separation is an important computer vision application
[
4
,
29
,
43
,
10
]
. Background subtraction is often used in this task in order to detect moving foreground objects. Hence, accurate modeling of the video background under complex, diverse, and cluttered conditions is of paramount importance for most of the background/foreground separation methods. One of the more promising research directions in the field focuses on utilizing matrix decomposition methods. By vectorizing video frames into vectors and stacking them column-wise into a matrix, decomposition methods such as the robust principal component analysis (RPCA)
[
4
]
, and the dynamic mode decomposition (DMD)
[
27
]
method can be used to separate the video data matrix into a stationary background and moving foreground components which are usually assumed to be low-rank and sparse respectively.

However, flattening three-dimensional video data into a matrix presents disadvantages. For instance, vectorization destroys the intrinsic spatial structure within frames. Moreover, complex disturbances within the background can be ignored after flattening, which would potentially lead to a lower background reconstruction quality
[
31
]
. To overcome these difficulties for matrix-based methods, an increasing number of tensor decomposition methods have been proposed dealing with background subtraction
[
31
,
21
,
19
,
40
]
. Similar to matrix-based methods, the tensor-based techniques model the background video with a low tensor rank component and obtain the sparse foreground by subtracting the background from the video data. Although for most of the aforementioned methods the low-rank property of the background suggests that it is also possible to compress the stationary background for more efficient data storage, the step of background subtraction does not guarantee a compressed representation of the foreground video.

4.1
Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD)

Though initially introduced in the fluid dynamics community for extracting spatiotemporal patterns
[
27
]
, DMD has also been effective in the video context for separating the stationary background from foreground motions by differentiating between the DMD modes with near-zero frequency and remaining modes with frequencies bounded away from the origin
[
17
]
. Better visual results and superior computational efficiency compared to the Robust-PCA algorithm of the DMD method have drawn great attention in the computer vision community with new algorithms developed based on DMD for improved accuracy and efficiency. A few examples include the compressed DMD
[
8
]
, randomized DMD
[
9
]
, DMD via dictionary learning
[
18
]
, and multi-resolution DMD for object tracking with varying motion rates
[
28
]
.

More recently, the connection between the general DMD method and the CP decomposition has been studied
[
39
]
. When multiple experiments were conducted, data matrices generated from the experiments were collected and ordered as frontal slices of a third-order tensor. Then performing the DMD on each frontal slice of the data tensor decomposes it into a sum of vector outer products of the DMD vector triplets consisting of the DMD modes, DMD eigenfunctions, and the corresponding eigenvalues. It is easily shown that this decomposition is equivalent to taking a CP decomposition of the third-order tensor when the experimental data are collected from distinct sources with a single exponential growth or decay and/or oscillatory dynamics.

More specifically, for the video background/foreground separation application

{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{\text{DMD}}^{\text{Low-Rank}}=b_{p}{\bm{\mathbf{{\varphi}}}}_{p}e^{\omega_{p}{\bm{\mathbf{{t}}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn\times 1},

(3)

In the present work, we can view the DMD results obtained from decomposing a single data matrix as a tensor BM decomposition by Theorem

5
Generative Model for Surveillance Video

In this section, we consider a BM product-based model of one or two moving objects on a static background.
Since in our model the stationary background image and the moving foreground object can be incorporated into separate BM-rank 1 components, a low BM-rank tensor decomposition separates the video data into compressed representations of both the background and the foreground simultaneously.

5.1
Generative Spatiotemporal Model With Low BM-rank

Suppose we have a static background image
{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

\alpha

0,\text{ otherwise }\\
\end{cases}.

(6)

5.2
SS-SVD - BMD Connection for Our Generative Model

We are now in a position, using the generative model, to explain why the frontal slices of the surveillance video will have low (matrix) rank and connect our BMD-based model to the SS-SVD.
Consider our generative model in which only a single pixel-size object is traversing from left to right in the
i
-th row of the background image
{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}
. This video tensor
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
will have a BM-rank of two. Each frame is a
lateral
slice
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}_{:,j,:}
. Then it is easy to see that the rank of each
frontal
slice
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}_{:,:,k}
is either 1 or 2, because
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}_{:,j,k}
is either
{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{:,k}
(if no object is present at time
j
on the
k
-th column of the background image) or

{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{:,k}+(-{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{i,k}+\alpha){\bm{\mathbf{{e}}}}_{i}
(the single pixel object is present), where
{\bm{\mathbf{{e}}}}_{i}
is the
i
-th standard basis vector. Thus, indeed, a constant multiple of the left singular vector
{\bm{\mathbf{{u}}}}_{1}^{(k)}
will accurately approximate the background column pixel
{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{:,k}
. However, by orthogonality and the fact that
c_{k}{\bm{\mathbf{{u}}}}_{1}^{k}\approx{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{:,k}
for some constant
c_{k}\neq 0
,
{\bm{\mathbf{{u}}}}_{2}^{(k)}
is approximately some multiple of
(-{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{i,k}+\alpha){\bm{\mathbf{{e}}}}_{i}-\kappa_{k}{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{:,k}
where
\kappa_{k}
is also a non-zero constant.
In sum, for
t=1
, we approximate

\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\rm bg}
in (
9
) and the
t=2
term approximates
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\rm fg}
in (
9
). So both the foreground and the background have compressed representations in the BMD form. We will utilize this observation in our choice of starting guess for our algorithm.

6
Low BM-rank Tensor Approximation

Given a third-order video tensor
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times n}
with BM-rank
r
, our goal is to compute a decomposition with BM-rank
\ell
,
1\leq\ell\leq r
, which best approximates
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
such that

\min_{\tilde{\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}}\|\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}-\tilde{\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}\|_{F}^{2}\text{ with }\tilde{\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}=\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}{\mbox{\tt bmp}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}_{:,t,:},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}_{:,:,t},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}_{t,:,:}\right).

(10)

As we will show in the next section, in fact the BMD is not unique, a point which was not addressed in
[
32
]
. Thus, our ultimate goal will be to replace this problem with a regularized one which imposes desirable features in the context of our application. However, the regularized approach involves only straightforward modification to the unregularized subproblems below, so for the sake of readability, we present the unregularized version first.

Since the BM-product is a ternary multiplication of the
m\times\ell\times n,m\times p\times\ell
and
\ell\times p\times n
factor tensors
\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}
, and
\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}
, respectively, finding a decomposition of
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
in terms of the factor tensors could be done by treating this as a nonlinear least-squares problem.
The Jacobian of the residual vector,
{\mbox{\tt vec}}(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}-{\mbox{\tt bmp}}(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}))
will have
3\ell
non-zero entries per row,
mpn
rows (more, in the regularized case) and
\ell(mn\!+\!mp\!+\!np)
columns. Computing each
search direction for a nonlinear least squares solver, therefore, would require a call to an iterative method for large tensors.

Keeping in mind issues of memory and data movement, and having a preference for parallelizability, we focus instead on deriving an alternating least-squares (ALS) algorithm to solve the BMD problem, which we call BMD-ALS. We show the work involved per iteration can be decoupled into
mp
small problems that can be solved independently of one another. The ALS algorithm has been widely used for computing the tensor CP decomposition
[
5
,
26
]
and tensor block term decomposition
[
7
]
. Despite the limitations of the algorithm such as the slow convergence, the dependency on the starting guesses, the swamp effect
[
37
]
and more
[
30
,
45
]
, the simplicity of understanding and implementing the ALS algorithm with superior quality of results still marks it as today’s “workhorse” algorithm for CP decomposition
[
26
]
. We will show in the following subsections that BMD-ALS is also straightforward to implement, with relatively small, dense, independent subproblems that can be solved in parallel. Moreover, we will show that the slicewise SVDs serve as an excellent starting guess for BMD-ALS, particularly for our video application (see Sec.
4
), and for which we can compute the initial error easily.

6.1
Phase I - Starting Guess

Two ways of obtaining starting guesses are outlined in Sec.
3
: we can either take a suitable low rank matrix approximation from the unfolded data, and refold accordingly to get
\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}^{0},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}^{0},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{0}
as in Theorem.
4
; or we can take a low-rank matrix approximation (with fixed allowable rank) to each frontal slice, and refold according to Theorem.
6
to get our starting guesses. The first choice will allow us to directly improve upon the DMD method discussed in Sec.
4.1
. The 2nd choice will allow us a direct comparison to the SS-SVD approach given in Sec.
4.2
.

6.2
Alternating Least-Squares (ALS) Algorithm

Given the data tensor
\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times n}
, and the initial factor tensor triplet
\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times\ell\times n}
,
\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times\ell}
and
\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times p\times n}
obtained from phase I (Sec.
6.1
), we solve the following ALS subproblems for iterations
k=0,1,2,\dots

\begin{split}\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}^{k+1}&=\min_{\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times\ell}}\left\|\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}-{\mbox{\tt bmp}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}^{k},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{k}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2},\\
\left(\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{\top}\right)^{k+1}&=\min_{\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n\times\ell}}\left\|\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}^{\top}-{\mbox{\tt bmp}}\left(\left(\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}^{\top}\right)^{k+1},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{\top},\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}^{\top}\right)^{k}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2},\\
\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}^{\top^{2}}\right)^{k+1}&=\min_{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}^{\top^{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m\times\ell}}\left\|\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}^{\top^{2}}-{\mbox{\tt bmp}}\left(\left(\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{\top^{2}}\right)^{k+1},\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}^{\top^{2}},\left(\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}^{\top^{2}}\right)^{k+1}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}.\end{split}

(11)

In each ALS subproblem, we are holding the first and the third factor tensors fixed and solving for the middle tensor. Taking the first subproblem in the ALS algorithm as an example, we show that the problem of updating
\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}
reduces to a linear least-squares problem as discussed in Phase II below (Sec.
6.3
), and updating
\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}
and
\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}
factor tensors can be solved similarly using the same algorithm.

6.3
Phase II - Linear Least-Squares Problem

Taking the first subproblem for updating tensor
\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times\ell}
given in the ALS algorithm (Eq.
11
) as an example, we will show in this section that this tensor least-squares problem can be reduced to a linear least-squares problem. Furthermore, the problem can be decoupled into many (
mn
for updating
\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}
,
np
for updating
\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}
, and
mn
for updating
\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}
) smaller sized independent subproblems making the computation massively parallelizable.

Holding the pair
\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}
and
\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}
fixed, we optimize for
\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}
by solving

\widehat{\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}}=\min_{\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times\ell}}\|\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}-{\mbox{\tt bmp}}(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}})\|_{F}^{2}.

(12)

We will show in the following derivations that the tensor least-squares problem given in Eq. (
12
) can be equivalently written as a matrix least-squares problem.

By the definitions of the Frobenius norm and tensor BM-product, we have

\left\|\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}-{\mbox{\tt bmp}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}_{i,j,k}-\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}_{i,t,k}\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}_{i,j,t}\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}_{t,j,k}\right|^{2}.

Holding the indices
i
and
j
fixed, we have

\begin{split}&\sum_{(i-1)p+j=1}^{mp}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}_{i,j,k}-\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}_{i,t,k}\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}_{t,j,k}\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}_{i,j,t}\right|^{2}\\
=&\sum_{(i-1)p+j=1}^{mp}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}}^{\left(i,j\right)}(k,1)-\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}^{(i,j)}_{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}}(k,t){\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}_{t}^{\left(i,j\right)}\right|^{2}=\sum_{(i-1)p+j=1}^{mp}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}}^{\left(i,j\right)}-{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}^{(i,j)}_{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}}{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{\left(i,j\right)}\right\|_{F}^{2},\end{split}

where
{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}}^{(i,j)}={\mbox{\tt squeeze}}(\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}_{i,j,:})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
,
{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{(i,j)}={\mbox{\tt squeeze}}(\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}_{i,j,:})\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times 1}
, and
{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}}^{(i,j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
with the
(k,t)
-th entry specified as
{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}^{(i,j)}_{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}}(k,t)=\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}_{i,t,k}\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}_{t,j,k}
.

Let us stack the vectors
{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}}^{(i,j)}
and
{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{(i,j)}
as

{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}}^{(1,1)}\\
\vdots\\
{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}}^{(i,j)}\\
\vdots\\
{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}}^{(m,p)}\end{array}\right]={\mbox{\tt Tvec}}(\bm{\mathscr{{T}}})\in\mathbb{R}^{mpn\times 1}\text{, and }{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{(1,1)}\\
\vdots\\
{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{(i,j)}\\
\vdots\\
{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{(m,p)}\end{array}\right]={\mbox{\tt Tvec}}(\bm{\mathscr{{B}}})\in\mathbb{R}^{mp\ell\times 1}.

Moreover, we take direct-sums of
{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}^{(i,j)}_{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}}
and define

{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}}:=\underset{i,j}{\oplus}{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}^{(i,j)}_{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}}={\mbox{\tt Mat}}(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}})

\widehat{{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}}=\min_{{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn\ell\times 1}}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}}-{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}}{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

(13)

Furthermore, Eq. (
13

mp

n\times\ell
such that

\widehat{{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}}^{(i,j)}=\min_{{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{(i,j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times 1}}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}}^{(i,j)}-{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}}^{(i,j)}{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{(i,j)}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

(14)

We adopt the same flattening scheme described above to obtain the following equivalent linear least-squares subproblems for updating the factor
\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}

\widehat{{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}}=\min_{{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{pn\ell\times 1}}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}^{\top}}-{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}}{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}\right\|_{F}^{2},

(15)

where
{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}={\mbox{\tt Tvec}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{\top}\right)
,
{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}^{\top}}={\mbox{\tt Tvec}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}^{\top}\right)
, and

Remark

The cost of implementing SVD for a matrix of size
m\times n
with
m\geq n
is
\mathcal{O}(mn^{2}+n^{2})
flops.
Moreover, the cost of solving a least-squares problem with the matrix of size
m\times n
,
m\geq n
, has the same time complexity as computing the SVD of the matrix
[
15
]
. In phase I, the first choice of obtaining a starting guess is to compute a DMD of the vectorized video matrix. The total cost for DMD is dominated by taking SVD of the data matrix of size
mn\times(p-1)
where
mn\geq(p-1)
and solving a least-squares problem with the coefficient matrix of size
mn\times\ell
. Hence, the total cost is
\mathcal{O}\left((mn+1)((p-1)^{2}+\ell^{2})\right)
for the DMD choice. For the second choice, the total cost is

\mathcal{O}\left(n(m+1)p^{2}\right)
for computing the
n
frontal slice matrix SVDs, assuming
m\geq p
. In phase II, the total cost of solving the
mp
smaller least-squares problem is
\mathcal{O}\left(mp(n+1)\ell^{2}\right)
. The least-squares solutions are obtained using e.g.,
Matlab
’s
lsqminnorm
function for the minimum norm solution.

We also emphasize that both phase I (with the slice-wise SVD choice) and phase II computations can be done in parallel since we can compute matrix SVDs simultaneously on the individual frontal slices of the input tensor, and the second phase of the algorithm is also parallelizable since the
mp
smaller least-squares problems can be solved independently and concurrently. So parallel computation methods can potentially improve the execution time significantly, though further discussion is beyond the scope of this study and hence will not be discussed further in the current work.

We note that the ALS algorithm given in Alg. (
1
) for computing low BM-rank tensor approximation is also closely connected to the nonlinear GS method. Hence, several convergence results follow directly from its framework. By Proposition 2.1 in
[
46
]
, let
{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k}
denote the
k
-th solution vector, i.e.
{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k}=\left({\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}^{k},{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{k},{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}^{k}\right)
. When the normal equation matrix in each of the linear least-squares subproblems (Eq.
13
– Eq.
17
) is positive definite, i.e.
{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}^{\top}{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}\succ 0
for any
{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}\in\{{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{C}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}},{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}},{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}}\}
, then each least-squares subproblem has a unique solution and the sequence
\{{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by ALS converges to a limit point. Moreover, by Theorem 3.1 in
[
46
]
, if
\{{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded, then the limit point of the sequence is a stationary point of the problem. Detailed discussions on the ALS convergence analysis is referred to the Supplement.

As discussed in
[
30
,
37
]
, there is no guarantee for the ALS algorithm in CP-decomposition to converge to a stationary point when the coefficient matrices in the subproblems are rank-deficient, in which case, the objective function is not strictly quasiconvex. This is also the case for the ALS subproblems for third-order tensor BM-decomposition. The coefficient matrices given may not have full column rank. As a result, each of the least-squares subproblems will not be strictly quasiconvex, and hence the objective function
f({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}})
is not strictly quasiconvex with respect to each component of
{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}
. Therefore, similar to the CP-decomposition, the BMD-ALS algorithm may produce a sequence with limit points that are not critical points to the original problem resulting in slow convergence. The remedy for the convergence issue of the ALS algorithm for CP decomposition has been discussed in several studies
[
16
,
30
,
37
,
46
]
.
In the next section, we address the BMD-ALS convergence issue by adding regularization.

7
Regularized ALS

Grouping the slicewise singular values with the corresponding left singular vectors as in Theorem
8
was arbitrarily made in order to preserve the scaling constants of the left-singular vectors. This illustrates a nonuniqueness property of the BMD. To see this more generally, let
{\bm{\mathbf{{A}}}}
be
m\times n
,
{\bm{\mathbf{{B}}}}
be
m\times p
, and
{\bm{\mathbf{{C}}}}
be
p\times n
. Then define two tensor triplets
(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}),(\tilde{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}},\tilde{\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}},\tilde{\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times 1\times n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times 1}\times\mathbb{R}^{1\times p\times n}
such that

\begin{split}\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}_{:,1,:}={\bm{\mathbf{{A}}}},\qquad\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}_{:,:,1}&={\bm{\mathbf{{B}}}},\qquad\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}_{1,:,:}={\bm{\mathbf{{C}}}},\\
\tilde{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}}_{:,1,:}={\bm{\mathbf{{D}}}}_{1}{\bm{\mathbf{{A}}}},\qquad\tilde{{\bm{\mathbf{{B}}}}}_{:,:,1}=&{\bm{\mathbf{{D}}}}_{1}^{-1}{\bm{\mathbf{{B}}}}{\bm{\mathbf{{D}}}}_{2}^{-1},\qquad\tilde{\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}}_{1,:,:}={\bm{\mathbf{{D}}}}_{2}{\bm{\mathbf{{C}}}},\end{split}

where
{\bm{\mathbf{{D}}}}_{1}
and
{\bm{\mathbf{{D}}}}_{2}
are invertible
m\times m
and
p\times p
diagonal matrices respectively. Then

{\mbox{\tt bmp}}(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}})={\mbox{\tt bmp}}(\tilde{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}},\tilde{\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}},\tilde{\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}}),

showing that the factors are non-unique.

To this end, we might wish to add regularization. Recall our generative model suggests that one of the lateral slices of
\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}
should give information about the background scene, and the subsequent SS-SVD analysis suggests that it should correspond to the largest singular value. The
\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}
and
\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}
tensors each have information about one spatial dimension and time. So, the simpliest regularization model would be

\min_{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}}\left\|\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}-{\mbox{\tt bmp}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\|{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{a}(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}})\|_{F}^{2}+\|{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{b}(\bm{\mathscr{{B}}})\|_{F}^{2}+\|{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{c}(\bm{\mathscr{{C}}})\|_{F}^{2}\right)

(19)

where
{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{a},{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{b}
, and
{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{c}
are linear operators acting on the vectorized factor tensors
\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}
, and
\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}
respectively. More specifically, for all
1\leq i\leq m,1\leq j\leq p,1\leq k\leq n
,
{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{a}=\underset{k,i}{\oplus}{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}
where
{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}={\mbox{\tt diag}}([\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{\ell}])
,
{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{b}=\underset{i,j}{\oplus}\beta{\bm{\mathbf{{I}}}}_{\ell\times\ell}
and
{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{c}=\underset{j,k}{\oplus}\gamma{\bm{\mathbf{{I}}}}_{\ell\times\ell}
. Here, we set
\lambda_{1}
to be at least an order of magnitude smaller than
\lambda_{2}
. This is consistent with the fact that the dominant feature is the background (requiring the least penalty).

Importantly, this choice keeps the subproblems decoupled: the only change required is to add a constraint term to each of Eq. (
13
), Eq. (
15
), and Eq. (
17
) respectively, in the form of either
\kappa\|{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}\|_{2}^{2}
for
{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}={\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{(i,j)}
or
{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}={\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}^{(i,j)}
(Eq.
14
, Eq.
16
respectively) or
0.5\|{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}^{(k,i)}\|_{2}
(to Eq.
18
).

Let
\Psi(\bm{\omega})
denote the objective function given in Eq. (
19
)
where
\bm{\omega}
is the solution vector, i.e.
\bm{\omega}=({\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}},{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}},{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}})
. Then the sequence
\{\bm{\omega}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by the regularized alternating least-squares algorithm converges to a critical point of
\Psi
. We provide the proof in the Supplement.

Decomposing a Fourth-Order Tensor

The formal definition of the BM product for fourth order tensors described in
[
12
]
requires four factor tensors. The computation time and storage quickly become intractable for high order BM products. In the context we consider here, using a fourth order BMP would be overkill and too expensive. We therefore define a new BM-product-like operation, called
bmp4
, and corresponding BM-product-like decomposition for fourth order tensors from triples of fourth order tensors:

Definition 9
.

Given a tensor triplet
\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times\ell\times n\times q},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times\ell\times q}
, and
\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times p\times n\times q}
, which are conformable on the first three indices, then
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}={\mbox{\tt bmp4}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times n\times q}
, is specified entry-wise as follows,

\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}_{i,j,k,z}=\sum_{1\leq t\leq\ell}\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}_{i,t,k,z}\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}_{i,j,t,z}\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}_{t,j,k,z}

(20)

for all
1\leq i\leq m,1\leq j\leq p,1\leq k\leq n
, and
1\leq z\leq q
.

When
\ell=1
, a
\text{BM}_{4}
-outer product corresponds to the
\text{BMP}_{4}
of the conformable order-3 tensor slices
\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times 1\times n\times q}
,
\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times 1\times n\times q}
, and
\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times p\times n\times q}
. Then the corresponding notion of rank can be defined as follows.

Definition 10
.

The
\text{BM}_{4}
-rank of
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times n\times q}
is the minimum number of the
\text{BM}_{4}
-outer products of conformable tensor slices that sum up to
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
.

We note that our definition of the fourth-order BM-product given above multiplies a tensor triplet, in which case, the multiplication is orientation dependent with one of the dimensions held fixed. This is different than the orientation independent fourth order BMP given in
[
12
]
which would have required an additional tensor factor and would not scale well for the video application. Our product is designed to respect relationship among the color channels.

9.1
An ALS Algorithm of BMD for Fourth-Order Tensors

Using
bmp4
defined above, we can extend the BM-decompostion to fourth-order video tensor. Given
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times n\times q}
, a low
\text{BM}_{4}
-rank approximation to
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
is obtained by solving

\min_{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}}\left\|\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}-\text{bmp4}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2},

(21)

where
\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times\ell\times n\times q}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times\ell\times q}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times p\times n\times q}
. We can similarly solve the problem by an alternating least-squares algorithm to obtain the fourth order tensor BMD.

First, we set the initial guess
\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}^{0}
to be all ones of size
m\times p\times\ell\times q
. We then obtain the initial guess pair
\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}^{0}
and
\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{0}
by fixing the fourth dimension and applying either a matrix decomposition based or tensor slice-wise decomposition based methods as described in Phase I (Sec.
6.1
) to the order-3 tensor slices
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}_{:,:,:,z}
, for all
1\leq z\leq q
. The order-3 tensor slice decomposition results in the pair
\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}^{0}
and
\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{0}
. Next, to update
\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}
, we modify the algorithm given in Phase II (Sec.
6.3
) which requires solving the following problem

\widehat{\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}}=\min_{\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times\ell\times q}}\left\|\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}-{\mbox{\tt bmp4}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}^{0},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{0}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}.

Similar to the third-order case, we show that this problem can be reduced to parallelizable smaller least-squares subproblems.

By the definitions of the Frobenius norm and forth-order tensor BMP, we have

\left\|\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}-{\mbox{\tt bmp4}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}^{0},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{0}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{z=1}^{q}\left|\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}_{i,j,k,z}-\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}_{i,t,k,z}^{0}\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}_{i,j,t,z}\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}_{t,j,k,z}^{0}\right|^{2}.

(22)

Holding the indices
i
,
j
, and
z
fixed, we regroup the remaining indices and rewrite the right-hand side of Eq. (
22
) as

\sum_{(i-1)p+j=1}^{mp}\sum_{(z-1)n+k=1}^{nq}\left|{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{k}^{\left(i,j\right)}-\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}_{k,t}^{\left(i,j\right)}{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}_{t}^{\left(i,j\right)}\right|^{2},

where
{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}^{(i,j)}={\mbox{\tt vec}}({\mbox{\tt squeeze}}(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}_{i,j,:,:}))\in\mathbb{R}^{nq\times 1}
,
{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{(i,j)}={\mbox{\tt vec}}({\mbox{\tt squeeze}}(\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}_{i,j,:,:}))\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell q\times 1}
, and
{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}^{(i,j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{nq\times\ell q}
with entries specified as
{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}^{(i,j)}_{k,t}=\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}_{i,t,k,z}^{0}\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}_{t,j,k,z}^{0}
. This expression suggests that
\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}
can be updated via solving following parallelizable smaller linear least-squares subproblems

\widehat{{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}}^{\left(i,j\right)}=\min_{{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{\left(i,j\right)}}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}^{\left(i,j\right)}-{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}^{\left(i,j\right)}{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{\left(i,j\right)}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

(23)

We additionally define the fourth order tensor transposes to help expressing the ALS algorithm using
bmp4
.

Definition 11
.

Given
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times n\times q}
, we define

\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\top_{4}}={\mbox{\tt permute}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}},\left[2,3,1,4\right]\right),\quad\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\top^{2}_{4}}={\mbox{\tt permute}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}},\left[3,1,2,4\right]\right).

Note the above definition of the fourth order tensor transpose is the same for the third-order case holding the last dimension fixed.

Under this definition, we can show the following convenient fact around which we can base a fourth-order BMD-ALS algorithm.

Theorem 12
.

Given
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}={\mbox{\tt bmp4}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\right)
,

\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\top_{4}}={\mbox{\tt bmp4}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\right)^{\top_{4}}={\mbox{\tt bmp4}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}^{\top_{4}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{\top_{4}},\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}^{\top_{4}}\right).

We can then formulate the following ALS algorithm based on the order-4 BMD of a given tensor
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times n\times q}
.

\begin{split}\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}^{k+1}&=\min_{\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times\ell\times q}}\left\|\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}-{\mbox{\tt bmp4}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}^{k},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{k}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2},\\
\left(\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{\top_{4}}\right)^{k+1}&=\min_{\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{\top_{4}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n\times\ell\times q}}\left\|\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\top_{4}}-{\mbox{\tt bmp4}}\left(\left(\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}^{\top_{4}}\right)^{k+1},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{\top_{4}},\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}^{\top_{4}}\right)^{k}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2},\\
\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}^{\top^{2}_{4}}\right)^{k+1}&=\min_{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}^{\top^{2}_{4}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m\times\ell\times q}}\left\|\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\top^{2}_{4}}-{\mbox{\tt bmp4}}\left(\left(\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{\top^{2}_{4}}\right)^{k+1},\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}^{\top^{2}_{4}},\left(\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}^{\top^{2}_{4}}\right)^{k+1}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}.\end{split}

Similar to the discussion for the third order case, each ALS subproblem can be solved in parallel as smaller sized linear least-squares problems.

9.2
Application to Color Video with Regularization

Consider a given video with three color channels
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times p\times n\times 3}
. Each frame of the video is of size
m\times n\times 3
, where the third dimension encodes color. The total number of frames is
p
. Fixing the color channel dimension, our goal is to obtain a tensor triplet
\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times\ell\times n\times 3}
,
\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\times\ell\times 3}
, and
\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times p\times n\times 3}
from the fourth order BMD of
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
such that
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}\approx{\mbox{\tt bmp4}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\right)
.

Since the different tensor slices along the color channel for the factors associated with the temporal dimension (for example, if the frames are oriented as lateral slices, the factor tensors with temporal dimension is
\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}
and
\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}
) should be the same, or the difference is small. We consider adding the following constraints to mitigate the non-uniqueness of the solutions present in the order-4 BMD. We note that comparing to the regularized ALS given in Sec.
7
, this constraint is specific for color video decomposition applications but does not promote the background/foreground separation task.

Consider solving for the factor tensor
\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times p\times\ell\times 3}
, we add the following regularization

\left\|\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}_{i,j,t,1}-\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}_{i,j,t,2}\right\|_{2}<\epsilon\text{ and }\left\|\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}_{i,j,t,2}-\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}_{i,j,t,3}\right\|_{2}<\epsilon.

(24)

Notice that, from Eq. (
23
), holding the indices
(i,j)
fixed, we obtain the vector
{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{(i,j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\ell\times 1}
by column major order vectorization of the slice
\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}_{i,j,:,:}
, i.e.
{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{(i,j)}={\mbox{\tt vec}}({\mbox{\tt squeeze}}(\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}_{i,j,:,:}))
, for all
1\leq i\leq m,1\leq j\leq p
.

Let us define the difference operator
{\bm{\mathbf{{R}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\ell\times 3\ell}
such that, for all
1\leq t\leq\ell,1\leq v\leq 2
,

{\bm{\mathbf{{R}}}}_{(v-1)\ell+t,v\ell+t}=-1\text{ and }{\bm{\mathbf{{R}}}}_{(v-1)\ell+t,(v-1)\ell+t}=1

So the constraint given in Eq. (
24
) is equivalent to applying the difference operator
{\bm{\mathbf{{R}}}}
on the vectorized slice
\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}_{i,j,:,:}
such that

\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{R}}}}{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{(i,j)}\right\|_{2}<\epsilon
.

Similarly, for the factor tensor
\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{\top_{4}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n\times\ell\times 3}
, we have

\left\|\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{\top_{4}}_{j,k,t,1}-\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{\top_{4}}_{j,k,t,2}\right\|_{2}<\epsilon\text{ and }\left\|\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{\top_{4}}_{j,k,t,2}-\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{\top_{4}}_{j,k,t,3}\right\|_{2}<\epsilon.

we can rewrite in an equivalent form with the difference operator
{\bm{\mathbf{{R}}}}
applied to the vectorized tensor slice
{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}^{(j,k)}={\mbox{\tt vec}}({\mbox{\tt squeeze}}(\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}^{\top_{4}}_{j,k,:,:}))
as
\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{R}}}}{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}^{(j,k)}\right\|<\epsilon
, for all
1\leq j\leq p,1\leq k\leq n
.

Thus, a constrained ALS algorithm for the fourth order BMD of a the color video tensor
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
has the following decoupled subproblems

\hat{{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}}^{(i,j)}=\min_{{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{\left(i,j\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\ell\times 1}}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}^{\left(i,j\right)}-{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}}^{\left(i,j\right)}{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{\left(i,j\right)}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{R}}}}{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{(i,j)}\right\|_{F}^{2},

(25)

and the factor tensor
{\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}}
is approximated by letting
\widehat{\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}}_{i,j,:,:}={\mbox{\tt reshape}}\left(\hat{{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}}^{(i,j)},[\ell,3]\right)
. Similarly, we update
{\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}}
by solving

\hat{{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}}^{(j,k)}=\min_{{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}^{\left(j,k\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\ell\times 1}}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\top_{4}}}^{\left(j,k\right)}-{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}}^{\left(j,k\right)}{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}^{\left(j,k\right)}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{R}}}}{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}^{(j,k)}\right\|_{F}^{2},

(26)

and hence
\widehat{\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}}^{\top_{4}}_{j,k,:,:}={\mbox{\tt reshape}}\left(\hat{{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}}^{(j,k)},[\ell,3]\right)
. Lastly, we update
{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}}
by solving

\hat{{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}}^{(k,i)}=\min_{{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}^{\left(k,i\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\ell\times 1}}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}^{\top^{2}_{4}}}^{\left(k,i\right)}-{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{C}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}}^{\left(k,i\right)}{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}^{\left(k,i\right)}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

(27)

Then
\hat{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}}^{\top^{2}_{4}}_{k,i,:,:}={\mbox{\tt reshape}}\left(\hat{{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}}^{(k,i)},[\ell,3]\right)
.

Conclusions and future work

We have demonstrated that the third-order tensor BM-decomposition can be effectively used for grayscale video background/foreground separation with compression. Using our generative spatiotemporal video model, we have shown that videos with a stationary background are naturally low BM-rank tensors. As a result, we can model the stationary background with significant image quality comparable to existing methods of tensor SS-SVD and DMD, while obtaining compressible representation of the the foreground video. Without the need of using the background subtraction technique, our BMD estimated foreground video is less affected by the errors resulted from a poor background reconstruction quality. Moreover, we have shown that the alternating least-squares algorithm for computing the BMD is closely connected to the block nonlinear Gauss–Seidel (GS) method, from which the convergence analysis of the algorithm follows naturally. Additionally, we gave new theoretical insight into the BM-rank bounds and non-uniqueness, where the later was overcame by adding a regularization tailored to the video background/foreground separation application. Lastly, we extend the regularized ALS algorithm for color video compression and separation. In the future, we will investigate the use of constraints for a better posed problem, the feasibility of the extension of computation to higher dimensions, and we will investigate the utility of the BMD applied to other types of spatiotemporal data.

Appendix A
ALS Convergence Analysis

The alternating least-squares algorithm has been widely used for computing the tensor CP decomposition
[
26
]
and the block term decomposition
[
37
,
7
]
among others. The local and global convergence of the ALS algorithm for the tensor CP decomposition has been studied in
[
30
,
45
,
46
]
based on its connection to the block nonlinear Gauss–Seidel (GS) method. In this section, we will show that the ALS algorithm for computing low BM-rank tensor approximation is also closely connected to the nonlinear GS method, and hence several convergence results follow directly from its framework.

Recall the nonlinear GS method solves the following minimization problem

\begin{split}\min\ &f\left({\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}\right)\\
\text{subject to }&{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}\in X=X_{1}\times X_{2}\times\cdots\times X_{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1},\end{split}

where
f
is a continuously differentiable function from
\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}
to
\mathbb{R}
and
X
is a Cartesian product of closed, nonempty and convex subsets
X_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}\times 1}
, for
i=1,\dots,M
with
\sum_{i=1}^{M}N_{i}=N
. If the vector
{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is partitioned into
M
component vectors
{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}\times 1}
, then we can consider
f
is a function from
\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times 1}\times\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}\times 1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{N_{M}\times 1}
to
\mathbb{R}
with
f\left({\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}\right)=f\left({\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{1},{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{2},\dots,{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{M}\right)
.
The solution to the nonlinear optimization function can then be found by the block Gauss-Seidel method via the following iteration in a cyclic order,

{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{i}^{k+1}=\underset{{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{i}\in X_{i}}{\min}f\left({\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{1}^{k+1},\dots,{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{i-1}^{k+1},{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{i},{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{i+1}^{k},\dots,{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{M}^{k}\right),

which updates the components of
{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}
. The iterative technique starts from a given initial guess
{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}^{0}=\left({\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{1}^{0},{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{2}^{0},\dots,{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{M}^{0}\right)
and generates a sequence
\{{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}^{k}\}=\left\{\left({\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{1}^{k},{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{2}^{k},\dots,{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{M}^{k}\right)\right\}
.

The connection between the nonlinear block Gauss-Seidel method and the ALS-BMD algorithm is made evident by noting the cost function we want to minimize,

\left\|\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}-\hat{\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i,j,k}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}_{i,j,k}-\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}_{i,t,k}\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}_{i,j,t}\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}_{t,j,k}\right)^{2}=f\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\right),

is a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{\left(mn+mp+pn\right)\ell\times 1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. By letting
{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}=\left[{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}};{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}};{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{\left(mn+mp+pn\right)\ell\times 1}
where
{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}},{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}
and
{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}
are the vectorized factor tensors, then we can see that

f\left({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}\right)=f\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\right).

The BMD problem therefore can be reformulated into the following problem

\begin{split}\min\ &f\left({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}\right)\\
\text{subject to }&{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn\ell\times 1}\times\mathbb{R}^{mp\ell\times 1}\times\mathbb{R}^{pn\ell\times 1}.\end{split}

The ALS algorithm updates the components of
{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}
by

\begin{split}{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{k+1}&=\underset{{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{mp\ell\times 1}}{\min}f\left({\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}^{k},{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}},{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}^{k}\right),\\
{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}^{k+1}&=\underset{{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{pn\ell\times 1}}{\min}f\left({\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}^{k},{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{k+1},{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}\right),\\
{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}^{k+1}&=\underset{{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn\ell\times 1}}{\min}f\left({\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}},{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{k+1},{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}^{k+1}\right).\end{split}

This is exactly the nonlinear block Gauss-Seidel method.

By Proposition 2.1 in
[
46
]
, let
{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k}
denote the
k
-th solution vector, i.e.
{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k}=\left({\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}^{k},{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{k},{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}^{k}\right)
. When the normal equations matrix in each of the linear least-squares subproblems given in Eq. (6.3), Eq. (6.5), and Eq. (6.6) in the main text is positive definite, i.e.
{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}^{\top}{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}\succ 0
for any
{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}\in\{{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}},{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}},{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}}\}
, then each least-squares subproblem has a unique solution and the whole sequence
\{{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k}\}
generated by ALS converges to a limit point. In particular,

f\left({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k+1}\right)\leq f\left({\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}^{k},{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{k+1},{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}^{k+1}\right)\leq f\left({\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}^{k},{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{k+1},{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}^{k}\right)\leq f\left({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k}\right)\leq\cdots\leq f\left({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{0}\right),

shows that ALS monotonically reduces the cost function. Since
f
is bounded below,
\{f({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k})\}
has a limit point
f^{*}\geq 0
. Moreover, by Theorem 3.1 in
[
46
]
, if
\{{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k}\}
is bounded, then the limit point of the sequence is a stationary point of the problem.

Regularized ALS Convergence Analysis

Convergence analysis for the ALS algorithm with Tikhonov regularization has been studied for the CP decomposition
[
20
]
. We note that a similar analysis can be derived for the regularized ALS algorithm for BM-decomposition given in Section. 7.

Given a third order data tensor
\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times n}

\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times\ell\times n}
,
\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times\ell}
, and
\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times p\times n}
from the constrained problem

\min_{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}}\left\|\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}-\text{bmp}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{a}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}+\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{b}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}+\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{c}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}\right),

(28)

where
{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{a}
,
{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{b}
, and
{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{c}
are linear operators acting on the vectorized factor tensors
\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}
,
\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}
, and

\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}
respectively. More specifically, for all
1\leq i\leq m,1\leq j\leq p,1\leq k\leq n
,
{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{a}=\underset{k,i}{\oplus}{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}
where
{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}={\mbox{\tt diag}}([\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{\ell}])
,
{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{b}=\underset{i,j}{\oplus}\beta{\bm{\mathbf{{I}}}}_{\ell\times\ell}
and
{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{c}=\underset{j,k}{\oplus}\gamma{\bm{\mathbf{{I}}}}_{\ell\times\ell}
.

Then the regularized ALS subproblems are given by

\hat{\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}}\left[i,j,:\right]=\min_{{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{\left(i,j\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times 1}}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}}^{\left(i,j\right)}-{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}}^{\left(i,j\right)}{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{\left(i,j\right)}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{b}^{\left(i,j\right)}{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{\left(i,j\right)}\right\|_{F}^{2},

\hat{\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}}^{\top}\left[j,k,:\right]=\min_{{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}^{\left(i,j\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times 1}}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}^{\top}}^{\left(j,k\right)}-{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}}^{\left(j,k\right)}{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}^{\left(j,k\right)}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{c}^{\left(j,k\right)}{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}^{\left(j,k\right)}\right\|_{F}^{2},

\hat{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}}^{\top^{2}}\left[k,i,:\right]=\min_{{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}^{\left(i,j\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times 1}}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}^{\top^{2}}}^{\left(k,i\right)}-{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}}^{\left(k,i\right)}{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}^{\left(i,j\right)}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{a}^{\left(k,i\right)}{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}^{\left(k,i\right)}\right\|_{F}^{2},

for all
0\leq i<m
,
0\leq j<n
, and
0\leq k<p
.
Let
\Psi
represents the objective function in Eq.
28
, then
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{\ell\left(mp+mn+np\right)}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
, where

\Psi\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\right)=f\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{a}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}+\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{b}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}+\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{c}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}\right)

(29)

We will first show that the objective functions in the subproblems are
\mu
-strongly convex.

Definition 13
.

A differentiable function
h:\text{dom}\left(h\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where
\text{dom}\left(h\right)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
\mu
-strongly convex if there exists a constant
\mu>0
such that

h\left(y\right)\geq h\left(x\right)+\nabla h\left(x\right)^{\top}\left(y-x\right)+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|y-x\right\|_{2}^{2},\forall\,x,y\in\text{dom}\left(h\right).

Theorem 14
.

The objective function

f\left({\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}},{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{k},{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}^{k}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{a}{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}\right\|_{F}^{2}

is
\mu
-strongly convex.

Proof.

The least-squares problem
f\left({\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}},{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}^{k},{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}^{k}\right)=\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}^{\top^{2}}}-{\bm{\mathbf{{H}}}}_{\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}}{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}\right\|_{F}^{2}
is convex. The regularization term
\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{a}{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}\right\|_{F}^{2}
has the following first derivative

\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{a}{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}\right\|_{F}^{2}\right\}={\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{a}^{\top}{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{a}{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}

and second derivative

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}\partial{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}^{\top}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{a}{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}\right\|_{F}^{2}\right\}=\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}}\left\{{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{a}^{\top}{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{a}{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}\right\}={\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{a}^{\top}{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{a},

which is positive-semidefinite, since
{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{a}
is a direct sum of
mn
diagonal matrices with non-negative entries. So there exists a constant
\mu>0
such that
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}^{2}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{L}}}}_{a}{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}\right\|_{F}^{2}\right\}\succcurlyeq\mu{\bm{\mathbf{{I}}}}
.

A linear combination of a convex and a
\mu
-strongly convex function is also
\mu
-strongly convex. Hence, the objective function of the subproblem with respect to
\bm{\mathscr{{A}}}
is
\mu
-strongly convex.

Similarly, the objective function of the subproblems with respect to
\bm{\mathscr{{B}}}
and
\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}
are also
\mu
-strongly convex.

Lemma 15
.

Suppose
{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}^{k+1}
is obtained by solving the subproblem have decrease in the objective function after a single update of
{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}
, i.e.

\Psi\left(\mathbf{a}^{k}\right)-\Psi\left(\mathbf{a}^{k+1}\right)\geq\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|\mathbf{a}^{k}-\mathbf{a}^{k+1}\right\|_{2}^{2}

for some constant
\mu>0
.

Proof.

By the first order optimality, we have

\nabla_{a}\Psi\left(\mathbf{a}^{k+1}\right)=\nabla_{a}f\left(\mathbf{a}^{k+1}\right)+\mathbf{L}_{a}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{a}\mathbf{a}=0.

Then the strongly convexity of the objective function yields

\Psi\left(\mathbf{a}^{k}\right)-\Psi\left(\mathbf{a}^{k+1}\right)\geq\nabla_{a}\Psi\left(\mathbf{a}^{k+1}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{a}^{k}-\mathbf{a}^{k+1}\right)+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|\mathbf{a}^{k}-\mathbf{a}^{k+1}\right\|_{F}^{2}\geq\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|\mathbf{a}^{k}-\mathbf{a}^{k+1}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Similar results hold for
\mathbf{b}
and
\mathbf{c}
.

Next we want to show that
\Psi
decreases monotonically at each iteration.

Theorem 16
.

(Sufficiently decrease property) Let
\Psi
represent the objective function in Eq.
29
, let
\bm{\omega}^{k}=\left(\mathbf{a}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k},\mathbf{c}^{k}\right)
, then we have

\Psi\left(\bm{\omega}^{k}\right)-\Psi\left(\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right)\geq\rho\left\|\bm{\omega}^{k}-\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right\|_{2}^{2}

for some constant
\rho>0
. In addition, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\|\bm{\omega}^{k}-\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right\|_{2}^{2}<\infty.

Proof.

By the previous result in 2, we have

\Psi\left(\mathbf{\bm{\omega}}^{k}\right)-\Psi\left(\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\left\{\mu_{1}\left\|\mathbf{a}^{k}-\mathbf{a}^{k+1}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\mu_{2}\left\|\mathbf{b}^{k}-\mathbf{b}^{k+1}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\mu_{3}\left\|\mathbf{c}^{k}-\mathbf{c}^{k+1}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right\},

Then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left\|\bm{\omega}^{k}-\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\rho}\left(\Psi\left(\mathbf{\bm{\omega}}^{0}\right)-\Psi\left(\bm{\omega}^{n}\right)\right)<\infty

\implies\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\|\bm{\omega}^{k}-\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right\|_{2}^{2}<\infty.

Remark 2
.

The sequence
\left\{\bm{\omega}^{k}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded, since the regularization terms in the objective function
\Psi
bounds the blocks
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}
, and
\mathbf{c}
, and
\Psi\left(\bm{\omega}^{k}\right)
is non-increasing.

Theorem 17
.

Let
\left\{\bm{\omega}^{k}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence generated by our regularized ALS algorithm, then there exists a constant
\nu>0
such that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a vector
\eta^{k+1}\in\partial\Psi\left(\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right)
such that

\left\|\eta^{k+1}\right\|\leq\nu\left\|\bm{\omega}^{k}-\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right\|

Proof.

Let
k
be a positive integer. By the first order optimality condition of the subproblems, we have

\begin{split}&\nabla_{b}f\left(\mathbf{a}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k+1},\mathbf{c}^{k}\right)+\mathbf{L}_{b}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{b}\mathbf{b}^{k+1}=0,\\
&\nabla_{c}f\left(\mathbf{a}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k+1},\mathbf{c}^{k+1}\right)+\mathbf{L}_{c}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{c}\mathbf{c}^{k+1}=0,\\
&\nabla_{a}f\left(\mathbf{a}^{k+1},\mathbf{b}^{k+1},\mathbf{c}^{k+1}\right)+\mathbf{L}_{a}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{a}\mathbf{a}^{k+1}=0.\end{split}

Let us define
\eta_{2}^{k+1}:=\nabla_{b}f\left(\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right)-\nabla_{b}f\left(\mathbf{a}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k+1},\mathbf{c}^{k}\right)
, then

\eta_{2}^{k+1}=\nabla_{b}f\left(\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right)+\mathbf{L}_{b}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{b}\mathbf{b}^{k+1}=\nabla_{b}\Psi\left(\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right).

Similarly, define
\eta_{3}^{k+1}:=\nabla_{c}f\left(\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right)-\nabla_{c}f\left(\mathbf{a}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k+1},\mathbf{c}^{k+1}\right)
implies
\eta_{1}^{k+1}=\nabla_{c}\Psi\left(\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right)
, and define
\eta_{3}^{k+1}:=\nabla_{a}f\left(\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right)-\nabla_{a}f\left(\mathbf{a}^{k+1},\mathbf{b}^{k+1},\mathbf{c}^{k+1}\right)
implies
\eta_{2}^{k+1}=\nabla_{b}\Psi\left(\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right)
. Hence the vector
\eta^{k+1}=\left(\eta_{1}^{k+1},\eta_{2}^{k+1},\eta_{3}^{k+1}\right)\in\partial\Psi\left(\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right)
.

Moreover, since the sequence
\left\{\bm{\omega}^{k}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded, and the objective function
f
is twice continuously differentiable, then by the mean value theorem,
\nabla f
is Lipschitz continuous. Hence, there exists a constant
C_{2}>0
such that

\begin{split}\left\|\eta_{2}^{k+1}\right\|&=\left\|\nabla_{b}f\left(\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right)-\nabla_{b}f\left(\mathbf{b}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k+1},\mathbf{c}^{k}\right)\right\|\\
&=\left\|\nabla_{b}f\left(\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right)-\nabla_{b}f\left(\mathbf{a}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k+1},\mathbf{c}^{k}\right)+\nabla_{b}f\left(\mathbf{a}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k+1},\mathbf{c}^{k}\right)-\nabla_{b}f\left(\mathbf{a}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k},\mathbf{c}^{k}\right)\right\|\\
&\leq\left\|\nabla_{b}f\left(\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right)-\nabla_{b}f\left(\mathbf{a}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k+1},\mathbf{c}^{k}\right)\right\|+\left\|\nabla_{b}f\left(\mathbf{a}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k+1},\mathbf{c}^{k}\right)-\nabla_{b}f\left(\mathbf{a}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k},\mathbf{c}^{k}\right)\right\|\\
&\leq C_{1}\left\|\bm{\omega}^{k+1}-(\mathbf{a}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k+1},\mathbf{c}^{k})\right\|+C_{2}\left\|\bm{\omega}^{k}-\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right\|\\
&\leq P_{2}\left\|\bm{\omega}^{k}-\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right\|\end{split}

for some constant
P_{2}\geq C_{1}+C_{2}

Similarly, there exists cosntants
P_{1},P_{3}>0
such that
\left\|\eta_{1}^{k+1}\right\|\leq P_{1}\left\|\bm{\omega}^{k}-\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right\|
and
\left\|\eta_{3}^{k+1}\right\|\leq P_{3}\left\|\bm{\omega}^{k}-\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right\|

Setting

gives
\left\|\eta^{k+1}\right\|\leq\nu\left\|\bm{\omega}^{k}-\bm{\omega}^{k+1}\right\|
.

Appendix B
Nonlinear Solution for Computing BMD

Given
\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times n}
, solving BMD with non-linear optimization

\underset{\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}}{\arg\min}\|\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}-{\mbox{\tt bmp}}(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}})\|_{F}^{2}

for the factor tensor triplet
(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times\ell\times n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times\ell}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times p\times n}
.

Let us take the vectorized factor tensors

{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}}={\mbox{\tt vec}}(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}});\quad{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}}={\mbox{\tt vec}}(\bm{\mathscr{{B}}});\quad{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}={\mbox{\tt vec}}(\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}),

and let
{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}=[{\bm{\mathbf{{a}}}},{\bm{\mathbf{{b}}}},{\bm{\mathbf{{c}}}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{(mn+mp+pn)\ell\times 1}
. Define the residual vector
{\bm{\mathbf{{r}}}}({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}})
to be

{\bm{\mathbf{{r}}}}({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}})={\mbox{\tt vec}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{T}}}\right)-{\mbox{\tt vec}}\left({\mbox{\tt bmp}}\left(\bm{\mathscr{{A}}},\bm{\mathscr{{B}}},\bm{\mathscr{{C}}}\right)\right).

Every entry of
{\bm{\mathbf{{r}}}}
corresponds to one
(i,j,k)
triple, so we adopt this indexing scheme,

{\bm{\mathbf{{r}}}}_{s}=r_{ijk}=t_{ijk}-\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}a_{itk}b_{ijt}c_{tjk},

where
s=i+(j-1)m+(k-1)mp
.
Then the Jacobian matrix
{\bm{\mathbf{{J}}}}
of the residual
{\bm{\mathbf{{r}}}}({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}})
is of size
mpn\times(m\ell n+mp\ell+\ell pn)
and is given by

{\bm{\mathbf{{J}}}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}&\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\cdots&\frac{\partial{r}_{ijk}}{\partial{a}_{itk}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial{r}_{ijk}}{\partial{b}_{ijt}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial{r}_{ijk}}{\partial{c}_{tjk}}&\cdots\\
&\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\end{array}\right),

where, for fixed
(i,j,k)
, the only non-zero entries are

\frac{\partial r_{ijk}}{\partial a_{itk}}=-b_{ijt}c_{tjk},\frac{\partial r_{ijk}}{\partial b_{ijt}}=-a_{itk}c_{tjk}
and
\frac{\partial r_{ijk}}{\partial c_{tjk}}=-a_{itk}b_{ijt}
, for
1\leq t\leq\ell
.

If
{\bm{\mathbf{{J}}}}({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k})
has full column rank, then the Gauss-Newton method updates the solution by

{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k+1}={\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k}-\left({\bm{\mathbf{{J}}}}({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k})^{\top}{\bm{\mathbf{{J}}}}({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k})\right)^{-1}{\bm{\mathbf{{J}}}}({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k})^{\top}{\bm{\mathbf{{r}}}}({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k})

However, the Jacobian matrix for the BM-decomposition is singular in general.
As a result, the Gauss-Newton method does not converge to a local minimum. On the other hand, the Levenberg-Marquardt method adds an additional penalty term in the least-squares problem by solving the following problem

{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k+1}=\underset{{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}}{\arg\min}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{r}}}}({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}})+{\bm{\mathbf{{J}}}}({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k})({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}-{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k})\right\|_{F}^{2}+\lambda_{k}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}-{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

where
{\bm{\mathbf{{r}}}}({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}})+{\bm{\mathbf{{J}}}}({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k})({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}-{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k})
is the first-order linear approximation to
{\bm{\mathbf{{r}}}}({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}})
. Then the solution is given by

{\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k+1}={\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k}-\left({\bm{\mathbf{{J}}}}({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k})^{\top}{\bm{\mathbf{{J}}}}({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k})+\lambda_{k}{\bm{\mathbf{{I}}}}\right)^{-1}{\bm{\mathbf{{J}}}}({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k})^{\top}{\bm{\mathbf{{r}}}}({\bm{\mathbf{{v}}}}^{k}).

Appendix C
From DMD to Tensor BMP

Given a video data of
p
frames and each frame is of size
m\times n
, let
{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn\times 1}
be the vectorized
k
-th video frame for
1\leq k\leq p
, then the DMD method groups the data into matrices
{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{1},{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn\times(p-1)}
as follows

{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{1}=\left[{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{1}\ {\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{2}\ {\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{3}\cdots{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{p-1}\right];\quad{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{2}=\left[{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{2}\ {\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{3}\ {\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{4}\cdots{\bm{\mathbf{{x}}}}_{p}\right].

The method then finds a linear map

C.1
DMD factor interpretation

We note that the first step of the DMD method which applies a truncated SVD to
{\bm{\mathbf{{X_{1}}}}}
is similar to taking the SVDs of spatiotemporal slices of the video tensor (with one frame less). Instead of applying SVD to individual spatiotemporal slices, DMD applies SVD to all slices at the same time. As a result, the left-singular matrix
{\bm{\mathbf{{U}}}}
similarly captures the spatial information of the video. Particularly, as discussed in Sec. 4.2, the first left-singular vector captures the dominant background scene. However, the DMD method that models the static background image in-fact models a weighted linear combination of all the left-singular vectors (spatial information).

From the eigendecomposition of
\tilde{{\bm{\mathbf{{A}}}}}
, we have

\tilde{{\bm{\mathbf{{A}}}}}{\bm{\mathbf{{W}}}}={\bm{\mathbf{{U}}}}^{\top}{\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{2}{\bm{\mathbf{{V}}}}{\bm{\mathbf{{\Sigma}}}}^{-1}{\bm{\mathbf{{W}}}}={\bm{\mathbf{{W}}}}\bm{\Lambda}
, which then gives

\bm{\Phi}={\bm{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{2}{\bm{\mathbf{{V}}}}{\bm{\mathbf{{\Sigma}}}}^{-1}{\bm{\mathbf{{W}}}}={\bm{\mathbf{{U}}}}{\bm{\mathbf{{W}}}}\bm{\Lambda}
. Therefore, the DMD modes given in Eq. (
30
) can be re-written as

\bm{\Phi}_{i,j}=\sum_{1\leq s\leq\ell}\lambda_{j}{\bm{\mathbf{{U}}}}_{i,s}{\bm{\mathbf{{W}}}}_{s,j}\implies\bm{\Phi}_{:,j}=\sum_{1\leq s\leq\ell}\lambda_{j}{\bm{\mathbf{{U}}}}_{:,s}{\bm{\mathbf{{W}}}}_{s,j}.

(35)

Thus, the background DMD mode represented by
\bm{\Phi}_{:,\alpha}
when
\|\lambda_{\alpha}\|\approx 1
for all
\alpha,1\leq\alpha\leq\ell
, is given by

\bm{\Phi}_{:,\alpha}=\sum_{1\leq s\leq\ell}\lambda_{\alpha}{\bm{\mathbf{{U}}}}_{:,s}{\bm{\mathbf{{W}}}}_{s,\alpha}.

(36)

From Eq. (
36
), we see that the DMD reconstruction of the stationary mode is a weighted linear combination of the spatial information captured by the left-singular vectors of the spatiotemporal video matrix. This could potentially explain the observations made in the numerical experiments of the videos in
[
17
]
: the DMD reconstructed background scene tends to include spurious foreground pixels. Since in the DMD method, the SVD step is applied to vectorized video frames across all
p
frames, taking the rank-truncation with
\ell=p-1
almost recovers the original video matrix. The spatial information of the foreground objects moving through time is captured by the set of left-singular vectors
{\bm{\mathbf{{U}}}}_{:,t}
with
2\leq t\leq\ell
, which are also contained in the background DMD mode
\bm{\Phi}_{:,\alpha}
. This could also explain another phenomenon about the foreground motions in
[
17
]
: the moving objects in the foreground create movement trails extrapolating both past and future motions of the objects. Since the uncompressed foreground video by the DMD algorithm is obtained by subtracting the background from the original video, the erroneous foreground motion trails contained in the background DMD reconstruction persisted in the foreground video.

Importantly, the DMD method does not provide an approximation to the generative video model from Sec. 5.1 in the main text. In addition to the background reconstruction with spurious object motion discussed above, the DMD compressed foreground video frames exhibit another issue. Since the foreground modes
{\bm{\mathbf{{\varphi}}}}_{t}
,
1\leq t\neq\alpha\leq\ell
, change over time linearly scaled by
\lambda_{t}^{j-1}
at time
j
, the non-linear foreground object motions cannot be exactly modeled by the linear change.

We also observe the following:

•

The DMD method requires processing video segments rather than computing the decomposition of the whole video at once. One reason for taking smaller segments is that it helps with keeping shorter processing time than data acquisition time. Also, longer videos produce longer erroneous movement trails on the approximated background.

•

DMD modes and frequencies are complex-valued. In
[
17
]
, the real-valued video pixels are obtained by taking the modulus of the complex values. The code from
[
27
]
uses the real part of the complex-valued video frame entries for display. In our BMD–ALS video reconstruction with DMD initialization, we will also use real-part solutions.

•

The DMD method has the potential of a compressed reconstruction of the background scene by setting
\ell=1
in the SVD step
[
17
]
. In our work, by allowing
\ell
to be small, i.e.
1<\ell\ll p
, we are interested in obtaining the compressed representations of the background scene and the foreground object videos.
