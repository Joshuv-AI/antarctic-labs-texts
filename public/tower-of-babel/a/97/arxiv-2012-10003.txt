1.
Introduction

Turing reduction
\leq_{T}
is a partial order over reals. It naturally induces an equivalence relation
\equiv_{T}
. Given a real
x
, its corresponding Turing degree
{\mathbf{x}}
is a set of reals defined by
\{y\mid y\equiv_{T}x\}
. We say
{\mathbf{x}}\leq\mathbf{y}
if
x\leq_{T}y
. We use
\mathcal{D}
to denote the set of Turing degrees. An
upper cone

u_{{\mathbf{x}}}
of Turing degrees is the set
\{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{y}\geq\mathbf{x}\}
.

Definition 1.1
.

Turing determinacy, or
{\mathrm{TD}}
, says that for any set
A
of Turing degrees, either
A
or
\mathcal{D}\setminus A
contains an upper cone of Turing degrees.

Martin proves the following famous theorem.

Theorem 1.2

(Martin
[
5
]
)
.

Over
{\mathrm{ZF}}
, the Axiom of determinacy, or
{\mathrm{AD}}
, implies
{\mathrm{TD}}
.

Definition 1.3
.

•

Countable choice axiom for sets of reals, or
{\mathrm{CC}_{\mathbb{R}}}
, says that for any countable sequence
\{A_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
of nonempty sets of reals, there is a function
f:\omega\to\mathbb{R}
so that for every
n
,
f(n)\in A_{n}
.

•

Dependent choice axiom for sets of reals, or
{\mathrm{DC}_{\mathbb{R}}}
, says that for any binary relation
R
over reals so that
\forall x\exists yR(x,y)
, there is a function
f:\omega\to\mathbb{R}
so that for every
n
,
R(f(n),f(n+1))
.

Though
{\mathrm{AD}}
contradict the Axiom of choice, or
\mathrm{AC}
, Mycielski proves the following theorem.

Theorem 1.4

(Mycielski
[
6
]
)
.

Over
{\mathrm{ZF}}
,
{\mathrm{AD}}
implies
{\mathrm{CC}_{\mathbb{R}}}
.

It is a long standing question whether
{\mathrm{AD}}
implies
{\mathrm{DC}_{\mathbb{R}}}
.

The following question has been also circulated among set theorists (for example, see
[
2
]
).

Question 1.5
.

Over
{\mathrm{ZF}}
, does
{\mathrm{TD}}
imply
{\mathrm{CC}_{\mathbb{R}}}
?

In this paper, we answer the question.

2.
The main theorem

Throughout the section, we assume
{\mathrm{ZF}}+{\mathrm{TD}}
.

We identify
\mathbb{R}
as
2^{\omega}
or
\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N})
, the power set of
\mathbb{N}
. So if
x\in\mathbb{R}
, then
x(n)
, the
n
-th bit of
x
, belongs to
\{0,1\}
. The structure of the Turing degrees is an upper semi-lattice. I.e. for any reals
x
and
y
,
x\oplus y=\{2n\mid x(n)=1\wedge n\in\mathbb{N}\}\wedge\{2n+1\mid y(n)=1\wedge n\in\mathbb{N}\}
has the least Turing degree above both
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}
.

We prove the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

Over
{\mathrm{ZF}}
,
{\mathrm{TD}}
implies
{\mathrm{CC}_{\mathbb{R}}}

The following reformulation of
{\mathrm{CC}_{\mathbb{R}}}
is helpful to understand the idea behind the subsequent proofs.

Proposition 2.2
.

{\mathrm{CC}_{\mathbb{R}}}
is equivalent to that for any function
f:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
, there is an upper cone on which
f
is constant.

Proof.

Assume
{\mathrm{CC}_{\mathbb{R}}}
. For any
i
, there is a
j_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
so that
A_{i}=\{{\mathbf{x}}\mid f(\mathbf{x})(i)=j_{i}\}
contains an upper cone. By
{\mathrm{CC}_{\mathbb{R}}}
, we can choose, for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbf{z}_{i}
such that
u_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\subset A_{i}
and some
\mathbf{z}
above all
\mathbf{z}_{i}
. Then
f
is constant on
u_{{\mathbf{z}}}
.

Now suppose that
\{A_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
is a sequence of nonempty sets of reals witnessing the failure of
{\mathrm{CC}_{\mathbb{R}}}
. For any degree
\mathbf{x}
, let
i_{\mathbf{x}}
be the least
i
so that there is no real in
A_{i}
Turing below
\mathbf{x}
. By the assumption,
i_{{\mathbf{x}}}
exists for any degree
{\mathbf{x}}
. Define a function
f:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
as follows:

f(\mathbf{x})(n)=\left\{\begin{aligned} 0,&\ &n<i_{\mathbf{x}};\\
1,&\ &otherwise.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

By the assumption,
f
is well defined for every degree. But clearly
f
cannot be constant on any upper cone, a contradiction.
∎

The following technical lemma, which can be proved with
{\mathrm{ZF}}
, is folklore in recursion theory.

Lemma 2.3
.

For any degree
{\mathbf{x}}
, there is a family Turing degrees
\{{\mathbf{y}}_{r}\mid r\in\mathbb{R}\}
satisfying the following property:

(1)

For any

,
{\mathbf{x}}<{\mathbf{y}}_{r}
;

(2)

For any

and
{\mathbf{z}}<{\mathbf{y}}_{r_{0}},{\mathbf{y}}_{r_{1}}
, we have that
{\mathbf{z}}\leq{\mathbf{x}}
;

(3)

For any
{\mathbf{z}}\geq{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime\prime}
, the Turing double jump of
{\mathbf{x}}
, there is an infinite set

so that
{\mathbf{y}}_{r}^{\prime\prime}={\mathbf{z}}
for any

.

Proof.

Fix a real
x
. It is routine (see
[
4
]
) to prove, by a recursive Sacks forcing relative to
x
, that there is a perfect tree
S\subset 2^{<\omega}
with
S\leq_{T}x^{\prime\prime}
satisfying the following property:

(a)

For any
y\in[S]
, the collection of infinite paths through
S
, we have that
x<_{T}y
and there is no real
z
so that
x<_{T}z<_{T}y
;

(b)

For any
y_{0}\neq y_{1}\in[S]
,
y_{0}
is Turing incomparable with

;

(c)

For any
y\in[S]
,
y^{\prime\prime}\equiv_{T}y\oplus x^{\prime\prime}
.

Then for any reals
y_{0}\neq y_{1}\in[S]
,
z<_{T}y_{0}
and
z<_{T}y_{1}
, we have that
x\leq_{T}z\oplus x\leq_{T}y_{0}
and
x\leq_{T}z\oplus x\leq_{T}y_{1}
. Thus, by (a) and (b),
x\equiv_{T}z\oplus x
and so
z\leq_{T}x
.

Also for any
z\geq_{T}x^{\prime\prime}
, it is clear that there is an infinite set
C_{z}\subset[S]
so that for any
y\in C_{z}
, we have that
y\oplus S\equiv_{T}z
. Then for any
y\in C_{z}
,
y^{\prime\prime}\equiv_{T}y\oplus x^{\prime\prime}\geq_{T}y\oplus S\equiv_{T}z
. Also
y^{\prime\prime}\equiv_{T}y\oplus x^{\prime\prime}\leq_{T}z
. So
y^{\prime\prime}\equiv_{T}z
.

Now let
f
be a bijection between
\mathbb{R}
and
[S]
and set
{\mathbf{y}}_{r}
to be the Turing degree of
f(r)
for any
r\in\mathbb{R}
. Then the collection
\{{\mathbf{y}}_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{R}}
is exactly what we want.
∎

Firstly, we prove the following weak form of
{\mathrm{CC}_{\mathbb{R}}}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Suppose that
\{A_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
is a countable family of countable sets of reals, then
A=\bigcup_{n}A_{n}

Proof.

Suppose not. Let
\{A_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
is a countable family of countable sets of reals so that
A=\bigcup_{n}A_{n}
is not countable. Given a real
x
, let
n_{{\mathbf{x}}}
be the least number

so that there is a real

that is not Turing below

. Then
n_{{\mathbf{x}}}
is defined for every
{\mathbf{x}}
. Moreover,
n_{{\mathbf{x}}}\leq n_{{\mathbf{y}}}
for any
{\mathbf{x}}\leq{\mathbf{y}}
.

Now fix a degree
{\mathbf{x}}
. Let
\{{\mathbf{y}}_{r}\mid r\in\mathbb{R}\}
be as in Lemma
2.3
.
Since
A_{n_{{\mathbf{x}}}}
is countable, by (3) of the lemma, there is an uncountable set

so that for any

,
n_{{\mathbf{y}}_{r}^{\prime\prime}}>n_{{\mathbf{x}}}
.

We claim that there must be some

and hence

. Otherwise, for any

Then by

, there is some

so that for any

n_{{\mathbf{y}}}<n_{{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime\prime}}
. Then

is undefined where
({\mathbf{x}}_{0})^{(\omega)}
is the

∎

By Proposition

Corollary 2.5
.

Every countable set of Turing degrees has an upper bound.

Lemma 2.7

Every function
F:\mathcal{D}\to Ord

Proof.

It suffices to prove that

It is clear that
