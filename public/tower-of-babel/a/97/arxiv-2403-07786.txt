Generative deep learning-enabled ultra-large field-of-view lens-free imaging
Ronald B. Liu, Zhe Liu, Max G.A. Wolf, Krishna P. Purohit, Gregor Fritz, Yi Feng, Carsten G. Hansen, Pierre O. Bagnaninchi, Xavier Casadevall i Solvas, Yunjie Yang

Abstract
Advancements in high-throughput biomedical applications necessitate real-time, large field-of-view (FOV) imaging capabilities. Conventional lens-free imaging (LFI) systems, while addressing the limitations of physical lenses, have been constrained by dynamic, hard-to-model optical fields, resulting in a limited one-shot FOV of approximately 20 mm 2 20\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}^{2} . This restriction has been a major bottleneck in applications like live-cell imaging and automation of microfluidic systems for biomedical research. Here, we present a deep-learning (DL)–based imaging framework - GenLFI - leveraging generative artificial intelligence (AI) for holographic image reconstruction. We demonstrate that GenLFI can achieve a real-time FOV over 550 mm 2 550\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}^{2} , surpassing the current LFI system by more than 20-fold, and even larger than the world’s largest confocal microscope by 1.76 times. The resolution is at the sub-pixel level of 5.52 µ ​ m 5.52\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m} , without the need for a shifting light source. The unsupervised learning-based reconstruction does not require optical field modeling, making imaging dynamic 3D samples (e.g., droplet-based microfluidics and 3D cell models) in complex optical fields possible. This GenLFI framework unlocks the potential of LFI systems, offering a robust tool to tackle new frontiers in high-throughput biomedical applications such as drug discovery.

Existing biomedical imaging systems all encounter a significant constraint: limited throughput through one-shot FOV. However, a modest FDA-approved drug library can contain around 2000 samples and 2 million or more pairwise combinations

, where the time delay to image different samples heavily decreases the ability to interrogate dynamic events. Notably, the repetitive nature of these samples’ high-content data presents an opportunity. In the context of high-throughput imaging for drug screening, previous studies

By leveraging these recurrent patterns as robust data guidance, it is possible to enhance the reconstruction of sample morphology more effectively, thus mitigating FOV limitations and enhancing the efficiency of high-throughput imaging.

Here, we propose a generative deep learning-enabled LFI framework, namely GenLFI, employing DL-based computer vision techniques to address the limitations of state-of-the-art LFI systems. GenLFI employs a universal optical setup and significantly simplifies the imaging process by eliminating the need for data pre-processing, annotation, and optical field modeling that traditional LFI systems depend on. Our framework also incorporates an unsupervised learning model, LensGAN, capable of autonomously learning image features from unpaired and unaligned holograms and bright-field light microscopy to generate high-resolution image reconstructions. This is achieved through a comprehensive analysis of diffraction and projection principles, establishing physical correlations between holograms and reconstructed bright-field images.

Through a series of imaging experiments, we demonstrate how the GenLFI framework advances LFI systems in three key aspects: (1) Our LensGAN’s robust learning capability enables the reconstruction of high-quality images from low-resolution holograms, achieving an ultra-large FOV in a single shot. This FOV is solely constrained by the sensor size, with our validation on a full-frame CMOS sensor demonstrating a FOV exceeding
550\,mm^{2}
, while still preserving sub-sensor-pixel resolution. (2) Unlike state-of-the-art LFI methods, GenLFI requires no optical field control or modeling, allowing it to image complex 3D samples (
i.e.,
microfluidics, 3D cell models). (3) LensGAN boasts a faster inference speed than current hologram reconstruction algorithms

;
Ronneberger et al. 2015
;
Rivenson et al.
. These advancements collectively extend the boundaries of LFI technology in high-throughput biomedical imaging tasks. The expanded, streamlined, and enhanced observational capacities of GenLFI can create new possibilities for broad dynamic imaging applications in complex cell-based microfluidic systems, phenotypic screening, and AI-enabled digital pathology.

structures) of the hologram and the bright-field image. These improvements eliminate the need for optical field modeling required by existing LFI methods and manual annotation in typical supervised learning frameworks.

Discussion

GenLFI, a generative deep learning framework for LFI, eliminates the need for traditional forward imaging models by introducing data-driven guidance through unsupervised learning. This framework enables imaging in dynamic, noisy optical fields, previously not achievable with LFI. GenLFI achieves an over 20 times larger FOV than current LFI systems, enabling real-time monitoring for in vivo (
e.g.,
zebrafish, see Fig.S1 in Supplementary Information) or in vitro (
e.g.,
cell-in-droplets, 3D cell models) studies. Images are reconstructed using a DL network, significantly faster than traditional iterative techniques
Barkley et al.
;
Castañeda et al.
;
Berdeu et al.
;
Denis et al.
;
Rivenson et al.
, while achieving sub-pixel resolution without requiring adjustments to the light source. These advancements demonstrate the potential of leveraging generative AI to overcome optical limitations.

The dynamic nature of our imaging scenarios makes it impractical to obtain paired ground truth and raw holograms for training. Therefore, GenLFI leverages unsupervised learning algorithms, inspired by those designed for image-to-image (I2I) transformation tasks in computer vision. This approach prioritizes hardware-agnostic and reproducible workflows with simple setups. While the current implementation utilizes a telescope cold camera with a resolution of
5.9\,\mu m/px
, GenFLI is compatible with higher-resolution sensors and multi-angle illumination for potential 3D LFI capabilities.

Our investigation into the physical correlation between holograms and bright-field images achieved leading results based on FID and SSIM metrics. However, challenges remain with such I2I-based approaches, primarily concerning data quality, quantity, and diversity. These factors directly impact reconstruction results, as demonstrated by artifacts observed in reconstructions from limited datasets (see the results from single-sample, paired datasets Fig.
2
b
and Fig.S7 in Supplementary Information). Additionally, training GANs can be challenging due to potential issues like model collapse and non-convergence. While exploring diffusion models
Ho et al.
;
Zhao et al.
;
Sasaki et al.
;
Liu et al.
as an alternative shows promise, their limitations in handling unpaired and unaligned image translation tasks require further investigation. Furthermore, existing evaluation metrics like FID and KID primarily assess image-level distributions and lack the ability to evaluate the alignments between objects across different image patches. Developing new metrics accurately reflecting alignment between different images captured from the same objects is crucial for future advancements.

The combination of GenLFI and large vision models (LVM)
Cui et al.
;
Bai et al.
holds promise for achieving a more generalized reconstruction capability for various samples and light field conditions. In addition, leveraging processors with advanced and specified architectures
Jia et al.
;
Arcelin
;
Peng et al.
could enable real-time image reconstruction with these large models.

Despite limitations due to optical conditions, GenLFI’s high-throughput capability opens doors for various biomedical applications. GenLFI offers a large FOV, enabling rapid analysis of large biological samples with minimal equipment. This capability is particularly valuable in resource-limited settings lacking traditional histopathology services. By incorporating digital staining through generative deep learning, GenLFI can bring advanced imaging techniques to such settings. Furthermore, GenLFI’s cost-effective and compact setup facilitates routine monitoring of cell culture and spheroid growth directly within incubators, reducing phototoxicity while enabling continuous monitoring of dynamic cellular processes across a large FOV. The testing of drug libraries based on dynamic processes, utilizing single-cell/cell models or droplet-based microfluidic systems, can now be obtained simultaneously from microplate-like platforms, paving the way for developing next-generation, autonomous, high-throughput drug screening systems.

LensGAN architecture

LensGAN, a core component of GenLFI, reconstructs high-resolution images from captured holograms. LensGAN generally considers the measured and reconstructed holograms (
i
and
\hat{i}
) and the measured and generated bright-field images (
o
and
\hat{o}
) into two domains,
X
and
Y
, respectively. It leverages a GAN framework with a cycle-consistent training strategy to learn the complex mapping between holograms (domain
X
) and corresponding bright-field images (domain
Y
). The architecture of LensGAN comprises 6 networks: generators of reconstruction and reverse mappings
G:X\rightarrow Y
and
F:Y\rightarrow X
, selectors
\beta_{X}
and
\beta_{Y}
, and discriminators
D_{X}
and
D_{Y}
. During training, all 6 networks function following the training loop as shown in Fig.
1
a
. In the testing phase, only the trained generator
G
is used for reconstruction. The outputs of LensGAN are reconstructed bright-field images
\hat{o}=G(i)
and generated holograms
\hat{i}=F(o)
.

Generators
G
and
F
handle the fundamental image transformation task, while the remaining 4 networks contribute to the training process.
G
and
F
, each composed of 9 residual blocks (ResNet
He et al.
), feature an image encoder for extracting image characteristics and a decoder for generating images. The selector network
\beta

Xie et al.
is responsible for pairing images based on feature alignments and is constructed with an adaptive average pooling layer, 4 convolutional networks, and one fully connected network (FCN) with a Softmax layer. The discriminator
D
is designed to differentiate between images, emphasizing structural information and intricate details within the complex amplitude. Recent advancements in Transformers
Vaswani et al.
have demonstrated the equivalence of multi-layer and one-layer attention mechanisms
Wu et al.
. By leveraging this finding, we designed
D
with a Markovian architecture, merging a one-layer attention module with 5 downsampling convolutional layers to augment amplitude details while maintaining minimal computational overhead (see Supplementary Information 4, 5).

Combining these networks, the training loop adheres to the cycle structure
Zhu et al.
, guided by several loss functions, depicted in Fig.
1
b
and Fig.S2 in Supplementary Information. To augment phase structural feature information during the training, we devised a
phase contrast module
positioned to constrain the physical correlation between
X
and
Y
domains. The observed samples in LFI are generally light-permeable, resulting in duotone holograms captured under a single-wavelength light source. In such scenarios, phase contrast
Zernike
and dark-field microscopes are commonly employed, where a phase ring and other lenses are combined to create a
phase contrast
. Inspired by this technique, our approach is to develop a module that learns the phases of the two domains contrastively, thereby mimicking a similar mechanism and enhancing the physical correlations between the object’s hologram and bright-field images. Technically, we create the
phase-contrast loss function

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PC}}
by a multi-scale structural similarity index measure (MS-SSIM)
Wang et al.
for the amplitude spectra:

\min_{G}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PC}}(X,Y)=\mathbb{E}_{i\sim P_{X}}\left[1-\operatorname{MS-SSIM}(|\mathcal{FT}(i)|,|\mathcal{FT}(\hat{o})|)\right],

(3)

where
|\mathcal{FT}(\cdot)|
denotes the magnitudes of the individual frequency components obtained from the Fourier transform (
\mathcal{FT}
) of an image, and
P_{X}
is the probability distribution for
i
.
\operatorname{MS-SSIM}
is defined as:

\operatorname{MS-SSIM}(x,y)=\left[l_{M}(x,y)\right]^{\gamma_{M}}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{M}\left[c_{j}(x,y)\right]^{\delta_{j}}\left[s_{j}(x,y)\right]^{\epsilon_{j}},

(4)

with the terms

\displaystyle l(x,y)=\frac{2\mu_{x}\mu_{y}+C_{1}}{\mu_{x}^{2}+\mu_{y}^{2}+C_{1}},

(5)

\displaystyle c(x,y)=\frac{2\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}+C_{2}}{\sigma_{x}^{2}+\sigma_{y}^{2}+C_{2}},

\displaystyle s(x,y)=\frac{\sigma_{xy}+C_{3}}{\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}+C_{3}},

where
x
and
y
and two input image patches,
M=5
denotes the highest scale number set for the system to iteratively apply a low-pass filter between
x
and
y
.
\mu
and
\sigma^{2}
stand for mean value and variance, respectively, which are implemented by 2D convolutions.
l(x,y)
,
c(x,y)
, and
s(x,y)
represent luminance, contrast, and structure comparison measures between
x
and
y
, with control weights
\delta_{1}=\epsilon_{1}=0.0448
,
\delta_{2}=\epsilon_{2}=0.2856
,
\delta_{3}=\epsilon_{3}=0.3001
,
\delta_{4}=\epsilon_{4}=0.2363
, and
\gamma_{5}=\delta_{5}=\epsilon_{5}=0.1333
. LensGAN enforces all inputs as 8-bit images with a pixel value range of 0 to 255; the constants are set as
C_{1}=(0.01\times 255)^{2}
,
C_{2}=(0.03\times 255)^{2}
, and
C_{3}=C_{2}/2
.

Note that in (
3
), we added
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PC}}
only for the mapping
G:X\rightarrow Y
, the transformation process of holographic reconstruction, with a control weight
\tau_{\mathrm{PC}}
. Similarly, only for
G

\beta_{X}
is regularized by the effective sample size loss
Xie et al.
for
\beta_{X}

\min_{\beta_{X}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ESS}}(X)=\|\beta_{X}\|_{2},

(6)

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the L2 norm.

For the whole cycle (reconstruction mapping
G:X\rightarrow Y
and reverse mapping
F:Y\rightarrow X
), there is a GAN loss function
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GAN}}
to constrain
G
,
F
and
D

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cyc}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{idt}}
to further regularize
G
and
F

Mao et al.
;
Zhu et al.
;
Xie et al.

\displaystyle\min_{G,\beta_{X}}\max_{D_{Y}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GAN}}(X,Y)

(7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i\sim P_{X}}\beta_{X}(i)\left[\left(1-D_{Y}(\hat{o}\right)^{2}\right]+

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{o\sim P_{Y}}\beta_{Y}(o)\left[D_{Y}(o)^{2}\right],

\displaystyle\min_{G,F}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cyc}}(X,Y)=\mathbb{E}_{i\sim P_{X}}\beta_{X}(i)\|i-F(\hat{o})\|_{1}

(8)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{o\sim P_{Y}}\beta_{Y}(o)\|o-G(\hat{i})\|_{1},

and reweighted identity loss

\displaystyle\min_{G,F}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{idt}}(X,Y)=\mathbb{E}_{i\sim P_{X}}\beta_{X}(i)\|i-F(i)\|_{1}

Training, testing and post-processing

For all experiments, we set hyperparameters
\tau_{\mathrm{cyc}}=10
,
\tau_{\mathrm{PC}}=8
, and
\tau_{\mathrm{ESS}}=\tau_{\mathrm{idt}}=1
. We use Adam solver
Kingma and Ba
to initiate training of the networks from scratch, maintaining a consistent learning rate of 0.0001 for the initial 50 epochs. Subsequently, we linearly decrease the learning rate to zero over the following 50 epochs. Each epoch processed 10,000 images (we set the input dimension as
256\times 256\,px
) with a batch size 20. The training was on a single Nvidia RTX A100 GPU; for dataset
‘GenLFI-Microfluidics’
, it took 29 hours. All tests were conducted on a workstation (CPU Intel i9-13900K, GPU Nvidia RTX3090). We utilized FID for quantification and comparative evaluation. Following fast reconstruction, we performed post-processing for large sample results using image stitching algorithms. As illustrated in Fig.
5
b
, we employed local object stitching
Weber and Schenk
and global panorama stitching (see Supplementary Information 6) separately.

Further discussion on complex optical field

Here we further discuss the optical field with large axial size samples and mathematically prove the inadequacy of conventional model-based 3D lens-free holographic microscopy (LFHM) tomographic reconstruction approaches. To this end, we analyze the current 3D LFHM algorithm based on scalar diffraction theory for 3D objects.

As shown in Fig.1 in the main text, the solution to the light propagation problem is described as:

u(\boldsymbol{r})=u^{inc}(\boldsymbol{r})+u^{sct}(\boldsymbol{r})

(11)

where
u^{inc}(\boldsymbol{r})
represents the incident light and
u^{sct}(\boldsymbol{r})
represents the scattered light. The Lippmann-Schwinger equation (LSE) depicts the relationship between the 3D scattering potential of the sample and the resulting scalar field at region
V
:

u(\boldsymbol{r})=u^{inc}(\boldsymbol{r})+\int_{V}G(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})f(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})y(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})dr^{\prime}

(12)

where
G(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi R}\exp(jk_{b}R)
,
k_{b}=k_{0}n_{b}
is the wavenumber of the light in the background medium, and
R
denotes the length of the Euclidean vector
\boldsymbol{r}
. Discretized and denoted by vectors, the resulting scalar field
\boldsymbol{\hat{u}}
:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{inc}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{G}}\boldsymbol{\diag(f)}\boldsymbol{u}^{inc}=\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{f})

(13)

where
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
is the light propagator,
\boldsymbol{\diag(f)}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
denotes a diagonal matrix whose main diagonal entries consist of elements from the vector
\boldsymbol{f}
. With multiple light sources, the final
forward model
of the 3D LFHM can be presented by the intensity of the optical field
\widehat{\boldsymbol{H}}
under
p^{th}
illumination:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{H}}=\left|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{inc}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{G}}\boldsymbol{\diag(f)}\boldsymbol{u}^{inc}\right|^{2}=\left|\mathcal{L}_{p}(\boldsymbol{f})\right|^{2}

(14)

The reconstruction algorithm can be formulated as the optimization problem:

\boldsymbol{f}^{*}=\arg\min_{f\in\mathbb{C}^{N}}\{\Sigma_{p=1}^{P}d(\left|\mathcal{L}_{p}(\boldsymbol{f})\right|^{2},\widehat{\boldsymbol{H}}_{p})\},

(15)

where
\mathcal{L}_{p}(\cdot)
denotes the light propagator,
\boldsymbol{f}^{*}
denotes the amplitude of the light,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{H}}_{p}
represents the measured light intensity under the
p^{th}
illumination.

In the microfluidics experiment, the mediums consist of air, PDMS, water, oil, and glass. In this condition, different components of the electromagnetic field may make the vector diffraction theory of
u(\boldsymbol{r})
in (
11
) ineffective. Furthermore, the model-based algorithm’s approximation of the hologram in the 2D space is affected by the thickness of the sample. For example, the Born approximation for (
13
) is valid only if
\boldsymbol{u}^{sct}\ll\boldsymbol{u}^{inc}
. This corresponds to
l(n_{s}-n_{b})\ll\lambda
, where
l
is the thickness,
(n_{s}-n_{b})
describes the medium’s refractive index variation, and
\lambda
is the wavelength of the incident light. Consider a PDMS chip that has water inside, with a thickness of
5000\,\mu m
; there are
l(n_{s}-n_{b})
=
5000\times(1.40-1.33)=350\,\mu m
, while
\lambda=0.42\,\mu m
. It is obvious that the model is not applicable to the condition of Born approximation for large samples with strong scattering.

To provide a more accurate description of the physical process, some non-linear models have been proposed
Luo 2021
. These iterative methods involve either multiple iterations by variations or dividing the sample into a series of thin layers. However, these methods are impractical to apply in real experiments and are restricted to sample-specific tools.

In summary, real-world scattered fields are complex and difficult to model. The existing forward model-based LFI systems are limited to small and thin samples.

Post-processing: Global panorama image stitching

After processing the image blocks separately, the results will be stitched together to create the final panorama. This operation is divided into two stages. The first stage
involves intensity correction and image assembly. The second stage aims to remove slowly varied artifacts.

For the first stage, considering two image blocks to be stitched, the color difference usually occurs between them, causing a noticeable seam. We define one image as the reference image
I^{r}
and the other image as the target image
I^{t}
. Pixel points at the seam line are denoted by
p_{i},i=1,2,...,n
. Then the color difference
\Delta C(p_{i})
between
I^{r}
and
I^{t}
at the seam line is defined as:

\Delta C(p_{i})=V^{r}(p_{i})-V^{t}(p_{i}),~~i=1,2,...,n

(19)

where
V^{r}(p_{i})
denotes the average pixel intensity at
p_{i}
in the
I^{r}
and
V^{t}(p_{i})
represents the average pixel intensity at
p_{i}
in the
I_{t}
.
V^{r}(p_{i})
is calculated by averaging the pixel intensity at
p_{i}
with its nearest three neighbors at the same row in the
I^{r}
. And the same approach is adopted to estimate
V^{t}(p_{i})
. For each pixel
p^{t}
in the
I^{t}
, the intensity change
\Delta V
is defined as
Fang et al. 2019
:

\Delta V(p^{t})=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\Delta C(p_{i}),

(20)

where the weight
w_{i}
is expressed as:

w_{i}=\mathrm{exp}\left(-\left(\frac{||V^{t}(p_{i})-I^{t}(p^{t})||^{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}+\frac{||L(p_{i})-L(p^{t})||^{2}}{\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right)\right).

(21)

Then, the corrected intensity at
p^{t}
is formulated as:

\overline{I^{t}(p^{t})}=I^{t}(p^{t})+\Delta V(p^{t}).

(22)

There exist slowly varied artifacts in the generated panorama at the first stage. In the second stage, we treat this type of artifact as the low-frequency component of the image. Therefore, we can use the Gaussian filter to estimate these artifacts. Suppose the initial panorama is denoted by
P
, the artifact image is estimated by:

A=P\ast\kappa,

(23)

where
\ast
represents the convolution, and
\kappa
is the Gaussian kernel. Therefore, the final panorama
\overline{P}
is expressed as:

\overline{P}=P-A.

(24)

To fabricate the mold from which the microfluidic chip is made, photolithography is used. A
35\,\mu m
thick layer of photoresist (SU-8 2015) is spin-coated, pre-baked, exposed, post-baked, and developed.

After the silicone wafer mold containing the design is created, a PDMS base and curing agent (Sylgard 184) are combined in a 10:1 ratio in a plastic cup and stirred for several minutes using a spatula. The plastic cup is placed in a vacuum desiccator to extract trapped air in the PDMS/curing agent mix. After approximately 20 minutes the mix was poured over the earlier created master, after which it was placed in the vacuum desiccator until no bubbles were visible anymore within the PDMS/curing agent mix. After this, the mix was placed in an oven at
65^{\circ}
C for 3 hours to cure the PDMS.

After the PDMS was cured, the PDMS was removed from the master, and separate devices were cut from this removed PDMS slab. Inlet and outlet holes were punched using a
1.2\,mm
biopsy punch. Using scotch tape, dust was removed from both the glass slide and the PDMS devices, after which both were exposed to oxygen plasma in a plasma cleaner (Harrick PDC-002). The PDMS-coated glass slides and PDMS devices were then bonded by carefully connecting them.

Finally, the PDMS chip is silanized using a 5 w% silane and HFE-7500 mixture.
