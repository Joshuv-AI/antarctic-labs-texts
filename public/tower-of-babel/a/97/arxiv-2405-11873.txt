Equilibria in multiagent online problems with predictions
Gabriel Istrate, Cosmin Bonchiş, Victor Bogdan

Abstract
We study the effect of predictions on competitive ratio equilibria in multi-agent online problems, using a recently defined multi-agent ski-rental problem as a testbed. We characterize predictionless equilibria, formally define equilibria with predictions, and show that (for multiagent ski-rental) they have properties reminscent of their predictionless counterparts. We also investigate how using an algorithm with predictions can benefit an individual agent when playing an equilibrium strategy profile for multiagent ski-rental. We give a theoretical characterization of the consistency and robustness achieved by the algorithm, and experimentally benchmark its average competitive ratio.

Introduction

In many real-life scenarios agents interact strategically, increasingly assisted by
predictors
which provide forecasts of the behavior of the other agents (as well as on the optimal course of action to adopt). A convincing example is the use of navigation assistants: in such apps the driver receives information about the current congestion in the network, as well as an estimate of the quickest route to the desired destination, given current traffic conditions. It is up to the individual agent to follow the recommendations of such an assistant: whereas such apps are generally trustworthy, they may fail to display last-minute accidents that impact the choice of the optimal route. Similarly, the availability of pre-election polls may induce voters (in elections with more than two candidates) to behave strategically. Conversely, (doctored) opinion polls can been used strategically, aiming to help certain candidates by influencing voter turnout and behavior
1
1

1

This has recently happened in real-life elections in Romania. The strategic role of predictions in voting is a fascinating topic for further research.
. From an individual voter perspective one cannot, therefore, trust such ”predictions”, and must use a decision algorithm that is robust to the perceived risk of using untrusted polls.

Given these considerations, it should come as no surprise that augmenting algorithms by
predictors

[
Mitzenmacher and Vassilvitskii(2021)
]
is one of the most exciting recent research directions in algorithmics. In the special case of online algorithms, a framework
[
Lykouris and Vassilvtiskii(2018)
,
Kumar et al.(2018)Kumar, Purohit, and Svitkina
]
that already became standard benchmarks the quality of an algorithm by two competitive ratios: its
consistency
,
the competitive ratio obtained for a perfect predictor, and its
robustness
, defined as the competitive ratio for pessimal (even adversarial) predictors. The two measures are generally opposed, and algorithms have been proposed
[
Kumar et al.(2018)Kumar, Purohit, and Svitkina
]
trading one for the other.

Recently a proposal for exteding the analysis of online algorithms with preditions to a multiagent setting has been made
[
Anonymized Author(s)(2025)
]
. A multiagent extension of the ski-rental problem was introduced, and studied assuming that each agent is endowed with
two predictors
: one for its (future) behavior, and one for the behavior of all other agents. The goal of
[
Anonymized Author(s)(2025)
]
was to characterize the best possible competitive ratios attainable in the four cases, corresponding to each of these predictors being exact and pessimal, respectively. However, no attempt was made in
[
Anonymized Author(s)(2025)
]
to actually define a game-theoretic notion of equilibrium appropriate for agents using algorithms with predictions.

Contributions.
Our main conceptual contribution is
the definition of a notion of equilibrium with predictions
for multiagent competitive online problems
[
Borodin and El-Yaniv(2005)
]
. We investigate such equilibria in the multiagent ski-rental problem. We aim to answer the following questions:

(1).

what properties do equilibria with (and without) predictions for the multiagent ski rental problem have? what is the competitive ratio achieved by agents playing such equilibria?

(2).

(how) can algorithms employ predictions to improve their competitive ratio when playing an equilibrium strategy profile?

We give the following answers to the questions above:

-

We characterize (Theorem
1
) (predictionless) competitive ratio equilibria, as well as (Theorem
4
) those arising when agents predict others (and respond rationally), but don’t self-predict.

-

We define equilibria with predictions and show (Theorem
5
) that they have properties reminiscent of their counterparts in the case with no self-predictions.

We also give an answer to question (2) by studying (theoretically and experimentally) an algorithm (introduced in
[
Anonymized Author(s)(2025)
]
) with a tunable parameter
\lambda=1
. One can implement the equilibrium behavior of Theorem
4
by having all agents use Algorithm
1
with parameter
\lambda=1
. We investigate the scenario when (unknown to other agents) one particular agent deviates from this equilibrium behavior by using a different value of
\lambda
. Our conclusions, in a nutshell, are as follows:

-

We can precisely characterize theoretically (Theorem
6
) the worst-case consistency and robustness of Algorithm
1
on all equilibria. Giving such guarantees for the algorithm was not feasible when run assuming random models for others’ behavior
[
Anonymized Author(s)(2025)
]
. In particular, for certain values of
\lambda

using correct predictors provably helps in achieving a lower competitive ratio
.

-

When investigated experimentally, some of the equilibria turn out to be ”trivial”, in that the predictionless algorithm is able to find optimal (1-competitive) solutions for most instances. For such equilibria adding self-predictions is pointless.

-

For ”nontrivial” equilibria, the behavior of is Algorithm
1
is qualitatively similar to that of the corresponding algorithm for the multiagent ski-rental with prediction problem
[
Kumar et al.(2018)Kumar, Purohit, and Svitkina
]
: the performance of the algorithm with predictions is best for exact predictors, and degrades when prediction error increases. It seems to become more effective the later the day of license acquisition at equilibrium is.

Broader impact.
The classical notion of Nash equilibrium implicitly assumes that agents perfectly predict the behavior of the other agents, and best respond to this prediction. It is natural to inquire about a notion of ”equilibrium with predictions” in settings where agents may be risk-averse (rather than risk neutral). Such agents are able to strategically exploit the predictor, obtaining better results when the predictor is correct, even in the presence of strategic behavior.

Our paper provides a plausible first possible formulation for such a notion for competitive online games with predictions, and provides a proof-of-concept analysis for the newly introduced formulation that showcases its feasibility. It is our hope that the current problem brings attention to the important aspects of the interplay of prediction and optimization in multiagent systems, and that the formalism we introduce proves useful beyond multiagent ski-rental.

More generally, we hope that our research brings more attention from the scientific community to the strategic (game-theoretic) aspects of algorithms with predictions, and that it is adapted to general non-cooperative models, as well as to more realistic versions of game-theoretic scenarios, such as
dynamic games

[
Başar and Olsder(1998)
]
, in particular to open-loop models of such games
[
Fudenberg and Levine(1988)
]
.

Outline.
The plan of the paper is as follows: in Section
3

In this manuscript we will present the main ideas of our proofs

An

Other standard concepts in the competitive analysis of online algorithms are reviewed e.g. in

]
.

An online algorithm

x\in\Omega

\widehat{Z}

in a ”prediction space”

. The following approach was proposed in
[

]

\widehat{Z}
for the future, the performance of an online algorithm is measured not by one, but by a pair of competitive ratios:

[
Kumar et al.(2018)Kumar, Purohit, and Svitkina
,
Gollapudi and Panigrahi(2019)
,

Wei and Zhang(2020)
]
consistency/robustness are

Definition 1
.

An online algorithm with predictions

is

(that may depend on
\gamma
, but not on
\widehat{Z}
) such that for all values
\widehat{Z}
of the predictor

cost(A(x,\widehat{Z}))\leq\gamma\cdot cost(OPT(x))+d

(1)

Strong

-robustness is obtained in the special case when

. The algorithm is
\beta
-consistent
if

cost(A(x,\widehat{Z}=Z))\leq\beta\cdot cost(OPT(x)).

(2)

If the guarantees hold for every input
x
(with the same constant

) we will call the algorithm (strongly)
\gamma
-robust and
\beta
-consistent.

Consider a
n
-person competitive online game
[
Engelberg and Naor(2016)
]
.
A

in such a game is a vector of online algorithms

, one for each player. Given strategy profile

denote by

the set of strategy profiles where the
i
’th agent plays a best-response (i.e. an algorithm minimizing its competitive ratio) to the other agents playing
W
. A

is a strategy profile
(W_{1},W_{2},\ldots,W_{n})
such that each

is a best-response to the programs of the other agents.

A
competitive online game with predictions
is the variant of the previous setting obtained by assuming that agents employ instead algorithms with predictions. We will consider such games under a
two-predictor model
: we assume that every agent
i
comes with two predictors - a

for its own future, and an

, for the actions of other agents.
A formalization of this concept was sketched in
[
Anonymized Author(s)(2025)
]
. The reader might want to consult this paper (although such a general definition is not really needed for understanding the results in this paper).

The Multiagent Ski-Rental Problem

The main problem we are concerned with in this paper is the following
multiagent ski-rental problem
:

Definition 2

(Multiagent Ski Rental
[
Anonymized Author(s)(2025)
]
)
.

n
agents are initially active but may become inactive.
2
2

2

once an agent becomes inactive it will be inactive forever.
Active agents need a resource for their daily activity. Each day, active agents have the option to (individually) rent the resource, at a cost of 1$/day. They can also cooperate in order to to buy a group license that will cost
B>1
dollars. For this, each agent
i
may pledge some
amount
w_{r}>0
or
refrain from pledging
(equivalently, pledge 0).
3
3

3

We assume that both pledges
w_{r}
and the price
B
are integers.
If the total sum pledged is at least
B
then the group license is acquired,
4
4

4

When the license is overpledged agents pay their pledged sums.
and the use of the resource becomes free for all remaining active agents from that moment on. Otherwise (we call such pledges
inconsequential
) the pledges are nullified. Instead, every active agent must (individually) rent
the resource for the day. Agents are strategic, in that they care about their overall costs. They are faced, on the other hand, with deep uncertainty concerning the number of days they will be active. So they choose instead to minimize their competitive ratio, rather than their total cost.

We will also use the following variant of classical ski-rental:

Definition 3

(Ski Rental With Known Varying Prices)
.

An agent is facing a ski-rental problem where the buying price varies from day to day (as opposed to the cost of renting, which is always 1$). Denote by
0\leq p_{i}\leq B
the cost of buying skis
exactly on day
i
, and let
P_{i}=i-1+p_{i}
be the
total cost if buying exactly on day
i
(including the cost of renting on the previous
i-1
days). Finally, let
p=(p_{i})_{i\geq 1}
. We truncate
p
at the first
i
such that
p_{i}=0
(such an
i
will be called a
free day
). Call a day
i
such that
p_{i}=1
a
bargain day
. Define
M_{*}(p)=min(P_{i}:i\geq 1)
and, for
t\geq 1
,
Q_{t}=min(P_{i}:i\geq t)
.

The following classes of algorithms pertain to ski-rental with varying prices and multiagent ski-rental:

-

A
predictionless algorithm
is a function
f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
.
f(t)
represents the amount pledged by the agent at time
t
. A predictionless algorithm uses no information: it does not employ any predictions (about self-behavior or the behavior of others).

-

An
algorithm with (self and others) predictions
is a function
f(k,\widehat{T},\widehat{p})
. Here
k\geq 1
is an integer,
\widehat{T}\geq 1
is a prediction of the true active time of the agent, and
\widehat{p}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\{0,\ldots,B\}

-

An algorithm with predictions is

if for every

, the agent either pledges just enough to buy the license (according to the others’ predictor) or pledges 0.

-

An algorithm with predictions is
simple
if whenever
\widehat{T_{1}},\widehat{T_{2}}<M_{*}(\widehat{p})
or
\widehat{T_{1}},\widehat{T_{2}}\geq M_{*}(\widehat{p})
then
f(k,\widehat{T_{1}},\widehat{p})=f(k,\widehat{T_{2}},\widehat{p})
. That is, what matters for the prediction is whether or not
\widehat{T}\geq M_{*}(\widehat{p})
.

Theorem 1
.

The following are true:

(a).

In every competitive ratio equilibrium the license is eventually bought on some day.

(b).

(ii).

for all

Equilibria with Predictions: Decision-Theoretic Perspective and Definition

Suppose
n
agents play a given game. Each agent is endowed with some beliefs(predictions), possibly probabilistic, on the behavior of the other players. Decision theory (see e.g.
[
Bonanno(2017)
]
) has considered several possible courses of action for an individual agent, including:

-

MaxiMin:
The agent chooses an action maximizing its payoff,
assuming worst-case(adversarial) behavior of other agents
.

-

MaxiMax:
the agent chooses a course of action maximizing its payoff,
assuming best-case behavior of other agents
.

-

Best Response:
The agent chooses the action that maximizes its expected utility, given its beliefs about the actions of other players. When all players employ this strategy
and are able to perfectly predict their opponents’ behavior
, the resulting concept is the well-known Nash Equilibrium.

The first and third courses of action listed above represent two extremes in acting upon one’s beliefs: in one case (MaxiMin) one completely disregards them and ”act safely”. In the other case, the appropriateness of the (best-response) action of a given agent is contingent on the exactness of its prediction on the behavior of its opponents. In reality, agents may want to play strategies other than expected maximization - this is, for instance, the case if agents are
risk-seeking
. Nash equilibria with different optimization strategies have been investigated (several relevant references include
[
Fiat and Papadimitriou(2010)
,
Slumbers et al.(2023)Slumbers, Mguni, Blumberg, Mcaleer, Yang, and
Wang
,
Mavronicolas and Monien(2015)
,
Mavronicolas and Monien(2020)
]
). However, sometimes one may want to combine several courses of action: for instance, the Hurwicz index of pessimism (e.g.
[
Bonanno(2017)
]
, Section 6.4) is defined as a weighted linear combination of MaxiMin and MaxiMax.

A risk-reward framework for the analysis of online algorithms was proposed in
[
al Binali(1999)
]
(see also
[
Ding et al.(2005)Ding, Xin, and Chen
,
Ma and Wang(2010)
,
Ni et al.(2009)Ni, Xu, and Xu
]
for subsequent work using it), and rediscovered in an isomorphic formulation in
[
Lykouris and Vassilvtiskii(2018)
,
Kumar et al.(2018)Kumar, Purohit, and Svitkina
]
, in the context of measuring the performance of online algorithms with predictions. Specifically, given a predictor
\widehat{T}
for the future, the performance of an online algorithm is measured not by one, but by a pair of real numbers:
consistency
, the competitive ratio achieved in the case where the predictor is perfect, and
robustness
, the competitive ratio in the case where the predictor is pessimal. Unlike the Hurwicz index of pessimism, in
[
Kumar et al.(2018)Kumar, Purohit, and Svitkina
,
Gollapudi and Panigrahi(2019)
,
Angelopoulos et al.(2020)Angelopoulos, Dürr, Jin, Kamali, and
Renault
,
Wei and Zhang(2020)
]
consistency/robustness are
not
aggregated into a single performance measure. Instead, one computes the Pareto frontier of the two measures, and/or optimizes consistency for a desired degree of robustness.

We can employ a similar approach to define equilibria with predictions.
Specifically, we will keep from Nash equilibria (that count competitive ratio equilibria as a special case) the crucial assumption that

In Theorem

8
Limitations of our Study

As with results in
[
Anonymized Author(s)(2025)
]
, our results only deal with
pure
equilibria. It is an open problem to adapt the results from that paper (and this one) to the case of mixed equilibria.

Second, our results are currently limited by our present inability to provide a convincing measure of the accuracy of the others’ prediction, interpolating between perfect and pessimal predictions. This limits our definition of equilibria with predictions to self-predictions. Giving a definition of equilibria with predictions that deals with others’ predictions as well is an interesting challenge.

9
Conclusions and Open Problems

Our work raises many open questions.
The most important of them are, we believe, those discussed in the previous section. Equally important is the problem of obtaining more general results on equilibria with predictions, perhaps in the general framework sketched in
[
Anonymized Author(s)(2025)
]
.

On the other hand predictions in this paper are one-shot events: agents get this knowledge from the outset. A more natural model is that of an algorithm with
continuous, time-limited predictions
8
8

8

real-life predictions, such as weather forecasts, have this nature.
. Perhaps a study of (a multiagent version of)
the Bahncard problem

[
Fleischer(2001)
]
in a model with an evolving prediction window is feasible. Note that the single-agent version of this problem has already been considered in a perspective with predictions
[
Ding et al.(2005)Ding, Xin, and Chen
]
, but we believe that even this single-agent case deserves more attention (and work).

There exists a huge, multi-discipline literature that deals with learning game-theoretic equilibria. In such a setting, an equilibrium arises through repeated interactions (see, e.g.
[
Kalai and Lehrer(1993)
,
Young(2004)
,
Narang et al.(2023)Narang, Faulkner, Drusvyatskiy, Fazel, and
Ratliff
]
). Applying such results to the setting with predictions and, generally, allowing predictions to be
adaptive
(i.e. depend on previous interactions) is left to further research.

Also interesting is the issue of obtaining tradeoffs between parameters of approximate equilibria (similar to
[
Gollapudi and Panigrahi(2019)
,
Angelopoulos et al.(2020)Angelopoulos, Dürr, Jin, Kamali, and
Renault
,
Wei and Zhang(2020)
]
for the one-agent case). This amounts, in the setting of Theorem
5
, to deciding for which
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n},\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
do such equilibria exist.

Finally, our notion of equilibrium with predictions builds on
Nash equilibria. But there exist other game-theoretic alternatives: A
Kantian competitive ratio equilibrium
(see e.g.
[
Roemer(2019)
,
Istrate(2021)
]
for details about Kantian optimization) is a strategy profile
\Delta_{P}=(P,P,\ldots,P)
that minimizes the competitive ratio of
P
among all inputs of type
\Delta_{Q}
. A model studied in
[
Engelberg and Naor(2016)
]
characterized (without using this terminology) Kantian equilibria (without predictions) for a number of problems. It would be interesting to understand predictionless Kantian equilibria for multiagent ski-rental. It would be even
more
interesting to define a suitable notion of Kantian equilibria with predictions.
