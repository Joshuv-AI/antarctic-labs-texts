Abstract
. We introduce a spatial economic growth model where space is described as a network of interconnected geographic locations and we study a corresponding finite-dimensional optimal control problem on a graph with state constraints. Economic growth models on networks are motivated by the nature of spatial economic data, which naturally possess a graph-like structure: this fact makes these models well-suited for numerical implementation and calibration. The network setting is different from the one adopted in the related literature, where space is modeled as a subset of a Euclidean space, which gives rise to infinite dimensional optimal control problems. After introducing the model and the related control problem, we prove existence and uniqueness of an optimal control and a regularity result for the value function, which sets up the basis for a deeper study of the optimal strategies. Then, we focus on specific cases where it is possible to find, under suitable assumptions, an explicit solution of the control problem. Finally, we discuss the cases of networks of two and three geographic locations.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation of the paper

The classical theory of economic growth is related, from the technical viewpoint, to the study of optimal control problems with state and control constraints.
Such problems were studied in various papers in the mathematical and economic literature; we refer the reader to the book by
Barro and Sala-i Martin 2004
, on the economic side, and to the books by
Seierstad and Sydsæter 1987
and by
Yong and Zhou 1999
, on the mathematical side.

In recent years, some research papers dealt with spatial heterogeneity of economic growth, which is due to the geographic distribution of the relevant economic variables. In the literature, various dynamic economic models of growth appeared, in which a spatial dimension is explicitly taken into account.
Most of these models considered a continuum of geographic locations distributed on a subset of a Euclidean space (typically, the unit circle or the unit sphere) and a state equation given by a parabolic Partial Differential Equation (PDE). From a mathematical point of view, this choice led to the study of infinite-dimensional optimal control problems with state constraints, see, e.g.,
Boucekkine et al. 2013
,
Boucekkine et al. 2009
,
Boucekkine et al. 2018
,
Brito 2004
,
Calvia et al. 2021
,
Fabbri 2016
,
Gozzi and Leocata 2022
,
Xepapadeas and Yannacopoulos 2016
,
Xepapadeas and Yannacopoulos 2023
.

One of the main drawbacks of these models is that, except for special cases where the explicit form of the value function can be found, it is very difficult to get information on the regularity of the value function and, consequently, to prove verification theorems and to find optimal feedback strategies.
Moreover, difficulties arise in numerical simulations due to the curse of dimensionality and it is also nontrivial to model the spatial movement of the economic variables in such a setting.

In light of these facts, it appears reasonable and interesting to study models of economic growth where the space variable is described as a network of interconnected geographic locations. Mathematically speaking, by network we mean a weighted graph, whose nodes are the locations (such as cities, regions, etc.), arcs are the connections between some of them, and weights are given importance levels of each connection.
The weighted graph structure, in contrast to models with a continuum of locations, gives more realism to capital transport and matches the nature of spatial economic data, which have exactly such a network feature (see, e.g.,
Allen and Arkolakis 2014
). This fact makes these models well-suited for numerical implementation and calibration.

In this paper, we consider an economic growth problem on networks, where at each site (i.e., node) there is capital creation and consumption. We consider the case where the production function is linear, i.e., the so-called AK production function, a choice in line with most of the aforementioned literature. We model capital mobility through a linear operator
L
, which can be, for instance, the discrete Laplacian (cf. Remark
2.2
). The choice of a general linear operator allows for more flexibility of our model, as opposed to the ones studied in the previously mentioned literature. In particular, it allows us to take into account different economic phenomena, such as capital concentration or dispersion (see, e.g.,
Xepapadeas and Yannacopoulos 2016
).

We study an optimal control problem where a centralized social planner aims at finding the optimal consumption path at each geographic location to maximize a given aggregate discounted utility. Clearly, capital levels must remain non-negative at each time and at each node of the graph, and hence we face an optimal control problem in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with state constraints, where
n
is the number of nodes in the graph.

1.2.
Main contributions of the paper

In this paper we establish some results (of which we give a brief summary below) for the class of economic growth models on networks that we introduce. These results are key towards a complete analysis of such models; our ultimate goals are to characterize the value function of the control problem and the optimal paths of the state and the control variables (i.e., capital and consumption), and to uncover how they depend on the network structure. These goals require a deeper analysis of the state constrained optimal control problem that we face, mainly because standard results, as the ones given in
Soner 1986
, cannot be applied or directly generalized. Indeed, we consider linear state dynamics for capital movements across the network and an unbounded utility of consumption. This is in contrast with the cases usually considered in the literature, which assume the vector fields in the state equations and the running gain/cost functions to be bounded and Lipschitz. In particular, we consider a strictly increasing and concave utility function, thus neither linear nor quadratic, a fact that prevents us from applying a more direct approach to solve the optimization problem.
Thus, to achieve our goals new techniques need to be developed, which is a task left for future research.

Nonetheless, as anticipated above, we prove in this paper the following important results:

(i)

We show in Theorem
4.6
that the value function is a bilateral viscosity solution of a Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation (in the sense of
Bardi and Capuzzo-Dolcetta 1997
, i.e., it is a viscosity solution of the HJB and of its opposite) in the positive orthant of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
n
is the number of nodes in the graph. We also prove in Theorem
4.7
that the value function is continuously differentiable in the interior of the positive orthant of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(ii)

We give the optimal feedback map in the interior of the positive orthant of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, cf. Corollary
4.8
.

(iii)

In some specific cases, where we describe more precisely capital mobility on the network, we write explicitly the value function, under suitable conditions on the data, cf. Equation (
5.17
), by studying an associated auxiliary optimal control problem. See Proposition
5.5
and Theorem
5.7
.

(iv)

In the specific cases mentioned above, we establish a result on the long run behavior of optimal capital and consumption paths, cf. Proposition
5.11
and Theorem
5.12
. We also analyze in Proposition
5.13
the behavior of the growth rate with respect to the connectivity of the graph.

(v)

We study the two-nodes case with symmetric graph weights, where we further characterize the properties of the value function in the cases in which it can be explicitly computed, cf. Proposition
5.14
and Proposition
5.15
. Finally, we discuss some numerical simulations in the three-nodes case with symmetric graph weights.

Our main result is the regularity, i.e., continuous differentiability, of the value function in the interior of the positive orthant of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
On the one hand, this allows us to obtain a feedback characterization of optimal controls in the interior of the positive orthant of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is crucial to qualitatively study and numerically approximate optimal paths.
On the other hand, this extends earlier results by
Cannarsa and Soner 1987
(see also
Bardi and Capuzzo-Dolcetta 1997
,
Cannarsa and Sinestrari 2004
, and
Freni et al. 2008
in a economic growth problem) to cases where the utility function may be unbounded at infinity and/or near zero.

To find the explicit form of the value function, as mentioned above in point
(iii)
, and to study the corresponding optimal paths, we adapt the ideas used in the infinite-dimensional case analyzed in
Boucekkine et al. 2018
and in
Calvia et al. 2021
.
It is worth noting that we provide a sufficient condition in Proposition
5.5
to have explicit solutions of the HJB equation related to the optimal control problem in terms of a (possibly nonlinear) eigenvalue problem. This result and the condition given in (
5.17
), do not have equivalent counterparts in the infinite-dimensional setting cited above.

Finally, we note that the results given in Section
5.2
and the numerical simulations discussed in Section
5.3
provide some starting ideas to understand the connection between the graph structure and the qualitative properties of the solution of the optimization problem. We believe that it is interesting to develop this study, which is left for future research.

We conclude the introduction by outlining the plan of the paper. In Section
2
we introduce the economic growth model and the optimal control problem. In Section
3
we establish existence and uniqueness of the optimal control and some useful properties of the value function, which are then exploited in Section
4
to prove our main results, outlined in points
(i)
and
(ii)
above.
In Section
5
we detail the connection of the control problem with the network structure and we provide the results on the explicit solutions mentioned in point
(iii)
above. Finally, in Sections
5.2
and
5.3
we discuss the two-nodes and the three-nodes cases, respectively.

2.
The optimal control problem

In this section we introduce the optimal control problem under study.
We are given a
network
, describing a finite number of geographically distributed locations,
n\geq 2
, where capital is created and there is consumption. In the first part of the paper, we are not going to introduce an explicit modelization of said network, as we will only be interested in providing general results on the optimization problem. Starting from Section
5
, we model this network as a weighted graph and we will derive more specific results.
At each site
i=1,\dots,n
, capital is created according to an AK production function: the output of the economy at location
i
is
(\bar{A}_{i}-\delta_{i})k_{i}
, where
k_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is the current level of capital,
\bar{A}_{i}>0
is the technological level of site
i
, and
\delta_{i}\geq 0
parameterizes a capital deterioration effect. In order to spare parameters, we are given the coefficients
1
1

1

Positivity of
A_{i}
is assumed to make the economic problem meaningful.

A_{i}\coloneqq(\bar{A}_{i}-\delta_{i})>0
,
i=1,\dots,n
, i.e., we assume that each technological level incorporates a capital deterioration effect.
We are also given

•

An operator
L\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, modeling capital mobility on the network, that is, the fact that capital may enter or exit any location as a result of interactions between them;

•

An operator
N\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, that describes the impact of consumption levels on the state of the system, i.e., capital.

In what follows, we denote the capital and consumption levels at site
i=1,\dots,n
and time
t\geq 0
by
K_{i}(t)
and
C_{i}(t)
, respectively. We also introduce the vectors
K(t)\coloneqq(K_{1}(t),\dots,K_{n}(t))
,
C(t)\coloneqq(C_{1}(t),\dots,C_{n}(t))
, and the diagonal matrix
A=\mathrm{diag}(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
.

Given the data above, a vector
k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
of initial capital endowments, and a consumption path
C\in{\mathrm{L}}^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,+\infty);\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
, we assume that the dynamics of
K
are described by the following ODE

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dfrac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}K(t)=(L+A)K(t)-NC(t),\quad t\geq 0,\\
&K(0)=k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}.\end{aligned}\right.

Standard results ensure that ODE (
2.1
) has a unique solution, denoted by
K^{k,C}
, which verifies

(2.2)

K^{k,C}(t)={\mathrm{e}}^{t(L+A)}k-\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-s)(L+A)}NC(s)\,{\mathrm{d}}s,\quad t\geq 0.

The following assumption will be in force throughout the paper.

Assumption 2.1
.

(i)

L
is a linear operator generating a positive
{\mathrm{C}}_{0}
-semigroup
({\mathrm{e}}^{tL})_{t\geq 0}
, i.e.,
{\mathrm{e}}^{tL}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, for all
t\geq 0
.

(ii)

N
is a positivity preserving linear operator, i.e.,
N(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
. Moreover,
N{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\neq{\mathbf{0}}
, for all
i=1,\dots,n
.

Thanks to Assumption
2.1
-
(i)
, the operator
L
is represented by an
n\times n
matrix, still denoted by
L
, with entries
\ell_{ij}
,
i,j=1,\dots,n
. This assumption also ensures that
({\mathrm{e}}^{tL})_{t\geq 0}
is a positive linear system, that is, for every non-negative
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, we have that
{\mathrm{e}}^{tL}k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, for all
t\geq 0
, cf.
[
16
, Definition 2]
. This is equivalent to the requirement that
L
is a Metzler matrix, i.e.,
\ell_{ij}\geq 0
, for all
i\neq j
,
i,j=1,\dots,n
, cf.
[
16
, Theorem 2]
. Since
A
is diagonal, also
L+A
is a Metzler matrix, that is,
({\mathrm{e}}^{t(L+A)})_{t\geq 0}
is a positive linear system. This implies that, if we choose a zero-consumption path, i.e., if
C(t)=0
, for all
t\geq 0
, then the solution (
2.2
) to (
2.1
) is non-negative for every non-negative initial capital endowment. Positive linear systems enjoy various important properties (see, e.g.,
[
16
]
), that are used throughout the paper and recalled whenever useful.

Assumption
2.1
-
(ii)
ensures that operator
N
is represented by an
n\times n
matrix, still denoted by
N
, with entries
n_{ij}\geq 0
, for all
i,j=1,\dots,n
. Indeed, invariance of the closed positive orthant with respect to
N
is equivalent to
N
being a non-negative matrix. Moreover, the second part of this hypothesis entails that, for each
i=1,\dots,n
, there exists
j=1,\dots,n
such that
n_{ij}>0
. From an economic point of view, this means that each consumption level affects on the evolution of capital in one node at least.

We now proceed to introduce the optimal control problem that we aim to study. Given a discount factor
\rho>0
and, for fixed
\gamma>0
, let
u_{\gamma}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
be a utility function of the form

(2.3)

u_{\gamma}(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{x^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma},&\text{if }\gamma\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty),\\
\log(x),&\text{if }\gamma=1,\end{cases}\qquad x\geq 0.

Consider a social planner, who aims at controlling consumption levels
C
to maximize the gain functional

(2.4)

J(C)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\rho t}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}u_{\gamma}(C_{i}(t))\right){\mathrm{d}}t,\quad C\in{\mathcal{A}}_{+}(k),\,k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},

where
p_{i}>0
,
i=1,\dots,n
, are given weights and the set of admissible controls
{\mathcal{A}}_{+}(k)
is defined as

(2.5)

{\mathcal{A}}_{+}(k)\coloneqq\{C\in{\mathrm{L}}^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,+\infty);\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}),\text{ s.t. }K^{k,C}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\forall t\geq 0\}.

We are, thus, interested in non-negative controls (i.e., consumption plans) that keep the capital levels non-negative.
The value function of this optimal control problem is

(2.6)

V(k)=\sup_{C\in{\mathcal{A}}_{+}(k)}J(C),\quad k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}.

In the following, we denote by
U_{\gamma}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
,
\gamma>0
, the function

(2.7)

U_{\gamma}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}u_{\gamma}(x_{i}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},

so that the gain functional introduced in (
2.4
) can be written as

J(C)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\rho t}U_{\gamma}(C(t)){\mathrm{d}}t,\quad C\in{\mathcal{A}}_{+}(k),\,k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}.

Remark 2.2
.

A natural choice for
L
is the so-called
discrete Laplacian
, that we adopt starting from Section
5
. A possible choice for
N
, different from the identity matrix, is to consider
N=\mathrm{diag}(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
, where
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
are the weights appearing in functional

introduced in (
2.4
). These weigths may be related to the population level at each location

or other subjective criteria taken into account by the social planner.

Remark 2.3
.

Various results proved in Sections
3
and
4
can be extended to the general case of utility functions that are increasing, concave, and either bounded from above or from below. To do so, one can adopt the approach adopted in
[
1
,
5
]
.

3.
Existence of an optimal control and properties of the value function

In this section we are going to prove that, under suitable assumptions, there exists a unique optimal control for the optimization problem introduced in Section
2
. This is a fundamental result that will be used in Section
4
, to characterize the value function
V
, given in (
2.6
), as a viscosity solution of a suitable HJB equation.
Then, we are going to establish important properties of the value function.

To begin with, we note that Assumption
2.1
-
(i)
ensures that also
(L+A)^{T}
is a Metzler matrix, and thus
({\mathrm{e}}^{t(L+A)^{T}})_{t\geq 0}
is a positive linear system, too. This implies (cf.
[
16
, Theorem 11]
) that the dominant eigenvalue of
(L+A)^{T}
is unique and real, called the
Frobenius eigenvalue
and denoted by
\lambda_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}
.

We introduce the following assumption, that will be used through Section
3
and Section
4
.

Assumption 3.1
.

(i)

There exists an eigenvector
b^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
of
(L+A)^{T}
, with eigenvalue
\lambda_{\star}>0
.

(ii)

\rho>\lambda_{\star}(1-\gamma)
.

In the Section
5
, certain assumptions will be determined, which are sufficient conditions for the assumption
3.1
.

Remark 3.2
.

Positivity of
({\mathrm{e}}^{t(L+A)^{T}})_{t\geq 0}
grants the existence of positive eigenvectors (i.e., in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
) associated to the Frobenius eigenvalue, called
Frobenius eigenvectors
. However, this fact alone is not enough to guarantee that Assumption
3.1
-
(i)
is satisfied, since
\lambda_{\star}
may be nonpositive. It is also worth recalling that eigenvectors associated to eigenvalues different from the Frobenius eigenvalue cannot be strictly positive, cf.
[
16
, Theorem 11]
.

Our first result of this section concerns the existence and uniqueness of an optimal strategy with finite utility. We omit the proof, which can be carried out as in
[
17
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Proposition 3.3
.

The value function
V
is finite and for every initial state
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, there exists a unique optimal strategy
\widehat{C}\in\mathcal{A}_{+}(k)
for problem (
2.6
).

Remark 3.4
.

Proposition
3.3
can be extended to more general utility functions using the approach adopted in
[
1
,

]
.

In the next proposition, we show important regularity properties of value function
V
, introduced in (
2.6
).
We recall that, given
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
v\in{\mathrm{C}}(E)
, and
k\in E
, the
superdifferential
of
v
at
k
is the set

{\mathrm{D}}^{+}v(k)\coloneqq\left\{q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon\limsup_{x\to k,\,x\in E}\dfrac{v(x)-v(k)-\langle p,x-k\rangle}{\lVert x-k\rVert}\leq 0\right\}.

Proposition 3.5
.

(i)

V
is component-wise increasing in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, i.e., for all
i=1,\dots,n
and for all
k,k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, such that
k_{i}\leq k^{\prime}_{i}
and
k_{j}=k^{\prime}_{j}
,

, it holds

.

(ii)

V
is concave in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
and locally Lipschitz continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
.

(iii)

For any
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
,
{\mathrm{D}}^{+}V(k)
is non-empty and
{\mathrm{D}}^{+}V(k)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
.

(iv)

If
\gamma\neq 1
, then
V
is
(1-\gamma)
-homogeneous
, i.e.,
V(\alpha k)=\alpha^{1-\gamma}V(k)
, for each
\alpha>0
and
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
.

(v)

If
0<\gamma<1
, then the value function

is continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
.

Proof.

Points
(i)
and
(ii)
can be shown as in
[
18
, Proposition 4.2-(i), (iii)]
, exploiting the linearity of the state equation (

) and the concavity of
U_{\gamma}
, defined in (

). Similarly, the proof of point
(iv)
can be carried out as in
[
18
, Proposition 4.2-(ii)]
.

We prove, next, point
(iii)
. Fix
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
. By
[
13
, Theorem A.1.13]
, concavity of
V
entails that
{\mathrm{D}}^{+}V(k)\neq\emptyset
. It also implies that
{\mathrm{D}}^{+}V(k)
coincides with the definition of the semidifferential of

at
k
from convex analysis (cf.
[

, Remark 3.3.2]
), i.e.,
q\in{\mathrm{D}}^{+}V(k)
if and only if

(3.1)

V(x)-V(k)\leq\langle q,x-k\rangle,\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}.

Suppose, by contradiction, that there exists
q\in{\mathrm{D}}^{+}V(k)
such that
q_{i}\leq 0
, for some
i=1,\dots,n
. Since
V
is component-wise increasing and concave, this implies (by (

)) that the restriction of

to the half-line
k+\alpha e_{i}

Let
C^{\star}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{+}(k)
be the optimal control for

\alpha,\varepsilon>0

\bar{k}=k+\alpha e_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
, and let
\bar{C}(t)=C^{\star}(t)+\mathbf{1}_{[0,\varepsilon]}(t)\delta e_{i}
,

4.
Dynamic Programming Principle and the HJB equation

In this section we are going to show that the value function
V
, given in (
2.6
), satisfies both the Dynamic Programming Principle and the Backward Dynamic Programming Principle. A key result towards proving that
V
is a bilateral viscosity solution (precise definitions are given below) of a suitable HJB equation.

The following lemma asserts that
V
verifies the Dynamic Programming Principle. We omit the proof, since it is standard (see, e.g.,
[
3
]
).

Lemma 4.1

(Dynamic Programming Principle)
.

For all
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
and
t>0
,

(4.1)

V(k)=\sup_{C\in\mathcal{A}_{+}(k)}\left[\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\rho s}U_{\gamma}(C(s))\,{\mathrm{d}}s+{\mathrm{e}}^{-\rho t}V(K^{k,C}(t))\right].

If Assumption
3.1
is verified, then the supremum in (
4.1
) is attained at
\widehat{C}
, where
\widehat{C}
is optimal for
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, i.e.,

(4.2)

V(k)=\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\rho s}U_{\gamma}(\widehat{C}(s))\,{\mathrm{d}}s+{\mathrm{e}}^{-\rho t}V(K^{k,\widehat{C}}(t)).

A similar result can be obtained for backward trajectories of the linear system (
2.1
). We need, first, some definitions. For each
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, we denote by
\mathcal{A}_{-}(k)
the set of locally integrable strategies such that the positivity constraint is satisfied by the backward trajectory, namely,

\mathcal{A}_{-}(k)\coloneqq\{C\in{\mathrm{L}}^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}((-\infty,0];\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})\text{ s.t. }K^{k,C}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\,\forall t\leq 0\},

where
K^{k,C}
is the unique solution to the backward ODE,

\begin{cases}\dfrac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}K(t)=(L+A)K(t)-NC(t),\quad t<0,\\
K(0)=k,\end{cases}

which is given by (
2.2
).

Definition 4.2
.

We say that
k_{0}
is an optimal point if there exists an initial condition
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
,
t>0
, and an optimal strategy
\widehat{C}\in\mathcal{A}_{+}(k)
such that
k_{0}=K^{k,\widehat{C}}(t)
. We denote by
\mathcal{O}
, the set of optimal points.

Lemma 4.3

(Backward Dynamic Programming Principle)
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
,
t>0
, and
C\in\mathcal{A}_{-}(k)
the value function satisfies the following inequality,

(4.3)

V(k)\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{\rho t}V(K^{k,C}(-t))-\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{\rho s}U_{\gamma}(C(-s))\,{\mathrm{d}}s.

Moreover, if
k_{0}\in\mathcal{O}
, there exists
\overline{C}\in\mathcal{A}_{-}(k_{0})
such that

(4.4)

V(k_{0})={\mathrm{e}}^{\rho t}V(K^{k_{0},\overline{C}}(-t))-\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{\rho s}U_{\gamma}(\overline{C}(-s))\,{\mathrm{d}}s.

Proof.

Consider
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
,
t>0
, and
C\in\mathcal{A}_{-}(k)
. Set
k_{0}\coloneqq K^{k,C}(-t)
, define
\widetilde{C}(s)\coloneqq C(s-t)
, with
0\leq s\leq t
, and extend it to zero for
s>t
. Then, using (
2.2
), it is quite straightforward to prove that
K^{k_{0},\widetilde{C}}(\tau)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, for
\tau\leq t
; moreover, by the positivity of
({\mathrm{e}}^{t(L+A)})_{t\geq 0}
,
K^{k_{0},\widetilde{C}}(\tau)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, for
\tau>t
. Therefore,
\widetilde{C}\in\mathcal{A}_{+}(k_{0})
.
By applying the Dynamic Programming Principle, i.e., (
4.1
), we get

V(k_{0})\geq\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\rho s}U_{\gamma}(\widetilde{C}(s))\,{\mathrm{d}}s+{\mathrm{e}}^{-\rho t}V(K^{k_{0},\widetilde{C}}(t)).

Multiplying both sides by
{\mathrm{e}}^{\rho t}
, recalling that
K^{k_{0},\widetilde{C}}(t)=k
and
k_{0}=K^{k,C}(-t)
, and making a change of variables in the integral, we conclude that

{\mathrm{e}}^{\rho t}V(K^{k,C}(-t))\geq\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{\rho s}U_{\gamma}(C(-s))\,{\mathrm{d}}s+V(k),

whence (
4.3
). To prove (
4.4
), let us fix
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
and recall that, by Proposition
3.3
,
there exists an optimal control
\widehat{C}\in\mathcal{A}_{+}(k)
. Set
k_{0}\coloneqq K^{k,\widehat{C}}(t)\in{\mathcal{O}}
, define
\overline{C}(s)=\widehat{C}(s+t)
, with
-t\leq s\leq 0
, and extend it to zero for
s<-t
. Reasoning as before, we have that
\overline{C}\in\mathcal{A}_{-}(k_{0})
. Applying (
4.2
), we get

V(k)=\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\rho s}U_{\gamma}(\widehat{C}(s))\,{\mathrm{d}}s+{\mathrm{e}}^{-\rho t}V(K^{k,\widehat{C}}(t)).

Therefore, making similar computations as above, we get (
4.4
).
∎

The HJB equation associated to the optimal control problem (
2.6
) is

(4.5)

\rho v(k)=\langle(L+A)k,{\mathrm{D}}v(k)\rangle+H_{\gamma}({\mathrm{D}}v(k)),\quad k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},

where the Hamiltonian
H_{\gamma}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined, for all
q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, as

(4.6)

H_{\gamma}(q)\coloneqq\sup_{c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}\{-\langle Nc,q\rangle+U_{\gamma}(c)\}.

Standard computations show that the Hamiltonian can be explicitly written as

(4.7)

H_{\gamma}(q)=\begin{cases}\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}[N^{T}q]_{i}^{1-\frac{1}{\gamma}},&\text{if }q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++},\,\gamma\neq 1,\\
\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\left[\log\left(\dfrac{p_{i}}{[N^{T}q]_{i}}\right)-1\right],&\text{if }q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++},\,\gamma=1,\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

with maximizer
\widehat{c}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
given by

(4.8)

\widehat{c}_{i}(q)=\left(\dfrac{p_{i}}{[N^{T}q]_{i}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}},\quad i=1,\dots,n,\quad q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}.

Note that
\widehat{c}
is continuous and its strict positivity derives from the strict positivity of parameters
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
and from Assumption
2.1
-
(ii)
.

Thanks to the dynamic programming principles stated in Lemma
4.1
, we are able to characterize the value function
V
as a bilateral viscosity solution to (
4.5
), following the approach presented in
[
18
]
(see also
[
3
]
). The precise sense of this solution concept is specified in the next definitions.

Definition 4.4
.

A function
v\in{\mathrm{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++})
is a
viscosity subsolution
of (
4.5
) if, for any
\phi\in{\mathrm{C}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++})
and any local maximum point
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
of

,

\rho v(k)-\langle(L+A)k,{\mathrm{D}}\phi(k)\rangle-H_{\gamma}({\mathrm{D}}\phi(k))\leq 0.

Analogously,
v\in{\mathrm{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++})
is a
viscosity supersolution
of (
4.5
) if, for any
\phi\in{\mathrm{C}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++})
and any local minimum point
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
of
v-\phi
,

\rho v(k)-\langle(L+A)k,{\mathrm{D}}\phi(k)\rangle-H_{\gamma}({\mathrm{D}}\phi(k))\geq 0.

Finally,
v\in{\mathrm{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++})
is a
viscosity solution
of (
4.5
) if it is both a viscosity subsolution and supersolution of (
4.5
).

Definition 4.5
.

A function
v\in{\mathrm{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++})
is a
bilateral viscosity solution
of (
4.5
) if it is a viscosity solution of both (
4.5
) and

(4.9)

-\rho v(k)+\langle(L+A)k,{\mathrm{D}}v(k)\rangle+H_{\gamma}({\mathrm{D}}v(k))=0.

We also need to introduce the following family of sets, parameterized by
k\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
,

(4.10)

\mathcal{U}(k)=\{c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\colon\exists\,C\in\mathcal{A}_{-}(k),\tau>0,\text{ s.t. }C(t)=c,\,\forall t\in(-\tau,0]\}

and the so-called
inward Hamiltonian

(4.11)

H_{\mathrm{in}}(k,q)=\sup_{c\in\mathcal{U}(k)}\{-\langle Nc,q\rangle+U_{\gamma}(c)\},\quad(k,q)\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Theorem 4.6
.

The value function
V
is a bilateral viscosity solution of (
4.5
) in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
, namely,

(4.12)

\rho V(k)=\langle(L+A)k,q\rangle+H_{\gamma}(q),\quad\forall\,k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++},\,q\in{\mathrm{D}}^{+}V(k).

Moreover,

(4.13)

\rho V(k)-\langle(L+A)k,q\rangle-H_{\gamma}(q)\leq 0,\quad-\rho V(k)+H_{\mathrm{in}}(k,q)\leq 0,\quad\forall\,k\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\,q\in{\mathrm{D}}^{+}V(k),

where

is the inward Hamiltonian defined in (

).

Proof.

We briefly sketch the proof. To prove that
V
is a bilateral viscosity solution of (
4.5
) in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
it is enough to show that
V
is a viscosity subsolution of both (
4.5
) and (

) in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
, i.e., that, for any
\phi\in{\mathrm{C}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++})
and any local maximum point

of

,

(4.14)

\begin{cases}\rho V(k)-\langle(L+A)k,{\mathrm{D}}\phi(k)\rangle-H_{\gamma}({\mathrm{D}}\phi(k))\leq 0,\\
-\rho V(k)+\langle(L+A)k,{\mathrm{D}}\phi(k)\rangle+H_{\gamma}({\mathrm{D}}\phi(k))\leq 0.\end{cases}

The subsolution property, that is, (

), can be proved as in
[

, Proposition 4.10]
and this is equivalent to showing (

[

, Chapter II, Equation (1.7)]
.
As a consequence of (

is also a viscosity supersolution of both (

. Indeed, since

is continuous and concave on

Theorem 4.7
.

The value function
V
is continuously differentiable in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
,
V\in{\mathrm{C}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++})
, and it is a classical solution for (

.

Proof.

Since

and suppose, by contradiction, that there exist

. Then, since

Corollary 4.8
.

Fix

, consider the corresponding optimal control
\widehat{C}\in\mathcal{A}_{+}(k)
for problem (

), and let
\widehat{K}^{k,\widehat{C}}
be the solution to (

\widehat{C}(t)=\widehat{c}({\mathrm{D}}V(K^{k,\widehat{C}}(t))),

where
\widehat{c}
is the function given in (

Proof.

From Theorem
4.7
, we know that

. By (

The AK model on networks

In this section we specify a model for the network of
n\geq 2
geographically distributed locations introduced in Section
2
. We show that for this model all the previously introduced assumptions are verified, and hence all results proved in Sections
3
and
4
are satisfied. Moreover, we study classical solutions to the HJB equation (
4.5
) and, by introducing a suitable auxiliary optimization problem, we establish conditions under which we can write explicitly the value function of the optimization problem (
2.6
). Finally, in Section
5.2
we discuss some further results in the case where only two geographical locations are involved, i.e., where
n=2
, and in Section
5.3
we provide a numerical study of the case where three locations are considered, i.e.,
n=3
.

5.1.
The general case

We model the network of
n\geq 2
geographically distributed locations by a graph
{\mathscr{G}}\coloneqq({\mathscr{V}},{\mathscr{E}})
, where
{\mathscr{V}}
is a set of vertices, that corresponds to locations, and
{\mathscr{E}}
is a set of edges connecting vertices. We assume that the graph is simple (i.e., not directed), finite, and weighted. We identify
{\mathscr{V}}
with the set
\{1,\dots,n\}
and
{\mathscr{E}}
as a subset of
\{(i,j)\in\{1,\dots,n\}^{2}\text{ s.t. }i\neq j\}
.
We say that two vertices
i,j\in{\mathscr{V}}
are connected, and we write
i\sim j
if there exist an edge connecting them, i.e.,

i\sim j\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(i,j)\in{\mathscr{E}}.

Finally, we denote by
W=(w_{ij})_{i,j\in{\mathscr{V}}}
the matrix of weigths of the graph, with
w_{ij}\geq 0
, for all
i,j\in{\mathscr{V}}
. As we assumed the graph to be simple,
W
is symmetric. To ease the notation, we assume that vertices
i,j\in{\mathscr{V}}
are not connected if and only if
w_{ij}=0
. In particular, since there are no self-loops,
w_{ii}=0
, for all
i\in{\mathscr{V}}
. Weights can be interpreted as parameters describing the strength of the economic connection between each pair of nodes. They also allow us to deduce the dynamics of the capital flow in the form of a book keeping equation, as follows.
Fix a node
i\in{\mathscr{V}}
. We assume that at each time
t>0
there is an inflow of capital from each node
j\sim i
, proportional to the capital levels
k_{j}
at these connected nodes at time
t
, with proportionality factor
w_{ij}
. The total inflow to site
i
from connected sites
j
is, thus,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in{\mathscr{V}}\\
j\sim i\end{subarray}}w_{ij}k_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{ij}k_{j}.

Similarly, we assume that at each time
t>0
there is an outflow of capital to each node
j\sim i
, proportional to the capital level
k_{i}
at time
t
, with proportionality factor
w_{ji}=w_{ij}
. Therefore, the total outflow from site
i
to connected sites
j
is

-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in{\mathscr{V}}\\
j\sim i\end{subarray}}w_{ij}k_{i}=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{ij}k_{i}.

Putting together these facts, we can express the dynamics of the capital flow as in (
2.1
), by introducing the symmetric matrix
L=(\ell_{ij})_{i,j\in{\mathscr{V}}}
, with

(5.1)

\ell_{ij}=\begin{cases}w_{ij},&\text{if }i\neq j,\\
-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in{\mathscr{V}}\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}w_{ij},&\text{if }i=j.\end{cases}

We are going to consider the case where the operator
N
appearing in (
2.1
) is the identity and
\gamma\neq 1
. This choice, together with the fact that
L
is a Metzler matrix, entails that Assumption
2.1
is satisfied. Thus, we obtain the optimization problem

(5.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{C\in{\mathcal{A}}_{+}(k)}\,\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\rho t}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\dfrac{C_{i}(t)^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\right){\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dfrac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}K(t)=(L+A)K(t)-C(t),\quad t\geq 0,\\
&K(0)=k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\end{aligned}\right.

where the set of admissble controls
{\mathcal{A}}_{+}(k)
is defined in (
2.5
).

Remark 5.1
.

Matrix
-L
, where
L
is defined in (
5.1
), corresponds to the standard definition of the graph Laplacian operator on a weighted graph (see, e.g.,
[
6
]
). It is well known that
-L
generates the positive semidefinite quadratic form

\langle z,(-Lz)\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{ij}(z_{i}-z_{j})^{2}\geq 0
,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Therefore,
-L
admits
n
non-negative eigenvalues, the lowest of which is
0
with corresponding eigenvector
{\mathbf{1}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\cdots&1\end{bmatrix}^{T}
. Naturally,
L
is negative semidefinite
with the same eigenvectors as
-L
and eigenvalues equal in absolute value to those of
L
, but with opposite sign. This fact will be fundamental in what follows.

To introduce the main assumption of this section, we need the following definitions. To the linear system
({\mathrm{e}}^{tL})_{t\geq 0}
we can associate a graph with
n
vertices, called
influence graph
, cf.
[
16
, Chapter 3]
. A directed arc
(i,j)
connects vertices
i
and
j
of the influence graph if and only if
\ell_{ji}\neq 0
, i.e., if the
i
-th variable has a direct influence on the
j
-th one.
If the associated influence graph is connected, i.e., if there is a path between any pair of vertices
i
and
j
, we say that
({\mathrm{e}}^{tL})_{t\geq 0}
is
irreducible
. In other words, irreducibility means that any variable of the linear system
({\mathrm{e}}^{tL})_{t\geq 0}
influences (directly or indirectly) all other variables. Since
L
is symmetric, the influence graph of the linear system
({\mathrm{e}}^{tL})_{t\geq 0}
coincides with graph
{\mathscr{G}}
, except on vertices, because influence graphs may have self-loops. Therefore, to assume that
({\mathrm{e}}^{tL})_{t\geq 0}
is irreducible is equivalent to the fact that
{\mathscr{G}}
is connected, which is in turn equivalent to requiring that the matrix
L
is itself irreducible.
The next proposition collects some useful results on the matrix
L+A
.

Proposition 5.2
.

If the matrix
L
is irreducible, the following results hold true:

(i)

The semigroup
({\mathrm{e}}^{t(L+A)})_{t\geq 0}
is positive and irreducible.

(ii)

Matrix
L+A
is symmetric, therefore the eigenvalues of
L+A
are real and there exists an eigenvector orthonormal basis
\{b^{0},b^{1},\dots,b^{n-1}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with associated eigenvalues
\lambda_{0}\geq\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n-1}
.

(iii)

Eigenvector
b^{0}
is the unique (up to multiplication by a scalar) strictly positive eigenvector of
L+A
; moreover, the corresponding eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
is simple and satisfies

(5.3)

0<\min_{i\in{\mathscr{V}}}A_{i}\leq\lambda_{0}\leq\max_{i\in{\mathscr{V}}}A_{i}.

Proof.

Point
(i)
is immediate, since matrix
A
is diagonal. Therefore,
L+A
is a Metzler matrix and the influence graph of
({\mathrm{e}}^{t(L+A)})_{t\geq 0}
is still connected. Clearly, point
(ii)
is due to the symmetry of
L+A
.

Point
(iii)
follows from point
(i)
. Indeed, we deduce by
[
16
, Theorem 17]
that the eigenvector
b^{0}
associated to the highest eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
of
L+A
is unique (up to multiplication by scalar) and strictly positive, and that
\lambda_{0}
is simple. Using the fact that
L+A
is symmetric and applying
[
16
, Theorem 12]
, we obtain (
5.3
).∎

Thus, in the rest of the section, we assume the following

Assumption 5.3
.

(i)

The matrix
L
is irreducible.

(ii)

\rho>\lambda_{0}(1-\gamma)
.

Notice that Assumption
5.3
ensures that the Assumption

Indeed, symmetry of

and Proposition

-
(ii)
.
In this way, all the results of Sections

the value function

) associated to the optimal control problem (

and to provide suitable conditions under which these solutions coincide with the value function

. To be precise, we give the following definition.

Definition 5.4
.

A function

is called a

to (

and

(5.4)

\rho v(k)=\langle(L+A)k,{\mathrm{D}}v(k)\rangle+H_{\gamma}({\mathrm{D}}v(k)),\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++},

where
\gamma>0
,
\gamma\neq 1
, and

is defined in (

We begin with the following result.

Proposition 5.5
.

Consider
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
,
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, such that
\mu(1-\gamma)>0
, and define

(5.5)

v(k)\coloneqq\dfrac{1}{1-\gamma}\left[\dfrac{\gamma}{\mu(1-\gamma)}\right]^{\gamma}\langle k,u\rangle^{1-\gamma},\quad k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}.

If
L
is irreducible and if the pair
(\mu,u)
is a solution to the nonlinear eigenvalue problem

(5.6)

(L+A)u=\left[\frac{\rho}{1-\gamma}-\mu\Phi(u)\right]u,

where

(5.7)

\Phi(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}u_{i}^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}},\quad u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++},

then
v
is a classical solution to (
5.4
), .

Proof.

We note, first, that the function
v
given in (
5.5
) is continuously differentiable in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
, with partial derivatives given by

(5.8)

v_{k_{i}}(k)=\left[\dfrac{\gamma}{\mu(1-\gamma)}\right]^{\gamma}\langle k,u\rangle^{-\gamma}u_{i},\quad k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++},\,i=1,\dots,n.

Since
{\mathrm{D}}v(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
, for all
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
, we can use (
4.7
) to rewrite HJB equation (

) as

\dfrac{\rho}{1-\gamma}\left[\dfrac{\gamma}{\mu(1-\gamma)}\right]^{\gamma}\langle k,u\rangle^{1-\gamma}=\left[\dfrac{\gamma}{\mu(1-\gamma)}\right]^{\gamma}\langle k,u\rangle^{-\gamma}\langle(L+A)k,u\rangle\\
+\dfrac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\left[\dfrac{\gamma}{\mu(1-\gamma)}\right]^{\gamma-1}\langle k,u\rangle^{1-\gamma}\Phi(u),\quad k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}.

Using the symmetry of

, the expression above simplifies to

\langle k,(L+A)u\rangle=\left[\dfrac{\rho}{1-\gamma}-\mu\Phi(u)\right]\langle k,u\rangle,\quad k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++},

which is clearly verified if
(\mu,u)
is a solution to (

).
∎

Remark 5.6
.

It is interesting to note that (
5.6
) can be seen as a nonlinear Schrödinger equation on a graph (see, e.g.,
[
20
,
25
]
). Nonlinear eigenvalue problems, like (
5.6
), are interesting in their own right and can be studied, for instance, using variational techniques based on critical point theory, as in
[
21
]
.

The Frobenius eigenvalue-eigenvector pair
(\lambda_{0},b^{0})
of matrix
L+A
, cf. Proposition
5.2
-
(iii)
, provides us with a particular solution to the nonlinear eigenvalue problem (

), which is given by

(5.9)

\mu=\dfrac{1}{\Phi(b^{0})}\left(\dfrac{\rho}{1-\gamma}-\lambda_{0}\right),\qquad u=b^{0}.

This observation leads us to study the optimization problem (
5.2
) with the approach of
[
12
]
, namely, by introducing the half-space
X_{++}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon\langle x,b^{0}\rangle>0\}
and the auxiliary optimization problem

(5.10)

V_{b^{0}}(k)\coloneqq\sup_{C\in{\mathcal{A}}_{++}^{b^{0}}(k)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\rho t}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\dfrac{C_{i}(t)^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}t,\quad k\in X_{++},

where the set of admissible strategies is

{\mathcal{A}}_{++}^{b^{0}}(k)\coloneqq\{C\in{\mathrm{L}}^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,+\infty);\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})\,\text{ s.t. }\langle K^{k,C}(t),b^{0}\rangle>0,\forall t\geq 0\}.

The HJB equation associated to the auxiliary problem is

(5.11)

\rho v(k)=\langle(L+A)k,{\mathrm{D}}v(k)\rangle+H_{\gamma}({\mathrm{D}}v(k)),\quad k\in X_{++},

where the Hamiltonian
H_{\gamma}
is given in (
4.6
).
We have the following result.

Theorem 5.7
.

Suppose that Assumption
5.3
is satisfied. Then,

(i)

The function
v
defined as

(5.12)

v(k)\coloneqq\dfrac{\alpha^{\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\langle k,b^{0}\rangle^{1-\gamma},\quad k\in X_{++},

where

(5.13)

\alpha=\dfrac{\gamma\Phi(b^{0})}{\rho-\lambda_{0}(1-\gamma)},

and
\Phi
is the function given in (
5.7
), is a solution to (
5.11
).

(ii)

For each
k\in X_{++}
, the unique maximizer of
H_{\gamma}({\mathrm{D}}v(k))
is given by
\widehat{c}({\mathrm{D}}v(k))=Fk
, where
F
is the
n\times n
matrix with entries

(5.14)

f_{ij}\coloneqq\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\left(\dfrac{p_{i}}{b^{0}_{i}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}b^{0}_{j},\quad i,j\in{\mathscr{V}}.

(iii)

The value
g\!\coloneqq\!\frac{\lambda_{0}-\rho}{\gamma}
is an eigenvalue of
(L\!+\!A\!-\!F)^{T}
, with associated eigenvector
b^{0}
.

(iv)

The
closed loop equation
, i.e., the ODE

(5.15)

\begin{cases}\dfrac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}K(t)=(L+A-F)K(t),&t\geq 0,\\
K(0)=k,\end{cases}

has a unique solution
\widehat{K}^{k}
, for each
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover, if
k\in X_{++}
, then

\langle\widehat{K}^{k}(t),b^{0}\rangle=\langle k,b^{0}\rangle{\mathrm{e}}^{gt},\quad t\geq 0,

where

is given in point
(iii)
, and hence
\widehat{K}^{k}(t)\in X_{++}
, for all
t\geq 0
.

(v)

Let
k\in X_{++}
. The control
\widehat{C}(t)
,
t\geq 0
, with components

(5.16)

\widehat{C}_{i}(t)\coloneqq[F\widehat{K}^{k}(t)]_{i}=\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\left(\dfrac{p_{i}}{b^{0}_{i}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}\langle k,b^{0}\rangle{\mathrm{e}}^{gt},\quad t\geq 0,\,i\in{\mathscr{V}},

belongs to
{\mathcal{A}}_{++}^{b^{0}}(k)
and it is optimal for problem (
5.10
) starting at

. Moreover, the value function
V_{b^{0}}
of the auxiliary problem (
5.10
) verifies
V_{b^{0}}(k)=v(k)
, where

is the function given in (
5.12
). If, in addition,
\widehat{C}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{+}(k)
, then
\widehat{C}
is optimal for problem (
5.2
) and

.

(vi)

Control
\widehat{C}
is optimal for problem (
5.2
) for any
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
if and only if

(5.17)

w_{ij}\geq f_{ij},\quad\text{for all }i,j\in{\mathscr{V}},\,i\neq j.

In this case, the value functions
V
and
V_{b^{0}}
of the original and auxiliary problems coincide on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
and
V_{b^{0}}
solves the HJB equation (
5.4
).

Proof.

(i)

With the same arguments used for the proof of Proposition
5.5
and after some simplifications, we can rewrite HJB equation (
5.11

\dfrac{\rho}{1-\gamma}\langle k,b^{0}\rangle=\langle(L+A)k,b^{0}\rangle+\dfrac{\gamma}{\alpha(1-\gamma)}\langle k,b^{0}\rangle\Phi(b^{0}),\quad k\in X_{++}.

Since
(L+A)b^{0}=\lambda_{0}b^{0}
,
\langle k_{0},b^{0}\rangle>0
, and
\alpha>0
, the equation above simplifies to

\dfrac{\rho}{1-\gamma}=\lambda_{0}+\dfrac{\gamma\Phi(b^{0})}{\alpha(1-\gamma)},

which yields the value of

given in (
5.13
). Hence,
v
is a solution to (
5.11
).

(ii)

Note that
v
is continuously differentiable in
X_{++}
with partial derivatives given by
v_{k_{i}}(k)=\alpha^{\gamma}\langle k,b^{0}\rangle^{-\gamma}b^{0}_{i}
,
k\in X_{++}
,
i\in{\mathscr{V}}
.
Since
{\mathrm{D}}v(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
, for all
k\in X_{++}
, we can use (

\widehat{c}_{i}({\mathrm{D}}v(k))=\left(\dfrac{p_{i}}{\alpha^{\gamma}\langle k,b^{0}\rangle^{-\gamma}b^{0}_{i}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}=\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\left(\dfrac{p_{i}}{b^{0}_{i}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}\langle k,b^{0}\rangle,\quad i\in{\mathscr{V}},\,k\in X_{++}.

It is clear that the expression on the right hand side of the last equation is well-defined for all

and that it is linear. Hence, it can be represented by an
n\times n
matrix

. To determine its entries, we impose, for all

and all

[Fk]_{i}=\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\left(\dfrac{p_{i}}{b^{0}_{i}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}\langle k,b^{0}\rangle\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sum_{j\in{\mathscr{V}}}f_{ij}k_{j}=\sum_{j\in{\mathscr{V}}}\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\left(\dfrac{p_{i}}{b^{0}_{i}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}b^{0}_{j}k_{j}.

(iii)

We verify, first, that

is an eigenvector of

, we have that

[F^{T}b^{0}]_{i}=\sum_{j\in{\mathscr{V}}}\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\left(\dfrac{p_{j}}{b^{0}_{j}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}b^{0}_{i}b^{0}_{j}=\dfrac{\rho-\lambda_{0}(1-\gamma)}{\gamma\Phi(b^{0})}\Phi(b^{0})\,b^{0}_{i}=\dfrac{\rho-\lambda_{0}(1-\gamma)}{\gamma}b^{0}_{i}.

Therefore,

(L+A-F)^{T}b^{0}=(L+A)^{T}b^{0}-F^{T}b^{0}=\lambda_{0}b^{0}-\dfrac{\rho-\lambda_{0}(1-\gamma)}{\gamma}b^{0}=gb^{0}.

(iv)

Standard results ensure uniqueness of the solution

to ODE (

, which can be written as

\widehat{K}^{k}(t)={\mathrm{e}}^{t(L+A-F)}k,\quad t\geq 0.

Thanks to point

proved above, we get

\langle\widehat{K}^{k}(t),b^{0}\rangle=\langle{\mathrm{e}}^{t(L+A-F)}k,b^{0}\rangle=\langle k,({\mathrm{e}}^{t(L+A-F)})^{T}b^{0}\rangle={\mathrm{e}}^{gt}\langle k,b^{0}\rangle,\quad t\geq 0.

Since

(v)

The solution to (

to (

. The last statement of point

(vi)

Control
\widehat{C}
is optimal for problem (

if and only if

for all

, i.e., if and only if

, for all

and for all

. In turn, this is equivalent to verifying that the linear system

Remark 5.8
.

The value
g\coloneqq\frac{\lambda_{0}-\rho}{\gamma}
is the optimal growth rate of the economy.

Remark 5.9
.

Condition (
5.17
) is implicit, as the entries of

depend on
\lambda_{0}
(through

) and

which, in turn, depend on the graph weigths

Theorem

of the auxiliary problem coincides on

with the value function

Let us discuss, by contrast, the extreme case where the graph weights are all equal to

verify

, for all

.
Since the objective functional in (

More precisely, assuming that

(5.18)

and that the optimal consumption

\widehat{C}_{i}(t)=\dfrac{\rho-A_{i}(1-\gamma)}{\gamma}k_{i}{\mathrm{e}}^{g_{i}t},\quad\widehat{K}_{i}^{k,\widehat{C}}(t)=k_{i}{\mathrm{e}}^{g_{i}t},

where

Remark 5.10
.

It is important to stress that, except for the cases where condition (
5.17
) or where the graph weigths are all zero, the value function
V
of problem (
5.2
) is not known in explicit form. These cases are clearly of great interest, both from an economic and from a mathematical point of view. In particular, the cases that are not covered by the results stated in this section are those where the state constraint is binding, and hence a more detailed analysis is necessary. This is left for future research.

We now want to study the convergence to a steady state of the detrended optimal capital path
\widehat{K}_{g}^{k}
, defined as

(5.20)

\widehat{K}_{g}^{k}(t)\coloneqq{\mathrm{e}}^{-gt}\widehat{K}^{k}(t),\quad t\geq 0,\,k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},

where
\widehat{K}^{k}
is the unique solution to the closed loop equation (
5.15
), with initial condition
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
. It is evident that
\widehat{K}_{g}^{k}
solves the ODE

(5.21)

\begin{cases}\dfrac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}K(t)=(B-g)K(t),&t\geq 0,\\
K(0)=k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\end{cases}

where
B\coloneqq L+A-F
. The analysis of the asymptotic behaviour of
\widehat{K}_{g}^{k}
is an important aspect in economics, as it provides us information on the long run behaviour of the economy under study. In particular, an interesting question concerns the existence of steady states. However, it is essential to recall that
\widehat{K}_{g}^{k}
is the detrended optimal capital path of the auxiliary problem (
5.10
), which may not coincide with the detrended optimal capital path of the original problem (
5.2
). Indeed, we require the latter to be non-negative, i.e., to make sense from an economic point of view.

Clearly, to study the convergence to a steady state of
\widehat{K}_{g}^{k}
we need to address the question of the stability of the dynamical linear system associated to (
5.21
). We have the following preliminary result.

Proposition 5.11
.

Suppose that Assumption
5.3
is verified and that
g>\lambda_{1}
(the last two assertions are equivalent to saying that
\lambda_{0}>g>\lambda_{1}
). Let us define the vector

(5.22)

y\coloneqq\alpha b^{0}+\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\dfrac{\beta_{m}}{\lambda_{m}-g}b^{m},

where
\beta_{m}\coloneqq\langle\widehat{c}(b^{0}),b^{m}\rangle
,
m=1,\dots,n-1
, and
\widehat{c}
is the function defined in (
4.8
).

Then,
\{y,b^{1},\dots,b^{n-1}\}
is a basis for
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of eigenvectors of
B
, with corresponding eigenvalues
g,\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n-1}
. In particular,
g
is the dominant eigenvalue of
B
and it is simple.

Proof.

As an immediate consequence of Theorem
5.7
-
(iii)
, we get that

(ii)

Theorem 5.12
.

Suppose that Assumption

\widehat{K}_{g}^{k}(t)\longrightarrow\overline{K}_{g}^{k}\coloneqq\dfrac{\langle k,b^{0}\rangle}{\alpha}y,\quad\text{as }t\to+\infty.

If, moreover,

, then

Proof.

Thanks to Proposition

We conclude this section with a result connected to an interesting question from the economic viewpoint: is it possible to choose the graph
{\mathscr{G}}
so that the growth rate
g
of optimal consumption path, given in (
5.16
), is maximized? As
g=\frac{\lambda_{0}-\rho}{\gamma}
, this value depends on the graph structure, that is, its weights, only through the Frobenius eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
. Hence, the problem above reduces to determining graph weights such that
\lambda_{0}
is maximized.
Clearly (cf. Remark
5.10
), we can interpret
g
as the growth rate of the economy if condition (
5.17
) is verified, i.e., if the optimal control provided in (
5.16
) is also optimal for problem (
5.2
). In this case, the optimal capital path is given by (
5.23
) and Theorem
5.12
ensures that the growth rates of each node in the economy will converge to
g
in the long run.

We provide a first answer in this direction studying the case where we optimize
\lambda_{0}
by modifying a single graph weight. In what follows we arbitrarily fix
h,\ell\in{\mathscr{V}}
,
h\neq\ell
, and we define
\omega\coloneqq w_{h\ell}\geq 0
. We treat
\omega
as a parameter, while keeping fixed all other graph weights. We denote by
\lambda_{0}(\omega)
the Frobenius eigenvalue of
L+A
corresponding to each possible
\omega\geq 0
.

Proposition 5.13
.

The map
\omega\mapsto\lambda_{0}(\omega)
is decreasing on
[0,+\infty)
.

Proof.

Let us fix
0\leq\omega_{1}<\omega_{2}
and denote by
L_{1}
(resp.,
L_{2}
) the matrices with entries as in (
5.1
) and
w_{h\ell}=\omega_{1}
(resp.,
w_{h\ell}=\omega_{2})
. Then, for each
i,j\in{\mathscr{V}}
,

(L_{2}-L_{1})_{ij}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }i\neq h,j\neq\ell,\text{ or }i=h,j\neq\ell,\text{ or }j=h,i\neq\ell,\\
\omega_{2}-\omega_{1},&\text{if }i=h,j=\ell,\\
\omega_{1}-\omega_{2},&\text{if }i=j=h,\text{ or }i=j=\ell.\end{cases}

It is not difficult to show that
P(\lambda)=(-1)^{n}\lambda^{n-1}[\lambda-2(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})]
is the characteristic polynomial of
L_{2}-L_{1}
, whence we deduce that
L_{2}-L_{1}
has only two distinct eigenvalues: zero and
2(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})<0
. Therefore,
L_{2}-L_{1}
is negative semidefinite, i.e.,

z^{T}L_{2}z\leq z^{T}L_{1}z
, for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This also implies
z^{T}(L_{2}+A)z\leq z^{T}(L_{1}+A)z
, for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Let
b^{0}(\omega_{2})
be the unit Frobenius eigenvector of
L_{2}+A
. Then, by the Rayleigh’s principle

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}(\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
\lVert z\rVert=1\end{subarray}}z^{T}(L_{2}+A)z=b^{0}(\omega_{2})^{T}(L_{2}+A)b^{0}(\omega_{2})\leq b^{0}(\omega_{2})^{T}(L_{1}+A)b^{0}(\omega_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
\lVert z\rVert=1\end{subarray}}z^{T}(L_{1}+A)z=\lambda_{0}(\omega_{1}),

whence the claim.
∎

This result implies that, in the case where it is possible to tweak only one graph weight, one should choose the lowest possible weight guaranteeing that (
5.17
) is verified, in order to maximize the growth rate of the economy
g
, i.e., the asymptotic growth rate of each node in the economy. This suggests that connections between vertices that are too strong create a congestion effect, which is negative from the economic viewpoint.

\displaystyle=\langle{\mathrm{e}}^{t(L+A)}k,b^{\star}\rangle-\int_{0}^{t}\langle{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-s)(L+A)}NC(s),b^{\star}\rangle\,{\mathrm{d}}s

\displaystyle={\mathrm{e}}^{t\lambda_{\star}}\left[\langle k,b^{\star}\rangle-\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{-s\lambda_{\star}}\langle C(s),N^{T}b^{\star}\rangle\,{\mathrm{d}}s\right],\quad t\geq 0.

Thus, for all

,

\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{-s\lambda_{\star}}\langle C(s),N^{T}b^{\star}\rangle\,{\mathrm{d}}s\leq\langle k,b^{\star}\rangle\,.

Thanks to Assumption
2.1
-
(ii)
, we have that

. Hence, recalling that

, for all

, and setting

, we have that

Therefore, for all

,

\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{-s\lambda_{\star}}\lVert C(s)\rVert\,{\mathrm{d}}s\leq\dfrac{1}{\chi}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{-s\lambda_{\star}}\langle C(s),N^{T}b^{\star}\rangle\,{\mathrm{d}}s\leq\dfrac{1}{\chi}\langle k,b^{\star}\rangle,

whence we deduce (
A.1
).
∎

We need to introduce the following spaces of functions, defined for all

as

(A.2)

{\mathrm{L}}^{p}_{\lambda_{\star}}([0,+\infty);\mathbb{R}^{n})\coloneqq\left\{f\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ s.t. }\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{-t\lambda_{\star}}\lVert f(t)\rVert^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}t<+\infty\right\},

is the Frobenius eigenvalue of

Lemma A.2
.

Let

and consider a family

\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{-s\lambda_{\star}}\lVert C_{m}(s)\rVert\,{\mathrm{d}}s\leq\Psi,

, for all

, such that, up to subsequences,

(A.4)

C_{m}^{1-\gamma}\rightarrow C_{0}^{1-\gamma},\quad\text{weakly in}\,\,{\mathrm{L}}^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}_{\lambda_{\star}}([0,+\infty);\mathbb{R}^{n}),\text{ as }m\to\infty.

Moreover, for all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and all

,

(A.5)

\int_{0}^{t}\langle{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-s)(L+A)}NC_{0}(s),w\rangle{\mathrm{d}}s\leq\liminf_{m\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\langle{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-s)(L+A)}NC_{m}(s),w\rangle{\mathrm{d}}s,

and, if Assumption
3.1
-
(ii)
is verified (recalling that

\lim_{m\to\infty}J(C_{m})=J(C_{0}).

Proof.

Fix
\gamma\in(0,1)
. By Jensen’s inequality,
\lVert x^{1-\gamma}\rVert\leq n^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\lVert x\rVert^{1-\gamma}
, for any

, where

is the vector whose components are

This implies that
