Momentum approach to the 1 / r 2 1/r^{2} potential as a toy model of the Wilsonian renormalization
Jan Dereziński

Abstract
The Bessel operator, that is, the Schrödinger operator on the halfline with a potential proportional to 1 / x 2 1/x^{2} , is analyzed in the momentum representation. Many features of this analysis are parallel to the approach à la K. Wilson to Quantum Field Theory: one needs to impose a cutoff, add counterterms, study the renormalization group flow with its fixed points and limit cycles.



Keywords: Schrödinger operators, Bessel operators, momentum
representation, renormalization group

MSC2020: 47E99, 81Q10, 81Q80

Introduction

Our paper is devoted to one of the most curious families of operators in mathematical physics,

L_{\alpha}\coloneqq-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}+\Big(\alpha-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\frac{1}{x^{2}},

We will use the symbol

Let us briefly list their most important properties.

Bessel operators are Hermitian (symmetric)

One of the most striking properties of Bessel operators is their homogeneity of degree

There is an interesting analogy between the action of

The most obvious approach to Bessel operators is to study them in
the position representation, as it is usually done in the
literature. Our paper is devoted to an analysis of Bessel operators in
the momentum representation. We believe that this is interesting in
itself—in fact, many things look very different on the momentum
side. Our main motivation, however, comes from the physics literature,
where some authors try
to explain the renormalization group approach to
QFT by using Bessel operators as a toy model.

In QFT one can work both in the position and in the
momentum space. The position representation is used in the so-called

Epstein-Glaser approach
. In practice, however, physicists prefer to
work in the momentum representation, which is usually more convenient
for computations.
In order to compute various useful quantities one typically needs to
impose a cutoff at momentum
\Lambda
and to add appropriate

\Lambda

are obtained in the limit
\Lambda\to\infty
, provided that various
parameters are appropriately adjusted.

This procedure was greatly clarified by the Nobel prize winner
K. Wilson, who stressed the role of scaling transformations in the
process of renormalization. He also pointed out that it is not so
important whether QFT has a well defined limit for
\Lambda\to\infty
:
what matters is a weak dependence of low energy quantities on the high energy cutoff.

We start with a brief analysis of the momentum approach to general

We encounter the following issue: the usual Fourier transformation is

Therefore, we discuss both approaches. Actually, from
the 3-dimensional point of view the sine
transformation should be preferred, because the 3-dimensional Laplacian in
the
s-wave sector reduces to the Dirichlet Laplacian on the half-line.

Let us briefly describe the momentum approach to Schrödinger
operators on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
for sufficiently nice potentials.
There are two basic Schrödinger operators with the
potential
V
on the halfline:
-\Delta_{\mathrm{D}}+V(x)

and
-\Delta_{\mathrm{N}}+V(x)
.
By applying the sine transformation to the former and
the cosine transformation to the latter we obtain
the operators

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{D}}\psi(p)=p^{2}\psi(p)+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}(\tilde{V}(p-q)-\tilde{V}(p+q)\big)\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q,

(1.7)

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{N}}\psi(p)=p^{2}\psi(p)+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}(\tilde{V}(p-q)+\tilde{V}(p+q)\big)\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q.

(1.8)

Here,
\tilde{V}(p)
is the Fourier transform of the
even extension of
V
from
\mathbb{R}_{+}
to
\mathbb{R}
.

If the potential is singular at
0

1.

We cannot use the standard Dirichlet/Neumann boundary conditions.

2.

We need to interpret
V
as an irregular distribution.

3.

The Fourier transform of
V
will grow at infinity, making the formulas (
1.7
) and (
1.8
) problematic.

All these problems are present for
Bessel operators, where
V
is proportional to
\frac{1}{x^{2}}
.
In particular,
\frac{1}{x^{2}}
does not define a regular distribution.
There exists, however, a well-known one-parameter family of even distributions that outside of
0
coincide with
\frac{1}{x^{2}}
. Their Fourier transforms are

-\pi|p|+a,

(1.9)

where
a
is a constant.

A priori it is not obvious which transformation is more appropriate
for Bessel operators: the sine or the cosine transformation. Let us consider both, setting

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}L_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}},

(1.10)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}L_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}},

(1.11)

where
L_{\alpha}
is treated as a formal expression,

\cal{F}_{\mathrm{D}/\mathrm{N}}
is the sine/cosine transformation.

Let us use
-\pi|p|
as the Fourier transform of
\frac{1}{x^{2}}
.
(We set

1.9

(

) and (

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}\psi(p)

\displaystyle=p^{2}\psi(p)+\Big(\alpha-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(\theta(p-q)q+\theta(q-p)p\big)\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q,

(1.12)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}\psi(p)

\displaystyle=p^{2}\psi(p)-\Big(\alpha-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(\theta(p-q)p+\theta(q-p)q\big)\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q,

(1.13)

where
\theta
is the Heaviside function.
Both expressions are problematic for
large momenta. In what follows we give two constructions of
self-adjoint realizations of (

) and (

).
The first construction will be called
“mathematicians’ style”, and the second
“physicists’ style”.

The construction in “mathematicians’ style” directly describes
the domains and actions
of the operators. It starts with a construction of the

minimal Bessel operators
, denoted

\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}^{\min}
and
\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}^{\min}
.
They are defined by (
1.12
) and (
1.13
) on
appropriate domains consisting of rapidly
decaying functions. In addition, for (
1.12
) we need to assume
that
\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(p)p\mathrm{d}p=0
and for (
1.13
)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(p)\mathrm{d}p=0
.

The adjoints of
the minimal Bessel operators are called the

,
and denoted

\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}^{\max}
and
\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}^{\max}
.
If
\alpha\geq 1
, they coincide with the minimal Bessel operators,
and also with their unique self-adjoint realizations. This is not the
case for
\alpha<1
.
The construction of maximal operators in the momentum representation
is somewhat tricky. To obtain the maximal domains,
\mathcal{D}(\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}^{\min})
and
\mathcal{D}(\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}^{\min})

have to be extended by adjoining appropriate 2-dimensional
subspaces.
We cannot directly use
the expressions (

) and (

), since now
they usually contain divergent integrals.
If
\alpha\neq\frac{1}{4}
(that is, when there is a

as

p^{-\frac{3}{2}+m}
and
p^{-\frac{3}{2}-m}
. Curiously, the case

\alpha=\frac{1}{4}
is more problematic and has to be treated separately.

Finally, we define

realizations of Bessel operators by selecting appropriate domains
larger than the minimal but smaller than the maximal domain.

The above construction of self-adjoint Bessel operators is
mathematically correct, but it sounds rather artificial. In the
remaining part of Sect.
4
we describe “physicists’
style” construction, directly inspired by

7
]

The counterterms will be constructed out of (unbounded and non-closable) operators

and

\displaystyle K_{\mathrm{D}}\psi(p)

\displaystyle:=p\int_{0}^{\infty}q\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q,

(1.14)

\displaystyle K_{\mathrm{N}}\psi(p)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q,

Suppose that

\displaystyle\Lambda\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\Lambda}f_{\Lambda}

\displaystyle\Lambda\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\Lambda}g_{\Lambda}

Let

Consider the operators

\displaystyle\theta(\Lambda-p)\Big(\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}+\frac{f_{\Lambda}}{\Lambda}K_{\mathrm{D}}\Big)\theta(\Lambda-p),

\displaystyle\theta(\Lambda-p)\Big(\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}+\Lambda g_{\Lambda}K_{\mathrm{N}}\Big)\theta(\Lambda-p).

According to “physicists’ style construction”
self-adjoint realizations of Bessel operators are

Note also

What is the take-home message of our paper? We do not insist that
Schrödinger operators should be always studied in the momentum
representation. However, we think that it is useful to know how the
world looks “on
the momentum side of the Fourier transform”. And it does
look
quite differently from the position representation.

To our knowledge, mathematicians rarely use the
momentum representation to study Schrödinger operators.
In particular, we have never seen the formulas
(
1.7
) and (
1.8
) in mathematics papers. In physics
papers, the
momentum representation seems to be more common. For instance, the formula
(
1.7
) can be found in
[

]
(in the 3-dimensional
context).

The original purpose of our paper was to present the ideas of

[
7
]
in clear and rigorous terms. We think that these ideas
are important and instructive. One can try to formulate them as
follows.
In renormalizable Quantum Field Theories
most terms in the Lagrangian have the same homogeneity wrt the
scaling. E.g in the Standard Model only the Higgs mass term has a
different scaling dimension. Bessel operators are also almost
homogeneous, with scaling invariance broken only by the boundary
condition at
0
. This homogeneity is related to the fact that the naive
formulations of both QFT and Bessel operators
in the momentum representation are highly singular.
Computations in QFT usually involve
the four steps described in (
1.6
):
imposing a momentum cutoff
\Lambda
,
adding counterterms, solving the
differential equation for the coupling constant and taking the
limit
\Lambda\to\infty
.
Our paper shows that the same steps have to
be followed in a much simpler context of Bessel operators. These steps
are not restricted to the difficult and complicated formalism
of QFT. They are typical of the momentum approach.

Equations (
1.16
) and (
1.17
) have a
one-parameter family of solutions for any

\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. However, Bessel operators have a one-parameter
family of self-adjoint realizations only for
\alpha<1
. In the
language of QFT,
“nontrivial renormalized theories” exist only for
\alpha<1
. For

\alpha\geq 1
the ”flows of operators” (
1.18
) and
(
1.19
) do not have limits in the sense of
the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, apart from
the unique self-adjoint realizalion of

L_{\alpha}
. Hence for
\alpha\geq 1
, as Wilson suggested,
“one can never
remove the cutoff completely in a nontrivial theory”.

In particular, this is the case for the borderline value
\alpha=1
.
The Bessel operator
L_{1}
is the radial part of the Laplacian in 4
dimensions, see (
1.3
).
We believe that it is not a coincidence that our spacetime also has 4 dimensions.

2.1
Extensions of Hermitian operators

Let us recall basic concepts of the theory of self-adjoint extensions
of Hermitian operators (often called symmetric operators). A comprehensive treatment of this topic can be found in
[
22
]
.

Let
A
and
B
be operators with domains
\mathcal{D}(A)
and

\mathcal{D}(B)
respectively. We say that
A
is
contained in

B
if
\mathcal{D}(A)\subseteq\mathcal{D}(B)
and
A=B
on
\mathcal{D}(A)
. We then write

A\subseteq B
.

An operator
A
is
Hermitian
if for all
x,y\in\mathcal{D}(A)

(Ax|y)=(x|Ay),

(2.1)

where
(\cdot|\cdot)
is the scalar product.
An operator
A
is self-adjoint if
A^{*}=A
, where the star denotes the Hermitian adjoint.

2.2
Self-adjoint realizations of
L_{\alpha}

Recall that operators given by (
1.1
) with specified domains are called realizations of
L_{\alpha}
. Following
[
4
]
(see Sec. 4 and
App. A therein for details), we first introduce the
maximal

and

of
L_{\alpha}
. The maximal realization, denoted

L^{\mathrm{max}}_{\alpha}
, has the domain

\mathcal{D}(L^{\mathrm{max}}_{\alpha})=\big\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\>|\>L_{\alpha}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\big\}.

(2.2)

and the
minimal realization of
L_{\alpha}
, denoted
L_{\alpha}^{\min}
, is the closure of the restriction of
L_{\alpha}
to
C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Obviously,
L^{\mathrm{min}}_{\alpha}\subset L^{\mathrm{max}}_{\alpha}
. Moreover, one can show that

\big(L^{\mathrm{min}}_{\alpha}\big)^{*}=L^{\mathrm{max}}_{\alpha}.

(2.3)

Hence,
L^{\mathrm{min}}_{\alpha}
is Hermitian.

One can show that for
\alpha\geq 1

L_{\alpha}^{\max}=L_{\alpha}^{\min}
is self-adjoint
[
4
]
. In what follows it will be denoted
H_{m}
, where
m:=\sqrt{\alpha}
is the positive square root of
\alpha
.

For
\alpha<1

the domain of

L_{\alpha}^{\min}
is strictly smaller than that of
L_{\alpha}^{\max}

and neither operator is self-adjoint. Therefore, both operators are of little use in physical applications: e.g. eigenvalues of
L_{\alpha}^{\max}
cover almost the whole complex plane and corresponding eigenvectors are not mutually orthogonal.

Self-adjoint realisations of
L_{\alpha}
are self-adjoint operators
H

such that

L^{\mathrm{min}}_{\alpha}\subset H=H^{*}\subset L^{\mathrm{max}}_{\alpha}.

(2.4)

They are constructed as follows. Firstly, one finds solutions of
L_{\alpha}\psi=0
. These are of the form

\psi(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}a\cdot x^{1/2+m}+b\cdot x^{1/2-m},&m\neq 0;\\
a\cdot\sqrt{x}+b\cdot\sqrt{x}\ln x,&m=0.\end{array}\right.

(2.5)

Here,
m:=\sqrt{\alpha}
. Note that

m\in\mathbb{R}
or
m\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}
is defined up to a sign.

For
\alpha\geq 1
only
x^{1/2+m}
with
m
positive is square
integrable near zero. This is the key element of the proof of the
essential self-adjointness of
H_{m}
on
C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.

For
\alpha<1
all the solutions (
2.5
) are
square integrable on
]0,\varepsilon[
for any

\varepsilon>0
. Let
\kappa,\nu
be complex numbers. Let
\xi\in C_{\mathrm{c}}^{\infty}[0,\infty[
and
\xi=1
near
0
.
The following spaces lie between
\mathcal{D}(L_{\alpha}^{\min})
and

\mathcal{D}(L_{\alpha}^{\max})
and do not depend on the choice of
\xi
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(H_{m,\kappa})=\{f\in\mathcal{D}(L^{\mathrm{max}}_{\alpha})\quad|\quad\text{for some}\>c\in\mathbb{C}\quad f(x)-c\xi(x)(\kappa x^{1/2-m}+x^{1/2+m})\in\mathcal{D}(L^{\mathrm{min}}_{\alpha})\},

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(H_{m,\infty})=\{f\in\mathcal{D}(L^{\mathrm{max}}_{\alpha})\quad|\quad\text{for some}\>c\in\mathbb{C}\quad f(x)-c\xi(x)x^{1/2-m}\in\mathcal{D}(L^{\mathrm{min}}_{\alpha})\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(H_{0}^{\nu})=\{f\in\mathcal{D}(L^{\mathrm{max}}_{0})\quad|\quad\text{for some}\>c\in\mathbb{C}\quad f(x)-c\xi(x)(x^{1/2}\ln x+\nu x^{1/2})\in\mathcal{D}(L^{\mathrm{min}}_{0})\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(H_{0}^{\infty})=\{f\in\mathcal{D}(L^{\mathrm{max}}_{0})\quad|\quad\text{for some}\>c\in\mathbb{C}\quad f(x)-c\xi(x)x^{1/2}\in\mathcal{D}(L^{\mathrm{min}}_{0})\}.

They define realizations of
L_{\alpha}
denoted
H_{m,\kappa}
and
H_{0}^{\nu}
.
The above definition implies immediately that

H_{m,\kappa}=H_{-m,\kappa^{-1}}
.
Moreover, Hermitian adjoints of these operators are

(H_{m,\kappa})^{*}=H_{\bar{m},\bar{\kappa}}
and
(H_{0}^{\nu})^{*}=H^{\bar{\nu}}_{0}
(with the convention
\bar{\infty}=\infty
).

To prove it, let us consider functions
f\in\mathcal{D}(H_{m,\kappa})
,
g\in\mathcal{D}(H_{\bar{m},\bar{\kappa}})
. One has

(H_{m,\kappa}f|g)-(f|H_{\bar{m},\bar{\kappa}}g)=\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}\Big(\bar{f}(x)\partial_{x}g(x)-g(x)\partial_{x}\bar{f}(x)\Big).

By analysis of behaviour of
f
and
g
near
x=0
we conclude that the Wronskian at
0
vanishes. Calculations for
H^{\nu}_{0}
are analogous.

Thus, self-adjoint extensions of
L^{\mathrm{min}}_{m^{2}}
with
m^{2}<1
fall into the following 3 categories:

•

H_{m,\kappa}
, with
m\in]0,1[
and
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
,

•

H_{m,\kappa}
, with
m\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
|\kappa|=1
,

•

H_{0}^{\nu}
with
\nu\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
.

In the second line,
|\kappa|=1
is a consequence of

H_{m,\kappa}=H_{-m,\kappa^{-1}}
and
(H_{m,\kappa})^{*}=H_{\bar{m},\bar{\kappa}}
.

Note that
H_{\frac{1}{2},0}=H_{-\frac{1}{2},\infty}
and

H_{-\frac{1}{2},0}=H_{\frac{1}{2},\infty}

are the Laplacians with the
Dirichlet
, resp.
Neumann boundary
condition at

. They will be often denoted
H_{\mathrm{D}}
,
resp.
H_{\mathrm{N}}
.

\bullet\quad
For
m\in]0,1[
and
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
:

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}(H_{m,\kappa})=\bigg\{-4\Big(\frac{\kappa\Gamma(-m)}{\Gamma(m)}\Big)^{-1/m}\bigg\}\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\kappa\in]-\infty,0[,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}(H_{m,\kappa})=\emptyset\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\kappa\in[0,\infty].

\bullet\quad
For
m=\mathrm{i}m_{I}\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
|\kappa|=1
:

\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}(H_{\mathrm{i}m_{I},\kappa})=\bigg\{-4\exp\bigg(\frac{\arg\Big(\frac{\kappa\Gamma(-\mathrm{i}m_{I})}{\Gamma(\mathrm{i}m_{I})}\Big)+2\pi n}{m_{I}}\bigg)\quad|\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}\bigg\}.

(2.8)

It implies that for
\alpha<0
the point spectrum of self-adjoint extensions of
L^{\mathrm{min}}_{\alpha}
has infinitely many elements with accumulation points at
0
and
-\infty
.

\bullet\quad
For
\nu\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}(H_{0}^{\nu})=\{-4\mathrm{e}^{2(\nu-\gamma)}\}\quad\nu\in\mathbb{R},

(2.9)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}(H_{0}^{\infty})=\emptyset.

\gamma
denotes the Euler constant. In all cases, bound-state solutions to the eigenvalue problem
H_{m,\kappa}\psi=-k^{2}\psi
or
H_{0}^{\nu}\psi=-k^{2}\psi
are of the form

\psi(x)=\sqrt{kx}K_{m}(kx)\quad\mathrm{or}\quad\sqrt{kx}K_{0}(kx),

(2.10)

where
k>0
and
K_{m}(z)
is the MacDonald function of order
m

[
24
]
. The proof can be found e.g. in
[
8
]
, Sec. 5.2.

2.4
Renormalization group

Let us introduce the

dilation (scaling) operator

U_{\tau}
. It is a unitary transformation on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
acting on functions in the following way:

U_{\tau}f(x)=\mathrm{e}^{\tau/2}f(\mathrm{e}^{\tau}x).

(2.11)

We say that an operator
B
is
homogeneous of degree

n
if

U_{\tau}BU_{-\tau}=\mathrm{e}^{n\tau}B.

L^{\mathrm{max}}_{m}
and
L^{\mathrm{min}}_{m}
are homogeneous of
degree
-2
. However, the realizations
H_{m,\kappa}
are homogeneous
only for
\kappa=0
or
\kappa=\infty
. Realizations

H_{0}^{\nu}
are homogeneous only for
\nu=\infty
. Moreover
[
8
]
,

\displaystyle U_{\tau}H_{m,\kappa}U_{-\tau}=\mathrm{e}^{-2\tau}H_{m,\mathrm{e}^{-2m\tau}\kappa}

(2.12)

\displaystyle U_{\tau}H_{0}^{\nu}U_{-\tau}=\mathrm{e}^{-2\tau}H_{0}^{\nu+\tau}.

Indeed, if
f\in\mathcal{D}(H_{m,\kappa})
, then
U_{\tau}f\in\mathcal{D}(H_{m,\mathrm{e}^{-2m\tau}\kappa})
, and if
f\in\mathcal{D}(H_{0}^{\nu})
, then
U_{\tau}f\in\mathcal{D}(H_{0}^{\nu+\tau})
.

For the purpose of this article, the action of

\mathbb{R}\ni\tau\mapsto R_{\tau}
defined in (
1.5
) can be called
the
renormalization group
.
In the set of self-adjoint realizations of
L_{\alpha}

we have the following
behaviors of the renormalization group flow:

•

For
\alpha\geq 1
the set is one point.

•

for
0<\alpha<1
there are two fixed points:
attractive
H_{m,0}
and
repulsive
H_{m,\infty}
. The former is the
Friedrichs extension of
L_{m^{2}}^{\min}
and
the latter is its Krein extension; see
[
8
,
14
]
.
(We choose
m
as the positive square root of
\alpha
).

•

for
\alpha=0
there is only one fixed point:

H^{\infty}_{0}
.

•

for
\alpha<0
there are no fixed points and the renormalization group generates a cyclic flow.

Figure 1:

Schematic illustration of the renormalization group flow on the spaces
of self-adjoint realizations of Bessel operators. It is borrowed from
[
9
]
. Heavy dots represent
fixed points. The letters
K
and
F
correspond to the Krein and Friedrichs extensions
of
L_{\alpha}^{\min}
. Dashed lines represent realizations with a single
bound state. Dotted line represents an infinite number of bound
states. The arrow indicates the direction of the flow.

The names of the various phases can be treated as
jokes. One can also try to justify them as follows. For
\alpha>1

the space of realizations is trivial. This is the simplest
phase—hence the name ”gas”.
For
\alpha<0
the continuous scaling symmetry is broken to a discrete
subgroup, as in crystals—this justifies the name “solid”.
The intermediate situation,
0<\alpha<1
is consequently called the “liquid phase”.

3
“Wilsonian approach” to inverse square potential

In this section we give a partly heuristic
theory of Bessel operators in the spirit of the Wilsonian
renormalization. We will mostly follow the
presentation of
[
7
]
, preserving to a large extent its style
and language. In the next section we give a rigorous description of
all steps of this section.

For a description of the Wilsonian renormalization procedure applied
to quantum field theory the reader can consult
[
12
,
13
,
26
]
.

The main object of the analysis of

[
7
]
is the formal expression

\tilde{L}_{\alpha,3}\psi(\vec{p})=|\vec{p}|^{2}\psi(\vec{p})+\frac{1}{4\pi}\Big(\alpha-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\int d^{3}\vec{q}\frac{1}{|\vec{p}-\vec{q}|}\psi(\vec{q}).

(3.1)

Formally, it is the momentum representation of

L_{\alpha,3}:=-\Delta_{3}+\Big(\alpha-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\frac{1}{r^{2}},

(3.2)

where
\Delta_{3}
is the
3
-dimensional Laplacian and
r
is the radial coordinate.

More precisely, if
\mathcal{F}f(\vec{p})
is the Fourier transform of
f(\vec{x})
, then

\tilde{L}_{\alpha,3}:=\mathcal{F}L_{\alpha,3}\mathcal{F}^{-1}.

(3.3)

Let us note that the 3-dimensional formulation is equivalent to the
1-dimensional one. Indeed, every
\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
can be
written in spherical coordinates
r,\vartheta,\phi
as

\psi=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}Y_{l,m}(\vartheta,\phi)\frac{1}{r}f_{l,m}(r),

where
Y_{l,m}
are spherical harmonics. It follows that on
l
degree spherical harmonics

L_{\alpha,3}
is equivalent to

-\partial_{r}^{2}+\Big((l+1/2)^{2}+\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\Big)\frac{1}{r^{2}}.

For further convenience let us denote
p:=|\vec{p}|
. We will
restrict our attention to the s-wave sector, that is, to
spherically symmetric functions.
In the s-wave sector we can write
\psi(\vec{p})=\psi(p)
with
p:=|\vec{p}|
and the operator Eq. (
3.1
)
can be written in the following way (see Eqs. (3) and (4) in
[
7
]
):

\tilde{L}_{\alpha}\psi(p)=p^{2}\psi(p)+\Big(\alpha-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}q\>q^{2}\Big(\theta(p-q)p^{-1}+\theta(q-p)q^{-1}\Big)\psi(q),

(3.4)

where
\theta(x)
denotes the Heaviside step function and we dropped
3
from the subscript.

Unfortunately, (
3.4
) does not define a Hermitian operator. To find a self-adjoint realisation, we introduce an ultraviolet cutoff
\Lambda
and a counter-term
\frac{f_{\Lambda}}{\Lambda}
. Thus we consider a family of cutoff Hamiltonians

\tilde{H}_{\alpha}(\Lambda,f_{\Lambda})\psi(p)\coloneqq\begin{cases}p^{2}\psi(p)+\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\mathrm{d}q\>q^{2}\Big(V(p,q)+\frac{f_{\Lambda}}{\Lambda}\Big)\psi(q),&\mathrm{for}\>p\leq\Lambda\\
0,&\mathrm{for}\>p>\Lambda.\end{cases}

(3.5)

where

V(p,q)=\Big(\alpha-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\Big(\theta(p-q)p^{-1}+\theta(q-p)q^{-1}\Big).

(3.6)

Let
\tilde{U}
denote the dilation in the momentum representation, that is

\tilde{U}(\tau)\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}U(\tau).

(3.7)

Note that in the momentum representation the dilation acts in the opposite way than in the position representation:

\tilde{U}(\tau)\psi(p)=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{3}{2}\tau}\psi(\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}p).

(3.8)

It is easy to see that

\tilde{U}(\tau)\tilde{H}_{\alpha}(\Lambda,f_{\Lambda})\tilde{U}(-\tau)=\mathrm{e}^{-2\tau}\tilde{H}_{\alpha}(\mathrm{e}^{\tau}\Lambda,f_{\Lambda}).

(3.9)

Thus changing
\Lambda
can be interpreted as the change of a scale.

We would like to find out for what kind of dependence of
f_{\Lambda}

on
\Lambda
we can expect the existence of a limit
H_{\alpha}(\Lambda,f_{\Lambda})

for
\Lambda\to\infty
as a self-adjoint operator. To this end we assume that we have a fixed eigenfunction
\psi
satisfying

H_{\alpha}(\Lambda,f_{\Lambda})\psi(p)=E\psi(p),\quad p<\Lambda.

(3.10)

Following the terminology of
[
7
]
,
we will say that two Hamiltonians
\tilde{H}_{m}(\Lambda,f_{\Lambda})
and
\tilde{H}_{\alpha}(\Lambda^{\prime},f_{\Lambda^{\prime}})
are equivalent if

\tilde{H}_{\alpha}(\Lambda,f_{\Lambda})\psi(p)=\tilde{H}_{\alpha}(\Lambda^{\prime},f_{\Lambda^{\prime}})\psi(p)\quad\mathrm{for}\>p<\mathrm{min}(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}).

Two Hamiltonians are equivalent if the function
\gamma(\Lambda)\equiv f_{\Lambda}/\Lambda
satisfies the equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma(\Lambda)}{\mathrm{d}\Lambda}=\Big[\gamma(\Lambda)-\frac{1/4-\alpha}{\Lambda}\Big]^{2}.

(3.11)

To prove it, we consider an infinitesimal reduction of the cut-off
\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda-\mathrm{d}\Lambda
. Let
\psi
be a solution of

\tilde{H}_{\alpha}(\Lambda,f_{\Lambda})\psi(p)=-k^{2}\psi(p).

(3.12)

By splitting the integral

\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\mathrm{d}q\>q^{2}\Big(V(p,q)+\gamma(\Lambda)\Big)\psi(q)\approx\int_{0}^{\Lambda-d\Lambda}\mathrm{d}q\>q^{2}\Big(V(p,q)+\gamma(\Lambda)\Big)\psi(q)+\mathrm{d}\Lambda\Big(V(p,\Lambda)+\gamma(\Lambda)\Big)\psi({\Lambda}),

and using the relation resulting from Eqs. (
3.5
) and (
3.12
):

\psi(\Lambda)=-\frac{1}{k^{2}+\Lambda^{2}}\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\mathrm{d}q\>q^{2}\Big((\alpha-\frac{1}{4})\Lambda^{-1}+\gamma(\Lambda)\Big)\psi(q),

assuming the large cut-off
k^{2}\ll\Lambda^{2}
we conclude that the Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{\alpha}(\Lambda,f_{\Lambda})
is equivalent to
\tilde{H}_{\alpha}(\Lambda-\mathrm{d}\Lambda,f_{\Lambda-\mathrm{d}\Lambda})
such that

\tilde{H}_{\alpha}(\Lambda-\mathrm{d}\Lambda,f_{\Lambda-\mathrm{d}\Lambda})\psi(p)\coloneqq p^{2}\psi(p)+\int_{0}^{\Lambda-\mathrm{d}\Lambda}\mathrm{d}q\>q^{2}\Big(V(p,q)+\gamma(\Lambda-\mathrm{d}\Lambda)\Big)\psi(q),

where

\gamma(\Lambda-\mathrm{d}\Lambda)=\gamma(\Lambda)-\mathrm{d}\Lambda\Big[\gamma(\Lambda)-\frac{1/4-\alpha}{\Lambda}\Big]^{2}.

(3.13)

Taking the limit
\mathrm{d}\Lambda\rightarrow 0
we obtain the differential
equation (
3.11
). Its solutions are of the form

\gamma(\Lambda)=\Lambda^{-1}f_{\Lambda}
. Let us analyze the flow

\Lambda\to f_{\Lambda}
.

•

For
\alpha<0
, we set
m_{I}:=\sqrt{-\alpha}
,

f_{\Lambda}=m_{I}\tan\bigg(\arctan\Big(\frac{f_{\Lambda_{0}}-m_{I}^{2}+1/4}{m_{I}}\Big)+m_{I}\log\frac{\Lambda}{\Lambda_{0}}\bigg)+m_{I}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}.

(3.14)

There are no fixed points. Moreover, there is a discrete scaling symmetry: for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, Hamiltonians
\tilde{H}(\Lambda,f_{\Lambda})
and
\tilde{H}(\Lambda\cdot\exp(\pi n/m_{I}),f_{\Lambda})
are equivalent.

•

For
\alpha=0
,

f_{\Lambda}=\frac{f_{\Lambda_{0}}+1/4}{1-(f_{\Lambda_{0}}+1/4)\log\frac{\Lambda}{\Lambda_{0}}}-\frac{1}{4}.

(3.15)

There is one fixed point corresponding to
f_{\Lambda_{0}}=-1/4
,
for which Hamiltonians
\tilde{H}_{m}(\Lambda,f_{\Lambda_{0}})
are equivalent for all values of
\Lambda
.

•

for
\alpha>0
, we set
m:=\sqrt{\alpha}>0

f_{\Lambda}=-m\tanh\bigg(-\mathrm{atanh}\Big(\frac{f_{\Lambda_{0}}+m^{2}+1/4}{m}\Big)+m\log\frac{\Lambda}{\Lambda_{0}}\bigg)-m^{2}-\frac{1}{4}.

(3.16)

There are two fixed point: an attractive one
f_{\Lambda_{0}}^{+}=-\big(m+\frac{1}{2}\big)^{2}
and a repulsive one
f_{\Lambda_{0}}^{-}=\big(m-\frac{1}{2}\big)^{2}
, for which Hamiltonians
\tilde{H}_{m}(\Lambda,f^{\pm}_{\Lambda_{0}})
are equivalent for all values of
\Lambda
.

We thus reproduce the the three first pictures from
Figure
1
.

4
Momentum approach

4.1
Momentum approach to Schrödinger operators on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

Self-adjoint operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

of the form

H=-\Delta+V(x)

(4.1)

are usually called
Schrödinger operators
.
They are typically studied by methods of the configuration space.
However, they can also be approached from the momentum point of view.

We will typically use
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for the generic variable
in the position representation and
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the momentum representation.
Let us recall the two most common conventions for the Fourier
transformation:

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(p)

\displaystyle:=\int\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}xp}f(x)\mathrm{d}x,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}f(p)

\displaystyle:=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\int\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}xp}f(x)\mathrm{d}x=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\tilde{f}(p).

(4.3)

Note that
\mathcal{F}
is unitary. Let
\tilde{H}
denote the operator
H
in the momentum representation, that is

\tilde{H}:=\mathcal{F}H\mathcal{F}^{-1}.

(4.4)

If the potential
V
is well-behaved, then
\tilde{H}
can be written as

\tilde{H}\psi(p)=p^{2}\psi(p)+(2\pi)^{-d}\int\tilde{V}(p-q)\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q.

(4.5)

4.2
Momentum approach to Schrödinger operators on
\mathbb{R}_{+}

Often we consider Schrödinger operators on a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then the representation (
4.5
) becomes problematic, because
it needs to take into account the boundary conditions. Besides, it is not obvious what should replace the Fourier transformation.

A case of particular interest is
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. In this case we
have two distinguished realizations of the 1-dimensional Laplacian:
the
Dirichlet Laplacian

\Delta_{\mathrm{D}}
and the
Neumann Laplacian

\Delta_{\mathrm{N}}
.
Instead of the Fourier transformation it is natural to use the
sine transformation

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}
or the
cosine transformation

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}
:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}f)(p)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sin(px)f(x)\mathrm{d}x,

(4.6)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}f)(p)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos(px)f(x)\mathrm{d}x.

(4.7)

Both are involutions, that is,

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}^{2}\psi=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}^{2}\psi=\psi,

(4.8)

they are unitary and diagonalize the
Dirichlet/Neumann Laplacian:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}(-\Delta_{\mathrm{D}})\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}\psi(p)

\displaystyle=p^{2}\psi(p),

(4.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}(-\Delta_{\mathrm{N}})\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}\psi(p)

\displaystyle=p^{2}\psi(p).

(4.10)

Consider now a Schrödinger operator on the half-line with a potential
V
.
Let us first assume that
V
is sufficiently regular, say,
V\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Then one can impose the Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions at
0
, so that one has two Schrödinger operators

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{D}}

\displaystyle:=-\Delta_{\mathrm{D}}+V(x),

(4.11)

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{N}}

\displaystyle:=-\Delta_{\mathrm{N}}+V(x).

(4.12)

As suggested by Eqs. (
4.9
)-(
4.10
), to obtain their momentum versions it is natural to apply the sine transform to the first and the cosine transform to the second Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{D}}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}H_{\mathrm{D}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}},

(4.13)

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{N}}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}H_{\mathrm{N}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}.

(4.14)

Let us extend the potential
V
to an even function on the whole
\mathbb{R}
, so that
V(-x)=V(x)
. Let
\tilde{V}(p)
be the Fourier transform of
V
, as in (
4.2
).
Then (
4.13
) and (
4.14
) are given by the following
expressions, which easily follow from
(
4.5
) and (
A.7
):

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{D}}\psi(p)=p^{2}\psi(p)+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}(\tilde{V}(p-q)-\tilde{V}(p+q)\big)\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q,

(4.15)

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{N}}\psi(p)=p^{2}\psi(p)+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}(\tilde{V}(p-q)+\tilde{V}(p+q)\big)\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q.

(4.16)

4.3
Minimal realization in the momentum approach

A priori it is not obvious which transformation is more appropriate for the
Bessel operator: the sine or the cosine transformation. Let us consider both, setting

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}L_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}},

(4.17)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}L_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}},

(4.18)

(where, as before,
L_{\alpha}
is treated as a formal expression).

Then, using
-\pi|p|
as the Fourier transform of
\frac{1}{x^{2}}
,
(
4.15
) and (
4.16
) yield

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}\psi(p)

\displaystyle=p^{2}\psi(p)+\Big(\alpha-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(\theta(p-q)q+\theta(q-p)p\big)\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q,

(4.19)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}\psi(p)

\displaystyle=p^{2}\psi(p)-\Big(\alpha-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(\theta(p-q)p+\theta(q-p)q\big)\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q.

(4.20)

So far, we have treated

\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}
and
\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}

as formal expressions. We would like to make them well defined, first
in the “minimal sense”.

In the position representation, an easy way to make
the expression
L_{\alpha}
well defined was to restrict its domain to

C_{\mathrm{c}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, which after the closure led to the
minimal realisation. Of course, it was not necessary to demand
that the support is away from zero, however functions
in the domain should have vanished
of an appropriate order at zero. In the momentum representation
it is useful to restrict the domain to rapidly decaying
functions—in the position representation this guarantees the
smoothness.
On the position side, this also guarantees that even/odd derivatives
at zero
vanish in the Dirichlet, resp. Neumann case, see (
A.14
). This does not
suffice—in addition, we need to impose conditions that on the position
side yield vanishing first/zeroth derivative.
Thus, we introduce the spaces (see Appx
A
):

L_{j}^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}):=\left\{\psi\in L^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\ |\ \int_{0}^{\infty}|\psi(p)|^{2}p^{n}\mathrm{d}p<\infty,\quad n=0,1,\dots;\quad\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(p)p^{j}\mathrm{d}p=0\right\},\quad j=0,1.

(4.21)

Theorem 4.1

1.

The operator

\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}

given by the
formula (
4.19
) restricted to
L_{1}^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

is well defined and closable. Let us denote its
closure by
\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}^{\min}
. We then have

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}^{\min}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}L_{\alpha}^{\min}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}.

(4.22)

2.

The operator

\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}

given by the formula (
4.20
) restricted to
L_{0}^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

is well defined and closable. Let us denote its
closure by

\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}^{\min}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}^{\min}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}L_{\alpha}^{\min}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}.

(4.23)

Proof.
Consider first the Dirichlet case.
Let
\psi\in L_{1}^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
x>0
. Consider

\displaystyle\phi(p)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(\theta(p-q)q+\theta(q-p)p\big)\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q=\int_{p}^{\infty}(p-q)\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q,

(4.24)

where in the last transition we used the property of Eq. (
4.21
).
Clearly,
\phi(0)=0
and
\lim\limits_{p\to\infty}\phi(p)=0
. Moreover,

\phi^{\prime}(p)=\int_{p}^{\infty}\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q

(4.25)

and
\lim\limits_{p\to\infty}\phi^{\prime}(p)=0
. We can integrate twice
obtaining

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\sin(px)\phi(p)\mathrm{d}p

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos(px)\phi^{\prime}(p)\mathrm{d}p

(4.26)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sin(px)\psi(p)\mathrm{d}p.

(4.27)

By (
A.15
),

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\sin(px)p^{2}\phi(p)\mathrm{d}p

\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sin(px)\phi(p)\mathrm{d}p.

(4.28)

Therefore,

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}\psi=L_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}\psi.

(4.29)

Thus
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}

coincides with
L_{\alpha}
on

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}L_{1}^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.

Suppose now that
\phi=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}\psi
with
\psi\in L_{1}^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. We have
\phi^{(n)}(0)=0
,
n=0,2,\dots

(by (
A.14
), because
\psi\in L^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
). Moreover,
\phi^{\prime}(0)=0
(by (
A.16
), because

\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}p\psi(p)\mathrm{d}p=0
).
Hence
\phi(x)=O(x^{3})
.

Let
\chi,\xi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
,
\chi=1
,

\xi=0
near
0
and
\chi=0
,
\xi=1
near
\infty
.
Set
\phi_{\epsilon}(x):=\chi(x/\epsilon)\xi(x\epsilon)\psi(x)
.
Then
we easily show that

\|L_{\alpha}(\phi-\phi_{\epsilon})\|^{2}+\|\phi-\phi_{\epsilon}\|^{2}\to 0
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
. Clearly,
\phi_{\epsilon}\in C_{\mathrm{c}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Therefore
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}L_{1}^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\subset\mathcal{D}(L_{\alpha}^{\min})
.

Clearly,
C_{\mathrm{c}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\subset\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}L_{1}^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.
Therefore,
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}L_{1}^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is dense in
\mathcal{D}(L_{\alpha}^{\min})
.

This proves that the closure of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}

restricted to
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}L_{1}^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

coincides with
L_{\alpha}^{\min}
.

Consider next the Neumann case.
Let
\psi\in L_{0}^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Consider

\displaystyle\phi(p)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}q\big(\theta(p-q)p+\theta(q-p)q\big)\psi(q)=\int_{p}^{\infty}(q-p)\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q.

(4.30)

Clearly,
\lim\limits_{p\to\infty}\phi(p)=0
. Moreover,

\phi^{\prime}(p)=\int_{0}^{p}\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q

(4.31)

and
\phi^{\prime}(p)=0
,
\lim\limits_{p\to\infty}\phi^{\prime}(p)=0
. We can integrate twice
obtaining

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos(px)\phi(p)\mathrm{d}p

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sin(px)\phi^{\prime}(p)\mathrm{d}p

(4.32)

\displaystyle={-}\frac{1}{x^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos(px)\psi(p)\mathrm{d}p.

(4.33)

Clearly,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos(px)p^{2}\phi(p)\mathrm{d}p

\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos(px)\phi(p)\mathrm{d}p.

(4.34)

Therefore

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}\psi=L_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}\psi.

(4.35)

Thus
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}

coincides with
L_{\alpha}
on

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}L_{0}^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.

Suppose now that
\phi=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}\psi
with
\psi\in L_{0}^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. We have
\phi^{(n)}(0)=0
,
n=1,3,\dots

(because
\psi\in L^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
). Moreover,
\phi(0)=0
(because

\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\psi(p)\mathrm{d}p=0
).
Hence
\phi(x)=O(x^{2})
.

The remaining arguments are the same as in the Dirichlet case.

\Box

4.4
Maximal realization in the momentum approach

In this subsection we present a construction of the
maximal operator

L_{\alpha}^{\max}
for
\alpha<1
. The construction involves two
steps:

1.

the choice of two vectors that together with

\mathcal{D}(L_{\alpha}^{\min})
span
\mathcal{D}(L_{\alpha}^{\max})
;

2.

the action of
L_{\alpha}^{\max}
on these two vectors.

We will first describe the construction for the generic case

\alpha\neq\frac{1}{4}
.
Let
\alpha<1
,
and

\alpha=m^{2}
. As we remember, if
\alpha\neq 0
, elements of
\mathcal{D}(L^{\mathrm{max}}_{\alpha})

behave like
c_{+}x^{1/2+m}+c_{-}x^{1/2-m}
as
x\rightarrow 0
.
By
(
A.18
) and (
A.19
),
the sine and cosine transforms of
x^{\frac{1}{2}\pm m}
are proportional to

p^{-\frac{3}{2}\mp m}
.
This suggests to enlarge the domain of
the minimal operators by functions that behave like

p^{-\frac{3}{2}\mp m}
as
p\rightarrow\infty
.
However, we cannot simply take
p^{-\frac{3}{2}\mp m}
, because they are not
square integrable near zero. This complicates the whole story.
(For
\alpha=0
, additionally, we need to modify all this using the logarithm.)

More precisely, let us fix

\psi_{\pm m,\mathrm{D}}
,
\psi_{\pm m,\mathrm{N}}
,
\psi_{\log,\mathrm{D}}
and
\psi_{\log,\mathrm{N}}

be functions in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
satisfying the following condition:
there exists
\Lambda_{0}>0
such that

\displaystyle p>\Lambda_{0}\ \Rightarrow\quad\psi_{\pm m,\mathrm{D}}(p)=p^{-\frac{3}{2}\mp m},

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{0}^{\Lambda_{0}}\psi_{\pm m,\mathrm{D}}(q)q\mathrm{d}q=\frac{\Lambda_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}\mp m}}{\frac{1}{2}\mp m},\quad{m\neq\pm\frac{1}{2}},

(4.36)

\displaystyle p>\Lambda_{0}\ \Rightarrow\quad\psi_{\log,\mathrm{D}}(p)=p^{-\frac{3}{2}}\log(p),

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{0}^{\Lambda_{0}}\psi_{\log,\mathrm{D}}(q)q\mathrm{d}q=-4\Lambda_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}+2\Lambda_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\log\Lambda_{0},

(4.37)

\displaystyle p>\Lambda_{0}\ \Rightarrow\quad\psi_{\pm m,\mathrm{N}}(p)=p^{-\frac{3}{2}\mp m},

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{0}^{\Lambda_{0}}\psi_{\pm m,\mathrm{N}}(q)\mathrm{d}q=\frac{\Lambda_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}\mp m}}{-\frac{1}{2}\mp m},\quad{m\neq\mp\frac{1}{2}},

(4.38)

\displaystyle p>\Lambda_{0}\ \Rightarrow\quad\psi_{\log,\mathrm{N}}(p)=p^{-\frac{3}{2}}\log(p),

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{0}^{\Lambda_{0}}\psi_{\log,\mathrm{N}}(q)\mathrm{d}q=-4\Lambda_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}-2\Lambda_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\log\Lambda_{0}.

(4.39)

Note that (
4.36
) and (
4.38

\displaystyle\Lambda>\Lambda_{0}\ \Rightarrow

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\psi_{\pm m,\mathrm{D}}(q)q\mathrm{d}q=\frac{\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}\mp m}}{\frac{1}{2}\mp m},\quad{m\neq\pm\frac{1}{2}},

(4.40)

\displaystyle\Lambda>\Lambda_{0}\ \Rightarrow

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\psi_{\log,\mathrm{D}}(q)q\mathrm{d}q=-4\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}+2\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\log\Lambda,

(4.41)

\displaystyle\Lambda>\Lambda_{0}\ \Rightarrow

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\psi_{\pm m,\mathrm{N}}(q)\mathrm{d}q=\frac{\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}\mp m}}{-\frac{1}{2}\mp m},\quad{m\neq\mp\frac{1}{2}},

(4.42)

\displaystyle\Lambda>\Lambda_{0}\ \Rightarrow

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\psi_{\log,\mathrm{N}}(q)\mathrm{d}q=-4\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}-2\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\log\Lambda.

We set

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}^{\max})

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{D}(\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}^{\min})+\mathbb{C}\psi_{m,\mathrm{D}}+\mathbb{C}\psi_{-m,\mathrm{D}},\quad{\alpha\neq\frac{1}{4}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\tilde{L}_{0,\mathrm{D}}^{\max})

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{D}(\tilde{L}_{0,\mathrm{D}}^{\min})+\mathbb{C}\psi_{0,\mathrm{D}}+\mathbb{C}\psi_{\log,\mathrm{D}}

(4.45)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}^{\max})

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{D}(\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}^{\min})+\mathbb{C}\psi_{m,\mathrm{N}}+\mathbb{C}\psi_{-m,\mathrm{N}},\quad{\alpha\neq\frac{1}{4}},

(4.46)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\tilde{L}_{0,\mathrm{N}}^{\max})

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{D}(\tilde{L}_{0,\mathrm{N}}^{\min})+\mathbb{C}\psi_{0,\mathrm{N}}+\mathbb{C}\psi_{\log,\mathrm{N}}.

(4.47)

Clearly, the difference of two functions
satisfying (

), (
4.37
), (
4.38
) or (
4.39

4.46
), (
4.45

In order to define the maximal operators in the momentum representation, we note that every
\psi

\displaystyle=p^{2}\psi(p)+\Big(\alpha-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\lim_{\Lambda\to\infty}\Bigg(\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\mathrm{d}q\big(\theta(p-q)q+\theta(q-p)p\big)\psi(q)+\frac{\tilde{c}_{+}p\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}-m}}{\frac{1}{2}+m}+\frac{\tilde{c}_{-}p\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}+m}}{\frac{1}{2}-m}\Bigg),

(4.50)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{0,\mathrm{D}}^{\max}\psi(p)

(4.51)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}^{\max}\psi(p)

Integration by parts shows that on considered domains the discussed operators are indeed self-adjoint.
\Box
.

The case
\alpha=\frac{1}{4}
needs a separate treatment. Let

\kappa\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{0\}
,
\kappa\neq 0
. Choose functions

\psi_{\mathrm{D},\underline{\kappa}},\psi_{\mathrm{D},\underline{\kappa}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
satisfying

\displaystyle p>\Lambda_{0}\ \Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{D},\underline{\kappa}}(p)=p^{-1},\qquad\int_{0}^{\infty}p\big(\psi_{\mathrm{D},\underline{\kappa}}(p)-p^{-1}\big)\mathrm{d}p=\frac{\pi}{2\kappa};

(4.87)

\displaystyle p>\Lambda_{0}\ \Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{N},\underline{\kappa}}(p)=p^{-2},\qquad\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi_{\mathrm{N},\underline{\kappa}}(p)\mathrm{d}p=-\frac{\pi}{2\kappa}.

(4.88)

(The underline below
\kappa
is meant to stress a different role of

\kappa

than that of
\tilde{\kappa}
for

\alpha\neq\frac{1}{4}
). We set

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\tilde{H}_{\frac{1}{2},\underline{0},\mathrm{D}})

\displaystyle:=L^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}_{+});

(4.89)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\tilde{H}_{\frac{1}{2},\underline{\kappa},\mathrm{D}})

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{D}(\tilde{L}_{\frac{1}{4},\mathrm{D}}^{\min})+\mathbb{C}\psi_{\mathrm{D},\underline{\kappa}},\quad\kappa\neq 0;

(4.90)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\tilde{H}_{-\frac{1}{2},\underline{\kappa},\mathrm{D}})

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{D}(\tilde{H}_{\pm\frac{1}{2},\underline{\kappa}^{-1},\mathrm{D}});

(4.91)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\tilde{H}_{-\frac{1}{2},\underline{0},\mathrm{N}})

\displaystyle:=L^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}_{+});

(4.92)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\tilde{H}_{-\frac{1}{2},\underline{\kappa},\mathrm{N}})

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{D}(\tilde{L}_{\frac{1}{4},\mathrm{N}}^{\min})+\mathbb{C}\psi_{\mathrm{N},\underline{\kappa}},\quad\kappa\neq 0;

(4.93)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\tilde{H}_{\frac{1}{2},\underline{\kappa},\mathrm{N}})

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{D}(\tilde{H}_{-\frac{1}{2},\underline{\kappa}^{-1},\mathrm{N}}).

(4.94)

We define
\tilde{H}_{\pm\frac{1}{2},\underline{\kappa},\mathrm{D}}

and
\tilde{H}_{\pm\frac{1}{2},\underline{\kappa},\mathrm{N}}
to be the
restrictions of

\tilde{L}_{\pm\frac{1}{4},\mathrm{D}}

resp.
\tilde{L}_{\pm\frac{1}{4},\mathrm{N}}
to the corresponding
domains specified above.

Theorem 4.5

For
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
the above operators are
self-adjoint and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}H_{\pm\frac{1}{2},\kappa}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{H}_{\pm\frac{1}{2},\underline{\kappa},\mathrm{D}},

(4.95)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}H_{\pm\frac{1}{2},\kappa}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{H}_{\pm\frac{1}{2},\underline{\kappa},\mathrm{N}}.

(4.96)

Proof.

It is enough to show

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}H_{\frac{1}{2},\kappa}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{H}_{\frac{1}{2},\underline{\kappa},\mathrm{D}},

(4.97)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}H_{-\frac{1}{2},\kappa}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{H}_{-\frac{1}{2},\underline{\kappa},\mathrm{N}}

(4.98)

for
\kappa\neq 0
.

Let us first consider the case
\kappa<0
, which is simpler. Every function in
\mathcal{D}(H_{\frac{1}{2},\kappa})=\mathcal{D}(H_{-\frac{1}{2},1/\kappa})
can be unambiguously written as a sum of an element of the minimal domain and
ce^{-\epsilon x}
, where
\epsilon=-1/\kappa>0
and
c\in\mathbb{C}
.
The sine transform of
\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon x}
is proportional to

\frac{p}{p^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}
. Now

\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(p\frac{p}{p^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}-1\Big)\mathrm{d}p=-\epsilon\frac{\pi}{2}=\frac{\pi}{2\kappa}.

(4.99)

Hence
\frac{p}{p^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}
belongs
to
\mathcal{D}(\tilde{H}_{\frac{1}{2},\underline{\kappa},\mathrm{D}})
.

The cosine transform of
\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon x}
is proportional to

\frac{1}{p^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}
, and

\int\frac{1}{p^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}\mathrm{d}p=\frac{\pi}{2\epsilon}=-\frac{\pi}{2\kappa}.

(4.100)

Hence
\frac{1}{p^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}
belongs
to
\mathcal{D}(\tilde{H}_{-\frac{1}{2},\underline{\kappa},\mathrm{N}})
.

To obtain the analogous decomposition of vectors from
\mathcal{D}(H_{\frac{1}{2},\kappa})=\mathcal{D}(H_{-\frac{1}{2},1/\kappa})
for
\kappa>0
and
\kappa=\infty
, we consider
a function
2\mathrm{e}^{-x}-\mathrm{e}^{-\delta x}
.
for
\delta\geq 2
.
Near
x=0
it behaves like
1+(\delta-2)x
, so that it is an element of
\mathcal{D}(H_{\frac{1}{2},\kappa})=\mathcal{D}(H_{-\frac{1}{2},1/\kappa})
for
\kappa=\frac{1}{\delta-2}
.
Its sine transform is proportional to
2\frac{p}{p^{2}+1}-\frac{p}{p^{2}+\delta^{2}}
, and

\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(2\frac{p}{p^{2}+1}-\frac{p}{p^{2}+\delta^{2}}-1\Big)=\frac{\pi}{2}(\delta-2)=\frac{\pi}{2\kappa}.

(4.101)

Its cosine transform is proportional to
2\frac{1}{p^{2}+1}-\frac{1}{p^{2}+\delta^{2}}
, and

\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(2\frac{1}{p^{2}+1}-\frac{1}{p^{2}+\delta^{2}}\Big)=\frac{\pi}{2}(2-\delta)=-\frac{\pi}{2\kappa}.

(4.102)

The case
\kappa=0
is treated in the same way, by taking e.g.
\mathrm{e}^{-x}-\mathrm{e}^{-2x}
.

\Box

4.6
Self-adjoint realizations in the momentum approach
II

We will now present what we called “physicists’ style
construction” of selfa-adjoint Bessel operators. As we discussed in the introduction, this construction
can be viewed as a “toy
illustration” of the Wilsonian renormalization group.
It will be valid for all
\alpha<1
, including
\alpha=\frac{1}{4}
.

The first step of “physicists’ construction” is imposing a cut off
on
(
4.19
) and (
4.20
):

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}(\Lambda)\psi(p):=\theta(\Lambda-p)\bigg(p^{2}\psi(p)+\Big(\alpha-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\big(\theta(p-q)q+\theta(q-p)p\big)\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q\bigg),

(4.103)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}(\Lambda)\psi(p):=\theta(\Lambda-p)\bigg(p^{2}\psi(p)-\Big(\alpha-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\big(\theta(p-q)p+\theta(q-p)q\big)\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q\bigg).

(4.104)

Note that (
4.103
) and (
4.104
) are bounded and self-adjoint.
We also need
counterterms, which will be proportional to the following rank-one operators:

\displaystyle K_{\mathrm{D}}(\Lambda)\psi(p)

\displaystyle:=\theta(\Lambda-p)\int_{0}^{\Lambda}pq\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q,

(4.105)

\displaystyle K_{\mathrm{N}}(\Lambda)\psi(p)

\displaystyle:=\theta(\Lambda-p)\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q.

(4.106)

To gain the intuition about the above operators let us note that
for a large class of functions
\psi
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\Lambda\to\infty}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}\psi|K_{\mathrm{D}}(\Lambda)\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}\psi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}|\psi^{\prime}(0)|^{2},

(4.107)

\displaystyle\lim_{\Lambda\to\infty}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}\psi|K_{\mathrm{N}}(\Lambda)\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}\psi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}|\psi(0)|^{2}.

(4.108)

We have already noted an important role played by the dilation in the
position representation. In principle, we have two kinds of momentum
representations–obtained by the sine and cosine transformation. The dilation coincides for both kinds of the momentum representation, and it will be denoted by

\tilde{U}(\tau)
:

\tilde{U}(\tau)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}U(\tau)\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}^{-1}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}U(\tau)\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}^{-1}.

(4.109)

Note that it acts in the opposite way than in the position representation:

\tilde{U}(\tau)\psi(p)=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}\tau}\psi(\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}p).

(4.110)

It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\tilde{U}(\tau)\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}(\Lambda)\tilde{U}(-\tau)=\mathrm{e}^{-2\tau}\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}(\mathrm{e}^{\tau}\Lambda),

\displaystyle\qquad\tilde{U}(\tau)K_{\mathrm{D}}(\Lambda)\tilde{U}(-\tau)=\mathrm{e}^{-3\tau}K_{\mathrm{D}}(\mathrm{e}^{\tau}\Lambda);

(4.111)

\displaystyle\tilde{U}(\tau)\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}(\Lambda)\tilde{U}(-\tau)=\mathrm{e}^{-2\tau}\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}(\mathrm{e}^{\tau}\Lambda),

\displaystyle\qquad\tilde{U}(\tau)K_{\mathrm{N}}(\Lambda)\tilde{U}(-\tau)=\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}K_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathrm{e}^{\tau}\Lambda).

(4.112)

We still need to multiply
K_{\mathrm{D}}
and

K_{\mathrm{N}}
by cutoff-dependent (“running”) coupling constants.
(
4.111
) and (
4.112
) suggest to write these coupling
constants as

\frac{f_{\Lambda}}{\Lambda}
, resp.

\Lambda g_{\Lambda}
. Thus
we expect that for suitably chosen functions
\Lambda\mapsto f_{\Lambda},g_{\Lambda}
, the following operators approximate
our Hamiltonians:

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}(\Lambda)+\frac{f_{\Lambda}}{\Lambda}K_{\mathrm{D}}(\Lambda),

(4.113)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}(\Lambda)+\Lambda g_{\Lambda}K_{\mathrm{N}}(\Lambda).

(4.114)

Let us guess the dependence
of
f_{\Lambda}
and
g_{\Lambda}
on the cut-off
\Lambda
.
Assume that for large momenta
\psi(p)\sim p^{-\frac{3}{2}-m}+\tilde{\kappa}p^{-\frac{3}{2}+m}
.
Acting with (
4.113
) and (
4.114
) on
\psi

for large
p<\Lambda
we obtain
the following leading-order dependence on the cut-off:

\displaystyle\Big(\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{D}}(\Lambda)+\frac{f_{\Lambda}}{\Lambda}K_{\mathrm{D}}(\Lambda)\Big)\psi(p),

(4.115)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle p\Big(m^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\Big(\frac{\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}-m}}{(-\frac{1}{2}-m)}+\tilde{\kappa}\frac{\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}+m}}{(-\frac{1}{2}+m)}\Big)+pf_{\Lambda}\Big(\frac{\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}-m}}{(\frac{1}{2}-m)}+\tilde{\kappa}\frac{\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}+m}}{(\frac{1}{2}+m)}\Big),

(4.116)

\displaystyle\big(\tilde{L}_{\alpha,\mathrm{N}}(\Lambda)+\Lambda g_{\Lambda}K_{\mathrm{N}}(\Lambda)\big)\psi(p)

(4.117)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle-\Big(m^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\Big(\frac{\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}-m}}{(\frac{1}{2}-m)}+\tilde{\kappa}\frac{\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}+m}}{(\frac{1}{2}+m)}\Big)+g_{\Lambda}\Big(\frac{\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}-m}}{(\frac{1}{2}+m)}+\tilde{\kappa}\frac{\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}+m}}{(\frac{1}{2}-m)}\Big).

(4.118)

Demanding that (
4.116
) and (
4.118
) vanish yields

\displaystyle f_{m,\tilde{\kappa},\Lambda}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Big((\frac{1}{2}-m)\Lambda^{-m}+\tilde{\kappa}(\frac{1}{2}+m)\Lambda^{m}\Big)}{\Big((\frac{1}{2}-m)^{-1}\Lambda^{-m}+\tilde{\kappa}(\frac{1}{2}+m)^{-1}\Lambda^{m}\Big)},

(4.119)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}-m^{2}-m\tanh\big(m\log(\Lambda/\lambda_{\mathrm{D}})\big),\qquad\lambda_{\mathrm{D}}^{-2m}=\frac{(\frac{1}{2}-m)}{(\frac{1}{2}+m)}\tilde{\kappa};

(4.120)

\displaystyle g_{m,\tilde{\kappa},\Lambda}

\displaystyle={-}\frac{\Big((\frac{1}{2}+m)\Lambda^{-m}+\tilde{\kappa}(\frac{1}{2}-m)\Lambda^{m}\Big)}{\Big((\frac{1}{2}+m)^{-1}\Lambda^{-m}+\tilde{\kappa}(\frac{1}{2}-m)^{-1}\Lambda^{m}\Big)},

(4.121)

\displaystyle={-}\frac{1}{4}{-}m^{2}{+}m\tanh\big(m\log(\Lambda/\lambda_{\mathrm{N}})\big),\qquad\lambda_{\mathrm{N}}^{{-}2m}=\frac{(\frac{1}{2}+m)}{(\frac{1}{2}-m)}\tilde{\kappa}.

(4.122)

It is easy to check that
g_{m,\tilde{\kappa},\Lambda}
can be obtained from
f_{m,\tilde{\kappa},\Lambda}
by changing

and
\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda^{-1}
. In terms of an initial value at the scale

\displaystyle f_{m,\tilde{\kappa},\Lambda}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}-m^{2}-m\frac{\big((\frac{1}{2}-m)^{2}+f_{\Lambda_{0}}\big)\Lambda^{2m}+\big((\frac{1}{2}+m)^{2}+f_{\Lambda_{0}}\big)\Lambda_{0}^{2m}}{-\big((\frac{1}{2}-m)^{2}+f_{\Lambda_{0}}\big)\Lambda^{2m}+\big((\frac{1}{2}+m)^{2}+f_{\Lambda_{0}}\big)\Lambda_{0}^{2m}};

(4.123)

\displaystyle g_{m,\tilde{\kappa},\Lambda}

\displaystyle={-}\frac{1}{4}{-}m^{2}+m\frac{\big((\frac{1}{2}+m)^{2}{+}g_{\Lambda_{0}}\big)\Lambda^{2m}+\big((\frac{1}{2}-m)^{2}{+}g_{\Lambda_{0}}\big)\Lambda_{0}^{2m}}{{-}\big((\frac{1}{2}+m)^{2}{+}g_{\Lambda_{0}}\big)\Lambda^{2m}+\big((\frac{1}{2}-m)^{2}{+}g_{\Lambda_{0}}\big)\Lambda_{0}^{2m}}.

(4.124)

For
m=0
the parameter
\tilde{\kappa}
does not work. Applying the de l’Hopital rule with
\tilde{\kappa}=-1-2m\tilde{\nu}
we obtain

\displaystyle f_{0,\Lambda}^{\tilde{\nu}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{(\log\Lambda+\tilde{\nu}+2)}{4(\log\Lambda+\tilde{\nu}-2)},

\displaystyle=-\frac{(\log\Lambda+\tilde{\nu}-2)}{4(\log\Lambda+\tilde{\nu}+2)}.

(4.126)

Note that (for both

and
m=0

and

\displaystyle=\Big(f_{\Lambda}+\frac{1}{4}+m^{2}\Big)^{2}-m^{2};

(4.127)

\displaystyle\Lambda\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\Lambda}g_{\Lambda}

\displaystyle={-}\Big(g_{\Lambda}{+}\frac{1}{4}{+}m^{2}\Big)^{2}{+}m^{2}.

(4.128)

The above heuristic analysis can be transformed into the following
rigorous statement:

Theorem 4.6

Let
m^{2}<1
. Let
f_{\Lambda}
and
g_{\Lambda}
be solutions of equations
(
4.127
), resp. (
4.128

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{m^{2},\mathrm{D}}(\Lambda)+\frac{f_{\Lambda}}{\Lambda}K_{\mathrm{D}}(\Lambda),

(4.129)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{m^{2},\mathrm{N}}(\Lambda)+\Lambda g_{\Lambda}K_{\mathrm{N}}(\Lambda)

(4.130)

converge as

to one of self-adjoint realizations of
Bessel operators
\tilde{L}_{m^{2}}
in the strong resolvent sense.

In
particular, for

take

and

g_{\Lambda}=g_{m,\tilde{\kappa},\mathrm{N}}
as in
(
4.123
), (
4.125
), resp. (
4.124

4.126

, resp.
\tilde{H}_{m,\tilde{\kappa},\mathrm{N}}
.

Proof.
The proof will be based on Theorem
VIII.25 (a) from
[
21

and
A

be self-adjoint operators and suppose that
\mathcal{D}
is a common core for
all
A_{n}
and

A_{n}\phi\rightarrow A\phi
for each

, then
A_{n}\rightarrow A
in the strong resolvent sense.

We start with the case
m^{2}\neq\frac{1}{4}
, so
that we can assume that
f_{\Lambda}=f_{m,\tilde{\kappa},\mathrm{D}}
and

g_{\Lambda}=g_{m,\tilde{\kappa},\mathrm{N}}
.
We can also use the description of maximal operators and

4.4
.

(4.131)

\displaystyle K_{\mathrm{D}}(\Lambda)\psi_{\mathrm{D}}(p)=\theta(\Lambda-p)\tilde{c}\Big(\Lambda+\frac{\pi}{2\kappa}\Big)p+O(\Lambda^{-\infty}),

(4.154)

\displaystyle K_{\mathrm{N}}(\Lambda)\psi_{\mathrm{N}}(p)=-\theta(\Lambda-p)\tilde{c}\Big(\frac{\pi}{2\kappa}+\Lambda^{-1}\Big)+O(\Lambda^{-\infty}).

(4.155)

Hence, from formulas (
4.73
)-(
4.74
) we obtain

\displaystyle\Big(\tilde{L}_{\frac{1}{4},\mathrm{D}}(\Lambda)+\frac{f_{\frac{1}{2},\underline{\kappa},\Lambda}}{\Lambda}K_{\mathrm{D}}(\Lambda)\Big)\psi\underset{\Lambda\to\infty}{\to}\tilde{L}_{\frac{1}{4},\mathrm{D}}^{\max}\psi=\tilde{H}_{\frac{1}{4},\underline{\kappa},\mathrm{D}}\psi,

(4.156)

\displaystyle\Big(\tilde{L}_{\frac{1}{4},\mathrm{N}}(\Lambda)+\Lambda g_{\frac{1}{2},\underline{\kappa}}K_{\mathrm{N}}(\Lambda)\Big)\psi\underset{\Lambda\to\infty}{\to}\tilde{L}_{\frac{1}{4},\mathrm{N}}^{\max}\psi=\tilde{H}_{\frac{1}{4},\underline{\kappa},\mathrm{N}}\psi.

(4.157)

The case
m=-\frac{1}{2}
is treated in the analogous way, by taking
f_{-\frac{1}{2},\underline{\kappa}}=f_{\frac{1}{2},\underline{\kappa}^{-1}}
and
g_{-\frac{1}{2},\underline{\kappa}}=g_{\frac{1}{2},\underline{\kappa}^{-1}}
.

\Box

Let us observe, that the
norm of the counter-terms converges to infinity both in the Dirichlet
and Neumann cases. In particular, we cannot neglect the counterterms
even in the Dirichlet case with
|\mathrm{Re}(m)|<\frac{1}{2}
(when one
can omit the counter-terms in Eq. (
4.50
)).

Note also that the above analysis
of
\tilde{H}_{m,\tilde{\kappa},\mathrm{D}}
is essentially an expanded version
of the Wilsonian approach of
[
7
]
, which we described in
Section
3
,
translated from 3 dimensions to 1 dimension.

5
Conclusion

Treatment of the Schrödinger equation with potential
1/r^{2}
requires an appropriate definition of the domain. This can be achieved directly, in the position representation, as it was presented in the Section 2. It can be also equivalently done in the momentum representation, essentially following the Wilsonian renormalization scheme. Despite having been devised as an approximate method, this scheme when rigorously implemented yields a construction of self-adjoint realizations of the Schrödinger operator
with potential
1/r^{2}
.

Appendix A
Fourier analysis on a halfline

Fourier analysis on the line is well-known. Somewhat less
known is Fourier analysis on the half-line, where the role of the
Fourier transformation is played by two transformations: the
cosine and sine transformation, which are the main subject of this
appendix.

A.1
Cosine and sine transformation

Let us start with recalling some aspects of
Fourier analysis on the line.
Let

\displaystyle H^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\ |\ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\phi^{(n)}(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x<\infty,\quad n=0,1,\dots\Big\},

(A.1)

\displaystyle L^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\ |\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi(p)|^{2}|p|^{n}\mathrm{d}p<\infty,\quad n=0,1,\dots\Big\}

(A.2)

be the Sobolev space and the weighted space of the infinite order–two
examples of Frechet
spaces.
The Fourier transformation swaps these spaces:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}L^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle=H^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}).

(A.3)

Functions on the line can be decomposed
into even and odd functions

L_{\pm}^{2}(\mathbb{R}):=\{\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\ |\ \psi(-x)=\pm\psi(x)\},\quad L^{2}(\mathbb{R})=L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\oplus L_{-}^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

(A.4)

Even and odd functions are preserved by the Fourier transformation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}L_{\pm}^{2}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle=L_{\pm}^{2}(\mathbb{R})

(A.5)

Every function on the halfline can be extended to an even or odd function:

J_{\pm}\psi(p):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\psi(p)&p\geq 0\\
\pm\psi(-p)&p<0.\end{array}\right.

(A.6)

J_{\pm}
maps
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
onto
L_{\pm}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. The restriction to the
positive halfline is the left inverse of
J_{\pm}
:

(J_{\pm}\psi)\Big|_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}=\psi,\quad\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}).

(A.7)

The sine and cosine transformations can be defined
as the composition of the Fourier transformation with the above
extension and the restriction, more precisely,

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}\psi=\mathrm{i}(\mathcal{F}J_{-}\psi)\Big|_{\mathbb{R}_{+}},\quad\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}\psi=(\mathcal{F}J_{+}\psi)\Big|_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}.

(A.8)

Introduce also the Frechet spaces analogous to (
A.1
) and
(
A.2
) corresponding to the halfline:

\displaystyle H^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\ |\ \int_{0}^{\infty}|\phi^{(n)}(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x<\infty,\quad n=0,1,\dots\Big\},

(A.9)

\displaystyle L^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\ |\int_{0}^{\infty}|\psi(p)|^{2}p^{n}\mathrm{d}p<\infty,\quad n=0,1,\dots\Big\}.

(A.10)

We will also need the following closed subspaces of
H^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
:

\displaystyle H_{+}^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

\displaystyle:=\{\phi\in H^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\ |\ \phi^{(n)}=0,\quad n=1,3,5,\dots\},

(A.11)

\displaystyle H_{-}^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

\displaystyle:=\{\phi\in H^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\ |\ \phi^{(n)}=0,\quad n=0,2,4,\dots\}.

(A.12)

The following proposition is straightforward:

Proposition A.1

\displaystyle J_{+}H_{+}^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

\displaystyle=H^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R})\cap L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R}),

\displaystyle J_{-}H_{-}^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

\displaystyle=H^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R})\cap L_{-}^{2}(\mathbb{R}),

(A.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}L^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

\displaystyle=H_{+}^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}L^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

\displaystyle=H_{-}^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}).

(A.14)

For
\psi\in L^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
we have

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2n}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}\psi

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}p^{2n}\psi,

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2n}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}\psi

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}p^{2n}\psi,

(A.15)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2n+1}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}\psi

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n+1}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}p^{2n+1}\psi,

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2n+1}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{N}}\psi

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}p^{2n+1}\psi.

(A.16)

A.2
Homogeneous functions on the halfline

Let
f\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. We say that
f
possesses an
oscillatory integral if for any
\phi\in C_{\mathrm{c}}^{\infty}([0,\infty[)
such that
\phi=1
near
0

\lim_{\Lambda\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(p)\phi(p/\Lambda)\mathrm{d}p

(A.17)

exists and does not depend on the choice of
\phi
. The value
(
A.17
) is then called the
oscillatory integral of
f
.

Note that the integrals that appear in the definitions of the cosine
and sine transforms (
4.6
), (
4.7
) for functions, say, from

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
can always be understood as oscillatory
(but not always in the usual Lebesgue sense).

In the following
formulas, valid for
x>0
, one needs to use oscillatory
integrals for
\lambda>-1
:

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sin(px)x^{\lambda}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{p^{-\lambda-1}}{\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}\lambda)\Gamma(-\lambda)},\quad\lambda>-2,

(A.18)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos(px)x^{\lambda}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{p^{-\lambda-1}}{\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}\lambda)\Gamma(-\lambda)},\quad\lambda>-1.

(A.19)

Lemma A.2

Suppose that
\psi\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
such
that for large
p
we have
\psi(p)=p^{\lambda}
. Set

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(p)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\Lambda\to\infty}\Big(-\int_{p}^{\Lambda}\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q+\frac{\Lambda^{\lambda+1}}{\lambda+1}\Big),

(A.20)

\displaystyle\psi_{2}(p)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\Lambda\to\infty}\Big(\int_{p}^{\Lambda}(p-q)\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q-\frac{p\Lambda^{\lambda+1}}{\lambda+1}+\frac{\Lambda^{\lambda+2}}{\lambda+2}\Big).

(A.21)

(The above limits exist, because the functions after the limit sign are
constant for large
\Lambda
).
Clearly,

\displaystyle\psi_{2}^{\prime}(p)=\psi_{1}(p),\qquad\psi_{1}^{\prime}(p)=\psi(p),

(A.22)

for large
p
,

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(p)=\frac{p^{\lambda+1}}{\lambda+1},\quad\psi_{2}(p)=\frac{p^{\lambda+2}}{(\lambda+1)(\lambda+2)}.

(A.23)

Moreover, the following holds:

1.

Suppose that

\lim_{\Lambda\to\infty}\Big(-\int_{0}^{\Lambda}q\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q+\frac{\Lambda^{\lambda+2}}{\lambda+2}\Big)=0.

(A.24)

Then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\sin(px)\psi(p)\mathrm{d}p=-x\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos(px)\psi_{1}(p)\mathrm{d}p=-x^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sin(px)\psi_{2}(p)\mathrm{d}p.

(A.25)

2.

Suppose that

\lim_{\Lambda\to\infty}\Big(\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\psi(q)\mathrm{d}q-\frac{\Lambda^{\lambda+1}}{\lambda+1}\Big)=0.

(A.26)

Then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos(px)\psi(p)\mathrm{d}p=x\int_{0}^{\infty}\sin(px)\psi_{1}(p)\mathrm{d}p=-x^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos(px)\psi_{2}(p)\mathrm{d}p.

(A.27)

(The above integrals are not always defined as Lebesgue
integrals—they are always defined as oscillatory integrals).

Proof.
1.
Let
\phi
be as in the definition of the oscillatory integral.
We integrate by parts:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(p/\Lambda)\sin(px)\psi(p)\mathrm{d}p

(A.28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-x\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(p/\Lambda)\cos(px)\psi_{1}(p)\mathrm{d}p-\Lambda^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi^{\prime}(p/\Lambda)\sin(px)\psi_{1}(p)\mathrm{d}p

(A.29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-x^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(p/\Lambda)\sin(px)\psi_{2}(p)\mathrm{d}p-\Lambda^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi^{\prime}(p/\Lambda)\sin(px)\psi_{1}(p)\mathrm{d}p

(A.30)

\displaystyle-x\psi_{2}(0)+x\Lambda^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi^{\prime}(p/\Lambda)\cos(px)\psi_{2}(p)\mathrm{d}p.

(A.31)

Now
\psi_{2}(0)=0
because of (
A.24
).
Terms involving
\phi^{\prime}

are
O(\Lambda^{-\infty})
, what can be checked by integration by parts.

2. The proof is similar:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(p/\Lambda)\cos(px)\psi(p)\mathrm{d}p

(A.32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(p/\Lambda)\sin(px)\psi_{1}(p)\mathrm{d}p-\psi_{1}(0)+\Lambda^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi^{\prime}(p/\Lambda)\cos(px)\psi_{1}(p)\mathrm{d}p

(A.33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-x^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(p/\Lambda)\cos(px)\psi_{2}(p)\mathrm{d}p-\psi_{1}(0)+\Lambda^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi^{\prime}(p/\Lambda)\cos(px)\psi_{1}(p)\mathrm{d}p

(A.34)

\displaystyle+x\Lambda^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi^{\prime}(p/\Lambda)\sin(px)\psi_{2}(p)\mathrm{d}p.

(A.35)

We use (
A.26
) to get
\psi_{1}(0)=0
.
Again, the terms involving
\phi^{\prime}

are
O(\Lambda^{-\infty})
.

\Box
