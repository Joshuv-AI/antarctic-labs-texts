Quasi-Isometric Bounded Generation by ℚ {\mathbb{Q}} -Rank-One Subgroups
Dave Witte MORRIS

Abstract
We say that a subset X X quasi-isometrically boundedly generates a finitely generated group Γ \Gamma if each element γ \gamma of a finite-index subgroup of Γ \Gamma can be written as a product γ = x 1 x 2 ⋯ x r \gamma=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{r} of a bounded number of elements of X X , such that the word length of each x i x_{i} is bounded by a constant times the word length of γ \gamma . A. Lubotzky, S. Mozes, and M.S. Raghunathan observed in 1993 that SL ⁡ ( n , ℤ ) {\rm SL}(n,{\mathbb{Z}}) is quasi-isometrically boundedly generated by the elements of its natural SL ⁡ ( 2 , ℤ ) {\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{Z}}) subgroups. We generalize (a slightly weakened version of) this by showing that every S S -arithmetic subgroup of an isotropic, almost-simple ℚ {\mathbb{Q}} -group is quasi-isometrically boundedly generated by standard ℚ {\mathbb{Q}} -rank-1 subgroups.

Introduction

A subset
X
“boundedly generates” a group
\Gamma
if every element
\gamma
of
\Gamma
can be written as a product
\gamma=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{r}
of a bounded number of elements of
X
(cf.
[
8
, p. 203]
). This is a very powerful notion in abstract group theory, but geometric group theorists may need the bounded generation to be “quasi-isometric”, which means that the word length of every
x_{i}
is bounded by a constant times the word length of
\gamma
(after passing to a finite-index subgroup). Lubotzky, Mozes, and Raghunathan
[
5
, Corollary 3]
observed in 1993 that
\SL(n,\mathbb{Z})
is quasi-isometrically boundedly generated by the elements of its natural
\SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
subgroups (but did not use this terminology). This implies that every finite-index subgroup
\Gamma
of
\SL(n,\mathbb{Z})
is quasi-isometrically boundedly generated by finitely many subgroups, each of which is the intersection of
\Gamma
with a conjugate of a natural
\SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
subgroup of
\SL(n,\mathbb{Z})
.

We generalize this by showing that every arithmetic subgroup of an isotropic, almost-simple
\mathbb{Q}
-group is quasi-isometrically boundedly generated by standard
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-1 subgroups. The proof is a minor modification of an argument of Lubotzky, Mozes, and Raghunathan
[
6
, Section 4]
that establishes a slightly weaker statement. (One could describe their result by saying that it omits the word “boundedly” from the conclusion.)

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be an arithmetic subgroup of a semisimple algebraic
\mathbb{Q}
-group
\mathbf{G}
. (So
\Gamma
is commensurable with
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Z})
.) We say that
\Gamma
is
quasi-isometrically boundedly generated by standard
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-
1
subgroups
if there exist constants
r=r(\mathbf{G},\Gamma)\in\mathbb{N}
and
C=C(\mathbf{G},\Gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, a finite subset
\Gamma_{0}=\Gamma_{0}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma)
of
\Gamma
, and a finite collection
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma)
of Zariski closed subgroups of
\mathbf{G}
, such that:

1.

Every element
\gamma
of
\Gamma
can be written in the form
\gamma=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{r}
, where, for each
i
, we have either:

(a)

x_{i}\in\mathbf{L}\cap\Gamma
, for some
\mathbf{L}\in\mathcal{L}
, and
\log\|x_{i}\|\leq C\log\|\gamma\|
, or

(b)

x_{i}\in\Gamma_{0}
.

2.

Each
\mathbf{L}\in\mathcal{L}
is a standard
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-1 subgroup of
\mathbf{G}
. This means that
\mathbf{L}
is a connected, almost
\mathbb{Q}
-simple subgroup of
\mathbf{G}
, with
\rank_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbf{L}=1
, and that there is a root
\alpha
of some maximal
\mathbb{Q}
-split torus
\mathbf{T}
of
\mathbf{G}
, such that the Lie algebra of
\mathbf{L}
is generated by the root spaces
\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}
for
\beta\in\big\{{\pm}\alpha,\pm 2\alpha,\pm\frac{1}{2}\alpha\big\}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Every arithmetic subgroup of an isotropic, almost-simple
\mathbb{Q}
-group is quasi-isometrically boundedly generated by standard
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-
1
subgroups.

Remarks 1.3
.

1.

In the special case where the arithmetic subgroup is
\SL(n,\mathbb{Z})
, the Comptes Rendus note of Lubotzky, Mozes, and Raghunathan
[
5
, Corollary 3]
not only proves the full strength of our theorem, but also provides the much more explicit conclusion that the collection
\mathcal{L}
of standard
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-1 subgroups can be taken to be the set of natural copies of
\SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
in
\SL(n,\mathbb{Z})
, that the length
r
of the word can be taken to be
n^{2}-n
, and that no additional finite set
\Gamma_{0}
is required. (See Proposition
2.3
below.)

2.

Assuming that
\rank_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{G}\geq 2
(which is the only nontrivial case of Definition
1.1
), a famous theorem of Lubotzky, Mozes, and Raghunathan
[
6
, Theorem A]
tells us that the word length
\ell_{\Gamma}(\gamma)
is within a bounded multiplicative constant of
\log\|\gamma\|
. Therefore, the bound on
\log\|x_{i}\|
in Definition
1.1
(1a) tells us that
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\ell_{\Gamma}(x_{i})\leq C\ell_{\Gamma}(\gamma)
, for some constant
C
. This is the motivation for the use of the term “quasi-isometric” in Definition
1.1
.

3.

See Section
5
for extensions of Theorem
1.2
to the setting of
S
-arithmetic subgroups.

The Margulis arithmeticity theorem (cf.
[
7
, Theorem 5.2.1, p. 90]
) implies that Theorem
1.2
can also be stated in the language of Lie groups:

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
\Gamma
be a noncocompact, irreducible lattice in a connected, semisimple Lie group
G
with finite center. Then
\Gamma
is quasi-isometrically boundedly generated by
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-
1
subgroups. More precisely, there exist constants
r=r(\mathbf{G},\Gamma)\in\mathbb{N}
and
C=C(\mathbf{G},\Gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, a finite subset
\Gamma_{0}=\Gamma_{0}(G,\Gamma)
of
\Gamma
, and a finite collection
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(G,\Gamma)
of closed, connected, semisimple subgroups of
G
, such that:

1.

Every element
\gamma
of
\Gamma
can be written in the form
\gamma=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{r}
, where, for each
i
, we have either:

(a)

x_{i}\in L\cap\Gamma
, for some
L\in\mathcal{L}
, and
\log\|x_{i}\|\leq C\log\|\gamma\|
,
or

(b)

x_{i}\in\Gamma_{0}
.

2.

For each
L\in\mathcal{L}
, the intersection
L\cap\Gamma
is an irreducible lattice in
L
, with
\rank_{\mathbb{Q}}(L\cap\Gamma)=1
.

Remarks 1.5
.

In the statement of the corollary:

1.

The notion of
\mathbb{Q}
-rank is the natural extension that applies to all lattices in semisimple Lie groups, not only the arithmetic ones: non-arithmetic irreducible lattices arise only in real rank one, where the
\mathbb{Q}
-rank is defined to be
0
for cocompact lattices, and to be
1
for non-cocompact lattices (see
[
7
, Definition 9.1.8, pp. 193–194]
).

2.

\|x\|
can be taken to be the norm of
x
in a finite-dimensional matrix representation of
G
that has finite kernel, or
\log\|x\|
could be replaced with either the word length
\ell_{\Gamma}(x)
, or the distance from the identity to
x
, with respect to a left-invariant Riemannian metric on
G
.

3.

Each subgroup in
\mathcal{L}(G,\Gamma)
may be assumed to be “standard”, as in Dedinition
1.1
(2). This condition is a bit complicated to state in Lie-theoretic terms, but see Definition
6.1
.

4.

The assumption that
G
has finite center can be eliminated (see Corollary
6.2
).

2
Idea of the proof: the example of
\boldsymbol{\SL(n,\boldsymbol{\mathbb{Z}})}

This section is purely expository, and may therefore be passed over. It presents the proof in the (known) easy special case where
\Gamma=\SL(n,\mathbb{Z})
, and then describes the main issues that are involved in generalizing the argument.

Notation 2.1
.

For
s,t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, we write
s\prec t
if
s
is bounded by a polynomial function of
t
:
s\leq t^{C}+C
for some constant
C>0
. (The constant must be independent of
s
and
t
, but may depend on the parameter
n
in
\SL(n,\mathbb{Z})
.) Equivalently,
\log s\leq\max\{C^{\prime}\log t,C^{\prime}\}
, for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
.

Notation 2.2
.

Let
L_{k,\ell}
be the copy of
\SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
in
\SL(n,\boldsymbol{\mathbb{Z}})
that is supported on the matrix entries
(k,k)
,
(k,\ell)
,
(\ell,k)
, and
(\ell,\ell)
.

Proposition 2.3

(Lubotzky–Mozes–Raghunathan
[
5
, Corollary 3]
)
.

Let
r=n^{2}-n
. For all
\gamma\in\SL(n,\mathbb{Z})
, there exist
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\in\SL(n,\mathbb{Z})
, such that:

1)

\gamma=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{r}
,

2)

for each
i
, there exist
k,\ell
, such that
x_{i}\in L_{k,\ell}
,
and

3)

\|x_{i}\|\prec\|\gamma\|
for all
i
.

Proof
(Lubotzky–Mozes–Raghunathan).

For each nonzero
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, it is easy to find
x\in\SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
, such that
xv=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}+\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
, and
\|x\|\prec\|v\|
.
This implies there is
x_{2,1}\in L_{2,1}
, such that
(x_{2,1}\gamma)_{2,1}=0
.
Then, similarly, there is
x_{3,1}\in L_{3,1}
, such that
(x_{3,1}x_{2,1}\gamma)_{3,1}=0
. It is important to note that multiplying on the left by
x_{3,1}
does not affect the second row (indeed, it only affects the 1st row and the 3rd row), so we have
(x_{3,1}x_{2,1}\gamma)_{2,1}=(x_{2,1}\gamma)_{2,1}=0
. Continuing in this way, we have
x_{4,1},x_{5,1},\ldots,x_{n,1}
, so that if we let

\gamma^{(1)}=x_{n,1}x_{n-1,1}\cdots x_{2,1}\gamma,

then
\gamma^{(1)}_{i,1}=0
for
i=2,3,\ldots,n
.
(We may also assume
\gamma^{(1)}_{1,1}>0
. Since
\gamma^{(1)}\in\SL(n,\mathbb{Z})
, it is easy to see that this implies
\gamma^{(1)}_{1,1}=1
.)

Next, we work on the second column: for
i=3,4,\ldots,n
, there exists
x_{i,2}\in L_{i,2}
, such that if
\gamma^{(2)}=x_{n,2}x_{n-1,2}\cdots x_{3,2}\gamma^{(1)}
, then
\gamma^{(2)}_{i,2}=0
for
i=3,4,\ldots,n
. (Also,
\gamma^{(2)}_{2,2}=1
.)
Continuing in this way, column-after-column, we obtain
\gamma^{(n-1)}
, such that
\gamma^{(n-1)}_{i,j}=0
for
i>j
. Also,
\gamma^{(n-1)}_{i,i}=1
for all
i
.

So
\gamma^{(n-1)}
is a unipotent upper-triangular matrix. It is then easy to reduce to the identity matrix by multiplying by unipotent upper-triangular elements of the various
L_{k,\ell}
.
∎

Extending these ideas to a proof of Theorem
1.2
encounters a few complications. All but the first are dealt with by the argument of Lubotzky, Mozes, and Raghunathan in
[
6
, Section 4]
.

The first complication

In order to retain the progress that has been made in earlier steps, the matrix entries need to be annihilated in a carefully chosen order.
For example, suppose we have
\gamma_{2,1}=0
. There exists
x\in L_{2,3}
, such that
(x\gamma)_{3,2}=0
. However, it will probably not be the case that
(x\gamma)_{2,1}=0
. So we should not annihilate the
(3,2)
-entry until after the
(3,1)
-entry has been annihilated.

Also, there may not be a convenient matrix representation of
\Gamma
. However, the off-diagonal entries of matrices in
\SL(n,\mathbb{Z})
correspond to roots of the algebraic group, so, instead of annihilating “matrix entries”, we will instead write a (generic) element of
\Gamma
in the form
\gamma=\bigl(\prod\limits_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}u_{\alpha}\bigr)\cdot p
, where
u_{\alpha}
is in the root subgroup of
\mathbf{G}
corresponding to the positive root
\alpha
, and
p
is in a minimal parabolic
\mathbb{Q}
-subgroup.
The product is taken with respect to an appropriately chosen order of the positive roots, and we will annihilate each
u_{\alpha}
one-by-one, in this order, by multiplying by an element of the standard
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-1 subgroup
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
that corresponds to
\alpha
.

When
\rank_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbf{G}=2
, the root system is in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and we will use the clockwise ordering of the positive roots (or, in other words, left-to-right, if the positive roots are in the upper half-plane). For higher rank, we project the root system to a generic 2-dimensional plane, and then use the clockwise order in this 2-dimensional representation.

Remark 2.4
.

The argument of Lubotzky, Mozes, and Raghunathan in
[
6
, Section 4]
annihilates the root elements in a different order; it allows the annihilation of a particular
u_{\alpha}
to have side effects that undermine some of the previous work. Thus, the inductive argument re-annihilates some roots an unbounded number of times. The only new idea in the present paper is the observation that the roots can be annihilated in an order that never requires a root to annihilated more than once.

The second complication

A general arithmetic subgroup of
\mathbb{Q}
-rank one may have more than one cusp. This means that multiplying by an element of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\cap\Gamma
might not be able to annihilate
u_{\alpha}
. But there are only finitely many cusps, which means that
u_{\alpha}
can be moved into a finite set. It turns out that each element of this set simply applies a conjugation to the calculations. Therefore, to deal with this issue, it suffices to allow (finitely many) conjugates of the
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-one subgroups that we started with.

The third complication

The above arguments reduce to the case where
\gamma
is in a minimal parabolic
\mathbb{Q}
-subgroup
\mathbf{P}
. In
\SL(n,\mathbb{Z})
, this implies (up to finite index) that
\gamma
is in the unipotent radical of
\mathbf{P}
, and it is easy to finish the proof from there. In general, however, the anisotropic part
\mathbf{M}
of the reductive Levi factor of
\mathbf{P}
may have infinitely many integer points.

Fortunately, this is a very minor issue. To deal with it, Lemma
3.7
points out that if
\rank_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbf{G}\geq 2
, then there are finitely many standard
\mathbb{Q}
-subgroups
\mathbf{L}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{L}_{k}
of
\mathbf{G}
, such that
\rank_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbf{L}_{i}<\rank_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbf{G}
for all
i
, and (up to finite index) every element of
\mathbf{M}(\mathbb{Z})
can be efficiently written as the product of a bounded number of elements of
\mathbf{L}_{1}(\mathbb{Z})\cup\mathbf{L}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\cup\cdots\cup\mathbf{L}_{k}(\mathbb{Z})
. By induction on
\rank_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbf{G}
, we may assume that each
\mathbf{L}_{i}
is quasi-isometrically boundedly generated by
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-1 subgroups. It is then easy to complete the proof.

3
Preliminaries

We assume familiarity with the basic theory of algebraic groups over
\mathbb{Q}
.

Notation 3.1
.

The symbol
\mathbf{G}
always denotes a simply connected,
\mathbb{Q}
-isotropic, almost
\mathbb{Q}
-simple algebraic group (except that
\mathbf{G}
is not assumed to be simply connected in Lemma
3.4
, and
\mathbb{Q}
is replaced by a more general number field in Proposition
5.2
).

Although Definition
1.1
refers to a subgroup
\Gamma
of
\mathbf{G}
, the following observation shows that it is actually a property of the
\mathbb{Q}
-group
\mathbf{G}
, rather than a property of the arithmetic group
\Gamma
(or the pair
(\mathbf{G},\Gamma)
).

Lemma 3.2
.

Quasi-isometric bounded generation by
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-
1
subgroups is invariant under commensurability:
if
\Gamma_{1}
is quasi-isometrically boundedly generated by
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-
1
subgroups, and
\Gamma_{2}
is a subgroup of
\mathbf{G}
that is commensurable to
\Gamma_{1}
, then
\Gamma_{2}
also has this property
(
but perhaps with a different choice of the constants
r
and
C
, the finite set
\Gamma_{0}
, and the collection
\mathcal{L})
.

Proof.

We may assume that
\Gamma_{2}
is either a finite-index subgroup of
\Gamma_{1}
, or a finite-index supergroup of
\Gamma_{1}
. We apply standard arguments from geometric group theory to each of these cases.

If
\Gamma_{2}
is a finite-index supergroup of
\Gamma_{1}
, let
\Lambda_{0}
be a set of coset representatives of
\Gamma_{2}/\Gamma_{1}
. Then every element of
\Gamma_{2}
is of the form
\lambda x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{r}
, with
\lambda\in\Lambda_{0}\subseteq\Gamma_{0}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{2})
, and either
x_{i}\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{1})
or
x_{i}\in\mathbf{L}\cap\Gamma_{1}\subseteq\mathbf{L}\cap\Gamma_{2}
, with
\mathbf{L}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{1})
. Therefore, we may let
\Gamma_{0}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{2})=\Gamma_{0}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{1})\cup\Lambda_{0}
,

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{2})=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{1})
and
r(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{2})=r(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{1})+1
.

We now consider the possibility that
\Gamma_{2}
is a finite-index subgroup of
\Gamma_{1}
. By passing to a further subgroup, we may assume that
\Gamma_{2}
is normal in
\Gamma_{1}
(and has finite index).
Let
\Lambda_{0}
be a symmetric finite subset of
\Gamma_{1}
that contains a set of coset representatives of
\Gamma_{1}/\Gamma_{2}
(and also contains the identity element). We know that every element
\gamma
of
\Gamma_{2}
is of the form
\gamma=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{r}
, with each
x_{i}
either in some
\mathbf{L}\cap\Gamma_{1}
or in
\Gamma_{0}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{1})
. By enlarging
\Gamma_{0}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{1})
to include a set of coset representatives of each
(\mathbf{L}\cap\Gamma_{1})/(\mathbf{L}\cap\Gamma_{2})
(and doubling
r
), we may assume each
x_{i}
is either in some
\mathbf{L}\cap\Gamma_{2}
or in
\Gamma_{0}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{1})
.
Let

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{2})=\bigl(\Lambda_{0}\cdot\Gamma_{0}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{1})\cdot\Lambda_{0}\bigr)\cap\Gamma_{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{2})=\big\{\lambda\mathbf{L}\lambda^{-1}\,|\,\mathbf{L}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{1}),\,\lambda\in\Lambda_{0}\big\}.

For each
i
, there exists
\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{0}
, such that
x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{i}\lambda_{i}\in\Gamma_{2}
. Note that if
x_{i}\notin\Gamma_{0}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{1})
, then we have

x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{i}\lambda_{i-1}=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{i-1}\lambda_{i-1}\cdot\lambda_{i-1}^{-1}x_{i}\lambda_{i-1}\in\Gamma_{2}\cdot\lambda_{i-1}^{-1}\Gamma_{2}\lambda_{i-1}=\Gamma_{2}

(since
\Gamma_{2}\triangleleft\Gamma_{1}
). Therefore, we may assume
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{i-1}
whenever
x_{i}\notin\Gamma_{0}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{1})
. We may also assume
\lambda_{r}=1
, since
x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{r}=\gamma\in\Gamma_{2}
.
Define

x_{i}^{\prime}=\lambda_{i-1}^{-1}x_{i}\lambda_{i}\qquad\text{(where $\lambda_{0}=1$)}.

Then each
x_{i}^{\prime}
is either in
\mathbf{L}\cap\Gamma_{2}
for some
\mathbf{L}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{2})
, or in
\Gamma_{0}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{2})
,
and we have

\displaystyle\gamma=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{r}=\big(\lambda_{0}^{-1}x_{1}\lambda_{1}\big)\big(\lambda_{1}^{-1}x_{1}\lambda_{2}\big)\cdots\big(\lambda_{r-1}^{-1}x_{r}\lambda_{r}\big)\lambda_{r}^{-1}=x_{1}^{\prime}x_{2}^{\prime}\cdots x_{r}^{\prime}

since
\lambda_{r}=1
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

The above proof shows that we may choose every element of
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{2})
to be
\Gamma_{1}
-conjugate to an element of
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{G},\Gamma_{1})
.

Since the image of an arithmetic subgroup under an isogeny is an arithmetic subgroup of the image, a similar argument yields the following result (which explains why we may assume that
\mathbf{G}
is simply connected in Notation
3.1
):

Lemma 3.4
.

Quasi-isometric bounded generation by
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-
1
subgroups is invariant under isogeny:
the arithmetic subgroups of
\mathbf{G}
are quasi-isometrically boundedly generated by
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-
1
subgroups if and only if the arithmetic subgroups of the universal cover
\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}
are quasi-isometrically boundedly generated by
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-
1
subgroups.

Let
\Gamma
be an arithmetic subgroup of
\mathbf{G}
. In order to prove Theorem
1.2
, we wish to show that
\Gamma
is quasi-isometrically boundedly generated by standard
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-1 subgroups.

Notation 3.5
.

1.

We assume
\Gamma=\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Z})
(see Lemma
3.2
).

2.

Let
\mathbf{T}
be a maximal
\mathbb{Q}
-split torus of
\mathbf{G}
. We may assume
\dim\mathbf{T}\geq 2
(in other words,
\rank_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbf{G}\geq 2
), for otherwise it is obvious that
\Gamma
has the desired property (by letting
\mathcal{L}=\{\mathbf{G}\}
,
r=C=1
, and
\Gamma_{0}=\varnothing
).

3.

Let
\Phi
be the
\mathbb{Q}
-root system of
\mathbf{G}
corresponding to
\mathbf{T}
.
(So
\Phi
is a subset of the character group
\mathbf{T}^{*}
.)

4.

Let
\eta\colon\mathbf{T}^{*}\otimes\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
be an
\mathbb{R}
-linear map, such that
\eta(\Phi)
is disjoint from
\mathbb{R}
, and
\eta
is injective on the set
\mathbb{R}\Phi
, which is a finite union of lines through the origin. (These conditions hold for a generic choice of
\eta
.)

5.

Let
\Phi^{+}=\Phi\cap\eta^{-1}(\mathfrak{H})
, where
\mathfrak{H}\subset\mathbb{C}
is the (open) upper half-plane.
Since
\eta^{-1}(\mathfrak{H})
is an open half-space in
\mathbf{T}^{*}\otimes\mathbb{R}
(and its boundary
\eta^{-1}(\mathbb{R})
is disjoint from
\Phi
), we know that
\Phi^{+}
is a choice of positive roots for
\Phi
.

6.

Let

be a list of the equivalence classes of positive roots, where two positive roots are equivalent if they are scalar multiples of each other. We specify the ordering of this list by requiring that

7.

For each

\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus\mathbb{R}\eta(\Phi^{+}_{i})
is the union of two open half-planes.

•

The half-plane on the

of

contains

for

(unless

), whereas

•

the half-plane on the
left

\eta(\Phi^{+}_{j})
for
j<i
(unless
i=k+1
and
j=0
).

We say that a root

of

if

of
\Phi_{i}

is in the half-plane that is on the right of
\eta(\Phi^{+}_{i})

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{\Phi_{i}}=\{\phi\in\Phi\,|\,\text{$\phi$ is to the right of $\Phi^{+}_{i}$}\},\qquad

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{\Phi^{+}_{i}}=\overrightarrow{\Phi_{i}}\cap\Phi^{+},

\displaystyle\overleftarrow{\Phi_{i}}=\{\phi\in\Phi\,|\,\text{$\phi$ is to the left of $\Phi^{+}_{i}$}\},\qquad

\displaystyle\overleftarrow{\Phi^{+}_{i}}=\overleftarrow{\Phi_{i}}\cap\Phi^{+}.

, for

.
Also note that

and
\Phi^{+}_{i}

\overrightarrow{\Phi^{+}_{0}}=\overleftarrow{\Phi^{+}_{k+1}}=\Phi^{+}

For

Assumption 3.6
.

Theorem
1.2
may be of interest to non-algebraists, so, to eliminate some algebraic technicalities, we will henceforth assume that
\mathbf{G}
is absolutely almost simple. This means we are assuming that the Lie group
G=\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
is almost simple and is not a complex Lie group. For example,
G
can be
\SL(n,\mathbb{R})
or
\SO(p,q)
(with
p+q\geq 5
) or
\SU(p,q)
or
\Sp(2n,\mathbb{R})
or
\Sp(p,q)
(but
G
cannot be a group such as
\SL(n,\mathbb{R})\times\SL(n,\mathbb{R})
that has more than one simple factor, and cannot be a complex group, such as
\SL(n,\mathbb{C})
or
\SO(n,\mathbb{C})
).
(Actually,
\SO(p,q)
is not simply connected as an algebraic group, so, although the theorem applies to it, the proof needs to consider the corresponding spin group, which is a double cover.)
See Remark
4.2
for minor modifications of the proof that remove this restriction.

To prove Theorem
1.2
, we need to show that each
\gamma\in\Gamma
can be written as an appropriate product
\gamma=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{r}
. The upshot of the following easy lemma (combined with induction on
\rank_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbf{G}
) is that it will suffice to write
\gamma=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{r}m
, where
m
is in a Levi factor of some member of a finite collection of proper parabolic
\mathbb{Q}
-subgroups of
\mathbf{G}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Recall that
\rank_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbf{G}\geq 2

(
see Notation
3.5
(2))
.
Let
\mathbf{M}
be a
(
reductive
)
Levi factor of some proper parabolic
\mathbb{Q}
-subgroup of
\mathbf{G}
. Then there is a finite set
\mathcal{L}
of subgroups of
\mathbf{G}
, a finite subset
\Gamma_{0}
of
\mathbf{M}(\mathbb{Z})
, and
r\in\mathbb{N}
, such that

1)

each
\mathbf{L}\in\mathcal{L}
is an isotropic almost-simple factor of a Levi factor of a parabolic
\mathbb{Q}
-subgroup of
\mathbf{G}
, such that
1\leq\rank_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbf{L}<\rank_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbf{G}
, and

2)

every element
\gamma
of
\mathbf{M}(\mathbb{Z})
can be written in the form
\gamma=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{r}
, where, for each
i

•

x_{i}\in\Gamma_{0}
, or

•

x_{i}\in\mathbf{L}(\mathbb{Z})
, for some
\mathbf{L}\in\mathcal{L}
, and
\|x_{i}\|\prec\|\gamma\|
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{S}
be a maximal
\mathbb{Q}
-torus of
\mathbf{M}
that contains a maximal
\mathbb{Q}
-split torus
\mathbf{T}
of
\mathbf{M}
, and let
\mathcal{L}
be the set of all subgroups that are an isotropic almost
\mathbb{Q}
-simple factor of the Levi factor of a maximal parabolic
\mathbb{Q}
-subgroup of
\mathbf{G}
that contains
\mathbf{S}
. (The Levi factor is also required to contain
\mathbf{S}
.) Note that
\mathcal{L}
is finite, since each element of
\mathcal{L}
is generated by root subgroups of the maximal torus
\mathbf{S}
.

By assumption,
\mathbf{M}
is a Levi factor of some parabolic
\mathbb{Q}
-subgroup
\mathbf{P}
of
\mathbf{G}
. By enlarging
\mathbf{M}
, we may assume
\mathbf{P}
is maximal, so
\mathbf{P}
corresponds to some circled vertex in the Tits index
\mathcal{D}
of
\mathbf{G}
. (More precisely, it corresponds to the orbit of this circled vertex under the
*
-action of the Galois group.)
This circled vertex represents a simple root
\beta
of
\mathbf{S}
that is nontrivial on the maximal
\mathbb{Q}
-split torus
\mathbf{T}
. Since
\rank_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbf{G}>1
, there is some other circled simple root
\phi
(that is not in the same orbit of the
*
-action of the Galois group).

We can write
\mathbf{M}
as an almost-direct product
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{S}_{0}\mathbf{M}_{1}\mathbf{M}_{2}\cdots\mathbf{M}_{k}
, where
\mathbf{S}_{0}
is the central torus, and each
\mathbf{M}_{i}
is almost
\mathbb{Q}
-simple.
Then the subgroup
\mathbf{S}_{0}(\mathbb{Z})\mathbf{M}_{1}(\mathbb{Z})\mathbf{M}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\cdots\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathbb{Z})
has finite index in
\mathbf{M}(\mathbb{Z})
, so there is no harm in assuming that
\gamma
is in either
\mathbf{S}_{0}
or some
\mathbf{M}_{i}
.

Consider first the case where
\gamma\in\mathbf{M}_{i}
.
Let
\overline{\mathcal{D}}
be the quotient of
\mathcal{D}
in which two vertices are identified if they are in the same orbit of the
*
-action. Then the simple factor
\mathbf{M}_{i}
is generated by the roots corresponding to the vertices in some component
C
of
\overline{\mathcal{D}}\smallsetminus\overline{\beta}
(cf.
[
11
, ???? 2.5.4, p. 41]
).
And we may assume
\mathbf{M}_{i}
is anisotropic. (Otherwise, we have
\mathbf{M}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}
, so the desired conclusion is obvious.) This means that
C
has no circled vertices, so, in particular, the circled vertex
\overline{\phi}
cannot be in
C
. Therefore
C\cup\{\overline{\beta}\}
is contained in a single connected component
C_{1}
of
\overline{\mathcal{D}}\smallsetminus\{\overline{\phi}\}

corresponds to an almost-simple factor of the Levi factor of the maximal parabolic that corresponds to (the
*

\phi
. This simple factor contains
\mathbf{M}_{i}
(because
C_{1}
contains
C
). And this simple factor is isotropic (because

contains a circled vertex, namely
\overline{\beta}
), so it is in
\mathcal{L}
.

We may now assume
\gamma\in\mathbf{S}_{0}
, so
\gamma
is in the maximal torus
\mathbf{S}
.
For each root
\alpha
in a base
\Sigma
of the root system
\Phi(\mathbf{T},\mathbf{G})
, let

\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}=\bigl(\langle\mathbf{U}_{\alpha},\mathbf{U}_{-\alpha}\rangle\cap\mathbf{S}\bigr)^{\circ},

so
\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}
is a subtorus of
\mathbf{S}
that is defined over
\mathbb{Q}
. Since
\mathbf{G}
has no anisotropic factors (indeed,
\mathbf{G}
is
\mathbb{Q}
-simple and isotropic), it is not difficult to see that

\langle\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}\,|\,\alpha\in\Sigma\rangle=\mathbf{S}
,
so

\langle\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}^{a}\,|\,\alpha\in\Sigma\rangle=\mathbf{S}^{a}
,
where
\mathbf{R}^{a}
denotes the anisotropic part of a
\mathbb{Q}
-torus
\mathbf{R}
.
This implies that

\langle\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}^{a}(\mathbb{Z})\,|\,\alpha\in\Sigma\rangle
is a finite-index subgroup of
\mathbf{S}^{a}(\mathbb{Z})
.
Since
\mathbf{S}^{a}(\mathbb{Z})
is a finitely generated abelian group, we conclude that each

in a certain finite-index subgroup of

can be written in the form

\gamma=\prod\limits_{\alpha\in\Sigma}\gamma_{\alpha}
,
with

\mathbf{S}^{a}(\mathbb{Z})

is coarsely Lipschitz equivalent to

. And we know (for example, from the above argument for the case where

Definition 3.8

(cf.
[

)

For each simple root
\alpha

\Phi^{+}

\lambda_{\alpha}

-weight of
\mathbf{G}
that is orthogonal to all of the simple roots of
\Phi^{+}
, other than

. (This determines

up to a positive multiple: the condition determines

on

must be trivial on the anisotropic part of a chosen maximal
\mathbb{Q}
-torus that contains

\mathbb{Q}
.) After replacing
\lambda_{\alpha}
by a positive integral multiple, we may assume there is a finite-dimensional irreducible
\mathbb{Q}
-representation
V_{\alpha}
of
\mathbf{G}
whose lowest weight is
-\lambda_{\alpha}
, and such that the lowest weight space
V_{\alpha}^{-\lambda_{\alpha}}

\lambda_{\alpha}

Let

V_{\alpha}
. Choose nonzero
\mathbb{Q}

v_{\alpha}
in the lowest weight space of
V_{\alpha}
, and
v_{\alpha}^{*}
in the highest weight space of
V_{\alpha}^{*}

\omega_{\alpha}
on
\mathbf{G}

\omega_{\alpha}(g)=v_{\alpha}^{*}(gv_{\alpha}).

Since
V_{\alpha}
is a
\mathbb{Q}

\displaystyle\omega_{\alpha}(\Gamma)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}.

(3.1)

The following result must be well known, but we do not know of a reference for the statement in this generality.

Lemma 3.9

(cf.
[
6
, Lemma 4.11(i)]
)
.

We have
\Omega=\{g\in\mathbf{G}\,|\,\text{$\omega_{\alpha}(g)\neq 0$ for all~$\alpha$}\}
.

Proof.

For each simple root
\alpha
of
\Phi^{+}
, let
\pi^{-}_{\alpha}
and
\pi^{+}_{\alpha}
be the
\mathbf{T}
-invariant projections of
V_{\alpha}
onto its lowest weight space and its highest weight space, respectively. (So the kernel of
\pi^{\pm}_{\alpha}
is the sum of the other weight spaces.)

Let
g\in\Omega
, and let
\alpha
be any simple root of
\Phi^{+}
. Since the lowest weight space of
V_{\alpha}
is
\mathbf{P}^{-}
-invariant and
1
-dimensional, we have
p^{-}(g)v_{\alpha}=mv_{\alpha}
, for some nonzero scalar
m
. Also, since
u^{+}(g)\in\mathbf{U}^{+}
, we know that
\pi^{-}_{\alpha}\bigl(u^{+}(g)v_{\alpha}\bigr)=v_{\alpha}
. Putting these together, we see that

\pi^{-}_{\alpha}(gv_{\alpha})=\pi^{-}_{\alpha}\bigl(u^{+}(g)p^{-}(g)v_{\alpha}\bigr)=\pi^{-}_{\alpha}\bigl(mv_{\alpha}\bigr)=mv_{\alpha},

so
\omega_{\alpha}(g)=v_{\alpha}^{*}(gv_{\alpha})=mv_{\alpha}^{*}(v_{\alpha})\neq 0
.

Conversely, suppose
\omega_{\alpha}(g)\neq 0
, for all
\alpha
.
Then
\pi^{-}_{\alpha}(gv_{\alpha})\neq 0
.
Let
w
be the longest element of the
\mathbb{Q}
-Weyl group of
\mathbf{G}
. (This means that
w
sends every positive
\mathbb{Q}
-root to a negative
\mathbb{Q}
-root, and vice-versa.)
Then
w
interchanges the lowest weight with the highest weight, so
\pi^{+}_{\alpha}(wgv_{\alpha})\neq 0
.
By the Bruhat decomposition, we may write
wg=u^{-}w^{\prime}p^{-}
, for some
u^{-}\in\mathbf{U}^{-}
,
p^{-}\in\mathbf{P}^{-}
, and
w^{\prime}
in the
\mathbb{Q}
-Weyl group.
Then, since
\pi^{+}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{U}^{-}v)=\pi^{+}_{\alpha}(v)
, for all
v
, we have

\pi^{+}_{\alpha}(w^{\prime}p^{-}v_{\alpha})=\pi^{+}_{\alpha}(u^{-}w^{\prime}p^{-}v_{\alpha})=\pi^{+}_{\alpha}(wgv_{\alpha})\neq 0.

However, we also know that
p^{-}v_{\alpha}
is in the lowest weight space. Therefore,
w^{\prime}
must map the lowest weight to the highest weight. Since
-\lambda_{\alpha}
is the lowest weight and
w(-\lambda_{\alpha})
is the highest weight, this means
w^{\prime}(-\lambda_{\alpha})=w(-\lambda_{\alpha})
. Since this is true for all
\alpha
(and
\{-\lambda_{\alpha}\}
spans the dual of
\mathbf{T}
), we conclude that
w^{\prime}=w
. So

g=w^{-1}u^{-}w^{\prime}p^{-}=w^{-1}u^{-}wp^{-}\in w^{-1}\mathbf{U}^{-}w\mathbf{P}^{-}=\mathbf{U}^{+}\mathbf{P}^{-}=\Omega,

as desired.
∎

4
Proof of the main result

We will see that Theorem
1.2
is an easy consequence of the following lemma, which is a slight modification of a result of Lubotzky–Mozes–Raghunathan (and is proved by essentially the same argument).

Lemma 4.1

(cf.
[
6
, paragraphs 4.19, 4.21, and 4.22]
)
.

Let
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
, and let
F_{i}
be a finite subset of
\mathbf{U}^{+}(\mathbb{Q})
.
There is a finite subset
F_{i+1}
of
\mathbf{U}^{+}(\mathbb{Q})
, such that if
\gamma\in\Omega(\mathbb{Z})
, and
\overleftarrow{u^{+}_{i}}(\gamma)\in F_{i}
, then there exists
x_{i}\in{}^{\overleftarrow{u^{+}_{i}}(\gamma)}\mathbf{G}_{i}\cap\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Z})
, such that

1)

\overleftarrow{u^{+}_{i+1}}(x_{i}\gamma)\in F_{i+1}
, and

2)

\|x_{i}\|\prec\|\gamma\|
.

Proof.

For convenience, let

u=u^{+}(\gamma),\qquad\overleftarrow{u}=\overleftarrow{u^{+}_{i}}(\gamma),\qquad\widehat{u}=u^{+}_{i}(\gamma),\ \qquad\overrightarrow{u}=\overrightarrow{u^{+}_{i}}(\gamma),\qquad\text{and}\qquad p=p^{-}(\gamma).

Since
\overleftarrow{u}\in\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})
, we may fix a finite-index subgroup
\Gamma_{i}
of
\mathbf{G}_{i}(\mathbb{Z})
, such that
{}^{\overleftarrow{u}}\Gamma_{i}\subseteq\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Z})
.

We claim there is a finite subset
F^{0}
of
\mathbf{U}^{+}_{i}(\mathbb{Q})
, such that
\mathbf{G}_{i}(\mathbb{Q})=\Gamma_{i}F^{0}\mathbf{P}_{i}^{-}(\mathbb{Q})
. (This is implicit in the proof of
[
6
, Lemma 4.19]
, but, for completeness, we record the argument.) It is well known that there is a finite subset
F^{0}
of
\mathbf{G}_{i}(\mathbb{Q})
, such that
\mathbf{G}_{i}(\mathbb{Q})=\Gamma_{i}F^{0}\mathbf{P}_{i}^{-}(\mathbb{Q})

[
1
, Proposition 15.6]
. There is no harm in multiplying elements of
F^{0}
on the left by elements of
\Gamma_{i}
and on the right by elements of
\mathbf{P}_{i}^{-}(\mathbb{Q})
; we show that, by doing this, we may assume
F^{0}\subseteq\mathbf{U}^{+}_{i}(\mathbb{Q})
.
Note that the set
\mathbf{U}^{+}_{i}\mathbf{P}_{i}^{-}
is Zariski open in
\mathbf{G}_{i}
(“big cell”).
Also, for any
f\in F^{0}
, the Borel Density Theorem
[
8
, Theorem 4.10, p. 205]
implies that the set
\Gamma_{i}f
is Zariski dense in
\mathbf{G}_{i}
(because
\mathbf{G}_{i}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma_{i}
has finite volume
[
8
, Theorem 4.13, p. 213]
). These two sets must therefore intersect. Since the map
\mathbf{U}^{+}_{i}\times\mathbf{P}_{i}^{-}\to\mathbf{U}^{+}_{i}\mathbf{P}_{i}^{-}
is a biregular
\mathbb{Q}
-isomorphism
[
2
, Proposition 3.24, p. 84]
, this implies
(\Gamma_{i}f)\cap\bigl(\mathbf{U}^{+}_{i}(\mathbb{Q})\mathbf{P}_{i}^{-}(\mathbb{Q})\bigr)\neq\varnothing
. Hence, after multiplying
f
on the left by an appropriate element of
\Gamma_{i}
and on the right by an appropriate element of
\mathbf{P}_{i}^{-}(\mathbb{Q})
, we may assume
f\in\mathbf{U}^{+}_{i}
. This completes the proof of the claim.

Now, the claim tells us that we may write

\widehat{u}=xfq\qquad\text{with}\quad x\in\Gamma_{i},\quad f\in F^{0}\subset\mathbf{U}^{+}_{i}(\mathbb{Q}),\quad\text{and}\quad q\in\mathbf{P}_{i}^{-}(\mathbb{Q}).

Note that

It is now easy to prove the main theorem by using the argument (due to Lubotzky–Mozes–Raghunathan

:

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Recall that we may assume
\Gamma=\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Z})
(see Notation
3.5
(1)).
Thus, given
\gamma\in\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Z})
, we wish to show that
\gamma
can be written as an appropriate product
\gamma=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{r}
. Since
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Z})
is Zariski dense in
\mathbf{G}

[
8
, Theorem 4.10, p. 205]
and
\Omega
is Zariski open in
\mathbf{G}
, we have
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Z})\Omega=\mathbf{G}
. Then the ascending chain condition on Zariski-open subsets implies there is a finite subset
\Gamma_{1}
of
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Z})
, such that
\Gamma_{1}\Omega=\mathbf{G}
. This implies
\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}\Omega(\mathbb{Z})
, so, by adding the elements of
\Gamma_{1}
to
\Gamma_{0}
, we may assume
\gamma\in\Omega(\mathbb{Z})
.

Let
F_{1}=\{e\}\subset\mathbf{U}^{+}(\mathbb{Q})
. Repeated application of Lemma
4.1
yields elements
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}
of
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Z})
and finite subsets
F_{2},F_{3},\ldots,F_{k+1}
of
\mathbf{U}^{+}(\mathbb{Q})
, such that, for each

, we have

1)

\overleftarrow{u^{+}_{k+1}}(x_{i}x_{i-1}\cdots x_{1}\gamma)\in F_{i+1}
,

2)

x_{i}\in{}^{f}\mathbf{G}_{i}\cap\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Z})
, for some
f\in F_{i}
, and

3)

\|x_{i}\|\prec\gamma
for all

.

Letting
\gamma_{1}=x_{k}x_{k-1}\cdots x_{1}\gamma
, we see (from the case where

) that

is in a finite set, so there exists

in a finite subset
\Gamma_{0}
of
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Z})
, such that

is trivial, which means

.

Write

(Langlands decomposition). Multiplying

on the left by an element of

(and an element of a finite set) yields

is the product of a bounded number of root elements of controlled norm, and each root element is contained in a standard

-rank-one subgroup.) Then Lemma
3.7
(and induction on

∎

Remark 4.2
.

To eliminate the simplifying assumption Assumption
3.6
that
\mathbf{G}
is absolutely almost simple, one should modify the above argument to adhere a bit more closely to
[
6
]
, where there is no such assumption.
Begin by noting that
\mathbf{G}
is the restriction of scalars of some absolutely almost-simple group over a number field
K

[
2
, paragraph 6.21(ii), p. 113]
. Then replace
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{Z}
with
K
and
\mathcal{O}
(the ring of integers of
K
), and replace the norm
\|\gamma\|
with

\|\gamma\|_{\infty}=\max_{v\in S_{\infty}}\|\gamma\|_{v},\qquad\text{where}\quad\text{$S_{\infty}$ is the set of all archimedean places of~$K$.}

Also, as in
[
6
, p. 35]
, let

|t|^{*}=\prod\limits_{v\in S_{\infty}}|t|_{v}
, for
t\in K
.
(When
v
is a complex place,
|t|_{v}
denotes
t\cdot\overline{t}
, not
\sqrt{t\cdot\overline{t}}
.)
Thus,
|t|^{*}\in\mathbb{Z}
for
t\in\mathcal{O}
.

In the proof of Corollary
3.11
, note that
\omega_{\alpha}(\gamma)\in\mathcal{O}
(and
\omega_{\alpha}(\gamma)\neq 0
), so
|\omega_{\alpha}(\gamma)|^{*}\geq 1
; this implies
\max\limits_{v\in S_{\infty}}|1/\omega_{\alpha}(\gamma)|_{v}\prec\max\limits_{v\in S_{\infty}}|\omega_{\alpha}(\gamma)|_{v}
.

In the proof of Lemma
4.1
, instead of cocompact lattices in
{}^{\overleftarrow{u}}\mathbf{U}^{+}_{i}(\mathbb{R})
and
(\mathbf{M}_{i}\mathbf{N}_{i})(\mathbb{R})
, we have cocompact lattices in
\prod\limits_{v\in S_{\infty}}{}^{\overleftarrow{u}}\mathbf{U}^{+}_{i}(K_{v})
and
(\mathbf{P}_{i}^{-})_{S_{\infty}}^{(1)}
(see
[
8
, Theorem 5.7(2), p. 268]
and cf.
[
8
, Theorem 5.7, p. 264]
). With this in mind, we write a decomposition
q=ma
, where
m\in(\mathbf{P}_{i}^{-})_{S_{\infty}}^{(1)}
and where, after identifying the
1
-dimensional
K
-split torus
\mathbf{A}_{i}
with
\mathbb{G}_{m}
, we have
a\in\mathbb{G}_{m}(\mathbb{Q})=\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
. Note that
\omega_{\alpha}(a)\in\mathbb{Q}
(because every character of
\mathbb{G}_{m}
is defined over
\mathbb{Q}
), so

|\omega_{\alpha}(a)|\asymp|\omega_{\alpha}(a)|^{*}
.
Therefore, the above arguments show:

\displaystyle\|x\|_{\infty}\prec\|a\|\prec\max\bigl(|\omega_{\alpha}(a)|,\big|\omega_{\alpha}\big(a^{-1}\big)\big|\bigr),

\displaystyle\big|\omega_{\alpha}\big(a^{-1}\big)\big|\succ\big|\omega_{\alpha}\big(a^{-1}\big)\big|^{*}=|\omega_{\alpha}(xf)|^{*}>\frac{1}{O(1)},

\displaystyle\max\bigl(|\omega_{\alpha}(a)|,\big|\omega_{\alpha}\big(a^{-1}\big)\big|\bigr)=\max\bigl(O(1),\big|\omega_{\alpha}\big(a^{-1}\big)\big|\bigr),

\displaystyle\big|\omega_{\alpha}\big(a^{-1}\big)\big|\prec\big|\omega_{\alpha}\big(a^{-1}\big)\big|^{*}\prec\|\gamma\|_{\infty}.

These yield the required bound
\|x\|_{\infty}\prec\|\gamma\|_{\infty}
.

5
Generalization to
\boldsymbol{S}
-arithmetic groups

Theorem
1.2
is stated only for arithmetic subgroups of
\mathbf{G}
, but it generalizes in a natural way to the
S
-arithmetic setting:

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be an isotropic, almost-simple algebraic group over
\mathbb{Q}
, let
S
be a finite set of valuations of
\mathbb{Q}
that includes the archimedean valuation
\infty
, and let
\Gamma
be an
S
-arithmetic subgroup of
\mathbf{G}
. Then
\Gamma
is quasi-isometrically boundedly generated by standard
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-
1
subgroups.

In fact, the methods of Lubotzky–Mozes–Raghunathan
[
6
]
also apply in the following more general situation:

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be an isotropic, absolutely almost-simple algebraic group over a number field
K
, let
S
be a finite set of valuations of
K
that includes all of the archimedean valuations, and let
\Gamma
be an
S
-arithmetic subgroup of
\mathbf{G}
. Then
\Gamma
is quasi-isometrically boundedly generated by standard
K
-rank-
1
subgroups.

The statement of this proposition assumes that Definition
1.1
has been adapted to this situation by replacing
\mathbb{Q}
with
K
, replacing
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Z})
with
\mathbf{G}(\mathcal{O}_{S})
, where
\mathcal{O}_{S}
is the ring of
S
-integers of
K
, and replacing
\|\gamma\|
with

\|\gamma\|_{S}=\max_{v\in S}\|\gamma\|_{v}
.

Sketch of the proof of Proposition
5.2
.

The argument is essentially the same as the modification described in Remark
4.2
, except that it uses
S
and the ring
\mathcal{O}_{S}
of
S
-integers of
K
, in the place of
S_{\infty}
and
\mathcal{O}
. In particular, we let
|t|^{*}=\prod\limits_{v\in S}|t|_{v}
, so
|t|^{*}\in\mathbb{Z}
for
t\in\mathcal{O}_{S}
.

However,
x
is taken to be an element of
\mathbf{G}(\mathcal{O})
; this is a crucial instance where
\mathcal{O}
is not replaced with
\mathcal{O}_{S}
. This implies that
\|x\|_{v}
and
\big\|x^{-1}\big\|_{v}
are bounded, for all nonarchimedean

. Hence, although the goal is to show
\|x\|_{S}\prec\|\gamma\|_{S}
, it suffices to show
\|x\|_{\infty}\prec\|\gamma\|_{S}
.

Note that, after multiplying
a
by an element of
\mathbf{A}_{i}(\mathcal{O}_{S})=(\mathcal{O}_{S})^{\times}
(and multiplying
m
by the inverse of this element), we may assume that
\|a\|_{v}
and
\big\|a^{-1}\big\|_{v}
are bounded, for all nonarchimedean
v\in S
. Also, since
a\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
, we have
|\omega_{\alpha}(a)|_{v_{1}}=|\omega_{\alpha}(a)|_{v_{2}}
for all archimedean
v_{1}
,
v_{2}
. Therefore, we may write
|\omega_{\alpha}(a)|
, omitting the subscript. And then we have
|\omega_{\alpha}(a)|\asymp|\omega_{\alpha}(a)|^{*}
.
It is now easy to generalize the argument in the final paragraph of Remark
4.2
.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

The results of Lubotzky–Mozes–Raghunathan
[
6
]
are proved over all global fields, not only those of characteristic zero, but I do not have the expertise to speculate on whether there is a similar generalization of Proposition
5.2
.

6
Semisimple Lie groups with infinite center

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\widetilde{\Gamma}
be a noncocompact, irreducible lattice in a connected, semisimple Lie group
\widetilde{G}
that has no compact factors. (The center of
\widetilde{G}
may be infinite, but it is a well-known and easy consequence of the Borel density theorem that
\widetilde{\Gamma}
contains a finite-index subgroup of
Z\big(\widetilde{G}\big)
, so
\Ad_{\widetilde{G}}\widetilde{\Gamma}
is a lattice in
\Ad\widetilde{G}
.)

1.

If
\rank_{\mathbb{R}}\widetilde{G}=1
, then
\widetilde{G}
is the only
standard
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-
1
subgroup
of
\widetilde{G}
.

2.

If
\rank_{\mathbb{R}}\widetilde{G}\geq 2
, then the Margulis arithmeticity theorem implies that (up to finite index)
\Ad\widetilde{G}
can be viewed as the real points of an algebraic group over
\mathbb{Q}
, in such a way that
\Ad_{\widetilde{G}}\widetilde{\Gamma}
is commensurable to
\big(\Ad\widetilde{G}\big)_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Therefore, we can speak of standard
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-1 subgroups of
\Ad\widetilde{G}
. For any such subgroup
L
of
\Ad\widetilde{G}
, the identity component of the inverse image
\Ad_{\widetilde{G}}^{-1}L
is a
standard
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-
1
subgroup
of
\widetilde{G}
.

The main theorem
1.2
has the following consequence:

Corollary 6.2
.

If
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is a noncocompact, irreducible lattice in a connected, semisimple Lie group
\widetilde{G}
that has no compact factors, then
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is quasi-isometrically boundedly generated by standard
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-
1
subgroups.

More precisely, there exist constants
r=r\big(\widetilde{G},\widetilde{\Gamma}\big)\in\mathbb{N}
and
C=C\big(\widetilde{G},\widetilde{\Gamma}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, a finite subset
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{0}=\widetilde{\Gamma}_{0}\big(\widetilde{G},\widetilde{\Gamma}\big)
of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
, and a finite collection
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}\big(\widetilde{G},\widetilde{\Gamma}\big)
of standard
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-
1
subgroups of
\widetilde{G}
, such that every element
\gamma
of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
can be written in the form
\gamma=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{r}
, where, for each
i
, we have either:

1)

x_{i}\in L\cap\widetilde{\Gamma}
, for some
L\in\mathcal{L}
, and

,

2)

x_{i}\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{0}
.

If the center of
\widetilde{G}
is finite, then this is simply Corollary
1.4
(and is immediate from Theorem
1.2
). In the case where the center is infinite, the proof is completed by the following observation:

Lemma 6.3
.

There is a finite set
\mathcal{L}
of standard
\mathbb{Q}
-rank-
1
subgroups of
\widetilde{G}
, such that
\prod\limits_{L\in\mathcal{L}}\bigl(L\cap Z\big(\widetilde{G}\big)\bigr)

.

To prove this lemma, let us first make another definition:

Definition 6.4

A closed, connected, almost-simple subgroup
L
of a connected, semisimple Lie group
\widetilde{G}
is a

\mathbb{R}
-rank-

\alpha
of some maximal

-split torus

is generated by the root spaces

for

. (This implies that

If we choose a maximal
\mathbb{R}
-split torus that contains a maximal

-split torus, then every real root space is contained in either a
\mathbb{Q}
-root space or the
\mathbb{Q}
-anisotropic kernel. Since all maximal

-rank-1 subgroup is contained in a conjugate of a standard

Lemma 6.5

There is a finite set
\mathcal{L}

. Hence (as is well known),

\langle G_{\omega}\,|\,\text{$\omega$ is a white simple root}\rangle

is the entire semisimple part of
K
. Therefore, any root
\alpha
that is not in the subalgebra generated by the white simple roots must be noncompact. (This means
\theta(x)=-x
for all
x
in
\mathfrak{u}_{\alpha}^{\mathbb{C}}
, where
\theta
is the Cartan involution determined by
K
.) In particular, since the minimal root
\mu
is not a simple root, we know that
G_{\mu}
is noncompact, and therefore contains a nontrivial
\mathbb{R}
-split torus
S_{\mu}

[
4
, pp. 390–391]
.

A quick look at Table

\beta
is not joined by an edge to the minimal root

m\leq n
, so the black vertex is in the left half, but
m>1

G_{\beta}

\mathbb{R}
-split torus (namely
S_{\mu}
). This implies there is a proper parabolic
\mathbb{R}

P

G
, with Langlands decomposition
P=MAN
, such that
G_{\beta}\subseteq M
. Let
M^{\prime}
be the almost simple factor of
M^{\circ}
that contains
G_{\beta}
.

Let
\Delta
be the set of simple roots. (So
\Delta
is the set of nodes of the Vogan diagram.) It is clear that
T=\langle(G_{\delta}\cap T)^{\circ}\,|\,\delta\in\Delta\rangle
.
Also, since
Z(K)^{\circ}\subset T
, we know that the lift of
T
to
\widetilde{G}
contains an infinite subgroup of
Z\big(\widetilde{G}\big)
. Therefore, this must also be true of
(G_{\delta}\cap T)^{\circ}
, for some
\delta\in\Delta
. However, if
\delta
is a white root, then
G_{\delta}
is a compact simple group, so its universal cover has finite center. Hence, the lift of
G_{\beta}
(where
\beta
is the black simple root) must contain an infinite subgroup of
Z\big(\widetilde{G}\big)
. Since
G_{\beta}\subset M^{\prime}
, we conclude that the lift of
M^{\prime}
contains a subgroup of
Z\big(\widetilde{G}\big)
that is infinite, and therefore has finite index.

By induction on
\dim G
, there is a standard
\mathbb{R}
-rank-1 subgroup
L
of
M^{\prime}
, such that the lift of
L
to the universal cover
\widetilde{M^{\prime}}
of
M^{\prime}
contains a finite-index subgroup of
Z\big(\widetilde{M^{\prime}}\big)
. Then the lift of
L
to
\widetilde{G}
contains a finite-index subgroup of
Z\big(\widetilde{G}\big)
.
Furthermore, it is easy to see that
L
is a standard
\mathbb{R}
-rank-1 subgroup of
G
. (If
A^{\prime}

\mathbb{R}

M
