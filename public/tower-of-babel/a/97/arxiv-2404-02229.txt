C. Cabrelli, U. Molter, F. Negreira

Abstract
. This paper explores woven frames in separable Hilbert spaces with an initial focus on the finite-dimensional case. We begin by simplifying the problem to bases, for which we obtain a unique characterization. We establish a condition that is both necessary and sufficient for vector reconstruction, which is applicable to Fourier matrices. Furthermore, we show that these characterizations are still valid in the infinite dimensional case, for Riesz bases. Finally, we obtain several results for weaving Riesz bases of translations.

1.
Introduction

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be a separable Hilbert space and
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
a countable subset of vectors of
{\mathcal{H}}
. We say that
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a frame in
{\mathcal{H}}
if there exists constants
A,B>0
such that

A{\left\|{f}\right\|}^{2}\leqslant\sum_{i\in I}{\left|{{\left\langle{f,v_{i}}\right\rangle}}\right|}^{2}\leqslant B{\left\|{f}\right\|}^{2}

holds for all
f\in{\mathcal{H}}
.

Now for the same index set
I
consider another frame
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}
. We say that
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}
are
woven
if for every
J\subset I
the sequence
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in J}\cup\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I\setminus J}
is a frame of
{\mathcal{H}}
.
This notion naturally extends to the case of
n
frames, where partitions of the index set
I
into
n
subsets are considered.

Originally introduced in
[
2
]
, the motivation for studying woven frames stems from questions in distributed signal processing, with potential applications in areas such as wireless sensor networks and signal preprocessing. Subsequent research has broadened the concept, applying it to generalized frames such as for example Shauder bases
[
4
]
, g-frames
[
10
]
, fusion frames
[
7
]
and K-frames
[
8
]
and multi-window Gabor frames
[
9
]
.

In this note we focus first on woven finite frames (Section
2
), specifically examining the scenario with two frames. We limit our investigation to the case in which the frames are Riesz bases, exploiting the fact that in finite dimensions, every frame contains a basis.
Recall that a Riesz basis in a Hilbert space is the image of an orthonormal basis under a bounded, invertible operator.
Additionally, we restrict our study to woven frames ’up to permutation’, where two bases are considered woven if there exists a permutation of one that makes them woven.

Even in this seemingly straightforward case, the complexity of the problem becomes apparent, giving rise to a multitude of challenging aspects and numerous unresolved questions.

We provide various illustrative examples to shed light on the intricacies involved and comment on some of the inquiries that remain open.

We establish necessary and sufficient conditions, along with several sufficient conditions, for bases to be woven. We analyze the case of bases of complex exponentials and establish connections with the Fourier transform on finite groups. In particular, we prove that when
p
is prime we can always reconstruct a vector
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{p}
or its Fourier transform
\widehat{x},
from any weaving of the components of
x
and
\widehat{x}.
We also propose a conjecture regarding the minors of a Fourier matrix.
We extend our findings to the infinite-dimensional case in Section
3
, providing new necessary and sufficient conditions for woven Riesz bases in this context.

Finally, in Section
4
we consider the case of Riesz bases of translations in shift-invariant spaces (SIS). We study two problems. First, we give necessary and sufficient conditions
in order that two sets of Riesz generators of a SIS can be woven without losing the property of being a Riesz set of generators. For this characterization we
use the fiberization techniques of the range function associated to the SIS
[
3
]
.
In the second problem, we provide sufficient conditions for two Riesz bases of translates to be woven. As a particular example we consider the condition in a Paley-Wiener space.

Throughout this text we will always consider Hilbert spaces over
{\mathbb{C}}
as a field but all results -excepting the ones in
\lx@sectionsign
2.3
- are valid over
{\mathbb{R}}
as well.

In this article we will use a fact that is observed in
[
2
]
: The property of being woven is preserved by bounded invertible linear maps. This is, if
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
are Hilbert spaces and
T\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}}_{1},{\mathcal{H}}_{2})
is invertible then two frames in
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
are woven if and only if their images by
T
are woven in
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
.

2.
Finite case

As one might suspect, weaving frames are highly susceptible to order. For example if
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is an orthonormal basis then any of its non-trivial permutations are not woven with
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
(in fact not even complete). This motivates the following variation of the initial question: when are two given frames
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}
woven ‘up to permutations’? Since we have not found this definition elsewhere let us precise what we mean by this.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that two frames
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}
indexed by the same set
I
in the same Hilbert space are
woven up to permutations
, if there exists a permutation
\sigma
of the index set
I
, such that
\{v_{\sigma(i)}\}_{i\in I}
is woven with
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}
.

In finite dimensional spaces, frames are just generators of the space, so if
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{m}\}
and
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{m}\}
are frames in a vector space
V
of dimension
n
, then there exist two permutations
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
of
\{1,\dots,m\}
such that
\{v_{\sigma_{1}(1)},\dots,v_{\sigma_{1}(n)}\}
and
\{w_{\sigma_{2}(1)},\dots,w_{\sigma_{2}(n)}\}
are bases.

We have the immediate but surprising property.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let

\{v_{1},\dots,v_{m}\}
and
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{m}\}
be frames in a vector space
V
of dimension
n
, and let
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
be permutations of
\{1,\dots,m\}
such that
\{v_{\sigma_{1}(1)},\dots,v_{\sigma_{1}(n)}\}
and
\{w_{\sigma_{2}(1)},\dots,w_{\sigma_{2}(n)}\}
are bases.

If
\{v_{\sigma_{1}(1)},\dots,v_{\sigma_{1}(n)}\}
and
\{w_{\sigma_{2}(1)},\dots,w_{\sigma_{2}(n)}\}
are woven, then denoting by
\sigma:=\sigma_{1}^{-1}\sigma_{2}
we have that
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{m}\}
and
\{w_{\sigma(1)},\dots,w_{\sigma(m)}\}
are woven.

We do not know however if the converse statement is true, that is: given two finite frames which are woven, do they contain woven bases?

Also, let us remark at this point, that there exist bases for which no permutation makes them woven:

Example 2.3
.

In
{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
take
\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
to be the canonical basis and
\{e_{1}+e_{2},e_{2}+e_{3},e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3}\}
the other basis. There exists no permutation that makes these bases woven. Actually, since being woven is an invariant property under isomorphisms, in an arbitrary
3
-dimensional vector space
V

any
base
\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
is not woven with
\{v_{1}+v_{2},v_{2}+v_{3},v_{1}+v_{2}+v_{3}\}

This leads us to study when two bases in a finite dimensional space are woven. As we will see, this can be characterized through the change of bases matrix.

2.1.
A necessary and sufficient condition

Definition 2.4
.

Given two bases
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
and
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\}
in a finite dimensional real (or complex) vector space, we say that
A=(a_{i,j})^{n}_{i,j=1}
is the change of basis matrix from
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
to
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\}
if

A^{t}\left(\begin{array}[]{l}v_{1}\\
\hskip 1.99997pt\vdots\\
v_{n}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{l}w_{1}\\
\hskip 1.99997pt\vdots\\
w_{n}\end{array}\right)

in the sense that for each
i=1,\dots,n
we have

w_{i}=a_{1,i}v_{1}+\dots+a_{n,i}v_{n}.

Particularly, we will be looking at some sub-matrices of this matrix, which we call
central
.

Definition 2.5
.

The square
central
sub-matrices of a real or complex matrix
A=(a_{i,j})^{n}_{i,j=1}
are those that can be written as
(a_{j,k})_{j,k\in J}
where
J
is any subset of
\{1,\dots,n\}
.

We have the following theorem.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
V
be a vector space of finite dimension, Let
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
,
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\}
two bases of
V
and denote by
A
the change of basis matrix. Then
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
and
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\}
are woven if and only if all central sub-matrices of
A
are invertible.

Proof.

Define
T:V\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
as the isomorphism such that
Tv_{j}=e_{j}
for all
j=1,\dots,n
where

\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
is the canonical base of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.

As
A=(a_{i,j})^{n}_{i,j=1,\dots,n}
is the change of basis matrix from
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
to
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\}
then for each
i=1,\dots,n
we have

w_{i}=a_{1,i}v_{1}+\dots+a_{n,i}v_{n}.

Thus
Tw_{i}
is the
i^{th}
-column of
A
,
a_{i}=(a_{1,i},\dots,a_{n,i})
. Then, as being woven is invariant under isomorphisms,
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
and
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\}
are woven in
V
if and only if
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
and
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
are woven in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.

But
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
and
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
are woven if for any subset
J\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
the set
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in J}\cup\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I\setminus J}
is a basis. In other words, the matrix
A(J)
in which the columns of
A
indexed in
J
are changed by the corresponding canonical vectors, must be invertible.

Calculating the determinant using the expansion by precisely those rows indexed by elements in
J
we see that
\det(A(J))
is the determinant of the central sub-matrix of
A
,
(a_{i,j})_{i,j\in J}
. And all such determinants can be obtained by choosing an appropriate subset of
\{1,\dots,n\}
.
∎

2.2.
The class
{\mathcal{W}}

Definition 2.7
.

We define the class
{\mathcal{W}}
as the set of square matrices with entries in
{\mathbb{C}}
such that all its central sub-matrices are invertible. The subclass
{\mathcal{W}}_{n}
are the elements of
{\mathcal{W}}
of size
n\times n
.

Corollary 2.8
.

Assume that
A\in{\mathcal{W}}_{n}
and let
J\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then any
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
can be recovered from the samples
Y_{J}(x)=\{(Ax)(j)\}_{j\in J}\cup\{x(j)\}_{j\in I\setminus J}
(i.e. the map
x\mapsto Y_{J}(x)
is injective.) Moreover, the converse result is also true, this is, if for a given
A\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
the map
x\mapsto Y_{J}(x)
is injective for all
J\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
then
A\in{\mathcal{W}}_{n}
.

Proof.

Given
J\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
let
A(J)
be the matrix whose
j^{\rm th}
-row is equal to
Ae_{j}
when
j\in J
and
e_{j}
when
j\in I\setminus J
. Thus,
Y_{J}(x)=A(J)x
.

Assume now
A=(a_{i,j})_{i,j=1}^{n}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{n}
. Then, as
\det A(J)=\det(a_{i,j})_{i,j\in J}
,
A(J)
is invertible and we can therefore write
x=A(J)^{-1}(A(J)(x))
.

Conversely, if
x\mapsto Y_{J}(x)
is injective then multiplication by
A(J)
is also an injective map. Furthermore, as
A(J)
is a square matrix, then it must be invertible. Thus
\det(a_{i,j})_{i,j\in J}=\det A(J)\neq 0
.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

More generally, if
A
and
B
are invertible matrices in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, then the rows of
A
are woven with the rows of
B
if and only if for any
J\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
any vector
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
can be uniquely recovered from
\{(Ax)(j):j\in J\}\cup\{(Bx)(j):j\in I\setminus J\}
. This can also be seen by applying the generalized Cramer’s rule, see e.g.
[
1
]
.

Just like with other properties, it is natural to wonder how often we come across matrices in
{\mathcal{W}}
? Or, more rigorously, is the set
{\mathcal{W}}
generic in some sense (topologically, probabilistically)? In fact, since the determinant is a polynomial function on the entries of a matrix, matrices in
{\mathcal{W}}_{n}
are in the complement of a manifold of dimension strictly lower than
n\times n
. This is, a Zariski open set, see e.g.
[
11
]
.

Proposition 2.10
.

Given a natural number
n
, the set of matrices in
{\mathcal{W}}_{n}
is a Zariski (dense) open set within the matrices in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
.

Proof.

For each
J\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
consider the function
P_{J}:{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
given by
P_{J}A=\det(a_{j,k})_{j,k\in J}
where
A=(a_{j,k})_{j,k=1}^{n}
. Clearly,
P_{J}
is a polynomial on
n\times n
variables and, by definition,
{\mathcal{W}}_{n}^{c}=\cup_{J}P_{J}^{-1}(0)
. This means that
{\mathcal{W}}_{n}^{c}
is a Zariski closed set.
∎

Viewed this way, the subclass
{\mathcal{W}}_{n}
is a huge subset of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
. Finding a structure for this set, however, can be tricky. It is not a vector space with the usual sum since clearly
0\notin{\mathcal{W}}_{n}
, nor is it closed by multiplication e.g.
A=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}1&-1\\
1&1\end{array}\right)\in{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
but
A^{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}0&-2\\
2&0\end{array}\right)\notin{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
. It has, nonetheless, some symmetries and other properties that we list below.

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
A
be a complex square matrix.

(1)

A\in{\mathcal{W}}
if and only if
A
is invertible and
A^{-1}\in{\mathcal{W}}
.

(2)

A\in{\mathcal{W}}\Leftrightarrow A^{t}\in{\mathcal{W}}\Leftrightarrow A^{*}\in{\mathcal{W}}
.

(3)

If
A\in{\mathcal{W}}_{n}
and
B\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
is either a diagonal invertible or a permutation matrix then
B^{*}AB\in{\mathcal{W}}_{n}
.

(4)

If
A
is symmetric and either positive definite or negative definite, then
A\in{\mathcal{W}}
.

(5)

If
A
is lower or upper triangular and invertible then
A\in{\mathcal{W}}
.

(6)

If
A\in{\mathcal{W}}
and
B
is a central sub-matrix of
A
, then
B
is in
{\mathcal{W}}
.

Proof.

(1).
From Theorem
2.6
, we know that
A\in{\mathcal{W}}
if and only if
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
and
\{Ae_{1},\dots,Ae_{n}\}
are woven bases. And using
A^{-1}
as an isomorphism we have that
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
and
\{Ae_{1},\dots,Ae_{n}\}
are woven if and only if

\{A^{-1}e_{1},\dots,A^{-1}e_{n}\}\text{ and }\{A^{-1}Ae_{1},\dots,A^{-1}Ae_{n}\}=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}

are woven bases, which then again by Theorem
2.6
is equivalent to
A^{-1}\in{\mathcal{W}}
.

(2).
This follows from the fact that central sub-matrices of
A^{t}
and
A^{*}
are just the transpose and adjoint respectively of those in
A
.

(3).
A central sub-matrix of
B^{*}AB
for
B
a diagonal invertible matrix, is a central sub-matrix of
A
with rows and columns multiplied by non-zero complex numbers. The result then follows from the linearity of the determinant.

As for permutation matrices, it is enough to show the invariance under a single permutation since the set of matrices that fix property under conjugation
{\mathcal{W}}
is a group. Consider then
R_{i,j}
the matrix that switches the
i^{\rm th}
-row with the
j^{\rm th}
-row and define
\widetilde{A}:=(R_{i,j})^{*}AR_{i,j}
. Then
\widetilde{A}=R_{i,j}AR_{i,j}
is the matrix constructed by first switching rows
i
and
j
, and then switching columns
i
and
j
. Take now central sub-matrix
\widetilde{A}_{S}
of
\widetilde{A}
,
S\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
. If
a_{i,i}
and
a_{j,j}
are not entries in
\widetilde{A}_{S}
then neither the rows nor the columns
i,j
are in
\widetilde{A}_{S}
and so
\widetilde{A}_{S}=A_{S}
the corresponding central sub-matrix in
A
. If both
a_{i,i}
and
a_{j,j}
are entries in
\widetilde{A}_{S}
then
\widetilde{A}_{S}
is a matrix obtained by row and column permutations of
A_{S}
and so
\det(\widetilde{A}_{S})=\det(A_{S})
. If just one of either
a_{i,i}
, or
a_{j,j}
is an entry in
\widetilde{A}_{S}
, say
a_{i,i}
, then
\widetilde{A}_{S}
is a matrix obtained by row and column permutations of
A_{S^{\prime}}
the square sub-matrix of
A
with
S^{\prime}=S\setminus\{i\}\cup\{j\}
.

(4).
If
A\notin{\mathcal{W}}_{n}
then there exist a central sub-matrix
A_{J}
which is not invertible. This means that there exist a non-trivial vector
y\in\ker A_{J}
. Define now
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
with
x_{i}=0
for all
i\notin J
and
x_{i}=y_{i}
for
i\in J
. Thus
x^{*}Ax=0
and
x\neq 0.
In particular
A
cannot be symmetric definite.

(5).
This follows from the fact that central sub-matrices of a triangular matrix are triangular, and they are invertible if and only if all its diagonal entries are non-zeros.

(6).
This is straightforward.
∎

We conjecture that the matrices
B\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
listed in
(3)
are the only ones that leave the subclass
{\mathcal{W}}_{n}
invariant under conjugation. Arbitrary unitary matrices, for example, can be outside this set. Indeed, let
A=R_{1,2}I\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
be the matrix consisting of switching the first two rows of the identity. Then
A\notin{\mathcal{W}}_{n}
but since
A
is unitary and invertible there exists another unitary matrix
U
such that
U^{*}AU
is diagonal and invertible and thus belongs to
{\mathcal{W}}_{n}
.

In general, permutation by columns or rows of matrix in
{\mathcal{W}}
needs not to remain in
{\mathcal{W}}
. Actually for this to happen all the minors of the matrix must be non-zero.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
A
be a square complex matrix. Then all column permutations of
A
belong to
{\mathcal{W}}
if and only if all the minors of
A
are non-zero.

Proof.

Assume first that
A=(a_{i,j})_{i,j=1}^{n}
and all its permutations by columns are in
{\mathcal{W}}
, and take
M=(a_{i_{s},j_{s}})_{s=1}^{k}
a square sub-matrix of
A
. Let
P_{i_{s},j_{s}}
be the permutation that switches the
i_{s}
-column with the
j_{s}
-column, and define
P:=P_{i_{1},j_{1}}\dots P_{i_{k},j_{k}}
. Then
M
is a central sub-matrix of
PA
. Indeed, the entry in
(i_{1},i_{1})
of
PA
is
a_{i_{1},j_{1}}
and the entry in
(i_{k},i_{k})
is
a_{i_{k},j_{k}}
. Thus, as
PA\in{\mathcal{W}}
,
\det M\neq 0
.

Conversely, suppose now that all minors of
A
are non-zero and take
P
a permutation column matrix. Then, central sub-matrices of
PA
are just permutations of square sub-matrices of
A
. Therefore
PA\in{\mathcal{W}}
.
∎

2.3.
Fourier matrices

Fourier matrices are good candidates to belong to the class
{\mathcal{W}}
. Recall that, given a natural number
n
, the Fourier matrix of order
n
is defined by
F_{n}=\{e^{2\pi ijk/n}\}_{k,j=0}^{n-1}
. It is known for example that
all
square sub-matrices of
F_{n}
are invertible if and only if
n
is a prime number (see e.g.
[
12
]
and the references therein). This means that
F_{n}
satisfies Proposition
2.12
if and only if
n
is prime. In particular this means that for
n
prime,
F_{n}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{n}
. In this subsection we try to identify if there are other natural numbers
n
for which
F_{n}
is in
{\mathcal{W}}_{n}
.

To begin with, to see whether a Fourier matrix is in
{\mathcal{W}}
it suffices to check the invertibility of a subset of its central sub-matrices.

Proposition 2.13
.

The Fourier matrix
F_{n}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{n}
if and only if all its central sub-matrices
(F_{n})_{J}
with
1\in J
are invertible.

Proof.

Let
\zeta=e^{2\pi i/n}
. Then
F_{n}=(\zeta^{j\ell})_{\ell,j=0}^{n-1}
. For
0\leqslant j_{0}<\dots<j_{k}<n
, we consider the following central
k+1\times k+1
sub-matrix of
F_{n}
:

\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}\zeta^{j_{0}^{2}}&\dots&\zeta^{j_{0}j_{k}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\zeta^{j_{k}j_{0}}&\dots&\zeta^{j_{k}^{2}}\end{array}\right)

Multiplying each row by the inverse of its first element, by linearity of the determinant with respect to rows, we obtain

\det\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}\zeta^{j_{0}^{2}}&\dots&\zeta^{j_{0}j_{k}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\zeta^{j_{k}j_{0}}&\dots&\zeta^{j_{k}^{2}}\end{array}\right)=\prod_{s=0}^{k}\zeta^{-j_{0}j_{s}}\det\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}1&\dots&\zeta^{j_{0}(j_{k}-j_{0})}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\dots&\zeta^{j_{k}(j_{k}-j_{0})}\end{array}\right).

Now, multiplying the resulting
k+1
-columns by the inverse of the top element we have

\det\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}1&\dots&\zeta^{j_{0}(j_{k}-j_{0})}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\dots&\zeta^{j_{k}(j_{k}-j_{0})}\end{array}\right)=\prod_{s=0}^{k}\zeta^{-j_{0}(j_{s}-j_{0})}\det\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}1&\dots&1\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\dots&\zeta^{(j_{k}-j_{0})^{2}}\end{array}\right).

So that if
l_{s}:=j_{s}-j_{0},s=0,\dots,k
, the entries of this last matrix are
(\zeta^{l_{s}l_{r}})_{r,s=1}^{k}
which is a central sub-matrix of
F_{n}
with its first entry
1
since
0=l_{0}<\dots<l_{k}<n
.
∎

Corollary 2.14
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
be a natural number. Then all central
2\times 2
sub-matrices of
F_{n}
are invertible if and only if
n
is square free.

Proof.

By the argument in the proof of
Proposition
2.13
we know that any central sub-matrix of
F_{n}
is invertible if and only if there exists an invertible central sub-matrix of the same order whose set of indexes contain 1.
As a consequence of that and the fact that the elements in the diagonal of
F_{n}
are of the form
\zeta^{j^{2}}
for
j=0,\dots,n-1
, we only need to look at the case when the considered central sub-matrix is of the form,

\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}1&1\\
1&\zeta^{j^{2}}\end{array}\right)\quad\text{ for some $0<j<n$.}

The determinant of this sub-matrix is
\zeta^{j^{2}}-1
and thus it is invertible if and only if
\zeta^{j^{2}}\neq 1
. As
\zeta=e^{2\pi i/n}
, then
\zeta^{j^{2}}=1
if and only if
n
divides
j^{2}
i.e.
j^{2}=kn
.

Suppose first that
n
is square free and let
n=p_{1}\dots p_{m}
be its decomposition into primes with
p_{r}\neq p_{s}
for
r\neq s
. If
n
divides
j^{2}
then
p_{r}
divides
j^{2}
for
1\leqslant r\leqslant m
. But then
p_{r}
divides
j
for
1\leqslant r\leqslant m
, so that
n
divides
j
which is impossible since
0<j<n
.

If
n
is not square free then we can decompose it as
n=p^{2}q
with
p
prime. Take
j=pq
. Then
0<j<n
and
n
divides
j^{2}
, so that there exists a
2\times 2
central sub-matrix of
F_{n}
which is not invertible.
∎

Corollary 2.15
.

If
n\geqslant 2
is a natural number such that
F_{n}\in{\mathcal{W}}
then
n
is square free.

That is, for
n
to be square-free is a necessary condition for
F_{n}\in{\mathcal{W}}
.

We conjecture that the converse result of Corollary
2.15
is also true. i.e.

Conjecture 2.16
.

If
n
is square free then
F_{n}\in{\mathcal{W}}
. That is, if
n
is square free then every central submatrix of
F_{n}
is invertible.

This would make for a larger subset than only prime numbers to
F_{n}
belong to
{\mathcal{W}}
.

Remark 2.17
.

Let
\widehat{x}
denote the discrete Fourier transform of
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, that is
\widehat{x}=F_{n}x
. Applying Corollary
2.8
for the case of Fourier matrices which are in
{\mathcal{W}}
reads as follows: if
n
is such that
F_{n}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{n}
then for any
J\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
and any
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
we can reconstruct
x
from the measurements
\{\widehat{x}(j)\}_{j\in J}\cup\{x(j)\}_{j\in I\setminus J}
.

3.
Infinite case, Riesz basis

We move now to the case of Riesz bases of infinite-dimensional separable Hilbert spaces. Let us first recall the definition of a Riesz basis: in a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
we say that a countable subset of vectors
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a
Riesz sequence
if there exists constants
A,B>0
such that

A{\left\|{c}\right\|}_{2}\leqslant{\left\|{\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}v_{i}}\right\|}\leqslant B{\left\|{c}\right\|}_{2}

holds for all finite sequences of coefficients
c=(c_{i})_{i\in I}
. We say that
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a
Riesz basis
if in addition it is a frame.

A result in
[
2
, Theorem 5.3]
shows that if two Riesz bases
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}
are woven frames then every ‘weaving choice’
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in J}\cup\{w_{i}\}_{i\in J^{c}}
is actually a Riesz basis.

We choose Riesz bases because they are the generalization that best fits the results of the previous section. Indeed, if
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}
are two Riesz basis in a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
then there is an isomorphism
T:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}
that sends
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
into
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}
. Going down to
\ell^{2}(I)
via the isomorphism
D_{v}:{\mathcal{H}}\to\ell^{2}(I)
that sends
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
to the canonical basis
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
then gives an isomorphism
A^{t}:\ell^{2}(I)\to\ell^{2}(I)
. In other words, we have the following commuting diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{H}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{H}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ell^{2}(I)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ell^{2}(I)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D_{v}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D_{v}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A^{t}}

Finally, writing
a_{i,j}={\left\langle{e_{i},A^{t}e_{j}}\right\rangle}
allows to define the change of basis matrix in this context.

Definition 3.1
.

Given
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}
as before we say that
A=(a_{i,j})_{i,j\in I}
is the change of basis matrix from
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
to
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}
if

A^{t}\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}=\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}

in the sense that for each
i\in I
we have

w_{i}=\sum_{j\in I}a_{j,i}v_{j}.

As before, the characterization of woven bases will come from the central sub-matrices of the change of basis matrix.

Definition 3.2
.

As before the square
central
sub-matrices of the complex matrix
A=(a_{i,j})_{i,j\in I}
are those sub-matrices that can be written as
A_{J}:=(a_{i,j})_{i,j\in J},
where
J
is any subset of
I
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be a Hilbert space. Let
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
,
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be two Riesz bases of
{\mathcal{H}}
and denote by
A
the change of basis matrix. Then
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}
are woven if and only if all central sub-matrices of
A
define uniformly bounded invertible operators, i.e. there exist constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that
C_{1}{\left\|{\alpha}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}^{2}\leqslant{\left\|{A_{J}\alpha}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}^{2}\leqslant C_{2}{\left\|{\alpha}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}^{2}
, for any
J\subset I
and
\alpha\in\ell^{2}(J)
.

Proof.

First, note that the isomorphism
D_{v}:{\mathcal{H}}\to\ell^{2}(I)
that sends
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
into the canonical basis
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, also sends
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}
into the columns of
A
. Indeed,
as

w_{i}=\sum_{j\in I}a_{j,i}v_{j}\quad\text{ for all $i\in I$},

and then

D_{v}w_{i}=\sum_{j\in I}a_{j,i}Dv_{j}=\sum_{j\in I}a_{j,i}e_{j}=:a_{i},

where
a_{i}
denotes the
i^{\rm th}
-column of
A
. Thus, using
D_{v}
as an isomorphism, we have that
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}
are woven if and only if
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I}
are woven. We can then work in
\ell^{2}(I)
in the sense that to prove the theorem it is enough to show that
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I}
are woven if and only if the central sub-matrices of
A
are uniformly bounded invertible operators.

Given a subset
J\subset I
we will denote by
E_{J}:\ell^{2}(J)\to\ell^{2}(I)
the immersion given by

E_{J}(\alpha)_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\alpha_{i}&\text{if }i\in J\\
0&\text{if }i\notin J.\end{array}\right.

Then the adjoint operator,
E_{J}^{*}:\ell^{2}(I)\to\ell^{2}(J)
is the restriction to the indexes of
J
. Further,
P_{J}:=E_{J}E_{J}^{*}:\ell^{2}(I)\to\ell^{2}(I)
is the projection onto the image of
E_{J}
.
Finally, note that if
A_{J}=(a_{i,j})_{i,j\in J}
is a central sub-matrix and
\alpha\in\ell^{2}(J)
then

A_{J}(\alpha)=E_{J}^{*}AE_{J}(\alpha)

whenever
A_{J}(\alpha)
is defined.

Assume first that
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I}
are woven and fix
J\subset I
. As
A
defines a bounded operator in
\ell^{2}(I)
we have that for any
\alpha\in\ell^{2}(J)

\displaystyle{\left\|{A_{J}(\alpha)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}={\left\|{E_{J}^{*}AE_{J}(\alpha)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}\leqslant{\left\|{AE_{J}(\alpha)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}

\displaystyle\leqslant{\left\|{A}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)\to\ell^{2}(I)}{\left\|{E_{J}(\alpha)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}

\displaystyle\leqslant{\left\|{A}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)\to\ell^{2}(I)}{\left\|{\alpha}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}.

Therefore,
A_{J}:\ell^{2}(J)\to\ell^{2}(J)
is a bounded operator and its norm is controlled by an independent constant. Thus, we are left to show that
A_{J}
is injective and uniformly bounded from below.

Let
\alpha\in\ell^{2}(J)
and note that

{\left\|{A_{J}(\alpha)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}={\left\|{E_{J}^{*}AE_{J}(\alpha)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}={\left\|{E_{J}E_{J}^{*}AE_{J}(\alpha)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}={\left\|{\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}E_{J}E_{J}^{*}(a_{i})}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)},

where the second inequality holds because
E_{J}:\ell^{2}(J)\to\ell^{2}(I)
is an immersion. Next notice that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}E_{J}E_{J}^{*}(a_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}P_{J}(a_{i})=\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}(a_{i}-P_{I\setminus J}(a_{i}))=\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}a_{i}-\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}P_{I\setminus J}(a_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}a_{i}-P_{I\setminus J}\left(\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}a_{i}\right)=\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}a_{i}-\sum_{j\in I\setminus J}\left(\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}a_{j,i}\right)e_{j}.

Defining
\gamma\in\ell^{2}(I)
as

\gamma_{i}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\alpha_{i}&i\in J\\
-\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}a_{j,i}&i\in I\setminus J,\end{array}\right.

the previous computation yields

{\left\|{A_{J}(\alpha)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}={\left\|{\sum_{i\in J}\gamma_{i}a_{i}+\sum_{i\in I\setminus J}\gamma_{i}e_{i}}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}.

Now, as
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I}
are woven then there exists an independent constant
C>0
such that

{\left\|{\sum_{i\in J}\gamma_{i}a_{i}+\sum_{i\in I\setminus J}\gamma_{i}e_{i}}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}\geqslant C{\left\|{\gamma}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}.

On the other hand

{\left\|{\gamma}\right\|}^{2}_{\ell^{2}(I)}\geqslant\sum_{i\in J}{\left|{\gamma_{i}}\right|}^{2}=\sum_{i\in J}{\left|{\alpha_{i}}\right|}^{2}={\left\|{\alpha}\right\|}^{2}_{\ell^{2}(J)},

and thus in conclusion,

{\left\|{A_{J}(\alpha)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}\geqslant C{\left\|{\alpha}\right\|}^{2}_{\ell^{2}(J)},

which is what we wanted to prove.

For the other implication, assume now that all central sub-matrices of
A
define uniformly bounded invertible operators. We want to show that
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I}
are woven. From
[
2
, Theorem 5.2]
we know that it is enough to show that for any
J\subset I
the set
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in J}\cup\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I\setminus J}
is a Riesz sequence with uniform bounds independent of
J
.

Since
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I}
are Riesz bases in
\ell^{2}(I)
there exist some constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle{\left\|{\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}a_{i}+\sum_{i\in I\setminus J}\alpha_{i}e_{i}}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}\leqslant{\left\|{\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}a_{i}}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}+{\left\|{\sum_{i\in I\setminus J}\alpha_{i}e_{i}}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{1}{\left\|{\alpha}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}+C_{2}{\left\|{\alpha}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}

\displaystyle\leqslant(C_{1}+C_{2}){\left\|{\alpha}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}

holds for all
\alpha\in\ell^{2}(I)
and all
J\subset I
. Therefore, we only need to show the existence of a lower bound
C_{3}>0
such that

C_{3}{\left\|{\alpha}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}\leqslant{\left\|{\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}a_{i}+\sum_{i\in I\setminus J}\alpha_{i}e_{i}}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}

holds for all
\alpha\in\ell^{2}(I)
and all
J\subset I
.

We proceed by contradiction. Suppose that for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\alpha\in\ell^{2}(I)
and
J\subset I
with
{\left\|{\alpha}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}=1
and such that

{\left\|{\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}a_{i}+\sum_{i\in I\setminus J}\alpha_{i}e_{i}}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}\leqslant\varepsilon.

Let
\rho:=\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}a_{i}+\sum_{i\in I\setminus J}\alpha_{i}e_{i}
. Using the projections defined before, we will now estimate
{\left\|{P_{J}(\rho)}\right\|}
and
{\left\|{P_{I\setminus J}(\rho)}\right\|}
to yield the desired contradiction. First note that projecting gives

{\left\|{P_{J}(\rho)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}\leqslant{\left\|{\rho}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}\leqslant\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad{\left\|{P_{I\setminus J}(\rho)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}\leqslant{\left\|{\rho}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}\leqslant\varepsilon.

Now, for each
i\in J
let
b_{i}\in\ell^{2}(J)
be defined by
b_{i}:=(E_{J}^{*})a_{i}
. Also let
\beta\in\ell^{2}(J)
given by
\beta:=(E_{J}^{*})\alpha
. Thus

\displaystyle{\left\|{P_{J}(\rho)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}

\displaystyle={\left\|{P_{J}\left(\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}a_{i}+\sum_{i\in I\setminus J}\alpha_{i}e_{i}\right)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}={\left\|{\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}P_{J}(a_{i})}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}={\left\|{\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}E_{J}E_{J}^{*}(a_{i})}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}

\displaystyle={\left\|{\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}E_{J}(b_{i})}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}={\left\|{\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}b_{i}}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}={\left\|{\sum_{i\in J}E_{J}^{*}(\alpha_{i}b_{i})}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}={\left\|{A_{J}(\beta)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}.

Since
A_{J}
is uniformly bounded from below there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
J
and such that
C{\left\|{\beta}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}\leqslant{\left\|{A_{J}(\beta)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}
. Altogether this yields

C{\left\|{\beta}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}\leqslant{\left\|{A_{J}(\beta)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}={\left\|{P_{J}(\rho)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}\leqslant{\left\|{\rho}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}\leqslant\varepsilon.

(1)

Next, we look at the projection onto
I\setminus J
:

\displaystyle{\left\|{P_{I\setminus J}(\rho)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}

\displaystyle={\left\|{P_{I\setminus J}\left(\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}a_{i}\right)+\sum_{i\in I\setminus J}\alpha_{i}e_{i}}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}

\displaystyle\geqslant{\left\|{\sum_{i\in I\setminus J}\alpha_{i}e_{i}}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}-{\left\|{P_{I\setminus J}\left(\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}a_{i}\right)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}.

(2)

For the first term, noting that
{\left\|{P_{J}(\alpha)}\right\|}^{2}_{\ell^{2}(I)}={\left\|{E_{J}^{*}(\alpha)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}^{2}={\left\|{\beta}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle{\left\|{\sum_{i\in I\setminus J}\alpha_{i}e_{i}}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}

\displaystyle={\left\|{P_{I\setminus J}(\alpha)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}={\left\|{\alpha-(P_{J})\alpha}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{{\left\|{\alpha}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}^{2}-{\left\|{P_{J}(\alpha)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}^{2}}=\sqrt{1-{\left\|{\beta}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}^{2}}

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-{\left\|{\beta}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)},

since
{\left\|{\beta}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}\leqslant{\left\|{\alpha}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}=1
.

For the second term we have

{\left\|{P_{I\setminus J}\left(\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}a_{i}\right)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}\hskip-3.00003pt\leqslant{\left\|{\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}a_{i}}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}\hskip-3.00003pt={\left\|{\sum_{i\in I}P_{J}(\alpha)_{i}a_{i}}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}\hskip-3.00003pt\leqslant C_{1}{\left\|{P_{J}(\alpha)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}=C_{1}{\left\|{\beta}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)},

where, as before,
C_{1}
denotes the upper bound for the Riesz basis
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I}
. Finally, plugging in the estimates for both terms in (
2
) we have

1-(1+C_{1}){\left\|{\beta}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(J)}\leqslant{\left\|{P_{I\setminus J}(\rho)}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}\leqslant{\left\|{\rho}\right\|}_{\ell^{2}(I)}\leqslant\varepsilon.

(3)

Combining now (
1
) and (
3
) we have

1\leqslant\left(1+\frac{1+C_{1}}{C}\right)\varepsilon,

which, letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, gives a contradiction. Therefore
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in J}\cup\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I\setminus J}
must be a Riesz sequence with bounds independent of
J
. This completes the proof.
∎

Definition 3.4
.

Similar to the finite dimensional case we define the class
{\mathcal{W}}
as the set of matrices in
\ell^{2}(I)
such that all its central sub-matrices are invertible.

Therefore, in view of Theorem
3.3
, we have that two Riesz bases
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
,
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}
in a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
are woven if and only if its change of basis matrix
A
belongs to the class
{\mathcal{W}}
.

This result is stronger than the one given in
[
2
, Proposition 7.1]
which says that if
\{v_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a Riesz basis in
\ell^{2}(I)
and its Grammian (change of basis with the canonical basis) can be written as
D+R
where
D
is diagonal and
2{\left\|{R}\right\|}\leqslant\sup_{n}{\left|{d_{n,n}}\right|}
then
\{v_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is woven with
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
.

Indeed, one can construct an example which satisfies the hypothesis of Theorem
3.3
but not the ones of
[
2
, Proposition 7.1]
: consider the set
\{v_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
given by
v_{1}=e_{1}+2e_{2}
,
v_{2}=2e_{1}+e_{2}
and
v_{n}=e_{n}
for
n\geqslant 3
. Then clearly
\{v_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a Riesz basis and the change of basis matrix from the canonical basis
\{e_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is given by
the Gramian

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{lllll}1&2&0&\dots&\dots\\
2&1&0&\dots&\dots\\
0&0&1&0&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots&0&\ddots&\ddots\end{array}\right)

The central matrices of
A
are either the identity in
\ell^{2}
or
A
itself which are both invertible. Thus
\{v_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is woven with the canonical basis
\{e_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.

On the other hand when we write
A=D+R
with
D
diagonal we obtain that
D=Id
and
Re_{2}=2e_{1}
so that
2{\left\|{R}\right\|}>\sup_{n}{\left|{d_{n,n}}\right|}
.

As in the finite case, there is a description of the class
{\mathcal{W}}
in terms of a reconstruction result.

Proposition 3.5
.

Assume that
A\in{\mathcal{W}}
and let
J\subset I
. Then any
x\in\ell^{2}(I)
can be recovered from the samples
Y_{J}(x)=\{(Ax)(j)\}_{j\in J}\cup\{x(j)\}_{j\in I\setminus J}
. Conversely, if
A
is a matrix in
I
and the maps
x\mapsto Y_{J}(x)
are injective and uniformly bounded for all
J\subset I
then
A\in{\mathcal{W}}
.

Proof.

If
A
is a bounded matrix in
{\mathcal{W}}
and
J\subset I
, then the matrix
A(J)
defined for
j\in I
, whose
j^{\rm th}
-row is equal to
Ae_{j}
when
j\in J
and
e_{j}
when
j\in I\setminus J
defines a bounded operator in
\ell^{2}(I)
with
A(J)x=Y_{J}(x)
.

Further, when
A\in{\mathcal{W}}
, its columns
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I}
form a woven Riesz basis with the canonical orthonormal basis
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, as
A
is an invertible matrix that represents the change of bases matrix between
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I}
. Thus, in such case, the columns of
A(J)
form a Riesz basis and in particular
A(J)
is invertible so that
x=A(J)^{-1}(A(J)(x))
for all
x\in\ell^{2}(I)
.

Conversely, if
x\mapsto Y_{J}(x)
defines a bounded injective operator in
\ell^{2}(I)
then multiplication by
A(J)
is also a bounded injective operator in
\ell^{2}(I)
. If the lower and upper bounds of
Y_{J}
are independent of
J
, then the bounds of
A(J)
are independent of
J
and therefore its columns form a Riesz sequence with uniform bounds. But from
[
2
, Theorem 5.2]
this is enough to claim that
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I}
are woven and thus its change of basis
A
is in the class
{\mathcal{W}}
.
∎

Since any Hilbert space can be isomorphically linked to an
\ell^{2}(I)
space through any of its Riesz bases, we can also adapt definition of woven with respect to a given basis.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
T:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}
a bounded operator on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
and let
v=\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a Riesz basis of
H
. We say that
T
is
weaving
with respect to
v
if
T_{v}=(t_{i,j})_{i,j\in I}\in{\mathcal{W}}
, where

T_{v}v_{i}=\sum_{j\in I}t_{j,i}v_{j}

holds for each
i\in I
. In other words
T_{v}
is a matrix operator in
\ell^{2}(I)
corresponding to
T
and the basis
v
.

Note that
T:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}
is weaving with respect to a Riesz basis
v=\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
if and only if
Tv=\{Tv_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a Riesz basis woven with
v
. From Proposition
3.5
we have the following result.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
T:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}
a woven operator on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
with respect to a Riesz basis
v=\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
H

Proof.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let

(1)

4.
Weaving in SIS

We now show a result about woven Riesz basis in shift-invariant spaces.

Definition 4.1
.

We say that a closed subspace
V\subset L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is shift-invariant if
T_{k}f(\cdot):=f(\cdot-k)\in V
for all
f\in V
and
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.

A subset of functions
\Phi\subset V
is said to be a Riesz generator set of
V
if
\{T_{k}\phi:\phi\in\Phi,k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\}
is a Riesz basis of
V
.

Proposition 4.2
.

The map
\tau:L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}^{d},\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d}))
defined by

\tau f(\omega):=\{\widehat{f}(\omega+k)\}_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}

is an isometric isomorphism.

We will denote by
\widehat{V}=\tau V
, the image of a shift-invariant space
V
under
\tau
.

Definition 4.3
.

A range function is a mapping

J:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to\{\text{ closed subspaces of }\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})\}

that is measurable in the sense that its projections are weakly measurable functions.

Given a shift invariant space
V
, its associated range function is
J_{V}(\omega)=\{\tau f(\omega):f\in V\}
. When there is no confusion we will just denote this function by
J
.

For a comprehensive study about characterizations of SIS using range functions see
[
3
]
and references therein.

4.1.
Weaving Riesz generators

In this section we give conditions in order that the weavings of two Riesz generator sets of a SIS preserve the property of being a Riesz generator set.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
V\subset L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
be a shift invariant space and suppose it admits a Riesz generator set. If
\{\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n}\}
and
\{\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{n}\}
are two Riesz generator sets, we say that they are woven if for all
I\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
the set
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i\in I}\cup\{\psi_{i}\}_{i\notin I}
is a Riesz generator set.

Proposition 4.5
.

In the same context as above,
\{\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n}\}
and
\{\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{n}\}
are woven if and only if there exists
A,B>0
such that
\{\tau\phi_{1}(\omega),\dots,\tau\phi_{n}(\omega)\}
and
\{\tau\psi_{1}(\omega),\dots,\tau\psi_{n}(\omega)\}
are woven in
J(\omega)
with frame bounds
A
and
B
for almost every
\omega
.

Proof.

This follows from the definition and
[
3
, Theorem 2.3]
.
∎

Now, due to Theorem
2.6
, we know that
\{\tau\phi_{1}(\omega),\dots,\tau\phi_{n}(\omega)\}
and
\{\tau\psi_{1}(\omega),\dots,\tau\psi_{n}(\omega)\}
are woven in
J(\omega)
if and only if its change of basis matrix
A_{\omega}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{n}
. There is a natural way to lift this matrix to a linear map in
J(\omega)
: define the change of basis map as

R_{\omega}:J(\omega)\to J(\omega)\text{ with }R_{\omega}(\tau\phi_{i}(\omega))=\tau\psi_{i}(\omega),i=1,\dots,n,

and coordinate map for
\Phi
as

T_{\omega}^{\Phi}:J(\omega)\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\text{ with }T_{\omega}^{\Phi}(\tau\phi_{i}(\omega))=e_{i},i=1,\dots,n.

Then the following diagram commutes

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces J(\omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces J(\omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R_{\omega}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{\omega}^{\Phi}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{\omega}^{\Phi}}

Further lifting to
\widehat{V}
we can define
\widetilde{R}:\widehat{V}\to\widehat{V}
by

\widetilde{R}\widetilde{f}(\omega):=R_{\omega}(\widetilde{f}(\omega))

for each
\widetilde{f}\in\widetilde{V}
and
\omega\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
. Finally, going back using
\tau
, this defines an operator
R:V\to V
with
\tau(Rf)=\widetilde{R}\widetilde{f}
. Thus
R
commutes with all translates
T_{k}
,
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. Further, note that it is the only one with this property such that
R\phi_{i}=\psi_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,n
.

4.2.
Weaving Riesz bases of translations

In the previos section,
we gave necessary and sufficient conditions in order that two Riesz generator sets of a SIS are woven.
The weavings in that case interchange the generators with all their translations. In this section we will look at the general case where we allow to interchange any translate
of a generator with the translate of a possibly different generator. That is, we study when two Riesz bases of a SIS, given by the translations of generator sets in a SIS,
\{t_{k}\phi_{j}\}_{k,j}
and
\{t_{k}\psi_{j}\}_{k,j}
are woven as Riesz basis.

Riesz generator sets can be characterized through their Grammians as shown in
[
6
]
. Suppose
\Phi=\{\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n}\}
is a finite set of functions in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and let
G_{\Phi}:[-1/2,1/2)^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
be given by

G_{\Phi}(\zeta)_{j,l}=\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\widehat{\phi_{j}}(\zeta+m)\overline{\widehat{\phi_{l}}(\zeta+m)}

for each
j,l=1,\dots,n
. Thus,
G_{\Phi}(\zeta)
is a positive semidefinite matrix since

{\left\langle{v,G_{\Phi}(\zeta)v}\right\rangle}=\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}{\left|{\sum_{j=1}^{n}v_{j}\widehat{\phi_{j}}(\zeta+m)}\right|}^{2}

holds for all
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and almost all
\zeta\in[-1/2,1/2)^{d}
. Further, it can be proved
[
6
]
that
\Phi
is a Riesz generator of
\overline{\text{span}}\{t_{k}\phi_{j}:k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d},j=1,\dots,n\}
if and only if
G_{\Phi}(\zeta)
is positive definite with uniform bounds, i.e. there exists
A,B>0
such that

A{\left\|{v}\right\|}^{2}\leqslant{\left\langle{v,G_{\Phi}(\zeta)v}\right\rangle}\leqslant B{\left\|{v}\right\|}^{2}

holds for all
v\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and almost all
\zeta\in[-1/2,1/2)^{d}
. When
\Phi
has only one element
\phi
this last condition reads as

A\leqslant\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}{\left|{\widehat{\phi}(\zeta)}\right|}^{2}\leqslant B\quad\text{a.e. }\zeta\in[-1/2,1/2)^{d}.

We are now ready to prove our wieving result in this context. For simplicity, we only prove the case of generator sets with one element and in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
as the proof of the more general case is similar.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
V\subset L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
be a shift-invariant space with a single Riesz generator
\phi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
with lower and upper Riesz bounds
A
and
B
respectively.
If

is such that there exists a constant

\{t_{k}\psi:k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}

\{t_{k}\phi:k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
.

In order to prove this proposition we will use the following perturbation result.
