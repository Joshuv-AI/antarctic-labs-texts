On the analytic structure of double and triple points in the target of finite holomorphic multi-germs
Juan J. Nuño-Ballesteros, Guillermo Peñafort Sanchis, Cinzia Villa

Abstract
. We study the analytic structure of the double and triple point spaces M 2 ​ ( f ) M_{2}(f) and M 3 ​ ( f ) M_{3}(f) of finite multi-germs f : ( X , S ) → ( ℂ n + 1 , 0 ) f\colon(X,S)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0) , based on results of Mond and Pellikaan for the mono-germ case. We show that these spaces are Cohen-Macaulay, provided that certain dimensional conditions are satisfied, and give explicit expressions for their defining ideals in terms of those of their mono-germ branches.

1.
Introduction

In this text we study double and triple points of finite holomorphic multi-germs

f\colon(X,S)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0),

where
X
stands for a Cohen-Macaulay analytic space of dimension
n
. By saying that
(X,S)
is a multi-germ, the set
S
is assumed to be finite, and by saying that
X
is an analytic space (in contrast to a complex space), it is understood that
X
is reduced. These assumptions and the fact that
f
is holomorphic will not be repeated every time these objects appear, but extra hypothesis will sometimes be added on top of them.

There are several ways to define double, triple and, in general,
k
-multiple point spaces, and this work focusses on the spaces
M_{k}(f)\subseteq(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)
, defined by means of Fitting ideals of the pushforward module
f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{(X,S)}
. These spaces were introduced by Mond and Pellikaan
[
5
]
, who studied their analytic structures in the mono-germ case. They show that, for mono-germs, the double and triple point spaces
M_{2}(f)
and
M_{3}(f)
are determinantal if they have the right dimension. As it turns out their proofs do not apply in the multi-germ case, as explained in Example
1.4
. The goal of this work is to study the analytic structure of
M_{2}(f)
and
M_{3}(f)
for multi-germs, giving conditions for them to be Cohen-Macaulay and showing explicit expressions for their ideals in terms of those of the spaces
M_{k}
of the mono-germ branches of
f
.

For a finite multi-germ
f
as above, the pushforward
f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{(X,S)}
is a finitely presented
\mathcal{O}_{n+1}
-module, and it admits a square presentation matrix
\lambda

[
5
, Section 2.2]
. Assuming
\lambda
to be of size
q\times q
, the
k
th Fitting ideal of
f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{(X,S)}
is defined as follows:

F_{k}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{(X,S)})=\begin{cases}\langle\text{$q-k$ size minors of $\lambda$}\rangle,&\text{ if $k<q$}\\
\mathcal{O}_{n+1},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Then, the
(target)
k
-multiple point space
of
f
is the complex subspace
M_{k}(f)\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)
, defined as

M_{k}(f)=V(F_{k-1}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{(X,S)})).

This definition is based on the fact that, set-theoretically,
M_{k}(f)
is the germ of the set of points
y\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
where
(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X})_{y}
requires at least a number
k
of generators
[
5
, Proposition 1.5]
. Geometrically, this means that
M_{k}(f)
is given by the points
y\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
with at least
k
preimages, counting with multiplicity. Observe that
M_{1}(f)
is the hypersurface with equation
\det\lambda
in
(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)
and coincides, as a set-germ, with the image
(f(X),0)
.

Definition 1.1
.

Given
f\colon(X,S)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)
, we say that
M_{k}(f)
is
dimensionally correct
if
\operatorname{dim}M_{k}(f)=n-k+1
.

From the fact that
(X,S)
is assumed to be reduced and
f
is assumed to be finite, it follows that
f
must be locally an embedding on an open dense subset of
X
. From this it follows that
M_{2}(f)
is dimensionally correct if and only if
f
is generically one-to-one. Moreover,

f
is generically one-to-one if and only if
M_{1}(f)
is reduced
[
5
, Proposition 3.2]
.

Theorem 1.2

(Mond-Pellikaan)
.

Any mono-germ
f\colon(X,0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)
satisfies
\operatorname{dim}M_{2}(f)\geq n-1
. If the equality holds, then
M_{2}(f)
is Cohen-Macaulay. If furthermore
M_{3}(f)=\emptyset
, then
M_{2}(f)
is a complete intersection.

The proof of this result consists on showing that the presentation matrix can be chosen to be symmetric and in such a way that
F_{1}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X,0})
is generated by minors of the submatrix obtained by removing the first row
[
5
, Theorem 3.4]
. This shows
M_{2}(f)
to be determinantal whenever it has dimension
n-1
(observe that in codimension two the determinantal and Cohen-Macaulay conditions are equivalent, by the Hilbert-Burch theorem). The complete intersection case also follows from the fact that we can remove the first row, taking into account that the hypothesis that
M_{3}(f)=\emptyset
is equivalent to the fact that the above matrix has size
2\times 2
.

In the case where
X
is Gorenstein, the presentation matrix may be chosen in such a way that
F_{2}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X,0})
is generated by minors of the submatrix obtained by removing the first row and first column
[
5
, Theorem 4.3]
. This implies what follows:

Theorem 1.3

(Mond-Pellikaan)
.

Any mono-germ
f\colon(X,0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)
, with
(X,0)
Gorenstein, satisfies
\operatorname{dim}M_{3}(f)\geq n-2
. If the equality holds, then
M_{3}(f)
is Cohen-Macaulay.

Unfortunately, in the case of multi-germs, sometimes no proper submatrix of the presentation matrix has the right minors to generate

F_{1}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{(X,S)})
nor
F_{2}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{(X,S)})
. This is illustrated by the following example:

Example 1.4
.

Consider the multi-germ
f\colon\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{3}(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{3},0)
, parametrizing the planes
\{X=0\}
,
\{Y=0\}
and
\{Z=0\}
in
(\mathbb{C}^{3},0)
. Since the branches are immersive mono-germs, their presentation matrices are
1\times 1
matrices whose only entry are the generators of the ideals of the corresponding planes. The presentation matrix of
f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{(X,S)}
is then the diagonal matrix

\lambda=\begin{bmatrix}X&0&0\\
0&Y&0\\
0&0&Z\\
\end{bmatrix}.

As expected,
M_{2}(f)
is the union of the coordinate axes of
(\mathbb{C}^{3},0)
, and
M_{3}(f)
is the origin, both spaces with reduced structure. One sees that no proper submatrix of
\lambda
has the adequate entries to generate the corresponding equations.

Remark 1.5
.

There are multiple point spaces
M_{k}(f)
for finite (global) mappings
f\colon X\to Y
, defined by the corresponding sheaf theoretical versions
\mathcal{F}_{k}
of the Fitting ideals
F_{k}
. The properties that we show for multi-germs imply the corresponding properties for mappings, because the stalks of
\mathcal{F}_{k}
are the ideals
F_{k}
associated to a multi-germ. We omit these translations to the global setting, as they are quite obvious.

2.
Target multiple-point spaces for multi-germs

To simplify writing, for the remainder of the text we fix some notations regarding multi-germ branches. Given
f\colon(X,S)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)
, with
S=\{x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(r)}\}
, for each
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
, we write

f^{(i)}=f|_{(X,x^{(i)})},\qquad F_{j}^{(i)}=F_{j}(f^{(i)}_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X,x^{(i)}})\quad\text{and}\quad M_{j}^{(i)}=M_{j}(f^{(i)}).

From the equality
f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{(X,S)}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}f^{(i)}_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X,x^{(i)}}
, one observes that the associated Fitting ideals are generated by minors of a block diagonal matrix, and then

F_{k}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{(X,S)})=\sum_{j_{1}+\dots+j_{r}=k}F_{j_{1}}^{(1)}\cdot\ldots\cdot F_{j_{r}}^{(r)}.

In combination with the inclusions
M_{j}^{(i)}\subseteq M_{j-1}^{(i)}
, the previous equality gives the
set theoretical
decomposition

(1)

M_{k}(f)=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt1\leq\ell\leq r\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{\ell}\leq r\\
j_{1}+\dots+j_{\ell}=k\end{subarray}}M_{j_{1}}^{(i_{1})}\cap\ \dots\ \cap M_{j_{\ell}}^{(i_{\ell})}.

This decomposition corresponds to the intuitive idea of what multiple points of multi-germs should be. For example, it states that, as a set, the triple points of a bigerm are the union of the triple points of the first branch, the intersection of the double points of the first branch with the image of the second, the intersection of the image of the first branch with the double points of the second and, finally, the triple points of the second branch.

It is natural to ask to what extent this decomposition gives information about
M_{k}(f)
as a complex space. To be precise, we want to know if the ideal
F_{k}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{(X,S)})
can be expressed as

F_{k}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{(X,S)})=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt1\leq\ell\leq r\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{\ell}\leq r\\
j_{1}+\dots+j_{\ell}=k-\ell+1\end{subarray}}F_{j_{1}}^{(i_{1})}+\dots+F_{j_{\ell}}^{(i_{\ell})}.

We will use the following three basic lemmas. The first follows easily from localizing at associated primes and the second follows from the interpretation of
\operatorname{depth}
in terms of
\operatorname{Ext}
, by looking at the long exact sequence associated to a short exact sequence (see e.g.
[
8
,
Tag 00LX
]
). For completeness, we include a proof of the third lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
R
be a noetherian local ring, and let
J_{1},\dots,J_{r}
and
I
be ideals in
R
. Assume that for all
i\neq j
the condition
J_{i}\not\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
holds for all
\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Ass}(J_{j}+I)
. Then,
\bigcap_{i}(J_{i}+I)=(\bigcap_{i}J_{i})+I
.

Lemma 2.2

(Depth Lemma)
.

Let
R
be a local Noetherian ring and let
0\to N^{\prime}\to N\to N^{\prime\prime}\to 0
be a short exact sequence of nonzero finitely generated
R
-modules.

(1)

\operatorname{depth}N\geq\min\{\operatorname{depth}N^{\prime},\operatorname{depth}N^{\prime\prime}\}
.

(2)

\operatorname{depth}N^{\prime\prime}\geq\min\{\operatorname{depth}N,\operatorname{depth}N^{\prime}-1\}
.

(3)

\operatorname{depth}N^{\prime}\geq\min\{\operatorname{depth}N,\operatorname{depth}N^{\prime\prime}+1\}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
I
and
J
be ideals in a local noetherian ring
R
, such that
\operatorname{depth}\frac{R}{I}<\operatorname{depth}R
,
\operatorname{depth}\frac{R}{J}<\operatorname{depth}R
and
\operatorname{depth}\frac{R}{IJ}<\operatorname{depth}R
. Then

\operatorname{depth}\frac{R}{IJ}\geq\operatorname{depth}\frac{R}{I}+\operatorname{depth}\frac{R}{J}-\operatorname{depth}R+1.

Proof.

The hypotheses imply
\operatorname{depth}\frac{R}{I}=\operatorname{depth}I-1
,
\operatorname{depth}\frac{R}{J}=\operatorname{depth}J-1
and
\operatorname{depth}\frac{R}{IJ}=\operatorname{depth}IJ-1
. By the third of these equalities, it suffices to show that
\operatorname{depth}IJ\geq\operatorname{depth}\frac{R}{I}+\operatorname{depth}\frac{R}{J}-\operatorname{depth}R+2
and, by the Auslander-Buchsbaum formula, this amounts to showing
\operatorname{pd}IJ\leq 2\operatorname{depth}R-\operatorname{depth}\frac{R}{I}-\operatorname{depth}\frac{R}{J}-2
. Again by the Auslander-Buchsbaum formula, we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{pd}IJ=\operatorname{pd}(I\otimes J)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{pd}I+\operatorname{pd}J=2\operatorname{depth}R-\operatorname{depth}I-\operatorname{depth}J

\displaystyle=2\operatorname{depth}R-\operatorname{depth}\frac{R}{I}-\operatorname{depth}\frac{R}{J}-2.\qed

Now we are ready to prove that, when they are dimensionally correct, double points of multi-germs are Cohen-Macaulay and to show how to compute them under favorable circumstances:

Theorem 2.4
.

If
f\colon(X,S)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)
is finite and generically one-to-one, then
M_{2}(f)
is Cohen-Macaulay of dimension
n-1
. If furthermore
M_{3}(f)
is dimensionally correct, then the ideal of
M_{2}(f)
satisfies

F_{1}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{(X,S)})=\bigcap_{1\leq i\leq r}F_{1}^{(i)}\cap\bigcap_{1\leq i<j\leq r}\big(F_{0}^{(i)}+F_{0}^{(j)}\big).

Proof.

We proceed by induction over
r=|S|
. If
r=1
, then
M_{2}(f)
is Cohen-Macaulay by Theorem
1.2
and the expression for the defining ideal reads
F_{1}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{(X,S)})=F_{1}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{(X,S)})
, so there is nothing to prove. Now assume the statement to be true for multi-germs with no more than
r-1
branches.

Showing
M_{2}(f)
to be Cohen-Macaulay amounts to showing

\operatorname{depth}\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F_{1}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X,S})}=n-1,

because the generically one-to-one condition implies
\operatorname{dim}M_{2}(f)=n-1
. To exploit the inductive hypothesis, consider
S^{\prime}=\{x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(r-1)}\}
and let

f^{\prime}=f_{(X,S^{\prime})}\quad\text{and}\quad F^{\prime}_{j}=F_{j}(f^{\prime}_{*}\mathcal{O}_{(X,S^{\prime})}),

so that
F_{1}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X,S})=F_{1}^{\prime}F_{0}^{(r)}+F_{0}^{\prime}F_{1}^{(r)}
.

Since
F_{0}^{\prime}
and
F_{0}^{(r)}
are principal, it follows from Lemma
2.3
and the induction hypothesis that

\operatorname{depth}\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F_{1}^{\prime}F_{0}^{(r)}}\geq n-1\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{depth}\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F_{0}^{\prime}F_{1}^{(r)}}\geq n-1.

Applying Item (
2
) of the
Depth Lemma
to the short exact sequence

0\to\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F_{1}^{\prime}F_{0}^{(r)}\cap F_{0}^{\prime}F_{1}^{(r)}}\to\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F_{1}^{\prime}F_{0}^{(r)}}\oplus\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F_{0}^{\prime}F_{1}^{(r)}}\to\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F_{1}^{\prime}F_{0}^{(r)}+F_{0}^{\prime}F_{1}^{(r)}}\to 0

reduces the problem to showing that

\operatorname{depth}\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F_{1}^{\prime}F_{0}^{(r)}\cap F_{0}^{\prime}F_{1}^{(r)}}\geq n.

From the inclusions
F_{0}^{\prime}\subseteq F_{1}^{\prime}
and
F_{0}^{(r)}\subseteq F_{1}^{(r)}
, one obtains

F^{\prime}_{1}F^{(r)}_{0}\cap F^{\prime}_{0}F^{(r)}_{1}\subseteq F^{\prime}_{1}\cap F^{(r)}_{0}\cap F^{\prime}_{0}\cap F^{(r)}_{1}=F^{\prime}_{0}\cap F^{(r)}_{0},

F^{\prime}_{1}F^{(r)}_{0}\cap F^{\prime}_{0}F^{(r)}_{1}\supseteq F^{\prime}_{0}F^{(r)}_{0}.

Since
F_{0}^{\prime}
and
F_{0}^{(r)}
are principal ideals defining the images of
f^{\prime}
and
f^{(r)}
, the generically one-to-one condition forces
F^{\prime}_{0}F^{(r)}_{0}=F^{\prime}_{0}\cap F^{(r)}_{0}
. From the two inclusions above, now we obtain
F^{\prime}_{1}F^{(r)}_{0}\cap F^{\prime}_{0}F^{(r)}_{1}=F^{\prime}_{0}F^{(r)}_{0}
. Being the ideal of a hypersurface in
(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)
, its quotient must have depth
n
, as desired. This finishes the proof that
M_{2}(f)
is Cohen-Macaulay.

Now assume
M_{3}(f)
to be dimensionally correct. This forces
F_{0}^{\prime}
and
F_{0}^{(r)}
to be generated by regular elements in
\mathcal{O}_{n+1}/F_{1}^{(r)}
and
\mathcal{O}_{n+1}/F^{\prime}_{1}
. This is true because
M_{2}(f^{\prime})
and
M_{2}^{(r)}
are Cohen-Macaulay of dimension
n-1
, and points in
M_{2}(f^{\prime})\cap M_{1}(f^{(r)})
or
M_{1}(f^{\prime})\cap M_{2}(f^{(r)})
are triple points of
f
, which are assumed to be have dimension
(n-2)
.
Then, we obtain

\displaystyle F_{1}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{(X,S)})

\displaystyle=F_{1}^{\prime}F_{0}^{(r)}+F_{0}^{\prime}F_{1}^{(r)}

\displaystyle=\big(F_{1}^{\prime}\cap F_{0}^{(r)}\big)+\big(F_{0}^{\prime}\cap F_{1}^{(r)}\big)

\displaystyle=F_{1}^{\prime}\cap F_{1}^{(r)}\cap\big(F_{0}^{\prime}+F_{0}^{(r)}\big)

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{1\leq i\leq r-1}F_{1}^{(i)}\cap\bigcap_{1\leq i<j\leq r-1}\big(F_{0}^{(i)}+F_{0}^{(j)}\big)\cap F_{1}^{(r)}\cap\big(F_{0}^{\prime}+F_{0}^{(r)}\big),

where the third equality follows from the inclusions
F_{0}^{\prime}\subseteq F_{1}^{\prime}
and
F_{0}^{(r)}\subseteq F_{1}^{(r)}
and the last equality follows from the induction hypothesis (observe that
M_{3}(f^{\prime})
is dimensionally correct, because every ingredient in decomposition of
M_{3}(f^{\prime})
in (
1
) appears in the decomposition of
M_{3}(f)
as well). Comparign this last expression to the formula in the statement shows that it is enough to prove that

F_{0}^{\prime}+F_{0}^{(r)}=\bigcap_{1\leq i\leq r-1}\big(F_{0}^{(i)}+F_{0}^{(r)}\big).

The generically one-to-one condition implies

F_{0}^{\prime}=\bigcap_{1\leq i\leq r-1}F_{0}^{(i)}

and the fact that
F_{0}^{(r)}
is generated by a regular element in
\mathcal{O}_{n+1}/F_{0}^{\prime}
. The desired equality now follows, taking into account that the ideals
F_{0}^{(i)}
are principal.
∎

Example 2.5
.

Let
f\colon(\mathbb{C}^{1},0)\sqcup(\mathbb{C}^{1},0)\sqcup(\mathbb{C}^{1},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
be the map-germ with branches
f^{(1)}(x)=(x,0)
,
f^{(2)}(x)=(0,x)
and
f^{(3)}(x)=(x,x)
. Since
f^{(i)}
is immersive, it follows that
F_{0}^{(i)}
is the reduced ideal of the image of
f^{(i)}
and
F_{1}^{(i)}=\mathcal{O}_{2}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle F_{1}(f_{*}(\mathcal{O}_{(X,S)})

\displaystyle=F_{1}^{(1)}F_{0}^{(2)}F_{0}^{(3)}+F_{0}^{(1)}F_{1}^{(2)}F_{0}^{(3)}+F_{0}^{(1)}F_{0}^{(2)}F_{1}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\langle X(X-Y),Y(X-Y),XY\rangle.

At the same time, the right hand side of the equality in Theorem
2.4
gives

F_{1}^{(1)}\cap F_{1}^{(2)}\cap F_{1}^{(2)}\cap\big(F_{0}^{(1)}+F_{0}^{(2)}\big)\cap\big(F_{0}^{(1)}+F_{0}^{(3)}\big)\cap\big(F_{0}^{(2)}+F_{0}^{(3)}\big)=\langle X,Y\rangle.

The disagreement comes from the fact that the origin is a triple point of
f
and, in these dimensions,
M_{3}(f)
must be empty to be dimensionally correct.

To study triple points, we will use a typical trick in the theory of singular mappings, consisting on replacing the original mapping by an unfolding having desired properties. When studying map-germs
f\colon(\mathbb{C}^{n},S)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)
, an unfolding of
f
is simply a map germ of the form
(f_{t},t)\colon(\mathbb{C}^{n},S)\times(\mathbb{C}^{r},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\times\mathbb{C}^{r},0)
, but when working with map-germs
f\colon(X,S)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)
, it is convenient to extend this notion to allow for the source space
X
to be deformed as well. A possible definition is as follows:

Given a multi-germ
f\colon X\to Y
between complex spaces, an unfolding of
f
over
\Delta=(\mathbb{C}^{r},0)
is a multi-germ
\phi\colon\mathcal{X}\to Y\times\Delta
such that, taking the commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y\times\Delta}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Delta}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}

where
Y\times\Delta\to\Delta
is the usual projection from the product, the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

\mathcal{X}\to\Delta
is flat family, with central fibre
\mathcal{X}_{0}=\mathcal{X}\times_{\Delta}\{0\}
isomorphic to
X
.

(2)

The above isomorphism
\mathcal{X}_{0}\to X
produces a commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{X}_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi_{0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}

In particular, this provides a family of mappings
\mathcal{X}_{t}\xrightarrow{\phi_{t}}Y
so that
\phi_{0}
is equivalent to
f
.

For our purposes, the crucial property is that unfoldings of finite multi-germs satisfy

M_{k}(f)\cong M_{k}(\phi)\times_{\Delta}\{0\}.

This can be found in more general form in
[
4
, Proposition 11.6]
. Now we are ready to state our main result about triple points in the target.

Theorem 2.6
.

Given
f\colon(X,S)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)
, assume
f
to be generically one-to-one and that
M_{3}^{(i)}=\emptyset
, for
2\leq i\leq r
. If
M_{3}^{(1)}\neq\emptyset
, then assume
(X,x^{(1)})
to be Gorenstein. Assume also that
f
admits an unfolding
\phi
such that
M_{3}(\phi)
and
M_{4}(\phi)
are dimensionally correct. If
M_{3}(f)
is dimensionally correct, then it is Cohen-Macaulay. If furthermore
M_{4}(f)
is dimensionally correct, then the ideal of
M_{3}(f)
satisfies

\displaystyle F_{2}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{(X,S)})=

\displaystyle F_{2}^{(1)}\cap\bigcap_{1\leq i<j\leq r}\big(F_{1}^{(i)}+F_{0}^{(j)}\big)\cap\big(F_{0}^{(i)}+F_{1}^{(j)}\big)

\displaystyle\cap\bigcap_{1\leq i<j<k\leq r}\big(F_{0}^{(i)}+F_{0}^{(j)}+F_{0}^{(k)}\big).

Proof.

From the isormorphism
M_{k}(f)\cong M_{k}(\phi)\times_{\Delta}\{0\}
and the fact that
\{0\}
is regularly embedded in
\Delta
, it follows that, if
M_{3}(\phi)
is Cohen-Macaulay, then so is
M_{3}(f)
, because the hypothesis that
M_{3}(f)
is dimensionally correct implies that it is regularly embedded in
M_{3}(\phi)
. To keep the notation we are used to, instead of replacing
f
by
\phi
and showing
M_{3}(\phi)
to be Cohen Macaulay, we may just assume
M_{3}(f)
and
M_{4}(f)
to be dimensionally correct and show
M_{3}(f)
to be Cohen-Macaulay.

We proceed by induction on
|S|
as in the proof of Theorem
2.4
. The case of
r=1
is precisely Theorem
1.3
and, for
r\geq 2
, we again use the notation
S^{\prime}=\{x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(r-1)}\}
and let

f^{\prime}=f_{(X,S^{\prime})}\quad\text{and}\quad F^{\prime}_{j}=F_{j}(f^{\prime}_{*}\mathcal{O}_{(X,S^{\prime})}).

The ideal
F_{0}^{(r)}
is principal and, since
f^{(r)}
is generically one-to-one and
M_{3}^{(r)}(f)=\emptyset
, the ideal
F_{1}^{(r)}
is generated by a regular sequence, by Theorem
1.2
. From the fact that
M_{4}(f)
is dimensionally correct, it follows that the generators of
F_{0}^{(r)}
and
F_{1}^{(r)}
may be chosen so that they form regular sequences in
\mathcal{O}_{n+1}/F_{2}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{O}_{n+1}/F_{1}^{\prime}
, respectively. This implies
F_{2}^{\prime}F_{0}^{(r)}=F_{2}^{\prime}\cap F_{0}^{(r)}
and
F_{1}^{\prime}F_{1}^{(r)}=F_{1}^{\prime}\cap F_{1}^{(r)}
. Then, taking into account that
F_{2}^{(r)}=\mathcal{O}_{n+1}
, and exploiting the inclusions
F_{0}^{\prime}\subseteq F_{1}^{\prime}\subseteq F_{2}^{\prime}
and
F_{0}^{(r)}\subseteq F_{1}^{(r)}
, one obtains

\displaystyle F_{2}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X,S})

\displaystyle=F_{2}^{\prime}F_{0}^{(r)}+F_{1}^{\prime}F_{1}^{(r)}+F_{0}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=F_{2}^{\prime}\cap F_{0}^{(r)}+F_{1}^{\prime}\cap F_{1}^{(r)}+F_{0}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=F^{\prime}_{2}\cap\big(F^{\prime}_{1}+F^{(r)}_{0}\big)\cap\big(F^{\prime}_{0}+F^{(r)}_{1}\big).

The same inclusions also imply that

\Big(F^{\prime}_{2}\cap\big(F^{\prime}_{1}+F^{(r)}_{0}\big)\Big)+F^{\prime}_{0}+F^{(r)}_{1}=\Big(F^{\prime}_{2}\cap\big(F^{\prime}_{1}+F^{(r)}_{0}\big)\Big)+F^{(r)}_{1}=F_{1}^{\prime}+F_{1}^{(r)}.

This gives a short exact sequence

0\to\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F_{2}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X,S})}\to\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F^{\prime}_{2}\cap\big(F^{\prime}_{1}+F^{(r)}_{0}\big)}\oplus\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F^{\prime}_{0}+F^{(r)}_{1}}\to\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F^{\prime}_{1}+F^{(r)}_{1}}\to 0.

The hypotheses that
M_{3}(f)
and
M_{4}(f)
are dimensionally correct imply that

\operatorname{depth}\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F^{\prime}_{0}+F^{(r)}_{1}}=n-2\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{depth}\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F^{\prime}_{1}+F^{(r)}_{1}}=n-3.

Therefore, by Item (
3
) of Lemma
3
, it suffices to show that

\operatorname{depth}\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F^{\prime}_{2}\cap\big(F^{\prime}_{1}+F^{(r)}_{0}\big)}\geq n-2.

Since
F_{1}^{\prime}\subseteq F_{2}^{\prime}
, this last ring fits into the short exact sequence

0\to\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F^{\prime}_{2}\cap\big(F^{\prime}_{1}+F^{(r)}_{0}\big)}\to\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F^{\prime}_{2}}\oplus\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F^{\prime}_{1}+F^{(r)}_{0}}\to\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F^{\prime}_{2}+F^{(r)}_{0}}\to 0.

That the depth is the desired one follows again by Item (
3
) of Lemma
3
, taking into account that the dimensionality hypotheses imply

\operatorname{depth}\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F^{\prime}_{2}}=\operatorname{depth}\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F^{\prime}_{1}+F^{(r)}_{0}}=n-2\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{depth}\frac{\mathcal{O}_{n+1}}{F^{\prime}_{2}+F^{(r)}_{0}}=n-3.

This completes the proof of the statement that
M_{3}(f)
is Cohen-Macaulay.

Now assume
M_{3}(f)
and
M_{4}(f)
are dimensionally correct. We have shown before that, under these hypotheses,

F_{2}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X,S})=F^{\prime}_{2}\cap\big(F^{\prime}_{1}+F^{(r)}_{0}\big)\cap\big(F^{\prime}_{0}+F^{(r)}_{1}\big).

From the fact that
f
is generically one-to-one, it follows that
F^{\prime}_{0}=\bigcap_{1\leq i\leq r-1}F_{0}^{(i)}
. Applying induction and Theorem
2.4
, we obtain the expression

\displaystyle F_{2}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{(X,S)})=

\displaystyle F_{2}^{(1)}\cap\bigcap_{1\leq i<j\leq r-1}\big(F_{1}^{(i)}+F_{0}^{(j)}\big)\cap\big(F_{0}^{(i)}+F_{1}^{(j)}\big)

\displaystyle\cap\bigcap_{1\leq i<j<k\leq r-1}\big(F_{0}^{(i)}+F_{0}^{(j)}+F_{0}^{(k)}\big)

\displaystyle\cap\bigg(\Big(\bigcap_{1\leq i\leq r-1}F_{1}^{(i)}\cap\bigcap_{1\leq i<j\leq r-1}\big(F_{0}^{(i)}+F_{0}^{(j)}\big)\Big)+F^{(r)}_{0}\bigg)

\displaystyle\cap\Big(\big(\bigcap_{1\leq i\leq r-1}F_{0}^{(i)}\big)+F^{(r)}_{1}\Big).

Comparing this to the desired expression shows that it suffices to prove the equality

\Big(\bigcap_{1\leq i\leq r-1}F_{1}^{(i)}\cap\bigcap_{1\leq i<j\leq r-1}\big(F_{0}^{(i)}+F_{0}^{(j)}\big)\Big)+F^{(r)}_{0}=\\
\bigcap_{1\leq i\leq r-1}\big(F_{1}^{(i)}+F_{0}^{(r)}\big)\cap\bigcap_{1\leq i<j\leq r-1}\big(F_{0}^{(i)}+F_{0}^{(j)}+F_{0}^{(r)}\big)

and the equality

\big(\bigcap_{1\leq i\leq r-1}F_{0}^{(i)}\big)+F^{(r)}_{1}=\bigcap_{1\leq i\leq r-1}\big(F_{0}^{(i)}+F^{(r)}_{1}\big).

The conditions that
M_{4}(f)
and
M_{3}(f)
are dimensionally correct imply that, given
i,j,k
, with
i\neq j
, no irreducible component of
V(F_{0}^{(i)}+F_{0}^{(j)}+F_{0}^{(r)})
is contained in
M_{2}^{(k)}=V(F_{1}^{(k)})
, no irreducible component of
M_{2}^{(k)}\cap M_{1}^{(r)}
is contained in
V(F_{0}^{(i)}+F_{0}^{(j)})
, no irreducible
M_{2}^{(i)}\cap M_{1}^{(r)}
is contained in
M_{2}^{(j)}
and no irreducible component of

is contained in

.
The first equality then follows from Lemma
2.1
. The same dimensional correctness conditions imply that no irreducible component of

is contained in

, for

. Then by Lemma

the second equality holds as well.
∎

and

3.
Triple and quadruple points in the source

Given a finite multi-germ
f\colon(X,S)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)
, the multiple point spaces
M_{k}(f)
of the previous section lie on the target of
f
, that is, on
(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)
. There are however other multiple point spaces that one can define, and the ones this section is devoted are subspace of the source of
f
, instead of its target. For simplicity, we consider only the case where the source is smooth. Given a multi-germ

f\colon(\mathbb{C}^{n},S)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0),

the idea is as follows: Start by defining a subspace
D^{2}(f)\subseteq(\mathbb{C}^{n},S)\times(\mathbb{C}^{n},S)
which, naively, should be the closure of the set of pairs
(x,x^{\prime})
with
x\neq x^{\prime}
and
f(x)=f(x^{\prime})
(the proper definition is given below). If
f
is finite, then the projection
(\mathbb{C}^{n},S)\times(\mathbb{C}^{n},S)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n},S)
on the first coordinate restricts to a finite mapping

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D^{2}(f)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\mathbb{C}^{n},S)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}

The
source double point space

D(f)=D^{2}_{1}(f)
is defined as the image of
p
, with the analytic structure given by the
0
th Fitting ideal, that is,

D^{2}_{1}(f)=M_{1}(p).

This makes sense since, given a pair
(x,x^{\prime})
, with
x\neq x^{\prime}
and
f(x)=f(x^{\prime})
, the point
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
should be considered a double point of
f
.

The same idea tells us that
M_{2}(p)\subseteq(\mathbb{C}^{n},S)
should be regarded as a triple point space, because two pairs
(x,x^{\prime})
and
(\tilde{x},\tilde{x}^{\prime})
, with
x^{\prime}\neq x
,
f(x^{\prime})=f(x)
,
\tilde{x}^{\prime}\neq\tilde{x}
,
f(\tilde{x}^{\prime})=f(\tilde{x})
,
(x,x^{\prime})\neq(\tilde{x},\tilde{x}^{\prime})
and
x=\tilde{x}
, carry the same information than a tuple
(x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})
, with
x\neq x^{\prime}\neq x^{\prime\prime}\neq x
and
f(x)=f(x^{\prime})=f(x^{\prime\prime})
, by setting
x^{\prime\prime}=\tilde{x}^{\prime}
, and then
x
should be a triple point of
f
. By the same token, points in
M_{3}(p)
should be regarded as quadruple points in the source. Before making this into a formal definition, we must define the space
D^{2}(f)
.

Consider the ideal
I_{\Delta}
defining the diagonal
(\Delta\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)
in
(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)\times(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)
. The fibered product
(\mathbb{C}^{n},S)\times_{(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)}(\mathbb{C}^{n},S)
is the zero locus of the pullback
(f\times f)^{*}I_{\Delta}
. Given
x^{(i)},x^{(j)}\in S
, we may take coordinates
x
and
x^{\prime}
of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, centered at
x^{(i)},x^{(j)}
, so that

((f\times f)^{*}I_{\Delta})_{(x^{(i)},x^{(j)})}=\big\langle f^{(i)}_{1}(x^{\prime})-f^{(j)}_{1}(x),\dots,f^{(i)}_{n+1}(x^{\prime})-f^{(j)}_{n+1}(x)\big\rangle.

Away from the diagonal,
(\mathbb{C}^{n},S)\times_{(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)}(\mathbb{C}^{n},S)
corresponds with the idea of what a double point should be, but around a point
(x^{(i)},x^{(i)})
, it contains all points
(x,x)\in\Delta\mathbb{C}^{n}
, which, a priori, should not be regarded as triple points. To remedy this, we must add extra conditions on the diagonal. If we think of
f^{(i)}(x^{\prime})-f^{(i)}(x)
and
x^{\prime}-x
, the fact that
f^{(i)}(x^{\prime})-f^{(i)}(x)
vanishes on

translates, by Hilbert Nullstellensatz, into the fact that there is an

\mathcal{O}_{2n}

f^{(i)}(x^{\prime})-f^{(i)}(x)=\alpha^{(i)}(x,x^{\prime})\cdot(x^{\prime}-x).

The

D^{2}(f)
is the disjoint union of the germs

\left(D^{2}(f),{(x^{(i)},x^{(j)})}\right)=V(I^{2}_{ij}),

where

I_{ij}=\begin{cases}((f\times f)^{*}I_{\Delta})_{(x^{(i)},x^{(j)})}&\text{ if $i\neq j$}\\
((f\times f)^{*}I_{\Delta})_{(x^{(i)},x^{(i)})}+\big\langle\text{$n\times n$ minors of $\alpha$},\big\rangle&\text{ if $i=j$}\end{cases}

The space
D^{2}(f)
has dimension at least

and it is Cohen-Macaulay when it has dimension exactly

4
, Section 9.4]

f
has corank one at

, then, after a change of coordinates in source and target,

takes the form of the germ at the origin of a mapping of the form

Then,
\left(D^{2}(f),{(x^{(i)},x^{(i)})}\right)
is isomorphic
[
4
, Section 9.5]
to germ of

From this, it follows that
\left(D^{2}(f),{(x^{(i)},x^{(i)})}\right)
is a complete intersection whenever it has dimension
n-1
.
