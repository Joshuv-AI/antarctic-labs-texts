Karatsuba’s divisor problem and related questions
Mikhail R. Gabdullin, Vitalii V. Iudelevich, Sergei V. Konyagin

Abstract
. We prove that ∑ p ⩽ x 1 τ ⁡ ( p − 1 ) ≍ x ( log ⁡ x ) 3 / 2 and ∑ n ⩽ x 1 τ ⁡ ( n 2 + 1 ) ≍ x ( log ⁡ x ) 1 / 2 , \sum_{p\leqslant x}\frac{1}{\tau(p-1)}\asymp\frac{x}{(\log x)^{3/2}}\quad\mbox{\text{and}}\quad\sum_{n\leqslant x}\frac{1}{\tau(n^{2}+1)}\asymp\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1/2}}, where τ ⁡ ( n ) = ∑ d | n 1 \tau(n)=\sum_{d|n}1 is the number of divisors of n n , and the summation in the first sum is over primes.



1.
Introduction

In 2004, A. A. Karatsuba in his seminar “Analytic number theory and applications” posed the following problem: find the asymptotics for the sum

\Phi(x)=\sum_{p\leqslant x}\frac{1}{\tau(p-1)}

as
x\to\infty
, where
\tau(n)=\sum_{d|n}1
is the divisor function and the summation is over primes not exceeding
x
. This is a natural “hybrid” of the following two classical problems from analytic number theory.

The first one (so-called Titchmarsh divisor problem) is asking for the asymptotic behaviour of the sum

D(x)=\sum_{p\leqslant x}\tau(p-1).

It is well-known (see
[
13
]
,
[
1
]
,
[
6
]
) that

D(x)\sim\frac{\zeta(2)\zeta(3)}{\zeta(6)}\,x,\quad\quad x\to\infty,

where
\zeta(s)
denotes the Riemann zeta-function. The other problem is to find the asymptotics for the sum

T(x)=\sum_{n\leqslant x}\frac{1}{\tau(n)}.

This problem was solved by S. Ramanujan
[
10
]
, who showed that

(1.1)

T(x)=c_{0}\frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log x}}\right)\right),

where

c_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\prod_{p}\sqrt{p^{2}-p}\log\frac{p}{p-1}=0.5486\ldots

The sum
\Phi(x)
has been studied before. In the recent work
[
4
]
it was shown that

\Phi(x)\leqslant 4K\dfrac{x}{(\log x)^{3/2}}+O\left(\frac{x\log\log x}{(\log x)^{5/2}}\right),

where

K=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\prod_{p}\sqrt{\dfrac{p}{p-1}}\left(p\log\dfrac{p}{p-1}-\dfrac{1}{p-1}\right)=0.2532\ldots

Note that the bound
\Phi(x)\ll\frac{x}{(\log x)^{3/2}}
follows from Corollary 1.2 of
[
7
]
, and can also be derived by arguing similarly to the proof of upper bound in Theorem
1.2
below. We make the conjecture that

\Phi(x)\sim K\frac{x}{(\log x)^{3/2}},\quad\quad x\to\infty,

but this is probably hard to show.

In this paper, we prove that the aforementioned upper bound for
\Phi(x)
is sharp up to a constant.

Theorem 1.1
.

We have

\Phi(x)\gg\frac{x}{(\log x)^{3/2}}.

Thus, we find the correct order of magnitude of the sum
\Phi(x)
:

\Phi(x)\asymp\frac{x}{(\log x)^{3/2}},

which answers the question of Karatsuba in the first approximation.

In addition to
\Phi(x)
, we consider the sum

F(x)=\sum_{n\leqslant x}\frac{1}{\tau(n^{2}+1)},

and establish the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

We have

F(x)\asymp\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1/2}}.

Now we discuss the main ideas of the proofs. In the sum
\Phi(x)
, for each prime
p\leqslant x
, we write
p-1
in the form
ab
, where
a
consists of prime factors not exceeding
z
, and
b
has only prime factors greater than
z
; here
z=x^{\varepsilon}
for some small fixed
\varepsilon>0
. Now
\Phi(x)
can be rewritten as

(1.2)

\Phi(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\leqslant x\\
p|a\Rightarrow p\leqslant z\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\tau(a)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\leqslant\frac{x-1}{a}\\
p|b\Rightarrow p>z\\
ab+1\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\tau(b)},

and, since
\tau(b)=O_{\varepsilon}(1)
, we have

\Phi(x)\gg_{\varepsilon}\sum_{a\leqslant x^{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{\tau(a)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\leqslant\frac{x-1}{a}\\
p|b\Rightarrow p>x^{\varepsilon}\\
ab+1\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}1.

Using the Brun-Hooley sieve, one can estimate the inner sum from below by a quantity of order

\frac{x}{a(\log x)^{2}}-R(x;a),

where the contribution of
R(x;a)
to the outer sum will be negligible. Combining these estimates, we see that

\Phi(x)\gg_{\varepsilon}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{2}}\sum_{a\leqslant x^{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{a\tau(a)}\gg_{\varepsilon}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{3/2}},

which gives the desired lower bound. Let us note that this argument does not yield any upper bound for
\Phi(x)
, since estimation of the contribution of numbers
a>x^{1-\varepsilon}
to (
1.2
) is actually equivalent to the initial problem.

The upper bound for
F(x)
follows from Theorem 1 of
[
8
]
. We also note that the same upper bound can be derived from the inequality
\tau(n)\geqslant 2^{\omega(n)}
(here
\omega(n)
denotes the number of distinct prime divisors of
n
) and a bound for the number of
n\leqslant x
with a given value of
\omega(n^{2}+1)
. For completeness, we provide this argument.

We observe that the methods of this work can also be applied for other functions similar to
\tau(n)
. For instance, let
\tau_{k}(n)
be the generalized divisor function,
\tau_{k}(n)=\sum_{n=d_{1}d_{2}\ldots d_{k}}1
; then one can show that

\sum_{p\leqslant x}\frac{1}{\tau_{k}(p-1)}\asymp_{k}x(\log x)^{\frac{1}{k}-2}.

2.
Notation

By
\varphi(n)=\#\{k\leqslant n:(k,n)=1\}
we denote the Euler totient function, and we use
P^{+}(n)
and
P^{-}(n)
for the least and the greatest prime divisors of a number
n>1
respectively; by convention,
P^{+}(1)=0
and
P^{-}(1)=\infty
. By
\pi(x)
we denote the number of primes up to
x
, and
\pi(x;q,a)
stands for the number of primes up to
x
belonging to the arithmetic progression
a\allowbreak\mkern 7.0mu({\operator@font mod}\,\,q)
; we also set
R(x;q,a)=\pi(x;q,a)-\frac{\pi(x)}{\varphi(q)}
. The notation
f(x)\ll g(x)
, as well as
f(x)=O(g(x))
, means that
|f(x)|\leqslant Cg(x)
for some absolute constant
C>0
and all possible values of
x
. We write
f(x)\asymp g(x)
, if
f(x)\ll g(x)\ll f(x)
, and write
f(x)\ll_{k}g(x)
if we want to stress that the implied constant depends on
k
.

Now we recall some notation from the sieve methods. Let
\mathcal{A}
be a finite set of positive integers, and
\mathcal{P}
be a finite subset of primes. Define
P=\prod\limits_{p\in\mathcal{P}}p
and
S(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})=\#\{a\in\mathcal{A}:(a,P)=1\}
. Let also
\mathcal{A}_{d}=\#\{a\in\mathcal{A}:a\equiv 0\allowbreak\mkern 7.0mu({\operator@font mod}\,\,d)\}
. We assume that, for all
d|P
,

(2.1)

\mathcal{A}_{d}=Xg(d)+r_{d},

where
g(d)
is a multiplicative function such that
0<g(p)<1
for
p\in\mathcal{P}
and
g(p)=0
for
p\not\in\mathcal{P}
.

Further, let the set
\mathcal{P}
be divided into disjoint subsets
\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{P}_{t}
, and let
P_{j}=\prod\limits_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{j}}p
. Finally, let

\{k_{j}\}_{r=1}^{t}
be a sequence of even numbers. We set
V_{j}=\prod\limits_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{j}}(1-g(p))
,

L_{j}=\log V_{j}^{-1}
,

E=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{t}\frac{e^{L_{j}}(L_{j})^{k_{j}+1}}{(k_{j}+1)!}
,

R=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{j}|P_{j}\\
\omega(d_{j})\leqslant k_{j}\end{subarray}}|r_{d_{1}\ldots d_{t}}|
, and
R^{\prime}=\sum\limits_{l=1}^{t}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{j}|P_{j}\\
\omega(d_{j})\leqslant k_{j},j\neq l\\
\omega(d_{l})=k_{l}+1\end{subarray}}|r_{d_{1}\ldots d_{t}}|
.

3.
Auxiliary results

We need the following version of the Brun-Hooley sieve, which is due to Ford and Halberstam
[
2
]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let (
2.1
) be met. Then

S(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})\leqslant X\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}}(1-g(p))e^{E}+R

and

S(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})\geqslant X\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}}(1-g(p))(1-E)-R-R^{\prime}.

Proof.

See
[
2
]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
a\leqslant x^{1/40}
be an even positive integer and

F_{a}(x)=\#\left\{n\leqslant(x-1)/a:an+1\text{ prime}\textit{ and }P^{-}(n)>x^{{1}/{40}}\right\}.

Then, for
x\geqslant x_{0}
,

F_{a}(x)\geqslant\frac{c_{1}\pi(x)}{\varphi(a)\log x}-R_{1},

where
c_{1}>0
is an absolute constant and

0\leqslant R_{1}\leqslant\sum_{d\leqslant x^{13/40}}|R(x;ad,1)|

Remark
.

Analogous upper bounds for
F_{a}(x)
without the error term
R_{1}
can be obtained by sifting the set
\{(an+1)(n+P):n\leqslant(x-1)/a\}
, where
P=\prod_{p\leqslant z}p
.

Proof.

We apply Theorem
3.1
to the sets
\mathcal{A}=\{n\leqslant(x-1)/a:an+1\,\text{ prime}\}
and
\mathcal{P}=\{p\leqslant z\}
, where
z=x^{1/40}
(the other parameters will be chosen later). Then
F_{a}(x)=S(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})
, and, for any
d|P=\prod_{p\leqslant z}p
, we have

\mathcal{A}_{d}=\#\{k\leqslant(x-1)/(ad):adk+1\,\text{ prime}\}=\pi(x;ad,1)=\frac{\pi(x)}{\varphi(ad)}+R(x;ad,1).

Now we write a number
d|P
in the form
d=d_{1}d_{2}
, where
(d_{1},a)=1
and
d_{2}
consists only of primes which divide
a
. Then, since
\varphi(ad_{2})=ad_{2}\prod_{p|a}(1-1/p)=d_{2}\varphi(a)
, we find that

\frac{1}{\varphi(ad)}=\frac{1}{\varphi(d_{1})\varphi(ad_{2})}=\frac{1}{\varphi(d_{1})d_{2}\varphi(a)},

and, hence, the equality (
2.1
) holds with
X=\frac{\pi(x)}{\varphi(a)}
, the multiplicative function
g
defined by

g(p)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{p-1},\ \quad\text{for}\ (p,a)=1,\\
\frac{1}{p},\,\quad\quad\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and

r_{d}=R(x;ad,1).

Besides, we have

\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}}(1-g(p))\asymp\frac{1}{\log x},

with an absolute implied constant.

Now we choose the partition of
\mathcal{P}
and define numbers
\{k_{j}\}_{j=1}^{t}
. Set
z_{j}=z^{2^{1-j}}
and

\mathcal{P}_{j}=\mathcal{P}\cap(z_{j+1},z_{j}],

where
t
is the unique positive integer with
z_{t+1}<2\leqslant z_{t}
. Let
k_{j}=b+2(j-1)
, where
b\geqslant 2
is even and will be chosen later. We also set

C=\prod_{p>2}\left(1+\frac{1}{p^{2}-2p}\right)\leqslant 1.52.

By
[
11
]
, Theorem 7 (see (3.26), (3.27)), for any
x>1
,

(3.1)

\frac{e^{-\gamma}}{\log x}\left(1-\frac{1}{\log^{2}x}\right)<\prod_{p\leqslant x}\left(1-1/p\right)<\frac{e^{-\gamma}}{\log x}\left(1+\frac{1}{2\log^{2}x}\right),

where
\gamma
is Euler’s constant. Hence, for any
z\geqslant\sqrt{2}
,

(3.2)

\prod_{z<p\leqslant z^{2}}(1-1/p)^{-1}\leqslant 3;

indeed, it follows from (
3.1
) for
z\geqslant 4
, and can be checked manually for
\sqrt{2}\leqslant z<4
. Thus

V_{j}^{-1}=\prod_{z_{j+1}<p\leqslant z_{j}}(1-g(p))^{-1}\leqslant C\prod_{z_{j+1}<p\leqslant z_{j}}(1-1/p)^{-1}\leqslant 3C\leqslant 5.

Hence
L_{j}=\log V_{j}^{-1}\leqslant L=\log 5
and

E=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\frac{e^{L_{j}}L_{j}^{k_{j}+1}}{(k_{j}+1)!}\leqslant e^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\frac{L^{b+2j-1}}{(b+2j-1)!}.

Now we estimate
R+R^{\prime}
. If a number
d
corresponds to a summand from
R
, we have
d=d_{1}\ldots d_{t}
with
d_{j}|{P}_{j}
and
\omega(d_{j})\leqslant k_{j}=b+2(j-1)
. Therefore,

d\leqslant z_{1}^{k_{1}}\ldots z_{t}^{k_{t}}\leqslant z^{b+(b+2)/2+(b+4)/4+...}=z^{2b+4}.

If a number
d
corresponds to a summand from
R^{\prime}
, we similarly find that
d\leqslant z^{2b+4}z=z^{2b+5}
.
Since the numbers
d
corresponding to the sums
R
and
R^{\prime}
are distinct and do not exceed
z^{2b+5}
, we see that

R+R^{\prime}\leqslant\sum_{d\leqslant z^{2b+5}}|r_{d}|.

Now we take
b=4
. Then
2b+5=13
,

E\leqslant 5\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\log 5)^{2j+3}}{(2j+3)!}\leqslant 0.48,

and

R+R^{\prime}\leqslant\sum_{d\leqslant x^{13/40}}|R(x;ad,1)|.

The claim follows.
∎

Define

\mathcal{M}=\left\{a\geqslant 1:a\mid n^{2}+1\text{ for some }n\geqslant 1\right\}.

It is well-known that
a\in\mathcal{M}
if and only if
4\nmid a
and
p\nmid a
for any
p\equiv 3\,(\text{mod}\,4)
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
2\leqslant a,z\leqslant x^{1/30}
,
a\in\mathcal{M}
and
P^{+}(a)\leqslant z
. Let

W_{a}(x,z)=\#\left\{n\leqslant x:a|(n^{2}+1)\text{ and }P^{-}\left(\frac{n^{2}+1}{a}\right)>z\right\}.

Then

W_{a}(x,z)\asymp\frac{2^{\omega(a)}}{\varphi(a)}\frac{x}{\log z}.

Proof.

Setting
\mathcal{A}=\{k:ak=n^{2}+1\mbox{ for some }n\leqslant x\}
and

\mathcal{P}=\begin{cases}\{p\leqslant z:p\equiv 1\allowbreak\mkern 7.0mu({\operator@font mod}\,\,4)\},&\text{ if $a$ is even};\\
\{2\}\cup\{p\leqslant z:p\equiv 1\allowbreak\mkern 7.0mu({\operator@font mod}\,\,4)\},&\text{ if $a$ is odd,}\end{cases}

we have
W_{a}(x,z)=S(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})
. As before, we write each
d|P=\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}}p
as
d=d_{1}d_{2}
, where
(d_{1},a)=1
and
d_{2}
consists only of primes dividing
a
.
Suppose that one of the numbers
d
and
a
is even. Then by the Chinese remainder theorem and the fact that the congruence
x^{2}+1\equiv 0\allowbreak\mkern 7.0mu({\operator@font mod}\,\,p)
has two solutions
p\equiv 1\allowbreak\mkern 7.0mu({\operator@font mod}\,\,4)
and one solution for
p=2
, we get

\mathcal{A}_{d}=\#\{n\leqslant x:n^{2}+1\equiv 0\!\!\allowbreak\mkern 7.0mu({\operator@font mod}\,\,ad)\}=\frac{x2^{\omega(ad)-1}}{ad}+O(2^{\omega(ad)})\\
=\frac{x2^{\omega(a)+\omega(d_{1})-1}}{ad_{1}d_{2}}+O(2^{\omega(ad)}).

If both
a
and
d
are odd, a similar argument shows that

\mathcal{A}_{d}=\frac{x2^{\omega(a)+\omega(d_{1})}}{ad_{1}d_{2}}+O(2^{\omega(ad)}).

In both cases we have

\mathcal{A}_{d}=\frac{x2^{\omega(a)+\omega(d_{1})-{\mathbb{I}}(2|ad)}}{ad_{1}d_{2}}+O(2^{\omega(ad)})=\frac{x2^{\omega(a)-{\mathbb{I}}(2|a)}}{a}\dfrac{2^{\omega(d_{1})-{\mathbb{I}}(2|d_{1})}}{d_{1}d_{2}}+O\left(\tau(ad)\right),

where
{\mathbb{I}}(2|l)
is equal to one if
l
is even and equals to zero otherwise. Thus we see that the condition (
2.1
) holds with
X=\frac{x2^{\omega(a)-{\mathbb{I}}(2|a)}}{a}
, the multiplicative function
g
defined on the primes from
\mathcal{P}
by

g(p)=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{p},\quad p\nmid a,\\
\frac{1}{p},\quad p|a,\end{cases}

(and also
g(2)=1/2
in the case of odd
a
), and

r_{d}=O(\tau(ad)).

It is well-known that

(3.3)

\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant x\\
p\equiv\,1(\text{mod}\,4)\end{subarray}}\left(1-\dfrac{1}{p}\right)\asymp\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\log x}},

(see
[
14
]
). Thus, in both cases we have

\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}}(1-g(p))\asymp\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P},\,p>2}(1-2/p)\prod_{p|a,\,p>2}\frac{1-1/p}{1-2/p}\asymp\frac{a}{\varphi(a)\log z}

with an absolute implied constant.

Now we choose the partition of
\mathcal{P}
and define the numbers
\{k_{j}\}_{j=1}^{t}
. We again set
z_{j}=z^{2^{1-j}}
and

\mathcal{P}_{j}=\mathcal{P}\cap(z_{j+1},z_{j}],

where
t
is a unique positive integer such that
z_{t+1}<2\leqslant z_{t}
and
k_{j}=b+2(j-1)
with some even
b\geqslant 2
. Now (
3.2
) implies that

V_{j}^{-1}=\prod_{z_{j+1}<p\leqslant z_{j}}(1-g(p))^{-1}\leqslant 2\prod_{z_{j+1}<p\leqslant z_{j},\,p\neq 2}(1-2/p)^{-1}\\
=2\prod_{z_{j+1}<p\leqslant z_{j},\,p\neq 2}(1-1/p)^{-2}\frac{(1-1/p)^{2}}{(1-2/p)}\leqslant 18C\leqslant 28.

Therefore,
L_{j}=\log V_{j}^{-1}\leqslant L=\log 28
and

E=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\frac{e^{L_{j}}L_{j}^{k_{j}+1}}{(k_{j}+1)!}\leqslant e^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\frac{L^{b+2j-1}}{(b+2j-1)!}.

Now we take
b=10
; then
E\leqslant 0.43
and
2b+5=25
. As in the proof of Lemma
3.2
, we get

R+R^{\prime}\leqslant\sum_{d\leqslant z^{2b+5}}|r_{d}|\ll\tau(a)\sum_{d\leqslant z^{25}}\tau(d)\ll x^{1/30}z^{25}\ll x^{26/30},

and the main term is at least

X\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}}(1-g(p))\asymp\frac{x2^{\omega(a)}}{\varphi(a)\log z}\gg\frac{x^{1-1/30}}{\log x}\,.

Applying Theorem
3.1
concludes the proof.
∎

The next lemma is a special case of the result of J. Tenenbaum
[
12
]
. For completeness, we provide the proof.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
R>0
be fixed. There exist positive constants
A=A(R)
and
B=B(R)
such that, for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant R\log\log x
,

\#\{n\leqslant x:\omega(n^{2}+1)=k\}\leqslant\frac{Ax(\log\log x+B)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!\log x}.

Proof.

For
m\geqslant 2
and
y\geqslant 2
, we define “the
y
-smooth part” of
m
:

d(m,y)=\max\{d|m:P^{+}(d)\leqslant y\}.

For each
n\leqslant x
, the number
n^{2}+1
can be written as the product
ab
, where

a=a(n)=\max\{d(n^{2}+1,y):d(n^{2}+1,y)\leqslant x^{1/30}\}

and
b=b(n)=(n^{2}+1)/a
; note that
(a,b)=1
. Now we set

A_{1}=\{n\leqslant x:a\leqslant x^{1/60},P^{-}(b)>x^{1/60}\mbox{ and }b>1\},

A_{2}=\{n\leqslant x:a\leqslant x^{1/60},P^{-}(b)\leqslant x^{1/60}\mbox{ and }b>1\},

and

A_{3}=\{n\leqslant x:x^{1/60}<a\leqslant x^{1/30}\mbox{ and }b>1\}.

Since the number of
n\leqslant x
for which
b(n)=1
does not exceed
x^{1/60}
, we conclude that

(3.4)

\#\{n\leqslant x:\omega(n^{2}+1)=k\}=N_{1}+N_{2}+N_{3}+O(x^{1/60}),

where

N_{i}=\#\{n\in A_{i}:\omega(n^{2}+1)=k\},\quad i=1,2,3.

First, we estimate
N_{1}
. In this case
1\leqslant\omega(b)\leqslant 120
; by Lemma
3.3
, we have

(3.5)

N_{1}\leqslant\sum_{l=k-120}^{k-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\leqslant x^{1/60},\,a\in{\mathcal{M}}\\
\omega(a)=l\end{subarray}}W_{a}(x,x^{1/60})\ll\sum_{l=k-120}^{k-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\leqslant x^{1/60},\,a\in{\mathcal{M}}\\
\omega(a)=l\end{subarray}}\frac{x2^{\omega(a)}}{\varphi(a)\log x}\\
\leqslant\frac{x}{\log x}\sum_{l=k-120}^{k-1}2^{l}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\leqslant x^{1/60},\,a\in{\mathcal{M}}\\
\omega(a)=l\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\varphi(a)}.

Taking the logarithms of both sides of (
3.3
), we get

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant x\\
p\equiv 1(\text{mod}\,4)\end{subarray}}\dfrac{1}{p}=\frac{1}{2}\log\log x+O(1).

Hence, for each
l\geqslant 0
, the inner sum in (
3.5
) is at most

\frac{1}{l!}\left(\sum_{p\leqslant x^{1/60},\,p\nequiv 3\!\!\allowbreak\mkern 7.0mu({\operator@font mod}\,\,4)}\frac{1}{\varphi(p)}+\frac{1}{\varphi(p^{2})}+...\right)^{l}\leqslant\frac{1}{l!}\left(0.5\log\log x+B_{1}\right)^{l}

for some absolute constant
B_{1}>0
. Thus

(3.6)

N_{1}\ll\frac{x(\log\log x+B_{1})^{k-1}}{(k-1)!\log x}\left(1+R+...+R^{120}\right).

Now we estimate
N_{2}
. Let
p=P^{-}(b)
, and let
r
be the largest integer such that
p^{r}|b
. Then, by definition of
a
, we have
ap^{r}>x^{1/30}
. Thus, for any
n\in A_{2}
we have
p^{r}>x^{1/60}
, and since
p\leqslant x^{1/60}
, we see that
r\geqslant 2
. Let
\nu=\min\{u\geqslant 1:p^{u}>x^{1/60}\}
. Then
2\leqslant\nu\leqslant r
and
p^{\nu-1}\leqslant x^{1/60}
. Hence,
p^{\nu}\leqslant x^{1/60}p\leqslant x^{1/30}
. We conclude that, for each
n\in A_{2}
, the number
n^{2}+1
is divided by
p^{\nu}
, where
p
is a prime such that
x^{1/60}<p^{\nu}\leqslant x^{1/30}
and
\nu\geqslant 2
. Therefore,
