Inverting the wedge map and Gauss composition
Kok Seng Chua

Abstract
. Let 1 ≤ k ≤ n , 1\leq k\leq n, and let v 1 , … , v k v_{1},\ldots,v_{k} be integral vectors in ℤ n {\mathbb{Z}}^{n} . We consider the wedge map α n , k : ( ℤ n ) k / S L k ( ℤ ) → ∧ k ( ℤ n ) \alpha_{n,k}:({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})^{k}/SL_{k}({\mathbb{Z}})\rightarrow\wedge^{k}({\mathbb{Z}}^{n}) , ( v 1 , … , v k ) → v 1 ∧ ⋯ ∧ v k (v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\rightarrow v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k} . In his Disquisitiones, Gauss proved that α n , 2 \alpha_{n,2} is injective when restricted to a primitive system of vectors when defining his composition law for binary quadratic forms. He also gave an algorithm for inverting α 3 , 2 \alpha_{3,2} in a different context on the representation of integers by ternary quadratic forms. We give here an explicit algorithm for inverting α n , 2 \alpha_{n,2} , and observe via Bhargava’s composition law for ℤ 2 ⊗ ℤ 2 ⊗ ℤ 2 {\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\otimes{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\otimes{\mathbb{Z}}^{2} cube that inverting α 4 , 2 \alpha_{4,2} is the main algorithmic step in Gauss’s composition law for binary quadratic forms. This places Gauss’s composition as a special case of the geometric problem of inverting a wedge map which may be of independent interests. We also show that a given symmetric positive definite matrix A A induces a natural metric on the integral Grassmannian G n , k ​ ( ℤ ) G_{n,k}({\mathbb{Z}}) so that the map X → X T ​ A ​ X X\rightarrow X^{T}AX becomes norm preserving.

1.
Introduction

Let
1\leq k\leq n
and
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, consider the

k
-wedge product map

X=(v_{1},\cdots,v_{k})\rightarrow Y=v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k}\in\wedge^{k}{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{n_{k}},\;n_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}n\cr k\end{pmatrix}

where for the index
I=i_{1}\cdots i_{k},\;\;1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n,
the
I
th Plücker coordinate of
Y
is
given by

(1.1)

Y_{I}=\det\begin{pmatrix}(v_{1})_{i_{1}}&\cdots(v_{k})_{i_{1}}\cr...&...\cr(v_{1})_{i_{k}}&\cdots(v_{k})_{i_{k}}\end{pmatrix}.

Since for any
k\times k

matrix
A=(a_{ij})
, we have

(1.2)

(\sum_{j}a_{1j}v_{j})\wedge\cdots\wedge(\sum_{j}a_{kj}v_{j})=(\det A)\;\;v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k},

the wedge map descends to a
map of the orbits of
({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})^{k}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{nk}
(identifying
X

with a
n\times k
integral matrix) under right multiplication by

SL_{k}({\mathbb{Z}})
, and writing
G_{n,k}({\mathbb{Z}}):=Im(\alpha_{n,k})
,

(1.3)

\alpha_{n,k}:({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})^{k}/SL_{k}({\mathbb{Z}})\rightarrow G_{n,k}({\mathbb{Z}})\subset\wedge^{k}{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}.

In general
\alpha_{n,k}
is not one-one since for example, we have

(1,2,2)^{T}\wedge(1,0,4)^{T}=(0,1,-1)^{T}\wedge(2,0,8)^{T}=(8,2,-2)^{T},

and the two pairs of bi-vectors are not
SL_{2}
equivalent, but note
that the vector
(8,2,-2)^{T}
is not primitive. In general we will
say that a set of
k
vectors
v_{1},\cdots,v_{k}
in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
forms a
primitive system if the gcd of the
n\choose k
components of
v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k}
is one.

In his Disquisitiones (
[
6
]
Art. 234), where he defined his
composition law for binary quadratic forms, Gauss proved that the restriction of

\alpha_{n,k}
to primitive systems of vectors, which we denote by
\alpha_{n,k}^{*}
is injective in
the case
k=2
. This actually holds for all
k
and we prove the
case
k=3
in Lemma 3.4 below which constructs an explicit
SL_{3}

transformation relating any two pre-images in
({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})^{3}
. The
construction generalizes obviously for arbitrary
k
. Note that
geometrically this just says that the ”Plücker embedding” is
indeed an embedding over Spec
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

In his work on the representation of integers by ternary quadratic forms (
[
6
]
Art.279), Gauss gave an
algorithm for inverting
\alpha_{3,2}
. This is used in the
construction of a
correspondence between the representation of a binary quadratic form by the
adjoint of a ternary quadratic form and the representation of the determinant of
the binary form by the ternary form. This led him to his famous result
expressing the number of representations of an integer as a sum of
3 squares in term of class numbers. Inverting

\alpha_{n,n-1}
is algorithmically the computation of the kernel of a
rank one integral matrix which may be effected by the Hermite normal form algorithm (see section 2.2).

In general
G_{n,k}({\mathbb{Z}})=Im(\alpha_{n,k})
is only a proper subset
of
\wedge^{k}{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
when
1<k<n-1
, but a simple parameter count (see
comment after corollary 3.5) shows that
G_{n,k}({\mathbb{Z}})
lies inside a
subvariety of codimension

d_{n,k}:=\begin{pmatrix}n\cr k\end{pmatrix}-(kn-(k^{2}-1))
of
\wedge^{k}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. This is just the
codimension of the Grassmannian in its Plücker embedding.
Clearly,
d_{n,k}=d_{n,n-k}=0
for
k=1,n-1
and
d_{n,k}=1
occurs
only once for
n=4,k=2
. This is perhaps the most interesting case
since the single relation allows one to impose a group law, and this
is exactly Gauss’s composition law for binary quadratic forms or
more generally Bhargava’s
[
1
]
composition law for
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\otimes{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\otimes{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
cubes. Indeed the
invariance of the discriminants of the binary forms
Q_{i}^{A},i=1,2,3
attached to a Bhargava’s

2\times 2\times 2
cube
A
is
equivalent to
Im(\alpha_{4,2})
lying on the Pfaffian
subvariety

(1.4)

X_{12}X_{34}-X_{13}X_{24}+X_{14}X_{23}=0,

of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{6}
.

So the algorithmic problem of
given any two binary quadratic forms
Q_{i}(x,y),i\in\{2,3\}
of the same
discriminant, to find a Bhargava’s cube
A
so that
Q_{i}^{A}=Q_{i}
(see
section 3.2) is equivalent to inverting
\alpha_{4,2}
. Both D. Shanks
(see
[
9
]
F. Lemmermeyer ) and
[
11
]
Alf van der Poorten have considered
this algorithmic problem and
[
11
]
also observed that it is
equivalent to inverting
\alpha_{4,2}
. In section 3.1 (Theorem
3.2), we give an explicit
algorithm for inverting
\alpha_{n,2}
which involves only a
single GCD calculation and for general
n
, and this appears to be new.
By specializing, it gives rise to an explicit
composition law for
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\otimes{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\otimes{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
cube and in particular recovers an old explicit rule for composing binary quadratic forms given
by Arndt (see Theorem 3.3).

As Bhargava
[
1
]
showed, the above gives an algorithm for
composing
2\times 2\times 2
cubes (Theorem 2
[
1
]
) which
additionally gives rise to algorithms for four new composition
laws.

In section 4, we show that Gauss’s argument on inverting

\alpha_{3,2}
may be generalized so that any given symmetric
positive definite matrix
A
induces a natural metric on

G_{n,k}({\mathbb{Z}})
so that the map
X\rightarrow X^{T}AX
becomes norm
preserving, in the sense that an
X
of
\hat{A}
norm
m
is mapped
to a
k-ary
form of determinant
m
.

2.
Duality and Inverting
\alpha_{n,n-1}

2.1.
Notations and Duality

We will write
\wedge^{k}{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
for the products of

\begin{pmatrix}n\cr k\end{pmatrix}
copies of
{\mathbb{Z}}
indexed by

I=i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}
with
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n
and
we let
|I|=k
,
||I||=i_{1}+\cdots+i_{k}
. We also write

G_{n,k}({\mathbb{Z}}):=Im(\alpha_{n,k})\subset\wedge^{k}{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
for
the subset of vectors expressible as
Y=v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k}
.

There is a map

\wedge^{k}{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\rightarrow\wedge^{n-k}{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}:Y\rightarrow\hat{Y},\;\;\;\hat{Y}_{I}=(-1)^{||\hat{I}||}Y_{\hat{I}},

where for any index
I
,

\hat{I}=J=j_{1}\ldots j_{n-k}
is the dual index with
I\cup J=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
j_{i}
increasing. Note that
\hat{\hat{I}}=I

but
\hat{\hat{Y}}=(-1)^{(n(n+1))/2}Y
. We also write
gcd(Y)
for

gcd\{Y_{I}:|I|=k\}
. We will always identify an
X=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\in({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})^{k}
with the
n\times k
matrix
X=[v_{1}\cdots v_{k}]
and
we will write
X_{I}
for its
I
-th Plücker coordinate as given
in (1.1). We will say that
X
is primitive system if

gcd_{I}(X_{I})=1
and we note that this is equivalent to

\alpha_{n,k}(X)
is a primitive vector in
\wedge^{k}{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
in the
usual sense. Note that a necessary and sufficient condition for
primitivity of
X
is that it should be
extendable to a
GL_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
matrix (see
[
12
]
).

By the Cauchy-Binet identity
[
4
]
, we have a semidefinite
pairing on
({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})^{k}
given by

(2.1)

<X,Y>:=\det(X^{T}Y)=\sum_{|I|=k}X_{I}Y_{I}.

The semidefiniteness follows from a Pythagoras theorem :

||X||^{2}:=\det(X^{T}X)=\sum_{|I|=k}(X_{I})^{2},

which says that the square of the volume of the parallelepiped
spanned by
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is the sum
of squares of the volume of its projections on the coordinate
k
-
planes. Moreover we have

<X,\hat{Y}>=<\hat{X},Y>

for
X\in({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})^{k},Y\in({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})^{n-k}
, though there is no linearity so that
<.,.>
is
only a semi-metric.

If in (2.1),
\alpha_{n,k}(X)=v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k}\in G_{n,k}({\mathbb{Z}})
and
\alpha_{n,n-k}(Y)=w_{1}\wedge w_{2}\cdots\wedge w_{n-k}\in G_{n,k}({\mathbb{Z}}),
are decomposable vectors of complementary
dimensions then

(2.2)

<X,\hat{Y}>=\det([v_{1},\ldots,v_{k},w_{1},\ldots,w_{n-k}])=X\wedge Y,

by
the generalized column expansion of determinant.

2.2.
Inverting
\alpha_{n,n-1}

We will consider algorithms for inverting
\alpha_{n,k}
, by which
we mean finding any one of its pre-images. Note that by Lemma 3.4 we
can get all the images via an
SL_{k}
transformation once we have
found one.

We consider first the co-dimension one case.
Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n-1}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, by (2.2),

<v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n-1},\hat{v_{j}}>=\det([v_{1},\cdots v_{n-1},v_{j}])=0,

for
1\leq j\leq n-1
. This says
v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n-1}
is orthogonal to
all the
v_{j}
. So given
0\neq v\in\wedge^{n-1}{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{n},gcd(v)=1
, the algorithm for inverting
\alpha_{n,n-1}
is to
first find an integral basis
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n-1}\}
for the
rank-one system
v^{T}x=0
and we will have
\alpha_{n,n-1}(v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n-1})=v
. If
gcd(v)=\lambda
, one simply replaces

v_{1}
by
\lambda v_{1}
. So the problem reduces to computing the
kernel of a single row integral matrix.

Computing a basis of the kernel of an integral matrix
A
can be
effected by the well known algorithm for computing Hermite normal
form. We transform
A
to HNF :
H=AU
where
U\in GL_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
,
then the columns of
U
corresponding to zero columns of
H
gives
a basis (see for example,
[
3
]
Proposition 2.4.9). In PARI,
all information can be read off by a single command

a=mathnf(A,2)
.

The above algorithm for inverting
\alpha_{n,n-1}
should be
viewed as an algorithm for inverting
\alpha_{n,n-k}
given that
one can invert
\alpha_{n,k}
as given by the next lemma .

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
0<k<n-k
and given
w_{1},\cdots,w_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}

such that
W=w_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge w_{k}\in\wedge^{k}({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})

is not the zero vector. Then one can find vectors
v_{1},\cdots,v_{n-k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
with
v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n-k}=\hat{W}
.

Proof.

We can clearly assume
gcd(W)=1
as before. We
compute a basis
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n-k}
for the kernel of the
k\times n
matrix with the
w_{i}
as rows by a HNF algorithm so that

w_{i}\cdot v_{j}=0,i=1,\ldots,k,j=1,\ldots,n-k,\;gcd(v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n-k})=1
. By (2.2),
both
\hat{W}
and
V=v_{1}\wedge...\wedge v_{n-k}
are orthogonal to the adjoint of

v_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{i_{k}},\;(v_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{i_{k-1}})\wedge w_{j_{1}},\;(v_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{i_{k-2}})\wedge(w_{j_{1}}\wedge w_{j_{2}}),\cdots,v_{i_{1}}\wedge(w_{j_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge w_{j_{k-1}})

which spans a space of codimension

\begin{pmatrix}n\cr n-k\end{pmatrix}-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\begin{pmatrix}n-k\cr k-j\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}k\cr j\end{pmatrix}=1
in
\wedge^{k}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

so that
\hat{W}=\pm v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n-k}
since both
have components with gcd 1.
∎

Since inverting
\alpha_{n,1}
is trivial, we get the algorithm
for inverting
\alpha_{n,n-1}
above. For
k>1
one needs to first
determine which points in
\wedge^{k}{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
are decomposable and a
separate algorithm is needed. We will do this first for
k=2

which tells us how to generalize to arbitrary
k
.

3.
Inverting
\alpha_{n,2}
and constructing Bhargava’s
cube

3.1.
Inverting
\alpha_{n,2}

If
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})^{T},y=(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4})^{T}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{4}
and

X=x\wedge y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{6}
, a simple calculation shows that

X_{12}X_{34}+X_{14}X_{23}=X_{13}X_{24}.

It follows that
G_{n,2}({\mathbb{Z}})
must lie in the intersections given
by the Plücker relations

(3.1)

X_{ij}X_{kl}-X_{ik}X_{jl}+X_{il}X_{jk}=0,\;\;\;1\leq i<j<k<l\leq n,

of
\wedge^{2}{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
. There are
\begin{pmatrix}n\cr 4\end{pmatrix}
relations in (3.1), but they are clearly not independent. Given any non-zero
X
(say with
X_{12}\neq 0)
, we will show that (theorem 3.2 below) we can find
x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
with
x\wedge y=X
using only those relations where

X_{12}
occurs :

(3.2)

X_{12}X_{kl}-X_{1k}X_{2l}+X_{1l}X_{2k}=0,\;\;\;3\leq k<l\leq n.

We note that this gives exactly
\begin{pmatrix}n-2\cr 2\end{pmatrix}
independent relations, which agree with the codimension
d_{n,2}
.

We will need first a Lemma from D. Cox
(
[
5
]
, Lemma 3.5) who may have anticipated the geometric view as he stated his result
in the exact determinantal form that we need.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{r})^{T},q=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{r})^{T}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}
and
y
be an integer such that
gcd(y,p_{1},\ldots,p_{r})=1
. Then the congruence
px\equiv q
mod
y
has a
unique solution mod
y
if and only if
p\wedge q=0
mod
y
.

We note that moreover
x
in the above is easily computable by a
single GCD computation. Let
L\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r},\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}
be such that

L\cdot p+\ell y=1
. Then
L\cdot p\equiv 1
mod
y
, so we
have

(3.3)

x\equiv L\cdot q\;\;mod\;\;y.

Theorem 3.2
.

The Plücker relations (3.1) defined the integral Grassmannian

G_{n,2}({\mathbb{Z}})=Im(\alpha_{n,2})
in the Plücker embedding
in
\wedge^{2}{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
. There is an explicit algorithm
(involving only a single GCD computation) for finding a pre-image of
any
0\neq X\in G_{n,2}({\mathbb{Z}})
using only
d_{n,2}
of the
relations
containing a particular
X_{ij}
where
X_{ij}\neq 0
.

Proof.

Let
X\in Im(\alpha_{n,2})
, the relations (3.1) are
clearly necessary. Without loss of generality we may assume that

X_{12}\neq 0
, and we will find
x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
with
x\wedge y=X
. By the
SL_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
invariance, if we have a solution, we can bring the first two
rows of
[x,y]
into Hermite normal form, so we can expect to
find
x,y
with
y_{1}=0,x_{1}y_{2}=X_{12}
and
|x_{2}|<|y_{2}|
which
leads us to the following algorithm.

We first compute integers
\lambda_{j}
(and this is the only computation
needed) such that

(3.4)

x_{1}=gcd(X_{12},\ldots,X_{1n})=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\lambda_{j}X_{1j},

and set

y_{1}=0,\;\;\;y_{j}=\frac{X_{1j}}{x_{1}},j=2,\ldots,n.

We then let
p=(y_{3},\ldots y_{n})^{T},q=(X_{23},\ldots,X_{2n})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n-2}
. Since

gcd(y_{2},y_{3},\ldots,y_{n})=gcd(X_{12}/x_{1},\ldots,X_{1n}/x_{1})=1
, and by (3.2) for
3\leq k<l\leq n
,

p_{k}q_{l}-q_{k}p_{l}=\frac{X_{1k}X_{2l}-X_{1l}X_{2k}}{x_{1}}=\frac{-X_{12}X_{kl}}{x_{1}}=-y_{2}X_{kl},

so we have
p\wedge q\equiv 0
mod
y_{2}
.
By Lemma
3.1 there is a unique
x_{2}
mod
y_{2}
such that

x_{2}y_{j}\equiv X_{2j}\;\;mod\;\;y_{2},j=3,\ldots n,

which by (3.3) and the above, is given explicitly by

(3.5)

x_{2}=\sum_{j=3}^{n}\lambda_{j}X_{2j}\;\;mod\;\;y_{2},

where the
\lambda_{j}
is computed earlier in (3.4)
so that

(3.6)

x_{j}:=\frac{x_{2}y_{j}-X_{2j}}{y_{2}}\in{\mathbb{Z}},\;\;j=3,\ldots,n.

We have thus found vectors
x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
with

(3.7)

X_{ij}=x_{i}y_{j}-x_{j}y_{i},1\leq i<j\leq n,i\leq 2.

It then follows from (3.2) and (3.7) that for
2<i<j\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle X_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{X_{1i}X_{2j}-X_{1j}X_{2i}}{X_{12}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(x_{1}y_{i}-y_{1}x_{i})(x_{2}y_{j}-y_{2}x_{j})-(x_{1}y_{j}-y_{1}x_{j})(x_{2}y_{i}-y_{2}x_{i}))}{x_{1}y_{2}-y_{1}x_{2}}=x_{i}y_{j}-y_{i}x_{j}.

So we have found
x,y
with
X=x\wedge y
using only (3.2), but
(3.1) must now holds since each
X_{ij}=x_{i}y_{j}-x_{j}y_{i}
.
∎

3.2.
Constructing Bhargava’s cube by inverting

\alpha_{4,2}

We now show via Bhargava’s
[
1
]
composition law
on
2\times 2\times 2
cubes that inverting
\alpha_{4,2}
is the
main algorithmic step in Gauss’s composition law for binary
quadratic forms. This seems to give an interesting geometric and
simple way to view this important algorithm.

Let
A
be a Bhargava’s
[
1
]

2\times 2\times 2
cube of
integers, which is an optimally symmetric description. We will fix
one of the three (say the front/back) slicing and let

(3.8)

M_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x_{2}\cr x_{3}&x_{4}\end{pmatrix},\;\;N_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}&y_{2}\cr y_{3}&y_{4}\end{pmatrix},

be the front and back faces. We also have

(3.9)

\displaystyle M_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x_{3}\cr y_{1}&y_{3}\end{pmatrix},\;\;

\displaystyle N_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{2}&x_{4}\cr y_{2}&y_{4}\end{pmatrix},

(3.10)

\displaystyle M_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&y_{1}\cr x_{2}&y_{2}\end{pmatrix},\;\;

\displaystyle N_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{3}&y_{3}\cr x_{4}&y_{4}\end{pmatrix},

corresponding to the left/right, top/bottom faces. The
associated binary quadratic forms are

Q_{i}^{A}(x,y)=-\det(M_{i}x-N_{i}y),\;\;i=1,2,3,

and these sum to zero in the class group.

Corresponding to our slicing are two vectors

x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})^{T},y=(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4})^{T}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{4}
and their
wedge product
X=x\wedge y
with components
X_{ij}=\det\begin{pmatrix}x_{i}&y_{i}\cr x_{j}&y_{j}\end{pmatrix}
satisfying (1.4). A
calculation gives

(3.11)

Q_{1}^{A}=(-\det M_{1},\det\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x_{2}\cr y_{3}&y_{4}\end{pmatrix}-\det\begin{pmatrix}x_{3}&x_{4}\cr y_{1}&y_{2}\end{pmatrix},-\det N_{1}),

(3.12)

\displaystyle Q_{2}^{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-X_{13},X_{14}+X_{23},-X_{24}),

(3.13)

\displaystyle Q_{3}^{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-X_{12},X_{14}-X_{23},-X_{34}),

and we note the
invariance of
Disc(Q_{i}^{A})
is equivalent to (1.4).

Suppose now we are given two primitive binary quadratic forms

Q_{2}=(a_{2},b_{2},c_{2}),Q_{3}=(a_{3},b_{3},c_{3})
of the same discriminant, then
the vector

(3.14)

X=\begin{pmatrix}X_{12}\cr X_{13}\cr X_{14}\cr X_{23}\cr X_{24}\cr X_{34}\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}-a_{3}\cr-a_{2}\cr(b_{2}+b_{3})/2\cr(b_{2}-b_{3})/2\cr-c_{2}\cr-c_{3}\end{pmatrix}\in Im(\alpha_{4,2}),

since
X
satisfies (1.4) by the equality of the discriminants.
Furthermore by the primitivity of
Q_{2},Q_{3}
,
X
is primitive
(
gcd(X)=1
).
By our algorithm for inverting
\alpha_{4,2}
, we can find
x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{4}
with
x\wedge y=X
. We can now construct a
2\times 2\times 2
cube
A

with
x,y
as the front/back face and we get
