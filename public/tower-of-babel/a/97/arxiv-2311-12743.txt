Insider trading with penalties, entropy and quadratic BSDEs
Umut Çetin

Abstract
. Kyle model in continuous time where the insider may be subject to legal penalties is considered. In equilibrium the insider internalises this legal risk by trading less aggressively. The equilibrium is characterised via the solution of a backward stochastic differential equation (BSDE) whose terminal condition is determined as the fixed point of a non-linear operator in equilibrium. The insider’s expected penalties in equilibrium is non-monotone in the fee structure and is given by the relative entropy of the law of a particular h h -transformation of Brownian motion.



1.
Introduction

Kyle
[
14

(together with its formulation in continuous time by Back
[
1

) is the canonical model for analysing the dissemination of private information into prices in financial markets. In a market consisting of noise traders, competitive market makers and an informed trader (insider), it predicts that the insider gradually reveals all her private information to the market in equilibrium. At the end of the finite horizon, the prices become fully informative in the sense that there is no remaining uncertainty about the private valuation of the insider.

The Kyle model does not distinguish between legal informed trading, i.e. the investor paying a fee to obtain private information typically as a result of fundamental research, and insider trading when the trader receives private information from an insider via illegal means. As there is no legal penalties in the Kyle model, both types of informed traders behave identically.

Incorporation of legal risk into the Kyle model is a relatively less studied extension. The previous works in the literature typically assume a one period setting (see, e.g., Shin

Remark 1
.

The square integrability condition on
H
is standard in the literature on the Kyle-Back models. The new condition on
\alpha
is almost redundant since the expected transaction costs become infinite when it is not satisfied. However, it could be possible for the insider to manipulate the prices so that the gains from trading
\int_{0}^{1}(V-S_{t})\alpha_{t}dt
is bigger than the loss. The extra condition prevents such doubling strategies.

On the other hand, the total wealth at the end of trading is given by

\int_{0}^{1}(V-H(t,Y_{t}))\alpha_{t}dt-\frac{c}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\alpha^{2}_{t}dt\leq\int_{0}^{1}(V-H(t,Y_{t}))^{2}dt+\frac{1-c}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\alpha_{t}^{2}dt.

Thus, if the penalties are high enough, i.e.
c>1
, the insider will never use strategies that are not square integrable if the prices are. In such case, the condition on
\alpha
is indeed redundant.

3.
A recipe for equilibrium

Since the market maker’s pricing rule leads to the martingale property of
S
and one typically expects the insider’s strategy to be inconspicuous, i.e.
Y
is a Brownian motion in its own filtration, in view of the past literature, it is not unreasonable to restrict oneself to
H
satisfying a heat equation:

H_{t}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}H_{yy}=0,\qquad t\in[0,1].

(3.10)

Keeping the above equation in mind a formal HJB equation for the value function of the insider will be obtained in the first part of this section. To this end define

J(t,y)=\sup_{\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}(H)}E^{0,v}\bigg[\int_{t}^{1}(v-H(u,Y_{u}))\alpha_{u}du-\frac{c}{2}\int_{t}^{1}\alpha^{2}_{t}dt\Big|Y_{t}=y\bigg].

Direct calculations lead to

J_{t}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}J_{yy}+\sup_{\alpha}\Big\{\alpha(J_{y}+v-H)-\frac{c\alpha^{2}}{2}\Big\}=0.

Note that the presence of the penalty factor
c>0
yields that the optimal
\alpha
can be obtained in the feedback form:

\alpha^{*}=\frac{J_{y}(t,y)+v-H(t,y)}{c}.

(3.11)

This leads to the following HJB equation:

J_{t}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}J_{yy}+\frac{(J_{y}+v-H)^{2}}{2c}=0.

(3.12)

Recall that in the classical Kyle model
c=0
, i.e. no penalty, the optimal strategy cannot be obtained in the feedback form from the HJB equation and instead one has
J_{y}=H-z
, which is one should expect when letting
c\rightarrow 0
in (
3.11
) to arrive at a finite limit (see Section 6.2 in
[
9
]
).

Next suppose there exists a smooth function
J^{0}
such that

\begin{split}J^{0}_{t}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}J^{0}_{yy}&=0,\\
J^{0}_{y}&=H-v,\\
J^{0}(1,y)&=\int_{h^{-1}(v)}^{y}(h(x)-v)dx+\mbox{ constant},\end{split}

(3.13)

for some
h
to be determined and a constant possibly depending on
v
. Such a function exists and is the value function of the insider in the classical Kyle model with
c=0
whenever the pricing rule (not necessarily the equilibrium one) satisfies (
3.10
) (see Theorem 5.1 in
[
10
]
). In this case,
h=H(1,\cdot)
and the constant is zero.

Thus, if one defines
u=J-J^{0}
and conjectures that
J(1,\cdot)\equiv 0
, one obtains

u_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}u_{yy}+\frac{1}{2c}u_{y}^{2}=0,\quad u(1,y)=-j^{0}(y,v):=-J^{0}(1,y).

(3.14)

Note that
J^{0}
would be the value function of the insider had there been no penalties on her trading strategies, i.e.
c=0
. Thus,
-u
can be viewed as the expected additional transaction costs (or legal penalties) to the insider in equilibrium.

It is now a common folklore that the above has a backward stochastic differential equation (BSDE) formulation:

dU_{t}=\sigma Z_{t}dB_{t}-\frac{1}{2c}Z_{t}^{2}dt,\quad U_{1}=u(1,\sigma B_{1}).

(3.15)

The above quadratic BSDE is known to have an explicit solution, which in turn yields

u(t,x)=c\sigma^{2}\log\rho(t,x,v),\quad\rho(t,x,v):=E^{0,v}\Big[\exp\Big(-\frac{j^{0}(\sigma B_{1},v)}{c\sigma^{2}}\Big)\Big|\sigma B_{t}=x\Big].

(3.16)

It immediately follows from Theorem 4.3.6 in
[
13
]
that for each
v\in f(\mathbb{R})
,
\rho(\cdot,\cdot,v)\in C^{1,2}([0,1)\times\mathbb{R}])
..

In view of (
3.11
) and (
3.13
), the above implies the optimal control of the insider at time

is
\alpha^{*}(t,Y_{t},V)
, where

\alpha^{*}(t,y,v):=\sigma^{2}\frac{\rho_{y}(t,y,v)}{\rho(t,y,v)}.

(3.17)

Recall that the above assumed that the pricing rules satisfied (
3.10
). Combined with the requirement that prices follow martingales in market makers’ filtration in equilibrium this in particular yields that
Y
must be a Brownian motion in its own filtration. Observe that if
\kappa(t)\rho(t,Y_{t},v)\Pi(dv)=\mathbb{P}(V\in dv|{\mathcal{F}}^{Y}_{t})
for some constant
\kappa(t)
for each
t
, then

is a Brownian motion in its own filtration (see, e.g., Corollary 3.1 in
[
9
]
) since

\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{\rho_{y}(t,Y_{t},V)}{\rho(t,Y_{t},V)}\Big|{\mathcal{F}}^{Y}_{t}\Big]=\int_{f(\mathbb{R})}\rho_{y}(t,Y_{t},v)\kappa(t)dv=\frac{d}{dy}\int_{f(\mathbb{R})}\rho(t,y,v)\kappa(t)dv\Big|_{y=Y_{t}}=0,

provided one can interchange the order of integration and differentiation.

To this end, suppose that
\rho^{0}(t,y,v):=\kappa(t)\rho(t,y,v)
is the conditional density of
V
with respect to the measure
\Pi
given
{\mathcal{F}}^{Y}_{t}

]

\rho^{0}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\rho^{0}_{yy}=0.

(3.18)

On the other hand,

\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa}\equiv 0

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose that there exists a continuous function
j^{0}:\mathbb{R}\times f(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}

\kappa\rho(0,0,\cdot)\equiv 1

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\frac{\rho_{y}(s,Y_{s},V)}{\rho(s,Y_{s},V)}\Big)^{2}\Big]<\infty,\quad t\in[0,1].

Then,

\kappa\rho(t,Y_{t},v)\Pi(dv)=\mathbb{P}(V\in dv|{\mathcal{F}}^{Y}_{t}),\qquad t\in[0,1].

This is an immediate consequence of Theorem 3.3 in

Proposition 3.2

Consider

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}Z_{t}^{2}dt\Big]<\infty,

U_{t}\geq M_{t}(v):=-E^{0,v}\big[j^{0}(B_{1},v)\big|B_{t}\big],\;P^{0,v}\mbox{-a.s..}.

Since

This yields the claim by the independence of

Remark 2

In view of the rationality of the pricing rule, i.e. (

However, this already follows from the properties of

in Step (1) alone. Indeed, since

is a proper density for each

0=\frac{d}{dy}1=\frac{d}{dy}\kappa\int_{f(\mathbb{R})}\exp\Big(-\frac{j^{0}(y,v)}{\sigma^{2}c}\Big)\Pi(dv)=\frac{\kappa}{\sigma^{2}c}\int_{f(\mathbb{R})}(v-h(y))\exp\Big(-\frac{j^{0}(y,v)}{\sigma^{2}c}\Big)\Pi(dv),

provided one can differentiate under the integral sign. Thus,

h(y)=h(y)\kappa\int_{f(\mathbb{R})}\exp\Big(-\frac{j^{0}(y,v)}{c\sigma^{2}}\Big)\Pi(dv)=\kappa\int_{f(\mathbb{R})}v\exp\Big(-\frac{j^{0}(y,v)}{c\sigma^{2}}\Big)\Pi(dv)

as required.

4.
A fixed point algorithm

The recipe from the previous section shows that the equilibrium mainly boils down to finding a function
j^{0}
with certain properties. The condition on its derivative implies

j^{0}(y,v)=\Psi(v)+\phi(y)-yv,

(4.23)

where
\psi
and
\phi
are continuous functions on
f(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathbb{R}
, respectively. This section will describe an operator on the space of continuous functions, whose fixed point will determine the functions
\psi
and
\phi
that will appear in equilibrium. Although the main theorem of this section will be proved under a stronger assumption, the condition below is sufficient for a number of important estimates.

Assumption 4.1
.

All exponential moments of
V
exist. That is,

M_{V}(r):=\int_{f(\mathbb{R})}e^{rv}\Pi(dv)<\infty,\qquad\forall r\in\mathbb{R}.

(4.24)

The following notation will also be used to ease the exposition:

Notation:

\hat{c}:=c\sigma^{2}
.

Since
\exp(-\frac{1}{\hat{c}}j^{0}(y,\cdot))\kappa
will be a probability density function itself for all

\kappa
independent of
y

by incorporating it into
\Psi
or
\phi

\int_{f(\mathbb{R})}\exp\Big(\frac{yv-\Psi(v)}{\hat{c}}\Big)\Pi(dv)=\exp\Big(\frac{\phi(y)}{\hat{c}}\Big),\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}.

The second relationship follows from the initial condition (
3.22
):

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\Big(\frac{yv-\phi(y)}{\hat{c}}\Big)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\Big(-\frac{y^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big)dy=\exp\Big(\frac{\Psi(v)}{\hat{c}}\Big),\qquad v\in f(\mathbb{R}).

Thus one obtains the following two operators on the space of measurable functions:

\begin{split}T_{1}(\phi)(v):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\Big(\frac{yv-\phi(y)}{\hat{c}}\Big)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\Big(-\frac{y^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big)dy&=\exp\Big(\frac{\Psi(v)}{\hat{c}}\Big),\qquad v\in f(\mathbb{R});\\
T_{2}(\Psi)(y):=\int_{f(\mathbb{R})}\exp\Big(\frac{yv-\Psi(v)}{\hat{c}}\Big)\Pi(dv)&=\exp\Big(\frac{\phi(y)}{\hat{c}}\Big),\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}\end{split}

(4.25)

Consequently, one can define an operator
T_{0}
on the space of measurable functions by

T_{0}\phi=\hat{c}\log T_{2}(T_{1}(\phi)),

(4.26)

whose domain consists of functions so that
T_{0}(\phi)
is finite for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
. Once this is done, one can apply a normalisation so that
\phi(0)=0
. This leads to the operator
T
defined on the domain of
T_{0}
as follows:

T\phi=\hat{c}\log\frac{T_{2}(T_{1}(\phi))}{T_{2}(T_{1}(\phi))(0)}.

(4.27)

By similar reasoning the domain
{\mathcal{D}}(T_{i})
of the operator
T_{i}
consist of functions
g
such

takes values in
(0,\infty)
. Consequently,

{\mathcal{D}}(T)={\mathcal{D}}(T_{0})=\{\phi\in{\mathcal{D}}(T_{1}):T_{1}(\phi)\in{\mathcal{D}}(T_{2})\}.

Due to the extra normalisation involved it is not immediately obvious that the fixed points of
T
and
T_{0}
are related. However, as the next proposition shows, there is a one-to-one correspondence.

Proposition 4.1
.

There exists a fixed point for
T_{0}
if and only if there exists a fixed point for
T
. In particular, any fixed point of

is a fixed point of
T_{0}
.

Proof.

By direct calculations, if
T_{0}\phi=\phi
,
T\phi_{0}=\phi_{0}
for
\phi_{0}=\phi-\phi(0)
.

Now, suppose
T\phi=\phi
. Then,
T_{2}\Psi=k\exp(\frac{\phi}{\hat{c}})
for some

, where
\Psi=\hat{c}\log T_{1}\phi
. That is,

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\Big(\frac{yv-\phi(y)-\Psi(v)}{\hat{c}}\Big)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\Big(-\frac{y^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big)dy&=1,\qquad v\in f(\mathbb{R});\\
\int_{f(\mathbb{R})}\exp\Big(\frac{yv-\Psi(v)-\phi(y)}{\hat{c}}\Big)\Pi(dv)&=k,\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}.\end{split}

Integrating both sides of the first identity with respect to
\Pi
yields

k\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\Big(-\frac{y^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big)dy=1,

in view of the second identity in the previous display and Fubini’s theorem. Thus,

and
T_{0}\phi=\phi
.
∎

Although Theorem

Assumption 5.2
.

The distribution of

In the Kyle model with Gaussian fundamental value but no transaction costs, the equilibrium price is an affine function of the demand
Y
. Thus, one expects
H^{*}(t,y)=\lambda y+\mu
for some
\lambda>0
. The conjecture that
H^{*}(t,0)=\mu
is a simple consequence of the rationality of the pricing rule that requires
\mu=\mathbb{E}[V]=S_{0}=H^{*}(0,0).
Combined with the normalisation that
\phi^{*}(0)=0
, one expects that
\phi^{*}(y)=\frac{\lambda y^{2}}{2}+\mu y
. This leads to the following:

Theorem 5.2
.

Suppose that Assumpton
5.2
holds. Then,
T_{0}\phi^{*}=\phi^{*}
with
\phi^{*}(y)=\frac{\lambda^{*}y^{2}}{2}+\mu y
for

\lambda^{*}=\frac{-c+\sqrt{c^{2}+4\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}}{2},

(5.51)

which is the positive root of
\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}+\hat{c}\lambda-\gamma^{2}=0
.
Moreover,

\Psi^{*}(v)=\hat{c}\log T_{1}\phi^{*}(v)=\frac{\hat{c}}{2}\log\frac{c}{c+\lambda^{*}}+\frac{(\mu-v)^{2}}{2(c+\lambda^{*})}.

(5.52)

Proof.

By completing the squares, it is easily seen that

T_{1}\phi^{*}(y)=\frac{\Sigma}{\sigma}\exp\Big(\frac{\Sigma^{2}(\mu-v)^{2}}{2\hat{c}^{2}}\Big),

where
\frac{1}{\Sigma^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{\lambda}{\hat{c}}=\frac{c+\lambda}{\hat{c}}
. That is,

T_{1}\phi^{*}(y)=\frac{\Sigma}{\sigma}\exp\Big(\frac{(\mu-v)^{2}}{2\hat{c}(c+\lambda)}\Big).

Thus, to have
T_{0}\phi^{*}=\phi^{*}
, one must have

\begin{split}\exp\big(\frac{\lambda y^{2}+2\mu y}{2\hat{c}}\big)&=\frac{\sigma}{\Sigma\gamma}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Big(\frac{yv}{\hat{c}}-\frac{(\mu-v)^{2}}{2\hat{c}(c+\lambda)}-\frac{(\mu-v)^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\Big)dv\\
&=\frac{\sigma\Sigma_{0}}{\Sigma\gamma}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\Sigma_{0}^{2}}}\exp\Big(\frac{yv}{\hat{c}}-\frac{(\mu-v)^{2}}{2\Sigma_{0}^{2})}\Big)dv\\
&=\frac{\sigma\Sigma_{0}}{\Sigma\gamma}\exp\Big(\frac{y\mu}{\hat{c}}+\frac{y^{2}\Sigma_{0}^{2}}{2\hat{c}^{2}}\Big),\end{split}

where
\frac{1}{\Sigma_{0}^{2}}=\frac{1}{\hat{c}(c+\lambda)}+\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}=\frac{\gamma^{2}+\hat{c}(c+\lambda)}{\gamma^{2}\hat{c}(c+\lambda)}
.

Thus,
\lambda

\lambda=\frac{\Sigma_{0}^{2}}{\hat{c}}=\frac{\gamma^{2}(c+\lambda)}{\gamma^{2}+\hat{c}(c+\lambda)}.

(5.53)

Note that one must also have

(5.54)

That is,
\lambda

5.53

\lambda c\sigma^{2}(c+\lambda)=c\gamma^{2}.

Dividing both sides by
c

5.54

\lambda

\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}+\lambda c\sigma^{2}-\gamma^{2}=0,

which is given by
\lambda^{*}

Consequently,

\Psi^{*}(v)=\frac{\hat{c}}{2}\log\frac{\Sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{(\mu-v)^{2}}{2(c+\lambda^{*})}=\frac{\hat{c}}{2}\log\frac{c}{c+\lambda^{*}}+\frac{(\mu-v)^{2}}{2(c+\lambda^{*})}.

6.
Conclusion

When there are quadratic penalties, the equilibrium in the continuous time version of the Kyle model changes drastically. The insider no longer finds optimal to bring the market prices to her own valuation at the end ot trading. The noise traders lose less on average as the potential penalties on the insider increase; however, the total welfare, i.e the sum of insider and noise profit, is not monotone in the rate of penalties when the fundamental value is normally distributed. Interestingly, the maximum expected penalty that is accrued by the insider occurs when the rate of penalties is sufficiently small.

The solution of the equilibrium reveals an interesting connection between a class of quadratic BSDEs and h-transforms. The interesting extension to equilibrium with general convex costs are left for future research. Such an extension will be valuable to regulators searching for the optimal penalty structure to control illegal insider trading as discussed by
[
7
]
in a one-period setting.
