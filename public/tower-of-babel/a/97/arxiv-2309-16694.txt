A Direct k k -Way Hypergraph Partitioning Algorithm for Optimizing the Steiner Tree Metric
Tobias Heuer

Abstract
Minimizing wire-lengths is one of the most important objectives in circuit design. The process involves initially placing the logical units (cells) of a circuit onto a physical layout, and subsequently routing the wires to connect the cells. Hypergraph partitioning (HGP) has been long used as a placement strategy in this process. However, it has been replaced by other methods due to the limitation that common HGP objective funtions only optimize wire-lengths implicitly . In this work, we present a novel HGP formulation that maps a hypergraph H H , representing a logical circuit, onto a routing layout represented by a weighted graph G G . The objective is to minimize the total length of all wires induced by the hyperedges of H H on G G . To capture wire-lengths, we compute minimal Steiner trees - a metric commonly used in routing algorithms. For this formulation, we present the first direct k k -way multilevel mapping algorithm that incorporates techniques used by the highest-quality partitioning algorithms. We contribute a greedy mapping algorithm to compute an initial solution and three refinement algorithms to improve the initial mapping: Two move-based local search heuristics (based on label propagation and the FM algorithm) and a refinement algorithm based on max-flow min-cut computations. Our experiments demonstrate that our new algorithm achieves an improvement in the Steiner tree metric by 7 % 7\% (median) on VLSI instances when compared to the best performing partitioning algorithm that optimizes the mapping in a postprocessing step. Although computing Steiner trees is an NP-hard problem, we achieve this improvement with only a 2 2 – 3 3 times slowdown in partitioning time compared to optimizing the connectivity metric.

Introduction

The design of complex integrated circuits is a key driver of innovation in modern technology.
The advancements in the field are attributed not only to progress in semiconductor technology but also to
algorithmic innovations enabling the realization of very large-scale circuits. The physical design of a modern chip
involves three stages: In modeling stage, a logical circuit is designed that consists of logical units
(cells) interconnected by wires. In the placement phase, the cells are assigned to
non-overlapping locations within a chip area. Subsequently, during the routing phase, the cells
are interconnected using well-separated wires.
There exist multiple metrics to evaluate the quality of a physical layout,
but a common approach is to minimize the total length of all wires.
This effectively reduces signal delays, power consumption, and the layout area of the chip
[
38
,
5
,
36
]
.
The placement phase plays an important role in this process as the locations of cells significantly influence
the achievable wire-lengths in the routing phase.

Algorithms based on hypergraph partitioning have been a key approach for placing cells on a chip in the past.
A hypergraph is a generalization of a graph where a
hyperedge
can connect more than two nodes.
This makes hypergraphs an appropriate model to represent logical circuits since a wire can connect an abitrary number of cells.
The
hypergraph partitioning
problem (HGP) is to partition the node set of a hypergraph into
k
blocks of
roughly the same size while simultanously minimizing an objective function defined on the hyperedges.
HGP is used to partition a logical circuit into densely-connected blocks by minimizing the number of blocks spanned by each hyperedge
(
connectivity
metric). These blocks are subsequently assigned to subregions of the layout area for which the process is repeated recursively
until the regions are small enough
[
70
,
11
,
41
]
.
An end-case placer then maps the cells to locations on the chip
[
11
]
.
In recent years, partitioning-based placement techniques have fallen out of fashion since they were outperformed by numerical methods
[
79
,
59
]
.
This can be attributed to the fact that minimizing the connectivity metric
only minimizes wire-lengths
implicitly

[
78
,
41
,
53
]
.

Preliminaries

Hypergraphs.

A
weighted hypergraph

H=(V,E,c,\omega)
is defined as a set of
n
nodes
V
and a set of
m

hyperedges
E
(also called
nets
)
with node weights
c:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and net weights
\omega:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, where each net
e
is
a subset of the node set
V
. The nodes of a net are called its
pins
.
We extend
c
and
\omega
to sets in a natural way, i.e.,
c(U):=\sum_{u\in U}c(u)
and
\omega(F):=\sum_{e\in F}\omega(e)
.
A node
u
is
incident
to a net
e
if
u\in e
.

{I}(u):=\{e\mid u\in e\}
is the set of all incident nets of
u
.
The set
{N}(u):=\{v\mid\exists e\in E:\{u,v\}\subseteq e\}
denotes the neighbors of
u
.
Two nodes
u
and
v
are
adjacent
if
v\in{N}(u)
.
The
degree
of a node
u
is
d(u):=|\mathrm{I}(u)|
.
The
size

|e|
of a net
e
is the number of its pins.
We denote the number of pins of a hypergraph with
p:=\sum_{e\in E}|e|=\sum_{v\in V}d(v)
.
Given a subset
V^{\prime}\subset V
, the
subhypergraph

H[V^{\prime}]
is defined as
H[V^{\prime}]:=(V^{\prime},\{e\cap V^{\prime}\mid e\in E:e\cap V^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\},c,\omega^{\prime})

where
\omega^{\prime}(e\cap V^{\prime})
is the weight of hyperedge
e
in
H
.

An undirected and weighted graph
G=(V,E,c,\omega)
can be considered as a hypergraph where each net contains only two pins (also called an
edge
).
We therefore use the same notation for undirected graphs.
We define the weight of an edge
e=\{u,v\}\in E
as
\omega(u,v):=\omega(e)
.

Partitions.

A
k
-way partition
of a hypergraph
H
is a partition of its node set
V
into
k
disjoint blocks

{\Pi}=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}\}
.
A
2
-way partition is also called a
bipartition
.
We denote the block to which a node
u
is assigned by
{\Pi}[u]
.
For each net
e
,
{\Phi}(e,V_{i}):=|e\cap V_{i}|
denotes the number of pins of net
e
in block
V_{i}
.
The
connectivity set

\Lambda(e):=\{V_{i}\mid{\Phi}(e,V_{i})>0\}
contains the blocks to which pins of net
e
are assigned.
Similarly,
{R}(u):=\{{\Pi}[v]\mid v\in{N}(u)\}
are the blocks to which node
u
is adjacent.
The
connectivity

\lambda(e)
of a net
e
is
\lambda(e):=|\Lambda(e)|
.
A net is called a
cut net
if
\lambda(e)>1
.
Similarly, a node
u
is a
boundary node
if
|{R}(u)|>1
.

Balanced Hypergraph Partitioning.

The
balanced hypergraph partitioning problem
(HGP) is to
find a
k
-way partition
{\Pi}
of a hypergraph
H
that minimizes an objective function defined on the hyperedges, where
each block
V^{\prime}\in{\Pi}
satisfies the
balance constraint
:
c(V^{\prime})\leq L_{\max}:=(1+\varepsilon)\lceil\frac{c(V)}{k}\rceil

for some
imbalance ratio

\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
If
{\Pi}
satisfies the balance constraint, we call
{\Pi}

\varepsilon
-balanced
.
For
k=2
, we refer to the problem as the
bipartitioning
problem.
The two most prominent objective functions are the
cut-net
metric
\mathfrak{f}_{c}({\Pi}):=\sum_{e\in E_{\text{Cut}}({\Pi})}\omega(e)

and
connectivity
metric
\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda-1}({\Pi}):=\sum_{e\in E_{\text{Cut}}({\Pi})}(\lambda(e)-1)\cdot\omega(e)

where
E_{\text{Cut}}({\Pi})
denotes the set of all cut nets.
HGP is NP-hard for both objective functions
[
50
,
27
]
.

Steiner Trees.

For a weighted graph
G=(V,E,\omega)
and
N

terminal sets

T_{1},\ldots,T_{N}\subseteq V
, the

Steiner tree packing problem
asks for
N
edge-disjoint trees
S_{1},\ldots,S_{N}\subseteq E

such that each
S_{i}
spans the nodes in
T_{i}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega(S_{i})
is minimal.
For a single terminal set
T
, the problem reverts to the
Steiner tree problem
, which is known
to be NP-hard
[
26
]
. If
T=V
, the problem can be reduced to the
minimum spanning tree problem
(MST),
which is solvable in polynomial time. There exists a
2
-approximation for the Steiner tree problem by computing an MST
on the metric completion
G^{T}:=(T,T\times T,d)
of
G
induced by
T
, where
d(u,v)
is the
shortest distance from
u
to
v
in
G

[
47
]
.

VLSI Design.

A logical circuit can be represented as a hypergraph
H=(V,E)
, where the
netlist

E=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\}
describes
the wiring between the cells.
In VLSI design, cells are assigned to locations on a 2D grid, and subsequently
interconnected through disjoint
routing channels
, as illustrated in Figure
1
(left).
The routing area can be modeled with a
routing graph

G^{R}=(V,E,\omega)
where nodes are
cell locations and each edge connects two cells
u
and
v
with distance
\omega(u,v)
.
The routing problem can be formulated as a Steiner tree packing problem on
G^{R}
with terminal sets

e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}

[
36
,
38
]
.

The process of finding a feasible routing is typically decomposed into two steps:
global
and
detailed
routing
[
72
]
.
Global routing partitions the layout area into subregions and solves the routing problem on a coarser approximation of
G^{R}
.
This approximation is represented by a
global routing graph

G^{GR}=(V,E,c,\omega)
, where
the node set are the regions and two regions are connected if they share a common border, as illustrated in Figure
1
(right).
Additionally, each edge
\{u,v\}\in E
has a capacity
c(u,v)
denoting the maximum number of wires that can cross the
border
[
72
]
, and is associated with the distance
\omega(u,v)
between the centers of both regions
[
36
]
.
The global routing problem can be also expressed as a slightly modified version of the Steiner tree packing problem on
G^{GR}

with all nets that span multiple regions as terminal sets and the relaxation that each edge
\{u,v\}\in E
can be used
in up to
c(u,v)
different trees
[
36
]
.
In detailed routing, the cells within each region are routed.

Wire-Length Minimization via Partitioning.

Previous attempts to minimize wire-lengths via HGP include methods that optimize metrics
such as the half-perimeter of the bounding boxes around each net
[
68
,
17
]
(HPWL), or Rectilinear Steiner Minimal trees induced
by the nets on the physical layout
[
63
]
(RSMT, Steiner trees based on Manhatten distance).
These algorithms use recursive bipartitioning (RB) to recursively assign the cells to subregions of the chip.
However, before each bipartitioning step, the weights of the nets are adjusted to ensure that the cut-net metric is equal
to either the HPWL or RSMT metric.
A min-cut algorithm is then used to obtain a bipartition.
However, these approaches can not be generalized to abitrary values of
k
and it has been shown that

-way partitions far from optimal
[
71
]
.
Moreover, these methods rely on geometric information, making them less
applicable to other problems.

Huang et al.
[
41
]
implemented a direct
k
-way partitioning algorithm that optimizes a metric based on
computing MSTs. However, their algorithm is restricted to at most four blocks since the space complexity grows exponentially with
k
.

Process Mapping.

For a terminal set
T=\{u,v\}
with two nodes, finding the optimal Steiner tree reverts to computing the shortest distance between them.
A metric based on shortest distances has gained attention in the context of mapping
graphs onto communication networks, which models the communication links and
costs between processors in a distributed computing system.
The communication costs induced by an edge
\{u,v\}
is the shortest
distance between the two processors to which
u
and
v
are assigned.
This problem is known as the
general process mapping problem
(GPMP).
Algorithms for solving GPMP employ either a two-phase approach
[
40
,
42
,
16
,
75
]
or directly optimize
the mapping during partitioning
[
60
,
76
,
23
,
62
]
.
The former first computes a
k
-way partition
{\Pi}
of the
input graph optimizing the number of cut edges. Afterwards, each block of
{\Pi}

is assigned to a node of the communication network, to which we refer
as the
one-to-one process mapping problem
(OPMP, graph and communication network have the same number of nodes). Algorithms for solving OPMP include
greedy construction
[
28
,
55
]
and local search methods based on exchanging the block
assignment of two nodes
[
9
,
37
,
75
]
.

4
The Steiner Tree Metric

In this work, we revisit wire-length minimization via hypergraph partitioning,
but approach it from a graph-theoretical perspective that does not require any geometric
information. The following definition can be seen as a generalization of the general process mapping problem
from graphs to hypergraphs, making it applicable to a wide range of applications.

Definition 1
(The Steiner Tree Metric)

Given a weighted hypergraph
H=(V,E,c,\omega)
and a target graph
G^{T}=(V_{T},E_{T},\omega_{T})
,
the task is to find an
\varepsilon
-balanced mapping
{\Pi}:V\rightarrow V_{T}
that minimizes the
Steiner tree metric

\mathfrak{f}_{\text{\tiny ST}}:=\sum_{e\in E}\text{{dist}}(\Lambda(e))\cdot\omega(e)

where
\text{{dist}}(\Lambda(e))
is the weight of the minimal Steiner tree connecting the blocks
\Lambda(e)
spanned by
net
e\in E
in
G^{T}
.

The target graph
G^{T}
can represent a global routing graph using
an abitrary distance metric, or a communication network modeling communication links and costs between processors.
Moreover, the Steiner tree metric reverts to the objective function for GPMP on plain graphs and to the
connectivity metric for a complete target graph with unit edge weights.

Note that the mapping
{\Pi}
represents a
k
-way partition into
k:=|V_{T}|
blocks.
We therefore apply partition-related notations and definitions also to mappings.

Calculating
\mathfrak{f}_{\text{\tiny ST}}
.

The computation of the Steiner tree metric involves solving an NP-hard problem for each net
e\in E
.
However, it has been observed that real-world VLSI instances contain a large number of small nets, with
only a few nets connecting several hundred or thousands of cells
[
10
,
58
,
65
]
.
This observation suggests that computing a small number of optimal Steiner trees suffices to obtain a near-optimal
approximation of
\mathfrak{f}_{\text{\tiny ST}}
.
We therefore precompute all Steiner trees for terminal sets up to a certain size
t

using an algorithm proposed by Dreyfus and Wagner
[
22
]
. The algorithm is based
on dynamic programing and has a running time of
\mathcal{O}(k^{3}+k^{2}(2^{t}-t)+k(3^{t}-2^{t+1}+3))
.
For nets
e\in E
with
\lambda(e)>t
, we calculate a
2
-approximation of the minimal Steiner tree in

\mathcal{O}(\lambda(e)^{2}+\lambda(e)\log{\lambda(e)})
time by using Prim’s MST algorithm
[
47
]
.
We additionally cache the result of a MST computation in a hash table for subsequent retrievals.

We store the connectivity set
\Lambda(e)
of
each net
e\in E
as a bitset of size
k
, and use
count-leading-zeroes
instructions to iterate
over
\Lambda(e)
and
pop-count
instructions to compute
\lambda(e)
.
Therefore, retrieving a precomputed or cached
\text{{dist}}(\Lambda(e))
value takes

\mathcal{O}(\lambda(e))
time since we have to calculate a hash value based on
\Lambda(e)
.
In the following, we assume that
\text{{dist}}(\Lambda(e))
can be calculated in constant time
to simplify complexity discussions.

Gain Concept.

The
gain
of a node move is defined as the change in the objective function when we move a node
u

from its current block
{\Pi}[u]
to a target block
V_{t}
. This concept is central for all move-based local search
algorithms
[
24
,
46
,
67
]
.
Clearly, the Steiner tree metric
\mathfrak{f}_{\text{\tiny ST}}
only changes if a block is added or removed from the connectivity set
\Lambda(e)

of a net
e
. We therefore introduce
\Delta\text{{dist}}(e,\Lambda^{\prime}):=\text{{dist}}(\Lambda(e))-\text{{dist}}(\Lambda^{\prime})
which is the difference
in the weight of the minimal Steiner tree if
\Lambda(e)
changes to
\Lambda^{\prime}\subseteq{\Pi}
.
If
\Delta\text{{dist}}(e,\Lambda^{\prime})>0
, then
\Lambda^{\prime}
induces a shorter Steiner tree than
\Lambda(e)
on
G^{T}
.

A node move from block
V_{s}
to
V_{t}
can either remove
V_{s}
, add
V_{t}
, or replace
V_{s}
with
V_{t}
in
\Lambda(e)
.
We can compute the state of
\Lambda(e)
after the node move based on the pin count values
{\Phi}(e,V_{s})
and

{\Phi}(e,V_{t})
of net
e
. For example, if
{\Phi}(e,V_{s})=1
and
{\Phi}(e,V_{t})>0
then the node move
removes
V_{s}
from
\Lambda(e)
.
We can define the gain
g_{u}(V_{t})
of moving a node
u
from its current block
V_{s}:={\Pi}[u]
to
V_{t}

for the Steiner tree metric as follows:

\displaystyle g_{u}(V_{t}):=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in{I}(u)\\
{\Phi}(e,V_{s})=1\\
{\Phi}(e,V_{t})>0\end{subarray}}\Delta\text{{dist}}(e,\Lambda(e)\setminus\{V_{s}\})\cdot\omega(e)+

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in{I}(u)\\
{\Phi}(e,V_{s})>1\\
{\Phi}(e,V_{t})=0\end{subarray}}\Delta\text{{dist}}(e,\Lambda(e)\cup\{V_{t}\})\cdot\omega(e)+

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in{I}(u)\\
{\Phi}(e,V_{s})=1\\
{\Phi}(e,V_{t})=0\end{subarray}}\Delta\text{{dist}}(e,\Lambda(e)\setminus\{V_{s}\}\cup\{V_{t}\})\cdot\omega(e)

Our algorithm stores and maintains the pin count values of each net and block.
Thus, we can compute
g_{u}(V_{t})
in
\mathcal{O}(|{I}(u)|)
time and the target block with the highest gain for a node
u
in

\mathcal{O}(|{R}(u)||{I}(u)|)
time.

Algorithm 1

The Mapping Algorithm

6
Coarsening

Our coarsening algorithm repeatedly finds a clustering
\mathcal{C}
of the nodes and subsequently
contracts it until the hypergraph is small enough (
\approx 160k
nodes).

The clustering algorithm initially assigns each node to a
singleton
cluster (
\mathcal{C}=V
).
Then, it iterates over all nodes
u\in V
in parallel, and assigns each node
u
to the cluster
C\in\mathcal{C}

that maximizes the heavy-edge rating function
r(u,C):=\sum_{e\in{I}(u)\cap{I}(C)}\frac{\omega(e)}{|e|-1}
.
This function is commonly used in the partitioning literature
[
15
,
44
,
1
]
and prefers clusters connected to
u
via
a large number of heavy nets with small size.

The contraction step replaces each cluster
C_{i}\in\mathcal{C}
with one supernode
u_{i}
with weight
c(u_{i})=\sum_{v\in C_{i}}c(v)
.
For each net
e\in E
, it replaces each pin
v\in e
with its corresponding supernode.

7
Initial Mapping

Our initial mapping algorithm follows the two-phase approach. We first compute a
k
-way partition
of the hypergraph using
Mt-KaHyPar
’s initial partitioning algorithm for optimizing
the connectivity metric
\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda-1}
. Subsequently, we contract each block
of the partition to obtain a one-to-one process mapping problem (OPMP).
To solve OPMP, we implement a greedy strategy to construct an initial mapping, which we
subsequently refine using a local search algorithm. Recall that the hypergraph and target graph
have the same number of nodes when solving OPMP (
k=n
).

Greedy Mapping.

We generalized the greedy construction method of Glantz et al.
[
28
]
for OPMP on graphs to hypergraphs.
The algorithm starts by assigning a seed node
u
of
H
to a block
b
of
G^{T}

with the smallest communication volume (
b:=\argmin_{u\in V_{T}}\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E_{T}}\omega(u,v)
).
Afterwards, each neighbor
v\in N(u)
is inserted into a priority queue (PQ) with the weight of all nets connecting
v
to the actual
partial mapping as rating. In each step, the algorithm repeatedly (i) extracts a node
u\in V
from the PQ,
(ii) assigns it to a
free
block with the highest gain, and (iii) updates the ratings of all neighbors
v\in N(u)
in the PQ.
A block is free if no node has been previously assigned to it.
The complexity of the algorithm is dominated by the second step, which is

\mathcal{O}(k\sum_{u\in V}|{R}(u)||{I}(u)|)=\mathcal{O}(k^{2}p)
.
We compute several initial mappings in parallel using each node
u\in V
as seed node and take the best out of all runs as solution.

Local Search.

We implement the Kerninghan-Lin algorithm
[
46
,
67
]
to improve an initial mapping.
The algorithm proceeds in passes. At the beginning of each pass, all node pairs are inserted into a PQ
with the gain of exchanging their block IDs as rating. This can be done in
\mathcal{O}(k^{2}(p+\log{k}))
time.
The algorithm then repeatedly extracts a node pair
(u,v)
from the PQ and applies the exchange operation
on the mapping. Before applying the operation, we recompute the exchange gain
and reinsert
(u,v)
into the PQ when the recomputed gain differs from the gain in the PQ. This approach lazily updates
the gain values in the PQ. We ignore
(u,v)
, if either
u
or
v
has already been moved before.
The algorithm also performs exchange operations that intermediately worsen
\mathfrak{f}_{\text{\tiny ST}}
and
is therefore able to escape from local optima. At the end, we revert to the best seen solution during the pass.
We perform several passes until no further improvement is possible.

If the recomputed gain always matches the gain in the PQ, the running time of a pass is
\mathcal{O}(p+k^{2}\log{k})
.
The algorithm extracts
k^{2}
node pairs from the PQ, but applies only
\nicefrac{{k}}{{2}}
on the mapping for which we
recompute the gain in
\mathcal{O}(|{I}(u)|+|{I}(v)|)
time.
In the worst case, the gains of all node pairs are updated in each step, resulting in a running
time of
\mathcal{O}(k^{3}(p+\log{k}))
.

Label Propagation Refinement

The most widely used refinement technique in parallel partitioning
algorithms is label propagation
[
77
,
45
,
54
,
2
,
33
,
73
,
52
]
.
The algorithm works in rounds. In each round, it iterates over all nodes in parallel, and whenever it
visits a node
u
, it moves it to the block
V_{t}
maximizing its move gain
g_{u}(V_{t})
.
The algorithm only performs moves with postive gain and therefore cannot escape from local optima.

A fundamental challenge for parallel refinement algorithms is that the gain of a node move can change between its
initial calculation and actual execution due to concurrent node moves in its neighborhood
[
45
]
. We therefore
double-check the gain of a node move by comparing it to an
attributed gain
value that we compute
based on syncronized data structure updates
[
34
,
30
]
. If we move a node
u
from

V_{s}
to
V_{t}
, we iterate over all nets
e\in{I}(u)
and update
{\Phi}(e,V_{s})
,
{\Phi}(e,V_{t})
,
and
\Lambda(e)
. This is done while holding a spin lock for net
e
. The updated values are used to compute
the attributed gain
\Delta
. If
\Delta<0
, we immediately revert the node move as we assume
that it has worsen the solution quality.

FM Local Search

The Fiduccia-Mattheyses (FM) algorithm
[
24
]
is the most widely used local search technique in sequential partitioning
algorithms
[
66
,
64
,
15
,
44
]
. The algorithm works similar to the Kerninghan-Lin algorithm but
performs one node move at a time instead of exchanging node pairs. Our parallel version of the algorithm is based on
the
localized multi-try FM
algorithm of Sanders and Schulz
[
64
,
2
]
.

Algorithm Overview.

The parallel FM implementation proceeds in several rounds (at most
10
).
In each round, all boundary nodes are inserted into a globally shared task queue
Q
. The threads then poll a fixed number
of nodes (= 25) from
Q
that they use as seed nodes for their
localized FM
searches. The localized FM searches initializes
a PQ storing the move with the highest gain for each seed node. The algorithm then repeatedly extracts a node move from the PQ,
applies it to the mapping, and inserts or updates the gain of all neighbors of the moved node in the PQ.
The localized FM algorithm proceeds until the PQ is empty
or it becomes unlikely to find further improvements
[
57
]
.
At the end, the algorithm reverts to the best seen solution.
We repeatedly start localized FM searches until the task queue
Q
is empty.
In each round, each node is moved at most once.

The localized FM searches use a globally shared
gain table
to lookup gain values in constant time.
The gain table stores and maintains the gain values for all possible node moves (
nk
entries).
We use the gain table to insert new node moves or update gain values in the PQ.
It has been shown that this technique speeds up the FM algorithm significantly in practice
[
65
,
29
]
.
In the following, we present an efficient algorithm to update gain table entries for the Steiner tree metric.

Delta-Gain Updates.

If we move a node
u
from block
V_{s}
to
V_{t}
, we iterate over all nets
e\in{I}(u)
and decide
based on
{\Phi}(e,V_{s})
and
{\Phi}(e,V_{t})
if the gain value of a pin
v\in e
has changed.
The input for the
delta-gain update
procedure is a copy of the pin count values and connectivity set obtained from
the synchronized data structure updates described in the previous section.

The contribution of net
e
to the gain value of a pin
v\in e
can only change if moving
u

removes
V_{s}
or adds
V_{t}
to
\Lambda(e)
(i.e.,
{\Phi}(e,V_{s})=0
or
{\Phi}(e,V_{t})=1
),
or when moving
v
will now remove
V_{s}
(i.e.,
{\Phi}(e,V_{s})=1
and
{\Pi}[v]=V_{s}
), or does not remove
V_{t}

from
\Lambda(e)
anymore (i.e.,
{\Phi}(e,V_{t})=2
and
{\Pi}[v]=V_{t}
).
Thus, there are four cases that trigger a gain update.
In the former two cases, the gain values of all pins
v\in e
are affected. In the latter two cases, only
the gain value of the (previously) last pin
v\in e
in block (
V_{t}
)
V_{s}
changes.

Figure 2:
After moving the red node from
V_{4}
to
V_{3}
, moving the blue node from
V_{2}
to
V_{1}
does not
reduce the weight of the minimal Steiner tree (red edges) anymore.

For the connectivity metric
\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda-1}
, only the gain value for moving
v
to
V_{s}
or
V_{t}
can change if
v\in e
is affected by a gain update
[
66
]
.
Unfortunately, this is not the case for
\mathfrak{f}_{\text{\tiny ST}}
which we illustrate with an example in Figure
2
.
Thus, we have to update all
k
gain table entries of
v
.

If we identify an entry
g_{v}(V_{j})
affected by a gain update, we subtract the previous (before moving
u
) and add the actual contribution
(after moving
u
) of net
e
to the gain value
g_{v}(V_{j})
.
We compute the previous contribution by reconstructing the pin count values and connectivity set of net
e
before the node move.
This can be done in constant time by incrementing
{\Phi}(e,V_{s})
and decrementing
{\Phi}(e,V_{t})
, and adding
V_{s}
or removing

V_{t}
from
\Lambda(e)
accordingly.

Lemma 9.1

The running time of gain updates for moving all nodes once is
\mathcal{O}(k^{2}p)
.

The four different update cases are exactly the same as in the original FM algorithm
[
24
]
.
Fiduccia and Mattheyses showed that they are only triggered a constant number of times per net and block.
The most time consuming update takes
\mathcal{O}(k|e|)
time (
{\Phi}(e,V_{s})=0
or
{\Phi}(e,V_{t})=1
).
Thus, the running time of the gain updates for moving all nodes once is
\mathcal{O}(k\sum_{e\in E}k|e|)=\mathcal{O}(k^{2}p)

The gain updates for the Steiner tree metric are significantly more expensive compared to the gain updates for the connectivity
metric, which can be performed in
\mathcal{O}(kp)
time
[
30
]
. As an optimization, we only store and maintain gain values
to the adjacent blocks of a node. This reduces the running time to
\mathcal{O}(k{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{R}(u)\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}p)
where

{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{R}(u)\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}
is the average number of adjacent blocks per node. On real-world hypergraphs, we observed running time
much closer to
\mathcal{O}({\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{R}(u)\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}p)
since many nets have small size or connectivity.

Flow-Based Refinement

Flow-based refinement is considered as the most powerful improvement heuristic for (hyper)graph partitioning and is used
in the highest-quality partitioning algorithms
[
66
,
64
,
31
]
.
The technique is based on the well-known max-flow min-cut theorem
[
25
]
that relates the minimum cut separating two nodes
s

and
t
of a graph
G
to the maximum flow between
s
and
t
in the corresponding flow network of
G
.

The flow-based refinement algorithm of
Mt-KaHyPar
works on bipartitions and is
scheduled on different block pairs in parallel to improve a
k
-way partition
{\Pi}
. To improve a bipartition,
the algorithm extracts a region
B:=B_{1}\penalty\ \cup\penalty\ B_{2}
with
B_{1}\subseteq V_{1}
and
B_{2}\subseteq V_{2}
around the cut nets
of a bipartition
\{V_{1},V_{2}\}\subseteq{\Pi}
. This yields a flow network
\mathcal{N}_{B}
induced by the region
B
.
The algorithm then uses incremental maximum flow computations on
\mathcal{N}_{B}
to compute a balanced minimum cut that improves
{\Pi}
.

Max-flow min-cut computations can be used to improve the cut-net metric
\mathfrak{f}_{c}
on
\mathcal{N}_{B}
.
However, we can improve the Steiner tree metric
\mathfrak{f}_{\text{\tiny ST}}
on the input hypergraph
H

with flow computations on
\mathcal{N}_{B}
if the constructed flow network
\mathcal{N}_{B}
satisfies the following equation:

(10.1)

\mathfrak{f}_{\text{\tiny ST}}({\Pi}^{\prime})=\mathfrak{f}_{\text{\tiny ST}}({\Pi})+\Delta\mathfrak{f}_{c}(\mathcal{N}_{B},{\Pi}\rightarrow{\Pi}^{\prime})

Here,
\Delta\mathfrak{f}_{c}(\mathcal{N}_{B},{\Pi}\rightarrow{\Pi}^{\prime})
is the difference in the cut-net metric
if the actual partition
{\Pi}
changes to
{\Pi}^{\prime}
on
\mathcal{N}_{B}
.
Note that only nodes contained in
B
are allowed to change their blocks.
In the following, we show how to construct
\mathcal{N}_{B}
for plain graphs and then generalize the construction
to hypergraphs.

Graph Model.

Our flow network
\mathcal{N}_{B}=(\mathcal{V}:=\{s,t\}\cup B,\mathcal{E},\mathpzc{c})
is a graph with
a dedicated source
s\in\mathcal{V}
and sink
t\in\mathcal{V}
in which each edge
e\in\mathcal{E}

has capacity
\mathpzc{c}(e)\geq 0
. Recall that
\text{{dist}}(\Lambda(e))
reverts to shortest distance between the blocks
connected by edge
e
in
G^{T}
for plain graphs. The following construction algorithm is illustrated in Figure
3
.

For an input graph
G
, we obtain
\mathcal{N}_{B}
from the subgraph
G[V_{1}\cup V_{2}]
induced by the bipartition
\{V_{1},V_{2}\}\subseteq{\Pi}

by contracting all nodes in
V_{1}\setminus B
to the source
s
and all nodes in
V_{2}\setminus B
to the sink
t
.
We set the capacity of each edge
e\in\mathcal{E}
to
\mathpzc{c}(e):=\text{{dist}}(\{V_{1},V_{2}\})\cdot\omega(e)
(blue edges in Figure
3
).
If a non-cut edge of
{\Pi}
becomes a cut edge in
{\Pi}^{\prime}
, then the cut-net metric increases by
\text{{dist}}(\{V_{1},V_{2}\})\cdot\omega(e)

on
\mathcal{N}_{B}
which is also the increase in the Steiner tree metric on the input graph
G
(vice versa if we remove an edge from the cut).
However, this does not suffice to satisfy Equation
10.1
.

We have to add additional edges to
\mathcal{N}_{B}
for each edge
e=\{u,v\}
of
G
that w.l.o.g. connect
u\in B
to a block
{\Pi}[v]\notin\{V_{1},V_{2}\}

as follows:

1.

If
u\in V_{1}
and moving
u
from
V_{1}
to
V_{2}
worsens resp. improves
\mathfrak{f}_{\text{\tiny ST}}
on
G
, we add an edge
e^{\prime}=\{s,u\}
resp.
e^{\prime}=\{u,t\}
to
\mathcal{E}

with capacity
\mathpzc{c}(e^{\prime})=|\Delta\text{{dist}}(e,\Lambda(e)\setminus\{V_{1}\}\cup\{V_{2}\})|\cdot\omega(e)
(green edges in Figure
3
).

2.

If
u\in V_{2}
and moving
u
from
V_{2}
to
V_{1}
worsens resp. improves
\mathfrak{f}_{\text{\tiny ST}}
on
G
, we add an edge
e^{\prime}=\{u,t\}
resp.
e^{\prime}=\{s,u\}
to
\mathcal{E}

with capacity
\mathpzc{c}(e^{\prime})=|\Delta\text{{dist}}(e,\Lambda(e)\setminus\{V_{2}\}\cup\{V_{1}\})|\cdot\omega(e)
(red edges in Figure
3
).

The capacities are chosen to accurately reflect the difference in
\mathfrak{f}_{\text{\tiny ST}}
on
G
if
u
is moved to the other block.
If, e.g., moving
u\in V_{1}
to
V_{2}
improves
\mathfrak{f}_{\text{\tiny ST}}
on
G
, the node move would remove the edge
(u,t)
in
\mathcal{N}_{B}

from the cut. The difference in
\mathfrak{f}_{c}
on
\mathcal{N}_{B}
is then the same as the difference in
\mathfrak{f}_{\text{\tiny ST}}
on
G

(analogously for all other cases).

Figure 3:
The flow network
\mathcal{N}_{B}
induced by a region
B:=B_{1}\cup B_{2}
constructed for a target
graph
G^{T}
with six nodes.

Hypergraph Model.

For an input hypergraph
H
and a bipartition
\{V_{1},V_{2}\}\subseteq{\Pi}
, we construct
\mathcal{N}_{B}
similarly to our graph model.
However, the main difference is how we model nets
e
of
H
that connect
B
to a block different from
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
(
\Lambda(e)\not\subset\{V_{1},V_{2}\}
).
For such nets, we distingush between two cases:
|e\cap B|=1
and
|e\cap B|>1
.
In the case of
|e\cap B|=1
, where only one pin
u
of
e
is contained in
\mathcal{N}_{B}
,
we can use the same approach as in our graph model. If
|e\cap B|>1
, then
the connectivity set
\Lambda(e)
of
e
can be in one of the following three states if the actual
partition
{\Pi}
changes to
{\Pi}^{\prime}
:
\Lambda(e)\setminus\{V_{1}\}\cup\{V_{2}\}
,

\Lambda(e)\setminus\{V_{2}\}\cup\{V_{1}\}
, or
\Lambda(e)\cup\{V_{1},V_{2}\}
.
This induces three different Steiner trees on
G^{T}
. Thus, we cannot model
the difference in the Steiner tree metric with a single capacity on the nets in
\mathcal{N}_{B}
.
We therefore set the capacity of such nets to a lower bound for the actual improvement,
which is

\displaystyle\min(

\displaystyle\Delta\text{{dist}}(e,\Lambda(e)\setminus\{V_{1}\}\cup\{V_{2}\}),

\displaystyle\Delta\text{{dist}}(e,\Lambda(e)\setminus\{V_{2}\}\cup\{V_{1}\}))\cdot\omega(e)

if
\{V_{1},V_{2}\}\subset\Lambda(e)
, and
\Delta\text{{dist}}(e,\Lambda(e)\cup\{V_{1},V_{2}\})\cdot\omega(e)
if
\{V_{1},V_{2}\}\not\subset\Lambda(e)
.
Consequently,
\mathfrak{f}_{c}(\mathcal{N}_{B},{\Pi}\rightarrow{\Pi}^{\prime})
is a lower bound for the actual improvement in the Steiner tree metric
in our hypergraph flow network model. Note that our maximum flow algorithm only works on graph flow networks.
We therefore convert the hypergraph flow network into an equivalent graph model using the
Lawler expansion

[
49
]
.

Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we introduced a HGP formulation based on the Steiner tree metric to
accurately model wire-lengths in VLSI design and communication costs in distributed systems.
Our main algorithmic contribution is a direct
k
-way multilevel algorithm for optimizing the Steiner tree metric.
We implemented an efficient gain table data structure for constant-time lookup of gain values.
Furthermore, we devised a novel flow network model to optimize the metric via max-flow min-cut computations.
Our experimental results demonstrates that our new algorithm has the potential to significantly reduce wire-lengths in VLSI design.

Future research includes a more efficient and memory-friendly algorithm for precomputing all Steiner trees,
enabling the mapping of hypergraphs onto larger target graphs.
Additionally, we want to develop a placement and routing algorithm for VLSI design leveraging our new algorithm.
