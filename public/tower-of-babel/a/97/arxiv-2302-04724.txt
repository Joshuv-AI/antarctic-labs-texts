Domain Generalization by Functional Regression
Markus Holzleitner a , Sergei V. Pereverzyev b and Werner Zellinger b

Abstract
The problem of domain generalization is to learn, given data from different source distributions, a model that can be expected to generalize well on new target distributions which are only seen through unlabeled samples. In this paper, we study domain generalization as a problem of functional regression. Our concept leads to a new algorithm for learning a linear operator from marginal distributions of inputs to the corresponding conditional distributions of outputs given inputs. Our algorithm allows a source distribution-dependent construction of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces for prediction, and, satisfies finite sample error bounds for the idealized risk. Numerical implementations and source code are available 1 1 1 Source code: https://github.com/wzell/FuncRegr4DomGen .

Introduction

Most problems in learning theory assume identically distributed data.
In contrast, in domain generalization

,
Muandet et al. 2013
,
Blanchard et al. 2021
, the learner observes
N\in\mathbb{N}
data samples

2

2

A sample

from a probability measure
P

\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
at independent events

.

(x_{i}^{(1)},y_{i}^{(1)})_{i=1}^{n},\ldots,(x_{i}^{(N)},y_{i}^{(N)})_{i=1}^{n}\in\left((\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{n}\right)^{N}

These

The goal of domain generalization is to find a model

that is able to derive a predictor
\widehat{f}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
from only

The performance of

)

This work is concerned with the worst-case sample complexity of domain generalization.
More precisely, we study the question of
whether and how the model

N
given samples, such that it satisfies small bounds on
\mathcal{E}^{\infty}(g)-\inf_{h}\mathcal{E}^{\infty}(h)
?

Although many finite sample results are available in the similar settings of meta-learning (cf.
Maurer 2005
) and multi-task learning (cf.
Evgeniou et al. 2005
), relatively less is known for domain generalization.
Indeed, the inaccessibility of target labels requires new techniques for domain generalization.
For example, to learn from unlabeled data of a target distribution
P^{T}
, it is required to relate the marginal distribution
P_{\mathcal{X}}^{T}(x)
(of inputs
x\in\mathcal{X}
) to the conditional distribution
P^{T}(y|x)
(of outputs
y\in\mathcal{Y}
w.r.t. inputs), where
P^{T}(x,y)=P_{\mathcal{X}}^{T}(x)P^{T}(y|x)
.
This is implicitly done in the seminal
marginal transfer
approach of
Blanchard et al. 2011
,
Blanchard et al. 2021
, where a
universal consistent
algorithm is proposed, i.e., an algorithm computing
g
such that
\mathcal{E}^{\infty}(g)\to\inf_{h}\mathcal{E}^{\infty}(h)
almost surely for
n,N\to\infty
.
However, to the best of our knowledge, specifying the rate of this convergence is still an open research problem.

The main conceptual contribution of this work is to recast domain generalization as a problem of functional regression, which allows for analytical results from that field.
More precisely, we propose a new algorithm for
explicitly
learning (from the input samples) an operator, which maps (kernel mean embeddings of) input marginal distributions
P_{\mathcal{X}}(x)
to approximations of the regression functions (Bayes predictors)
f_{P}:=\argmin_{f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}(f(x)-y)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}P(x,y)
of the corresponding conditional distributions
P(y|x)
.
We, here, in a first step, focus on learning a linear operator with slope functions residing in an RKHS, which allows us to apply analytical arguments from
Mollenhauer et al. 2022
,
Jin et al. 2022
,
Tong et al. 2022
resulting in explicit finite sample bounds.
However, our new concept opens new directions for domain generalization by linear and non-linear operator learning.

Another, particularly practical, advantage of our method is the possibility to choose different reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs) for regression on different source distributions
(P^{(i)})_{i=1}^{N}
.
This allows expert-choices and automation, e.g., by choosing between well-known kernels by cross-validation.
We provide a numerical example which illustrates this advantage and gives a simple implementation of the proposed algorithm.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We provide a new concept for approaching domain generalization by functional regression.

•

We propose a new algorithm which allows a domain-specific data-based construction of predictors, e.g., different learned RKHSs for different domains. As one consequence, new target predictors are not needed to be well approximable by pre-defined (e.g. Gaussian) RKHSs.

•

We provide (to the best of our knowledge first) finite sample bounds for
\mathcal{E}^{\infty}(g)-\inf_{h}\mathcal{E}^{\infty}(h)
in the domain generalization setting of
Blanchard et al. 2011
.

•

We provide a numerical implementation showing the advantage of our algorithm.

2.2
Marginal Transfer Learning

In the seminal works
Blanchard et al. 2011
,
Muandet et al. 2013
,
Blanchard et al. 2021
, the predictor

\displaystyle f_{{\bf z}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bf z}^{(N)}}:\mathcal{M}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{X})\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}

that is computed from the (by the input marginals

.
This approach is referred to as
marginal transfer learning
.
More precisely,
Blanchard et al. 2021

\overline{k}

,

\mathcal{M}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{X})
and

.

5

\displaystyle f_{{\bf z}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bf z}^{(N)}}^{\lambda}:=\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{\overline{k}}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left(f(\widehat{P}_{\mathcal{X}}^{(i)},x_{j}^{(i)})-y_{j}^{(i)}\right)^{2}+\lambda\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\overline{k}}}^{2}.

(6)

Using

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\infty}(g_{{\bf z}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bf z}^{(N)}}^{\lambda})\to\inf_{g:\mathcal{M}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{X})\to\left\{f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\right\}}\mathcal{E}^{\infty}(g)

Problem

Two issues appear: The first issue, concerns the final predictor
g(\widehat{P}_{\mathcal{X}}^{T})(.)=f_{{\bf z}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bf z}^{(N)}}^{\lambda(N)}(\widehat{P}_{\mathcal{X}}^{T},\cdot)
computed as defined in Eq. (
6

2
.
The space

For example the regression functions

6

The second issue concerns the rate of the convergence in Eq. (
7
), which is unknown.
To the best of our knowledge, no domain generalization algorithm is known with quantified convergence rate of Eq. (
7

This work presents a domain generalization algorithm
A
as in Eq. (
2
) (i.e., mapping the source samples

to a function

that allows one to choose different RKHSs

with kernels

for each domain
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, and, which has a quantified rate of the convergence in Eq. (
7
) (i.e., of
\mathcal{E}^{\infty}(g_{{\bf z}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bf z}^{(N)}})\to\inf_{g:\widehat{P}_{\mathcal{X}}^{T}\mapsto(f:x\mapsto y)}\mathcal{E}^{\infty}(g)
for increasing number of samples
{\bf z}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bf z}^{(N)}
with increasing sizes.

Summary of Results

Linear Operator Ansatz

Remark 1
.

The main conceptual contribution of this paper is the Ansatz above, which recasts domain generalization as a problem of functional regression.
More precisely, our Ansatz allows domain generalization by learning the operator
G
, which maps
(functional) mean embeddings to domain-specific regression functions.
This enables to estimate the regression functions in each domain by different kernels, and, to apply explicit finite-sample bounds from the field of functional regression, e.g.
Mollenhauer et al. 2022
,
Jin et al. 2022
,
Tong et al. 2022

The independence of the noise is for simplicity and can be removed at the price of slightly more involved proofs, see
[

We further assume that the integral operator

is of the form

\displaystyle G\cdot m_{P_{\mathcal{X}}}(\cdot):=a_{0}(\cdot)+\int_{\mathcal{X}}m_{P_{\mathcal{X}}}(x)\beta(\cdot,x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

(11)

with

and

4.2
New Algorithm

For simplicity, in the following, we assume equal sample sizes
n:=n_{1}=\ldots=n_{N}=n_{T}
.
Following our Ansatz in Eq. (
10
) and Eq. (
11
), we propose the following two-step procedure:

1.

Regularized estimation of

for every source sample

of domain

\displaystyle f_{{\bf z}^{(i)}}^{\lambda_{i}}:=\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{k^{(i)}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(f(x_{j}^{(i)})-y_{j}^{(i)})^{2}+\lambda_{i}\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{k^{(i)}}}.

2.

\displaystyle\beta_{{\bf z}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bf z}^{(N)}}^{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N},\lambda}:=\argmin_{\beta(x,\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert f_{{\bf z}^{(i)}}^{\lambda_{i}}-\int\beta(\cdot,x^{\prime})m_{{\bf x}^{(i)}}(x^{\prime})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}^{2}+\lambda\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left\lVert\beta(x,\cdot)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

We define the final model

Remark 2
.

Note that the final predictor

(as, e.g., a Gaussian RKHS in

4.3
Finite Sample Error Bound

For our algorithm defined in Subsection
4.2
, we are able to provide finite sample bounds under certain assumptions, which can be essentially summarized by four categories: Classical regularity conditions on the involved kernels (mean embeddings, domain-specific regression, operator slope), classical assumptions on the effective dimension of the involved integral operators (domain-specific regression, operator slope learning), new assumptions on the (functional) data generating process, and new assumptions relating the domain-specific regression problems with the global functional regression.

Under these assumptions, it holds with probability at least
1-\delta
that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\infty}(g_{{\bf z}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bf z}^{(N)}}^{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N},\lambda})-\inf_{g:\mathcal{M}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{X})\to\left\{f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\right\}}\mathcal{E}^{\infty}(g)\leq\frac{\log\frac{4}{\delta}}{\delta^{2}}\mathcal{O}(N^{-\frac{1}{1+c_{6}}})\left(\mathcal{O}({n^{-\frac{1}{1+c_{3}}}})+\mathcal{O}(1)\right)

(14)

for some
0\leq c_{3},c_{6}\leq 1
independent of
P\in\mathcal{M}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
and regularization parameter choices

\lambda=N^{-\frac{1}{1+c_{6}}},\lambda_{1}=\cdots=\lambda_{N}=n^{-\frac{1}{1+c_{3}}}.

Remark 3
.

Our finite sample bound in Eq. (
14
) accounts for properties of the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{k}
in which the slope
\beta\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
is assumed to reside, see (A5) in Subsection
5.2
below.
However, it does not take into account the smoothness of
\beta

Mollenhauer et al. 2022
.

Our assumptions can be grouped in two parts: The first part deals with assumptions that are used in related works.

The second part discusses assumptions specific for our setting.
All enumerated constants are independent from

Assumptions on effective dimensions for domain-specific regression:

(A5)

Assumptions on effective dimensions for functional regression:

(A6)

(B1)

7
Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we study domain generalization as a problem of functional regression, i.e., regression with functional input and output, by directly learning the relationship between domain-specific marginal distributions of inputs and corresponding conditional distributions of outputs given inputs.
Our new conceptualization leads to an operator learning algorithm with finite sample bounds.
We also provide numerical illustrations showing its advantage of explicit computation of domain-specific predictors in possibly different reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces.

Our work aims at setting the ground for (to the best of our knowledge first) finite sample bounds for domain generalization.
However, it leaves an entirely exhaustive statistical analysis for future research, e.g., by taking the smoothness of the operator slope and more general non-linear functional regression, into account.

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}^{n}}\left\lVert m_{{\bf x}}-m_{P_{\mathcal{X}}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}^{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left\lVert\beta(y,x)\right\rVert_{L_{x}^{2}(\mathcal{X})}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}P_{\mathcal{X}}^{n}({\bf x})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}^{n}}\left\lVert m_{{\bf x}}-m_{P_{\mathcal{X}}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}^{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\kappa^{2}\left\lVert\beta(y,\cdot)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}P_{\mathcal{X}}^{n}({\bf x})

\displaystyle\leq c_{4}\kappa^{2}\int_{\mathcal{M}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})}\int_{\mathcal{X}^{n}}\left\lVert m_{{\bf x}}-m_{P_{\mathcal{X}}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}P_{\mathcal{X}}^{n}({\bf x})

\displaystyle\leq c_{4}\kappa^{2}\int_{\mathcal{M}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})}\int_{\mathcal{X}^{n}}\kappa^{2}\left\lVert m_{{\bf x}}-m_{P_{\mathcal{X}}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}P_{\mathcal{X}}^{n}({\bf x})

\displaystyle\leq c_{4}\frac{\kappa^{6}}{n},

where the first inequality is Cauchy-Schwartz and the last inequality follows from Lemma
3
.
∎

In order to prove Lemma
5
, we will discuss the following Corollary first, which applies the main arguments from
Tong et al. 2022
to our setting.
For readers convenience, we provide an overview here:

Corollary 7
.

Consider the algorithm introduced in Subsection
4.2
. Under the assumptions stated in Subsection
5.2
, if we set
\lambda=N^{-\frac{1}{1+c_{6}}}
and
\lambda_{i}=n^{-\frac{1}{1+c_{3}}}
, we have that for any
0<\delta<1
with probability
1-\delta
:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{X})}\int_{\mathcal{X}^{n}}\left\lVert(G_{{\bf z}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bf z}^{(N)}}^{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N},\lambda}-G)m_{{\bf x}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}P_{\mathcal{X}}^{n}({\bf x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}E(P)\leq C(\bar{\sigma}^{2})\frac{\log\frac{4}{\delta}}{\delta^{2}}N^{-\frac{1}{1+c_{6}}}.

Proof of Corollary
7
.

Step 1:

Let us start by reformulating our linear operator Ansatz
4.1
as follows:

f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda}=G\cdot m_{{\bf x}}+\underbrace{f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda}-f_{P}+G\cdot m_{P_{\mathcal{X}}}-G\cdot m_{{\bf x}}+\varepsilon}_{:=\bar{\varepsilon}}.

(26)

We further decompose the noise term as
\bar{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}+\varepsilon
with

\varepsilon_{1}=f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda}-f_{P}

(27)

and

\varepsilon_{2}=G\cdot(m_{P_{\mathcal{X}}}-m_{{\bf x}}).

(28)

Assumption (B3) ensures that
\bar{\varepsilon}
is independent from the sampling process of
m_{\bf x}
.
(B4) ensures that both
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
are unbiased.
Now we take care of the associated variances
\sigma_{1}^{2}
and
\sigma_{2}^{2}
of
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
, respectively (this is where the data sample size
n
enters the bound).
In particular,
\varepsilon_{1}
is handled by Lemma
2
since

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}^{2}=\int_{\mathcal{M}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})}\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{n}}\left\lVert f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda}-f_{P}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(P)}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}P^{n}({\bf z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}E(P)

and
\varepsilon_{2}
is handled by Lemma
4
.
Using both lemmas together, we end up with Eq. (
23
).

Step 2:

From assumption (A4) we get
G_{k}(L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))=\mathcal{H}_{k}
.
That is, there are
f_{0},f_{N,\lambda}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})
such that

\displaystyle\beta_{{\bf z}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bf z}^{(N)}}^{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N},\lambda}(t,.)=G_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}f_{N,\lambda}(t,.)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\beta(t,.)=G_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}f_{0}(t,.)

(29)

for all
t\in\mathcal{X}
. Using the definition of the operators and Fubini’s theorem, it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{X})}\int_{\mathcal{X}^{n}}\left\lVert(G_{{\bf z}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bf z}^{(N)}}^{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N},\lambda}-G)m_{{\bf x}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}P_{\mathcal{X}}^{n}({\bf x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}E(P)

\displaystyle=\left\langle f_{0}-f_{N,\lambda},T_{k}(f_{0}-f_{N,\lambda})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\lVert T_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}(f_{0}-f_{N,\lambda})\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})},

(30)

where for
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})
,
T_{k}f(s,t)=T_{k}(f(s,.))(t)
.

Step 3:
The empirical counterpart
C_{N}
of the covariance kernel
C
is defined by
C_{N}(s,t):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{{\bf x}_{i}}(s)m_{{\bf x}_{i}}(t)
and we denote the associated integral operator by
G_{C_{N}}
. Moreover,
T_{k,N}=T_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}G_{C_{N}}T_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Next, Eq. (
13
) can be written as

\displaystyle f_{N}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert G\cdot m_{{\bf x}_{i}}+\bar{\varepsilon}_{i}-\int f(\cdot,x^{\prime})(G_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}m_{{\bf x}^{(i)}})(x^{\prime})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}^{2}+\lambda\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert\underbrace{G\cdot m_{{\bf x}_{i}}+\bar{\varepsilon}_{i}}_{=Y_{i}}-\int_{\mathcal{X}}(G_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}f(\cdot,x^{\prime}))m_{{\bf x}^{(i)}}(x^{\prime})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}^{2}+\lambda\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}^{2}.

(31)

It can be shown by similar techniques as in
Engl et al. 1996
that

\displaystyle f_{N}^{\lambda}=(T_{k,N}+\lambda I)^{-1}(T_{k,N}f_{0}+g_{N}),

(32)

where

\displaystyle g_{N}(s,t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{\varepsilon}_{i}(s)(G_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}m_{{\bf x}^{(i)}})(t).

(33)

For the reader’s convenience, we will also include a short proof of Eq. (
32
) in the following, which is mostly considered as standard in the literature on functional regression.

Let us therefore define
\tilde{T}_{k}^{i}:L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
by
(\tilde{T}_{k}^{i}f)(y)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}(G_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}f(y,x^{\prime}))m_{{\bf x}^{(i)}}(x^{\prime})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}
.
It is easy to see that
(\tilde{T}_{k}^{i})^{*}g(x,y)=(G_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}m_{{\bf x}^{(i)}})(x)g(y)
and
T_{k,N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\tilde{T}_{k}^{i})^{*}\tilde{T}^{i}_{k}
. Eq. (
31
) can now be rewritten as
\argmin_{f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert Y_{i}-\tilde{T}_{k}^{i}f\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}+\lambda\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}
.
Next, we take the Frechét derivative of
F(f)=\left\lVert Y_{i}-\tilde{T}_{k}^{i}f\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}+\lambda\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}
in direction
v\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})
and get

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(f)(v)

\displaystyle=2\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\left\langle Y_{i}-\tilde{T}_{k}^{i}f,\tilde{T}^{i}_{k}v\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}+2\lambda\left\langle f,v\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}\right)

\displaystyle=2\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\tilde{T}_{k}^{i})^{*}Y_{i}+(T_{k,N}+\lambda)f,v\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}.

(34)

Setting Eq. (
34
) to zero and using
Y_{i}=\tilde{T}_{k}^{i}\beta+\bar{\varepsilon_{i}}
, we end up with Eq. (
32
), since all functionals are convex.

Step 4:
Combining Eq. (
30
) and Eq. (
32
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\left\lVert T_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}(f_{0}-f_{N,\lambda})\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}\leq

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left\lVert T_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}(T_{k,N}+\lambda I)^{-1}g_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}}_{[A]}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\left\lVert T_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}((T_{k,N}+\lambda I)^{-1}T_{k,N}f_{0}-f_{0})\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}}_{[B]}

(35)

Step 5:
To get bounds on [A] and [B], we rely on Hoeffding-like concentration inequalities in Hilbert spaces.

Lemma 8

(
Pinelis 1994
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and
\xi
be a random variable with values in
\mathcal{H}
. Assume that
\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq M
almost surely. Let
\left\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{N}\right\}
be a sample of
N
independent observations for
\xi
. Then for any
0<\delta<1
,

\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\xi_{i}-\text{E}(\xi)\right]\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\frac{2M\log(2/\delta)}{N}+\sqrt{\frac{2\text{E}\left(\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)\log(2/\delta)}{N}}

with confidence at least
1-\delta
.

Lemma
8
can now be applied to the random variable
\xi_{A}=(T_{k}+\lambda I)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left\langle G_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}m_{{\bf x}},.\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}G_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}m_{{\bf x}}
, which takes values in the space of Hilbert Schmidt operators in
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
.
We denote the associated norm by
\left\lVert.\right\rVert_{\text{HS}(L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))}
.
The expectation
E
is to be taken with respect to the sampling process of the functional input data, i.e. the empirical mean embeddings in our case.
Combining Lemma
1
(which gives a uniform bound on the input functional data) with the spectral decomposition of
T_{k}
, one obtains
\left\lVert\xi_{A}\right\rVert_{\text{HS}(L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))}\leq\frac{\kappa^{10}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}
and
\text{E}(\left\lVert\xi_{A}\right\rVert^{2}_{\text{HS}(L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))})\leq\kappa^{10}\gamma_{T_{k}}(\lambda)
. Applying Lemma
8
yields

\displaystyle\left\lVert(T_{k}+\lambda I)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(T_{k}-T_{k,N})\right\rVert_{\text{Op}(L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\kappa^{5}}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\frac{\kappa^{5}}{\sqrt{\lambda N}}+\sqrt{\gamma_{T_{k}}(\lambda)}\right)\log(2/\delta)

\displaystyle=:C_{1}(N,\gamma)\log(2/\delta),

(36)

see
[
Tong et al. 2022
, Proposition 3.2]
for details.

Step 6:
Using the relation

\displaystyle BA^{-1}=(B-A)B^{-1}(B-A)A^{-1}+(B-A)B^{-1}+I

for the product of invertible operators
A
and
B
on Banach spaces, together with Eq. (
36
) and the bounds
\left\lVert(T_{k,N}+\lambda I)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\rVert_{\text{Op}(L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}
,
\left\lVert(T_{k}+\lambda I)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\rVert_{\text{Op}(L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}
(which follow from the spectral theorem), we get

\displaystyle\left\lVert(T_{k}+\lambda I)(T_{k,N}+\lambda I)^{-1}\right\rVert_{\text{Op}(L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\left\lVert(T_{k}+\lambda I)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(T_{k}-T_{k,N})\right\rVert^{2}_{\text{Op}(L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\left\lVert(T_{k}+\lambda I)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(T_{k}-T_{k,N})\right\rVert_{\text{Op}(L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))}+1

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{C_{1}(N,\lambda)\log(4/\delta)}{\sqrt{\lambda}}+1\right)^{2}

(37)

with confidence at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
, see
[
Tong et al. 2022
, Proposition 3.3]
for details.

Step 7:
The inequality

\displaystyle\left\lVert A^{\alpha}B^{\alpha}\right\rVert_{\text{Op}(L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))}\leq\left\lVert AB\right\rVert^{\alpha}_{\text{Op}(L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))},

valid for positive operators
A
,
B
on Hilbert spaces and
0<\alpha<1
, allows us to bound [A] in Eq. (
32
) as follows:

\displaystyle\left\lVert T_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}(T_{k,N}+\lambda I)^{-1}g_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}\leq\left\lVert(T_{k}+\lambda I)(T_{k,N}+\lambda I)^{-1}\right\rVert_{\text{Op}(L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))}\left\lVert(T_{k}+\lambda I)^{-\frac{1}{2}}g_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}.

To deal with
\left\lVert(T_{k}+\lambda I)^{-\frac{1}{2}}g_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}
, we apply Chebyshev’s inequality to the random variable

\displaystyle\xi_{B}=(T_{k}+\lambda I)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\varepsilon}(s)(G_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}m_{{\bf x}})(t)

with values in
L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})
. Using the spectral decomposition of
T_{k}
, and the assumptions (B3) and (B4) from
5.2
on
\bar{\varepsilon}
, we obtain
\text{E}(\xi_{B})=0
and
\text{E}(\left\lVert\xi_{B}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}^{2})=\sigma^{2}\gamma_{T_{k}}(\lambda)
and thus:

\displaystyle\left\lVert(T_{k}+\lambda I)^{-\frac{1}{2}}g_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}\leq\frac{2\bar{\sigma}}{\delta}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{T_{k}}(\lambda)}{n}}\leq\frac{\bar{\sigma}C_{1}(N,\lambda)}{\kappa^{5}\delta},

with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
.
Using Eq. (
8
), we get

\displaystyle[A]\leq\frac{\bar{\sigma}(\log(4/\delta))^{2}}{\kappa^{5}\delta}\left(\frac{C_{1}(N,\lambda)}{\sqrt{\lambda}}+1\right)^{2}C_{1}(N,\lambda),

(38)

with probability
1-\delta
. See
[
Tong et al. 2022
, Theorem 3.4]
for details.

Step 8:
Using
(T_{k,N}+\lambda I)^{-1}T_{k,N}f_{0}-f_{0}=-\lambda(T_{k,N}+\lambda I)^{-1}f_{0}
we obtain

\displaystyle[B]\leq\sqrt{\lambda}\left(\frac{C_{1}(N,\lambda)\log(4/\delta)}{\sqrt{\lambda}}+1\right)\left\lVert f_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})},

(39)

with probability
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
on the same event as Eq. (
36
). See
[
Tong et al. 2022
, Theorem 3.5]
for details.

Step 9:

To finish the proof of Corollary
7
, we set
\lambda=N^{-\frac{1}{1+c_{6}}}
and observe that

\displaystyle C_{1}(N,\lambda)\leq 2\kappa^{5}(\kappa^{5}+\sqrt{c_{5}})\sqrt{\lambda}.

(40)

Putting everything together, we end up with

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{X})}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}^{n}}\left\lVert(G_{{\bf z}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bf z}^{(N)}}^{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N},\lambda}-G)m_{{\bf x}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}P_{\mathcal{X}}^{n}({\bf x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}E(P)\leq 2([A]+[B])

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log\frac{4}{\delta}}{\delta^{2}}N^{-\frac{1}{1+c_{6}}}2\left(\frac{\bar{\sigma}^{2}\left(2\kappa^{5}\left(\kappa^{5}+\sqrt{c_{5}}\right)+1\right)^{6}}{\kappa^{10}}+\left(2\kappa^{5}\left(\kappa^{5}+\sqrt{c_{5}}\right)+1\right)^{2}\left\lVert f_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}^{2}\right),

where the first inequality follows from Eq. (
30
), Eq. (
32
) and Eq. (
8
), and the last one from Eq. (
38
), Eq. (
39
) in combination with Eq. (
40
), assumption (A5) (to control the effective dimension) and the specific choice of
\lambda
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
5
.

To upper bound
\left\lVert G_{{\bf z}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bf z}^{(N)}}^{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N},\lambda}-G\right\rVert^{2}_{\text{Op}(L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))}
, we first observe that it is enough to upper bound
\left\lVert\beta_{{\bf z}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bf z}^{(N)}}^{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N},\lambda}-\beta\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}
(i.e. the Hilbert Schmidt norm of the associated integral operators). Thus, recalling (
29
), we equivalently aim to control

\left\lVert G_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}(f_{0}-f_{N,\lambda})\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}
. Using the coercivity assumption (B2), we deduce that

\displaystyle\left\lVert G_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}f\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}

\displaystyle=\left\langle G_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}f,G_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}\leq c_{7}\left\langle G_{C}G_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}f,G_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}

\displaystyle=c_{7}\left\langle G_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}G_{C}G_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}f,f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}=c_{7}\left\langle T_{k}f,f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}=c_{7}\left\lVert T_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}f\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}

for all
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})
. Setting
f=f_{0}-f_{N,\lambda}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\left\lVert G_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}(f_{0}-f_{N,\lambda})\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})}\leq c_{7}\left\lVert T_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}(f_{0}-f_{N,\lambda})\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}^{2})},

so that the lemma is proven by Eq. (
30
) applying the result from Corollary
7
.
∎
