On the moments of the Ulam-Kac adder
Gage Bonner

Abstract
. Let { U ⁡ ( n ) } n ≥ 0 \{U(n)\}_{n\geq 0} be a sequence of independent random variables such that U ⁡ ( n ) U(n) is distributed uniformly on { 0 , 1 , 2 ​ … ​ n } \{0,1,2\dots n\} . The Ulam-Kac adder is the history-dependent random sequence defined by X n + 1 = X n + X U ⁡ ( n ) X_{n+1}=X_{n}+X_{U(n)} with the initial condition X 0 = 1 X_{0}=1 . We show that for each m ≥ 1 m\geq 1 , it holds that log ⁡ E ⁡ [ X n m ] / n \log E[X_{n}^{m}]/\sqrt{n} approaches a constant c m c_{m} as n → ∞ n\to\infty . Loose bounds are provided for the constants c m c_{m} .



1.
Introduction

History-dependent random sequences are sequences in which the distribution of the outcome of the
n^{\text{th}}
step depends non-trivially on the entire history of the process. Such sequences have numerous applications in sciences such as in polymer physics where basic models of confined polymer chains in small enclosures consist of lattice sites which are occupied sequentially. The points evolve according to a self-avoiding random walk
[
8
,
11
,
17
]
and the investigation of the analytical properties of such processes remains an active area of research
[
5
,
18
]
.

The focus of this paper is the so-called Ulam-Kac adder, following the “Ulam-Kac process” defined in
[
7
]
. Let
\{U(n)\}_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of independent random variables such that
U(n)
is distributed uniformly on
\{0,1,2\dots n\}
. The Ulam-Kac adder is the history-dependent random sequence defined by

(1.1)

X_{n+1}=X_{n}+X_{U(n)};\quad X_{0}=1.

This sequence was first investigated by Ulam

The first explicit results on the behavior of the sequence were obtained by Kac
[
14
]
where it was shown using generating functions and the method of steepest descent that

(1.2)

\displaystyle\log E[X_{n}]

\displaystyle\sim 2\sqrt{n},

(1.3)

\displaystyle\log E[X_{n}^{2}]

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{2n\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(5+\sqrt{17}}}\right)}.

where we write
f(n)\sim g(n)
if
\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)/g(n)=1
. In
[

]
, Eq. (
1.1
) is generalized to

(1.4)

X_{n+1}=X_{n}+\gamma X_{U(n)};\quad X_{0}=1,

and the analogues of Eqs. (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) are obtained. The Ulam-Kac adder was also considered by
[
3
]
in the context of extensions of random Fibonacci sequences. There, the following results are conjectured based on numerical evidence:

Conjecture 1.1
.

There is an increasing sequence of positive constants
(c_{m})_{m\geq 1}
such that

(1.5)

\log E[X_{n}^{m}]\sim c_{m}\sqrt{n}\quad\forall m\geq 1.

Conjecture 1.2
.

There are positive constants
\mu,\sigma
such that

(1.6)

\frac{\log X_{n}-\mu n^{1/2}}{\sigma n^{1/4}}\to\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})\text{ in law}.

In
[
7
]
, a Poisson-regularized continuous time analogue of the Ulam-Kac adder was introduced as an alternate method of analyzing the asymptotic behavior of moments. This was applied to the first and second moment in several generalizations and also in
[
21
]
to analyze the tail distribution of related sequences.

We mention here a connection between the Ulam-Kac adder and addition chains which does not appear to have been explicitly stated previously. An addition chain for
n
is a sequence
(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2}\dots a_{m-1},a_{m})
such that
a_{0}=1,a_{m}=n
where
a_{i}=a_{j}+a_{k}
for each
1\leq i\leq m
and some
0\leq j,k<i
. An open problem in computer science is to obtain efficiently the shortest addition chain for arbitrary
n

[
22
,
6
,
24
,
23
]
. This remains an active area of research in high-performance computing
[
2
,
15
]
. In the case
j=i-1
, the chain is said to have taken a
star step.

With reference to Figure
1
, we note that each realization of the Ulam-Kac adder is addition chain consisting only of star steps. Such chains are called Brauer chains, or simply star chains. Computing
P(X_{k}=n)
is equivalent to computing the number of Brauer chains for
n
that have length
k
. The first passage times of the Ulam-Kac adder are therefore directly related to minimal length Brauer chains.

The main result of this paper is Theorem
1.3
, which provides an affirmative answer to Conjecture
1.1
as well as bounds on the constants
c_{m}
appearing therein.

Theorem 1.3

(Main result)
.

For all
m\geq 1
, there exists a
c_{m}\in[2m,2^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tfrac{m}{2}+1}}\right)e^{3\sqrt{m}}}]
such that

(1.7)

\log E[X_{n}^{m}]\sim c_{m}\sqrt{n}.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2

m=1,2
moments. Then, Section
3
contains our outline of the main steps of the proof. In Section

5
contains all the proofs of the intermediate results required to obtain the main theorem. Finally, Section
6
contains our remarks on the tightness of the bounds in the main result as well as some additional conjectures related to this work. Appendix
A
contains supplementary proofs.

2.
The
m=1,2
moments

The basic method for the analysis of the Ulam-Kac adder is to condition on possible values of
U(n)
and telescoping the resulting series
[
14
]
, which we will now demonstrate. Defining
\mu_{n}\coloneqq E[X_{n}]
, Eq. (
1.1
) gives

(2.1)

\mu_{n+1}=\mu_{n}+E[X_{U(n)}]=\mu_{n}+\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\mu_{\ell},

where we have applied
E[X_{U(n)}|U(n)=\ell]=\mu_{\ell}.
We then take
n\to n+1
in Eq. (
2.1
) and eliminate the remaining sum to obtain

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mu_{n+2}

\displaystyle=\mu_{n+1}+\frac{1}{n+2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[mu_{n+1}+\sum_{m=0}^{n}\mu_{m}}}\right]

(2.3)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(n+2)\mu_{n+2}-2(n+2)\mu_{n+1}+(n+1)\mu_{n},

along with the initial conditions
\mu_{0}=1,\mu_{1}=2
. The classical Laguerre polynomials
L_{n}(x)
satisfy the recurrence relation
[
25
]

(2.4)

(2+k)L_{k+2}(x)-(2k+3-x)L_{k+1}(x)+(k+1)L_{k}(x)=0,\quad L_{0}=1,L_{1}=1-x.

Comparing this to Eq. (
2.3
), we see that they coincide when
x=-1
and therefore

(2.5)

\mu_{n}=L_{n}(-1)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\binom{n}{\ell}\frac{1}{\ell!}.

This directly leads to
\log\mu_{n}\sim 2\sqrt{n}
using the known asymptotics of Laguerre polynomials. See
[
3
,
16
]
for alternative calculations of the same result.

We will now compute the second moment of the Ulam-Kac adder
\sigma_{n}\coloneqq E[X_{n}^{2}]
by determining an ODE satisfied by the generating function of the sequence
\{\sigma_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
. By squaring Eq. (
1.1
), we obtain

(2.6)

X_{n+1}^{2}=X_{n}^{2}+2X_{n}X_{U(n)}+X_{U(n)}^{2}.

We would like to calculate the expected value of this equation, but the cross term
X_{n}X_{U(n)}
is not easily identified with
\sigma_{n}
. To obtain a closed system of recurrence relations, we define
\alpha_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}E[X_{n}X_{\ell}]
, then taking the expectation of Eq. (
2.6
) gives

(2.7)

\sigma_{n+1}=\sigma_{n}+\frac{2}{n+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{n}+\alpha_{n}}}\right)+\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\sigma_{\ell}.

Furthermore, by applying Eq. (
1.1
) to
\alpha_{n+1}
, we obtain

(2.8)

\alpha_{n+1}=\sigma_{n}+\alpha_{n}+\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{\ell}+2\alpha_{\ell}}}\right).

We define the generating functions
G(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sigma_{n}z^{n}
and
M(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}z^{n}
which have radii of convergence equal to 1 as will be shown in Section
3
. Multiplying Eqs. (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) through by
(n+1)z^{n}
and summing the resulting expressions over
n\geq 0
gives

(2.9a)

\displaystyle G^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(zG)^{\prime}+2(G+M)+\frac{1}{1-z}G,\quad G(0)=1

(2.9b)

\displaystyle M^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(zG)^{\prime}+(zM)^{\prime}+\frac{1}{1-z}(G+2M),\quad M(0)=0.

We can eliminate
M(z)
using Eq. (
2.9a
) to obtain

(2.10)

(z^{2}-6z+7)G(z)+(3z-8)(1-z)^{2}G^{\prime}(z)+(1-z)^{4}G^{\prime\prime}(z)=0,\quad G(0)=1,G^{\prime}(0)=4,

which is the ODE we sought. Substitution of the asymptotic form
e^{S(x)}
into Eq. (
2.10
) and application of the method of dominant balance
[
4
]
provides that
G(z)\sim\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{5+\sqrt{17}}{2}}}\right)\frac{1}{1-z}}}\right]
near
z=1
which implies that
\log\sigma_{n}\sim\sqrt{2(5+\sqrt{17})n}
.

3.
Outline of Proof

We will begin by constructing a closed set of recurrence relations for the
m^{\text{th}}
moment. Then, we will create the system of linear differential equations for the associated generating functions. By closely studying the properties of the matrices defining this system, we are able to apply certain technical results for the asymptotic behavior of their solutions and thereby obtain Theorem
1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{\geq 1}
be the set which contains the empty set
\emptyset
plus all nonempty multisets of finite cardinality on positive integers. We write an element
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{M}_{\geq 1}
as
\mathfrak{a}=[p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\dots p_{n}]
, where
p_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
and it is allowed that
p_{i}=p_{j}
for
i\neq j
. We call each
p_{i}
a
part
of
\mathfrak{a}
and write
|\mathfrak{a}|=n
when
\mathfrak{a}
has
n
parts. We reserve
\lVert\mathfrak{a}\rVert
to denote the sum of all parts of
|\mathfrak{a}|
. We also recall that addition and subtraction can be defined on multisets as follows. If
|\mathfrak{a}|,|\mathfrak{b}|\in\mathcal{M}_{\geq 1}
where
|\mathfrak{a}|=m
and
|\mathfrak{b}|=n
then
\mathfrak{a}+\mathfrak{b}
is the multiset with
m+n
parts created from the disjoint union of all parts of
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{b}
. Also,
\mathfrak{a}-\mathfrak{b}
is the unique multisubset
\mathfrak{c}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}
such that
\mathfrak{c}+\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{a}
. Successive addition and subtraction should be applied from left to right, e.g. we have
\mathfrak{a}+\mathfrak{b}-\mathfrak{c}=(\mathfrak{a}+\mathfrak{b})-\mathfrak{c}
.

Let
\mathcal{P}(n)=\{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{M}_{\geq 1}:\lVert\mathfrak{p}\rVert=n\}
be the set of all integer partitions of
n\geq 0
with the definition
\mathcal{P}(0)=\emptyset
. We define
\mathcal{C}^{(m)}
as the set of all terms that can occur in our system of recurrence relations for the
m^{\text{th}}
moment,

(3.1)

\mathcal{C}^{(m)}=\{C_{n}^{(m)}(q;\mathfrak{p}):n\geq 1,1\leq q\leq m,\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}(m-q)\},\quad m\geq 1,

such that each element of
\mathcal{C}^{(m)}
is given by

(3.2)

C_{n}^{(m)}(q;\mathfrak{p})=\sum_{\alpha_{1}=0}^{n-1}\sum_{\alpha_{2}=0}^{n-1}\cdots\sum_{\alpha_{|\mathfrak{p}|}=0}^{n-1}E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{n}^{q}\prod_{\ell=1}^{|\mathfrak{p}|}X^{p_{\ell}}_{\alpha_{\ell}}}}\right].

Motivated by the patterns observed in Eqs. (
2.7
) and (
2.8
), we generally expect to obtain recurrence relations each with three types of terms:
x_{n},x_{n}/(n+1)
and
\tfrac{1}{n+1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}x_{\ell}
. We therefore introduce the following notation:

(3.3a)

\displaystyle\overline{x}_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{x_{n}}{n+1},

(3.3b)

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}x_{\ell}.

We can now compute the most general recurrence relation, which is the result of the following Lemma whose proof is found in Section
5.1
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For a fixed
m\geq 1
, each
C_{n+1}^{(m)}(q;\mathfrak{p})\in\mathcal{C}^{(m)}
satisfies

(3.4a)

\displaystyle C_{n+1}^{(m)}(q;\mathfrak{p})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathfrak{b}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}}C_{n}^{(m)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q+\lVert\mathfrak{b}\rVert;\mathfrak{p}-\mathfrak{b}}}\right)

(3.4b)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\beta=1}^{q-1}\binom{q}{\beta}\sum_{\mathfrak{b}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}+[q-\beta]}\overline{C}_{n}^{(m)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\beta+\lVert\mathfrak{b}\rVert;\mathfrak{p}+[q-\beta]-\mathfrak{b}}}\right)

(3.4c)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{b}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}+[q]\\
\mathfrak{b}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\hat{C}_{n}^{(m)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lVert\mathfrak{b}\rVert;\mathfrak{p}+[q]-\mathfrak{b}}}\right).

We will show how Eq. (
3.4
) can be written as a finite linear system of first order difference equations. This will allow us to obtain preliminary results concerning the asymptotic behavior of the system. We first collect the
C_{n}^{(m)}(q;\mathfrak{p})
into a vector,
h^{(m)}_{n}
. The following definition is important for this construction. For a fixed
m\geq 1
, the vector
h^{(m)}_{n}=(C_{n}^{(m)}(q;\mathfrak{p}))
is said to be in
canonical ordering
if its entries are sorted first by ascending
|\mathfrak{p}|
then by descending
q
. The number of entries in this vector is
\mathfrak{C}(m)
.

Now, Eq. (
3.4
) can first be expressed generically as

(3.5)

h^{(m)}_{n+1}=\mathbf{C}_{1}h^{(m)}_{n}+\frac{1}{n+1}\mathbf{C}_{2}h^{(m)}_{n}+\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\mathbf{C}_{3}h^{(m)}_{\ell}.

Each of the matrices
\mathbf{C}_{1},\mathbf{C}_{2}
and
\mathbf{C}_{3}
have entries which depend only on
m
and can be extracted from Eqs. (
3.4a
), (
3.4b
) and (
3.4c
), respectively. Define

(3.6)

u^{(m)}_{n+1}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\mathbf{C}_{3}h^{(m)}_{\ell}=\mathbf{C}_{3}h^{(m)}_{n}+u^{(m)}_{n}.

Then, we have

(3.7)

\begin{pmatrix}h^{(m)}_{n+1}\\
u^{(m)}_{n+1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{C}_{1}+\tfrac{1}{n+1}\mathbf{C}_{2}&\tfrac{1}{n+1}\mathbf{C}_{3}\\
\mathbf{C}_{3}&\mathbf{I}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}h^{(m)}_{n}\\
u^{(m)}_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

We can now apply the following result due to Pituk on asymptotically constant systems of linear difference equations.

Theorem 3.2

(
[
20
]
, Theorem 1)
.

Let
x=(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a solution of the difference equation
x_{n+1}=\mathbf{L}_{n}x_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}
. If
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{L}_{n}=\mathbf{L}
for a constant matrix
\mathbf{L}
then either
x_{n}=0
for all large
n
or the limit

(3.8)

\rho(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}||x_{n}||^{1/n}

exists and is equal to the modulus of one of the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{L}
.

Examining Eq. (
3.7
), Theorem
3.2
applies with

(3.9)

\mathbf{L}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{C}_{1}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{C}_{3}&\mathbf{I}\end{pmatrix}.

We require the following proposition to compute the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{L}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

The matrix
\mathbf{C_{1}}
is lower triangular and each entry on its diagonal is
1
.

Proof.

The entries of
\mathbf{C}_{1}
are controlled by Eq. (
3.4a
); it implies that
(q;\mathfrak{p})
depends on each
(q+\lVert\mathfrak{b}\rVert;\mathfrak{p}-\mathfrak{b})
for
\mathfrak{b}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
. Each
(q+\lVert\mathfrak{b}\rVert;\mathfrak{p}-\mathfrak{b})
comes before
(q;\mathfrak{p})
in the canonical ordering, which implies the lower triangular structure. There is a unique
\mathfrak{b}
such that
(q+\lVert\mathfrak{b}\rVert;\mathfrak{p}-\mathfrak{b})=(q;\mathfrak{p})
, namely the empty set, and hence there are ones on the diagonal of
\mathbf{C}_{1}
.
∎

By Proposition
3.3
,
\mathbf{L}
and by Eq. (
3.9
),
\mathbf{L}
is a lower triangular matrix whose diagonal entries are all equal to 1, and hence
\mathbf{L}
has one eigenvalue equal to 1 of multiplicity
2\mathfrak{C}(m)
. Hence, by Theorem
3.2
, we have that each increasing solution
x_{n}
of Eq. (
3.7
) satisfies

(3.10)

\lim_{n\to\infty}||x_{n}||^{1/n}=1.

We remark that a theorem of Birkhoff and Trjitzinsky (Theorem 1 of
[
27
]
) then implies that
\log(x_{n})\sim n^{p}
for a rational
p\in(0,1)
.

To each sequence
(C_{n}^{(m)}(q;\mathfrak{p}))_{n\geq 0}
we associate a generating function
G^{(m)}_{q,\mathfrak{p}}(z)
defined by

(3.11)

G^{(m)}_{q,\mathfrak{p}}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}^{(m)}(q;\mathfrak{p})z^{n},

which has radius of convergence equal to 1 since the growth of
(C_{n}^{(m)}(q;\mathfrak{p}))_{n\geq 0}
is sub-exponential. We will apply Lemma
3.1
to obtain a system of linear differential equations satisfied by these generating functions. Therefore, we collect them into a vector
g^{(m)}(z)=(G^{(m)}_{q,\mathfrak{p}}(z))
in the canonical ordering. We will refer to generating functions by just their indices
(q;\mathfrak{p})
. Also,
(q;\mathfrak{p})_{i}
will refer to the
i^{\text{th}}
generating function when listed in the canonical ordering. We will similarly refer to the partition appearing in the label of the
i^{\text{th}}
generating function as
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
. The
m
label will generally be omitted for brevity except where required.

Now, we multiply each term in Eq. (
3.4
) by
(n+1)z^{n}
and sum the resulting equation over all
n\geq 0
to transform the recurrence relations to differential equations. Recalling that the generating functions of
a_{n},\overline{a}_{n}
and
\hat{a}_{n}
are
(zA(z))^{\prime},A(z)
and
A(z)/(1-z)
, respectively, we obtain the following system of differential equations

(3.12)

g^{\prime}(z)=z\mathbf{C}_{1}g^{\prime}(z)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbf{C}_{1}+\mathbf{C}_{2}+\frac{1}{1-z}\mathbf{C}_{3}}}\right]g(z).

The matrix
(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{C}_{1})^{-1}
is invertible for
0\leq z<1
(by Proposition
3.3
), so we can write

(3.13)

g^{\prime}(z)=\mathbf{R}(z)g(z),\quad\mathbf{R}(z)=(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{C}_{1})^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbf{C}_{1}+\mathbf{C}_{2}+\frac{1}{1-z}\mathbf{C}_{3}}}\right].

We make the substitution

(3.14)

g(z)=\mathbf{P}(z)h(z),\quad\mathbf{P}(z)=\text{diag}\{(1-z)^{-|\mathfrak{p}_{1}|},(1-z)^{-|\mathfrak{p}_{2}|},\dots,(1-z)^{-|\mathfrak{p}_{\mathfrak{C}(m)}|}\},

as well as
u=1/(1-z)
which results in a new system

(3.15)

h^{\prime}(u)=\mathbf{S}(u)h(u).

In Section
5.2
, we establish the following lemma which collects the relevant properties of
\mathbf{S}(u)
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For
u\geq 1
, and for some
N\geq 1
there is a sequence of matrices
\{\mathbf{M}_{i}\}_{0\leq i\leq N}
such that
\mathbf{M}_{0}
is primitive with Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue bounded above by
2^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tfrac{m}{2}+1}}\right)e^{3\sqrt{m}}}
and such that the matrix
\mathbf{S}(u)
defined in Eq. (
3.15
) can be expressed as

(3.16)

\mathbf{S}(u)=\sum_{i=0}^{N}u^{-i}\mathbf{M}_{i}.

To obtain the asymptotic behavior of
h(u)
, we will apply the following lemma, a consequence of the Hartman-Wintner theorem
[
13
]
whose proof is in Section
5.3
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
d,n\geq 1
, let
(\mathbf{M}_{i})_{0\leq i\leq n}
be
d\times d
matrices for each
i
and let
y(t)=(y_{i})_{1\leq i\leq d}
be a column vector. Consider the system of differential equations

(3.17)

y^{\prime}(t)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\mathbf{M}_{\ell}t^{-\ell}}}\right)y(t);\quad y(1)=y_{0},t\geq 1.

Then, if
\mathbf{M}_{0}
is a non-negative, irreducible matrix with Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue
\lambda
, it holds for some
\alpha,\beta>0
that

(3.18)

y_{i}(t)\sim\alpha(t^{\beta}+o(t^{\beta}))e^{\lambda t},\quad 1\leq i\leq d.

We can now prove Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

By Lemmas
3.4
and
3.5
, for a given
m\geq 1
, Eq. (
3.15
) admits solutions
(h_{i}^{(m)}(u))_{1\leq i\leq\mathfrak{C}(m)}
such that for some
\alpha,\beta>0
,

(3.19)

h_{i}^{(m)}(u)\sim\alpha(u^{\beta}+o(u^{\beta}))e^{\lambda_{m}u},\quad 1\leq i\leq\mathfrak{C}(m),

where
\lambda_{m}
is the Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue of the matrix
\mathbf{M}_{0}
defined in Lemma
3.4
. Hence, we have
G_{m,\emptyset}^{(m)}(z)\sim\frac{\alpha}{(1-z)^{\beta}}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\lambda_{m}}{1-z}}}\right)
which by the method of saddle point asymptotics
[
10
]
implies that
\log E[X_{n}^{m}]\sim 2\sqrt{\lambda_{m}n}
.

We will now put the required bounds on
\lambda_{m}
. Note that a lower bound can be obtained quickly; by Jensen’s inequality, we have

(3.20)

E[X_{n}^{m}]\geq E[X_{n}]^{m}\implies\log E[X_{n}^{m}]\sim c_{m}\sqrt{n}\quad\text{for }c_{m}\geq 2m.

To obtain an upper bound, we apply Lemma
3.4
to obtain
\lambda_{m}\leq 2^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tfrac{m}{2}+1}}\right)e^{3\sqrt{m}}}
and hence that

(3.21)

\log E[X_{n}^{m}]\sim c_{m}\sqrt{n}\quad\text{for }c_{m}\leq 2^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tfrac{m}{2}+1}}\right)e^{3\sqrt{m}}}.

Combining Eqs. (
3.20
) and (
3.21
) completes the proof.
∎

4.
Example:
m=3

We will now compute the exact value of the third moment of the Ulam-Kac adder,
E[X_{n}^{3}]
. This has not been explicitly computed previously, although
[
3
]
estimated
\log E[X_{n}^{3}]\sim 6.5\sqrt{n}
based on numerical evidence. When
m=3
, there are four types of sequences in
\mathcal{C}^{(3)}
, namely

Proposition 5.3
.

The matrix
\mathbf{B}_{1}\mathbf{E}
is primitive, where
\mathbf{B}_{1}

Proof.

To show that
\mathbf{B}_{1}\mathbf{E}
is primitive we will show that the adjacency graph defined by
\mathbf{B}_{1}\mathbf{E}
with vertices labelled by the
(q,\mathfrak{p})_{i}
is strongly connected. Recall that the entries of
\mathbf{E}
imply that
(q;\mathfrak{p})
can reach either
(\beta;\mathfrak{p}+[q-\beta])
for some
1\leq\beta\leq q-1
or by
(p;\mathfrak{p}-[p])
where
[p]\subseteq\mathfrak{p}+[q]
and the entries of
\mathbf{B}_{1}
imply that
(q;\mathfrak{p})
can reach
(q+\lVert\mathfrak{b}\rVert,\mathfrak{p}-\mathfrak{b})
for any
\mathfrak{b}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
. To demonstrate that the associated adjacency graph is connected, we will show that there is a bidirectional path between any
(q;\mathfrak{p})
and
(m,\emptyset)
. Suppose first that we are starting at some
(q;\mathfrak{p})
and want to find a path to
(m;\emptyset)
. Then,
\mathbf{E}
provides a path from
(q;\mathfrak{p})
to itself and
\mathbf{B}_{1}
provides a path from
(m;\emptyset)
. Now we would like to find a path starting from
(m;\emptyset)
back to
(q;\mathfrak{p})
. First,
\mathbf{E}
provides a path from
(m;\emptyset)
to
(m-p_{1};[p_{1}])
then
\mathbf{B}_{1}
provides a path from
(m-p_{1};[p_{1}])
to itself. Iterating this procedure,
\mathbf{E}
provides a path from
(m-p_{1},[p_{1}])
to
(m-p_{1}-p_{2},[p_{1},p_{2}])
and
\mathbf{B_{1}}
provides a path from
(m-p_{1}-p_{2},[p_{1},p_{2}])
to itself. It’s clear that that there is a
1\leq k\leq\mathfrak{C}(m)
such that
q=m-\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}p_{\ell}
and hence there will be a path from
(m;\emptyset)
to
(q;\mathfrak{p})
as required. This completes the proof that the matrix
\mathbf{B}_{1}\mathbf{E}
is primitive.

Now we will establish the appropriate bounds on the Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue
\lambda
of
\mathbf{B}_{1}\mathbf{E}
. To do this, we will use the fact that
\lambda
is bounded above by the maximum sum of the columns of
\mathbf{B}_{1}\mathbf{E}
. By Lemma
3.1
, the largest entry that can appear in
\mathbf{C}_{1}
is
\binom{m}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\leq 2^{m}
. Since
\mathfrak{C}(m)\leq e^{3\sqrt{m}}

[
12
]
, Proposition
5.1
, implies via induction that the largest entry of
\mathbf{B}_{1}
is bounded above by

. Similarly, the largest entry that can appear in
\mathbf{E}
is
\binom{m}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\leq 2^{m}
. It follows that each entry of
\mathbf{B}_{1}\mathbf{E}
is bounded above by
2^{(m+1)e^{3\sqrt{m}}}e^{3\sqrt{m}}
and hence that
\lambda
is bounded above by
2^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tfrac{m}{2}+1}}\right)e^{3\sqrt{m}}}
.
∎

To prove Lemma

Theorem 5.4

(

Let
\mathbf{A}

y(t)=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mu(t-t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\delta(\tau)\,\text{d}\tau}}\right]\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[v+o(1)}}\right]\quad\text{as }t\to\infty,

Theorem 5.5
