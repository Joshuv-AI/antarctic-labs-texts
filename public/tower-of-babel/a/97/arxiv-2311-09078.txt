Majority dynamics on random graphs: the multiple states case
Jordan Chellig Nikolaos Fountoulakis

Abstract
We study the evolution of majority dynamics with more than two states on the binomial random graph G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p) . In this process, each vertex has a state in { 1 , … , k } \{1,\ldots,k\} , with k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 , and at each round every vertex adopts state i i if it has more neighbours in state i i than in any other state. Ties are resolved randomly. We show that with high probability the process reaches unanimity in at most three rounds, if n ​ p ≫ n 2 / 3 np\gg n^{2/3} .



1
Majority dynamics on a graph

In this paper, we will study the
synchronous
version of majority dynamics on a graph
G=(V,E)
.
After each round, every vertex
v\in V
will be in one of the states in
\mathcal{S}_{k}:=\{1,\ldots,k\}
, for an integer
k\geq 2
.
For
t\geq 1
, let
S_{t}:V\to\mathcal{S}_{k}
be the

state
of the vertices after the completion of the
t
th round, whereas
S_{0}:V\to\mathcal{S}_{k}
denotes the initial state.
The version of the process we will analyse evolves as follows. Let
t\geq 1
and for each vertex
v\in V
and

i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
, let
n_{i}(v;t-1)=\left|N_{G}(v)\cap\{u:S_{t-1}(u)=i\}\right|
; this is the number of neighbours
of
v
in
G
which are at state
i
after round
t-1
.
Then
S_{t}(v)
is selected uniformly at random from the set
\argmax\{n_{i}(v;t-1)\}_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}
. In particular,
if there is
i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
such that
n_{i}(v;t-1)>n_{j}(v;t-1)
for all
j\not=i
, that is,

\argmax\{n_{j}(v;t-1)\}_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}=\{i\}
, then
S_{t}(v)=i
.

Models of this flavour were introduced in other fields several decades ago.
A prominent example is a model that was introduced by M. Granovetter
[
12
]
, aiming to
explain the emergence of collective behaviour through the interaction of individual preferences and their aggregation. More specifically, the aim was to explain social phenomena such as the diffusion of innovations as well as the spread of rumours and diseases. In this setting the choice is among two states,
active
or
inactive
, and each vertex in the network has a different
activation threshold
.
In particular, a vertex which has threshold
r
having observed at least
r
other vertices adopting a new behaviour it adopts it too. The distribution of the thresholds among the members of the population is assumed to have cdf
F
.This process is assumed to take place on the complete graph on
n
vertices and

F(x)
denotes the fraction of vertices that have threshold at most
xn
. If after round
t
there is an
r(t)
fraction of individuals that are active and
F(r(t))>r(t)
, then
r(t+1)=F(r(t))
. This process reaches an equilibrium which is a fixed point of
F
.
Processes of similar flavour were also described in
[
6
,
7
,
25
]
.

In physics, threshold models where all vertices have the same activation threshold have been used in order to
explain magnetisation in materials whose molecular structure is described by a lattice. Such is the class of
bootstrap processes, which were introduced by Challupa, Leath and Reich
[
5
]
in that
context, but have generated a long line of research within combinatorics and probability theory. A key difference between these two models and the process that is our main focus is that the former are
monotone
: once a vertex is activated it remains active. However, in the class of processes we will consider a vertex may switch between states an arbitrary number of times.

Another threshold model was developed in the 1940s by McCulloch and Pitts
[
17
]
to describe neuronal activity. In this model, a neuron receives positive and negative inputs and it fires, if their sum is positive. Neurons are interconnected forming a large hierarchical network.

From a purely mathematical perspective, it would make sense to study the behaviour of such a process in a general graph, which may be thought of as a representation of a population. A simpler nevertheless non-trivial version of the above would be to consider a population represented by a graph and there are two opinions/states available; during each round each vertex adopts an opinion if at least half of the neighbours have adopted it. This modification gives rise to
majority dynamics
with
k=2
states.

When two states are available one can adopt a deterministic resolution rule, whereby a vertex does not change
state if there is a tie among the numbers of neighbours having each one of the two states. In this case, a result of Goles and Olivos
[
11
]
implies that if the underlying graph is finite, then the system will eventually become periodic with period at most 2. In other words, there is a limiting behaviour as the number of rounds grows and one may further ask how does this limit look like.

A natural question is about the effect the geometry of the underlying graph has to the
limiting behaviour.
Mossel et al.
[
19
]
considered majority dynamics with two states, say 0 and 1, where each vertex is initially assigned state 0 with probability
1/2+\delta
, independently of any other vertex, for some
fixed
\delta>0
.
They consider a classification of the vertices into
social types

where two vertices have the same social type if there is an automorphism of the graph that maps one vertex to the other. In other words, vertices of the same social type are indistinguishable within
G
. Let
m(G)
be the size of the smallest class of the social types. Their main result shows that the probability that the dynamics arrives at unanimity of one of the two states converges to 1 polynomially fast in
1/m(G)
with exponent proportional to
\delta/\log(1/\delta)
.
(The social types are not directly related to the states as the initial assignment of states is independent of the social type.)
On the other hand, they show the existence of a sequence of graphs
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
on
n
vertices for which this probability stays bounded away from 1 as
n\to\infty
. In this example,
m(G_{n})
is small and does not tend to infinity.

The other parameter they considered is the expansion of the underlying graph. In particular, Mossel et al.
[
19
]
considered a sequence
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
d
-regular graphs which are
\lambda
-expanders (the absolute value of the second largest eigenvalue of their adjacency matrix is no larger than
\lambda
)
with
\lambda/d\leq 3/16
.
In majority dynamics with
k\geq 2
states
\{1,\ldots,k\}
starting from a random initial configuration where the probability of state
1
exceeds the probability of state
i\not=1
by at least
c\log k/\sqrt{d}
, they show that state
1
eventually dominates and unanimity is achieved with high probability as
n\to\infty
.

Benjamini et al.
[
2
]
considered this question in the context of the binomial
random graph
G(n,p)
, where each pair of vertices from the set
V_{n}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
appears as an edge with probability
p=p(n)
independently of any other pair and the initial state is selected uniformly at random.
They conjectured that if
np\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, then this dynamics with two states starting from a uniformly random configuration will arrive at almost unanimity with one of the two states occupying at least

(1-\varepsilon)n
of the vertices with high probability as
n\to\infty
, for any
\varepsilon>0
.
They proved that unanimity is attained for
np\gg n^{1/2}
with probability that is asymptotically
bounded away from 0. Later Fountoulakis et al.
[
8
]
showed that this is the case with
probability tending to
1
as
n\to\infty
. Moreover, they showed that unanimity occurs in at most 4 rounds.
This result was refined by Berkowitz and Devlin
[
3
]
. They showed that for any given initial configuration, the number of vertices in one of the two states after the execution of one round satisfies a central limit theorem (over the random choice of the graph). Using this result, they obtained more precise results for the number of rounds until unanimity is achieved, when the initial configuration is uniformly random, provided that
np\gg n^{1/2}
. Tran and Vu
[
24
]
also gave another proof of the main theorem of
[
8
]
.
Furthermore, Chakraborti et al.
[
4
]
reduced the value of
p
for which unanimity is reached by majority dynamics under a uniformly random initial configuration to
p\gg n^{-3/5}\log n
.
More recently, Tran and Kim
[
15
]
and independently Jaffe
[
13
]
reduced this lower bound to
p\gg n^{-2/3}
.

Tran and Vu
[
24
]
also explored non-random initial configurations and, in particular, configurations
where one of the states has a very small majority over the other. They showed that when the number of vertices in the most popular state is at least 6 more than the number of vertices in the other state, majority dynamics on
G(n,1/2)
reaches unanimity with the dominating state with probability at least
0.9
. They extended this to
G(n,p)
for any fixed
p\in(0,1)
and majority
c_{n}
(possibly depending on
n
).
This result was strengthened by Sah and Sawhney
[
21
]
, who gave a precise estimate on
the probability that the initial majority of one of the two states becomes dominance of that state, for values of
p
such that
(\log n)^{-1/16}\leq p\leq 1-(\log n)^{-1/16}
.

On the other hand, initial configurations with relatively high initial majority have been proved to be reinforced and
lead to unanimity for much smaller values of
p
. For example, Tran and Vu
[
24
]
showed that if

np\geq(2+o(1))\log n
an initial majority of order
1/p
is re-enforced to unanimity with high probability as
n\to\infty
. Zehmakan
[
26
]
had shown this for
np>(1+o(1))\log n
(that is, just above the connectivity threshold) and an initial majority that is asymptotically larger than
(n/p)^{1/2}
. In the same work,
Zehmakan refined the results of Mossel et al.
[
19
]
for
d
-regular expander graphs.
Earlier versions of this result were shown by Gärtner and Zehmakan in
[
10
]
and in
[
9
]
(where the underlying graph is a grid).

The asynchronous version of majority dynamics with two states in which vertices update their states one-at-a-time
was studied by Mohan and Prałat in
[
18
]
. More precisely, at each round a vertex is selected
uniformly at random and updates their state, where in the case of a tie the vertex does not change state.
The initial state is not selected uniformly at random but there is a
\delta
-bias towards one of the two states, for some fixed
\delta>0
.
They show that when the underlying graph is a random graph distributed as
G(n,p)
with
np\gg\log n
as
n\to\infty
but
p=o(1)
, this state dominates. However, this is not the case when
p=\Omega(1)
. They show that the other state may dominate with probability that is asymptotically bounded away from
0
.

1.1
Results

The aim of this contribution is to consider the synchronous process for
k
states with
k>2
and study the limiting behaviour of
S_{t}
as
t\to\infty
when the underlying graph is
G(n,p)
, the
binomial random graph
on
n
vertices. As it is customary in the theory of random graphs, we will be interested in asymptotics as
n\to\infty
.
Let
\boldsymbol{\lambda}:=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})
be such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}=1
and
\lambda_{i}>0
, for
i=1,\ldots,k
; we write that
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\triangle_{1}
.
The initial configuration
S_{0}
will be selected randomly, where each
v\in V_{n}
will have
S_{0}(v)=i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
with probability
\lambda_{i}
, independently of all other vertices and the edges of
G(n,p)
.
We write
S_{0}\sim\boldsymbol{\lambda}
to denote this random choice.

Our main result is that for any choice of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\triangle_{1}
, if
p
is not too rapidly decaying as a function of
n
, then the dynamics will reach unanimity in a bounded number of rounds with high probability.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\triangle_{1}
.
Consider majority dynamics on
G(n,p)
with
np\gg n^{2/3}
but
\limsup_{n\to\infty}p<1
. For any
n
sufficiently large,
with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
the following holds:
if
S_{0}\sim\boldsymbol{\lambda}
,
then
S_{3}(v)=s
, for all
v\in V_{n}
, for some
s\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
.

The lower bound on
p
appears also in the context of the analysis of the label propagation algorithm on the
binomial random graph by Kiwi et al.
[
16
]
. This is an algorithm whose purpose is community
detection in a network. Initially, each vertex selects a label from
[0,1]
uniformly at random independently of any
other vertex. (So with probability 1, all vertices begin with different labels, but this may not be the case in subsequent rounds.) Thereafter,
the labels are updated
synchronously by adopting the label that appears most frequently in the neighbourhood of each vertex, but
resolving ties towards the smallest label, in the first round, and randomly in subsequent rounds. Among other results, the authors show that if
np\gg n^{2/3}
,
then a.a.s. the smallest label dominates the entire random graph.

1.2
Outline of the proof and the structure of this work

The proof of Theorem
1.1
relies on a careful analysis of the first round.

1.

We begin with showing that with high probability during the first round all states but those which are in
\mathcal{M}_{0}:=\argmax\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}
are eliminated - this is Claim
2.4
.
We do this using a first moment argument, showing that with high probability for any two states
i,j\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
with
\lambda_{i}<\lambda_{j}
, all vertices have more neighbours at state
j
in
S_{0}
than in state
i
.

2.

Thereafter, for any vertex
v\in V_{n}
and any
i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
, we estimate the
probability that
S_{1}(v)=i
. To this end we show first that with high probability a small number of
vertices face a tie during the execution of the first round (Claim
2.3
).
So most vertices adopt their state
i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
in round 1 through a clear majority within the corresponding subset of vertices that have been set at state
i
initially.
For such a vertex
v
we say that
S_{1}(v)=i
in
a strong sense
.

3.

We estimate the probability that
S_{1}(v)=i
in a strong sense in Section
3.1
. In particular, we estimate this probability conditional on the size of the neighbourhood of
v
among the vertices who are initially in a state in

\mathcal{M}_{0}
. The main tool that we deploy here is a local limit theorem (Lemma
2.6
), through which we obtain an asymptotic estimate on the probability that
v
has a certain number of neighbours in each subset of vertices that are initially at some state in
\mathcal{M}_{0}
.
The other tool that we use is an anti-concentration result (Lemma
2.11
) for the initial configuration, which we prove in Section
2.3
.
The latter states that with high probability any two parts of the initial configuration differ in size by
\Omega(n^{1/2})
.
We combine these tools in Lemma
3.1
to deduce a lower bound on the difference between the probability that

v
takes the state corresponding to the
i
th largest part after the first round in a strong sense and the probability that it takes the state
which corresponds to the
i+1
st largest part.

4.

Thereafter, a second moment argument in Section
3.2
shows that the number
of vertices that adopt state
i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
is concentrated around its expected value. So after the execution of the first round, the vertex set is partitioned into as many sets as
|\mathcal{M}_{0}|
together with a smaller set that consists of the vertices that face a tie during the first round.

5.

Ordering those parts that correspond to states in
\mathcal{M}_{0}
according to their size, we conclude that consecutive parts differ in size by
\Omega(np^{1/2})
.
The problem that arises at this stage of the proof is that we have exposed all edges of the random graph.
To bypass this issue, Lemma
4.1
in Section
4
uses a union bound in order to show that most vertices will have many neighbours in the largest among those parts and the corresponding state will dominate after the execution of the second round. It is for the union bound to work that we need the assumption that
np\gg n^{2/3}
. This is a general lemma which implies that with high probability for any partition which is similar to the partition we end up with after the first round most vertices will have most of their neighbours inside the largest part.

6.

We have proved that all vertices in
V_{n}
, except for a subset of size
o(n)
, will have adopted that state. We finish the proof showing that during the third round all vertices will have switched to that state.

1.3
Some notation and concentration bounds

We denote by
\mathbb{N}_{0}
the set of non-negative integers, that is
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}
.

For a graph
G=(V,E)
a vertex
v\in V
and a subset
S\subseteq V
, we let
N_{S}(v)
be the set of neighbours of
v
in
S
. We also set
N_{G}(v)=N_{V}(v)
, that is, the set of all neighbours of
v
in the graph
G
.
The binomial random graph
G(n,p)
will have
V_{n}
as its vertex set.

For
i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
, let
S_{0}^{-1}(i)=\{v\in V_{n}\ :\ S_{0}(v)=i\}
, and
n_{i}=|S_{0}^{-1}(i)|
.
For two distinct vertices
v,v^{\prime}\in V_{n}
, we set
n_{i,v}=|S_{0}^{-1}(i)\setminus\{v\}|
and

n_{i,v,v^{\prime}}=|S_{0}^{-1}(i)\setminus\{v,v^{\prime}\}|
.

Furthermore, for any vertex
v\in V_{n}
, we denote by
n_{i}(v)
the number of neighbours of
v
in

S_{0}^{-1}(i)
, that is,
n_{i}(v):=|N_{S_{0}^{-1}(i)}(v)|
. For an arbitrary subset
S\subseteq V
, we let

d_{S}(v)=|N_{S}(v)|
; we will use the latter primarily in Section
4
.

As we have already pointed out in the outline of the proof of our main theorem, it is the classes

\{S_{0}^{-1}(i)\}_{i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}}
that play a significant role, as every other state will disappear after the first
round. We will denote by
S^{*}
the set
\cup_{i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}}S_{0}^{-1}(i)
and let
n^{*}:=|S^{*}|
. Also,
we set
S_{*}=V_{n}\setminus S^{*}
.
For two distinct vertices
v,v^{\prime}\in V_{n}
, we set
n_{v}^{*}=|S^{*}\setminus\{v\}|
and

n^{*}_{v,v^{\prime}}=|S^{*}\setminus\{v,v^{\prime}\}|
.

For a vertex
v\in V_{n}
, we set
n^{*}(v):=|N_{S^{*}}(v)|
.
Similarly, we set
n^{*}_{v}(v^{\prime})=|N_{S^{*}\setminus\{v\}}(v^{\prime})|
and

n^{*}_{v,v^{\prime}}(v)=|N_{S^{*}\setminus\{v,v^{\prime}\}}(v)|
. In Appendix
A
, we will use the notation
n_{j,v^{\prime}}(v)=|N_{S_{0}^{-1}(j)\setminus\{v^{\prime}\}}(v)|
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in[0,1]
, we denote by
\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)
the binomial distribution with parameters
n
and
p
. For a vector
\boldsymbol{q}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k-1})
such that
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k-1},1-\sum_{j<k}q_{j})\in\triangle_{1}
,
we denote by
\mult(n;\boldsymbol{q})
the multinomial distribution on a collection of i.i.d. random variables that has size

i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
with probability

, if
i<k
, and
1-\sum_{j<k}q_{j}
,

.

random variables. The inequality we will use follows from Theorem 2.1 in
[
14
]
.
If
X
is a random variable such that

, then for any

we have

\mathbb{P}\left(|X-nq|\geq\delta nq\right)\leq 2e^{-\delta^{2}nq/3}.

(1)

A Chernoff-type bound also holds for the hypergeometric distribution

. Suppose that on a groundset

of size

Then

\mathbb{P}\left(|S\cap X_{M}|-s(M/N)|\geq\lambda\sqrt{s}\right)\leq 2e^{-2\lambda^{2}/(1-f_{s})},

(2)

where

We will also use the local limit theorem for the binomial distribution - see for example the monograph by Petrov
[

Let

Finally, we say that a sequence of events

2
Some observations about the initial configuration

2.1
On the concentration of state classes and the vertex degrees therein

The definition of the initial state implies that the vector
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})
follows the multinomial
distribution
\mult(n;(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k-1}))
.
In particular,
n_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,\lambda_{i})
for any
i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
.

Claim 2.1
.

With probability at least
1-o(n^{-1})
, for all
i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
we have
|n_{i}-n\lambda_{i}|\leq\log n\sqrt{n}
.

Proof.

The Chernoff bound (
1
)
implies that
\mathbb{P}\left(|n_{i}-n\lambda_{i}|>\log n\sqrt{n}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\Omega(\log^{2}n)\right)
.
Now, the union bound over all
i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
(as
k
does not depend on
n
)
implies that a.a.s. for all
i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
we have
|n_{i}-n\lambda_{i}|\leq\log n\sqrt{n}
.
∎

We denote this event
\mathcal{E}_{0}
and write
S_{0}\in\mathcal{E}_{0}
to say that
\mathcal{E}_{0}
is realised. (The choice of the
\log
function is somewhat arbitrary as
k
is fixed. Here and below, we will not try to make optimal choices, unless we need to do so.)
Let
\lambda_{*}=\sum_{i\not\in\mathcal{M}_{0}}\lambda_{i}
and, with
k_{0}=|\mathcal{M}_{0}|
,
\lambda^{*}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}}\lambda_{i}=k_{0}\lambda_{\max}
.
Recall that we let
n^{*}=|S^{*}|
.
Claim
2.1
implies that
with probability
1-o(n^{-1})
,
\left|n^{*}-n\lambda^{*}\right|<k_{0}n^{1/2}\log n
.
We denote this event by
\mathcal{P}_{0}
.
(Note that if
\mathcal{E}_{0}
is realised, then
\mathcal{P}_{0}
is realised as well.)

Another important random variable is
n_{i}(v)
for
i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
and any
v\in V_{n}
.
Conditional on an initial configuration
\{S_{0}^{-1}(1),\ldots,S_{0}^{-1}(k)\}
, we have
n_{i}(v)\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n_{i}-1_{S_{0}(v)=i},p)
, where
1_{S_{0}(v)=i}=1
, if
S_{0}(v)=i
and 0 otherwise.

Claim 2.2
.

Conditional on an initial configuration
S_{0}\in\mathcal{E}_{0}
,
we have that a.a.s. all vertices
v\in V_{n}
have
|n_{i}(v)-n_{i}p|\leq\log n\sqrt{np}
for all
i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
.

Proof.

This
follows from the Chernoff bound and Markov’s inequality. Indeed, conditional on a configuration
\{S_{0}^{-1}(1),\ldots,S_{0}^{-1}(k)\}
that satisfies
|n_{i}-n\lambda_{i}|\leq\log n\sqrt{n}
for all
i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
(that is, which
satisfies
\mathcal{E}_{0}
), the Chernoff bound yields that for a vertex
v\in V_{n}

\mathbb{P}\left(|n_{i}(v)-n_{i}p|>\log n\sqrt{np}\right)=\exp\left(-\Omega(\log^{2}n)\right)=o(n^{-1})
.
The union bound (over all vertices and states
i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
) and Markov’s inequality imply that a.a.s. all vertices
v\in V_{n}
satisfy
|n_{i}(v)-n_{i}p|\leq\log n\sqrt{np}
.
∎

Recall also that
N_{S^{*}}(v)
denotes the neighbourhood
of
v
in
S^{*}
and
n_{v}^{*}=|S^{*}\setminus\{v\}|
.
Then the random variable
n^{*}(v)=|N_{S^{*}}(v)|
has
n^{*}(v)\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n_{v}^{*},p)
.
We will consider the event

\mathcal{N}^{*}_{v}:=\{|n^{*}(v)-n_{v}^{*}p|\leq k_{0}\log n\cdot(np)^{1/2}\}
.

With a slight abuse of notation, for an event
\mathcal{E}
and
1_{\mathcal{E}}
denoting its indicator function, and random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
we write,

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{E}|X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[1_{\mathcal{E}}|X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\right].

We saw that on the event
\mathcal{E}_{0}
we have
|n^{*}-n\lambda^{*}|<k_{0}n^{1/2}\log n
.
So Claim
2.2
and its proof yield that on the event
\mathcal{E}_{0}

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{N}^{*}_{v}|n^{*}\right)=1-o(n^{-1}).

(3)

We next discuss a bound on the (conditional) probability that a vertex
v
has the same number of neighbours in two
parts
S_{0}^{-1}(i)
and
S_{0}^{-1}(j)
, for
i\not=j
. This will be useful as any vertex which ends up choosing its state during
round 1 through the resolution of a tie should have the same number of neighbours in at least two parts.
Let
T_{1}
denote the set of vertices that face a tie during round 1.

Claim 2.3
.

The set
T_{1}\subset V_{n}
a.a.s. has

|T_{1}|\leq\log^{2}n\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}.

We call this event
\mathcal{E}_{T_{1}}
.

Proof.

Note that conditional on a realisation of
\{S_{0}^{-1}(1),\ldots,S_{0}^{-1}(k)\}
the random variables
n_{\ell}(v)\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n_{\ell}-1_{S_{0}(v)=\ell},p)
, for
\ell\in\{i,j\}
, are independent.
In particular, we condition on a realisation of
\{S_{0}^{-1}(1),\ldots,S_{0}^{-1}(k)\}
that satisfies
\mathcal{E}_{0}
; by Claim
2.1
the probability that this does not occur is
o(n^{-1})
.
To bound the (conditional) probability that
n_{i}(v)=n_{j}(v)
we will make use of the local limit theorem (Theorem
1.2
).
Firstly, the proof of Claim
2.2
implies that with probability
1-o(n^{-1})
we have

|n_{\ell}(v)-(n_{\ell}-1_{S_{0}(v)=\ell})p|\leq\log n\sqrt{np}
for
\ell\in\{i,j\}
.
Theorem
1.2
implies that for
s_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|s_{i}-(n_{i}-1_{S_{0}(v)=i})p|\leq\log n\sqrt{np}

we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(n_{i}(v)=n_{j}(v)=s_{i}\mid S_{0}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(n_{i}(v)=s_{i}\mid S_{0}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(n_{j}(v)=s_{i}\mid S_{0}\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{np}\right).

Moreover, this upper bound is uniform over these choices of
s_{i}
. As there are
O(\log n\sqrt{np})
choices for the value
of
s_{i}
we conclude that

\mathbb{P}\left(n_{i}(v)=n_{j}(v)\right)=O\left(\frac{\log n}{(np)^{1/2}}\right)+o(n^{-1})=O\left(\frac{\log n}{(np)^{1/2}}\right).

(4)

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|T_{1}|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in V_{n}}\mathbb{P}\left(\exists i\not=j\ n_{i}(v)=n_{j}(v)\right)\leq\sum_{v\in V_{n}}\sum_{i\not=j\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\mathbb{P}\left(n_{i}(v)=n_{j}(v)\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:prb_eq_nbs}}}{{=}}O\left(k^{2}\log n\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\right)=O\left(\log n\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\right).

Markov’s inequality then implies the claim.
∎

Recall that we set
\mathcal{M}_{0}=\argmax\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}
and now let
\lambda_{\max}=\max\mathcal{S}_{k}
; thus, any
i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
has
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{\max}
.
We will show that all states outside
\mathcal{M}_{0}
are eliminated in the first round and only the states in

\mathcal{M}_{0}
survive a.a.s. This means that, with high probability, what determines
S_{1}(v)
, for any
v\in V_{n}
are the random variables
\{n_{i}(v)\}_{i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}}
.

Claim 2.4
.

A.a.s. all
v\in V_{n}
have
S_{1}(v)\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
.

Proof.

Firstly, let us observe that Claim
2.1
implies that there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that a.a.s. for any
j\not\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
and any
i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
, we have
\frac{1}{n}\left(n_{i}-n_{j}\right)>\varepsilon
.

Let us condition on a realisation of
\{S_{0}^{-1}(1),\ldots,S_{0}^{-1}(k)\}
such that the above event is realised as well.
A simple application of the Chernoff bound (cf. the proof of Claim
2.2
) together with the union bound imply that a.a.s.
all vertices
v\in V_{n}
have

n_{j}(v)<n_{i}(v)
for any
j\not\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
and any
i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
. Thus, the claim holds.
∎

Another useful bound will be on the absolute difference
|n_{i}(v)-n^{*}(v)(n_{i}/n^{*})|
, having set
n^{*}(v)=|N_{S^{*}}(v)|
, conditional on a realisation of
S_{0}\in\mathcal{E}_{0}
.
Conditioning on
S_{0}\in\mathcal{E}_{0}
and
the value of
n^{*}(v)
, the neighbourhood of
v
in
S^{*}
can be any subset of
S^{*}
of size

n^{*}(v)
with the same probability. Therefore, for any
i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
we have

n_{i}(v)\sim\mathrm{Hyper}(n^{*},n_{i};n^{*}(v))
.

Claim 2.5
.

Let
v\in V_{n}
.
On the events
\mathcal{E}_{0}
and
\mathcal{N}^{*}_{v}
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(|n_{i}(v)-n^{*}(v)(n_{i}/n^{*})|\geq\log n\sqrt{n^{*}(v)}\mid S_{0},n(v)^{*}\right)=\exp\left(-\Omega(\log^{2}n)\right).

for all
i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
.

Proof.

For a given vertex
v\in V_{n}
, we condition on the values of
n_{i},n_{v}^{*}
, so that
\mathcal{E}_{0}
(and therefore
\mathcal{P}_{0}
) are satisfied, and
n^{*}(v)
being such that
\mathcal{N}_{v}^{*}
is satisfied. But
|n_{v}^{*}-n^{*}|\leq 1
.
So on
\mathcal{N}_{v}^{*}
we have
n^{*}(v)/n_{v}^{*}\sim n^{*}(v)/n^{*}\sim p
.
We apply the Chernoff-type bound for the hypergeometric distribution (
2
) with

\lambda=\log n
,
s=n^{*}(v)
,
M=n_{i}
and
N=n_{v}^{*}
.
So
f_{s}=s/N=n^{*}(v)/n_{v}^{*}\sim p
. But as
\limsup_{n\to\infty}p<1
, we have
\lim\inf_{n\to\infty}(1-f_{s})>0
.
With these parameters, (
2
) yields

\mathbb{P}\left(|n_{i}(v)-n^{*}(v)(n_{i}/n^{*})|\geq\log n\sqrt{n^{*}(v)}\mid n_{i},n^{*},n^{*}(v)\right)=\exp\left(-\Omega(\log^{2}n)\right).

∎

This lemma suggests that conditional on the values of
n_{i},n^{*}_{v}
and
n^{*}(v)
on the events
\mathcal{E}_{0}
and

\mathcal{N}^{*}_{v}
, the random variable
n_{i}(v)
is concentrated around its expected value in an interval
of size
2(n^{*}(v))^{1/2}\log n
. In the next section, we will give the detailed asymptotic distribution of the vector
(n_{i}(v))_{i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}}
within this range.

2.2
A local limit theorem

Without loss of generality, suppose that
\mathcal{M}_{0}=\{1,\ldots,k_{0}\}
.
If we condition on a realisation of
S_{*},S^{*}
, then each vertex
v\in S^{*}
will take state
i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
with
probability
1/k_{0}
independently of any other vertex in
S^{*}
as well as of the random graph on
V_{n}
.
Thus, it is natural to express the sizes of the sets
S_{0}^{-1}(i)
, for
i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
, in terms of their deviation from
n^{*}/k_{0}
.

Let
c_{1},\ldots,c_{k_{0}}\in\mathbb{R}
be such that
n_{i}=n^{*}/k_{0}+c_{i}\sqrt{n^{*}}
; thus
\sum_{i=1}^{k_{0}}c_{i}=0
.
As
n_{i,v}=|S_{0}^{-1}(i)\setminus\{v\}|
, it follows that
n_{i,v}=n^{*}/k_{0}+c_{i}\sqrt{n^{*}}+O(1)
.
Note that
n_{i}-1\leq n_{i,v}\leq n_{i}
.
Furthermore, we write
n_{i,v}=n_{v}^{*}p_{i}
.
Since
n_{v}^{*}=n^{*}-1_{v\in S^{*}}
, it follows that
p_{i}=1/k_{0}+c_{i}/\sqrt{n^{*}}+O(n^{-1})
.

Let us condition on
n^{*}(v)
taking a particular value such that
\mathcal{N}^{*}_{v}
occurs.
For
i=1,\ldots,k_{0}-1
let us write
m_{i}=\lceil n^{*}(v)p_{i}\rceil
and

m_{k_{0}}=n^{*}(v)-\sum_{i=1}^{k_{0}-1}m_{i}
. The quantities
m_{i}
are essentially the expected number of
neighbours that
v
has inside
S_{0}^{-1}(i)
, if we condition on
n^{*}(v)
and on a realisation of
S_{0}^{-1}(1),\ldots,S_{0}^{-1}(k_{0})
. Lemma
2.6
below provides a local limit theorem for
n_{1}(v),\ldots,n_{k_{0}}(v)
when these are
centralised around
m_{1},\ldots,m_{k_{0}}
.

We will give an estimate on the probability that

(n_{1}(v),\ldots,n_{k_{0}}(v))=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k_{0}})
conditional on the values of
n_{v}^{*},n^{*}(v)
and on

\boldsymbol{c}^{*}:=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k_{0}})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{0}}
. (Note that the random vector

\boldsymbol{c}^{*}
determines the deviation of
n_{i}
from
n^{*}/k_{0}
, for
i=1,\ldots,k_{0}
.)
This probability is:

\mathbb{P}\left((n_{1}(v),\ldots,n_{k_{0}}(v))=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k_{0}})\mid n_{v}^{*},n^{*}(v),\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\right)=\frac{\binom{n_{1,v}}{s_{1}}\cdots\cdot\binom{n_{k_{0},v}}{s_{k_{0}}}}{\binom{n_{v}^{*}}{n^{*}(v)}}.

(5)

We will show that this probability is approximated by the
probability density function of a multivariate gaussian
random vector, provided that
s_{i}
is close to
m_{i}
for all

i=1,\ldots,k_{0}-1
.
Let
\mathbf{\mathrm{I}}_{\ell}
denote the
\ell\times\ell
unit matrix and
\mathbf{1}_{\ell\times\ell}
the all-ones
\ell\times\ell
matrix.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
p=p(n)\in[0,1]
be such that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}p(n)<1
and
n^{1/2}p\geq\log^{3}{n}
.
Consider a realisation of
S_{0}\in\mathcal{E}_{0}
, and for
v\in V_{n}

let
n^{*}(v)
be such that
\mathcal{N}^{*}_{v}
occurs.
Suppose that
s_{i}=m_{i}+\delta_{i}
with
|\delta_{i}|\leq\log n\cdot(n^{*}(v))^{1/2}
, for
i=1,\ldots,k_{0}-1
. Then uniformly over these values of
(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{k_{0}-1})
and
\boldsymbol{c}^{*}
such that
\|\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq\log n
,
with
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k_{0}-1})^{T}:=(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{k_{0}-1})^{T}/n^{*}(v)^{1/2}
and

\Sigma=\frac{k_{0}}{1-n^{*}(v)/n_{v}^{*}}\left(\mathbf{\mathrm{I}}_{k_{0}-1}+\mathbf{1}_{(k_{0}-1)\times(k_{0}-1)}\right),

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left((n_{1}(v),\ldots,n_{k_{0}}(v))=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k_{0}})\mid n_{v}^{*},n^{*}(v),\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\right)=

\displaystyle\hskip 99.58464pt\frac{1}{n^{*}(v)^{\frac{k_{0}-1}{2}}}\cdot\phi(\boldsymbol{x})\left(1+o(p^{1/2})\right),

where
\phi(\boldsymbol{x})=\left(\frac{|\Sigma|}{2\pi}\right)^{\frac{k_{0}-1}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\Sigma\boldsymbol{x}\right)
and

|\Sigma|=\mathrm{det}(\Sigma)
.

Proof.

For notational convenience we will write
n(v)
for
n^{*}(v)
,
k
for
k_{0}
and
n
for

n_{v}^{*}
and
n_{i}
for
n_{i,v}
. This should cause no confusion in regards to the asymptotics we derive,
as
n^{*}(v)
is a.a.s. of the same order of magnitude as
n(v)
, and
n_{i,v}
differs by at most 1 from
n_{i}
, respectively.
We will give asymptotic estimates for each one of the binomial coefficients that appear in (
5
).
Before we do this, let us give some
relations, which we will use in our
estimates later.
Firstly, let us observe that since
np\gg n^{2/3}

\frac{\log^{2}n}{np}\ll\frac{\log^{3}{n}}{\sqrt{np}}\leq p^{1/2}.

(6)

Therefore,

\frac{\log^{2}n}{np}=o\left(p^{1/2}\right).

(7)

This is equivalent to
\log^{2}n/n=o(p^{3/2})
. But

\frac{\log^{4/3}n}{n^{2/3}}\ll\frac{1}{n^{1/2}}\ll p.

We begin with the denominator of (
5
). Let
H(x)=-x\ln x-(1-x)\ln(1-x)
denote the entropy function defined for
x\in(0,1)
.
We recall a standard estimate for this binomial coefficient
which relies on the Stirling approximation for the factorial:

n!=\sqrt{2\pi n}n^{n}e^{-n}(1+O(1/n))
.
Using this, we write

\displaystyle\binom{n}{n(v)}=\frac{n!}{n(v)!(n-n(v))!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1+O(1/n(v))}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{n}{n(v)(n-n(v))}}\cdot\frac{n^{n}e^{-n}}{n(v)^{n(v)}e^{-n(v)}(n-n(v))^{n-n(v)}e^{-(n-n(v))}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1+O(1/np)}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{n}{n(v)(n-n(v))}}\cdot e^{nH\left(n(v)/n\right)}.

Therefore,

\binom{n}{n(v)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:est3}}}{{=}}(1+o(p^{1/2}))\sqrt{\frac{1}{1-n(v)/n}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n(v)}}\cdot e^{nH\left(n(v)/n\right)}.

(8)

Now, we will consider
\binom{n_{i}}{s_{i}}
. In fact, we will
express this in terms of
\binom{n_{i}}{m_{i}}
. In turn, we will
express the latter using (
8
).
Recalling that
s_{i}=m_{i}+\delta_{i}
, we write

\displaystyle\binom{n_{i}}{s_{i}}/\binom{n_{i}}{m_{i}}=\frac{m_{i}!}{(m_{i}+\delta_{i})!}\cdot\frac{(n_{i}-m_{i})!}{(n_{i}-m_{i}-\delta_{i})!}.

Suppose first that
\delta_{i}>0
. Then

\frac{m_{i}!}{(m_{i}+\delta_{i})!}=\frac{1}{m_{i}^{\delta_{i}}}\prod_{j=1}^{\delta_{i}}\left(1+\frac{j}{m_{i}}\right)^{-1}.

Writing
1+j/m_{i}=\exp\left(\ln(1+j/m_{i})\right)
and
expanding the
\ln
function around 1, we get that

\prod_{j=1}^{\delta_{i}}\left(1+\frac{j}{m_{i}}\right)^{-1}=\exp\left(-\frac{\delta_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}+O\left(\frac{|\delta_{i}|}{m_{i}}+\frac{|\delta_{i}|^{3}}{m_{i}^{2}}\right)\right).

Similarly, we get

\frac{(n_{i}-m_{i})!}{(n_{i}-m_{i}-\delta_{i})!}=(n_{i}-m_{i})^{\delta_{i}}\exp\left(-\frac{\delta_{i}^{2}}{2(n_{i}-m_{i})}+O\left(\frac{|\delta_{i}|}{n_{i}-m_{i}}+\frac{|\delta_{i}|^{3}}{(n_{i}-m_{i})^{2}}\right)\right).

As
n_{i}-m_{i}=\Omega(n)
, we deduce that

\frac{|\delta_{i}|}{n_{i}-m_{i}}+\frac{|\delta_{i}|^{3}}{(n_{i}-m_{i})^{2}}=O\left(\frac{\log n\cdot(np)^{1/2}}{n}+\frac{\log^{3}n\cdot(np)^{3/2}}{n^{2}}\right)=o(p^{1/2}).

Furthermore, as
|\delta_{i}|\leq(n(v))^{1/2}\log{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{N}^{*}_{v}}}{{=}}O((np)^{1/2}\log{n})
,

\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}|\delta_{i}|}{np}+\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}|\delta_{i}|^{3}}{(np)^{2}}=O\left(\frac{\log^{3}{n}}{\sqrt{np}}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:est1}}}{{=}}o(p^{1/2}).

We thus conclude that

\begin{split}&\binom{n_{i}}{s_{i}}=\left(1+o(p^{1/2})\right)\binom{n_{i}}{m_{i}}\cdot\left(\frac{n_{i}-m_{i}}{m_{i}}\right)^{\delta_{i}}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{\delta_{i}^{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{n_{i}}{m_{i}(n_{i}-m_{i})}\right).\end{split}

(9)

Note that as
m_{i}=\lceil n(v)p_{i}\rceil=n(v)p_{i}+r
, for some
0\leq r<1
, and
n_{i}=np_{i}
, we have

\frac{n_{i}}{m_{i}}=\frac{np_{i}}{n(v)p_{i}+r}=\frac{n}{n(v)}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{n(v)}\right)\right).

(10)

Since
|\delta_{i}|=O(\log n\cdot(np)^{1/2})
,
we deduce that

\begin{split}\left(\frac{n_{i}-m_{i}}{m_{i}}\right)^{\delta_{i}}&=\left(\frac{n}{n(v)}-1+O\left(\frac{n}{n(v)^{2}}\right)\right)^{\delta_{i}}\\
&=\left(\frac{n}{n(v)}-1\right)^{\delta_{i}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{|\delta_{i}|}{n(v)}\right)\right)\\
&=\left(\frac{n}{n(v)}-1\right)^{\delta_{i}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\log n}{(np)^{3/2}}\right)\right)\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:est3}}}{{=}}\left(\frac{n}{n(v)}-1\right)^{\delta_{i}}(1+o(p^{1/2})).\end{split}

Furthermore,

\frac{\delta_{i}^{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{n_{i}}{m_{i}(n_{i}-m_{i})}=\frac{\delta_{i}^{2}}{2n(v)p_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{1-n(v)/n}\right)+O(\delta_{i}^{2}/(np)^{2}).

But
\delta_{i}^{2}=O(\log^{2}n\cdot(np))
and therefore

\frac{\delta_{i}^{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{n_{i}}{m_{i}(n_{i}-m_{i})}=\frac{\delta_{i}^{2}}{2n(v)p_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{1-n(v)/n}\right)+O(\log^{2}n/(np)).

By (
7
),
\log^{2}n/(np)=o(p^{1/2})
and, thereby,

\exp\left(-\frac{\delta_{i}^{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{n_{i}}{m_{i}(n_{i}-m_{i})}\right)=(1+o(p^{1/2}))\exp\left(-\frac{\delta_{i}^{2}}{2n(v)p_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{1-n(v)/n}\right)\right).

As
n_{i}=\Theta(n)
and
m_{i}=\Theta(np)
, applying (
8
), we can also deduce that

\binom{n_{i}}{m_{i}}=(1+o(p^{1/2}))\sqrt{\frac{1}{1-n(v)/n}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi m_{i}}}\cdot e^{n_{i}H(m_{i}/n_{i})}.

(11)

Combining (
8
),
(
9
) and (
11
), we
deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left((n_{1}(v),\ldots,n_{k}(v))=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})\mid n,n(v),\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\right)=

(12)

\displaystyle\left(1+o(p^{1/2})\right)\times

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-n(v)/n)^{\frac{k-1}{2}}}\cdot\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{k-1}{2}}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{n(v)}{\prod_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}}}\cdot\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{n}{n(v)}-1\right)^{\delta_{i}}\right)\times

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\delta_{i}^{2}}{n(v)}\frac{1}{p_{i}(1-n(v)/n)}\right)\times

\displaystyle e^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}H(m_{i}/n_{i})-nH(n(v)/n)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\delta_{1}+\cdots+\delta_{k}=0}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\left(1+o(p^{1/2})\right)\times

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-n(v)/n)^{\frac{k-1}{2}}}\cdot\frac{1}{(2\pi n(v))^{\frac{k-1}{2}}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}}}\times

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\delta_{i}^{2}}{n(v)}\frac{1}{p_{i}(1-n(v)/n)}\right)\times

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pte^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}H(m_{i}/n_{i})-nH(n(v)/n)}.

Claim 2.7
.

With
\boldsymbol{x}=(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{k-1})^{T}/n(v)^{1/2}

and

\Sigma=\frac{k}{1-n(v)/n}\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}2&\cdots&1\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\cdots&2\end{array}\right],

we have

\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\delta_{i}^{2}}{n(v)}\frac{1}{p_{i}(1-n(v)/n)}\right)=(1+o(p^{1/2}))\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\Sigma\boldsymbol{x}\right).

Proof.

Since
\delta_{1}+\cdots+\delta_{k}=0

we write, with
\boldsymbol{\delta}=(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{k-1})^{T}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\delta_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{\delta_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}}+\frac{(\delta_{1}+\cdots+\delta_{k-1})^{2}}{p_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\delta_{i}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{p_{i}}+\frac{1}{p_{k}}\right)+\sum_{i\not=j}\delta_{i}\delta_{j}\frac{1}{p_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}^{T}\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{k}}&\cdots&\frac{1}{p_{k}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{1}{p_{k}}&\cdots&\frac{1}{p_{k-1}}+\frac{1}{p_{k}}\end{array}\right]\boldsymbol{\delta}.

Thus, setting

\Sigma^{\prime}=\frac{1}{1-n(v)/n}\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{k}}&\cdots&\frac{1}{p_{k}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{1}{p_{k}}&\cdots&\frac{1}{p_{k-1}}+\frac{1}{p_{k}}\end{array}\right],

and using the scaling

\boldsymbol{x}=(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{k-1})^{T}/n(v)^{1/2}
we get

\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\delta_{i}^{2}}{n(v)}\frac{1}{p_{i}(1-n(v)/n)}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\Sigma^{\prime}\boldsymbol{x}\right).

Now, note that
p_{i}=1/k+\Theta(\log n\cdot n^{-1/2})
. Therefore, uniformly for
\boldsymbol{x}
such that

\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\infty}\leq\log n
, we have

\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\Sigma^{\prime}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\Sigma\boldsymbol{x}+O(\log^{3}n/n^{1/2})=\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\Sigma\boldsymbol{x}+o(p^{1/2})
.
The claim then follows.
∎

We now calculate
|\Sigma|
.

Claim 2.8
.

We have

|\Sigma|=\frac{k^{k}}{\left(1-n(v)/n\right)^{k-1}}.

Proof.

We will consider first the matrix
\mathbf{\mathrm{I}}_{k-1}+\mathbf{1}_{(k-1)\times(k-1)}
. We will show that

|\mathbf{\mathrm{I}}_{k-1}+\mathbf{1}_{(k-1)\times(k-1)}|=k.

(14)

We will show this by finding the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{\mathrm{I}}_{k-1}+\mathbf{1}_{(k-1)\times(k-1)}
and using the fact that

|\mathbf{\mathrm{I}}_{k-1}+\mathbf{1}_{(k-1)\times(k-1)}|
is equal to their product.
The eigenvectors of
\mathbf{\mathrm{I}}_{k-1}+\mathbf{1}_{(k-1)\times(k-1)}
are
\mathbf{1}_{k-1}
(the all-ones vector), and the
vectors
e_{1j}
, for
1<j\leq k-1
with 1 in the first component and
-1
at the
j
th
component and
0
everywhere else. The associated eigenvalues are
k
for
\mathbf{1}_{k-1}
and
1
for the eigenvectors
e_{1j}
, for
j=2,\ldots,k-1
.
Thus, the product of the eigenvalues is equal to
k
whereby (
14
) follows.
Therefore,

|\Sigma|=\left(\frac{k}{1-n(v)/n}\right)^{k-1}\cdot|\mathbf{\mathrm{I}}_{k-1}+\mathbf{1}_{(k-1)\times(k-1)}|=\frac{k^{k}}{\left(1-n(v)/n\right)^{k-1}}.

∎

Returning to (
12
), we will show that the
term

\left(\frac{1}{1-n(v)/n}\right)^{\frac{k-1}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{p_{1}\cdots p_{k}}}=(1+o(p^{1/2}))|\Sigma|^{1/2}.

By the above claim, it suffices to show that

\sqrt{\frac{1}{p_{1}\cdots p_{k}}}=(1+o(p^{1/2}))k^{k/2}.

Recall that
p_{i}=1/k+O(\log n\cdot n^{-1/2})
, for
i=1,\ldots,k
.
Therefore,

\sqrt{\frac{1}{p_{1}\cdots p_{k}}}=k^{k/2}\left(1+O(\log n\cdot n^{-1/2})\right)=k^{k/2}\left(1+o(p^{1/2}))\right),

as
p\gg\log^{2}n/n
.

Finally, we will deal with last exponential in (
12
).

Claim 2.9
.

We have

\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}H(m_{i}/n_{i})-nH(n(v)/n)=O(1/np).

∎

Proof.

We will approximate
H(m_{i}/n_{i})
by
H(n(v)/n)
using (the second order) Taylor’s theorem around

n(v)/n
. Let
i\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
. Then

m_{i}\geq n(v)p_{i}
, whereby
n(v)/n\leq m_{i}/n_{i}
. Furthermore, for
n
sufficiently large
m_{i}/n_{i}<1
and therefore
[n(v)/n,m_{i}/n_{i}]\subset(0,1)
.
Since the entropy function
H
is twice differentiable in
(0,1)
,
there exists
\xi\in[n(v)/n,m_{i}/n_{i}]

H\left(\frac{m_{i}}{n_{i}}\right)=H\left(\frac{n(v)}{n_{i}}\right)+H^{\prime}\left(\frac{n(v)}{n}\right)\left(\frac{m_{i}n-n_{i}n(v)}{nn_{i}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}H^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\left(\frac{m_{i}n-n_{i}n(v)}{nn_{i}}\right)^{2}.

By (
10
), we have that

\left(\frac{m_{i}n-n_{i}n(v)}{nn_{i}}\right)^{2}=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

Also,
H^{\prime\prime}(x)=-\frac{1}{x(1-x)}
, for any
x\in(0,1)
. As
\xi=p+o(p)
and

p
is asymptotically bounded away from 1, we have

|H^{\prime\prime}(\xi)|=\frac{1}{\xi(1-\xi)}=O(1/\xi)=O(1/p).

We can deduce the same for
i=k
, except that in that case

m_{k}/n_{k}\leq n(v)/n
.
Using these, we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}H\left(\frac{m_{i}}{n_{i}}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}H\left(\frac{n(v)}{n}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}H^{\prime}\left(\frac{n(v)}{n}\right)\left(\frac{m_{i}n-n_{i}n(v)}{nn_{i}}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}p}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle nH\left(\frac{n(v)}{n}\right)+H^{\prime}\left(\frac{n(v)}{n}\right)\left(\frac{n\sum_{i}m_{i}-n(v)\sum_{i}n_{i}}{n}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{np}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle nH\left(\frac{n(v)}{n}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{np}\right),

since
\sum_{i}m_{i}=n(v)
and
\sum_{i}n_{i}=n
.
∎

Therefore,

e^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}H(m_{i}/n_{i})-nH(n(v)/n)}=e^{O(1/np)}=1+O(1/(np))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:est1}}}{{=}}1+o(p^{1/2}).

(15)

We thus conclude that

\mathbb{P}\left((n_{1}(v),\ldots,n_{k}(v))=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})\mid n,n(v),\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\right)=\frac{1}{n(v)^{\frac{k-1}{2}}}\cdot\phi(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\left(1+o(p^{1/2})\right).

2.3
Some basic (anti-)concentration properties of the initial configuration

In this section we will prove that with high probability the random variables
n_{i}
, for
i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
, are not too close to or too far from
n^{*}/k_{0}
. Moreover, we show that it is also unlikely for the
c_{i}
s to be too close to each other.

Lemma 2.10
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that
with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
, for
i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
we have

\delta<\frac{1}{\sqrt{n^{*}}}|n_{i}-n^{*}/k_{0}|\leq 1/\delta,

(16)

and for distinct
i=1,\ldots,k_{0}-1

|c_{i}-c_{i+1}|>\delta,

(17)

with
c_{k_{0}}=-\sum_{j<k_{0}}c_{j}
.

We denote the event that is described by (
16
) and (
17
) by
\mathcal{E}_{\delta,n}
.

Proof.

Conditional on
|S^{*}|=n^{*}
, for any
i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
we have
n_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n^{*},1/k_{0})
. The local limit theorem (Theorem
1.2
) implies that the probability that

n_{i}
is too close to
n^{*}/k_{0}
is small.

Lemma 2.11
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|n_{i}-n^{*}/k_{0}\right|\leq\delta\sqrt{n^{*}}\mid n^{*}\right)<\varepsilon.

Proof.

We use the local limit theorem (Theorem
1.2
).
We denote the variance of
n_{i}
(conditional on
n^{*}
) by

\sigma_{*}^{2}=n^{*}(1/k_{0})(1-1/k_{0})
and write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|n_{i}-n^{*}/k_{0}|\leq\delta\sqrt{n^{*}}\mid n^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k:|k-n^{*}/k_{0}|\leq\delta\sqrt{n^{*}}}\mathbb{P}\left(n_{i}=k\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{*}^{2}}}\sum_{k:|k-n^{*}/k_{0}|\leq\delta\sqrt{n^{*}}}e^{-\frac{(k-n^{*}/k_{0})^{2}}{2\sigma_{*}}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n^{*}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\delta\sqrt{n^{*}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{*}^{2}}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n^{*}}}\right)=O(\delta).

The lemma follows.
∎

Also, the Chernoff bound (
1
) implies that
n_{i}
is not too far
from
n^{*}/k_{0}
too.

Lemma 2.12
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
C>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(|n_{i}-n^{*}/k_{0}|>C\sqrt{n^{*}}\mid n^{*}\right)<\varepsilon/6.

Furthermore, we will show the following.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k_{0}-1})
be a random vector with

X\sim\mathrm{Mult}(n^{*};(1/k_{0},\ldots,1/k_{0}))
and let

\boldsymbol{c}^{*}=(n^{*})^{-1/2}\cdot(X-n^{*}(1/k_{0},\ldots,1/k_{0}))
.
Let
\boldsymbol{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{0}-1}
be such that
\boldsymbol{C}\not=\boldsymbol{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{0}-1}
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon,\boldsymbol{C})>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(|\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{C}|<\delta\mid n^{*}\right)<\varepsilon,

for any
n^{*}
that is sufficiently large.

Proof.

For
j=1,\ldots,k_{0}-1
, the component
X_{j}
is binomially distributed with parameters

n^{*}
and
1/k_{0}
. The same holds for
X_{k_{0}}=n^{*}-\sum_{j<k_{0}}X_{j}
.
Thus, for any
\varepsilon>0
and any
j\in[k_{0}]
, there is
\Lambda_{j}=\Lambda_{j}(\varepsilon)>0
, such that

\mathbb{P}\left((n^{*})^{-1/2}\cdot\left|X_{j}-n^{*}/k_{0}\right|>\Lambda_{j}\right)<\varepsilon/k_{0}.

By the union bound, for any
\varepsilon
there is
\Lambda=\max\{\Lambda_{j}(\varepsilon/3)\}_{j\in[k_{0}]}
, such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}>\Lambda\right)<\varepsilon/3.

(18)

For a given
\varepsilon>0
, select this
\Lambda
.

We will use the local limit theorem for the multinomial distribution. This was first shown by Arenbaev
[
1
]
(see Lemma 2 therein), but we will use a version that was shown by Siotani and Fujikoshi in
[
23
]
.
Let
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k_{0}-1})
be the random vector that follows the multinomial distribution with parameters

n^{*}
and
\boldsymbol{q}:=(1/k_{0},\ldots,1/k_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times(k_{0}-1)}
.
Let
\phi
denote the pdf of the
k_{0}-1
-dimensional normal distribution with expected value equal to
\boldsymbol{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{0}-1}
and covariance matrix
\mathrm{diag}(1/k_{0},\ldots,1/k_{0})-\boldsymbol{q}^{T}\boldsymbol{q}
.
The Local Limit Theorem for the multinomial distribution implies that for any

\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k_{0}-1})
such that
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\infty}\leq\Lambda
:

\mathbb{P}\left(\boldsymbol{c}^{*}=\boldsymbol{x}\right)\leq\frac{1}{(n^{*})^{\frac{k_{0}-1}{2}}}\phi(\boldsymbol{x})(1+O((n^{*})^{-1/2})).

(19)

For a vector
\boldsymbol{C}=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{k_{0}-1})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{0}-1}
, we define the set

S_{\delta,\Lambda,\boldsymbol{C}}=\{\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k_{0}-1}):|\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{C}|<\delta,\left\|\boldsymbol{x}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\Lambda,\ \mbox{$\forall j\in[k_{0}-1]$}\ n^{*}/k_{0}+x_{j}\sqrt{n^{*}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\ \}.

We claim that
|S_{\delta,\Lambda,\boldsymbol{C}}|=O(\delta\cdot(n^{*})^{\frac{k_{0}-1}{2}}\cdot\Lambda^{k_{0}-1})
.
Indeed, we can select freely
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k_{0}-2}
, whereby there are
\left(2\Lambda\sqrt{n^{*}}\right)^{k_{0}-2}
such choices.
For each such choice, the component
x_{k_{0}-1}
must be selected so that
-\delta<\sum_{j=1}^{k_{0}-2}x_{j}C_{j}+x_{k_{0}-1}C_{k_{0}-1}<\delta
.
There are at most
2\delta\sqrt{n^{*}}\Lambda/|C_{k_{0}-1}|
such choices.
We conclude that

|S_{\delta,\Lambda,\boldsymbol{C}}|\leq\delta\cdot(n^{*})^{\frac{k_{0}-1}{2}}\cdot(2\Lambda)^{k_{0}-1}/|C_{k_{0}-1}|.

Now, choose
\delta=\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon(2\Lambda)^{-(k_{0}-1)}\cdot|C_{k_{0}-1}|
; this implies
that

|S_{\delta,\Lambda,\boldsymbol{C}}|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\cdot(n^{*})^{\frac{k_{0}-1}{2}}.

(20)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{C}|<\delta\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:LLL}}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle(1+o(1))\frac{1}{(n^{*})^{\frac{k_{0}-1}{2}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in S_{\delta,\Lambda,\boldsymbol{C}}}\phi(\boldsymbol{x})+\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}>\Lambda\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+o(1))\frac{1}{(n^{*})^{\frac{k_{0}-1}{2}}}|S_{\delta,\Lambda,\boldsymbol{C}}|+\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}>\Lambda\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:cv_infty_norm},\eqref{eq:S_bound}}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle(1+o(1))\varepsilon/2+\varepsilon/3<\varepsilon.

∎

The two lemmas together with the union bound imply that for any
\varepsilon
there exists
\delta>0
such that
with probability at least
1-\varepsilon/2
, for
i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
we have

\delta<\frac{1}{\sqrt{n^{*}}}|n_{i}-n^{*}/k_{0}|\leq 1/\delta.

Now, applying Lemma
2.13
with
\boldsymbol{C}=(0,\ldots,0,1,-1,0,\ldots,0)^{T}
, where
1
and
-1
are in places
i
and
i+1
, respectively, with
i<k_{0}-1
,
or taking
\boldsymbol{C}=(1,\ldots,1,2)^{T}
, we deduce that
for
i=1,\ldots,k_{0}-1

|c_{i}-c_{i+1}|>\delta,

with
c_{k_{0}}=-\sum_{j<k_{0}}c_{j}
with probability at least
1-\varepsilon/2
. The union bound implies the statement of
the lemma.
∎

In what follows, we will condition on
\mathcal{E}_{\delta,n}
.
More specifically, we shall assume that the initial configuration on
S^{*}
is such that for each
i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}

n_{i}=n^{*}/k_{0}+c_{i}\sqrt{n^{*}},

with
\delta<c_{i}<1/\delta
and
c_{i+1}-c_{i}>\delta
, for any
i=1,\ldots,k_{0}-1
.
We write
S_{0}\in\mathcal{E}_{\delta,n}
.

3

3.1
Estimating the probability of a certain state after one round

For
i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
, we let
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(i)}
denote the subset of vertices
v\in V_{n}
such that
S_{1}(v)=i
in a
strong sense
, that is,
n_{i}(v)>n_{j}(v)
for any
j\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
with
j\not=i
.
For a vertex
v\in V_{n}
, we will give a tight approximation of the probability that

v\in\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(i)}
, for
i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
.
Our aim is to show the following result.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
v\in V_{n}
. There is a constant
\Lambda>0
such that for
n
sufficiently large, on the events

S_{0}\in\mathcal{E}_{0}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\delta,n}
and
\mathcal{N}^{*}_{v}
and
assuming that
\boldsymbol{c}^{*}
is such that
c_{1}>c_{2}>\cdots>c_{k_{0}}

i=1,\ldots,k_{0}-1
:

\mathbb{P}\left(v\in\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(i)}\mid n^{*}(v),n_{v}^{*},\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(v\in\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(i+1)}\mid n^{*}(v),n_{v}^{*},\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\right)>\Lambda\cdot(c_{i}-c_{i+1})p^{1/2}.

(21)

Proof.

Recall that
p_{i}=1/k_{0}+\frac{c_{i}}{\sqrt{n^{*}}}+O(1/n)
and we wrote

n_{i}(v)=m_{i}+\delta_{i}
, where

m_{i}=\lceil n^{*}(v)p_{i}\rceil
, for
i=1,\ldots,k_{0}-1
, but

m_{k_{0}}=n^{*}(v)-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}m_{i}
.

Now, we write
m_{i}=n^{*}(v)p_{i}+r_{i}
, for

0\leq r_{i}<1
, and using that
p_{i}=1/k_{0}+\frac{c_{i}}{\sqrt{n^{*}}}+O(1/n)
, we have that

\displaystyle m_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{*}(v)/k_{0}+\frac{n^{*}(v)}{\sqrt{n^{*}}}c_{i}+r_{i}+O\left(\frac{n^{*}(v)}{n^{*}}\right)

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle n^{*}(v)/k_{0}+h_{i}(\boldsymbol{c}^{*},n^{*}(v)).

In other words, we have set
h_{i}(\boldsymbol{c}^{*},n^{*}(v)):=m_{i}-n^{*}(v)/k_{0}
. Note that
\sum_{i=1}^{k_{0}}h_{i}(\boldsymbol{c}^{*},n^{*}(v))=0
and, moreover,

n_{i}(v)=n^{*}(v)/k_{0}+h_{i}(\boldsymbol{c}^{*},n^{*}(v))+\delta_{i}.

Rescaling by
n^{*}(v)^{1/2}
and recalling that
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{\delta}/n^{*}(v)^{1/2}
we get

\displaystyle n^{*}(v)^{-1/2}n_{i}(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{*}(v)^{1/2}/k_{0}+\frac{h_{i}(\boldsymbol{c}^{*},n^{*}(v))}{n^{*}(v)^{1/2}}+\frac{\delta_{i}}{n^{*}(v)^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{*}(v)^{1/2}/k_{0}+\sqrt{\frac{n^{*}(v)}{n^{*}}}c_{i}+x_{i}+O(n^{*}(v)^{-1/2}).

Let
\mathcal{L}
denote the set of points

\{n^{*}(v)^{-1/2}\cdot(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{k_{0}-1}):\delta_{i}\in\mathbb{Z},i=1,\ldots,k_{0}-1\}
and let
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
denote

.
By Claim
2.5
, conditional on a realisation of
\{S_{0}^{-1}(1),\ldots,S_{0}^{-1}(k_{0})\}
that satisfies the event
\mathcal{E}_{\delta,n}\cap\mathcal{E}_{0}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mbox{for some $i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}$ }(n^{*}(v))^{-1/2}|n_{i}(v)-n^{*}(v)(n_{i}/n^{*})|>\log n\mid n^{*}(v),n_{v}^{*},\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\right)=

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\exp\left({-\Omega(\log^{2}n)}\right)=o(p^{1/2}).

(22)

In other words, realisations of
(n^{*}(v))^{-1/2}(n_{i}(v))_{i\in\mathcal{M}_{0}}
that do not belong to
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
have probability
o(p^{1/2})
.
For
i=1,\dots,k_{0}
, we let
\mathcal{D}_{i}=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{0}-1}\ :\ x_{i}>x_{j},\mbox{for all $j=1,\ldots,k_{0}$ such that $j\not=i$}\}

2.6

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(v\in\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(i)}\mid n^{*}(v),n_{v}^{*},\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\right)=(1+o(p^{1/2}))\cdot\frac{1}{n^{*}(v)^{\frac{k_{0}-1}{2}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\phi(\boldsymbol{x})+o(p^{1/2}).

(23)

Let us now assume, without loss of generality, that
c_{1}>\ldots>c_{k_{0}}
.
Recall that if the event

is realised, then

We will show the following.

Lemma 3.2
.

There is
\Lambda>0
such that for any
n
sufficiently large,
on the events
\mathcal{P}_{0},\mathcal{E}_{\delta,n}
and

n^{*}(v)^{-\frac{k_{0}-1}{2}}\cdot\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\phi(\boldsymbol{x})-\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{D}_{i+1}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\phi(\boldsymbol{x})\right)>\Lambda(c_{i}-c_{i+1})p^{1/2}.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{h}_{n}=(h_{1}(\boldsymbol{c}^{*},n^{*}(v)),\ldots,h_{k_{0}-1}(\boldsymbol{c}^{*},n^{*}(v)))^{T}

We use the transformation
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{h}_{n}

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\phi(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{D}_{i}:\ \boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{h}_{n}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\phi(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{h}_{n}).

Our aim now is to replace

\phi(\boldsymbol{y})

To estimate the error, we need to consider the difference
\phi(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{h}_{n})-\phi(\boldsymbol{y})

We write:

\displaystyle\phi(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{h}_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi(\boldsymbol{y})\cdot\exp\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{T}\Sigma\boldsymbol{h}_{n}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}_{n}^{T}\Sigma\boldsymbol{h}_{n}\right).

For
i=1,\ldots,k_{0}-2
, we will define a bijection between the sets
\mathcal{D}_{i}
and
\mathcal{D}_{i+1}

\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{i},y_{i+1},\ldots,y_{k_{0}-1})\mapsto\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{i+1},y_{i},\ldots,y_{k_{0}-1})\in\mathcal{D}_{i+1},

\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{i},y_{i+1},\ldots,y_{k_{0}-1})\mapsto\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{i+1},y_{i},\ldots,-\sum_{i<k_{0}}y_{i})\in\mathcal{D}_{k_{0}}.

Claim 3.3
.

For any
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{D}_{i}
, with
i\in\{1,\ldots,k_{0}-1\}
, we have

\boldsymbol{y}^{T}\Sigma\boldsymbol{y}=\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}^{T}\Sigma\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}.

Proof.

Indeed, with
\Sigma^{\prime}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}2&\cdots&1\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\cdots&2\end{array}\right]
, it suffices to prove that
\boldsymbol{y}^{T}\Sigma^{\prime}\boldsymbol{y}=\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}^{T}\Sigma^{\prime}\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}
, as
\Sigma
is a multiple of
\Sigma^{\prime}
by a scalar.
For
i<k_{0}-1
, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}^{T}\Sigma^{\prime}\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{j=1}^{k_{0}-1}y_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j^{\prime}\not=j}y_{j}y_{j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{j=1}^{k_{0}-1}\hat{y}_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j^{\prime}\not=j}\hat{y}_{j}\hat{y}_{j^{\prime}}.

For
i=k_{0}-1
,

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}^{T}\Sigma^{\prime}\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}

\displaystyle=

Proof of Claim
3.4
.

We argue by considering an approximation of the sum of
\phi(\boldsymbol{y})
, for

such that

\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{h}_{n}\in\mathcal{L^{\prime}}

\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k_{0}-1})

\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}}
denote the box

\prod_{j=1}^{k_{0}-1}[x_{i},x_{i}+n^{*}(v)^{-1/2})

Claim 3.5
.

For any
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
we have

\lvert\phi(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-\phi(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime\prime})\rvert=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n^{*}(v)}}\right),

\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime\prime}

Proof.

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{k_{0}-1})^{T}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime\prime}=(x^{\prime\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime\prime}_{k_{0}-1})^{T},
both belonging to
\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
.
We appeal to the multidimensional mean value theorem: there exists some
c\in(0,1)
such that,

\lvert\phi(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-\phi(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime\prime})\rvert\leq\lVert\nabla\phi(\left(1-c)\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}+c\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime\prime}\right)\rVert_{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime\prime}\rVert_{2}.

For
i=1,\ldots,k_{0}-1

z_{i}=x^{\prime}_{i}(1-c)+x^{\prime\prime}_{i}c
and consider the order of
\lVert\nabla\phi((z_{1},\ldots z_{k_{0}-1}))\rVert_{2}.
We remark that the exponent in
\phi((z_{1},\ldots z_{k_{0}-1}))
is a quadratic form in
\boldsymbol{z}=(z_{1},\ldots z_{k_{0}-1})^{T}
. We set

-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{z}^{T}\Sigma\boldsymbol{z}=:h(\boldsymbol{z}).

Hence it follows from the definition of
\phi
that

\lVert\nabla\phi(\boldsymbol{z})\rVert_{2}=\lvert\phi(\boldsymbol{z})\rvert\lVert\nabla h(\boldsymbol{z})\rVert_{2}.

Now we observe that as

is quadratic in

it follows that,

\lVert\nabla h(\boldsymbol{z})\rVert_{2}=O(\left|\boldsymbol{z}\right|_{\infty}^{2})=O(1).

We also have that
\phi(\boldsymbol{z})\leq 1
. Therefore,

\lvert\phi(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-\phi(\boldsymbol{x^{\prime\prime}})\rvert=O(\lVert\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime\prime}\rVert_{2}).

However, as
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
then we have that
\lVert\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime\prime}\rVert_{2}=O\left(1/\sqrt{n^{*}(v)}\right),
which concludes the proof of this claim.
∎

Thus, applying the above, we can consider an approximation of the above sums by a Gaussian integral. Recall that

\mathcal{B}^{\infty}_{\log{n}}=\{\boldsymbol{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k_{0}-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{0}-1}:\lVert\boldsymbol{z}\rVert_{\infty}<\log{n}\}
.

For each
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
such that
\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{h}_{n}\in\mathcal{D}_{i}^{(\zeta)}
, let us consider

\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{x})\in\argmax_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\{\phi(\boldsymbol{z})\},

a maximiser of
\phi
inside
\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

We write:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{*}(v)^{\frac{k_{0}-1}{2}}}\cdot\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{D}_{i}^{(\zeta)}:\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{h}_{n}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\phi(\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{1}{n^{*}(v)^{\frac{k_{0}-1}{2}}}\cdot\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{D}_{i}^{(\zeta)}:\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{h}_{n}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\phi(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{y}))

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt-\frac{1}{n^{*}(v)^{\frac{k_{0}-1}{2}}}\cdot\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{D}_{i}^{(\zeta)}:\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{h}_{n}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\lvert\phi(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{y}))-\phi(\boldsymbol{y})\rvert.

The first sum is asymptotically bounded from below by an integral:

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{*}(v)^{\frac{k_{0}-1}{2}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{D}_{i}^{(\zeta)}:\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{h}_{n}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\phi(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{x}))\geq\int_{\mathcal{D}_{i}^{(\zeta)}}\phi(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}.

For the second term, let us observe that
|\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{D}_{i}^{(\zeta)}:\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{h}_{n}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\}|\leq|\mathcal{L}^{\prime}|=O(n^{*}(v)^{(k_{0}-1)/2}\log^{2}{n})
.
By Claim
3.5

\lvert\phi(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{y}))-\phi(\boldsymbol{y})\rvert=O\left(n^{*}(v)^{-1/2}\right).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{*}(v)^{\frac{k_{0}-1}{2}}}\cdot\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{D}_{i}^{(\zeta)}:\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{h}_{n}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\lvert\phi(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{x}))-\phi(\boldsymbol{x})\rvert

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=O\left(n^{*}(v)^{-1/2-(k_{0}-1)/2}\right)\cdot|\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{D}_{i}^{(\zeta)}:\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{h}_{n}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\}|

As

and, since

is realised,

We conclude that

for any

\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{D}_{i}^{(\zeta)}
.
Therefore,

3.2
Concentration after the first step

Let
X_{i}
denote the random variable that is the number of vertices that obtain state

Proof of (
27
).

To see this, we write

\displaystyle X_{i}-X_{i+1}=\sum_{v\in V_{n}}1_{v\in\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(i)}}-\sum_{v\in V_{n}}1_{v\in\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(i+1)}}.

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}-X_{i+1}|n^{*},\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{v\in V_{n}}1_{v\in\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(i)}}-\sum_{v\in V_{n}}1_{v\in\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(i+1)}}|n^{*},\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\right].

\mathbb{E}\left[1_{v\in\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(i)}}-1_{v\in\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(i+1)}}|n^{*},\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\right]=\Omega(1)p^{1/2}(c_{i}-c_{i+1}).

We write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[1_{v\in\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(i)}}-1_{v\in\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(i+1)}}|n^{*},\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\right]=

(28)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[1_{v\in\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(i)}}-1_{v\in\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(i+1)}}|n^{*}(v)\right]|n^{*},\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[1_{\mathcal{N}_{v}^{*}}\mathbb{E}\left[1_{v\in\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(i)}}-1_{v\in\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(i+1)}}|n^{*}(v)\right]|n^{*},\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt-\mathbb{E}\left[1-1_{\mathcal{N}_{v}^{*}}|n^{*},\boldsymbol{c}^{*}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:Nv_prob}}}{{=}}

27

|S_{1}^{(i)}|=b_{i}(n)
, where

b_{i}(n)-b_{i+1}(n)>\Omega(1)(c_{i}-c_{i+1})np^{1/2}
, for
i=1,\ldots,k_{0}-1
. Moreover, as

\mathcal{E}_{\delta,n}
is realised,
c_{i}-c_{i+1}>\delta
.
We prove the following.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\omega=\omega(n):\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be such that
\omega(n)\to\infty

as
n\to\infty
and
n^{1/3}p\geq\omega
.
Suppose that for
i=1,\ldots,k_{0}
, the functions
b_{i}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy

b_{i}(n)-b_{i+1}(n)>\Omega(1)np^{1/2}
, for
i=1,\ldots,k_{0}-1
.
For any
\delta>0

a.a.s. the following holds: for any partition
(P_{1},\ldots,P_{k_{0}},R)
of
V_{n}
with

|P_{i}|=b_{i}(n)
and
|R|=O(\log^{2}n\sqrt{n/p})
, all, but fewer than

np/\omega^{1/2}
, vertices
v\in V_{n}
have

d_{P_{i}}(v)-d_{P_{i+1}}(v)>\frac{\delta}{4}np^{3/2},

for all
i=1,\ldots,k_{0}-1
.

Proof.

We will use the union bound to show that a.a.s. there is no subset
S\subset V_{n}
with

|S|=np/\sqrt{\omega}
and a partition
(P_{1},\ldots,P_{k_{0}},R)
with the property that for any
v\in S

d_{P_{i}}(v)-d_{P_{i+1}}(v)\leq\frac{\delta}{4}np^{3/2}
, for some
1\leq i\leq k_{0}-1
.
In particular, we fix such a subset
S
and a partition
(P_{1},\ldots,P_{k_{0}},R)
and we show that a.a.s.
at least half of the vertices of
S
(a subset
S^{\prime}\subset S
)
have degree inside
P_{i}
that is close to
|P_{i}\setminus S|p
for all

1\leq i\leq k_{0}-1
. This yields a lower bound on
d_{P_{i}\setminus S}(v)-d_{P_{i+1}\setminus S}(v)
for all vertices in
S^{\prime}
. The choice of
S^{\prime}
is such that all vertices inside
S^{\prime}
have degree inside
S
that is proportional to
|S|p
. This together with the lower bound on
|P_{i+1}|-|P_{i}|
ensures that for all
1\leq i\leq k_{0}-1
the lower bound on
d_{P_{i}}(v)-d_{P_{i+1}}(v)
holds. We now proceed with the details of this argument.

Let
S\subset V_{n}
be such that

|S|=np/\sqrt{\omega}
and let
(P_{1},\ldots,P_{k_{0}},R)
be a partition of
V_{n}
as specified above.
Firstly, we apply the Chernoff bound to deduce the concentration of the number of edges within
S
; we denote it by
e(S)
.
We have

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|e(S)-\binom{|S|}{2}p\right|\geq\frac{p|S|^{2}}{\sqrt{\omega}}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\Omega\left(\frac{p|S|^{2}}{\omega}\right)\right).

As
p\geq\omega n^{-1/3}
, we deduce that
p|S|^{2}/\omega=\Omega(n\omega)
. Hence with probability at least
1-2^{-\Omega(\omega\cdot n)}
, we have that
e(S)
is suitably concentrated about its expected value. Thereby, on this event, the average degree of
G(n,p)[S]
is

\frac{2e(S)}{|S|}\leq p|S|(1+o(1)).

So on the above event and for
n
sufficiently large, there exists a subset
S^{\prime}\subset S

with
|S^{\prime}|=|S|/2
such that every vertex
v\in S^{\prime}
has
d_{S}(v)\leq 3|S|p
.

Let us set
\alpha(n)=np^{1/2}
and consider
P_{i}
. We will first bound the probability that a given
v\in S^{\prime}
has

\left|d_{P_{i}\setminus S}(v)-|P_{i}\setminus S|p\right|>\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\alpha(n)p,

where
\varepsilon>0
is some constant.
To this end we can apply the Chernoff bound since
d_{P_{i}\setminus S}(v)\sim\mathrm{Bin}(|P_{i}\setminus S|,p)
.
Inequality (
1
) yields:

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|d_{P_{i}\setminus S}(v)-|P_{i}\setminus S|p\right|>\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\alpha(n)p\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle|P_{i}\setminus S|\leq n}}{{=}}\exp\left(-\Omega\left(\frac{\alpha(n)^{2}p^{2}}{np}\right)\right).

Note that by the choice of
\alpha(n)
, the quantity in the exponent on the right-hand side is

\frac{\alpha(n)^{2}p^{2}}{np}=\frac{n^{2}p^{3}}{np}=np^{2}.

Moreover,
np^{2}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, since we have assumed that
p\gg n^{-1/3}
.

Now, the union bound implies that for any
v\in S^{\prime}
and any fixed
\varepsilon>0
with probability

1-\exp(-\Omega(np^{2}))
, we have for
1\leq i<k_{0}

\left|d_{P_{i}\setminus S}(v)-|P_{i}\setminus S|p\right|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\alpha(n)p.

On this event,

\displaystyle d_{P_{i}\setminus S}(v)-d_{P_{i+1}\setminus S}(v)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(|P_{i}\setminus S|-|P_{i+1}\setminus S|\right)p-\varepsilon\alpha(n)p

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(b_{i}(n)-b_{i+1}(n)-2|S|\right)p-\varepsilon\alpha(n)p.

Now, we write

\displaystyle d_{P_{i}}(v)-d_{P_{i+1}}(v)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle d_{P_{i}\setminus S}(v)-d_{P_{i+1}\setminus S}(v)-d_{P_{i+1}\cap S}(v)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle d_{P_{i}\setminus S}(v)-d_{P_{i+1}\setminus S}(v)-d_{S}(v)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle d_{P_{i}\setminus S}(v)-d_{P_{i+1}\setminus S}(v)-3|S|p

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(b_{i}(n)-b_{i+1}(n)-5|S|\right)p-\varepsilon\alpha(n)p.

But for some
\delta>0
,
we have
b_{i}(n)-b_{i+1}(n)\geq\delta np^{1/2}
, for any
n
sufficiently large, whereas
|S|=o(np)
.
Therefore, the right-hand side of the above is for
n
sufficiently large

\left(b_{i}(n)-b_{i+1}(n)-5|S|\right)p-\varepsilon\alpha(n)p>\frac{\delta}{2}np^{3/2}-\varepsilon np^{3/2}>\frac{\delta}{4}np^{3/2},

if
\varepsilon<\delta/4
. Thus, we have shown that if
\varepsilon<\delta/4
and
n
is sufficiently large, then

d_{P_{i}}(v)-d_{P_{i+1}}(v)>\frac{\delta}{4}np^{3/2}.

Note that the family
\{d_{P_{i}\setminus S}(v)\}_{v\in S^{\prime},i=1,\dots,k_{0}}
consists of i.i.d. random variables which are binomially distributed with parameters
|P_{i}\setminus S|,p
.
The Chernoff bound (
1
) together with the union bound implies that for any
v\in S^{\prime}

\mathbb{P}\left(\mbox{for some $1\leq i\leq k_{0}$}\ |d_{P_{i}\setminus S}(v)-|P_{i}\setminus S|p|>\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\alpha(n)p\right)=\exp\left(-\Omega\left(\frac{\alpha(n)^{2}p^{2}}{np}\right)\right).

Since the family
\{d_{P_{i}\setminus S}(v)\}_{v\in S^{\prime},i=1,\dots,k_{0}}
is independent we then deduce that

\mathbb{P}\left(\mbox{$\forall v\in S^{\prime}$ $\exists 1\leq i\leq k_{0}$:}\ |d_{P_{i}\setminus S}(v)-|P_{i}\setminus S|p|>\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\alpha(n)p\right)=\exp\left(-\Omega\left(\frac{|S^{\prime}|\alpha(n)^{2}p^{2}}{np}\right)\right).

But since
|S^{\prime}|=|S|/2
and
|S|=np/\sqrt{\omega}
we have

\frac{|S^{\prime}|\alpha(n)^{2}p^{2}}{np}=\Omega\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}(n)p^{2}}{\sqrt{\omega}}\right).

As
\alpha(n)=np^{1/2}
, the numerator on the right hand side is

\alpha(n)^{2}p^{2}=\Omega(n^{2}p^{3}).

Hence as
p\geq\omega n^{-1/3}
we have that
\alpha^{2}(n)p^{2}/\sqrt{\omega}\geq n\omega^{5/2}
,
and, therefore, this probability is
e^{-\Omega(\omega^{2}n)}
.
As there are
2^{O(n)}
choices for
S
and
S^{\prime}\subset S
and the partition
(P_{1},\ldots,P_{k_{0}},R)
, the union bound implies the lemma.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Note that it is in the proof of Lemma
4.1
that the assumption
p\gg n^{-1/3}
is needed. This ensures that the bad events occur with probability at most
2^{-\omega\cdot n}
and, therefore makes the union bound work.

We now apply Lemma
4.1
to deduce the evolution of the behaviour of all but at most
np/\omega^{1/2}
vertices from round 1 into round 2. In particular, as
np^{1/2}\gg\log n\sqrt{n/p}
, the above
lemma implies that all but at most
np/\omega^{1/2}
will adopt state 1 after round 2.
Finally, a union bound over all partitions of
V_{n}
into two parts where one of the parts has size at most
n/\omega^{1/2}
and the Chernoff bound imply that conditional on the above evolution of the process
after the third round a.a.s. all vertices will have adopted state 1. This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

5
Conclusions and some further directions

In this paper we generalised the results of
[
3
,
4
,
8
]
showing that
majority dynamics on the random graph
G(n,p)
and
k>2
states will reach unanimity after at most 3 rounds,
provided that
np\gg n^{2/3}
. As in the case of 2 states, the proof relies on a tight analysis of the configuration after the first round.

We do not believe that the lower bound imposed on
p
is tight. It is needed in the proof of Lemma
4.1
for the union bound argument to work. It could be possible to extend Theorem
1.1
to
p\gg n^{-1/2}
or to even lower densities as in
[
4
]
.

The “power-of-the-few” question becomes more intriguing in the context of more than 2 states.
If the initial configuration is random, then one of the states dominates over each one of the other states
by
\sqrt{n}
. What would happen if we started the process from a configuration where two or more of the
states had exactly the same size? Could one extend the results of Tran and Vu
[
24
]
and those
of Sah and Sawhney
[
21
]
in this context?
