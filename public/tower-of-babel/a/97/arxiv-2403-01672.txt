Pseudo-inverse reconstruction of bandlimited signals from nonuniform generalized samples with orthogonal kernels
Nguyen T. Thao, Dominik Rzepka, Marek Miśkowicz

Abstract
Contrary to the traditional pursuit of research on nonuniform sampling of bandlimited signals, the objective of the present paper is not to find sampling conditions that permit perfect reconstruction, but to perform the best possible signal recovery from any given set of nonuniform samples, whether it is finite as in practice, or infinite to achieve the possibility of unique reconstruction in L 2 ​ ( ℝ ) L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}) . This leads us to consider the pseudo-inverse of the whole sampling map as a linear operator of Hilbert spaces. We propose in this paper an iterative algorithm that systematically performs this pseudo-inversion under the following conditions: (i) the input lies in some closed space 𝒜 {\mathscr{A}} (such as a space of bandlimited functions); (ii) the samples are formed by inner product of the input with given kernel functions; (iii) these functions are orthogonal at least in a Hilbert space ℋ {\mathscr{H}} that contains 𝒜 {\mathscr{A}} . This situation turns out to appear in certain time encoders that are part of the increasingly important area of event-based sampling. As a result of pseudo-inversion, we systematically achieve perfect reconstruction whenever the samples uniquely characterize the input, we obtain minimal-norm estimates when the sampling is insufficient, and the reconstruction errors are controlled in the case of noisy sampling. The algorithm consists in alternating two projections according to the general method of projections onto convex sets (POCS) and can be implemented by iterating time-varying discrete-time filtering. We finally show that our signal and sampling assumptions appear in a nontrivial manner in other existing problems of data acquisition. This includes multi-channel time encoding where ℋ {\mathscr{H}} is of the type L 2 ​ ( ℝ ) M L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})^{M} , and traditional point sampling with the adoption of a Sobolev space ℋ {\mathscr{H}} . This thus uncovers the unexpected possibility of sampling pseudo-inversion in existing applications, while indicating the potential of our formalism to generate future sampling schemes with systematic pseudo-inverse reconstructions.

In traditional LTI processing, errors of approximation are well controlled by Fourier analysis. In this generalization, the content of these references is revisited and reformulated to reach its most fundamental ingredients and obtain a self-sufficient theory that is independent of the applications. Our formalism contains theoretical results as well as efficient techniques of practical implementations. A goal is to propose a new framework of nonuniform sampling schemes for which a pseudo-inverse input reconstruction method by successive filtering is readily available. While this objective is meant to influence the design of future sampling schemes, we also show in this paper an immediate impact of the proposed theory by applying it to two existing sampling/reconstruction schemes: one in multi-channel time encoding

But on the theoretical side, an important role of these two applications is to show that the abstract condition (

II
Consistent reconstruction from generalized samples

In this section, we review the background on the use of POCS for the reconstruction of bandlimited signals from non-uniform generalized samples. Without loss of generality, we assume that the considered bandlimited signals have Nyquist period 1 and call their space
{\mathscr{B}}
.

II-A

Nonuniform generalized samples

To understand the specific contribution of POCS to signal reconstruction from samples, we first need to give the most general definition of signal sampling. Let
x
be a function in some Hilbert space
{\mathscr{H}}
equipped with an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. We call a generalized sample of
x\in{\mathscr{H}}
any scalar value of the type

\mathrm{s}=\langle x,g\rangle

where
g
is a known function of
{\mathscr{H}}

[
28
]
, which we call a sampling kernel function. In the most basic example of bandlimited signal sampling, the Hilbert space is
{\mathscr{H}}={\mathscr{B}}
equipped with the canonical inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2}
of
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. The point sample of
x\in{\mathscr{B}}
at instant
\tau
yields the form

x(\tau)=\langle x,g\rangle\qquad\mbox{with}\qquad g(t):=\mathrm{sinc}(t-\tau)

(4)

where

\mathrm{sinc}(t):=\sin(\pi t)/(\pi t).

(5)

The point sample of the
n
th derivative of
x\in{\mathscr{B}}
at
\tau
is also viewed as a generalized sample of
x
as it can be shown using integration by parts that

\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{n}x}{{\mathrm{d}}t^{n}}$}(\tau)=\langle x,g\rangle\qquad\mbox{with}\qquad g(t):=(-1)^{n}\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{n}\mathrm{sinc}}{{\mathrm{d}}t^{n}}$}(t-\tau).

We also trivially have generalized samples by integration as illustrated by the following example,

\textstyle\int_{a}^{b}x(t)\,{\mathrm{d}}t=\langle x,g\rangle\qquad\mbox{with}\qquad g(t):=1_{[a,b]}(t)

where
1_{[a,b]}(t)
is the indicator function of
[a,b]
.

When
{\mathscr{H}}
is a space of functions on
{\mathbb{R}}
, we say that a set of samples

(\mathrm{s}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}=(\langle x,g_{k}\rangle)_{k\in{\sf Z}}
is uniform when
{\sf Z}={\mathbb{Z}}
and there exists a function
g\in{\mathscr{H}}
and a period
T
such that
g_{k}(t)=g(t-kT)
for all
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Shannon’s sampling theorem applies to the particular case where
g(t)=\mathrm{sinc}(t)
and
T=1
in our bandlimited setting. Nonuniform sampling then consists in all of the other cases. This could be when
g_{k}(t)=g(t-t_{k})
where the instants
t_{k}
are not regularly spaced, or when
(g_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
are just not the shifted versions of a single function.

II-B

Set theoretic view of sampling

The output of each sample
\mathrm{s}_{k}=\langle x,g_{k}\rangle
gives us the deterministic knowledge that
x
belongs to the hyperplane

{\mathscr{H}}_{k}:=\big\{v\in{\mathscr{H}}:\langle v,g_{k}\rangle=\mathrm{s}_{k}\big\}.

(6)

Thus, a full sampling sequence
{\mathbf{s}}=(\mathrm{s}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
tells us that
x
belongs to the intersection

{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}}:=\textstyle\bigcap\limits_{k\in{\sf Z}}{\mathscr{H}}_{k}.

(7)

Perfect reconstruction is possible if and only if
{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}}
is limited to a singleton. When this is not the case, there is no deterministic knowledge from
(\mathrm{s}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
to distinguish
x
from any other element of
{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}}
. We call the functions of
{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}}
the
consistent estimates
of
x
. The next proposition gives some general knowledge on the algebraic structure of
{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}
.

Proposition II.1

Let
{\mathbf{s}}=(\mathrm{s}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
be any sequence. If
{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}\neq\emptyset
, then, for any
u\in{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}
,

{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}=u+{\mathscr{G}}^{\perp}

(8)

where
{\mathscr{G}}
is the closed linear span of
(g_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}

{\mathscr{G}}:=\overline{\mathrm{span}}(g_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}

and
{\mathscr{G}}^{\perp}
is its orthogonal complement in
{\mathscr{H}}
.

Proof:

By assumption,
\mathrm{s}_{k}=\langle u,g_{k}\rangle
for each
k\in{\sf Z}
. Hence,
v\in{\mathscr{H}}_{k}

\Leftrightarrow

\langle v,g_{k}\rangle=\mathrm{s}_{k}=\langle u,g_{k}\rangle

\Leftrightarrow

\langle v{-}u,g_{k}\rangle=0
. Thus,
v\in{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}
if and only if
v{-}u\in{\mathscr{G}}^{\perp}
.
In other words,
{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}=u+{\mathscr{G}}^{\perp}
.
∎

Since a linear subspace always contains the 0 vector, then
{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}
is a singleton if and only if
{\mathscr{G}}^{\perp}=\{0\}
. This is equivalent to
{\mathscr{G}}={\mathscr{H}}
, which means that the linear span of
(g_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
is dense in the whole space
{\mathscr{H}}
. Thus, the unique reconstruct of
x
solely depends on the sampling kernels
(g_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
, and not on the input
x
itself.

II-C

Systematic estimation

As mentioned in the introduction, the objective of this paper is not to study the question of unique reconstruction of an input
x
from its samples. The goal is to perform the best possible approximation of
x
from given samples
{\mathbf{s}}
, whatever they are. While the term of “best possible” would require some definition, it is at least intuitive that any reconstruction of
x
that is not consistent with its samples cannot be optimal. This idea is in fact rationally supported by the following property.

Proposition II.2

Let
{\mathscr{C}}
be a closed affine subspace of
{\mathscr{H}}
that contains
x
. Then,

\forall u\in{\mathscr{H}},\qquad\|P_{\mathscr{C}}u-x\|\leq\|u-x\|

(9)

where
P_{\mathscr{C}}
designates the orthogonal projection of
{\mathscr{H}}
onto
{\mathscr{C}}
and
\|\cdot\|
is the norm induced by the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
of
{\mathscr{H}}
. Moreover, the inequality is strict whenever
u\notin{\mathscr{C}}
.

Proof:

For
u\in{\mathscr{H}}
given,
P_{\mathscr{C}}u
is the unique element
v\in{\mathscr{C}}
such that
u{-}v\perp v{-}w
for all
w\in{\mathscr{C}}
. By the Pythagorean theorem,
\|u{-}w\|^{2}=\|u{-}v\|^{2}+\|v{-}w\|^{2}\geq\|v{-}w\|^{2}
with a strict inequality when
u\neq v
, which happens whenever
u\notin{\mathscr{C}}
. The result of (
9
) is the particular case
w=x
.
∎

Then, a systematic reconstruction procedure is to pick the consistent estimate

\hat{x}:=P_{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}

(10)

where
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
is some initial estimate proposed by the user. While
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
can be obtained by heuristic or statistical means,
\hat{x}
is an estimate of
x
that is guaranteed to be better than
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
and cannot be further improved deterministically. It is also the consistent estimate that is closest to
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
with respect to
\|\cdot\|
. The strength of this procedure is that whenever uniqueness of reconstruction is effective, whether one is able to prove it or not,
\hat{x}
is guaranteed to be the perfect reconstruction of
x
. In the case of non-unique reconstruction, the type (
10
) of reconstruction was first considered by Yen in
[
2
]
for the estimation of a bandlimited signal of
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
from a finite number of point samples, with the specific choice of
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}=0
. In this case,
\hat{x}
is the consistent estimate that is closest to 0, and hence of minimum norm. It can be easily shown from the knowledge of (
8
) that this
\hat{x}
must be in
{\mathscr{G}}
. We note here that
[
17
]
studied the case where consistent reconstruction is constrained to a linear subspace that may be different from
{\mathscr{G}}
.

The next proposition gives a more analytical description of
P_{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}}
.

Proposition II.3

If
x\in{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}
, then

\forall u\in{\mathscr{H}},\qquad P_{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}}u=u+P_{\mathscr{G}}(x-u)

(11)

where
{\mathscr{G}}
was defined in Proposition
II.1
.

Proof:

Let
v:=u-P_{\mathscr{G}}(x{-}u)
. While
v\in u+{\mathscr{G}}
,
v=x+(u{-}x)-P_{\mathscr{G}}(u{-}x)\in x+{\mathscr{G}}^{\perp}
. So
v
is the orthogonal projection of
u
onto
x+{\mathscr{G}}^{\perp}={\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}
.
∎

Note that while
x
is unknown,
P_{\mathscr{G}}(x{-}u)
can still be theoretically evaluated as it can be shown to only depend on
u
and
(\mathrm{s}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
.

II-D

POCS algorithm

In the most general case, there is unfortunately no closed form expression for
P_{\mathscr{G}}u
. When
{\sf Z}
is a small finite set,
P_{\mathscr{G}}
can only be obtained by inversion (or pseudo-inversion) of a matrix whose ill conditioning in nonuniform sampling rapidly grows with the size of
{\sf Z}

[
29
]
. The basic technique used in this paper to find
P_{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}}u
is the POCS method
[
22
,
23
]
whose basic function is to retrieve an element in the intersection of
N
closed convex sets. As closed affine spaces, the sets
{\mathscr{H}}_{k}
of (
6
) are a particular case of closed convex sets. Assuming a finite set
{\sf Z}=\{1,{\cdots},N\}
, the basic version of the POCS method applied to (
7
) consists in the iteration

u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}:=P_{{\mathscr{H}}_{N}}\cdots P_{{\mathscr{H}}_{1}}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)},\qquad n\geq 0.

(12)

Since
x
belongs to every set
{\mathscr{H}}_{k}
, we conclude from Proposition
II.2
that the estimate error
\|u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\!-x\|
strictly decreases with
n
as long as
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
has not reached
{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}}
. It is actually known
[
22
, §III.B]
that not only
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
always eventually converges to an element
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(\infty)}
in
{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}}
in norm, but the limit is more precisely

u^{\scriptscriptstyle(\infty)}=P_{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}.

As an intersection of closed affine subspaces, note that
{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}}
is itself a closed affine subspace. Thus,
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(\infty)}
is the consistent estimate of
x
that is closest to the initial iterate
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
.

II-E

Kaczmarz algorithm

The Kaczmarz algorithm is the specific name that is given to the iteration of (
12
) when the sets
{\mathscr{H}}_{k}
are simply hyperplanes, as is the case of (
6
). Given the explicit description of (
6
),
P_{{\mathscr{H}}_{k}}
yields the following simple expression
[
23
, p.403]

\forall u\in{\mathscr{H}},\qquad P_{{\mathscr{H}}_{k}}u=u+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{s}_{k}-\langle u,g_{k}\rangle}{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}$}\,g_{k}

(13)

(this can also be obtained from (
11
) in the case where
{\mathscr{G}}
is the linear span of
g_{k}
alone). The Kaczmarz algorithm was first used in sampling by Yeh and Stark in
[
30
]
for solving numerically the problem of Yen in
[
2
]
. Since then however, this method has not known much development in nonuniform sampling of bandlimited signals, primarily due to its slow convergence.

A randomized version of the Kaczmarz algorithm was later introduced in
[
31
]
for potential statistical accelerations of the convergence. A standard version of it consists in performing a random permutation of the
N
projections of (
12
) at each iteration. But this variant is till not suitable for real-time causal signal processing.

III
Orthogonal sampling kernels

The slow convergence of the Kaczmarz method is primarily due to the non-orthogonality of the sampling kernels
(g_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
. Another fundamental shortcoming of this method is the impossibility to reduce it to an iteration of the type
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}=Ru^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
with some fixed transformation
R
when
{\sf Z}
is infinite. While the number of samples is always finite in practice, an infinite index set
{\sf Z}
is always needed when dealing with the theoretical question of perfect reconstruction of a bandlimited signal in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Under the assumption of condition (
3
) where
{\mathscr{A}}
is the input space, we show in this section that there is a way to reduce the POCS algorithm to alternating two projections only. This even allows
{\sf Z}
to be infinite.
After giving some practical examples of this situation in data acquisition, we present this special POCS algorithm and its properties in absence of noise.

III-A

Practical examples of orthogonal sampling kernels

The cases of orthogonal kernels that have appeared in the literature in nonuniform sampling until now
[
20
,
21
]
take place in the space
{\mathscr{H}}:=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
with
{\mathscr{A}}:={\mathscr{B}}
as the closed subspace of inputs. Consider a sampling scheme where the samples of
x\in{\mathscr{B}}
are of the type

\mathrm{s}_{k}:=\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}f_{k}(t)\,x(t)\,{\mathrm{d}}t,\qquad k\in{\sf Z}

(14)

for some increasing sequence of time instants
(t_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
and some family of functions
(f_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. This takes the form of (
1
) with

g_{k}(t):=f_{k}(t)\,1_{[t_{k-1},t_{k}]}(t),\qquad k\in{\sf Z}.

Clearly, the functions
(g_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
are orthogonal since their supports do not overlap (up to discrete points). The samples of (
14
) are those of leaky integrate-and-fire encoding (LIF) when the functions
(f_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
are of the type

f_{k}(t):=e^{-\alpha(t-t_{k-1})}

for some constant
\alpha>0

[
21
]
. In the case
\alpha=0
, the samples are of the simple form

\mathrm{s}_{k}:=\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}x(t)\,{\mathrm{d}}t,\qquad k\in{\sf Z}

(15)

which are also the type of samples that one extracts from integrate-and-fire
[
8
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
32
]
or from an asynchronous Sigma-Delta modulator (ASDM)
[
16
,
20
]
.

III-B

Consistent reconstruction

Under the new assumptions,
x
belongs to both
{\mathscr{A}}
and
{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}
when
{\mathbf{s}}=(\mathrm{s}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
is obtained from (
1
). So the set of consistent estimates is

\displaystyle{\mathscr{A}}_{\mathbf{s}}:={\mathscr{A}}\cap{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}.

(16)

We are going to see that
{\mathscr{A}}_{\mathbf{s}}
has a similar structure to
{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathbf{s}}
within
{\mathscr{A}}
. Let

{\widetilde{g}}_{k}:=P_{\mathscr{A}}g_{k},\qquad k\in{\sf Z}.

(17)

Since
g_{k}-\widetilde{g}_{k}
is orthogonal to
{\mathscr{A}}

\displaystyle={\mathscr{A}}\cap{\mathscr{H}}_{k}

III-C

POCS for orthogonal sampling kernels

The main point of the previous section was to prepare the detailed notation of the new sampling framework and reformulate the theoretical condition of unique reconstruction. Now, the new contribution is that the POCS method can be directly applied to the 2-set decomposition of (

IV
Sampling operator and pseudo-inverse

We have assumed until now that the sampling sequence
{\mathbf{s}}=(\mathrm{s}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
is given exactly by (
1
). In practice however, samples are often corrupted by noise. The question is what happens to the POCS iteration of (
24
) when
{\mathbf{s}}
is deviated by some error. The worst case is when
{\mathbf{s}}
can no longer be the sampled version of any signal in
{\mathscr{A}}
. We will see that the POCS iteration is guaranteed to converge with any
{\mathbf{s}}
(up to some theoretical condition on its norm) when the linear operator
S
of (
2
) that maps any
x\in{\mathscr{A}}
into the sequence
(\langle x,g_{k}\rangle)_{k\in{\sf Z}}
has a pseudo-inverse
S^{\dagger}
. In this case, the limit
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(\infty)}
will appear to be exactly
S^{\dagger}{\mathbf{s}}
when
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}=0
. For the moment, the present section reviews some necessary material on operator theory that will be needed in Section
V
to prove the above mentioned POCS behavior.

IV-A

Sampling operator
S

In finite dimension, the above mentioned transformation
S
is typically formalized as a matrix. One would then naturally estimate
x
by applying the matrix pseudo-inverse
S^{+}
on
{\mathbf{s}}
. Under certain conditions, this is also possible in infinite dimension, but the setting is substantially more involved. In this case, a linear transformation typically involves infinite summations whose convergence must be guaranteed with respect to some norm. With the needed tool of orthogonal projection and the objective to invert
S
, this requires both the domain and the destination of
S
to be Hilbert spaces. A rigorous way to construct our operator
S
is to present it as follows:

S:\begin{array}[t]{rcl}{\mathscr{A}}&\rightarrow&{\mathscr{D}}\\
u&\mapsto&Su:=\big(\langle u,g_{k}\rangle\big)_{k\in{\sf Z}}\end{array}

(29)

where

{\mathscr{D}}:=\Big\{{\mathbf{c}}:\big(\mathrm{c}_{k}/\|g_{k}\|\big)_{k\in{\sf Z}}\in\ell^{2}({\sf Z})\Big\}

(30)

and
\ell^{2}({\sf Z})
is the set of square-summable sequences indexed by
{\sf Z}
. We call
S
the
sampling operator
. While
{\mathscr{A}}
is by default seen as a Hilbert space with respect to the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
of the ambient space
{\mathscr{H}}
,
{\mathscr{D}}
is a Hilbert space with respect to the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathscr{D}}
defined by

\forall{\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{d}}\in{\mathscr{D}},\qquad\langle{\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{d}}\rangle_{\mathscr{D}}:={\textstyle\sum\limits_{k\in{\sf Z}}}\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{c}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}_{k}}{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}$}.

(31)

Next, note that
Su
does belong to
{\mathscr{D}}
for all
u\in{\mathscr{A}}
. Writing
Su=(\mathrm{c}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
, this is because
(\mathrm{c}_{k}/\|g_{k}\|)_{k\in{\sf Z}}=\big(\big\langle u,g_{k}/\|g_{k}\|\big\rangle\big)_{k\in{\sf Z}}
, which belongs to
\ell^{2}({\sf Z})
since
(g_{k}/\|g_{k}\|)_{k\in{\sf Z}}
is orthonormal. When
{\sf Z}
is finite, note also that
\ell^{2}({\sf Z})
is just
{\mathbb{R}}^{\sf Z}
. Finally, when the sampling is uniform (implying that
\|g_{k}\|
is constant), note that
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathscr{D}}
coincides with the canonical inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2}
of
\ell^{2}({\sf Z})
, up to a scaling factor. Otherwise,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathscr{D}}
can be interpreted as a weighted version of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2}
to compensate for the non-uniformity.

The sampling operator leads to a more concise way to characterize the set of consistent estimates
{\mathscr{A}}_{\mathbf{s}}
. Indeed, it follows from (
19
) and (
29
) that

{\mathscr{A}}_{\mathbf{s}}=\big\{v\in{\mathscr{A}}:Sv={\mathbf{s}}\big\}=S^{-1}({\mathbf{s}}).

(32)

With this presentation,

{\mathscr{A}}_{\mathbf{s}}\neq\emptyset\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad{\mathbf{s}}\in\mathrm{ran}(S)

(33)

where
\mathrm{ran}(S)
denotes the range
S
. Finally, it will be useful to note that the null space
\mathrm{null}(S)
of
S
is given by

\mathrm{null}(S)={\mathscr{F}}^{\perp}

(34)

as a result of (
21
) and (
18
).

IV-B

V-C

In the previous section, we focused on the case where

For all

V-E

Consequence on noisy sampling

In absence of sampling noise. we already know the signal implications of the POCS limit

\displaystyle=P_{{\mathscr{F}}^{\perp}}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}\!+S^{\dagger}\hat{\mathbf{s}}=\big(P_{{\mathscr{F}}^{\perp}}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}\!+S^{\dagger}{\mathbf{s}}\big)+S^{\dagger}{\mathbf{e}}

Proposition V.7

Let

S^{\dagger}{\mathbf{e}}=S^{\dagger}\mathbf{\bar{e}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\|\mathbf{\bar{e}}\|_{\mathscr{D}}\leq\|{\mathbf{e}}\|_{\mathscr{D}}

It follows from (

\mathrm{null}(S^{\dagger})=\mathrm{null}(S^{*})=\mathrm{ran}(S)^{\perp}

By construction,

∎

Hence, only the component

VI
Practical implementation aspects

We present a rigorous way to implement the iterative part of (
50
) in discrete-time, without the need to involve generic discrete-time decompositions of the signals of
{\mathscr{A}}
(such as sinc-basis decompositions for bandlimited signals). Although not studied in this paper, we will then touch on the issue of finite-complexity implementations.

VI-A

Discrete-time implementation of iteration

Using (
49
), (
50
) takes the form

u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}=u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}+\lambda_{n}S^{*}\!({\mathbf{s}}-Su^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}),\qquad n\geq 0.

(61)

This is in practice an iteration of continuous-time functions. For digital signal processing, one expects this iteration to be discretized. Now, whether the space
{\mathscr{A}}
has a countable basis or not, there is a way to obtain
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
by a pure discrete-time iteration in
{\mathscr{D}}
. The principle is as follows. Note from (
61
) that
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}-u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\in\mathrm{ran}(S^{*})
for all
n\geq 0
. Hence,
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}-u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
must be in
\mathrm{ran}(S^{*})
as well. This implies that there exists some discrete-time sequence
{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\in{\mathscr{D}}
such that
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}=u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}+S^{*}{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
. The next proposition implies a way to construct
{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
recursively.

Proposition VI.1

For any given initial estimate
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
, the iterate
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
of (
50
) is equivalently obtained by iterating the system

\displaystyle{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}+\lambda_{n}({\mathbf{s}}_{0}-SS^{*}{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})

(62a)

\displaystyle u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}

\displaystyle=u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}+S^{*}{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}

(62b)

for
n\geq 0
, where
{\mathbf{s}}_{0}:={\mathbf{s}}-Su^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
, starting with
{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}={\bf 0}
.

Proof:

It follows from (
62b
) and (
62a
) that

\displaystyle u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}

\displaystyle=u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}\!+S^{*}{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}=u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}\!+S^{*}\big({\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}{+}\lambda_{n}({\mathbf{s}}_{0}{-}SS^{*}{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})\big)

\displaystyle=\big(u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}\!+S^{*}{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\big)+\lambda_{n}S^{*}\!\big({\mathbf{s}}-Su^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}\!-SS^{*}{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\big)

\displaystyle=u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}+\lambda_{n}S^{*}\!({\mathbf{s}}-Su^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})

which leads to (
61
).
∎

The outstanding contribution of (
62
) compared to (
50
) is that the pure discrete-time operation of (
62a
) can be iterated
alone
until the targeted iteration number
m
. Then, the continuous-time operation of (
62b
) just needs to be executed
once
at
n=m
. The operator
SS^{*}
involved in (
62a
) can be seen as a square matrix of coefficients

SS^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{\langle{\widetilde{g}}_{k^{\prime}},g_{k}\rangle}{\|g_{k^{\prime}}\|^{2}}\end{bmatrix}_{(k{,}k^{\prime})\in{\sf Z}\times{\sf Z}}

(63)

which needs to be predetermined before the iteration. In general, the coefficients can only be obtained numerically. It was proposed in
[
20
,
21
]
to obtain them from a
single-argument
lookup table.

VI-B

Finite-complexity implementation

A remaining issue is the finite-complexity implementation of (
62
). The most critical part is the recurrent operation of
SS^{*}
in (
62a
). For any
{\mathbf{c}}=(\mathrm{s}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}\in{\mathscr{D}}
, the
k
th component of
SS^{*}{\mathbf{c}}
is

\left.SS^{*}{\mathbf{c}}\right|_{k}={\textstyle\sum\limits_{k^{\prime}\in{\sf Z}}}\mathrm{h}_{k,k^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{s}_{k^{\prime}}

(64)

where
\mathrm{h}_{k,k^{\prime}}:=\langle{\widetilde{g}}_{k^{\prime}},g_{k}\rangle_{2}/\|g_{k^{\prime}\!}\|_{2}^{2}
. For concrete analysis, let us assume the traditional case where
{\mathscr{H}}=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
,
{\mathscr{A}}={\mathscr{B}}
and
{\sf Z}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}
. When the sampling is uniform, i.e.,
g_{k}(t)=g(t{-}kT)
for some

,

reduces to

where
\mathrm{h}_{k}:=\langle\widetilde{g},g_{k}\rangle_{2}/\|g\|_{2}^{2}
. So

is just a convolution operation. Note that

are simply the squared uniform samples of
\widetilde{g}(t)
with some scaling factor. With the bandlimitation,
\mathrm{h}_{k}
decays towards infinity in a sinc-like manner. For operation of finite complexity, it is necessary to approximate
\mathrm{h}_{k}
using standard FIR windowing methods, which creates reconstruction distortions. When the sampling is nonuniform,

loses its time invariance. But it can still be seen as a linear filter of time-varying impulse response

, with expected decays when
k^{\prime}
gets far away from
k
.
However, the windowing of time-varying filters plus the effect of filter distortions to the POCS iteration remain virgin topics that require new substantial investigations not tackled in this article and to be addressed in the future. Some preliminary experiments can been found in
[
20
]
.

VIII
Bandlimited interpolation by iterative piecewise-linear corrections

Until now, the examples of orthogonal sampling kernels we have provided all result from sampling by integration. An ultimate goal would be to have such a type of kernels for point sampling. For
x(t)
that is at least continuous on
{\mathbb{R}}
, one wishes to have
x(t_{k})=\langle x,h_{k}\rangle_{2}
where the functions
(h_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
have non-overlapping supports so that they are orthogonal in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. The only way to do that would be to take
h_{k}(t):=\delta(t{-}t_{k})
where
\delta(t)
is the Dirac impulse. However, this is not a function of
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. We show in this section that orthogonal kernels can actually be obtained for point sampling by choosing as ambient space
{\mathscr{H}}
a homogeneous Sobolev space. The resulting POCS method turns out to coincide with an existing algorithm by Grochenig
[
4
, §4.1]
. However, as in the example of Section
VII
, this algorithm was only analyzed in a case of noise-free perfect reconstruction, while our analysis leads to a complete set of pseudo-inversion properties.

VIII-A

Initial idea

Assume that a bandlimited function
x
is given by point samples
(x(t_{k}))_{k\in{\sf Z}}
at some known increasing sequence of instants
(t_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
. For functions
u(t)
of sufficient regularity, the point samples
(u(t_{k}))_{k\in{\sf Z}}
yield the relation

u(t_{k}){-}u(t_{k-1})=\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\!\!u^{\prime}(t)\,{\mathrm{d}}t=\left\langle u^{\prime},1_{[t_{k-1},t_{k}]}\right\rangle_{2}=\left\langle u^{\prime},g^{\prime}_{k}\right\rangle_{2}

(78)

where

g_{k}(t):=\mbox{\small$\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}0&,&t\in(-\infty,t_{k-1})\\[1.93748pt]
t-t_{k-1}&,&t\in[t_{k-1},t_{k})\\
t_{k}\!-t_{k-1}&,&t\in[t_{k},\infty)\end{array}\right.$},\quad k\in{\sf Z}.

(79)

Using the notation

\Delta a_{k}:=a_{k}\!-a_{k-1}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\langle u,v\rangle:=\langle u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\rangle_{2},

(80)

we obtain from (
78
) that

\Delta u(t_{k})=\langle u,g_{k}\rangle,\qquad k\in{\sf Z}.

(81)

From the point samples
(x(t_{k}))_{k\in{\sf Z}}
, we can then form the generalized samples

\mathrm{s}_{k}=\langle x,g_{k}\rangle\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad\mathrm{s}_{k}:=\Delta x(t_{k}),\qquad k\in{\sf Z}.

(82)

Moreover, since

,
(g^{\prime}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
is an orthogonal family in

. So
(g_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
is orthogonal with respect to

. The main issue is to find a Hilbert space
{\mathscr{H}}
in which
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is a well defined inner product.

VIII-B

Ambient Hilbert space

A suitable candidate for
{\mathscr{H}}
is the homogeneous Sobolev space
[
25
,
26
]
defined by

\displaystyle\dot{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})=\Big\{u(t):u\mbox{ is absolutely continuous on }{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\mbox{and }u^{\prime}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})

\displaystyle\Big\}

where absolute continuity is here in the local sense. In this case,
u
is absolutely continuous on
{\mathbb{R}}
if and only if it is differentiable almost everywhere of locally integrable derivative
u^{\prime}
such that

u(b)=u(a)+\int_{a}^{b}u^{\prime}(t)\,{\mathrm{d}}t
for any
a\leq b

[
40
, §11.4.6]
.
Since
u^{\prime}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
for any
u\in\dot{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
, then the function
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
of (
80
) is well defined in
\dot{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
. The remaining issue is that the induced function

\|u\|:=\langle u,u\rangle^{1\!/2}=\|u^{\prime}\|_{2}

(83)

is only a seminorm, as
\|u\|=0
only implies that
u(t)
is a constant function. In the construction of
\dot{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
, it is in fact implied that its functions are uniquely defined up to a constant component (similarly to the functions of
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
that are uniquely defined pointwise only up to a set of measure 0). Under this setting,
\|\cdot\|
is a norm and
\big(\dot{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\big)
is rigorously a Hilbert space. Qualitatively,
\|u\|^{2}
is the total slope energy of
u(t)
. We have the following list of properties.

Proposition VIII.1

(i)

(g_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
is an orthogonal family of
\dot{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
.

(ii)

For all
u\in\dot{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
,
u
satisfies (
81
).

(iii)

The subspace
{\mathscr{A}}
of all bandlimited functions of
\dot{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
of Nyquist period 1 is closed.

Proof:

(i) As we already saw the orthogonality of
(g_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, we just need to verify that
g_{k}\in\dot{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
. For each

is easily seen to be absolutely continuous. Meanwhile,

so that

.

(ii) By absolute continuity, every function

satisfies (
78
) and hence (
81
).

(iii) Let
U(\omega)
be the Fourier transform of a function

. For any interval
I
, let

be the function whose Fourier transform is

. For any given

, we can write

where

and

∎

IX
Summary

We have introduced in this paper an abstract framework where a bandlimited input signal can be reconstructed/estimated from nonuniform generalized samples by pseudo-inversion of the sampling operation, using an algorithm that consists of iterated time-varying filters. This requires that the sampling kernel functions be orthogonal at least in a Hilbert space that is larger than the input space. We prove in this paper the full pseudo-inversion properties of our algorithm. They include perfect reconstruction whenever the samples uniquely define the input, minimum-norm reconstruction when the sampling is insufficient, and a noise-shaping effect on sampling errors. While the required condition on the sampling kernels was previously observed in the time encoder of Lazar and Tóth, this condition is shown in this paper to appear in a non-trivial manner in a recent multi-channel time-encoding system as well as in traditional point sampling, Our resulting algorithms turn out to coincide with existing reconstruction methods in these cases, But our framework reveals the pseudo-inversion properties of these methods, while proposing efficient discrete-time implementations.
