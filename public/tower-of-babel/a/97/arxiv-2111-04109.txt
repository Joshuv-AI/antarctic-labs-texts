Perturbed Bessel operators
Jan Dereziński, Jérémy Faupin

Abstract
. We study perturbed Bessel operators L m 2 = − ∂ x 2 + ( m 2 − 1 4 ) 1 x 2 + Q ( x ) L_{m^{2}}=-\partial^{2}_{x}+(m^{2}-\frac{1}{4})\frac{1}{x^{2}}+Q(x) on L 2 ] 0 , ∞ [ L^{2}]0,\infty[ , where m ∈ ℂ m\in\mathbb{C} and Q Q is a complex locally integrable potential. Assuming that Q Q is integrable near ∞ \infty and x ↦ x 1 − ε ​ Q ​ ( x ) x\mapsto x^{1-\varepsilon}Q(x) is integrable near 0 0 , with ε ≥ 0 \varepsilon\geq 0 , we construct solutions to L m 2 ​ f = − k 2 ​ f L_{m^{2}}f=-k^{2}f with prescribed behaviors near 0 0 . The special cases m = 0 m=0 and k = 0 k=0 are included in our analysis. Our proof relies on mapping properties of various Green’s operators of the unperturbed Bessel operator. Then we determine all closed realizations of L m 2 L_{m^{2}} and show that they can be organized as holomorphic families of closed operators.



1.
Introduction

One of the most important families of exactly solvable 1-dimensional
Schrödinger operators
is the family of
Bessel operators

(1.1)

\displaystyle-\partial^{2}_{x}+\frac{\mathrm{c}}{x^{2}}.

As is well-known, it is convenient to set
\mathrm{c}=m^{2}-1/4
, so that (
1.1
) is rewritten as

(1.2)

\displaystyle L_{m^{2}}^{0}:=-\partial^{2}_{x}+\Big(m^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\frac{1}{x^{2}}.

There exists large literature devoted to Bessel operators, mostly
restricted to the case
m^{2}\in\mathbb{R}
(see e.g.
[
11
,
20
,
21
,
26
,
35
]
and references therein).
They are also interesting for complex
m^{2}
.
Their closed realizations on
L^{2}]0,\infty[

were studied in
[
7
,
16
]
.

Many operators in mathematics and physics
can be reduced to Bessel operators. Here are a few examples:

(1)

the
usual
Laplacian in dimension
d\geq 3
,

m=\frac{d}{2}-1+\ell
,

\ell=0,1,2,\dots
, see e.g.
[
16
, Section 3]
;

(2)

the
2d Aharonov-Bohm Hamiltonian with magnetic flux
\theta
,

m=\frac{\theta}{2\pi}+n
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
see e.g.
[
3
,
9
,
34
]
and
[
7
, Appendix B]
;

(3)

the
Laplacian on a conical surface of angle
\alpha
,

m=\frac{2\pi n}{\alpha}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
;

(4)

the
Laplacian on a wedge of angle
\alpha
with Dirichlet
or Neumann boundary conditions
,

m=\frac{\pi n}{\alpha}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
;

(5)

perturbed Bessel operators with
m
complex are used to define

Regge poles
, e.g.
[
11
,
5
]
;

(6)

three-body systems with contact interactions.

In this paper we would like to investigate Bessel operators with
complex
m

perturbed by complex valued locally integrable potentials
Q(x)
.
Our goal is to show that under some assumptions on
Q
boundary
conditions for
perturbed Bessel operators can be described in a similar way as for
unperturbed ones.

Before
describing our results, let us
review general Schrödinger operators on the half-line, and then
unperturbed Bessel operators.

1.1.
Basic facts about Schrödinger operators on the half-line

We follow mostly

[
13
]
.
Suppose that
]0,\infty[\ni x\mapsto V(x)
is a function in

L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}]0,\infty[
, possibly complex valued. Consider the expression

(1.3)

L:=-\partial_{x}^{2}+V(x).

The basic meaning of
L
used in our paper
will be that of
a linear map from
AC^{1}]0,\infty[
to
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}]0,\infty[
. Recall that
AC]0,\infty[
denotes the set of absolutely continuous functions from
]0,\infty[
to
\mathbb{C}
, that is, functions whose distributional derivative belong to
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}]0,\infty[
, and
AC^{1}]0,\infty[
is the set of functions from
]0,\infty[
to
\mathbb{C}
whose distributional derivative belongs to
AC]0,\infty[
.

Let
\mathcal{N}(L)
denote all functions in
AC^{1}[0,\infty[

annihilated by
L
. The space

\mathcal{N}(L)
is always 2-dimensional.

Let
h_{1}
,
h_{2}
be two linearly independent elements of
\mathcal{N}(L)
.
The
canonical bisolution
of
L
, denoted
G_{\leftrightarrow}
, is
defined by the integral kernel

(1.4)

G_{\leftrightarrow}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\mathscr{W}(h_{1},h_{2})}\big(h_{1}(x)h_{2}(y)-h_{2}(x)h_{1}(y)\big),

where
\mathscr{W}(h_{1},h_{2})
denotes the
Wronskian
of
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
.
Note that (
1.4
) does not depend on the choice of

h_{1},h_{2}
. The operator (
1.4
) is usually unbounded on
L^{2}]0,\infty[
,
however it is very useful in the study of
L
.

We will use the term
Green’s operator
as a synonym for a right inverse of

L
.
In other words, the integral kernel

G_{\bullet}(x,y)
of Green’s operator
G_{\bullet}
satisfies

(1.5)

\big(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V(x)\big)G_{\bullet}(x,y)=\delta(x-y).

Again, we do not insist on the boundedness of
G_{\bullet}
on
L^{2}]0,\infty[
.

We have various types of Green’s operators:

(1)

the
forward Green’s operator

G_{\to}
:

(1.6)

G_{\to}(x,y):=\theta(x-y)G_{\leftrightarrow}(x,y),

(2)

the
backward Green’s
operator

G_{\leftarrow}
:

(1.7)

G_{\leftarrow}(x,y):=-\theta(y-x)G_{\leftrightarrow}(x,y).

These two Green’s operators are forward,
resp. backward Volterra operators: when they
act on a function, they do not extend its
support to the left, resp. to the right.
They are uniquely defined given
L
: they do not depend on the choice of
h_{1},h_{2}
.

We also have

(3)

the
two-sided Green’s operator corresponding to the
boundary condition given by
h_{1}
near
0
and
h_{2}
near

\infty
:

(1.8)

G_{\bullet}(x,y):=\frac{1}{\mathscr{W}(h_{1},h_{2})}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}h_{1}(x)h_{2}(y),&x<y,\\
h_{2}(x)h_{1}(y),&y<x.\end{array}\right.

The expression (
1.8
)
depends only on the choice of the 1-dimensional subspaces
\mathbb{C}h_{1}
,
\mathbb{C}h_{2}
.

Let us now discuss realizations of
L
as closed densely defined operators on

L^{2}]0,\infty[
.
There are two obvious choices: the minimal realization
L^{\min}
and
the maximal realization
L^{\max}
. Their domains are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})

\displaystyle:=\big\{f\in L^{2}]0,\infty[\,\cap\,AC^{1}]0,\infty[\quad\mid\ Lf\in L^{2}]0,\infty[\big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(L^{\min})

\displaystyle:=\text{the closure of }\{f\in\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})\ \mid\ f=0\text{ near }0\text{ and }\infty\},

the closure being taken with respect to the graph norm of
L^{\mathrm{max}}
. In general, there may exist other
operators

L^{\bullet}
such that
L^{\min}\subset L^{\bullet}\subset L^{\max}
defined
by boundary conditions at
0

and
\infty
.

Potentials
V
considered in this paper usually vanish at
\infty
. In this
case
we do not need to specify boundary
conditions for
L
near
\infty
. This implies that either

(1.9)

\displaystyle L^{\min}=L^{\max}

,

(1.10)

\displaystyle\text{or }\qquad\dim\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})/\mathcal{D}(L^{\min})

\displaystyle=2.

If (
1.10
) is true, there exists a one-parameter family of
operators
L^{\bullet}

that
satisfy

(1.11)

L^{\min}\subsetneq L^{\bullet}\subsetneq L^{\max}.

Suppose that
L^{\bullet}
satisfies (
1.11
) or coincides with
(
1.9
). Let
\lambda
belong to the resolvent set of

L^{\bullet}
. Then the integral kernel of
(L^{\bullet}-\lambda)^{-1}
has the
form of a two-sided Green’s operator
(
1.8
) with appropriate
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
.

1.2.
Basic facts about unperturbed Bessel operators

We mostly follow

[
7
,
16
]
.
Let
m\in\mathbb{C}
and
k\in\mathbb{C}
.
Consider the space
\mathcal{N}(L_{m^{2}}^{0}+k^{2})
, that is, solutions to the eigenvalue equation

(1.12)

L_{m^{2}}^{0}f=-k^{2}f.

Solving (
1.12
) for
k=0
is
easy:

\mathcal{N}(L_{m^{2}}^{0})
is spanned by

(1.13)

\displaystyle x^{\frac{1}{2}+m},\quad x^{\frac{1}{2}-m},

\displaystyle\quad m\neq 0;

(1.14)

\displaystyle x^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad x^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{ln}(x),

\displaystyle\quad m=0.

For
k\neq 0
, (
1.12
) can be reduced to the Bessel
equation.
This justifies the name
Bessel operator
for (
1.2
).
Here is a pair of solutions of (
1.12
) for
k\neq 0
:

(1.15)

\displaystyle u_{m}^{0}(x,k)

\displaystyle:=\Big(\frac{2}{k}\Big)^{m}\sqrt{x}I_{m}(kx),

(1.16)

\displaystyle v_{m}^{0}(x,k)

\displaystyle:=\Big(\frac{k}{2}\Big)^{m}\sqrt{x}K_{m}(kx),

where
I_{m}
is the modified Bessel function and
K_{m}
the Macdonald
function (see Subsection
2.1
below). Note that
u_{m}^{0}(\cdot,k)

behaves as
\frac{x^{\frac{1}{2}+m}}{\Gamma(m+1)}
near zero and
v_{m}^{0}
decays exponentially
at infinity.
Their normalization is chosen in such a way that
their Wronskians
are
1

and they have a limit at
k=0
:

(1.17)

\displaystyle u_{m}^{0}(x,0)

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{\frac{1}{2}+m}}{\Gamma(m+1)},

(1.18)

\displaystyle v_{m}^{0}(x,0)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(m)x^{\frac{1}{2}-m}}{2},\quad\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 0,\,m\neq 0.

It is convenient to introduce another
solution
of the unperturbed eigenequation (
1.12
), which differs from
v^{0}_{m}(x,k)
only
by a different normalization:

(1.19)

w_{m}^{0}(x,k)=\sqrt{\frac{2k}{\pi}}\Big(\frac{2}{k}\Big)^{m}v_{m}^{0}(x,k)=\sqrt{\frac{2xk}{\pi}}K_{m}(kx).

Note that
w_{m}^{0}(x,k)=w_{-m}^{0}(x,k)\sim\e^{-kx}
for
x\to\infty
.

The Bessel operator for
m=0
often needs a separate treatment.
Note, for instance, that

v_{0}^{0}(\cdot,k)
does not have a limit at
k=0
. To treat the case

m=0
in a satisfactory way it is useful to introduce a family of eigenfunctions of
L_{0}^{0}
:

(1.20)

\displaystyle p_{0}^{0}(x,k)

\displaystyle:=-v_{0}^{0}(x,k)-\Big(\mathrm{ln}\Big(\frac{k}{2}\Big)+\gamma\Big)u_{0}^{0}(x,k),

where
\gamma
denotes Euler’s constant. At
k=0
it coincides with the logarithmic solution:

\displaystyle p_{0}^{0}(x,0)

\displaystyle=x^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{ln}(x).

We will often assume that

\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 0
, because
L_{m^{2}}^{0}
depends only on
m^{2}
. Based on the
behavior near zero of its eigenfunctions, one can
distinguish 3 regimes:

(1)

\mathrm{Re}(m)>0
. Eigensolutions of
L_{m^{2}}^{0}
can be divided into

principal
, that means proportional to
u_{m}^{0}
, and
non-principal
, all the others. Principal solutions
behave as
x^{\frac{1}{2}+m}
and are more regular than non-principal
ones, which behave as
x^{\frac{1}{2}-m}
.

(2)

\mathrm{Re}(m)=0
,
m\neq 0
.
Eigensolutions of
L_{m^{2}}^{0}

are spanned by
u_{m}^{0}
and
u_{-m}^{0}
,
with a comparable behavior
x^{\frac{1}{2}+m}
and
x^{\frac{1}{2}-m}

near zero.

(3)

m=0
.
Eigensolutions of
L_{m^{2}}^{0}

are spanned by
u_{0}^{0}
and
p_{0}^{0}
. Those proportional to

u_{0}^{0}
are again called
principal
, the remaining
ones are called
non-principal
. Principal
solutions behave as
x^{\frac{1}{2}}
and are more regular than
non-principal ones behaving as
x^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{ln}(x)
.

As explained in the previous subsection, with

L_{m^{2}}^{0}+k^{2}

one can associate various
Green’s operators
and the canonical bisolution. The most important are

(1)

The canonical bisolution

G_{m^{2}\leftrightarrow}^{0}(-k^{2})
;

(2)

the
forward Green’s operator

G_{m^{2},\to}^{0}(-k^{2})
;

(3)

the
backward Green’s
operator

G_{m^{2},\leftarrow}^{0}(-k^{2})
;

(4)

the
two-sided Green’s operator with homogeneous boundary
conditions

G_{m^{2},\bowtie}^{0}(-k^{2})
.

Additionally, for
m=0
we will use

(5)

the
two-sided Green’s
operator logarithmic near zero

G_{0,\vartriangle}^{0}(-k^{2})
.

Green’s operators
G_{m^{2}\leftrightarrow}^{0}(-k^{2})
,
G_{m^{2},\to}^{0}(-k^{2})
,

G_{m^{2},\leftarrow}^{0}(-k^{2})
and
G_{m^{2},\bowtie}^{0}(-k^{2})
are defined as in (
1.4
),
(
1.6
), (
1.7
), resp. (
1.8
)
where we put

h_{1}(x)=u_{m}^{0}(x,k)
,
h_{2}(x)=v_{m}^{0}(x,k)
, and use the fact that their Wronskian is

1
.

G_{0,\vartriangle}^{0}(-k^{2})
is defined as in (
1.8
) where
we put
h_{1}(x)=p_{0}^{0}(x)
,
h_{2}(x)=u_{0}^{0}(x)
and again
replace the Wronskian by

1
.

For the sake of brevity, we will often abuse terminology, calling

G_{m^{2},\bowtie}^{0}(-k^{2})
"two-sided" and

G_{0,\vartriangle}^{0}(-k^{2})
"logarithmic".
However, both
are two kinds of two-sided Green’s operators according to the
terminology of Subsection
1.1
.

Let us now sketch the theory of closed realizations

L_{m^{2}}^{0}
on
L^{2}]0,\infty[
.
First of all, we can define the minimal and maximal
realization of
L_{m^{2}}^{0}
denoted by
L_{m^{2}}^{0,\min}
and

L_{m^{2}}^{0,\max}
, respectively. They satisfy

(1.21)

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Re}(m)|\geq 1\text{ implies }

\displaystyle\quad L_{m^{2}}^{0,\min}=L_{m^{2}}^{0,\max},

(1.22)

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Re}(m)|<1\text{ implies }

\displaystyle\quad\dim\mathcal{D}(L_{m^{2}}^{0,\max})/\mathcal{D}(L_{m^{2}}^{0,\min})=2.

Thus for
|\mathrm{Re}(m)|<1
there exists a 1-parameter family of closed
realisations of
L_{m^{2}}^{0}
between

L_{m^{2}}^{0,\min}
and
L_{m^{2}}^{0,\max}
defined
by boundary conditions at zero. To describe these realizations
one can introduce
the following three families of Bessel operators

(1.23)

\displaystyle\{-1<\mathrm{Re}(m)\}\ni m

\displaystyle\mapsto H_{m}^{0},

(1.24)

\displaystyle\{-1<\mathrm{Re}(m)<1\}\times\big(\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}\big)\ni(m,\kappa)

\displaystyle\mapsto H_{m,\kappa}^{0},

(1.25)

\displaystyle\big(\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}\big)\ni\nu

\displaystyle\mapsto H_{0}^{0,\nu}.

The family
H_{m}^{0}
is the most basic one.
It is holomorphic on
\{-1<\mathrm{Re}(m)\}
(see Appendix
B
for the definition of holomorphic families of closed operators). For
1\leq\mathrm{Re}(m)
it is
the unique closed realization of
L_{m^{2}}^{0}
. Then it is extended to the
strip
-1<\mathrm{Re}(m)<1
by analytic continuation. Its domain is defined
by the boundary condition
\sim x^{\frac{1}{2}+m}
at zero, called
homogeneous
or
pure
. In other words, functions in the domain of
H_{m}^{0}
belong to the domain of the maximal operator
L_{m^{2}}^{0,\max}
and behave as
x^{\frac{1}{2}+m}
near
0
.

The operator
H_{m,\kappa}^{0}

is defined by the boundary condition
\sim x^{\frac{1}{2}+m}+\kappa x^{\frac{1}{2}-m}
at zero. For
m=0
and all
\kappa
we simply have

H_{0,\kappa}^{0}=H_{0}^{0}
. The map (
1.24
) is holomorphic except for a
singularity at
(m,\kappa)=(0,-1)
(see
Proposition 3.11(ii) in
[
12
]
).

Finally, for the special case
m=0
,

H_{0}^{0,\nu}
is defined by the boundary condition

\sim x^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{ln}(x)+\nu x^{\frac{1}{2}}
at zero. The map (
1.25
) is
holomorphic.

For
\mathrm{Re}(m)>-1
and
\mathrm{Re}(k)>0
the two-sided Green’s operator (with
pure boundary conditions) is
bounded on
L^{2}]0,\infty[
and coincides with the resolvent of
H_{m}^{0}
:

G_{m^{2},\bowtie}^{0}(-k^{2})=\big(H_{m}^{0}+k^{2}\big)^{-1}.

It should, however, be remarked that
the integral kernel
G_{m^{2},\bowtie}^{0}(-k^{2},x,y)
is well
defined and useful also for other values of
k
and
m
, when it
does not define a bounded operator.

1.3.
Overview of main results

Our paper is devoted to
perturbed Bessel operators
, that is,
to operators of the form

\displaystyle L_{m^{2}}:=-\partial^{2}_{x}+\Big(m^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\frac{1}{x^{2}}+Q(x).

We allow
m
to be complex and
Q
to be complex-valued. Throughout the paper, we will assume that
Q\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}]0,\infty[
.

Note that the condition
\mathrm{Re}(m)>-1
which we saw e.g in
(
1.23
)
appears in several places in our analysis. One can argue that the
case
\mathrm{Re}(m)\leq-1
is less important for applications, because then

x^{\frac{1}{2}+m}
is not square integrable at zero.
Nevertheless, if possible we keep
m
arbitrary, without restricting
it to
\mathrm{Re}(m)>-1
.

Our first concern in this paper is the construction of solutions
in
AC^{1}]0,\infty[
to the equation

(1.26)

L_{m^{2}}f=-k^{2}f

with a prescribed behavior near
0
or near
\infty
.
We will show that under some conditions on perturbations these
solutions are quite similar to solutions of the unperturbed
eigenequation (
1.12
).

First of all we show that if the perturbation is slightly weaker than
1/x^{2}
near zero, then
there exists a solution of the perturbed equation approximating the
principal solution, as described
in the following proposition:

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 0
,
k\in\mathbb{C}

and suppose that

(1.27)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}x|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle<\infty,\quad\text{if }m\neq 0;

(1.28)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}x(1+|\mathrm{ln}(x)|)|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle<\infty,\quad\text{if }m=0.

Suppose that
g^{0}
is a solution of (
1.12
) such that

g^{0}(x)=\mathcal{O}(x^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)})
near
0
.
Then,
there exists a unique solution
g\in AC^{1}]0,\infty[
to (
1.26
) such that,

\displaystyle g(x)-g^{0}(x)

\displaystyle=o(x^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}),

\displaystyle\partial_{x}g(x)-\partial_{x}g^{0}(x)

\displaystyle=o(x^{-\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}),\quad x\to 0.

In order to be able to well approximate all unperturbed solutions,
including the more singular ones, we need to strengthen the assumption
on the perturbation.

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 0
,
k\in\mathbb{C}

and suppose that

(1.29)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}x^{1-2\mathrm{Re}(m)}|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle<\infty,\quad\text{if }m\neq 0;

(1.30)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}x\big(1+(\mathrm{ln}(x))^{2}\big)|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle<\infty,\quad\text{if }m=0.

Then for any

g^{0}\in AC^{1}]0,\infty[

solving (
1.12
)
there exists a unique
g\in AC^{1}]0,\infty[
solving
(
1.26
)
such that

\displaystyle g(x)-g^{0}(x)

\displaystyle=o(x^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}),

\displaystyle\partial_{x}g(x)-\partial_{x}g^{0}(x)

\displaystyle=o(x^{-\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}),\quad x\to 0.

Here are consequences of Propositions
1.1
and
1.2
:

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{C}
,
k\in\mathbb{C}

and suppose that

(1.31)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}x^{1-\varepsilon}|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle<\infty,\quad\varepsilon\geq 0,\quad\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq-\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\quad m\neq 0;

(1.32)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}x(1+|\mathrm{ln}(x)|)|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle<\infty,\quad m=0.

Then
there exists a unique
u_{m}(\cdot,k)\in AC^{1}]0,\infty[
that solves (
1.26
)
and satisfies

\displaystyle u_{m}(x,k)-u_{m}^{0}(x,k)

\displaystyle=o(x^{\frac{1}{2}+|\mathrm{Re}(m)|}),

\displaystyle\partial_{x}u_{m}(x,k)-\partial_{x}u_{m}^{0}(x,k)

\displaystyle=o(x^{-\frac{1}{2}+|\mathrm{Re}(m)|}),\quad x\to 0.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{C}
and suppose that

(1.33)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}x(1+(\mathrm{ln}(x))^{2})|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle<\infty,\quad m=0.

Then
there exists a unique
p_{0}(\cdot,k)\in AC^{1}]0,\infty[
that solves (
1.26
)
and satisfies

\displaystyle p_{0}(x,k)-p_{0}^{0}(x,k)

\displaystyle=o(x^{\frac{1}{2}}),

\displaystyle\partial_{x}p_{0}(x,k)-\partial_{x}p_{0}^{0}(x,k)

\displaystyle=o(x^{-\frac{1}{2}}),\quad x\to 0.

Note that if
|Q(x)|\lesssim|x|^{\alpha}
near
0
, then Condition
(
1.31
) is satisfied for
\alpha>-2+\varepsilon
.

Conditions (
1.27
) and (
1.28
)
are the minimal assumptions near zero in the context of our paper.
They are enough to guarantee that
the behavior near zero of non-principal solutions
is roughly as in the unperturbed case:

Proposition 1.5
.

Let
\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 0
,
\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0
. Under the assumptions (
1.27
) and (
1.28
),
for all
g\in\mathcal{N}(L_{m^{2}}+k^{2})
, we have

(1.34)

\displaystyle g(x)=\mathcal{O}(x^{\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)}),\qquad\partial_{x}g(x)=\mathcal{O}(x^{-\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)}),

(1.35)

\displaystyle g(x)=\mathcal{O}(x^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{ln}(x)),\qquad\partial_{x}g(x)=\mathcal{O}(x^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{ln}(x)),

\displaystyle\qquad x\to 0.

As described in Proposition
1.1
,

Let us now consider the behavior near infinity.
To prove the existence of solutions well approximating exponentially
decaying solutions, called
Jost solutions
, we need the so-called short-range
condition on the potential.

Proposition 1.7
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{C}
. Suppose that

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

Let
k\neq 0
be such that
\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0
. Then there exists a
unique solution
w_{m}(\cdot,k)=w_{-m}(\cdot,k)\in AC^{1}]0,\infty[
to
(

\displaystyle w_{m}(x,k)-w_{m}^{0}(x,k)

\displaystyle=o(\e^{-x\mathrm{Re}(k)}),

\displaystyle\partial_{x}w_{m}(x,k)-\partial_{x}w_{m}^{0}(x,k)

\displaystyle=o(\e^{-x\mathrm{Re}(k)}),\qquad x\to\infty.

Similarly as in unperturbed case, it is often convenient to use differently
normalized Jost solutions
v_{m}(x,k):=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2k}}\Big(\frac{k}{2}\Big)^{m}w_{m}(x,k)

Proposition

To handle this case we need to impose stronger conditions
on the decay of perturbations, as described in the following two

Proposition 1.8
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{C}
,
k=0
. Suppose that

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{\delta}|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle<\infty,\quad\text{ if }m\neq 0,\quad\text{with }\delta=1+2\max\big(\mathrm{Re}(m),0\big);

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}x(1+\mathrm{ln}(x))|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle<\infty,\quad\text{ if }m=0.

Then there exists a unique

q_{m}\in AC^{1}]0,\infty[
solving (
1.26
) at
k=0
such that,

\displaystyle q_{m}(x)-x^{\frac{1}{2}+m}

\displaystyle=o(x^{\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)}),

\displaystyle\partial_{x}q_{m}(x)-\partial_{x}x^{\frac{1}{2}+m}

\displaystyle=o(x^{-\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)}),\quad x\to\infty.

Proposition 1.9
.

Let
m=0
,
k=0
. Suppose that

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}x(1+(\mathrm{ln}(x))^{2})|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle<\infty.

Then there exists a unique

q_{0,\mathrm{ln}}\in AC^{1}]0,\infty[
solving (
1.26

such that,

\displaystyle q_{0,\mathrm{ln}}(x)-x^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{ln}(x)

\displaystyle=o(x^{\frac{1}{2}}),

\displaystyle\partial_{x}q_{0,\mathrm{ln}}(x)-\partial_{x}x^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{ln}(x)

\displaystyle=o(x^{-\frac{1}{2}}),\quad x\to\infty.

The zero energy eigenequation near infinity
is equivalent
to the zero energy eigenequation near zero. This follows from the identity

(1.36)

\displaystyle-\partial_{x}^{2}+\Big(m^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\frac{1}{x^{2}}+Q(x)

\displaystyle=y^{3}\Big(-\partial_{y}^{2}+\Big(m^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\frac{1}{y^{2}}+\tilde{Q}(y)\Big)y,

(1.37)

\displaystyle y=\frac{1}{x},\qquad

\displaystyle\quad\tilde{Q}(y):=y^{-4}Q(y^{-1}).

Note also a simple relationship between the integral conditions
near zero on
Q
and near infinity on

:

(1.38)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}x^{1-\varepsilon}|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{1+\varepsilon}|{\tilde{Q}}(y)|\mathrm{d}y,

(1.39)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}x(1+|\ln(x)|^{\alpha})|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}y(1+\ln(y)^{\alpha})|{\tilde{Q}}(y)|\mathrm{d}y.

Thus one can derive Propositions
1.8

and
1.9
from
the

1.3
and
1.4

If (
1.31

Unfortunately,

{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}+G_{m,\bowtie}^{0}(-k^{2})Q

The corresponding compressed Green’s operator is denoted

In the case

Proposition 1.10
.

Suppose the assumptions of Proposition

(1.46)

\displaystyle p_{0}^{\vartriangle(a)}(\cdot,k)

\displaystyle:=\big({\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}+G_{0,\vartriangle}^{0(a)}(-k^{2})Q\big)^{-1}p_{0}^{0}(\cdot,k).

In the case

Unfortunately, the construction (

{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}+G_{0,\vartriangle}^{0(a)}(-k^{2})Q

We will describe below
an alternative approach, which leads to a simpler
description of non-principal solutions for
\mathrm{Re}(m)>0

We choose a non-negative integer
n
.
We
expand the denominator (
1.45

into a formal power series,
retaining

(quite arbitrarily)

(1.47)

\displaystyle u_{{-}m}^{0[n]}(x,k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-G_{\bowtie}^{0(1)}(0)Q)^{j}u_{{-}m}^{0}(x,k).

Proposition 1.11
.

Let
\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0
.
Let
n
be a nonnegative integer such that Condition
(
1.31

-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(n+1)\leq\mathrm{Re}(-m)\leq 0

Then
there exists a unique solution

of

(1.48)

\displaystyle u_{-m}^{[n]}(x,k)-u_{-m}^{0[n]}(x,k)

\displaystyle=o(x^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}),

(1.49)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}u_{-m}^{[n]}(x,k)-\partial_{x}u_{-m}^{0[n]}(x,k)

\displaystyle=o(x^{-\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}),\quad x\to 0.

Thus for sufficiently large

u_{-m}^{[n]}(\cdot,k)

determines uniquely a non-principal element of
\mathcal{N}(L_{m^{2}}+k^{2})

under much weaker
assumptions than before.

Boundary conditions determined by

u_{{-}m}^{0[n]}(\cdot,k)

still have an unpleasant feature – they depend on

If we want

(1.50)

u_{-m}^{0[n]}(x):=u_{-m}^{0[n]}(x,0).

In particular, under the condition
|\mathrm{Re}(m)|<1
in Proposition
1.11

we can replace
u_{{-}m}^{0[n]}(\cdot,k)
with
u_{{-}m}^{0[n]}(\cdot)
.
This condition
is also important in the

L^{2}
theory of perturbed Bessel operators as we explain below.

In concrete cases, the function
u_{-m}^{0[n]}
can be easily computed.
For instance, if

has a Coulomb
singularity at
0
, such as
Q(x)=-\frac{\beta}{x}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}_{]0,1]}(x)
with

, then we need to take

to

|\mathrm{Re}(m)|<1

Then in the generic case
m\neq\frac{1}{2}
we have

u_{-m}^{0[1]}(x)=\frac{j_{\beta,-m}(x)}{\Gamma(1-m)}+\mathcal{O}(x^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}),\quad j_{\beta,-m}(x):=x^{\frac{1}{2}-m}\big(1-\frac{\beta x}{1-2m}\big),

the function

being precisely the function used to define Whittaker operators in

[
17
,
12
]
.

An important object of our analysis is the
Jost function

\mathscr{W}_{m}(k)
, that
is the Wronskian of the two main solutions
u_{m}(\cdot,k)
and

v_{m}(\cdot,k)
.
We prove that it is well-behaved as a function of

k
:

Proposition 1.12
.

Assume
\mathrm{Re}(m)>-1
, as well as (
1.31
) if
m\neq 0
, or (
1.33
) if
m=0
. Then

(1.51)

\displaystyle\lim_{|k|\to\infty}\mathscr{W}_{m}(k)=1,\quad\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0.

Note the assumption
\mathrm{Re}(m)>-1
that appears in the above proposition – which again anticipates
the basic condition needed in the
L^{2}
analysis.

The last section of our paper, Section
6
, is devoted to closed realizations of
L_{m^{2}}

on the Hilbert space

L^{2}]0,\infty[
. First we prove that
under the assumptions of Propositions
1.1
and
1.7
,
we have

(1.52)

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Re}(m)|\geq 1\text{ implies }

\displaystyle\quad L_{m^{2}}^{\min}=L_{m^{2}}^{\max},

(1.53)

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Re}(m)|<1\text{ implies }

\displaystyle\quad\dim\mathcal{D}(L_{m^{2}}^{\max})/\mathcal{D}(L_{m^{2}}^{\min})=2.

Thus the basic picture is the same as in the
unperturbed case described in (
1.21
) and (
1.22
)

In particular, for
|\mathrm{Re}(m)|<1
, beside the minimal and maximal
realizations, there exists a 1-parameter family of closed
realizations of
L_{m^{2}}

defined by boundary conditions
at zero. Boundary conditions can be fixed by specifying continuous linear
functionals on
\mathcal{D}(L_{m^{2}}^{\max})
vanishing on

\mathcal{D}(L_{m^{2}}^{\min})
, called
boundary functionals
.
The method to descibe boundary functionals
which seems to work the best in our context uses the Wronskian at zero, that is

\mathscr{W}(f,\cdot;0):=\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\mathscr{W}(f,\cdot;x)
,
for appropriately chosen functions
f
. In practice the most
convenient
f
are approximate zero energy eigenfunctions
of
L_{m^{2}}
.

One can ask about distinguished bases of the boundary space

\mathcal{B}_{m^{2}}:=\big(\mathcal{D}(L^{\max}_{m^{2}})/\mathcal{D}(L^{\min}_{m^{2}})\big)^{\prime},

where the prime denotes the dual.
Under the assumptions of Proposition
1.1

one can always distinguish the
principal boundary functional
.
For
0\leq\mathrm{Re}(m)<1
it can be defined as

\mathscr{W}(x^{\frac{1}{2}+m},\cdot;0)
. There are also
“non-principal boundary functional”, which lead to boundary conditions
roughly of the type

x^{\frac{1}{2}-m}
for
m\neq 0
, or
x^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{ln}(x)
for
m=0
. In general their
choice is less canonical: under the assumptions of Proposition
1.1
, a basis of
\mathcal{B}_{m^{2}}
,
0\leq\mathrm{Re}(m)<1
,
m\neq 0
, is given by

\big(\mathscr{W}(x^{\frac{1}{2}+m},\cdot;0),\mathscr{W}(u_{-m}^{\bowtie(a)}(\cdot,k),\cdot;0)\big),

with
a
small enough as in Proposition
1.10
. Likewise, if
m=0
, then

\big(\mathscr{W}(x^{\frac{1}{2}},\cdot;0),\mathscr{W}(p_{0}^{\vartriangle(a)},\cdot;0)\big),

is a basis of
\mathcal{B}_{0}
.

Let us now impose the assumption

(1.54)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}x^{1-\varepsilon}|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle<\infty.

If
2>\varepsilon>0
, then
for
0\leq\mathrm{Re}(m)\leq\varepsilon/2
we have a distinguished
non-principal boundary functional given by

\mathscr{W}(x^{\frac{1}{2}-m},\cdot;0)
if
m\neq 0
and
\mathscr{W}(x^{\frac{1}{2}}\ln(x),\cdot;0)
if
m=0
.
Thus we obtain three families of perturbed Bessel operators

(1.55)

\displaystyle\Big\{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}<\mathrm{Re}(m)\Big\}\ni m

\displaystyle\mapsto H_{m},

(1.56)

\displaystyle\Big\{|\mathrm{Re}(m)|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Big\}\times\big(\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}\big)\ni(m,\kappa)

\displaystyle\mapsto H_{m,\kappa},

(1.57)

\displaystyle\big(\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}\big)\ni\nu

\displaystyle\mapsto H_{0}^{\nu},

fully analogous to the families of the unperturbed case.
All three families are holomorphic except for
a singularity of (
1.56
) at
(m,\kappa)=(0,-1)
.
They are defined as the restrictions of
L_{m^{2}}
to the domains:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(H_{m})

\displaystyle:=\Big\{f\in\mathcal{D}(L_{m^{2}}^{\max})\mid\,\mathscr{W}(x^{\frac{1}{2}+m},f;0)=0\Big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(H_{m,\kappa})

\displaystyle:=\Big\{f\in\mathcal{D}(L_{m^{2}}^{\max})\mid\mathscr{W}\big(x^{\frac{1}{2}+m}+\kappa x^{\frac{1}{2}-m},f;0\big)=0\Big\},\quad\kappa\in\mathbb{C},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(H_{m,\infty})

\displaystyle:=\Big\{f\in\mathcal{D}(L_{m^{2}}^{\max})\mid\mathscr{W}\big(x^{\frac{1}{2}-m},f;0\big)=0\Big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(H_{0}^{\nu})

\displaystyle:=\Big\{f\in\mathcal{D}(L_{0}^{\max})\mid\mathscr{W}\big(\nu x^{\frac{1}{2}}+x^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{ln}(x),f;0\big)=0\Big\},\quad\nu\in\mathbb{C},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(H_{0}^{\infty})

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{D}(H_{0}).

The maps
m\mapsto H_{m}
and
(m,\kappa)\mapsto H_{m,\kappa}
are also continuous on
\{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\leq\mathrm{Re}(m)\}
, respectively
\{|\mathrm{Re}(m)|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\kappa\in\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\},(m,\kappa)\neq(0,-1)\}
(continuous families of closed operators are defined similarly as holomorphic families of closed operators, see Appendix

).

) for
\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 1
coincides with

. It involves the boundary conditions
that can be viewed as “the most natural”, and which we call

. Note that (

) is restricted to
\{\mathrm{Re}(m)>-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\}
. This leaves the following open question.

Open Problem 1.13
.

Under the minimal conditions of Proposition
1.1
,
does

extend to
\{\mathrm{Re}(m)>-1\}
holomorphically, or at least meromorphically?

Let us now consider a nonnegative integer
n
. Under the assumption (

),

and

can use
the function

that was defined in (

Then every

.
For

are two

) are

and

, then their difference is proportional

.

\mathscr{W}(\Gamma(1-m)u_{-m}^{0[n]},\cdot;0)

The boundary functional (
1.58
) can be
used to define a family of perturbed Bessel operators which includes

:

(1.59)

\displaystyle\Big\{|\mathrm{Re}(m)|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(n+1)\Big\}\times\big(\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}\big)\ni(m,\kappa)

\displaystyle\mapsto H_{m,\kappa}^{[n]}.

Note that (
1.59
) is
less canonical than (
1.56
), however
it is defined on a wider region.

The distinguished solutions to (
1.26
)
can be used to write down the resolvent of

H_{m}
and its cousins with mixed boundary conditions.
For instance, the integral kernel of
(H_{m}+k^{2})^{-1}
coincides with
(

) with

and
h_{2}(x)=v_{m}(x,k)
.

One of the main difficulties of the analysis
comes from the need to consider separately the case

, because
generic estimates are not true due to logarithmic terms. This case is
actually very important – it corresponds to the 2-dimensional
Laplacian in the
s-wave sector
.

The case
k=0
also requires special care and is particularly important.
One can argue that the most natural way to define
boundary conditions at zero involves zero-energy eigenfunctions

[
12
]
.
Moreover,
the behavior of zero energy eigenfunctions at large distances
described by the so-called
scattering length
is responsible for
large scale properties of quantum systems, see Subsection

6.9
and
[
30
]
.

this excludes not only the
1/x^{2}
potential, but even the
1/x
potential, and makes the
theory of boundary conditions very straightforward.

Self-adjoint extensions for potentials
1/x^{2}
and
1/x
are
of course also discussed in the literature by many authors, e.g. in
[
22
,
6
,
25
,
1
,
2
,
28
]
.

There are also many treatments of
d
-dimensional Schrödinger
operators. They are closely related to the Bessel operators for

m=\frac{d}{2}-1
,
especially in the spherically
symmetric case.
We are convinced that for many readers our analysis of perturbed Bessel operators can
serve as a good introduction to the subject of Schrödinger operators
in various dimensions.

Perturbed Bessel operators with complex
m
were considered to be an important
subject already in the 70’s, especially in view of applications to
the so-called Regge poles
[
11
]
.

There exists large literature about defining boundary conditions
with the help of the
so-called
boundary triplets
, see e.g.
[
4
]
. In order to
define a boundary triplet one needs to select a
transversal pair of Lagrangian subspaces inside the boundary space. In the
case of perturbed Bessel
operators this amounts to selecting two complementary 1-dimensional
subspaces,
such as (if possible) those defined by
\mathscr{W}(x^{\frac{1}{2}+m},\cdot,0)
and
\mathscr{W}(x^{\frac{1}{2}-m},\cdot;0)
. Thus the analysis of
our paper can be treated as a preparation for an application of the
boundary triplets formalism.

The concept of a holomorphic family of closed operators goes back to

[
26
]
.
The usefulness of organizing perturbed Bessel operators
in holomorphic families was recognized by
Kato
[
26
,
27
]
.

The behavior of zero energy eigensolutions near infinity and the
related concept of the scattering length is a standard tool of
contemporary physics, at least in dimension
3
(sometimes also

2
). Mathematical treatment of this concept for all dimensions can be
found in
[
30
]
.

Many elements and partial results of our paper can be found in
the literature.
We are not aware, however, of previous work
about all closed realizations of
L_{m^{2}}
, their pure point
spectra and their holomorphic properties under the general (and
rather weak) assumptions on
Q
that we consider. In this respect,
we believe that our results are not far from being sharp, at least
concerning the behavior of
Q
near zero. As we see in our paper,
the full picture is quite complex. Note in particular that the cases
m=0
and

k=0
are quite subtle, both near
0
and
\infty
.
We have also never seen a systematic
analysis involving the boundary conditions given by
u_{m}^{0[n]}
, see
(
1.59
),
which shows how to deal with a perturbation where the most
straightforward approach fails.

The direction where our results could be
somewhat strengthened is the regularity of
Q
. This can be
done e.g. using the method
of Shkalikov and Savchuk
[
36
,
37
]
, however it would introduce an extra layer of technical complication
in our analysis.

1.5.
Notations

On
L^{2}]0,\infty[
, the notation
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
stands for the bilinear form defined by

(1.60)

\langle f|g\rangle:=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)g(x)\mathrm{d}x,\quad f,g\in L^{2}]0,\infty[.

More generally, we will use the notation

\langle f|g\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)g(x)\mathrm{d}x,

for any measurable functions
f,g
such that
fg\in L^{1}]0,\infty[
.

The
transpose
of an operator
A
, that is, the adjoint with respect to
(
1.60
) will be denoted
A^{\#}
, as in

[
13
]
.

The Wronskian of two differentiable functions
f,g
is denoted by

(1.61)

\mathscr{W}(f,g;x):=f(x)g^{\prime}(x)-f^{\prime}(x)g(x),\quad x\in]0,\infty[.

Moreover,

\mathscr{W}(f,g;0):=\lim_{x\to 0}\mathscr{W}(f,g;x),\quad\mathscr{W}(f,g;\infty):=\lim_{x\to\infty}\mathscr{W}(f,g;x),

if these limits exist. If
f,g\in AC^{1}]0,\infty[
are two solutions to the equation
(L^{\bullet}_{m^{2}}+k^{2})u=0
, where
L^{\bullet}_{m^{2}}
stands for
L^{0}_{m^{2}}
or
L_{m^{2}}
, then their Wronskian is constant and is denoted by
\mathscr{W}(f,g)
.

To shorten notations below, for
b,c\in\mathbb{R}\cup\infty
, we use the shorthand

(m,k)\in\{\mathrm{Re}(m)>b,\mathrm{Re}(k)>c\},

with the obvious meaning that
(m,k)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
are such that
\mathrm{Re}(m)>b
,
\mathrm{Re}(k)>c
, and likewise if
\mathrm{Re}(m)>b
is replaced by
\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq b
and so on.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}
. We will say that a function

\Omega\ni(m,k)\mapsto f(m,k)

is
regular
on
\Omega
if it is continuous
and
for any
m_{0},k_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
it is analytic
on

\displaystyle\{k\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ (m_{0},k)\in\Omega\}^{\circ}\ni k

\displaystyle\mapsto f(m_{0},k),

\displaystyle\{m\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ (m,k_{0})\in\Omega\}^{\circ}\ni m

\displaystyle\mapsto f(m,k_{0}),

where
K^{\circ}
denotes the interior of a set
K\subset\mathbb{C}
. Note that if
\Omega
is open then by Hartog’s theorem
f
is regular if and only if it is analytic.

In several proofs,
C
will stand for a positive constant depending on the parameters and which may vary from line to line. Moreover the notation
a\lesssim b
stands for
a\leq Cb
where
C
is a positive constant depending on the parameters.

If
A
is an operator, then
\mathcal{D}(A)
will denote its domain and
\mathcal{N}(A)
its
kernel (nullspace).

1.6.
Hypotheses

Recall that throughout the paper, we assume that
Q\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}]0,\infty[
. Depending on the results, we will require further integrability conditions near
0
and/or
\infty
. Our minimal conditions will be

\begin{split}Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{0}:=\Big\{Q\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}]0,\infty[\,\Big|\,\int_{0}^{1}x|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x<\infty\Big\}&,\quad\text{if }m\neq 0;\\
Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{0,\mathrm{ln}}:=\Big\{Q\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}]0,\infty[\,\Big|\,\int_{0}^{1}x(1+|\mathrm{ln}(x)|)|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x<\infty\Big\}&,\quad\text{if }m=0,\end{split}

near
0
and

\begin{split}Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(\infty)}_{0}:=\Big\{Q\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}]0,\infty[\,\Big|\,\int_{1}^{\infty}|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x<\infty\Big\},\end{split}

near
\infty
. We will sometimes strengthen these conditions to

\begin{split}Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{\varepsilon}:=\Big\{Q\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}]0,\infty[\,\Big|\,\int_{0}^{1}x^{1-\varepsilon}|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x<\infty\Big\}&;\\
Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{\varepsilon,\mathrm{ln}^{\beta}}:=\Big\{Q\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}]0,\infty[\,\Big|\,\int_{0}^{1}x^{1-\varepsilon}(1+|\mathrm{ln}(x)|^{\beta})|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x<\infty\Big\}.&\end{split}

with
\varepsilon\geq 0
,
\beta\geq 0
and

\begin{split}Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(\infty)}_{\delta}:=\Big\{Q\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}]0,\infty[\,\Big|\,\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{\delta}|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x<\infty\Big\},\\
Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(\infty)}_{\delta,\mathrm{ln}}:=\Big\{Q\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}]0,\infty[\,\Big|\,\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{\delta}(1+\mathrm{ln}(x))|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x<\infty\Big\}.\end{split}

with
\delta\geq 0
.

Obviously, if
0\leq\varepsilon<\varepsilon^{\prime}
,
0\leq\beta<\beta^{\prime}
, then

\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\subset\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{\varepsilon,\mathrm{ln}^{\beta^{\prime}}}\subset\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{\varepsilon,\mathrm{ln}^{\beta}}\subset\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{\varepsilon}.

Likewise, if
0\leq\delta<\delta^{\prime}
then
\mathscr{L}^{(\infty)}_{\delta^{\prime}}\subset\mathscr{L}^{(\infty)}_{\delta,\mathrm{ln}}\subset\mathscr{L}^{(\infty)}_{\delta}
. Moreover, since
Q\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}]0,\infty[
, the integrability conditions on
]0,1[
are equivalent to the same integrability conditions on
]0,a[
for any
a>0
and the integrability conditions on
]1,\infty[
are equivalent to the same integrability conditions on
]a,\infty[
for any
a>0
.

2.
Solutions of the unperturbed eigenequation

In this section we describe
solutions to the unperturbed eigenequation

(2.1)

\displaystyle L_{m^{2}}^{0}g=-k^{2}g.

2.1.
Bessel equation

The eigenequation (
2.1
) with the eigenvalue
-k^{2}=-1
has the
form

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Big(-\partial_{z}^{2}+\Big(m^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\frac{1}{z^{2}}+1\Big)g=0.

We will call (
2.2
) the
hyperbolic Bessel equation for dimension

1
, or the
hyperbolic 1d Bessel equation
.

Eq. (
2.2
) is fully equivalent to the usual modified Bessel
equation, which corresponds
to dimension
2
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\Big(-\partial_{z}^{2}-\frac{1}{z}\partial_{z}+\frac{m^{2}}{z^{2}}+1\Big)g=0.

We use the name the
hyperbolic 2d Bessel equation
for
(
2.3
). In general, we will prefer to use
(
2.2
) as our standard form of the Bessel equation.

In this subsection we briefly describe solutions of the hyperbolic 1d Bessel equation,
following mostly
[
17
,
12
]
. There are two kinds of standard solutions to the hyperbolic 1d Bessel equation (
2.2
).

The
hyperbolic 1d Bessel function

\mathcal{I}_{m}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{m}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2n+m+\frac{1}{2}}}{n!\Gamma(m+n+1)}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi z}{2}}I_{m}(z)=\sqrt{\pi}\Big(\frac{z}{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}+m}\mathbf{F}_{m}\Big(\frac{z^{2}}{4}\Big).

Here
I_{m}
is the usual modified Bessel function, which solves the
hyperbolic 2d Bessel equation and
\mathbf{F}_{m}
is the appropriately normalized

(0,1)
-hypergeometric function

\mathbf{F}_{m}(w):=\frac{{}_{0}F_{1}(m+1;w)}{\Gamma(m+1)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{w^{n}}{n!\Gamma(m+n+1)}.

Note that for
m\in\mathbb{Z}

(2.4)

\mathcal{I}_{m}(z)=\mathcal{I}_{-m}(z),\qquad\mathbf{F}_{-m}(w)=w^{2m}\mathbf{F}_{m}(w).

The analytic continuation around
0
by the angle
\pm\pi
multiplies

\mathcal{I}_{m}
by a phase factor, namely

\mathcal{I}_{m}(\e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi}z)=\e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi(m+\frac{1}{2})}\mathcal{I}_{m}(z).

The
1d Macdonald function

\mathcal{K}_{m}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{m}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{z}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{z}{2}(s+s^{-1})\right)s^{-m-1}\mathrm{d}s=\sqrt{\frac{2z}{\pi}}K_{m}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sin(\pi m)}\big(\mathcal{I}_{-m}(z)-\mathcal{I}_{m}(z)\big).

Here
K_{m}
is the usual Macdonald function, which solves the
hyperbolic 2d Bessel equation.

For any
m\in\mathbb{C}
we have
\mathcal{K}_{m}(z)=\mathcal{K}_{-m}(z)
.

For any fixed
m\in\mathbb{C}
, the maps
z\mapsto\mathcal{I}_{m}(z)
and
z\mapsto\mathcal{K}_{m}(z)
are analytic except for a branch point at
z=0
.
Thus the natural domain for these solutions is the Riemann surface of
the logarithm.
One can parametrize this surface by
|z|\in]0,\infty[
and

\arg(z)\in\mathbb{R}
. It is often convenient to
restrict the domain to
\mathbb{C}\setminus]-\infty,0]
, that is, to
|\mathrm{arg}(z)|<\pi
. One can also include
two copies of
]-\infty,0]
, from above and from below, that is
\mathrm{arg}(z)=\pm\pi
. For any fixed
z
in this domain, the maps
m\mapsto\mathcal{I}_{m}(z)
and
m\mapsto\mathcal{K}_{m}(z)
are analytic on
\mathbb{C}
.

The functions

z\mapsto\mathcal{K}_{m}(\e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi}z)
, obtained from
\mathcal{K}_{m}
by
analytic continuation,
are also solutions of
(
2.2
).
Typically, it is natural to consider the pairs of functions

z\mapsto\mathcal{K}_{m}(z),\quad z\mapsto\mathcal{K}_{m}(\e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi}z),

on
0\leq\mp\mathrm{arg}(k)\leq\pi
. Both pairs are linearly independent.
In particular,
\mathcal{I}_{m}(z)
can be expressed in terms of these functions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{m}(\e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi m}z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mp\mathrm{i}}{\sin(\pi m)}\left(\e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi m}\mathcal{I}_{m}(z)-\e^{\mp\mathrm{i}\pi m}\mathcal{I}_{-m}(z)\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{m}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{K}_{m}(\e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi}z)\mp\mathrm{i}\e^{\mp\mathrm{i}\pi m}\mathcal{K}_{m}(z)\right).

Here are the asymptotics of the solutions
\mathcal{I}_{m}
and
\mathcal{K}_{m}
near
0
:

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{m}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\Gamma(m+1)}\Big(\frac{z}{2}\Big)^{m+\frac{1}{2}}+\mathcal{O}(|z|^{\frac{5}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}),

\displaystyle m\neq-1,-2,\dots;

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{m}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(m)}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Big(\frac{z}{2}\Big)^{-m+\frac{1}{2}}+\mathcal{O}(|z|^{\frac{5}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}(m)>1;

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{m}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(m)}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Big(\frac{z}{2}\Big)^{-m+\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{\Gamma(-m)}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Big(\frac{z}{2}\Big)^{m+\frac{1}{2}}+\mathcal{O}(|z|^{\frac{5}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)}),

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Re}(m)|<1,\ m\neq 0;

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{0}(z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{2z}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Big(\mathrm{ln}\Big(\frac{z}{2}\Big)+\gamma\Big)+\mathcal{O}\big(|z|^{\frac{5}{2}}\mathrm{ln}|z|\big),

\displaystyle m=0;

(2.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Big(\frac{z}{2}\Big)^{-\frac{1}{2}}+\mathcal{O}(|z|^{\frac{3}{2}}\mathrm{ln}|z|),

\displaystyle m=\pm 1.

Recall that
\gamma
denotes Euler’s constant.

Using
the integral representation of

\mathcal{K}_{m}(z)
one can prove the following asymptotics at infinity: for any
\epsilon>0
,

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{m}(z)

\displaystyle=\e^{-z}\big(1+\mathcal{O}(z^{-1})\big),\quad|\mathrm{arg}(z)|\leq\frac{3}{2}\pi-\epsilon,\quad|z|\to\infty.

Note that the sector
|\mathrm{arg}(z)|<\frac{3}{2}\pi
is maximal for the estimate (
2.10
). Beyond this sector the estimate no longer holds.

The following estimates near zero, say, for
|z|\leq 1
, follow from the
series expansions:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{I}_{m}(z)|

\displaystyle\lesssim|z|^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)};

\displaystyle|\mathcal{K}_{m}(z)|

\displaystyle\lesssim|z|^{\frac{1}{2}-|\mathrm{Re}(m)|},\quad m\neq 0;

\displaystyle|\mathcal{K}_{0}(z)|

\displaystyle\lesssim|z|^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+|\mathrm{ln}(z)|),\quad m=0.

We also have the following estimates near
\infty
, say, for
|z|\geq 1
(and any
\epsilon>0
):

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{K}_{m}(z)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\e^{-\mathrm{Re}(z)}\quad|\mathrm{arg}(z)|\leq\frac{3}{2}\pi-\epsilon;

\displaystyle|\mathcal{K}_{m}(\e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi}z)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\e^{\mathrm{Re}(z)},\quad|\mathrm{arg}(z)\mp\pi|\leq\frac{3}{2}\pi-\epsilon;

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{I}_{m}(z)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\e^{\mathrm{Re}(z)}+\e^{-\mathrm{Re}(z)}.

Here are global estimates:

(2.11)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{K}_{m}(z)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\min(1,|z|)^{\frac{1}{2}-|\mathrm{Re}(m)|}\e^{-\mathrm{Re}(z)},\hskip 9.24994pt|\mathrm{arg}(z)|\leq\frac{3}{2}\pi-\epsilon,\hskip 9.24994ptm\neq 0;

(2.12)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{K}_{m}(\e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi}z)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\min(1,|z|)^{\frac{1}{2}-|\mathrm{Re}(m)|}\e^{\mathrm{Re}(z)},\hskip 9.24994pt|\mathrm{arg}(z)\mp\pi|\leq\frac{3}{2}\pi-\epsilon,\hskip 9.24994ptm\neq 0;

(2.13)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{K}_{0}(z)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\min(1,|z|)^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+|\mathrm{ln}\min(1,|z|)|)\e^{-\mathrm{Re}(z)},\hskip 9.24994pt|\mathrm{arg}(z)|\leq\frac{3}{2}\pi-\epsilon,\hskip 9.24994ptm=0;

(2.14)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{K}_{0}(\e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi}z)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\min(1,|z|)^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+|\mathrm{ln}\min(1,|z|)|)\e^{\mathrm{Re}(z)},\hskip 9.24994pt|\mathrm{arg}(z)\mp\pi|\leq\frac{3}{2}\pi-\epsilon,\hskip 9.24994ptm=0;

(2.15)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{I}_{m}(z)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\min(1,|z|)^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}\big(\e^{-\mathrm{Re}(z)}+\e^{\mathrm{Re}(z)}\big).

Here are the Wronskians of
various solutions of the hyperbolic 1d Bessel equation:

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}(\mathcal{I}_{m},\mathcal{I}_{-m})

\displaystyle=-\sin(\pi m),

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}(\mathcal{K}_{m},\mathcal{I}_{m})

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}\big(\mathcal{K}_{m},\mathcal{K}_{m}(\e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi}\cdot)\big)

\displaystyle=2,

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}\big(\mathcal{I}_{m},\mathcal{K}_{m}(\e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi}\cdot)\big)

\displaystyle=\mp\mathrm{i}\e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi m}.

2.2.
Equation
L_{m^{2}}^{0}g=-k^{2}g

Let us now analyze the eigenvalue equation for
L_{m^{2}}^{0}
and
an arbitrary eigenvalue
-k^{2}\neq 0
:

(2.16)

\displaystyle\Big(-\partial_{x}^{2}+\Big(m^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\frac{1}{x^{2}}\Big)g=-k^{2}g.

A direct computation shows that
for
k\neq 0

(
2.16
)
is solved by the following functions

(2.17)

\displaystyle u_{m}^{0}(x,k)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi k}}\Big(\frac{2}{k}\Big)^{m}\mathcal{I}_{m}(kx),

(2.18)

\displaystyle v_{m}^{0}(x,k)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2k}}\Big(\frac{k}{2}\Big)^{m}\mathcal{K}_{m}(kx).

For
m=0
we also introduce the solution

(2.19)

\displaystyle p_{0}^{0}(x,k)

\displaystyle:=-\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2k}}\mathcal{K}_{0}(kx)-\Big(\mathrm{ln}\Big(\frac{k}{2}\Big)+\gamma\Big)\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi k}}\mathcal{I}_{0}(kx)

\displaystyle=-v_{0}^{0}(x,k)-\Big(\mathrm{ln}\Big(\frac{k}{2}\Big)+\gamma\Big)u_{0}^{0}(x,k).

Here are their Wronskians:

(2.20)

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}\big(u_{m}^{0}(\cdot,k),u_{-m}^{0}(\cdot,k)\big)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\sin(\pi m)}{\pi},

(2.21)

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}\big(v_{m}^{0}(\cdot,k),u_{m}^{0}(\cdot,k)\big)

\displaystyle=1,\quad\mathscr{W}\big(u_{0}^{0}(\cdot,k),p_{0}^{0}(\cdot,k)\big)=1.

We define these functions also for
k=0
:

(2.22)

\displaystyle u_{m}^{0}(x,0)

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{\frac{1}{2}+m}}{\Gamma(m+1)},

(2.23)

\displaystyle v_{m}^{0}(x,0)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(m)x^{\frac{1}{2}-m}}{2},\quad\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 0,\quad m\neq 0,

(2.24)

\displaystyle p_{0}^{0}(x,0)

\displaystyle=x^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{ln}(x).

Clearly (
2.22
), (
2.23
) and (
2.24
) are annihilated
by
L_{m^{2}}^{0}
.
Note that for any fixed
x>0
,
u_{m}^{0}(x,k)
and
p_{0}^{0}(x,k)
are continuous in

k
down to
k=0
. If
\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 0
,
m\neq 0
, the same is true for

v_{m}^{0}(x,k)
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
x>0
. Then

(i)

The function
(m,k)\mapsto u_{m}^{0}(x,k)
is analytic on

\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}
.

(ii)

The function
(m,k)\mapsto v_{m}^{0}(x,k)
is
regular on

\mathbb{C}{\times}\{\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0\}\,\backslash\,\big(\{\mathrm{Re}(m)<0\}{\times}\{k=0\}\cup\{m=0\}{\times}\{k=0\}\big).

(iii)

The function
k\mapsto p_{0}^{0}(x,k)
is regular on

\{\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0\}.

Proof.

We can rewrite the definitions (
2.17
) and (
2.18
) as

(2.25)

\displaystyle u_{m}^{0}(x,k)

\displaystyle:=x^{\frac{1}{2}+m}\mathbf{F}_{m}\Big(\frac{k^{2}x^{2}}{4}\Big),

(2.26)

\displaystyle v_{m}^{0}(x,k)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\pi x^{\frac{1}{2}-m}}{2\sin(\pi m)}\Bigg(\mathbf{F}_{-m}\Big(\frac{k^{2}x^{2}}{4}\Big)-\Big(\frac{k^{2}x^{2}}{4}\Big)^{m}\mathbf{F}_{m}\Big(\frac{k^{2}x^{2}}{4}\Big)\Bigg).

From (
2.25
) the reguarity
of
u_{m}^{0}(x,k)
is obvious.
(
2.26
) directly shows the regularity of

v_{m}^{0}(x,k)
on the considered domain at
m\not\in\mathbb{Z}
. To see the regularity of

v_{m}^{0}(x,k)
at
m\in\mathbb{Z}
it suffices to use
(
2.4
) and the de l’Hopital rule.

(iii) follows from (i) and (ii) at
k\neq 0
. At
k=0
, as mentioned above, a direct computation shows that
p_{0}^{0}(x,k)\to p_{0}^{0}(x,0)
, as
k\to 0
.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

Note that
v_{m}^{0}(x,k)
can be continued across the cut

\arg(k)=\pm\frac{\pi}{2}
, so that
k=0
becomes its branch point. The
restriction to
\{\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0\}=\{|\arg(k)|\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\}
is convenient in view of
applications. It is however interesting to note that for

m\in\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}
, after this continuation, one obtains an
univalent function of
k
.

For
\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0
, we have the following estimates:

(2.27)

\displaystyle|u_{m}^{0}(x,k)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\min(|k|^{-1},x)^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}\e^{\mathrm{Re}(k)x};

(2.28)

\displaystyle|v_{m}^{0}(x,k)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\min(|k|^{-1},x)^{\frac{1}{2}-|\mathrm{Re}(m)|}\e^{-\mathrm{Re}(k)x},\quad\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 0,\quad m\neq 0;

(2.29)

\displaystyle|v_{0}^{0}(x,k)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\min(|k|^{-1},x)^{\frac{1}{2}}(1-\mathrm{ln}\min(1,|k|x))\e^{-\mathrm{Re}(k)x},\quad m=0;

(2.30)

\displaystyle|p_{0}^{0}(x,k)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\min(|k|^{-1},x)^{\frac{1}{2}}(1-\mathrm{ln}\min(|k|^{-1},x))\e^{\mathrm{Re}(k)x},\quad m=0.

The reason for complicated prefactors in
(
2.17
) and (
2.18
) is the good behavior near
k=0
. When we are
interested in
k
large, we usually prefer to replace
v_{m}^{0}
with a
differently normalized solution

(2.31)

w_{m}^{0}(x,k):=\sqrt{\frac{2k}{\pi}}\Big(\frac{2}{k}\Big)^{m}v_{m}^{0}(x,k)=\mathcal{K}_{m}(kx)

behaving as
\e^{-kx}
for
x\to\infty
.

2.3.
Canonical bisolution

In this and the following subsection we introduce a few
integral kernels naturally associated with
L_{m^{2}}^{0}+k^{2}
. The
corresponding operators are not always bounded on

L^{2}]0,\infty[
, however, they
will play an important role in our paper.

Let
h_{1}^{0},h_{2}^{0}
be any pair of solutions to
L_{m^{2}}^{0}f=-k^{2}f

satisfying
\mathscr{W}(h_{1}^{0},h_{2}^{0})\neq 0
. Then, following
[
13
]
, we introduce the operator

G^{0}_{\leftrightarrow}=G^{0}_{m^{2},\leftrightarrow}(-k^{2}):=\frac{1}{\mathscr{W}(h_{1}^{0},h_{2}^{0})}\Big(h^{0}_{1}\langle h^{0}_{2}|\cdot\rangle-h^{0}_{2}\langle h^{0}_{1}|\cdot\rangle\Big).

It has the kernel

G^{0}_{\leftrightarrow}(x,y)=G^{0}_{m^{2},\leftrightarrow}(-k^{2};x,y)=\frac{1}{\mathscr{W}(h_{1}^{0},h_{2}^{0})}\big(h^{0}_{1}(x)h^{0}_{2}(y)-h^{0}_{2}(x)h^{0}_{1}(y)\big).

Note that
G^{0}_{\leftrightarrow}
does not depend on the choice
of the functions
h_{1}^{0},h_{2}^{0}
, which justifies the adjective
canonical
.
The operator
G^{0}_{\leftrightarrow}
is called the
canonical bisolution of

L^{0}_{m^{2}}+k^{2}
. It satisfies

(L^{0}_{m^{2}}+k^{2})G^{0}_{\leftrightarrow}=G^{0}_{\leftrightarrow}(L^{0}_{m^{2}}+k^{2})=0,

which justifies calling it
bisolution
.
In particular, since the Wronskian
of
v_{m}^{0}
and
u_{m}^{0}
is
1
, one has

\displaystyle G^{0}_{m^{2},\leftrightarrow}(-k^{2};x,y)

\displaystyle=v_{m}^{0}(x,k)u_{m}^{0}(y,k)-u_{m}^{0}(x,k)v_{m}^{0}(y,k).

For
m=0
we also have

\displaystyle G^{0}_{0,\leftrightarrow}(-k^{2};x,y)

\displaystyle=-p_{0}^{0}(x,k)u_{0}^{0}(y,k)+u_{0}^{0}(x,k)p_{0}^{0}(y,k).

The canonical bisolution is defined also for
k=0
:

\displaystyle G_{m^{2},\leftrightarrow}^{0}(0;x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2m}\big(x^{\frac{1}{2}-m}y^{\frac{1}{2}+m}-x^{\frac{1}{2}+m}y^{\frac{1}{2}-m}\big),\quad m\neq 0;

\displaystyle G_{0,\leftrightarrow}^{0}(0;x,y)

\displaystyle=x^{\frac{1}{2}}y^{\frac{1}{2}}\big(\mathrm{ln}(y)-\mathrm{ln}(x)\big),\quad m=0.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
x,y>0
. Then the map

(m^{2},k^{2})\mapsto G^{0}_{m^{2},\leftrightarrow}(-k^{2};x,y),

is analytic on
\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

We can write

\displaystyle G_{\leftrightarrow}^{0}(x,y)=\frac{\pi\sqrt{xy}}{\sin(\pi m)}

\displaystyle x^{m}y^{-m}\Bigg(\mathbf{F}_{m}\Big(\frac{k^{2}x^{2}}{4}\Big)\mathbf{F}_{-m}\Big(\frac{k^{2}y^{2}}{4}\Big)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle x^{-2m}y^{2m}\mathbf{F}_{-m}\Big(\frac{k^{2}x^{2}}{4}\Big)\mathbf{F}_{m}\Big(\frac{k^{2}y^{2}}{4}\Big)\Bigg).

For
m\not\in\mathbb{Z}
, the analyticity in
m,k^{2}
is obvious from this
expression. For

m\in\mathbb{Z}
, we apply first the de l’Hopital
rule. Then we obtain a function analytic in
m,k^{2}
.

G_{\leftrightarrow}^{0}
is invariant with respect to the change
m\to-m
. Together
with the analyticity in
m
, it implies the analyticity in
m^{2}
.
∎

2.4.
Green’s operators

We will need several kinds of Green’s operators.
The
forward Green’s
operator

G^{0}_{m^{2},\rightarrow}(-k^{2})
and the
backward Green’s operator

G^{0}_{m^{2},\leftarrow}(-k^{2})

are defined by

\displaystyle G^{0}_{\rightarrow}(x,y)=G^{0}_{m^{2},\rightarrow}(-k^{2};x,y)

\displaystyle:=\theta(x-y)G^{0}_{m^{2},\leftrightarrow}(-k^{2};x,y),

\displaystyle G^{0}_{\leftarrow}(x,y)=G^{0}_{m^{2},\leftarrow}(-k^{2};x,y)

\displaystyle:=-\theta(y-x)G^{0}_{m^{2},\leftrightarrow}(-k^{2};x,y).

Here
\theta
is the Heaviside function:

\displaystyle\theta(x)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{if }\,x<0,\\
1&\text{if }\,x\geq 0.\end{array}\right.

Many properties of forward and backward Green’s operators can be
directly deduced
from those of the canonical bisolution.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
x,y>0
. Then the maps

\displaystyle(m^{2},k^{2})\,\mapsto\,

\displaystyle G^{0}_{m^{2},\to}(-k^{2};x,y),\quad G^{0}_{m^{2},\leftarrow}(-k^{2};x,y)

are analytic on
\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}
.

L_{m^{2}}^{0}
possesses various Green’s operators defined by
imposing boundary conditions at
0
and
\infty
, which can be called

two-sided
. Among them we
should distinguish
G_{m,\bowtie}^{0}(-k^{2})

given for
\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0
,
k\neq 0
, by its
integral kernel

\displaystyle G_{\bowtie}^{0}(x,y)=\,

\displaystyle G_{m,\bowtie}^{0}(-k^{2};x,y)

\displaystyle:=\,

\displaystyle\theta(x-y)v_{m}^{0}(x,k)u_{m}^{0}(y,k)+\theta(y-x)u_{m}^{0}(x,k)v_{m}^{0}(y,k).

We will call it
the
two-sided Green’s operator with pure boundary conditions
,
often abusing the terminology and shortening the name to just the
two-sided Green’s operator
.
Note that it depends on
m
and not on
m^{2}
.
Note also that at the moment we do not insist on the
conditions
\mathrm{Re}(m)>-1
and
\mathrm{Re}(k)>0
, which will be needed to make it a bounded operator.

Note the connection between the forward and two-sided Green’s
operators:

(2.32)

\displaystyle G_{m,\bowtie}^{0}(-k^{2};x,y)

\displaystyle=u_{m}^{0}(x,k)v_{m}^{0}(y,k)+G_{m^{2},\to}^{0}(-k^{2};x,y),

(2.33)

\displaystyle=v_{m}^{0}(x,k)u_{m}^{0}(y,k)+G_{m^{2},\leftarrow}^{0}(-k^{2};x,y).

For
m\neq 0
,
G_{m,\bowtie}^{0}(-k^{2})
can be also defined for
k=0
, when its integral
kernel is

\displaystyle G_{m,\bowtie}^{0}(0;x,y)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2m}\big(x^{\frac{1}{2}-m}y^{\frac{1}{2}+m}\theta(x-y)+x^{\frac{1}{2}+m}y^{\frac{1}{2}-m}\theta(y-x)\big).

For
m=0
and
k=0
Green’s operator
G_{\bowtie}^{0}
is not well
defined. This motivates us to introduce another kind of
Green’s operator at
m=0
, which will be called

Green’s operator logarithmic near zero
:

G_{\vartriangle}^{0}(x,y)=G_{0,\vartriangle}^{0}(-k^{2};x,y):=-u_{0}^{0}(x,k)p_{0}^{0}(y,k)\theta(x-y)-p_{0}^{0}(x,k)u_{0}^{0}(y,k)\theta(y-x).

It has a limit at
k=0
:

G_{0,\vartriangle}^{0}(0;x,y):=-x^{\frac{1}{2}}y^{\frac{1}{2}}\big(\mathrm{ln}(x)\theta(x-y)+\mathrm{ln}(y)\theta(y-x)\big).

Observe that
G_{\vartriangle}^{0}
and
G_{\bowtie}^{0}
differ by a
term that diverges as
k\to 0
:

G_{0,\vartriangle}^{0}(-k^{2};x,y)=G_{0,\bowtie}^{0}(-k^{2};x,y)+\Big(\mathrm{ln}\Big(\frac{k}{2}\Big)+\gamma\Big)u_{0}^{0}(x,k)u_{0}^{0}(y,k).

For
m=0
it is also natural to introduce
Green’s operator
logarithmic near infinity
:

G_{0,\triangledown}^{0}(x,y)=G_{0,\triangledown}^{0}(-k^{2};x,y):=p_{0}^{0}(x,k)u_{0}^{0}(y,k)\theta(x-y)+u_{0}^{0}(x,k)p_{0}^{0}(y,k)\theta(y-x).

It also has a limit at
k=0
:

G_{0,\triangledown}^{0}(0;x,y):=x^{\frac{1}{2}}y^{\frac{1}{2}}\big(\mathrm{ln}(y)\theta(x-y)+\mathrm{ln}(x)\theta(y-x)\big).

One could compare

G_{0,\triangledown}^{0}
and
G_{0,\leftarrow}^{0}
:

G_{0,\triangledown}^{0}(-k^{2};x,y)=G_{0,\leftarrow}^{0}(-k^{2};x,y)+p_{0}^{0}(x,k)u_{0}^{0}(y,k).

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
x,y>0
.

(i)

The function
(m,k)\mapsto G_{m,\bowtie}^{0}(-k^{2};x,y)

is regular on

\mathbb{C}\times\{\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0\}\,\backslash\,\{\mathrm{Re}(m)\leq 0\}{\times}\{k=0\}.

(ii)

The function
k\mapsto G_{0,\vartriangle}^{0}(-k^{2};x,y)
is
regular on

\{\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0\}
.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of Proposition
2.1
.
∎

Let
a>0
.
If
G_{\bullet}^{0}
is one of Green’s operators, then we introduce the
corresponding Green’s operator
compressed to the interval

]0,a[

by

(2.34)

G_{\bullet}^{0(a)}(x,y):=G_{\bullet}^{0}(x,y)\theta(a-x)\theta(a-y).

For

m\in\mathbb{C},\kappa\in\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
and
\nu\in\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}

one can also introduce Green’s operators with mixed boundary
conditions

(2.35)

\displaystyle G_{m,\kappa}^{0}(-k^{2};x,y)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\frac{1}{\Gamma(-m)}(k/2)^{-m}G_{m,\bowtie}^{0}(-k^{2})-\frac{\kappa}{\Gamma(m)}(k/2)^{m}G_{-m,\bowtie}^{0}(-k^{2})}{\frac{1}{\Gamma(-m)}(k/2)^{-m}-\frac{\kappa}{\Gamma(m)}(k/2)^{m}},\quad m\neq 0;

(2.36)

\displaystyle G_{0,\kappa}^{0}(-k^{2};x,y)

\displaystyle:=G_{0,\bowtie}^{0}(-k^{2};x,y);

(2.37)

\displaystyle G_{0}^{0,\nu}(-k^{2};x,y)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\big(\nu-\gamma-\ln(k/2)\big)G_{0,\bowtie}^{0}(-k^{2})+G_{0,\bowtie}^{0\prime}(-k^{2})}{\nu-\gamma-\ln(k/2)};

where
G_{0,\bowtie}^{0\prime}(-k^{2})
denotes

\partial_{m}G_{m,\bowtie}^{0}(-k^{2})\big|_{m=0}
.
Eq. (
2.35
) is (6.3) of
[
16
]
(generalized to

m\in\mathbb{C}
).
Eq. (
2.37
) follows from (
2.35
) by the
de l’Hopital method if we set
\kappa=\frac{\nu m-1}{\nu m+1}
as in
Remark 2.5 of
[
16
]
.
Note that (
2.37
) is consistent with (7.1) of
[
16
]
.

3.
Solutions of the perturbed eigenequation
with prescribed behavior near origin

In this section we construct solutions to the equation

(3.1)

\displaystyle L_{m^{2}}g=-k^{2}g,

and study their properties.
We will try to find solutions whose behavior near origin is
similar to solutions of the unperturbed equation

(3.2)

L_{m^{2}}^{0}g^{0}=-k^{2}g^{0}.

To shorten notations below, we will often write

u^{0}(x)=u^{0}_{m}(x)=u^{0}_{m}(x,k),\qquad v^{0}(x)=v^{0}_{m}(x)=v^{0}_{m}(x,k),

where
u^{0}_{m}(x,\cdot)
,
v^{0}_{m}(x,\cdot)
are the solutions of (
3.2
) introduced in (
2.17
) and (
2.18
). Recall that the space of solutions
in
AC^{1}]0,\infty[
to (
3.1
), respectively to (
3.2
) is denoted

{\mathcal{N}(L_{m^{2}}+k^{2})}
, respectively
\mathcal{N}(L_{m^{2}}^{0}+k^{2})
.

3.1.
Weights

One of our main tools will be various weighted

L^{\infty}
spaces. Let us introduce notation that we will
use to denote such spaces.

Let
]a,b[\,\subset\,]0,\infty[
. For any positive measurable function
\phi
on
]a,b[
, we define the following Banach space of (equivalence classes of) measurable functions on
]a,b[
:

(3.3)

\displaystyle L^{\infty}(]a,b[,\phi)

\displaystyle:=\left\{f:]a,b[\to\mathbb{C}\ \mid\ \left\|\frac{f}{\phi}\right\|_{\infty}:=\underset{x\in]a,b[}{\textrm{ess\,sup}}\left|\frac{f(x)}{\phi(x)}\right|<\infty\right\},

(3.4)

\displaystyle L_{0}^{\infty}(]0,b[,\phi)

\displaystyle:=\left\{f\in L^{\infty}(]0,b[,\phi)\ \mid\ \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{f(x)}{\phi(x)}=0\right\},

(3.5)

\displaystyle L_{\infty}^{\infty}(]a,\infty[,\phi)

\displaystyle:=\left\{f\in L^{\infty}(]a,\infty[,\phi)\ \mid\ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{f(x)}{\phi(x)}=0\right\}.

We will use the following convention. Suppose that the operator
{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}+G_{\bullet}^{0}Q
is invertible on
L^{\infty}(]0,a[,\phi)
for some
a>0
and some positive measurable function
\phi
on
]0,a[
, where
G^{0}_{\bullet}
is a Green’s operator. If
f:]0,\infty[\to\mathbb{C}
is such that its restriction to
]0,a[
belongs to
L^{\infty}(]0,a[,\phi)
, then
({\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}+G_{\bullet}^{0}Q)^{-1}f
should be understood as
({\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}+G_{\bullet}^{0}Q)^{-1}
applied to the restriction of
f
on
]0,a[
. Clearly, if in addition
f\in\mathcal{N}(L_{m^{2}}^{0}+k^{2})
, then
({\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}+G_{\bullet}^{0}Q)^{-1}f
is a solution to (
3.1
) on
]0,a[
. The unique solution on
]0,\infty[
which coincides with
({\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}+G_{\bullet}^{0}Q)^{-1}f
on
]0,a[
will be denoted by the same symbol.

In order to make the notation more compact, we
introduce the following

k
-dependent weights on
]0,\infty[
:

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(x)

\displaystyle:=\min(|k|^{-1},x),\quad\eta_{\pm k}(x):=\e^{\pm\mathrm{Re}(k)x},

(3.7)

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{k}(x)

\displaystyle:=1-\mathrm{ln}\big(|k|\mu_{k}(x)\big).

Note that for
k=0
we have
\mu_{k}(x)=x
and
\lambda_{k}
is
ill defined. For
x>|k|^{-1}
, we have
\mu_{k}(x)=|k|^{-1}
and
\lambda_{k}(x)=1
.

With these shorthands we can concisely rewrite our basic estimates on
unperturbed eigenfunctions (
2.27
)–(
2.30
):

(3.8)

\displaystyle|u_{m}^{0}(x,k)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\mu_{k}(x)^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}\eta_{k}(x);

(3.9)

\displaystyle|v_{m}^{0}(x,k)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\mu_{k}(x)^{\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)}\eta_{-k}(x),\quad\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 0,\quad m\neq 0;

(3.10)

\displaystyle|v_{0}^{0}(x,k)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\mu_{k}(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda_{k}(x)\eta_{-k}(x),\quad m=0,\quad k\neq 0;

(3.11)

\displaystyle|p_{0}^{0}(x,k)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\mu_{k}(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\big(1+|\mathrm{ln}\,\mu_{k}(x)|\big)\eta_{k}(x),\quad m=0.

Note that estimates (
3.8
), (
3.9
), (
3.10
) and
(
3.11
) are
uniform in
x\in]0,\infty[
and
\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0
.

3.2.
The forward Green’s operator

In this subsection we study the boundedness of the operator
G_{\rightarrow}^{0}Q
between
suitable weighted
L^{\infty}
-spaces. The forward Green’s operator is insensitive to the change of the sign
at
m
. Therefore, without limiting generality, we can assume that

\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 0
.

The first lemma is devoted to global properties of

G_{\rightarrow}^{0}Q
on the whole
]0,\infty[
. Note that, if
\varepsilon\geq 0
and
k\neq 0
, the condition (
3.12
) is equivalent to
Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{\varepsilon}\cap\mathscr{L}^{(\infty)}_{0}
, while (
3.13
) is equivalent to
Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{\varepsilon,\mathrm{ln}^{\beta}}\cap\mathscr{L}^{(\infty)}_{0}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0
and
Q\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}]0,\infty[
.

(i)

Let
\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 0
,
m\neq 0

and

\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon\geq\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)
.
Suppose that

(3.12)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu_{k}(y)^{1-\varepsilon}|Q(y)|\mathrm{d}y<\infty.

Then

G_{\rightarrow}^{0}Q:L^{\infty}\big(]0,\infty[,\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\eta_{k}\big)\to L_{0}^{\infty}\big(]0,\infty[,\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon}\eta_{k}\big)

is bounded by
C\times
(
3.12
) uniformly in
k
.

(ii)

Let
m=0
,
k\neq 0
,

1\leq\beta-\alpha
and

\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon\geq\frac{1}{2}
. Suppose that

(3.13)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu_{k}(y)^{1-\varepsilon}\lambda_{k}(y)^{\beta}|Q(y)|\mathrm{d}y<\infty.

Then

G_{\rightarrow}^{0}Q:L^{\infty}\big(]0,\infty[,\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}\eta_{k}\big)\to L_{0}^{\infty}\big(]0,\infty[,\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha+1-\beta}\eta_{k}\big)

is bounded by
C\times
(
3.13
) uniformly in
k
.

Proof.

For
m\neq 0
we use

\displaystyle(G_{\rightarrow}^{0}Qf)(x)=v^{0}(x)\int_{0}^{x}u^{0}(y)Q(y)f(y)\mathrm{d}y-u^{0}(x)\int_{0}^{x}v^{0}(y)Q(y)f(y)\mathrm{d}y.

By (
3.8
)–(
3.9
), we have

\displaystyle\left|u^{0}(x)\int_{0}^{x}v^{0}(y)Q(y)f(y)\mathrm{d}y\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\mu_{k}(x)^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}\eta_{k}(x)\int_{0}^{x}\mu_{k}(y)^{\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)+\varepsilon_{1}}\eta_{-k}(y)|Q(y)|\eta_{k}(y)\mathrm{d}y\left\|\frac{f}{\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\eta_{k}}\right\|_{\infty},

and

\displaystyle\left|v^{0}(x)\int_{0}^{x}u^{0}(y)Q(y)f(y)\mathrm{d}y\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\mu_{k}(x)^{\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)}\eta_{-k}(x)\int_{0}^{x}\mu_{k}(y)^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)+\varepsilon_{1}}\eta_{k}(y)|Q(y)|\eta_{k}(y)\mathrm{d}y\left\|\frac{f}{\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\eta_{k}}\right\|_{\infty}.

Using the fact that
y\mapsto\mu_{k}(y)
and
y\mapsto\eta_{k}(y)
are increasing together with

-\frac{1}{2}\mp\mathrm{Re}(m)+\varepsilon+\varepsilon_{1}\geq 0
,
we estimate both expressions by

\displaystyle\lesssim\mu_{k}(x)^{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon}\eta_{k}(x)\int_{0}^{x}\mu_{k}(y)^{1-\varepsilon}|Q(y)|\mathrm{d}y\left\|\frac{f}{\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\eta_{k}}\right\|_{\infty}.

Since

\int_{0}^{x}\mu_{k}(y)^{1-\varepsilon}|Q(y)|\mathrm{d}y=o(x^{0}),

we obtain

\left|(G_{\rightarrow}^{0}Qf)(x)\right|\leq o(x^{0})\mu_{k}(x)^{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon}\eta_{k}(x)\left\|\frac{f}{\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\eta_{k}}\right\|_{\infty}.

This proves (i).

For
m=0
, using (
3.8
) and (
3.10
), we have

\displaystyle\left|u^{0}(x)\int_{0}^{x}v^{0}(y)Q(y)f(y)\mathrm{d}y\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\mu_{k}(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\eta_{k}(x)\int_{0}^{x}\mu_{k}(y)^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon_{1}}\lambda_{k}(y)^{1+\alpha}\eta_{-k}(y)|Q(y)|\eta_{k}(y)\mathrm{d}y\left\|\frac{f}{\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}\eta_{k}}\right\|_{\infty},

and

\displaystyle\left|v^{0}(x)\int_{0}^{x}u^{0}(y)Q(y)f(y)\mathrm{d}y\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\mu_{k}(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda_{k}(x)\eta_{-k}(x)\int_{0}^{x}\mu_{k}(y)^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon_{1}}\lambda_{k}(y)^{\alpha}\eta_{k}(y)|Q(y)|\eta_{k}(y)\mathrm{d}y\left\|\frac{f}{\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}\eta_{k}}\right\|_{\infty}.

Besides the arguments used in (i), we need to
notice that

y\mapsto\lambda_{k}(y)
is decreasing and that
1+\alpha-\beta\leq 0
. We then
estimate both above expressions by

\displaystyle\lesssim\mu_{k}(x)^{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon}\lambda_{k}(x)^{1+\alpha-\beta}\eta_{k}(x)\int_{0}^{x}\mu_{k}(y)^{1-\varepsilon}\lambda_{k}(y)^{\beta}|Q(y)|\mathrm{d}y\left\|\frac{f}{\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}\eta_{k}}\right\|_{\infty}.

Since

\int_{0}^{x}\mu_{k}(y)^{1-\varepsilon}\lambda_{k}(y)^{\beta}|Q(y)|\mathrm{d}y=o(x^{0}),

we obtain

\left|(G_{\rightarrow}^{0}Qf)(x)\right|\leq o(x^{0})\mu_{k}(x)^{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon}\lambda_{k}(x)^{1+\alpha-\beta}\eta_{k}(x)\left\|\frac{f}{\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}\eta_{k}}\right\|_{\infty}.

This proves (ii).
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0

and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(i)

Let
\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 0
,
m\neq 0
. Suppose that
Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{0}
.
Then, for all
a>0
, for all

and

,

\displaystyle\frac{|(G_{\rightarrow}^{0}Q)^{n}f(x)|}{\mu_{k}(x)^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}\eta_{k}(x)}\leq\frac{C^{n+1}}{n!}\Big(\int_{0}^{x}\mu_{k}(y)|Q(y)|\mathrm{d}y\Big)^{n}\sup_{y<x}\frac{|f(y)|}{\mu_{k}(y)^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}\eta_{k}(y)}.

(ii)

Suppose
k\neq 0
. Let
m=0
and
\beta\geq 1
. Suppose that
Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{0,\mathrm{ln}^{\beta}}
.
Then, for all
a>0

Lemma 3.3

Let

\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0
,

(3.16)

Proof.

Suppose (
3.15
). Recall that the solution
p_{0}^{0}(\cdot,k)
has been introduced in (
2.19
). We write
p_{0}^{0}(x)=p_{0}^{0}(x,k)
and

to shorten notations. We then have that

\displaystyle(G_{\rightarrow}^{0}Qf)(x)=-p_{0}^{0}(x)\int_{0}^{x}u_{0}^{0}(y)Q(y)f(y)\mathrm{d}y+u_{0}^{0}(x)\int_{0}^{x}p_{0}^{0}(y)Q(y)f(y)\mathrm{d}y.

Now, for
0<x\leq 1
, by (
3.8
) and (
3.11

\displaystyle\left|u_{0}^{0}(x)\int_{0}^{x}p_{0}^{0}(y)Q(y)f(y)\mathrm{d}y\right|\lesssim x^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{x}y^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon_{1}}(1+|\mathrm{ln}(y)|^{1+\alpha})|Q(y)|\mathrm{d}y\left\|\frac{f}{x^{\varepsilon_{1}}(1+|\mathrm{ln}(x)|^{\alpha})}\right\|_{\infty},

\displaystyle\left|p_{0}^{0}(x)\int_{0}^{x}u_{0}^{0}(y)Q(y)f(y)\mathrm{d}y\right|\lesssim x^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+|\mathrm{ln}(x)|)\int_{0}^{x}y^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon_{1}}(1+|\mathrm{ln}(y)|^{\alpha})|Q(y)|\mathrm{d}y\left\|\frac{f}{x^{\varepsilon_{1}}(1+|\mathrm{ln}(x)|^{\alpha})}\right\|_{\infty}.

\displaystyle\lesssim x^{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon}(1+|\mathrm{ln}(x)|^{1+\alpha-\beta})\int_{0}^{x}y^{1-\varepsilon}(1+|\mathrm{ln}(y)|^{\beta})|Q(y)|\mathrm{d}y\left\|\frac{f}{x^{\varepsilon_{1}}(1+|\mathrm{ln}(x)|^{\alpha})}\right\|_{\infty}.

we obtain

\left|(G_{\rightarrow}^{0}Qf)(x)\right|\leq o(x^{0})x^{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon}(1+|\mathrm{ln}(x)|^{1+\alpha-\beta})\left\|\frac{f}{x^{\varepsilon_{1}}|(1+|\mathrm{ln}(x)|^{\alpha})}\right\|_{\infty}.

This concludes the proof of (
3.16
).

g
. Obviously the solutions to

g(x)u^{\prime}(x)-g^{\prime}(x)u(x)=0,

are given by
g(x)=\lambda u(x)
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
and we seek a particular solution to (
3.28
) of the
form
g(x)=\lambda(x)u(x)
, with
\lambda\in C^{1}]0,\infty[
. This gives

\lambda^{\prime}(x)u(x)^{2}=W.

By (
2.17
) we know that for some
C_{0}\neq 0

\displaystyle u(x)-C_{0}x^{\frac{1}{2}+m}

\displaystyle=o(x^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}).

This implies that there exists
\alpha>0
such that
u(x)\neq 0
for
0<x\leq\alpha
, and hence

\displaystyle\lambda(x)-\lambda(\alpha)=\int_{\alpha}^{x}\frac{W}{u(y)^{2}}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{\alpha}^{x}W\Big(C_{0}y^{-1-2m}+o(y^{-1-2\mathrm{Re}(m)})\Big)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=Cx^{-2m}+o(x^{-2\mathrm{Re}(m)}).

Now

g(x)=\Big(\lambda(\alpha)+\int_{\alpha}^{x}\frac{W}{u(y)^{2}}\mathrm{d}y\Big)u(x),

implies (
3.24
) and (
3.25
).
∎

Note that Proposition
3.10
implies, under rather
weak assumptions, that
u_{m}(\cdot,k)
is the only solution square
integrable near zero if
\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 1
.

3.5.
The two-sided Green’s operator

Mapping properties of the two-sided Green’s
operator
G_{\bowtie}^{0}
will be needed to construct solutions with a
prescribed behavior near zero in situations where we cannot apply the forward Green’s operator
G_{\to}^{0}
.
Note that the two-sided Green’s operator is not invariant with respect to the
change
m\to-m
. The following lemma is meaningful only for
\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 0
.

The operator
G_{\bowtie}^{0}Q
is not Volterra. In order to make

{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}+G_{\bowtie}^{0}Q
invertible, we will compress it to a sufficiently small
interval
]0,a[
.
Recall from (
2.34
) that

G_{\bowtie}^{0}
compressed to the interval
]0,a[

is denoted
G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0
,
0<a\leq\infty
and
Q\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}]0,\infty[
.

(i)

Let
m\neq 0
,

\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)\leq\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)

and

(3.29)

\int_{0}^{a}\mu_{k}(y)^{1-\varepsilon}|Q(y)|\mathrm{d}y<\infty.

Then

(3.30)

G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q:L^{\infty}\big(]0,a[,\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\eta_{-k}\big)\to L^{\infty}\big(]0,a[,\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon}\eta_{-k}\big)

is bounded by
C\times
(
3.29
) uniformly in
k
and
a
.
Moreover, if
\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)
,
then the image of
(
3.30
) is in
L_{0}^{\infty}\big(]0,a[,\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon}\eta_{-k}\big)
.

(ii)

Let
m=0
,
k\neq 0
,

\frac{1}{2}=\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon

and
0\leq\beta-\alpha\leq 1
. Let

(3.31)

\int_{0}^{a}\mu_{k}(y)^{1-\varepsilon}\lambda_{k}(y)^{\beta}|Q(y)|\mathrm{d}y<\infty.

Then

(3.32)

G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q:L^{\infty}\big(]0,a[,\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}\eta_{-k}\big)\to L^{\infty}\big(]0,a[,\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha-\beta+1}\eta_{-k}\big)

is bounded by
C\times
(
3.31
) uniformly in
k
and
a
.
Moreover, if
\beta-\alpha<1
, then the image of (
3.32
) is in

L_{0}^{\infty}\big(]0,a[,\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha-\beta+1}\eta_{-k}\big)
.

Proof.

For simplicity, let
a=\infty
.
We prove (i). We have

\displaystyle G_{\bowtie}^{0}Qf(x)

\displaystyle=u^{0}(x)\int_{x}^{\infty}v^{0}(y)Q(y)f(y)\mathrm{d}y+v^{0}(x)\int_{0}^{x}u^{0}(y)Q(y)f(y)\mathrm{d}y.

The second term is treated as in the proof
of Lemma
3.1
,
using
\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)\leq\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon
, namely

\displaystyle\left|v^{0}(x)\int_{0}^{x}u^{0}(y)Q(y)f(y)\mathrm{d}y\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\mu_{k}(x)^{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon}\eta_{-k}(x)\int_{0}^{x}\mu_{k}(y)^{1-\varepsilon}|Q(y)|\mathrm{d}y\left\|\frac{f}{\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\eta_{-k}}\right\|_{\infty}.

Consider now the first term. We estimate

\displaystyle\Big|u^{0}(x)\int_{x}^{\infty}v^{0}(y)Q(y)f(y)\mathrm{d}y\Big|

\displaystyle\lesssim\mu_{k}(x)^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}\eta_{k}(x)\int_{x}^{\infty}\mu_{k}(y)^{\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)+\varepsilon_{1}}\eta_{-k}(y)^{2}|Q(y)|\mathrm{d}y\left\|\frac{f}{\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\eta_{-k}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mu_{k}(x)^{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon}\eta_{-k}(x)\int_{x}^{\infty}\mu_{k}(y)^{1-\varepsilon}|Q(y)|\mathrm{d}y\left\|\frac{f}{\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\eta_{-k}}\right\|_{\infty},

where we used
\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon
. This proves (
3.30
).

Suppose now that
\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)
. Since
y\mapsto\mu_{k}(y)^{1-\varepsilon}|Q(y)|
is integrable on

]0,\infty[
,
we can apply Lemma
C.1
with
h(y)=\mu_{k}(y)^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)-\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon}\eta_{k}(x)
, which gives

\int_{x}^{\infty}\mu_{k}(y)^{\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)+\varepsilon_{1}}\eta_{-k}(y)^{2}|Q(y)|\mathrm{d}y=o\big(\mu_{k}(x)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)+\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon}\eta_{-k}(x)^{2}\big),\quad x\to 0.

This yields

G_{\bowtie}^{0}Qf(x)=o\big(\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon}(x)\eta_{-k}(x)\big)\left\|\frac{f}{\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\eta_{-k}}\right\|_{\infty},

and hence concludes the proof of (i).

To prove (ii), we proceed similarly, replacing the estimate (
3.9
) on
v_{m}^{0}
by the estimate (
3.10
) for
m=0
.
∎

Remark 3.12
.

Applying Lemma
3.11
with
\varepsilon=0
, it follows that, for
m\neq 0
and
\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)\leq\varepsilon_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)
,

\big\|G^{0(a)}_{\bowtie}Q\big\|\leq C\int_{0}^{a}\mu_{k}(y)^{1-\varepsilon}|Q(y)|\mathrm{d}y\text{ on }L^{\infty}\big(]0,a[,\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\eta_{-k}\big),

where the constant
C
is independent of
k
and
a
, but dependent on

m
. However, if the values of
m
are restricted to

|m|>m_{0}
,
0\leq\mathrm{Re}(m)\leq M
for some
m_{0}>0
,
M>0
, then one infers from the proof that the constant
C
can be chosen uniformly.

In the next corollary we show the invertibility of
{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}+G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q
for small enough
a>0
.

Corollary 3.13
.

Let
\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0
.

(i)

Let
m\neq 0
and

\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)\leq\varepsilon_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)
. Suppose that

.
Then, for small
enough

, we have

\big\|G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q\big\|<1\text{ on }L^{\infty}(]0,a[,\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\eta_{-k}),

so that

({\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}+G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q)^{-1}
exists.

(ii)

Let
m=0
,
k\neq 0
. Suppose that

Q\in\mathscr{L}_{0,\mathrm{ln}}^{(0)}
and

. Then, for
small
enough
a>0
, we have

\big\|G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q\big\|<1\text{ on }L^{\infty}(]0,a[,\mu_{k}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}\eta_{-k}),

so that

({\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}+G_{\bowtie}^{0,(a)}Q)^{-1}

To prove (i), it suffices to apply Lemma
3.11
(i) with

.

To prove (ii), we apply Lemma
3.11
(ii) with

and

.
∎

3.6.
Solutions constructed with the help of the two-sided
Green’s operator

The goal of this subsection is similar to that of Subsection
3.3
: to construct solutions to (
3.1
)
that approximate near
0
the solutions to the unperturbed equation
(
3.2
).
In this subsection we cover a different parameter range than in Subsection
3.3
. This is accomplished by using a different tool. Instead
of the forward Green’s operator, we use the
two-sided Green’s
operator compressed to a sufficiently small interval

]0,a[
,

G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}
, which was studied in the previous subsection.
The construction here will be
less canonical than in Subsection
3.3
– it will depend on the
parameter
a
.

Theorem 3.14
.

Let
\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0
.

(i)

Let
m_{0}>0
,
M>0
and

. Then for small enough

, for all

such that

,

, for all

is a solution in
AC^{1}]0,\infty[
to (
3.1
). If in addition
0\leq\varepsilon<2\mathrm{Re}(m)

(3.33)

\displaystyle g^{\bowtie}(x)-g^{0}(x)

\displaystyle=o(x^{\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)+\varepsilon}),

(3.34)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}g^{\bowtie}(x)-\partial_{x}g^{0}(x)

\displaystyle=o(x^{-\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)+\varepsilon}).

(ii)

Let
m=0
and assume that
k\neq 0
. Suppose that
Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{0,\mathrm{ln}}
. Then for small enough
a>0
, for all
g^{0}\in\mathcal{N}(L_{0}^{0}+k^{2})
,

g^{\bowtie}:=({\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}+G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q)^{-1}g^{0}

is a solution in
AC^{1}]0,\infty[

to (
3.1
) such that

\displaystyle g^{\bowtie}(x)-g^{0}(x)

\displaystyle=o(x^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{ln}(x)),

\displaystyle\partial_{x}g^{\bowtie}(x)-\partial_{x}g^{0}(x)

\displaystyle=o(x^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{ln}(x)).

Proof.

To prove (i), we apply Corollary
3.13
(i) (and Remark
3.12
) with
\varepsilon_{1}=\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)
: By making
a>0
small
enough, we can thus make sure that for

|m|>m_{0}
,
0\leq\mathrm{Re}(m)\leq M
,
m\neq 0
, the operator
G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q
has
the norm
<1
on the space
L^{\infty}(]0,a[,\mu_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)}\eta_{-k})
. Hence
g^{\bowtie}
is well-defined and is a solution to (
3.1
).

We have
g^{\bowtie}-g^{0}=(-G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q)g^{\bowtie}
. Since
g^{\bowtie}=\mathcal{O}(x^{\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)})
by Proposition
3.10
and since
\varepsilon<2\mathrm{Re}(m)
, we can apply Lemma
3.11
(i) with
\varepsilon_{1}=\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)
. This yields (
3.33
)–(
3.34
).

To prove (ii) we proceed in the same way, using Corollary

3.13
(ii) and Lemma
3.11
(ii)
with
\varepsilon=0
and
\alpha=\beta=1
.
∎

We can apply Theorem
3.14
(i)
to the unperturbed solutions

g^{0}
equal to
u_{-m}^{0}(\cdot,k)
and

u_{m}^{0}(\cdot,k)
, obtaining solutions
u_{-m}^{\bowtie(a)}(\cdot,k)
and

u_{m}^{\bowtie(a)}(\cdot,k)
.
The solutions
u_{m}^{\bowtie(a)}(\cdot,k)
for
\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 0
are not very useful,
since we have then
u_{m}(\cdot,k)
at our disposal.
Therefore, in the following proposition we restrict ourselves to

u_{-m}^{\bowtie(a)}(\cdot,k)
. They can serve as a non-principal
solution defined when
u_{-m}
is not available.

Proposition 3.15
.

Let
\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0
. Suppose that
Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{0}
. Let

m_{0}>0
,
M>0
. Let

a>0
be small
enough as in the previous theorem. Then for

|m|>m_{0}
,
0\leq\mathrm{Re}(m)\leq M
, setting

u_{-m}^{\bowtie(a)}(\cdot,k):=({\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}+G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q)^{-1}u_{-m}^{0}(\cdot,k)

we obtain a solution in
AC^{1}]0,\infty[

to (
3.1
).
If we impose the assumption

Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{\varepsilon}
for

0\leq\varepsilon<2\mathrm{Re}(m)
, then

(3.35)

\displaystyle u_{-m}^{\bowtie(a)}(x,k)-u_{-m}^{0}(x,k)

\displaystyle=o(x^{\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)+\varepsilon}),

(3.36)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}u_{-m}^{\bowtie(a)}(x,k)-\partial_{x}u_{-m}^{0}(x,k)

\displaystyle=o(x^{-\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)+\varepsilon}).

If
Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{\varepsilon}
with

0\leq 2\mathrm{Re}(m)\leq\varepsilon
and
m\not\in\mathbb{N}
,
then there exists
c_{m}^{\bowtie(a)}(k)\in\mathbb{C}
such that

(3.37)

u_{-m}^{\bowtie(a)}(x,k)=u_{-m}(x,k)+c_{m}^{\bowtie(a)}(k)u_{m}(x,k).

Proof.

The first part of the proposition is a direct consequence of Theorem

3.14
(i).
To see (
3.37
) note that by Proposition
3.6
, both
u_{m}(\cdot,k)
and
u_{-m}(\cdot,k)
are well-defined. Moreover, if
m\notin\mathbb{N}
, they are linearly independent, as follows from their asymptotics near
0
.
∎

The eigensolutions
u_{{-}m}^{\bowtie(a)}
constructed in
Proposition
3.15
are given by convergent
expansions

(3.38)

\displaystyle u_{{-}m}^{\bowtie(a)}(x,k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q)^{j}u_{{-}m}^{0}(x,k).

Note that the individual terms on the right hand side of
(
3.38
) are well defined under rather weak
assumptions and their behavior near zero weakly depends on
a
.

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0
. Assume that
\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 0
,
m\neq 0
and

Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{0}
, or
m=0
,
Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{0,\ln}
and
k\neq 0
. Let
0<a,b
. Then for any
j\in\mathbb{N}

there
exists
c_{m}^{j}(k)
such that

(3.39)

\displaystyle(G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q)^{j}u_{{-}m}^{0}(x,k)-(G_{\bowtie}^{0(b)}Q)^{j}u_{{-}m}^{0}(x,k)=c_{m}^{j}(k)u^{0}_{m}(x,k)+o(x^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}).

Proof.

Suppose that
m\neq 0
. We will prove (
3.39
) by induction with respect to
j
. Let us denote
the left hand side of (
3.39
) by
z^{j}(x,k)
. Clearly,
z^{0}(x,k)=0
.
Assume that (
3.39
) is true for a given
j
. Let

0\leq x\leq a<b
. Now

\displaystyle z^{j+1}(x)

\displaystyle=(G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q-G_{\bowtie}^{0(b)}Q)(G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q)^{j}u_{{-}m}^{0}(x)+G_{\bowtie}^{0(b)}Qz^{j}(x)

\displaystyle=u^{0}_{m}(x)\int_{a}^{b}v^{0}_{m}(y)Q(y)(G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q)^{j}u_{{-}m}^{0}(y)\mathrm{d}y+u^{0}_{m}(x)\int_{0}^{b}v^{0}_{m}(y)Q(y)z^{j}(y)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\quad-u^{0}_{m}(x)\int_{0}^{x}v^{0}_{m}(y)Q(y)z^{j}(y)\mathrm{d}y+v^{0}_{m}(y)\int_{0}^{x}u_{m}^{0}(y)Q(y)z^{j}(y)\mathrm{d}y.

The first term is clearly proportional to
u_{m}^{0}
.
By the induction assumption
z_{j}=\mathcal{O}(x^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)})
. Therefore
the integral in the second term is finite, and hence the second term
is also proportional to
u_{m}^{0}
. By the same argument the
third term is
o(x^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)})
. Finally,
since
z_{j}=\mathcal{O}(x^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)})
,
the integral in the fourth term is
o(x^{2\mathrm{Re}(m)})
. Hence the
fourth term is also
o(x^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)})
.

The case
m=0
with
Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{0,\ln}
and
k\neq 0
can be treated in the same way.
∎

Under the assumptions of Lemma
3.16
, we introduce
the following notation for a partial sum of the series (
3.38
):

(3.40)

\displaystyle u_{{-}m}^{0(a)[n]}(x,k)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q)^{j}u_{{-}m}^{0}(x,k),

\displaystyle u_{{-}m}^{0[n]}(x,k)

\displaystyle:=u_{{-}m}^{0(1)[n]}(x,k).

Thus we choose (quite arbitrarily)
a=1
as the “standard value” in (

).

The next proposition shows that the functions

u_{{-}m}^{0[n]}(\cdot,k)

well approximate non-principal solutions under the assumption

0\leq\mathrm{Re}(m)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(n+1)
,
m\neq 0
.

Proposition 3.17
.

Let
\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0
and
\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 0
,
m\neq 0
. Assume that
\varepsilon\geq 0
and
Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{\varepsilon}
.
Let
n
be a nonnegative integer such that

\varepsilon\geq\frac{2}{n+1}\mathrm{Re}(m)
. Suppose that

a>0
is small
enough, so that

is well defined, as described in Proposition

.
Then there exists

c_{m}^{(a)[n]}(k)\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\displaystyle u_{{-}m}^{\bowtie(a)}(x,k)-u_{{-}m}^{0[n]}(x,k)-c_{m}^{(a)[n]}(k)u_{m}(x,k)

\displaystyle=o(x^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}),

\displaystyle\partial_{x}u_{{-}m}^{\bowtie(a)}(x,k)-\partial_{x}u_{{-}m}^{0[n]}(x,k)-c_{m}^{(a)[n]}(k)\partial_{x}u_{m}(x,k)

\displaystyle=o(x^{-\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}).

Applying

(3.42)

(G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Qf)(x)=u^{0}(x)\int_{0}^{a}v^{0}(y)Q(y)f(y)\mathrm{d}y+G_{\to}^{0}Qf(x),

we can write

\displaystyle u_{{-}m}^{\bowtie(a)}(x,k)=

\displaystyle u_{{-}m}^{0(a)[n]}(x,k)+(-G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q)^{n+1}u_{{-}m}^{\bowtie(a)}(x,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{{-}m}^{0(a)[n]}(x,k)-u_{m}^{0}(x,k)\int_{0}^{a}v_{m}^{0}(x,k)Q(y)(-G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q)^{n}u_{{-}m}^{\bowtie(a)}(y,k)\mathrm{d}y

(3.43)

\displaystyle-G_{\to}^{0}Q(-G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q)^{n}u_{{-}m}^{\bowtie(a)}(x,k).

Suppose
n>0
. Let
\tilde{n}\leq n
be a positive integer such that
\frac{2}{\tilde{n}}\mathrm{Re}(m)>\varepsilon\geq\frac{2}{\tilde{n}+1}\mathrm{Re}(m)
. We apply repeatedly Lemma
3.11
(i) with

(-G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q)^{\tilde{n}}u_{{-}m}^{\bowtie(a)}(x,k)=o(x^{\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)+\tilde{n}\varepsilon}).

Applying then again repeatedly Lemma
3.11
(i) with
\varepsilon=0
we deduce that

(3.45)

(-G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q)^{n}u_{{-}m}^{\bowtie(a)}(x,k)=o(x^{\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)+\tilde{n}\varepsilon}).

Because of this, and since
\varepsilon\geq\frac{2}{\tilde{n}+1}\mathrm{Re}(m)
,

(3.46)

\int_{0}^{a}v_{m}^{0}(y,k)Q(x)(-G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q)^{n}u_{{-}m}^{\bowtie(a)}(y,k)\mathrm{d}y

is finite. Now we apply Lemma
3.1
(i) with

, noting that

\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon\geq\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)
, to show that

(3.47)

G_{\to}^{0}Q(-G_{\bowtie}^{0(a)}Q)^{n}u_{{-}m}^{\bowtie(a)}(x,k)=o(x^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}).

If
n=0
, applying Lemma
3.1
(i) with

\varepsilon_{1}=\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{Re}(m)
, we see that (

) still holds.
Finally, by Lemma
3.16
we can replace

u_{{-}m}^{0(a)[n]}(x,k)
with

u_{{-}m}^{0[n]}(x,k)
.
∎

We can use the functions
u_{{-}m}^{0[n]}(\cdot,k)
to describe
boundary conditions near zero of non-principal solutions.

Proposition 3.18
.

Let
\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0
and
\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 0
,
m\neq 0
.
Suppose that
Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{\varepsilon}
,
\varepsilon\geq 0
.
Let
n
be a nonnegative integer such that

\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(n+1)\geq\mathrm{Re}(m)
.

4.3.
Asymptotics of non-principal solutions near
\infty
.

In this subsection, under the minimal assumptions

Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(\infty)}_{0}
, we show that all elements of

\mathcal{N}(L_{m^{2}}+k^{2})
not proportional to Jost solutions behave like
non-principal unperturbed solutions near
\infty
. In particular, the
following proposition shows that they
are not square integrable.

Proposition 4.11
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{C}
,
\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0
and
k\neq 0
. Suppose that
Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(\infty)}_{0}
. Let
g
be a solution of (
3.1
) linearly independent with
w_{m}(\cdot,k)
constructed in Proposition
4.5
. Then there exists a constant
C\neq 0
such that

(4.17)

g(x)=C\e^{kx}+o(\e^{\mathrm{Re}(k)x}),\quad x\to\infty.

Proof.

Similarly as in the proof of Proposition
3.10
,
assuming that
w\equiv w_{m}(\cdot,k)
and
W
are known, we solve the ordinary differential equation

g(x)w^{\prime}(x)-g^{\prime}(x)w(x)=W.

The ansatz
g(x)=\lambda(x)w(x)

yields

\lambda^{\prime}(x)w(x)^{2}=W.

By Proposition
4.5
and (
2.10
), we know that

w(x)=\e^{-kx}+o(\e^{-kx}),\quad x\to\infty.

This implies that there exists
\alpha>0
such that
w(x)\neq 0
for
x\geq\alpha
, and hence

\displaystyle\lambda(x)-\lambda(\alpha)=\int_{\alpha}^{x}\frac{W}{w(y)^{2}}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{\alpha}^{x}W\Big(\e^{2ky}+o(\e^{2ky})\Big)\mathrm{d}y=\frac{W}{2k}\e^{2kx}+o(\e^{2kx}).

Now

g(x)=\Big(\lambda(\alpha)+\int_{\alpha}^{x}\frac{W}{w(y)^{2}}\mathrm{d}y\Big)w(x),

implies the estimate (
4.17
).
∎

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}v^{0}_{m}(x,k)+\langle u^{0}_{m}|Qv_{m}\rangle\partial_{x}v^{0}_{m}(x,k)+o(x^{-\frac{1}{2}-|\mathrm{Re}(m)|}).

Now (
2.31
), (
5.7
)–(
5.8
) and (
5.9
)–(
5.10
) yield

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}(v_{m},u_{m};x)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{W}(v^{0}_{m},u_{m};x)\Big(1+\langle u^{0}_{m}|Qv_{m}\rangle\Big)+o(x^{0}).

Moreover it follows from (
5.7
) and (
5.8
) that

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\mathscr{W}(v_{m}^{0},u_{m};x)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{W}(v^{0}_{m},u^{0}_{m};x)+o(x^{0})=1+o(x^{0}).

Then note that
\mathscr{W}(v_{m},u_{m};x)

does not depend on
x

and use the definition (
5.1
) of
\mathscr{W}_{m}(k)
.

In the case
m=0
,
we use Proposition
4.13
(iii).
Then we repeat the same arguments, using in addition

\mathscr{W}(u^{0}_{0},u_{0};x)=\mathscr{W}(u^{0}_{0},u^{0}_{0};x)+o(x^{0}),\quad\mathscr{W}(u^{0}_{0},u^{0}_{0};x)=0.

This ends the proof of (
5.6
).
∎

The asymptotic behavior of the Jost function can be deduced from Proposition
5.2
together with the following lemma.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{C}
. Suppose that
Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{\varepsilon}\cap\mathscr{L}^{(\infty)}_{0}
with
\varepsilon=\max(0,-2\mathrm{Re}(m))
if
m\neq 0
, or

Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{0,\mathrm{ln}^{2}}\cap\mathscr{L}^{(\infty)}_{0}
if
m=0
.
Then

(5.11)

\displaystyle\langle u_{m}^{0}|Qv_{m}\rangle=o(|k|^{0})+\mathcal{O(}{|k|^{-1+\varepsilon}}),\qquad|k|\to\infty,\quad\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0.

Proof.

Assume that
m\neq 0
.
We have the estimate

(5.12)

|u^{0}_{m}(x,k)|\lesssim\mu_{k}(x)^{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{Re}(m)}\eta_{k}(x),

uniformly in
k
. Next, in the estimate of Proposition
4.12
, we first note that the big exponential on the right hand side
is uniformly bounded for large
k
. Therefore, uniformly for large enough
|k|
,

(5.13)

|v_{m}(x,k)|\lesssim\mu_{k}(x)^{\frac{1}{2}-|\mathrm{Re}(m)|}\eta_{-k}(x).

Hence

\displaystyle\big|\langle u^{0}_{m}|Qv_{m}\rangle\big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{0}_{m}(y)Q(y)v_{m}(y)\mathrm{d}y\Big|

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu_{k}(x)^{1-\varepsilon}|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{|k|}}x^{1-\varepsilon}|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x+|k|^{-1+\varepsilon}\int_{\frac{1}{|k|}}^{1}|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x+|k|^{-1+\varepsilon}\int_{1}^{\infty}|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x\Big).

First note that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{|k|}}x^{1-\varepsilon}|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x=o(1)\quad\text{ and }\quad\int_{1}^{\infty}|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x=\mathcal{O}(1).

Therefore, the first term on the right is
\mathop{}\mathopen{}o\mathopen{}\left(|k|^{0}\right)
and the third is
\mathcal{O}(|k|^{-1+\varepsilon})
.

If
1>\varepsilon\geq 0
, then applying Lemma
C.1
with
h(y)=y^{1-\varepsilon}
and

x=\frac{1}{|k|}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{1}{|k|}}^{1}|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x=o(|k|^{1-\varepsilon}).

So the middle term is
o(|k|^{0})
.

If
\varepsilon\geq 1
, then

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{1}{|k|}}^{1}|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x=o(|k|^{1-\varepsilon}).

So the middle term is
\mathcal{O}(|k|^{-1+\varepsilon})
.

Finally, if
m=0
, the proof is identical, the only difference being that

\displaystyle\big|\langle u^{0}_{0}|Qv_{0}\rangle\big|

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{|k|}}x(1-|\mathrm{ln}(|k|x)|)|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x+|k|^{-1}\int_{\frac{1}{|k|}}^{1}|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x+|k|^{-1}\int_{1}^{\infty}|Q(x)|\mathrm{d}x\Big).

∎

We deduce from the previous lemma that, for
\mathrm{Re}(m)>-1
,

\mathscr{W}_{m}
cannot constantly vanish except on a discrete set. Corollary
5.4
implies Proposition
1.12
from the introduction.

Corollary 5.4
.

In addition to the assumptions of Proposition
5.2
,
suppose that
\mathrm{Re}(m)>-1
.
Then

(5.14)

\displaystyle\lim_{|k|\to\infty}\mathscr{W}_{m}(k)=1,\quad\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0.

Therefore,

\big\{k\in\mathbb{C},\,\mathrm{Re}(k)>0,\,\mathscr{W}_{m}(k)=0\big\}\quad\text{ is discrete.}

Proof.

Under the condition
\mathrm{Re}(m)>-1
the right hand side of (
5.11
) becomes

o(|k|^{0})
. Hence
(
5.14
) follows by (
5.6
).

The fact
that
\{k\in\mathbb{C},\,\mathrm{Re}(k)>0,\,\mathscr{W}_{m}(k)=0\}
is discrete is then a consequence of the analyticity of
\mathscr{W}_{m}

stated in Proposition
5.1
.
∎

Note that Corollary
5.4
is the second place in our paper
where the condition
\mathrm{Re}(m)>-1
appears (see also Theorem
3.20
). This condition will play an
important role in Section
6
about closed realizations.

Wronskians – refined results

If
u_{-m}
is ill-defined, we can often use

instead.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0
,
k\neq 0
.
Suppose that

,

.

n
be a nonnegative integer,

\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(n+1)\geq\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 0

), which follows from (

), using also Propositions

. This allows us to compute

5.3.
Green’s functions for perturbed Bessel operators

As for every 1-dimensional Schrödinger operator, we can define the
canonical bisolution and various Green’s functions for perturbed
Bessel operators. The solutions that we constructed allow us to
give explicit expressions for these Green’s functions.

As usual, when defining Green’s operators we will always
assume that
\mathrm{Re}(k)\geq 0
(although sometimes an extension to a larger
domain is possible). Let

Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(\infty)}_{0}\cap\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{0}
for
m\neq 0
, and

Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(\infty)}_{0}\cap\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{0,\ln}
, for
m=0
.
The canonical bisolution associated with the operator
L_{m^{2}}+k^{2}
is

(5.18)

G_{m^{2},\leftrightarrow}(-k^{2};x,y)=\frac{1}{\mathscr{W}_{m}(k)}\big(v_{m}(x,k)u_{m}(y,k)-u_{m}(x,k)v_{m}(y,k)\big),

where
v_{m}(\cdot,k)
,
u_{m}(\cdot,k)
are the solutions to
(
1.26
) constructed in Propositions
3.6
and

4.5
, and
\mathscr{W}_{m}(k)
is the Jost function defined in
(
5.1
). The expression (
5.18
) is well-defined when
\mathscr{W}_{m}(k)\neq 0
and has a limit at
k=0
. For
m=0
we can use

\displaystyle G_{0,\leftrightarrow}(-k^{2};x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathscr{W}_{0}(k)}\big(-p_{0}(x,k)u_{0}(y,k)+u_{0}(x,k)p_{0}(y,k)\big).

From the canonical bisolution, we can construct in the usual way the
forward Green’s
operator

G_{m^{2},\rightarrow}(-k^{2})
and the
backward Green’s operator

G_{m^{2},\leftarrow}(-k^{2})
of
L_{m^{2}}+k^{2}
:

(5.19)

\displaystyle G_{m^{2},\rightarrow}(-k^{2};x,y)

\displaystyle:=\theta(x-y)G^{0}_{m^{2},\leftrightarrow}(-k^{2};x,y),

(5.20)

\displaystyle G_{m^{2},\leftarrow}(-k^{2};x,y)

\displaystyle:=-\theta(y-x)G^{0}_{m^{2},\leftrightarrow}(-k^{2};x,y).

Green’s operators defined by
specifying boundary conditions at zero and at infinity will be called

two-sided
.
They will often be bounded on
L^{2}]0,\infty[
and
coincide with the resolvents of various closed realizations of

L_{m^{2}}
. However, they are of
interest even when they do not define bounded operators and do
not correspond to closed realizations of

L_{m^{2}}
.

The most natural two-sided Green’s operator corresponds to
pure boundary
conditions
. In the unperturturbed case they are usually called
homogeneous boundary
conditions
, but in the perturbed case this name seems no
longer appropriate. It can be defined for

0\leq\varepsilon
,

Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(\infty)}_{0}\cap\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{\varepsilon}
,
m\neq 0
,

-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\leq\mathrm{Re}(m)
, and

Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(\infty)}_{0}\cap\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{0,\ln}
,
m=0
. Moreover, if
\mathrm{Re}(m)\leq 0
we neeed to assume

k\neq 0
. Then if
\mathscr{W}_{m}(k)\neq 0
we set

(5.21)

G_{m,\bowtie}(-k^{2};x,y):=\frac{1}{\mathscr{W}_{m}(k)}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}u_{m}(x,k)v_{m}(y,k),&x<y,\\
v_{m}(x,k)u_{m}(y,k),&y<x.\end{array}\right.

We also have Green’s operators with mixed boundary conditions.
The cleanest situation we have under the assumption

0\leq\varepsilon
,

Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(\infty)}_{\varepsilon}\cap\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{0}
,
m\neq 0
,

|\mathrm{Re}(m)|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
,

k\neq 0
.
Then if
k\neq 0
,
\kappa\in\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
and

\mathscr{W}_{m}(k)+\kappa\frac{\Gamma(1-m)}{\Gamma(1+m)}\frac{k^{2m}}{2^{2m}}\mathscr{W}_{-m}(k)\neq 0
, we define

(5.22)

\displaystyle G_{m,\bowtie,\kappa}(-k^{2};x,y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\big(\mathscr{W}_{m}(k)+\kappa\frac{\Gamma(1-m)}{\Gamma(1+m)}\frac{k^{2m}}{2^{2m}}\mathscr{W}_{-m}(k)\big)}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}(u_{m}+\kappa\frac{\Gamma(1-m)}{\Gamma(1+m)}u_{-m})(x,k)v_{m}(y,k),&x<y,\\
v_{m}(x,k)(u_{m}+\kappa\frac{\Gamma(1-m)}{\Gamma(1+m)}u_{-m})(y,k),&y<x.\end{array}\right.

Note that

(5.25)

G_{m,\bowtie,\kappa}(-k^{2};x,y)=G_{-m,\bowtie,\kappa^{-1}}(-k^{2};x,y).

Similarly, for

Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(\infty)}_{0}\cap\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{0,\ln}
,

m=0
, if

\nu\mathscr{W}_{0}(k)+\mathscr{W}(v_{0}(\cdot,k),p_{0}(\cdot,k))\neq 0
,
then we define

(5.26)

\displaystyle G^{\nu}_{0,\bowtie}(-k^{2};x,y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\big(\nu\mathscr{W}_{0}(k)+\mathscr{W}(v_{0}(\cdot,k),p_{0}(\cdot,k))\big)}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}(\nu u_{0}+p_{0})(x,k)v_{0}(y,k),&x<y,\\
v_{0}(x,k)(\nu u_{0}+p_{0})(y,k),&y<x.\end{array}\right.

Without the assumption

\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
we are not guaranteed the existence of

u_{m}
, and hence we are not sure whether Green’s function with pure
boundary conditions can be extended. However, we can use the boundary
conditions given by
u_{-m}^{[n]}
.
Choose
\varepsilon>0
,
Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(\infty)}_{\varepsilon}\cap\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{0}
,
m\neq 0
, and a nonnegative integer
n
.
Then for
-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(n+1)\leq\mathrm{Re}(m)<0
if

k\neq 0
,
\kappa\in\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
and

\mathscr{W}\big(v_{m}(\cdot,k),u_{m}^{[n]}(\cdot,k)\big)+\kappa\frac{\Gamma(1-m)}{\Gamma(1+m)}\frac{k^{2m}}{2^{2m}}\mathscr{W}_{-m}(k)\neq 0
,
we can define

(5.29)

\displaystyle G_{m,\bowtie,\kappa}^{[n]}(-k^{2};x,y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathscr{W}\big(v_{m}(\cdot,k),u_{m}^{[n]}(\cdot,k)\big)+\kappa\frac{\Gamma(1-m)}{\Gamma(1+m)}\frac{k^{2m}}{2^{2m}}\mathscr{W}_{-m}(k)}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}(u_{m}^{[n]}+\kappa\frac{\Gamma(1-m)}{\Gamma(1+m)}u_{-m})(x,k)v_{m}(y,k),&x<y,\\
v_{m}(x,k)(u_{m}^{[n]}+\kappa\frac{\Gamma(1-m)}{\Gamma(1+m)}u_{-m})(y,k),&y<x.\end{array}\right.

6.
Closed realizations of
L_{m^{2}}

In this section we describe realizations of
L_{m^{2}}
as closed operators
on
L^{2}]0,\infty[
. We will see that under certain assumptions on the perturbation
Q
one can impose the boundary condition at
0
in a similar way as in the unperturbed case. If we fix
Q
, it is also natural to organize these operators in holomorphic families, analogous to the holomorphic families studied in
[
12
]
.

In the first two subsections we recall
the basics of the theory of 1d Schrödinger operators and their boundary conditions.

6.1.
1-dimensional Schrödinger operators on the halfline

We will follow
[
13
]
, other references include
[
20
,
21
]
.

Suppose that
]0,\infty[\ni x\mapsto V(x)
is a function in

L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}]0,\infty[
, possibly complex valued. Consider the expression

L:=-\partial_{x}^{2}+V(x),

viewed as a linear map from
AC^{1}]0,\infty[
to
L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}]0,\infty[
. Let us describe the four most obvious closed realizations
of
L
on
L^{2}]0,\infty[
.

First define

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})

\displaystyle:=\big\{f\in L^{2}]0,\infty[\,\cap\,AC^{1}]0,\infty[\quad\mid\ Lf\in L^{2}]0,\infty[\big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(L^{\min})

\displaystyle:=\text{the closure of }\{f\in\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})\ \mid\ f=0\text{ near }0\text{ and }\infty\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(L^{0})

\displaystyle:=\text{the closure of }\{f\in\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})\ \mid\ f=0\text{ near }0\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(L^{\infty})

\displaystyle:=\text{the closure of }\{f\in\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})\ \mid\ f=0\text{ near }\infty\}.

Above,

\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})
is treated as a Hilbert space with the norm given by

\|f\|_{L}^{2}:=\|Lf\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}
.
We define

L^{\max}:=L\big|_{\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})},\quad L^{\min}:=L\big|_{\mathcal{D}(L^{\min})},\quad L^{0}:=L\big|_{\mathcal{D}(L^{0})},\quad L^{\infty}:=L\big|_{\mathcal{D}(L^{\infty})}.

Then
L^{\max}
,
L^{\min}
,
L^{0}
and
L^{\infty}
are closed operators satisfying

L^{\max}\supset L^{\infty}\supset L^{\min},\quad L^{\max}\supset L^{0}\supset L^{\min}.

Let us quote some general results.
The following proposition is well-known, it is e.g. proven as Proposition 5.15 of
[
13
]
:

Proposition 6.1
.

If

(6.1)

\displaystyle\limsup_{c\to\infty}\int_{c}^{c+1}|V(x)|\mathrm{d}x<\infty,

then

(6.2)

L^{\max}=L^{\infty},\quad L^{0}=L^{\min}.

Thus, there is no need to set boundary conditions at infinity.

By
[
13
, Theorem 6.12]
, we have

Proposition 6.2
.

Suppose that (
6.2
) holds.
Then we have the following alternative:

\displaystyle\text{(i) either }\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})/\mathcal{D}(L^{\min})=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{ and }\dim\big\{f\in\mathcal{N}(L-\lambda)\ \mid\ f\text{ is square integrable near }0\big\}\leq 1\quad\text{ for all $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$ };

\displaystyle\text{(ii) or }\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})/\mathcal{D}(L^{\min})=2

\displaystyle\quad\text{ and }\dim\{f\in\mathcal{N}(L^{\max}-\lambda)\ \mid\ f\text{ is square integrable near }0\}=2\quad\text{ for all $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$}.\quad

Until the end of this subsection
we suppose that alternative (ii) of Proposition
6.2
holds.
Fix
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
\xi\in C_{\mathrm{c}}[0,\infty[
equal
1
near

0
. Then by
[
13
]
we have a direct sum decomposition

(6.3)

\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})=\mathcal{D}(L^{\min})\oplus\{\xi f\ |\ f\in\mathcal{N}(L)\},

where
\mathcal{N}(L)
denotes all functions in
AC^{1}[0,\infty[

annihilated by
L
.

We are interested in operators
L^{\bullet}
lying “in the middle” between

L^{\min}
and
L^{\max}
, that is satisfying

L^{\min}\subset L^{\bullet}\subset L^{\max},

where both inclusions are of codimension
1
.
All such operators correspond to one-dimensional
subspaces of
\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})/\mathcal{D}(L^{\min})
. To specify
such a subspace it is enough to choose

(6.4)

r\in\mathcal{D}(L^{\max}),\quad r\notin\mathcal{D}(L^{\min}),

and to define

(6.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(L^{r}):

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}(L^{\min})+\mathbb{C}r,

(6.6)

\displaystyle L^{r}:

\displaystyle=L^{\max}\big|_{\mathcal{D}(L^{r})}.

6.2.
Boundary functionals

We continue to analyze general 1d Schrödinger operators.
Until the end of this subsection we assume (
6.2
).
We will give a method to describe boundary conditions which is often
more practical than (
6.5
).

First recall the concept of Wronskian (
1.61
). If
f,g\in\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})
, then
f,g\in AC^{1}]0,\infty[\subset C^{1}]0,\infty[
,
hence their Wronskian at

x\in]0,\infty[
, denoted

\mathscr{W}(f,g;x)
, is well defined. Interestingly, the
Wronskian extends to the boundary
x=0
, as follows e.g. from
[
13
, Theorem 4.4]
:

Proposition 6.3
.

For
f,g\in\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})

\lim_{x\searrow 0}\mathscr{W}(f,g;x)=:\mathscr{W}(f,g;0)

exists and defines a continuous
bilinear form.
If in addition (
6.2
) holds, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(L^{\mathrm{min}})

\displaystyle=\Big\{f\in\mathcal{D}(L^{\mathrm{max}})\ \mid\ \mathscr{W}(f,g;0)=0\text{ for all }g\in\mathcal{D}(L^{\mathrm{max}})\Big\}.

Let us define the
boundary space

\mathcal{B}:=\big(\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})/\mathcal{D}(L^{\min})\big)^{\prime},

where the prime denotes the dual.

Let
r
be as in (
6.4
).
Let
\phi\neq 0
be a boundary functional (that
is, an element of
\mathcal{B}
) such that

\phi(r)=0
. Obviously,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(L^{r}):

\displaystyle=\{f\in\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})\mid\phi(f)=0\}

is equivalent to (
6.5
).

The boundary functional
\phi

can be simply written as

(6.7)

\phi=c\mathscr{W}(r,\cdot;0),

where
c\neq 0
.
Using (
6.3
) and (
6.7
) we obtain

Corollary 6.4
.

Suppose that the alternative (ii) of Proposition
6.2

holds. Fix
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.
Then we have a natural isomorphism of
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{N}(L-\lambda)
:

(6.8)

\mathcal{B}=\{\mathscr{W}(f,\cdot;0)\ |\ f\in\mathcal{N}(L-\lambda)\}.

We will say that a function
f\in C^{1}]0,\infty[

possesses the
Wronskian at zero on
\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})

if

\mathscr{W}(f,g;0):=\lim_{x\searrow 0}\mathscr{W}(f,g;x),\quad g\in\mathcal{D}(L^{\max}),

exists. Proposition
6.3
says that each function in

\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})
possesses the Wronskian at zero on
\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})
.

In practice, it may be difficult to make explicit an element
r
in

\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})
describing the functional
\phi
by (
6.7
).
Instead, we can often find a
simpler function
r_{1}
, not necessarily in
\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})
,
which also possesses the Wronskian at zero on
\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})
and
such that

(6.9)

\mathscr{W}(r,\cdot;0)=\mathscr{W}(r_{1},\cdot;0).

Then
instead of (
6.5
) the domain of
L^{r}
can be equivalently
characterized as :

(6.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(L^{r}):

\displaystyle=\{f\in\mathcal{D}(L^{\max})\mid\mathscr{W}(r_{1},f;0)=0\}.

6.3.
The maximal and minimal realizations of
L_{m^{2}}

As everywhere in this paper, we assume
that
]0,\infty[\ni x\mapsto Q(x)
belongs to
L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}]0,\infty[
.
For
m\in\mathbb{C}
, set

V_{m^{2}}(x):=\Big(m^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\frac{1}{x^{2}}+Q(x).

We consider the differential expression

L_{m^{2}}:=-\partial_{x}^{2}+V_{m^{2}}(x).

as a linear map on
AC^{1}]0,\infty[
.
By applying the definitions of Subsection

6.1
, we can introduce
the closed operators

L_{m^{2}}^{\max}
,
L_{m^{2}}^{\min}
such that

\big(L^{\mathrm{min}}_{m^{2}}\big)^{\#}=L^{\mathrm{max}}_{m^{2}},\qquad\big(L^{\mathrm{max}}_{m^{2}}\big)^{\#}=L^{\mathrm{min}}_{m^{2}}.

Until the end of this section we assume that
Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(\infty)}_{0}
.

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{C}
.
Suppose that

\displaystyle Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{0},

\displaystyle\text{ if }m\neq 0,\qquad Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{0,\mathrm{ln}},\text{ if }m=0.

Then the following holds:

(i)

If
1\leq|\mathrm{Re}(m)|
, then
L^{\mathrm{min}}_{m^{2}}=L^{\mathrm{max}}_{m^{2}}
.

(ii)

If
|\mathrm{Re}(m)|<1
, then
\mathcal{D}(L^{\mathrm{min}}_{m^{2}})
is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{D}(L^{\mathrm{max}}_{m^{2}})
of codimension
2
.

Proof.

Obviously,
the condition (
6.1
) holds. Therefore,
only the boundary conditions at zero matter.

We can assume that

6.4.
Closed realizations of the unperturbed Bessel operator

Let us recall the basic theory of closed realizations of
L_{m^{2}}^{0}
.
We will essentially follow
[
16
]
,
except that we will put the superscript
0
on the symbols of various operators.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{m^{2}}^{0}
denote the boundary space of
L_{m^{2}}^{0}
. Below we
give natural bases of
\mathcal{B}_{m^{2}}^{0}
:

(6.13)

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}(x^{\frac{1}{2}-m},\cdot;0),

\displaystyle\quad\mathscr{W}(x^{\frac{1}{2}+m},\cdot;0),\quad m\neq 0;

(6.14)

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}(x^{\frac{1}{2}},\cdot;0),

\displaystyle\quad\mathscr{W}(x^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{ln}(x),\cdot;0),\quad m=0.

Note that for
|\mathrm{Re}(m)|<1
,

(6.15)

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}(x^{\frac{1}{2}-m},x^{\frac{1}{2}+m})

\displaystyle=2m,

(6.16)

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}(x^{\frac{1}{2}},x^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{ln}(x))

\displaystyle=1,

which implies the linear independence of (
6.13
) and (
6.14
).

Let us describe the basic families of closed realizations of
Bessel operators. We will use two kinds of definitions of their
domains.
In what follows,
\xi
is a smooth compactly supported function equal to
1
near
x=0
.

We have the family of realizations with pure boundary conditions
defined for
\mathrm{Re}(m)>-1
:

(6.17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(H_{m}^{0}):=

\displaystyle\,\mathcal{D}(L_{m^{2}}^{0})+\mathbb{C}x^{\frac{1}{2}+m}\xi(x)

(6.18)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\Big\{f\in\mathcal{D}(L_{m^{2}}^{0,\max})\mid\,\mathscr{W}(x^{\frac{1}{2}+m},f;0)=0\Big\},

(6.19)

\displaystyle H_{m}^{0}:=

\displaystyle\,L_{m^{2}}^{0}\big|_{\mathcal{D}(H_{m}^{0})}.

We have also two families with mixed boundary conditions: The first is the generic family defined for
-1<\mathrm{Re}(m)<1
,

m\neq 0
,

\kappa\in\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
:

(6.20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(H_{m,\kappa}^{0})

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{D}(L_{m^{2}}^{0,\min})+\mathbb{C}\big(x^{\frac{1}{2}+m}+\kappa x^{\frac{1}{2}-m}\big)\xi(x)

(6.21)

\displaystyle=\Big\{f\in\mathcal{D}(L_{m^{2}}^{0,\max})\mid\mathscr{W}\big(x^{\frac{1}{2}+m}+\kappa x^{\frac{1}{2}-m},f;0\big)=0\Big\},

(6.22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(H_{m,\infty}^{0})

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{D}(H_{-m}^{0}),\quad H_{m,\kappa}^{0}:=L_{m^{2}}^{0}\big|_{\mathcal{D}(H_{m,\kappa}^{0})}.

The second family corresponds to
m=0
and depends on

\nu\in\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
:

(6.23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(H_{0}^{0,\nu})

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{D}(L_{m^{2}}^{0,\min})+\mathbb{C}\big(x^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{ln}(x)+\nu x^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)\xi(x)

(6.24)

\displaystyle=\Big\{f\in\mathcal{D}(L_{0}^{0,\max})\mid\mathscr{W}\big(\nu x^{\frac{1}{2}}+x^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{ln}(x),f;0\big)=0\Big\},

(6.25)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(H_{0}^{0,\infty})

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{D}(H_{0}^{0}),\quad H_{0}^{0,\nu}:=L_{0}^{0}\big|_{\mathcal{D}(H_{0}^{0,\nu})}.

The families of closed operators defined in (
6.19
),
(
6.22
) and (
6.25
) are clearly independent of the cutoff
\xi
. They
are holomorphic with respect to the parameters
m,\kappa,\nu
. They are
situated between
L_{m^{2}}^{0,\min}
and
L_{m^{2}}^{0,\max}
.

6.5.
Boundary functionals for perturbed Bessel operators

In this subsection, as well as in Subsections
6.6
,
6.7
and
6.8
, we
analyze boundary conditions near zero and the corresponding closed
realizations of perturbed Bessel operators. For definiteness, throughout these four
subsections we assume that

It does not seem practical to
define boundary conditions for perturbed Bessel operators
analogously as in (

), (

) and (

stronger conditions on

to

)
and (

The precise choice of a boundary functional is in general

Let us first describe some properties of the minimal domain.

Lemma 6.6

\displaystyle\quad\partial_{x}h(x)=o(x^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{ln}(x)).

Let

and

be such that

6.6.
The perturbed Bessel operator with pure boundary conditions

In this subsection we introduce the most natural family
of perturbed Bessel operators. It is parallel to what in the
unperturbed case was called the family of homogeneous
Bessel operators. (In the perturbed case the homogeneity is no longer
true, therefore the name has to be changed).

Let
m\in\mathbb{C}
,
-1<\mathrm{Re}(m)
. We assume that

\displaystyle Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{\varepsilon},\quad m\neq 0,\quad\varepsilon=\max\big(0,-2\mathrm{Re}(m)\big);

\displaystyle Q\in\mathscr{L}^{(0)}_{0,\mathrm{ln}^{2}},\quad m=0.

We can then define

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(H_{m})

\displaystyle:=\Big\{f\in\mathcal{D}(L_{m^{2}}^{\max})\mid\,\mathscr{W}(x^{\frac{1}{2}+m},f;0)=0\Big\},

\displaystyle H_{m}

\displaystyle:=L_{m^{2}}\big|_{\mathcal{D}(H_{m})},

which we will call
the
perturbed Bessel operator with pure boundary conditions
.

Using Theorem
6.8
we see that the operator

H_{m}
is
closed,

\displaystyle L^{\mathrm{min}}_{m^{2}}=H_{m}=L^{\mathrm{max}}_{m^{2}},\quad\mathrm{Re}(m)\geq 1;

\displaystyle L^{\mathrm{min}}_{m^{2}}\subset H_{m}\subset L^{\mathrm{max}}_{m^{2}},\quad-1<\mathrm{Re}(m)<1;

and both inclusions are of codimension
1
.

Proposition 6.10
.

Suppose that the assumptions on
Q
stated at the beginning of this subsection
hold.
Let
\mathrm{Re}(k)>0
. Then

if and only if

. Moreover,

Appendix A
Integral operators

In this appendix we recall a well-known property of Volterra operators that was used in Section
3
.
We start with the following easy lemma:

Lemma A.1
.

Suppose that
(x,y)\mapsto K(x,y)
is a measurable function on
]0,\infty[\times]0,\infty[
such that

\sup_{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}|K(x,y)|\mathrm{d}y=:C<\infty.

Then the operator
K
defined by

Kf(x):=\int_{0}^{\infty}K(x,y)f(y)\mathrm{d}y

is bounded on
L^{\infty}]0,\infty[
and
\|K\|\leq C
.

Given an integral operator
K
as in the previous lemma and
a>0
, the operator
K^{(a)}
is defined as an
operator on
L^{\infty}]0,a[
by the kernel

K^{(a)}(x,y):=\theta(a-x)\theta(a-y)K(x,y).

The operator
K^{(a)}
is called
the compression of
K
to
]0,a[
.

We will say that the operator
K
with the kernel
K(x,y)
is a

forward
, respectively
backward Volterra operator
if
K(x,y)=0
for
x<y
,
respectively
x>y
. The following proposition can be proven by an induction argument.

Proposition A.2
.

Suppose that
K
is a forward Volterra operator and

\int_{0}^{\infty}|K(x,y)|\mathrm{d}y=:C(x)<\infty.

Then for any
a>0
,
K^{(a)}
is bounded
and
{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}^{(a)}+K^{(a)}
is invertible on

L^{\infty}]0,a[
. Besides,
for all
x>0
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

|(K^{n}f)(x)|\leq\frac{1}{n!}C(x)^{n}\mathop{\mathrm{ess\,sup}}_{y<x}|f(y)|.

so that for
f\in L^{\infty}]0,a[
the series

({\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}+K)^{-1}f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-K)^{n}f(x)

is convergent.

Appendix B
Holomorphic families of closed operators

In this appendix we recall the concept of a holomorphic family of
operators on a complex Banach space
\mathcal{H}
.

Let
\Theta
be an open subset of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
We say that a family
\{B(z)\}_{z\in\Theta}
of bounded
operators on
\mathcal{H}
is a
holomorphic family of bounded
operators

if for any
f,g\in\mathcal{H}

(B.1)

\Theta\ni z\mapsto(f|B(z)g)

is holomorphic. Note that this is equivalent to a weaker condition:

\{B(z)\}_{z\in\Theta}
is locally bounded on
\Theta
and
there exists a dense subspace
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{H}
such
that, for all
f,g\in\mathcal{D}
, the map (
B.1
) is holomorphic.

One can also introduce another concept: that of
holomorphic families of
closed operators
. We will not give here its general definition, which
can be found e.g. in
[
26
,
7
,
18
]
and will not be
used here.
We will restrict ourselves to defining this concept for families that
have nonempty resolvent set.

More precisely,
suppose that
\{H(z)\}_{z\in\Theta}
is a function with values
in closed operators on
\mathcal{H}
.
Suppose that for any
z_{0}\in\Theta
, there exist
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and a neighborhood
\Theta_{0}\subset\Theta
of
z_{0}
such
that, for all
z\in\Theta_{0}
,
\lambda
is in the resolvent set of

H(z)
. Then we say that
\{H(z)\}_{z\in\Theta}
is holomorphic
if for all such
\Theta_{0}
the map
\Theta_{0}\ni z\mapsto(H(z)-\lambda)^{-1}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is holomorphic as a family of bounded operators.

Appendix C
Technical lemma

The following easy lemma was used several times in the main part of the manuscript.

Lemma C.1
.

Let
a>0
or
a=\infty
. Let
f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}]0,a[
and
h:]0,a[\to\mathbb{R}
be a positive increasing function such that
h(x)\to 0
as
x\to 0
and

\int_{0}^{a}h(x)|f(x)|\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

Then

\int_{x}^{a}f(y)\mathrm{d}y=o\big(h(x)^{-1}\big),\quad x\to 0.

Proof.

Let

w(x,y):=h(x)|f(y)|\mathds{1}_{[x,a[}(y).

Clearly, for a.e.
y\in]0,a[
,
w(x,y)\to 0
as
x\to 0
. Moreover, since
h
is increasing,

w(x,y)\leq h(y)|f(y)|,

for all
x,y\in]0,a[
. Since
y\mapsto h(y)|f(y)|
is integrable on
]0,a[
by assumption, the dominated convergence theorem implies that

\int_{0}^{a}w(x,y)\mathrm{d}y\to 0,\quad x\to 0.

This proves the lemma.
∎
