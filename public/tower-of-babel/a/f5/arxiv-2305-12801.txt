The topological shadow of 𝔽 1 {{\mathbb{F}}_{1}} -geometry: congruence spaces
Oliver Lorscheid, Samarpita Ray

Abstract
. In this paper we introduce congruence spaces, which are topological spaces that are canonically attached to monoid schemes and that reflect closed topological properties. This leads to satisfactory topological characterizations of closed morphisms and closed immersions as well as separated and proper morphisms. We study congruence spaces thoroughly and extend standard results from usual scheme theory to monoid schemes: a closed immersion is the same as an affine morphism for which the pullback of sections is surjective; a morphism is separated if and only if the image of the diagonal is a closed subset of the congruence space; a valuative criterion for separated and proper morphisms.

This example shows that the underlying topological space of a monoid scheme does not lead to a satisfactory notion of closed morphisms, which is a prominent concept in usual scheme theory. Several subclasses of closed morphisms allow for equivalent characterizations generalize to monoid schemes. For example:

\bullet

Closed immersions

\bullet

\bullet

There is no notion of

In this paper, we introduce a secondary topological space associated with monoid schemes, which we call the
congruence space
and which captures closed conditions for monoid schemes. This leads to a satisfactory notion of closed morphisms for monoid schemes and a topological characterization of the above mentioned classes of morphisms.

Earlier works on congruences

The idea to use congruences in the context of monoid schemes is not novel to this paper. Berkovich develops in the unpublished text
[
1
]
a scheme theory for monoids that is based on congruences, or quotients, rather than on ideals, or submodules. In fact, this text features many concepts that appear in our text.

Berkovich’s approach leads, however, to a quite different notion of
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-scheme since the space of prime congruences does not reflect the same notion of open coverings as monoid schemes (cf. section
A.2.3
). In particular, there are topological coverings in the congruence setting, which are not canonical. This leads to technical ramifications in Berkovich’s theory in contrast to monoid schemes in the sense of our text.

Deitmar develops in
[
12
]
a variant of congruence schemes for sesquiads, which are algebraic objects that generalize monoids. Also Deitmar’s theory deviates from monoid schemes in the sense that it features non-canonical open coverings.

Follow-up work

Jarra builds up on the theory of congruence spaces as developed in this text: he introduces in
[
19
]
an intrinsic subspace of the congruence space that exhibits the expected dimension of monoid schemes over algebraically closed monoids. In a second text
[
18
]
, Jarra shows that this subspace coincide with the spaces in Smirnov’s approach to the ABC-conjecture via Hurwitz formulas in
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-geometry.

Closed properties

The congruence space turns out to be a suitable space to capture closed properties of (morphisms between) monoid schemes.

We study several morphism classes in this paper and explain our results in the following. The systematic approach is to replace closed morphisms in usual scheme theory by morphisms
\myvarphi:Y\to X
of monoid schemes for which
\tilde{\myvarphi}:{\widetilde{Y}}\to{\widetilde{X}}
is closed. Note that this interpolation between the monoid scheme and its congruence space makes proofs significantly more subtle and difficult than in usual scheme theory.

Closed points

As a first insight (Proposition
2.7
), we find canonical bijections

X({{\mathbb{F}}_{1}})\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mychi_{X}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\ \big\{\text{closed points of ${\widetilde{X}}$}\big\}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tilde{\mypi}_{X}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\ X

where
X({{\mathbb{F}}_{1}})=\Hom(\MSpec{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}},\,X)
and where
\tilde{\mypi}_{X}
is the restriction of the surjection
\mypi_{X}:{\widetilde{X}}\to X
to the subset of closed points of
{\widetilde{X}}
.

Closed subschemes

A

is strongly reduced for every open subset

A

Closed immersions

Let

(1)

Separated morphisms

In

Let

Closed and proper morphisms

We say that a morphism
\myvarphi:Y\to X
of monoid schemes is

\bullet

closed
if the map
\tilde{\myvarphi}:{\widetilde{Y}}\to{\widetilde{X}}
between congruence spaces is closed;

\bullet

universally closed
if for every morphism
\mypsi:X^{\prime}\to X
the canonical projection
X^{\prime}\times_{X}Y\to X^{\prime}
is closed;

\bullet

proper
if it is of finite type, separated and universally closed.

1.1.
Pointed monoids

A
(commutative) pointed monoid
is a commutative semigroup
A
with a zero (absorbing element)
0
and an identity
1
, satisfying
a.0=0.a=0
and
a.1=1.a=a
for all
a\in A
. A
morphism of pointed monoids
is a semigroup homomorphism that preserves the zero and identity.

An
ideal
of a pointed monoid
A
is a subset
I
of
A
such that
0\in I
and
IA\subseteq A
. The
quotient monoid
is the set
A/I=(A-I)\cup\{0\}
together with the multiplication given by
a\cdot b=ab
if
a,b\in A-I
and
a\cdot b=0
otherwise. An ideal
P
of
A
is
prime
if
ab\in P
implies
a\in P
or
b\in P
or, in other words,
A/P
is without zero divisors. The complement of the set of units
A^{\times}
of a pointed monoid
A
forms an ideal and is therefore the unique maximal ideal of
A
which we denote by
M_{A}
.

Let
S
be a
multiplicative subset
of a pointed monoid
A
, i.e.
1\in S
and
ab\in S
for all
a,b\in S
. The
localization of
A
at
S
is the monoid
S^{-1}A=S\times A/\sim
where
(s,a)\sim(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})
if and only if
tsa^{\prime}=ts^{\prime}a
for some
t\in S
. We denote the equivalence class of
(s,a)
by
\tfrac{a}{s}
. The association
a\mapsto\tfrac{a}{1}
defines the
localization map

\myiota_{S}:A\to S^{-1}A
. Particular examples of localizations are
A[h^{-1}]=S_{h}^{-1}A
with
S_{h}=\{h^{i}\}_{i\geqslant 0}
for
h\in A
and
A_{P}=S_{P}^{-1}A
with
S_{P}=A-P
for a prime ideal
P
of
A
.

The category of pointed monoids is complete and cocomplete. In particular, it comes with a tensor product
A\otimes_{C}B
of any pair of morphisms
C\to A
and
C\to B
. The pointed monoid
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}=\{0,1\}
is its initial object.

Let
A
be a pointed monoid. The
free monoid in
t_{1},\dotsc,t_{n}
over
A
is the pointed monoid

A[t_{1},\dotsc,t_{n}]\ =\ \big\{at_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots t_{n}^{e_{n}}\,\big|\,e_{1},\dotsc,e_{n}\in{\mathbb{N}}\big\}

with product

(at_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots t_{n}^{e_{n}})\ \cdot\ (bt_{1}^{f_{1}}\cdots t_{n}^{f_{n}})\ =\ (ab)t_{1}^{e_{1}+f_{1}}\cdots t_{n}^{e_{n}+f_{n}}

and identification
0\cdot t_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots t_{n}^{e_{n}}=0\cdot t_{1}^{f_{1}}\cdots t_{n}^{f_{n}}
for all
e_{i},f_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

1.2.
Integral and zero divisor free monoids

An
integral monoid
is a pointed monoid
A
such that
ab=ac
implies
b=c
for all
a,b,c\in A
with
a\neq 0
. A
zero divisor free monoid
is a pointed monoid
A
for which
S=A-\{0\}
is multiplicative subset.

Let
A
be zero divisor free,
S=A-\{0\}
and
i_{S}:A\to S^{-1}A
be the localization map. The
group completion of
A
is
\Frac A=S^{-1}A
. The
integral quotient of
A
is
A^{{\overline{\textup{int}}}}=i_{S}(A)
.

Note that
\Frac A
is a pointed group and
A^{\overline{\textup{int}}}
is an integral monoid. The localization map defines canonical maps
A\to\Frac A
and
A\to A^{\overline{\textup{int}}}
. We mention the following universal properties of
\Frac A
and
A^{\overline{\textup{int}}}
without proof.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
A
be a pointed monoid without zero divisors. Then every morphism
f:A\to B
into a pointed group
B
factors uniquely through
\Frac A
and every morphism
f:A\to B
into an integral monoid

factors uniquely through
A^{{\overline{\textup{int}}}}
.

1.3.
Monoid schemes

Let
A
be a pointed monoid. The
prime spectrum of
A
is the set

\MSpec(A)\ =\ \{P\subseteq A\mid P\text{\penalty\ is a prime ideal of }A\}

that carries the topology generated by the open subsets
U_{h}=\{P\mid h\notin P\}
for
h\in A
, together with a sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
of monoids on
X=\MSpec(A)
that is characterized by its values
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(U_{h})=A[h^{-1}]
and its stalks
{\mathcal{O}}_{X,P}=A_{P}
.

A
monoid scheme
is a topological space
X
together with a sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
of monoids that is locally isomorphic to spectra of pointed monoids. A monoid scheme is
affine
if it isomorphic the spectrum of a pointed monoid.

A
morphism of monoid schemes
X
and
Y
is a continuous map
\myvarphi:X\to Y
together with a morphism of sheaves
\myvarphi^{\#}:{\mathcal{O}}_{X}\to\myvarphi_{\ast}{\mathcal{O}}_{Y}
of pointed monoids for which the morphism of stalks
\myvarphi_{x}:{\mathcal{O}}_{X,x}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{Y,y}
are
local
for every
x\in X
and
y=\myvarphi(x)
in the sense that
\myvarphi_{x}^{-1}({\mathcal{O}}_{Y,y}^{\times})={\mathcal{O}}_{X,x}^{\times}
. We denote the category of monoid schemes by
\MSch
.

Note that
\MSpec
extends to an anti-equivalence of
\Mon
with the category of affine monoid schemes: a morphism
f:A\to B
of pointed monoids defines the morphism
f^{\ast}:\MSpec(B)\to\MSpec(A)
with
f^{\ast}(P)=f^{-1}(P)
. It has the global section functor
\Gamma:\MSch\to\Mon
, which sends a monoid scheme
X
to
\Gamma X={\mathcal{O}}_{X}(X)
, as an adjoint:

\Hom(X,\MSpec(A))\ =\ \Hom(A,\Gamma X).

1.4.
Base extensions

The tensor product of pointed monoids extends to a fibre product
X\times_{Z}Y
of monoid schemes, which defines the base extension
X_{B}=X\otimes_{A}B
of a monoid scheme
X
over
A
to a
A
-algebra
B
.

Given a pointed monoid
A
, we denote its
associated ring
by
A^{+}={\mathbb{Z}}[A]/\langle 0\rangle
, which is equal to
{\mathbb{Z}}[A]-\{0\}
as an abelian group. Given a monoid scheme
X
over
A
and a morphism
f:A\to k
from a pointed monoid
A
to (the underlying monoid of) a ring

, we obtain a functorial base extension
X_{k}^{+}=X\otimes_{A}k
that is characterized by the properties that it commutes with colimits of open immersions and that for affine
X=\MSpec(B)
,

X_{k}\ =\ \Spec\big(B^{+}\otimes_{A^{+}}k\big).

1.5.
Finite type morphisms

A morphism
f:A\to B
of pointed monoids is
of finite type
if there are elements
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\in B
such that every element of
B
can be expressed as a monomial in the
b_{i}
and elements of
f(A)
.

A morphism
\myvarphi:X\to Y
of monoid schemes is
of finite type
if there are affine open coverings
Y=\bigcup_{i\in I}V_{i}
and
\myvarphi^{-1}(V_{i})=\bigcup_{j\in J_{i}}U_{i,j}
for all
i\in I
such that
J_{i}
is finite for all
i\in I
and such that
\Gamma{\myvarphi_{i,j}}:\Gamma{V_{i}}\to\Gamma{U_{i,j}}
is of finite type for all
i\in I
and
j\in J_{i}
.

2.
Congruence spaces

In this section, we introduce congruence spaces for monoid schemes, which are secondary and subordinate topological spaces that translate topological property from usual scheme theory appropriately to the world of monoid schemes.

2.1.
Congruences

The algebraic backbone for congruence spaces are congruences. We review their definition and first properties. For more details, see
[
22
, Chapter 3]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
congruence
of a pointed monoid
A
is an equivalence relation
\mathfrak{c}
on
A
that is closed under multiplication by
A
, i.e.
(a,b)\in\mathfrak{c}
implies
(ac,bc)\in\mathfrak{c}
for all
a,b,c\in A
. The
nullideal of
{\mathfrak{c}}
is

I_{\mathfrak{c}}=\{a\in A\mid(a,0)\in\mathfrak{c}\}.

The
quotient of
A
by a congruence
{\mathfrak{c}}
is the set
A/{\mathfrak{c}}
of equivalence classes with multiplication given by
[a]\cdot[b]=[ab]
.

Note that the product of elements of
A/{\mathfrak{c}}
is well-defined on representatives and turns
A/{\mathfrak{c}}
into a pointed monoid with zero
[0]
and identity
[1]
. The quotient map
\mypi:A\to A/{\mathfrak{c}}
is a pointed monoid morphism.

Every subset
S
of
A\times A
is contained in a smallest congruence

\langle S\rangle\ =\ \bigcap_{S\subset{\mathfrak{c}}}\ {\mathfrak{c}}

that contains
S
, which we call the
congruence generated by
S
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
f:A\to B
be a morphism of pointed monoids. The
congruence kernel of
f
is

\congker(f):=\{(a,a^{\prime})\in A\times A\mid f(a)=f(a^{\prime})\}.

The congruence kernel of a morphism is indeed a congruence and that every congruence
{\mathfrak{c}}
of
A
is the congruence kernel of the quotient map
A\to A/{\mathfrak{c}}
. This establishes a bijective correspondence between the quotients of
A
and the congruences on
A
.

Definition 2.3
.

A
prime congruence
of
A
is a congruence
\mathfrak{c}
such that
A/\mathfrak{c}
is integral, i.e.,
(ab,ac)\in\mathfrak{c}
implies
(a,0)\in\mathfrak{c}
or
(b,c)\in\mathfrak{c}
for all
a,b,c\in A
.

If
\mathfrak{c}
is a prime congruence, then
I_{\mathfrak{c}}
is a prime ideal. The congruence kernel
\congker(f)
of a morphism
f:A\to B
into an integral monoid
B
is a prime congruence.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
f:A\to B
be a morphism of pointed monoids,
{\mathfrak{c}}
a congruence on
A
and
{\mathfrak{d}}
a congruence on
B
. The
push-forward of
{\mathfrak{c}}
along
f
is the congruence

f_{\ast}({\mathfrak{c}})\ =\ \langle(f(a),f(b))\in B\times B\mid(a,b)\in{\mathfrak{c}}\}\rangle

of
B
and the
pullback of
{\mathfrak{d}}
along
f
is the congruence

f^{\ast}({\mathfrak{d}})\ =\ \{(a,b)\in A\times A\mid(f(a),f(b))\in{\mathfrak{d}}\}

on
A
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
A
be a pointed monoid,
{\mathfrak{c}}
a congruence on
A
and
\mypi:A\to A/{\mathfrak{c}}
the quotient map. Then the pushforward and pullback define mutually inverse bijections

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\big\{\text{congruences on $A$ that contain ${\mathfrak{c}}$}\big\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\big\{\text{congruences on $A/{\mathfrak{c}}$}\big\}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mypi_{\ast}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mypi^{\ast}}

that restrict to bijections between the respective subsets of prime congruences.

Proof.

This result follows immediately from the identification of congruences with quotients of

and the fact that the quotients of

correspond to the quotients of

that factor through

. The additional claim about prime congruences follows from the characterization of prime congruences

in terms of the quotient

, which agrees on both sides of the bijection.
∎

2.2.
Compatibility with localizations

Definition 2.6
.

Let
A
be a pointed monoid,
S
a multiplicative subset and
\mathfrak{c}
a congruence of
A
. The
localization of
\mathfrak{c}
at
S
is

S^{-1}\mathfrak{c}\ =\ \big\{(\tfrac{a}{s},\tfrac{a^{\prime}}{s^{\prime}})\in S^{-1}A\times S^{-1}A\penalty\ \big|\penalty\ (ts^{\prime}a,tsa^{\prime})\in\mathfrak{c}\text{\penalty\ for some\penalty\ }t\in S\big\}.

Note that
S^{-1}\mathfrak{c}
is a congruence of
S^{-1}A
. Localizations of monoids are exact in the following sense.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
f:A\to B
be a morphism of pointed monoids. Let
S
be a multiplicative subset of
A
and
f_{S}:S^{-1}A\to f(S)^{-1}B
the induced morphism. Then
\ker(f_{S})=S^{-1}\ker(f)
and
\congker(f_{S})=S^{-1}\congker(f)
. In particular,
f_{S}
is injective if
f
is so.

Proof.

An element
\frac{a}{s}\in S^{-1}A
is in the kernel of
f_{S}
if and only if
\frac{f(a)}{f(s)}=\frac{0}{1}
, which means that
f(t)f(1)f(a)=f(t)f(s)f(0)=0
for some
t\in S
. Equivalently
ta\in\ker(f)
or, in other words,
\frac{a}{s}=\frac{at}{st}\in S^{-1}(\ker(f))
. This shows
\ker(f_{S})=S^{-1}(\ker(f))
.

A pair
(\frac{a}{s},\frac{a^{\prime}}{s^{\prime}})
is in the congruence kernel of
f_{S}
if and only if
f_{S}(\frac{a}{s})=f_{S}(\frac{a^{\prime}}{s^{\prime}})
, which means that
f(tsa^{\prime})=f(t)f(s)f(a^{\prime})=f(t)f(s^{\prime})f(a)=f(ts^{\prime}a)
for some
t\in S
. Equivalently
(tsa^{\prime},ts^{\prime}a)\in\congker(f)
or, in other words,
\frac{a}{s}=\frac{at}{st}\in S^{-1}(\ker(f))
. This shows
\congker(f_{S})=S^{-1}(\congker(f))
.

If
f
is injective, then
\congker(f)
is the trivial congruence
{\mathfrak{c}}_{\textup{triv}}=\{(a,a)\in\mid a\in A\}
. Thus
\congker(f_{S})=S^{-1}{\mathfrak{c}}_{\textup{triv}}
is trivial and consequently
f_{S}
is injective.
∎

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\myiota_{S}:A\to S^{-1}A
be a localization of the pointed monoid
A
at
S
. Then the maps

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\big\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{prime congruences on }S^{-1}A\end{array}\big\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Big\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{prime congruences ${\mathfrak{c}}$}\\
\text{on $A$ with $I_{\mathfrak{c}}\cap S=\emptyset$}\end{array}\Big\}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\myiota_{S}^{\ast}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\myiota_{S,\ast}}

are mutually inverse bijections.

Proof.

We first check that the maps are well defined. Let
{\mathfrak{d}}
be a prime congruence on
S^{-1}A
. Then
(ac,bc)\in\myiota_{S}^{\ast}({\mathfrak{d}})=\{(a,a^{\prime})\in A\times A\penalty\ |\penalty\ (\frac{a}{1},\frac{a^{\prime}}{1})\in{\mathfrak{d}}\}
implies
(\frac{ac}{1},\frac{bc}{1})\in{\mathfrak{d}}
which implies
(\frac{c}{1},\frac{0}{1})\in{\mathfrak{d}}
or
(\frac{ac}{1},\frac{bc}{1})\in{\mathfrak{d}}
. Thus we have
(c,0)\in\myiota_{S}^{\ast}({\mathfrak{d}})
or
(a,b)\in\myiota_{S}^{\ast}({\mathfrak{d}})
which shows that
\myiota_{S}^{\ast}({\mathfrak{d}})
is a prime congruence on
A
. Also, if
(\frac{a}{1},\frac{0}{1})\in{\mathfrak{d}}
then clearly
\frac{a}{1}\notin(S^{-1}A)^{\times}
which implies
a\notin S
. Thus
I_{\myiota_{S}^{\ast}({\mathfrak{d}})}\cap S=\emptyset
and this shows that
\myiota_{S}^{\ast}
is well defined.

Let
{\mathfrak{c}}
be a prime congruence on
A
such that
I_{\mathfrak{c}}\cap S=\emptyset
. Consider
(\frac{a}{s}\frac{c}{r},\frac{b}{t}\frac{c}{r})\in\myiota_{S,\ast}({\mathfrak{c}})=\langle\{(\frac{a}{1},\frac{b}{1})\in S^{-1}A\times S^{-1}A\penalty\ |\penalty\ (a,b)\in{\mathfrak{c}}\}\rangle
. Since
\frac{a}{s}\frac{c}{r}=\frac{act}{srt}
and
\frac{b}{t}\frac{c}{r}=\frac{bcs}{trs}
in
S^{-1}A
, we have
(\frac{act}{srt},\frac{bcs}{trs})\in\myiota_{S,\ast}({\mathfrak{c}})
. Therefore
(\frac{act}{1},\frac{bcs}{1})\in\myiota_{S,\ast}({\mathfrak{c}})
which implies
(actr^{\prime},bcsr^{\prime})\in{\mathfrak{c}}
for some
r^{\prime}\in S
. Thus either
(cr^{\prime},0)\in{\mathfrak{c}}
or
(at,bs)\in{\mathfrak{c}}
. This implies either
(\frac{cr^{\prime}}{1},\frac{0}{1})\in\myiota_{S,\ast}({\mathfrak{c}})
or
(\frac{at}{1},\frac{bs}{1})\in\myiota_{S,\ast}({\mathfrak{c}})
. Since
(\frac{cr^{\prime}}{1},\frac{0}{1})\in\myiota_{S,\ast}({\mathfrak{c}})
implies
(\frac{cr^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}},\frac{0r^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}})=(\frac{c}{1},\frac{0}{1})\in\myiota_{S,\ast}({\mathfrak{c}})
and
(\frac{at}{1},\frac{bs}{1})\in\myiota_{S,\ast}({\mathfrak{c}})
implies
(\frac{at}{ts},\frac{bs}{ts})=(\frac{a}{s},\frac{b}{t})\in\myiota_{S,\ast}({\mathfrak{c}})
it follows that
\myiota_{S,\ast}({\mathfrak{c}})
is a prime congruence on
S^{-1}A
. Therefore
\myiota_{S,\ast}
is well defined.

Clearly, we have
{\mathfrak{c}}\subseteq\myiota_{S}^{*}(\myiota_{S,\ast}({\mathfrak{c}}))
and
{\mathfrak{d}}\subseteq\myiota_{S,\ast}(\myiota_{S}^{\ast}({\mathfrak{d}}))
. Consider any
(a,a^{\prime})\in\myiota_{S}^{*}(\myiota_{S,\ast}({\mathfrak{c}}))
. Then
(\frac{a}{1},\frac{a^{\prime}}{1})\in\myiota_{S,\ast}({\mathfrak{c}})
which implies
(sa,sa^{\prime})\in{\mathfrak{c}}
for some
s\in S
. Since
I_{\mathfrak{c}}\cap S=\emptyset
, we have
(s,0)\notin{\mathfrak{c}}
and therefore we must have
(a,a^{\prime})\in{\mathfrak{c}}
as desired. Consider any
(\frac{a}{s},\frac{b}{t})\in\myiota_{S,\ast}(\myiota_{S}^{\ast}({\mathfrak{d}}))
. Then
(\frac{ast}{s},\frac{bst}{t})=(\frac{at}{1},\frac{bs}{1})\in\myiota_{S,\ast}(\myiota_{S}^{\ast}({\mathfrak{d}}))
. This implies
(atr,bsr)\in\myiota_{S}^{\ast}({\mathfrak{d}})
for some
r\in S
. But
(r,0)\notin\myiota_{S}^{\ast}({\mathfrak{d}})
since
I_{\myiota_{S}^{\ast}({\mathfrak{d}})}\cap S=\emptyset
. Therefore
(at,bs)\in\myiota_{S}^{\ast}({\mathfrak{d}})
which implies
(\frac{at}{1},\frac{bs}{1})\in{\mathfrak{d}}
or equivalently,
(\frac{a}{s},\frac{b}{t})\in{\mathfrak{d}}
. This shows that
\myiota_{S}^{\ast}
and
\myiota_{S,\ast}
are mutually inverse bijections.
∎

2.3.
The congruence space of a pointed monoid

Definition 2.9
.

Let
A
be a pointed monoid. The
congruence space of
A
is the topological space
\Cong(A)
of all prime congruences on
A
whose topology is generated by the open subsets

U_{a,b}\ =\ \{{\mathfrak{p}}\in\Cong(A)\mid(a,b)\notin{\mathfrak{p}}\}

where
a
and
b
vary through all pairs of elements of
A
. We denote the complement
U_{a,b}
by
V_{a,b}=\{{\mathfrak{p}}\in\Cong(A)\mid(a,b)\in{\mathfrak{p}}\}
.

Example 2.10
.

The collection
\{U_{a,b}\mid a,b\in A\}
of open subsets is in general not a basis for the topology on
\Cong(A)
, as in the example of the pointed monoid
A=\mathbb{F}_{1}[t]
. We have

\Cong(A)\ =\ \big\{\mathfrak{c}_{\textup{triv}},\ \langle(t,0)\rangle,\ \langle(t,t^{k})\rangle\mid k\geqslant 0\big\}.

The topology of
\Cong(A)
is generated by the open subsets of the forms
U_{t^{n},0}
and
U_{t^{n},t^{m}}
for
n>m\geqslant 0
. The intersection
U_{t,0}\cap U_{t,1}
contains the prime congruence
\langle t,t^{2}\rangle
, but it does not contain any non-empty open subset of the form
U_{a,b}
. This shows that the family
\{U_{a,b}\mid a,b\in A\}
does not form a basis for the topology of
\Cong(A)
, but only a subbasis.

The following result upgrades the congruence space to a functor
\Cong:\Mon\to\Top
.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
f:A\to B
be a morphism of pointed monoids and
{\mathfrak{q}}
a prime congruence of
B
. Then
f^{\ast}({\mathfrak{q}})
is a prime congruence of
A
, and the map
f^{\ast}:\Cong(B)\to\Cong(A)
is continuous.

Proof.

We begin with the verification that
{\mathfrak{p}}=f^{\ast}({\mathfrak{q}})
is prime. Consider
(ca,cb)\in{\mathfrak{p}}
, i.e.
f(c)f(a)=f(ca)\sim f(cb)=f(c)f(b)
in
{\mathfrak{q}}
. Since
{\mathfrak{q}}
is prime, this means that either
f(c)\sim 0
or
f(a)\sim f(b)
in
{\mathfrak{q}}
. Thus
c\sim 0
or
a\sim b
in
{\mathfrak{p}}
, which shows that
{\mathfrak{p}}
is prime.

It suffices to verify the continuity of
f^{\ast}
on a subbasis. For
U_{a_{1},a_{2}}\subseteq\Cong(A)
, we have

\displaystyle(f^{\ast})^{-1}(U_{a_{1},a_{2}})\

\displaystyle=\ \{\mathfrak{d}\in\Cong(B)\mid(a_{1},a_{2})\notin f^{\ast}(\mathfrak{d})\}

\displaystyle=\ \{\mathfrak{d}\in\Cong(B)\mid(f(a_{1}),f(a_{2}))\notin\mathfrak{d})\}=\ U_{f(a_{1}),f(a_{2})}

which shows
f^{\ast}
is continuous.
∎

2.4.
The congruence space of a monoid scheme

The results from section
2.2
allow us to extend the definition of the congruence space from pointed monoids, or affine monoid schemes, to arbitrary monoid schemes.

Definition 2.12
.

Let
X
be a monoid scheme and
\mathcal{U}_{X}
the category of all affine open subschemes of
X
together with all open immersions. Let
{\mathcal{U}}^{\textup{cong}}_{X}

\myiota_{U}:\Cong(\Gamma U)\to X^{\textup{cong}}

of

.

Note that since every affine open covering of an affine monoid scheme

must contain

itself as its largest open subset, the canonical inclusion
\myiota_{\MSpec(A)}:\Cong(A)\to(\MSpec(A))^{\textup{cong}}

Recall that a morphism

of monoid schemes is an

if it is an open topological embedding and if
\mathcal{O}_{Y}
is the restriction of
\mathcal{O}_{X}
to

.

Proposition 2.13
.

The assignment

extends uniquely to a functor

such that for every morphism

of pointed monoids and

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Cong(B)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Cong(A)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\MSpec(B))^{\textup{cong}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\MSpec(A))^{\textup{cong}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\myvarphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\myiota_{\MSpec(B)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cong}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\myiota_{\MSpec(A)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cong}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\myvarphi^{\textup{cong}}}

is an open immersion, then

, then

.

Proof.

The functor

is defined on objects, and the commutativity requirement determines

for morphisms

of affine monoid schemes. If we can extend

to a functor on all monoid schemes, then uniqueness follows from the functoriality of

and the local nature of morphisms; namely, given

, there are affine open coverings

of

and

, and

to affine monoid schemes.

We are left with the construction of

for an arbitrary morphism

. As a first step, we establish property (
1
) for an open immersion

between affine monoid schemes

and

. Since

is an open immersion, it follows from

, Lemma 1.3]
and

, Lemma 2.4]
that
B\Cong(A)[s^{-1}]
for some

. Next we verify that

is injective. Let

be such that

, i.e.,

of the form

Y^{\textup{cong}}
. We leave it to the reader to verify that
{\myvarphi}^{\textup{cong}}
is also independent of the choice of affine covers considered in its definition by taking suitable common refinements and using similar arguments as above. This shows that
\myvarphi^{\textup{cong}}:Y^{\textup{cong}}\to X^{\textup{cong}}
is well defined as a map. It is clear that
\myvarphi^{\textup{cong}}
is continuous since each
\myvarphi_{\myalpha}^{\textup{cong}}
is continuous by Lemma
2.3
.

The only thing left to show is property (
1
) for general
X
and
Y
. We note that an open immersion
\myiota:Y\to X
is a morphism of monoid schemes which decomposes uniquely into an isomorphism of monoid schemes and the identity inclusion of an open subscheme. We have already shown that for any open subscheme
U
of
X
, we have
U^{\textup{cong}}
is an open subspace of
X^{\textup{cong}}
and we leave it to the reader to verify that any isomorphism of monoid schemes induces a homeomorphism of congruence spaces. It follows that
\myiota^{\textup{cong}}:Y^{\textup{cong}}\to X^{\textup{cong}}
is an open topological embedding.
∎

Notation
.

Given a morphism
\myvarphi:Y\to X
of monoid schemes, we often write
\widetilde{\myvarphi}:\widetilde{Y}\to\widetilde{X}
for
\myvarphi^{\textup{cong}}:Y^{\textup{cong}}\to X^{\textup{cong}}
.

2.5.
Projection onto the monoid scheme

The congruence space
X^{\textup{cong}}
of a monoid scheme
X
comes equipped with a continuous surjection
\mypi_{X}:X^{\textup{cong}}\to X
, which sends a prime congruence
{\mathfrak{p}}
to its nullideal
I_{\mathfrak{p}}
in the affine case. More precisely,
\mypi_{X}
is characterized by the following properties.

Proposition 2.14
.

There are continuous maps
\mypi_{X}:X^{\textup{cong}}\to X
, one for every monoid scheme
X
, which are uniquely determined by the following properties:

(1)

If
X=\MSpec(A)
is affine, then
\mypi_{X}({\mathfrak{p}})=I_{\mathfrak{p}}
for all prime congruences

.

(2)

The maps

are functorial in

, i.e.,

and let

2.6.
Stalks and residue fields

The points of a monoid scheme
X
come with the notion of a stalk and a residue field, which are as follows: let
{\mathcal{U}}_{x}
be the system of all affine open neighbourhood of the point
x
in
X
. The stalk at
x
is the pointed monoid

{\mathcal{O}}_{X,x}\ =\ \underset{U\in{\mathcal{U}}_{x}}{\colim}\ \Gamma U

and the residue field at
x
is the pointed group

k(x)\ =\ {\mathcal{O}}_{X,x}\,/\,M_{x}

where
M_{x}
is the maximal ideal of
{\mathcal{O}}_{X,x}
. Stalks and residue fields come with canonical morphisms

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\myomega_{x}:\MSpec({\mathcal{O}}_{X,x})\ \longrightarrow\ X\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mykappa_{x}:\MSpec(k(x))\ \longrightarrow\ X}

that commute with the morphism
\MSpec(k(x))\to\MSpec({\mathcal{O}}_{X,x})
that is induced by the quotient map
{\mathcal{O}}_{X,x}\to k(x)
.

Both stalks and residue fields generalize to invariants for points of the congruence space of
X
as follows.

Definition 2.16
.

Let
X
be a monoid scheme with congruence space
{\widetilde{X}}
and projection
\mypi_{X}:{\widetilde{X}}\to X
. Let
\tilde{x}\in{\widetilde{X}}
and
x=\mypi_{X}(\tilde{x})
. The
stalk at
\tilde{x}
is the pointed monoid

{\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}},\tilde{x}}\ =\ \underset{U\in{\mathcal{U}}_{x}}{\colim}\ \Gamma U\ =\ {\mathcal{O}}_{X,x}.

where the colimit is a filtered colimit with respect to the restriction maps
\res_{U,V}:\Gamma U\to\Gamma V
if
V\subseteq U
.

For
U\in{\mathcal{U}}_{x}
, let
i_{U}:\Gamma U\to{\mathcal{O}}_{X,x}={\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}},\tilde{x}}
the canonical map into the colimit. Let
{\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x},U}
be the prime congruence of
\Gamma U
that corresponds to
\tilde{x}
and define the congruence

{\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x}}\ =\ \big\langle\bigcup_{U\in{\mathcal{U}}_{x}}i_{U,\ast}({\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x},U})\big\rangle

of
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}},\tilde{x}}
. The
residue field at
\tilde{x}
is the pointed group

k(\tilde{x})\ =\ {\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}},\tilde{x}}\,/\,{\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x}}.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
X
be a monoid scheme with congruence space
{\widetilde{X}}
. Let
\tilde{x}\in{\widetilde{X}}
and
x=\mypi_{X}(\tilde{x})
. Then
{\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x}}
is a prime congruence of
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}},\tilde{x}}
and
k(\tilde{x})
is a pointed group. More precisely,
{\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x}}=i_{U,\ast}({\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x},U})
for any
U\in{\mathcal{U}}_{x}
and
k(\tilde{x})
is isomorphic to
\Frac(\Gamma U/{\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x},U})
.

Proof.

For any
U,V\in\mathcal{U}_{x}
, we want to show that
i_{U,\ast}({\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x},U})=i_{V,\ast}({\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x},V})
. In fact, it is enough to check this for any basic affine open
V
of
U
. Since we have
i_{V}\circ\res_{U,V}=i_{U}
it follows that
i_{V,\ast}({\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x},V})=i_{U,\ast}({\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x},U})
. Thus
{\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x}}=i_{U,\ast}({\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x},U})
. Also, we have
k(\tilde{x})=\ {\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}},\tilde{x}}\,/\,{\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x}}=\ {\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{U}},\tilde{x}}\,/\,i_{U,\ast}({\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x},U})=S^{-1}A\,/\,S^{-1}({\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x},U})
for any
U=\MSpec(A)\in\mathcal{U}_{x}
and
S=A-\mypi_{U}({\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x},U})=\{a\in A\penalty\ |\penalty\ (a,0)\notin{\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x},U}\}
. Let
\myvarphi:A\to A/{\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x},U}
denote the projection map. Since localizations of monoids are exact (in the sense of Lemma
2.2
), we have
S^{-1}A\,/\,S^{-1}({\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x},U})=\myvarphi(S)^{-1}(A\,/\,{\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x},U})=\Frac(A\,/\,{\mathfrak{p}}_{\tilde{x},U})
.
∎

Note that a morphism
\myvarphi:X\to Y
of monoid schemes induces morphisms

{{\tilde{\myvarphi}}}_{\mathfrak{p}}:\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y},\mathfrak{q}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{X},\mathfrak{p}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad k(\mathfrak{q})\longrightarrow k(\mathfrak{p})

between stalks and residue fields for every
\mathfrak{p}\in\widetilde{X}
and
{\tilde{\myvarphi}}(\mathfrak{p})=\mathfrak{q}\in\widetilde{Y}
. Note further that the morphism
k(\mathfrak{q})\to k(\mathfrak{p})
is injective in contrast to the induced maps between residue fields associated with prime ideals.

2.7.
Fibres over the monoid scheme

Let

be a monoid scheme with congruence space
{\widetilde{X}}
and projection
\mypi_{X}:{\widetilde{X}}\to X
. In this section, we study the fibres

Proposition 2.18
.

Let

and

Proof.

Since

Proposition 2.20
.

Let
X
be a monoid scheme with congruence space
{\widetilde{X}}
.

(1)

The image of
\mychi_{X}:X({{\mathbb{F}}_{1}})\to{\widetilde{X}}
is the set of closed points of
{\widetilde{X}}
.

(2)

The composition
\mypi_{X}\circ\mychi_{X}:X({{\mathbb{F}}_{1}})\to X
is a bijection and
\mytau_{X}
is the composition of its inverse bijection with
\mychi_{X}
. In particular,
\mytau_{X}
is functorial in
X
and gives a bijection between
X
and the set of closed points of
{\widetilde{X}}
.

Proof.

All assertions are local in nature, so we can assume that
X=\MSpec(A)
is affine.

We begin with (
1
). Consider a congruence
\mathfrak{c}
in the image of
\mychi_{A}:\Hom(A,\mathbb{F}_{1})\to\Cong(A)
, i.e.
\mathfrak{c}=\congker(f)
for some

. Assume that
\mathfrak{c}
is not closed in
{\widetilde{X}}
. Then there exists a prime congruence
\mathfrak{c^{\prime}}
that properly contains
\mathfrak{c}
. Thus there is an
(a_{1},a_{2})\in\mathfrak{c^{\prime}}
with
f(a_{1})\neq f(a_{2})
. Without loss of generality, let
f(a_{1})=0
and
f(a_{2})=1
, i.e.
(0,a_{1}),(a_{2},1)\in\mathfrak{c}\subseteq\mathfrak{c^{\prime}}
. By transitivity,
(0,1)\in\mathfrak{c^{\prime}}
which implies
\mathfrak{c^{\prime}}=A\times A
which yields the desired contradiction. Hence
\mathfrak{c}
is a closed point in
\Cong(A)
.

Conversely, let
\mathfrak{c}
be any closed point in
\Cong(A)
. It follows from the maximality of
\mathfrak{c}
that
A/\mathfrak{c}\cong\mathbb{F}_{1}
and thus
\mathfrak{c}=\congker(A\to A/\mathfrak{c}\cong\mathbb{F}_{1})
. This proves (
1
).

We continue with (

. Since

2

. Thus

is injective. For

. Then
\mypi_{X}\circ\mychi_{X}(f_{P})=P
, which shows that
\mypi_{X}\circ\mychi_{X}
is surjective. This shows that
\mypi_{X}\circ\mychi_{X}:X({{\mathbb{F}}_{1}})\to X
is a bijection.

Next we show that
\mytau_{X}=\mychi_{X}\circ(\mypi_{X}\circ\mychi_{X})^{-1}
. Let

and

. Then
\mytau_{X}\circ\mypi_{X}\circ\mychi_{X}(f)=\mytau_{X}(P)
. Let
\myvarphi:A\to A_{P}\to A_{P}/PA_{P}\cong k(P)
be the natural map and let

to

Remark 2.21

The natural maps

The concept of fine topologies was introduced in

]
. We recall the definition tailored to monoid schemes.

a monoid scheme. The

is the finest topology such that for every morphism

from an affine monoid scheme

into

is continuous where

carries the compact open topology with respect to the discrete topology for

The initial topology for

that is induced by the discrete topology for

The morphism

is not.

3.
Strongly reduced monoid schemes

In this section, we develop the theory of (strong) reductions of monoids and monoid schemes. Namely, as a consequence of the discrepancy between ideals and congruences, we obtain to distinct concepts of reduction of a monoid: the (weak) reduction quotients out the intersection of all prime ideals, the strong reduction quotients out the intersection of all prime congruences. These concepts carry over to the monoid schemes.

3.1.
Radicals

Definition 3.1
.

Let
A
be a pointed monoid and
I
an ideal of
A
. The
radical of
I
is the ideal

\sqrt{I}\ =\ \{a\in A\mid a^{n}\in I\text{ for some }n\geqslant 0\}.

A
radical ideal of
A
is an ideal
I
of
A
with
\sqrt{I}=I
. The
nilradical of
A
is the radical ideal
\Nil(A)=\sqrt{\{0\}}
.

Let

be a congruence on

. The
radical of
{\mathfrak{c}}
is the congruence

\sqrt{{\mathfrak{c}}}\ =\ \big\{(a,b)\in A\times A\,\big|\,(aa^{n},ba^{n})\in{\mathfrak{c}}\text{ and }(ab^{n},bb^{n})\in{\mathfrak{c}}\text{ for some }n\geqslant 0\big\}.

A
radical congruence
is a congruence
{\mathfrak{c}}
with
\sqrt{{\mathfrak{c}}}={\mathfrak{c}}
. The
strong nilradical of

is the radical
\nil(A)=\sqrt{{\mathfrak{c}}_{\textup{triv}}}
of the trivial congruence
{\mathfrak{c}}_{\textup{triv}}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let

Proof.

As a first step, we prove the claims for

and

Conversely consider

Next we consider

. This proves that

Consider any

for some integer

We turn to the general case and consider an arbitrary ideal

of

\Nil(A/I)=\bigcap_{P\in\MSpec(A/I)}\penalty\ P
. Since the prime ideals of

To conclude the proof, note that

Definition 3.3
.

Let
A
be a pointed monoid. The
reduction of

is

. The
strong reduction of

is

. We say that

is

if

and that

is

if

Lemma 3.4
.

Let

be a morphism of pointed monoids. If

is reduced, then
f
factors uniquely through
A\to A^{\textup{red}}
. If
B
is strongly reduced, then

factors uniquely through

. This defines reflections

onto the respective subcategories of reduced and strongly reduced pointed monoids.

Proof.

Let

be a morphism of pointed monoids. If
a^{n}=0
for some

and

, then
f(a)^{n}=0
in
B
. Thus if

is reduced, then

and

factors (necessarily uniquely) through

. If

and

for some

and

is strictly reduced, then

in

and

∎

Remark 3.5

The difference between the nilradical and the strong nilradical extends the difference of zero divisor free and integral monoids in the following sense. If

4.
Closed immersions and vanishing sets

In this section, we investigate the supports of closed subschemes of a monoid scheme

{\widetilde{X}}

{\widetilde{X}}
. However, not every closed subset of
{\widetilde{X}}
is the support of a closed subscheme. The aim of this section is to characterize supports of closed subschemes in terms of the vanishing sets of quasi-coherent congruence sheaves. This study leads eventually to a topological characterization of closed immersions.

4.1.
Closed subschemes

Closed immersions of monoid schemes were introduced in
[
7

. We recall the definition.

Definition 4.1

A morphism
\myvarphi:Y\to X
of monoid schemes is

if the inverse image
\myvarphi^{-1}(U)
of every affine open subset

of

is affine. A

is an affine morphism

for which the pullback
\Gamma U\to\Gamma V
of sections is surjective for every affine open

of

and

represented by a closed immersion

is the image of
{\widetilde{\myvarphi}}
in
{\widetilde{X}}

Note that isomorphic closed immersions have the same image in
{\widetilde{X}}

has a canonical representative

of

together with a quotient sheaf of

appears as a subset of

and is thus equal to the support of the closed subscheme

The relevance of congruence spaces is that they have enough closed subsets to include the supports of closed subschemes. This is made precise in the following result.

The claim of the proposition is local in

since for any affine open

of

\mypi_{X}(V_{\mathfrak{C}})\cap U\ =\ \mypi_{X}(V_{\mathfrak{C}}\cap\widetilde{U})\ =\ \mypi_{X}(V_{\mathfrak{C}(U)})\\
=\ \mypi_{X}(\Cong(A/\mathfrak{C}(U)))\ =\ \MSpec(A/\mathfrak{C}(U)).

In addition,

is the quasi-coherent sheaf of monoids

associated with the

where

is viewed as an

-module. Since an affine cover of

is given by

. Therefore

is also well defined.

We turn to the proof that
\Phi
and
\Psi
are mutually inverse bijections. Let
\mathfrak{C}
be a quasi-coherent congruence sheaf on

. For any affine open

of

\Phi(\Psi(\mathfrak{C}))|_{U}=\Congker(\myvarphi)|_{U}
where

denotes the closed subscheme associated with

. Since

\Congker(\myvarphi)|_{U}\ =\ {\congker(\myvarphi^{\#}(U))}^{\sim}\ =\ {\mathfrak{C}(U)}^{\sim}\ =\ \mathfrak{C}|_{U},

it follows that

.

Conversely, let

. Therefore

∎

.

The results of this section can be summarized in the following diagram:

The upper horizontal arrow identifies a vanishing set with the unique strongly reduced closed subscheme supported on it and the lower horizontal arrow stands for the bijection from Theorem

The downwards arrow on the left hand side sends a vanishing set to the corresponding reduced quasi-coherent congruence sheaf, the upwards arrow sends a quasi-coherent congruence sheaf to its vanishing set. The downwards arrow on the right hand side is the natural inclusion, the upwards arrow sends a closed subscheme to its strong reduction.

All of these arrows are functorial in, respectively, inclusions of subspaces, surjections of sheaves and closed immersions of closed subschemes. The horizontal arrows commute with the downwards arrows and with the upwards arrows.

4.5.
Quasi-compact morphisms

Definition 4.15
.

A morphism
\myvarphi:Y\to X
of monoid schemes is
quasi-compact
if
\myvarphi^{-1}(U)
is quasi-compact for all affine open subsets
U
of
X
.

Note that an affine monoid scheme

is always quasi-compact since it has a unique closed point. Therefore affine morphisms are quasi-compact. Since finite unions of affine opens are also quasi-compact, morphisms of finite type are quasi-compact. In general, quasi-compactness of morphisms can be verified on a chosen open affine covering
{\mathcal{U}}
of
X
. That is,
\myvarphi:Y\to X
is quasi-compact if
\myvarphi_{i}^{-1}(U)
is quasi-compact for all
U\in{\mathcal{U}}
.

Vanishing closure

Lemma 4.18
.

Let
\myvarphi:Y\to X
be a quasi-compact morphism of monoid schemes and
\tilde{\myvarphi}:{\widetilde{Y}}\to{\widetilde{X}}
the associated map of congruence spaces. Then

V_{\Congker(\myvarphi)}\ =\ \textup{im}(\tilde{\myvarphi})^{\textup{vcl}}.

Proof.

Since
\myvarphi
is quasi-compact, the congruence kernel
\Congker(\myvarphi)
defines a quasi-coherent congruence sheaf by

, which means that it defines a closed subscheme
Z
of

with vanishing set

.

is, by definition of the congruence kernel and its quasi-coherence, the smallest closed subscheme of

such that

contains the image of

. Since every vanishing set is the support of the congruence space of some closed subscheme of

, this establishes the claim of the lemma.
∎

4.7.
A topological characterization of closed immersions

Theorem 4.19
.

Let
\myvarphi:Y\to X
be a morphism of monoid scheme and
\tilde{\myvarphi}:{\widetilde{Y}}\to{\widetilde{X}}
the associated map of congruence spaces. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

\myvarphi:Y\to X
is a closed immersion.

(2)

\myvarphi:Y\to X
is a quasi-compact topological embedding,
\myvarphi^{\#}:{\mathcal{O}}_{X}\to\myvarphi_{\ast}{\mathcal{O}}_{Y}
is surjective and the image of
\tilde{\myvarphi}
is a vanishing set.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.1
, (
1
) implies (
2
). To prove the converse implication, assume (
2
) and let
{\mathfrak{C}}
be the congruence kernel of
\myvarphi
. By Lemma
4.6
, the image of
\tilde{\myvarphi}
is equal to the vanishing set
V_{\mathfrak{C}}
of
{\mathfrak{C}}
. Thus
\myvarphi
factors through the closed immersion
\mypsi:Z\to X
corresponding to
{\mathfrak{C}}
. After substituting
X
by
Z
, we can thus assume that
\myvarphi^{\#}:{\mathcal{O}}_{X}\to\myvarphi_{\ast}{\mathcal{O}}_{Y}
is an isomorphism of sheaves and that
\tilde{\myvarphi}
is a homeomorphism.

Since the problem is local in
X
, we can assume that
X
is affine, i.e.
X=\MSpec(\Gamma X)\simeq\MSpec(\Gamma Y)
. This reduces us to the situation that
\myvarphi
is the canonical morphism
\myvarphi:Y\to\MSpec(\Gamma Y)=X
. Under these assumptions, it suffices to show that
Y
is affine, since this implies that
Y=\MSpec(\Gamma Y)=X
and that
\myvarphi:Y\to X
is the identity.

Let
M
be the maximal prime ideal of
B=\Gamma X
. Then its fibre
\mypi_{X}^{-1}(M)
in
{\widetilde{X}}
has a unique closed point
{\mathfrak{m}}
, which is the congruence kernel of the composition
B\to M/B\to{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
of the quotient map
B\to B/M
with the unique map
B/M\to{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
that maps all units of the pointed group
B/M
to
1
. Let
Z=\myvarphi^{-1}({\mathfrak{m}})
and
{\mathfrak{n}}\in{\widetilde{Y}}
be the unique point that maps to
{\mathfrak{m}}
. Since
\mypi_{X}\circ\tilde{\myvarphi}=\myvarphi\circ\mypi_{Y}
and since
\tilde{\myvarphi}
is a homeomorphism,
{\mathfrak{n}}
is the unique closed point of
\mypi_{Y}^{-1}(Z)
. Let
N=\mypi_{Y}({\mathfrak{n}})
.

Let
y
be any closed point of
Y
. Since
\myvarphi
is a topological embedding,
\myvarphi(y)=M
and thus
y\in Z
. Since
y
is closed,
\mypi_{Y}^{-1}(y)
is closed in
{\widetilde{Y}}
and thus contains
{\mathfrak{n}}
. This shows that
y=N
. We conclude that
Y
has a unique closed point and is therefore affine, which concludes the proof.
∎

4.8.
Dominant morphisms

Definition 4.20
.

Let
X
be a monoid scheme and
{\widetilde{X}}
its congruence space. A subset

of

is

if its vanishing closure is

. A morphism
\myvarphi:Y\to X

\tilde{\myvarphi}:{\widetilde{Y}}\to{\widetilde{X}}
of congruence spaces has a strictly dense image.

.

If

.

Proof.

Since the morphisms

Remark 4.22
.

Note that

does not need to be dominant if

. For example, the diagonal

.

Let

5.
Valuation monoids

The notion of a valuation monoid was first introduced in
[
7
]
.

Definition 5.1
.

A
valuation monoid
is an integral monoid
A
such that for every non-zero element
\myalpha\in\Frac A
, either
\myalpha\in A
or
\myalpha^{-1}\in A
.

For our study, we require an existence result of valuation monoids that dominate a given integral monoid. All of this notions are introduced in the following.

5.1.
Dominant maps of monoids

Definition 5.2
.

A morphism
f:A\to B
of pointed monoids is
dominant
, and
B

dominates
A
, if
f^{-1}(M_{B})=M_{A}
where
M_{A}
is the maximal ideal of
A
and
M_{B}
is the maximal ideal of
B
.

Note that for any morphism
f:A\to B
of pointed monoids, it is always true that
f(A^{\times})\subseteq B^{\times}
and so
f^{-1}(M_{B})\subseteq M_{A}
. Thus the morphism
f
is dominant if and only if
f^{-1}(B^{\times})\subseteq A^{\times}
. Also note that composition of dominant morphisms is dominant.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
B
be a pointed monoid and let
A
be an integral submonoid of
B
. An element
b
of
B
is
algebraic over
A
if there is an
a\in A
and an integer
n\geqslant 1
such that
ab^{n}\in A
. Elements of
B
that are not algebraic over
A
are called
transcendental over
A
.

An
integral extension of monoids
is an injective morphism
f:A\to B
of pointed monoids such that for any
b\in B
there is an integer
n\geqslant 1
such that
b^{n}\in A
.

Lemma 5.4
.

An integral extension
f:A\to B
is dominant.

Proof.

We want to show
B^{\times}\cap A\subseteq A^{\times}
(we identify
A
with
f(A)\subseteq B
). We consider
a\in B^{\times}\cap A
, i.e.,
ab=1
for some
b\in B
. Since
A\hookrightarrow B
is an integral extension, there exists some integer
n\geqslant 1
such that
c:=b^{n}\in A
. Thus
a(a^{n-1}c)=a(a^{n-1}b^{n})=(ab)^{n}=1
which shows
a\in A^{\times}
.
∎

5.2.
Characterization and existence of valuation monoids

Proposition 5.5
.

An integral monoid
A
is a valuation monoid if and only if
A
is a maximal submonoid of
\Frac A
with respect to domination.

Proof.

Suppose
A
is maximal in
\Frac A
with respect to domination and consider
a\in\Frac A-A
. We define
B:=A[a]=\{ca^{i}\in\Frac A\mid c\in A,i\geqslant 0\}
. Since
A
is maximal with respect to domination and
A\neq B
, the inclusion
A\hookrightarrow B
is not a dominant morphism, i.e.,
M_{A}\nsubseteq M_{B}\cap A
. Therefore there exists some
d\in M_{A}-M_{B}=M_{A}\cap B^{\times}
. Let
d=ca^{i}
for some
c\in A
and some integer
i\geqslant 0
. If
i\geqslant 1
, we have
a\in B^{\times}
. If
i=0
, then
c=d\in M_{A}\cap B^{\times}
and so there exists
c^{-1}=c^{\prime}a^{j}\in B^{\times}
for some
c^{\prime}\in A
and integer
j\geqslant 1
(since
c\notin A^{\times}
). This shows that
a\in B^{\times}
also in the case
i=0
. Let
a^{-1}=\tilde{c}a^{k}
for some
\tilde{c}\in A
and integer
k\geqslant 0
. Thus
(a^{-1})^{k+1}=\tilde{c}\in A
and
A[a^{-1}]=\{ca^{-i}\in\Frac A\mid c\in A,i\geqslant 0\}
is an integral extension of
A
. Therefore by Lemma
5.1
, the inclusion
A\hookrightarrow A[a^{-1}]
is a dominant morphism. Since
A
is maximal with respect to domination, we must have
A\Cong(A)[a^{-1}]
which implies
a\in A^{\times}
as desired.

Conversely, suppose
A
is a valuation monoid. Let
B
be a pointed submonoid of
\Frac A
and
f:A\to B
is a dominant morphism such that the canonical injection
A\hookrightarrow\Frac A
factorizes through
f
. We want to show that
f
is an isomorphism. We consider any
b\in B-\{0\}\subseteq\Frac A
. Since
A
is a valuation monoid, either
b\in A
or
b^{-1}\in A
(we identify
A
with
f(A)\subseteq B
). If
b^{-1}\in A
, we have
b^{-1}\in B^{\times}\cap A=A^{\times}
since
B
dominates
A
. Thus
b=(b^{-1})^{-1}\in A
.
∎

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
K
be a pointed group and let
A\subseteq K
be a pointed submonoid. Then there exists a valuation monoid
A
dominating
A
such that
K
is the pointed group completion of
A
.

Proof.

We consider the collection
\mathscr{S}
of all pointed submonoids of
K
dominating
A
as a partially ordered set using the relation of domination, i.e., for any
B,C\in\mathscr{S}
, we say
B\leqslant C
if and only if
B\subseteq C
and
M_{B}=M_{C}\cap B
. Suppose that
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a totally ordered chain in
\mathscr{S}
. Then
D=\bigcup A_{i}
is clearly a pointed submonoid of
K
which dominates all of the
A_{i}
and hence
D
dominates
A
as well. Therefore by Zorn’s Lemma,
\mathscr{S}
has a maximal element, say
\overline{A}
. Since
\overline{A}
is a maximal element in
\mathscr{S}
, by Proposition
5.2
,
A_{\operatorname{max}}
is a valuation monoid. We are left to check that
K
is the pointed group completion of
\overline{A}
. Suppose, if possible,
\Frac\overline{A}\neq K
. We consider
t\in K
such that
t\notin\Frac\overline{A}
. If
t
is algebraic over
\overline{A}
, there exists an integer
n\geqslant 1
such that
t^{n}\in\overline{A}
. Thus
\overline{A}\to\overline{A}[t]
is an integral extension and therefore it is dominant by Lemma
5.1
. This contradicts that
\overline{A}
is a maximal element in
\mathscr{S}
. On the other hand, if
t
is transcendental over
\overline{A}
, it follows that
C:=\overline{A}[t]=\{kt^{i}\in K\mid k\in\overline{A},i\geqslant 0\}\subseteq K
is a pointed submonoid of
K
with
M_{C}=\langle M_{\overline{A}},t\rangle
and that
C
dominates
\overline{A}
, i.e.,
M_{C}\cap\overline{A}=M_{\overline{A}}
. It follows that
C
dominates
A
. Hence
C
is a pointed submonoid of
K
dominating
A
and strictly containing
\overline{A}
. This gives the required contradiction.
∎

6.
Universally closed morphisms

In this section, we introduce closed morphisms and universally closed morphisms. Under the assumption of quasi-compactness, we characterize universally closed morphisms in terms of a valuative criterion.

6.1.
Closed images

Proposition 6.1

6.2.
The valuative criterion for universally closed morphisms

Definition 6.2
.

A morphism
\myvarphi:Y\to X
is
closed
if the associated map
\tilde{\myvarphi}:{\widetilde{Y}}\to{\widetilde{X}}
between the respective congruence spaces is a closed continuous map. A morphism
\myvarphi:Y\to X
of monoid schemes is
universally closed
if for every morphism
\mypsi:X^{\prime}\to X

\myvarphi^{\prime}:Y^{\prime}=X^{\prime}\times_{X}Y\to X^{\prime}

\myvarphi
along
\mypsi
is a closed morphism.

As a first example, we have the following.

Lemma 6.3
.

Closed immersions are universally closed.

Proof.

Since surjective monoid morphisms are stable under cobase change, it follows that closed immersions are stable under base change. Therefore the result follows from Proposition
4.1
.
∎

7.
Separated morphisms

In this section, we develop a topological characterization of separated morphisms and extend the characterization in terms of the valuative criterion from finite type morphisms, as treated in
[
7
]
, to arbitrary morphisms.

Definition 7.1
.

A morphism
\myvarphi:Y\to X
of monoid schemes is
separated
if the diagonal
\Delta_{\myvarphi}\colon Y\to Y\times_{X}Y
is a closed immersion. A morphism
\myvarphi:Y\to X
of monoid schemes is
quasi-separated
if the diagonal
\Delta_{\myvarphi}:Y\to Y\times_{X}Y
is quasi-compact.

7.1.
Locally closed immersions

Our topological characterization of separated morphisms relies on some general facts for locally closed immersions.

Definition 7.2
.

A morphism
\myvarphi:Y\to X
is a
locally closed immersion
if it factors into a closed immersion
Y\to U
followed by an open immersion
U\to X
.

8.
Proper morphisms

In this section, we introduce proper morphisms of monoid schemes and characterize them in terms of the valuative criterion for properness in the finite type case. This shows, in particular, that our notion of proper morphisms extends that of Cortiñaz, Haesemeyer, Walker and Weibel; cf.

Definition 8.1
.

A morphism
\myvarphi:Y\to X
of monoid schemes is

Corollary 8.3

Let

∎

Lemma 8.4
.

(1)

Closed immersions are proper.

(2)

Proper morphisms are closed under composition and base change.

(3)

Properness is local on the base.

Proof.

We begin with (
1
). By definition, closed immersions are of finite type. By Lemma
6.2
, closed immersions are universally closed. Closed immersions are affine and thus separated. Thus closed immersions are proper.

We continue with (
2
). Finite type morphisms and universally closed morphisms are clearly stable under composition. If
Y\to X
and
X\to Z
are separated, then the composition

Y\xrightarrow{\Delta_{Y/X}}Y\times_{X}Y\longrightarrow Y\times_{Z}Y

is a closed immersion because the composition of affine morphisms is affine and the composition of surjections of sheaves is surjective. This shows that proper morphisms are closed under composition. Proper morphisms are also closed under base change because the property of being finite type, separated and universally closed are all stable under base change.

Finally we turn to (
3
). All defining properties of proper morphisms, finite type, separated and universally closed, are local on the base and therefore so is properness.
∎

We recall from
[
7
, §7]
that a morphism
Y\to X
of monoid schemes is
projective
, if locally on
X
, it factors as a closed immersion
Y\to{\mathbb{P}^{n}_{X}}
for some
n\geqslant 0
followed by the projection
{\mathbb{P}^{n}_{X}}\to X
.

Corollary 8.5
.

Projective morphisms are proper.

Proof.

Since closed immersions are proper by Lemma
8.1
, it is enough to show that morphisms
{\mathbb{P}^{n}_{X}}\to X
are proper. Since
\mathbb{P}^{n}_{X}\cong\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\times_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}X
, it also suffices to show that morphisms
{\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}}\to{\mathbb{F}_{1}}
are proper. We know that
{\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
are proper morphisms. Therefore the result follows from Corollary
8.1
.
∎

Appendix A
Alternatives, ramifications, and why not

Certain concepts from ring theory and algebraic geometry allow for different generalizations to monoids and monoid schemes. From the outset, it is not clear which choices to make—and this is one of the struggles in the development of
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-geometry.

We comment in this appendix on alternative perspectives on the congruence space and why we choose to dismiss them.

A.1.
Prime congruences

There are alternative concepts for prime congruences, which alters the notion of points of the congruence space.

A more restrictive choice of prime congruences is considered by Manoel Jarra in
[
18
]
and
[
19
]
, which leads to subspaces of the congruence space
{\widetilde{X}}
of a monoid scheme
X
. This theory has different goals in mind than the present text: Jarra shows that his subspaces reflect suitably the dimension of monoid schemes and link to Smirnov’s approach to the ABC-conjecture.

There is also a more lax notion of prime congruence. To facilitate the language in our discussion, we say that a congruence
{\mathfrak{c}}
on a monoid
A
is
weak prime
if
A/{\mathfrak{c}}
is without zero divisors. As a first observation, note that every prime congruence is weakly prime, but not vice versa. For example, the trivial congruence on
A=\{0,e,1\}
with idempotent
e=e^{2}
is weak prime, but not prime.

We define the
weak congruence space of
A
as the topological space
\Cong^{w}(A)
of all weak prime congruence of
A
together with the topology generated by the open subsets of the form

U_{a,b}\ =\ \{{\mathfrak{p}}\in\Cong^{w}(A)\mid(a,b)\notin{\mathfrak{p}}\}

with
a,b\in A
. It comes with a topological embedding
\myiota_{A}:\Cong(A)\hookrightarrow\Cong^{w}(A)
.

A large part of the theory of this paper transfer
mutatis mutandis
to weak congruence spaces: the weak congruence space generalizes to monoid schemes
X
in terms of open coverings, which yields a topological space
{\widetilde{X}}^{w}
and a commutative diagram of continuous maps

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{\widetilde{X}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{\widetilde{X}}^{w}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\myiota_{X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mypi_{X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mypi_{X}^{w}}

Open (closed) immersions induce open (closed) topological embeddings of weak congruence spaces. The topological characterization of closed immersions and separated morphisms carries over to weak congruence spaces.

Remark A.1

(Uncertainty about closed morphisms and valuative criteria)
.

At the point of writing, we do not know, however, if the weak congruence space characterizes the same class of closed morphisms as the congruence space. It seems very possible that they do, and in this case, the weak congruence space would be a natural extension of the congruence space.

If not, then this infers a discrepancy for (universally) closed and proper morphisms. The valuative criteria for these classes of morphisms serve as a strong argument that the definitions of this paper are satisfactory. This is our primary reason to work with the congruence space
{\widetilde{X}}
in this paper opposed to the weak congruence space
{\widetilde{X}}^{w}
.

Zooming in into the proof of the valuative criteria of universally closed and proper morphisms, we see that a direct transfer of the arguments to the setting of weak congruence spaces faces the difficulty that the congruence kernel of morphisms into valuation monoids are prime congruences. This means that weak prime congruence that are not prime are not seen by test diagrams in the sense of this paper.

A natural variant that might come to mind is a larger class of test objects, such as pointed monoids without zero divisors whose integral closure is a valuation monoid. A simple example of this nature is the projection
f:A=\{0,e,1\}\to{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
, which sends the idempotent element
e=e^{2}
of
A
to
f(e)=1
. However, this particular choice of test object fails to satisfy the lifting property for the induced closed immersion
\myvarphi=f^{\ast}:U=\MSpec({{\mathbb{F}}_{1}})\to\MSpec(A)=T
and the tautological test diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{id}_{U}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\myvarphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\myvarphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{id}_{T}}

since
\myvarphi:U\to T
is not an isomorphism.

To conclude, it might be possible that the weak congruence space leans itself for a characterization of closed morphisms (in the sense of this paper) and therefore is
a posteriori
a suitable extension of the notion of congruence space.
A priori
, we do not know whether this holds and therefore refrain from a consideration of weak congruence spaces in the main body of this paper.

A.2.
Structure sheaf for the congruence space

There are approaches to endow the congruence space
{\widetilde{X}}=\Cong A
of a monoid
A
with a structure sheaf of local coordinate monoids. In contrast to the structure sheaf of the prime spectrum
X=\MSpec A
, this is more subtle since it is not clear what the coordinate monoids for the open subsets of the form
U_{a,b}=\{{\mathfrak{p}}\mid(a,b)\notin{\mathfrak{p}}\}
should be.

There are different solutions to this, which we discuss in the following. However, in every case the conclusion is that a refinement of the structure sheaf of
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
to a sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}}}
on
{\widetilde{X}}
results in the fact that for some pointed monoids
A
, the canonical map
A\to{\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}}}({\widetilde{X}})
is not an isomorphism.

Topos theory sheds light on this phenomenon: if we endow the category
{\mathcal{A}}
of affine monoid schemes with the Grothendieck topology of all covering families
\{U_{i}\to X\}_{i\in I}
by principal opens that appear in the canonical topology for
{\mathcal{A}}
, then we find that each such covering has an
i\in I
for which
U_{i}\to X
is an isomorphism. As a consequence, the locale of open subschemes of
X
is the locale of the prime spectrum of
A=\Gamma X
; in essence this follows from Marty’s work in
[
30
]
. This means that any refinement of the topology of
\MSpec(A)
towards
\Cong(A)
destroys the property of
A\to{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(X)
being an isomorphism.

This insight is the reason for us to consider the congruence space merely as a topological space and to dismiss the notion of a structure sheaf. Nevertheless, we describe some natural sheaves on the congruence space in the following. The main reason for the existence of different natural choices is that there are several reasonable alternatives for coordinate monoids of open subsets of the form
U_{a,b}
of the congruence space
\Cong(A)
of a pointed monoid.

A.2.1.
Pullback sheaf

The first candidate for a structure sheaf for the congruence space
{\widetilde{X}}
of a monoid scheme

that might come to mind is the pullback
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}}}=\mypi_{X}^{\ast}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
of the structure sheaf of
X
. Explicitly
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}}}
is the sheafification of the presheaf with values

{\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}}}(V)\ =\ \colim{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(U)

where
U
ranges over all open subsets of
X
that contain
\mypi_{X}(V)
, modulo the identifications coming from the restriction maps.

For general
X
, a sheafification of the presheaf pullback is necessary. This means, in particular, that for affine
X=\MSpec(A)
, the canonical morphism
A={\mathcal{O}}_{X}(X)\to{\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}}}({\widetilde{X}})
is in general not an isomorphism, as the following example shows.

.

Consider
A=\{0,e,1\}
with idempotent

. Then

has two prime ideals
\langle 0\rangle=\{0\}

\langle e\rangle=\{0,e\}
and
{\widetilde{X}}
has two prime congruences
{\mathfrak{p}}_{0}=\langle(e,1)\rangle
and

\mypi_{X}:{\widetilde{X}}\to X
is a bijection with
\mypi_{X}({\mathfrak{p}}_{0})=\langle 0\rangle
and
\mypi_{X}({\mathfrak{p}}_{e})=\langle e\rangle
, but not a homeomorphism since

, but
\mypi_{X}(U_{e,1})=\{\langle e\rangle\}

.

In particular,
{\widetilde{X}}=U_{e,0}\myamalg U_{e,1}=\{{\mathfrak{p}}_{e}\}\myamalg\{{\mathfrak{p}}_{0}\}
is discrete and thus
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}}}({\widetilde{X}})={\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}}}(U_{e,0})\times{\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}}}(Y_{e,1})
. We find that

{\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}}}(U_{e,0})\ =\ {\mathcal{O}}_{X}(\{\langle 0\rangle\})\ =\ {{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}}}(U_{e,1})\ =\ {\mathcal{O}}_{X}(X)\ =\ A.

We see that in this case the canonical map

A.2.2.

The failure of the presheaf pullback of

is caused by open subsets of

that are not inverse images of open subsets of

.

There is a trivial solution to fix this failure: we consider
{\widetilde{X}}
together with the topology generated by the inverse image of open subsets of

X=\MSpec(A)
, these are unions of open subsets of
{\widetilde{X}}
of the form
U_{a,0}
with
a\in A
.

For this coarser topology on
{\widetilde{X}}
, the presheaf pullback
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}}}^{\prime}
of
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
along
\mypi_{X}
is a sheaf and satisfies

{\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}}}^{\prime}(U_{a,0})\ =\ {\mathcal{O}}_{X}(U_{a})\ =\ A[a^{-1}].

In particular, we have
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}}}^{\prime}({\widetilde{X}})=A
in the affine case
{\widetilde{X}}=\Cong(A)
. This point of view does, however, not provide any additional insight into the nature of monoid schemes and is therefore not of interest.

A.2.3.
Berkovich’s approach: open subsets with coordinate monoid

In contrast to the sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}}}
from section
A.2.1
, which endows open subsets of
{\widetilde{X}}
with (colimits of) local coordinate monoids for
X
, Berkovich takes another route in
[
1
]
.

Namely, there are more open subsets of
{\widetilde{X}}
than the inverse images of opens in
X
(as considered in section
A.2.2
) that come with a canonical coordinate monoid. Let us call an open subset
U
of
{\widetilde{X}}

affine-like
if there is a monoid morphism
f:A\to A_{U}
such that the induced morphism
\myvarphi=f^{\ast}:Y\to X
with
Y=\MSpec A_{U}
satisfies the following properties:

(1)

The image of the associated map
\tilde{\myvarphi}:{\widetilde{Y}}\to{\widetilde{X}}
between congruence spaces has image
U
.

(2)

Every morphism
\mypsi:Z\to X
with
\textup{im}(\tilde{\mypsi})\subset U
factors uniquely through
\myvarphi:Y\to X
.

(3)

For every
y\in{\widetilde{Y}}
and
x=\myvarphi(y)
, the induced morphism
k(y)\to k(x)
be the respective residue fields is an isomorphism.

For such open subsets
U
of
{\widetilde{U}}
, the monoid
A_{U}
is a canonical choice of coordinate monoid. In fact, affine-like open subsets of
{\widetilde{X}}
are closed under finite intersections and form a basis for a topology on
{\widetilde{X}}
that is in general strictly coarser than the topology of all open subsets and strictly finer than the topology induced from
X
(as considered in section
A.2.2
).

If we attempt to extend the sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}}}
to this finer topology, then we gain only a presheaf that fails in general to satisfy the sheaf axiom. Sheafification defines a sheaf of monoids on this finer topology, whose global sections do not necessarily agree with
A
, as the following example shows.

Example A.3
.

We reconsider the monoid
A=\{0,e,1\}
with idempotent
e=e^{2}
from Example
A.2.1
. The only opens of
{\widetilde{X}}=\Cong A
that stem from
X
are
\emptyset
,
{\widetilde{X}}
and
U_{e,0}=\{\langle e\sim 1\rangle\}
. The open subset
U_{e,1}=\{\langle e\sim 0\rangle\}
is affine-like as witnessed by the morphism
A\to A/\langle e\sim 0\rangle\simeq{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
. Thus the coordinate monoid of
U_{e,1}
is
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
, equally to
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\widetilde{X}}}(U_{e,0})\simeq{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
. The monoid of global sections of the sheaf on affine-like opens is
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}\times{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
, which contains
A
as a proper submonoid.

A.2.4.
Deitmar: sheaf for all opens via enriched structures

Deitmar uses in
[
12
]
a richer structure, called sesquiads, that incorporate additive relations, which allows him to define localizations
A[\frac{1}{a-b}]
for difference of elements
a
and
b
in
A
. This generalized localization
A[\frac{1}{a-b}]
serves as the coordinate object for
U_{a,b}
. Also this theory fails to be subcanonical; in particular, the global sections of the resulting structure sheaf on
\Cong A
do in general not equal
A
.
