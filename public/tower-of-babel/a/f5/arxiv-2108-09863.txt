Kippenhahn Varieties and the Weyl Calculus for Several Matrices I
Brian Jefferies

Abstract
. The paper reviews properties of the Weyl functional calculus for several operators and its relation to the generalised numerical range of n hermitian matrices. The support and singular support of the Weyl functional calculus for n n hermitian matrices are determined by Kippenhahn varieties in algebraic geometry.



Dedicated to the memory of Alan McIntosh.

1.
Introduction

Let
n
be an integer and
{\boldsymbol{A}}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
an
n
-tuple of linear operators densely defined in a complex Banach space
X

whose joint spectra satisfy the spectral reality condition

(1)

{\sigma}({\langle{{\boldsymbol{A}}},\xi\rangle})\subset{\mathbb{R}},\qquad\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

with
{\langle{{\boldsymbol{A}}},\xi\rangle}=A_{1}\xi_{1}+\dots+A_{n}\xi_{n}
. Such a system
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is called
hyperbolic
.

The term comes from the case when
{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
consists of
N\times N
matrices with
N=2,3,\dots
. With the characteristic
polynomial of a matrix
B
defined by
p_{B}(z)=\det(B-zI)
for
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, the
n
-tuple
{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
is hyperbolic
exactly when the only solutions
z
of the equation
p_{{\langle{{\boldsymbol{A}}},\xi\rangle}}(z)=0
are real for any
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
\xi\neq 0
,
or that
P^{{\boldsymbol{A}}}:\xi\longmapsto\det(\xi_{0}I+{\langle{{\boldsymbol{A}}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\rangle})
,
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
, is a
homogeneous
hyperbolic polynomial
with respect to
e_{0}=(1,0,\dots,0)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
. Here the vector
\xi=(\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
has been written
as
\xi=\xi_{0}e_{0}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
with
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{j}e_{j}
for the standard basis
(e_{0},\dots,e_{n})
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}

and the same notation is used for
{\zeta}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}
.

General hyperbolic polynomials
p:{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\to{\mathbb{R}}
were studied by L. Gårding
[
16
]
in relation to hyperbolic partial differential equations in order that the equation
p(\tau,{\boldsymbol{D}})={\delta}_{0}
be well-posed in the sense of distributions with respect to
the differential operators

\tau=\frac{1}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial t},\qquad{\boldsymbol{D}}=\bigg(\frac{1}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}},\dots,\frac{1}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{n}}\bigg).

The unique distributional solution
F_{p}
of
p(\tau,{\boldsymbol{D}})={\delta}_{0}

[
20
, Theorem 12.5.1]
may then be expressed by a formula of Herglotz-Petrovsky-Leray
systematically studied in
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
and
[
20
, Chapter 12]
. The associated hyperbolic system

(2)

I\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+{\langle{{\boldsymbol{A}}},\nabla\rangle}=i\delta_{0}I

then has a unique solution
F_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}=\Phi({{\boldsymbol{A}}})F_{P^{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}
for a matrix differential operator
\Phi({{\boldsymbol{A}}})
of order
N-1

in
(\tau,{\boldsymbol{D}})

formed by multiplying the Fourier transform of
F_{P^{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}
by the
(N-1)
matrix minors of
\xi_{0}I+{\langle{{\boldsymbol{A}}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\rangle}
,
\xi=\xi_{0}e_{0}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
.

The Fourier transform of the Schwartz function
f\in{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is taken to be

\hat{f}(\xi)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{-i{\langle x,\xi\rangle}}f(x)\,dx,\quad\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

On the other hand, taking the Fourier transform of equation (
2
) in the space variables only
for
t>0

and solving for the initial value problem, we get the
distribution
{\mathcal{W}}_{t{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}
,
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. At
t=1
, the operator valued distribution
{\mathcal{W}}_{{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}
given by

(3)

{\langle{\mathcal{W}}_{{{\boldsymbol{A}}}},f\rangle}\ =\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{i{\langle{{\boldsymbol{A}}},\xi\rangle}}\hat{f}(\xi)\,d\xi,\quad f\in{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}),

is the
Weyl functional calculus
for
{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
studied since the late 60’s by E. Nelson
[
28
]
, M. Taylor
[
35
]

and R.F.V. Anderson
[
1
,
2
]
. This expression has the advantage that it does not involve determinants
so
{\mathcal{W}}_{{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}
makes sense
for any system
{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
of collectively densely defined linear operators which satisfy an exponential estimate of the
form

(4)

\|e^{i{\langle{{\boldsymbol{A}}},\xi\rangle}}\|\leq C(1+|\xi|)^{s},\quad\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

for some
C,s>0
, for example, selfadjoint operators defined in a Hilbert space with
s=0
.
A single bounded linear operator satisfying the estimate
(\ref{eq:type})
is a generalised scalar operator

[
13
, Theorem 5.4.5]
. More precisely, if
{{\boldsymbol{A}}}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
are densely defined selfadjoint operators such that every real linear
combination has a selfadjoint closure
{\langle{{\boldsymbol{A}}},\xi\rangle}
on the intersection of the relevant domains,
then (
4
) follows with
C=1
and
s=0
from successive applications of the Lie-Kato-Trotter product formula
(see
[
14
, Chapter III, Corollary 5.8]
)

The prime candidate for the Weyl functional calculus
is the system
({\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{D}})
of densely defined operators. Here
{\boldsymbol{X}}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
with
X_{j}
the operator of multiplication by the variable
x_{j}
,

j=1,\dots,n
, so that
{\mathcal{W}}_{({\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{D}})}
is a
{\mathcal{L}}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))
-valued Schwartz distribution on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
.
This is the original Weyl functional calculus and much studied as a pseudodifferental operator
[
18
]
,
[
36
, VII §14]
. The more general definition of
{\mathcal{W}}_{{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}
for selfadjoint
operators
{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
formulated by E. Nelson
[
28
]
was inspired by ideas of R. Feynman on a general
operator calculus and “disentangling” procedure
[
15
]
,
[
24
]
. In the case that
{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
are selfadjoint elements of a von Neumann
algebra with a given trace
\rho
, then the scalar distribution
\rho\circ{\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
is the
Wigner transform

[
12
,
33
]
.

In a similar fashion, the Kohn-Nirenberg functional calculus
{\sigma}\mapsto{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{D}})_{\text{KN}}
defined for
symbols
{\sigma}\in{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
by

{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{D}})_{\text{KN}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}e^{i{\langle{{\boldsymbol{X}}},q\rangle}}e^{i{\langle{{\boldsymbol{D}}},p\rangle}}\hat{\sigma}(q,p)\,dq\,dp

is an important tool in the analysis of partial differential equations
[
36
, VII (1.10)]
.

If
{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
are bounded linear operators on a Banach space
X
satisfying the exponential bound (
4
) then
by the Paley-Wiener theorem
{\mathcal{W}}_{{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}
has compact support and there exist
r>0
such that

(5)

\|e^{i{\langle{{\boldsymbol{A}}},{\zeta}\rangle}}\|\leq C^{\prime}(1+|\Re{\zeta}|)^{s^{\prime}}e^{r|\Im{\zeta}|},\quad{\zeta}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n},

for some
C^{\prime},s^{\prime}>0
. The operator valued distribution
{\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
has support in the ball
B_{r}
of radius
r
centred at zero.
Any number
r
strictly greater than
\|{{\boldsymbol{A}}}\|=(\|A_{1}\|^{2}+\dots+\|A_{n}\|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}
will do.
All such linear operators satisfy the spectral reality condition (
1
).

Back to hyperbolic
(N\times N)
matrices
{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
, the solution of (
2
) has an expression
by the Herglotz-Petrovsky-Leray formula so the bound (
5
) is satisfied with

s^{\prime}
at most
N-1
. In the infinite dimensional situation this may no longer be the case.
The paper of Nelson
[
28
]
also contains a formula for hermitian matrices

{\boldsymbol{A}}
acting on
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
expressing
{\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
as a differential operator of order
(N-1)

acting on the measure
\mu\circ n_{{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}^{-1}
. Here
\mu
is the unitarily invariant probability
measure on the unit sphere
S({\mathbb{C}}^{N})
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
centred at zero and

(6)

n_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}:h\longmapsto(\langle A_{1}h,h\rangle,\dots,\langle A_{n}h,h\rangle),\qquad h\in S({\mathbb{C}}^{N}),

is the
joint numerical range map
. The Payley-Wiener theorem shows that
{\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
is supported
by the convex hull of the
joint numerical range

N_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}=n_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}(S({\mathbb{C}}^{N}))
, even for unbounded selfadjoint operators (with a common dense domain
so that
{\langle{{\boldsymbol{A}}},\xi\rangle}
has a selfadjoint closure for all
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
).

The joint numerical range
N_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
of an
n
-tuple
{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
of hermitian matrices has attracted recent interest
because it arises in many areas such as optimisation theory, quantum optics, quantum statistical
mechanics, Wigner transforms and quantum error correction, see
[
30
]
for an overview. In the
Herglotz-Petrovsky-Leray formula the joint numerical range
N_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
manifests as the trace of
the propagation cone
K(P^{\boldsymbol{A}})
at time
t=1
and the fundamental formula

(7)

\mu\circ n_{{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}^{-1}=(-i)^{N}(N-1)!F_{P^{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}(1,{\,\cdot\,})

holds as distributions linking the Nelson and Herglotz-Petrovsky-Leray representations for systems of
hermitian matrices. It is not immediately obvious why
F_{p}(1,{\,\cdot\,})
should be a distribution of order zero
in the case that
p=P^{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
is a hyperbolic determinantal polynomial for a system
{{\boldsymbol{A}}}

of hermitian matrices, see Theorem
5.2
below.

For the case
n=2
of two hermitian matrices
{{\boldsymbol{A}}}=(A_{1},A_{2})
, the set
N_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
may be identified with the usual
numerical range in
{\mathbb{C}}
of the single matrix
A=A_{1}+iA_{2}
under the identification
j:(x,y)\mapsto x+iy
,
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Then
jN_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
is the convex hull of an algebraic curve
C(A)=jC({{\boldsymbol{A}}})
studied by R. Kippenhahn
[
26
]
.
In this case M. Atiyah, R. Bott and L. Gårding show that the singular support
\operatorname{ss}({\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}})
of the Weyl
calculus for two hermitian matrices
{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
is exactly
C({{\boldsymbol{A}}})

[
4
, Theorem 14.20]
.
That
\operatorname{ss}({\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}})\subset C({{\boldsymbol{A}}})
was shown by J. Bazer and D. Yen
[
6
]
.
None of these authors reference the numerical range studies of Kippenhahn
[
26
]
. Bazer and Yen
manage to avoid discussing hyperbolic polynomials by invoking a plane wave decomposition of the
distribution
{\mathcal{W}}_{t{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}
whose density with respect to Lebesgue measure is the “Riemann matrix”
for equation (
2
).

At this stage it is prudent to introduce some modern developments. The
Cauchy transform
of a Schwartz distribution

T\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}})
is the function
\tilde{T}:z\mapsto{\langle T,g_{z}\rangle}
with

g_{z}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{1}{x-z}
,
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
,
x\in{\mathbb{R}}

[
10
, §5.1]
. Then

(8)

T=\lim_{{\epsilon}\to 0+}\tilde{T}(x+i{\epsilon})-\tilde{T}(x+i{\epsilon})

in the sense of distribution
[
10
, §5.6]
.
If we want a similar formula for distributions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
like the Weyl functional calculus
{\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
, then we need an analogue of the
normalised Cauchy kernel
\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{1}{z}
,
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
z\neq 0
, in higher dimensions.
Then we may obtain a representation for for the density of
{\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
with respect to Lebesgue measure
like the Riemann matrix of Bazer and Yen
[
5
,
6
]
. This is the point where Clifford analysis enters
as a functional calculus technique.

The
Clifford algebra

{\mathbb{C}}_{(n)}
over the field
{\mathbb{C}}
is generated by standard basis vectors

(e_{0},e_{1},\dots,e_{n})

in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
with multiplication so that
e_{0}
is the unit and the formula
x^{2}=-|x|^{2}
holds for
x=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}e_{j}
,

x_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Then we obtain
e_{j}e_{k}=-e_{k}e_{j}
for
j\neq k
,
j,k=1,\dots,n
and
e_{j}^{2}=-1
for
j=1,\dots,n
.
The
Kelvin inverse
of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
is the vector
x^{-1}={\overline{x}}/|x|^{2}
,
x\neq 0
.

The Cauchy-Riemann operator is
D=\sum_{j=0}^{n}e_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}
and for
\Sigma_{n}=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{n+1}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}
the function

E(x)=\frac{1}{\Sigma_{n}}\frac{{\overline{x}}}{|x|^{n+1}},\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1},\ x\neq 0,

is the corresponding Cauchy kernel with
DE=0
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}
. The involution is given by
{\overline{x}}=x_{0}-{\boldsymbol{x}}

for
x=x_{0}e_{0}+{\boldsymbol{x}}
with
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\equiv\{0\}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
. For product vectors

{\overline{e_{j}e_{k}}}={\overline{e}}_{k}\,{\overline{e}}_{j}
,
j\neq k
,
j,k\neq 0
, and so on. It is easily checked that
E={\overline{D}}{\Gamma}_{n+1}
for the fundamental
solution
{\Gamma}_{n+1}
of the Laplacian operator
\Delta e_{0}=D{\overline{D}}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
so that
{\Gamma}_{n+1}=\frac{1}{\Sigma_{n}}\frac{1}{|x|^{n-1}}

and
\Delta{\Gamma}_{n+1}={\delta}_{0}
for
n\geq 2
. Setting
G_{\omega}(x)=E({\omega}-x)
,
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, for any Schwartz distribution
T\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
we have

(9)

T=\lim_{{\epsilon}\to 0+}\tilde{T}(x+{\epsilon}e_{0})-\tilde{T}(x-{\epsilon}e_{0})

in the sense of distributions for the Cauchy transform
\tilde{T}({\omega})={\langle T,G_{\omega}\rangle}
,
{\omega}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
,
{\omega}_{0}\neq 0

[
9
, Theorem 27.7]
. When the Weyl functional calculus
{\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
exists for operators
{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
,
the operator valued function
{\omega}\mapsto G_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{A}})=\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}({\omega})
defined for all

{\omega}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
with
{\omega}_{0}\neq 0
is called the
Cauchy kernel
for
{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
.
For any bounded linear operators
{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
on a Banach space
X
, the Cauchy kernel
G_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{A}})
can be defined by a
series expansion for
{\omega}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
with
|{\omega}|
large enough but this is not very useful. For
n=1
,

G_{\omega}(A)=\frac{1}{2\pi}(j{\omega}-A)^{-1}
in
{\mathcal{L}}(X)
if
j{\omega}\notin{\sigma}(A)
.

Finally, if
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is a hyperbolic system of bounded linear operators (or unbounded with a common dense domain
and uniform resolvent bounds) then
G_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{A}})
may be defined by a
plane wave decomposition
which agrees
with the definition in case
{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
satisfies the exponential bounds (
5
) and so
{\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
exists.
This central idea is due to Alan McIntosh in 1988 out of which the present investigation grew.

When
n
is even, the plane wave formula and equation (
9
) tell us that the distribution
{\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
is a constant times the limit

(10)

\lim_{{\epsilon}\to 0+}\int\limits_{S^{n-1}}\left(\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}I-{\boldsymbol{A}},s\rangle-{\epsilon}sI\right)^{-n}+\left(\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}I-{\boldsymbol{A}},s\rangle+{\epsilon}sI\right)^{-n}\/ds

in the sense of distributions for
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Here
S^{n-1}
is the unit sphere in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Even for matrices this is a useful observation because (
10
) is similar to the plane wave formula
employed by Bazer and Yen
[
5
]
in their study of the Riemann matrix but with the pleasing advantage that
it also works for
n\geq 2
. The formula for odd
n
is

(11)

\lim_{{\epsilon}\to 0+}\int\limits_{S^{n-1}}s\left(\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}I-{\boldsymbol{A}},s\rangle+{\epsilon}sI\right)^{-n}-s\left(\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}I-{\boldsymbol{A}},s\rangle-{\epsilon}sI\right)^{-n}\/ds.

By perturbing the integrals over
S^{n-1}
into the complex domain and observing that we have a closed
matrix valued differential form by homogeneity, the limits can be evaluated
pointwise

and converge to the density of the distribution
{\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
with respect to Lebesgue measure. This
is essentially the argument of Bazer and Yen in the case
n=2
for two hermitian matrices.
The method just described is limited to finite dimensional operators but works for all hyperbolic

n
-tuples
{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
of matrices and uses the full force of the Herglotz-Petrovsky-Leray representation
devised by Atiyah, Bott and Gårding.

In addition, we need the analogue
C({\boldsymbol{A}})
of the Kippenhahn curves in dimensions
n=3,4,\dots

for which
N_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}=\operatorname{co}(C({\boldsymbol{A}}))
. These semi-algebraic sets have only recently been determined by Plaumann, Sinn and Weis
[
30
]
and promise to have applications to the many areas where joint numerical range is a central concept.
After settling the many issues involved, the position of the singular support
\operatorname{ss}({\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}})
of the Weyl
functional calculus with respect to
the
boundary generating set

C({\boldsymbol{A}})
or
Kippenhahn variety
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is examined.

2.
The Kippenhahn Curves

Let
{\boldsymbol{A}}=(A_{1},A_{2})
be a pair of
(N\times N)
hermitian
matrices. Set
A=A_{1}+iA_{2}
.
As mentioned in the Introduction, an application of the Paley-Wiener Theorem
yields that the convex hull of the
support
\hbox{\rm supp}({\mathcal{W}}_{{{\boldsymbol{A}}}})
of the associated Weyl
distribution
{\mathcal{W}}_{{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}
coincides with the

numerical range

\displaystyle K(A)

\displaystyle:=jN_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}

\displaystyle=\bigl\{\langle Ax,x\rangle\,\bigm|\,x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N},\ |x|=1\bigr\}

of the matrix
A
. For more precise information
on the location of
\hbox{\rm supp}({\mathcal{W}}_{{{\boldsymbol{A}}}})
within the
numerical range of
A
, we need to have a closer look at
the fine structure of
K(A)
.

Of particular interest are certain plane algebraic
curves associated with
A
that were first investigated
by R. Kippenhahn
[
26
]
in 1952. We briefly recall the concepts
involved.

Let
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{R}}
or
{\mathbb{C}}
. For
0\leq k\leq 3
, the

Grassmannian

G_{3,k}{\mathbb{F}}
, defined as the set of
all
k
-dimensional
{\mathbb{F}}
-subspaces of
{\mathbb{F}}^{3}
, is a compact
analytic
{\mathbb{F}}
-manifold
of dimension
k(3-k)
. It
has a natural topology, induced by the differential
structure of the manifold, which is determined, for
example, by the metric
h
on
G_{3,k}{\mathbb{F}}
with

h(U,V)\ =\ \sup_{v\in V,\ |v|=1}\inf_{\phantom{\mbox{l}}u\in U,\ |u|=1\phantom{\mbox{l}}}|\!|u-v|\!|\qquad\mbox{ for all }U,V\in G_{3,k}{\mathbb{F}}\,.

The
projective plane

\hbox{PG}({\mathbb{F}}^{3})
over
{\mathbb{F}}
is given by

\hbox{PG}({\mathbb{F}}^{3})\ =\ \bigcup_{0\leq k\leq 3}G_{3,k}{\mathbb{F}}\,.

The one and two dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{F}}^{3}
are
usually called the
points
and
lines

in
\hbox{PG}({\mathbb{F}}^{3})
, respectively.

By common abuse of notation we introduce
homogeneous
coordinates
for the points in
\hbox{PG}({\mathbb{F}}^{3})
as

(u_{1}:u_{2}:u_{3})={\mathbb{F}}(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
. The coordinates of a vector
in
{\mathbb{F}}^{3}
are expressed with respect to the standard basis
for
{\mathbb{F}}^{3}
.

A
polarity
of
\hbox{PG}({\mathbb{F}}^{3})
is a bijection
on
\hbox{PG}({\mathbb{F}}^{3})
which reverses the inclusion of subspaces
and the square of which equals the identity mapping. The

standard polarity

\pi
is characterised by

u^{\pi}\ =\ \bigl\{v\in{\mathbb{F}}^{3}\,\bigm|\,\sum_{j=1}^{3}u_{j}v_{j}=0\bigr\}\qquad\mbox{ for all }u\in G_{3,1}{\mathbb{F}}\,,

which gives
u^{\pi}\in G_{3,2}{\mathbb{F}}
. Using the polarity
\pi
,
we can also introduce homogeneous coordinates for the lines
in
\hbox{PG}({\mathbb{F}}^{3})
by setting
[v_{1}:v_{2}:v_{3}]=(v_{1}:v_{2}:v_{3})^{\pi}
.

A nonempty subset
C
of
G_{3,1}{\mathbb{F}}
is called a

plane
{\mathbb{F}}
-algebraic curve
if it is the zero
locus of a homogeneous
3
-variate polynomial over
{\mathbb{F}}
.
The defining polynomial of
C
is not uniquely determined:
if
f
defines the curve, then so does, for example,
f^{k}

for any
k\geq 1
. However, every curve
C
has a defining
polynomial of minimal degree which is unique up to a
constant factor. A curve is said to be
irreducible

if it has an irreducible defining polynomial. Since a
polynomial ring over a field is a unique factorisation
domain, each algebraic curve
C
is the union of finitely
many irreducible curves. If
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}
are the
irreducible components of
C
with irreducible defining
polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
, then
f=f_{1}\cdots f_{k}
is
a defining polynomial of
C
of minimal degree. We call

f
a
minimal polynomial of
C
. Note that an
irreducible real algebraic curve is not necessarily
connected.

Let
f
be a minimal polynomial of the algebraic curve

C=\bigl\{u\in G_{3,1}{\mathbb{F}}\,\bigm|\,f(u)=0\bigr\}.

A point
u\in C
is
called
singular
or a

singularity
of
C
if

({\partial f}/{\partial u_{j}})(u)=0
for
j=1,2,3
. Observe
that
C
has at most finitely many singular points. These
are the singular points of the irreducible components of
C

together with the points of intersection of any two of these
components. A nonsingular point
u\in C
is called a

simple
point of
C
. The curve
C
is the topological
closure of its simple points. Also, to every simple point

u\in C
, there exists a neighbourhood of
u
in which
C

admits a smooth parametrization.

Let
C
be an irreducible plane algebraic curve with minimal
polynomial
f
. At each simple point
u\in C
, we have
a unique tangent line to
C
which is given by

{\mathcal{T}}_{u}C\ =\ \left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{1}}(u):\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{2}}(u):\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{3}}(u)\right].

If
C
is not a projective line or a point, then it is
well-known that the set

\bigl\{({\mathcal{T}}_{u}C)^{\pi}\,\bigm|\,u\in C\mbox{~simple}\bigr\}

is
contained in a unique irreducible algebraic curve
C^{*}
,
the so-called
dual curve
of
C
. In fact, since
an algebraic curve has at most finitely many singularities,
the dual curve is the topological closure of the set

\bigl\{({\mathcal{T}}_{u}C)^{\pi}\,\bigm|\,u\in C\mbox{ simple}\bigr\}
. We have

C^{**}=C
. If
C
is a projective line, then

\bigl\{({\mathcal{T}}_{u}C)^{\pi}\,\bigm|\,u\in C\bigr\}
consists of a single
point
u
in
\hbox{PG}({\mathbb{F}}^{3})
. In this case, we set

C^{*}=\{u\}
and define
C^{**}
to be the image under
\pi

of the set of all lines in
\hbox{PG}({\mathbb{F}}^{3})
which pass
through
u
. This again yields
C^{**}=C
. The dual curve
of a general plane algebraic curve
C
is the union of
the dual curves of its irreducible components. In
particular,
C
and
C^{*}
have the same number of
irreducible components.

In general, it is difficult to derive an explicit equation
for the dual curve
C^{*}
from the given equation of a
curve
C
. However, from the above we obtain the following
criterion for a point in
\hbox{PG}({\mathbb{F}}^{3})
to belong to
C^{*}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(x_{1}:x_{2}:1)\in G_{3,1}{\mathbb{F}}
. If there exists a smooth
local parametrization

\zeta\longmapsto(c(\zeta):s(\zeta):\mu(\zeta))

of
C
, for
\zeta
in an open set
U\subseteq{\mathbb{F}}
, and
a point
z\in U
such that
x_{1}c(z)+x_{2}s(z)+\mu(z)=0

and
x_{1}c^{\prime}(z)+x_{2}s^{\prime}(z)+\mu^{\prime}(z)=0
, then the
point
(x_{1}:x_{2}:1)
belongs to
C^{*}
.

Proof.

The two points
(c(z):s(z):\mu(z))
and

(c^{\prime}(z):s^{\prime}(z):\mu^{\prime}(z))
span the tangent line

{\mathcal{T}}_{(c(z):s(z):\mu(z))}C
to
C

at
(c(z):s(z):\mu(z))
. The equations

x_{1}c(z)+x_{2}s(z)+\mu(z)=0
and

x_{1}c^{\prime}(z)+x_{2}s^{\prime}(z)+\mu^{\prime}(z)=0
imply that

(x_{1}:x_{2}:1)=({\mathcal{T}}_{(c(z):s(z):\mu(z))}C)^{\pi}
.
Hence
(x_{1}:x_{2}:1)
belongs to the dual curve
C^{*}

of
C
.
∎

3.
Clifford Analysis

The basic idea of forming a
Clifford algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
with
n
generators
is to take the smallest real or complex algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
with an identity
element
e_{0}
such that
{\mathbb{R}}\oplus{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is embedded in
{\mathcal{A}}
via the
identification of
(x_{0},{\boldsymbol{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}\oplus{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with
x_{0}e_{0}+{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathcal{A}}

and the identity

{\boldsymbol{x}}^{2}=-|{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}e_{0}=-(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2})e_{0}

holds for all
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Then we arrive at the following
definition.

Let
{\mathbb{F}}
be either the field
{\mathbb{R}}
of real numbers or the
field
{\mathbb{C}}
of complex numbers. The
Clifford algebra

{\mathbb{F}}_{(n)}
over
{\mathbb{F}}
is a

2^{n}
-dimensional algebra with unit defined as follows. Given the standard basis vectors

e_{0},e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
of the vector space
{\mathbb{F}}^{n+1}
, the basis vectors
e_{S}
of

{\mathbb{F}}_{(n)}
are indexed by all finite subsets
S
of
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. The basis vectors are
determined by the following rules for multiplication on
{\mathbb{F}}_{(n)}
:

\displaystyle e_{0}=1,\qquad

\displaystyle e_{j}^{2}=-1,\qquad

\displaystyle{\rm for}\quad 1\leq j\leq n

\displaystyle e_{j}e_{k}=-e_{k}e_{j}=e_{\{j,k\}},\qquad

\displaystyle{\rm for}\quad 1\leq j<k\leq n

\displaystyle e_{j_{1}}e_{j_{2}}\cdots e_{j_{s}}=e_{S},\qquad

\displaystyle{\rm if}\quad 1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\dots<j_{s}\leq n

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad{\rm and}\quad S=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{s}\}.

Here the identifications
e_{0}=e_{\emptyset}
and
e_{j}=e_{\{j\}}
for
1\leq j\leq n
have been made.

Suppose that
m\leq n
are positive integers. The vector space
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is identified with a
subspace of
{\mathbb{F}}_{(n)}
by virtue of the embedding
(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\longmapsto\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{j}e_{j}
. On
writing the coordinates of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
as
x=(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
, the space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
is
identified with a subspace of
{\mathbb{F}}_{(n)}
with the embedding
(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\longmapsto\sum_{j=0}^{n}x_{j}e_{j}
.

The product of two elements
u=\sum_{S}u_{S}e_{S}
and
v=\sum_{S}v_{S}e_{S},v_{S}\in{\mathbb{F}}
with coefficients
u_{S}\in{\mathbb{F}}
and
v_{S}\in{\mathbb{F}}
is
uv=\sum_{S,R}u_{S}v_{R}e_{S}e_{R}
. According
to the rules for multiplication,

e_{S}e_{R}
is
\pm 1
times a basis vector of
{\mathbb{F}}_{(n)}
. The
scalar part
of
u=\sum_{S}u_{S}e_{S},u_{S}\in{\mathbb{F}}
is the term
u_{\emptyset}
, also denoted as
u_{0}
.

The Clifford algebras
{\mathbb{R}}_{(0)},{\mathbb{R}}_{(1)}
and
{\mathbb{R}}_{(2)}
are the real,

complex numbers and the
quaternions, respectively. In the case of
{\mathbb{R}}_{(1)}
, the vector
e_{1}
is identified with

i
and for
{\mathbb{R}}_{(2)}
, the basis vectors
e_{1},e_{2},e_{1}e_{2}
are
identified with
i,j,k
respectively.

The conjugate
\overline{{e_{S}}}
of a basis element
e_{S}
is defined so that
e_{S}\overline{e_{S}}=\overline{e_{S}}e_{S}=1
. Denote the complex conjugate of a number
c\in{\mathbb{F}}
by

\overline{c}
. Then the operation of
conjugation

u\longmapsto\overline{u}
defined by

\overline{u}=\sum_{S}\overline{u_{S}}\,\overline{e_{S}}
for every
u=\sum_{S}u_{S}e_{S},u_{S}\in{\mathbb{F}}
is an involution of the Clifford algebra
{\mathbb{F}}_{(n)}
and
\overline{vu}=\overline{u}\,\overline{v}
for all elements
u
and
v
of
{\mathbb{F}}_{(n)}
. Because
e_{j}^{2}=-1
, the conjugate

\overline{e_{j}}
of
e_{j}
is
-e_{j}
.
An inner product is defined on
{\mathbb{F}}_{(n)}
by
the formula
(u,v)=[u\overline{v}]_{0}=\sum u_{S}\overline{v_{S}}
for every
u=\sum_{S}u_{S}e_{S}

and
v=\sum_{S}v_{S}e_{S}
belonging to
{\mathbb{F}}_{(n)}
. The corresponding norm is written as
|\cdot|
.

For a Banach space
X
, the tensor product
X_{(n)}:=X\otimes{\mathbb{C}}_{(n)}
denotes the
module of all sums
u=\sum_{S}u_{S}e_{S}
with coefficients
u_{S}\in X
endowed with the norm

\|u\|_{X_{(n)}}=\left(\sum_{S}\|u_{S}\|_{X}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The left product

\lambda u
and right product
u\lambda
are defined in the obvious way for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}_{(n)}
.
The space
{\mathcal{L}}_{(n)}(X_{(n)})
of right-module homomorphisms is identified with
{\mathcal{L}}(X)_{(n)}
by writing

\left(\sum_{S}T_{S}e_{S}\right)\left(\sum_{S^{\prime}}u_{S^{\prime}}e_{S^{\prime}}\right)=\sum_{S}(T_{S}u_{S^{\prime}})e_{S}e_{S^{\prime}},

so that
T(u\lambda)=(Tu)\lambda
for all
u\in X_{(n)}
,
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}_{(n)}
and
T={\mathcal{L}}(X)_{(n)}
.
The linear subspace
\{Te_{0}:T\in{\mathcal{L}}(X)\}
of
{\mathcal{L}}(X)_{(n)}\equiv{\mathcal{L}}_{(n)}(X_{(n)})
is identified with
{\mathcal{L}}(X)
.
The norm induced on
{\mathcal{L}}_{(n)}(X_{(n)})\equiv{\mathcal{L}}(X)_{(n)}
by the norm of
X_{(n)}
is given by

\big\|\sum_{S}T_{S}e_{S}\big\|_{{\mathcal{L}}_{(n)}(X_{(n)})}=\left(\sum_{S}\|T_{S}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(X)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

For an
n
-tuple
{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
of bounded linear operators on
X
, it is convenient to write
\|{{\boldsymbol{A}}}\|
for the norm

\|A_{1}e_{1}+\dots+A_{n}e_{n}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}_{(n)}(X_{(n)})}=\big(\sum_{j}\|A_{j}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(X)}^{2}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

Because
x=(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
is identified
with the element
\sum_{j=0}^{n}x_{j}e_{j}
of
{\mathbb{R}}_{(n)}
, the conjugate
\overline{x}

of
x
in
{\mathbb{R}}_{(n)}
is
x_{0}e_{0}-x_{1}e_{1}-\cdots-x_{n}e_{n}
. A useful feature of Clifford algebras
is that a nonzero vector
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
has an inverse
x^{-1}
in the algebra

{\mathbb{R}}_{(n)}
(the
Kelvin inverse
) given by

x^{-1}=\frac{\overline{x}}{|x|^{2}}=\frac{x_{0}e_{0}-x_{1}e_{1}-\cdots-x_{n}e_{n}}{x_{0}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2}}.

The vector
x=(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
will often be written as
x=x_{0}e_{0}+{\boldsymbol{x}}
with

{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Because
{\mathbb{C}}_{(n)}
is an algebra over the complex numbers, the
spectrum

{\sigma}(i{\boldsymbol{x}})

of the vector
i{\boldsymbol{x}}
with
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is the set of all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
for which

\lambda e_{0}-i{\boldsymbol{x}}
is
not invertible
in
{\mathbb{C}}_{(n)}
. The formula

(\lambda e_{0}-i{\boldsymbol{x}})^{-1}=\frac{\lambda e_{0}+i{\boldsymbol{x}}}{\lambda^{2}-|{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}},\quad\lambda\neq\pm|{\boldsymbol{x}}|,

ensures that
{\sigma}(i{\boldsymbol{x}})=\{\pm|{\boldsymbol{x}}|\}
if
{\boldsymbol{x}}\neq 0
and
{\sigma}(0)=\{0\}
.
For
{\boldsymbol{x}}\neq 0
, the spectral representaion

i{\boldsymbol{x}}=|{\boldsymbol{x}}|\smash{\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{$\chi$}}_{+}({\boldsymbol{x}})+(-|{\boldsymbol{x}}|)\smash{\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{$\chi$}}_{-}({\boldsymbol{x}})

holds with respect to the spectral idempotents

\smash{\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{$\chi$}}_{\pm}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\left(e_{0}\pm i\frac{{\boldsymbol{x}}}{|{\boldsymbol{x}}|}\right).

The vector
i{\boldsymbol{x}}
is actually selfadjoint with respect to the inner product of
{\mathbb{C}}_{(n)}
defined above.
For every function
f:\{\pm|{\boldsymbol{x}}|\}\to{\mathbb{C}}
there is an element

(12)

f(i{\boldsymbol{x}})=f(|{\boldsymbol{x}}|)\smash{\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{$\chi$}}_{+}({\boldsymbol{x}})+f(-|{\boldsymbol{x}}|)\smash{\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{$\chi$}}_{-}({\boldsymbol{x}})

of
{\mathbb{C}}_{(n)}
associated with
f
by the functional calculus for selfadjoint operators.
For a polynomial
p(z)=a_{0}+a_{1}z+\dots+a_{k}z^{k}
, the expression

p(i{\boldsymbol{x}})=a_{0}e_{0}+a_{1}(i{\boldsymbol{x}})+\dots+a_{k}(i{\boldsymbol{x}})^{k}

expected in
{\mathbb{C}}_{(n)}
is obtained. The identities
\smash{\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{$\chi$}}_{{\mathbb{R}}_{\pm}}(i{\boldsymbol{x}})=\smash{\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{$\chi$}}_{\pm}({\boldsymbol{x}})

hold for the characteristic functions
\smash{\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{$\chi$}}_{{\mathbb{R}}_{\pm}}
of the half lines
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}=\{t>0\}

and
{\mathbb{R}}_{-}=\{t<0\}
.

Given an
n
-tuple of operators
{\boldsymbol{A}}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
acting on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
,
the expression
{\mathcal{A}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}A_{j}
acts on
{{\mathcal{H}}}_{(n)}:={\mathcal{H}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}_{(n)}
via the formula

{\mathcal{A}}u=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{S}(e_{j}e_{S})(A_{j}u_{S}),\quad u=\sum_{S}u_{S}e_{S}.

The coefficients
u_{S}
are elements of the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
and
u_{S}\otimes e_{S}
is written simply as
u_{S}e_{S}
for all
S\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}

.

Using Fourier theory, the functional calculus for the selfadjoint differential operator

{\mathcal{D}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}

acting in the Hilbert space
L^{2}_{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}):=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\otimes{\mathbb{C}}_{(n)}
can be calculated explicitly.

If
\hat{u}(\xi)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{-i\langle x,\xi\rangle}u(x)\,dx
denotes the Fourier transform of
u\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,
then according to the Fourier-Plancherel Theorem,
the linear map
u\longmapsto(2\pi)^{-n/2}\hat{u}
,
u\in L^{1}\cap L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, extends to an isometry of
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. For each
j=1,\dots,n
, the selfadjoint operator
\frac{1}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}

defined in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
satisfies

\left(\frac{1}{i}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}\right)\widehat{\phantom{\big|}}(\xi)=\xi_{j}\hat{u}(\xi)

almost everywhere for each
u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
in its domain. Furthermore, for any bounded measurable function
\varphi
defined on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the operator
\varphi\left(\frac{1}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}},\dots,\frac{1}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{n}}\right)
satisfies

\left(\varphi\left(\frac{1}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}},\dots,\frac{1}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{n}}\right)u\right)\widehat{\phantom{\big|}}(\xi)=\varphi(\xi)\hat{u}(\xi)

almost everywhere for
u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.
Similarly,

\big(f({\mathcal{D}})u)\big)\widehat{\phantom{|}}(\xi)=f(i\xi)\hat{u}(\xi),\quad u\in L^{2}_{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}),\ \xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

is valid for any bounded measurable function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
with the understanding that
for each
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the vector
f(i\xi)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is given by the functional calculus (
12
) of the selfadjoint element
i\xi
of the Clifford algebra
{\mathbb{C}}_{(n)}
.

What is usually called
Clifford analysis
is the
study of functions of finitely many real variables,
which take values in a Clifford algebra,
and which satisfy higher dimensional analogues of the
Cauchy-Riemann equations.

It is worthwhile to spell out the direction this analogy takes. The
Cauchy-Riemann equations for a complex valued function
f
defined
in an open subset of the complex plane may be represented as
\overline{\partial}f=0

for the operator

\overline{\partial}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+i\frac{\partial}{\partial y},\quad z=x+iy\in{\mathbb{C}}.

The fundamental solution
E
of the operator
\overline{\partial}
is the solution
in the sense of Schwartz distributions of the equation
\overline{\partial}E=\delta_{0}

for the unit point mass
\delta_{0}
at zero. Then

E(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\frac{1}{z}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\frac{\overline{z}}{|z|^{2}},\quad\hbox{for }z=x+iy\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}.

A function
f
satisfying
\overline{\partial}f=0
in a neighbourhood of a simple closed
contour
C
together with its interior can be represented as

f(z)=\frac{1}{i}\int_{C}E(\zeta-z)f({\zeta})\,d{\zeta}=\int_{C}E(\zeta-z){\boldsymbol{n}}({\zeta})f({\zeta})\,d|{\zeta}|

at all points
z
inside
C
. Here
{\boldsymbol{n}}({\zeta})
is the outward unit normal at
{\zeta}\in{\mathbb{C}}
,

d|{\zeta}|
is arclength measure so that
i{\boldsymbol{n}}({\zeta})d|{\zeta}|=d{\zeta}
.
The higher dimensional analogue for functions taking values in
a Clifford algebra is as follows.

A function
f:U\to{\mathbb{F}}_{(n)}
defined in an
open subset
U
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
has a unique representation
f=\sum_{S}f_{S}e_{S}
in terms of

{\mathbb{F}}
-valued functions
f_{S}
,
S\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
in the sense that
f(x)=\sum_{S}f_{S}(x)e_{S}
for all
x\in U
. Then
f
is continuous, differentiable and so on, in the
normed space
{\mathbb{F}}_{(n)}
, if and only if for all finite subsets
S
of
\{1,\dots,n\}
, its scalar
component functions
f_{S}
have the corresponding property. Let
\partial_{j}
be the operator of
differentiation of a scalar function in the

j
’th coordinate in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
– the coordinates of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
are written as
x=(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
. For a continuously differentiable function
f:U\to{\mathbb{F}}_{(n)}
with
f=\sum_{S}f_{S}e_{S}
defined in an
open subset
U
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
, the functions
Df
and
fD
are defined by

\displaystyle Df

\displaystyle=\sum_{S}\left((\partial_{0}f_{S})e_{S}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\partial_{j}f_{S})e_{j}e_{S}\right)

\displaystyle fD

\displaystyle=\sum_{S}\left((\partial_{0}f_{S})e_{S}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\partial_{j}f_{S})e_{S}e_{j}\right).

Similarly, the conjugate operator is defined by

\overline{D}=e_{0}\partial_{0}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}\partial_{j}

so that the Laplace operator
\Delta=\partial_{0}^{2}+\dots+\partial_{n}^{2}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
has factorizations

\Delta=D\overline{D}=\overline{D}D.

Now suppose that
f
is an
{\mathbb{F}}_{(n)}
-valued, continuously differentiable function defined in an
open subset
U
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
. Then
f
is said to be

left monogenic
in

U
if

Df(x)=0
for all
x\in U
and
right monogenic
in
U
if

fD(x)=0
for all
x\in U
.

The following result connects
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
-valued monogenic functions with systems of conjugate harmonic functions.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
F=u_{0}e_{0}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{j}e_{j}
be an
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
-valued function defined on an open subset
\Omega
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
. Conditions (1)-(4) below are equivalent in
\Omega
.

(1)

The
(n+1)
-tuple
U=(u_{j})_{j=0}^{n}
is a system of conjugate harmonic functions in
\Omega
, that is,
U
satisfies the generalised Cauchy-Riemann equations
\hbox{\rm div}\,U=0
and
\hbox{\rm curl}\,U=0
.

(2)

F
is left monogenic.

(3)

F
is right monogenic.

(4)

The 1-form
\omega:=u_{0}dx_{0}-u_{1}dx_{1}-\dots-u_{n}dx_{n}
satisfies
d{\omega}=0
and
d^{*}{\omega}=0
, where
d
and
d^{*}
are the exterior differential operator and its formal transpose, respectively.

(5)

In the case that
\Omega
is simply connected, then the above conditions are equivalent to
the existence of a real valued harmonic function
v
in
\Omega
such that
U=\hbox{\rm grad}\,v
, so that
F=\overline{D}v
.

For each
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
, the function
G({\,\cdot\,},x)
defined by

(13)

G(\omega,x)=G_{\omega}(x)=\frac{1}{\Sigma_{n}}\,\frac{\overline{\omega-x}}{|\omega-x|^{n+1}}

for every
\omega\neq x
is both left and right monogenic as a function of
\omega
. Here the volume

{2\pi^{\frac{n+1}{2}}/\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}
of the unit

n
-sphere in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
has been denoted by
\Sigma_{n}
and we have used the identification of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}

with a subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}_{(n)}
mentioned earlier.

The function
G({\,\cdot\,},x)
,
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
plays the role in Clifford analysis
of a
Cauchy kernel
. Rewriting
G(\omega,x)
as
E(\omega-x)
for all

&\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x|,&x\neq 0,\ n=1,\end{array}\right.

of the Laplace operator

.
Then a function satisfying

In terms of differential forms, the

4.
The Cauchy kernel

Armed with the Cauchy integral formula (
14
) for monogenic functions, formula
(
25
) is established for the
n
-tuple
{\boldsymbol{A}}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
of bounded linear operators on
a Banach space
X
by substituting the
n
-tuple
{\boldsymbol{A}}
for the vector
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
If
n
is odd and
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is a commutative
n
-tuple, that is,
A_{j}A_{k}=A_{k}A_{j}
for
j,k=1,\dots,n
,
and each operator
A_{j}
has real spectrum
{\sigma}(A_{j})\subset{\mathbb{R}}
for
j=1,\dots,n
, then for suitable
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
, the expression

(15)

G_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{A}})=\frac{1}{\Sigma_{n}}|\omega I-{\boldsymbol{A}}|^{-n-1}(\overline{\omega I-{\boldsymbol{A}}})

makes sense as an element of

{\mathcal{L}}_{(n)}(X_{(n)})
. For an even integer
m

|\omega I-{\boldsymbol{A}}|^{-m}=\bigg(\big(\omega_{0}^{2}I+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\omega_{j}I-A_{j})^{2}\big)^{-1}\bigg)^{m/2}

and

\overline{\omega I-{\boldsymbol{A}}}=\omega_{0}I-\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\omega_{j}I-A_{j})e_{j}
for
{\omega}={\omega}_{0}e_{0}+{\boldsymbol{{\omega}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
,

{\boldsymbol{{\omega}}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\omega}_{j}e_{j}
.

An appeal to the Spectral Mapping Theorem shows that the operator

\omega_{0}^{2}I+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\omega_{j}I-A_{j})^{2}

is invertible in
{\mathcal{L}}(X)
for each
\omega_{0}\neq 0
, so the
{\mathcal{L}}_{(n)}(X_{(n)})
-valued function

{\omega}\longmapsto G_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{A}})
is defined on the set
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\setminus(\{0\}\times{\gamma}({\boldsymbol{A}}))
with

{\gamma}({\boldsymbol{A}})=\left\{({\omega}_{1},\dots,{\omega}_{n}):\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\omega_{j}I-A_{j})^{2}\hbox{ is not invertible in }{\mathcal{L}}(X)\,\right\}.

Off
{\gamma}({\boldsymbol{A}})
, the function
{\omega}\longmapsto G_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{A}})
is left and right monogenic
and formula (
25
) defines a functional calculus
f\longmapsto f({\boldsymbol{A}})
which coincides with Taylor’s functional calculus
\tilde{f}\longmapsto\tilde{f}({\boldsymbol{A}})
. Any left monogenic function

f
defined in a neighbourhood
U
of
{\gamma}({\boldsymbol{A}})
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
has a holomorphic counterpart
\tilde{f}

defined in a neighborhood
\tilde{U}
of
{\gamma}({\boldsymbol{A}})
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
by taking the power series expansion about points of

U\cap(\{0\}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n})

[
9
]
. The left monogenic function
f
is referred to as
the
Cauchy-Kowaleski extension
of
\tilde{f}\restriction(\tilde{U}\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}

[
9
]
.

In the case of even
n=2,4,\dots
, the operator
|\omega I-{\boldsymbol{A}}|^{-n-1}
needs to be defined suitably. The direct formulation employs Taylor’s functional calculus, but by using the

plane wave decomposition
of the Cauchy kernel (see Section 4.2), the case of even
n
and noncommuting operators can be treated simultaneously.

For an
n
-tuple
{\boldsymbol{A}}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
of commuting bounded linear operators on
a Banach space
X
with real spectra, the nonempty compact subset
{\gamma}({\boldsymbol{A}})
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

coincides with Taylor’s joint spectrum defined in terms of the Koszul complex
[
37
, Definition III.6.4]
.

In general, the symbol
{\gamma}({\boldsymbol{A}})
is used to denote the set of points of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
in the complement of the domain where the Cauchy kernel
{\omega}\longmapsto G_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{A}})
is defined and monogenic in the Banach module
{\mathcal{L}}_{(n)}(X_{(n)})
. For a single operator
A
, its spectrum
{\sigma}(A)
is precisely the set of singularities
of the Cauchy kernel or resolvent
\lambda\longmapsto(\lambda I-A)^{-1}
for
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
, that is, the set of
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
for which
\lambda I-A
is not invertible in
{\mathcal{L}}(X)
.

A commuting
n
-tuple
{\boldsymbol{A}}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
of bounded selfadjoint operators on a Hilbert space
H
has a ready-made
functional calculus given by formula (
25
). The support of the joint spectral measure
P_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}

is naturally interpreted as the joint spectrum
{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{A}})
of
{\boldsymbol{A}}
. The observation that

{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{A}})
is actually the Gelfand spectrum of the commutative
C^{*}
-algebra generated by
{\boldsymbol{A}}
lends
credence to the interpretation of
{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{A}})
as the joint spectrum. By setting

G_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{A}})=\int_{{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{A}})}G_{\omega}(\lambda)\,dP_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}(\lambda)
for all
{\omega}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\setminus(\{0\}\times{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{A}}))
, it is easy to check by the vector valued version of Fubini’s Theorem, that
with the assumptions of formula (
25
) below for the left monogenic function
f:U\to{\mathbb{C}}_{(n)}
and the open set
\Omega
, the equalities

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}G_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{A}}){\boldsymbol{n}}({\omega})f({\omega})\,d\mu({\omega})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\left(\int_{{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{A}})}G_{\omega}(\lambda)\,dP_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}(\lambda)\right){\boldsymbol{n}}({\omega})f({\omega})\,d\mu({\omega})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{A}})}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}G_{\omega}(\lambda){\boldsymbol{n}}({\omega})f({\omega})\,d\mu({\omega})\right)\,dP_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{A}})}f(\lambda)\,dP_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f({\boldsymbol{A}})

hold. Furthermore
{\gamma}({\boldsymbol{A}})={\sigma}({\boldsymbol{A}})
.

4.1.
The Weyl calculus

There is an operator valued distribution
{\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
associated with any
n
-tuple
{\boldsymbol{A}}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
of selfadjoint operators on a Hilbert space
H
. Now the operators
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}

need not commute with each other. The distribution
{\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
is a substitute for the
joint spectral measure
P_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
. If
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is a commuting
n
-tuple of bounded selfadjoint
operators, then
{\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}:f\longmapsto\int_{{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{A}})}f\,dP_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
for all smooth functions
defined in a neighbourhood of
{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{A}})
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Suppose that
T:C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\to{\mathcal{L}}(X)
is an operator valued distribution with compact support supp
(T)
acting on a Banach space
X
. For any smooth
{\mathbb{C}}_{(n)}
-valued function function
f=\sum_{S}f_{S}e_{S}
defined in a neighborhood of supp
(T)
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the element
Tf
of
{\mathcal{L}}_{(n)}(X_{(n)})
is defined by

Tf=\sum_{S}e_{S}T(f_{S})

The Cauchy integral formula (
14
) may be viewed as an equality

f=\int_{\partial\Omega}G_{\omega}{\boldsymbol{n}}(\omega)f(\omega)\,d\mu(\omega)

between smooth
{\mathbb{C}}_{(n)}
-valued functions defined in a neighbourhood
U\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of the support of
T

when
f
is left monogenic on
U
, supp
(T)\subset\Omega
and
\overline{\Omega}\subset U
, so that

\displaystyle Tf

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T\int_{\partial\Omega}G_{\omega}{\boldsymbol{n}}(\omega)f(\omega)\,d\mu(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}T(G_{\omega}){\boldsymbol{n}}(\omega)f(\omega)\,d\mu(\omega).

The last inequality is a property of the Bochner integral for functions with
values in the Fréchet space
C^{\infty}_{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. The
{\mathcal{L}}_{(n)}(X_{(n)})
-valued function
{\omega}\longmapsto T(G_{\omega})

is left and right monogenic in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
away from supp
(T)
. If
\varphi
is a smooth function with compact support in a neighbourhood of supp
(T)
,
x\in X
and
\xi\in X^{\prime}
, then according to
[
9
, Theorem 27.7]
,

\langle(T\varphi)x,\xi\rangle=\lim_{t\to 0+}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\langle\left(T(G_{u+te_{0}})-T(G_{u-te_{0}})\right)x,\xi\rangle\varphi(u)\,du,

which may be compared with equation (
8
).

For any
n
-tuple
{\boldsymbol{A}}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
of bounded selfadjoint operators on a Hilbert space
H
, the Weyl functional calculus
is the
{\mathcal{L}}(H)
-valued distribution

(16)

{\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\left(e^{i\langle\xi,{\boldsymbol{A}}\rangle}\right)\hat{}\phantom{|}.

The operator
\langle\xi,{\boldsymbol{A}}\rangle=\langle{\boldsymbol{A}},\xi\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{j}A_{j}
is selfadjoint for each
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and the Fourier transform is taken with respect
\xi
in the sense of distributions. If
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is a commuting system, then the distribution
{\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
is integration with respect to the joint spectral measure
P_{\boldsymbol{A}}
.

Setting

G_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{A}})={\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}(G_{\omega}\restriction{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
for
{\omega}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\setminus(\{0\}\times\hbox{supp}({\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}))
, the equality
f({\boldsymbol{A}})={\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}(f\restriction{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
holds
for the element
f({\boldsymbol{A}})
of
{\mathcal{L}}_{(n)}(H_{(n)})
defined by formula (
25
) below and
{\gamma}({\boldsymbol{A}})=\hbox{supp}({\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}})
. E. Nelson
[
28
]
has identified
{\gamma}({\boldsymbol{A}})
with the Gelfand spectrum
of a certain commutative Banach algebra.

Example 4.1
.

Let
n=3
and consider the simplest noncommuting example of the Pauli
matrices,

(17)

\sigma_{1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right),\qquad\sigma_{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&{-i}\\
{i}&0\end{array}\right),\qquad\sigma_{3}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&{-1}\end{array}\right),

viewed as linear transformations acting on
H={\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. Set
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})
. Then

\langle\xi,{\boldsymbol{{\sigma}}}\rangle^{2}=|\xi|^{2}I
, so the exponential series gives

e^{it\langle\xi,{\boldsymbol{{\sigma}}}\rangle}=\cos(t|\xi|)I+i\langle\xi,{\boldsymbol{{\sigma}}}\rangle\frac{\sin(t|\xi|)}{|\xi|},\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Because
(2\pi)^{-3}({\sin(t|\xi|)}/|\xi|)\,\hat{}=t\mu_{t}
, with
\mu_{t}
the unit surface measure on the sphere

tS^{2}
of radius
t>0
centred at zero in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and

\cos(t|\xi|)=\frac{d}{ds}\,\frac{\sin(s|\xi|)}{|\xi|}\bigg|_{s=t},

for each
f\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
, the matrix
{\mathcal{W}}_{t{\boldsymbol{{\sigma}}}}(f)
is
given by

(18)

{\mathcal{W}}_{t{\boldsymbol{{\sigma}}}}(f)=I\int_{tS^{2}}\left(f+{\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot\nabla f\right)\,d\mu_{t}+t\int_{tS^{2}}{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}\cdot\nabla f\,d\mu_{t},\quad t>0.

Here
{\boldsymbol{n}}(x)
is
the outward unit normal at
x\in S^{2}
. Thus,
\hbox{\rm supp}\left({\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{{\sigma}}}}\right)=S^{2}
.

For all
\omega={\omega}_{0}+{\boldsymbol{{\omega}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
such that
{\boldsymbol{{\omega}}}\notin S^{2}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, the Cauchy kernel

G_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{{\sigma}}})\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbb{C}}^{2})_{(3)}

is given by

G_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{{\sigma}}})={\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{{\sigma}}}}(G_{\omega})=I\int_{S^{2}}\left(G_{\omega}+{\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot\nabla G_{\omega}\right)\,d\mu+\int_{S^{2}}{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}\cdot\nabla G_{\omega}d\mu.

By the operator valued version of the Paley-Wiener Theorem (see
[
31
, Theorem 7.23]
for the scalar version), the distribution (
16
) exists
for
n
-tuple
{\boldsymbol{A}}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
of bounded linear operators on a Banach space
X
provided that
the exponential growth estimate

(19)

\left\|e^{i\langle{\zeta},{\boldsymbol{A}}\rangle}\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(X)}\leq C(1+|\xi|)^{s}e^{r|\eta|},\quad{\zeta}=\xi+i\eta,\ \xi,\eta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

for
\langle{\zeta},{\boldsymbol{A}}\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\zeta}_{j}A_{j}
holds, for some positive numbers
C,s,r
independent of
{\zeta}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Then supp
({\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}})
is contained in the ball of radius
r>0
centred at zero in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
The estimate (
19
) holds if
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is finite system of simultaneously triangularisable matrices with real eigenvalues
[
21
, Theorem 5.10]
.

The Weyl functional calculus
{\mathcal{W}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
has the property that operator products are

symmetrically
ordered. For an
n
-tuple
{\boldsymbol{A}}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
of bounded selfadjoint operators on a Hilbert space
H
, other choices of operator ordering define an operator valued distribution
{\mathcal{F}}_{{\boldsymbol{A}},{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}
. The weighting for operator products is determined by an
n
-tuple

of continuous Borel probability measures on
[0,1]

[

.

The collection of single operators

satisfying the bound (
19
) is called the class of

and these have been extensively studied
[
13
]
.

4.2.
Plane wave decomposition of the Cauchy kernel

The exponential growth estimate (
19
) leads to a
C^{\infty}
-functional calculus
{\mathcal{W}}_{\boldsymbol{A}}
, so it is desirable to have a condition weaker than (
19
) for which the Cauchy kernel can be defined in a way that agrees with the preceding definition.

It turns out that it suffices for
{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
to be
hyperbolic
to make sense of the Cauchy kernel
G_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{A}})
in the
Cauchy integral formula (
25
). For
matrices
, this is equivalent to the bound (
19
), because
as noted in the Introduction it says that

I\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}

is a
hyperbolic differential operator
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
.

The key to constructing the Cauchy kernel
G_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{A}})
for a hyperbolic
n
-tuple
{\boldsymbol{A}}
of bounded linear operators is the plane wave decomposition of the fundamental solution

E:x\longmapsto\frac{1}{\Sigma_{n}}\frac{\overline{x}}{|x|^{n+1}},\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\},

of the generalised Cauchy-Riemann operator
D=\sum_{j=0}^{n}e_{j}\partial_{j}
. The plane wave decomposition of
E
was first
given by F. Sommen
[
34
]
and is most simply realised with the proof of Li, McIntosh, Qian
[
27
]
using
Fourier analysis.
The unit hypersphere
S^{n-1}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is the set
\{s\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:|s|=1\,\}
.

The Fourier transform of
u\in L^{1}\cap L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is
\hat{u}(\xi)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{-i\langle x,\xi\rangle}u(x)\,dx

and the inverse map
u(x)=(2\pi)^{-n}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{i\langle x,\xi\rangle}\hat{u}(\xi)\,d\xi
recovers
u
from
\hat{u}
when
\hat{u}\in L^{1}\cap L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. For any
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the linear function
{\boldsymbol{x}}\longmapsto i\langle{\boldsymbol{x}},\xi\rangle
,
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, defined in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
extends monogenically to
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
to the function

x\longmapsto i\langle{\boldsymbol{x}},\xi\rangle e_{0}-i{\boldsymbol{x}}x_{0},\quad x=x_{0}e+{\boldsymbol{x}},x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}},\ {\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

According to the functional calculus for the selfadjoint element
i{\boldsymbol{x}}
of
{\mathbb{C}}_{(n)}
,
the unique monogenic extension of the function
{\boldsymbol{x}}\longmapsto e^{i\langle{\boldsymbol{x}},\xi\rangle}
,
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, is given by

\displaystyle\exp(i\langle{\boldsymbol{x}},\xi\rangle e_{0}-i{\boldsymbol{x}}x_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i\langle{\boldsymbol{x}},\xi\rangle-|{\boldsymbol{x}}|x_{0}}\smash{\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{$\chi$}}_{+}({\boldsymbol{x}})+e^{i\langle{\boldsymbol{x}},\xi\rangle+|{\boldsymbol{x}}|x_{0}}\smash{\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{$\chi$}}_{-}({\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{+}(x,\xi)+e_{-}(x,\xi)

for
x=x_{0}e+{\boldsymbol{x}}
with
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
x=x_{0}e_{0}+{\boldsymbol{x}}
be an element of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}

with
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

(20)

If
x_{0}<0
, then

(21)

E(x)=(-1)^{n+1}\frac{(n-1)!}{2}\left(\frac{i}{2\pi}\right)^{n}\int_{S^{n-1}}(e_{0}+is)\left(\langle{{\boldsymbol{x}}},s\rangle-x_{0}s\right)^{-n}\/ds.

Now suppose that
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is a hyperbolic
n
-tuple of bounded linear operators on a Banach space

. Then

\langle{{\boldsymbol{A}}},s\rangle-x_{0}sI
is an element of the space
{\mathcal{L}}_{(n)}(X_{(n)})
of module homomorphisms for each

When

and

(aI-\langle{{\boldsymbol{A}}},s\rangle)e_{0}-x_{0}sI

Because
{\sigma}(\langle\xi,{\boldsymbol{A}}\rangle)\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, the Spectral Mapping Theorem ensures that
the bounded linear operator
(aI-\langle{{\boldsymbol{A}}},s\rangle)^{2}+x_{0}^{2}I
is invertible and

{\sigma}((aI-\langle{{\boldsymbol{A}}},s\rangle)^{2}+x_{0}^{2}I)=\varphi_{a}({\sigma}(\langle s,{{\boldsymbol{A}}}\rangle))

for the function

,
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Moreover,

((aI-\langle{{\boldsymbol{A}}},s\rangle)e_{0}-x_{0}sI)^{-n}=\left((aI-\langle{{\boldsymbol{A}}},s\rangle)e_{0}+x_{0}sI\right)^{n}((aI-\langle{{\boldsymbol{A}}},s\rangle)^{2}+x_{0}^{2}I)^{-n}

in
{\mathcal{L}}_{(n)}(X_{(n)})
. To define
G_{x}({\boldsymbol{A}})
, the promised substitution
{\boldsymbol{y}}\longrightarrow{\boldsymbol{A}}

in the Cauchy kernel
G_{x}({\boldsymbol{y}})=E(x-{\boldsymbol{y}})
is now made by setting

(22)

Note that for

4.3.
The McIntosh functional calculus

For a general matrix or operator
A
, the Riesz-Dunford formula

(24)

f(A)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C}(\lambda I-A)^{-1}f(\lambda)\,d\lambda

is valid for all functions
f
holomorphic in a neighbourhood of the spectrum
{\sigma}(A)
in the complex plane.
The simple closed contour
C
surrounds
{\sigma}(A)
and is contained in the domain of
f
.
The higher-dimensional analogue of the Riesz-Dunford formula (
24

f({\boldsymbol{A}})=\int_{\partial\Omega}G_{x}({\boldsymbol{A}}){\boldsymbol{n}}(x)f(x)\,d\mu(x)

for the hyperbolic
n
-tuple
{\boldsymbol{A}}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
of bounded linear operators on
a Banach space
X
, simply by substituting the
n
-tuple
{\boldsymbol{A}}
for the vector

in the Cauchy integral formula (

paid is that Clifford regular functions have values in the Clifford algebra

{\mathbb{C}}_{(n)}
, which is noncommutative for
n=2,3,\dots
and the correspondence between Clifford regular functions and their holomorphic counterparts needs to be investigated. The algebra
{\mathbb{R}}_{(1)}

is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{C}}
and formulas (
25
) and (
24

in the case
n=1
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is a hyperbolic
n
-tuple of bounded linear operators on a Banach space

so that

is defined by the plane wave decomposition.
If

[

The
