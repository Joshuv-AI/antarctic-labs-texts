Plücker Coordinates and the Rosenfeld Planes
Jian Qiu

Abstract
The exceptional compact hermitian symmetric space EIII is the quotient E 6 / S p i n ( 10 ) × ℤ 4 U ( 1 ) E_{6}/Spin(10)\times_{\mathbb{Z}_{4}}U(1) . We introduce the Plücker coordinates which give an embedding of EIII into ℂ ​ P 26 \mathbb{C}P^{26} as a projective subvariety. The subvariety is cut out by 27 Plücker relations. We show that, using Clifford algebra, one can solve this over-determined system of relations, giving local coordinate charts to the space. Our motivation is to understand EIII as the complex projective octonion plane ( ℂ ⊗ 𝕆 ) ​ P 2 (\mathbb{C}\otimes\mathbb{O})P^{2} , whose construction is somewhat scattered across the literature. We will see that the EIII has an atlas whose transition functions have clear octonion interpretations, apart from those covering a sub-variety X ∞ X_{\infty} of dimension 10. This subvariety is itself a hermitian symmetric space known as DIII, with no apparent octonion interpretation. We give detailed analysis of the geometry in the neighbourhood of X ∞ X_{\infty} . We further decompose X = EIII X={\rm EIII} into F 4 F_{4} -orbits: X = Y 0 ∪ Y ∞ X=Y_{0}\cup Y_{\infty} , where Y 0 ∼ ( 𝕆 ​ P 2 ) ℂ Y_{0}\sim(\mathbb{O}P^{2})_{\mathbb{C}} is an open F 4 F_{4} -orbit and is the complexification of 𝕆 ​ P 2 \mathbb{O}P^{2} , whereas Y ∞ Y_{\infty} has co-dimension 1, thus EIII could be more appropriately denoted as ( 𝕆 ​ P 2 ) ℂ ¯ \overline{(\mathbb{O}P^{2})_{\mathbb{C}}} . This decomposition appears in the classification of equivariant completion of homogeneous algebraic varieties by Ahiezer [ Ahi83 ] .

1
Introduction

According to the classification of compact hermitian symmetric spaces (chapter 8
[
Wol11
]
), there are four infinite families denoted AIII:
SU(p+q)/S(U(p)\times U(q))
, DIII:
SO(2n)/U(n)
, CI:
Sp(n)/U(n)
and BDI
2
:
SO(p+q)/SO(p)\times SO(q)
for
p
or
q
equal to 2. There are also two exceptional ones denoted EIII and EVII that are quotients of
E_{6}
and
E_{7}
respectively

\displaystyle{\rm EIII}:~~E_{6}/Spin(10)\times_{\mathbb{Z}_{4}}U(1);~~~~{\rm EVII}:~~E_{7}/E_{6}\times U(1).

(1.1)

What is interesting about these spaces is that they all possess a bi-hamiltonian integrable structure.
In
[
BQTb
]
, we studied the integrability of the four classical cases A, D, C and BD and gave a uniform treatment of the involutive hamiltonians. However, the exceptional case turned out quite recalcitrant, only a partial solution was given in
[
BQTV22
]
. In a coming paper
[
BQTa
]
, we solve the EIII case.

The current paper serves two purposes. Firstly, in solving EIII, we made crucial use of a very useful coordinate system which we call the
Plücker coordinates
due to their similarity with the namesake coordinates on the Grassmannians. That such coordinates should exist follows directly from the fact that the space is a projective variety. But to use these coordinates effectively for the calculation, we need to made them very concrete. This is what is done in this paper, in a way, this paper serves as a tech depot for
[
BQTa
]
.

Secondly, in the process of the calculation, we become interested in the relationship of EIII and EVII to the real octonion projective planes

\displaystyle\mathbb{O}\mathbb{P}^{2}=F_{4}/Spin(9).

(1.2)

More specifically, EIII is expected to be
(\mathbb{C}\otimes\mathbb{O})P^{2}
while EVII is related to
(\mathbb{H}\otimes\mathbb{O})P^{2}
.

The projective space (
1.2
) has a construction using exceptional Jordan algebra of
3\times 3
octonion matrices.
But similar constructions for
(\mathbb{C}\otimes\mathbb{O})P^{2}
or
(\mathbb{H}\otimes\mathbb{O})P^{2}
remain folklore and are dispersed in the literature.
One can complexify/modify the previous octonion matrix in such a way that the entries are precisely the Plücker coordinates, while the Plücker relations express certain co-linearity. We refer the reader to sec 4.4 of
[
Bae02
]
for a review on the current status of the subject.

We will review the fact that for
X
=EIII, there are 27 Plücker coordinates that induce an embedding of
X
into
\mathbb{C}P^{26}
.
The image of the embedding is cut out by 27 algebraic equations: the Plücker relations.
We will proceed to use these coordinates to analyse the geometry of the space. In particular, we give coordinate charts to
X
and the transition functions in between. Our hope was that the transition functions would allow for a clear octonion interpretation, and thereby justifying
X
being called the complex octonion projective plane. But we found, quite interestingly, that there is a locus
X_{\infty}\subset X
of complex dimension 10, which is itself a hermitian symmetric space

\displaystyle X\supset X_{\infty}=DIII=SO(10)/U(5),

i.e. it is the Grassmannian of Lagrangian subspace in
\mathbb{R}^{10}
.
The coordinate chart in the neighbourhood of
X_{\infty}
does not seem to permit a natural interpretation as octonions.

Though the exceptional groups have elegant octonion constructions, however, octonions remain unfamiliar to most people. Even for experts, they can be unwieldy for technical computations. In this paper, we rather approach the exceptional groups using Adam’s construction, based on spinors and Clifford algebra. That Clifford algebra should enter is not surprising:
the triality of the representation of
Spin(8)
is what underlies the octonion algebra. In our approach the representations of
Spin(10)
will be crucial in terms of organising the computation and making the Plücker coordinates/relations concrete.

To make everything easily accessible for the reader, we have a large appendix, reviewing the essentials of the Clifford algebra, Spin groups and how the exceptional groups arise from these.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows:
We will first review the familiar Plücker coordinates of Grassmannians and then move to the exceptional case. Here the 27 dimensional representation of
E_{6}
plays a star role. In fact, 27 is the dimension of a
3\times 3
hermitian octonion matrix, while the Jordan algebra structure arises from a cubic invariant tensor of the 27 dimensional representation.
We then obtain the coordinate patches for
X
and the transition function between the patches. We further study the
F_{4}
-decomposition of
X
, and show that
X
is the equivariant completion of the complexification of the octonion projective plane.
The punch line of the article is that EIII is the completion of the naive complexification of
\mathbb{O}\mathbb{P}^{2}=F_{4}/Spin(9)
in the sense of
[
Ahi83
]
(see prop.
3.12
). Therefore

\displaystyle{\rm EIII}=E_{6}/Spin(10)\times_{\mathbb{Z}_{4}}U(1)=\overline{(\mathbb{O}P^{2})_{\mathbb{C}}}.

(1.3)

-invariant. In fact, a second way of realising

.

A third way is to simply use the inhomogeneous coordinates. For

Summary and Outlook

This paper’s main technical result is the Plücker coordinates that embed the space EIII in
\mathbb{C}P^{26}
. These Plücker coordinates satisfy some quadratic relations, in the same way as the classical Grassmannian case.

The central conceptual result is the precise sense in which EIII can be interpreted as the complex octonion projective plane: the naive complexification of
\mathbb{O}P^{2}
needs completion by adding another
F_{4}
-orbit at infinity. This structural result is new, to the best of my knowledge.

The
E_{6}
group has an important role in theoretical physics, e.g. the grand unified theories (GUT): its gauge group
E_{6}
contains a possible GUT gauge group
SO(10)
. So when
E_{6}
is (spontaneously) broken to this subgroup, the vev of the scalar fields responsible for the breaking would live in a manifold closely related to our quotient space
E_{6}/Spin(10)\times_{\mathbb{Z}_{4}}U(1)
. The geometry of this quotient directly dictates the low energy physics, so any means that gives us a better understanding of its geometry should be helpful in some way.

We thank the referee for pointing out further applications in supergravity.
Though we have only been considering the compact
E_{6}
, part of our results can be readily generalised to the non-compact form
E_{6(-14)}
and the quotient symmetric space
E_{6(-14)}/Spin(10)\times_{\mathbb{Z}_{4}}U(1)
. This space appears as the (extended) scalar manifold of
{\cal N}=10
supergravity in
D=2+1
(see table 11 of
[
FM08
]
). Furthermore the same quotient appears as the moduli space of the ‘non-BPS
Z=0
’ critical points of the black hole effective
potential in the ‘magical exceptional’
{\cal N}=2
Maxwell-Einstein (ungauged) supergravity
[
GST83
]
in

D=3+1
. In this context,
E_{6(-14)}/Spin(10)\times_{\mathbb{Z}_{4}}U(1)
is characterised
as a proper submanifold of the special Kähler symmetric vector multiplets’ scalar manifold

E_{7(-25)}/E_{6}\times U(1)
(see table 2 of
[
FM08
]
), which in turn is the Riemannian, totally non-compact form of the
Hermitian symmetric space EVII.

The referee also brought
[
TB82
]
to our attention. The authors of that paper defined a Jordan pair, i.e. a pair of
3\times 3
complex hermitian octonion matrices with some idempotency consditions. There are three types of such idempotents, type I consists of pairs
(\Psi,\Phi)\in{\bf 27}\times{\bf 27}^{*}
, such that both
\Psi
and
\Phi
satisfy the Plücker relations and
\langle\Psi,\Phi\rangle=1
.
Upon normalising
|\Psi|^{2}=|\Phi|^{2}=1
, then
\Psi=\Phi^{*}
, that is, one can also describe EIII as normalised the Jordan pairs of type I.

Take two normalised pairs of idempotents
x_{i}=(\Psi_{i},\Phi_{i})
,
i=1,2
. Then they define both points
p(x_{i})
and lines
\ell(x_{i})
.
The points are just the line
[\Psi_{i}]
(upon projectivisation) and so concurs with the description of the current paper. As for the lines, one says that
p(x_{1})
is incident to
\ell(x_{2})
iff
(\Psi_{1}\diamond\Psi_{2})\diamond\Phi_{2}=\Psi_{1}
, where
\diamond
is the product introduced in prop.
B.6
.

It was shown in
[
TB82
]
that one can have two points in certain configuration (called connected) such that more than one line can pass both points. Dually, two lines may also intersect at more than one point. Thus, despite the name octonion projective plane, the geometry does not satisfy the axioms of projective planes. The curious incidence/intersection relations also lead to novel quantum logic. The formulation in
[
TB82
]
was in terms of octonion matrices, while our Plücker coordinate (in particular, manifestly
E_{6}
covariant) approach seems to simplify many calculations. We hope to explore this further in some future work.

Spinors and Exceptional Groups

We follow the treatment of exceptional Lie algebras in
[
AMM96
]
. This treatment relies on manipulations of Clifford algebra and spinors extensively, we encourage the reader to take a look at chapter 2,3,4 in
[
AMM96
]
. Here we just gather the minimal amount of material for setting the notations.

A.1
Clifford Algebra

The main reference here would be the classic work
[
ABS64
]
.
Let
V=\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
\{e^{i},\;i=1,\cdots,m\}
as its standard basis. The Clifford algebra is defined as the quotient of the tensor algebra
T(V)

\displaystyle Cl(V)=T(V)/J,~~~J=\langle v\otimes v-(v\cdotp v)1|v\in V\rangle,

where
\cdotp
is the standard inner-product of
V
.
We will omit the tensor symbol
\otimes
in the following. Put simply,
Cl(V)
consists of words generated by the alphabet
\{e^{i}\}
, with the relation
e^{i}e^{j}+e^{j}e^{i}=2\delta^{ij}
.
Here our convention is slightly different from
[
ABS64
]
, which has
e^{i}e^{j}+e^{j}e^{i}=-2\delta^{ij}
, this entails a small modification of def.
A.1
below.

In view of the fact that the relation reduces word length, we have the isomorphism of vector spaces

\displaystyle Cl(V)\simeq\oplus_{0\leq p\leq m}\wedge^{p}V.

Since the relation
J
preserves the tensor grading mod 2, we let
Cl_{0}(V)
and
Cl_{1}(V)
be the even and odd part of
Cl(V)
. Let
\alpha:\,Cl(V)\to Cl(V)
be the automorphism that adds a negative sign to
Cl_{1}
but leaves
Cl_{0}
untouched. We also define an anti-automorphism
()^{t}
, for
x=e^{i_{1}}\cdots e^{i_{k}}
a monomial, set

\displaystyle x^{t}=e^{i_{k}}\cdots e^{i_{1}}.

(A.1)

Lastly let
Cl^{\times}(V)
be the set of invertible elements.

Given
x\in Cl^{\times}(V)
, we have a twisted adjoint action

\displaystyle\rho(x):\;Cl(V)\to Cl(V),~~~y\to\alpha(x)yx^{-1}.

(A.2)

Define the Clifford group
\Gamma(V)
as the set

\displaystyle\Gamma(V)=\{x\in Cl^{\times}(V)|\rho(x)V\subset V\}.

Set also

\displaystyle N(x)=x\alpha(x^{t}).

Prop.3.8 of
[
ABS64
]
shows that if
x\in\Gamma(V)
, then
N(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
.

Definition A.1
.

The Pin group is defined as

\displaystyle Pin(V)=\{x\in\Gamma(V)|N(x)=\pm 1\}.

The Spin group is defined as
Spin(V)=Pin(V)\cap Cl_{0}(V)
. It is a double cover of
SO(V)
.

Remark A.2
.

One can be even more specific in describing
Pin(V)
or
Spin(V)
. The former consists of monomials of
x=x_{1}\cdots x_{k}
with
x_{i}
of unit length, and the latter are those monomials with
k
even.
Indeed, such monomials fulfil the definition above. On the other hand, the transformation
\alpha(x)vx^{-1}=\alpha(x)vx^{t}
is given by reflecting
v
across the plane perpendicular to
x_{i}
. It is well-known that any
O(V)
can be written as a succession of such reflections.

We will only deal with
V
of even dimension, so we let
m=2n
.

Example A.3
.

(Compare with table 1 in
[
ABS64
]
)

The Dirac representation
\Delta
of the Clifford algebra has dimension
2^{n}
. If we set
\Delta=\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
, then the generators
\{e^{i}\}
of the Clifford algebra have the matrix representation

\displaystyle e_{1}=\sigma_{1}\otimes 1\otimes\cdots\otimes 1,

\displaystyle e_{2}=\sigma_{2}\otimes 1\otimes\cdots\otimes 1,

\displaystyle e_{3}=\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{1}\otimes 1\otimes\cdots\otimes 1,

\displaystyle e_{4}=\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{2}\otimes 1\otimes\cdots\otimes 1,

\displaystyle\cdots

\displaystyle e_{2n-1}=\sigma_{3}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{1},

\displaystyle e_{2n}=\sigma_{3}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{2},

(A.3)

where there are
n
tensor factors, and
\sigma_{1,2,3}
are the Pauli matrices

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},~~\sigma_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{bmatrix},~~\sigma_{3}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}.

But
\Delta
is reducible as a representation of
Spin(V)
. One can decompose it into
\Delta=\Delta^{+}\oplus\Delta^{-}
of dimension
2^{n-1}
each.
In physics literature,
\Delta^{\pm}
are denoted as right/left handed spinors, and are characterised by their eigen-value under the ‘chirality operator’

\displaystyle\gamma=(-i)^{n}e_{1}e_{2}\cdots e_{2n},~~~\gamma^{2}=1,~~\gamma\Delta^{\pm}=\pm\Delta^{\pm}.

(A.4)

The choice of
(-i)^{n}
will become clear later. The important property of
\gamma
is

\displaystyle\{\gamma,e^{i}\}=0

thus an odd number of
e^{i}
would flip the chirality, however
\mathfrak{spin}(V)
has the form
e^{i}e^{j}
and so preserves the chirality.

The conjugate (or dual) representation of
\Delta^{\pm}
are not new ones, but rather

\displaystyle(\Delta^{\pm})^{*}\simeq\Delta^{\pm},~~n~\operatorname{even},

\displaystyle(\Delta^{\pm})^{*}\simeq\Delta^{\mp},~~n~\operatorname{odd},

(A.5)

see prop.4.3 in
[
AMM96
]
. For us, it is important to know the isomorphism above explicitly. We define the ‘charge conjugation’ matrices
C
, according to the physics terminology

\displaystyle n~\operatorname{even}:

\displaystyle C=e_{2}e_{4}\cdots e_{2n},

\displaystyle n~\operatorname{odd}:

\displaystyle C=(-1)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}e_{1}e_{3}\cdots e_{2n-1}.

The
C
matrices are chosen so that

\displaystyle C^{-1}x^{T}C=x^{t}.

(A.6)

The notation here is:
we have identified
x\in Cl(V)
with the matrix representing it, so the left
T
is the matrix transposition, while the right
t
is the anti-automorphism (
A.1
).

Note that all the
e^{i}
’s are represented as Hermitian matrices and so transposition is the same as conjugation. So we can define the charge conjugation that explicitly realises the isomorphism (
A.5
)

\displaystyle n~\operatorname{even}:

\displaystyle\Delta^{\pm}\ni\phi\to C\phi^{*}\in\Delta^{\pm}

(A.7)

\displaystyle n~\operatorname{odd}:

\displaystyle\Delta^{\pm}\ni\phi\to C\phi^{*}\in\Delta^{\mp}.

(A.8)

where
*
is the complex conjugation. In particular for
\dim=4k+2
, the charge conjugation flips chirality, while for
\dim=4k
, the chirality is preserved. Thus one may well ask if one can impose the reality for
\Delta^{+}
and
\Delta^{-}
, i.e. if there exists
\psi\in\Delta^{+}
with
C\psi^{*}=\psi
. This turns out only possible if
\dim=8k
.
For this work 8D spinors play a lead role and the fact that we can have real left/right handers of real dimension 8, and hence identical to the dimension of the vector representation of
SO(8)
, is crucial. This is known as the
triality
and responsible for the octonion multiplication rules.

As a vector space
\mathfrak{spin}(V)=\mathfrak{so}(V)
is generated abstractly by
\{x^{pq}|x^{pq}=-x^{qp}\}
, with the Lie bracket

\displaystyle[x^{pq},x^{rs}]=\delta^{qr}x^{ps}+\textrm{anti-sym in}\;[pq][rs].

The following definition shows how the
\mathfrak{spin}
action covers the
\mathfrak{so}
action.

Definition A.4
.

The action of the Lie algebra
x^{pq}=e^{p}\wedge e^{q}\in\mathfrak{spin}(V)
on
\Delta
or
\Delta^{\pm}
is via the Clifford multiplication

\displaystyle x^{pq}\circ\psi=\frac{1}{4}e^{[p}e^{q]}\psi.

On
V
, we deduce from (
A.2
) that
x^{pq}
acts as the commutator of Clifford algebra

\displaystyle x^{pq}\circ v=[\frac{1}{4}e^{[p}e^{q]},v].

We use
\langle\;,\;\rangle
to denote the Hermitian inner-product for
\Delta^{+}
and
\Delta^{-}
, i.e.
\langle\psi,\phi\rangle=\psi^{{\dagger}}\phi
for
\psi,\phi
of the same chirality. The dualities (
A.7
), (
A.8
) provides another non-degenerate bi-linear pairing

\displaystyle n~\operatorname{even}:

\displaystyle\Delta^{\pm}\otimes\Delta^{\pm}\ni\phi\otimes\psi\to(\phi,\psi):=\phi^{T}C\psi

(A.9)

\displaystyle n~\operatorname{odd}:

\displaystyle\Delta^{\pm}\otimes\Delta^{\mp}\ni\phi\otimes\psi\to(\phi,\psi):=\phi^{T}C\psi.

(A.10)

We will often omit the commas and write
(\phi\psi)
or
\langle\phi\psi\rangle
instead.

A.2
Tensor Product of Spinors and Fierz Identities

The tensor product of two spin representations can be decomposed into direct sums of tensor representations of

, see theorem 4.6
[

]
. Here we list some decompositions relevant for us

\displaystyle\dim=16:

\displaystyle\Delta^{\pm}\otimes\Delta^{\pm}={\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\wedge^{8}_{\pm}V^{*}}\oplus{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\wedge^{6}V^{*}}\oplus{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\wedge^{4}V^{*}}\oplus{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\wedge^{2}V^{*}}\oplus{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bf 1},

\displaystyle\Delta^{-}\otimes\Delta^{+}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\wedge^{7}V^{*}}\oplus{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\wedge^{5}V^{*}}\oplus{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\wedge^{3}V^{*}}\oplus{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V^{*}}

\displaystyle\dim=8:

\displaystyle\Delta^{\pm}\otimes\Delta^{\pm}={\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\wedge^{4}_{\pm}V^{*}}\oplus{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\wedge^{2}V^{*}}\oplus{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bf 1},

\displaystyle\Delta^{-}\otimes\Delta^{+}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\wedge^{3}V^{*}}\oplus{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V^{*}},

\displaystyle\dim=10:

where the blue summands are symmetric under the exchange of the two factors on the lhs, while the red ones are anti-symmetric.

Remark A.5
.

Explicitly, the mappings above are given as follows. Let, say
\dim=16
,
\phi\in\Delta^{-}
and

, then

\displaystyle e^{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge e^{i_{k}}\mapsto(\phi,e^{i_{1}}\cdots e^{i_{k}}\psi)

gives the map

(for

While for

now involves odd number of

We will not go into the proof of the decompositions (

A.12

The rhs of (

The

are some seemingly magical identities involving the spinor bilinears, responsible for some miraculous cancellations that make certain supersymmetric field theories free of ultra-violet divergence.
But magic does not exist: the Fierz identities are but some manifestations of representation theory of

To start, take

and

\displaystyle(\Delta^{+}\otimes\Delta^{+})\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bigotimes$}}(\Delta^{+}\otimes\Delta^{+})=(\wedge^{4}_{\pm}V^{*}\oplus\wedge^{2}V^{*}\oplus{\bf 1})\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bigotimes$}}(\wedge^{4}_{\pm}V^{*}\oplus\wedge^{2}V^{*}\oplus{\bf 1})\mapsto{\bf 1}\oplus{\bf 1}\oplus{\bf 1},

\displaystyle(\psi_{1}\otimes\psi_{2})\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bigotimes$}}(\psi_{3}\otimes\psi_{4})\mapsto\frac{1}{4!}(\psi_{1}e^{ijkl}\psi_{2})(\psi_{3}e^{ijkl}\psi_{4})\oplus\frac{1}{2!}(\psi_{1}e_{ij}\psi_{2})(\psi_{3}e^{ij}\psi_{4})\oplus(\psi_{1}\psi_{2})(\psi_{3}\psi_{4}),

\displaystyle(\psi_{1}\otimes\psi_{4})\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bigotimes$}}(\psi_{3}\otimes\psi_{2})\to\frac{1}{4!}(\psi_{1}e^{ijkl}\psi_{4})(\psi_{3}e^{ijkl}\psi_{2})\oplus\frac{1}{2!}(\psi_{1}e^{ij}\psi_{4})(\psi_{3}e^{ij}\psi_{2})\oplus(\psi_{1}\psi_{4})(\psi_{3}\psi_{2}).

Proposition A.12

The multiplication satisfies

\displaystyle u\star\bar{u}=u\cdotp u,~~~u\star v+v\star u=2(u_{0}v+v_{0}u-u\cdotp v),~~~u\star\bar{v}+v\star\bar{u}=2u\cdotp v,

\displaystyle(u\star v)\star u=u\star(v\star u)=-(u\cdotp u)\bar{v}+2(u\cdotp\bar{v})u,

The exceptional Lie algebra can be constructed using the octonions, see

One can define the Lie bracket between

for

C.1
The
\diamond
Product

We saw earlier that the dim 27 representation of
E_{6}
, there is a totally symmetric cubic form
d(\textrm{-},\textrm{-},\textrm{-})
, which can be regarded as a map
{\bf 27}\otimes^{s}{\bf 27}\to{\bf 27}^{*}
.
But as representation of
F_{4}
, they are isomorphic via the map
\sigma
in lem.
B.12
. As a result

Definition C.1
.

We have a symmetric
F_{4}
-invariant pairing
\langle\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}\rangle
, defined by sending
\Psi_{2}
to
\sigma^{-1}\Psi_{2}\in{\bf 27}^{*}
, where
\sigma
is the map in lem.
B.12
, followed by a natural pairing with
\Psi_{1}
.
The concrete formula is

\displaystyle\langle[u_{1}+\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}t_{1}e^{10};\xi_{1};\eta_{1};s_{1}],[u_{2}+\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}t_{2}e^{10};\xi_{2};\eta_{2};s_{2}]\rangle=u_{1}\cdotp u_{2}+s_{1}s_{2}+\frac{1}{2}t_{1}t_{2}+(\xi_{1}\xi_{2})+(\eta_{1}\eta_{2}).

We have a bi-linear map of representations of
F_{4}

\displaystyle{\bf 27}\otimes^{s}{\bf 27}\to{\bf 27},

denoted as
\Psi_{1}\diamond\Psi_{2}
:

\displaystyle\Psi_{1}\diamond\Psi_{2}=\sigma d(\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2},\textrm{-}).

The concrete formula for
\diamond
is given in (
3.4
).

We will relate next the diamond product to a certain exceptional Jordan product.

C.2
Exceptional Jordan algebra

Consider
3\times 3
Hermitian Octonion matrices

\displaystyle J=\begin{bmatrix}a&q_{1}&q_{3}\\
\bar{q}_{1}&b&q_{2}\\
\bar{q}_{3}&\bar{q}_{2}&c\\
\end{bmatrix}

where the bar denotes the operation in def.
A.14
. For real octonions, this is the conjugation, but for
\mathbb{C}\otimes\mathbb{O}
, this conjugation does not touch the
\mathbb{C}
factor.

Definition C.2
.

The Jordan product is defined as the anti-commutator

\displaystyle J_{1}\star J_{2}:=\frac{1}{2}\{J_{1},J_{2}\}.

(C.1)

This is the unique exceptional Euclidean Jordan algebra, known as the Albert algebra, according to the classification of Jordan, von Neumann and Wigner
[
JvNW34
]
.

We now prove thm.
3.2
, which shows that the Jordan product above is the diamond product, induced from the invariant tensor
d
.

Proof.

(Of thm.
3.2
)
We recall the notation, a vector
\Psi\in{\bf 26}\oplus{\bf 1}
is denoted as

\displaystyle\Psi=\begin{bmatrix}u\oplus\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}te^{10}\\
\psi\\
s\end{bmatrix},

where
u\in\mathbb{R}^{9}
and
\Psi\in\Delta^{+}_{10}
.
We say
\Psi
is real if
u,t,s
are real and
\psi=ie^{10}C_{10}\psi^{*}
. There is an inner-product

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{1}\cdotp u_{2}+\frac{1}{2}t_{1}t_{2}-i(\psi_{1}e^{10}\psi_{2})+s_{1}s_{2}

(C.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\tt u}_{1}\cdotp{\tt u}_{2}+\frac{1}{2}(r_{1}r_{2}+t_{1}t_{2})+(\eta_{1}\eta_{2})+(\xi_{1}\xi_{2})+s_{1}s_{2},

where we have further split
u
, and
\psi
in terms of 8 dimension quantities

\displaystyle u={\tt u}\oplus\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}e^{9};~~~~~~\psi=\begin{bmatrix}\xi\\
\eta\end{bmatrix},~~~\xi\in\Delta_{8}^{+},~~\eta\in\Delta^{-}_{8}

When
\Psi
is real, then
\xi,\eta
are Majorana, i.e.
\xi=C_{8}\xi^{*}
and
\eta=C_{8}\eta^{*}
.

We arrange
\Psi
into a matrix as in (
3.5
)

\displaystyle J(\Psi)=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}(r-s)&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\xi&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}{\tt u}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bar{\xi}&\frac{1}{2}(s-t)&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\eta\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bar{\tt u}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bar{\eta}&-\frac{1}{2}(r+s)\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Remark C.3
.

We remind the reader that for
\xi,\eta
, we implicitly use the maps (
A.57
), (
A.58
) to identify them with octonions. For example
\xi,\eta
should really read
t^{-1}(\xi),\,s^{-1}(\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{8}
. We do not make these maps explicit, due to our unfortunate choice of notation.

The bars are placed in the matrix precisely so that when multiplying two matrices, the resulting octonion multiplication is naturally realised as operations in the Clifford algebra. For example, from prop.
A.15
, we have
J_{21}J_{13}=t^{-1}(\bar{\xi})\star{\tt u}=s^{-1}({\tt u}\xi)
, i.e.
\star
is simply the Clifford action. Similarly
J_{12}J_{21}=t^{-1}(\xi)\star t^{-1}(\bar{\xi})=(\xi\xi)
, i.e. the diagonal entries of the product will be given by the natural pairings of spinors.

First, since
\Psi
can be projected to the
{\bf 1}
summand

\displaystyle\Psi\to\langle\Psi,\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle=\frac{s+t}{\sqrt{3}},

while the naive trace of
J(\Psi)
gives
\operatorname{Tr}[J(\Psi)]=-(s+t)/2
, so we conclude that the naive trace

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[J(\Psi)]=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\langle\Psi,\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle

(C.3)

and so is
F_{4}
invariant.

Next we want to show that

\displaystyle J_{1}\star J_{2}=-\frac{1}{2}J(\Psi_{1}\diamond\Psi_{2})+\frac{1}{4}\langle\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}\rangle{\bf 1}_{3}.

(C.4)

It is actually a direct computation. For example for 13 entry

\displaystyle 2(J_{1}\star J_{2})_{13}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}s_{1}{\tt u}_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\xi_{1}\star\eta_{2}+(1\leftrightarrow 2)=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}s_{1}{\tt u}_{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\xi_{1}{\tt e}^{i}\eta_{2}){\tt e}^{i}+(1\leftrightarrow 2)

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}J(\Psi_{1}\diamond\Psi_{2})_{13}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({\tt u}_{1}s_{2}+{\tt u}_{2}s_{1})-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\psi_{1}{\tt e}^{i}\psi_{2}){\tt e}^{i}=({\tt u}_{1}s_{2}+{\tt u}_{2}s_{1})-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\xi_{1}{\tt e}^{i}\eta_{2}){\tt e}^{i}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\xi_{2}{\tt e}^{i}\eta_{1}){\tt e}^{i}.

We see that the 13 entry of (
C.4
) match.
Note that to evaluate
(\psi_{1}e^{i}\psi_{2})
based on our explicit choice of Clifford algebra representation, we have

\displaystyle(\psi_{1}e^{i}\psi_{2})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}(\xi_{1}{\tt e}^{i}\eta_{2})+(\xi_{2}{\tt e}^{i}\eta_{1})&i=1,\cdots,8\\
-(\eta_{1}\eta_{2})+(\xi_{1}\xi_{2})&i=9\\
i(\eta_{1}\eta_{2})+i(\xi_{1}\xi_{2})&i=10\\
\end{array}\right.

Checking the 12 entry (here
J_{1,2}:=J(\Psi_{1,2})
)

\displaystyle 2(J_{1}\star J_{2})_{12}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}(r_{1}-t_{1})\xi_{2}+\frac{1}{2}{\tt u}_{1}\star\bar{\eta}_{2}+(1\leftrightarrow 2)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}(r_{1}-t_{1})\xi_{2}+\frac{1}{2}{\tt u}_{1}\eta_{2}+(1\leftrightarrow 2)

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}J(\Psi_{1}\diamond\Psi_{2})_{12}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}{\tt u}_{1}\eta_{2}+\frac{1}{2}(t_{1}-r_{1})\xi_{2}+(1\leftrightarrow 2).

It matches again. Let us check also the 23 entry

\displaystyle 2(J_{1}\star J_{2})_{23}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}(r_{1}+t_{1})\eta_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\bar{\xi}_{1}\star{\tt u}_{2}+(1\leftrightarrow 2)=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}(r_{1}+t_{1})\eta_{2}+\frac{1}{2}{\tt u}_{1}\xi_{2}+(1\leftrightarrow 2)

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}J(\Psi_{1}\diamond\Psi_{2})_{23}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}{\tt u}_{1}\xi_{2}+\frac{1}{2}(t_{1}+r_{1})\eta_{2}+(1\leftrightarrow 2).

Match again.

The diagonal entries are more complicated, we compute the lhs below

\displaystyle 4(J_{1}\star J_{2})_{11}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(r_{1}-s_{1})(r_{2}-s_{2})+\xi_{1}\star\bar{\xi}_{2}+{\tt u}_{1}\star\bar{\tt u}_{2}+(1\leftrightarrow 2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(r_{1}-s_{1})(r_{2}-s_{2})+2(\xi_{1}\xi_{2})+2{\tt u}_{1}\cdotp{\tt u}_{2},

\displaystyle 4(J_{1}\star J_{2})_{22}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(s_{1}-t_{1})(s_{2}-t_{2})+\bar{\xi}_{1}\star\xi_{2}+\eta_{1}\star\bar{\eta}_{2}+(1\leftrightarrow 2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(s_{1}-t_{1})(s_{2}-t_{2})+2(\eta_{1}\eta_{2})+2(\xi_{1}\xi_{2}),

\displaystyle 4(J_{1}\star J_{2})_{33}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(r_{1}+s_{1})(r_{2}+s_{2})+\bar{\eta}_{1}\star\eta_{2}+\bar{\tt u}_{1}\star{\tt u}_{2}+(1\leftrightarrow 2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(r_{1}+s_{1})(r_{2}+s_{2})+2(\eta_{1}\eta_{2})+2{\tt u_{1}}\cdotp{\tt u_{2}}.

On the other hand

\displaystyle 2J(\Psi_{1}\diamond\Psi_{2})_{11}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r_{(1}s_{2)}-(\psi_{1}e^{9}\psi_{2})-{\tt u}_{1}\cdotp{\tt u}_{2}-\frac{1}{2}r_{1}r_{2}+\frac{1}{2}t_{1}t_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r_{(1}s_{2)}-(\xi_{1}\xi_{2})+(\eta_{1}\eta_{2})-{\tt u}_{1}\cdotp{\tt u}_{2}-\frac{1}{2}r_{1}r_{2}+\frac{1}{2}t_{1}t_{2},

\displaystyle 2J(\Psi_{1}\diamond\Psi_{2})_{22}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\tt u}_{1}\cdotp{\tt u}_{2}+\frac{1}{2}(r_{1}r_{2}-t_{1}t_{2})+t_{(1}s_{2)}-(\xi_{1}\xi_{2})-(\eta_{1}\eta_{2}),

\displaystyle 2J(\Psi_{1}\diamond\Psi_{2})_{33}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\tt u}_{1}\cdotp{\tt u}_{2}-\frac{1}{2}(r_{1}r_{2}-t_{1}t_{2})-r_{(1}s_{2)}+(\xi_{1}\xi_{2})-(\eta_{1}\eta_{2}).

Comparing two sides, we get

\displaystyle 4(J_{1}\star J_{2})+2J(\Psi_{1}\diamond\Psi_{2})=\frac{1}{2}(r_{1}r_{2}+t_{1}t_{2})+s_{1}s_{2}+(\xi_{1}\xi_{2})+(\eta_{1}\eta_{2})+{\tt u}_{1}\cdotp{\tt u}_{2}.

Comparing with (
C.2
), we get (
C.4
).
