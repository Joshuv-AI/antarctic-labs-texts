On the Order of Power Series and the Sum of Square Roots Problem
Louis Gaillard, Gorav Jindal

Abstract
This paper focuses on the study of the order of power series that are linear combinations of a given finite set of power series. The order of a formal power series, known as ord ⁡ ( f ) \operatorname{ord}(f) , is defined as the minimum exponent of x x that has a non-zero coefficient in f ⁡ ( x ) f(x) . Our first result is that the order of the Wronskian of these power series is equivalent up to a polynomial factor, to the maximum order which occurs in the linear combination of these power series. This implies that the Wronskian approach used in (Kayal and Saha, TOCT’2012) to upper bound the order of sum of square roots is optimal up to a polynomial blowup. We also demonstrate similar upper bounds, similar to those of (Kayal and Saha, TOCT’2012), for the order of power series in a variety of other scenarios. We also solve a special case of the inequality testing problem outlined in (Etessami et al., TOCT’2014). In the second part of the paper, we study the equality variant of the sum of square roots problem, which is decidable in polynomial time due to (Blömer, FOCS’1991). We investigate a natural generalization of this problem when the input integers are given as straight line programs. Under the assumption of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH), we show that this problem can be reduced to the so-called “one dimensional” variant. We identify the key mathematical challenges for solving this “one dimensional” variant.



Keywords
Formal power series, Sum of square roots, Wronskian, Differential equations, Straight line Programs.

Introduction

For numerous decision problems that require determining the sign of expressions with real numbers, their complexity class (e.g., if they belong to
{\mathsf{P}}
or not) is often unknown. A notable instance is the Sum of Square Roots problem, which can be described as:

Problem 1

(Sum of Square Roots (
\operatorname{SSR}
))
.

Given a list
(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})
of positive integers and
(\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\dots,\delta_{n})\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
, decide whether

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\sqrt{a_{i}}>0
.

The complexity of this problem has been extensively investigated and remains an open question according to Garey, Graham, and Johnson
[
GGJ76
]
. Additionally, it has been hypothesized that it lies in
{\mathsf{P}}
, as proposed by Malajovich in 2001
[
Mal01
]
. The
\operatorname{SSR}
problem shares deep connections with classical geometric problems such as the Euclidean Traveling Salesman Problem (ETSP), which is not known to be in
{\mathsf{NP}}
. It is readily seen to be in
{\mathsf{NP}}
relative to an oracle of
\operatorname{SSR}
. An important related problem where the task is to determine the sign of an integer (encoded by a straight line program), is the so called
\operatorname{PosSLP}
problem. A sequence
P
of integers
(b_{0},b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{\ell})
is said
to be a straight line program (SLP) if
b_{0}=1
and for all
1\leq i\leq\ell
,
b_{i}=b_{j}\circ_{i}b_{k}
, where
\circ_{i}\in\{+,-,*\}
and
j,k<i
. We say that this SLP
P
computes the integer
b_{\ell}
. We say that
\ell
is the size (or length) of this SLP
P
.

Problem 2

(
\operatorname{PosSLP}
)
.

Given an SLP
P
, decide if the integer
n_{P}
computed by
P
is positive.

The approximation of
\sqrt{a_{i}}
in
1
to an appropriate precision leads to the reduction of
\operatorname{SSR}
to
\operatorname{PosSLP}
, as demonstrated in
[
ABKPM09
]
.
\operatorname{PosSLP}
was introduced in this work to bridge the gap between classical models of computation and computation over the reals in the Blum-Shub-Smale (BSS) model
[
BCSS97
]
, which is a widely studied model for the study of computational complexity in numerical analysis.

It was shown in
[
ABKPM09
]
that
\operatorname{PosSLP}
captures the “Boolean part” of languages decidable in the BSS model in polynomial time, with polynomial time Turing reductions. Additionally,
\operatorname{PosSLP}
was proven to lie within the counting hierarchy
{\mathsf{CH}}
, implying that
\operatorname{SSR}
also falls within
{\mathsf{CH}}
. To date, this represents the best known upper bound for the complexity of
\operatorname{SSR}
.

Our contributions can be found in
section

1.2
after having introduced some useful previous work on
1
in
section

1.1
.

1.1
Related work

In different scenarios, there is a need to determine if the sum of square roots is equal to zero or not, rather than determining the sign of the expression. As a result, we encounter an intriguing problem known as
\operatorname{SSR}_{\operatorname{eq}}
.

Problem 3

(
\operatorname{SSR}_{\operatorname{eq}}
)
.

Given a list
(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})
of integers and
(\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\dots,\delta_{n})\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
, decide whether
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\sqrt{a_{i}}=0
.

Blömer
[
Blö91
]
gave a polynomial time algorithm to compute sums of radicals and also proved that
\operatorname{SSR}_{\operatorname{eq}}
is in
{\mathsf{P}}
. Thus, testing if a signed sum
of square roots is zero seems to be easier than deciding its sign. A natural idea for an algorithm for the
\operatorname{SSR}
problem would be to approximate the sum with
a floating point number thanks to classical numerical algorithms. However, one would need a result on the required number of bits of precision of the
approximation to ensure that the two signs coincide. It is known (
[
Bre76
]
) that for an integer of bit size at most
B
, its square root can be
approximated up to
m
bits of precision in time
\mathrm{poly}(B,m)
. And this implies that a solution of the following number theoretic problem would lead to a
polynomial time algorithm for the
\operatorname{SSR}
problem.

Problem 4

(Lower-bound on a nonzero sum of square roots)
.

Given a sum
S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\sqrt{a_{i}}
, with
\delta_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
and
1\leq a_{i}<2^{B}
, can we find a polynomial
q(n,B)
such that

\displaystyle S\neq 0\implies\left|S\right|\geq\frac{1}{2^{q(n,B)}}?

In contrast to
4
, one can also try to find
a_{i}
’s and corresponding
\delta_{i}
’s such that the absolute value of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\sqrt{a_{i}}
is
small
. In this direction, it was shown in
[
QW06
]
that
\left|\sum_{i=0}^{m}\binom{m}{i}(-1)^{i}\sqrt{n+i}\right|=O(n^{-m+\frac{1}{2}})
. Kayal and Saha
[
KS12
]
chose to approach
4
by formulating a related question over polynomials. This approach proved to be simpler. They focused on non-zero sums of square roots of polynomials, which they viewed as power series, and demonstrated that the valuation (or order) of such a series cannot be
too high
.

Theorem 1

(Bounding the order of sum of square roots of polynomials
[
KS12
]
)
.

For
1\leq i\leq n
, let
c_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
and
f_{i},g_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[x]
of degree at most
d
with
f_{i}(0)\neq 0
. We denote and fix

\sqrt{f_{i}(x)}\in\mathbb{C}[[x]]
one of the two square roots of
f_{i}(x)
. If the sum
S(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}g_{i}(x)\sqrt{f_{i}(x)}
is non-zero, then
\operatorname{ord}(S)\leq dn^{2}+n
.

The main technical argument of this proof is the study of the Wronskian determinant of the family
(g_{i}\sqrt{f_{i}})_{1\leq i\leq n}
, because by Cramer’s rule,
one can easily bound the order of
S
with respect to the order of this Wronskian.

Next, they apply this result to a set of integers by representing them as polynomials and they confirm that the solution to
4
is affirmative for a significant subclass of integers known as
polynomial integers
.

Theorem 2

(
\operatorname{SSR}
for
polynomial integers

[
KS12
]
)
.

Suppose

S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\sqrt{a_{i}}
is non-zero with
\delta_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
, such that
every positive integer
a_{i}
is of the form
a_{i}=X^{d_{i}}+b_{1,i}X^{d_{i}-1}+\dots+b_{d_{i},i}
with
d_{i}>0
,
X
a positive integer and
d_{i},b_{j,i}
are
integers. Let
B=\max\left(\left\{\left|b_{j,i}\right|\right\}_{j,i},1\right)
and
d=\max_{i}d_{i}
.
There exist two fixed integer polynomials
p(n,d),q(n,d)
in
n
and
d
such that if
X\geq(B+1)^{p(n,d)}
, then
S
is lower bounded as
\left|S\right|\geq\frac{1}{X^{q(n,d)}}
.

Based on these observations, it raises the question of whether the concept of bounding the order of sums of power series can be generalized to other families beyond square roots. This could potentially yield significant insights into the complexity of determining the positivity of expressions involving irrational real numbers.

From now on,
\mathbb{K}
denotes a field of characteristic 0. All the logarithms in this paper are natural logarithms with base

, unless otherwise stated. We now define some measures to formally state our results.

Definition 1
.

Suppose
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{K}[[x]]
is a finite dimensional linear subspace of
\mathbb{K}[[x]]
, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})\vcentcolon=\sup\{\operatorname{ord}(f)\left|f\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\{0\}\right\}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\infty.

And for
\mathbf{f}=(f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n})
a family of linearly independent power series, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n})=\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{f})\vcentcolon=\mathcal{O}(\spn(f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n})).

A set
\{f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n}\}
of
n
functions over
\mathbb{K}
is said to be linearly dependent if there exist scalars
c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n}\in\mathbb{K}
(not all zero)
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}f_{i}
is zero. To define the Wronskian of
\{f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n}\}
, we assume that each
f_{i}
is
n-1
times differentiable. The Wronskian of

\{f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n}\}
, denoted

is defined as the determinant of the following matrix:

\displaystyle W(\mathbf{f})=W(f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n})\vcentcolon=\det\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}&\dots&f_{n}\\
f_{1}^{(1)}&\dots&f_{n}^{(1)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
f_{1}^{(n-1)}&\dots&f_{n}^{(n-1)}\end{pmatrix}

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{K}[[x]]
be an
n
-dimensional linear subspace. For any basis
(f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n})
of
\mathcal{F}
,
\operatorname{ord}(W(f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n}))
does not depend on the choice of the basis
(f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n})
. We denote this quantity by

W_{\operatorname{ord}}(\mathcal{F})
.

Proof.

Let
(g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{n})
be another basis of
\mathcal{F}
. There exists an invertible
n\times n
matrix
A
with entries in
\mathbb{K}
such that

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}g_{1}&\dots&g_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}f_{1}&\dots&f_{n}\end{bmatrix}\cdot A.

By linearity of
the differentiation, we also have for all
0\leq j<n
,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}g_{1}^{(j)}&\dots&g_{n}^{(j)}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}f_{1}^{(j)}&\dots&f_{n}^{(j)}\end{bmatrix}\cdot A.

Thus we have
W(g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{n})=W(f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n})\cdot\det(A)
. By using the fact that
\det(A)\in\mathbb{K}^{*}
, the result follows. ∎

2
Order of the Wronskian determinant

In this section we show that for a family of power series, the maximal order that can occur in a non-zero linear combination is the order of the Wronskian determinant of the family up to a polynomial factor. Our proof of
theorem

4
is inspired from the ideas developed in
[
BD10
]
. For
d,k\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote

\displaystyle(d)_{k}\vcentcolon=d(d-1)\dots(d-k+1),

with the convention
(d)_{0}=1
.

Definition 2

(Vandermonde determinant)
.

Let
(d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{n})\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
, we define the corresponding Vandermonde determinant as follows:

\displaystyle V(d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{n})\vcentcolon=\det\left((d_{j}^{i-1})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\right)=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(d_{j}-d_{i}).

Lemma 7

(Wronskian of monomials, Lemma 1 in
[
BD10
]
)
.

The Wronskian of the monomials
a_{1}x^{d_{1}},\dots,a_{n}x^{d_{n}}
is

\displaystyle W(a_{1}x^{d_{1}},\dots,a_{n}x^{d_{n}})=V(d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{n})\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\right)x^{d_{1}+\dots+d_{n}-\binom{n}{2}}.

Lemma 8

(Lemma 2 in
[
BD10
]
)
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n}
be a family of
\mathbb{K}[[x]]
which are linearly independent over
\mathbb{K}
. There
exists an invertible
n\times n
matrix
A
with entries in
\mathbb{K}
such that the power series
g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{n}
defined by

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}g_{1}&\dots&g_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}f_{1}&\dots&f_{n}\end{bmatrix}\cdot A

(1)

are all non-zero and have mutually distinct
orders.

Lemma 9

(Wronskian of distinct order power series)
.

If the non-zero power series
g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{n}\in\mathbb{K}[[x]]
have mutually distinct orders
d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{n}
, then
their Wronskian
W(g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{n})
is non-zero and satisfies:

\displaystyle\operatorname{ord}(W(g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{n}))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}-\binom{n}{2}.

Proof.

If the
g_{j}
’s are monomials,
i.e
.

g_{j}=a_{j}x^{d_{j}}
, the result is a direct consequence of
lemma

7
, and in this case, the
(i,j)
entry of the Wronskian matrix is
w_{i,j}=a_{j}(d_{j})_{i-1}x^{d_{j}-i+1}
. In the general case, let
g_{j}=a_{j}x^{d_{j}}+u_{j}

with
u_{j}\in\mathbb{K}[[x]]
of order
>d_{j}
. Then the
(i,j)
entry of the Wronskian matrix now becomes
w_{i,j}\times(1+xr_{i,j})
for some
r_{i,j}\in\mathbb{K}[[x]]
.

So we have
W(g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{n})=a_{1}\dots a_{n}x^{d_{1}+\dots+d_{n}-\binom{n}{2}}\det(D)
where
D
is the
n\times n
matrix
D=((d_{j})_{i-1}(1+xr_{i,j}))_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
.
Now we evaluate
D
at
x=0
, and we obtain
D(0)=((d_{j})_{i-1})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
. With elementary row operations (which preserve the determinant), as in the proof of
lemma

7
in
[
BD10
]
, we can transform
D(0)
into the Vandermonde matrix associated to
d_{1},\dots,d_{n}
. So,
\det(D(0))=V(d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{n})\neq 0
, because the
d_{i}
’s are distinct. Thus
\det(D)
is non zero modulo
x
, so
\det D
has order zero and the result follows.
∎

S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\underbrace{\log\left(1+b_{1,i}y+\dots+b_{d_{i},i}y^{d_{i}}\right)}_{=h_{i}(y)}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\underbrace{y^{j}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}h_{i,j}}_{S_{j}}

\text{with }h_{i}(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}h_{i,j}y^{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}\left(b_{1,i}y+\dots+b_{d_{i},i}y^{d_{i}}\right)^{k}.

By applying

is such that

. The idea of the proof is to show that
for every

\displaystyle\frac{\left|S_{\ell+t}\right|}{\left|S_{\ell}\right|}\leq\frac{1}{2^{t+1}},

(8)

because in this case, we would have

\displaystyle\left|S\right|\geq\left|\left|S_{\ell}\right|-\left|S_{\ell+1}\right|-\dots\right|\geq\left|\left|S_{\ell}\right|-\frac{1}{2}\left|S_{\ell}\right|\right|=\frac{1}{2}\left|S_{\ell}\right|,

(9)

and
one can obtain a lower bound for
\left|S\right|
via a lower bound for
\left|S_{\ell}\right|
. But to satisfy

5.1
Bounds for the Proof of
theorem

6

We recall that we have

S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\underbrace{\log\left(1+b_{1,i}y+\dots+b_{d_{i},i}y^{d_{i}}\right)}_{=h_{i}(y)}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\underbrace{y^{j}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}h_{i,j}}_{S_{j}}

\text{with }h_{i}(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}h_{i,j}y^{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}\left(b_{1,i}y+\dots+b_{d_{i},i}y^{d_{i}}\right)^{k}.

The goal is to show that
p_{1}(n,d)
and
p_{2}(n,d)
satisfying
eqs.

5
and
6
give both an upper bound and a lower bound on
S_{j}
that allows us to satisfy
eq.

8
.

An upper bound on
S_{j}
:

For any
j
,
h_{i,j}
is contributed by the term of order
j
in the sum
\sum\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}\left(b_{1,i}y+\dots+b_{d_{i},i}y^{d_{i}}\right)^{k}
for
k
in range
\left[1,j\right]
. Then

\displaystyle h_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{j}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}v_{k,i,j},\text{ with }

\displaystyle v_{k,i,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+2k_{2}+\dots+d_{i}k_{d_{i}}=j\\
k_{1}+k_{2}+\dots+k_{d_{i}}=k\end{subarray}}\binom{k}{k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{d_{i}}}b_{1,i}^{k_{1}}\dots b_{d_{i},i}^{k_{d_{i}}}.

where
k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{d_{i}}
are non-negative integers. Notice that for
k<\frac{j}{d_{i}},v_{k,i,j}=0
as the sum is empty. Then

\displaystyle\left|v_{k,i,j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}+\dots+k_{d_{i}}=k}\binom{k}{k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{d_{i}}}\left|b_{1,i}\right|^{k_{1}}\dots\left|b_{d_{i},i}\right|^{k_{d_{i}}}

\displaystyle\leq(Bd_{i})^{k}\leq(Bd)^{k}.

(Multinomial theorem)

Since
\left|\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}\right|\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\left|h_{i,j}\right|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{j}(Bd)^{k}\leq(Bd)^{j+1}.

Hence, let
S=\sum_{j\geq 1}S_{j}
, with
S_{j}=y^{j}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}h_{i,j}
satisfies for all
j\geq 1

\displaystyle\left|S_{j}\right|\leq y^{j}nC(Bd)^{j+1}.

A lower bound on
S_{\ell}
:

Now we prove a lower bound for
\left|S_{\ell}\right|=y^{\ell}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}h_{i,\ell}\right|\neq 0
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}h_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}v_{k,i,\ell}=\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}\underbrace{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}v_{k,i,\ell}\right)}_{\in\mathbb{Z}}.

So
\ell!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}h_{i,\ell}\right)
is an integer. Then, if
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}h_{i,\ell}\neq 0
,

\displaystyle\ell!\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}h_{i,\ell}\right|\geq 1.

And finally, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|S_{\ell}\right|\geq\frac{y^{\ell}}{\ell!}.

With the obtained upper and lower bound, we can now deduce a requirement that ensures
eq.

8
to be satisfied. Actually, it is sufficient
that for every
t\geq 1
,

\displaystyle ny^{t}C(Bd)^{\ell+t+1}\ell!\leq\frac{1}{2^{t+1}},\emph{i.e}.\hbox{}

(10)

\displaystyle X^{t}\geq 2^{t+1}nC(Bd)^{\ell+t+1}\ell!.

(11)

Recall that
C\leq X^{1/2}
and
\ell\leq dn
, so it suffices that

\displaystyle X^{t-\frac{1}{2}}\geq 2^{t+1}n(Bd)^{nd+t+1}(nd)!.

By applying
\log
, it is sufficient that for all
t\geq 1
,

\displaystyle(t-\frac{1}{2})p_{1}(n,d)\log(B+1)\geq

\displaystyle(t+1)\log(2)+\log(n)

\displaystyle+(nd+t+1)\log(Bd)+nd\log(nd).

Therefore, one can show that
p_{1}(n,d)\geq 20dn\log(dn)
is sufficient (this bound is
not optimized at all). And in this case, by
eq.

9
, we have

\displaystyle\left|S\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}\left|S_{\ell}\right|\geq\frac{1}{2\ell!X^{\ell}}\geq\frac{1}{2^{\ell\log\ell+1}X^{\ell}}\geq\frac{1}{X^{p_{2}(n,d)}},

with
p_{2}(n,d)\geq dn(\log(dn)+1)+1
.

6
Conclusion and Open Questions

In
theorem

5
, we greatly generalized the ideas developed in
[
KS12
]
. This leads to the fact that the results in
[
KS12
]
can be generalized to various other families of power series, as evident in
corollary

1
. The Wronskian approach of
[
KS12
]
was also proven to be tight, up to a polynomial blowup. Our work also made significant progress in the straight line variant of the classical SSR equality problem. We also extended the results of
[
KS12
]
for sign testing of the sum of square roots of polynomial integers to a linear combination of logarithms of polynomial integers, solving a special case of Problem 2 proposed in
[
ESY14
]
. There are several directions for future research:

1.

item

ii
in
corollary

1
applies to some special cases of solutions of 2nd order differential equations, like
\cos f
where
f
is a polynomial. It would be intriguing to see if this applies to generic solutions of higher order equations.

2.

Can we generalize
theorem

1
by bounding the order of sums of arbitrary algebraic power series? Square roots of polynomials are examples of algebraic functions. However, finding a satisfying way to bound the order of the Wronskian determinant of a family of algebraic power series is challenging. The best upper bound we could find is about
r^{\mathrm{poly}(n)}d
where
(r,d)
is a bound on the bi-degree of the polynomial equations satisfied by the algebraic power series.

3.

We saw that both the sign testing of the sum of square roots and logarithms are specific examples of
\operatorname{PosSLP}
. In order to advance in understanding the complexity of
\operatorname{PosSLP}
, it is important to consider other special cases. For instance, the following are additional special cases of
\operatorname{PosSLP}
that are worth exploring.

(a)

Given positive integers
a,b,c,n
in binary, determine the sign of
a^{n}+b^{n}-c^{n}
. To our knowledge, even this very special case of
\operatorname{PosSLP}
remains open.

(b)

Given an integer trinomial
f(x)\vcentcolon=c_{1}+c_{2}x^{a}_{2}+c_{3}x^{a_{3}}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
and a rational
\frac{p}{q}
, determine the sign of
f\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)
. This problem was posed in
[
Koi19
]
. Significant progress was made on it in
[
BDR22
]
but the general form of the problem remains open.
