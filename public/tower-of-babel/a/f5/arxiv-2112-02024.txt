A formalism for the long-distance magnetic field generated by populations of neurons
Alexis O. García Rodríguez, Alain Destexhe

Abstract
Brain activity can be measured using magnetic fields located at some distance from the brain , a technique called magneto-encephalography (MEG). The origin of such magnetic fields are the ionic currents involved in neuronal activity. While these processes are well known at the microscopic scale, it is less clear how large populations of neurons generate magnetic fields. Here, we attempt to make such a link by deriving a mean-field theory of magnetic field generation by populations of neurons. We use the concept of current dipoles to build a theory that can be applied to populations of neurons. We also evaluate the contributions of neuronal current sources such as the synaptic currents or action potential currents. This theory should be useful to calculate the magnetic field located at long distances from neuronal populations, such as in MEG.



Introduction

Magnetic field measurements constitute a very attractive method to measure neuronal activity, in particular through magneto-encephalography (MEG), which measures the magnetic field at some distance from the brain surface, as reviewed previously
Hämäläinen et al. 1993
;
Gulrajani 1998
. Contrary to the genesis of electric fields and potentials,
membrane currents do not contribute much to extracellular magnetic fields, because they cancel due to the cylindrical shape of neuronal cables, but that the main contribution to the extracellular magnetic field is the axial current
in neuronal dendrites

Hämäläinen et al. 1993
. It was shown previously that the “microscopic” magnetic fields measured for muscle fibers using miniaturized sensors, could be quantitatively matched by computational models taking into account the axial currents
Barbieri et al. 2016
.

Another difference between electric and magnetic fields is that the electric potential around neurons crucially depends on the electric properties of the extracellular medium, while magnetic fields are believed to have negligible interactions with the extracellular medium
Gulrajani 1998
. Note that some studies have found that the electric properties of extracellular media also affect magnetic fields, because they influence the neuronal current sources that produce the field
Bedard and Destexhe 2014
. We will assume here that there is no such dependence and will consider neurons embedded in resistive (Ohmic) media, with no capacitive or diffusive effects.

There exists well established theories to calculate the microscopic currents generating extracellular electric potentials, such as the neuronal cable theory
Rall 1995
. Such theories can be formulated macroscopically, using a mean-field formulation of Maxwell equations
Bédard and Destexhe 2013
, yielding expressions for the extracellular potential at scales larger than the variations of resistivity and permittivity. Such theories can easily integrate the influence of multiple neuronal current sources, and thus can be used to calculate the electric potential resulting from populations of neurons.

However, how to account for “macroscopic” neuronal magnetic fields is less clear. One of the reasons is that the vectorial nature of the magnetic field will lead to partial cancellation of the fields generated by single neurons, in a way that critically depends on their orientation. Taking advantage of the fact that the principal neurons in cerebral cortex form electric dipoles, which are arranged in parallel, we derive here a mean-field formulation to account for macroscopic neuronal magnetic fields.
We start from the notion of current dipole, which we contrast with the notion of electric dipole, showing that they constitute distinct concepts with their own definition of the dipole moment. We next evaluate the contribution to different types of current sources, such as the synaptic currents and action potential currents, to generate the magnetic field located far away (centimenters) from neurons, such as typically in MEG measurements.

Biot–Savart law and the nonstatic case

II.1
The classic Biot-Savart law and its generalization

The Biot–Savart law for volume currents is

{\bf\it B}({\bf\it r})=\frac{\mu}{4\pi}\int{\bf\it J}({\bf\it r}^{\prime})\crossproduct\frac{{\bf\it r}-{\bf\it r}^{\prime}}{\vqty{\vb*r-\vb*r'}^{3}}\differential[3]{r'},

(8)

where the integration is over all space and
\displaystyle{\bf\it J}
is the free current density which is assumed to be confined to a finite region. As mentioned earlier, we consider a linear and isotropic medium with uniform permeability
\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle\partialderivative*{\rho}{t}=0

\displaystyle\divergence{\bf\it J}+\partialderivative*{\rho}{t}=0

On the other hand, the brain tissue represents a nonstatic situation and the use of the Biot–Savart law must be justified. Here the charge density, given by the ion concentrations, depends on time at the different points in space. As noted below, Eq. (

11

\displaystyle{\bf\it J}({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)

{\bf\it Q}_{reg}^{s}(t)\approx-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{reg}^{(i)}(t)\int\limits_{\mathcal{V}_{reg}(i)}{\bf\it r}^{\prime}{\nabla^{\prime}}^{2}\Phi({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)\differential[3]{r'}.

(75)

Here, different from (
73
), the synaptic current dipole moment is in terms of the second spatial derivatives of the scalar potential inside each homogeneous part.

The last expression can also be written in terms of the free charge density
\displaystyle\rho
. In the electro-quasistatic approximation we have
\displaystyle{\bf\it E}({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)\approx-\gradient^{\prime}\Phi({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)
. This leads to the Poisson’s expression

{\nabla^{\prime}}^{2}\Phi({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)\approx-\gradient^{\prime}\dotproduct{\bf\it E}({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t).

(76)

Describing the
\displaystyle i
th part by a linear and isotropic medium with uniform permittivity
\displaystyle\epsilon_{reg}^{(i)}(t)
, we have
\displaystyle{\bf\it D}({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)=\epsilon_{reg}^{(i)}(t){\bf\it E}({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)

13

{\bf\it Q}_{reg}^{s}(t)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\sigma_{reg}^{(i)}(t)}{\epsilon_{reg}^{(i)}(t)}\int\limits_{\mathcal{V}_{reg}(i)}{\bf\it r}^{\prime}\rho({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)\differential[3]{r'}.

(77)

This can be written in a more compact manner by using

\tau_{reg}^{(i)}(t)=\frac{\epsilon_{reg}^{(i)}(t)}{\sigma_{reg}^{(i)}(t)},

(78)

which is the Maxwell–Wagner time for the
\displaystyle i
th part, and

{\bf\it p}_{reg}^{(i)}(t)=\int\limits_{\mathcal{V}_{reg}(i)}{\bf\it r}^{\prime}\rho({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)\differential[3]{r'},

(79)

which is the electric dipole moment for the
\displaystyle i
th part. Then

{\bf\it Q}_{reg}^{s}(t)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{{\bf\it p}_{reg}^{(i)}(t)}{\tau_{reg}^{(i)}(t)}.

(80)

Thus, through the corresponding Maxwell–Wagner time, the synaptic current dipole moment is related to the electric dipole moment of each homogeneous part.

IV
Discussion

Here we have developed a method for calculating the long-distance magnetic field generated by neuronal populations. Since MEG measurements show magnetic signals that change over time, we first considered the time-dependent generalization of the Biot–Savart law given by Eq. (
18
). As the main contributions in neuromagnetism come from relatively low frequencies that are below a typical value
\displaystyle f_{max}=$\displaystyle 100\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$

Hämäläinen et al. 1993
, we then followed the approach of
Griffiths and Heald 1991
to approximate Eq. (
18
) in the quasistatic case. This method differs from previous ones
Hämäläinen et al. 1993
;
Wagner et al. 2014
;
Haus and Melcher 1989
;
Gratiy et al. 2017
where the treatment does not rely on the general solution of Maxwell’s equations, but on the quasistatic approximation of these equations themselves.

The time dependency of the current density and that of the magnetic field can be described via the different frequency components. We found that in the quasistatic case, the general nonstatic solution (
18
) can be approximated by expression (
32
) provided
\displaystyle w_{max}^{2}t_{trav}^{2}({\bf\it r},\bar{{\bf\it r}}^{\prime})\ll 1
. This is expression (
30
) and describes the quasistatic case through the current frequency component with the shortest oscillation period
\displaystyle 2\pi/w_{max}=1/f_{max}
. This time must be large enough compared to the largest traveling time
\displaystyle t_{trav}({\bf\it r},\bar{{\bf\it r}}^{\prime})
. The latter is the time needed for an electromagnetic signal to travel from the most distant source point
\displaystyle\bar{{\bf\it r}}^{\prime}
to the measurement point
\displaystyle{\bf\it r}
.

We note that a similar expression to (
30
) is well known for the magneto-quasistatic approximation of Maxwell’s equations
Wagner et al. 2014
;
Haus and Melcher 1989
, as well as their electro-quasistatic approximation
Wagner et al. 2014
;
Haus and Melcher 1989
;
Gratiy et al. 2017
. In these cases, however, instead of the traveling distance
\displaystyle\vqty{\vb*r-\bar{\vb*r}'}
, one typical or characteristic length scale of the medium is employed. The system dimensions are assumed to be all within a factor of two or so of each other
Haus and Melcher 1989
. The typical length of gray matter is estimated by the cortical thickness
Wagner et al. 2014
;
Gratiy et al. 2017
. On the other hand, Eq. (
18
) leads to expression (
30
) and therefore, to the traveling distance
\displaystyle\vqty{\vb*r-\bar{\vb*r}'}
as the appropriate length to define the quasistatic dynamics.

A clearly different expression from (
30
) can be found in Ref.
1
where the quasistatic approximation of the Ampère–Maxwell equation was examined. It is given by
\displaystyle w_{max}\tau_{MW}^{\mathstrut}\ll 1
, where
\displaystyle\tau_{MW}^{\mathstrut}=\epsilon/\sigma
is the Maxwell–Wagner reaction time. That is expression (
35
) which we discussed in greater detail earlier. Here
\displaystyle\epsilon
and
\displaystyle\sigma
are the permittivity and the electrical conductivity of the medium, respectively. We note that a permittivity
\displaystyle\epsilon\approx$\displaystyle{10}^{5}$\epsilon_{0}^{\mathstrut}
, where
\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}^{\mathstrut}
is the permittivity of free space, was considered in Ref.
1
. This gives
\displaystyle w_{max}\tau_{MW}^{\mathstrut}\approx$\displaystyle 2\text{\times}{10}^{-3}$\ll 1
. Here, however, based on Refs.
15
and
19
, the permittivity for gray matter at frequencies below
\displaystyle 100\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
has been underestimated by two orders of magnitude. Therefore, the condition
\displaystyle w_{max}\tau_{MW}^{\mathstrut}\ll 1
is actually fulfilled in a minimal manner.

The quasistatic approximation (
32
) of the Biot–Savart law was justified with (
30
) being well satisfied in the form
\displaystyle(2\pi f_{max})^{2}\vqty{\vb*r-\bar{\vb*r}'}^{2}\epsilon\mu\approx$\displaystyle 4\text{\times}{10}^{-7}$\ll 1
. Here we estimated
\displaystyle\vqty{\vb*r-\bar{\vb*r}'}\approx$\displaystyle 0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$
, mainly given by the average size of the human brain. Additionally, we took the permittivity
\displaystyle\epsilon\approx$\displaystyle{10}^{7}$\epsilon_{0}^{\mathstrut}
for gray matter at frequencies below
\displaystyle 100\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}

Wagner et al. 2014
;
Gabriel et al. 1996
. Finally, typical for biological tissues
Wagner et al. 2014
, the permeability
\displaystyle\mu
was approximated by that of free space,
\displaystyle\mu_{0}^{\mathstrut}
.

We then focused on the magnetic field of the various currents.
Following previous work
Geselowitz 1967
;
Ilmoniemi 1994
,
the current density was split into conduction and impressed current densities. The conduction current is caused by the electric field and its density is specified in the electro-quasistatic approximation by
\displaystyle{\bf\it J}^{c}({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)\approx-\sigma({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)\gradient^{\prime}\Phi({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)
, where
\displaystyle\sigma({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)
is the nonhomogeneous conductivity of the medium and
\displaystyle\Phi({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)
is the scalar potential. We assumed the impressed current density mostly given by a synaptic current density
\displaystyle{\bf\it J}^{s}({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)
and an action potential current density
\displaystyle{\bf\it J}^{a}({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)
. The magnetic field case of conduction currents has been worked out exactly
Geselowitz 1970
. The long-distance magnetic field was studied in the synaptic and action potential cases.

Each population of neurons was described as a relatively small region such that its dimensions are small compared to the average distance from the region to the field point
\displaystyle{\bf\it r}
. Taking any point
\displaystyle{\bf\it r}_{reg}^{\mathstrut}
in the region, we must have
\displaystyle\vqty{\vb*r'-\vb*r_{reg}^{\mathstrut}}\ll\vqty{\vb*r-\vb*r_{reg}^{\mathstrut}}
, for every region point
\displaystyle{\bf\it r}^{\prime}
. Considering the distance to the measurement point in the order of centimeters, each region volume
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{reg}
can be thought in the order of
\displaystyle 1\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}^{3}
. Using known values of the cortical surface, the average cortical thickness, and the total number of neurons in the human cerebral cortex
Van Essen et al. 2018
, we can find a quantity of approximately
\displaystyle 4.7\text{\times}{10}^{5}
regions of
\displaystyle 1\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}^{3}
in the human cerebral cortex, each one with an average estimate of about
\displaystyle 3.4\text{\times}{10}^{4}
neurons. The contributions from the different regions must be added together to obtain the magnetic field at a certain point. This is indicated through Eqs. (
47
) and (
81
) for synaptic currents and action potential currents, respectively.

The magnetic induction produced by synaptic currents at a relatively distant point
\displaystyle{\bf\it r}
, for a region located at
\displaystyle{\bf\it r}_{reg}^{\mathstrut}
, can be approximated by expression (
56
). Here
\displaystyle{\bf\it Q}_{reg}^{s}(t)
is the (time-dependent) synaptic current dipole moment defined by Eq. (
54
). This was related to the conduction current density leading to two equivalent expressions of
\displaystyle{\bf\it Q}_{reg}^{s}(t)
in terms of the scalar potential.

In conclusion, we have derived here the formalism to relate the magnetic field at large distances (centimeters) from populations of neurons and estimated the contributions from various current sources in neurons. The next step in this approach is to apply this population-level description to mean-field models of neuronal populations, in order to link these models with the genesis of the MEG signal.

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathcal{V}_{reg}}{\bf\it J}({\bf\it\mathcal{R}}+{\bf\it r}^{\prime\prime},t)\differential[3]{r''},

so that

{\bf\it\mathcal{Q}}_{reg}(t)=\int\limits_{\mathcal{V}_{reg}}{\bf\it J}({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)\differential[3]{r'}={\bf\it Q}_{reg}(t).

(119)

The mentioned independence on the coordinate origin must be verified for expressions (
65
) and (
67
) of the total current dipole moment. Below, when using a new origin as above, the conduction current density is described by

{\bf\it\mathcal{J}}^{c}({\bf\it r}^{\prime\prime},t)={\bf\it J}^{c}({\bf\it\mathcal{R}}+{\bf\it r}^{\prime\prime},t)={\bf\it J}^{c}({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t).

(120)

A.3.1
First case

Regarding expression (
65
) (or (
101
)), we have

\displaystyle{\bf\it\mathcal{Q}}_{reg}(t)

\displaystyle\approx\int\limits_{\mathcal{S}_{reg}}{\bf\it r}^{\prime\prime}\bqty{\vb*{\mathcal J}^c(\vb*r'',t)\vdot\dd{\vb*S''}}

(121)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathcal{S}_{reg}}\pqty{\vb*r'-\vb*{\mathcal R}}\bqty{\vb*J^c(\vb*r',t)\vdot\dd{\vb*S'}},

so that

\displaystyle{\bf\it\mathcal{Q}}_{reg}(t)

\displaystyle\approx\int\limits_{\mathcal{S}_{reg}}{\bf\it r}^{\prime}\bqty{\vb*J^c(\vb*r',t)\vdot\dd{\vb*S'}}

(122)

\displaystyle-{\bf\it\mathcal{R}}\int\limits_{\mathcal{S}_{reg}}{\bf\it J}^{c}({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)\dotproduct\differential{\vb*S'}.

Concerning the second term on the right-hand side here, by integrating (
111
) we get

\int\limits_{\mathcal{S}_{reg}}{\bf\it J}^{c}({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)\dotproduct\differential{\vb*S'}\approx-\int\limits_{\mathcal{S}_{reg}}{\bf\it J}^{i}({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)\dotproduct\differential{\vb*S'}.

(123)

Hence, for expression (
65
) to be independent of the coordinate origin, it is enough, and necessary, that the net impressed current crossing the region boundary is approximately zero. Namely, if, and only if, we have

\int\limits_{\mathcal{S}_{reg}}{\bf\it J}^{i}({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)\dotproduct\differential{\vb*S'}\approx 0,

(124)

then

\int\limits_{\mathcal{S}_{reg}}{\bf\it J}^{c}({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)\dotproduct\differential{\vb*S'}\approx 0,

(125)

and consequently

{\bf\it\mathcal{Q}}_{reg}(t)\approx\int\limits_{\mathcal{S}_{reg}}{\bf\it r}^{\prime}\bqty{\vb*J^c(\vb*r',t)\vdot\dd{\vb*S'}},

(126)

which gives the same result as expression (
65
).

A.3.2
Second case

As for expression (
67
) (or (
116
)), when we employ a new origin it reads

\displaystyle{\bf\it\mathcal{Q}}_{reg}(t)

\displaystyle\approx\int\limits_{\mathcal{V}_{reg}}{\bf\it r}^{\prime\prime}\gradient^{\prime\prime}\dotproduct{\bf\it\mathcal{J}}^{c}({\bf\it r}^{\prime\prime},t)\differential[3]{r''}

(127)

\displaystyle+\int\limits_{\mathcal{V}_{reg}}{\bf\it\mathcal{J}}^{c}({\bf\it r}^{\prime\prime},t)\differential[3]{r''},

which, using the divergence theorem and (
123
), leads to

\displaystyle{\bf\it\mathcal{Q}}_{reg}(t)

\displaystyle\approx\int\limits_{\mathcal{V}_{reg}}{\bf\it r}^{\prime}\gradient^{\prime}\dotproduct{\bf\it J}^{c}({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)\differential[3]{r'}+{\bf\it Q}_{reg}^{c}(t)

(128)

\displaystyle+{\bf\it\mathcal{R}}\int\limits_{\mathcal{S}_{reg}}{\bf\it J}^{i}({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)\dotproduct\differential{\vb*S'}.

Thus, similar to the case of expression (
65
), if, and only if, (
124
) is met, we have

{\bf\it\mathcal{Q}}_{reg}(t)\approx\int\limits_{\mathcal{V}_{reg}}{\bf\it r}^{\prime}\gradient^{\prime}\dotproduct{\bf\it J}^{c}({\bf\it r}^{\prime},t)\differential[3]{r'}+{\bf\it Q}_{reg}^{c}(t),

(129)

which produces the same result as expression (
67
).
