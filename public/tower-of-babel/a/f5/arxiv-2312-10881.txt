Dirac walks on regular trees
Nicolas Delporte, Saswato Sen, Reiko Toriumi

Abstract
The study of matter fields on an ensemble of random geometries is a difficult problem still in need of new methods and ideas. We will follow a point of view inspired by probability theory techniques that relies on an expansion of the two point function as a sum over random walks. An analogous expansion for Fermions on non-Euclidean geometries is still lacking. Casiday et al. [ Laplace and Dirac operators on graphs , Linear and Multilinear Algebra (2022) 1] proposed a classical “Dirac walk” diffusing on vertices and edges of an oriented graph with a square root of the graph Laplacian. In contrast to the simple random walk, each step of the walk is given a sign depending on the orientation of the edge it goes through. In a toy model, we propose here to study the Green functions, spectrum and the spectral dimension of such “Dirac walks” on the Bethe lattice, a d d -regular tree. The recursive structure of the graph makes the problem exactly solvable. Notably, we find that the spectrum develops a gap and that the spectral dimension of the Dirac walk matches that of the simple random walk ( d s = 1 d_{s}=1 for d = 2 d=2 and d s = 3 d_{s}=3 for d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 ).

0&0&0&3&-3&0\\
0&0&0&0&3&-3\\
-3&3&0&0&0&0\\
0&-3&3&0&0&0\\
3&0&-3&0&0&0\end{array}\right)\,,

(35)

with the corresponding possible three-step walks starting from the vertex
v_{1}

\displaystyle\omega_{1}=v_{1}\rightarrow e_{3}\rightarrow v_{3}\rightarrow e_{2}

\displaystyle\quad\operatorname{sgn}\left(\omega_{1}\right)=-1\,,

\displaystyle\omega_{2}=v_{1}\rightarrow e_{1}\rightarrow v_{2}\rightarrow e_{2}

\displaystyle\quad\operatorname{sgn}\left(\omega_{2}\right)=1\,,

\displaystyle\omega_{3}=v_{1}\rightarrow e_{3}\rightarrow v_{3}\rightarrow e_{3}

\displaystyle\quad\operatorname{sgn}\left(\omega_{3}\right)=1\,,

\displaystyle\omega_{4}=v_{1}\rightarrow e_{3}\rightarrow v_{1}\rightarrow e_{3}

\displaystyle\quad\operatorname{sgn}\left(\omega_{4}\right)=1\,,

\displaystyle\omega_{5}=v_{1}\rightarrow e_{1}\rightarrow v_{1}\rightarrow e_{3}

\displaystyle\quad\operatorname{sgn}\left(\omega_{5}\right)=1\,,

\displaystyle\omega_{6}=v_{1}\rightarrow e_{1}\rightarrow v_{2}\rightarrow e_{1}

\displaystyle\quad\operatorname{sgn}\left(\omega_{6}\right)=-1\,,

\displaystyle\omega_{7}=v_{1}\rightarrow e_{1}\rightarrow v_{1}\rightarrow e_{1}

\displaystyle\quad\operatorname{sgn}\left(\omega_{7}\right)=-1\,,

\displaystyle\omega_{8}=v_{1}\rightarrow e_{3}\rightarrow v_{1}\rightarrow e_{1}

\displaystyle\quad\operatorname{sgn}\left(\omega_{8}\right)=-1.

(36)

There are two walks,
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
, between
v_{1}
and
e_{2}
with opposite signs,
therefore the matrix element in
\not{D}^{3}
corresponding to three-step walks between
v_{1}
and
e_{2}
is zero.

3
Walks on the Bethe lattice

Let us start this section by introducing the graph of our concern. The Bethe lattice is a
d
-regular infinite tree, i.e., each vertex in the tree is connected to
d
other vertices. We are interested in counting Dirac walks and simple random walks between two lattice sites on the Bethe lattice. When we study simple random walks, the lattice sites refer to vertices of the graph; and when we discuss Dirac walks, lattice sites refer to both vertices and the directed edges of the underlying graph.

In this Section
3
, we will start by reviewing well known results of simple random walks on the Bethe lattice. At first, we will study the
d=2
Bethe lattice which is simply the integer line
\mathbb{Z}
. We will then present the results for
d\geq 3
Bethe lattice by mapping the Bethe lattice to a half-line as discussed in
[
52
,
53
]
.
Based on the generating function techniques used by
[
52
,
53
]
, we will adapt our technique to understand the Dirac walk and present new results on its combinatorics on the Bethe lattice.

3.1
Review of the simple random walk

3.1.1
Simple random walk on the line

For understanding simple random walks on a line, we choose a coordinate system in such a way that vertices are labeled by some
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
x=0
is referred to as the origin.
For our system of focus, let the probability of a random walker going from some vertex
x
to
x+1
in a single step be
\alpha
and going from
x
to
x-1
in one step be
1-\alpha
(cf. Figure
3
).

Figure 3
:
A section of the integer line
\mathbb{Z}
. The probability of moving towards
+\infty
is
\alpha
(e.g.
-2\to-1
and
0\to 1
) and probability of moving towards
-\infty
is
1-\alpha
(e.g.
-1\to-2
and
1\to 0
).

Here,
\alpha
is a real number such that
0<\alpha<1
.
We define
h_{\tau}(0,x)
as the probability that a random walker is at some lattice site
x
in
\tau
steps where
\tau
is a non-negative integer, starting from some origin
0
; therefore
0\leq h_{\tau}(0,x)\leq 1
. The quantity
h_{\tau}(0,x)
follows the recursion

\displaystyle h_{\tau+1}(0,x)=\alpha\,h_{\tau}(0,x-1)+(1-\alpha)\,h_{\tau}(0,x+1)\,,

(37)

where the boundary condition reads

h_{0}(0,x)=\delta_{x,0}\,.

(38)

We define the generating function of the transition probability as

H_{0,x}(\lambda)=\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{\tau}h_{\tau}(0,x)\,.

(39)

This generating function will become the cornerstone of our analysis of the Green function. We multiply Equation (
37
) by
\lambda^{\tau+1}
and perform a summation to get

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{\tau+1}h_{\tau+1}(0,x)

\displaystyle=\lambda\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{\tau}\left(\alpha\,h_{\tau}(0,x-1)+(1-\alpha)\,h_{\tau}(0,x+1)\right)\,,

\displaystyle=H_{0,x}(\lambda)-\delta_{x,0}=\lambda\left(\alpha\,H_{0,x-1}(\lambda)+(1-\alpha)\,H_{0,x+1}(\lambda)\right)\,.

(40)

Owing to the linearity and homogeneity in
H_{0,x}
of (
40
) when
x
is non-zero, we can define two index shifting operators,
T
and
S
, such that

TH_{0,x}(\lambda)=H_{0,x+1}(\lambda)\,,\,\quad\text{if}\,\,x\geq 0\,,

(41)

and

SH_{0,x}(\lambda)=H_{0,x-1}(\lambda)\,,\,\quad\text{if}\,\,x\leq 0\,.

(42)

When
x\geq 1
, the recursion (
40
) can be rewritten as

TH_{0,x-1}(\lambda)=\lambda\left(\alpha\,H_{0,x-1}(\lambda)+(1-\alpha)T^{2}\,H_{0,x-1}(\lambda)\right)\,.

(43)

The condition
x\geq 1
ensures that by applying the operator
T
, the L.H.S. of (
40
) does not reach
H_{0,0}(\lambda)
, as the recursion has a different form for the latter.
As
\alpha
is independent of
x
, we can factorize (
43
) and get

\big(T-t_{+}(\lambda)\big)\big(T-t_{-}(\lambda)\big)H_{0,x-1}(\lambda)=0\,.

(44)

T-\lambda\left(\alpha+(1-\alpha)T^{2}\right)=0
is the characteristic equation and
t_{+}(\lambda)
and
t_{-}(\lambda)
are called the characteristic roots. The roots are written as follows

t_{\pm}(\lambda)=\frac{1\pm\sqrt{1-4(1-\alpha)\alpha\lambda^{2}}}{2(1-\alpha)\lambda}\,.

(45)

The recursion
40
now boils down to two independent linear recursions when
x\geq 1
.

(T-t_{\pm}(\lambda))H_{0,x-1}(\lambda)=0\,.

(46)

We pick the linear recursion with
t_{-}(\lambda)
which is regular for
\lambda\to 0
. We set
t_{-}(\lambda)=t(\lambda)
. Therefore,

TH_{0,x-1}(\lambda)=H_{0,x}(\lambda)=t(\lambda)H_{0,x-1}(\lambda)\,,\,\quad\text{if}\,\,x\geq 1

(47)

which leads to

H_{0,x}(\lambda)=(t(\lambda))^{x}H_{0,0}(\lambda)\,,\quad\text{if}\,x\geq 0\,.

(48)

A similar exercise can be performed when
x\leq-1
and we obtain the characteristic equation

\Big(S-\lambda\left(\alpha S^{2}+(1-\alpha)\right)\Big)H_{0,x+1}(\lambda)=0\,,

(49)

with characteristic roots

s_{\pm}(\lambda)=\frac{1\pm\sqrt{1-4(1-\alpha)\alpha\lambda^{2}}}{2\alpha\lambda}\,.

(50)

We similarly pick up the characteristic root
s_{-}(\lambda)
, which is regular for
\lambda\to 0
and set
s_{-}(\lambda)=s(\lambda)
,

SH_{0,x}(\lambda)=H_{0,x-1}(\lambda)=s(\lambda)H_{0,x}(\lambda)\,,

(51)

and therefore,

H_{0,x}(\lambda)=(s(\lambda))^{|x|}H_{0,0}(\lambda)\,,\quad\text{if }\,x\leq 0\,.

(52)

For
x=0
, Eq. (
40
) becomes

H_{0,0}(\lambda)-1=\lambda(\alpha\,H_{0,-1}(\lambda)\,+(1-\alpha)\,H_{0,1}(\lambda))\,.

(53)

By substituting
H_{0,1}(\lambda)=H_{0,0}(\lambda)t(\lambda)
and
H_{0,-1}(\lambda)=H_{0,0}(\lambda)s(\lambda)
we get

\displaystyle H_{0,0}(\lambda)-1

\displaystyle=\lambda\big(\alpha\,H_{0,0}(\lambda)\,s(\lambda)+(1-\alpha)\,t(\lambda)H_{0,0}(\lambda)\big)\,,

(54)

\displaystyle H_{0,0}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-4(1-\alpha)\alpha\lambda^{2}}}\,,

(55)

implying from (
48
) and (
52
),

H_{0,x}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}\frac{\left(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4(1-\alpha)\alpha\lambda^{2}}}{2(1-\alpha)\lambda}\right)^{x}}{\sqrt{1-4(1-\alpha)\alpha\lambda^{2}}}\,,&\quad\text{if}\,x\geq 0\,,\\
\frac{\left(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4(1-\alpha)\alpha\lambda^{2}}}{2\alpha\lambda}\right)^{|x|}}{\sqrt{1-4(1-\alpha)\alpha\lambda^{2}}}\,,&\quad\text{if}\,x\leq 0\,.\end{cases}

(56)

For
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
, we have
s(\lambda)=t(\lambda)
,
and we get

H_{0,x}(\lambda)=H_{0,0}(\lambda)(t(\lambda))^{|x|}\,,

(57)

and

H_{0,x}(\lambda)=\frac{\left(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}}{\lambda}\right)^{|x|}}{\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}}\,.

(58)

From Eq. (
58
), we can derive the transience or the recurrence of a random walk. A random walk is called transient if

\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}h_{\tau}(0,0)=\lim_{\lambda\to 1}H_{0,0}(\lambda)<\infty\,,

(59)

otherwise, they are called recurrent
[
44
]
.
For the case of biased random walk (
\alpha\neq\frac{1}{2}
), we have

\lim_{\lambda\to 1}H_{0,0}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-4(1-\alpha)\alpha}}\,,

(60)

and for an unbiased random walk (
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
)

\lim_{\lambda\to 1}H_{0,0}(\lambda)\to\infty\,.

(61)

Therefore, the unbiased simple random walk in one dimension is recurrent, whereas one can easily read off that the biased random walk is transient.

3.1.2
Simple random walk on the
d\geq 3
Bethe lattice

Random walks on
d\geq 3
Bethe lattice have been extensively studied in
[
53
,
52
]
and references within.
They used a coordinate system where some vertex is chosen as a origin and is labeled
0
. As all the vertices are topologically equivalent, one is free to choose any vertex as the origin. All the vertices which are
x
edges away from the origin are labeled
x
, where
x
is a non-negative integer. There are
d(d-1)^{x-1}
such vertices which are
x
edges away from the origin. To compute the simple random walk statistics, one then maps all the vertices
x
edges away from the origin of the Bethe lattice to a vertex
x
edges away from the origin of the half-line. A visual depiction of a Bethe lattice can be found in Figure
5
and corresponding mapping to the halfline in Figure
5
.

Figure 4
:

A
d=3
Bethe lattice. The vertex labels give their distance to the origin of the lattice in units of edge length.

Figure 5
:

The half-line to which the Bethe lattice is mapped. The (anomalous) weight 1 on the step between vertices
0
and
1
reflects the boundary conditions (
63
).

If a walk is on a vertex at
x\neq 0
edges away, then in one step, the probability of going one step further away from
0
is
\frac{d-1}{d}
and to go to one step closer to
0
is
\frac{1}{d}
. If a simple random walker is at
x=0
, it ends up at
x=1
in one step with probability
1
.
This observation allows us to map the simple random walk on the Bethe lattice to a random walk on the half-line with drift at the cost of losing the information about the angular degrees of freedom.
The mapping is obtained through the recursion (
37
) with
\alpha=\frac{d-1}{d}
.
For the moment, we will perform the calculation for general
\alpha
and then substitute
\alpha=\frac{d-1}{d}
for the final result. The recursion for a simple random walk on the half-line with a drift starting from
0
is, when
x\geq 2
,

h_{\tau+1}(0,x)=\alpha h_{\tau}(0,x-1)+(1-\alpha)h_{\tau}(0,x+1)\,,

(62)

and with reflecting boundary conditions at
0
,

\displaystyle h_{0}(0,x)

\displaystyle=\delta_{x,0}\,,

(63)

\displaystyle h_{\tau+1}(0,0)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)h_{\tau}(0,1)\,,

(64)

\displaystyle h_{\tau+1}(0,1)

\displaystyle=h_{\tau}(0,0)+(1-\alpha)h_{\tau}(0,2)\,.

(65)

The prefactor for
h_{\tau}(0,1)
is
1
as we will surely go to lattice sites labeled
1
in the
(\tau+1)
-th step if we are at
0
after
\tau
steps.
These equations can be solved with the generating function methods introduced in the Section
3.1
.
The resulting generating functions concerning the boundary are

\displaystyle H_{0,0}(\lambda)-1

\displaystyle=\lambda(1-\alpha)H_{0,1}(\lambda)\,,

(66)

\displaystyle H_{0,1}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\lambda\left(H_{0,0}(\lambda)+(1-\alpha)H_{0,2}(\lambda)\right),

(67)

and for the interior (
x\geq 2
) is

{}H_{0,x}(\lambda)=\lambda\left(\alpha\,H_{0,x-1}(\lambda)+(1-\alpha)\,H_{0,x+1}(\lambda)\right)\,.

(68)

Similarly to (
40
), for
x\geq 2
, Equation (
68
) can be written in terms of the index shift operator
T
, defined in (
41
).
This leads to same characteristic equation for the positive sector of (
40
), with different boundary conditions with the solution

TH_{0,x-1}(\lambda)=H_{0,x}(\lambda)=t(\lambda)H_{0,x-1}(\lambda)\,,\quad\text{if }x\geq 2\,,

(69)

and thus,

H_{0,x}(\lambda)=t(\lambda)^{x-1}H_{0,1}(\lambda)\,,\,\quad\text{if}\,\,x\geq 1\,.

(70)

We then use
H_{0,2}(\lambda)=H_{0,1}(\lambda)t(\lambda)
to solve for
H_{0,1}(\lambda)
and
H_{0,0}(\lambda)
for Equations (
66
) and (
67
).
After substitution, one finds

H_{0,0}(\lambda)=\frac{2\alpha}{\sqrt{1-4(1-\alpha)\alpha\lambda^{2}}+2\alpha-1}\,,

(71)

and

H_{0,1}(\lambda)=\frac{2\lambda}{1-2(1-\alpha)\lambda^{2}+\sqrt{1-4(1-\alpha)\alpha\lambda^{2}}}\,.

(72)

Going further using (
70
),

H_{0,x}(\lambda)=\frac{2\left(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4(1-\alpha)\alpha\lambda^{2}}}{2(1-\alpha)\lambda}\right)^{x}}{\sqrt{1-4(1-\alpha)\alpha\lambda^{2}}+2\alpha-1}\,,\quad\,\text{if}\,x\geq 1\,.

(73)

Now we are in a position to replace
\alpha=\frac{d-1}{d}
. We get

\displaystyle H_{0,0}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{2(d-1)}{\sqrt{d^{2}-4(d-1)\lambda^{2}}+d-2}\,,

(74)

\displaystyle H_{0,x}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{2d\left(\frac{d-\sqrt{d^{2}-4(d-1)\lambda^{2}}}{2\lambda}\right)^{x}}{\sqrt{d^{2}-4(d-1)\lambda^{2}}+d-2}\,,\quad\,\text{if}\,x\geq 1\,.

(75)

We are interested in computing the probability of reaching one particular vertex at
x
edges away on the Bethe lattice.
We can then, by symmetry, extract the information of probability of reaching a particular site on the Bethe lattice at
x
by computing
\hat{H}_{0,x}(\lambda):=\frac{H_{0,x}(\lambda)}{d(d-1)^{x-1}}
, if
x\geq 1
and
\hat{H}_{0,0}(\lambda):=H_{0,0}(\lambda)
,

\hat{H}_{0,x}(\lambda)=\frac{2(d-1)\left(\frac{d-\sqrt{d^{2}-4(d-1)\lambda^{2}}}{2(d-1)\lambda}\right)^{x}}{\sqrt{d^{2}-4(d-1)\lambda^{2}}+d-2}\,.

(76)

Remark that
\hat{H}_{x,y}(\lambda)
is the generating function for the quantity
\hat{h}_{\tau}(x,y)
, which will be introduce later in Section
3.3.2
as the transition probability that a random walker starts on a particular vertex
x
and ends at a particular vertex
y
in
\tau
steps on a Bethe lattice.
In the case of the Bethe lattice, we observe that

\lim_{\lambda\to 1}\hat{H}_{0,0}(\lambda)=\frac{d-1}{d-2}<\infty\,,

(77)

implying that the simple random walk on the Bethe lattice for
d\geq 3
is transient.

3.2
Dirac walk

3.2.1
Dirac walk on the line

Based on the techniques reviewed in section
3.1.1
,
we want to present some new results on the combinatorics of the process generated by the Dirac operator, the Dirac walk, on the Bethe lattice. We assign a coordinate system on the
d=2
Bethe lattice such that a vertex is labeled as the origin, and the edge incident to the right of the origin is labeled
1
, and the edge incident to the left of
0
is
-1
.
Furthermore, every edge is labeled by an odd number
2x+1
and every vertex is labeled by an even number
2x
, where
x\in\mathbb{Z}
. We will assume that all the edges are oriented from
-\infty
to
+\infty
as shown in Figure
6
.

We introduce
\alpha
(resp.
1-\alpha
) as the magnitude of the weight of the Dirac walk from a vertex to an edge towards
+\infty
(resp.
-\infty
).
Similarly, we introduce
\beta
(resp.
1-\beta
) as the magnitude of the weight of the Dirac walk from an edge to a vertex towards
+\infty
(resp.
-\infty
) direction.
If the edge of concern is incoming (resp. outgoing) with respect to the vertex of concern, then we furthermore assign
+
(resp.
-
) to the weight as in Figure
1
.
As a result, on the
d=2
Bethe lattice with all the orientations of all the edges directed from
-\infty
to
+\infty
, the weight of a Dirac walk starting from
2x
to the neighboring edge
2x+1
(resp.
2x-1
) is
-\alpha
(resp.
+(1-\alpha)
).
Similarly, we assign the weight
+\beta
(resp.
-(1-\beta)
) for the Dirac walk starting from
2x+1
to
2x+2
(resp.
2x
).
See Figure
6
.

Figure 6
:
A directed line graph with all edges pointing from
-\infty
to
\infty
and the associated weights for a single step of a Dirac walk, from vertex to edge and edge to vertex.

We define
p_{\tau}(z,2x)
to be the weight of a walk starting from some lattice site
z\in\mathbb{Z}
and arriving at any vertex
2x
in
\tau
steps, and similarly,
f_{\tau}(z,2x+1)
as the weight of a walker arriving at any edge
2x+1
in
\tau
steps.
The corresponding generating functions are

\displaystyle P_{z,2x}(\lambda)=\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{\tau}p_{\tau}(z,2x)\,,

(78)

\displaystyle F_{z,2x+1}(\lambda)=\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{\tau}f_{\tau}(z,2x+1)\,.

(79)

In the following computation we will consider the walker starting from the vertex
z=0
. This implies that we will be on a vertex in even number of steps and on an edge in odd number of steps.

The weights of the possible steps do not add up to one owing to the signed nature of the walk, so the Dirac walk is not probabilistic. Nevertheless, the weights are defined in such a way that, if we multiply by the total number of walks in
2\tau
steps, i.e.,
2^{2\tau}
and compute
2^{2\tau}p_{2\tau}(0,2x)
, we will get the signed sum of Dirac walks from
0
to
2x
in
2\tau
steps, i.e
\not{D}^{2\tau}(0,2x)
. This definition of weights will be instrumental in the discussion of Green function (
30
) in Section
4
.
The weights are related by the following recursion for any
x
,

\displaystyle f_{2\tau+1}(0,2x+1)=(1-\alpha)\,p_{2\tau}(0,2x+2)-\alpha\,p_{2\tau}(0,2x)\,,

(80)

\displaystyle p_{2\tau+2}(0,2x)=\beta\,f_{2\tau+1}(0,2x-1)-(1-\beta)\,f_{2\tau+1}(0,2x+1)\,

(81)

and the boundary condition

p_{0}(0,2x)=\delta_{2x,0}\,.

(82)

At the level of generating functions, the recursions turn out to be, for any
x
,

\displaystyle F_{0,2x+1}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\lambda\big((1-\alpha)\,P_{0,2x+2}(\lambda)-\alpha\,P_{0,2x}(\lambda)\big)\,,

(83)

\displaystyle P_{0,2x}(\lambda)-\delta_{2x,0}

\displaystyle=\lambda\big(\beta\,F_{0,2x-1}(\lambda)-(1-\beta)\,F_{0,2x+1}(\lambda)\big)\,.

(84)

It is easier to deal with a two-step recursion as it decouples the edges and vertex.
The recursion now stands as, for any
x
,

\displaystyle p_{2\tau+2}(0,2x)

\displaystyle=-\alpha\beta\,p_{2\tau}(0,2x-2)-(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)\,p_{2\tau}(0,2x+2)

\displaystyle+\big(\alpha(1-\beta)+\beta(1-\alpha)\big)\,p_{2\tau}(0,2x)\,,

(85)

\displaystyle f_{2\tau+1}(0,2x+1)

\displaystyle=-\alpha\beta\,f_{2\tau-1}(0,2x-1)-(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)\,f_{2\tau-1}(0,2x+3)

\displaystyle+\big(\alpha(1-\beta)+\beta(1-\alpha)\big)\,f_{2\tau-1}(0,2x+1)\,,

(86)

and at the level of generating functions, we have

\displaystyle P_{0,2x}(\lambda)-\delta_{2x,0}=\lambda^{2}\Big(-\alpha\beta\,P_{0,2x-2}(\lambda)+\big(\alpha(1-\beta)+\beta(1-\alpha)\big)P_{0,2x}(\lambda)-(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)\,P_{0,2x+2}(\lambda)\Big)\,,

(87)

\displaystyle F_{0,2x+1}(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}\Big(-\alpha\beta\,F_{0,2x-1}(\lambda)+\big(\alpha(1-\beta)+\beta(1-\alpha)\big)F_{0,2x+1}(\lambda)-(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)\,F_{0,2x+3}(\lambda)\Big)\,.

(88)

Similarly to our analysis of the simple random walk, by carefully paying attention to the fact that the origin is special in the recursion relations, we introduce index forward and backward shift operators,
R
and
Q
, for some
x
such that

\displaystyle RP_{0,2x}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=P_{0,2x+2}(\lambda)\,,\,\quad\text{if}\,\,x\geq 0\,,

(89)

\displaystyle QP_{0,2x}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=P_{0,2x-2}(\lambda)\,,\,\quad\text{if}\,\,x\leq 0\,.

(90)

Solving for
P
, we will then be able to retrieve
F
from Eq. (
84
). On the positive half-line, we have

\Bigg(R-\lambda^{2}\Big(-(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)R^{2}+\big(\alpha(1-\beta)+\beta(1-\alpha)\big)R-\alpha\beta\Big)\Bigg)P_{0,2x}(\lambda)=0\,,\,\quad\text{if}\,\,x\geq 0\,,

(91)

which gives us the characteristic equation
that has two solutions

r_{P}{}_{\pm}(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}(-2\alpha\beta+\alpha+\beta)\pm\sqrt{\lambda^{4}(\alpha-\beta)^{2}+2\lambda^{2}(\alpha(2\beta-1)-\beta)+1}-1}{2(\alpha-1)(\beta-1)\lambda^{2}}\,.

(92)

We keep the solution regular at
\lambda=0
, rewriting it as
r_{P}{}_{+}(\lambda)=r_{P}(\lambda)
.
The recursion is solved by

P_{0,2x}(\lambda)=r_{P}(\lambda)^{x}P_{0,0}(\lambda)\,,\,\quad\text{if}\,\,x\geq 0\,.

(93)

Similarly, we can compute the characteristic roots for the negative half-line,
x\leq 0
.
We obtain,

{\Bigg(Q-\lambda^{2}\Big(-\alpha\beta Q^{2}+\big(\alpha(1-\beta)+\beta(1-\alpha)\big)Q-(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)\Big)\Bigg)P_{0,2x}(\lambda)=0\,,}

(94)

with solutions

q_{P\pm}(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}(-2\alpha\beta+\alpha+\beta)-1\pm\sqrt{\lambda^{4}(\alpha-\beta)^{2}+2\lambda^{2}(\alpha(2\beta-1)-\beta)+1}}{2\alpha\beta\lambda^{2}}\,.

(95)

If we pick up the solution
q_{P+}(\lambda)
which is regular for
\lambda\to 0
, we obtain by setting
q_{P+}(\lambda)=q_{P}(\lambda)
,

P_{0,2x}(\lambda)=q_{P}(\lambda)^{|x|}P_{0,0}(\lambda)\,,\quad\text{if}\,x\leq 0\,.

(96)

We solve for
P_{0,0}(\lambda)
by substituting
P_{0,\pm 2}(\lambda)
in terms of
P_{0,0}(\lambda)
using (
93
) and (
96
) in (
87
)

\displaystyle P_{0,0}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=1+\lambda^{2}P_{0,0}(\lambda)\Big(-\alpha\beta q_{P}(\lambda)-(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)r_{P}(\lambda)+\big(\alpha(1-\beta)+(1-\alpha)\beta\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda^{4}(\alpha-\beta)^{2}+2\lambda^{2}(\alpha(2\beta-1)-\beta)+1}}\,.

(97)

We can then compute the vertex to edge transition generating function using (
83

3.4
Asymptotic expansion

The spectral dimension
d_{s}
of a geometry, with respect to simple random walks is determined by the power law scaling (assuming that a power law behavior exists) of the self return probability in
\tau
steps
h_{\tau}(z,z)
in the limit
\tau\to\infty

[
18
,
54
,
55
]
. If the simple random walk starts at some vertex
z
,

h_{\tau}(z,z)\sim\frac{C^{-\tau}}{\tau^{\frac{d_{s}}{2}}}\,,

(166)

where
C
is some constant such that
|C|\geq 1
(which may have different numerical value in different equations) and the symbol
\sim
represents asymptotic scaling at large
\tau
.

14
14

14

By asymptotic scaling, we mean that the quantities on the left and right of the symbol differ only by a multiplicative constant in the appropriate limit, and the constant is independent of the limiting variable.

Analogously, we wish to generalize the definition to self return transition weights for the Dirac walk, and define the spectral dimension of a geometry with respect to the Dirac walk starting at some vertex
2z
as

p_{\tau}(2z,2z)\sim\frac{C^{-\tau}}{\tau^{\frac{d_{s}}{2}}}\,,

(167)

and similarly for edges, using the transition weight
f_{\tau}
.
We will derive the power law scaling of self return transition weights for the Dirac walks and probabilities for simple random walks using asymptotic analysis of the generating functions.
In the context of asymptotic analysis, it is useful to view our generating functions as complex-valued functions in
\lambda
. Our strategy would be to use singularity analysis described in
[
56
]
. It was shown that for a function of type
(b-a\lambda)^{-\alpha}
,
where
\alpha
is a real number barring negative integers (
\alpha\notin\mathbb{Z}_{-}
),
having a dominant singularity at
\frac{b}{a}
,

\left[\lambda^{\tau}\right](b-a\lambda)^{-\alpha}\sim\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{\tau}\frac{\tau^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\right)\right)\,.

(168)

The symbol
\mathcal{O}(|f(x)|)
represents terms that are bounded by

c|f(x)|

for some positive constant
c
, and the singularity closest to
0
is called the dominant singularity.

\left[\lambda\right]^{\tau}
represents the
\tau^{\text{th}}
coefficient in series expansion of
(b-a\lambda)^{-\alpha}
.

3.4.1

On the line (or the

4
QFT on the Bethe lattice

In this section, we combine the results obtained in Section
2
to discuss the two-point function
C(x,y)
obtained for free scalars in (
14
) and free Fermions in (
30
) on the Bethe lattice.
The analytic properties of the Green function of an operator contain information about its spectrum. For details, one can see
[
57
]
, and some relevant results have been reviewed in Section
4.1
.
After recalling the Green function of free scalars, we will present our results for free Fermions on the Bethe lattice.

4.1
Brief overview of Green function

The Green function
G(m)
of a linear Hermitian differential operator
L
is defined as the resolvent with respect to some complex-valued parameter
m
. In operator form, it can be written as

G(m)=\frac{1}{m-L}

(177)

and in terms of matrix elements,

[m-L(x)]G(x,y;m)=\delta(x-y).

Simple real poles correspond to discrete eigenvalues of
L
, while branch cuts on the real
m
-axis correspond to continuous eigenvalues. It is worth noting that there could be other types of singularities in
G(m)

[
57
]
, but that discussion is beyond our scope. If
m=E
, where
E
belongs to the continuous spectrum, it is useful to define the following Green functions, analytic in the upper and lower half-plane respectively

\displaystyle G^{+}(x,y;E)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}G(x,y;E+\mathrm{i}s)\,,

(178)

\displaystyle G^{-}(x,y;E)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}G(x,y;E-\mathrm{i}s)\,.

(179)

The density of states per unit volume is given by the discontinuity across the cut

\varrho(x;E)=\mp\frac{1}{\pi}\mathrm{Im}{G^{\pm}(x,x;E)}=\absolutevalue{\frac{1}{\pi} \mathrm{Im}{G^{\pm}(x, x; E)}}.

(180)

Detailed derivations can be found in the book
[
57
]
.

4.2
Scalar QFT on the Bethe lattice

We will review here the Green functions and spectra of scalar fields on the Bethe lattice, first
d=2
, corresponding to the line, then
d\geq 3
, retrieving results from
[
57
]
.

We recall that for the scalar field theory on a graph, by setting
L=-\Delta_{\Gamma}
which is the negative graph Laplacian in (
177
), the Green function takes the form

G(m)=\frac{1}{\Delta_{\Gamma}+m\mathbf{1}}=\frac{1}{D-A+m\mathbf{1}}=\frac{1}{D+m\mathbf{1}}\sum^{\infty}_{\tau=0}\left(\frac{1}{D+m\mathbf{1}}A\right)^{\tau}\,.

(181)

As the Bethe lattice is
d
-regular, the degree matrix takes a rather simple form
D=d\mathbf{1}
. Therefore, we can write

G(m)=\frac{1}{d+m}\sum^{\infty}_{\tau=0}\left(\frac{A}{d+m}\right)^{\tau}\,.

(182)

Reparametrizing
m=\frac{d(1-\lambda)}{\lambda}
, we get in the sum of (
181
) for each matrix element

\sum^{\infty}_{\tau=0}\left(\frac{A}{d+m}\right)^{\tau}(x,y)=\sum^{\infty}_{\tau=0}\lambda^{\tau}\left(\frac{A}{d}\right)^{\tau}(x,y)\,.

(183)

The right-hand side is the generating function
H_{x,y}(\lambda)
for the line and
\hat{H}_{x,y}(\lambda)
for
d\geq 3
Bethe lattice respectively, as discussed in Section
2.1
.

4.2.1
Free scalar on the line

For a line, one can compute the Green function by setting

\lambda=\frac{d}{d+m}=\frac{2}{2+m}
and using (
58
),

G(x,y;m)=\frac{H_{x,y}(2/(2+m))}{2+m}=\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(m+2-\sqrt{m(m+4)}\right)\right)^{|g(x,y)|}}{\sqrt{m(m+4)}}\,.

(184)

We look at the auto-correlation function

G(x,x;m)=\frac{H_{x,x}(2/(2+m))}{2+m}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m(m+4)}}\,.

(185)

The function
G(x,x;m)
has a branch cut between
-4<m<0
. If
m=E\pm is
, where
E
is an eigenvalue of our (negative) Laplacian, we compute the density of states per site
\varrho(x;E)
as

\varrho(x;E)=\mp\frac{1}{\pi}{\rm Im}\{G^{\pm}(x,x;E)\}=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{-E(E+4)}}\,,

(186)

where
E\in(-4,0)
as explained in (
180
).
The branch points, denoted by
E_{g}
, are called the band edges of the Green function.
The behavior of
G^{\pm}(x,x;E)
around the band edge characterizes the singularities of the Green function. For a free scalar field on a line,
E_{g}=0

5
Conclusions

To summarize, our work studied various spectral properties of a random process diffusing on the Bethe lattice of degree
d\geq 2
, with the square root of the Laplacian, analogous to a Dirac operator.
We have in particular obtained its spectral dimension as well as the Green functions between any two sites of the lattice.
We relied on the combinatorial reformulation of the Dirac operator in terms of signed random walks suggested by
[
28
]
for which we computed the generating functions through recursion relations. In order to study Dirac walks starting at a vertex, we used the well-known mapping of the Bethe lattice to the half-line
[
36
]
. Here, because of oriented edges, walks starting from an edge needed a mapping to a line with a defect orientation.
The spectral dimensions of the Dirac walk match those of the simple random walk, that is,
d_{s}=1
when
d=2
and
d_{s}=3
when
d\geq 3
. The spectra differ from those of the Laplacian by developing a gap. Although we computed the Dirac operator spectra for a fixed orientation, it is well-known that they are independent of orientations
[
25
]
.

As future outlook, a natural field theoretical extension is the inclusion of self-interactions. One could follow self-consistent methods inspired from Dynamical Mean Field Theory
[
58
]
to calculate the spectrum and higher
n
-point functions.
Another question is to evaluate the asymptotics of the off-diagonal generating function of the transition weights, in order to compute the exponential factor of the associated heat kernel.

There are a few further research avenues that motivated our random processes on trees.
First, trees stand as the most elementary lowest dimensional structure that can admit some discrete probability measure.
They also enjoy full solvability from recurrence relations as done with Galton-Watson trees
[
59
]
.
Secondly, tensor models
[
60
]
, whose Feynman diagram duals provide a discretization of piecewise-linear manifolds at finite
N
, display in their large-
N
limit a branching behavior of the type of Galton-Watson trees, with Hausdorff
d_{H}=2
and spectral dimension
d_{s}=4/3

[
61
]
.
Can we relate couplings between additional fields and random tensors to a random process on such trees?
Another interesting line of thought is the application of our results to a discrete version of holography,
p
-adic AdS/CFT
[
62
]
, where the boundary is the
p
-adic line and the Bethe lattice (also called the Bruhat-Tits tree) stands for the bulk.
Spin fields in the bulk were first considered in
[
63
]
.
Their Fermionic part of the action is similar to ours, but they include a gauge field as well.
Field theory on the
p
-adic numbers has attracted a lot of interest due to its solvability inherited from its hierarchical structure, see for example
[
64
,
65
]
.
Finally, an obviously important problem is to continue our exploration of the Dirac walk on more general graphs that involve loops (higher complexes, random regular, Erdös-Renyi, etc.) to see if this walk is at all related to the standard notion of Fermions. Recalling the generalization of the Dirac operator
\not{D}
to higher order complexes, introduced in
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
, it would be interesting to search for a combinatorial interpretation of such Dirac operators, that could generalize the expansions over spanning forests
[
24
]
.
