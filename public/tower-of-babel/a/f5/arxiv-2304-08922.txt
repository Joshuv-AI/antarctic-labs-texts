A uniform bound for solutions to a thermo-diffusive system
Joonhyun La, Jean-Michel Roquejoffre, Lenya Ryzhik

Abstract
We obtain uniform in time L ∞ L^{\infty} -bounds for the solutions to a class of thermo-diffusive systems. The nonlinearity is assumed to be at most sub-exponentially growing at infinity and have a linear behavior near zero.



1
Introduction

The thermo-diffusive system

We investigate the global boundedness of the positive solutions to the initial
value problem for reaction-diffusion systems of the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=\nu\Delta u-ug(v),

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v=\kappa\Delta v+ug(v),

posed in the whole space
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, with the nonlinear term
g(v)

having possibly super-linear growth. It has been known since Fujita’s work
[
14
]

that solutions to the nonlinear heat equation

\partial_{t}v=\Delta v+v^{p}

may blow up in finite time. On the other hand, in the special case
\kappa=\nu
of the
system (
1.1
), the function
z(t,x)=u(t,x)+v(t,x)
solves the linear heat equation

\partial_{t}z=\kappa\Delta z,

so that both
u(t,x)
and
v(t,x)
are globally bounded in time. In this work,
we address the question of how the interplay between the two equations
in (
1.1
) may lead to uniform boundedness
of the solutions.

In (
1.1
), we think of
u(t,x)
as fuel concentration that is consumed
in the chemical reaction, and of
v(t,x)
as the concentration of the products of the reaction, or as the reactant temperature.
The systems we discuss in this paper
appear naturally, for example, in the exothermic combustion modeling and ecology
[
9
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
.
In accordance with
this interpretation, we will assume that the reaction rate
g(v)
is positive:

g(v)\geq 0,~~\hbox{ for all $v\geq 0$.}

(1.2)

This assumption, together with the comparison principle, ensures that

\hbox{$u(t,x)>0$ and $v(t,x)>0$ for all $x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$ and $t>0$},

(1.3)

as long as the initial conditions satisfy

u_{0}(x):=u(0,x)\geq 0
and
v_{0}(x):=v(0,x)\geq 0
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

(1.4)

and neither
u_{0}(x)\equiv 0
nor
v_{0}(x)\equiv 0
.
We will always assume that (
1.2
) and (
1.4
) hold
and will only consider non-negative solutions to (
1.1
).

In addition, the initial conditions
u_{0}(x)
and
v_{0}(x)
are assumed to be uniformly bounded: there exists
K>0
so that
at
t=0
we have

\displaystyle 0\leq u_{0}(x)\leq K,~~0\leq v_{0}(x)\leq K,~~\hbox{ for all $x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$.}

(1.5)

The last assumption on the initial conditions is that there is a uniformly positive lower bound on the sum
u_{0}(x)+v_{0}(x)
:

0<K^{-1}\leq u_{0}(x)+v_{0}(x)\leq K,~~\hbox{ for all $x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$.}

(1.6)

For example, assumption (
1.6
) is satisfied if the fuel is present everywhere initially:
u_{0}(x)\geq K
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
It also holds if the initial conditions
are front-like, in the sense that

u_{0}(x)+v_{0}(x)=K,

(1.7)

where
K>0
is a non-negative constant, and there exists a direction
e\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
so that
u_{0}(y+re)\to 0
as
r\to+\infty
and
v_{0}(y+re)\to 0
as
r\to-\infty
,
for each
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
fixed.

We will make two extra assumptions on the nonlinearity
g(v)
, in addition to (
1.2
):
g(0)=0
and there exist
c_{1}>0
,
c_{2}>0
,
\rho\in[0,1)
and
Z>0
so that

\displaystyle c_{1}v\leq g(v)\leq c_{2}\exp(Zv^{\rho})\text{ for all $v\geq 0$.}

(1.8)

To avoid minor extra technicalities, we also suppose that the function
g(v)
is smooth. It follows
that there exists a constant
c_{3}>0
so that

c_{1}v\leq g(v)\leq c_{3}v,~~\hbox{ for all $0\leq v\leq 1$.}

(1.9)

The positivity of
g(v)
guarantees a uniform upper bound
for
u(t,x)
. More, precisely,
the maximum principle for the standard heat equation implies that

0\leq u(t,x)\leq K,\hbox{ for all $t\geq 0$ and $x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$},

(1.10)

as long as (
1.5
) holds at
t=0
.

Let us comment that one can consider systems with more general nonlinearities of the form

\begin{split}\partial_{t}u=\nu\Delta u-f_{1}(u,v),\\
\partial_{t}v=\kappa\Delta v+f_{2}(u,v),\end{split}

(1.11)

with appropriate assumptions on the nonlinearities
f_{1,2}(u,v)
but in this paper we prefer to keep the setting as simple as possible.

Global bounds on the solutions: bounded domains

Let us first recall some of the existing results on global existence and bounds
for the solutions to the thermo-diffusive systems and closely related models
in bounded domains.
The literature is truly vast, as they arise in a wide range of applied sciences, from chemistry to atmospheric sciences.

The question of global existence and uniform bound for such models was
originally posed by R. Martin in the 1970’s.
An important step was the work of Hollis, Martin and Pierre
[
19
]

on a system of the form (
1.11
) with at most
polynomially growing nonlinearities, typically with
the assumptions that
(i) the reactions are balanced, in the sense that

-M\leq f_{1}(u,v)+f_{2}(u,v)\leq M,

(1.12)

and that (ii) an
a priori
estimate, such as (
1.10
), holds for
one of the unknowns, say, for
u(t,x)
.
To give a glimpse of the needed ingredients, let us briefly
review a simplified proof of the main results of
[
19
]
in the special case

M=0
in (
1.12
), and with the Dirichlet boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
:

u(t,x)=v(t,x)=0\ \ \hbox{on $\partial\Omega$}.

(1.13)

The key observation is
that an auxiliary unknown

W(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(u(s,x)+\frac{\nu}{\kappa}v(s,x)\bigl)~ds,

satisfies a forced heat equation

\partial_{t}W-\nu\Delta W=\frac{\nu}{\kappa}\bigl(u_{0}(x)+v_{0}(x)\bigl)+(1-\frac{\nu}{\kappa})u(t,x),

(1.14)

with the Dirichlet boundary condition
W(t,x)=0
on
\partial\Omega
.
As the right side of (
1.14
) is bounded by assumption (ii) above, and the domain
\Omega
is bounded, the function
W(t,x)
is
uniformly bounded, by a comparison to a multiple of the time-independent
solution to

-\nu\Delta\bar{W}=1,

(1.15)

with
\bar{W}=0
on
\partial\Omega
.
The standard parabolic regularity results in bounded domains
[
23
]

imply then that
\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}
is uniformly bounded for
all
p\in(1,+\infty)
. Because
f_{2}(u,v)
has at most polynomial growth in
v
,
while
u
is bounded
by assumption (ii), we deduce that

\|f_{2}\bigl(u(t,\cdot),v(t,\cdot))\bigl\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}
is also uniformly bounded for all
p\in(1,+\infty)
. Another application of parabolic regularity
gives the uniform boundedness of
v
, finishing the proof.

In
[
19
]
, the bounds are derived from a more sophisticated duality method that
has had applications to more general systems than (
1.11
). Surprisingly,
the
assumption (
1.12
) cannot be much relaxed, as
Pierre and Schmitt
[
31
]
constructed examples of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}

where
the left side bound
in (
1.11
) does not hold, and where solutions blow up in a finite time.

When
f_{1}
and/or
f_{2}
grow faster than any polynomial, less is known. Global existence of weak solutions for systems of the form (
1.11
) is proved in Pierre
[
30
]
under the additional conditions that one of the
f_{i}
’s grows linearly, while no assumption is made on the other. Whether the solutions are classical is not known. The question is also still open for an arbitrary
g(v)
in the case of our original system (
1.1
). We refer to
[
29
]
for an extensive literature review, especially for numerous results on bounded domains,
with either Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions.

Another line of investigation concern systems modeling reversible reactions. With no intention of being general, let us provide an example of such systems. For a chemical process involving four reactants
A_{i},1\leq i\leq 4
with mass fractions
Y_{i}
, the progress of the reaction

A_{1}+A_{2}\rightleftharpoons A_{3}+A_{4}

can be described by the system

\partial_{t}Y_{i}-d_{i}\Delta Y_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}(Y_{1}Y_{2}-Y_{3}Y_{4}),

(1.16)

with
\varepsilon_{i}>0
if
i=1,2
and
<0
if
i=3,4
. Interestingly,
such models may be derived from reactive Boltzmann equations, as shown by
Bisi and Desvillettes
[
4
]
, which gives a natural way to
introduce an entropy. This has opened fruitful research directions, such as global existence questions in Desvillettes et al.
[
11
]
, trend to equilibrium
in Devillettes, Fellner and Tang
[
10
]
,
and systems coupling inside to surface dynamics in Fellner, Latos and Tang
[
13
]
.

The case of the whole space

The global existence proof for solutions to (
1.1
) with polynomially growing
nonlinearities could be
reproduced essentially
verbatim in the whole space. However, the lack of a spectral gap estimate on the Laplacian
makes the uniform bounds on the whole space a much
more challenging problem. In addition, there is,
potentially, an infinite supply of fuel, unlike in a bounded domain.
This, in
principle, could lead to an unbounded growth of the temperature. On the other hand,
there is an elegant elementary
observation from the paper
[
24
]
by Martin and
Pierre that contains many beautiful insights into reaction-diffusion systems.

Proposition 1.1

Consider the system (
1.1
) posed in the whole space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
under the assumptions (
1.2
) and (
1.5
).
If
\nu\geq\kappa
then the solution to (
1.1
) exists for all
t\geq 0
and there exists a constant
C_{0}
such that

0\leq u(t,x)\leq C_{0}
and

0\leq v(t,x)\leq C_{0}
for all
t>0
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

(1.17)

The proof is very short and uses two simple but crucial ideas: first,
u(t,x)
is
bounded from above and remains non-negative by the comparison principle. Second, if
\nu\geq\kappa

then the heat kernels have the comparison

\nu^{n/2}G_{\nu}(t,x)\geq\kappa^{n/2}G_{\kappa}(t,x),~~\hbox{for all $t>0$ and $x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$.}

(1.18)

These two facts together with the Duhamel formula imply the result more or less
immediately. We recall the details in Section
4
.
Here, we used the notation

G_{\kappa}(t,x)=\frac{1}{(4\kappa\pi t)^{n/2}}e^{-|x|^{2}/(4\kappa t)},

(1.19)

for the standard heat kernel.
We will use the convention that
G_{\kappa}(t,x)=0
for
t\leq 0
.

Thus, the problem of uniform bounds for the solutions to (
1.1
) is trivial in the case
\nu\geq\kappa
which seemingly
can allow the concentration to arrive very fast into the places where the temperature is high, promoting its growth. It is, strangely, quite difficult
in the physically supposedly simpler situation when
\nu\leq\kappa
. We will solely focus on that situation in the rest of this paper.

A localized Lyapunov functional was adopted by Collet and Xin in
[
8
]

to obtain the global existence of solutions to (
1.1
) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

with bounded non-negative initial conditions
for polynomial nonlinearities of the form
g(v)=v^{m}
. They established an
L^{\infty}
-bound
of the form

\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\log\log t,~~\hbox{as $t\to+\infty$.}

(1.20)

The authors of
[
8
]
considered nonlinearities of the
form
uv^{m}
, but their argument extends to
u^{m}e^{v}
with appropriate modifications. The Lyapunov functional method was used to prove a
global bound in a more elaborate system with more variables with a polynomial nonlinearity as well in
[
20
]
.

Existence of the global in time solution for the exponential
nonlinearities
g(v)=\exp v

in the same setting was proved in
[
18
]
by Herrero, Lacey and Velasquez.
They do not state explicitly any bound on the growth of the solution,
but, to the best of our understanding, the proof in
[
18
]
produces an estimate
of the type

\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\log t,~~\hbox{as $t\to+\infty$.}

(1.21)

Chen and Qi considered in
[
7
]
the Fisher-KPP thermo-diffusive system, with
g(v)=v
and in one dimension:

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}u=\nu\partial_{x}^{2}u-uv,\\
&\partial_{t}v=\kappa\partial_{x}^{2}v+uv,\end{split}

(1.22)

with the initial condition
u_{0}(x)\equiv 1
.
They were able to show not only that the solution is uniformly bounded in time but also that the front has a finite width
and is located at the position

m(t)=2c_{*}t-\frac{3}{2\lambda_{*}}\log t+x_{0},~~\hbox{as $x\to+\infty$,}

(1.23)

with
c_{*}=2\sqrt{\kappa}
,
\lambda_{*}=1/\sqrt{\kappa}
.
This is exactly the Bramson asymptotics for the location of the front of the solution to the single Fisher-KPP equation

v_{t}=\kappa v_{xx}+v-v^{2},

(1.24)

with a compactly supported initial condition
v_{0}(x)
, originally proved in
[
5
,
6
]
. Bramson’s results were recently extensively
revisited both in the PDE and probabilistic literature – we refer
to
[
15
]
for the finest asymptotics and further references.
While the proof in
[
7
]
relied
heavily on the probabilistic results by Bramson for the single equation, the above recent results would allow to adapt their proof to purely analytic
arguments. One key observation of
[
7
]
will be used extensively in the present paper.

We should also mention that related bounds for thermo-diffusive
systems and traveling waves in cylinders in systems with advection, with extra dissipative mechanisms,
have been considered, for instance, in
[
2
,
3
,
12
,
16
,
17
]
. However, the extra dissipation played a key role in most arguments
in these references.

The main result

The main result of the paper is the following uniform bound for the solutions to the thermo-diffusive system.

Theorem 1.2

Let
u(t,x),v(t,x)
be a solution to (
1.1
) with the initial conditions
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)
and
v(0,x)=v_{0}(x)

that satisfy (
1.5
) and (
1.6
), with some
K>0
. Suppose that
g(v)

satisfies the assumption (
1.8
) and is smooth.
Then,
u(t,x)
and
v(t,x)
are globally bounded in time: there exists
C>0
that depends
on the constants in the above assumptions, so that

0\leq u(t,x)\leq C,~~0\leq v(t,x)\leq C,

(1.25)

for all
t>0
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Outline of the proof and organization of the paper

Let us now briefly explain the main ingredients in the proof of Theorem
1.2
. It is not difficult to see that,
given the uniform bound (
1.10
) on the fuel concentration,
the first essential issue is the following. Let
f(t,x)
and
h(t,x)
be the solutions to the standard heat equations

\partial_{t}f=\nu\Delta f+Z(t,x),

(1.26)

and

\partial_{t}h=\kappa\Delta h+Z(t,x),

(1.27)

with the initial conditions
g(0,x)=h(0,x)=0
. Note that (
1.26
) and (
1.27
) have two different diffusivities
\kappa\neq\nu
but the forcing
term
Z(t,x)
in the two equations is the same. Suppose that we know that
Z(t,x)\geq 0
for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and that
f(t,x)
satisfies
a uniform bound

0\leq f(t,x)\leq K,~~\hbox{for all $t\geq 0$ and $x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$.}

(1.28)

The question is what kind of bound on the function
h(t,x)
one can infer from (
1.28
). The main observation of
[
18
]

is that one can not deduce a uniform bound on
h(t,x)
but it does obey a parabolic BMO bound: see Proposition
2.1
in Section
2
.
The basic reason for this estimate is that
h
is related to
f
by a singular integral type operator that maps
L^{\infty}
to a BMO space.
The proof of Proposition
2.1
below,
which is the first crucial step in the proof of Theorem
1.2
, is different from that in
[
18
]
and seems to us to be
easier to follow. This parabolic BMO bound does not use essentially any information on the forcing
Z(t,x)
except for (
1.28
).

The next step is to use the parabolic BMO bound on the solution, together with
the John-Nirenberg inequality, the upper bound on
g(v)
in (
1.8
)
and
standard parabolic regularity estimates, to obtain an estimate that controls
the pointwise values of the fuel temperature
v(t,x)
in a parabolic cylinder
by its averages over a slightly larger parabolic cylinder. The precise statement is found in Corollary
3.6

in Section
3
. This step only requires an upper exponential bound
on the reaction rate
g(v)
.
Thus, the proof of Theorem
1.2
is reduced to bounding the space time averages

(v)_{Q}=\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}v(t,x)dxdt,

(1.29)

where
Q
is a parabolic cylinder (see Section
2
for the precise definitions).

In order to bound the averages
(v)_{Q}
, we first
obtain some properties of the solutions
to (
1.1
) that are consequences of
the hair trigger effect
for the thermo-diffusive system (
1.1
), described in
Section
4
. The term is borrowed from the seminal paper of
Aronson and Weinberger
[
1
]
. It accounts for the fact that the rest state
v\equiv 0
in the Fisher-KPP equation is unstable with respect
to any nontrivial small perturbation. An ingenious observation of
[
7
]
, paralleling that in Proposition
1.1
above,
is that the fuel temperature
v(t,x)
is a super-solution to
a differential inequality of the form

v_{t}\geq\kappa\Delta v+(d_{0}-d_{1}v)g(v),

(1.30)

with some constants
d_{0}>0
and
d_{1}>0
that depend on the initial conditions
u_{0}
and
v_{0}
. It is here that one uses the lower bound
(
1.6
) on the sum
u_{0}+v_{0}
. In
[
7
]
, that considered the special case
g(v)=v
in one spatial dimension, this inequality, together with Bramson’s
estimates on the front location for the single Fisher-KPP equation in one dimension, essentially finished the proof because it gives tight
bounds on the region where anything non-trivial can happen.
In the general case
g(v)\neq v
, the situation is very different
but (
1.30
) is still crucial.
The reason is that (
1.30
) implies that, as soon as the local average
(v)_{Q}
is not too small, the solution spreads at a speed that may be very small but positive. This is the hair trigger effect
and the lower bound in assumption (
1.8
) for
v>0
but small is critical here. This effect gives us three estimates: first, a forward in time lower bound
on
v(t,x)
described in Proposition
4.2
, second, a forward in time upper bound on
u(t,x)
in Corollary
4.3
and, third,
an absolutely crucial backward in time upper bound on
v(t,x)
in a linearly growing region, presented in Corollary
4.4
,

The last step is to obtain an upper bound on the spatial averages
(v)_{Q}
of the fuel temperature. This is done in Section
5
.
First, we deduce in Section
5.1
from some algebraic manipulations that an upper bound on the spatial averages
(v)_{Q}
of the fuel temperature
is equivalent to a uniform in time Hölder bound on the solution
J(t,x)
to the forced heat equation

J_{t}-\kappa\Delta J=\bar{u}(t,x)-u(t,x),

(1.31)

Here,
\bar{u}(t,x)
is the solution to the heat equation

\bar{u}_{t}=\nu\Delta\bar{u},~~\bar{u}(0,x)=u_{0}(x).

(1.32)

The proof of the Hölder bound on
J(t,x)
relies on the consequences of the
hair trigger effect proved in Section
4
. They
allow us to control the time intervals on which
u(s,y)
,
may behave non-trivially, for
(s,y)
in a parabolic cylinder
centered at
(t,x)
. Roughly,
u(s,y)
obeys a nice Hölder bound
at what we call “the early times”, before reaction really starts, because
v(s,y)

has to be small by the backward-in-time bound in Corollary
4.4
.
On the intermediate time scales, when reaction actually happens, we can say nothing about
u(s,y)
apart from the fact that it is bounded. However, the
intermediate times can not be too long by the forward-in-time lower bound
on
v(s,y)
in Proposition
4.2
. Therefore, the contribution of this
time interval to the Hólder constant of
J(t,x)
is bounded.
Finally, at the late times, after the reaction has passed, the fuel
concentration
u(s,y)
has to be small by the forward-in-time upper bound
in
in Corollary
4.3
. All together this leads to a Hölder bound on
J(t,x)
.
This is explained in detail in Section
5.3
.

Notation.

We use the notation
C
for constants that may change from line to line but do not depend
on the time
t>0
.

2
A parabolic BMO bound on the solution

As in
[
18
]
, the first step in the proof of Theorem
1.2
is a global
in time parabolic BMO bound on the solution. Before we state the result, let us set some notation
and recall the
definition of the parabolic BMO semi-norm. A parabolic cylinder of size
R>0
centered at a point
(t,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a
set of the form

Q=Q_{R}(t,x)=\{(s,y):~|x-y|<R,~|t-s|<R^{2}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

(2.1)

Later, we will use parabolic cylinders containing past times only. We will denote such cylinders by

Q^{-}=Q_{R}^{-}(t,x)=\{(s,y):~|x-y|<R,~t-R^{2}<s<t\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

(2.2)

For
f\in L_{loc}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we denote the average of
f
over a measurable set
Q
by

(f)_{Q}=\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}f(x,t)dxdt.

Here,
|Q|
is the
(n+1)
-dimensional Lebesgue measure of
Q
.
The parabolic BMO semi-norm is defined by

\|f\|_{\pbmo}=\sup\Big\{\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|f-(f)_{Q}|dxdt\Big\},

with the supremum is taken over all parabolic
cylinders
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Here is the main result of this section.

Proposition 2.1

Let
u(t,x)
,
v(t,x)
be the solution to the system (
1.1
)
with the initial conditions
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)
and
v(0,x)=v_{0}(x)

that satisfy (
1.5
). Assume, in addition, that
g(v)\geq 0
for
all
v\geq 0
.
There exists a constant
C_{1}
that depends on the constant
K
in (
1.5
),
and also on the dimension
n
and the diffusivities
\kappa>0
and
\nu>0
but not on the function

g(v)
such that

\|v\|_{\pbmo({\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq C_{1}.

(2.3)

Let us emphasize that the proof of Proposition
2.1
uses neither the upper sub-exponential
bound nor the lower bound in (
1.8
) on the reaction rates. Its conclusion holds
as long as the solution to (
1.1
) exists and the production rate
g(v)
is non-negative for
v\geq 0
.

2.1
The proof of Proposition
2.1

The idea behing the proof of Proposition
2.1
is very simple.
We may use the Duhamel formula
to represent the solution to (
1.1
) as

u(t,x)=e^{t\nu\Delta}u_{0}(x)-F(t,x),~~v(t,x)=e^{t\kappa\Delta}v_{0}(x)+H(t,x).

(2.4)

Here, the functions
F
and
H
are the solutions to the heat equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F=\nu\Delta F+ug(v),~~t>0,

(2.5)

\displaystyle F(0,x)\equiv 0,

and

\displaystyle\partial_{t}H=\kappa\Delta H+ug(v),~~t>0,

(2.6)

\displaystyle H(0,x)\equiv 0.

The maximum principle for the standard heat equation together with (
1.5
) implies that

0\leq\bar{u}(t,x):=e^{t\nu\Delta}u_{0}(x)\leq K,~~0\leq\bar{v}(t,x):=e^{t\kappa\Delta}v_{0}(x)\leq K,~~\hbox{ for all $t>0$ and $x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$.}

(2.7)

Hence, a parabolic BMO bound on
v(t,x)
would follow from such bound on
H(t,x)
.

Moreover the comparison principle for the heat equation implies that
0\leq u(t,x)\leq\bar{u}(t,x)
, and thus

0\leq F(t,x)\leq K,~\hbox{ for all $t\geq 0$ and $x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$.}

(2.8)

Note that (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) are heat equations with identical forcing but different diffusivities.
One may then ask: does the uniform bound on
F(t,x)
in (
2.8
)
imply some bound on the solution
H(t,x)
to (
2.6
) even though the diffusivities in the two equations
are different? The physical issue here is the following: suppose, for instance,
that the diffusivity
\kappa
in the equation (
2.6
) for
H
is larger than the diffusivity
\nu
in the equation (
2.5
)
for
F
. On one hand, the larger diffusivity should help the
solution ”cool” faster, so that if
F
is bounded, one may expect that the function
H
should be bounded as well.
On the other, the larger diffusivity in the equation for
H
may take the underlying Brownian particles in the Feynman-Kac representation of the solution to an inhomogeneous heat
equation

H(t,x)={\mathbb{E}}_{x}\int_{0}^{t}U(t-s,x+X_{s})g(V(t-s,x+X_{s}))ds,

(2.9)

to the regions where the forcing
g(v(s,x))
is large. This would
promote the growth of
H
that is faster than the growth of
F
. Here,
X_{s}
is the Brownian motion with diffusivity
\kappa
starting at the position
x
at the time
s=0
.
This non-trivial competition between cooling by faster spreading and heating by potentially visiting the hot spots is crucial in understanding the eventual behavior of the solution, and our understanding is far from complete. In this paper, we show that
in the setting of thermo-diffusive systems satisfying assumption (
1.8
)
the heating is eventually controlled by the consumption of the fuels by the reaction
but this control is far from being simple.

Thus, our goal is to analyze

H(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{\kappa(t-s)\Delta}p(s,\cdot)(x)ds=\int_{0}^{t}\int G_{\kappa}(t-s,x-y)p(s,y)dyds

(2.10)

when we already know that

0\leq F(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{\nu(t-s)\Delta}p(s,\cdot)(x)ds=\int_{0}^{t}\int G_{\nu}(t-s,x-y)p(s,y)dyds\leq K_{1}.

(2.11)

We assume here that the function
p(s,y)
is non-negative but essentially nothing else.

The key observation is that
H
can be written as a space-time singular integral of
the bounded function
F
.
This is seen as as follows. The difference
\Phi=H-F
satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Phi=\kappa\Delta H-\nu\Delta F=\kappa\Delta(H-F)+(\kappa-\nu)\Delta F=\kappa\Delta\Phi+(\kappa-\nu)\Delta F,

(2.12)

with the initial condition
\Phi(0,x)=0
. Therefore, symbolically, we can write

\Phi=(\kappa-\nu)\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F,

(2.13)

and

H=F+(\kappa-\nu)\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F.

(2.14)

Here, we have set

\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F:=\Delta(\partial_{t}-\kappa\Delta)^{-1}F.

(2.15)

The main ingredient in the proof of Proposition
2.1
is the following
\pbmo
bound.

Lemma 2.2

There exists a constant
C_{\mathcal{T}}
, which depends on the diffiusivity
\kappa>0
and the dimension
n
such that for all
F\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,
we have

\|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F\|_{\pbmo}\leq C_{\mathcal{T}}\|F\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

(2.16)

This lemma corresponds to Lemma 3.3 of
[
18
]
.
The proof presented here is different and is based on the estimates on the singular
kernel of the operator
\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}
. Note that Lemma
2.2

gives immediately the parabolic BMO bound (
2.3
) in Proposition
2.1
, as we have,
from (
2.4
) and (
2.14
):

\displaystyle 0\leq v(t,x)

\displaystyle=\bar{v}(t,x)+H(t,x)=\bar{v}(t,x)+F(t,x)+(\kappa-\nu)\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F(t,x).

(2.17)

The function
F(t,x)
is uniformly bounded by (
2.8
),
hence also bounded in the parabolic BMO space,
and the term
\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F
is bounded in the parabolic BMO space by Lemma
2.2
.
This gives (
2.3
). Thus, to finish the proof of
Proposition
2.1
, we only need to prove Lemma
2.2
.

2.2
The proof of Lemma
2.2

Recall that for
F\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})
, the function
\Psi={\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}}F
is the solution to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Psi=\kappa\Delta\Psi+\Delta F,

(2.18)

\displaystyle\Psi(x,0)=0.

Extending
F
by 0 to negative times, the action of
\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}
can be written as a singular integral operator

\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}}K(t-s,x-y)F(s,y)dyds,

(2.19)

with the kernel

K(t,x)=\Delta G_{\kappa}(t,x)=\frac{1}{(4\pi\kappa t)^{{n}/{2}}}e^{-{|x|^{2}}/{(4\kappa t)}}\Big(\frac{1}{4\kappa^{{2}}}\frac{|x|^{2}}{{t^{2}}}-\frac{n}{2\kappa t}\Big),~~t>0,

(2.20)

and the convention that
K(t,x)=0
for
t\leq 0
.

We need to show that there exists a constant
C_{\mathcal{T}}
such that for any parabolic cylinder

Q=Q_{R}(x_{0},t_{0})=\{(t,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times:~|x-x_{0}|<R,|t-t_{0}|<R^{2}\},

(2.21)

and any function
F\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, we have

\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F-(\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F)_{Q}|dxdt\leq C_{\mathcal{T}}\|F\|_{L^{\infty}}.

(2.22)

Note that for any constant
a
, we can write

a-(\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F)_{Q}=\frac{1}{Q}\int_{Q}(a-\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F)dxdt,

so that

\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F-(\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F)_{Q}|dxdt\leq\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F-a|dxdt+|a-(\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F)_{Q}|\leq\frac{2}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F-a|dxdt.

(2.23)

Therefore, it suffices to show that there exists a constant
K_{\mathcal{T}}
, such that for any parabolic cylinder
Q
and
function
F\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n})

we can find a constant
a_{Q}\in{\mathbb{R}}
so that

\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F-a_{Q}|dxdt\leq K_{\mathcal{T}}\|F\|_{L^{\infty}}.

(2.24)

The constant
K_{\mathcal{T}}
should not depend on
Q
or
F
but the constant
a_{Q}
may.

Given a cylinder
Q=Q_{R}(t_{0},x_{0})
, we use the notation
2Q=Q_{2R}(t_{0},x_{0})
and decompose

F=(F-(F)_{Q})\mathbbm{1}_{2Q}+(F-(F)_{Q})\mathbbm{1}_{(2Q)^{c}}+(F)_{Q}=:F^{(1)}+F^{(2)}+(F)_{Q}.

(2.25)

Note that, as
(F)_{Q}
is a constant,
\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}(F)_{Q}=0
, so that

\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F=\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F^{(1)}+\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F^{(2)}.

(2.26)

We will treat the two terms in (
2.26
) separately.

A bound on
\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F^{(1)}

For the first term in (
2.26
), we write

\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F^{(1)}|dxdt\leq\frac{1}{|Q|}\Big(\int_{Q}|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F^{(1)}|^{2}dxdt\Big)^{{1}/{2}}|Q|^{{1}/{2}}\leq\Big(\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F^{(1)}|^{2}dxdt\Big)^{{1}/{2}}.

(2.27)

To bound the right side of (
2.27
), let us show that the operator
\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}
is bounded on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. This is a consequence
of Mikhlin’s theorem
[
32
]
but let us give a short direct proof. Recall that

\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F=\Delta\tilde{\Phi},

(2.28)

and the function
\tilde{\Phi}
satisfies

\partial_{t}\tilde{\Phi}=\kappa\Delta\tilde{\Phi}+F,~~t>0,

(2.29)

with

\tilde{\Phi}(x,t)=0
for
t\leq 0
.
Multiplying (
2.29
) by
\Delta\tilde{\Phi}
, integrating, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\int|\nabla\tilde{\Phi}|^{2}(0,x)dx-\frac{1}{2}\int|\nabla\tilde{\Phi}|^{2}(T,x)dx=\kappa\|\Delta\tilde{\Phi}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T])}^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]}F\Delta\tilde{\Phi}dxdt,

As
\nabla\tilde{\Phi}(0,x)=0
, this gives

\displaystyle\kappa\|\Delta\tilde{\Phi}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T])}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int|\nabla\tilde{\Phi}|^{2}(T,x)dx\leq\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T])}\|\Delta\tilde{\Phi}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T])},

(2.30)

whence

\displaystyle\|\Delta\tilde{\Phi}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T])}\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T])}.

(2.31)

Taking into account (
2.28
) and passing to the limit
T\to+\infty
, we get an
L^{2}-L^{2}
bound on the operator
\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}
:

\|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R})}\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R})}.

(2.32)

Going back to (
2.27
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F^{(1)}|dxdt

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F^{(1)}|^{2}dxdt\Big)^{{1}/{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\frac{1}{|Q|^{{1}/{2}}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}}|F^{(1)}|^{2}dxdt\Big)^{{1}/{2}}

(2.33)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa}\Big(\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{2Q}|F-(F)_{Q}|^{2}dxdt\Big)^{{1}/{2}}\leq\frac{2}{\kappa}\|F\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})}.

Thus, taking
a=0
in (
2.23
), we see that

\|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F^{(1)}\|_{\pbmo}\leq\frac{2}{\kappa}\|F\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})}.

(2.34)

A bound on
\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F^{(2)}

Next, we show that if we take

b=\int K(x_{0}-y,t_{0}-s)F^{(2)}(y,s)dyds,

(2.35)

then

\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F^{(2)}-b|dxdt\leq C\|F\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})},

(2.36)

with a constant
C
that does not depend on
Q
or
F
. Recalling (
2.23
) and the remark below (
2.32
), we see that
this will give the desired bound

\|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F^{(2)}\|_{\pbmo}\leq C\|F\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})}.

(2.37)

We first show that

|b|\leq C_{1}(\kappa,n)\frac{t_{0}}{R^{2}}\|F\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})}.

(2.38)

To this end, we use (
2.20
) to write

\displaystyle|b|

\displaystyle\leq\int|K(t_{0}-s,x_{0}-y)||F_{2}(s,y)|dyds\leq I\cdot\|F_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})},

(2.39)

with

\displaystyle I=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\int_{|x_{0}-y|\geq 2R}\frac{e^{-{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}/{(4\kappa(t_{0}-s))}}}{(4\pi\kappa(t_{0}-s))^{{n}/{2}}}\Big(\frac{1}{4\kappa}\frac{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}{{t_{0}-s}}+\frac{n}{2\kappa}\Big)\frac{1}{\kappa(t_{0}-s)}dyds

(2.40)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\int_{|y|\geq 2R}\frac{e^{-{|y|^{2}}/{(4\kappa s)}}}{(4\pi\kappa s)^{{n}/{2}}}\Big(\frac{1}{4\kappa}\frac{|y|^{2}}{s}+\frac{n}{2\kappa}\Big)\frac{1}{\kappa s}dyds\leq C(n,\kappa)\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\int_{|y|\geq 2R}\frac{e^{-{|y|^{2}}/{(8\kappa s)}}}{s^{{n}/{2}+1}}dyds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(n,\kappa)}{R^{2}}\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\int_{|y|\geq 2R}\frac{|y|^{2}e^{-{|y|^{2}}/{(8\kappa s)}}}{s^{{n}/{2}+1}}dyds\leq\frac{C(n,\kappa)}{R^{2}}\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\int_{|y|\geq 2R}\frac{e^{-{|y|^{2}}/{(16\kappa s)}}}{s^{{n}/{2}}}dyds\leq\frac{C(n,\kappa)t_{0}}{R^{2}}.

Together with (
2.39
), this gives (
2.38
), since

\|F^{(2)}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})}\leq 2\|F\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})}.

Next we show that (
2.36
) holds. Since
F_{2}(t,x)
is supported away from
(t_{0},x_{0})
, we can integrate without taking the principal value,
and use the zero integral property of
K(t,x)
to write

\displaystyle\int_{Q}|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F^{(2)}-b|dxdt=\int_{Q}\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}}(K(t-s,x-y)-K(t_{0}-s,x_{0}-y))F^{(2)}(s,y)dyds\Big|dxdt

(2.41)

\displaystyle=\int_{Q}\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}}(K(t-s,x-y)-K(t_{0}-s,x_{0}-y))(F(s,y)-(F)_{Q})\chi_{(2Q)^{c}}(s,y)dyds\Big|dxdt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{Q}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\setminus 2Q}|F(s,y)-(F)_{Q}||K(t-s,x-y)-K(t_{0}-s,x_{0}-y)|dyds\Big)dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\setminus 2Q}|F(s,y)-(F)_{Q}|\Big(\int_{Q}|K(t-s,x-y)-K(t_{0}-s,x_{0}-y)|dxdt\Big)dyds

\displaystyle\leq 2\|F\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R})}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int_{2^{j+1}Q\setminus 2^{j}Q}\Big(\int_{Q}|K(t-s,x-y)-K(t_{0}-s,x_{0}-y)|dxdt\Big)dyds.

The kernel
K(t,x)
satisfies the following key bounds:

\displaystyle|K(t,x)|\leq\frac{B_{2}}{(\sqrt{t}+|x|)^{n+2}},~~|\nabla_{x}K(t,x)|\leq\frac{B_{2}}{(\sqrt{t}+|x|)^{n+3}},~~|\partial_{t}K(x,t)|\leq\frac{B_{2}}{\left(\sqrt{t}+|x|\right)^{n+4}},

(2.42)

with a constant
B_{2}
that depends on
n
and
\kappa
.
Using (
2.42
), we obtain

\displaystyle|K(x_{1},t_{1})-K(x_{2},t_{2})|\leq|K(x_{1},t_{1})-K(x_{2},t_{1})|+|K(x_{2},t_{1})-K(x_{2},t_{2})|

(2.43)

\displaystyle\leq|x_{1}-x_{2}||(\nabla_{x}K)(\lambda x_{1}+(1-\lambda)x_{2},t_{1})|

\displaystyle+(|K(x_{2},t_{1})|+|K(x_{2},t_{2})|)^{{1}/{2}}|t_{2}-t_{1}|^{{1}/{2}}|\partial_{t}K(x_{2},\lambda^{\prime}t_{1}+(1-\lambda^{\prime})t_{2})|^{{1}/{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{B_{2}|x_{1}-x_{2}|}{(\sqrt{t_{1}}+|\lambda x_{1}+(1-\lambda)x_{2}|)^{n+3}}

\displaystyle+B_{2}\Big(\frac{|t_{2}-t_{1}|^{1/2}}{(\sqrt{t_{1}}+|x_{2}|)^{(n+2)/2}}+\frac{|t_{2}-t_{1}|^{1/2}}{\sqrt{t_{2}}+|x_{2}|)^{(n+2)/2}}\Big)\frac{1}{((\lambda^{\prime}t_{1}+(1-\lambda^{\prime})t_{2})^{{1}/{2}}+|x_{2}|)^{(n+4)/2}},

with some
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in[0,1]
.

We will apply (
2.43
) with

t_{1}=t-s
,
t_{2}=t_{0}-s
,
x_{1}=x-y
,
x_{2}=x_{0}-y
,

(2.44)

as appears in the last integral in the right side of (
2.41
).
Since the point
(t_{0},x_{0})
is the center of the parabolic cylinder
Q
, and the point
(t,x)
lies in
Q
, while the point
(s,y)
lies outside the parabolic cylinder
2Q
,
there exists a constant
B_{3}=B_{3}(n)>1
such that

\frac{1}{B_{3}}\leq\frac{|\lambda x+(1-\lambda)x_{0}-y|}{|x_{0}-y|},\Big(\frac{|t-s|}{|t_{0}-s|}\Big)^{{1}/{2}},\Big(\frac{|\lambda^{\prime}t+(1-\lambda^{\prime})t_{0}-s|}{|t_{0}-s|}\Big)^{{1}/{2}}\leq B_{3}.

(2.45)

Inserting the points given by (
2.44
) into (
2.43
) and keeping in mind (
2.45
), we get

\displaystyle|K(x-y,t-s)-K(x_{0}-y,t_{0}-s)|\leq B_{4}(n,\kappa)\frac{|x-x_{0}|+|t-t_{0}|^{{1}/{2}}}{\left(|t_{0}-s|^{{1}/{2}}+|x_{0}-y|\right)^{n+3}}.

(2.46)

Therefore, for each
(s,y)\in 2^{j+1}Q\setminus 2^{j}Q
, we have, since
(t,x)\in Q_{R}(t_{0},x_{0})
:

\displaystyle\int_{Q}|K(x-y,t-s)-K(x_{0}-y,t_{0}-s)|dxdt\leq\frac{2B_{4}R|Q|}{\left(|t_{0}-s|^{{1}/{2}}+|x_{0}-y|\right)^{n+3}}\leq\frac{B_{5}}{2^{(n+3)j}},

(2.47)

with the constant
B_{5}
that depends on
n
and
\kappa
.
Going back to (
2.41
) and uisng (
2.47
) gives

\displaystyle\int_{Q}|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F^{(2)}-b|dxdt\leq 2B_{5}\|F\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R})}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{|2^{j+1}Q\setminus 2^{j}Q|}{2^{(n+3)j}}\leq 2B_{5}\|F\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R})}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{(j+1)(n+2)}}{2^{(n+3)j}}|Q|

(2.48)

\displaystyle=2^{n+3}B_{5}\|F\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R})}|Q|=B_{6}(n,\kappa)\|F\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R})}|Q|.

This gives (
2.36
).

With the bounds (
2.33
) and (
2.36
) in hand, we get (
2.24
) with
a_{Q}=b
simply by writing

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F-b|dxdt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F^{(1)}+\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F^{(2)}-b|dxdt\!

(2.49)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F^{(1)}|dxdt+\!\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|\mathcal{T}_{\kappa}F^{(2)}-b|dxdt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\kappa}\|F\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R})}+B_{6}(n,\kappa)\|F\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R})},

finishing the proof of Lemma
2.2
.
\Box

3
From bounded averages to bounded pointwise values

In this section, we show that the point-wise values of the function

v(t,x)
are bounded by its local averages.
The first step is to show that if the average of the function
v(t,x)
is small
in a unit parabolic cube then the higher
L^{p}
-norms of
v(t,x)
in that ball are also small.
Let us recall the representation (
2.17
) for the function
v(t,x)
:

v(t,x)=\bar{v}(t,x)+F(t,x)+(\kappa-\nu)\Psi(t,x)

(3.1)

Here,
\bar{v}(t,x)
is the solution to the heat equation, as defined in (
2.7
), the function

F(t,x)
is the solution to (
2.5
) and is uniformly bounded by (
2.8
),
while
\Psi(t,x)={\mathcal{T}}_{\kappa}F(t,x)
satisfies (
2.18
):

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}-\kappa\Delta\Psi=\Delta F,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\Psi(0,x)=0.

We will need the
following exponential moment bound.

Proposition 3.1

For any
Z\geq 0
and
\rho\in(0,1)

there exists a constant
C_{Z,\rho}>0

that depends on all of our constants so that
for all
t_{0}>1
and
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we have

\frac{1}{|Q_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})|}\int_{Q_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})}e^{Zv^{\rho}(t,x)}dxdt\leq C_{Z,\rho}\exp\Big(C_{Z,\rho}\big|(\Psi)_{Q_{1}}\big|^{\rho}\Big).

(3.3)

Proof.
It follows from (
2.4
)
that there exists
C>0
such that

v(t,x)\leq C(K_{2}+H(t,x)),~~\hbox{for all $t>0$ and $x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$.}

(3.4)

Here,
H(t,x)
is the (non-negative) solution to (
2.6
).
Thus, (
3.3
) would follow if we can show that for every
Z>0

and
\rho\in(0,1)
there exists a constant
C_{Z}>0
so that
for all unit parabolic cylinders
Q_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})
we have

\frac{1}{|Q_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})|}\int_{Q_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})}\exp\Big\{ZH^{\rho}(t,x)\Big\}dxdt\leq C_{Z,\rho}\exp\Big(C_{Z,\rho}\big|(\Psi)_{Q_{1}}\big|^{\rho}\Big).

(3.5)

The proof of (
3.5
) uses the parabolic John-Nirenberg inequality
that bounds the deviation of a
\pbmo
function from its local
average.

Proposition 3.2

(John-Nirenberg inequality)
There exist constants
A,B>0
, such that for all parabolic cylinders
Q=Q_{R}(t_{0},x_{0})
and all
\lambda>0
, we have

\big|\{(t,x)\in Q:~|g(t,x)-(g)_{Q}|\geq\lambda\}|\leq B\exp\Big(-\frac{A\lambda}{\|g\|_{\pbmo}}\Big)|Q|,

(3.6)

for all
g\in\pbmo
. Here,
|Q|
is the
(n+1)
-dimensional Lebesgue measure of
Q
.

Let us use (
2.14
) to bound
H(t,x)
as

H(t,x)\leq C+(\kappa-\nu)\Psi(t,x).

(3.7)

This leads to the following estimate

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\exp\Big\{ZH^{\rho}(t,x)\Big\}dxdt\leq C\int_{Q}\exp\Big\{CZ|\Psi(t,x)-(\Psi)_{Q}|^{\rho}+CZ|(\Psi)_{Q}|^{\rho}\Big\}dxdt

(3.8)

\displaystyle\leq C\exp\Big(CZ|(\Psi)_{Q}|^{\rho}\Big)\int_{Q}\exp\Big\{CZ|\Psi(t,x)-(\Psi)_{Q}|^{\rho}\Big\}dxdt.

Here, we have set
Q=Q_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})
, and denoted by
C
constants that do not depend
the parabolic cylinder
Q
.

We estimate the integral in the right side above as

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\exp\Big\{CZ|\Psi(t,x)\ -(\Psi)_{Q}|^{\rho}\Big\}dxdt

(3.9)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\exp\left(CZ(q+1)^{\rho}\right)\Big|\{(t,x)\in Q:~|\Psi(t,x)-(\Psi)_{Q}|\in[q,q+1]\}\Big|.

We can use the John-Nirenberg inequality to bound the right side of (
3.9
) by

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\exp\Big\{CZ|\Psi(t,x)\ -(\Psi)_{Q}|^{\rho}\Big\}dxdt\leq C\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\exp\big(CZq^{\rho}\big)B\exp\Big(-\frac{Aq}{\|\Psi\|_{\pbmo}}\Big)|Q|.

(3.10)

We now use the parabolic BMO bound (
2.16
) on
\Psi(t,x)

to obtain from (
3.10
) that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}\exp\Big\{CZ|\Psi(t,x)-(\Psi)_{Q}|^{\rho}\Big\}dxdt\leq C\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\exp\big(CZq^{\rho}\big)\exp\Big(-\frac{Aq}{C}\Big)

(3.11)

\displaystyle=C\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\exp\Big(CZq^{\rho}-\frac{A}{C}q\Big)\leq C_{Z,\rho}.

Combining this with (
3.8
) gives

\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}e^{ZH^{\rho}(t,x)}dxdt\leq C\exp\Big(CZ\big|(\Psi)_{Q}\big|^{\rho}\Big),

finishing the proof of (
3.5
) and thus that of Proposition
3.1
.
\Box

Corollary 3.3

For any
Z\geq 0
and
\rho\in(0,1)

that depends on all of our constants so that
for all

and

, we have

\frac{1}{|Q_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})|}\int_{Q_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})}e^{Zv^{\rho}(t,x)}dxdt\leq C_{Z,\rho}\exp\Big(C_{Z,\rho}(v)_{Q_{1}}\Big).

(3.12)

Proof.
This follows from (
3.3
) because of (
2.4
)
and (
2.14
) and since the function

is uniformly bounded.
\Box

Corollary 3.4

For any
\varepsilon>0
and

there exists a constant
C_{p,\varepsilon}>0

that depends on all of our constants so that
for all

and

\frac{1}{|Q_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})|}\int_{Q_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})}[g(v(t,x))]^{p}dxdt\leq C_{p,\varepsilon}[(v)_{Q}]^{1-\varepsilon}\exp\big(C_{p,\varepsilon}(v)_{Q}\big).

(3.13)

Proof.
Let us write, using Hölder’s inequality, with some

and

such

:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|Q_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})|}\int_{Q_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})}[g(v(t,x))]^{p}dxdt

(3.14)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|Q_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})|}\Big(\int_{Q_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})}v(t,x)dx\Big)^{1/p^{\prime}}\Big(\int_{Q_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})}\Big(\frac{g^{p}(v(t,x))}{v^{1-1/q^{\prime}}(t,x)}\Big)^{q^{\prime}}dx\Big)^{1/q^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|Q_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})|^{1/q}}[(v)_{Q}]^{1/p^{\prime}}\Big(\int_{Q_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})}e^{Mv^{\rho}(t,x)}dx\Big)^{1/q^{\prime}}.

We used the upper bound for

in (
1.9
) in the last step above.
Now, (
3.13
) follows after taking

large enough and taking (
3.12
) into account.
\Box

4
Consequences of the hair trigger effect

In this section, we quantify the hair trigger effect for the thermo-diffusive system (
1.1
).
This is the claim of Proposition
4.2
that follows from the simple observation in Proposition
4.1
.
It has two important consequences: the forward in time bound on the fuel concentration
u(t,x)
in Corollary
4.3

and a backward in time upper bound on the fuel temperature in Corollary
4.4
. Both of them will play a role in Section
5

in the proof of
the bound on the space-time averages of
v(t,x)
in Proposition
5.1
.

Let us first recall that a uniform in time bound for the solutions to (
1.1
)
with
\nu\geq\kappa
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
can be proved by comparing the heat kernels
with the diffusivities
\nu
and
\kappa
, with no restrictions on the function
g(v)
whatsoever using a beautiful
idea by Martin and Pierre
[
24
]
. Namely, we have an elementary inequality

\nu^{n/2}G_{\nu}(t,x)\geq\kappa^{n/2}G_{\kappa}(t,x),~~\hbox{ for all $t>0$ and $x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$ if $\nu\geq\kappa>0$.}

(4.1)

Let
\bar{u}(t,x)
and
\bar{v}(t,x)
be as defined in (
2.7
).
It follows from (
4.1
) that if
\nu\geq\kappa
we can write

u(t,x)=\bar{u}(t,x)-\int_{0}^{t}\int G_{\nu}(t-s,x-y)u(s,y)g(v(s,y))dyds,

(4.2)

and

\displaystyle v(t,x)

\displaystyle=\bar{v}(t,x)+\int_{0}^{t}\int G_{\kappa}(t-s,x-y)u(s,y)g(v(s,y))dyds

(4.3)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{v}(t,x)+\frac{\nu^{n/2}}{\kappa^{n/2}}\int_{0}^{t}\int G_{\nu}(t-s,x-y)u(s,y)g(v(s,y))dyds

\displaystyle\leq\bar{v}(t,x)+\frac{\nu^{n/2}}{\kappa^{n/2}}(\bar{u}(t,x)-u(t,x))\leq CK.

Thus, in the case
\nu\geq\kappa
the upper bound on
v(t,x)
is very simple.

If
\nu\leq\kappa
, the above argument gives a lower bound that played a crucial role in the analysis
in
[
7
]
in the special case
g(v)=v
and dimension
n=1

v(t,x)\geq\bar{v}(t,x)+\frac{\nu^{n/2}}{\kappa^{n/2}}(\bar{u}(t,x)-u(t,x)),

(4.4)

or, equivalently,

u(t,x)\geq\frac{\kappa^{n/2}}{\nu^{n/2}}\bar{v}(t,x)-\frac{\kappa^{n/2}}{\nu^{n/2}}v(t,x)+\bar{u}(t,x).

(4.5)

Note that if the lower bound in (
1.6
) holds and
\nu\leq\kappa
then we have

\displaystyle\bar{u}(t,x)+{\frac{\kappa^{n/2}}{\nu^{n/2}}}\bar{v}(t,x)

\displaystyle={\frac{\kappa^{n/2}}{\nu^{n/2}}}G_{\kappa}(t,\cdot)\star v_{0}+G_{\nu}(t,\cdot)\star u_{0}

(4.6)

\displaystyle\geq G_{\nu}(t,\cdot)\star v_{0}+G_{\nu}(t,\cdot)\star u_{0}=G_{\nu}(t,\cdot)\star(u_{0}+v_{0})\geq K^{-1}:=d_{0}.

We deduce that

u(t,x)\geq d_{0}-d_{1}v,

(4.7)

with

d_{1}=\frac{\kappa^{n/2}}{\nu^{n/2}}.

(4.8)

Let us summarize this as follows.

Proposition 4.1

Let
v(t,x)
and
u(t,x)
be the solutions to (
1.1
) with the initial conditions
u_{0}(x)
and
v_{0}(x)
that satisfy
(
1.5
) and (
1.6
).
If
\nu\leq\kappa
then
v(t,x)
satisfies a differential inequality

v_{t}\geq\kappa\Delta v+(d_{0}-d_{1}v)g(v),

(4.9)

with
d_{0}
and
d_{1}
given, respectively, by (
4.6
) and (
4.8
).

We should stress that the conclusion of this proposition relies crucially on the lower bound on the sum
u_{0}(x)+v_{0}(x)
in (
1.6
)
that makes the constant
d_{0}
in (
4.6
) be positive.

The main consequence of Proposition
4.1
is that it controls the function
v(t,x)

from below on a set that grows linearly in time. Here, we rely on the lower bound in (
1.8
) for the function
g(v)
for
v>0
small.
We should mention that the upper bound in (
1.8
) is not required here.

Proposition 4.2

Let
v(t,x)
and
u(t,x)
be the solutions to (
1.1
) with the initial conditions
u_{0}(x)
and
v_{0}(x)
that satisfy
(
1.5
) and (
1.6
). Assume
also that
g(v)
satisfies the lower bound

g(v)\geq c_{1}v
for all
v\geq 0
,with some
c_{1}>0
.

(4.10)

There exist
\gamma_{0}>0
,
\lambda_{0}>0
,
c_{0}>0
and
C>0

that depend on the constant
K
in (
1.5
) and (
1.6
), the diffusivities

\kappa
and
\nu
, and the constant
c_{1}
so that the following holds.
Assume that at a time
t_{0}>0
we have, for some
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

\int_{|x-x_{0}|\leq 1}v(t_{0},x)dx\geq\delta_{0},

(4.11)

with some
\delta_{0}\in(0,1)
.
Then, we have

v(t,x)\geq\min\Big(\gamma_{0},C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{\lambda_{0}(t-t_{0})}\Big),~~\hbox{ for all $t\geq t_{0}+1$ and $|x-x_{0}|\leq 1$,}

(4.12)

and

v(t,x)\geq\gamma_{0},~~\hbox{for all $\displaystyle t\geq t_{1}:=t_{0}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}|\log\delta_{0}|+C$ and $|x-x_{0}|\leq c_{0}(t-t_{1})$.}

(4.13)

Proof.

Step 1. A sub-solution for
v(t,x)
.

First, we construct a sub-solution for
v(t,x)
.
Observe that, due to the assumption (
4.10
), if we choose
\alpha_{0}=d_{0}/(2d_{1})
, then there exists
\beta_{0}>0
so that

(d_{0}-d_{1}s)g(s)\geq\beta_{0}s,~~\hbox{ for all $0\leq s\leq\alpha_{0}$.}

(4.14)

Let us now choose
L
sufficiently large, so that the principal eigenvalue of the Dirichlet problem

\displaystyle-\kappa\Delta\varphi_{L}=\lambda_{L}\varphi_{L},~~\hbox{ for $x\in B(0,L)$},

(4.15)

\displaystyle\varphi_{L}=0,~~\hbox{ on $\partial B(0,L)$},

satisfies

0<\lambda_{L}<\beta_{0}/2.

(4.16)

We normalize the non-negative
principal eigenfunction
\varphi_{L}(x)
so that

\varphi_{L}(0)=\sup_{x\in B(0,L)}\varphi_{L}(x)=1.

The point is that the function
\varphi_{L}
satisfies the differential inequality

\kappa\Delta\varphi_{L}+\beta_{0}\varphi_{L}\geq 0,~~\hbox{for $x\in B(0,L)$.}

(4.17)

We see from (
4.14
) and (
4.17
) that for
0<\delta\leq\alpha_{0}
the function

\varphi_{\delta,L}(x)=\delta\varphi_{L}(x-x_{0})
obeys the differential inequality

\kappa\Delta\varphi_{\delta,L}+(d_{0}-d_{1}\varphi_{\delta,L})g(\varphi_{\delta,L})\geq 0,~~\hbox{in $B(x_{0},L)$.}

(4.18)

Thus, by a standard argument, the function

\tilde{\omega}_{\delta}(x)=\delta\varphi_{L}(x-x_{0})\mathbbm{1}(|x-x_{0}|\leq L)

is a time-independent sub-solution to (
4.19
) on the whole space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Consider the function
\omega_{\delta}(t,x)
, the solution to

\partial_{t}\omega_{\delta}=\kappa\Delta\omega_{\delta}+(d_{0}-d_{1}\omega_{\delta})g(\omega_{\delta}),~~t\geq t_{0}+1/2,

(4.19)

with the initial condition

\omega_{\delta}(t_{0}+1/2,x)=\tilde{\omega}_{\delta}(x).

(4.20)

As the initial condition
\tilde{\omega}_{\delta}(x)
is a time-independent sub-solution to (
4.19
),
the comparison principle shows that
\omega_{\delta}(t_{0}+1/2+h,x)-\tilde{\omega}_{\delta}(x)>0
for any
h>0
.
In addition, the function

z(t,x)=\omega_{\delta}(t+h,x)-\omega_{\delta}(t,x)

satisfies a parabolic equation of the form

z_{t}=\kappa\Delta z(t,x)+c(t,x)z,

(4.21)

with a bounded function
c(t,x)
given by

c(t,x)=\frac{(d_{0}-d_{1}\omega_{\delta}(t+h,x))g(\omega_{\delta}(t+h,x))-(d_{0}-d_{1}\omega_{\delta}(t,x))g(\omega_{\delta}(t,x))}{\omega_{\delta}(t+h,x){-}\omega_{\delta}(t,x)}{\mathbbm{1}(\omega_{\delta}(t+h,x)\neq\omega_{\delta}(t,x))},

(4.22)

and a positive initial condition

z(t_{0}+1/2,x)=\omega_{\delta}(t_{0}+1/2+h,x)-\tilde{\omega}_{\delta}(x)\geq 0.

(4.23)

The comparison principle implies that
z(t,x)>0
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
t>t_{0}+{1}/{2}
, for any
h>0
.
Thus, the function
\omega_{\delta}(t,x)
is increasing in time.

In addition, the function

\mu(t,x)=\delta e^{(\beta_{0}-\lambda_{L})(t-t_{0}-1/2)}\varphi_{L}(x-x_{0}),

(4.24)

satisfies

\mu_{t}-\kappa\Delta\mu=\beta_{0}\mu\leq(d_{0}-d_{1}\mu)g(\mu),~~\hbox{ in $B(x_{0},L)$, as long as $\|\mu(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\alpha_{0}$,}

(4.25)

with the boundary condition
\mu(t,x)=0
for
x\in\partial B(x_{0},L)
. We deduce from the comparison principle that

\omega_{\delta}(t,x)\geq\delta e^{(\beta_{0}-\lambda_{L})(t-t_{0}-1/2)}\varphi_{L}(x-x_{0}),~~\hbox{ as long as $\delta e^{(\beta_{0}-\lambda_{L})(t-t_{0})}\leq C$,}

(4.26)

with a constant
C>0
that depends on
\alpha_{0}
,
\beta_{0}
and
\lambda_{L}
. Since the function
\omega_{\delta}(t,x)
is increasing in
t
and the eigenfunction

\varphi_{L}(x)
is uniformly bounded from below on
B(x_{0},1)
, as long as
L>2
, we deduce that there exist
\gamma>0
and
C>0
so that

\omega_{\delta}(t,x)\geq\min\Big(\gamma,C\delta e^{(\beta_{0}-\lambda_{L})(t-t_{0}-1/2)}\Big),~~\hbox{for $t>t_{0}+1/2$ and $|x-x_{0}|\leq 1$.}

(4.27)

Step 2. Using the sub-solution.
We now use the sub-solution
\omega_{\delta}(t,x)
constructed above to bound the function
v(t,x)
from
below. Suppose that
v(t_{0},x)
satisfies assumption (
4.11
) with some
\delta_{0}\in(0,1)
.
Let us fix
L>0
so that (
4.16
) holds.
As
v(t,x)
is a super-solution of the heat equation:

v_{t}\geq\kappa\Delta v,

(4.28)

it follows that there exists a constant
k_{L}>0
so that the assumption (
4.11
) implies that

v(t_{0}+1/2,x)\geq k_{L}\delta_{0},~~\hbox{ for all $|x-x_{0}|\leq L$.}

(4.29)

We choose
\delta=\min(k_{L}\delta_{0},\alpha_{0})
. Then, at the time
t=t_{0}+1/2
we have

v(t_{0}+1/2,x)\geq\omega_{\delta}(t_{0}+1/2,x),~~\hbox{ for all $x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$.}

(4.30)

It follows from (
4.9
) and (
4.19
) that

v(t,x)\geq\omega_{\delta}(t,x),~~\hbox{ for all $t>t_{0}+1/2$ and $x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$.}

(4.31)

Going back to (
4.27
), we deduce that

v(t,x)\geq\omega_{\delta}(t,x)\geq\min\Big(\gamma,C\delta e^{(\beta_{0}-\lambda_{L})(t-t_{0}-1/2)}\Big),~~\hbox{for $t>t_{0}+1/2$ and $|x-x_{0}|\leq 1$.}

(4.32)

As
0<\delta_{0}<1
, we have

\delta=\min(k_{L}\delta_{0},\alpha_{0})\geq\delta_{0}\min(k_{L},\alpha_{0})=C\delta_{0}.

(4.33)

Using this in (
4.32
) gives

v(t,x)\geq\min\Big(\gamma,C\delta_{0}e^{(\beta_{0}-\lambda_{L})(t-t_{0}-1/2)}\Big),~~\hbox{for $t>t_{0}+1/2$ and $|x-x_{0}|\leq 1$,}

(4.34)

which is (
4.12
).

A consequence of (

) is that there is
C>0
so that at the time

t_{1}=t_{0}+\frac{1}{\beta_{0}-\lambda_{L}}|\log\delta_{0}|+C

(4.35)

v(t_{1},x)\geq\gamma,~~\hbox{for all $|x-x_{0}|\leq 1$.}

(4.36)

Now, the bound (
4.13
) follows from (
4.36
) and the classical
results on spreading for solutions to the Fisher-KPP type equations for solutions that are initially strictly positive
on a unit ball
[
1
]
. More precisely, let
\tilde{v}(t,x)
be the solution to the initial value problem

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{t}=\kappa\Delta\tilde{v}+(d_{0}-d_{1}\tilde{v})g(\tilde{v}),~~t\geq t_{1},

(4.37)

\displaystyle\tilde{v}(t_{1},x)=\gamma\mathbbm{1}(|x-x_{0}|\leq 1),

(4.39)

It follows from the comparison principle, (
4.9
), (
4.36
), (

) and (

v(t,y)\geq\tilde{v}(t,y)\geq\frac{d_{0}}{4d_{1}},~~\hbox{ for all $t>t_{1}+T_{0}$ and $|y-x_{0}|\leq c(t-t_{1})$,}

which is (

).
In particular, we can
let the speed

in (

\Box

Proposition
4.2
has several important corollaries that will be crucial for us. First, we have a forward-in-time upper bound on the fuel.

Corollary 4.3

Assume that the assumptions of Proposition
4.2
hold.
There exist
c_{2}>0
and
\gamma_{2}>0
so that the following holds.
Suppose that at some time
t_{0}>0
the inequality (
4.11
) holds with some
\delta_{0}\in(0,1)
.
Then, we have

u(t,x)\leq Ke^{-\gamma_{2}(t-t_{1})},~~\hbox{for all $t\geq t_{1}=t_{0}+|\log\delta_{0}|+C$ and $|x-x_{0}|\leq c_{2}(t-t_{1})$.}

(4.41)

Proof.
Informally, the reason for this estimate is that
Proposition
4.2
implies that

v(t,x)\geq\gamma_{0}
in the region
|x-x_{0}|\leq c_{0}(t-t_{1})
that is
much bigger than the domain
|x-x_{0}|\leq c_{2}(t-t_{1})
which appears in (
4.41
),
by a factor growing linearly in
t-t_{1}
,
if we take, say,
c_{2}<c_{0}/2
. This gives the exponential decay on the fuel
u(t,x)
.

To make this precise, let
\gamma_{0}
be a lower bound for
v(t,x)
in the cone

\mathcal{C}_{t_{1},x_{0}}:=\{|x-x_{0}|\leq c_{0}|t-t_{1}|\},

as in (
4.13
), and let

q_{0}=\inf_{v\geq\gamma_{0}}g(v).

(4.42)

Then, in the cone
\mathcal{C}_{t_{1},x_{0}}
the function
u(t,x)
satisfies

u_{t}-\nu\Delta u+q_{0}u\leq 0.

(4.43)

Let us look for a super-solution to (
4.43
) in the form

\bar{u}(t,x)=Me^{-\alpha(t-t_{1})}\cosh~(\beta|x-x_{0}|),

(4.44)

with the positive constants
M
,
\alpha
and
\beta
to be chosen.
Note that we have, in the polar coordinates

\displaystyle\bar{u}_{t}-\nu\Delta\bar{u}+q_{0}\bar{u}=\bar{u}_{t}-\nu\bar{u}_{rr}-\nu\displaystyle\frac{n-1}{r}\bar{u}_{r}+q_{0}\bar{u}=\Big(-\alpha-\nu\beta^{2}-(n-1)\nu\beta\displaystyle\frac{\tanh~r}{r}+q_{0}\Big)\bar{u}.

(4.45)

As
\tanh r
is concave for
r>0
, we have

\frac{\tanh~r}{r}\leq 1,~~~\hbox{for all $r>0$}.

Using this in (
4.45
) gives

\bar{u}_{t}-\nu\Delta\bar{u}+q_{0}\bar{u}\geq(q_{0}-\alpha-\nu\beta^{2}-(n-1)\nu\beta)\bar{u}.

(4.46)

Therefore, we may choose
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
suitably small so that the
right side in (
4.46
) is positive. In addition, we may choose

in the cone

5
A bound on the averages

The last step in the proof of Theorem
1.2
is to prove a bound on the space-time averages of the function
v(t,x)
.
As we have seen in Corollary
3.6
, such bound would provide
a global in time point-wise bound on
v(t,x)
as well, finishing the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Proposition 5.1

Let
u(t,x)
,
v(t,x)
satisfy the system (
1.1
)
under the assumptions (
1.5
), (
1.6
) and (
1.8
).
There exists a constant
C>0
that depends on the constants in the above
assumptions, and also on the dimension
n
, and diffusivities
\kappa
and
\nu
,
such that for any parabolic cylinder
Q_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})
, with
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

and
t_{0}>1
, we have

|(v)_{Q_{1}}|\leq C.

(5.1)

The rest of this section contains the proof of Proposition
5.1
.

5.1
Reduction to a Hölder in time bound

Once again, let us recall the representation (
2.17
) for the function
v(t,x)
:

v(t,x)=\bar{v}(t,x)+F(t,x)+(\kappa-\nu)\Psi(t,x)

(5.2)

Here,
\bar{v}(t,x)
is the solution to the heat equation, as defined in (
2.7
), the function

F(t,x)
is the solution to (
2.5
) and is uniformly bounded by (
2.8
), while
\Psi(t,x)
satisfies

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}-\kappa\Delta\Psi=\Delta F,

(5.3)

\displaystyle\Psi(0,x)=0.

Next, let
J(t,x)
be the solution to

J_{t}-\kappa\Delta J=F,

(5.4)

with the initial condition

J(0,x)=0
.
Then, we claim that

\Psi(t,x)=\frac{1}{\kappa}(\partial_{t}J-F).

(5.5)

Observe that
the derivative
\partial_{t}J
satisfies

\partial_{t}(\partial_{t}J)-\kappa\Delta(\partial_{t}J)=\partial_{t}F,

(5.6)

with the initial condition

(\partial_{t}J)(0,x)=F(0,x)
.
Hence,
(\partial_{t}J-F)
satisfies

\partial_{t}(\partial_{t}J-F)-\kappa\Delta(\partial_{t}J-F)=\partial_{t}F-\partial_{t}F+\kappa\Delta F=\kappa\Delta F,

(5.7)

with
(\partial_{t}J-F)(0,x)=0
. This gives (
5.5
).

Altogether we now have

v(t,x)=\bar{v}(t,x)+F(t,x)+(\kappa-\nu)\frac{1}{\kappa}(\partial_{t}J(t,x)-F(t,x))=\bar{v}(t,x)+\frac{\nu}{\kappa}F(t,x)+\frac{\kappa-\nu}{\kappa}\partial_{t}J(t,x).

(5.8)

As the functions
\bar{v}(t,x)
and
F(t,x)
are bounded, it follows that for any parabolic cube
Q
we have

(v)_{Q}\leq C(1+(\partial_{t}J)_{Q}).

(5.9)

Let us write

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}J)_{Q_{R}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|Q_{R}|}\int_{|x-x_{0}|<R}\int_{|t-t_{0}|<R^{2}}\partial_{t}J(t,x)dtdx

(5.10)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{R^{n+2}|Q_{1}|}\int_{|x-x_{0}|<R}\left(J(t_{0}+R^{2},x)-J(t_{0}-R^{2},x)\right)dx.

Thus the boundedness of the averages of
v(t,x)
and the conclusion of Proposition
5.1

would follow from the following Hölder in time bound on
J(t,x)
.

Lemma 5.2

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, there exists a constant
C_{\alpha}>0
so that

|J(t_{2},x)-J(t_{1},x)|\leq C_{\alpha}|t_{2}-t_{1}|^{\alpha},~~\hbox{ for all $t_{1}+1\geq t_{2}\geq t_{1}\geq 2$ and $x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$.}

(5.11)

The rest of this section contains the proof of Lemma
5.2
.

5.2
Reduction to a bound on the spatial variations of the fuel in a ”parabola”

We now begin the proof of Lemma
5.2
.
Assume that
t_{2}\geq t_{1}\geq 1
, and write

\displaystyle J(t_{1},x)-J(t_{2},x)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\Big(\frac{e^{-|x-y|^{2}/4\kappa(t_{1}-s)}}{(4\pi\kappa(t_{1}-s))^{n/2}}-\frac{e^{-|x-y|^{2}/4\kappa(t_{2}-s)}}{(4\pi\kappa(t_{2}-s))^{n/2}}\Big)F(s,y)dyds

(5.12)

\displaystyle-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{e^{-|x-y|^{2}/4\kappa(t_{2}-s)}}{(4\pi\kappa(t_{2}-s))^{n/2}}F(s,y)dyds=I_{1}(t_{1},t_{2},x)+I_{2}(t_{1},t_{2},x).

The second term above satisfies the simple estimate

|I_{2}(t_{1},t_{2},x)|\leq\|F\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n})}|t_{2}-t_{1}|\leq K|t_{2}-t_{1}|,

(5.13)

thus we only need to look at
I_{1}
.
For this term, we write, using the Newton-Leibniz formula in the
t
variable, for a fixed
s\in[0,t_{1}]

\frac{e^{-|x-y|^{2}/4\kappa(t_{2}-s)}}{(4\pi\kappa(t_{2}-s))^{n/2}}-\frac{e^{-|x-y|^{2}/4\kappa(t_{1}-s)}}{(4\pi\kappa(t_{1}-s))^{n/2}}=C_{\kappa}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\frac{h(z)}{(\tau-s)^{n/2+1}}d\tau,~~z=\frac{x-y}{\sqrt{\kappa(\tau-s)}},

(5.14)

with an integrable function

h(z)=\Big(-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{|z|^{2}}{4}\Big)e^{-|z|^{2}/4}.

(5.15)

Note that
h(z)
has mean zero:

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}h(z)dz=0.

(5.16)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle I_{1}(t_{1},t_{2},x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\Big(\frac{e^{-|x-y|^{2}/4\kappa(t_{1}-s)}}{(4\pi\kappa(t_{1}-s))^{n/2}}-\frac{e^{-|x-y|^{2}/4\kappa(t_{2}-s)}}{(4\pi\kappa(t_{2}-s))^{n/2}}\Big)F(s,y)dyds

(5.17)

\displaystyle=C_{\kappa}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}h\Big(\frac{x-y}{\sqrt{4\kappa(\tau-s)}}\Big)F(s,y)\frac{d\tau dyds}{(\tau-s)^{n/2+1}}

\displaystyle=C_{\kappa}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}h(z)F(s,x-z\sqrt{4\kappa(\tau-s)})\frac{d\tau dsdz}{\tau-s}.

Since
h(z)
has zero integral, this can be written as

\displaystyle I_{1}(t_{1},t_{2},x)

\displaystyle=C_{\kappa}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}h(z)\big(F(s,x-z\sqrt{4\kappa(\tau-s)})-F(s,x)\big)\frac{d\tau dsdz}{\tau-s}.

(5.18)

We may now use (
2.4
) and (
5.16
) to split
I_{1}
as

\displaystyle I_{1}(t_{1},t_{2},x)=I_{0}(t_{1},t_{2},x)+\bar{I}_{1}(t_{1},t_{2},x)-I_{3}(t_{1},t_{2},x),

(5.19)

with

\displaystyle I_{0}(t_{1},t_{2},x)

\displaystyle=C_{\kappa}\int_{0}^{t_{1}/2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}h(z)F(s,x-z\sqrt{4\kappa(\tau-s)})\frac{d\tau dsdz}{\tau-s},

(5.20)

and

\displaystyle\bar{I}_{1}(t_{1},t_{2},x)

\displaystyle=C_{\kappa}\int_{t_{1}/2}^{t_{1}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}h(z)\big(\bar{u}(s,x-z\sqrt{4\kappa(\tau-s)})-\bar{u}(s,x)\big)\frac{d\tau dsdz}{\tau-s},

(5.21)

and

\displaystyle I_{3}(t_{1},t_{2},x)

\displaystyle=C_{\kappa}\int_{t_{1}/2}^{t_{1}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}h(z)\big(u(s,x-z\sqrt{4\kappa(\tau-s)})-u(s,x)\big)\frac{d\tau dsdz}{\tau-s}.

(5.22)

The term
I_{0}
is bounded in the same way as
I_{2}
:

\displaystyle|I_{0}(t_{1},t_{2},x)|

\displaystyle\leq C\|F\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{0}^{t_{1}/2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}|h(z)|\frac{d\tau dsdz}{\tau-s}\leq C\|F\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{0}^{t_{1}/2}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\frac{d\tau dsdz}{\tau-s}\leq C|t_{2}-t_{1}|,

(5.23)

since
t_{2}\geq t_{1}\geq 2
.

As the function
u_{0}(x)
is bounded, there exists
a constant
C
so that

|\bar{u}(t,x)-\bar{u}(t,y)|\leq\frac{C}{t^{1/2}}|x-y|,~~\hbox{for all $t\geq 1$ and $x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$.}

(5.24)

We also have the uniform bound
0\leq\bar{u}(t,x)\leq K
for all
t\geq 0
and
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
This gives the following bound on
\bar{I}_{1}

\displaystyle|\bar{I}_{1}(t_{1},t_{2},x)|\leq C\int_{t_{1}/2}^{t_{1}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}|h(z)|\frac{C|z|(\tau-s)^{1/2}}{s^{1/2}}\frac{d\tau dsdz}{\tau-s}=C\int_{t_{1}/2}^{t_{1}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\frac{d\tau ds}{s^{1/2}(\tau-s)^{1/2}}

(5.25)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{t_{1}^{1/2}}\int_{t_{1}/2}^{t_{1}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\frac{d\tau ds}{(\tau-s)^{1/2}}\leq\frac{C}{t_{1}^{1/2}}\int_{t_{1}/2}^{t_{1}}(\sqrt{t_{2}-s}-\sqrt{t_{1}-s})ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{t_{1}^{1/2}}\int_{t_{1}/2}^{t_{1}}\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{\sqrt{t_{2}-s}+\sqrt{t_{1}-s}}ds\leq C|t_{2}-t_{1}|.

Thus, our focus is now on the term
I_{3}
. We choose
q\in(0,1/10)
and split this term as

I_{3}(t_{1},t_{2},x)=C_{\kappa}(A_{3}(t_{1},t_{2},x)+R_{3}(t_{1},t_{2},x)),

(5.26)

with

\displaystyle A_{3}(t_{1},t_{2},x)

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{1}/2}^{t_{1}}\int_{|z|\leq(t_{1}-s)^{q}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}h(z)\big(u(s,x-z\sqrt{4\kappa(\tau-s)})-u(s,x)\big)\frac{d\tau dsdz}{\tau-s}

(5.27)

and

\displaystyle R_{3}(t_{1},t_{2},x)

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{1}/2}^{t_{1}}\int_{|z|\geq(t_{1}-s)^{q}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}h(z)\big(u(s,x-z\sqrt{4\kappa(\tau-s)})-u(s,x)\big)\frac{d\tau dsdz}{\tau-s}.

(5.28)

The term
R_{3}
can be bounded as

\displaystyle|R_{3}(t_{1},t_{2},x)|

\displaystyle\leq C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{t_{1}/2}^{t_{1}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}e^{-c(t_{1}-s)^{q}}\frac{d\tau ds}{\tau-s}

(5.29)

\displaystyle\leq C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{t_{1}/2}^{t_{1}}e^{-c(t_{1}-s)^{q}}\big(\log(t_{2}-s)-\log(t_{1}-s)\big)ds

\displaystyle\leq C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-cs^{q}}\big(\log(t_{2}-t_{1}+s)-\log s\big)ds.

Thus, for any
m>0
and
N>10
we have

\displaystyle|R_{3}(t_{1},t_{2},x)|\leq C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{0}^{N}\big(\log(t_{2}-t_{1}+s)-\log s\big)ds+C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{N}^{\infty}e^{-cs^{q}}\big(\log(1+s)-\log s\big)ds

(5.30)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}}{N^{m}}+C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{0}^{N}\big(\log(t_{2}-t_{1}+s)-\log s\big)ds=\frac{C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}}{N^{m}}

\displaystyle+{C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}}\Big((N+t_{2}-t_{1})[\log(N+t_{2}-t_{1})-1]-(t_{2}-t_{1})(\log(t_{2}-t_{1})-1)-N(\log N-1)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}}{N^{m}}+{C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}}\Big((N+t_{2}-t_{1})\Big(\log N+C\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{N}-1\Big)-N(\log N-1)

\displaystyle-(t_{2}-t_{1})(\log(t_{2}-t_{1})-1)\Big)\leq\frac{C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}}{N^{m}}+{C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}}\Big((t_{2}-t_{1})\log N+(t_{2}-t_{1})|\log(t_{2}-t_{1})|\Big).

We used the fact that
0\leq t_{2}-t_{1}\leq 1
above.
Choosing
N=1/(t_{2}-t_{1})
gives

\displaystyle|R_{3}(t_{1},t_{2},x)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\alpha}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}|(t_{2}-t_{1})^{\alpha},

(5.31)

for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

The above analysis shows that
the main task is to bound the term
A_{3}
defined in (
5.27
).
As a preliminary step, note that for any fixed
T>0
it suffices consider
only
t_{1}>10T
. This follows from the local existence bounds already established in
[
8
]
and
[
18
]
. In addition, observe that for any
T>0
fixed and
t_{1}>10T
we have

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{3,T}(t_{1},t_{2},x):=\Big|\int_{t_{1}-T}^{t_{1}}\int_{|z|\leq(t_{1}-s)^{q}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}h(z)\big(u(s,x-z\sqrt{4\kappa(\tau-s)})-u(s,x)\big)\frac{d\tau dsdz}{\tau-s}\Big|

(5.32)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{t_{1}-T}^{t_{1}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\frac{d\tau ds}{\tau-s}=C\int_{t_{1}-T}^{t_{1}}(\log(t_{2}-s)-\log(t_{1}-s))ds

\displaystyle=C\int_{0}^{T}(\log(t_{2}-t_{1}+s)-\log s)ds

\displaystyle=C\Big((t_{2}-t_{1}+T)(\log(t_{2}-t_{1}+T)-1)-(t_{2}-t_{1})(\log(t_{2}-t_{1})-1)-T(\log T-1))

\displaystyle\leq C\Big((t_{2}-t_{1}+T)(\log T+C\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{T})-1)-(t_{2}-t_{1})(\log(t_{2}-t_{1})-1)-T(\log T-1))

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\log T)|t_{2}-t_{1}|^{\alpha},

for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

5.3
A bound for the spatial variations of the fuel in the parabola

The previous analysis shows that
the main task is to bound the term
A_{3,T}
:

\displaystyle A_{3,T}(t_{1},t_{2},x)

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{1}/2}^{t_{1}-T}\int_{|z|\leq(t_{1}-s)^{q}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}h(z)\big(u(s,x-z\sqrt{4\kappa(\tau-s)})-u(s,x)\big)\frac{d\tau dzds}{\tau-s}.

(5.33)

Recall that here we have
10T\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq t_{1}+1
, and
T
is fixed but sufficiently large, to be chosen.
To estimate
A_{3,T}
, we need to show that the variations of the fuel concentration
u(s,y)
are small for most times
s
in the time interval
t_{1}/2\leq s\leq t_{1}

inside the domain of integration in (
5.33
) that we will call a “parabola”, centered at the point
x
and the time
t_{1}
.
We also define, for
A>10
and for all
s\leq t_{1}-T
the region

P^{A}_{s}=\big\{(s^{\prime},y^{\prime}):~0\leq s^{\prime}\leq s,~|y^{\prime}-x|\leq A\sqrt{4\kappa}(t_{1}-s^{\prime})^{{q}}{(t_{1}+1-s^{\prime})^{{1}/{2}}}\big\}

(5.34)

for the part of the parabola at times earlier than
s
that we will refer to as sub-parabola.
The constant
A
will be
chosen to be large, and since
t_{2}\leq t_{1}+1
, it is clear that the points
(s,x)
and
(s,x-z\sqrt{4\kappa(\tau-s)})
in (
5.33
) belong to
P^{A}_{s}

if
|z|<(t_{1}-s)^{q}
.

An intuitive reason for the smallness of oscillations
inside the parabola is that until the front arrives into this region, the fuel is
not consumed while its initial variations are quickly uniformized by the diffusion. Once the front arrives, it spreads through the parabola with a linear speed,
consuming the fuel. The fuel variations during this period can not be controlled but this time interval is relatively short. After the front has passed,
there is no fuel left in the parabola, hence its variations are small again. Let us also comment that, according to Proposition
4.2
, once the
temperature is“large” at a given point, it never becomes “too small” again.

According to the above intuition, given a point
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and a time
t_{1}>10T
, with a sufficiently large but fixed
T>0
,
we will consider three cases: (1) the front “has reached the parabola not long before
t_{1}
”, (2) the front “has reached the
parabola long before
t_{1}
”, and (3) the front “has not reached the parabola by the time
t_{1}
”.

To quantify the above informal notions, let us first clarify the meaning of the phrase “the front reached the parabola”:
if there is a time
s^{\prime}\in[t_{1}/2,t_{1}-T]
such that there exists a point
x^{\prime}
,
so that

|x-x^{\prime}|\leq{A}\sqrt{4\kappa}(t_{1}-s^{\prime})^{{q}}{(t_{1}+1-s^{\prime})^{{1}/{2}}},

(5.35)

and the spatial average of the temperature around
x^{\prime}
is not small, in the sense that

\int_{|y-x^{\prime}|\leq 1}v(s^{\prime},y)dy\geq\frac{1}{(t_{1}-s^{\prime})^{m}},

(5.36)

with
m>0
to be chosen, we say that the
t_{1}
-front has reached the parabola centered at
x
by the time
s^{\prime}
.
Then, we define the “front-reaching time”
s_{0}
as the infimum of all times such that (
5.36
) holds:

s_{0}:=\inf\Big\{s^{\prime}\in[\frac{t_{1}}{2},t_{1}-T]~\text{s.t. there is $x^{\prime}$ so that (\ref{oct2529}) and (\ref{oct2528}) hold.}\Big\}

(5.37)

It follows that there is
x_{0}
such that

{|x-x_{0}|\leq A\sqrt{4\kappa}(t_{1}-s_{0})^{q}(t_{1}+1-s_{0})^{{1}/{2}},}

(5.38)

and

\int_{|y-x_{0}|\leq 1}v(s_{0},y)dy\geq\frac{1}{(t_{1}-s_{0})^{m}}.

(5.39)

Once the front-reaching time
s_{0}
is set, we define the late-stage time
s_{2}
.
Intuitively, it is the time when
u
is already decaying exponentially near the point
x
due to the presence of front. First, we let
s_{1}
be

s_{1}:=s_{0}+m\log(t_{1}-s_{0})+C.

(5.40)

Corollary
4.3
implies that then the fuel concentration
u(s,y)
obeys an upper bound

u(s,y)\leq Ke^{-\gamma_{2}(s-s_{1})},~~\hbox{for all $s\geq s_{1}=s_{0}+m\log(t_{1}-s_{0})+C$
and $|y-x_{0}|\leq c_{2}(s-s_{1})$.}

(5.41)

Here, we require
T>1
so that Corollary
4.3
applies.
Next, we define the late-stage time
s_{2}
by

s_{2}:=s_{1}+\frac{1}{c_{2}}10A\sqrt{4\kappa}(t_{1}-s_{0})^{q}(t_{1}-s_{0}+1)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(5.42)

so that

c_{2}(s_{2}-s_{1})=10A\sqrt{4\kappa}(t_{1}-s_{0})^{q}(t_{1}-s_{0}+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(5.43)

holds. The intuition behind the choice of
s_{2}
is clear: we know that
u
is small
near
x_{0}
after the time
s_{1}
. Then, by Corollary
4.3
, after waiting for additional time
s_{2}-s_{1}
,
the smallness of

is propagated and
u
is exponentially decaying throughout the parabola after the time
s_{2}
.

Consider a time
s\in[s_{2},t_{1}]
and
(s,y)\in P^{A}_{s}
, that is,

|y-x|\leq A\sqrt{4\kappa}(t_{1}-s)^{q}(t_{1}-s+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(5.44)

Then, due to (
5.38
), (
5.43
) and (
5.44
), we have

|y-x_{0}|\leq A\sqrt{4\kappa}(t_{1}-s)^{q}(t_{1}-s+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}+A\sqrt{4\kappa}(t_{1}-s_{0})^{q}(t_{1}-s_{0}+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq c_{2}(s_{2}-s_{1})\leq c_{2}(s-s_{1}).

(5.45)

We deduce from (
5.41
) and (
5.45
) that the fuel concentration inside the parabola is, indeed, very small for
s>s_{2}
by Corollary
4.3
:

u(s,y)\leq Ke^{-\gamma_{2}(s-s_{1})},~~\hbox{for all $s\in[s_{2},t_{1}]$
and $|y-x|\leq A\sqrt{4\kappa}(t_{1}-s)^{q}(t_{1}-s+1)^{{1}/{2}}$.}

(5.46)

Let us comment that for convenience we require
T
to be large enough, so that the function

t\rightarrow\frac{1}{2}t-m\log t-\frac{10A\sqrt{4\kappa}}{c_{2}}t^{q}(t+1)^{{1}/{2}}-C

is positive for

. it follows, in particular, that

t_{1}-s_{2}>\frac{1}{2}(t_{1}-s_{0})\geq\frac{T}{2},

that is,
s_{2}<t_{1}-{T}/{2}
.

Brief outline of the strategy.

With the above definitions of the times
s_{0}
and
s_{2}
, we will split the integral (
5.33
) into three pieces:
first, the early times
s\in[t_{1}/2,s_{0}-C\log(t_{1}-s_{0})-C]
. Here,
C>0
is a constant independent of
t_{1},s_{0}
that we will choose later.
The intermediate times cover the interval
s\in[s_{0}-C\log(t_{1}-s_{0})-C,s_{2}]
, and late times include
s\in[s_{2},t_{1}-T]
.
We will see that the integral over each time interval gives a contribution of the form
C|t_{2}-t_{1}|
, where
C
is independent of
t_{1}
, but for
very different reasons.

First, for the early times
s
, by the backward in time estimate in Corollary
4.4
and (
5.37
),
we know that the 1-ball average of the fuel temperature
v
near
a point
x\in P^{A}_{s}
decays exponentially. To get this decay,
we needed to subtract from
s_{0}
the additional term involving
\log(t_{1}-s_{0})
in the definition of the upper bound for the early times.
From Corollary
3.6
, it follows that
v
itself is small and moreover by the parabolic regularity theory, the gradient
\nabla v
is
also small in the smaller region
P^{A^{\prime}}_{s}
, with some
A^{\prime}<A
. The application of the parabolic regularity theory explains why
we needed to subtract the additional
O(1)
term in the definition
of the upper bound for the early times.
Thus, during the early times and inside the slightly smaller parabola the fuel concentration

u
is a solution to the heat equation with a small potential that also has a small gradient: see equation (
5.83
) below.
Using Bernstein’s method for the standard heat equation
[
21
]
, we then obtain a point-wise upper bound on

\nabla u(s,x)
inside an early times parabolic cylinder of size
R>0
. This bound is controlled by

\frac{1}{R^{2}}+R^{2}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{y}(Q_{R}^{-}(s,x))}^{2}.

By adjusting the size
R
of the cylinder by the time
s
, to take advantage from smallness of
\nabla v
in
Q_{R}^{-}(s,x)
, we end up with
the bound (
5.91
) below for
\nabla u(s,y)
. This allows us to control the integral in (
5.33
) over the early times.

Second, to control the integral in (
5.33
) over the late times, we use the exponential decay of
u
in (
5.46
).

Finally, on the intermediate time interval, neither
u
nor
v
are necessarily small. The key point is that if we only use the
L^{\infty}
bound for the function

u
, the integral in (
5.33
) is controlled by

\int\big(\log(t_{2}-s)-\log(t_{1}-s)\big)ds\leq\int\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{t_{1}-s}ds.

However, in the intermediate time interval, the difference
t_{1}-s
varies within the range

[(t_{1}-s_{0})-m\log(t_{1}-s_{0})-C-\frac{1}{c_{2}}10A\sqrt{4\kappa}(t_{1}-s_{0})^{q}(t_{1}-s_{0}+1)^{1/2},(t_{1}-s_{0})+C\log(t_{1}-s_{0})+C].

Since
T
is sufficiently large and
t_{1}-s_{0}\geq T
, this means that integral of
(t_{1}-s)^{-1}
within this range of

remains bounded uniformly in
t_{1}
.

According to the above scenario, we
estimate
A_{3,T}
as

|A_{3,T}|\leq A_{e}+A_{m}+A_{\ell},

(5.47)

with

\displaystyle A_{e}(t_{1},t_{2},x)

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{1}/2}^{s_{0}-C\log(t_{1}-s_{0})-C}\int_{|z|\leq(t_{1}-s)^{q}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}|h(z)|\big|u(s,x-z\sqrt{4\kappa(\tau-s)})-u(s,x)\big|\frac{d\tau dzds}{\tau-s},

(5.48)

\displaystyle A_{m}(t_{1},t_{2},x)

\displaystyle=\int_{s_{0}-C\log(t_{1}-s_{0})-C}^{s_{2}}\int_{|z|\leq(t_{1}-s)^{q}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}|h(z)|\big|u(s,x-z\sqrt{4\kappa(\tau-s)})-u(s,x)\big|\frac{d\tau dzds}{\tau-s},

(5.49)

and

\displaystyle A_{\ell}(t_{1},t_{2},x)

\displaystyle=\int_{s_{2}}^{t_{1}-T/2}\int_{|z|\leq(t_{1}-s)^{q}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}|h(z)|\big|u(s,x-z\sqrt{4\kappa(\tau-s)})-u(s,x)\big|\frac{d\tau dzds}{\tau-s}.

(5.50)

We have extended the domain of integration in

in the term

to the upper limit

simply to avoid considering separately the cases
s_{2}<t_{1}-T
and
s_{2}>t_{1}-T
,
while we do know that

.

5.3.1
An estimate of the middle term
A_{m}

For the ”middle” term

, we note that it follows from the definitions of the times
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
in (

) and (

) that
for a sufficiently large

, we have

\frac{t_{1}-s_{2}}{(t_{1}-s_{0})+C\log(t_{1}-s_{0})+C}\in[1/3,1]

(5.51)

since
t_{1}-s_{0}\geq T
.

Therefore, we have the following estimate for
A_{m}

\displaystyle|A_{m}(t_{1},t_{2},x)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s_{0}{-C\log(t_{1}-s_{0})-C}}^{s_{2}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\frac{d\tau ds}{\tau-s}=\int_{s_{0}{-C\log(t_{1}-s_{0})-C}}^{s_{2}}[\log(t_{2}-s)-\log(t_{1}-s)]

(5.52)

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{1}-s_{2}}^{t_{1}-s_{0}{+C\log(t_{1}-s_{0})+C}}\big(\log(t_{2}-t_{1}+s)-\log s)ds\leq C\int_{t_{1}-s_{2}}^{t_{1}-s_{0}{+C\log(t_{1}-s_{0})+C}}\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{s}ds

\displaystyle=C(t_{2}-t_{1})\log\Big(\frac{t_{1}-s_{0}{+C\log(t_{1}-s_{0})+C}}{t_{1}-s_{2}}\Big)\leq C(t_{2}-t_{1}).

5.3.2
An estimate of the late term
A_{\ell}

The ”late” term
A_{\ell}
can be bounded using the upper bound (
5.46
):

\displaystyle|A_{\ell}(t_{1},t_{2},x)|

\displaystyle\leq C\!\int_{s_{2}}^{t_{1}-T/2}\!\!\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}e^{-\gamma_{2}(s-s_{1})}\frac{d\tau dsdz}{\tau-s}\leq\!C\int_{s_{{2}}}^{t_{1}{-T/2}}e^{-\gamma_{2}(s-s_{1})}\big(\log(t_{2}-s)-\log(t_{1}-s))ds

(5.53)

\displaystyle=C\int_{s_{{2}}}^{t_{1}-T/2}e^{-\gamma_{2}(s-s_{1})}\log\Big(\frac{t_{2}-s}{t_{1}-s}\Big)ds=C\int_{s_{{2}}}^{t_{1}-T/2}e^{-\gamma_{2}(s-s_{1})}\log\Big(1+\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{t_{1}-s}\Big)ds.

As

and

, we can estimate the integral above as

\displaystyle|A_{\ell}(t_{1},t_{2},x)|

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{s_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}2}}}^{t_{1}-T/2}e^{-\gamma_{2}(s-s_{1})}\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{t_{1}-s}ds\leq\frac{C}{T}|t_{2}-t_{1}|.

(5.54)

5.3.3
An estimate of the early term
A_{e}

The last and longer step is to estimate the early contribution
A_{e}
. To do this, we will estimate
the Hölder constant of the fuel concentration
u(t,x)
inside the parabola for times

s
in the interval

t_{1}/2<s<s_{0}-C\log(t_{1}-s_{0})-C.

The idea is as follows. We already know that the
temperature
v(s,y)
is small in all of the sub-parabola
P^{A}_{s_{0}}
. The parabolic regularity estimates
that we detail below will also imply that the gradient
\nabla v(s,y)
is small in the sub-parabola
P^{A^{\prime}}_{s_{0}}
for
A^{\prime}<A
sufficiently close to
A
.
Therefore, the fuel concentration is a solution to a parabolic equation of the form

u_{t}=\Delta u-V(t,x)u,

(5.55)

with the potential
V(t,x)=g(v(t,x))
that is very small and
positive and also has a very small gradient in any sub-parabola
P_{s}^{A^{\prime}}
,
with
0\leq s\leq s_{0}
.

In addition, if we take a time
s<s_{0}
, and a point
y\in P_{s_{0}}^{A^{\prime}}
that is not too close to the boundary of the parabola, then
we will be able to fit a parabolic cylinder
Q_{R}(s,y)
of a radius

R\sim(t_{1}-s)^{1/2+q^{\prime}},

with some
0<q^{\prime}<q
, inside the parabola
P_{s}^{A^{\prime}}
. Hence, the fuel concentration
u(t,x)
will basically solve the heat equation inside
a very large parabolic cylinder
Q_{R}(s,y)
. This will show that
|\nabla u(s,y)|\sim O(R^{-1})
,
and such bound will suffice for us to estimate
A_{e}
.

We now follow the above outline.
From the definition of
s_{0}
, we know that either
s_{0}={t_{1}}/{2}
, which is the case where
A_{e}=0
, or
s_{0}>{t_{1}}/{2}
and
the local spatial averages of the temperature satisfy

\int_{|y-z|\leq 1}v(s_{0},z)dz\leq\frac{1}{|t_{1}-s_{0}|^{m}},~~\hbox{for each $y\in P^{A}_{s}$, that is, $|x-y|\leq A\sqrt{4\kappa}(t_{1}-s_{0})^{q}(t_{1}-s_{0}+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}$. }

(5.56)

It follows from Corollary
4.4
that then the spatial averages at the
earlier times satisfy

\displaystyle\int_{|y-z|\leq 1}v(s,z)dz

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{|t_{1}-s_{0}|^{m}}e^{-\gamma_{3}(s_{0}-s)},~~\hbox{for all $s\leq s_{0}{-C\log(t_{1}-s_{0})-C}$ and }

(5.57)

\displaystyle|x-y|\leq{\frac{c_{0}}{2}(s_{0}-s)+A\sqrt{4\kappa}(t_{1}-s_{0})^{q}(t_{1}-s_{0}+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

A key point is that Corollary
3.6

\displaystyle v(s,y)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{|t_{1}-s_{0}|^{m(1-\varepsilon)}}e^{-\gamma_{4}(s_{0}-s)},~~\hbox{for all $s\leq s_{0}{-C\log(t_{1}-s_{0})-(C+4)}$ and }

(5.58)

\displaystyle|x-y|\leq\frac{c_{0}}{2}(s_{0}-s)+{A^{\prime}\sqrt{4\kappa}(t_{1}-s_{0})^{q}(t_{1}-s_{0}+1)^{{1}/{2}}},

for some
A^{\prime}\in(1,A)
. Note that we assume that

(A-A^{\prime})\sqrt{4\kappa}T^{q}(T+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq C\geq 2,

Observe that, given

that is, we have

{A^{\prime}\sqrt{4\kappa}(t_{1}-s)^{q}(t_{1}-s+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq A^{\prime}\sqrt{4\kappa}(t_{1}-s_{0})^{q}(t_{1}-s_{0}+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{c_{0}}{2}(s_{0}-s),}~~\hbox{for all $0\leq s\leq s_{0}$,}

since the function

It follows from (
5.58
) and
(

at the times

s_{0}{-C\log(t_{1}-s_{0})-C}
, after a suitable relabelling of the constant

:

\displaystyle v(s,y)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{|t_{1}-s_{0}|^{m(1-\varepsilon)}}e^{-\gamma_{4}(s_{0}-s)},~~\hbox{for all $s\leq s_{0}{-C\log(t_{1}-s_{0})-C}$ and }

(5.60)

\displaystyle|x-y|\leq{A^{\prime}\sqrt{4\kappa}(t_{1}-s)^{q}(t_{1}-s+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

Here, we again assume that
T

.

We will use the following estimate for the solutions to the forced

[

[

We deduce from (

\displaystyle\|v\|_{C^{\alpha/2,\alpha}(Q_{1}{{}^{-}}(s,y))}

\displaystyle|x-y|\leq{A^{\prime\prime}\sqrt{4\kappa}(t_{1}-s)^{q}(t_{1}-s+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}}

Similarly, it also follows from (

\displaystyle\|u\|_{C^{\alpha/2,\alpha}(Q_{1}^{-}(s,y))}

\displaystyle\|ug(v)\|_{C^{\alpha/2,\alpha}(Q{{}^{-}}_{1}(s,y))}

\displaystyle|x-y|\leq{A^{\prime\prime}\sqrt{4\kappa}(t_{1}-s)^{q}(t_{1}-s+1)^{{1}/{2}}}.

\displaystyle\|v\|_{C^{1+\alpha/2,2+\alpha}(Q^{-}_{1/10}(s,y))}

\displaystyle\|\nabla v\|_{L^{\infty}(Q^{-}_{1/10}(s,y))}

The next step is to translate the estimate (

in

\psi(t,x)
be a positive solution to

\psi_{t}=\Delta\psi-V(t,x)\psi,

with a small non-negative potential

We will assume without loss of generality that

Our goal is to get a bound on

We follow the argument in

\displaystyle w_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{R^{2}}\zeta\zeta_{t}\Big(\frac{t}{R^{2}},\frac{x}{R}\Big)|\nabla\psi(t,x)|^{2}+2\zeta_{R}^{2}\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla\psi_{t}+2\mu\psi\psi_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{R^{2}}\zeta\zeta_{t}\Big(\frac{t}{R^{2}},\frac{x}{R}\Big)|\nabla\psi(t,x)|^{2}+2\zeta_{R}^{2}\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla(\Delta\psi)-2\zeta_{R}^{2}\psi\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla V-2\zeta_{R}^{2}V|\nabla\psi|^{2}

\displaystyle+2\mu\psi\Delta\psi-2\mu V\psi^{2},

\displaystyle\Delta w=\frac{2}{R^{2}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}(\zeta\Delta\zeta+|\nabla\zeta|^{2})\Big(\frac{t}{R^{2}},\frac{x}{R}\Big)\!+2\zeta_{R}^{2}(\psi_{kj}\psi_{kj}+\psi_{k}\Delta\psi_{k})\!+\!\frac{8}{R}\zeta\zeta_{k}\psi_{j}\psi_{jk}+2\mu\psi\Delta\psi+2\mu|\nabla\psi|^{2}.

This gives

\displaystyle w_{t}-\Delta w

\displaystyle=|\nabla\psi|^{2}\Big(\frac{2}{R^{2}}\zeta\zeta_{t}-2\zeta_{R}^{2}V-\frac{2}{R^{2}}(\zeta\Delta\zeta+|\nabla\zeta|^{2})-2\mu\Big)+2\zeta_{R}^{2}\big(\psi_{k}\psi_{kjj}-\psi_{kj}\psi_{kj}-\psi_{k}\psi_{kjj}\big)
