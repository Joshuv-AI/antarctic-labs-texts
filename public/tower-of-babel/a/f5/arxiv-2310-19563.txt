Data-driven optimal control via linear programming: boundedness guarantees
Lucia Falconi, Andrea Martinelli, John Lygeros

Abstract
The linear programming (LP) approach is, together with value iteration and policy iteration, one of the three fundamental methods to solve optimal control problems in a dynamic programming setting. Despite its simple formulation, versatility, and predisposition to be employed in model-free settings, the LP approach has not enjoyed the same popularity as the other methods. The reason is the often poor scalability of the exact LP approach and the difficulty to obtain bounded solutions for a reasonable amount of constraints. We mitigate these issues here, by investigating fundamental geometric features of the LP and developing sufficient conditions to guarantee finite solutions with minimal constraints. In the model-free context, we show that boundedness can be guaranteed by a suitable choice of dataset and objective function.



I
Introduction

I-A

Literature review

Optimal control problems arise in most engineering and scientific disciplines, from robotics to bioengineering to finance
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, to name but a few. One approach to solve optimal control problems was developed in the 1950’s by Bellman
[
4
]
. It is based on the so-called
principle of optimality
to derive a functional equation in the state and input variables, the Bellman equation, characterizing the optimal feedback controller. The methods to solve the Bellman equation are collectively known as
dynamic programming
(DP), and typically rely on three fundamental techniques: value iteration, policy iteration, and linear programming (LP)
[
5
,
6
]
. The foundations of all three techniques lie in the monotonicity and contractivity properties of the Bellman operator, implicitly defined by the Bellman equation
[
7
]
. The main difficulty affecting DP methods is the rapid growth of the computation needed to solve the Bellman equation with the number of variables involved, known as the
curse of dimensionality
. Throughout the years, several approximation techniques have been developed to mitigate this problem, collectively known as
approximate
dynamic programming (ADP)
[
6
,
8
,
9
]
.

In this context, the present manuscript focuses on the LP approach to ADP. Introduced by Manne in the 1960’s
[
10
]
, the method is based on the idea that the solution to the Bellman equation can be cast as the optimizer of an LP. In the mid-1980’s, Schweitzer presented an approximate version of the LPs for finite state-action spaces, by restricting the function space to a finite family of linearly parameterized functions
[
11
]
. Nevertheless, it is only in the early 2000’ with the work by De Farias and Van Roy
[
12
,
13
]
that error bounds due to function approximation and constraint sampling are developed.

When dealing with infinite state and action spaces, the exact infinite dimensional LPs have to be approximated by
finite dimensional ones
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
. Several methods that build upon these approximation schemes have been discussed in recent years, for instance by using Sum-of-Squares polynomials
[
18
]
or iterated Bellman inequalities
[
19
]
.

In many real-world applications one has to operate a system without the knowledge of its dynamics. Learning how to optimally regulate a system is possible by letting it interact with the environment and collecting information about the resulting state transitions and costs (or rewards) over time, an approach classically known as
reinforcement learning
(RL)
[
20
,
21
]
. To extend RL methods in the LP approach framework, one can reformulate the Bellman equation in terms of the
Q
-function
[
22
]
, and set up a new class of LPs based on the Bellman operator for
Q
-functions. The main advantage of the
Q
-function formulation is that one can extract a policy directly from
Q
, without knowledge of the system dynamics or stage cost.

Cogill and co-authors were the first to introduce an LP formulation based on
Q
-functions in 2006
[
23
]
. A first analysis on the associated error bounds can be found in
[
24
]
for finite state-action spaces, and in
[
25
]
for continuous ones. Learning the optimal
Q
-function for deterministic systems by mixing LP with approximate value iteration and policy iteration schemes is investigated in
[
26
]
and
[
27
]
. The relaxed Bellman operator introduced in
[
28
]
allows one to design efficient LPs based on
Q
-functions even for stochastic systems. In case of linear or affine dynamics, one can generate new constraints offline starting from a small dataset
[
29
]
, and approximate the expectation in the LP constraints with the estimators introduced in
[
30
]
. A taxonomy of this literature is provided in Table
I
.

I-B

Motivations

The exact LP formulation for finite state-action spaces was viewed as primarily a theoretical result for many years, since it requires formulating an LP where the number of constraints is equal to the number of state-action pairs
[
9
, Ch. 3.8]
. The method has seen a resurgence in recent years, concurrently with major advances in LP solution methods
[
31
]
, and especially via approximation schemes to deal with continuous state-action spaces and model-free approaches
[
8
]
. Indeed, most of the above-mentioned literature has shown encouraging results, particularly for low-dimensional systems. To finally become competitive even for higher-dimensional problems, we argue that the main obstacle is the difficulty of systematically obtaining bounded solution.

As first mentioned in
[
28
]
, the number of constraints needed to keep the LP bounded grows dramatically with the dimension of the system. Indeed, we note that most of the (even recent) literature on the topic only propose control examples with minimal dimension and/or massive dataset
[
12
,
13
,
19
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
. Some papers deal with this problem by introducing a regularizer to bound a certain norm of the optimal value/
Q
-function
[
24
]
. This, however, can introduce a significant and hard to quantify distortion of the optimizers, since in case of unboundedness the solution is effectively determined by the regularizer and not the constraints. To the best of our knowledge, the only work to explicitly consider sufficient conditions to obtain bounded solutions without regularizers is
[
32
]
. They consider the “optimal” LP formulation, that is, the LP whose solution is the optimal value/
Q
-function, and provide a sufficient condition based on the rank of a certain data-based covariance matrix. We will review this condition in Section
III-C
together with additional insights.

I-C

Outline and Contributions

The main goal of this manuscript is to provide systematic and practical conditions on the constraints to guarantee finite solution of the LPs “associated to a policy”, that is, the LPs whose solution is the value/
Q
-function associated to a fixed control policy. To do so, we first investigate some fundamental geometric features of the LPs feasible regions.

In Section
III-A
we focus on the exact LP formulations, showing that (i) the feasible region of the LPs associated to a policy is a convex cone open-ended in the “south-west” direction, and (ii) the feasible region of the optimal LPs is a convex set that can be either bounded or unbounded.
Section
III-B
is devoted to approximate LPs, where the function space is restricted to a finite dimensional linear subspace
and the constraints are either sampled or observed from system exploration. We prove that the approximate LPs enjoy similar geometric features to
their exact counterparts. Then, we use Farkas’ Lemma to prove that the LPs associated to a policy are bounded if and only if the objective function lies in the conical hull of the rows of a certain data matrix. Finally, under mild assumptions, we show that if the solution is bounded, then it is optimal.
In Section
IV-B
we focus on linear quadratic problems. We show that the LP feasible region is bounded if and only if the system is reachable. Then, we specialize the previous results and discuss two systematic methods to guarantee bounded (hence optimal) solutions by properly selecting the gradient of the objective function of the LP based on the observed data.
The numerical results in Section
V
provide evidence for the difficulty of obtaining bounded solutions for the approximate LPs when random sampling is employed. On the other hand, they illustrate the effectiveness of the strategies in Section
IV-B
where we consistently obtain bounded solutions with minimal constraints.

II
Background on Dynamic Programming

II-A

General formulation

We start with the necessary background on the DP formulation of
infinite-horizon, discrete-time, deterministic
optimal control problems.
We refer the reader to
[
5
,
6
]
for a comprehensive review on the DP theory.

We consider a deterministic discrete-time dynamical system

x_{t+1}=f(x_{t},u_{t}),

(1)

where
x_{t}\in\mathbf{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
represents the state of the system at time
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
u_{t}\in\mathbf{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
the control action,
and
f:\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{U}\to\mathbb{R}
describes the evolution of the system.
Whenever convenient, we will use the shorthand
x_{u}^{+}
to denote
f(x,u).

We restrict attention to
stationary deterministic feedback policies

\Pi=\{\pi\;|\;\pi:\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{U}\}.
A non-negative
stage-cost
function
l:\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{U}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is associated to each state-action pair.
Given the initial state
x
, the infinite-horizon
\gamma
-discounted cost for a policy
\pi
is

v_{\pi}(x)=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}l(x_{t},\pi(x_{t})),

(2)

where
x_{0}=x
,
x_{t+1}=f(x_{t},\pi(x_{t}))
for any
t
, and
\gamma\in(0,1]
is a discount factor. We say that the policy
\pi
is
proper
if
v_{\pi}(x)<\infty
for all
x\in\mathbf{X}
. In this case
v_{\pi}
is the unique solution to the Bellman equation
[
6
, Corollary 1.2.2.1]

v_{\pi}(x)=l(x,\pi(x))+\gamma v_{\pi}(f(x,\pi(x)),\qquad\forall x\in\mathbf{X}.

(3)

The aim of the optimal control problem is to find an optimal policy
\pi^{*}
which minimizes the cost (
2
),

v^{*}(x):=v_{\pi^{*}}(x)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}v_{\pi}(x)\qquad\forall x\in\mathbf{X},

(4)

where
v^{*}(x)
is the optimal cost function. Throughout the paper, we work under the following standard assumptions:

Assumption 1

The stage cost function
l(x,u)
is non-negative and inf-compact, the system dynamics
f(x,u)
is continuous, and a proper policy
\pi(x)
exists.

These assumptions ensure that the problem is well-posed, in the sense that
v^{*}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{X})
,
\pi^{*}
is measurable and the infimum in (
4
) is attained
[
34
, Lemma 2.20]
.

The optimal value function
v^{*}
is the unique solution to the
Bellman optimality equation

[
6
, Proposition 1.2.2]

v^{*}(x)=\inf_{u\in\mathbf{U}}\{l(x,u)+\gamma v^{*}(f(x,u))\},\qquad\forall x\in\mathbf{X}.

(5)

If
v^{*}
is known, then an optimal policy can be derived as

\pi^{*}(x)=\argmin_{u\in\mathbf{U}}\left\{l(x,u)+\gamma v^{*}(f(x,u))\right\},\qquad\forall x\in\mathbf{X}.

However, evaluating an optimal policy requires not only the availability of the optimal value function
v^{*}
, but also the dynamics function
f
and the stage cost function
l
.
If
f
or
l
are not known, then
v^{*}
is not sufficient for deriving an optimal policy. In such cases, one can introduce the
q
-function
[
22
]
associated to the policy
\pi
,

q_{\pi}(x,u):=l(x,u)+\gamma v_{\pi}(f(x,u)),\quad\forall(x,u)\in\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{U}.

(6)

This can be interpreted as the cost of playing the input
u
at the initial state
x
and the policy
\pi
thereafter. The
q
-function also satisfies a Bellman equation,

q_{\pi}(x,u)=\underbrace{l(x,u)+\gamma q_{\pi}(f(x,u),\pi(f(x,u)))}_{:=(F_{\pi}q_{\pi})(x,u)},

(7)

\forall(x,u)\in\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{U}
, and the optimal
q
-function
q^{*}
is defined as

q^{*}(x,u):=q_{\pi^{*}}(x,u)=l(x,u)+\gamma v^{*}(f(x,u))\quad

\forall(x,u)\in\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{U}.
Thus
q^{*}
is the unique solution of the
Bellman optimality equation for q-functions
,

q^{*}(x,u):=\underbrace{l(x,u)+\gamma\inf_{w\in\mathbf{U}}q^{*}(f(x,u),w)}_{:=(Fq)(x,u)},

(8)

\forall(x,u)\in\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{U}.
The operators
F_{\pi},F:\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{X})\to\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{X})
are monotone,
\gamma
-contractive with respect to the sup-norm, and have unique fixed points
q_{\pi}
,
q^{*}
, respectively
[
23
]
. The advantage of the
q
-function reformulation is that, once
q^{*}
is known, the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
can be derived by

\pi^{*}(x)=\argmin_{u\in\mathbf{U}}q^{*}(x,u),\qquad\forall x\in\mathbf{X},

without the knowledge of
f
and
l.

II-B

Linear quadratic problem

We consider the
\gamma
-discounted, deterministic, linear quadratic regulator (LQR) problem where the system is governed by
the linear dynamics

x_{t+1}=f(x_{t},u_{t})=Ax_{t}+Bu_{t},

(9)

and it is subject to a quadratic stage cost

l(x,u)=\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix}^{\top}L\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix},\quad L\in\mathbb{S}^{m+n}.

(10)

The matrix
L=\begin{bmatrix}L_{1}&L_{12}\\
L_{12}^{\top}&L_{2}\end{bmatrix},
with
L_{1}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
L_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{m},
is positive semidefinite and
L_{2}\succ 0.

We consider linear policies of the form

\pi(x)=Kx,\quad K\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}.

(11)

The next result follows from standard linear system theory arguments
[
35
]
.

Proposition 1

A linear policy
\pi(x)=Kx
is proper if and only if the spectral radius of the closed-loop matrix
\rho(A+BK)
is smaller than
1/\sqrt{\gamma}.
Hence, Assumption
1
is satisfied if and only if the pair
(\sqrt{\gamma}A,\sqrt{\gamma}B)
is stabilizable.

The fixed point
q_{\pi}
of the Bellman operator
F_{\pi}
is
[
36
]

q_{\pi}(x,u)=\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix}^{\top}Q_{\pi}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix},\quad Q_{\pi}\in\mathbb{S}^{m+n},

(12)

where the matrix
Q_{\pi}
solves the Lyapunov equality

Q_{\pi}=L+\gamma A_{K}^{\top}Q_{\pi}A_{K},

(13)

with

A_{K}=\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
KA&KB\end{bmatrix}.

(14)

Since the spectral radius of
A_{K}
is identical to that of
A+BK

[
37
, Lemma1]
, then from Proposition
1
we conclude that
\sqrt{\gamma}A_{K}
is asymptotically stable
and the Lyapunov equation (
13
) admits a unique positive definite solution. Finally, we point out that also the fixed point of
F
is a quadratic form given by (
12
) with
K=K^{*}
, where
K^{*}
is the optimal control gain
[
36
]
. Moreover, even in the case of affine dynamics, additive noise, or generalized quadratic stage costs, the fixed points of
F_{\pi}
and
F
belong to the space of generalized quadratic functions
[
30
]
. Here, for the sake of simplicity, we focus on LQR problems described by (
9
)-(
11
).

We introduce the LP approach to solve the Bellman Equations (
7
)-(

)
and we analyse the feasible regions of the resulting LPs.

III-A

Exact linear program for
q_{\pi}

The monotonicity and contractivity properties of the operator
F_{\pi}
imply that any
q\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{U})
satisfying

q\leq F_{\pi}q

is a lower bound for
q_{\pi}
.
Maximising among these lower bounds leads to the infinite-dimensional LP

\displaystyle\sup_{q\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{U})}\;\int_{\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{U}}q(x,u)c(dx,du)

(15)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad q(x,u)\leq F_{\pi}q(x,u)\;\;\forall(x,u),

where
c\in\mathcal{M}^{+}
is a finite non-negative measure over
\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{U}
.

Proposition 2

[
26
]

The function
q_{\pi}
coincides for
c
-almost all
(x,u)\in\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{U}
with a maximizer of the LP problem (
15
).

In the LP literature,
c(\cdot)
is typically selected to be a probability measure
[
25
,
12
]
.
For example, if the state-action space is unbounded one can use a Gaussian distribution, or if it is compact a uniform distribution.

Next, we establish some properties of the feasible region

\mathcal{F}_{\pi}:=\left\{q\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{U}):q(x,u)\leq F_{\pi}q(x,u)\;\;\forall(x,u)\right\}.

Proposition 3

The set
\mathcal{C}_{\pi}:=\mathcal{F}_{\pi}-q_{\pi}
is a salient convex cone (see Appendix
B
for the definitions).
Moreover, for any
\bar{q}\in\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
and for any
\lambda\geq 0
,
\bar{q}-\lambda\in\mathcal{C}_{\pi}.

Proof:

Convexity of
\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
easily follows from the fact that the inequality constraint defining
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
is affine, thus convex, in
q.
For the rest of the proof, consider an arbitrary
q\in\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
and let
\bar{q}=q-q_{\pi}\in\mathcal{C}_{\pi}.

To show that
\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
is a cone,
we have to show that
\lambda\bar{q}\in\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
for any
\lambda\geq 0.

Note that

\lambda\bar{q}=(\lambda q+(1-\lambda)q_{\pi})-q_{\pi}

belongs to
\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
if and only if
\lambda q+(1-\lambda)q_{\pi}\in\mathcal{F}_{\pi}.

Since
q\in\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
and
q_{\pi}
satisfies (
7
), we have that

\displaystyle\lambda q(x,u)+(1-\lambda)q_{\pi}(x,u)\leq\lambda\left(l(x,u)+\gamma q(x_{u}^{+},\pi(x_{u}^{+}))\right)

\displaystyle+(1-\lambda)\left(l(x,u)+\gamma q_{\pi}(x_{u}^{+},\pi(x_{u}^{+}))\right)

\displaystyle=l(x,u)+\gamma\left(\lambda q(x_{u}^{+},\pi(x_{u}^{+}))+(1-\lambda)q_{\pi}(x_{u}^{+},\pi(x_{u}^{+}))\right)

for any
(x,u).
We conclude that
\lambda q+(1-\lambda)q_{\pi}\in\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
, hence
\lambda\bar{q}\in\mathcal{C}_{\pi}.

Recall that the cone
\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
is salient if and only if
-\bar{q}\in\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
implies that
\bar{q}
is the identically zero function.
From
q\in\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
and the Bellman equality (
7
), we obtain that

\displaystyle

\displaystyle-q(x,u)+2q_{\pi}(x,u)\geq-l(x,u)-\gamma q(x_{u}^{+},\pi(x_{u}^{+}))

(16)

\displaystyle+2l(x,u)+2\gamma q_{\pi}(x_{u}^{+},\pi(x_{u}^{+}))

\displaystyle=l(x,u)+\gamma\left(-q(x_{u}^{+},\pi(x_{u}^{+}))+2q_{\pi}(x_{u}^{+},\pi(x_{u}^{+}))\right)

for any
(x,u).

If
-\bar{q}=-q+q_{\pi}=(-q+2q_{\pi})-q_{\pi}
belongs to
\mathcal{C}_{\pi},
then
(-q+2q_{\pi})\in\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
, that is

\displaystyle-q(x,u)+2q_{\pi}(x,u)\leq

\displaystyle l(x,u)+\gamma\big(-q(x_{u}^{+},\pi(x_{u}^{+}))

(17)

\displaystyle+2q_{\pi}(x_{u}^{+},\pi(x_{u}^{+}))\big).

Inequalities (
16
) and (
17
) hold if only if

\displaystyle-q(x,u)+2q_{\pi}(x,u)=

\displaystyle l(x,u)+\gamma\big(-q(x_{u}^{+},\pi(x_{u}^{+}))

\displaystyle+2q_{\pi}(x_{u}^{+},\pi(x_{u}^{+}))\big)

for any state-action pair. This means that
-q+2q_{\pi}
is a fixed point of the Bellman policy operator
F_{\pi}.

However, since
q_{\pi}
is the unique fixed point of the operator
F_{\pi},
we must have
-q+2q_{\pi}=q_{\pi}.
Hence
q=q_{\pi}
and
\bar{q}=q-q_{\pi}
is identically zero.
Finally we show that
\bar{q}-\lambda\in\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
for any
\lambda\geq 0.

Indeed,

\displaystyle q(x,u)-\lambda

\displaystyle\leq l(x,u)+\gamma q(x_{u}^{+},\pi(x_{u}^{+}))-\lambda

\displaystyle\leq l(x,u)+\gamma\left(q(x_{u}^{+},\pi(x_{u}^{+}))-\lambda\right)

where the first equality holds because
q\in\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
and the second because
\lambda\geq 0
and

\gamma\in(0,1].

∎Proposition
3
states that
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
is an affine convex cone with apex
q_{\pi}
.
This gives an intuitive explanation of the reason why
q_{\pi}
is a maximizer of the LP (
15
) for all non-negative measures: since non-negative measures always push towards one direction in the non-negative orthant, they force the apex of the cone to be an optimizer.

III-B

Approximate linear program for
q_{\pi}

Computing the q-function by solving the optimization problem (
15
) is in general computationally intractable for the following reasons:

1.

The optimization variable
q(x,u)
lies in an infinite dimensional space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{U});

2.

The number of constraints is infinite since the inequalities need to be satisfied for all
(x,u)\in\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{U}.

To overcome these sources of intractability, the original infinite problem is approximated by a finite one.
This approach is referred to as ADP
[
23
,
25
,
13
]
.

The first approximation involves restricting the q-functions to a finite dimensional linear subspace of
\mathcal{S}
as suggested in
[
11
]
.
Specifically, we assume that the q-functions belong to the span of a finite family of linearly independent functions
\Phi_{i}(x,u)
for
i=1,2\dots,r,

and we parameterize the restricted function space as

\mathcal{S}^{\Phi}(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{U}):=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}\Phi_{i}(x,u)=\Phi(x,u)\alpha\;|\;\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

Here
\alpha=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{1}&\dots&\alpha_{r}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
is the parameters vector and

\Phi(x,u)=\begin{bmatrix}\Phi_{1}(x,u)&\dots&\Phi_{r}(x,u)\end{bmatrix}
is the features (row) vector
evaluated at the point
(x,u).

Substituting
\mathcal{S}
with
\mathcal{S}^{\Phi}
in (
15
) leads to a semi-infinite approximation of the original problem.
Important classes of approximation for q-functions are based on radial basis functions, quadratic functions
and sum-of-squares polynomials
[
20
,
6
,
18
]
.
We note that, while for any positive measure
c
solving the problem (
15
) yields
q_{\pi}
, this is no longer the case for the approximate LP.
When restricting the functions to a subspace, the choice of state-action relevance weights
c
may have a significant impact
on the quality of the resulting approximation. Indeed, the measure
c
can be interpreted as allocating approximation quality over the state-action space
[
12
,
25
]
.

For the infinite number of constraints, if the dynamics

and the cost

are available and have a certain structure, and the features vector

is selected appropriately,
the constraint set can sometimes be represented exactly or relaxed via the

-procedure or sum-of-squares
[

,
18
]
.
However, if we assume that the dynamics is unknown, we cannot approximate the feasible set using these tools.
In this case it is possible to relax the constraints via sampling
[
13
,
17
]
.
If we measure the state
x_{i}\in\mathbf{X}
, apply the input
u_{i}\in\mathbf{U}
, and observe the new state
x_{i}^{+}=f(x_{i},u_{i})
and the incurred cost

we can obtain a set of tuples
D=\{(x_{i},u_{i},x_{i}^{+},l_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
. Note that we use index

instead of

to emphasize that data tuples do not have to be consecutive in time.
If we consider only a finite number of constraints, one for each tuple, we obtain the following data-driven finite dimensional approximation of the LP (

\displaystyle\tilde{J}_{\pi}=\sup_{q\in\mathcal{S}^{\Phi}}\;\int_{\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{U}}q(x,u)c(dx,du)

(18)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad q(x_{i},u_{i})\leq l_{i}+\gamma q(x_{i}^{+},\pi(x_{i}^{+}))\quad i=1,\dots N.

The constraint set

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\pi}:=\{q\in\mathcal{S}^{\Phi}\;|\;q(x_{i},u_{i})\leq F_{\pi}q(x_{i},u_{i}),\;i=1,\dots,N\}

will of course implicitly depend on the unknown dynamics
f
and the cost function
l
, but once the dataset in known,
the explicit dependence is not needed.

Assumption 2

The set
\mathcal{S}^{\Phi}
contains the fixed point
q_{\pi}
of the Bellman equation (
7
).

While for LQR problems this assumption is easy to satisfy by restricting attention to the space of quadratic functions, for most other problems it is difficult to choose the right
\Phi
such that this hypothesis holds.
Nonetheless, since below we focus on the effect of constraint sampling, rather than of q-function parameterization, it is convenient to work under this simplifying assumption.

Assumption 3

The data matrix

M=\begin{bmatrix}\Phi(x_{1},u_{1})-\gamma\Phi(x_{1}^{+},\pi(x_{1}^{+}))\\
\vdots\\
\Phi(x_{N},u_{N})-\gamma\Phi(x_{N}^{+},\pi(x_{N}^{+}))\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times r}

(19)

has full column rank.

Assumption
3
can be thought of as a
persistence of excitation
requirement
[
38
,
39
]
. It implies that
N\geq r
and
r
constraints in Problem (
18
) are linearly independent.
Note that the rank of
M
depends on the choice of the state-input pairs
(x_{i},u_{i}),
but also on the choice of
\Phi.

Proposition 4

The set
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}:=\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\pi}-q_{\pi}
is a polyhedral salient convex cone.
Moreover, for any
\bar{q}\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}
and
\lambda\geq 0
, if
\bar{q}-\lambda\in\mathcal{S}^{\Phi}
then
\bar{q}-\lambda\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}

Proof:

The proof that
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}
is a convex cone follows arguments similar to the ones of Proposition
3
.
To prove that the cone is salient we show that if

\bar{q}\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}
and
-\bar{q}\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\pi},
then
\bar{q}
is the identically zero function.
Indeed, using the same reasoning as in Proposition
3
, we obtain that

\displaystyle-\Phi(x_{i},u_{i})\alpha+2\Phi(x_{i},u_{i})\alpha_{\pi}=

\displaystyle l_{i}-\gamma\Phi(x_{i}^{+},\pi(x_{i}^{+}))\alpha+2\Phi(x_{i}^{+},\pi(x_{i}^{+}))\alpha_{\pi}

for
i=1,\dots,N
.
Equivalently,

M(2\alpha_{\pi}-\alpha)=\begin{bmatrix}l_{1}&\dots&l_{N}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

This is a linear system of equations which has the unique solution
2\alpha_{\pi}-\alpha=\alpha_{\pi}
under the assumption that
M
is full-rank.
We conclude that
\alpha=\alpha_{\pi}
and therefore
\bar{q}=0.

The last statement of the theorem is proved following the same steps of the analogous claim in Proposition
3
.
∎

The effect of the constraint sampling is to construct a polyhedral cone approximation
of the infinitely-generated cone

\{q\in\mathcal{S}^{\Phi}\;|\;q(x,u)\leq F_{\pi}q(x,u)\,,\forall(x,u)\}.

Specifically, each scenario
(x_{i},u_{i},l_{i},x_{i}^{+})
restricts the feasible region to a half-space of the
S^{\Phi}
-space, separated by the hyperplane

\left(\Phi(x_{i},u_{i})-\gamma\Phi(x_{i}^{+},\pi(x_{i}^{+})\right)\alpha-l(x_{i},u_{i})=0,

which passes through
q_{\pi}
and is tangent to the infinitely-generated cone.
Because of this polyhedral approximation,
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\pi}
might be unbounded in the direction of growth of some non-negative measures, as shown in the numerical simulations of Section
V
. The assumption that
\mathcal{S}^{\Phi}
contains
q_{\pi}
is crucial;
otherwise there is no guarantee that there is a function in
\mathcal{S}^{\Phi}
that satisfies all the constraints with equality.

From the geometry of feasible region
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\pi}
, it seems plausible that, under boundedness assumptions,
the optimization problem (
18
) can recover “more often than not” the solution
q_{\pi}
exactly. We can envision three possible outcomes:

1.

\tilde{J}_{\pi}=\infty

2.

\tilde{J}_{\pi}<\infty
and there is a unique optimizer equal to
{q}_{\pi}.

3.

\tilde{J}_{\pi}<\infty
and there is an infinite number of optimizers which lie on the boundary of the feasible set
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\pi}.

In the last two cases, the set of optimizers always includes
{q}_{\pi}.
Indeed, since
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\pi}
is a convex polyedral cone
with unique extreme point
{q}_{\pi},
by the Fundamental Lemma of Linear Programming
[
40
, Theorem 2.7]
,
{q}_{\pi}
is a solution of the LP problem.
If the solution is not unique, then it must lie on one face
of the feasible region, that happens to be orthogonal to the measure
c
(see Appendix
B
for the definition of face).
This third case is degenerate in that, perturbing
c
or the constraints would break the symmetry and lead to one of the previous cases.
Hence, the statement that “more often than not” if the problem is bounded, the optimizer is equal to
{q}_{\pi}.

Problem (
18
) is always feasible, but there is nothing to ensure that
\tilde{J}_{\pi}
is finite.
This is indeed a major challenge, especially for large-scale systems.
Next, we exploit
duality theory
to derive a necessary and sufficient condition for the LP (
18
) to be bounded.

Theorem 1

The optimal solution to Problem (
18
) is finite if and only if there exists a row vector
\lambda=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}&\dots&\lambda_{N}\end{bmatrix},
with
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
for
i=1,\dots,N,
such that

\int_{\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{U}}\Phi(x,u)c(dx,du)=\lambda M.

(20)

Proof:

The dual LP associated to (
18
) is
[
41
, Ch.6]
.

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}l_{i}

(21)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\geq 0,\quad i=1,\dots,N

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{U}}\Phi(x,u)c(dx,du)=\lambda M.

From the
duality theorem
, the primal problem (
18
) is bounded if and only if the dual problem (
21
) is feasible, concluding the proof.
∎

Leveraging on Theorem
1
, we derive a sufficient condition to rule out the degenerate outcome 3) above.

Corollary 1

Let

M_{i}=\Phi(x_{i},u_{i})-\gamma\Phi(x_{i}^{+},u_{i}^{+})
for
i=1,\dots,N,

and assume that

\int_{\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{U}}\Phi(x,u)c(dx,du)\notin\text{span}\{M_{i_{1}},\dots,M_{i_{r-1}}\}

(22)

for any set
\{i_{1},\dots,i_{r-1}\}\subset\{1,2,\dots,N\}.

Then, if Problem (
18
) is bounded,
its optimizer is unique and coincides with the fixed point
q_{\pi}
of the Bellman operator
T_{\pi}.

Proof:

Let
\tilde{q}_{\pi}(x,u)=\Phi(x,u)\tilde{\alpha}_{\pi}
be an optimizer of (
18
). By Theorem
1
, there exists a positive vector
\lambda
such that (
20
) holds. Equations (
20
) and (

) imply that there exists
\{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}\}\subset\{1,2,\dots,N\}
such that
\lambda_{i_{1}},\dots,\lambda_{i_{r}}
are strictly positive with
\{M_{i_{1}},\dots M_{i_{r}}\}
linearly independent.
Then, from the complementary slackness conditions
[
31
, Section 5.5.2]
we have that

\begin{bmatrix}M_{i_{1}}^{\top}&\dots&M_{i_{r}}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\tilde{\alpha}_{\pi}=\begin{bmatrix}l_{i_{1}}&\dots&l_{i_{r}}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

Since
\begin{bmatrix}M_{i_{1}}^{\top}&\dots&M_{i_{r}}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
is full rank and

is such that
\begin{bmatrix}M_{i_{1}}^{\top}&\dots&M_{i_{r}}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\alpha_{\pi}=\begin{bmatrix}l_{i_{1}}&\dots&l_{i_{r}}\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

we conclude that

∎

Theorem

and its Corollary

will be exploited in Section

to formulate a finite LP for

with boundedness guarantees in the LQR setting.

The set

satisfies:

1.

\mathcal{F}^{*}
is a convex set with

2.

Part 1 is straightforward since the constraint in (

VI
Conclusions

The LP approach is a promising method to tackle model-free infinite-dimensional optimal control problems. The main obstacle in its implementation is the difficulty of obtaining bounded solutions for reasonable amount of data. In this work we explore this question, navigating from exact to approximate, from infinite to finite programs, from LPs based on a policy to optimal LPs, and from nonlinear to linear systems. We discuss several fundamental properties on the geometry of the LPs, that culminate with necessary and sufficient condition for their boundedness. Extensive analysis is devoted to the data-driven LQR problem, where we show how to design the gradient in the objective function and construct a dataset to guarantee boundedness and optimality.

A first research direction we envision is to specialize Theorem
1
to a larger class of dynamical systems,
e.g.
, polynomial, and give practical conditions on how to construct the dataset or the direction of growth to guarantee boundedness in the spirit of Algorithms
1
and
2
. Another challenging direction is to extend the present results to stochastic dynamical systems. In that case, one might start from the LP formulations introduced in
[
23
]
and
[
28
]
.

C.1
there exists
\bar{Q}_{1,\min}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
such that
\bar{Q}_{1}\succeq\bar{Q}_{1,\min}.

We conclude that
Q_{1}\succeq Q_{1,\min}
where
Q_{1,\min}:=\tilde{Q}_{1,\min}+\bar{Q}_{1,\min}.

Next, we show that
Q_{12}
is bounded for any
Q\in\mathcal{F}^{*}.

Indeed, from
\text{LMI}(Q)\succeq 0
it follows that

\begin{bmatrix}L_{2}+B^{\top}Q_{1}B-Q_{2}&B^{\top}Q_{12}\\
Q_{12}^{\top}B&Q_{2}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0.

Hence, by using the generalized Schur complement
[
48
, Theorem 1.20]
,

L_{2}+B^{\top}Q_{1}B-Q_{2}-B^{\top}Q_{12}Q_{2}^{\dagger}Q_{12}^{\top}B\succeq 0,

(C.15)

where the symbol
†
denotes the Moore–Penrose inverse.
Moreover, there exists
M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},
such that

B^{\top}Q_{12}=MQ_{2}

[
48
, Theorem 1.19]
.
Substituting into (
C.15
)
we obtain

MQ_{2}M^{\top}\preceq L_{2}+B^{\top}Q_{1}B-Q_{2}\preceq L_{2}+B^{\top}Q_{1,\max}B.

It follows that
\|MQ_{2}^{1/2}\|\leq\|(L_{2}+B^{\top}Q_{1,\max}B)^{1/2}\|,
hence
\|B^{\top}Q_{12}\|\leq\|(L_{2}+B^{\top}Q_{1,\max}B)^{1/2}\|\|Q_{2,\max}^{1/2}\|.

Following the same steps for the principal submatrix of
\text{LMI}(Q)
obtained by removing the columns and the rows corresponding to the second block, we also have that

\|A^{\top}Q_{12}\|
is bounded.
Since the system is reachable
\ker(B^{\top})\cap\ker(A^{\top})=\{0\}.

Hence boundedness of
\|B^{\top}Q_{12}\|
and
\|A^{\top}Q_{12}\|
implies that
\|Q_{12}\|
is itself bounded, concluding the proof.
