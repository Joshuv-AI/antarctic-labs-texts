Effects of classical drivings on the power broadening of atomic lineshapes
L. Hernández-Sánchez, I. A. Bocanegra-Garay, I. Ramos-Prieto, F. Soto-Eguibar, H. M. Moya-Cessa

Abstract
In the framework of the Jaynes-Cummings model, we investigate how atomic lineshapes are affected by coherently driving the atom-field interaction. We pay particular attention to the two-level atom interaction with a thermal cavity field, when both are influenced by external classical fields. Adopting a density matrix formalism, we calculate the average atomic inversion and demonstrate how the corresponding lineshapes vary as a function of the average number of thermal photons, and the atom-field classical coupling. Furthermore, we compare these results with those obtained from the standard Jaynes-Cummings model and validate our findings through numerical calculations.



I
Introduction

Over the past few decades, the Jaynes-Cummings model (JCM) has emerged as a fundamental cornerstone in the development of quantum optics
Jaynes and Cummings 1963
. This model describes the interaction between electromagnetic radiation and matter in its simplest form, considering only the relationship between a single mode of radiation and a two-level atom, which enables a simple and elegant mathematical formulation. Due to this simplicity, the model has been extensively studied from both theoretical and experimental perspectives, providing a solid foundation for understanding fundamental quantum-optical phenomena over the years
Jaynes and Cummings 1963
;
Meschede et al. 1985
;
Filipowicz et al. 1986
;
Shore and Knight 1993
.

However, the field of quantum optics has encountered more involved and realistic scenarios that demand additional considerations. In consequence, generalizations of the standard JCM have been developed
Gerry and Knight 2004
;
Walls and Milburn 2008
;
Klimov and Chumakov 2009
; these generalizations span various areas, including extensions to multiple atomic levels and field modes
Sukumar and Buck 1981
;
Kochetov 1988
;
Bužek 1990
;
Abdalla et al. 1991
, as well as the incorporation of nonlinear effects
Abdalla et al. 1990
;
Rodríguez-Lara et al. 2013
;
de los Santos-Sánchez and Récamier 2011
;
de los Santos-Sánchez et al. 2016
, among others
Phoenix and Knight 1991
;
Moya-Cessa et al. 1995
;
Ramos-Prieto et al. 2014
;
Ramos-Prieto et al. 2020
;
Medina-Dozal et al. 2020
. Furthermore, a wide variety of initial conditions have been explored for both the atomic and field subsystems, encompassing semi-classical and non-classical states, such as: coherent states
Shore and Knight 1993
;
Eberly et al. 1980
;
Gerry and Knight 2004
, superpositions of coherent states
Bužek et al. 1992a
;
Bužek et al. 1992b
;
Moya-Cessa and Vidiella-Barranco 1995
;
Zúniga-Segundo 2003
;
Ahmad and Liu 2009
;
Hernández-Sánchez et al. 2024
, squeezed coherent states
Moya-Cessa and Vidiella-Barranco 1992
;
Alebachew and Fesseha 2007
, as well as diverse combinations of these
Vidiella-Barranco and Moya-Cessa 1995
;
Hernández-Sánchez et al. 2023
.

The standard JCM predicts the emergence of entanglement between the two-level system and the cavity field
Alsing et al. 1992
. Previous research has demonstrated that an appropriate way to analyze this phenomenon is by means of an additional external classical field
Alsing et al. 1992
;
Dutra et al. 1994
;
Bocanegra-Garay et al. 2024a
. This approach led Alsing et al.
Alsing et al. 1992
to conduct a theoretical study of a driven JCM, which was later experimentally verified by Thompson and his team
Thompson et al. 1992
. In the new model an external classical electromagnetic field driving the system is considered. Adding this external field has a significant impact on the dynamics of the system, resulting in a plethora of new quantum phenomena
Alsing et al. 1992
;
Dutra et al. 1994
;
Bocanegra-Garay et al. 2024a
.

On the other hand, spectral lineshapes are pivotal in spectroscopy, furnishing vital insights into the interplay between matter and electromagnetic radiation
Svanberg 1992
. However, these lineshapes are susceptible to various influences, such as nearby non-resonant levels
Moya-Cessa et al. 1991
;
Hernández-Sánchez et al. 2024
;
Hernández-Sánchez et al. 2023
, as evidenced in experimental studies with micromasers
Filipowicz et al. 1986
. Moreover, recent research has underscored the significant impact of atom temperature and trapping effects on these spectral characteristics
Shih and Chapman 2013
;
Bianchet et al. 2022
. Additionally, temperature also plays a critical role; at lower temperatures, thermal asymmetries become less discernible
Rempe et al. 1990
;
Rempe and Walther 1990
, emphasizing the influence of environmental factors on these spectral features.

In this study, we focus on analyzing a driven JCM, which allows for the simultaneous excitation of both the atom and the cavity field mode due to the presence of an external classical field
Bocanegra-Garay et al. 2024a
;
Hernández-Sánchez et al. 2023
. Our main objective is to investigate how the external classical field influences the shapes of atomic lines when the initial state of the quantum field is thermal and the atom is initially in the excited state. Through this research, we aim to highlight the importance of atomic lineshapes in the field of quantum optics and spectroscopy, and their utility as powerful tools for exploring complex quantum systems. Additionally, we aim to provide exact analytical results detailing the effect that an external classical driving field produces on them.

II
The driven Jaynes-Cummings Model

Let us consider a system consisting of a two-level atom, with states denoted as
\ket{g}
(ground state) and
\ket{e}
(excited state), having a transition frequency
\omega_{eg}
. The atom is placed within a cavity formed by perfectly reflecting mirrors sustaining a single quantized electromagnetic field mode with frequency
\omega_{c}
. Additionally, we consider an external classical field with frequency
\omega_{0}
, driving both the atom and the cavity field. This setup is depicted in Fig.
1
.

Figure 1:
Scheme of a lossless cavity formed by perfectly reflecting mirrors. Within the space between the mirrors, a two-level atom with a transition frequency
\omega_{eg}
interacts with a cavity field of frequency
\omega_{c}
. Additionally, both the atom and the cavity field, are influenced by an external classical field with frequency
\omega_{0}
.

The time-dependent Hamiltonian that describes this system can be written as
Alsing et al. 1992
;
Bocanegra-Garay et al. 2024a

\begin{split}\hat{\mathcal{H}}&=\;\frac{\omega_{eg}}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}+\omega_{c}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+g\left(\hat{\sigma}_{+}\hat{a}+\hat{\sigma}_{-}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right)\\
&+\zeta\left(\hat{\sigma}_{-}e^{\ii\omega_{0}t}+\hat{\sigma}_{+}e^{-\ii\omega_{0}t}\right)+\xi\left(\hat{a}e^{\ii\omega_{0}t}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}e^{-\ii\omega_{0}t}\right),\end{split}

(1)

where the real parameters
g
,
\zeta
and
\xi
are the coupling constants between the atom and the cavity field, the external classical field and the atom, and the cavity and classical fields, respectively. Furthermore, throughout this work, we adopt the convention that
\hbar=1
. As is customary, we consider the creation and annihilation operators
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
and
\hat{a}
, which satisfy the usual bosonic commutation relation
[\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}]=1
, to describe the cavity field mode. In turn, for the atomic part of the system, we employ the operators
\hat{\sigma}_{+}=\ket{e}\bra{g}
,
\hat{\sigma}_{-}=\ket{g}\bra{e}
, and
\hat{\sigma}_{z}=\ket{e}\bra{e}-\ket{g}\bra{g}
, which satisfy the commutation relations
[\hat{\sigma}_{+},\hat{\sigma}_{-}]=\hat{\sigma}_{z}
and
[\hat{\sigma}_{z},\hat{\sigma}_{\pm}]=\pm 2\hat{\sigma}_{\pm}
.

The dynamics of the system associated to the Hamiltonian in (
1
) is governed by the Schrödinger equation

\ii\frac{\partial\ket{\psi(t)}}{\partial t}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}\ket{\psi(t)}.

(2)

Through an invariant approach, it has been demonstrated in reference
Bocanegra-Garay et al. 2024a
;
Hernández-Sánchez et al. 2023
that employing the pair of unitary transformations
\hat{T}=\exp\left[\ii\omega_{0}t(\hat{n}+\hat{\sigma}_{z}/2)\right]
and
\hat{D}(\alpha)=\exp\left[\alpha(\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\hat{a})\right]
, where
\hat{n}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}
is the photon-number operator and
\hat{D}(\alpha)
is the displacement operator
Gerry and Knight 2004
;
Shore and Knight 1993
;
Walls and Milburn 2008
with
\alpha=\zeta/g
(see also Ref.
Bocanegra-Garay et al. 2024b
for the implementation of displacement operators in a different context), it is possible to transform the Hamiltonian of the driven system into the Hamiltonian of the standard JCM, namely

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{JC}}=\Delta_{c}\hat{n}+\frac{\Delta_{eg}}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}+g\left(\hat{\sigma}_{+}\hat{a}+\hat{\sigma}_{-}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right),

(3)

where
\Delta_{c}=g\xi/\zeta=\omega_{c}-\omega_{0}
and
\Delta_{eg}=\omega_{eg}-\omega_{0}
represent the detunings between the cavity and classical fields, and the atomic and classical fields, respectively. This straightforwardly leads to the general solution of the Schrödinger equation (
2
) as follows

\ket{\psi(t)}=\hat{T}^{\dagger}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha)\hat{U}_{\mathrm{JC}}(t)\hat{D}(\alpha)\ket{\psi(0)},

(4)

where
\hat{U}_{\mathrm{JC}}(t)
is the evolution operator associated with the standard Jaynes-Cummings Hamiltonian, given by
Bužek et al. 1992a
;
Gerry and Knight 2004
;
Klimov and Chumakov 2009

\hat{U}_{\mathrm{JC}}(t)=e^{-\ii t\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{JC}}}=e^{-\ii\Delta_{c}t(\hat{n}+\hat{\sigma}_{z}/2)}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{U}_{11}(t)&\hat{U}_{12}(t)\\
\hat{U}_{21}(t)&\hat{U}_{22}(t)\end{bmatrix},

(5)

where

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{11}(t)

\displaystyle=\cos\left(\Omega_{\hat{n}+1}t\right)-\ii\frac{\Delta}{2}\frac{\sin\left(\Omega_{\hat{n}+1}t\right)}{\Omega_{\hat{n}+1}},

(6a)

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{12}(t)

\displaystyle=-\ii g\hat{a}\frac{\sin\left(\Omega_{\hat{n}}t\right)}{\Omega_{\hat{n}}},

(6b)

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{21}(t)

\displaystyle=-\ii g\hat{a}^{\dagger}\frac{\sin\left(\Omega_{\hat{n}+1}t\right)}{\Omega_{\hat{n}+1}},

(6c)

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{22}(t)

\displaystyle=\cos\left(\Omega_{\hat{n}}t\right)+\ii\frac{\Delta}{2}\frac{\sin\left(\Omega_{\hat{n}}t\right)}{\Omega_{\hat{n}}},

(6d)

and

\Omega_{\hat{n}}=\sqrt{\frac{\Delta^{2}}{4}+g^{2}\hat{n}},\qquad\Delta=\Delta_{eg}-\Delta_{c}=\omega_{eg}-\omega_{c}.

(7)

It is important to note the constriction
\Delta_{c}=g\xi/\zeta
, meaning that there are only five free parameters out of the initial six parameters in the Hamiltonian of (
1
). From here on, we set
\omega_{0}=\omega_{c}-g\xi/\zeta
.

Once the solution (
4
) to the Schrödinger equation of the total system has been obtained, it is possible to write the corresponding density matrix
\hat{\rho}(t)
as

\begin{split}\hat{\rho}(t)&=\ket{\psi(t)}\bra{\psi(t)}\\
&=\hat{T}^{\dagger}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha)\hat{U}_{\mathrm{JC}}(t)\hat{D}(\alpha)\hat{\rho}(0)\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha)\hat{U}_{\mathrm{JC}}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{D}(\alpha)\hat{T},\end{split}

(8)

where
\hat{\rho}(0)=\hat{\rho}^{F}(0)\otimes\hat{\rho}^{A}(0)
. Here,
\hat{\rho}^{F}(0)
represents the initial density matrix for the field and
\hat{\rho}^{A}(0)
represents the initial density matrix for the atom, respectively.

The versatility of the density matrix makes it a fundamental tool for studying systems, both pure and mixed, with particular relevance in the analysis of composite or entangled systems. Its application allows the calculation of the dynamic variables of the system, requiring only the establishment of initial conditions, for both the atom and the field. From now on we focus on the scenario where the initial field is in a thermal state, a choice supported by the usual presence of a small number of photons in the cavity due to temperature in micromaser experiments
Meschede et al. 1985
. Additionally, we consider that the atom starts in the excited state
\ket{e}
. With these premises, we calculate the atomic inversion and proceed to determine the expression for the time-averaged atomic inversion, commonly known in the literature as
lineshapes
. This approach will allow us to understand how temperature affects the characteristics of atomic lines, when an external classical field drives both the atom and the cavity field.

III
Atomic inversion

From the density matrix in (
8
), we can compute the expected value of any operator
\hat{A}
or observable of the system using the following expression

\braket{\hat{A}}=\text{Tr}\bigl[\hat{\rho}\hat{A}\bigr]=\text{Tr}\bigl[\hat{A}\hat{\rho}\bigr].

(9)

In particular, in this work, we are interested in examining the atomic inversion, defined as the difference between the probability of the atom being in the excited state and the probability of it being in the ground state. This can be calculated as the expected value of the operator
\hat{\sigma}_{z}
, namely

\begin{split}\braket{\hat{\sigma}_{z}}&=\text{Tr}\left[\hat{T}^{\dagger}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha)\hat{U}_{\mathrm{JC}}(t)\hat{D}(\alpha)\hat{\rho}(0)\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha)\hat{U}_{\mathrm{JC}}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{D}(\alpha)\hat{T}\;\hat{\sigma}_{z}\right]\\
&=\text{Tr}\left[\hat{U}_{\mathrm{JC}}^{\dagger}(t)\;\hat{\sigma}_{z}\;\hat{U}_{\mathrm{JC}}(t)\hat{D}(\alpha)\hat{\rho}(0)\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha)\right],\end{split}

(10)

where in the last equality we have employed trace properties, as well as the fact that the operators
\hat{D}(\alpha)
and
\hat{T}
commute with the operator
\hat{\sigma}_{z}
.

Assuming that initially the atom is in an excited state and the field is in a thermal state, the density matrix at
t=0
is given by

\hat{\rho}(0)=\frac{1}{1+\bar{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\bar{n}}{1+\bar{n}}\right)^{k}\ket{k,e}\bra{k,e}.

(11)

Using the matrix representation of the operators and states involved, and meticulously performing the calculations, we obtain that

\begin{split}\braket{\hat{\sigma}_{z}}&=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\bra{m}\left\{\left(\hat{U}_{11}^{\dagger}\hat{U}_{11}-\hat{U}_{21}^{\dagger}\hat{U}_{21}\right)\right.\\
&\times\hat{D}(\alpha)\left[\frac{1}{1+\bar{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\bar{n}}{1+\bar{n}}\right)^{k}\ket{k}\bra{k}\right]\left.\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha)\right\}\ket{m}\\
&=\frac{e^{-|\alpha|^{2}}}{1+\bar{n}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\Omega_{m+1}^{2}}\left[\frac{\Delta^{2}}{4}+g^{2}\left(m+1\right)\cos\left(2\Omega_{m+1}t\right)\right]\\
&\times\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left(\frac{\bar{n}}{1+\bar{n}}\right)^{k}\frac{k!}{m!}\left|\alpha\right|^{2\left(m-k\right)}\left[L_{k}^{\left(m-k\right)}\left(\left|\alpha\right|^{2}\right)\right]^{2}\right.\\
&+\left.\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\bar{n}}{1+\bar{n}}\right)^{k}\frac{m!}{k!}\left|\alpha\right|^{2\left(k-m\right)}\left[L_{m}^{\left(k-m\right)}\left(\left|\alpha\right|^{2}\right)\right]^{2}\right\},\end{split}

(12)

where
\Omega_{m+1}=\sqrt{\frac{\Delta^{2}}{4}+g^{2}(m+1)}
is the corresponding (modified) Rabi frequency and
L_{\nu}^{\left(\mu\right)}(z)
are the associated Laguerre polynomials
Arfken and Weber 2005
.

It is important to note that when we omit the external classical field (
\alpha=0
), the atomic inversion reduces to the more compact expression

\braket{\hat{\sigma}_{z}}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\bar{n}^{m}}{(1+\bar{n})^{m+1}}\frac{1}{\Omega_{m+1}^{2}}\left[\frac{\Delta^{2}}{4}+g^{2}\left(m+1\right)\cos\left(2\Omega_{m+1}t\right)\right],

(13)

which corresponds to the standard JCM when the initial quantum field is a thermal field
Eberly et al. 1980
;
Klimov and Chumakov 2009
;
Hernández-Sánchez et al. 2023
.

In Fig.

, the atomic inversion is shown for different values of the average photon number. For
\bar{n}=0.1
(solid lines) and for
\bar{n}=4.0
(dashed lines), along with parameter values such as
\omega_{c}=0.4
,
\omega_{eg}=0.9
,
g=1.0
,
\zeta=0.7
,
\xi=0.2
, and
\omega_{0}=\omega_{c}-g\xi/\zeta
. In subfigure

(a), the atomic inversion for the standard JCM is depicted, while in subfigure
2
(b), the atomic inversion for the driven JCM is displayed. From these figures, it is noticeable that in the driven JCM, the amplitude of oscillations in the atomic inversion is less pronounced than in the case of the standard JCM, indicating the effect of the external classical field on atomic transitions. Additionally, it can be observed that as the average photon number
\bar{n}
increases, the atomic inversion for both models tends to have the same shape, suggesting that for a large average number of photons (at high temperatures), the action of the classical field on atomic transitions becomes insignificant.

Indeed, the values assigned to parameters such as the coupling strength (
g
), cavity frequency (
\omega_{c}
), and transition frequency (
\omega_{eg}
) directly influence the outcomes of our simulations. The parameter values are carefully calibrated to balance computational tractability with fidelity to the physical system under investigation. By offering insights into the interplay between theoretical modeling and experimental factibility, we aim to enhance the rigor of our analysis.

IV
Average atomic inversion

In this section, we analyze how the average (in time) transition probability between the ground state and the excited state is modified as a function of the detuning. In addition, we show how this depends on both the (average) temperature and the intensity of the driving classical field. To carry out this analysis, we employ the average atomic inversion
\overline{W}(\Delta)

Moya-Cessa et al. 1991
;
Hernández-Sánchez et al. 2023
,

\overline{W}(\Delta)=\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\braket{\hat{\sigma}_{z}}dt.

(14)

Since

\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\cos(2\Omega_{n+1}t)\;dt=0,

(15)

using (
12
), we have

\begin{split}\overline{W}(\Delta)=&\frac{e^{-|\alpha|^{2}}}{1+\bar{n}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\Delta}{2\Omega_{m+1}}\right)^{2}\\
&\times\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left(\frac{\bar{n}}{1+\bar{n}}\right)^{k}\frac{k!}{m!}\left|\alpha\right|^{2\left(m-k\right)}\left[L_{k}^{\left(m-k\right)}\left(\left|\alpha\right|^{2}\right)\right]^{2}\right.\\
&+\left.\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\bar{n}}{1+\bar{n}}\right)^{k}\frac{m!}{k!}\left|\alpha\right|^{2\left(k-m\right)}\left[L_{m}^{\left(k-m\right)}\left(\left|\alpha\right|^{2}\right)\right]^{2}\right\}.\end{split}

(16)

In the absence of an external classical field, the average atomic inversion reduces to

\overline{W}(\Delta)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\bar{n}^{m}}{(1+\bar{n})^{m+1}}\left(\frac{\Delta}{2\Omega_{m+1}}\right)^{2}.

(17)

It is crucial to highlight that the difference between the lineshapes of the standard JCM and the driven JCM becomes more pronounced as the parameter
\alpha

\zeta

Consequently, this demonstrates how the classical driving field influences the transitions between the two levels of the atom. Furthermore, such transitions can be finely manipulated by means of the classical driving field, this last then acting as our control signal.

V
Conclusions

We have explored the effects of an external classical driving field on the atomic lineshapes (specifically, the average atomic inversion) within the framework of the conventional Jaynes-Cummings model. Departing from the density matrix corresponding to the exact solution of the Schrödinger equation for the Hamiltonian describing the driven system, we considered a specific initial condition: an excited atomic state and a thermal field, varied across different average photon number values. Our results highlight the significant influence of the external classical field on the broadening of atomic lineshapes.

Furthermore, we have demonstrated that the external classical field can serve as a control signal for adjusting the width of the atomic lineshapes, even in the presence of a non-zero average temperature (see Fig.
5
). By precisely tuning the external classical driving field, it becomes feasible to finely manipulate the atomic transitions of the two-level system (qubit), which holds importance across various fields of research, including quantum information theory (for instance, see the final comment in Ref.
Brune et al. 1996
), quantum communications (see the Section regarding Cavity QED in Ref.
Kimble 2008
), quantum optics (refer to Larson’s exceptional book
Larson and Mavrogordatos 2022
), spectroscopy, quantum control (see the description of Problem 7.3 in Nielsen’s book
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
), and quantum computing (refer, for instance, to Section 2.6.4 of Haroche and Raimond’s book
Haroche and Raimond 2006
).

Moreover, the consistency between analytical and numerical results underlines the robustness of our study. Additionally, we observe that these findings converge with those of the standard Jaynes-Cummings model when the interaction of the system with the classical field is disregarded. Ultimately, our research opens new perspectives for future investigations into the dynamics of quantum systems under the influence of external classical driving fields.
