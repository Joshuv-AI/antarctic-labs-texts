Compute–Forward Multiple Access (CFMA): Practical Code Design
Erixhen Sula, Jingge Zhu, Adriano Pastore, Sung Hoon Lim, and Michael Gastpar

Abstract
We present a practical strategy that aims to attain rate points on the dominant face of the multiple access channel capacity using a standard low complexity decoder. This technique is built upon recent theoretical developments of Zhu and Gastpar on compute-forward multiple access (CFMA) which achieves the capacity of the multiple access channel using a sequential decoder. We illustrate this strategy with off-the-shelf LDPC codes. In the first stage of decoding, the receiver first recovers a linear combination of the transmitted codewords using the sum-product algorithm (SPA). In the second stage, by using the recovered sum-of-codewords as side information, the receiver recovers one of the two codewords using a modified SPA, ultimately recovering both codewords. The main benefit of recovering the sum-of-codewords instead of the codeword itself is that it allows to attain points on the dominant face of the multiple access channel capacity without the need of rate-splitting or time sharing while maintaining a low complexity in the order of a standard point-to-point decoder. This property is also shown to be crucial for some applications, e.g., interference channels. For all the simulations with single-layer binary codes, our proposed practical strategy is shown to be within 1.7 dB 1.7\text{\,}\mathrm{dB} of the theoretical limits, without explicit optimization on the off-the-self LDPC codes.



I
Introduction

The interference channel
[
1
]
has been considered as a canonical model to understand the design principles of cellular networks with inter-cell interference. For example, the Han-Kobayashi scheme
[
1
,
2
]
has been developed for the interference channel and is currently the best known strategy for the two-user interference channel. The Han-Kobayashi scheme operates by superposition encoding and sending two code components, a private part and a common part. Both components are optimally recovered by using simultaneous joint decoding
[
3
,
4
]
. While the theoretical aspects of this strategy is well understood, how to practically implement it with low complexity remains as an important question. Since recovering the private part can be understood as a simple application of point-to-point codes,
the main challenge is to send the common messages, i.e., each receiver treats the interference channel as a
multiple access channel
and recovers
both
messages (decoding interference). The goal of this work is to design practical low-complexity decoders that can closely attain the promised performance of simultaneous decoding over multiple access channels.

Compared to the multiple access channel, the major difficulty in designing practical strategies for decoding interference in an interference channel is the following. In a multiple access channel, it is well known that sequential decoding (e.g. successive cancellation) and time sharing or rate-splitting can achieve the capacity region of the multiple–access channel
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
. However, this is not the case when we treat the interference channel as a combination of
two
MACs (compound MAC), that is, a channel in which the pair of encoders send messages to both decoders. It was well observed in
[
8
,
9
]
that successive cancellation decoding with time sharing is strictly sub-optimal compared to joint decoding for this case.

Recently, there has been some advances in designing low-complexity sequential decoding strategies that can overcome this issue.
In particular, Wang, Şaşoğlu, and Kim
[
9
]
presented the sliding-window superposition coding (SWSC) strategy that achieves the performance of simultaneous decoding with existing point-to-point codes. A case study using standard LTE codes of the SWSC scheme was given in
[
10
,
11
]
. An important component of this strategy is that they use block Markov encoding which requires multi-block transmissions. In another line of work, Zhu and Gastpar
[
12
]
proposed the compute–forward multiple access (CFMA) strategy based on the compute–and–forward framework proposed in
[
13
]
. It is shown that CFMA achieves the capacity region of the Gaussian MAC with sequential decoding when the SNR of both decoders is greater than
1+\sqrt{2}
, and the strategy is also extended to the interference channel in
[
14
]
. Under this condition, the optimal performance is achieved using single block transmissions. The main component of CFMA is that it utilizes lattice codes to first compute a linear combination of the codewords sent by the transmitters, which is accomplished by extending the compute–and–forward strategy originally proposed by Nazer and Gastpar
[
13
]
. In the next stage, by using the linear combination of the codewords as side information, the decoder recovers any one of the messages
1
1

1

In general the CFMA strategy finds and computes the best two linearly independent combinations.
. By having one linear combination and one of the other messages, the receiver can recover both messages. By appropriately scaling the lattice codes, it is shown in
[
12
]
that the dominant face of the capacity region of the multiple access channel can be attained with sequential decoders without time sharing, and thus, is optimal for the compound MAC setting under the SNR condition. Following the theoretical study of CFMA
[
12
]
which was attained with lattice codes and lattice decoding
[
15
]
, the main goal of our work is to design practical codes and efficient decoding algorithms that can attain the achievable rates of CFMA. To accomplish this goal and for applications to current systems, we will use off-the-shelf
low-density parity check
(LDPC) codes for point-to-point communications as our basic code component. However, we emphasize the fact that this technique can be applied in conjunction with any linear codes, given appropriate modifications. Several works on the design of practical compute–forward strategies have also been considered in
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
. The main difference from the previous works is that we design a practical compute–forward strategy by explicitly using linear codes and the sum-product decoding algorithm.

Our main contribution in this work is summarized as follows:

\bullet

Following the theoretical insights developed in
[
20
]
for compute–and–forward using nested linear codes, we design
nested
LDPC codes based on off-the-shelf LDPC codes for a practical implementation of CFMA.

\bullet

Our decoding algorithm which is designed in a successive manner has complexity in the order of a conventional sum-product decoder. In the first step of the decoding algorithm, we recover a linear combination of LDPC codewords using the sum-product algorithm (SPA). In the next stage, we recover a codeword with another sum-product decoder using the previously recovered linear combination as side information. In order to adapt the standard SPA in our scenario, we need to modify the initial input log-likelihood ratio (LLR) accordingly for each decoding step.

\bullet

To support higher data rates using existing off-the-shelf binary codes, we propose a
multilevel binary
LDPC code construction with higher order modulation. By appropriately modifying the input log likelihood ratio (LLR), we show that the standard SPA can be used in the decoding procedure. In particular, the standard SPA will be applied to every bit in the binary representation of the codeword separately with a modified initial input LLR, and the previously decoded bits will be used as side information for decoding the bits in the next level.

\bullet

We further extend our technique to more complicated scenarios. Specifically, we discuss how to adapt our implementation to complex-valued channel models, to multiple-access channels with more than two users, and to interference channels.

the basic CFMA with binary codes is studied. In section
IV
extension of CFMA with multilevel binary codes is examined in details. In section
V-A
extension to complex channel is treated. Section
VI
provides simulations results for various scenarios.

III
CFMA with binary codes

In this section we devise a practical design of CFMA for a two-user Gaussian MAC, based on off-the-shelf binary linear error-correcting codes. One important feature of the proposed implementation is that while operating near the dominant face of the achievable rate region (other than the two corner points), we keep the decoding algorithm essentially the same as that for a point-to-point system. This low complexity design makes it attractive for practical implementations.

We should also point out that although this paper exclusively considers CFMA with LDPC codes, the same methodology can be applied to any linear channel codes (e.g., convolutional codes) with appropriate modifications.

III-A

Code construction and encoding

Let
(R_{1},R_{2})
be the target rate pair (
R_{1},R_{2}\leq 1
for binary codes) and assume
R_{1}\geq R_{2}
w.l.o.g. In principle, we need to find two good channel codes

Code construction with binary LDPC codes
: Assume
R_{1}\geq R_{2}
, we pick an LDPC code
\mathcal{C}_{2}
of rate

for user

Remark 2

A problem which could potentially rise from this merging process is that, some variable nodes can be left isolated after two check nodes are merged. For example consider a code where the variable node
x_{1}
is only connected to two check nodes as

\displaystyle x_{1}\oplus x_{2}\oplus x_{3}=0,\quad x_{1}\oplus x_{4}\oplus x_{5}=0.

After merging these two check nodes, a new check node is formed to give the constraint
x_{2}\oplus x_{3}\oplus x_{4}\oplus x_{5}=0
and the variable node
x_{1}
is left isolated because it was not connected to any other check node. This is a situation we want to avoid in merging check nodes. A sufficient condition is that we will only merge two check nodes, if they have disjoint neighbors. This condition is not as stringent as it seems for LDPC codes, and is satisfied for most check nodes due to the sparsity of the code. Also notice that if we merge check nodes using a LDPC code where all check nodes have odd degree, this issue will not arise.

III-B

Decoding algorithm

Now we derive the decoding algorithm for the CFMA scheme using binary LDPC codes, and show that the same sum-product algorithm for the point-to-point LDPC decoding can be directly applied to our scheme with only a slight modification to the initialization step. We define
\bm{s}=\bm{u}_{1}\oplus\bm{u}_{2}
, and derive the algorithm for decoding the pair
(\bm{s},\bm{u}_{1})
. The decoding procedure for the pair
(\bm{s},\bm{u}_{2})
is similar.

The decoder uses a bit-wise maximum
a posteriori
(MAP) estimation for decoding, i.e.

\bullet

Decode
\bm{s}
:

{\hat{s}}_{i}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\limits_{s_{i}}p(s_{i}|\bm{y})

\bullet

Decode
\bm{u}_{1}
:

{\hat{u}}_{1,i}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\limits_{u_{1,i}}p(u_{1,i}|\bm{y},\bm{s})

In the following derivation we use
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
to denote the codes with the larger rate among
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}
and
\tilde{\bm{H}}
to denote its parity check matrix. Ideally, we target a bit-wise maximum
a posteriori
(MAP) estimation, i.e.,
\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{s_{i}\in\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\}}p(s_{i}|\bm{y})
. However, since
p(\bm{y}|\bm{s})
is not memoryless, the sum-product algorithm does not directly approximate the bit-wise MAP in this case. Nonetheless, as an approximation to the bit-wise MAP rule, we perform a bit-wise MAP estimation as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{s}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{s_{i}\in\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\}}\sum_{\sim s_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(y_{i}|s_{i})\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt\tilde{\bm{H}}\bm{s}=\bm{0}\bigr\},

(5)

where the summation is over all coordinates of
\bm{s}
except
s_{i}
. We also use the fact that
\bm{s}=\bm{u}_{1}\oplus\bm{u}_{2}
is uniformly distributed over
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
as a consequence of
\bm{u}_{1}
and
\bm{u}_{2}
being uniform over the nested codebooks
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, respectively, hence

\displaystyle p(\bm{s})=\frac{\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt\tilde{\bm{H}}\bm{s}=\bm{0}\bigr\}}{|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|}.

As shown in
[
22
]
, the formulation in (
5
) has complexity in the order of standard sum-product algorithm for the bit-wise MAP estimation of the sum codewords
\bm{s}
. Also notice that the complexity of this algorithm is the same as for a point-to-point system where the receiver decodes one codeword from the code described by
\tilde{\bm{H}}
.

Similarly,
given the sum codeword

\bm{s}=\bm{u}_{1}\oplus\bm{u}_{2}\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
, we can rewrite the second decoding step as

\displaystyle\hat{u}_{1,i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{u_{1,i}\in\left\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\right\}}p(u_{1,i}|\bm{y},\bm{s})=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{u_{1,i}\in\left\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\right\}}\sum_{\sim u_{1,i}}p(\bm{y}|\bm{u}_{1},\bm{s})p(\bm{u}_{1},\bm{s})

(6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{u_{1,i}\in\left\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\right\}}\sum_{\sim u_{1,i}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(y_{i}|u_{1,i},s_{i})\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt\bm{H}_{1}\bm{u}_{1}=\bm{0}\bigr\}.

For the last equality we have used the fact that the channel is memoryless, as well as the fact that
(\bm{u}_{1},\bm{s})
is uniform over
\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
, hence

\displaystyle p(\bm{u}_{1},\bm{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt\bm{u}_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\bigr\}\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt\bm{s}\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}\bigr\}}{|\mathcal{C}_{1}|\,|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|},

(7)

where we recall that
\bm{s}
is the decoded codeword from
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
hence it always holds that
\bm{s}\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
, namely
\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt\bm{s}\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}\bigr\}=1
. Furthermore
\bm{u}_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
is equivalent to
\bm{H}_{1}\bm{u}_{1}=\bm{0}
.

It is important to realize that Eq. (
6
) also takes the same form as Eq. (
5
), hence allow us to carry out the optimization efficiently using the same sum-product algorithm. More precisely, both decoding steps in (
5
) and (
6
) can be implemented using the standard sum-product algorithm used in the LDPC decoder for a point-to-point system, with a modified initial log-likelihood ratio (LLR) values (derivations in Appendix
-A
):

\displaystyle\mathrm{LLR}_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\log\frac{p(y_{i}|s_{i}=0)}{p(y_{i}|s_{i}=1)}=\log\cosh\bigl(y_{i}2\sqrt{P}\bigr)-2P

(8a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{LLR}_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\log\frac{p(y_{i}|u_{1,i}=0,s_{i})}{p(y_{i}|u_{1,i}=1,s_{i})}=\begin{cases}4y_{i}\sqrt{P}&\text{for $s_{i}=0$}\\
0&\text{for $s_{i}=1$}\end{cases}

(8b)

IV-A

Code construction and encoding

Let
L
denote the number of code levels. User 1 employs a collection
(\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(L)})
of binary
(k_{1}^{(\ell)},n)
-codes,
\ell=1,\dotsc,L
, such that
k_{1}^{(1)}+\dotso+k_{1}^{(L)}=k_{1}
. Similarly, user 2 employs a collection
(\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(L)})
of binary
(k_{2}^{(\ell)},n)
-codes,
\ell=1,\ldots,L
, such that
k_{2}^{(1)}+\dotso+k_{2}^{(L)}=k_{2}
. Moreover, we require that the codebooks of level
\ell\in\{\kern 0.5pt1,\ldots,L\}
be nested between users. For simplicity we assume that
\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(\ell)}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(\ell)}
for all
\ell=1,\ldots,L
. These nested codebooks may be constructed using the merging method described in Section
III-A
.

Encoding and modulation
: For user
1
, we split the message
w_{1}
, of length
k_{1}
, into
L
submessages
w_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,w_{1}^{(L)}
, of respective lengths
k_{1}^{(\ell)},\ \ell=1,\dotsc,L
. Each submessage
w_{1}^{(\ell)}
is then encoded independently by the corresponding
(k_{1}^{(\ell)},n)
-binary code into a length-
n
subcodeword
\bm{u}_{1}^{(\ell)}\in\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\}^{n}
,
\ell=1,\ldots,L
.
We proceed likewise for user
2
.
Hence, the rates of both users are respectively

R_{1}=\frac{k_{1}}{n}=\frac{\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}k_{1}^{(\ell)}}{n}

and

R_{2}=\frac{k_{2}}{n}=\frac{\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}k_{2}^{(\ell)}}{n}.

The bijective modulation mapping

\varphi_{1}\colon\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\}^{L}\to\mathcal{X}

is a symbol-by-symbol mapping expressible as the composition of two functions: first, for every
i=1,\dotsc,n
, a
2^{L}
-ary codeword symbol

u_{1,i}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}2^{\ell-1}u_{1,i}^{(\ell)}

(9)

is formed from the binary subcodeword symbols; secondly, the
2^{L}
-ary symbol is mapped one-to-one to a signal-space symbol
x_{1,i}\in\mathcal{X}
. For simplicity, we will choose the latter to be affine-linear. All in all, the signal-space codeword
\bm{x}_{1}
can thus be represented as

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}\bigl(\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)},\dotsc,\bm{u}_{1}^{(L)}\bigr)=\sqrt{\frac{3P}{2^{2L}-1}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}2^{\ell-1}\bigl(2u_{1}^{(\ell)}-1\bigr)

(10)

where it is understood that
\varphi_{1}
is applied symbolwise. The factor to the left of the summation symbol in (
10
) ensures that the average power constraint is met. The mapping
\varphi_{2}
is defined similarly.

IV-B

Decoding algorithm

We will view codewords
\bm{u}_{1}
and
\bm{u}_{2}
constructed as in (
9
) as vectors in
\mathbb{Z}_{2^{L}}
, and first decode the sum codeword
\bm{s}
defined as

\displaystyle\bm{s}\coloneqq[\bm{u}_{1}+\bm{u}_{2}]\mod 2^{L}

(11)

where the sum is performed element-wise between
\bm{u}_{1}
and
\bm{u}_{2}
. Importantly, we will show that the same sum-product algorithm for the point-to-point LDPC decoding can be applied to the proposed scheme as well.

Since
\bm{s}
is an element in
\mathbb{Z}_{2^{L}}
, decoding
\bm{s}
directly would require an algorithm which can handle symbols in
\mathbb{Z}_{2^{L}}
. As we wish to reuse the existing sum-product algorithm for binary codes, we will first represent the sum
\bm{s}
in its binary form. That is, each entry
s_{i}
is written as

s_{i}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}2^{\ell-1}s_{i}^{(\ell)}

for some
s_{i}^{\ell}\in\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\}
, for all
i=1,\ldots,n
.

The following lemma relates the binary representation of
\bm{s}
with
\bm{u}_{1},\bm{u}_{2}
.

Lemma 1

Let
\bm{u}_{1},\bm{u}_{2}
be two
n
-length strings in
\mathbb{Z}_{2^{L}}^{n}
constructed as in (
9
) using
\bm{u}_{1}^{(\ell)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(\ell)}\text{ for }\ell=1,\ldots,L
. Let
\bm{s}
take the form as in (
11
). Then we have the following relationships:

1.

\bm{s}^{(1)}=\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)}\oplus_{2}\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)}

2.

For
2\leq\ell\leq L
and for each
i=1,\ldots,n
define the partial sum

s_{i}^{\ell_{+}}:=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}2^{j-1}(u_{1,i}^{(j)}+u_{2,i}^{(j)}).

Then,

\bullet

if
s_{i}^{\ell_{+}}<2^{\ell-1}
, we have
s_{i}^{(\ell)}=u_{1,i}^{(\ell)}\oplus_{2}u_{2,i}^{(\ell)}
;

\bullet

if
s_{i}^{\ell_{+}}\geq 2^{\ell-1}
, we have
s_{i}^{(\ell)}=u_{1,i}^{(\ell)}\oplus_{2}u_{2,i}^{(\ell)}\oplus_{2}1
.

Proof:

To prove 1), notice that for any
i=1,\ldots n
, if
(u_{1,i}^{(1)},u_{2,i}^{(1)})
equals to
(1,1)
or
(0,0)
, then
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}2^{\ell-1}u^{(\ell)}_{1,i}
and
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}2^{\ell-1}u^{(\ell)}_{2,i}
are either both odd numbers or both even numbers, for
i=1,\ldots,n
. As a result
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}2^{\ell-1}s^{(\ell)}_{i}
is an even number hence
s_{i}^{(1)}=u_{1,i}^{(1)}\oplus_{2}u_{2,i}^{(1)}=0
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
. On the other hand if we have
u_{1,i}^{(1)}+u_{2,i}^{(1)}=1
, then
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}2^{\ell-1}s^{(\ell)}_{i}
is an odd number hence
s_{i}^{(1)}=1
.

Now we consider
\bm{s}_{i}^{(\ell)}
for
\ell\geq 2
, in which case the relationship between
s_{i}^{(\ell)}
and
(u_{1,i}^{(\ell)},u_{2,i}^{(\ell)})
is more complicated because of the carry over from the lower digits. First notice that we have

\displaystyle s_{i}=

\displaystyle 2^{L-1}(u_{1,i}^{(L)}+u_{2,i}^{(L)})+\cdots+2(u_{1,i}^{(2)}+u_{2,i}^{(2)})+(u_{1,i}^{(1)}+u_{2,i}^{(1)})+d_{i}\cdot 2^{L}

where
d_{i}
equals either
0
or
-1
. Meanwhile we also have
s_{i}=2^{L-1}s_{i}^{(L)}+\ldots+2s_{i}^{(2)}+s_{i}^{(1)}.

For a given
\ell\geq 2
, it is easy to see that if the partial sum
s_{i}^{\ell_{+}}
satisfies
s_{i}^{\ell_{+}}<2^{\ell-1}
, then we have

s_{i}^{\ell_{+}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}2^{j-1}s_{i}^{(j)}.

In this case
s_{i}^{(\ell)}
will not be affected by the carry over from the lower digits
s_{i}^{(1)},\ldots,s_{i}^{(\ell-1)}
and is solely determined by
u_{1,i}^{(\ell)},u_{2,i}^{(\ell)}
. Particularly, it is straightforwardly to check that
s_{i}^{(\ell)}=0
if
(u_{1,i}^{(\ell)},u_{2,i}^{(\ell)})
equals
(0,0)
or
(1,1)
(in the latter case there is a carry over to
\bm{s}_{i}^{(\ell+1)}
), and
s_{i}^{(\ell)}=1
if
(u_{1,i}^{(\ell)},u_{2,i}^{(\ell)})
equals
(0,1)
or
(1,0)
. Namely we have
s_{i}^{(\ell)}=u_{1,i}^{(\ell)}\oplus_{2}u_{2,i}^{(\ell)}
in this case.

If the partial sum
s_{i}^{\ell_{+}}
satisfies
s_{i}^{\ell_{+}}\geq 2^{\ell-1}
, then we have

s_{i}^{\ell_{+}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}2^{j-1}s_{i}^{(j)}+2^{\ell-1}.

In this case
s_{i}^{(\ell)}
is determined by
u_{1,i}^{(\ell)},u_{2,i}^{(\ell)}
as well as the carry over from the lower digits. It is also straightforward to check that in this case we have
s_{i}^{(\ell)}=0
if
(u_{1,i}^{(\ell)},u_{2,i}^{(\ell)})
equals
(0,1)
or
(1,0)
,
s_{i}^{(\ell)}=1
if
(u_{1,i}^{(\ell)},u_{2,i}^{(\ell)})
equals
(0,0)
, and
s_{i}^{(\ell)}=1
if
(u_{1,i}^{(\ell)},u_{2,i}^{(\ell)})
equals
(1,1)
because of the carry over. Namely we have
s_{i}^{(\ell)}=u_{1,i}^{(\ell)}\oplus_{2}u_{2,i}^{(\ell)}\oplus_{2}1
in this case.
∎

The above lemma helps us design the appropriate decoding algorithm of this construction using existing sum-product algorithm. Briefly, our decoding algorithm will decode the codewords
(\bm{s},\bm{u}_{1})
in consecutive steps, in particular the decoder will output the codewords in the order:
\bm{s}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)},\bm{s}^{(2)},\bm{u}_{1}^{(2)},\ldots,\bm{s}^{(L)},\bm{u}_{1}^{(L)}
.

Recall that the codes
\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(\ell)},\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(\ell)}
are nested for all
\ell=1,\ldots,L
. Let
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(\ell)}
denote one of the two codes with the larger rate, and
\tilde{\bm{H}}^{(\ell)}
denote its corresponding parity check matrix. We start our derivation for decoding the bit-string
\bm{s}^{(1)}
, i.e., the string of the least significant bits in the binary representation of the sum
\bm{s}
. Similar to the single-layer case in the previous section, we perform the following estimation as an approximation to the bit-wise MAP rule for
i=1,\ldots,n
:

\displaystyle{\hat{s}}_{i}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{s_{i}^{(1)}\in\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\}}\sum_{\sim s_{i}^{(1)}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(y_{i}|s_{i}^{(1)})p(\bm{s}^{(1)})

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{s_{i}^{(1)}\in\left\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\right\}}\sum_{\sim s_{i}^{(1)}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(y_{i}|s_{i}^{(1)})\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt\tilde{\bm{H}}^{(1)}\bm{s}^{(1)}=\bm{0}\bigr\}

(12)

where step
(a)
follows from Lemma
1
and that we have
\bm{s}^{(1)}=\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)}\oplus\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)}
. This ensures that
\bm{s}^{(1)}
is a codeword from
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(1)}
, hence

p(\bm{s}^{(1)})=\frac{\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt\tilde{\bm{H}}^{(1)}\bm{s}^{(1)}=\bm{0}\bigr\}}{|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(1)}|}.

As for the input for the sum-product algorithm, the initial LLR value is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{LLR}_{1}^{(1)}=\log\frac{p(y_{i}|s_{i}^{(1)}=0)}{p(y_{i}|s_{i}^{(1)}=1)}

(13)

where the explicit expression is given in Appendix
-B
.

With the above expression, the bit string
\bm{s}^{(1)}
can readily be decoded using the sum-product algorithm for binary codes. Next we decode
\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)}
(or
\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)}
) using channel output
\bm{y}
and the decoded bit string
\bm{s}^{(1)}
. For
i=1,\ldots,n

\displaystyle{\hat{u}}_{1,i}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{u_{1,i}^{(1)}\in\left\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\right\}}p(u_{1,i}^{(1)}|\bm{y},\bm{s}^{(1)})

\displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{u_{1,i}^{(1)}\in\left\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\right\}}\sum_{\sim u_{1,i}^{(1)}}p(\bm{y}|\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)},\bm{s}^{(1)})p(\bm{s}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)})

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{u_{1,i}^{(1)}\in\left\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\right\}}\sum_{\sim u_{1,i}^{(1)}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(y_{i}|s_{i}^{(1)},u_{1,i}^{(1)})\mathbbm{1}{\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt{\bm{H}_{1}^{(1)}}\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)}=\bm{0}\bigr\}}

(14)

whereas
(b)
follows from the similar argument as in (
7
), namely

p(\bm{s}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)})=\frac{\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt\bm{s}^{(1)}\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(1)}\bigr\}\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(1)}\bigr\}}{|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(1)}||\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(1)}|}.

Note that
\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt\bm{s}^{(1)}\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(1)}\bigr\}=1
in this case because
\bm{s}^{(1)}
is the decoded codeword in
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(1)}
. Furthermore
\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(1)}
is equivalent to
\bm{H}_{1}^{(1)}\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)}=\bm{0}
. Also notice that at this point we have
\bm{s}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)}
, and thus, we can reconstruct
\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)}=\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)}\oplus_{2}\bm{s}^{(1)}
from Lemma
1
.

The initial LLR value for the sum-product algorithm is given by

\mathrm{LLR}_{2}^{(1)}=\log\frac{p(y_{i}|s_{i}^{(1)},u_{1,i}^{(1)}=0)}{p(y_{i}|s_{i}^{(1)},u_{1,i}^{(1)}=1)}

with the exact expression given by (
31
) in Appendix
-B
. Now we start to decode the
2
-nd level codes
\bm{u}_{1}^{(2)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(2)}
, given the decoded information
\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)}
and the channel output
\bm{y}
. To first decode the bit string
\bm{s}^{(2)}
from the sum
\bm{s}
, we use the same MAP approximation for the bit-wise estimation

\displaystyle{\hat{s}}_{i}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{s_{i}^{(2)}\in\left\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\right\}}p(s_{i}^{(2)}|\bm{y},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)})

\displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{s_{i}^{(2)}\in\left\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\right\}}\sum_{\sim s_{i}^{(2)}}p(\bm{y}|\bm{s}^{(2)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)})p(\bm{s}^{(2)}|\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)})p(\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)})

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{=}\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{s_{i}^{(2)}\in\left\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\right\}}\sum_{\sim s_{i}^{(2)}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(y_{i}|s_{i}^{(2)},u_{2,i}^{(1)},u_{1,i}^{(1)})\cdot\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt\tilde{\bm{H}}^{(2)}(\bm{s}^{(2)}\oplus_{2}\bm{c}^{(2)})=\bm{0}\bigr\}

(15)

where
(c)
follows from the following argument. First observe that
(\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)})
is uniform over
\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(1)}\times\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(1)}
, hence

p(\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)})=\frac{\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(1)}\bigr\}\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(1)}\bigr\}}{|\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(1)}|\,|\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(1)}|}

where
\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)}
are guaranteed to be codewords as they are decoded from the previous step. Notice that we already have
\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)}
hence can construct another
n
-length binary vector
\bm{c}^{(2)}
defined as follows

\displaystyle c_{i}^{(2)}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }u_{1,i}^{(1)}+u_{2,i}^{(1)}<2\\
1,&\text{if }u_{1,i}^{(1)}+u_{2,i}^{(1)}=2\end{cases}

for all
i=1,\ldots,n
. The value
c_{i}^{(2)}
in this vector indicates if there is carry over for
s_{i}^{(2)}
from the lower digit. Using the result in Lemma
1
, we have

\displaystyle\bm{s}^{(2)}=\bm{u}_{1}^{(2)}\oplus_{2}\bm{u}_{2}^{(2)}\oplus_{2}\bm{c}^{(2)}

(16)

As
\bm{c}^{(2)}
is completely determined by
\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)}
, the term
p(\bm{s}^{(2)}|\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)})
can be further developed as

\displaystyle p(\bm{s}^{(2)}|\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)})

\displaystyle=p({\bm{s}^{(2)}|\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)},\bm{c}^{(2)}})

\displaystyle\overset{(d)}{=}p(S^{(2)}=\bm{s}^{(2)}|C^{(2)}=\bm{c}^{(2)})

\displaystyle\overset{(e)}{=}p(U_{1}^{(2)}\oplus_{2}U_{2}^{(2)}=\bm{s}^{(2)}\oplus_{2}\bm{c}^{(2)}|C^{(2)}=\bm{c}^{(2)})

\displaystyle\overset{(f)}{=}p(U_{1}^{(2)}\oplus_{2}U_{2}^{(2)}=\bm{s}^{(2)}\oplus_{2}\bm{c}^{(2)})

\displaystyle\overset{(g)}{=}\frac{1}{|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}|}\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt\tilde{\bm{H}}^{(2)}(\bm{s}^{(2)}\oplus_{2}\bm{c}^{(2)})=\bm{0}\bigr\}

(17)

where
(d)
follows since
\bm{s}^{(2)}
and
(\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)})
are conditionally independent given
\bm{c}^{(2)}
. Step
(e)
follows because of (
16
) and step
(f)
follows because
\bm{c}^{(2)}
is a function of
\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)}
which are independent from
\bm{u}_{1}^{(2)}
and
\bm{u}_{2}^{(2)}
. The last step holds because
\bm{u}_{1}^{(2)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(2)}
are from nested codebooks
\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(2)}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(2)}
. This completes the argument for step
(c)
.
Also notice that the expression (
15
) already admits a sum-product algorithm for decoding the sequence
\bm{s}^{(2)}
.
Furthermore, the corresponding initial LLR value is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{LLR}_{1}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\log\frac{p(y_{i}|u_{1,i}^{(1)},u_{2,i}^{(1)},s_{i}^{(2)}\oplus c_{i}^{(2)}=0)}{p(y_{i}|u_{1,i}^{(1)},u_{2,i}^{(1)},s_{i}^{(2)}\oplus c_{i}^{(2)}=1)}

(18)

with an explicit expression given by (
-B
) Appendix
-B
. The next step is to decode
\bm{u}_{1}^{(2)}
:

\displaystyle{\hat{u}}_{1,i}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{u_{1,i}^{(2)}\in\left\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\right\}}p(u_{1,i}^{(2)}|\bm{y},\bm{s}^{(2)},\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)})

\displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{u_{1,i}^{(2)}\in\left\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\right\}}\sum_{\sim u_{1,i}^{(2)}}p(\bm{y}|\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)},\bm{s}^{(2)},\bm{u}_{1}^{(2)})\cdot p(\bm{s}^{(2)},\bm{u}_{1}^{(2)},\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)})

\displaystyle\overset{(h)}{=}\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{u_{1,i}^{(2)}\in\left\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\right\}}\sum_{\sim u_{1,i}^{(2)}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(y_{i}|u_{1,i}^{(1)},u_{2,i}^{(1)},s_{i}^{(2)},u_{1,i}^{(2)})\cdot\mathbbm{1}{\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt{\bm{H}_{1}^{(2)}}\bm{u}_{1}^{(2)}=\bm{0}\bigr\}}

(19)

where the equality
(h)
follows from a similar argument as in (
14
). In particular, we can show that

is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt\bm{s}^{(2)}\oplus\bm{c}^{(2)}\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}\bigr\}\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt\bm{u}_{1}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(2)}\bigr\}\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(1)}\bigr\}\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(1)}\bigr\}}{|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}|\,|\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(2)}|\,|\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(1)}|\,|\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(1)}|}

where
\bm{s}^{(2)}\oplus\bm{c}^{(2)}
,
\bm{u}_{1}^{(1)}
and
\bm{u}_{2}^{(1)}
are guaranteed to be codewords as they are decoded from the previous steps. Furthermore, the corresponding initial LLR value is given by

\mathrm{LLR}_{2}^{(2)}=\log\frac{p(y_{i}|u_{1,i}^{(1)},u_{2,i}^{(1)},s_{i}^{(2)},u_{1,i}^{(2)}=0)}{p(y_{i}|u_{1,i}^{(1)},u_{2,i}^{(1)},s_{i}^{(2)},u_{1,i}^{(2)}=1)}

with the explicit expression given by (

) in Appendix

.

The consecutive decoding procedure should be clear from the foregoing two steps. In general, for any
\ell\geq 2
, we decode the bit string
\bm{s}^{(\ell)}
in the sum
\bm{s}
, and one of the codewords, say
\bm{u}_{1}^{(\ell)}
. To simplify the notation we use
\bm{u}_{k}^{(1:\ell)}

Based on the decoded bit strings
\bm{u}_{1}^{(1:\ell)}
and
\bm{u}_{2}^{(1:\ell)}
, we have

\displaystyle{\hat{s}}_{i}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{s_{i}^{(\ell)}\in\left\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\right\}}p(s_{i}^{(\ell)}|\bm{y},\bm{u}_{1}^{(1:\ell-1)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1:\ell-1)})

\displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{s_{i}^{(\ell)}\in\left\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\right\}}\sum_{\sim s_{i}^{(\ell)}}p(\bm{y}|\bm{s}^{(\ell)},\bm{u}_{1}^{(1:\ell-1)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1:\ell-1)})\cdot p(\bm{s}^{(\ell)}|\bm{u}_{1}^{(1:\ell-1)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1:\ell-1)})\cdot p(\bm{u}_{1}^{(1:\ell-1)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1:\ell-1)})

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{=}\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{s_{i}^{(\ell)}\in\left\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\right\}}\sum_{\sim s_{i}^{(\ell)}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(y_{i}|s_{i}^{(\ell)},u_{1,i}^{(1:\ell-1)},u_{2,i}^{(1:\ell-1)})\cdot\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt{\tilde{\bm{H}}^{(\ell)}(\bm{s}^{(\ell)}\oplus_{2}\bm{c}^{(\ell)})=\bm{0}}\bigr\}

(20)

\bm{u}_{1}^{(1:\ell-1)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1:\ell-1)}
are guaranteed to be codewords as they are decoded from the previous steps. Furthermore, with decoded
\bm{u}_{1}^{(1:\ell-1)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1:\ell-1)}
, we define
\bm{c}^{(\ell)}
as

\displaystyle c_{i}^{(\ell)}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}2^{j-1}(u_{1,i}^{(j)}+u_{2,i}^{(j)})<2^{\ell-1}\\
1,&\text{if }\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}2^{j-1}(u_{1,i}^{(j)}+u_{2,i}^{(j)})\geq 2^{\ell-1}\end{cases}

and observe that

\bm{s}^{(\ell)}=\bm{u}_{1}^{(\ell)}\oplus_{2}\bm{u}_{2}^{(\ell)}\oplus_{2}\bm{c}^{(\ell)}

according to Lemma
1
. Using a similar argument as in (
17
), we have

\displaystyle p(\bm{s}^{(\ell)}|\bm{u}_{1}^{(1:\ell-1)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1:\ell-1)})=\frac{1}{|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(\ell)}|}\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt{\tilde{\bm{H}}^{(\ell)}(\bm{s}^{(\ell)}\oplus\bm{c}^{(\ell)})=\bm{0}}\bigr\}

(21)

where we have used the same arguments as in
(d)
-
(g)
. By substituting back to

we have a complete explanation on decoding the bit string
\bm{s}^{(\ell)}
with the initial LLR value for sum-product algorithm given by

\mathrm{LLR}_{1}^{(\ell)}=\log\frac{p(y_{i}|u_{1,i}^{(1:\ell-1)},u_{2,i}^{(1:\ell-1)},s_{i}^{(\ell)}\oplus c_{i}^{(\ell)}=0)}{p(y_{i}|u_{1,i}^{(1:\ell-1)},u_{2,i}^{(1:\ell-1)},s_{i}^{(\ell)}\oplus c_{i}^{(\ell)}=1)}.

Similarly we decode, say
\bm{u}_{1}^{(\ell)}
, as follows

\displaystyle{\hat{u}}_{1,i}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{u_{1,i}^{(\ell)}\in\left\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\right\}}p(u_{1,i}^{(\ell)}|\bm{y},\bm{u}_{1}^{(1:\ell-1)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1:\ell-1)},\bm{s}^{(\ell)})

\displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{u_{1,i}^{(\ell)}\in\left\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\right\}}\sum_{\sim u_{1,i}^{(\ell)}}p(\bm{y}|\bm{s}^{(\ell)},\bm{u}_{1}^{(1:\ell)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1:\ell-1)})\cdot p(\bm{s}^{(\ell)},\bm{u}_{1}^{(1:\ell)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(1:\ell-1)})

\displaystyle\overset{(j)}{=}\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{u_{1,i}^{(\ell)}\in\left\{\kern 0.5pt0,1\right\}}\sum_{\sim u_{1,i}^{(\ell)}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(y_{i}|s_{i}^{(\ell)},u_{1,i}^{(1:\ell)},u_{2,i}^{(1:\ell-1)})\cdot\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\kern 0.5pt{{\bm{H}_{1}^{(\ell)}}\bm{u}_{1}^{(\ell)}=\bm{0}}\bigr\}

(22)

where
(j)
follows from similar argument as in (

) together with the fact that
\bm{s}^{(\ell)}\oplus\bm{c}^{(\ell)}
,
\bm{u}_{1}^{(1:\ell-1)}
and

The explicit expressions of

is given in the following proposition.

Proposition 1
(LLR for CFMA with multilevel binary codes)

The LLR values for the sum-product algorithm in the
\ell
-th level are given as

\displaystyle\mathrm{LLR}_{1}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathrm{LLR}_{*1}^{(\ell)}&\text{for $c_{i}^{(\ell)}=0$}\\
-\mathrm{LLR}_{*1}^{(\ell)}&\text{for $c_{i}^{(\ell)}=1$}\end{cases}

(23)

where

\mathrm{LLR}_{*1}^{(\ell)}=\log\frac{p(y_{i}|u_{1,i}^{(1:\ell-1)},u_{2,i}^{(1:\ell-1)},s_{i}^{(\ell)}=0)}{p(y_{i}|u_{1,i}^{(1:\ell-1)},u_{2,i}^{(1:\ell-1)},s_{i}^{(\ell)}=1)},

and the conditional probability density function is

\displaystyle p(y_{i}|s_{i}^{(\ell)},u_{1,i}^{(1:\ell-1)},u_{2,i}^{(1:\ell-1)})=\sum_{s_{i}^{(\ell+1:L)},u_{1,i}^{(\ell:L)}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp{\left(-\frac{1}{2}(y_{i}-\varphi_{1}(u_{1,i})-\varphi_{2}(u_{1,i}\oplus_{2^{L}}s_{i}))^{2}\right)}.

V
Extensions

In this section, we briefly discuss possible extensions of the CFMA framework to complex channels and the multi-user case.

V-A

CFMA for complex channel

The complex channel model is given by

Y=h_{1}x_{1}+h_{2}x_{2}+Z
,
where
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
denote the complex-valued channel gain and
Z\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
is the circularly symmetric complex additive white Gaussian noise.

Conceptually, the extension can be done simply by mapping the odd and even code symbols to the complex and real channels, respectively. Formally, we have

\displaystyle(x_{1,i},x_{2,i})

\displaystyle=(\varphi_{1}(u_{1,{2i-1}},u_{1,{2i}}),\varphi_{2}(u_{2,{2i-1}},u_{2,{2i}}))

(26)

where,

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}(u_{1,{2i-1}},u_{2,{2i}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{3P}{2(2^{2L}-1)}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}2^{\ell-1}\times\left(\jmath(2u_{1,{2i-1}}^{(\ell)}-1)+(2u_{2,{2i}}^{(\ell)}-1)\right)

\displaystyle\varphi_{2}(u_{2,{2i-1}},u_{2,{2i}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{j\theta}\cdot\varphi_{1}(u_{2,{2i-1}},u_{2,{2i}}).

One notable feature in the above mapping is the parameter
\theta
that relatively rotates the constellation between the two users. When we do not have channel state information at the transmitter (CSIT), the parameter
\theta
is simply zero. In situations where we have CSIT, the parameter can further improve the performance. The parameter
\theta
gives another degree of freedom to change the modulation (or the effective channel gain) between the users when we use QAM modulation. In some cases where the channel coefficients are unfavorable for compute-forward, the parameter
\theta
can be used to tune the effective channel gains. By changing the parameter
\theta
we can achieve more points on the dominant face of the MAC capacity. The decoding procedure is similar to that of the real-valued channel model developed in previous sections
III
and
IV
, with the only exception that all LLR values will be evaluated with respect to the complex channel

\displaystyle p(y_{i}|u_{1,2i-1},u_{1,2i},u_{2,2i-1},u_{2,2i})=\frac{1}{\pi}\exp\left(-\bigl|y_{i}-\varphi_{1}(u_{1,2i-1},u_{1,2i})-\varphi_{2}(u_{2,2i-1},u_{2,2i})\bigr|^{2}\right).

By analogy, each decoding step also has a complexity of the order of the point-to-point communication system. Simulation results for complex-valued channel models are provided in Section
VI-B
and
VI-C
.

V-B

\mathcal{X}_{1}
,
\mathcal{X}_{2}

where

\bm{e}^{m}=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{m}\bm{u}_{k}

\bullet

Decode
\bm{e}^{K}

{\hat{e}^{K}}_{i}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\limits_{e^{K}_{i}}p(e^{K}_{i}|\bm{y})

⋮

\bullet

\bm{e}^{2}

\bullet

Decode
\bm{u}_{1}
:

{\hat{u}}_{1,i}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\limits_{u_{1,i}}p(u_{1,i}|\bm{y},\bm{e}^{K},\bm{e}^{K-1},\dots,\bm{e}^{2})

For each decoding stage, the bit-wise MAP rule (in particular, the LLR values) can be derived in a similar way as in Section
IV-B

VII
Conclusion

In this paper, we presented a practical CFMA coding strategy with low complexity sequential decoders. We have shown that the CFMA strategy achieves (non-corner) points on the dominant face MAC capacity region without time-sharing or rate-splitting. This property leads to more flexible rate (resource) allocations for multi-user networks and rate improvement for applications such as the interference channel. Several case studies have been presented with off-the-self point-to-point binary LDPC codes that show the potential of our strategy. This property itself is desirable in many cases where backward compatibility is an issue. As future work, it would be interesting to see how the performance of the CFMA strategy can be improved by further optimization of the codes.
