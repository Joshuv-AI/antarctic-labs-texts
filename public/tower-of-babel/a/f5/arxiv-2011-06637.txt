On approximation of maps into real algebraic homogeneous spaces
Jacek Bochnak, Wojciech Kucharz

Abstract
Let X X be a real algebraic variety (resp. nonsingular real algebraic variety) and let Y Y be a homogeneous space for some linear real algebraic group. We prove that a continuous (resp. 𝒞 ∞ \mathcal{C}^{\infty} ) map f : X → Y f\colon X\to Y can be approximated by regular maps in the 𝒞 0 \mathcal{C}^{0} (resp. 𝒞 ∞ \mathcal{C}^{\infty} ) topology if and only if it is homotopic to a regular map. Taking Y = 𝕊 p Y=\mathbb{S}^{p} , the unit p p -dimensional sphere, we obtain solutions of several problems that have been open since the 1980’s and which concern approximation of maps with values in the unit spheres. This has several consequences for approximation of maps between unit spheres. For example, we prove that for every positive integer n n every 𝒞 ∞ \mathcal{C}^{\infty} map from 𝕊 n \mathbb{S}^{n} into 𝕊 n \mathbb{S}^{n} can be approximated by regular maps in the 𝒞 ∞ \mathcal{C}^{\infty} topology. Up to now such a result has only been known for five special values of n n , namely, n = 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 n=1,2,3,4 or 7 7 .



Key words and phrases.
Real algebraic variety, regular map, approximation, homotopy, real algebraic group, homogeneous space, unit sphere.

Introduction

In the present paper, we study approximation of continuous or
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}

maps by real regular maps, that is, real algebraic morphisms. Our
results concern maps with values in homogeneous spaces for linear real
algebraic groups. Special attention is paid to maps into unit spheres.
The main result, Theorem
1.1
, and its consequences provide
answers to some approximation problems that have been open since the
1980’s.

Throughout this work, by an
algebraic variety
we always mean a
quasiprojective variety. To be precise, a
real algebraic variety

is a ringed space with structure sheaf of
\mathbb{R}
-algebras of
\mathbb{R}
-valued
functions, which is isomorphic to a Zariski locally closed subset of
real projective
n
-space
\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
, for some
n
, endowed with the
Zariski topology and the sheaf of regular functions. This is compatible
with
[
3
]
, which contains a detailed exposition of real algebraic
geometry. We use the analogous definition of a
complex algebraic
variety
, replacing
\mathbb{R}
by
\mathbb{C}
. Recall that each real algebraic
variety in the sense used here is actually affine, that is, isomorphic
to an algebraic subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for some
n
, see

[
3
, Proposition 3.2.10 and Theorem 3.4.4]
. Morphisms of
algebraic varieties are called
regular maps
(in some of our
references they are called
entire rational maps
, see
[
4
,
5
,
48
,
49
]
).

A
real algebraic group
is a real algebraic variety
G
endowed
with the structure of a group such that the group operations
G\times G\to G
,
(a,b)\mapsto ab
, and
G\to G
,
a\mapsto a^{-1}
are
regular maps.
Morphisms of real algebraic groups
are regular maps
that are group homomorphisms. A real algebraic group is said to be

linear
if it is isomorphic to a Zariski closed subgroup of the
general linear group
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, for some
n
. Obviously, the familiar
subgroups of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, the orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}(n)
and special
orthogonal group
\mathrm{SO}(n)
, are linear real algebraic groups. A

complex algebraic group
, linear or not, is defined analogously,
replacing
\mathbb{R}
by
\mathbb{C}
. Clearly, each real or complex algebraic group

G
is a nonsingular algebraic variety of pure dimension. Moreover, if

G
is linear, then each Zariski closed subgroup of
G
is a linear
algebraic group.

Any complex algebraic variety
V
carries the obvious underlying
structure
\real{V}
of a real algebraic variety, called the

realification
of
V
(for example,
\real{(\mathbb{C}^{n})}=\mathbb{R}^{2n}
). If
G
is a complex algebraic group, then its realification

\real{G}
is a real algebraic group with the same group operations as
in
G
. If
H
is a Zariski closed subgroup of
G
, then
\real{H}
is a
Zariski closed subgroup of
\real{G}
. Moreover, if the group
G
is
linear, then so is the group
\real{G}
; indeed, it suffices to note that
the real algebraic group
{}_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is isomorphic to the image
of the real regular embedding

{}_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathrm{GL}_{2n}(\mathbb{R}),\quad A+\sqrt{-1}\,B\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}A&-B\\
B&A\end{pmatrix},

where
A
,
B
are real
n
-by-
n
matrices. Consequently, each Zariski
closed subgroup of
{}_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is a linear real algebraic
group. In particular, the unitary group
\mathrm{U}(n)
and special unitary
group
\mathrm{SU}(n)
are linear real algebraic groups, being Zariski closed
subgroups of
{}_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

The general linear group
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{H})
, where
\mathbb{H}
is the (skew)
field of quaternions, can also be viewed as a linear real algebraic
group via the standard embedding into
\mathrm{GL}_{4n}(\mathbb{R})
. Therefore the
symplectic subgroup
\mathrm{Sp}(n)
of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{H})
is a linear real algebraic
group.

Let
G
be a real algebraic group. A
G
-space
(or a

G
-variety
) is a real algebraic variety
Y
on which
G
acts,
the action
G\times Y\to Y
,
(a,y)\mapsto a\cdot y
being a
regular map. A
homogeneous space
for
G
is a
G
-space on which

G
acts transitively. Note that each homogeneous space for
G
is a
nonsingular real algebraic variety of pure dimension.

Besides the Zariski topology, every real algebraic variety is endowed
with the Euclidean topology determined by the standard metric on
\mathbb{R}
.
Unless explicitly stated otherwise, all topological notions relating to
real algebraic varieties will refer to the Euclidean topology.

Given real algebraic varieties
X
and
Y
, we denote by
\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
the
set of all regular maps from
X
into
Y
. We regard
\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
as a
subset of the space
\mathcal{C}^{0}(X,Y)
of all continuous maps endowed with the

\mathcal{C}^{0}
(that is, compact-open) topology. We say that a continuous map
f\colon X\to Y
can be
approximated by regular maps in the
\mathcal{C}^{0}

topology
if, for every neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
f
in
\mathcal{C}^{0}(X,Y)
, there
is a regular map
g\colon X\to Y
which belongs to
\mathcal{U}
. If both
varieties
X
and
Y
are nonsingular, then
\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
is a subset of
the space
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(X,Y)
of all
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
maps endowed with the
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}

topology (see
[
29
, p. 36]
or
[
57
, p. 311]
for the
definition of this topology and note that in
[
29
]
it is called
the weak
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
topology); therefore the concept of approximation of
a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
map
f\colon X\to Y
by regular maps in the
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}

topology is well-defined.

Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
X
be a real algebraic
variety (resp. nonsingular real algebraic
variety) and let
Y
be a homogeneous space for some linear real algebraic
group. Then, for a continuous (resp.
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
) map
f\colon X\to Y
,
the following conditions are equivalent:

(a)

f
can be approximated by regular maps in the

\mathcal{C}^{0}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
) topology.

(b)

f
is homotopic to a regular map.

The implication
(a)
\Rightarrow
(b)
holds because
X
deformation retracts to some compact subset
K\subseteq X

[
3
, Corollary 9.3.7]
, and any two continuous maps from
K
into
Y
that are sufficiently close in the
\mathcal{C}^{0}
topology are homotopic (the latter assertion is valid if
Y
is an arbitrary
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
manifold). The proof of
(b)
\Rightarrow
(a)
is given in Section
4
and depends on the
techniques developed in Sections 2 and 3. With notation as in Theorem
1.1
, if
X
is a compact nonsingular real algebraic curve, then, by the recent result of Benoist and Wittenberg
[
2
, Theorem A(3)]
, each
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
map from
X
into
Y
can be
approximated by regular maps in the
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
topology. Some special cases of
Theorem
1.1
have been anticipated since the 1980’s, however,
the results obtained heretofore have been very incomplete.

As immediate consequences of Theorem
1.1
we get the following
two corollaries.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
X
be a real algebraic
variety (resp. nonsingular real algebraic
variety) and let
Y
be a homogeneous space for some linear real
algebraic group. Then every continuous (resp.
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
) null homotopic
map from
X
to
Y
can be approximated by regular maps in the
C^{0}

(resp.
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
) topology. ∎

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
Y
be a homogeneous space for some linear real algebraic group. Then every continuous (resp.
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
) map
Y\to Y

that is homotopic to the identity map can be approximated by regular
maps in the
\mathcal{C}^{0}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}^{\infty})
topology. ∎

It should be mentioned that no direct proofs of these corollaries, which
do not make use of Theorem
1.1
, are available.

In the rest of this section, we discuss the impact of
Theorem
1.1
within the framework described in the following
example.

Example 1.4
.

Here are some homogeneous spaces of interest.

(i)

For every nonnegative integer
n
the unit

n
-sphere

\mathbb{S}^{n}=\{(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:x_{0}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2}=1\}

is a homogeneous space for the real orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}(n+1)
.

(ii)

Let
\mathbb{F}
stand for one of the fields
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}

or
\mathbb{H}
. Denote by
\mathbb{G}_{r}(\mathbb{F}^{n})
the Grassmannian of
r

\mathbb{F}
-vector subspaces of
\mathbb{F}^{n}
, regarded as a real algebraic variety
(see
[
3
, pp. 72, 73, 352]
). Clearly,
\mathbb{G}_{r}(\mathbb{F}^{n})
is a
homogeneous space for the group
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F})
viewed as a linear real
algebraic group.

(iii)

Denote by
\mathbb{V}_{r}(\mathbb{F}^{n})
the Stiefel manifold of
all orthonormal
r
-frames in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
, regarded as a real algebraic
variety. Clearly,
\mathbb{V}_{r}(\mathbb{F}^{n})
is a homogeneous space for the linear
real algebraic group
\mathrm{O}(n)
if
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
,
\mathrm{U}(n)
if
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
, and

\mathrm{Sp}(n)
if
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{H}
.

(iv)

Every real algebraic group
G
is a homogeneous
space for
G
under action by left translations.

In view of Theorem
1.1
and Example

(i)
,
we get at once the following result on maps into the unit
p
-sphere

\mathbb{S}^{p}

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
X
be a real algebraic
variety (resp. nonsingular real algebraic
variety) and let
p
be a positive integer. Then, for a continuous (resp.

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
) map
f\colon X\to\mathbb{S}^{p}
, the following conditions are
equivalent:

(a)

f
can be approximated by regular maps in the

\mathcal{C}^{0}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
) topology.

(b)

f
is homotopic to a regular map.∎

Until now Corollary
1.5
with
X
compact and
\dim X\geq p
has only been
known for a few special values of

, namely, for

or
4
(see

[
4
]
) and

(see
[

]
). Of course, if

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(X,\mathbb{S}^{p})

\mathbb{S}^{p}
with one

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
) map from

into
\mathbb{S}^{p}
can be approximated in the space

\mathcal{C}^{0}(X,\mathbb{S}^{p})
(resp.

Conjecture I

Let

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
X
be a compact connected nonsingular real algebraic variety of odd
dimension

. Then a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
map
f\colon X\times\mathbb{S}^{2n-k}\to\mathbb{S}^{2n}
can be approximated by regular maps in the

if and only if it is null homotopic.

Proof.

By
[
6
, Theorem 2.4]
, each regular map from
X\times\mathbb{S}^{2n-k}

into
\mathbb{S}^{2n}
is null homotopic, and hence the conclusion follows from

1.5
.
∎

The behavior of regular maps into odd-dimensional spheres is entirely
different.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
X
be a compact connected oriented nonsingular real algebraic
variety of odd dimension
k
. Then either

(i)

the set
\mathcal{R}(X,\mathbb{S}^{k})
is dense in the space

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(X,\mathbb{S}^{k})
, or

(ii)

the closure of
\mathcal{R}(X,\mathbb{S}^{k})
in
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(X,\mathbb{S}^{k})
coincides with the set

\{f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(X,\mathbb{S}^{k}):\deg(f)\in 2\mathbb{Z}\},

where
\deg(f)
is the topological degree of the map

.

Proof.

By
[
5
, Corollary 2.5]
, the set

\{m\in\mathbb{Z}:m=\deg(g),\ g\in\mathcal{R}(X,\mathbb{S}^{k})\}

is equal either to
\mathbb{Z}
or

, so the statement follows by combining

1.5
.
∎

Let

\mathcal{R}(X,\mathbb{S}^{k})

Let

2
Malleable real algebraic varieties

The notions and results of this section will be generalized in the next
one. Nevertheless, it seems to be beneficial to discuss first the key
special case.

In what follows we work with vector bundles, which are always

\mathbb{R}
-vector bundles. Let
Y
be a real algebraic variety. Given a vector
bundle
p\colon E\to Y
over
Y
, with total space
E
and bundle
projection
p
, we sometimes refer to
E
as a vector bundle over
Y
.
If
y
is a point in
Y
, we let
E_{y}\coloneqq p^{-1}(y)
denote the
fiber of
E
over
y
and write
0_{y}
for the zero vector in
E_{y}
. As
usual, we call the set
Z(E)\coloneqq\{0_{y}\in E_{y}:y\in Y\}
the
zero section of
E
.

The general theory of algebraic vector bundles over real algebraic
varieties is discussed in
[
3
, Section 12.1]
. For each algebraic
vector bundle
E
over
Y
there exists an algebraic vector bundle
E^{\prime}

over
Y
such that the direct sum
E\oplus E^{\prime}
is algebraically
trivial, see
[
3
, Theorem 12.1.7]
. Moreover, if
E_{1}
is an
algebraic vector subbundle of
E
, then there exists an algebraic vector
subbundle
E_{2}
of
E
such that
E_{1}\oplus E_{2}=E
. This is the case
since
E
can be regarded as an algebraic vector subbundle of the
product vector bundle
Y\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for some
n
, and hence as
E_{2}

one can take the orthogonal complement (with respect to the standard
scalar product on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
) of
E_{1}
in
E
.

Assuming that
Y
is a nonsingular real algebraic variety, we write
TY

for the tangent bundle to
Y
and
T_{y}Y
for the tangent space to
Y
at
y\in Y
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
Y
be a nonsingular real algebraic variety.

(i)

A
spray
for
Y
is a triple

(E,p,s)
, where
p\colon E\to Y
is an algebraic vector bundle
over
Y
and
s\colon E\to Y
is a regular map
such that
s(0_{y})=y
for all
y\in Y
.

(ii)

A spray
(E,p,s)
for
Y
is said
to be
dominating
if the derivative

d_{0_{y}}s\colon T_{0_{y}}E\to T_{y}Y

maps the subspace
E_{y}=T_{0_{y}}E_{y}
of
T_{0_{y}}E
onto
T_{y}Y
, that is,

d_{0_{y}}s(E_{y})=T_{y}Y

for all
y\in Y
.

(iii)

The variety
Y
is called
malleable
if
it admits a dominating spray.

The simplest malleable variety is
Y=\mathbb{R}^{n}
which admits a dominating
spray
(E,p,s)
, where
p\colon E\coloneqq Y\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to Y
is the product vector bundle and
s\colon E\to Y

is defined by
s(y,v)=y+v
for all
(y,v)\in E
. More substantial
and useful examples are provided by Examples
2.5
and

2.6
, and above all by Propositions
2.7
and
2.8
.

In the following two lemmas we describe some basic properties of
dominating sprays.

Lemma 2.2
.

Any malleable nonsingular real algebraic variety
Y
admits a dominating
spray
(E,p,s)
such that
p\colon E\to Y
is an
algebraically trivial vector bundle.

Proof.

Suppose that
(\tilde{E},\tilde{p},\tilde{s})
is a
dominating spray for
Y
. Choose an algebraic vector bundle

\tilde{p}^{\prime}\colon\tilde{E}^{\prime}\to Y
such that the direct sum
p\colon E\coloneqq\tilde{E}\oplus\tilde{E}^{\prime}\to Y
is an algebraically trivial
vector bundle. Defining
s\colon E\to Y
by
s(v,v^{\prime})=\tilde{s}(v)
, we get a
dominating spray
(E,p,s)
for
Y
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
Y
be a malleable nonsingular real algebraic variety and let

(E,p,s)
be a dominating spray for
Y
. Then there exists a
dominating spray
(\hat{E},\hat{p},\hat{s})
for
Y

such that
\hat{p}\colon\hat{E}\to Y
is an algebraic vector subbundle
of
p\colon E\to Y
,
\hat{s}=s|_{\hat{E}}
, and the restriction

d_{0_{y}}\hat{s}|_{\hat{E}_{y}}\colon\hat{E}_{y}\to T_{y}Y

of the derivative
d_{0_{y}}\hat{s}\colon T_{0_{y}}\hat{E}\to T_{y}Y
is a
linear isomorphism for all
y\in Y
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\colon E\to TY
be the morphism of algebraic vector bundles
over
Y
defined by

\alpha(v)=d_{0_{p(v)}}s(v)\quad\text{for all }v\in E.

Since
\alpha
is a surjective morphism, its kernel
\operatorname{Ker}\alpha
is an
algebraic vector subbundle of
E
. It follows that
E=\hat{E}\oplus\operatorname{Ker}\alpha
is the direct sum for some algebraic vector subbundle
\hat{p}\colon\hat{E}\to Y
of
p\colon E\to Y
. This completes the proof.
∎

As a consequence of Lemma
2.3
, we obtain the following.

Proposition 2.4
.

Any malleable nonsingular real algebraic variety
Y
admits a
dominating spray of the form
(TY,\pi,\sigma)
, where

\pi\colon TY\to Y
is the tangent bundle to
Y
.

Proof.

With notation as in Lemma
2.3
, the vector bundle
\hat{E}
is
isomorphic to the tangent bundle
TY
, which completes the proof.
∎

Next we identify some malleable real algebraic varieties.

Example 2.5
.

Let
G
denote either the real orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}(n)
or the real
special orthogonal group
\mathrm{SO}(n)
. Then each homogeneous space
Y
for

G
is malleable. This assertion can be established by direct
computation as follows.

Let
\mathrm{Mat}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
be the
\mathbb{R}
-algebra of all
n
-by-
n

matrices with real entries. Let
0
and
1
stand for the zero matrix
and the identity matrix in
\mathrm{Mat}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. Using the determinant function

\det
, we obtain a Zariski open neighborhood

\Omega\coloneqq\{x\in\mathrm{Mat}_{n}(\mathbb{R}):\det(1+x)\neq 0\}

of the zero matrix. We identify the tangent space to
G
at
1
with the
vector subspace
V
of
\mathrm{Mat}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
comprising all skew symmetric
matrices;
V\cong\mathbb{R}^{d}
where
d=n(n-1)/2
. Thus

\varphi\colon V\to G\cap\Omega,\quad v\mapsto(1-v)(1+v)^{-1}

is a well-defined biregular isomorphism with
\varphi(0)=1
.
We get a dominating
spray
(E,p,s)
for
Y
, where
p\colon E\coloneqq Y\times V\to Y
is the product vector bundle and
s\colon E\to Y
is defined by
s(y,v)=\varphi(v)\cdot y
for all
(y,v)\in E
.

In particular, for unit spheres we get the following.

Example 2.6
.

For every positive integer
n
the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n}
is a malleable
real algebraic variety. Indeed, this is the case by Example
2.5

because
\mathbb{S}^{n}
is a homogeneous space for the real orthogonal group

\mathrm{O}(n+1)
. Actually, as observed by János Kollár, one obtains directly a dominating spray
(T\mathbb{S}^{n},\pi,\sigma)
for
\mathbb{S}^{n}
, where
\pi\colon T\mathbb{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{S}^{n}
is the tangent bundle to
\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
\sigma\colon T\mathbb{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{S}^{n}
restricted to the tangent space
T_{p}\mathbb{S}^{n}
to
\mathbb{S}^{n}
at
p
, viewed as a hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
passing through
p
, is the inverse of stereographic projection from
-p
to
T_{p}\mathbb{S}^{n}
.

For the proof of Theorem
1.1
it is essential to generalize
Example
2.5
. First we recall some terminology and notation
which is compatible with
[
16
,
21
,
51
]
. Let
V
be an
algebraic
\mathbb{R}
-variety
, that is, a complex algebraic variety
defined over
\mathbb{R}
(equivalently,
V
can be viewed as a reduced
quasiprojective scheme over
\mathbb{R}
). The set of real points of
V
will be
denoted by
V(\mathbb{R})
. We emphasize the distinction between algebraic

\mathbb{R}
-varieties and real algebraic varieties. An
algebraic

\mathbb{R}
-group
is an algebraic
\mathbb{R}
-variety
\Gamma
endowed with the
structure of a group such that the identity element of
\Gamma
is in

\Gamma(\mathbb{R})
, and the group operations are regular maps defined over

\mathbb{R}
. An algebraic
\mathbb{R}
-group is said to be
linear
if, for some
positive integer
n
, it is isomorphic over
\mathbb{R}
to a Zariski closed
subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
defined over
\mathbb{R}
. If
\Gamma
is an algebraic

\mathbb{R}
-group, then
\Gamma(\mathbb{R})
is a real algebraic group. Moreover, if

\Gamma
is a linear algebraic
\mathbb{R}
-group, then
\Gamma(\mathbb{R})
is a linear
real algebraic group. Conversely, each linear real algebraic group
G

is up to isomorphism of the form
\Gamma(\mathbb{R})
for some linear algebraic

\mathbb{R}
-group
\Gamma
. Indeed, we may assume that
G
is a Zariski closed
subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and take as
\Gamma
the Zariski closure of
G

in
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, see
[
51
, Lemma 2.2.4]
. In view of this
discussion we can make use of Chevalley’s paper
[
21
]
in the
proof of Proposition
2.7
below.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
G
be a linear real algebraic group. Then each homogeneous space
Y
for
G
is a malleable variety.

Proof.

Let
d=\dim G
and let
G^{0}
be the irreducible component of the
variety
G
that contains the identity element
1
of
G
. By a theorem
of Chevalley
[
21
, Theorem 2]
or
[
16
, Corollary 7.12]
,

G^{0}
is a unirational variety, and hence there exist a Zariski open
subset
U
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a regular map
\psi\colon U\to G^{0}
such that
the image
\psi(U)
is Zariski dense in
G^{0}
. Clearly, at some point
a\in U
the derivative
d_{a}\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to T_{\psi(a)}G^{0}
is a
linear isomorphism. Using a translation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
we may assume that

a=0\in U
. The map
\varphi\colon U\to G
,
v\mapsto\psi(v)\psi(0)^{-1}
is regular,
\varphi(0)=1
, and the derivative of

\varphi
at
0
is a linear isomorphism. As noted by János Kollár in no.
A.2
of the Appendix, taking the map

h\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad h(v)=\frac{cv}{1+\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}},

where
c
is a small positive real number, we obtain a regular map
g\coloneqq\varphi\circ h\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to G
such that
g(0)=1
and the derivative of
g
at
0
is a linear isomorphism.
Now we obtain a dominating spray
(E,p,s)
for
Y
, where
p\colon E\coloneqq Y\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to Y
is the product vector bundle
and
s\colon E\to Y
is defined by
s(y,v)=g(v)\cdot y
for
all
(y,v)\in E
.
∎

In an earlier version of Proposition
2.7
we only used the map
s(y,v)=\varphi(v)\cdot y
defined on the Zariski open subset
Y\times U
of
E
. This was sufficient for all our main results, but Kollár’s observation helped us to improve the presentation.

Prompted by some comments by Olivier Benoist, we next discuss a generalization of Proposition
2.7
.

Let
Y
be a real algebraic variety and let
G
be a linear real algebraic group acting on
Y
. The
G
-space
Y
is called
good
if
Y
is nonsingular and for each point
y\in Y
the derivative of the map
G\to Y
,
a\mapsto a\cdot y
at the identity element of
G
is surjective. If
Y
is homogeneous for
G
, then the
G
-space
Y
is good. The converse is not true in general. Indeed, let
V
be an algebraic
\mathbb{R}
-variety, with
V(\mathbb{R})
nonempty, and let
\Gamma
be a linear algebraic
\mathbb{R}
-group acting on
V
. Then the real algebraic group
\Gamma(\mathbb{R})
acts on the real algebraic variety
V(\mathbb{R})
. Clearly, if the action of
\Gamma
on
V
is transitive, then the
\Gamma(\mathbb{R})
-space
V(\mathbb{R})
is good, but it can happen, as is well-known, that
V(\mathbb{R})
is not homogeneous for
\Gamma(\mathbb{R})
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
G
be a linear real algebraic group. Then each good
G
-space
Y
is a malleable variety.

Proof.

The same as that of Proposition
2.7
.
∎

Proposition
2.8
is of interest in view of Theorems
4.2
and
4.3
.

3
Malleable submersions

This section is devoted to developing techniques used in the proof of
Theorem
1.1
. Of independent interest are Theorem
3.9

and Corollary
3.10
.

Notation 3.1
.

Throughout the present section
X
,
Z
are nonsingular real algebraic
varieties, and
h\colon Z\to X
is a regular map which is surjective
and submersive. Furthermore,
V(h)
denotes the algebraic vector
subbundle of the tangent bundle
TZ
to
Z
defined by

V(h)_{z}=\operatorname{Ker}(d_{z}h\colon T_{z}Z\to T_{h(z)}X)\quad\text{for all }z\in Z.

Observe that
V(h)_{z}
is the tangent space to the fiber
h^{-1}(h(z))
.

Let
U
be an open subset of
X
. A map
f\colon U\to Z
is called a

section
of
h\colon Z\to X
if
h(f(x))=x
for all
x\in U
. A
section which is a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
map is called a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
section
.
We say that a section
g\colon U\to Z
is
regular
if there
exist a Zariski open neighborhood
\tilde{U}\subseteq X
of
U
and a
section
\tilde{g}\colon\tilde{U}\to Z
such that
\tilde{g}|_{U}=g
and

\tilde{g}
is a regular map. Obviously, every regular section is a

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
section. We say that a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
section
f\colon U\to Z

can be
approximated by regular sections in the
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
topology

if, for every neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
f
in the space
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,Z)
of

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
maps, there exists a regular section
g\colon U\to Z
that
belongs to
\mathcal{U}
. By a
homotopy of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
sections
we mean a
continuous map
F\colon U\times[0,1]\to Z
such that
F_{t}\colon U\to Z
,
x\mapsto F(x,t)
is a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
section for every
t\in[0,1]
. Two
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
sections
f_{0},f_{1}\colon U\to Z
are said to be

homotopic through
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
sections
if there exists a homotopy
F\colon U\times[0,1]\to Z
of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
sections with
F_{0}=f_{0}
and

F_{1}=f_{1}
. To study approximation of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
sections by regular
sections we need several notions and auxiliary results.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
h\colon Z\to X
be the submersion of Notation
3.1
.

(i)

A
spray
for
h\colon Z\to X
is a triple

(E,p,s)
, where
p\colon E\to Z
is an algebraic vector bundle
over
Z
and
s\colon E\to Z
is a regular map
such that

s(E_{z})\subseteq h^{-1}(h(z))\quad\text{and}\quad s(0_{z})=z\quad\text{for all }z\in Z.

(ii)

A spray
(E,p,s)
for
h\colon Z\to X
is said to be

dominating
if the derivative

d_{0_{z}}s\colon T_{0_{z}}E\to T_{z}Z

maps the subspace
E_{z}=T_{0_{z}}E_{z}
of
T_{0_{z}}E
onto
V(h)_{z}
, that
is,

d_{0_{z}}s(E_{z})=V(h)_{z}

for all
z\in Z
.

(iii)

The submersion
h\colon Z\to X
is called
malleable
if it
admits a dominating spray.

Significant examples of malleable submersions are provided by combining
Proposition
2.7
and Lemma
4.1
. Note that if
X
is
reduced to a point, then Definition
3.2
coincides with
Definition
2.1
. The next two lemmas are generalizations of
Lemmas
2.2
and
2.3
.

Lemma 3.3
.

If the submersion
h\colon Z\to X
is malleable, then it admits a
dominating spray
(E,p,s)
such that
p\colon E\to Z
is
an algebraically trivial vector bundle.

Proof.

Suppose that
(\tilde{E},\tilde{p},\tilde{s})
is a
dominating spray for
h\colon Z\to X
. Choose an algebraic
vector bundle
\tilde{p}^{\prime}\colon\tilde{E}^{\prime}\to Z
such that the direct
sum
p\colon E\coloneqq\tilde{E}\oplus\tilde{E}^{\prime}\to Z
is an
algebraically trivial vector bundle. Defining
s\colon E\to Z
by
s(v,v^{\prime})=\tilde{s}(v)
, we get a
dominating spray
(E,p,s)
for
h\colon Z\to X
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose that
(E,p,s)
is a dominating spray for the
submersion
h\colon Z\to X
. Then there exists a dominating
spray
(\hat{E},\hat{p},,\hat{s})
for
h\colon Z\to X
such
that
\hat{p}\colon\hat{E}\to Z
is an algebraic vector subbundle of
p\colon E\to Z
,
\hat{s}=s|_{\hat{E}}
,
and the restriction

d_{0_{z}}\hat{s}|_{\hat{E}_{z}}\colon\hat{E}_{z}\to V(h)_{z}

of the derivative
d_{0_{z}}\hat{s}\colon T_{0_{z}}\hat{E}\to T_{z}Z
is a
linear isomorphism for all
z\in Z
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\colon E\to V(h)
be a morphism of algebraic vector bundles
over
Z
defined by

\alpha(v)=d_{0_{p(v)}}s(v)\quad\text{for all }v\in E.

Since
\alpha
is a surjective morphism, its kernel
\operatorname{Ker}\alpha
is an
algebraic vector subbundle of
E
. It follows that
E=\hat{E}\oplus\operatorname{Ker}\alpha
is the direct sum for some algebraic vector subbundle
\hat{p}\colon\hat{E}\to Z
of
p\colon E\to Z
. This completes the proof.
∎

Notation 3.5
.

Suppose that
(E,p,s)
is a dominating spray for the
submersion
h\colon Z\to X
. Let
U
be an open subset of
X
and let

f\colon U\to Z
be a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
section of
h\colon Z\to X
. Denote
by
p_{f}\colon E_{f}\to U
the pullback of the vector bundle
p\colon E\to Z
under the map
f\colon U\to Z
. Recall that
p_{f}\colon E_{f}\to U
is a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
vector bundle over
U
, where

E_{f}\coloneqq\{(x,v)\in U\times E:f(x)=p(v)\},\quad p_{f}(x,v)=x.

We define

s_{f}\colon E_{f}\to Z,\quad s_{f}(x,v)=s(v).

Lemma 3.6
.

Using Notation
3.5
, assume that

d_{0_{z}}s|_{E_{z}}\colon E_{z}\to V(h)_{z}

is a linear isomorphism for all
z\in Z
. Then
s_{f}\colon E_{f}\to Z

maps diffeomorphically an open neighborhood of the zero section
Z(E_{f})

in
E_{f}
onto an open neighborhood of
f(U)
in
Z
.

Proof.

Let
x\in U
. The zero vector in the fiber
(E_{f})_{x}
is
(x,0_{f(x)})
, where
0_{f(x)}
is the zero vector in the fiber

E_{f(x)}
. Since
s_{f}(x,0_{f(x)})=s(0_{f(x)})=f(x)
, it follows
that
s_{f}
induces a diffeomorphism between
Z(E_{f})
and
f(U)
.
Moreover, the derivative

d_{(x,0_{f(x)})}s_{f}\colon T_{(x,0_{f(x)})}E_{f}\to T_{f(x)}Z

is an isomorphism because

d_{0_{z}}s|_{E_{z}}\colon E_{z}\to V(h)_{z}

is an isomorphism for all
z\in Z
. Consequently,
s_{f}
is a local
diffeomorphism at the point
(x,0_{f(x)})
. Now the lemma follows from
[
20
, (12.7)]
.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Suppose that
(E,p,s)
is a dominating spray for the
submersion
h\colon Z\to X
. Let
U
be an open subset of
X
and let

F\colon U\times[0,1]\to Z
be a homotopy of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
sections of

h\colon Z\to X
. Let
U_{0}
be an open subset of
X
whose closure

\overline{U_{0}}
is compact and contained in
U
. Let
t_{0}
be a point
in
[0,1]
. Then there exist a neighborhood
I_{0}
of
t_{0}
in
[0,1]

and a continuous map
\xi\colon U_{0}\times I_{0}\to E
such that

(3.7.1)

p(\xi(x,t))=F(x,t_{0})
for all
(x,t)\in U_{0}\times I_{0}
,

(3.7.2)

\xi(x,t_{0})=0_{F(x,t_{0})}
for all
x\in U_{0}
,

(3.7.3)

s(\xi(x,t))=F(x,t)
for all
(x,t)\in U_{0}\times I_{0}
,

(3.7.4)

for every
t\in I_{0}
the map
U_{0}\to E^{0}
,
x\mapsto\xi(x,t)
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.4
, we may assume without loss of generality that

d_{0_{z}}s|_{E_{z}}\colon E_{z}\to V(h)_{z}

is an isomorphism for all
z\in Z
. Defining
f\colon U\to Z
by

f(x)=F(x,t_{0})
, in view of Lemma
3.6
, there exist an open
neighborhood
M\subseteq E_{f}
of the zero section
Z(E_{f})
and an

The first result of this section can be viewed as a generalization of

Theorem 5.1

Let

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}

\mathbb{S}^{n}

Suppose that

By
[

\varphi_{d}\colon\mathbb{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{S}^{n}
with
\deg(\varphi_{d})=d

Since the composite map
\varphi_{d}\circ f
is regular and

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}

\mathbb{S}^{n}
is homotopic to a regular map.
Therefore the set
\mathcal{R}(X,\mathbb{S}^{n})
is dense in the space

. The converse is obvious.
∎

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(X_{k},\mathbb{S}^{2})
for all

.
Furthermore, if

is odd, then for every algebraic model

of

the set

is dense in
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(X,\mathbb{S}^{2})

is even, then there exists an

null homotopic

.

It is convenient to bring into play the homotopy groups. Let

be a
positive integer. As a base point in the unit

-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n}
we
choose

. For any given real algebraic variety

, let
\pi^{\mathrm{alg}}_{n}(Y,y_{0})
denote the subset of
the
n
th homotopy group
\pi_{n}(Y,y_{0})
comprising the homotopy classes
represented by regular maps from
\mathbb{S}^{n}
into

that preserve the base
points. We write
\pi^{\mathrm{alg}}_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{p})
,
\pi_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{p})
instead of

\pi^{\mathrm{alg}}_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{p},s_{p})
,
\pi_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{p},s_{p})
, respectively. It is an open
problem whether
\pi^{\mathrm{alg}}_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{p})
is a subgroup of
\pi_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{p})
for

(n,p)
, see
[
3
, p. 366]
,

[
50
]
and Proposition
5.5
for partial results.

Theorem
1.1
implies the following.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
Y
be a homogeneous space for a linear real algebraic group
G
and
let
y_{0}
be a point in
Y
. Assume that
\pi^{\mathrm{alg}}_{n}(Y,y_{0})=\pi_{n}(Y,y_{0})
for some positive integer
n
. Then the set of regular
maps
\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{S}^{n},Y)
is dense in the space of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
maps

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n},Y)
.

Proof.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{S}^{n}\to Y
be a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
map. Choose an element
a\in G
such that
a\cdot f(s_{n})=y_{0}
. Since
\pi^{\mathrm{alg}}_{n}(Y,y_{0})=\pi_{n}(Y,y_{0})
, the
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
map
g\colon\mathbb{S}^{n}\to Y
,
x\mapsto a\cdot f(x)
is homotopic to a regular map, and hence, by
Theorem
1.1
, it can be approximated by regular maps in the

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
topology. Consequently, the map
f
can be approximated by
regular maps in the
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
topology because
f(x)=a^{-1}\cdot g(x)

for all
x\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
.
∎

In particular, for every pair
(n,p)
of positive integers, the set

\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{S}^{n},\mathbb{S}^{p})
is dense in the space of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
maps

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n},\mathbb{S}^{p})
if and only if
\pi^{\mathrm{alg}}_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{p})=\pi_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{p})
.
The following result is another generalization of Theorem
1.6

and provides and additional support for Conjecture

.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let

(ii)

Proof.

(i)

(ii)

(iii)

Recall a basic notion from algebraic topology. An
H-space
is a
pointed topological space
X
with base point
e
, together with a
continuous map (H-space multiplication)
{\mu\colon X\times X\to X}

such that
\mu(e,e)=e
, and the maps
X\to X
defined by

x\mapsto\mu(x,e)\quad\text{and}\quad x\mapsto\mu(e,x)

are homotopic to the identity map of
X
through homotopies that keep
the base point
e
fixed. For every positive integer
n
, the group
operation in the homotopy group
\pi_{n}(X)\coloneqq\pi_{n}(X,e)
is
induced by the H-space multiplication
\mu
, see
[
19
, p. 443,
Theorem 4.1]
.

For our purpose relevant examples of H-spaces are real algebraic groups
(in particular, the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{3}
with the multiplication of the
quaternions of norm
1
) and the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{7}
, the latter with
the multiplication of the octonions of norm
1
.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
Y
be either a real algebraic group or the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{7}
.
Then, for every positive integer
n
, the subset
\pi^{\mathrm{alg}}_{n}(Y)
is a
subgroup of the homotopy group
\pi_{n}(Y)
.

Proof.

In the case under consideration, the H-space multiplication
Y\times Y\to Y
,
(a,b)\mapsto ab
and the inverse operation
Y\to Y
,
a\mapsto a^{-1}
are regular maps. The assertion follows since the group
operation in
\pi_{n}(Y)
is induced by the H-space multiplication.
∎

The following example has already been alluded to in connection with
Conjecture
II
in Section
1
.

Example 5.8
.

Let
(n,p)
be a pair of integers,
n>p\geq 1
. Then there exist an
algebraic model
X
of the
n
-dimensional torus
(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{n}
and a

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
map
f\colon X\to\mathbb{S}^{p}
such that

is not homotopic to
any regular map from
X
into
\mathbb{S}^{p}
.

This assertion can be proved as follows. Let
h\colon(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{p}\to\mathbb{S}^{p}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
map of topological degree
1
. Then the induced
homomorphism in homology
{h_{*}\colon H_{p}((\mathbb{S}^{1})^{p};\mathbb{Z}/2)\to H_{p}(\mathbb{S}^{p};\mathbb{Z}/2)}
is an isomorphism. Let
w
be a generator of the cohomology group

H^{p}(\mathbb{S}^{p};\mathbb{Z}/2)\cong\mathbb{Z}/2
; so
w
corresponds via the Poincaré
duality to a point in
\mathbb{S}^{p}
. Clearly, if
[(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{p}]\in H_{p}((\mathbb{S}^{1})^{p};\mathbb{Z}/2)
is the fundamental class of
(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{p}
, then the
Kronecker index
\langle w,h_{*}([(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{p}])\rangle
is nonzero.
Therefore the assertion holds by
[
14
, Theorem 2.8]
(with

K=(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{p}
,
L=(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{n-p}
,
Y=\mathbb{S}^{p}
).

Consequently, the set of regular maps

\mathcal{R}(X,\mathbb{S}^{p})
is not dense in the space of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
maps
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(X,\mathbb{S}^{p})
.

Next we illustrate the behavior of regular maps from the product of
spheres
\mathbb{S}^{p}\times\mathbb{S}^{q}
into the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{p+q}
.

Example 5.9
.

The results depend strongly on the specific values of

and

. In
what follows by a degree of a map we mean a topological degree.

(i)

According to

, there
exists a regular map

.

, for every integer

{\mathbb{S}^{4}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{6}}

.
Now, it follows from Hopf’s theorem that each
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
map
\mathbb{S}^{4}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{6}
is homotopic to a regular map. Consequently, by

, the set of regular maps
\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{S}^{4}\times\mathbb{S}^{2},\mathbb{S}^{6})
is dense in the space of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
maps
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{4}\times\mathbb{S}^{2},\mathbb{S}^{6})

(ii)

The same argument shows that

(iii)

(iv)

Assuming that both

Proposition 6.1

(i)

,

,

or

(iii)

,

,

or

(iv)

Y=\mathbb{V}_{r}(\mathbb{F}^{m})
with

Then the set of regular maps

[

]
(see also

[

, Chap. 8, Remark 4.2, and 12.2]
) the

th homotopy group

\pi_{n}(Y)
is trivial in cases
(i)
,
(ii)
and

. Furthermore, by

,
the homotopy group

.
Therefore the proof is complete in view of Corollary

.
∎

Next we consider regular maps from
\mathbb{S}^{n}
into
\mathrm{U}(m)
or
\mathrm{SU}(m)
, for

n
odd with
n\leq 2m-1
. This is harder to handle than
Proposition
6.1
(ii)
since the homotopy groups

\pi_{n}(\mathrm{U}(m))
and
\pi_{n}(\mathrm{SU}(m))
are nontrivial if
n
is odd (
n\neq 1
for the latter group). We have the following partial result.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
(n,m)
be a pair of positive integers and let
G(m)
denote either

\mathrm{U}(m)
or
\mathrm{SU}(m)
. Assume that
n=2k-1
is odd and
m\geq 2^{k-1}
.
Then the set of regular maps
\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{S}^{n},G(m))
is dense in the space of

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
maps
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n},G(m))
.

As a preparation for the proof of Theorem
6.2
, we briefly
summarize the discussion contained in
[
17
, Part III B]
. Let

k,p
be two integers with
1\leq k\leq p
. For any continuous map
f\colon\mathbb{S}^{2k-1}\to\mathrm{GL}_{p}(\mathbb{C})
, its degree
\deg(f)
is an integer
defined as follows. The map
f
is homotopic to the composite of some
continuous map
h\colon\mathbb{S}^{2k-1}\to\mathrm{GL}_{k}(\mathbb{C})
and the inclusion
map
\mathrm{GL}_{k}(\mathbb{C})\hookrightarrow\mathrm{GL}_{p}(\mathbb{C})
. The first column
h_{1}
of

h
determines a continuous map
h_{1}\colon\mathbb{S}^{2k-1}\to\mathbb{C}^{k}\setminus\{0\}
, and therefore one gets a continuous map

\psi=\frac{h_{1}}{\left\lVert h_{1}\right\rVert}\colon\mathbb{S}^{2k-1}\to\mathbb{S}^{2k-1},

where
\mathbb{S}^{2k-1}
is regarded as a subset of
\mathbb{C}^{k}=\mathbb{R}^{2k}
. The
topological degree
\deg(\psi)
of
\psi
is divisible by
(k-1)!
, and
one sets

\deg{f}=(-1)^{k-1}\frac{\deg(\psi)}{(k-1)!}.

The definition of
\deg(f)
does not depend on the choice of
h
.
Furthermore, one has the following variant of Bott’s periodicity
theorem: If
1\leq k\leq p
, then

\deg\colon\pi_{2k-1}(\mathrm{GL}_{p}(\mathbb{C}))\to\mathbb{Z},\quad[f]\mapsto\deg(f)

is a well-defined group isomorphism.

As usual, for any complex matrix
A
, let
A^{*}
denote its conjugate
transpose. For any positive integer
r
, denote by
I_{r}
the identity

r
-by-
r
matrix.

Suppose given two continuous maps
f\colon\mathbb{S}^{2k-1}\to\mathrm{GL}_{p}(\mathbb{C})

and
g\colon\mathbb{S}^{2l-1}\to\mathrm{GL}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
, where
1\leq k\leq p
and
1\leq l\leq q
. The product

f\#g\colon\mathbb{S}^{2(k+l)-1}\to\mathrm{GL}_{2pq}(\mathbb{C})

is a continuous map defined by

(x,y)\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}F(x)\otimes I_{q}&-I_{p}\otimes G(y)^{*}\\
I_{p}\otimes G(y)&F(x)^{*}\otimes I_{q}\end{pmatrix},

where
x\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
,
y\in\mathbb{C}^{l}
,
\left\lVert(x,y)\right\rVert=1
, while
F
and

G
are the homogeneous extensions of
f
and
g
, respectively. To be
precise,

F\colon\mathbb{C}^{k}\to\mathrm{Mat}_{p}(\mathbb{C}),\quad F(x)=\begin{cases}\left\lVert x\right\rVert f\big(\frac{x}{\left\lVert x\right\rVert}\big)&\text{for }x\in\mathbb{C}^{k}\setminus\{0\},\\
0&\text{for }x=0,\end{cases}

where
\mathrm{Mat}_{p}(\mathbb{C})
is the space of all complex
p
-by-
p
matrices, and

G\colon\mathbb{C}^{l}\to\mathrm{Mat}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
is defined analogously. One has the
formula

\deg(f\#g)=\deg(f)\deg(g).

Now, starting with the map

a\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathrm{GL}_{1}(\mathbb{C})=\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\},\quad z\mapsto z,

we define a sequence of continuous maps

a_{k}\colon\mathbb{S}^{2k-1}\to\mathrm{GL}_{2^{k-1}}(\mathbb{C}),\quad k=1,2,\ldots

by a recursive formula:
a_{1}=a
and
a_{k}=a_{k-1}\#a
for
k\geq 2
.
Since
\deg(a)=1
, it follows that
\deg(a_{k})=1
for all
k\geq 1
.
Thus, by the variant of Bott’s periodicity theorem stated above, for
every
k\geq 1
the homotopy group
\pi_{2k-1}(\mathrm{GL}_{2^{k-1}}(\mathbb{C}))\cong\mathbb{Z}
is generated by the homotopy class represented by
a_{k}
.

The maps
a_{k}
have some other useful properties. Let
A_{k}\colon\mathbb{C}^{k}\to\mathrm{Mat}_{2^{k-1}}(\mathbb{C})
be the homogeneous extension of
a_{k}
,
k\geq 1
. Clearly,
A_{1}(z_{1})=z_{1}
and

A_{k}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})=\begin{pmatrix}A_{k-1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k-1})&-\bar{z}_{k}I_{2^{k-2}}\\
z_{k}I_{2^{k-2}}&A_{k-1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k-1})^{*}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{for }k\geq 2,

where
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
and
\bar{z}_{k}
is the conjugate of

z_{k}
. It follows that
A_{k}
is an
\mathbb{R}
-linear map for
k\geq 1
.
Furthermore,

\displaystyle A_{k}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})A_{k}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})^{*}=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}z_{j}\bar{z}_{j}\Big)I_{2^{k-1}}\quad\text{for }k\geq 1,

\displaystyle\det A_{1}(z_{1})=z_{1}\bar{z}_{1},\quad\det A_{k}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}z_{j}\bar{z}_{j}\Big)^{2^{k-2}}\quad\text{for }k\geq 2.

In particular,
a_{k}
can be regarded as a regular map
a_{k}\colon\mathbb{S}^{2k-1}\to\mathrm{SU}(2^{k-1})
for
k\geq 2
.

Proof of Theorem
6.2
.

By Proposition
5.5
, it is sufficient to show that

\pi^{\mathrm{alg}}_{2k-1}(G(m))=\pi_{2k-1}(G(m))
.

Case 1
.

Suppose that
(k,m)=(1,m)
with
m\geq 1
. It is well known that

{\pi_{1}(\mathrm{SU}(m))=0}
, and the inclusion map
\mathrm{U}(1)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{U}(m)

induces an isomorphism
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{U}(1))\cong\pi_{1}(U(m))
. Case
1

follows since
\mathrm{U}(1)=\mathbb{S}^{1}
and
\pi^{\mathrm{alg}}_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})=\pi_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
.

Case 2
.

Suppose that
k\geq 2
and
m\geq 2^{k-1}
. The inclusion map

G(2^{k-1})\hookrightarrow\mathrm{GL}_{2^{k-1}}(\mathbb{C})
induces an isomorphism
of the homotopy groups
\pi_{2k-1}(G(2^{k-1}))
and

\pi_{2k-1}(\mathrm{GL}_{2^{k-1}}(\mathbb{C}))
. As explained above, the latter group is
generated by the homotopy class represented by the map
a_{k}
. Since

a_{k}
is a regular map with values in
G(2^{k-1})
, we get

\pi^{\mathrm{alg}}_{2k-1}(G(2^{k-1}))=\pi_{2k-1}(G(2^{k-1}))
by
Proposition
5.7
. Moreover, the inclusion map
G(2^{k-1})\hookrightarrow G(m)
induces an isomorphism of the homotopy groups

\pi_{2k-1}(G(2^{k-1}))
and
\pi_{2k-1}(G(m))
(see
[
31
, Chap. 8, Remark 4.2]
), and hence
\pi^{\mathrm{alg}}_{2k-1}(G(m))=\pi_{2k-1}(G(m))
.∎
