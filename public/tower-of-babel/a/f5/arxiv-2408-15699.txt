Strongly interacting fermions are non-trivial yet non-glassy
Eric R. Anschuetz, Chi-Fang Chen, Bobak T. Kiani, Robbie King

Abstract
Random spin systems at low temperatures are glassy and feature computational hardness in finding low-energy states. We study the random all-to-all interacting fermionic Sachdev–Ye–Kitaev (SYK) model and prove that, in contrast, (I) the low-energy states have polynomial circuit depth, yet (II) the annealed and quenched free energies agree to inverse-polynomially low temperatures, ruling out a glassy phase transition in this sense. These results are derived by showing that fermionic and spin systems significantly differ in their commutation index , which quantifies the non-commutativity of Hamiltonian terms. Our results suggest that low-temperature strongly interacting fermions, unlike spins, belong in a classically nontrivial yet quantumly easy phase.

This quantity pinpoints a crucial and often overlooked distinction between fermionic and spin Hamiltonians: low-degree fermionic monomials have a very different commutation structure than low-weight Pauli operators. The commutation index captures this difference, quantifying the fundamental distinction in the physics of local spin systems and local fermionic systems. This disparity, we argue, is the origin of a potential quantum advantage in simulating strongly interacting fermionic systems.

More precisely, we first show that all low-energy states (including constant-temperature thermal states) of the SYK model have high circuit complexity (‘classically non-trivial’):

(3)

II
Commutation structure of local operators

The
commutation index

\Delta(\mathcal{S})
of a given set of operators
\mathcal{S}=\{\bm{A}_{1},\dots,\bm{A}_{m}\}
is defined to be
[
25
]
:

\Delta(\mathcal{S}):=\sup_{|\psi\rangle}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle\psi|\bm{A}_{i}|\psi\rangle^{2}.

(5)

When all
\left\lVert\bm{A}_{i}\right\rVert\leq 1
the commutation index takes values
0<\Delta(\mathcal{S})\leq 1
. Roughly, a more ‘commuting’ set of observables
\mathcal{S}
gives a larger value of
\Delta\left(\mathcal{S}\right)
. For example, if the operators in
\mathcal{S}
are all mutually commuting Pauli operators, choosing
|\psi\rangle
to be a simultaneous eigenstate gives
\Delta(\mathcal{S})=1
.

The commutation index has strong implications for the physics of the model
\bm{H}=m^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}g_{i}\bm{A}_{i}
with Gaussian coefficients
g_{i}
. Crucially, it controls the sensitivity of many physical properties when varying the couplings of the model. For instance, the norm of the energy gradient of a given state with respect to the disorder is bounded by:

\big|\big|\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{g}}\langle\phi|\bm{H}|\phi\rangle\big|\big|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle\phi|\bm{A}_{i}|\phi\rangle^{2}\leq\Delta(\mathcal{S}).

(6)

Our key observation is that the commutation index of the set
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{q}
of
{n\choose q}
degree-
q
Majorana operators is very small:

Theorem II.1
.

Let
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{q}
be the set of degree-
q
Majorana operators on
n
fermionic modes. Then for any constant, even
q
:

\Delta(\mathcal{S}^{n}_{q})=\Theta_{q}(n^{-q/2}).

(7)

The decay with system size
n
is unique to the fermionic setting—for local Pauli operators,
\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is constant with respect to
n
(see

scales with

III
Circuit lower bound for the SYK model

An almost direct consequence of a decaying commutation index is a lower bound on the complexity of any ansatz in constructing near-ground states.
For any random Hamiltonian
\bm{H}=m^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}g_{i}\bm{A}_{i}
with i.i.d. Gaussian coefficients
g_{i}
, the commutation index
\Delta(\{\bm{A}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m})
characterizes the maximum variance of the energy
\langle\psi|\bm{H}|\psi\rangle
for an arbitrary fixed state
|\psi\rangle
. Standard concentration bounds then imply that the probability a state
|\psi\rangle
has energy
t
is bounded as
\exp(-\Omega(t^{2}/\Delta))
.
This concentration is so strong that one can bound the maximum energy over extremely large sets of states (or
\epsilon

\mathcal{S}
via a simple union bound argument with high probability over the disorder. In particular, we obtain a lower bound
|\mathcal{S}|=\exp(\Omega(t^{2}/\Delta))
on the cardinality of the class of ansatzes needed to achieve a given energy
t
.

IV
Annealed approximation for the SYK model

The commutation index also has direct implications for the concentration of various physical properties of interest around their disordered expectation. One manifestation of this is in the relation between the
quenched
and
annealed
free energies:

\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\ln{Z_{\beta}}}_{\text{quenched}}\quad\leq\quad\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\ln{\mathbb{E}Z_{\beta}}}_{\text{annealed}},

(8)

where
Z_{\beta}
is the partition function of the model
\sqrt{n}\bm{H}
at an inverse temperature
\beta
. The quenched free energy assumes the disorder induced by the random couplings is fixed when averaging over thermal fluctuations; the annealed free energy treats these fluctuations on an equal footing. While the two quantities agree at high temperature, at low temperature the latter is incapable of accounting for frustration induced by the disorder of the random couplings which can induce a spin glass phase
[
52
,
53
]
. Their disagreement is thus indicative of the presence of a spin glass phase (see
Appendix

A
). Motivated by this we bound the difference in quenched and annealed free energies as a function of the temperature and the commutation index of the model:

\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\ln{Z_{\beta}}\leq\frac{1}{n}\ln{\mathbb{E}Z_{\beta}}\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\ln{Z_{\beta}}+4\beta^{2}\Delta.

(9)

For the SYK model this directly implies
I.2
. Informally, this bound is due to controlling the growth of the moment generating function of
\ln\left(Z_{\beta}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[\ln\left(Z_{\beta}\right)\right]
using the commutation index
\Delta
. We formally prove Eq. (
9
) in

V
Lower Bound for the SYK Maximum Eigenvalue

Finally, we show how commutation properties of local Hamiltonians can give rise to lower bounds on the maximum eigenvalue. In particular, for any random Hamiltonian
\bm{H}=m^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}g_{i}\bm{A}_{i}
where all
\bm{A}_{i}^{2}=\mathbf{I}
, the scaling of the maximum eigenvalue is related to what we call the
commutation degree

h_{\text{comm}}\left(\left\{\bm{A}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{m}\right)
of the operators comprising the Hamiltonian:

h_{\text{comm}}\left(\left\{\bm{A}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{m}\right):=\frac{1}{2}\sup\limits_{i}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\left\lVert\left[\bm{A}_{i},\bm{A}_{j}\right]\right\rVert.

(10)

The name ‘commutation degree’ is derived from the fact that it is the maximal degree of the commutation graph associated with the set
\mathcal{S}=\{\bm{A}_{1},\dots,\bm{A}_{m}\}
; see
Section

B.2
. The commutation degree can be interpreted as controlling the maximal amount of operator spreading of any
\bm{A}_{i}
under the dynamics of
\bm{H}
. Using this intuition we are able to control how sensitive the expected partition function is to the inverse temperature:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\mathbb{E}\Tr[\mathrm{e}^{\beta\bm{H}}]\geq\beta\left(1-\frac{c_{1}\beta^{2}h_{\text{comm}}}{m}\right)\mathbb{E}\Tr[\mathrm{e}^{\beta\bm{H}}],

(11)

where
c_{1}>0
is an absolute constant. Using the lower bound of
\Tr[\mathrm{e}^{\beta\bm{H}}]\leq\exp\left(\beta O(n)+\beta\lambda_{\text{max}}\left(\bm{H}\right)\right)
, we prove in
Appendix

D
that, for all
\beta
,

\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\beta\lambda_{\text{max}}\left(\bm{H}\right)\right)\geq\exp\left(\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}(1-\frac{c_{1}\beta^{2}h_{\text{comm}}}{2m})\right).

(12)

Using the fact that the maximal eigenvalue concentrates (as in, e.g.,
Table

3
) and maximizing the bound over
\beta
then implies:

\mathbb{E}\lambda_{\text{max}}(\bm{H})\geq\frac{\sqrt{m}}{4\sqrt{c_{1}h_{\text{comm}}}}\left(1-16\Delta\right).

(13)

By the same concentration results this bound also holds for
\lambda_{\text{max}}(\bm{H})
with high probability over the disorder, not just in expectation. Intruigingly, the overall scaling depends only on the commutation degree
h_{\text{comm}}
. This quantity (when normalized by
m
) agrees to leading order for both the
q
-body quantum spin glass model
\bm{H}_{q}^{\text{SG}}
and the SYK model
\bm{H}_{q}^{\text{SYK}}
. As long as
\Delta<1/16
—which is true for the former when
q\geq 3
, and always for the latter for sufficiently large
n
—this implies that for
both
models:

\lambda_{\text{max}}\left(\bm{H}_{q}^{\text{SYK}}\right)\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{q}\right),\quad\lambda_{\text{max}}\left(\bm{H}_{q}^{\text{SG}}\right)\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{q}\right).

(14)

We here allow the locality
q
to potentially grow with
n
as
n\to\infty
. This strengthens the previously-known
\Omega\left(\sqrt{n}\right)
scaling for both when
q
is constant
[
19
,
29
,
30
]
. For
q=\omega(\sqrt{n})
it is known that both models exhibit a phase transition in their spectrums to a semicircle law
[
22
,
54
]
which is consistent with our results.

Appendix A
Background on related results for classical spin glass models

Spin glass models are a now-canonical object in the study of disordered systems in physics and mathematics. Properties of the energy landscape of this model feature phase transitions which govern the complexity of the near ground states and have various algorithmic and physical implications. We briefly summarize these important implications here, focusing on the (classical) Ising spin glass model.

In the Ising spin model, configurations
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
are each a point in the space
\Sigma=\{-1,+1\}^{n}
with energy given by the random Hamiltonian

H_{C}(\bm{\sigma})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\binom{n}{p}}}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{p}\leq n}J_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\sigma_{i_{1}}\cdots\sigma_{i_{p}},

(15)

where
J_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
are coefficients drawn i.i.d. from the standard Normal distribution. This model can equivalently be viewed as a qubit or matrix model on the vector space
\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
. Denoting
\bm{Z}_{i}
as the Pauli Z matrix acting on qubit
i
, the Hamiltonian
\bm{H}_{C}
takes the form

\bm{H}_{C}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\binom{n}{p}}}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{p}\leq n}J_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\bm{Z}_{i_{1}}\cdots\bm{Z}_{i_{p}}.

(16)

Note that the eigenvectors of the above Hamiltonian map onto spins in Eq.
15
with energy given by the corresponding eigenvalue. For this model, the (quenched) free energy takes the form

F(\beta)=n^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\log Z_{\beta}\right],\quad Z_{\beta}=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}\in\Sigma}\exp(\beta\sqrt{n}H_{C}\left(\bm{\sigma}\right)),

(17)

with associated Gibbs measure

\mu_{\beta}(A)=Z_{\beta}^{-1}\sum_{\bm{\sigma}\in A}\exp(-\beta\sqrt{n}H_{C}\left(\bm{\sigma}\right)).

(18)

Given that for any
\bm{\sigma}
the random variable
H_{C}(\bm{\sigma})
is distributed as Gaussian with unit variance, one can show that the limiting annealed free energy is

\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-1}\log\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{\beta}\right]=\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-1}\log\left(2^{n}\exp(\beta^{2}n/2\right))=\log(2)+\beta^{2}/2.

(19)

Explicitly calculating the quenched free energy is far more challenging. Nonetheless, Kirkpatrick and Thirumalai
[
55
]

Such a shattering phase is a characteristic of ‘glassiness’ and a source of formal proofs for algorithmic hardness of finding near-ground states. We summarize these findings below.

Evidence of shattering was provided in
[

and only recently fully rigorously proven in

for the large

(20)

Talagrand shows in Theorem 1.1 of
[
52

that

(1-2^{-p})\sqrt{2\log(2)}\leq\beta_{p}\leq\sqrt{2\log(2)}.

(21)

\beta_{p}\to\sqrt{2\log(2)}
in the limit

and is notable for also being the transition point where the quenched and annealed free energies fail to agree for the Random Energy Model
[
57
]
. In the Random Energy Model, the

different configurations each have an energy given by independent draws of a Gaussian random variable with variance
n
. In studying potential ‘glassiness’ in SYK energy landscapes,
[
58
]
study the distribution of low energy eigenvalues of SYK model. There, numerical evidence is presented that extremal eigenvalues of the SYK model have level repulsion, which is also observed in many random matrix theory eigenspectra but which is not a feature of the Random Energy Model.

Failure of the quenched free energy to agree with the annealed free energy is typically an indication that an exponentially small fraction of low energy states dominate contributions to the Gibbs distribution. Note that the quenched free energy for a particular draw of the coefficients is typically close to its expectation (i.e., self-averaging in the terminology of statistical mechanics), but this is not true of the partition function
Z_{\beta}
. Fluctuations in low energy states can cause
Z_{\beta}
to oscillate significantly. When these outliers dominate the contribution to the Gibbs distribution, the annealed free energy can disagree with the quenched free energy.

The point
\beta_{p}
intuitively indicates the transition into a regime where low-energy states are rare but nonetheless dominate the contribution to the Gibbs distribution. This fact has many implications.
[
52
]
shows that whether

More explicitly, treating

\displaystyle\beta>\beta_{p}

\displaystyle\beta>\sqrt{2\log 2}

(25)

where

Similar replica symmetry breaking behavior has also been proven to exist for Sherrington–Kirkpatrick models with a quantum transverse field (i.e.,

We now turn toward the SYK model. Let us denote the corresponding value of

For a set

The commutation index of the set of

Let

Let

k
-local
n

B.2
Commutation graph

Given a set

Definition B.3

(Commutation graph.)

The

•

The vertices of

•

We now introduce a key graph property which reveals the anticommutativity of the operators

Definition B.4

(Lovász theta function.)

\displaystyle\max\ \{\

\displaystyle\Tr\left(\mathbb{J}\bm{X}\right)\ ,\ \bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\ \bm{X}\succeq 0\ ,\ \Tr\left(\bm{X}\right)=1\ ,\ \bm{X}_{jl}=0\ \forall(j,l)\in E\ \}.

(32)

It has dual

\displaystyle\min\ \{\

\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\ \exists\ \bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}\ ,\ \bm{Y}_{jj}=1\ \forall j\ ,\ \bm{Y}_{jl}=0\ \forall(j,l)\notin E\ ,\ \lambda\bm{Y}\succeq\mathbb{J}\ \}.

-a_{1}\cdot g^{(q)}(q)
, and the terms
d>1
are subleading order in
n
since they scale like
a_{d}=\Theta(n^{-(d-1)/2})
by assumption. Thus the constraint
p(q)\geq-1
is satisfied so long as

a_{1}=\frac{C_{1}(n^{-1})}{g^{(q)}(q)}

(59)

for some polynomial
C_{1}
of degree
(q/2-1)
with
C_{1}(0)=1
and
C_{1}(n^{-1})\leq 1
for all
n
.

For
p(q-1)
, all the Hahn polynomials appearing are positive to leading order in
n
, so
p(q-1)
is large and positive eventually. The same holds for any odd value of
x
.

Now consider
p(q-2)
. The
d=1
term is
a_{1}\cdot h^{(q)}(q-2)\cdot n
to leading order in
n
, and the
d=3
term is
-a_{3}\cdot g^{(q)}(q-2)\cdot n^{2}
to leading order. The terms
d>3
are subleading order in
n
since they scale like
a_{d}\cdot\Theta(n^{2})=\Theta(n^{-(d+1)/2})
. Thus we can satisfy the constraint
p(q-2)\geq-1
by picking

\displaystyle a_{3}

\displaystyle=C_{3}(n^{-1})\cdot\frac{h^{(q)}(q-2)}{g^{(q)}(q-2)}\cdot n^{-1}\cdot a_{1}

(60)

\displaystyle=C_{3}(n^{-1})\cdot C_{1}(n^{-1})\cdot\frac{h^{(q)}(q-2)}{g^{(q)}(q-2)}\cdot\frac{1}{g^{(q)}(q)}\cdot n^{-1}

(61)

for some polynomial
C_{3}
of degree
(q/2-1)
with
C_{3}(0)=1
and
C_{3}(n^{-1})\leq 1
for all
n
.

Continue like this for
p(q-3),\dots,p(1)
. For each even
x
, we will set

\displaystyle a_{q-x+1}

\displaystyle=C_{q-x+1}(n^{-1})\cdot\frac{h^{(q)}(x)}{g^{(q)}(x)}\cdot n^{-1}\cdot a_{q-x-1}

(62)

\displaystyle=C_{q-x+1}(n^{-1})\cdot\dots\cdot C_{1}(n^{-1})\cdot\frac{h^{(q)}(x)\cdot h^{(q)}(x+2)\cdot\dots\cdot h^{(q)}(q-2)}{g^{(q)}(x)\cdot g^{(q)}(x+2)\cdot\dots\cdot g^{(q)}(q-2)}\cdot\frac{1}{g^{(q)}(q)}\cdot n^{-q/2+(x/2)}

(63)

For each even
t
,
C_{q-t+1}
is a polynomial of degree
(q/2-1)
satisfying
C_{q-t+1}(0)=1
and
C_{q-t+1}(n^{-1})\leq 1
for all
n
. Notice that

\frac{h^{(q)}(t)}{g^{(q)}(t)}=\frac{(q-t)^{2}}{t}

(64)

and
g^{(q)}(q)=q
, so defining

\hat{a}_{q-x+1}:=\frac{(q-x)^{2}(q-x-2)^{2}\dots 2^{2}}{(q-2)(q-4)\dots x}\cdot\frac{1}{q}

(65)

we can write

a_{q-x+1}=C_{q-x+1}(n^{-1})\cdot\dots\cdot C_{1}(n^{-1})\cdot\hat{a}_{q-x+1}\cdot n^{-q/2+(x/2)}

(66)

Finally, let us look at
p(0)
. For large
n
, we have

\displaystyle p(0)

\displaystyle=a_{q-1}\cdot h^{(q)}(0)\cdot n^{q-1}+\dots

(67)

\displaystyle=C(n^{-1})\cdot\frac{q}{(q-1)!}\cdot\hat{a}_{q-1}\cdot n^{q/2}-c(q)n^{q/2-1}

(68)

where
C=C_{q-1}\dots C_{1}
is a polynomial of degree
(q/2)(q/2-1)
satisfying
C(0)=1
and
C(n^{-1})\leq 1
for all
n
, and
c(q)
is some function of
q
. (Note
h^{(q)}(0)=q/(q-1)!
.) The product in
\hat{a}_{q-1}
telescopes to give

\hat{a}_{q-1}=\frac{1}{q}(q-2)(q-4)\dots 2

(69)

so we get

p(0)=C(n^{-1})\cdot\frac{1}{(q-1)(q-3)\dots 1}\cdot n^{q/2}-c(q)n^{q/2-1}

(70)

as promised. This establishes
B.4
.

Let us now examine
p(0)/n^{q/2}
.

\displaystyle p(0)/n^{q/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{q/2}}\Big(a_{1}\tilde{H}^{n,q}_{1}(0)+a_{3}\tilde{H}^{n,q}_{3}(0)+\dots+a_{q-1}\tilde{H}^{n,q}_{q-1}(0)\Big)

(71)

\displaystyle=\hat{a}_{1}{q\choose 1}{n-q\choose 1}\cdot n^{-q/2}\cdot C_{1}(n^{-1})+\hat{a}_{3}{q\choose 3}{n-q\choose 3}\cdot n^{-q/2-1}\cdot(C_{1}C_{3})(n^{-1})+

(72)

\displaystyle\qquad\dots+\hat{a}_{q-1}{q\choose q-1}{n-q\choose q-1}\cdot n^{-q+1}\cdot(C_{1}\dots C_{q-1})(n^{-1})

(73)

recalling
\tilde{H}^{n,q}_{d}(0)={q\choose d}{n-q\choose d}
. Note the coefficients
\hat{a}_{d}
are independent of
n
. From this expression, we can readily see that
p(0)/n^{q/2}
is a polynomial in
n
of degree
(q/2)(q/2-1)
, establishing
B.5
.

It remains to establish
B.6
. For all
d
,
(C_{1}\dots C_{d})(n^{-1})\leq 1
and
\hat{a}_{d}\leq\hat{a}_{q-1}=2^{q/2-1}(q/2-1)!
. Further, for all
d

{q\choose d}{n-q\choose d}\leq\left(\frac{2e^{q}}{d^{2}}\right)^{d}n^{d}\leq(2e^{q})^{q-1}n^{d}

(74)

using the general bound
{m\choose r}\leq(em/r)^{r}
. Using these facts, we can bound

\displaystyle\big|p(0)/n^{q/2}\big|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{q/2-1}(q/2-1)!\cdot(2e^{q})^{q-1}\cdot\big(n^{-q/2+1}+n^{-q/2+2}+\dots+1\big)

(75)

\displaystyle\leq e^{3q/2-3}q^{3q/2}/4

(76)

using
x!\leq x^{x+1}/e^{x-1}
in the final step.

B.7
Alternative definition of commutation index

Lemma B.8
.

When the left and right test vectors are different, we still have

\Delta(\mathcal{S})\leq\sup_{\|{\ket{u}}\|=\|{\ket{v}}\|=1}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{A}\in\mathcal{S}}\left|{\bra{u}\bm{A}\ket{v}}\right|^{2}=\sup_{\bm{O}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{A}\in\mathcal{S}}\left|{\operatorname{Tr}[\bm{A}\bm{O}]}\right|^{2}}{\|{\bm{O}}\|_{1}^{2}}\leq 16\Delta(\mathcal{S}).

(77)

Proof.

The first inequality is trivial. The middle equality follows from the first by taking the singular value decomposition of
\bm{O}
. To prove the final inequality, for every
u,v
, define polarizations

\displaystyle\ket{s}:=\frac{\ket{u}+s\ket{v}}{2}\quad\text{where}\quad s=\pm 1,\pm i.

(78)

Then, for each
\bm{A}
,

\displaystyle\left|{\bra{u}\bm{A}\ket{v}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|{4\mathbb{E}_{s}s\bra{s}\bm{A}\ket{s}}\right|^{2}

(79)

\displaystyle\leq 16\mathbb{E}_{s}\left|{\bra{s}\bm{A}\ket{s}}\right|^{2}.

(80)

Hence,

\displaystyle\sup_{\|{\ket{u}}\|=\|{\ket{v}}\|=1}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{A}\in\mathcal{S}}\left|{\bra{u}\bm{A}\ket{v}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 16\sup_{\|{\ket{u}}\|=\|{\ket{v}}\|=1}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{A}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}_{s}\left|{\bra{s}\bm{A}\ket{s}}\right|^{2}

(81)

\displaystyle=16\sup_{\|{\ket{u}}\|=\|{\ket{v}}\|=1}\mathbb{E}_{s}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{A}\in\mathcal{S}}\left|{\bra{s}\bm{A}\ket{s}}\right|^{2}

(82)

\displaystyle\leq 16\Delta(\mathcal{S}).

(83)

Appendix C
Annealed approximation and concentration results

Consider the model

\displaystyle\bm{H}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}g_{i}\bm{A}_{i},

(84)

where
g_{i}\sim_{i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
are standard independent Gaussians and
\bm{A}_{i}
are deterministic matrices. The
Gibbs state

\bm{\rho}_{\beta}
at inverse temperature
\beta
is defined by

\bm{\rho}_{\beta}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\sqrt{n}\bm{H}}}{Z_{\beta}}\quad,\quad Z_{\beta}=\Tr\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\sqrt{n}\bm{H}}\right),

(85)

where
Z_{\beta}
is called the
partition function
at inverse temperature
\beta
. The factor
\sqrt{n}
ensures that the free energy is extensive and scales proportionally to
n
.

In this section we show that the commutation index of the terms
\bm{A}_{i}
has an important effect on the concentration properties of the random model
\bm{H}
. Denote the commutation index by

\Delta:=\Delta(\{\bm{A}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}).

(86)

Recall that this quantity characterizes the variance of the energy with respect to a fixed state
\bm{\rho}
:

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{\rho}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{H}}\left|{\Tr(\bm{H}\bm{\rho})}\right|^{2}=\sup_{\bm{\rho}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\Tr(\bm{A}_{i}\bm{\rho}))^{2}=\Delta.

(87)

The value of
\Delta
has implications for relations between the normalized quenched and annealed free energies:

\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\ln{Z_{\beta}}}_{\text{quenched}}\quad vs.\quad\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\ln{\mathbb{E}Z_{\beta}}}_{\text{annealed}}.

(88)

The quenched free energy is physical but hard to calculate while the annealed free energy is much easier to calculate but nonphysical. The first result is that this variance quantity controls the difference between the two.

Theorem C.1

(Quenched and annealed free energy)
.

\displaystyle n^{-1}\mathbb{E}\ln{Z_{\beta}}\leq n^{-1}\ln\mathbb{E}Z_{\beta}\leq n^{-1}\mathbb{E}\ln{Z_{\beta}}+4\beta^{2}\Delta.

(89)

The first inequality always holds, and the non-trivial part is the second inequality.
Therefore, a small variance
\Delta\ll\beta^{-2}
means that the annealed free energy well-approximates the quenched free energy, which indicates the absence of spin glass order
[
13
]
.
The next three results give concentration of expectation values, energy, and two-point correlators of the thermal state of
\bm{H}
. Two-point correlators are of special interest in the study of the SYK model
[
13
,
58
,
69
,
70
]
. Concentration results for Lipschitz bounded functions of the spectrum of the SYK model have also been established in
[
71
]
.

Theorem C.2

(Concentration of expectation values)
.

For any fixed bounded Hermitian operator
\bm{X}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\big|\Tr\left(\bm{X}\bm{\rho}_{\beta}\right)-\mathbb{E}{\Tr\left(\bm{X}\bm{\rho}_{\beta}\right)}\big|\geq t\big)\leq 2e^{-t^{2}/(18\beta^{2}||\bm{X}||^{2}\Delta)}.

(90)

Theorem C.3

(Concentration of energy)
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\big|\Tr\left(\bm{H}\bm{\rho}_{\beta}\right)-\mathbb{E}{\Tr\left(\bm{H}\bm{\rho}_{\beta}\right)}\big|\geq t\big)\leq 4\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\Delta}\left(\sqrt{\frac{t^{2}}{12\beta^{2}n}+\alpha^{2}}-\alpha\right)\right)

(91)

where
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}\big(1/(4\beta^{2}n)+\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{\max}(\bm{H})]^{2}\big)
.

Theorem C.4

(Concentration of two-point correlators)
.

For any fixed bounded operators
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
, denoting

\bm{Y}\left(\tau\right)=\exp\left(i\sqrt{n}\bm{H}\tau\right)\bm{Y}\exp\left(-i\sqrt{n}\bm{H}\tau\right)

(92)

for any
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\frac{1}{2}\big|\Tr\left(\bm{X}\bm{Y}\left(\tau\right)\bm{\rho}_{\beta}\right)-\mathbb{E}{\Tr\left(\bm{X}\bm{Y}\left(\tau\right)\bm{\rho}_{\beta}\right)}\pm\text{h.c.}\big|\geq t\big)\leq 2e^{-t^{2}/(6n(5\beta^{2}+16\tau^{2})\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert^{2}\Delta)}.

(93)

Instantiating for example Theorems
C.1
,
C.2
,
C.3
,
C.4
with the upper bound on the commutation index of Majoranas in
B.1
yields the following results for the SYK model.

Corollary C.1
.

(SYK model is annealed.)

For the SYK model
\bm{H}^{\syk}_{q}
where
q
is even,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\ln{Z_{\beta}}\leq\frac{1}{n}\ln\mathbb{E}Z_{\beta}\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\ln{Z_{\beta}}+\mathcal{O}_{q}(\beta^{2}n^{-q/2}),

(94)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\big|\Tr\left(\bm{X}\bm{\rho}_{\beta}\right)-\mathbb{E}{\Tr\left(\bm{X}\bm{\rho}_{\beta}\right)}\big|\geq t\big)\leq 2e^{-\Omega_{q}(\beta^{-2}n^{q/2-1}t^{2})},

(95)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\big|\Tr\left(\bm{H}^{\syk}_{q}\bm{\rho}_{\beta}\right)-\mathbb{E}{\Tr\left(\bm{H}^{\syk}_{q}\bm{\rho}_{\beta}\right)}\big|\geq t\big)\leq\begin{cases}4e^{-\Omega_{q}\left(\beta^{-1}n^{q/2-1/2}t\right)}&t=\Omega\left(1+\beta n\right)\\
4e^{-\Omega_{q}\left(\min\left(1,\beta^{-2}n^{-2}\right)n^{q/2}t^{2}\right)}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

(96)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\frac{1}{2}\big|\Tr\left(\bm{X}\bm{Y}\left(\tau\right)\bm{\rho}_{\beta}\right)-\mathbb{E}{\Tr\left(\bm{X}\bm{Y}\left(\tau\right)\bm{\rho}_{\beta}\right)}\pm\text{h.c.}\big|\geq t\big)\leq 2e^{-\Omega_{q}\left(\min\left(\beta^{-2},\tau^{-2}\right)n^{q/2-1}t^{2}\right)}.

(97)

\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
are any fixed bounded operators.

Importantly, the above result shows that for the standard SYK model with
q=4
, the quenched free energy in the limit of
n\to\infty
always equal its annealed approximation for physical temperatures where
\beta=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
. This stands in stark contrast with spin glasses where a transition occurs for some critical temperature
\beta_{p}
into a clustered or ‘glassy’ phase (see
Appendix

A
).

C.1
,
C.2
,
C.3
,
C.4
for concentration of various observables and free energies.

C.1
Preliminaries

Let us first state a useful fact about Lipschitz functions of Gaussian variables.

Fact C.1

(Gaussian concentration of Lipschitz functions, Theorem 2.26 of
[
72
]
)
.

Let
\bm{g}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{m})
be i.i.d. standard Gaussian variables, and
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

L
-Lipschitz. Then for any
t\geq 0
:

\mathbb{P}\big(|f(\bm{g})-\mathbb{E}{f(\bm{g})}|\geq t\big)\leq 2e^{-t^{2}/(2L^{2})}

(98)

We also state a useful fact on the concentration of the operator norm of random matrices of the form of
\bm{H}
.

Fact C.2

(Concentration of the maximal eigenvalue
[
73
, Corollary 4.14]
)
.

Let
\lambda_{\text{max}}(\bm{H})
be the maximal eigenvalue of
\bm{H}
. We have:

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\lambda_{\text{max}}(\bm{H})-\mathbb{E}\lambda_{\text{max}}(\bm{H})\geq t\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\Delta}\right).

(99)

In the course of proving our results we will also use an equivalent formulation of
C.1
that follows from its sub-Gaussianity
[
74
,
75
,
76
]
.

Lemma C.1

(Sub-Gaussian MGF bound, Lemma 1.5 of Ref.
[
74
]
)
.

Given a random variable
X
with sub-Gaussian concentration bound

\mathbb{P}\big(|X-\mathbb{E}{X}|\geq t\big)\leq 2e^{-t^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})},

(100)

it holds that

\mathbb{E}{\left[\exp\left(t\left(X-\mathbb{E}{X}\right)\right)\right]}\leq\exp\left(4\sigma^{2}t^{2}\right).

(101)

C.2
Proof of
C.1

We directly compute the derivatives of
\ln{Z_{\beta}}
with respect to each Gaussian
g_{i}

\displaystyle\partial_{g_{i}}\ln{Z_{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}}\Tr[\partial_{g_{i}}e^{\beta\sqrt{n}\bm{H}}]

(102)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}}\Tr\left[\beta\sqrt{\frac{n}{m}}\int_{0}^{1}e^{\beta\sqrt{n}\bm{H}(1-s)}A_{i}e^{\beta\sqrt{n}\bm{H}}ds\right]

(Derivative of matrix exponential
[
77
]
)

\displaystyle=\beta\sqrt{\frac{n}{m}}\Tr[A_{i}\bm{\rho}_{\beta}].

(Cyclic property of trace)

Therefore, the Lipschitz constant
L
of
\ln{Z_{\beta}}
with respect to the disorder has the gradient bound:

L^{2}\leq\frac{\beta^{2}n}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Tr[A_{i}\bm{\rho}_{\beta}]^{2}\leq\beta^{2}n\Delta.

(103)

Now we can bound

\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{\beta}\right]}{\exp(\mathbb{E}[\ln{Z_{\beta}}])}=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\ln{Z_{\beta}}-\mathbb{E}[\ln{Z_{\beta}}])\right]\leq\exp(4\beta^{2}n\Delta).

(104)

The inequality uses
C.1
and
C.1
with
t=1
. Taking logarithms and rearrange to obtain

\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta}]\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\ln{Z_{\beta}}]+4\beta^{2}\Delta,

(105)

as stated.

C.3
Proof of
C.2

The result once again follows from a Lipschitz bound. We use the well-known expression for the derivative of a matrix exponential
[
77
]
:

\partial_{g_{i}}\exp\left(\bm{H}\right)=\int\limits_{0}^{1}\exp\left(t\bm{H}\right)\left(\partial_{g_{i}}\bm{H}\right)\exp\left(\left(1-t\right)\bm{H}\right)\,dt.

(106)

From the chain rule we then have:

\partial_{g_{i}}\Tr\left(\bm{\rho}_{\beta}\bm{X}\right)=\frac{\beta\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{m}}\left(Z_{\beta}^{-1}\Tr\left(\bm{X}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\exp\left(t\beta\sqrt{n}\bm{H}\right)\bm{A}_{j}\exp\left(\left(1-t\right)\beta\sqrt{n}\bm{H}\right)\,dt\right)-\Tr{\left(\bm{X}\bm{\rho}_{\beta}\right)}\Tr{\left(\bm{A}_{j}\bm{\rho}_{\beta}\right)}\right).

(107)

Consider now the operator:

\bm{\sigma}_{\beta}\equiv Z_{\beta}^{-1}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\exp\left(t\beta\sqrt{n}\bm{H}\right)\bm{X}\exp\left(\left(1-t\right)\beta\sqrt{n}\bm{H}\right)\,dt.

(108)

We can check that
\bm{\sigma}_{\beta}
has trace norm bounded by
\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert
. Denoting by
\Sigma_{i}\left(\cdot\right)
the
i
th singular value of
\cdot
in nonincreasing order, we have by the majorization inequality
[
78
]
:

\sum\limits_{i}\Sigma_{i}\left(\bm{A}\bm{B}\right)\leq\sum\limits_{i}\Sigma_{i}\left(\bm{A}\right)\Sigma_{i}\left(\bm{B}\right)

(109)

that

\begin{split}\sum\limits_{i}\Sigma_{i}\left(\exp\left(t\beta\sqrt{n}\bm{H}\right)\bm{X}\exp\left(\left(1-t\right)\beta\sqrt{n}\bm{H}\right)\right)&\leq\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert\sum\limits_{i}\Sigma_{i}\left(\exp\left(t\beta\sqrt{n}\bm{H}\right)\right)\Sigma_{i}\left(\exp\left(\left(1-t\right)\beta\sqrt{n}\bm{H}\right)\right)\\
&\leq\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert Z_{\beta}.\end{split}

(110)

This implies that
\bm{\sigma}_{\beta}
has trace norm bounded by
\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert
. However,
\bm{\sigma}_{\beta}
is Hermitian but not necessarily positive semidefinite. We instead consider
\bm{\sigma}_{\beta}
as the difference of two positive semidefinite matrices:

\bm{\sigma}_{\beta}=\bm{\sigma}_{\beta}^{+}-\bm{\sigma}_{\beta}^{-},

(111)

where each of
\bm{\sigma}_{\beta}^{\pm}
is a (unnormalized) quantum state. By the cyclic property of the trace we can then write:

\partial_{g_{i}}\Tr\left(\bm{\rho}_{\beta}\bm{X}\right)=\frac{\beta\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{m}}\left(\Tr\left(\bm{\sigma}_{\beta}^{+}\bm{A}_{j}\right)-\Tr\left(\bm{\sigma}_{\beta}^{-}\bm{A}_{j}\right)-\Tr\left(\bm{X}\bm{\rho}_{\beta}\right)\Tr\left(\bm{A}_{j}\bm{\rho}_{\beta}\right)\right).

(112)

We thus have:

\displaystyle\big|\big|\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{g}}\Tr\left(\bm{\rho}_{\beta}\bm{X}\right)\big|\big|_{2}^{2}

(115)

The result then follows from
C.1
.

Appendix D
Lower bound on SYK optimum

Consider a Hamiltonian weighted by i.i.d. Gaussian coefficients

\displaystyle\bm{H}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}g_{i}\bm{A}_{i}\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbb{E}g^{2}_{i}=1

(140)

where we here assume that all
\bm{A}_{i}^{2}=\|{\bm{A}_{i}}\|^{2}\mathbf{I}
. We show a lower bound for the maximal eigenvalue for such a Hamiltonian.

Theorem D.1

(Lower bound on the maximal eigenvalue)
.

There is an absolute constant
c_{1}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\lambda_{\text{max}}(\bm{H})\geq\frac{\sqrt{m}}{4\sqrt{c_{1}h_{\text{comm}}}}\left(h_{\text{glo}}^{2}-16\Delta\right).

(141)

The commutation index
\Delta=\Delta(\{\bm{A}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m})
is defined in
B.1
. We have also defined the
commutation degree

h_{\text{comm}}:=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\|{\bm{A}_{j}}\|\cdot\|{[\bm{A}_{i},\bm{A}_{j}]}\|}{\|{\bm{A}_{i}}\|}

(142)

and the
global norm

h_{\text{glo}}:=\sqrt{\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\|{\bm{A}_{i}}\|^{2}}.

(143)

Our results in the main text are only reported in the case all
\|{\bm{A}_{i}}\|=1
for simplicity. Note that here, both
h_{comm}
and
h_{glo}
feature a quadratic sum (instead of a linear sum) due to the randomness of the Hamiltonian.

This immediately gives lower bounds for the SYK maximal eigenvalue.

Corollary D.1
.

With high probability over the disorder, the maximum eigenvalue of the SYK model is

\lambda_{\max}(\bm{H}^{\syk}_{q})=\Omega(\sqrt{n}/q).

(144)

Proof.

We have
h_{\text{comm}}=\mathcal{O}(q\binom{n-1}{q-1})
and
m=\binom{n}{q}
for SYK.
\Delta=o\left(1\right)
by
B.2
. The result then follows from
D.1
and the concentration of the maximal eigenvalue (
C.2
).
∎

Our theorem also lower bounds the maximal eigenvalue of a
k
-local quantum spin glass:

\bm{H}_{k}^{\text{SG}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3^{k}\binom{n}{k}}}\sum\limits_{k\text{-local }\bm{\sigma}}g_{\bm{\sigma}}\bm{\sigma}.

(145)

.

With high probability over the disorder, the maximum eigenvalue of the
k
-local quantum spin glass model is

Proof.

We have
h_{\text{comm}}=\mathcal{O}(k3^{k-1}\binom{n-1}{k-1})
and
m=3^{k}\binom{n}{k}

145
).
\Delta
is also less than

when

. The result then follows from
D.1
and the concentration of the maximal eigenvalue (

).

The strategy to prove

is to lower bound the optimum by calculating the exponential

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\beta\mathbb{E}\lambda_{\text{max}}(\bm{H})}

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{E}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\lambda_{\text{max}}(\bm{H})}

(Concentration of the maximal eigenvalue:
C.2
)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\lambda_{i}(\bm{H})}=\mathbb{E}\operatorname{\overline{Tr}}[\mathrm{e}^{\beta\bm{H}}],

(147)

where we use
\operatorname{\overline{Tr}}\left[\cdot\right]:=\frac{1}{N}\Tr\left[\cdot\right]
to denote the normalized trace. We begin by lower bounding the right-hand side.

Lemma D.1

(Lower bounds on the exponential)
.

There is an absolute constant
c_{1}
such that for each
\beta,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\operatorname{\overline{Tr}}[\mathrm{e}^{\beta\bm{H}}]\geq\exp\left(\frac{\beta^{2}h_{\text{glo}}^{2}}{2}(1-\frac{c_{1}\beta^{2}h_{\text{comm}}}{2m})\right).

In proving Lemma

we will use the below two facts.

Fact D.1

(Integration by parts)
.

For standard Gaussian random variable
g
and a function

whose derivative is absolutely integrable w.r.t. the Gaussian measure, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[gf(g)]=\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(g)].

Fact D.2

(Multivariate Hölder for random matricese.g.,
[
38

)
.

For any family
(\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{k})
of square random matrices, possibly statistically dependent,
the product satisfies the trace inequality

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\operatorname{\overline{Tr}}\left|{\prod_{i=1}^{k}\bm{X}_{i}}\right|={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\prod_{i=1}^{k}\bm{X}_{i}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{1}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{k}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\bm{X}_{i}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{p_{i}}\quad\text{whenever}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{p_{i}}=1\quad\text{and}\quad p_{i}\geq 0,

(150)

where

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\bm{X}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{p}:=\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\operatorname{\overline{Tr}}[\left|{\bm{X}}\right|^{p}]\right)^{1/p}.

(151)

.

Take derivative w.r.t.
\beta

\syk_{q}
model is a random ensemble of Hamiltonians defined by

\bm{H}^{\syk}_{q}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\choose q}}\sum_{\bm{A}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{q}}g_{\bm{A}}\bm{A}\quad,\quad g_{\bm{A}}\sim_{i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

(164)

Theorem E.1
.

(SYK model low-energy states have high circuit complexity.)

Let
\operatorname{circ}(G)
denote the set of unitaries generated by quantum circuits with at most
G
gates each taken from a finite universal set of
2
-local unitary gates. Fix an arbitrary initial state
\ket{\phi}
.
With high probability, for any even
q\geq 2
, it holds that the minimum circuit complexity to construct a state achieving at least
t\sqrt{n}
on
\bm{H}^{\syk}_{q}
is at least

\min\left\{G:\;\exists U\in\operatorname{circ}(G),\;\bra{\phi}U^{\dagger}\bm{H}^{\syk}_{q}U\ket{\phi}\geq t\sqrt{n}\right\}=\tilde{\Omega}_{q}(n^{(q/2)+1}t^{2}).

(165)

Meanwhile, we can recall
D.1
from
Appendix

D
, which gives a lower bound
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{H}^{\syk}_{q})=\Omega_{q}(\sqrt{n})
on the maximum eigenvalue of SYK.

The proof of
E.1
will proceed via a concentration argument, followed by a union bound over the circuit family. This resembles the circuit lower bound of
[
79
, Appendix D]
. We establish the necessary concentration now, which relies crucially on
B.1
concerning the commutation index of low-degree Majorana operators.

Lemma E.1
.

Fix any state
|\psi\rangle
. The energy
\langle\psi|\bm{H}^{\syk}_{q}|\psi\rangle
sharply concentrates:

\mathbb{P}\big(\langle\psi|\bm{H}^{\syk}_{q}|\psi\rangle\geq t\big)\leq\exp(-\Omega_{q}(n^{q/2}t^{2})).

(166)

Proof.

Since a sum of Gaussians is Gaussian, we have

\mathbb{P}\big(\langle\psi|\bm{H}^{\syk}_{q}|\psi\rangle\geq t\big)\leq\exp(-t^{2}/2\sigma^{2})

(167)

where

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{n\choose q}}\sum_{\bm{A}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{q}}\langle\psi|\bm{A}|\psi\rangle^{2}\leq\Delta(\mathcal{S}^{n}_{q})\leq\frac{(q)!}{2^{q/2}(q/2)!}n^{-q/2}+\mathcal{O}(e^{\mathcal{O}(q\log{q})}n^{-(q/2)-1})=\mathcal{O}_{q}(n^{-q/2}).

(168)

Here
\Delta
is from
B.1
, and we used the upper bound of
B.1
.
∎

Proof of
E.1
.

Let
M
be the number of gates in the universal gate set. Then the number of circuits in
\operatorname{circ}(G)
is at most

|\operatorname{circ}(G)|\leq\left(M{n\choose 2}\right)^{G}=\exp(\mathcal{O}(G\log(n))).

(169)

Performing a union bound on
E.1
yields

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left[\max_{U\in\operatorname{circ}(G)}\bra{\phi}U^{\dagger}\bm{H}^{\syk}_{q}U\ket{\phi}\geq t\sqrt{n}\right]\leq\exp\left(-\Omega_{q}(n^{q/2+1}t^{2})+\mathcal{O}(G\log(n))\right).\end{split}

(170)

For this to be non-vanishing in
n
, it requires

G=\Omega_{q}(n^{q/2+1}t^{2}/\log{n}).

(171)

∎

Remark E.1.1
.

The proof above can be extended to gates with continuous parameters by forming an
\epsilon
-net over the gates. This comes at the cost of additional
\log(1/\epsilon)
factors in the bound of
E.1
.

Remark E.1.2
.

A similar union bound can be applied to show that any state from the set of Gaussian states cannot be a near ground state for the SYK Hamiltonian for
q\geq 4
, since an
\epsilon
-net over the set of Gaussian states has cardinality
\exp(\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}+\operatorname{poly}\log(1/\epsilon)))
. This reproduces results from prior works
[
80
,
29
]
.

E.1
Relation to NLTS results

Our circuit lower bound is closely related to the study of ‘no low-energy trivial states’ (NLTS) Hamiltonians. Introduced in
[
51
]
, a Hamiltonian
H=\sum_{i}g_{i}\bm{A}_{i}
has the NLTS property if there is no constant-depth circuit preparing a state whose energy is above the ground energy by less than some constant fraction of the
\ell_{1}
norm
\sum_{i}|g_{i}|
. Such Hamiltonians were first proven to exist in
[
36
]
using quantum LDPC codes.

The circuit lower bounds we give are not quite comparable to the traditional notion of NLTS. This is because we compare the energy of our low-energy states to the Hamiltonian’s maximum eigenvalue rather than the
\ell_{1}
norm of the coefficients. Unlike the quantum code Hamiltonian studied in
[
36
,
37
]
, the SYK model is highly frustrated and thus the operator norm and
\ell_{1}
norms have vastly different scalings:
\Theta(n^{2})
and
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
, respectively.

Despite these differences from the standard NLTS setting, the circuit lower bounds we can establish are much stronger in two ways when compared to current progress on NLTS
[
81
,
82
,
83
,
37
,
36
]
. First, our circuit lower bounds hold for states at
any
energy which is a constant fraction of the ground state energy, rather than for states below some constant-fraction energy threshold. Second, we can achieve arbitrary polynomial circuit depth lower bounds, whereas current constructions of NLTS only give a logarithmic depth lower bound.

E.2
Other notions of non-triviality

Though our focus so far has been on quantum circuit lower bounds, our results readily generalize to lower bounds for other classes of ansatzes via the construction of covering nets.
We begin by discussing tensor networks, focusing on matrix product states (MPSs) as a particular example. Implemented at any finite precision the number of configurations of a matrix product state on
n
sites grows with the
bond dimension

\chi
as
\left\lvert\left\{\ket{\psi_{j}}\right\}\right\rvert=\exp\left(\operatorname{\Theta}\left(\chi^{2}+\log n\right)\right)
.
It is thus apparent from the same argument that the minimum bond dimension such that there is an MPS achieving an energy
t\sqrt{n}
is

\chi=\operatorname{\Omega}_{q}\left(n^{q/4+1/2}t\right)

(172)

with high probability.
Similarly, a classical neural network representation of the state with
W
weights has a number of configurations growing as
\left\lvert\left\{\ket{\psi_{j}}\right\}\right\rvert=\exp\left(\operatorname{\Theta}\left(W\right)\right)
,
yielding the growth condition to achieve an energy
t\sqrt{n}
with high probability:

W=\operatorname{\Omega}_{q}\left(n^{q/2+1}t^{2}\right).

(173)

E.3
Product state approximations for spin Hamiltonians

It is worth pointing out that there cannot be a
k
-local spin Hamiltonian with the property in
E.1
. For any traceless
k
-local spin Hamiltonian
\bm{H}
, there is a product state achieving energy at least
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{H})/3^{k}
. The argument is imported from
[
24
, proof of Theorem 2]
, and the proof technique bears a remarkable resemblence to the classical shadows protocol
[
84
]
, which provides a learning algorithm for
k
-local spin operators.

Proposition E.1
.

For any
k
-local Hamiltonian
\bm{H}
on
n
qubits, there is a product state
|\psi\rangle
achieving energy

\langle\psi|\bm{H}|\psi\rangle\geq\lambda_{\max}(\bm{H})/3^{k}.

(174)

Proof.

Let
|\phi\rangle
be the true (possibly entangled) maximum-energy state achieving

\langle\phi|\bm{H}|\phi\rangle=\lambda_{\max}

(175)

For each qubit, pick a random basis out of
\{\sigma_{X},\sigma_{Y},\sigma_{Z}\}
, and measure in this basis. This gives a product state of single-qubit stabilizer states. Let
\rho
be the resulting ensemble of pure product states we will analyze
\Tr\left(\bm{H}\rho\right)
. It turns out that

\rho=\mathcal{E}_{1/3}^{\otimes n}(|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|)

(176)

where
\mathcal{E}_{p}
is the depolarizing channel

\mathcal{E}_{p}(\tau)=p\tau+(1-p)\mathbbm{1}

(177)

Thus

\Tr\left(\bm{H}\rho\right)=\Tr\left(\bm{H}\mathcal{E}_{1/3}^{\otimes n}(|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|)\right)=\Tr\left(\mathcal{E}_{1/3}^{\otimes n}(\bm{H})|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|\right)=\langle\phi|\big(\bm{H}/3^{k}\big)|\phi\rangle=\lambda_{\max}(\bm{H})/3^{k}

(178)

Here we used that
\bm{H}
is traceless and
k
-local.
∎
