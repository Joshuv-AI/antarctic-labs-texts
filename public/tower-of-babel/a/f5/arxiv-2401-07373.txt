Non-convexity of level sets for k k -Hessian equations in convex ring Thanks: The first author is supported by NSFC Grants No.12141105.
Zhizhang Wang, Ling Xiao

Abstract
. In this paper we construct explicit examples that show the sublevel sets of the solution of a k k -Hessian equation defined on a convex ring do not have to be convex.



1.
Introduction

A domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a
convex ring
if

\Omega=\Omega_{1}\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{2},

where
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
are two bounded convex domains in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\overline{\Omega}_{2}\subset\Omega_{1}.
In this paper, we will concern with the non-convexity of sublevel sets for the solution
u
to
the
k
-Hessian equation defined on a convex ring

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} F_{k}(D^{2}u)&=C>0\,\,&\mbox{in $\Omega=\Omega_{1}\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{2},$}\\
u&=0\,\,&\mbox{on $\partial\Omega_{1},$}\\
u&=-1\,\,&\mbox{on $\partial\Omega_{2}.$}\end{aligned}\right.

Here,
0<C\leq C_{0}
,
C_{0}=C_{0}(\Omega)
is a positive constant depending on the domain
\Omega,
and the
k
-Hessian operator
F_{k}
is defined by

F_{k}[u]=\sigma_{k}(D^{2}u)=\sigma_{k}(\lambda(D^{2}u)),

where
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n})
denotes the eigenvalues of the Hessian matrix of
D^{2}u,
and
\sigma_{k}
is the
k
-th elementary symmetric function on

\mathbb{R}^{n}.

It is believed that solutions of boundary value problems for elliptic equations often inherit important geometric properties of the domain with the influence of the structures of the corresponding equations. Therefore, a typical question to ask is

Question 1.1
.

If
u
is a non-positive solution to equation (
1.1
), is it true that the sublevel set of
u,

that is,
\{x\mid u\leq c\}
are all convex?

For
k=n
, since the admissible solution for (
1.1
) is strictly convex, it is trivial that the above question has a positive answer. However, for
k<n
, the admissible solutions are strictly
k
-convex, the above question becomes very interesting.

In the literature, for general nonlinear equations

(1.2)

F(D^{2}u,Du,u,x)=0,

Question
1.1
has been studied intensively.

The convexity of level-sets of solutions for harmonic equations was first studied by Gabriel
[
9
]
. Lewis
[
14
]
extended Gabriel’s results to
p
-harmonic functions. Caffarelli and Spruck
[
6
]
treated this problem for inhomogeneous Laplace equations that are in connection with free boundary problems. Kawhol
[
12
]
proposed an approach of using quasi-concave envelop to study the convexity of the level-set of solutions to certain equations. Colesanti-Salani
[
7
]
carried out this approach for a class of elliptic equations. The technique was extended by Cuoghi-Salani
[
8
]
and Longinetti-Salani
[
15
]
for general equations (
1.2
) defined in convex ring under various structure conditions on
F
. In
[
1
]
, Bianchini-Longinetti-Salani furthered this technique and proved the convexity of level-sets for solutions to (
1.2
) with milder structure conditions on
F
.

A different approach to study the convexity of level-sets of solutions is to use the microscopic convexity principle. In particular, the constant rank theorem for the second fundamental forms of level sets of solutions to certain type of quasilinear equations was established by Korevaar
[
13
]
, see also Xu
[
24
]
. For
p
-harmonic function, the corresponding constant rank theorem is proved by Ma-Ou-Zhang
[
17
]
. More specifically, they gave a positive lower bound for the Gauss curvature of the convex level set of
p
-harmonic functions that depends on the Gauss curvature of
\partial\Omega
. In
[
3
]
, Chang-Ma-Yang proved a similar result for inhomogeneous Laplace equations. Later, Bian-Guan-Ma-Xu
[
2
]
and Guan-Xu
[
10
]
gave a lower bound for the second fundamental form of the level surface of solutions to (
1.2
) in convex ring for a large class of elliptic operators
F
by establishing the constant rank theorem.

It is equally interesting to find examples of solutions of PDEs in convex ring that have non-convex level-sets. When
n=2,
Monneau-Shahgholian
[
18
]
showed that there exists a solution
u
to the equation
\Delta u=f(u)
defined in a convex ring
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with level-sets not all convex. Later, Hamel-Nadirashvili-Sire
[
11
]
constructed examples for the same equation but in arbitrary dimensions. Moreover, the conditions that the function
f
needs to satisfy in
[
11
]
are more relaxed than in
[
18
]
. However, to the best of the authors knowledge, it seems that there is no such examples for fully nonlinear equations in literature. In this paper, we extend the result of
[
11
]
to
k
-Hessian equations (
1.1
) and construct such examples.

Before we state our main result, we need the following definition.

Definition 1.2
.

For a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a function
u\in C^{2}(\Omega)

is called strictly
k
-convex if the
eigenvalues
\lambda(D^{2}u)=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n})
of the hessian
D^{2}u
is in
\Gamma_{k}
for all
x\in\Omega,
where

\Gamma_{k}
is the Garding’s cone

\Gamma_{k}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\sigma_{j}(\lambda)>0,j=1,\cdots,k\}.

Let us consider the
k
-Hessian equations
in a convex ring
\Omega=\Omega_{1}\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{2},

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \sigma_{k}(D^{2}u)&=1\,\,&\mbox{in $\Omega=\Omega_{1}\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{2},$}\\
u&=0\,\,&\mbox{on $\partial\Omega_{1},$}\\
u&=-M\,\,&\mbox{on $\partial\Omega_{2},$}\end{aligned}\right.

where
M>0
is a positive real number. For any classical solution
u
of (
1.3
), we define the continuous function

\tilde{u}\in C(\overline{\Omega}_{1})
by

\tilde{u}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &u(x)\,\,&\mbox{if $x\in\overline{\Omega},$}\\
&-M\,\,&\mbox{if $x\in\Omega_{2}.$}\end{aligned}\right.

We say that
u
is
quasiconvex
in
\Omega
if
\tilde{u}
is so in
\Omega_{1},
that is,
if the sublevel sets

\bar{\Omega}^{\lambda}:=\{x\in\Omega_{1}:\tilde{u}(x)\leq\lambda\}

are convex for all
\lambda\leq 0.

Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Omega_{1}
be any smooth bounded convex domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}.
Then there exits a constant
M_{1}=M_{1}(\Omega_{1})>0

such that for all
M>M_{1},
there are some smooth convex rings
\Omega=\Omega_{1}\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{2}
for which problem (
1.3
) with
n\geq 2k

has a unique solution
u
that is NOT quasiconvex.

Remark 1.4
.

Our technique can be extended to the case when the right hand side of (
1.3
) is not a constant. Here, we use the constant function to keep the proof clean. At this moment, we do not know if Theorem
1.3
is true for
2k>n
. We think it may be an interesting problem to investigate.

The problem (
1.3
) can be rewritten to (
1.1
). In fact, suppose
u
is the solution of (
1.3
), let
u_{1}=u/M
then
u_{1}
satisfies (
1.1
) with

C=1/(M)^{k}
. We can also rescale the domain
\Omega
to keep the right hand side of the equation to be 1. In particular, we let

u_{2}(y)=\frac{u(\sqrt{M}y)}{M}.

Then
u_{2}
satisfies (
1.1
) with
C=1
while the domain becomes
\Omega/\sqrt{M}.

We follow the frame work of
[
11
]
to construct counterexamples for
k
-Hessian equations. Our biggest obstacle is that the pure interior estimates are not true for
k
-Hessian equations when
k\geq 3
(see
[
19
]
). Therefore, we adapt the ideas of Hessian measure (see
[
21
,
22
]
) to overcome this difficulty, which is the novelty of this paper.

2.
solvability of the Dirichlet problem

In this section, we will consider the solvability of the following Dirichlet problem

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \sigma_{k}(D^{2}u)&=1\,\,&\mbox{in $\Omega^{\epsilon}:=\Omega_{1}\setminus\overline{B}_{\epsilon}(0),$}\\
u&=0\,\,&\mbox{on $\partial\Omega_{1},$}\\
u&=-M\,\,&\mbox{on $\partial B_{\epsilon}(0).$}\end{aligned}\right.

Here
B_{\epsilon}(0)\subset\Omega_{1}
is a ball centered at the origin with radius
\epsilon
and
M\geq M_{1}(\Omega_{1})>0
is a positive real number. In this paper, we will always assume
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0})
to be a small constant.

2.1.
Choice of
M_{1}
and
C^{0}
estimates

In this subsection, we will discuss the choice of
M_{1}
as well as the
C^{0}
estimates of the solution to (
2.1
), which we denote by
u^{\epsilon}.

First, let us consider the following Dirichlet problem

(2.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \sigma_{k}(D^{2}u)&=1\,\,&\mbox{in $\Omega_{1}$,}\\
u&=0\,\,&\mbox{on $\partial\Omega_{1}.$}\end{aligned}\right.

By Theorem 1 in
[
5
]
, we know that there exists a unique strictly
k
-convex solution
\psi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}_{1})
to (
2.2
). In view of the standard maximum principle,
we have
\psi<0
in
\Omega_{1}.
We will choose
M_{1}>-\min\limits_{\overline{\Omega}_{1}}\psi
such that
\psi
is a supersolution of (
2.1
).

Second, it is easy to verify that

(2.3)

\underline{u}^{\epsilon}_{M}=\frac{|x|^{2}}{2C_{n}^{k}}-M-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2C_{n}^{k}}

is a rotationally symmetric solution to the equation
\sigma_{k}(D^{2}u)=1.

Moreover,
\underline{u}^{\epsilon}_{M}
also satisfies the inside boundary condition of (
2.1
), that is,
\underline{u}^{\epsilon}_{M}=-M
on
\partial B_{\epsilon}(0).

We will choose
M_{1}=M_{1}(\Omega_{1})>0
such that
\underline{u}^{\epsilon}_{M}
is a subsolution of (
2.1
). In other words, we will choose
M_{1}>0
large enough such that

\underline{u}^{\epsilon}_{M_{1}}=\frac{|x|^{2}}{2C_{n}^{k}}-M_{1}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2C_{n}^{k}}\leq 0\,\,\mbox{on $\partial\Omega_{1}.$}

Finally, we conclude that in this paper,
M_{1}>0
is a positive constant only depending on
\Omega_{1}.
In particular,
M_{1}
is chosen such that
\psi
and
\underline{u}^{\epsilon}_{M}
are the supersolution and subsolution to (
2.1
) respectively. In the rest of this paper, for our convenience we will write
\underline{u}^{\epsilon}
instead of
\underline{u}^{\epsilon}_{M}.

Combining the above discussions with maximum principle we obtain the following
C^{0}
estimate for the solution of (
2.1
) directly.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
u^{\epsilon}
be the solution of (
2.1
), then
u^{\epsilon}
satisfies

\underline{u}^{\epsilon}<u^{\epsilon}<\psi,\,\,\mbox{in $\Omega^{\epsilon}.$}

2.2.
C^{1}
estimates of
u^{\epsilon}

In order to obtain the
C^{1}
estimates of
u^{\epsilon},
we need to divide the discussion into three cases, i.e.,
\frac{n}{k}>2,

\frac{n}{k}=2,
and
\frac{n}{k}<2.

Case 1. when
\frac{n}{k}>2,
let

\phi=-C|x|^{2-n/k}+C\epsilon^{2-n/k}-M

for some

C=C_{0}\epsilon^{n/k-2}>0
such that
\phi|_{\partial\Omega_{1}}\geq 0.
Then we can check that
\phi
is a supersolution of (
2.1
) satisfying
\sigma_{k}(D^{2}\phi)=0
and
\phi|_{\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)}=-M.
Moreover, it’s easy to see that here
C_{0}
is a positive constant only depending on
\Omega_{1}.

Case 2. when
\frac{n}{k}=2,
let

\phi=C\log|x|-C\log\epsilon-M

for some

C=\frac{C_{0}}{|\log\epsilon|}>0
such that
\phi|_{\partial\Omega_{1}}\geq 0.
Then we can check that
\phi
is a supersolution of (
2.1
) satisfying
\sigma_{k}(D^{2}\phi)=0
and
\phi|_{\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)}=-M.
Moreover, it’s easy to see that here
C_{0}
is a positive constant only depending on
\Omega_{1}.

Case 3. when
\frac{n}{k}<2,
let

\phi=C_{0}|x|^{2-n/k}-C_{0}\epsilon^{2-n/k}-M

for some

C_{0}>0
such that
\phi|_{\partial\Omega_{1}}\geq 0.
Then we can check that
\phi
is a supersolution of (
2.1
) satisfying
\sigma_{k}(D^{2}\phi)=0
and
\phi|_{\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)}=-M.
Moreover, it’s easy to see that here
C_{0}
is a positive constant only depending on
\Omega_{1}.

In the following, when there is no confusion, we will not differentiate the three cases and just use
\phi
to denote the supersolution of (
2.1
) that
satisfies

\sigma_{k}(D^{2}\phi)=0\,\,\text{and}\,\,\phi|_{\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)}=-M.

Combining with the subsolution
\underline{u}^{\epsilon}
constructed in Subsection
2.1
and the maximum principle we get

Lemma 2.2
.

[
C^{1}
- bounds on
\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)
]

Let
u^{\epsilon}
be the solution of (
2.1
), then on
\partial B_{\epsilon}(0),

u^{\epsilon}
satisfies

\frac{\partial\underline{u}^{\epsilon}}{\partial\nu}<\frac{\partial u^{\epsilon}}{\partial\nu}<\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\nu}.

Here
\nu
is the inward unit normal of
\partial\Omega^{\epsilon},
i.e.,
\nu
points into
\Omega_{1}\setminus\overline{B}_{\epsilon}(0).

Remark 2.3
.

We should keep in mind that on
\partial B_{\epsilon},
when
\frac{n}{k}>2,

|Du^{\epsilon}|<C_{0}\left(\frac{n}{k}-2\right)\epsilon^{-1};

when
\frac{n}{k}=2,

|Du^{\epsilon}|<C_{0}\epsilon^{-1}|\log\epsilon|^{-1};
and when
\frac{n}{k}<2,

|Du^{\epsilon}|<C_{0}\left(2-\frac{n}{k}\right)\epsilon^{1-n/k}.

Here
C_{0}>0
is a positive constant only depending on
\Omega_{1}.

Next, we want to obtain the
C^{1}
bound on
\partial\Omega_{1}.
In Subsection
2.1
we already constructed a supersolution
\psi
of (
2.1
)
that satisfies
\psi<0
in
\Omega_{1}
and
\psi=0
on
\partial\Omega_{1}.
It is easy to see that for all
0<\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
, we have
\psi|_{\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)}<-c_{0}.
Here
c_{0}>0
only depends on

\epsilon_{0}.
Now, let
C=C(M,\epsilon_{0})>1
be a large constant such that
c_{0}C\geq M.
Then
C\psi
is a subsolution of (
2.1
) satisfying
C\psi=0
on
\partial\Omega_{1}.

Applying the maximum principle again we get

Lemma 2.4
.

[
C^{1}
- bounds on
\partial\Omega_{1}
]

Let
u^{\epsilon}
be the solution of (
2.1
), then on
\partial\Omega_{1},

u^{\epsilon}
satisfies

C\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nu}<\frac{\partial u^{\epsilon}}{\partial\nu}<\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nu}.

Here
\nu
is the inward unit normal of
\partial\Omega^{\epsilon},
i.e.,
\nu
points into
\Omega_{1}\setminus\overline{B}_{\epsilon}(0).

Finally, we will give a
C^{1}
bound for
u^{\epsilon}
in
\Omega^{\epsilon}.

Lemma 2.5
.

[
C^{1}
- bound in
\Omega^{\epsilon}
]

Let
u^{\epsilon}
be the solution of (
2.1
), then

\max\limits_{\Omega^{\epsilon}}|Du^{\epsilon}|=\max\limits_{\partial\Omega^{\epsilon}}|Du^{\epsilon}|.

Proof.

Let
V=|Du^{\epsilon}|^{2},
a direct calculation yields

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}^{ij}V_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{k}^{ij}(2u^{\epsilon}_{l}u^{\epsilon}_{lij}+2u^{\epsilon}_{li}u^{\epsilon}_{lj})

\displaystyle=2\sigma_{k}^{ij}u^{\epsilon}_{li}u^{\epsilon}_{lj}>0,

where
\sigma_{k}^{ij}=\frac{\partial\sigma_{k}}{\partial u_{ij}}.
Lemma
2.5
then follows from the maximum principle.
∎

We also need the following interior gradient estimates, which is proved in Theorem 3.1 of
[
20
]
.

Lemma 2.6
.

[
C^{1}
- interior estimates in
\Omega^{\epsilon}
]

Let
u^{\epsilon}
be the solution of (
2.1
), then for any ball
B=B_{r}(y)\subset\Omega^{\epsilon}
we have the estimate

|Du^{\epsilon}(y)|\leq\frac{C}{r}\text{osc}_{B}u^{\epsilon},

where
C
is a constant depending on
k
and
n.

Now, denote
U_{\delta}:=\{x\in\Omega^{\epsilon}:\text{dist}(x,\partial\Omega_{1})<\delta\}
, then by Lemma
2.1
, Lemma
2.4
,
Lemma
2.5
, and Lemma
2.6
we conclude

Lemma 2.7
.

[
C^{1}
-estimates near
\partial\Omega_{1}
]

Let
u^{\epsilon}
be the solution of (
2.1
), then in
U_{\delta}
we have the estimate

|Du^{\epsilon}|\leq C,

where
C=C(\delta)
is a constant depending on
\delta
but independent of
\epsilon.

2.3.
C^{2}
estimates of
u^{\epsilon}

Let
p\in\partial B_{\epsilon}
be an arbitrary point on
\partial B_{\epsilon}.
Without loss of generality, we may choose local coordinates

\{\tilde{x}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{x}_{n}\}
in a neighborhood of
p
such that
p
is the origin. Let
\tilde{x}_{n}
axis be the inward normal of

\partial B_{\epsilon}
(pointing into
\Omega^{\epsilon}
) then following the argument in
[
5
]
(see page 271, equation (1.8)) we obtain

(2.4)

u^{\epsilon}_{\alpha\beta}(p)=u^{\epsilon}_{\nu}\epsilon^{-1}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,\,\mbox{for $p\in\partial B_{\epsilon}$,}

where
u^{\epsilon}_{\nu}:=\frac{\partial u^{\epsilon}}{\partial\tilde{x}_{n}}.

In view of Remark
2.3
we have

Remark 2.8
.

On
\partial B_{\epsilon}
when
\frac{n}{k}>2,

|u^{\epsilon}_{\alpha\beta}|<C_{0}\left(\frac{n}{k}-2\right)\epsilon^{-2};

when
\frac{n}{k}=2,

|u^{\epsilon}_{\alpha\beta}|<C_{0}\epsilon^{-2}|\log\epsilon|^{-1};
and when
\frac{n}{k}<2,

|u^{\epsilon}_{\alpha\beta}|<C_{0}\left(2-\frac{n}{k}\right)\epsilon^{-n/k}.

Here
C_{0}>0
is a positive constant only depending on
\Omega_{1}.

In the following, we will establish the
C^{2}
-boundary estimates in the tangential normal directions and in the double normal directions.

We start with estimating the
C^{2}
estimates in the tangential normal directions on
\partial B_{\epsilon}(0).

We denote the angular derivative
x_{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{l}}-x_{l}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}

by
\partial_{k,l}.
For our convenience, we let
\partial:=x_{\alpha}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{n}}-x_{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\alpha}}.

Here, we assume
x_{n}
to be the radial direction and
1\leq\alpha\leq n-1
is a fixed integer.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
u^{\epsilon}
be the solution of (
2.1
), then we have

|\partial u^{\epsilon}|\leq C\,\,\mbox{in $\bar{\Omega}^{\epsilon},$}

where
C>0
is a constant independent of
\epsilon.

Proof.

It is clear that on
\partial B_{\epsilon}
we have
\partial u^{\epsilon}=0.
Moreover, by the virtue of
Lemma
2.4
we get on
\partial\Omega_{1},

|\partial u^{\epsilon}|\leq C
for some
C>0
only depending on
\Omega_{1}.

By
[
4
]
we know that

\sigma_{k}^{ij}(\partial u^{\epsilon})_{ij}=\partial\sigma_{k}(D^{2}u^{\epsilon})=0.

The Lemma follows from the maximum principle.
∎

In view of Subsection
2.1
, we know that by our choice of
M,

\underline{u}^{\epsilon}=\frac{|x|^{2}}{2C_{n}^{k}}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2C_{n}^{k}}-M

is a subsolution of (
2.1
). Let
h=u^{\epsilon}-\underline{u}^{\epsilon}
and
\mathcal{L}:=\sigma_{k}^{ij}\partial_{ij}.

It’s clear that
h=0
on
\partial B_{\epsilon},

h>c_{0}>0
on
\partial\Omega_{1}
for some
c_{0}
independent of
\epsilon,

and
\mathcal{L}h\leq 0
in
\Omega^{\epsilon}.
Combining with the results of Lemma
2.9
and the standard maximum principle, we conclude that there exists a positive constant
A such that
Ah>|\partial u^{\epsilon}|
in
\Omega^{\epsilon}.
Here
A>0
is a constant independent of
\epsilon.
Therefore, for any
p\in\partial B_{\epsilon},
we can rotate

\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\}
such that
p=(0,\cdots,0,\epsilon).
Since
Ah>|\partial u|
in
\Omega^{\epsilon}
and
Ah=|\partial u^{\epsilon}|=0
on
\partial B_{\epsilon}
we obtain

\pm(\partial u^{\epsilon})_{n}<Ah_{n}=A\left[u^{\epsilon}_{n}-\frac{2\epsilon}{(C_{n}^{k})^{1/k}}\right]<A_{1}u^{\epsilon}_{n}.

Here, by our choice of the orientation,
x_{n}
points into
\Omega^{\epsilon}.

We conclude

Lemma 2.10
.

(
C^{2}
bound on
\partial B_{\epsilon}
in mixed directions
)
Let
u^{\epsilon}
be the solution of (
2.1
), then on
\partial B_{\epsilon},
we have

|(u^{\epsilon})_{\tau\nu}|\leq\frac{C}{\epsilon}u^{\epsilon}_{\nu}\,\,\mbox{on $\partial{B_{\epsilon}},$}

where
\tau
is an arbitrary unit tangential vector of
\partial B_{\epsilon},

\nu
is the inward unit normal of
\partial B_{\epsilon}
(pointing into
\Omega^{\epsilon}
), and
C>0
is a constant independent of
\epsilon.

In the following, we will derive the
C^{2}
bound of
u^{\epsilon}
on
\partial B_{\epsilon}
in the double normal directions.
For any
p\in\partial B_{\epsilon},
let
\{\tau_{1},\cdots,\tau_{n-1}\}
be the orthonormal frame of the tangent hyperplane of
\partial B_{\epsilon}
at
p,

and let
\tau_{n}
be the inward unit normal of
\partial B_{\epsilon}
at
p.
Then at
p
we have

(2.5)

\sigma_{k-1}(\bar{D}^{2}u^{\epsilon})u^{\epsilon}_{nn}+\sigma_{k}(\bar{D}^{2}u^{\epsilon})-\sum\limits_{\beta=1}^{n-1}\sigma_{k-2}(\bar{D}^{2}u^{\epsilon}|u^{\epsilon}_{\beta\beta})(u^{\epsilon}_{\beta n})^{2}=1,

where
\bar{D}^{2}u^{\epsilon}:=(u^{\epsilon}_{\alpha\beta})_{1\leq\alpha,\beta\leq n-1}.

Plugging (
2.4
) into (
2.5
) we get

C_{n-1}^{k-1}u^{\epsilon}_{nn}+C_{n-1}^{k}u^{\epsilon}_{n}\epsilon^{-1}=\epsilon^{k-1}(u^{\epsilon}_{n})^{1-k}+C_{n-2}^{k-2}\epsilon(u^{\epsilon}_{n})^{-1}\sum\limits_{\beta=1}^{n-1}(u^{\epsilon}_{\beta n})^{2}.

In view of Lemma
2.2
and Lemma
2.10
we obtain

Lemma 2.11
.

(
C^{2}
bound on
\partial B_{\epsilon}
in double normal directions
)
Let
u^{\epsilon}
be the solution of (
2.1
), then on
\partial B_{\epsilon},
we have

-C_{1}u^{\epsilon}_{\nu}\epsilon^{-1}\leq(u^{\epsilon})_{\nu\nu}\leq C_{2}u^{\epsilon}_{\nu}\epsilon^{-1}\,\,\mbox{on $\partial{B_{\epsilon}},$}

where
C_{1},C_{2}>0
are positive constants independent of
\epsilon
and
\nu
is the inward unit normal of
\partial B_{\epsilon}
(pointing in to
\Omega^{\epsilon}
).

Combining Remark
2.3
, Remark
2.8
, Lemma
2.10
, and Lemma
2.11
we conclude

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
u^{\epsilon}
be the solution of (
2.1
). Then on
\partial B_{\epsilon}
we have,
when
\frac{n}{k}>2,

|D^{2}u^{\epsilon}|<C_{1}\epsilon^{-2};

when
\frac{n}{k}=2,

|D^{2}u^{\epsilon}|<C_{2}\epsilon^{-2}|\log\epsilon|^{-1};
and when
\frac{n}{k}<2,

|D^{2}u^{\epsilon}|<C_{3}\epsilon^{-n/k}.

Here

and
C_{3}>0
are positive constants independent of
\epsilon.

Next, we will establish the
C^{2}
estimates of
u^{\epsilon}
on
\partial\Omega_{1}.
Let
p\in\partial\Omega_{1}
be an arbitrary point on
\partial\Omega_{1}.

Without loss of generality, we may choose local coordinates
\{\tilde{x}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{x}_{n}\}
at

such that
\tilde{x}_{n}
axis is the inward normal of

\partial\Omega_{1}.
Then the boundary near

can be written as

\tilde{x}_{n}=\rho(\tilde{x}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{\alpha=1}^{n-1}\kappa_{\alpha}\tilde{x}_{\alpha}^{2}+O(|\tilde{x}^{\prime}|^{3}),

where
\kappa_{1},\cdots,\kappa_{n-1}
are the principal curvatures of
\partial\Omega_{1}
at
p

and
\tilde{x}^{\prime}=(\tilde{x}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{x}_{n-1}).

Let
T=\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{x}_{\alpha}}+\kappa_{\alpha}\left(\tilde{x}_{\alpha}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{x}_{n}}-\tilde{x}_{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{x}_{\alpha}}\right),

and denote
\tilde{B}_{\delta_{0}}=B_{\delta_{0}}(p)\cap\Omega^{\epsilon}.
Let
\mathcal{L}:=\sigma_{k}^{ij}\partial_{ij},
then we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}Tu

\displaystyle=0\,\,\mbox{in $\tilde{B}_{\delta_{0}}$}

\displaystyle Tu

\displaystyle=O(|\tilde{x}^{\prime}|^{2})\,\,\mbox{on $\partial\tilde{B}_{\delta_{0}}\cap\partial\Omega_{1}.$}

In view of Lemma
2.7
, we also know that
|Tu|\leq C
on
\partial\tilde{B}_{\delta_{0}}\setminus\partial\Omega_{1}

for some
C>0
independent of
\epsilon
and
\delta_{0}.
Here we always assume
\delta_{0}<\delta
and
\delta>0
is the constant in Lemma
2.7
.

Let
\underline{u}=C\psi
be a subsolution of (
2.1
) for some
C>2
. Since
\underline{u}
is
k
-convex, it is easy to see that there exists

\theta>0
such that

\lambda[D^{2}(\underline{u}-\theta|\tilde{x}|^{2})]\in\Gamma_{k}

and

\sigma_{k}[D^{2}(\underline{u}-\theta|\tilde{x}|^{2})]>(3/2)^{k}\,\,\mbox{in $\tilde{B}_{\delta_{0}}.$}

Here
\theta
is a small constant independent of
\epsilon
and
\delta_{0}.
Consider

we get
h\geq\theta\delta_{0}^{2}
on
\partial\tilde{B}_{\delta_{0}}\setminus\partial\Omega_{1}

and
h\geq 0
on
\partial\tilde{B}_{\delta_{0}}\cap\partial\Omega_{1}
with

Moreover, by the concavity of

\sigma_{k}^{1/k}
we have
\mathcal{L}h\leq-\frac{1}{2}k.
In view of the standard maximum principle we conclude

\pm Tu<Ah
for some
A>0
independent of
\epsilon
(depending on
\delta_{0}
though). Therefore, we have

|u_{\alpha n}(p)|<Ah_{n}(p)<A_{1}.

Here
A_{1}
is a constant independent of
\epsilon.

Following a similar argument as Lemma
2.10
, we obtain
|u_{nn}|<C
on
\partial\Omega_{1}
for some
C>0
independent of
\epsilon.
We conclude

Lemma 2.13
.

(
C^{2}
boundary estimates on
\partial\Omega_{1}
)

Let
u^{\epsilon}
be the solution of (
2.1
), then on
\partial\Omega_{1},
we have

|D^{2}u^{\epsilon}|<C\,\,\mbox{on $\partial{\Omega_{1}},$}

where
C>0
is a positive constant independent of
\epsilon
.

Lemma 2.14
.

(
C^{2}
global estimates
)

Let
u^{\epsilon}
be the solution of (
2.1
),then we have

|D^{2}u^{\epsilon}|<C(1+\sup\limits_{\partial\Omega^{\epsilon}}|D^{2}u^{\epsilon}|)

where
C>0
is a positive constant independent of
\epsilon
.

Proof.

In the following we will drop the superscript
\epsilon
and write
u
instead of
u^{\epsilon}.
Since
u
is a solution of (
2.1
),
u
satisfies
\sigma_{k}^{1/k}(D^{2}u)=1.
Differentiating this equality twice we get

F^{ij}(\Delta u)_{ij}+F^{pq,rs}u_{pql}u_{rsl}=0,

where
F^{ij}=\frac{\partial\sigma_{k}^{1/k}}{\partial u_{ij}}
and
F^{pq,rs}=\frac{\partial^{2}\sigma_{k}^{1/k}}{\partial u_{pq}\partial u_{rs}}.

In view of the concavity of
\sigma_{k}^{1/k}
we get

Therefore,

\Delta u<C\left(1+\sup\limits_{\partial\Omega^{\epsilon}}\Delta u\right),

which implies

|D^{2}u|<C(1+\sup\limits_{\partial\Omega^{\epsilon}}|D^{2}u|).

∎

Combining Lemmas
2.1
,
2.5
, and

.

For any
\epsilon>0,
there exists a unique
k
-convex solution

satisfying (
2.1
) with

\|u\|_{C^{2}}<C,

where
C=C(\epsilon)>0
depends on
\epsilon.

Note that similar results and techniques of section

also appeared in Ma-Zhang
[
16
]
and Xiao
[

]
.

3.
Hessian measures

Let
\{u^{\epsilon_{m}}\}
be a sequence of solutions of (
2.1
) with
\epsilon_{m}\rightarrow 0
as
m\rightarrow\infty.
In this section, we will show that
\{u^{\epsilon_{m}}\}
converges locally in measure to a function

, and

satisfies

in the viscosity sense.

The following definition of
k
-convex function is an extension of the Definition
1.2
.

Definition 3.1
.

(See
[
21
]
)
An upper semi-continuous function
u:\Omega\rightarrow[-\infty,\infty)
is called
k
-convex
in
\Omega

if

for all quadratic polynomials
q
for which the difference
u-q
has a finite local maximum in
\Omega.

We shall also call a
k
-convex function
proper
if it doesn’t assume the value
-\infty
identically on any component of
\Omega.
We denote the class of
proper
k
-convex functions in
\Omega
by
\Phi^{k}(\Omega).

Since we will need to use mollifier to smooth our functions, we want to extend the domain of definition a little bit. Recall that
\partial\Omega_{1}
is smooth and

u^{\epsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega}^{\epsilon}),
where
u^{\epsilon}
is the solution of (
2.1
). We can extend
u^{\epsilon}
to the other side of
\partial\Omega_{1}
by Taylor’s expansion in the normal bundle. We will still denote this expansion by
u^{\epsilon},
and
u^{\epsilon}
is defined on
\Omega_{1}^{\delta}\setminus B_{\epsilon}(0),
where

\Omega_{1}^{\delta}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\text{dist}(x,\Omega_{1})<\delta\}

for some fixed small
\delta>0.
Moreover,
u^{\epsilon}
satisfies

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(D^{2}u^{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=1\,\,\mbox{in $\Omega_{1}\setminus\bar{B}_{\epsilon}(0)$}

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(D^{2}u^{\epsilon})

\displaystyle>1/2\,\,\mbox{in $\Omega_{1}^{\delta}\setminus\bar{B}_{\epsilon}(0)$}.

We will denote

\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}=\left\{\begin{aligned} u^{\epsilon}\,\,&\mbox{in $\Omega^{\delta}_{1}\setminus B_{\epsilon}(0)$}\\
-M\,\,&\mbox{in $\bar{B}_{\epsilon}(0).$}\end{aligned}\right.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}
be defined as above, then
\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}\in\Phi^{k}(\Omega_{1}^{\delta}).

Proof.

It is clear that
\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}
is a continuous function in
\Omega^{\delta}_{1}.
In the following we will show
\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}
is
k
-convex.

Let
q
be any quadratic polynomials such that
\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}-q
has a local maximum at some point
x\in\Omega_{1}^{\delta}.

When
x\in\Omega_{1}^{\delta}\setminus\partial B_{\epsilon}(0),
since
\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}
is smooth in a small neighborhood of
x
and

\sigma_{k}(D^{2}\tilde{u}^{\epsilon})\geq 0
in this neighborhood, we have
\sigma_{k}(D^{2}q)\geq 0.

Now, consider the case when
x\in\partial B_{\epsilon}(0).
Let
\underline{u}^{\epsilon}
be the subsolution of (
2.1
) defined in (
2.3
). Since
\underline{u}^{\epsilon}=\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}
on
\partial B_{\epsilon}
and
\underline{u}^{\epsilon}<\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}

in
\Omega_{1}.
It is clear that
\underline{u}^{\epsilon}-q
also achieves a local maximum at
x
and we have
\sigma_{k}(D^{2}q)\geq 1.

∎

In the following we will look at the mollification of
\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}.
Let
\rho\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a spherically symmetric mollifier
satisfying
\rho(x)>0
for
|x|<1,

\rho(x)=0
for
|x|\geq 1,
and
\int\rho=1.
The mollification,
\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}_{h}
is defined by

\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}_{h}=h^{-n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho\left(\frac{x-y}{h}\right)\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}(y)dy

for

From the definition of mollification and Lemma 2.3 of
[
21
]
we obtain

Lemma 3.3
.

\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}_{h}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})\cap\Phi^{k}(\Omega^{\prime})
for any
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\Omega_{1}^{\delta}
satisfying
\text{dist}(\Omega^{\prime},\partial\Omega_{1}^{\delta})\geq h.

Moreover, as
h\rightarrow 0,
the sequence
\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}_{h}\rightarrow\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}.

Notice that
\tilde{u}^{\epsilon_{1}}(x)\geq\tilde{u}^{\epsilon_{2}}(x)
in
\Omega_{1}
whenever
\epsilon_{1}<\epsilon_{2}.
In conjunction with earlier
C^{0}
and
C^{1}
interior estimates, see Lemma
2.1
and Lemma
2.6
, we have

\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}\longrightarrow v
in
C_{loc}^{1}\left(\Omega_{1}\setminus\{0\}\right).
Note that since
\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}(0)=-M
for any
\epsilon>0
, we get
v(0)=-M,
thus
v
may not be an upper-semi continuous function.
We redefine
v(0)=\limsup\limits_{x\rightarrow 0}v(x),
and in the following, all
v(x)
refers to this redefined
v(x).

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
v(x)
be defined as above, then
v(x)\in\Phi^{k}(\Omega_{1}).

Proof.

We will prove by contradiction. If
v(x)\notin\Phi^{k}(\Omega_{1})
then there exists a quadratic polynomial such that

v(x_{0})-q(x_{0})=0,

v(x)-q(x)\leq 0
for all
x\in B_{\delta}(x_{0})\Subset\Omega_{1},
and
F_{k}[q]<0.
Without loss of generality, we may also assume

v(x)-q(x)<c_{0}<0
on
\partial B_{\delta}(x_{0}).
If not, we will consider
\hat{v}(x):=v(x)-\beta|x-x_{0}|^{4}
instead, and correspondingly, we will replace

\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}
by
\hat{\tilde{u}}^{\epsilon}(x):=\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}-\beta|x-x_{0}|^{4}
. Here,
\beta>0
is a very small constant.

Case 1. When
x_{0}\neq 0,
we may assume
0\notin B_{\delta}(x_{0}).
Moreover, when
\epsilon>0
small, we also have
\bar{B}_{\delta}(x_{0})\cap\bar{B}_{\epsilon}(0)=\emptyset.
Since
\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}(x)\rightarrow v(x)
uniformly in

\bar{B}_{\delta}(x_{0}),
we get for any
\eta>0
there exists
\epsilon_{\eta}>0
such that when
\epsilon<\epsilon_{\eta}
we have
\left|\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}(x)-v(x)\right|<\eta
for all
x\in\bar{B}_{\delta}(x_{0}).

Therefore, we get
\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}(x_{0})-q(x_{0})>-\eta
and

\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}(x)-q(x)<v(x)-q(x)<c_{0}<0\,\,\mbox{on $\partial B_{\delta}(x_{0}).$}

Here, the first inequality comes from as
\epsilon\searrow 0,

\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}\nearrow v(x).

We can see that when
\eta<|c_{0}|,

\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}(x)-q(x)
achieves its local maximum in
B_{\delta}(x_{0}).
Recall that
\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}
satisfies
F_{k}[\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}]=1
in
B_{\delta}(x_{0}),
we have
F_{k}[q]\geq 1.
This leads to a contradiction. When we consider
\hat{\tilde{u}}^{\epsilon}(x)
, for
\beta>0
small we have
F_{k}[\hat{\tilde{u}}^{\epsilon}]\geq 1/2
in
B_{\delta}(x_{0}),
thus
F_{k}[q]\geq 1/2.

Case 2. When
x_{0}=0,
we recall that
v(0)=\limsup\limits_{x\rightarrow 0}v(x).
For any
\eta>0
small, there exists

\{x_{n}\}\subset B_{\delta}(0)\setminus\{0\}
and
x_{n}\rightarrow 0
such that when
n>N,

|v(x_{n})-v(0)|<\eta/3.
We may also assume
when
n>N,

|q(x_{n})-q(0)|<\eta/3.
Moreover, same as in case 1 we have,

\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}(x)-q(x)<v(x)-q(x)<c_{0}<0\,\,\mbox{on $\partial B_{\delta}(0).$}

Now, for any
n>N,
we fix
x_{n},
then there exists
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon_{1}(\eta,x_{n})>0
such that when
\epsilon<\epsilon_{1}
we have

|\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}(x_{n})-v(x_{n})|<\eta/3.

Therefore, for this
x_{n}\in B_{\delta}(0),
when
\epsilon>0
small enough we get

|\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}(x_{n})-q(x_{n})|<|\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}(x_{n})-v(x_{n})|+|v(x_{n})-v(0)|+|q(0)-q(x_{n})|<\eta.

Choosing
\eta<|c_{0}|
yields
\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}-q(x)
achieves its local maximum in
B_{\delta}(0).
By virtue of Lemma
3.2
we obtain
F_{k}[q]\geq 0,
which leads to a contradiction.
Since in this case, when replacing
\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}
by
\hat{\tilde{u}}^{\epsilon}
the argument in the last step is not so straightforward, we will include the details below.

We will assume
\max_{\bar{B}_{\delta}(0)}\hat{\tilde{u}}^{\epsilon}-q(x)
is achieved at
x^{\epsilon}.

Subcase 1. There exists
\epsilon_{2}>0
such that for all
\epsilon<\epsilon_{2},

|x^{\epsilon}|>\eta_{1}>0.
In this case we have when
\epsilon>0
small enough,
at
x^{\epsilon},

F_{k}[\hat{\tilde{u}}^{\epsilon}]\geq 1/2.
This implies
F_{k}[q]\geq 1/2,
which leads to a contradiction.

Subcase 2. There exists a sequence
\{\epsilon_{i}\}\rightarrow 0
such that
|x^{\epsilon_{i}}|\rightarrow 0.

In the following, without causing confusions, we will denote the subsequence of
\{x^{\epsilon_{i}}\}
by
\{x^{\epsilon_{i}}\}
as well.

When
\{x^{\epsilon_{i}}\}\subset\Omega_{1}\setminus\bar{B}_{\epsilon_{i}}(0),
we have
F_{k}[\hat{\tilde{u}}^{\epsilon_{i}}]\geq 1/2
at
x^{\epsilon_{i}}.
This gives

F_{k}[q]\geq 1/2,
which leads to a contradiction.

When
\{x^{\epsilon_{i}}\}\subset\partial B_{\epsilon_{i}}(0),
we will consider
\underline{u}^{\epsilon_{i}}-\beta|x-x_{0}|^{4}
instead of
\hat{\tilde{u}}^{\epsilon}.

It’s clear that
\underline{u}^{\epsilon_{i}}-\beta|x-x_{0}|^{4}-q(x)
achieves a local maximum at
x^{\epsilon_{i}}.
Therefore, at
x^{\epsilon_{i}}
we again have

F_{k}[q]\geq 1/2,
which leads to a contradiction.

When
\{x^{\epsilon_{i}}\}\subset B_{\epsilon_{i}}(0),
we know that
\hat{\tilde{u}}^{\epsilon}=-M-\beta|x-x_{0}|^{4}.

Let
\lambda:=\lambda[D^{2}q]=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n})
be the eigenvalue vectors of
\{D^{2}q\}

and let
\lambda_{\min}=\min\{\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n}\}.
Then at
x^{\epsilon_{i}}
we have

\lambda_{\min}\geq-12\beta|x^{\epsilon_{i}}|^{2}.
Therefore,
F_{k}[q]\geq-C|x^{\epsilon_{i}}|^{2}
for some positive constant
C
that is independent of
\epsilon_{i}.

Let
\epsilon_{i}\rightarrow 0
we obtain
F_{k}[q]\geq 0,
which leads to a contradiction.
∎

Now, let
\{u^{\epsilon_{m}}\}
be any sequence of solutions of (
2.1
) with
\epsilon_{m}\rightarrow 0
as
m\rightarrow\infty.
We denote
u^{m}:=\tilde{u}^{\epsilon_{m}}_{h_{m}}
to be the mollification of
\tilde{u}^{\epsilon_{m}}.
Moreover, as
m\rightarrow\infty
we have

\epsilon_{m},h_{m}\rightarrow 0.
Here, we always assume
h_{m}\ll\epsilon_{m}.
Combining Lemma
2.1
,
2.6
, and
3.4
with the fact that
v(0)
is finite,
it is clear that

\{u^{m}\}\subset\Phi^{k}(\Omega_{1})\cap C^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})
converges in
L_{loc}^{1}(\Omega_{1})\cap C^{1}_{loc}\left(\Omega_{1}\setminus\{0\}\right)
to
v\in\Phi^{k}(\Omega_{1}).

By Theorem 1.1 of
[
21
]
we know
\mu_{k}[u^{m}]\rightharpoonup\mu_{k}[v].
In view of the Portmanteau theorem this is equivalent to say, for any
B=B_{r}(x)\Subset\Omega_{1}
we have

\mu_{k}[v](B)\leq\liminf\limits_{m\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{k}[u^{m}](B)

and

\mu_{k}[v](\bar{B}_{\sigma r})\geq\limsup\limits_{m\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{k}[u^{m}](\bar{B}_{\sigma r})

for any
\sigma\in(0,1).

Lemma 3.5
.

For
k\leq n/2,
we have
\mu_{k}[v]=\nu_{E}
in
\Omega_{1},
where
\nu_{E}
is the standard measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Proof.

We only need to prove for any
B=B_{r}(x)\Subset\Omega_{1}
we have
\mu_{k}[v](B)=\nu_{E}(B).

Let
A_{m}=B_{3\epsilon_{m}/2}(0)\setminus\bar{B}_{\epsilon_{m}/2}(0)
then

B=\{B\cap(\Omega_{1}\setminus B_{3\epsilon_{m}/2})\}\cup\{B\cap A_{m}\}\cup\{B\cap\bar{B}_{\epsilon_{m}/2}\}:=I_{1}\cup I_{2}\cup I_{3}

Now, for any
\eta>0,
by the well known properties of mollifications we know there exists
h_{\eta}>0
such that
when
0<h_{m}<h_{\eta},

\displaystyle|u^{m}-\tilde{u}^{\epsilon_{m}}|

\displaystyle<\eta\,\,\mbox{in $\{\bar{\Omega}_{1}\setminus B_{4\epsilon_{m}/3}\}\cup\bar{B}_{2\epsilon_{m}/3}$}

\displaystyle|Du^{m}-D\tilde{u}^{\epsilon_{m}}|

\displaystyle<\eta\,\,\mbox{in $\{\bar{\Omega}_{1}\setminus B_{4\epsilon_{m}/3}\}\cup\bar{B}_{2\epsilon_{m}/3}$}

\displaystyle|D^{2}u^{m}-D^{2}\tilde{u}^{\epsilon_{m}}|

\displaystyle<\eta\,\,\mbox{in $\{\bar{\Omega}_{1}\setminus B_{4\epsilon_{m}/3}\}\cup\bar{B}_{2\epsilon_{m}/3}$}.

Therefore, for
n/k>2
we have

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mu_{k}[u^{m}](B)

\displaystyle=\int_{I_{1}}F_{k}[u^{m}]dx+\int_{I_{2}}F_{k}[u^{m}]dx+\int_{I_{3}}F_{k}[u^{m}]dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{I_{1}}\sigma_{k}(D^{2}\tilde{u}^{\epsilon_{m}}+\eta I)+\int_{\partial B_{3\epsilon_{m}/2}(0)}\sigma_{k}^{ij}u_{i}^{m}\gamma_{j}d\sigma

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial B_{\epsilon_{m}/2}}\sigma_{k}^{ij}u_{i}^{m}\gamma_{j}d\sigma+\int_{I_{3}}\sigma_{k}(D^{2}\tilde{u}^{\epsilon_{m}}+\eta I)dx

\displaystyle\leq\nu_{E}(I_{1})+C_{1}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{k-1}\eta^{k-j}\int_{I_{1}}\sigma_{j}[\tilde{u}^{\epsilon_{m}}]

\displaystyle+C_{2}\epsilon_{m}^{n-1}\frac{1}{|\epsilon_{m}^{2}-\eta|^{k-1}|\epsilon_{m}-\eta|}+C_{3}\eta^{k}\epsilon_{m}^{n}.

Here,
\gamma
is the unit exterior normal to
\partial A_{m}.
Moreover, we have used the divergence theorem to derive the first inequality, and we have used Remark
2.3
,
Lemma
2.12
, and Lemma
2.14
to derive the second inequality.
We also note that
C_{1},C_{2},
and
C_{3}
are positive constant only depending on
n,k,
and
\Omega_{1}.
Now let
\eta\leq\epsilon_{m}^{2k}
then we have

\mu_{k}[u^{m}](B)\leq\nu_{E}(B)+C_{4}\epsilon_{m}^{n-2k}+C_{5}\eta\epsilon_{m}^{-2(k-1)}

for some
C_{4},C_{5}>0
are independent of the choice of

Therefore, we obtain
\mu_{k}[v](B)\leq\nu_{E}(B).

Similarly, we can show that for any
\sigma\in(0,1)

\mu_{k}[v](\bar{B}_{\sigma r})\geq\nu_{E}(\bar{B}_{\sigma r}).

Let
\sigma\rightarrow 1
we obtain
\mu_{k}[v](B)\geq\nu_{E}(B).
When
n/k=2
the proof is similar. This completes the proof of this Lemma.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Proof.

The strategy of the proof follows
[

]
. We have proved the existence of a solution
u^{\epsilon}

of equation (

u^{\epsilon}
has some non-convex sublevel sets for some
\epsilon>0
small enough.
In Section

we have shown that
\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}\rightarrow v
in

and

\displaystyle\mu_{k}[v]

\displaystyle=1\,\,\mbox{in $\Omega_{1}$}

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=0\,\,\mbox{on $\partial\Omega_{1}$}.

By the uniqueness of weak solutions, which is proved in Lemma 4.2 of
[

]
, we get
v=\psi
in
\bar{\Omega}_{1}.

Here,
\psi
has been defined in Subsection
2.1
.
Now, let us assume by contradiction that for each
\epsilon>0
all sublevel sets of
\tilde{u}^{\epsilon}
are convex. Then there exists a sequence

\{\epsilon_{n}\}\subset(0,\epsilon_{0})
such that
\epsilon_{n}\rightarrow 0
as

and for each

the sublevel sets of

\tilde{u}^{\epsilon_{n}}
are all convex. Fix a point

such that

\psi(y)=\inf\limits_{\bar{\Omega}_{1}}\psi=-M_{0},

we may choose
x_{0}\neq y
such that
\psi(x_{0})>-M_{0}.
In the following we consider
(
2.1

\Omega_{1}\setminus B_{\epsilon}(x_{0}).

Let

\bar{\Omega}_{1}

Since

we know that

as

Therefore there exists

\tilde{u}^{\epsilon_{n}}(y)\leq\psi(y)+\eta.

Here

By our assumption that the sublevel sets of

\tilde{u}^{\epsilon_{n}}(x)\leq\psi(y)+\eta\,\,\mbox{for all $x\in[x_{n},y].$}

By our choice of
