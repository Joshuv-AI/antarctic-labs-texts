Towards Neural Network based Cognitive Models of Dynamic Decision-Making by Humans
Changyu Chen, Shashank Reddy Chirra, Maria José Ferreira, Cleotilde Gonzalez, Arunesh Sinha, Pradeep Varakantham

Abstract
Modeling human cognitive processes in dynamic decision-making tasks has been an endeavor in AI for a long time because such models can help make AI systems more intuitive, personalized, mitigate any human biases, and enhance training in simulation. Some initial work has attempted to utilize neural networks (and large language models) but often assumes one common model for all humans and aims to emulate human behavior in aggregate. However, the behavior of each human is distinct, heterogeneous, and relies on specific past experiences in certain tasks. For instance, consider two individuals responding to a phishing email: one who has previously encountered and identified similar threats may recognize it quickly, while another without such experience might fall for the scam. In this work, we build on Instance Based Learning (IBL) that posits that human decisions are based on similar situations encountered in the past. However, IBL relies on simple fixed form functions to capture the mapping from past situations to current decisions. To that end, we propose two new attention-based neural network models to have open form non-linear functions to model distinct and heterogeneous human decision-making in dynamic settings. We experiment with two distinct datasets gathered from human subject experiment data, one focusing on detection of phishing email by humans and another where humans act as attackers in a cybersecurity setting and decide on an attack option. We conducted extensive experiments with our two neural network models, IBL, and GPT3.5, and demonstrate that the neural network models outperform IBL significantly in representing human decision-making, while providing similar interpretability of human decisions as IBL. Overall, our work yields promising results for further use of neural networks in cognitive modeling of human decision making. Our code is available at https://github.com/shshnkreddy/NCM-HDM.

Introduction

Since the early days of AI, cognitive approaches to AI have aimed to emulate human cognitive processes with the goal of designing computer agents that exhibit human-like decision making
(
Riedl and Bulitko 2012
;
Gonzalez 2023
)
and (possibly superior) intelligence
(
Newell 1994
)
. While recent advances in AI have relied on neural networks, the goal of making realistic human agents is still very relevant. Work in this space has built human-like agents, particularly in game play
(
Laird 2001
;
Miyashita et al. 2017
;
Siu et al. 2021
;
Asfour and Murillo 2023
)
. Prior approaches aim to replicate human decision making in the aggregate (e.g., have one model for all humans) or within broad categories of human personalities (e.g., impulsive, cautious) or simple personalized models.
However, human decision-making is complex and heterogeneous, which does not neatly fit into categories of personalities.
The need for
high fidelity personalized model
of observed humans’ decision-making arises in many domains such as human detection of phishing email, understanding human attackers in cyber attacks, knowledge consumption in education settings, and many others. In the application of phishing, attacks rely on heterogeneity of decision-making, including human fallacies, to be successful. Developing realistic dynamic decision-making models for a particular human from
observed past decisions
can lead to the development of personalized interventions that make humans better at detecting phishing emails. In this work, we
aim
to build high fidelity, fine-grained and data driven models of human behavior in
specific complex tasks
.

We base our work on a popular cognitive model of human behavior, namely Instance-Based Learning (IBL).
IBL is a decision-making algorithm guided by the
recognition and retrieval of past experiences (instances)
by their similarity to the current decision situation
(
Gonzalez, Lerch, and Lebiere 2003
)
.
An
instance
is a memory unit that consists of three elements: a situation (set
of attributes that give context to the decision, or state s); a
decision (the action taken); and a feedback (utility or
experienced outcome of the action). The IBL model has been widely used in various contexts to model human behavior
(
Gonzalez, Lerch, and Lebiere 2003
;
Cranford et al. 2021a
)
; however, IBL uses simple fixed-form formulas to represent the mapping from past memory instances to current decision. Other works in human behavior modeling produce a monolithic model
(
Cheung and Bell 2021
;
Binz and Schulz 2024
)
or model generic behavior on many tasks
(
Mao et al. 2023
;
Park et al. 2023
)
. In contrast, we aim to mimic diverse and complex behavior for a
specific task
.

We build two different attention-based neural network models. The core cognitive idea borrowed from IBL is to personalize the model to each human by conditioning it on the memory of the human and our
main novelty
is in using the rich class of neural network based functions to map the past memory units, i.e., tuples of (situation, action, feedback), and the current situation to the next decision. We posit that the neural network’s ability to represent mappings with greater richness, compared to the simple fixed-form functional representation in IBL, allows our model to more accurately capture complex human behavior, resulting in higher fidelity. Our first model, Token-Level Personalized Memory Integrated Model (TL-PMIM), is based on fine-tuning a Large Language Model (LLM), namely Llama 7B, with the given limited dataset, that provides a highly expressive model but at the cost of interpretability. Our second hybrid model, Instance-Level Personalized Memory Integrated Model (IL-PMIM), balances expressivity and interpretability by building a simple attention layered model, where the attention weights provide direct interpretability of decision making. We also consider GPT3.5 as another alternative.

We conducted thorough experiments using data gathered in prior work from two separate human subject experiments: one experiment involved participants detecting phishing emails and another experiment involved participants acting as attackers in a cyber-security deception game. We analyze the results from IBL, TL-PMIM, IL-PMIM, and GPT3.5. We find that TL-PMIM best mimics individual human behavior, outperforming other approaches.
We also show that the prior knowledge inbuilt in GPT may not always be beneficial in modeling a single human’s behavior. Overall, we believe that our work at the intersection of behavioral science and AI will open doors for further research in this area.

Related Work

Human Behavioral Modeling
: There is a vast amount of literature on modeling human behavior in different areas of research
(
Fuchs, Passarella, and Conti 2023
;
Kahneman and Smith 2002
)
, all of which is not possible to describe in short. IBL differs from other prominent cognitive models such as Soar
(
Laird 2022
)
(cognitive architecture) and ACT-R (uses a production rule system), in its reliance on specific instances rather than abstract rules or distributed representations. More related, IBL and the Generalized Context Model
(
Nosofsky 2011
)
share similarities in the use of similarity judgments by comparing new stimuli with instances in memory and considering context sensitivity. However, IBL models can be applied to general decision-making and problem-solving across various domains, including categorization and decisions from experience, while GCM models are specifically designed to explain categorization processes. Reinforcement learning (RL) also has cognitive basis, however, not without criticism
(
Eckstein, Wilbrecht, and Collins 2021
)
. But, the tasks considered in this work do not have the flavor of being concerned about future rewards, which is why building on IBL (that has extensive usages and is very well established on these tasks) is promising.
Binz and Schulz (2024)
finetune an LLM on simple psychological experiments, unlike the real-world complex tasks considered in this work. They also lack a memory module and hence do not allow for ready personalization, instead they build a joint model and simulate a variety of behaviors by adding random effects. Similarly,
Xiong et al. (2023)
propose an RNN based model but mention a memory module as future work.

Human Behavioral Modeling in Phishing Email Domain
: Researchers have thoroughly investigated human behavior when tasked to identify a new (phishing or ham) email. Prior work has studied the factors that make a phishing email hard to be detected
(
Singh et al. 2020
)
. Moreover, prior work has applied instance-based learning (IBL) in modeling human’s response to the emails from observed data
(
Cranford et al. 2019
;
Xu, Singh, and Rajivan 2022
)
. A recent work
(
Asfour and Murillo 2023
)
has attempted to simulate human behavior response to phishing emails using LLMs by asking the LLM to simulate broad personality traits; this work is not data driven and does not provide a personalized modeling of a particular human (based on observed past decisions) and hence such work cannot be used for developing personalized interventions for training humans to detect emails. Prior work on interventions has also mostly relied on domain expert advice
(
Sheng et al. 2009
;
Kumaraguru et al. 2010
)
rather than building a personalized model of human sub-optimality.

Somewhat further away, there are works that focus on generating phishing emails by LLMs
(
Hazell 2023
;
Karanjai 2022
;
Sharma et al. 2023
)
and on detecting phishing emails by LLMs
(
Heiding et al. 2023
)
.
Some works have built autonomous agents using LLMs with memory components
(
Mao et al. 2023
;
Park et al. 2023
)
. These works build more generic agents and study emergent behavior, unlike our goal of heterogeneous personalized models built from observed past decisions in a specific task.

Human Behavioral Modeling of Attackers in Cyber-security
: This is a relatively less explored research topic. Some research in this area has originated from behavioral game theory that explores beyond the rational model of human behavior
(
Cheung and Bell 2021
;
Thakoor et al. 2020
)
originating in Stackelberg security games
(
Sinha et al. 2018
)
. Recently, there have been applications of the IBL model in more realistic modeling of attacker behavior
(
Cranford et al. 2021a
)
. More broadly, work in physical security has explored different aspects of human behavior in games
(
Kar et al. 2015
)
.

Behavioral Models with Memory Mechanism

We describe the different approaches that we consider in this work. We start with the known IBL model in order to introduce terminology and concepts that help describe the new proposed models.

IBL Model

In Instance Base Learning
(
Gonzalez, Lerch, and Lebiere 2003
)
, a human action is modeled as the activation of memory instances tied to past experiences associated with each action or decision. A memory instance is a tuple
(s,a,u)
denoting a situation (state)
s
in which action/decision
a
was taken resulting in outcome/utility of a scalar
u
. A state
s_{i}
is represented by
K
attributes, with
s_{i,k}
denoting the
k
-th attribute. An action
a\in A
belongs to the given set of valid actions
A
. The memory is composed of a sequence of such tuples. Memory activation of the memory instance
i
is modulated by at least two processes (i.e., free parameters in the model): memory decay and noise associated with the retrieval of these memories. At time step
t
, let there be
n<t
unique memory instances in memory and suppose a new state
s_{t}
is encountered at the current time
t
, then the activation score
A_{i}(t)
of memory instance
i

(s_{i},a_{i},u_{i})
, is given by:

\displaystyle\ln\big(\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{i}(t)}(t-t^{\prime})^{-d}\big)+\mu\sum_{j=1}^{K}\omega_{j}Sim(s_{i,j},s_{t,j})+\sigma\xi

(1)

where
d
is a decay parameter,
\mathcal{T}_{i}(t)
is the set of time steps of occurrence of instance
i
in memory before time
t
,
Sim
is a domain specific similarity function with range in
[0,1]
,
\mu,\omega_{j}>0
are weights (typically
1
), and noise
\sigma\xi
introduces stochasticity in retrieval and it is a
\sigma
scaled random variable
\xi
with zero mean logistic distribution.
The activation score of each instance in memory determines its retrieval probability. Consider an action
a
and the set of all instances in memory with action
a
as
M_{a}
.
The
retrieval probability
is calculated for each instance
i\in M_{a}
using the Boltzman softmax function
P_{i,t}=\frac{e^{A_{i}(t)/\tau}}{\sum_{j\in M_{a}}e^{A_{j}(t)/\tau}}
and then the model chooses the action
a^{*}
with the highest
blended
value
V
:

V_{t}(a)=\sum_{i=1}^{|M_{a}|}P_{i,t}u_{i}\;,\;a^{*}=\arg\!\max_{a\in A}V_{t}(a)

(2)

where
\tau
is a temperature parameter with a default value of
\tau=\sigma\sqrt{2}
. We note that the
blending
operation here is exactly like
the soft attention weighted retrieval in modern deep learning but the blending concepts in cognitive science (IBL and ACT-R) predate these modern notions. Blending concepts are themselves inspired by similar ideas present in early AI literature
(
Gonzalez, Lerch, and Lebiere 2003
)
. Another concept used in IBL is called
tracing

(
Cranford et al. 2020
;
Lebiere et al. 2023
)
inspired from feedback in intelligent tutoring systems
(
Anderson et al. 1995
)
. When tracing, the model fills the memory using the true ground memory instances due to the human participant action and outcome for each new prediction, instead of the model using its own prediction and outcome.
This aspect is analogous to teacher forcing
(
Williams and Zipser 1989
)
in recurrent neural networks in AI literature.
The connections of IBL to AI concepts inspired us to explore modern neural network techniques, as described next.

Token-Level Model

Pretrained on extensive datasets, LLMs have demonstrated significant potential in tasks such as text generation and semantic analysis. Previous work has found that fine-tuning a pre-trained LLM enhances its ability to address new problems effectively. Following the IBL model, we incorporate personalized memory into the LLM, where this memory comprises a sequence of memory instances. This approach is called Token-Level Personalized Memory Integrated Model (TL-PMIM). This approach is implemented by integrating the memory into the context. Mathematically, we concatenate the instance sequences in the memory with the current situation as the input
s_{t}
and construct the input-output pair
(x_{t},a_{t})
where
x_{t}
is:

x_{t}=s_{1}||a_{1}||u_{1}\ldots s_{t-1}||a_{t-1}||u_{t-1}||s_{t}

We then fine-tune an LLM with the data
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{t},a_{t})\}^{N}_{i=1}
. A concrete input example is provided in the appendix.

Instance-Level Model

While TL-PMIM offers substantial model expressivity, it is associated with higher computational costs and reduced interpretability. To address these issues, we propose the Instance-Level Personalized Memory Integrated Model (IL-PMIM), which balances expressivity and interpretability through a simple attention-layer-based architecture. The simple attention layer provides a direct interpretation of importance in terms of the learned attention weights.
Similar to TL-PMIM and IBL, the memory is a sequence of memory instances. However, IL-PMIM introduces an additional processing step. Specifically, each memory instance
(s,a,u)
is encoded into two latent vectors by a text encoder
E
. One latent vector represents the state and utility, denoted by
e^{s,u}
, and the other represents the action, denoted by
e^{a}
. The attention layer then processes this sequence of encodings along with the encoding of the current state to produce the action for the current step. We note that the choice of producing two encoded vectors per memory instance is a trade-off: using a single latent vector for a memory instance led to the attention ignoring the action probably because likely the action information was lost in the encoding. On the other hand, using three separate encoded vectors for state, action, and utility leads to a long-memory sequence which is not processed well by the simple attention layer. Mathematically, IL-PMIM can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle e^{s,u}_{i}=E(s_{i}||u_{i}),\;e^{a}_{i}=E(a_{i})\text{ for }i<t-1,\;e^{s}_{t}=E(s_{t}),

\displaystyle x_{t}=(e_{1}^{s,u},e_{1}^{a},\ldots,e_{t-1}^{s,u},e_{t-1}^{a},e^{s}_{t}),

\displaystyle\{A_{j}\}_{j=1}^{2t-1},\{V_{j}\}_{j=1}^{2t-1}=Att_{w}(x_{t}),\;\hat{a}_{t}=f_{\theta}(\sum_{j=1}^{2t-1}A_{j}V_{j})\;

(3)

where
f_{\theta}
is a multilayer perception producing the predicted action
\hat{a}_{t}
and
Att_{w}
is a simple attention layer with
w
the weights of the query, key, and value transformation from each component of
x_{t}
. Note that the attention weights
A_{j}
for
j\!=\!1,3,\ldots,2t\!-\!1
correspond to the retrieval probabilities in IBL, thereby providing interpretability similar to IBL.

GPT3.5 Approach

We also experiment with the zero-shot performance of LLMs as cognitive models. Similarly to the TL-PMIM model, the GPT3.5 model is incorporated with a personalized memory module. Due to cost reasons, we cannot pass the entire memory in the prompt, hence we process the memory to retrieve the top
k
instances. Let
s_{t}
be the current email in consideration, then each instance in the memory
(s_{i},a_{i})
for
i\in[1,t-1]
is given a score
Sim(s_{i},s_{t})
, where
Sim
is a domain specific similarity function. The top
k
instances
{s_{i},a_{i}}
are then retrieved to create the input
x_{t}
as,

x_{t}=s_{1}||a_{1}||u_{1}\ldots s_{k}||a_{k}||u_{k}||s_{t}

The LLM is then prompted to provide action
a_{t}
. Note that the full prompt also contains additional information such as task description and an in-context example to help the LLM. It is also asked to provide a justification for its decision to ensure that the LLM uses the memory to make the decision vs its own prior knowledge. An example of the prompt and justification given by the LLM is provided in the appendix.

Experiments

The experiments are designed to evaluate if our approach can model human responses with high fidelity, i.e., decision pattern of our model is similar to that seen in the data. For TL-PMIM, we have fine-tuned Llama 2 7B base using LongLora
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
. For the encoding
E
in IL-PMIM we use a sentence transformer, namely all-mpnet-base-v1
(
Hugging Face 2024
)
.

We consider two baselines from the literature. One is the IBL model
(
Cranford et al. 2019
;
Cranford et al. 2021b
)
. Another is the direct use of the GPT3.5 model.
All neural network experiments were run on NVIDIA Quadro RTX 5000 GPUs except for TL-PMIM, which was run on NVIDIA H100 GPUs. The hyperparameters for TL-PMIM are constant learning rate 2e-5 with a warmup of 20 steps, batch size 32, 1 training epoch with AdamW optimizer. The hyperparameters for IL-PMIM are a constant learning rate
5e
-
6
with a batch size of
64
and a dropout of
0.1
. The key, query and value embedding dimensions are
256
. The model is trained for
1000
epochs with an Adam optimizer.

Domain and Data Description

In this work, we explore two dynamic decision-making domains. The two domains chosen require somewhat varying skills: one requires visual and memory skills to detect phishing email, the other requires quantitative risk assessment skills to decide the best course of action. Data for both domains were obtained from human subject studies in prior works. We describe these human subject studies in brief and the data gathered from these studies below.

Phishing Dataset
: We use the Phishing dataset from
(
Singh et al. 2019
)
. The data is collected from the human participants in the human subject experiment. The experiment had three phases: pre-training, training, and post-training. Each participant received 10 emails in the pre- and post-training phases, respectively, while they received 40 emails in the training phase. The 10 post-training emails (2 phishing, 8 ham) were deliberately chosen from a different distribution to test the response to the distribution. For every email, they are asked to answer if the email is phishing or ham. These emails were selected from a pool of 239 emails, including legitimate (ham) and phishing emails.
The dataset had data from 297 participants. Three groups (same size) of participants receive three different percentages of training emails that were phishing emails: 25%, 50%, and 75%. In addition to this data, we also have auxiliary data which provide six binary labels for every email used in the study. These labels are from domain experts and characterize why the email is phishing or not. The label names are self-explanatory: (1) sender mismatch, (2) request credentials, (3) subject suspicious, (4) asks to do something urgently, (5) has some offer(s), and (6) mismatch in link text and location. We do not use these auxiliary data in training, but we use them for evaluation of our model.

Figure 2:
Phishing confusion matrix with 50:10 split

The email information is the state for our memory instance; this state data has the sender, subject and body of email represented in words. The action is binary: yes or no for phishing. The utility is 0 or 1 based on the correct detection of phishing or ham. To investigate the effects of data size on training our models, we split the dataset in two ways. In the first way, the first 10 emails are used for training while the remaining 50 emails are used for testing (10:50 split). In the second way, the first 50 emails are used for training while the last 10 emails are used for testing (50:10 split).

As stated earlier, we need similarity scores between emails. In IBL, we followed prior work
(
Xu, Singh, and Rajivan 2022
)
where cosine similarity was computed on BERT embeddings. For GPT, we used an embedding tool from
(
Ni et al. 2021
)
to embed emails and then used cosine similarity.

IAG dataset
: The Insider Attack Game (IAG) is designed as an online game that simulates real-world scenarios in the lab such as in
(
Cooney et al. 2020
)
. It involves two stages: first, defenders protect nodes (computers) by a randomized allocation of limited resources and the attacker chooses a node to attack. In the second stage, the defender chooses probabilistically to either warn the attacker that the node is defended (which could be a lie) or not provide such warning, and then the attackers choose to continue the attack or not. The defender’s defense and deceptive strategies are decided based on prior work in Stackelberg games
(
Xu et al. 2015
)
. The game has six nodes (computers), with the resource constrained defender able to protect only two.

Figure 3:
Phishing confusion matrix with 10:50 split

In the human subject study, participants acted as insider attackers, aiming to gain points by attacking nodes. Attackers earn points if the node they attack is undefended but lose points if caught. The exact number of points depends on the node, ranging from -10 to +10. If an attacker withdraws the attack in the second stage, they don’t earn any points. Here, we aim to model the second stage decision of the human participant, i.e., whether to continue the attack or not. The decision to continue the attack is optimal if the ground truth status of the node is uncovered (undefended) and not optimal if the node is covered.
We obtain the dataset from
(
Aggarwal et al. 2023
)
. The dataset has information on which node was chosen (target number, target location), the probability of the node being defended, the reward (or penalty) points if the node was undefended (or defended), the actual defended or not status of the node, and the outcome expressed in the number of points obtained by the participant based on his decision to continue attack or not. In the dataset, participants played the game 100 times each for different settings (different node points, defender strategy, etc.) for a total of 107,900 game instances played. Given the larger number of data points (than the phishing dataset), we perform a 50% split for training and testing for the IAG dataset.

As stated in earlier sections, we need similarity scores between the state features. In IBL, we follow the approach in prior work
(
Cranford et al. 2018
;
Nguyen, Phan, and Gonzalez 2023
)
where similarity is calculated for each feature and then summed over the features. For both reward and penalty features the similarity is
1-\frac{\left|x-y\right|}{10}
and for the probability of defended feature the similarity is
1-\left|x-y\right|
. For other features (target number, target location, and defended) an exact
match is
1
or else
0
.
As described in GPT3.5 approach, we use the same similarity score to trim the prompt.

Interpretability
:

Next, we look at which emails in memory do the interpretable models IBL and IL-PMIM pay attention to when deciding about the next email
e_{0}
. In particular, we can extract the relevance of emails in memory for IBL using the
retrieval probability
defined before Equation
2
. For IL-PMIM we can do the same using the
attention weights
defined in Equation
3
. Then, we rank the emails in memory and extract the top five relevant ones
R
and the bottom five relevant ones
N
. Recall from the phishing data description, that for every email
e
we have an auxiliary vector
v_{e}
of size six, denoting the presence or absence of characteristics that make the email a phishing email or not. Then, we compute
(1/5)\sum_{e\in R}Sim(v_{e_{0}},v_{e})
and
(1/5)\sum_{e\in N}Sim(v_{e_{0}},v_{e})
where
Sim
is cosine similarity. These numbers measure how relevant the top five and bottom five emails are to the current email from the perspective of phishing characteristics.
We expect to see a difference between these two numbers, which is what we show in Figure
7

We notice that with sufficient data (phishing 50:10 or IAG), the models generally do better when the human decisions align with the ground truth. This is not true for GPT which has similar performance in most scenarios, suggesting it relies on its prior knowledge more than trying to mimic the human’s observed performance.

Further, for the phishing 10:50 split, TL-PMIM shows improvement similar to the sufficient data scenario; this further suggests that fine-tuning an LLM helps in better mimicking human behavior, especially when the human behavior exhibits learnable patterns such as being able to detect phishing email. We also conjecture that when the human does not predict the ground truth well, then there may be a mix of various suboptimal behaviors by a single human. This is likely why for the low data domain of phishing (per participant) the performance at lower human accuracy is much worse than in the high data domain of IAG.

Conclusion and Limitations

Our work aims to show how neural networks can enrich cognitive modeling of dynamic decision-making.
We note a limitation that is generally true for most human subject studies. We showed our results with data collected in a structured lab setting for real world tasks where the environment can be very complex. E.g., when detecting phishing emails in an office setting, humans can be juggling many other tasks, and therefore factors such as the time available to make the decision become important; these have been considered in cognitive science
(
Kerstholt 1995
)
. It is always possible to expand our neural network models with these additional factors (if measured and recorded) but such a work is beyond the scope of a single paper.
Overall, our work has shown that there is positive synergy to be gained from bringing together concepts from modern deep learning and human cognition sciences; we envision neural networks being adopted extensively in cognitive sciences for higher-fidelity modeling of human behavior in complex tasks.

Appendix C
Additional IBL Details

While IBL is a baseline for our work, we performed some tuning. We conducted model fitting to determine the optimal decay parameter for each participant, taking into account previous research indicating that effective adjustment to choice environments is linked to higher decay levels (
d
)
(
Konstantinidis, Harman, and Gonzalez 2022
)
. This suggests that a weaker memory may actually lead to better adaptation. Our decay search involved testing decay values ranging from
0.1
to
3
, in increments of
0.01
.
We analyzed the synchronization of model predictions with participant decisions for each of the models generated (291 models) for each participant. At each trial
t
, we assigned a value of
1
when the model prediction aligned with the human action and a value of
0
when it did not for each participant. Subsequently, we calculated the proportion of synchronization, which we call SyncRate, for each model and participant. The model exhibiting the highest SyncRate was selected as the best fit for the participant’s data. In cases where multiple SyncRates had the same highest value, the highest decay value was chosen.

The IBL model is computationally cheap and ran on a CPU of 1.8GHz speed and 16GB RAM.
