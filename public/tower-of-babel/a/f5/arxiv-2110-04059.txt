Exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials via isospectral deformation
María Ángeles García-Ferrero, David Gómez-Ullate, Robert Milson, James Munday

Abstract
. We show a method to construct isospectral deformations of classical orthogonal polynomials. The construction is based on confluent Darboux transformations, and it allows to construct Sturm-Liouville problems with polynomial eigenfunctions that have an arbitrary number of continuous parameters. We propose to call these new orthogonal polynomial systems exceptional polynomials of the second kind . We illustrate this construction by describing the class of exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials of the second kind.



1.
Introduction

Classical orthogonal polynomials (OPs) have been traditionally characterized as the only orthogonal polynomial bases of an
\rm L^{2}
space that are also eigenfunctions of a Sturm-Liouville problem. Since the mid-nineteenth century until today, classical OPs appear in ubiquitous applications in mathematical physics, numerical analysis, approximation theory or statistics, among other fields.

As it is well known, classical OPs can be classified into three main families, depending on whether they are defined on the whole real line (Hermite), the half-line (Laguerre) or a compact interval (Jacobi). These polynomial families are characterized by two free real parameters in the case of Jacobi, one for Laguerre and none for Hermite.

This article tackles the following question:
is it possible to construct an orthogonal basis of an
\rm L^{2}
space, which is also formed by polynomial eigenfunctions of a Sturm-Liouville problem, but contains a higher (possibly arbitrary) number of free real parameters?

The flexibility of deforming classical OPs to contain so many free real parameters and yet mantain many of their defining properties would open the way to many potential applications in all of the fields where classical OPs appear naturally.

In this paper we show how to construct Sturm-Liouville problems with polynomial eigenfunctions that contain an arbitrary number of real parameters, thus providing a positive answer to the previous question.
More specifically, let
T
be a
classical differential operator
, i.e. a second-order differential operator whose eigenfunctions are classical OPs,

T(z,D_{z}):=p(z)D_{z}^{2}+q(z)D_{z}+r(z),

and let
\{P_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
be a set of polynomial eigenfunctions of
T

TP_{i}=\lambda_{i}P_{i},\quad i=0,1,2,\dots

We will show how to
construct a new operator
\hat{T}
and polynomials
\hat{P}_{i}
such that

\hat{T}(z,D_{z};t_{m_{1}},\dots,t_{m_{n}}):=p(z)D_{z}^{2}+\hat{q}(z;t_{m_{1}},\dots,t_{m_{n}})D_{z}+\hat{r}(z;t_{m_{1}},\dots,t_{m_{n}})

where
t_{m_{1}},\dots,t_{m_{n}}
are
n
real parameters,
\hat{q}
and
\hat{r}

are rational functions of
z
, and

\hat{T}\hat{P}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\hat{P}_{i},\quad i\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}.

The transformed eigenvalue problem has the same spectrum

\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
as the original one, and the leading
coefficient
p(z)
does not change in the transformation. We speak
thus of a
isospectral deformation
because we will also see that

\hat{T}(z,D_{z};0,\dots,0)=T(z,D_{z}),\qquad\hat{P}_{i}(z;0,\dots,0)=P_{i}(z).

So much extra freedom and
flexibility comes at a cost. Although the set of new polynomials is
still
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-complete, there is a finite number of exceptional
degrees for which no polynomial of that degree exists in the basis. In
general we will have that

i=\deg P_{i}\neq\deg\hat{P}_{i},\quad i\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}

The new polynomial families introduced in this paper fit thus into the definition of
exceptional orthogonal polynomials
(XOPs) originally introduced in
[
20
]
. In the past ten years, the mathematical study of XOPs has been
concerned with their classification
[
12
,
18
]
, the
properties of their zeroes
[
22
,
25
,
30
]
, and their
recurrence relations
[
9
,
21
,
32
,
33
]
. The spectral-theoretic aspects of exceptional operators (the
second-order differential operators whose eigenfunctions are the
XOPs) have been studied in
[
28
,
29
,
16
]
.
It is known that every exceptional operator can be related to a
classical Bochner operator by a finite number of state-adding,
state-deleting, or isospectral Darboux transformations
[
12
, Theorem
1.2]
.

In the context of mathematical physics, XOPs appear as exact
solutions to Dirac’s equation
[
36
]
and as bound states of
exactly-solvable rational extensions
[
15
,
34
,
35
]
. Additionally, they are connected to
finite-gap potentials
[
26
]
and super-integrable systems

[
31
,
35
]
.

The contribution of this paper is to present a new, different construction of exceptional orthogonal polynomials, that we propose to call (rather unimaginatively)
exceptional orthogonal polynomials of the second kind
. There are a number of important differences between the class of known XOPs (of the first kind) and the new XOPs of the second kind, including:

(a)

XOPs of the first kind have the same number of real parameters as their classical counterparts, while XOPs of the second kind have as many real parameters as desired.

(b)

XOPs of the first kind are not a continuous deformation of a classical polynomial family, while XOPs of the second kind become classical when we set all deformation parameters to zero.

(c)

XOPs of the first kind have a different spectrum with respect to their classical counterparts while XOPs of the second kind have the same spectrum.

(d)

The degree sequence for XOPs of the first kind is an increasing sequence, which is no longer true for XOPs of the second kind.

As an illustration of this new construction, we show how to define an isospectral
deformation of the classical Gegenbauer operator

(1)

T^{(\alpha)}:=(1-z^{2})D_{z}^{2}-(2\alpha+1)zD_{z}

through the application of a finite number of confluent Darboux
transformations (CDTs), also referred to as the “double
commutator” method
[
14
]
.

Exceptional Jacobi polynomials of the first kind are indexed by discrete parameters
[
5
,
10
]
and, as a result, cannot be continuously deformed into their classical
counterparts. By contrast, every CDT introduces a new
deformation parameter. Therefore, by performing a chain of
n
CDTs on
the classical Gegenbauer operator (
1
) at distinct
eigenvalues, we will arrive at an exceptional operator that depends on
n
discrete parameters and
n
real parameters. The eigenpolynomials of the resulting exceptional
operator are exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials of the second kind, which also depend on
n
real parameters, and can be continuously deformed to the
classical Gegenbauer polynomials by letting the parameters tend to zero.

The new construction of exceptional polynomials and weights described in this paper can also be understood from the point of view of the theory of inverse scattering

[
1
,
6
,
37
]
, and it is conceptually related to the
construction of KdV multi-solitons. While KdV solitons are obtained by applying a
state-adding deformation on the zero potential, the exceptional
operators in this paper are related to isospectral deformations of
particular instances of the Darboux–Pöschl–Teller (DBT) potential

[
23
]
. In spectral-theoretic terms, the consequence of our
construction is the continuous modification of the norming constants
of a finite number of the bound states. However, since our focus
is on orthogonal polynomials, rather than quantum mechanics or
evolution equations, our approach is formulated in the gauge and coordinates of
the classical differential operator rather than working with Schrödinger
operators. The resulting procedure can be easily implemented using a
computer algebra system.

For the remainder of the paper, we develop the theory of confluent Darboux transformations in the algebraic gauge, and
apply it in a construction of new exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials of the second kind. The paper is organized as follows:
in Section
2
we describe the formal theory of rational multi-step Darboux-Crum transformations in the algebraic gauge. In Section
3
we describe confluent Darboux transformations as a 2-step Darboux transformation whose seed functions are an eigenfunction and a generalized eigenfunction at the same eigenvalue.
In Section
4
, we describe the class of exceptional Gegenbauer operators and their factorizations and we provide a recursive
construction for the operators and eigenfunctions connected by CDTs. In Section
5
we provide matrix formulas for exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials of second type, we prove the equivalence of
the matrix and recursive definitions and thereby establish the proofs
of our main results concerning the Sturm–Liouville properties and

\mathrm{L}^{2}
-completeness of the exceptional Gegenbauer
polynomials. Finally, in Section
6
, we provide explicit
examples of families of exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials of the second kind, for both one and two deformation parameters.

Although this is the first paper to provide a detailed description of the construction of exceptional polynomials of the second kind via confluent Darboux transformations, we would like to mention that exceptional polynomials obtained by confluent Darboux transformations were investigated earlier in the 1-step case (with one deformation parameter) by Grandati and Quesne
[
24
]
. The family of exceptional Legendre polynomials of the second kind was recently introduced by some of the present authors
[
13
]
, showing for the first time that deformations of classical polynomials with an arbitrary number of real parameters exist. Shortly after that, Durán has shown that there is an alternative way to construct exceptional polynomials of the second kind, by first perturbing the measure of the discrete Hahn polynomials, dualizing and taking a suitable limit
[
11
]
. The extra continuous parameters enter in this construction in the polynomial perturbation of the classical discrete measure. Summarizing, there are three rather different constructions leading to the same mathematical objects:

(i)

one based in iterating the action of differential operators, leading to Wronskian determinants whose seed functions are generalized eigenfunctions,

(ii)

one based in matrix and integral formulas, coming from the inverse scattering method,

(iii)

one based in Durán’s bispectral construction, coming from a perturbation of a classical discrete measure.

In this paper we show the equivalence of the first two approaches. Although there is a strong evidence that the last construction leads to the same objects, establishing the equivalence of all three methods remains an open question, which can possibly trigger further research.

2.
Darboux transformations and factorization chains

The formal theory of Darboux transformations for Schödinger operators in mathematical physics (also known as supersymmetric quantum mechanics) has been developed in numerous works, mostly with the aim of generating new solvable problems from known ones,
[
7
,
6
,
19
]
, but also in the construction of solutions to Painlevé type equations,
[
3
,
17
]
. It was recently shown that every exceptional polynomial family must be related to a classical family by a sequence of Darboux transformations,
[
12
]
. We first describe here Darboux transformations with seed functions that have no repeated eigenvalues. This is the class of transformations that leads to exceptional orthogonal polynomials of the first kind, when applied on the classical polynomial families,
[
5
,
9
,
10
,
15
,
18
]
.

In this Section we revise some of these well known results, albeit with a little twist: we describe Darboux transformations for the class of general second order differential operators, which include Schrödinger operators as a particular case. For the purpose of this paper, we focus on second order differential operators with rational coefficients that have an infinite number of polynomial eigenfunctions, and we restrict to rational Darboux transformations of these operators that preserve this property by construction. However, the results derived in this section can be trivially extended to general second order operators. All of this section is written at a purely formal level, so in an abuse of notation we speak of differential operators without specifying their domain, or we speak of eigenfunctions as solutions of an eigenvalue problem, without defining a proper spectral theoretic setting.

Definition 2.1
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, let
T_{0},T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}
be second-order rational
operators. We say that
T_{0}\to T_{1}\to\cdots\to T_{n}
is an

n
-step rational Darboux transformation
if there exist
first-order rational operators
A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{n}
such that

(2)

A_{k}T_{k-1}=T_{k}A_{k},\quad k=1,\ldots,n.

The next Proposition states that if
A_{k}
is a first order intertwiner operator between
T_{k-1}
and
T_{k}
as in (
2
), then its kernel must be spanned by a formal eigenfunction of
T_{k-1}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, let
T_{0}\to T_{1}\to\dots\to T_{n}
be an
n
-step rational Darboux transformation, and
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
be the first-order rational operators satisfying the
intertwining relation
(
2
). Let
b_{k}(z)
and
w_{k}(z)
be rational functions such that

(3)

A_{k}=b_{k}\left(D-w_{k}\right),

and define (up to a constant factor) the quasi-rational functions

(4)

\psi_{k}(z):=\exp\left(\int^{z}w_{k}(u)du\right).

We then have that

(5)

T_{k-1}\psi_{k}=\lambda_{k}\psi_{k},\qquad k=1,\ldots,n,

where
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
are constants.

Proof.

By (
3
) and (
4
)we have that
A_{k}\psi_{k}=0
, so (
2
) implies that

A_{k}T_{k-1}\psi_{k}=0
.
Since
A_{k}
is a first-order operator, its kernel is
1-dimensional and it is therefore spanned by
\psi_{k}
. This implies
that there exists a constant
\lambda_{k}
such that
(
5
) holds.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

Assume that
T_{k}
has the form

(6)

T_{k}=pD^{2}+q_{k}D+r_{k},\quad k=0,1,\ldots,n.

Observe that its coefficients
p,q_{k}
and
r_{k}
are related to the rational functions
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}
defined in (
3
) by the following
Ricatti-type equations

(7)

p(w_{k}^{\prime}+w_{k}^{2})+q_{k-1}w_{k}+r_{k-1}=\lambda_{k},\quad k=1,\ldots,n.

For a given
n
-step Darboux transformation, the
corresponding
\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{n}
are unique, up to a choice of
multiplicative constant. In light of this remark and future ones, we introduce the following defintions:

Definition 2.4
.

We call the quasi-rational functions

\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{n}

factorization eigenfunctions
, the set of numbers

\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}

factorization eigenvalues
, and the rational functions

b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}

factorization gauges
.

A chain of Darboux transformations is more often defined as a factorization of each second-order operator
T_{k}
for
k=0,\ldots,n
, followed by a permutation of the two factors to yield the next operator
T_{k+1}
. We make precise the notion of a factorization chain in the next definition and establish later that both approaches (intertwining of operators and factorization) coincide.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
T_{0},T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}
be second-order rational operators. We
say that
T_{0}\to T_{1}\to\cdots\to T_{n}
is a
factorization chain
if
there exist first-order rational operators
A_{k},B_{k},\;k=1,\ldots,n
and constants
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
such that

(8)

T_{k-1}=B_{k}A_{k}+\lambda_{k},\quad T_{k}=A_{k}B_{k}+\lambda_{k},\qquad k=1,\ldots,n,

The
intertwining relations given in (
2
) then follow as a
consequence. We now show that these two formulations of Darboux
transformations are equivalent.

Proposition 2.6
.

A multi-step rational Darboux transformation is necessarily a factorization
chain, and viceversa.

Proof.

Suppose that (
8
) holds. Then, the intertwining
relations (
2
) follow by the associativity of operator
composition.

Conversely, suppose that (
2
) holds, let
A_{k}
be as in (
3
), and set

(9)

\displaystyle B_{k}:=\hat{b}_{k}(D-\hat{w}_{k}),

where

(10)

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{k}:=-w_{k}-\frac{q_{k-1}}{p}+\beta_{k},\quad\beta_{k}:=\frac{b_{k}^{\prime}}{b_{k}},\quad\hat{b}_{k}:=\frac{p}{b_{k}}.

Let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
denote the eigenvalues as per
(
5
). By (
7
) and a direct calculation,
we have

(11)

\displaystyle B_{k}A_{k}

\displaystyle=\hat{b}_{k}(D-\hat{w}_{k})b_{k}(D-w_{k})

\displaystyle=p(D-\hat{w}_{k}+\beta_{k})(D-w_{k})

\displaystyle=p(D+w_{k})(D-w_{k})+q_{k-1}(D-w_{k})

\displaystyle=pD^{2}+q_{k-1}D-p(w_{k}^{\prime}+w_{k}^{2})-q_{k-1}w_{k}

\displaystyle=T_{k-1}-\lambda_{k}.

Hence, it follows that

\displaystyle(T_{k}-A_{k}B_{k}-\lambda_{k})A_{k}=A_{k}(T_{k-1}-B_{k}A_{k}-\lambda_{k})=0.

The ring of differential operators does not have zero
divisors, so relations (
8
) follow immediately.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

As a direct consequence we may
observe that Darboux transformations are invertible. Indeed, by
(
8
), we have that

\displaystyle B_{k}T_{k}=T_{k-1}B_{k},\quad k=1,\ldots,n,

where
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}
are the rational operators defined by
(
9
) and (
10
). Thus, the chain

T_{n}\to T_{n-1}\to\cdots\to T_{0}
also satisfies the definition of
an
n
-step Darboux transformation.

2.1.
Seed eigenfunctions and generalized Crum formula

So far we have defined
n
-step Darboux transformations referring to the intertwining or factorization of operators
T_{0},T_{1},\dots,T_{n}
at each step of the chain, which requires an eigenfunction
\psi_{k}
for each operator
T_{k-1}
in the chain. In this section we show how to define an
n
-step Darboux transformation using only eigenfunctions of the first operator
T_{0}
, that we will call
seed functions
. In the case of Darboux chains for Schrödinger operators, this construction leads to the well known Darboux-Crum Wronskian formula,
[
8
]
. Theorem
2.1
in this section can thus be seen as a generalization of Crum’s formula.

Let
T_{0}
be a second-order rational operator and let

\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\ldots,\phi_{n}
be quasi-rational eigenfunctions of

T_{0}
. Explicitly, we have

\displaystyle T_{0}\phi_{k}=\lambda_{k}\phi_{k},\quad k=1,\ldots,n,

where
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
are constants. We
refer to
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}
as
seed eigenfunctions
,
because, as we show below, a Darboux transformation is determined by a
choice of seed eigenfunctions and factorization gauges.

Going forward, for a set of indices
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we write

(12)

\phi_{(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k})}:=\Wr[\phi_{i_{1}},\phi_{i_{2}},\ldots,\phi_{i_{k}}].

We now arrive at the key result of this section: explicit formulas for the coefficients of an operator obtained by a multi-step Darboux transformation.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
T_{0}
be a second-order rational operator as in (
6
),

\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}\}
be quasi-rational eigenfunctions of
T_{0}

with distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
and let
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\}
be a set of non-zero rational functions. Define the operators

(13)

T_{k}:=pD^{2}+q_{k}D+r_{k},\qquad A_{k}:=b_{k}(D-w_{k}).

where

(14)

\sigma_{k}:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(\log b_{j})^{\prime},\quad\upsilon_{k}:=\frac{\phi_{(1,2,\ldots,k)}^{\prime}}{\phi_{(1,2,\ldots,k)}},\qquad k=1,\ldots,n;

and

(15)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&q_{k}:=q_{0}+kp^{\prime}-2p\sigma_{k},\\
&r_{k}:=r_{0}+kq_{0}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}k(k-1)p^{\prime\prime}+\upsilon_{k}p^{\prime}-\sigma_{k}(q_{0}+kp^{\prime})+(\sigma_{k}^{2}-\sigma_{k}^{\prime}+2\upsilon_{k}^{\prime})p,\end{split}

(16)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&w_{1}:=\upsilon_{1},\quad w_{k}:=\sigma_{k-1}+\upsilon_{k}-\upsilon_{k-1},\quad k=2,\dots,n.\end{split}

Then, the sequence of operators
T_{0}\to T_{1}\to\cdots\to T_{n}
is an
n
-step Darboux
transformation in the sense of Definition
2.1
, i.e. (
2
) holds with the operators
A_{k}
defined above.

Since we assume that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
are distinct,
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}
are linearly independent. Hence, the Wronskians in the denominator of (
14
) are non-zero, and the functions
\upsilon_{k}
are well-defined.

Remark 2.8
.

The well-known Crum formula for an
n
-step Darboux transformation of a
Schrödinger operator

T_{n}=T_{0}-2(\log\phi_{(1,2,\ldots,n)})^{\prime\prime}

is a special case of Theorem
2.1
that corresponds to starting from a Schrödinger operator (
p=-1
and
q_{0}=0
) and choosing the gauge
b_{1}=\ldots=b_{n}=1
. Thus, this result can be regarded as an extension of the Darboux-Crum formula from Schrödinger operators to general second order operators. Although for the purpose of the paper we restrict to second order operators with rational coefficients and polynomial eigenfunctions, the generalized Crum formula in Theorem
2.1
is valid on a general setting.

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
2.1
. The strategy is to first establish the result for the particular gauge
b_{1}=\cdots=b_{n}=1
and then show the transformation rules under a different gauge. Most of the proofs proceed by induction and make use of differential algebra and properties of Wronskian determinants. We will first need to state and prove a number of auxiliary lemmas, where operators with tilde denote the restriction of the same objects in Theorem
2.1
to the special case
b_{1}=\cdots=b_{n}=1
.

Lemma 2.9
.

Under the same setting as in Theorem
2.1
, let
\tilde{A}_{k}=D-\tilde{w}_{k}
for
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
with
\tilde{w}_{k}=\upsilon_{k}-\upsilon_{k-1}
. Then,

(17)

\displaystyle(\tilde{A}_{k-1}\tilde{A}_{k-2}\cdots\tilde{A}_{1})\phi_{j}=\frac{\phi_{(1,2,\ldots,k-1,j)}}{\phi_{(1,2,\ldots,k-1)}},\quad 2\leq k\leq j\leq n.

Proof.

For convenience, set

(18)

\displaystyle\varphi_{k{\color[rgb]{0,0,1},}j}:=(\tilde{A}_{k-1}\tilde{A}_{k-2}\cdots\tilde{A}_{1})\phi_{j}.

We argue by induction. The case of
k=2
follows directly from the definition. Indeed,

\displaystyle\varphi_{2,j}=\tilde{A}_{1}\phi_{j}=\frac{1}{\phi_{1}}\Wr[\phi_{1},\phi_{j}]=\frac{\phi_{1,j}}{\phi_{1}}.

Now, we suppose that (
17
) holds for a
particular
k<n
. By the inductive hypothesis, in particular we have

\varphi_{k,k}=\frac{\phi_{(1,2,\ldots,k)}}{\phi_{(1,2,\ldots,k-1)}},\quad\tilde{\omega}_{k}=\frac{\varphi_{k,k}^{\prime}}{\varphi_{k,k}}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\tilde{A}_{k}=D-\frac{\varphi_{k,k}^{\prime}}{\varphi_{k,k}}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\varphi_{k+1,j}

\displaystyle=(\tilde{A}_{k}\cdots\tilde{A}_{1})\phi_{j}=\tilde{A}_{k}\varphi_{k,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varphi_{k,k}}\Wr\left[\varphi_{k,k},\varphi_{k,j}\right]=\frac{1}{\varphi_{k,k}}\Wr\left[\frac{\phi_{(1,2,\ldots,k-1,k)}}{\phi_{(1,2,\ldots,k-1)}},\frac{\phi_{(1,2,\ldots,k-1,j)}}{\phi_{(1,2,\ldots,k-1)}}\right].

By well-known properties of the Wronskian operator, we finally have

\varphi_{k+1,j}=\frac{1}{\varphi_{k,k}}\frac{\Wr[\phi_{1,2,\ldots,k-1,k},\phi_{(1,2,\ldots,k-1,j)}]}{\left(\phi_{(1,2,\ldots,k-1)}\right)^{2}}=\frac{\phi_{(1,2,\ldots,k-1)}\phi_{(1,2,\ldots,k,j)}}{\varphi_{kk}\left(\phi_{(1,2,\ldots,k-1)}\right)^{2}}=\frac{\phi_{(1,2,\ldots,k,j)}}{\phi_{(1,2,\ldots,k)}}.

∎

The following lemma establishes the desired result in the restricted gauge

Lemma 2.10

Under the same setting as in Theorem

Then

(21)

\tilde{A}_{k}\tilde{T}_{k-1}=\tilde{T}_{k}\tilde{A}_{k},\quad k=1,\ldots,n.

Proof.

By direct

\displaystyle=(pD^{2}+\tilde{q}_{k}D+\tilde{r}_{k})(D-\tilde{w}_{k})

which establishes (

\displaystyle=(\tilde{r}_{k}-\tilde{r}_{k-1})\tilde{w}_{k}+\tilde{q}_{k}\tilde{w}_{k}^{\prime}+p\tilde{w}_{k}^{\prime\prime}+\tilde{r}_{k-1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(\tilde{q}_{k-1}^{\prime}+p^{\prime}\tilde{w}_{k}+2p\tilde{w}_{k}^{\prime})\tilde{w}_{k}+(\tilde{q}_{k-1}+p^{\prime})\tilde{w}_{k}^{\prime}+p\tilde{w}_{k}^{\prime\prime}+\tilde{r}_{k-1}^{\prime}

Let

Remark 2.12
.

In light of Lemma

results in a gauge transformation of the operators

. It is for this reason that we refer to

as factorization gauges. Moreover, by

are defined directly in

and

is alternating in its indices and its

.

3.
Confluent Darboux transformations

In this section we generalize the concept of Darboux transformations to allow
for repeated eigenvalues. Notice that the construction in Section
2
fails
if the eigenvalues of the factorization eigenfunctions are not all distinct, because then some of the seed
functions may not be linearly independent, which leads to the
vanishing of the Wronskians in the denominator of (
14
). In order to allow for
repeated eigenvalues, we will allow some of our seed eigenfunctions to
become generalized eigenfunctions.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that two second-order rational operators
T_{0}
and
T_{2}
are connected by a
confluent Darboux transformation
if there exists a second-order
rational operator
T_{1}
such that
T_{0}\to T_{1}\to T_{2}
is a 2-step
Darboux transformation and the corresponding eigenvalues, as
defined in Proposition
2.2
, satisfy

\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
.

As we shall see, the factorization eigenfunction for the second step
T_{1}\to T_{2}
will not be related to an eigenfunction of
T_{0}
but to a generalized eigenfunction, which motivates the following definition.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
T
be a linear differential operator. We say that
\phi
is an

n^{\text{th}}
order generalized eigenfunction
of
T
if

(T-\lambda)^{n}\phi=0
, but
(T-\lambda)^{n-1}\phi\neq 0
.

For our purposes it will be sufficient to use only
first-order generalized eigenfunctions; however, the more general
construction can be found in
[
6
]
. We now show that a CDT
(confluent Darboux transformation) can be generated by a seed
eigenfunction and a corresponding second-order generalized
eigenfunction.

In order to build a CDT, we start with a second-order rational operator

T_{0}=pD^{2}+q_{0}D+r_{0}
, a quasi-rational seed eigenfunction

\phi
with eigenvalue
\lambda
and rational factorization gauges

b_{1}
and
b_{2}
. Let
T_{0}\to T_{1}
be a 1-step Darboux
transformation with factorization function
\phi
at factorization eigenvalue
\lambda
. We now wish to perform a second Darboux transformation on
T_{1}
using the repeated
eigenvalue
\lambda
and factorization gauge
b_{2}
. As shown below,
this requires that the second seed function be a generalized
eigenfunction of
T_{0}
. We therefore seek to construct a function

\phi^{(1)}
such that
(T_{0}-\lambda)\phi^{(1)}=\phi
. The following lemma shows how to achieve this.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
T_{0}=pD^{2}+q_{0}D+r_{0}
and let
\phi
an eigenfunction of
T_{0}
with eigenvalue
\lambda
, i.e.
T_{0}\phi=\lambda\phi
.
Then the particular solution of the inhomogeneous equation
(T_{0}-\lambda)y=\phi
is given by

(26)

\displaystyle\phi^{(1)}(z)

\displaystyle=\phi(z)\int^{z}\left(\frac{\mu(u)}{\phi(u)^{2}}\int^{u}\frac{\phi^{2}(s)}{p(s)\mu(s)}ds\right)du,

where

(27)

\mu(z):=\exp\left(-\int^{z}\frac{q_{0}(u)}{p(u)}du\right),

Also, a linearly independent solution of the homogenous equation
(T_{0}-\lambda)y=0
is given by

(28)

\phi^{\perp}(z):=\phi(z)\int^{z}\frac{\mu(u)}{\phi(u)^{2}}du.

Remark 3.4
.

Having fixed
\mu
as in (
27
), the above definition of
\phi^{(1)}
incorporates two additional constants of integration which correspond to linear combination of the solutions of the homogeneous equation,
\phi
and
\phi^{\perp}
. We will fix one of these constants once we consider an explicit form for
p
, by imposing an appropriate lower bound for the integral. The other constant of integration serves as a natural deformation parameter in the CDT construction. This will be explained in more detail later on.

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

We first consider a
complementary solution to the eigenvalue equation
T_{0}y=\lambda y
,
which we will denote
\phi^{\perp}
. We can obtain an explicit formula
for
\phi^{\perp}
via reduction of order. Substituting the form

\phi^{\perp}:=f\phi
into the equation

T\phi^{\perp}=\lambda\phi^{\perp}
yields the equation

\displaystyle\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}=-\frac{q_{0}}{p}-2w,\quad\mbox{ with }w=\frac{\phi^{\prime}}{\phi}.

Noticing that
\frac{\mu^{\prime}}{\mu}=-\frac{q_{0}}{p}
, it follows that

\displaystyle f(z)=\int^{z}\frac{\mu(u)}{\phi(u)^{2}}du.

The above definitions are purely formal in that we have not specified
the lower bound of the integrals. This means that
\phi^{\perp}
is
defined up to a constant multiple of
\phi
, and that
\mu
is defined
up to a choice of positive multiplicative constant.

We now construct
\phi^{(1)}

using variation of parameters. We set

(29)

\phi^{(1)}:=\hat{\rho}\phi+\rho\phi^{\perp},

where
\hat{\rho}
and
\rho
are unknown functions satisfying

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}{}^{\prime}\phi+\rho^{\prime}\phi^{\perp}=0.

Since
(T_{0}-\lambda)\phi^{(1)}=\phi
, then they also satisfy

\displaystyle p\big(\hat{\rho}^{\prime\prime}\phi+2\hat{\rho}^{\prime}\phi^{\prime}+\rho^{\prime\prime}\phi^{\perp}+2\rho^{\prime}(\phi^{\perp})^{\prime}\big)=\hat{\rho}\phi+\rho\phi^{\perp}.

Solving for the functions
\hat{\rho}
and
\rho
satisfying the above
system of equations, we find that

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(z)

\displaystyle=-\int^{z}\phi(u)\phi^{\perp}(u)W(u)du,

(30)

\displaystyle\rho(z)

\displaystyle=\int^{z}\phi^{2}(u)W(u)du,

where

(31)

W(z):=\big(p(z)\mu(z)\big)^{-1}=p(z)^{-1}\exp\left(\int^{z}\frac{q_{0}(u)}{p(u)}du\right).

Now integrating by parts it follows that we can express
\phi^{(1)}
as

(32)

\displaystyle\phi^{(1)}(z)

\displaystyle=\phi(z)(f(z)\rho(z)+\hat{\rho}(z))=\phi(z)\left(f(z)\rho(z)-\int^{z}f(u)\rho^{\prime}(u)du\right)

\displaystyle=\phi(z)\int^{z}\rho(u)f^{\prime}(u)du=\phi(z)\int^{z}\frac{\rho(u)\mu(u)}{\phi(u)^{2}}du.

∎

After the first Darboux transformation
T_{0}\to T_{1}
at factorization eigenvalue
\lambda
, we define the intertwiner
A_{1}=b_{1}(D-w_{1})
, with
w_{1}=(\log\phi)^{\prime}
. The first candidate for factorization function for the second Darboux transformation
T_{1}\to T_{2}
would be the image of
\phi^{\perp}
under
A_{1}
:

(33)

\psi^{\perp}:=A_{1}\phi^{\perp}=b_{1}\frac{\mu}{\phi}.

Indeed, since
T_{1}\psi^{\perp}=\lambda\psi^{\perp}
, we could employ
\psi^{\perp}
as a factorization eigenfunction for a 1-step Darboux transformation on
T_{1}
. However, this choice of eigenfunction produces the

inverse Darboux transformation

T_{1}\to T_{0}
.

In order to construct an operator
T_{2}
distinct from
T_{0}
, we need to consider another candidate: the image of the generalized eigenfunction
\phi^{(1)}
under
A_{1}
. Indeed, we define the factorization eigenfunction for
T_{1}
to be

(34)

\psi_{2}:=A_{1}\phi^{(1)}=\rho\psi^{\perp},

with
\rho
as in (
30
). The second equality is true because

\Wr[\phi,\phi^{(1)}]=\Wr\left[\phi,\phi\int^{z}\frac{\rho\mu}{\phi^{2}}\right]=\rho\mu

and therefore

\psi_{2}=\frac{b_{1}}{\phi}\Wr[\phi,\phi^{(1)}]=\frac{b_{1}}{\phi}\rho\mu=\rho\psi^{\perp}.

The key observation is that although
\phi^{(1)}
is only a generalized eigenfunction of
T_{0}
, its image
\psi_{2}
is a true eigenfunction of
T_{1}
at eigenvalue
lambda
:

(35)

(T_{1}-\lambda)\psi_{2}=(T_{1}-\lambda)A_{1}\phi^{(1)}=A_{1}(T_{0}-\lambda)\phi^{(1)}=A_{1}\phi_{1}=0,

and thus it can be employed for the second Darboux transformation
T_{1}\to T_{2}
.
We summarize the construction of a confluent Darboux transformation
T_{0}\to T_{2}
with a generalized eigenfunction in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
T_{0}=pD^{2}+q_{0}D+r_{0}
be a second-order rational operator,
\phi
a
quasi-rational eigenfunction of
T_{0}
with eigenvalue
\lambda
, and

b_{1},b_{2}
a choice of non-zero rational functions. Let
\mu,\rho

be defined as per (
27
), (
30
), respectively, and assume
that
\rho
is a quasi-rational function. Let
T_{2}=pD^{2}+q_{2}D+r_{2}
, where
p_{2}
and
r_{2}
are defined in (
15
) with

\upsilon_{1}:=(\log\phi)^{\prime},\quad\upsilon_{2}:=\big(\log(\rho\mu)\big)^{\prime}.

Then

T_{0}
and
T_{2}
are connected by a confluent Darboux transformation

Proof.

Firstly, we observe that
T_{2}
is a second-order rational operator because of the assumption on
\mu
.
Then, it remains to show there is
T_{1}
such that
T_{0}\to T_{1}\to T_{2}
is a 2-step DT with
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
.
Defining
T_{1},A_{1},A_{2}

as per (
13
)-(
16
), with
b_{j},\upsilon_{j}
,
j\in\{1,2\}
as in the statement, the result then follows.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Observe that the construction of a confluent Darboux transformation amounts to applying the extended Crum Wronskian formula derived in Theorem
2.1
for
k=2
with the only modification that one of the seed functions of
T_{0}
is a true eigenfunction, but the second one is a generalized eigenfunction, i.e. apply the formulas in Theorem
2.1
with

\phi_{1}=\phi,\quad\phi_{2}=\phi^{(1)},\quad\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=\lambda.

Remark 3.7
.

The definition (
26
) of the second seed function
\phi^{(1)}
involves two indefinite integrals. The outer integral provides no extra freedom, because it means that
\phi^{(1)}
is defined up to an additive term
C\phi
but this term will vanish in the Wronskian
\phi_{(1,2)}
. The lower bound of the inner integral, however, introduces a free real parameter, which is a characteristic feature of the CDT.

Remark 3.8
.

The assumption that
\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{\rho}
be rational ensures that

\upsilon_{2}
, as defined above, is rational. This assumption may be restated as the condition that
\phi^{2}W
, the integrand of
(
30
), is a rational function with vanishing residues. Verifying the rationality of the CDT is key to ensure that the transformed operator has polynomial eigenfunctions.

Remark 3.9
.

The confluent aspect of a CDT comes from a conceptual formula for the generalized eigenvalue equation
(T_{0}-\lambda)\phi^{(1)}=\phi
. We note that, despite not being made explicit, the seed
eigenfunction
\phi
depends on the eigenvalue
\lambda
. This
dependence can be recovered by imposing initial conditions on
\phi

and
\phi^{\prime}
. Thus, starting from the eigenvalue equation

(T_{0}-\lambda)\phi=0
, we can differentiate with respect to

\lambda
to find that

(36)

\displaystyle(T_{0}-\lambda)\left[\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\lambda}\right]=\phi.

This equation implies a rather simple formula for defining
\phi^{(1)}
,
which is

(37)

\displaystyle\phi^{(1)}=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\lambda}.

This formula is not of much practical use, since the functional
dependence on
\lambda
is typically impossible to express
explicitly. However, this expression is of conceptual importance and
the derivative can be seen as a limiting case of the ordinary Darboux
transformation, where the eigenvalues converge as

\lambda_{2}\to\lambda_{1}
. Hence the name
confluent
Darboux
transformation.

In this section we have seen how to build a confluent Darboux transformation as a 2-step Darboux transformation with a generalized eigenfunction, introducing in the process a free real parameter.
This construction can be iterated at different eigenvalues to create chains of CDTs: perform a CDT
on the first operator
T_{0}
at eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
, which is
followed by a CDT on
T_{2}
at eigenvalue
\lambda_{2}\neq\lambda_{1}
, which yields the operator
T_{4}
, etc.

Chains of operators may be constructed through an arbitrary finite number of CDTs in this
fashion, thereby leading to an operator with an arbitrary number of free real parameters, which is Darboux connected to the original
T_{0}
. In the following section we show how to construct a CDT chain starting on the Gegenbauer polynomials, and leading to the exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials of the second kind.

4.
Exceptional Gegenbauer operators and polynomials

In this Section we apply the theory developed in Sections
2
and
3
to construct a chain of confluent Darboux transformations on the classical Gegenbauer operator.

4.1.
Definition of exceptional Gegenbauer operators and polynomials

In
[
12
]
it was shown that an exceptional operator in the Hermite, Laguerre or Jacobi class must have a very specific form. We define in this section exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials and operators attending to this particular form (as a particular class of exceptional Jacobi operators), postponing for later sections the discussion on how the construction of specific families is achieved.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\tau=\tau(z)
be a non-zero polynomial and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. We say that the differential expression

(38)

T^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}(z,D_{z}):=(1-z^{2})\left(D_{z}^{2}-2\frac{\tau_{z}}{\tau}D_{z}+\frac{\tau_{zz}}{\tau}\right)-(2\alpha+1)zD_{z}+(2\alpha-1)z\frac{\tau_{z}}{\tau}.

is an
exceptional Gegenbauer operator
if
T^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}
admits
eigenpolynomials
\{\pi_{i}(z)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
such that the degree
sequence
\{\deg\pi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is missing finitely many
“exceptional” degrees.

In the above definition, it should be stressed that only very specific
polynomials
\tau(z)
in (
38
) will lead to
T^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}

being an exceptional Gegenbauer operator, i.e. having an infinite
number of polynomial eigenfunctions. The following sections are
devoted to describing a class of polynomials
\tau
obtained by
applying a multi-step CDT on
\tau=1
, which ensures that this is
indeed the case.
Note that there is no restriction on the
parameter
\alpha
at this stage. In the following section we will see
that
\alpha
must be a half-integer for the Confluent Darboux
transformations to be rational. This means that for standard Darboux
transformations (see Section
2
) the parameter
\alpha

can be real, leading to
X
-Gegenbauer polynomials of the first kind,
but for confluent Darboux transformations (see Section
3
)
the parameter
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}+1/2
, leading to excecptional Gegenbauer
polynomials of the second kind.

Observe also, by contrast to classical orthogonal polynomials, that we
are not

assuming that
\deg\pi_{i}=i
. Furthermore, without loss of
generality, it will be convenient to assume that

\displaystyle\deg\pi_{i}\neq\deg\pi_{j}\;\mbox{ if }i\neq j.

As usual, we speak of exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials when the
eigenpolynomials
\{\pi_{i}(z)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
define a complete
orthogonal polynomial system.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\tau(z)
be a polynomial and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. We say that the set

\{\pi_{i}(z)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a family of
exceptional
Gegenbauer polynomials
with weight

(39)

W^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}(z):=\frac{(1-z^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}}{\tau(z)^{2}},

if the following conditions hold:

(a)

\tau(z)
does not vanish on
I=[-1,1]
;

(b)

\{\pi_{i}(z)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
are eigenpolynomials of an
X
-Gegenbauer operator (
38
);

(c)

The polynomials
\{\pi_{i}(z)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
form a complete set
in the Hilbert space
\mathrm{L}^{2}(I,W^{(\alpha)}_{\tau})
.

Note that there is no need to include an explicit orthogonality
assumption in the above definition, because orthogonality of the
eigenpolynomials follows from assumptions (a) and (b). Also note that
there is no need for supplementary assumptions regarding the
corresponding eigenvalues as these are necessarily
quadratic functions of the degree sequence. More specifically, we establish these results in the next two lemmas.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
T^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}
be an exceptional Gegenbauer operator and let
\{\pi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be its associated eigenpolynomials, i.e.

(40)

T^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}\pi_{i}=\lambda_{i}\pi_{i}

Then, necessarily

(41)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle=-d_{i}(2\alpha+d_{i}),\quad\text{where }d_{i}=\deg\pi_{i}-\deg\tau

Proof.

Let
\tau(z)
be a polynomial and
\pi_{i}(z)
an eigenpolynomial of

T^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
.
Expliciltly, by (
38
), we have

(42)

(1-z^{2})\left(\tau\pi_{i}^{\prime\prime}-2\tau^{\prime}\pi_{i}^{\prime}+\tau^{\prime\prime}\pi_{i}\right)-(2\alpha+1)z\tau\pi_{i}^{\prime}+(2\alpha-1)z\tau^{\prime}\pi_{i}=\lambda_{i}\tau\pi_{i}.

Let
n=\deg\pi_{i}
and
m=\deg\tau
and without loss of generality
suppose that both
\tau(z),\pi_{i}(z)
are monic; i.e.

\tau(z)=z^{m}+\ldots
and
\pi(z)=z^{n}+\ldots
. Notice that
the highest power of
z
on either side of (
42
) is

m+n
. Hence, for the above equation to hold, the two coefficients
on
z^{m+n}
must be equal. Considering only the highest power of

z
in each term, yields the equation

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}z^{m+n}=\left(-n(n-1)+2mn-m(m-1)-(2\alpha+1)n+(2\alpha-1)m\right)z^{m+n}.

Hence

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle=-n(n-1)+2mn-m(m-1)-(2\alpha+1)n+(2\alpha-1)m

\displaystyle=-n^{2}+2mn-m^{2}-2\alpha n+2\alpha m

\displaystyle=-(n-m)^{2}-2\alpha(n-m)

\displaystyle=-(n-m)(2\alpha+n-m),

as was to be shown.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

A family of exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials
\{\pi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is necessarily
orthogonal with respect to the corresponding weight (
39
):

(43)

\int_{I}\pi_{i}(z)\pi_{j}(z)W^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}(z)dz=0\;\mbox{ if }i\neq j.

Proof.

Multiplying the
eigenvalue equation
T^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}y=\lambda y
by
W^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}
yields
a Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue equation

(44)

(P^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}y^{\prime})^{\prime}+R^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}y=W^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}\lambda y,

where

\displaystyle P^{(\alpha)}

\displaystyle:=(1-z^{2})^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\tau^{-2}

\displaystyle R^{(\alpha)}

\displaystyle:=(1-z^{2})^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\tau_{zz}\tau^{-3}+(2\alpha-1)z(1-z^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\tau_{z}\tau^{-3}

Lagrange’s identity now gives

(45)

\int\left(y_{1}T^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}y_{2}-y_{2}T^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}y_{1}\right)W^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}=P^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}\Wr[y_{1},y_{2}].

Since the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
are distinct,
assumption (a) and (
45
) imply that the
eigenpolynomials satisfiy orthogonality relations.
∎

The base case of the class of exceptional Gegenbauer operators is the
classical Gegenbauer operator
T^{(\alpha)}=T^{(\alpha)}_{\tau_{0}}
, where

\tau_{0}(z)=1
. In this case the general form (
38
)
simplifies to (
1
) and the
eigenpolynomials are the classical Gegenbauer polynomials
[
2
]

(46)

C^{(\alpha)}_{i}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor i/2\rfloor}(-1)^{k}\frac{\Gamma(i-k+\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)k!(i-2k)!}(2z)^{i-2k}.

These classical orthogonal polynomials do have
\deg C^{(\alpha)}_{i}=i
, and they satisfy the eigenvalue relation

\displaystyle T^{(\alpha)}C^{(\alpha)}_{i}=\lambda_{i}C^{(\alpha)}_{i},\quad i\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

with
\lambda_{i}=-i(2\alpha+i)
. If
\alpha>-\frac{1}{2}
the polynomials
\{C^{(\alpha)}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
form
a complete set in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(I,W^{(\alpha)})
and they satisfy the orthogonality relation

\int_{I}C^{(\alpha)}_{i}(u)C^{(\alpha)}_{j}(u)W^{(\alpha)}(u)du=\nu^{(\alpha)}_{i}\delta_{ij},\quad i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

where

(47)

\displaystyle W^{(\alpha)}(z)

\displaystyle:=W^{(\alpha)}_{\tau_{0}}(z)=(1-z^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}};

(48)

\displaystyle\nu^{(\alpha)}_{i}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\pi 2^{1-2\alpha}\Gamma(i+2\alpha)}{i!(i+\alpha)\Gamma(\alpha)^{2}},\quad i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

We can now apply the theory of CDTs to generate exceptional orthogonal
polynomials.

Once the class of exceptional Gegenbauer operators and polynomials has been defined, we will show that this class is not empty by describing in the next sections the construction of exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials of the second kind via a sequence of confluent Darboux transforamtions.

4.2.
Factorizations of exceptional Gegenbauer operators

Let us start by describing the factorization of an
X
-Gegenbauer operator (
38
) in order to define a
1
-step Darboux transformation. After that, we will combine two Darboux transformations to define a CDT.

Given rational functions

\tau(z)
and
\pi(z)
, and a real constant
\alpha
, we define the
following two first-order rational operators:

(49)

\displaystyle A_{\tau\pi}(z,D_{z})

\displaystyle:=\tau(z)^{-1}(\pi(z)D_{z}-\pi^{\prime}(z)),

\displaystyle B^{(\alpha)}_{\pi\tau}(z,D_{z})

\displaystyle:=(1-z^{2})A_{\pi\tau}(z,D_{z})-(2\alpha+1)z\tau(z)\pi(z)^{-1}.

It will be useful to express the above
operators in terms of Wronskian determinants by setting

(50)

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}(z)

\displaystyle:=(1-z^{2})^{-\alpha-\frac{3}{2}}\pi(z),

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}(z)

\displaystyle:=(1-z^{2})^{-\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\tau(z),

so that

\displaystyle A_{\tau\pi}y=\tau^{-1}\Wr[\pi,y],

\displaystyle B^{(\alpha)}_{\pi\tau}y=\hat{\pi}^{-1}\Wr[\hat{\tau},y].

Observe that it would be sufficient to define only
A_{\tau\pi}
since
the two operators are related by

(51)

\displaystyle B^{(\alpha)}_{\pi\tau}=A_{\hat{\pi}\hat{\tau}}.

However, defining both operators separately will make for simpler notation going forward.
With these first order operators, we can now describe the factorization of an
X
-Gegenabuer operator (
38
) in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
\tau(z)
be a polynomial,
and
T^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}
be an exceptional Gegenabuer operator as in (
38
). Assume that
\pi(z)
is an eigenpolynomial
of
T^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}
with eigenvalue

\lambda
, i.e.
T^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}\pi=\lambda\pi
. We then have the following factorizations:

(52)

\displaystyle B^{(\alpha)}_{\pi\tau}A_{\tau\pi}

\displaystyle=T^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}-\lambda,

\displaystyle A_{\tau\pi}B^{(\alpha)}_{\pi\tau}

\displaystyle=T^{(\alpha+1)}_{\pi}-\hat{\lambda},

where

(53)

\hat{\lambda}=\lambda+2\alpha+1.

Proof.

The proof follows by a straightforward computation.
∎

We say that the transformation

T^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}\to T^{(\alpha+1)}_{\pi}-(2\alpha+1)
is a formally

state-deleting
Darboux transformation, because the second
operator no longer has an eigenpolynomial at the eigenvalue
\lambda
.
Likewise, we refer to the transformation

T^{(\alpha+1)}_{\pi}\to T^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}{+(2\alpha+1)}
as a formally
state-adding

Darboux transformation, because the operator
T^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}
gains an extra
polynomial eigenfunction
\pi
at eigenvalue
\lambda
. The following
proposition describes the factorization eigenfunction for this dual
factorization.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
\tau(z)
and
\pi(z)
be non-zero polynomials and let
\hat{\tau}
be as
in (
50
).
Suppose that the following eigenvalue equation holds:

T^{(\alpha+1)}_{\pi}\hat{\tau}=\hat{\lambda}\hat{\tau}
. Then the factorizations (
52
)
also hold.

Proof.

By direct calculation, we obtain that

(54)

\pi T_{\pi}^{(\alpha+1)}\hat{\tau}=\hat{\tau}T_{\tau}^{(\alpha)}\pi+(2\alpha+1)\pi\hat{\tau}.

Hence,
T^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}\pi=\lambda\pi
is equivalent to

T^{(\alpha+1)}_{\pi}\hat{\tau}=\hat{\lambda}\hat{\tau}
.
This, together with (
51
), implies the result.

∎

4.3.
Confluent Darboux transformations of exceptional Gegenbauer operators

In this section, we apply the theory of confluent Darboux transformations developed in Section
3
to exceptional Gegenbauer operators. With the factorizations introduced in the previous section, we will realize a CDT of an exceptional
Gegenbauer operator
T_{0}
by a state-deleting
transformation
T_{0}\to T_{1}
followed by a 1-parameter family of
state-adding transformations
T_{1}\to T_{2}
at the same eigenvalue. Next we derive certain recursive formulas that connect the
\tau
-functions and eigenpolynomials of two
X
-Gegenbauer operators connected by a CDT.

Starting from the classical Gegenbauer operator we will construct a chain of CDTs and a recursive construction of exceptional Gegenbauer operators of second kind.
This recursive construction will be discussed in more detail in the
following section.

Proposition 4.9

For a given

\displaystyle T_{1}:=T^{(\alpha+1)}_{\pi_{m}}-(2\alpha+1),\quad T_{2}:=T^{(\alpha)}_{\tau_{m}},

\displaystyle A_{1}:=A_{\tau,\pi_{m}},\quad A_{2}:=B^{(\alpha)}_{\pi_{m},\tau_{m}}

4.5

(58)

A_{1}T_{0}=T_{1}A_{1}.

We now claim that the transformation

(59)

\displaystyle\pi_{m}^{(1)}(z;t):=\pi_{m}(z)\int_{-1}^{z}(1+t\rho_{mm}(s))\hat{\tau}(s)^{2}W_{\pi_{m}}^{(\alpha+1)}(s)ds,

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}(z):=(1-z^{2})^{-\alpha-1/2}\tau(z)

(61)

Proposition 4.10
.

The polynomials
\pi_{m,i}(z;t)
as defined in (

are eigenfunctions of

.

Proof.

Combining the intertwining relations in the preceding proof, we have

(62)

A_{21}T_{0}=T_{2}A_{21},\quad\text{where }A_{21}:=A_{2}A_{1}.

(63)

A_{21}\pi_{i}=(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{m})\pi_{m,i},\quad i\neq m.

\displaystyle\Wr[\pi_{m},\pi_{i}]=(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{m})\mu\,\rho_{mi}.

\displaystyle A_{1}\pi_{i}=\tau^{-1}\Wr[\pi_{m},\pi_{i}]=(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{m})\hat{\tau}\rho_{mi}.

\displaystyle A_{2}y

\displaystyle=\hat{\pi}_{m}^{-1}\Wr[\hat{\tau}_{m},y]=\hat{\pi}_{m}^{-1}\Wr[\hat{\tau}(1+t\rho_{mm}),y]

\displaystyle A_{21}\pi_{i}

The last proposition of this section shows that the CDT of an

\displaystyle\rho_{m;ij}(z)

Proposition 4.11
.

Let

\displaystyle\rho_{m;ij}(z,t)=\rho_{ij}(z)-\frac{t\rho_{mi}(z)\rho_{mj}(z)}{1+t\rho_{mm}(z)},\quad m,i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

\displaystyle\left(\rho_{ij}-\frac{t\rho_{mi}\rho_{mj}}{1+t\rho_{mm}}\right)^{\prime}=\rho_{ij}^{\prime}-\frac{t\rho_{mi}^{\prime}\rho_{mj}}{1+t\rho_{mm}}-\frac{t\rho_{mi}\rho_{mj}^{\prime}}{1+t\rho_{mm}}+\frac{t^{2}\rho_{mi}\rho_{mj}}{(1+t\rho_{mm})^{2}}\rho_{mm}^{\prime}

The

∎

In order to demonstrate that the

Proof.

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}_{i}(z)

\displaystyle:=\left(W^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}(z)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\pi_{i}(z)=(1-z^{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{1}{4}}\frac{\pi(z)}{\tau(z)},

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}_{m;i}(z)

\displaystyle:=\left(W^{(\alpha)}_{\tau_{m}}(z)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\pi_{m;i}(z)=(1-z^{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{1}{4}}\frac{\pi_{m;i}(z)}{\tau_{m}(z)}.

By assumption, the eigenpolynomials

\mathrm{L}^{2}(I,W^{(\alpha)}_{\tau}(z)dz)

. We seek

\mathrm{L}^{2}(I,dz)

Following an argument adapted from the appendix of

[
1
]

in

is equivalent to

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\nu_{i}^{-1}\tilde{\pi}_{i}(z)\tilde{\pi}_{i}(w)=\delta(z-w),

where the equality must be understood in distributional sense on

I\times I
.
Let

be piece-wise continuous function in
I
and set

(72)

[f]_{i}=\nu_{i}^{-1}\int_{I}\tilde{\pi}_{i}(u)f(u)du.

Relation (
71

(73)

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}[f]_{i}\,\tilde{\pi}_{i}(z)=f(z),\quad\text{a.e. }z\in I.

Moreover, if

is another piece-wise continuous in
I

function, then

(74)

\int_{I}f(u)g(u)du=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\nu_{i}[f]_{i}[g]_{i}.

For a given
w\in I
, and a function

, define the
truncation operator

(\mathcal{T}_{w}f)(z):=\theta(w-z)f(z),\quad z\in I,

where

(75)

\theta(u):=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }u>0,\\
\frac{1}{2}&\text{ if }u=0,\\
0&\text{ if }u<0;\end{cases}

denotes the Heaviside step function. We may now rewrite
(
55

\rho_{ij}(w)=\int_{I}(\mathcal{T}_{w}\tilde{\pi}_{j})(u)\,\tilde{\pi}_{i}(u)\,du=\nu_{i}[\mathcal{T}_{w}\tilde{\pi}_{j}]_{i},\quad i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\;w\in I.

Applying (
73

(76)

\theta(w-z)\tilde{\pi}_{j}(z)=(\mathcal{T}_{w}\tilde{\pi}_{j})(z)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\nu_{i}^{-1}\rho_{ij}(w)\tilde{\pi}_{i}(z),\quad z,w\in I.

Moreover, (
74
) implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\nu_{i}^{-1}\rho_{ij}(z)\rho_{ij}(w)

\displaystyle=\int_{I}(\mathcal{T}_{w}\tilde{\pi}_{j})(u)(\mathcal{T}_{z}\tilde{\pi}_{j})(u)du,\quad z,w\in I,\;j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Observe that

\theta(z)\theta(w)=\theta(\min(z,w)),\quad z,w\in I.

Hence,

\int_{I}(\mathcal{T}_{w}\tilde{\pi}_{j})(u)(\mathcal{T}_{z}\tilde{\pi}_{j})(u)du=\int_{I}\theta(w-u)\theta(z-u)\tilde{\pi}_{j}(u)\tilde{\pi}_{j}(u)\,du=\rho_{jj}(\min(z,w)).

Therefore,

(77)

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\nu_{i}^{-1}\rho_{ij}(z)\rho_{ij}(w)=\theta(w-z)\rho_{jj}(z)+\theta(z-w)\rho_{jj}(w).

By (
56
), (
57
) and (
70
),

(78)

\displaystyle 1+t\rho_{mm}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau_{m}}{\tau};

(79)

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}_{m;i}

\displaystyle=(1-z^{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{1}{4}}\frac{1}{\tau_{m}}\left(\frac{\tau_{m}}{\tau}\pi_{i}-t\rho_{im}\pi_{m}\right)=\tilde{\pi}_{i}-t\rho_{im}\tilde{\pi}_{m;m};

(80)

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}_{m}

\displaystyle=(1+t\rho_{mm})\tilde{\pi}_{m;m}.

By (
66
), (
79
),
(
71
), (
76
), (
77
) and
(

), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}

\displaystyle\nu_{m;i}^{-1}\tilde{\pi}_{m;i}(z)\tilde{\pi}_{m;i}(w)

\displaystyle=t\tilde{\pi}_{m;m}(z)\tilde{\pi}_{m;m}(w)+\sum_{i}\nu_{i}^{-1}\tilde{\pi}_{m;i}(z)\tilde{\pi}_{m;i}(w)

\displaystyle=t\tilde{\pi}_{m;m}(z)\tilde{\pi}_{m;m}(w)+\sum_{i}\nu_{i}^{-1}\left(\tilde{\pi}_{i}(z)-t\rho_{im}(z)\tilde{\pi}_{m;m}(z)\right)\left(\tilde{\pi}_{i}(w)-t\rho_{im}(w)\tilde{\pi}_{m;m}(w)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\nu_{i}^{-1}\tilde{\pi}_{i}(z)\tilde{\pi}_{i}(w)+t\tilde{\pi}_{m;m}(z)\tilde{\pi}_{m;m}(w)\left(1+t\sum_{i}\nu_{i}^{-1}\rho_{im}(z)\rho_{im}(w)\right)

\displaystyle\quad-t\,\tilde{\pi}_{m;m}(z)\sum_{i}\nu_{i}^{-1}\rho_{im}(z)\tilde{\pi}_{i}(w)-t\,\tilde{\pi}_{m;m}(w)\sum_{i}\nu_{i}^{-1}\rho_{im}(w)\tilde{\pi}_{i}(z)

\displaystyle=\delta(z-w)+t\tilde{\pi}_{m;m}(z)\tilde{\pi}_{m;m}(w)\left(1+\theta(w-z)t\rho_{mm}(z)+\theta(z-w)t\rho_{mm}(w)\right)

\displaystyle\quad-t\,\theta(z-w)\tilde{\pi}_{m;m}(z)\tilde{\pi}_{m}(w)-t\,\theta(w-z)\tilde{\pi}_{m}(z)\tilde{\pi}_{m;m}(w)

\displaystyle=\delta(z-w)+t\tilde{\pi}_{m;m}(z)\tilde{\pi}_{m;m}(w)\Big(1+\theta(w-z)t\rho_{mm}(z)+\theta(z-w)t\rho_{mm}(w)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\theta(z-w)(1+t\rho_{mm}(w))-\theta(w-z)(1+t\rho_{mm}(z))\Big)

\displaystyle=\delta(z-w)+t\tilde{\pi}_{m;m}(z)\tilde{\pi}_{m;m}(w)\Big(1-\theta(w-z)-\theta(z-w)\Big)

\displaystyle=\delta(z-w).

We conclude that the set

\mathrm{L}^{2}(I,W^{(\alpha)}_{\tau_{m}}(z)dz)

5.
Exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials of the second kind

In this Section we provide explicit formulas for the construction of exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials of the second kind and their associated operators in terms of a
matrix whose entries involve classical Gegenbauer
polynomials. Throughout this section, we also assume that
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}
is a
positive half-integer. Note that, with this assumption in place, the
weight
W^{(\alpha)}(z)
in (
47
) becomes a polynomial.

We next define several objects that allow us to describe the exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials and operators below.
Set

(81)

\rho^{(\alpha)}_{ij}(z):=\int_{-1}^{z}C^{(\alpha)}_{i}(u)C^{(\alpha)}_{j}(u)W^{(\alpha)}\!(u)\,du,\quad i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

and observe that the above functions are polynomials precisely because

\alpha
is a positive half-integer. Given an
n
–tuple
{\boldsymbol{m}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}

and the associated
\boldsymbol{t}_{\boldsymbol{m}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we then define

{\mathcal{R}}^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}={\mathcal{R}}^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}(z;\boldsymbol{t}_{\boldsymbol{m}})
as the
n\times n
matrix with
polynomial entries given by

(82)

\displaystyle[{\mathcal{R}}^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}]_{k\ell}=\delta_{k\ell}+t_{m_{\ell}}\rho^{(\alpha)}_{m_{k}m_{\ell}}(z),\quad k,\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\},

We denote its determinant by

(83)

\displaystyle\tau^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}:=\det{\mathcal{R}}^{(\alpha)}_{\boldsymbol{m}}.

Next, define the
n
–tuple of polynomials

(84)

\displaystyle\left({\boldsymbol{Q}}^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}{}\right)^{T}:=\tau^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}\left({\mathcal{R}}^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}\right)^{-1}\left(C^{(\alpha)}_{m_{1}},\ldots,C^{(\alpha)}_{m_{n}}\right)^{T},

We are now ready to define the fundamental objects of this Section.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{m}}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
be a tuple of distinct integers and
{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}
the
n
-tuple of polynomials defined by (
81
)-(
84
). We define the
exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials of the second kind
associated to
{\boldsymbol{m}}
as

(85)

\displaystyle C^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};i}:=\big[{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{(\alpha)}_{({\boldsymbol{m}},i)}\big]_{n+1},\quad i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Remark 5.2
.

Note that, by construction,
\tau^{(\alpha)}_{\boldsymbol{m}}=\tau^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}(z;\boldsymbol{t}_{\boldsymbol{m}})
is
invariant with respect to permutations of the indices

{\boldsymbol{m}}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})
and that

{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}={\boldsymbol{Q}}^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}(z;\boldsymbol{t}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}})
is equivariant with respect to
such permutations. In addition,

C^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};i}=C^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};i}(z;\boldsymbol{t}_{\boldsymbol{m}})
is symmetric in
{\boldsymbol{m}}
and does
not depend on
t_{i}
since

\tau^{(\alpha)}_{({\boldsymbol{m}},i)}\big[\left({\mathcal{R}}^{(\alpha)}_{({\boldsymbol{m}},i)}\right)^{-1}\big]_{n+1,j}

correspond to the minors of the last column of
{\mathcal{R}}^{(\alpha)}_{({\boldsymbol{m}},i)}
, the
only column where
t_{i}
appears.

The main result of this section states that the polynomials

\big\{C^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};i}(z;\boldsymbol{t}_{\boldsymbol{m}})\big\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
defined above are indeed exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials (in the sense of Definition
4.2
), provided the
real parameters
\boldsymbol{t}_{\boldsymbol{m}}
satisfy certain constraints to ensure that

\tau^{(\alpha)}_{\boldsymbol{m}}(z;t_{\boldsymbol{m}})
is positive on
z\in[-1,1]
. We first state that the polynomial
\tau_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}^{(\alpha)}
inserted in (
38
) is an exceptional Gegenbauer operator in the sense of Definition
4.1
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}
and
{\boldsymbol{m}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
. Consider the
n
-parameter family of
operators
T^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}:=T^{(\alpha)}_{\tau^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}}
given by
(
38
) and (
83
). For each value of the
parameters
\boldsymbol{t}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}
, this operator is an exceptional Gegenbauer
operator that satisfies

(86)

T^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}C^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};i}=\lambda_{i}C^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};i},\quad i\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

with
\lambda_{i}=-i(2\alpha+i)
.

The following theorem provides necessary and sufficient conditions for the polynomials
\{C^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
to be a family of exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials according to Definition
4.2
. If these conditions hold the family

\{C^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};i}(z;\boldsymbol{t}_{\boldsymbol{m}})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is orthogonal and
complete, like their classical counterparts.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}
and
{\boldsymbol{m}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
with
m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}
distinct. Then the polynomial

\tau^{(\alpha)}_{\boldsymbol{m}}(z;\boldsymbol{t}_{\boldsymbol{m}})
in (
83
) has no zeros on

z\in[-1,1]
if and only if

(87)

t_{m_{j}}>-\left(\nu^{(\alpha)}_{m_{j}}\right)^{-1},\quad j=1,\ldots,n,

with
\nu^{(\alpha)}_{m_{j}}
as in (

If the above conditions hold, then

\big\{C^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};i}(z;\boldsymbol{t}_{\boldsymbol{m}})\big\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
are exceptional
Gegenbauer polynomials with weight
W^{(\alpha)}_{\tau_{m}}
and norms
given by

(88)

\int_{I}\left(C^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};i}(u)\right)^{2}W^{(\alpha)}_{\tau_{m}}(u)du\ =\frac{\nu^{(\alpha)}_{i}}{1+\delta_{i,{\boldsymbol{m}}}t_{i}\nu^{(\alpha)}_{i}}

where

(89)

\delta_{i,{\boldsymbol{m}}}:=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }i\in\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}\};\\

As mentioned above, the degree of the
i
-th exceptional Gegenbauer
polynomial

is not necessarily
i
. The next
proposition provides this result. It is also worth noting that, as

sequence of the exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials is not an
increasing sequence, which is further evidence of the different

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}
and
{\boldsymbol{m}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
with
m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}
distinct. Let

\tau^{(\alpha)}_{\boldsymbol{m}},C^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};i}

(90)

\displaystyle\deg_{z}\tau^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}=2(m_{1}+\ldots+m_{n}+\alpha n),

(91)

\displaystyle\deg_{z}C^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};i}=2(m_{1}+\ldots+m_{n}+\alpha n)+i-2\delta_{i,{\boldsymbol{m}}}(i+\alpha),\quad i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Moreover,

(92)

C^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};m_{k}}=C^{(\alpha)}_{m_{1},\ldots,\widehat{m_{k}},\ldots,m_{n};m_{k}},\quad k=1,\ldots,n,

where the hat symbol denotes the omission of the
k^{\text{th}}
entry
of
{\boldsymbol{m}}
.

Remark 5.4
.

From Proposition
5.3
, we see that the codimension
(number of missing degrees) of the exceptional Gegenbauer family
indexed by
{\boldsymbol{m}}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})
is
2(m_{1}+\cdots+m_{n}+\alpha n)
. As
it happens for all
exceptional polynomials
[
12
]
, this coincides with the degree of
\tau^{(\alpha)}_{\boldsymbol{m}}
.

Remark 5.5
.

Below we will show that the
n
-parameter family

\big\{C^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};i}(z;t_{\boldsymbol{m}})\big\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is the result of applying
n
confluent Darboux transformations to the classical family

\big\{C^{(\alpha)}_{i}(z)\big\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. Identity (
88
) then tells us that a single
CDT leaves invariant all but one of the norming
constants. By contrast, identity (
92
) tells us that after a
single CDT there is exactly one polynomial that remains the same but
whose norm undergoes a change. It is this phenomenon that accounts
for the Kronecker delta term in (
91
).

Remark 5.6
.

The degree formulas (
90
), (
91
) allow us to
relate the eigenvalue formula (
41
) with the value

\lambda_{i}=-i(2\alpha+i)
given in Theorem

. Indeed if

then

in full agreement with
(

for some

then

and therefore

d_{i}(2\alpha+d_{i})=(i+2\alpha)i.

Hence, (
41

In Theorem
5.2
and Proposition
4.3
we have considered the case when
{\boldsymbol{m}}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})
contains
distinct indices. Let us now show that this choice entails no loss of

Proposition 5.7

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}

By (

), we have

\tilde{\pi}_{j;m_{j}}=\tilde{\pi}_{j-1,m_{j}},\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

Identity (
92
) then follows by (
98
).
Thanks to (
92
) no generality is lost by assuming that

i\notin\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}\}
. We use induction to show that

(99)

\displaystyle\deg\tilde{\tau}_{j}

\displaystyle=2(m_{1}+\cdots+m_{j}+\alpha j),\quad j=0,1,\ldots,n.

(100)

\displaystyle\deg\tilde{\pi}_{j;i}

\displaystyle=\deg\tilde{\tau}_{j}+i.

The desired relations (
90
), (
91
) then
follow by (
97
) and (
98
).

By inspection, (
99
) and (
100
) hold for

j=0
. Assume that these relations hold for a given
j<n
. By
(
96
),

\tilde{\rho}_{j,m_{j+1}m_{j+1}}(z)=\int^{z}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}-1}}\left(\frac{\tilde{\pi}_{m_{j+1}}(u)}{\tilde{\tau}_{j}(u)}\right)^{2}\tilde{W}_{0}(u)du.

Since
m_{j+1}\notin\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{j}\}
, by the inductive hypothesis,

\deg_{z}\tilde{\rho}_{j,m_{j+1}m_{j+1}}=2m_{j+1}+2\alpha,

where the degree of a rational function is
understood as the
difference between the degrees of the numerator and
the denominator. Hence,

\deg_{z}\tilde{\tau}_{j+1}=2m_{j+1}+2\alpha+\deg_{z}\tilde{\tau}_{j},

which agrees with (
99
) for the
j+1
case.

By (
94
)
and (
96
),
we have

\tilde{\pi}_{j+1;i}=\tilde{\pi}_{j;i}+t_{m_{j+1}}\Pi,

where

\Pi(z)=\tilde{\pi}_{j;i}(z)\int^{z}_{-1}\frac{\tilde{\pi}_{j;m_{j+1}}(u)}{\tilde{\tau}_{j}(u)}\frac{\tilde{\pi}_{j;m_{j+1}}(u)}{\tilde{\tau}_{j}(u)}\tilde{W}_{0}(u)du-\tilde{\pi}_{j;m_{j+1}}(z)\int^{z}_{-1}\frac{\tilde{\pi}_{j;i}(u)}{\tilde{\tau}_{j}(u)}\frac{\tilde{\pi}_{j;m_{j+1}}(u)}{\tilde{\tau}_{j}(u)}\tilde{W}_{0}(u)du.

Since
i\neq m_{j+1}
, the leading degree terms in the above difference do
not cancel and hence

\deg_{z}\tilde{\pi}_{j+1;i}=\deg_{z}\Pi=2m_{j+1}+2\alpha+\deg\tilde{\pi}_{j;i}.

Hence (
100
) also holds for
j+1
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
5.7
.

We apply Proposition
5.9
and the definitions
(

), (

) twice to obtain

\displaystyle\tau^{(\alpha)}_{({\boldsymbol{m}},j,j)}

\displaystyle=\left(1+t^{\prime}_{j}\rho^{(\alpha)}_{({\boldsymbol{m}},j);jj}\right)\tau^{(\alpha)}_{({\boldsymbol{m}},j)}

\displaystyle=\left(1+t^{\prime}_{j}\rho^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};jj}-\frac{(t^{\prime}_{j})^{2}\left(\rho^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};jj}\right)^{2}}{1+t^{\prime}_{j}\rho^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};jj}}\right)\left(1+t_{j}\rho^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};jj}\right)\tau^{(\alpha)}_{\boldsymbol{m}}

\displaystyle=\left(1+(t_{j}+t_{j}^{\prime})\rho^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};jj}\right)\tau^{(\alpha)}_{\boldsymbol{m}}

\displaystyle=\tau^{(\alpha)}_{({\boldsymbol{m}},j)}(z;{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}(}\boldsymbol{t}_{\boldsymbol{m}},t_{j}+t_{j}^{\prime})),

\displaystyle C^{(\alpha)}_{({\boldsymbol{m}},j,j);i}

\displaystyle=\left(1+t^{\prime}_{j}\rho^{(\alpha)}_{({\boldsymbol{m}},j);jj}\right)C^{(\alpha)}_{({\boldsymbol{m}},j);i}-t^{\prime}_{j}\rho^{(\alpha)}_{({\boldsymbol{m}},j);ij}C^{(\alpha)}_{({\boldsymbol{m}},j);j}

6.
Examples

We conclude by showing some explicit examples of exceptional Gegenbauer
polynomials of the second kind, together with their properties. It can be readily checked that these families are an isospectral deformation of the classical Gegenbauer polynomials.

6.1.
1-parameter exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials of the second kind

The 1-parameter exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials arise after a
single CDT on the classical operator. For

m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we follow definitions
(
56
)–(
57
) to write

\displaystyle\tau^{(\alpha)}_{m}(z,t_{m})=1+t_{m}\rho^{(\alpha)}_{mm}(z),

\displaystyle C^{(\alpha)}_{m;i}(z,t_{m})=\left(1+t_{m}\rho^{(\alpha)}_{mm}(z)\right)C^{(\alpha)}_{i}(z)-t_{m}\rho^{(\alpha)}_{im}(z)C^{(\alpha)}_{m}(z),

with
\rho^{(\alpha)}_{ij}
as per (
81
) and
C^{(\alpha)}_{i}
the classical
Gegenbauer polynomials. Notice that by construction, we have

C^{(\alpha)}_{m;m}=C^{(\alpha)}_{m}
. The
\{C^{(\alpha)}_{m;i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a
family of exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials with weight

W^{(\alpha)}_{m}(z)=\frac{(1-z^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}}{\big(\tau^{(\alpha)}_{m}(z)\big)^{2}},

as long as
t_{m}
satisfies the inequality

\displaystyle t_{m}>-\left(\nu^{(\alpha)}_{m}\right)^{-1}=-\frac{2^{2\alpha-1}m!(m+\alpha)\Gamma(\alpha)^{2}}{\pi\Gamma(m+2\alpha)}.

The orthogonality relations are

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}C^{(\alpha)}_{m;i}(z,t_{m})C^{(\alpha)}_{m;j}(z,t_{m})W^{(\alpha)}_{m}(z)dz=\delta_{ij}\nu^{(\alpha)}_{i}=\delta_{ij}\frac{\pi 2^{1-2\alpha}\Gamma(i+2\alpha)}{i!(i+\alpha)\Gamma(\alpha)^{2}},\quad i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\backslash\{m\}

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}C^{(\alpha)}_{m;m}(z,t_{m})^{2}W^{(\alpha)}_{m}(z,t_{m})dz=\frac{\nu^{(\alpha)}_{m}}{1+t\nu^{(\alpha)}_{m}}=\frac{\pi 2^{1-2\alpha}\Gamma(m+2\alpha)}{m!(m+\alpha)\Gamma(\alpha)^{2}+t_{m}\pi 2^{1-2\alpha}\Gamma(m+2\alpha)}.

The function
\tau^{(\alpha)}_{m}
is shown below in Figure

, for a particular choice of the parameters
\alpha,m,t_{m}
.

6.2.
2-parameter exceptional Gegenbauer polynomials of the second kind

We construct the 2-parameter exceptional polynomials by applying the
recursive construction to the 1-parameter formulas. We start with

{\boldsymbol{m}}=(m_{1},m_{2})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}
and the associated tuple

\boldsymbol{t}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}=(t_{m_{1}},t_{m_{2}})
. Following equations
(
55
)–(
57
), and using (
68
), we
find that

\displaystyle\tau^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}(z;\boldsymbol{t}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}})=\tau^{(\alpha)}_{m_{1}}(z,t_{m_{1}})\tau^{(\alpha)}_{m_{2}}(z,t_{m_{2}})-t_{m_{1}}t_{m_{2}}\rho^{(\alpha)}_{m_{1}m_{2}}(z)^{2},

\displaystyle C^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};i}(z;\boldsymbol{t}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}})=C^{(\alpha)}_{i}(z)\tau^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}(z;\boldsymbol{t}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}})-t_{m_{1}}C^{(\alpha)}_{m_{1}}(z)\tau^{(\alpha)}_{m_{2}}(z;t_{m_{2}})\rho^{(\alpha)}_{m_{2};m_{1}i}(z,t_{m_{2}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-t_{m_{2}}C^{(\alpha)}_{m_{2}}(z)\tau^{(\alpha)}_{m_{1}}(z;t_{m_{2}})\rho^{(\alpha)}_{m_{1};m_{2}i}(z,t_{m_{1}}),

where

\displaystyle\rho^{(\alpha)}_{m;ij}(z,t_{m})

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{z}C^{(\alpha)}_{m;i}(u,t_{m})C^{(\alpha)}_{m;j}(u,t_{m})W^{(\alpha)}_{m}(u)du

\displaystyle=\rho^{(\alpha)}_{ij}(z)-\frac{t_{m}\rho^{(\alpha)}_{mi}(z)\rho^{(\alpha)}_{mj}(z)}{1+t_{m}\rho^{(\alpha)}_{mm}(z)},\qquad m,i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Once again, the polynomials form a complete orthogonal basis relative
to the weight

W^{(\alpha)}_{\boldsymbol{m}}(z)=(1-z^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\tau^{(\alpha)}_{m_{1}m_{2}}(z;t_{m_{1}},t_{m_{2}})^{-2},

provided

t_{m_{i}}>-\frac{2^{2\alpha-1}m_{i}!(m_{i}+\alpha)\Gamma(\alpha)^{2}}{\pi\Gamma(m_{i}+2\alpha)},\quad i=1,2.

The orthogonality relations are

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}C^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};i}(z,\boldsymbol{t}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}})C^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};j}(z,\boldsymbol{t}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}})W^{(\alpha)}_{\boldsymbol{m}}(z,\boldsymbol{t}_{\boldsymbol{m}})dz=\delta_{ij}\frac{\pi 2^{1-2\alpha}\Gamma(i+2\alpha)}{i!(i+\alpha)\Gamma(\alpha)^{2}},\quad i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\backslash\{m_{1},m_{2}\},

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}C^{(\alpha)}_{{\boldsymbol{m}};m_{i}}(z,\boldsymbol{t}_{{\boldsymbol{m}}})^{2}W^{(\alpha)}_{\boldsymbol{m}}(z,\boldsymbol{t}_{\boldsymbol{m}})dz=\frac{\pi 2^{1-2\alpha}\Gamma(m_{i}+2\alpha)}{m_{i}!(m_{i}+\alpha)\Gamma(\alpha)^{2}+t_{m_{i}}\pi 2^{1-2\alpha}\Gamma(m_{i}+2\alpha)},\quad i=1,2.
