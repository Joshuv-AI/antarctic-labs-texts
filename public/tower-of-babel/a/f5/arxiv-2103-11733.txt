The giant in random graphs is almost local
Remco van der Hofstadlabel=e1]r.w.v.d.hofstad@tue.nl

Abstract
Local convergence techniques have become a key methodology to study sparse random graphs. However, convergence of many random graph properties does not directly follow from local convergence. A notable, and important, such random graph property is the size and uniqueness of the giant component. We provide a simple criterion that guarantees that local convergence of a random graph implies the convergence of the proportion of vertices in the maximal connected component. We further show that, when this condition holds, the local properties of the giant are also described by the local limit. We give several examples where this method gives rise to a novel law of large numbers for the giant, based on results proved in the literature. Aside from these examples, we apply our novel method to the configuration model as a proof of concept, reproving a well-established result. As a side result of this proof, we identify the small-world nature of the configuration model.

Introduction

Local convergence
techniques, as introduced by Aldous and Steele
[
3
]
and Benjamini and Schramm
[
11
]
, have become the main methodology to study random graphs in sparse settings where the average degree remains bounded. Local convergence roughly means that the proportion of vertices whose neighborhoods have a certain shape converges to some limiting value, which is to be considered as a measure on rooted graphs. We refer to
[
43
, Chapter 2]
for details.

The
giant component
problem has received enormous attention ever since the very first and seminal results by Erdős and Rényi on the Erdős-Rényi random graph
[
34
]
, see also
[
17
,
53
]
for detailed further results for this model. The simplest form of this question is whether there exists a linear-size connected component or not, in many cases a sharp transition occurs depending on a certain graph parameter. See
[
6
,
27
,
33
,
37
,
38
,
42
,
43
,
55
]
, as well as the references therein, for details.

This paper combines these two threads by investigating the size of the giant component when the random graph converges locally. Consider a sequence of random graphs
(G_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, where we will simplify the notation by assuming that
G_{n}=(V(G_{n}),E(G_{n}))
is such that
|V(G_{n})|=n
. For
v\in V(G_{n})
, we let
\mathscr{C}(v)
denote its connected component. We let

|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|=\max_{v\in V(G_{n})}|\mathscr{C}(v)|

(1.1)

denote the maximal component size. When the random graph converges locally to some limit, one would expect that also
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\zeta
, where
\zeta
is the survival probability of the local limit. However, while the
number
of connected components is well behaved in the local topology, the
proportion of vertices in the giant
is not. Indeed, since local convergence is all about proportions of vertices whose
finite
(but arbitrarily large) neighborhoods converge to a limit, there is an enormous gap between surviving locally and being in the giant. In this paper, we identify the extra condition that is necessary and sufficient for the above natural implication to hold.

2
Asymptotics and properties of the giant

In this section, we investigate the behavior of the giant component
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|
for random graphs that converge locally. In Section
2.1
, we introduce the notion of local convergence in probability. In Section
2.2
, we study the asymptotics of
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|
for random graphs that converge locally in probability under necessary and sufficient ‘giant-is-almost-local’ condition, and in Section
2.3
, we investigate local properties of the giant. In Section
2.4
, we provide examples for the law of large numbers of the giant, obtained through bounds on the second largest component proved elsewhere. In Section
2.5
, we give an alternative for the ‘giant-is-almost-local’ condition that is often more convenient to establish. We start by introducing some useful notation used throughout this paper.

Notation.

We abbreviate left- and right-hand side by lhs and rhs, respectively.
A sequence of random variables
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}

converges in probability
to a random variable
X
, denoted as
X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\rightarrow}}X
, if, for all
\varepsilon>0
,
{\mathbb{P}}(|X_{n}-X|>\varepsilon)\rightarrow 0
.
We write
X_{n}=o_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}(1)
when
X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
.
A sequence of events
(\mathcal{E}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is said to hold
with high probability
(whp) if
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E}_{n})=1
.

2.1
Local convergence in probability

Local weak convergence was introduced independently by Aldous and Steele in
[
3
]
and Benjamini and Schramm in
[
11
]
. The purpose of Aldous and Steele in
[
3
]
was to describe the local structure of the so-called ‘stochastic mean-field model of distance’, meaning the complete graph with i.i.d. exponential edge weights. This local description allowed Aldous to prove the celebrated
\zeta(2)
limit of the random assignment problem
[
1
]
. Benjamini and Schramm in
[
11
]
instead used local weak convergence to show that limits of planar graphs are with probability one recurrent. Since its conception, local convergence has proved a key ingredient in random graph theory. In this section, we provide some basics of local convergence. For more detailed discussions, we refer the reader to
[
21
]
or
[
43
, Chapter 2]
. Let us start with some definitions.

A
rooted graph
is a pair
(G,o)
, where
G=(V(G),E(G))
is a graph with vertex set
V(G)
and edge set
E(G)
, and
o\in V(G)
is a vertex.
Further, a rooted or non-rooted graph is called
locally finite
when each of its vertices has finite degree (though not necessarily uniformly bounded). Two (finite or infinite) graphs
G_{1}=(V(G_{1}),E(G_{1}))
and
G_{2}=(V(G_{2}),E(G_{2}))
are called
isomorphic
, which we write as
G_{1}\simeq G_{2}
, when there exists a bijection
\phi\colon V(G_{1})\mapsto V(G_{2})
such that
\{u,v\}\in E(G_{1})
precisely when
\{\phi(u),\phi(v)\}\in E(G_{2}).
Similarly, two rooted (finite or infinite) graphs
(G_{1},o_{1})
and
(G_{2},o_{2})
, with
G_{i}=(V(G_{i}),E(G_{i}))
for
i\in\{1,2\}
, are called
isomorphic
, abbreviated as
(G_{1},o_{1})\simeq(G_{2},o_{2})
, when there exists a bijection
\phi\colon V(G_{1})\mapsto V(G_{2})
such that
\phi(o_{1})=o_{2}
and
\{u,v\}\in E(G_{1})
precisely when
\{\phi(u),\phi(v)\}\in E(G_{2}).
These notions can easily be adapted to multigraphs (which we will need below), for which
G=(V(G),(x_{i,j})_{i,j\in V(G)})
, where
x_{i,j}
denotes the number of edges between
i
and
j
, and
x_{i,i}
the number of self-loops at
i
. There, instead, the isomorphism
\phi\colon V(G)\mapsto V(G^{\prime})
is required to satisfy that
x_{i,j}=x^{\prime}_{\phi(i),\phi(j)}
, where
(x_{i,j})_{i,j\in V(G)}
and
(x_{i,j}^{\prime})_{i,j\in V(G^{\prime})}
are the edge multiplicities of
G
and
G^{\prime}
respectively.

We let
\mathscr{G}_{\star}
be the space of (possibly infinite) connected rooted graphs, where we consider two rooted graphs to be equal when they are isomorphic. Thus, we consider
\mathscr{G}_{\star}
as the set of equivalence classes of rooted graphs modulo isomorphisms. The space
\mathscr{G}_{\star}
of rooted graphs is a nice metric space, with an explicit metric, see
[
43
, Chapter 2 and Appendix A]
for details.

For a graph
G
and
u,v\in V(G)
, we let
d_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(u,v)
denote the graph distance between
u
and
v
, where, by convention,
d_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(u,v)=\infty
when
u
and
v
are not connected in
G
. For a rooted graph
(G,o)
, we let
B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}(o)
denote the (rooted) subgraph of
(G,o)
of all vertices at graph distance at most
r
away from
o
. Formally, this means that
B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}(o)=((V(B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}(o)),E(B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}(o)),o)
, where

\displaystyle V(B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}(o))

\displaystyle=\{u\colon d_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(o,u)\leq r\},

(2.1)

\displaystyle E(B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}(o))

\displaystyle=\{\{u,v\}\in E(G)\colon d_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(o,u),d_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(o,v)\leq r\}.

We say that the graph sequence
(G_{n})_{n\geq 1}
converges
locally in probability
to a limit
(G,o)\sim\mu
, when, for every
r\geq 0
and
H^{\star}\in\mathscr{G}_{\star}
,

\frac{1}{|V(G_{n})|}\sum_{v\in V(G_{n})}\mathbbm{1}_{\{B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(v)\simeq H^{\star}\}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu(B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}(o)\simeq H^{\star}).

(2.2)

This means that the subgraph proportions in the random graph
G_{n}
are close, in probability, to those given by
\mu
. Let
o_{n}\in V(G_{n})
be chosen uniformly at random (uar) in
V(G_{n})
. Then, (
2.2
) is equivalent to the statement that

{\mathbb{P}}(B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(o_{n})\simeq H^{\star}\mid G_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu(B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}(o)\simeq H^{\star}).

(2.3)

There are related notions of local convergence, such as
local weak convergence
, where (
2.2
) is replaced by the convergence of
expectations
, and
local almost sure convergence
, where the convergence holds almost surely. For our purposes, local convergence in probability is the most convenient, for example since it implies that the neighborhoods of
two
uniformly chosen vertices are asymptotically independent (see e.g.,
[
43
, Corollary 2.18]
), which is central in our proof.

2.2
Asymptotics of the giant

Given a random graph sequence
G_{n}
that converges locally in probability to
(G,o)\sim\mu
, one would expect that
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\zeta:=\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|=\infty)
. However, the proportion of vertices in the largest connected component
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|/n
is
not
continuous in the local convergence topology, as it is a
global
object. In fact, also
|\mathscr{C}(o_{n})|/n
does not necessarily converge in distribution when
G_{n}
converges locally in probability to
(G,o)\sim\mu
. However, local convergence still tells us a useful story about the existence of a giant, as well as its size. Indeed, Corollary
2.1
shows that the upper bound is always valid, while Theorem
2.2
shows that a relative simple condition suffices to yield the lower bound as well:

Corollary 2.1

(Upper bound on the giant)
.

Let
(G_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of graphs having size
|V(G_{n})|=n
. Assume that
G_{n}
converges locally in probability to
(G,o)\sim\mu
. Write
\zeta=\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|=\infty)
for the survival probability of the limiting graph
(G,o)
.
Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
fixed, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

{\mathbb{P}}(|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|\leq n(\zeta+\varepsilon))\rightarrow 1.

(2.4)

In particular, Corollary
2.1
implies that
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
when
\zeta=0
, so that there can only be a giant when the local limit has a positive survival probability.

Proof.

Define

Z_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}=\sum_{v\in V(G_{n})}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}(v)|\geq k\}}.

(2.5)

Assume that
G_{n}
converges locally in probability to
(G,o)\sim\mu
as defined in (
2.2
).
Since
\{|\mathscr{C}(v)|\geq k\}=\{|B_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}(v)|\geq k\}
, with
\zeta_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}=\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|\geq k)
,

\frac{Z_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\zeta_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}.

(2.6)

For every
k\geq 1
,

\{|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|\geq k\}=\{Z_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}\geq k\},

(2.7)

and, on the event that
Z_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}\geq 1
, also
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|\leq Z_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}
. Note that
\zeta=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\zeta_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}=\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|=\infty)
.
We take
k
so large that
\zeta\geq\zeta_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}-\varepsilon/2
. Then, for every
k\geq 1
,
\varepsilon>0
, and all
n
large enough such that
k\leq n(\zeta+\varepsilon)
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|\geq n(\zeta+\varepsilon))

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}(Z_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}\geq n(\zeta+\varepsilon))

(2.8)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}(Z_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}\geq n(\zeta_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}+\varepsilon/2))=o(1).

∎

We conclude that while local convergence cannot determine the size of the largest connected component, it
does
prove an upper bound on
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|
. There are many results that extend this to
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\zeta
(see Section
4
for pointers to the literature), but this is no longer a consequence of local convergence alone. Therefore, in general, more involved arguments must be used. We next prove that one, relatively simple, condition suffices. In its statement, and for
x,y\in V(G_{n})
, we write
x\longleftrightarrow{\hskip-12.05553pt/}\hskip 7.74998pty
when
x\not\in\mathscr{C}(y)
:

Theorem 2.2

(The giant is almost local)
.

Let
G_{n}=(V(G_{n}),E(G_{n}))
denote a random graph of size
|V(G_{n})|=n
. Assume that
G_{n}
converges locally in probability to
(G,o)\sim\mu
. Assume further that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\#\big\{(x,y)\in V(G_{n})\times V(G_{n})\colon|\mathscr{C}(x)|,|\mathscr{C}(y)|\geq k,x\longleftrightarrow{\hskip-12.05553pt/}\hskip 7.74998pty\big\}\Big]=0.

(2.9)

Then, with
\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
denoting the second largest connected component (with ties broken arbitrarily),

\frac{|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\zeta=\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|=\infty),\qquad\frac{|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}|}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

(2.10)

Theorem
2.2
shows that a relatively mild condition as in (
2.9
) suffices for the giant to have the expected limit. In fact, we will see that it is necessary
and sufficient
(see Remark
2.3
below).

In the proof below, for
k\geq 1
, it will be convenient to write
X_{n,k}=o_{k,{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}(1)
when

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(|X_{n,k}|>\varepsilon)=0.

(2.11)

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.
By Corollary
2.1
, it suffices to prove Theorem
2.2
for
\zeta>0
, which we assume from now on.

Let the vector
(|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|)_{i\geq 1}
denote the component sizes ordered in size, from large to small with ties broken arbitrarily, so that
\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}=\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}
. Since
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|\geq|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|
for all
i\geq 1
, we can bound

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|}{n}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\geq 1}|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\geq k\}}}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\geq 1}|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\geq k\}}}.

(2.12)

We investigate both numerator and denominator of this expression. For the denominator, by (
2.6
),

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\geq 1}|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\geq k\}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in V(G_{n})}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}(v)|\geq k\}}=\frac{1}{n}Z_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\zeta_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k},

(2.13)

where we recall that
\zeta_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}=\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|\geq k)
. Thus, since
\zeta_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}\rightarrow\zeta
as
k\rightarrow\infty
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\geq 1}|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\geq k\}}=\zeta+o_{k,{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}(1).

(2.14)

For the numerator, we let

X_{n,k}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\geq 1}|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\geq k\}}-\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\geq 1}|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\geq k\}}\Big)^{2},

(2.15)

so that the numerator in (
2.12
) equals

\big(Z_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}/n\big)^{2}+X_{n,k}=\zeta^{2}+X_{n,k}+o_{k,{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}(1).

(2.16)

It remains to investigate
X_{n,k}
, which we rewrite as

\displaystyle X_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\large i,j\geq 1}}}{{i\neq j}}}|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}||\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(j)}|\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|,|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(j)}|\geq k\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\#\big\{(x,y)\in V(G_{n})\times V(G_{n})\colon|\mathscr{C}(x)|,|\mathscr{C}(y)|\geq k,x\longleftrightarrow{\hskip-12.05553pt/}\hskip 7.74998pty\big\}.

(2.17)

By the Markov inequality,

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(X_{n,k}\geq\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\varepsilon n^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}[X_{n,k}]=0,

by our main assumption in (
2.9
). As a result,
X_{n,k}=o_{k,{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}(1)
.
We conclude that, by (
2.12
),

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|}{n}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\zeta^{2}+o_{k,{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}(1)}{\zeta+o_{k,{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}(1)}=\zeta+o_{k,{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}(1).

(2.18)

Since
k
is arbitrary, this proves that
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|\geq n\zeta(1+o_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}(1))
. Further, by Corollary
2.1
, also
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|\leq n\zeta(1+o_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}(1))
. Therefore,
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\zeta.

In turn, since
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\zeta
and by (
2.13
),

\frac{1}{n}|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}|\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}|\geq k\}}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\geq 2}|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\geq k\}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\geq 1}|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\geq k\}}-\frac{|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|}{n}=o_{k,{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}(1),

(2.19)

which, again since
k
is arbitrary, implies that
|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
.
∎

Remark 2.3

(Proof of necessity of (
2.9
))
.

Condition (
2.9
) is also
necessary
for
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\zeta
to hold. Indeed, (
2.2
) implies that when (
2.9
) fails, there exists a
\kappa>0
such that

\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}[X_{n,k}]=\kappa>0.

(2.20)

Then, there exists a subsequence
(n_{l})_{l\geq 1}
for which
\lim_{l\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}[X_{n_{l},k}]=\kappa
, and, by (
2.13
) and (
2.15
),

\displaystyle\lim_{l\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n_{l}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}[|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|\geq k\}}]

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{l\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n_{l}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\sum_{i}|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\geq k\}}\Big]

(2.21)

\displaystyle=\lim_{l\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n_{l}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}^{2}-X_{n_{l},k}]=\zeta_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}^{2}-\kappa.

Obviously, the same holds for
{\mathbb{E}}[|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|^{2}]/n_{l}^{2}
. Letting
k\rightarrow\infty
and using that
\zeta_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}\rightarrow\zeta
, we obtain

\lim_{l\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n_{l}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}[|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|^{2}]\leq\zeta^{2}-\kappa.

(2.22)

By (
2.22
), we conclude that
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\zeta
cannot hold, as by bounded convergence, this would imply that also
{\mathbb{E}}[|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|^{2}]/n^{2}\rightarrow\zeta^{2}
.

2.3
Local properties of the giant

We next extend Theorem
2.2
by investigating the structure of the giant. For this, we first let
v_{k}(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max})
denote the number of vertices with degree
k
in the giant component, and
|E(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max})|
the number of edges in the giant component. Further, for a graph
G
and
v\in V(G)
, we write
d^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}_{v}
for the degree of
v
:

Theorem 2.4

(Properties of the giant)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.2
, when
\zeta=\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|=\infty)>0
,

\frac{v_{\ell}(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max})}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|=\infty,d_{o}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}=\ell).

(2.23)

Further, assume that
D_{n}=d_{o_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}
is uniformly integrable. Then,

\frac{|E(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max})|}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\Big[d_{o}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}(o)|=\infty\}}\Big].

(2.24)

Proof.

We now define, for
k\geq 1
and
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
and with
d_{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}
the degree of
v
in
G_{n}
,

Z_{\scriptscriptstyle A,\geq k}=\sum_{v\in[n]}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}(v)|\geq k,d_{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}\in A\}}.

(2.25)

Assume that
G_{n}
converges locally in probability to
(G,o)\sim\mu
. Then, as in (
2.13
),

\frac{Z_{\scriptscriptstyle A,\geq k}}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|\geq k,d_{o}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}\in A).

(2.26)

Since
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|\geq k
whp by Theorem
2.2
, we thus obtain, for every
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
, on the high-probability event
\{|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|\geq k\},

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{a\in A}v_{a}(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max})\leq\frac{Z_{\scriptscriptstyle A,\geq k}}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|\geq k,d_{o}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}\in A).

(2.27)

Applying this to
A=\{\ell\}^{c}
, we obtain that, for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\Big[|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|-v_{\ell}(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max})\Big]\leq\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|\geq k,d_{o}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}\neq\ell)+\varepsilon/2\Big)=1.

(2.28)

We argue by contradiction. Suppose that, for some
\ell
,

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\frac{v_{\ell}(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max})}{n}\leq\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|=\infty,d_{o}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}=\ell)-\varepsilon\Big)=\kappa>0.

(2.29)

Then, along the subsequence
(n_{l})_{l\geq 1}
that attains the limsup in (
2.29
), with asymptotic probability
\kappa>0
, and using (
2.28
) and (
2.29
),

\frac{|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|}{n}=\frac{1}{n}[|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|-v_{\ell}(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max})]+\frac{v_{\ell}(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max})}{n}\leq\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|=\infty)-\varepsilon/2,

(2.30)

which contradicts Theorem
2.2
. We conclude that (
2.29
) cannot hold, so that (
2.23
) follows.

For (
2.24
), we note that

|E(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max})|=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\ell v_{\ell}(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}).

(2.31)

We divide by
n
and split the sum over
\ell
in
\ell\in[K]=\{1,\ldots,K\}
and
\ell>K
as

\frac{|E(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max})|}{n}=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{\ell\in[K]}\ell v_{\ell}(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max})+\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{\ell>K}\ell v_{\ell}(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}).

(2.32)

For the first term in (
2.32
), by (
2.23
),

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2n}\sum_{\ell\in[K]}\ell v_{\ell}(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell\in[K]}\ell\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|=\infty,d_{o}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}=\ell)

(2.33)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\Big[d_{o}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}(o)|=\infty,d_{o}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}\in[K]\}}\Big].

For the second term in (
2.32
), we bound, with
n_{\ell}
the number of vertices in
G_{n}
of degree
\ell
,

\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{\ell>K}\ell v_{\ell}(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max})\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell>K}\ell\frac{n_{\ell}}{n}=\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\big[d_{o_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}\mathbbm{1}_{\{d_{o_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}>K\}}\mid G_{n}\big].

(2.34)

By uniform integrability of
(d_{o_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})})_{n\geq 1}
,

\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\big[d_{o_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}\mathbbm{1}_{\{d_{o_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}>K\}}\big]=0.

(2.35)

As a result, by the Markov inequality and for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
K=K(\varepsilon)<\infty
such that

{\mathbb{P}}\Big({\mathbb{E}}\big[d_{o_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}\mathbbm{1}_{\{d_{o_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}>K\}}\mid G_{n}\big]>\varepsilon\Big)\rightarrow 0.

(2.36)

This completes the proof of (
2.24
).
∎

It is not hard to extend the above analysis to the local convergence in probability of the giant, as well as its complement, as formulated in the following theorem:

Theorem 2.5

(Local limit of the giant)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.2
, when
\zeta=\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|=\infty)>0
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(v)\simeq H_{\star}\}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|=\infty,B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}(o)\simeq H_{\star}),

(2.37)

and

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\not\in\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(v)\simeq H_{\star}\}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|<\infty,B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}(o)\simeq H_{\star}).

(2.38)

Proof.

The convergence in (
2.38
) follows from that in (
2.37
) combined with the fact that, by assumption,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in V(G_{n})}\mathbbm{1}_{\{B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(v)\simeq H_{\star}\}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu(B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}(o)\simeq H_{\star}).

(2.39)

The convergence in (
2.37
) can be proved as for Theorem
2.4
, now using that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}Z_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathscr{H}_{\star},\geq k}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in V(G_{n})}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}(v)|\geq k,B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(v)\in\mathscr{H}_{\star}\}}

(2.40)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|\geq k,B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}(o)\in\mathscr{H}_{\star}),

and, since
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\zeta>0
by Theorem
2.2
, on the high-probability event
\{|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|\geq k\},

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(v)\in\mathscr{H}_{\star}\}}\leq\frac{1}{n}Z_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathscr{H}_{\star},\geq k}.

(2.41)

We leave the details to the reader.
∎

2.4
Examples through bounds on second largest component

In this section, we give examples of how we can apply our results to obtain a law of large numbers for the giant, given that a bound on the second largest component has been proved. These examples will follow from the following corollary:

Corollary 2.6

(Law of large numbers giant given bound second largest component)
.

Let
(G_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of graphs having size
|V(G_{n})|=n
. Assume that
G_{n}
converges locally in probability to
(G,o)\sim\mu
. Write
\zeta=\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|=\infty)
for the survival probability of the limiting graph
(G,o)
. Assume further that

|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

(2.42)

Then, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\zeta.

(2.43)

Further, Theorem
2.5
and (
2.23
) in Theorem
2.4
also hold, while (
2.24
) in Theorem
2.4
holds when
D_{n}=d_{o_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}
is uniformly integrable.

Proof.

Recall
X_{n,k}
from (
2.15
), and recall also (
2.2
), so that

\displaystyle X_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\large i,j\geq 1}}}{{i\neq j}}}|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}||\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(j)}|\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|,|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(j)}|\geq k\}}\leq\frac{2|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|}{n}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\geq 1}|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(j)}|\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(j)}|\geq k\}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

(2.44)

Therefore, by bounded convergence, also
{\mathbb{E}}[X_{n,k}]=o(1)
, so that (
2.9
) holds. The conclusion on
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|
follows from Theorem
2.2
, while the other conclusions follow from the proofs of Theorems
2.4
and
2.5
.
∎

Remark 2.7

(Examples)
.

There are many examples for which linear size lower bounds on the giant have been proved, jointly with a sublinear upper bound on the second component. The latter then shows that the giant is
unique
, and implies that
\zeta>0
. Further assuming local convergence in probability, Corollary
2.6
immediately implies the
law of large numbers
of the giant, which is obviously very interesting. For example, for the geometric inhomogeneous random graph studied in
[
26
]
, under appropriate conditions, the local limit was identified in
[
46
]
, and the upper bound on the second largest component proved in
[
26
]
. For the hyperbolic random graph, again the local limit was identified in
[
46
]
, the lower bound on the giant proved in
[
15
]
, and the bound on the second largest component in
[
60
]
. Such results highlight the power of the ‘giant is almost local’ method.

2.5
The ‘giant is almost local’ condition revisited

The ‘giant is almost local’ condition (
2.9
) is sometimes not so convenient to verify directly, and we now give an alternative form that is often easier to work with:

Lemma 2.8

(Condition (
2.9
) revisited)
.

Consider
(G_{n})_{n\geq 1}
under the conditions of Theorem
2.2
. Assume further that there exists
r=r_{k}\rightarrow\infty
such that

\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|\geq k,|\partial B^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}_{r}(o)|<r_{k})\rightarrow 0,\qquad\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|<k,|\partial B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}(o)|\geq r_{k})\rightarrow 0.

(2.45)

Then, the ‘giant is almost local’ condition in (
2.9
) holds when

\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\#\big\{(x,y)\in V(G_{n})\times V(G_{n})\colon|\partial B^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}_{r}(x)|,|\partial B^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}_{r}(y)|\geq r,x\longleftrightarrow{\hskip-12.05553pt/}\hskip 7.74998pty\big\}\Big]=0.

(2.46)

Proof.

Denote

\displaystyle P_{k}

\displaystyle=\#\big\{(x,y)\in V(G_{n})\times V(G_{n})\colon|\mathscr{C}(x)|,|\mathscr{C}(y)|\geq k,x\longleftrightarrow{\hskip-12.05553pt/}\hskip 7.74998pty\big\},

(2.47)

\displaystyle P_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}

\displaystyle=\#\big\{(x,y)\in V(G_{n})\times V(G_{n})\colon|\partial B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(x)|,|\partial B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(y)|\geq r,x\longleftrightarrow{\hskip-12.05553pt/}\hskip 7.74998pty\big\}.

(2.48)

Then,

|P_{k}-P_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}|\leq 2n\big[Z_{\scriptscriptstyle<r,\geq k}+Z_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq r,<k}\big],

(2.49)

where

\displaystyle Z_{\scriptscriptstyle<r,\geq k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V(G_{n})}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\partial B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(v)|<r,|\mathscr{C}(v)|\geq k\}},\qquad Z_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq r,<k}=\sum_{v\in V(G_{n})}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\partial B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(v)|\geq r,|\mathscr{C}(v)|<k\}}.

(2.50)

Therefore, by local convergence in probability,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}|P_{k}-P_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}\big[Z_{\scriptscriptstyle<r,\geq k}+Z_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq r,<k}\big]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}2\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|\geq k,|\partial B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}(o)|<r)+2\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|<k,|\partial B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}(o)|\geq r).

(2.51)

Take
r=r_{k}
as in (
2.45
), so that the rhs of (
2.5
) vanishes, and, by Dominated Convergence, also

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[|P_{k}-P_{r_{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}|\big]=0.

(2.52)

We arrive at

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[P_{k}\big]\leq\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[P_{r_{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\big]=0,

(2.53)

by (
2.46
) and since
r_{k}\rightarrow\infty
when
k\rightarrow\infty
.
∎

Remark 2.9

(Alternative ‘giant is almost local’ condition)
.

The assumption in (
2.46
) is sometimes more convenient than (
2.9
), as it requires that most pairs of vertices with many vertices at distance
r
are connected to one another. In many random graphs, there is some weak dependence between
B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(x)
and the graph outside of it. This is more complicated when dealing with
|\mathscr{C}(x)|\geq k
.

The assumption in (
2.45
) on the local limit is often easily verified. For example, for the unimodular branching processes with bounded offspring, to which we will apply it below, we can take
r_{k}=k
and use that, on the event of survival,

\nu^{-r}|\partial B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}(o)|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}M,

where
(G,o)
denotes the unimodular branching process with bounded offspring,
M>0
on the event of survival by
[
42
, Theorem 3.9]
, and
\nu
the expected offspring of this branching process. Therefore,
\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|\geq k,|\partial B_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}(o)|<k)\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
. Further,
\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|<k,|\partial B_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G)}(o)|\geq k)=0
trivially. However, there are examples where (
2.45
) fails, so that (
2.9
) and (
2.46
) may not be equivalent then.

3
Application to the configuration model

In this section, we apply our results to the configuration model as introduced by Bollobás
[
16
]
in the context of random regular graphs. The giant in the configuration model has a long history. It was first investigated by Molloy and Reed
[
63
,
64
]
in a setting where the degrees are general. The problem was revisited by Janson and Luczak
[
54
]
and Bollobás and Riordan
[
19
]
, amongst others. This section is organised as follows. We start by introducing the model and stating our results in Section
3.1
. We then prove the main result in Section
3.2
, and state a consequence on typical graph distances, that is proved along the way, in Section
3.3
. Some proofs of technical ingredients are deferred to Appendix
A
.

3.1
Model definition and results

The configuration model has the nice property that, when conditioned on being simple, it yields a uniform random graph with the prescribed degree distribution. We refer to
[
42
, Chapter 7]
for an extensive introduction.

Model definition and assumptions.

Fix an integer
n
that will denote the number of vertices in the random graph. Consider a sequence of degrees
\boldsymbol{d}=(d_{i})_{i\in[n]}
. Without loss of generality, we assume throughout this paper that
d_{j}\geq 1
for all
j\in[n]
, since when
d_{j}=0
, vertex
j
is isolated and can be removed from the graph. We assume that the total degree

\ell_{n}=\sum_{j\in[n]}d_{j}

is even.

To construct the multigraph where vertex
j
has degree
d_{j}
for all
j\in[n]
, we have
n
separate vertices and incident to vertex
j
, we have
d_{j}
half-edges. We number the half-edges in an arbitrary order from
1
to
\ell_{n}
, and start by randomly connecting the first half-edge with one of the
\ell_{n}-1
remaining half-edges. Once paired, two half-edges form a single edge of the multigraph, and the half-edges are removed from the list of half-edges that need to be paired. We continue the procedure of randomly choosing and pairing the half-edges until all half-edges are connected, and call the resulting graph the
configuration model with degree sequence
\boldsymbol{d}
, abbreviated as
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
. A careful reader may worry about the order in which the half-edges are being paired. In fact, this ordering turns out to be irrelevant since the random pairing of half-edges is completely
exchangeable
. It can even be done in a
random
fashion, which will be useful when investigating neighborhoods in the configuration model. See e.g.,
[
42
, Definition 7.5 and Lemma 7.6]
for more details.

We denote the degree of a uniformly chosen vertex
o
in
[n]
by
D_{n}=d_{o}
. The random variable
D_{n}
has distribution function
F_{n}
given by

F_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\in[n]}\mathbbm{1}_{\{d_{j}\leq x\}},

(3.1)

which is the
empirical distribution of the degrees.
Equivalently,
{\mathbb{P}}(D_{n}=k)=n_{k}/n
, where
n_{k}
denotes the number of vertices of degree
k
.
We assume that the vertex degrees satisfy the following
regularity conditions:

Condition 3.1

(Regularity conditions for vertex degrees)
.

(a) Weak convergence of vertex weight.

There exists a distribution function
F
such that, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

D_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}D,

(3.2)

where
D_{n}
and
D
have distribution functions
F_{n}
and
F
, respectively.
Further, we assume that
F(0)=0
, i.e.,
{\mathbb{P}}(D\geq 1)=1
.

(b) Convergence of average vertex degrees.
As
n\rightarrow\infty
,

{\mathbb{E}}[D_{n}]\rightarrow{\mathbb{E}}[D]<\infty,

(3.3)

where
D_{n}
and
D
have distribution functions
F_{n}
and
F
from part (a), respectively.

Note that Conditions
3.1
(a)-(b) are equivalent to Condition
3.1
(a) and uniform integrability of
(D_{n})_{n\geq 1}
. While Conditions
3.1
(a)-(b) may appear to be quite strong, in fact, they are quite close to assuming uniform integrability of
(D_{n})_{n\geq 1}
. Indeed, when
(D_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is uniformly integrable, then there is a subsequence along which
D_{n}
converges in distribution as in Condition
3.1
(a), and along this subsequence also Condition
3.1
(b) holds. We can then apply our results along this subsequence.

The giant in the configuration model.

We now come to our result on the connected components in the configuration model
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
, where the degrees
\boldsymbol{d}=(d_{i})_{i\in[n]}
satisfy Conditions
3.1
(a)-(b).
For a graph
G
, we write
v_{k}(G)
for the number of vertices of degree
k
in
G
, and
|E(G)|
for the number of edges. The main result concerning the size and structure of the largest connected components of
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
is the following:

Theorem 3.2

(Phase transition in
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
)
.

Suppose that Conditions
3.1
(a)-(b) hold and consider the random graph
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
, letting
n\rightarrow\infty
. Assume that
p_{2}={\mathbb{P}}(D=2)<1
.

(a)

If
\nu={\mathbb{E}}[D(D-1)]/{\mathbb{E}}[D]>1
, then there exist

\xi\in[0,1),\zeta\in(0,1]
such that

\displaystyle|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|/n

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle\zeta\text{,}

\displaystyle v_{k}(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max})/n

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle p_{k}(1-\xi^{k})\quad\text{ for every }k\geq 0\text{,}

\displaystyle|E(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max})|/n

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}[D](1-\xi^{2}).

while
|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
and
|E(\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
.

(b)

If
\nu={\mathbb{E}}[D(D-1)]/{\mathbb{E}}[D]\leq 1
, then
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
and
|E(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max})|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
.

We prove Theorem
3.2
in Section
3.2
below. We now remark upon the result and on the conditions arising in it.

Local structure of
\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}
.

Theorem
3.2
is the main result for
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
proved in
[
54
]
. The result in
[
19
]
only concerns
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|/n
. Related results are in
[
63
,
64
,
67
]
. Many of these proofs use an
exploration
of the connected components in the graph, in
[
63
,
64
]
in discrete time and in
[
54
]
in continuous time. Further,
[
19
]
relies on concentration inequalities combined with a sprinkling argument. For
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
,
[
19
, Theorem 25]
identifies the local structure of the giant that follows from Theorem
2.5
.
[
19
, Theorem 25]
also applies to uniform simple graphs with given degrees.

Local convergence of configuration models.

The fact that the configuration model converges locally in probability is well-established, and is the starting point for the proof of Theorem
3.2
using Theorems
2.2
and
2.4
. We refer to Appendix
A.1
for more details on local convergence of
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
. Dembo and Montanari
[
28
]
crucially rely on it in order to identify the limiting pressure for the Ising model on the configuration model. Many alternative proofs exist. We use some of the ingredients in
[
43
, Proof of Theorem 4.1]
, since these are helpful in the proof of Theorem
3.2
as well. We also refer to the lecture notes by Bordenave
[
21
]
for a nice exposition of local convergence proofs for the configuration model. Further, Bordenave and Caputo
[
22
]
prove that the neighborhoods in the configuration model satisfy a large deviation principle at speed
n
in the context where the degrees of the configuration model are bounded.

Let us now describe the local limit of
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
subject to Conditions
3.1
(a)-(b). The root has offspring distribution
(p_{k})_{k\geq 1}
where
p_{k}={\mathbb{P}}(D=k)
and
D
is from Condition
3.1
(a), while all other individuals in the tree have offspring distribution
(p_{k}^{\star})_{k\geq 0}
given by

p_{k}^{\star}=\frac{k+1}{{\mathbb{E}}[D]}{\mathbb{P}}(D=k+1).

(3.4)

The distribution
(p_{k}^{\star})_{k\geq 0}
has the interpretation of the
forward degree
of a uniform edge. The above branching process is a so-called
unimodular
branching process (recall
[
2
]
) with root offspring distribution
(p_{k})_{k\geq 1}
.

Reformulation in terms of branching processes.

We next interpret the results in Theorem
3.2
in terms of our unimodular branching processes. In terms of this,
\xi
is the extinction probability of a branching process with offspring distribution
(p_{k}^{\star})_{k\geq 0}
, and
\zeta
as the survival probability of the unimodular branching process with root offspring distribution
(p_{k})_{k\geq 1}
. Thus,
\zeta
satisfies

\zeta=\sum_{k\geq 1}p_{k}(1-\xi^{k}),

(3.5)

with
\xi
the smallest solution to

\xi=\sum_{k\geq 0}p_{k}^{\star}\xi^{k}.

(3.6)

Clearly,
\xi=1
precisely when
\nu\leq 1
, where

\nu=\sum_{k\geq 0}kp_{k}^{\star}=\frac{1}{{\mathbb{E}}[D]}\sum_{k\geq 0}k(k+1)p_{k+1}={\mathbb{E}}[D(D-1)]/{\mathbb{E}}[D],

(3.7)

by (
3.4
). This explains the condition on
\nu
in Theorem
3.2
(a). Further, to understand the asymptotics of
v_{k}(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max})
, we note that there are
n_{k}\approx np_{k}
vertices with degree
k
. Each of the
k
direct neighbors of a vertex of degree
k
survives with probability close to
1-\xi
, so that the probability that at least one of them survives is close to
1-\xi^{k}
. When one of the neighbors of the vertex of degree
k
survives, the vertex itself is part of the giant component, which explains why
v_{k}(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max})/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}p_{k}(1-\xi^{k})
. Finally, an edge consists of two half-edges, and an edge is part of the giant component precisely when one of the vertices incident to it is, which occurs with asymptotic probability
1-\xi^{2}
. There are in total
\ell_{n}/2=n{\mathbb{E}}[D_{n}]/2\approx n{\mathbb{E}}[D]/2
edges, which explains why
|E(\mathscr{C}_{\rm max})|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}[D](1-\xi^{2})
. Therefore, all results in Theorem
3.2
have a simple explanation in terms of the branching process approximation of the connected component for
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
of a uniform vertex
in
[n]
.

The condition
{\mathbb{P}}(D=2)=p_{2}<1
.

The case
p_{2}=1
, for which
\nu=1
, is quite exceptional, and quite different limits for
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}|/n
can occur, see
[
43
, Section 4.2]
and
[
35
]
for more details.

3.2
The ‘giant component is almost local’ proof

In this section, we prove Theorem
3.2
using the ‘giant is almost local’ results in Theorems
2.2
and
2.4
. We start by setting the stage.

Setting the stage for the proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Theorem
3.2
(b) follows directly from Corollary
2.1
combined with the local convergence in probability discussed below Theorem
3.2
and the fact that, for
\nu\leq 1
, the unimodular branching process with root offspring distribution
(p_{k})_{k\geq 0}
given by
p_{k}={\mathbb{P}}(D=k)
dies out.

Theorem
3.2
(a) follows from Theorem
2.4
, together with the facts that, for the unimodular branching process with root offspring distribution
(p_{k})_{k\geq 0}
given by
p_{k}={\mathbb{P}}(D=k)
,

\mu(|\mathscr{C}(o)|=\infty,d_{o}=\ell)=p_{k}(1-\xi^{k}),

(3.8)

and

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\Big[d_{o}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathscr{C}(o)|=\infty\}}\Big]={\mathbb{E}}[D](1-\xi^{2}).

(3.9)

Thus, it suffices to check the assumptions to Theorem
2.4
. The uniform integrability of
D_{n}
follows from Conditions
3.1
(a)-(b).
For the conditions in Theorem
2.2
, the local convergence in probability is discussed below Theorem
3.2
, so we are left to proving the crucial hypothesis in (
2.9
), which the remainder of the proof will do.

Coupling to
n
-dependent branching process.

We next relate the neighborhood in a random graph to a certain

(3.19)

Branching process neighborhood growth.

The previous step relates the graph exploration process to two independent
n
-dependent unimodular branching process
({\sf BP}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)},{\sf BP}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})_{k\geq 1}
. In this step, we investigate the growth of these branching processes. Fix
r
, and denote
b_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}=|{\sf BP}_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|
, which we assume to be at least
r
(and which is true on the event
\mathcal{C}_{n}\cap\{|\partial B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(o_{1})|,|\partial B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(o_{2})|\geq r\}
).

Let
\nu_{n}=\frac{1}{\ell_{n}}\sum_{v\in[n]}d_{v}(d_{v}-1)
denote the expected forward degree of a uniform half-edge in
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
, which also equals the expected offspring of the branching processes
({\sf BP}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})_{k\geq 0}
. Define

\mathcal{D}_{n}=\big\{\underline{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\leq|{\sf BP}_{r+k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\leq\bar{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}~\forall i\in[2],~k\geq 0\big\},

(3.20)

where
(\underline{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})_{k\geq 0}
and
(\bar{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})_{k\geq 0}
satisfy the recursions
\underline{b}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}=\bar{b}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}=b_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
, while, for some
\alpha\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)
,

\underline{b}_{k+1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}=\nu_{n}\underline{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}-(\bar{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})^{\alpha},\qquad\bar{b}_{k+1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}=\nu_{n}\bar{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}+(\bar{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})^{\alpha}.

(3.21)

The following lemma investigates the asymptotics of
(\underline{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})_{k\geq 1}
and
(\bar{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})_{k\geq 1}
:

Lemma 3.6

(Asymptotics of
\underline{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
and
\bar{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
)
.

Assume that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{n}=\nu>1
, and assume that
b_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}={\sf BP}_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\geq r
. Then, there exists an
A=A_{r}>1
with
A_{r}<\infty
for
r
large enough, such that, for all
k\geq 0
,

\bar{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\leq Ab_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\nu_{n}^{k},\qquad\underline{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\geq b_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\nu_{n}^{k}/A.

(3.22)

Proof.

First, obviously,
\bar{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\geq b_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\nu_{n}^{k}
. Thus, since
\alpha<1
and also using that
\nu_{n}>1
,

\displaystyle\bar{b}_{k+1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}

\displaystyle=\nu_{n}\bar{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}+(\bar{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})^{\alpha}=\nu_{n}\bar{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}(1+(\bar{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})^{\alpha-1})\leq\nu_{n}\bar{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\big(1+r^{-(1-\alpha)}\nu_{n}^{-(1-\alpha)k}\big),

(3.23)

By iteration, this implies the upper bound with
A
replaced by
\bar{A}_{r}
given by

\bar{A}_{r}=\prod_{k\geq 0}\big(1+r^{-(1-\alpha)}\nu_{n}^{-(1-\alpha)k}\big)<\infty.

(3.24)

Further, note that
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\bar{A}_{r}=1
. For the lower bound, we use that
\bar{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\leq\bar{A}_{r}b_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\nu_{n}^{k}
to obtain

\underline{b}_{k+1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\geq\nu_{n}\underline{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}-\bar{A}_{r}^{\alpha}(b_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})^{\alpha}\nu_{n}^{\alpha k}.

(3.25)

We now use induction to show that

\underline{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\geq a_{k}b_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\nu_{n}^{k},

(3.26)

where
a_{0}=1
and

a_{k+1}=a_{k}-\bar{A}_{r}^{\alpha}r^{1-\alpha}\nu_{n}^{(\alpha-1)k-1}.

(3.27)

The initialization follows, since
\underline{b}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}=b_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
and
a_{0}=1
. To advance the induction hypothesis, we substitute the induction hypothesis to obtain that

\displaystyle\underline{b}_{k+1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}

\displaystyle\geq a_{k}b_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\nu_{n}^{k+1}-\bar{A}_{r}^{\alpha}(b_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})^{\alpha}\nu_{n}^{\alpha k}

(3.28)

\displaystyle=b_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\nu_{n}^{k+1}\big(a_{k}-\bar{A}_{r}^{\alpha}(b_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})^{\alpha-1}\nu_{n}^{(\alpha-1)k-1}\big)

\displaystyle\geq b_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\nu_{n}^{k+1}\big(a_{k}-\bar{A}_{r}^{\alpha}r^{\alpha-1}\nu_{n}^{(\alpha-1)k-1}\big)=a_{k+1}b_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\nu_{n}^{k+1},

by (
3.27
). Finally,
a_{k}
is decreasing, and thus
a_{k}\searrow a\equiv 1/\underline{A}_{r},
where

\underline{A}_{r}^{-1}=1-\sum_{k\geq 0}\bar{A}_{r}^{\alpha}r^{-(1-\alpha)}\nu_{n}^{-(1-\alpha)k}<\infty

for
r
large enough, so that the claim follows with
A=A_{r}=\max\{\bar{A}_{r},\underline{A}_{r}\}
. Note further that
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\underline{A}_{r}=1
, since
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\bar{A}_{r}=1
.
∎

The following lemma shows that
\mathcal{D}_{n}=\big\{\underline{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\leq|{\sf BP}_{r+k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\leq\bar{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}~\forall i\in[2],~k\geq 0\big\}
occurs whp when first
n\rightarrow\infty
followed by
r\rightarrow\infty
:

Lemma 3.7

(
\mathcal{D}_{n}
occurs whp)
.

Assume that
b_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}=|{\sf BP}_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\geq r
. Then

\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})=1.

(3.29)

Proof.

We will show that
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{D}_{n}^{c})=0
. The inequalities
\underline{b}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\leq|{\sf BP}_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\leq\bar{b}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
hold by definition. We thus write

{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{D}_{n}^{c})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{D}_{n,k}^{c}\cap\mathcal{D}_{n,k-1}),

(3.30)

where

\mathcal{D}_{n,k}=\big\{\underline{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\leq|{\sf BP}_{r+k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\leq\bar{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}~\forall i\in[2]\big\}.

(3.31)

Note that, when
|{\sf BP}_{r+k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|>\bar{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
and
|{\sf BP}_{r+k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\leq\bar{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
,

|{\sf BP}_{r+k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|-\nu_{n}|{\sf BP}_{r+k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|>\bar{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}-\nu_{n}\bar{b}_{k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}=(\bar{b}_{k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})^{\alpha},

(3.32)

while, when
|{\sf BP}_{r+k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|<\underline{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
and
|{\sf BP}_{r+k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\geq\underline{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
,

|{\sf BP}_{r+k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|-\nu_{n}|{\sf BP}_{r+k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|<\underline{b}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}-\nu_{n}\underline{b}_{k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}=(\bar{b}_{k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})^{\alpha},

(3.33)

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{n,k}^{c}\cap\mathcal{D}_{n,k-1}

(3.34)

\displaystyle\quad\subseteq\big\{\big||{\sf BP}_{r+k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}|-\nu_{n}|{\sf BP}_{r+k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}|\big|\geq(\bar{b}_{k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)})^{\alpha}\big\}\cup\big\{\big||{\sf BP}_{r+k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}|-\nu_{n}|{\sf BP}_{r+k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}|\big|\geq(\bar{b}_{k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})^{\alpha}\big\}.

By the Chebychev inequality, conditionally on
\mathcal{D}_{n,k-1}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\big(\big||{\sf BP}_{r+k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|-\nu_{n}|{\sf BP}_{r+k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\big|\geq(\bar{b}_{k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})^{\alpha}\mid\mathcal{D}_{n,k-1}\big)

(3.35)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{\mathrm{Var}(|{\sf BP}_{r+k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|\mid|{\sf BP}_{r+k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|)}{(\bar{b}_{k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})^{2\alpha}}\leq\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}|{\sf BP}_{r+k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|}{(\bar{b}_{k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})^{2\alpha}}\leq\sigma_{n}^{2}(\bar{b}_{k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})^{1-2\alpha},

where
\sigma_{n}^{2}
is the variance of the offspring distribution given by

\sigma_{n}^{2}=\frac{1}{\ell_{n}}\sum_{v\in[n]}d_{v}(d_{v}-1)^{2}-\nu_{n}^{2},

(3.36)

which is uniformly bounded. Thus, by the union bound for
i\in\{1,2\}
,

{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{D}_{n,k}^{c}\cap\mathcal{D}_{n,k-1})\leq 2\sigma_{n}^{2}(\bar{b}_{k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})^{1-2\alpha},

(3.37)

and we conclude that

{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{D}_{n}^{c})\leq 2\sigma_{n}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\big((\bar{b}_{k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)})^{1-2\alpha}+(\bar{b}_{k-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})^{1-2\alpha}\big).

(3.38)

The claim now follows from Lemma
3.6
and the fact that
\sigma_{n}^{2}\leq b(b-1)^{2}
remains uniformly bounded.
∎

\partial B_{\underline{k}_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(o_{1})\cap\partial B_{\bar{k}_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(o_{2})=\varnothing
. By Lemma
3.6
, on
\mathcal{G}_{n}
and when

and

and
Z_{\overline{k}_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
incident to

and

Z_{\overline{k}_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=\Theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}(\overline{m}_{n})

\partial B_{\underline{k}_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(o_{1})

and

is not

(3.42)

since at least

{\mathbb{P}}(|\partial B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(o_{1})|,|\partial B_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(o_{2})|\geq r,o_{1}\longleftrightarrow{\hskip-12.05553pt/}\hskip 7.74998pto_{2};\mathcal{G}_{n})=o(1),

(3.43)

as required. This completes the proof of (

) for

with bounded degrees, and even shows that

whp on the event that

.

Extension of the proof to unbounded degrees.

To extend the proof to configuration models whose degrees satisfy Conditions

(a)-(b), we apply a
degree-truncation technique
that allows us to go from such a configuration model to one whose degrees are uniformly bounded. This result makes the proof of the ‘giant is almost local’ condition in (
2.46
) simpler, and is interesting in its own right:

Theorem 3.8

(Degree truncation for configuration models)
.

Consider
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
with general degrees. Fix
b\geq 1
. There exists a related configuration model
{\rm CM}_{n^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime})
that is coupled to
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
and satisfies that

(a)

the degrees in
{\rm CM}_{n^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime})
are truncated versions of those in
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
, i.e.,
d_{v}^{\prime}=(d_{v}\wedge b)
for
v\in[n]
, and
d_{v}^{\prime}=1
for
v\in[n^{\prime}]\setminus[n]
;

(b)

the total degree in
{\rm CM}_{n^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime})
is the same as that in
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
, i.e.,

4
Discussion and open problems

In this section, we discuss our proof and results, and state some open problems.

Minimal conditions for the proof.

Inspection of the proof of Corollary
2.1
and Theorem
2.2
shows that
Z_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\zeta_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}
, with
\zeta_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}\searrow\zeta
, combined with (
2.9
), suffices to obtain Theorem
2.2
. Theorem
2.4
requires a little more (see (
2.26
)), while only Theorem
2.5
requires the full local convergence in probability. The use of
Z_{\scriptscriptstyle\geq k}
to study connected components (particularly close to criticality) has a long history, and is implicitly present in
[
25
]
, while being formally introduced in the high-dimensional percolation context in
[
23
]
, see
[
24
,
49
,
50
]
for applications to percolation on the
n
-cube.
[
41
]
used it to study the critical behavior for rank-1 inhomogeneous random graphs, and
[
42
]
for the phase transition on the Erdős-Rényi random graph.

Related models.

We use the configuration model as a proof of concept for the method in this paper, and gave several further examples using Corollary
2.6
in Remark
2.7
. This shows that our approach is quite versatile, and here we give some further examples in the literature where the method is used. In
[
43
, Section 2.5]
, the method is performed for the Erdős-Rényi random graph. That proof can easily be adapted to finite-type inhomogeneous random graphs as studied in the seminal paper by Bollobás, Janson and Riordan
[
18
]
, as performed in
[
43
, Chapters 3 and 6]
. In turn, such an approach also yields the type distribution of the giant, as identified in greater generality in
[
56
, Lemma 4.10]
. Further, and in line with Remark
2.7
, our results were recently used as a by-product of the work
[
58
]
(see also
[
59
]
) on component tails in spatial inhomogeneous random graphs. This work, combined with the local convergence in
[
46
]
, shows that the survival probability of the local limit indeed is the limit of the proportion of vertices in the giant. Here, the local limit is
not
a tree. This shows that our result has the potential to be widely applicable. Further, the identification of the local structure of the giant that follows from Theorem
2.5
now follows immediately, as it was done in
[
19
, Theorem 25]
for
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
, and applies for example also to spatial inhomogeneous random graphs as studied in
[
58
]
.

Local convergence.

There are many models for which local convergence has been established, and thus our results might be applied. For inhomogeneous random graphs, local convergence was not established explicitly in the seminal paper by Bollobás, Janson and Riordan
[
18
]
, but the methodology is very related and does allow for a simple proof of it (see
[
43
, Chapter 3]
for a formal proof, and
[
28
]
for a proof for Erdős-Rényi random graph). For the configuration model, and the related uniform random graph with prescribed degrees, it was proved explicitly by Dembo and Montanari
[
28
]
, see also
[
21
,
29
]
as well as
[
43
, Chapter 4]
for related proofs. Berger, Borgs, Chayes and Saberi
[
12
]
establish local convergence for preferential attachment models (see also
[
43
, Chapter 5]
for some extensions). A related preferential attachment model with conditionally independent edges is treated by Dereich and Mörters
[
32
]
, who again do not formally prove local convergence, but state a highly related result. Random intersection graphs are treated in
[
61
]
, see also
[
48
]
. In most of these models, the size of the giant is established, though mostly in ways that are quite different from the current proof (see
[
43
]
for an extensive overview). It would be of interest to see whether the current proof simplifies some of these analyses.

Limiting properties that do or do not follow from local convergence.

As stated in the introduction, local convergence is a versatile tool. The number of connected components, the clustering coefficient, local neighborhoods, and edge-neighborhoods are all local quantities (see
[
43
, Chapter 2]
for many examples of local quantities). In some cases, some extra local conditions (typically on the degree distribution) need to be made in order to be able to use such results. Some less obvious properties also converge when the graph converges locally. An important and early example is the Ising model partition function
[
28
]
. Also the PageRank distribution is local
[
40
]
. A property that is almost local is the density of the densest subgraph in a random graph, as shown by Anantharam and Salez
[
7
]
. Lyons
[
62
]
shows that the exponential growth rate of the number of spanning trees of a finite connected graph can be computed through the local limit. See also
[
68
]
for weighted spanning subgraphs,
[
39
]
for maximum-weight independent sets, and
[
13
]
for the limiting spectral distribution of the graph adjacency matrix.

Our approach shows that while the proportion of vertices in the giant is not a local property, it is ‘almost local’ in the sense that one additional necessary and sufficient condition does identify the limiting proportion of vertices in the giant in terms of the local limit.
It would be interesting to investigate whether the idea of ‘almost local’ properties can be extended to other properties as well. For example, while Theorem
3.9
suggests that also logarithmic distances are an almost local property, the statement in Theorem
3.9
follows from the proof of Theorem
3.2
, rather than from a general ‘almost local’ result as in Theorem
2.2
. It would be interesting to explore this further, to see whether also related typical distance results might be proved in a similar way (see
[
30
,
31
,
44
,
45
,
47
]
for such related results).

Percolation on finite graphs.

Another area where our results may be useful is percolation on finite graphs. There, it is often not even clear how to precisely
define
the critical value or critical behavior. We refer to Janson and Warnke
[
57
]
and Nachmias and Peres
[
66
]
for extensive discussions on the topic. Percolation on random graphs has also attracted substantial attention, see for example Janson
[
51
]
or Fountoulakis
[
36
]
for the derivation of the limiting percolation threshold for the configuration model. This is related to the locality of the percolation threshold as investigated by Benjamini, Nachmias and Peres
[
10
]
in the context of
d
-regular graphs that have large girth, and inspired by a question by Oded Schramm in 2008. Indeed, Schramm conjectured that the critical percolation threshold on a converging sequence of infinite graphs
G_{n}
should converge to that of the graph limit. In a transitive setting, local convergence can be used also on infinite graphs (as every vertex is basically the same, thus skipping the necessity of drawing a root uar). See also
[
5
,
9
,
65
]
for related work on sharp threshold for the existence of a giant in the context of
expanders
.

Recently, Alimohammadi, Borgs and Saberi
[
4
]
brought the discussion significantly forward, by showing that the percolation critical value is indeed local for
expanders
. Further, interestingly, they applied their results to the Barabási-Albert preferential attachment model
[
8
]
, in the version of Bollobás et al. in
[
20
]
, to show that
p_{c}=0
and identify the giant for all
p>0
. The proof relies on a sprinkling argument. It would be interesting to study the relation between our central assumption in (
2.9
) and
(G_{n})_{n\geq 1}
being a sequence of expander graphs. Further, it would be very interesting to see whether local convergence has consequences for the
critical
behavior of percolation on locally convergent graph sequences.

A.1
Local convergence of the configuration model

As mentioned already below Theorem
3.2
, there are several ways in which local convergence in probability, as formalised in (

r

below (see the discussion right below it). Concentration can be proved using martingale techniques (by pairing edges one by one and using Azuma-Hoefding), see

[

A.2
A useful degree-truncation argument for the configuration model

In this section, we prove Theorem
3.8
. The proof relies on an ‘explosion’ or ‘fragmentation’ of the vertices
[n]
in
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
inspired by
[
51
]
. Label the half-edges from 1 to
\ell_{n}
. We go through the vertices
v\in[n]
one by one. When
d_{v}\leq b
, we do nothing. When
d_{v}>b
, then we let
d_{v}^{\prime}=b
, and we keep the
b
half-edges with the lowest labels. The remaining
d_{v}-b
half-edges are exploded from vertex
v
, in that they are incident to vertices of degree 1 in
{\rm CM}_{n^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime})
, and are given vertex labels above
n
. We give the exploded half-edges the remaining labels of the half-edges incident to
v
. Thus, the half-edges receive labels both in
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
as well as in
{\rm CM}_{n^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime})
, and the labels of the half-edges incident to
v\in[n]
in
{\rm CM}_{n^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime})
are a subset of those in
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
. In total, we thus create an extra
n^{+}=\sum_{v\in[n]}(d_{v}-b)_{+}
‘exploded’ vertices of degree 1, and
n^{\prime}=n+n^{+}
.

We then pair the half-edges randomly, in the same way in
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
as in
{\rm CM}_{n^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime})
. This means that when the half-edge with label
x
is paired to the half-edge with label
y
in
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
, the half-edge with label
x
is also paired to the half-edge with label
y
in
{\rm CM}_{n^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime})
, for all
x,y\in[\ell_{n}]
.

We now check parts (a)-(c). Obviously parts (a) and (b) follow. For part (c), we note that all created vertices have degree 1. Further, for vertices
u,v\in[n]
, if there exists a path in
{\rm CM}_{n^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime})
connecting them, then the intermediate vertices have degree at least 2, so that they cannot correspond to exploded vertices. Thus, the same path of paired half-edges also exists in
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
, so that
u
and
v
are connected in
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
as well.
∎

A.3
Coupling of configuration neighborhoods and branching processes

In this section, we prove Lemma
3.3
and Remark
3.4
, as well as Lemma
3.5
.

Proof of Lemma
3.3
and Remark
3.4
.

Fix
m
. We next explain how to
jointly
construct
(\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(t),\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(t))_{t\in[m]}

given
that we have already constructed
(\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(t),\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(t))_{t\in[m-1]}
. Further, for each half-edge, we record its status as
real
or
ghost
, where the real half-edges correspond to the ones present in
both

\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(m-1)
and
\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(m-1)
, while the ghost half-edges are only present in
\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(m-1)
or
\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(m-1)
.

To obtain
\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(m)
, we take the first unpaired half-edge
x_{m}
in
\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(m-1)
. When this half-edge has the ghost status, then we draw a uniform
unpaired
half-edge
y_{m}^{\prime}
and pair
x_{m}
to
y_{m}^{\prime}
to obtain
\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(m)
, and we give all sibling half-edges of
y_{m}^{\prime}
the ghost status (where we recall that the sibling half-edges of a half-edge
y
are those half-edges unequal to
y
that are incident to the same vertex as
y
is).

When the half-edge has the real status, it needs to be paired both in
\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(m)
and
\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(m)
. To obtain
\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(m)
, this half-edge needs to be paired to a uniform ‘free’ half-edge, i.e., one that has not been paired so far. For
\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(m)
, this restriction does not hold. We now show how these two choices can be conveniently coupled.

For
\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(m)
, we draw a uniform half-edge
y_{m}
from the collection of
all
half-edges, independently of the past. Let
U_{m}
denote the vertex to which
y_{m}
is incident. We then let the
m
th individual in
(\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(t))_{t\in[m-1]}
have precisely
d_{U_{m}}-1
children. Note that
d_{U_{m}}-1
has the same distribution as
D_{n}^{\star}-1
and, by construction, the collection
\big(d_{U_{t}}-1\big)_{t\geq 1}
is iid. This constructs
\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(m)
, except for the statuses of the sibling half-edges incident to
U_{t}
, which we describe below.

For
\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(m)
, when
y_{m}
is still free, i.e., has not yet been paired in
(\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(t))_{t\in[m-1]}
, then we let
x_{m}
be paired to
y_{m}
in
\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(m)
, and we have thus also constructed
(\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(t),\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(t))_{t\in[m]}
. We give all the other half-edges of
U_{m}
the status real when
U_{m}
has not yet appeared in
\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(m-1)
, and otherwise we give them the ghost status. The latter case implies that a cycle appears in
(\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(t))_{t\in[m]}
. By construction, such a cycle does not occur in
(\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(t))_{t\in[m]}
, where re-used vertices are simply repeated several times.

A difference in the coupling arises when
y_{m}
has already been paired in
(\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(t))_{t\in[m-1]}
, in which case we give all the sibling half-edges of
U_{t}
the ghost status. For
\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(m)
, we draw a uniform
unpaired
half-edge
y_{m}^{\prime}
and pair
x_{m}
to
y_{m}^{\prime}
instead to obtain
\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(m)
, and give all the sibling half-edges of
y_{m}^{\prime}
the ghost status. Clearly, this might give rise to a difference between
\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(m)
and
\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(m)
.

We continue the above exploration algorithm until it terminates at some time
T_{n}
. Since each step pairs exactly one half-edge, we have that
T_{n}=|E(\mathscr{C}(o))|
, so that
T_{n}\leq\ell_{n}/2
steps. The final result is then
(\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(t),\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(t))_{t\in[T_{n}]}
. At this moment, however, the branching process tree
(\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(t))_{t\geq 1}
has
not
been fully explored, since all the tree vertices corresponding to ghost half-edges in
(\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(t))_{t\geq 1}
have not been explored. We complete the the tree exploration
(\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(t))_{t\geq 1}
by iid drawing children of all the ghost tree vertices until the full tree is obtained.

We emphasise that the law of
(\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(t))_{t\geq 1}
obtained above is not the same as that of
({\sf BP}_{n}(t))_{t\geq 1}
, since the order in which half-edges are paired is chosen so that
(\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(t))_{t\in[T_{n}]}
has the same law as the graph exploration process
({\sf G}_{n}(t))_{t\in[T_{n}]}
. However, with
\sigma_{n}
the first moment that a ghost half-edge is paired, we have that
(\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(t))_{t\in[\sigma_{n}]}

does
have the same law as
({\sf BP}_{n}(t))_{t\geq 1}
.

There are two sources of differences between
(\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(t))_{t\geq 0}
and
(\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(t))_{t\geq 0}
:

Half-edge re-use:

In the above coupling, a half-edge re-use occurs when
y_{m}
had already been paired and is being re-used in the branching process. As a result, for
(\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(t))_{t\in[m]}
, we need to redraw
y_{m}
to obtain
y_{m}^{\prime}
that is instead used in
(\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(t))_{t\in[m]}
;

Vertex re-use:

A vertex re-use occurs when
U_{m}=U_{m^{\prime}}
for some
m^{\prime}<m
. In the above coupling, this means that
y_{m}
is a half-edge that has not yet been paired in
(\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(t))_{t\in[m-1]}
, but it is
incident
to a half-edge that has already been paired in
(\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(t))_{t\in[m-1]}
. In particular, the vertex
U_{m}
to which it is incident has already appeared in
(\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(t))_{t\in[m-1]}
, and it is being re-used in the branching process. In this case, a
copy
of
U_{m}
appears in
(\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(t))_{t\in[m]}
, while a
cycle
appears in
(\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(t))_{t\in[m]}
.

We continue by providing a bound on both contributions:

Half-edge re-use.

Up to time
m-1
, exactly
2m-1
half-edges are forbidden to be used by
(\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(t))_{t\leq m}
. The probability that the half-edge
y_{m}
equals one of these these
2m-1
previously chosen half-edges is at most

\frac{2m-1}{\ell_{n}}.

(A.1)

Hence the expected number of half-edge re-uses before time
m_{n}
is at most

\sum_{m=1}^{m_{n}}\frac{2m-1}{\ell_{n}}=\frac{m_{n}^{2}}{\ell_{n}}=o(1),

(A.2)

when
m_{n}=o(\sqrt{n}).

Vertex re-use.

The probability that vertex
i
is chosen in the
m
th draw is equal to
d_{i}/\ell_{n}
. The probability that vertex
i
is drawn twice before time
m_{n}
is at most

\frac{m_{n}(m_{n}-1)}{2}\frac{d_{i}^{2}}{\ell_{n}^{2}}.

(A.3)

By the union bound, the probability that a vertex re-use has occurred before time
m_{n}
is at most

\frac{m_{n}(m_{n}-1)}{2\ell_{n}}\sum_{i\in[n]}\frac{d_{i}^{2}}{\ell_{n}}\leq m_{n}^{2}\frac{d_{\mathrm{max}}}{\ell_{n}}=o(1),

(A.4)

by Conditions
3.1
(a)-(b) and when
m_{n}=o(\sqrt{n/d_{\mathrm{max}}})
. This completes the coupling part of Lemma
3.3
, including the bound on
m_{n}
as formulated in Remark
3.4
. It is straightforward to check that the exploration can be performed from the two sources
(o_{1},o_{2})
independently, thus establishing the requested coupling to two independent branching processes claimed in Remark
3.4
.
∎

Finally, we adapt the above argument to prove Lemma
3.5
:

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

Define
a_{n}=(\bar{m}_{n}^{2}/\ell_{n})^{1+\delta}
where
\delta>0
.
Recall from (
3.17
) that

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n}(2)\equiv\mathcal{C}_{n}(2,1)\cap\mathcal{C}_{n}(2,2)

\displaystyle=\big\{(|\partial B_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(o_{2})|)_{k\leq\underline{k}_{n}}=(|{\sf BP}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}|)_{k\leq\underline{k}_{n}}\big\}

(A.5)

\displaystyle\qquad\cap\big\{\big||\partial B_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(o_{2})|-|{\sf BP}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}|\big|\leq(\overline{m}_{n}^{2}/\ell_{n})^{1+\delta}~\forall k\in[\overline{k}_{n}+1]\big\},

where
\mathcal{C}_{n}(2,1)
occurs whp. Also recall the definition of
\mathcal{D}_{n}
right above Lemma
3.7
.

We apply a first-moment method and bound

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{C}_{n}(2,2)^{c}\cap\mathcal{C}_{n}(2,1)\cap\mathcal{D}_{n})

(A.6)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{1}{a_{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}(2,1)}\big||B_{\bar{k}_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(o_{2})|-|{\sf BP}_{\bar{k}_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}|\big|\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{a_{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}(2,1)\cap\mathcal{D}_{n}}\big(|B_{\bar{k}_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(o_{2})|-|{\sf BP}_{\bar{k}_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}|\big)_{+}+\big(|B_{\bar{k}_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(G_{n})}(o_{2})|-|{\sf BP}_{\bar{k}_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}|\big)_{-}\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{1}{a_{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}(2,1)\cap\mathcal{D}_{n}}\big(|\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(m_{n})|-|\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(m_{n})|\big)_{+}+\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}(2,1)\cap\mathcal{D}_{n}}\big(|\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(m_{n})|-|\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(m_{n})|\big)_{-}\Big],

where the notation is as in Lemma
3.3
and
|\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(m_{n})|
and
|\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(m_{n})|
denote the number of half-edges and individuals found up to the
m_{n}
th step of the exploration starting from
o_{2}
, and
m_{n}=(b+1)\bar{m}_{n}
, while
x_{+}=\max\{0,x\}
and
x_{-}=\max\{0,-x\}
.

To bound these expectations, we adapt the proof of Lemma
3.3
to our setting. We start with the first term in (
A.6
), for which we use the exploration up to size
\bar{m}_{n}
used in the proof of Lemma
3.3
. We note that the only way that
|\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(t+1)|-|\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(t)|
can be larger than
|\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(t+1)|-|\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(t)|
is when a half-edge re-use occurs. This gives rise a
primary
ghost, which then possibly gives rise to some further
secondary
ghosts, i.e., ghost vertices that are found by pairing a ghost half-edge. Since all degrees are bounded by
b
, on
\mathcal{C}_{n}(2,1)
where the first
\underline{k}_{n}
generations are perfectly coupled, the total number of secondary ghosts, together with the single primary ghost, is at most

c_{n}\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{\bar{k}_{n}-\underline{k}_{n}}(b-1)^{k}=\frac{(b-1)^{\bar{k}_{n}-\underline{k}_{n}+1}-1}{b-2},

(A.7)

which tends to infinity. On
\mathcal{D}_{n}
, we can let
\bar{k}_{n}-\underline{k}_{n}\rightarrow\infty
arbitrarily slowly. Therefore, on
\mathcal{C}_{n}(2,1)\cap\mathcal{D}_{n}
,

|\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(\bar{m}_{n})|-|\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(\bar{m}_{n})|\leq c_{n}\#\{\text{half-edge re-uses up to time }\bar{m}_{n}\}.

(A.8)

Thus, by (
A.2
),

{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}(2,1)\cap\mathcal{D}_{n}}\big(|\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(\bar{m}_{n})|-|\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(\bar{m}_{n})|\big)_{+}\Big]\leq c_{n}\frac{m_{n}^{2}}{\ell_{n}}.

(A.9)

We continue with the second term in (
A.6
), which is similar. We note that
|\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(t+1)|-|\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(t)|
can be smaller than
|\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(t+1)|-|\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(t)|
when a half-edge re-use occurs, or when a vertex re-use occurs. Thus, again using that the total number of secondary ghosts, together with the single primary ghost, is at most
c_{n}
, on
\mathcal{C}_{n}(2,1)\cap\mathcal{D}_{n}
,

|\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(\bar{m}_{n})|-|\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(\bar{m}_{n})|\leq c_{n}\#\{\text{half-edge and vertex re-uses up to time }\bar{m}_{n}\}.

(A.10)

As a result, by (
A.2
) and (
A.4
),

{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}(2,1)\cap\mathcal{D}_{n}}\big(|\widehat{\sf G}_{n}(\bar{m}_{n})|-|\widehat{\sf BP}_{n}(\bar{m}_{n})|\big)_{+}\Big]\leq c_{n}\frac{\bar{m}_{n}^{2}}{\ell_{n}}.

(A.11)

We conclude that

{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{C}_{n}(2)^{c})\leq\frac{1}{a_{n}}2c_{n}\frac{\bar{m}_{n}^{2}}{\ell_{n}}=O\Big(\Big(\frac{\ell_{n}}{\bar{m}_{n}^{2}}\Big)^{\delta/2}\Big)=o(1),

(A.12)

when taking
c_{n}\rightarrow\infty
such that
c_{n}=o((\bar{m}_{n}^{2}/\ell_{n})^{\delta/2})
.
∎
