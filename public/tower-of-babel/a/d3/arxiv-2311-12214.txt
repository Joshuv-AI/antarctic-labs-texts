Random Fourier Signature Features
Csaba Tóth, Harald Oberhauser, Zoltán Szabó

Abstract
Tensor algebras give rise to one of the most powerful measures of similarity for sequences of arbitrary length called the signature kernel accompanied with attractive theoretical guarantees from stochastic analysis. Previous algorithms to compute the signature kernel scale quadratically in terms of the length and number of the sequences. To mitigate this severe computational bottleneck, we develop a random Fourier feature-based acceleration of the signature kernel acting on the inherently non-Euclidean domain of sequences. We show uniform approximation guarantees for the proposed unbiased estimator of the signature kernel, while keeping its computation linear in the sequence length and number. In addition, combined with recent advances on tensor projections, we derive two even more scalable time series features with favourable concentration properties and computational complexity both in time and memory. Our empirical results show that the reduction in computational cost comes at a negligible price in terms of accuracy on moderate size datasets, and it enables one to scale to large datasets up to a million time series. We release the code publicly available at https://github.com/tgcsaba/ksig .

Introduction

Machine learning has successfully been applied to tasks that require learning from complex and structured data types on non-Euclidean domains. Feature engineering on such domains is often tackled by exploiting the geometric structure and symmetries existing within the data
[
5
]
.
Learning from sequential data (such as video, text, audio, time series, health data, etc.) is a classic, but an ongoing challenge due to the following properties:

•

Non-Euclidean data.
The data domain is nonlinear since there is no obvious and natural way of adding sequences of different length.

•

Time-space patterns.

Statistically significant patterns can be distributed over time and space, that is, capturing the order structure in which “events” arise is crucial.

•

Time-warping invariance.
The meaning of many sequences is often invariant to reparametrization also frequently called time-warping, at least to an extent; e.g. a sentence spoken quicker or slower contains (essentially) the same information.

•

Discretization and irregular sampling.

Sequences often arise by sampling along an irregularly spaced grid of an underlying continuous time process. A general methodology should be robust as the sampling gets finer, sequences approximate paths (continuous-time limit), or as the discretization grid varies between sequences.

•

Scalability.
Sequence datasets can quickly become massive, so the computational complexity should grow subquadratically, in terms of all of the state-space dimension, and the length and number of sequences.

The signature kernel
\texttt{k}_{\texttt{Sig}}
is the state-of-the-art kernel for sequential data
[
87
,
71
,
49
]
that addresses the first 4 of the above questions and can rely on the modular and powerful framework of kernel learning
[
74
]
.
Its construction is motivated by classic ideas from stochastic analysis that give a structured description of a sequence by developing it into a series of tensors.
We refer to
[
48
]
for a recent overview of its various constructions and applications. In the real-world, various phenomena are well-modelled by systems of differential equations. The path signature arises naturally in the context of controlled differential equations. The role of the signature here is to provide a basis for the effects of a driving signal on systems of controlled differential equations. In essence, it captures the interactions of a controlling signal with a nonlinear system. This explains the widespread applicability of signatures to various problems across the sciences
[
55
]
. There is also geometric intuition behind signatures, see Section 1.2.4 in
[
10
]
.

Features vs Kernel/Primal vs Dual

Kernel learning circumvents the costly evaluation of a high- or infinite-dimensional feature map by replacing it with the computation of a Gram matrix which contains as entries the inner products of features between all pairs of data points.
This can be very powerful since the inner product evaluation can often be done cheaply by the celebrated "kernel trick", even for infinite-dimensional feature spaces, but the price is that now the computational cost is quadratic in the number of samples, and downstream algorithms further often incur a cubic cost usually in the form of a matrix inversion. On the other hand, when finite-dimensional features can used for learning, the primal formulation of a learning algorithm can perform training and inference in a cost that is linear with respect to the sample size assuming that the feature dimension is fixed. This motivates the investigation of finite-dimensional approximations to kernels that mimic their expressiveness at a lower computational cost. It is an interesting question how the feature dimension should scale with the dataset size to maintain a given (optimal) learning performance in downstream tasks,
which is investigated for instance by
[
68
,
7
,
80
,
50
,
78
,
45
]
.

Computational Cost of the Signature kernel

In the context of the signature kernel, one data point is itself a whole sequence.
Hence, given a data set
\mathbf{X}
consisting of
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
sequences where each sequence
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{\ell_{\mathbf{x}}})
is of maximal length
\ell_{\mathbf{x}}\leq\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and has sequence entries
\mathbf{x}_{i}
in a state-space of dimension
d
, then the existing algorithms to evaluate the Gram matrix of the
\texttt{k}_{\texttt{Sig}}
scale quadratically, i.e. as
O(N^{2}\ell^{2}d)
, both in sequence length
\ell
and number of sequences
N
.
So far this has only been addressed by subsampling (either directly the sequence elements to reduce the length or by column subsampling via the Nyström approach
[
94
]
), which can lead to crude approximations and performance degradation on large-scale datasets.

Contribution

Random Fourier Features (
RFF
)
[
61
]
is a classic technique to enjoy both the benefits of the primal and dual approach.
Here, a low-dimensional and random feature map is constructed, which although does not approximate the feature map of a translation-invariant kernel, its inner product is with high probability close to the kernel itself.
The main contribution of this article is to carry out such a construction for the signature kernel.
Concretely, we construct a random feature map on the domain of sequences called Random Fourier Signature Features (
RFSF
), such that its inner product is a random kernel
\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{\texttt{Sig}}
for sequences that is both

(i)

an unbiased estimator for
\texttt{k}_{\texttt{Sig}}
, and

(ii)

has analogous probabilistic approximation guarantees to the classic
RFF
kernel.

The challenge is that a direct application of the classic
RFF
technique is not feasible since this relies on Bochner’s theorem which does not apply since the sequence domain is not even a linear space and the feature domain is non-Abelian, which makes the use of (generalizations of
[
29
]
) Bochner’s theorem difficult due to the lack of sufficiently explicit representations.
We tackle this challenge by combining the algebraic structure of signatures with probabilistic concentration arguments; a careful analysis of the error propagation yields uniform concentration guarantees similar to the
RFF
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then, we introduce dimensionality reduction techniques for random tensors further approximating
\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{\texttt{Sig}}
to define the extremely scalable variants
\tilde{\texttt{k}}^{\texttt{DP}}_{\texttt{Sig}}
and
\tilde{\texttt{k}}^{\texttt{TRP}}_{\texttt{Sig}}
called
RFSF-DP
and
RFSF-TRP
saving considerable amounts of computation time and memory by low-dimensional projection of the feature set of the
RFSF
.

Hence, analogously to the classic
RFF
construction, the random kernels
\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{\texttt{Sig}},\tilde{\texttt{k}}^{\texttt{DP}}_{\texttt{Sig}},\tilde{\texttt{k}}^{\texttt{TRP}}_{\texttt{Sig}}
simultaneously enjoy the expressivity of an infinite-dimensional feature space as well as linear complexity in sequence length.
This overcomes the arguably biggest drawback of the signature kernel, which is the quadratic complexity in sample size and sequence length; the price for reducing the complexities by an order is that this approximation only holds with high probability. As in the case of the classic
RFF
, our experiments show that this is in general a very attractive tradeoff. Concretely, we demonstrate in the experiments that the proposed random features

(1)

provide comparable performance on moderate sized datasets to full-rank (quadratic time) signature kernels,

(2)

outperform other random feature approaches for time series on both moderate- and large-scale datasets,

(3)

allow scaling to datasets of a million time series

.

Random Fourier Features

Kernel methods allow to implicitly use an infinite-dimensional feature map
\varphi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{H}
by evaluation of the inner product
\texttt{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\langle\varphi(\mathbf{x}),\varphi(\mathbf{y})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\mathcal{H}
is a Hilbert space. This inner product can often be evaluated without direct computation of

and
\varphi(\mathbf{y})

\texttt{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
momentarily, kernel methods require the computation of a Gram matrix with
O(N^{2})
entries, that further incurs an
O(N^{3})
computational cost by most downstream algorithms, such as
KRR

[
75
]
,
GP

[
66
]
, and

[
74
]

RFF
) technique of
[
61
,
62
,
63
]
, which can be applied to any continuous, bounded, translation-invariant kernel on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

1

\texttt{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\texttt{k}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z})

\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(4)

[

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}:\mathcal{X}\to\tilde{\mathcal{H}}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{2\tilde{d}},\quad\tilde{\varphi}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tilde{d}}}\left(\cos\left(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right),\sin{\left(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)}\right),

Tensors and the tensor product

First, we provide a brief overview of tensors and tensor products of Hilbert spaces, which we will use to construct our feature space called the

The construction we adapt was first proposed by

\displaystyle(h_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes h_{m})(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})\coloneqq\left\langle h_{1},f_{1}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}\cdots\left\langle h_{m},f_{m}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{m}}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes^{\prime}\cdots\otimes^{\prime}\mathcal{H}_{m}\coloneqq\Span\left\{h_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes h_{m}\,:\,h_{1}\in\mathcal{H}_{1},\dots,h_{m}\in\mathcal{H}_{m}\right\},

Path Signatures

A classic way to obtain a structured and hierarchical description of a path
\mathbf{x}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathbf{x}
given as tensors of increasing degrees

\displaystyle S_{m}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\idotsint\limits_{0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{m}<T}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}(t_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}(t_{m})=\idotsint\limits_{0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{m}<T}\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t_{m})\mathrm{d}t_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}t_{m}.

Signature Features for Sequential Data

A challenge in machine learning when constructing feature maps for datasets of sequences is that the sequence length can vary from instance to instance; the space of sequences
\seq=\left\{(\mathbf{x}_{i})_{i=1}^{\ell}\,:\,\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\in\mathcal{X}\hskip 5.0pt\text{and}\hskip 5.0pt\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\right\}

[

turns concatentation into multiplication provides a principled approach to construct features for sequences. Below we recall the construction of discrete-time signatures based on
[

]

The key idea is to define the discrete-time signature of
1

Now assume we are given a static feature map

into some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}

\mathcal{X}

A natural first step is to apply the feature map

of

Simple aggregation of the individual features fails; e.g. summation of the individual features

Signature Kernels

The signature is a powerful feature set for nonlinear regression on paths and sequences.
A computational bottleneck associated with it is the dimensionality of the feature space

Subsequently,

3
Random Fourier Signature Features

The goal of this section is to build random features for sequences, that enjoy the benefit of linear sequence length and low-dimensional feature complexity with theoretical guarantees that the corresponding inner product is close to the
M
-truncated (discretized) signature kernel
\texttt{k}_{\texttt{Sig}_{\leq M}}
with high probability. We construct these random features in a two step process: firstly, we reduce the feature space from infinite to finite (but high) dimensionality through a careful construction using random Fourier features (RFFs), and in the second step we apply further dimensionality reduction to reduce the complexity to an even lower dimensional space in order to aid in scalability. Although we present this construction as conceptually distinct steps, the steps are coupled during the computation, and the features can be computed directly without going through the initial step.

From infinite to finite dimensions

Dimensionality Reduction: Diagonal Projection

Previously, we introduced a featurized approximation

Dimensionality Reduction: Tensor Random Projection

Previously, we built the
RFSF-DP
map by subsampling an independent set from the samples that constitute
RFSF
kernel. Here, we propose an alternative dimensionality reduction technique that starts again from the
RFSF
map, and uses random projections to project this generally high-dimensional tensor onto a lower dimension. Random projections are a classic technique in data science for reducing the data dimension, while preserving its important structural properties. They are built upon the celebrated Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma
[
37
]
, which states that a set of points in a high-dimensional space can be embedded into a space of much lower dimension, while approximately preserving their
geometry. Exploiting this property, we construct a tensor random projected (
TRP
) variant of our random kernel called
RFSF-TRP
, such that the computation is coupled between the
RFSF
and
TRP
maps, similarly to a kernel trick.

Tensorized random projections
[
81
,
64
]
construct random projections for tensors with concise parametrization that respects their tensorial nature. Given tensors
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)^{\otimes m}
for
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, the
TRP
map with
CP
(
CANDECOMP/PARAFAC

[
41
]
) rank-
1
is built via a random functional

\texttt{Pr}:\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)^{\otimes m}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\texttt{Pr}(\mathbf{s})=\left\langle\mathbf{p}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{p}_{m},\mathbf{s}\right\rangle_{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)^{\otimes m}}
, where
\mathbf{p}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{p}_{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{d})
are
d
-dimensional component vectors sampled from a standard normal distribution. Then, the inner product can be estimated as
\texttt{Pr}(\mathbf{s})\texttt{Pr}(\mathbf{t})\approx\mathbb{E}\left[\texttt{Pr}(\mathbf{s})\texttt{Pr}(\mathbf{t})\right]=\left\langle\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\right\rangle_{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)^{\otimes m}}
. Variance reduction is achieved by stacking
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such random projections, each with
i.i.d.
component vectors
\mathbf{p}_{1}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{p}_{m}^{(n)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{d})
. Hence, the
TRP
operator is defined as

\displaystyle\texttt{TRP}:\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)^{\otimes m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad\texttt{TRP}(\mathbf{s})\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\left\langle\mathbf{p}_{1}^{(i)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{p}_{m}^{(i)},\mathbf{s}\right\rangle\right)_{i=1}^{n}.

(37)

On the one hand, this allows to represent the random projection map onto
\mathbb{R}^{n}

parameters as opposed to the

\tilde{\texttt{k}}^{\texttt{TRP}}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}
approximates
\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}
;

(ii)

the quality of the approximation of
\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}
with respect to
\texttt{k}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}
.

Note that
(ii)
has already been discussed in Theorem
3
. Here, we state the following theoretical result which quantifies
(i)
. Combining these two results by means of triangle inequality and union bounding quantifies that
\tilde{\texttt{k}}^{\texttt{TRP}}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}
is a good estimator of
\texttt{k}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}
.

Theorem 9
.

Let
\texttt{k}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous, bounded, translation-invariant kernel with spectral measure
\Lambda
, which satisfies (
27
). Then, the following bound holds for
RFSF-TRP
kernel for signature level
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
sequences
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\seq
and
\epsilon>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lvert\tilde{\texttt{k}}^{\texttt{TRP}}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right\rvert\geq\epsilon\right]\leq C_{d,\Lambda}\exp\left(-\left(\frac{m^{2}\tilde{d}^{\frac{1}{2m}}\epsilon^{\frac{1}{m}}}{2\sqrt{2}e^{3}R\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{1\text{-}\mathrm{var}}\left\lVert\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{1\text{-}\mathrm{var}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right),

(42)

where the absolute constant is defined as
C_{d,\Lambda}\coloneqq 2\left(1+\frac{S}{2R}+\frac{S^{2}}{4R^{2}}\right)^{d}
.

The proof is given in the supplement under Theorem
38
utilizing the hypercontractivity of Gaussian polynomials
[
36
]
that is used to quantify the concentration of the
TRP
estimator. The concentration of the
RFSF-TRP
kernel is then governed by Theorems
3
and
9
combined. Together, they show that for smaller values of
\epsilon
(i.e. the regime change as discussed below Theorem
3
), the probability has a polynomial plus a sub-Gaussian tail, while for large
\epsilon

\left(\frac{1}{2m}\right)
-subexponential tail due to (
42

\tilde{d}
is
\left(\frac{1}{4m}\right)
-subexponential. This means that in terms of convergence,
RFSF-TRP
is the slowest among the 3 variations introduced so far. However, it is also the most efficient in terms of overall dimension, hence downstream computational complexity as well, since
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\texttt{Sig}}^{\texttt{TRP}}=\mathbb{R}^{M\tilde{d}}
.

Hyperparameter selection

For each dataset-kernel, we perform cross-validation to select the optimal hyperparameters that are optimized over the Cartesian product of the following options. For each method that requires a static kernel, we use the
RBF
kernel with bandwidth hyperparameter
\sigma>0
. This is specified in terms of a rescaled median heuristic, i.e.

\displaystyle\sigma=\alpha\med\left\{\left\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{2}/2\,:\,i\in[\ell_{\mathbf{x}}],j\in[\ell_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}],\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbf{X}\right\},\quad\text{for}\hskip 5.0pt\alpha>0,

(43)

where
\alpha
is chosen from
\alpha\in\{10^{-3},\dots,10^{3}\}
on a logarithmic grid with
19
steps. For each kernel that is not normalized by default (i.e. the
GAK
and
RBF
kernels are normalized, the former is because without normalization it blows up) , we select whether to normalize to unit norm in feature space via
\texttt{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\mapsto\texttt{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})/\sqrt{\texttt{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})\texttt{k}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y})}
. The
SVM
hyperparameter
C>0
is selected from
C\in\{10^{0},10^{1},10^{2},10^{3},10^{4}\}
. Further, motivated by previous work that investigates the effect of path augmentations in the context of signature methods
[
56
]
, we chose 3 augmentations to cross-validate over. First is parametrization encoding, which gives the classifier the ability to remove the warping invariance of a given sequence kernel, adding the time index as an additional coordinate, i.e. for each time series in the dataset
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X}
, we augment it via
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{i})_{i=1}^{\ell_{\mathbf{x}}}\mapsto(\beta i/\ell_{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}_{i})_{i=1}^{\ell_{\mathbf{x}}}
, where
\beta>0
is the parametrization intensity chosen from
\beta\in\{10^{0},10^{1},10^{2},10^{3},10^{4}\}
. The second augmentation is the basepoint encoding, the role of which is to remove the translation invariance of signature features. Note that when the static base kernel is chosen to be a nonlinear kernel other than the Euclidean inner product, the signature kernel is not completely translation-invariant due to the state-space nonlinearities, but it is close being that by the
L
-Lipschitz property in Lemma
28
valid for of the static kernels considered in this work. The basepoint encoding adds an initial
\mathbf{0}
step at the beginning of each time series, i.e. for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X}

\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{\ell_{\mathbf{x}}})\mapsto(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{\ell_{\mathbf{x}}})

\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{\ell_{\mathbf{x}}})\mapsto\left((\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{1}),(\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{1}),(\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{2}),\dots,(\mathbf{x}_{\ell_{\mathbf{x}}},\mathbf{x}_{\ell_{\mathbf{x}}-1}),(\mathbf{x}_{\ell_{\mathbf{x}}},\mathbf{x}_{\ell_{\mathbf{x}}})\right)

from

as suggested by the authors. This makes

\sigma>0
of the warping series from the same grid as
\alpha
in (
43

5
Conclusion

We constructed a random kernel
\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{\texttt{Sig}_{\leq M}}
for sequences that benefits from

(i)

lifting the original sequence to an infinite-dimensional
RKHS

\mathcal{H}
,

(ii)

linear complexity in sequence length,

(iii)

being with high probability close to the signature kernel
\texttt{k}_{\texttt{Sig}}
.

Thereby it combines the strength of the signature kernel
\texttt{k}_{\texttt{Sig}}
which is to implicitly use the iterated integrals of a sequence that has an infinite-dimensional
RKHS

\mathcal{H}
as state-space with the strength of (unkernelized) signature features
\varphi_{\texttt{Sig}}
that only require linear time complexity.
Our main theoretical result extends the theoretical guarantees for translation-invariant kernels on linear spaces to the signature kernel defined on the nonlinear domain
\seq
; however, the proofs differ from the classic case and require to analyse the error propagation in tensor space.
A second step is more straightforward, and combines this approach with random projections in finite-dimensions for tensors to reduce the complexity in memory further.
The advantages and disadvantages of the resulting approach are analogous to the classic
RFF
technique on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, namely a reduction of computational complexity by an order for the price of an approximation that only holds with high probability.
As in the classic
RFF
case, our experiments indicate that this is in general a favourable tradeoff.

In the future, it would be interesting both theoretically and empirically to replace the vanilla Monte Carlo integration in the
RFF
construction by block-orthogonal random matrices as done in
[
97
]
.
Further, our random features can also be used to define an unbiased appoximation to the inner product of expected signatures, which has found usecases, among many, in nonparametric hypothesis testing and market regime detection
[
11
,
33
]
, training of generative models
[
58
,
34
]
, and graph representation learning
[
86
]
.

Classic inequalities

The following inequalities are classic, and their proofs are in analysis textbooks, e.g.
[
22
,
69
]
. Firstly, Jensen’s inequality is useful for convex (concave) expectations.

Lemma 11
(Jensen’s inequality).

Let
X
be an integrable random variable, and
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
a convex function, such that
f\left(X\right)
is also integrable. Then, the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle f\left(\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(X\right)\right].

(44)

Hölder’s inequality is a generalization of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to
L^{p}
spaces.

Lemma 12
(Hölder’s inequality).

Let
p,q\geq 1

such that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
. Let
X
and
Y
respectively be
L^{p}
and
L^{q}
integrable random variables, i.e.
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert X\right\rvert^{p}\right]<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert Y\right\rvert^{q}\right]<\infty
. Then,
XY
is integrable, and it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert XY\right\rvert\right]\leq\mathbb{E}^{1/p}\left[\left\lvert X\right\rvert^{p}\right]\mathbb{E}^{1/q}\left[\left\lvert Y\right\rvert^{q}\right].

(45)

Although not inherently a probabilistic inequality, Young’s inequality can be used to decouple products of random variables.

Lemma 13
(Young’s inequality).

Let
p,q>0
with
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
. Then, for every
a,b\geq 0

\displaystyle ab\leq\frac{a^{p}}{p}+\frac{b^{q}}{q}.

(46)

Lemma 14
(Reverse Young’s inequality).

Let
p,q>0
such that
\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}=1
. Then, for every
a\geq 0
and
b>0
, it holds that

\displaystyle ab\geq\frac{a^{p}}{p}-\frac{b^{-q}}{q}.

(47)

Proof.

Apply Young’s inequality with
p^{\prime}=\frac{1}{p}
and
q^{\prime}=\frac{q}{p}
to
a^{\prime}=(ab)^{p}
and
b^{\prime}=b^{-p}
.

Subexponential concentration

Next, we state a variation on the well-known Bernstein inequality, which holds for random variables in the subexponential class. The condition (
56
) on a random variable
X
, in this case, is formulated as a moment-growth bound, called a Bernstein moment condition. We show in Lemmas
21
and
22
(in a more general setting) that (
56
) is equivalent to the random variable being subexponential. Further, note that the condition itself is given in terms of non-centered random variables, while the statement of the theorem is about their centered counterparts. For similar results, see
[
89
, Sec. 2.2.2]
.

Theorem 15
(Bernstein inequality - one-tailed).

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent random variables satisfying the following moment-growth condition for some
S,R>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}^{k}\right]\leq\frac{k!S^{2}R^{k-2}}{2}\quad\text{for all}\hskip 5.0ptk\geq 2.

(48)

Then, it holds for
\tilde{X}_{i}\coloneqq X_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}\right]
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i}\geq t\right]\leq\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{2(nS^{2}+Rt)}\right).

(49)

Proof.

We have for
\lambda>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i}\geq t\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\exp(-\lambda t)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i}\right)\right]

(50)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\exp\left(-\lambda t-\lambda\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}\right]\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\lambda X_{i})\right],

(51)

where (a) holds for any
\lambda>0
by the Chernoff bound applied to
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i}
, and in (b) we used the independence of
X_{i}
-s. Bounding the moment-generating function of
X_{i}
, one gets

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\lambda X_{i})\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(d)}}}{{=}}1+\lambda\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}\right]+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}^{k}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(e)}}}{{\leq}}1+\lambda\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}\right]+\frac{S^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{k}R^{k}

(52)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(f)}}}{{=}}1+\lambda\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}\right]+\frac{S^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2(1-\lambda R)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(g)}}}{{\leq}}\exp\left(\lambda\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}\right]+\frac{S^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2(1-\lambda R)}\right),

(53)

where (d) is due to the Taylor expansion of the exponential function, (e) implied by (
48
), (f) holds by the sum of geometric series for any
\lambda<1/R
, (g) follows from the inequality
1+x\leq\exp(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Using this to bound (
51
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i}\geq t\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\lambda t-\lambda\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}\right]\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(\lambda\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}\right]+\frac{S^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2(1-\lambda R)}\right)

(54)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\lambda t+\frac{nS^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2(1-\lambda R)}\right).

(55)

Finally, choosing
\lambda=t/(nS^{2}+Rt)
gives the statement.

Corollary 16
(Bernstein inequality - two-tailed).

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent random variables satisfying the the following moment-growth condition for some
S,R>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert X_{i}\right\rvert^{k}\right]\leq\frac{k!S^{2}R^{k-2}}{2}\quad\text{for all}\hskip 5.0ptk\geq 2.

(56)

Then, it holds for
\tilde{X}_{i}\coloneqq X_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}\right]
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lvert\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i}\right\rvert\geq t\right]\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{2(nS^{2}+Rt)}\right).

(57)

Proof.

Applying Theorem
15
to
\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i}
and
-\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i}
, and combining the two bounds gives the two-tailed result.

In this section, we introduce a specific class of Orlicz norms for heavy-tailed random variables, which generalizes subexponential distributions.

Definition 17
(

Let

\displaystyle\Psi_{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\Psi_{\alpha}(x)\coloneqq\exp\left(x^{\alpha}\right)-1\quad\text{for all}\hskip 5.0ptx\in\mathbb{R}.

The

-exponential Orlicz (quasi-)norm of a random variable

\displaystyle\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha}}\coloneqq\inf\left\{t>0:\mathbb{E}\left[\Psi_{\alpha}\left(\frac{\left\lvert X\right\rvert}{t}\right)\right]\leq 1\right\},

(59)

adhering to the standard convention that
\inf\emptyset=\infty
.

If a random variable
X
satisfies
\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha}}<\infty
, it is either called an
\alpha
-(sub)exponential random variable (if
\alpha<1

31

9
]
, or sub-Weibull of order-
\alpha

[

An alternative characterization of the

Remark 18
(Tail bound).

Let
\alpha>0
and
X
be a random variable such that
\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha}}<\infty
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lvert X\right\rvert\geq\epsilon\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{\alpha}}{\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha}}^{\alpha}}\right).

(60)

Proof.

Due to Definition
17
,
\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\left\lvert X\right\rvert^{\alpha}}{\left\lVert X\right\rVert^{\alpha}_{\Psi_{\alpha}}}\right)\right]\leq 2
, hence by Markov’s inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lvert X\right\rvert\geq\epsilon\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{\left\lvert X\right\rvert^{\alpha}}{\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha}}^{\alpha}}\geq\frac{\epsilon^{\alpha}}{\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha}}^{\alpha}}\right]\leq e^{-\frac{\epsilon^{\alpha}}{\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha}}^{\alpha}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\left\lvert X\right\rvert^{\alpha}}{\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha}}^{\alpha})}\right)\right]\leq 2e^{-\frac{\epsilon^{\alpha}}{\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha}}^{\alpha}}}.

(61)

Note that although

is often referred to as a norm, it does not satisfy the triangle inequality for

Lemma 19
(Generalized triangle inequality for Orlicz norm).

It holds for any random variables

and

\displaystyle\left\lVert X+Y\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha}}\leq C_{\alpha}\left(\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha}}+\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha}}\right),

(62)

where
C_{\alpha}=2^{1/\alpha}
if

and
1
otherwise.

Proof.

See Lemma A.3. in

Lemma 20
(Hölder inequality for Orlicz norm).

It holds for any random variables

\displaystyle\left\lVert\prod\nolimits_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha}}\leq\prod\nolimits_{i=1}^{k}\left\lVert X_{i}\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha_{i}}},

See Lemma A.1. in

Next, we show in the following two lemmas that a random variable

is
\alpha
-exponential if and only if

satisfies a Bernstein moment-growth condition.

Lemma 21
(Bernstein condition implies Orlicz norm bound).

Let
\alpha>0
and
X
be a random variable that satisfies for
S,R>0
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert X\right\rvert^{k\alpha}\right]\leq\frac{k!S^{2}R^{k-2}}{2}\quad\text{for all}\hskip 5.0ptk\geq 2.

(64)

Then, it holds that

\displaystyle\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha}}\leq\left(S\vee R\right)^{1/\alpha}.

(65)

Proof.

Firstly, due to Jensen’s inequality (Lemma
11
) and (
64
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert X\right\rvert^{\alpha}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}^{1/2}\left[\left\lvert X\right\rvert^{2\alpha}\right]\leq S.

(66)

We proceed similarly to the proof of Theorem
15
. For
t>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp{\frac{\left\lvert X\right\rvert^{\alpha}}{t^{\alpha}}}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(a)}}}{{=}}1+\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert X\right\rvert^{\alpha}\right]}{t^{\alpha}}+\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert X\right\rvert^{m\alpha}\right]}{t^{m\alpha}m!}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(b)}}}{{\leq}}1+\frac{S}{t^{\alpha}}+\frac{S^{2}}{2t^{2\alpha}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{R}{t^{\alpha}}\right)^{m}

(67)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(c)}}}{{=}}\underbrace{1+\frac{S}{t^{\alpha}}+\frac{S^{2}}{2t^{\cancel{2}\alpha}}\frac{\cancel{t^{\alpha}}}{t^{\alpha}-R}}_{f(t)},

(68)

where (a) is due to the Taylor expansion of the exponential function, (b) is due to (
64
) and (
66
), (c) is the sum of a geometric series for
R<t^{\alpha}
. Defining
f(t)\coloneqq 1+\frac{S}{t^{\alpha}}\left(1+\frac{S}{2(t^{\alpha}-R)}\right)
and solving for

leads to the quadratic inequality

\displaystyle 0\leq t^{2\alpha}-(S+R)t^{\alpha}+SR-\frac{S^{2}}{2},

(69)

which has roots
t^{\alpha}_{1,2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(S+R\pm\sqrt{\left(S-R\right)^{2}+2S^{2}}\right)
. We discard the left branch, which violates the condition

since

\displaystyle t_{2}^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\left(S+R-\sqrt{(S-R)^{2}+2S^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(S+R-\left\lvert S-R\right\rvert\right)=S\wedge R\leq R.

(70)

Now, as the inequality
\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\leq\left\lvert x\right\rvert+\left\lvert y\right\rvert
holds for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, we get

\displaystyle t_{1}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(S+R+\sqrt{(S-R)^{2}+2S^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(S+R+\left\lvert S-R\right\rvert+\sqrt{2}S\right)

(71)

\displaystyle=S\vee R+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}S\leq 2(S\vee R)

(72)

and hence choosing

implies

, and we are done.

Lemma 22

where

We have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert X\right\rvert^{k\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(a)}}}{{=}}k!\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha}}^{k\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{k!}\left(\frac{\left\lvert X\right\rvert}{\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha}}}\right)^{k\alpha}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(b)}}}{{\leq}}k!\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha}}^{k\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\left\lvert X\right\rvert^{\alpha}}{\left\lVert X\right\rVert^{\alpha}_{\Psi_{\alpha}}}\right)-1\right]

(74)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(c)}}}{{\leq}}k!\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha}}^{k\alpha}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(d)}}}{{=}}\frac{k!\left(\sqrt{2}\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha}}^{\alpha}\right)^{2}\left(\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\Psi_{\alpha}}^{\alpha}\right)^{k-2}}{2},

(75)

where (a) is simply multiplying and dividing by the same values, (b) is the inequality

for

, (c) follows from Definition
17

\displaystyle\left[\sup_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\lvert\partial^{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\tilde{\texttt{k}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\partial^{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right\rvert\geq\epsilon\right]\leq 16\left(\frac{C_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q},\mathcal{X}}}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{d}{d+1}}\exp\left(\frac{-\tilde{d}\epsilon^{2}}{4(d+1)(2S^{2}+R\epsilon)}\right).

(87)

Proof.

We adapt the proof of
[
61
,
77
]
. Note that as
\mathcal{X}
is compact, so is
\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}
, and it can be covered by an
\epsilon
-net of at most
T\coloneqq(4\left\lvert\mathcal{X}\right\rvert/r)^{d}
balls of radius
r>0

[
17
, Prop. 5]
with centers
\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{T}\in\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}
. Since for all
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}
there exists
i\in\{1,\dots,T\}
such that
\left\lVert\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}\leq r
, it holds for
f(\mathbf{z})\coloneqq\partial^{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\tilde{\texttt{k}}(\mathbf{z})-\partial^{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{z})
and
L_{f}\coloneqq\sup_{\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}}\left\lVert\nabla f(\mathbf{s})\right\rVert_{2}
that

\displaystyle\left\lvert f(\mathbf{z})-f(\mathbf{z}_{i})\right\rvert\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(a)}}}{{\leq}}\sup_{\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}}\left\lVert\nabla f(\mathbf{s})\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(b)}}}{{\leq}}\sup_{\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}}\left\lVert\nabla f(\mathbf{s})\right\rVert_{2}r=L_{f}r,

(88)

where (a) is due to the mean-value theorem followed by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and (b) is since
\left\lVert\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\right\rVert\leq r

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla f(\mathbf{z})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert\nabla\left[\partial^{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\tilde{\texttt{k}}(\mathbf{z})\right]\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\nabla\left[\partial^{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{z})\right]\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert\nabla\left[\partial^{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\tilde{\texttt{k}}(\mathbf{z})\right]\right\rVert_{2}+D_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q},\mathcal{X}}.

(89)

Differentiating

as defined in (

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla\left[\partial^{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\tilde{\texttt{k}}(\mathbf{z})\right]\right\rVert_{2}=\frac{1}{\tilde{d}}\left\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{d}}\mathbf{w}_{j}\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}}\cos^{(\left\lvert\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}\right\rvert)}(\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{z})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\tilde{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{d}}\left\lvert\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert\left\lVert\mathbf{w}_{j}\right\rVert_{2},

(90)

where

\cos
for

.
The main idea of the proof is then

\displaystyle\bigcap_{i=1}^{T}\{\left\lvert f(\mathbf{z}_{i})\right\rvert<\epsilon/2\}\bigcap\{L_{f}<\epsilon/2r\}\subseteq\{\left\lvert f(\mathbf{z})\right\rvert<\epsilon\,:\,\forall\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}\},

(91)

since
\left\lvert f(\mathbf{z})\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert f(\mathbf{z})-f(\mathbf{z}_{i})\right\rvert+\left\lvert f(\mathbf{z}_{i})\right\rvert
and (
88

\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}}\left\lvert f(\mathbf{z})\right\rvert\geq\epsilon\right]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lvert f(\mathbf{z}_{i})\right\rvert\geq\epsilon/2\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[L_{f}\geq\epsilon/2r\right].

(92)

Now, we need to bound all probabilities on the RHS. First, we deal with

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[L_{f}\geq\epsilon/2r\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(c)}}}{{\leq}}\mathbb{E}\left[L_{f}\right]\frac{2r}{\epsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(d)}}}{{\leq}}\left(D_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q},\mathcal{X}}+\frac{1}{\tilde{d}}\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{\tilde{d}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}\sim\Lambda}\left[\left\lvert\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert\left\lVert\mathbf{w}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}\right]\right)\frac{2r}{\epsilon}

(93)

\displaystyle\leq\left(D_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q},\mathcal{X}}+E_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\right)\frac{2r}{\epsilon},

(94)

where (c) is Markov’s inequality, (d) is (
89
) and (
90
).
To deal with the centers in (
92

\partial^{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\tilde{\texttt{k}}(\mathbf{z})
can be written as a sample average of
\tilde{d}

i.i.d.
terms as per (
6

\displaystyle\partial^{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\tilde{\texttt{k}}(\mathbf{z})=\partial^{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{d}}\cos(\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{z})\right)=\frac{1}{\tilde{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{d}}\partial^{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\cos(\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{z})

(95)

so that the Bernstein inequality (Cor.
16
) is applicable. For
j=1,\dots,\tilde{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}\sim\Lambda}{\left\lvert\partial^{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\cos\left(\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\right)\right\rvert^{m}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}\sim\Lambda}{\left\lvert\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}}\cos^{(\left\lvert\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}\right\rvert)}(\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{z})\right\rvert^{m}}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}\sim\Lambda}{\left\lvert\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert^{m}}\leq\frac{k!S^{2}R^{k-2}}{2},

(96)

and that
f(\mathbf{z})=\partial^{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\tilde{\texttt{k}}(\mathbf{z})-\partial^{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{z})=\partial^{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\tilde{\texttt{k}}(\mathbf{z})-\mathbb{E}\left[\partial^{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\tilde{\texttt{k}}(\mathbf{z})\right]
by the dominated convergence theorem.
Hence, we may call the Bernstein inequality (Cor.
16
) to control
f(\mathbf{z}_{i})
so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lvert f(\mathbf{z}_{i})\right\rvert\geq\epsilon/2\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lvert\partial^{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\tilde{\texttt{k}}(\mathbf{z})-\partial^{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{z})\right\rvert\geq\epsilon/2\right]\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-\tilde{d}\epsilon^{2}}{4(2S^{2}+R\epsilon)}\right).

(97)

Combining the bounds for
\left\lvert f(\mathbf{z}_{i})\right\rvert
(
97
), and for
L_{f}
(
94
), into (
92
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}}\left\lvert f(\mathbf{z})\right\rvert\geq\epsilon\right]\leq 2\left(\frac{4\left\lvert\mathcal{X}\right\rvert}{r}\right)^{d}\exp\left(\frac{-\tilde{d}\epsilon^{2}}{4(2S^{2}+R\epsilon)}\right)+\left(D_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q},\mathcal{X}}+E_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\right)\frac{2r}{\epsilon},

(98)

which has the form
g(r)=\tau_{1}r^{-d}+\tau_{2}r
, that is minimized by choosing

, so that by substituting back in

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}}\left\lvert f(\mathbf{z})\right\rvert\geq\epsilon\right]

\displaystyle\leq F_{d}2^{\frac{3d+1}{d+1}}\left(\frac{\left\lvert\mathcal{X}\right\rvert(D_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q},\mathcal{X}}+E_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}})}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{d}{d+1}}\exp\left(\frac{-\tilde{d}\epsilon^{2}}{4(d+1)(2S^{2}+R\epsilon)}\right).

(99)

Finally, we note that for

and

.

Distance bounds in the RKHS

Definition 27
(Lipschitz kernel).

Let
(\mathcal{X},d)
be a metric space. We call a kernel
\texttt{k}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
with RKHS
\mathcal{H}
an
L
-Lipschitz kernel over
\mathcal{X}
for some
L>0
if it holds for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{X}
that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\texttt{k}_{\mathbf{x}}-\texttt{k}_{\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}=\sqrt{\texttt{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})+\texttt{k}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y})-2\texttt{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}\leq Ld(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),

(100)

where
\texttt{k}_{\mathbf{x}}\coloneqq\texttt{k}(\mathbf{x},\cdot),\texttt{k}_{\mathbf{y}}\coloneqq\texttt{k}(\mathbf{y},\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}
.

Example 28
(Finite 2
nd
spectral moment implies Lipschitz).

Let
\texttt{k}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous, bounded and translation-invariant kernel with RKHS
\mathcal{H}
and spectral measure
\Lambda
, such that
\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}\sim\Lambda}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\right]<\infty
. Then, it holds that
k
is
\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}\sim\Lambda}\left[\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{2}^{1/2}
-Lipschitz, so for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
one has

\displaystyle\left\lVert\texttt{k}_{\mathbf{x}}-\texttt{k}_{\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}\sim\Lambda}\left[\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{2}^{1/2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}.

(101)

Proof.

We have for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\texttt{k}_{\mathbf{x}}-\texttt{k}_{\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(a)}}}{{=}}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})+\texttt{k}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y})-2\texttt{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(b)}}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}2-2\exp\left(i\mathbf{w}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\right)\mathrm{d}\Lambda(\mathbf{w})

(102)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(c)}}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}2-2\cos\left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\right)\mathrm{d}\Lambda(\mathbf{w})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(d)}}}{{\leq}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}\Lambda(\mathbf{w})

(103)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(e)}}}{{=}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}\sim\Lambda}\left[\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\top}\right](\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(f)}}}{{\leq}}\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}\sim\Lambda}\left[\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2},

(104)

where (a) holds due to the reproducing property, (b) is Bochner’s theorem, (c) is because the imaginary part of the integral evaluates to
0
as the kernel is real-valued, (d) is due to the inequality
1-t^{2}/2\leq\cos(t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, (e) is because
\left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\right)^{2}=(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^{\top}\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})
, and (f) is Cauchy-Schwarz inequality combined with the definition of the spectral norm.

Example 29
(Random Lipschitz bound for
RFF
).

Let
\tilde{\texttt{k}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be an
RFF
kernel defined as in (
5
) corresponding to some spectral measure
\Lambda
, and let
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
denote its feature space corresponding to the
RFF
map
\tilde{\varphi}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
defined as in (
5
), so that given
\mathbf{w}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{w}_{\tilde{d}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\Lambda
, we have for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that

\displaystyle\tilde{\texttt{k}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{\tilde{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{d}}\cos\left(\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\right).

(105)

Let
\mathbf{W}=(\mathbf{w}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{w}_{\tilde{d}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times\tilde{d}}
be the random matrix with column vectors
\mathbf{w}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{w}_{\tilde{d}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\Lambda
. Then,
\tilde{\texttt{k}}
is
\left(\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{W}\right\rVert_{2}}{\sqrt{\tilde{d}}}\right)
-Lipschitz, so that for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have the inequality

\displaystyle\left\lVert\tilde{\varphi}(\mathbf{x})-\tilde{\varphi}(\mathbf{y})\right\rVert_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}}\leq\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{W}\right\rVert_{2}}{\sqrt{\tilde{d}}}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}.

(106)

Proof.

The proof follows analogously to that of Example
28
. Let
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then

\displaystyle\left\lVert\tilde{\varphi}(\mathbf{x})-\tilde{\varphi}(\mathbf{y})\right\rVert_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(a)}}}{{=}}2-\frac{2}{\tilde{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{d}}\cos(\mathbf{w}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(b)}}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{\tilde{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{d}}\left(\mathbf{w}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\right)^{2}

(107)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(c)}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{\tilde{d}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(d)}}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{\tilde{d}}\left\lVert\mathbf{W}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert^{2}_{2},

(108)

where (a) is due to the cosine identity
\cos(a-b)=\cos(a)\cos(b)+\sin(a)\sin(b)
for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, (b) is due to the inequality
1-t^{2}/2\leq\cos(t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, (c) is because
\left(\mathbf{w}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\right)^{2}=(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{i}\mathbf{w}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})
and
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}=\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{d}}\mathbf{w}_{i}\mathbf{w}_{i}^{\top}
, (d) is due to the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality combined with the definition of the spectral norm.

Bounds for the Signature Kernel

A well-known property of signature features that they decay factorially fast with respect to the tensor level
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 30
(Norm bound for signature features).

Let
L>0
and
\texttt{k}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be an
L
-Lipschitz kernel with RKHS
\mathcal{H}
. Then, we have for the level-
m
signature features
\varphi_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}(\mathbf{x})
of the sequence
\mathbf{x}\in\seq
that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\varphi_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}(\mathbf{x})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes m}}\leq\frac{\left(L\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{{1\text{-}\mathrm{var}}}\right)^{m}}{m!}.

(109)

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\varphi_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}(\mathbf{x})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes m}}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\Delta_{m}(\ell_{\mathbf{x}}-1)}\delta\texttt{k}_{\mathbf{x}_{i_{1}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\delta\texttt{k}_{\mathbf{x}_{i_{m}}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes m}}

(110)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(a)}}}{{\leq}}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\Delta_{m}(\ell_{\mathbf{x}}-1)}\left\lVert\delta\texttt{k}_{\mathbf{x}_{i_{1}}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\cdots\left\lVert\delta\texttt{k}_{\mathbf{x}_{i_{m}}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}

(111)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(b)}}}{{\leq}}L^{m}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\Delta_{m}(\left\lvert\mathbf{x}-1\right\rvert)}\left\lVert\delta\mathbf{x}_{i_{1}}\right\rVert_{2}\cdots\left\lVert\delta\mathbf{x}_{i_{m}}\right\rVert_{2}

(112)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(c)}}}{{\leq}}\frac{\left(L\sum_{i=1}^{\ell_{\mathbf{x}}-1}\left\lVert\delta\mathbf{x}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}\right)^{m}}{m!}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(d)}}}{{=}}\frac{\left(L\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{1\text{-}\mathrm{var}}\right)^{m}}{m!},

(113)

where (a) follows from triangle inequality followed by factorizing the tensor norm, (b) is the
L
-Lipschitzness property, (c) from completing the multinomial expansion and normalizing by the number of permutations (note that
\Delta_{m}(\ell_{\mathbf{x}}-1)
contains a single permutation of all such multi-indices that have nonrepeating entries), and (d) is the definition of sequence
1
-variation.

The following bound for the signature kernel is a direct consequence of the previous lemma.

Corollary 31
(Upper bound for signature kernel).

Let
\texttt{k}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be an
L
-Lipschitz kernel, and
\texttt{k}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}:\seq\times\seq\to\mathbb{R}
the level-
m

k
. Then, we have the following bound for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\seq

\displaystyle\left\lvert\texttt{k}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right\rvert\leq\frac{\left(L^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{1\text{-}\mathrm{var}}\left\lVert\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{1\text{-}\mathrm{var}}\right)^{m}}{(m!)^{2}}.

(114)

Proof.

Note that without a kernel trick,
\texttt{k}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}
is written for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\seq
as the inner product

\displaystyle\left\lvert\texttt{k}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\left\langle\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\Delta_{m}(\ell_{\mathbf{x}}-1)}\delta\texttt{k}_{\mathbf{x}_{i_{1}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\delta\texttt{k}_{\mathbf{x}_{i_{m}}},\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\Delta_{m}(\ell_{\mathbf{y}}-1)}\delta\texttt{k}_{\mathbf{y}_{j_{1}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\delta\texttt{k}_{\mathbf{y}_{j_{m}}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes m}}\right\rvert

(115)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(a)}}}{{\leq}}\left\lVert\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\Delta_{m}(\ell_{\mathbf{x}}-1)}\delta\texttt{k}_{\mathbf{x}_{i_{1}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\delta\texttt{k}_{\mathbf{x}_{i_{m}}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes m}}\left\lVert\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\Delta_{m}(\ell_{\mathbf{y}}-1)}\delta\texttt{k}_{\mathbf{y}_{j_{1}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\delta\texttt{k}_{\mathbf{y}_{j_{m}}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes m}}

(116)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(b)}}}{{\leq}}\frac{\left(L^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{1\text{-}\mathrm{var}}\left\lVert\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{1\text{-}\mathrm{var}}\right)^{m}}{(m!)^{2}},

(117)

where (a) follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and (b) is implied by Lemma
30
.

A similar upper bound to Lemma
30
also holds for the

\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}
, that now depends on the norms of the random matrices
\mathbf{W}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{W}^{(m)}

Proof.

\displaystyle\left\lVert\tilde{\varphi}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}(\mathbf{x})\right\rVert_{{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}}^{\otimes m}}

Corollary 33
(Random upper bound for

RFSF

Lemma 34
(2
nd
order mean-value theorem).

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a twice differentiable function, and
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex and compact set. Then, we have for any
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{X}
that

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v})-f(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{y})+f(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\leq\sup_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\lVert\partial^{2}_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}f(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{v}\right\rVert_{2},

(124)

where
\partial^{2}_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}f(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})\coloneqq\left(\frac{\partial^{2}f(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})}{\partial s_{i}\partial t_{j}}\right)_{i,j=1}^{d}
refers to the submatrix of the Hessian of cross-derivatives.

Proof.

Keeping
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{X}
as fixed, we may define
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
as
g(\cdot)\coloneqq f(\cdot,\mathbf{y})-f(\cdot,\mathbf{v})
, so that the expression above can be written as
g(\mathbf{x})-g(\mathbf{u})
, and by the convexity of
\mathcal{X}
, we may apply the mean-value theorem to find that
\exists s\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{x})-g(\mathbf{u})=\left\langle\nabla g(s\mathbf{x}+(1-s)\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u}\right\rangle\leq\sup_{\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\lVert\nabla g(\mathbf{s})\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u}\right\rVert_{2},

(125)

where
\nabla g(\mathbf{s})\coloneqq\left(\frac{\partial g(\mathbf{s})}{\partial s_{i}}\right)_{i=1}^{d}
denotes the gradient of
g
, while the second inequality follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the compactness of
\mathcal{X}
(so that the
\sup
exists). Also note that
\nabla g(\mathbf{s})=\partial_{\mathbf{s}}f(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{y})-\partial_{\mathbf{s}}f(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{v})
, so defining
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
h(\cdot)\coloneqq\partial_{\mathbf{s}}f(\mathbf{s},\cdot)
and applying the vector-valued mean-value inequality
[
69
, Thm. 9.19]
to
h
gives that
\exists t\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\left\lVert h(\mathbf{y})-h(\mathbf{v})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert\Jac_{h}(t\mathbf{y}+(1-t)\mathbf{v})\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{v}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\lVert\Jac_{h}(\mathbf{t})\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{v}\right\rVert_{2},

(126)

where
\Jac_{h}(\mathbf{t})\coloneqq\left(\frac{\partial h_{i}(\mathbf{t})}{\partial t_{j}}\right)_{i,j=1}^{d}
refers to the Jacobian of
h
. Putting the inequalities (
125
) and (
126
) together and substituting back the function
f
gives the desired result.

This is our final lemma in our exposition of supplementary results about the (random) signature kernels, and it will be our main tool for proving Theorem
3
.

and Jensen’s inequality (Lemma

), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert w_{i}\right\rvert^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}^{1/2}\left[\left\lvert w_{i}\right\rvert^{4}\right]<\infty\quad\text{for all}\hskip 5.0pti\in\left[d\right].

(155)

Hence, by linearity of the expectation
\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert^{2}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[w_{i}^{2}\right]<\infty
. Next, due to Hölder’s inequality (Lemma
12
), it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[w_{i}w_{j}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert w_{i}w_{j}\right\rvert\right]\leq\mathbb{E}^{1/2}\left[\left\lvert w_{i}\right\rvert^{2}\right]\mathbb{E}^{1/2}\left[\left\lvert w_{j}\right\rvert^{2}\right]<\infty\quad\text{for all}\hskip 5.0pti,j\in\left[d\right],

(156)

therefore
L<\infty
. Further, applying Hölder’s inequality twice followed by Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert w_{i}w_{j}w_{k}\right\rvert\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}^{2/3}\left[\left\lvert w_{i}w_{j}\right\rvert^{3/2}\right]\mathbb{E}^{1/3}\left[\left\lvert w_{k}\right\rvert^{3}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}^{1/3}\left[\left\lvert w_{i}\right\rvert^{3}\right]\mathbb{E}^{1/3}\left[\left\lvert w_{j}\right\rvert^{3}\right]\mathbb{E}^{1/3}\left[\left\lvert w_{k}\right\rvert^{3}\right]

(157)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}^{1/6}\left[\left\lvert w_{i}\right\rvert^{6}\right]\mathbb{E}^{1/6}\left[\left\lvert w_{j}\right\rvert^{6}\right]\mathbb{E}^{1/6}\left[\left\lvert w_{k}\right\rvert^{6}\right]<\infty\quad\text{for all}\hskip 5.0pti,j,k\in\left[d\right],

(158)

which is finite due to (
152
) with
m=3
. Now, because of the
\ell^{1}
-
\ell^{2}
norm inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert w_{i}w_{j}\right\rvert\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert w_{i}w_{j}\right\rvert\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{1}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert w_{i}w_{j}w_{k}\right\rvert\right]<\infty,

(159)

hence
\bar{E}<\infty
. Next, as per
[
73
, Thm. 1.2.1.(iii)]
, as (
158
) holds for all
i,j,k\in\left[d\right]
,
k
is
3
-times continuously differentiable, which combined with the compactness of
\mathcal{X}
, hence that of
\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}
, gives
\sup_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}}\left\lvert\frac{\partial^{3}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{z})}{\partial z_{i}\partial z_{j}\partial z_{k}}\right\rvert<\infty
. Finally, from the
\ell^{1}
-
\ell^{2}
inequality again, we get that

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}}\left\lVert\nabla\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{z})}{\partial z_{i}\partial z_{j}}\right]\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sup_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}}\left\lVert\nabla\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{z})}{\partial z_{i}\partial z_{j}}\right]\right\rVert_{1}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sup_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}}\left\lvert\frac{\partial^{3}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{z})}{\partial z_{i}\partial z_{j}\partial z_{k}}\right\rvert<\infty,

(160)

which shows the finiteness of
\bar{D}
. This finishes showing that the stated quantities are finite.

Splitting the bound.

To start proving our main inequality, first note that as per Example
28
,
k
is
L
-Lipschitz (see Def.
27
). Hence, Lemma
35
yields that

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\seq\\
\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{1\text{-}\mathrm{var}},\left\lVert\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{1\text{-}\mathrm{var}}\leq V\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\left\lvert\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right\rvert

(161)

\displaystyle\leq V^{2m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{L^{2(m-k)}}{\tilde{d}^{k-1}((k-1)!)^{2}}\sup_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\lVert\partial^{2}_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})-\partial^{2}_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})\right\rVert_{2}\prod_{p=1}^{k-1}\left\lVert\mathbf{W}^{(p)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(162)

We bound the summand in the previous line in probability for each
k\in\left[m\right]
.
For brevity, denote
\alpha_{m,k}\coloneqq\frac{V^{2m}L^{2(m-k)}}{((k-1)!)^{2}}
, and first consider the case
k\geq 2
, so that we have

\displaystyle P_{k}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{\alpha_{m,k}}{\tilde{d}^{k-1}}\left\lVert\mathbf{W}^{(1)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\cdots\left\lVert\mathbf{W}^{(k-1)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\sup_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\lVert\partial_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})-\partial_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})\right\rVert_{2}\geq\epsilon\right]

(163)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(a)}}}{{\leq}}\mathbb{P}\left[\alpha_{m,k}\left(\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{W}^{(1)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\ldots+\left\lVert\mathbf{W}^{(k-1)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\tilde{d}(k-1)}\right)^{k-1}\sup_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\lVert\partial_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})-\partial_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})\right\rVert_{2}\geq\epsilon\right]

(164)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(b)}}}{{=}}\mathbb{P}\left[\underbrace{\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{W}^{(1)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\ldots+\left\lVert\mathbf{W}^{(k-1)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\tilde{d}(k-1)}}_{(A_{k})}\underbrace{\sup_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\lVert\partial_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})-\partial_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})\right\rVert_{2}^{\frac{1}{k-1}}}_{(B_{k})}\geq\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha_{m,k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\right],

(165)

where in (a) we used the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality, and in (b) we divided both sides by
\alpha_{m,k}
and took the
(k-1)
th root.
Further, setting
t\coloneqq\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha_{m,k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}
, we have for
\gamma>0

\displaystyle P_{k}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[A_{k}\cdot B_{k}\geq t\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(c)}}}{{\leq}}\mathbb{P}\left[\left(A_{k}-\gamma\right)B_{k}\geq\frac{t}{2}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[B_{k}\geq\frac{t}{2\gamma}\right]

(166)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(d)}}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\inf_{\tau>0}\left\{\mathbb{P}\left[A_{k}-\gamma\geq\frac{\tau}{2}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[B_{k}\geq\frac{t}{\tau}\right]\right\}+\mathbb{P}\left[B_{k}\geq\frac{t}{2\gamma}\right],

(167)

where in (c) we added and subtracted
\gamma B_{k}
and applied a union bound, while in (d) we combined a union bound with the relation
\{XY\geq\epsilon\}\subseteq\{X\geq\tau\}\bigcup\{Y\geq\epsilon/\tau\}
which holds for any
\tau>0
.

Our aim is now to obtain good probabilistic bounds on
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
to use in (
167
) with the specific choice of
\gamma=\mathbb{E}\left[A_{k}\right]=\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}
.

Bounding
A_{k}
.

By the inequality between the spectral and Frobenius norms, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{k-1}\left\lVert\mathbf{W}^{(p)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{p=1}^{k-1}\left\lVert\mathbf{W}^{(p)}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{p=1}^{k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{d}}\left(w_{i,j}^{(p)}\right)^{2}.

(168)

Now note that
w_{i,j}^{(p)}
are
i.i.d.
copies of the
i
th
marginal of
\Lambda
for all
j\in\left[\tilde{d}\right]
and
p\in\left[k-1\right]
, so that via (
152
)
\left(w_{i,j}^{(p)}\right)^{2}
satisfies the Bernstein moment condition

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(w_{i,j}^{(p)}\right)^{2k}\right]\leq\frac{k!S^{2}R^{k-2}}{2}\quad\text{for all}\hskip 5.0pti\in[d],j\in[\tilde{d}],p\in[m],k\geq 2.

(169)

Hence, we may apply the Bernstein inequality from Theorem
15
so that for
i\in\left[d\right]

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{\tilde{d}(k-1)}\sum\nolimits_{p=1}^{k-1}\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{\tilde{d}}\left(w_{i,j}^{(p)}\right)^{2}-\sigma_{i}^{2}\geq\epsilon\right]\leq\exp\left(\frac{-\tilde{d}(k-1)\epsilon^{2}}{2(S^{2}+R\epsilon)}\right),

(170)

where
\sigma_{i}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}\sim\Lambda}\left[w_{i}^{2}\right]
.
Combining these bounds for all
i\in\left[d\right]
and denoting
\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[A_{k}-\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}\geq\epsilon\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{\tilde{d}(k-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{p=1}^{k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{d}}\left(w_{i,j}^{(p)}\right)^{2}-\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}\geq\epsilon\right]

(171)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{\tilde{d}(k-1)}\sum\nolimits_{p=1}^{k-1}\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{\tilde{d}}\left(w_{i,j}^{(p)}\right)^{2}-\sigma_{i}^{2}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{d}\right]

(172)

\displaystyle\leq d\exp\left(\frac{-\tilde{d}(k-1)\left(\frac{\epsilon}{d}\right)^{2}}{2\left(S^{2}+R\frac{\epsilon}{d}\right)}\right).

(173)

Hence, we have the required probabilistic bound for the term in (
167
) containing
A_{k}
.

Bounding
B_{k}
.

One can bound the spectral norm by the max norm so that

\displaystyle B_{k}^{k-1}=\sup_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\lVert\partial_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})-\partial_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\lVert\partial_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})-\partial_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})\right\rVert_{\max}

(174)

\displaystyle=\max_{i,j=1,\dots,d}\sup_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\lvert\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})}{\partial s_{i}\partial t_{j}}-\frac{\partial^{2}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})}{\partial s_{i}\partial t_{j}}\right\rvert.

(175)

Let
i,j\in\left[d\right]
and denote
E_{i,j}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}\sim\Lambda}\left[\left\lvert w_{i}w_{j}\right\rvert\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{2}\right]
and
D_{i,j}\coloneqq\sup_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}}\left\lVert\nabla\left[\partial^{\mathbf{e}_{i},\mathbf{e}_{j}}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{z})\right]\right\rVert_{2}
, which are finite as previously shown. Due to Hölder’s inequality (Lemma
12
) and (
152
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert w_{i}w_{j}\right\rvert^{m}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}^{1/2}\left[w_{i}^{2m}\right]\mathbb{E}^{1/2}\left[w_{j}^{2m}\right]<\infty.

(176)

Recall that
k
is
3
-times continuously differentiable, so that the conditions required by Theorem
26
are satisfied, that we now call to our aid in controlling the
RFF
kernel derivatives,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\lvert\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})}{\partial s_{i}\partial t_{j}}-\frac{\partial^{2}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})}{\partial s_{i}\partial t_{j}}\right\rvert\geq\epsilon\right]\leq 16C^{\prime}_{d,\mathcal{X},i,j}\epsilon^{-\frac{d}{d+1}}\exp\left(\frac{-\tilde{d}\epsilon^{2}}{4(d+1)(2S^{2}+R\epsilon)}\right),

(177)

where we defined
C^{\prime}_{d,\mathcal{X},i,j}\coloneqq\left(\left\lvert\mathcal{X}\right\rvert(D_{i,j}+E_{i,j})\right)^{\frac{d}{d+1}}
.
Hence, noting that the max satisfies the relation
\{\max_{i}\xi_{i}\geq\epsilon\}=\bigcup_{i}\{\xi_{i}\geq\epsilon\}

and union bounding (
175
) in probability, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[B_{k}^{k-1}\geq\epsilon\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\lvert\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})}{\partial s_{i}\partial t_{j}}-\frac{\partial^{2}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})}{\partial s_{i}\partial t_{j}}\right\rvert\geq\epsilon\right]

(178)

\displaystyle\leq 16C^{\prime}_{d,\mathcal{X}}\epsilon^{-\frac{d}{d+1}}\exp\left(\frac{-\tilde{d}\epsilon^{2}}{4(d+1)(2S^{2}+R\epsilon)}\right),

(179)

where we denote
C^{\prime}_{d,\mathcal{X}}\coloneqq\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}C^{\prime}_{d,\mathcal{X},i,j}=\left\lvert\mathcal{X}\right\rvert^{\frac{d}{d+1}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\left(D_{i,j}+E_{i,j}\right)^{\frac{d}{d+1}}
.

Putting it together.
Now that we have our bounds for
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
, we put everything together, that is, plug the bounds (
173
) and (
179
) into (
167
), so that we get

\displaystyle P_{k}(\epsilon)\leq

\displaystyle\inf_{\tau>0}\left\{d\exp\left(\frac{-\tilde{d}(k-1)\left(\frac{\tau}{2d}\right)^{2}}{2\left(S^{2}+R\frac{\tau}{2d}\right)}\right)+16C^{\prime}_{d,\mathcal{X}}\left(\frac{\tau}{t}\right)^{\frac{d(k-1)}{d+1}}\exp\left(\frac{-\tilde{d}\left(\frac{t}{\tau}\right)^{2(k-1)}}{4(d+1)\left(2S^{2}+R\left(\frac{t}{\tau}\right)^{k-1}\right)}\right)\right\}

(180)

\displaystyle+16C^{\prime}_{d,\mathcal{X}}\left(\frac{2\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}}{t}\right)^{\frac{d(k-1)}{d+1}}\exp\left(\frac{-\tilde{d}\left(\frac{t}{2\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}}\right)^{2(k-1)}}{4(d+1)\left(2S^{2}+R\left(\frac{t}{2\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}}\right)^{k-1}\right)}\right)

(181)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(e)}}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle d\exp\left(\frac{-\tilde{d}(k-1)\left(\frac{t^{\frac{k-1}{k}}}{2d}\right)^{2}}{2\left(S^{2}+R\frac{t^{\frac{k-1}{k}}}{2d}\right)}\right)+16C^{\prime}_{d,\mathcal{X}}\left(\frac{1}{t}\right)^{\frac{d(k-1)}{(d+1)k}}\exp\left(\frac{-\tilde{d}t^{\frac{2(k-1)}{k}}}{4(d+1)\left(2S^{2}+Rt^{\frac{k-1}{k}}\right)}\right)

(182)

\displaystyle+16C^{\prime}_{d,\mathcal{X}}\left(\frac{2\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}}{t}\right)^{\frac{d(k-1)}{d+1}}\exp\left(\frac{-\tilde{d}\left(\frac{t}{2\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}}\right)^{2(k-1)}}{4(d+1)\left(2S^{2}+R\left(\frac{t}{2\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}}\right)^{k-1}\right)}\right)

(183)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(f)}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle d\exp\left(\frac{-\tilde{d}(k-1)\left(\frac{\left(\epsilon/\alpha_{m,k}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}}{2d}\right)^{2}}{2\left(S^{2}+R\frac{\left(\epsilon/\alpha_{m,k}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}}{2d}\right)}\right)+16C^{\prime}_{d,\mathcal{X}}\left(\frac{\alpha_{m,k}}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{d}{(d+1)k}}\exp\left(\frac{-\tilde{d}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha_{m,k}}\right)^{\frac{2}{k}}}{4(d+1)\left(2S^{2}+R\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha_{m,k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}\right)}\right)

(184)

\displaystyle+16C^{\prime}_{d,\mathcal{X}}\left(\frac{\alpha_{m,k}\left(2\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}\right)^{k-1}}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{d}{d+1}}\exp\left(\frac{-\tilde{d}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha_{m,k}\left(2\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}\right)^{k-1}}\right)^{2}}{4(d+1)\left(2S^{2}+R\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha_{m,k}\left(2\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}\right)^{k-1}}\right)}\right),

(185)

where (a) follows from substituting (
179
) and (
173
) into (
167
) with the choice of
\gamma=\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}
, (b) from choosing
\tau=t^{\frac{k-1}{k}}
, and (c) from putting back
t=(\epsilon/\alpha_{m,k})^{\frac{1}{k-1}}
.

Note that the previous applies for all
k\geq 2
. For
k=1
, we have by (
179
) that

\displaystyle P_{1}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\lVert\partial_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{1}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})-\partial_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}\texttt{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})\right\rVert_{2}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha_{m,1}}\right]

(186)

\displaystyle\leq 16C^{\prime}_{d,\mathcal{X}}\left(\frac{\alpha_{m,1}}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{d}{d+1}}\exp\left(\frac{-\tilde{d}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha_{m,1}}\right)^{2}}{4(d+1)\left(2S^{2}+R\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha_{m,1}}\right)}\right).

(187)

Combining and simplifying.
We can now combine the bounds for
P_{1},\dots,P_{m}
into (
162
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\seq\\
\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{1\text{-}\mathrm{var}},\left\lVert\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{1\text{-}\mathrm{var}}\leq V\end{subarray}}\left\lvert\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\texttt{k}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right\rvert\geq\epsilon\right]\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}P_{k}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{m}\right)

(188)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(g)}}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle 2^{\frac{1}{d+1}}8C^{\prime}_{d,\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(\frac{2^{k}{\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}}^{k-1}m\alpha_{m,k}}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{d}{d+1}}\exp\left(-\frac{\tilde{d}}{2(d+1)}\cdot\frac{\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2^{k}\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2(k-1)}m\alpha_{m,k}}\right)^{2}}{S^{2}+R\frac{\epsilon}{2^{k}\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2(k-1)}m\alpha_{m,k}}}\right)

(189)

\displaystyle+2^{\frac{1}{d+1}}8C^{\prime}_{d,\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=2}^{m}\left(\frac{2^{k}m\alpha_{m,k}}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{d}{(d+1)k}}\exp\left(-\frac{\tilde{d}}{2(d+1)}\cdot\frac{\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2^{k}m\alpha_{m,k}}\right)^{\frac{2}{k}}}{S^{2}+R\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2^{k}m\alpha_{m,k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}}\right)

(190)

\displaystyle+d\sum_{k=2}^{M}\exp\left(-\frac{\tilde{d}(k-1)}{2}\frac{\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2^{k}d^{k}m\alpha_{m,k}}\right)^{\frac{2}{k}}}{S^{2}+R\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2^{k}d^{k}m\alpha_{m,k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}}\right)

(191)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(h)}}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle 2^{\frac{1}{d+1}}8C^{\prime}_{d,\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(\frac{2^{k}\left(\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}\vee d\right)^{k}m\alpha_{m,k}}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{d}{d+1}}\exp\left(-\frac{\tilde{d}}{2(d+1)}\cdot\frac{\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2^{k}\left(\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}\vee d\right)^{k}m\alpha_{m,k}}\right)^{2}}{S^{2}+R\frac{\epsilon}{2^{k}\left(\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}\vee d\right)^{k}m\alpha_{m,k}}}\right)

(192)

\displaystyle+2^{\frac{1}{d+1}}8C^{\prime}_{d,\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(\frac{2^{k}\left(\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}\vee d\right)^{k}m\alpha_{m,k}}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{d}{(d+1)k}}\exp\left(-\frac{\tilde{d}}{2(d+1)}\cdot\frac{\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2^{k}\left(\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}\vee d\right)^{k}m\alpha_{m,k}}\right)^{\frac{2}{k}}}{S^{2}+R\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2^{k}\left(\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}\vee d\right)^{k}m\alpha_{m,k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}}\right)

(193)

\displaystyle+d\sum_{k=1}^{m}\exp\left(-\frac{\tilde{d}}{2(d+1)}\frac{\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2^{k}\left(\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}\vee d\right)^{k}m\alpha_{m,k}}\right)^{\frac{2}{k}}}{S^{2}+R\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2^{k}\left(\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}\vee d\right)^{k}m\alpha_{m,k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}}\right)

(194)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(f)}}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle 2^{\frac{1}{d+1}}8C^{\prime}_{d,\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(\frac{\beta_{d,m,V}}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{d}{d+1}}\exp\left(-\frac{\tilde{d}}{2(d+1)}\frac{\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\beta_{d,m,V}}\right)^{2}}{S^{2}+R\frac{\epsilon}{\beta{d,m,V}}}\right)

(195)

\displaystyle+2^{\frac{1}{d+1}}8C^{\prime}_{d,\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(\frac{\beta_{d,m,V}}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{d}{(d+1)k}}\exp\left(-\frac{\tilde{d}}{2(d+1)}\frac{\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\beta_{d,m,V}}\right)^{\frac{2}{k}}}{S^{2}+R\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\beta_{d,m,V}}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}}\right)

(196)

\displaystyle+d\sum_{k=1}^{m}\exp\left(-\frac{\tilde{d}}{2(d+1)}\frac{\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\beta_{d,m,V}}\right)^{\frac{2}{k}}}{S^{2}+R\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\beta_{d,m,V}}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}}\right),

(197)

where (g) follows from rearranging the expressions from (f), while (h) from unifying the coefficients and that
1\leq d\leq\max(\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2},d)
and
f(x)=x^{2}/(a+bx)
is monotonically increasing in
x
on the positive half-line for
a,b>0
, while (f) from
\alpha_{m,k}=V^{2m}L^{2(m-k)}/((k-1)!)^{2}\leq(VL)^{2m}
, using that
f(x)
is increasing, and defining
\beta_{d,m,V}\coloneqq m\left(2V^{2}(L^{2}\vee 1)(\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}\vee d)\right)^{m}
.

Conclusion.

Finally, we split the bound into two cases: the first case is if the error is big, i.e.
\epsilon\geq\beta_{d,m,V}=m\left(2V^{2}(L^{2}\vee 1)(\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}\vee d)\right)^{m}
, when we decrease all the exponents to
\frac{1}{m}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\seq\\
\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{1\text{-}\mathrm{var}},\left\lVert\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{1\text{-}\mathrm{var}}\leq V\end{subarray}}\left\lvert\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\texttt{k}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right\rvert\geq\epsilon\right]

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lvert\tilde{\texttt{k}}^{\texttt{TRP}}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right\rvert\geq\epsilon\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lvert\tilde{\texttt{k}}^{\texttt{TRP}}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right\rvert\geq\epsilon\,\middle|\,\mathbf{W}\right]\right]

(240)

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{\tilde{d}_{\texttt{TRP}}^{\frac{1}{2m}}\tilde{d}\epsilon^{\frac{1}{m}}}{2\sqrt{2}e\beta_{m}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\frac{1}{2m}}\prod_{p=1}^{m}\left\lVert\mathbf{W}^{(p)}\right\rVert^{\frac{2}{m}}_{2}}\right)\right]

(241)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(k)}}}{{\leq}}2\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda\tilde{d}_{\texttt{TRP}}^{\frac{1}{2m}}\epsilon^{\frac{1}{m}}}{2\sqrt{2}e\beta_{m}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\frac{1}{2m}}}\frac{\tilde{d}}{\lambda\prod_{p=1}^{m}\left\lVert\mathbf{W}^{(p)}\right\rVert^{\frac{2}{m}}_{2}}\right)\right]

(242)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(l)}}}{{\leq}}2\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-2\left(\frac{\lambda\tilde{d}_{\texttt{TRP}}^{\frac{1}{2m}}\epsilon^{\frac{1}{m}}}{2\sqrt{2}e\beta_{m}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\frac{1}{2m}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{\lambda\prod_{p=1}^{m}\left\lVert\mathbf{W}^{(p)}\right\rVert^{\frac{2}{m}}_{2}}{\tilde{d}}\right)\right]

(243)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(m)}}}{{\leq}}2\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}\lambda\tilde{d}_{\texttt{TRP}}^{\frac{1}{2m}}\epsilon^{\frac{1}{m}}}{e\beta_{m}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\frac{1}{2m}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{\lambda\sum_{p=1}^{m}\left\lVert\mathbf{W}^{(p)}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}}{m\tilde{d}}\right)\right],

(244)

where (k) follows form multiplying and dividing with a
\lambda>0
, (l) from applying Lemma
14
with
p=\frac{1}{2}
and
q=1
, and (m) from the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality.

Bounding the MGF of
{w_{i,j}^{(p)}}^{2}
for
p\in[m],i\in[d],j\in[\tilde{d}]
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda{w^{(p)}_{i,j}}^{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(n)}}}{{\leq}}\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{E}\left[{w_{i,j}^{(p)}}^{k}\right]\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(o)}}}{{\leq}}1+\lambda S+\frac{\lambda^{2}S^{2}}{2}\sum_{k\geq 0}(\lambda R)^{k}

(245)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(p)}}}{{=}}1+\lambda S+\frac{\lambda^{2}S^{2}}{2}\frac{1}{1-\lambda R}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(q)}}}{{=}}1+\frac{S}{2R}+\frac{S^{2}}{4R^{2}},

(246)

where (n) is the Taylor expansion, (o) is the condition (
217
) and applying Jensen inequality to the degree-
1
term, (p) is the geometric series for
\lambda<\frac{1}{R}
, and (q) is choosing
\lambda=\frac{1}{2R}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\lambda\sum_{p=1}^{m}\left\lVert\mathbf{W}^{(p)}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}}{m\tilde{d}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\lambda\sum_{p=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{d}}{w_{i,j}^{(p)}}^{2}}{m\tilde{d}}\right)\right]

(247)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(r)}}}{{\leq}}\mathbb{E}^{1/(m\tilde{d})}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{p=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{d}}{w_{i,j}^{(p)}}^{2}\right)\right]

(248)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(s)}}}{{\leq}}\left(1+\frac{S}{2R}+\frac{S^{2}}{4R^{2}}\right)^{d},

(249)

where (r) is due to the Jensen inequality (Lemma
11
), and (s) follows from the independence of the
w_{i,j}^{(p)}
’s for
p\in[m],i\in[d],j\in[\tilde{d}]
.
Finally, plugging this into (
244
), we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lvert\tilde{\texttt{k}}^{\texttt{TRP}}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\tilde{\texttt{k}}_{\texttt{Sig}_{m}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right\rvert\geq\epsilon\right]\leq 2\left(1+\frac{S}{2R}+\frac{S^{2}}{4R^{2}}\right)^{d}\exp\left(-\left(\frac{\tilde{d}_{\texttt{TRP}}^{\frac{1}{2m}}\epsilon^{\frac{1}{m}}}{\sqrt{2}eR\beta_{m}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\frac{1}{2m}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right).

(250)

Finally, note that
\beta_{m}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\frac{1}{2m}}=\left(\frac{3^{m}+1}{(m!)^{4}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2m}}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{{1\text{-}\mathrm{var}}}\left\lVert\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{{1\text{-}\mathrm{var}}}\leq\frac{2e^{2}}{m^{2}}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{{1\text{-}\mathrm{var}}}\left\lVert\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{{1\text{-}\mathrm{var}}}
, since

for

, and
\frac{1}{m!}\leq\left(\frac{e}{m}\right)^{m}

Algorithms

We adopt the following notation for describing vectorized algorithms from
[
40
,
85
]
. For arrays,
1
-based indexing is used. Let
A
and
B
be
k
-fold arrays with shape
(n_{1}\times\dots\times n_{k})
, and let
i_{j}\in[n_{j}]
for
j\in[k]
. We define the following array operations:

(i)

The cumulative sum along axis
j
:

\displaystyle A[\dots,:,\boxplus,:,\dots][\dots,i_{j-1},i_{j},,i_{j+1}\dots]:=\sum_{\kappa=1}^{i_{j}}A[\dots,i_{j-1},\kappa,i_{j+1},\dots].

(251)

(ii)

The slice-wise sum along axis
j
:

\displaystyle A[\dots,:,\Sigma,:,\dots][\dots,i_{j-1},i_{j+1},\dots]:=\sum_{\kappa=1}^{n_{j}}A[\dots,i_{j-1},\kappa,i_{j+1},\dots].

(252)

(iii)

The shift along axis
j
by
+m
for
m\in\mathbb{Z}+
:

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle[\dots,:,+m,:,\dots][\dots,i_{j-1},i_{j},i_{j+1},\dots]

(253)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}A[\dots,i_{j-1},i_{j}-m,i_{j+1},\dots],&\text{if}\hskip 5.0pti_{j}>m\\
0&\text{if}\hskip 5.0pti_{j}\leq m.\end{array}\right.

(iv)

The Hadamard product of arrays
A
and
B
:

\displaystyle(A\odot B)[i_{1},\dots,i_{k}]:=A[i_{1},\dots,i_{k}]B[i_{1},\dots,i_{k}].

(256)

(v)

Now, if
A
has shape
(n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{j}\times\cdots\times n_{k})
and
B
has shape
(n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{j}^{\prime}\times\cdots\times n_{k})
, then their (batch) outer product along axis
j
is defined for
i_{j}\in[n_{j}n_{j}^{\prime}]
as

\displaystyle(A\boxtimes_{j}B)[i_{1},\dots,i_{j},\dots,i_{k}]:=A[i_{1},\cdots,\lceil i_{j}/n_{j}^{\prime}\rceil,\cdots i_{k}]B[i_{1},\dots,i_{j}\bmod n_{j}\dots,i_{k}],

(257)

where
\lceil\cdot\rceil:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{Z}
refers to the ceiling operation, and
\left(\cdot\bmod n\right):\mathbb{Z}\to[n]
to the modulo
n
operation that maps onto
[n]
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
