Multilinear POD-DEIM model reduction for 2D and 3D semilinear systems of differential equations Thanks: The author is a member of Indam-GNCS, which support is gratefully acknowledged.


Abstract
. We are interested in the numerical solution of coupled semilinear partial differential equations (PDEs) in two and three dimensions. Under certain assumptions on the domain, we take advantage of the Kronecker structure arising in standard space discretizations of the differential operators and illustrate how the resulting system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) can be treated directly in matrix or tensor form. Moreover, in the framework of the proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) and the discrete empirical interpolation method (DEIM) we derive a two- and three-sided model order reduction strategy that is applied directly to the ODE system in matrix and tensor form respectively. We discuss how to integrate the reduced order model and, in particular, how to solve the tensor-valued linear system arising at each timestep of a semi-implicit time discretization scheme. We illustrate the efficiency of the proposed method through a comparison to existing techniques on classical benchmark problems such as the two- and three-dimensional Burgers equation.

Introduction

We are interested in the computationally efficient numerical solution of systems of semilinear partial differential equations (PDEs) of the form

\begin{cases}\dot{u}_{1}&=\mathcal{L}_{1}(u_{1})+f_{1}(\nabla u_{1},u_{1},\ldots,u_{g},t)\\

The success of the reduced order modelling is due to the fact that the solution trajectories are commonly attracted to low-dimensional manifolds

8

A shortcoming of the existing procedures, however, is the massive computational and storage demand in the offline phase. Even in the online phase, several vectors of length
N

To this end, we illustrate that under certain hypotheses on the operators and the discretization basis in particular domains,

In addition to a better structural interpretation of the discrete quantities, this can also lead to reduced memory requirements and computational costs. We discuss how to integrate the system in array form, and particularly also how to solve the tensor-structured linear system arising from the semi-implicit time integration when
d=3

Furthermore, inspired by
[

The presentation in this paper is, however, more general as it extends this idea to the higher dimensional multilinear array setting, including first order nonlinearities. Furthermore, a more in-depth experimental analysis of the two-dimensional case is presented than in
[
28

3.
Review of POD-DEIM

In this section we review the standard POD-DEIM method and its application to the
dynamical system
2
, with
g=1
and a nonlinear function independent of the gradient. We aim to reduce the dimension and complexity of a system of ODEs of the form

\dot{\bf u}(t)={\bf L}{\bf u}(t)+{\bf f}({\bf u},t),\qquad{\bf u}(0)={\bf u}_{0},

(7)

with
{\bf u}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Commonly POD is used to reduce the dimension of the state space by projecting onto a subspace of dimension
k\ll N
. In particular, given a set of
n_{s}
trajectories (commonly known as snapshots) of the solution
\{{\bf u}(t_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n_{s}}
, the POD basis is determined as the best rank
k
approximation in the 2-norm of the space of snapshots. In particular, for

{\bf S}=[{\bf u}(t_{1}),\ldots,{\bf u}(t_{n_{s}})]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times n_{s}},

the POD basis
\{{\bf v}_{1},\ldots,{\bf v}_{k}\}
is determined as the first
k
dominant left singular vectors of
{\bf S}
. Therefore, if
{\bf V}=[{\bf v}_{1},\ldots,{\bf v}_{k}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times k}
, then we determine an approximation to
{\bf u}(t)
as
{\bf u}(t)\approx{\bf V}\hat{\bf u}(t)
, where
\hat{\bf u}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is determined as the solution of the reduced problem

\dot{\hat{\bf u}}(t)=\widehat{\bf L}\hat{\bf u}(t)+\widehat{\bf f}(\hat{\bf u},t),\qquad\hat{\bf u}(0)={\bf V}^{\top}{\bf u}_{0},

(8)

with
\widehat{\bf L}={\bf V}^{\top}{\bf L}{\bf V}
and
\widehat{\bf f}(\hat{\bf u},t)={\bf V}^{\top}{\bf f}({\bf V}\hat{\bf u},t)
. By definition,
\widehat{\bf f}(\hat{\bf u},t)
first needs to be evaluated in full dimension, that is at the entries of
{\bf V}\hat{\bf u}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, before projection onto the low-dimensional subspace. Hence, the overall cost of evaluating
8
still depends on the full dimension
N
. One way to treat this bottleneck is through DEIM
[
8
]
.

DEIM is used to interpolate a nonlinear function on the columns of an empirical basis. In particular if we consider a set of snapshots of the nonlinear function
\{{\bf f}({\bf u},t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n_{s}}
, then the DEIM basis
\{{\bf\phi}_{1},\ldots,{\bf\phi}_{p}\}
is determined as the first
p
left singular vectors of the matrix

{\bf N}=[{\bf f}({\bf u},t_{i}),\ldots,{\bf f}({\bf u},t_{n_{s}})]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times n_{s}}.

Then, if we set
{\bf\Phi}=[{\bf\phi}_{1},\ldots,{\bf\phi}_{p}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p}
and consider
{\bf P}=[{\bf e}_{\rho_{1}},\ldots,{\bf e}_{\rho_{p}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p}
as a subset of columns of the
N\times N
identity matrix, then the DEIM approximation of the nonlinear function is written as

{\bf f}({\bf u},t)\approx{\bf\Phi}\left({\bf P}^{\top}{\bf\Phi}\right)^{-1}{\bf P}^{\top}{\bf f}({\bf u},t).

(9)

The interpolation indices

8
]
, or through the pivoted

decomposition of

[
14
]

is evaluated elementwise at the entries of
{\bf u}

To complete the reduction of

(10)

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}\times\cdots\times k_{d}}

(k_{m}\ll n)
satisfies the low-dimensional equation

\dot{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}}=\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}})+\widehat{\mathcal{F}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}},t),\qquad\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}(0)=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{0},

(12)

where

\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}):=\sum_{m=1}^{d}\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}\times_{m}\widehat{\bf A}_{m},\quad\widehat{\bf A}_{m}={\bf V}_{m}^{\top}{\bf A}_{m}{\bf V}_{m},\quad\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}(0)=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{0}\bigtimes_{m=1}^{d}{\bf V}_{m}^{\top}

(13)

and

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}},t)=\mathcal{F}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}(t),t)\bigtimes_{m=1}^{d}{\bf V}_{m}^{\top}.

(14)

For the time discretization of
12
, several alternatives can be considered, however it is well known that these type of semilinear equations are typically characterized by a stiff linear term and a nonstiff nonlinear term; see e.g.,
[
49
]
. That is, explicit methods will require unrealistically small timesteps to ensure stability in the linear term, whereas fully implicit schemes require the application of an expensive iterative nonlinear solver at each timestep. Instead, a good compromise is reached through semi-implicit (also called implicit-explicit) schemes, where the linear term is treated implicitly and the nonlinear term explicitly
[
24
, chapter IV.3]
. To this end, we consider a second order implicit-explicit scheme, also known as IMEX 2–SBDF; see e.g.,
[
12
,
2
]
. Therefore, if
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}^{(j)}
is an approximation of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}(t_{j})
, then the linear system

(3\,\widehat{\mathcal{I}}-2\Delta t\widehat{\mathcal{A}})(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}^{(j)})=\widehat{\mathcal{G}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}^{(j-1)},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}^{(j-2)})

(15)

needs to be solved for each
t_{j}
, where

\widehat{\mathcal{G}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}^{(j-1)},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}^{(j-2)})=4\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}^{({j-1})}-\,\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}^{({j-2})}+2\Delta t\left(2\widehat{\mathcal{F}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}^{({j-1})},t_{j-1})-\widehat{\mathcal{F}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}^{({j-2})},t_{j-2})\right),

and
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}:\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}\times\cdots\times k_{d}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}\times\cdots\times k_{d}}
is the identity operator in the reduced dimension. To initiate the procedure,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}^{({1})}
can be determined by a semi-implicit Euler scheme from the known array
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}^{({0})}
. In what follows we discuss how
15
is solved for
d=2,3
.

4.1.
The solution of the linear system
15

The solution of
15
is not trivial, especially when
d=3
, given that the matrices
\widehat{\bf A}_{m}
, for
m=1,\ldots,d
are necessarily dense due to the projection. When
d=2
, it holds that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}^{(j)}=\widehat{\bf U}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}\times k_{2}}
, and
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
is a Sylvester operator
[
47
]
, so that
15
is equivalent to the Sylvester equation (see e.g.,
[
12
]
)

(3{\bf I}_{k_{1}}-2\Delta t\widehat{\bf A}_{1})\widehat{\bf U}^{(j)}+\widehat{\bf U}^{(j)}(-2\Delta t\widehat{\bf A}_{2}^{\top})=\widehat{\mathcal{G}}(\widehat{\bf U}^{(j-1)},\widehat{\bf U}^{(j-2)}).

Details on how to solve the Sylvester equation can be found in
[
47
]
. In our experiments we make use of the built-in MATLAB function
lyap
.

For the case
d=3
, a direct method designed specifically for dense third order tensor linear systems has recently been introduced in
[
46
]
for tensors with a rank-one right hand side. Here we illustrate how this method can be applied to solve the linear system
15
, accounting for a right hand side with rank greater than one. To ease the readability, we drop the superscript
(j)
for the description of the inner solver, when it is clear from the context.

By definition, when
d=3
, the left hand side of
15
can be vectorized as

\left({\bf I}_{k_{3}}\otimes 3{\bf I}_{k_{2}}\otimes{\bf I}_{k_{1}}-{\bf I}_{k_{3}}\otimes{\bf I}_{k_{2}}\otimes 2\Delta t\widehat{\bf A}_{1}-{\bf I}_{k_{3}}\otimes 2\Delta t\widehat{\bf A}_{2}\otimes{\bf I}_{k_{1}}-2\Delta t\widehat{\bf A}_{3}\otimes{\bf I}_{k_{2}}\otimes{\bf I}_{k_{1}}\right)\mbox{vec}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}\right).

Therefore, if we let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{{{X}}}}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{(3)}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}k_{2}\times k_{3}}
, then by the use of property
4
,
15
can be recast into the Sylvester equation

\left(3\,{\bf I}_{k_{2}}\otimes{\bf I}_{k_{1}}-{\bf I}_{k_{2}}\otimes 2\,\Delta t\widehat{\bf A}_{1}-2\,\Delta t\widehat{\bf A}_{2}\otimes{\bf I}_{k_{1}}\right)\widehat{\boldsymbol{{{X}}}}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{{{X}}}}\left(-2\,\Delta t\widehat{\bf A}_{3}^{\top}\right)=\widehat{\bf G}.

(16)

Here
\widehat{\bf G}=\widehat{\mathcal{G}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{{{X}}}}^{({j-1})},\widehat{\boldsymbol{{{X}}}}^{({j-2})})\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}k_{2}\times k_{3}}
.
Due to the large left dimension of this Sylvester equation, solving this directly is still not feasible. Instead, as is shown in
[
46
]
, it is possible to solve a sequence of much smaller Sylvester equations. To this end, let
\widehat{\bf A}_{3}^{\top}={\bf Q}{\bf R}{\bf Q}^{\top}
be the Schur decomposition of
\widehat{\bf A}_{3}^{\top}
. Then, if
\widehat{\boldsymbol{{{Y}}}}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{{{X}}}}{\bf Q}
, it holds that

\left(3\,{\bf I}_{k_{2}}\otimes{\bf I}_{k_{1}}-{\bf I}_{k_{2}}\otimes 2\,\Delta t\widehat{\bf A}_{1}-2\,\Delta t\widehat{\bf A}_{2}\otimes{\bf I}_{k_{1}}\right)\widehat{\boldsymbol{{{Y}}}}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{{{Y}}}}\left(-2\,\Delta t{\bf R}\right)=\widehat{\bf G}{\bf Q},

(17)

where
{\bf R}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k_{3}\times k_{3}}
is block upper triangular. Therefore, by following the ideas of
[
46
]
and repeatedly using the property
3
, the solution of
15
, unfolded in the third mode, is given by

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{(3)}=(\widehat{\bf Y}{\bf Q}^{\top})^{\top}={\bf Q}\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{vec}({\bf Z}_{1})^{\top};\ldots;\mbox{vec}({\bf Z}_{k_{3}})^{\top}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{3}\times k_{1}k_{2}}.

Here,
{\bf Z}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}\times k_{2}}

(h=1,2,\ldots,{k_{3}})
solves the smaller Sylvester equation

\left((3-2\,\Delta t{\bf R}_{h,h}){\bf I}_{k_{1}}-2\,\Delta t\widehat{\bf A}_{1}\right){\bf Z}_{h}+{\bf Z}_{h}\left(-2\,\Delta t\widehat{\bf A}_{2}^{\top}\right)={\bf H}_{h}+2\,\Delta t{\bf J}_{{h}-1},

where
\widehat{\bf G}{\bf Q}={\bf H}=\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{vec}({\bf H}_{1}),\ldots,\mbox{vec}({\bf H}_{k_{3}})\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}k_{2}\times{k_{3}}}
, and
{\bf J}_{h-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}\times k_{2}}
is the matricization of

\mbox{vec}({\bf J}_{h-1})=\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{vec}({\bf Z}_{1}),\ldots,\mbox{vec}({\bf Z}_{{h}-1})\end{pmatrix}{\bf R}_{h,1:h-1}.

(18)

In the special case where all coefficient matrices are symmetric and positive definite, the procedure can be even further accelerated; see
[
46
]
for further details. We refer to this inner solver as the
t3-sylv
solver.

Proposition 1
.

Let
{\bf Q}_{m}={\bf\Phi}_{m}({\bf P}_{m}^{\top}{\bf\Phi}_{m})^{-1}{\bf P}_{m}^{\top}
, and consider an arbitrary tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\cdots\times n}
, so that

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\bigtimes_{m=1}^{d}{\bf\Phi}_{m}({\bf P}_{m}^{\top}{\bf\Phi}_{m})^{-1}{\bf P}_{m}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\bigtimes_{m=1}^{d}{\bf Q}_{m}.

Then,

\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}}\|_{F}\leq{c_{1}}{c_{2}}\cdots c_{d}\,\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\bigtimes_{m=1}^{d}{\bf\Phi}_{m}{\bf\Phi}_{m}^{\top}\|_{F}

(21)

where
c_{m}=\left\|({\bf P}_{m}^{\top}{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{m})^{-1}\right\|_{2}
, for
m=1,\ldots,d
.

Proof.

Let
{\bf f}=\mbox{vec}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Then, by the properties of the Kronecker product

\begin{split}\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}}\|_{F}&=\|\mbox{vec}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}})-\mbox{vec}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}})\|_{2}=\|{\bf f}-({\bf Q}_{d}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\bf Q}_{1}){\bf f}\|_{2}\\
&=\left\|{\bf f}-({\mathbf{\Phi}}_{d}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{1})\left(({\bf P}_{d}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{1})^{\top}({\mathbf{\Phi}}_{d}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{1})\right)^{-1}({\bf P}_{d}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{1})^{\top}{\bf f}\right\|_{2}\end{split}

Therefore, by
[
8
, Lemma 3.2]
,

\displaystyle{\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}}\|_{F}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\left\|\left(({\bf P}_{d}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{1})^{\top}({\mathbf{\Phi}}_{d}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{1})\right)^{-1}\right\|_{2}\left\|{\bf f}-({\mathbf{\Phi}}_{d}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{1})({\mathbf{\Phi}}_{d}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{1})^{\top}{\bf f}\right\|_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\left\|({\bf P}_{d}^{\top}{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{d})^{-1}\right\|_{2}\cdots\left\|({\bf P}_{2}^{\top}{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{2})^{-1}\right\|_{2}\left\|({\bf P}_{1}^{\top}{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{1})^{-1}\right\|_{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\bigtimes_{m=1}^{d}{\bf\Phi}_{m}{\bf\Phi}_{m}^{\top}\right\|_{F}.}\,\,

∎

The accuracy of the
ho-deim
approximation therefore depends on the contraction coefficients
c_{m}
, which are minimized by the use of

; see, e.g.,
[
14
]
. Furthermore it depends on the accuracy of the

\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\bigtimes_{m=1}^{d}{\bf\Phi}_{m}{\bf\Phi}_{m}^{\top}\right\|_{F}
.

{\mathbf{V}}_{m,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k_{mi}}

i=1,2,\ldots,g

and
m=1,2,\ldots,d
, where
k_{mi}\ll n
. That is, we consider
d
basis matrices for each of the
g
equations in
6
. Approximations to each
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}(t)
, for
t\in[0,t_{f}]
, can then be written as

{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}(t)\approx\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}(t)=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}(t)\bigtimes_{m=1}^{d}{\bf V}_{m,i},\qquad i=1,2,\ldots,g.

The functions
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{1i}\times k_{2i}\times\cdots\times k_{di}}
are determined as an approximation to the solution of the reduced, coupled problem

\begin{cases}\dot{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}}_{1}&=\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{1}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{1})+\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{1}\left(\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{1}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{1}),\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{1},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{2},\cdots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{g},t\right)\\
&\vdots\\
\dot{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}}_{g}&=\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{g}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{g})+\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{g}\left(\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{g}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{g}),\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{1},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{2},\cdots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{g},t\right),\\
\end{cases}

(22)

where

\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}):=\sum_{m=1}^{d}\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}\times_{m}\widehat{\bf A}_{mi},\quad\widehat{\bf A}_{mi}={\bf V}_{m,i}^{\top}{\bf A}_{mi}{\bf V}_{m,i},\quad\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}(0)={\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i0}\bigtimes_{m=1}^{d}{\bf V}_{m,i}^{\top}

(23)

and

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{i}\left(\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{i}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}),\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{1},\cdots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{g},t\right)=\mathcal{F}_{i}\left(\mathcal{D}_{i}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}),\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{1},\cdots,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{g},t\right)\bigtimes_{m=1}^{d}{\bf V}_{m,i}^{\top}.

(24)

We use the
ho-pod
procedure from the previous section to determine the basis matrices. In particular, given the set of snapshot solutions
\{{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}(t_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n_{s}}
, the basis matrices
{\mathbf{V}}_{m,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k_{mi}}
,
m=1,2,\ldots,d
, are determined following the
ho-pod
procedure from section
4
, for each
i=1,2,\ldots,g
.

The reduced order model
22
, can also be integrated by means of the IMEX 2 - SBDF scheme for systems. Indeed, the
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{1}^{(j)},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{2}^{(j)},\ldots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{g}^{(j)}

approximations to
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{1}(t_{j}),\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{2}(t_{j}),\ldots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{g}(t_{j})
are determined by solving the linear systems

\begin{cases}(3\,\widehat{\mathcal{I}}-2\,\Delta t\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{1})(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{1}^{(j)})&=\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{1}\left(\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}^{(j-1)}\}_{i=1}^{g},\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}^{(j-2)}\}_{i=1}^{g}\right)\\
&\vdots\\
(3\,\widehat{\mathcal{I}}-2\,\Delta t\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{g})(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{g}^{(j)})&=\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{g}\left(\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}^{(j-1)}\}_{i=1}^{g},\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}^{(j-2)}\}_{i=1}^{g}\right),\\
\end{cases}

(25)

at each
t_{j}
, where

\begin{split}\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{i}\left(\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}^{(j-1)}\}_{i=1}^{g},\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}^{(j-2)}\}_{i=1}^{g}\right)=4\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}^{({j-1})}&+4\Delta t\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{i}\left(\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{i}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}^{(j-1)}),\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{1}^{(j-1)},\cdots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{g}^{(j-1)},t_{j-1}\right)\\
-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}^{({j-2})}&-2\Delta t\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{i}\left(\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{i}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}^{(j-2)}),\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{1}^{(j-2)},\cdots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{g}^{(j-2)},t_{j-2}\right).\end{split}

Each of the
g
linear systems in
25
can be solved via the procedures set forth in section
4.1
for
d=2,3
.

Adjacent to the setting discussed in section
4.2
, the nonlinear functions need to be interpolated to avoid evaluating the functions in full dimension at each timestep. To this end,
we approximate
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{i}\left(\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{i}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}),\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{1},\cdots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{g},t\right)
in the space spanned by the columns of the matrices
{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{m,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p_{mi}}
,
m=1,2,\ldots,d
, for each
i=1,2,\ldots g
, where
p_{mi}\ll n
. Given the selection matrices
{\bf P}_{m,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p_{mi}}
,
m=1,2,\ldots,d
, we obtain

\begin{split}\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{i}(\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{i}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}),\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{g},t)&\approx\mathcal{F}_{i}(\mathcal{D}_{i}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}),\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{g},t)\bigtimes_{m=1}^{d}{\bf V}_{m,i}^{\top}{\bf Q}_{m,i},\\
\end{split}

(26)

with the oblique projectors

{\bf Q}_{m,i}={\bf\Phi}_{m,i}({\bf P}_{m,i}^{\top}{\bf\Phi}_{m,i})^{-1}{\bf P}_{m,i}^{\top},\quad\mbox{for}\quad m=1,2,\ldots,d\quad\mbox{and}\quad i=1,2,\ldots,g.

The basis matrices
{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{m,i}
,
m=1,2,\ldots,d
are determined via the
ho-pod
procedure described in section
4
, given the set of nonlinear snapshots

\left\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\left(\mathcal{D}_{i}({\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i}(t_{j})),{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{1}(t_{j}),{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{2}(t_{j}),\cdots,{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{g}(t_{j}),t_{j}\right)\right\}_{j=1}^{n_{s}},

(27)

whereas the selection matrices
{\mathbf{P}}_{m,i}
are determined via
q-deim
with inputs
{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{m,i}^{\top}
respectively, for each
i=1,2,\ldots,g
. In this paper we assume that there is a componentwise relationship between the arrays
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{1}(t_{j}),{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{2}(t_{j}),\cdots,{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{g}(t_{j})
and the approximation to the gradient
\mathcal{D}_{i}({\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{i})
in the nonlinear function
\mathcal{F}_{i}
. Therefore, since the matrix
{\mathbf{P}}_{m,i}
is merely responsible for selecting rows in the respective modes, it holds that

6.
Numerical experiments

In this section we illustrate the efficiency of the discussed methods via benchmark problems from biology and engineering. For all problems, the accuracy of the reduced order model is tested through the average error measure

\bar{\mathcal{E}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}})=\frac{1}{n_{\mathfrak{t}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathfrak{t}}}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}^{(j)}\|_{F}}{\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}\|_{F}},

(28)

and the truncation of the singular values is done by monitoring the quality of the approximation in the Frobenius norm. That is, if
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{\kappa}
are the singular values of the matrix that needs to be truncated, then the new dimension
\nu\leq\kappa
is determined as

\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{i=\nu+1}^{\kappa}{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{\kappa}{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}<\tau.

(29)

We first illustrate the efficiency with two examples where
d=2
, after which we investigate the three-dimensional coupled Burgers equation.

(30)

where the functions

and

7.
Conclusion

In this paper we have illustrated that systems of the form
1
, with linear operators with separable coefficients, discretized by a tensor basis on certain domains, can be treated directly in array form. In this setting, we have extended the
pod-deim
model order reduction method to the multilinear setting and illustrated how it can be used to massively reduce the dimension and complexity of systems of ODEs in two and three spatial dimensions. Some very encouraging numerical experiments on difficult problems such as the 2D and 3D viscous Burgers equation, indicate a dramatic decrease in both CPU time and memory requirements in the offline phase for constructing the bases, especially when
d=3
.

Nevertheless, the dense Kronecker structure of the reduced order model obtained by the
ho-pod-deim
projection would incur unnecessary computational costs in the online phase when
d=3
. To this end we have shown how the novel
t3-sylv
linear system solver from
[
46
]
can exploit the structure of the reduced order model, resulting in a large decrease in computational time in the online phase as well.

Future work would entail an analysis of the number of snapshots required by
ho-pod-deim
in comparison to
pod-deim
. It could also be of interest to extend the
t3-sylv
solver to higher dimensions so that the
ho-pod-deim
strategy can be applied to PDEs with
d>3
. Furthermore, the extension to the parameter dependent setting can also be considered. This will result in an additional dimension that needs to be treated. Finally, the presented algorithm can certainly also benefit from a dynamic implementation as presented in the companion manuscript
[
28
]
.
