Creating Subgraphs in Semi-Random Hypergraph Games
Natalie Behague, Paweł Prałat, Andrzej Ruciński

Abstract
The semi-random hypergraph process is a natural generalisation of the semi-random graph process, which can be thought of as a one player game. For fixed r < s r<s , starting with an empty hypergraph on n n vertices, in each round a set of r r vertices U U is presented to the player independently and uniformly at random. The player then selects a set of s − r s-r vertices V V and adds the hyperedge U ∪ V U\cup V to the s s -uniform hypergraph. For a fixed (monotone) increasing graph property, the player’s objective is to force the graph to satisfy this property with high probability in as few rounds as possible. We focus on the case where the player’s objective is to construct a subgraph isomorphic to an arbitrary, fixed hypergraph H H . In the case r = 1 r=1 the threshold for the number of rounds required was already known in terms of the degeneracy of H H . In the case 2 ≤ r < s 2\leq r<s , we give upper and lower bounds on this threshold for general H H , and find further improved upper bounds for cliques in particular. We identify cases where the upper and lower bounds match. We also demonstrate that the lower bounds are not always tight by finding exact thresholds for various paths and cycles.

In this paper, we consider a hypergraph generalization of the
semi-random graph process
suggested by Peleg Michaeli (see
[

[

) and studied recently in
[

,

,

,

,

,

,

that can be viewed as a “one player game”. Such a generalization was first proposed in
[

]
and also studied in
[
16
]
.

The semi-random process on hypergraphs,
(G^{(r,s)}_{t})_{t}
, is defined as follows. Fix integers
r\geq 1
to be the number of randomly selected vertices per step, and
s>r
to be the uniformity of the hypergraph. The process starts from
G^{(r,s)}_{0}
, the empty hypergraph on the vertex set
[n]:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, where
n\geq s
(throughout, we often suppress the dependence on
n
). In each step
t\geq 1
, a set
U_{t}
of
r
vertices is chosen uniformly at random from
[n]
. Then, the player replies by selecting a set of
s-r
vertices
V_{t}
, and ultimately the edge
e_{t}:=U_{t}\cup V_{t}
is added to
G^{(r,s)}_{t-1}
to form
G^{(r,s)}_{t}
. In order for the process to be well defined, we allow parallel edges. For instance, they are necessary if an
r
-element set
U
has been chosen more than
\binom{n-r}{s-r}
times.

Note that the resulting hypergraph is
s
-uniform, or shortly an
s
-graph
. If
r=1
and
s=2
, then this is the semi-random graph process. Further, if we allowed the degenerate case
r=s
(that is, the player chooses
V_{t}=\emptyset
for all
t
), then
G_{t}^{(r,r)}=(U_{1},\dots,U_{t})
would be just a uniform random
r
-graph process with
t
edges selected with repetitions.

To avoid ambiguity in using the notions of uniform hypergraph and uniform distributions, we will use the synonym
equiprobable
for the latter.

Let us mention briefly some other variants of the semi-random process. In
[
15
]
, sharp thresholds were studied for a more general class of processes that includes the semi-random process. In
[
4
]
, a random spanning tree of
K_{n}
is presented, and the player keeps one of the edges. In
[
13
]
, vertices are presented by the process in a random permutation. In
[
17
]
, the process presents
k
random vertices, and to create an edge the player selects one of them, and freely chooses a second vertex.

The goal of the player is to build an
s
-graph
G_{t}^{(r,s)}
satisfying a given monotone property
\mathcal{P}
as quickly as possible. To make it more precise we define the notions of a strategy and a threshold.

A
strategy

\mathcal{S}
of the player consists, for each
n\geq s
, of a sequence of functions
(f_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
, where for each
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
V_{t}:=f_{t}(U_{1},V_{1},\ldots,U_{t-1},V_{t-1},U_{t})\in\binom{[n]}{s-r}
. Thus, the player’s response,
V_{t}
, is fully determined by
U_{1},V_{1},\ldots,U_{t-1},V_{t-1},U_{t}
, that is, by the history of the process up until step
t-1
, and by the random set
U_{t}
chosen at step
t
. Given
t:=t(n)
, let
G_{t}^{(r,s)}[\mathcal{S}]
be the sequence of semi-random (multi)-
s
-graphs obtained by following strategy
\mathcal{S}
for
t
rounds; we shorten
G_{t}^{(r,s)}[\mathcal{S}]
to
G_{t}^{(r,s)}
when clear.

Throughout the paper we write
a_{n}\gg b_{n}
if
b_{n}=o(a_{n})
, and say that an event holds
asymptotically almost surely
(
a.a.s.
) if it holds with probability tending to one as
n\to\infty
.
For a monotonically increasing property
\mathcal{P}
of
s
-graphs, we say that a function
\tau^{(r)}_{\mathcal{P}}(n)
is
a threshold for
\mathcal{P}
if the following two conditions hold:

(a)

there exists a strategy
\mathcal{S}
such that if
t:=t(n)\gg\tau^{(r)}_{\mathcal{P}}(n)
, then a.a.s.
G^{(r,s)}_{t}\in\mathcal{P},\;

(b)

for every strategy
\mathcal{S}
, if
t:=t(n)=o(\tau^{(r)}_{\mathcal{P}}(n))
, then a.a.s.
G^{(r,s)}_{t}\not\in\mathcal{P}
.

Observe that
\tau^{(r)}_{\mathcal{P}}(n)\geq\tau^{(r-1)}_{\mathcal{P}}(n)
for all
r\geq 2
. Indeed, one can couple the two games by always including one of the
r
random vertices chosen in the
G^{(r,s)}_{t}
process among the
s-(r-1)
vertices selected by the player in the
G^{(r-1,s)}_{t}
process.

In
[
16
]
it was shown for any
s\geq 2
and
r\in\{1,2\}
that for both,
\mathcal{P}
being the property of having a perfect matching and
\mathcal{P}
being the property of having a loose Hamilton cycle,
\tau^{(r)}_{\mathcal{P}}(n)=n
(in fact, the results are even sharper).

In this paper we focus on the problem of constructing a sub-
s
-graph of
G^{(r,s)}_{t}
isomorphic to an arbitrary,
fixed

s
-graph
H
.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{H}
be the property that
H\subseteq G^{(r,s)}_{t}
. We abbreviate
\tau^{(r)}_{\mathcal{P}_{H}}(n)
to
\tau^{(r)}(H,n)
and often suppress the dependence on
n
, writing simply
\tau^{(r)}(H)

It was proved by the authors and T. Marbach in
[
1
]
that for
r=1
, that is, when just a single vertex is selected randomly at each step, the threshold
\tau^{(1)}(H)
can be determined fully in terms of the degeneracy of

.
For a given
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, a hypergraph
H
is

if every sub-hypergraph
H^{\prime}\subseteq H
has minimum degree
\delta(H^{\prime})\leq d
. The
degeneracy

of

is the smallest value of

for which
H
is
d

d(H)=\max_{H^{\prime}\subseteq H}\delta(H^{\prime})
, where
\delta(H)
is the minimum vertex degree of a hypergraph

Theorem 1

(Behague, Marbach, Prałat, Ruciński
[
1
]
)
.

Let
s\geq 2
and
H
be a fixed
s
-uniform hypergraph of degeneracy
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then,
\tau^{(1)}(H)=n^{1-1/d}
.

Note that, in particular, for
s=2
and any tree
T
we have
d(T)=1
, and so
\tau^{(1)}(T)=1
. In fact, in this case one can easily show a stronger statement: there exists a strategy
\mathcal{S}
such that a.a.s.
T\subseteq G^{(1,2)}_{t}[\mathcal{S}]
for
t=|E(T)|
, as a.a.s. the first
t
random vertices
u_{1},\dots,u_{t}
, selected in the semi-random process
G^{(1,2)}_{t}
, are all distinct from each other, as well as, from a fixed vertex
u_{0}\in[n]
. On the other hand, for any (graph) cycle
C

d(C)=2
, yielding
\tau^{(1)}(C)=\sqrt{n}
by Theorem
1
.

A similar contrast takes place for
s>2
. A
tight cycle

C_{m}^{(s)}
is an
s
-graph with
m
vertices and
m
edges, whose vertices can be ordered cyclically so that the edges are formed by the consecutive
s
-element segments in this ordering.
(E.g., the set of triples

123,234,345,456,567,671,712
forms a copy of
C_{7}^{(3)}
on
[7]
.)
A
tight path

P_{m}^{(s)}
is an
s
-graph with
k=m+s-1
vertices and
m
edges, whose vertices can be ordered linearly so that the edges are formed by the consecutive
s
-element segments in this ordering. Alternatively, it can be obtained from
C_{m+s-1}^{(s)}
by removing
s-1

123,234,345,456,567
forms a copy of
P_{5}^{(3)}
on
[7]

We have
d(P_{m}^{(s)})=1
, so
\tau^{(1)}(P_{m}^{(s)})=1
, while
d(C_{m}^{(s)})=s

\tau^{(1)}(C_{m}^{(s)})=n^{1-1/s}

20

2
New results

Our understanding of semi-hypergraph processes with
r\geq 2
is far from complete. For property
\mathcal{P}_{H}
, we can only prove a general lower bound, show its optimality for certain classes of hypergraphs and its suboptimality for others. We defer the proofs of these results to later sections. Throughout, for a hypergraph
H
, we will be using notation
v_{H}=|V(H)|
and
e_{H}=|E(H)|
.

2.1
Lower bound

Our general lower bound on
\tau^{(r)}(H)
, proved in Section
3
, depends only on the number of vertices and edges of
H
so, in a sense, it is also quite generic. Surprisingly, it provides the right answer for a broad class of
s
-graphs.

Theorem 2
.

Let
k\geq s\geq r\geq 1
, and let
H
be an
s
-graph with
k
vertices and
m
edges. Then, for every strategy
\mathcal{S}
, if
t=o\left(n^{r-(k-s+r)/m}\right)
, then a.a.s.
G^{(r,s)}_{t}\not\in\mathcal{P}_{H}.

It follows that

\tau^{(r)}(H)\geq n^{r-(k-s+r)/m}.

Example 1
.

Let
H
be a 3-graph consisting of 5 vertices
a,b,c,d,e
and 5 edges made by all triples from
\{a,b,c,d\}
plus
\{c,d,e\}
(see Figure
1
). Then, with
r=2
,
s=3
, and
k=m=5
,
we get
\tau^{(2)}(H)\geq n^{6/5}
.

Figure 1:
The
3
-graph
H
described in Example
1
.

Of course, it might happen that the main “bottleneck” is not the original hypergraph
H
as a whole, but one of its sub-hypergraphs
H^{\prime}\subseteq H
. Trivially, creating
H
requires creating
H^{\prime}
in the first place, so we immediately obtain the following corollary. For
2\leq r\leq s
and an
s
-graph
H
with at least
s
vertices, define

f^{(r)}(H)=\frac{e_{H}}{v_{H}-s+r}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mu^{(r)}(H)=\max_{H^{\prime}\subseteq H,\ v_{H^{\prime}}\geq s}f^{(r)}(H^{\prime}).

Corollary 3
.

Let
1\leq r\leq s
and
H
be an
s
-graph with at least
s
vertices. Then,

\tau^{(r)}(H)\geq n^{r-1/\mu^{(r)}(H)}.

If
\mu^{(r)}(H)=f^{(r)}(H)
, then we call such an
H

r
-balanced
. This is, for instance, the case for the tight cycle
C_{m}^{s}
(see Appendix, Claim
20
). For
r
-balanced
H
, the bounds in Theorem
2
and Corollary
3
coincide. However, for non-
r
-balanced
H
Corollary
3
may give a significantly better lower bound on
\tau^{(r)}(H)
.

Example 2
.

Let
H
be as in Example
1
(see Figure
1
) and
H^{\prime}
be the clique
K_{4}^{(3)}
on vertices
a,b,c,d
. Then,
H^{\prime}\subseteq H
and
f^{(2)}(H^{\prime})=4/3>5/4=f^{(2)}(H)
. It is easy to see that
\mu^{(2)}(H)=4/3
and, in fact,
\tau^{(2)}(H)\geq n^{2-3/4}=n^{5/4}
.

Note that, by considering a single edge as
H^{\prime}
, we always have
\mu_{H}^{(r)}\geq 1/r
, and so the lower bound in Corollary
3
cannot be less than 1. However, for most hypergraphs
H
we have
\mu^{(r)}(H)>1/r
, giving us a nontrivial lower bound on
\tau^{(r)}(H)
.
Note also that in the special case when
r=s\geq 2
, we are looking at the random
s
-graph (with repeated edges) and the bound in Corollary
3
corresponds to the threshold for appearance of a copy of a given
s
-graph
H
(see
[
14
, Chapter 3]
for the case
r=s=2
or
[
6
]
for non-uniform hypergraphs, though neither model allows edge repetitions, as we do here).

Finally, let us mention that we included the case
r=1
, already covered by Theorem
1
, to emphasize the potential weakness of this general lower bound. Indeed, note that
\mu^{(1)}(H)>d(H)
unless
\mu^{(1)}(H)=d(H)=1
, so in most cases the bound in Corollary
3
is weaker than the optimal bound in Theorem
1
. However, for some
s
-graphs it is optimal. For example, when
H
is the tight path
P_{m}^{(s)}
, we have
\mu^{(1)}(H)=d(H)=1
and Theorem
1
yields
\tau^{(1)}(H)=1
(see the comment after Theorem
1
above).

2.2
Upper bounds which match lower bound

We now identify a class of
s
-graphs
H
for which we are able to establish an upper bound on
\tau^{(r)}(H)
which matches the lower bound in Theorem
2
.

For integers
1\leq c<s\leq k
, a
k
-vertex
s
-graph
S
is called a
c
-star
if each of its edges contains a fixed vertex set
C
of size
|C|=c
. The set
C
is then called the
center
of the star and the
(s-c)
-graph
S_{1}:=\{e\setminus C:\;e\in S\}
is the
flower
of the star
S
. A
c
-star is
full
when it has all
\binom{k-c}{s-c}
edges, that is, if its flower is the complete
(s-c)
-graph
K_{k-c}^{(s-c)}
. A full star will be denoted by
S_{k}^{(s,c)}
.

An
(s,c)
-starplus with
\lambda_{1}
rays and excess
\lambda_{2}
is defined as an
s
-graph obtained from a
c
-star
S
with
\lambda_{1}
edges by arbitrarily adding to it
\lambda_{2}
edges not containing
C
(but not adding any new vertices). For
c>1
, the additional edges may intersect the center
C
(but not contain it).
Call the
(s-c)
-subgraph
H_{1}:=\{e\setminus C:\;C\subseteq e\in H\}
, the
flower
of the starplus
H
, and the
s
-graph
H_{2}
consisting of the
\lambda_{2}
excess edges of
H
– the
cap
of
H
. (Note that
H_{1}=S_{1}
, the flower of
S
.)

Example 3
.

Let
V(S)=\{a,b,c,d,e,f\}
and
E(S)
consist of all 4-tuples containing
C:=\{a,b\}
and one pair from
\{c,d,e,f\}
except
\{e,f\}
(see Figure
2
). Then
S
is a
2
-star, though not full (as the edge
\{a,b,e,f\}
is missing and thus its flower
S_{1}=K_{4}-\{e,f\}
). By adding to
S
three edges:
\{c,d,e,f\},\{a,c,d,e\}
, and
\{b,c,e,f\}
, we obtain a
(4,2)
-starplus
H
with 5 rays and excess 3. Its flower is the graph
H_{1}=S_{1}=K_{4}-\{e,f\}
on vertex set
\{c,d,e,f\}
, while its cap
H_{2}
consists of the three 4-tuples we have added to
S
.

Figure 2:
The
(4,2)
-starplus
H
described in Example

, where the black solid edges are the edges of the
2
-star

and the red dashed edges are the excess edges.

For an
r
-graph
F
,
r\geq 2
, let us define its
density

g(F)
as
1/r
if
e_{F}=1
, and
\frac{e_{F}-1}{v_{F}-r}
if
e_{F}>1
. We call
F

edge-balanced
if all sub-
r
-graphs
F^{\prime}\subset F
with
e_{F^{\prime}}>0
satisfy
g(F^{\prime})\leq g(F)
.
For starpluses which are not too dense and whose flowers are edge-balanced, we can prove (see Section
4
) the following.

Theorem 4
.

For
r\geq 2
and
s>r
,
let
H
be an
(s,s-r)
-starplus on
k
vertices with
\lambda_{1}
rays and excess
\lambda_{2}
, such that

\frac{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}}{\lambda_{1}-1}\leq\frac{k-s+r}{k-s},

(1)

and whose flower
H_{1}
is edge-balanced.
Then, there exists a strategy
\mathcal{S}
such that, if

t\gg n^{r-\frac{k-s+r}{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}}},

then a.a.s.
G_{t}^{(r,s)}\in\mathcal{P}_{H}
.
Thus, combined with Theorem
2
,

\tau^{(r)}(H)=n^{r-\frac{k-s+r}{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}}}.

It follows that, in view of Corollary
3
, any
(s,s-r)
-starplus
H
satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
4
is
r
-balanced, a fact whose direct proof would be quite tedious (see Appendix, Proposition
18
, for a proof in the special case of full
(s,s-r)
-starplus defined prior to Corollary
5
below).

Note also that the assumptions of Theorem
4
do not impose any structural restrictions on the cap
H_{2}
. Therefore, once the flower
H_{1}
of an
(s-r)
-star

is edge-balanced and the parameters satisfy (
1
), we can take any
s
-graph with
\lambda_{2}
edges and
k
vertices (edge-disjoint from
S
) as a cap, obtaining a whole family of
(s,s-r)
-starpluses to which Theorem
4
applies.

Example 4
.

One such class of starpluses is defined in terms of tight cycles.
Every tight cycle is edge-balanced (see Appendix, Claim
21
).
Thus, every
(s,s-r)
-starplus
H
on
k

H_{1}=C_{k-s+r}^{(r)}
and whose cap
H_{2}
has no more than
\frac{r-1}{k-s}(k-s+r)
edges, satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
4
. In particular, for

the wheel

H=W_{k}^{(s,s-r)}
defined as an
(s,s-r)
-starplus
H
with
H_{1}=C_{k-s+r}^{(r)}
and
H_{2}=C_{k-s+r}^{(s)}
, and for
k\leq s+r-1
, we have
\tau^{(r)}(H)=n^{r-1/2}
(see Figure
3
for the wheel
W_{8}^{(5,1)}
which satisfies the above assumptions with

and

).

:
The wheel
W_{8}^{(5,1)}

Assumption (
1
) is quite restrictive, because its left-hand-side cannot be too large. It becomes more relaxed, though, when we enlarge
\lambda_{1}
. At the extreme,
\lambda_{1}
can be as large as
\binom{k-s+r}{r}
. This leads to the following notion.

For integers
1\leq c\leq s\leq k
, an
(s,c)
-starplus
H
with
\lambda_{1}
rays and surplus
\lambda_{2}
is called a
full
(s,c)
-starplus with excess
\lambda
if
H_{1}=K^{(s-c)}_{k-c}
, that is, the flower is a complete

-graph (and so
\lambda_{1}=\binom{k-c}{r}
), while
\lambda_{2}=\lambda
. Alternatively, an
(s,c)
-starplus with excess
\lambda
is an edge-disjoint union of a full

-star
S_{k}^{(s,c)}
and an

-graph
H_{2}
with
\lambda
edges and the same vertex set as the star. Note that, in this case,

is edge-balanced (see Appendix, Claim

immediately implies the following result.

Corollary 5
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
s>r
, and
let
H
be a full
(s,s-r)
-starplus on
k

\lambda\leq\frac{r\binom{k-s+r}{r}-(k-s+r)}{k-s}.

(2)

Then,

\tau^{(r)}(H)=n^{r-\frac{k-s+r}{\binom{k-s+r}{r}+\lambda}}.

Example 5
.

For
r=2
and

the upper bound on excess in (

) is

, so Corollary

applies in this case to all

-graphs whose cap has the same number of edges and vertices. One example is the 3-uniform clique

(3,1)
-starplus consisting of the full 1-star and the cap forming a copy of

; so,
\tau^{(2)}(K_{5}^{(3)})=n^{2-\frac{4}{10}}=n^{8/5}
.

Another example, this time for

, is presented in Figure
6

is the full
3
-uniform 1-star on 8 vertices topped with the Fano plane; thus,
\tau^{(2)}(H)=n^{7/4}.
Our last example is the full
(3,1)
-starplus on
k

is a tight cycle

\tau^{(r)}(H)=n^{2-\tfrac{2}{k}}.

As hinted at in Example

-starpluses with excess

, assumption (

\binom{k}{s}\leq\frac{k-s+r}{k-s}\left(\binom{k-s+r}{r}-1\right).

Thus, in particular, Corollary

, and cliques

At the other extreme, when

),

,

Finally, let us identify examples of hypergraphs

2.5
Probabilistic tools

Here we gather some elementary probabilistic facts and estimates to be used later throughout the proofs. We begin with a version of the second moment method, useful for so called
counting
random variables, where the variance is being expressed in terms of the second factorial moment. Let
Y
be a nonnegative, integer-valued random variable. Then, for every
\epsilon>0
, Chebyshev’s inequality gives

\mathbb{P}\left(|Y-\mathbb{E}Y|\geq\epsilon\mathbb{E}Y\right)\leq\frac{\mathrm{Var}(Y)}{\epsilon^{2}(\mathbb{E}Y)^{2}}=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}(Y(Y-1))}{(\mathbb{E}Y)^{2}}+\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}Y}-1\right).

(8)

Assuming that
\mathbb{E}Y\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, in order to show that the above probability tends to 0, it suffices to show that
\mathbb{E}(Y(Y-1))\sim(\mathbb{E}Y)^{2}
.

Next, we give an estimate of the probability that the random multi-
r
-graph
R_{t}^{(r)}:=\{U_{1},\dots,U_{t}\}
contains a fixed sub-multi-
r
-graph. More precisely, let
F
be a multi-
r
-graph with the vertex set
V(F)\subset[n]
,
h
vertices,
m
edges, and multiplicities
m_{1},\dots,m_{\binom{h}{r}}
(some of which may be equal to 0). We want to estimate
\mathbb{P}(F\subset R_{t}^{(r)})
.

Consider
first a small example. Let
r=2
and
T
be the triangle on a fixed vertex set
\{1,2,3\}
with the edge
\{1,2\}
doubled, that is, the multiplicities are
2,1,1
. If one insists that the times of hitting particular edges are fixed, say, at
1\leq t_{13}<t_{12}<t_{23}<t_{12}^{\prime}\leq t
, then the probability of actually creating
T
at these designated times is precisely

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{3}{\binom{n}{2}}\right)^{t_{13}-1}\times\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\times\left(1-\frac{2}{\binom{n}{2}}\right)^{t_{12}-t_{13}-1}\times\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\times

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{2}{\binom{n}{2}}\right)^{t_{23}-t_{12}-1}\times\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\times\left(1-\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\right)^{t_{12}^{\prime}-t_{23}-1}\times\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\sim\left(\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\right)^{4},

as long as
t=o(n^{2})
. The number of ways to select the four hitting times and assign them to the four edges, due to the exchangeability of
t_{12}
and
t_{12}^{\prime}
, is
\binom{t}{4}\times 4!/2
. Thus,

\mathbb{P}(T\subset R_{t}^{(2)})\sim\binom{t}{4}\frac{4!}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\right)^{4}\sim\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{t}{\binom{n}{2}}\right)^{4}.

Similarly, in the general case, setting
p=t/\binom{n}{r}
,

\mathbb{P}(F\subset R_{t}^{(r)})\sim\frac{p^{m}}{m_{1}!\cdots m_{\binom{h}{r}}!}.

(9)

The proof of Proposition
8
uses the following simple lemma.

Lemma 9
.

For all
\lfloor x/2\rfloor\leq q\leq r
, a.a.s. every
q
-element subset of
[n]
is contained in at most three sets
U_{i}
,

.

Proof.

Let
X
be the number of
q
-element sets contained in at least four sets
U_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,t
.
For a fixed
q
-element set
Q
, the probability that it is contained in at least four sets

is
O(t^{4}/n^{4q})
. Thus, as there are
\binom{n}{q}<n^{q}
such sets and

\mathbb{E}X=O(t^{4}/n^{3q})=\omega^{4}n^{4x/3-3q}=o(1)
.
∎

For the proof of Theorem

we will need the following lemma which is a straightforward generalization of Theorem 3.29 from
[

(the edge-disjoint case) to hypergraphs. Given integers

, a real

\Phi_{F}:=\Phi_{F}(n,p)=\min\{n^{v_{F^{\prime}}}p^{e_{F^{\prime}}}:\;F^{\prime}\subseteq F,\;e_{F^{\prime}}>0\}

of the largest number

Lemma 10

(Janson, Łuczak, Ruciński
[

)

For all integers

and every

However, in order to apply this lemma in our context, we need first to address two issues: (i) the appearance of repeated edges and (ii) the uniformity of the model – as opposed to the binomial model

we will use a consequence of an asymptotic model equivalence result from

Lemma 11

(Janson, Łuczak, Ruciński
[

For all integers

The issue of repeated edges can be resolved by taking an appropriate random subsequence of the process

Lemma 12
.

For all integers
r\geq 2
and every sequence
t:=t(n)=o(n^{r})
, there is a joint distribution of the random multi-
r
-graph
R_{t}^{(r)}
and the random equiprobable

G^{(r)}(n,t^{\prime})

t^{\prime}=t-t^{3/2}/n^{r/2}=t-o(t)

Proof.

The expected number of times a repetition occurs in

(that is, an edge is selected again) is at most

such times. This means that along with

one can a.a.s. generate its sub-process

(U_{j_{1}},\dots,U_{j_{t^{\prime}}})
with the “static” equiprobable

. Finally, note that when

, we have
t^{3/2}/n^{r/2}=o(t)
. ∎

Edge-balanced implies balanced

Recall that an
r

. Further, we call

for all sub-
r
-graphs
F^{\prime}
of

Claim 17
.

If an
r
-graph
F
is edge-balanced, then it is also balanced.

Proof.

Let
F
be an edge-balanced
r
-graph. Observe that
\delta(F)\geq 1
, since otherwise it would not be edge-balanced. Set

and

. Let

Our goal is to show that

(20)

Equation (

ve^{\prime}+(v+re)\leq v^{\prime}e+(v+re^{\prime})

as well as to

It follows from (

then (

) must hold. Now, (

) follows from (

\frac{e-1}{v-r}\geq\frac{1}{r}.

(24)

Finally, (

which is trivially true, as

It has been mentioned earlier that
s-r\leq\ell_{k}(r,s)\leq k-r
. However, for
\ell_{k}(2,3)
the upper bound can be sharpened under a mild assumption on
k
.
Indeed, dropping the ceiling,

\ell_{k}\leq k+\frac{5}{2}-\sqrt{6k+1/4}\leq k-a

(31)

for all
k\geq a(a+5)/6+1
. E.g.,
\ell_{k}\leq k-3
for
k\geq 5
, while
\ell_{k}\leq k-4
for
k\geq 7
.

It is not easy to compute
\ell_{k}(r,s)
in general. We have made an attempt at the next smallest case:
r=2
,
s=4
.
In this case (
28
) becomes

k-\ell-2\frac{4!}{f_{4}(k,\ell)}\left\{\binom{\ell}{3}(k-\ell-2)+\binom{\ell}{2}\left[\binom{k-\ell}{2}-1\right]\right\}\leq 0,

equivalently

k^{4}+8k\ell^{3}+20k\ell^{2}+26k\ell+23k^{2}+12\leq 3\ell^{4}+8k^{2}\ell+6k^{2}\ell^{2}+8k^{3}+4\ell^{3}+5\ell^{2}+20\ell,

which, after setting
x=k-\ell
becomes

4kx^{3}+8kx^{2}+21k^{2}+10kx+20x+12\leq 3x^{4}+20k^{2}x+5x^{2}+20k.

Assuming
k
is large and focusing on the leading terms,
4kx^{3}
on the left and
3x^{4}+20k^{2}x
on the right, it is easy to show that
x=O(\sqrt{k})
, and so
\ell_{k}(2,4)=k-\Omega(\sqrt{k})
. Indeed, first note that
x=O(k^{2/3})
, since otherwise
4kx^{3}-3x^{4}>x^{4}\gg k^{2}x
, a contradiction. So, assume that
x=\omega(k)\sqrt{k}
, where
\omega(k)\to\infty
, but
\omega(k)=O(k^{1/6})
. Now the left-hand-side is
\Theta(\omega(k)^{3}k^{5/2})
, while the right-hand-side is
\Theta(\omega(k)^{4}k^{2}+\omega(k)k^{5/2})
, a contradiction again.
