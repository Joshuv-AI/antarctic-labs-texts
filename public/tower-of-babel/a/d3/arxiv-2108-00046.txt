On the finite element approximation of a semicoercive Stokes variational inequality arising in glaciology
Gonzalo G. de Diego, Patrick E. Farrell, Ian J. Hewitt

Abstract
Stokes variational inequalities arise in the formulation of glaciological problems involving contact. We consider the problem of a two-dimensional marine ice sheet with a grounding line, although the analysis presented here is extendable to other contact problems in glaciology, such as that of subglacial cavitation. The analysis of this problem and its discretisation is complicated by the nonlinear rheology commonly used for modelling ice, the enforcement of a friction boundary condition given by a power law, and the presence of rigid modes in the velocity space, which render the variational inequality semicoercive. In this work, we consider a mixed formulation of this variational inequality involving a Lagrange multiplier and provide an analysis of its finite element approximation. Error estimates in the presence of rigid modes are obtained by means of a specially-built projection operator onto the subspace of rigid modes and a Korn-type inequality. These proofs rely on the fact that the subspace of rigid modes is at most one-dimensional. Numerical results are reported to validate the error estimates.

non-Newtonian Stokes, glaciology, variational inequality, semicoercive, convergence analysis

Introduction

We consider the problem of a marine ice sheet resting on a bedrock and sliding into the ocean, where it goes afloat. Such a configuration is found in Greenland and Antarctica, and the dynamics of the grounding line, the point where ice loses contact with the bedrock, is of crucial importance for predicting future sea level rise and comprehending large scale climate dynamics
[
44
,
37
,
35
,
21
]
. This contact problem is modelled by coupling a Stokes problem for the ice flow with a time-dependent advection equation for the free surface. At each instant in time, the Stokes equation must be solved with contact boundary conditions that allow the detachment of the ice from the bedrock. These contact conditions transform the instantaneous Stokes problem into a variational inequality. Similar contact conditions appear in a related problem of subglacial cavitation (see Remark
1
below), which is also of fundamental importance in glaciology
[
18
,
36
,
45
]
, and the results presented in this work are extendable to such problems
[
10
]
.

Numerous finite element simulations of these equations have been carried out
[
13
,
16
,
41
]
. However, to the best of our knowledge, no formal analysis of these problems and their approximation exist in the mathematical literature. Moreover, we believe that the discretisations used in these computations can be improved upon, by exploiting the structure of the variational inequality. Although the Stokes variational inequality is superficially similar to the elastic contact problem, which has been widely studied
[
33
,
27
]
, the Stokes problem in the context of marine ice sheets with a grounding line includes three substantial difficulties that must be addressed carefully: the presence of rigid body modes in the space of admissible velocities, the nonlinear rheological law used to model ice as a viscous fluid, and the nonlinearity of the friction boundary condition.

In this work we analyse the instantaneous Stokes variational inequality and its approximation. The presence of rigid body modes renders this problem semicoercive. Although semicoercive variational inequalities have been studied in the past
[
26
,
40
,
2
]
, existing analyses use purely indirect arguments which give very limited information on how different meshes and finite elements affect the discretisation. Here, we present a novel constructive approach based on the use of a specially designed projection operator onto the subspace of rigid modes that satisfies a Korn type inequality. Error estimates are obtained for the rigid component of the velocity error by exploiting the fact that the dimension of the subspace of rigid modes is at most one. The nonlinear rheology and friction boundary condition complicate the estimation of errors for the discrete problem. Here, we use the techniques from
[
4
,
29
]
to establish a convergence analysis.

We propose a mixed formulation of the Stokes variational inequality where a Lagrange multiplier is used to enforce the contact conditions. This formulation permits a structure-preserving discretisation that explicitly enforces a discrete version of the contact conditions, up to rounding errors. This allows for a precise distinction between regions where the ice detaches from the bed and those where it remains attached. This precision is extremely useful when coupling the Stokes variational inequality with the time-dependent advection equation for the free surface. Numerical results with this scheme in the context of subglacial cavitation can be found in
[
10
]
.

1.1
Outline of the paper

In Section
2
, the Stokes variational inequality and its mixed formulation are presented. We prove a Korn-type inequality involving a projection operator onto the subspace of rigid modes that will be used throughout the analysis, and we demonstrate that the mixed formulation is well posed. In Section
3
, we analyse a family of finite element approximations of the mixed problem and present error estimates in terms of best approximation results for the velocity, pressure and Lagrange multiplier. Finally, in Section
4
, a concrete finite element scheme involving quadratic elements for the velocity and piecewise constant elements for the pressure and the Lagrange multiplier is introduced. We then present error estimates for this scheme and we solve a problem with a manufactured solution to calculate convergence rates and compare these with our estimates.

Formulation of the problem

2.1
A model for ice flow

We consider a two-dimensional symmetrical marine ice sheet resting on a bedrock and sliding into the ocean. This is the most common configuration considered when studying marine ice sheets
[
38
,
37
,
13
]
and is generally used as a benchmark test case
[
34
]
. We denote by
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
the domain which represents one half of the ice sheet and we assume it to be connected and polygonal. The latter assumption is made to simplify the analysis, but we expect the essential results presented here to extend to domains with smooth enough boundaries. Ice is generally modelled as a viscous incompressible flow whose motion is described by the Stokes equation
[
19
]
:

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\left(2\eta(|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u}|)\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u}\right)+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f}

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega$},

(1a)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega$}.

(1b)

In the equations above,
\boldsymbol{u}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
represents the ice velocity,
p:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
the pressure and
\boldsymbol{f}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a prescribed body force, generally due to gravitational forces. The tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u}
is the symmetric part of the velocity gradient, that is,

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}\right).

The coefficient
\eta(|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u}|)
is the effective viscosity of ice, which relates the stress and strain rates. A power law, usually called Glen’s law
[
23
]
, is the most common choice of rheological law for ice:

\displaystyle\eta(|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u}|)=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{A}^{-1/n}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1-n}{2n}}.

(2)

Here,
|\cdot|
represents the Frobenius norm of a matrix: for
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
with components
B_{ij}
we have
|B|^{2}=\sum_{ij}B_{ij}
. The field
\mathcal{A}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
is a prescribed function for which
\mathrm{ess\,inf}\mathcal{A}>0
. The parameter
n
is constant and is usually set to
n=3
; for
n=1
we recover the standard linear Stokes flow. From now on, we simply write

\displaystyle\eta(|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u}|)=\frac{1}{2}\alpha|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u}|^{r-2},

(3)

where
\alpha=(1/2)^{(r-2)/2}\mathcal{A}^{1-r}
is in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and satisfies
\alpha\geq\alpha_{0}
a.e. on
\Omega
for some
\alpha_{0}>0
. Moreover,
r=1+1/n
is in
(1,2]
for
n\geq 1
. This expression for
\eta
reveals the
r
-Stokes nature of the problem when considered as a variational problem in the setting of Sobolev spaces.

2.2
Boundary conditions

For a given velocity and pressure field, we define the stress tensor
\sigma=\sigma(\boldsymbol{u},p)
by

\sigma=\alpha|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u}|^{r-2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u}-pI,

where
I:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
is the identity tensor field. Let
\boldsymbol{n}
denote the unit outward-pointing normal vector to the boundary
\partial\Omega
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}-\boldsymbol{n}\boldsymbol{n}^{\top}
the orthogonal projection onto the tangential component to the boundary. We define the normal and tangential stresses at the boundary as

\displaystyle\sigma_{nn}=(\sigma\boldsymbol{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{nt}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\sigma\boldsymbol{n}.

The boundary
\partial\Omega
is partitioned into three disjoint open sets
\Gamma_{s}
,
\Gamma_{b}
and
\Gamma_{d}
of positive measure, see Figure
1
. The subset
\Gamma_{s}
represents the part of the boundary in contact with the atmosphere and the ocean. Here we enforce

\displaystyle\sigma_{nn}=p_{s}\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{nt}=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{on $\Gamma_{s}$},

(4)

where
p_{s}:\Gamma_{s}\to\mathbb{R}
represents a prescribed surface traction force. On
\Gamma_{b}
the ice is in contact with the bedrock. Here, we enforce the contact conditions which allow the ice to detach from but not penetrate the bedrock. In particular, detachment can occur if the normal stress equals the subglacial water pressure, which is defined everywhere along a thin lubrication layer in between the ice and the bedrock. We also assume that the ice slides along the bedrock according to a power law. Then, the boundary conditions on
\Gamma_{b}
are given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\leq 0,\quad\sigma_{nn}\leq-p_{w}\quad\text{and}\quad\left(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\right)\left(\sigma_{nn}+p_{w}\right)=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{on $\Gamma_{b}$},

(5a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{nt}=-\tau|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{u}|^{r-2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\quad\text{on $\Gamma_{b}$},

(5b)

where
p_{w}:\Gamma_{b}\to\mathbb{R}
is the water pressure at the ice-bedrock interface and
\tau>0
a constant. A power-law boundary condition as in (
5b
) was first proposed by Weertman
[
43
]
and has since become a popular model for glacier sliding
[
25
,
19
]
.

Finally,
\Gamma_{d}
represents the ice divide of the ice sheet, which is essentially its symmetry axis. As such, it is a vertical surface on which we enforce the symmetry conditions

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{nt}=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{on $\Gamma_{d}$}.

(6)

2.3
The mixed formulation

We now present the mixed formulation whose analysis and approximation is the focus of this work. To do so, we first write (
1
) with boundary conditions (
4
)-(
6
) as a variational inequality. Then, we introduce the mixed formulation by defining a Lagrange multiplier which enforces a constraint that arises due to the contact boundary conditions (
5a
). In Appendix
A
we specify and prove the sense in which these different formulations are equivalent.

In order to build a weak formulation of (
1
) and (
4
)-(
6
), we must first define suitable function spaces in which to seek the velocity and the pressure. For
r^{\prime}=1/(r-1)
, we write

\displaystyle V=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{W}^{1,r}(\Omega):\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0\quad\text{on $\Gamma_{d}$}\right\},\quad Q=L^{r^{\prime}}(\Omega).

We denote by
\gamma_{n}:V\to L^{r}(\Gamma_{b})
the normal trace operator onto
\Gamma_{b}
. This operator is built by extending to
V
the operator
\boldsymbol{v}\mapsto\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}
on
\Gamma_{b}
, defined on smooth functions. The closed convex subset
K
of
V
is then defined by

\displaystyle K=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in V:\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{v}\leq 0\quad\text{a.e. on $\Gamma_{b}$}\right\}.

We also introduce the operators
A:V\to V^{\prime}
,
G:V\to V^{\prime}
and
B:Q\to V^{\prime}
, defined by

\displaystyle\langle A\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{V}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\alpha|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u}|^{r-2}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{v}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x,

(7)

\displaystyle\langle G\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{V}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{b}}\tau|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{u}|^{r-2}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{v}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s,

(8)

\displaystyle\langle Bq,\boldsymbol{v}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\right)q\,\mathrm{d}x.

(9)

Moreover, the action of the applied body and surface forces on the domain
\Omega
is expressed via the function
F\in V^{\prime}
, defined as

\displaystyle\langle F,\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{V}=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma_{s}}p_{s}\left(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s-\int_{\Gamma_{b}}p_{w}\left(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s.

(10)

In order for (
10
) to make sense, we require
\boldsymbol{f}\in L^{r^{\prime}}(\Omega)
,
p_{s}\in L^{r^{\prime}}(\Gamma_{s})
and
p_{w}\in L^{r^{\prime}}(\Gamma_{b})
. Then, (
1
) with boundary conditions (
4
)-(
6
) can be reformulated as the variational inequality: find
(\boldsymbol{u},p)\in K\times Q
such that

\displaystyle\langle A\boldsymbol{u}+G\boldsymbol{u}-Bp-F,\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{V}+\langle Bq,\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{V}\geq 0\quad\forall(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in K\times Q.

(11)

In the mixed formulation, the constraint
\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\leq 0
on
\Gamma_{b}
is enforced via a Lagrange multiplier. We denote the range of
\gamma_{n}
by
\Sigma
and equip this space with the
W^{1-1/r,r}(\Gamma_{b})
norm. We assume the geometry of
\Omega
and
\Gamma_{b}
to be sufficiently regular for this space to be a Banach space, see
[
33
, Section 5]
,
[
27
, Chapter III]
and
[
1
, Chapter 7]
for discussions on normal traces and trace spaces. The Lagrange multiplier is sought in the convex cone of multipliers

\Lambda=\left\{\mu\in\Sigma^{\prime}:\langle\mu,\zeta\rangle_{\Sigma}\geq 0\quad\text{$\forall\zeta\in\Sigma$ s.t. $\zeta\leq 0$}\right\}.

The equivalent mixed formulation of (
11
) is: find
(\boldsymbol{u},p,\lambda)\in V\times Q\times\Lambda
such that

\displaystyle\langle A\boldsymbol{u}+G\boldsymbol{u}-Bp-F,\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{V}-\langle\lambda,\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in V,

(12a)

\displaystyle\langle Bq,\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{V}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall q\in Q,

(12b)

\displaystyle\langle\mu-\lambda,\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall\mu\in\Lambda.

(12c)

The Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
essentially coincides with
\sigma_{nn}+p_{w}
on
\Gamma_{b}
. Indeed, if the solution to (
12
) is sufficiently smooth for integration by parts to hold, we arrive at
\lambda=\sigma_{nn}+p_{w}
on
\Gamma_{b}
. Moreover, the conditions
\lambda\in\Lambda
and (
12c
) are equivalent to

\displaystyle\langle\mu,\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{\Sigma}\geq 0\quad\forall\mu\in\Lambda,\quad\lambda\in\Lambda\quad\text{and}\quad\langle\lambda,\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{\Sigma}=0,

(13)

which is a weak representation of the contact boundary conditions (
5a
).

2.4
Well posedness of the mixed formulation

Questions on the existence and uniqueness of solutions of the mixed system (
12
) can be answered by studying an equivalent minimisation problem. This equivalence depends on the so-called inf-sup property holding for the operators
B
and
\gamma_{n}
. Let

\displaystyle V_{b}=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in V:\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{v}=0\quad\text{a.e.~on $\Gamma_{b}$}\right\}.

(14)

These inf-sup conditions can be stated as

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V_{b}}\frac{\langle Bq,\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{V}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{V}}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\left\lVert q\right\rVert_{Q}\quad\forall q\in Q,

(15)

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V}\frac{\langle\mu,\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{\Sigma}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{V}}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\quad\forall\mu\in\Sigma^{\prime}.

(16)

Condition (
15
) is proved in
[
31
, Lemma 3.2.7]
and (
16
) follows from the inverse mapping theorem because
\gamma_{n}
is surjective onto the Banach space
\Sigma
. We also define the space of divergence-free functions
\mathring{V}
and the convex set
\mathring{K}
as

\displaystyle\mathring{V}=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in V:\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=0\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathring{K}=\mathring{V}\cap K.

Then, (
12
) is equivalent to the minimisation of the functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{v})=\frac{1}{r}\int_{\Omega}\alpha|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{v}|^{r}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{r}\int_{\Gamma_{b}}\tau|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{v}|^{r}\,\mathrm{d}s-\langle F,\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{V}

(17)

over
\mathring{K}
, see Appendix
A
. The existence of minimisers of
\mathcal{J}
over
\mathring{K}
hinges on whether the set

\displaystyle R_{V}=\left\{\boldsymbol{r}\in V:\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{r}|^{r}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma_{b}}|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{r}|^{r}\,\mathrm{d}s=0\right\}

is equal to or larger than the trivial set
\{0\}
. As shown in
[
33
, Lemma 6.1]
, the kernel of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}
coincides with the set of rigid modes in
\Omega
, defined by

\displaystyle R=\left\{\boldsymbol{r}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega):\boldsymbol{r}(x,y)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a\\
b\end{array}\right)+\omega\left(\begin{array}[]{r}-y\\
x\end{array}\right),\quad(a,b,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\right\}.

Hence,
R_{V}
is the set of rigid modes
\boldsymbol{r}\in R
satisfying
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{r}=0
on
\Gamma_{b}
and
\boldsymbol{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
on
\Gamma_{d}
. For this reason, the dimension of
R_{V}
can be at most 1 whenever
\Gamma_{b}
is a flat surface perpendicular to
\Gamma_{d}
. In this case,
R_{V}
is given by purely vertical translations.

Remark 1
.

Although a flat bedrock may appear to be unrealistic, these are considered in many theoretical studies of marine ice sheets
[
38
,
34
,
41
]
. One-dimensional subspaces of rigid modes in

also arise in marine ice sheets which can slide freely (

) and in the subglacial cavity problem considered in

]
(whenever a horizontal velocity boundary condition is imposed at the top boundary). An analysis of these two problems and their discretisation can be completed using the techniques and steps presented in this paper.

We define the projection operator
\mathbb{P}:V\to R_{V}
by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{v})=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\int_{\Gamma_{b}}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\,\mathrm{d}s}{\int_{\Gamma_{b}}\boldsymbol{e}_{R}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\,\mathrm{d}s}\boldsymbol{e}_{R}&\text{if $\dim{R_{V}}=1$,}\\
0&\text{if $\dim{R_{V}}=0$,}\end{cases}

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{R}\in R_{V}
is a basis function that spans
R_{V}
when
\dim{R_{V}}=1
. We choose this projection operator because it satisfies
\mathbb{P}(K)\subset K
. The operator
\mathbb{Q}=\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{P}
then maps elements in
V
onto a closed subspace whose intersection with

is
\{0\}
. As a result, we have the following variation of Korn’s inequality:

Lemma 2
.

The inequality

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{Q}\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{V}\lesssim\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{L^{r}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{L^{r}(\Gamma_{b})}

holds uniformly for all

Proof.

Following the proof of

8

, we first notice that (

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{v}|^{r}\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{v}|^{r}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma_{b}}|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{v}|^{r}\,\mathrm{d}s\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\Ran{\mathbb{Q}}

(19)

. Since

Whenever

\displaystyle\delta\left\lVert\boldsymbol{r}\right\rVert_{V}\leq-\langle F,\boldsymbol{r}\rangle_{V}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{r}\in R_{V}\cap K,

\displaystyle\delta=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{r}\in R_{V}\cap K,\\
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{r}\right\rVert_{V}=1\end{subarray}}{-\langle F,\boldsymbol{r}\rangle_{V}}.

The importance of the compatibility condition (

[
8
]
. We may prove existence when
R_{V}\neq\{0\}
and (
20
) holds with the method used in
[
17
, 1.II]
. If a minimising sequence
(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})
in
\mathring{K}
contains a bounded subsequence, then we may extract a subsequence that converges weakly to a point
\boldsymbol{u}
. By the weak lower semicontinuity of
\mathcal{J}
, we can then show that
\boldsymbol{u}
minimises
\mathcal{J}
. We must therefore show that a minimising sequence
(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})
has a bounded subsequence. Assume it does not; we must then have that
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rVert_{V}\to\infty
. From inequality (
18
) we deduce that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{Q}\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rVert^{r}_{V}\lesssim\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})+\langle F,\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\rangle_{V}.

(24)

We define
\boldsymbol{w}_{n}=\boldsymbol{u}_{n}/\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rVert_{V}
and deduce from (
24
) that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{Q}\boldsymbol{w}_{n}\right\rVert^{r}_{V}\lesssim\frac{1}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rVert_{V}^{r}}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})+\frac{\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{V^{\ast}}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rVert^{r-1}_{V}}.

Hence,
\mathbb{Q}\boldsymbol{w}_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Also, since
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert_{V}=1
, we have that
\mathbb{P}\boldsymbol{w}_{n}
is bounded in
R_{V}
, so there is a subsequence, which we also denote by
(\boldsymbol{w}_{n})
, that converges to a
\boldsymbol{r}\in R_{V}
. In fact, since
\mathbb{Q}\boldsymbol{w}_{n}\to 0
, we have that
\boldsymbol{w}_{n}\to\boldsymbol{r}
and therefore
\boldsymbol{r}\in(\mathring{K}\cap R_{V})\setminus\{0\}
. We reach a contradiction when we write (
24
) as

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rVert_{V}^{r-1}\left\lVert\mathbb{Q}\boldsymbol{w}_{n}\right\rVert^{r}_{V}\lesssim\frac{1}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rVert_{V}}\left(\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})-\mathcal{J}(0)\right)+\langle F,\boldsymbol{w}_{n}\rangle_{V}.

and observe that the
\limsup
of the left-hand side is strictly positive, while the
\liminf
of the right-hand side is strictly negative due to the compatibility condition (
20
).

Regarding the uniqueness of solutions, if
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{2}
are two minimisers for
\mathcal{J}
in
\mathring{K}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\langle A\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-A\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\rangle_{V}+\langle G\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-G\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\rangle_{V}\leq 0.

Since the operator
A+G
is monotone, the inequality above must be an equality and using (
18
) we deduce that
\left\lVert\mathbb{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2})\right\rVert_{V}=0
. Therefore, if
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathring{K}
minimises
\mathcal{J}
, any other minimiser must be of the form
\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{r}
with
\boldsymbol{r}\in R_{V}
. If
R_{V}=\{0\}
, we see that any solution must be unique. On the other hand, if
R_{V}\neq\{0\}
, since
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{r})=\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u})+F(\boldsymbol{r})
, the function
\boldsymbol{r}\in R_{V}
must also satisfy
F(\boldsymbol{r})=0
. Moreover, whenever (
20
) holds, we have that
F(\boldsymbol{r})=0
if and only if
\boldsymbol{r}=0
because
\dim{R_{V}}=1
. As a result,
\boldsymbol{r}=0
and the solution is unique.

For the converse statement, assume
R_{V}\neq\{0\}
and let
(\boldsymbol{u},p,\lambda)
solve (
12
). Then, from (
12a
) we deduce that

\displaystyle\langle F,\boldsymbol{r}\rangle_{V}=-\langle\lambda,\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{r}\rangle_{\Sigma}\leq 0\quad\forall\boldsymbol{r}\in K\cap R_{V}.

To prove (
23
), we first note that (
12a
) and (
18
) lead to

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{Q}\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert_{V}^{r}\lesssim\langle A\boldsymbol{u}+G\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{V}=\langle F,\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{V}.

(25)

If
R_{V}=\{0\}
, we have that
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert_{V}^{r-1}\lesssim\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{V^{\ast}}
. If
R_{V}\neq\{0\}
and (
20
) holds, then
\langle F,\mathbb{P}\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{V}\leq 0
and we find that
\left\lVert\mathbb{Q}\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert_{V}^{r-1}\lesssim\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{V^{\ast}}
. By using the inf-sup conditions and Hölder’s inequality, we can establish the bounds

\displaystyle\left\lVert\lambda\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{\prime}}+\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{Q}\lesssim\left\lVert\mathbb{Q}\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert_{V}^{r-1}+\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{V^{\ast}}\lesssim\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{V^{\ast}}.

(26)

We then use (
21
) to show that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{P}\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert_{V}\lesssim-\langle F,\mathbb{P}\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{V}=\langle\lambda,\gamma_{n}(\mathbb{Q}\boldsymbol{u})\rangle_{\Sigma}\lesssim\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{V^{\ast}}^{r^{\prime}}.

(27)

We finally establish the bound (
23
) by putting together (
25
), (
26
), and (
27
), and noting that
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert_{V}\leq\left\lVert\mathbb{P}\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert_{V}+\left\lVert\mathbb{Q}\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert_{V}
.

3
Abstract discretisation

In this section we propose an abstract discretisation of the mixed system (
12
) built in terms of a collection of finite dimensional spaces satisfying certain key properties. We can then introduce a discrete system analogous to (
12
) and investigate the conditions under which we have a unique solution. Then, we prove Lemmas
5
,
7
, and
9
, which establish upper bounds for the errors of the discrete solutions.

3.1
The discrete mixed formulation

For each parameter
h>0
, let
V_{h}\subset V
,
Q_{h}\subset Q
and
\Sigma_{h}\subset L^{2}(\Gamma_{b})
be finite dimensional subspaces. We also assume that
R_{V}\subset V_{h}
to avoid the need of introducing discrete compatibility conditions. We define the discrete convex sets

\Lambda_{h}=\left\{\mu_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}:\mu_{h}\leq 0\text{ on $\Gamma_{b}$}\right\},

and

K_{h}=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h}:\langle\mu_{h},\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{\Sigma}\geq 0\quad\forall\mu_{h}\in\Lambda_{h}\right\}.

An immediate consequence of the definitions of
\Lambda_{h}
and
K_{h}
is that
\Lambda_{h}\subset\Lambda
but
K_{h}\not\subset K
unless
\gamma_{n}(V_{h})\subset\Sigma_{h}
. By the assumption
R_{V}\subset V_{h}
and the fact that
R_{V}
is given by purely vertical translations whenever
R_{V}\neq\{0\}
, we have that
K\cap R_{V}=K_{h}\cap R_{V}
.

The discrete analogue of the variational inequality (
11
) is: find
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h})\in K_{h}\times Q_{h}
such that

\displaystyle\langle A\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+G\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-Bp_{h}-F,\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rangle_{V}+\langle Bq_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rangle_{V}\geq 0\quad\forall(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})\in K_{h}\times Q_{h}.

(28)

This discrete variational inequality can be written as a mixed problem by introducing a Lagrange multiplier. This results in the discrete mixed formulation that is the counterpart of (
12
): find
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h},\lambda_{h})\in V_{h}\times Q_{h}\times\Lambda_{h}
such that

\displaystyle\langle A\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+G\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-Bp_{h}-F,\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{V}-\langle\lambda_{h},\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h},

(29a)

\displaystyle\langle Bq_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rangle_{V}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall q_{h}\in Q_{h},

(29b)

\displaystyle\langle\mu_{h}-\lambda_{h},\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rangle_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall\mu_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}.

(29c)

An advantage of using a mixed formulation at the discrete level is that we explicitly enforce a discrete version of the contact conditions (
5a
). Just as in (
13
), it is possible to show that the conditions
\lambda_{h}\in\Lambda_{h}
and (
29c
) are equivalent to

\displaystyle\langle\mu_{h},\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rangle_{\Sigma}\geq 0\quad\forall\mu_{h}\in\Lambda_{h},\quad\lambda_{h}\in\Lambda_{h}\quad\text{and}\quad\langle\lambda_{h},\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rangle_{\Sigma}=0.

(30)

In order to state a minimisation problem equivalent to (
29
), we must introduce the subspace of
V_{h}
of discretely divergence-free functions and the discrete convex set
\mathring{K}_{h}
:

\mathring{V}_{h}=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h}:b(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})=0\quad\forall q_{h}\in Q_{h}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathring{K}_{h}=\mathring{V}_{h}\cap K_{h}.

Then, the discrete mixed problem (
29
) is equivalent to the minimisation over
\mathring{K}_{h}
of the functional
\mathcal{J}:V\to\mathbb{R}
defined in (
17
), provided that two discrete inf-sup conditions hold. For
V_{b,h}=V_{h}\cap V_{b}
, these discrete conditions can be stated as

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{b,h}}\frac{\langle Bq_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{V}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\right\rVert_{V}}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\left\lVert q_{h}\right\rVert_{Q}\quad\forall q_{h}\in Q_{h},

(31)

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h}}\frac{\langle\mu_{h},\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{\Sigma}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\right\rVert_{V}}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\left\lVert\mu_{h}\right\rVert_{\Sigma}\quad\forall\mu_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}.

(32)

When the conditions (
31
) and (
32
) hold, then (
28
), (
29
) and the minimisation of
\mathcal{J}
over
\mathring{K}
are equivalent problems. The proofs for such equivalences require the same arguments as the proofs presented in Appendix
A
. If
\mathcal{J}
admits a unique minimiser over
\mathring{K}_{h}
, the discrete inf-sup conditions guarantee a unique solution for (
29
) and set constraints on the choice of spaces
V_{h}
,
Q_{h}
and
\Sigma_{h}
used when approximating solutions of (
12
). As in the continuous case, the well-posedness of (
29
) requires the compatibility condition (
20
) to hold. The theorem below can be proved in the same way as Theorem
3
.

Theorem 4
.

Assume that the discrete inf-sup conditions (
31
) and (
32
) hold. If
R_{V}=\{0\}
, then a solution to (
29
) exists and is unique. If
R_{V}\neq\{0\}
, then there is a unique solution to (
29
) if the compatibility condition (
20
) holds. Conversely, if
R_{V}\neq\{0\}
and a solution exists, (
22
) must hold. The solution of (
12
) is bounded from above independently of
h
, provided (
20
) holds when
R_{V}\neq\{0\}
.

3.2
Upper bounds for the velocity error

An important tool presented in
[
4
,
29
]
for establishing error estimates for non-Newtonian flows is the use of the function
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}
. Here, for ease of notation, we denote by
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}
an operator that acts on both
\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
and
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\mathrm{A})=|\mathrm{A}|^{\frac{r-2}{2}}\mathrm{A}\quad\text{for $\mathrm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}$ or $\mathrm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$}.

(33)

This operator is closely related to the operators
A
and
G
. Let the operator
\mathrm{E}:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}
be given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u})-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{v})\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{u})-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{v})\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{b})}.

We then have that

\mathrm{E}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\sim\langle A\boldsymbol{u}-A\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{V}+\langle G\boldsymbol{u}-G\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{V}

(34)

for all
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in V
. The following variation of Young’s inequality,

\displaystyle\langle A\boldsymbol{u}-A\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{w}\rangle_{V}+\langle G\boldsymbol{u}-G\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{w}\rangle_{V}\leq\varepsilon\mathrm{E}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+c_{\varepsilon}\mathrm{E}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}),

(35)

is valid for any
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\in V
and
\varepsilon>0
, with the constant
c_{\varepsilon}>0
depending on
\varepsilon
. Additionally, the inequalities

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{r}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{v})-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{w})\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\lVert|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{v}|+|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{w}|\right\rVert_{L^{r}(\Omega)}^{2-r},

(36)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{r}(\Gamma_{b})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{v})-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{w})\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{b})}\left\lVert|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{v}|+|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{w}|\right\rVert_{L^{r}(\Gamma_{b})}^{2-r},

(37)

hold for any
\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\in\boldsymbol{W}^{1,r}(\Omega)
. A proof for inequalities (
34
) and (
36
) can be found in
[
29
, Lemmas 2.3, 2.4]
, and
[
4
, Lemma 2.7]
for (
35
), for the case without friction. The presence of the operator
G
requires a version of
[
29
, Lemmas 2.3, 2.4]
and
[
4
, Lemma 2.7]
stated in terms of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Since these results are based on algebraic inequalities for matrices, the extension to vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be proved by considering diagonal matrices.

By applying the triangle inequality, (
18
) and (
36
)-(
37
), the velocity error can be decomposed into two components as

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right\rVert_{V}\lesssim\left\lVert\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right\rVert_{V}+\mathrm{E}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}_{h}).

(38)

For the first term on the right of (
38
), which represents the rigid component of the error, we present the following result:

Lemma 5
.

Assume that
R_{V}\neq\{0\}
and that the compatibility condition (
20
) holds. Let
(\boldsymbol{u},p,\lambda)\in V\times Q\times\Lambda
be the solution to (
12
) and
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h},\lambda_{h})\in V_{h}\times Q_{h}\times\Lambda_{h}
to (
29
). Then,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right\rVert_{V}\lesssim\mathrm{E}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})+\left\lVert\lambda-\mu_{h}\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\quad\forall\,\mu_{h}\in\Lambda_{h}.

(39)

Proof.

Under the assumption that
R_{V}\neq\{0\}
, we either have that
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\in R_{V}\cap K
or
-\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\in R_{V}\cap K
. If
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\in R_{V}\cap K
, then inequality (
21
) and the continuous mixed system (
12
) allow us to write

\left\lVert\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right\rVert_{V}\lesssim-\langle F,\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\rangle_{V}=\langle\lambda,\gamma_{n}(\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}))\rangle_{\Sigma},

where the equality follows from
\langle A\boldsymbol{u}+G\boldsymbol{u}-Bp,\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\rangle_{V}=0
. Then, by noting that
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})=-\mathbb{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})+\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
and
\langle\lambda,\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{\Sigma}=0
, using inequalities (
18
) and (
36
)-(
37
), and using the uniform in
h
boundedness of solutions to (
29
) (see Theorem
4
), we arrive at

\left\lVert\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right\rVert_{V}\lesssim\mathrm{E}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})-\langle\lambda,\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rangle_{\Sigma}.

(40)

On the other hand, if
-\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\in R_{V}\cap K
, then, by appealing to (
20
) and the discrete mixed system (
29
),

\left\lVert\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right\rVert_{V}\lesssim\langle F,\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\rangle_{V}=-\langle\lambda_{h},\gamma_{n}(\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}))\rangle_{\Sigma}.

Following the same steps as before, we deduce that

\left\lVert\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right\rVert_{V}\lesssim\mathrm{E}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})-\langle\lambda_{h},\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{\Sigma}\leq\mathrm{E}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}_{h}),

(41)

where the final inequality follows from the fact that
\lambda_{h}\leq 0
on
\Gamma_{b}
by the definition of
\Lambda_{h}
. As a result of (
40
) and (
41
), we have that

\left\lVert\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right\rVert_{V}\lesssim\mathrm{E}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})+\max{\left\{0,-\langle\lambda,\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rangle_{\Sigma}\right\}}

in all cases. Finally, given a
\mu_{h}\in\Lambda_{h}
, we have

\displaystyle-\langle\lambda,\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rangle_{\Sigma}\leq\langle\mu_{h}-\lambda,\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rangle\lesssim\left\lVert\lambda-\mu_{h}\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{\prime}}

because
\langle\mu_{h},\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rangle_{\Sigma}\geq 0
.

Remark 6
.

As mentioned in the introduction, previous analyses of finite element approximations of semicoercive variational inequalities either only consider the error in a seminorm
[
30
]
or use indirect arguments to prove the convergence of the approximate solution in the complete norm
[
42
,
26
,
40
,
2
,
7
]
. In these cases, arguments by contradiction involving a sequence of triangulations are used. In Lemma
5
, on the other hand, we provide a fully constructive proof for bounding the rigid component of the velocity error from above. This result is a key ingredient in obtaining the error estimates for the finite element scheme presented in the next section. The proof of Lemma
5
relies on
\dim{R_{V}}\leq 1
, which holds for almost all Stokes variational inequalities considered in glaciology
[
13
,
34
,
16
,
41
,
10
]
.

The second term on the right of (
38
) can be bounded from above by using the properties of the operator
\mathrm{E}
.

Lemma 7
.

Let the triples
(\boldsymbol{u},p,\lambda)\in V\times Q\times\Lambda
and
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h},\lambda_{h})\in V_{h}\times Q_{h}\times\Lambda_{h}
be solutions to (
12
) and (
29
), respectively. Then

\mathrm{E}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\lesssim\mathrm{E}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+\left\lVert p-q_{h}\right\rVert^{2}_{Q}+\langle\lambda-\lambda_{h},\gamma_{n}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\rangle_{\Sigma}

(42)

holds for all
(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})\in\mathring{V}_{h}\times Q_{h}
.

Proof.

From (
12a
) and (
29a
), we see that, for any
(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})\in\mathring{V}_{h}\times Q_{h}
, we have

\begin{split}\langle A\boldsymbol{u}-A\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rangle_{V}+\langle G\boldsymbol{u}-G\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rangle_{V}=\\
\langle A\boldsymbol{u}-A\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{V}+\langle G\boldsymbol{u}-G\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{V}\\
+\langle B(p-q_{h}),\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rangle_{V}+\langle\lambda-\lambda_{h},\gamma_{n}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\rangle_{\Sigma}\end{split}

Using (
34
) and (
35
)

\begin{split}\mathrm{E}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})&\lesssim\varepsilon_{1}\mathrm{E}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})+c_{\varepsilon_{1}}\mathrm{E}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\\
&+\langle B(p-q_{h}),\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rangle_{V}+\langle\lambda-\lambda_{h},\gamma_{n}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\rangle_{\Sigma}\end{split}

for an arbitrary
\varepsilon_{1}>0
. Additionally, by using Young’s inequality,

\displaystyle\langle B(p-q_{h}),\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rangle_{V}\lesssim c_{\varepsilon_{2}}\left\lVert p-q_{h}\right\rVert^{2}_{Q}+\varepsilon_{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{r}(\Omega)}

for any
\varepsilon_{2}>0
. Then, via (
36
), and by setting
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
sufficiently small, inequality (
42
) is established.

Remark 8
.

If the pair
V_{h}\times Q_{h}
is divergence free in the sense that
\langle Bq_{h},\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\rangle_{V}=0
for all
q_{h}\in Q_{h}
implies that
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{w}_{h}=0
, then the term
\left\lVert p-q_{h}\right\rVert^{2}_{Q}
in inequality (
42
) can be removed.

3.3
Upper bounds for the pressure and Lagrange multiplier errors

We finalise the analysis of the abstract discretisation by bounding the errors for the pressure and the Lagrange multiplier from above.

Lemma 9
.

Assume that the discrete inf-sup conditions (
31
) and (
32
) hold. Let
(\boldsymbol{u},p,\lambda)\in V\times Q\times\Lambda
be the solution of (
12
) and
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h},\lambda_{h})\in V_{h}\times Q_{h}\times\Lambda_{h}
of (
29
). Then

\displaystyle\left\lVert p-p_{h}\right\rVert_{Q}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathrm{E}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})^{1/r^{\prime}}+\left\lVert p-q_{h}\right\rVert_{Q},

(43)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\lambda-\lambda_{h}\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathrm{E}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})^{1/r^{\prime}}+\left\lVert p-q_{h}\right\rVert_{Q}+\left\lVert\lambda-\mu_{h}\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{\prime}},

(44)

for all
q_{h}\in Q_{h}
and
\mu_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}
.

Proof.

Since
Q_{h}
and
\Sigma_{h}
are subsets of
Q
and
\Sigma
respectively, we can obtain the following equality from (
12a
) and (
29a
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle A\boldsymbol{u}-A\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{V}+\langle G\boldsymbol{u}-G\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{V}=\\
\langle B(p-p_{h}),\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{V}+\langle\lambda-\lambda_{h},\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{\Sigma}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h}.\end{split}

(45)

The inf-sup condition (
31
) for the pressure space holds over the space
V_{b,h}\subset V_{h}
of vector fields with a normal component vanishing on
\Gamma_{b}
. For
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{b,h}
, from equation (
45
) we derive

\langle B(p_{h}-q_{h}),\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{V}=\langle A\boldsymbol{u}-A\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{V}+\langle B(p-q_{h}),\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{V}.

From the inf-sup condition (
31
) it follows that

\displaystyle\left\lVert p_{h}-q_{h}\right\rVert_{Q}\lesssim\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{b,h}}{\left(\frac{\langle A\boldsymbol{u}-A\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{V}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\right\rVert_{V}}\right)}+\left\lVert p-q_{h}\right\rVert_{Q}.

(46)

Then, by Hölder’s inequality and
[
29
, Lemma 2.4]
, we have that

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{b,h}}{\left(\frac{\langle A\boldsymbol{u}-A\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{V}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\right\rVert_{V}}\right)}\lesssim\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u})-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2/r^{\prime}}.

(47)

Finally, (
43
) follows by applying the triangle inequality to
\left\lVert p-p_{h}\right\rVert_{Q}
and using (
46
) and (
47
). The bound (
44
) follows in the same way. However, in this case, the inf-sup condition (
32
) is set over the whole space
V_{h}
, so the friction term in (
45
) does not vanish. Following the argument used in
[
29
, Lemma 2.4]
, we can show that

\left\lVert|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{u}|^{r-2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{u}-|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}|^{r-2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{b})}\lesssim\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{u})-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right\rVert^{2/r^{\prime}}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{b})}

and therefore

\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h}}{\left(\frac{\langle G\boldsymbol{u}-G\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{V}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\right\rVert_{V}}\right)}\lesssim\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{u})-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right\rVert^{2/r^{\prime}}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{b})}.

Lemmas
5
,
7
, and
9
give discretisation error estimates in terms of best approximation results. To derive a convergence result, we require bounds on these best approximations. We discuss this in the context of a finite element discretisation in the next section.

4
A finite element scheme

We now consider a particular finite element discretisation of the mixed problem (
12
). We introduce a non-degenerate (in the sense of
[
6
, Definition 4.4.13]
) sequence of triangulations
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, where
h>0
denotes the maximum cell diameter in
\mathcal{T}_{h}
. The set of edges in
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is denoted by
\mathcal{E}_{h}
; we assume that every edge
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}
in
\partial\Omega
is either in
\overline{\Gamma}_{s}
,
\overline{\Gamma}_{b}
or
\overline{\Gamma}_{d}
. We write
\mathcal{E}_{h}(\Gamma_{s})
and
\mathcal{E}_{h}(\Gamma_{b})
to denote the edges in
\overline{\Gamma}_{s}
and
\overline{\Gamma}_{b}
respectively. Associated to each
\mathcal{T}_{h}
are the finite element spaces
V_{h}
,
Q_{h}
and
\Sigma_{h}
, defined by

\displaystyle V_{h}

\displaystyle=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}(\Omega):\boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{c}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}_{2}(c)\quad\forall c\in\mathcal{T},\quad\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0\quad\text{on $\Gamma_{d}$}\right\},

(48a)

\displaystyle Q_{h}

\displaystyle=\left\{q_{h}\in L^{2}(\Omega):q_{h}|_{c}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(c)\quad\forall c\in\mathcal{T}\right\},

(48b)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{h}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mu_{h}\in L^{2}(\Gamma_{b}):\mu_{h}|_{e}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(e)\quad\forall e\in\mathcal{E}(\Gamma_{b})\right\}.

(48c)

4.1
Analysis of the scheme

The first step in analysing this discretisation is to investigate whether the discrete mixed problem (
29
) is well-posed for this choice of
V_{h}\times Q_{h}\times\Sigma_{h}
, subject to the compatibility condition (
20
). Specifically, we must verify the discrete inf-sup conditions (
31
) and (
32
). The pair
V_{h}\times Q_{h}
is well-known to satisfy (
31
), see
[
5
, Proposition 8.4.3]
for the case of
r=2
; the general case
r\in[1,\infty]
follows from the same arguments by using the interpolation operator
\pi_{V}
discussed in Appendix
B.2
. A proof for (
32
) is presented below using a similar argument to the one presented in
[
9
, Proposition 3.3]
.

Lemma 10
.

The finite element pair
V_{h}
and
\Sigma_{h}
defined in (
48
) is inf-sup stable in the sense of (
32
).

Proof.

Let
\mu_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}
. By the Hahn-Banach theorem, there is a
\psi\in\Sigma
such that
\langle\mu_{h},\psi\rangle_{\Sigma}=\left\lVert\mu_{h}\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
and
\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{\Sigma}=1
. In Appendix
B.3
we construct an extension operator
\Phi:\Sigma\to V_{h}
which is bounded uniformly with respect to
h
, i.e.
\left\lVert\Phi\psi\right\rVert_{V}\lesssim\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{\Sigma}
, and

\langle\mu_{h},\gamma_{n}(\Phi\psi)\rangle_{\Sigma}=\langle\mu_{h},\psi\rangle_{\Sigma}\quad\forall\mu_{h}\in\Sigma_{h},

for all
\psi\in\Sigma
. Then

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mu_{h}\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{\prime}}=\frac{\langle\mu_{h},\psi\rangle_{\Sigma}}{\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{\Sigma}}\lesssim\frac{\langle\mu_{h},\gamma_{n}(\Phi\psi)\rangle_{\Sigma}}{\left\lVert\Phi\psi\right\rVert_{V}}\leq\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h}}\frac{\langle\mu_{h},\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{\Sigma}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\right\rVert_{V}}

and the result follows.

We end this section with a discussion on the approximability of the mixed system (
12
). We show that the approximate solutions
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h},\lambda_{h})
converge to the exact solutions of (
12
) as
h\to 0
under a regularity condition, and we establish a rate of convergence for these approximations.

Theorem 11
.

Assume that the compatibility condition (
20
) holds whenever
R_{V}\neq\{0\}
. Let the triple
(\boldsymbol{u},p,\lambda)\in V\times Q\times\Lambda
be the solution to (
12
) and
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h},\lambda_{h})\in V_{h}\times Q_{h}\times\Lambda_{h}
to (
29
). Additionally, assume that
(\boldsymbol{u},p,\lambda)\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)\times W^{1,r^{\prime}}(\Omega)\times W^{1-1/r^{\prime},r^{\prime}}(\Gamma_{b})
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u})\in\boldsymbol{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{u})\in\boldsymbol{W}^{1,2}(\Gamma_{b})
. Then

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right\rVert_{V}

\displaystyle\lesssim h,

(49a)

\displaystyle\left\lVert p-p_{h}\right\rVert_{Q}+\left\lVert\lambda-\lambda_{h}\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\lesssim h^{2/r^{\prime}}+h.

(49b)

Proof.

We proceed by first finding a suitable upper bound for the term involving the Lagrange multiplier in (
42
). Since
\langle\lambda_{h},\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rangle_{\Sigma}=0
and
\langle\mu_{h},\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{\Sigma}\geq 0
for all
(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\mu_{h})\in K_{h}\times\Lambda_{h}
, one can show that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle\lambda-\lambda_{h},\gamma_{n}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\rangle_{\Sigma}&\leq\langle\lambda-\mu_{h},\gamma_{n}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u})\rangle_{\Sigma}\\
&+\langle\lambda-\mu_{h},\gamma_{n}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\rangle_{\Sigma}+\langle\mu_{h},\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{\Sigma}\end{split}

(50)

for all
(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\mu_{h})\in K_{h}\times\Lambda_{h}
. By applying Young’s inequality in (
50
) and using inequalities (
38
), (
39
), and (
42
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right\rVert_{V}\lesssim\mathrm{E}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})^{1/2}+\left\lVert p-q_{h}\right\rVert_{Q}+\left\lVert\lambda-\mu_{h}\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{\prime}}+\langle\mu_{h},\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle_{\Sigma}

(51)

for all
(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h},\mu_{h})\in\mathring{K}_{h}\times Q_{h}\times\Lambda_{h}
.

Let
\pi_{V}:V\to V_{h}
by the interpolation operator introduced in Appendix
B.2
. Additionally, let
\pi_{Q}:Q\to Q_{h}
and
\pi_{\Sigma}:L^{2}(\Gamma_{b})\to\Sigma_{h}
be standard interpolation operators onto the space of piecewise-constant functions. We refer to
[
14
]
and the results in Appendix
B.1
for proofs of optimal interpolation error estimates in the
Q
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
norms. From the properties of these interpolation operators it follows that
\pi_{V}\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathring{K}_{h}
and
\pi_{\Sigma}\lambda\in\Lambda_{h}
. Additionally, we have that
\int_{e}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{e}(\pi_{V}\boldsymbol{u})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\,\mathrm{d}s
for all
e\in\mathcal{E}
, so

\displaystyle\langle\pi_{\Sigma}\lambda,\gamma_{n}\pi_{V}\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{\Sigma}=\langle\lambda,\pi_{\Sigma}(\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u})\rangle_{\Sigma}=\langle\lambda,\pi_{\Sigma}(\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u})-\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{\Sigma}.

Since
\langle\pi_{\Sigma}\lambda,\pi_{\Sigma}(\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u})-\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{\Sigma}=0
, we have that

\displaystyle\langle\pi_{\Sigma}\lambda,\gamma_{n}\pi_{V}\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{\Sigma}=\langle\lambda-\pi_{\Sigma}\lambda,\pi_{\Sigma}(\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u})-\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{\Sigma}.

(52)

Therefore, by setting
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}=\pi_{V}\boldsymbol{u}
,
q_{h}=\pi_{Q}p
, and
\mu_{h}=\pi_{\Sigma}\lambda
in (
51
) and using (
52
), we can show that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right\rVert_{V}\lesssim\mathrm{E}(\boldsymbol{u},\pi_{V}\boldsymbol{u})^{1/2}+\left\lVert p-\pi_{Q}p\right\rVert_{Q}+\left\lVert\lambda-\pi_{\Sigma}\lambda\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{\prime}}+\left\lVert\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u}-\pi_{\Sigma}(\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{u})\right\rVert_{\Sigma}.

(53)

We then establish (
49a
) with the approximation properties of the interpolation operators presented in Appendices
B.1
and
B.2
for
\pi_{\Sigma}
and
\pi_{V}
respectively, and
[
14
]
for
\pi_{Q}
. The estimate (
49b
) then follows from Lemma
9
.

Remark 12
.

The velocity and pressure error estimates coincide with those obtained in
[
4
, Theorem 2.14]
and in
[
29
, Theorem 3.1]
for the
r
-Stokes system without contact or friction boundary conditions. This indicates that these boundary conditions and the Lagrange multiplier do not reduce the order of convergence. This may be due to the use of piecewise constant elements for
\Sigma_{h}
. In
[
9
]
, a proof with non-optimal convergence rates is presented for the case when continuous piecewise quadratic polynomials are used for the Lagrange multiplier.

4.2
Discrete algebraic formulation

We now present an algebraic counterpart of (
29
) using the finite element spaces specified in (
48
) in terms of matrices and vectors. Let
V_{h}=\Span{\{\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\}}_{i=1}^{N_{v}}
,
Q_{h}=\Span{\{q_{j}\}}_{j=1}^{N_{q}}
and
\Sigma_{h}=\Span{\{\mu_{k}\}}_{k=1}^{N_{\mu}}
, where
N_{v}=\dim{V_{h}}
,
N_{q}=\dim{Q_{h}}
and
N_{\mu}=\dim{\Sigma_{h}}
. For the functions
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h},\lambda_{h})\in V_{h}\times Q_{h}\times\Sigma_{h}
, we write
\mathbf{u}
,
\mathbf{p}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
for the vectors containing the respective degrees of freedom (DoFs) in
\mathbb{R}^{N_{v}}
,
\mathbb{R}^{N_{q}}
and
\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mu}}
. In order to write an algebraic counterpart of (
29c
), we need to introduce the discrete normal trace operator

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma_{n}}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{v}}\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mu}}

that returns the average normal components of a vector
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h}
along the edges on
\Gamma_{b}
. That is, for each
i\in\{1,2,...,N_{\mu}\}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\gamma_{n}}\mathbf{v})_{i}=\frac{1}{|e_{i}|}\int_{e_{i}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\,\mathrm{d}s,

where
e_{i}\in\mathcal{E}(\Gamma_{b})
is the unique edge along
\Gamma_{b}
associated to the degree of freedom in
\Sigma_{h}
with index
i
. Then, the algebraic counterpart of (
29
) can be written in terms of matrices and vectors as

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})+\mathbf{G}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f},

(54a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=0,

(54b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=0.

(54c)

Here, we have introduced the matrices
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{v}\times N_{q}}
and
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{v}\times N_{\mu}}
, the vector
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{v}}
and the nonlinear operators
\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{v}}\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{v}}
,
\mathbf{G}_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{v}}\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{v}}
and
\mathbf{C}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mu}}\times\mathbb{R}^{N_{v}}\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mu}}
. The matrices are given by the elements
\mathbf{B}_{ij}=\langle Bq_{j},\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\rangle_{V}
and
\mathbf{D}_{ij}=\langle\mu_{j},\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\rangle_{\Sigma}
and the vector by
\mathbf{f}_{i}=\langle F,\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\rangle_{V}
. The nonlinear operators are defined as

\displaystyle[\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})]_{i}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\alpha\left(\varepsilon+|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u}|\right)^{r-2}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{v}_{i})\,\mathrm{d}x,

(55)

\displaystyle[\mathbf{G}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})]_{i}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\tau\left(\varepsilon+|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{u}|\right)^{r-2}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{v}_{i})\,\mathrm{d}x,

(56)

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\max{\left\{0,-\boldsymbol{\lambda}+c(\boldsymbol{\gamma_{n}}\mathbf{u})\right\}},

(57)

for an arbitrary
c>0
and a regularisation term
\varepsilon>0
. This regularisation term is commonly used to avoid numerical complications caused when
|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u}|
or
|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{u}|
is equal to or very close to zero
[
32
]
. In (
57
), the
\max
operation is understood to be carried out componentwise on each of the elements in the vector
-\boldsymbol{\lambda}+c(\boldsymbol{\gamma_{n}}\mathbf{u})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mu}}
. The use of the operator
\mathbf{C}
in (
57
) is a common way of expressing contact conditions. A particular advantage is that the nonlinear system (
54
) can be solved with a semi-smooth Newton method that enjoys superlinear convergence in a neighbourhood of the solution
[
28
]
. Equation (
54c
) is equivalent to (
29c
) whenever
\Sigma_{h}
is defined as in (
48c
). By solving (
54c
) we enforce

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma_{n}}\mathbf{u}\leq 0,\quad\boldsymbol{\lambda}\leq 0\quad\text{and}\quad(\boldsymbol{\gamma_{n}}\mathbf{u})\cdot\boldsymbol{\lambda}=0,

(58)

exactly, which is the algebraic equivalent of the discrete contact conditions (
30
).

4.3
Numerical results

We present numerical results computed for a Stokes variational inequality with a manufactured solution on the domain
\Omega=(0,1)^{2}
. Computations of subglacial cavitation with the algorithm presented in this document can be found in
[
10
]
. Subglacial cavitation is a time-dependent problem in which a free surface is evolved with an advection equation. We do this in
[
10
]
by coupling the algorithm presented in this study with a solver for the advection equation.

The manufactured solution considered here is taken from
[
4
]
and is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x})=|\boldsymbol{x}|^{\alpha-1}(x_{2},-x_{1})^{\top},\quad\hat{p}(\boldsymbol{x})=|\boldsymbol{x}|^{\gamma},

(59)

where the parameters
\alpha
and
\gamma
are chosen such that
\boldsymbol{u}\in\boldsymbol{W}^{2,r}(\Omega)
,
p\in W^{1,r^{\prime}}(\Omega)
,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{u})\in\boldsymbol{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{u})\in\boldsymbol{W}^{1,2}(\Gamma_{b})
hold. This is ensured whenever
\alpha>1
and
\gamma>-1+\frac{2}{r}
, so we set
\alpha=1.01
and
\gamma=-1+\frac{2}{r}+0.01
in order to be critically close to the regularity assumed in Theorem
11
.

Contact boundary conditions are enforced on the lower boundary
\{y=0\}
. Given the velocity and pressure fields defined in (
59
), we have that

\displaystyle(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})(x_{1})=-x_{1}^{\alpha},\quad\hat{\lambda}(x_{1})=-x_{1}^{\gamma},

on
\{y=0\}
. In order to define the contact boundary conditions in such a way that both the kinematic and dynamic conditions are active, we define the “obstacles”

\displaystyle\chi(x_{1})=\begin{cases}(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})(x_{1})&\text{if $x_{1}\leq 0.5$}\\
-2^{-\alpha}&\text{if $x_{1}>0.5$}\\
\end{cases},\quad\rho(x_{1})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $x_{1}\leq 0.5$}\\
\hat{\lambda}(x_{1})&\text{if $x_{1}>0.5$}\\
\end{cases}.

Then, for this numerical test we solve the
r
-Stokes system (
1
) together with the boundary conditions

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\leq\chi,\quad\lambda\leq\rho\quad\text{and}\quad(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}-\chi)(\lambda-\rho)=0\quad\text{on $\{y=0\}$},

(60a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\quad\text{on $\{x=0\}$},

(60b)

\displaystyle\sigma_{nn}=\hat{\sigma}_{nn}\quad\text{on $\partial\Omega\setminus(\{y=0\}\cup\{x=0\})$},

(60c)

\displaystyle\sigma_{nt}=\hat{\sigma}_{nt}\quad\text{on $\partial\Omega$},

(60d)

where
\hat{\sigma}=\sigma(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}},\hat{p})
. Boundary conditions are set for the normal velocity along
\{x=0\}
in (
60b
) to mimic the boundary conditions enforced at
\Gamma_{d}
and make
\dim{R_{V}}=1
. In this case,
R_{V}
is a one-dimensional vector space containing vertical motions. Therefore,

R_{V}\cap K=\left\{(0,\theta):\theta\leq\min{\chi}\right\},

and, since
F=A_{\varepsilon}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}+G_{\varepsilon}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}-B\hat{p}-\gamma_{n}^{\prime}\hat{\lambda}
, we have that

\langle F,\boldsymbol{r}\rangle_{V}=\theta\int_{0}^{1}x^{\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}x<0

for all
\boldsymbol{r}=(0,\theta)
with
\theta<0
(note that
\min{\chi}<0
). This proves that the compatibility condition (
20
) holds and the system is well-posed. Although the functional setting of this numerical test differs slightly from the setting studied in this paper, the numerical test contains the fundamental elements of the setting analysed.

49a
). In Table
2
we see that the orders of convergence for the velocity in the
L^{r}(\Omega)
-norm appear to increase by one when compared to the orders computed with the
V
-norm. On the other hand, the orders of convergence obtained for the pressure appear to be independent of
r
. A closely related problem (without contact boundary conditions) is solved in the work of Belenki et al.
[
4
]
. In the work of Belenki et al., the problem is formulated as an
r
-Stokes problem with Dirichlet boundary conditions and the MINI element is used for the velocity and pressure. Interestingly, their numerical results deliver the predicted orders of convergence for the pressure error. Hence, the apparent suboptimality of (
49b
) for the pressure could be due to the finite elements used here or to the presence of contact boundary conditions and a Lagrange multiplier. Regarding the Lagrange multiplier, the estimated orders of convergence are exceeded, but a dependence on
r
is observed.

5
Conclusions

In this study, we present a Stokes variational inequality that arises when modelling a symmetrical marine ice sheet. We prove the well-posedness of this system whenever the subspace of rigid modes in the velocity space is of dimension at most one under the condition that a compatibility condition holds in Theorem
3
. We consider a family of finite element discretisations for this problem in Section
3
and prove an analogous well-posedness result for the discrete system in Theorem
4
. Using the techniques from
[
4
,
29
]
, we then establish error estimates for a particular finite element discretisation in Theorem
11
. These error estimates, which are verified with a numerical test using a manufactured solution, indicate that the presence of rigid modes and the nonlinearity of the friction boundary condition do not affect the order of convergence of the scheme.

The results from this study give a theoretical justification for using a finite element discretisation belonging to the family considered in Section
3
in glaciological applications. Moreover, this analysis can be extended to different contact problems in glaciology such as the subglacial cavitation problem, where the subspace of rigid modes present in the velocity space is of dimension one whenever Dirichlet boundary conditions are enforced for the tangential component of the velocity on the top boundary, as done in
[
20
]
. This extension would require taking into consideration the presence of non-homogeneous boundary conditions for the velocity and the use of a periodic domain. However, if instead of Dirichlet boundary conditions, we enforce Neumann boundary conditions on the top boundary, as described in
[
10
]
, the space of rigid modes is then of dimension two. This situation would require a more complicated extension that should be considered in future work. Subglacial cavitation is considered in
[
10
]
and the Stokes variational inequality that arises is solved using the finite element discretisation from Section
4
.

A major assumption of this paper is that the domain is two-dimensional. An extension of the analysis presented here to three dimensions would require a careful consideration of the rigid modes present in the velocity space, since the space of rigid modes in three dimensions is larger than in two dimensions. However, in most problems of interest, three dimensional marine ice sheets are considered to be enclosed within two lateral walls, see for example
[
16
]
. In this case, if the lateral walls and the bedrock are flat, the space of rigid modes in
V
is once again reduced to vertical movements and is therefore one-dimensional. As a consequence, much of the analysis from this paper would still be valid in three dimensions. However, the extension operator presented in Appendix
B.3
, used to prove Lemma
10
, relies heavily on the fact that the domain is two-dimensional. Therefore, the choice of finite elements used to solve the variational inequality would have to be chosen and studied carefully.

Lemma 13

Proof.

For the next step, we need to use the inf-sup condition (

Appendix B
Technical results on finite element spaces

B.1
Approximation properties in negative order Sobolev spaces

Several technicalities arise from the need to handle the dual space of the fractional Sobolev space
W^{1-1/r,r}(\Gamma_{b})
and its finite element approximation
\Sigma_{h}
. Let
s\in(0,1)
and
m\in[1,\infty)
; on
\Gamma_{b}
, the norm of the fractional Sobolev space
W^{s,m}(\Gamma_{b})
with
s\in(0,1)
and
m\in[1,\infty]
can be defined by

\displaystyle\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert^{m}_{W^{s,m}(\Gamma_{b})}=\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert^{m}_{L^{m}(\Gamma_{b})}+[\phi]^{m}_{W^{s,m}(\Gamma_{b})},

(65)

where

[\phi]^{m}_{W^{s,m}(\Gamma_{b})}=\int_{\Gamma_{b}}\int_{\Gamma_{b}}\frac{|\phi(x)-\phi(y)|^{m}}{|x-y|^{1+sm}}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,

see
[
11
]
. In order to prove certain approximation properties on
\Sigma_{h}
we need to introduce some theoretical results. We start by defining the following pair of spaces

L_{0}^{m}(e)=\left\{\phi\in L^{m}(e):\int_{e}\phi\,\mathrm{d}x=0\right\},\quad W^{s,m}_{0}(e)=W^{s,m}(e)\cap L^{r}_{0}(e)

for an edge
e\in\mathcal{E}(\Gamma_{b})
. We can use the fractional normed Poincaré inequality proved in
[
14
, Lemma 7.1]
to show that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{L^{m}(e)}\leq|e|^{s}[\phi]_{W^{s,m}(e)}\quad\forall\phi\in W_{0}^{s,m}(e).

(66)

Inequality (
66
) can be extended to negative norms by writing the
L^{m}(e)
norm for any
\phi\in L^{m}(\Gamma_{b})
as

\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{L^{m}(e)}=\sup_{\psi\in L^{m^{\prime}}(e)}{\frac{\int_{e}\phi\psi\,\mathrm{d}s}{\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{m^{\prime}}(e)}}}

and deducing that, if
\phi\in L^{m}_{0}(e)
, we have

\begin{split}\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{\left(W^{s,m}(e)\right)^{\prime}}&=\sup_{\psi\in W_{0}^{s,m}(e)}{\frac{\int_{e}\phi\psi\,\mathrm{d}s}{\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{W^{s,m}(e)}}}\\
&\leq|e|^{s}\sup_{\psi\in L^{m^{\prime}}(e)}{\frac{\int_{e}\phi\psi\,\mathrm{d}s}{\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{m^{\prime}}(e)}}}=|e|^{s}\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{L^{m^{\prime}}(e)}.\end{split}

(67)

For the finite element space
\Sigma_{h}
defined in (
48c
), let
\pi_{\Sigma}:L^{m}(\Gamma_{b})\to\Sigma_{h}
be the standard interpolation operator onto piecewise constant polynomials which takes the average of functions over each
e\in\mathcal{E}(\Gamma_{b})
. Then, from inequality (
66
) we can prove error estimates in fractional norms. Moreover, (
67
) leads to

\displaystyle\left\lVert\phi-\pi_{\Sigma}\phi\right\rVert_{\left(W^{s,m}(\Gamma_{b})\right)^{\prime}}\lesssim h^{2s}\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{W^{s,m^{\prime}}(\Gamma_{b})},

(68)

where
h=\max{\{|e|:e\in\mathcal{E}(\Gamma_{b})\}}
.

B.2
An interpolation operator for the velocity

Here, we compile a variety of results from different sources and prove a result regarding an interpolation operator for the velocity that preserves the discrete divergence and maps elements of
K
into
K_{h}
. We denote by
\pi_{V}
the interpolation operator introduced in
[
22

that is defined as follows for each component of a vector-valued function: for a non-degenerate simplex
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
with edges
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}
and vertices
\{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}

\phi_{x}
with
x\in\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}\cup\{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}

\displaystyle\phi_{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{a}_{j})

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij},\quad\int_{e_{j}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}s=0,\quad\int_{e_{j}}\phi_{e_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}s=\delta_{ij},\quad\phi_{e_{i}}(\boldsymbol{a}_{j})=0,

for all
i,j\in\{1,2,3\}
. For each vertex
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}
we choose a an edge
e_{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}\in\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}

\displaystyle\int_{e_{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}\phi_{x}\,\mathrm{d}s=\delta_{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}x},\quad\psi_{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}\in\mathbb{P}_{2}(e_{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}),

where

denotes the edge
e_{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}
or its two end-points. We then define (the scalar version of)

\displaystyle(\pi_{V}u)(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(\left[\int_{e_{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}}u\psi_{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}s\right]\phi_{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{x})+\left[\int_{e_{i}}u\,\mathrm{d}s\right]\phi_{e_{i}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right).

of
\Omega

If the spaces

,

and

\displaystyle\langle Bq_{h},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{V}

\displaystyle\langle\mu_{h},\gamma_{n}\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle\forall(\boldsymbol{v},\mu_{h})\in V\times\Sigma_{h}.

(70)

Additionally, the interpolation operator

has two key approximation properties. First, the optimal approximation property

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}-\pi_{V}\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{W}^{s,m}(\Omega)}\lesssim h^{k}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{W}^{s+k,m}(\Omega)}

(71)

holds for all

and

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{v})-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\pi_{V}\boldsymbol{v})\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim h\left\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{v})\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(72)

Finally, we may also prove that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{v})-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\pi_{V}\boldsymbol{v})\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{b})}\lesssim h\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in\mathcal{E}(\partial\Omega)\\

B.3
An extension operator

In this section we prove an auxiliary result required for the proof of Lemma
10
. We build an extension operator
\Phi:\Sigma\to V_{h}
which is uniformly bounded and satisfies

\displaystyle\langle\mu_{h},\gamma_{n}(\Phi\phi)\rangle_{\Sigma}=\langle\mu_{h},\phi\rangle_{\Sigma}\quad\forall\mu_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}.

(74)

Step 1
. We will first find a uniformly bounded linear operator
\Pi:\Sigma\to\gamma_{n}(V_{h})
with the property that

\displaystyle\int_{e}(\phi-\Pi\phi)\,\mathrm{d}s=0\quad\text{for any $e\in\mathcal{E}(\Gamma_{b})$ and $\phi\in\Sigma$.}

(75)

Let

Z_{h}=\left\{\phi_{h}\in\mathcal{C}(\Gamma_{b}):\phi_{h}|_{e}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(e)\quad\forall e\in\mathcal{E}(\Gamma_{b})\right\}

and note that
Z_{h}\subset\gamma_{n}(V_{h})
. For
\phi\in\Sigma
and
e\in\mathcal{E}(\Gamma_{b})
, we define
\Pi_{2}:\Sigma\to Z_{h}
by setting

\displaystyle(\Pi_{2}\phi)(a)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{for the endpoints $a$ in $e$},

\displaystyle\int_{e}\Pi_{2}\phi\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{e}\phi\,\mathrm{d}s.

We clearly have that
\Pi_{2}\phi=0
if and only if
\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}(\Gamma_{b}}\int_{e}|\phi|\,\mathrm{d}s=0
, so the latter defines a norm on
\Pi_{2}(\Sigma)
. By exploiting this fact and the norm equivalence on finite dimensional spaces, one can see that

\left\lVert\Pi_{2}\phi\right\rVert_{W^{1-1/r,r}(e)}\lesssim|e|^{-1/r^{\prime}}\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{L^{r}(e)}\quad\forall e\in\mathcal{E}(\Gamma_{b})

for all
\phi\in\Sigma
. Now, let
\pi_{Z}:\Sigma\to Z_{h}
be the quasi-interpolation operator defined in
[
14
]
. This operator is uniformly bounded in the
W^{1-1/r,r}(\Gamma_{b})
norm and satisfies

\left\lVert\phi-\pi_{Z}\phi\right\rVert_{L^{r}(e)}\lesssim|e|^{1-1/r}\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{W^{1-1/r,r}(e)}

for any edge
e\in\mathcal{E}(\Gamma_{b})
and function
\phi\in\Sigma
. As a result, the operator
\Pi=\pi_{Z}+\Pi_{2}(I-\pi_{Z})
is uniformly bounded and possesses the required property (
75
).

Step 2.
For the final step, we define a uniformly bounded operator
\gamma_{n,h}^{-1}:\gamma_{n}(V_{h})\to V_{h}
for which
\gamma_{n,h}^{-1}\phi_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\phi_{h}
on
\Gamma_{b}
. This operator can be defined as the solution of the problem:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla(\gamma_{n,h}^{-1}\phi_{h}):\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h},

\displaystyle\gamma_{n,h}^{-1}\phi_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\phi_{h}

\displaystyle\text{on $\Gamma_{b}$},

\displaystyle\gamma_{n,h}^{-1}\phi_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on $\Gamma_{d}$}.

Then, the operator
\Phi=\gamma_{n,h}^{-1}\circ\Pi
is uniformly bounded and property (

) holds.

Remark 16
.

The construction of the uniformly bounded operator
