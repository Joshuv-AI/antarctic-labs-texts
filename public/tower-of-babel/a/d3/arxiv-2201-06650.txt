Galois Connections in Persistent Homology
Aziz Burak Gülen, Alexander McCleary

Abstract
We present a new language for persistent homology in terms of Galois connections. This language has two main advantages over traditional approaches. First, it simplifies and unifies central concepts such as interleavings and matchings. Second, it provides access to Rota’s Galois connection theorem– a powerful tool with many potential applications in applied topology. To illustrate this, we use Rota’s Galois connection theorem to give a substantially easier proof of the bottleneck stability theorem. Finally, we use this language to establish relationships between various notions of multiparameter persistence diagrams.



1
Introduction

High-dimensional data sets are typically very difficult to understand.
Statistics provides a wealth of quantitative tools for tackling this problem.
Topological data analysis (TDA) on the other hand offers qualitative invariants for understanding high-dimensional data sets
[
8
]
.
Persistent homology is the main tool used in TDA.
It takes as input a nested sequence of spaces and outputs an invariant that captures where holes were born and died in the sequence of spaces.
This invariant is called the persistence diagram or barcode.

Figure 1:
A filtration indexed by the totally ordered set
\{0\leq 1\leq 2\leq 3\leq 4\leq 5\}
. There is 1-dimensional homology born at 2 and dying at 5.

These nested sequences of spaces are known as
filtrations
.
In applications, they usually arise from data in the form of finite metric spaces.
Given a finite metric space
X
, there are numerous ways of obtaining a filtration.
These include Vietoris-Rips complexes, Čech complexes, and witness complexes.
Each of these methods produces a simplicial complex
K_{t}
for each
t\in[0,\infty)
with inclusions
K_{t}\hookrightarrow K_{s}
for all
t\leq s
; see Figure
1
.

The fundamental idea of persistent homology is to refrain from choosing a single
K_{t}
to represent the geometry of
X
and instead consider the entire filtration at once.
The persistence diagram accomplishes this by encoding where and how the homology of the filtration changes.
This turns out to be surprisingly simple to implement and relatively quick to compute– current algorithms are matrix multiplication time and often faster with real-world data
[
1
]
.

The first step towards defining persistence diagrams is to apply homology with coefficients in a fixed field
\mathbb{K}
to the filtration
\{K_{t}\}
.
This produces a sequence of vector spaces
H_{d}(K_{t})
.
The inclusions
K_{t}\hookrightarrow K_{s}
induce linear maps from
H_{d}(K_{t})
to
H_{d}(K_{s})
for each
t\leq s\in[0,\infty)
.
This data is known as a
persistence module
and can be more succinctly defined as a functor from the poset category
[0,\infty)
to the category of finite-dimensional
\mathbb{K}
-vector spaces
{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
.

To convert a persistence module into a persistence diagram, we use an intermediate step known as the
birth-death function

[
16
]
.
This function takes as input intervals
(a,b)\subseteq[0,\infty)
and captures the number of homological features that were born at or before
a
and die at or before
b
.
We now employ a tool from order theory to convert the birth-death function into a persistence diagram: the Möbius inversion.

Möbius inversion can be thought of as a combinatorial analog of a derivative.
Just as a derivative captures how a continuous function changes, the Möbius inversion captures where, and how, a function defined on a poset changes.
Birth-death functions change precisely where homology is born or dies which is exactly the data we sought to isolate in the persistence diagram
[
25
]
.

The persistence diagram of totally ordered filtrations is remarkably well-behaved.
It is a complete invariant
[
32
]
, meaning no information is lost when passing from a persistence module to a persistence diagram.
Additionally, the persistence diagram is a stable invariant
[
12
]
, meaning that changes to a persistence module induce proportional changes to its persistence diagram.

1.1
Historical Context

Here we present a brief, and by no means complete, overview of some historical developments related to our results.
Persistent homology arose independently in the works of Robins
[
28
]
; Frosini and Landi
[
20
]
; and Edelsbrunner, Letscher, and Zomorodian
[
13
]
.

One of the most significant developments since the inception of persistent homology was the bottleneck stability theorem
[
12
]
.
This theorem implies that if a data set is perturbed by
\varepsilon
, the resulting persistence diagram will be perturbed by at most
\varepsilon
.
This result is crucial to justify applications of persistence diagrams as real world data always has some amount of noise present.

In 2009 Carlsson and Zomorodian began the study of multiparameter persistence with
[
9
]
.
In multiparameter persistence, the totally ordered set
[0,\infty)
is replaced with the partially ordered set
[0,\infty)^{n}
.
Filtrations indexed by
[0,\infty)^{n}
arise frequently and have a wealth of potential applications
[
3
]
.
Unfortunately, Carlsson and Zomorodian showed that there is no complete, discrete invariant for multiparameter persistent homology unlike the 1-parameter setting.
This setback has not slowed down the search for rich invariants of multiparameter persistence modules and it remains a very active area of study today.

Current approaches to multiparameter persistence revolve around partial invariants.
In their 2009 paper, Carlsson and Zomorodian proposed the rank function as an alternative to persistence diagrams.
Cerri et al. reduced the information of the rank invariant to the fibered barcode by taking linear slices of a multiparameter persistence module and computing 1-parameter persistence
[
10
,
19
]
.
Lesnick and Wright developed a software package, RIVET, for computing fibered barcodes
[
22
]
.

The algebraic-combinatorial approach to persistent homology was pioneered by Patel in
[
27
]
where persistence diagrams were constructed by a Möbius inversion.
This has allowed for typical methods of constructing persistence diagrams to be extended to multiparameter persistence modules
[
25
]
.
Since then, Möbius inversion has been used extensively throughout persistent homology
[
2
,
4
,
15
,
18
,
24
]
.
A major development with the algebraic-combinatorial approach to persistence was the introduction of the birth-death function by Henselman-Petrusek
[
16
]
.
The birth-death function plays a key role in this paper.

1.2
Our Contributions

Galois connections offer a new framework for the study of persistent homology.
This framework integrates seamlessly with the main existing algebraic-combinatorial tool used in persistent homology: Möbius inversion.
In Section
3
, we examine a relationship between Galois connections and the Möbius inversion operator.
In particular, we prove a new formulation of Rota’s Galois connection theorem (RGCT); see Theorem
3.1
.
Traditional statements of RGCT
[
17
,
29
]
involve the Möbius function while our version states RGCT directly through the Möbius inversion operator.
Our approach not only simplifies the statement of the theorem but also facilitates its use in persistent homology.

In Section
4
, we refine the category of filtrations defined over finite lattices from
[
25
]
to a category of persistence modules defined over arbitrary posets.
The morphisms in this category are given by pullbacks along right adjoints.
The main tool in this refinement is the notion of Galois connections as they yield a natural way to extend the definition of morphisms.
In section
5
, we extend the birth-death function of
[
25
]
from filtrations to persistence modules by using free presentations.
This allows us to define a notion of persistence diagram for any persistence module (satisfying a finiteness condition) over an arbitrary poset via a Möbius inversion.
We then organize persistence diagrams into a category where the morphisms are pushforwards along left adjoints.
RGCT, which implies that Möbius takes pullbacks along right adjoints to pushforwards along left adjoints, immediately implies that the assignment of a persistence diagram to every persistence module is functorial; see Proposition
5.9
.

In Section
6
, we interpret interleavings between persistence modules and matchings between persistence diagrams in our language.
Both interleavings and matchings turn out to simply be spans in their respective categories, see Figure
2
.
These notions of interleavings and matchings are significantly more flexible than traditional approaches but induce the same distance.
We exploit this flexibility together with the functoriality of persistence diagrams to give a significantly easier proof of the bottleneck stability, Theorem
7.1
.

The persistence diagram of a persistence module is a function capturing information that persists over intervals in a poset.
In order to define the persistence diagram of a module, we first need to define posets of intervals.

Definition 2.4
:

For any poset

\big\{[a,b]\in P\times P\,|\,a\leq b\big\}.

It will occasionally be useful for us to loosen the definition of a poset by dropping the antisymmetry condition.

Definition 2.5
:

A
preordered set
is a set
P
with a relation
\leq
satisfying reflexivity and transitivity.

The main reason preordered sets are sometimes useful is that colimits are easier to construct in the category of preordered sets than they are in the category of posets.
The reason for this is that the forgetful functor taking a poset to its underlying set admits a left adjoint: taking a set to its lattice of finite subsets; but not a right adjoint.
Due to this, limits of posets can be computed in the category of sets but colimits cannot.
The forgetful functor on preordered sets admits both a left adjoint: taking a set to its lattice of finite subsets; and a right adjoint: taking a set
X
to the preordered set
(X,\leq)
with
x\leq y
for all
x,y\in X
.

Preordered sets have a disadvantage that prevents us from using them more often: Möbius inversions are not unique.
This issue can be overcome with a little finesse, however it is not necessary for this paper and so we leave it out.

2.2
Galois Connections

Galois connections play a fundamental role in Möbius inversion based persistence.
They are the key component of morphisms between persistence modules as well as morphisms between persistence diagrams.

:

A

between two posets

f(p)\leq q

A

Similarly, if

]

2.3
Möbius Inversion

The Möbius inversion is the core tool at the heart of the algebraic-combinatorial approach to persistence developed in
[
27
]
.
It can be thought of as a combinatorial version of the derivative: for a function
m:P\to{\mathbb{Z}}
, its Möbius inversion
\partial m:P\to{\mathbb{Z}}
captures where, and how,
m
changes.
Here we cover only the necessary background for this paper and refer the reader to
[
29
]
for a more thorough overview.

Definition 2.12
:

Let
P
be a finite poset and
m:P\to{\mathbb{Z}}
be an integral function.
The
Möbius inversion
of
m
is the unique function
\partial m:P\to Z
satisfying

m(b)=\sum_{a\leq b}\partial m(a)

(4)

for all
b\in P
.
We refer to this equation as the Möbius inversion formula.

The relationship between a function
m
and its Möbius inversion
\partial m
is analogous to the relationship between the cumulative distribution function (CDF) and the probability density function (PDF) of a random variable.
Explicitly, if
\alpha
is an absolutely continuous probability measure on
{\mathbb{R}}
with CDF
F_{\alpha}
then its PDF is the (almost everywhere) unique function
f_{\alpha}
satisfying

F_{\alpha}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f_{\alpha}(t)\,dt.

Every function
m:P\to{\mathbb{Z}}
can be identified with a vector in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{P}
in the natural way.
Under this identification, the Möbius inversion of
m
can be calculated with a matrix multiplication as follows.
Let
\zeta
be the
P\times P
-indexed matrix defined by

\zeta_{a,b}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }a\leq b\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Multiplication by the
\zeta
matrix has the following effect on functions

m\zeta(b)=\sum_{a\leq b}m(a).

(5)

The Möbius inversion formula can now be restated as
m=(\partial m)\zeta
.
The matrix
\zeta
has an inverse matrix
\mu

[
29
]
. Therefore the Möbius inversion of
m
is
\partial m=m\mu
.
This approach implies that Möbius inversion is a linear map on the space
{\mathbb{Z}}^{P}
, a property we will make use of.

Definition 2.13
:

Let
P
and
Q
be posets and
f:P\to Q
any function.
For any
m:P\to{\mathbb{Z}}
, the
pushforward
of
m
along
f
is the function
f_{\sharp}m:Q\to{\mathbb{Z}}
given by

f_{\sharp}m:q\mapsto\sum_{p\in f^{-1}(q)}m(p).

For any
n:Q\to{\mathbb{Z}}
, the
pullback
of
n
along
f
is the function
f^{\sharp}n:P\to{\mathbb{Z}}
defined by
f^{\sharp}n(p)=\big(n\circ f\big)(p)
.

3
Rota’s Galois Connection Theorem

The following version of Rota’s Galois connection theorem is the main tool used in this paper.
This statement of the theorem was developed as a generalization of Proposition 6.6 in
[
25
]
.
An equivalent version also appears independently in
[
30
]
.
We take some liberties by referring to Theorem
3.1
as Rota’s Galois connection theorem however we believe this is justified by Remark
3.2
.

Theorem 3.1
:

Let
P
be a finite poset,
Q
be any poset, and
f:P\leftrightarrows Q:g
a Galois connection.
Then

\partial_{Q}\circ g^{\sharp}=f_{\sharp}\circ\partial_{P}.

Proof.

Fix an
m:P\to{\mathbb{Z}}
.
We will check that
f_{\sharp}(\partial_{P}(m))
satisfies the Möbius inversion formula for
g^{\sharp}(m)
.
Explicitly, we need to verify that
\sum_{a\leq b}f_{\sharp}(\partial_{P}m)(a)=m(g(b))
for any
b\in Q
. This follows from

\displaystyle\sum_{a\leq b}\big(f_{\sharp}\partial_{P}m\big)(a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\leq b}\sum_{p\in f^{-1}(a)}\partial_{P}m(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in P\\
f(p)\leq b\end{subarray}}\partial_{P}m(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\leq g(b)}\partial_{P}m(p)

\displaystyle=m(g(b)).

∎

Remark 3.2
:

The usual statement of Rota’s Galois connection theorem
[
17
,
29
]
says that if
f:P\leftrightarrows Q:g
is a Galois connection, then for any
p\in P
and
q\in Q
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in Q\\
g(v)=p\end{subarray}}\mu_{Q}(v,q)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in P\\
f(u)=q\end{subarray}}\mu_{P}(p,u).

This is equivalent to Theorem
3.1
as follows. Fix a
p\in P
and
q\in Q
. Let
\mathbf{1}_{p}:P\to{\mathbb{Z}}
be the indicator function

\mathbf{1}_{p}(u):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }u=p\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Applying Lemma
3.1
and evaluating both sides at
q
gives

\displaystyle\big(\partial(g^{\sharp}\mathbf{1}_{p})\big)(q)

\displaystyle=\big(f_{\sharp}\partial\mathbf{1}_{p}\big)(q)

\displaystyle\big((\mathbf{1}_{p}\circ g)\mu_{Q}\big)(q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in f^{-1}(q)}\partial\mathbf{1}_{p}(u)

\displaystyle\sum_{v\leq q}\mathbf{1}_{p}(g(v))\mu_{Q}(v,q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in f^{-1}(q)}\,\sum_{a\leq u}\mathbf{1}_{p}(a)\mu_{P}(a,u)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in Q\\
g(v)=p\end{subarray}}\mu_{Q}(v,q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in P\\
f(u)=q\end{subarray}}\mu_{P}(p,u).

This argument shows that Theorem
3.1
applied to indicator functions implies Rota’s theorem. The usual statement of RGCT implies Theorem
3.1
by viewing indicator functions as a basis for all functions from
P
to
{\mathbb{Z}}
together with the linearity of Möbius inversion, pushforwards, and pullbacks. For more details on the reverse direction, see
[
30
]
.

3.1
Constructible Functions

The functions arising in applied topology are typically not defined over finite posets however they do satisfy a finiteness condition that allows us to extend our tools from finite posets.

Definition 3.3
:

Let
P
be any poset,
m:P\to{\mathbb{Z}}
a function, and
S\subseteq P
a finite subposet.
We say that
m
is
S
-constructible
if there is a co-closure operator
c:P\to P
with image
S
such that
m=m\circ c
.
A function
m
is
constructible
if there is some finite poset
S
such that
m
is
S
-constructible.

While not every function defined on an infinite poset admits a Möbius inversion, all constructible functions do.

Proposition 3.4
:

Let
m:P\to{\mathbb{Z}}
be an
S
-constructible function as in Definition
3.3
.
Then
\partial m
exists and is given by
\partial m=\iota_{\sharp}(\partial m|_{S})
where
\iota
is the inclusion of
S
into
P
.

Proof.

Let
c:P\to P
be the co-closure operator on
P
with image
S
.
By Proposition
2.11
, the inclusion
\iota
has a right adjoint
\pi:P\to S
.
Moreover,
\pi(p)=c(p)
for any
p\in P
.
Therefore,
m=m\circ c=m|_{S}\circ\pi
and so by RGCT,
\partial m=\iota_{\sharp}(\partial m|_{S})
.
∎

We now extend Theorem
3.1
to the setting of constructible functions.

Proposition 3.5
:

Let
P
and
Q
be (not necessarily finite) posets with a Galois connection
f:P\leftrightarrows Q:g
.
Then for any constructible function
m:P\to{\mathbb{Z}}
,

\big(\partial_{Q}\circ g^{\sharp}\big)(m)=\big(f_{\sharp}\circ\partial_{P}\big)(m).

Proof.

By definition of constructibility, there exists a finite poset
S
, a Galois insertion
\alpha:S\leftrightarrows P:\beta
and a function
m^{\prime}:S\to{\mathbb{Z}}
with
m=m^{\prime}\circ\beta
.
Composing the Galois connections
(\alpha,\beta)
and
(f,g)
gives a Galois connection
f\circ\alpha:S\leftrightarrows Q:\beta\circ g
.
Applying Theorem
3.1
to
m^{\prime}
and reducing gives

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{Q}\circ(\beta\circ g)^{\sharp}\big)(m^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(f\circ\alpha)_{\sharp}(\partial_{S}m^{\prime})

\displaystyle\partial_{Q}(m^{\prime}\circ\beta\circ g)

\displaystyle=f_{\sharp}\big(\alpha_{\sharp}(\partial_{S}m^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle\partial_{Q}(m\circ g)

\displaystyle=f_{\sharp}(\partial_{P}m)

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{Q}\circ g^{\sharp}\big)(m)

\displaystyle=\big(f_{\sharp}\circ\partial_{P}\big)(m).

∎

4
Persistence Modules

Persistence modules are the typical starting point for studying persistent homology from a theoretical point of view.
They arise in practice by applying homology to a filtration.
In this section we define persistence modules and morphisms of persistence modules.
Subsection
4.1
concerns free persistence modules, a key step in defining birth-death functions for persistence modules.

Let
{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
be the category of finite dimensional vector spaces over a fixed field
\mathbb{K}
.

Definition 4.1
:

A functor
{\mathsf{M}}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
is
constructible
if there is a co-closure operator
c:P\to P
with finite image
S\subseteq P
such that
{\mathsf{M}}\circ c\cong{\mathsf{M}}
.
In this case, we say that
{\mathsf{M}}
is
S
-
constructible
.

In general, constructible sets need not be unique.
The following two results show that every constructible functor has a unique smallest (under the containment order) constructible set.

Lemma 4.2
:

Suppose a constructible functor
{\mathsf{M}}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
is both
S_{0}
-constructible and
S_{1}
-constructible.
Then
{\mathsf{M}}
is
S_{0}\cap S_{1}
-constructible.

Proof.

Let
c_{0},c_{1}:P\to P
be the co-closure operators with images
S_{0}
and
S_{1}
respectively.
Consider the poset map
C:=\cdots\circ c_{1}\circ c_{0}\circ c_{1}\circ c_{0}
.
We will show that
C
is a co-closure operator with image
S_{0}\cap S_{1}
.
Being the composition of deflationary maps,
C
must itself be deflationary.
Since both
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
are deflationary and their images
S_{0}
and
S_{1}
are finite, the compositions defining
C
must stabilize.
That is,
C=c_{1}\circ c_{0}\circ c_{1}\circ c_{0}\circ\cdots\circ c_{0}=c_{0}\circ c_{1}\circ c_{0}\circ\cdots\circ c_{0}
.
As a consequence of this,
C
is idempotent and so
C
is a co-closure operator.

Any element in the image of
C
must be in the image of both
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
.
Conversely, if an element
x\in P
is in the image of both
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
then by the idempotent property of co-closure operators,
c_{0}(x)=c_{1}(x)=x
and so
x
must be in the image of
C
.
Now, since
{\mathsf{M}}\circ c_{0}\cong{\mathsf{M}}
and
{\mathsf{M}}\circ c_{1}\cong{\mathsf{M}}
,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{M}}\circ C

\displaystyle\cong{\mathsf{M}}\circ c_{0}\circ c_{1}\circ c_{0}\circ\cdots\circ c_{0}

\displaystyle\cong{\mathsf{M}}\circ c_{1}\circ c_{0}\circ\cdots\circ c_{0}

\displaystyle\cong{\mathsf{M}}\circ c_{0}\circ\cdots\circ c_{0}

\displaystyle\hskip 48.36958pt\vdots

\displaystyle\cong{\mathsf{M}}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition 4.3
:

Let
{\mathsf{M}}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
be a constructible functor.
Then there exists a unique smallest set
S_{\mathsf{M}}\subseteq P
such that
{\mathsf{M}}
is
S_{\mathsf{M}}
-constructible.

Proof.

By induction, Lemma
4.2
extends to say that finite intersections of constructible sets are constructible sets.
Let
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be the set of all possible constructible sets for
{\mathsf{M}}
and
S_{\mathsf{M}}:=\bigcap_{i\in I}S_{i}
.
In general,
\mathcal{S}
will not be finite.
Choose some
S_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
.
We have that

S_{\mathsf{M}}=\bigcap_{i\in I}S_{i}=\bigcap_{i\in I}\big(S_{i}\cap S_{0}\big)

and, since
S_{0}
is a finite set, there are only finitely many possibilities for
S_{i}\cap S_{0}
.
Therefore there are only finitely many distinct terms in the intersection and so we can apply Lemma
4.2
to deduce that
{\mathsf{M}}
is
S_{\mathsf{M}}
-constructible.
∎

Remark 4.4
:

Note that there are no constructible functors defined on
{\mathbb{R}}
.
This is due to the fact that there are no co-closure operators with finite images on
{\mathbb{R}}
.
There are, however, plenty of constructible functors defined on the posets
[-\infty,\infty]
,
[-\infty,\infty)

[0,\infty]
, and
[0,\infty)
.

We now define a category of persistence modules.
The objects are functors from posets to the category of finite dimensional vector spaces.
A morphism from
{\mathsf{M}}
to
{\mathsf{N}}
can be interpreted as
{\mathsf{N}}
being a marginal of
{\mathsf{M}}
in the measure theoretic sense; see Remark
6.2
.

Definition 4.5
:

A
persistence module
is a functor
{\mathsf{M}}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
over some poset
P
. A morphism from
{\mathsf{M}}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
to

is a Galois insertion

satisfying
{\mathsf{M}}\circ g\cong{\mathsf{N}}
. Persistence modules with these morphisms form a category
{\mathsf{PMod}}
. A constructible functor
{\mathsf{M}}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
over some poset
P
is called a

. The full subcategory of
{\mathsf{PMod}}
that consists of constructible persistence modules is denoted by
{\mathsf{CPMod}}
.

Note that there is another frequently used notion of morphism between persistence modules: natural transformations.
Unfortunately, a natural transformation of persistence modules does not induce a relation between their persistence diagrams.
Despite this, natural transformations are still quite useful in this setting.
To avoid confusion with the morphisms in Definition
4.5
, we use the notation
\Rightarrow
to represent a natural transformation and
\to
to represent a morphism.

Let

4.1
Free Persistence Modules

Free persistence modules are necessary to extend the birth-death function from filtrations
[
25
]
to persistence modules. Here we introduce free persistence modules and show that every constructible persistence module admits a (finite-dimensional) free presentation.

Definition 4.9
:

A persistence module
\mathsf{F}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
is
free
if it is a finite direct sum of persistence modules of the form
\mathbb{K}^{\uparrow a}
for some
a\in P
.
A
free presentation
of a persistence module
M:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
consists of a free persistence module
\mathsf{F}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
and a surjective natural transformation
\varphi:\mathsf{F}\Rightarrow{\mathsf{M}}
.

Since we are working in the category
{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
of finite dimensional vector spaces the free presentations here must always be finite dimensional.
This differs from the setting of Miller
[
26
]
where possibly infinite dimensional free presentations are considered.

Example 4.10
:

Figure

.
The natural transformation
\varphi:\mathsf{F}\Rightarrow{\mathsf{M}}
is given by
\varphi_{a}:(x_{1},\ldots,x_{5})\mapsto(x_{1},\ldots,x_{4})
and
\varphi_{p}={\mathsf{M}}(a\leq p)\circ\varphi_{a}
for any other
p\in P
.

The following two lemmas show that free presentations behave well with morphisms of persistence modules.

Lemma 4.11
:

Let
f:P\leftrightarrows Q:g
be a Galois connection between any posets
P
and

\mathbb{K}^{\uparrow a}\circ g\cong\mathbb{K}^{\uparrow f(a)}
for any

Proof.

For any

\displaystyle\big(\mathbb{K}^{\uparrow a}\circ g\big)(q)

\displaystyle\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{K}&\text{if }a\leq g(q)\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{K}&\text{if }f(a)\leq q\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\cong\mathbb{K}^{\uparrow f(a)}(q).

∎

Lemma 4.12
:

Let
{\mathsf{M}}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
and
{\mathsf{N}}:Q\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}

{\mathsf{M}}

{\mathsf{N}}

Proof.

Since precomposition is additive, Lemma

\mathsf{F}\circ g

That

\varphi

5
Persistence Diagrams

While persistence modules are the usual starting point for theoretical foundations of persistent homology, persistence diagrams are the typical end point.
Persistence diagrams offer a “topological summary” of filtrations or persistence modules, capturing where homological features are born and die.
In this section we construct persistence diagrams out of constructible persistence modules.
We organize persistence diagrams into a category and show that the map taking a constructible persistence module to its persistence diagram is functorial.

5.1
Birth-Death and Kernel Functions

The birth-death functions used in
[
25
]
do not immediately extend to persistence modules.
There, birth-death functions are defined using the cycles and boundaries of a filtration.
This information is lost after applying homology to obtain a persistence module.
We overcome this by defining the birth-death function using free presentations.
Since free presentations are not unique, birth-death functions of a persistence module also fail to be unique.
However, after applying a Möbius inversion, different free presentations of a persistence module induce equivalent persistence diagrams – they differ only along the diagonal; see Corollary
5.8
.

Definition 5.1
:

Let
{\mathsf{M}}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
be a persistence module with a free presentation
{\varphi:\mathsf{F}\Rightarrow{\mathsf{M}}}
.
The
birth-death function
of
{\mathsf{M}}
induced by
\varphi
is the map
m_{\varphi}:\bar{P}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
defined by

m_{\varphi}:(a,b)\mapsto\dim\big(\mathsf{F}(a)\cap\ker(\varphi_{b})\big)

where the intersection is given by the pullback
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(a)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(b)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(a)\cap\ker(\varphi_{b})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ker(\varphi_{b}).}

In order to show that birth-death functions induce a well-defined notion of persistence diagram, we introduce a new function: the kernel function.
This function is closely related to the birth-death function and does not depend on a choice of free presentation.

Definition 5.2
:

For any persistence module
M:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}_{k}
its
kernel function
is the function
\ker_{M}:\bar{P}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
given by

\ker_{M}:(a,b)\mapsto\dim\big(\ker M(a\leq b)\big).

The following lemma relates the kernel function with birth-death functions.

Lemma 5.3
:

Let
{\mathsf{M}}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}_{k}
be a persistence module with a free presentation
\varphi:\mathsf{F}\Rightarrow{\mathsf{M}}
.
Then

\ker_{\mathsf{M}}(a,b)=m_{\varphi}(a,b)-m_{\varphi}(a,a)

for any
(a,b)\in\bar{P}
.

Proof.

The commutative diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(a)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(b)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{M}}(a)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{M}}(b)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(a\leq b)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{a}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{b}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{M}}(a\leq b)}

implies via a diagram chase

\ker\big({\mathsf{M}}(a\leq b)\big)\cong\frac{\mathsf{F}(a)\cap\ker(\varphi_{b})}{\ker(\varphi_{a})}

and so

\ker_{\mathsf{M}}(a,b)=\dim\big(\mathsf{F}(a)\cap\ker(\varphi_{b})\big)-\dim\big(\mathsf{F}(a)\cap\ker(\varphi_{a})\big)=m_{\varphi}(a,b)-m_{\varphi}(a,a).

∎

Proposition 5.4
:

Let
{\mathsf{M}}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
be a persistence module with a constructible set
S\subseteq P
.
There exists a free presentation
\varphi:\mathsf{F}\Rightarrow{\mathsf{M}}
such that
m_{\varphi}
is a
\bar{S}
-constructible function.

Proof.

Let
c:P\to P
be the co-closure operator with image
S
.
By Proposition
4.13
, we can choose a free presentation
\varphi:\mathsf{F}\Rightarrow{\mathsf{M}}
where
\mathsf{F}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
is an
S
-constructible persistence module.
Now since
\mathsf{F}\cong\mathsf{F}\circ c
, we have

\displaystyle m_{\varphi}(a,b)

\displaystyle=\dim\big(\mathsf{F}(a)\cap\ker(\varphi_{b})\big)

\displaystyle=\dim\Big(\mathsf{F}\big(c(a)\big)\cap\ker(\varphi_{c(b)})\Big)

\displaystyle=m_{\varphi}\big(c(a),c(b)\big)

for any
(a,b)\in\bar{P}
.
Therefore
m_{\varphi}=m_{\varphi}\circ\bar{c}
and so
m_{\varphi}
is
\bar{S}
constructible.
∎

Lemma
5.3
together with Proposition
5.4
imply that the kernel function of an
S
-constructible persistence module is
\bar{S}
-constructible.

5.2
Persistence Diagrams

Here we use birth-death functions to define a notion of persistence diagram.
We use kernel functions to show that the persistence diagrams we have defined do not depend on the choice of free resolution.

Definition 5.5
:

A
persistence diagram
is a finitely supported (not necessarily order-preserving) function
w:\bar{P}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
for some poset
P
.
Two persistence diagrams
w_{1},w_{2}:P\to{\mathbb{Z}}
are
equivalent
, if
w_{1}(I)=w_{2}(I)
for all
I\in P-\Delta_{P}
.
We write
[w]
for the equivalence class of persistence diagrams containing
w
.
A
morphism
from
[w]:\bar{P}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
to
[v]:\bar{Q}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
is a Galois insertion
f:P\leftrightarrows Q:g
such that there exist
w^{\prime}\in[w]
and
v^{\prime}\in[v]
with
\bar{f}_{\sharp}w^{\prime}=v^{\prime}

Equivalence classes of persistence diagrams with their morphisms form a category
{\mathsf{Dgm}}
.

Definition 5.6
:

Let
{\mathsf{M}}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
be a constructible persistence module with a free presentation
\varphi:\mathsf{F}\Rightarrow{\mathsf{M}}
.
The
persistence diagram of
{\mathsf{M}}
with respect to
\varphi
is the Möbius inversion of its birth-death function
\partial m_{\varphi}:\bar{P}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
.
The
persistence diagram of
{\mathsf{M}}
is the equivalence class
[\partial m_{\varphi}]
.

The persistence diagram of a constructible persistence module is uniquely defined up to equivalence; see Corollary
5.8
.
We can alternatively define the persistence diagram of
{\mathsf{M}}
as the Möbius inversion of its kernel function.
The following proposition says that this alternative definition is equivalent to the one in Definition
5.6

Proposition 5.7
:

Let
{\mathsf{M}}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
be a constructible persistence module with a free presentation
\varphi:\mathsf{F}\Rightarrow{\mathsf{M}}
.
Then
\partial m_{\varphi}
and
\partial\ker_{\mathsf{M}}
are equivalent.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.3
,
m_{\varphi}(a,b)-m_{\varphi}(a,a)=\ker_{\mathsf{M}}(a,b)
.
Let
n:\bar{P}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
be the function defined by
n:(a,b)\mapsto m_{\varphi}(a,a)
.
Then
m_{\varphi}-n=\ker_{\mathsf{M}}
and so the claim reduces to showing that
[\partial n]=[0]
.

Let
\ell:P\to{\mathbb{Z}}
be the function
\ell:a\mapsto m_{\varphi}(a,a)
.
Define
f:P\to\bar{P}
by
f(a):=(a,a)
and
g:\bar{P}\to P
by
g(b,c):=b
.
The maps
(f,g)
are a Galois connection as
(a,a)\leq(b,c)
if and only if
a\leq b
.
Since
\ell\circ g=n
, by Theorem
3.1

\partial n(a,b)=f_{\sharp}\partial\ell(a,b)=\sum_{c\in f^{-1}(a,b)}\partial\ell(c).

Now because
f^{-1}(a,b)
is empty if
a\neq b
, it follows that
\partial n(a,b)=0
if
a\neq b
and so
[\partial n]=[0]
as desired.
∎

The following corollary is an immediate consequence of Proposition
5.7
.

Corollary 5.8
:

If
{\mathsf{M}}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
is a constructible persistence module with free presentations
\varphi:\mathsf{F}\Rightarrow{\mathsf{M}}
and
\psi:\mathsf{G}\Rightarrow{\mathsf{M}}
then
[\partial m_{\varphi}]=[\partial m_{\psi}]
.

Proposition 5.9

(Functoriality)
:

Let
{\mathsf{M}}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
be a constructible persistence module with free presentation
\varphi
.
The map taking
{\mathsf{M}}
to
[\partial m_{\varphi}]
is functorial.

Proof.

Let
{\mathsf{M}}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
and
{\mathsf{N}}:Q\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
be two cosntructible persistence modules with a morphism
f:P\leftrightarrows Q:g
from
{\mathsf{M}}\to{\mathsf{N}}
. By Lemma
4.12
, a free presentation
\varphi:\mathsf{F}\Rightarrow{\mathsf{M}}
gives rise to a free presentation
\psi:\mathsf{F}\circ g\Rightarrow{\mathsf{N}}
. Now for any
(a,b)\in\bar{Q}
,

\big(m_{\varphi}\circ g\big)(a,b)=\dim\Big(\mathsf{F}\big(g(a)\big)\cap\ker\varphi_{g(b)}\Big)=n_{\psi}(a,b)

or, equivalently,
\bar{g}^{\sharp}m_{\varphi}=n_{\psi}
. Applying Proposition
3.5
gives
\partial n_{\psi}=\bar{f}_{\sharp}(\partial m_{\varphi})
and therefore,
(\bar{f},\bar{g})
is a morphism from
[\partial m_{\varphi}]
to
[\partial n_{\psi}]
.
∎

Proposition 5.10

(Positivity)
:

If
P=\{p_{1}<...<p_{n}\}
is a finite totally ordered set and
{\mathsf{M}}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
is a persistence module with free presentation
\varphi:\mathsf{F}\Rightarrow{\mathsf{M}}
then
\partial m_{\varphi}(I)\geq 0
for all
I\in\bar{P}
.

Proof.

Suppose
I=(p_{k},p_{\ell})\in\bar{P}
. By the product formula for Möbius functions
[
29
]

\partial m_{\varphi}(I)=m_{\varphi}(p_{k},p_{\ell})-m_{\varphi}(p_{k-1},p_{\ell})-m_{\varphi}(p_{k},p_{\ell-1})+m_{\varphi}(p_{k-1},p_{\ell-1}).

Moreover, we have that

\big(\mathsf{F}(p_{k-1})\cap\ker(\varphi_{p_{\ell}})\big)\cap\big(\mathsf{F}(p_{k})\cap\ker(\varphi_{p_{\ell-1}})\big)=\mathsf{F}(p_{k-1})\cap\ker(\varphi_{p_{\ell-1}}).

Now by the inclusion-exclusion formula for vector spaces,

\displaystyle\dim\Big(\big(\mathsf{F}(p_{k-1})\cap\ker(\varphi_{p_{\ell}})\big)\cup\big(\mathsf{F}(p_{k})\cap\ker(\varphi_{p_{\ell-1}})\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=m_{\varphi}(p_{k-1},p_{\ell})+m_{\varphi}(p_{k},p_{\ell-1})-m_{\varphi}(p_{k-1},p_{\ell-1})

This implies that

\partial m_{\varphi}(I)=\dim\big(\mathsf{F}(p_{k})\cap\ker(\varphi_{p_{\ell}})\big)-\dim\Big(\big(\mathsf{F}(p_{k-1})\cap\ker(\varphi_{p_{\ell}})\big)\cup\big(\mathsf{F}(p_{k})\cap\ker(\varphi_{p_{\ell-1}})\big)\Big)

and since both
\mathsf{F}(p_{k-1})\cap\ker(\varphi_{p_{\ell}})
and
\mathsf{F}(p_{k})\cap\ker(\varphi_{p_{\ell-1}})
are subspaces of
\mathsf{F}(p_{k})\cap\ker(\varphi_{p_{\ell}})
, their union is as well.
Therefore,

\dim\big(\mathsf{F}(p_{k})\cap\ker(\varphi_{p_{\ell}})\big)\geq\dim\Big(\big(\mathsf{F}(p_{k-1})\cap\ker(\varphi_{p_{\ell}})\big)\cup\big(\mathsf{F}(p_{k})\cap\ker(\varphi_{p_{\ell-1}})\big)\Big)

and so
\partial m_{\varphi}(I)\geq 0
as desired.
∎

This result extends to constructible functors
{\mathsf{M}}
defined on a (not necessarily finite) totally ordered set by Proposition
3.4
.

6
The Interleaving and Bottleneck Distances

The interleaving distance and the bottleneck distance are the two most important metrics considered in persistent homology.
They are the foundation of the most important result in the field: the bottleneck stability theorem.
While it is known that the interleaving distance between two persistence modules and the bottleneck distance between their persistence diagrams are actually equal
[
21
]
, the definitions of interleavings and matchings seem to have very little in common.
We show that interleavings and matchings are both closely related to the notion of coupling in optimal transport.

6.1
Interleavings

Here we present a new definition of interleavings, building off of Bubenik et al.
[
7
]
.
Our definition induces the same metric as the classical definition for persistence modules defined on
[0,\infty)^{n}
and highlights a deep, and previously unknown, connection between interleavings and couplings in optimal transport.

Definition 6.1
:

Let
P
and
Q
be posets embedded in some metric space
(X,d_{X})
.
An interleaving between two persistence modules
{\mathsf{M}}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
and
{\mathsf{N}}:Q\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
consists of a persistence module
\Gamma:R\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
with morphisms
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Gamma}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{M}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{N}}.}

We interpret an interleaving between
{\mathsf{M}}
and
{\mathsf{N}}
as a persistence module
\Gamma
with marginals
{\mathsf{M}}
and
{\mathsf{N}}
; see Remark
6.2
.
We can break down the definition of an interleaving to see that
\Gamma
is an interleaving if there exist Galois insertions
(f_{\mathsf{M}},g_{\mathsf{N}})
and
(f_{\mathsf{N}},g_{\mathsf{N}})
such that the solid arrows in the following diagram commute
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Q}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{\mathsf{Vec}}}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{\mathsf{M}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{\mathsf{N}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Gamma}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{M}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g_{\mathsf{M}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{N}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g_{\mathsf{N}}}

The cost of an interleaving
\Gamma
is

||\Gamma||=||f_{\mathsf{M}}-f_{\mathsf{N}}||_{\infty}=\max_{r\in R}\,d_{X}(f_{\mathsf{M}}(r),f_{\mathsf{N}}(r)).

The interleaving distance between
{\mathsf{M}}
and
{\mathsf{N}}
is

d_{I}({\mathsf{M}},{\mathsf{N}})=\inf_{\Gamma}||\Gamma||

where the infimum is taken over all interleavings between
{\mathsf{M}}
and
{\mathsf{N}}
.

Typically, the posets
P
and
Q
will both be
[0,\infty)
for one-parameter persistent homology or
[0,\infty)^{n}
for multiparameter persistent homology so the assumption that
P
and
Q
are embedded in some common metric space is quite reasonable.

Remark 6.2
:

The notion of interleaving in Definition

is closely related to the notion of couplings between probability measures used in optimal transport.
A coupling between two probability measures
\alpha
and
\beta
on

is a probability measure
\gamma
on
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}
with
\gamma(A,{\mathbb{R}})=\alpha(A)
and
\gamma({\mathbb{R}},B)=\beta(B)
for any measurable sets

and

.
Let
F_{\alpha},F_{\beta}:[-\infty,\infty]\to[0,1]
be the cummulative distribution functions(CDFs) of
\alpha
and
\beta
respectively.
We can equivalently say that a measure
\gamma
on
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}
with CDF

is a coupling if the following diagram commutes
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{[-\infty,\infty]}^{2}}

where

and

.

The maps

and

Their left adjoints are given by the projection maps

and

.
Suppose now that

, and

all have probability density functions

Then Rota’s Galois connection theorem (Theorem

) can be easily adapted to show that

Integrating then implies

.

6.2
Matchings

Just as interleavings provide a means of measuring distance between persistence modules, matchings offer a way of measuring distance between persistence diagrams.
Matchings, much like interleavings, are closely related to the notion of couplings in measure theory.

Definition 6.5
:

Let
P
and
Q
be two posets embedded in some metric space
(X,d_{X})
.
A
matching
between two equivalence classes of persistence diagrams
[w_{1}]:\bar{P}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
and
[w_{2}]:\bar{Q}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
consists of a nonnegative function
\nu:\bar{R}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
for some poset
R
with morphisms
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{[\nu]}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{[w_{1}]}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{[w_{2}]}.}

Breaking this down, a matching consists of a nonnegative function
\nu:\bar{R}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
with functions
f_{1}:R\to P
and
f_{2}:R\to Q
such that

\displaystyle[w_{1}](I)

\displaystyle=\sum_{J\in\bar{f}_{1}^{-1}(I)}\nu(J)\text{ for all }I\in\bar{P}-\Delta_{P}\text{ and}

\displaystyle[w_{2}](K)

\displaystyle=\sum_{J\in\bar{f}_{2}^{-1}(K)}\nu(J)\text{ for all }K\in\bar{Q}-\Delta_{Q}

where
\Delta_{P}=\{(p,p)\mid p\in P\}
and
\Delta_{Q}=\{(q,q)\mid q\in Q\}
.
The cost of a matching
\nu
is

||\nu||=||f_{1}-f_{2}||_{\infty}=\sup_{r\in R}d_{X}\big(f_{1}(r),f_{2}(r)\big).

The bottleneck distance between
[w_{1}]
and
[w_{2}]
is

d_{B}([w_{1}],[w_{2}])=\inf_{\nu}||\nu||

where the infimum is taken over all matchings
\nu
between
[w_{1}]
and
[w_{2}]
.

A matching between two equivalence classes of persistence diagrams exists if and only if there are nonnegative representatives of both equivalence classes; see
6.7
.

:

(0,0)

\overline{\{0\}}
to

are equivalent.
Define

be the function taking
(0,0)
to 0.
Then there exist morphisms
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{[w_{1}]}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{[w_{2}]}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{[w_{1}]}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{[w_{3}]}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{[w_{2}]}.}

Now by the triangle inequality, there is a matching between

and

.
∎

The matching constructed in Proposition

will typically not be an optimal matching.

6.3
The Triangle Inequalities

In this section we show that the interleaving and bottleneck distances satisfy the triangle inequalities.
We accomplish this by first proving two lemmas.
The first characterizes pullbacks of posets along Galois insertions.
The second shows that pulling back does not increase costs.

Lemma 6.8
:

Suppose
R_{1},R_{2}
, and
Q
are posets with Galois insertions
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Q.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g_{2}}

Then the pullback
R_{1}\times_{Q}R_{2}
is the poset

R_{1}\times_{Q}R_{2}=\{(r_{1},r_{2})\in R_{1}\times R_{2}\mid f_{1}(r_{1})=f_{2}(r_{2})\}

(6)

with the product order.
Moreover, the canonical projection maps
\pi_{1}:R_{1}\times_{Q}R_{2}\to R_{1}
and
{\pi_{2}:R_{1}\times_{Q}R_{2}\to R_{2}}
are left adjoints of Galois insertions.

Proof.

That the pullback is given by Equation
6
follows from standard results in category theory.
The right adjoints
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are not necessary for this characterization of pullbacks but are crucial to ensure that
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
have adjoints.

To see that the projection maps are left adjoints of Galois insertions, we will construct their right adjoints.
Define
\iota_{1}:R_{1}\to R_{1}\times_{Q}R_{2}
as the poset map

\iota_{1}:a\mapsto\Big(a,g_{2}\big(f_{1}(a)\big)\Big).

Since
f_{2}\circ g_{2}=\mathsf{id}_{R_{2}}
, we have
f_{1}(a)=\big(f_{2}\circ g_{2}\circ f_{1}\big)(a)
and so
\iota_{1}(a)\in R_{1}\times_{Q}R_{2}
.
The equivalencies

\displaystyle(r_{1},r_{2})\leq\iota_{1}(a)

\displaystyle\iff(r_{1},r_{2})\leq\Big(a,g_{2}\big(f_{1}(a)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\iff r_{1}\leq a\text{ and }r_{2}\leq g_{2}\big(f_{1}(a)\big)

\displaystyle\iff r_{1}\leq a\text{ and }f_{2}(r_{2})\leq f_{1}(a)

\displaystyle\iff r_{1}\leq a\text{ and }f_{1}(r_{1})\leq f_{1}(a)

\displaystyle\iff r_{1}\leq a

\displaystyle\iff\pi_{1}(r_{1},r_{2})\leq a

imply that
(\pi_{1},\iota_{1})
is a Galois connection.
That
\pi_{1}\circ\iota_{1}=\mathsf{id}_{R_{1}}
is immediate and so
(\pi_{1},\iota_{1})
is a Galois insertion. The same reasoning shows that
(\pi_{2},\iota_{2})
is a Galois insertion.
∎

Lemma 6.9
:

Let
P_{0},P_{1},P_{2}
be posets embedded into some metric space
(X,d_{X})
.
Suppose there are posets
R_{1},R_{2}
with poset maps
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P_{2}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{3}}

Let
\pi_{1}:R_{1}\times_{P_{1}}R_{2}\to R_{1}
and
\pi_{2}:R_{1}\times_{P_{1}}R_{2}\to R_{2}
be the canonical projection maps.
Then

||f_{0}\circ\pi_{1}-f_{3}\circ\pi_{2}||_{\infty}\leq||f_{0}-f_{1}||_{\infty}+||f_{2}-f_{3}||_{\infty}.

Proof.

For any
(r_{1},r_{2})\in R_{1}\times_{P_{1}}R_{2}
we have

\displaystyle d_{X}\Big(f_{0}\big(\pi_{1}(r_{1},r_{2})\big),f_{3}\big(\pi_{2}(r_{1},r_{2})\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=d_{X}\big(f_{0}(r_{1}),f_{3}(r_{2})\big)

\displaystyle\leq d_{X}\big(f_{0}(r_{1}),f_{1}(r_{1})\big)+d_{X}\big(f_{1}(r_{1}),f_{3}(r_{2})\big)

\displaystyle=d_{X}\big(f_{0}(r_{1}),f_{1}(r_{1})\big)+d_{X}\big(f_{2}(r_{2}),f_{3}(r_{2})\big).

Taking the supremum over all
(r_{1},r_{2})\in R_{1}\times_{P_{1}}R_{2}
concludes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to prove the triangle inequalities for the interleaving and bottleneck distances.

Proposition 6.10

(Triangle inequality for the interleaving distance)

Let

7
Bottleneck Stability

The bottleneck stability theorem, originally proved in
[
12
]
, is arguably the most important result in persistent homology.
Here we present a new and simpler proof using Rota’s Galois connection theorem.

Theorem 7.1

(Bottleneck Stability)
:

Let
{\mathsf{M}}_{0},{\mathsf{M}}_{1}:[0,\infty)\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
be constructible persistence modules with persistence diagrams
[\partial m_{0}]
and
[\partial m_{1}]
respectively. Then,

d_{B}([\partial m_{0}],[\partial m_{1}])\leq d_{I}({\mathsf{M}}_{0},{\mathsf{M}}_{1})

.

It may appear that functoriality (Proposition
5.9
) immediately implies the result since an interleaving
{\mathsf{M}}_{0}\leftarrow\Gamma\rightarrow{\mathsf{M}}_{1}
induces morphisms
[\partial m_{0}]\leftarrow[\partial\gamma]\rightarrow[\partial m_{1}]
.
Unfortunately though, we cannot guarantee that
[\partial\gamma]
is nonnegative.
Instead, we proceed by breaking up the interleaving
\Gamma
into simpler interleavings whose persistence diagrams we can guarantee are nonnegative.

Before we dive into the proof of Theorem
7.1
, we argue that an interleaving between
{\mathsf{M}}_{1},{\mathsf{M}}_{0}:[0,\infty)\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
with cost
\varepsilon
can be captured via an interleaving between persistence modules over finite posets with cost
\varepsilon
. To be precise, we have the following lemma.

Definition 7.5
:

Let
\{{\mathsf{M}}_{t}\}
be an interpolation between persistence
{\mathsf{M}}_{0}
and
{\mathsf{M}}_{1}
with

as defined in the proof of Lemma

.

t\in[0,1]
is

if there exist distinct pairs
(f_{0}(r),f_{1}(r))\neq(f_{0}(r^{\prime}),f_{1}(r^{\prime}))
for
r,r^{\prime}\in R
with

(1-t)f_{0}(r)+tf_{1}(r)=(1-t)f_{0}(r^{\prime})+tf_{1}(r^{\prime}).

Because there are only finitely many distinct pairs
(f_{0}(r),f_{1}(r))
for
r\in R
and the map
t\mapsto(1-t)f_{0}(r)+tf_{1}(r)
is monotone for every
r\in R
, there are finitely many critical points in an interpolation.

Proposition 7.6
:

Let
{\mathsf{M}}_{0}:S_{0}\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
and
{\mathsf{M}}_{1}:S_{1}\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
be two persistence modules interleaved by
\Gamma:R\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
and let
\{{\mathsf{M}}_{t}\}
be the interpolation induced by
\Gamma
.
If
t\in[0,1]
is a non-critical point and
c\in[0,1]
is any (possibly critical) point with no critical points strictly between
t
and
c
then there is a morphism
\alpha_{t,c}:S_{t}\leftrightarrows S_{c}:\beta_{c,t}
from
{\mathsf{M}}_{t}
to
{\mathsf{M}}_{c}
.
See Figure
9
for an illustration.

Proof.

We reuse the notation from the proof of Lemma
7.3
here and begin by defining a poset map
\alpha_{t,c}:S_{t}\to S_{c}
.
As
t
is non-critical, each
x\in S_{t}
can be written uniquely as
(1-t)f_{0}(r)+tf_{1}(r)
for some pair
(f_{0}(r),f_{1}(r))
for some
r\in R
.
We can then define
\alpha_{t,c}
as

\alpha_{t,c}:x=(1-t)f_{0}(r)+tf_{1}(r)\mapsto(1-c)f_{0}(r)+cf_{1}(r).

Since there are no critical points strictly between
t
and
c
, the map
\alpha_{t,c}
is order-preserving.
Because of this, we have that for any
A\in\mathsf{Down}(S)

\alpha_{t,c}\big(f_{t}(A)\big)=\alpha_{t,c}\Big(\max_{a\in A}\big\{(1-t)f_{0}(a)+t\,f_{1}(a)\big\}\Big)=\max_{a\in A}\big\{(1-c)f_{0}(a)+c\,f_{1}(a)\big\}=f_{c}(A).

Let
\beta_{c,t}:S_{c}\to S_{t}
be the right adjoint of
\alpha_{t,c}
. Then

\displaystyle g_{t}\big(\beta_{c,t}(x)\big)

\displaystyle=\{r\in R\mid f_{t}(\downarrow r)\leq\beta_{c,t}(x)\}

\displaystyle=\{r\in R\mid\alpha_{t,c}\big(f_{t}(\downarrow r)\big)\leq x\}

\displaystyle=\{r\in R\mid f_{c}(\downarrow r)\leq x\}

\displaystyle=g_{c}(x).

Therefore,
\tilde{\Gamma}\circ g_{t}\circ\beta_{c,t}\cong\tilde{\Gamma}\circ g_{c}
and so
{\mathsf{M}}_{t}\circ\beta_{c,t}\cong{\mathsf{M}}_{c}
which concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
7.1
.

By Lemma
7.2
, we can assume that
{\mathsf{M}}_{0}:S_{0}\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
and
{\mathsf{M}}_{1}:S_{1}\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
are persistence modules over finite subsets
S_{0},S_{1}\subseteq[0,\infty)
and we have an interleaving
\Gamma:R\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
between them with cost
\varepsilon
and
R
is a finite poset. Let
\{M_{t}\}
be an interpolation by Lemma
7.3
and let
C:=\{0=c_{0}<c_{1}<\cdots<c_{k}=1\}\subseteq[0,1]
be the set of critical points for the interpolation
\{{\mathsf{M}}_{t}\}
and choose a point
t_{i}
in the open interval
(c_{i},c_{i+1})
for each
i\in\{0,1,\ldots k-1\}
.
By Proposition
7.6
, there are morphisms
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{M}}_{t_{0}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{M}}_{t_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{M}}_{c_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{M}}_{c_{2}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{M}}_{1}.}

Here we have broken down the interleaving
\Gamma
into a sequence of interleavings
{\mathsf{M}}_{t_{i}}
indexed by totally ordered sets.
By Proposition
5.9
, this induces a sequence of morphisms
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{[\partial m_{t_{0}}]}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{[\partial m_{t_{1}}]}}

5.10
, each

Then the cost of the matching

is

\displaystyle||\alpha_{t_{i},c_{i}}-\alpha_{t_{i},c_{i+1}}||_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\max_{r\in R}\big|(1-c_{i})f_{0}(r)+c_{i}f_{1}(r)-\big((1-c_{i+1})f_{0}(r)+c_{i+1}f_{1}(r)\big)\big|

8
Multiparameter Persistence Diagrams

In this section we use Rota’s Galois connection theorem to establish a relationship between rank function persistence diagrams and birth-death function persistence diagrams.
We then show that the fibered barcode can be obtained from the birth-death function persistence diagram in linear time.

8.1
Rank Functions

Persistence diagrams have been historically defined using the rank function rather than birth-death functions
[
12
,
27
]
.
The disadvantage of the rank function is that its natural domain is the poset
\hat{P}
rather than
\bar{P}
; see Definition
2.4
.
While a Galois connection between posets
P
and
Q
induces a Galois connection between
\bar{P}
and
\bar{Q}
, it fails to induce a Galois connection between
\hat{P}
and
\hat{Q}
.
In fact, order preserving maps from
P
to
Q
in general do not induce order preserving maps from
\hat{P}
to
\hat{Q}
.
For this reason, birth-death functions are much more natural to work with in our language.

Definition 8.1
:

The
rank function
of a persistence module
{\mathsf{M}}:P\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
is the map
{{\mathsf{rk\,}}}_{\mathsf{M}}:\hat{P}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
defined by

{{\mathsf{rk\,}}}_{\mathsf{M}}:(a,b)\mapsto\dim\Big(\mathsf{im}\big({\mathsf{M}}(a\leq b)\big)\Big).

Remark 8.2
:

For any persistence module
{\mathsf{M}}
, its kernel function and rank function carry the same information. This follows from the rank-nullity theorem:

\dim{\mathsf{M}}(a)={{\mathsf{rk\,}}}_{\mathsf{M}}(a,b)+\ker_{\mathsf{M}}(a,b)

for any
a\leq b
.
Using the fact that
\dim{\mathsf{M}}(a)={{\mathsf{rk\,}}}_{\mathsf{M}}(a,a)=\ker_{\mathsf{M}}(a,\infty)
we get

{{\mathsf{rk\,}}}_{\mathsf{M}}(a,b)=\ker_{\mathsf{M}}(a,\infty)-\ker_{\mathsf{M}}(a,b)\hskip 28.45274pt\text{and}\hskip 28.45274pt\ker_{\mathsf{M}}(a,b)={{\mathsf{rk\,}}}_{\mathsf{M}}(a,a)-{{\mathsf{rk\,}}}_{\mathsf{M}}(a,b).

8.2
The Fibered Barcode

The fibered barcode
[
10
,
22
]
captures information about a multiparameter persistence module
{\mathsf{M}}:[0,\infty)^{n}\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
by restricting
{\mathsf{M}}
to linear slices. Along a fixed line
\ell\subseteq[0,\infty)^{n}
with nonnegative slope, the persistence module
{\mathsf{M}}|_{\ell}
is a 1-parameter persistence module and so any of the usual methods of obtaining a persistence diagram can be implemented. The fibered barcode of
{\mathsf{M}}
is the set of all persistence diagrams over each linear slice with nonnegative slope.

For a fixed line
\ell\subseteq[0,\infty)^{n}
with nonnegative slope, let
\iota:[0,\infty)\hookrightarrow[0,\infty)^{n}
be the inclusion of
\ell
into
[0,\infty)^{n}
. Since
\ell
has nonnegative slope,
\iota
is an order-preserving map. Moreover,
\iota
has a left adjoint
\pi:[0,\infty)^{n}\to\ell
defined by
\pi(x)=\max\{a\in\ell\mid\iota(a)\leq x\}
; see Figure
10
.

Proposition 8.4
:

The persistence diagram of the 1-parameter persistence module
{\mathsf{M}}|_{\ell}
is given by
[\pi_{\sharp}(\partial m)]
.

Proof.

We have that
{\mathsf{M}}|_{\ell}\cong{\mathsf{M}}\circ\iota
which is a morphism from
{\mathsf{M}}
to
{\mathsf{M}}_{\ell}
and so, by functoriality,
[\pi_{\sharp}(\partial m)]=[\partial(m|_{\ell})]
.
∎

In particular, this proof gives a linear time algorithm for computing the fibered barcode from the birth-death function persistence diagram.
