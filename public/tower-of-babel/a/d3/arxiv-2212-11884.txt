The central limit theorem via doubling of variables
Louigi Addario-Berry, Gavin Barill, Erin Beckman, Jessica Lin

Abstract
. We give a new, self-contained proof of the multidimensional central limit theorem using the technique of “doubling variables,” which is traditionally used to prove uniqueness of solutions of partial differential equations (PDEs). Our technique also yields quantitative bounds for random variables with finite 2 + γ 2+\gamma moment for some γ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] \gamma\in(0,1] ; when γ = 1 \gamma=1 , this proves a version of the Berry–Esseen theorem in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} .

1.
Introduction

The central limit theorem, under its moniker of “the bell curve,” is perhaps the best-known mathematical result outside of the mathematical sciences. The purpose of this paper is to provide a new, self-contained proof of the central limit theorem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
via
doubling of variables
, a key technique for establishing uniqueness of solutions of first- and second-order PDEs
[
13
,
8
]
.
Some of the authors of this article have recently used related techniques from the analysis of PDEs to prove distributional convergence for several random processes
[
3
,
1
,
2
]
. We believe that this family of methods will be broadly useful in probabilistic settings, and the central limit theorem seems to us a useful proving ground.

Throughout the paper we let
(X_{i},i\geq 1)
be
iid
, centered,
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued random variables with non-degenerate, finite covariance matrix
\Sigma
, defined on a common probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, and for integers
n\geq 1
we set
S_{n}=X_{1}+\ldots+X_{n}
.

We prove the central limit theorem in the following form.

Theorem 1.1
.

For all bounded, uniformly continuous
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,

(1.1)

\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(\frac{S_{n}}{n^{1/2}}\right)\right]\to\mathbb{E}\left[f(\xi)\right]\,

as
n\to\infty
, where
\xi\sim\normal(0,\Sigma)
.

The notation
\xi\sim\normal(0,\Sigma)
means that
\xi
is a centered Gaussian with covariance matrix
\Sigma
. By the Portmanteau theorem, the above formulation is equivalent to the formulation in terms of cumulative distribution functions.

We write
C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for the set of functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
for which all partial derivatives of order up to
k
exist and are uniformly bounded. For test functions
f\in C^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
which vanish at infinity, our proof technique directly yields a quantitative rate of convergence in (
1.1
) as soon as the random variables
X_{i}
have a
2+\gamma
moment for some
\gamma>0
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For all
f\in C^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
|f(x)|\to 0
as
|x|\to\infty
, there exists
C=C(d,\|f\|_{C^{4}},\Sigma)
such that for all
\gamma\in(0,1]
, for all
n\geq 1
,

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(\frac{S_{n}}{n^{1/2}}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[f(\xi)\right]\right|\leq\frac{C\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{1}|^{2+\gamma}|\right]}{n^{\gamma/2}}\,,

where
\xi\sim\normal(0,\Sigma)
.

The
C^{4}
norm
\|f\|_{C^{4}}
appearing in the statement is defined in (
1.7
), below.
When
\gamma=1
, Theorem
1.2
is a version of the multidimensional Berry-Esseen theorem
[
5
,
11
]
. For
\gamma\in(0,1]
, this is a version of a result of Bhattacharya and Rao
[
6
, Theorem 18.1, Corollaries 18.2-3]
; however, their result allows for more general test functions
f
and for steps which may not be identically distributed; this leads to more complicated dependency on the tail behaviour of the
X_{i}
and of
f
.

Here is the key idea underlying the proof.
For any bounded continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and any integer
n\geq 1
, define a function
u_{n}=u_{n,f}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}

(1.2)

u_{n}\left(x,t\right):=\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(x+\mbox{\large$\tfrac{S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}{n^{1/2}}$}\right)\right].

Note that
u_{n}
is continuous in
x
, while for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
u_{n}(x,\cdot)
is constant on time intervals of the form
[kn^{-1},(k+1)n^{-1})
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}=\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}
.
For
f
and
u_{n}=u_{n,f}
as above, the following proposition is straightforward.

Proposition 1.1
.

For all
t\geq 0
and all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(1.3)

\displaystyle u_{n}(x,t+n^{-1})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[u_{n}(x+n^{-1/2}X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1},t)\right]

Proof.

By definition,

u_{n}(x,t+n^{-1})=\mathbb{E}\left[f(x+n^{-1/2}S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(x+{n^{-1/2}}\left(S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}+X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1}\right)\right)\right].

Since
X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1}
is independent of
S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}
, writing
\mathcal{L}
for the law of
X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1}
(which is also the law of
X_{i}
for all
i\geq 1
), Fubini’s theorem yields that

\displaystyle u_{n}(x,t+{n^{-1}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(x+{n^{-1/2}}r+{n^{-1/2}}S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\right)\right]\,\mathcal{L}(dr)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u_{n}(x+{n^{-1/2}}r,t)\,\mathcal{L}(dr)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[u_{n}(x+{n^{-1/2}}X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1},t)\right].\qed

Subtracting
u_{n}(x,t)
from each side of (
1.3
) and dividing by
n^{-1}
yields

(1.4)

\frac{u_{n}\left(x,t+n^{-1}\right)-u_{n}\left(x,t\right)}{n^{-1}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{u_{n}\left(x+n^{-1/2}X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1},t\right)-u_{n}\left(x,t\right)}{(n^{-1/2})^{2}}\,\right].

The left-hand side looks like a discrete time derivative, while the right-hand side is, in fact, acting like a second-order discrete space derivative. Indeed, if
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then also
u_{n}(\cdot,t)\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and one can perform a Taylor approximation of
u_{n}
on the right-hand side of (
1.4
). Since
X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1}
is centered, the expectation then causes the first-order term of the Taylor expansion to vanish.
More precisely, writing
D^{2}u_{n}
to denote the Hessian of
u_{n}
in the spatial coordinates,
we show in Lemma
2.3
that the right-hand side closely approximates half the trace of
\Sigma D^{2}u_{n}(x,t)
: for any
T>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{u_{n}\left(x+n^{-1/2}X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1},t\right)-u_{n}\left(x,t\right)}{(n^{-1/2})^{2}}\right]-\frac{1}{2}\tr(\Sigma D^{2}u_{n}(x,t))\right|=0\,.

Thus, if as
n\to\infty
,
u_{n}
converges to some limiting function
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
, then in principle,
u
should satisfy the PDE

(1.5)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=\frac{1}{2}\tr(\Sigma D^{2}u)&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty),$}\\
u(x,0)=f(x)&\text{on $\mathbb{R}^{d}$}\,.\end{cases}

This is nothing but the heat equation; its
unique solution
u=u_{f}
is given by

(1.6)

u(x,t)=\mathbb{E}\left[f(x+t^{1/2}\xi)\right]\,,

where
\xi\sim\normal(0,\Sigma)
.
The function
u
defined by (
1.6
) is
twice continuously differentiable in space and continuously differentiable in time for
t>0
; we reserve the notation
C^{2,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty))
for such functions.
1
1

1

In fact,
u
is infinitely differentiable in both space and time, but we do not need this.
Thus, we can establish the CLT by showing that
(
1.4
) is indeed a good approximation of (
1.5
). By the definitions of
u_{n,f}
and
u_{f}
, proving (
1.1
) is equivalent to proving that
u_{n,f}(0,1)\rightarrow u_{f}(0,1)
as
n\to\infty
for all bounded uniformly continous
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
.
This is where doubling of variables comes in.

To introduce the technique, we use a simplified version of it (which does not actually involve any doubling of variables) to prove the uniqueness of solutions to (
1.5
) among
C^{2}
functions which vanish at infinity. Uniqueness in fact holds within a much broader class, but proving this is more involved and less useful for the expository purposes of this article.

Proposition 1.2
.

Fix continuous
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
with
|f(x)|\to 0
as
|x|\to\infty
, fix
T>0
, and let
u,v\in C^{2,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty))
be such that

\limsup_{|x|\to\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\max(|u(x,t)|,|v(x,t)|)=0\,.

If
u(x,0)=f(x)=v(x,0)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\partial_{t}u\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\tr(\Sigma D^{2}u)
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty)

and
\partial_{t}v\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\tr(\Sigma D^{2}v)
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty)
,
then
u\geq v
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]
.

This establishes the claimed uniqueness of solutions to (
1.5
) since if
\partial_{t}u=\tfrac{1}{2}\tr(\Sigma D^{2}u)

and
\partial_{t}v=\tfrac{1}{2}\tr(\Sigma D^{2}v)
, then the proposition implies that
u\geq v
, and by swapping the roles of
u
and
v
in the proposition, we see that also
v\geq u
.

Proof of Proposition
1.2
.

Let
T>0
be as in the statement of the proposition, and let
\sigma:=\sup(\left(v(x,t)-u(x,t)\right)_{+}:(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T])
. If
\sigma=0
, then the claim is immediate, so instead assume that
\sigma>0
. Let

\Psi(x,t):=v(x,t)-u(x,t)-t\cdot\sigma/(2T).

Then
\Psi(x,0)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and

\Psi^{*}:=\sup(\Psi(x,t):(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T])\geq\sigma/2
.

Observe that if
((x^{\ell},t^{\ell}),\ell\geq 1)
is any sequence of elements in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]
such that
|x^{\ell}|\to\infty
as
\ell\to\infty
, then
v(x^{\ell},t^{\ell})\to 0
and
u(x^{\ell},t^{\ell})\to 0
, and thus
\limsup\Psi(x^{\ell},t^{\ell})\leq 0
. Now suppose that
((x^{\ell},t^{\ell}),\ell\geq 1)
is a sequence such that
\Psi(x^{\ell},t^{\ell})\to\Psi^{*}
; then since
\Psi^{*}>0
, we conclude that
(x^{\ell},\ell\geq 1)
is bounded, so there exists a subsequence of
((x^{\ell},t^{\ell}),\ell\geq 1)
which converges to a point
(x^{*},t^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]
. By continuity, the limit satisfies
\Psi(x^{*},t^{*})=\Psi^{*}
, so since
\Psi(x,0)=0
for all
x
we conclude that
t^{*}>0
.

Since
\Psi
is maximized at
(x^{*},t^{*})
, necessarily
\partial_{t}\Psi(x^{*},t^{*})\geq 0
, with equality if
t^{*}<T
; by the definition of
\Psi
this yields that

\partial_{t}v(x^{*},t^{*})-\partial_{t}u(x^{*},t^{*})\geq\sigma/(2T).

By the assumptions on
u
and
v
, the preceding inequality implies that

\tr(\Sigma D^{2}v(x^{*},t^{*}))-\tr(\Sigma D^{2}u(x^{*},t^{*}))\geq\sigma/T.

To complete the proof, note that since
\Psi
is maximized at
(x^{*},t^{*})
, the Hessian of
\Psi
in the spatial variable must be non-positive semidefinite, i.e.
D^{2}\Psi(x^{*},t^{*})\preceq 0
.
Since
\Sigma
is positive semidefinite, it follows that
\Sigma D^{2}\Psi(x^{*},t^{*})\preceq 0
,
so

\tr(\Sigma D^{2}\Psi(x^{*},t^{*}))=\tr(\Sigma D^{2}u(x^{*},t^{*}))-\tr(\Sigma D^{2}v(x^{*},t^{*}))\leq 0\,.

Combined with the prior display, this contradicts the assumption that
\sigma>0
.
∎

To prove the central limit theorem, we wish to use the same style of argument as in the above proof, with
u=u_{f}
the solution of the heat equation (
1.5
) but with
v
replaced by the approximation
u_{n}=u_{n,f}
. The idea is still to consider the location where a function of the form
u_{n}(x,t)-u(x,t)-ct
is maximized, then argue that the maximum must be small. However, since the time variable in
u_{n}
is effectively discrete, and that in
u
is continuous, the cost of replacing
v
by
u_{n}
is that we need to replace the function
\Phi
in the proof by a function with two time variables; we use a function of the form

\Phi_{n}(x,t,s)=u_{n}(x,t)-u(x,s)-c_{n}(t+s)-C_{n}(t-s)^{2},

for suitably chosen constants
c_{n},C_{n}
.
The role of the additional term
C_{n}(t-s)^{2}
is a penalization to enforce that the maximum of this function is achieved at a point where the difference
|t-s|
is small. The details of this argument appear in the proof of Proposition
3.1
, below.

The question of whether the function
u_{n}
approximates
u
can also be posed in the framework of
convergence of finite difference schemes
. A fundamental issue in the numerical analysis of PDEs is to construct discrete, finite difference schemes which correctly approximate the true solution of a given PDE. Indeed, our proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
are inspired by the work of Crandall and Lions
[
9
, Theorem 1]
, where the authors obtain a quantitative estimate measuring the difference between discrete functions
u_{n}
satisfying a monotone finite difference scheme and the viscosity solution
u
of a first-order nonlinear Hamilton-Jacobi equation. For second-order fully nonlinear equations, the question of convergence of finite difference schemes (and not rates of convergence) was established by Barles and Souganidis
[
4
]
. In our setting, the proofs are much more straightforward than those in
[
9
]
; the fact that
u_{n}
is continuous and regular (differentiable) in the spatial argument and the linearity of the PDE both simplify the arguments. This also allows us to prove our results without introducing the machinery of viscosity solutions.

Related work

The history of the development of the CLT is described in the book
[
12
]
. In addition to the classical proof based on characteristic functions, a number of others have appeared in the literature. The paper
[
10
]
, which itself provides a new proof based on expanding the random variables in the statement of the CLT with respect to the Haar basis, also describes other proofs of the CLT, including those by Berry
[
5
]
and Trotter
[
14
]
; another new, elegant proof recently appeared in
[
7
]
.
The perspective taken in this paper is inspired by the recent works
[
3
,
1
,
2
]
, which introduced the idea of using probabilistic interpretations of approximation schemes for PDEs in order to prove convergence in distribution for random processes. The most similar argument to our own that we have found in the literature is a proof of the one-dimensional CLT for
iid
random variables with finite third moments, by Zong and Hu
[
15
]
. Their argument is rather different from ours, and in particular, it uses as input the theory of viscosity solutions and regularity theory of parabolic PDEs, whereas ours is self-contained. However, their proof is similar to ours in that its fundamental idea is also to use the connection to the heat equation.

Notation

Write
\mathbb{S}^{d}
for the set of symmetric
d\times d
real matrices. For functions
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
, we view
[0,\infty)
as a time dimension, and write
Du\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
D^{2}u\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
to denote the gradient and Hessian of
u
with respect to space, when these are defined.

A
multi-index

\alpha
is a
d
-tuple of nonnegative integers
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{d})
. For a multi-index
\alpha
, we define

|\alpha|=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i},\quad\partial^{\alpha}f=\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|}f}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\partial x_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\dots\partial x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}}.

When
|\alpha|=0
we take
\partial^{\alpha}f=f
by definition.
For
f\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we define the
C^{k}
norm of
f
by

(1.7)

\|f\|_{C^{k}}=\sum_{0\leq|\alpha|\leq k}\|\partial^{\alpha}f\|_{\infty}.

If
f\not\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
then we set
\|f\|_{C^{k}}=\infty
.
Note that
\|f\|_{C^{0}}=\|f\|_{\infty}
and that
\|f\|_{C^{k}}
is non-decreasing in
k
.

For a matrix
M\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
, we use the the notation

\lambda_{\max}(M)=\max(|\lambda|:\lambda\mbox{ is an eigenvalue of }M)\,.

Finally, we write
\mathbb{Z}^{+}=\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}
, and let
\llbracket n\rrbracket=\{0,1,\dots,n\}
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.

2.
Approximation Results for Discrete Derivatives

In this section, we prove a series of lemmas which control “discrete derivative” expressions in terms of their true derivatives.
First, note that if
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
then by the definition of
u=u_{f}
and the dominated convergence theorem,

\displaystyle\limsup_{|h|\downarrow 0}\frac{u(x+h,t)-u(x,t)}{|h|}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{|h|\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|f(x+h+t^{1/2}\xi)-f(x+t^{1/2}\xi)|}{|h|}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\limsup_{|h|\downarrow 0}\frac{|f(x+h+t^{1/2}\xi)-f(x+t^{1/2}\xi)|}{|h|}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{C^{1}}\,,

which implies that
\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{C^{1}}\leq\|f\|_{C^{1}}
, and a similar argument shows that
\|u_{n}(\cdot,t)\|_{C^{1}}\leq\|f\|_{C^{1}}
.
These inequalities also automatically hold if
f\not\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, since in this case
\|f\|_{C^{1}}=\infty
.
More generally, for all integers
\ell\geq 1
and all
t\geq 0
we have

(2.1)

\|u_{n}(\cdot,t)\|_{C^{\ell}}\vee\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{C^{\ell}}\leq\|f\|_{C^{\ell}}.

This in particular implies that if
\|f\|_{C^{2}}<\infty
then the Hessians
D^{2}u_{n}
and
D^{2}u
are both defined.

We next prove a lemma which we will use to obtain bounds on finite differences of
u_{n}
and its derivatives, as well as a bound on the modulus of continuity of
u
.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is Borel measurable and
Y
is a square-integrable random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with

\mathbb{E}[Y]=0
,
\cov(Y)=\Sigma
, and
\mathbb{E}[|g(Y)|]<\infty
, then

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathbb{E}\left[g(x+Y)-g(x)\right]|\leq\|g\|_{C^{2}}\tr(\Sigma)/2\,.

Proof.

If
\|g\|_{C^{2}}=\infty
then the bound is automatic so we may assume
\|g\|_{C^{2}}<\infty
. By Taylor’s theorem, we may then write

g(x+Y)-g(x)=\langle Dg(x),Y\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle D^{2}g(x+CY)Y,Y\rangle\,,

for some
C=C(Y)\in[0,1]
; it is not hard to see that
C
may be chosen as a Borel measurable function of
Y
. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[g(x+Y)-g(x)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\langle Dg(x),Y\rangle]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[\langle(D^{2}g(x+CY))Y,Y\rangle]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[{\langle(D^{2}g(x+CY))Y,Y\rangle}]\,,

the second equality holding since
\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right]=0
. Since
|\langle(D^{2}g(x+CY))Y,Y\rangle|\leq\|g\|_{C^{2}}|Y|^{2}
, it follows that

|\mathbb{E}\left[g(x+Y)-g(x)\right]|\leq\|g\|_{C^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[|Y|^{2}\right]/2=\|g\|_{C^{2}}\tr(\Sigma)/2\,.\qed

Corollary 2.2
.

Fix a bounded continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and write
u_{n}=u_{n,f}
and
u=u_{f}
. Then for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t\geq 0
, we have

|u_{n}(x,t+n^{-1})-u_{n}(x,t)|\leq\|f\|_{C^{2}}\tr(\Sigma)/(2n)\,,

and if
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, so that
D^{2}u_{n}
is defined, then also

\left|\tr(D^{2}u_{n}(x,t+{n^{-1}}))-\tr(D^{2}u_{n}(x,t))\right|\leq d\cdot\|f\|_{C^{4}}\tr(\Sigma)/(2n)\,.

Finally, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t,h\geq 0
,

|u(x,t+h)-u(x,t)|\leq h\|f\|_{C^{2}}\tr(\Sigma)/2.

Proof.

Fix
t\geq 0
and define
g(x)=u_{n}(x,t)
. Then by (
2.1
) we have

\|g\|_{C^{2}}\leq\|f\|_{C^{2}}
,
so applying
Lemma
2.1
with this choice of
g
and with
Y=X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1}n^{-1/2}
yields that

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[u_{n}(x+X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1}{n^{-1/2}},t)-u_{n}(x,t)\right]\right|\leq\|f\|_{C^{2}}\tr(\Sigma)/(2n);

note that
\cov{Y}=n^{-1}\cov{X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1}}
.
By (
1.3
), we have
\mathbb{E}\left[u_{n}(x+X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1}{n^{-1/2}},t)\right]=u_{n}(x,t+{n^{-1}})
and the first bound now follows.

For the second inequality, we assume
\|f\|_{C^{4}}<\infty
since otherwise the bound is automatic. Fix
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, define
g(x)=\tfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}u_{n}(x,t)
, and note that
\|g\|_{C^{2}}\leq\|f\|_{C^{4}}
by the same reasoning as for (
2.1
). Then again applying Lemma
2.1
, with this choice of
g
and with
Y=X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1}n^{-1/2}
, we obtain

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}u_{n}(x+X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1}{n^{-1/2}},t)-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}u_{n}(x,t)\right]\right|\leq\|f\|_{C^{4}}\tr(\Sigma)/(2n)\,.

Next, since
u_{n}(x,t+{n^{-1}})=\mathbb{E}\left[u_{n}(x+X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1}{n^{-1/2}},t)\right]
by (
1.3
), by differentiating under the integral sign we have

D^{2}u_{n}(x,t+{n^{-1}})-D^{2}u_{n}(x,t)=\mathbb{E}\left[D^{2}u_{n}(x+X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1}{n^{-1/2}},t)-D^{2}u_{n}(x,t)\right]\,;

the right-hand side is a matrix with

\mathbb{E}\left[\tfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(u_{n}(x+X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1}n^{-1/2},t)-u_{n}(x,t))\right]
as its
(i,j)
entry. (Differentiation under the integral sign is justified by (
2.1
) and the assumption that
\|f\|_{C^{4}}<\infty
.)
By the preceding displayed equation, the entries of this matrix are all at most
\|f\|_{C^{4}}\tr(\Sigma)/(2n)
in absolute value; since the trace is the sum of the diagonal entries, the second bound of the corollary follows.

For the last bound, letting
\xi
and
\xi^{\prime}
be independent and
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
-distributed, then
h^{1/2}\xi+t^{1/2}\xi^{\prime}
has the same distribution as
(h+t)^{1/2}\xi
. Since
f
is bounded, we may thus apply Fubini’s theorem to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[u(x+h^{1/2}\xi^{\prime},t)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[f(x+h^{1/2}\xi^{\prime}+t^{1/2}\xi)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[f(x+(h\!+\!t)^{1/2}\xi^{\prime})\right]=u(x,t\!+\!h)

The final bound of the corollary then follows from Lemma
2.1
applied with
g(x)=u(x,t)
and
Y=h^{1/2}\xi^{\prime}
,
which has covariance matrix
h\Sigma
.
∎

We conclude this section with a lemma which allows us to formalize the Taylor expansion argument from the introduction. We also provide a quantitative version in the case when
X_{i}
have a
2+\gamma
moment.

Lemma 2.3
.

Fix
f\in C^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and write
u_{n}=u_{n,f}
. Fix
T>0
and define

\varepsilon_{n}=\sup_{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]}\bigg|\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{u_{n}(x+X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1}{n^{-1/2}},t)-u_{n}(x,t)}{n^{-1}}\right]-\frac{1}{2}\tr(\Sigma D^{2}u_{n}(x,t))\bigg|.

Then
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, and there exists
C=C(d)>0
such that for all
\gamma\in(0,1]
, it holds that
\varepsilon_{n}\leq C\|f\|_{C^{3}}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{1}|^{2+\gamma}\right]n^{-\gamma/2}
.

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

Define
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
by

(2.2)

g(h,x,t)=u_{n}(x+h,t)-u_{n}(x,t)-\langle Du_{n}(x,t),h\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle D^{2}u_{n}(x,t)h,h\rangle.

By Taylor’s theorem and (
2.1
), there exists
C=C(d)
such that for every
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]
,

(2.3)

|g(h,x,t)|\leq C\|u_{n}\|_{C^{3}}(|h|^{3}\wedge|h|^{2})\leq C\|f\|_{C^{3}}(|h|^{3}\wedge|h|^{2})\leq C\|f\|_{C^{3}}(|h|^{2+\gamma}\wedge|h|^{2}).

In this paragraph, it is convenient to write
X=X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1}
for simplicity.
Substituting
h=X{n^{-1/2}}
into (
2.2
) yields

\displaystyle g(Xn^{-1/2}

\displaystyle,x,t)

\displaystyle=u_{n}(x+X{n^{-1/2}},t)-u_{n}(x,t)-\frac{\langle Du_{n}(x,t),X\rangle}{n^{1/2}}-\frac{\langle D^{2}u_{n}(x,t)X,X\rangle}{2n}.

Note that
\mathbb{E}[\langle Du_{n}(x,t),X\rangle]=0
since
\mathbb{E}[X]=0
. Dividing both sides by
n^{-1}
and taking expectations, we thus obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{g(Xn^{-1/2},x,t)}{n^{-1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{u_{n}(x+X{n^{-1/2}},t)-u_{n}(x,t)}{n^{-1}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\langle D^{2}u_{n}(x,t)X,X\rangle}{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{u_{n}(x+X{n^{-1/2}},t)-u_{n}(x,t)}{n^{-1}}\right]-\frac{1}{2}\tr(\Sigma D^{2}u_{n}(x,t)),

and this equality and the fact that
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}X_{1}
together imply that

(2.4)

\varepsilon_{n}\leq\sup_{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\left|\frac{g(X_{1}{n^{-1/2}},x,t)}{n^{-1}}\right|\bigg].

If
\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{1}|^{2+\gamma}\right]<\infty
for some value
\gamma\in(0,1]
, then using that
|g(h,x,t)|\leq C\|f\|_{C^{3}}|h|^{2+\gamma}
from (
2.3
), the bound (
2.4
) yields that

\varepsilon_{n}\leq\sup_{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[C\|f\|_{C^{3}}|X_{1}|^{2+\gamma}n^{-\gamma/2}\right]=C\|f\|_{C^{3}}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{1}|^{2+\gamma}\right]n^{-\gamma/2}\,,

and this yields the second claim of the result.

To complete the proof of the first claim, it suffices to show that in all cases

(2.5)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\left|\frac{g(X_{1}{n^{-1/2}},x,t)}{n^{-1}}\right|\bigg]=0.

To this end, fix
\delta>0
and choose a sequence
((x_{n}^{\delta},t_{n}^{\delta}),n\geq 1)
of elements of
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]
such that for all
n\geq 1
,

\sup_{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\left|\frac{g(X_{1}{n^{-1/2}},x,t)}{n^{-1}}\right|\bigg]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{g(X_{1}{n^{-1/2}},x_{n}^{\delta},t_{n}^{\delta})}{n^{-1}}\right|\right]+\delta.

Note that by the bound
|g(h,x,t)|\leq C\|f\|_{C^{3}}|h|^{2}
from (
2.3
), we have

\left|\frac{g(X_{1}{n^{-1/2}},x_{n}^{\delta},t_{n}^{\delta})}{n^{-1}}\right|\leq\frac{C\|f\|_{C^{3}}|X_{1}{n^{-1/2}}|^{2}}{n^{-1}}=C\|f\|_{C^{3}}|X_{1}|^{2}.

Because
\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{1}|^{2}\right]<\infty
, we may thus apply the dominated convergence theorem, then use the bound
|g(h,x,t)|\leq C\|f\|_{C^{3}}|h|^{3}
from (
2.3
), to obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\llbracket 2n\rrbracket}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\left|\frac{g(X_{1}{n^{-1/2}},x,t)}{n^{-1}}\right|\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\left|\frac{g(X_{1}{n^{-1/2}},x_{n}^{\delta},t_{n}^{\delta})}{n^{-1}}\right|\bigg]+\delta

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{g(X_{1}{n^{-1/2}},x_{n}^{\delta},t_{n}^{\delta})}{n^{-1}}\right|\right]+\delta

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\limsup_{n\to\infty}C\|f\|_{C^{3}}|X_{1}|^{3}{n^{-1/2}}\right]+\delta

\displaystyle=\delta,

where for the last equality we used that
|X_{1}|^{3}{n^{-1/2}}\xrightarrow{a.s.}0
. Since
\delta>0
was arbitrary, this establishes (
2.5
) and completes the proof.
∎

3.
Doubling of variables

In this section, we carry out the doubling of variables argument discussed in the introduction. The following proposition almost establishes Theorem

, but for a slightly more restrictive class of test functions

Proposition 3.1
.

Fix

with

as

. Then writing
u_{n}=u_{n,f}
and

(3.1)

\sup_{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,2]}|u_{n}(x,t)-u(x,t)|\to 0\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Moreover, there exists

such that for all

Recall that we write
\llbracket n\rrbracket=\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
for

. Let

Let

The following lemma will be used in the proof.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
\phi_{n}
defined above,
\sup_{(x,k,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\llbracket 2n\rrbracket\times[0,2]}\phi_{n}(x,k,s)>\sigma_{n}/2
. Also, there exists
C=C(d,\|f\|_{C^{2}},\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma))>0
such that for all
n
, for any point
(x_{0},k_{0},s_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\llbracket 2n\rrbracket\times[0,2]
such that

(3.2)

\phi_{n}(x_{0},k_{0},s_{0})=\sup_{(x,k,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\llbracket 2n\rrbracket\times[0,2]}\phi_{n}(x,k,s),

it holds that

In the coming proofs, for a function

, we write

to mean there exists

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}n^{1/2}|a(n)|\leq C

Proof.

First note that

\displaystyle\sup_{(x,k,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\llbracket 2n\rrbracket\times[0,2]}\phi_{n}(x,k,s)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{(x,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\llbracket 2n\rrbracket}\phi_{n}(x,k,k{n^{-1}})

\displaystyle=\sup_{(x,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\llbracket 2n\rrbracket}\left(u_{n}(x,k{n^{-1}})-u(x,k{n^{-1}})-2c_{n}k{n^{-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sigma_{n}}{2}\,,

the last inequality holding by the definition of
\sigma_{n}
and since for all
k\in\llbracket 2n\rrbracket
we have
2c_{n}k{n^{-1}}=(\sigma_{n}/8)2k{n^{-1}}\leq\sigma_{n}/2
.

Now let
(x_{0},k_{0},s_{0})
be any point achieving the supremum in (
3.2
). We bound
|k_{0}{n^{-1}}-s_{0}|
in cases, depending on the value of
s_{0}
.

First suppose that
s_{0}=0
. If
k_{0}=0
then
s_{0}=k_{0}{n^{-1}}
. Otherwise, the fact that
\phi_{n}(x_{0},k_{0}-1,s_{0})\leq\phi_{n}(x_{0},k_{0},s_{0})
may be rewritten (using that

) as

u_{n}(x,(k_{0}-1){n^{-1}})-C_{n}((k_{0}-1){n^{-1}})^{2}\leq u_{n}(x,k_{0}{n^{-1}})-c_{n}{n^{-1}}-C_{n}(k_{0}{n^{-1}})^{2}\,.

Rearranging this, and using that
(k_{0}{n^{-1}})^{2}-((k_{0}-1){n^{-1}})^{2}\geq k_{0}n^{-2}
,
it follows that

\displaystyle C_{n}\cdot k_{0}{n^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{u_{n}(x,k_{0}{n^{-1}})-u_{n}(x,(k_{0}-1){n^{-1}})}{{n^{-1}}}-c_{n}\leq\frac{\|f\|_{C^{2}}\tr(\Sigma)}{2}\,,

where for the last inequality we have used Corollary
2.2
and the fact that
c_{n}\geq 0
. It follows that
k_{0}{n^{-1}}-s_{0}=k_{0}{n^{-1}}\leq\|f\|_{C^{2}}\tr(\Sigma)/(2C_{n})=O(n^{-1/2})
,

Next suppose that
s_{0}>0
.
Since
u\in C^{2,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty))
, by the definition of
\phi_{n}
we have

\partial_{s}\phi_{n}(x,k,s)=-\partial_{s}u(x,s)-c_{n}+2C_{n}(k{n^{-1}}-s).

Since
(x_{0},k_{0},s_{0})
is a maximum of
\phi_{n}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\llbracket 2n\rrbracket\times[0,2]
, it follows that if
s_{0}\in(0,2)
then
\partial_{s}(\phi_{n}(x,k,s))|_{(x,k,s)=(x_{0},k_{0},s_{0})}=0
, and if
s_{0}=2
then this partial derivative is at least zero.
Since
\partial_{t}u=\tfrac{1}{2}\tr(\Sigma D^{2}u)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty)
, if
s_{0}\in(0,2)
, we obtain that

2C_{n}(k_{0}{n^{-1}}-s_{0})=c_{n}+\tfrac{1}{2}\tr(\Sigma D^{2}u)\,,

so

|k_{0}{n^{-1}}-s_{0}|\leq\frac{1}{2C_{n}}\cdot\left(c_{n}+\frac{1}{2}|\tr(\Sigma D^{2}u)|\right)=O(n^{-1/2})\,.

If instead
s_{0}=2
, then using that
\partial_{s}(\phi_{n}(x,k,s))|_{(x,k,s)=(x_{0},k_{0},s_{0})}\geq 0
we similarly obtain that

k_{0}{n^{-1}}\geq s_{0}-\frac{1}{2C_{n}}\cdot\left(c_{n}+\frac{1}{2}|\tr(\Sigma D^{2}u)|\right)=s_{0}-O(n^{-1/2})\,,

so since also
k_{0}{n^{-1}}\leq 2=s_{0}
we again conclude that
|k_{0}{n^{-1}}-s_{0}|=O(n^{-1/2})
.

To complete the proof of the lemma, suppose
((x^{\ell},k^{\ell},s^{\ell}),\ell\geq 1)
is a sequence of elements of
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\llbracket 2n\rrbracket\times[0,2]
.
If
x^{\ell}\to\infty
as
\ell\to\infty
, then since
|f(x)|\to 0
as

, and
u_{n}(x,k{n^{-1}})=\mathbb{E}\left[f(x+S_{k}{n^{-1/2}})\right]
and

where

,
it follows that as
\ell\to\infty
,

\sup_{k\in\llbracket 2n\rrbracket}|u_{n}(x^{\ell},k{n^{-1}})|\to 0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\sup_{t\in[0,2]}|u(x^{\ell},t)|\to 0.

Thus, whenever
x_{\ell}\to\infty
as
\ell\to\infty
, we have

\limsup_{\ell\to\infty}\phi_{n}(x^{\ell},k^{\ell},s^{\ell})\leq 0.

On the other hand, choosing
((x^{\ell},k^{\ell},s^{\ell}),\ell\geq 1)
so that

\phi_{n}(x^{\ell},k^{\ell},s^{\ell})\to\sup_{(x,k,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\llbracket 2n\rrbracket\times[0,2]}\phi_{n}(x,k{n^{-1}},s)\,,

if
\sigma_{n}>0
then, since the last supremum is at least
\sigma_{n}/2
, it follows that

\liminf_{\ell\to\infty}\phi_{n}(x^{\ell},k^{\ell},s^{\ell})>0,

so
(x^{\ell},\ell\geq 1)
must be a bounded sequence. Since
\llbracket 2n\rrbracket
is finite,
[0,2]
is compact and
\phi_{n}
is continuous in
x
and
s
, it follows that, after passing to a subsequence,
((x^{\ell},k^{\ell},s^{\ell}),\ell\geq 1)
converges to some
(x_{0},k_{0},s_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\llbracket 2n\rrbracket\times[0,2]
as
\ell\to\infty
and that the supremum is achieved at the limit.
∎

Equipped with this lemma, we are now ready to prove the main result of this section.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Recall that

\sigma_{n}=\sup_{(x,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\llbracket 2n\rrbracket}(u_{n}((x,k{n^{-1}})-u(x,k{n^{-1}}))_{+}\,

and let

\widetilde{\sigma}_{n}=\sup_{(x,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\llbracket 2n\rrbracket}(u(x,k{n^{-1}})-u_{n}(x,k{n^{-1}}))_{+}
, so that

\sup_{(x,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\llbracket 2n\rrbracket}|u_{n}(x,k{n^{-1}})-u(x,k{n^{-1}})|=\max(\sigma_{n},\widetilde{\sigma}_{n}).

Since
u_{n}(x,\cdot)
is constant on time intervals of the form
[kn^{-1},(k+1)n^{-1})
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,2]}(u_{n}((x,t)-u(x,t))_{+}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{n}+\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},|t-s|\leq n^{-1}}|u(x,t)-u(x,s)|

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{n}+\|f\|_{C^{2}}\tr(\Sigma)/(2n),

the last inequality holding by the final bound of Corollary
2.2
. It likewise holds that

\sup_{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,2]}(u(x,t)-u_{n}((x,t))_{+}\leq\widetilde{\sigma}_{n}+\|f\|_{C^{2}}\tr(\Sigma)/(2n),

so to prove the proposition, it suffices to show that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{n}=0
and
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\widetilde{\sigma}_{n}=0
(or equivalently for the quantitative estimate, to show
\max\left(\sigma_{n},\widetilde{\sigma}_{n}\right)\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{1}|^{2+\gamma}\right]n^{-\gamma/2}
). We prove this only for
\sigma_{n}
as the bound for
\widetilde{\sigma}_{n}
follows by a symmetric argument.

Fix
n
with
\sigma_{n}>0
; by Lemma
3.2
, there exists
(x_{0},k_{0},s_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\llbracket 2n\rrbracket\times[0,2]
such that

\phi_{n}(x_{0},k_{0},s_{0})=\sup\phi_{n}(x,k,s)
. The proof breaks into cases depending on where the supremum is achieved.

Case 1
:
k_{0}>0,s_{0}>0
.

By the definition of
\phi_{n}
, and since
u(x,s)
is smooth for
s>0
, we have

\partial_{s}\phi_{n}(x,k,s)=-\partial_{s}u(x,s)-c_{n}+2C_{n}(k{n^{-1}}-s).

Since
(x_{0},k_{0},s_{0})
is a maximum of
\phi_{n}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\llbracket 2n\rrbracket\times[0,2]
, by the same reasoning as in the prior lemma, we conclude that

(3.3)

c_{n}\leq 2C_{n}(k_{0}{n^{-1}}-s_{0})-\frac{1}{2}\tr(\Sigma D^{2}u(x_{0},s_{0})).

We now perform a similar “maximizing” argument for
k_{0}
; the fact that
\phi_{n}(x_{0},k_{0},s_{0})\geq\phi_{n}(x_{0},k_{0}-1,s_{0})
may be rewritten as

u_{n}(x_{0},k_{0}{n^{-1}})-c_{n}(k_{0}{n^{-1}})-C_{n}(k_{0}{n^{-1}}-s_{0})^{2}\\
\geq u_{n}(x_{0},(k_{0}-1){n^{-1}})-c_{n}((k_{0}-1){n^{-1}})-C_{n}((k_{0}-1){n^{-1}}-s_{0})^{2}\,.

Rearranging and dividing by
{n^{-1}}
yields

\displaystyle c_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{u_{n}(x_{0},k_{0}{n^{-1}})-u_{n}(x_{0},(k_{0}-1){n^{-1}})}{{n^{-1}}}

\displaystyle\quad+C_{n}\frac{1}{{n^{-1}}}\bigl(((k_{0}-1){n^{-1}}-s_{0})^{2}-(k_{0}{n^{-1}}-s_{0})^{2}\bigr)

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{n}(x_{0},k_{0}{n^{-1}})-u_{n}(x_{0},(k_{0}-1){n^{-1}})}{{n^{-1}}}-2C_{n}(k_{0}{n^{-1}}-s_{0})+\frac{C_{n}}{n}\,.

Adding this inequality to (
3.3
) and using that
2c_{n}=\sigma_{n}/4
, we obtain that

\frac{\sigma_{n}}{4}\leq\frac{u_{n}(x_{0},k_{0}{n^{-1}})-u_{n}(x_{0},(k_{0}-1){n^{-1}})}{{n^{-1}}}-\frac{1}{2}\tr(\Sigma D^{2}u(x_{0},s_{0}))+\frac{C_{n}}{n}\,.

Using the equality
u_{n}(x,k{n^{-1}})=\mathbb{E}\left[u_{n}(x+X{n^{-1/2}},(k-1){n^{-1}})\right]
and
applying Lemma
2.3
, we have that

\left|\frac{u_{n}(x_{0},k_{0}{n^{-1}})-u_{n}(x_{0},(k_{0}-1){n^{-1}})}{{n^{-1}}}-\frac{1}{2}\tr(\Sigma D^{2}u_{n}(x_{0},(k_{0}-1){n^{-1}}))\right|\leq\varepsilon_{n},

so

\frac{\sigma_{n}}{4}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\tr(\Sigma D^{2}u_{n}(x_{0},(k_{0}-1){n^{-1}}))-\tr(\Sigma D^{2}u(x_{0},s_{0}))\right)+\frac{C_{n}}{n}+\varepsilon_{n}\,.

To bound the difference of traces, we first note that since
\phi_{n}
is maximized at
(x_{0},k_{0},s_{0})
, its Hessian is non-positive semidefinite at this point:

D^{2}\phi_{n}(x_{0},k_{0},s_{0})=D^{2}u_{n}(x_{0},k_{0}{n^{-1}})-D^{2}u(x_{0},s_{0})\preceq 0.

Since
\Sigma
is a covariance matrix, it is positive semidefinite, and therefore
\Sigma\big(D^{2}u_{n}(x_{0},k_{0}{n^{-1}})-D^{2}u(x_{0},s_{0})\big)\preceq 0
, and so the linearity of the trace implies

\tr\bigl(\Sigma D^{2}u_{n}(x_{0},k_{0}{n^{-1}})\bigr)\leq\tr\bigl(\Sigma D^{2}u(x_{0},s_{0})\bigr)\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\tr\big(\Sigma D^{2}u_{n}

\displaystyle(x_{0},(k_{0}-1){n^{-1}})\big)-\tr\big(\Sigma D^{2}u(x_{0},s_{0})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\tr\big(\Sigma\big(D^{2}u_{n}(x_{0},(k_{0}-1){n^{-1}})-D^{2}u_{n}(x_{0},k_{0}{n^{-1}})\big)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma)\left|\tr\Bigl(D^{2}u_{n}(x_{0},(k_{0}-1){n^{-1}})-D^{2}u_{n}(x_{0},k_{0}{n^{-1}})\Bigr)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma)\cdot d\cdot\|f\|_{C^{4}}\tr(\Sigma)/(2n)

\displaystyle\leq d^{2}\cdot\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma)^{2}\cdot\|f\|_{C^{4}}/(2n)\,,

where in the second-to-last line we have used the second bound of Corollary
2.2
, and in the last line we have used that
\tr(\Sigma)\leq d\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma)
.
Combining this with (
3.5
), we obtain that

\frac{\sigma_{n}}{4}\leq\frac{d^{2}\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma)^{2}\|f\|_{C^{4}}}{4n}+\frac{C_{n}}{n}+\varepsilon_{n}\,.

Since
C_{n}=2\|f\|_{\infty}n^{1/2}
, the right-hand side tends to
0
as

; also, by the bound on
\varepsilon_{n}
from Lemma
2.3
, for any

the right hand-side is at most
C\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{1}|^{2+\gamma}||\right]n^{-\gamma/2}
for some constant
C=C(d,\|f\|_{C^{4}},\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma))
. (For this we are using that
\|f\|_{C^{k}}
is increasing in

and that
\|f\|_{C^{0}}=\|f\|_{\infty}
.)

Case 2
:
k_{0}=0,s_{0}\geq 0
.

Recall from Lemma
3.2
that
\phi_{n}(x_{0},k_{0},s_{0})\geq\sigma_{n}/2
. Using the definition of
\phi_{n}
, we thus have

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}/2\leq\phi_{n}(x_{0},0,s_{0})\leq u_{n}(x_{0},0)-u(x_{0},s_{0})

\displaystyle=f(x_{0})-\mathbb{E}[f(x_{0}+s_{0}^{1/2}\xi)]

\displaystyle\leq\left|\mathbb{E}\left[f(x_{0}+s_{0}^{1/2}\xi)-f(x_{0})\right]\right|.

By the last bound of Corollary
2.2
, we have

\Big|\mathbb{E}\left[f(x_{0}+s_{0}^{1/2}\xi)-f(x_{0})\right]\Big|=|u(x_{0},s_{0})-u(x_{0},0)|\leq s_{0}\|f\|_{C^{2}}\tr(\Sigma)/2\,,

which combined with the preceding inequality yields that

\sigma_{n}\leq s_{0}\|f\|_{C^{2}}\tr(\Sigma)\,.

By Lemma
3.2
, we have

s_{0}=|s_{0}-k_{0}n^{-1}|=O(n^{-1/2})
, so the preceding bound implies that
\sigma_{n}=O(n^{-1/2})
.

Case 3
:
k_{0}>0,s_{0}=0
.
The argument is similar to that from Case 2. Using that
\phi_{n}(x_{0},k_{0},s_{0})\geq\sigma_{n}/2
and the definition of
\phi_{n}
gives us

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}/2\leq\phi_{n}(x_{0},k_{0},0)

\displaystyle\leq u_{n}(x_{0},k_{0}{n^{-1}})-u(x_{0},0)

\displaystyle=u_{n}(x_{0},k_{0}{n^{-1}})-u_{n}(x_{0},0)

\displaystyle\leq k_{0}\max_{1\leq k\leq k_{0}}|u_{n}(x_{0},k{n^{-1}})-u_{n}(x_{0},(k-1){n^{-1}})|\,.

Using that
u_{n}(x,k{n^{-1}})=\mathbb{E}\left[u_{n}(x+X{n^{-1/2}},(k-1){n^{-1}})\right]
and
applying Corollary
2.2
, it follows that for all
1\leq k\leq k_{0}
,

(3.6)

|u_{n}(x_{0},k{n^{-1}})-u_{n}(x_{0},(k-1){n^{-1}})|\leq\|f\|_{C^{2}}\tr(\Sigma)/(2n)\,,

which together with the previous bound implies that

\sigma_{n}\leq k_{0}\|f\|_{C^{2}}\tr(\Sigma)n^{-1}.

By Lemma
3.2
we have that

k_{0}n^{-1}=|k_{0}n^{-1}-s_{0}|=O(n^{-1/2})
, so this bound again yields that
\sigma_{n}=O(n^{-1/2})
.
∎

Theorem
1.2
is an immediate consequence of the second bound of Proposition
3.1
, so all that remains for us to do is to deduce Theorem
1.1
from the proposition.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

For any bounded continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, there exists an approximating sequence of functions
f^{(k)}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, all bounded and uniformly continuous, with compact support and with
\|f^{(k)}\|_{\infty}\leq\|f\|_{\infty}
, such that
f^{(k)}
and
f
agree on
[-k,k]^{d}
. For such functions, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|f(S_{n}n^{-1/2})-f^{(k)}(S_{n}n^{-1/2})|\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\|f\|_{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[|S_{n}|_{\infty}\geq kn^{1/2}\right]\,,

where for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
|x|_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq i\leq d}|x_{i}|
, so by Chebyshev’s inequality,

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\big|\mathbb{E}\left[f(S_{n}n^{-1/2})\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[f^{(k)}(S_{n}n^{-1/2})\right]\big|=0\,.

To prove Theorem
1.1
it thus suffices to prove that for all bounded, uniformly continuous
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
with compact support, we have
\mathbb{E}[f(S_{n}n^{-1/2})]\to\mathbb{E}[f(\xi)]
. For the remainder of the proof, we fix such a function
f
; we will use the smoothing effect of convolution with a Gaussian in order to be able to apply Proposition
3.1
.

Write
u=u_{f}
and
u_{n}=u_{n,f}
, like in the rest of the paper.
Using
\eta_{t}
for the probability density function of
t^{1/2}\xi
, then

u(x,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x+t^{1/2}y)\eta_{t}(y)dy\,;

since
\eta_{t}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
f
is bounded, it follows by differentiation under the integral sign that
u(\cdot,t)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
t>0
. Since
f
has compact support and
\eta_{t}(y)\to 0
as
|y|\to\infty
, this identity likewise implies that
u(x,t)\to 0
as
|x|\to\infty
. Writing
f^{t}(x):=u(x,t)
, it thus follows that for every
t>0
,
f^{t}
satisfies the assumptions of Proposition
3.1
; this in particular implies that

u_{n,f^{t}}(0,1)\to u_{f^{t}}(0,1)

as
n\to\infty
. Rewritten using the definitions of
u_{n,f^{t}}
and
u_{f^{t}}
, this convergence states that

\mathbb{E}\left[f(S_{n}n^{-1/2}+t^{1/2}\xi)\right]\to\mathbb{E}\left[f(\xi+t^{1/2}\xi^{\prime})\right]\,

as
n\to\infty
, where
\xi,\xi^{\prime}
are independent and
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
-distributed (and
\xi
is independent of
S_{n}
). Finally, since
f
is bounded and uniformly continuous, for any
\varepsilon>0
we may choose
t>0
small enough that

\mathbb{E}\left[|f(x+t^{1/2}\xi)-f(x)|\right]\leq\varepsilon
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the triangle inequality then implies that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\,

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{E}\left[f(S_{n}n^{-1/2})\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[f(\xi)\right]\big|

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon+\limsup_{n\to\infty}\big|\mathbb{E}\left[f(S_{n}n^{-1/2}+t^{1/2}\xi)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[f(\xi+t^{1/2}\xi^{\prime})\right]\big|

\displaystyle=2\varepsilon\,.

Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, this completes the proof.
