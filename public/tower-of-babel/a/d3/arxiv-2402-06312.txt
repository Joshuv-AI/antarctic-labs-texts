1.
Introduction

The notion of zero divisor
[
7
]
is well known from ring theory. In a ring
\mathfrak{R},
an element
z
is termed a left
(right)
zero divisor if
zx=0~(xz=0)
for some
0\neq x\in\mathfrak{R}
and if it is either a left zero divisor or a right zero divisor, we call it a zero divisor.

The notion of topological divisors of zero(
TDZ
) is an important generalization in a Banach algebra, introduced by Shilov in
[
16
]
with significant later contributions by Zelasko, Kaplansky et al. In a Banach algebra
\mathfrak{B},
an element
z
is called a TDZ if there exists a sequence
\{z_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
\mathfrak{B}
with
\|z_{n}\|=1,
for each
n\geq 1
such that either
zz_{n}\to 0
or
z_{n}z\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

In
[
15
]
F. Schulz, R. Brits, M. Hasse gave a characterization of Banach algebras in which every element is a TDZ. The purpose of the present paper is to determine zero divisors and TDZ in some Banach algebras which do not fall in the preceding category.

In this paper, we consistently employ the notation
\mathbb{N}
to signify the set of positive integers,
\mathbb{C}
designates the set of complex numbers,
\mathcal{B}(X)
denotes the Banach algebra of all bounded linear operators on
X,

\mathcal{R}(f)
denotes the range of a function
f,

[f]
denotes the linear span of
f,

C(X)
denotes the space of all complex-valued continuous functions on
X
and
Z(f)=\{x:f(x)=0\}
refers to the zero set of
f
.

Let
(X,\Omega,\mu)
be a
\sigma
-finite measure space. For
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we abbreviate the Lebesgue space
L^{p}(X,\Omega,\mu)
to
L^{p}(\mu),
and denote the
L^{p}
-norm by
\|\cdot\|_{p}.
Note that
L^{p}(\mu)
is a Banach space under the
L^{p}
-norm
(1\leq p\leq\infty)
(see
[
1
,
3
,
14
]
).

In the scenario where
X
equals
\mathbb{N},
and
\mu
represents the counting measure on
\mathbb{N},
the
L^{p}(\mu)
becomes
\ell^{p}.

Let
(X,\Omega,\mu)
be a
\sigma
-finite measure space and
u\in L^{\infty}(\mu).
Let
\phi
be a measurable transformation on
X
and suppose that the measure
\mu_{\phi}
defined by
\mu_{\phi}(E)=\mu(\phi^{-1}(E))~\forall~E\in\Omega,
is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\mu
(we write
\mu_{\phi}\ll\mu,
as usual). Then we can define the linear transformation
uC_{\phi}:L^{p}(\mu)\to L^{p}(\mu)
as

uC_{\phi}f(x)=u(x)f(\phi(x))~\text{for all}~x\in X~\text{and}~f\in L^{p}(\mu)

and the multiplication operator
M_{u}:L^{p}(\mu)\to L^{p}(\mu)
defined as

M_{u}(f)(x)=u(x)f(x)~\forall~x\in X.

Assumption
\mu_{\phi}\ll\mu
is used in the well-definedness of
uC_{\phi}
. Further, if the transformation
uC_{\phi}
is bounded, then it is called a weighted composition operator on
L^{p}(\mu).
Specifically, when considering the constant function
1
as
u
, the associated operator
uC_{\phi}
on
L^{p}(\mu)
becomes
C_{\phi}
and is called a composition operator (see
[
3
,
21
,
23
,
24
]
).

In
[
22
]
, R. K. Singh and B. S. Komal have shown that a linear transformation
C_{\phi}
on
\ell^{p}~(1\leq p<\infty)
defined as

C_{\phi}(f)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f(n)\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{\phi^{-1}(n)}

is bounded if and only if
\{|\phi^{-1}(n)|:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a bounded set, where
|\phi^{-1}(n)|
represents the cardinality of the set
\phi^{-1}(n).
Further, in
[
11
]
, it is proved that a linear transformation
uC_{\phi}
on
L^{p}(\mu)
is bounded if and only if
\frac{d\mu\phi^{-1}}{d\mu}:X\setminus Z(u)\to\mathbb{C}
is essentially bounded, where
\frac{d\mu\phi^{-1}}{d\mu}
represents the Radon-Nikodym derivative.

Weighted composition operators constitute a significant and diverse class of operators. There are several important reasons to study these operators. For example, Lamperti
[
14
]
showed that every isometry on
L^{p}(\mu)
spaces is a weighted composition operator. Most recently, in
[
2
]
, it is shown that analytic m-isometries without the wandering subspace property are weighted composition operators which can be realised as weighted shifts, thus providing a partial answer to the problem raised by Shimorin in [
[
17
]
, p. 185].

The current article is aimed at finding weighted composition operators on the spaces
\ell^{p}
and
L^{p}(\mu)
as zero divisors in
\mathcal{B}(\ell^{p})
and
\mathcal{B}(L^{p}(\mu))
respectively. Further, we find TDZ in the Banach algebras
C(X)
in its full generality. Consequently, we determine condition under which the multiplication operator
M_{h}
is a TDZ in
\mathcal{B}(C(X)).
We show that every element in
C(X)
and in
L^{\infty}(\mu)
is a polynomially TDZ. We also prove that every multiplication operator
M_{h}
is a polynomially TDZ in
\mathcal{B}(C(X))
and
\mathcal{B}(L^{p}(\mu))
respectively. Finally, we show that if
H
is a separable Hilbert space, then each
T\in\mathcal{B}(H)
is a strongly TDZ. Lastly, we construct a class of compact operators in
\mathcal{B}(\ell^{p})
which are also TDZ. The paper is arranged as follows.

In section
2
, we state some definitions and basic results which are relevant. Section
3.1
is devoted for the characterization of weighted composition operators on the
\ell^{p}
spaces as zero divisors in
\mathcal{B}(\ell^{p})
and in section
3.2
, we characterize composition operators on the
L^{p}(\mu)
spaces as zero divisors in
\mathcal{B}(L^{p}(\mu)).
In section
3.3
, we completely characterize the TDZ in the Banach algebras
C(X)
in its full generality. Section
3.4
is devoted for the characterization of polynomially TDZ. In this section we show that every element in
C(X)
and
L^{\infty}(\mu)
is a polynomially TDZ. Further, we prove that every multiplication operator
M_{h}
is a polynomially TDZ in
\mathcal{B}(C(X))
and
\mathcal{B}(L^{p}(\mu))
respectively.
In section
3.5
, we show that every TDZ is also a strongly TDZ. We also show that if
H
is a separable Hilbert space, then each
T\in\mathcal{B}(H)
is a strongly TDZ. Lastly, we construct a class of compact operators in
\mathcal{B}(\ell^{p})
which are also TDZ.

3.
Main results

3.1.
Weighted composition operators on
\ell^{p}~(1\leq p<\infty)
spaces as zero divisors

In this section, we give a characterization of the weighted composition operators on
\ell^{p},
which are zero divisors in
\mathcal{B}(\ell^{p}).

We begin with the following theorem, which shows that under a certain condition on
\phi
and the zero set
Z(u)
of the weight function
u
, the weighted composition operator
uC_{\phi}
is a right zero divisor.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
u\in\ell^{\infty}
and
Z(u)
denote the zero set of
u.
If
\phi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is such that there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
with
\phi^{-1}(n_{0})\neq\emptyset
and
\phi^{-1}(n_{0})=\{n_{1},n_{2},...,n_{k}\}\subset Z(u)
then
uC_{\phi}
is right zero divisor.

Proof.

Let
\phi^{-1}(n_{0})=\{n_{1},n_{2},...,n_{k}\}.
Then
T
defined as

T(f)=\begin{cases}f,&\text{ if }f=\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{n_{1}},\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{n_{2}},...,\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{n_{k}}\\
0,&\text{ if }f=\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{n}\text{ and }n\neq n_{1},n_{2},...,n_{k}\end{cases}

linearly extends to a bounded linear operator
T
on
\ell^{p}.

For any
g=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g_{n}\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{n}\in\ell^{p}

\displaystyle T(g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g_{n}T(\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}g_{n_{i}}\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{n_{i}}

and

(T\circ uC_{\phi})(\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{n})=\begin{cases}\sum_{i=1}^{k}u(n_{i})\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{n_{i}},&\text{ if }n=n_{0}\\
0,&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}.

Since
\phi^{-1}(n_{0})\subset Z(u),
therefore
T\circ uC_{\phi}=0.
Hence
uC_{\phi}
is right zero divisor.
∎

The following theorem gives a necessary and sufficient condition for
uC_{\phi}
to be a right zero divisor when
Z(u)=\emptyset.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
and suppose
u\in\ell^{\infty}
is such that
u(n)\neq 0~\forall~n\geq 1.
Then
uC_{\phi}
is a right zero divisor if and only if
\phi
is not injective.

Proof.

Suppose
\phi
is injective and
u\in\ell^{\infty}
is such that
u(n)\neq 0~\forall~n\geq 1.
Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\ell^{p})
be such that
T(uC_{\phi})=0.
This implies that

\displaystyle T(uC_{\phi})(\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{n})

\displaystyle=T(u\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{\phi^{-1}(n)})

\displaystyle=u(\phi^{-1}(n))T(\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{\phi^{-1}(n)})

\displaystyle=0~~\text{for each~}n\geq 1.

Since
u(n)\neq 0~\forall~n\geq 1.
Therefore

T(\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{\phi^{-1}(n)})=0\ \forall\ n\geq 1.

Again, as
\phi
is injective, hence

|\phi^{-1}(n)|\leq 1\ \forall\ n\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\phi^{-1}(n)=\mathbb{N}.

Therefore, it follows that
T(\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{n})=0~\forall~n\geq 1.
Thus
T=0.
Hence
uC_{\phi}
can not be a right zero divisor.

Conversely, suppose
\phi
is not injective and
u(n)\neq 0~\forall~n\geq 1.
Now we first prove that
uC_{\phi}
is not surjective.

Since
\phi
is not injective, then by Theorem
2.3
,
C_{\phi}
is not surjective. Hence, there exists
f_{0}\in\ell^{p}
such that
f_{0}\notin\mathcal{R}(C_{\phi}).
We claim that
uf_{0}\notin\mathcal{R}(uC_{\phi}).

For, if
uf_{0}\in\mathcal{R}(uC_{\phi}),
then there exists
g_{0}\in\ell^{p}
such that
uf_{0}=uC_{\phi}g_{0}.
This implies that

u(n)(f_{0}(n)-(g_{0}\circ\phi)(n))=0~~\text{for each }~n\geq 1.

Since
u(n)\neq 0~\forall~n\geq 1,
hence
f_{0}=g_{0}\circ\phi\in\mathcal{R}(C_{\phi}).
This is a contradiction.

Thus, if
\phi
is not injective and
u(n)\neq 0~\forall~n\geq 1,
then
uC_{\phi}
is not surjective.

Hence there exists an
f_{0}\in\ell^{p}\backslash\mathcal{R}(uC_{\phi})
. Let
M=[f_{0}].
Since
M
is a finite dimensional subspace of
\ell^{p},
it follows from Hahn-Banach theorem that there exists a closed subspace
N
such that
\ell^{p}=M\bigoplus N.

Let
T
denote the projection on
M.

Then
T
is a non-zero bounded linear operator on
\ell^{p}
and
\mathcal{R}(uC_{\phi})
is contained in
N.
Hence

~(T\circ uC_{\phi})(f)=T(uC_{\phi}f)=0~\forall~f\in\ell^{p}.

This implies that
uC_{\phi}
is a right zero divisor.
∎

We now give examples of two weighted composition operators, which are right zero divisors.

Example 3.3
.

Define
\phi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
as
\phi(1)=1,~\phi(n)=n+1~\forall~n\geq 2
and
u\in\ell^{\infty}
as
u(1)=0,~u(n)=\frac{1}{n}~\forall~n\geq 2.
Then, by Theorem
3.1
,
uC_{\phi}
is a right zero divisor.

Example 3.4
.

Define
\phi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
as
\phi(1)=\phi(2)=1,~\phi(n)=n~\forall~n\geq 3
and
u\in\ell^{\infty}
as
u(n)=\frac{1}{n}~\forall~n\geq 1.
Then, by Theorem
3.2
,
uC_{\phi}
is a right zero divisor.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
u\in\ell^{\infty}
and
\phi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be such that
uC_{\phi}
is a bounded linear operator on
\ell^{p}.
Then
uC_{\phi}
is a left zero divisor if
\phi
is not surjective.

Proof.

Suppose
\phi
is not surjective. Then there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\phi^{-1}(n_{0})=\emptyset.

Let
f_{0}=\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{n_{0}}
and
M=[f_{0}].
Since
M
is a one dimensional subspace of
\ell^{p},
then there exists a closed subspace
N
of
\ell^{p}
such that
\ell^{p}=M\bigoplus N.

Let
T
denote the projection on
M.

Clearly,
0\neq T
defines a bounded linear operator on
\ell^{p}
.
Observe that for
f=\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{n_{0}}

\displaystyle(uC_{\phi}\circ T)(\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{n_{0}})

\displaystyle=u\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{\phi^{-1}(n_{0})}

\displaystyle=0

and for
f\notin M

\displaystyle(uC_{\phi}\circ T)(f)

\displaystyle=uC_{\phi}(T(f))

\displaystyle=0.

This implies that
uC_{\phi}\circ T=0.
Hence,
uC_{\phi}
is a left zero divisor.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

The following example shows that the converse of the above Theorem
3.5
is not true.

Example 3.7
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be defined as
\phi(2n-1)=\phi(2n)=n~\forall~n\geq 1
and
u\in\ell^{\infty}
as
u(1)=u(2)=0
and
u(n)=1~\forall~n\geq 3.
It is easy to see that null space
M
of
uC_{\phi}
is one dimensional. Let
T
denote the projection on
M.
Then
T
is a non-zero bounded linear operator on
\ell^{p}
and
uC_{\phi}\circ T=0.
Hence
uC_{\phi}
is a left zero divisor.

Theorem 3.8
.

Suppose
u\in\ell^{\infty}
is such that
u(n)\neq 0~\forall~n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
uC_{\phi}
is a left zero divisor if and only if
\phi
is not surjective.

Proof.

Suppose
\phi
is surjective. Since
u(n)\neq 0~\forall~n\in\mathbb{N},
hence by Theorem
2.4
,
uC_{\phi}
is injective. Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\ell^{p})
be such that
uC_{\phi}\circ T=0.
Then

(uC_{\phi}\circ T)(\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{n})=uC_{\phi}(T(\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{n}))=0\text{ for each }n\geq 1.

Now injectivity of
uC_{\phi}
implies that
T(\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{n})=0~\forall~n\geq 1.
Therefore
T=0.
Hence,
uC_{\phi}
can not be a left zero divisor.

Conversely, suppose
\phi
is not surjective. Then, again by Theorem
2.4
,
uC_{\phi}
is not injective. Hence there exists
0\neq f_{0}\in\ell^{p}
such that
uC_{\phi}(f_{0})=0.
Now, define a linear operator
T:\ell^{p}\to\ell^{p}
as follows.

T(f)=f(1)f_{0}~\forall~f\in\ell^{p}.

Clearly,
T\neq 0
since
T(\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{1})=f_{0}\neq 0.
Also, for each
f\in\ell^{p},

\|Tf\|=|f(1)|\|f_{0}\|\leq\|f\|\|f_{0}\|.

Hence
T
is bounded. Further, observe that for each
f\in\ell^{p},

uC_{\phi}(T(f))=uC_{\phi}(f(1)f_{0})=f(1)uC_{\phi}(f_{0})=0.

Hence
uC_{\phi}\circ T=0.
Therefore
uC_{\phi}
is a left zero divisor.
∎

Then by Theorem

,

Then by Theorem

,

.

Suppose
u\in\ell^{\infty}
is such that
u(n)\neq 0~\forall~n\in\mathbb{N}
. If
\phi
is not invertible, then

Proof.

The proof follows from Theorem

∎

Theorem 3.12
.

Suppose
u\in\ell^{\infty}
is bounded away from zero. Then
uC_{\phi}\in\mathcal{B}(\ell^{p})
is a zero divisor if and only if

If

The subsequent theorem establishes a connection between the antinormality of a weighted composition operator and its characteristic of being a zero divisor.

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
u\in\ell^{\infty}
be bounded away from zero such that
uC_{\phi}

Proof.

Here is an example of an antinormal weighted composition operator that is also a zero divisor.

Example 3.14

Theorem 3.15
.

Suppose

Let

\ell^{p}

\ell^{p}
.

\displaystyle(uC_{\phi}\circ T)(\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{n_{0}})

∎

Theorem 3.16
.

Let

∎

Theorem 3.17
.

Let
u\in\ell^{\infty}
and
\phi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}.

\displaystyle u\raisebox{0.0pt}[6.19998pt][6.19998pt]{$\chi$}_{\phi^{-1}(n)}\neq 0~~\text{for each}~n\geq 1

∎

Conversely, suppose

3.3.
Topological divisors of zero in
C(X)

Consider
X
as a compact Hausdorff space, and let
C(X)
represent the Banach algebra consisting of all continuous complex-valued functions on X. In
[
4
]
, F. Azarpanah, D. Esmaeilvandi and A.R. Salehi characterized the regular elements in
C(X).

With this context, the following results have been obtained:

Theorem 3.22
.

Let
f\in C(X).
Then
f
is a TDZ if and only if it is singular in
C(X).

Proof.

If
f
is a TDZ in
C(X)
then it is clearly a singular element.

Conversely, suppose that
f\in C(X)
is a singular. Then there exists
x_{0}\in X
such the
f(x_{0})=0.
Now, by continuity of
f,
for each
n\geq 1,
there exists an open set
E_{n}
containing
x_{0}
such that

|f(x)|<\frac{1}{n}~\text{for each}~x\in E_{n}.

Let
A_{n}=E^{c}_{n}
and
B_{n}=\{x_{0}\}.
By Urysohn’s Lemma, there exists a function
f_{n}:X\to[0,1]
such that

f_{n}(B_{n})=1~\text{and}~f_{n}(A_{n})=0.

Clearly
\|f_{n}\|=1
and
\|ff_{n}\|<\frac{1}{n}.
This implies that
\|ff_{n}\|\to 0
as
n\to\infty.
Hence
f
is a TDZ.
∎

Theorem 3.23
.

Let
h\in C(X)
and
M_{h}:C(X)\to C(X)
be the multiplication operator defined as
M_{h}f=hf.
Then
M_{h}
is a TDZ in
\mathcal{B}(C(X))
if and only
h
is a TDZ in
C(X)
.

Proof.

Suppose
h\in C(X)
be a TDZ. Let
\{h_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence in
C(X)
with
\|h_{n}\|=1\ \forall\ n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\|hh_{n}\|\to 0
as
n\to\infty.

For each
n\geq 1,
define
M_{h_{n}}:C(X)\to C(X)~\text{by}~M_{h_{n}}(f)=h_{n}f.
Observe that

\|M_{h_{n}}\|=\|h_{n}\|=1\ \forall\ n\in\mathbb{N}~\text{and}~M_{h}M_{h_{n}}(f)=hh_{n}f.

Then
\|M_{h}M_{h_{n}}\|=\|hh_{n}\|\to 0~\text{as}~n\to\infty.
Therefore,
M_{h}
is a TDZ in
\mathcal{B}(C(X))
.

Conversely, suppose
h\in C(X)
is not a TDZ in
C(X)
. Then
h
is a regular element in
C(X)
. Therefore there exists a
g\in C(X)
such that
h\cdot g=g\cdot h=1.
This implies that
M_{hg}=\text{I}.
Hence
M_{hg}=M_{h}M_{g}=M_{g}M_{h}=\text{I}.
This shows that
M_{h}
is regular element in
\mathcal{B}(C(X))
. Hence
M_{h}
can not be a TDZ.

∎

Theorem 3.24
.

Let
\phi:X\to X
be a continuous map and
C_{\phi}:C(X)\to C(X)
be the composition operator defined as
C_{\phi}f=f\circ\phi.
Then
C_{\phi}
is a bounded linear operator on
C(X)
with
\|C_{\phi}\|=1.

Further, the following statements hold.

(1) If
\phi
is bijective, then
C_{\phi}
is invertible in
\mathfrak{B}(C(X)).

(2)
C_{\phi}
is injective if and only if
\phi
is surjective.

(3)
C_{\phi}
is surjective if and only if
\phi
is injective.

Proof.

(1) If
\phi:X\to X
is bijective and continuous, then it is a homeomorphism. Hence
C^{-1}_{\phi}=C_{\phi^{-1}}.

(2) Suppose
\phi
is surjective and let
C_{\phi}f=0.
Then
(f\circ\phi)(x)=0~\forall~x\in X.
Now, surjectivity of
\phi
implies that
f(x)=0~\forall~x\in X.
Therefore
f=0.
This implies
C_{\phi}
is injective.

Conversely, suppose
\phi
is not surjective. Then
\phi(X)
is a proper closed and compact subset of
X.
Since
X
is completely regular, therefore for
x_{0}\in X\setminus\phi(X)
there exists a non-zero continuous function
f_{0}:X\to[0,1]
such that

f_{0}(x_{0})=1~\text{and}~f_{0}(\phi(X))=0.

This implies that
C_{\phi}f_{0}=0.
Since
f_{0}\neq 0,
therefore
C_{\phi}
is not injective. This completes the proof.

(3) Suppose
\phi
is injective. Let
g\in C(X)
and
\phi^{-1}:\phi(X)\to X.
Define
f=g\circ\phi^{-1}.
By Tietz extension theorem,
f
can be continuously extended on
X.
Clearly,

\displaystyle C_{\phi}(f)

\displaystyle=f\circ\phi

\displaystyle=g\circ\phi^{-1}\circ\phi

\displaystyle=g.

Hence
C_{\phi}
is surjective.

Conversely, suppose
\phi
is not injective. Then there exists two distinct elements
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
such that
\phi(x_{1})=\phi(x_{2})
and a continuous function
f_{0}:X\to[0,1]
such that

f_{0}(x_{1})=0,~f_{0}(x_{2})=1.

We, claim that
f_{0}\notin\mathcal{R}(C_{\phi}).
Observe that if
g\in\mathcal{R}(C_{\phi}),
then
g=f\circ\phi
for some
f\in C(X).

Then,

\displaystyle g(x_{1})=f\circ\phi(x_{1})=f\circ\phi(x_{2})=g(x_{2}).

This implies that
f_{0}\notin\mathcal{R}(C_{\phi}).
Hence
C_{\phi}
is not surjective.
∎

3.4.
Polynomially topological divisors of zero

Motivated by the notion of polynomially compact operators
[
12
]
, we give the the following definition.

Definition 3.25
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}
be a Banach algebra. An element
z\in\mathfrak{B}
is said to be polynomially TDZ if
p(z)
is a TDZ for some non-zero complex polynomial
p
.

Theorem 3.26
.

Every element in
C(X)
is a polynomially TDZ.

Proof.

If
f\in C(X)
is a TDZ. For the polynomial
p(x)=x,~f
becomes a polynomially TDZ.

Now, let
f\in C(X)
be such that
f(x)\neq 0~\forall~x\in X.
Let
x_{0}\in X
and
p(x)=x-f(x_{0}).
Then,
p(f)(x_{0})=0.
Then
p(f)
is a TDZ in
C(X).

∎

Theorem 3.27
.

Let
X
be a compact Hausdorff space and
h:X\to X
be a continuous map. Let
M_{h}:C(X)\to C(X)
be the multiplication operator defined as
M_{h}f=hf.
Then
M_{h}
is a polynomially TDZ in
\mathcal{B}(C(X))
.

Proof.

Let
x_{0}\in X
and
p(z)=z-h(x_{0}).
Clearly
p(h(x))\in C(X)
and
p(h(x_{0}))=0.
Hence by Theorem
\ref{CHS TDZ},~p(h(x))
is a TDZ in
C(X)
which is also a singular element. For each
f\in C(X)

p(M_{h})f=(M_{h}-h(x_{0})I)f

=(h-h(x_{0}))f

=M_{h-h(x_{0})}f.

Hence
p(M_{h})=M_{p(h)}.
By Theorem
3.23
,
p(M_{h})
is a TDZ in
\mathcal{B}(C(X))
. Hence
M_{h}
is a polynomially TDZ in
\mathcal{B}(C(X))
.
∎

Theorem 3.28
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}
be a Banach algebra and let
z\in\mathfrak{B}
be the TDZ. Then
z
is also a polynomially TDZ.

Proof.

Let
z\in\mathfrak{B}
be the TDZ in
\mathfrak{B}.
For the polynomial
p(x)=x,
clearly
p(z)
is a TDZ. Hence
z
is a polynomially TDZ in
\mathfrak{B}.

∎

Remark 3.29
.

The converse of above theorem is not true. Let
\mathfrak{B}
be an arbitrary unital Banach algebra with the unit element denoted as
e
in
\mathfrak{B}.
Let

then

which is a TDZ, while identity
e
is not a TDZ in
\mathfrak{B}
.

Theorem 3.30
.

In
L^{\infty}(\mu)
every element is a polynomially TDZ.

Proof.

Let
h\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
and

Then for the polynomial

clearly

Hence by the Theorem
2.9
,

is a TDZ. Therefore

is a polynomially TDZ.
∎

Theorem 3.31
.

Let
h\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
and

Define the multiplication operator
M_{h}:L^{p}(\mu)\to L^{p}(\mu)
by

. Then

is a polynomially TDZ in

Let

Then by Theorem

2.10
,

∎

3.5.
Strongly topological divisors of zero

The following notion is a generalization of the concept of TDZ.

Definition 3.32
.

Let
X
be a non-empty set. An operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
is called strongly TDZ if there exists a sequence
\{T_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
\mathcal{B}(X)
such that

(1)

\|T_{n}\|=1~\forall~n\in\mathbb{N}

(2)

and
\|T_{n}Tx\|\to 0
or
\|TT_{n}x\|\to 0
as
n\to 0~~\forall x\in X.

Theorem 3.33
.

Let
X
be a Banach space. If
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
is a TDZ then
T
is a strongly TDZ.

Proof.

If
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
is a TDZ then there exists a sequence
\{T_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that
\|T_{n}\|=1~\forall~n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\|T_{n}T\|\to 0
or
\|TT_{n}\|\to 0
as
n\to\infty.
Let
x\in X
then

\|TT_{n}x\|\leq\|TT_{n}\|\|x\|\to 0~or~\|T_{n}Tx\|\leq\|T_{n}T\|\|x\|\to 0~as~n\to 0.

Thus
TT_{n}x\to 0
or
T_{n}Tx\to 0~\forall x\in X.
Hence
T
is a strongly TDZ.
∎

Remark 3.34
.

The subsequent example illustrates that the converse of the above theorem
3.33
is not true.

Example 3.35
.

Let
T=I\in\mathcal{B}(\ell^{p})~(1\leq p\leq\infty)
be the indentity operator. For each
n\in\mathbb{N},
define
T_{n}:\ell^{p}\to\ell^{p}
as

T_{n}(x)=(0,0,...,x_{n+1},0,0,...).

Clearly
\|T_{n}\|=1~\forall~n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\|TT_{n}(x)\|=|x_{n+1}|\to 0
as
n\to\infty.
Hence
T
is a strongly TDZ but not a TDZ in
\mathcal{B}(\ell^{p})
.

Theorem 3.36
.

Let
H
be a separable Hilbert space and
T\in\mathcal{B}(H).
Then
T
is a strongly TDZ in
\mathcal{B}(H)
.

Proof.

Let
\{e_{n}:n\geq 1,\|e_{n}\|=1\}
be an orthonormal basis for
H
. Then for each
x\in H
there exists a unique sequence of scalars
\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that
x=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}e_{k}.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N},
define
T_{n}:H\to H
by

T_{n}(x)=\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}e_{k}.

Clearly
\|T_{n}(x)\|\leq\|x\|
and
T_{n}(e_{n+1})=e_{n+1}.
This implies that
\|T_{n}\|=1~\forall~n\in\mathbb{N}.
Now observe that

\|T_{n}(x)\|=\|\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}e_{k}\|\to 0~\text{as}~n\to\infty.

Hence for each
T\in\mathcal{B}(H)
and
H\in H

\|TT_{n}(x)\|\leq\|T\|\|T_{n}(x)\|\to 0~as~n\to\infty.

Therefore
T
is a strongly TDZ in
\mathcal{B}(H)
.
∎

Remark 3.37
.

We conjecture that in the above theorem
H
can be replaced by any Banach space with Schauder basis.

Remark 3.38
.

Since the space
\ell^{p}~(1\leq p\leq\infty)
has
\{e_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
as a Schauder basis, where each
e_{n}
is defined as
e_{n}=(0,0,...,1,0,0,...).
Therefore by Theorem
3.36
, each
T\in\mathcal{B}(\ell^{p})~(1\leq p<\infty)
is a strongly TDZ in
\mathcal{B}(\ell^{p})
.

In the following example, we construct a class of TDZ in
\mathcal{B}(\ell^{p})~(1\leq p\leq\infty).

Example 3.39
.

Let
c_{0}=\{(y_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}:y_{n}\in\mathbb{C}~\forall~n\in\mathbb{N}~\text{and}~y_{n}\to 0~\text{as}~n\to\infty\}.
Let
y\in c_{0}
and
T:\ell^{p}\to\ell^{p}
be defined by
T(x):=(x_{1}y_{1},x_{2}y_{2},...,x_{n}y_{n},...)
. Then
T
is TDZ in
\mathcal{B}(\ell^{p}).

Proof.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N},
define
T_{n}:\ell^{p}\to\ell^{p}
by

T_{n}(x)=(0,0,...,x_{n+1}y_{n+1},x_{n+2}y_{n+2},...).

Then
\|T_{n}\|=1
and
T_{n}T(x)=(0,0,...,x_{n+1}y_{n+1},x_{n+2}y_{n+2},...).
Clearly, for each
x\in\ell^{p}

\|T_{n}Tx\|\leq\sup_{k\geq n+1}|y_{k}|\|x\|.

Since
y\in c_{0},
then
\sup_{k\geq n+1}|y_{k}|\to 0
as
n\to 0.
Therefore
\|T_{n}Tx\|\to 0
as
n\to 0.
Hence
T
is a TDZ.
∎

Remark 3.40
.

We note that the class of operators in the above Example
3.39
forms a class of compact operators.
