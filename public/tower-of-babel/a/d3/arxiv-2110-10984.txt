The popular assignment problem: when cardinality is more important than popularity
Telikepalli Kavitha, Tamás Király, Jannik Matuschke, Ildikó Schlotter, Ulrike Schmidt-Kraepelin

Abstract
We consider a matching problem in a bipartite graph G = ( A ∪ B , E ) G=(A\cup B,E) where nodes in A A are agents having preferences in partial order over their neighbors, while nodes in B B are objects without preferences. We propose a polynomial-time combinatorial algorithm based on LP duality that finds a maximum matching or assignment in G G that is popular among all maximum matchings, if there exists one. Our algorithm can also be used to achieve a trade-off between popularity and cardinality by imposing a penalty on unmatched nodes in A A . We also provide an O ∗ ​ ( | E | k ) O^{*}(|E|^{k}) algorithm that finds an assignment whose unpopularity margin is at most k k ; this algorithm is essentially optimal, since the problem is 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -complete and 𝖶 l ​ [ 1 ] \mathsf{W}_{l}[1] -hard with parameter k k . We also prove that finding a popular assignment of minimum cost when each edge has an associated binary cost is 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -hard, even if agents have strict preferences. By contrast, we propose a polynomial-time algorithm for the variant of the popular assignment problem with forced/forbidden edges. Finally, we present an application in the context of housing markets.

The preference order

The model of one-sided preferences is often called the

Usually one seeks a matching in

in

.

The

This is a well-studied problem from 2005,
and there is an efficient algorithm to solve it

The popular assignment problem.

Consider applications where the
size
of the matching is of primary importance. It is natural that as many students as possible be assigned campus housing. Another application is in assigning final year medical and nursing students to hospitals during emergencies (such as a pandemic) to overcome staff shortage
[
11

maximum matching
, i.e., among maximum matchings, a best one.
Our approach to prioritize the cardinality of the matching is in stark contrast with most existing results in the area of popular matchings,
where the foremost requirement is usually popularity.

By augmenting

admits a perfect matching, i.e., an

. So
our problem becomes the popular perfect matching problem—we will call this the

problem in
G
.
In other words, we seek an assignment of objects to agents such that every agent is assigned an object and, roughly speaking, there is no assignment
that makes more agents happier than it makes unhappier.

A perfect matching

\Delta(M,N)\geq 0

.

Thus, a popular assignment is a

Weak Condorcet winners need not exist in a general voting instance; in our setting as well, a popular assignment need not exist in

Consider the following simple example where

, i.e.,

For any assignment here, there exists a more popular assignment, e.g.,

is more popular than

\{(a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2}),(a_{3},b_{3})\}

The
popular assignment
problem.

Given a bipartite graph
G=(A\cup B,E)
where every
a\in A
has preferences in partial order
over her neighbors, does

It is easy to show instances that admit popular assignments but do not have any popular matching (see Section

). Interestingly, an algorithm for the

Furthermore, we introduce a family of problems that interpolates between these two problems by introducing a penalty (parameterized by

popular matching

By adjusting the usage of worst-choice objects appropriately, an algorithm for

the following more general variant of both the

Popularity with diversity.

Public housing programs constitute an application where such problems arise.

1.1
Our contribution

Our first result is that the
popular assignment
problem (with general partial order preferences) can be solved in polynomial time. Let
|A|=|B|=n
and
|E|=m
.

Theorem 1.1

The
popular assignment
problem in
G=(A\cup B,E)
can be solved in
O(m\cdot n^{5/2})
time.

Note that Theorem
1.1
implies that the
popular matching
problem with general partial order preferences can be solved in polynomial time, using the reduction described in Section
2.3
.
Curiously, the popular matching algorithm in
[
2
]
does not generalize to partial order preferences; we include an example that illustrates this in Section
2.1
.
To the best of our knowledge, no polynomial-time algorithm for solving the
popular matching
problem with partial order preferences has been known prior to Theorem
1.1
.

Partial order preferences arise naturally, e.g., when agents have cardinal utilities for the objects that then induce
semiorder
preferences.
This means that
for any agent, a pair of objects with widely differing utilities get compared by their utilities, while a pair of objects with utilities that are within a small threshold
are deemed equivalent, thus the agent is indifferent between two such objects.

2

Yu Yokoi posed the
popular matching
problem with semiorder preferences as an open problem in a talk on “Approximation Algorithms for Matroidal and Cardinal Generalizations of Stable Matching” at RIMS, Kyoto in May 2023 (she was not aware that the conference version of our paper
[
20
]
solves the
popular matching
problem with partial order preferences).
Observe that the indifference relation here need not be transitive.

When a popular assignment does not exist in
G
, a natural extension is to ask for an

popular assignment, i.e., an assignment with
low

unpopularity. How do we measure the unpopularity of an assignment? A well-known measure is the
unpopularity margin

[
23
]

defined for any assignment

as
\mu(M)=\max_{N}(\phi(N,M)-\phi(M,N))=\max_{N}\Delta(N,M)
,
where the maximum is taken over all assignments, that is, all perfect matchings
N
in
G
. Thus
\mu(M)
is the maximum margin by which another assignment defeats
M
.

An assignment

is popular if and only if
\mu(M)=0
. Let the
k
-unpopularity margin
problem be the problem of deciding if
G
admits an
assignment with unpopularity margin at most
k
. We generalize Theorem
1.1
to show the following result.

Theorem 1.2

For any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, the
k
-unpopularity margin
problem in
G=(A\cup B,E)
can be solved in
O(m^{k+1}\cdot n^{5/2})
time.

So when

, this is a polynomial-time algorithm to find a
k
-unpopularity margin assignment if
G
admits one.
Via the reduction described in Section
2.3
, which preserves the unpopularity margin of any matching, Theorem
1.2
extends to the
k
-unpopularity margin
version of the
popular matching
problem, i.e., to decide if
G
admits a matching with unpopularity margin at most
k
.
Though the measure of unpopularity margin was introduced in 2008, no polynomial-time algorithm was known so far to solve the
k
-unpopularity margin matching
problem for any

.

Rather than the exponential dependency on the parameter
k
in Theorem
1.2
,
can we solve the
k
-unpopularity margin
problem in polynomial time?
Or at least can we achieve a running time of the form
f(k)\mathsf{poly}(m,n)
for some function

so that the degree of the polynomial is independent of

?
That is, can we get a
fixed-parameter tractable
algorithm with parameter

?
The following hardness result shows that the algorithm of Theorem

is
essentially optimal for the
k
-unpopularity margin
problem. See Section

\mathsf{W}_{l}[1]
-hardness.

Theorem 1.3

The

-unpopularity margin
problem is
\mathsf{W}_{l}[1]
-hard with parameter

and it is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete

Popularity with penalty for unmatched agents.

We also
consider a model that allows for a trade-off between popularity and cardinality by imposing a
penalty
for unmatched agents. A matching
M
in
G=(A\cup B,E)
is
popular with penalty

if it does not lose a pairwise

any matching
N
in
G
, where votes of agents that are matched in

but unmatched in

(or vice versa) are counted with weight

see Section

If

is a matching that is popular with penalty

|M|\geq\frac{t}{t+1}|M_{\max}|

is a maximum matching in

We prove that a

that is popular with penalty

popular assignment
problem (without penalty). Moreover, we show that a truncated variant of our algorithm for the
popular assignment
problem can find an
assignment
that is popular with penalty

(when compared against any matching), if such an assignment exists.

Minimum-cost popular assignment.

We next consider the
minimum-cost popular assignment
problem: suppose that, in addition to our instance
G=(A\cup B,E)
of the
popular assignment
problem
where every agent has preferences in partial order
over her adjacent objects,
there is a cost function
c:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
on the edges and a budget
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, and we want to decide whether

admits a popular assignment whose sum of edge costs is at most

.
Computing a minimum-cost popular assignment efficiently would also imply an efficient algorithm for finding a popular assignment with
forced/forbidden
edges. We show the following hardness result.

Theorem 1.4

The
minimum-cost popular assignment
problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete, even if each edge cost is in
\{0,1\}

and agents have strict preferences.

This result reveals a surprising difference between the
popular assignment
problem and the
popular matching
problem; for the latter problem, arbitrary linear cost functions can be minimized efficiently when preferences are
weak rankings
[
2
]
.
The

\mathsf{NP}
-hard (even for strict preferences) to find a popular assignment that contains the maximum number of desired edges.
Interestingly, in spite of the above hardness result,
we show the following positive result that

Note that the assignment

assignments, not only those adhering to the forced and forbidden edge constraints.

Theorem 1.5

Given a set

such that

Thus the

problem is reminiscent of the well-known

problem

;
in a roommates instance,

even with forced/forbidden edges
[

1.2
Background

The notion of popularity in a marriage instance (where preferences are
two-sided
and strict) was introduced by Gärdenfors
[
15
]
in 1975.
Popular matchings always exist in such an instance, since any stable matching is popular
[
15
]
. When preferences are one-sided, popular matchings
need not exist.
For the case when preferences are weak rankings, a simple and clean combinatorial characterization of popular matchings (see Section
2.1
) was given in
[
2
]
,
leading to an
O(m\sqrt{n})
time algorithm
[
2
]
for the
popular matching
problem.
By contrast, a combinatorial characterization of popular assignments (even for strict rankings) remains elusive.
Finding a minimum unpopularity margin matching was proved to be
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
[
23
]
.

In the last fifteen years, popularity has been a widely studied concept.
Researchers have considered extensions of the
popular matching
problem
where one aims for a popular matching satisfying some additional optimality criteria such as rank-maximality or fairness
[
22
,
24
]
,
or where the notion of popularity is adjusted to incorporate capacitated objects or weighted agents
[
26
,
29
]
.
Another variant of the popular matching problem was considered in
[
9
]
where nodes in
A
have strict preferences and nodes in
B
, i.e.,
objects, have no preferences, however each object cares to be matched to any of its neighbors. We refer to
[
8
]
for a survey on results in this area.

Among the literature on popular matchings, only a few studies have considered a setting that focuses on popularity
within a restricted set of admissible solutions.
The paper that comes closest to our work is
[
17
]
which considered the popular maximum matching problem
in a marriage instance (where preferences are two-sided and strict). It was shown there that a popular maximum matching always exists in a marriage instance and
one such matching can be computed in
O(mn)
time; the following size-popularity trade-off in a marriage instance was also shown in
[
17
]
: for any integer
t\geq 2
, there exists a matching
M_{t}
with
|M_{t}|\geq\frac{t}{t+1}|M_{\max}|
and
unpopularity factor
at most
t-1
.
4
4

4

The unpopularity factor of a matching
M
is
\max_{N}\phi(N,M)/\phi(M,N)
, where the maximum is taken over all matchings
N\neq M
in
G

[
23
]
.

Very recently, it was shown in
[
19
]
that a minimum-cost popular maximum matching in a marriage
instance can be computed in polynomial time. These results use the rich machinery of stable matchings in a marriage instance
[
14
,
27
]
.
In contrast to these positive results for popular maximum matchings, computing an
almost-
stable maximum matching (one with the least number of blocking edges)
in a marriage instance is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
[
4
]
.

1.3
Techniques

Our popular assignment algorithm is based on LP duality. We show that a matching
M
is a popular assignment if and only if it
has a
dual certificate

\vec{\alpha}\in\{0,\pm 1,\ldots,\pm(n-1)\}^{2n}
fulfilling certain constraints induced by the matching
M
.
Our algorithm (see Section
4
) can be viewed as a search for such a dual certificate.
It associates a
level

\ell(b)
with every
b\in B
.
This level function
\ell
guides us in constructing a subgraph
G_{\ell}
of
G
.
If
G_{\ell}
contains a perfect matching, then this matching is a popular assignment in
G
and the levels determine a corresponding dual certificate.
If
G_{\ell}
has no perfect matching, then we increase some
\ell
-values and update
G_{\ell}
accordingly, until eventually
G_{\ell}
contains a perfect matching or the level of an object increases beyond the permitted range, in which case we prove that no popular assignment exists.

The LP method for popular matchings was introduced in
[
21
]
and dual certificates for popular (maximum) matchings in marriage instances were shown in
[
18
,
19
]
.
However, dual certificates for popular matchings in instances with one-sided preferences have not been investigated so far. The existence of simple dual certificates
for popular assignments is easy to show (see Section
3
), but this does not automatically imply polynomial-time solvability. The main novelty of our paper lies in showing a combinatorial algorithm to search for dual certificates in an instance
G
and in
using this approach to solve the
popular assignment
problem, as well as several related problems, in polynomial time.

Our other results.

Our algorithm for the variant of the
popular assignment
problem with
forced/ forbidden
edges (see Section
5
)
is a natural extension of the above algorithm where
certain edges are excluded. The
k
-unpopularity margin
algorithm (see Section
6
) associates a
load
with every edge such that the total load is at most
k
and the overloaded edges are treated as
forced
edges.
Our
W_{l}[1]
-hardness result shows that this
O^{*}(m^{k})
algorithm for the
k
-unpopularity margin
problem is essentially optimal, i.e., it is highly unlikely that this problem admits an
f(k)m^{o(k)}
algorithm for any computable function
f
.

The preferences of node

is a

.

.

2.1
A characterization of popular matchings from
[
2
]
for weak rankings

In order to characterize popular matchings
in the case when agents’ preferences are weak rankings,
as done in
[
2
]
, it will be convenient to add artificial worst-choice or last
resort objects to the given instance
G=(A\cup B,E)
. So
B=B\cup\{l(a):a\in A\}
, i.e., corresponding to each
a\in A
, a node
l(a)

gets added to
B
and we set this node
l(a)
as the worst-choice object for
a
. Thus we have
E=E\cup\{(a,l(a)):a\in A\}
.

We say that an edge
(a,b)

dominates
an edge
(a,b^{\prime})
, if
b\succ_{a}b^{\prime}
. We let
E_{1}
denote the set of edges that are not dominated by any edge in
E
, we will call these
first-choice edges
.
We call an edge
e\in E

critical
if the maximum matching in
G[E_{1}\cup\{e\}]
is larger than a maximum matching in
G[E_{1}]
.
We define the set
E_{2}
of
second-choice edges
as those edges that are critical, and are not dominated by any critical edge.

Theorem 2.1
(
[
2
]
)

A matching
M
in
G=(A\cup B,E)
is popular if and only if

(1)

M
is a matching in
G[E_{1}\cup E_{2}]
that matches all agents in
A
, and

(2)

M\cap E_{1}
is a maximum matching in
G[E_{1}]
.

The above characterization was given in
[
2
]
for instances where agents’ preferences are weak rankings, and
the following example shows that it does not
hold for general partial orders. Let
G=(A\cup B,E)
where
A=\{a,b,c\}
and
B=\{x,y,z\}
, each agent is adjacent to each object, and the preference orders of the agents are defined by

\begin{array}[]{lll}a:&x\succ_{a}z,&y\succ_{a}z;\\
b:&x\succ_{b}z;\\
c:&y\succ_{c}x,&y\succ_{c}z.\\
\end{array}

Thus
x\sim_{a}y
, similarly,
x\sim_{b}y
and
y\sim_{b}z
, and also
x\sim_{c}z
.
The set of first-choice edges is then
E_{1}=\{(a,x),(a,y),(b,x),(b,y),(c,y)\}
, the set of second-choice edges is
E_{2}=\{(a,z),(b,z),(c,z)\}
. Consider now the matching
M=\{(a,x),(b,y),(c,z)\}
.
It is straightforward to verify that

However,

and

and

2.2
An instance without popular matchings that admits a popular assignment

We describe a simple example that does not admit any popular matching, but admits a popular assignment. Let

where

and

and the preference order of both

and

while the preference order of

It follows from the characterization of popular matchings from

that a popular matching

has to match

2.3
Some simple reductions to the popular assignment problem

We will first show a reduction from the
popular matching
problem to the
popular assignment
problem.
Let
G=(A\cup B,E)
be an instance of the
popular matching
problem.
Let
B^{\prime}=B\cup\{l(a):a\in A\}
. That is, corresponding to each
a\in A
, an object
l(a)
(the last resort of
a
)
is in
B^{\prime}
and we set this object
l(a)
as the worst-choice of
a
. Let
A^{\prime}=A\cup\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{|B|}\}
, i.e., there are
|B|
many dummy agents in
A^{\prime}
.
Each dummy agent
d_{i}
is adjacent to all objects in
B^{\prime}
and is indifferent between any two of them.

It is easy to see that every matching
M
in
G
can be extended to a
perfect matching

M^{\prime}
in this new graph
G^{\prime}=(A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime},E^{\prime})
and conversely,
every perfect matching
M^{\prime}
in
G^{\prime}
projects to a matching
M
in
G
. For any pair of matchings
M
and
N
in
G
, observe that

\Delta(M,N)=\Delta(M^{\prime},N^{\prime})
. Thus
M
and
M^{\prime}
have the same unpopularity margin and, in particular, an algorithm that finds a popular assignment in
G^{\prime}
solves the
popular matching
problem in
G
.

Popularity with diversity.

Recall this problem defined in Section
1
where every agent in an instance
G=(A\cup B,E)

has one of
k
colors associated with it, and admissible matchings are those that for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
match

c_{i}
agents of color
i
so that
s_{i}\leq c_{i}\leq t_{i}
for some given bounds
s_{i}
and
t_{i}
.
We seek a matching that is popular within the set of admissible matchings.

We augment
B
by adding
n_{i}-s_{i}
artificial objects for each
i
, where
n_{i}
is the number of agents colored
i
.
For each
i
, these
n_{i}-s_{i}
objects are tied as the worst-choices of all agents colored
i
.
Let

, where

.
Every dummy agent

is adjacent to all objects in

some fixed
t_{i}-s_{i}

introduced above—as before,

is indifferent between any two of its neighbors.
So for each

, there are

artificial objects not adjacent to any dummy agent. Let

It is easy to see that an algorithm that finds a popular assignment in

3
Dual certificates for popular assignments

Let
G=(A\cup B,E)
be an input instance and let
\nu
be the size of a maximum matching in
G
. Let us augment
G
with
|B|-\nu
dummy agents that are adjacent to all objects in
B
(and indifferent among them), along with
|A|-\nu
artificial objects that are tied as the worst-choice neighbors of all non-dummy agents. Any maximum matching
M
in
the original graph extends to a perfect matching (i.e., assignment)
M^{\prime}
in the augmented graph; moreover,
\Delta(M,N)=\Delta(M^{\prime},N^{\prime})
for any pair of maximum matchings
M
and
N
in
G
.
Thus, we can assume without loss of generality that the input instance
G
admits a perfect matching.

Let
|A|=|B|=n
and
|E|=m
. Let
M
be any perfect matching in
G
.
The following edge weight function
\mathsf{wt}_{M}
in
G
will be useful. For any
(a,b)\in E

\mathrm{let}\ \mathsf{wt}_{M}(a,b)=\begin{cases}\phantom{-}1&\text{if\ $a$\ prefers\ $b$\ to\ $M(a)$;}\\
-1&\text{if\ $a$\ prefers\ $M(a)$\ to\ $b$;}\\
\phantom{-}0&\text{otherwise,\ i.e., if\ $b\sim_{a}M(a)$.}\end{cases}

Let
\mathsf{wt}_{M}(N)=\sum_{e\in N}\mathsf{wt}_{M}(e)
for any edge set
N\subseteq E
.
Consider the following linear program
LP1
and its dual
LP2
.

\displaystyle\max\sum_{e\in E}

\displaystyle\mathsf{wt}_{M}(e)\cdot x_{e}

(LP1)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_{e\in\delta(u)}x_{e}=1

\displaystyle\mbox{\hskip 7.22743pt}\forall\,u\in A\cup B

\displaystyle x_{e}\geq 0

\displaystyle\mbox{\hskip 7.22743pt}\forall\,e\in E.

\displaystyle\min\sum_{u\in A\cup B}y_{u}

(LP2)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad y_{a}+y_{b}\geq\mathsf{wt}_{M}(a,b)

\displaystyle\mbox{\hskip 7.22743pt}\forall\,(a,b)\in E.

LP1
is well known to be integral, and hence its optimal value is
\max_{N}\mathsf{wt}_{M}(N)
where
N
is a perfect matching in
G
. The definition of
\mathsf{wt}_{M}
implies
that
\mathsf{wt}_{M}(N)=\Delta(N,M)
; recall the definition
\Delta(N,M)=\phi(N,M)-\phi(M,N)
.
So
M
is a popular assignment if and only if the optimal value of
LP1
is at most 0. In fact, the optimal
value of
LP1
is then exactly 0, by
\Delta(M,M)=0
. Hence for a popular assignment
M
,
the edge incidence vector of
M
is an optimal solution to
LP1
.

Theorem
3.1
gives a useful characterization of popular assignments.
The proof of Theorem
3.1
is given in
Section
6
along with the proof of a related result
(Theorem
6.1
).

Theorem 3.1

M
is a popular assignment if and only if there exists an optimal solution
\vec{\alpha}
to
LP2
such that

\alpha_{a}\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,(n-1)\}
for all
a\in A
,

\alpha_{b}\in\{0,-1,-2,\ldots,-(n-1)\}
for all
b\in B
, and
\sum_{u\in A\cup B}\alpha_{u}=0
.

A
dual certificate
for a popular assignment
M
is an optimal solution
\vec{\alpha}
to
LP2
satisfying the conditions given in Theorem
3.1
.

4
The popular assignment algorithm

The goal of our algorithm is to construct a perfect matching
M
in
G
along with a dual certificate
\vec{\alpha}
.
Every
b\in B
will have an associated
level

\ell(b)
in this algorithm and the
\alpha
-value
of
b
will be
-\ell(b)
,
i.e., we set
\alpha_{b}=-\ell(b)
.

Given a function
\ell:B\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
called a
level function
, for any
a\in A
let
\ell^{*}(a)=\max_{b\in\mathsf{Nbr}(a)}\ell(b)

be the highest level at which agent
a
has neighbors.
Now we define the subgraph
G_{\ell}=(A\cup B,E_{\ell})

induced by levels
\ell(\cdot)
by putting an edge
(a,b)\in E
into
E_{\ell}
if and only if

(i)

b
has level
\ell^{*}(a)
, and
a
has no neighbor in level
\ell^{*}(a)
that she prefers to
b
, or

(ii)

b
has level
\ell^{*}(a)-1
, and
a
prefers
b
to each of her neighbors in level
\ell^{*}(a)
, and moreover,
a
prefers
none of her neighbors in level
\ell^{*}(a)-1
to
b
.

Thus in the subgraph
G_{\ell}
,
every agent has edges to her favorite
highest-level
neighbors and to her favorite neighbors one level below, provided these neighbors
are preferred to all of her highest-level neighbors (see Fig.
2
for an illustration). The following lemma will be very useful.

Lemma 1

A matching
M
in
G
is a popular assignment if and only if there exists a level function
\ell
such that
M
is a perfect matching in

G_{\ell}
. Furthermore, this happens if and only if
there is a level function
\ell
and
a dual certificate
\vec{\alpha}
for
M
where

\ell(b)=|\alpha_{b}|
for all
b\in B
and
M
is a perfect matching in
G_{\ell}
.

Proof

Let us first show that if there exists a level function
\ell
such that
M
is a perfect matching in
G_{\ell}
, then
M
is a popular assignment in
G
.
We construct a dual certificate for
M
as follows.
Let
\alpha_{b}=-\ell(b)
for all
b\in B
and
\alpha_{a}=\ell(M(a))
for all
a\in A
.
Note that the value of
\vec{\alpha}
as a solution for
LP2
is
\sum_{v\in A\cup B}\alpha_{v}=\sum_{a\in A}(\alpha_{a}+\alpha_{M(a)})=0=\sum_{e\in M}\mathsf{wt}_{M}(e)
. Thus
\vec{\alpha}
is optimal for
LP2
if it is feasible.
It remains to show that
\alpha_{a}+\alpha_{b}\geq\mathsf{wt}_{M}(a,b)
for every
(a,b)\in E
.

So let
(a,b)\in E
and let
b^{\prime}:=M(a)
. Note that
\alpha_{a}+\alpha_{b}=\ell(b^{\prime})-\ell(b)
. We show that
\ell(b^{\prime})-\ell(b)\geq\mathsf{wt}_{M}(a,b)
.
Because
(a,b^{\prime})\in E_{\ell}
, one of the following cases holds:

•

Case (i):
\ell(b^{\prime})=\ell^{*}(a)
, so
a
prefers no neighbor of hers in level
\ell^{*}(a)
to
b^{\prime}
. We have two subcases:

–

If
\ell(b)=\ell^{*}(a)
, then
a
does not prefer
b
to
b^{\prime}
and hence
\mathsf{wt}_{M}(a,b)\leq 0=\ell(b^{\prime})-\ell(b)
.

–

If
\ell(b)<\ell^{*}(a)
, then
\ell(b^{\prime})-\ell(b)\geq 1\geq\mathsf{wt}_{M}(a,b)
.

•

Case (ii):
\ell(b^{\prime})=\ell^{*}(a)-1
, so
a
prefers
b^{\prime}
to each of her neighbors in level
\ell^{*}(a)
, and
a
prefers
none of her neighbors in level
\ell^{*}(a)-1
to
b^{\prime}
.
We have three subcases:

–

If
\ell(b)=\ell^{*}(a)
, then
a
prefers
b^{\prime}
to
b
and hence
\mathsf{wt}_{M}(a,b)=-1=\ell(b^{\prime})-\ell(b)
.

–

If
\ell(b)=\ell^{*}(a)-1
, then
a
does not prefer
b
to
b^{\prime}
and hence
\mathsf{wt}_{M}(a,b)\leq 0=\ell(b^{\prime})-\ell(b)
.

–

If
\ell(b)<\ell^{*}(a)-1
, then
\ell(b^{\prime})-\ell(b)\geq 1\geq\mathsf{wt}_{M}(a,b)
.

Thus in each of these cases
\mathsf{wt}_{M}(a,b)\leq\ell(b^{\prime})-\ell(b)=\alpha_{a}+\alpha_{b}
. Hence
\vec{\alpha}
is a dual certificate for
M
, and thus
M
is a popular assignment by Theorem
3.1
.

\alpha_{a}+\alpha_{b}\geq\mathsf{wt}_{M}(a,b)

The algorithm.

Consider Algorithm
1
on input
G=(A\cup B,E)
.
In search for a dual certificate, this algorithm will maintain a
level

\ell(b)
for every
b\in B
.
Initially,
\ell(b)=0
for every
b\in B
.

The algorithm checks whether there exists
a popular assignment by computing a perfect matching in the graph
G_{\ell}
.
If no such matching exists, the levels of unmatched objects are increased, the graph
G_{\ell}
is updated accordingly, and the search continues.

Eventually, either a perfect matching in
G_{\ell}
is found,
or the level of an object exceeds
n-1
. In the latter case we can conclude that no popular assignment exists, as we will show below.

Algorithm 1

Finding a popular assignment in
G=(A\cup B,E)

1:

for all

b\in B

do

\ell(b)=0
.

2:

while

\ell(b)<n
for all
b\in B

do

3:

Construct the graph
G_{\ell}
and compute a maximum matching
M
in
G_{\ell}
.

4:

if

M
is a perfect matching
then

return

M
.

5:

for all

b\in B
unmatched in
M

do

\ell(b)=\ell(b)+1
.

6:

return
“
G
has no popular assignment”.

Running time.

Computing a maximum matching in
G_{\ell}
takes
O(m\sqrt{n})
time.
In every iteration of the algorithm, the value
\sum_{b\in B}\ell(b)
increases.
So the number of iterations is at most
n^{2}
. Hence the running time of our algorithm is
O(m\cdot n^{5/2})
.

Theorem 4.1

If our algorithm returns a matching
M
, then
M
is a popular assignment in
G
.

Theorem
4.1
follows immediately from Lemma
1
. The more difficult part in our proof of correctness is to show that whenever our algorithm says that
G
has no popular assignment, the instance
G
indeed has no popular assignment. This is implied by Theorem
4.2
.

Theorem 4.2

Let
M^{\star}
be a popular assignment in
G
and let
\vec{\alpha}
be a dual certificate of
M^{\star}
.
Then for every
b\in B
, we have
|\alpha_{b}|\geq\ell(b)
, where
\ell(b)
is the level of
b
when our algorithm terminates.

If our algorithm terminates because
\ell(b)=n
for some
b\in B
, then
|\alpha_{b}|\geq n
for any dual certificate
\vec{\alpha}
by Theorem
4.2
. However
|\alpha_{b}|\leq n-1
by definition, a contradiction. So
G
has no popular assignment.

The following lemma is crucial for proving Theorem
4.2
. It guarantees that when the algorithm increases
\ell(b)
for some unmatched object
b\in B
, then the new level function does not exceed
|\alpha_{b}|
.

Lemma 2

Let
M^{\star}
be a popular assignment, let
\vec{\alpha}
be a dual certificate of
M^{\star}
, and let

\ell:B\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
be such that
\ell(b)\leq|\alpha_{b}|
for all
b\in B
.
Let
M
be a maximum matching in
G_{\ell}
and let
b_{0}\in B
be an object that is left unmatched in
M
.
Then
\ell(b_{0})<|\alpha_{b_{0}}|
.

Before we turn to the proof of Lemma
2
, we point out that Theorem
4.2
follows from this lemma by a simple induction.

Proof
(of Theorem
4.2
)

Let
\ell_{i}
be the level function on the set
B
at the start of the
i
-th iteration of our
algorithm.
We are going to show by induction that for every

we have
|\alpha_{b}|\geq\ell_{i}(b)
for all
b\in B
.
This is true for
i=1
, since
\ell_{1}(b)=0
for all
b\in B
.
Now suppose that
|\alpha_{b}|\geq\ell_{i}(b)
for all
b\in B
.
Let
b_{0}\in B
.
If
b_{0}
is matched in the maximum matching

in
G_{\ell_{i}}
, then we know
\ell_{i+1}(b_{0})=\ell_{i}(b_{0})\leq|\alpha_{b_{0}}|
. If
b_{0}
is
left unmatched in

, then
\ell_{i+1}(b_{0})=\ell_{i}(b_{0})+1
and
\ell_{i}(b_{0})<|\alpha_{b_{0}}|
by Lemma

. Thus
\ell_{i+1}(b_{0})\leq|\alpha_{b_{0}}|
in either case, completing the induction. ∎

Proof
(of Lemma
2
)

By Lemma
1
,
M^{\star}
is a perfect matching in
G_{\ell_{\vec{\alpha}}}
where
\ell_{\vec{\alpha}}(b)=|\alpha_{b}|
for

.
Thus, the symmetric difference
M\oplus M^{\star}
in

contains an
M

P
starting at

.
However, as

is of maximum size in

must contain an edge that is not in

.

Let
(b_{0},a_{0},b_{1},a_{1},\dots,b_{t},a_{t})

such that

(see Fig.

Note that

and

.
Thus

for all

.
We will show that

We first show that

Assume for contradiction that

Using the fact that

\bullet

Now suppose there exists some

Recall the reduction from the

problem to the
popular assignment
problem, as described in Section
2.3
.
It can be shown that for graphs obtained
through this reduction
(i.e., those with last resort objects and dummy agents)
we can terminate our algorithm as soon as some object reaches level
2
.
This is because in any such instance, there exists a dual certificate
\vec{\alpha}
for a popular assignment that satisfies
|\alpha_{b}|\leq 1
for all
b\in B
, as shown in Proposition
1
below.

Proposition 1

Let
G=(A\cup B,E)
be an instance of the
popular matching
problem, and let
G^{\prime}=(A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime},E^{\prime})
be the corresponding instance of
popular assignment
as constructed in Section
2.3
. If a popular matching exists in
G
, then there exists a dual certificate
\vec{\alpha}
for a popular assignment
M^{\prime}
in
G^{\prime}
that satisfies
|\alpha_{b}|\leq 1
for all
b\in B^{\prime}
.

Proof

It is straightforward to check that the reduction in Section
2.3
is correct, so
G
admits a popular matching if and only if
G^{\prime}
admits a popular assignment.
If
M^{\prime}
is a popular assignment in
G^{\prime}
, then by Theorem
3.1
there exists a dual certificate
\vec{\alpha}
for
M^{\prime}
.

We may assume that there exists some object
b_{0}\in B^{\prime}
for which
\alpha_{b_{0}}=0
.
Otherwise we have
\alpha_{b}\leq-1
for each
b\in B^{\prime}
, and since
\alpha_{b}+\alpha_{M^{\prime}(b)}=0
for each
b\in B^{\prime}
by complementary slackness, we also have
\alpha_{a}\geq 1
for each
a\in A^{\prime}
.
Therefore, decreasing the
\alpha
-value of each agent by
\min_{a\in A^{\prime}}\alpha_{a}
while increasing the
\alpha
-value of each object by the same amount results in a dual certificate where some object
b_{0}
has
\alpha
-value
0
.

Assume now for the sake of contradiction that
\alpha_{b}\leq-2
for some
b\in B^{\prime}
, and consider the agent
M^{\prime}(b_{0})
. Recall that
\alpha_{M^{\prime}(b_{0})}=0
follows from
\alpha_{b_{0}}=0
.

On the one hand, if
M^{\prime}(b_{0})
is a dummy agent
d
, then by construction it is adjacent to
b
, and moreover,
\mathsf{wt}_{M^{\prime}}(d,b)=0
, because
d
is indifferent between all objects in
B^{\prime}
. However, then
\alpha_{d}+\alpha_{b}\leq-2<\mathsf{wt}_{M^{\prime}}(d,b)=0
contradicts the feasibility of
\vec{\alpha}
.

On the other hand, if
M^{\prime}(b_{0})
is a non-dummy agent
a\in A
, then
\alpha_{l(a)}\geq-1
follows for the last resort object
l(a)
of
a
, due to
\mathsf{wt}_{M^{\prime}}(a,l(a))\geq-1
.
Since
a
is the unique non-dummy neighbor of
l(a)
in
G^{\prime}
and
M^{\prime}(a)=b_{0}
, we know that
l(a)
must be assigned to some dummy agent
d^{\prime}
in
M^{\prime}
.
By
\alpha_{l(a)}\geq-1
we know
\alpha_{d^{\prime}}\leq 1
; however, then the edge
(d^{\prime},b)
contradicts the feasibility of
\vec{\alpha}
by
\alpha_{d^{\prime}}+\alpha_{b}\leq-1<\mathsf{wt}_{M^{\prime}}(d^{\prime},b)=0
. ∎

Thanks to Proposition
1
, we can solve the
popular matching
problem by using Algorithm
1
and terminating it with rejection as soon as some object reaches level
2
. This implies that the number of iterations the algorithm performs is at most the number of objects.
Thus we have the following corollary.

Corollary 1

The
popular matching
problem where agents’ preferences are partial orders can be solved in
O(m\cdot n^{3/2})
time on a graph with
n
vertices and
m
edges.

Note that for weak rankings, a two-level truncation of Algorithm
1

essentially leads to the popular matching algorithm by Abraham et al.
[
2
]
.
However these two algorithms are quite different for partial order preferences, e.g., consider the example in
Section
2.1
. Running Algorithm
1
on this instance, we obtain
\ell(x)=\ell(y)=0
and
\ell(z)=1
at the last iteration.
This implies
(b,y)\notin E_{\ell}
, hence the unpopular matching
\{(a,x),(b,y),(c,z)\}
is not contained in
E_{\ell}
.

4.2
Popularity with penalties

In the following, we describe a family of problems that interpolates between the
popular matching
and
popular assignment
problem: given an instance
G=(A\cup B,E)
of
popular matching
, we say that a matching
M
in
G
is
popular with penalty
t
for some positive integer
t
, if there is no matching
N
in
G
such that
N
beats
M
in an election where agents vote between
M
and
N
according to their preferences, but votes of unmatched agents are counted with weight
t
. Formally, given two matchings
N
and
M
, define for any agent
a

\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a}(N,M)=\begin{cases}\phantom{-}1&\text{if\ $a$\ is matched in~$N$ and~$M$, and prefers\ $N(a)$\ to\ $M(a)$;}\\
-1&\text{if\ $a$\ is matched in~$N$ and~$M$, and prefers\ $M(a)$\ to\ $N(a)$;}\\
\phantom{-}t&\text{if\ $a$\ is matched in~$N$ but not in~$M$;}\\
-t&\text{if\ $a$\ is matched in~$M$ but not in~$N$;}\\
\phantom{-}0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then, the matching
M
is
popular with penalty
t
, if
\sum_{a\in A}\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a}(N,M)\leq 0
for each matching
N
. Clearly, for
t=1
, this property corresponds to the notion of popularity for matchings. Moreover, as long as
G
contains an assignment (perfect matching), a matching
M
is popular with penalty
t=n
if and only if
M
is a popular assignment. More generally, any augmenting path
P
with respect to a matching
M
that is popular with penalty
t
contains at least
t+1
agents, otherwise
\sum_{a\in A}\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a}(M\oplus P,M)>0
. Hence
|M|\geq\frac{t}{t+1}|M_{\max}|
where
M_{\max}
is a maximum matching in
G
.

In the following we show that a matching that is popular with penalty
t
can be found via a reduction to an instance of the
popular assignment
problem.
Moreover, we also show that we can determine whether there is an assignment that is popular with penalty
t
(when compared with matchings from
G
) by running a truncated version of Algorithm
1
.

Reduction for finding a matching that is popular with penalty
t
.

Given an instance
G=(A\cup B,E)
of
popular matching
and an integer
t
, let us construct an instance of
popular assignment

G^{\prime}=(A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime},E^{\prime})
as follows:

•

The set
A^{\prime}
of agents
contains all agents in
A
, a set
D=\{d_{1},\dots,d_{|B|}\}
of
|B|
dummy agents, and a set
\{p_{1}(a),\dots,p_{t-1}(a)\}
of new agents for each
a\in A
.

•

The set
B^{\prime}
of objects contains all objects in
B
and a set
\{l_{1}(a),\dots,l_{t}(a)\}
of new objects for each
a\in A
.

The edge set
E^{\prime}
of
G^{\prime}
contains the following edges in addition to the ones in the original set of edges
E
:

•

For each
a\in A
, a path
P(a)=(a,l_{1}(a),p_{1}(a),l_{2}(a),\dots,p_{t-1}(a),l_{t}(a))
is added;
moreover, the dummy agents are adjacent to every object in
B\cup L_{t}
where
L_{t}=\{l_{t}(a):a\in A\}
.

The preferences of the original instance are extended as follows:
Each original agent
a\in A
keeps her original preference order and in addition, prefers any object
b\in B
with
(a,b)\in E
to object
l_{1}(a)
, i.e.,
l_{1}(a)
is
a
’s unique worst-choice object.
For
a\in A
and
i\in[t-1]
, agent
p_{i}(a)
prefers object
l_{i}(a)
to object
l_{i+1}(a)
.
Finally, each dummy agent
d\in D
is indifferent among her adjacent objects.

For a matching
M
in
G
, we define a
corresponding assignment

M^{\prime}
in
G^{\prime}
as follows.
For each agent
a\in A
that is matched in
M
, define
M^{\prime}(a)=M(a)
and
M^{\prime}(p_{i}(a))=l_{i}(a)
for
i\in[t-1]
.
For each agent
a\in A
that is left unmatched in
M
, define
M^{\prime}(a)=l_{1}(a)
and
M^{\prime}(p_{i}(a))=l_{i+1}(a)
for
i\in[t-1]
.
Finally, we add an arbitrary perfect matching between the dummy agents in
D
and the
|B|
objects in
\{b\in B:b\text{ is left unmatched in }M\}\cup\{l_{t}(a):a\text{ is matched in }M\}
.

Note that, by construction of
G^{\prime}
, for every assignment
N^{\prime}
in
G^{\prime}
and every
a\in A
, either
N^{\prime}
contains the edges
(a,l_{1}(a))
,
(p_{1}(a),l_{2}(a))
, …,
(p_{t-1}(a),l_{t}(a))
or we have
N^{\prime}(a)\in B
,
N^{\prime}(l_{t}(a))\in D
, and
N^{\prime}
contains the edges
(p_{1}(a),l_{1}(a))
, …,
(p_{t-1}(a),l_{t-1}(a))
.
Hence, up to permutation of the dummy agents (which, due their indifference, do not influence pairwise comparisons) there is a one-to-one correspondence between matchings in
G
and assignments in
G^{\prime}
.

Proposition 2

Let
M
and
N
be two matchings in
G
and let
M^{\prime}
and
N^{\prime}
be the corresponding assignments in
G^{\prime}
. Then
\Delta(N^{\prime},M^{\prime})=\sum_{a\in A}\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a}(N,M)
.

Proof

When comparing assignments
N^{\prime}
and
M^{\prime}
, we write
\mathsf{vote}_{a}(N^{\prime},M^{\prime})
instead of
\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a}(N^{\prime},M^{\prime})
, since no agent is unmatched in an assignment. We claim that

\mathsf{vote}_{a}(N^{\prime},M^{\prime})+\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\mathsf{vote}_{p_{i}(a)}(N^{\prime},M^{\prime})=\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a}(N,M)

(1)

for each agent
a\in A
.
Note that for every agent
a\in A
that is either matched in both
M
and
N
or unmatched in both
M
and
N
, every agent
p_{i}(a)
for
i\in[t-1]
is matched to the same object in both
M^{\prime}
and
N^{\prime}
.
Hence (
1
) holds for such agent
a
.
Now consider an agent
a\in A
that is matched in
N
but not in
M
.
Note that in this case
\mathsf{vote}_{a}(N^{\prime},M^{\prime})+\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\mathsf{vote}_{p_{i}(a)}(N^{\prime},M^{\prime})=t
, because
\mathsf{vote}_{a}(N^{\prime},M^{\prime})=1
and because
p_{i}(a)
prefers
N(p_{i}(a))=l_{i}(a)
to
M(p_{i})=l_{i+1}(a)
for each
i\in[t-1]
,
so (
1
) holds again.
By a symmetric argument,
\mathsf{vote}_{a}(N^{\prime},M^{\prime})+\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\mathsf{vote}_{p_{i}(a)}(N^{\prime},M^{\prime})=-t
for all agents
a\in A
that are unmatched in
N^{\prime}
but matched in
M^{\prime}
. This proves our claim.
Because
\mathsf{vote}_{d}(N^{\prime},M^{\prime})=0
for all
d\in D
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\Delta(N^{\prime},M^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in A^{\prime}}\mathsf{vote}_{a}(N^{\prime},M^{\prime})=\sum_{a\in A}\left(\mathsf{vote}_{a}(N^{\prime},M^{\prime})+\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\mathsf{vote}_{p_{i}(a)}(N^{\prime},M^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in A}\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a}(N,M).

This completes the proof. ∎

By Proposition
2
, a matching
M
in
G
is popular with penalty
t
in
G
if and only if the corresponding assignment
M^{\prime}
in
G^{\prime}
is popular in
G^{\prime}
.
In particular, we can find a matching in
G
that is popular with penalty
t
, if it exists, in polynomial time by applying Algorithm
1
.
Furthermore, the structure of
G^{\prime}
allows us to show that the
(t+1)
-level truncation
of Algorithm
1
suffices to solve
the
popular assignment
problem in
G^{\prime}
, i.e.,
we terminate the algorithm with rejection as soon as some object reaches level
t+1
.
The proof of the following proposition is given in the appendix.

Proposition 3

If
M^{\prime}
is a popular assignment in
G^{\prime}
, then there exists a dual certificate
\vec{\alpha}
for
M^{\prime}
such that
|\alpha_{b}|\leq t
for each object
b\in B^{\prime}
.

Recall that there are
O(nt)
nodes and
O(n^{2})
edges in
G^{\prime}
where
n=|A\cup B|
and
m=|E|
.
Thus, the following result follows immediately from Proposition
2
and Proposition
3
.

Theorem 4.3

Given an instance
G=(A\cup B,E)
of the
popular matching
problem where
|A\cup B|=n
along with
t\in[n-1]
,
a matching that is popular with penalty
t
can be computed in
O(n^{7/2}\cdot t^{5/2})
time, if
G
admits such a matching.

Finding a popular assignment with penalty
t
.

In the following, we show that it is possible to determine whether an instance of
popular matching with penalty
t
has a solution
M
that is an assignment.
For any assignment
N
, we have
\sum_{a\in A}\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a}(N,M)=\Delta(N,M)
, hence any such solution
M
is also a popular assignment. Moreover,
for popularity with penalty
t
, an assignment is also compared with matchings in
G
.
This means that for an assignment, popularity with a given penalty is a stronger requirement than popularity within the set of assignments.

Below, we show that the
(t+1)
-level truncation of Algorithm
1
finds an assignment that is popular with penalty
t
(if it exists). In contrast to the previous result, we can directly execute the truncated algorithm on
G
without applying the reduction.

Theorem 4.4

For any positive integer
t
, the
(t+1)
-level truncation of Algorithm
1
finds an assignment that is popular with penalty
t
, if such an assignment exists.
The running time on a graph with
n
vertices and
m
edges is
O(t\cdot m\cdot n^{3/2})
.

Proof

Suppose first that Algorithm
1
outputs an assignment
M
while all objects have level at most
t
; by the correctness of Algorithm
1
we know that
M
is a popular assignment. We claim that
M
is also popular with penalty
t
.

For the sake of contradiction, assume that
N
is a matching that beats
M
, i.e., for which
\sum_{a\in A}\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a}(N,M)>0
, and consider the symmetric difference
N\oplus M
of
N
and
M
.
For each connected component
K
in
N\oplus M
, let
A_{K}
and
B_{K}
denote the set of agents and objects in
K
, respectively.
Observe that there must exist a connected component
K
of
N\oplus M
for which we have
\sum_{a\in A_{K}}\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a}(N,M)>0
.

First, if
K
is a cycle, then the matching
M^{\prime}=M\oplus K
is an assignment, and we get that
\sum_{a\in A}\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a}(M^{\prime},M)=\sum_{a\in A_{K}}\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a}(M^{\prime},M)=\sum_{a\in A_{K}}\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a}(N,M)>0
, which contradicts the fact that
M
is a popular assignment.

Second, if
K
is a path, then since
M
is a perfect matching, the two endpoints of
K
must be an agent
a
and an object
b
, both matched in
M
but unmatched in
N
.
Let the vertices of the path
K
be
a=a_{0},b_{0},a_{1},b_{1},\dots,a_{t},b_{t}=b
for some
t\in\mathbb{N}
, so that
M(a_{i})=b_{i}
for
i=0,1,\dots,t
and
N(a_{i})=b_{i-1}
for
i=1,\dots,t
.
Let
\vec{\alpha}
be the dual certificate for
M

for which
\ell_{\vec{\alpha}}
is the level-function obtained by Algorithm
1
.

On the one hand, by complementary slackness we know
\sum_{i=0}^{t}(\alpha_{a_{i}}+\alpha_{b_{i}})=0
.
On the other hand, for each
i\in[t]
we know
\mathsf{wt}_{M}(a_{i},b_{i-1})=\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a_{i}}(N,M)
by definition, which implies

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{t}(\alpha_{a_{i}}+\alpha_{b_{i-1}})\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathsf{wt}_{M}(a_{i},b_{i-1})=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a_{i}}(N,M)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t}\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a_{i}}(N,M)\right)-\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a_{0}}(N,M)=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t}\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a_{i}}(N,M)\right)+t>t

because
\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a_{0}}(N,M)=-t
.
This leads to

0=\sum_{i=0}^{t}(\alpha_{a_{i}}+\alpha_{b_{i}})=\alpha_{a_{0}}+\alpha_{b_{t}}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\left(\alpha_{a_{i}}+\alpha_{b_{i-1}}\right)>\alpha_{b_{t}}+t

by
\alpha_{a_{0}}\geq 0
. However, this means
\ell_{\vec{\alpha}}(b_{t})=|\alpha_{b_{t}}|>t
which contradicts our assumption that Algorithm
1
returned
M
while all objects had level at most
t
.
This proves that
M
is indeed popular with penalty
t
.

For the other direction, we show that if
M
is an assignment that is popular with penalty
t
, then it admits a dual certificate
\vec{\alpha}
such that
\alpha_{b}\geq-t
for each object
b\in B
. Since Algorithm
1
returns a level function
\ell
satisfying
\ell(b)\leq\ell_{\vec{\alpha}}(b)=|\alpha_{b}|
for each
b\in B
, this means that the
(t+1)
-level truncation of Algorithm
1
will return an assignment as desired.

Recall first that
M
is a popular assignment, so let
\vec{\alpha}
be a dual certificate for
M
minimizing
\sum_{a\in A}\alpha_{a}
.
Assume for the sake of contradiction
that
\alpha_{b^{\star}}<-t
for some

b^{\star}\in B
.
Let us call an edge
(a,b)
in
G

tight
, if
\alpha_{a}+\alpha_{b}=\mathsf{wt}_{M}(a,b)
; clearly, all edges of
M
are tight by complementary slackness.
Let us consider the set
A^{\star}
of agents and the set
B^{\star}
of objects in
G
that are reachable from
b^{\star}
via an
M
-alternating path in
G
consisting only of tight edges and starting with the edge
(M(b^{\star}),b^{\star})
; for convenience, let us add
b^{\star}
to
B^{\star}
.

We show that there exists some
a^{\star}\in A^{\star}
with
\alpha_{a^{\star}}=0
. If not, then consider the modification
\vec{\beta}
of
\vec{\alpha}
obtained through increasing the
\alpha
-value of each object in
B^{\star}
by
1
and decreasing the
\alpha
-value of each agent in
A^{\star}
by
1
. We claim that
\vec{\beta}
is a dual certificate.
First note that an agent
a
is contained in
A^{\star}
if and only if
M(a)
is contained in
B^{\star}
, by construction.
Hence, as no agent in
A^{\star}
has level
0
, the
\beta
-value of each agent remains non-negative, and the
\beta
-value of each object remains non-positive.
Observe also that
\vec{\beta}
is a feasible solution for
LP2
: assuming that
LP2
is violated on some edge
(a,b)
, it must be the case that
a\in A^{\star}
but
b\notin B^{\star}
and
(a,b)
is tight, otherwise
\beta_{a}+\beta_{b}\geq\mathsf{wt}_{M}(a,b)
still holds, since
\vec{\alpha}
is a feasible solution for
LP2
. However, then the edge
(a,b)
is not in
M
but is tight, which implies that appending
(a,b)
to an
M
-alternating path from
b^{\star}
to
a
witnessing
a\in A^{\star}
yields a path that shows

, a contradiction. Hence,
\vec{\beta}
is also a dual certificate, contradicting the minimality of
\vec{\alpha}
. This proves that there exists some
a^{\star}\in A^{\star}
with
\alpha_{a^{\star}}=0
.

Now, let
P
be an
M
-alternating path of tight edges between
b^{\star}
and
a^{\star}
that
witnesses
a^{\star}\in A^{\star}
,
and let
A_{P}
and
B_{P}
denote the agents and objects appearing on

, respectively.
Consider the matching
N=M\oplus P
in which
a^{\star}
and

are unmatched, and observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A}\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a}(N,M)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in A_{P}}\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a}(N,M)=\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a^{\star}}(N,M)+\sum_{a\in A_{P}\setminus\{a^{\star}\}}\mathsf{vote}^{t}_{a}(N,M)

\displaystyle=-t+\sum_{a\in A_{P}\setminus\{a^{\star}\}}\mathsf{wt}_{M}(a,N(a))=-t+\sum_{a\in A_{P}\setminus\{a^{\star}\}}\alpha_{a}+\sum_{b\in B_{P}\setminus\{b^{\star}\}}\alpha_{b}

\displaystyle=-t+\left(\sum_{a\in A_{P}}\alpha_{a}+\sum_{B\in B_{P}}\alpha_{b}\right)-\alpha_{a^{\star}}-\alpha_{b^{\star}}=-t+0-0-\alpha_{b^{\star}}>0

which contradicts our assumption that

is an assignment that is popular with penalty

.

Finally, the running time of the

-truncation of Algorithm

is indeed as claimed, since there can be at most

time. ∎

5
Finding a popular assignment with forced/forbidden edges

In this section we consider a variant of the
popular assignment
problem where, in addition to our instance,
we are given a set
F^{+}
of
forced
edges and a set
F^{-}
of
forbidden
edges,
and what we seek is a popular assignment that contains
F^{+}
and is disjoint from
F^{-}
.
Observe that it is sufficient to deal with forbidden edges, since putting an edge
(a,b)
into
F^{+}

is the same as putting all the edges in the set
\{(a,b^{\prime}):b^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Nbr}(a)\textrm{ and }b^{\prime}\neq b\}

into
F^{-}
.

The
popular assignment with forbidden edges
problem.

Given a bipartite graph
G=(A\cup B,E)

where every
a\in A
has preferences in partial order
over her neighbors,
together with a set
F\subseteq E
of forbidden edges,
does
G
admit a popular assignment
M
avoiding
F
, i.e., one where
M\cap F=\emptyset
?

We will show that in order to deal with forbidden edges, it suffices
to modify our algorithm in Section
4
as follows; see Algorithm
2
.
The only difference from the earlier algorithm is that we find a maximum matching in the subgraph

G_{\ell}-F
, i.e., on the edge set
E_{\ell}\setminus F
.

Algorithm 2

Finding a popular assignment with forbidden set
F\subset E

1:

for all

b\in B

do

\ell(b)=0
.

2:

while

\ell(b)<n
for all
b\in B

do

3:

Construct
G_{\ell}=(A\cup B,E_{\ell})
and find a maximum matching
M
in
G_{\ell}-F
.

4:

if

M
is a perfect matching
then

return

M
.

5:

for all

b\in B
unmatched in
M

do

\ell(b)=\ell(b)+1
.

6:

return
“
G
has no popular assignment with forbidden set
F
”.

Theorem 5.1

The above algorithm outputs a popular assignment avoiding
F
, if such an assignment exists in
G
.

Proof

7
Popular allocations in housing markets

A housing market, as introduced by Shapley and Scarf in their seminal paper
[
28
]
,
models a situation where agents are initially endowed
with one unit of an indivisible good, called a house, and agents may trade
their houses according to their preferences without using monetary transfers.
In such markets, trading results in a reallocation of houses in a way that each agent
ends up with exactly one house.

Let
A
denote the set of agents, and let
\omega(a)
denote the house of agent
a\in A
;
note that agents do not share houses, so
\omega(a)\neq\omega(a^{\prime})
whenever
a\neq a^{\prime}
.
The classic representation of a
housing market
over
A
is
a directed graph
D=(A,F)
where each agent
a\in A
has preferences over the arcs leaving
a
in
D
.
In this model, an arc
(a,a^{\prime})\in F
means that
a
prefers
\omega(a^{\prime})
to her own house (i.e.,
\omega(a)
),
and is therefore willing to participate in a trade where she obtains
\omega(a^{\prime})
.
Thus, preferences over the arcs leaving
a
describe her preferences of the houses she finds acceptable (i.e., prefers to her own).
An
allocation
in a housing market
D=(A,F)
is a set
S\subseteq F
of edges that form disjoint
cycles in
D
called
trading cycles
. An agent
trades
in
S
if it is covered by a trading cycle of
S
.

The solution concept used by Shapley and Scarf is the so-called
core

[
28
]
, but there have been several other stability notions studied in the literature on housing markets.
We may consider popularity as an alternative concept, with a straightforward interpretation as follows.
Given allocations
S
and
S^{\prime}
in
D
, some agent
a\in A

prefers

S
to
S^{\prime}
,
if either
S
covers
a
but
S^{\prime}
does not,
or
(a,a_{1})\in S
,
(a,a_{2})\in S^{\prime}
and
a
prefers
\omega(a_{1})
to
\omega(a_{2})
.
We say that
S
is
more popular than
S^{\prime}
, if the number of agents preferring
S
to
S^{\prime}
is more than the number of agents
preferring
S^{\prime}
to
S
. An allocation
S
is
popular
in
D
, if there is no allocation in
D
that is more popular than
S
.
A natural question is the following: can we find a popular allocation in a given housing market efficiently?

7.1
Popular allocations in a housing market: reduction to
popular assignment

We propose a reduction from the problem of finding a popular allocation in a housing market
to the problem of finding a popular assignment in a bipartite graph.
The main idea of the reduction is to associate a bipartite graph
G_{D}

with a given housing market
D=(A,F)
as follows.
We define the graph

G_{D}=(A\cup B,E)
where objects are houses, i.e.,
B=\{\omega(a):a\in A\}
,
and the edge set is
E=\{(a,\omega(a^{\prime})):(a,a^{\prime})\in F)\}\cup\{(a,\omega(a)):a\in A\}
.
Thus, each arc
(a,a^{\prime})\in F
corresponds to an edge
(a,\omega(a^{\prime}))
in
E
, and we also add
an edge
(a,\omega(a))
for each agent
a\in A
, corresponding to the possibility of
a
staying in her own house.
Preferences of an agent
a
over her outgoing arcs in
D
naturally yield her preferences in
G_{D}
over objects
in
\mathsf{Nbr}_{G_{D}}(a)\setminus\{\omega(a)\}
, while
\omega(a)
is the unique worst-choice object of
a
.

Observe that there is a one-to-one correspondence between allocations in
D
and assignments in
G_{D}

that preserves the preferences of any agent: an allocation
S
in
D

corresponds to an assignment
M_{S}=\{(a,\omega(a^{\prime})):(a,a^{\prime})\in S\}\cup\{(a,\omega(a)):a
does not trade in
S\}
;
note that this indeed yields a bijection.
It is easy to see that an allocation
S
is popular in
D
if and only if
M_{S}
is a popular assignment in
G_{D}
.
Hence, we can find a popular allocation in a housing market
D
by using Algorithm
1

to find a popular assignment in
G_{D}
; thus the following is a corollary to Theorem
1.1
.

Corollary 2

For any housing market
D=(A,F)

on
n
nodes and
m
arcs where each agent has preferences over her outgoing arcs,
we can decide in time
O((m+n)\cdot n^{5/2})
whether
D
admits a popular allocation or not, and if so, find one.

7.2
Maximum-size popular allocation in a housing market

Given the above positive result (Corollary
2
), an interesting question is whether it is
possible to find a maximum-size popular allocation in a housing market
D=(A,F)
,
where the
size
of an allocation
S
in
D
is
|S|
, that is, the number of agents trading in
S
.
A natural approach for finding a maximum-size popular allocation in
D
would be to assign
cost
1
to the edge
(a,\omega(a))
for each

in
G_{D}
, assign cost
0
to all remaining edges of
G_{D}
,
and ask for a minimum-cost popular assignment in

.
However, as we prove in Theorem
7.1
, finding a popular allocation of maximum size in a housing market is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard;
by the above reasoning, this implies that finding a minimum-cost popular assignment in the classic setting of the

popular assignment

\mathsf{NP}
-hard, even for binary costs.
Thus, Theorem

.

Theorem 7.1

-hard, even if agents’ preferences are strict,

corresponds to a housing market

We present a reduction from the

We assume an arbitrary, fixed ordering on the vertices of

We begin by defining two types of gadgets.

We let

If

.
In fact, the sole purpose of the differences between the two types of gadgets

Let us set

of size

,

, and

•

for each

.

(5)

Open problems

We proposed a polynomial-time algorithm for computing a popular assignment in an instance
G=(A\cup B,E)
with one-sided preferences,
if one exists.
Our algorithm solves
O(n^{2})
instances of the maximum matching problem in certain subgraphs of
G
(where
|A|=|B|=n
) and
it is easy to show instances where our algorithm indeed solves
\Theta(n^{2})
instances of the maximum matching problem.
The running time of our algorithm is
O(m\cdot n^{5/2})
(where
|E|=m
) and
by using the almost-linear time algorithm for maximum flow
[
6
]
to solve the bipartite maximum matching problem, we can
get a faster randomized algorithm for the
popular assignment
problem. Can we
show faster deterministic or randomized algorithms for the
popular assignment
problem
by solving
o(n^{2})
instances of the maximum matching problem or via some other approach?

Another open problem is the computational complexity of the
minimum-cost popular matching
problem when agents’ preferences are partial orders.
For weak rankings, a polynomial-time algorithm for this problem follows from the combinatorial characterization of popular matchings given in
[
2
]
. However as illustrated in an example in Section
2.1
, this characterization does not hold for partial order preferences.
So the computational complexity of the
minimum-cost popular matching
problem for partial order preferences is open.

One more open problem is to show a short
witness
that a given instance
G
does not admit a popular assignment. Rather than run our
algorithm and discover that
G
has no popular assignment, is there a forbidden structure that causes
G
not to admit a popular assignment?
Can we characterize instances that admit popular assignments? Interestingly, such a result is known for the stable roommates problem
[
30
]
;
recall our discussion in Section
1
on the similarity between results for the
popular assignment
problem and the

stable roommates
problem.

Our
\mathsf{W}_{l}[1]
-hardness proof for the
k
-unpopularity margin
problem with parameter
k
needs weak rankings. We are able to show that this problem remains
\mathsf{NP}
-hard for strict rankings (Lemma
10
). However, the parameterized complexity of this case is still open: is the
k
-unpopularity margin
problem in FPT with parameter
k
, if agents’ preferences are strict rankings?

Part II.

Due to part I, we can assume that the agents in
G

and

and
