Generalization of the multiplicative and additive compounds of square matrices and contraction in the Hausdorff dimension Thanks: The research of MM is supported in part by a research grant from the Israel Science Foundation.
Chengshuai Wu, Raz Pines, Michael Margaliot, Jean-Jacques Slotine

Abstract
The k k multiplicative and k k additive compounds of a matrix play an important role in geometry, multi-linear algebra, the asymptotic analysis of nonlinear dynamical systems, and in bounding the Hausdorff dimension of fractal sets. These compounds are defined for integer values of k k . Here, we introduce generalizations called the α \alpha multiplicative and α \alpha additive compounds of a square matrix, with α \alpha real. We study the properties of these new compounds and demonstrate an application in the context of the Douady and Oesterlé Theorem. This leads to a generalization of contracting systems to α \alpha contracting systems, with α \alpha real. Roughly speaking, the dynamics of such systems contracts any set with Hausdorff dimension larger than α \alpha . For α = 1 \alpha=1 they reduce to standard contracting systems.

Introduction

Consider a matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
, and fix

The

matrix that includes all the minors of order

,

, and

, then
A^{(2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 1}

and is given by

A^{(2)}=\begin{bmatrix}A(\{1,2\}|\{1,2\})\\
A(\{1,3\}|\{1,2\})\\
A(\{2,3\}|\{1,2\})\end{bmatrix},

where

denotes the minor of
A

obtained by taking the rows indexed by
\alpha

and the columns indexed by
\beta
.
Note that this implies that
A^{(1)}=A
, and if

then
A^{(n)}=\det(A)
.

The Cauchy-Binet formula
[
9
]
asserts that for any
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
,
B\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times r}
, and any

(AB)^{(k)}=A^{(k)}B^{(k)}.

(1)

This justifies the term multiplicative compound. For example, if

are

then (

\det(AB)=\det(A)\det(B)

When

, i.e.

is a square matrix, the
k
additive compound matrix of

is defined by

A^{[k]}:=\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}(I+\varepsilon A)^{(k)}|_{\varepsilon=0}.

(2)

This implies that

(I+\varepsilon A)^{(k)}=I+\varepsilon A^{[k]}+o(\varepsilon),

(3)

i.e.
A^{[k]}
is the first-order term in the Taylor series of
(I+\varepsilon A)^{(k)}
. For example, if

is diagonal, denoted

, and

then

\displaystyle(I

\displaystyle+\varepsilon A)^{(2)}

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{diag}(1+\varepsilon\lambda_{1},\dots,1+\varepsilon\lambda_{n}))^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}((1+\varepsilon\lambda_{1})(1+\varepsilon\lambda_{2}),\dots,(1+\varepsilon\lambda_{n-1})(1+\varepsilon\lambda_{n}))

\displaystyle=I+\varepsilon\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{n-1}+\lambda_{n})+o(\varepsilon),

so

A^{[2]}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{n-1}+\lambda_{n}).

Note that every eigenvalue of

is the sum of two eigenvalues of

.

It can be shown

This justifies the term additive compound.

Compound matrices have found numerous applications in multi-linear algebra, geometry, and dynamical systems theory.

The exterior product (or wedge product) of vectors

For

Recall that the magnitude of

Recall that

II-A

Hausdorff dimension

Let
K
be a set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For
d,\epsilon>0
, the
d
-measured volume of
\epsilon
-coverings of
K
is:

\displaystyle\zeta(K,\epsilon,d):=\inf

\displaystyle\{\sum_{i}r_{i}^{d}:\text{there exists a countable}

\displaystyle\text{cover of }K\text{ by balls with radii }r_{i}\leq\epsilon\}.

Note that the covering may include balls of different sizes, but all are bounded by
\epsilon
.
Note also that if
K
is compact then it would suffice to use finite coverings, since every open cover of
K
has a finite subcover.

By definition,
\zeta(K,\epsilon,d)
is non-increasing in
\epsilon
.
The Hausdorff
d
-measure of
K
is

m(K,d):=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\zeta(K,\epsilon,d),

where the limit may be infinite.

For any
s>0
, we have

\zeta(K,\epsilon,d+s)\leq\epsilon^{s}\zeta(K,\epsilon,d),

implying that if
m(K,d)<\infty

then
m(K,d)=0
. Thus, there is a unique
d^{*}\in[0,n]
such that

m(K,d)=0
for all
d>d^{*}
,
and
m(K,d)=\infty
for all
d<d^{*}
.
The Hausdorff dimension of
K

is

\dim_{H}K:=d^{*}.

Intuitively, if we try to cover a square (which is a 2D set) by 1D balls (i.e. lines) lines then we need an infinite number of lines, but once we try a cover with 2D balls, the number of balls needed is finite.
So,
d^{*}
is exactly
the
dimension for which
the “volume” of
K
becomes finite.

For smooth shapes, or shapes with a small number of “corners”, the Hausdorff dimension is an integer agreeing with the more standard topological dimension.
For example, suppose that
K
is an
\ell
-dimensional cube in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Intuitively speaking, for any
\epsilon>0

we require
\theta\left((1/\epsilon)^{\ell}\right)

balls of radius
\epsilon

to cover
K
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\zeta(K,\epsilon,d)\approx(1/\epsilon)^{\ell}\epsilon^{d}.

(12)

As
\epsilon\downarrow 0
, the right-hand side of (
12
) goes to
\infty
if
d<\ell
, and to zero if
d>\ell
.
It is not difficult to see that using balls with varying sizes does not change the analysis, so

\dim_{H}K=\ell.

However,
for fractal sets (e.g. sets that contain strange
attractors of chaotic dynamical systems) the Hausdorff dimension is typically not an integer.
1
1

1

In fact, one possible definition of a fractal set is:
a set whose Hausdorff dimension strictly
exceeds its topological dimension
[
40
]
.

In this context,
the Hausdorff dimension is useful in
quantifying sets of Lebesgue measure zero which are nevertheless “substantial”.
The next example from
[
51
]

demonstrates this.

Example 1
.

The Cantor set
E\subset[0,1]
is defined inductively as follows.
Let
E_{0}:=[0,1]
. For
j\geq 1
, the set
E_{j}
is obtained by removing the open middle third in any interval
in
E_{j-1}
. For example,

E_{1}=[0,1/3]\cup[2/3,1]
, and

E_{2}=[0,1/9]\cup[2/9,1/3]\cup[2/3,7/9]\cup[8/9,1]
. Cantor’s set is
E:=\cap_{j=0}^{\infty}E_{j}
. Each
E_{j}
is the union of
2^{j}
intervals of length
3^{-j}
.
The topological dimension
of
E
is thus

\lim_{j\to\infty}(2/3)^{j}=0.

We now determine
\dim_{H}E
.
It can be shown
[
17
, Ch. 3]
that it is enough to consider the cover of
E_{j}
by
2^{j}
intervals, each of length
3^{-j}
, so for any
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small,
\zeta(E_{j},\epsilon,d)=2^{j}3^{-jd}
.
Thus,
m(E_{j},d)=\left(\frac{2}{3^{d}}\right)^{j}

and

m(E,d)=\lim_{j\to\infty}\left(\frac{2}{3^{d}}\right)^{j}.

If
d>\log(2)/\log(3)
then
m(E,d)=0
, and if
d<\log(2)/\log(3)
then
m(E,d)=\infty
.
Thus,

\dim_{H}E=\log(2)/\log(3)\approx 0.631.

Intuitively, this implies that the Cantor set
is less than a line, but more than a discrete set of points.

The next result summarizes some useful properties of
\dim_{H}
.

Proposition 1

(
[
17
, Chapter 3]
)
.

The Hausdorff dimension satisfies the following properties:

•

\dim_{H}\emptyset=0
;

•

monotonicity: If
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
A\subseteq B
then
\dim_{H}A\leq\dim_{H}B
;

•

countable subadditivity: If
A_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

, then

\dim_{H}(\cup_{i}A_{i})\leq\sum_{i}\dim_{H}(A_{i});

•

If

are such that

\inf\{\operatorname{dist}(x,y):x\in A,y\in B\}>0

then

\dim_{H}(A\cup B)=\dim_{H}A+\dim_{H}B.

The first three properties imply that
\dim_{H}
is an outer measure, and the fourth one that it is a metric outer measure.

Real power of a square non-singular matrix

We first recall the definition of the real power of a complex number. Any complex number

a=|a|\exp(j\theta(a)),

where

,

For example, for

~&\ell_{i}&\ddots&~\\

with

, and every

.

\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}
is closed under conjugation. Note that
f(\mathbb{R}\cap(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}))=f(\mathbb{R}_{>0})\subset\mathbb{R}
. Therefore, the conditions given in
[
14
, Thm. 1.18]
are satisfied. This ensures that
f(\overline{A})=\overline{f(A)}
. Since
A
is real, i.e.,
\overline{A}=A
, this implies that
f(A)=\overline{f(A)}
, that is,
f(A)
is real.
∎

The next result provides a formula for
the derivative of a
real power of a parameter-dependent matrix.
This result will be used later on to
derive a simple expression for the generalized additive compound of a matrix.

Lemma 1
.

Consider a matrix-valued mapping
A:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
. Assume that
A(\varepsilon)
is non-singular and has constant (generalized) eigenvectors, that is, it can be written in the Jordan canonical form

A(\varepsilon)=T^{-1}J(\varepsilon)T,~J(\varepsilon)=\operatorname{diag}(J_{1}(\varepsilon),J_{2}(\varepsilon),\dots,J_{p}(\varepsilon)),

where
T\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, and every
J_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}^{m_{i}\times m_{i}}
,
\sum_{i=1}^{p}m_{i}=n
, is a Jordan block as described in (
15
), where
\ell_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
,
i=1,\dots,p
, is
C^{1}
.
Then for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}A^{\alpha}(\varepsilon)=\alpha A^{\alpha-1}(\varepsilon)\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}A(\varepsilon).

(19)

Proof:

By (
18
),

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}J_{i}^{\alpha}(\varepsilon)=\alpha J_{i}^{\alpha-1}(\varepsilon)\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\ell_{i}(\varepsilon)
, so

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}J^{\alpha}(\varepsilon)=

\displaystyle\alpha J^{\alpha-1}(\varepsilon)\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\ell_{1}(\varepsilon)I_{m_{1}},\dots,\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\ell_{p}(\varepsilon)I_{m_{p}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha J^{\alpha-1}(\varepsilon)\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}J(\varepsilon),

where
I_{s}
denotes the
s\times s
identity matrix. Thus,

\begin{split}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}A^{\alpha}(\varepsilon)=&T^{-1}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}J^{\alpha}(\varepsilon)T\\
=&\alpha T^{-1}J^{\alpha-1}(\varepsilon)TT^{-1}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}J(\varepsilon)T,\end{split}

and using Definition

yields (

).
∎

II-D

Kronecker product and Kronecker sum of matrices

The Kronecker product
of
two matrices
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
and
B\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times q}
is

A\otimes B:=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}B&a_{12}B&\cdots&a_{1m}B\\
a_{21}B&a_{22}B&\cdots&a_{2m}B\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{n1}B&a_{n2}B&\cdots&a_{nm}B\end{bmatrix},

(20)

where
a_{ij}
denotes the
ij
th entry of
A
. Hence,
A\otimes B\in\mathbb{C}^{(np)\times(mq)}
.

The Kronecker sum of two
square matrices
X\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and
Y\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
is

X\oplus Y:=X\otimes I_{m}+I_{n}\otimes Y,

(21)

where
I_{r}
denotes the
r\times r
identity matrix.

We list several properties of the Kronecker product and Kronecker sum that will be used later on.
For
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
, let
A^{*}
to
denote the conjugate transpose of
A
.

Lemma 2

(see e.g.
[
13
]
)
.

Consider matrices
A,C\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
,
B,D\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times q}
,
F\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times\ell}
,
G\in\mathbb{C}^{q\times r}
,
X\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, and
Y\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
. Then,

(a)

(cA)\otimes B=A\otimes(cB)=c(A\otimes B)
for any
c\in\mathbb{C}
;

(b)

(A+C)\otimes B=A\otimes B+C\otimes B
;

(c)

A\otimes(B+D)=A\otimes B+A\otimes D
;

(d)

(A\otimes B)(F\otimes G)=(AF)\otimes(BG)
;

(e)

(A\otimes B)^{*}=A^{*}\otimes B^{*}
;

(f)

(A\otimes B)^{T}=A^{T}\otimes B^{T}
;

(g)

if
X,Y
are non-singular then
(X\otimes Y)^{-1}=X^{-1}\otimes Y^{-1}
;

(h)

Let
\lambda_{i}(X)
,
i=1,\dots,n
, and
\lambda_{j}(Y)
,
j=1,\dots,m
, denote the eigenvalues of
X
and
Y
, respectively. Then,
X\otimes Y
has eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}(X)\lambda_{j}(Y)
,
i=1,\dots,n
,
j=1,\dots,m
;

(i)

X\oplus Y
has eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}(X)+\lambda_{j}(Y)
,
i=1,\dots,n
,
j=1,\dots,m
;

(j)

\exp(X)\otimes\exp(Y)=\exp(X\oplus Y)
.

Property (
a
) implies
that we can write
cA\otimes B:=(cA)\otimes B
or
A\otimes(cB)
, without any ambiguity.

For a real number
p\geq 1
, let

|x|_{p}:=(|x_{1}|^{p}+\cdots+|x_{n}|^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}},

(22)

denote the
L_{p}
vector norm of
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
and let
||\cdot||_{p}
denote the induced matrix norm.
Recall
[
18
]
that
a norm
|\cdot|:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is called monotonic if for any
x,y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that

|x_{i}|\leq|y_{i}|
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\},

we have
|x|\leq|y|
.
The
L_{p}
norm for any
p\geq 1
is monotonic.

The next result uses ideas from
[
18
]
to determine the induced
L_{p}
matrix norms of a Kronecker product of two matrices.

Proposition 5
.

Fix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{\ell\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times m}
, and
p\geq 1
. Then,

\|A\otimes B\|_{p}=\|A\|_{p}\|B\|_{p}.

(23)

Proof:

It is easy to verify that

|x\otimes y|_{p}=|x|_{p}|y|_{p},\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\;y\in\mathbb{C}^{m}.

(24)

Hence, in the terminology of
[
18
]
,
the
L_{p}
norms are
cross norms
.
Let

\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m}:=\{z=x\otimes y:x\in\mathbb{C}^{n},y\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\}.

Note that
\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{nm}
. Then,

\displaystyle\|A\otimes B\|_{p}=

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{nm}}\frac{|(A\otimes B)z|_{p}}{|z|_{p}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m}}\frac{|(A\otimes B)z|_{p}}{|z|_{p}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n},y\in\mathbb{C}^{m}}\frac{|(A\otimes B)(x\otimes y)|_{p}}{|x\otimes y|_{p}},

and applying Property (
d
) in Lemma
2
and (
24
) yields

\displaystyle\|A\otimes B\|_{p}\geq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}}\frac{|Ax|_{p}}{|x|_{p}}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{C}^{m}}\frac{|By|_{p}}{|y|_{p}}=\|A\|_{p}\|B\|_{p}.

(25)

Thus, to complete the proof we need to show that
\|A\otimes B\|_{p}\leq\|A\|_{p}\|B\|_{p}
.
Note that
A\otimes B=(A\otimes I_{r})(I_{n}\otimes B)
. Since induced matrix norms are sub-multiplicative,

\|A\otimes B\|_{p}\leq\|A\otimes I_{r}\|_{p}\|I_{n}\otimes B\|_{p}.

(26)

Let
\{e^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
\{f^{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
denote the canonical basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\mathbb{C}^{m}
, respectively.
Any
z\in\mathbb{C}^{nm}
can be written as

z=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{i}\otimes y^{i}\text{ and }z=\sum_{j=1}^{m}x^{j}\otimes f^{j},

(27)

for some vectors
y^{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
,
x^{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. As in
[
18
]
, consider the norms

\displaystyle|z|_{p,e}:=|\sum_{i=1}^{n}|y^{i}|_{p}e^{i}|_{p},\quad|z|_{p,f}:=|\sum_{j=1}^{m}|x^{j}|_{p}f^{j}|_{p}.

(28)

It is easy to show that

|z|_{p,e}=|z|_{p,f}=|z|_{p},\text{ for any }z\in\mathbb{C}^{nm}.

(29)

Now,

\displaystyle|(I_{n}\otimes B)z|_{p,e}

\displaystyle=|(I_{n}\otimes B)(\sum e^{i}\otimes y^{i})|_{p,e}

\displaystyle=|\sum e^{i}\otimes By^{i}|_{p,e}

\displaystyle=|\sum|By^{i}|_{p}e^{i}|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\|B\|_{p}|\sum|y^{i}|_{p}e^{i}|_{p}

\displaystyle=\|B\|_{p}|z|_{p,e}.

Thus,

\|I_{n}\otimes B\|_{p,e}\leq\|B\|_{p}.

On the other hand, (
25
) and (
29
) imply that
\|I_{n}\otimes B\|_{p,e}=\|I_{n}\otimes B\|_{p}\geq\|I_{n}\|_{p}\|B\|_{p}=\|B\|_{p}
. Hence,

\|I_{n}\otimes B\|_{p}=\|B\|_{p}.

A similar argument using
the norm
|\cdot|_{p,f}
yields

\|A\otimes I_{r}\|_{p}=\|A\|_{p}
.
Now (
26
) yields

\|A\otimes B\|_{p}\leq\|A\|_{p}\|B\|_{p}
.
∎

II-E

Matrix measures

Let
|\cdot|:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
denote a vector norm. The induced matrix norm
||\cdot||:\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is
||A||:=\max_{|x|=1}|Ax|
, and the induced matrix measure
\mu:\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{R}
is

\mu(A):=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{||I+\varepsilon A||-1}{\varepsilon}.

(30)

Matrix measures (also called logarithmic norms) play an important role in numerical linear algebra
[
46
]
and in systems and control theory
[
23
,
3
]
. The reason for this is two-fold. First, if there exists a matrix measure such that the matrix
A(t)
in (
6
) satisfies

\mu(A(t))\leq\eta,\text{ for all }t\geq 0,

(31)

then

||X(t)||\leq\exp(\eta t)||X(0)||,\text{ for all }t\geq 0.

In particular, if
\eta<0
then this implies exponential convergence to zero with rate
\eta
.
Second, let
\mu_{p}
denote the matrix measure induced by the
L_{p}
vector norm, with
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
.
Then for any
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
,

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(A)

\displaystyle=\max_{j\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\Big(\operatorname{Re}(a_{jj})+\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{i=1}{i\not=j}}^{n}|a_{ij}|\Big),

\displaystyle\mu_{2}(A)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\max}(A_{\text{sym}}),

(32)

\displaystyle\mu_{\infty}(A)

\displaystyle=\max_{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\Big(\operatorname{Re}(a_{ii})+\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{i=1}{i\not=j}}^{n}|a_{ij}|\Big),

where
A_{\text{sym}}:=(A+A^{*})/2
,
and
\lambda_{\max}(B)
denotes the largest eigenvalue of a Hermitian matrix
B
.
Using these explicit formulas, it is sometimes possible to easily verify that (
31
) indeed holds, without computing
X(t)
itself.

Let
Q^{k,n}
denote the
sequence of
k
-tuples of distinct numbers from
\{1,\dots,n\}
, in lexicographic order.
For example,

Q^{2,3}=\{\{1,2\},\{1,3\},\{2,3\}\}.

Note that there are
\binom{n}{k}
such
k
-tuples.
Let
Q^{k,n}_{\ell}
denote the
\ell
th tuple in
Q^{k,n}
. For example,

Q^{2,3}_{2}=\{1,3\}.

For
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and any
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the matrix measures for
A^{[k]}
are
[
32
]
:

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(A^{[k]})=

\displaystyle\max_{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\in Q^{k,n}}\Big(\sum_{p=1}^{k}\operatorname{Re}(a_{i_{p},i_{p}})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\notin\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\end{subarray}}(|a_{j,i_{1}}|+\cdots+|a_{j,i_{k}}|)\Big),

\displaystyle\mu_{2}(A^{[k]})=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\left(A_{\text{sym}}\right),

(33)

\displaystyle\mu_{\infty}(A^{[k]})=

\displaystyle\max_{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\in Q^{k,n}}\Big(\sum_{p=1}^{k}\operatorname{Re}(a_{i_{p},i_{p}})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\notin\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\end{subarray}}(|a_{i_{1},j}|+\cdots+|a_{i_{k},j}|)\Big).

Note that for
k=1
, (
II-E
) reduces to (
II-E
).

Remark 1
.

For an integer
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the system (
9
)
is said to be
k
-order contracting
if there exists a matrix measure
\mu
such that

\mu(J_{f}^{[k]}(t,x))\leq-\eta<0,

(34)

for any
t\geq 0
and any
x
in the state-space
[
53
]

(see also
[
19
,
25
]
). Roughly speaking, this implies that the dynamics contracts
k
-dimensional volumes. To explain this,
consider the system
\dot{x}(t)=A(t)x(t)
, and
suppose that it is
n
-order contracting. Since
A^{[n]}(t)=\operatorname{trace}(A(t))
, this implies that
\operatorname{trace}(A(t))\leq-\eta<0
for all
t\geq 0
.
Combining this with (
7
) implies the following. Fix
n
initial conditions
a^{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
,
and let

\displaystyle X(t):=\begin{bmatrix}x(t,a^{1})&x(t,a^{2})&\dots&x(t,a^{n})\end{bmatrix}.

Then
|\det(X(t))|\leq\exp(-\eta t)|\det(X(0))|
. Therefore,
n
-dimensional volumes contract
at an exponential rate.
Following the terminology used in physics, we say that (

)
is

if it is

From (
II-E
) and (
II-E
), it is straightforward to obtain the next result.

Proposition 6

(
[
53
, Corollary 1]
)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
. Suppose that there exists an integer
\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}
such that

\mu_{p}(A^{[\ell]})\leq 0,

(35)

then

\mu_{p}(A^{[\ell]})\geq\mu_{p}(A^{[\ell+1]})\geq\cdots\geq\mu_{p}(A^{[n]}).

(36)

In other words, if
\dot{X}=AX
is
\ell
-order contracting w.r.t.

then it is also
(\ell+1)
-order contracting,

(\ell+2)
-order contracting, and so on
[
53
]
.

The next two sections describe our main results.

III
\alpha
compounds

In this section, we
define the new notions of the
\alpha
multiplicative and
\alpha
additive compound of a square matrix, and analyze the properties of these compounds.
In the next section, we show how this leads to the new notion of
\alpha
contracting systems, with
\alpha

real
.

III-A

\alpha
multiplicative compound

Consider a non-integer real number
\alpha\in(1,n)\setminus\mathbb{Z}
. From here on we decompose
\alpha
as

\alpha=k+s,~k\in\{1,2,\dots,n-1\},~s\in(0,1).

Definition 2
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be non-singular. The
\alpha
multiplicative compound matrix of
A

is

A^{(\alpha)}:=(A^{(k)})^{1-s}\otimes(A^{(k+1)})^{s}.

(37)

Note that
A^{(\alpha)}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
, where
r:=\binom{n}{k}\binom{n}{k+1}
, and that
A^{(\alpha)}
may be complex (non-real) even if
A
is real. Since
A
is non-singular,
A^{(\ell)}
is non-singular for all
\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, so
(A^{(k)})^{1-s}
and
(A^{(k+1)})^{s}
in (
37
) are well-defined.

For example, for
\alpha=2.5
, we have
k=2
and
s=1/2
, so

A^{(2.5)}=(A^{(2)})^{1/2}\otimes(A^{(3)})^{1/2},

which can be interpreted as a
“multiplicative interpolation”, with equal
weights, between
A^{(2)}

and
A^{(3)}
.

Example 2
.

Suppose that
D=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{4})
is non-singular. Fix
\alpha\in(2,3)
, so that
k=2
and
s=\alpha-2\in(0,1)
.
Then

\displaystyle D^{(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=(D^{(2)})^{1-s}\otimes(D^{(3)})^{s}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}((d_{1}d_{2})^{1-s},(d_{1}d_{3})^{1-s},\dots,(d_{3}d_{4})^{1-s}))

\displaystyle\otimes\operatorname{diag}((d_{1}d_{2}d_{3})^{s},(d_{1}d_{2}d_{4})^{s},(d_{1}d_{3}d_{4})^{s},(d_{2}d_{3}d_{4})^{s})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1}d_{2}d_{3}^{s},d_{1}d_{2}d_{4}^{s},\dots,d_{2}^{s}d_{3}d_{4}),

so, any eigenvalue of
D^{(\alpha)}
is a “multiplicative interpolation” between eigenvalues of
D^{(2)}
and
D^{(3)}
.

Remark 2
.

If
\alpha
is allowed to be an integer, say,
\alpha=k
then
s=0
and (
37
) becomes

A^{(\alpha)}=(A^{(k)})^{1}\otimes(A^{(k+1)})^{0}=A^{(k)}\otimes I_{r},

where
r:=\binom{n}{k+1}
. This is not equal to
A^{(k)}
(but, ignoring multiplicity,
it has the same eigenvalues as
A^{(k)}
). Therefore, Definition
2
only considers the case where
\alpha
is not an integer. For the integer case, we will just use the standard definition for the
k
multiplicative compounds.

Remark 3
.

Suppose that
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is non-singular.
Using Property (
b
) in Prop.
3
, Property (
f
) in Lemma
2
, and the fact that
(X^{(\ell)})^{T}=(X^{T})^{(\ell)}
for any
X\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and
\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}
yields

\displaystyle(A^{(\alpha)})^{T}

\displaystyle=\left((A^{(k)})^{1-s}\otimes(A^{(k+1)})^{s}\right)^{T}

\displaystyle=((A^{(k)})^{1-s})^{T}\otimes((A^{(k+1)})^{s})^{T}

\displaystyle=((A^{T})^{(k)})^{1-s}\otimes((A^{T})^{(k+1)})^{s}

\displaystyle=(A^{T})^{(\alpha)},

In particular, if
A=A^{T}
then
A^{(\alpha)}=(A^{(\alpha)})^{T}
.

An alternative possible definition of the
\alpha
multiplicative compound matrix is

A^{(\alpha)}_{\text{alt}}:=(A^{1-s})^{(k)}\otimes(A^{s})^{(k+1)}.

(38)

The next result shows that (
37
) and (
38
) are equivalent.
This is useful because as we will see below
some results
are easier to derive using the definition in (
37
)
and others using the alternative definition (
38
).

Theorem 1
.

Consider a non-singular matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and fix
\alpha\in(1,n)\setminus\mathbb{Z}
.
Then

A^{(\alpha)}=A^{(\alpha)}_{\text{alt}}.

(39)

Proof:

Fix
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
s\in(0,1)
. It is enough to show that

(A^{(k)})^{s}=(A^{s})^{(k)}.

(40)

We first
consider the case when
A
is diagonalizable,
that is, there exist a non-singular
T\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, and a diagonal matrix
D\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
such that

A=T^{-1}DT,

which is also the Jordan canonical form of
A
.
Then

(A^{(k)})^{s}=((T^{-1})^{(k)}D^{(k)}T^{(k)})^{s}.

Using the fact that
(T^{-1})^{(k)}=(T^{(k)})^{-1}
and Property (
c
) in Prop.
3
gives

(A^{(k)})^{s}=(T^{(k)})^{-1}(D^{(k)})^{s}T^{(k)}.

Since
D^{(k)}
is also diagonal,
(D^{(k)})^{s}=(D^{s})^{(k)}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle(A^{(k)})^{s}

\displaystyle=(T^{-1})^{(k)}(D^{s})^{(k)}T^{(k)}

\displaystyle=(T^{-1}D^{s}T)^{(k)}

\displaystyle=(A^{s})^{(k)}.

We conclude that (
40
)
holds when
A
is diagonalizable.
The proof in the general case follows from the fact
that diagonalizable matrices are dense in
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
(see, e.g.,
[
20
, Corollary 7.3.3]
).
∎

The next example demonstrates
Theorem
1
.

Example 3
.

Consider

A=\begin{bmatrix}a&1&0\\
0&a&0\\
0&0&b\end{bmatrix},

with
a,b\not=0
. Note that
A
is not diagonalizable.
A straightforward computation yields

A^{(2)}=\begin{bmatrix}a^{2}&0&0\\
0&ab&b\\
0&0&ab\end{bmatrix}
, and the Jordan decomposition of this matrix is

A^{(2)}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&b&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a^{2}&0&0\\
0&ab&1\\
0&0&ab\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1/b&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.

Definition
1
gives

\displaystyle(A^{(2)})^{s}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&b&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a^{2s}&0&0\\
0&a^{s}b^{s}&s(ab)^{s-1}\\
0&0&a^{s}b^{s}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1/b&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}a^{2s}&0&0\\
0&a^{s}b^{s}&sa^{s-1}b^{s}\\
0&0&a^{s}b^{s}\end{bmatrix}.

On the other-hand,

\displaystyle(A^{s})^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}a^{s}&sa^{s-1}&0\\
0&a^{s}&0\\
0&0&b^{s}\end{bmatrix}^{(2)}=\begin{bmatrix}a^{2s}&0&0\\
0&a^{s}b^{s}&sa^{s-1}b^{s}\\
0&0&a^{s}b^{s}\end{bmatrix},

so
(A^{(2)})^{s}=(A^{s})^{(2)}
. Since
A^{(1)}=A
and
A^{(3)}=\det(A)
, this implies that
A^{(\alpha)}=A^{(\alpha)}_{\text{alt}}
for any
\alpha\in(1,3)\setminus\mathbb{Z}
.

The following discussion shows that, unlike the standard multiplicative compound matrix, the formula
(AB)^{(\alpha)}=A^{(\alpha)}B^{(\alpha)}
does not hold in general.

Remark 4
.

Eq. (
37
) yields

\begin{split}(AB)^{(\alpha)}=&((AB)^{(k)})^{1-s}\otimes((AB)^{(k+1)})^{s}\\
=&(A^{(k)}B^{(k)})^{1-s}\otimes(A^{(k+1)}B^{(k+1)})^{s}.\end{split}

(41)

On the other-hand, using Property (
d
) in Lemma
2
gives

\begin{split}A^{(\alpha)}B^{(\alpha)}=&((A^{(k)})^{1-s}\otimes(A^{(k+1)})^{s})((B^{(k)})^{1-s}\otimes(B^{(k+1)})^{s})\\
=&((A^{(k)})^{1-s}(B^{(k)})^{1-s})\otimes((A^{(k+1)})^{s}(B^{(k+1)})^{s}).\end{split}

(42)

This shows that
(AB)^{(\alpha)}\neq A^{(\alpha)}B^{(\alpha)}
in general.
However it may hold in some special cases.
Suppose for example that both
A
and
B
are diagonalizable, and that
A
commutes with
B
. Then, the same holds for
A^{(\ell)},B^{(\ell)}
,
\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. This implies that
A^{(\ell)}
and
B^{(\ell)}
are simultaneously diagonalizable. Then it is easy to show that for any
s\in(0,1)
, we have
(A^{(\ell)}B^{(\ell)})^{s}=(A^{(\ell)})^{s}(B^{(\ell)})^{s}
, and this implies that in this special case
(AB)^{(\alpha)}=A^{(\alpha)}B^{(\alpha)}.

III-B

Spectral properties of
A^{(\alpha)}

Let
\lambda_{i}(A)\in\mathbb{C},\sigma_{i}(A)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
i=1,\dots,n
, denote the eigenvalues and singular values of
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, respectively, ordered such that

\displaystyle\text{Re}(\lambda_{1}(A))\geq\text{Re}(\lambda_{2}(A))\geq\cdots\geq\text{Re}(\lambda_{n}(A)),

(43)

and

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(A)\geq\sigma_{2}(A)\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{n}(A)\geq 0.

(44)

Lemma 3
.

Fix a non-singular matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\alpha\in(1,n)\setminus\mathbb{Z}
. Write
\alpha=k+s
,
with
k
an integer and
s\in(0,1)
.

(i)

The eigenvalues of
A^{(\alpha)}
are

\prod_{i\in Q^{k,n}_{\ell}}(\lambda_{i}(A))^{1-s}\prod_{i\in Q^{k+1,n}_{j}}(\lambda_{i}(A))^{s}

for
\ell\in\{1,\dots,\binom{n}{k}\}
,

j\in\{1,\dots,\binom{n}{k+1}\}
.

(ii)

The eigenvalues of
(A^{T}A)^{(\alpha)}
are

\prod_{i\in Q^{k,n}_{\ell}}(\sigma_{i}(A))^{2(1-s)}\prod_{i\in Q^{k+1,n}_{j}}(\sigma_{i}(A))^{2s}

for
\ell\in\{1,\dots,\binom{n}{k}\}
,

j\in\{1,\dots,\binom{n}{k+1}\}
.

Proof:

It is well-known
[
32
]

that
\eta\in\mathbb{C}
is an eigenvalue of
A^{(k)}
iff it
is the product of
k

eigenvalues of
A
, that is, there exists
\ell\in\{1,\dots,\binom{n}{k}\}
such that

\eta=\prod_{i\in Q^{k,n}_{\ell}}\lambda_{i}(A).

By Definition
1
,

\eta^{1-s}=\prod_{i\in Q^{k,n}_{\ell}}(\lambda_{i}(A))^{1-s}

is an eigenvalue of
(A^{(k)})^{1-s}
.
Similarly, every eigenvalue of
(A^{(k+1})^{s}
has the form

\prod_{i\in Q^{k+1,n}_{j}}(\lambda_{i}(A))^{s}

for some
j\in\{1,\dots,\binom{n}{k+1}\}
.
Using Property (
h
) in Lemma
2
proves Property (
i
).

To prove Property (
ii
), note that

(A^{T}A)^{(\alpha)}=((A^{T}A)^{(k)})^{1-s}\otimes((A^{T}A)^{(k+1)})^{s}.

The eigenvalues of
A^{T}A
are

\sigma^{2}_{i}(A)
,
i=1,\dots,n
, and using
Property (
i
) yields Property (
ii
).
∎

Remark 5
.

Fix a real
\alpha\geq 1
,
and define
\omega_{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
by

\omega_{\alpha}(A):=\sigma_{1}(A)\cdots\sigma_{k}(A)\left(\sigma_{k+1}(A)\right)^{s}.

This function plays a crucial role in the Douady
and Oesterlé Theorem
[
8
]
, see Section
IV
below.
Combining Property (
ii
)
with the
ordering of the eigenvalues and singular values implies that

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}((A^{T}A)^{(\alpha)})=

\displaystyle\left(\sigma_{1}(A)\cdots\sigma_{k}(A)\right)^{2(1-s)}

\displaystyle\left(\sigma_{1}(A)\cdots\sigma_{k+1}(A)\right)^{2s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\omega_{\alpha}(A))^{2}.

(45)

Since
A^{T}A
is symmetric, it follows from Remark
3
that so
is
(A^{T}A)^{(\alpha)}
.
Hence,
\lambda_{1}((A^{T}A)^{(\alpha)})=\sigma_{1}((A^{T}A)^{(\alpha)})=||(A^{T}A)^{(\alpha)}||_{2}
, so we conclude that

\displaystyle||(A^{T}A)^{(\alpha)}||_{2}

\displaystyle=(\omega_{\alpha}(A))^{2}.

(46)

Thus, the
\alpha
multiplicative
compound provides a matrix norm expression for
\omega_{\alpha}
.
This was our original motivation for introducing the
\alpha

multiplicative compound.

Note also that in general

\displaystyle(\sigma_{1}(A^{(\alpha)}))^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}((A^{(\alpha)})^{T}A^{(\alpha)})

\displaystyle\not=\lambda_{1}((A^{T}A)^{(\alpha)}).

We now turn to defining a generalization of the
k
additive compound.

III-C

\alpha
additive compound

The definition of the
\alpha
additive compound matrix
follows (
2
).

Definition 3
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\alpha\in(1,n)\setminus\mathbb{Z}
. The
\alpha
additive compound matrix of
A
is

A^{[\alpha]}:=\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}(I+\varepsilon A)^{(\alpha)}|_{\varepsilon=0}.

(47)

Note that for any
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small and any
k\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
,
(I+\varepsilon A)^{(k)}
is non-singular
and
(I+\varepsilon A)^{(k)}\in\Omega_{n}
. Hence, Proposition
4
and Definition
2
guarantee that
A^{[\alpha]}
is well-defined and is a real matrix. Note also that (
47
)
implies that

(I+\varepsilon A)^{(\alpha)}=I+\varepsilon A^{[\alpha]}+o(\varepsilon).

(48)

Example 4
.

Suppose that
D=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{4})
is non-singular. Fix
\alpha\in(2,3)
, so that
k=2
and
s=\alpha-2\in(0,1)
. Let
h_{i}(\varepsilon):=1+\varepsilon d_{i}
.
By Example
2
,

\displaystyle(I+\varepsilon D)^{(\alpha)}=

\displaystyle\operatorname{diag}(h_{1}(\varepsilon)h_{2}(\varepsilon)h_{3}^{s}(\varepsilon),h_{1}(\varepsilon)h_{2}(\varepsilon)h_{4}^{s}(\varepsilon),

\displaystyle\dots,h_{2}^{s}(\varepsilon)h_{3}(\varepsilon)h_{4}(\varepsilon))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I+\varepsilon\operatorname{diag}(d_{1}+d_{2}+sd_{3},d_{1}+d_{2}+sd_{4},

\displaystyle\dots,sd_{2}+d_{3}+d_{4})+o(\varepsilon),

and (
47
) yields

D^{[\alpha]}=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1}+d_{2}+sd_{3},d_{1}+d_{2}+sd_{4},\dots,sd_{2}+d_{3}+d_{4}).

Note that every eigenvalue of
D^{[\alpha]}

is an “additive interpolation” of three eigenvalues of
D
.

The next result provides an
expression for
A^{[\alpha]}
in terms of
A^{[k]}
and
A^{[k+1]}
.

Theorem 2
.

Fix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\alpha\in(1,n)\setminus\mathbb{Z}
. Then

A^{[\alpha]}=((1-s)A^{[k]})\oplus(sA^{[k+1]}).

(49)

Note that this also shows that
A^{[\alpha]}
is real, as
A^{[\ell]}
is real for any
\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

Proof:

Consider the case where
A
is diagonalizable, that is,
A=T^{-1}DT,

where
T\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is non-singular, and
D\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is a diagonal matrix. Let
B(\varepsilon):=I+\varepsilon A
.
Fix
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Then

\displaystyle(B(\varepsilon))^{(k)}

\displaystyle=(T^{-1}(I+\varepsilon D)T)^{(k)}

\displaystyle=(T^{-1})^{(k)}(I+\varepsilon D)^{(k)}T^{(k)}.

Since
D
is diagonal, so is

(I+\varepsilon D)^{(k)}
. Therefore,
(B(\varepsilon))^{(k)}
satisfies the conditions in Lemma
1
.
We use Lemma
1
to determine the derivative of
(B(\varepsilon))^{(\alpha)}
with respect to
\varepsilon
. To simplify the notation, we write
B
for
B(\varepsilon)
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}B^{(\alpha)}=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\left((B^{(k)})^{1-s}\otimes(B^{(k+1)})^{s}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}(B^{(k)})^{1-s}\right)\otimes(B^{(k+1)})^{s}

\displaystyle+(B^{(k)})^{1-s}\otimes\left(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}(B^{(k+1)})^{s}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left((1-s)(B^{(k)})^{-s}\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}B^{(k)}\right)\otimes(B^{(k+1)})^{s}

\displaystyle+(B^{(k)})^{1-s}\otimes\left(s(B^{(k+1)})^{s-1}\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}B^{(k+1)}\right).

Setting
\varepsilon=0
and using the fact that
B(\varepsilon)|_{\varepsilon=0}=I
and (
2
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}(I+\varepsilon A)^{(\alpha)}|_{\varepsilon=0}=

\displaystyle(1-s)A^{[k]}\otimes I_{r_{1}}+I_{r_{2}}\otimes(sA^{[k+1]})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((1-s)A^{[k]})\oplus(sA^{[k+1]}),

where
r_{1}:=\binom{n}{k+1}
, and
r_{2}:=\binom{n}{k}
.
This completes the proof when
A
is diagonalizable, and the general case follows from using a similar argument as in the proof of Theorem
1
.
∎

Remark 6
.

Suppose that
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
Then it is easy to see that Theorem
2

implies that

(A^{[\alpha]})^{T}=(A^{T})^{[\alpha]}.

In particular, if
A
is symmetric then so is
A^{[\alpha]}
.

The next result shows that the
\alpha
additive compound satisfies the same additivity
property as the
k
additive compound.

Theorem 3
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\alpha\in(1,n)\setminus\mathbb{Z}
. Then,

(A+B)^{[\alpha]}=A^{[\alpha]}+B^{[\alpha]}.

Proof:

Using (
49
)
gives

\displaystyle(A+B)^{[\alpha]}=

\displaystyle((1-s)(A+B)^{[k]})\oplus(s(A+B)^{[k+1]})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-s)(A+B)^{[k]}\otimes I_{r_{1}}+sI_{r_{2}}\otimes(A+B)^{[k+1]}.

Applying (
4
) and Properties (
a
)-(
c
) in Lemma
2
yields

\displaystyle(A+B)^{[\alpha]}=

\displaystyle(1-s)A^{[k]}\otimes I_{r_{1}}+sI_{r_{2}}\otimes A^{[k+1]}

\displaystyle+(1-s)B^{[k]}\otimes I_{r_{1}}+sI_{r_{2}}\otimes B^{[k+1]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((1-s)A^{[k]})\oplus(sA^{[k+1]})

\displaystyle+((1-s)B^{[k]})\oplus(sB^{[k+1]})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A^{[\alpha]}+B^{[\alpha]},

and this completes the proof.
∎

The next result shows that (
8
) also holds for the
\alpha
compounds.

Theorem 4
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\alpha\in(1,n)\setminus\mathbb{Z}
. Then,

\exp(A^{[\alpha]}t)=(\exp(At))^{(\alpha)},\text{ for any }t\in\mathbb{R}.

(50)

Proof:

Using the alternative definition (
38
) gives

\displaystyle(\exp(At))^{(\alpha)}_{\text{alt}}=

\displaystyle((\exp(At))^{1-s})^{(k)}\otimes((\exp(At))^{s})^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\exp(At(1-s)))^{(k)}\otimes(\exp(Ats))^{(k+1)},

and applying (
8
) and
Properties (
a
) and (
j
) in Lemma
2
,
gives

\displaystyle(\exp(At))^{(\alpha)}_{\text{alt}}=

\displaystyle\exp(A^{[k]}t(1-s))\otimes\exp(A^{[k+1]}ts)

(51)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left((A^{[k]}t(1-s))\oplus(A^{[k+1]}ts)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(((A^{[k]}(1-s))\oplus(A^{[k+1]}s))t\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp(A^{[\alpha]}t).

Applying Theorem
1
completes the proof.
∎

Combining Lemma
3
, Theorem
4
, and the fact that the eigenvalues of
\exp(At)
are

\exp(\lambda_{i}(A)t)
,
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, yields the following result.

Corollary 1
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\alpha\in(1,n)\setminus\mathbb{Z}
.
The eigenvalues of
A^{[\alpha]}
are

(1-s)\sum_{i\in Q^{k,n}_{\ell}}\lambda_{i}(A)+s\sum_{i\in Q^{k+1,n}_{j}}\lambda_{i}(A)

for
\ell\in\{1,\dots,\binom{n}{k}\}
,

j\in\{1,\dots,\binom{n}{k+1}\}
.

This implies in particular that
\lambda_{1}(A^{[\alpha]})
, that is, the eigenvalue of
A^{[\alpha]}
with the largest real part is

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(A^{[\alpha]})

\displaystyle=(1-s)\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}(A)+s\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\lambda_{i}(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}(A)+s\lambda_{k+1}(A).

(52)

Remark 7
.

By the spectral properties of the standard additive compounds
[
32
]
,
\sum_{i\in Q^{k,n}_{\ell}}\lambda_{i}(A)
and
\sum_{i\in Q^{k+1,n}_{j}}\lambda_{i}(A)
are the eigenvalues of
A^{[k]}
and
A^{[k+1]}
, respectively. Now Property (
i
) in Lemma
2
implies that
(1-s)\sum_{i\in Q^{k,n}_{\ell}}\lambda_{i}(A)+s\sum_{i\in Q^{k+1,n}_{j}}\lambda_{i}(A)
is an eigenvalue of
A^{[\alpha]}
. This provides another proof for Corollary
1
.

The next result follows from Thm.
4
.

Corollary 2
.

Suppose that
X(t)
is the solution of the linear time-invariant matrix differential equation

\frac{d}{dt}{X}(t)=AX(t),~X(0)=I,

(53)

with
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Fix
\alpha\in(1,n)\setminus\mathbb{Z}
. Then

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}X^{(\alpha)}(t)=A^{[\alpha]}X^{(\alpha)}(t),\quad X^{(\alpha)}(0)=I.

(54)

Proof:

The solution of (
53
) is
X(t)=\exp(At)
. By (
50
),
X^{(\alpha)}(t)=\exp(A^{[\alpha]}t)
, and this yields (
54
).
∎

It is important to note that
Eq. (
54
) holds only when
A
is a constant matrix. The next example demonstrates this.

Example 5
.

Consider the linear time-varying matrix differential equation

\dot{X}(t)=A(t)X(t),\quad X(0)=I,

with

A(t)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
1&t\end{bmatrix}.

In this case,

X(t)=\begin{bmatrix}a(t)&0\\
b(t)&c(t)\end{bmatrix}
with

\displaystyle a(t)

\displaystyle:=\exp(t),~b(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(\sigma+\frac{t^{2}-\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma,

\displaystyle c(t)

\displaystyle:=\exp({t^{2}}/2).

For any
t\in(0,\infty)\setminus\{2\}
,
the Jordan decomposition of
X
is

X=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{a-c}{b}&0\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\
0&c\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{b}{a-c}&0\\
-\frac{b}{a-c}&1\end{bmatrix},

so

X^{s}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{a-c}{b}&0\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a^{s}&0\\
0&c^{s}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{b}{a-c}&0\\
-\frac{b}{a-c}&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a^{s}&0\\
-\frac{a^{s}-c^{s}}{a-c}b&c^{s}\end{bmatrix}.

Let
\alpha=1+s
, with
s\in(0,1)
. Then Definition
2

gives

X^{(\alpha)}=\left(ac\right)^{s}X^{1-s}
,
and (
49
) gives

A^{[\alpha]}=\begin{bmatrix}1+st&0\\
1-s&s+t\end{bmatrix}
.
In particular, substituting
s=0.5
and
t=1
yields

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}X^{(1.5)}(1)=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}5.23551&0\\
4.48053&4.07742\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle A^{[1.5]}(1)X^{(1.5)}(1)=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}5.23551&0\\
4.26352&4.07742\end{bmatrix},

so
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}X^{(\alpha)}(t)\not=A^{[\alpha]}(t)X^{(\alpha)}(t)
.

It is well-known
[
32
]

that for any
A,T\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, with
T
non-singular, and any integer
\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we have

(TAT^{-1})^{[\ell]}=T^{(\ell)}A^{[\ell]}(T^{-1})^{(\ell)}=T^{(\ell)}A^{[\ell]}(T^{(\ell)})^{-1}.

(55)

The next result shows
the
\alpha
additive compounds
under a coordinate transformation.

Theorem 5
.

Let
A,T\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n},
with
T
non-singular, and
pick
\alpha\in(1,n)\setminus\mathbb{Z}
.
Then

(TAT^{-1})^{[\alpha]}=(T^{(k)}\otimes T^{(k+1)})A^{[\alpha]}(T^{(k)}\otimes T^{(k+1)})^{-1}.

(56)

Proof:

Let
B:=TAT^{-1}
.
Using (
49
) and (
55
) yields

\displaystyle B^{[\alpha]}=

\displaystyle((1-s)(TAT^{-1})^{[k]})\oplus(s(TAT^{-1})^{[k+1]})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-s)(T^{(k)}A^{[k]}(T^{-1})^{(k)})\otimes(T^{(k+1)}I_{r_{1}}(T^{(k+1)})^{-1})

\displaystyle+s(T^{(k)}I_{r_{2}}(T^{(k)})^{-1})\otimes(T^{(k+1)}A^{[k+1]}(T^{-1})^{(k+1)}).

Using Properties (
d
) and (
g
) in Lemma
2
gives

\displaystyle B^{[\alpha]}=

\displaystyle(1-s)(T^{(k)}\otimes T^{(k+1)})(A^{[k]}\otimes I_{r_{1}})

\displaystyle\left((T^{(k)})^{-1}\otimes(T^{(k+1)})^{-1}\right)+s(T^{(k)}\otimes T^{(k+1)})

\displaystyle(I_{r_{2}}\otimes A^{[k+1]})\left((T^{(k)})^{-1}\otimes(T^{(k+1)})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(T^{(k)}\otimes T^{(k+1)})\left((1-s)(A^{[k]}\otimes I_{r_{1}})+sI_{r_{2}}\otimes A^{[k+1]}\right)

\displaystyle(T^{(k)}\otimes T^{(k+1)})^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(T^{(k)}\otimes T^{(k+1)})A^{[\alpha]}(T^{(k)}\otimes T^{(k+1)})^{-1},

and this completes the proof.
∎

Remark 8
.

For a non-singular
T\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, note that

T^{(k+\frac{1}{2})}=(T^{(k)})^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes(T^{(k+1)})^{\frac{1}{2}}
,
and thus

\displaystyle(T^{(k+\frac{1}{2})})^{2}=

\displaystyle\left((T^{(k)})^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes(T^{(k+1)})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left((T^{(k)})^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes(T^{(k+1)})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left((T^{(k)})^{\frac{1}{2}}(T^{(k)})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\otimes\left((T^{(k+1)})^{\frac{1}{2}}(T^{(k+1)})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{(k)}\otimes T^{(k+1)}.

Therefore, (
56
) can be rewritten in the more compact form

(TAT^{-1})^{[\alpha]}=(T^{(k+\frac{1}{2})})^{2}A^{[\alpha]}(T^{(k+\frac{1}{2})})^{-2}.

The next subsection analyzes the
matrix measure
of the
\alpha
additive compound. This will play an important role in the analysis of
\alpha
contracting systems.

III-D

Matrix measures of the
\alpha
additive compound

It is well-known
[
5
]

that if
|\cdot|
is monotonic then
the induced matrix norm satisfies

||D||=\max\big(|d_{1}|,\dots,|d_{n}|\big),

for any diagonal matrix
D=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
. This implies that
the induced matrix measure satisfies

\displaystyle\mu(D)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\varepsilon^{-1}(||I+\varepsilon D||-1)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\varepsilon^{-1}(\max_{i}\{|1+\varepsilon d_{i}|\}-1)

\displaystyle=\max_{i}\{\operatorname{Re}(d_{i})\}.

(57)

Therefore, for any
\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we have

\displaystyle\mu(D^{[\ell]})=\max_{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell}\}\in Q^{\ell,n}}\left(\sum_{p=1}^{\ell}\operatorname{Re}(d_{i_{p}})\right).

(58)

Eqs. (
49
) and (
58
) imply that

\displaystyle\mu(D^{[\alpha]})=(1-s)\mu(D^{[k]})+s\mu(D^{[k+1]}).

(59)

Our next goal is to show that this holds for general matrices.
Towards this end, we first provide a useful expression for the matrix measure of a Kronecker sum of matrices.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\mu
denote a matrix measure associated with a induced matrix norm
\|\cdot\|
such that

\|A\otimes B\|=\|A\|\|B\|

(60)

for any matrices
A,B
.
Then

\mu(X\oplus Y)=\mu(X)+\mu(Y),

(61)

for any
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
.

Proof:

Fix
\varepsilon>0
.
Properties (
a
) and (
j
) in Lemma
2
yield

\displaystyle||\exp(\varepsilon(X\oplus Y))||

\displaystyle=||\exp(\varepsilon X\oplus\varepsilon Y)||

\displaystyle=||\exp(\varepsilon X)\otimes\exp(\varepsilon Y)||.

By (
60
),
||\exp(\varepsilon(X\oplus Y))||=||\exp(\varepsilon X)||||\exp(\varepsilon Y)||
,
and thus

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}||\exp(\varepsilon(X\oplus Y))||

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}||\exp(\varepsilon X)||\right)||\exp(\varepsilon Y)||

\displaystyle+||\exp(\varepsilon X)||\left(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}||\exp(\varepsilon Y)||\right).

It follows from (
30
) that

\mu(A)=\left(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}||\exp(\varepsilon A)||\right)|_{\varepsilon=0},

for any
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\mu(X\oplus Y)=\left(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}||\exp(\varepsilon(X\oplus Y))||\right)|_{\varepsilon=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}||\exp(\varepsilon X)||\right)|_{\varepsilon=0}+\left(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}||\exp(\varepsilon Y)||\right)|_{\varepsilon=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu(X)+\mu(Y),

and this completes the proof.
∎

We can now provide a useful expression for the matrix measure of
A^{[\alpha]}
.

Corollary 3
.

Let
\mu_{p}
denote a matrix measure induced by some
L_{p}
norm with
p\geq 1
. For any
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and any
\alpha\in(1,n)\setminus\mathbb{Z}
,
we have

\mu_{p}(A^{[\alpha]})=(1-s)\mu_{p}(A^{[k]})+s\mu_{p}(A^{[k+1]}).

(62)

Proof:

IV

In this section,
\alpha\in[1,n)
,
and the special case where

The Hausdorff dimension of a set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted by
\dim_{H}K
.
Let
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set, and let
g:D\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
C^{1}
mapping.
Let

J_{g}(x):=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}g(x).

A set

is said to be
negatively invariant under

if
K\subseteq g(K)
. Intuitively speaking,
g

“increases”
K
.
The next result is the Douady
and Oesterlé theorem
[
8
]
.
We state in the form given in
[
44
]
.

Theorem 7
.

Suppose that
K\subset D
is compact and negatively invariant under
g
.
Fix
\alpha\in[1,n)
, and write
\alpha=k+s
,
with
k
an integer and
s\in[0,1)
.
Let

\omega(K,\alpha,g):=\max_{x\in K}\left(\sigma_{1}(J_{g}(x))\cdots\sigma_{k}(J_{g}(x))(\sigma_{k+1}(J_{g}(x)))^{s}\right).

(63)

If
\omega(K,\alpha,g)<1

then

\dim_{H}K<\alpha.

Intuitively speaking,
(
63
) implies that

g
is a “contraction in dimension
\alpha
”, uniformly in
K
.
If
g
“increases”
K

then necessarily
\dim_{H}K<\alpha
.

The next simple example demonstrates Thm.
7
.

Example 7
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be the linear mapping given by

. Then the singular values of the Jacobian of

are
1,1/2,1/4
,
and (
63
) holds for any
\alpha>1
. Thus, Thm.
7
implies that for any compact set

such that

, we have
\dim_{H}K\leq 1
. For example, the set
K:=[0,1]\times\{0\}\times\{0\}
satisfies

and
\dim_{H}K=1
.

Using the
\alpha
multiplicative compound we can express condition (

) in a more elegant form. Indeed,
it follows from (

so the condition in Thm.

becomes

\max_{x\in K}||(J_{g}^{T}(x)J_{g}(x))^{(\alpha)}||_{2}<1.

This provides a more intuitive description for “contraction in dimension

Remark 9

J_{cl}:=J_{f}+J_{g}
, so

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(J_{cl}^{[\alpha]})

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}(J_{f}^{[\alpha]}+J_{g}^{[\alpha]})\leq\mu_{1}(J_{f}^{[\alpha]})+\mu_{1}(J_{g}^{[\alpha]})

\displaystyle\leq 1-2b-s(b+1)+\mu_{1}(J_{g}^{[\alpha]}).

This implies that the closed-loop system is
\alpha
contracting if

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(J^{[\alpha]}_{g}(x))<s(b+1)+2b-1\text{ for all }x\in D.

(77)

Consider, for example, the controller

g(x_{1},x_{2})=\operatorname{diag}(c,c,0)x,\text{ with }c<0.

Then

J_{g}^{[\alpha]}=c\operatorname{diag}(2,1+s,1+s)

and for any
c<0
condition (
77
) becomes

\displaystyle(1+s)c<s(b+1)+2b-1.

(78)

This provides a simple recipe for
determining the gain
c
so that the closed-loop system is
2+s
contracting. For example,
when
s\to 0
, Eq. (
78
) yields

c<2b-1

and this guarantees that the closed-loop system is
2
-order contracting. Recall that in a
2
-order contracting system
every nonempty omega limit set is a single equilibrium,
thus ruling out chaotic attractors and even non-trivial limit cycles
[
21
]
.
Fig.
2
depicts the behaviour of the closed-loop system with
b
as in (
76
) and
c=2b-1.1
. The closed-loop system is thus
2
-order contracting, and as expected
every solution converges to an equilibrium.

V
Conclusion

The
k
multiplicative and
k
additive compounds of a matrix play an important role in geometry, multi-linear algebra, dynamical systems, and more. These compounds are based on
k\times k
minors and are thus defined for integer values of
k
only. The
k
compounds were recently used to study an extension of contracting systems to
k
-order contracting systems
[
53
]
.

Here, we generalised
k
compounds to
\alpha
compounds, with
\alpha
real, and analyzed the properties of these compounds.
As an application, we showed that these compounds provide more direct and intuitive interpretation of important functions, e.g.
\omega(K,\alpha,g)
appearing in the seminal work of Douady
and Oesterlé. We also introduced the new notion of
\alpha
contracting systems, with
\alpha
real, generalizing the
notion of
k
-order contracting systems with
k
an integer, recently analyzed in
[
53
]
. Thus, rather than a binary choice
-

contracting or not contracting in a given metric
-
one can place any
system on a continuous axis of contraction levels.

Due to space limitations, we focused here on theoretical results, but we believe that many applications are possible.
First, there exist nonlinear systems where the “level of contraction” naturally changes in a continuous way, for example, systems that involve a continuous-time dynamics and discrete-time switching (see, e.g.
[
24
]
).
Second, contraction theory (i.e. the theory of 1-order contracting systems
[
23
]
) has found many applications in control synthesis (see e.g.,
[
39
,
43
,
27
,
38
,
28
,
36
,
55
]
). An interesting research direction is to apply the generalization described here to
control synthesis in such contexts. Finally, our results could be used to define generalized notions of convexity in optimization and machine learning. Just as Riemannian convexity of a scalar function with respect to a metric is equivalent to contraction in that metric of natural gradient descent
[
50
]
, notions of
\alpha
Riemannian convexity could similarly be defined through equivalent
\alpha
contracting autonomous dynamical systems.
