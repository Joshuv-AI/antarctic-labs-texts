Zeros of optimal polynomial approximants in ℓ A p \ell^{p}_{A}
Raymond Cheng, William T. Ross, Daniel Seco

Abstract
. The study of inner and cyclic functions in ℓ A p \ell^{p}_{A} spaces requires a better understanding of the zeros of the so-called optimal polynomial approximants. We determine that a point of the complex plane is the zero of an optimal polynomial approximant for some element of ℓ A p \ell^{p}_{A} if and only if it lies outside of a closed disk (centered at the origin) of a particular radius which depends on the value of p p . We find the value of this radius for p ≠ 2 p\neq 2 . In addition, for each positive integer d d there is a polynomial f d f_{d} of degree at most d d that minimizes the modulus of the root of its optimal linear polynomial approximant. We develop a method for finding these extremal functions f d f_{d} and discuss their properties. The method involves the Lagrange multiplier method and a resulting dynamical system.

Primary 47A15; Secondary 30C15, 30H99.

1.
Introduction

For
1<p<\infty
, the space
\ell_{A}^{p}
is the set of power series
f
whose Taylor coefficients
(a_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
belong to the standard sequence space
\ell^{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
. This paper concerns the zeros of optimal polynomial approximants in
\ell^{p}_{A}
. Every
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
is an analytic function on the open unit disk
\mathbb{D}=\{z:|z|<1\}
and, endowed with the norm
\|f\|_{p}:=\|(a_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}\|_{\ell^{p}}
,
\ell^{p}_{A}
is a Banach space. When
p=2
,
\ell^{2}_{A}
is a Hilbert space with inner product
\langle f,g\rangle:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\overline{b_{n}}
, where
g(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}z^{n}
. In fact,
\ell^{2}_{A}
is the well-studied Hardy space.

Our motivation stems from the ongoing exploration of the invariant subspaces and the cyclic vectors for the shift operator

(Sf)(z)=zf(z)
on
\ell^{p}_{A}
. By
invariant subspace
, we mean a closed subspace
\mathscr{M}\subseteq\ell^{p}_{A}
for which
S\mathscr{M}\subseteq\mathscr{M}
. By
cyclic vector,
we mean an
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
for which
\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{S^{n}f:n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}=\ell^{p}_{A}.

Beurling’s seminal paper
[
6
]
determined both the cyclic vectors and invariant subspaces of
\ell^{2}_{A}
. Indeed,
\mathscr{M}
is an invariant subspace of
\ell^{2}_{A}
if and only if
\mathscr{M}=\Theta\ell^{2}_{A}=\{\Theta f:f\in\ell^{p}_{A}\}
for some inner function
\Theta
. A vector
f\in\ell^{2}_{A}
is cyclic if and only if
f
is an outer function. Both inner functions and outer functions have specific formulas through classical theorems of Nevanlinna and Riesz
[
12
,
13
]
. Beurling pondered the invariant subspaces and cyclic vectors for
p\not=2
, but the question remains very much unresolved. In fact, when
p>2
, the structure of the invariant subspaces of
\ell^{p}_{A}
is very complicated
[
1
]
.

In this paper, we focus on one of the many obstacles to understanding the invariant subspaces and cyclic vectors for
\ell^{p}_{A}
when
p\not=2
– the “extra zeros” that appear in optimal polynomial approximants and in the related
p
-inner functions.
To better explain what we mean here, let
\mathscr{P}_{n}
be the set of polynomials of degree at most
n
, and let
\mathscr{P}:=\bigcup_{n\geqslant 0}\mathscr{P}_{n}
.
Observe that
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
is cyclic if and only if there is a sequence
(p_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
\mathscr{P}
such that
\|1-p_{n}f\|_{p}\to 0
. Various papers
[
3
,
4
]
have discussed
optimal polynomial approximants
. These are polynomials
p_{n,f}
defined as follows: For a non-constant
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
p_{n,f}
be the unique
p_{n,f}\in\mathscr{P}_{n}
such that

\|1-p_{n,f}f\|_{p}=\operatorname{dist}(1,f\mathscr{P}_{n}).

When
1<p<\infty
, the uniform convexity of
\ell^{p}_{A}
ensures the uniqueness of
p_{n,f}
. When
p=2
, it is known that the zeros of
p_{n,f}
lie outside
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, the closure of
\mathbb{D}

[
5
]
(see also Proposition
3.12
). Perhaps one might argue this is the way it should be since the polynomials that optimally attempt to approximate
1/f
should not carry any zeros in
\mathbb{D}
. When
p\neq 2
, however, we will see that an optimal polynomial approximant
p_{n,f}
might have zeros inside
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. This stands in the way of it truly being a close approximation of
1/f
.

These extra zeros also appear in several ways when trying to extend Beurling’s treatment of the invariant subspaces for the
p=2
case. One of the innovative tricks of Beurling was to show that if
f\in\ell^{2}_{A}
, then the unique solution
\widehat{f}
to

\|f-\widehat{f}\|_{2}=\inf_{\phi\in\mathscr{P}}\|f(z)-z\phi(z)f(z)\|_{2}

has the property that
f-\widehat{f}
is an inner function (up to a multiplicative constant) and that
f
and
f-\widehat{f}
generate the same invariant subspace. This was taken up with much success in various other function spaces such as the Bergman and Dirichlet spaces in
[
2
,
15
]
. What complicates matters when
p\neq 2
is that the corresponding function
f-\widehat{f}
might have a zero in
\mathbb{D}
that is not a zero of
f
(this is the origin of the term “extra zero”). Thus, unlike when
p=2
, the functions
f
and
f-\widehat{f}
do not generate the same invariant subspace. To see a concrete example of how an extra zero can arise when
p=\frac{4}{3}
, we refer the reader to
[
8
]
.

To measure the extent to which these extra zeros of
p_{n,f}
occur, we define the following set
\Omega_{p}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
.

Definition 1.1
.

For fixed
1<p<\infty
, a point
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
belongs to
\Omega_{p}
if there exists an
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
with
f(0)\not=0
and an
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
p_{n,f}(z_{0})=0
. In other words
z_{0}\in\Omega_{p}
when
z_{0}
is the zero of the optimal polynomial approximant for some
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
with
f(0)\not=0
.

The assumption that
f(0)\neq 0
is equivalent to
p_{n,f}
not being identically
0
(Proposition
3.3
). When
p=2
, previous work from
[
5
]
shows that
\Omega_{2}=\mathbb{C}\backslash\overline{\mathbb{D}}
(see also Proposition
3.12
). In particular, no
p_{n,f}
for any non-constant
f\in\ell^{2}_{A}
has a zero in
\mathbb{D}
. Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

For
1<p<\infty
, with
p\not=2
, there exists a
\tau_{p}\in(1,2)
such that
\Omega_{p}=\mathbb{C}\backslash\frac{1}{\tau_{p}}\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

Thus there is a striking difference between the
p=2
case, where
\Omega_{p}\cap\mathbb{D}=\varnothing
, and the
p\not=2
case, where
\Omega_{p}\cap\mathbb{D}\not=\varnothing
. In the latter case, extra zeros for optimal polynomial approximants exist in abundance.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
involves the following steps to “fill out”
\Omega_{p}
: Lemma
3.4
says that
z_{0}\in\Omega_{p}
if and only if there is some
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
with
f(0)\not=0
such that the optimal
linear
approximant
p_{1,f}
vanishes at
z_{0}
, thus reducing the complexity of the problem. Proposition
3.11
shows that

\mathbb{C}\backslash\overline{\mathbb{D}}\subseteq\Omega_{p}.

Furthermore, Proposition
3.13
yields that
\Omega_{p}
is rotationally symmetric, and Corollary
3.15
, that it is path connected. At that point, we know that
\Omega_{p}
is the complement of a disk. Two things remain to be shown:
\tau_{p}\in(1,2)
and
\Omega_{p}
is open.

Birkhoff-James orthogonality, and the associated Pythagorean inequalities (Lemma
5.3
), have been successfully used in
[
7
,
10
,
11
]
to study
\ell^{p}_{A}
and related spaces. Here, these concepts are applied in Proposition
5.4
to produce radii
\tfrac{1}{2}<r_{p}<1
such that

\Omega_{p}\cap r_{p}\mathbb{D}=\varnothing.

In particular, this proves that

\Omega_{p}\cap\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}\overline{\mathbb{D}}=\varnothing
for all
1<p<\infty
.
In Section
6
we provide specific examples which show that when
p\neq 2
there are functions
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
(in fact polynomials) and
n\in\mathbb{N}
for which the optimal approximants
p_{n,f}
have zeros in
\mathbb{D}
. This proves that
\tau_{p}>1
and hence
\Omega_{p}\cap\mathbb{D}\not=\varnothing
.

Trying to determine
\tau_{p}
exactly leads to the most difficult part of this paper. We reduce the problem of determining
\tau_{p}
in several ways, and thanks to the Lagrange multiplier method, we show it is equivalent to finding polynomials whose coefficients give solutions to a set of nonlinear recurrence relations. The behavior of these solutions as the degree of the polynomials grows will determine the value of
\tau_{p}
. In the penultimate section of this paper, we use a dynamical systems approach to solve the recurrence relations mentioned above and show that the limit of the solutions is not attained by a function in
\ell^{p}_{A}
.
Thus,
1/\tau_{p}
is not the zero of an optimal approximant for any function.
At that point we know that
\Omega_{p}
is open and the proof of Theorem
1.2
is complete. Although we cannot compute
\tau_{p}
in closed form, in Theorem
8.7
we describe it implicitly and provide numerical estimates for it.

As a byproduct of the proof of Theorem
1.2
, we provide a connection between zeros of
p
-inner functions (i.e., those that arise as a metric co-projection
f-\widehat{f}
) and those of the optimal polynomial approximants (Theorem
4.10
).

2.
Notation

Let us begin with some basic notation. Let
\mathbb{D}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}
be the open unit disk in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
, and
\operatorname{Hol}(\mathbb{D})
the analytic functions on
\mathbb{D}
. We set
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3\ldots\}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Let
\mathscr{P}=\mathbb{C}[z]
denote the vector space of all polynomials in the complex variable
z
with complex coefficients, and for
d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, let
\mathscr{P}_{d}
denote the vector space of polynomials of degree at most
d
. A reference for the material below is the book
[
9
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

For
1<p<\infty
, let
\ell^{p}_{A}
be the set of power series

f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}z^{k}

for which

\|f\|_{p}:=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|a_{k}|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}<\infty.

(2.2)

One can show that
\ell^{p}_{A}\subseteq\operatorname{Hol}(\mathbb{D})
and that
\ell^{p}_{A}
is a uniformly convex Banach space.

The uniform convexity of
\ell^{p}_{A}
guarantees that for a closed subspace
\mathscr{V}\subseteq\ell^{p}_{A}
and
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
, there is a unique
v_{f}\in\mathscr{V}
such that

\|f-v_{f}\|_{p}=\operatorname{dist}(f,\mathscr{V}):=\inf_{v\in\mathscr{V}}\|v-f\|_{p}.

(2.3)

We say that
v_{f}
is the
metric projection
of
f
onto
\mathscr{V}
.
When
p=2
,
\ell^{2}_{A}
is a Hilbert space and the map
f\mapsto v_{f}
is the orthogonal projection of
\ell^{2}_{A}
onto
\mathscr{V}
(and hence linear). When
p\not=2
, the map
f\mapsto v_{f}
is in general nonlinear. However, it is continuous.

Since the metric projection
f\mapsto v_{f}
on
\ell^{p}_{A}
is at the core of this paper, we omit the cases
p=1
and
p=\infty
from our discussion. This is because the spaces
\ell^{1}_{A}
and
\ell^{\infty}_{A}
are not uniformly convex, and hence the closest point to
\mathscr{V}
is generally not attained; furthermore, when attained, the nearest point need not be unique. In fact, this failure of uniqueness is relevant to the study of optimal polynomial approximants
[
16
]
.

When dealing with approximation theory in Banach spaces without a natural concept of orthogonality, a useful substitute is Birkhoff-James orthogonality. This generalization of Hilbert space orthogonality is valid in arbitrary normed spaces and has proven to be useful in exploring invariant subspaces and cyclicity in various Banach spaces of analytic functions
[
10
,
11
,
16
]
.

Definition 2.4
.

For
f,g\in\ell^{p}_{A}
we say that
f
is
Birkhoff-James orthogonal
to
g
if

\|f+\alpha g\|_{p}\geqslant\|f\|_{p}\quad\mbox{for all $\alpha\in\mathbb{C}$},

and in this case we write
f\perp_{p}g
.

When
p=2
, Birkhoff-James orthogonality coincides with orthogonality in the usual Hilbert space sense, since the definition above says that
0
is the closest point in the subspace
\mathbb{C}g
to
f
. The ordering in the relation
f\perp_{p}g
is important since, when
p\not=2
, it is possible that
f\perp_{p}g
but
g\not\perp_{p}f
. There is the following tangible condition for Birkhoff-James orthogonality involving a semi-inner product on
\ell^{p}_{A}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

If
f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}z^{k}
and
g(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}z^{k}
belong to
\ell^{p}_{A}
, then

f\perp_{p}g\iff\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|a_{k}|^{p-2}\overline{a_{k}}b_{k}=0,

(2.6)

where, in the above sum, we understand any occurrence of “
|0|^{p-2}0
” as zero.

This lemma implies that the relation
f\perp_{p}g
is linear in the second slot; that is, if
f\perp_{p}g
and
f\perp_{p}h
, then for any
\alpha
,
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
,
f\perp_{p}(\alpha g+\beta h)
.
This is reminiscent of an orthogonality relation in
\ell^{2}
, and even more so after introducing the following notation: For
z=re^{i\theta}\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
and
s\geqslant 0
define

z^{\langle s\rangle}:=r^{s}e^{-i\theta}.

(2.7)

If
z=0
then
z^{\langle s\rangle}
is defined to be
0
. A way to interpret this is that the mapping
z\mapsto z^{\langle s\rangle}
generalizes complex conjugation.
With this notation, (
2.6
) tells us that

f\perp_{p}g\iff\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}^{\langle p-1\rangle}b_{k}=0.

(2.8)

In connection with this notation for powers, we will need the following derivative formulas in the sections ahead. Their verification entails routine calculus.

Lemma 2.9
.

If
t
is a real variable, and
s>1
, then

\frac{d}{dt}|t|^{s}=st^{\langle s-1\rangle}\ \ \mbox{and}\,\ \ \frac{d}{dt}t^{\langle s\rangle}=s|t|^{s-1}.

3.
Basic properties of
\Omega_{p}

Fix
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
and consider the finite dimensional vector space
f\mathscr{P}_{n}
, where we recall that
\mathscr{P}_{n}
are the polynomials of degree at most
n
.
Since
f\mathscr{P}_{n}
is closed (being finite dimensional), (
2.3
) says there is a unique
p_{n,f}\in\mathscr{P}_{n}
such that

\|1-p_{n,f}f\|_{p}=\operatorname{dist}(1,f\mathscr{P}_{n}),

and that

p_{n,f}f
is the metric projection of
1
onto the subspace
f\mathscr{P}_{n}
.

Definition 3.1
.

For
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the unique
p_{n,f}\in\mathscr{P}_{n}
for which

\|1-p_{n,f}f\|_{p}=\operatorname{dist}(1,f\mathscr{P}_{n})

is called the
optimal polynomial approximant (OPA) of
1/f
of degree at most
n
, abbreviated
p_{n,f}
. We call
p_{1,f}
the
optimal linear approximant.

Remark 3.2
.

Here are some simple observations about
p_{n,f}
.

(a)

If
f
is a constant function, then
p_{n,f}
is a constant polynomial for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

(b)

If
\operatorname{deg}p_{n,f}=k<n
, then
p_{n,f}=p_{t,f}
for all
k\leqslant t\leqslant n
.

The following helps us understand why the assumption
f(0)\neq 0
is made in numerous results to follow, in order to eliminates trivial cases.

Proposition 3.3
.

For
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
, the following are equivalent:

(a)

f(0)=0
.

(b)

p_{n,f}\equiv 0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

(c)

There exists some
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
p_{n,f}\equiv 0
.

Proof.

Clearly
(b)\Rightarrow(c)
. To check
(a)\Rightarrow(b)
, take
f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}z^{k}
, and suppose that
a_{0}=f(0)=0
. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\phi\in\mathscr{P}_{n}
,
let
(c_{k})_{k=0}^{\infty}
be the sequence of Taylor coefficients of
\phi f
. Then

\|1-\phi f\|_{p}\geqslant\Big(1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|c_{k}|^{p}\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}}\geqslant 1=\|1-0f\|_{p}.

Thus,
p_{n,f}\equiv 0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
To see that
(c)\Rightarrow(a)
, suppose to the contrary that
f(0)\neq 0
. Then, for any
c\in\mathbb{C}
,

\|1-cf\|_{p}^{p}=|1-cf(0)|^{p}+|c|^{p}\|f-f(0)\|_{p}^{p}.

For
c
sufficiently close to zero, the above expression can be made less than
1
. Thus
p_{0,f}\not\equiv 0
.
Since

\inf_{\phi\in\mathscr{P}_{n}}\|1-\phi f\|_{p}\leqslant\|1-p_{0,f}f\|_{p}<1,

it follows from (b) that
p_{n,f}\not\equiv 0
for all
n
.
∎

Next, we see that OPAs are well behaved with respect to removing a root.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose
z_{0}
is a root of
p_{n,f}
. Then

g(z)=f(z)\,(z-z_{0})
belongs to
\ell^{p}_{A}
and

p_{n-1,g}(z)=\frac{p_{n,f}(z)}{z-z_{0}}.

Proof.

From Definition
3.1
,

\displaystyle\inf_{q\in\mathscr{P}_{n-1}}\|1-qg\|_{p}

\displaystyle=\inf_{q\in\mathscr{P}_{n-1}}\|1-q(z)(z-z_{0})f(z)\|_{p}

\displaystyle\geqslant\inf_{\phi\in\mathscr{P}_{n}}\|1-\phi(z)f(z)\|_{p}

\displaystyle=\|1-p_{n,f}(z)f(z)\|_{p}.

Now observe that

q(z)=\frac{p_{n,f}(z)}{z-z_{0}}

minimizes the left-hand side. By Definition
3.1
,
q=p_{n-1,g}
.
∎

Repeated application of Lemma
3.4
yields the statement below.

Corollary 3.5
.

For
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
, the following are equivalent.

(a)

There is an
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
with
f(0)\not=0
and an
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
p_{n,f}(z_{0})=0
.

(b)

There is an
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
with
f(0)\not=0
such that
p_{1,f}(z_{0})=0
.

The previous corollary is a significant reduction of the problem of describing
\Omega_{p}
since one just needs to focus on finding the zeros of optimal
linear
approximants. For a given
1<p<\infty
, we will see that not every complex number
z_{0}
arises as the root of an OPA. With this in mind we can redefine the region of the plane containing the eligible roots.

Corollary 3.6
.

For
1<p<\infty
,

\Omega_{p}:=\{z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}:\exists f\in\ell^{p}_{A},f(0)\not=0\ni p_{1,f}(z_{0})=0\}.

Let us establish some basic geometric properties of
\Omega_{p}
. We start by connecting
p_{1,f}
to a simpler minimization problem. Here, we write
p_{1,f}(z)=c(1-t_{f}z)
, so that
p_{1,f}
has a root at
z=1/t_{f}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

If
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
with
f(0)\neq 0
, then

(1-t_{f}z)f(z)\perp_{p}zf(z)
, and

\|(1-t_{f}z)f(z)\|_{p}=\min_{t\in\mathbb{C}}\|(1-tz)f(z)\|_{p}.

Proof.

Since

\|1-p_{1,f}(z)f(z)\|_{p}\leqslant\|1-p_{1,f}(z)f(z)+\alpha f(z)\|_{p}

and

\|1-p_{1,f}(z)f(z)\|_{p}\leqslant\|1-p_{1,f}(z)f(z)+\alpha zf(z)\|_{p}

hold for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
,
the definition of Birkhoff-James orthogonality (Definition
2.4
) implies that

\displaystyle 1-p_{1,f}(z)f(z)

\displaystyle\perp_{p}f(z)

(3.8)

\displaystyle 1-p_{1,f}f(z)

\displaystyle\perp_{p}zf(z).

(3.9)

Next, (
3.9
) can be written as

1-c(1-t_{f}z)f(z)\perp_{p}zf(z).

Since the constant term in
zf(z)
is zero, one can use Lemma
2.5
to see that

c(1-t_{f}z)f(z)\perp_{p}zf(z)
. Another application of Lemma
2.5
says we can also drop the
c
on the left side to obtain
(1-t_{f}z)f(z)\perp_{p}zf(z)
. The second claim follows immediately from the first.
∎

Here is a simple example of a non-inhabitant of
\Omega_{p}
.

Proposition 3.10
.

0\not\in\Omega_{p}
for all
1<p<\infty
.

Proof.

With the hypothesis that
f(0)\not=0
, it must be that
p_{1,f}(0)\not=0
. Otherwise,
p_{1,f}(z)=cz
and so

\|1-p_{1,f}f\|^{p}_{p}=1+|c|^{p}\|f\|_{p}^{p}.

The right hand side of the above is minimized when
c=0
, which would make
p_{1,f}\equiv 0
. But we already excluded this possibility.
∎

Here are some inhabitants of
\Omega_{p}
.

Proposition 3.11
.

\mathbb{C}\backslash\overline{\mathbb{D}}\subseteq\Omega_{p}
for all
1<p<\infty
.

Proof.

For
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\overline{\mathbb{D}}
,
set
f(z)=(z-z_{0})^{-1}
which, under the hypothesis that
|z_{0}|>1
, belongs to
\ell^{p}_{A}
. Then
p_{1,f}(z)=z-z_{0}
, since
1-p_{1,f}f\equiv 0
.
∎

Therefore, the problem of describing
\Omega_{p}
reduces to looking at
\Omega_{p}\cap\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. When
p=2
, there is the following special case of a result from
[
5
]
. We include a proof here for completeness.

Proposition 3.12
.

\Omega_{2}=\mathbb{C}\backslash\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

Proof.

When
p=2
, Birkhoff-James orthogonality
\perp_{2}
agrees with Hilbert space orthogonality. From Proposition
3.11
we just need to show that
\Omega_{2}\cap\overline{\mathbb{D}}=\varnothing
. From Lemma
3.7
for
p=2
we have

\displaystyle f(z)

\displaystyle=(1-t_{f}z)f(z)+t_{f}zf(z)

\displaystyle\langle zf(z),f(z)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle zf(z),(1-t_{f}z)f(z)\rangle+t_{f}\langle zf(z),zf(z)\rangle

\displaystyle\langle zf(z),f(z)\rangle

\displaystyle=0+t_{f}\|f\|_{2}^{2}.

Consequently
t_{f}
satisfies

|t_{f}|=\frac{|\langle zf(z),f(z)\rangle|}{\|f\|_{2}^{2}}\leqslant\frac{\|zf(z)\|_{2}\|f\|_{2}}{\|f\|_{2}^{2}}=\frac{\|f\|_{2}\|f\|_{2}}{\|f\|_{2}^{2}}=1.

Equality is attained precisely when
f(z)
and
zf(z)
are linearly dependent, and this is exactly when
f\equiv 0
.
∎

As we proceed through the paper, we will show that when
p\not=2
,
\Omega_{p}
is the complement of a disk of radius less than one and thus, unlike the
p=2
case,
\Omega_{p}\cap\mathbb{D}\not=\varnothing
.

Next we show that
\Omega_{p}
has the following rotational symmetry property.

Proposition 3.13
.

e^{i\theta}\Omega_{p}=\Omega_{p}
for all
0\leqslant\theta<2\pi
.

Proof.

If
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
,
q\in\mathscr{P}_{n}
, and
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
, the nonzero Taylor coefficients
(c_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
of
1-q(z)f(z)
and those of
1-q(e^{i\theta}z)f(e^{i\theta}z)
have the same modulus. Therefore,

\displaystyle\|1-q(z)f(z)\|_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle=|1-q(0)f(0)|^{p}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|c_{k}|^{p}

\displaystyle=\|1-q(e^{i\theta}z)f(e^{i\theta}z)\|_{p}^{p}.

Thus,
p_{n,f}(z)
is optimal for
f(z)
if and only if
p_{n,f}(e^{i\theta}z)
is optimal for
f(e^{i\theta}z)
. Finally,
z_{0}
is a zero of
p_{n,f}(z)
if and only if
e^{-i\theta}z_{0}
is a zero of
p_{n,f}(e^{i\theta}z)
.
∎

Our last goal in this section is to show that
\Omega_{p}
is path connected. Together with Propositions
3.10
and
3.11
, and the rotational symmetry of
\Omega_{p}
, this will show that
\Omega_{p}
is either the punctured plane or the complement of some disk (either open or closed) centered at the origin. The path connectedness of
\Omega_{p}
will be a consequence of the following lemma.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
[a,b]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
.
Suppose that
F:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\longmapsto\mathbb{R}
is continuous and satisfies the property that
for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists a unique
t_{x}\in[a,b]
such that
F(x,t_{x})=0
. Then the mapping
\Upsilon:x\mapsto t_{x}
is continuous.

Proof.

Observe that
\Upsilon
is well defined. Suppose that
x_{n}\to x
in
\mathbb{R}
. The continuity of
F
shows that the set

S:=\{(x,t):\ F(x,t)=0\}=F^{-1}(\{0\})

is closed.

Consider the collection of pairs
\{(x_{n},\Upsilon(x_{n}))\}
. The collection of second entries lies in the bounded interval
[a,b]
, and hence there is an accumulation point
y\in[a,b]
, and a subsequence
(x_{n_{k}})_{k\geqslant 1}
for which
\Upsilon(x_{n_{k}})\to y
. The closedness of
S
then implies that
\Upsilon(x)=y
. In a similar way we see that any subsequence of
(x_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
has a further subsequence
(x_{\nu_{k}})_{k\geqslant 1}
for which

\Upsilon(x_{\nu_{k}})\to\Upsilon(x).

This implies that

\Upsilon(x_{n})\to\Upsilon(x).

∎

Corollary 3.15
.

\Omega_{p}
is path connected for any
1<p<\infty
.

Proof.

From the rotational symmetry of
\Omega_{p}
, it suffices to show that
\Omega_{p}\cap\mathbb{R}^{+}
is an interval. To apply the previous lemma, fix a polynomial
f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}a_{k}z^{k}
such that its linear approximant has a real positive zero, and define

F(x,t)=\frac{d}{dt}\Big\|(1-tz)\big(x+\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}z^{k}\big)\Big\|_{p}^{p}=:h^{\prime}(t),\ \,(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}.

Then
F
satisfies the hypothesis of the lemma and for each
x
the corresponding
t_{x}
is the zero of
h^{\prime}
. The intermediate value theorem says that these values of
t_{x}
constitute an interval. Repeat this argument, changing the definition of
F
by replacing
a_{j}
by
x
. We see that the solution
t
of
h^{\prime}(t)=0
depends continuously on the each of coefficients
a_{j}
of
f
.
∎

4.
\Omega_{p}
and
p
-inner functions

A point
z_{0}
belongs to
\Omega_{p}
precisely when there is an
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
with
f(0)\not=0
such that
p_{1,f}(z_{0})=0
. As we have seen earlier, and will reinforce later in this section, the problem of finding the zero of
p_{1,f}
reduces to the extremal problem of
finding a
t_{f}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\|(1-t_{f}z)f(z)\|_{p}=\min_{t\in\mathbb{C}}\|(1-tz)f(z)\|_{p}.

Furthermore, the definition of
\Omega_{p}
its rotational symmetry imply that
e^{i\theta}/t_{f}\in\Omega_{p}
for every
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
. We now connect this with another extremal problem involving the concept of a
p
-inner function.
Most of the material in this section comes,
mutatis mutandis
, from the book
[
9
, Ch. 8]
.

Definition 4.1
.

A function
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}\backslash\{0\}
is
p
-inner
if

f\perp_{p}S^{n}f\quad\mbox{for all $n\geqslant 1$}.

Examples of
p
-inner functions include the monomials
\{z^{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
as well as

f_{w}(z)=\frac{1-z/w}{1-w^{\langle p^{\prime}-1\rangle}z},\quad\mbox{where $w\in\mathbb{D}\backslash\{0\}$}.

In the above,
p^{\prime}
denotes the Hölder conjugate index to
p

[
9
, p. 111]
and
w^{\langle p^{\prime}-1\rangle}
comes from (
2.7
).

When
p=2
,
\ell^{2}_{A}
is a Hilbert space with inner product

\langle f,g\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\overline{b_{k}},

where
f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}z^{k}
and
g(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}z^{k}
.
By Parseval’s theorem, this can be written in integral form as

\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(e^{i\theta})\overline{g(e^{i\theta})}\,\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}.

The criterion for
2
-inner from Definition
4.1
becomes

\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(e^{i\theta})|^{2}e^{in\theta}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}=0\quad\mbox{for all $n\geqslant 1$}.

The equation above, along with its complex conjugate (and with a consideration of the Fourier coefficients of
|f(e^{i\theta})|^{2}
), shows that a function
f
is
2
-inner precisely when
|f(e^{i\theta})|
is a nonzero constant almost everywhere; that is to say, apart from a nonzero multiplicative constant,
f
is inner in the traditional sense from Beurling’s paper
[
6
]
.

For
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}\backslash\{0\}
let

[f]:=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{S^{n}f:n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}

denote the shift-invariant subspace of
\ell^{p}_{A}
generated by
f
. Let

J=f-\widehat{f},

where

\widehat{f}
is the unique function in
\ell^{p}_{A}
for which

\|f-\widehat{f}\|_{p}=\inf_{g\in[Sf]}\|f-g\|_{p}.

It turns out that
J

is
p
-inner
and
[
9
, p. 106]
shows that every
p
-inner function arises in this manner.

Classical theory shows that inner functions (in the sense of Beurling) satisfy certain extremal problems. It turns out that
p
-inner functions do something analogous
[
9
, p. 103]
.

Proposition 4.2
.

For
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
with
f(0)=1
, let

M:=\sup\left\{|\phi(0)|:\|\phi\|_{p}=1,\phi\in[f]\right\}

(4.3)

and

I:=\inf\left\{\|g\|_{p}:g(0)=1,\ g\in[f]\right\}.

(4.4)

Then,

(a)

IM=1
;

(b)

Each of the extremal problems above have unique solutions
\phi
and
g
respectively with

\phi(z)=\frac{g(z)}{\|g\|_{p}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad g(z)=\frac{\phi(z)}{\phi(0)}.

Let
G
be the (unique) solution to the infimum problem (
4.4
). Then by definition,

\|G\|_{p}\leqslant\|G(z)+z\Psi(z)\|_{p}\quad\mbox{for all
$\Psi\in[f]$.}

Conversely, this condition characterizes
G
.
In particular, we have
G\perp_{p}S^{n}G
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and thus
G
is
p
-inner.
In fact
G=f-\widehat{f}
.

The above propositions also hold, with essentially the same proof, if instead we consider the extremal problems

M_{N}:=\sup\left\{|\phi(0)|:\|\phi\|_{p}=1,\phi\in f\mathscr{P}_{N}\right\}

(4.5)

and

I_{N}:=\inf\left\{\|g\|_{p}:g(0)=1,g\in f\mathscr{P}_{N}\right\}

(4.6)

for fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
.

Proposition 4.7
.

Suppose that
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
,
f(0)=1
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

(a)

I_{N}M_{N}=1
.

(b)

Each of the extremal problems (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) have unique solutions
\phi_{N}
and
J_{N}
respectively which differ by a nonzero multiplicative constant.

We retain the notation
J_{N}
for the unique solution to the infimum problem (
4.6
) since it ties into our typical usage of
J=f-\widehat{f}
for the
p
-inner function associated with
f
.
Then, by definition,

\|J_{N}\|_{p}\leqslant\|J_{N}(z)+z\Psi(z)\|_{p}\quad\mbox{for all
$\Psi\in f\mathscr{P}_{N-1}$.}

Conversely, this condition characterizes
J_{N}
.

For fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}
, recall the original optimal polynomial approximation problem from Definition
3.1
:

\inf\{\|1+Pf\|_{p}:P\in\mathscr{P}_{N}\}.

(4.8)

By the nearest point property for uniformly convex spaces, there is a unique
\Phi_{N}\in\ell^{p}_{A}
for which
\Phi_{N}-1\in f\mathscr{P}_{N}
and the infimum in (
4.8
) is attained:

\|\Phi_{N}\|_{p}=\inf\{\|1+Pf\|_{p}:P\in\mathscr{P}_{N}\}.

(4.9)

An argument similar to the one in
[
9
, Ch. 8]
shows that

\Phi_{N}=1-\frac{J_{N}}{1+(\|J_{N}\|_{p}^{p}-1)^{p^{\prime}-1}}.

Write
\Phi_{N}=1-P_{N}f
, thereby defining the OPA
P_{N}=p_{N,f}
. The preceding argument justifies the following result:

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
1<p<\infty
. Then

p_{N,f}=\frac{J_{N}/f}{1+(\|J_{N}\|_{p}^{p}-1)^{p^{\prime}-1}}.

This extends Theorem 2.4 in
[
3
]
to the context of
\ell^{p}_{A}
spaces.
Notice that
J_{N}/f
is a polynomial and
p_{n,f}
has the same roots (counting multiplicities, of course). Any extra zeros (i.e., those not of
f
) of
J_{N}
will also be extra zeros of
p_{N,f}
.

Of special importance here is the case where
N=1
. Here, for
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
with
f(0)=1
, we have

I_{1}=\inf\{\|g\|_{p}:g(0)=1,g\in f\mathscr{P}_{1}\}=\inf\{\|(1-tz)f(z)\|_{p}:t\in\mathbb{C}\}.

From the definition of
J_{1}
as the unique solution to the problem, we see that

J_{1}(z)=(1-t_{f}z)f(z).

5.
Regions excluded from
\Omega_{p}

At this point, the skeptical reader might wonder about the possibility that
\Omega_{p}=\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
. The purpose of this section is to determine radii
r_{p}\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)
such that
\Omega_{p}\cap r_{p}\mathbb{D}=\varnothing
. This will be accomplished by using Birkhoff-James orthogonality in
\ell^{p}_{A}
along with the Pythagorean inequalities.

Two important tools in our analysis are the forward and backward shift operators defined on
\ell^{p}_{A}
by

(Sf)(z):=zf(z)\quad\mbox{and}\quad(Bf)(z):=\frac{f(z)-f(0)}{z}.

(5.1)

These operators are easily checked to be bounded on
\ell^{p}_{A}
with

\|B\|=\|S\|=1
. Furthermore,
S
is an isometry on
\ell^{p}_{A}
.
Related to these operators is the difference quotient operator
[
9
, p. 91]
.

Lemma 5.2
.

If
w\in\mathbb{D}
the difference quotient operator

(Q_{w}f)(z):=\frac{f(z)-f(w)}{z-w}

is bounded on
\ell^{p}_{A}
with norm
\|Q_{w}\|
which satisfies

\|Q_{w}\|\leqslant\frac{1}{1-|w|}.

Another important tool for us is the following extension of the Pythagorean theorem to
\ell^{p}_{A}
spaces, which takes the form of a family of inequalities
[
9
, p. 58]
.

Lemma 5.3
.

(a)

For
2\leqslant p<\infty
,

f\perp_{p}g\implies\|f\|_{p}^{p}+\frac{1}{2^{p-1}-1}\|g\|_{p}^{p}\leqslant\|f+g\|_{p}^{p}.

(b)

For
1<p\leqslant 2
,

f\perp_{p}g\implies\|f\|_{p}^{2}+(p-1)\|g\|_{p}^{2}\leqslant\|f+g\|_{p}^{2}.

The following proposition says that certain disks centered at the origin are excluded from
\Omega_{p}
.

Proposition 5.4
.

(a)

For
p\geqslant 2
,

\Omega_{p}\cap\frac{1}{s}\overline{\mathbb{D}}=\varnothing,
whenever

(s-1)^{p}+\frac{s^{p}}{2^{p-1}-1}\geqslant 1.

(5.5)

(b)

For
1<p<2
,

\Omega_{p}\cap\frac{1}{s}\overline{\mathbb{D}}=\varnothing,

whenever

s\geqslant(2/p)^{1/p}.

(5.6)

Proof.

Firstly, assume that
p\geqslant 2
. By Lemma
3.7
write

f(z)=(1-tz)f(z)+tzf(z)
and note that the two summands are Birkhoff-James orthogonal.

Consequently, by Lemma
5.3
,

\|f\|^{p}_{p}\geqslant\|(1-tz)f(z)\|^{p}_{p}+\frac{1}{2^{p-1}-1}\|tzf(z)\|^{p}_{p},

(5.7)

Next,

\displaystyle f(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-tz)f(z)}{1-tz}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-tz)f(z)-(1-t/t)f(1/t)}{(-t)(z-1/t)}

\displaystyle=(-1/t)Q_{1/t}([1-tz]f(z)),

(5.8)

where
Q_{1/t}
is the difference quotient operator at the point
1/t
from Lemma
5.2
. Notice we needed
1/t\in\mathbb{D}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\|f\|^{p}_{p}

\displaystyle\geqslant\|(1-tz)f(z)\|^{p}_{p}+\frac{1}{2^{p-1}-1}\|tzf(z)\|^{p}_{p}

(by (
5.7
))

\displaystyle\geqslant\|(1-tz)f(z)\|^{p}_{p}+\frac{|t|^{p}}{2^{p-1}-1}\|f(z)\|^{p}_{p}

(
S
is an isometry)

This implies that

\Big(1-\frac{|t|^{p}}{2^{p-1}-1}\cdot 1\Big)\|f\|^{p}_{p}\geqslant\|(1-tz)f(z)\|^{p}_{p}.

Now use (
5.8
) to get

\Big(1-\frac{|t|^{p}}{2^{p-1}-1}\Big)\|(-1/t)Q_{1/t}([1-tz]f(z))\|^{p}_{p}\geqslant\|(1-tz)f(z)\|^{p}_{p}

from which

\Big(1-\frac{|t|^{p}}{2^{p-1}-1}\Big)(1/|t|^{p})\|Q_{1/t}\|^{p}\geqslant 1.

Lemma
5.2
yields

\Big(1-\frac{|t|^{p}}{2^{p-1}-1}\Big)\frac{(1/|t|^{p})}{(1-|1/t|)^{p}}\geqslant 1.

This yields the bound in (
5.5
),
which proves (a).

A similar calculation, but using the second Pythagorean inequality in Lemma
5.3
, yields (b).
∎

Since both inequalities in the previous proposition are met when
s=2
, we have the following:

Corollary 5.9
.

\Omega_{p}\cap\frac{1}{2}\overline{\mathbb{D}}=\varnothing
for any
1<p<\infty
.

6.
Zeros inside the disk

Proposition
3.12
says that
\Omega_{2}\cap\overline{\mathbb{D}}=\varnothing
. Through some specific examples, this section will show that
\Omega_{p}\cap\mathbb{D}\neq\varnothing
when
p\not=2
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
1<p<2
. If

f_{k}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(j+1)z^{j},

then for large enough
k
, the zero of
p_{1,f_{k}}
lies in
\mathbb{D}
.

Proof.

From Lemma
3.7
, we have that

1-p_{1,f_{k}}f_{k}(z)\perp_{p}zf_{k}(z).

Since
p_{1,f}(z)=c(1-t_{f}z)
unique, it must have real coefficients (otherwise, since the coefficients of
f_{k}
are real,
\overline{c}(1-\overline{t_{f}}z)
would give another optimal approximant of the same degree). It follows from Lemma
2.5
that a sufficient condition for
t=t_{f}
is for
t_{f}
to be a zero of

g(t):=\sum_{j=1}^{k}((j+1)-tj)^{\langle p-1\rangle}j+(-t(k+1))^{\langle p-1\rangle}(k+1).

(6.2)

Then we have

g(2)=-\sum_{j=1}^{k}(j-1)^{\langle p-1\rangle}j-2(k+1)^{p}<0.

It remains to check that
g(1)>0
. Notice that

g(1)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}j-(k+1)^{p}.

Now use the fact that
1<p<2
to see that

\sum_{j=1}^{k}j=\frac{k(k-1)}{2}\gg(k+1)^{p}

for large enough
k
. Thus, for large enough
k
,
g(1)>0
.
∎

Let us now deal with the remaining case
p>2
.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
p>2
. If

f_{k}(z)=1+\sum_{j=1}^{2k}\left(2-\frac{j-1}{k}\right)z^{j},

then for large enough
k
, the zero of
p_{1,f_{k}}
lies in
\mathbb{D}
.

Proof.

Once more, denote by
p_{1,f}(z)=c(1-t_{f}z)
the optimal linear approximant for
f_{k}
. As in the proof of the previous proposition,
c,t_{f}\in\mathbb{R}
and

1-p_{1,f}f_{k}(z)\perp_{p}zf_{k}(z).

From Lemma
3.7
, this translates into the condition on
t_{f}
that

h(s):=\|(1-sz)f_{k}(z)\|^{p}_{p}

must be minimized at
s=t_{f}
.
Since
h
is a differentiable function of
s
for all
p>2
, we must have
h^{\prime}(t_{f})=0
and this condition will determine the value of
t_{f}
. By convexity, we must have that the limit as
s\rightarrow\infty
of
h^{\prime}(s)
is positive, and thus, for
t_{f}
to be larger than
1
it is enough to show that

h^{\prime}(1)<0.

(6.4)

Let
a_{j}
be the Taylor coefficient of order
j
of
f_{k}
. Then

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{ds}\Big\{|a_{0}|^{p}+\sum_{j=0}^{2k}|a_{j+1}-sa_{j}|^{p}\Big\}\Big|_{s=1}

\displaystyle=p\sum_{j=0}^{2k}a_{j}\left(a_{j}-a_{j+1}\right)^{p-1}.

(6.5)

With the understanding that
a_{2k+1}=0
, we have

(a_{j}-a_{j+1})^{p-1}=\begin{cases}-1&\mbox{if }j=0\\
k^{1-p}&\mbox{if }1\leqslant j\leqslant 2k\end{cases}.

At the same time
|a_{j}|\in[0,2]
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
. This means that the right-hand side of (
6.5
) is bounded above by

p(-1+Ck^{2-p}),
where
C>0
is independent of
k
. For large enough
k
this is negative and thus the condition in (
6.4
) is met.
∎

Corollary 6.6
.

\Omega_{p}\cap\mathbb{D}\not=\varnothing
for
1<p<\infty
and
p\not=2
.

In other words, there are extra zeros inside the disk
\mathbb{D}
when
p\neq 2
.

7.
A method for finding the optimal radius

So far we have shown that for any
1<p<\infty
,
\Omega_{p}
is the complement of a certain disk centered at the origin. Moreover, when
p\not=2
, this disk as radius less than
1
. Our objective in this section is to develop tools for finding the radius of this disk. The rotational symmetry of
\Omega_{p}
enables us to focus on the zeros of optimal linear approximants on
\mathbb{R}
, and in fact, on
[\frac{1}{2},1]
.

7.1.
A smaller problem

A nice reduction of the problem is that we can restrict to studying the case that
f
is a polynomial, since polynomials are dense in
\ell^{p}_{A}
(from the definition of
\ell^{p}_{A}
) and linear approximants are well behaved under limits. Recall that for
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
,
t_{f}
is the unique complex number that satisfies

\|(1-t_{f}z)f(z)\|_{p}=\min_{t\in\mathbb{C}}\|(1-tz)f(z)\|_{p}.

Proposition 7.1
.

If
f_{n}\to f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
, then
t_{f_{n}}\to t_{f}
.

Proof.

Since
(f_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a convergent sequence,

M=\sup_{n\geqslant 1}\|f_{n}\|_{p}<\infty.

By Proposition
5.4
,

\sup_{n\geqslant 1}|t_{f_{n}}|=K<\infty.

By the latter bound, there is a subsequence
(t_{f_{n_{k}}})_{k=1}^{\infty}
which converges to some
t
. Then for any
0<|w|<1
,

\displaystyle\|(1-tz)f(z)\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|(1-t_{f_{n_{k}}}z)f_{n_{k}}(z)\|_{p}+|t-t_{f_{n_{k}}}|\|f_{n_{k}}\|_{p}

\displaystyle\qquad+(1+|t|)\|f-f_{n_{k}}\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|(1-z/w)f_{n_{k}}(z)\|_{p}+|t-t_{f_{n_{k}}}|M

\displaystyle\qquad+(1+|t|)\|f-f_{n_{k}}\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|(1-z/w)f(z)\|_{p}+|t-t_{f_{n_{k}}}|M

\displaystyle\qquad+(2+|t|+1/|w|)\|f-f_{n_{k}}\|_{p},

where we have used Young’s inequality, and the optimality assumption on
t_{f_{n_{k}}}
. Now let
k\to\infty
to see that

\|(1-tz)f(z)\|_{p}\leqslant\|(1-z/w)f(z)\|_{p}.

That is,
(1-tz)f(z)
is optimal. Such
t
must be unique, since it arises from estimating a vector by a member of a subspace in a uniformly convex space. Thus, any subsequence of
(t_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
must have a further subsequence that converges to this same
t
. Thus,
t_{n}\to t
and
t=t_{f}
.
∎

The previous problem allows us to concentrate on the problem of finding, for fixed
1<p<\infty
,

T_{d,p}:=\sup_{f\in\mathscr{P}_{d}}|t_{f}|\quad\mbox{and}\quad\tau_{p}:=\sup_{d\in\mathbb{N}}T_{d,p}.

Note that
\tau_{p}
is what we need in Theorem
1.2
.

Is
T_{d,p}
attained for some
f\in\mathscr{P}_{d}
? We argue that it is. Let
(f_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence in
\mathscr{P}_{d}
such that

t_{f_{k}}\longrightarrow T_{d,p}
. Since
t_{f}
does not change when
f
is scaled by a non-zero constant, we may assume that all of the Taylor coefficients of the
f_{k}
are uniformly bounded. Thus, there is a subsequence
(f_{n_{k}})_{k=1}^{\infty}
for which all of the separate coefficient sequences converge. By relabeling, we may assume that the subsequence is
(f_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
. We have shown that
f_{n}\longrightarrow f^{*}
in
\ell^{p}_{A}
, for some polynomial
f^{*}\in\mathscr{P}_{d}
, and
t_{f^{*}}=T_{d,p}
.

This also shows that
1/T_{d,p}\in\Omega_{p}
for all
d
.

To analyze this extremal problem further, we once again consider for fixed
1<p<\infty
and
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
the function

h(t)=h_{p,f}(t):=\|(1-tz)f(z)\|_{p}^{p}.

Thus
h
has a unique minimum at
t_{f}=1/z_{0}
, where
z_{0}
is the zero of
p_{1,f}
.

This next result says in our search for the extremal functions
f
for which maximal values of
|t|
are attained, we can assume that the coefficients of
f
are in
\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

Proposition 7.2
.

If
p\neq 2
and
f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}z^{k}\in\ell^{p}_{A}
, then
|t_{f}|\leqslant|t_{g}|
, where

g(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|a_{k}|z^{k}.

(7.3)

In particular, for
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
d>2
, there is an
f^{*}\in\mathscr{P}_{d}
whose coefficients are nonnegative and such that
T_{d,p}=t_{f^{*}}
.

Proof.

By replacing
f(z)
with
f(e^{i\gamma}z)
, for a suitable value of
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, we may assume that
t_{f}>0
. Let

h(t):=\|(1-tz)f(z)\|_{p}^{p}=|a_{0}|^{p}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|a_{k}-ta_{k-1}|^{p},

where
t
is a real variable.
Then

\displaystyle-h^{\prime}(t)/p

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\big(|a_{k}|^{2}-2t\Re(\bar{a}_{k}a_{k-1})+t^{2}|a_{k-1}|^{2}\big)^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\big(\Re(\bar{a}_{k}a_{k-1})-t|a_{k-1}|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(a_{k}-ta_{k-1})^{\langle p-1\rangle}a_{k-1}.

To check that convergence is not an issue, we apply Hölder’s inequality with the effect

\displaystyle|h^{\prime}(t)|

\displaystyle\leqslant p\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|a_{k}-ta_{k-1}|^{p-1}|a_{k-1}|

\displaystyle\leqslant p\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|a_{k}-ta_{k-1}|^{q(p-1)}\Big)^{1/q}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|a_{k-1}|^{p}\Big)^{1/p}

\displaystyle<\infty,

since
q(p-1)=p
.

The function
\phi(t)=|t|^{p}
is convex in
t
for any
p>1
, and so are its translates and sums, as well as limits of such functions.
Hence
h(t)
is convex, and consequently its derivative
h^{\prime}(t)
is monotone nondecreasing in
t
.

Setting that aside for the moment, suppose that
H
is a real function on
\mathbb{R}
such that
H^{\prime}
exists and is monotone nondecreasing, and that the expression

H(t)+|at-b|^{p}

attains its minimum value when
t=T
. Assume further that
a>0
and
c<b
.

By elementary calculus,

H^{\prime}(T)+a|aT-b|^{p-2}(aT-b)=0.

There is a value of
t
, call it
t^{*}
, such that

H^{\prime}(t^{*})+a|at^{*}-c|^{p-2}(at^{*}-c)=0,

where
b
has been replaced by
c
.
Since
c<b
we have
we have

at-c>at-b

and thus

a|at-c|^{p-2}(at-c)>a|at-b|^{p-2}(at-b)

(the function
\psi(t)=t^{\langle p-1\rangle}
is monotone increasing when
p>1
).

We see that
H^{\prime}(t)+a|at-c|^{p-2}(at-c)=0
for some
t
satisfying
H^{\prime}(t)<H^{\prime}(T)
. By the assumption that
H^{\prime}(t)
is nondecreasing, this means that
t^{*}<T
. In other words, the minimum is achieved at a lower value of
t
.

Now apply the above observation to the function

H(t)=|a_{0}|^{p}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(|a_{k}|-t|a_{k-1}|)^{p},

where we are identifying
a=|a_{0}|
,
b=|a_{1}|
, and
c=|a_{1}|\cos\theta
. Notice that
H(t)
is just
h_{p,g}(t)
, where
g
is given by (
7.3
).

The conclusion is that
H(t)
is critical at a higher value of
t
than the function

|a_{0}|^{p}+(|a_{1}|\cos\theta-t|a_{0}|)^{p}+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}(|a_{k}|-t|a_{k-1}|)^{p}

for any choice of
\theta
. Repeat this argument successively for each term in
H(t)
. That is, replace
|a_{k}|-t|a_{k-1}|
with
|a_{k}|\cos\theta_{k}-t|a_{k-1}|
in the
k
th instance. The conclusion is
H(t)
is critical at a greater value of
t
when
\theta_{k}
is chosen to be zero.
If we choose the values of
\theta_{k}
along each step so that the final function is
h_{p,f}(t)
, we find that
f
can be the function that optimizes
t
only if its coefficients are nonnegative.

In particular, if we restrict our attention to the case
f
is a polynomial of degree up to
d
, then
we have already seen that
T_{d,p}
is attained. The above argument applies, showing that we may assume that the extremal
f^{*}
has nonnegative coefficients.
∎

7.2.
Lagrange multipliers and recurrence relations

For each
1<p<\infty
, and each
d\in\mathbb{N}
, we have seen that there is a polynomial
f\in\mathscr{P}_{d}
for which the optimal value
T_{d,p}
is attained. Furthermore, we reduced the search for
f
to polynomials with nonnegative coefficients.
This enables us to use the Lagrange multiplier method to solve for the optimizing polynomial
f
, and compute the value of
T_{d,p}
.

Given

f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}a_{k}z^{k},

let
t_{f}=t(a_{0},...,a_{d})
be that value of
t
that minimizes

\|(1-tz)f(z)\|_{p}.

We want to find

T_{d,p}:=\max_{a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}t(a_{0},...,a_{d}).

By rescaling
f
, which does not change the zeros of the optimal linear approximant of
f
, we may assume
a_{0}=1
. We will apply the Lagrange multiplier method to this positive real finite-dimensional optimization problem.

The Lagrange multiplier analysis yields the following recurrence relations for
t
and the coefficients of
f
, a function for which the optimal
t
is attained.

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
1<p<\infty
, with
p\neq 2
, and let
d\in\mathbb{N}
with
d>2
. Then:

(a)

T_{d,p}
is attained for some

f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}a_{k}z^{k}\in\mathscr{P}_{d}

whose coefficients satisfy the recurrence relation

(pa_{k}t-a_{k+1})|a_{k+1}-ta_{k}|^{p-2}=(p-1)a_{k-1}|a_{k}-ta_{k-1}|^{p-2},

(7.5)

where
t=T_{d,p}
and we understand that
a_{-1}=a_{d+1}=0
.

(b)

For that choice of
f
,
T_{d,p}
is the unique solution to the equation

h^{\prime}(T_{d,p})=0,

(7.6)

where
h(s)=\|(1-sz)f(z)\|_{p}^{p}
.

Proof.

Since the coefficients of
f
are assumed to be real and nonnegative (Proposition
7.2
), we know that
h^{\prime}(t)=0
when the linear approximant for
f
is of the form
c(1-tz),
where
c>0
and
t>0
.
The problem of determining
T_{d,p}
is equivalent to maximizing
t
subject to the restrictions that
h^{\prime}(t)=0
, for
t,a_{0},...,a_{d}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. We apply the Lagrange multiplier method to this maximization problem and we obtain

\nabla(t)-\lambda\nabla(h^{\prime}(t))=0,

(7.7)

where the gradient
\nabla
is taken with respect to the
(d+2)
-tuple of variables
(t,a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})
.

The first term in the left hand side of (
7.7
),
\nabla(t)
, is just the constant vector
(1,0,...,0)
. The first scalar equation in the system (
7.7
) will merely determine the value of
\lambda
, which will not be of any use. The remaining scalar equations arising from (
7.7
) are of the form

\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{j}}h^{\prime}(t)=0

where
j=0,...,d
.
The result comes from calculating

\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{j}}\frac{h^{\prime}(t)}{p}=|ta_{j}-a_{j+1}|^{p-2}(pta_{j}-a_{j+1})-a_{j-1}(p-1)|ta_{j-1}-a_{j}|^{p-2}

and setting this equal to zero.
∎

Let us abbreviate by
\mathscr{L}_{d}
the set of recurrence relations (
7.5
).

The recurrence relations in Theorem
7.4
include the requirement

a_{1}=ta_{0}\ \ \mbox{or}\ \ a_{1}=pta_{0}.

(7.8)

For the moment assume that the first of the choices is made in (
7.8
). Consider the polynomial
F(z):=b+zf(z)
, which has coefficient sequence
b,a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{d}
. If we insist that

a_{0}=tb,

then
F
satisfies the recurrence relations for
k=0
, since we are essentially adding the term
0=p(a_{0}-tb)^{p-1}b
to the right side.
Similarly, by definition,
F
satisfies condition (
7.8
), in the form
a_{0}=tb
.
Lastly, the recurrence relations remain true for the indices
k
,
2\leqslant k\leqslant d+1
. Thus
F
also has an optimal linear approximant having a root at
1/t
(
F
is not necessarily extremal since the degree of
F
is
d+1
).
Applying this observation repeatedly yields the following result.

Proposition 7.9
.

Let
1<p<\infty
, with
p\neq 2
, and let
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose that the real polynomial
f
of degree
d
satisfies the conditions (
7.5
) for some value of
t
. Then so does the polynomial

a_{0}+a_{0}tz+a_{0}t^{2}z^{2}+\cdots+a_{0}t^{m-1}z^{m-1}+t^{m}z^{m}f(z).

If
a_{0}=0
the linear approximant would be identically zero. Thus, without loss of generality we could assume
a_{0}=1
.
In other words, in order for a
(d+2)
-tuple
(t,a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})
to solve the optimization problem as described in Theorem
7.4
, it must satisfy the recurrence relations

\displaystyle a_{0}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=t\mbox{\ or\ }pt

\displaystyle(pa_{k}t-a_{k+1})|a_{k+1}-ta_{k}|^{p-2}

\displaystyle=(p-1)a_{k-1}|a_{k}-ta_{k-1}|^{p-2},\ 1\leqslant k<d

\displaystyle pa_{d}t|-ta_{d}|^{p-2}

\displaystyle=(p-1)a_{d-1}|a_{d}-ta_{d-1}|^{p-2}

as well as the constraint
h^{\prime}(t)=0
, where

h(t)=h_{f,p}(t):=\|(1-tz)f(z)\|_{p}^{p}

and
f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}a_{k}z^{k}
.

Proposition 7.10
.

Let
1<p<\infty
, with
p\neq 2
, and
d\in\mathbb{N}
with
d>2
. If
f\in\mathscr{P}_{d}
satisfies
t_{f}=T_{d,p}
, then
a_{d}\neq 0
.

Proof.

If
a_{d}=0
, then by working backwards from the recurrence relations, we find that
a_{d-1}=0
,
a_{d-2}=0
, etc., concluding with
a_{1}=0
, a contradiction.
∎

From this we may obtain another important property of the optimal
t
. From the definition of
T_{d,p}
we clearly have
T_{d+1,p}\geqslant T_{d,p}
. This next proposition gives us something stronger.

Proposition 7.11
.

For
1<p<\infty
, with
p\neq 2
, the quantity
T_{d,p}
is strictly increasing in
d
.

Proof.

There is a
(d+1)
-tuple
(T_{d-1,p},a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{d-1})
that satisfies
\mathcal{L}_{d-1}
. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that
T_{d-1,p}=T_{d,p}
. Then the
(d+2)
-tuple
(T_{d-1,p},a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{d-1},0)

must satisfy
\mathcal{L}_{d}
, in violation of Proposition
7.10
.
∎

From this last result, together with Proposition
7.9
, it turns out that for the polynomial
f
to correspond to an optimal
T_{d,p}
, we need to make the selection
a_{1}=a_{0}pt
. We can exploit this and it leads to further reductions.

Proposition 7.12
.

Let
1<p<\infty
, with
p\neq 2
and let
d\in\mathbb{N}
with
d>2
. To find
T_{d,p}
by means of the recurrence relations
\mathscr{L}_{d}
, it suffices to consider polynomials

f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}a_{k}z^{k}\in\mathscr{P}_{d}

such that
a_{0}=1
,
a_{1}=pt
, and all of the coefficients
a_{2},a_{3},\ldots,a_{d}
are positive.

Proof.

Suppose that the
(d+2)
-tuple
(t,a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})
solves
\mathcal{L}_{d}
. We have already shown that the assumption
a_{0}=1
is no loss of generality, and that the selection of
a_{1}=pta_{0}
is necessary for optimality.

Next, suppose that
a_{k}=0
for some
k
,
2\leqslant k<d
. By inspection of the resulting recurrence relation

pa_{k-1}t|-ta_{k-1}|^{p-2}=(p-1)a_{k-2}|a_{k-1}-ta_{k-2}|^{p-2},

we find that the
(k+1)
-tuple
(T_{d,p},a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1})
solves the recurrence relations of
\mathcal{L}_{k-1}
. Furthermore, the constraint equation
h^{\prime}(t)=0
holds for the polynomial
a_{0}+a_{1}z+\cdots+a_{k-1}z^{k-1}
, since the assumption
a_{1}=pt
was made. Therefore

(T_{d,p},a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1})
solves all the requirements of
\mathcal{L}_{k-1}
. This contradicts Proposition
7.11
.
∎

There is yet a further reduction in the scope of the problem. Recall that solving the Lagrange multiplier problem entails satisfying all of the recurrence relations
\mathscr{L}_{d}
, along with the constraint equation
h^{\prime}(t)=0
, where we recall that

h^{\prime}(t)=-\sum_{k=1}^{d}p(a_{k}-ta_{k-1})^{\langle p-1\rangle}a_{k-1}+pt^{\langle p-1\rangle}|a_{d}|^{p}.

(7.13)

It turns out that
the condition
h^{\prime}(t)=0
in Theorem
7.4
is automatic if
a_{1}=pt
(which, by the previous Proposition, we may assume).

Proposition 7.14
.

Let
1<p<\infty
, with
p\neq 2
and let
d\in\mathbb{N}
with
d>2
. Suppose
f(z)=a_{0}+a_{1}z+a_{2}z^{2}+\cdots+a_{d}z^{d}
satisfies the equations

\displaystyle a_{0}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=pt

\displaystyle(pa_{k}t-a_{k+1})|a_{k+1}-ta_{k}|^{p-2}

\displaystyle=(p-1)a_{k-1}|a_{k}-ta_{k-1}|^{p-2},\ 1\leqslant k<d

(7.15)

\displaystyle pa_{d}t|-ta_{d}|^{p-2}

\displaystyle=(p-1)a_{d-1}|a_{d}-ta_{d-1}|^{p-2}.

(7.16)

Then
h^{\prime}(t)=0
, where
h(t):=\|(1-tz)f(z)\|_{p}^{p}
.

Proof.

We begin with the first three terms of the formula for
h^{\prime}(t)
from (
7.13
). Use substitution and simplification to get

\displaystyle(p^{2}t^{2}-a_{2})|a_{2}-pt^{2}|^{p-2}

\displaystyle=(p-1)^{p-1}t^{p-2}

(7.17)

\displaystyle(pa_{2}t-a_{3})|a_{3}-ta_{2}|^{p-2}

\displaystyle=(p-1)pt|a_{2}-pt^{2}|^{p-2}

(7.18)

\displaystyle pa_{3}^{\langle p-1\rangle}t^{p-1}

\displaystyle=(p-1)a_{2}|a_{3}-ta_{2}|^{p-2}

(7.19)

recalling that
t>0
.

Straightforward calculation yields

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{k=1}^{3}(a_{k}-ta_{k-1})^{\langle p-1\rangle}a_{k-1}

(7.20)

\displaystyle=-(p-1)^{p-1}t^{p-1}-(a_{2}-pt^{2})^{\langle p-1\rangle}pt-(a_{3}-ta_{2})^{\langle p-1\rangle}a_{2}

\displaystyle=-(p-1)^{p-1}t^{p-1}-|a_{2}-pt^{2}|^{p-2}(a_{2}-pt^{2})pt

\displaystyle\qquad-|a_{3}-ta_{2}|^{p-2}(a_{3}-ta_{2})a_{2}.

(7.21)

Use (
7.17
) to rewrite the first term in (
7.21
). The result is

\displaystyle=-t(p^{2}t^{2}-a_{2})|a_{2}-pt^{2}|^{p-2}-(a_{2}-pt^{2})^{\langle p-1\rangle}pt

\displaystyle\quad-(a_{3}-ta_{2})^{\langle p-1\rangle}a_{2}

\displaystyle=-(p-1)a_{2}t|a_{2}-pt^{2}|^{p-2}-(a_{3}-ta_{2})^{\langle p-1\rangle}a_{2}.

(7.22)

Next, use (
7.18
) to rewrite the first term of (
7.22
) with the result

\displaystyle=-(p-1)a_{2}t\cdot\frac{1}{(p-1)pt}(pa_{2}t-a_{3})|a_{3}-ta_{2}|^{p-2}-(a_{3}-ta_{2})^{\langle p-1\rangle}a_{2}

\displaystyle=[-(1/p)(pa_{2}t-a_{3})]|a_{3}-ta_{2}|^{p-2}a_{2}-(a_{3}-ta_{2})^{\langle p-1\rangle}a_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{p-1}{p}a_{2}a_{3}|a_{3}-ta_{2}|^{p-2}.

We have just established that

-\sum_{k=1}^{3}(a_{k}-ta_{k-1})^{\langle p-1\rangle}a_{k-1}=-\frac{p-1}{p}a_{2}a_{3}|a_{3}-ta_{2}|^{p-2}.

By (
7.15
) with
k=3
, we have

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{3}(a_{k}-ta_{k-1})^{\langle p-1\rangle}a_{k-1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{p-1}{p}a_{2}a_{3}|a_{3}-ta_{2}|^{p-2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{p}a_{3}(pa_{3}t-a_{4})|a_{4}-ta_{3}|^{p-2}

\displaystyle=(-a_{3}^{2}t+a_{3}a_{4}/p)|a_{4}-ta_{3}|^{p-2}.

From this it follows that

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{4}(a_{k}-ta_{k-1})^{\langle p-1\rangle}a_{k-1}

\displaystyle=(-a_{3}^{2}t+a_{3}a_{4}/p)(a_{4}-ta_{3})^{p-2}-(a_{4}-ta_{3})^{\langle p-1\rangle}a_{3}

\displaystyle=\big[-a_{3}^{2}t+a_{3}a_{4}/p-a_{3}a_{4}+a_{3}^{2}t\big]|a_{4}-ta_{3}|^{p-2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{p-1}{p}a_{3}a_{4}|a_{4}-ta_{3}|^{p-2}.

Repeating this argument leads to

-\sum_{k=1}^{d}(a_{k}-ta_{k-1})^{\langle p-1\rangle}a_{k-1}=-\frac{p-1}{p}a_{d-1}a_{d}|a_{d}-ta_{d-1}|^{p-2}.

Finally, from (
7.16
) we find that the last term of
h^{\prime}(t)
is

t^{p-1}|a_{d}|^{p}=\frac{p-1}{p}a_{d-1}a_{d}|a_{d}-ta_{d-1}|^{p-2}.

In conclusion,
h^{\prime}(t)=0
.
∎

This shows that if we make the selection
a_{1}=a_{0}pt
(rather than the other choice,
a_{1}=a_{0}t
), then the recurrence relations already imply that
h^{\prime}(t)=0
.

A dynamical systems approach

By definition,
z_{0}\in\Omega_{p}
if the optimal polynomial for some non-constant
f\in\ell^{p}_{A}
vanishes at
z_{0}
. The results of the previous sections have shown that
\tau_{p}\in(1,2)
and either

\Omega_{p}=\mathbb{C}\backslash\textstyle{\frac{1}{\tau_{p}}}\mathbb{D},\quad\text{or}\quad\Omega_{p}=\mathbb{C}\backslash\textstyle{\frac{1}{\tau_{p}}}\overline{\mathbb{D}},

where

\tau_{p}=\lim_{d\to\infty}T_{d,p}.

Recall that the limit above exists since
T_{d}
is bounded and strictly increasing. As mentioned, when
p=2
,
\Omega_{2}=\mathbb{C}\backslash\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. We know from previous sections that when
p\neq 2
,
1<\tau_{p}<2
. In the present section, we will calculate
\tau_{p}
by means of a dynamical system derived from the recurrence relations of Theorem
7.4
. The qualitative nature of the dynamical system depends on whether
1<p<2
or
2<p<\infty
. Details will be provided for the latter case, and the former can be handled in an analogous way.

Fix
p>2
. We transform the recurrence relations (
7.5
) for the coefficients
\{a_{k}\}
into recurrence relations for the ratios

R_{k}=\frac{a_{k}}{a_{k-1}},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

These take the form

(pt-R_{k+1})|R_{k+1}-t|^{p-2}=(p-1)\frac{1}{R_{k}}\Big|1-\frac{t}{R_{k}}\Big|^{p-2},

(8.1)

1\leqslant k<d
.
The equation (
8.1
) can equivalently be expressed as

\Phi(R_{k+1})=\Psi(R_{k}),\quad 1\leqslant k<d,

(8.2)

where

\Phi(x):=(pt-x)|x-t|^{p-2}

and

\Psi(x):=(p-1)\frac{1}{x}\Big|1-\frac{t}{x}\Big|^{p-2}.

See Figure
1
.
Let us
stress that
\Phi
and
\Psi
depend on the values of
p
and
t
.
If we are able to find
R_{1}
,
R_{2}
,…,
R_{d}
and
t
satisfying (
8.2
), then by taking
a_{0}=1
we obtain a solution
a_{0}
,
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
,…,
a_{d}
and
t
for (
7.5
).

Our plan is to implement the relation (
8.2
) graphically, along with the requirements that

R_{1}=pt\quad\mbox{and}\quad R_{d+1}=0.

This gives rise to an implicit dynamical system with two parameters,
p
and
t
. By analyzing this dynamical system we will be able to identify solutions to the recurrence relations (
8.1
) for the ratios
R_{k}
, and discern which solutions correspond to optimal values of
t
.

The following lemmas concerning the structure of
\Phi
and
\Psi
are easy to verify from basic calculus.

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
p>2
and
t\geqslant 1
.
Then

(a)

\Phi
is decreasing on the intervals
(-\infty,t]
and
[(p-1)t,\infty)
and increasing on the interval
[t,(p-1)t]
;

(b)

\Phi
intercepts the axes at the points
(0,pt^{p-1})
,
(t,0)
and
(pt,0)
;

(c)

\Phi
attains a local minimum at the point
(t,0)
and a local maximum at the point
((p-1)t,(p-2)^{p-2}t^{p-1})
.

Lemma 8.4
.

Let
p>2
and
t\geqslant 1
.
Then

(a)

\Psi(x)\geqslant 0
if and only if
x>0
, with equality precisely when
x=t
;

(b)

\Psi
is decreasing on the intervals
(-\infty,0)
,
(0,t)
and
[(p-1)t,\infty)
, and increasing on the interval
[t,(p-1)t]
;

(c)

\Psi
has a vertical asymptote at
x=0
, with
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0-}\Psi(x)=-\infty
and
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0+}\Psi(x)=+\infty
, and a horizontal asymptote at
y=0
, with
\lim_{x\rightarrow\pm\infty}\Psi(x)=0
;

(d)

\Psi
has an
x
-intercept at the point
(t,0)
;

(e)

\Psi
attains a local minimum at the point
(t,0)
, and a local maximum at
\Big((p-1)t,\frac{1}{t}\big(\frac{p-2}{p-1}\big)^{p-2}\Big)
.

In addition, the graphs of the functions
\Phi
and
\Psi
interact in the following ways. Figure

captures some of these effects.

Lemma 8.5
.

Let
p>2
and
t\geqslant 1
.
Then

(a)

If
t>1
, there exists a neighborhood of
t
in which
\Phi(x)\geqslant\Psi(x)
, with equality precisely at
x=t
;

(b)

If
t=1
, then there exists a neighborhood of
t
in which
\Phi(x)>\Psi(x)
when
x>t
, and
\Phi(x)<\Psi(x)
when
x<t
;

(c)

If
t>1
, then there are exactly three values of
x
for which
\Phi(x)=\Psi(x)
, including
x=t
, with
x=t
lying between the other two such points (we will call these “fixed points”);

(d)

If
t=1
, then there are exactly two values of
x
for which
\Phi(x)=\Psi(x)
, including
x=t=1
, with the other such point being greater;

(e)

As the parameter
t
increases,
\Psi(pt)
decreases, and
\Phi(\xi_{1})
increases, where
\xi_{1}
is the least of the fixed points.

Proof.

Assertions (a) and (b) follow from substituting
x=t+\epsilon
into
\Phi
and
\Psi
, and finding that for small
\epsilon
, we have

\displaystyle\Phi(t+\epsilon)

\displaystyle\approx(p-1)t\epsilon^{p-2}

\displaystyle\Psi(t+\epsilon)

\displaystyle\approx(p-1)\frac{\epsilon^{p-2}}{t^{p-1}}.

In order to establish (c), based on (a) and the behavior of
\Phi
and
\Psi
near
x=0
, it is elementary that there are at least one fixed point in
(0,t)
, one at
t
and one between
t
and
pt
. If
x\neq t
, the fixed point condition can be simplified from

(pt-x)|x-t|^{p-2}=(p-1)\frac{1}{x}\Big|1-\frac{t}{x}\Big|^{p-2}

to the much simpler

g(x):=x^{p}-ptx^{p-1}+(p-1)=0.

Since the function
g
has only one change of direction for
x>0
, there cannot be more than 2 fixed points apart from the one at
x=t
.
We can make the same simplifications in order to derive (d) and (e) (we already know that
x=t
is a fixed point). This time, when
t=1
, one of the solutions to the simplified equation is still at
x=t
.

Next, consider the graphs of the two equations

y=pt-x\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ y=\frac{p-1}{x^{p-1}}.

They intersect at two points; as
t
increases, the rightmost intersection point
\xi_{2}
tends to the right, while the leftmost intersection point
\xi_{1}
tends to the left. It is elementary to see that
\Psi(pt)
decreases as
t
increases;
\Phi(\xi_{1})
increases with
t
due to Lemma
8.3
, part (a).

Remark 8.6
.

All of the figures appearing in this paper were created out of Bezier curves using Tikz. They are intended to illustrate the qualitative behavior of the functions, and present a graphical solution to the Lagrange multiplier problem. They are not, however, the actual graphs of
\Phi
and
\Psi
; this is because there is no linear scale at which the critical features of mathematically accurate graphs could be discerned by the human eye, and a nonlinear scale would distort the straight line segments that will play a central part in the solution.

We now describe a dynamical system associated with
\Phi
and
\Psi
. Suppose that a point
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
is selected. If this point represents the value of
R_{1}
, then
R_{2}
can be determined by the equation
\Phi(R_{2})=\Psi(x_{1})
. To do this graphically, we first draw a vertical line segment connecting the points
(x_{1},0)
and
(x_{1},\Psi(x_{1}))
. Then we consider the horizontal line passing through the latter point. This horizontal line must intersect the graph of
y=\Phi(x)
at one, two or three points. If
(x_{2},\Phi(x_{2}))
is such an intersection point, then this tells us
x_{2}
is a candidate for the value of
R_{2}
. Let us accordingly draw a horizontal line segment connecting the points
(x_{1},\Psi(x_{1}))
and
(x_{2},\Phi(x_{2}))
. By continuing in this fashion, we obtain a path consisting of alternating vertical and horizontal line segments, intersecting the graphs of
\Psi
and
\Phi
. Generally, there are multiple such paths, reflecting that there may be multiple candidates for
x_{n}
at the
n
th step. Each associated sequence of abscissas,
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
,
x_{3}
,…, serves as a candidate for the sequence of values of the coefficient ratios
R_{1}
,
R_{2}
,
R_{3}
,…. These paths are called the “orbits” or “trajectories” of the initial point
x_{1}
. Of course we are principally interested in choosing
x_{1}=pt
.

The vast majority of these orbits do not correspond to valid solutions of the Lagrange multiplier problem for extremal functions
f
. This is because they do not “terminate,” or to be more precise, there is no value of
d\in\mathbb{N}
for which
x_{d}=0
. It is easy to see that there are orbits that converge to the fixed points of the system, that is, the points
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
at which
\Phi(\xi)=\Psi(\xi)
. In the proof of Lemma
8.5
we have already found that there are three fixed points
\xi_{1}
,
t
and
\xi_{2}
, satisfying
0<\xi_{1}<t<\xi_{2}
. In order for
an orbit to terminate, it must be that the orbit enters the region of the graph where
0\leqslant x<\xi_{1}
. It must further be the case that the orbit exactly reaches the point
(0,pt^{p-1})
, where the graph of
\Phi
and the
y
-axis intersect. In this way, a finite set of coefficients satisfying (
7.5
) is produced.

The following theorem provides a means to calculate
\tau_{p}
, and hence the radius
1/\tau_{p}
of the largest disk excluded from
\Omega_{p}
.
To prove this theorem, we need to look at the pre-orbits of the exit point
(0,pt^{p-1})
.

Theorem 8.7
.

1<p<\infty
, with
p\neq 2
, and for
t>1
, let
\xi_{1}=\xi_{1}(t)
and
\xi_{2}=\xi_{2}(t)
denote the two distinct positive solutions to the equation

x^{p-1}(pt-x)=p-1,

with
\xi_{1}<\xi_{2}
.
Then there is a unique value
\tau
of
t
for which

(pt-\xi_{1})|\xi_{1}-t|^{p-2}=(pt-\xi_{2})|\xi_{2}-t|^{p-2},

and it satisfies

\tau=\tau_{p}=\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}T_{d,p}.

Moreover, there is no solution in
\ell^{p}_{A}
to the recurrence relations with
t=\tau_{p}
.

Proof.

We proceed under the assumption that
p>2
. The case when
1<p<2
is similar, but the graphs of
\Phi
and
\Psi
have a different shape.

Previously we have seen that as
t
increases from the value 1,
\Phi(\xi_{1})
increases relative to
\Psi(\xi_{2})
. Therefore, for some value
t=\tau
, we have
\Phi(\xi_{1})=\Psi(\xi_{2})
. There is also a value
t=T
such that
\Psi(pT)=\Phi(\xi_{1})
. It is clear that if
t\leqslant T
, then no orbit originating from
(pt,0)
will reach the exit point
(0,pt^{p-1})
: Indeed, otherwise the orbit must converge towards the fixed point at
x=t
.

For any
T<t<\tau
fixed, there are infinitely many pre-orbits of
(0,pt^{p-1})
such that
\Phi(x_{m})<\Psi(\xi_{2})
for some
x_{m}
satisfying
0<x_{m}<\xi_{1}
. In between the fixed points these paths tend toward the horizontal line
y=\Phi(\xi_{1})
.

Of course these pre-orbits generally need not strike the point
(pt,0)
, which would signal a candidate solution to the Lagrange system (
7.5
).

However, the entirety of the graph of
\Phi
and
\Psi
and the associated orbits (viewed as a subset of the Euclidean plane) is a continuous object in the parameter
t
for
1<t<\tau_{p}
.
As
t
increases, the collection of origination points for the pre-orbits of
(0,pt^{p-1})
are continuously swept toward the left, relative to the point
(pt,0)
.
Thus as
t
increases toward
\tau_{p}
, there are countably many values of
t
for which the pre-orbit of the exit point
(0,pt^{p-1})
originates from
(0,pt)
. The corresponding collections of ratios
R_{1}
,
R_{2}
,…,
R_{d}
gives rise to a solution to (
7.5
) for this particular value of
t
. In fact, by the continuity of the graph relative to
t
, there is such a solution for all
d
from some integer onward.

This situation is illustrated in Figure
5
. Pre-orbits of the exit point are shown in various shades of green. As
d
increases, they get ever closer to the orange path, which traces back to the condition
y=\Phi(\xi_{1})
. As
t
increases, the
x
-intercepts of the green paths are dragged toward the left where in succession they hit the point
x=pt
. In this way, we obtain solutions to the Lagrange system for the optimal coefficients, and the corresponding values of
t
, for all
d
sufficiently large.

9.
Further directions

The work in
[
4
]
deals with more general Hilbert spaces. Most of what we presented here can be extended with minimal changes to the context of
weighted

\ell^{p}_{A}
spaces (like the ones in
[
16
]
, that is, defined by a norm
\|f\|^{p}_{p,\omega}:=\sum|a_{k}|^{p}\omega_{k}
for some weight
\omega
) but the nonlinearity of the recurrence relations is a key obstruction then and the dynamical systems approach of the previous section is not directly feasible.

A few other minor complications are the following:

(a)

The fact that
\tau_{p}<2
will not be preserved unless strong assumptions are made on the weights.

(b)

The examples to show that
\tau_{p}>1
will need to be adapted but they will cover reasonable non-decreasing weights (doubling, perhaps with restrictions on their growth) and essentially all decreasing weights produce extra zeros for the function
1+p\sum_{j=1}^{k}z^{j}
for large
k
. In the weighted case for
p=2
, zeros of OPA intersect the disk if and only if the weight is decreasing. Once more, this establishes a clear dichotomy between the case
p=2
and
p\neq 2
.

Finally, it seems natural to extend this study to any other convex space where polynomials are dense, such as Hardy spaces
H^{p}
or Bergman spaces
A^{p}
, for
1<p<\infty
. When
p=2
, both these spaces were dealt with in
[
4
]
giving qualitatively different behaviors. The Hardy space
H^{2}
corresponds to the case mentioned here of
\ell^{2}_{A}
, where no extra zeros are found, while
A^{2}
produces a region playing the role of our
\Omega_{p}
but with the difference of
being closed
and the complement of a disk of radius strictly smaller than 1.
Partly, the case
p=2
is special in that the recurrence relations (
7.5
) become linear, and
a_{k+1}
may be obtained directly from the values of
a_{k}
and
a_{k-1}
. The approach in
[
4
]
is based on the classical Favard’s Theorem, which establishes a correspondence between solutions to linear recurrence relations,
L^{2}
norms of Jacobi matrices and differential equations. This allows us to use the tools from several different mathematical worlds. The main obstruction in the non-Hilbert space case is that the recurrence relations (
7.5
) become non-linear and Favard’s Theorem is not applicable: the solutions to each of the recurrence relations is non-unique and can only be described implicitly.
At least for bounded functions, one can expect the zero of their linear approximants to move with some form of continuity when varying the parameter
p
defining the space
H^{p}
or
A^{p}
. Thus, extra zeros could appear in
A^{p}
at least for all
p
in some small region around
p=2
, while one should, in principle, expect
H^{p}
to present no extra zeros. The natural candidate for a function producing extra zeros in
A^{p}
is

f(z)=\left(1-\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{-3},

the extremizer found in
[
4
]
.
