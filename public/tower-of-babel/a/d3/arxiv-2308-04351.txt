Quenched decay of correlations for random contracting Lorenz maps
Andrew Larkin, Marks Ruziboev

Abstract
. In this work we consider i.i.d. random perturbations of contracting Lorenz maps sufficiently close to a Rovella parameter. We prove that the quenched correlations of the random dynamical system decays exponentially.



1.
Introduction

The Lorenz system
was introduced in
[
30
]
as a simplified model for atmospheric convection. Numerical simulations have shown that the Lorenz system admits a strange attractor, called the Lorenz attractor, which became one of the most iconic examples in the field.

A rigorous mathematical approach was developed with the introduction of the so called geometric Lorenz flow by
[
1
,
26
]
, which mimicks simulation of the dynamics of the Lorenz flow, and which has a robust strange attractor under
C^{1}
perturbations. Later in
[
37
,
38
]
it was shown that the actual Lorenz attractor is indeed a singular hyperbolic attractor, further showing that the geometric Lorenz attractor represents the Lorenz attractor well. Moreover, it admits the so called Sinai-Ruelle-Bowen (SRB) measure, which is ergodic
[
11
]
. Its statistical properties, such as mixing rates, limit theorems and their stability under various perturbations, were studied intensively (see for example,
[
31
,
10
,
9
,
7
,
14
,
13
,
24
]
).

Another class of systems with similar properties was introduced in
[
35
]
called the
contracting Lorenz flow
. A fundamental difference between these is that the attractor of the system introduced by Rovella is not robust under perturbations, but still abundant in a measure theoretic sense. The set of measures for which the system is chaotic is called Rovella parameters and satisfies strong chaotic properties
[
8
]
; moreover, restricted to this set the system is stochastically stable
[
32
,
33
]
. In
[
34
]
the authors addressed thermodynamic formalism for it. Up to now, the contracting Lorenz flow and one dimensional maps with critical points remain a profound example of a truly nonuniformly hyperbolic systems, which is studied via construction of induced schemes. We refer to
[
3
]
for a comprehensive account of these constructions.

Recently, there has been increased interest in studying statistical properties of random dynamical systems, especially quenched (path-wise) properties. When the system has good uniformly hyperbolic properties, spectral techniques are still applicable and imply strong statistical properties; we refer to
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
23
,
22
]
and references therein for results on quenched decay of correlations, limit theorems and stability results in this case.

For the non-uniformly expanding (or non-uniformly hyperbolic) case, spectral techniques are not applicable directly. In this regards, it is customary to employ inducing techniques, in particular randomised version of Young Tower
[
39
]
construction called random Young towers. This was first carried out in
[
15
]
for random towers with exponential tails, where the results were applied to random compositions of unimodal maps. Later, similar towers with polynomial tails were studied in
[
12
]
. An alternative approach for random towers was given in
[
25
]
. More recently, random Young Towers for hyperbolic systems were introduced in
[
4
]
, where the construction of such towers was carried out for nonuniformly hyperbolic random maps and in
[
5
]
for non-uniformly expanding systems with an ergodic base. As in the deterministic case, the construction of the Random Young Towers is highly non-trivial
[
28
]
.

In section 2, we introduce the setup of the random dynamical system we consider and state our main theorem, Theorem
2.1
, and the strategy of its proof. In section 3, we prove Theorem
2.1
in a series of steps.

2.
Setup

2.1.
Rovella maps

In the following section, we discuss two separate families of perturbed maps, the first one being obtained by perturbing some initial contracting Lorenz map (in the sense of
[
35
]
), and the second one being obtained from perturbing an initial Rovella map taken from the first family of maps (in the sense of
[
36
]
). This second family is the main focus of this paper, so for clarity, we denote maps in the first family using
f
and maps in the second family by
T
.

The contracting Lorenz map
f:I\to I
, with
I=[-1,1]
, is piecewise
C^{3}
which satisfies the following conditions:

(C1)

f
has a singularity at the point
x=0
with

(1)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}f(x)=-1,\quad\lim_{x\to 0^{-}}f(x)=1;

(C2)

Df(x)>0
for all
x\neq 0
with
\sup_{x\in(0,1]}Df(x)=Df(1)
,
\sup_{x\in[-1,0)}Df(x)=Df(-1)
and

(2)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{Df(x)}{|x|^{s-1}}\quad\text{exists and is nonzero for some }s>1,

which implies there exist constants
K_{1},K_{2}>0
such that for all
x\in I\setminus\{0\}
we have

(3)

\displaystyle K_{1}|x|^{s-1}\leq Df(x)\leq K_{2}|x|^{s-1};

(C3)

f
has negative Schwarzian derivative, i.e. for all
x\in I\setminus\{0\}
we have

(4)

\displaystyle S(f):=D\Big(\frac{D^{2}f}{Df}\Big)-\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{D^{2}f}{Df}\Big)^{2}<0.

Furthermore, in
[
35
]
it was shown that there exists an interval
[0,a_{*})\subset\mathbb{R}
, where
a^{*}>0
is small, and that there exists family of
C^{3}
maps
\{f_{a}:I\to I\}_{a\in[0,a_{*})}
, where
f_{0}
corresponds to some initial unperturbed contracting Lorenz map satisfying conditions C1-C3, such that, for the remaining
a\in(0,a_{*})
, all maps
f_{a}
are close to the original
f_{0}
. Here, by ‘close’ we mean their corresponding flows from which they are projected are in a small
C^{3}
neighborhood of the original contracting Lorenz flow. Moreover, there exists a Cantor-like set
E\subset(0,a^{*})
with positive Lebesgue measure such that, for all
a\in E
,
f_{a}
is chaotic and satisfies the following conditions:

(R1)

there exists a constant
\lambda>1
such that for all
a\in E
, the points
x=1
and
x=-1
we have

Df_{a}^{n}(\pm 1)>\lambda^{n},\quad\text{for all }n\geq 0;

(R2)

there exists a constant
\alpha>0
such that for all
a\in E
we have

|f_{a}^{n-1}(\pm 1)|>e^{-\alpha n},\quad\text{for all }n\geq 1;

(R3)

the orbits of the points
x=\pm 1
under
f_{a}
are dense in
[-1,1]
for all
a\in E
.

These maps are often referred to as
Rovella maps
. Note that if
a\in(0,a_{*})\setminus E
, then
f_{a}
will not necessarily satisfy all of these conditions. Additionally, it is worth noting that property (R1) implies the following three conditions for any
a\in E
:

1.

Large derivatives condition:

\lim_{n\to\infty}|Df_{a}^{n}(T(0))|=\infty;

2.

Summability condition of exponent 1:

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|Df_{a}^{n}(T(0))|}\leq\infty;

3.

Collet-Eckmann condition:

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\log\frac{|Df_{a}^{n}(T(0))|}{n}>0.

2.2.
Random dynamical systems setup

Taking sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, we assume that there exists a one-parameter admissible family of maps
\{T_{t}:I\to I\}_{t\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]}
, where we assume that for
t=0
the map
T_{0}
corresponds to some Rovella map
f_{a}
with
a\in E
(i.e.
T_{0}
satisfies conditions C1-C3, R1-R3). We call a family
admissible
if the following conditions hold:

1.

|\partial_{t}F(t,x)|\leq 1
for all
x\in I
and
t\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
, with
F(t,x)=T_{t}(x)
;

2.

for all
t\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
, the map
T_{t}
will satisfy conditions C1 to C3;

3.

there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
x,y\in I
and
t\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
, we have

2|x-y|<|x|\implies\Big|\log\frac{DT_{t}(x)}{DT_{t}(y)}\Big|\leq C\frac{|x-y|}{|x|}.

Now, let
\nu_{\varepsilon}
denote the normalized Lebesgue measures on
[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
. Furthermore, we define
\Omega_{\varepsilon}=[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]^{\mathbb{Z}}
, and we take the product measure
\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}=\nu_{\varepsilon}^{\mathbb{Z}}
on
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. We define our random dynamical system with the usual skew product

S:\Omega_{\varepsilon}\times I\to\Omega_{\varepsilon}\times I,\quad S(\omega,x)=(\sigma\omega,T_{\omega}(x)).

2.3.
Main Result

Here we state the main result of this paper:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be sufficiently small. The random contracting Lorenz system
\{T_{\omega}\}
admits a unique equivariant family of absolutely continuous probability measures
\{\mu_{\omega}\}
. Moreover, there exists a
\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}
-integrable constant
C(\omega)
, and there exists a constant
b>0
such, for almost every
\omega\in\Omega
, we have that for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{\eta}(I)
with
\eta\in(0,1)
and
\psi\in L^{\infty}(I)
, we have

\left|\int(\varphi\circ T_{\omega}^{n})\psi d\mu_{\omega}-\int\varphi d\mu_{\sigma^{n}\omega}\int\psi d\mu_{\omega}\right|\leq C(\omega)C_{\varphi,\psi}e^{-bn}

and

\Big|\int(\varphi\circ T^{n}_{\sigma^{-n}\omega})\psi d\mu_{\sigma^{-n}\omega}-\int\varphi d\mu_{\omega}\int\psi d\mu_{\sigma^{-n}\omega}\Big|\leq C(\omega)C_{\varphi,\psi}e^{-bn}

for some
C_{\varphi,\psi}>0
which only depends on
\varphi
and
\psi
and is uniform for all
\omega\in\Omega
.

Remark 2.2
.

One should note that even though
T_{0}
satisfies conditions (R1)-(R3), the other maps in the family do not necessarily satisfy these conditions. Because of this, for certain random compositions of maps, we may need to wait a long time before we see a map with the desired properties. This means that we will need to introduce a waiting time before we see the desired asymptotic behaviour for the tails of return times and the decay of correlations. We express this equivalently using a large constant factor
C(\omega)
in the upper bound of the random correlation function for almost every
\omega\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
.

2.4.
Strategy of the Proof

We prove Theorem
2.1
by showing that the random system
\{T_{\omega}\}
admits a random Young tower structure in the sense of
[
15
,
25
]
for almost every
\omega\in\Omega
with exponential tails of the return times. For convenience, in the Appendix we include the definition of random Young towers as well as Theorem
A.1
from
[
15
,
25
]
, which we use in the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

To build a random Young tower, we use a similar construction to that in Section 8.3 of
[
25
]
, where a random Young tower is constructed for a system of random unimodal maps. However, before we can use such a construction, we must prove that certain results hold for our random contracting Lorenz system that are analoguous to the results proved in
[
36
]
for random non-uniformly expanding interval maps. In particular, we must prove results regarding random non-uniform expansion and the tails of ‘bad’
\omega
’s, where expansion is not guaranteed. Once we have these, then we move onto the tower construction, which involves the use of
hyperbolic return times
, originating from the works of Alves.

Thus, the structure of the rest of the paper is as follows:

1.

Prove that if the random orbit stays outside of a particular neighborhood around the origin (which we will use as the base of our random tower), then we have exponential expansion. This will involve first proving some results regarding the deterministic dynamics;

2.

Prove that the set of ‘good’
\omega
’s grows to a full measure subset of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
as
n\to\infty
, thereby guaranteeing that we eventually have expansion for almost all
\omega\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
;

3.

Prove that we have exponential tails of hyperbolic return times and that the hyperbolic return times of a point
x
in the base give us a neighborhood around
x
on which
T_{\omega}^{n}(x)
is diffeomorphic, has bounded distortion, and uniform expansion. These neighborhoods will be used to define the return partition;

4.

Prove that we can construct a return partition using the neighborhoods from the previous step, and that we have exponential tails of return times along with the other necessary randon tower conditions to apply Theorem
A.1
.

3.
Proofs

3.1.
Random Expansion

In
[
8
]
, Alves and Soufi already proved expansion results for a Rovella map with a ‘good’ parameter
a\in E
when the orbit stays outside of a neighbourhood of the form
(-\delta,\delta)
for sufficiently small
\delta>0
. This can easily be further extended to random orbits using a continuity argument, which we will show later in this section. However, for our tower construction, we will require expansion results of a different form, which we state below.

Let us denote
\tilde{B}(\delta)=T^{-1}(B_{\delta}(1))\cup T^{-1}(B_{\delta}(-1))
and
\mathcal{D}(\delta)=\frac{|B_{\delta}(0)|}{|\tilde{B}(\delta)|}
. Additionally, throughout this chapter we assume that there exists a small constant
\delta_{*}>0
such that for every
x\in\tilde{B}(\delta_{*})
,
\delta=\max\{x,\delta_{*}\}
and
\omega\in\Omega_{\delta}
, we have

(5)

\displaystyle DT_{\omega}(x)\geq\mathcal{D}(\delta).

We prove the following Proposition, which is an adaption of Theorem 2.1 in
[
36
]
and Lemma 8.1.6 in
[
25
]
:

Proposition 3.1
.

There exists uniform constants
A,\kappa>0
, and for sufficiently small
\delta>0
there exists a constant
\Lambda(\delta)>0
satisfying
\lim_{\delta\to 0}\Lambda(\delta)=\infty
such that for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, all
\omega\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
and all
x\in I
, we have

1.

if
x,T_{\omega}(x),\dots,T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x)\notin\tilde{B}(\delta)
and
T_{\omega}^{n}(x)\in\tilde{B}(2\delta)
, then

(6)

\displaystyle|DT_{\omega}^{n}(x)|\geq Ae^{\kappa n};

2.

if
|x-T(0)|\leq 4\delta
,
x,T_{\omega}(x),\dots,T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x)\notin\tilde{B}(\delta)
and
T_{\omega}^{n}(x)\in\tilde{B}(2\delta)
, then

(7)

\displaystyle|DT_{\omega}^{n}(x)|\geq\frac{\Lambda(\delta)}{\mathcal{D}(\delta)}e^{\kappa n}.

To prove this, we follow the steps used by Shen in
[
36
]
to prove similar estimates for random non-uniformly expanding interval maps.

First, let us introduce the relevant notation and prove a series of smaller initial lemmas. Set
I_{+}=(0,1]
and
I_{-}=[-1,0)
. Given some subset
J
, satisfying either
J\subset I_{+}
or
J\subset I_{-}
for every
\omega\in\Omega
, we define

\text{Dist}(T_{\omega}|J):=\sup_{x,y\in J}\log\frac{DT_{\omega}(x)}{DT_{\omega}(y)}

as well as

\mathcal{N}(\varphi|J):=\sup_{J^{\prime}\subset J}\text{Dist}(T_{\omega}|J^{\prime})\frac{|J|}{|J^{\prime}|}.

Additionally, we set

A(\omega,x,n)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{DT_{\omega}^{i}(x)}{|T_{\omega}^{i}(x)|}.

Lemma 3.2
.

For our family of maps
\{T_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
, there exists a constant
\theta_{0}>0
(independent of
\varepsilon
) such that for all
\omega\in\Omega
and
x\in I
, and for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that
A(\omega,x,n)<\infty
, setting

J=\Big[x-\frac{\theta_{0}}{A(\omega,x,n)},x+\frac{\theta_{0}}{A(\omega,x,n)}\Big]\cap I_{\pm},

we have that
T_{\omega}|J
is a diffeomorphism and that
\mathcal{N}(T_{\omega}^{n}|J)\leq 1
. Furthermore, for every
y\in J\setminus\{0\}
, we have

(8)

\displaystyle e^{-1}\frac{DT_{\omega}^{n}(x)}{A(\omega,x,n)}\leq\frac{DT_{\omega}^{n}(y)}{A(\omega,y,n)}\leq e\frac{DT_{\omega}^{n}(x)}{A(\omega,x,n)}.

Proof.

Let
\omega\in\Omega
and let
L\subset I
be some subinterval in either
I_{-}
or
I_{+}
. Recall the definition of
\mathcal{N}(T_{\omega}|L)
:

\mathcal{N}(T_{\omega}|L)=\sup_{L^{\prime}\subset L}\text{Dist}(T_{\omega}|L^{\prime})\frac{|L|}{|L^{\prime}|}=\sup_{L^{\prime}\subset L}\sup_{x_{1},x_{2}\in L^{\prime}}\log\frac{DT_{\omega}(x_{1})}{DT_{\omega}(x_{2})}\frac{|L|}{|L^{\prime}|}.

We want to show that for any
y\in L
, we have

(9)

\displaystyle 2|L|\leq|y|\implies\mathcal{N}(T_{\omega}|L)\leq C\frac{|L|}{|y|}.

First, we should confirm that
\mathcal{N}(T_{\omega}|L)
actually has a finite value for all
L
either in
I_{-}
or
I_{+}
. For a given
L
, we may have the trivial case where there does exist a subinterval
L^{\prime}\subset L
of positive length that maximizes
\text{Dist}(T_{\omega}|L^{\prime})/|L^{\prime}|
, in which case
\mathcal{N}(T_{\omega}|L)
is clearly finite. Now, consider the less trivial and more likely case that there exists no such
L^{\prime}
. If so, we are effectively taking

\mathcal{N}(T_{\omega}|L)=|L|\cdot\Big(\lim_{h\to 0}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}L^{\prime}\subset L\\
|L^{\prime}|=h\end{subarray}}\sup_{x_{1},x_{2}\in L^{\prime}}\log\frac{DT_{\omega}(x_{1})}{DT_{\omega}(x_{2})}\frac{1}{h}\Big).

Since the derivative is monotonically decreasing and increasing on
I_{-}
and
I_{+}
respectively, then the
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
that maximize the distortion are just the end points of
L^{\prime}
, i.e.
|x_{2}|=|x_{1}|-h
. Thus, we have

\mathcal{N}(T_{\omega}|L)=|L|\cdot\Big(\lim_{h\to 0}\sup_{x_{1},\in L^{\prime}}\big(\log DT_{\omega}(x_{1})-\log DT_{\omega}(x_{1}\pm h)\big)\frac{1}{h}\Big).

But this is just the derivative of
\log DT_{\omega}(x_{1})
, and since we already know
T_{\omega}
is
C^{3}
for all
\omega\in\Omega
, we therefore have

(10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(T_{\omega}|L)=\sup_{x_{1}\in L}\frac{D^{2}T_{\omega}(x_{1})}{DT_{\omega}(x_{1})}|L|.

Notice that by using the order of singularity condition, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(T_{\omega}|L)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x_{1}\in L}\frac{(s-1)K_{2}|x_{1}|^{s-2}}{K_{1}|x_{1}|^{s-1}}|L|=\frac{K_{2}(s-1)}{K_{1}}\sup_{x_{1}\in L}\frac{1}{|x_{1}|}|L|.

Now, clearly the
x_{1}\in L
that maximizes this expression is the one closest to
0
. Thus, we have
|x_{1}|\geq|y|-|L|
. Furthermore, using the assumption
2|L|\leq|y|
, we therefore have
|x_{1}|\geq 1/2|y|
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(T_{\omega}|L)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K_{2}(s-1)}{K_{1}}\frac{2}{|y|}|L|.

We can assume that
C
is small, say smaller than
1.5
. Therefore, we can choose some
\theta_{0}\in(0,(6e)^{-1})
such that

\displaystyle 2|L|\leq|y|\implies\mathcal{N}(T_{\omega}|L)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4e\theta_{0}}\frac{|L|}{|y|}.

Now, let us set
n_{0}
as the largest integer in the set
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
such that

\sum_{i=0}^{n_{0}-1}\frac{|T_{\omega}^{i}(J)|}{|T_{\omega}^{i}(x)|}\leq 2e\theta_{0}<\frac{1}{3}.

Note that
|J|=2\theta_{0}/A(\omega,x,n)
. Also note that for any
\omega\in\Omega
and
x\in I
, we have
A(\omega,x,1)=1
. Thus, the above inequality holds for
n_{0}=1
.

In fact, we want to show that
n_{0}=n
. First, we note that for all
0\leq i<n_{0}
, the above inequality implies that
2|T_{\omega}^{i}(J)|\leq|T_{\omega}^{i}(x)|
, otherwise one of the terms in the sum would be equal or greater than
1/2
, thereby breaking the above inequality. Thus, for every
1\leq m\leq n_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\text{Dist}(T_{\omega}^{m}|J)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y,z\in J}\log\frac{DT_{\omega}^{m}(x)}{DT_{\omega}^{m}(y)}

\displaystyle=\sup_{y,z\in J}\log\Big(\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{DT_{\sigma^{j}\omega}(x)}{DT_{\sigma^{j}\omega}(y)}\Big)=\sup_{y,z\in J}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\log\frac{DT_{\sigma^{j}\omega}(x)}{DT_{\sigma^{j}\omega}(y)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\text{Dist}\big(T_{\sigma^{j}\omega}|T_{\omega}^{j}(J)\big)\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\mathcal{N}\big(T_{\sigma^{j}\omega}|T_{\omega}^{j}(J)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4e\theta_{0}}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{|T_{\omega}^{i}(J)|}{|T_{\omega}^{i}(x)|}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Clearly, this implies using the mean value theorem that there exists some
y\in J
such that

DT_{\omega}^{m}(y)=\frac{|T_{\omega}^{m}(J)|}{|J|},

and therefore
|T_{\omega}^{m}(J)|\leq e(T_{\omega}^{m})^{\prime}(x)|J|
. Now, for contradiction, assume
n_{0}<n
. Consider

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n_{0}}\frac{|T_{\omega}^{i}(J)|}{|T_{\omega}^{i}(x)|}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{n_{0}}\frac{eDT_{\omega}^{m}(x)|J|}{|T_{\omega}^{i}(x)|}

\displaystyle=eA(\omega,x,n_{0}+1)|J|\leq 2e\theta_{0}.

But this contradicts the definition of
n_{0}
. Thus, it must be that
n_{0}=n
.

For any subinterval
J^{\prime}\subset J
, using the bounded distortion we have
|T_{\omega}^{i}(J^{\prime})|\leq e|J^{\prime}||T_{\omega}^{i}(J)|/|J|
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\text{Dist}(T_{\omega}^{n}|J^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4e\theta_{0}}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{|T_{\omega}^{i}(J^{\prime})|}{|T_{\omega}^{i}(x)|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4e\theta_{0}}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{|J^{\prime}|}{|J|}\frac{|T_{\omega}^{i}(J)|}{|T_{\omega}^{i}(x)|}\leq\frac{|J^{\prime}|}{2|J|}.

Thus, we have

\mathcal{N}(\varphi|J):=\sup_{J^{\prime}\subset J}\text{Dist}(\varphi|J^{\prime})\frac{|J|}{|J^{\prime}|}\leq\sup_{J^{\prime}\subset J}\frac{|J^{\prime}|}{2|J|}\frac{|J|}{|J^{\prime}|}\leq\frac{1}{2}<1.

Now, notice that for any
y\in J
and for
0\leq m<n
we have

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{|T_{\omega}^{m}(y)|}{|T_{\omega}^{m}(x)|}-1\Big|

\displaystyle=\frac{|T_{\omega}^{m}(y)-T_{\omega}^{m}(x)|}{|T_{\omega}^{m}(x)|}\leq\frac{|T_{\omega}^{m}(J)|}{|T_{\omega}^{m}(x)|}\leq\frac{1}{2},

which clearly implies
1/2\leq{|T_{\omega}^{m}(y)|}/{|T_{\omega}^{m}(x)|\leq 3/2}
. Using the bounded distortion, we have
e^{-1/2}(T_{\omega}^{m})^{\prime}(x)\leq(T_{\omega}^{m})^{\prime}(y)\leq e^{1/2}(T_{\omega}^{m})^{\prime}(x)
. Thus, combining these two, we obtain

e^{-1}\frac{DT_{\omega}^{m}(x)}{|T_{\omega}^{m}(x)|}\leq\frac{DT_{\omega}^{m}(y)}{|T_{\omega}^{m}(y)|}\leq e\frac{DT_{\omega}^{m}(x)}{|T_{\omega}^{m}(x)|}

To prove inequality (
9
), we work with the reciprocal since it is slightly easier to work with:

\displaystyle\frac{A(\omega,y,n)}{DT_{\omega}^{n}(y)}=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{DT_{\omega}^{i}(y)}{|T_{\omega}^{i}(y)|}}{DT_{\omega}^{n}(y)}\leq\frac{\frac{3}{2}e^{1/2}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{DT_{\omega}^{i}(x)}{T_{\omega}^{i}(x)}}{e^{-1/2}|T_{\omega}^{n}(x)|}=\frac{3}{2}e\frac{A(\omega,x,n)}{DT_{\omega}^{n}(x)}.

Likewise for the lower bound.

It will also be useful to introduce the concept of a
binding period
along with a relevant lemma:

Definition 3.3

(Binding Period)
.

For a given
\varepsilon>0
,
v\in I\setminus\{0\}
and
C>0
, we say that
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
is a
C
-binding period for
(v,\varepsilon)
if for every
y\in I\setminus\{0\}
such that
|v-y|\leq\varepsilon
and for every
0\leq j<N
and
\omega\in\Omega
, we have the following:

(11)

\displaystyle 2|T_{\omega}^{j}(y)-T^{j}(v)|

\displaystyle\leq|T^{j}(v)|;

(12)

\displaystyle e^{-1}|DT^{j+1}(v)|

\displaystyle\leq|DT_{\omega}^{j+1}(y)|\leq e|DT^{j+1}(v)|;

(13)

\displaystyle C\varepsilon|DT^{j+1}(v)|

\displaystyle\geq|T_{\omega}^{j+1}(y)-T^{j+1}(v)|.

Lemma 3.4
.

There exists a constant
\theta_{1}>0
such that for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, for any point
v\in[-1,1]\setminus\{0\}
, if there exists
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that

W:=\sum_{j=0}^{N}|DT_{\omega}^{j}(v)|<\infty\text{ and }A(0,v,N)W\leq\delta_{1}/\varepsilon.

Then
N
is a
\varepsilon W
- binding period for
(v,\varepsilon).

Proof.

Using the same proof as in one of the previous lemmas, we know there exists some
\theta_{1}\in(0,(4e)^{-1})
such that for any interval
L\subset[-1,1]\setminus\{0\}
,
z\in L
and
\omega\in\Omega
, we have

(14)

\displaystyle 2|L|\leq|z|\implies\mathcal{N}(T_{\omega}|L)\leq\frac{1}{2e\theta_{1}}\frac{|L|}{|z|}.

Now, given some
y\in[-1,1]\setminus\{0\}
such that
|v-y|\leq\varepsilon
and some
\omega\in\Omega
, we define
L_{i}=[v_{i},y_{i}]
(or alternatively
L_{i}=[y_{i},v_{i}]
), where
v_{i}=T^{i}(v)
and
y_{i}=T_{\omega}^{i}(y)
. Trivially we have

|I_{i+1}|\leq|T(I_{i})|+\varepsilon.

Now, we want to show that the following three inequalities hold for all
n
:

(15)

\displaystyle|I_{n}|\leq eW\varepsilon DT^{n}(v);

(16)

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq i<n}\frac{|I_{i}|}{|T^{i}(v)|}\leq e\theta_{1};

(17)

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq i<n}\text{Dist}(T|I_{i})\leq\frac{1}{2}.

For
n=0
this is trivial. We have

|I_{0}|=|v-y|\leq\varepsilon\leq eW\varepsilon;\quad 0\leq e\theta_{1};\quad 0\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Now, we prove the statement by induction and assume that we have proved this up to some time
0\leq n_{0}<N
. For
n=n_{0}+1
, the proof is straightforward:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n_{0}}\frac{|I_{i}|}{|v_{i}|}

\displaystyle\leq eW\varepsilon\sum_{i=0}^{n_{0}}\frac{DT^{n}(v)}{|v_{i}|}

\displaystyle\leq eW\varepsilon A(0,\omega,n_{0}+1)\leq eW\varepsilon A(0,\omega,N)\leq e\theta_{1}.

For the first inequality we have used the fact that inequality
15
holds for
n_{0}
, and for the last inequality we have used our starting assumption. Note that this implies that for
0\leq i\leq n_{0}
, we have
2|I_{i}|\leq|v_{i}|
. Thus, we can apply inequality
14
to obtain the following:

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq i<n_{0}+1}\text{Dist}(T|I_{i})\leq\frac{1}{2e\theta_{1}}\sum_{0\leq i<n_{0}+1}\frac{|I_{i}|}{|v_{i}|}\leq\frac{1}{2}<1,

where in the last step we just use the fact that inequality
17
holds for
n_{0}
.

Finally, by the mean value theorem there exists some
\xi_{i}\in I_{i}
for all
i=0,1,\dots,n_{0}
such that
|I_{i+1}|\leq DT(x_{i})|I_{i}|+\varepsilon
. Iterating this, we have

\displaystyle|I_{n_{0}+1}|\leq\prod_{i=0}^{n_{0}}DT(x_{i})|I_{0}|+\varepsilon\Big(1+\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}\prod_{j=k}^{n_{0}}DT(x_{j})\Big).

As we have already shown that inequality
17
holds for
n=n_{0}+1
, we thus have for all
0\leq k\leq n_{0}

\displaystyle\prod_{i=k}^{n_{0}}DT(x_{i})\leq e\prod_{i=k}^{n_{0}}DT(v_{i})=e\frac{DT^{n_{0}+1}(v)}{DT^{k}(v)}

Plugging this into the previous inequality, we have

\displaystyle|I_{n_{0}+1}|

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=0}^{n_{0}}eDT(v_{i})|I_{0}|+\varepsilon\Big(1+\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}\prod_{j=k}^{n_{0}}DT(x_{j})\Big)

\displaystyle=eDT^{n_{0}+1}(v)\Big(|I_{0}|+\frac{\varepsilon}{eDT^{n_{0}+1}(v)}+\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}\frac{\varepsilon}{DT^{k}(v)}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq e\varepsilon DT^{n_{0}+1}(v)\Big(1+\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}+1}\frac{1}{(T^{k})^{\prime}(v)}\Big)\leq eWDT^{n_{0}+1}(v)\varepsilon.

Our focus now is to show these imply that
eW
is a binding time for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
. Condition
11
is clearly implied by inequality
16
, and likewise for condition
12
and inequality
17
. To prove the remaining condition, we need to show that for
0\leq j\leq N-1
we have

(18)

\displaystyle\log\Big(\frac{|DT_{\omega}^{j+1}(y)|}{|DT^{j+1}(v)|}\Big)\leq 1.

We split the above in the following way:

\displaystyle\log\Big(\frac{|DT_{\omega}^{j+1}(y)|}{|DT^{j+1}(v)|}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{j}\log\Big(\frac{|DT_{\sigma^{i}\omega}(y_{i})|}{|T^{\prime}(v_{i})|}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{j}\log\Big(\frac{|DT_{\sigma^{i}\omega}(y_{i})|}{|DT(v_{i})|}\cdot\frac{|DT(y_{i})|}{|DT(y_{i})|}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{j}\log\Big(\frac{|DT(y_{i})|}{|DT(v_{i})|}\Big)+\sum_{i=0}^{j}\log\Big(\frac{|DT_{\sigma^{i}\omega}(y_{i})|}{|DT(y_{i})|}\Big).

For the first sum, we already know from
17
that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{j}\log\Big(\frac{DT(y_{i})}{DT(v_{i})}\Big)\leq\sum_{i=0}^{j}\text{Dist}(T|I_{i})\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Thus, we need to show the same upper bound for the second sum. To this end, we use the fact that
\log(1+x)\leq x
for
x\geq 0
to obtain the following:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{j}\log\Big(\frac{|DT_{\sigma^{i}\omega}(y_{i})|}{|T^{\prime}(y_{i})|}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{j}\log\Big(\frac{|DT_{\sigma^{i}\omega}(y_{i})|}{|DT(y_{i})|}+\frac{|DT(y_{i})|}{|DT(y_{i})|}-\frac{|DT(y_{i})|}{|DT(y_{i})|}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{j}\log\Big(1+\frac{|DT_{\sigma^{i}\omega}(y_{i})-DT(y_{i})|}{|DT(y_{i})|}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{j}\frac{|DT_{\sigma^{i}\omega}(y_{i})-DT(y_{i})|}{|DT(y_{i})|}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\frac{e\varepsilon}{|DT(v_{i})|},

where in the last inequality we use bounded distortion and the assumption that for all
x\in I\setminus\{0\}
and al
\omega,\tilde{\omega}\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, we have
|DT_{\omega}(x)-DT_{\tilde{\omega}}(x)|\leq\varepsilon
. Now, using this Lemma’s assumptions and the definitions of
W
and
A
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\theta_{1}}{\varepsilon}\geq A(0,v,N)W

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\frac{DT^{i}(v)}{|v_{i}|}\Big)\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{DT^{i+1}(v)}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\frac{DT^{i}(v)}{|v_{i}|}\frac{1}{DT^{i+1}(v)}=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{DT(v_{i})|v_{i}|}\geq\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{DT(v_{i})}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{j}\log\Big(\frac{|DT(T_{\omega}^{i}(y))|}{|DT(T^{i}(y))|}\Big)\leq e\theta_{1}<\frac{1}{2},

thus completing the proof.
∎

Now, we recall that condition H2. above, proved in
[
8
, Lemma 2.1]
by Alves and Soufi, tells us that the deterministic system
T_{0}
has expansion when the orbit stays outside a small neighbourhood of
0
. Analogously to
[
36
, Proposition 2.7 (i)]
, we prove that the same holds true for our perturbed system as well:

Lemma 3.5
.

There exists a constant
\eta>0
, and for sufficiently small
\delta>0
there exists a constant
C=C(\delta)>0
, such that, for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, all
\omega\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, all
n\geq 1
and all
x\in I\setminus\{0\}
, if
x,T_{\omega}(x),\dots,T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x)\notin(-\delta,\delta)
, then
DT_{\omega}^{n}(x)\geq Ce^{\eta n}
.

Proof.

Assume we have
\delta>0
already fixed sufficiently small. For the first result, we already know from condition H2) that if
x,T(x),\dots,T^{n-1}(x)\notin(-\delta,\delta)
, then
DT^{n}(x)\geq c(\delta)\lambda^{n}
. Now, depending on how small our constant
c(\delta)
is, let us fix a sufficiently large iterate M. Clearly, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, for each
\omega\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
the orbit
\big\{x,T_{\omega}(x),\dots,T_{\omega}^{M-1}(x)\big\}
will not deviate too much from the unperturbed orbit, especially since we are only considering finite iterates. Thus, if
DT^{M}(x)\geq c(\delta)\lambda^{M}
, then by continuity we can say that
DT_{\omega}^{M}(x)\geq c(\delta)\hat{\lambda}^{M}
, where
\lambda>\hat{\lambda}>1
is some smaller suitably chosen constant.

Now, suppose
n\leq M
, and suppose
x,T_{\omega}(x),\dots,T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x)\notin(-\delta,\delta)
. We have already assumed that
\varepsilon
is small enough so that all perturbed orbits are close to the unperturbed orbit up to iterate
M-1
. However, one must note that
x,T_{\omega}(x),\dots,T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x)\notin(-\delta,\delta)
does not necessarily imply
x,T(x),\dots,T^{n-1}(x)\notin(-\delta,\delta)
because it may be that there exists some iterate
1\leq j\leq n-1
such that
T^{j}(x)\in(-\delta,\delta)
, but
T^{j}(x)
lands close enough to edge of
(-\delta,\delta)
that the perturbed orbit can land outside. But this can be handled, as we can simply decompose the unperturbed orbit, with
x,T(x),\dots,T^{j-1}(x)\notin(-\delta,\delta)
and
T^{j}(x)\in(-\delta,\delta)
, implying
DT^{j}(x)\geq\lambda^{j}
, and then
T^{j+1}(x),\dots,T^{n-1}(x)\notin(-\delta,\delta)
implying
DT^{n-(j+1)}(T_{\omega}^{j+1}(x))\geq c(\delta)\lambda^{j}
, and by using continuity again, we have
x,T_{\omega}(x)
,
\dots
,
T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x)\notin(-\delta,\delta)
implies
DT_{\omega}^{n}(x)\geq c(\delta)\hat{\lambda}^{n}
. Additionally, if
T_{\omega}^{n}(x)\in(-\delta,\delta)
, again this does not imply that
T^{n}(x)\in(-\delta,\delta)
because it may be that
T^{n}(x)
lands just outside
(-\delta,\delta)
but the perturbation is large enough that
T^{n}(x)\in(-\delta,\delta)
. Thus, we cannot claim
DT^{n}(x)\geq\lambda^{n}
. Nonetheless, by the size of
\varepsilon
,
DT^{n}(x)
must be extremely close to
(-\delta,\delta)
, so by continuity we can instead say
DT^{n}(x)\geq\tilde{\lambda}^{n}
for some
\lambda>\tilde{\lambda}>1
, and thus
DT_{\omega}^{n}(x)\geq\hat{\lambda}^{n}
for
\tilde{\lambda}>\hat{\lambda}>1
.

Now assume that
M<n\leq 2M
. After
M-1
iterates, we lose that the perturbed orbits of our original
x
are close to the unperturbed orbit of
x
, but this is not an issue. Take
x
such that
x,T_{\omega}(x),\dots,T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x)\notin(-\delta,\delta)
, and let us decompose its orbit into
x,T_{\omega}(x),\dots,T_{\omega}^{M-1}(x)\notin(-\delta,\delta)
and
T_{\omega}^{M}(x),\dots,T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x)\notin(-\delta,\delta)
. Along the first part of the perturbed orbit, clearly we have
DT_{\omega}^{M}(x)\geq c(\delta)\hat{\lambda}^{M}
. As for the second part, let us take
y=T_{\omega}^{M}(x)
and then consider its unperturbed orbit
\{y,T(y),\dots,T^{n-1-M}(y)\}
. We can then use the exact same continuity argument as before to show that
DT^{n-M}(y)\geq c(\delta)\lambda^{n-M}
implies
DT_{\sigma^{M}\omega}^{n-M}(T_{\omega}^{M}(x))\geq c(\delta)\hat{\lambda}^{n-M}
. Thus, we have
DT_{\omega}^{n}(x)=\big[DT_{\omega}^{M}(x)\big]\cdot\big[DT_{\sigma^{M}\omega}^{n-M}(T_{\omega}^{M}(x))\big]\geq c(\delta)^{2}\hat{\lambda}^{n}
. Furthermore, we can use the same argument as above to show that if
T_{\omega}^{n}(x)\in(-\delta,\delta)
, then
DT_{\omega}^{n}(x)\geq c(\delta)\hat{\lambda}^{n}
.

For any
\ell M<n\leq(\ell+1)M
, for
x,T_{\omega}(x),\dots,T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x)\notin(-\delta,\delta)
we simply decompose the orbit as before, giving us
DT_{\omega}^{n}(x)\geq(c(\delta)\hat{\lambda}^{M})^{\ell}c(\delta)\hat{\lambda}^{n-\ell M}=c(\delta)^{\ell+1}\hat{\lambda}^{n}
. Clearly this
c(\delta)^{\ell+1}
may be an issue if it shrinks too quickly, so we choose
M
large enough and
\varepsilon
small enough (which prevents
\hat{\lambda}
from becoming too small) so that
c(\delta)\hat{\lambda}^{M}\geq 1
. Thus, choosing an even smaller
\hat{\lambda}>\hat{\lambda}_{0}>1
so that
\hat{\lambda}_{0}^{\ell M}\leq(c(\delta)\hat{\lambda}^{M})^{\ell}
, we have
DT_{\omega}^{n}(x)\geq c(\delta)\hat{\lambda}_{0}^{n}
. Furthermore, using this and the argument above, if
T_{\omega}^{n}(x)\in(-\delta,\delta)
, then
DT_{\omega}^{n}(x)\geq\hat{\lambda}_{0}^{n}
.
∎

Additionally, we want to prove the tails of perturbed return times decays exponentially as well:

Lemma 3.6
.

There exists a constant
\eta>0
, and for sufficiently small
\delta>0
there exists a constant
C=C(\delta)>0
, such that, for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, all
\omega\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, and all
n\geq 1
, we have

\big|\big\{x\in I:\hskip 3.00003ptT_{\omega}^{j}(x)\notin(-\delta,\delta)\text{ for }0\leq j\leq n-1\big\}\big|\leq Ce^{-\eta n}

Proof.

Let
\delta>0
and
\varepsilon>0
already be fixed sufficiently small. Setting
U=(-\delta,\delta)
, for every
\omega\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
and
n\geq 1
we define

\Lambda_{n}^{\omega}(U)=\big\{x\in I:\hskip 3.00003ptT_{\omega}^{j}(x)\notin U\text{ for }0\leq j\leq n-1\big\}

as well as
\Lambda_{\infty}^{\omega}(U)=\cap_{n=1}^{\infty}\Lambda_{n}^{\omega}(U)
. Furthermore, let
U_{0}\subset U
also be an open neighbourhood of the critical point at
0
.

We note that there exists constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that for all
x\in\Lambda_{n}^{\omega}(U)
, we have

C_{1}DT_{\omega}^{n}(x)\leq A(\omega,x,n)\leq C_{2}DT_{\omega}^{n}(x).

Indeed, recalling the definition of
A(\omega,x,n)
, we have

\displaystyle A(\omega,x,n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{DT_{\omega}^{i}(x)}{|T_{\omega}^{i}(x)|}=\frac{1}{|x|}+\frac{DT_{\omega}(x)}{|T_{\omega}(x)|}+\dots+\frac{DT_{\omega}^{n-1}(x)}{|T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x)|}.

Since by definition of
x\in\Lambda_{n}^{\omega}(U)
we have
x,T_{\omega}(x),\dots,T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x)\notin U
, we clearly have

\displaystyle A(\omega,x,n)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\delta}\Big(1+DT_{\omega}(x)+\dots+DT_{\omega}^{n-1}(x)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\delta}\Big(1+(n-1)\sup DT_{\omega}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}c(\delta)e^{\eta n}\leq C_{1}DT_{\omega}^{n}(x)

The second-last inequality holds because the line above grows linearly with
n
, while the last line grows exponentially, thus we can just choose some large constant
C_{1}
and put it in front. The last inequality holds by the previous lemma. The lower bound is trivial:

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{1}{|x|}+\frac{DT_{\omega}(x)}{|T_{\omega}(x)|}+\dots+\frac{DT_{\omega}^{n-1}(x)}{|T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x)|}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\Big(1+DT_{\omega}(x)+\dots+DT_{\omega}^{n-1}(x)\Big)

\displaystyle\geq DT_{\omega}^{n-1}(x)\geq C_{2}DT_{\omega}^{n}(x),

where for the last inequality we just use the fact that
DT_{\omega}
is bounded for all
\omega\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, i.e.
C_{2}=1/\sup_{\omega\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\sup T_{\omega}^{\prime}
.

Now, by Lemma
3.2
, we can choose some constant
\tau>0
such that for all
x\in I\setminus\{0\}
and
\omega\in\Omega
, there exists some neighbourhood of
x
,
J_{n}(x)
, which
T_{\omega}^{n}
maps diffeomorphically onto some interval of length
\tau
and such that and such that
\mathcal{N}(T_{\omega}^{n}|J_{n}(x))\leq 1
. By choosing a sufficiently small
\tau
, we have
J_{n}(x)\subset\Lambda_{n}^{\omega}(U_{0})
for all
x\in\Lambda_{n}^{\omega}(U)
.

Let
\Lambda_{n}^{0}
correspond to the unperturbed orbit. Since
\Lambda_{\infty}^{0}(U_{0})
is of measure zero, then clearly for every
\rho>0
there exists some
N=N(\rho)\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that
|\Lambda_{N}^{0}(U_{0})|<\rho
. Now, if
\varepsilon>0
is sufficiently small, then for every
\omega\in\Omega
we have that
\Lambda_{N}^{\omega}(U_{0})
is contained in a small neighbourhood of
\Lambda_{N}^{0}(U_{0})
. Thus we also have
|\Lambda_{N}^{\omega}(U_{0})|<\rho
for all
\omega\in\Omega
.

Let us define
\eta_{k}=\sup_{\omega\in\Omega}|\Lambda_{kN}^{\omega}(U)|
. In order for us to obtain the desired result, clearly we must show that
\eta_{k+1}<\frac{1}{2}\eta_{k}
. Recall that the elements of the set
\mathcal{A}=\big\{J_{N}(x)|x\in\Lambda_{N}^{\omega}(U)\big\}
form a cover of
\Lambda_{N}^{\omega}(U)
. Thus, using Besicovitch’s covering lemma, we can say the following: there exists a constant
c_{1}
and there exist subsets
A_{1},\dots,A_{c_{1}}
of
I
such that for each
i=1,\dots,c_{1}
the elements of the set
\big\{J_{N}(x)|x\in A_{i}\big\}
are all disjoint. Furthermore, we can say

\Lambda_{N}^{\omega}(U)\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{c_{N}}\Big(\bigcup_{x\in A_{i}}J_{N}(x)\Big).

Note that this implies each point
x\in\Lambda_{N}^{\omega}(U)
can only be covered by at most
c_{1}
open neighbourhoods from these
c_{1}
sub-collections.

Clearly
\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U)\subset\Lambda_{N}^{\omega}(U)
, thus
\mathcal{A}
is also an open cover of
\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U)
, thus we can write

(19)

\displaystyle|\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U)|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{c_{1}}\sum_{x\in A_{i}}|J_{N}(x)\cap\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U)|

Therefore, we need to find an upper bound for
|J_{N}(x)\cap\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U)|
.

Trivially, we have

|J_{N}(x)\cap\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U)|=\frac{|T_{\omega}^{N}(J_{N}(x))|}{|T_{\omega}^{N}(J_{N}(x))|}\cdot\frac{|T_{\omega}^{N}(J_{N}(x)\cap\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U))|}{|T_{\omega}^{N}(J_{N}(x)\cap\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U))|}\cdot|J_{N}(x)\cap\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U)|

Recall that we have
1/|T_{\omega}^{N}(J_{N}(x))|=\tau^{-1}
by definition. Additionally, we have
T_{\omega}^{N}(\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U))\subset\Lambda_{kN}^{\sigma^{N}\omega}(U)
for all
\omega\in\Omega
, thus

|T_{\omega}^{N}(J_{N}(x)\cap\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U))|\leq|T_{\omega}^{N}(\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U))|\leq|\Lambda_{kN}^{\sigma^{N}\omega}(U))|\leq\eta_{k}.

Now, for the remaining
|T_{\omega}^{N}(J_{N}(x))|/|T_{\omega}^{N}(J_{N}(x)\cap\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U))|
, we want to use bounded distortion. However,
J_{N}(x)\cap\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U)
is not necessarily a connected interval, so we cannot directly use the usual mean value theorem combined with bounded distortion technique on it. Instead, one needs to split
J_{N}(x)\cap\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U)
into its connected components and then apply the technique and recombine the results. Thus, let
\{A_{i}\}_{i}
be the set of connected components of
J_{N}(x)\cap\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U)
. We can write

\displaystyle\frac{|T_{\omega}^{N}(J_{N}(x)\cap\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U))|}{|T_{\omega}^{N}(J_{N}(x))|}=\sum_{i}\frac{|T_{\omega}^{N}(A_{i})|}{|T_{\omega}^{N}(J_{N}(x))|}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i}e^{-1}\frac{|A_{i}|}{|J_{N}(x)|}=e^{-1}\frac{|J_{N}(x)\cap\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U)|}{|J_{N}(x)|}.

Taking the reciprocal of this and using the preceding two statements, we have

\displaystyle|J_{N}(x)\cap\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U)|

\displaystyle\leq e\tau^{-1}\eta_{k}\frac{|J_{N}(x)|}{|J_{N}(x)\cap\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U)|}\cdot|J_{N}(x)\cap\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U)|

(20)

\displaystyle=e\tau^{-1}\eta_{k}|J_{N}(x)|

Now, define

\hat{\Lambda}^{\omega}(U)=\bigcup_{x\in\Lambda_{N}^{\omega}(U)}J_{N}(x).

Recall that
J_{n}(x)\subset\Lambda_{n}^{\omega}(U_{0})
for all
x\in\Lambda_{n}^{\omega}(U)
. Thus,
|\hat{\Lambda}^{\omega}(U)|\leq|\Lambda_{N}^{\omega}(U_{0})|<\rho

Plugging in the previous inequality into (
19
), we have

\displaystyle|\Lambda_{(k+1)N}^{\omega}(U)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{c_{1}}\sum_{x\in A_{i}}e\tau^{-1}\eta_{k}|J_{N}(x)|

\displaystyle=e\tau^{-1}\eta_{k}c_{1}|\hat{\Lambda}^{\omega}(U)|<e\tau^{-1}\eta_{k}c_{1}\rho,

where in the second line we use the fact that any point
x\in\Lambda_{N}^{\omega}(U)
is covered by at most
c_{1}
open neighbourhoods
J_{N}(y)
with
y\in A_{1}\cup\dots\cup A_{c_{1}}
.

To finish the proof, all we need to do is take
\rho
to be small enough such that
e\tau^{-1}c_{1}\rho\leq 1/2
. Since the above inequality holds for all
\omega
, it also holds for
\eta_{k+1}
, thus giving us our desired result.
∎

.

There exists a constant
\kappa_{0}>0

We have the following backwards contraction lemma, which is an adaption of Lemma 1 in
[
18
]
:

Lemma 3.9
.

Assume that for sufficiently small
\delta>0
, we have a constant
\hat{v}(\delta)>1
, satisfying
\hat{v}(\delta)\to\infty
and
\hat{v}(\delta)\delta\to 0
as
\delta\to 0

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and every component

3.2.
Good and Bad Parameter sets

In order for us to use the random Young tower to obtain the desired result, we must ensure that we satisfy the expansion criteria. However, since the derivative goes to zero near the discontinuity at zero, if a typical orbit goes too close to the discontinuity too often, then we lose expansion. In this section, I prove that as
n\to\infty
, the measure of
\omega
’s for any given fixed
x
for which this happens decays exponentially.

Definition 3.18

(Return depth)
.

For some small
\delta>0
and for fixed
\omega\in\Omega
, we denote

r_{\delta}(x)=r_{\delta}(\omega,x):=\inf\big\{r\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\hskip 3.00003pt|\hskip 3.00003ptT_{\omega}^{\prime}(x)\cdot|x|\geq e^{-r}\delta\big\}.

Note that for shorthand we will write
r_{\delta}(T_{\omega}^{j}(x))=r_{\delta}(\sigma^{j}\omega,T_{\omega}^{j}(x))
.

One should note that by definition, one can show an equivalent condition for return depth using the order of singularity condition: if
r_{\delta}(x)=r^{*}
, then using the order of singularity condition we have

\displaystyle|x|\geq\frac{e^{-r^{*}}\delta}{T_{\omega}^{\prime}(x)}\geq\frac{e^{-r^{*}}\delta}{K_{2}|x|^{s-1}},

which implies

|x|^{s}\geq\frac{e^{-r^{*}}\delta}{K_{2}}\ratio\Leftrightarrow|x|\geq\frac{e^{-r^{*}}\delta^{\frac{1}{s}}}{K_{2}}.

We now need to define a ‘bad’ set of pairs
(\omega,x)\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}\times I
for a given iterate
n
and show that the measure of this set decays exponentially. To understand what we mean by ‘bad’, note the following proposition:

Proposition 3.19
.

There exist constants
c,C,\lambda>0
such that for any sufficiently small
\delta>0
and
0<\varepsilon\leq\delta
, and for all
\omega\in\Omega

and

In the proof in

It is clear that for fixed

Lemma 3.26

3.3.
Hyperbolic times and Hyperbolic Return Times

The idea of hyperbolic times originates in
[
2
]
and was then adapted to the random case in
[
15
]
. In
[
25
]
the definition of the return depth is changed slightly to be in terms of the product of the return depth and the derivative, rather than just the return depth. We give a formal definition here.

Definition 3.27

(Hyperbolic times)
.

Let us fix two constants
\delta>0
and
c^{\prime}>0
. An iterate
n
is considered a
(\delta,c^{\prime})
-hyperbolic time for
(\omega,x)
if for all
0\leq k\leq n-1
we have

(56)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=k}^{n-1}r_{\delta}(T_{\omega}^{j}(x))<c^{\prime}(n-k)

We should note two particular properties of
(\delta,c^{\prime})
-hyperbolic times stemming from this definition. The first is that if
n_{1}\geq 1
is a
(\delta,c^{\prime})
-hyperbolic time for
(\omega,x)
, and
n_{2}-n_{1}\geq 1
is a hyperbolic time for
(\sigma^{n_{1}}\omega,T_{\omega}^{n_{1}}(x))
, then
n_{2}
is also a hyperbolic time for
(\omega,x)
. Indeed, for any
n_{1}\leq k<n_{2}
, we just have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=k}^{n_{2}-1}r_{\delta}(T_{\omega}^{j}(x))=\sum_{j=k-n_{1}}^{n_{2}-n_{1}-1}r_{\delta}(T_{\sigma^{n_{1}}\omega}^{j}(x))<c^{\prime}((n_{2}-n_{1})-(k-n_{1}))=c^{\prime}(n_{2}-k).

Likewise, if
0\leq k<n_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=k}^{n_{2}-1}r_{\delta}(T_{\omega}^{j}(x))

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=k}^{n_{1}-1}r_{\delta}(T_{\omega}^{j}(x))+\sum_{j=n_{1}}^{n_{2}-1}r_{\delta}(T_{\omega}^{j}(x))

\displaystyle<c^{\prime}(n_{1}-k)+c^{\prime}(n_{2}-n_{1})=c^{\prime}(n_{2}-k).

The second property is that if
n\geq 1
is a
(\delta,c^{\prime})
-hyperbolic time for
(\omega,x)
, then
n-m
is a
(\delta,c^{\prime})
-hyperbolic time for
(\sigma^{m}\omega,T_{\omega}^{m}(x))
for each
1\leq m<n
. Indeed, we have for every
0\leq k<n-m

\displaystyle\sum_{j=k}^{n-m-1}r_{\delta}(T_{\sigma^{m}\omega}^{j}(x))=\sum_{j=k+m}^{n-1}r_{\delta}(T_{\omega}^{j}(x))<c^{\prime}(n-(k+m))=c^{\prime}((n-m)-k)).

We denote by
h(\omega,x)
the first
(\delta,c^{\prime})
-hyperbolic time for
(\omega,x)
. We want to show that the set of
(\omega,x)
’s that have not experienced a
(\delta,c^{\prime})
-hyperbolic time by time
n
, i.e.
h(\omega,x)>n
, decays exponentially as
n
goes to infinity:

Lemma 3.28
.

For any fixed
0<\delta<1
and
c^{\prime}>c
, all
(\omega,x)\notin B_{n}
experience a
(\delta,c^{\prime})
-hyperbolic time smaller than or equal to
n
. Furthermore, there exist some
C=C(\delta)
and
\gamma=\gamma(\delta)
such that, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, we have

(\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}\times\text{Leb})\big(\big\{(\omega,x)\hskip 3.00003pt|\hskip 3.00003pth(\omega,x)>n\big\}\big)\leq Ce^{-\gamma n}.

Before we prove this, we make use of
Pliss’s Lemma
:

Lemma 3.29

(Pliss’s Lemma)
.

Let us fix some constants
A\geq c_{2}>c_{1}
, and set
\theta=(c_{2}-c_{1})/(A-c_{1})
. For any real numbers
a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{n-1}\leq A
satisfying

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_{j}>c_{2}n,

there exists some constant
\ell\geq\theta n
and integers
1\leq n_{1}<n_{2}<\dots<n_{\ell}\leq n
such that, for every
i=1,2,\dots,\ell
and every
0\leq k<n_{i}
, we have

\sum_{j=k}^{n_{i}-1}a_{j}>c_{1}(n_{i}-k).

Proof of Lemma
3.28
.

We follow the same steps as in
[
25
]
. First, we want to show that
(\omega,x)\notin E_{n}
implies that
h(\omega,x)\leq n
. To this end, we set
a_{i}=c-r_{\delta}(T_{\omega}^{i}(x))
. By definition, for any
(\omega,x)\notin E_{n}
, we have
\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_{j}>0
. For any
c^{\prime}>c
, if we set
A=c
,
c_{2}=0
and
c_{1}=c-c^{\prime}<0
(and thus
A\geq c_{2}>c_{1}
), we can apply Pliss’s Lemma: there exists some integer
1\leq n_{1}\leq n
such that for all
0\leq k<n_{1}
, we have

\sum_{j=k}^{n_{1}-1}a_{j}>c_{1}(n_{1}-k)=(c-c^{\prime})(n_{1}-k).

Using how we have defined
a_{i}
, this can be rewritten as

\sum_{j=k}^{n_{1}-1}r_{\delta}(T_{\omega}^{j}(x))<c^{\prime}(n_{1}-k).

Thus,
n_{1}
is a
(\delta,c^{\prime})
-hyperbolic time for
(\omega,x)
.

For the tails of first hyperbolic times, we simply use the contrapositive that
h(\omega,x)>n
implies
(\omega,x)\in E_{n}
, which we already know from the previous proposition decays exponentially. Thus, the proof is complete.
∎

Remark 3.30
.

The above lemma implies that almost every
(\omega,x)\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}\times I
has infinitely many hyperbolic times. Furthermore, the proof of the above not only implies that if
(\omega,x)\in I\setminus E_{n}
there will be at least one hyperbolic time less than or equal to
n
, but between
1
and
n
there will in fact be at least
(1-\frac{c}{c^{\prime}})n
hyperbolic times. This means that, provided
(\omega,x)
is not in
E_{n}
at time
n
, we can choose whatever lower bound for proportion of times that must be hyperbolic times that we like, depending on our choice of
c
and
c^{\prime}
.

Now, for our tower construction to work, we must satisfy the expansion, Markov and bounded distortion conditions. We prove these properties in the following proposition:

Proposition 3.31
.

For some fixed
c^{\prime}\in(c,\kappa)
, assume that
(\omega,x)
has a
(\delta,c^{\prime})
-hyperbolic time
n
. There exists some open neighborhood
V_{x,n}^{\omega}
of
x
and some constant
\delta_{0}
dependent on
\delta
such that for all
0\leq k<n
, we have the that the map

T_{\sigma^{m}\omega}^{n-k}:T_{\omega}^{k}(V_{x,n}^{\omega})\to B(T_{\omega}^{n}(x),\delta_{0})

is a diffeomorphism (i.e. the map is Markov) of bounded distortion. Furthermore, there exist constants
C,\lambda^{\prime}>0
such that, for all elements
y\in V_{x,n}^{\omega}
, we have

DT_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}(T_{\omega}^{k}(y))\geq Ce^{\lambda^{\prime}(n-k)/2}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{N}(T_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}|T_{\omega}^{k}(V_{x,n}^{\omega}))<1.

Proof.

Let us fix some
0\leq k\leq n-1
. First, we prove that there exists constants
C,\lambda^{\prime}>0
such that

(57)

\displaystyle DT_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}(T_{\omega}^{k}(x))\geq Ce^{\lambda^{\prime}(n-k)}.

We note that even though we know that
n
is a
(\delta,c^{\prime})
-hyperbolic time for
(\omega,x)
, we do not know anything about the positions of
T_{\omega}^{k}(x)
and
T_{\omega}^{n}(x)
, and in particular we do not know if they are in
\tilde{B}(\delta)
. We need to consider all possible cases.

The simplest possible case is when
T_{\omega}^{k}(x)\notin B(\delta)
and there are no return times between iterate
k
and
n
, regardless of if
n
is a return time. In this case, we can simply directly apply Proposition
3.1
to obtain

DT_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}(T_{\omega}^{k}(x))\geq Ce^{\kappa(n-k)}.

or if
T_{\omega}^{n}(x)\in B(\delta)
then

DT_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}(T_{\omega}^{k}(x))\geq e^{\kappa(n-k)}.

However, if there are return times between iterates
n
and
k
or if
T_{\omega}^{k}(x)\in B(\delta)
then we need to use Lemma
3.20
.

As in Lemma
3.20
, let
\Gamma
denote the number of returns between iterates
k
and
n
, and let
\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{\Gamma}
be those returns. Setting
y_{k}=T_{\omega}^{k}(x)
and assuming that
y_{k}\in\tilde{B}(\delta)
, by Lemma
3.20
we have

\log\Big(\frac{DT_{\omega}^{n-k}(y_{k})|y_{k}||T_{\omega}^{r_{1}}(y_{k})|\dots|T_{\omega}^{r_{\Gamma}}(y_{k})|}{|\tilde{B}(\delta)|^{\Gamma}}\Big)\geq\log C+\kappa(n-k)-\sum_{j=0}^{n-k-1}r_{\delta}(T_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{j}(y_{k})).\\
\\

Or equivalently

DT_{\omega}^{n-k}(y_{k})\geq C\exp\Big(\kappa(n-k)-\sum_{j=0}^{n-k-1}r_{\delta}(T_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{j}(y_{k}))\Big)

Now, we adapt the proof of Proposition
3.19
. Using the definition of
n
being a
(\delta,c^{\prime})
-hyperbolic time, we have

\sum_{j=0}^{n-k-1}r_{\delta}(T_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{j}(y_{k}))=\sum_{j=k}^{n-1}r_{\delta}(T_{\omega}^{j}(x))<c^{\prime}(n-k).

Thus, we have

\exp(\kappa(n-k)-\sum_{i=n-k}^{n-1}r_{\delta}(T_{\omega}^{i}(y_{k})))\geq\exp(\kappa(n-k)-c^{\prime}(n-k)).

Setting
\lambda^{\prime}=\kappa-c^{\prime}
, we therefore have

DT_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}(y_{k})\geq e^{\lambda^{\prime}n}.

Likewise, for the case where
y_{k}\notin\tilde{B}(\delta)
, we just follow the same steps as in the proof of Proposition
3.19
: setting
\nu
as the first return time for
(\sigma^{k}\omega,y_{k})
, we have

\displaystyle DT_{\sigma^{k+\nu}\omega}^{n-(k+\nu)}(y_{k+\nu})

\displaystyle\geq\exp\big({\kappa(n-(k+\nu))-\sum_{i=0}^{n-k-\nu-1}r_{\delta}(T_{\sigma^{k+\nu}\omega}^{i}(y_{k+\nu}))}\big)

\displaystyle=\exp\big({\kappa(n-(k+r))-\sum_{i=k+\nu}^{n-1}r_{\delta}(T_{\omega}^{i}(x))}\big)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\big({\kappa(n-(k+r))-\sum_{i=k}^{n-1}r_{\delta}(T_{\omega}^{i}(x))}\big)

\displaystyle>\exp(\kappa(n-(k+\nu))-c^{\prime}(n-k)\big).

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda^{\prime}(n-k)-\kappa\nu}.

Then using Proposition
3.19
we have

DT_{\omega}^{k+\nu}(y_{k})\geq Ce^{\kappa\nu},

and thus

DT_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}(y_{k})=DT_{\sigma^{k+\nu}\omega}^{n-(k+\nu)}(y_{k+\nu})\cdot DT_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{\nu}(y_{k})\geq Ce^{\lambda^{\prime}(n-k)}.

Thus, inequality (
57
) holds, where we assume for convience we just assume
\lambda^{\prime}\leq\kappa
.

Now, we choose
\delta_{0}>0
such that
C^{\prime}C^{-1}K_{2}\delta_{0}\delta^{1/s}<\lambda^{\prime}/2
, where
C^{\prime}
is the constant from the admissibility condition,
C
is from Proposition
3.19
, and
s
is the order of singularity. Notice that the definition of hyperbolic time implies that for each
0\leq k\leq n-1
, we have
r_{\delta}(y_{k})<c^{\prime}(n-k)
, which therefore implies

(58)

\displaystyle|T_{\omega}^{k}(x)|>\frac{1}{K_{2}}\delta^{\frac{1}{s}}e^{-c^{\prime}(n-k)}.

This implies that for any
0\leq k\leq n-1
and
y\in B(T_{\omega}^{k}(x),C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\lambda^{\prime}(n-k)/2})
, we have
|x-y|<|x|
. Indeed, from the condition on
\delta_{0}
, we have

\delta_{0}<\frac{C}{K_{2}}\frac{\lambda^{\prime}\delta^{s}}{2C^{\prime}}<\frac{C}{K_{2}}\delta^{\frac{1}{s}},

where we assume we have taken
\delta
small enough that
\lambda^{\prime}\delta^{s}/2C^{\prime}<\delta^{1/s}
. Thus, we have

(59)

\displaystyle C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}(n-k)}<\frac{1}{K_{2}}\delta^{\frac{1}{s}}e^{-c^{\prime}(n-k)},

where we assume that we have chosen
c^{\prime}
sufficiently small such that
0<c^{\prime}<\kappa/3
. This means we can use the admissibility condition in the following way:

\displaystyle\Big|\log\frac{DT_{\sigma^{k}\omega}(T_{\omega}^{k}(x))}{DT_{\sigma^{k}\omega}(y)|}

\displaystyle\Big|\leq C^{\prime}\frac{|T_{\omega}^{k}(x)-y|}{|T_{\omega}^{k}(x)|}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\frac{C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\lambda^{\prime}(n-k)/2}}{\frac{1}{K_{2}}\delta^{\frac{1}{s}}e^{-c^{\prime}(n-k)}}

\displaystyle=C^{\prime}C^{-1}K_{2}\delta_{0}\delta^{\frac{1}{s}}e^{-(\lambda^{\prime}/2-c^{\prime})(n-k)}<\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}.

Clearly, this indicates

(60)

\displaystyle T_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{\prime}(y)>e^{-\lambda^{\prime}/2}T_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{\prime}(T_{\omega}^{k}(x)).

Now, let us consider the case when
k=n-1
. Then by Proposition
3.19
and inequality (
60
), for all
y\in B(T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x),C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}})
we have

\displaystyle DT_{\sigma^{n-1}\omega}(y)>e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}}DT_{\sigma^{n-1}\omega}(T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x))\geq Ce^{\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}}.

This clearly means that, if we consider some subinterval
J\subset B(T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x),C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}})
, since the above lower bound on the derivative applies to all
y\in B(T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x),C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}})
, we have

|T_{\sigma^{n-1}\omega}(J)|\geq Ce^{\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}}|J|.

Thus, taking
J=B(T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x),\delta_{0}e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}})
itself, we have

|T_{\sigma^{n-1}\omega}(J)|\geq Ce^{\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}}C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}}=\delta_{0},

and therefore

(61)

\displaystyle B(T_{\omega}^{n}(x),\delta_{0})\subset T_{\sigma^{n-1}\omega}(B(T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x),C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}})).

Now, clearly we want to show that for our fixed
k
we have

(62)

\displaystyle B(T_{\omega}^{n}(x),\delta_{0})\subset T_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}(B(T_{\omega}^{k}(x),C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}(n-k)})).

This is automatically true for
k=n-1
by the calculations above. We now prove this for all
0\leq k<n-1
.

For the case,
k=n-2
, we use proof by contradiction and assume that (
62
) is not true. Then there exists a point
z\in(T_{\sigma^{n-2}\omega}^{2})^{-1}(B(T_{\omega}^{n}(x),\delta_{0}))
such that
|T_{\omega}^{n-2}(x)-z|=C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\lambda^{\prime}}
. Without loss of generality, assume
z<T_{\omega}^{k}(x)
. For any point
y\in(z,T_{\omega}^{n-2}(x))
, clearly we have

y\in B(T_{\omega}^{n-2}(x),C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\lambda^{\prime}}).

In addition, trivially by construction we also have

y\in(T_{\sigma^{n-2}\omega}^{2})^{-1}(B(T_{\omega}^{n}(x),\delta_{0})),

which implies

T_{\sigma^{n-2}\omega}(y)=T_{\omega}^{n-1}(y^{*})\in(T_{\sigma^{n-1}\omega})^{-1}(B(T_{\omega}^{n}(x),\delta_{0}))\subset B(T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x),C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}}),

where
y^{*}
is just some point that satisfies
T_{\omega}^{n-2}(y^{*})=y
, and where the subset in the above statement comes from simply applying
(T_{\sigma^{n-1}\omega})^{-1}
to both sides of (
61
). Thus, for any
y
and
T_{\sigma^{n-2}\omega}(y)
we have by inequaltiy (
60
)

\displaystyle DT_{\sigma^{n-2}\omega}(y)>e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}}DT_{\sigma^{n-2}\omega}(T_{\omega}^{n-2}(x))

and

\displaystyle DT_{\sigma^{n-1}\omega}(T_{\sigma^{n-2}\omega}(y))>e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}}DT_{\sigma^{n-1}\omega}(T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x)).

Thus, we have

\displaystyle DT_{\sigma^{n-2}\omega}^{2}(y)

\displaystyle=DT_{\sigma^{n-1}\omega}(T_{\sigma^{n-2}\omega}(y))\cdot DT_{\sigma^{n-2}\omega}(y)

\displaystyle>e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}}DT_{\sigma^{n-1}\omega}(T_{\omega}^{n-1}(x))\cdot e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}}DT_{\sigma^{n-2}\omega}(T_{\omega}^{n-2}(x))

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda^{\prime}}DT_{\sigma^{n-2}\omega}^{2}(T_{\omega}^{n-2}(x))\geq Ce^{\lambda^{\prime}}.

This therefore means we have

\displaystyle|T_{\omega}^{n}(x)-T_{\sigma^{n-2}\omega}^{2}(z)|>Ce^{\lambda^{\prime}}|T_{\omega}^{n-2}(x)-z|=Ce^{\lambda^{\prime}}C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\lambda^{\prime}}=\delta_{0}.

But this implies that
z\notin(T_{\sigma^{n-2}\omega}^{2})^{-1}(B(T_{\omega}^{n}(x),\delta_{0}))
, which contradicts our assumption on
z
. Thus, we must have

B(T_{\omega}^{n}(x),\delta_{0})\subset T_{\sigma^{n-2}\omega}^{2}(B(T_{\omega}^{n-2}(x),C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\lambda^{\prime}})).

One can prove the rest of the cases
0\leq k<n-2
by induction using the same steps as above. We assume that (
62
) has already been proven for all
k<j\leq n-1
. We then assume that (
62
) does not hold for
k
for proof by contradiction. Therefore, we can choose a point
z\in(T_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k})^{-1}(B(T_{\omega}^{n}(x),\delta_{0}))
such that
|z-T_{\omega}^{k}(x)|=C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}(n-k)}
. We can then show that for all
y\in(z,T_{\omega}^{k}(x))
we have

T_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{\ell}(y)\in B(T_{\omega}^{k+\ell}(x),C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\lambda^{\prime}(n-(k+\ell))/2})

for
\ell=0,1,\dots,n-(k+1)
by construction and using the fact(
62
) has already been proven for
k<j\leq n-1
. We can therefore use (
60
) to obtain the lower bound on the derivate

DT_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}(y)>Ce^{\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}(n-k)},

which can then be used to show

|T_{\omega}^{n}(x)-T_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}(z)|>\delta_{0},

thereby contradicting our assumption and proving our desired result.

We denote the set

V_{x,n}^{\omega}=(T_{\omega}^{n})^{-1}\big(B(T_{\omega}^{n}(x),\delta_{0})\big)\cap B(x,C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}n}).

First, we want to show that
T_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}|_{T_{\omega}^{k}(V_{x,n}^{\omega})}:T_{\omega}^{k}(V_{x,n}^{\omega})\to B(T_{\omega}^{n}(x),\delta_{0})
is a diffeomorphism for all
0\leq k\leq n-1
. It is not immediately obvious why this should be the case. Like in the unimodal case, for all
\omega\in\Omega
,
T_{\omega}
is clearly not bijective. In the unimodal case, the map restricted to some subinterval
J\subset I
is not bijective when
J
covers any two-sided neighborhood of
0
. However, in our case
T_{\omega}
would have to be restricted to some interval
J
such that there exists a
0\leq k<n
such that
T_{\omega}^{k}(J)
covers all or nearly all of one half of
I
and a sufficiently large part of the other half, depending on the values of
T_{\sigma^{k}\omega}(1)
and
T_{\sigma^{k}\omega}(-1)
. Despite this, because of the discontinuity at
0
, there is the possibility that if there is a
0\leq k<n
such that
T_{\omega}^{k}(J)
covers any neighbourhood of
0
, then
T_{\omega}^{k+1}(J)
will be split into two components, and the images of these components under further iterations may intersect. Thus, to prove this does not happen for
n
and
V_{x,n}^{\omega}
, we need to show that
T_{\omega}^{k}(V_{x,n}^{\omega})
never intersects
0
and is contained entirely in one half of
I
or the other for all
0\leq k<n-1
.

But this is just a consequence of the fact that for all
0\leq k\leq n-1
and
y\in B(T_{\omega}^{k}(x),C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\lambda^{\prime}(n-k)/2})
, we have
|x-y|<|x|
. Indeed, notice that using (
62
) we have

\displaystyle T_{\omega}^{k}(V_{x,n}^{\omega})

\displaystyle\subset(T_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}|_{T_{\omega}^{k}(V_{x,n}^{\omega})})^{-1}\big(B(T_{\omega}^{n}(x),\delta_{0}\big)\cap T_{\omega}^{k}\big(B(x,C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}(n-k)})\big)

\displaystyle\subset(T_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}|_{T_{\omega}^{k}(V_{x,n}^{\omega})})^{-1}\big(B(T_{\omega}^{n}(x),\delta_{0}\big)

\displaystyle\subset(T_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}|_{T_{\omega}^{k}(V_{x,n}^{\omega})})^{-1}\big(T_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}(B(T_{\omega}^{k}(x),C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}(n-k)})))

\displaystyle=B(T_{\omega}^{k}(x),C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}(n-k)}).

We now need to show that for each
0\leq k<n-1
, the set
T_{\omega}^{k}(V_{x,n}^{\omega})\subset B(T_{\omega}^{k}(x),C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}(n-k)})
is connected and does not intersect 0. Indeed, this follows from
58
and
59
, which respectively imply that
T_{\omega}^{k}(x)
is a minimum distance away from 0 and that the distance between
T_{\omega}^{k}(x)
and
y\in B(T_{\omega}^{k}(x),C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}(n-k)})
is small enough that
y
must be on the same side of
I
as
T_{\omega}^{k}(x)
. Thus, we have the diffeomorphism property.

For the bounded distortion, we make use of the admissibility condition again: for all
y\in T_{\omega}^{k}(V_{x,n}^{\omega})
and all
0\leq k<n
, we have

\displaystyle\Big|\log\frac{DT_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}(T_{\omega}^{k}(x))}{DT_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}(y)}\Big|=\sum_{j=0}^{n-k-1}\Big|\log\frac{DT_{\sigma^{k+j}\omega}(T_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{j}(y))}{DT_{\sigma^{k+j}\omega}(T_{\omega}^{k+j}(x))}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{n-k-1}C^{\prime}\frac{|T_{\omega}^{k+j}(x)-T_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{j}(y)|}{|T_{\omega}^{k+j}(x)|}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{n-k-1}C^{\prime}\frac{C^{-1}\delta_{0}e^{-\lambda^{\prime}(n-k-j)/2}}{\frac{1}{K_{2}}\delta^{\frac{1}{s}}e^{-c^{\prime}(n-k-j)}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}C^{-1}K_{2}\delta_{0}\delta^{-\frac{1}{s}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}e^{-(\lambda^{\prime}/2-c^{\prime})j}<1,

where we have placed an extra condition on
\delta_{0}
that

\delta_{0}<\Big(C^{\prime}C^{-1}K_{2}\delta^{-\frac{1}{s}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}e^{-(\lambda^{\prime}/2-c^{\prime})j}\Big)^{-1}.

Thus, for all
0\leq k<n
and all
y\in T_{\omega}^{k}(V_{x,n}^{\omega})
, we have

e^{-1}DT_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}(y)<DT_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}(T_{\omega}^{k}(x))<eDT_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}(y).

Furthermore, this combined with (
57
) prove the expansion property for all
0\leq k<n
and all
y\in T_{\omega}^{k}(V_{x,n}^{\omega})
:

\displaystyle DT_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}(y)>e^{-1}DT_{\sigma^{k}\omega}^{n-k}(T_{\omega}^{k}(x))\geq Ce^{\lambda^{\prime}(n-k)},

where we just absorb the
e^{-1}
term into the constant
C
. This completes the proof.
∎

3.4.
Hyperbolic Return Times

Now we introduce the concept of hyperbolic
return times
:

Definition 3.32

(Hyperbolic Return Times)
.

Let us set sufficiently small
\kappa,c>0
and sufficiently small
\delta,\delta_{0}(\delta)>0
according the previous lemmas and propositions. For a fixed
c^{\prime}\in(c,\kappa)
, we consider an iterate
n
to be a
(\delta,c^{\prime})
-hyperbolic return time for
(\omega,x)
if
n
is a
(\delta,c^{\prime})
-hyperbolic time and
T_{\omega}^{j}(x)\in\tilde{B}(\delta_{0}/2)
.

We denote
h_{\omega}^{*}(x)
as the first hyperbolic return time for the point
x
under
T_{\omega}
, and we denote
\hat{R}_{\omega}(x)=\min\{s\geq 1\hskip 3.00003pt|\hskip 3.00003ptT_{\omega}^{s}(x)\in\tilde{B}(\delta_{0}/2)\}
. We wish to show that almost every point has a finite hyperbolic return time:

Proposition 3.33
.

For sufficiently small
\delta>0
, there exist constants
C_{0}=C_{0}(\delta)>0
and
\gamma_{0}=\gamma_{0}(\delta)>0
such that, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\big(\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}\times\mathrm{Leb}\big)\big(\big\{(\omega,x):h^{*}(\omega,x)>n\big\}\big)\leq C_{0}e^{-\gamma n}

Proof.

First, we note the following:

(63)

\displaystyle\big\{h_{\omega}^{*}(x)>n\big\}\subset\big\{h_{\omega}(x)

\displaystyle>n/2\big\}\cup\Big(\bigcup_{j=1}^{n/2}\big\{h_{\omega}(x)=j,\hat{R}_{\sigma^{j}\omega}(T_{\omega}^{j}(x))>n/2\}\Big).

Indeed, if
h_{\omega}(x)>n>n/2
, then that automatically implies
h_{\omega}^{*}(x)>n
, and thus
x\in\big\{h_{\omega}(x)>n/2\big\}
. Likewise, if
n/2<h_{\omega}(x)\leq n
, then we also have
x\in\big\{h_{\omega}(x)>n/2\big\}
. Finally, if
h_{\omega}(x)=j
with
0<j\leq n/2
, we must have
\hat{R}_{\sigma^{j}\omega}(T_{\omega}^{j}(x))>n-j\geq n/2
.

We have already shown that
\{h_{\omega}(x)>n/2\}
decays exponentially in Proposition
3.28
, so all that remains to do is to estimate an upper bound for the second union of sets.

Recall from Lemma
3.6
the set

\Lambda_{n}^{\omega}(U)=\big\{x\in I:\hskip 3.00003ptT_{\omega}^{j}(x)\notin U\text{ for }0\leq j\leq n-1\big\}.

Let us denote
\Lambda_{n}^{\omega}(\delta)=\Lambda_{n}^{\omega}(B(\delta))
. We have that for any sufficiently small
\delta>0
, all
\omega\in\Omega
, and all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, if a subinterval
J\subset I_{\pm}
maps diffeomorphically under
j\geq 1
iterations onto some interval of length
\tau>0
and has bounded distortion, then

|J\cap\Lambda_{n}^{\omega}(\delta)|\leq Ce^{-\eta n}|J|.

Indeed, simply recall inequality (
20
) combined with the exponential decay result of Lemma
3.6
.

Now, let us denote
H_{j}(\omega)=\big\{x\in I\hskip 3.00003pt|\hskip 3.00003pth_{\omega}(x)=j\big\}
. Recall from Proposition
3.31
that if
j
is a hyperbolic time for every
x\in H_{j}(\omega)
, then for every
x\in H_{j}(\omega)
there will exist a neighbourhood
V_{x,j}^{\omega}
of
x
such that the map
T_{\omega}^{j}|_{V_{x,j}^{\omega}}:V_{x,j}^{\omega}\to B(T_{\omega}^{j}(x),\delta_{0})
is diffeomorphic and has bounded distortion. Using this, we note that
e^{-1/2}|B(T_{\omega}^{j}(x),\delta_{0})|\leq|V_{x,j}^{\omega}|\leq e^{1/2}|B(T_{\omega}^{j}(x),\delta_{0})|
, and thus

\displaystyle\frac{\big|\big\{y\in V_{x,j}^{\omega}\hskip 3.00003pt|\hskip 3.00003pt\hat{R}_{\sigma^{j}\omega}(T_{\omega}^{j}(x))>n/2\big\}\big|}{|V_{x,j}^{\omega}|}\leq e\frac{|B(T_{\omega}^{j}(x),\delta_{0})\cap\Lambda_{n/2}^{\sigma^{j}\omega}(\delta_{0}/2)|}{|B(T_{\omega}^{j}(x),\delta_{0})|}\leq e^{1-\eta n/2},

or equivalently

\big|\big\{y\in V_{x,j}^{\omega}\hskip 3.00003pt|\hskip 3.00003pt\hat{R}_{\sigma^{j}\omega}(T_{\omega}^{j}(x))>n/2\big\}\big|\leq e^{1-\eta n/2}|V_{x,j}^{\omega}|.

Now, clearly the set
\big\{V_{x,j}^{\omega}\hskip 3.00003pt|\hskip 3.00003ptx\in H_{j}(\omega)\big\}
forms an open covering of
H_{j}(\omega)
. Thus, similar to the proof of Lemma
3.6
, we know from the Besicovitch covering lemma that there exist finite subsets
A_{1},\dots,A_{c_{1}}
of
H_{j}(\omega)
such that for every
i=1,\dots,c_{1}
, all
V_{x,j}^{\omega}
are disjoint for every
x\in A_{i}
. Furthermore, we have

H_{j}(\omega)\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{c_{N}}\bigcup_{x\in A_{i}}V_{x,j}^{\omega}.

Using these, we have

\displaystyle\big|\big\{y\in H_{j}(\omega)\hskip 3.00003pt|\hskip 3.00003pt\hat{R}_{\sigma^{j}\omega}(T_{\omega}^{j}(x))>n/2\big\}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\Big|\bigcup_{i=1}^{c_{N}}\bigcup_{x\in A_{i}}\big\{y\in V_{x,j}^{\omega}\hskip 3.00003pt|\hskip 3.00003pt\hat{R}_{\sigma^{j}\omega}(T_{\omega}^{j}(x))>n/2\big\}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{c_{1}}\sum_{x\in A_{i}}\big|\big\{y\in V_{x,j}^{\omega}\hskip 3.00003pt|\hskip 3.00003pt\hat{R}_{\sigma^{j}\omega}(T_{\omega}^{j}(x))>n/2\big\}\big|

\displaystyle\leq e^{1-\eta n/2}\sum_{i=1}^{c_{1}}\sum_{x\in A_{i}}|V_{x,j}^{\omega}|\leq Ce^{1-\eta n/2}|H_{j}(\omega)|.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\big|\big\{h_{\omega}^{*}(x)>n\big\}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\big|\big\{h_{\omega}(x)>n/2\}|+\sum_{j=1}^{n/2}\big|\big\{h_{\omega}(x)=j\hskip 3.00003pt|\hskip 3.00003pt\hat{R}_{\sigma^{j}\omega}(T_{\omega}^{j}(x))>n/2\big\}\big|

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{-\gamma n/2}+Ce^{1-\eta n/2}\sum_{j=1}^{n/2}|H_{j}(\omega)|\leq Ce^{-\gamma_{0}n}.

∎

3.5.
Construction of the Return Partition

Now, we use the previously state results regarding hyperbolic return times in order to construct the return partition for our random tower. First, we set
\delta^{\prime}=\delta_{0}/2
. Now, by definition, if
n
is a
(\delta,c^{\prime})
-hyperbolic return time, then
T_{\omega}^{n}(x)\in\tilde{B}(\delta^{\prime})
. Furthermore, if
\partial I\cap V_{x,n}^{\omega}=\emptyset
, then
T_{\omega}^{n}|_{V_{x,n}^{\omega}}:V_{x,n}^{\omega}\to B(T_{\omega}^{n}(x),\delta_{0})
is an expanding diffeomorphism with
\mathcal{N}(T_{\omega}^{n}|_{V_{x,n}^{\omega}})<1
. Moreover, since
\tilde{B}(\delta^{\prime})\subset B(T_{\omega}^{m}(x),\delta_{0})=B(T_{\omega}^{m}(x),2\delta^{\prime})
, we have
\tilde{B}(\delta^{\prime})\subset T_{\omega}^{n}(V_{x,n}^{\omega}).

Now, we define
J_{x,n}^{\omega}=(T_{\omega}^{n})^{-1}(B(\delta^{\prime}))\cap V_{x,n}^{\omega}
. Cleary if
J_{x,n}^{\omega}\cap\{\pm 1\}=\emptyset
, then the mapping
T_{\omega}^{n}|_{J_{x,n}^{\omega}}:J_{x,n}^{\omega}\to B(\delta^{\prime})
inherits the properties of being diffeomorphic, expanding and of bounded distortion. Furthermore, recalling Proposition
3.31
that for all
y\in V_{x,n}^{\omega}
we have

DT_{\omega}^{n}(y)\geq Ce^{\lambda^{\prime}n/2}.

Thus, the maximum size of
J_{x,n}^{\omega}
is

|J_{x,n}^{\omega}|\leq\frac{|B(\delta^{\prime})|}{Ce^{\lambda^{\prime}n/2}}=C\delta^{\prime}e^{-\lambda^{\prime}n/2}.

We prove the following proposition that both helps us to define the return partition and also gives us the aperiodicity condition:

Proposition 3.34
.

There exist four points
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\in B(\delta^{\prime})
with respective associated
(\delta,c^{\prime})
-hyperbolic return times

\text{g.c.d.}(t^{*}_{i})=1

\varepsilon>0

Proof.

First, we begin by proving aperiodicity in general. Recall that our original unperturbed contracting Lorenz system is mixing. Thus, we can proceed as in [Remark 3.14,
[
4
]
]. Since the unperturbed map admits a unique invariant probability measure, it can be lifted to the induced map over
\Delta^{\ast}
. Moreover, the lifted measure is invariant and mixing for the tower map. Therefore, there exists a partition
\mathcal{Q}^{0}
of
B(\delta^{\prime})
and a return time

such that
\tau_{i}^{0}=\tau^{0}(Q_{i})
,

. Now, by shrinking
\varepsilon

N_{0}

\mathcal{Q}^{\omega}

Now, assuming

Proof of Proposition

Case 1:

0<\theta_{3}<1
such that

e^{2\mu(c_{1})}C^{c_{1}}e^{-\gamma c_{3}}<\theta_{3}.

Thus, we have

\Big|\Big\{x\in I\hskip 3.00003pt|\hskip 3.00003pt\sum_{j=1}^{t(x)}(h^{*}_{p_{j}(x)+1}-h^{*}_{p_{j}(x)})\Big\}\Big|\leq c_{3}M^{-1}n^{2}(e^{2\mu(c_{1})}C^{c_{1}}e^{-\gamma c_{3}})^{n}<C\theta_{3}^{n}.

Now, for the case where
\sum_{j=1}^{m}(h^{*}_{k_{j}+1}-h^{*}_{k_{j}})>c_{3}n
instead, we simply need to set
c_{2}=c_{1}
and, using the argument from case 2, this gives us

\Big|\Big\{x\in I\hskip 3.00003pt|\hskip 3.00003pt\sum_{j=1}^{t(x)}(h^{*}_{k_{j}(x)+1}-h^{*}_{k_{j}(x)})\Big\}\Big|\leq C\theta_{3}^{n}.

Case 4

Suppose that for our fixed
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, our fixed
x\in I\setminus\bigcup_{k\leq n}\bm{U}_{k}^{\omega}
is not in any of the previous three cases. We denote the set of such points as
\tilde{X}_{\omega}^{n}\subset I\setminus\bigcup_{k\leq n}\bm{U}_{k}^{\omega}
. Furthermore, we denote
\hat{X}_{\omega}^{n}=\hat{X}_{\omega}^{n}(t,\{p_{i}\},\{q_{i}\})=\tilde{X}_{\omega}^{n}\cap X_{\omega}(n,t,\{p_{i}\},\{q_{i}\})
.

By construction, for any
1\leq j\leq t
and any
V\in\mathcal{U}_{q_{j}}
, the number of elements of the set
\big\{J_{x,h^{*}_{p_{j}}}\hskip 3.00003pt|\hskip 3.00003ptx\in\tilde{X}_{\omega}^{n}\cap X_{\omega}(n,t,\{p_{i}\},\{q_{i}\})\big\}
is either
1
or
2
. Furthermore, using the same argument as in case 1 with bounded distortion, we can show that

\frac{|J_{x,h^{*}_{p_{j}}}|}{|V|}\leq Ce^{-\lambda_{0}(h^{*}_{p_{i}}-q_{i})}.

Thus, combining these two we obtain

\Big|\bigcup_{x\in\hat{X}_{\omega}^{n}}J_{x,h^{*}_{p_{j}}}\Big|\leq 2Ce^{-\lambda_{0}(h^{*}_{p_{i}}-q_{i})}|\bm{V}_{j}|.

Now, clearly we need to show that
|\bm{V}_{j+1}|
decays exponentially. Consider the case where
q_{j+1}>h^{*}_{p_{j}}
. Then for any
V\in\mathcal{V}_{q_{j}+1}
that intersects the set
J_{x,h^{*}_{p_{j}}}
, by case 1 we know that
V\subset(1+\alpha)J_{x,h^{*}_{p_{j}}}
. Plugging this into the above estimate, this gives us

|\bm{V}_{j+1}|\leq(1+\alpha)\Big|\bigcup_{x\in\hat{X}_{\omega}^{n}}J_{x,h^{*}_{p_{j}}}\Big|\leq 2(1+\alpha)Ce^{-\lambda_{0}(h^{*}_{p_{i}}-q_{i})}|\bm{V}_{j}|.

Alternatively, consider the case when
q_{j+1}\leq h^{*}_{p_{j}}
. Notice that for any
V\in\mathcal{V}_{j+1}
, if
V\cap J_{x,h^{*}_{p_{j}}}\neq\emptyset
, then we in fact have

V\cap(I\setminus J_{x,h^{*}_{p_{j}}})\neq\emptyset.

Indeed, by construction we have
T_{\omega}^{q_{j+1}}(V)=B(\delta^{\prime})
, which contains the point
0
, whereas
T_{\omega}^{q_{j+1}}(J_{x,h^{*}_{p_{j}}})
is bounded away from
0
. Thus, there are points in
T_{\omega}^{q_{j+1}}(V)
which are not in
T_{\omega}^{q_{j+1}}(J_{x,h^{*}_{p_{j}}})
, and therefore the same can be said about the original
V
and
J_{x,h^{*}_{p_{j}}}
. Thus, we have

|\bm{V}_{j+1}|\leq\Big|\bigcup_{x\in\hat{X}_{\omega}^{n}}J_{x,h^{*}_{p_{j}}}\Big|\leq 2Ce^{-\lambda_{0}(h^{*}_{p_{i}}-q_{i})}|\bm{V}_{j}|\leq 2Ce^{-\lambda_{0}(q_{i+1}-q_{i})}|\bm{V}_{j}|.

Combining this all together, we have

\displaystyle|\hat{X}_{\omega}^{n}|

\displaystyle\leq\Big|\bigcup_{x\in\hat{X}_{\omega}^{n}}J_{x,h^{*}_{p_{t}}}\Big|\leq Ce^{-\lambda_{0}(h^{*}_{p_{t}}-q_{t})}|\bm{V}_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}e^{-\lambda_{0}(h^{*}_{p_{t}}-q_{t-1})}|\bm{V}_{t-1}|e^{\max\{0,q_{t}-h^{*}_{p_{t-1}}\}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\vdots

\displaystyle\leq C^{t}e^{-\lambda_{0}(h^{*}_{p_{t}}-q_{1})}e^{\lambda_{0}\sum\max\{0,q_{i}-h^{*}_{p_{i-1}}\}}|\bm{V}_{1}|

\displaystyle\leq 2C^{c_{1}n}e^{-\lambda_{0}n}e^{\lambda_{0}(q_{1}+n-h^{*}_{p_{t}})}e^{\lambda_{0}\sum\max\{0,q_{i}-h^{*}_{p_{i-1}}\}}.

Clearly, we need to show that the last line above decays exponentially. Indeed, we will show that

(q_{1}+n-h^{*}_{p_{t}})+\sum\max\big\{0,q_{i}-h^{*}_{p_{i-1}}\big\}\leq 2c_{3}n.

For any
i<t
, let
p_{i+1}^{\prime}(x)=\min\big\{j>p_{i}\hskip 3.00003pt|\hskip 3.00003ptJ_{x,h^{*}_{k}}\cap\bm{V}_{i+1}\neq\emptyset\big\}
. By construction, this implies that
q_{i}<h^{*}_{p_{i}^{\prime}}
and that for any
p_{i}<k<p_{i+1}^{\prime}
we have
J_{x,h^{*}_{p_{j}}}\cap(\partial I\cup\partial B(\delta^{\prime}))\neq\emptyset
. Likewise, if
p_{s}\neq p
, then the same holds for any
p_{t}<k\leq p
. Thus, we can write

\displaystyle(q_{1}+n-h^{*}_{p_{t}})+\sum\max\big\{0,q_{i}-h^{*}_{p_{i-1}}\big\}

\displaystyle<(h^{*}_{p_{1}^{\prime}}+n-m_{p_{t}})+\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\max\big\{0,q_{i}-h^{*}_{p_{i-1}}\big\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{s}(h^{*}_{p_{i}+1}-h^{*}_{p_{i}})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(h^{*}_{k_{i}+1}-h^{*}_{k_{i}})\leq 2c_{3}n,

where we have used the fact that we assume our
x
is not in case 3. Thus, we can write

|\hat{X}_{\omega}^{n}|\leq 2(C^{c_{1}}e^{-(\lambda_{0}-2c_{3})})^{n}=2\theta_{4}^{n},

where we have set
0<c_{3}<\lambda_{0}/(2+\log C)
and set
\theta_{4}=C^{c_{1}}e^{-(\lambda_{0}-2c_{3})}
.

Now, since
\hat{X}_{\omega}^{n}=\hat{X}_{\omega}^{n}(t,\{p_{i}\},\{q_{i}\})
is only for specific values
s,\{p_{i}\},
and
\{q_{i}\}
, we need to sum this over all possible values. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\Big|\bigcup_{t\leq c_{1}n}\bigcup_{\{p_{i}\},\{q_{i}\}}\hat{X}_{\omega}^{n}(t,\{p_{i}\},\{q_{i}\})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t\leq c_{1}n}\sum_{\{p_{i}\},\{q_{i}\}}\big|\hat{X}_{\omega}^{n}(t,\{p_{i}\},\{q_{i}\})\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t\leq c_{1}n}\sum_{\{p_{i}\},\{q_{i}\}}2\theta_{4}^{n}\leq c_{1}n{n\choose c_{1}n}{n\choose c_{1}n}2\theta_{4}^{n}

\displaystyle\leq 2c_{1}n(e^{2\hat{\mu}(c_{1})}\theta_{4})^{n}\leq C_{4}\hat{\theta}_{4}^{n},

where
\hat{\mu}(c_{1})
is some suitably chosen constant satisfying
\lim_{c_{1}\to 0}\hat{\mu}(c_{1})=0
, and where we have chosen
c_{1}
small enough so that
\hat{\theta}_{4}=e^{2\hat{\mu}(c_{1})}\theta_{4}<1
.

Since we have now exhausted all possibilities for
x\in I\setminus\bigcup_{k\leq n}\bm{U}_{k}^{\omega}
, we therefore have the desired result:

\displaystyle\Big|I\setminus\bigcup_{k\leq n}\bm{U}_{k}^{\omega}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\theta_{1}^{n}+C_{2}\theta_{2}^{n}+C_{3}\theta_{3}^{n}+C_{4}\hat{\theta}_{4}^{n}\leq Ce^{-\gamma n}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Thus, we now have exponential tails of return times. This, combined with the random tower axioms having been satisfied, means that we can apply Theorem
A.1
to obtain exponential quenched decay of correlations on the random Young tower we have constructed. Furthermore, using the exact same arguments as at the end of Chapter 3, we can show that almost sure mixing on the random tower implies almost sure random mixing on the original system, thereby proving Theorem
2.1
.

Let us also define the following function spaces:

(67)
