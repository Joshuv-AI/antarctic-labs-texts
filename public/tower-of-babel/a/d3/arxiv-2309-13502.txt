Globally Solving a Class of Bilevel Programs with Spatial Price Equilibrium Constraints
Akshit Goyal, Jean-Philippe P. Richard

Abstract
Bilevel programs with spatial price equilibrium constraints are strategic models that consider a price competition at the lower level. These models find application in facility location-price models, optimal bidding in power networks, and integration of renewable energy sources in distribution networks. In this paper, for the case where the equilibrium at the lower level can be formulated as an optimization problem, we introduce an enhanced single-level formulation based on duality and show that its relaxation is stronger than the single-level formulation obtained using KKT conditions. Compared to the literature Friesz et al. 1988 ; Miller et al. 1992 , this new formulation ( i i ) is computationally friendly to global solution strategies using branch-and-bound, and ( i ​ i ii ) can tackle instances of larger size. Further, we develop a heuristic procedure to find feasible solutions inside of the branch-and-bound tree that is effective on instances of large size and produces solutions whose objective values are close to the relaxation bound. We demonstrate the benefits of this formulation and heuristic through an extensive numerical study on synthetic instances of Equilibrium Facility Location Miller et al. 1995 and on standard IEEE bus networks for planning renewable generation capacity under uncertainty.

1
Introduction

Bilevel programs incorporating spatial price equilibrium (SPE) constraints at the lower level are used to model competitive facility location on networks
Tobin and Friesz 1986
;
Friesz et al. 1988
;
Miller et al. 1995
and analyzing bidding decision of a generating firm in an electric power network
Hobbs et al. 2000
.
At the core of these bilevel programs lies the concept of SPE, which involves computing the supply price, demand price, and commodity flow in a network while satisfying the equilibrium condition that the demand price equals the supply price plus the transportation cost if there is a non-zero flow between a pair of demand and supply nodes.
In the literature, the general SPE problem has been formulated as a variational inequality (VI) problem
Florian and Los 1982
and several VI-based iterative solution procedures, such as Frank-Wolfe and projection methods, have been proposed
Friesz et al. 1984
;
Nagurney 1987
.
Algorithms based on complementarity formulations of the SPE problem have also been developed
Pang 1981
;
Pang and Lee 1981
.
We refer to
Nagurney 2021
for a comprehensive review of spatial price equilibria in networks.

The models we study in this paper utilize SPE as the lower-level problem within a bilevel program.
This approach is necessary to model how the upper-level decisions affect the equilibrium market price of a commodity, taking into account market competition.
The resulting equilibrium price then directly impacts the objective function at the upper-level.
In
Tobin and Friesz 1986
, the authors formulate a bilevel model of this type to locate a firm’s production facilities and to determine production decisions at the upper-level in order to maximize the firm’s profit, which depends on the prices arising from the resulting equilibrium at the lower level.
In
Friesz et al. 1988
, the authors argue the existence of solutions to this equilibrium facility location (EFL) model whereas
Tobin and Friesz 1986
;
Friesz et al. 1988
provide heuristic approaches which involve successive linearization of the nonlinear upper-level objective based on the sensitivity analysis results for VIs discussed in
Tobin 1986
.
The authors of
Miller et al. 1992
extend the work of
Tobin and Friesz 1986
by allowing for additional shipping decisions to be made at the upper-level.
These articles, however, focus on heuristic solution methods that do not provide guarantees on the quality of solutions obtained.
Further, these heuristics are tested on problem instances of small size.

In this paper, we present an approach to obtain globally optimal solutions to a class of bilevel programs with SPE constraints which encompasses the EFL application.
This paper makes the following contributions:

1.

We derive a new Duality-based single-level reformulation for bilevel programs with SPE constraints that is stronger than the KKT-based reformulation when the variational inequality of the lower level can be cast as an optimization problem.
This new reformulation has a provably bounded root node relaxation, which is advantageous for branch-and-bound.
This is in contrast with the KKT-based formulation, which often has an unbounded root relaxation as we argue theoretically and show computationally in Sections
3
and
4
, respectively.

2.

We conduct extensive numerical experiments on randomly generated instances of EFL
Tobin and Friesz 1986
of varying sizes.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first extensive computational study for this class of bilevel programs. This study establishes that the Duality-based formulation allows substantial computational speed ups.

3.

We introduce an application of bilevel SPE models that provides a novel approach to optimize renewable generation units planning (RGUP) in power distribution networks under uncertainty, extending
the work of

Zhang et al. 2017
.
We perform numerical experiments on standard IEEE bus networks that show the numerical potential of the approach.

4.

We develop a generic rounding heuristic procedure for these applications.
For larger-sized instances, this heuristic helps solve most instances within an optimality gap of less than
1\%
in a reasonable amount of time using branch-and-bound solvers.

In Section
2
, we introduce the problem, notations, and assumptions.
We derive a single-level formulation based on KKT conditions in Section
3.1
that we further reformulate using Lagrangian duality in Section
3.2
.
The theoretical properties of relaxations of these two formulations are discussed in Section
3.3
.
Lastly, in Section
4
, we conduct an extensive computational study on the two applications described above.

4.1

Consider a directed network

A leader firm wishes to locate production facilities at a subset of a set of potential nodes, say
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{0}\subseteq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{f}}=({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{f}}_{ij},\ (i,j)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{A}})

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}=({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}_{i},\ i\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}})

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}=({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}_{j},\ j\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}})

1.

In the time limit of 600 seconds, the KKT-based formulation only solves instances with approximately 10 to 20 nodes and 35 to 45 arcs whereas the Duality-based formulation solves all instances within the optimality gap tolerance of 0.01%.

2.

When considering only instances successfully solved by the KKT-based formulation, the runtime and explored nodes count is several orders of magnitude higher as compared to that of the Duality-based formulation.
In fact, the Duality-based formulation solves most instances at the root node and all of them within 1 second of computation time.

We conjecture that the result of Lemma
3
holds for the EFL constraints (
33b
)-(
33h
) stated in Appendix
6.1
, which explains the unboundedness of the root relaxation of the KKT-based formulation observed in Table
1
, and results in severe computational limitations, even for medium-sized networks.
As already mentioned, the root relaxation of the Duality-based formulation is bounded by Lemma
4
.

Hence, we restrict our attention to the Duality-based formulation for large-sized networks.

Only one instance is considered for each value of

.
Specifically,
|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{A}}|

With the Duality-based formulation, GUROBI can handle instances with fewer than 3400 arcs but struggles to find a lower bound (
i.e.
, to find a feasible solution) for instances having 3800 arcs or more.
The bounded objective of the Duality-based formulation at the root node ensures that an upper bound is found for all instances when the root relaxation is solved within the time limit.

We develop a simple rounding heuristic (
RH
) that can be called during branch-and-bound for finding good quality feasible solutions.
A pseudo-code of this procedure is given in Appendix
6.2
; see Algorithm
1
.
Given a fractional solution
(\widehat{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{z}},\widehat{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Q}})
available at any point of the branch-and-bound procedure, this heuristic rounds the components of
\widehat{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{z}}
larger than rounding threshold
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}RndTh\_RH}
to
1
, sets the others to zeros, and decreases the production quantities of facilities that were just closed to
0
.
Clearly, the solution
(\widetilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{z}}},\widetilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Q}}})
so obtained satisfies the upper level constraints
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{EFL}}
.
For this vector of leader variables, the heuristic then solves the follower problem.
Because we selected affine lower-level costs in our instances, the primal and dual problems are convex quadratic programs that can be efficiently solved using GUROBI; see (
36
) and (
37
) in Appendix
6.3
.

We invoke
RH
at every node of the branch-and-bound tree until the point where two feasible solutions have been generated.
After that point,
RH
is run at a node only if there is a successful trial of a Bernoulli distribution with success probability 5%.

is utilized.

4.2
Application 2: RGUP in power distribution networks

Due to rising electricity demand, power grids are often burdened with very large loads, which may result in power outages in the worst case.
A possible solution is to integrate renewable generation units (RGUs) into power distribution networks to improve reliability.
As a result, the optimal deployment of RGUs in distribution networks has attracted recent attention from the research community; see
Zhang et al. 2017
.
In a power distribution network, there are several firms, each controlling a number of generating units.
Each generation unit submits a bid to the independent system operator (ISO).
This bid defines the supply-price curve at each of the supply nodes.
ISO then decides how much power to buy from the different units, how much to deliver to consumers, and what prices to charge based on the solution of an Optimal Power Flow (OPF) problem.

Consider a power distribution system represented as a directed network
G=({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{A}})

(
where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}
is the set of nodes/buses and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{A}}
is the set of arcs/lines
)
with demand nodes
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}}\subseteq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}
and supply nodes
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}}\subseteq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}
.
Define
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{0}
to be the set of nodes/buses under control of the leader firm where RGUs with capacity
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Q}}_{i}
for
i\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{0}
can be located. The upper-level feasible set is
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RGUP}}=\left\{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{z}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Q}})\in\mathbb{R}^{2|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{0}|}\ \big|\ 0\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Q}}_{i}\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Q}}}}}_{i}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{z}}_{i}\;\forall i\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{0},\;{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{z}}\in\{0,1\}^{|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{0}|}\right\}

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{z}}_{i}
is the RGU location decision and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Q}}}}}_{i}
is the maximum possible RGU capacity at node
i\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{0}
.
We assume that there is one generation unit per node which means
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}}\cap{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{0}=\emptyset
and that ISO accepts all the RGU generation so that there is no bidding for nodes in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{0}
.
The lower level is the OPF problem faced by the ISO, which can be understood as a single commodity SPE problem (with additional constraints due to Kirchoff’s voltage law)
Hobbs et al. 2000
where the supply curves are the bid submitted by generation units at nodes
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}}
.
To simplify the derivations, we consider a DC OPF model where resistance is assumed negligible relative to reactance and is ignored.
Denote

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{f}}=({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{f}}_{ij},\ (i,j)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{A}})
,

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}=({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}_{i},\ i\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}})
,

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}=({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}_{j},\ j\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}})
to be the vectors of power flows, demands, and supplies in the network, respectively.
Assuming competition within the network, the installation of RGU capacity
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Q}}
increases total power generation capacity and impacts equilibrium prices and power flows in the distribution network.
We also consider uncertainty in RGU generation
Zhang et al. 2017
using
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\xi}}=({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\xi}}_{i},\ i\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{0})
where each
0\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\xi}}_{i}\leq 1
. Here,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\xi}}_{i}
is the fraction of capacity
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Q}}_{i}
that is realized into actual RGU generation.
For a given
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\xi}}
, the resulting power flows, demand, and supply will produce a new competitive equilibrium
({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}^{*},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}^{*})
, which is obtained by solving the variational inequality

\displaystyle-\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\beta}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}^{*}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}^{*}\rangle+\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}^{*}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}^{*}\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}})\in\mathrm{proj}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Omega}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Q}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\xi}}),

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\beta}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}})
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}})
are the inverse demand cost vector and supply bid functions, respectively.
The set
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Omega}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Q}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\xi}})
corresponds to the power flow balance, Kirchoff’s voltage law
Hobbs et al. 2000
together with line and generation capacity constraints given as

\displaystyle\Omega({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Q}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\xi}})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c|c}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{f}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}})&\begin{array}[]{ll}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{I}_{0{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{f}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{I}_{0{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}-\mathsf{diag}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\xi}}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Q}}=0,\\
&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{I}_{1{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{f}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{I}_{1{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{I}_{1{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}=0,\\
&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}R}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{f}}=0,\\
&0\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{f}}\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{f}}}}},\;0\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}}}},\;{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}\geq 0\end{array}\end{array}\right\},

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{I}_{1{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{f}}}}
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{I}_{1{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}}}
, and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{I}_{1{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}}}
are the node-arc incidence, demand node incidence, supply node incidence matrices corresponding to the set of nodes
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{1}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}\setminus{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{0}
, respectively, and matrices
({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{I}_{0{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{f}}}})_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{0}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{A}}}
and
({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{I}_{0{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}}})_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{0}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}}}
are defined similarly for the set
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{0}
.
The definitions of these incidence matrices are given in Section
4.1
.
Further,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{f}}}}}
is the vector of line capacities,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}}}}
is the vector of generator capacities, and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}R}
is the incidence matrix of signed reactance coefficients
Hobbs et al. 2000
,
i.e.
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}R}_{m,ij}=s_{ijm}r_{ij}
if
(i,j)\in L_{m}
and
0
otherwise,
where
m
indexes Kirchoff voltage
loops,

L_{m}
is the ordered set of arcs in loop
m
,
s_{ijm}=\pm 1
depending on the orientation of arc
(i,j)
in loop
m
, and
r_{ij}
is the reactance of line
(i,j)
.

The single-level reformulation of RGUP is a two-stage stochastic program given in Appendix
7.1
that maximizes the leader firm’s expected profit.
We draw finite samples for the uncertainty
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\xi}}^{\langle{k}\rangle}\}_{{k}=1}^{{K}}
, and use sample average approximation for the expectation.
The KKT-based and the Duality-based formulations of RGUP can be obtained from (
12
) and (
17
) using the application specific constraints described above.
Their explicit forms can be found in Appendix
7.1
.

4.2.1
Test instances

We use the standard IEEE bus systems summarized in Table
3
as the power distribution networks in our numerical study.
As is common in the power generation literature, we combine all lines between each pair of nodes in the data set into an equivalent single line.
Each line is then transformed into a pair of opposite arcs in order to obtain a directed network which allows power flow in either direction between a pair of nodes.

The set of potential RGU locations
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{0}
is randomly selected from set
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}\setminus{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}}
where
|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{0}|
is given in Table
3
.
The first-stage cost parameters of the upper-level problem are randomly generated as
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}c}_{i}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(150,200)
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}v}_{i}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(3,5)
for each
i\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{0}
.
The lower-level cost vectors are chosen to be affine functions of the form:

[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\beta}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}})]_{i}=-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\beta_{i}^{1}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}_{i}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\beta_{i}^{0}}\;\;\forall i\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{D}}}
,
[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}})]_{j}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma_{j}^{1}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}_{j}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma_{j}^{0}}\;\;\forall j\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{S}}}
,
where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\beta_{i}^{1}}>0
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma_{j}^{1}}>0
so that Assumptions
1
-
2
are satisfied.
Since
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{h}}=\bm{0}
in this application, we have from Lemma
4
that the root relaxation of the Duality-based formulation is bounded.
For a load bus
i
, the intercept parameter
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\beta_{i}^{0}}
is set to
40
and the slope
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\beta_{i}^{1}}
is determined so that the resulting cost is
30
at the rated load in megawatt (MW).
More specifically, we set

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\beta_{i}^{1}}=\frac{40-30}{\text{Load MW rating at bus $i$}}.

For a generator bus
j
, the intercept and slope parameters
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma_{j}^{0}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma_{j}^{1}}
are fixed so that
10<{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma_{j}^{0}}<33
and
0.03<{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma_{j}^{1}}<0.70
.
The uncertainty samples are drawn
from a uniform distribution
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\xi}}_{i}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
for
i\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{N}}_{0}
where the sample size
{K}\in\{10,25,50,100\}
.
All models are solved using GUROBI (v9.5.2) in Python 3.7 with the
parameters and machine settings described in Section

5
Conclusion

We introduce a new Duality-based formulation for bilevel programs with spatial price equilibrium constraints.
Together with the use of specially designed heuristics, this new bounded formulation allows the global solution of instances of EFL and RGUP orders of magnitude larger than is possible with the classical KKT-based formulation.
