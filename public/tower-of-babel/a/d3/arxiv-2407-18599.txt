Tight Bounds for the Number of Absent Scattered Factors
Duncan Adamson, Pamela Fleischmann, Annika Huch, Max Wiedenhöft

Abstract
A scattered factor of a word w w is a word u u that can be obtained by deleting arbitary letters from w w and keep the order of the remaining. Barker et al. introduced the notion of k k -universality , calling a word k k -universal, if it contains all possible words of length k k over a given alphabet Σ \Sigma as a scattered factor. Kosche et al. introduced the notion of absent scattered factors to categorise the words not being scattered factors of a given word. In this paper, we investigate tight bounds on the possible number of absent scattered factors of a given length k k (also strictly longer than the shortest absent scattered factors) among all words with the same universality extending the results of Kosche et al. Specifically, given a length k k and universality index ι \iota , we characterize ι \iota -universal words with both the maximal and minimal number of absent scattered factors of length k k . For the lower bound, we provide the exact number in a closed form. For the upper bound, we offer efficient algorithms to compute the number based on the constructed words. Moreover, by combining old results, we present an enumeration with constant delay of the set of scattered factors of a fixed length in time O ⁡ ( | Σ | ​ | w | ) O(|\Sigma||w|) .

Introduction

For a given word
w
, a
scattered factor
(
(scattered) subword or subsequence
) of
w
is a word obtained by deleting letters from
w
, formally a word
u
such that there exists some set of indices
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{|u|}\leq|w|
where
u=w[i_{1}]w[i_{2}]\dots w[i_{|u|}]
and
|u|
(resp.
|w|
) denotes the length of
u
(resp.
w
). For instance,
\mathtt{mega},\mathtt{meat}
, and
\mathtt{gate}
are scattered factors of
\mathtt{pomegranate}
while
\mathtt{magnet},\mathtt{goat}
and
\mathtt{great}
are not since the letters do not occur in the correct order in
\mathtt{pomegranate}
. Scattered factors serve as a complexity measure for a word’s absent or existing information, with the relation between words and their scattered factors studied in combinatorics on words
[
29
,
35
]
, algorithms and stringology
[
3
,
4
,
6
,
18
,
33
,
41
]
, logics
[
20
,
31
,
30
]
, as well as language and automata theory
[
1
,
22
,
23
,
39
,
40
,
43
]
(see
[
32
, Chapter 6, Subwords]
for a profound overview on the topic). The set of scattered factors of a word reveals information about existing and absent information of the word, and thus are not only of theoretical interest but of practical interest too. When examining discrete data, scattered factors are often used to model corrupted or lossy data
[
17
,
10
,
34
]
associated with the theoretical problem of reconstruction. These problems appear in a wide variety of fields, including bioinformatics in the context of protein sequences
[
9
,
42
]
and formal software verification
[
20
,
43
]
.

In terms of theoretical work, the study of scattered factors is strongly related to the 1972 work of Simon
[
39
]
. There the famous congruence relation regarding piecewise-testable languages is introduced, nowadays known as
Simon’s congruence
. Two words
u
and
v
are called
Simon
k
-congruent
w.r.t. a natural number
k
if they have the same set of scattered factors of length at most
k
. For example,
\mathtt{aaba}
and
\mathtt{abaa}
are Simon
2
-congruent since both of them have the set
\{\mathtt{aa},\mathtt{ab},\mathtt{ba}\}
as scattered factors of length
2
. However, they are not Simon
3
-congruent, which can be witnessed by the scattered factor
\mathtt{aab}
that is contained in
\mathtt{aaba}
but not in
\mathtt{abaa}
. For a more detailed overview of Simon’s congruence, we refer the reader to
[
32
, Chapter 6]
by Sakarovich and Simon (see the surveys
[
37
,
38
]
and the references therein for the theory around Simon’s congruence). Despite heavy investigations,
[
12
,
19
,
40
]
and several attempts toward giving the number of congruence classes, i.e., the index of Simon’s congruence, this problem remains open and continues to attract interest
[
15
,
16
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
. Current developments inspect both shortest absent scattered factors of words
[
29
]
and relations to universality on the other hand
[
5
,
8
,
13
]
. This notion of universality serves as a measure of the containment of all scattered factors of up to a given length and originates from two sources: Karandikar et al.
[
22
]
introduced the notion of
k
-richness in the context of piecewise testable languages
[
25
,
36
]
- a word is
k
-rich for
k\in\mathbb{N}
if all words of length
k
occur in
w
as scattered factors, i.e,
\ScatFact_{k}(w)=\Sigma^{k}
. That coincides with the notion of
k
-universality. The language of
k
-universal words is extensively studied and characterised in
[
1
,
5
,
8
,
13
,
21
]
. One of the main insights on
k
-universal words is that a word is
k
-universal if and only if the arch factorisation of
w
(cf.
[
21
]
) has at least
k
arches where the arch factorisation factorises the word in shortest possible factors such that each factor contains the whole alphabet. For instance, the word
tomatoatm
has the arches
toma
and
toatm
since after the first
\mathtt{a}
the complete alphabet -
\Sigma
is
\{\mathtt{a},\mathtt{m},\mathtt{o},\mathtt{t}\}
- has been seen, and first after reading the complete remaining part of the word, the alphabet has been seen again.

One approach followed in
[
14
,
29
]
is to characterise absent scattered factors of words in order to determine the index of Simon’s congruence, i.e., parameterise the problem by the number of absent scattered factors. In
[
29
]
, the authors gave in implicit characterisation and tree-based construction of all shortest absent scattered factors of a given word.

Own Contribution.

An open question that we address is to generalise those ideas to absent scattered factors that are longer than the shortest ones. A first generalisation is to ask for the minimal and maximal number of absent scattered factors of a word in relation to the number of arches and the size of the alphabet, asking for a given pair of values
\iota
and
k
where
\iota<k
, what the word containing exactly
\iota
arches with the greatest (resp. fewest) number of absent factors of length
k
is. In this way, we build on the work of
[
1
]
in looking at the properties of
k
-universality within languages. While
[
1
]
considered the problem of finding if a word is
k
-universal in a given language, we look for the word that is closest (resp. furthest) to
k
-universality within the restricted language of
\iota
-universal words.
We give a tight characterisation for both. First, we show the structure of words
w\in\Sigma^{*}
that have the maximal number of existing scattered factors, i.e., the minimal number of absent scattered factors, of any given length
k\in\mathbb{N}
among all words
w^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
with the same number of arches. Additionally, we characterise the words fulfilling this property that have the shortest possible length. After that, we show the structure of words
w_{min}\in\Sigma^{*}
with a minimal number of existing scattered factors, i.e., a maximal number of absent scattered factors among all words
w^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
with the same number of arches. Afterwards, we show that the specific number of scattered factors of these words
w_{min}
can be determined in linear time
O(|w_{min}|)
and that we can enumerate all scattered factors of any length
k\in[|w_{min}|]
with constant delay
O(1)
.

Structure of the Work.

We begin, in Section 2, with basic definitions used in this paper. After that, in Section 3, we show the results regarding words with a minimal number of absent scattered factors. Finally, in Section 4, we show the results regarding words with a maximal number of absent scattered factors and quickly discuss their scattered factor counting and enumeration properties.

Preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{N}
be the set of all natural numbers,
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
,
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
[i,j]=[j]\backslash[i-1]
, and
[n]_{0}:=[n]\cup\{0\}
. An
alphabet

\Sigma
is a non empty, finite set whose elements are called
letters
or
symbols
.
For a given
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}
, we fix
\Sigma=\{\mathtt{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathtt{a}_{\sigma}\}
. A
word
is a finite sequence of letters from
\Sigma
. Let
\Sigma^{*}
be
the set of all finite words over
\Sigma
with concatenation and the empty word
\varepsilon
as
neutral element.
Set
\Sigma^{+}:=\Sigma^{*}\setminus\{\varepsilon\}
. Let
w\in\Sigma^{\ast}
.
For all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
define inductively,
w^{0}=\varepsilon
and
w^{n}=ww^{n-1}
.
The
length
of
w
is the number of
w
’s letters; thus
|\varepsilon|=0
.
For all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
set
\Sigma^{k}:=\{w\in\Sigma^{*}\mid|w|=k\}
(define
\Sigma^{\leq k}
,
\Sigma^{\geq k}
analogously). We denote
w
’s
i
{}^{\text{th}}
letter by
w[i]

and set
w[i..j]=w[i]\cdots w[j]
if
i<j
, and
\varepsilon
if
i>j
for all
i,j\in[|w|]
.
Set
\letters(w)=\{\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma\mid\exists i\in[|w|]:w[i]=\mathtt{a}\}
as
w
’s alphabet and
for each
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
set
|w|_{\mathtt{a}}=|\{i\in[|w|]\mid w[i]=\mathtt{a}\}|
. The word
u\in\Sigma^{*}
is called a
factor
of
w
if there exist
x,y\in\Sigma^{*}
such that
w=xuy
.
In the case
x=\varepsilon
, we call
u
a
prefix
of
w
and
suffix
if
y=\varepsilon
.
Define the
reverse
of
w
by
w^{R}=w[|w|]\cdots w[1]
.
Let
<_{\Sigma}
be a total order on
\Sigma
and denote the
lexicographical
order
on
\Sigma^{*}
by
<
. Define
w_{\Sigma}
as the word in
\Sigma^{\sigma}
with

w_{\Sigma}[i]<_{\Sigma}w_{\Sigma}[i+1]
and
\letters(w)=\Sigma
.
For further definitions see
[
32
]
. After these basic notations, we introduce the scattered factors.

Definition 1
.

Let
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. A word
u\in\Sigma^{n}
is called a
scattered factor
of w - denoted as
u\in\ScatFact(w)
- if there exist
v_{1},\dots,v_{n+1}\in\Sigma^{*}
such that
w=v_{1}u[1]v_{2}u[2]\cdots v_{n}u[n]

v_{n+1}
.
Set
\ScatFact_{k}(w)=\{u\in\ScatFact(w)|\,|u|=k\}
.

The words
\mathtt{cau},\mathtt{cafe}
,
\mathtt{life}
and
\mathtt{ufo}
are all scattered factors of
\mathtt{cauliflower}
but neither
\mathtt{flour}
nor
\mathtt{row}
are scattered factors. Tightly related to the notion of scattered factors is the famous
Simon congruence
.

Definition 2
.

Two words
w,v\in\Sigma^{*}
are
Simon congruent
w.r.t.
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
(
w\sim_{k}v
) if
\operatorname{ScatFact}_{\ell}(w)=\operatorname{ScatFact}_{\ell}(v)
for all
\ell\leq k
.

Since
\ScatFact_{k}(w)\subseteq\Sigma^{k}
holds for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, determining the index of Simon’s
congruence can be split into the parametrised problem on determining how many scattered factor sets
- or equivalently how many different words - exist with
|\ScatFact_{k}(w)|=\sigma^{k}-m
for all

m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
(cf.
[
5
,
13
,
8
]
for
m=0
).
We disregard all
w\in\Sigma^{<k}
because they form singleton classes. For all
w\in\Sigma^{\geq k}
it suffices to consider the set
\ScatFact_{k}(w)
because all shorter scattered factors are contained in a scattered factor of length
k
.

Definition 3
.

A word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
is called
m
-nearly
k
-universal
if
|\ScatFact_{k}(w)|
=\sigma^{k}-m
. Let
\NUniv_{\Sigma,m,k}
be the set of all
m
-nearly
k
-universal words over
\Sigma
.
If
m=0
, the words are called
k
-universal, and if
m=1
, they are called nearly
k
-universal. The respective sets are
\Univ_{\Sigma,k}
and
\NUniv_{\Sigma,k}
. The largest
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, such that a word is
k
-universal, is called the universality index
\iota(w)
.

In the unary alphabet all words are of the form
\mathtt{a}^{\ell}
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and the scattered factors can be determined easily. Therefore we only consider at least binary alphabets. Moreover, we assume
\Sigma=\letters(w)

for a given
w
, if not stated otherwise.

One of the main tools for the investigation of
m
-nearly
k
-universal words is the
arch factorisation
which was introduced by Hébrard
[
21
]
.

Definition 4
.

For a word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
the
arch factorisation
is given by
w=\ar_{1}(w)\cdots\ar_{k}(w)\operatorname{r}(w)
for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with

(a)
\iota(\ar_{i}(w))=1
for all
i\in[k]
,

(b)
\ar_{i}(w)[|\ar_{i}(w)|]\notin\letters(\ar_{i}(w)[1..|\ar_{i}(w)|-1])
for all
i\in[k]
, and

(c)
\letters(\operatorname{r}(w))\subsetneq\Sigma
.

The words
\ar_{i}(w)
are called
arches
of
w
and
\operatorname{r}(w)
is the
rest
of
w
.
The modus
\operatorname{m}(w)
is given by
\ar_{1}(w)[|\ar_{1}(w)|]\cdots\ar_{k}(w)[|\ar_{k}(w)|]
, i.e., it is the word containing the unique last letters of each arch.
The
inner of the
i^{th}
arch of
w
is defined as the prefix of

\ar_{i}(w)
such that
\ar_{i}(w)=\inner_{i}(w)\operatorname{m}(w)[i]
holds.

To visualise the arch factorisation in explicit examples we use parenthesis. For example we write
(\mathtt{aab})\cdot(\mathtt{bba})\cdot\mathtt{a}
to mark the two arches, namely
\mathtt{aab}
and
\mathtt{bba}
and the rest,
\mathtt{a}
, which is denoted without parenthesis. Here, the modus is
\mathtt{b}\mathtt{a}
and the two inners are
\mathtt{a}\mathtt{a}
and
\mathtt{b}\mathtt{b}
resp.

Based on the arch factorisation we define (minimal) perfect universal words.

Definition 5
.

We call a word
w\in\Sigma^{*}

perfect
k
-universal
if
\iota(w)=k
and
\operatorname{r}(w)=\varepsilon
. The set of all these words with
\letters(w)=\Sigma
is denoted by
\PerfUniv_{\Sigma,k}
.
Additionally, we call
w

minimal perfect
k
-universal
if
w\in\PerfUniv_{\Sigma,k}
and
w
is of minimal length among all words in
\PerfUniv_{\Sigma,k}
.
The set of those words over
\Sigma
is denoted by
\MinPerfUniv_{\Sigma,k}
.

For the algorithmic results we use the standard
computational model RAM with logarithmic word-size (see, e.g.,
[
24
]
), i.e., we
follow a standard assumption from stringology, that if
w
is the
input word for our algorithms, then we assume
\Sigma=\letters(w)=\{1,2,\ldots,\sigma\}
.

In the following we investigate the ranges
m
can have for fixed values of
\sigma,k,\iota\in\mathbb{N}
with
\iota<k
serving as the word’s universality index, i.e., we search for words that are
\iota
-universal and omit the least and the most scattered factors of length
k
. Rephrased, we are determining the minimal and maximal values for
m
such that there exists a word
w
with
\iota(w)=\iota
that is
m
-nearly
k
-universal.

3
Lower Bound for
m

In this section, we consider the following problem: Given a specific universality
\iota\in\mathbb{N}
and a number
k
, what is the minimum number of absent scattered factors of length
k
for any word
w
where
\iota(w)=\iota
? Further, we are interested in finding the shortest word
w
fulfilling this property. We start by determining which words are always absent from
\ScatFact_{k}(w)
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k>\iota(w)
. Then, there exists some
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
such that, for every
v\in\Sigma^{k}
with
\operatorname{m}(w)\mathtt{a}\in\ScatFact_{\iota(w)+1}(v)
, we have
v\notin\ScatFact_{k}(w)
.

Proof.

Note first that
\alphabet(\operatorname{r}(w))\neq\Sigma
. Therefore, there exists some
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
with
\mathtt{a}\notin\alphabet(\operatorname{r}(w))
. As every letter of
\operatorname{m}(w)
is unique within its arch, we know that any
v\in\ScatFact_{k}(w)

with
\operatorname{m}(w)\mathtt{a}\in\ScatFact_{\iota(w)+1}(v)
implies
\mathtt{a}\in\operatorname{r}(w)
- a contradiction.
∎

Lemma
6
gives us the number of scattered factors of length
k
which are in any word missing, i.e., a lower bound on
m
. The following lemma shows that this bound is tight.

Lemma 7
.

Given
\iota,k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k>\iota
, and
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
, there exists
w\in\Sigma^{*}
with
\iota(w)=\iota
such that we have
\operatorname{m}(w)\mathtt{a}\in\ScatFact_{\iota+1}(v)
for all
v\in\Sigma^{k}\setminus\ScatFact_{k}(w)
.

Proof.

Let
k,\iota\in\mathbb{N}
and set
\Sigma^{-}=\{\mathtt{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathtt{a}_{\sigma-1}\}
. W.l.o.g. we assume
\mathtt{a}=\mathtt{a}_{\sigma}
(otherwise we could permute
\Sigma
).
Set
w=(w_{\Sigma^{-}}^{k}\mathtt{a}_{\sigma})^{\iota}w_{\Sigma^{-}}^{k}
with
\iota(w)=\iota
,
\operatorname{m}(w)=\mathtt{a}_{\sigma}^{\iota}
.
Let
v\in\Sigma^{k}\backslash\ScatFact_{k}(w)
. By the construction of
w
, each word
u\in\Sigma^{k}
with
|u|_{\mathtt{a}_{\sigma}}\leq\iota
is a scattered factor of
w
. Thus, we have
|v|_{\mathtt{a}_{\sigma}}>\iota
and we have
\operatorname{m}(w)\mathtt{a}\in\ScatFact_{k}(v)
.
∎

These lemmata yield the following proposition.

Proposition 8
.

Given
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
w\in\Sigma^{*}
with
k>\iota(w)
, the following equivalence holds

1) for all
w^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
with
\iota(w^{\prime})=\iota(w)
we have
|\ScatFact_{k}(w^{\prime})|\leq|\ScatFact_{k}(w)|
,

2) there exists exactly one
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
with

\ScatFact_{k}(w)=\{v\in\Sigma^{k}|\,\operatorname{m}(w)\mathtt{a}\not\in\ScatFact_{\iota(w)+1}(v)\}
.

Proof.

For the first direction the existence follows directly from Lemma
6
and

7
. Suppose there exists
\mathtt{a},\mathtt{b}\in\Sigma
for which we have for all
v\in\Sigma^{k}
that

\operatorname{m}(w)\mathtt{a},\operatorname{m}(w)\mathtt{b}\in\ScatFact_{\iota(w)+1}(v)
implies
v\not\in\ScatFact_{k}(w)
.
Since
w
has the most scattered factors among all words with
\iota(w)
arches, we have
\mathtt{a}=\mathtt{b}
.

For the second direction, let
w^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{\ast}
with
\iota(w^{\prime})=\iota(w)
.
For all
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma\backslash\letters(\operatorname{r}(w))
, we have that
\operatorname{m}(w^{\prime})\mathtt{a}\not\in\ScatFact_{k}(w^{\prime})
.
There is always at least one
\mathtt{a}\notin\alphabet(\rest(w^{\prime}))
.
The more letters do not occur in the rest, the fewer scattered factors
w^{\prime}
has.
Hence,
|\ScatFact_{k}(w^{\prime})|\leq|\ScatFact_{k}(w)|
.
∎

Now, we have the structure of the absent scattered factors of
w
, which we may use to derive a bound on the minimum number of scattered factors.

Proposition 9
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
w\in\Sigma^{*}
with
k>\iota(w)
and for all
w^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
with
\iota(w)=\iota(w^{\prime})
we have
|\ScatFact_{k}(w)|\geq|\ScatFact_{k}(w^{\prime})|
. Then,

|\ScatFact_{k}(w)|=\sum_{j\in[0,\iota(w)]}\binom{k}{j}(\sigma-1)^{k-j}.

Proof.

By Proposition
8
, we have to determine the cardinality of

\ScatFact_{k}(w)=\{v\in\Sigma^{k}|\,\operatorname{m}(w)\mathtt{a}\not\in\ScatFact_{\iota(w)+1}(v)\}
. Thus, we have to count the number of words of
\Sigma^{k}
which do not contain a given
v^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{\iota(w)+1}
as a scattered factor. We achieve this
by considering words
v
in
\Sigma^{k}
such that some proper prefix of
v^{\prime}
is not a scattered factor of
v
.
First, the number of words that contain no prefix of
v^{\prime}
(equivalently, only the
0
length prefix corresponding to the empty word) is
(\sigma-1)^{k}
, corresponding to the set of words that do not contain the symbol
v^{\prime}[1]
.
To count the number of words that do not contain the prefix
v^{\prime}[1..2]
, but do contain the prefix
v^{\prime}[1]
, observe that any such word
v
must contain some prefix
v[1..\ell]
that does not contain the symbol
v^{\prime}[1]
, followed the suffix
v^{\prime}[1]v[\ell+2..k]
where
v[\ell+2..k]
does not contain the symbol
v^{\prime}[2]
. As there are
k
possible positions at which
v^{\prime}[1]
could occur, and
(\sigma-1)^{k-1}
possible values of the remaining symbols for each such position
\ell\in[k]
, we have
(\sigma-1)^{k-1}k
possible words containing
v^{\prime}[1]
as a scatted factor, and not
v^{\prime}[1..2]
.

In general, consider the number of words in
\Sigma^{k}
that contain
v^{\prime}[1..j]
as a scattered factor, but not
v^{\prime}[1..j+1]
. First, observe that any such word
v
can be expressed as
v[1..\ell_{1}-1]v^{\prime}[1]v[\ell_{1}+1..\ell_{2}-1]v^{\prime}[2]\dots v^{\prime}[j-1]v[\ell_{j-1}+1..\ell_{j}-1]v^{\prime}[j]v[\ell_{j}+1..k]
, with
v[\ell_{x}]=v^{\prime}[x]
for every
x\in[j]
and
v^{\prime}[j+1]\not\in\letters(v[\ell_{j}+1..k])
.

For any given set of indices
\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\dots,\ell_{j}
we have for the number of different
v\in\Sigma^{k}

\prod_{x\in[j+1]}(\sigma-1)^{\ell_{x}-1-\ell_{x-1}}=(\sigma-1)^{k+1-0}\prod_{x\in[j+1]}(\sigma-1)^{-1}=(\sigma-1)^{k-j},

with
\ell_{0}=0
and
\ell_{j+1}=k+1
.
As there are
\binom{k}{j}
possible indices
1\leq\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}<\dots<\ell_{j}\leq k
, we get
\binom{k}{j}\sigma^{k-j}
such words. By extension, we get the total number of scattered factors of
w
as
\sum_{j\in[0,\iota(w)]}\binom{k}{j}\sigma^{k-j}
.
∎

This gives us immediately, the number of absent scattered factors which are absent in any case and thus a lower bound of
m
.

Corollary 10
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
w\in\Sigma^{*}
with
k>\iota(w)
such that for all
w^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
with
\iota(w)=\iota(w^{\prime})
and
|\ScatFact_{k}(w)|\geq|\ScatFact_{k}(w^{\prime})|
. Then, the number of absent scattered factors of length
k
of
w
is

\sigma^{k}-\sum_{j\in[0,\iota^{\prime}]}\binom{k}{j}(\sigma-1)^{k-j}.

First, we define the following helper function
\nextAlphPos_{w}(i,s)

in

Definition 21

Let

\nextAlphPos_{w}(i,s)=\begin{cases}j&,\text{ if }|\alphabet(w[i+1..j])|=s\text{ and }|\alphabet(w[i+1..j-1])|<s,\\
-1&,\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Lemma 22
.

Given a word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and
u\in\ScatFact_{k}(w)
, for some given
k\in[|w|]
, there exists a sequence in indices
1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\dots<j_{k}\leq|w|
such that:

1.

w[j_{1}]\cdots w[j_{k}]=u
,

2.

|w[1..j_{1}-1]|_{w[j_{1}]}=0
, and

3.

\nextAlphPos_{w}(j_{i},|\alphabet(w[j_{i}+1,j_{i+1}])|)=j_{i+1}

Further, this set of indices satisfies
j_{i}\leq j_{i}^{\prime}
, for any set
i\in[1,k]
and set of indices

such that
w[j_{1}^{\prime}]w[j_{2}^{\prime}]\dots w[j_{k}^{\prime}]=u
.

Proof.

Let
s_{i}=|\alphabet(w[j_{i-1}+1,j_{i}])|
, for every
i\in[2,k]
and let
s_{1}=|\alphabet(w[1,j_{1}])|
. As
u\in\ScatFact_{k}(w)
, there exists some set of indices
j_{1}^{\prime},j_{2}^{\prime},\dots,j_{k}^{\prime}
such that
w[j_{1}^{\prime}]w[j_{2}^{\prime}]\dots w[j_{k}^{\prime}]=u
. Now, observe that
j_{1}^{\prime}\geq j_{1}
, as there exists no position
i_{1}
such that
i_{1}<j_{1}
and
w[i_{1}]=u[1]
. Further,
w[j_{1}]w[j_{2}^{\prime}]\dots w[j_{k}^{\prime}]=u

such that

,

. By repeating this argument for each

, for any set of indices

where

, and further that

, proving the statement.

k\in[|w|]
, amongst the set of words
\{v\in\Sigma^{*}|\iota(v)=\iota(w_{\min})\}
, noting that for any
k\leq\iota(w_{\min})
, all words in this set contain every word in
\Sigma^{k}
as a scattered factor, and thus this condition holds.
First, we show that
w_{\min}
has length
|w_{\min}|=\iota(w_{\min})\sigma
.

Lemma 24
.

Let
w\in\Sigma^{*}
. If
|w|>\iota(w)\cdot\sigma
, then there exists a word
w^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
with

\iota(w^{\prime})=\iota(w)
and
|w^{\prime}|=\iota(w)\cdot\sigma
such that for all
k\in[|w|]
we have
|\ScatFact_{k}(w^{\prime})|\leq|\ScatFact_{k}(w)|
.

Proof.

Let
w
satisfy
|w|>\iota(w)\cdot\sigma
. Now, observe that there must exist some index
i\in[|w|]
such that
\iota(w[1..i-1]w[i+1..|w|])=\iota(w)
. Further, given any
u\in\ScatFact(w[1..i-1]w[i+1..|w|])
,
u\in\ScatFact(w)
by construction. Thus
\ScatFact(w[1..i-1]w[i+1..|w|])\subseteq\ScatFact(w)
, and by extension
|\ScatFact_{k}(w[1..i-1]w[i+1..|w|])|\leq|\ScatFact_{k}(w)|
for all
k\in[|w|]
, completing the proof.∎

Lemma
24
allows us to only consider words
w\in\Sigma^{*}
with
|w|=\iota(w)\cdot\sigma
.
We now give an upper bound on the indices that
\nextAlphPos_{w}
can return for such a word.

Lemma 25
.

Let
w\in\Sigma^{\iota(w)\cdot\sigma}
. Then, for all
s\in[\sigma]
and
j\in[|w|]
,

\nextAlphPos_{w}(j,s)\leq\left\lfloor\frac{j}{\sigma}\right\rfloor\cdot\sigma+\sigma+s.

Proof.

Let
r=j\bmod\sigma
if
j\bmod\sigma\neq 0
, otherwise let
r=\sigma
.
Note, first, that if
s+r\geq\sigma
, then
\alphabet(w[j+1..j+\sigma+s])=\Sigma
, and thus
\nextAlphPos_{w}(j,s)\leq\left\lfloor\frac{j}{\sigma}\right\rfloor\cdot\sigma+\sigma+s
by definition.
Now, letting
s+r<\sigma
, observe that given any factor
u
of
w
with
u=w[j+1..i\sigma+s]
, where
i
is the value such that the position
j
is in the
i^{th}
arch of
w
,
u
has a prefix of length
\sigma-r
in the
i^{th}
arch, and a suffix of length
s
in the
(i+1)^{th}
arch. Therefore,
|\alphabet(u)|\geq\max(\sigma-r,s)\geq s
. Further, if
j>|w|-\sigma
, and thus
j
is in the last arch of
w
, either
r\leq\sigma-s
, and thus
|\alphabet(w[j+1..j+s])|=s
, or
r>\sigma-s
, in which case
|\alphabet(w[j+1..|w|])|<s
and thus
\nextAlphPos_{w}(j,s)=-1
, satisfying the statement.∎

Lemma 26
.

Let
w\in\Sigma^{\iota(w)\sigma}
.
For all
s\in[\sigma]
and
j\in[|w|]
, if either
j\bmod\sigma=0
or
s\leq\sigma-(j\bmod\sigma)
, then

\nextAlphPos_{w}(j,s)=j+s.

Proof.

First, if
j\bmod\sigma=0
, then the factor
w[j+1..j+s]
is the prefix of an arch of length
\sigma
containing
s
unique symbols, and thus
|\alphabet(w[j+1..s])|=s
. Otherwise, if
s\leq\sigma-(j\bmod\sigma)
, then
w[j+1..j+s]
is a factor of some arch in
w
, and hence must contain
s
unique symbols. Therefore, in either case,
\nextAlphPos_{w}(j,s)=j+s
.
∎

With the previous two lemmata, we have upper bounds on possible values obtainable with
\nextAlphPos_{w}

in the case of
|w|=\iota(w)\cdot\sigma
.
The next result shows that a word of the structure assumed for
w_{\min}
reaches exactly those upper bounds.

Lemma 27
.

Consider
w_{\min}=\arch_{1}(w_{\min})\arch_{1}(w_{\min})^{R}\cdots\arch_{\iota(w_{\min})-1}(w_{\min})^{R}
as defined before. Assume
r=j\bmod\sigma
. Then, for each
s\in[\sigma]
and
j\in[|w|]
we have

\nextAlphPos_{w_{\min}}(j,s)=\begin{cases}j+s&\text{, if either }r=0\text{ or }s\leq\sigma-r,\\
\left\lfloor\frac{j}{\sigma}\right\rfloor\cdot\sigma+\sigma+s&\text{, if }r\neq 0,\left\lfloor\frac{j}{\sigma}\right\rfloor+1<\iota(w_{\min}),\text{ and }s>\sigma-r,\\
-1&\text{, otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Case (1) follows directly from Lemma
26
.
Case (2) can be derived from the same arguments as in Lemma
25
, by noting that we have
\alphabet(\arch_{i}(w_{\min})[\sigma-x+1..\sigma])=\alphabet(\arch_{i+1}(w_{\min})[1..x])
, for every
x\in[\sigma],i\in[\iota(w_{\min})-1]
. As
s>\sigma-r
, the suffix of the arch
\arch_{x}(w_{\min})
in which
j
is located, starting at
j+1
and ending with the end of that arch, has length less than
s
. Hence, the shortest factor of
w_{\min}
starting at
j+1
containing
s
symbols has the prefix of length
s
of arch
\arch_{x+1}(w_{\min})
as its suffix, ending at position
\left\lfloor\frac{j}{\sigma}\right\rfloor\cdot\sigma+\sigma+s
. Finally, in case (3),
j+s>\iota(w_{\min})\sigma
, and thus
|\alphabet(w_{\min}[j+1..|w_{\min}|)|<s
. By extension,
\nextAlphPos_{w_{\min}}(j,s)=-1
, completing the proof.
∎

We can now construct an injective mapping of scattered factors from words structured like
w_{\min}
to scattered factors of any other word with the same universality and the same size.

Proposition 28
.

Consider
w_{\min}
as defined before. Then for each
w^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
with

\iota(w)=\iota(w^{\prime})
and
|w|=|w^{\prime}|
we have that there exists an injective function

f:\ScatFact_{k}(w)\rightarrow\ScatFact_{k}(w^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Consider
w_{\min}
as defined before and let
w^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
with
\iota(w_{\min})=\iota(w^{\prime})

and
|w|=|w^{\prime}|
. Let
k\in[|w|]
.
Let
f:\ScatFact_{k}(w_{\min})\rightarrow\ScatFact_{k}(w^{\prime})
and define
f(u)=v

if there exist
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\in[|w_{\min}|]
,
j_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,j_{k}^{\prime}\in[|w^{\prime}|]
, and
s_{1},...,s_{k-1}\in[\sigma]
such that

1.

u=w_{\min}[j_{1}]\cdots w_{\min}[j_{k}],

2.

v=w^{\prime}[j_{1}^{\prime}]\cdots w^{\prime}[j_{k}^{\prime}],

3.

j_{1}=j_{1}^{\prime},

4.

\nextAlphPos_{w}(j_{i},s_{i})=j_{i+1},\text{ and }

5.

\nextAlphPos_{w}(j_{i}^{\prime},s_{i})=j_{i+1}^{\prime}.

We now show that
f
is actually a function and that
f
is injective.
Let
u\in\ScatFact_{k}(w_{\min})
.
By Lemma
22
we know that there exist
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\in[|w_{\min}|]
and

s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1}\in[\sigma]
such that
|w_{\min}[1..j_{1}]|_{w_{\min}[j_{1}]}=0
and
\nextAlphPos_{w_{\min}}(j_{i},s_{i})=j_{i+1}

for all
i\in[k-1]
.
By Lemma
25
, Lemma
26

and Lemma
27
we know that for all
i\in[k-1]
that
\nextAlphPos_{w_{\min}}(j,i)

hits its upper bound over all words
w^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{|w_{\min}|}
with
\iota(w_{\min})=\iota(w^{\prime})
. Therefore,
\nextAlphPos_{w_{\min}}(j_{i},s_{i})\geq\nextAlphPos_{w^{\prime}}(j_{i}^{\prime},s_{i})
, for every
i\in[k]
, and thus the word constructed by
f
must be a scattered factor of
w^{\prime}
. To show that
f(u_{1})\neq f(u_{2})
, for any pair
u_{1},u_{2}\in\ScatFact_{k}(w_{\min})
where
u_{1}\neq u_{2}
, let
i
be the index such that
u_{1}[1..i-1]=u_{2}[1..i-1]
, and
u_{1}[i]\neq u_{2}[i]
. Further, let
j_{1},\dots,j_{k}
be the leftmost set of indices such that
u_{1}=w_{\min}[j_{1}]\dots w_{\min}[j_{k}]
, and
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k}
be the leftmost set of indices such that
u_{2}=w_{\min}[\ell_{1}]\dots w_{\min}[\ell_{k}]
. First, as
u_{1}[1,i-1]=u_{2}[1,i-1]
, we have by definition
j_{1},\dots,j_{i-1}=\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{i-1}
. Further, as
u_{1}[i]\neq u_{2}[i]
,
j_{i}\neq\ell_{i}
, and thus
|\alphabet(w_{\min}[j_{i-1}+1..j_{i}])|\neq|\alphabet(w_{\min}[\ell_{i-1}+1..\ell_{i}])|
. Now, let
j_{1}^{\prime},\dots,j_{k}^{\prime}
be the leftmost set of indices embedding
f(u_{1})
into
w^{\prime}
, and let
\ell_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\ell_{k}^{\prime}
be the leftmost set of indices embedding
f(u_{2})
into
w^{\prime}
. Again, we note that
j_{1}^{\prime},\dots,j_{i-1}^{\prime}=\ell_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\ell_{i-1}^{\prime}
. Now, we have
j_{i}^{\prime}=\nextAlphPos(j_{i-1}^{\prime},|\alphabet(w_{\min}[j_{i-1}+1..j_{i}])|)
and
\ell_{i}^{\prime}=\nextAlphPos_{w_{\min}}(j_{i-1}^{\prime},|\alphabet(w_{\min}[\ell_{i-1}+1..\ell_{i}])|)
. As
|\alphabet(w_{\min}[j_{i-1}+1..j_{i}])|\neq|\alphabet(w_{\min}[\ell_{i-1}+1..\ell_{i}])|
,
w^{\prime}[j^{\prime}_{i}]\neq w^{\prime}[\ell_{i}^{\prime}]
, and thus
f(u_{1})[i]\neq f(u_{2})[i]
. Therefore,
f(u_{1})\neq f(u_{2})
, and thus the statement holds.
∎

Using this construction, we can conclude the result of this section in the following theorem.

Theorem 29
.

W.l.o.g. assume
\Sigma:=\{\mathtt{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathtt{a}_{\sigma}\}
for some
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
w\in\Sigma^{*}
such that w.l.o.g.
\arch_{1}(w)=\mathtt{a}_{1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{\sigma}
and
\arch_{i}(w)=\arch_{i-1}(w)^{R}
for all
i\in[\iota(w)]\setminus\{1\}
. Then for all
w^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
with
\iota(w)=\iota(w^{\prime})
we have
|\ScatFact_{k}(w)|\leq|\ScatFact_{k}(w^{\prime})|
.

Proof.

Let
w
be given as in the claim and let
k\in[|w|]
.
Let
w^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
be any other word with
\iota(w)=\iota(w^{\prime})
.
If
|w^{\prime}|>\iota(w^{\prime})\cdot\sigma
, then we know by Lemma
24
that there exists
a word
w^{\prime\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
with
\iota(w^{\prime\prime})=\iota(w^{\prime})
,
|w^{\prime\prime}|=\iota(w)\cdot\sigma
, and
|\ScatFact_{k}(w^{\prime\prime})|\leq|\ScatFact_{k}(w^{\prime})|
.
Now, by Proposition
28
we know that there exists an injective function

f:\ScatFact_{k}(w)\rightarrow\ScatFact_{k}(w^{\prime\prime})
.
By that, we know that
|\ScatFact_{k}(w)|\leq|\ScatFact_{k}(w^{\prime\prime})|
and as
|\ScatFact_{k}(w^{\prime\prime})|\leq|\ScatFact_{k}(w^{\prime})|
, we also
have
|\ScatFact_{k}(w)|\leq|\ScatFact_{k}(w^{\prime})|
. This concludes this theorem.
∎

Conclusion

Within this work we investigated the minimal and maximal number of
absent scattered factors for a given alphabet
\Sigma
, scattered
factor length
k
and universality index
\iota
. During the investigation we gave two tight bounds for
m
as well as
constructions on
\iota
-universal words over
\Sigma
that omit
this number of absent scattered factors of length
k
. With these
results we obtained a futher restriction on the index of Simon’s Congruence. Two words that are Simon
k
-congurent do have the
same sets of scattered factors of length
k
, i.e., the same set
of absent scattered factors. By giving bounds on the values
m
origin from we decreased the number of words that have to be compared when asking for a tuple of Simon
k
-congruent words. Even though the exact value of the index of Simon’s congruence
\sim_{k}
is still unknown, we gave a lower and upper bound on the absent scattered factors. In contrast to only investigate shortest absent scattered factors, we allow
k
to be arbitrary. Thus, this works extends the previous works by
[
29
]
and
[
14
]
into a better understanding of two words being Simon
k
-congruent for
k
strictly greater than the number of arches plus one.
