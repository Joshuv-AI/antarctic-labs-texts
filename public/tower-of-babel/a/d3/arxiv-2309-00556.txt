The Effect of Punishment and Reward on Cooperation in a Prisoners’ Dilemma Game
Alexander Kangas

Abstract
This work studies the effect of incentives (in the form of punishment and reward) on the equilibrium fraction of cooperators and defectors in an iterated n-person prisoners’ dilemma game. With a finite population of players employing a strategy of nice tit-for-tat or universal defect, an equilibrium fraction of each player-type can be identified from linearized payoff functions. Incentives take the form of targeted and general punishment, and targeted and general reward. The primary contribution of this work is in clearly articulating the design and marginal effect of these incentives on cooperation. Generalizable results indicate that while targeted incentives have the potential to substantially reduce but never entirely eliminate defection, they exhibit diminishing marginal effectiveness. General incentives on the other hand have the potential to eliminate all defection from the population of players. Applications to policy are briefly considered.



1
Introduction

Social dilemmas are characterized by an asymmetry between the payoff to an individual’s self-interested actions and the payoff to acting cooperatively. Pursuit of individual self-interest within groups has the potential to lead to lower total payoffs, particularly when the interaction is expected by those involved to be finite. This stylized picture of group behavior is what has imbued the study of cooperation in social dilemmas with theoretical richness when applied to longer-term repeated interactions (Ostrom 1990). This research aims to delineate the effect of incentives on cooperation using a comparative static approach and to contribute to the literature by delimiting the extent of their influence.

1

Throughout this research, incentives refer to punishment and rewards within the context of cooperation.

Contemporary research on the conditions under which cooperation can be established and maintained within social dilemmas largely took shape in the second half of the twentieth century (Olson 1965; Hardin 1968; Axelrod 1984; Axelrod and Hamilton 1981; Fehr and Gachter 2000), and the literature has both deepened and expanded since (Balliet, Mulder, and Van Lange 2011). This literature is multidisciplinary (Van Lange, Rockenbach, and Yamagishi 2014), drawing from fields such as economics (Bowles and Gintis 2011), psychology (Dawes 1980), political science (Ostrom, Walker, and Gardner 1992), and biology (Nowak 2011). It is also methodologically pluralistic, using both theoretical (Van Lange, Rockenbach, and Yamagishi 2014) and experimental methods (Andreoni, Harbaugh, and Vesterlund 2003).

One strand of this literature explores the influence and effectiveness of pro-social incentives on cooperation (Chen, Sasaki, Brannstrom, and Dieckmann 2014; Rand, Dreber, Ellingsen, Fudenberg, and Nowak 2009). Pro-social incentives include rewards for cooperation and punishments or sanctions for opportunism, and include second-order considerations such as punishments for those who fail to punish opportunism or rewards for those who reward cooperation (Oliver 1980; Okada, Yamamoto, Toriumi, and Sasaki 2015). Although there is some evidence that cooperation has the potential to be spontaneous and self-sustaining within smaller groups (Sugden 1989; Ostrom 2000), efforts to promote cooperation within larger societies is most often explored through peer-to-peer or institutionally enforced mechanisms (Yamagishi 1986; Chen, Sasaki, Brannstrom, and Diekmann 2015).

Across many social dilemmas, rewards and punishments may differ in their comparative effectiveness in promoting cooperation, yet both have the potential to change the behavior of individuals in pro-social ways (Balliet, Mulder, and Van Lange 2011). By design, pro-social incentives – such as a tax on defection (Clarke 1980; Tideman and Tullock 1975) – motivate players to act cooperatively by punishing and thus deterring those otherwise inclined to pursue anti-social opportunistic behavior, or by rewarding cooperative behavior. Confidence in the soundness and effectiveness of such incentives make it possible for participants to forego short-term opportunistic self-interest because they can be reasonably sure that others will forego opportunism as well. In this way, incentives can be thought of as a commitment device, allowing participants to act in concert with group interests without undue concern that others may be gaining a relative advantage by acting opportunistically (Schelling 1997).

This research contributes to the literature on the design and impact of institutional incentives on cooperation by introducing a model and comparative static analysis to better understand the marginal effects and limitations of incentives on cooperation. In what follows, a prisoners’ dilemma model is developed to describe how the intensity of punishment or reward can influence cooperation in a population of players. A calibration of the model is then followed by a discussion of targeted versus general incentives, which extends the simple model. A conclusion briefly describes potential policy applications.

5
Conclusion

The prisoners’ dilemma model developed in this research and concomitant comparative static analysis has shown that incentives are effective in promoting cooperation in a repeated interaction. The two types of incentives explored above include targeted incentives (specific to the resulting payoffs) and general incentives (specific to a strategy profile or player-type). Targeted pro-social incentives are characterized by diminishing marginal effectiveness and are unable to entirely eliminate from the population of players those willing to play the always defect strategy. General incentives on the other hand have the potential to eliminate from the population of players those willing to defect, yet suffer from the fact that they must be applied not to a specific outcome but rather to a player-type, for example applying to those players who have been found (or are suspected of) employing the defect strategy, which raises both implementation cost considerations as well as social and ethical questions that lie outside the scope of this research.

In a stylized way, one mechanism to conceptualize a general punishment is through informal social norms as opposed to centralized institutional implementation. Social norms are rules about behavior, and a violation of norms confer negative reputational effects, effectively dispensing social punishment and promoting cooperation. This imparts meaning to the general punishment set forth in this research which penalizes a player type by amount
\nu
, lowering future payoffs relative to cooperators. The opposite can be said of general reward, which could be thought of as positive reputational effects that are not specifically implemented or controlled by an institutional authority, yet conferred on individuals for engaging in pro-social, cooperative ways.

5.1
Policy Applications

Potential policy applications of the targeted and general punishments described above include incentives meant to deter tax avoidance or evasion or discourage the creation of systemically risky financial products.

Tax evasion is a social dilemma whereby despite the collective benefits conferred through taxation, an individual has the potential to benefit by acting opportunistically (i.e., not paying taxes knowing others do so), which will deter at least some other individuals from a willingness to cooperate and abide by tax law. This in turn causes further tax evasion and a general erosion of cooperation with respect to tax compliance. A targeted penalty on tax non-compliance increases overall cooperation. A general penalty (described above) is conferred on those engaging in tax non-compliance by making known to others more broadly those who cheat, thereby creating negative reputational effects or through general reward by making tax compliance a norm.

Financial regulation provides another example. One goal of financial regulation is to ensure safe and stable financial intermediation. In practice, this means enabling risk-takers to pass over short-term super-profits in order to take a longer-term, systemically sound approach, which is more easily achieved as long as its common knowledge that others are doing so as well. Of course, its true that anti-social “rewards” (a negative value for
\theta
) exist in the form of large bonuses for risky trading behavior, which inhibit the pursuit of socially optimal levels of cooperation within the financial system and lead to a suboptimal Nash equilibrium. The sanctions and penalties embodied in financial regulation have the potential to serve as a commitment device that holds financial actors to the “both cooperate” payoff despite the existence of a higher profit opportunity through defection. The aforementioned bonuses limit this potential, as demonstrated above. In a similar way, general punishments and rewards work to enable a shared norm of systemically robust behavior by impacting certain jobs, companies, or industries.

In all instances described above, individuals face a conflict between long-term group and short-term individual self-interest. This research has demonstrated that effective punishment and reward better enable individuals to cooperate by reducing the potential for
and
incentive to take advantage of others.
