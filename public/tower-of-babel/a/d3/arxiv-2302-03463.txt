The θ \theta -formulation of the 2D elastica – Buckling and boundary layer theory.
Gregory Kozyreff 1 , Emmanuel Siéfert 2 , Basile Radisson 2 and Fabian Brau 2

Abstract
The equations of a planar elastica under pressure can be rewritten in a useful form by parametrising the variables in terms of the local orientation angle, θ \theta , instead of the arc length. This “ θ \theta -formulation” lends itself to a particularly easy boundary layer analysis in the limit of weak bending stiffness. Within this parameterization, boundary layers are located at inflexion points, where θ \theta is extremum, and they connect regions of low and large curvature. A simple composite solution is derived without resorting to elliptic functions and integrals. This approximation can be used as an elementary building block to describe complex shapes. Applying this theory to the study of an elastic ring under uniform pressure and subject to a set of point forces, we discover a snapping instability. This instability is confirmed by numerical simulations. Finally, we carry out experiments and find good agreement of the theory with the experimental shape of the deformed elastica.



I
Introduction

The problem of finding the shape of an elastica under various loads and constraints appears in many contexts of the applied sciences and at a wide variety of scales, from biological cells to suspended bridges. After centuries of analysis
Love1927
;
Levien2008
, one may consider it to be solved, at least as far as two-dimensional deformations is concerned.
A general solution of the associated differential equations is quoted in Love’s treatise
Love1927
and
further developments abound in the literature on its basis
Linner1998
;
Pozrikidis2002
;
Vassilev2008
;
Djondjorov2011
;
Mora2012
;
Batista2014
;
Marple2015
;
Giomi2019
;
Barbieri2020
. It is thus often possible to explicitly write the coordinate of any point of the elastic curve as a function of arc length in a wide range of situations. A weakness of the known solutions, however, is that they involve several elliptic functions, which can themselves appear as arguments of inverse trigonometric functions. Furthermore, the parameters of these elliptic functions are themselves functions of the roots of a fourth order polynomial in which the mechanical parameters of the system appear. Hence, despite being undeniably useful, the exact solution can be quite hard to analyse by inspection. Neither does it simplify in the limit of a small bending stiffness or long elastica. In response to this issue, the purpose of this paper is to put forward another way of parameterising the elastic curve that may lead to great simplification. Very simply, we propose to express the curvature and the internal forces not as a function of arc length but as a function of the local angle,
\theta
, made by the tangent of the elastic curve with a reference direction. We thus call it the
\theta
-formulation of the elastica. It is no more than the application of an old trick to reduce the order of a differential system
BObook
, which has been used before
Shao-guang1996
;
Shao-guang1997
. What hasn’t been appreciated so far, however, is its great advantage for asymptotic analysis,
i.e.
, in the treatment of boundary layers.

After deriving this
\theta
-formulation, we first show how it gives way to an economical resolution of some classical problems. Next, we exploit the limit of a long elastica or, equivalently, of weak bending stiffness. We assume that a net pressure acts on one side of the elastica and allow that a set of point forces be applied. In particular, we devise a general framework to treat closed rings that are either compressed or inflated while being either locally stretched or pinched. We also carry out experiments of such elastic rings and find that the asymptotic approximation faithfully reproduce observations. In the case of pinching with prescribed forces, we identify a snapping instability, past which the ring collapses to self-contact. Finally, we indicate how the same technique could be applied to more general problems.

II
Derivation of the
\theta
-formulation

The differential equations governing the shape of an elastica in a plane can be written as

\displaystyle\partial_{s}n

\displaystyle=p-\kappa t,

(1)

\displaystyle\partial_{s}t

\displaystyle=\kappa n,

(2)

\displaystyle B\partial_{s}\kappa

\displaystyle=-n,

(3)

where
\kappa
,
t
, and
n
are the curvature, tension and shear force, respectively,
s
is the arc length, and
B
is the bending stiffness. Given the 1D nature of the elastica, the pressure
p
appearing in Eq. (
1
) has dimensions of Nm
-1
. Knowing
\kappa
, the local angle with respect to the horizontal direction is deduced from

\partial_{s}\theta=\kappa

(4)

and the cartesian coordinates along the elastic curve are solution of
\partial_{s}x=\cos\theta
,

\partial_{s}y=\sin\theta
.
Multiplying Eq. (
3
) by
\kappa
and summing with (
2
), one easily finds that

B\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}+t=H,

(5)

where
H
is a constant (it is equivalent to the constant
\mu
appearing in various papers
Arreaga2002
;
Vassilev2008
;
Djondjorov2011
and
C
in
Marple2015
). This allows one to eliminate the variable
t
and reduce the order of the differential system (
1
)-(
4
) by one. Note that if we write
H
as
H=B\kappa_{0}^{2}/2
, then a state with
\kappa=\kappa_{0}
for all
s
corresponds to
t=n=p=0
. In that case,
H
measures the curvature of a stress-free elastic ring. On the other hand,
\kappa=0
everywhere corresponds to the state of a straight elastica with
n=p=0
and a uniform tension
t=H
. Let us also note that, if
t>0
anywhere in the elastica, then
H>0
. Stated differently, the elastica must be everywhere under axial compression for
H
to be negative.

Using (
5
) to eliminate
t
, Eq. (
1
) yields

\partial_{s}n=p-\kappa\left(H-B\kappa^{2}/2\right),

(6)

which, together with Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
), yields a third-order differential system. A further reduction of order is achieved by expressing
n
and
\kappa
as functions of
\theta
. Firstly, writing
n=n\left(\theta(s)\right)
, we have
\partial_{s}n=\kappa\partial_{\theta}n
. Hence, Eq. (
6
) becomes

\partial_{\theta}n=\frac{p}{\kappa}-H+B\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}.

(7)

Secondly, if we write

\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}=q\left(\theta(s)\right),

(8)

we obtain, by differentiating each side with respect to
s
,

\partial_{s}\kappa=\partial_{\theta}q.

(9)

Hence, Eq. (
3
) yields

n=-B\partial_{\theta}q.

(10)

Eventually, Eq. (
7
) becomes

B\left(\partial^{2}_{\theta}q+q\right)+\frac{p}{\kappa}=H.

(11)

This equation, which we presently call the
\theta
-formulation, is not particularly useful from a numerical point of view but can lead to significant analytical progress, particularly in the limit of small and large curvature. Here, “small” and “large” curvature respectively refer to situations where
p/\kappa
or
B(\partial^{2}_{\theta}q+q)
dominate the left hand side, respectively. Evidently, both limits considerably simplify the resolution of Eq. (
11
). To make this statement more systematic, let us rescale
\kappa
(and therefore
q
) as

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{H}\,K,

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{p}{H}\right)^{2}Q,

(12)

provided that
p
is constant and not zero. With this scaling, we still have
K^{2}/2=Q
and Eq. (
11
) becomes

\epsilon^{2}\left(\partial^{2}_{\theta}Q+Q\right)+\frac{1}{K}=1,

(13)

where

\epsilon^{2}=\frac{Bp^{2}}{H^{3}}.

(14)

Viewing
x
and
y
as functions of
\theta
, we have
\partial_{s}(x,y)=\kappa\partial_{\theta}(x,y)
. Hence, given
\kappa(\theta)
, the shape of the elastica is obtained by integrating

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}x

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos\theta}{\kappa(\theta)},

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}y

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin\theta}{\kappa(\theta)},

(15)

or, in a more compact way,

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}z

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{i\theta}}{\kappa(\theta)},

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=x+iy.

(16)

The advantage of a complex representation is that translation by a vector
(x_{0},y_{0})
amount to adding of
x_{0}+iy_{0}
to
z
, while rotation by an angle
\alpha
is achieved by a multiplication by
e^{i\alpha}
. This is useful for constructing elastic curves as pieces of solutions of the above differential equation joined together.
Furthermore, from Eqs. (
5
) and (
10
), we have

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=H\left(1-\epsilon^{2}Q\right),

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=-\epsilon^{2}H\,\partial_{\theta}Q.

(17)

So far, our analysis has been exact. In Secs.
III
and
IV
, we show how the above formulation can be useful to treat some classical problems. Next, in Sec.
V
, we derive asymptotic approximations of
K
(hence, of
Q
) in the small-
\epsilon
limit using the method of matched asymptotic expansions
BObook
. More specifically, we derive simple formulas describing how the elastica switches between a small positive curvature to a large negative one. As a function of
\theta
,
K
displays boundary layers near extremal value of
\theta
, which are inflexion points. By convention here, the positivity of the curvature is defined relative to the sign of the pressure: a positive curvature is understood here as being of the same sign as
p/H
. With this convention in mind, we may treat in one go elastic rings that are subjected to an inflating pressure or to an external compression.

III
Application 0: Euler’s elastica revisited

Before investigating boundary layers, we wish to highlight the usefulness of writing Eq. (
11
) for the elastica problem posed and solved by the Bernoullis and Euler
Levien2008
. In that case,
p=0
, so the rescaling (
12
) does not apply. However, Eq. (
11
) for the curvature is now turned into a linear equation of the simplest kind. Its solution is simply and exactly

\displaystyle q(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{H}{B}\left(1+c\cos\theta\right),

\displaystyle\kappa(\theta)

\displaystyle=\pm\sqrt{\frac{H}{B}}\sqrt{2\left(1+c\cos\theta\right)}.

(18)

where
c
is an arbitrary constant and
\theta=0
defines a direction of reference. Specifying this direction of reference is equivalent to imposing the second constant of integration of the equation for
q
. With
x
and
y
respectively defined as the cartesian coordinates along and perpendicular to the reference direction, they satisfy Eqs. (
15
).
They yield

\displaystyle x(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2B}{H}}\Bigg\{x_{0}\pm\frac{\sqrt{1+c}}{c}\left[E\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\Big|\frac{2c}{1+c}\right)-\frac{1}{1+c}F\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\Big|\frac{2c}{1+c}\right)\right]\Bigg\},

(19)

\displaystyle y(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2B}{H}}\left[y_{0}\mp\frac{1}{c}\left(\sqrt{1+c\cos\theta}-1\right)\right].

(20)

Above,
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
are arbitrary constants, while the function
F(\phi|m)
and
E(\phi|m)
are the incomplete elliptic integrals of the first and second kinds, respectively
Abramowitz
. Note that, up to a constant vertical shift, the above expression makes it directly apparent that
y\propto\kappa
.

Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
) are an alternative parametric expression of the classical elastica curves to what is found,
e.g.
in Refs.
Love1927
;
Vassilev2008
. It appears that the
\theta
-formulation affords a somewhat more economical and direct route to the solution, as well as a minimal use of elliptic functions. The shape of the elastica is presently determined by the parameter
c
, while
H
simply contributes to a scale factor, see Fig.
1
. It is required that
-1<c<\infty
, with
c=0
obviously corresponding to a perfect circle (
\kappa
is constant in that case). The value
c=1
corresponds to a single loop and so does the limiting case
c\to-1
. For
c>1
,
q(\theta)
vanishes for some values of
\theta
, indicating inflexion points. At these, different instances of Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
) can be used with distinct values of
c_{x}
and
c_{y}
and possibly a change of the sign of
\kappa
to construct a smooth elastica curve in a piecewise manner. As an illustration of this last point, we draw in Fig.
1
the elastic curve for
c=2.16279
. This is the limiting value for the existence of self-crossing.

Figure 1:
Some examples of classic elastic curves (
p=0
) obtained for different values of
c
in Eqs. (
20
) and (
19
). With
c>1
, the curve contains inflexion points, at which copies of Eqs. (
20
) and (
19
) with different integration constants
c_{x,y}
can be smoothly joined. An example is given with
c=2.16279
, value above which such composite curves without self-crossing can be constructed.

It will be useful in what follows to detail further the one-loop solution with
c=-1
, also known as syntractix curve
(
Salmon1873
, p. 277)
. Then
q=2(H/B)\sin^{2}\theta/2
,
\kappa=\pm 2\sqrt{H/B}\sin\theta/2
and the equation for
x
and
y
can be integrated as

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=x_{0}\pm\sqrt{\frac{B}{H}}\left(2\cos\frac{\theta}{2}+\ln\left|4\tan\frac{\theta}{4}\right|\right),

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=y_{0}\pm 2\sqrt{\frac{B}{H}}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}.

(21)

In complex notation (
z=x+iy
), this is

z=z_{0}\pm\sqrt{\frac{B}{H}}\left(2e^{i\theta/2}+\ln\left|4\tan\frac{\theta}{4}\right|\right).

(22)

(The factor
4
inside the logarithm above is introduced for convenience, in view of studying this expression in the limit where the argument
\theta\to 0
.)

IV
Application 1: buckling instability of a compressed elastic ring

To further demonstrate the interest of Eq. (
11
) or, equivalently, Eq. (
13
), let us consider a circular ring subject to uniform inward pressure and compute the buckling threshold at which it ceases to be circular. We thus look for a solution of the form

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle\sim K_{0}+\delta\cos(n\theta)+\dotsb,

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle\sim K_{0}^{2}/2+\delta K_{0}\cos\left(n\theta\right)+\dotsb,

(23)

where
\delta\ll 1
and
n
is a positive integer. Above,
K_{0}
is the rescaled curvature prior to buckling. Substituting this ansatz in Eq. (
13
), we get (
\epsilon^{2}
is not necessarily small, here, and not necessarily positive either)

\epsilon^{2}\frac{K_{0}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{K_{0}}-1+\delta\left[\epsilon^{2}K_{0}\left(-n^{2}+1\right)-\frac{1}{K_{0}^{2}}\right]+O\left(\delta^{2}\right)=0.

(24)

This yields two equations for the two unknowns
K_{0}
and
\epsilon^{2}
:

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}\frac{K_{0}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{K_{0}}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}K_{0}\left(-n^{2}+1\right)-\frac{1}{K_{0}^{2}}

\displaystyle=0.

(25)

Given that
n
was defined as a positive integer, it directly follows from the second equation above that
n\neq 1
and, hence,
n\geq 2
. Given the ring radius
R=1/\kappa
prior to buckling, we have from (
12
) that
K_{0}=H/(pR)
. Substituting this expression in the second of Eqs. (
25
) and using Eq. (
14
) to eliminate
\epsilon^{2}
, one directly obtains the classical buckling pressure derived by Halphen
Halphen1888
(see also
Arreaga2002
):

-p=\frac{B}{R^{3}}\left(n^{2}-1\right).

(26)

Meanwhile, from the first of Eqs. (
25
), we obtain

H=\frac{B}{2R^{2}}+pR=\frac{B}{R^{2}}\left(\frac{3}{2}-n^{2}\right),

(27)

which completes the description. Note that
H<0
, and hence
\epsilon^{2}<0
in the present situation. We thus see that the
\theta
-formulation provides a particularly short route to the answer of this classical problem. Incidentally, by Eq. (
5
), the compression along the elastica at buckling threshold is
-t=(n^{2}-1)B/R^{2}
. In terms of its half-perimeter
L
, this is
\pi^{2}(n^{2}-1)B/L^{2}
, to be compared with the critical buckling load of compression
\pi^{2}n^{2}B/L^{2}
for a hinged straight beam of the same length
Howell09
. Recently, the dynamical aspects of this buckling instability have been examined both theoretically and experimentally
Box2020
;
Kodio2020
. Interestingly, they used the analogy with a straight beam under effective compression as a toy model for their experiment and, in
Kodio2020
, started out their analysis by introducing the dimensionless parameter
P=pR^{3}/B
. We will return to this adimensional parameter in §
VI
.

V
Application 2: small-
\epsilon
hook shape

From now on, we assume a small bending stiffness, in the sense that
0<\epsilon\ll 1
, and exploit this limit using boundary layer analysis. Implicitly, we restrict our attention to
H>0
, which includes all situations where
t>0
at least at one point in the elastica. We first consider an elementary curve made up of two segments, one with small curvature, the other with a large negative curvature. The resulting hook-curve will be used in latter sections as a building block to construct more complex shapes, such as the experimental shape that will be produced in §
VI
, see Figs
3
and
5
.

V.1
Region of small curvature

In Eq. (
13
), let us assume that
K=O(1)
. Then, letting
\epsilon\to 0
, we obtain

K\sim K_{I}=1+O(\epsilon^{2}).

(28)

In view of Eq. (
12
), the above expression means
p\sim\kappa H
. Next,
H\sim t
, see Eq. (
17
). Therefore,
K\sim 1
simply expresses that

p\sim\kappa t,

(29)

which is nothing but the classical force balance equation of a membrane. Furthermore, since the curvature is approximately constant, the elastica is locally circular and we may infer without further calculation that,

\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}\sim\begin{pmatrix}x_{I}\\
y_{I}\end{pmatrix}\sim\begin{pmatrix}x_{0}\\
y_{0}\end{pmatrix}+\frac{H}{p}\begin{pmatrix}\sin\theta\\
-\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}+O(\epsilon^{2}),

(30)

where
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
are constants of integration. In complex notation, this is

z\sim z_{I}\sim z_{0}-\frac{iH}{p}e^{i\theta}.

(31)

V.2
Region of large absolute curvature

Where the curvature is large, let us assume that

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{-1}K_{II},

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{-2}Q_{II},

(recall that
Q=K^{2}/2
)

(32)

where
K_{II},Q_{II}=O(1)
. This leads to a different balance between terms in
Eq. (
13
), which becomes

\partial^{2}_{\theta}Q_{II}+Q_{II}+\frac{\epsilon}{K_{II}}=1,

(33)

Neglecting the
O(\epsilon)
term above, we directly obtain

\displaystyle Q_{II}

\displaystyle\sim Q_{II}^{(0)}=1+c\cos\left(\theta-\alpha\right),

\displaystyle\to K_{II}

\displaystyle\sim K_{II}^{(0)}=-\sqrt{2\left[1+c\cos\left(\theta-\alpha\right)\right]},

(34)

where
c
and
\alpha
are constants to be determined. Since the pressure term
\epsilon/K_{II}
is negligible in first approximation, we recover, to leading order, the classical Euler elastica of §
III
with the horizontal direction rotated by an angle
\alpha
. The negative sign of
K_{II}
will be justified in the next section. In particular, we may directly infer in the case
c=-1
that [see Eq. (
22
)]

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=-1:

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\sim z_{II}\sim\hat{z}_{0}\pm\sqrt{\frac{B}{H}}e^{i\alpha}\left(2e^{i(\theta-\alpha)/2}+\ln\left|4\tan\frac{\theta-\alpha}{4}\right|\right).

(35)

In this last expression,
\sqrt{B/H}
is in fact equal to
\epsilon H/p
, which makes explicit that the region of space spanned by the large-curvature region is a factor
\epsilon
smaller than in low-curvature region. The factor
e^{i\alpha}
in (
35
) indicates that the elastica is rotated by an angle
\alpha
in the complex plane, and the argument
\theta-\alpha
in the trigonometric functions come from the fact that the direction of reference for this functional dependence was also rotated by an angle
\alpha
compared to §
III
. Alternatively, one can note that
\kappa(\theta)=\kappa_{\text{E}}(\theta-\alpha)
, where
\kappa_{\text{E}}(\theta)
is the curvature derived in §
III
. Then Eq. (
16
) becomes

\partial_{\theta}z=\frac{e^{i\theta}}{\kappa_{\text{E}}(\theta-\alpha)}=e^{i\alpha}\frac{e^{i(\theta-\alpha)}}{\kappa_{\text{E}}(\theta-\alpha)},

(36)

and the link between (
22
) and (
35
) directly follows. Finally, the
\pm
sign in (
35
) is deduced as follows: in (
34
),
-\sqrt{2[1-\cos(\theta-\alpha)]}=\pm 2\sin[(\theta-\alpha)/2]
. Hence, if
\theta<\alpha
, a
+
sign corresponds to a negative curvature and
vice versa
.

V.3
Inflexion point

We now examine the vicinity of the inflexion point, where
\kappa
vanishes,
i.e.
, where
\theta
reaches an extremum. As a function of
\theta
, the curvature undergoes rapid variations in order to achieve the connection between the two limiting behaviours,
K_{I}
and
\epsilon^{-1}K_{II}
. Denoting the extremum value of
\theta
by
\theta_{*}
, let us write

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=K_{III}(\xi),

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=Q_{III}(\xi),

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta-\theta_{*}}{\epsilon}.

(37)

Written in this way, the functions
K_{III}
and
Q_{III}
vary rapidly in the vicinity of
\theta_{*}
in the small-
\epsilon
limit. Eq. (
13
) then becomes

\partial_{\xi}^{2}Q_{III}+\frac{1}{K_{III}}=1+O\left(\epsilon^{2}\right).

(38)

Expressing this equation in terms of
K_{III}
only yields

\partial_{\xi}\left(K_{III}\partial_{\xi}K_{III}\right)+\frac{1}{K_{III}}\sim 1.

(39)

Multiplying by
K_{III}\partial_{\xi}K_{III}
and integrating, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\left(K_{III}\partial_{\xi}K_{III}\right)^{2}+K_{III}=\frac{1}{2}K_{III}^{2}+\mathcal{E}.

(40)

where
\mathcal{E}
is a constant. On the positive-curvature side of the inflexion point, we have the matching condition

\lim_{\xi\to\pm\infty}K_{III}\sim\lim_{\theta\to\theta_{*}}K_{I}=1.

(41)

Above, the limit
\xi\to-\infty
is implied if
\theta_{*}
is a maximum and
\xi\to\infty
if
\theta_{*}
is a minimum. In either case, it follows that
\mathcal{E}=1/2
in Eq. (
40
), which can now be rewritten in terms as

\left(K_{III}\partial_{\xi}K_{III}\right)^{2}=\left(K_{III}-1\right)^{2},

(42)

that is:

K_{III}\partial_{\xi}K_{III}=\pm\left(K_{III}-1\right).

(43)

This is easily integrated as

e^{K_{III}-1}(K_{III}-1)=-e^{\pm\xi-1},

(44)

where we used the fact
\xi=0
is the inflexion point,
i.e.
that
K_{III}(0)=0
. In order for the solution
K_{III}
of the above equation to be real, it is necessary that
\pm\xi<0
. If the inflexion angle
\theta_{*}
is a local maximum, then
K_{III}
is defined for negative values of
\xi
. Conversely, if
\theta_{*}
is a local minimum, then
K_{III}
is defined for positive values of
\xi
. Therefore, in the right hand side of Eq. (
44
), we must have

\displaystyle-e^{\xi-1}

if
\theta_{*}
is maximum,

\displaystyle-e^{-\xi-1}

if
\theta_{*}
is minimum.

The transcendental equation
we^{w}=z
possesses two real solutions for
w
, namely the Lambert functions
W_{0}(z)
and
W_{-1}(z)
. Hence, two solutions exist for
K_{III}
. The former is

K_{III}=1+W_{0}(-e^{\pm\xi-1}),

(45)

with far field behaviour

\displaystyle K_{III}

\displaystyle\sim 1,

\displaystyle Q_{III}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{2},

\displaystyle\text{as }\pm\xi\to-\infty.

(46)

The latter is

K_{III}=1+W_{-1}(-e^{\pm\xi-1}),

(47)

with

\displaystyle K_{III}

\displaystyle\sim\pm\xi,

\displaystyle Q_{III}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}=\frac{\left(\theta-\theta_{*}\right)^{2}}{2\epsilon^{2}},

\displaystyle\text{as }\pm\xi\to-\infty.

(48)

The two solutions coincide and vanish at the inflexion point
\xi=0
. Moreover, it is immediate to check [compare (
28
) and (
46
)] that the solution involving the function
W_{0}
naturally matches with the low-curvature solution
K_{I}
. Let us now consider the limiting behaviour of
Q
in region
II
as
\theta\to\theta_{*}
. Expanding (
34
) to second order in
\theta-\theta_{*}
and recalling the scaling (
32
):

\epsilon^{-2}Q_{II}\sim\epsilon^{-2}\left[1+c\cos\left(\theta_{*}-\alpha\right)-c\sin\left(\theta_{*}-\alpha\right)\left(\theta-\theta_{*}\right)-c\cos\left(\theta_{*}-\alpha\right)\frac{\left(\theta-\theta_{*}\right)^{2}}{2}\right].

(49)

This matches (
48
) if
\alpha=\theta_{*}
and
c=-1
. Furthermore, with
W_{-1}
, the function
K_{III}
is negative and, hence, so is the curvature, in the high-curvature region. This justifies the negative sign of
K_{II}
in (
34

Finally, let us compute local approximations of the elastic curve in the vicinity of the inflexion point:

Snap-through buckling

VI.3

In order to quantitatively assess the regime of validity of the asymptotic theory, we enquire under which condition self-contact arises in both scenarios (force- or displacement-controlled).

\displaystyle\sim p\pi R\frac{\cos\theta_{1}}{\theta_{1}}+O\left(\epsilon\right),

\displaystyle 0<\theta_{1}.

(78)

This formula for
f
is consistent with intuition: flatter shapes correspond to smaller
\theta_{1}
and hence to larger stretching forces. Above, there is a maximum allowed value of
\theta_{1}
to prevent self-crossing of the two segments of low curvature. The maximum allowed value of
\theta_{1}
increases with
\epsilon
as follows. In the low-curvature region (
K\sim 1
), the curvature is nearly constant, so
\theta(s)\sim\kappa_{0}s
. At the inflexion point
\theta_{1}\sim\kappa_{0}s_{1}
. Meanwhile, the vertical difference between the center of the stretched elastica and the inflexion point is approximately
\kappa_{0}s_{1}^{2}/2=\theta_{1}^{2}/(2\kappa_{0})
. To prevent self-crossing this vertical elevation must be met by an equivalent opposite vertical displacement in the region of large curvature (see Fig.
6
), the latter being on the order of
\epsilon/\kappa_{0}
. Hence, the maximum allowed value of
\theta_{1}
is
O\left(\epsilon^{1/2}\right)
.

At this stage, it is clear that the above configuration can easily be generalized to a larger number of stretching forces, just as in the previous section.

VIII
Generalization with body forces

The success of Eq. (
11
) to address complex elastica shape was due the reduction of the original problem by two differential orders: one thanks to the existence of the first integral
H
, the other by switching the curve parameterization to
\theta
. In the presence of body forces,
H
is not constant anymore but an asymptotic treatment along the previous lines can still be envisaged. Eqs. (
1
) to (
3
) become

\displaystyle\partial_{s}n

\displaystyle=p-\kappa t-\mathcal{F}_{n},

\displaystyle\partial_{s}t

\displaystyle=\kappa n-\mathcal{F}_{t},

\displaystyle B\partial_{s}\kappa

\displaystyle=-n.

(79)

where
\mathcal{F}_{t}
and
\mathcal{F}_{n}
are external axial and shear force per unit length, respectively. Recently, for instance, an analytically solvable model for elastic wrinkling on a nonlinear foundation was introduced, in which
\mathcal{F}_{t}=0
while
\mathcal{F}_{n}
scales as some power of the local displacement of the elastica
Foster2022a
;
Foster2022
;
Foster2022c
(See also Refs.
Brau2010
;
Diamant2011
;
Gordillo2019
;
Michaels2019
;
Michaels2021
for more experimental and theoretical results on the subject.)

We may continue to define
H
as
B\kappa^{2}/2+t
. It now satisfies

\partial_{s}H=-\mathcal{F}_{t}.

(80)

In the
\theta
-formulation, we have

\displaystyle B\left(\partial^{2}_{\theta}q+q\right)+\frac{p-\mathcal{F}_{n}}{\kappa}

\displaystyle=H,

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}H

\displaystyle=\frac{-\mathcal{F}_{t}}{\kappa}.

(81)

In regions of large curvature, we continue to benefit from the simple leading order problem

\displaystyle B\left(\partial^{2}_{\theta}q+q\right)

\displaystyle\sim H,

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle\sim\text{const.},

(82)

and we recover the approximation of previous sections. This time, however, the value of
H
generally varies between boundary layers. On the other hand, if
\kappa
is ‘small’, we have
H\sim t
and we may neglect terms proportional to
B
in Eqs. (
81
). We thus have

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle\sim\frac{p-\mathcal{F}_{n}}{H},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{H}\partial_{\theta}H

\displaystyle\sim\frac{-\mathcal{F}_{t}}{p-\mathcal{F}_{n}}.

(83)

Suppose for instance that gravity acts in the vertical direction, so that
\mathcal{F}_{n}=-\rho g\cos\theta
,
\mathcal{F}_{t}=-\rho g\sin\theta
, where
\rho
is the mass per unit length. The equation for
H
above is easily solved in that case. We have

H\sim\frac{\rho gh}{a+\cos\theta},

(84)

for some constant
h
, where we define

a=\frac{p}{\rho g}.

(85)

Meanwhile
\kappa\sim\left(a+\cos\theta\right)^{2}/h
. Then, integration of Eqs. (
15
) yields, up to an arbitrary translation,

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2h}{\left(1-a^{2}\right)^{3/2}}\arctanh\left(\frac{1-a}{\sqrt{1-a^{2}}}\tan\frac{\theta}{2}\right)-\frac{ah\sin\theta}{\left(1-a^{2}\right)\left(a+\cos\theta\right)},

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle\sim\frac{h}{a+\cos\theta}.

(86)

Finally near an inflexion point, the same local analysis as in Sec.
V.3
applies to leading order.

IX
Conclusions and perspectives

The present boundary layer theory leads to a composite solution that can be used as a building-block to describe a wide variety of complex shapes. With
\theta
as the curve parameter, the solution takes an elementary form. The only concession to the use of special functions is in the vicinity of inflexion points, where the Lambert functions
W_{0}
and
W_{1}
arise. Thanks to this simplified picture, simple local expressions of force balance can be derived, allowing intuitive mechanical analysis. This gives confidence, in particular, in the surprising prediction of snapping instability of pinched peanuts and
N
-foil shapes past a certain applied force. It would be interesting to see if such a scenario could find application in engineering or in microbiology. Furthermore, a good agreement was obtained both with direct numerical simulations and experiments.

Not all possible analyses afforded by the simple
\theta
-formulation have been exposed here. First, it is in principle not difficult to compute the first corrections to the leading order expressions that we have derived to obtain more accurate formula and enlarge the range of values of the small parameter
\epsilon
where they are reliable. Secondly, not all possible shapes have been envisaged within the present framework. We have already mentioned how contact points with friction,
i.e.
, with a non vanishing tangent component, can be handled. Another situation that we haven’t explored is the possibility to have segments of large, opposite curvature in succession, rather than the low-high curvature detailed in Sec.
V
. Let us briefly explain how this could in principle be handled. One would need to match two regions of large curvatures with opposite signs, each described asymptotically by expressions of the form (
34
). The intermediate region is now still described as in Sec.
V.3
, only this time the constant of integration
\mathcal{E}
appearing in (
40
) differs from
1/2
. This equation can still be integrated implicitly, yielding
\xi
as a function of
K_{III}
. Setting
\mathcal{E}=1/2+b^{2}
, one can derive

\sqrt{b^{2}+\left(K_{III}-1\right)^{2}}+\arcsinh\left(\frac{K_{III}-1}{b}\right)=\pm\xi+c

(87)

where
c
is a constant, but the inversion of the resulting formula is not straightforward. In the limit
\xi=\pm\infty
, one obtains the desired behaviour
K_{III}\sim\pm\xi
. One limit in which the above expression can be inverted asymptotically is
b\to\infty
. We do not pursue the analysis here but note that this limit could be relevant in situations where two point forces of opposite signs are applied at close locations of the elastica rather than far appart.
