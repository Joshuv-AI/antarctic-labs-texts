Last passage isometries for the directed landscape
Duncan Dauvergne

Abstract
Consider the restriction of the directed landscape ℒ ⁡ ( x , s , y , t ) \mathcal{L}(x,s;y,t) to a set of the form { x 1 , … , x k } × { s 0 } × ℝ × { t 0 } \{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\times\{s_{0}\}\times\mathbb{R}\times\{t_{0}\} . We show that on any such set, the directed landscape is given by a last passage problem across k k locally Brownian functions. The k k functions in this last passage isometry are built from certain marginals of the extended directed landscape. As applications of this construction, we show that the Airy difference profile is locally absolutely continuous with respect to Brownian local time, that the KPZ fixed point started from two narrow wedges has a Brownian-Bessel decomposition around its cusp point, and that the directed landscape is a function of its geodesic shapes. † † Keywords : last passage percolation, directed landscape, KPZ universality, Airy sheet, Brownian local time † † MSC Class: 60K35 † † Data availability statement: Data sharing not applicable to this article as no datasets were generated or analysed during the current study.

Introduction

Let
f
be a sequence of continuous functions
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},i\in\mathbb{Z}
. For a nonincreasing cadlag function
\pi
from
[x,y]
to the integer interval
\left\llbracket m,n\right\rrbracket
, henceforth a
path
from
(x,n)
to
(y,m)
, define the length of
\pi
with respect to the environment
f
by

\|\pi\|_{f}=\sum_{i=m}^{n}f_{i}(\pi_{i})-f_{i}(\pi_{i+1}).

(1)

Here
\pi_{i}=\inf\{t\in[x,y]:\pi(t)<i\}
is the time when
\pi_{i}
jumps off of line
i
, and if this set is empty we set
\pi_{i}=y
. We will think of the space
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z}
in matrix coordinates, so that paths move up across the page, see Figure
1
. For
x\leq y\in\mathbb{R}
and
m\leq n\in\mathbb{Z}
, define the
last passage value across the environment
f
by

f[(x,n)\to(y,m)]=\sup_{\pi}\|\pi\|_{f},

(2)

where the supremum is over all paths from
(x,n)
to
(y,m)
.
A path
\pi
that achieves this supremum is a
geodesic
. When the environment is a collection of independent two-sided Brownian motions
B=\{B_{i}:i\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, the Brownian last passage percolation
(x,m;y,n)\mapsto B[(x,m)\to(y,n)]
has a four-parameter scaling limit, recently constructed in
[
12
]
. This limit is the directed landscape
\mathcal{L}
. It is a random continuous function from the parameter space

\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}=\{u=(p;q)=(x,s;y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:s<t\}

to
\mathbb{R}
. More recently,
\mathcal{L}
was shown to be the scaling limit of other integrable models of last passage percolation
[
14
]
, and
\mathcal{L}
is conjectured to be the scaling limit of all random growth and random metric models in the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) universality class. For background on last passage percolation and the KPZ universality class, see the books and review articles
[
32
,
9
,
31
,
36
]
and references therein.

A priori, there is no reason to expect that
\mathcal{L}
retains characteristics particular to any one of its prelimits. However, one might guess that Brownian last passage percolation bears a stronger connection with
\mathcal{L}
than other models since
\mathcal{L}
is known to have locally Brownian behaviour as we vary
x
and
y
. In this article we explore this connection. Quite surprisingly, certain marginals of the directed landscape can essentially be expressed as Brownian last passage problems!

For this theorem and throughout the paper we say that
B
is a
k
-dimensional Brownian motion of variance
\alpha
if
B=\sqrt{\alpha}B^{\prime}
, where
B^{\prime}
is a standard
k
-dimensional Brownian motion. We also write
X\ll Y
for two random variables
X,Y
if the law of
X
is absolutely continuous with respect to the law of
Y
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
s<t\in\mathbb{R},b>0
and
x_{1}<\dots<x_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\mathcal{L}
denote the directed landscape and let
B
be a collection of
k
independent Brownian motions of variance
2
. Consider the random continuous functions
f_{\mathcal{L}},f_{B}:\left\llbracket 1,k\right\rrbracket\times[-b,b]\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{L}}(i,y)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(x_{i},s;y,t)-\mathcal{L}(x_{i},s;-b,t)

\displaystyle f_{B}(i,y)

\displaystyle=B[(-b-1,i)\to(y,1)]-B[(-b-1,i)\to(-b,1)].

Then
f_{\mathcal{L}}\ll f_{B}
.

The recentering by
\mathcal{L}(x_{i},s;-b,t)
and
B[(-b-1,i)\to(-b,1)]
is necessary to deal with the fact that the last passage values
B[(-b-1,i)\to(y,1)]
are increasing in
i
, but the corresponding landscape values are not. Note that the
k=1
case of Theorem
1.1
is just local absolute continuity of the Airy
2
process with respect to Brownian motion; this was first shown in
[
10
]
.

Theorem
1.1
follows from a more refined structural theorem – Theorem
1.2
– that expresses values of the form
\mathcal{L}(x_{i},s;y,t),i\in\left\llbracket 1,k\right\rrbracket,y\in\mathbb{R}
as a last passage problem across
k
locally Brownian functions. To state that theorem and explain how Theorem
1.1
arises, we need to introduce a few more notions.

The fundamental building block in the directed landscape is the
Airy sheet

\mathcal{S}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
given by
\mathcal{S}(x,y):=\mathcal{L}(x,0;y,1)
. The directed landscape is built from independent Airy sheets in an analogous way to how Brownian motion is built from independent normal distributions. The Airy sheet was constructed by understanding how a continuous version of the Robinson-Schensted-Knuth (RSK) correspondence interacts with random inputs.

Let
\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
be the space of
n
-tuples of continuous functions
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n}),f_{i}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
with
f(0)=0
. The
continuous RSK correspondence
is a map
W:\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}\to\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
. It can be presented in purely geometric terms by looking at
multi-point last passage percolation
.
Consider an environment
f=(f_{i},i\in\mathbb{Z})

and vectors
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{k}),\mathbf{q}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{k})
, where
p_{i}=(x_{i},n_{i}),q_{i}=(y_{i},m_{i})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z}
and
x_{i}\leq x_{i+1},y_{i}\leq y_{i+1},n_{i}\geq n_{i+1},m_{i}\geq m_{i+1}
for all
i
. Let

f[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]=\sup_{\pi}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|\pi\|_{f}

(3)

where the supremum is over all
disjoint
k
-tuples
of paths
\pi=(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k})
, where each
\pi_{i}
goes from
(x_{i},n)
to
(y_{i},m)
, and
\pi_{i}(z)<\pi_{i+1}(z)
for
z\in(x_{i},y_{i})\cap(x_{i+1},y_{i+1})
. We call a
k
-tuple that achieves this supremum a
(disjoint) optimizer
.
For certain choices of
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
, this supremum is taken over the empty set, in which case we set
f[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]=-\infty
.
For
f\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
, define
Wf\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
by

\sum_{i=1}^{k}Wf_{i}(y)=f[(0,n)^{k}\to(y,1)^{k}]

(4)

for
k\in\left\llbracket 1,n\right\rrbracket,t\in[0,\infty)
. Here and throughout we write
p^{k}
for the vector consisting of
k
copies of a point
p
. Remarkably, the RSK map
W
is an
isometry
between the upper and lower boundaries of
f
. For
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{\leq}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:x_{1}\leq\dots\leq x_{k}\}
, we let
(\mathbf{x},m)
denote the
k
-tuple of points
(x_{1},m),\dots,(x_{k},m)
.
For any vectors
(\mathbf{x},n),(\mathbf{y},1)
with
0\leq x_{1}
, we have

f[(\mathbf{x},n)\to(\mathbf{y},1)]=Wf[(\mathbf{x},n)\to(\mathbf{y},1)].

(5)

The isometry (
5
) was shown in
[
12
, Proposition 4.1]
, though closely related formulas had been previously observed by Biane, Bougerol, and O’Connell
[
6
]
and Noumi and Yamada
[
25
]
.

The RSK correspondence behaves well with certain random inputs. In particular, if
B\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
is a sequence of independent Brownian motions, then
WB
is a sequence of nonintersecting Brownian motions, see
[
27
,
26
]
. In the KPZ scaling limit,
WB
converges to the
parabolic Airy line ensemble

\mathcal{W}
, constructed by Prähofer and Spohn
[
30
]
, and realized as a sequence of infinity many nonintersecting, locally Brownian functions by Corwin and Hammond
[
10
]
.

The Airy sheet was built in
[
12
]
by understanding how the isometry (
5
) passes to the KPZ limit for single points, see Definition
3.2
. In
[
14
]
, this analysis was extended to multiple points to construct an extended Airy sheet and an extended directed landscape. To describe the construction, we need to talk about metric notions for
\mathcal{L}
.

The value
\mathcal{L}(p;q)=\mathcal{L}(x,s;y,t)
is best thought of as a distance between two points
p
and
q
in the space-time plane.
Here
x,y
are spatial coordinates and
s,t
are time coordinates. We cannot move backwards or instantaneously in time, so
\mathcal{L}(x,s;y,t)
is not defined for
s\geq t
. Unlike with an ordinary metric,
\mathcal{L}
is not symmetric and may take negative values. As in last passage percolation, it also satisfies the triangle inequality backwards:

\mathcal{L}(p;r)\geq\mathcal{L}(p;q)+\mathcal{L}(q;r)\qquad\mbox{ for all }(p;r),(p;q),(q;r)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}.

(6)

Just as in true metric spaces, we can define path lengths in
\mathcal{L}
, see
[
12
, Section 12]
. In
\mathcal{L}
, a
path
from
(x,s)
to
(y,t)
is a continuous function
\pi:[s,t]\to\mathbb{R}
with
\pi(s)=x
and
\pi(t)=y
, with
length

\|\pi\|_{\mathcal{L}}=\inf_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\inf_{s=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{k}=t}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{L}(\pi(t_{i-1}),t_{i-1};\pi(t_{i}),t_{i})\,.

(7)

This is analogous to defining the length of a curve in Euclidean space by piecewise linear approximation.
A path
\pi
is a
geodesic
if
\|\pi\|_{\mathcal{L}}
is maximal among all paths with the same start and end points. Equivalently, a geodesic is any path
\pi
with
\|\pi\|_{\mathcal{L}}=\mathcal{L}(\pi(s),s;\pi(t),t)
. Almost surely, geodesics exist between every pair of points
(x,s),(y,t)
with
s<t
.

Now, for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{\leq},s<t
let

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},s;\mathbf{y},t)=\sup_{\pi}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|\pi_{i}\|_{\mathcal{L}},

(8)

where the supremum is over all
k
-tuples of paths
\pi_{i}:[s,t]\to\mathbb{R}
with
\pi_{i}(s)=x_{i},\pi_{i}(t)=y_{i}
and
\pi_{i}(r)<\pi_{i+1}(r)
for all
r\in(s,t)
. This extension of
\mathcal{L}
is called the
extended directed landscape
, abbreviated as extended landscape. The extended landscape is continuous on its domain, and satisfies a limiting RSK isometry. Define an infinite collection of functions
W\mathcal{L}_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},i\in\mathbb{N}
by

\sum_{i=1}^{k}W\mathcal{L}_{i}(y)=\mathcal{L}(0^{k},0;y^{k},1).

(9)

Then
W\mathcal{L}
is a parabolic Airy line ensemble and for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{\leq}
, we have

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},0;\mathbf{y},1)=W\mathcal{L}[\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{y}].

(10)

Of course, one needs to make sense of the right side of (
10
), see Theorem
3.3
for details.

1.2
Other isometries

In addition to
Wf
, for a given
f\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
there are other interesting environments
g\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
that satisfy the isometric property (
5
). Our main theorems in this paper come from exploring these other isometries in the limit
\mathcal{L}
.

One of the standard methods for constructing
Wf
(via iterated Pitman transforms) yields a set of environments
W_{\tau}f\in\mathcal{C}^{n}
satisfying (
5
) indexed by permutations
\tau\in S_{n}
.
The environments
W_{\tau}f
can also be described in terms of certain multi-point last passage values in analogy with (
4
). Moreover, if
B
is a collection of independent Brownian motions, then while
W_{\tau}B
does not have the tractable nonintersecting structure of
WB
,
its paths are still locally absolutely continuous with respect to Brownian motion, as is the case with
WB
. See Section
2
for more details.

This story has an analogue in the directed landscape. The environments we construct will no longer satisfy (
10
) for all possible
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
, but rather only for
\mathbf{x}
with entries in a particular finite set
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}
. On the other hand, these environments are in one sense significantly simpler than
W\mathcal{L}
: they will consist of only
k
lines. As in the finite setting, while these isometric environments no longer have the integrable or nonintersecting structure of
W\mathcal{L}
, they are still locally Brownian and their lines can be described via certain marginals of the extended directed landscape, similarly to (
9
).

To set up our main theorem, we need a notion of last passage from
-\infty
. Let
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{k}),f_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be an environment of continuous functions. For
I=\{i_{1}<\dots<i_{\ell}\}\subset\left\llbracket 1,k\right\rrbracket
and a
k
-tuple
\mathbf{p}
, define

f[(-\infty,I)\to\mathbf{p}]=\lim_{z\to-\infty}f[(z,I)\to\mathbf{p}]+\sum_{i\in I}f_{i}(z).

(11)

In (
11
) and throughout we write
(z,I)
for the
|I|
-tuple
(z,i_{1}),\dots,(z,i_{\ell})
. This type of last passage can equivalently be defined using a supremum over disjoint paths, see Remark
3.8
. Now let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{\leq}
with
x_{1}<\dots<x_{k}
. For a set
I\subset\left\llbracket 1,k\right\rrbracket
, we write
\mathbf{x}^{I}
for the vector in
\mathbb{R}^{|I|}
consisting only of coordinates
x_{i}
of
\mathbf{x}
with
i\in I
.
Define a sequence of
k
functions
W_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}=\{W_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},i\in\left\llbracket 1,k\right\rrbracket\}
by the formula

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}W_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}_{i}(y)=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}^{\left\llbracket 1,\ell\right\rrbracket},0;y^{\ell},1),\qquad\text{ for }\ell\in\left\llbracket 1,k\right\rrbracket,y\in\mathbb{R}.

(12)

Theorem 1.2
.

Almost surely, for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{\leq}
with
x_{1}<\dots<x_{k}
we have the following:

1.

(Asymptotics and stability) For any
i\in\left\llbracket 1,k\right\rrbracket
, we have

\lim_{z\to\pm\infty}\frac{W_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}_{i}(z)+z^{2}}{z}=2x_{i}.

(13)

In particular, this implies that for
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists a random
Z_{0}(y_{0})\in(-\infty,y_{0})\cap\mathbb{Z}
such that

W_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}[(-\infty,I)\to(\mathbf{y},1)]=W_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}[(z,I)\to(\mathbf{y},1)]+\sum_{i\in I}\mathcal{W}_{i}(z)

(14)

for all
I\subset\left\llbracket 1,k\right\rrbracket,z\leq Z_{0}
and
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{|I|}_{\leq}
with
y_{1}\geq y_{0}
.

2.

(Locally Brownian) Conditional on
W_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}(0)
the function
W_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}(\cdot)-W_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}(0)
is locally absolutely continuous with respect to a
k
-dimensional Brownian motion
B
of variance
2
. In other words, if we let
B,\mathcal{L}
be independent then on any interval
[a,b]
, we have
(W_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}(0),W_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}|_{[a,b]}-W_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}(0))\ll(W_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}(0),B|_{[a,b]})
.

3.

(Isometry) For any
I\subset\left\llbracket 1,k\right\rrbracket
and
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{|I|}_{\leq}
, we have

W_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}[(-\infty,I)\to(\mathbf{y},1)]=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}^{I},0;\mathbf{y},1).

Remark 1.3
.

1.

Theorem
1.2
allows us to represent complicated marginals of the directed landscape and the Airy sheet in terms of last passage in a finite environment of locally Brownian continuous functions. In particular, this allows us to show that almost sure properties of Brownian last passage percolation across
finitely many
lines hold in
\mathcal{L}
. We discuss a few consequences of this in Section
1.3
.

2.

One could set up a version of Theorem
1.2
for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{\leq}
without the strict ordering condition. This is a technical extension that we do not pursue here. We point out that the case where
x_{i}=0
for all
i
is implicit in Proposition 5.9 in
[
14
]
. In this special case,
W_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}
returns the top
k
lines of
W\mathcal{L}
.

3.

Even though we have fixed the times in Theorem
1.2
equal to
0
and
1
, the result also holds for arbitrary times
s<t
by invariance properties of
\mathcal{L}
.

4.

We believe the construction in Theorem
1.2
should be useful for studying Busemann functions for the extended landscape. Consider the limiting field

B_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{L}(t\mathbf{x},-t;0,\mathbf{y})-\mathcal{L}(t\mathbf{x},-t;0,0^{k}),

(15)

which should give the Busemann function in a direction
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{\leq}
between locations
(0,\mathbf{y})
and
(0,0^{k})
. Taking a limit of the construction in Theorem
1.2
should yield insight into the nature
B_{\mathcal{L}}
. Indeed, for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{\leq},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{\leq}
and
I\subset\left\llbracket 1,k\right\rrbracket
, we should have

B_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x}^{I},\mathbf{y})=W^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}[\mathbf{x}^{I}\to\mathbf{y}],\qquad\text{ where }\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}W^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}_{i}(y):=B_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x}^{\left\llbracket 1,\ell\right\rrbracket},y^{\ell}).

(16)

Moreover, each of the lines
W^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}_{i}
should be an independent, variance
2
Brownian motion with drift
2x_{i}
. We do not attempt to make this limiting picture rigorous, or to justify the existence of the limit in (
15
).

Busemann functions have been studied in detail in last passage models, yielding remarkable insights about the geometry of infinite geodesics and other last passage structures, e.g. see
[
8
,
18
,
21
,
34
]
.
At the level of single points, a Busemann function structure closely related to (
16
) was shown for exponential last passage percolation by Fan and Seppäläinen
[
15
]
, building on work of Ferrari and Martin
[
16
]
studying stationary measures in multi-type tasep. In
[
15
]
, the analogue of the first equality in (
16
) is distributional. The lines corresponding to
W^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}_{i}
are constructed via certain ‘multiclass processes’ which, a priori, are unrelated to multi-point last passage values.

1.3
Consequences

In addition to Theorem
1.1
, we present three other fairly straightforward consequences of Theorem
1.2
.
First, fix
x_{1}<x_{2}
and define the
Airy difference profile

A^{x_{1},x_{2}}(y)=\mathcal{L}(x_{2},0;y,1)-\mathcal{L}(x_{1},0;y,1).

The function
A^{x_{1},x_{2}}
is a continuous increasing function, and hence is the CDF of a random measure
\mu_{x_{1},x_{2}}
. Moreover, basic symmetries of
\mathcal{L}
imply that the process
A^{x_{1},x_{2}}-\mathbb{E}A^{x_{1},x_{2}}
is stationary and

\mathbb{E}A^{x_{1},x_{2}}(y)=-x_{2}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}+2y(x_{2}-x_{1}),

so on average,
A^{x_{1},x_{2}}
increases linearly. However, this is not the case for individual realizations of
A^{x_{1},x_{2}}
; in fact,
\mu_{x_{1},x_{2}}
is supported on a lower dimensional set.

In
[
4
]
, Basu, Ganguly and Hammond showed that the support of
\mu_{x_{1},x_{2}}
a.s. has Hausdorff dimension
1/2
, and in
[
5
]
, Bates, Ganguly and Hammond proved that this support coincides with a set of exceptional events for geodesics in
\mathcal{L}
. Moving beyond a Hausdorff dimension estimate, Ganguly and Hegde
[
17
]
showed that
A^{x_{1},x_{2}}
can be represented as a ‘patchwork quilt’ of objects that are locally absolutely continuous with respect to the running maximum of a Brownian motion. The techniques used in
[
17
]
have similarities with our methods, i.e. they also use properties of iterated Pitman transforms to study
A^{x_{1},x_{2}}
.

As a consequence of Theorem
1.2
in the special case when
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})
, we recover all of these previous results and more. In particular, we show that
A^{x_{1},x_{2}}
is locally absolutely continuous with respect to the running maximum of a Brownian motion without any need for a patchwork quilt.

Theorem 1.4
.

Fix
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})
with
x_{1}<x_{2}
.

1.

For all
y\in\mathbb{R}
,

A^{x_{1},x_{2}}(y)=\sup_{z\leq y}W_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}_{2}(z)-W_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}_{1}(z)=\sup_{z\leq y}\mathcal{L}((x_{1},x_{2}),0;z^{2},1)-2\mathcal{L}(x_{1},0;z,1).

2.

For any compact set
[a,b]
, the law of
A^{x_{1},x_{2}}|_{[a,b]}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the law of the running maximum
M
of a Brownian motion
B
on
[a-1,b]
of variance
4
, i.e.
M(x)=\sup_{a-1\leq y\leq x}B(y)
.

3.

The support of
\mu_{x_{1},x_{2}}
is the set of
y\in\mathbb{R}
where

\mathcal{L}((x_{1},x_{2}),0;y^{2},1)=\mathcal{L}(x_{1},0;y,1)+\mathcal{L}(x_{2},0;y,1).

Equivalently, this is the of set of
y\in\mathbb{R}
where there exist geodesics
\pi_{1}
from
(x_{1},0)\to(y,1)
and
\pi_{2}
from
(x_{2},0)\to(y,1)
such that
\pi_{1}(r)<\pi_{2}(r)
for all
r\in[0,1)
.

The disjoint geodesic characterization of
\text{supp}(\mu_{x_{1},x_{2}})
in Theorem
1.4
.
3
was shown in
[
4
]
. We have included it above to highlight how it can alternately be obtained as an immediate consequence of our main theorem and one of the main results of
[
14
]
.

As a consequence of Theorem
1.4
, we can show that
\mathcal{L}
can be reconstructed from only the
shapes
of its geodesics, without any information about their lengths.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\mathbb{Q}^{4}_{\uparrow}=\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{4}

Our final consequence concerns the KPZ fixed point, constructed by Matetski, Quastel, and Remenik
[
22
]
. The KPZ fixed point is a Markov process
\mathfrak{h}_{t},t\in[0,\infty)
taking values in the space of upper semicontinuous functions
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
with a growth bound at
\pm\infty
. It is the scaling limit of random growth models in the KPZ universality class. By results of
[
24
,
13
]
, it is related to the directed landscape
\mathcal{L}
by the following formula. Letting
h_{0}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
denote the initial condition of the KPZ fixed point, we can write

\mathfrak{h}_{t}(y)=\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}}h_{0}(x)+\mathcal{L}(x,0;y,t).

(17)

In
[
20
]
, Hammond (see also
[
19
,
7
]
) demonstrated how for fixed
t
,
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
can be decomposed as a patchwork quilt made of Brownian pieces. More precisely, he showed that under mild assumptions on the initial condition,
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
has the following description:

•

There exists an ordered (finite or countable) random sequence
\dots A_{-1}<A_{0}<A_{1}<\dots
which is finite when restricted to any compact set, and an (unordered) random sequence
p_{i},i\in\mathbb{Z}
, such that

\mathfrak{h}_{t}(x)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbf{1}(x\in[A_{i},A_{i+1}))[Y_{i}(x)+p_{i}].

(18)

•

Each of the functions
Y_{i},i\in\mathbb{Z}
is locally absolutely continuous with respect to a Brownian motion of variance
2
.

In this description, each patch
Y_{i}(x)+p_{i}=\mathcal{L}(y_{i},0;x,t)+h_{0}(y_{i})
for some strictly increasing sequence
y_{i}
.

More recently, Sarkar and Virág
[
33
]
showed that in fact the KPZ fixed point started from any initial condition is locally absolutely continuous with respect to Brownian motion.
From this point of view, the cusp points
A_{i}
are not seen. However, we still expect interesting behaviour at these points. Using Theorem
1.2
, we can identify what is actually happening at these cusp points. For simplicity, we restrict ourselves to the easiest nontrivial case when the initial condition
h_{0}
is combination of two narrow wedge initial conditions.
In this case there is exactly one cusp, and the KPZ fixed point has the following basic structure.

Fact 1.6
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
denote the KPZ fixed point at time
t
started from an initial condition
h_{0}
which is equal to
-\infty
except at two points
p_{1}<p_{2}
where
h_{0}(p_{1})=a_{1},h_{0}(p_{2})=a_{2}
for some
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then there exists
A\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathfrak{h}_{t}(y)=[\mathcal{L}(p_{1},0;y,1)+a_{1}]\mathbf{1}(y<A)+[\mathcal{L}(p_{2},0;x,1)+a_{2}]\mathbf{1}(y\geq A).

We are concerned with understanding the joint law of
(A,\mathfrak{h}_{t})
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{R}
be any random variable with a positive Lebesgue density everywhere, let
B:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a two-sided standard Brownian motion, let
R:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a two-sided Bessel-
3
process. That is,
R=\|X\|_{2}
, where
X
is a
3
-dimensional standard Brownian motion. Suppose that all
3
objects are independent. Then with
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
as in the setting of Fact
1.6
, for any
t>0
and any compact interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, we have
(A,\mathfrak{h}_{t}(\cdot)|_{I}-\mathfrak{h}_{t}(A))\ll(X,[B+R](-X+\cdot)|_{I})
.

Theorem
1.7
shows that conditional on its cusp location
A
,
\mathfrak{h}_{t}(A+\cdot)-\mathfrak{h}_{t}(A)
is locally absolutely continuous with respect to a two-sided Brownian-Bessel process. If we start from a more general initial condition, then there will be many cusp points as in (
18
). If we condition on all of these cusp points, then similar ideas could be used to show that the process
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
is locally absolutely continuous with respect to a Brownian motion started at the cusp closest to
0
, plus a string of independent Bessel-
3
bridges running between all cusp points. For brevity, we do not pursue this technical extension here.

Properties of last passage percolation

2.1
Basics

Recall the definition of multi-point last passage from (
3
).
Multi-point last passage percolation satisfies the following useful
metric composition law
. Its proof is immediate from the definitions.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
f=(f_{i},i\in\mathbb{Z})
be a sequence of continuous functions and let
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. For any endpoints
\mathbf{p}\in(\mathbb{R}\times\{\dots,j+2,j+1\})^{k},\mathbf{q}\in(\mathbb{R}\times\{j,j-1,\dots\})^{k}
we have that

f[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]=\max_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{\leq}}f[\mathbf{p}\to(\mathbf{z},j+1)]+f[(\mathbf{z},j)\to\mathbf{q}].

Similarly, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
and endpoints
\mathbf{p}\in((-\infty,x]\times\mathbb{Z})^{k},\mathbf{q}\in([x,\infty)\times\mathbb{Z})^{k}
we have that

f[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]=\max_{I\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{\geq}}f[\mathbf{p}\to(x,I)]+f[(x,I)\to\mathbf{q}].

Here and throughout
\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{\geq}=\{I\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}:I_{1}\geq I_{2}\dots\geq I_{k}\}
.

We will also use the following facts about optimizers. First, suppose that
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
are paths from
(x,n)
to
(y,m)
and
(x^{\prime},m^{\prime})
to
(y^{\prime},m^{\prime})
, respectively. We write
\pi\leq\pi^{\prime}
and say that
\pi^{\prime}
is
to the right
of
\pi
if:

•

x\leq x^{\prime},y\leq y^{\prime},n\leq n^{\prime},m\leq m^{\prime}
.

•

For
z\in[x,y]\cap[x^{\prime},y^{\prime}]
, we have
\pi(z)\leq\pi^{\prime}(z)
.

For
k
-tuples of paths
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
, we write
\pi\leq\pi^{\prime}
if
\pi_{i}\leq\pi^{\prime}_{i}
for all
i\in\left\llbracket 1,k\right\rrbracket
.
Then we have the following, from
[
14
]
. The first part is Lemma 2.2 from that paper, and part (ii) is a slight variant of Lemma 2.3, whose proof goes through verbatim.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
f=(f_{i},i\in\mathbb{Z})
, and let
(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})
be a pair of
k
-tuples such that there is at least one disjoint
k
-tuple (of paths) from
\mathbf{p}
to
\mathbf{q}
.

(i)

There always exists an optimizer
\pi
in
f
from
\mathbf{p}
to
\mathbf{q}
such that for any optimizer
\tau
from
\mathbf{p}
to
\mathbf{q}
, we have
\tau\leq\pi
. We call
\pi
the
rightmost optimizer
from
\mathbf{p}
to
\mathbf{q}
.

(ii)

Let
(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
be a
k^{\prime}
-tuple of endpoints such that there is at least one disjoint
k^{\prime}
-tuple from
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
to
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}
, and let
s\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Let
(x_{i},n_{i}),(x_{i}^{\prime},n_{i}^{\prime}),(y_{i},m_{i}),(y_{i}^{\prime},m_{i}^{\prime})
denote the coordinates of
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{p}^{\prime},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}
, and define
x_{i},y_{i},m_{i},n_{i}=\infty
for integers
i>k
and
x_{i},y_{i},m_{i},n_{i}=-\infty
for integers
i\leq 0
. Similarly define
x_{i}^{\prime},y_{i}^{\prime},m_{i}^{\prime},n_{i}^{\prime}
for integers
i^{\prime}\notin\left\llbracket 1,k\right\rrbracket
.
With these definitions, suppose that
x_{i}\leq x_{i+s}^{\prime},y_{i}\leq y_{i+s}^{\prime},n_{i}\leq n_{i+s}^{\prime},m_{i}\leq m_{i+s}^{\prime}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Then if
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
are the rightmost optimizers from
\mathbf{p}
to
\mathbf{q}
and
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
to
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}
, we have
\pi_{i}\leq\pi_{i+s}^{\prime}
for all
i\in\left\llbracket 1,k\right\rrbracket\cap\left\llbracket 1-s,k-s\right\rrbracket
.

Part (ii) above is quite general. The concrete case when
s=0
and
k=k^{\prime}
is easier to understand. In this case, it amounts to a simple monotonicity of rightmost optimizers as we shift endpoints left and right, or up and down.

2.2
Pitman transforms

Recall from the introduction that
\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
is the space of
n
-tuples of continuous functions
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n}),f_{i}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
with
f(0)=0
. A fruitful way of studying last passage percolation across environments in
\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
is by sorting the environments two lines at a time using a series of two-line Pitman transforms, an approach introduced in
[
6
]
. The Pitman transform is simply the map
W:\mathcal{C}^{2}_{0}\to\mathcal{C}^{2}_{0}
from (
4
) for
2
lines. The following proposition records important properties of this map.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
W:\mathcal{C}^{2}_{0}\to\mathcal{C}^{2}_{0}
and
f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{0}
. We have

(i)

Wf_{2}\leq f_{2}\leq Wf_{1}
and
Wf_{2}\leq f_{1}\leq Wf_{1}
.

(ii)

For any endpoints
(\mathbf{x},2),(\mathbf{y},1)
with
0\leq x_{1}
, we have the
isometry

Wf[(\mathbf{x},2)\to(\mathbf{y},1)]=f[(\mathbf{x},2)\to(\mathbf{y},1)].

Items (i) is immediate from the definition, and (ii) is
[
12
, Lemma 4.3]
.
We can apply Pitman transforms two lines at a time to understand last passage percolation in
\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, an adjacent transposition
\sigma_{i}:=(i,i+1)\in S_{n}
and
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\left\llbracket 1,n\right\rrbracket}
, define

W_{\sigma_{i}}f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{i-1},W(f_{i},f_{i+1}),f_{i+2},\dots,f_{n}).

(19)

For more general permutations
\tau\in S_{n}
, we define

W_{\tau}=W_{\sigma_{i_{1}}}\cdots W_{\sigma_{i_{k}}}

(20)

where
\sigma_{i_{1}}\cdots\sigma_{i_{k}}=\tau
is a
reduced decomposition
of
\tau
, i.e. a minimal length decomposition of
\tau
as a product of adjacent transpositions. The right side of (
20
) is the same for any reduced decomposition, see the discussion preceding Proposition
2.8
in
[
6
]
. The
n
-line map
W
in (
4
) is equal to
W_{\text{rev}_{n}}
, where
\text{rev}_{n}=n\cdots 1
is the reverse permutation, see
[
6
]
or Section 3 in
[
11
]
.

In
[
12
]
, the isometry in Proposition
2.3
(ii) was extended to yield an isometry for
W=W_{\text{rev}_{n}}
by using the metric composition law, Proposition
2.1
. We will also extend Proposition
2.3
(ii) to get an isometric property for general permutations; the proof is essentially identical to the proof of
[
12
, Proposition 4.1]
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\tau\in S_{n}
be a permutation and suppose that for some interval
\left\llbracket a,b\right\rrbracket\subset\left\llbracket 1,n\right\rrbracket
, that
\tau
is the identity on
\left\llbracket a,b\right\rrbracket^{c}
. Then for any endpoints
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}
with
p_{i}\in\left\llbracket n,b\right\rrbracket\times[0,\infty)
and
q_{i}\in\left\llbracket a,1\right\rrbracket\times[0,\infty)
, we have

W_{\tau}f[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]=f[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}].

Proof.

By (
20
), it suffices to prove the proposition when
\tau
is an adjacent transposition
\sigma_{j}
and
\left\llbracket a,b\right\rrbracket=\{j,j+1\}
. First assume
j\neq 1,n-1
. For any endpoints
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}
of size
k
with
p_{i}\in\left\llbracket n,j+1\right\rrbracket\times[0,\infty)
and
q_{i}\in\left\llbracket j,1\right\rrbracket\times[0,\infty)
for all
i
, Proposition
2.1
ensures that

f[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]=\max_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{\leq}}f[\mathbf{p}\to(\mathbf{z},j+2)]+f[(\mathbf{z},j+1)\to(\mathbf{z}^{\prime},j)]+f[(\mathbf{z}^{\prime},j-1)\to\mathbf{q}].

(21)

Under the maximum in (
21
), the first and third terms are unchanged when we apply
W_{\sigma_{j}}
since
W_{\sigma_{j}}f_{k}=f_{k}
for
k\neq j+1,j
. The middle term is unchanged by Proposition
2.3
(ii). Hence the left side of (
21
) is unchanged when we apply
W_{\sigma_{j}}
. The cases when
j=1,n-1
are similar, except there will be fewer terms on the right side of (
21
).
∎

Next, for
j\leq i
define the permutation

\tau_{i,j}=\sigma_{j}\cdots\sigma_{i-1}.

We use the convention that
\tau_{i,i}=\text{id}_{n}
. The maps
W_{\tau_{i,j}}
are related to last passage by the following lemma, Lemma 3.10 from
[
11
]
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
j\leq i\in\left\llbracket 1,n\right\rrbracket
and
f\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
. For all
y\geq 0
, we have

f[(0,i)\to(y,j)]=W_{\tau_{i,j}}f_{j}(y).

(22)

The next proposition builds on Lemma
2.5
. Recall that for a finite set
J=\{j_{1}<\dots<j_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{Z}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we use the shorthand

(x,J)=((x,j_{1}),\dots,(x,j_{k})).

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
I=\{i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\}\subset\left\llbracket 1,n\right\rrbracket,f\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0},
set
\tau_{I}=\tau_{i_{k},k}\cdots\tau_{i_{1},1}
, and define
m:I\to\left\llbracket 1,n\right\rrbracket
by
m(i_{j})=j
for all
j
. For any nonempty subset
J\subset I
and any vector
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{|J|}_{\leq}
with
0\leq y_{1}
, we have

W_{\tau_{I}}f[(0,m(J))\to(\mathbf{y},1)]=f[(0,J)\to(\mathbf{y},1)].

(23)

We can also explicitly describe of the functions
W_{\tau_{I}}f_{1},\dots,W_{\tau_{I}}f_{k}
as follows:

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}W_{\tau_{I}}f_{i}(y)=f[(0,I^{\ell})\to(y^{\ell},1)],

for all
0\leq y,\ell\in\left\llbracket 1,k\right\rrbracket
, where
I^{\ell}=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell}\}
.

Proof.

For each
\ell\in\left\llbracket 0,k\right\rrbracket
, define a map
m_{\ell}:I\to\left\llbracket 1,n\right\rrbracket
by setting
m_{\ell}(i_{j})=j
for
j\leq\ell
, and
m_{\ell}(i_{j})=i_{j}
otherwise.
Let
\tau_{\ell}=\tau_{i_{\ell},\ell}\cdots\tau_{i_{1},1}
. We will inductively prove the stronger claim that for every
\ell\in\left\llbracket 0,k\right\rrbracket
, for any
J,\mathbf{y}
as in the proposition, we have

W_{\tau_{\ell}}f[(0,m_{\ell}(J))\to(\mathbf{y},1)]=f[(0,J)\to(\mathbf{y},1)].

(24)

The base case when
\ell=0
is trivially true. Now suppose that the claim holds at
\ell-1
. It is enough to show that for any
g\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
and
J,\mathbf{y}
as in the proposition, we have

W_{\tau_{i_{\ell},\ell}}g[(0,m_{\ell}(J))\to(\mathbf{y},1)]=g[(0,m_{\ell-1}(J))\to(\mathbf{y},1)].

(25)

Indeed, (
25
) implies (
24
) by taking
g=W_{\tau_{\ell-1}}f
and applying the inductive hypothesis. First, by Proposition
2.4
, (
25
) holds with
m_{\ell}(J)
replaced by
m_{\ell-1}(J)
. This immediately implies (
25
) if
i_{\ell}\notin J
, so from now on we may assume
i_{\ell}\in J
. Now,

W_{\tau_{i_{\ell},\ell}}g[(0,m_{\ell}(J))\to(\mathbf{y},1)]\leq W_{\tau_{i_{\ell},\ell}}g[(0,m_{\ell-1}(J))\to(\mathbf{y},1)],

(26)

since all disjoint
k
-tuples from
(0,m_{\ell}(J))
to
(\mathbf{y},1)
are also disjoint
k
-tuples from
(0,m_{\ell-1}(J))
because
m_{\ell}\leq m_{\ell-1}
. For the opposite inequality, consider any disjoint
k
-tuple
\pi
from
(0,m_{\ell-1}(J))
to
(\mathbf{y},1)
. Let
\pi_{\ell}
be the path starting at
(0,i_{\ell})
, and let
y=\sup\{t\geq 0:\pi(t)\geq\ell\}
, where we take
y=0
if this set is empty. Then

\|\pi_{\ell}|_{[0,y]}\|_{W_{\tau_{i_{\ell},\ell}}g}\leq W_{\tau_{i_{\ell},\ell}}g[(i_{\ell},0)\to(y,\ell)]=g[(i_{\ell},0)\to(y,\ell)]=W_{\tau_{i_{\ell},\ell}}g_{\ell}(y).

The first equality follows from the isometry in Proposition
2.4
, and the second equality follows from Lemma
2.5
. Therefore if we define a new path
\rho=\ell\wedge\pi_{\ell}
, we have

\|\pi_{\ell}\|_{W_{\tau_{i_{\ell},\ell}}g}\leq\|\rho\|_{W_{\tau_{i_{\ell},\ell}}g}.

Moreover, replacing the path
\pi_{\ell}
with
\rho
in the
k
-tuple
\pi
yields a new disjoint
k
-tuple
\pi_{*}
from
(0,m_{\ell}(J))
to
(\mathbf{y},1)
since all paths starting above
\pi_{\ell}
started at lines in
\left\llbracket 1,\ell-1\right\rrbracket
. This gives the opposite inequality in (
26
), yielding (
25
).
∎

Remark 2.7
.

Moving forward, we will also want to apply Pitman transforms to more general environments

, opened up at times other than

f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n}),f_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

W_{a,\tau}f(x)=W_{\tau}T_{a}f(x-a)

so that

for all

2.3
Brownian motion and the Pitman transform

When
B\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{0}
is a Brownian motion, Pitman’s
2M-X
theorem
[
29
]
identifies the law of
WB
.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
B=(B_{1},B_{2})\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{0}
be two independent standard Brownian motions. Then
(WB_{1}+WB_{2},WB_{1}-WB_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(\sqrt{2}R,\sqrt{2}B)
, where
R
is a Bessel-
3
process,
B
is a standard Brownian motion, and the two objects are independent.

Theorem
2.8
implies that for any interval
[a,b]\subset(0,\infty)
, we have
WB|_{[a,b]}\ll B|_{[a,b]}
. We will need a version of this result for iterated Pitman transforms
W_{\sigma}
. This will require the following strengthening of this absolute continuity observation.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
B\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
be a sequence of
n
independent Brownian motions. For any
\tau\in S_{n}
and any
[a,b]\subset(0,\infty)
, we have
W_{\tau}B|_{[a,b]}\ll B|_{[a,b]}
.

We use the following lemma to help with the inductive step.

Lemma 2.10

(Lemma 4.5,
[
17
]
)
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{0}
be a random function such that for every
0<\epsilon<T
, we have
X|_{[\epsilon,T]}\ll B|_{[\epsilon,T]}
where
B\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{0}
is a standard Brownian motion. Suppose also that a.s.,

\max_{x\in[0,\epsilon]}X_{2}(x)-X_{1}(x)>0

(28)

for all
\epsilon>0
. Then for every
0<\epsilon<T
, we have
WX|_{[\epsilon,T]}\ll B|_{[\epsilon,T]}
.

Note that in
[
17
]
, Ganguly and Hegde prove a version of Proposition
2.9
for particular permutations
\sigma
. Just as in our case, their key input is Lemma
2.10
.

Proof.

We prove the proposition by induction on
n
. The
n=2
base case follows from Theorem
2.8
, as discussed above. Now suppose the lemma holds at
n-1
, and consider
\tau\in S_{n}
. Let
m=\tau(n)
. Using the notation
\tau_{i,j}
introduced prior to Proposition
2.6
, we can write
\tau=\tau_{n,m}\rho
for some
\rho\in S_{n}
with
\rho(n)=n
. By the inductive hypothesis and the fact that
(W_{\rho}B)_{n}=B_{n}
, the proposition holds if
m=n
. We now induct backwards on
m\in\left\llbracket 1,n\right\rrbracket
. Let
m\leq n-1
and suppose the proposition holds for
m+1
. By Lemma
2.10
, the proposition also holds if the set of
x
where

W_{\tau_{n,m+1}\rho}B_{m+1}(x)-W_{\tau_{n,m+1}\rho}B_{m}(x)>0

(29)

has a limit point at
0
almost surely. Now,
W_{\tau_{n,m+1}\rho}B_{m}=W_{\rho}B_{m}
, and

W_{\rho}B_{m}(x)\leq W_{\rho}B[(0,n-1)\to(x,1)]=B[(0,n-1)\to(x,1)],

where the equality follows from Proposition
2.4
. Moreover, repeated applications of Proposition
2.3
(i) imply that
W_{\tau_{n,m+1}\rho}B_{m+1}(x)\geq W_{\rho}B_{n}(x)=B_{n}(x)
. Therefore to show (
29
), we just need to show that the set of
x
where

B_{n}(x)-B[(0,n-1)\to(x,1)]>0

(30)

has a limit point at
0
almost surely. Let
I_{m}
denote the indicator of the event in (
30

For Theorem

, we first need an analogue of Proposition
2.4
for side-to-side, rather than bottom-to-top, last passage values. We will need a notion of discrete last passage percolation. Consider an
m\times n
array
G=G_{i,j},i\in\left\llbracket 1,m\right\rrbracket,j\in\left\llbracket 1,n\right\rrbracket
. For

and vectors
I,J\in\left\llbracket 1,n\right\rrbracket^{k}_{\geq}
define the

G[(1,I)\to(m,J)]=\sup_{\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{v\in\pi}G_{v}.

Here the supremum is over all

, where

, ends at
