𝒫 \mathcal{P} -canonical forms and complete inverses
M. MOUÇOUF

Abstract
. This paper describes a new kind of inverse for elements in associative ring, that is the complete inverse, as the unique solution of a certain set of equations. This inverse exists for an element a a if and only if the Drazin inverse of a a exists. We also show that by plugging in − k -k for k k in the 𝒫 \mathcal{P} -canonical form of a square matrix A A , we get the 𝒫 \mathcal{P} -canonical form of the complete inverse of A A .

Introduction

Let
R
be an associative ring. A generalized inverse of an element
a
of
R
is an element that has some useful inverse properties and it is defined by the equation
aga=a
where if
a
is invertible we have
g=a^{-1}
. It is shown that the most natural generalized inverse is the Drazin inverse. Recall that the algebraic definition of the Drazin inverse
a_{d}
of an element

of
R
, for some positive integer
p
, is
[
1
]

a_{d}aa_{d}=a_{d},\,\,aa_{d}=a_{d}a,\,\,a^{p}a_{d}a=a^{p}

In this paper we consider a new generalization of the notion of
“inverse”
, the idea is to start from the relationship
aga-gag=a-g
which is consistent with the formula
aa^{-1}a-a^{-1}aa^{-1}=a-a^{-1}
. More precisely, we define the complete inverse
a_{c}
of

to be the unique element, if it exists, satisfying the equations

aa_{c}a-a_{c}aa_{c}=a-a_{c},\,\,aa_{c}=a_{c}a,\,\,a^{p}a_{c}a=a^{p},

for some positive integer
p
. Then we give some properties of this inverse.

Next we prove that, in the case of a matrix
A
over a field
F
by plugging in
-k
for
k
in the
\mathcal{P}
-canonical form of
A
we get the
\mathcal{P}
-canonical form of the matrix
A_{c}
.

\begin{array}[]{rlll}a^{n}xa&=&a^{n},&(1^{n})\\

of

[

]

Lemma 2.1
.

Let

-inverse of

is the
\{1^{n},3,5\}
-inverse of

.

2.

.

Proof.

1. This result is well known and easy to see

1
]
.

2. It is easy to see that

for all positive integer

. Hence

\displaystyle v^{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{m}(ax)^{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{m+1}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{m}.

∎

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
a
be an element of an associative ring
R
and
n
be a given positive integer. Suppose that
x
is the
\{1^{n},3,5\}
-inverse of

. Then

is the unique
\{1^{n},4,5\}
-inverse of

.

Proof.

It is clear that
z
commutes with
a
. Further, we have

\displaystyle a^{n+1}z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{n+2}+a^{n+1}x-a^{n+1}v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{n+2}+a^{n}-v^{n+2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{n},

and hence
z
is a
\{1^{n}\}
-inverse of
a
.

We also have

aza=a^{3}+a^{2}x-a^{2}v

and

zaz=a^{3}+ax^{2}+av^{2}+2a^{2}x-2a^{2}v-2axv.

Then

\displaystyle zaz-aza

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ax^{2}+av^{2}+a^{2}x-a^{2}v-2axv

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x+av^{2}+v-av^{2}-v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x+a^{2}x-2axv

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x-v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z-a.

For the uniqueness, let
u
be a
\{1^{n},4,5\}
-inverse of
a
. Observe first that
u
and
z
commute since
x
commutes with every element of
R
which commutes with
a
(see Theorem
1
of
[
1
]
). On the other hand, we have

a^{n+1}u=a^{n}\quad\text{and}\quad a^{n+1}z=a^{n}

Then

(2.1)

\displaystyle a^{n+1}(u-z)=0.

If we substract the equations

az^{2}-a^{2}z=z-a

and

au^{2}-a^{2}u=u-a,

we obtain

-(u-z)=a^{2}(u-z)-a(u-z)(u+z).

Hence

-a^{n}(u-z)=a^{n+2}(u-z)-a^{n+1}(u-z)(u+z).

Thus

a^{n}(u-z)=0,

and we have peeled off one factor of
a
from
2.1
to get
a^{n}(u-z)=0
. Clearly this process can be repeated as often as desired to obtain
u=z
.
∎
