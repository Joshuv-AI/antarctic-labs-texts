Abstract
. We consider two different versions of the double dimer model on a planar domain, where we either fold a single dimer cover on a symmetric domain onto itself across the line of symmetry, or we superimpose two independent dimer covers on two, almost identical, domains that differ only on a certain portion of the boundary. This results in a collection of loops and doubled edges that, unlike in the classical double dimer case of Kenyon, are accompanied by arcs emanating from the line of symmetry or the chosen portion of the boundary. We argue that these arcs together with the associated height function satisfy a discrete version of the coupling of Qian and Werner between the Arc loop ensemble (ALE) and two different variants of the Gaussian free field (with Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions). We also show that certain statistics of the arcs (when the loops are disregarded from the picture) converge to conformally invariant quantities in the small-mesh scaling limit, and moreover the limits are the same for the two versions of the model, and equal to the corresponding statistics of the arc loop ensemble (ALE). This gives evidence to the conjecture of [ 7 ] (that concerns one of these models).



1.
Introduction

Motivation

We consider two different but closely related versions of the double dimer model defined on planar graphs, that give rise not only to a collection of loops and doubled edges as in the original work of Kenyon
[
16
]
but also to a collection of arcs emanating from and ending at a chosen piece of the boundary (see Figure
1
). These arcs are a new feature of the model that does not appear in the original double dimers,
and our main goal is to study their statistics (when the loops are disregarded from the picture). One of these two constructions was described in
[
7
]
, where a conjecture about the scaling limit of the arcs was stated
claiming that the full collection of arcs should converge to the so-called
arc loop ensemble (ALE)
introduced by Aru, Sepúlveda and Werner in
[
4
]
.
Here we develop the methods of
[
16
]
to fit this setup and use them to give (partial) evidence for this conjecture. To be precise, we compute the scaling limits of certain observables of the arcs themselves, and they turn out to be conformally invariant and agree with the analogous quantities defined directly for the ALE.

Our motivation (outside of the dimer model) comes from the theory of the continuum
Gaussian free field (GFF)
and its level sets.
The GFF with
Dirichlet
boundary conditions in a domain
D
is a Gaussian field whose covariance kernel is given by the Green function
g(x,y)
of Brownian motion in the domain with Dirichlet boundary condition. In this article we assume that the Green function is normalised so that
g(x,y)\sim(-2\pi)^{-1}\log|x-y|
as
x\to y
in the interior of the domain.
We are guided by the construction of Qian and Werner
[
18
]
who coupled two different versions of the
GFF, with Dirichlet and
Neumann (free)
boundary conditions (we refer the reader to
[
18
]
for a definition of the latter), through a common set of level lines that form an ALE.

Even though the GFF is not a function, but only a generalised function (a Schwartz distribution acting on test functions), a beautiful theory of its level sets has been developed in recent years
[
4
,
3
,
2
]
.
An important notion in this geometric representation of the GFF is that of the two-valued sets
\mathbb{A}_{-a,b}
, where
a,b>0
,
a+b\geq 2\lambda
, and where
\lambda=\sqrt{{\pi}/8}

is the constant appearing in the height-gap phenomenon of Schramm and Sheffield
[
20
]
.
Formally they are thin local sets in the sense of
[
4
]
whose associated harmonic function takes only two values
-a
and
b
. Heuristically they are random sets naturally coupled with the GFF
that could be seen as sets of those points in
D
that are connected to
\partial D
by a path on which the values of the field (which is not defined pointwise)
lie between
-a
and
b
. The ALE is then the set of arcs given by the (restriction to
D
of the) connected components of
A_{-\lambda,\lambda}
. In
[
18
]
a surprising coupling between the ALE and two different types of the GFF was given. In this construction, the Dirichlet GFF (with zero boundary conditions) changes its value by
\pm 2\lambda
across the arcs of the ALE in a deterministic and alternating fashion, whereas the Neumann (free boundary condition) GFF changes its value by
\pm 2\lambda
in an i.i.d. fashion.

Discrete models and couplings

In this article we consider a set of discrete arcs in two different but related versions of double dimers that satisfy an analogous coupling when considered together with the associated height function (that is known to converge to the Gaussian free field in a variety of settings
[
15
,
19
,
6
,
13
]
).
To be precise, recall that the
dimer model
is a random perfect matching (which in the setting of this work is chosen uniformly at random) of a finite graph. We will assume that the graph is a finite connected subgraph of the rescaled square lattice

\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{2}
that is symmetric across the real line, and whose restriction to the upper half-plane approximates a simply connected continuum domain
U
. We will denote such graphs by
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}^{r}
.

In the first setup, referred to as
folded dimers
, that corresponds to the Neumann GFF in the coupling of
[
18
]
, we consider the dimer model on
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}^{r}
, and fold the resulting perfect matching along the real line as in Fig.
1
. This results in a collection of loops, doubled edges, and arcs starting
and ending on the real line. The associated height function on the faces of the folded graph can be defined (up to a global additive constant) by assigning orientations to each
dimer in the folded dimer cover: each dimer coming from the top half of
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}^{r}
is oriented from a black to a white vertex (in a fixed bipartite colouring of the lattice), and each dimer coming from the bottom half is oriented from
a white vertex to a black vertex. This results in a consistent orientation of each loop and arc, and finally one declares that the increment of the height function from a face
u
to a neighbouring face is
+1
(resp.
-1
) if there is
a loop or an arc separating the two faces such that
u
lies on the left-hand (resp. right-hand) side of the oriented dimer in the loop or arc.
Note each loop and arc can be oriented in the two possible ways with equal probability
and independently of other loops and arcs. This corresponds to the increment of the height function being
\pm 1
, and is analogous to the coupling of Qian and Werner between the ALE and the GFF with mixed Neuman/Dirichlet boundary conditions.

One can see that the height function in this setting does indeed converge to the continuum GFF with mixed Neuman/Dirichlet boundary conditions. Indeed, it is known from the original work of Kenyon
[
15
]
(and more recent generalisations
[
19
,
6
]
) that if the dimer model on
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}^{r}
has
Temperleyan
boundary conditions (which we assume to be the case in our arguments as is shown in Fig.
1
and
Fig.
12
), then the
centered
height function of the dimer model on
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}^{r}
, multiplied by
2\lambda
to match the height gap across the continuum level lines, converges as
\varepsilon\to 0

to
1/\sqrt{2}
times the Dirichlet GFF in the symmetric domain
U^{r}
whose restriction to the upper half plane is
U
(i.e.
U^{r}=U\cup\bar{U}\cup(\partial U\cap(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}))
).
We note that here we take into account the fact that the height function of a single dimer model defined in
[
15
]
is
4
times the height function we are using in this article. From this definition it is clear that the expectation of the height function on
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}^{r}
is antisymmetric across the real line, and that the height function at a face
u
in the
folded dimer model is by definition equal to the sum of the height functions at
u
and at its reflection
\bar{u}
in the single dimer model on
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}^{r}
. Since the continuum GFF in
U
with Neumann boundary conditions
on the real line and Dirichlet boundary conditions on the rest of
\partial U
can be obtained in the same way from
1/\sqrt{2}
times the Dirichlet GFF in
U^{r}
(as e.g. described in
[
18
,
7
]
), we can conclude that
2\lambda
times the height function
defined by the discrete loops and arcs converges to exactly the same GFF with mixed boundary conditions as in the coupling of
[
18
]
.

In the second setup, which we refer to as
shifted dimers
, we first remove the row of vertical edges of
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}^{r}
just below the real line (see Fig.
1
) and then repeat the folding procedure as in the first setup. This results in
a number of differences compared to the previous case. The first one is that now
the arcs emanating from the real line cannot have arbitrary orientations. Indeed, one quickly realises that due to parity reasons the arcs must deterministically alternate in orientation – just as in the part of the coupling in
[
18
]
concerning the Dirichlet GFF. Moreover, after the removal of edges the graph splits into two connected components
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}^{r}\cap(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
and
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}^{r}\cap(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{<0})
.
One can check that the the upper component has again Temperleyan boundary conditions, whereas the lower one has
piecewise Temperleyan
boundary conditions (see Fig.
1
) introduced by Russkikh
[
19
]
, who proved convergence of
2\lambda
times the
centered
height function to
1/\sqrt{2}
times the Dirichlet GFF. Later Berestycki and Liu showed
[
8
, Theorem 5.1]
that the mean
of
2\lambda
times the height function converges to
a harmonic function with piecewise constant boundary conditions with alternating jumps of size
\pm\lambda
located at the points of the
Temperleyan corners

(in our case these are the two extremities of
\partial U\cap(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R})
) plus a term that involves the winding of the boundary of
U
.
Since
U
and
\bar{U}
have opposite windings of the boundary, they cancel out when the two height functions are added, and we can conclude that
the mean of
2\lambda
times the height function of the superimposed configuration converges to the harmonic function in
U
with
\lambda
boundary conditions on
\partial U\cap(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R})
and zero elsewhere.
Since the sum of two independent fields that are both
1/\sqrt{2}
times the Dirichlet GFF (coming from the upper and lower domains) is (
1
times) the Dirichlet GFF, we can conclude that

2\lambda
times the height function of our model converges to the corresponding field described in
[
18
]
, i.e. a GFF in
U
with boundary conditions equal to
\lambda
on

\partial U\cap(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R})
and zero on the rest of
\partial U
.

We conjecture that the arcs in both models converge to the ALE in
U
(emanating from
\partial U\cap(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R})
as in
[
18
]
). This extends the conjecture of
[
7
]
which is the same statement only for the first “folded” model.
Our conjecture is strongly evidenced by the discussion above. However, convergence of the height function is known not to be enough to conclude convergence of interfaces in the double dimer model
[
16
,
12
,
5
]
.
Finally let us mention that the two models yield different measures in the discrete setting, when one forgets the orientation of loops and arcs. Indeed, in the first model each nontrivial arc (that is not a single edge)
comes with a combinatorial factor of two corresponding to the two different orientations, and it comes with a factor of one in the second model.
The number of nontrivial arcs is not constant (unlike the number of all arcs) and hence the measures are different. Nonetheless we conjecture that the two collections of arcs have the same scaling limit.

Main results

The main observables of interest in both models will be
n_{\varepsilon}(z)
– the number of arcs that surround a point
z
, i.e. arcs
that separate
z
from the part of the boundary with no arcs
\partial U\setminus(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R})
, and
o_{\varepsilon}(z)
– the parity of
n_{\varepsilon}(z)
.
Our main result can be summarised as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

In both models, as
\varepsilon\to 0
the laws of
n_{\varepsilon}(z)
and
o_{\varepsilon}(z)
converge in distribution to random variables
n(z)
and
o(z)

whose laws are independent of the model and moreover are conformally invariant.

We refer to Theorem
3.4
and Corollary
3.5
for the general statements, and to Example
3.7
for a particular example of the infinite strip
U^{r}=[-\infty,+\infty]\times[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
.

Moreover we give integral representations of the moments of
n_{\varepsilon}(z)
, and compute its mean explicitly.
In particular, on the infinite strip we show that, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[n_{\varepsilon}(z)]\to\frac{1}{4}-\frac{y^{2}}{\pi^{2}}-\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}\ln(\sin(y)),

which agrees with the mean of the same random variable defined directly for the ALE (as computed by Avelio Sepúlveda in Appendix
A
).
We note that our methods could be extended to analyse the distribution of the number of arcs separating two chosen points in the domain.

One of the main conceptual difficulties in approaching the problem of studying the arcs themselves is the issue of separating the loops from the arcs in the counting arguments.
This is achieved by developing a novel variant of Kenyon’s formula
for double dimers with arcs, and afterwards choosing the right weights (or more precisely SL(2) connections) in this formula. Afterwards an involved analysis of the scaling limit of the formula by means of discrete complex analysis techniques is required.

We note that the works
[
16
,
12
,
5
]
study multi-point functions of topological observables in the double dimer model. Basok and Chelkak
[
5
]
showed that these observables contain enough information to identify the law of the loops.
In our case, the fact that we want to disregard all the loops from the picture restricts greatly the number of useful topological observables, and we can only treat the one-point (or two-point) function.
However, it will be very interesting to develop the methods of
[
16
,
12
,
5
]
to study the full collection of both arcs and loops, that (based on the conjecture of Kenyon
[
16
]
) and our conjectures should converge to the ALE with an independent CLE
4
in its complement.

2.
Kenyon’s formula

2.1.
Setup and formula

We begin with some definitions. Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite, connected, bipartite, planar graph. Its vertices

are partitioned into white and black vertices. Let
\partial
be a
strict

, which contains the same number of black and white vertices. We form a graph
G^{\times}
which can be described as taking two copies of
G
, identifying the vertices in the two copies on
\partial
(and not the rest of the graph), and then adding “diagonal” edges which join a vertex
u
in one copy of
G
to a vertex
v
in the other copy, whenever
u
and
v
form an edge in
G
. One can think of the two copies of
G
being “folded” together along
\partial
. See Figure
2
for a small example.

.

Formally, we write
G^{\times}=(V^{\times},E^{\times})
for the graph with vertex set
V^{\times}:=\partial\cup((V\setminus\partial)\times\{1,2\})
, and edge set
E^{\times}
given by edges:

•

\{(u,i),(v,j)\}
for all
i,j=1,2
and
u,v\in V\setminus\partial
such that
\{u,v\}\in E
;

•

\{(u,i),w\}
for all

and
u\in V\setminus\partial
,

such that
\{u,w\}\in E
;

•

\{w,w^{\prime}\}
for all
w,w^{\prime}\in\partial
and
\{w,w^{\prime}\}\in E
.

This graph is also bipartite, and we define the colour (white or black) of

as the colour of

. We denote by
p
the

from

p(u)=u
, for all
(u,i)\in V^{\times}
,
p(u,i)=u\in V
and for all

A dimer configuration on
G^{\times}
is a set of edges

such that every vertex in
V^{\times}
is incident to exactly one edge in

\partial=\varnothing

is a configuration of disjoint closed loops and double edges on

\partial\neq\varnothing

is a configuration of disjoint closed loops, double edges, and also paths from
\partial

, which we call

(the arcs can be of length 1). Let
\Omega
denote the set of such configurations of loops, double edges, and arcs on
G
.

Let
\Phi
be some
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
connection in the bulk of
G
. That is,
\Phi
assigns each directed edge
uv
(
u,v\in V\setminus\partial
) a matrix
\phi_{u,v}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, such that
\phi_{v,u}=\phi_{u,v}^{-1}
. For a loop
C=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{2n}\}
in
G
, define the monodromy of
\Phi
around
C
,
\phi_{C}
to be the product
\phi_{e_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{e_{2n}}
.
Note this depends on the choice of
e_{1}
and the orientation or the loop;
\mathrm{tr}(\phi_{C})
, however, does not. For an edge
\{u,w\}
with
w\in\partial
and
u\in V\setminus\partial
, let
\psi_{\{u,w\}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Fix some basis of each copy of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
so that each
\phi_{v,u}
can be written as a matrix, and each
\psi_{\{u,w\}}

We say a set of Kasteleyn phases on the edges of a bipartite, planar graph
G
is a function
\xi:E\to\mathbb{C}
with
|\xi_{e}|=1
for all
e\in E
, such that for every simple loop of edges
e_{1}\dots e_{2n}
in
G
, one has

(1)

\frac{\xi_{e_{1}}\xi_{e_{3}}\cdots\xi_{e_{2n-1}}}{\xi_{e_{2}}\xi_{e_{4}}\cdots\xi_{e_{2n}}}=(-1)^{n+1}.

It is well known that such phases exist (see for example Theorem 4.1 of
[
10
]
) and in fact can be taken to be real i.e. signs
\pm 1
on the edges. One can show that (
1
) holds for all loops surrounding an even number of vertices. Moreover if one replaces
\xi_{x,y}
by
\varepsilon(x)\varepsilon(y)\xi_{x,y}
for some function
\varepsilon:V\to\{\pm 1\}
, the new phases are also a set of Kasteleyn phases: this is called a gauge transform.
Let
\bar{\partial}
be a connected, strict subset of the boundary vertices, with
\partial\subset\bar{\partial}
. By using a guage transform, one can assume that the phases on the boundary edges (incident to the external face) alternate between
+1
and
-1
in
\bar{\partial}
. We fix such real phases
\xi
for the rest of this section.

We assign weights
\nu(e)
to the (undirected) edges of
G^{\times}
as follows. If
\{(u,i),(v,j)\}
is some edge in the bulk of
G^{\times}
, with
u
white and
v
black, let
\nu_{\{(u,i),(v,j)\}}:=(\phi_{u,v})_{i,j}
, (the

\phi_{u,v}
.
If
w\in\partial
and
(u,i)
is adjacent to
w
but not in
\partial
, we set
\nu_{\{(u,i),w\}}=(\psi_{\{u,w\}})_{i}
. If

\nu_{\{w,w^{\prime}\}}=1
. Let
K
be an antisymmetric matrix whose rows and columns are indexed by vertices of
G^{\times}
, defined as follows. For
x,y\in V^{\times}
with
x

and let
K_{y,x}=-K_{x,y}
, where
\xi

We assign an ordering to

which will be used in the Pfaffian of Proposition

Remark 3.3

Let

. In particular, note that

is an analytic function of

is an analytic function of

is an analytic function of

. Hence, to have

(11)

\idotsint_{\gamma}\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}F^{(\sigma_{i})}_{-\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1}}(\overline{z_{i+1}},z_{i})\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}dz^{(\sigma_{i})}_{i},

where

and

, and similar for

and

3.2.
Main result and examples.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

c_{n}(z,z_{\partial})=2i^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\idotsint_{\gamma}\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\sigma_{i}F^{(\sigma_{i})}_{-\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1}}(\overline{z_{i+1}},z_{i})\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}dz^{(\sigma_{i})}_{i}.

For example,

(12)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}c_{1}(z,z_{\partial})&=-4\Im\left\{\int_{\gamma}F_{-}(\overline{z},z)dz\right\}\\
c_{2}(z,z_{\partial})&=4\Re\left\{\int\int_{\gamma}F_{+}(\overline{z_{1}},z_{0})\overline{F_{+}(\overline{z_{0}},z_{1})}dz_{0}\overline{dz_{1}}-\int\int_{\gamma}F_{-}(\overline{z_{1}},z_{0})F_{-}(\overline{z_{0}},z_{1})dz_{0}dz_{1}\right\}\end{array}\right.

Note that
c_{n}(z,z_{\partial})=c_{n}(z,z_{\partial},U)
is a conformally invariant quantity, in the sense that if
f:U\to U^{\prime}
is a conformal map, then
c_{n}(z,z_{\partial},U)=c_{n}(f(z),f(z_{\partial}),f(U))
. This follows from two facts: that each integrand is analytic (resp. antianalytic) in
z_{i}
when
\sigma_{i}=1
(resp.
\sigma_{i}=-1
), and that the functions
F_{\pm}=F_{\pm,U}
satisfy
F_{\pm,U}(u,v)=(f^{\prime}(u))^{(\pm)}F_{\pm,f(U)}(f(u),f(v))
, where the superscript
(\pm)
again denotes whether we take the complex conjugate or not. See Proposition 15 of
[
14
]
, as well as the discussion after Proposition 20 of the same paper.

Note also that
c_{n}(z,z_{\partial})
is always real, since the terms in the sum corresponding to
\sigma
and
-\sigma
are complex conjugates. Moreover, since
F_{+}
and
F_{-}
are analytic on
U
and remain bounded near
z_{\partial}
, the
c_{n}(z,z_{\partial})
grow at most exponentially in
n
: there exists an absolute constant
C
(depending only on
\gamma
and
U
) such that

(13)

\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},~c_{n}(z,z_{\partial})\leq C^{n}.\\

To state our main result, we need to introduce the
complete Bell polynomials
, defined by

B_{n}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})=n!\sum_{j_{1}+2j_{2}+\dots+nj_{n}=n}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{X_{k}^{j_{k}}}{(k!)^{j_{k}}(j_{k})!}.

For example
B_{0}=1,B_{1}=X_{1},B_{2}=X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}
.

Recall that
n_{\varepsilon}(z)
and
o_{\varepsilon}(z)
are respectively the number of arcs enclosing
z
, and the parity of the number of arcs enclosing
z
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\omega\in\Omega(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon})
be a folded or shifted dimer configuration, with law
\mu_{\varepsilon}=\mu_{\varepsilon}^{1}
or
\mu_{\varepsilon}=\mu_{\varepsilon}^{2}
, and expectation with respect to this law denoted by
\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}
. For all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, for all
\sigma\in\{0,1\}
,

\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[\binom{\frac{n_{\varepsilon}(z)-o_{\varepsilon}(z)}{2}}{n}o_{\varepsilon}(z)^{\sigma}\right]\overset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\frac{(-1)^{n+\sigma}}{(2n+\sigma)!}B_{2n+\sigma}\left(\left((-2)^{k-1}(k-1)!c_{k}(z,z_{\partial})\right)_{1\leq k\leq 2n+\sigma}\right).

The proof of this theorem given in Section
4
. The theorem gives the following corollary:

Corollary 3.5
.

The asymptotics of all joint moments of
(n_{\varepsilon}(z),o_{\varepsilon}(z))
can be computed explicitly: more precisely, for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
\sigma\in\{0,1\}
,
\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[n_{\varepsilon}(z)^{n}o_{\varepsilon}(z)^{\sigma}]
converges towards a polynomial in the
\left(c_{k}(z,z_{\partial})\right)_{1\leq k\leq 2n+\sigma}
. For example,

(14)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[o_{\varepsilon}(z)]&\overset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}-c_{1}(z,z_{\partial})\\
\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[n_{\varepsilon}(z)]&\overset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}2c_{2}(z,z_{\partial})-c_{1}(z,z_{\partial})^{2}-c_{1}(z,z_{\partial})\\
\mathrm{var}[n_{\varepsilon}(z)]&\overset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}-\tfrac{2}{3}c_{1}^{4}-\tfrac{4}{3}c_{1}^{3}-3c_{1}^{2}-c_{1}+\tfrac{32}{3}c_{1}c_{3}-4c_{2}^{2}+4c_{2}+\tfrac{16}{3}c_{3}-16c_{4},\end{array}\right.

where in the last line we write
c_{i}=c_{i}(z,z_{\partial})
for brevity. The parity of the number of arcs enclosing a given point
o_{\varepsilon}(z)
and the number of arcs surrounding a given point
n_{\varepsilon}(z)
converge in law towards random variables
o(z),n(z)
which are conformally invariant.

We explain how Theorem
3.4
implies Corollary
3.5
.

Proof.

We first observe that since
o_{\varepsilon}(z)\in\{0,1\}
,
o_{\varepsilon}(z)^{k}=o_{\varepsilon}(z)
for all
k\geq 1
, it is enough to compute
\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[n_{\varepsilon}(z)^{k}]
and
\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[n_{\varepsilon}(z)^{k}o_{\varepsilon}(z)]
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Theorem
3.4
applied with
n=0,\sigma=1
gives

\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[o_{\varepsilon}(z)]\overset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}-B_{1}(c_{1}(z,z_{\partial}))=-c_{1}(z,z_{\partial}).

Theorem
3.4
applied with
n=1,\sigma=0
gives

\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[n_{\varepsilon}(z)-o_{\varepsilon}(z)]=-B_{2}(c_{1}(z,z_{\partial}),-2c_{2}(z,z_{\partial}))+o_{\varepsilon\to 0}(1)=-c_{1}(z,z_{\partial})^{2}+2c_{2}(z,z_{\partial})+o_{\varepsilon\to 0}(1).

By induction, it is possible to obtain all joints moments of
(n_{\varepsilon}(z),o_{\varepsilon}(z))
in the same way. Assume that for some
n
, for all
0\leq k\leq n
and
\sigma\in\{0,1\}
,
\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[n_{\varepsilon}(z)^{k}o_{\varepsilon}(z)^{\sigma}]
is a polynomial in the
c_{i}(z,z_{\partial})
for
1\leq i\leq 2k+\sigma
. Developing the binomial coefficient, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[\binom{\frac{n_{\varepsilon}(z)-o_{\varepsilon}(z)}{2}}{n+1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n+1}(n+1)!}\left(\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[n_{\varepsilon}(z)^{n+1}]+\sum_{\underset{\sigma\in\{0,1\}}{0\leq k\leq n}}a_{k\sigma}\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[n_{\varepsilon}(z)^{k}o_{\varepsilon}(z)^{\sigma}]\right)

where the
a_{k\sigma}
are explicit coefficients. By induction, the
\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[n_{\varepsilon}(z)^{k}o_{\varepsilon}(z)^{\sigma}]
are explicit polynomials in the
(c_{k+\sigma}(z,z_{\partial}))_{1\leq 2k+\sigma\leq 2n+1}
(up to
o_{\varepsilon\to 0}(1)
, and by Theorem
3.4
applied at
n+1,\sigma=0
, the left-hand side is an explicit polynomial in the
(c_{k+\sigma}(z,z_{\partial}))_{1\leq 2n+2}
(up to
o_{\varepsilon\to 0}(1)
), so
\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[n_{\varepsilon}(z)^{n+1}]
is also an explicit polynomial in the
(c_{k+\sigma}(z,z_{\partial}))_{1\leq 2n+2}
up to
o_{\varepsilon\to 0}(1)
.

Similarly, Theorem
3.4
with
n+1,\sigma=1
enables to express
\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[n_{\varepsilon}(z)^{n+1}o_{\varepsilon}(z)]
in terms of the
\left(c_{k}(z,z_{\partial})\right)_{1\leq k\leq 2n+3}
, which concludes the induction step.

We obtained that the moments of
n_{\varepsilon}(z)
converge: for all
n
,
\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[n_{\varepsilon}(z)^{n}]\overset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}m_{n}
. To deduce the convergence in law of the random variable
n_{\varepsilon}(z)
, we only have to check the conditions of the moment problem, that is find a bound on
m_{n}
. Bounding crudely the binomial coefficient from below, we find that

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[\binom{\frac{n_{\varepsilon}(z)-o_{\varepsilon}(z)}{2}}{n}\right]\geq\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[\binom{\frac{n_{\varepsilon}(z)-1}{2}}{n}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[\mathds{1}_{n_{\varepsilon}(z)\geq 2n}\frac{(n_{\varepsilon}(z)-2n)^{n}}{2^{n}n!}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[\mathds{1}_{n_{\varepsilon}(z)\geq 4n}\frac{n_{\varepsilon}(z)^{n}}{4^{n}n!}\right]\geq\frac{1}{4^{n}n!}(\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[n_{\varepsilon}(z)^{n}]-(4n)^{n}).

and taking the limit in Theorem
3.4
with
n,\sigma=0
gives

m_{n}\leq(4n)^{n}+4^{n}n!\frac{\left|B_{2n}\left(\left((-2)^{k-1}(k-1)!c_{k}(z,z_{\partial})\right)_{1\leq k\leq 2n}\right)\right|}{(2n)!}.

Since the
c_{k}(z,z_{\partial})
grow at most exponentially by Equation (
13
), using the explicit expression of the Bell polynomial, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\left|B_{2n}\left(\left((-2)^{k-1}(k-1)!c_{k}(z,z_{\partial})\right)_{1\leq k\leq 2n}\right)\right|}{(2n)!}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j_{1}+2j_{2}+\dots+2nj_{2n}=2n}\prod_{i=1}^{2n}\frac{((2C)^{k}k!)^{j_{k}}}{(k!)^{j_{k}}(j_{k})!}

\displaystyle=(2C)^{2n}\sum_{j_{1}+2j_{2}+\dots+2nj_{2n}=2n}\frac{1}{(j_{k})!}

\displaystyle\leq(2C)^{2n}\left|\left\{(j_{1},\dots,j_{2n})~;~j_{1}+2j_{2}+\dots+2nj_{2n}=2n\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq(2C)^{2n}\frac{(2n)^{2n}}{(2n)!},

since there are at most
n
choices for
j_{1}
,
n/2
choices for
j_{2}
etc. Using Stierling’s formula, we conclude that
m_{n}\leq C^{n}n^{n}
for another absolute constant
C
, hence Carleman’s condition is verified and
n_{\varepsilon}(z)
converges towards a random variable
n(z)
which is uniquely determined by its moments
m_{n}
. Since these moments are conformally invariant (they are expressed as integrals of conformal quantities), the law of
n(z)
is also conformally invariant.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

The infinite strip does not fill the technical hypothesis of our theorem since it is not a bounded open set, although it fills the other conditions: it is symmetric and has a horizontal boundary. Nonetheless, since the formula for the moments depends only on conformally invariant quantities, the moments can be computed in any simply connected open set, and the computations transferred to any other domain by a Riemann map. Besides, if we take

3.4
still holds, at least for the folded dimer model: for the proof to extend to this folded case on

, the only part of the proof that has to be modified is Lemma
B.3
of the Appendix. The proof of this lemma still works because, to show that

is close to the partial derivative of the Green function

, we only need that

(the full-plane Green function) or

(the half-plane Green function) and

are

on the boundary of

to apply the Harnack lemma. This is also true here.

Example 3.7

(The infinite strip)
.

Let
U^{r}=[-\infty,+\infty]\times[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
. We give a particularly simple expression for
\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[o_{\varepsilon}(z)]
and
\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[n_{\varepsilon}(z)]
. To apply Corollary
3.5
, we need to identify
F_{-}
and
F_{+}
. The conformal map from this strip to the half plane is

\phi:U^{r}\to\mathbb{H},u\to\exp(u+i\pi/2)=i\exp(u).

On the half plane,
\tilde{g}_{\mathbb{H}}(u,v)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\frac{u-v}{\bar{u}-v}
with
\log
denoting the principal value of the complex logarithm (see the footnote in Section 6.3 of
[
16
]
). Hence, on the strip

\tilde{g}(u,v)=\tilde{g}_{\mathbb{H}}(\phi(u),\phi(v))=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\left(-\frac{e^{u}-e^{v}}{e^{\bar{u}}+e^{v}}\right).

By definition, for
u,v\in U^{r}
,

F_{+}(u,v)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{e^{u}}{e^{u}-e^{v}},\qquad F_{-}(u,v)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{e^{\bar{u}}}{e^{\bar{u}}+e^{v}}.

Let
z=x+iy
(with
x\in(0,\pi/2)
),
z_{\partial}=x+i\pi/2
. For
\gamma
, we choose the vertical straight line from
z
to
z_{\partial}
. We first compute the probability that there is an odd number of arcs enclosing
z
: for all
z\in U^{r}
,
F_{-}(\bar{z},z)=\frac{1}{4\pi}
so

c_{1}(z,z_{\partial})=-4\Im\left\{\int_{x+iy}^{x+i\pi/2}\frac{1}{4\pi}dz\right\}=-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{y}{\pi}

and by Corollary
3.5
,

\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[o_{\varepsilon}(z)]\overset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}-c_{1}(z,z_{\partial})=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{y}{\pi}.

To obtain the expected number of arcs in the limit, we first need to compute

c_{2}(z,z_{\partial})=4\Re\left\{\int\int_{\gamma}F_{+}(\overline{z_{1}},z_{0})\overline{F_{+}(\overline{z_{0}},z_{1})}dz_{0}\overline{dz_{1}}-\int\int_{\gamma}F_{-}(\overline{z_{1}},z_{0})F_{-}(\overline{z_{0}},z_{1})dz_{0}dz_{1}\right\}.

For all
z_{0}=x+iy_{0}
,
z_{1}=x+iy_{1}
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}F_{-}(\bar{z_{1}},z_{0})&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{e^{z_{1}}}{e^{z_{1}}+e^{z_{0}}}&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{e^{iy_{1}}}{e^{iy_{1}}+e^{iy_{0}}}&=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left(1+i\tan\left(\frac{y_{1}-y_{0}}{2}\right)\right)\\
F_{+}(\bar{z_{1}},z_{0})&=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{e^{\bar{z_{1}}}}{e^{\bar{z_{1}}}-e^{z_{0}}}&=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{e^{-iy_{1}}}{e^{-y_{1}}-e^{iy_{0}}}&=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\left(1+i\cot\left(\frac{y_{1}+y_{0}}{2}\right)\right).\end{array}\right.

After some algebra, we get

c_{2}(z,z_{\partial})=-\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\ln(\sin(y)),

so Corollary
3.5
implies

(15)

\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[n_{\varepsilon}(z)]\overset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}-\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}\ln(\sin(y))+\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{y^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\right).

Remark 3.8
.

This is coherent with what we expect of the continuous model: when
z
approaches the real axis, the number of arcs enclosing
z
explodes and it is odd or even with probability

. When
z
approaches the boundary of the strip, the probability that there is at least one arc enclosing
z
goes to
0
, and so does the probability that there is an odd number of arcs. Besides, the number of arcs above a point is always positive and explodes when this point approaches the real axis.

,

(16)

\mathrm{Pf}K_{\alpha}=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\prod_{\mathrm{loops}\ C}\mathrm{tr}(\phi_{C})\prod_{\mathrm{arcs}\ A}\psi_{b_{A}}^{\intercal}\phi_{A}\psi_{w_{A}}.

From here, the proof follows three main steps:

•

Show that the right-hand side of Equation (

) can be expressed as a power series in

involving the moments of
n_{\varepsilon}(z)
and
o_{\varepsilon}(z)
.

•

Show that the left hand side can be expanded as a power series in
\alpha
with coefficients
\mathrm{tr}((K^{-1}S)^{n})
where

is a discrete holomorphy matrix (see Appendix
B
for more details on the discrete holomorphy theory on the square lattice), and

is a matrix encoding the zipper.

•

Use the asymptotic expression of

in terms of the continuous Green function obtained by Kenyon in
[

]
(see Appendix

) to show that
\mathrm{tr}((K^{-1}S)^{n})
converges to an
n
-fold holomorphic integral.

First step: the right-hand side of Equation (
16
)

Let
\alpha>0
. Given
\omega\in\Omega(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon})
, we denote by
c_{\varepsilon}
its total number of loops and
n_{\varepsilon}
its total number of arcs (which is constant and equal to the number of white boundary vertices). We can write:

\mathrm{Pf}K=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}2^{c_{\varepsilon}}2^{n_{\varepsilon}},

which is the partition function of the loops and arcs model. Now let
\alpha\geq 0
. The possible monodromies for loops are
I_{2}
,
\begin{pmatrix}1&\alpha\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
and its inverse
\begin{pmatrix}1&\ {-\alpha}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
, which all have trace
2
. Hence each loop contributes a factor
2
to the right-hand side of the Kasteleyn formula. For an arc
A
with endpoints
w_{A}
and
b_{A}
, we say that the arc is clockwise if
w_{A}
is left of
b_{A}
. By definition of the zipper
E(\gamma)
, there are three possibilities for the monodromy along an arc. If
A
does not enclose
z
, then
\phi_{A}=I_{2}
and
\psi_{b_{A}}^{\intercal}\phi_{A}\psi_{w_{A}}=2
. If
A
is clockwise and encloses
z
, since
\phi_{A}
is computed by orienting the edges towards
w_{A}
,
\phi_{A}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\alpha\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
and
\psi_{b_{A}}^{\intercal}\phi_{A}\psi_{w_{A}}=2+\alpha
. If
A
is counterclockwise and encloses
z
, we get
\psi_{b_{A}}^{\intercal}\phi_{A}\psi_{w_{A}}=2-\alpha
. Given
\omega\in\Omega(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon})
, we denote by
r_{\varepsilon}(z)
(resp
l_{\varepsilon}(z)
) its number of clockwise (resp counterclockwise) arcs enclosing
z
. These quantities are random variables whose laws depends on
\varepsilon
. For fixed
\varepsilon
,

(17)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pf}K_{2\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}2^{c_{\varepsilon}}2^{n_{\varepsilon}-r_{\varepsilon}(z)-l_{\varepsilon}(z)}(2+2\alpha)^{r_{\varepsilon}(z)}(2-2\alpha)^{l_{\varepsilon}(z)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}2^{c_{\varepsilon}+n_{\varepsilon}}(1+\alpha)^{r_{\varepsilon}(z)}(1-\alpha)^{l_{\varepsilon}(z)}.

Recall the definition of
n_{\varepsilon}(z)
and
o_{\varepsilon}(z)
. We claim that

\forall\varepsilon\geq 0,~\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}n_{\varepsilon}(z)&=l_{\varepsilon}(z)+r_{\varepsilon}(z)\\
o_{\varepsilon}(z)&=l_{\varepsilon}(z)-r_{\varepsilon}(z)\end{array}\right..

The first equality is clear: an arc is either oriented clockwise or counterclockwise. We explain why the second one holds. For
\omega\in\Omega(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon})
, the arcs enclosing
z
are alternating in orientation. To see this, assume that two arcs
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
both enclosing
z
have the same orientation, say clockwise
w_{A_{1}}<w_{A_{2}}<b_{A_{2}}<b_{A_{1}}
, and that there is no other arc enclosing
z
between them. This cannot happen because there is an odd number of boundary vertices between
w_{A_{1}}
and
w_{A_{2}}
so they cannot be matched together. For the same reason, since the leftmost corner on the boundary is black while the rightmost is white by definition of the Temperleyan domain, there is always
0
or
1
more counterclockwise than clockwise arc which encloses
z
. Hence,
o_{\varepsilon}(z):=l_{\varepsilon}(z)-r_{\varepsilon}(z)=\mathds{1}\{n_{\varepsilon}(z)\text{ is odd}\}\in\{0,1\}
, and

\mathrm{Pf}K_{2\alpha}=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}2^{c_{\varepsilon}+n_{\varepsilon}}(1-\alpha^{2})^{r_{\varepsilon}(z)}(1-\alpha)^{o_{\varepsilon}(z)}=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}2^{c_{\varepsilon}+n_{\varepsilon}}(1-\alpha^{2})^{r_{\varepsilon}(z)}(1-o_{\varepsilon}(z)\alpha).

Dividing by
\mathrm{Pf}K
, we get

(18)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{Pf}K_{2\alpha}}{\mathrm{Pf}K}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}[(1-\alpha^{2})^{r_{\varepsilon}(z)}(1-o_{\varepsilon}(z)\alpha)]=\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\binom{r_{\varepsilon}(z)}{k}\alpha^{2k}(1-o_{\varepsilon}(z)\alpha)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\alpha^{2k}\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[\binom{r_{\varepsilon}(z)}{k}\right]-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\alpha^{2k+1}\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[\binom{r_{\varepsilon}(z)}{k}o_{\varepsilon}(z)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\alpha^{2k}\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[\binom{\frac{n_{\varepsilon}(z)-o_{\varepsilon}(z)}{2}}{k}\right]-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\alpha^{2k+1}\mathbbm{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[\binom{\frac{n_{\varepsilon}(z)-o_{\varepsilon}(z)}{2}}{k}o_{\varepsilon}(z)\right].

For every fixed
\varepsilon>0
and every
\alpha>0
small enough, the sums converge because the number of (counterclockwise) arcs surrounding any given point is bounded.
