Often, a graph comes with extra structure; for example, labeling of edges and/or vertices as in the example above.

Formally speaking, the described graph representation can be applied to nearly any puzzle.

Here is a small variation of another classic puzzle.

Four missionaries and four cannibals must cross a river using a boat that can carry at most two people.
The boat cannot cross the river by itself with no people on board.
If there are missionaries on one bank, they cannot be outnumbered by cannibals;

Can the missionaries and cannibals safely cross the river?

In this section, we review properties of binomial coefficients that will be needed further.

Binomial coefficients
will be denoted by

(a+b)^{n}=\tbinom{n}{0}{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}a^{0}{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}b^{n}+\tbinom{n}{1}{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}a^{1}{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}b^{n-1}+\dots+\tbinom{n}{n}{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}a^{n}{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}b^{0}

It follows that

A
Markov’s inequality

A
random variable
is a real number that depends on a random event.
We will consider random variables that take only finitely many values.

Two random variables are called
independent
if the occurrence of one does not affect the other.

The
expected value
is the average of a large number of independently selected outcomes of the random variable.
The expected value of a random variable
X
will be denoted by
\mathbb{E}[X]
.
Suppose a random variable
X
takes only values
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
with probabilities
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
, respectively,
so
P_{1}+\dots+P_{n}=1
.
Then

\mathbb{E}[X]=P_{1}{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}x_{1}+\dots+P_{n}{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}x_{n}.

➊

For example, if
X
is the result of rolling a die, then it takes each value
1,2,\dots,6
with probability
\tfrac{1}{6}
;
therefore

\mathbb{E}[X]=\tfrac{1}{6}{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}1+\dots+\tfrac{1}{6}{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}6=3.5.

3.1. Claim.

For any two random variables
X
and
Y
and real constant
c
we have

\mathbb{E}[X+Y]=\mathbb{E}[X]+\mathbb{E}[Y]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{E}[c{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}X]=c{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}\mathbb{E}[X].

Moreover, if
X
and
Y
are independent, then

\mathbb{E}[X{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}Y]=\mathbb{E}[X]{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}\mathbb{E}[Y].

(We assume that all expected values in the formulas are well defined.)

3.2. Markov’s inequality.

Suppose
Y
is a nonnegative random variable and
c>0
.
Denote by
P
the probability of the event
Y\geqslant c
.
Then

P{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}c\leqslant\mathbb{E}[Y].

➋

Proof.

Consider another random variable
\bar{Y}
such that
\bar{Y}=c
if
Y\geqslant c
and
\bar{Y}=0
otherwise.

Note that
\bar{Y}\leqslant\penalty Y
and therefore

\mathbb{E}[\bar{Y}]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[Y].

The random variable
\bar{Y}
takes the value
c
with probability
P
and
0
with probability
1-P
.
By
➊
,

\mathbb{E}[\bar{Y}]=P{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}c;

whence
➋
follows.
∎

B
Probabilistic method

The probabilistic method allows us to prove the existence of graphs with certain properties without explicitly constructing them.
The idea is to show that if one randomly chooses a graph or its coloring from a specified class, then the probability that the result has the needed property is more than zero.
This implies that a graph with required the property exists.

Despite using probability, the final conclusion is determined for certain, without any possible error.

3.3. Theorem.

Assume that the inequality

\tbinom{R}{n}<2^{{\binom{n}{2}}-1}

holds for a pair of positive integers
R
and
n
.
Then
r(n,n)>R
.

Proof.

We need to show that the complete graph
K_{R}

admits a coloring of edges in red and blue such that it has no monochromatic subgraph isomorphic to
K_{n}
.

Let us color the edges randomly;
that is, color each edge independently in red or blue with equal chances.

Fix a set
S
of
n
vertices.
Define the random variable
X(S)
to be
1
if every edge between the vertices in
S
has the same color; otherwise set
X(S)=0
.

The random variable
X(S)
may take the values
0
or
1
.
The expected value of
X(S)
is the probability that
X(S)=1
;
that is, all of the
\tbinom{n}{2}=\tfrac{n{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}(n-1)}{2}

edges in
S
have the same color.
The probability that all the edges with the ends in
S
are blue is
{2^{-\binom{n}{2}}}
, and with the same probability all the edges are red.
Since these two possibilities exclude each other,

\mathbb{E}[X(S)]={2}{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}{2^{-\binom{n}{2}}}.

This holds for any
n
-vertex subset
S
of the vertices of
K_{R}
.

Note that the number
Y
of monochromatic
n
-subgraphs in
K_{R}
is the sum of
X(S)
over all possible
n
-vertex subsets
S
.
The total number of such subsets is
\tbinom{R}{n}
.
By
3A
,

\mathbb{E}[Y]=2{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}\tbinom{R}{n}{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}2^{-\binom{n}{2}}.

Denote by
P
the probability that a random coloring of
K_{R}
has at least one monochromatic
K_{n}
’s.
By Markov’s inequality,

P\leqslant\mathbb{E}[Y].

Since the number of monochromatic
K_{n}
’s is an integer, it follows that
a random coloring of
K_{R}
has no monochromatic
K_{n}
’s with probability at least
1-P
.
If
\mathbb{E}[Y]<1
, it implies that this probability is positive;
in particular, at least one edge-coloring of
K_{R}
has no monochromatic
K_{n}
.
That is, if

\tbinom{R}{n}<2^{\binom{n}{2}-1},

then there is a coloring

with no monochromatic
n
-subgraph.
∎

3.4. Corollary.

r(n,n)>\tfrac{1}{8}{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}2^{\frac{n}{2}}

Set

C
Counting proof

In this section, we rewrite the proof of Theorem
3B
without the use of probability.
We do this to affirm that the probabilistic method provides a real proof, without any possible error.

In principle, any probabilistic proof admits such a translation,
but in most cases, the translation is less intuitive.

Counting proof of
3B
.

The graph
K_{R}
has
\tbinom{R}{2}
edges.
Each edge can be colored in blue or red;
therefore the total number of different colorings is

\Omega=2^{\binom{R}{2}}.

Fix a subgraph isomorphic to
K_{n}
in
K_{R}
.
Note that this graph is red in
\Omega/2^{\binom{n}{2}}
different colorings
and blue in
\Omega/2^{\binom{n}{2}}
colorings.

There are
\tbinom{R}{n}
different subgraphs isomorphic to
K_{n}
in
K_{R}
.
Therefore, the total number of monochromatic
K_{n}
’s in all the colorings
is

M=\tbinom{R}{n}{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}\Omega{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}2/2^{\binom{n}{2}}.

If
M<\Omega
, then by the pigeonhole principle,
there is a coloring with no monochromatic
K_{n}
.
Hence the result.
∎

Calculation of determinants

In this section, we recall key properties of the determinant which will be used in the next section.

Let
M

components
of the matrix.
The determinant
\det M
is a polynomial of the
n^{2}
components of
M

1.

The unit matrix has determinant 1; that is,

\det\left(\begin{matrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&\cdots&0&1\end{matrix}\right)=1.

2.

If we multiply each component of one of the rows of by a number
\lambda
, then for the obtained matrix
M^{\prime}
, we have

\det M^{\prime}=\lambda{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\penalty\hskip 0.5pt}\det M.

3.

If one adds (or subtracts) term-by-term components

has the same determinant; that is

\det M^{\prime}=\det M.

These three conditions define the determinant in a uniquely.
We will not give a proof of the statement; it is not evident and not complicated (sooner or later you will have to learn it if it is not done already).

There is a strong connection between counting spanning trees of a given graph,
calculations of currents in an electric chain
and random walks; a good survey is given in the book by Peter Doyle and Laurie Snell

Let us give some examples.

The following procedure calculates the current

along a given edge

Fix a direction of

Note that any spanning tree

(1) the edge

6.3. Deletion-minus-contraction formula.

Let
e
be an edge in a pseudograph
G

Then

P_{G}(x)=P_{G-e}(x)-P_{G/e}(x).

➊

Proof.

The valid colorings of

(1) those where the ends of the edge

get different colors — these remain valid colorings of

and (2) those where the ends of

get the same color — each of such colorings corresponds to a unique coloring of

Hence

P_{G-e}(x)=P_{G}(x)+P_{G/e}(x),

which is equivalent to the deletion-minus-contraction formula

.
∎

Note that if the pseudograph

has loops, then
P_{G}(x)=0
for any

Indeed, in a valid coloring, the ends of a loop should get different colors, which is impossible.

The latter can also be proved using the deletion-minus-contraction formula.
Indeed, if

G/e=G-e
.
Therefore,
P_{G-e}(x)=\penalty P_{G/e}(x)
and

P_{G}(x)=P_{G-e}(x)-P_{G/e}(x)=0.

Similarly, removing a parallel edge from a pseudograph

for any

Indeed, if

Therefore,

The same identity can be seen directly — any admissible coloring of

Summarizing the discussion above:

Recall that polynomial

Chapter 8
Minimum spanning trees

A
Optimization problems

We say that a graph
G
has
weighted edges
if each edge in
G
is labeled by a real number, called its
weight
.
In this case the
weight of a subgraph

H
(briefly
\mathop{\rm weight}\nolimits(H)
) is defined as the sum of the weights of its edges.

An
optimization problem
typically asks to minimize the weight of a subgraph of a certain type.
A classic example is the so-called
traveling salesman problem
.
It asks to find a Hamiltonian cycle with minimal weight (if it exists in the graph).

No fast algorithm is known to solve this problem and it is expected that no fast algorithm exists.
We say
fast
if the required time depends polynomially on the number of vertices in the graph.
The best-known algorithms require exponential time, which is just a bit better than brute force checking of all possible Hamiltonian cycles.

In fact, the traveling salesman problem is a classic example of the so-called
NP-hard problems
; it means that if you find a fast algorithm to solve it, then you solve the
P=NP problem
— the most important question in modern mathematics.

The so-called
nearest neighbor algorithm
gives a heuristic way to find a Hamiltonian cycle with small (but not necessarily the smallest) weight.
In the following description, we assume that the graph is complete.

1.

Start at an arbitrary vertex.

2.

Find the edge with minimal weight connecting the current vertex
v
and an unvisited vertex
w
and move to
w
.

3.

Repeat step
2
until all vertices are visited and then return to the original vertex.
This way we walked along a Hamiltonian cycle.

The nearest neighbor algorithm is an example of
greedy algorithms
;
in other words, it chooses the cheapest step at each stage.
The cheap choices at the beginning might force it to make expansive choices at the end,
so it may not find an optimal solution.
In fact a greedy algorithm might give the worst solution.
For example, if we start from the vertex
a
in the shown weighted graph, then the nearest neighbor algorithm produces the cycle
abcd
, which is the worst — its total weight is
11=1+1+5+4
, and the other two Hamiltonian cycles have weights
9
and
10
.

Let us list a few other classic examples of optimization problems.
Unlike the traveling salesman problem, there are fast algorithms which solve them.

\diamond

The
shortest path problem
asks for a path with minimal weight, connecting two given vertices.
It can be thaut of as optimal driving directions between two locations.

\diamond

The
assignment problem
asks for a matching of a given size with minimal weight, in a bipartite graph.
It can be used to minimize the total cost of work, assuming that available workers charge different prices for each task.
This problem is closely related to the subject of the next chapter.

\diamond

The
minimum spanning tree problem
asks for a spanning tree with minimal weight.
For example, we may think of minimizing the cost to build a computer network between given locations.
This problem will be the main subject of the remaining sections of this chapter.

Kruskal’s algorithm

Kruskal’s algorithm finds a minimum weight spanning tree in a given connected graph.
If the graph is not connected, the algorithm finds a spanning tree in each component of the graph.
Let us describe it informally.

1.

Suppose
G
is a graph with weighted edges.
Start with the spanning forest

in
G

2.

Remove from
G
an edge with minimal weight.
If this edge connects different trees in
F
, then add it to
F
.

3.

Repeat the procedure while
G
has any remaining edges.

Note that Kruskal’s algorithm is
greedy
; it chooses the cheapest step at each stage.

8.5. Theorem.

Kruskal’s algorithm finds a minimum weight spanning tree in any connected pseudograph with weighted edges.

Proof.

Evidently, the subgraph
F
remains a forest at all stages.
If at the end,
F
has more than one component, then the original graph
G
could not have edges connecting these components;
otherwise, the algorithm would choose it at some stage.
Therefore, the resulting graph is a tree.

Suppose the spanning tree

Then

does not satisfy Theorem

that is, there is a neighbor

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm weight}\nolimits(T)

\displaystyle>\mathop{\rm weight}\nolimits(T^{\prime})=

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm weight}\nolimits(T)+\mathop{\rm weight}\nolimits(e^{\prime})-\mathop{\rm weight}\nolimits(e).

Therefore,
\mathop{\rm weight}\nolimits(e^{\prime})<\mathop{\rm weight}\nolimits(e)

Denote by

Note that

A remark about forbidden minors

It is straightforward to see that if
G
is toroidal, then any graph obtained from
G
by the deletion of a vertex or an edge or by contracting an edge is also toroidal.

A graph that can be obtained from a given graph
G
by applying a sequence of such operations is called a
minor
of
G
.
The pseudograph
G
is considered to be a minor of itself.
The other minors require at least one deletion or contraction; they are called
proper minors
.

Note that the statement above implies that any minor of a toroidal graph is toroidal, or in other words,
toridality is inherited by minors
.

The following deep result was proved by Neil Robertson and Paul Seymour
[
38
]
.

10.11. Theorem.

Any property of pseudographs that is inherited by minors can be
described by a finite set of
forbidden minors
;
that is, a pseudograph meets the property if and only if none of its minors are forbidden.

For example, note that deletion and contraction do not create cycles;
that is, any minor of a forest is a forest.
Forests can be described by one forbidden minor — a pseudograph formed by one loop.
Indeed, if a pseudograph has a cycle, then by a sequence of deletions and contractions, one can get a single loop from it.

A more complicated example is given by the Pontryagin–Kuratowski theorem;
it states that
planar graphs are characterized by two forbidden minors:
K_{5}
and

Chapter 12
Rewriting systems

Suppose a certain set of objects and a set of elementary transformations for these objects are given, and we are interested in the following problem.

Under what conditions do these transformations reduce each object to a single form?

As we will see, this question is equivalent to an elegant statement about graphs (Lemma
12F
).

For a precise formulation, we need to introduce rewriting systems.
They are understood in two senses: narrow and broad.
In the narrow sense, certain word transformations are considered.
These are the so-called
string rewriting systems
, which will be defined in the next section.
In the broad sense, we talk about any objects and transformations; these are called
abstract rewriting systems
.

A

Let us choose a finite set of symbols and call it an

Any finite strings (including the empty string) of symbols in the alphabet will be called a

If the alphabet has only two symbols

to

.
More precisely, a word

can be exchanged for the word

;

and

In this case we write

.

For instance, we might be allowed to change

to

and

An

The vertices of the digraph are often referred to as

For two objects

We will be interested in the relation

Of course, if we know the digraph, then we know the relation

A string rewriting system can be interpreted as an abstract rewriting system with the set of vertices formed by

Let us introduce two new binary relations on the set of vertices of the digraph that will be denoted by

The first relation

More precisely, there is a sequence of objects

The expression

In other words,

A system is called

An object

In other words, there is no way out of

It is clear that in a terminating rewriting system, starting at vertex

In the latter case, we will say that

of

.

Any object in the terminating system has at least one normal form, and there can be many of them.
If

has a

For string rewriting systems, the words can be ordered first by length, and if the lengths are equal, lexicographically.

12.8. Diamond lemma.

A terminating rewriting system is confluent if and only if it is locally confluent.

Let us describe an induction-type argument that will be used in the proof.

12.9. Example.

\langle a,b\mid baaba\to a\rangle.

Applying the shortlex order, we find that the system is terminating.

To check confluence, we need to examine cases A and B.
There is only one word here, but it overlaps with itself in
ba
.
By gluing together two such words that overlap in
ba
, we come to checking the word
baabaaba
.

and

.

and

are equivalent to the original word
baabaaba
.

to the existing rules, then the word equivalence relation will remain unchanged.
It leads to the following example.

12.10. Example.

\langle a,b\mid baaba\to a,baaa\to aaba\rangle.

Again, applying the shortlex order, we find that the system is terminating.

Now we need to go thru all the options for overlapping words.
There are only two such options.
One of them is old and does not need to be checked.
Now the words
aaba
and
baaa
from the previous example are automatically converted to
aaba
in the new system.
But we have a new relationship, and therefore a new overlap has arisen:
baaba
overlaps with
baaa
by
ba
. (Here you must always be careful, since a new word can give several new overlaps;
two words can overlap not in one, but in many ways — all this must be checked.)

Gluing the overlapping words together leads to

and we can get
(baaba)aa\to aaa

and
baa(baaa)\to baaaaba
.
Since
(baaa)aba\to\penalty aa(baaba)\to aaa
, we get that the system is confluent,
and therefore convergent.
