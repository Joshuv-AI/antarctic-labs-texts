Divergence, thickness and hypergraph index for general Coxeter groups
Pallavi Dani, Yusra Naqvi, Ignat Soroko, Anne Thomas

Abstract
. We study divergence and thickness for general Coxeter groups W W . We first characterise linear divergence, and show that if W W has superlinear divergence then its divergence is at least quadratic. We then formulate a computable combinatorial invariant, hypergraph index, for arbitrary Coxeter systems ( W , S ) (W,S) . This generalises Levcovitz’s definition for the right-angled case. We prove that if ( W , S ) (W,S) has finite hypergraph index h h , then W W is (strongly algebraically) thick of order at most h h , hence has divergence bounded above by a polynomial of degree h + 1 h+1 . We conjecture that these upper bounds on the order of thickness and divergence are in fact equalities, and we prove our conjecture for certain families of Coxeter groups. These families are obtained by a new construction which, given any right-angled Coxeter group, produces infinitely many examples of non-right-angled Coxeter systems with the same hypergraph index. Finally, we give an upper bound on the hypergraph index of any Coxeter system ( W , S ) (W,S) , and hence on the divergence of W W , in terms of, unexpectedly, the topology of its associated Dynkin diagram.



1.
Introduction

We study the quasi-isometry invariants divergence and thickness for general Coxeter groups. We define a combinatorial invariant of any Coxeter system
(W,S)
, its
hypergraph index

h(W,S)\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}\cup\{\infty\}
, which is computed from its Coxeter presentation
W=\langle S\mid(st)^{m_{st}}\rangle
, and which conjecturally determines both the divergence and the order of thickness of
W
. This generalises the hypergraph index introduced by Levcovitz in the right-angled case
[
Lev19
]
.

Roughly speaking, the
divergence
of a pair of geodesic rays with common basepoint measures, as a function of
r
, the length of a shortest path connecting their time-
r
points which stays at least distance
r
from the basepoint.
Gersten
[
Ger94b
]
used this idea to define a quasi-isometry invariant, also called divergence. The divergence of a finitely generated group is then the divergence of one of its Cayley graphs.

In symmetric spaces of noncompact type, divergence is either linear or exponential, and Gromov suggested in
[
Gro93
,
6.\mathrm{B}_{2}
(h), p. 133]

that the same dichotomy should hold for more general nonpositively curved spaces. However for many important families, it is now known that divergence can be linear, quadratic or exponential; these families include
3
-manifold groups
[
Ger94a
]
, mapping class groups and Teichmüller space
[
Beh06
,
DR09
]
, and right-angled Artin groups
[
ABD+13
,
BC12
]
.
The first constructions of families of
\operatorname{CAT}(0)
groups with divergence polynomial of any integer degree are due to Macura
[
Mac13
]
and independently Behrstock–Druţu
[
BD14
]
. Finitely generated groups with divergence neither a polynomial of integer degree nor an exponential are constructed in
[
OOS09
,
GS18
]
. Recently, Brady and Tran
[
BT21
,
BT22
]
constructed finitely presented groups (including subgroups of
\operatorname{CAT}(0)
groups) with divergence
r^{\alpha}
for
\alpha
running through an irrational dense subset of
[2,\infty)
, and with divergence
r^{n}\log(r)
for all integers
n\geq 2
.

The quasi-isometry invariant
thickness
was introduced by Behrstock, Druţu and Mosher
[
BDM09
]
as a means to understand the geometry of non-relatively hyperbolic groups. A metric space
X
is (strongly) thick of order
0
if none of its asymptotic cones has a cut-point, and for integers
n\geq 1
, the space
X
is (strongly) thick of order
n
if, roughly speaking, any two points in
X
can be connected by a chain of subsets, each of which is thick of order
n-1
. Behrstock and Druţu proved in
[
BD14
, Corollary 4.17]
that a group which is strongly thick of order at most
n
has divergence at most
r^{n+1}
.

In the setting of right-angled Coxeter groups, the study of divergence was begun by Dani and Thomas
[
DT15
]
, and continued by Behrstock, Falgas-Ravry, Hagen and Susse
[
BFRHS16
]
, Behrstock, Hagen and Sisto
[
BHS17
]
, and Levcovitz
[
Lev18
,
Lev19
,
Lev22
]
; Levcovitz
[
Lev18
]
also considered divergence for certain non-right-angled Coxeter groups. We recall many results from these works later in this introduction. For arbitrary Coxeter systems
(W,S)
, Caprace
[
Cap09
,
Cap15
]
characterised the collections of special subgroups of
W
such that
W
is hyperbolic relative to such a collection, while Behrstock, Hagen, Sisto and Caprace proved in the Appendix to
[
BHS17
]
that
W
admits a canonical minimal relatively hyperbolic structure, whose peripheral subgroups are in fact special subgroups. In the same Appendix, these four authors also proved that any Coxeter group is either strongly algebraically thick, or is relatively hyperbolic, and provided an inductive construction of all Coxeter systems
(W,S)
such that
W
is strongly algebraically thick.

We are now ready to describe our contributions. Throughout, we assume
(W,S)
is a Coxeter system with
S
finite. We recall that right-angled Coxeter groups are
rigid
, meaning that up to isomorphism they have unique defining graphs
[
Rad03
]
, but in general Coxeter groups are not rigid (see, for instance,
[
BMMN02
]
). Thus we often refer to Coxeter systems rather than Coxeter groups.
In all our results on divergence, we assume
1
-endedness, to ensure the equivalence of Gersten’s definition of divergence and others in the literature (see the discussion in
[
DMS10
, Section 3]
). Given a Coxeter system
(W,S)
, there is a concrete condition for determining whether
W
is
1
-ended
[
Dav08
, Theorem 8.7.2]
, and we provide a corresponding function to check
1
-endedness in our accompanying code
[
Sor22
]
written for the computer algebra system
GAP

[
GAP21
]
.
We refer to Section
2.3
for additional background on Coxeter groups.

Our first results for general Coxeter groups concern linear and quadratic divergence. We prove:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system such that
W
is
1
-ended and
(W,S)
is irreducible and nonaffine. Then the divergence of
W
is at least quadratic.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is brief and combines results from the literature. We observe that
W
has a rank one element, by work of Caprace and Fujiwara
[
CF10
]
, and then apply a general result of Kapovich and Leeb
[
KL98
]
to conclude that the divergence of
W
is at least
r^{2}
. A similar strategy is used in, for instance, the proofs of
[
Hag13
, Theorem 6.10]
and
[
BHS17
, Theorem 3.3]
.

We next characterise linear divergence for arbitrary Coxeter groups.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system such that
W
is
1
-ended. Then
W
has linear divergence if and only if
(W,S)=(W_{1},S_{1})\times(W_{2},S_{2})
where either:

(1)

both
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are infinite; or

(2)

W_{1}
is finite (possibly trivial) and
(W_{2},S_{2})
is irreducible affine of rank
\geq 3
.

This generalises the characterisation of linear divergence for right-angled Coxeter groups (see
[
DT15
, Theorem 4.1]
and
[
BHS17
, Proposition 2.11]
). Note that possibility (2) does not occur in the right-angled case. We prove Corollary
1.2
using Theorem
1.1
and standard facts about Coxeter systems, together with an argument from
[
ABD+13
]
.

The following statement is an immediate consequence of Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.2
.

Corollary 1.3
.

If a
1
-ended Coxeter group has superlinear divergence, then its divergence is at least quadratic.∎

In the right-angled case this result seems to have been known to the authors of
[
BHS17
]
, as indicated by the proof of
[
BHS17
, Theorem 3.3]
, but it is not stated explicitly there.

The main theoretical contribution of this paper is the definition of
hypergraph index
for general Coxeter systems
(W,S)
, which generalises the definition introduced by Levcovitz in the right-angled setting
[
Lev19
]
. Given any
(W,S)
, we construct a
sequence
\Lambda_{0},\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}\dots
of sets of subsets of
S
, where
\Lambda_{0}
contains all
T\subset S
such that the corresponding special subgroup
W_{T}
is (strongly) thick of order
0
, and each
\Lambda_{i}
is the union of certain elements of
\Lambda_{i-1}
.
By definition, the hypergraph index
h(W,S)
is then the smallest
h
for which
S\in\Lambda_{h}
(if it exists), and
\infty
otherwise. It is evident from the definition that
h(W,S)
is either a non-negative integer or equals
\infty
. We provide detailed motivation for our formulation of hypergraph index in Section
4.1
.

A key feature of the construction is that
h(W,S)
can be explicitly computed from the Coxeter presentation
W=\langle S\mid(st)^{m_{st}}\rangle
.
In fact, in our general setting we show that for finite
m_{st}\geq 7
, replacing all relations
(st)^{m_{st}}
by
(st)^{7}
does not change the hypergraph index (see Proposition
4.20
). Thus the determination of possible hypergraph indexes for Coxeter systems with any fixed number of generators is a finite computational problem.

Our results relating hypergraph index, thickness and divergence are as follows.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system such that
W
is
1
-ended. Let
h=h(W,S)
. Then:

(1)

h=0
if and only if
W
has linear divergence.

(2)

If
h=1
, then
W
has quadratic divergence.

(3)

If
h
is finite, then
W
is strongly thick of order at most
h
, hence the divergence of
W
is bounded above by a polynomial of degree
h+1
.

(4)

h=\infty
if and only if
W
is relatively hyperbolic.

Parts (1)–(4) of Theorem
1.4
generalise the following results of
[
Lev19
]
, respectively: the equivalence of (4) and (5) in Theorem 6.1; the implication (5)
\Longrightarrow
(4) in Theorem 6.2; Theorem 6.3 and Corollary 6.4; Remark 3.10. Our proof of the first claim in (3) is considerably shorter and more direct than that of
[
Lev19
, Theorem 6.3]
, and makes natural use of strong algebraic thickness.

We expect that the converse to Theorem
1.4
(2) holds. (For right-angled Coxeter groups, this converse is the implication (4)
\Longrightarrow
(5) of
[
Lev19
, Theorem 6.2]
, and relies upon the characterisation of quadratic divergence for right-angled Coxeter groups from
[
DT15
,
Lev18
]
.) More generally, we make the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.5
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system such that
W
is
1
-ended. Let
h=h(W,S)
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

(W,S)
has finite hypergraph index
h
;

(2)

W
is strongly thick of order
h
; and

(3)

the divergence of
W
is polynomial of degree
h+1
.

In the right-angled case, this is Conjecture 1.1 of
[
Lev19
]
. Levcovitz recently proved this conjecture as Theorem A of
[
Lev22
]
, by showing that if
h
is finite, the Cayley graph of
W
contains a periodic geodesic with divergence polynomial of degree
h+1
.
If Conjecture
1.5
holds, there are many interesting corollaries, as in
[
Lev22
]
.
For instance, obtaining exact bounds on divergence and thickness is notoriously hard, but the equivalences in Conjecture
1.5
provide an algorithmic way to compute these. Furthermore, it follows that the divergence of any
1
-ended Coxeter group is either polynomial of integer degree or exponential. In addition, Conjecture
1.5
implies that hypergraph index is a (computable) quasi-isometry invariant of
1
-ended Coxeter groups, which is not obvious from the definition.

As evidence for Conjecture
1.5
, we prove that for all finite
h
, there are “many” examples of non-right-angled Coxeter systems for which this conjecture holds. For this, we introduce a procedure, called the
duplex construction
, which takes as input a right-angled Coxeter system
(W,S)
, and produces a Coxeter system on twice as many generators whose defining graph has edge labels
2
,
m\in\{2,3,\dots\}
and
n\in\{5,6,\dots\}\cup\{\infty\}
, and which has the same hypergraph index as
(W,S)
. Then by applying the duplex construction to a family of examples from
[
DT15
]
,
we obtain the following statement.

Corollary 1.6
.

For every integer
h\geq 0
, there are infinitely many pairwise nonisomorphic, non-right-angled Coxeter groups, obtained via the duplex construction, whose associated Coxeter systems have hypergraph index
h
. Moreover, if
m
is even and
n=\infty
in the duplex construction, these Coxeter groups are strongly thick of order
h
and have divergence polynomial of degree
h+1
.

In particular, Corollary
1.6
implies that there are families of non-right-angled Coxeter groups with divergence polynomial of any integer degree. An explicit construction of such groups is given in Proposition
5.21
.

The divergence statement in Corollary
1.6
is obtained by combining the upper bound provided by Theorem
1.4
(3) with a lower bound which we establish for these specific examples. Our proof of the lower bound is similar in outline to that of the analogous result in
[
DT15
]
, but the details are considerably more technical. We restrict to
m
even to ensure that each wall in the associated Davis complex has a well-defined type in
S
; the types of walls were used extensively in
[
DT15
,
Lev18
,
Lev19
,
Lev22
]
. In the right-angled case, two walls of the same type never intersect, but if
m\geq 4
is even then distinct walls of the same type can intersect, which introduces greater complexity to the arguments. We note that Levcovitz in
[
Lev18
]
obtained lower bounds on divergence for not necessarily right-angled Coxeter groups such that, among other conditions, some vertex in the defining graph has a triangle-free neighbourhood. However in the defining graphs of duplex groups, every vertex is contained in a triangle, so the lower bounds from
[
Lev18
]
cannot be applied.

We conclude by establishing a surprising upper bound on hypergraph index and hence divergence. Recall that if a (topological) graph
\Delta
has
v
vertices,
e
edges and
k
components, then its
first Betti number
is given by
\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta)=e-v+k
, and this number equals the rank of its first homology group.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system with Dynkin diagram
\Delta=\Delta(W,S)
and hypergraph index
h=h(W,S)
. If
h
is finite then
h\leq\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta)+1
.

Combining this with Theorem
1.4
(3) and (4), we obtain:

Corollary 1.8
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system with Dynkin diagram
\Delta
. If
W
is not relatively hyperbolic, then the divergence of
W
is bounded above by a polynomial of degree
\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta)+2
.

We do not know of any previous connection between large-scale geometry and the topology of Dynkin diagrams.
It follows from Corollary
1.8

that for all integers
n\geq 2
, if
G
is any non-relatively hyperbolic group with divergence
strictly greater than
r^{n}
, then
G
is not quasi-isometric to any Coxeter group whose Dynkin diagram satisfies
\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta)\leq n-2
.

We prove Theorem
1.7
by induction on
\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta)
, and en route obtain the following nesting result:

Corollary 1.9
.

Suppose
(W,S)
has finite hypergraph index
h\geq 1
. Then there exist subsets
S\supset T_{1}\supset T_{2}\supset\dots\supset T_{h}
such that the subsystem
(W_{T_{i}},T_{i})
has hypergraph index
h-i
.∎

If Conjecture
1.5
holds, this implies that any Coxeter group
W
with divergence polynomial of integer degree
n\geq 2
has nested special subgroups with divergence
r^{k}
, for all integers
1\leq k\leq n
.

We do not know whether the upper bound on hypergraph index provided by Theorem
1.7
is sharp, although we provide examples with
\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta)=0,1,2,3,4,5
and hypergraph index equal to
\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta)+1
(see Figure
11
). We found our examples for
\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta)=2,3,4,5
using our
GAP
code to compute hypergraph index

[
Sor22
]
.
For
\Delta
a tree (respectively, a cycle) we also give examples which realise all hypergraph indexes in the spectrum
\{0,1,\infty\}
(respectively,
\{0,1,2,\infty\}
). Hence we obtain the interesting fact that Coxeter systems whose Dynkin diagram is a tree form a family whose divergence takes on the same limited values as in the families of groups for which divergence was first studied:

Corollary 1.10
.

If the Dynkin diagram of
(W,S)
is a tree and
W
is
1
-ended, then
W
has divergence linear, quadratic or exponential only. Moreover, each of these possibilities is realised.

2.
Background

This section gives brief background. We discuss divergence in Section
2.1
, then in Section
2.2
recall relevant material concerning thickness and divergence from
[

. Section

2.1.
Divergence

We now recall Gersten’s definition of divergence from
[
Ger94b
]
.

Let
(X,d)
be a
1
-ended geodesic metric space. For
p\in X
, write
S(p,r)
and
B(p,r)
for the sphere and open ball of radius
r
about
p
, respectively. A path in
X
is
(p,r)
-avoidant
if it lies in

X\setminus B(p,r)
. Given
x,y\in X\setminus B(p,r)
, the
(p,r)
-avoidant distance

d^{\mathrm{av}}_{p,r}(x,y)

between them is the infimum of the lengths of all
(p,r)
-avoidant paths from
x
to
y
.

Now fix a basepoint
e\in X
. For each
0<\rho\leq 1
, let

\delta_{\rho}(r)=\sup_{x,y\in S(e,r)}d^{\mathrm{av}}_{e,\rho r}(x,y).

Then the
divergence
of
X
is defined to be the resulting collection of functions

\mathrm{div}_{X}=\{\delta_{\rho}\mid 0<\rho\leq 1\}.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system (with
S
finite) such that
W
is
1
-ended. Then the Cayley graph of
W
with respect to
S
, denoted
\mathcal{C}(W,S)
, has the geodesic extension property, meaning that any finite geodesic segment can be extended to an infinite geodesic ray (see, for example, Lemmas 4.7.2 and 4.7.3 of
[
Dav08
]
). It is not hard to show that if the metric space
X
has the geodesic extension property, then

\delta_{\rho}\simeq\delta_{1}
for all
0<\rho\leq 1
,
where
\simeq
is the equivalence relation on functions which is defined as follows. For arbitrary functions
f,g\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
we have
f\simeq g
if and only if
f\preceq g
and
g\preceq f
, where the relation
\preceq
is defined as:

f\preceq g\iff\;\exists\;A,B>0,C,D,E\geq 0\text{ such that }f(r)\leq Ag(Br+C)+Dr+E\text{ for all }r\geq 0.

Thus we will think of
\mathrm{div}_{X}
as a function of
r
, defining it to be equal to
\delta_{1}(r)
. Up to the equivalence relation
\simeq
, the divergence
\mathrm{div}_{X}
is a quasi-isometry invariant of the space
X
, which is independent of the chosen basepoint (see
[
Ger94b
, Prop. 2.1]
). Thus for a Coxeter system
(W,S)
as above, we define the divergence of the group
W
to be the divergence of the Cayley graph
\mathcal{C}(W,S)
.

We will also need the notion of the divergence of a geodesic. Let
\gamma\colon\mathbb{R}\to X
be a complete geodesic in a
1
-ended geodesic metric space
X
. We define
\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}(r)
to be the distance between points
\gamma(r)
and
\gamma(-r)
in
X\setminus B(\gamma(0),r)
with respect to the path metric, and we will say that a path between
\gamma(r)
and
\gamma(-r)
is
r
-avoidant
if it lies in
X\setminus B(\gamma(0),r)
.
The
\simeq
equivalence class of
\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}(r)
is called the
divergence of the geodesic
\gamma
, and it serves as a lower bound for the divergence
\mathrm{div}_{X}(r)
of the space
X
.

We will say that the divergence
\mathrm{div}_{X}(r)
or
\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}(r)
is
linear
if it is
\simeq
equivalent to
r
,
quadratic
if it is
\simeq
equivalent to
r^{2}
, and so on. We will also say that the divergence is
superlinear
if it is
\succeq r
and
\not\simeq r
, and that it is
subquadratic
if it is
\preceq r^{2}
and
\not\simeq r^{2}
. Finally, we say that the divergence is
exponential
if it is
\simeq
equivalent to
e^{r}
.

2.2.
Tight networks, thickness and divergence

In our proofs we will make careful use of several definitions and results from the work of Behrstock and Druţu
[
BD14
]
. We recall these ingredients below. Note that, unlike
[
Lev19
,
Lev22
]
, we have chosen to retain the adjective “strong” when referring to (algebraic) thickness.

We first recall some definitions related to networks of subspaces and of subgroups. Given
C,D\geq 0
and a subset
L
of a metric space
X
, write
\mathcal{N}_{C}(L)
for the open
C
-neighbourhood of
L
. We say
L
is
C
-path connected
if any two points in
L
can be connected by a path in
\mathcal{N}_{C}(L)
, and
(C,D)
-quasiconvex
if any two points in
L
can be connected in
\mathcal{N}_{C}(L)
by a
(D,D)
-quasigeodesic; if
C=D
then
L
is
C
-quasiconvex
.

Definition 2.1

(tight network of subspaces, Definition 4.1 of
[
BD14
]
)
.

Let
X
be a metric space,
C,D\geq 0
, and
\mathcal{L}
a collection of subsets of
X
. We say that
X
is a
(C,D)
-tight network with respect to
\mathcal{L}
if every
L\in\mathcal{L}
, with the induced metric, is
(C,D)
-quasiconvex,
X=\cup_{L\in\mathcal{L}}N_{C}(L)
, and for any two
L,L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}
and any
x\in X
such that
B(x,3C)
intersects both
L
and
L^{\prime}
, there is a finite sequence

L=L_{1},L_{2},\dots,L_{n-1},L_{n}=L^{\prime}

of elements of
\mathcal{L}
such that
n\leq D
and for all
1\leq i<n
, the intersection
\mathcal{N}_{C}(L_{i})\cap\mathcal{N}_{C}(L_{i+1})
has infinite diameter, is
D
-path connected, and intersects
B(x,D)
. We say that
X
is a
tight network with respect to
\mathcal{L}
if it is a
(C,D)
-tight network with respect to
\mathcal{L}
for some
C,D\geq 0
.

Definition 2.2

(tight algebraic network of subgroups, Definition 4.2 of
[
BD14
]
)
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group,
M\geq 0
and
\mathcal{H}
a collection of subgroups of
G
. We say that
G
is an
M
-tight algebraic network with respect to
\mathcal{H}
if the elements of
\mathcal{H}
are
M
-quasiconvex subgroups whose union generates a finite-index subgroup of
G
, and for any two
H,H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
there exists a finite sequence
H=H_{1},\dots,H_{n}=H^{\prime}
of subgroups in
\mathcal{H}
such that for all
1\leq i<n
, the intersection
H_{i}\cap H_{i+1}
is infinite and
M
-path connected. We say that
G
is a
tight algebraic network with respect to
\mathcal{H}
if it is an
M
-tight algebraic network with respect to
\mathcal{H}
for some
M\geq 0
.

The following result connects the two previous definitions.

Proposition 2.3

(Proposition 4.3 of
[
BD14
]
)
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group with
\mathcal{H}
a collection of subgroups forming a tight algebraic network inside
G
, and
G_{1}
the finite index subgroup of
G
generated by the elements of
\mathcal{H}
. Then
G
is a tight network with respect to the collection of left cosets

Hyperbolicity and thickness for Coxeter groups

We now recall the characterisations of hyperbolicity, relative hyperbolicity and strong algebraic thickness for Coxeter groups.

For hyperbolicity we have the following theorem of Moussong.

(Moussong, see Corollary 12.6.3 of

]

Let

(2)

(W_{T},T)=(W_{T_{1}},T_{1})\times(W_{T_{2}},T_{2})

3.
Linear and quadratic divergence

In this short section we prove Theorem
1.1
of the introduction, which provides a quadratic lower bound on divergence for irreducible nonaffine Coxeter groups. We also prove Corollary
1.2

of the introduction, which characterises linear divergence for Coxeter groups.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system. Recall that a
rank one geodesic
in a
\operatorname{CAT}(0)
space is a geodesic line which does not bound a flat half-plane. An element of
W
is then said to be
rank one
if it is hyperbolic in its action on the Davis complex
\Sigma=\Sigma(W,S)
and some (hence any) of its axes in
\Sigma
are rank one geodesics. We emphasise that we are considering geodesic lines in
\Sigma
in this definition, not combinatorial geodesics in the Cayley graph
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(W,S)
(since these are not in general geodesic in the
\operatorname{CAT}(0)
metric on
\Sigma
). Recall also that a
Coxeter element
of
W
is a product of the elements of
S
, in any order. A
parabolic subgroup
of
W
is a conjugate of a special subgroup, and the
parabolic closure
of an element
w\in W
is the smallest parabolic subgroup of
W
which contains
w
. The next result follows from work of Caprace–Fujiwara
[
CF10
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
(W,S)
is irreducible, nonspherical and nonaffine, then
any Coxeter element of
W
is a rank one element.

Proof.

By, for example, Corollary 4.3 of
[
CF10
]
, the parabolic closure of a Coxeter element of
W
is equal to
W
. Now by Proposition 4.5 of
[
CF10
]
, for general Coxeter groups
W
, an element
w\in W
is
not
of rank one if and only if
w\in P
, where
P
is parabolic with either (i)
P
finite, or (ii)
P=P_{1}\times P_{2}
with
P_{i}
both infinite parabolic, or (iii)
P=K\times P_{\mbox{\tiny{aff}}}
where
K
is a finite parabolic (possibly trivial) and
P_{\mbox{\tiny{aff}}}
is affine parabolic of rank at least
3
. So if we take
w
to be a Coxeter element of
W
with
(W,S)
infinite, irreducible and nonaffine, then
w
is a rank one element.
∎

We now use the following general result due to Kapovich and Leeb
[
KL98
]
(see also
[
KKL98
]
). We note that since
W
is finitely generated, the Davis complex
\Sigma
is locally compact.

Proposition 3.2

(Proposition 3.3 of
[
KL98
]
or Proposition 4.5 of
[
KKL98
]
)
.

Let
X
be a locally compact
\operatorname{CAT}(0)
space and let
\gamma
be a complete geodesic which is invariant under a cyclic group of hyperbolic isometries. If
\gamma
has subquadratic divergence (in the sense of Remark
3.3
), then it bounds a flat half-plane and hence has linear divergence.∎

Remark 3.3
.

The authors of
[
KL98
,
KKL98
]
use a slightly different notion of the subquadratic divergence of a geodesic than we do. Namely, they say that a geodesic
\gamma
has subquadratic divergence if
\lim_{r\to\infty}\alpha(r)/r^{2}=0
, where
\alpha(r)
is the infimum of lengths of
r
-avoidant paths connecting
\gamma(r)
and
\gamma(-r)
. However, a careful analysis of the proofs of Proposition 3.3 of
[
KL98
]
and Proposition 4.5 of
[
KKL98
]
shows that an even weaker condition on
\gamma
would yield the same conclusion, namely, that

there exists a sequence
r_{k}\to\infty
such that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\alpha(r_{k})/r_{k}^{2}=0
.

The negation of this condition will then imply that there exist real numbers
C,N>0
such that for all
r>N
we have
\alpha(r)/r^{2}>C
. For the purposes of Proposition
3.2
and the proof of Theorem
1.1
we will understand the term ‘subquadratic divergence’ in the above italicised sense.

Now we can prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Suppose the Coxeter system
(W,S)
is irreducible and nonaffine and the group
W
is
1
-ended. Choose a Coxeter element
w\in W
and let
\gamma
be an axis for
w
(recall that by Proposition
3.1
,
w
is a rank one element). Then
\gamma
is invariant under the infinite cyclic group
\langle w\rangle
of hyperbolic isometries. If the geodesic
\gamma
has subquadratic divergence (in the sense of Remark
3.3
) then by Proposition
3.2
,
\gamma
bounds a flat half-plane, which contradicts
w
being rank one. Hence
\gamma
does not have subquadratic divergence, and by Remark
3.3
we conclude that
\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}(r)\succeq r^{2}
.
This proves that the divergence of the Davis complex
\Sigma
is at least quadratic as well. Since
W
is quasi-isometric to
\Sigma
(see
[
Kra09
, Proposition B.5.2]
), and since the divergence function is invariant under quasi-isometries
[
Ger94b
, Proposition 2.1]
, we conclude that
W
also has at least quadratic divergence, as required.
∎

The following result establishes Corollary
1.2

of the introduction.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system such that
W
is
1
-ended. Then
W
has linear divergence if and only if

(W,S)=(W_{1},S_{1})\times(W_{2},S_{2})
where either:

(1)

both
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are infinite; or

(2)

W_{1}
is finite (possibly trivial) and
(W_{2},S_{2})
is irreducible affine of rank
\geq 3
.

Proof.

We first note that
W
itself must be infinite since finite groups are
0
-ended.
Since the divergence of
W
is invariant under passing to a subgroup of finite index, for any decomposition
W=W_{1}\times W_{2}
with
W_{1}
finite, the divergence of
W
and of
W_{2}
will be the same. Thus without loss of generality we may assume that either:

(1)

(W,S)=(W_{1},S_{1})\times(W_{2},S_{2})
with both
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
infinite; or

(2)

W
is irreducible.

In the first case, write
\ell_{i}
for the word length on
W_{i}
with respect to
S_{i}
, for
i=1,2
. Then since
W_{i}
is infinite, for all
w_{i}\in W_{i}
there is at least one
s\in S_{i}
such that
\ell_{i}(w_{i}s)>\ell_{i}(w_{i})
(this follows from Lemmas 4.7.2 and 4.7.3 of
[
Dav08
]
). Hence any
w_{i}\in W_{i}
lies on an infinite geodesic ray based at the identity vertex in the Cayley graph of
W_{i}
with respect to
S_{i}
. We can then apply the proof of Lemma 7.2 of
[
ABD+13
]
to conclude that
W=W_{1}\times W_{2}
has linear divergence.

In the second case, i.e. if
W
is irreducible,
W
may be either affine of type
\widetilde{A}_{1}
, or affine of rank
\geq 3
, or nonaffine. If
W
is of type
\widetilde{A}_{1}
then
W
is
2
-ended and must be discarded. If
W
is affine of rank
n+1\geq 3
then
W
is virtually
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with
n\geq 2
, and hence
W
has linear divergence.

In the remaining case when
W
is irreducible, nonspherical and nonaffine, Theorem
1.1
implies that the divergence of
W
is at least quadratic.
∎

4.
Hypergraph index

In Section
4.1
we provide motivation and an outline of our construction with a few technicalities omitted.
In Section
4.2
we define hypergraph index for general Coxeter systems and prove some easy properties. Our main results for hypergraph index are contained in Section
4.3
, where we prove Theorem
1.4
.
Throughout this section,
(W,S)
is a Coxeter system with
S

4.1.
Motivation and an outline of the construction

To motivate our definition of hypergraph index, we first recall that, as was proven in
[
BHS17
, Appendix]
, every Coxeter group is either hyperbolic relative to a system of proper special peripheral subgroups (and hence has exponential divergence), or it is thick (and hence has divergence bounded above by a polynomial of integer degree). Moreover, the inductive procedure of Definition
2.12
allows one to either build a minimal system of such peripheral subgroups, or to establish that the entire Coxeter group is thick. An upper bound on the order of thickness (respectively, degree of divergence) can then be calculated, using results of
[
BDM09
]
, as the total number of operations (2) and (3) from Definition
2.12
which have been applied (respectively, this number plus one).

However not all operations (2) and (3) actually increase the order of thickness (respectively, degree of divergence), and it is desirable to group several of them together to achieve a better upper bound. In Section
4.2

we describe a procedure which does exactly that: compacts multiple applications of operations (2) and (3) into fewer steps if they do not increase the order of thickness (respectively, degree of divergence). The total number of steps until stabilisation is our invariant,
the hypergraph index
, which we show is a nonnegative integer if the Coxeter group is thick, and equals infinity if the Coxeter group is relatively hyperbolic.
The construction
was inspired by the work of Levcovitz
[
Lev19
]
who introduced it for the case of right-angled Coxeter groups.

In more detail, the motivation for hypergraph index (as well as for the rules (RH1)–(RH3) of Caprace’s Theorem
2.11
and the operations (1)–(3) of Definition
2.12
) comes from the following facts about relatively hyperbolic groups:

(i)

If a relatively hyperbolic group has a subgroup isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
, then it should be contained in a peripheral subgroup (otherwise,
the coned-off Cayley graph
will not be
fine
, in the terminology of
[
GM08
, Definition 2.43]
).

(ii)

In a relatively hyperbolic group, the intersection of any two peripheral subgroups or their conjugates should be finite (see e.g.
[
DS08
, Lemma 4.20]
).

The goal is, for any Coxeter system
(W,S)
, to build a minimal system of peripheral special subgroups of
W
which satisfies conditions (i) and (ii) above. We start with the smallest collection of subsets of
S
such that the corresponding special subgroups contain
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
in an obvious way. These are the maximal subsets of the form
A\times B\subseteq S
with
A
and
B
both nonspherical (which makes
W_{A}
and
W_{B}
both infinite), or
A
irreducible affine of cardinality
\geq 3
and
B
spherical. We denote this family
\Omega(S)
and call it the family of
wide subsets
.

After that we need to ensure that condition (ii) is satisfied. We achieve this in an inductive process, by modifying the current family of subsets of
S
corresponding to prospective peripheral subgroups. Since the intersection of special subgroups is special, it is easy to see when two members of the current family of subsets of
S
have nonspherical intersection; in this case, the corresponding special subgroups must be absorbed into a bigger peripheral subgroup.
However it is not always straightforward to decide when, for two subsets of
S
, their corresponding special subgroups have
conjugates
with infinite intersection. Caprace’s rule (RH3) from Theorem
2.11
is the key to detecting such situations. Namely, if a subset
T\subset S
contains a nonspherical subset
U
whose elements commute with some
s\in S\setminus T
, then the subgroups
W_{T}
and
sW_{T}s^{-1}
contain the infinite special subgroup
W_{U}=sW_{U}s^{-1}
in their intersection.
So there are subsets of the form
U\times K\subset S
with
U
minimal nonspherical and
K
maximal spherical commuting with
U
such that
W_{U}\times W_{K}
is not allowed to have nonspherical intersection with any of the peripheral subgroups.
We denote the family of all such
U\times K
by
\Psi(S)
and call its elements the
slab subsets
.

These considerations allow us to keep track of a prospective family of peripheral special subgroups, as follows.
We start with
\Lambda_{0}=\Omega(S)\cup\Psi(S)
and construct a sequence
\Lambda_{0}
,
\Lambda_{1}
,
\Lambda_{2}
, … of sets of subsets of
S
, where each
\Lambda_{i}
consists of unions of those members of
\Lambda_{i-1}
which are forced to be united due to violation of condition (ii), as described above. We achieve this by introducing an equivalence relation on each of the
\Lambda_{i}
generated by the condition that two subsets have nonspherical intersection.

If, for the resulting sequence of subsets
\Lambda_{i}
, there is an index
h
such that
\Lambda_{h}
contains
S
, then the Coxeter system
(W,S)
is thick and the minimal such number
h
is the hypergraph index. We prove in Theorem
1.4
that
h
is an upper bound on the order of (strong) thickness, and hence obtain an upper bound on the degree of divergence.
However, if the sets
\Lambda_{i}
stabilise without reaching
S
, then it follows from Caprace’s Theorem
2.11
that the non-slab elements of
\Lambda_{i}

form a family of minimal peripheral special subgroups for a relatively hyperbolic structure on
(W,S)
, and
W
then has exponential divergence.

We remark that each set
\Lambda_{i}
in the construction can be thought of as a hypergraph with vertex set
S
and hyperedges corresponding to the subsets in
\Lambda_{i}
, although we do not use this terminology. This motivates the name hypergraph index, which was introduced by Levcovitz
[
Lev19
]
.

4.2.
Definitions and first properties

In this section we recall Levcovitz’s definition of hypergraph index for right-angled Coxeter groups
[
Lev19
, Section 3]
, in parallel with formulating our generalisation of hypergraph index to arbitrary Coxeter systems (see Definition
4.10
). We also establish some basic properties of hypergraph index.

First, we define a set of building blocks, called
wide subsystems
and
slab subsystems
, out of which all thick subsystems of
(W,S)
will be constructed.
These will respectively generalise the following notions, which were introduced in the right-angled case by Levcovitz
[
Lev19
, Definition 3.1]
. Let
\Gamma
be the defining graph of a right-angled Coxeter group. The
wide subgraphs

\Omega=\Omega(\Gamma)
are the maximal induced subgraphs of
\Gamma
such that every element of
\Omega
is the join of two induced subgraphs, each of which contains a pair of nonadjacent vertices (equivalently, the vertex sets of these two subgraphs commute and are both nonspherical). The
strip subgraphs

\Psi=\Psi(\Gamma)
are the maximal induced subgraphs of
\Gamma
such that every element of
\Psi
is the join of a pair of nonadjacent vertices with a nonempty clique (equivalently, the vertex sets of strip subgraphs generate
2
-ended special subgroups of the form
W_{A}\times C_{2}^{|K|}
, where
W_{A}\cong D_{\infty}
and
K
is the vertex set of a maximal nonempty clique).

We frame our generalisation of these notions in terms of subsets of
S
rather than induced subgraphs of the defining graph
\Gamma
.
Let
\mathcal{P}(S)
be the power set of
S
.

Definition 4.1
.

Consider the collection of subsets of
S
of the form
A\times B
such that either:

(1)

A
and
B
are both nonspherical; or

(2)

A
is irreducible affine of cardinality
\geq 3
and
B
is spherical (possibly empty).

The
wide subsets

\Omega(S)\subseteq\mathcal{P}(S)
are the maximal elements of this collection, with respect to the partial order induced by inclusion.
A
wide subsystem
is a pair
(W_{T},T)
where
T\in\Omega(S)
.

Remark 4.2
.

Since there are no right-angled irreducible affine Coxeter systems of rank
\geq 3
, when

is right-angled the elements of

from Definition

are exactly the vertex sets of the wide subgraphs from
[

The following observation is immediate from Theorem

Lemma 4.3

Let

be a Coxeter system. Then

Part (1) of the next result generalises

Let

(1)

(2)

The group

It follows from Remark

Lemma 4.13
.

A slab subset cannot contain a wide subset.

Proof.

Let
A\times K\in\Psi(S)
, where
A
is minimal nonspherical and
K
is spherical and
nonempty. Note that
A\times K
is not itself a wide subset, and so in particular
(W_{A},A)
is not irreducible affine of rank
\geq 3
.
Now suppose
B\times C\subseteq A\times K
for some
B\times C\in\Omega(S)
,
where (without loss of generality)
B
is nonspherical. Since
A

is minimal nonspherical,
A\cap B
is either spherical or equal to
A
. If
A\cap B
is spherical then, since
K\cap B
is spherical, we have that
B=(A\cap B)\cup(K\cap B)
is spherical, a contradiction.
So
A\cap B=A
, that is,
A\subseteq B
. If
(W_{B},B)
is irreducible affine of rank
\geq 3
, then
B
is minimal nonspherical, so
A=B
,
which contradicts
(W_{A},A)
being not irreducible affine of rank
\geq 3
. Thus
B
and
C
are both nonspherical, and since
A\subseteq B
, it follows that
C\subseteq K
, which contradicts
K
being spherical.
∎

The following lemma restricts the form of the sequences
T_{0},T_{1},\dots,T_{n}
which can appear in Definition
4.9
, and is related to
[
Lev19
, Remark 3.4]
.

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system. Let
T
and
T^{\prime}
be elements
of the form
T=A\times K
and
T^{\prime}=A^{\prime}\times K^{\prime}
, where
A
and
A^{\prime}
are minimal nonspherical (possibly irreducible affine of rank
\geq 3
, as in Definition
4.1
), and
K
and
K^{\prime}
are spherical. If
T\cap T^{\prime}
is nonspherical then
T=T^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Since
T=A\sqcup K
and
T^{\prime}=A^{\prime}\sqcup K^{\prime}
, we have

T\cap T^{\prime}=(A\cap A^{\prime})\sqcup(A\cap K^{\prime})\sqcup(K\cap A^{\prime})\sqcup(K\cap K^{\prime}).

Now
K
and
K^{\prime}
are spherical, so if
T\cap T^{\prime}
is nonspherical then
A\cap A^{\prime}
must be nonspherical. But since
A
and
A^{\prime}
are both minimal nonspherical, this implies
A\cap A^{\prime}=A=A^{\prime}
.
Now
A=A^{\prime}
commutes with
K\cup K^{\prime}
.
If
K\cup K^{\prime}
is nonspherical, then
A\times K\subsetneq A\times(K\cup K^{\prime})\subseteq T
for some
T\in\Omega(S)
. This violates the maximality in Definition
4.1
if

A\times K
is wide, and violates condition (3) in Definition
4.5
if it is slab.
Thus
K\cup K^{\prime}
is spherical, and
A\times(K\cup K^{\prime})
satisfies conditions (1)–(3) in Definition
4.5
(since
A\times K
does). By maximality of
A\times K
, we have

A\times K=A\times(K\cup K^{\prime})
, hence
K^{\prime}\subseteq K
. Similarly,
K\subseteq K^{\prime}
. It follows that
K=K^{\prime}
and
T=T^{\prime}
as required.
∎

We will need the following corollary in Section
5.1
.

Corollary 4.15
.

Assume that
\Omega(S)\neq\varnothing
.
For all
i\geq 0
, let
X\in\Lambda_{i+1}(S)
and let
\mathcal{C}
be an
\equiv_{i}
-equivalence class whose union is
X
. Then
X\in\Lambda_{i+1}(S)\setminus\Psi(S)
if and only if
\mathcal{C}
contains an element of
\Lambda_{i}(S)\setminus\Psi(S)
.

Proof.

We first prove that for each
i\geq 0
, if
X\in\Lambda_{i+1}(S)\setminus\Psi(S)
, then
\mathcal{C}
contains an element of
\Lambda_{i}(S)\setminus\Psi(S)
. If not, then
\mathcal{C}
consists only of elements from
\Psi(S)
. Now Lemma
4.14
implies that
\mathcal{C}
has a unique element, which is then necessarily
X
. Thus
X\in\Psi(S)
, a contradiction.

To establish the other direction, we first observe that an easy inductive proof using the statement proved in the previous paragraph and the fact that
\Omega(S)
is nonempty shows that for all
j\geq 0
, every element of
\Lambda_{j}(S)\setminus\Psi(S)
contains a wide subset.
Thus if
\mathcal{C}
contains an element
T
of
\Lambda_{i}(S)\setminus\Psi(S)
, then
X
contains a wide subset (since
T
does) and therefore cannot be in
\Psi(S)
by Lemma
4.13
.
∎

It will be sometimes be convenient to think of the elements of

, where

, as the vertices of a directed forest, as follows. For

, there is a directed edge from

to

if

is one of the elements in an

-equivalence class whose union is

.
Then for

we say that

feeds into

if there is a directed path (i.e., a path in which the edges are coherently oriented) from

to

.
We caution that even though

may hold set-theoretically for some

and

, it may not be true that

feeds into

(see for example
[

, Remark 3.3.1]
).

We now record for later use some monotonicity results.

Lemma 4.16
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system and let
T\subset T^{\prime}
be subsets of
S
. Let
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

(1)

If
T\in\Lambda_{i}(S)
then
T
feeds into some element of
\Lambda_{i+1}(S)
.

(2)

Given
Y\in\Lambda_{i}(T)
, there exists an element
Y^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{i}(T^{\prime})
such that the following conditions hold:

(a)

Y^{\prime}
contains
Y
; and

(b)

given
X\in\Lambda_{j}(T)
, with
j<i
, such that
X
feeds into
Y
, every
X^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{j}(T^{\prime})
containing
X
feeds into
Y^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The proof of (1) follows from the definition of the sets
\Lambda_{i}(S)
.

For (2), the proof is by induction on
i
. In the base case
i=0
, (a) follows easily from the definition of
\Lambda_{0}
, and (b) is vacuously true. Assume the conclusion is true for
i-1
and let
Y\in\Lambda_{i}(T)
. Let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
be the elements of an
\equiv_{i-1}
-equivalence class in
\Lambda_{i-1}(T)
whose union is
Y
(so in particular, each
Y_{j}
feeds into
Y
). Consider the set
\mathcal{Y}\subset\Lambda_{i-1}(T^{\prime})
defined by

\mathcal{Y}=\{Z\mid Z\in\Lambda_{i-1}(T^{\prime}),\;Y_{j}\subseteq Z\text{ for some }1\leq j\leq n\}.

Note that
\mathcal{Y}
is nonempty by the induction hypothesis.

We claim that the elements of
\mathcal{Y}
are pairwise equivalent in
\Lambda_{i-1}(T^{\prime})
. To see this, let
Z_{1},Z_{2}\in\mathcal{Y}
, and suppose
Y_{j}\subseteq Z_{1}
and
Y_{k}\subseteq Z_{2}
. If
j=k
, then since
Y_{j}
is nonspherical,
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
are equivalent. Otherwise, since
Y_{j}
and
Y_{k}
are equivalent in
\Lambda_{i-1}(T)
, there is a sequence

in
\Lambda_{i-1}(T)
such that

\{X_{1},\dots,X_{l}\}\subset\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\}
and

is nonspherical for

. By the induction hypothesis, there is a sequence
X_{1}^{\prime},\dots,X_{l}^{\prime}
in
\Lambda_{i-1}(T^{\prime})
such that

for
1\leq s\leq l
.
Now for
1\leq s<l
, the intersection
X^{\prime}_{s}\cap X^{\prime}_{s+1}
contains
X_{s}\cap X_{s+1}
, and so

X_{s}^{\prime}
is equivalent to
X_{s+1}^{\prime}
in
\Lambda_{i-1}(T^{\prime})
. Furthermore,
Z_{1}
is equivalent to
X_{1}^{\prime}
and

Z_{2}
is equivalent to
X_{l}^{\prime}
. Thus
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
are equivalent in
\Lambda_{i-1}(T^{\prime})
.

We have shown that
\mathcal{Y}
is a subset of an equivalence class of
\Lambda_{i-1}(T^{\prime})
. We now define

to be the union of all the sets in this equivalence class.
Then

and

by construction, i.e. (a) holds.
To prove (b), let

, with
j<i
, be an element which feeds into
Y
, and let
X^{\prime}
be any element of
\Lambda_{j}(T^{\prime})
containing
X
.
If
j=i-1
, the claim is true by construction. If
j<i-1
, then
X
feeds into
Y_{k}
for some

. By the induction hypothesis,
X^{\prime}
feeds into an element
Y_{k}^{\prime}
containing
Y_{k}
, and by construction,
Y_{k}^{\prime}
feeds into
Y^{\prime}
. Thus
X^{\prime}
feeds into
Y^{\prime}
.
∎

Next, we make some first observations relating to hypergraph index itself (Definition
4.10
). The following two results will allow us to focus on infinite irreducible Coxeter systems when considering hypergraph index below.

Lemma 4.17
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system. If
W
is finite then
h(W,S)=\infty
.

Proof.

In this case
\Omega(S)=\varnothing
and so
h(W,S)=\infty
.
∎

Lemma 4.18
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system.

(1)

If
(W,S)=(W_{1},S_{1})\times(W_{2},S_{2})
with
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
both infinite, then
h(W,S)=0
.

(2)

If
(W,S)=(W_{1},S_{1})\times(W_{2},S_{2})
with
(W_{1},S_{1})
irreducible and infinite and
W_{2}
finite, then
h(W,S)=h(W_{1},S_{1})
.

Remark 4.19
.

The proof of Lemma
4.18
(2) is more technical than might be expected because, although every element
A\times K
of
\Psi(S_{1})
yields an element
A\times(K\cup S_{2})
of
\Psi(S)
, we do not see how to rule out
\Psi(S)
also containing elements which are not of this form. However if Conjecture
1.5
of the introduction holds, then hypergraph index is a quasi-isometry invariant, and (2) is immediate.

Proof.

For (1), the set
S=S_{1}\times S_{2}
is in
\Omega(S)
, so
h(W,S)=0
.

We now prove (2). Consider the function
\theta\colon\mathcal{P}(S_{1})\to\mathcal{P}(S)
defined by
\theta(Y)=Y\cup S_{2}
. Since images and preimages of nonspherical sets under
\theta
are nonspherical,
it is easy to see from the definitions that
\theta
induces a map
\Lambda_{0}(S_{1})\to\Lambda_{0}(S)
which takes

\Omega(S_{1})
bijectively to
\Omega(S)
and takes

\Psi(S_{1})
into
\Psi(S)
. Let
\Sigma
denote the (possibly empty) set of
elements of
\Psi(S)
which are not in the image of
\theta
. Then every element of
\Sigma
is of the form

A\times S_{2}
where
A
is minimal nonspherical in
S_{1}
.

The previous paragraph serves as the base step for an inductive proof for the following claim:

for all
i\geq 0
, the map
\theta
induces a map
\Lambda_{i}(S_{1})\to\Lambda_{i}(S)
whose image contains
\Lambda_{i}(S)\setminus\Sigma
.

For the inductive step, assume the statement holds for
k
, and let
X\in\Lambda_{k+1}(S_{1})
. We wish to show that
\theta(X)=X\cup S_{2}
is an element of
\Lambda_{k+1}(S)
.

Let
T\in\Lambda_{k}(S_{1})
be an element feeding into
X
, then, by the induction hypothesis,
\theta(T)
belongs to
\Lambda_{k}(S)\setminus\Sigma
. Let
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}
be the
\equiv_{k}
-equivalence classes containing
T
and
\theta(T)
in
\Lambda_{k}(S_{1})
and
\Lambda_{k}(S)
, respectively, and let
\theta(\mathcal{C}_{1})=\{\theta(Y)\mid Y\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\}
.

Observe that for any
A,B\in\Lambda_{k}(S_{1})
, we have that
A\cap B
is nonspherical if and only if
\theta(A)\cap\theta(B)
is. It easily follows that if
T_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
, then
\theta(T)\equiv_{k}\theta(T_{1})
in

\Lambda_{k}(S)
, and so
\theta(\mathcal{C}_{1})\subset\mathcal{C}
.
We are going to show that every element of
\mathcal{C}
is a subset of an element of
\theta(\mathcal{C}_{1})
.

Let
T^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
. Then

\theta(T)\equiv_{k}T^{\prime\prime}
in
\Lambda_{k}(S)
, and there is a
sequence
\theta(T)=T_{1}^{\prime}
,
T_{2}^{\prime}
, …,
T_{m}^{\prime}=T^{\prime\prime}
of sets in
\Lambda_{k}(S)
, with
T^{\prime}_{i}\cap T^{\prime}_{i+1}
nonspherical for
1\leq i<m
. Let
j
be the smallest index (if any) such that
T_{j}^{\prime}
is not in the image of
\theta
, and note that
j>1
. Then by the induction hypothesis,
T_{j}^{\prime}\in\Sigma
and so
T_{j}^{\prime}=A\times S_{2}
for some minimal nonspherical
A
. Now since
T_{j-1}^{\prime}\cap(A\times S_{2})
is nonspherical, it follows that this intersection contains
A
, and since
T_{j-1}^{\prime}
is in the image of
\theta
, this intersection also contains
S_{2}
. Thus
T_{j}^{\prime}
is a subset of
T_{j-1}^{\prime}
. Consequently, if
j<m
, then

T_{j-1}^{\prime}\cap T_{j+1}^{\prime}
is nonspherical, and so
T_{j}^{\prime}
can be removed from the sequence.
Thus we may assume that
T_{i}^{\prime}
is in the image of
\theta
for all
i<m
.

Now if
T^{\prime\prime}=T_{m}^{\prime}
is also in the image, i.e.
T_{m}^{\prime}=\theta(T_{m})
for some
T_{m}\in\Lambda_{k}(S)
, then the preimage of the sequence
\theta(T)=T_{1}^{\prime}
,
T_{2}^{\prime}
, …,
T_{m}^{\prime}=T^{\prime\prime}

establishes that

T\equiv_{k}T_{m}
, i.e.
T_{m}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
. On the other hand, if
T^{\prime\prime}\in\Sigma
, we know (from the previous paragraph) that it is already contained in an element of
\theta(\mathcal{C}_{1})
, namely
T_{m-1}^{\prime}
.

Thus, if
X^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{k+1}(S)
denotes the union of the sets in
\mathcal{C}
, we have, as required:

X^{\prime}=\bigcup_{Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}Y^{\prime}=\bigcup_{Y^{\prime}\in\theta(\mathcal{C}_{1})}Y^{\prime}=\bigcup_{Y\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}\theta(Y)=\bigcup_{Y\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}Y\cup S_{2}=\theta(X).

Next we show that every
X^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{k+1}(S)\setminus\Sigma
is in the image of
\theta
. If
\mathcal{C}
denotes an equivalence class of sets in
\Lambda_{k}(S)
feeding into
X^{\prime}
, then
\mathcal{C}
contains an element
T^{\prime}
of
\Lambda_{k}(S)\setminus\Sigma
, for otherwise, Lemma
4.14
would imply that
\mathcal{C}
consists of a single element of
\Sigma
, forcing
X^{\prime}
to be in
\Sigma
. By the induction hypothesis,
T^{\prime}=\theta(T)
for some
T\in\Lambda_{k}(S_{1})
, and if
T
feeds into
X\in\Lambda_{k+1}(S)
, it follows from the above discussion that

\theta(X)=X^{\prime}
. This completes the induction.

Note that
\Omega(S_{1})
is empty if and only if
\Omega(S)
is empty, and in this case
h(W_{1},S_{1})=h(W,S)=\infty
. So we may assume
\Omega(S)
is nonempty.
Now if
S_{1}\in\Lambda_{i}(S_{1})
for some
i
, then
S=\theta(S_{1})\in\Lambda_{i}(S)
by the claim we proved by induction above. On the other hand, if
S\in\Lambda_{i}(S)
, then since
\Omega(S)
is nonempty, Lemma
4.13
implies that
S
is not in
\Psi(S)
, and so not in
\Sigma
. Then
S
is in the image of
\theta\colon\Lambda_{i}(S_{1})\to\Lambda_{i}(S)
, and it follows that
\theta^{-1}(S)=S_{1}\in\Lambda_{i}(S_{1})
. From this we conclude that

.
∎

We next prove that for the computation of hypergraph index, finite edge labels in the set
\{7,8,\dots\}
are indistinguishable.

Proposition 4.20
.

Let
I=\{1,\dots,n\}
and let
(W,S)
and
(W^{\prime},S^{\prime})
be Coxeter systems with
S=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}
and
S^{\prime}=\{s_{1}^{\prime},\dots,s_{n}^{\prime}\}
, so that

W=\langle s_{i}\mid(s_{i}s_{j})^{m_{ij}}=1\rangle\quad\mbox{and}\quad W^{\prime}=\langle s^{\prime}_{i}\mid(s^{\prime}_{i}s^{\prime}_{j})^{m^{\prime}_{ij}}=1\rangle,\quad i,j\in I.

Suppose that the matrices
(m_{ij})
and
(m^{\prime}_{ij})
differ only in one pair of positions corresponding to indices
(p,q)
and
(q,p)
, with both
m_{pq}
and
m^{\prime}_{pq}
finite numbers
\geq 7
.
Let
\iota\colon S\to S^{\prime}
,
s_{i}\mapsto s_{i}^{\prime}
be the natural bijection.
Then
\iota
induces bijections
\Omega(S)\to\Omega(S^{\prime})
,
\Psi(S)\to\Psi(S^{\prime})
, and
\Lambda_{k}(S)\to\Lambda_{k}(S^{\prime})
for all
k\geq 0
. In particular, the hypergraph indexes of
(W,S)
and
(W^{\prime},S^{\prime})
are equal.

Proof.

We notice that in the construction of the sets
\Omega(S)
,
\Psi(S)
and
\Lambda_{k}(S)
from Definitions
4.1
,
4.5
and
4.9
, respectively, we consider subsystems of the following classes:

(1)

spherical;

(2)

nonspherical;

(3)

irreducible affine of rank
\geq 3
;

(4)

minimal nonspherical:

(a)

irreducible affine of rank
\geq 2
; and

(b)

Lannér hyperbolic (see Figure
3
).

By analysing the Dynkin diagrams for irreducible affine systems and for Lannér hyperbolic ones, we observe that none of them has finite edge labels
\geq 7
. Also, among Dynkin diagrams for spherical Coxeter systems, finite edge labels
\geq 7
appear only in the rank
2
systems corresponding to finite dihedral groups.
It follows that for an arbitrary subset
T\subseteq S
,
T
is of any type listed in (1)–(4) above if and only if
\iota(T)
is of that same type. Thus
\iota
induces a bijection between the sets
\Omega(S)
and
\Omega(S^{\prime})
and also between the sets
\Psi(S)
and
\Psi(S^{\prime})
.

Now observe that, by similar arguments, the sets
T_{j}
appearing in Definition
4.9
have the property:
T_{j}\cap T_{j+1}
is nonspherical if and only if
\iota(T_{j})\cap\iota(T_{j+1})
is nonspherical. Hence by induction,
\iota
induces a bijection between
\Lambda_{k}(S)
and
\Lambda_{k}(S^{\prime})
for all
k\geq 0
.
∎

Remark 4.21
.

Proposition
4.20
tells us that for all purposes related to hypergraph index, we can work with a set of just seven edge labels, namely
m_{ij}\in\{2,3,4,5,6,7,\infty\}
. This makes the problem of exploration of possible hypergraph indexes (and possible configurations of the sets
\Lambda_{i}(S)
) on a fixed generating set

a finite computational problem.

We conclude this section by considering the relationship with tight (algebraic) networks (see Definitions
2.1
and
2.2
). The next two results do not directly generalise any results from
[
Lev19
]
, although they follow easily from the definitions.

Lemma 4.22
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system. For
i\geq 0
, let
U
be an element of
\Lambda_{i+1}(S)
, with
U
the union of the elements
T\in C_{U}
for
C_{U}
an
\equiv_{i}
-equivalence class in
\Lambda_{i}(S)
. Then
W_{U}
is a tight algebraic network with respect to the collection of special subgroups

\mathcal{H}_{U}=\{W_{T}\mid T\in C_{U}\}.

Proof.

Special subgroups of Coxeter groups are convex, so as each
T\in C_{U}
is a subset of
U
, each
W_{T}\in\mathcal{H}_{U}
is convex in
W_{U}
. As
U
is the union of the elements
T\in C_{U}
, the union of the subgroups in
\mathcal{H}_{U}
generates
W_{U}
itself. If
T,T^{\prime}\in C_{U}
then by definition of
\equiv_{i}
-equivalence, there are subsets
T=T_{0},T_{1},\dots,T_{n}=T^{\prime}
in
C_{U}
such that
W_{T_{j}}\cap W_{T_{j+1}}=W_{T_{j}\cap T_{j+1}}
is infinite for
0\leq j<n
. Since each
W_{T_{j}\cap T_{j+1}}
is convex in
W_{U}
, it is path-connected.
∎

Corollary 4.23
.

With all notation as in the statement of Lemma
4.22
,
W_{U}
is a tight network with respect to the collection of left cosets

\mathcal{L}_{U}=\{wW_{T}\mid w\in W_{U},T\in C_{U}\}.

Proof.

This is immediate from Lemma
4.22
and Proposition
2.3
.
∎

4.3.
Hypergraph index, thickness and divergence

In this section we establish our main results relating hypergraph index to thickness and divergence, which are stated as Theorem
1.4
of the introduction.

As mentioned in the introduction, we will use strong algebraic thickness (see Definition
2.5
). We will need the following result.

Proposition 4.24
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system. If
W
is strongly algebraically thick then
(W,S)
has finite hypergraph index.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.13
, we have
(W,S)\in\mathbb{T}
. Thus by the inductive definition of
\mathbb{T}
(see Definition
2.12
) it suffices to prove the following three claims:

(1)

If
T\subseteq S
and
(W_{T},T)\in\mathbb{T}_{0}
then
(W_{T},T)
has finite hypergraph index.

(2)

If
S_{0}\subseteq S
and
s\in S\setminus S_{0}
are such that
(W_{S_{0}},S_{0})\in\mathbb{T}
,
s^{\perp}\cap S_{0}
is nonspherical and
(W_{S_{0}},S_{0})
has finite hypergraph index
h
, then
(W_{S_{0}\cup\{s\}},S_{0}\cup\{s\})
has finite hypergraph index.

(3)

If
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq S
are such that
(W_{S_{i}},S_{i})\in\mathbb{T}
for
i=1,2
and
S_{1}\cap S_{2}
is nonspherical, and
(W_{S_{i}},S_{i})
has finite hypergraph index
h_{i}
for
i=1,2
, then
(W_{S_{1}\cup S_{2}},S_{1}\cup S_{2})
has finite hypergraph index.

For (1), by the definitions we have
T\in\Omega(T)
, and so
T\in\Lambda_{0}(T)=\Lambda_{0}(T)\setminus\Lambda_{-1}(T)
, and
(W_{T},T)
has hypergraph index
0
.

For (2), observe first that
\Omega(S_{0})\neq\varnothing
by the definition of finite hypergraph index, hence
\Omega(S_{0}\cup\{s\})\neq\varnothing
.
Furthermore,
S_{0}\in\Lambda_{h}(S_{0})
, so by Lemma
4.16
(2a), there exists
T\in\Lambda_{h}(S_{0}\cup\{s\})
such that
S_{0}\subseteq T
.
Let
A
be a minimal nonspherical subset of
s^{\perp}\cap S_{0}
. Then
A\times\{s\}
is contained in some wide or slab subset of

S_{0}\cup\{s\}
, which feeds into some set
T^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{h}(S_{0}\cup\{s\})
, by Lemma
4.16
(1).
Now
T\cap T^{\prime}
contains
A
, so is nonspherical. Thus
T\cup T^{\prime}
is a subset of an element of
\Lambda_{h+1}(S_{0}\cup\{s\})
.
Since
S_{0}\cup\{s\}\subseteq T\cup T^{\prime}
, we conclude that
S_{0}\cup\{s\}
has hypergraph index
\leq h+1
.

For (3), we have that
\Omega(S_{i})\neq\varnothing
for
i=1,2
, hence
\Omega(S_{1}\cup S_{2})\neq\varnothing
. Now let
h=\max\{h_{1},h_{2}\}
. Then since by assumption
S_{i}\in\Lambda_{h_{i}}(S_{i})
, by Lemma
4.16
(2a)
S_{i}
is contained in an element of
\Lambda_{h}(S_{1}\cup S_{2})
, say
T_{i}
. Now
T_{1}\cap T_{2}
is nonspherical, since it contains
S_{1}\cap S_{2}
. So
T_{1}\cup T_{2}
is a subset of an element of
\Lambda_{h+1}(S_{1}\cup S_{2})
. Then since
(S_{1}\cup S_{2})\subseteq(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
, we get
S_{1}\cup S_{2}\in\Lambda_{h+1}(S_{1}\cup S_{2})
. This completes the proof.
∎

We can now prove one implication of Theorem
1.4
(4), without requiring
W
to be
1
-ended (since we have not yet invoked the divergence of
W
).

Corollary 4.25
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system. If
(W,S)
has infinite hypergraph index, then
W
is relatively hyperbolic.

Proof.

This is immediate from the contrapositive to Proposition
4.24
together with Theorem
2.13
.
∎

We next show that hypergraph index
0
characterises linear divergence, proving Theorem
1.4
(1).

Theorem 4.26
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system such that
W
is
1
-ended. Then
(W,S)
has hypergraph index
0
if and only if
W
has linear divergence.

Proof.

Suppose first that
W
has linear divergence. Then by Lemma
4.4
(2),
\Omega(S)=\{S\}
, hence
S\in\Lambda_{0}=\Lambda_{0}\setminus\Lambda_{-1}
. Thus
(W,S)
has hypergraph index
0
.

Now suppose that
(W,S)
has hypergraph index
0
. Then
S
contains a wide subset, since
\Omega(S)\neq\varnothing
, and
S\in\Lambda_{0}=\Omega(S)\cup\Psi(S)
. Lemma
4.13
implies that
S\notin\Psi(S)
, and so we have
S\in\Omega(S)
. Hence
\Omega(S)=\{S\}
, and so by Lemma
4.4
(2) again,
W
has linear divergence.
∎

The next two results prove Theorem
1.4
(3).

Theorem 4.27
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system such that
W
is
1
-ended. Suppose
(W,S)
has finite hypergraph index
h
. Then
W
is strongly thick of order at most
h
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
h
. If
h=0
then by Theorem
4.26
,
W
has linear divergence. Thus
W
is strongly thick of order
0
, by Proposition
2.6
.

For the inductive step, since
S\in\Lambda_{h}
, by definition there is an
\equiv_{h-1}
-equivalence class
C_{S}
in
\Lambda_{h-1}
such that
S
is the union of the elements
T
of
C_{S}
. Hence by Corollary
4.23
,
W=W_{S}
is a tight network with respect to the collection of left cosets
\mathcal{L}_{S}=\{wW_{T}\mid w\in W,T\in C_{S}\}
. Now for each
T\in\Lambda_{h-1}
, the Coxeter system
(W_{T},T)
has hypergraph index at most
h-1
. Hence by inductive assumption, for each
T\in\Lambda_{h-1}
, the group
W_{T}
is strongly thick of order at most
h-1
. It follows that each coset
wW_{T}\in\mathcal{L}_{S}
is strongly thick of order at most
h-1
. Therefore
W
is strongly thick of order at most
h
, as required.
∎

Corollary 4.28
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system such that
W
is
1
-ended. If
(W,S)
has finite hypergraph index
h
, then the divergence of
W
is bounded above by a polynomial of degree
h+1
.

Proof.

This follows from Theorem
2.7
and Theorem
4.27
.
∎

We now prove the other direction of Theorem
1.4
(4).

Corollary 4.29
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system such that
W
is
1
-ended. If
W
is relatively hyperbolic, then
(W,S)
has infinite hypergraph index.

Proof.

Suppose
(W,S)
has finite hypergraph index
h
. Then by Corollary
4.28
, the divergence of
W
is bounded above by a polynomial of degree at most
h+1
. However, this is a contradiction, as finitely presented relatively hyperbolic groups have exponential divergence
[
Sis12
, Theorem 1.3]
. So
(W,S)
has infinite hypergraph index.
∎

Finally, we prove Theorem
1.4
(2).

Corollary 4.30
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system such that
W
is
1
-ended. Suppose
(W,S)
has hypergraph index
h=1
. Then
W
has quadratic divergence.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.26
,
W
does not have linear divergence. Hence if
(W,S)
is reducible, by Corollary
1.2

(W,S)
is the direct product of an irreducible nonspherical nonaffine Coxeter system with some finite (possibly reducible) Coxeter system. As divergence is a quasi-isometry invariant, we may thus assume without loss of generality that
(W,S)
is irreducible and nonaffine. Now by Theorem
1.1
, the divergence of
W
is at least quadratic, while by Corollary
4.28
, the divergence of
W
is at most quadratic. Thus
W
has quadratic divergence as required.
∎

Remark 4.31
.

In the right-angled case, by
[
Lev19
, Theorem 6.2]
the Coxeter system
(W,S)
having hypergraph index
h=1
is equivalent to
W
being strongly algebraically thick of order
1
; the proof relies on the characterisation of quadratic divergence for right-angled Coxeter groups. Using this equivalence as the base case of an induction, Theorem 6.5 of
[
Lev19
]
then shows that in right-angled systems,
(W,S)
having finite hypergraph index

implies that
W
is strongly algebraically thick of order at most
2h-1
. The inductive step in the proof of
[
Lev19
, Theorem 6.5]
goes through in the general setting (using Lemma

), but we do not know if in general
(W,S)
having hypergraph index
1
implies that
W
is strongly algebraically thick of order
1
.

5.
The duplex construction

We now introduce the procedure we call the
duplex construction
which, given any right-angled Coxeter system, produces infinitely many non-right-angled Coxeter systems of the same hypergraph index.
In Section
5.1
we define the duplex construction and prove that it preserves hypergraph index. We briefly discuss the relationships between the original right-angled Coxeter group and its various duplexes in Section
5.2
. Then in Section
5.3
, we apply the duplex construction to a family of examples from
[
DT15
]
to obtain, for every integer
d\geq 1
, infinitely many pairwise nonisomorphic Coxeter groups of hypergraph index
d-1
.
In Section
5.4
, we
show that certain duplexes with hypergraph index
d-1
have divergence
\simeq r^{d}
and are strongly thick of order
d-1
; thus Conjecture
1.5
holds for these examples. Since we are working with right-angled Coxeter systems, we will mostly use defining graphs rather than Dynkin diagrams in this section.

5.1.
Construction of the duplex

The duplex construction is the construction given by Definition
5.1
below, and the main result of this section is stated as Theorem
5.2
immediately afterwards.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
(W,S)
be a right-angled Coxeter system. Let
m
be an integer
\geq 2
and let
n
be either an integer
\geq 5
or equal to
\infty
. The
(m,n)
-duplex of
(W,S)
, denoted

, is the Coxeter system constructed as follows. The generating set
S_{2}
is the disjoint union of two copies of
S
, with each
u\in S
giving rise to the two elements
u^{\prime},u^{\prime\prime}\in S_{2}
. The edges of the defining graph of
(W_{2}(m,n),S_{2})
are then labelled as follows:

(1)

for all

Remark 5.3

Lemma 5.9
.

Let

be a right-angled Coxeter system with duplex
(W_{2}(m,n),S_{2})

(1)

(2)

Proof.

For (1), suppose

. Then

is nonspherical.
Let

be a minimal nonspherical subset of

, and therefore

5.5
.
From the definition of wide subsets, we conclude that

Thus by Lemma
5.8
, the projections

and

and

are disjoint, since

and

are disjoint, and

and

and

This implies that there exists a wide subset

are disjoint nonspherical commuting sets. Furthermore, their lifts

5.2.
Relationships between duplexes

In Proposition
5.16
we show that as we vary the parameters
m
and
n
in the duplex construction, we obtain infinitely many pairwise nonisomorphic Coxeter groups. We then state some open questions concerning the commensurability and quasi-isometry classification of duplex groups.

Proposition 5.16
.

Let
(W,S)
be a nonspherical right-angled Coxeter system. Let
m
and
m^{\prime}
be integers
\geq 2
and let
n
and
n^{\prime}
be either integers
\geq 5
or equal to
\infty
. Then the Coxeter groups
W_{2}(m,n)
and
W_{2}(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})
are abstractly isomorphic if and only if
(m,n)=(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Assume
W_{2}(m,n)
and
W_{2}(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})
are isomorphic. We wish to show that
(m,n)=(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})
. Since
(W,S)
is nonspherical,
(W_{2}(m,n),S_{2})
is nonspherical for all
m
and
n
by Lemma
5.8
. In particular, there exist edges labelled
n
in the Dynkin diagram of
(W_{2}(m,n),S_{2})
(and similarly for
n^{\prime}
and
W_{2}(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})
).
Up to symmetry, there are three cases possible:

(1)

both
n
and
n^{\prime}
are
\infty
;

(2)

n
is finite, but
n^{\prime}
is
\infty
;

(3)

both
n
and
n^{\prime}
are finite.

We will make use of the following facts:

(i)

every finite subgroup of a Coxeter group is conjugate into a finite special subgroup (see e.g.
[
BH93
, Proposition 1.3]
or
[
BB05
, Theorem 4.5.3]
); and, moreover,

(ii)

the conjugacy classes of the maximal finite subgroups of a Coxeter group are in one-to-one correspondence with the maximal spherical subsets of its Coxeter generating set (see
[
BMMN02
, Theorem 1.9]
).

In case (1) the finite subgroups of maximal order in
W_{2}(m,\infty)
are all isomorphic to the direct product of
N
dihedral groups of order
2m
, where
N
is the maximum number of vertices in a clique in the defining graph of
(W,S)
. (These dihedral subgroups of
W_{2}(m,\infty)
are generated by
\cup_{i=1}^{N}\{u_{i}^{\prime},u_{i}^{\prime\prime}\}
, where
\{u_{1},\dots,u_{N}\}\subseteq S
is the vertex set of a maximal clique in the defining graph for
(W,S)
.)
Thus, the maximum order of a finite subgroup of
W_{2}(m,\infty)
is
(2m)^{N}=2^{N}m^{N}
,
and this maximum is achieved. Similarly, the maximum order of a finite subgroup of
W_{2}(m^{\prime},\infty)
is
2^{N}(m^{\prime})^{N}
, and this maximum is achieved as well. It follows that
m=m^{\prime}
.

In case (2) we observe that, as in case (1) above, the maximal spherical subsets of
(W_{2}(m^{\prime},\infty),S_{2})
bijectively correspond to cliques in the defining graph of
(W,S)
. We notice that each such clique gives rise to a maximal spherical subset in the system
(W_{2}(m,n),S_{2})
as well. However, in the system
(W_{2}(m,n),S_{2})
there are also
2
-element spherical subsets consisting of edges labelled
n
and they are not contained in any of the maximal spherical subsets coming from cliques. By (ii) above, we conclude that
W_{2}(m,n)
contains more conjugacy classes of maximal finite subgroups than
W_{2}(m^{\prime},\infty)
, and therefore these Coxeter groups cannot be isomorphic. (Notice that our reasoning allows the situation when
n=m
.)

In case (3) we appeal to Theorem 1(b) of
[
FHM06
]
, which says that a nonspherical Coxeter system with all edge labels finite is rigid, meaning that the corresponding Dynkin diagram is unique up to the isomorphism of edge-labelled graphs. In our setting, this means there is a label preserving graph isomorphism
f\colon\Delta\to\Delta^{\prime}
, where
\Delta
and
\Delta^{\prime}
are the Dynkin diagrams corresponding to
(W_{2}(m,n),S_{2})
and
(W_{2}(m^{\prime},n^{\prime}),S_{2})
, respectively. Note that each vertex of
\Delta
has at most one incident edge with label
m
(one if
m\neq 2
and none if
m=2
), and
an even number of incident edges with label
n
.
In particular, every vertex has even valence if and only if
m=2
.
The
analogous statements hold for
\Delta^{\prime}
.
It follows that
m=2
if and only if
m^{\prime}=2
. Now if
m=m^{\prime}=2
, then
n=n^{\prime}
, as
n
and
n^{\prime}
are the only labels. Otherwise, if
m,m^{\prime}\neq 2
, the equality of edge labels of
\Delta
and
\Delta^{\prime}
gives

\{m,n\}=\{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}\}
. Now note that
since
(W,S)
is nonspherical, its Dynkin diagram has at least one edge with label
\infty
, and it follows that there is a vertex
v
in
\Delta
with at least two incident edges labelled
n
. Since
f(v)
is incident to just one edge labelled
m^{\prime}
, at least one of these
n
-labelled edges is mapped to an edge incident to
f(v)
labelled
n^{\prime}
. Thus
n=n^{\prime}
, and so
m=m^{\prime}
.
∎

The following open questions concern the coarse geometric relationships between duplexes. We say that two groups

\mathcal{C}
denote an equivalence class in
\Lambda_{h-1}
such that
S=\cup_{T\in\mathcal{C}}T
. Then given
T\in\Lambda_{i}
, with
i<h
, it is not necessarily true that
T
equals or feeds into an element of
\mathcal{C}
, even though
T\subseteq S
. On the other hand, there is a unique element of
\Lambda_{h+1}
, namely
S
, and
every subset of
S
at level less than
h+1
feeds into it.
Thus when
h
is finite, this directed forest is actually a tree.

Next, consider the set

\{S\}\cup\mathcal{C}\cup\{T\mid T\mbox{ feeds into an element of }\mathcal{C}\}.

We call the subtree induced by this set of vertices
the
principal subtree for
S
associated to
\mathcal{C}
. Note that
\mathcal{C}
, and hence principal subtrees, need not be unique.
We will often fix a principal subtree, and denote its set of level
i
vertices by
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{i}(S)
.
Note that
T\in\widetilde{\Lambda}_{i}(S)
can be thought of as a vertex of this tree or a subset of
S
and, by a slight abuse of notation, we will go back and forth between these interpretations without comment.

In order to use an inductive argument to prove Theorem
1.7
, we will need to find a subsystem of

(W,S)
with hypergraph index exactly
h-1
. This is provided by the following lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system with finite hypergraph index
h\geq 1
. Then there exists

T\subset S
such that
h(W_{T},T)=h-1
.

Proof.

Fix a principal subtree for
S
, associated to an equivalence class
\mathcal{C}
, and denote its level
i
vertices by
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{i}(S)
. Let

T_{1},\dots,T_{k}\subset S
be the elements of
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{h-1}(S)
; these are precisely the elements of the equivalence class
\mathcal{C}
of
\Lambda_{h-1}(S)
, and these all feed into the unique element
S
of
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{h}(S)
.

We first show that at least one of
T_{1},\dots,T_{k}
is not contained in any set at a level lower than
h-1
.
More precisely, we
claim that there exists
j
such that if
T_{j}\subseteq U\in\Lambda_{i}(S)
for some
i
, then
i\geq h-1
.
(Observe that in this statement,
U
need not be in our fixed principal subtree.)
To prove the claim, suppose instead that this fails for each of
T_{1},\dots,T_{k}
.
It follows that there is an index
l<h-1
, and (not necessarily distinct) elements

U_{1},\dots,U_{k}\in\Lambda_{l}(S)
such that
T_{i}\subseteq U_{i}
. Since
T_{1},\dots,T_{k}

form a complete
\equiv_{h-1}
-equivalence class in
\Lambda_{h-1}
,
it follows that
U_{1},\dots,U_{k}
are all
\equiv_{l}
-equivalent. Hence there is an element of
\Lambda_{l+1}(S)
that contains
\cup_{j}U_{j}
, which, by construction, is all of
S
. Since
l+1<h
, this contradicts the fact that the hypergraph index of
(W,S)
is
h
.
This proves the claim.

Now fix
T=T_{j}
which satisfies the claim. We wish to show that
h(W_{T},T)\leq h-1
.
It is immediate from Definitions
4.1
and
4.5
that any element of
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{0}(S)
which feeds into
T\in\widetilde{\Lambda}_{h-1}(S)
is contained in an element of
\Lambda_{0}(T)
. For
0<i\leq h-1
, each element
U\in\widetilde{\Lambda}_{i}(S)
which feeds into
T
is a union of elements of some equivalence class in
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{i}(S)
, consisting of elements which also feed into
T
. Hence it follows by induction that every such element is contained in some element of
\Lambda_{i}(T)
. In particular,
T
belongs to
\Lambda_{h-1}(T)
. This implies that

h(W_{T},T)\leq h-1
.

Now suppose
h(W_{T},T)<h-1
.
Then there exists some
n<h-1
such that
\Lambda_{n}(T)
contains
T
. By Lemma
4.16
(2a), it follows that there is an element of
\Lambda_{n}(S)
containing
T
. This contradicts the fact that
T=T_{j}\in\Lambda_{h-1}
satisfies the claim above. Thus
h(W_{T},T)=h-1
.
∎

Corollary
1.9
of the introduction follows from Lemma
6.1
.

The next lemma shows that in most cases, attaching finite trees to a Dynkin diagram along finitely many vertices does not increase the hypergraph index.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\Delta
be a Dynkin diagram with associated Coxeter system
(W,S)
such that
h(W,S)<\infty
.
Suppose there are subsets
T,T_{1},\dots,T_{k}\subset S
, which induce diagrams
\Delta_{T},\Delta_{T_{1}},\dots,\Delta_{T_{k}}
,
such that the following hold:

(1)

The sets
T,T_{1},\dots,T_{k}
are pairwise disjoint and their union is
S
.

(2)

Each
\Delta_{T_{j}}
is a tree.

(3)

The diagram
\Delta
is obtained by taking the disjoint union of
\Delta_{T},\Delta_{T_{1}},\dots,\Delta_{T_{k}}
and adding, for each
j
, a single edge connecting a vertex of
\Delta_{T_{j}}
to a vertex
v_{j}\in\Delta_{T}
.
(We do not require that
v_{j}\neq v_{j^{\prime}}
for
j\neq j^{\prime}
.)

If
h(W_{T},T)\neq 0,\infty
, then
h(W,S)\leq h(W_{T},T)
.

Proof.

Let
h_{T}=h(W_{T},T)
.
Fix a principal subtree associated to an equivalence class in
\Lambda_{h_{T}-1}(T)
whose union is
T
, and for
0\leq i\leq h_{T}
, let
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{i}(T)
denote the
set of level
i
vertices of this subtree.
Now if every element of
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{0}(T)
is of the form

A\times K
, where
A
is minimal nonspherical and
K
is spherical, then Lemma
4.14
implies that
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{0}(T)=\widetilde{\Lambda}_{1}(T)=\cdots=\widetilde{\Lambda}_{h_{T}}(T)
.
This means that
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{0}(T)
contains
T
, hence
h_{T}=0
, which contradicts the hypothesis of the lemma. Thus
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{0}(T)

has at least one set of the form
A\times B
, where
A
and
B
are both nonspherical.

Now consider
T_{j}
, for any
1\leq j\leq k
. By hypothesis,
v_{j}
is the only vertex in
\Delta
which is adjacent to
\Delta_{T_{j}}
.
At least one of the nonspherical sets
A
and
B
from the previous paragraph, say
A
, does not contain
v_{j}
.
Thus there is a set
A\times(B\cup T_{j})\subset S
, with both factors nonspherical. So there exists
U_{j}\in\Lambda_{0}(S)
with

A\times B\subset A\times(B\cup T_{j})\subseteq U_{j}
.

Recall that there is a unique element in
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{h_{T}}(T)
, namely
T
.
Let
T^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{h_{T}}(S)
denote a corresponding element provided by Lemma
4.16
(2). Thus
T\subseteq T^{\prime}
and given any element
X\in\widetilde{\Lambda}_{i}(T)
, and any
X^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{i}(S)
containing
X
, it follows that
X^{\prime}
feeds into
T^{\prime}
.
In particular,
U_{j}
feeds into
T^{\prime}
for each
j
, since
U_{j}
is an element of
\Lambda_{0}(S)
containing
A\times B\in\widetilde{\Lambda}_{0}(T)
.
Consequently
T^{\prime}

contains
T\cup\bigl(\bigcup_{1\leq j\leq k}U_{j}\bigr)=S
. It follows that
h(W,S)\leq h_{T}
.
∎

We now consider the situation where
\Delta
is a tree. The next result will be used to establish the base case in our inductive proof of Theorem
1.7
below.

Proposition 6.3
.

Suppose the Dynkin diagram
\Delta
of
(W,S)
is a tree. Then
(W,S)
has hypergraph index
0
,
1
or
\infty
only.

Proof.

Let
h
denote the hypergraph index of
(W,S)
. We may assume
\Lambda_{0}
is nonempty and does not contain
S
, for otherwise
h=\infty
or
h=0
by Definition
4.10
. Recall that every element of
\Lambda_{0}
is of one of the following types:

(1)

A\times B\in\Omega(S)
, where
A
and
B
are both nonspherical.

(2)

A\times K\in\Omega(S)\cup\Psi(S)
, with
A
minimal nonspherical and
K
spherical.

If every element of
\Lambda_{0}
is of type (2), then Lemma
4.14
implies that each

\equiv_{0}
-equivalence class contains a single element. Hence by induction
\Lambda_{h}=\Lambda_{0}
for all
h\geq 0
, and
h=\infty
, since
S\notin\Lambda_{0}
by assumption.
Thus we may assume that there is at least one element in
\Omega(S)
of type (1). We now describe the sets of this type.

Given
s\in S
, define
T_{s}=S\setminus\{s\}

and let
\Delta_{s}
be the subgraph of
\Delta
induced by
T_{s}
. Note that the special subgroups generated by the vertex sets of the components of
\Delta_{s}
pairwise commute. Thus if
\Delta_{s}
has at least two nonspherical components, then
T_{s}
is a set of type (1), as the maximality condition in the definition of
\Omega(S)
is clearly satisfied. Conversely, if
A\times B\in\Omega(S)
is a set of type (1), then there exists
s\in S\setminus A\times B
(as
S\notin\Lambda_{0}
).
Then by maximality of elements of
\Omega(S)
, we have
A\times B=T_{s}

and it follows that
\Delta_{s}
has at least two nonspherical components.

Suppose there are at least two sets of type (1) in
\Lambda_{0}
. Then
there are distinct
s,t\in S
such that both
\Delta_{s}
and
\Delta_{t}
have at least two nonspherical components. Write
\Delta_{s}(t)
for the component of
\Delta_{s}
which contains
t
. Then as
\Delta
is a tree, all components but one of
\Delta_{t}
are contained in
\Delta_{s}(t)
, hence
T_{s}\cap T_{t}
is nonspherical. Since
T_{s}\cup T_{t}=S
, it follows that
h=1
.

Finally, assume that there is a unique set
T_{s}
of type (1) in
\Lambda_{0}
.
Then every
T\neq T_{s}
in
\Lambda_{0}
is of type (2), and
contains
s
(for otherwise
T\subset T_{s}
, contradicting either maximality in Definition
4.1
or condition (3) in Definition
4.5
). If there exists such a
T
with
T_{s}\cap T
nonspherical, then
S=T_{s}\cup T
, and so
h=1
. Otherwise, if
T_{s}\cap T
is spherical for every
T\neq T_{s}
, then

T_{s}
is the unique element of its
\equiv_{0}
-equivalence class, as is each
T\neq T_{s}
, by Lemma
4.14
. Hence by induction
\Lambda_{h}=\Lambda_{0}
for all
h\geq 0
, and
h=\infty
in this last case.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.7
.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

The proof is by induction on the first Betti number.
The base case, where
\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta)=0
and so
\Delta
is a forest, follows easily by Proposition
6.3
and Lemma
4.18
. For the inductive step, we assume the statement is true for all Dynkin diagrams with first Betti number less than
b
, and let
\Delta
be a diagram with
\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta)=b\geq 1
, such that
\Delta
defines a Coxeter system
(W,S)
. Let
h=h(W,S)
. Observe that if
h\leq 2
, then the statement
h\leq b+1
is automatically true. So we assume from now on that
h>2
.
By hypothesis,
h<\infty
.

Case 1.
\Delta
is connected.

By Lemma
6.1
, there exists a set
T\subset S
such that
h(W_{T},T)=h-1
. Let
\Delta_{T}\subset\Delta
be the Dynkin diagram of
(W_{T},T)
.
If
\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta_{T})<\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta)
, then we can apply the induction hypothesis to conclude that

h-1=h(W_{T},T)\leq\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta_{T})+1\leq\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta)
, so that
h\leq b+1
as required.
Thus, we may assume that
\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta_{T})=\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta)=b
.

First suppose
\Delta_{T}
is not connected, so that
(W_{T},T)
is reducible. Since we are assuming
h>2
, which implies
h(W_{T},T)>1
, Lemma
4.18
implies that we can write

(W_{T},T)=(W_{T_{1}},T_{1})\times(W_{T_{2}},T_{2})
, where
(W_{T_{1}},T_{1})
is nonspherical and irreducible, and
(W_{T_{2}},T_{2})
is spherical. Moreover,
h(W_{T},T)=h(W_{T_{1}},T_{1})=h-1
.
Let
\Delta_{T_{1}}
be the subdiagram of
\Delta
induced by
T_{1}
. If
\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta_{T_{1}})<b
, then we may apply the induction hypothesis to conclude that
h(W_{T_{1}},T_{1})\leq\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta_{T_{1}})+1
, which
implies
h\leq b+1
. Thus we may assume that
\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta_{T_{1}})=b
.

Now define
U\subset S
as follows. Put

U=T
if
\Delta_{T}
is connected, and put
U=T_{1}
(from the previous paragraph) if
\Delta_{T}
is not connected. Let
\Delta_{U}
be the diagram induced by
U
. Then
by our assumptions,
\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta_{U})=\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta)
,
h(W_{U},U)=h-1
and
\Delta_{U}
is connected.
Consider the graph obtained from
\Delta
by deleting the interiors of all edges of
\Delta
which have exactly one vertex in
\Delta_{U}
, and let
\Delta_{U},\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\dots,\Delta_{k}
be its components.
Now
\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta_{U})+\Sigma_{i}\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta_{i})\leq\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta)
, since we have deleted edges.
Since
\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta_{U})=\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta)
, it follows that
\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta_{j})=0
for all
1\leq j\leq k
, and therefore,
\Delta_{j}
is a tree. Furthermore, there is exactly one edge in
\Delta
which is incident to both
\Delta_{U}
and
\Delta_{j}
. If there were two such edges, then there would be a cycle in
\Delta
which is not entirely contained in
\Delta_{U}
. It follows easily from the definition of the first Betti number that in this case
\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta_{U})<\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta)
, which contradicts our assumption. We have established that
\Delta
has the structure described in the statement of Lemma
6.2
, given by
\Delta_{U},\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\dots,\Delta_{k}
. By the conclusion of that lemma,
h=h(W,S)\leq h(W_{U},U)=h-1
, a contradiction.

Case 2.
\Delta
is not connected.
In this case
(W,S)
is reducible, and since it is nonspherical, it has at least one nonspherical factor. As we have assumed
h>2
, Lemma
4.18
implies that

(W,S)=(W_{1},S_{1})\times(W_{2},S_{2})
, with the first factor
nonspherical and irreducible and the second factor spherical, and furthermore,

h=h(W_{1},S_{1})
.
Now the Dynkin diagram
\Delta_{1}
of
(W_{1},S_{1})
is connected, and
\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta_{1})\leq\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta)=b
.
Since we have already established the conclusion of the lemma for connected Dynkin diagrams with first Betti number at most
b
, we conclude that

h(W_{1},S_{1})\leq\mathrm{b_{1}}(\Delta_{1})+1
.
Hence
h\leq b+1
as required.

This completes the proof of the inductive step.
∎

.

Questions 6.4
.

Let
\Delta
be a connected Dynkin diagram.

(1)

Which hypergraph indexes are realised as we vary over all connected Dynkin diagrams having the same first Betti number as
\Delta
? In particular, is hypergraph index
b_{1}(\Delta)+1
realised?

(2)

Which hypergraph indexes are realised as we vary over all connected Dynkin diagrams having the same underlying unlabelled graph as
\Delta
