Abstract
. In I981, Uchimura studied a divisor generating q q -series that has applications in probability theory and in the analysis of data structures, called heaps . Mainly, he proved the following identity. For | q | < 1 |q|<1 , ∑ n = 1 ∞ n ​ q n ​ ( q n + 1 ) ∞ = ∑ n = 1 ∞ ( − 1 ) n − 1 ​ q n ⁡ ( n + 1 ) 2 ( 1 − q n ) ​ ( q ) n = ∑ n = 1 ∞ q n 1 − q n . \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nq^{n}(q^{n+1})_{\infty}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}}{(1-q^{n})(q)_{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n}}{1-q^{n}}. Over the years, this identity has been generalized by many mathematicians in different directions. Uchimura himself in 1987, Dilcher (1995), Andrews-Crippa-Simon (1997), and recently Gupta-Kumar (2021) found a generalization of the aforementioned identity. Any generalization of the right most expression of the above identity, we name as divisor-type sum, whereas a generalization of the middle expression we say Ramanujan-type sum, and any generalization of the left most expression we refer it as Uchimura-type sum. Quite surprisingly, Simon, Crippa and Collenberg (1993) showed that the same divisor generating function has a connection with random acyclic digraphs. One of the main themes of this paper is to study these different generalizations and present a unified theory. We also discuss applications of these generalized identities in probability theory for the analysis of heaps and random acyclic digraphs.

Ramanujan recorded five interesting

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}c^{n}q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}}{(1-q^{n})(cq)_{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{c^{n}q^{n}}{1-q^{n}}.

(1.1)

Throughout this paper, we assume

\displaystyle(a)_{0}=(a;q)_{0}=1,

\displaystyle(a)_{n}=(a;q)_{n}=(1-a)(1-aq)\cdots(1-aq^{n-1}),~n\geq 1,

\displaystyle(a)_{\infty}=(a;q)_{\infty}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(a;q)_{n},~\textrm{for}~|q|<1.

The special case

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}}{(1-q^{n})(q)_{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n}}{1-q^{n}}.

(1.2)

This identity has been independently rediscovered by Fine

These polynomials have connections to the analysis of the data structures called

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nq^{n}(q^{n+1})_{\infty}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}}{(1-q^{n})(q)_{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n}}{1-q^{n}}.

(1.3)

This identity has been generalized by many mathematicians and it has a nice partition theoretic interpretation which will be discussed soon. One of the main aims of this paper is to study different generalizations of this identity. Before we mention these generalizations, let us fix some terminologies which will be used frequently. Any generalized form of the left most expression of the above identity we call as

At this moment, a natural question arises that ‘what will be the

sum for Ramanujan’s identity (

Bressoud and Subbarao
[

\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)}(-1)^{\#(\pi)-1}s(\pi)=d(n),

(1.4)

where

\pi

n

and

is number of parts and smallest part of the partition

is number of positive divisors of

[

also rediscovered (
1.4

Andrews

3
]
, in his famous paper on

-function, showed that the identity (
1.4
) can also be obtained by differentitating
q

[
2
, p. 20, Corollary 2.4]
.
Using purely combinatorical argument, Bressoud and Subbarao
[
9
]
further generalized (
1.4
). Mainly, they proved that, for
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)}(-1)^{\#(\pi)-1}\sum_{j=1}^{s(\pi)}(\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)+j)^{m}=\sigma_{m}(n):=\sum_{d|n}d^{m},

(1.5)

where
\ell(\pi)
denotes the largest part of the partition
\pi
. Letting
m=0
in the above equation gives (
1.4
).
Recently, the last three authors
[
7
]
established an identity that is not only a one variable generalization of (

from non-negative integers to the set of all integers. To prove their result, they used a differential and integral operator on a partition theoretic interpretation of Ramanujan’s identity (
1.1

The generalized identity

is as follows, for
m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
c\in\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)}(-1)^{\#(\pi)-1}\sum_{j=1}^{s(\pi)}(\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)+j)^{m}c^{(\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)+j)}=\sigma_{m,c}(n):=\sum_{d\mid n}d^{m}c^{d}.

(1.6)

Motivated from the combinatorial argument of Bressoud and Subbarao, the authors
[
1

recently showed that the above identity is in fact valid for any

.
Moreover, with the help of a fractional differential operator and analytic continuation the authors gave a simple proof of the generalized identity (
1.6
).

1.1.
A generalization by Uchimura

Apart from obtaining the leftmost expression in (
1.3
), Uchimura
[
27
]
in 1987, further gave a one variable generalization of the identity (
1.3
) in the following way. For a non-negative integer
m
, he defined

\displaystyle M_{m}:=M_{m}(q)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{m}q^{n}(q^{n+1})_{\infty},\quad\text{and}\quad K_{m+1}:=K_{m+1}(q)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{m}(n)q^{n}.

(1.7)

Then the following properties hold:

(1)

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}K_{m}t^{m}/m!\right)=1+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}M_{m}t^{m}/m!.

(2)

Let
Y_{m}
be the Bell polynomial defined by

Y_{m}\left(u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{m}\right)=\sum_{\Pi(m)}\frac{m!}{k_{1}!\dots k_{m}!}\left(\frac{u_{1}}{1!}\right)^{k_{1}}\dots\left(\frac{u_{m}}{m!}\right)^{k_{m}},

(1.8)

where
\Pi(m)
denotes a partition of
m
with

k_{1}+2k_{2}+\cdots+mk_{m}=m.

Then for any
m\geq 1
, we have

M_{m}=Y_{m}(K_{1},\dots,K_{m}).

(1.9)

This identity was later rediscovered by Andrews, Crippa and Simon
[
4
, Theorem 2.6]
.
Very recently, the authors
[
1
, Theorem 2.2]
gave a one variable generalization of the above result by defining, for a non-negative integer
m
and a complex number
c
with
|cq|<1
,

\displaystyle M_{m,c}

\displaystyle:=M_{m,c}(q)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{m}c^{n}q^{n}(q^{n+1})_{\infty},\hskip 14.22636pt

\displaystyle K_{m+1,c}

\displaystyle:=K_{m+1,c}(q)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{m,c}(n)q^{n}.

(1.10)

Then
M_{m,c}
and
K_{m,c}
satisfy the following relations:

\displaystyle\frac{(q)_{\infty}}{(cq)_{\infty}}\exp\bigg(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}K_{m,c}\frac{t^{m}}{m!}\bigg)=\frac{(q)_{\infty}}{(cq)_{\infty}}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}M_{m,c}\frac{t^{m}}{m!},\quad\text{and}

for any
m\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle M_{m,c}=\frac{(q)_{\infty}}{(cq)_{\infty}}Y_{m}(K_{1,c},\cdots,K_{m,c}),

(1.11)

where
Y_{m}(u_{1},u_{2},\cdots,u_{m})
denotes the Bell polynomial defined as in (
1.8
).
Letting
c=1
in (
1.11
) immediately yields (
1.9
).

Now one can clearly observe that, in both of the identities (
1.9
) and (
1.11
), the left hand side expressions are
Uchimura-type
generalized
q
-series, whereas the right hand side identities are
divisor-type
generalized sums. Thus, one can ask ‘what are the
Ramanujan-type
generalized sums for the identities (

) and (
1.11
)?’
We answer this question in Theorem
2.12
and Corollary
2.14
. Interestingly, we shall see the presence of Eulerian polynomials in the generalized
Ramanujan-type
sum for the identity (

1.2.
A generalization by Dilcher, and Andrews-Crippa-Simon

In 1995, Dilcher
[

\displaystyle\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}{n\choose k}q^{n}(q^{n+1})_{\infty}=q^{-{k\choose 2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}q^{{n+k\choose 2}}}{(1-q^{n})^{k}(q)_{n}}=\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{j_{1}}}{1-q^{j_{1}}}\cdots\sum_{j_{k}=1}^{j_{k-1}}\frac{q^{j_{k}}}{1-q^{j_{k}}}.

(1.12)

Substituting

in the above identity, we can easily recover (

For a fixed

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}q^{{n+1\choose 2}}}{(1-q^{n})^{k}(q)_{n}}=P_{k}\big(S_{0}(q),S_{1}(q),\dots,S_{k-1}(q)\big):=P_{k}(q),

(1.13)

where

\displaystyle S_{m}(q)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{m}q^{n}}{1-q^{n}}.

(1.14)

Note that the above generating function
S_{m}(q)
is same as the generating function

. An interesting fact is that the series

also appears in the Fourier series expansion of Eisenstien series

Now we point out that there is a small error in Dilcher’s proof of (

[
11
, Theorem 3]
is stated in the main results section (see Theorem

As the above identity (

) of Andrews, Crippa and Simon generalizes (

\displaystyle(q)_{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n}}{(q)_{n}}{k+n-1\choose k}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}q^{{n+1\choose 2}}}{(1-q^{n})^{k}(q)_{n}}.

2.
Main Results

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
a,~b,~c,~d
be complex numbers with
|a|<1,~|c|<1
and
f(z)
be an analytic function with power series
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}z^{j}
under the condition
|z|<1
. Then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(b/a)_{n}a^{n}}{(d)_{n}}f(cq^{n})=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}c^{j}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(ad/b)_{n}(-b)^{n}q^{{n\choose 2}+jn}}{(d)_{n}(aq^{j})_{n+1}}.

By letting
f(z)=(1-z)^{-\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, an extended version of the Gupta-Kumar’s identity (
1.17
) is obtained as an immediate corollary of the above theorem. This corollary is significant enough to be presented as a separate theorem as it will be frequently availed throughout the paper. Prior to revealing this identity, it is essential to define the binomial coefficient for complex numbers as the quotient of gamma functions. For a complex number
\alpha
such that
-\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\mathbb{N}
, and a natural number
n
, we define the binomial coefficient as follows:

\displaystyle{\alpha\choose n}:=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(\alpha-n+1)}.

(2.1)

We now move on to explore several interesting results stemming from Theorem
2.1
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
a,~b,~c,~d,~\alpha
be complex numbers with
|a|<1,~|c|<1
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(b/a)_{n}a^{n}}{(1-cq^{n})^{\alpha}(d)_{n}}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c^{j}{\alpha+j-1\choose j}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(ad/b)_{n}(-b)^{n}q^{{n\choose 2}+jn}}{(d)_{n}(aq^{j})_{n+1}}.

By substituting
d=q
into the above identity, we arrive at a two variable generalization to Gupta and Kumar’s identity (
1.17
). Mainly, we obtain the following identity.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
a,~b,~c,~\alpha
be complex numbers with
|c|<1
, then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(b/a)_{n}a^{n}}{(1-cq^{n})^{\alpha}(q)_{n}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c^{n}{\alpha+n-1\choose n}\frac{(bq^{n})_{\infty}}{(aq^{n})_{\infty}}.

Letting
b=q^{2}
and
c=q
in Corollary
2.3
and upon simplification, we get the following immediate extended form of (
1.17
).

Corollary 2.4
.

For complex numbers
a
and
\alpha
with
|a|<1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(q/a)_{n}a^{n}}{(1-q^{n})^{\alpha}(q)_{n}}=-\frac{(q)_{\infty}}{(a)_{\infty}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q^{n}{\alpha+n-1\choose\alpha}\frac{(a/q)_{n}}{(q)_{n}}.

Gupta and Kumar
[

, Theorem 1.1]
originally derived the above result under the assumption that

is a natural number. However, it is important to note that their identity is in fact valid for any complex number

Further, as an application of Theorem

).

Corollary 2.5
.

For a complex number
\alpha
and a natural number

, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}q^{{n\choose 2}+nr}}{(1-q^{n})^{\alpha}(q)_{n}}=\sum_{j=r}^{\infty}{\alpha+j-r\choose\alpha}q^{j}(q^{j+1})_{\infty}.

(2.2)

In particular, when

, it yields the first equality of Uchimura’s identity (

2.1.
A generalization of Dilcher’s identity

Before proceeding to the next theorem, we would like to introduce an identity formulated by Dilcher
[
11
, Theorem 3]
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the identity is the following:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}q^{n+1\choose 2}}{(1-q^{n})^{k}(q)_{n}}=\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left\{\sum_{j=0}^{k-t}{k-1\choose j+t-1}\frac{s(j+t,t)}{(j+t)!}\right\}U_{t}(q),

(2.3)

where
s(m,n)
represents the Stirling numbers of the first kind, and

U_{t}(q):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{t}q^{n}(q^{n+1})_{\infty}.

However, it is important to note that there is an error in the above identity (
2.3
). Instead of
U_{t}(q)
on the right side expression of (
2.3
), it should be the tail
U_{t,j+t}(q)
of
U_{t}(q)
, defined in (
2.6
).
We not only correct the above identity (
2.3
) but also give a one-variable generalization of it. Here is the generalized identity.

Theorem 2.6
.

For natural numbers
r
and
k
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}q^{{n\choose 2}+nr}}{(1-q^{n})^{k}(q)_{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-t}{k-1\choose j+t-1}A(j+t,r,t)U_{t,r+j+t-1}(q)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{k}{k-1\choose j-1}A(j,r,0)U_{0,r+j-1}(q),

(2.4)

where

\displaystyle A(j,r,t):=\sum_{l=t}^{j}\frac{s(j,l)}{j!}{l\choose t}(1-r)^{l-t},

(2.5)

and

\displaystyle U_{m,i}(q):=\sum_{n=i}^{\infty}n^{m}q^{n}(q^{n+1})_{\infty}

(2.6)

is the tail of Uchimura’s sum
M_{m}(q)
defined in (
1.9
).

Letting
r=1
in the left-hand side expression of (
2.4
), it transforms into the form presented in (
2.3
). However, substituting
r=1
on the right-hand side of (
2.4
), we see that the second finite sum vanishes as
A(j,1,0)=0
because
s(j,0)=0
for any
j\geq 1
. Then the first sum becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-t}{k-1\choose j+t-1}A(j+t,1,t)U_{t,j+t}(q).

(2.7)

Now from the definition (
2.5
) of
A(j+t,1,t)
, it follows that
A(j+t,1,t)=\frac{s(j+t,t)}{(j+t)!}
.
Thus, (
2.7
) becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-t}{k-1\choose j+t-1}\frac{s(j+t,t)}{(j+t)!}U_{t,j+t}(q),

which is slightly different from the right hand expression of Dilcher’s identity (
2.3
). The only difference is that the function
U_{t}(q)
in (
2.3
) is being replaced by
U_{t,j+t}(q)
.

Motivated by the identity (
2.3
), Dilcher
[
11
, p. 89]
defined an interesting sequence of rational numbers involving Stirling numbers of the first kind. Mainly, for

and

\displaystyle a(k,t)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-t}{k-1\choose j+t-1}\frac{s(j+t,t)}{(j+t)!}.

He further proved the following recursive relation for
a(k,t)
with the help of the recursion formula for the Stirling numbers of the first kind,

\displaystyle a(1,1)=1,\quad a(k+1,t+1)=\frac{k}{k+1}a(k,t+1)+\frac{1}{k+1}a(k,t).

(2.8)

Utilizing the above recursion formula, Dilcher
[
11
, Equation (4.10)]
obtained an interesting identity, namely,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}a(k,t)=1.

(2.9)

Now inspired by the generalization of Dilcher’s identity i.e., Theorem
2.6
, we define a new sequence of numbers.
First, we rewrite Theorem
2.6
as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}q^{{n\choose 2}+nr}}{(1-q^{n})^{k}(q)_{n}}=\sum_{t=1}^{k}C(k,r,t)U_{t,r+j+t-1}(q)+C(k,r,0)U_{0,r+j-1}(q)

where the coefficients
C(k,r,t)
and
C(k,r,0)
defined as

\displaystyle C(k,r,t)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=0}^{k-t}{k-1\choose j+t-1}A(j+t,r,t),

(2.10)

\displaystyle C(k,r,0)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}{k-1\choose j-1}A(j,r,0),

where
A(j,r,t)
is defined in (
2.5
).
Letting
r=1
, one can easily verify that
A(j,1,t)=\frac{s(j,t)}{(j)!}
and
C(k,1,t)
is nothing but Dilcher’s function
a(k,t)
. Analysing these coefficients, we prove the following beautiful recursion formulae:

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
r,j,k\in\mathbb{N}
. For
1\leq t\leq j
, we have

\displaystyle A(1,r,1)=1,\hskip 14.22636ptA(j,r,t)=(j+1)A(j+1,r,t+1)+(r+j-1)A(j,r,t+1),

(2.11)

and

\displaystyle C(1,r,1)=1,\hskip 14.22636ptC(k+1,r,t+1)=\frac{k+1-r}{k+1}C(k,r,t+1)+\frac{1}{k+1}C(k,r,t).

(2.12)

Remark 1
.

When
r=1
, the recursion (
2.11
) reduces to the well-known recursion
[
10
, p. 214]
of the Stirling numbers of the first kind, that is,

\displaystyle s(j,t)=s(j+1,t+1)+js(j,t+1),

and the recursion (
2.12
) reduces to the recursion (
2.8
) for
a(k,t)
.

Using the definitions (
2.5
), (
2.10
) of
A(j,r,t)
and
C(k,r,t)
, one can also verify that, for any
r,k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle C(k,r,k)=A(k,r,k)=\frac{1}{k!}.

Further, we prove the following interesting properties related to
A(j,r,t)
and
C(k,r,t)
.

Theorem 2.8
.

For any natural number
j
and
k
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{j}A(j,r,t)=\frac{(-1)^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}(r-1)r(r+1)\cdots(r+j-3),

(2.13)

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}C(k,r,t)=\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}(r-2)(r-3)(r-4)\cdots(r-k).

(2.14)

Substituting
r=1
, we obtain a trivial identity related to Stirling numbers of the first kind and also recover Dilcher’s identity (
2.9
).

Corollary 2.9
.

For any
j,k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{j}s(j,t)=0,\quad\sum_{t=1}^{k}a(k,t)=1.

2.2.
A generalization of identities of Gupta-Kumar and Andrews-Crippa-Simon

Now we state a one variable generalization of Gupta-Kumar’s identity (
1.18
). As a result, we obtain a two variable generalization of the identity (
1.13
) of Andrews, Crippa and Simon.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Let
a,c\in\mathbb{C}
such that
|ac|<1,|cq|<1
, we define
\mathfrak{S}_{m,a,c}(q)
by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{m,a,c}(q):=S_{m,c}(q)-R_{m,ac}(q),

(2.15)

where

\displaystyle S_{m,c}(q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{m}c^{n}q^{n}}{1-q^{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{m,c}(n)q^{n},

(2.16)

\displaystyle R_{m,ac}(q)

\displaystyle=\textup{Li}_{-m}\left(ac\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{m}a^{n}c^{n}q^{n}}{1-q^{n}}=\textup{Li}_{-m}\left(ac\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{m,ac}(n)q^{n}.

(2.17)

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a polynomial
P_{k}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k})
with rational coefficients, such that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(q/a)_{n}a^{n}}{(1-cq^{n})^{k+1}(q)_{n-1}}=-\frac{1}{c}\frac{(q)_{\infty}(ac)_{\infty}}{(cq)_{\infty}(a)_{\infty}}P_{k}(\mathfrak{S}_{0,a,c}(q),\mathfrak{S}_{1,a,c}(q),\dots,\mathfrak{S}_{k-1,a,c}(q)).

(2.18)

Substituting
c=1
in the above theorem and upon simplification, one can immediately recover the identity (
1.18
) of Gupta and Kumar. Furthermore, letting
a\rightarrow 0
in (
2.18
) gives us a new one variable generalization of the identity (
1.13
) of Andrews, Crippa and Simon.

Corollary 2.11
.

For
|c|<1
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a polynomial
P_{k}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k})
with rational coefficients, such that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}q^{n+1\choose 2}}{(1-cq^{n})^{k+1}(q)_{n-1}}=\frac{1}{c}\frac{(q)_{\infty}}{(cq)_{\infty}}P_{k}(S_{0,c}(q),S_{1,c}(q),\dots,S_{k-1,c}(q)).

In particular when
c=1
, one can easily verify that the above identity reduces to the identity (
1.13
).

Now we state a
Ramanujan-type
expression for the identity (
1.11
). Let
K_{m,c}(q)
be the generalized divisor generating function defined in (
1.10
) and
Y_{n}
be the Bell polynomial defined in (
1.8
). Let
A_{n}(x)
be the Eulerian polynomial of degree
n
defined as

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{n}(x)\frac{t^{n}}{n!}=\frac{(1-x)e^{t}}{e^{xt}-xe^{t}}.

Then we have the following result.

Theorem 2.12
.

For any non-negative integer
m
and a complex number
c
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{m}c^{n}q^{n}(q^{n+1})_{\infty}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}q^{{n\choose 2}}(cq^{n})A_{m}(cq^{n})}{(1-cq^{n})^{m+1}(q)_{n-1}}=\frac{(q)_{\infty}}{(cq)_{\infty}}Y_{m}(K_{1,c}(q),K_{2,c}(q),\cdots,K_{m,c}(q)).

Interestingly, letting
m=1
in the above theorem gives a
Uchimura-type
expression for the Ramanujan’s identity (
1.1
).

Corollary 2.13
.

For any complex number
c
, we found that

\displaystyle\frac{(cq)_{\infty}}{(q)_{\infty}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nc^{n}q^{n}(q^{n+1})_{\infty}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}c^{n}q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}}{(1-q^{n})(cq)_{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{c^{n}q^{n}}{1-q^{n}}.

(2.19)

Substituting
c=1
in (
2.19
) reduces to Uchimura’s identity (
1.3
). Further, putting
c=1
in Theorem
2.12
produces a
Ramanujan-type
expression for the Uchimura’s identity (
1.9
) which seems to be a new expression to the best of our knowledge.

Corollary 2.14
.

For any non-negative integer
m
, we have the following expression

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{m}q^{n}(q^{n+1})_{\infty}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}q^{{n\choose 2}+n}A_{m}(q^{n})}{(1-q^{n})^{m}(q)_{n}}=Y_{m}(K_{1}(q),K_{2}(q),\dots,K_{m}(q)).

Letting
m=1
in the above corollary and upon simplification, it yields Uchimura’s identity (
1.3
).

3.
Applications in probability theory and random graph theory

3.1.
Application of Uchimura’s work in probability theory

Uchimura
[
27
]
utilized his identity (
1.9
) to give applications in probability theory that can be used in the analysis of data structures called
heaps
. Here we briefly discuss his results. Uchimura
[
27
, p. 76]
considered a random variable
X
such that, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and
0<q<1
,

\displaystyle\textup{Pr}(X=n)=q^{n}(q^{n+1})_{\infty}.

(3.1)

Then the probability generating function is given by

\displaystyle G(x,q)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^{n}q^{n}(q^{n+1})_{\infty}.

(3.2)

Moreover, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the
m
-th moment is given by

E(X^{m})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^{m}q^{n}(q^{n+1})_{\infty},

which is nothing but the function
M_{m}
defined in (
1.7
). Therefore, utilizing (
1.9
), we have

E(X^{m})=M_{m}=Y_{m}(K_{1},\dots,K_{m}).

In particular, for
m=1
, it yields

\displaystyle E(X)=M_{1}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d(n)q^{n}.

(3.3)

Further, in the proof of (
1.9
), Uchimura
[
27
, p. 74-75]
showed that, if one writes

\displaystyle\log G(e^{t},q)=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}h_{m}\frac{t^{m}}{m!},

where the
m
-th coefficient
h_{m}
is known as the
m
-th cumulant, then

\displaystyle h_{m}=K_{m}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{m-1}q^{n}.

(3.4)

We know that the
2
nd cumulant
h_{2}
is equals to
\text{Var}(X)
. Hence, taking
m=2
, we have

\displaystyle\text{Var}(X)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma(n)q^{n}.

(3.5)

Uchimura
[
27
]
further explained a combinatorial interpretation
of the probability generation function
G(x,q)
as defined in (
3.2
) and gave immediate applications in the analysis of the data structures known as heaps. Mainly, he studied the average number of exchanges required to insert an element into a heap. Interested readers can see

[
27
, Section 3]
for more details.

3.2.
Works of Simon-Crippa-Collenberg and Andrews-Crippa-Simon

The divisor generating function appearing in (
3.3
) surprisingly also occured in the work of Simon, Crippa and Collenberg
[
25
]
in connection with random acyclic digraphs. To know more about random acyclic digraphs, see
[
22
]
. Let the
G_{n,p}
-model of a random acyclic digraph to be the vertex set
V=\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
and the probability of appearing a directed edge
(i,j)
, with
1\leq i<j\leq n
, be
p\in(0,1)
.
Now consider a random variable
\gamma_{n}^{*}(1)
that counts the number of vertices that can be connected by a directed path starting from the vertex
1
(including
1
itself). Simon et al.
[
25
]
proved that, for
1\leq h\leq n
,

\displaystyle\text{Pr}(\gamma_{n}^{*}(1)=h)=q^{n-h}\prod_{j=1}^{h-1}\left(1-q^{n-j}\right),

(3.6)

where
q=1-p
. In the same paper, they proved the following interesting result,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(n-E(\gamma_{n}^{*}(1)\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d(n)q^{n}.

(3.7)

The above identity was further extended by Andrews, Crippa and Simon
[
4
]
and they proved that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\text{Var}(\gamma_{n}^{*}(1))=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma(n)q^{n}.

(3.8)

Comparing (
3.5
) and (
3.8
), one can clearly see that

\text{Var}(X)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\text{Var}(\gamma_{n}^{*}(1)),

where
X
is the random variable studied by Uchimura, defined in (
3.1
). Here we recall that the generalized divisor generating function (
3.4
) is same as the
m
th cumulant
h_{m}
with respect to the random variable
X
. Thus, a subsequent question raises is that ‘what will be the interpretation of the generalized divisor generating function (
3.4
) with respect to the random variable
\gamma_{n}^{*}(1)
studied by Simon, Crippa and Collenberg?’

Around the same time, Andrews, Crippa, and Simon
[
4
, Theorem 3.1]
proved a more general result using
q
-series techniques.
Their result is as follows.

Let
\{t_{n}(q)\}
be a sequence of polynomials satisfying the following recurrence relation, for
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle t_{n}(q)=f(n)+(1-q^{n-1})t_{n-1}(q),~t_{0}(q)=0,

(3.9)

where
f(n)=\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{k}n^{k}\in\mathbb{Q}[n]
be a non-zero polynomial.
Let us recall the polynomial
P_{k}(q):=P_{k}\big(S_{0}(q),S_{1}(q),\cdots S_{k-1}(q)\big)
defined in (
1.13
). Then we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(i)-t_{n}(q)\right)=\sum_{j\geq 1}h_{j}P_{j}(q),

(3.10)

where coefficients
h_{j}
are given by

\displaystyle h_{1}=c_{0},~\text{and}~\text{for}~j\geq 2,~h_{j}=\sum_{i\geq j-1}(-1)^{i-j+1}{i-1\choose j-2}i!\sum_{k\geq i}c_{k}\tilde{s}(k,i),~

(3.11)

where
\tilde{s}(k,i)
denotes the Stirling numbers of the second kind.

Now letting
t_{n}(q):=E\left(\gamma_{n}^{*}(1)\right)
, they observed that
t_{n}(q)
satisfies the recurrence relation (
3.9
) with
f(n)=1
for all
n
. Thus, utilizing (
3.10
), one can easily obtain (
3.7
). Further, as an application of (
3.10
), Andrews et al. also obtained (
3.8
), see
[
4
, Theorem 4.2]
.

In the same paper, Andrews, Crippa and Simon conjectured that, if
f(n)=(-1)^{n}
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}f(i)-t_{n}(q)\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n^{2}}.

(3.12)

This conjecture was settled by Bringmann and Jennings-Shaffer
[
8
]
. Further, they proved the following result, for
f(n)=b^{n}
,
b\neq 1
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
b\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{1\}
and
f(n)=b^{n}
. Let
t_{n}(q)
be a sequence of polynomials defined as in (
3.9
). Then for
|q|<\min\left(1,|b|^{-1}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}b^{i}-t_{n}(q)\right)=\frac{b}{1-b}-\frac{b(q)_{\infty}}{(b)_{\infty}}.

Letting
b=-1
in the above theorem gives (
3.12
).
Further, Bringmann and Jennings-Shaffer proved that, if
f(n)
is a periodic function with period
N
and
t_{n}(q)
be the sequence of polynomials defined as in (
3.9
). Let
\zeta_{N}=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{N}}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}f(i)-t_{n}(q)\right)=c_{0}S_{0}(q)-(q)_{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\frac{c_{k}}{(\zeta_{N}^{k})_{\infty}}+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\frac{c_{k}}{1-\zeta_{N}^{k}},

(3.13)

where

c_{k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}f(j)\zeta_{N}^{(1-j)k}.

In a recent development, Gupta and Kumar
[
19
, Theorem 1.11]
attempted to extend the above result (
3.13
). However, they made a small error. In this paper, we rectify it and present a valid generalization of the aforementioned result.

First, we present a one variable generalization of the identity (
3.10
) of Andrews, Crippa and Simon. Consider the sequence of polynomials
t_{n}(a,q)
depending on
a
and
q
, defined as

\displaystyle t_{n}(a,q)=\frac{f(n)-af(n+1)}{1-aq^{n}}+\left(\frac{1-q^{n-1}}{1-aq^{n}}\right)t_{n-1}(a,q),~n\geq 1~\text{and}~t_{0}(a,q)=0,

(3.14)

where
f(n)=\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{k}n^{k}\in\mathbb{Q}[n]
be a polynomial in
n
with
af(1)=0
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
t_{n}(a,q)
be the sequence defined in (
3.14
). Then we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\ell}f(n)-\frac{a}{1-a}f(\ell+1)-\left(\frac{1-aq^{\ell}}{1-a}\right)t_{\ell}(a,q)\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}h_{j}P_{j}(a,q),

where the constant
h_{j}
is same as in (
3.11
)
and

P_{j}(a,q)

is a polynomial in
j
variables defined in (
1.18
).

Remark 2
.

Letting
a=0
in Theorem
3.2
, one can easily recover the identity (
3.10
) of Andrews, Crippa and Simon. Note that the above result is the corrected version of Theorem
4.2
in
[
19
]
.

Now we state a corrected version of another identity of Gupta and Kumar
[
19
, Theorem 1.11]
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
f(n)

be a periodic sequence with period
N
and
af(1)=0
. Let
t_{n}(a,q)
be the sequence defined in (
3.14
).
Then for
|a|<1
and
|q|<1
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\ell}f(n)-\frac{a}{1-a}f(\ell+1)-\left(\frac{1-aq^{\ell}}{1-a}\right)t_{\ell}(a,q)\right)

\displaystyle=c_{0}\mathfrak{S}_{0,a}(q)+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\frac{c_{k}}{1-\zeta_{N}^{k}}-\frac{(q)_{\infty}}{(a)_{\infty}}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}c_{k}\frac{(a\zeta_{N}^{k})_{\infty}}{(\zeta_{N}^{k})\infty},

where

\displaystyle c_{k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}f(j)\zeta_{N}^{(1-j)k}.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
f(n)
and
t_{n}(a,q)
be same as in Theorem
3.3
. Then for
|q|<1
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\ell}f(n)-\frac{a}{1-a}f(\ell+1)-\left(\frac{1-aq^{\ell}}{1-a}\right)t_{\ell}(a,q)\right)=\frac{(q)_{\infty}}{(a)_{\infty}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a/q)_{n}q^{n}}{(q)_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}f(j)\Big\lceil{{\frac{n+1-j}{N}}}\Big\rceil.

This is the corrected version of Corollary 4.6 in
[
19
]
. Letting
a=0
and
f(n)=(-1)^{n}
in Corollary
3.4
, one can recover the identity (
3.12
) of Bringmann and Jennings-Shaffer.

|z|<1

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(A)_{n}z^{n}}{(q)_{n}}=\frac{(Az)_{\infty}}{(z)_{\infty}}.

\displaystyle{}_{2}\phi_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}A,&B\\

\displaystyle{}_{2}\phi_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}A,&B\\

(4.3)

The following
{}_{3}\phi_{2}

\displaystyle{}_{3}\phi_{2}\left(\begin{matrix}A,&B,&C\\

&D,&\frac{DE}{BC}\end{matrix}\,;q,\frac{E}{A}\right).

(4.4)

Now we mention the Chu-Vandermonde identity,

\displaystyle{k+n-1\choose k}=\sum_{r=1}^{k}{n\choose r}{k-1\choose k-r}.

(4.5)

f(z)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(b/a)_{n}a^{n}}{(d)_{n}}f(cq^{n})=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}c^{j}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(b/a)_{n}}{(d)_{n}}(aq^{j})^{n}.

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}c^{j}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(b/a)_{n}}{(d)_{n}}(aq^{j})^{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}c^{j}\lim_{\gamma\rightarrow 0}{}_{3}\phi_{2}\left(\begin{matrix}q,&b/a,&\gamma d\\

{}_{3}\phi_{2}
transformation formula (
4.4

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}c^{j}\lim_{\gamma\rightarrow 0}\frac{(\gamma bq^{j})_{\infty}(aq^{j+1})_{\infty}}{(\gamma bq^{j+1})_{\infty}(aq^{j})_{\infty}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(ad/b)_{n}(1/\gamma)_{n}}{(d)_{n}(aq^{j+1})_{n}}(\gamma bq^{j})^{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}c^{j}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(ad/b)_{n}(-b)^{n}q^{{n\choose 2}+jn}}{(d)_{n}(aq^{j})_{n+1}}.

This completes the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

\alpha\in\mathbb{C}

f(z)=1/(1-z)^{\alpha}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}{\alpha+j-1\choose j}z^{j},

2.1
, where the binomial coefficient is defined as in (

), we can immediately obtain this result.

Replace

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(b/a)_{n}a^{n}}{(1-cq^{n})^{\alpha}(q)_{n}}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c^{j}{\alpha+j-1\choose j}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(aq/b)_{n}(-b)^{n}q^{{n\choose 2}+jn}}{(q)_{n}(aq^{j})_{n+1}}.

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{c^{j}}{1-aq^{j}}{\alpha+j-1\choose j}\lim_{\gamma\rightarrow 0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\frac{aq}{b}\right)_{n}\left(\frac{b}{\gamma}\right)_{n}}{(aq^{j+1})_{n}}\frac{(\gamma q^{j})^{n}}{(q)_{n}}.

Now applying

First, we define the following two generating functions for the sequence of polynomials

\displaystyle T(a,\alpha,q)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}t_{n}(a,q)\alpha^{n},

\displaystyle F(\alpha)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f(n)\alpha^{n}.

\displaystyle T(a,\alpha,q)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}(a/\alpha q^{n-1})_{n}F(\alpha q^{n-1})\alpha^{n-1}q^{\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}}}{(\alpha)_{n}}.

\displaystyle T(a,q,q)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}t_{n}(a,q)q^{n}

(5.22)

Utilizing (

\displaystyle t_{n}(a,q)-t_{n-1}(a,q)=f(n)-af(n+1)-q^{n-1}t_{n-1}(a,q)+aq^{n}t_{n}(a,q).

Taking summation over

6.
Concluding Remarks

One of the main goals of this paper was to study different generalizations of Uchimura’s identity (
1.3
). We named three sides of Uchimura’s identity (
1.3
) as
Uchimura-type
sum,
Ramanujan-type
sum and
divisor-type
sum. In view of these three sides, we tried to establish a unified theory for generalizations given by Uchimura (
1.9
), by the present authors (
1.11
), Dilcher (
1.12
), Andrews-Crippa-Simon (
1.13
), (
1.15
), and Gupta-Kumar (
1.17
), (
1.18
). We showed that all these generalizations can be obtained from our general identity, i.e., Theorem
2.1
. For example, at a glance, it may look as if Uchimura’s identity (
1.3
) and Dilcher’s identity (
1.12
) are not branches of a bigger tree, however, we have shown that both of these identities can be obtained from a single identity, namely, Corollary
2.5
.
We obtained a one-variable generalization of Gupta-Kumar’s identity (
1.18
) (see Theorem (
2.10
)), which is also a two variable generalization of the identity (
1.13
) of Andrews, Crippa and Simon.

Further, we established a
Ramanun-type
expression for our generalized identity (
1.11
), and as a by-product, we found a
Ramanujan-type
sum for Uchimura’s generalization (
1.9
) and also derived a
Uchimura-type
sum for Ramanujan’s identity (
1.1
).

We also corrected an identity (
2.3
) of Dilcher and while correcting it, we found two sequences of rational numbers involving Stirling numbers of the first kind, namely,
A(j,r,t)
(
2.5
) and
C(k,r,t)
(
2.10
). Quite interestingly, we observed that these numbers obey interesting recurrence relations. (see Theorem
2.7
).

In Section
3
, we discussed applications of the generalizations of Uchimura’s identities (
1.3
) in probability theory given by Uchimura and connection with theory of random acyclic digraphs developed by Simon-Crippa-Collenberg and Andrews-Crippa-Simon. We gave one variable generalization of an identity of Andrews-Crippa-Simon (see Theorem
3.2
), and a one variable generalization of an identity of Bringmann and Jennings-Shaffer (see Theorem
3.3
),
which is a corrected version of an identity of Gupta-Kumar.

Now we state a few problems that might be of interest to the readers of this article.

(1)

Note that Corollary
2.2
is a simultaneous generalization of Uchimura’s identity (
1.3
) and Dilcher’s identity (
1.12
). However it gives only the first equality. Thus, a natural question is that ‘what will be the
divisor-type
sum for Corollary
2.2
?’

(2)

Utilizing (
1.9
), Uchimura showed that, if
\textup{Pr}(X=n)=q^{n}(q^{n+1})_{\infty}
, then

\displaystyle E(X)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d(n)q^{n},\textup{Var}(X)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma(n)q^{n}.

More generally, he showed that, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle h_{m}=m\textrm{-th cumulant}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{m-1}(n)q^{n}.

(6.1)

Utilizing (
1.3
), Simon, Crippa and Collenberg established that, if
\text{Pr}(\gamma_{n}^{*}(1)=h)
is defined as in (
3.6
), then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(n-E(\gamma_{n}^{*}(1)\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d(n)q^{n},

Later, employing (
1.9
), Andrews, Crippa and Simon proved that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\text{Var}(\gamma_{n}^{*}(1))=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma(n)q^{n}.

Thus, it would be an interesting problem to know the interpretation of the generalized divisor generating function (
6.1
) with respect to the random variable
\gamma_{n}^{*}(1)
.

(3)

As an application of Uchimura’s identity (
1.9
), Andrews, Crippa and Simon proved the identity (
3.10
). In a similar way, as an application of Gupta-Kumar’s identity (
1.18
), we obtained Theorem
3.2
. In this paper, we obtained a one variable generalization of (
1.18
), i.e., Theorem
2.10
. Thus, it would be an exciting problem to find an application of Theorem
2.10
, which will generalize (
3.10
) as well as Theorem
3.2
.

(4)

By combining a result of van Hamme
[
20
]
and Guo and Zeng
[
18
, Equation (3.8)]
, we get a finite analogue of Uchimura’s identity (
1.3
), namely, for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nq^{n}(q^{n+1})_{N-1}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nq^{n+N}(q^{n+1})_{N-1}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}q^{{n+1\choose 2}}}{1-q^{n}}\left[\begin{matrix}N\\
n\end{matrix}\right]=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{q^{n}}{1-q^{n}}.

(6.2)

In this paper, we discussed various generalizations of Uchimura’s identity (
1.3
). Thus, it would be interesting to see finite analogues of these generalizations. Readers are encouraged to see
[
13
]
to know more about finite analogues of some of the results motivated from the above identity (
6.2
).
