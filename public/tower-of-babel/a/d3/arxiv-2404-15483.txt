Strategy Complexity of Büchi and Transience Objectives in Concurrent Stochastic Games
Stefan Kiefer, Richard Mayr, Mahsa Shirmohammadi, Patrick Totzke

Abstract
. We study 2-player stochastic games on countable graphs. Players Max and Min seek respectively to maximize and minimize the probability of satisfying the game objective. The Büchi objective is to visit a given set of states infinitely often. The Transience objective is to visit no state infinitely often. In Büchi games there exist ε \varepsilon -optimal Max strategies that use just a step counter plus 1 bit of public memory. This upper bound holds for all countable graphs, but is a new result even for finite graphs. It is tight, since Max strategies that use just a step counter, or just finite memory, are not sufficient even on finite game graphs. This upper bound follows from a slightly stronger new result: ε \varepsilon -optimal Max strategies for the combined Büchi and Transience objective require exactly 1 bit of public memory. Moreover, ε \varepsilon -optimal Max strategies for the Transience objective alone can be chosen as memoryless.

Introduction

Background

We study 2-player zero-sum concurrent (i.e., simultaneous move) stochastic games on
countable (finite or countably infinite) graphs.

Our proofs do not carry over to uncountable state spaces.
Apart from the usual issues with measurability in uncountable systems,
we also, e.g., partition events into as many parts as there are states
(in our case only countably many) and then rely on sigma-additivity.

Introduced by Shapley in his seminal 1953 work
(
Shapley, 1953
)
,
and generalized in
(
Gillette, 1958
)
and
(
Kumar and Shiau, 1981
)

to allow infinite state and action sets and non-termination,
such games play a central role in the solution of many problems
in
Evolutionary Biology
(
Raghavan et al., 2012
)
,
Computer Science
(
Bordais et al., 2022b
;
Bordais et al., 2022a
;
de Alfaro and Henzinger, 2001
;
Neyman and Sorin, 2003
;
Altman et al., 2005
;
Altman et al., 2007
;
Solan and Vieille, 2015
;
Svoreňová and Kwiatkowska, 2016
;
Bouyer et al., 2016
)
,
Economics
(
Sorin, 1992
;
Nowak and Szajowski, 2005
;
Jaskiewicz and Nowak, 2011
;
Solan and Vieille, 2015
;
Bacharach, 2019
)
,
and Operations Research

(
Mertens et al., 2009
;
Abdou et al., 2022
;
Solan and Vieille, 2001
;
Ponssard and Sorin, 1982
;
Osmani et al., 2026
;
Kiefer et al., 2026
)
.

General objectives are defined via measurable reward functions,
where the two players, Max and Min, aim to maximize (resp. minimize)
the reward of Max.
For the subclass of event-based objectives,
the reward function is the indicator function of some event
(a measurable set of plays), i.e., the players aim to maximize
(resp. minimize) the probability of this event.

The
Büchi objective
is an event-based objective where
Max aims to visit a given subset of the states
infinitely often.
It can be understood as a special case of the objective to maximize
the expected
\limsup
of the infinite sequence of daily rewards in a play.
If all daily rewards are in
\{0,1\}
then Max needs to maximize the
probability of visiting infinitely often the subset of the states with reward
1
.
(The term
Büchi objective
originates in automata theory, e.g.,
(
Grädel et al., 2002
)
.)

The
Transience
objective is to visit no state infinitely
often, i.e., every single state is visited at most finitely often (if at all).
This is equivalent to the condition that every
finite
subset of the
states is eventually left forever.
It can be interpreted as a notion of perpetual progress (pushing outward).

A central result in zero-sum 2-player stochastic games with finite action sets
is the existence of a
value
for the large class of Borel measurable
objectives
(
Martin, 1998
;
Maitra and Sudderth, 1998
)
.
That is, for Borel measurable functions
f
on plays,

\sup_{\it Max}\inf_{\it Min}f={\it value}=\inf_{\it Min}\sup_{\it Max}f

over Max and Min strategies.
This holds even under the weaker assumption that
one player has countable action sets and the other plays has finite action sets

(
Martin, 1998
)
(
Flesch et al., 2020
, Theorem 11)
.
Throughout this paper we assume that Max has countable action sets and Min has
finite action sets.
(While the reverse case also has value, our results do not hold for it; see below.)

The amount/type of memory and randomization required for an

\varepsilon
-optimal strategy for a
given player and objective is also called the
strategy complexity
.
One can also consider the strategy complexity of optimal strategies
in cases where optimal strategies exist.

Optimal Max strategies for Büchi objectives do not exist in general,
not even for the simpler reachability objective in finite-state concurrent games

(
de Alfaro et al., 1998
)
.
However, in finite-state concurrent Büchi games, if every state admits an
optimal Max strategy then
Max also has a memoryless strategy that is optimal from all states

(
Bordais et al., 2022a
, Proposition 17)
.
Optimal Max strategies for Transience do not exist even in countable
Markov decision processes (MDPs)

(
Kiefer et al., 2021
)
, and Transience trivially never holds in finite-state MDPs/games.
Thus we consider the strategy complexity of
\varepsilon
-optimal strategies.

Our contribution

We show (
Theorem

3.1
) that
\varepsilon
-optimal Max strategies for the Büchi objective in countable
concurrent stochastic games require just a step
counter plus 1 bit of public memory.
This is a new result even for the subclass of
finite-state
concurrent
stochastic games with finitely many actions,
where the strategy complexity of Büchi objectives had also been open.
(About the strategy complexity for the opposing Min player see
Section

7
.)

The step counter plus 1 bit upper bound on the strategy complexity
of
\varepsilon
-optimal Max strategies is tight, even for finite-state concurrent
Büchi games.
In the “Bad Match” game
(
Maitra and Sudderth, 1996
, Example 13.7)
,
both finite-memory strategies and Markov strategies (i.e., strategies that use just a step counter)
are worthless, i.e.,
they are not
\varepsilon
-optimal for Max for any
\varepsilon<1
; see
Section

4
.
(The fact that finite-memory Max strategies are worthless in finite-state
concurrent Büchi games was mentioned (without proof)
in
(
de Alfaro and Henzinger, 2000
)
, who considered a different
counterexample, namely a repeated version of
the classic “Snowball game” (aka Hide-or-Run game) of

(
Everett, 1957
, Example 1)
and
(
Kumar and Shiau, 1981
)
.)
In the special case of finite-state
turn-based

(aka alternating move)
Büchi games, the strategy
complexity is lower, because both players have optimal memoryless
deterministic strategies.
For countable MDPs, different counterexamples show that
neither finite-memory strategies nor Markov strategies can be

\varepsilon
-optimal for Max for the Büchi objective
for any
\varepsilon<1

(
Kiefer et al., 2019
)
.

Our upper bound (
Theorem

3.1
) is a consequence of a stronger new
result (
Theorem

3.2
) on the strategy complexity of the
combined
Büchi and
Transience objective in countable concurrent stochastic games with finite Min
action sets:

\varepsilon
-optimal Max strategies require just 1 bit of (public) memory.
This upper bound is tight, even for acyclic countable MDPs,
in the sense that memoryless Max strategies do not suffice
(
Kiefer et al., 2019
)
.
(Note that plays can only satisfy this combined objective if the
target set of the Büchi objective is infinite).
The stronger result on the combined Büchi and
Transience objective implies the result on the Büchi
objective above as follows.
We can apply the stronger result to a new derived acyclic game
where the step counter is encoded into the states (and thus Transience always
holds), and carry the obtained
1-bit Max strategy back to the original game as a step counter plus 1 bit
strategy; see
Proposition

6.9
.

Our upper bound on the strategy complexity of the combined Büchi and
Transience objective in countable concurrent stochastic games
generalizes a corresponding result for countable MDPs
(
Kiefer et al., 2021
, Lemma 4)
.
Similarly to
(
Kiefer et al., 2021
)
, 1 bit of public memory is used to
switch between two modes. Roughly speaking,
one mode aims to visit the target set in the short term
while the other mode records that this short-term
goal has been attained.
However, the proof techniques for stochastic games are very different from
those used for MDPs. Unlike in MDPs, Max cannot statically plan ahead which
states in the Büchi target set will be visited within a given time horizon with high
probability, because of the influence of Min’s decisions.
Similarly, for transience, Max cannot statically plan that certain states
are very unlikely to be visited again after a given time horizon.
Again it may depend on Min’s decisions whether certain states are (re-)visited
arbitrarily late in the game.
Therefore, our construction of the Max strategy is based on a different
principle.
It proceeds state by state (based on an enumeration of the states),
rather than phase by phase as was done for MDPs in
(
Kiefer et al., 2021
)
.

Our third result (
Theorem

3.3
) concerns the strategy complexity of the Transience objective
alone (which is only meaningful in infinite-state games).
There always exist memoryless
\varepsilon
-optimal Max strategies for Transience in
countable concurrent stochastic games.
This generalizes a corresponding result for countable MDPs

(
Kiefer et al., 2021
, Theorem 8)
. However, unlike for MDPs, these strategies cannot be
deterministic in concurrent games, i.e., they need to randomize.

Finally, our upper bounds do not carry over to countable stochastic
games with infinite Min action sets.
Even for the simpler reachability objective, there is
a counterexample where Max strategies that just use a
step counter plus finite (private) memory are not
\varepsilon
-optimal for any

\varepsilon<1
. In fact, the counterexample is a
countably infinite-state
turn-based
stochastic reachability
game with countably infinite Min branching and finite Max branching
(
Kiefer et al., 2024
)
.

2.
Preliminaries

A
probability distribution
over a countable set
S
is a function

\alpha:S\to[0,1]
with
\sum_{s\in S}\alpha(s)=1
.
The
support
of
\alpha
is the set
\{s\in S\mid\alpha(s)>0\}
.

\mathcal{D}(S)
is the set of all probability distributions over
S
.

Stochastic Games

We study stochastic games between two players, Max and Min.
A
(concurrent) game

{\mathcal{G}}
is played on a countable set of states
S
.
For each state
s\in S
there are non-empty countable
action
sets

A(s)
and
B(s)
for Max and Min, respectively.
A
mixed action
for Max (resp. Min) in state
s

is a distribution over
A(s)
(resp.
B(s)
).
Let
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}\bigcup_{s\in S}A(s)
and

B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}\bigcup_{s\in S}B(s)
.

Let
Z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}\{(s,a,b)\mid s\in S,\ a\in A(s),\ b\in B(s)\}
.
For every triple
(s,a,b)\in Z

there is a distribution
p(s,a,b)\in\mathcal{D}(S)
over successor states.
We call a state
s\in S
a
sink
state if

p(s,a,b)(s)=1
for all
a\in A(s)
and
b\in B(s)
.
We extend the
transition function

p
to mixed actions
\alpha\in\mathcal{D}(A(s))
and
\beta\in\mathcal{D}(B(s))
by letting

p(s,\alpha,\beta)\,\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}\sum_{a\in A(s)}\sum_{b\in B(s)}\alpha(a)\beta(b)p(s,a,b),

which is a distribution over
S
.
Let
s_{0}\in S
be an initial state.
A
play
from
s_{0}
is an infinite
sequence
(s_{0},a_{0},b_{0})(s_{1},a_{1},b_{1})\dots
in
Z^{\omega}
where

s_{n+1}
is in the support of
p(s_{n},a_{n},b_{n})
.
The game is played in steps,
where at every step
n\in\mathbb{N}
the play is in some state
s_{n}
,
and Max and Min independently and simultaneously choose mixed actions

\alpha_{n}\in\mathcal{D}(A(s_{n}))
and
\beta_{n}\in\mathcal{D}(B(s_{n}))
.
Then actions
a_{n}\in A
and
b_{n}\in B
are sampled according to
\alpha_{n}
and

\beta_{n}
, respectively, and the state
s_{n+1}\in S
is
chosen according to
p(s_{n},a_{n},b_{n})
,
before both players are informed of the outcomes

a_{n},b_{n},s_{n+1}
and the next step starts from
s_{n+1}
.
The game is called
turn-based
(or alternating move) if every

p(s,a,b)
either solely depends on
(s,a)
or solely on
(s,b)
,
i.e.,
for each
s
,
only the action of one player matters.

Strategies

We write
H_{n}
for the set of
histories
at step
n\in\mathbb{N}
, which is defined
as

H_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}S
and
H_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}Z^{n}\times S
for all
n>0
.
The set of all histories
is
H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}H_{n}
.
For each history
h=(s_{0},a_{0},b_{0})\cdots(s_{n-1},a_{n-1},b_{n-1})s_{n}\in H_{n}
,
let
s_{h}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}s_{n}
denote its final state.
Define the
length
of
h
by
\lvert h\rvert\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}n
.
For any history

h^{\prime}
starting in state
s_{h}
,
we write
hh^{\prime}

for the concatenation of the histories

(s_{0},a_{0},b_{0})\cdots(s_{n-1},a_{n-1},b_{n-1})h^{\prime}
.
A
strategy
for Max
is a function
\sigma
that to each history
h\in H
assigns
a mixed action
\sigma(h)\in\mathcal{D}(A(s_{h}))
.
It is called
deterministic
if
\sigma(h)
is a Dirac distribution for every history
h
.
Mixed actions and strategies
\pi
for Min are defined analogously.
We write
\Sigma

and
\Pi
for the sets of strategies for Max and Min, respectively.

Probability Measures and Optimality

An initial state
s_{0}
and a pair of strategies
\sigma,\pi

for Max and Min induce a probability measure
where the events, also called
objectives
here, are
measurable sets of plays
(see e.g.
(
Flesch et al., 2020
)
).
We write
{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathcal{G}},s_{0},\sigma,\pi}({E})
for the
probability of the event
E
starting from
s_{0}
.
(In the special case of MDPs with only one active player,
we only denote the strategy of that player,
\sigma
or
\pi
, resp.)
It is initially defined for the cylinder sets generated by the histories
and then extended to the sigma-algebra by Carathéodory’s
unique extension theorem
(
Billingsley, 1995
)
.
Given some Borel measurable reward function
v
on plays,
we will write
{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mathcal{G}},s_{0},\sigma,\pi}

for the expectation w.r.t.
{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathcal{G}},s_{0},\sigma,\pi}
and
v
.
We may drop
{\mathcal{G}}
from the subscript when it is understood.
For a game
{\mathcal{G}}
and objective
E
fixed,
the
lower value
and
upper value
of a state
s_{0}
are defined as

{\mathtt{val}^{\downarrow}_{{\mathcal{G}},E}(s_{0})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathcal{G}},s_{0},\sigma,\pi}({E})\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathtt{val}^{\uparrow}_{{\mathcal{G}},E}(s_{0})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathcal{G}},s_{0},\sigma,\pi}({E}).

The inequality
{\mathtt{val}^{\downarrow}_{{\mathcal{G}},E}(s_{0})}\leq{\mathtt{val}^{\uparrow}_{{\mathcal{G}},E}(s_{0})}
holds by definition.
If
{\mathtt{val}^{\downarrow}_{{\mathcal{G}},E}(s_{0})}={\mathtt{val}^{\uparrow}_{{\mathcal{G}},E}(s_{0})}
, then this quantity is called the

value
of
s_{0}
,
denoted by
{\mathtt{val}_{{\mathcal{G}},E}(s_{0})}
.
For all Borel objectives, the value exists if all action sets are finite,
and even if, for all states
s
, we have that
A(s)
is finite or
B(s)
is
finite
(
Martin, 1998
)
(
Flesch et al., 2020
, Theorem 11)
.
We always assume the latter,
so that
{\mathtt{val}_{{\mathcal{G}},E}(s_{0})}
exists.

For
\varepsilon\geq 0
, a Max strategy
\sigma
is called
\varepsilon
-optimal
from
s_{0}
if for all Min strategies
\pi
we have

{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathcal{G}},s_{0},\sigma,\pi}({E})\geq{{\mathtt{val}_{{\mathcal{G}},E}(s_{0})}-\varepsilon}.

It is called
multiplicatively
\varepsilon
-optimal
if
it satisfies

{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathcal{G}},s_{0},\sigma,\pi}({E})\geq{\mathtt{val}_{{\mathcal{G}},E}(s_{0})}(1-\varepsilon).

Clearly,
\sigma
is
\varepsilon
-optimal if it is multiplicatively
\varepsilon
-optimal.
A
0
-optimal strategy is also called
optimal
.

Objectives

Given a set
T\subseteq S
of target states, the
reachability
objective
\mathtt{Reach}(T)
is the set of plays that visit
T
at least once, i.e.,
s_{h}\in T
holds for some history
h
that is a prefix of the play.
For
Q\subseteq T
, the objective
\mathtt{Reach}_{Q}(T)
additionally requires that all states in

h

before
the visit to
T
are in
Q
.
The dual
safety
objective
\mathtt{Avoid}(T)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}Z^{\omega}\setminus\mathtt{Reach}(T)
consists of the plays that never visit
T
.
We are mainly interested in
Büchi
objectives
\mathtt{Bu}(T)

that consist of all those plays that visit the set of target states

T\subseteq S
infinitely often.
(If the set
T
is infinite then some plays that satisfy
\mathtt{Bu}(T)

might not visit any particular state in
T
infinitely often.)
Whenever possible, we will assume a fixed target set
T
and simply write
\mathtt{Reach},\mathtt{Avoid}
and
\mathtt{Bu}
for these objectives.
The
transience
objective
\mathtt{Tr}
contains those plays in which every state of the game is eventually avoided.
That is, a play

(s_{0},a_{0},b_{0})(s_{1},a_{1},b_{1})\cdots\in Z^{\omega}

is in

\mathtt{Tr}
if for every
s\in S
there exist
n_{s}
so that
s\neq s_{i}
for
i\geq n_{s}
.
The
transient Büchi
objective is
\mathtt{TB}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}\mathtt{Tr}\cap\mathtt{Bu}
.
Clearly,
\mathtt{TB}
can only be met if
T
is infinite.
An event
E\subseteq Z^{\omega}
is called
shift-invariant
iff

(s_{0},a_{0},b_{0})(s_{1},a_{1},b_{1})\cdots\in E\ \Leftrightarrow\ (s_{i},a_{i},b_{i})(s_{i+1},a_{i+1},b_{i+1})\cdots\in E
for any
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
i.e., a finite prefix is removed.
Note that the events
\mathtt{Bu},\mathtt{Tr},\mathtt{TB}
are all shift-invariant.

Strategy Classes

We formalize the amount of
memory
needed to implement strategies.
A Max (analogously Min) strategy is
based on
a set
M

of
memory modes
if it can be described by an
initial mode
m_{0}\in M
and a
pair of functions

{\sigma}_{\mathop{act}\nolimits}:S\times M\to\mathcal{D}(A)
and

{\sigma}_{\mathop{up}\nolimits}:S\times M\times A\times B\times S\to\mathcal{D}(M)
,
selecting mixed actions and
updating the memory mode, respectively.
For any history

h=(s_{0},a_{0},b_{0})\cdots(s_{n-1},a_{n-1},b_{n-1})s_{n}\in H_{n}
,

\sigma(h)={\sigma}_{\mathop{act}\nolimits}(s_{n},m_{n})
and

m_{n+1}
is drawn randomly according to
the distribution

{\sigma}_{\mathop{up}\nolimits}(s_{n},m_{n},a_{n},b_{n},s_{n+1}).

For
M
-based strategy
\sigma
and
m\in M
we will write
\sigma[m]
to denote the strategy with
{\sigma}_{\mathop{act}\nolimits}
and
{\sigma}_{\mathop{up}\nolimits}
as in
\sigma
but with initial mode
m_{0}=m
.

A memory-based strategy is
public
if at each step of the game, its current memory mode is known to the opponent,
and
private
otherwise.
Note that if all memory updates
{\sigma}_{\mathop{up}\nolimits}(.)
are Dirac then
\sigma
is
public, since the opponent can keep track of its memory.
A strategy
\sigma
is
memoryless

if it is based on a singleton set
M
, i.e.,
\sigma(h)=\sigma(h^{\prime})
for any two histories
h,h^{\prime}
with
s_{h}=s_{h^{\prime}}
.
A strategy is
1-bit
iff

M=\{0,1\}
.
A
step counter
is a special case of infinite memory in the form of a discrete
clock (by default starting as
0
) that gets incremented by
1
at every step, independently of the actions of the
players. Strategies that use just a step counter are also called
Markov
strategies.
A strategy
\sigma
is
1-bit Markov
if it is based on a step counter
and two extra memory modes, i.e.,
M=\mathbb{N}\times\{0,1\}

and at any step
n
the update function
{\sigma}_{\mathop{up}\nolimits}
guarantees that the mode
m_{n}\in\{n\}\times\{0,1\}
.

The Main Results

Our results concern the memory requirement of Max strategies for
the Büchi (
\mathtt{Bu}
), Transience (
\mathtt{Tr}
) and Transient Büchi (
\mathtt{TB}
) objectives in concurrent games.

Throughout the paper,
{\mathcal{G}}
refers to a concurrent 2-player game with a countable state space
S
,
where Max has countable action sets and Min has finite action sets.

Theorem 3.1.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be a game with Büchi objective and
\varepsilon>0

\sigma

(1)

\sigma[0]

\varepsilon

2
2

2

The strategy is multiplicatively
\varepsilon

0
.

and

(2)

all memory updates
{\sigma}_{\mathop{up}\nolimits}(\cdot)

Moreover, if
{\mathcal{G}}

The 1-bit Markov upper bound
for the Büchi objective of

,

(

(

.
We re-visit some of these lower bounds in

Our strongest result is that for transient Büchi objectives:
Max has public 1-bit strategies that are uniformly
\varepsilon

\varepsilon

7.
Strategy Complexity for Minimizer

We have shown that
\varepsilon
-optimal Max strategies in countable concurrent
Büchi games with finite Min action sets
require just a step counter plus 1 bit of public memory
(
Theorem

3.1
), but cannot use less
memory in general, even if the game is finite (
Section

4
).

This upper bound does not hold for the strategy complexity of the opposing Min player.
From player Min’s point of view, the objective is to maximize the probability
of satisfying the dual
co-Büchi

objective of visiting the set of target states only
finitely
often.
I.e., Min in a Büchi game is the same as Max in a co-Büchi game.
However, a step counter plus arbitrary finite (private) memory are
not

sufficient for
\varepsilon
-optimal Max strategies for the co-Büchi objective
in countable concurrent games. In fact, this lower bound holds even for
countable finitely branching
turn-based
co-Büchi games
(
Kiefer et al., 2024
, Remark 1)
.
On the other hand, in
finite-state
concurrent games,

\varepsilon
-optimal Max strategies for the co-Büchi objective can be chosen memoryless randomized

(
de Alfaro and Henzinger, 2000
)
, and this holds even for the
more general
\liminf
objective
(
Secchi, 1998
)
.

Optimal
Max strategies for the co-Büchi objective
need not exist, but might require memory if they do.

Bordais et al.
(
Bordais et al., 2022a
, Section 6)

give an example of a finite-state co-Büchi game where optimal Max strategies
exist but require infinite memory.

For the Transience objective,
\varepsilon
-optimal Max strategies can be chosen memoryless (
Theorem

3.3
),
but the strategy complexity is much higher for the opposing Min player.
From player Min’s point of view, the objective is to maximize the probability
of satisfying the dual
Recurrence

objective
\overline{\mathtt{Tr}}
of visiting some state infinitely often.
I.e., Min in a Transience game is the same as Max in a Recurrence game.
The main problem is that, in a Recurrence game with an infinite number of
states, Max cannot commit to visiting
any
particular
state (or any particular finite subset of the states) infinitely
often, because that might be under the opposing player’s control.
(Recurrence holds trivially if the game has only finitely many states.)
Even in countably infinite finitely branching turn-based Recurrence games,
Max strategies that use just a step counter plus arbitrary finite (private)
memory are worthless. This can be shown along similar lines as for
turn-based finitely branching co-Büchi
games in
(
Kiefer et al., 2024
, Remark 1)
.

Figure 5.
A reduction of infinite Min branching to finite Min branching, provided
that Min is trying to avoid recurrence.
(Adapted from
(
Kiefer et al., 2021
, Figure 3)
.)

Theorem 7.1.

There exists a countably infinite finitely branching turn-based game
{\mathcal{G}}
with initial
state
s_{0}
with the recurrence objective
\overline{\mathtt{Tr}}
such
that Max can win almost surely from
s_{0}
, but
every Max strategy
\sigma
that uses only a step counter plus arbitrary finite private
memory is worthless, i.e.,

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathcal{G}},s_{0},\sigma,\pi}(\overline{\mathtt{Tr}})=0
.

Proof.

Proof.
By
(
Kiefer et al., 2024
, Theorem 15)
, there exists a countably infinite turn-based
stochastic game
{\mathcal{G}}
, finitely branching for Max but infinitely branching for Min,
such that, for the reachability objective
\mathtt{Reach}(\{f\})
,
Max can win almost surely from
s_{0}
,
but every Max strategy
\sigma
that uses only a step counter plus arbitrary finite private
memory is worthless, i.e.,

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathcal{G}},s_{0},\sigma,\pi}(\mathtt{Reach}(\{f\}))=0
.

It follows immediately that
{\mathcal{G}}
can be modified such that the step counter
from
s_{0}
is encoded into the states, without affecting the properties
above. This modification makes the game acyclic.

We now modify the game further by making the target state
f
a sink with a
self-loop, and call the new game
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}
.
In
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}
a play is recurrent if and only if it reaches the state
f
, i.e.,
the objectives
\overline{\mathtt{Tr}}
and
\mathtt{Reach}(\{f\})
coincide.
Hence Max can win
\overline{\mathtt{Tr}}
almost surely from
s_{0}
,
but every Max strategy
\sigma
that uses only a step counter plus arbitrary finite private
memory is worthless, i.e.,

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime},s_{0},\sigma,\pi}(\overline{\mathtt{Tr}})=0
.

The only remaining problem is that Min has infinite branching in
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}
.
We now modify
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}
to obtain a finitely branching game
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime\prime}
with the
same properties.
To this end, we encode every infinite Min branching of the form

s\to s_{i}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
into binary branching by using the
gadget depicted in
Figure

5
. It closely resembles
the classic Gambler’s ruin with restarts and a
1/2
chance of winning.
Unless Min chooses some step
l_{i}\to s_{i}
, every state
l_{i}
will
be visited infinitely often with probability
1
.
This makes it possible for Min to choose any state
s_{i}
as before.
On the other hand, except for a nullset, all plays that don’t exit the
gadget are recurrent. So Min is forced to leave the gadget eventually,
or else she concedes the game to Max.

The last obstacle is that the gadget of
Figure

5
does
not preserve path lengths. I.e., the step counter in the new game is different,
and thus one cannot immediately carry step-counter-plus-finite-memory strategies between the games.

However, by the proof of
(
Kiefer et al., 2024
, Theorem 15)
, the only feature
required to negate the usefulness of the step counter for Max is the following.
Immediately before every Max decision, Min gets to choose
an arbitrary but finite delay.
In
(
Kiefer et al., 2024
, Theorem 15)
, Min chooses this delay via a gadget with
infinite branching. However, in the context of our recurrence objective,
the same can be encoded much simpler by giving Min an extra state with a
self-loop just before every Max-state. So Min can delay for as long as she
likes, but she cannot stay in such a self-loop forever, because that would be
recurrent and concede the game to Max.
The proof that this modified game
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime\prime}
has the required properties
is thus almost identical to the proof of
(
Kiefer et al., 2024
, Theorem 15)
.
