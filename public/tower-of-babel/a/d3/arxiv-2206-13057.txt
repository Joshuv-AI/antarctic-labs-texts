Beating Greedy Matching in Sublinear Time
Soheil Behnezhad, Mohammad Roghani, Aviad Rubinstein, Amin Saberi

Abstract
We study sublinear time algorithms for estimating the size of maximum matching in graphs. Our main result is a ( 1 2 + Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) ) (\frac{1}{2}+\Omega(1)) -approximation algorithm which can be implemented in O ⁡ ( n 1 + ε ) O(n^{1+\varepsilon}) time, where n n is the number of vertices and the constant ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 can be made arbitrarily small. The best known lower bound for the problem is Ω ⁡ ( n ) \Omega(n) , which holds for any constant approximation. Existing algorithms either obtain the greedy bound of 1 2 \frac{1}{2} -approximation [Behnezhad FOCS’21], or require some assumption on the maximum degree to run in o ⁡ ( n 2 ) o(n^{2}) -time [Yoshida, Yamamoto, and Ito STOC’09]. We improve over these by designing a less “adaptive” augmentation algorithm for maximum matching that might be of independent interest.

Introduction

Linear-time algorithms have long been considered the gold standard in algorithm design. With the rapid increase in the size of data, however, even linear-time algorithms may be slow in some settings. A natural question is whether it is possible to solve a problem of interest in
sublinear time
in the input size. That is, without even reading the whole input. In this work, we focus on the problem of estimating the size of
maximum matching
in sublinear time. Recall that a
matching
is a set of edges no two of which share an endpoint, and a

is a matching of the largest size. This is a central problem in the study of sublinear time algorithms and several general techniques of the area have emerged from the study of matchings

There is a simple greedy algorithm for constructing a

1/2

\Omega(n^{2})
edges one by one where
n

\Omega(n^{2})
time is necessary to
find
any

-approximate matching. Nonetheless, approximating the

Indeed, a beautiful line of work in the literature

led to an

Unfortunately, the main shortcoming of the greedy maximal matching algorithm is that it only provides a
1/2

[
9
]

[

1/2

\Omega(n^{2})

Question 1

Is it possible to

-approximate maximum matching size in

We remark that this question has been open even for bipartite graphs.

In this work, we answer

On Beating Greedy Matching in Various Settings

The greedy 1/2-approximation is a prevalent barrier for maximum matching across various settings. As a result, numerous works in the literature study the possibility of beating it — both on the upper bound side as well as the lower bound side.
The answer is not always the same. For instance, for the online model under
edge arrivals
,
[
13
]
showed that 1/2 is provably the best achievable approximation, which can be trivially matched by the greedy algorithm.
There are also settings where the answer remains unknown, despite a significant research effort. For instance, in the single-pass streaming setting, beating the greedy 1/2-approximation in
\widetilde{O}(n)
(or even subquadratic) space has been open for nearly two decades
[
11
]
, and is often considered as one of the most fundamental open problems of the area. Finally, there are settings for which the greedy 1/2-approximation has been broken. Various models of the online setting
[
10
,
13
]
, the random-order streaming setting
[
16
]
, and the stochastic matching setting
[
2
]
are examples of this. In many of these settings, the approximation has been improved well beyond 1/2 after the greedy bound was first broken. For instance, in the random-order streaming the current best known bound is slightly above 2/3
[
1
]
, and in the stochastic matching setting a
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximation has been achieved
[
3
]
. We hope that our work in this paper also inspires future work on going tangibly above 1/2-approximation in the sublinear time model.

Discovering short
augmenting paths
has been a central technique in many of the works discussed above in beating the greedy algorithm. What varies significantly is whether it is possible to find these augmenting paths effeciently in the particular model at hand. In particular, a common approach is to first construct a maximal matching in full, and then augment it via the vertices left unmatched. This “adaptivity” complicates things in our model, making it hard to estimate the size of the solution in subquadratic time. One of our main contributions in this work is to give a less “adaptive” algorithm that interleaves the construction of a maximal matching and the augmentation phase. We believe this technique, which is overviewed in
section

2
, might be of independent interest.

Consider a maximal matching

2.2
Our Contribution: A Less “Adaptive” Augmentation Algorithm

The two-stage algorithm we discussed earlier, constructs
M
fully and then adaptively picks the edges of
S
based on
M
. Our first step towards proving
theorem

1.1
is introducing a less “adaptive” algorithm that interleaves the construction of the two matchings
M
and
S
.

Our algorithm starts by constructing a sequence
T
, containing a single element
(e,\textsc{Extend})
and
K\geq 1
distinct elements
(e,\textsc{Start})
, corresponding to every edge

in the graph, where
K
is a parameter of the algorithm. We will process
T
in a
random order
. The role of having multiple copies of the
Start
elements is to bias them to appear earlier in the random permutation. We start by initializing two empty matchings
M
and
S
and then iterate over these randomly sorted
m(K+1)
elements of
T
. Whenever we see an
(e,\textsc{Start})
element, we add
e
to
M
iff both endpoints of
e
are unmatched in
M
. Therefore,

will be a random greedy maximal matching of
G
. Whenever we see an
(e,\textsc{Extend})
element, we add

to

under a few conditions. The first condition is that both endpoints of

must be yet unmatched by
S
; this is to ensure that

continues to be a matching. The second condition is that at most one endpoint of

can be already matched by
M
. Our final algorithm, formalized as
section

4.1
, also checks two more technical conditions before adding

to
S
which are needed for the approximation ratio analysis. Once all of

is processed, the algorithm returns a maximum matching of
M\cup S
.

Observe that even though at the time of adding an edge
(u,v)
to
S
, at most one of its endpoints is matched in
M
, the other endpoint may get matched in
M
later in the process. Such edges of
S
cannot be used in length-3 augmenting paths for

. One way to avoid these bad events is to set
K
large enough so that all the
Start
elements appear before all

elements. However, this will negatively impact the running time. Specifically, the local query process to determine whether a random vertex

is matched in either of

\widetilde{O}(\bar{d}\cdot K)

1.1

When

, we will inevitably have many edges of

So far we have only described an algorithm that finds a
(\frac{1}{2}+\Omega(1))
-approximate matching and have not yet described how to estimate its output size in sublinear time. To do this, we first define a query process akin to the one described above for maximal matching. That is, for a given edge

of its incident edges (one of its

calls during the course of

. Therefore,

Q(\ell,\pi)\leq\sum_{u\in V}Q(\ell,u,\pi)\leq n(4n-3)\leq O(n^{2}).\qed

The main contribution of this section is to show that the expected

for a random permutation
\pi
is
O(\log^{4}n)
which is formalized in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.8
.

For any element
\ell\in T
, we have
\E_{\pi}[Q(\ell,\pi)]=O(\log^{4}n)
.

Assuming the correctness of
Lemma

Proof of
Theorem

5.2
.

\displaystyle\E_{v,\pi}[F(v,\pi)]=\frac{1}{n}\E_{\pi}\left[\sum_{v\in V}F(v,\pi)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\E_{\pi}\left[\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{\ell\in T}Q(\ell,v,\pi)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\E_{\pi}\left[\sum_{\ell\in T}\sum_{v\in V}Q(\ell,v,\pi)\right]=\frac{1}{n}\E_{\pi}\left[\sum_{\ell\in T}Q(\ell,\pi)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell\in T}\E_{\pi}[Q(\ell,\pi)]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell\in T}O(\log^{4}n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}O(Km\cdot\log^{4}n)=O(K\bar{d}\cdot\log^{4}n).\qed

During the recursive calls to the edge oracles that start from

towards the other endpoint. We call such a directed path starting from the bottom of the stack all the way to the top, a

-query-path. For an edge

denote the directed edge from

to

denote a directed edge from

and

\vec{\reflectbox{$\ell$}}

represent the direction of

Let

be the set of all query paths that end at
\vec{\ell}
(with the same direction). In the following lemma, we bound the query complexity based on the direction of
\ell
. We use this lemma to prove
Lemma

.

Lemma 5.9
.

For any element
\ell\in T
, we have
\E_{\pi}[Q(\vec{\ell},\pi)]=O(\log^{4}n)
.

Proof of
Lemma

5.8
.

Since

\E_{\pi}[Q(\ell,\pi)]\leq\E_{\pi}[Q(\vec{\ell},\pi)]+\E_{\pi}[Q(\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$\ell$}}$},\pi)]=O(\log^{4}n)+O(\log^{4}n)=O(\log^{4}n).\qed

Given a permutation

\displaystyle(\sigma(\ell_{1}),\sigma(\ell_{2}),\ldots,\sigma(

\displaystyle\ell_{r-1}),\sigma(\ell_{r}))\coloneqq(\pi(\ell_{2}),\pi(\ell_{3}),\ldots,\pi(\ell_{r}),\pi(\ell_{1}))

\displaystyle\pi(\ell^{\prime})=\sigma(\ell^{\prime})\quad\quad\forall\ell^{\prime}\notin\vec{P}.

with two parts

and

Let

be the set of all query-paths for permutation

that ends at

\displaystyle L\coloneqq\left\{\pi\in\Pi\,\,\Big\lvert\,\,\max_{\vec{P}\in\mathcal{Q}(\vec{\ell},\pi)}|\vec{P}|\leq\beta\right\}\qquad\qquad U\coloneqq\Pi\setminus L.

.

If

.

Furthermore, we show that each vertex

neighbors among the likely permutations in part

Lemma 5.12
.

Let

be a vertex in

.

Proof of
Lemma

.

We provide an upper bound on

. First, note that by

. Furthermore, by

, degree of each vertex

which implies that the total number of edges incident to vertices of

Moreover, by

8
Conclusion

We presented a new algorithm for finding a
(\frac{1}{2}+\Omega(1))
-approximate maximum matching and showed how its output size can be estimated in sublinear time. The algorithm gives a multiplicative
(\frac{1}{2}+\Omega(1))
-approximation of the size of maximum matching in the adjacency list model that runs in
\widetilde{O}(n+\Delta^{1+\varepsilon})=O(n^{1+\varepsilon})
time, where constant
\varepsilon>0
can be made arbitrarily small. This is the first algorithm that beats half-approximation in
o(n^{2})
time for all graphs.
