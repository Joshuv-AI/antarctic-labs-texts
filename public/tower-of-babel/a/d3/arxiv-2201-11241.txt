Stochastic Local Volatility models and the Wei-Norman factorization method
Julio Guerrero, Giuseppe Orlando

Abstract
In this paper, we show that a time-dependent local stochastic volatility (SLV) model can be reduced to a system of autonomous PDEs that can be solved using the Heat kernel, by means of the Wei-Norman factorization method and Lie algebraic techniques. Then, we compare the results of traditional Monte Carlo simulations with the explicit solutions obtained by said techniques. This approach is new in the literature and, in addition to reducing a non-autonomous problem into an autonomous one, allows for reduced time in numerical computations.

Introduction

Stochastic local volatility (SLV) models lie in the conjunction of stochastic volatility and local volatility models. Stochastic volatility denotes the case in which the volatility of the underlying process is stochastic. Well known examples of those models are Hull and White (1987)
[
18
]
, Stein and Stein (1991)
[
24
]
, Heston (1993)
[
17
]
, etc.

The economic reason behind stochastic volatility models is linked to: a) the fact that equity of a firm can be seen "as the net present value of all its future income plus its assets minus its debt. These constituents have very different relative volatilities which gives rise to a leverage related skew"
[
20
]
; b) higher demand for downwards risk insurance because of intrinsic asymmetry of positions in equity (which is mostly held long than short); c) asymmetric effects of changes in prices on portfolio rebalancing (e.g. stop-loss condition and regulatory limits).

Local volatility models were initially developed for daily traded options as the arbitrage-free forward volatilities were inferred and ’locked-in’ by market quotes
[
1
]
. Early examples can be found in Dupire (1994)
[
14
]
, Derman and Kani (1994)
[
10
]
and Rubinstein (1994)
[
23
]
. Subsequently, by "local volatility" has been indicated any deterministic volatility model in which forward volatilities are a function of both time and the underlying.

Both, stochastic volatility and local volatility models compete for explaining from different angles the instantaneous volatility of the underlying which is unobservable. However, in terms of pricing and hedging contingent claims the two frameworks yield identical results
[
1
]
.
A difference between the two approaches lies in the way in which the absence of static or dynamic arbitrage is demonstrated. However, while for the stochastic (implied) volatility
framework the no-arbitrage condition requires specific constraints on the state probability space that are difficult to meet
[
5
]

[
9
]
, "the derivation of no-arbitrage conditions in a stochastic local volatility framework is relatively straightforward because the static no-arbitrage condition is simply that local volatilities are non-negative and the admissible state space is relatively simple"
[
1
]
.

As mentioned, SLV models provide a general framework combining practical features from stochastic volatility (SV) and local volatility (LV) models
[
7
]
. In this respect, one of the most prominent models is the Stochastic Alpha Beta Rho (SABR) model introduced by Hagan et al. (2002)
[
16
]
, because provides closed-form asymptotic implied volatility formulas. In addition, the SABR model leads to "fast and accurate pricing, hedging, and calibration for European-style derivatives with moderate duration" and
predicts the "co-movements between smile dynamics and the underlying
(a forward price or forward rate), which improves upon previous models"
[
7
]
.
Notwithstanding the many appealing features, SLV models pose a major challenge when it comes to the simulation of the two stochastic processes of the state variables due to the nonlinear local volatility structure.

To date the literature on the SLV models falls into either in Monte Carlo methods (e.g. Chen et al. (2012)
[
6
]
, Leitao et al. (2017)
[
22
]
) or in asymptotic probability density approximations (e.g. Hagan et al. (2002)
[
16
]
, Armstrong et al. (2017)
[
2
]
, Barger and Lorig (2017)
[
4
]
).

In this work, we contribute to the literature by using Lie algebraic methods to reduce a time-dependent SLV model to a system of autonomous PDEs that can be solved using appropriate kernels that can be derived from the usual Heat kernel.
Then, we take the explicit solutions obtained by Lie algebraic techniques and compare them with Monte Carlo simulations. The advantage of this approach lies in reducing a non-autonomous problem into various autonomous ones and in saving numerical calculations. Finally, we show how the obtained prices can be used for deriving the implied volatility.

2
SLV model

Local volatility (LV) models, first proposed by Dupire (1994)
[
14
]
and Derman et al. (1996)
[
11
]
, are of the type

dS_{t}=\mu S_{t}dt+\sigma_{LV}(S_{t},t)S_{t}dW_{t}

(1)

where
S_{t}
is the price of the underlying,
\mu
the drift,
\sigma_{LV}
the volatility, and
W_{t}
a Wiener process.

The advantage of these models is that, in an arbitrage-free setting, they provide a perfect fit to the implied volatility surface through Dupire’s formula.
The disadvantage of LV models is that the implied volatility shape soon flattens out as the maturity increases. Therefore, the longer the maturity, the less the resemblance to the near-term smile.

Stochastic volatility (SV) models are not affected by that problem and they are of the type

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}dS_{t}&=&S_{t}\mu dt+m(v_{t})S_{t}dW_{t}^{(1)}\\
dv_{t}&=&\mu(v_{t})dt+\sigma(v_{t})dW_{t}^{(2)}\end{array}\right.

(2)

with
\mathbb{E}[dW_{t}^{(1)}dW_{t}^{(2)}]=\rho\>dt
, where
W_{t}^{(1)}
and
dW_{t}^{(2)}
are two correlated Wiener processes through the correlation coefficient
\rho\in(-1,1)
.

Given the fact that the implied volatility surface generated from such models generally preserves the near-term smile shape, future smiles and term structure are more realistic but they are difficult to calibrate
[
8
]
.

Stochastic Alpha Beta Rho (SABR) model was first introduced by Hagan et al. (2002)
[
16
]
when they observed that market smiles and skews obtained by local volatility models produced a market smile opposite of observed market behaviour i.e. "when the price of the underlying decreases, local vol models predict that the smile shifts to higher prices; when the price increases, these models predict that the smile shifts to lower prices"
[
16
]
.
The SABR model, as initially proposed, is

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}dS_{t}&=&v_{t}S_{t}^{\beta}dW_{t}^{(1)}\\
dv_{t}&=&\alpha\,v_{t}dW_{t}^{(2)}\end{array}\right.

(3)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are some parameters and, as well as with the stochastic volatility framework, the two Wiener processes are linked through the correlation coefficient
\rho
. However as the "stochastic alpha beta rho model for interest rate derivatives was designed for an environment of 5% base rates", "its traditional implementation method based on a lognormal volatility expansion breaks down in today’s low-rate and high-volatility environment, returning nonsensical negative probabilities and arbitrage"
[
3
]
. For this reason a number of variants have been proposed to date (e.g. Doust (2012)
[
13
]
, Balland et al. (2013)
[
3
]
, Van der Stoep (2014)
[
26
]
, etc.).

Stochastic local volatility (SLV) models combine desirable features from both stochastic volatility models and local volatility models and enable a closer fit to the
volatility surface of term structures (for an introduction see
[
20
]
[
12
]
). SLV models are a generalization of the SABR ones and they are expressed as

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}dS_{t}&=&\omega(S_{t},v_{t})dt+m(v_{t})\Gamma(S_{t})dW_{t}^{(1)}\\
dv_{t}&=&\mu(v_{t})dt+\sigma(v_{t})dW_{t}^{(2)}\end{array}\right.

(4)

with
\mathbb{E}[dW_{t}^{(1)}dW_{t}^{(2)}]=\rho\>dt
, where
\rho\in(-1,1)

Define the pricing operator
P_{t}\Phi(S,v)=\mathbb{E}[\Phi(S_{t},v_{t})|S_{0}=S,v_{0}=v]

The family

{\cal L}^{S}

{\cal L}^{S}\Phi(S,v):=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{P_{t}\Phi(S,v)-\Phi(S,v)}{t}

From Eq. (
4

7
]

{\cal L}^{S}\Phi=\frac{(m(v)\Gamma(S))^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi}{\partial S^{2}}+\rho\>\Gamma(S)\sigma(v)\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi}{\partial S\partial v}+\frac{\sigma(v)^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi}{\partial v^{2}}+\omega(S,v)\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial S}+\mu(v)\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial v}

(6)

According to Îto’s lemmas, any function
\Phi(S_{t},v_{t})
of the stochastic variables
S_{t}
and
v_{t}
verifying the two-dimensional
time-homogeneous diffusion system (
4

\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t}={\cal L}^{S}\Phi

In the following, we shall work on the setting of the PDE (

4

To solve the PDE (
7
)
we shall use the Wei-Norman factorization method

[

The price to be paid by such a method is that it can be only applied when

\displaystyle\frac{(m(v)\Gamma(S))^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\ }{\partial S^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho\>\Gamma(S)\sigma(v)\frac{\partial^{2}\ }{\partial S\partial v}

\displaystyle K_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma(v)^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\ }{\partial v^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega(S)\frac{\partial\ }{\partial S}

\displaystyle K_{5}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu(v)\frac{\partial\ }{\partial v}

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}+\sigma_{1}v

g_{i}(t,t_{0})=t-t_{0}
),
\Delta^{(0)}=(m_{0}^{2}-\rho^{2})(t-t_{0})^{2}
and
this restriction reduces to

|\rho|<m_{0}
. Eventually,
m_{0}
can be absorbed in
\Gamma_{1}
and then we obtain the more standard restriction
|\rho|<1
.

Note that the expression of the kernel is exact and valid for time dependent coefficients (making use of equations (
16
)).

Price function for the case

) with initial condition

being the strike price and

) (using the variables (

)) and assuming
v\approx v_{0}
the following (independent of
X_{v}
) Price function is obtained:

\displaystyle V^{0}(t,t_{0},X_{S},K)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-\frac{(2k_{\mu}g_{2}(t,t_{0})+2\Gamma_{1}\rho g_{3}(t,t_{0})-\sigma_{1}g_{5}(t,t_{0}))^{2}}{8g_{5}(t,t_{0})}}}{2\sqrt{2\pi}\Gamma_{1}\sigma_{1}\sqrt{g_{5}(t,t_{0})}}\left(e^{X_{S}+\Gamma_{1}k_{\omega}g_{1}(t,t_{0})}\left(1+\text{Erf}(d_{1}+d_{2})\right)\right.

(28)

\displaystyle\left.-e^{X_{K}+\frac{\Gamma_{1}\rho g_{3}(t,to)(2k_{\mu}g_{2}(t,t_{0})+\Gamma_{1}\rho g_{3}(t,t_{0})-\sigma_{1}g_{5}(t,t_{0}))}{2g_{5}(t,t_{0})}}\left(1-\text{Erf}(d_{1}-d_{2})\right)\right)

where

\displaystyle d_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{g_{5}(t,t_{0})(2X_{K}-2X_{S}-2\Gamma_{1}k_{\omega}g_{1}(t,t_{0})-\Gamma_{1}\rho\sigma_{1}g_{3}(t,t_{0}))+\Gamma_{1}\rho g_{3}(t,t_{0})(2k_{\mu}g_{2}(t,t_{0})+\Gamma_{1}\rho g_{3}(t,t_{0}))}{2\sqrt{2}\Gamma_{1}\sqrt{g_{5}(t,t_{0})\Delta^{(0)}}}

\displaystyle d_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\Delta^{(0)}}{2g_{5}(t,t_{0})}}

(29)

6
Explicit computation of the kernels for the case
c=\pm 1

As in the case
c=0
, it is helpful to perform a change of variables, which in this case is:

\displaystyle X_{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2}{c}}m_{0}\Gamma_{1}\textrm{Erf}^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2c}{\pi}}\frac{S-\Gamma_{0}}{m_{0}\Gamma_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle X_{v}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log(\sigma_{0}+\sigma_{1}v)

(30)

contrarily to the case
c=0
, in the case
c=\pm 1
the allowed values of the variable
S
(and
K
) are restricted to a finite interval

S\in[\Gamma_{0}-\Delta S,\Gamma_{0}+\Delta S]
, with
\Delta S=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2c}}m_{0}\Gamma_{1}
. Notice that the said boundary may suit those option’s strategies such as butterfly or iron condor where the positive pay-off is limited between two values
[
25
]
.

In the new variables, the differential operators (
10
) have the same expressions as in (
25
), except for the case of
K_{1}
, which now is written as:

K_{1}=\frac{m_{0}^{2}\Gamma_{1}^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\ }{\partial X_{S}^{2}}-c\frac{X_{S}}{m_{0}^{2}\Gamma_{1}^{2}}\frac{\partial\ }{\partial X_{S}}\right)

(31)

The kernels are also the same (but in the new variables (
30
)) as for the case
c=0
, except for
k_{1}
, which now reads:

k_{1}(t,X_{S},X_{v},X_{S}^{\prime},X_{v}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}m_{0}\Gamma_{1}\sqrt{e^{ct/2}\frac{\textrm{sinh}(ct/2)}{c/2}}}e^{-\frac{(e^{ct/2}X_{S}-X_{S}^{\prime})^{2}}{2m_{0}^{2}\Gamma_{1}^{2}e^{ct/2}\frac{\textrm{sinh}(ct/2)}{c/2}}}\delta(X_{v}-X_{v}^{\prime})

(32)

The expression of the kernel (or fundamental solution) of the original PDE (
3
) particularized to the case

c=\pm 1
is:

\displaystyle k^{c}(t,X_{S},X_{v},X_{S}^{\prime},X_{v}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\Gamma_{1}\sigma_{1}\sqrt{\Delta^{(c)}}}

\displaystyle\times e^{-\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}g_{5}(t,t_{0})\left(e^{\frac{1}{2}cg_{4}(t,t_{0})}X_{S}-X_{S}^{\prime}\right)^{2}+\Gamma_{1}^{2}m_{0}^{2}(X_{v}-X_{v}^{\prime})^{2}\tilde{g}_{4}(t,t_{0})}{2\Gamma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}\Delta^{(c)}}}

\displaystyle\times e^{\frac{k_{\mu}\rho\sigma_{1}^{2}\Gamma_{1}g_{2}(t,t_{0})g_{3}(t,t_{0})e^{\frac{1}{2}cg_{4}(t,t_{0})}\left(e^{\frac{1}{2}cg_{4}(t,t_{0})}(X_{S}+\Gamma_{1}k_{\omega}g_{1}(t,t_{0}))-X_{S}^{\prime}\right)}{\Gamma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}\Delta^{(c)}}}

\displaystyle\times e^{-\frac{k_{\mu}\Gamma_{1}^{2}m_{0}^{2}\sigma_{1}\tilde{g}_{4}(t,t_{0})\left(\left(2(X_{v}-X_{v}^{\prime})-\sigma_{1}^{2}g_{5}(t,t_{0})\right)+k_{\mu}\sigma_{1}g_{2}(t,t_{0})^{2}\right)}{2\Gamma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}\Delta^{(c)}}}

\displaystyle\times e^{\frac{\Gamma_{1}\rho\sigma_{1}g_{3}(t,t_{0})e^{\frac{1}{2}cg_{4}(t,t_{0})}\left(2(X_{v}-X_{v}^{\prime})-\sigma_{1}^{2}g_{5}(t,t_{0})\right)\left(e^{\frac{1}{2}cg_{4}(t,t_{0})}(X_{S}+\Gamma_{1}k_{\omega}g_{1}(t,t_{0}))-X_{S}^{\prime}\right)}{2\Gamma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}\Delta^{(c)}}}

\displaystyle\times e^{-\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}g_{5}(t,t_{0})\Gamma_{1}e^{\frac{1}{2}cg_{4}(t,t_{0})}\left(8ck_{\omega}g_{1}(t,t_{0})(e^{\frac{1}{2}cg_{4}(t,t_{0})}X_{S}-X_{S}^{\prime})+\Gamma_{1}k_{\omega}g_{1}(t,t_{0}))+\Gamma_{1}m_{0}^{2}e^{\frac{1}{2}cg_{4}(t,t_{0})}\sigma_{1}^{2}g_{5}(t,t_{0})\right)}{8\Gamma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}\Delta^{(c)}}}

\displaystyle\times e^{\frac{-\Gamma_{1}^{2}m_{0}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}g_{5}(t,t_{0})^{2}+4\Gamma_{1}^{2}m_{0}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}g_{5}(t,t_{0})(X_{v}-X_{v}^{\prime})\tilde{g}_{4}(t,t_{0})}{8\Gamma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}\Delta^{(c)}}}

where
\Delta^{(c)}=m_{0}^{2}g_{5}(t,t_{0})\tilde{g}_{4}(t,t_{0})-\rho^{2}e^{cg_{4}(t,t_{0})}g_{3}(t,t_{0})^{2}
and

\tilde{g}_{4}(t,t_{0})=\frac{e^{cg_{4}(t,t_{0})}-1}{c}
.

This kernel is integrable if
\Delta^{(c)}>0
, which imposes a (time-dependent) restriction on the
possible values of
\rho
. For the simplest case of constant parameters
\alpha_{i}(t)=1,i=1,\ldots,5
, this restriction reduces to

|\rho|<m_{0}\frac{\sqrt{c(t-t_{0})(e^{c(t-t_{0})}-1)}}{2\left(e^{\frac{1}{2}c(t-t_{0})}-1\right)}
, from which we recover

|\rho|<m_{0}
in the limit
c\rightarrow 0
.

The following research will address the cases where
c=\pm 1
.

Let us remind that the SABR model is very similar to the Wei-Norman SLV when

Notice that volatility surfaces are characterized by volatility skew, that is why stochastic volatility models can describe well such as phenomenon. The skew might be caused by: a) leverage effect because assets tend to be more volatile at lower prices than at higher prices, b) anti-correlated volatility changes versus spot changes, asymmetric jumps that tend to be downwards rather than upwards, c) a nonzero probability for the asset to collapse if the issuer defaults, d) effect of supply and demand when investors are long and so tend to be net buyers of downside puts and sellers of upside call
[
21
]
.
This is in line with empirical evidence, in fact, for a given expiration date, implied volatilities increase as strike price decreases for strikes below the current stock price (spot) or current forward price
[
21
]
.
Then, the implied volatility surface (as a function of moneyness) is V-shaped but has a rounded vertex when both S and K increase.

8
Conclusions

In this work, we have shown how a time-dependent local stochastic volatility (SLV) model can be reduced to a system of autonomous PDEs via the Wei-Norman factorization method and Lie algebraic techniques. For the case
c=0
the Wei-Norman SLV is similar to the well-known SABR model with
\beta=1
. By comparing Monte Carlo simulations we have demonstrated that the Wei-Norman SLV model is more computationally efficient.
Next research will illustrate the results for
c=-1
and
c=1
.

Appendix A
The Heat kernel and other integral kernels

In this Appendix, we recall some known results for the explicit solutions of some simple PDEs in terms of integral kernels. For an account on this see, for instance,
[
15
,
19
]
.

A.1
The first-order kernel

Consider the simplest first-order PDE:

\frac{\partial\Phi(x,t)}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial\Phi(x,t)}{\partial x}

(33)

The solution can be expressed in terms of the integral kernel:

k_{\rm 1st}(t,x,x^{\prime})=\delta(x-x^{\prime}+t)

(34)

which in this case is trivial to integrate:

\Phi(x,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}k_{\rm 1st}(t-t_{0},x,x^{\prime})\Phi(x^{\prime},t_{0})=\Phi(x+(t-t_{0}),t_{0})

(35)

A.2
Transforming a first-order differential operator into a simpler form

Consider the differential operator:

K=f(x)\frac{\partial\ }{\partial x}\,,\qquad f(x)>0

(36)

This operator can be transformed into
\frac{\partial\ }{\partial\tilde{x}}
by means of the change in the independent variable:

\tilde{x}=\psi(x)\,,\qquad\psi(x)=\int\frac{dx}{f(x)}

(37)

and then

K=\frac{\partial\ }{\partial\tilde{x}}

(38)

With this transformation, a general first-order PDE

\frac{\partial\Phi(x,t)}{\partial t}=f(x)\frac{\partial\Phi(x,t)}{\partial x}\,\,\qquad f(x)>0

(39)

can be solved in terms of the kernel

k_{\rm mod1st}(t,x,x^{\prime})=\delta(\psi^{-1}(\psi(x)+t)-x^{\prime})\,,

(40)

where
\psi^{-1}(x)
is the inverse function of
\psi(x)
(which exists since
\psi^{\prime}(x)=f(x)>0
).
Thus,

\Phi(x,t)=\Phi(\psi^{-1}(\psi(x)+(t-t_{0})),t_{0})

(41)

A.3
The Heat Equation

The Heat equation

\frac{\partial\Phi(x,t)}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi(x,t)}{\partial x^{2}}

(42)

can be formally solved in terms of the integral kernel, known as Heat kernel:

k_{H}(t,x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi|t|}}e^{-\frac{(x^{\prime}-x)^{2}}{4t}}

(43)

in the form:

\Phi(x,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}k_{H}(t-t_{0},x,x^{\prime})\Phi(x^{\prime},t_{0})

(44)

where
\Phi(x^{\prime},t_{0})
is some specified function at
t=t_{0}
. Note that the Heat kernel satisfies:

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}k_{H}(t,x,x^{\prime})=\delta(x-x^{\prime})

(45)

A.4
Transforming a PDE into the Heat equation

Consider the PDE

\frac{\partial\Phi(x,t)}{\partial t}=f(x)\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi(x,t)}{\partial x^{2}}\,,\qquad f(x)>0

(46)

that can be solved using an integral kernel

\Phi(x,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}k_{f}(t-t_{0},x,x^{\prime})\Phi(x^{\prime},t_{0})

(47)

where
\Phi(x,t_{0})
is some specified function at
t=t_{0}
.

We wonder if Eq. (
46
) can be transformed into the Heat equation (
42
) with a suitable change in the independent and dependent variables:

\tilde{t}=\phi(t)\,,\qquad\tilde{x}=\psi(x,t)\,,\qquad\tilde{\Phi}(\tilde{x},\tilde{t})=\xi(x,t)\Phi(x,t)

(48)

in such a way that we recover the Heat equation (
42
) in the new variables:

\frac{\partial\tilde{\Phi}}{\partial\tilde{t}}=\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{\Phi}}{\partial\tilde{x}^{2}}

(49)

This is possible if
f
verifies:

f^{\prime\prime}(x)-\frac{3}{4}\frac{f^{\prime}(x)^{2}}{f(x)}=c_{1}g(x)^{2}+c_{2}

(50)

with
c_{1},c_{2}
constants and
g(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{dx^{\prime}}{\sqrt{f(x^{\prime})}}
.

In this case, we have that:

\displaystyle\phi(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}t&c_{1}=0\\
\frac{\tan(\sqrt{c_{1}}t)}{\sqrt{c_{1}}}&c_{1}\neq 0\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\psi(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\phi^{\prime}(t)}g(x)

(54)

\displaystyle\xi(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{c_{2}}{4}t}\left(\phi^{\prime}(t)f(x)\right)^{-1/4}e^{-\frac{1}{8}\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}(t)}{\phi^{\prime}(t)}g(x)^{2}}

(55)

Using the Heat kernel (
43
), and undoing the change (
48
), we obtain the expression for the kernel
k_{f}
:

k_{f}(t-t_{0},x,x^{\prime})=\frac{\partial\psi(x^{\prime},t_{0})}{\partial x^{\prime}}\frac{\xi(x^{\prime},t_{0})}{\xi(x,t)}k_{H}(\phi(t)-\phi(t_{0}),\psi(x,t),\psi(x^{\prime},t_{0}))

(56)

where it is possible to show that the right-hand side is a function of
t-t_{0}
only.

A.5
The Mixed Equation

Consider the PDE in 2D:

\frac{\partial\Phi(x,y,t)}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi(x,y,t)}{\partial x\partial y}

(57)

which can be formally solved in terms of the integral kernel:

k_{M}(t,x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}|t|}e^{-\frac{(x^{\prime}-x)(y^{\prime}-y)}{t}}

(58)

in the form:

\Phi(x,y,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}k_{M}(t-t_{0},x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\Phi(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},t_{0})

(59)

where
\Phi(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},t_{0})
is some specified function at
t=t_{0}
. Note that the Heat kernel satisfies:

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}k_{M}(t,x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\delta(x-x^{\prime})\delta(y-y^{\prime})

(60)
