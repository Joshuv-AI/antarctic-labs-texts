Mutually unbiased frames
Fabian Caro Pérez, Victor González Avella, Dardo Goyeneche

Abstract
In this work, the concept of mutually unbiased frames is introduced as the most general notion of unbiasedness for sets composed by linearly independent and normalized vectors. It encompasses the already existing notions of unbiasedness for orthonormal bases, regular simplices, equiangular tight frames, positive operator valued measure, and also includes symmetric informationally complete quantum measurements. After introducing the tool, its power is shown by finding the following results about the last mentioned class of constellations: (i) real fiducial states do not exist in any even dimension, and (ii) unknown d d -dimensional fiducial states are parameterized, a priori, with roughly 3 ​ d / 2 3d/2 real variables only, without loss of generality. Furthermore, multi-parametric families of pure quantum states having minimum uncertainty with regard to several choices of d + 1 d+1 orthonormal bases are shown, in every dimension d d . These last families contain all existing fiducial states in every finite dimension, and the bases include maximal sets of d + 1 d+1 mutually unbiased bases, when d d is a prime number.

Preliminaries

In this section, the required notions to understand the work are introduced. Firstly, pure quantum states, denoted in kets as
|\psi\rangle
, are normalized vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with regard to the norm induced by the standard scalar product. That is,
\|\psi\|^{2}=\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\langle j|\psi\rangle|^{2}=1
, where
|j\rangle
denotes the
j
th element of the canonical basis, also called computational basis in quantum information theory. Here,
|\psi\rangle
is an element in
\mathcal{H}_{d}
,
\langle\phi|
an element in the dual space
\mathcal{H}_{d}^{*}
,
\langle\phi|\psi\rangle
and
|\phi\rangle\langle\psi|
the inner and outer products, respectively. Besides, the symbol
|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|
denotes the rank-one projector associated to the direction
|\phi\rangle
. The first relevant ingredient is the concept of frames
[
24
]
, defined as follows:

Definition 1
.

A set of
n\geq d
pure states
\{|\phi_{i}\rangle\}_{i=1,\dots,n}
in
\mathcal{H}_{d}
defines a frame if constants
0<A\leq B<\infty
exist such that

A\leq\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}|\langle x|\phi_{i}\rangle|^{2}\leq B,\qquad\mbox{for all }|x\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{d}.

(1)

Here,
A
and
B
are called the lower and upper bounds of the frame, given by the minimal and maximal eigenvalues of the
frame operator

S=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}|\phi_{i}\rangle\langle\phi_{i}|
, respectively
[
8
]
. A frame is called
tight
if
A
equals
B
, having in such case that
A=B=n/d
. When
A=B=1
, a Parseval tight Frame arises, equivalent to orthonormal bases in finite dimensions. There is a simple way to detect whether a given set of vectors forms a frame: linearly independence of
d
out of
n\geq d
vectors is a necessary and sufficient condition to have a frame in
\mathcal{H}_{d}
. A gentle introduction to finite frames theory can be found in Ref.
[
25
]
.

A special kind of tight frames occurs when its
n
vectors are equiangular, known as
equiangular tight frames
(ETF)
[
26
]
, meaning that there is a constant
c>0
such that
|\langle\phi_{i}|\phi_{j}\rangle|^{2}=c
, for every
i\not=j
, with
i,j\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
. Here, the number
\sqrt{c}
is typically called
coherence
, but for convenient reasons, throughout this work the constant
c
is called the
overlap
of a MUF. See
[
27
]
for a summary of the current state of the art of the ETF problem.

Among all constellations in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{d}
, there is a specially distinguished one: mutually unbiased bases
[
28
]
.

Definition 2
.

Two orthonormal bases
\{|\phi_{j}\rangle\}_{j=0,\dots,d-1}
and
\{|\psi_{k}\rangle\}_{k=0,\dots,d-1}

\mathcal{H}_{d}
are unbiased if

m
orthonormal bases are MUB if they are pairwise MUB.

For instance, the eigenvectors bases of the three Pauli matrices define
m=3
MUB in
\mathcal{H}_{2}
. More generally, let

X=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}|k+1\rangle\langle k|\mbox{ and }Z=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\omega^{k}|k\rangle\langle k|,

(2)

the
shift
and
phase
operators, respectively, where
\omega=e^{2\pi i/d}
. Therefore, the eigenvectors bases of the operators

X,Z,XZ,XZ^{2},\dots,XZ^{d-1},

form
m=d+1
MUB in
\mathcal{H}_{d}

d
is a prime number. Here, and in the rest of the work, it is assumed addition modulo
d
in kets, i.e.
|(d-1)+1\rangle=|0\rangle

MUB have been extensively studied for the last 40 years. A maximal set of

MUB exists in every prime

]
and prime power
[
10
]
dimension
d
. On the other hand, the maximal number of MUB existing in any other composite dimension

]
.

Another remarkable constellations are the SIC-POVM

A set of

SIC-POVM are known to exist in dimensions

and in several higher dimensions
[

, being

achieved recently

. Also, numerical solutions are known in dimensions

On the one hand, the difficulty to solve the MUB and SIC-POVM problems have motivated researchers to find solution to its extensions to the space of full-rank density matrices, see
[

]
and
[

, respectively. On the other hand, the rank-one SIC-POVM problem has triggered the search of additional symmetries in fiducial pure states. In short, every known Weyl-Heisenberg fiducial state is eigenvector of an order 3 Clifford operator

The symmetries mentioned above allowed the construction of every known fiducial state. However, there is

3
Mutually unbiased frames

The notion of mutually unbiasedness for constellations of vectors has been extensively used in quantum mechanics. One of the reasons is because
two Von Neumann observables are complementary if and only if its eigenvectors bases are mutually unbiased
, e.g. position and momentum or orthogonal directions of spin
1/2
observables. Mutually unbiasedness has been extended to regular simplices
[
20
,
21
]
, equiangular tight frames
[
22
]
and POVM in general
[
23
]
. In this section, a notion of unbiasedness that includes all the above notions is introduced. The aim to present this generalization is to study the existence and construction of some inequivalent geometrical structures under the same framework, e.g. MUB and SIC-POVM. Despite our generalization goes beyond the set of quantum measurements, the new point of view reveals interesting properties when restricting the attention to POVM, as shown in Section
5
. The central concept of our work is defined as follows:

Definition 4
.

Let
\{|\phi_{j}\rangle\}_{j=0,\dots,n_{1}-1}
and
\{|\psi_{k}\rangle\}_{k=0,\dots,n_{2}-1}
be two frames in
\mathcal{H}_{d}
. They are called unbiased if there is a constant
c>0
such that

|\langle\phi_{j}|\psi_{k}\rangle|^{2}=c,

(3)

holds for every
j=0,\dots,n_{1}-1
and
k=0,\dots,n_{2}-1
. A set of
m
frames is called mutually unbiased (MUF) if they are pairwise unbiased. The constant value
c
is called the overlap of the MUF.

Figure 1:
(color online) Construction of two mutually unbiased frames composed by two vectors each for a qubit system in the Bloch sphere. Given a frame in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, associated to two normalized vectors on the surface of the Bloch sphere, red and gray vectors in the figure, one can always define another frame that is mutually unbiased to the above one as follows: construct two cones with axes given by the red and grey vectors, respectively. The apertures of the cones have to be identical and large enough to have intersection of the cones. The unbiased frame is given by vectors lying at the intersection of the cones, i.e. the blue vectors. When the red and gray vectors are anti-parallel then there is a unique way to define intersecting cones, i.e. the aperture of each cone is equal to
\pi
, and both cones become the same disk. In such case, any point in the border of the disk is unbiased to the given basis. In this way, 3 MUB arise as 3 orthogonal lines.

Fig.
1
shows an intuitive construction of two MUF for a qubit system. The notion of MUF reduces to mutually unbiased POVM
[
23
]
when both frames are tight, for any
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
. Additionally, if
n_{1}=n_{2}=d
, then they are MUB. Figure
2
illustrates the relation existing between different
notions of unbiasedness.

At first glance, a set of
m
MUF might look as a collection of equiangular lines. However, it is important to remark that the MUF structure has additional constraints, as the following example shows. Suppose that
4
MUF exist in dimension
3
, each of them having
3
elements, and arranged along 4 rows as follows:

\begin{array}[]{cccc}a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}&\quad\mbox{$\leftarrow$ Frame 1}\\
b_{1}&b_{2}&b_{3}&\quad\mbox{$\leftarrow$ Frame 2}\\
c_{1}&c_{2}&c_{3}&\quad\mbox{$\leftarrow$ Frame 3}\\
d_{1}&d_{2}&d_{3}&\quad\mbox{$\leftarrow$ Frame 4}\end{array}

Clearly,
\{a_{i},b_{i},c_{i},d_{i}\}
, is a set of 4 equiangular lines for any
i=1,2,3
, which might tempt one to claim that the above set of three equiangular lines is equivalent to a set of four MUF. However, the MUF constraint establishes that two elements of the above arrangement belonging to different rows and different columns (e.g.
a_{1}
and
b_{2}
), also have the same overlap
c
. Indeed, the MUF constraint is equivalent to say that
\{a_{i},b_{j},c_{k},d_{l}\}
forms a set of equiangular lines, for any
i,j,k,l=1,\dots,3
. This is an interesting combinatorial extension of the equiangular lines problem.

Given that all sub-normalized rank-one projectors associated to a tight frame sum up to the identity, it is simple to show that
c=1/d
holds in (
3
) when both frames are tight
[
23
]
. However, if the frames are not tight, the constant
c
might take another value, as shown below.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\{|\phi^{(\ell)}_{j}\rangle\}
be a set of
m
mutually unbiased frames in
\mathcal{H}_{d}
composed by
n
elements each, with overlap
c
. Also, assume that each frame has lower and upper frame bounds
A_{\ell}
and
B_{\ell}
, respectively, where
\ell=0,\dots,m-1
. Therefore,

\max_{\ell}A_{\ell}\leq cn\leq\min_{\ell}B_{\ell}
.

Proof.

From considering
|x\rangle=|\phi_{i}^{\ell}\rangle
, inequalities (
1
) imply

A_{1}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}|\langle\phi_{i}^{(\ell)}|\phi_{j}^{(\ell^{\prime})}\rangle|^{2}\leq B_{1},\,A_{2}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}|\langle\phi_{i}^{(\ell)}|\phi_{j}^{(\ell^{\prime})}\rangle|^{2}\leq B_{2},\dots,\,A_{m}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}|\langle\phi_{i}^{(\ell)}|\phi_{j}^{(\ell^{\prime})}\rangle|^{2}\leq B_{m},

(4)

for each
\ell=0,\dots,n-1
, which reduce to

A_{1}\leq cn\leq B_{1},A_{2}\leq cn\leq B_{2},\dotsc,A_{m}\leq cn\leq B_{m}
. Therefore, the most restrictive constraint for
c
is given by
\max_{\ell}A_{\ell}\leq cn\leq\min_{\ell}B_{\ell}
.
∎

For the case of orthonormal bases, Proposition

reduces to mutually unbiased bases, where
c=1/d
. Indeed, the same overlap
c
occurs when at least one of the frames is tight, thus forming a POVM.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\{|\phi^{(\ell)}_{j}\rangle\}
be a set of
m
mutually unbiased frames in
\mathcal{H}_{d}
with overlap
c
, composed by
n
elements each, such that at least one of them is tight. Therefore,

c=1/d
.

Proof.

The proof arises from Proposition
1
, where
\frac{n}{d}=A_{\ell^{\prime}}=B_{\ell^{\prime}}=cn
holds. Thus,
c=1/d
.
∎

From Corollary
1
, note that Parseval MUF are mutually unbiased bases in every finite dimension
d
. In Section
4
, examples of MUF in
\mathcal{H}_{d}
are shown, where the overlaps

are not equal to

, in general.

4
Matrix approach to mutually unbiased frames

In this section, a matrix approach to the problem of mutually unbiased frames is shown. In particular, the case of
m
MUF composed by
d
vectors each in
\mathcal{H}_{d}
is considered. Through this approach, entries of the
j
-th element of each frame are arranged as columns belonging to a square complex matrix of order
d
, for every
j=0,\dots,d-1
. Thus, there are
m
square matrices of order
d
, denoted
M_{0},\dots,M_{m-1}
, satisfying the following relations:

M_{j}^{{\dagger}}M_{k}=K^{(jk)},

(5)

where
|(K^{(jk)})_{st}|^{2}=c
, for every
s,t=0,\dots,d-1
, and
j\not=k
. Here,
(K^{(jk)})_{st}
denotes the entry of matrix
K^{(jk)}
located in row
s
and column
t
. Also, if
j=k
the relation
(K^{(jj)})_{ss}=1
occurs, since the column vectors of
M_{j}
are normalized, for every
j=0,\dots,m-1
, and
s=0,\dots,d-1
. As a general property, it is simple to show that
M_{j}
and
K^{(jk)}
are full rank matrices, as their columns have to be linearly independent in order to form a frame. However, the problem to find sets of
m
matrices
\{M_{j}\}
satisfying (
5
) is evidently hard, if no further assumptions are taken. In order to simplify this problem, the following symmetries are assumed: matrices
M_{j}
are normal, i.e.
[M^{{\dagger}}_{j},M_{j}]=0
, and they do commute, i.e.
[M_{j},M_{k}]=0
and
[M_{j},M_{k}^{{\dagger}}]=0
. These assumptions imply that
[M_{\ell},K^{(jk)}]=0
is satisfied, for every
j,k,\ell=0,\dots,d-1
. Thus, the problem simplifies to finding special relations existing between the spectrum of matrices
M_{j}
and
K^{(jk)}
.

At this stage, the choice of the unitary transformation
U
that diagonalizes all matrices
M_{j}
plays a fundamental role. For instance, if matrices
M_{j}
are diagonal in the computational basis, then there is no pair of MUF. Thus, an interesting question arises:
which choices of
U
provide a solution to (
5
)
for a given value of
m\geq 2
? This question is hard to solve, and it is not studied through the current research. In turns, the simplest case of matrices
M_{j}
diagonalized by the discrete Fourier transform is considered. That is, the matrices
M_{0},\dots,M_{m-1}
are circulant. This choice is justified by simplicity, as several useful properties are well-known for this class of matrices
[
47
]
. A square matrix
M
is called circulant if the
j+1
-th row is a cyclic permutation of the
j
-th row, for every
j=0,\dots-d-2
. That is,

M=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}m_{0}&m_{1}&m_{2}&\dots&m_{d-1}\\
m_{d-1}&m_{0}&m_{1}&\dots&m_{d-2}\\
\vdots&&&&\vdots\\
m_{1}&m_{2}&m_{3}&\dots&m_{0}\end{array}\right),

(6)

where
m_{0},\dots,m_{d-1}\in\mathbb{C}
.
Let
\vec{\mu}(M)
be the vector formed by all elements of the first row of
M
, i.e.
\vec{\mu}=(m_{0},\dots,m_{d-1})^{T}
, where
T
denotes transposition. Also, let
\vec{\lambda}(M)
be a column vector containing all eigenvalues of
M
, sorted according to the decomposition

M=F\mathrm{diag}[\vec{\lambda}(M)]F^{{\dagger}},

(7)

where
\mathrm{diag}[\vec{\lambda}(M)]
is a diagonal matrix containing the vector
\vec{\lambda}(M)
along its main diagonal, and
F
is the discrete Fourier transform, having entries
F_{jk}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\omega^{jk}
, where
\omega=e^{2\pi i/d}
. Along the work,
\vec{\lambda}(M)
is called
the vector defined by the eigenvalues of
M
.
From (
7
), it is simple to show that
\vec{\lambda}(M)=\sqrt{d}F^{{\dagger}}\vec{\mu}(M)
is satisfied. On the other hand, the unitary operator
F^{2}
transforms the first row of
M
, i.e.
\vec{\mu}(M)
, to the first column of
M
, that has unit norm. Therefore, normalized columns in
M
translates to the condition
\|\vec{\mu}(M)\|=1
, or equivalently
\|\vec{\lambda}(M)\|=\sqrt{d}
.

Similarly, any matrix
K
from (
5
) is circulant, having the form

K=\sqrt{c}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}e^{i\alpha_{0}}&e^{i\alpha_{1}}&e^{i\alpha_{2}}&\dots&e^{i\alpha_{d-1}}\\
e^{i\alpha_{d-1}}&e^{i\alpha_{0}}&e^{i\alpha_{1}}&\dots&e^{i\alpha_{d-2}}\\
\vdots&&&&\vdots\\
e^{i\alpha_{1}}&e^{i\alpha_{2}}&e^{i\alpha_{3}}&\dots&e^{i\alpha_{0}}\end{array}\right),

(8)

where
\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{d-1}\in[0,2\pi)
and
0<c<1
. The first row of
K
is arranged as the vector
\vec{\mu}(K)=\sqrt{c}(e^{i\alpha_{0}},\dots,e^{i\alpha_{d-1}})^{T}
, and the vector
\vec{\lambda}(K)
containing all eigenvalues of
K
is defined according to

K=F\mathrm{diag}[\vec{\lambda}(K)]F^{{\dagger}}.

(9)

As a basic property of circulant matrices, the relation
\vec{\lambda}(K)=\sqrt{d}F^{{\dagger}}\vec{\mu}(K)
occurs, where
\|\vec{\mu}(K)\|=\sqrt{cd}
and
\|\vec{\lambda}(K)\|=d\sqrt{c}
. Now, the first result of this section is established.

Proposition 2
.

Let
M_{0},\dots,M_{m-1}
be
m
circulant matrices that form
m
MUF composed by
d
vectors each, in dimension
d
. Then, the normalized vectors defined by the eigenvalues of these matrices form
m
equiangular lines in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, with overlap
c
.

Proof.

The result arises from direct calculation:

\displaystyle|\vec{\lambda}(M_{j})\cdot\vec{\lambda}(M_{k})|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(\mathrm{diag}[\vec{\lambda}(M_{j})]^{{\dagger}}\mathrm{diag}[\vec{\lambda}(M_{k})]\bigr)|^{2}

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Tr}(F^{{\dagger}}\vec{\lambda}(M_{j})]^{{\dagger}}FF^{{\dagger}}\vec{\lambda}(M_{k})]F)|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Tr}(M_{j}^{{\dagger}}M_{k})|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Tr}(K)|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle cd,

for every
j\not=k\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}
. Moreover, when eigenvectors
\vec{\lambda}(M_{0}),\dots,\vec{\lambda}(M_{m-1})
are normalized, they define
m
equiangular lines with overlap
c
, the same overlap of the MUF.
∎

An expert reader might recognize here the evidence of a well-known property holding for fiducial states of SIC-POVM: the Fourier transform of a fiducial state is a fiducial state. From combining Prop.
2
with results from equiangular lines theory
[
48
]
, the following constraints for the existence of MUF is obtained:

Proposition 3
.

Suppose that
M_{1},\dots,M_{m}
are circulant matrices of order
d
, forming
m
MUF. Then, the inequality
m\leq d^{2}
holds.

Proof.

The proof arises from the fact that there are no more than
d^{2}
equiangular lines in dimension
d
.
∎

Moreover, stronger bounds can be found when the eigenvalues of
M_{j}
are real.

Proposition 4
.

Suppose a set MUF composed of
m
hermitian circulant matrices, with overlap
c
, exists. Therefore, the following properties hold:

1.

m\leq d(d+1)/2
(see
[
49
]
).

2.

If
m\geq 2d
, then

Proof.

Further properties of eigenvalues of matrices

Proposition 5
.

Let
M
be a circulant matrix of order
d
with columns forming a normalized frame. Then, there is another matrix
\tilde{M}
inducing a frame such that
M
and
\tilde{M}
form a pair of MUF, in every dimension
d

Proof.

Matrices
M
and
\tilde{M}
are MUF if and only if
M^{{\dagger}}\tilde{M}=K
, with
|K_{jk}|^{2}=c
, for every
j,k=0,\dots,d-1
and some
0<c<1

\vec{\lambda}^{*}_{M}\circ\vec{\lambda}_{\tilde{M}}=\vec{\lambda}_{K}.

(15)

By assuming the polar decompositions

\lambda_{M}=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}r_{j}\omega^{\alpha_{j}}|j\rangle\quad\mbox{and}\quad\lambda_{\tilde{M}}=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}s_{j}\omega^{\beta_{j}}|j\rangle,

(16)

where
r_{j},s_{j},\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
j=0,\dots,d-1
, and the fact that

\vec{\lambda}_{K}=\sqrt{c}\,F^{{\dagger}}\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\omega^{\gamma_{j}}|j\rangle,

(17)

for some
\gamma_{0},\dots,\gamma_{d-1}\in[0,2\pi)
, one can re-write (
15

\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}r_{j}s_{j}\omega^{\beta_{j}-\alpha_{j}}|j\rangle=\sqrt{cd}\,F^{{\dagger}}\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\omega^{\gamma_{j}}|j\rangle.

(18)

Note that
r_{j}\not=0
and

, for any

. This is so because eigenvalues of matrices

and
\tilde{M}
cannot vanish, because their columns form a frame. Now, if there is an -unormalized- vector
\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\omega^{\gamma_{j}}|j\rangle
unbiased to the pair
\{\mathbb{I},F\}
, then there exists
\delta_{0},\dots,\delta_{d-1}\in[0,2\pi)
such that

. So, (

\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}r_{j}s_{j}\omega^{\beta_{j}-\alpha_{j}}|j\rangle=\sqrt{cd}\,\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\omega^{\delta_{j}}|j\rangle,

(19)

implying that

and
\beta_{j}-\alpha_{j}=\delta_{j}

\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}r^{2}_{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}s^{2}_{j}=d
. Thus, the suitable matrix

, implying that

. To conclude the proof, note that there are at least
d
vectors MU to the pair
\{\mathbb{I},F\}
in every dimension

, corresponding to the eigenvectors of

.
∎

5
Application to the SIC-POVM problem

Along this section, SIC-POVM are organized as
d
sets of mutually unbiased frames, composed by
d
vectors each. This slightly different way of seeing these constellations carries out remarkably interesting consequences, as shown below. Based on the matrix approach to MUF, introduced in Section
4
, one can assume that the
d^{2}
vectors of a SIC-POVM can be arranged as column vectors in
m=d
circulant matrices of order
d
,
M_{0},\dots,M_{d-1}
, each of them defining a frame. This assumption reduces the problem to finding a single suitable matrix of order
d
, called
\mathcal{M}
, that contains the normalized vectors
\vec{\lambda}(M_{0})/\sqrt{d},\dots,\vec{\lambda}(M_{d-1})/\sqrt{d}
along its columns. The problem of finding a solution
\mathcal{M}
is hard, since it has a quadratic number of free parameters as a function of the dimension
d
. In order to make the problem simpler, a further reasonable assumption is adopted: the matrix
\mathcal{M}
is circulant. Thus, all the problem reduces to find a suitable first row of
\mathcal{M}
, that has
2(d-1)
free parameters without loss of generality. After these intuitive, but drastic assumptions, one might wonder whether there is a SIC-POVM satisfying the above symmetries. Below, a positive answer is provided in every dimension
d
where a SIC-POVM is known.

Proposition 6
.

The assumption that matrices
M_{0},\dots,M_{d-1}
and
\mathcal{M}
are circulant is equivalent to the assumption that a SIC-POVM can be defined as an orbit of a fiducial state under the Weyl-Heisenberg group.

Proof.

The fact that the
d^{2}
vectors of a SIC-POVM are arranged as columns in circulant matrices
M_{0},\dots,M_{d-1}
imply that the
d
vectors within each of these matrices are connected by a shift of its entries. Evenmore, the fact that
\mathcal{M}
is circulant means that
\vec{\lambda}(M_{j})=X^{j}\vec{\lambda}(M_{0})
, implying that
M_{j}=Z^{j}M_{0}(Z^{j})^{{\dagger}}
. Thus, the
d^{2}
vectors of the SIC-POVM are given by
|\phi_{jk}\rangle=X^{j}Z^{k}|\phi_{00}\rangle
, where
|\phi_{00}\rangle
is an unknown fiducial state.
∎

Note that normalized vectors
\vec{\lambda}(M_{j})/\sqrt{d}
,
j=0,\dots,d-1
, are also fiducial states. This is simple to see, as the first row of
M_{j}
is connected with the normalized vector
\vec{\lambda}(M_{j})/\sqrt{d}
via the Fourier transform
F
, and the first row of
M_{j}
is connected with its first column via
F^{2}
. Given that
F
belongs to the Clifford group, it transforms fiducial states into fiducial states. Note that this result is in agreement with Prop.
2
.

A fundamental ingredient to have a frame is the linearly independence of its elements. In the matrix approach, this is equivalent to say that matrices
M_{0},\dots,M_{d-1}
do not have vanishing determinant, i.e. the vector
\lambda(M_{0})
has non-zero entries. As this vector is also a fiducial state, up to a rescaling factor, it is interesting to wonder whether a fiducial state can have a vanishing entry. Zauner already noted the existence of such fiducial states in dimension 3
[
33
]
. Some recent explorations revealed the existence of fiducials with zero entries in dimensions
d=26,28,62,98
and
228

[
52
]
. For an extended study about linearly dependencies of elements of a SIC-POVM see here
[
53
]
. When having a fiducial state
|\phi\rangle
with zero entries one might consider another fiducial
U|\phi\rangle
, where
U
is an element of the Clifford group. Most likely, many of these transformations produce a fiducial state having non-zero entries but, unfortunately, it was not possible to demonstrate it. This property is left as a conjecture.

There is a further symmetry that one can impose in order to reduce the number of free parameters in SIC-POVM. One might assume that all matrices
M_{0},\dots,M_{d-1}
are hermitian, which holds if and only if
\vec{\lambda}(M_{0})/\sqrt{d}
is a real fiducial state. Along this direction, it has been conjectured that real fiducial states exist in odd dimensions of the form
d=4n^{2}+3
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}

[
38
]
. This conjecture is supported with 18 real fiducials existing in dimensions of such form, the highest dimensional case corresponding to
d=19603

[
37
]
. For instance, in
d=3
, there is a family of real fiducial states
[
34
]
:

|\phi(r_{0})\rangle=r_{0}|0\rangle-(r_{0}/2+\sqrt{2-3r_{0}^{2}}/2)|1\rangle-(r_{0}/2-\sqrt{2-3r_{0}^{2}}/2)|2\rangle,

(24)

with
1/\sqrt{2}<r_{0}<\sqrt{2/3}
, inducing the following 1-parametric family of hermitian matrices:

\displaystyle M_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&a_{j}&a_{j}^{*}\\
a_{j}^{*}&0&a_{j}\\
a_{j}&a_{j}^{*}&0\end{array}\right),\quad j=0,1,2,

where

\displaystyle a_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{3}r_{0}+i\sqrt{2-3r_{0}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{12}\left(3-i\sqrt{3}\right)\left(\sqrt{6-9r_{0}^{2}}-3ir_{0}\right)

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{12}\left(3+i\sqrt{3}\right)\left(\sqrt{6-9r_{0}^{2}}-3ir_{0}\right).

Note that all existing real fiducial states are defined in odd dimensions
[

37

. Thus, an interesting question arises:
are there real fiducial states in even dimensions

Below, a conclusive answer to this question is provided.

Proposition 7
.

Real fiducial states, covariant under the standard Weyl-Heisenberg group, do not exist in any even dimension.

Proof.

Let
|\phi\rangle=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}c_{j}|\varphi_{j}\rangle
be a real pure state, expanded in the Fourier basis
|\varphi_{j}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\omega^{jk}|k\rangle
, where
\omega=e^{2\pi i/d}
. Taking in to account that
\langle\phi|=\sum_{j,k}c_{j}\omega^{jk}\langle k|
, it occurs that

\displaystyle|\langle\phi|X^{d/2}Z|\phi\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j,j^{\prime},k,k^{\prime}}c_{j}\omega^{jk}\langle k|\sum_{\ell=0}^{d-1}|\ell+d/2\rangle\langle\ell|\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}\omega^{m}|m\rangle\langle m|c_{j^{\prime}}\omega^{j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}|k^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j,j^{\prime},k,k^{\prime},\ell=0}^{d-1}c_{j}c_{j^{\prime}}\omega^{jk+j^{\prime}k^{\prime}+\ell}\langle k|\ell+d/2\rangle\langle\ell|k^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}(-1)^{j}c_{j}c_{d-j-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(c_{0}c_{d-1}+c_{2}c_{d-3}+\dots+c_{d-4}c_{3}+c_{d-2}c_{1})-

\displaystyle(c_{1}c_{d-2}+c_{3}c_{d-4}+\dots+c_{d-3}c_{2}+c_{d-1}c_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

for every even dimension
d
. Note that for SIC-POVM, the above overlap should be equal to
1/(d+1)
. As
|\phi\rangle
is an arbitrary real state, there is no real fiducial state with respect to the standard WH group in any even dimension
d
.
∎

Now, a hidden symmetry is revealed for the amplitudes of fiducial states.

Proposition 8
.

Any fiducial state
|\phi\rangle
, with respect to a Weyl-Heisenberg covariant SIC-POVM, satisfies

\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}|\langle j|\phi\rangle|^{2}|j\rangle=F^{{\dagger}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}|0\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{d(d+1)}}\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}e^{i\alpha_{j}}|j\rangle\right),

d

is the discrete Fourier transform of order

and

satisfies
\alpha_{d-j}=-\alpha_{j}
, for every
j=1,\dots,d-1
. As a consequence, any fiducial state
|\phi\rangle
depends on
\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor+d-1

\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor

26

d-1

Proof.

Matrix

M^{{\dagger}}_{0}M_{0}=\tilde{K},

(27)

\frac{1}{d+1}&j\not=k\end{array}\right..

\tilde{K}

\vec{\lambda}(\tilde{K})=\sqrt{\frac{d}{d+1}}F^{{\dagger}}\{\sqrt{d+1},e^{i\alpha_{1}},\dots,e^{i\alpha_{d-1}}\}^{T},

(29)

where the fact that the entries of the first row of
\tilde{K}
are given by
\tilde{K}_{00}=1

\tilde{K}_{0k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d+1}}e^{i\alpha_{k}}

\vec{\lambda}(M_{0})

∎

5.1
Minimum uncertainty states

Fiducial states are minimum uncertainty states for a complete set of MUB in prime dimensions
[
58
]
. To show this property, let
\{|\varphi^{j}_{k}\rangle\}
be a set of
d+1
MUB in prime dimension
d
and
H_{j}=-\log_{2}[\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}(p^{j}_{k})^{2}]
be the quadratic Rényi entropy
[
60
]
associated to the
j
th MUB, where
p^{j}_{k}=|\langle\phi|\varphi^{j}_{k}\rangle|^{2}
. Thus, it holds that
[
61
]
:

\sum_{m=0}^{d}H_{m}\geq(d+1)\log_{2}\left[\frac{d+1}{2}\right],

(31)

for any pure quantum state
|\phi\rangle
. The saturation of inequality (
31
) defines a minimum uncertainty states if and only if
\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}(p^{j}_{k})^{2}=\frac{2}{d+1}
, for every
j=0,\dots,d
. It has been shown that this last property is satisfied by any fiducial state
|\phi\rangle
in every prime dimension
d

[
58
]
. However, there are further quantum states minimizing (
31
). An interesting question is the following:
are there further sets of orthonormal bases such that fiducial states are minimum uncertainty states?
The following result, based on Prop.
8
, allows to provide a positive answer to the question, in every dimension where a fiducial state exists.

Proposition 9
.

Let
|\phi\rangle
be a
d
-dimensional quantum state satisfying (
26
),
|\xi_{0}\rangle,\dots,|\xi_{d-1}\rangle
the columns of any unitary matrix belonging to the Clifford group, and
p_{k}=|\langle\phi|\xi_{k}\rangle|^{2}
. Therefore,

\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}p_{j}p_{j+k}=\frac{1+\delta_{k,0}}{d+1}.

(32)

In particular, (
32
) holds for fiducial states
|\phi\rangle
.

Proof.

For
\{|\xi_{j}\rangle\}
equal to the computational basis, (
32
) arises by taking the inner product between the left hand side of (
26
) and the same expression after applying operator
X^{k}
. For the right hand side, it has to be used the fact that
X^{k}F^{{\dagger}}=F^{{\dagger}}Z^{k}
. Next, assume that
\{\xi_{j}\}
are the columns of any unitary
U
belonging to the Clifford group, and consider that
|\phi\rangle=U|\phi^{\prime}\rangle
, where
|\phi^{\prime}\rangle
is another fiducial state. Thus,
p_{k}=|\langle\phi|k\rangle|^{2}=|\langle\phi|U^{{\dagger}}U|k\rangle|^{2}=|\langle\phi^{\prime}|\xi_{k}\rangle|^{2}
. The generality of the fiducial state
|\phi^{\prime}\rangle
allows to conclude that (
32
) holds for any basis
|\xi_{k}\rangle=U|k\rangle
,
k=0\dots,d-1
, given by the columns of
U
.
∎

When considering the identity matrix as the chosen element of the Clifford group, Proposition
9
reduces to Corollary 2.3 in
[
54
]
, also shown in eq.(10) in
[
58
]
and as a special case of Theorem 1 in
[
62
]
. Proposition
9
has the following immediate consequence.

Corollary 2
.

Any
d
-dimensional quantum state
|\phi\rangle
that satisfies (
26
) is a minimum uncertainty state with respect to every subset of
d+1
orthonormal bases formed by the columns of
d+1
elements of the Clifford group. In particular, the result holds for any fiducial state in every dimension
d
, and for
d+1
MUB in every prime dimension
d
.

Proof.

From Prop.
9
, inequality (
31
) is saturated for any subset of
d+1

Proposition 10
.

Any eigenvector of the Zauner operator (

) has identical probability distributions with respect to the triplet of MUB given by the columns of
\{\mathbb{I},\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{Z}^{2}\}
, in every dimension
d

Proof.

Zauner’s operator
\mathcal{Z}
is unitary, thus having

|\langle k|\phi\rangle|=|\langle k|\mathcal{Z}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{Z}|\phi\rangle|=|\langle k|(\mathcal{Z}^{2})^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{Z}^{2}|\phi\rangle|
,
for every
k=0,\dots,d-1
. Furthermore, if
|\phi\rangle
is eigenvector of
\mathcal{Z}
then
|\langle k|\phi\rangle|=|\langle k|\mathcal{Z}^{{\dagger}}|\phi\rangle|=|\langle k|(\mathcal{Z}^{2})^{{\dagger}}|\phi\rangle|
, for every
k=0,\dots,d-1
. This implies that
|\phi\rangle
has identical probability distributions in the three orthonormal bases
\{k\}

6
Conclusions

This work introduced the ultimate notion of unbiasedness for sets of linearly independent and normalized vectors in
d
-dimensional Hilbert spaces:
mutually unbiased frames
(MUF). These sets are characterized by a constant overlapping that can take any value between the absolute bounds 0 and 1. The overlap reduces to
1/d
when at least one of the frames is tight, where tightness is equivalent to impose that the frame defines a rank-one positive operator valued measure (POVM). The introduced notion was illustrated by finding the most general pair of MUF composed by two vectors each for a qubit system. The concept of MUF allowed to think a Symmetric Informationally Complete (SIC)-POVM in dimension
d
as a set of
d
MUF composed by
d
vectors each, thus having a common root with the mutually unbiased bases problem. Interestingly, Zauner’s conjecture about the existence of fiducial states arose in a natural way when studying sets of
d
MUF in dimension
d
(see Prop.
6
). The approach also allowed to unlock two fundamental properties of SIC-POVM: real fiducial states do not exist in any even dimension (see Prop.
7
) and, any unknown
d
-dimensional fiducial state can be parameterized, a priori, with roughly
3d/2
real variables (see Prop.
8
). Additionally, some classes of quantum pure states, those satisfying (
26
), are minimum uncertainty states with respect to
d+1
orthonormal bases given by the columns of any subset of
d+1
Clifford operations. This result holds for any fiducial state in every dimension
d
where a Weyl-Heisenberg covariant SIC-POVM exists (see Prop.
9
and Corol.
2
). Finally, any eigenvector of the Zauner’s operator ‘looks the same’ for three MUB, in every dimension
d
(see Prop.
10
).
