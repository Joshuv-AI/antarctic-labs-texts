Consistency of randomized integration methods
Julian Hofstadler, Daniel Rudolf

Abstract
We prove that a class of randomized integration methods, including averages based on ( t , d ) (t,d) -sequences, Latin hypercube sampling, Frolov points as well as Cranley-Patterson rotations, consistently estimates expectations of integrable functions. Consistency here refers to convergence in mean and/or convergence in probability of the estimator to the integral of interest. Moreover, we suggest median modified methods and show for integrands in L p L^{p} with p > 1 p>1 consistency in terms of almost sure convergence.



Keywords:
median of means, Monte Carlo method, consistency, randomized integration methods.

Classification:
65C05; 65D30.

Introduction

In computational statistics and numerical analysis one of the major challenges is the development and investigation of estimators of expectations. A prototypical setting is the approximation of the integral

I(f)=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f(x)\,{\rm d}x

(1)

w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure for an integrable function
f\colon[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
with
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
Given a (sufficiently rich) probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, on which all random variables are defined, as well as
a measurable space
(G,\mathcal{G})
, a measurable function
g\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
and a
G
-valued random variable
Y
we can ask more generally for the computation of

I(g)=\mathbb{E}[g(Y)].

(2)

For this goal (structured) Monte Carlo methods have been constructed.
We call
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
a Monte Carlo method if for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and integrable
g
we have that
S_{n}g
is a real-valued random variable of the form
S_{n}g=\Phi_{n}(g(X_{1}),\dots,g(X_{N_{n}}))
, where
\Phi_{n}
is a suitable (random) function, each
X_{i}
is a
G
-valued random variable and
N_{n}
is a
\mathbb{N}
-valued random variable that determines the number of allowed function evaluations for
g
.
Classical examples of such methods (exploiting different properties of
g
) for the approximation of
I(g)
are for instance antithetic sampling, importance sampling or control variate based estimators, see e.g.
[
MGNR12
,
Owe13
]
.

We focus on the prototypical setting of the approximation of
I(f)
from (
1
), where
G=[0,1]^{d}
, and consider structured methods in that context.
A minimal requirement for a reasonable proxy is that for sufficiently large and increasing
n
it gets ‘close’ to
I(f)
whenever
f
is integrable. We ask for the sequence of random
variables
(S_{n}f)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
that it at least, either, converges in probability, i.e., for
any
\varepsilon>0
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[|S_{n}f-I(f)|>\eps\right]=0,

(3)

or
converges in (absolute) mean, i.e.,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{n}f-I(f)|\right]=0.

(4)

Note that also other formulations of convergence are feasible. For example, convergence in mean squared sense, that is,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[|S_{n}f-I(f)|^{2}]=0
. However, for
\mathbb{E}[|S_{n}f-I(f)|^{2}]
to be finite one requires
f
to be square integrable instead of just integrable, such that we do not further consider this concept. Another desirable asymptotic behavior, at least a more demanding property than (
3
), is given by almost sure convergence, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}\left[\lim_{n\to\infty}S_{n}f=I(f)\right]=1.

(5)

For the classical Monte Carlo estimator
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i})
, based on an iid sequence
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of random variables, uniformly distributed in
[0,1]^{d}
,
(
3
) is known as weak law of large numbers (WLLN) and (
5
) as strong law of large numbers (SLLN). Thus, we have convergence in probability and almost sure convergence for integrable
f
of the standard estimator.
Motivated by this fact, we also sometimes say a (general) Monte Carlo method
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies such a law of large numbers if the corresponding limit property ((
3
) or (
5
)) holds.

Depending on integrability properties of the integrand
f
we provide conditions for Monte Carlo methods
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
to satisfy different types of consistency, namely convergence in probability and in mean as well as almost sure convergence. In fact, our results apply to more general methods which are introduced in Section
2
.
For
p\geq 1
let
L^{p}[0,1]^{d}
be the set of all measurable functions
f\colon[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
with finite
\|f\|_{p}^{p}=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}|f(x)|^{p}{\rm d}x
.
We consider methods
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, any
f,g\in L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}

we have

1)

linearity, i.e.,
S_{n}(\alpha f+\beta g)=\alpha S_{n}f+\beta S_{n}g
almost surely,

2)

monotonicity, i.e.,
|S_{n}f|\leq S_{n}|f|
almost surely, and

3)

unbiasedness, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[S_{n}f]=I(f)
.

We call a method
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
linear, monotone or unbiased, respectively, if the corresponding property holds for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Generically in Theorem
2.1
we show that for this class of estimators convergence in mean and in probability is equivalent for
f\in L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
.
Moreover, we consider estimators of (
1
) based on randomized
(t,d)
-sequences
[
Owe95
]
, Cranley-Patterson rotations
[
CP76
]
, Latin hypercube sampling
[
MBC79
]
and randomized Frolov point sets
[
KN17
]

and show that all of these methods are consistent regarding convergence in probability and mean whenever
f\in L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
.
After that, we argue for
f\in L^{p}[0,1]^{d}
with
p>1
how to get consistency in terms of almost sure convergence for median modified methods. In the latter scenario we follow the approach of
[
OR21
]
.

Now let us discuss how these results fit into the literature and whether they can be improved.
Given a Monte Carlo method
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
f\in L^{p}[0,1]^{d}
with
p\geq 1
we define the expected absolute error as
\mathbb{E}[|S_{n}f-I(f)|]
and the
probability of failure
\varepsilon>0
as

\mathbb{P}[|S_{n}f-I(f)|>\eps]
.
Then, by virtue of

[
KR19
, Theorem 2.3]
there are constants
\eps_{0}>0
,
c>0
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
with the following properties:
For any
n\geq n_{0}
,
\eps\in(0,\eps_{0})
and any arbitrary Monte Carlo method
(S_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\mathbb{E}N_{m}\leq m
we have

\sup_{\|f\|_{1}\leq 1}\mathbb{P}\left[|S_{n}f-I(f)|>\eps\right]\geq c.

(6)

As a consequence, see also
[
Hei94
,
Nov88
]
, for the expected absolute error and
\eps=\eps_{0}/2
we obtain

\sup_{\|f\|_{1}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{n}f-I(f)|\right]\geq\frac{c\cdot\eps_{0}}{2}.

(7)

By (
6
) and (
7
), convergence in probability and convergence in mean cannot be improved to convergence uniformly
for all
f\in L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
with
\|f\|_{1}\leq 1
.
However, it is well known that the classical Monte Carlo estimator satisfies the SLLN for
f\in L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
and that this implies the WLLN.
Therefore, for fixed
f\in L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
and a given Monte Carlo method (or a randomized integration method as defined in Section
2
) it is reasonable to ask for consistency regarding the convergence concepts mentioned above, even when the uniform probability of failure and the uniform expected absolute error do not decrease.

Motivated by Bayesian optimization
[
BKJ+20
]
the recent work
[
OR21
]
provides a SLLN for
(t,d)
-sequences
randomized by a nested uniform scrambling for
f\in L^{p}[0,1]^{d}
with
p>1
. The arguments there are based on the interpolation theorem of Riesz-Thorin as well as a subsequence technique.
We consider a median of general estimators and show a SLLN without using the subsequence technique. However, we also require for the integrand
f\in L^{p}[0,1]^{d}
with
p>1
.
In contrast to that, our results about convergence in probability and in mean, see Theorem
2.1
, Corollary
2.4
and Corollary
2.5
, require only
f\in L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
.
We apply these to different settings including randomized digital nets and Latin hypercube sampling, therefore extending the WLLNs of
[
Loh96
,
OR21
]
.
Moreover, the consequence of the convergence in mean of those estimators on
L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
is to the best of our knowledge new.

Now we briefly outline the structure of the paper. Section
2
contains our results w.r.t. convergence in mean and in probability.
In particular, Theorem
2.1
, Corollary
2.4
and Corollary
2.5
establish both types of convergence for
f\in L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
.
We discuss estimators based on samples from randomized
(t,d)
-sequences, Latin hypercube sampling, Cranley-Patterson rotations and the randomized Frolov algorithm for which our results are applicable.
In Section
3
we describe how the median of independent realizations improves the
probability of failure
and how it can be used to derive a SLLN for
f\in L^{p}[0,1]^{d}
with
p>1
.

Convergence in mean and in probability

The following theorem and its consequences provide our main tools for establishing convergence in probability and in mean for the aforementioned Monte Carlo methods.
However, our results hold for a larger class of estimators. Namely, we call a sequence of operators
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
randomized integration method if
S_{n}\colon L^{1}[0,1]^{d}\to\mathcal{R}(\Omega)
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\mathcal{R}(\Omega)
denotes the set of all real-valued random variables on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
.
Clearly, any Monte Carlo method is a randomized integration method and,
moreover, the definitions of linearity, monotonicity and unbiasedness can be directly extended.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a randomized integration method that is linear, monotone and unbiased. Let

\mathcal{D}\subset L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
be dense w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{1}
.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

For any
f\in L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[|S_{n}f-I(f)|]=0
.

(ii)

For any
\phi\in\mathcal{D}
we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[|S_{n}\phi-I(\phi)|]=0
.

(iii)

For any
\phi\in\mathcal{D}
we have that
(S_{n}\phi)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in probability to
I(\phi)
.

(iv)

For any
f\in L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
we have that
(S_{n}f)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in probability to
I(f)
.

Proof.

First, observe that (
i
) implies (
ii
) and applying Markov’s inequality yields that (
ii
) implies (
iii
).

We argue now that (
iv
) is a consequence of (
iii
). For this let
f\in L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
and
\eps>0
be arbitrary. Moreover, let
\delta\in(0,1)
be arbitrary and choose
\phi\in\mathcal{D}
such that
\left\|f-\phi\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{\eps\delta}{6}<\frac{\eps}{3}
.
We observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|S_{n}f-I(f)\right|>\eps\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\left|S_{n}f-S_{n}\phi\right|>\frac{\eps}{3}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[\left|S_{n}\phi-I(\phi)\right|>\frac{\eps}{3}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left[\left|I(\phi)-I(f)\right|>\frac{\eps}{3}\right].

Combining Markov’s inequality with linearity, monotonicity and unbiasedness of
S_{n}
we deduce

\mathbb{P}\left[|S_{n}f-S_{n}\phi|>\frac{\eps}{3}\right]\leq\frac{3}{\eps}\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{n}(f-\phi)|\right]\leq\frac{3}{\eps}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{n}|f-\phi|\right]=\frac{3}{\eps}\left\|f-\phi\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{\delta}{2}.

The choice of
\phi
implies that
\mathbb{P}\left[|I(f)-I(\phi)|>\eps/3\right]=0
, since
|I(f)-I(\phi)|\leq\left\|f-\phi\right\|_{1}<\eps/3
.
By (
iii
) we have convergence in probability for
\phi\in\mathcal{D}
, hence there is some
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
n\geq n_{0}
holds
\mathbb{P}\left[|S_{n}\phi-I(\phi)|>\eps/3\right]\leq\delta/2
.
In conclusion

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|S_{n}f-I(f)\right|>\eps\right]\leq\delta,

for any
n\geq n_{0}
. By the fact that
\delta\in(0,1)
was arbitrary we have that (
iv
) follows.

It is left to show that (
i
) is a consequence of (
iv
).
For this we first consider an arbitrary non-negative
f\in L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
.
We aim to apply a well known characterization of convergence in mean w.r.t. convergence in probability that is (for the convenience of the reader) formulated in Lemma
A.1
.
Monotonicity of
S_{n}
implies that
S_{n}f\leq|S_{n}f|\leq S_{n}|f|=S_{n}f
almost surely.
Hence
S_{n}f=|S_{n}f|
almost surely and consequently

\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{n}f|\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[S_{n}f\right]=I(f).

This shows that
\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{n}f|\right]\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a constant sequence only taking the value
I(f)=\mathbb{E}[I(f)]=\mathbb{E}[|I(f)|]
, which is clearly also its limit. By assumption we know that
(S_{n}f)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in probability to
I(f)
and therefore we can apply Lemma
A.1
with
Y_{n}=S_{n}f
and
Y=I(f)
. We obtain

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[|S_{n}f-I(f)|]=0.

For the general case, i.e., when
f
is not necessarily non-negative, we split
f
into its positive and negative part,
f=f^{+}-f^{-}
, with non-negative (integrable) functions
f^{+},f^{-}
.
Linearity of
S_{n}
and the integral together with the triangle inequality imply

\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{n}f-I(f)|\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{n}f^{+}-I(f^{+})|\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{n}f^{-}-I(f^{-})|\right].

Since both,
f^{+}
and
f^{-}
, are non-negative we have that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[|S_{n}f^{+}-I(f^{+})|]=0\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[|S_{n}f^{-}-I(f^{-})|]=0.

Those limits give (
i
) and therefore the proof is finished.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

Theorem
2.1
can be considered as generalization of a classical result appearing in the context of deterministic quadrature rules, cf.
[
BP11
, Theorem 3.1.2]
.
There, one has an uniform operator norm requirement of the quadrature rule which in our setting is somehow hidden in the assumptions about
S_{n}
.
In particular, by monotonicity and unbiasedness of
S_{n}
we have

\left\|S_{n}f\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}=\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{n}f|\right]\leq\mathbb{E}[S_{n}|f|]=\left\|f\right\|_{1}.

Considering
f\equiv 1
, i.e., the function which only takes the value
1
, we obtain
\left\|S_{n}\right\|_{L^{1}[0,1]^{d}\to L^{1}(\Omega)}=\sup_{\|f\|_{1}\leq 1}\left\|S_{n}f\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}=1
independent of
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Remark 2.3
.

Linearity, monotonicity and unbiasedness are not sufficient to obtain consistency.
For instance, let
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}
be independent and uniformly distributed in
[0,1]^{d}
and set
X_{j}=Y_{k}
if
j\;\mbox{mod}\;m=k
.
Then, consider

S_{n}f=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(X_{j}),

where
f\in L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
.
It is clearly linear, monotone and unbiased, but it is not consistent.
Indeed, if
A=\left[0,\frac{1}{2}\right]\times[0,1]\times\dots\times[0,1]
,
\eps\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
and
g\equiv\mathds{1}_{A}
,
then for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
n\;\mbox{mod}\;m=0
follows that

S_{n}g=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}g(Y_{j})
and consequently

\mathbb{P}\left[|S_{n}g-I(g)|>\eps\right]\geq\mathbb{P}\left[S_{n}g=1\right]=\frac{1}{2^{m}},

such that we have no consistency.

The formerly stated theorem can be used to verify convergence in probability and in mean by exploiting
the equivalences regarding the dense subset
\mathcal{D}
.
To emphasize that, we add the following simple tool.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a linear, monotone and unbiased randomized integration method and let
p\geq 1
.
Assume that
(S_{n}f)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in probability or in mean for arbitrary
f\in L^{p}[0,1]^{d}
to
I(f)
.
Then, for any
f\in L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
we have that
(S_{n}f)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in probability and in mean to
I(f)
.

Proof.

Denote by
C^{\infty}_{c}=C^{\infty}_{c}(0,1)^{d}
the set of all functions that are infinitely often differentiable and whose support is a compact subset of
(0,1)^{d}
.
We have that
C^{\infty}_{c}\subset L^{p}[0,1]^{d}
for any
p\geq 1
, and it is a well known fact that
C^{\infty}_{c}
is dense in
L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
w.r.t.
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1}
.
(In fact one has that
C^{\infty}_{c}
is dense in
L^{1}(0,1)^{d}
. However, the open and closed unit cube only differ by a set of measure zero, so the denseness easily extends to
L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
.)

If
(S_{n}f)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in probability or in mean for any
f\in L^{p}[0,1]^{d}
, then in particular we have this property for
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}
and the statement of the corollary is a consequence of Theorem
2.1
.
∎

Many structured methods are designed in such a way that they are unbiased and for
f\in L^{2}[0,1]^{d}
their variance
\Var[S_{n}f]=\mathbb{E}[|S_{n}f-I(f)|^{2}]
decreases.
Assuming the underlying method is of this kind and satisfies the assumptions of the former corollary we can add the following tool for verifying the desired consistency for integrable functions.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a randomized integration method which is linear, monotone and unbiased.
Assume that for any
f\in L^{2}[0,1]^{d}
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Var[S_{n}f]=0.

Then, for
f\in L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
we have that
(S_{n}f)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in probability and mean to
I(f)
.

Proof.

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have for any
f\in L^{2}[0,1]^{d}
that

\mathbb{E}[|S_{n}f-I(f)|]\leq\Var[S_{n}f].

By assumption this yields convergence in mean on
L^{2}[0,1]^{d}
, which implies by Corollary
2.4
the claimed result.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

In Theorem
2.1
and its consequences Corollary
2.4
and Corollary
2.5
we restricted ourselves to
G=[0,1]^{d}
, however,
this assumption can be relaxed to obtain convergence in probability and mean for estimating
I(g)
as in (
2
).

Now we turn to examples.
First, we consider estimators of the form

S_{n}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in P_{n}}f(y),

(8)

where
S_{n}
uses exactly
n
function evaluations, that is,
P_{n}=\{X_{1}^{(n)},\dots,X_{n}^{(n)}\}\subset[0,1]^{d}
is a random point set, i.e.,
X_{i}^{(n)}
denotes a
[0,1]^{d}
-valued random variable for any
i=1,\dots,n
. Hence
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Monte Carlo method with
N_{n}\equiv~n
.
Obviously,
S_{n}
is linear, monotone and whenever
P_{n}
consists of uniformly distributed random variables in
[0,1]^{d}
it is also unbiased.

The first two examples that we consider rely on exploiting the convergence on dense subsets of
L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
, see Theorem
2.1
and Corollary
2.4
.

Example 2.7
.

We consider randomized
(t,d)
-sequences as in
[
Owe95
]
.
The underlying deterministic points are
(t,d)
-sequences, which also prove to be useful for numerical integration (cf.
[
DP10
]
), and randomization is done, roughly speaking, by randomly permuting digits.
One obtains a sequence of random points, denoted by
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, which consists of uniformly distributed points and satisfies crucial properties of a
(t,d)
-sequence with probability one, see
[
Owe95
]
.
Setting
P_{n}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
and defining
S_{n}
as specified in (
8
) we indeed have a linear, monotone and unbiased estimator.

For
p>1
the method
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

satisfies a SLLN, as recently shown in
[
OR21
]
, and consequently also a WLLN for
f\in L^{p}[0,1]^{d}
.
Thus all requirements to apply Corollary
2.4
are met and for
f\in L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
it follows that

(S_{n}f)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in probability and in mean to
I(f)
.

Example 2.8
.

A Cranley-Patterson rotation, cf.
[
CP76
]
, randomizes deterministic points
\{a_{1}^{(n)},\dots,a^{(n)}_{n}\}\subset[0,1]^{d}
to
P_{n}=\{X^{(n)}_{1},\dots,X^{(n)}_{n}\}
by setting
X^{(n)}_{i}:=a^{(n)}_{i}+U\mod 1
(coordinate-wise) for
i=1,\dots,n
, where
U\sim\text{Unif}[0,1]^{d}
.
The
X^{(n)}_{i}
’s are again uniformly distributed in
[0,1]^{d}
, hence
S_{n}
as given in (
8
) is linear, monotone and unbiased.
We refer to
[
Owe13
, Chapter 17.3]
for a more detailed discussion about this method.

Employing
[
DP10
, Proposition 2.18]
, which is a version of the Koksma-Hlawka inequality, and
[
Owe13
, Inequality (17.9)]
it follows that for
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}
with probability one

|S_{n}\phi-I(\phi)|\leq c_{1}\cdot D(\{X^{(n)}_{1},\dots,X^{(n)}_{n}\})\leq c_{2}\cdot D(\{a^{(n)}_{1},\dots,a^{(n)}_{n}\}).

Here
c_{1},c_{2}
are finite constants depending on
\phi,d
only. Moreover,
C^{\infty}_{c}
is as in the proof of Corollary
2.4
and
D(\cdot)
denotes the discrepancy (of a point set), see for instance the book
[
DP10
]
for details.

Assume now
\lim_{n\to\infty}D(\{a^{(n)}_{1},\dots,a^{(n)}_{n}\})=0
, then we immediately obtain convergence in mean of
S_{n}
for functions in
C^{\infty}_{c}
and by virtue of Theorem
2.1
it follows that
(S_{n}f)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in probability and in mean for
f\in L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
.

Under rather mild conditions, regarding the construction of a
Hammersley point set
or a
lattice point set
or a
(t,d)
-sequence

the discrepancy for any of these point sets decreases to zero.
However, it might be necessary to switch to a subsequence,
cf.
[
DP10
]
.

We want to emphasize here that no variance estimate for
S_{n}
has been used.
In particular obtaining useful variance estimates of
S_{n}
for Cranley-Patterson rotated general point sets
\{a_{1}^{(n)},\dots,a_{n}^{(n)}\}
with decreasing discrepancy seems to a be a difficult task, especially since we allow here that
a_{j}^{(n)}\neq a_{j}^{(k)}
for
n\neq k
and
1\leq j\leq\min\{n,k\}
.

In the following two examples it is very convenient to exploit variance estimates, i.e., we apply Corollary
2.5
.

Example 2.9
.

We study Latin hypercube sampling
[
MBC79
]
.
The corresponding algorithm produces uniformly distributed points satisfying stratification properties.
We refer to
[
Owe13
]
for a detailed introduction and for more recent results to
[
GH21
]
.

Using those points for
P_{n}
within
S_{n}
of (
8
) yields a linear, monotone and unbiased estimator.
From
[
St87
]
, see also
[
Owe13
, Proposition 10.1]
, for
f\in L^{2}[0,1]^{d}
it follows that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Var[S_{n}f]=0
such that from Corollary
2.5
we obtain the statement of convergence in mean and probability of
(S_{n}f)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
to
I(f)
for
f\in L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
.
Consistency of Latin hypercube sampling is also very briefly mentioned in
[
AHT12
,
PS08
]
.
There it is meant in terms of convergence in probability for
f\in L^{2}[0,1]^{d}
and one even has a SLLN in this case, cf.
[
Loh96
]
.

We add an example where
S_{n}
does not take the form of (
8
).

Example 2.10
.

Randomized Frolov points, as studied in
[
KN17
]
and later in
[
Ull17
]
,
rely on a random shift and dilation of (deterministic) Frolov points (cf.
[
Ull16
]
for an introductory paper), which themselves provide a powerful tool for numerical integration in Sobolev spaces, see

[
UU16
]
for a survey.

For technical reasons we extend any
f\colon[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
to a function defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by setting it zero outside the unit cube.
Hence for the integral of interest holds
I(f)=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f(x){\rm d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x){\rm d}x
.

Here for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
the estimator
S_{n}
is given by

S_{n}f=\frac{1}{|\det(A_{n})|}\sum_{y\in P_{n}}f(y),

where
A_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a suitable random matrix and
P_{n}
is the set of corresponding randomized Frolov points, which in particular depend on
A_{n}
.
We emphasize that
N_{n}=\mbox{card}(P_{n})
, where
\mbox{card}(P_{n})
denotes the cardinality of
P_{n}
, is a random variable and

\mathbb{E}\,[|\det(A_{n})|]=\mathbb{E}\left[N_{n}\right]=n.

For details we refer to
[
Ull17
, Section 2.1]
.
Obviously one has a linear and monotone estimator and
[
KN17
, Lemma 3]
guarantees that
S_{n}
is also unbiased.

For
f\in L^{2}[0,1]^{d}
, extended as above, we employ
[
Ull17
, Theorem 1.1]
and Plancherel’s identity (see e.g.
[
Gra14
, Theorem 2.2.14]
) to deduce that

\Var\left[S_{n}f\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{n}f-I(f)|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{c\cdot\left\|f\right\|_{2}^{2}}{n},

(9)

where
c\in(0,\infty)
does not depend on
f
and
n
.
Thus Corollary
2.5
is applicable and it follows that
(S_{n}f)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in probability and in mean to
I(f)
for
f\in L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
.
Note that in
[
Ull17
]
the definition and analysis of this method is done for more general domains than
[0,1]^{d}
.
Therefore taking Remark
2.6
into account this example can be generalized to different domains.

Almost sure convergence via median modification

In this section we derive almost sure convergence statements for
f\in L^{p}[0,1]^{d}
, with
p>1
, by using median modified methods and combining techniques that were recently used in
[
KR19
,
OR21
,
KNR19
]
.
Let
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be
a randomized integration method,
k\in\mathbb{N}
an
odd number and

f\in L^{p}[0,1]^{d}
for some
p>1
.
Throughout the whole section we write
S_{n,k}
for the median of
k
independent realizations of
S_{n}
, denoted by
S_{n}^{(1)},\dots,S_{n}^{(k)}
, i.e.,

S_{n,k}f=\med\{S_{n}^{(1)}f,\dots,S_{n}^{(k)}f\}.

We start with an auxiliary tool, see Proposition
3.4
, which is then applied to obtain SLLNs for median modified methods relying on Latin hypercube sampling and randomized Frolov points.

Slightly modifying
[
NP09
, Proposition 2.1, see also (2.6)]
in
[
KR19
, Proposition 1]
the following lemma was proven.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
f\in L^{1}[0,1]^{d}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\eps>0
assume that
S_{n}
satisfies

\mathbb{P}\left[|S_{n}f-I(f)|>\eps\right]\leq\alpha,

for some
\alpha\geq 0
.
Then, for any odd
k
we have

\mathbb{P}\left[|S_{n,k}f-I(f)|>\eps\right]\leq\alpha^{k/2}2^{k}.

Remark 3.2
.

In
[
KR19
]
Lemma
3.1
is proven in the case where
S_{n}
uses exactly
n
function evaluations.
However, reviewing the proof of
[
NP09
, Proposition 2.1]
it follows that the number of used function values does not matter
and Lemma
3.1
also holds for general randomized integration methods.

Remark 3.3
.

Recently for median modified randomized quasi-Monte Carlo methods, see
[
PO21
,
PO22
,
GL22
]
, worst case error bounds that hold with high probability have been proven. There, more regularity of the integrand is required and non-asymptotic statements similar as in
[
KR19
]
are available.

Combining Lemma
3.1
and the Riesz-Thorin interpolation theorem, see Theorem
A.2
below, we obtain the following result.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
p\in(1,2)
,
k>2/(p-1)
be an odd number and
let
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a linear, monotone and unbiased randomized integration method.
Assume there is a constant
c\in(0,\infty)
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any
f\in L^{2}[0,1]^{d}
we have

\Var\left[S_{n}f\right]\leq\frac{c\cdot\left\|f\right\|_{2}^{2}}{n}.

Then, the median of
k
independent copies of
S_{n}f
satisfies a SLLN for
f\in L^{p}[0,1]^{d}
, i.e., for any
f\in L^{p}[0,1]^{d}
the sequence of random variables
(S_{n,k}f)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges almost surely to
I(f)
.

Proof.

Define the measure spaces
\mathcal{A}_{1}=([0,1]^{d},\mathcal{B}([0,1]^{d}),\lambda_{d})
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, where
\lambda_{d}
denotes the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure.
Set
p_{1}=1
,
p_{2}=2
and
\theta=2-\frac{2}{p}
. For
i\in\{1,2\}
we denote by
L^{p_{i}}(\mathcal{A}_{1})
and
L^{p_{i}}(\mathcal{A}_{2})
the corresponding Lebesgue spaces.
Then, the operators
T_{n}\colon L^{p_{i}}(\mathcal{A}_{1})\to L^{p_{i}}(\mathcal{A}_{2})
with
T_{n}f=S_{n}f-I(f)
are linear and bounded.
An application of the Riesz-Thorin interpolation theorem, see Theorem
A.2
below, yields

\left\|T_{n}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{A}_{1})\to L^{p}(\mathcal{A}_{2})}\leq\frac{2^{1-\theta}c^{\theta/2}}{n^{\theta/2}}=\frac{c_{p}}{n^{1-\frac{1}{p}}},

with
c_{p}=2^{2/p-1}c^{1-1/p}
.
Here we used, with the same notation as in Theorem
A.2
, that
M_{1}\leq 2
since
S_{n}
is unbiased and
M_{2}\leq(c/n)^{1/2}
according to the assumption about the variance of
S_{n}
.
Therefore, by Markov’s inequality, for
f\in L^{p}[0,1]^{d}
and
\eps>0
we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[|S_{n}f-I(f)|>\eps\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\eps^{p}}\mathbb{E}\left[|T_{n}f|^{p}\right]

(10)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{\eps}\left\|T_{n}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{A}_{1})\to L^{p}(\mathcal{A}_{2})}\left\|f\right\|_{p}\right)^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{c_{p}\left\|f\right\|_{p}}{\eps}\right)^{p}\frac{1}{n^{p-1}}.

Applying Lemma
3.1
we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left[|S_{n,k}f-I(f)|>\eps\right]\leq 2^{k}\left(\frac{c_{p}\left\|f\right\|_{p}}{\eps}\right)^{pk/2}\frac{1}{n^{(p-1)k/2}}.

By the fact that
k>2/(p-1)
we have
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-(p-1)k/2}<\infty
and the result follows by the lemma of Borel-Cantelli.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Note that without using the median of several independent copies of
S_{n}
it is still possible to find SLLNs for
f\in L^{p}[0,1]^{d}
with
1<p<2
by considering
(S_{n_{j}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
for a suitably chosen (sub)sequence
(n_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Namely, for a method satisfying the same assumptions as in Proposition
3.4
we obtain by (
10
) that for
\eps>0
and any
n_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
holds

\mathbb{P}\left[|S_{n_{j}}f-I(f)|>\eps\right]\leq\left(\frac{c_{p}\left\|f\right\|_{p}}{\eps}\right)^{p}\frac{1}{n_{j}^{p-1}},

where
c_{p}
is as in the proof of Proposition
3.4
.
Thus by choosing the sequence
(n_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
in such a way that
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{n_{j}^{-p+1}}<\infty
it follows from the lemma of Borel-Cantelli that
(S_{n_{j}}f)_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
converges almost surely to
I(f)
.

We illustrate the applicability of the former proposition for Latin hypercube sampling and randomized Frolov points.

Example 3.6
.

We consider
S_{n}
with
P_{n}
based on Latin hypercube sampling as in Example
2.9
.
We argued there already that
S_{n}
is linear, monotone and unbiased.
By
[
Owe13
, Corollary 17.1]
a variance bound for
S_{n}
is established.
It allows us to use Proposition
3.4
and hence for the median
S_{n,k}
, with
k>2/(p-1)
odd, we obtain a SLLN.

Furthermore, it is also possible to deduce SLLNs for suitably chosen subsequences, for instance as suggested in Remark
3.5
.

Example 3.7
.

We continue with
S_{n}
from Example
2.10
based on randomized Frolov points, where it was already shown that
S_{n}
is linear, monotone and unbiased.
By (
9
) we have a variance estimate as required in Proposition
3.4
,
resulting in a SLLN for
f\in L^{p}[0,1]^{d}
, for the median
S_{n,k}
of
k>2/(p-1)
independent copies (
k
odd), or for
S_{n}
by passing to a suitable subsequence (see Remark
3.5
).

Appendix A
Auxiliary results

For the convenience of the reader we add here a standard result about the characterization of convergence in mean that can be, for example, found in
[
Kal21
, Theorem 5.12]
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of real-valued integrable random variables as well as
Y
being an integrable real-valued random variable.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

We have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[|Y_{n}-Y|\right]=0
.

(ii)

The sequence of random variables
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in probability to
Y
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[|Y_{n}|]=\mathbb{E}[|Y|]
.

In the proof of Proposition
3.4
we used the following version of the Riesz-Thorin interpolation theorem that can be found in
[
Gra14
, Theorem 1.3.4]
.

Theorem A.2

(Riesz-Thorin)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
be measure spaces.
Let
T
be a linear operator from
L^{p_{1}}(\mathcal{A}_{1})
to
L^{p_{1}}(\mathcal{A}_{2})
and also a linear operator from

L^{p_{2}}(\mathcal{A}_{1})
to
L^{p_{2}}(\mathcal{A}_{2})
, for some
1\leq p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\infty
.
Assume that

\left\|T\right\|_{L^{p_{1}}(\mathcal{A}_{1})\to L^{p_{1}}(\mathcal{A}_{2})}\leq M_{1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\|T\right\|_{L^{p_{2}}(\mathcal{A}_{1})\to L^{p_{2}}(\mathcal{A}_{2})}\leq M_{2}.

For
\theta\in(0,1)
define
p
via
\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1-\theta}{p_{1}}+\frac{\theta}{p_{2}}
.
Then,
T
is also a linear operator from
L^{p}(\mathcal{A}_{1})
to
L^{p}(\mathcal{A}_{2})
satisfying

\left\|T\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{A}_{1})\to L^{p}(\mathcal{A}_{2})}\leq M_{1}^{1-\theta}M_{2}^{\theta}.
