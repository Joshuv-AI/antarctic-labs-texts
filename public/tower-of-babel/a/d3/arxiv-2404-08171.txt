The Rank- 1 1 Completion Problem for Cubic Tensors
Jinling Zhou, Jiawang Nie, Zheng Peng, Guangming Zhou

Abstract
. This paper studies the rank- 1 1 tensor completion problem for cubic tensors. First of all, we show that this problem is equivalent to a special rank- 1 1 matrix recovery problem. When the tensor is strongly rank- 1 1 completable, we show that the problem is equivalent to a rank- 1 1 matrix completion problem and it can be solved by an iterative formula. For other cases, we propose both nuclear norm relaxation and moment relaxation methods for solving the resulting rank- 1 1 matrix recovery problem. The nuclear norm relaxation sometimes returns a rank- 1 1 tensor completion, while sometimes it does not. When it fails, we apply the moment hierarchy of semidefinite programming relaxations to solve the rank- 1 1 matrix recovery problem. The moment hierarchy can always get a rank- 1 1 tensor completion, or detect its nonexistence. Numerical experiments are shown to demonstrate the efficiency of these proposed methods.

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{F}
be the real filed
\mathbb{R}
or complex filed
\mathbb{C}
.
A tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{m}}

can be viewed as the multi-array indexed such that

\mathcal{A}=(\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}})_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i_{1}\leq n_{1},\ldots,1\leq i_{m}\leq n_{m}.\end{subarray}}

The integer
m
is called the
\mathnormal{order}
of
\mathcal{A}
.
When
m=3
, we call
\mathcal{A}
a cubic tensor.
For vectors
u_{k}\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{k}}
,
k=1,\cdots,m
,
the notation
u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m}

denotes the tensor in
\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{m}}
such that

(u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m})_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}=(u_{1})_{i_{1}}\cdots(u_{m})_{i_{m}},

for all indices
i_{1},\cdots,i_{m}
. Tensors of the form

u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m}

are called rank-
1
tensors. When
n_{1}=\cdots=n_{m}=n
,
tensors in
\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{m}}

are said to be
hypercubical
. We denote the hypercubical tensor space

\mathtt{T}^{m}(\mathbb{F}^{n})\,\coloneqq\,\mathbb{F}^{n}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{n}\qquad\text{($\mathbb{F}^{n}$ appears $m$ times)}.

When it is hypercubical, the tensor
\mathcal{A}

is said to be symmetric if

\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}=\mathcal{A}_{j_{1}\cdots j_{m}}

for every permutation
(i_{1}\cdots i_{m})
of
(j_{1}\cdots j_{m})
.
The subspace of symmetric tensors in
\mathtt{T}^{m}(\mathbb{F}^{n})

is denoted as
\mathtt{S}^{m}(\mathbb{F}^{n})
.
The dimension of
\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{m}}
is
n_{1}\cdots n_{m}
,
while the dimension of
\mathtt{S}^{m}(\mathbb{F}^{n})
is
\binom{n+m-1}{m}
.

An important concept for tensors is rank.
For
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{m}}
,
its rank is the smallest
r
such that

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}+\cdots+\mathcal{A}_{r},

where each
\mathcal{A}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{m}}
is of rank
1
.
The above equation is also often referenced as
Candecomp-Parafac (CP) decomposition
and the smallest
r
is referred as CP rank
in the literature
[
19
]
.
We refer to
[
3
,
6
,
10
,
17
,
36
,
38
,
43
,
47
]

for the work of computing tensor decompositions.
It is interesting to remark that the tensor rank
depends on the ground field
[
29
]
.
There also exist other types of tensor ranks, such as border rank
and multi-linear rank.
We refer to the work
[
9
,
20
,
25
,
29
]

for various types of ranks for tensors.

The tensor completion problem (TCP) is to find values for missing entries
of a partially given tensor so that it has certain property, e.g., it has low rank.
TCP has broad applications, such as
computer vision
[
31
,
39
,
40
]
,
recommendation systems
[
14
,
22
]
,
imaging and signal processing
[
30
,
50
]
.
We refer to
[
23
]
for more applications of tensors.

Tensor completion is an extension of matrix completion.
The nuclear norm relaxation is frequently used to get matrix completions

[
4
,
5
,
42
]
.
Nuclear norms are also defined for tensors
[

,
35
]
.
Tensor nuclear norm relaxations can be extended
to solve tensor completion or recovery problems

[
32
,
46
,
48
,
49
]
.
Optimization methods based on CP decompositions can be used to

[

,

,

]
.
Tucker decomposition based techniques are also applicable to get

[
31
,
41
]
.
Recently, there are Riemannian-manifold optimization methods
for getting tensor completions

[
11
,
15
,
24
,
45
]
.

Many tensor optimization problems can be formulated as
moment or polynomial optimization (see
[
34
, Chapter 11]
).
Tensor nuclear norms can be computed by solving
moment relaxations
[
35
,
46
]
.
Real eigenvalues of tensors can be obtained by solving

[

,
37
]
.
Stable rank-one matrix completions can be solved by
the level-
2
Moment-SOS relaxation
[

Contributions

This paper focuses on rank-
1
completions for cubic tensors.
It looks for a rank-
1
tensor that has given values for some of its entries.
Consider a partially given tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
,
i.e., there is a subset

\Omega\,\subseteq\,[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}]\times[n_{3}]

such that the entry
\mathcal{A}_{ijk}
is given for all
(i,j,k)\in\Omega
.
The rank-
1
tensor completion problem is to look for a rank-
1
tensor

a\otimes b\otimes c
such that

a_{i}b_{j}c_{k}=\mathcal{A}_{ijk}
for all
(i,j,k)\in\Omega
.
So, the problem can formulated as

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\text{find}&(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\mathcal{A}_{ijk}=a_{i}b_{j}c_{k},\,\,(i,j,k)\in\Omega.\end{array}\right.

For the above
a,b,c
, the equation
\mathcal{A}=a\otimes b\otimes c

is called a rank-
1
tensor completion for
\mathcal{A}
.
The geometric properties of the rank-
1
tensor completion problem
are well studied by Kahle et al.
[
21
]
.
We remark that finding a rank-
1
tensor completion
is equivalent to solving the polynomial optimization problem:

\min_{a,b,c}\quad\sum_{(i,j,k)\in\Omega}(\mathcal{A}_{ijk}-a_{i}b_{j}c_{k})^{2}.

It has
3
vector variables
a,b,c
and the polynomial has degree six.
However, solving the above polynomial optimization directly is not computationally attractive.
This is shown in Example
7.1
.

In this paper, we show that the rank-
1
tensor completion problem
is equivalent to a special rank-
1
matrix recovery problem.
To be precise, we show that (
1.1
)
is equivalent to

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\text{find}&X=ab^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}j_{1}k}X_{i_{2}j_{2}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{2}j_{2}k}X_{i_{1}j_{1}}=0\\
&\qquad\text{for}\quad(i_{1},\,j_{1},\,k),(i_{2},j_{2},k)\in\Omega,\,k=1,\ldots,n_{3}.\end{array}\right.

The equality constraint in (
1.2
)
is equivalent to the determinantal equation

\det\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}j_{1}k}&X_{i_{1}j_{1}}\\
\mathcal{A}_{i_{2}j_{2}k}&X_{i_{2}j_{2}}\end{bmatrix}=0.

The problem (
1.2
)
is a special class of rank-
1
matrix recovery.
The relationship between (
1.1
) and (
1.2
)
is studied in Subsection
3.1
.

A specially interesting case arises when
the values of
X_{ij}
for
(i,j,k)\in\Omega

are uniquely determined (up to scaling) by
equality constraints in (
1.2
).
For this case, the tensor completion problem can be reduced to
the rank-
1
matrix completion problem
and the tensor
\mathcal{A}
is said to be strongly rank-1 completable
(see Definition
3.3
),
if a rank-
1
tensor completion exists.
Furthermore, if the corresponding bipartite graph is connected,
the rank-
1
matrix completion problem can be solved
by an iterative formula.
These results are shown in Subsection
3.2
.

When
\mathcal{A}
is not strongly rank-1 completable,
a natural approach for solving (
1.2
)
is to apply the nuclear norm relaxation, shown in Subsection
3.3
.
The nuclear norm relaxation may fail to find a rank-
1
tensor completion.
However, we can always get a rank-
1
tensor completion
by solving the moment hierarchy of semidefinite programming relaxations,
or we detect its nonexistence.
This is shown in Section
4
.
When the tensor
\mathcal{A}
is symmetric, there are more attractive properties
for the nuclear norm and moment relaxations.
This is shown in Section
5
.
Our proposed method for cubic tensors can be naturally
extended to higher order tensors, shown in Section
6
.
Numerical experiments are provided in Section
7
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

The symbol
\mathbb{N}
(resp.,
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
)
denotes the set of nonnegative integers (resp., real, complex numbers).
For integer
k>0
,
[k]
stands for the set
\left\{1,\ldots,k\right\}
.
For a finite set
T
, its cardinality is denoted as
|T|
.
We denote by
\mathbb{R}[x]
the ring of polynomials in

x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
and with real coefficients.
The subset of polynomials of degree
d
in
\mathbb{R}[x]

is denoted by
\mathbb{R}[x]_{d}
.
The superscript
T
denotes the transpose of a matrix or vector.
For a symmetric matrix
X
, the inequality
X\succeq 0
(resp.,
X\succ 0
)
means that
X
is positive semidefinite (resp., positive definite).
The cone of all
N
-by-
N
real symmetric positive semidefinite matrices
is denoted as
\mathcal{S}_{+}^{N}
.
A subset
I

\subseteq\mathbb{R}\left[x\right]

is called an ideal if
pq\in I
for all
p\in I

and for all
q\in\mathbb{R}\left[x\right]
,
and
a+b\in I
for all
a,b\in I
. For a tuple
h=(h_{1},\cdots,h_{m})

of polynomials in
\mathbb{R}\left[x\right]
, we denote the ideal

\mathit{Ideal}[h]\coloneqq h_{1}\cdot\mathbb{R}[x]+\cdots+h_{m}\cdot\mathbb{R}[x].

For a set of polynomials
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}[x]
,
its real variety is the set

V_{\mathbb{R}}(P)\,\coloneqq\,\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\,p(x)=0\,\,\forall p\in P\right\}.

A polynomial
p
is said to vanish on a set
T
if
p(u)=0
for all
u\in T
.
If so, we write
p\equiv 0
on
T
or
p|_{T}\equiv 0
.

For
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, define

\left|\alpha\right|\coloneqq\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}
.
We denote the power set

\mathbb{N}_{d}^{n}\coloneqq\left\{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}:\,\left|\alpha\right|\leq d\right\}.

For
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, we denote the monomial power

x^{\alpha}\coloneqq x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}.

The column vector of all monomials in
x
and of degrees up to
d
is denoted

\left[x\right]_{d}\coloneqq\left[1\quad x_{1}\quad\cdots\quad x_{n}\quad x_{1}^{2}\quad x_{1}x_{2}\quad\cdots\quad x_{n}^{d}\right]^{T}.

The length of the vector
\left[x\right]_{d}
is
(\begin{subarray}{c}n+d\\
d\end{subarray})
.

A polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}\left[x\right]
is said to be a sum of squares (SOS)
if there exist polynomials
p_{1},\cdots,p_{k}\in\mathbb{R}\left[x\right]

such that
f=p_{1}^{2}+\cdots+p_{k}^{2}
. The set of all SOS polynomial in
\mathbb{R}[x]

is denoted as
\Sigma[x]
.
For a degree
d
, we denote the truncation

\Sigma\left[x\right]_{d}\,\coloneqq\,\Sigma\left[x\right]\cap\mathbb{R}[x]_{d}.

It is well known that (see
[
34
, Sec. 2.4]
)
a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[x]_{2d}
is SOS if and only if
there exists
X\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{N}
, with
N=\binom{n+d}{d}
, such that

(2.1)

f=\left[x\right]_{d}^{T}\cdot X\cdot\left[x\right]_{d}.

For a tuple
g=(g_{1},\cdots,g_{m})
, its quadratic module is the set

\mathit{QM}[g]\,\coloneqq\,\Sigma\left[x\right]+g_{1}\cdot\Sigma\left[x\right]+\cdots+g_{m}\Sigma\left[x\right].

When
g
is empty,
\mathit{QM}[\emptyset]=\Sigma[x]
.
For a degree
d
, we denote the truncation (let
g_{0}=1
)

\mathit{QM}[g]_{d}\,\coloneqq\,\Big\{\sum_{i=0}^{m}\sigma_{i}g_{i}:\sigma_{i}\in\Sigma[x],\,\deg(\sigma_{i}g_{i})\leq d\Big\}.

2.2.
Moment and localizing matrices

The notation
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}}
stands for the space of real vectors

y
that are indexed by
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}
, i.e.,

y=(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}}.

Such a vector
y
is called a truncated multi-sequence (tms) of degree
d
.
The tms
y
is said to admit a Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
if

y_{\alpha}=\int x^{\alpha}d\mu\quad\text{for all}\,\,\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}.

If it exists, such
\mu
is called a representing measure for
y
.
The support of
\mu
is the smallest closed set
T
such that

\mu(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus T)=0
, and we denote it by
\mbox{supp}(u)
.
The measure
u
is said to be supported in a set
K
if
\mbox{supp}(\mu)\subseteq K
.

For
f\in\mathbb{R}[x]_{d}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}}
,
we define the bilinear operation

\langle f,y\rangle\,\coloneqq\,\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}}f_{\alpha}y_{\alpha}\quad\text{for}\quad f=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}}f_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}.

For an integer
\ell\in[0,d/2]
,
the
\ell
th order moment matrix of
y
is the symmetric matrix

M_{\ell}[y]
such that

(2.2)

\langle p^{2},y\rangle\,\coloneqq\,\mbox{vec}(p)^{T}\cdot M_{l}[y]\cdot\mbox{vec}(p)\qquad\text{for all}\quad p\in\mathbb{R}[x]_{\ell}.

Here
\mbox{vec}(p)
denotes the coefficient vector of
p
,
listed in the graded lexicographical order.
Indeed, one can see that

M_{\ell}[y]=(y_{\alpha+\beta})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{\ell}^{n},\,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{\ell}^{n}}.

For instance, when
n=2
and
\ell=2
, we have

M_{2}[y]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccccc}y_{00}&y_{10}&y_{01}&y_{20}&y_{11}&y_{02}\\
y_{10}&y_{20}&y_{11}&y_{30}&y_{21}&y_{12}\\
y_{01}&y_{11}&y_{02}&y_{21}&y_{12}&y_{03}\\
y_{20}&y_{30}&y_{21}&y_{40}&y_{31}&y_{22}\\
y_{11}&y_{21}&y_{12}&y_{31}&y_{22}&y_{13}\\
y_{02}&y_{12}&y_{03}&y_{22}&y_{13}&y_{04}\\
\end{array}\right].

For a polynomial
h\in\mathbb{R}[x]_{d}
, denote by
\mathscr{V}_{h}^{(d)}[y]

the vector such that

\left\langle h\cdot(v^{T}[x]_{s}),y\right\rangle\,=\,v^{T}\mathscr{V}_{h}^{(d)}[y],

for every column vector
v

of length
\binom{n+s}{s}
, where
s=d-\deg(h)
.
The
\mathscr{V}_{h}^{(d)}[y]
is called the localizing vector of
h

and generated by
y

[
34
]
.
For instance, when
h=1-x_{1}x_{2}
and
n=2,d=4
, we have

\mathscr{V}_{h}^{(4)}[y]\,=\,\begin{bmatrix}y_{00}-y_{11}\\
y_{10}-y_{21}\\
y_{01}-y_{12}\\
y_{20}-y_{31}\\
y_{11}-y_{22}\\
y_{02}-y_{13}\end{bmatrix}.

Given a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[x]
and a finite set of polynomials
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}[x]
,
we consider the constrained optimization problem:

(2.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&f(x)\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&p(x)=0\,\,\,\text{for}\,\,\,p\in P.\end{array}\right.

Its global optimizers can be computed by moment relaxations.
For
\ell=1,2,\ldots
, the
\ell
th order moment relaxation is

(2.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\langle f,y\rangle\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\mathcal{V}_{p}^{(2\ell)}[y]=0\,\,\,\text{for}\,\,\,p\in P,\\
&M_{\ell}[y]\succeq 0,\\
&y_{0}=1,\,y\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}_{2\ell}^{\bar{n}}}.\end{array}\right.

Its dual optimization problem is the
\ell
th order SOS relaxation

(2.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\max&\gamma\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&f-\gamma\in\mathit{Ideal}[P]_{2\ell}+\Sigma[x]_{2\ell}.\end{array}\right.

The sequence of relaxations (
2.4
)–(
2.4
)
is often referenced as the Moment-SOS hierarchy for solving (
2.3
).
We refer to
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
34
]
for more detailed introductions
to the Moment-SOS hierarchy.

3.
Rank-1 tensor completion and matrix recovery

For a partially given tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
,
let
\Omega
be the set of indices
(i,j,k)

such that the tensor entry
\mathcal{A}_{ijk}
is given.
It is a subset of
[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}]\times[n_{3}]
.
We write
\Omega
as the union

(3.1)

\Omega\,=\,\bigcup_{k=1}^{n_{3}}\Omega_{k},

where the set
\Omega_{k}
is

\Omega_{k}\,\coloneqq\,\left\{(i_{1},j_{1},k),\,(i_{2},j_{2},k),\cdots(i_{m_{k}},j_{m_{k}},k)\right\}.

Let
m_{k}=\left|\Omega_{k}\right|
, the cardinality of
\Omega_{k}
.
If
\mathcal{A}=a\otimes b\otimes c
,
then for each
k=1,\ldots,n_{3}
,

(3.2)

\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}j_{1}k}\\
\mathcal{A}_{i_{2}j_{2}k}\\
\vdots\\
\mathcal{A}_{i_{m_{k}}j_{m_{k}}k}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{i_{1}}b_{j_{1}}\\
a_{i_{2}}b_{j_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
a_{i_{m_{k}}}b_{j_{m_{k}}}\end{bmatrix}c_{k}.

The above implies
\mbox{rank}\,B_{k}\leq 1
, for the matrix

B_{k}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}j_{1}k}&a_{i_{1}}b_{j_{1}}\\
\mathcal{A}_{i_{2}j_{2}k}&a_{i_{2}}b_{j_{2}}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\mathcal{A}_{i_{m_{k}}j_{m_{k}}k}&a_{i_{m_{k}}}b_{j_{m_{k}}}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Clearly,
\mbox{rank}\,B_{k}\leq 1
if and only if all

2
-by-
2
minors of
B_{k}
are zeros, i.e.,

(3.3)

\det\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}&a_{i_{s}}b_{j_{s}}\\
\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}&a_{i_{t}}b_{j_{t}}\end{bmatrix}=\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}a_{i_{t}}b_{j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}a_{i_{s}}b_{j_{s}}=0,

for all
1\leq s<t\leq m_{k}
.
This gives
\binom{m_{k}}{2}
quadratic equations.
There are totally
\sum_{k=1}^{n_{3}}\binom{m_{k}}{2}
such equations.
In the above, we have
\mbox{rank}\,B_{k}=1
if at least one entry of

B_{k}
is nonzero.

Let
(\hat{i},\hat{j},\hat{k})

be the index of any tensor entry of largest absolute value:

(3.4)

|\mathcal{A}_{\hat{i}\hat{j}\hat{k}}|\,\,=\max_{(i,\,j,\,k)\in\Omega}\left|\mathcal{A}_{{i}{j}{k}}\right|.

For a rank-
1
completion
\mathcal{A}=a\otimes b\otimes c
with nonzero
a,b
,
we can scale them such that
a_{\hat{i}}=b_{\hat{j}}=1
.
So, the rank-
1
tensor completion is equivalent to

(3.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\mbox{find}&(a,\,b,\,c)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}a_{i_{t}}b_{j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}a_{i_{s}}b_{j_{s}}=0\\
&\qquad\text{for}\quad(i_{s},j_{s},k),(i_{t},j_{t},k)\in\Omega_{k},\,k=1,\ldots,n_{3},\\
&\mathcal{A}_{ijk}=a_{i}b_{j}c_{k}\quad\text{for}\quad(i,\,j,\,k)\in\Omega,\\
&a_{\hat{i}}=b_{\hat{j}}=1.\\
\end{array}\right.

3.1.
Reduction to matrix recovery

If we let
X=ab^{T}
, then
(
3.3
)
is equivalent to

(3.6)

\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}X_{i_{t}j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}X_{i_{s}j_{s}}=0

for all feasible indices
s,t,k
.
If
\mathcal{A}
has a rank-
1
completion, i.e.,there is a pair
(a,b)

satisfying (
3.3
), then the matrix
X=ab^{T}
satisfies (
3.6
).
Therefore, we consider the matrix recovery problem

(3.7)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\text{find}&X=ab^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}X_{i_{t}j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}X_{i_{s}j_{s}}=0\\
&\qquad\text{for}\quad(i_{s},\,j_{s},\,k),(i_{t},\,j_{t},\,k)\in\Omega_{k},\,k=1,\ldots,n_{3},\\
&X_{\hat{i}\hat{j}}=1,\end{array}\right.

where the pair
(\hat{i},\hat{j})
is as in (
3.4
).
The relationship between the tensor completion problem (
3.5
)
and the matrix recovery problem (
3.7
)
is given as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}
is the partially given tensor as above.
If
X=ab^{T}
is feasible for (
3.7
) and
for each
k=1,\ldots,n_{3}
, the following equation

(3.8)

\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}j_{1}k}\\
\mathcal{A}_{i_{2}j_{2}k}\\
\vdots\\

3.2.
Strongly rank-1 completable tensors

We consider a special class of rank-
1
tensor completion problems
that can be reduced to rank-
1
matrix completions.
Denote the index set

(3.9)

\widetilde{\Omega}\,\coloneqq\,\left\{(i,\,j):\,(i,\,j,\,k)\in\Omega\right\}.

It is the projection of
\Omega
on the first two indices.
The equations of (
3.6
) are homogenous
in the vector of partial matrix entries

X_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\,\coloneqq\,(X_{ij})_{(i,j)\in\widetilde{\Omega}}.

Note that
X_{\widetilde{\Omega}}
can be viewed as a vector in

\mathbb{R}^{\widetilde{\Omega}}
.
The set of solutions to the linear system (
3.6
)
is a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{\widetilde{\Omega}}
.
We are interested in the case that (
3.6
)
has a unique solution (up to scaling), i.e.,
the subspace of its solutions is one dimensional.
This leads to the following definition.

Definition 3.3
.

The partially given tensor
\mathcal{A}
is strongly rank-
1
completable
if it has a rank-
1
completion and the subspace of solutions

X_{\widetilde{\Omega}}
to (
3.6
)
is one dimensional.

Let
(\hat{i},\hat{j},\,\hat{k})
be the index as in (
3.4
).
If
X_{\widetilde{\Omega}}
is a nonzero solution to (
3.6
),
we can scale it such that
X_{\hat{i}\hat{j}}=1
.
If the subspace of solutions
X_{\widetilde{\Omega}}
to (
3.6
)
is one dimensional, there exist scalars
w_{ij}
such that

(3.10)

X_{\hat{i}\hat{j}}=1,\quad X_{ij}=w_{ij}\quad\text{for}\,\,(i,j)\in\widetilde{\Omega}.

So, when
\mathcal{A}
is strongly rank-
1
completable,
the problem (
3.7
) is equivalent to
the rank-
1
matrix completion problem

(3.11)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\text{find}&X=ab^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&X_{ij}=w_{ij}\quad\text{for}\quad(i,\,j)\in\widetilde{\Omega},\\
&X_{\hat{i}\hat{j}}=1.\end{array}\right.

The rank-
1
matrix completion problem (
3.11
)
can be solved explicitly when the bipartite graph determined by

\widetilde{\Omega}
is connected (see
[
7
]
).
Let
V_{1}
,
V_{2}
be the sets:

V_{1}=\{i\in[n_{1}]:(i,j)\in\widetilde{\Omega}\},\quad V_{2}=\{j\in[n_{2}]:(i,j)\in\widetilde{\Omega}\}.

Consider the bipartite graph
G(V_{1},\,V_{2},\,\widetilde{\Omega})
with vertex sets

V_{1},V_{2}
and whose edge set is
\widetilde{\Omega}
.
When
G(V_{1},\,V_{2},\,\widetilde{\Omega})
is connected, the rank-
1
matrix

X
satisfying (
3.11
) is unique and
it can be determined by an iterative formula.
This can be seen as follows.
For
X_{\hat{i}\hat{j}}=1
, we can let

a_{\hat{i}}=1,\quad b_{\hat{j}}=1.

Since
G(V_{1},\,V_{2},\,\widetilde{\Omega})
is connected,
there exist indices
i_{1},j_{1}
such that

(i_{1},\hat{j})\in\widetilde{\Omega}
and

(\hat{i},j_{1})\in\widetilde{\Omega}
, so we can get

a_{i_{1}}=w_{i_{1}\hat{j}},\quad b_{j_{1}}=w_{\hat{i}j_{1}}.

Similarly, there exist indices
i_{2},j_{2}
such that

(i_{1},j_{2})\in\widetilde{\Omega}
and

(i_{2},j_{1})\in\widetilde{\Omega}
, so

a_{i_{2}}=w_{i_{2}j_{1}}/b_{j_{1}},\quad b_{j_{2}}=w_{i_{1}j_{2}}/a_{i_{1}}.

When the bipartite graph
G(V_{1},\,V_{2},\,\widetilde{\Omega})
is connected,
repeating the above can produce edge connections:

\begin{gathered}a_{i_{1}}\rightarrow b_{j_{2}}\rightarrow a_{i_{3}}\rightarrow b_{j_{4}}\rightarrow\cdots,\\
b_{j_{1}}\rightarrow a_{i_{2}}\rightarrow b_{j_{3}}\rightarrow a_{i_{4}}\rightarrow\cdots.\end{gathered}

So, the entries of
a,b
can be given by the iterative formula

(3.12)

a_{i_{l+1}}=w_{i_{l+1}j_{l}}/b_{j_{l}},\quad b_{j_{l+1}}=w_{i_{l}j_{l+1}}/a_{i_{l}},\quad l=1,2,\ldots.

When the graph
G(V_{1},\,V_{2},\,\widetilde{\Omega})
is not connected,
it is a union of connected subgraphs.
We can do similar things for each of them.
For such a case, the rank one completion for
X
is not unique.
We refer to
[
7
]
for this.

The following is an example of applying the iterative formula
(

) to get rank-
1

3.3.
Nuclear norm relaxation

We discuss the case that the partially given tensor
\mathcal{A}
is not strongly rank-
1
completable.
To find a feasible rank-
1
matrix
X
for (
3.7
),
a frequently used approach is to solve the nuclear norm relaxation
(
\|X\|_{*}
denotes the nuclear norm of
X
, i.e.,
the sum of all its singular values):

(3.15)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\left\|X\right\|_{\ast}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}X_{i_{t}j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}X_{i_{s}j_{s}}=0\\
&\qquad\text{for}\quad(i_{s},\,j_{s},\,k),(i_{t},\,j_{t},\,k)\in\Omega_{k},\,k=1,\ldots,n_{3},\\
&X_{\hat{i}\hat{j}}=1.\end{array}\right.

The matrix nuclear norm minimization
can be equivalently reformulated as a semidefinite program
(see
[
5
,
12
]
).
Indeed, up to applying the singular value decomposition of
X
,
one can show that
\|X\|_{\ast}
equals the minimum value of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\frac{1}{2}\big(\mbox{Trace}(W_{1})+\mbox{Trace}(W_{2})\big)\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\begin{bmatrix}W_{1}&X\\
X^{T}&W_{2}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0.\end{array}\right.

Therefore, (
3.15
)
is equivalent to the semidefinite program

(3.16)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\mbox{Trace}(W_{1})+\mbox{Trace}(W_{2})\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}X_{i_{t}j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}X_{i_{s}j_{s}}=0\\
&\qquad\text{for}\quad(i_{s},\,j_{s},\,k),(i_{t},\,j_{t},\,k)\in\Omega_{k},\,k=1,\ldots,n_{3},\\
&\begin{bmatrix}W_{1}&X\\
X^{T}&W_{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,\,X_{\hat{i}\hat{j}}=1.\end{array}\right.

We would like to remark that the nuclear norm relaxation (
3.15
)
sometimes returns a rank-
1
matrix
X
for (
3.7
),
while sometimes it does not. This is shown in the following example.

.

(i) Consider the tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4\times 4}

\begin{matrix}\mathcal{A}_{121}=2,&\mathcal{A}_{131}=4,&\mathcal{A}_{441}=1,&\mathcal{A}_{112}=4,\\

Solving the nuclear norm relaxation (

(ii) Consider the tensor

4.
The Moment-SOS Relaxations

The nuclear norm relaxation may not return a rank-
1
tensor completion.
When this is the case, we propose a moment hierarchy of
semidefinite programming relaxations to solve the problem.
As shown in Section
3

1
tensor completion
problem (
3.5

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\text{find}&(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}a_{i_{t}}b_{j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}a_{i_{s}}b_{j_{s}}=0\\

The above pair

To solve (

where

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&f(a,\,b)\\

We refer to

),
which are contained in the support of the representing measure for the subvector

y^{*}|_{2t}\,\coloneqq\,(y^{*}_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{\bar{n}}_{2t}}.

When coefficients of a polynomial are generically chosen
(i.e., the vector of its coefficients is chosen from a Zariski open set),
its optimizer is unique (see
[
16
, Section 6.3]
).
Since the objective
f
is generic,
the optimization problem (
4.2
) has a unique minimizer,
so the rank condition (
4.7
) holds.

Second, consider the case that
V_{\mathbb{R}}(\Phi)\neq\emptyset
is a smooth variety.
When
f
is generic, (
4.5
) has a unique minimizer
u
.
Since
V_{\mathbb{R}}(\Phi)
is smooth, there exists a finite set

P\subseteq\mathbb{R}[x]
such that
\mathit{Ideal}[P]=\mathit{Ideal}[\Phi]
and the gradient vector set

\{\nabla p(v)\}_{p\in P}
is linearly independent for every
v\in V_{\mathbb{R}}(\Phi)
.
So, the constraint in (
4.5
) is equivalent to that

p(x)=0
for every
p\in P
. This means
the classical linear independence qualification condition holds at
u

(see
[
34
, Section 5.1]
).
Since
f
is generic, the second order sufficiency condition also holds at
u

(see
[
34
, Section 5.5]
).
Since
V_{\mathbb{R}}(\Phi)
is compact,

\mathit{Ideal}[\Phi]+\Sigma[x]
is archimedean.
Therefore, the moment hierarchy of semidefinite programming relaxations (
4.6
)
has finite convergence. It is shown in
[
34
, Theorem 5.4.2]
that
when
\ell
is big enough,
the moment relaxation (
4.6
) has minimizers
and each minimizer
y^{*}
must satisfy the rank condition (
4.10
).
Moreover, there are
\mbox{rank}\,M_{t}[y^{*}]
minimizers for (
4.5
),
which are contained in the support of the representing measure for
y^{*}|_{2t}
.
Since
u
is the unique minimizer,
the rank condition (
4.7
) holds.
∎

5.
Symmetric rank-1 tensor completions

We discuss rank-
1
tensor completions for symmetric tensors.
Note that a symmetric rank-
1
tensor
\mathcal{A}
can be written as
a^{\otimes 3}
.
Therefore, we can set
a=b=c
in the previous sections.
In (
3.5
), the variable
b
can be replaced by
a
.
Consequently, the nuclear norm relaxation and moment relaxation
have smaller sizes for the matrix variables.
Assume the dimension

n_{1}=n_{2}=n_{3}=n.

For a partially given symmetric tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathtt{S}^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
we still let
\Omega
denote the set of
(i,j,k)

such that the value of
\mathcal{A}_{ijk}
is given.
Since
\mathcal{A}
is symmetric, we can assume
\Omega
is invariant under
permutations, i.e., for each
(i,j,k)\in\Omega
,
every permutation of
(i,j,k)
also belongs to
\Omega
.
The index set
\Omega
can still be decomposed as in (
3.1
).

The cubic symmetric tensor
\mathcal{A}
is of rank-
1
if and only if

\mathcal{A}=a^{\otimes 3}
, for some vector
0\neq a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
(\hat{i},\hat{j},\hat{k})
be the index as in (
3.4
).
Then, we can write
a
as

(5.1)

a=\sqrt[3]{\tau}v,\quad v_{\hat{i}}v_{\hat{j}}=1,\quad\tau\in\mathbb{R},\,\,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Therefore, we can look for
(v,\tau)
satisfying the system:

(5.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}v_{i_{t}}v_{j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}v_{i_{s}}v_{j_{s}}=0\\
\qquad\text{for}\quad(i_{s},\,j_{s},k),(i_{t},\,j_{t},k)\in\Omega_{k},\,k=1,\ldots,n,\\
\mathcal{A}_{ijk}=\tau v_{i}v_{j}v_{k}\quad\text{for}\,\,\,(i,\,j,\,k)\in\Omega,\\
v_{\hat{i}}v_{\hat{j}}=1,\,\,\tau\in\mathbb{R}.\end{array}\right.

Let
V=vv^{T}
be the symmetric matrix variable,
then (
5.2
) is equivalent to

(5.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\text{ find }&V=vv^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}V_{i_{t}j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}V_{i_{s}j_{s}}=0\\
&\qquad\text{for}\quad(i_{s},\,j_{s},k),(i_{t},\,j_{t},k)\in\Omega_{k},\,k=1,\ldots,n,\\
&V_{\hat{i}\hat{j}}=1,\,\,V\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}.\end{array}\right.

In the above,
\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}
denotes the cone of all

n
-by-
n
real symmetric positive semidefinite matrices.

When
\mathcal{A}
is strongly rank-
1
completable,
(
5.3
) can be reduced to the rank-
1
matrix completion problem:

(5.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\text{find}&V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\mbox{rank}\,V=1,\\
&V_{ij}=w_{ij}\quad\text{for}\,\,\,(i,\,j)\in\widetilde{\Omega},\\
&V\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n},\end{array}\right.

where the values
w_{ij}
are given in (
3.10
).
When the bipartite graph
G(V_{1},\,V_{2},\,\widetilde{\Omega})
is connected,
the rank-
1
matrix
V=vv^{T}
can be similarly found
by the iterative formula (
3.12
).

When
\mathcal{A}
is not strongly rank-
1
completable,
we can look for a rank-
1
matrix solution for (
5.3
)
by solving the nuclear norm relaxation.
Since
V
is symmetric positive semidefinite,
the nuclear norm
\|V\|_{*}=\mbox{Trace}(V)
.
So, the nuclear norm relaxation of (
5.3
) is

(5.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\mbox{Trace}(V)\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}V_{i_{t}j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}V_{i_{s}j_{s}}=0\\
&\qquad\text{for}\quad(i_{s},\,j_{s},k),(i_{t},\,j_{t},k)\in\Omega_{k},\,k=1,\ldots,n,\\
&V_{\hat{i}\hat{j}}=1,\,\,V\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}.\end{array}\right.

The nuclear norm relaxation (
5.5
) may or may not produce
a rank-
1
matrix completion (see Example
7.6
).

When (
5.5
) fails to return a rank-
1
matrix,
we can find a symmetric rank-
1
tensor completion by solving moment relaxations.
Note that (
5.2
) can be equivalently formulated as

(5.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}v_{i_{t}}v_{j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}v_{i_{s}}v_{j_{s}}=0\\
\qquad\text{for}\quad(i_{s},j_{s},k),(i_{t},j_{t},k)\in\Omega_{k},\,k=1,\ldots,n,\\
v_{\hat{i}}=1.\end{array}\right.

To get a feasible point
v
for (
5.6
),
we select a quadratic objective

f(v)=v^{T}Fv.

A feasible solution for (
5.6
)
can be found by solving

(5.7)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&f(v)\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}v_{i_{t}}v_{j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}v_{i_{s}}v_{j_{s}}=0\\
&\qquad\text{for}\quad(i_{s},\,j_{s},\,k),(i_{t},\,j_{t},\,k)\in\Omega_{k},\,k=1,\ldots,n.\\
&v_{\hat{i}}=1.\end{array}\right.

When
F
is a generic positive definite matrix,
the optimization problem (
5.7
) must have a minimizer
and the minimizer is unique, if (
5.6
) is feasible.
For cleanness of the paper, we omit the details and refer to Section
4
.

6.
Extension to higher order tensors

The previous sections focus on rank-
1
completions for cubic tensors.
However, the proposed methods can be naturally extended to higher order tensors.
A tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}

can be reshaped as a tensor of order
3
.
For instance, when
d=4
, one can reshape
\mathcal{A}
as the cubic tensor

\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times(n_{3}n_{4})}
such that

\mathcal{A}_{ijkl}\,=\,\mathcal{B}_{ij\ell}\quad\text{for}\quad\ell=(k-1)n_{4}+l.

The resulting
\mathcal{B}
is a partially given cubic tensor.
We can apply our earlier proposed methods to get a rank-
1
completion

\mathcal{B}\,=\,a\otimes b\otimes\hat{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times(n_{3}n_{4})}.

If
\hat{c}
can also be reshaped as a rank-
1
matrix, say,
cd^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}\times n_{4}}
,
then the above produces a rank-
1
tensor completion

\mathcal{A}=a\otimes b\otimes c\otimes d
.
However, if
\hat{c}
cannot be reshaped as a rank-
1
matrix,
then this does not give a rank-
1
completion for
\mathcal{A}
.

Example 6.1
.

(i)
Consider the tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2\times 2\times 2}

with given entries:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathcal{A}_{2211}=12,&\mathcal{A}_{1121}=4,&\mathcal{A}_{2121}=8,\\
\mathcal{A}_{2221}=4,&\mathcal{A}_{1112}=6,&\mathcal{A}_{2222}=2.\\
\end{array}

It can be reshaped as the cubic tensor
\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2\times 4}
such that

\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathcal{B}_{221}=12,&\mathcal{B}_{112}=4,&\mathcal{B}_{212}=8,\\
\mathcal{B}_{222}=4,&\mathcal{B}_{113}=6,&\mathcal{B}_{224}=2.\\
\end{array}

By solving the moment relaxation (
4.6
), we get

\mathcal{B}=a^{*}\otimes b^{*}\otimes\hat{c}
, with

\begin{array}[]{lcl}a^{*}=(\frac{1}{2},\,1),\quad b^{*}\,=\,(2,\,1),\quad\hat{c}=(12,\,4,\,6,\,2).\end{array}

The vector
\hat{c}
can be reshaped to the rank-
1
matrix
c^{*}{d^{*}}^{T}
with

c^{*}=(12,\,4),\quad d^{*}=(1,\,\frac{1}{2}).

Therefore, we get the rank-
1
completion

\mathcal{A}=a^{*}\otimes b^{*}\otimes c^{*}\otimes d^{*}
.

(ii)
Consider the tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3\times 3\times 3}

with given entries:

\begin{array}[]{lllll}\mathcal{A}_{1311}=6,&\mathcal{A}_{3111}=24,&\mathcal{A}_{3211}=12,&\mathcal{A}_{3311}=12,&\mathcal{A}_{2321}=4,\\
\mathcal{A}_{2231}=8,&\mathcal{A}_{3231}=8,&\mathcal{A}_{3331}=8,&\mathcal{A}_{2122}=4,&\mathcal{A}_{2322}=2,\\
\mathcal{A}_{3122}=4,&\mathcal{A}_{2313}=18,&\mathcal{A}_{1223}=3,&\mathcal{A}_{2233}=12,&\mathcal{A}_{2333}=12.\\
\end{array}

It can be reshaped as the cubic tensor
\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3\times 9}
such that

\begin{array}[]{lllll}\mathcal{B}_{131}=6,&\mathcal{B}_{311}=24,&\mathcal{B}_{321}=12,&\mathcal{B}_{331}=12,&\mathcal{B}_{232}=4,\\
\mathcal{B}_{223}=8,&\mathcal{B}_{323}=8,&\mathcal{B}_{333}=8,&\mathcal{B}_{215}=4,&\mathcal{B}_{235}=2,\\
\mathcal{B}_{315}=4,&\mathcal{B}_{237}=18,&\mathcal{B}_{128}=3,&\mathcal{B}_{229}=12,&\mathcal{B}_{239}=12.\\
\end{array}

By solving the moment relaxation (
4.6
), we get

\mathcal{B}=a^{*}\otimes b^{*}\otimes\hat{c}
, with

a^{*}=(1,\,2,\,2),\quad b^{*}\,=\,(2,\,1,\,1),\quad\hat{c}=(6,\,2,\,4,\,0,\,1,\,0,\,9,\,3,\,6).

There are no equations for
c_{4}
and
c_{6}
, so we give zero values for them.
The vector
\hat{c}
cannot be reshaped to a rank-
1
matrix,
so the above does not produce a rank-
1
tensor completion.
However, we remark that this tensor
\mathcal{A}
is rank-
1
completable,
e.g.,
\mathcal{A}=a\otimes b\otimes c\otimes d
with

a=(1,\,2,\,2),\quad b=(2,\,1,\,1),\quad c=(3,\,1,\,2),\quad d=(2,\,1,\,3).

Conclusions

This paper studies the rank-
1
tensor completion problem for cubic tensors.
It aims at finding missing values of a partially given tensor
so that it is of rank-
1
.
We reformulate this problem equivalently as
a special rank-
1
matrix recovery problem,
which looks for a rank-
1
matrix
satisfying a set of linear equations.
We propose both nuclear norm relaxation and moment relaxation methods
for solving the resulting rank-
1
matrix recovery problem.
The nuclear norm relaxation sometimes get a rank-
1
tensor completion,
but sometimes it does not.
The moment relaxation always get a rank-
1
tensor completion
or detect its nonexistence.
For computational comparison, the nuclear norm relaxation approach
solves relatively larger problems,
while the moment relaxation approach solves relatively smaller ones.
For the special class of strongly rank-
1
completable tensors,
the problem can be reduced to a rank-
1
matrix completion problem.
When the corresponding bipartite graph is connected,
a rank-
1
tensor completion can be obtained
by applying an iterative formula.
For strongly rank-
1
completable tensors,
much larger problems can be solved efficiently.
Numerical experiments are provided to demonstrate
the efficiency of these proposed methods.
