Studying Deformations of Fuchsian Representations with Higgs Bundles This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Geometry and Physics of Hitchin Systems. The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/hitchin-systems.html
Brian COLLIER

Abstract
This is a survey article whose main goal is to explain how many components of the character variety of a closed surface are either deformation spaces of representations into the maximal compact subgroup or deformation spaces of certain Fuchsian representations. This latter family is of particular interest and is related to the field of higher Teichmüller theory. Our main tool is the theory of Higgs bundles. We try to develop the general theory of Higgs bundles for real groups and indicate where subtleties arise. However, the main emphasis is placed on concrete examples which are our motivating objects. In particular, we do not prove any of the foundational theorems, rather we state them and show how they can be used to prove interesting statements about components of the character variety. We have also not spent any time developing the tools (harmonic maps) which define the bridge between Higgs bundles and the character variety. For this side of the story we refer the reader to the survey article of Q. Li [arXiv:1809.05747].

An introduction to the character variety

Let
S
be a closed oriented surface of genus
g\geq 2
. Denote the fundamental group of
S
by
\Gamma
, and recall that
\Gamma
has the standard presentation

\displaystyle\Gamma=\left\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{g},b_{1},\dots,b_{g}\,|\,\prod\limits_{j=1}^{g}[a_{j},b_{j}]=1\right\rangle.

Fix also a real reductive Lie group
{\mathsf{G}}
. For example
{\mathsf{G}}
could be one of the following groups

\textstyle{{\mathsf{GL}}(n,{\mathbb{R}}),}
\textstyle{{\mathsf{GL}}(n,{\mathbb{C}}),}
\textstyle{{\mathsf{SL}}(n,{\mathbb{C}}),}
\textstyle{{\mathsf{Sp}}(2n,{\mathbb{R}}),}
\textstyle{{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(n,{\mathbb{R}})={\mathsf{SL}}(n,{\mathbb{R}})/\pm{\mathrm{Id}},}
\textstyle{{\mathsf{U}}(n),}
\textstyle{{\mathsf{SO}}(n),}
\textstyle{{\mathsf{SU}}(p,q),}
\textstyle{{\mathsf{SO}}(p,q),}
\textstyle{{\mathsf{S}}({\mathsf{O}}(p)\times{\mathsf{O}}(q)),}

but
{\mathsf{G}}

cannot
be a group like

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}=\big\{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
0&c\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in{\mathsf{GL}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\big\}.

One main property of a reductive Lie group
{\mathsf{G}}
is that, up to conjugation, there is a unique maximal compact subgroup
{\mathsf{H}}<{\mathsf{G}}
. We will heavily use this property. In fact there is a homotopy equivalence

\displaystyle{\mathsf{H}}\simeq{\mathsf{G}}.

A group homomorphism
\rho\colon\Gamma\to{\mathsf{G}}
will be referred to as a
representation
. Consider the set
{\mathrm{Hom}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})
of all such representations. This set has a topological structure induced by the inclusion

\textstyle{{\mathrm{Hom}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{{\mathsf{G}}^{2g},}
\textstyle{\rho\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{(\rho(a_{1}),\dots,\rho(b_{g})).}

Since a representation
\rho
must satisfy
\prod\limits_{j=1}^{g}[\rho(a_{j}),\rho(b_{j})]={\mathrm{Id}}
, the image consists of
2g
-tuples of elements of
{\mathsf{G}}
which satisfy this relation. For semisimple groups, the dimension of
{\mathrm{Hom}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})
was computed in
[
13
]
to be

\displaystyle\dim({\mathrm{Hom}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}}))=2g\cdot\dim{\mathsf{G}}-\dim{\mathsf{G}}.

The group
{\mathsf{G}}
acts on
{\mathrm{Hom}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})
by conjugation: for
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
g\in{\mathsf{G}}
,

\displaystyle(\rho\cdot g)(\gamma)=g^{-1}\rho(\gamma)g.

The quotient space
{\mathrm{Hom}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})/{\mathsf{G}}
consists of conjugacy classes of representations. Unless
{\mathsf{G}}
is compact,
{\mathrm{Hom}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})/{\mathsf{G}}
is not Hausdorff.

Example 1.1
.

Let
\rho_{1}(\gamma)={\mathrm{Id}}
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
and define
\rho_{2}
by
\rho_{2}(a_{j})={\mathrm{Id}}
for all
j
and

\displaystyle\rho_{2}(b_{j})=\begin{cases}{\mathrm{Id}},&j\neq g,\\
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right),&j=g.\end{cases}

If
g_{t}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}t&0\\
0&t^{-1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, then
g_{t}^{-1}\rho_{2}(a_{j})g_{t}={\mathrm{Id}}
for all
j
and

\displaystyle g_{t}^{-1}\rho_{2}(b_{j})g_{t}=\begin{cases}{\mathrm{Id}},&j\neq g,\\
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&t^{-2}\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right),&j=g.\end{cases}

The
{\mathsf{G}}
-orbits through
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
are disjoint in
{\mathrm{Hom}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})
, but

\displaystyle\rho_{1}\in\overline{\rho_{2}\cdot G}.

Thus,
[\rho_{1}]
and
[\rho_{2}]
are not separable in
{\mathrm{Hom}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})/{\mathsf{G}}
.

To get a Hausdorff quotient we restrict to a subset of
{\mathrm{Hom}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})
. A representation
\rho\colon\Gamma\to{\mathsf{G}}
is called
reductive
if the composition with the adjoint representation

\textstyle{\Gamma\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\rho}
\textstyle{{\mathsf{G}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{{\operatorname{Ad}}}
\textstyle{{\mathsf{GL}}({\mathfrak{g}})}

decomposes as a direct sum of irreducible representations. This is equivalent to the
{\mathsf{G}}
-orbit through
\rho
being closed in
{\mathrm{Hom}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})
. When
{\mathsf{G}}
is a complex algebraic group, this is also equivalent to the Zariski closure of
\rho(\Gamma)
being a reductive subgroup.

Denote the set of reductive representations by
{\mathrm{Hom}}^{+}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})
. The
{\mathsf{G}}
-character variety of
\Gamma
is defined to be the Hausdorff space

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})={\mathrm{Hom}}^{+}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})/{\mathsf{G}}.

For semisimple groups, the dimension of
{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})
is
(2g-2)\dim({\mathsf{G}})

[
13
]
.

Remark 1.2
.

When
{\mathsf{G}}
is complex algebraic, one gets the same spaces as the GIT-quotient.

1.1
Fuchsian representations

Important examples of points in a character variety come from Fuchsian representations and are related to hyperbolic metrics on
S
. These examples will play a fundamental role throughout the article.

Let
X
be a Riemann surface structure on
S
. By the uniformization theorem,

\displaystyle X={\mathbb{H}}^{2}/\rho_{X}(\Gamma),

where
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
is the upper half-plane and
\rho_{X}\colon\Gamma\hookrightarrow{\mathsf{Bihol}}({\mathbb{H}}^{2})\cong{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
. By classical results of Riemann, there are
3g-3
complex parameters for the deformations of the Riemann surface
X
. Such deformations give rise to a
(6g-6)
-real dimensional subset of
{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}}))
. Moreover, such deformations define an open neighborhood of
\rho_{X}\in{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}}))
since
6g-6
is the dimension of
{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}}))
. This leads us to define the set of
Fuchsian representations
:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma)=\{\rho\colon\Gamma\to{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\,|\,\text{discrete and faithful}\}/{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}}).

Since being discrete and faithful is a closed condition in
{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})
,
{\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma)
is a closed subset. By the above argument,
{\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma)
is also open. Thus,
{\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma)\subset{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}}))
is an open and closed subset which is identified with the Teichmüller space of the oriented surface
S
and the surface
\bar{S}
with the opposite orientation

\displaystyle{\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma)\cong{\mathsf{Teich}}(S)\sqcup{\mathsf{Teich}}\big(\bar{S}\big).

The Teichmüller space of an oriented surface
S
is defined to be the space of isotopy classes of (marked) Riemann surface structures on
S
. This space is famously an open cell of real dimension
6g-6
.

The group
{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
is also the orientation preserving isometry group of the hyperbolic plane. Thus,
{\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma)
also parameterizes the set of isotopy classes of hyperbolic metrics on the surface
S
and
\bar{S}
.

Remark 1.3
.

The two connected components of
{\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma)\subset{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}}))
arise because elements of
{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
preserve the orientation of
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
. The group
{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{GL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
is the full isometry group of the hyperbolic plane. Thus, if we consider
{\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma)
as a subset of the
{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{GL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
-character variety, then there is only one component. Similarly, if we consider
{\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma)
as a subset of
{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-character variety, then it is a closed connected subset which is no longer open.

1.2
Connected components

One fundamental problem is to determine how many connected components a character variety has. Surprisingly, this question has not been answered in full generality. There is a topological invariant which helps distinguish connected components.

Denote the set of isomorphism classes of topological principal
{\mathsf{G}}
-bundles on
S
by
{\mathcal{B}}_{{\mathsf{G}}}(S)
, this is the set of homotopy classes of maps from
S
to the classifying space of
{\mathsf{G}}
. For connected groups we have
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathsf{G}}(S)\cong H^{2}(S,\pi_{1}{\mathsf{G}})
. Every representation
\rho\colon\Gamma\to{\mathsf{G}}
defines a principal
{\mathsf{G}}
-bundle
E_{\rho}\to S

\displaystyle E_{\rho}=\big(\tilde{S}\times{\mathsf{G}}\big)/\Gamma,

where
\Gamma
acts on its universal cover
\tilde{S}
by deck transformations and by multiplication by
\rho(\Gamma)
on
{\mathsf{G}}
. Thus, we have a map
{\mathrm{Hom}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})\to{\mathcal{B}}_{{\mathsf{G}}}(S)
. Moreover, this map is continuous and descends to a map

\displaystyle\tau\colon\ \lx@xy@svg{\hbox{\raise 2.5pt\hbox{\kern 27.46825pt\hbox{\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\hbox{\vtop{\halign{\entry@#!@&&\entry@@#!@\cr&\crcr}}}\ignorespaces{\hbox{\kern-27.46825pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 3.0pt\raise-2.5pt\hbox{$\textstyle{\pi_{0}({\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}}))\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}$}}}}}}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces{}{\hbox{\lx@xy@droprule}}\ignorespaces{\hbox{\kern 51.46825pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\lx@xy@tip{1}\lx@xy@tip{-1}}}}}}{\hbox{\lx@xy@droprule}}{\hbox{\lx@xy@droprule}}{\hbox{\kern 51.46825pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 3.0pt\raise-2.5pt\hbox{$\textstyle{{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathsf{G}}(S).}$}}}}}}}\ignorespaces}}}}\ignorespaces

If
{\mathcal{X}}^{\omega}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})=\tau^{-1}(\omega)
, then
{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})
decomposes as

\displaystyle\coprod\limits_{\omega\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathsf{G}}(S)}{\mathcal{X}}^{\omega}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}}).

Question 1.4
.

When is the map
\tau
from (
1.2
) injective? In other words, when does the topological invariant distinguish the connected components of the character variety?

Remark 1.5
.

Note that when
\tau
is injective, the question of the connected component count is not very interesting. We will mainly be interested in when
\tau
is
not
injective and understanding the special features of these components.

Question
1.4
has been answered for many groups, but is open in general. For compact groups the map
\tau
is injective, this was proven by Narasimhan–Seshadri
[
28
]
for
{\mathsf{G}}={\mathsf{U}}(n)
and Ramanathan
[
30
]
in general.

Theorem 1.6
.

If
{\mathsf{G}}
is compact
(
i.e.,
{\mathsf{G}}={\mathsf{H}})
, then
\tau
is injective. Furthermore, if
{\mathsf{G}}
is also semisimple, then
\tau
is a bijection.

Since
{\mathsf{H}}
and
{\mathsf{G}}
are homotopic, we have
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathsf{G}}(S)={\mathcal{B}}_{\mathsf{H}}(S)
. Moreover, for each
\omega\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathsf{H}}(S)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}^{\omega}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{H}})\subset{\mathcal{X}}^{\omega}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}}).

For complex groups, the map
\tau
is also injective. This was proven for by J. Li
[
24
]
for semisimple groups and García-Prada and Oliveira
[
12
]
for reductive groups.

Theorem 1.7
.

If
{\mathsf{G}}
is complex
(
i.e.,
{\mathsf{G}}={\mathsf{H}}^{\mathbb{C}})
, then
\tau
is injective.

We will prove these results using Higgs bundles in Section
6.5
. For
{\mathsf{G}}
a semisimple complex Lie group, the following corollary follows immediately from the two above theorems. It holds in general for complex reductive groups.

Corollary 1.8
.

For
{\mathsf{G}}
a complex reductive Lie group, every representation
\rho\colon\Gamma\to{\mathsf{G}}
can be continuously deformed to a compact representation
\Gamma\to{\mathsf{H}}\hookrightarrow{\mathsf{G}}
.

The above corollary says that, for complex groups, the connected components of the character variety are not interesting. For real groups, the situation is more subtle.

Example 1.9
.

For
{\mathsf{G}}={\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
, the maximal compact subgroup is
{\mathsf{H}}\cong{\mathsf{SO}}(2)
. Since a circle bundle on a closed surface is determined by its degree, we have
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathsf{H}}(S)\cong{\mathbb{Z}}
. Thus,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}}))=\coprod\limits_{d\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathcal{X}}^{d}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})).

However, the space
{\mathcal{X}}^{d}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}}))
is empty when
|d|>2g-2

[
27
]
. Moreover, when
|d|\leq 2g-2
, the space
{\mathcal{X}}^{d}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}}))
is nonempty and connected
[
14
]
. We will prove these statements using Higgs bundles in Section
4
.

For a
{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
representation
\rho
, the integer invariant can be defined as follows. Pick any
\rho
-equivariant map
f_{\rho}\colon\widetilde{S}\to{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})/{\mathsf{H}}\cong{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
. Such maps always exist since
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
is contractible. We have a principal
{\mathsf{H}}
-bundle
{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
. By equivariance, the pullback bundle
f_{\rho}^{*}{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}}))
descends to a circle bundle on
S
. Define
\tau
to be minus the degree of this bundle:

\displaystyle\tau(\rho)=-\deg((f_{\rho}^{*}{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}}))/\rho(\Gamma)).

The bundle
{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
is identified with the unit tangent bundle of
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
.
If
\rho
is a Fuchsian representation, then we may choose
f_{\rho}
to be the equivariant diffeomorphism uniformizing the Riemann surface
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}/\rho(\Gamma)
. In this case,
\tau
is given by the degree of the cotangent bundle. Namely,
\tau=2g-2
.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle{\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma)\subset{\mathcal{X}}^{2g-2}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}}))\sqcup{\mathcal{X}}^{2-2g}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})).

Since
{\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma)
is open and closed and
{\mathcal{X}}^{2g-2}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}}))
is connected, the above inclusion is an equality.

Example 1.10
.

For
{\mathsf{G}}={\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
the maximal compact subgroup is
{\mathsf{SO}}(n)
if
n
is odd and
{\mathsf{SO}}(n)/\pm{\mathrm{Id}}
when
n
is even. In this case,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}_{{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(n,{\mathbb{R}})}(S)\cong H^{2}(S,\pi_{1}{\mathsf{H}})\cong\begin{cases}{\mathbb{Z}}&\text{if $n=2$,}\\
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}&\text{if $n=2k+1$,}\\
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}&\text{if $n=4k$,}\\
{\mathbb{Z}}_{4}&\text{if $n=4k+2$.}\end{cases}

(1.3)

For
n=2k+1
, the invariant
\omega\in H^{2}(S,{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
is the second Stiefel–Whitney class of the
{\mathsf{SO}}(n)
bundle.

Example 1.11
.

For
{\mathsf{G}}={\mathsf{SO}}(p,q)
, the maximal compact subgroup is
{\mathsf{S}}({\mathsf{O}}(p)\times{\mathsf{O}}(q))
. We have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}_{{\mathsf{SO}}(p,q)}(S)\cong\begin{cases}H^{1}(S,{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{2g}&\text{if $p=q=1$},\\
H^{1}(S,{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})\times H^{2}(S,{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{2g+1}&\text{if $p=1$ and $2<q$},\\
H^{1}(S,{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})\times H^{2}(S,{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})\times H^{2}(S,{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{2g+2}&\text{if $2<p\leq q$}.\end{cases}

In the above cases, the element of
H^{1}(S,{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
is the first Stiefel–Whitney class of an orthogonal bundle and each element of
H^{2}(S,{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
is the second Stiefel–Whitney class of an orthogonal bundle.
The case of
p=2
or
q=2
is slightly more complicated.

2
Deforming Fuchsian representations

We have seen that the Teichmüller space of the surface
S
is identified with the connected components
{\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma)
of the
{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
-character variety. As a result, the representations in this component have special geometric significance. Given an embedding
\iota\colon{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathsf{G}}
, we have

\displaystyle\iota({\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma))\subset{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}}).

Question 2.1
.

What is the deformation space of
\iota({\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma))\subset{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})
like?

Here we use the word deformation space of
\iota({\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma))
to mean the connected component of
{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})
which contains
\iota({\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma))
. There are many examples of interesting embeddings of
{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
into other Lie groups, below we discuss some particular interesting ones.

2.1
Isometry groups of hyperbolic spaces

There is a natural embedding into
{\mathsf{Isom}}^{+}({\mathbb{H}}^{n})\cong{\mathsf{SO}}_{0}(1,n)
given by

\textstyle{{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\cong{\mathsf{Isom}}^{+}({\mathbb{H}}^{2})\cong{\mathsf{SO}}_{0}(1,2)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\ \ \ \ \ \ \iota_{1,n}}
\textstyle{{\mathsf{SO}}_{0}(1,n)\cong{\mathsf{Isom}}^{+}({\mathbb{H}}^{n}).}

This gives
\iota_{1,n}({\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma))\subset{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{SO}}_{0}(1,n))
, and small deformations of these representations are holonomies of complete hyperbolic
n
-manifolds called quasi-Fuchsian manifolds.

Definition 2.2
.

A representation
\rho\colon\Gamma\to{\mathsf{Isom}}({\mathbb{H}}^{n})
is called
convex cocompact
if it is discrete and faithful and
\rho(\Gamma)
acts cocompactly (properly discontinuously with compact quotient) on a convex domain in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
. The set of quasi-Fuchsian representations
{\mathsf{QFuch}}(\Gamma)\subset{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{SO}}_{0}(1,n))
is defined to be

\displaystyle{\mathsf{QFuch}}(\Gamma)=\{[\rho]\in{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{SO}}_{0}(1,n))\,|\,\text{$\rho$ is convex cocompact}\}.

Remark 2.3
.

If
[\rho]\in{\mathsf{QFuch}}(\Gamma)
, then can be deformed to
\iota_{1,n}({\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma))
. Moreover, the set of convex cocompact representations is open in
{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{SO}}_{0}(1,n))
. Thus, any sufficiently small deformation of a Fuchsian representation in
\iota_{1,n}({\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma))
is quasi-Fuchsian. However, unlike
{\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma)\subset{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{SO}}_{0}(1,2))
, the set
{\mathsf{QFuch}}(\Gamma)\subset{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{SO}}_{0}(1,n))
is
not closed
. Namely, there families of convex cocompact representations whose limit is discrete and faithful, but not convex cocompact.

In fact, we have the following:

Proposition 2.4
.

For
n>2
, any representation
\rho\in\iota_{1,n}({\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma))
can be continuously deformed to a compact representation.

Proof.

Note that it suffices to prove the statement for
{\mathsf{SO}}_{0}(1,3)
. Recall that there is an isomorphism of Lie groups
{\mathsf{SO}}_{0}(1,3)\cong{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
. The result now follows from Corollary
1.8
.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

Another interesting embedding is given by the isomorphism

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\cong{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{U}}(1,1)

and the embedding
{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{U}}(1,1)\to{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{U}}(1,n)\cong{\mathsf{Isom}}({\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{H}}^{n})
into the isometry group of the complex hyperbolic space. Deformations of
{\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma)\subset{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{U}}(1,n))
under this embedding satisfy a rigidity phenomenon
[
15
,
33
]
. This is a special case of the more general situation of maximal representations into a Hermitian Lie group of non-tube type (see for example
[
7
]
). We will not discuss this situation further.

2.2
Principal embedding

Recall that for each dimension
n
, there is a unique irreducible representation

\displaystyle\iota_{\rm pr}\colon\ {\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathsf{SL}}\big({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\big),

which is given by the
(n-1)^{\rm st}
-symmetric product of the standard representation. Moreover, it is straight forward to check that this induces an embedding

\displaystyle\iota_{\rm pr}\colon\ {\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(n,{\mathbb{R}}).

We will call this embedding the
principal embedding
.

More generally, if
{\mathsf{G}}
is a split real Lie group of adjoint type, there is a unique preferred (principal) embedding

\displaystyle\iota_{\rm pr}\colon\ {\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathsf{G}}.

(2.3)

We will not go into the Lie theory necessary to define the principal embedding in general, see
[
22
]
for more details on the general setup. We will explicitly describe
\iota_{\rm pr}
for the classical groups.

Example 2.6
.

When
n=2p+1
the principal embedding
\iota_{\rm pr}\colon{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathsf{SL}}\big({\mathbb{R}}^{2p+1}\big)
preserves a symmetric nondegenerate quadratic form of signature
(p,p+1)
. Thus, we have an embedding

\displaystyle\iota_{\rm pr}\colon\ {\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathsf{SO}}_{0}(p,p+1)\subset{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2p+1,{\mathbb{R}}).

This is the principal embedding (
2.3
) for the split group
{\mathsf{G}}={\mathsf{SO}}_{0}(p,p+1)
.

Similarly, when
n=2p
the principal embedding
\iota_{\rm pr}\colon{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathsf{SL}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2p})
preserves a nondegenerate symplectic form. Thus, we have an embedding

\displaystyle\iota_{\rm pr}\colon\ {\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{Sp}}(2p,{\mathbb{R}})\subset{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{SL}}(2p,{\mathbb{R}}).

This is the principal embedding (
2.3
) for the split group adjoint
{\mathsf{G}}={\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{Sp}}(2p,{\mathbb{R}})
.

The deformation space of
\iota_{\rm pr}({\mathsf{Fuch}}(\Gamma))\subset{\mathcal{X}}(\Gamma,{\mathsf{G}})
is called the
Hitchin component
or

3
Higgs bundles

We now shift our focus to a moduli space of holomorphic objects on a Riemann surface called Higgs bundles. Roughly, a Higgs bundle is a holomorphic bundle with some extra data, and the moduli space parameterizes isomorphism classes of Higgs bundles which are called polystable. This theory was developed by Hitchin
[
19
,
20
]
and Simpson
[
31
,
32
]
. At first glance, Higgs bundles and surface group representations seem to have little to do with each other. However, a remarkable theorem, known as the
nonabelian Hodge correspondence
, gives a homeomorphism between the two moduli spaces. Higgs bundles thus provide a powerful tool for addressing certain questions about the topology of the character variety.

Theorem 3.1

(nonabelian Hodge correspondence)
.

Let
S
be a closed orientable surface of genus at least two. For each Riemann surface structure
X
on
S
, the moduli space of
{\mathsf{G}}
-Higgs bundles on
X
is homeomorphic to the character variety
{\mathcal{X}}(\pi_{1}(S),{\mathsf{G}})
. Moreover, the smooth loci of each space are diffeomorphic.

Remark 3.2
.

One direction of the nonabelian Hodge correspondence asserts that, for each polystable
{\mathsf{G}}
-Higgs bundle, there is a special metric which can be used to construct a flat
{\mathsf{G}}
-connection. For principal bundles a metric is by definition a reduction of structure group to the maximal compact subgroup. The other direction asserts that, for each reductive representation and each choice of Riemann surface structure
X
on
S
, there is an equivariant harmonic map
\widetilde{X}\to{\mathsf{G}}/{\mathsf{H}}
from the universal cover to the Riemannian symmetric space. From such a map one constructs a polystable
{\mathsf{G}}
-Higgs bundle. For more details on the correspondence, we refer the reader to Q. Li’s survey article
[
25
]
.

3.2
Stability and the moduli space

The moduli space of Higgs bundle parameterizes isomorphism classes of Higgs bundles. The isomorphism group for Higgs bundles is called the gauge group. Just as with the character variety, to get a nice moduli we restrict to a special class of Higgs bundles whose gauge orbits are closed.

Given a smooth principal
{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathbb{C}}
-bundle
P\to X
, the
{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathbb{C}}
-gauge group
{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathbb{C}}}
is the group of bundle automorphisms
f\colon P\to P
. The elements of
{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathbb{C}}}
are given by sections of an associated bundle of groups
P[{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathbb{C}}]=P\times_{{\operatorname{Ad}}_{{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathbb{C}}}}{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathbb{C}}
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathbb{C}}}=\Omega^{0}\big(X,P\big[{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathbb{C}}\big]\big).

Recall that a holomorphic structure on a vector bundle
E
is equivalent to a Dolbeault operator. That is, a differential operator

\displaystyle\bar{\partial}_{E}\colon\ \Omega^{0}(E)\to\Omega^{0,1}(E)

so that
2
2

2

Dolbeault operators must also satisfy the integrability condition
\bar{\partial}_{E}^{2}=0

\bar{\partial}_{E}(fs)=\bar{\partial}f\otimes s+f\bar{\partial}_{E}s
for all functions

and sections

. Note that the
(0,1)
-part of a connection on
E
defines a Dolbeault operator. In particular, the space of holomorphic structures on
E
is an infinite-dimensional affine space with underlying vector space
\Omega^{0,1}({\mathrm{End}}(E))

For principal bundles, an analogous theory holds. Namely a holomorphic structure on a principal
{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathbb{C}}
-bundle

is equivalent to a section
\bar{\partial}_{P}\in\Omega^{0,1}\big(P,{\mathfrak{h}}^{\mathbb{C}}\big)

defines a Dolbeault operator on any associated vector bundle. The space of holomorphic structures on

is an infinite-dimensional affine space with the space of basic

If we fix a smooth

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}({\mathsf{G}})=\big\{\big(\bar{\partial}_{P},\varphi\big)\,|\,\bar{\partial}_{P}\varphi=0\big\}.

Fixing a holomorphic structure on

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}({\mathsf{G}})\hookrightarrow\Omega^{0,1}\big(P\big[{\mathfrak{h}}^{\mathbb{C}}\big]\big)\oplus\Omega^{1,0}\big(P\big[{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathbb{C}}\big]\big).

The Hitchin section

Let

6.3
Critical points of a Morse–Bott function

So far we have not used the full power of the nonabelian Hodge correspondence. Since we have a special metric associated to each polystable Higgs bundle, we can take the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-norm of the Higgs field. Namely, consider the nonnegative function
f\colon{\mathcal{M}}({\mathsf{G}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

\displaystyle f\big(\big[\bar{\partial}_{P},\varphi\big]\big)=\int_{X}|\varphi|^{2},

(6.3)

where the norm

is taken with respect to the Hermitian metric associated to

Remark 6.8
.

Note that the function

are given by points in the moduli of polystable
{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathbb{C}}
-bundles

For

and

4
4

4

This is not necessarily true when

.
Thus, the function

Since the moduli space

Remark 6.9

Recall from before that we have a lower bound on

We now have a strategy for counting the components of the character variety

Remark 7.14
