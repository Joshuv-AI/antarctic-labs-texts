The symmetric geometric rank of symmetric tensors
Julia Lindberg, Pierpaola Santarsiero

Abstract
. Inspired by recent work of Kopparty-Moshkovitz-Zuiddam and motivated by problems in combinatorics and hypergraphs, we introduce the notion of the symmetric geometric rank of a symmetric tensor. This quantity is equal to the codimension of the singular locus of the hypersurface associated to the tensor. We first derive fundamental properties of the symmetric geometric rank. Then, we study the space of symmetric tensors of prescribed symmetric geometric rank, which are spaces of homogeneous polynomials whose corresponding hypersurfaces have a singular locus of bounded codimension.

In the last few decades, several notions of tensor rank have arisen and decomposing a tensor in terms of a particular notion of rank has become a useful tool in recovering hidden information in the data of a given tensor

Recently, a new notion of rank for
tensors was introduced in
[
22
]
. Given a tensor
T=(t_{i,j,k})\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}+1}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n_{2}+1}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n_{3}+1}
, for
k=0,\dots,n_{3}
denote
T_{k}=(t_{i,j,k})_{i,j}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}+1}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n_{2}+1}
the
n_{3}+1
matrices obtained by slicing the tensor with respect to the third factor. The authors define the
geometric rank

\mathrm{GR}(T)
of
T
in terms of the codimension of an affine algebraic variety. Namely,

\mathrm{GR}(T):={\mathrm{codim}}\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}+1}\times\mathbb{C}^{n_{2}+1}~|~x^{T}T_{0}y=\cdots=x^{T}T_{n_{3}}y=0\}.

In
[
22
, Theorem 3.2]
the authors proved that the definition of geometric rank is invariant with respect to any of the three the slicings.
This notion of rank can be naturally extended for an arbitrary number of factors (cf.
[
22
, Section 2]
) and was introduced as a new tool for studying tensors and hypergraphs. A geometric study of this notion of rank was considered in
[
19
]
, where the authors made a classification of tensors with geometric rank one and two. In a subsequent work
[
18
]
, the first author of
[
19
]
classified tensors with geometric rank three.

A natural question is whether, as with other notions of tensor rank, there is a symmetric version of the geometric rank of a symmetric tensor.
In this paper we address this problem by introducing the notion of the
symmetric geometric rank
of a symmetric tensor. More precisely, let
T=(t_{i,j,k})\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{3}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
be a symmetric tensor, and as before, denote
T_{k}=(t_{i,j,k})_{i,j}
for
k=0,\dots,n
.
For a symmetric tensor
T\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{3}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
,
we define the
symmetric geometric rank
of
T
as

(1.1)

{\mathrm{SGR}}(T)={\mathrm{codim}}\{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}~|~x^{T}T_{0}x=\cdots=x^{T}T_{n}x=0\}.

A tensor
T\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{3}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
can naturally be seen as a degree three homogeneous polynomial
F\in\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
, for example by considering the
(i,j,k)
th entry of the tensor as the coefficient of
x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}
, and throughout the rest of this paper we interchangeably consider a tensor both as an element
T\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{3}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
or as a homogeneous polynomial
F\in\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]_{3}
.

By considering an order three tensor as a homogeneous cubic polynomial, it is easy to see that for all
k=0,\dots,n
the equation
x^{T}T_{k}x
=0 in (
1.1
) corresponds to considering

\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{k}}=0

and hence when
F
is the cubic polynomial corresponding to a tensor
T
, (
1.1
) is equivalent to

{\mathrm{codim}}\left\{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}~\middle|~\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{0}}(x)=\dots=\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{n}}(x)=0\right\}.

By passing to the projective setting, we recognize that the above codimension is actually the codimension in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
of the singular locus,
\mathrm{Sing}(F)
, of the (possibly non-reduced) hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
defined by
\{[x]\in\mathbb{P}^{n}~|~F(x)=0\}
.
Thus we define the symmetric geometric rank of an order
d
tensor as follows.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
[T]\in\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})
and let
F\in\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]_{d}
be the homogeneous degree
d
polynomial associated to
F
. The
symmetric geometric rank
of
T
is

\displaystyle{\mathrm{SGR}}(T):

\displaystyle={\mathrm{codim}}(\mathrm{Sing}(F))

\displaystyle={\mathrm{codim}}\left\{x\in\mathbb{P}^{n}~\middle|~\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{0}}(x)=\dots=\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{n}}(x)=0\right\},

where we emphasize that we are considering the codimension of
\mathrm{Sing}(F)
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
, not
V(F)
. We also emphasize that in contrast to
[
22
]
, we chose to work with projective spaces instead of affine spaces, but the notion of symmetric geometric rank is the same whether one works affinely or projectively.

It is clear that the notion of symmetric geometric rank is invariant with respect to any choice of the flattening.

We find interesting that the singular locus of a hypersurface, which is a classical object in algebraic geometry, appears in the context of tensors. This was also highlighted in
[
25
]
, where the authors gave a bound on the symmetric rank in terms of dimensions of singular loci
[
25
, Theorem 1.3]
. In addition, a detailed study on the rank of cubic hypersurfaces according to the classification of their singular loci was done in
[
27
]
.

Symmetric geometric rank as a useful tool for hypergraphs

We remark that the notion of the symmetric geometric rank of a symmetric tensor is relevant for the same applications that inspired the definition of geometric rank in
[
22
]
.
This is because many of the motivating applications in combinatorics consider a highly structured tensor, which is symmetric.
More precisely, one of these applications
comes from hypergraph theory (
[
22
, Section 1.3]
). We recall that an (undirected uniform)
hypergraph
is a couple
(V,E)
, where
V=\{1,\dots,n\}
is a finite set collecting the
vertices
of the hypergraph, while
E\subset 2^{V}
contains the
edges
and we require that all edges
e\in E
have the same cardinality
d
.
One interesting problem is to compute the
independence number
of the hypergraph, which is the cardinality of the largest set of vertices containing no edges of
(V,E)
. One way to bound such a quantity is to compute the subrank
\mathrm{Q}(T)
of the associated tensor
T
, which is the maximum identity tensor “contained” in
T
.
Indeed, we remark that given a hypergraph
(V,E)
as above, we can associate a tensor in
(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}
to the hypergraph as follows. We define
T=(t_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}})
as

(1.2)

t_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}:=\begin{cases}1,&\hbox{ if }\{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}\}\in E\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Computing the subrank of the associated tensor is computationally intractable in many cases. Therefore, since the geometric rank is an upper bound for the subrank, it can be seen as a new tool to bound this quantity.

However, an obvious observation is that the tensor defined in (
1.2
) is symmetric and hence it is desirable to exploit its structure. Taking advantage of the symmetric structure of the tensor associated to a hypergraph is also one of the motivations for the introduction of the symmetric subrank in
[
12
, Section 1, Section 2.2]
. Unfortunately, as with the notion of subrank, the symmetric subrank,
\mathrm{Q}_{s}(T)
, of a tensor
T
is hard to compute. Our new notion of rank is then useful to bound this quantity, since a consequence of the forthcoming
3.7
is that, for a symmetric tensor
T
,

\mathrm{Q}_{s}(T)\leq{\mathrm{SGR}}(T)\leq{\mathrm{GR}}(T).

Even though the purpose of this manuscript is to explore geometric aspects of this new notion of rank, the above discussion motivates further investigation of the symmetric geometric rank as a tool to study the aforementioned types of problems.

Parametrizing tensors with respect to symmetric geometric rank

With the introduction of any notion of rank, it is natural to consider spaces of tensors with that prescribed rank. We study these spaces for the symmetric geometric rank and stratify the symmetric tensor space by these spaces.

Definition 1.2
.

Fix a positive integer
d
and let
r\geq 0
. The
space of symmetric
d
-factors tensors with symmetric geometric rank at most
r
is

\mathcal{S}_{d,r}:=\{[T]\in\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})~|~{\mathrm{SGR}}(T)\leq r\}.

By considering a tensor
T\in\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})
as a homogeneous polynomial
F\in\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}
, we can define
\mathcal{S}_{d,r}
from the following incidence variety

\displaystyle\widetilde{Sing}=\{(x,[F])\,|\,V(F)\mbox{ is singular on $x$ }\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{P}^{{{n+d}\choose d}-1}.

Denote
\pi_{2}\colon\widetilde{Sing}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{{{n+d}\choose d}-1}
as the projection onto the second factor and
\pi_{1}
as the projection onto
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. With the above geometric construction we can rephrase the space of tensors having prescribed symmetric geometric rank as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{d,r}

\displaystyle=\{[F]\in\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}\,|\,\dim\mathrm{Sing}([F])\geq n-r\}

\displaystyle=\{[F]\in\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}\,|\,\dim\pi_{1}(\pi_{2}^{-1}([F]))\geq n-r\}.

This construction via the incidence variety
\widetilde{Sing}
and the upper semicontinuity of the dimension of a fiber, shows that each
\mathcal{S}_{d,r}
is a Zariski closed set in
\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}
.
This then implies that the following chain of inclusions holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})=\mathcal{S}_{d,n+1}\supset\mathcal{S}_{d,n}\supset\cdots\supset\mathcal{S}_{d,1}\neq\emptyset,

where we remark (and later prove) that
\mathcal{S}_{d,1}
contains the
d
th Veronese variety
\nu_{d}(\mathbb{P}^{n})
so it is not empty. Moreover, we recognize that
\mathcal{S}_{d,n}
is a classical object in algebraic geometry whose corresponding defining equation is known as the
discriminant

[
17
]
.
Notice also that the smallest element in the above chain should actually be
\mathcal{S}_{d,0}
which consists of classes of homogeneous polynomial whose corresponding hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
has an
n
-dimensional singular locus. The only such polynomial is the zero polynomial, so
\mathcal{S}_{d,0}=\emptyset
and hence it will not play a role in our discussion.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout the rest of this paper we work over
\mathbb{C}
.

Notation 2.1
.

We denote
e_{0},\dots,e_{n}
as the canonical basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
and when representing a tensor
T\in(\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{\otimes d}
in coordinate as
T=(t_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}})
, we will always consider it with respect to canonical basis on each factors.

Notation 2.2
.

Note that any tensor
T\in(\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{\otimes d}
can be seen as a multilinear polynomial,
F
, in the variable groups
\{x_{1}=\{x_{1,1},\ldots,x_{1,n+1}\},

\ldots,x_{d}=\{x_{d,1},\ldots,x_{d,n+1}\}\}
. Further, symmetric tensors can be seen as homogeneous polynomials. In the rest of the paper we will treat elements in
{\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n+1})
both as symmetric tensors and as homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
, exploiting the isomorphism
{\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\cong({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}
that sends
t_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}
to
t_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{d}}
.

Let us start by recalling basic facts about symmetric tensors and their geometry.

Definition 2.3
.

A symmetric tensor
T\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
is called
elementary
if it can be written as the tensor product of the same vector
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
, i.e.

T=\underbrace{v\otimes\cdots\otimes v}_{d\text{ times}}.

Elementary symmetric tensors are the building blocks of a symmetric tensor decomposition and any symmetric tensor can be decomposed as a sum of symmetric elementary tensors.

Definition 2.4
.

The minimum number of symmetric elementary tensors needed to decompose a tensor
T\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
is the
symmetric rank

r_{s}(T)
of
T
, also called
Waring rank
.

Symmetric elementary tensors are symmetric rank one tensors and projective classes of symmetric rank one tensors are parameterized via the following map.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
N={{n+d}\choose d}-1
. The
d
th Veronese embedding
is

\displaystyle\nu_{d}\colon\mathbb{P}^{n}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{N}=\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})

\displaystyle[v]

\displaystyle\mapsto[v^{\otimes d}].

The image of the above map
X_{d}:=\nu_{d}(\mathbb{P}^{n})
is the
d
th Veronese variety
.

Recall that a projective variety
\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
is
non-degenerate
if it is not contained in any hyperplane, i.e.
\mathrm{span}\{X\}=\mathbb{P}^{n}
.

Symmetric rank one tensors are parameterized by Veronese varieties but when dealing with higher rank tensors, it is useful to look at the following auxiliary varieties.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
be an irreducible, non-degenerate projective variety and fix a positive integer
r
.
The
r
th secant variety

\sigma_{r}(X)
of
X
is

\sigma_{r}(X)=\overline{\bigcup_{p_{1},\dots,p_{r}\in X}\mbox{span}\{p_{1},\dots,p_{r}\}}.

We recall that secant varieties form a chain of subvarieties in which the previous variety is contained
in the subsequential variety until we reach the ambient space:

X=\sigma_{1}(X)\subset\sigma_{2}(X)\subset\cdots\subset\sigma_{t}(X)=\mathbb{P}^{N},

where the smallest integer
t
such that
\sigma_{t}(X)=\mathbb{P}^{N}
is called the
generic rank
of
X
.

From a parameter count one can easily see that

\dim(\sigma_{r}(X))\leq\min\{r(\dim X+1)-1,N\},

where sometimes the above inequality is strict.

Definition 2.7
.

An irreducible non-degenerate variety
X
is
r
-
defective
if

\dim(\sigma_{r}(X))<\min\{r(\dim X+1)-1,N\}.

Let us introduce another variety that will be useful for understanding the symmetric geometric rank.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
be a projective variety.
The
tangential variety

\tau(X)
of
X
is the Zariski closure of the union of all tangent spaces at smooth points of
X
.

Remark 2.9
.

Let
X_{d}\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
be the
d
th Veronese variety. It is classically known that any element in
\tau(X_{d})
can be seen as
[L^{d-1}M]
for some linear forms
L,M

[
10

.

We recall that the general linear group
\mathrm{GL}_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})
acts on an elementary symmetric tensor
v^{\otimes d}\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
as

g\cdot v^{\otimes d}=(g\cdot v)^{\otimes d},

for
g\in\mathrm{GL}_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})
. By linearity, this action can be extended to any element of
{\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
. Moreover, the same type of operation can be extended to non invertible matrices. Let
M_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})
denote the set of
(n+1)\times(n+1)
matrices with coefficients in
\mathbb{C}
. For
A\in M_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})
and
T\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}

we have

A\cdot T:=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}t_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}Ae_{i_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes Ae_{i_{d}}.

This operation induces a partial ordering of symmetric tensors.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
S,T\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
, we write

S\leq T
if there exists a matrix
A\in M_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})
such that

S=A\cdot T.

Notation 2.11
.

For every
r\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
with
r\leq n
, denote
I_{r}\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
as the tensor

I_{r}=\sum_{i=0}^{r}(e_{i})^{\otimes d}.

Since it will be useful later, let us recall other relevant notions of symmetric rank.

Definition 2.12
.

Let
T\in(\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{\otimes d}
and denote
T(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
as the description of
T
as a multilinear polynomial

Let
S\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
.

\circ

The
Geometric rank
of

[

]

{\mathrm{GR}}(T)={\mathrm{codim}}\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d-1})\in(\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{d-1}~|~\forall x_{d}\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1},\ T(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=0\}.

\circ

The
Symmetric subrank
of
S

[

]
is

\mathrm{Q}_{s}(S)=\max\{r~|~A\cdot S=I_{r}\hbox{ for some }A\in M_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})\}.\quad

We conclude this section with the notion of a normal variety.

Definition 2.13
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
be a non-degenerate projective variety, so in particular
X
is covered by affine varieties
X_{i}=U_{i}\cap X
for affine charts
U_{i}
,

\mathcal{O}(X)

3.
Properties of symmetric geometric rank

We now wish to prove analogous properties for symmetric geometric rank as those established for geometric rank in
[
22
, Section 4]
. We first prove that the symmetric geometric rank is monotone.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
S,T\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
. If
S\leq T
, then
{\mathrm{SGR}}(S)\leq{\mathrm{SGR}}(T)
.

Proof.

Let
F(x)
be the polynomial associated to
T
. Then
F(A^{T}x)
is the polynomial associated to the tensor
S=A\cdot T
.
First observe that if
A
is invertible, then
{\mathrm{SGR}}(S)={\mathrm{SGR}}(T)
since the singular locus of
F(x)
and
F(A^{T}x)
are birationally equivalent. If
A
is of rank
r<n+1
, then without loss of generality, we can take
A
to be the projection onto the first
n+1-r
variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1-r}
. This implies that

\dim(\text{Sing}(F(A^{T}x))\geq\dim(\text{Sing}(F(x)),

giving the result.
∎

In the following lemma we prove that the symmetric geometric rank is additive under direct sum.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
T_{1},T_{2}\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
. Then

{\mathrm{SGR}}(T_{1}\oplus T_{2})={\mathrm{SGR}}(T_{1})+{\mathrm{SGR}}(T_{2}).

Proof.

The tensor
T=T_{1}\oplus T_{2}
is a block diagonal tensor with blocks
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
. Therefore, if we denote
F_{1}(x),F_{2}(y)
as the polynomials associated to
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
respectively, then the polynomial associated to
T
is
F(x,y)=F_{1}(x)+F_{2}(y)
where the variables
x
and
y
are disjoint. Suppose
\text{Sing}(F_{1})
is cut out by polynomials
\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
and
\text{Sing}(F_{2})
is cut out by polynomials
\{g_{0},\ldots,g_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{C}[y_{0},\ldots,y_{n}]
, then
\text{Sing}(F)
is cut out by polynomials
\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{n},g_{0},\ldots,g_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n},y_{0},\ldots,y_{n}\}
. Therefore,

\text{codim}(\text{Sing}(F))=\text{codim}(\text{Sing}(F_{1}))+\text{codim}(\text{Sing}(F_{2})).\qed

Element wise subadditivity is another property of the symmetric geometric rank.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
S,T\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
. Then

{\mathrm{SGR}}(S+T)\leq{\mathrm{SGR}}(S)+{\mathrm{SGR}}(T)
.

Proof.

Since
S+T\leq S\oplus T
, by
3.1
we have
{\mathrm{SGR}}(S+T)\leq{\mathrm{SGR}}(S\oplus T)
. By
3.2
,
{\mathrm{SGR}}(S\oplus T)={\mathrm{SGR}}(S)+{\mathrm{SGR}}(T)
, proving the result.
∎

We now move on to comparing the symmetric geometric rank to other notions of symmetric tensor rank. We begin by comparing the symmetric geometric rank with the geometric rank. Recall that any tensor
T\in(\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{\otimes d}
can be seen as a multilinear polynomial in variable groups
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\}
where
x_{i}=\{x_{i,1},\ldots,x_{i,n+1}\}
.

In this notation, the geometric rank of
T
is the codimension of the variety obtained by taking the partial derivatives of
F
with respect to one of the variable groups, namely

{\mathrm{GR}}(T)=\text{codim}\biggl\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1})\in\mathbb{C}^{(d-1)(n+1)}\ \bigg|\ \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{d,1}}=0,\ldots,\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{d,n+1}}=0\biggr\}.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
T\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
. Then
{\mathrm{SGR}}(T)\leq{\mathrm{GR}}(T)
.

Proof.

Let
T\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
be such that
{\mathrm{GR}}(T)=r
and let
F
be the multilinear polynomial in the variable groups
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\}
,
x_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
, associated to
T
and
G
the usual homogeneous polynomial in the variables
y\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
associated to
T
. Define,

\displaystyle V_{1}

\displaystyle=\biggl\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1},y)\in\mathbb{C}^{d(n+1)}\ \bigg|\ \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{d,1}}=0,\ldots,\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{d,n+1}}=0\biggr\}

\displaystyle V_{2}

\displaystyle=\biggl\{y\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\ \bigg|\ \frac{\partial G}{\partial y_{1}}=0,\ldots,\frac{\partial G}{\partial y_{n+1}}=0\biggr\}

Since
{\mathrm{GR}}(T)=r
,
\dim(V_{1})=d(n+1)-r
. Observe that
V_{2}
is in bijection with
V_{1}\cap V_{3}
where

V_{3}=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1},y)\in\mathbb{C}^{d(n+1)}\ |~x_{i,1}-y_{0}=0,\ldots,x_{i,n+1}-y_{n+1}=0,\ \forall 1\leq i\leq d-1\}.

Note that
\dim(V_{3})=n+1
.
Therefore by the Affine Dimension Theorem,

\displaystyle\dim(V_{1}\cap V_{3})

\displaystyle\geq\dim(V_{1})+\dim(V_{3})-d(n+1)

\displaystyle=d(n+1)-r+(n+1)-d(n+1)

\displaystyle=n+1-r

giving
{\mathrm{codim}}(V_{2})={\mathrm{codim}}(V_{1}\cap V_{3})\leq r
.
∎

The following example shows that there exist symmetric tensors for which the inequality in
3.4
is strict.

Example 3.5
.

Let
T=e_{1}\otimes e_{1}\otimes e_{2}+e_{1}\otimes e_{2}\otimes e_{1}+e_{2}\otimes e_{1}\otimes e_{1}\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{3}\mathbb{C}^{2}
. One can directly compute that
{\mathrm{GR}}(T)=2
and

{\mathrm{SGR}}(T)=1
.

We now compare the symmetric geometric rank with the symmetric subrank (
2.12
). To do this, we first need a preliminary lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
with
r\leq n
. Then
{\mathrm{SGR}}(I_{r})=r
where
I_{r}\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
is the identity tensor.

Proof.

By direct computation, the variety
\{x\in\mathbb{P}^{n}~|~dx_{1}^{d-1}=0,\ldots,dx_{r}^{d-1}=0\}
has codimension
r
.
∎

Theorem 3.7
.

For any tensor
T\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
we have
\mathrm{Q}_{s}(T)\leq{\mathrm{SGR}}(T)
.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathrm{Q}_{s}(T)=r
so
I_{r}\leq T
. Then by
3.6
,
r={\mathrm{SGR}}(I_{r})\leq{\mathrm{SGR}}(T)
.∎

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
T\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{2}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
be a symmetric order 2 tensor, i.e. a symmetric matrix. Then
{\mathrm{SGR}}(T)=\text{rank}(T)
.

Proof.

One can easily see that for matrices we have
{\mathrm{SGR}}(T)={\mathrm{GR}}(T)
and
[
22
, Section 1.2]
shows that for matrices
{\mathrm{GR}}(T)=\text{rank}(T)
.
∎

The above corollary shows that the inequality
\mathrm{Q}_{s}(T)\leq{\mathrm{SGR}}(T)
can be strict. For instance, take the
2n\times 2n
matrix defined in
[
12
, Example 2.7]
. Then the symmetric subrank is always
0
, but the symmetric geometric rank will be
2n
.

To summarize the second half of this section, we have that

\mathrm{Q}_{s}(T)\leq{\mathrm{SGR}}(T)\leq{\mathrm{GR}}(T).

4.
Classifying spaces with prescribed symmetric geometric rank

In this section we focus on classifying spaces of tensors with bounded symmetric geometric rank (
1.2
). As a first example, we consider the case of symmetric matrices, i.e. we describe
\mathcal{S}_{2,r}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

For degree two homogeneous polynomials the notion of symmetric geometric rank coincide with the notion of symmetric rank, i.e.

\mathcal{S}_{2,r}=\sigma_{r}(X_{2}),

where
X_{2}=\nu_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{n})
is the
2
nd Veronese variety.

Proof.

Let
F\in\mathrm{Sym}^{2}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
, since
F(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Ax
for some symmetric matrix
A\in M_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})
, then
\nabla F(x)=Ax
.
Therefore

\dim(\mathrm{Sing}(F))=\dim\{[x]\in\mathbb{P}^{n}~|~\nabla F(x)=0\}=\dim\{[x]\in\mathbb{P}^{n}~|~Ax=0\},

i.e.
{\mathrm{codim}}(\mathrm{Sing}(F))=\mathrm{rank}(A)
and hence

\mathcal{S}_{2,r}=\sigma_{r}(X_{2}).

∎

Remark 4.2
.

It is well known that for matrices the symmetric rank and rank coincide. Therefore, by
4.1
we have that for order two tensors, the symmetric rank, rank and symmetric geometric rank are all equal. This provides additional evidence that the notion of symmetric geometric rank is natural to consider.

Now we focus on describing
\mathcal{S}_{d,r}
when
d\geq 3
and
r\geq 1
. The following theorem gives a full geometric description of the spaces
\mathcal{S}_{d,r}
of symmetric tensors with prescribed symmetric geometric rank.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
d\geq 3
,
r\geq 1
,
n\geq 1
and for an
r
-plane
\ell\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
set
H_{\ell}:=\{[F]\in\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}~|~V(F)\mbox{ is tangent to }\ell\}
. Then

\mathcal{S}_{d,r}=\bigcap_{\ell\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}}H_{\ell},

where the intersection runs over all linear spaces
\ell\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
of dimension
r
.

Proof.

First we show that
\mathcal{S}_{d,r}\subseteq\bigcap_{\ell\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}}H_{\ell}
.
Let
[F]\in\mathcal{S}_{d,r}
, so
\dim(\mathrm{Sing}(F))\geq n-r
and hence for any
r
-plane
\ell\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
we can find a point in the intersection
\mathrm{Sing}(F)\cap\ell
which is a tangent point.

For the other direction, let
[F]\in\bigcap_{\ell\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}}H_{\ell}
and assume by contradiction that
[F]\not\in~\mathcal{S}_{d,r}
. Therefore
\dim(\mathrm{Sing}(F))\leq n-(r+1)
and the general
r
-plane
\ell
does not intersect
\mathrm{Sing}(F)
, which means that
V(F)
and
\ell
intersect away from the singular locus of
V(F)
. But then by Bertini Theorem we get that
V(F)
cannot be tangent to
\ell
which is a contradiction.
∎

The above theorem
gives a complete geometric description of
the spaces
\mathcal{S}_{d,r}
. We now wish to bound for the dimension of these varieties. In order to do so, notice that for all
r=0,\dots,n

\Bigg[\sum_{i=0}^{r}x_{i}^{d}\Bigg]\in\mathcal{S}_{d,r+1}.

This idea can be easily generalized by considering cones, i.e. hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
whose defining equation depends on less than
n+1
variables.

Remark 4.4
.

For all
[F]\in\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}
such that
F=F(x_{0},\dots,x_{r})
we have
{\mathrm{codim}}(\mathrm{Sing}(F))\geq n-(r+1)
. Therefore, we get the following lower bound on the dimension of
\mathcal{S}_{d,r+1}
:

\dim(\mathcal{S}_{d,r+1})\geq{d+r\choose r}-1.

4.1.
The space
\mathcal{S}_{d,1}

We now focus on symmetric tensors with symmetric geometric rank one by first understanding the shape of an element in
\mathcal{S}_{d,1}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
d\geq 2
and take
[F]\in\mathcal{S}_{d,1}
. Then
F=G^{i}\cdot H
with
i\geq 2
, for polynomials
G,H
such that
i\cdot\deg(G)+\deg(H)=d
.

Proof.

Since
\text{Sing}(F)
must be a hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
, the polynomial defining the hypersurface
\text{Sing}(F)
must appear with multiplicity in
F
.
∎

In this particular case of
r=1
, we can prove the specific version of
Theorem

4.3
in the following more elementary way.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
H_{\ell}=\{[F]\in\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}~|~V(F)
is tangent to a line
\ell\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}

}
. Then

(4.1)

\bigcap_{\ell\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}}H_{\ell}=\mathcal{S}_{d,1}.

Proof.

First we show
\mathcal{S}_{d,1}\subseteq\bigcap_{\ell\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}}H_{\ell}
. Let
[F]\in\mathcal{S}_{d,1}
. By
4.5
, we can write
F=G^{i}H
for polynomials
G,H\in\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
and
i\geq 2
. This implies that
V(G)\subseteq\text{Sing}(F)
for all lines
\ell\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
. Therefore, there exists a point
p\in\text{Sing}(F)\cap\ell
with multiplicity greater than or equal to
2
, meaning that
V(F)
is tangent to
\ell
at
p
.

For the reverse inclusion, let
[F]\in\bigcap_{\ell\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}}H_{\ell}
. Note that
\ell\subseteq V(F)
for all lines
\ell\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
if and only if
F
is identically zero, so we can assume
\ell
is not contained in
V(F)
. This means
\#(\ell\cap V(F))=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}\}
and, without loss of generality, we can assume
p_{1}
has multiplicity greater than or equal to
2
. This implies that
V(F)
is singular at
p_{1}
. Since
V(F)
must be singular at a point for every line
\ell\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
, this implies that
\text{Sing}(F)\cap\ell\neq\emptyset
for every line
\ell
, therefore the codimension of the singular locus of
V(F)
must be less than or equal to
1
.
∎

We point out that when
d=3
the space (
4.1
) also appears in problems in enumerative geometry where one wishes to compute the number of cubic hypersurfaces
tangent to a given number of lines
[
3
]
.
Moreover, for the particular case of
d=3
one can see that
the space
\mathcal{S}_{3,1}
is actually the tangential variety of the third Veronese variety.

Theorem 4.7
.

The space of 3-ways symmetric tensors having symmetric geometric rank one is

\mathcal{S}_{3,1}=\tau(X_{3}).

Proof.

We first prove that if
[T]\in\tau(X_{3})
then
{\mathrm{SGR}}(T)=1
. Let
F
be the form corresponding to a tensor in
\tau(X_{3})\setminus X_{3}
, i.e.
F=L^{2}M
, where
L=a_{0}x_{0}+\cdots+a_{n}x_{n}
and
M=b_{0}x_{0}+\cdots+b_{n}x_{n}
where vectors
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n})
and
(b_{0},\ldots,b_{n})
are not proportional (cf.
2.9
). Notice that

\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{i}}=\frac{\partial L^{2}M}{\partial x_{i}}=2a_{i}LM+b_{i}L^{2},

hence the ideal generating the singular locus of
V(F)
is given by

(2a_{0}LM+b_{0}L^{2},\dots,2a_{n}LM+b_{n}L^{2})=(L^{2},LM),

where the second equality follows since the coefficients of
L
and
M
are not proportional. The conclusion follows since the radical of the above ideal is generated by
L
.

To show the reverse inclusion, it suffices to show that any homogeneous cubic polynomial,
F\in\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]_{3}
, whose corresponding hypersurface has a singular locus of codimension one, is of the form
F=L^{2}M
where
L=a_{0}x_{0}+\ldots a_{n}x_{n}
and
M=b_{0}x_{0}+\ldots+b_{n}x_{n}
. Since
V(F)\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
is a hypersurface, for its singular locus to also have codimension one in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
,
F
must be reducible. Therefore
F=P\cdot Q
for homogeneous polynomials
P,Q\in\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
. The singular locus of
V(F)
then contains
V(P)\cap V(Q)
. In other words, if the codimension of the singular locus of
V(F)
is one, then the codimension of
V(P)\cap V(Q)
must be one. Therefore,
P
and
Q
must contain a common factor. Since
\deg(F)=3
, the only way for this to happen is if
Q=L
and
P=L\cdot M
for linear forms
L,M
.
∎

Combining both
and
4.7
allows us to give another interpretation of the tangential variety of the third Veronese variety. This is a straightforward consequence of the aforementioned theorems,
but for the sake of completeness we include it here.

Corollary 4.8
.

The following equality holds for the tangential variety
\tau(X_{3})
of the third Veronese variety
X_{3}

\tau(X_{3})=\bigcap_{\ell\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}}H_{\ell}=\mathcal{S}_{3,1}

where
H_{\ell}=\{[F]\in\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}~|~V(F)\hbox{ is tangent to a line }\ell\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}\}
and
\mathcal{S}_{3,1}
is the space of cubic polynomials with singular locus of codimension one in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
.

Continuing to work with
d=3
, we can understand the relation between higher secant varieties of the tangential variety and the spaces of tensors with prescribed symmetric geometric rank.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
\sigma_{s}(\tau(X_{3}))
be the
r
th secant variety of the tangential variety
\tau(X_{3})
of the third Veronese variety
X_{3}\subset\mathbb{P}^{{{n+3}\choose 3}-1}
. A general tensor
[F]\in\sigma_{r}(\tau(X_{3}))
has symmetric geometric rank
r
.

Proof.

A general element of
\sigma_{r}(\tau(X_{3}))
is of the form
F=\sum_{i=1}^{r}L_{i}^{2}M_{i}
, for linear forms
L_{i},M_{i}
. Taking the partial derivatives we have
{\mathrm{SGR}}(T)\leq r
. Indeed, let
I
be the ideal generating the singular locus of
F
i.e.
I=(\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{i}})_{i=0}^{n}
. Then
I=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{r})
.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

The
r
th secant variety of the tangential variety of the third Veronese
X_{3}
is defective if and only if
r=n=2,3
and
4
(cf.
[
1
, Thoerem 1.1]
, also
[
10
]
).
Hence, in the non defective cases

\dim\sigma_{r}(\tau(X_{3}))=\min\biggl\{r(2n+1)-1,{{n+3}\choose 3}-1\biggr\}.

Moreover,

\sigma_{r}(\tau(X_{3}))\subset\mathcal{S}_{3,r}

and the containment is strict since
\mathcal{S}_{3,r}
fills the ambient space for
r=n+1
, while we should set
r\simeq n^{2}
to get that
\sigma_{r}(\tau(X_{3}))
fills the ambient space.

Since we are connecting spaces of symmetric tensors with prescribed symmetric geometric rank with secant varieties, it is worth to also see the interplay between
\mathcal{S}_{3,r}
and secant varieties of the third Veronese varieties.

Remark 4.11
.

Since
X_{3}\subset\tau(X_{3})
then
\sigma_{r}(X_{3})\subset\sigma_{r}(\tau(X_{d}))
and by
4.9
we have
\sigma_{r}(X_{3})\subset\mathcal{S}_{3,r}
.

We now completely describe the space
\mathcal{S}_{d,1}
for all
d\geq 3
.

Theorem 4.12
.

Fix
d\geq 3
. The space of
d
-factor symmetric tensors with symmetric geometric rank at most 1 can be characterized as follows.

1.

If
d=3
then
\mathcal{S}_{d,1}=\tau(X_{3})
and
\dim(\mathcal{S}_{d,1})=2n
.

2.

Otherwise,
\mathcal{S}_{d,1}
is reducible and has a component with maximal dimension

{{\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor+n}\choose\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor}+{{d-\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor+n}\choose{d-\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor}}-2.

Moreover, each component is given by the image of the map

\displaystyle\phi_{d_{1}}:\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d_{1}}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}

\displaystyle\times\,\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d_{2}}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}

\displaystyle(\,[F]

\displaystyle\,,\,[G]\,)\quad\mapsto\qquad[F^{\,2}\cdot G],

where
d_{2}=d-2d_{1}
. Finally, all components intersect on the
d
th Veronese
X_{d}
.

Proof.

Let
d=3
. We have seen in
4.7
that
\mathcal{S}_{3,1}=\tau(X_{3})
, where
X_{3}
is the third Veronese variety. It is classically known that
\dim(\tau(X_{3}))=2n
and that the tangential variety is irreducible (cf. e.g.
[
10
, Proposition 1.1]
). This covers the case
d=3
.

Let now
d\geq 4
. By
4.5
, given an element
[F]\in\mathcal{S}_{d,1}
, we have that
F=G^{i}\cdot H
with
i\geq 2
, for polynomials
G,H
such that
i\cdot\deg(G)+\deg(H)=d=i\cdot d_{1}+d_{2}
. Therefore we can consider the following map

(4.2)

\displaystyle\phi_{d_{1},i}:\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d_{1}}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}

\displaystyle\times\,\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d_{2}}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}

\displaystyle(\,[F]

\displaystyle\,,\,[G]\,)\quad\mapsto\qquad[F^{\,i}\cdot G]

and look at the image of this map in order to get different components of
\mathcal{S}_{d,1}
. Notice that if
i>2
then a form of type
F^{\,i}\cdot G
can be factored as
F^{\,2}\cdot(F^{\,i-2}\cdot G)
. Therefore the component given by
\mathrm{Im}(\phi_{d_{1},i})
would be contained in the one given by
\mathrm{Im}(\phi_{d_{1},2}):=\mathrm{Im}(\phi_{d_{1}})
.
Since
\phi_{d_{1}}
parameterizes each component of the variety
\mathcal{S}_{d,1}
, to find the dimension of a component, it is enough to study the dimension of the image of this map.
Note that the map
\phi_{d_{1}}
is the composition of two maps:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d_{1}}\mathbb{C}^{n+1*})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{2d_{1}}\mathbb{C}^{n+1*})\rightarrow\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1*})

\displaystyle[F]\quad

\displaystyle\mapsto\qquad[F^{\,2}]\quad\mapsto\quad[F^{\,2}\cdot G],

where
[G]\in\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{d_{2}}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}
. The map sending
F\mapsto F^{\,2}
is 2 to 1 while the map sending
F\to F^{2}\cdot G
is injective.
Therefore, by the fiber dimension theorem we have

\dim(\mathrm{Im}(\phi_{d_{1}}))={{d_{1}+n}\choose d_{1}}+{{d_{2}+n}\choose d_{2}}-2.

In particular the maximum dimensional component is achieved for
d_{1}=\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor
.

Finally, it suffices to show that for
d\geq 4
,
\mathcal{S}_{d,1}
is reducible and all components intersect only on the
d
th Veronese variety. First, it is clear that the
d
th Veronese is the intersection of all components. To show
\mathcal{S}_{d,1}
is not irreducible it suffices to show that all components are not contained in one component. Specifically, it suffices to show that
\mathrm{Im}(\phi_{d_{1}})
is not contained in the maximal dimension component for all
d_{1}
such that
2d_{1}+d-2d_{1}=d
. Let
d_{1}<\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor
and take irreducible forms
F,G
of degrees
d_{1}
and
d-2d_{1}
respectively. We have that the class of
F^{\,2}\cdot G
is an element of
\mathrm{Im}(\phi_{d_{1}})
but it cannot be an element of
\mathrm{Im}\left(\phi_{\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor}\right)
.
∎

4.2.
The space
\mathcal{S}_{d,2}

We now consider symmetric tensors having symmetric geometric rank at most two.
Here we distinguish between irreducible hypersurfaces and reducible ones. Looking first at the component given by reducible hypersurfaces gives the following bound on the dimension of
\mathcal{S}_{d,2}
.

Lemma 4.13
.

One (possibly reducible)
component of

\mathcal{S}_{d,2}
is given by reducible polynomials
F=G\cdot H
where
\deg(G)+\deg(H)=d
. Furthermore,
\dim(\mathcal{S}_{d,2})\geq\binom{n+d-1}{d-1}+n
.

Proof.

If
F=G\cdot H
, then
V(G)\cap V(H)\subseteq\text{Sing}(F)
. If
G
and
H
have no common factors, then
V(G)\cap V(H)
has codimension two in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
, showing
[F]\in\mathcal{S}_{d,2}
.

The construction above shows that we can bound the dimension of
\mathcal{S}_{d,2}
by the space of polynomials
F=G\cdot H
where
\deg(G)=d_{1}
,
\deg(H)=d-d_{1}
. This will achieve the maximum dimension when
d_{1}=1
. In this case, the component of
\mathcal{S}_{d,2}
corresponding to polynomials with a linear factor has dimension
\binom{n+d-1}{d-1}+n
.
∎

We now consider irreducible polynomials
[F]\in\mathcal{S}_{d,2}
. By
2.14
, these are non-normal hypersurfaces.

Remark 4.14
.

Recall that for an irreducible, non-normal variety
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
, the singular locus of
X
is of codimension at most one in
X
(cf.
2.14
) and hence in the case of non-normal irreducible hypersurfaces

, we get
[F]\in\mathcal{S}_{d,2}
. Therefore, a component of
\mathrm{S}_{d,2}
contains irreducible non-normal hypersurfaces.

We focus now on the case
d=3
, and give a complete description of
\mathcal{S}_{3,2}
.
By
4.14
a component of
\mathcal{S}_{3,2}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{ir}}=\{[F]\in\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{3}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}\,|\,V(F)\mbox{ is irreducible and non-normal}\}.

Moreover, by
[
26
, Lemma 2.4]
if
X=V(F)\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
is a non-normal irreducible cubic hypersurface then the singular locus of
X
is linear. In
[
26
]
the authors classify non-normal, irreducible, cubic hypersurfaces and they show that if
n\geq 5
, the hypersurface given by an element in
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{ir}}
is a cone

.

n

Normal form of cubic polynomials with
{\mathrm{SGR}}

L_{1}^{2}\cdot M_{1}+L_{2}^{2}\cdot M_{2}+L_{1}\cdot L_{2}\cdot M_{3},

for linear forms

. Since the space of non-normal irreducible cubic polynomials is a component of

we would like to be more precise about this.

Proposition 4.15
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
F\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{ir}}
. Then,

F=L_{1}^{2}\cdot M_{1}+L_{2}^{2}\cdot M_{2}+L_{1}\cdot L_{2}\cdot M_{3}

for linear forms
L_{1},L_{2},M_{1},M_{2},M_{3}
such that
L_{1},L_{2}
are not proportional,
M_{1},M_{2}\neq 0
, and
M_{1},M_{2},M_{3}
are not proportional. Moreover,
\dim(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{ir}})=5n-2
.

Proof.

Let us first show that
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{ir}}
parametrizes all irreducible, cubic polynomials whose singular locus is of codimension two.
By
[
26
, Lemma 2.4]
, if
[F]\in\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{3}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}
is an irreducible, cubic polynomial such that
V(F)
has a singular locus of codimension two, then
\mathrm{Sing}(F)
is linear, which means that we can write
\text{Sing}(F)=V(L_{1},L_{2})
for independent linear forms
L_{1},L_{2}
. Clearly, if
F=L_{1}^{2}\cdot M_{1}+L_{2}^{2}\cdot M_{2}+L_{1}\cdot L_{2}\cdot M_{3}
, then the ideal of the singular locus
I_{\mathrm{Sing}(F)}\subseteq(L_{1},L_{2})
. Since we know that
\text{Sing}(F)
has codimension
2
and it is linear, this means that
\text{Sing}(F)=V(L_{1},L_{2})
.

We now have to show that any cubic hypersurface
V(F)
such that
\text{Sing}(F)=V(L_{1},L_{2})
for linear polynomials
L_{1},L_{2}
is of the form

F=L_{1}^{2}\cdot M_{1}+L_{2}^{2}\cdot M_{2}+L_{1}\cdot L_{2}\cdot M_{3}.

First of all, note that if
L_{1},L_{2}
are linearly dependent then
[F]\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
. Without loss of generality, we can consider a change of basis so that
L_{1}=x_{1}
and
L_{2}=x_{2}

By Euler’s identity, we can write
F=x_{1}\cdot G_{1}+x_{2}\cdot G_{2}
for some
G_{1},G_{2}
with
\deg(G_{1})=\deg(G_{2})=2
. This gives,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{1}}

\displaystyle=G_{1}+x_{1}\cdot\frac{\partial G_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}+x_{2}\cdot\frac{\partial G_{2}}{\partial x_{1}},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{2}}

\displaystyle=x_{1}\cdot\frac{\partial G_{1}}{\partial x_{2}}+G_{2}+x_{2}\cdot\frac{\partial G_{2}}{\partial x_{2}}.

Since both
\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{1}},\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{2}}
must vanish on
V(L_{1},L_{2})=V(x_{1},x_{2})
, we have that

\displaystyle G_{1}

\displaystyle=x_{1}\cdot P_{1}+x_{2}\cdot P_{2},

\displaystyle G_{2}

\displaystyle=x_{1}\cdot Q_{1}+x_{2}\cdot Q_{2},

for some linear forms
P_{1},P_{2},Q_{1},Q_{2}
. Substituting these expressions into
F
, we have that

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=x_{1}\cdot G_{1}+x_{2}\cdot G_{2}

\displaystyle=x_{1}(x_{1}\cdot P_{1}+x_{2}\cdot P_{2})+x_{2}(x_{1}\cdot Q_{1}+x_{2}\cdot Q_{2})

\displaystyle=x_{1}^{2}\cdot P_{1}+x_{2}^{2}\cdot Q_{2}+x_{1}x_{2}(P_{2}+Q_{1}).

This shows that
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{ir}}
parametrizes all irreducible cubic hypersurfaces whose singular locus is of codimension two.

We conclude this proof by computing the dimension of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{ir}}
.
Consider the following incidence variety

I:=\{([F],\mathrm{Sing}(F))~|~[F]\in\mathcal{C}_{ir}\}\subseteq\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{3}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}\times\mathrm{Gr}(n-1,n+1).

Once we fix hyperplanes
\ell_{1}=V(L_{1}),\ell_{2}=V(L_{2})
, then for any
M_{1},M_{2},M_{3}
we have that
\mathrm{Sing}(L_{1}^{2}\cdot M_{1}+L_{2}^{2}\cdot M_{2}+L_{1}\cdot L_{2}\cdot M_{3})=V(L_{1},L_{2})
. So a general fiber above the second projection from
I
has dimension
3n
.
Moreover, the image of
I
via the first projection is
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{ir}}
and so a general fiber above the first projection is
a singleton. Therefore, since the dimension of the Grassmannian is
\dim(\mathrm{Gr}(n-1,n+1))=(n-1)(n+1-n+1)=2n-2
then
\dim(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{ir}})=2n-2+3n=5n-2
.
∎

Now that we completely described irreducible, cubic hypersurfaces
V(F)
for which
{\mathrm{SGR}}(F)=2
, Let us restate
4.13
for the case
d=3
.

Lemma 4.16
.

Let
F
be a reducible cubic not of the form
F=L^{2}\cdot M
for linear forms
L,M
. Then
{\mathrm{SGR}}(F)=2
.

We are now ready to fully describe the space
\mathcal{S}_{3,2}
.

Theorem 4.17
.

The class of any element in
\mathcal{S}_{3,2}
is either of the form

F=L_{1}^{2}\cdot M_{1}+L_{2}^{2}\cdot M_{2}+L_{1}\cdot L_{2}\cdot M_{3}

for linear forms
L_{1},L_{2},M_{1},M_{2},M_{3}
satisfying the conditions in
4.15
or it is reducible. Specifically, the space
\mathcal{S}_{3,2}
can be decomposed as

\mathcal{S}_{3,2}=\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{ir}}\cup\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{re}},

where

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{ir}}
consists of polynomials of the above form and

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{re}}
consists of reducible cubics. Moreover,

\dim(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{re}})=\binom{n+2}{2}+n\hbox{ and }\dim(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{ir}})=5n-2,

and when
n\geq 6
the component
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{re}}
is of larger dimension.

Proof.

First note that
\mathcal{S}_{3,2}
is reducible, so
\mathcal{S}_{3,2}=\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{ir}}\cup\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{re}}
and the intersection of the two components is precisely
\mathcal{S}_{3,1}
. For
F\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{re}}\backslash\mathcal{S}_{3,1}
,
F=G\cdot H
, where
\deg(G)=2
,
\deg(H)=1
and
H\nmid G
. By
4.16
,
\text{Sing}(F)=V(G,H)
is codimension two. To compute the dimension of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{re}}
, observe that reducible cubics can be parameterized via

\displaystyle\psi:\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{2}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}

\displaystyle\times\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{1}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}({\mathrm{Sym}}^{3}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{*}

\displaystyle(\,[G]\,,

\displaystyle\,[H]\,)\quad\mapsto\quad[G\cdot H]

and the image of
\psi
gives us the dimension of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{re}}
. Hence
\dim(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{re}})=\binom{n+2}{2}+n
. For the dimension and the description of the irreducible component we refer to
4.15
.
∎

The above result completely characterize
\mathcal{S}_{3,2}
and it ends our detailed discussion on the case
(d,r)=(3,2)
. While we leave a complete description of
\mathcal{S}_{d,r}
as future work, one can still give information on the space
\mathcal{S}_{d,r}
for large
d
.

Remark 4.18
.

Observe that in
the space
\mathcal{S}_{3,2}
decomposes into reducible and irreducible polynomials. For the irreducible polynomials, these are precisely polynomials that are singular along a codimension two linear subspace. By
[
29
]
, for sufficiently large
d
, the largest irreducible component of the space
\mathcal{S}_{d,r}
will also consist of polynomials singular along a codimension
r
linear space. Therefore, making a construction analogous to the one used for computing the dimension of
\mathcal{C}_{\text{ir}}
in the proof of
4.15
can be used to approximate the largest dimensional component of
\mathcal{S}_{d,r}
so long as
d
is sufficiently large.

.

5.1.
Computing the symmetric geometric rank of relevant tensors

Let us start with a very standard example of symmetric tensor, i.e. the fully symmetric tensor.

Example 5.1

(Fully symmetric tensor)
.

Let
T\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{3}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
be the fully symmetric tensor with
n\geq 2
, i.e.

T=\sum_{i,j,k}e_{i}\otimes e_{j}\otimes e_{k}.

The polynomial representation
h_{3}
of
T
is given by the sum of all degree three monomials in the variables
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}
, namely

h_{3}=\sum_{0\leq i\leq j\leq k\leq n}x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}=x_{0}^{3}+x_{0}^{2}x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n-1}x_{n}^{2}+x_{n}^{3}.

Let us prove that
V(h_{3})
is smooth and hence
{\mathrm{SGR}}(T)
is maximal. We want to argue by induction on
n\geq 2
.
Before proving our result, let us notice that the complete homogeneous polynomial of degree two
h_{2}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{0\leq i\leq j\leq n}x_{i}x_{j}
is smooth.
Solving
\partial h_{2}(x)/\partial x_{i}=0
for all
i=0,\dots,n
corresponds to solving the linear system
Ax=0
where

A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&1&\dots&1\\
1&2&1&\dots&1\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
1&&&2&1\\
1&1&\dots&1&2\end{pmatrix}

Since
A
is invertible, the only possible solution is the trivial one and hence
V(h_{2})
is smooth.

Let us start now the induction. For the base case
n=2
one can directly compute that
V(h_{3})
is smooth. So let
n\geq 3
. Notice that for all
i=0,\dots,n
the polynomial
h_{3}
can be written as

h_{3}=x_{i}h_{2}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})+P_{i}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})

where
h_{2}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
is the complete homogeneous polynomial of degree two and
P_{i}
does not depend on
x_{i}
. Therefore, for all
i=0,\dots,n

(5.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h_{3}}{\partial x_{i}}=h_{2}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})+x_{i}\frac{\partial h_{2}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})}{\partial x_{i}}.

Moreover, by Euler’s formula

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h_{3}}{\partial x_{i}}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}x_{k}\frac{\partial h_{2}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})}{\partial x_{k}}+x_{i}\frac{\partial h_{2}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})}{\partial x_{i}}.

Having
\partial h_{3}/\partial x_{i}=0
for all
i
corresponds to solving the system

A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&1&\dots&1\\
1&2&1&\dots&1\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
1&&&2&1\\
1&1&\dots&1&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{0}\frac{\partial h_{2}}{\partial x_{0}}\\
\vdots\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}\frac{\partial h_{2}}{\partial x_{n}}\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{0}.

Let
x
be a solution of this system. Since
A
is invertible, we have
x_{i}\frac{\partial h_{2}}{\partial x_{i}}=0
for all
i
and hence we have that
h_{2}(x)=0
.
Therefore,
x
is also a solution for the system given by
\{h_{2}=0,\partial h_{2}/\partial x_{i}=0\}
.
Since we earlier proved that
h_{2}
is smooth, the constraints

\{h_{2}=0,\ x_{i}\partial h_{2}/\partial x_{i}=0\}

enforces that at least one of the
x_{i}
must be zero. Without loss of generality we may assume
x_{n}=0
. But now the tuple
(x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1})
is a common zero for
h_{3}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1})
and all its partial derivatives, so we can conclude by induction that
x_{i}=0
for all
i
and hence
h_{3}
is smooth.

The
Coppersmith-Winograd tensor
is a class of tensors introduced in
[
15
]
. These tensors have been helpful for giving bounds on the exponent characterizing the complexity of matrix multiplication (cf.
[
23
]
).

Example 5.2

(Big Coppersmith-Winograd tensor)
.

Fix a positive integer
q<n
and let
T\in(\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{\otimes 3}
be the tensor

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{q}(e_{0}\otimes e_{i}\otimes e_{i}+e_{i}\otimes e_{0}\otimes e_{i}+e_{i}\otimes e_{i}\otimes e_{0})

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 9.24994pt+e_{0}\otimes e_{0}\otimes e_{q+1}+e_{0}\otimes e_{q+1}\otimes e_{0}+e_{q+1}\otimes e_{0}\otimes e_{0}

\displaystyle=W_{1}+\cdots+W_{q}+\tilde{W},

where we denote
W_{i}=e_{0}\otimes e_{i}\otimes e_{i}+e_{i}\otimes e_{0}\otimes e_{i}+e_{i}\otimes e_{i}\otimes e_{0}
and
\tilde{W}=e_{0}\otimes e_{0}\otimes e_{q+1}+e_{0}\otimes e_{q+1}\otimes e_{0}+e_{q+1}\otimes e_{0}\otimes e_{0}
. We recognize that these are all representations of the
W
-state tensor which is an important tensor in the quantum information literature (cf.
[
9
]
). Clearly
T\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{3}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
and therefore it makes sense to compute its symmetric geometric rank. Looking at
T
as a homogeneous degree three polynomial
F
we have

F=\sum_{i=1}^{q}x_{0}x_{i}^{2}+x_{0}^{2}x_{q+1}=x_{0}(x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{q}^{2}+x_{0}x_{q+1})

and by direct computation one sees that
{\mathrm{SGR}}(F)={\mathrm{codim}}(\mathrm{Sing}(F))=2
.

Example 5.3

(Small Coppersmith-Winograd tensor)
.

Fix a positive integer
q<n
and let
T\in(\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{\otimes 3}
be the tensor

\displaystyle T=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{q}(e_{0}\otimes e_{i}\otimes e_{i}+e_{i}\otimes e_{0}\otimes e_{i}+e_{i}\otimes e_{i}\otimes e_{0})=W_{1}+\cdots+W_{q},

where, as before, we denote
W_{i}=e_{0}\otimes e_{i}\otimes e_{i}+e_{i}\otimes e_{0}\otimes e_{i}+e_{i}\otimes e_{i}\otimes e_{0}
. The polynomial representation of
T
is

F=\sum_{i=1}^{q}x_{0}x_{i}^{2}=x_{0}(x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{q}^{2})

and, as before, one can directly compute that
{\mathrm{SGR}}(T)=2
.

Another interesting example is the
Maximal compressibility tensor
(cf.
[
24

).

Example 5.4

(Maximal compressibility tensor)
.

Fix a positive integer
q\leq n
and let
T\in(\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{\otimes 3}
be the tensor

\displaystyle T=

\displaystyle e_{0}\otimes e_{0}\otimes e_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{q}(e_{0}\otimes e_{i}\otimes e_{i}+e_{i}\otimes e_{0}\otimes e_{i}+e_{i}\otimes e_{i}\otimes e_{0}).

The polynomial representation of
T
is

F=x_{0}^{3}+\sum_{i=1}^{q}x_{0}x_{i}^{2}=x_{0}(x_{0}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{q}^{2}),

{\mathrm{SGR}}(T)=2
.

Remark 5.5
.

The geometric rank of
Example

5.2
,
Example

5.3
and
Example

5.4
is computed in
[
19
, Examples 5.5, 5.6 and 5.8]
respectively. In all the three cases the geometric rank is three while the symmetric geometric rank is two.

The rest of this part is devoted to computing the symmetric geometric rank of the symmetrized part of the matrix multiplication tensor, namely

sM_{\langle n+1\rangle}(A,B,C):=\frac{1}{2}[\mathrm{trace}(ABC)+\mathrm{trace}(BAC)]\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{3}\mathbb{C}^{(n+1)^{2}},

where
A,B,C
are matrices of size
n+1
. In
[
11
, Proposition 2.3]
, the authors proved that the singular locus of
\{sM_{\langle n+1\rangle}=0\}
is the variety

\mathrm{Sing}(sM_{\langle n+1\rangle})=\{[A]\in\mathbb{P}M_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})~|~A^{2}=0\}.

Therefore, to determine the symmetric geometric rank, it suffices to find the dimension of this variety.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
n\geq 1
, then

\dim\{[A]\in\mathbb{P}M_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})~|~A^{2}=0\}=\frac{(n+1)^{2}}{2}-1.

Proof.

Denote
X=\{[A]\in\mathbb{P}M_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})~|~A^{2}=0\}
.
Notice that
[A]\in X
if and only if
\mathrm{Im}(A)\subset\mathrm{Ker}(A)
. For any matrix
A
, denote
\mathrm{Ker}(A)=K_{A}
and call

\Sigma=\{(K_{A},[f_{A}])~|~f_{A}:K_{A}^{\perp}\rightarrow K_{A}\mbox{ linear}\}.

Notice that the map

\displaystyle\Sigma\qquad

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\hskip 28.45274pt}\qquad X

\displaystyle(K_{A},[f_{A}])

\displaystyle\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}[A]:K_{A}\oplus K_{A}^{\perp}\xrightarrow{\hskip 11.38092pt}K_{A}\oplus K_{A}^{\perp}\\
k\in K_{A}\mapsto 0,\,k^{\prime}\in K_{A}^{\perp}\mapsto f_{A}(k^{\prime})\end{pmatrix}

is an isomorphism, so
\dim(X)=\dim(\Sigma)
. The space
\Sigma
can be decomposed as
\Sigma=\cup_{n\geq 0}\Sigma_{k}
where

\Sigma_{k}=\{(K_{A},[f_{A}])~|~f_{A}:K_{A}^{\perp}\rightarrow K_{A}\mbox{ linear},\,\dim K_{A}=k\}

and now we can compute the dimension of
\Sigma_{k}
by way of the Grasmannian
\mathrm{Gr}(k,n+1)
Consider the projection

\displaystyle\varphi:\Sigma_{k}

\displaystyle\,\xrightarrow{\hskip 25.6073pt}\,\mathrm{Gr}(k,n+1)

\displaystyle(K_{A},

\displaystyle[f_{A}])\mapsto K_{A}.

A general fiber of
\varphi
is given by all linear maps
f_{A}:K_{A}^{\perp}\rightarrow K_{A}
, so it is of dimension
k(n+1-k)-1
and hence
\dim(\Sigma_{k})=2k(n+1-k)-1
. Then
\dim(\Sigma)=\max\{\dim\Sigma_{k}\}
which occurs for
k=(n+1)/2
. Thus
\dim(X)=(n+1)^{2}/2-1
.
∎

Corollary 5.7
.

The symmetric geometric rank of the symmetrized part
sM_{\langle n+1\rangle}
of the matrix multiplication tensor is
{\mathrm{SGR}}(sM_{\langle n+1\rangle})=\frac{(n+1)^{2}}{2}
.
