The commuting graphs of certain cyclic-by-abelian groups
Timo Velten

Abstract
Let G G be a finite, non-abelian group of the form G = A ​ N G=AN , where A ≤ G A\leq G is abelian, and N ​ ⊴ ​ G N\trianglelefteq G is cyclic. We prove that the commuting graph Γ ⁡ ( G ) \Gamma(G) of G G is either a connected graph of diameter at most four, or the disjoint union of | G ′ | + 1 |G^{\prime}|+1 complete graphs. These results apply to all finite metacyclic groups, and to groups of square-free order in particular.

1
Introduction

The commuting graph
\Gamma(G)
of a non-abelian finite group
G
is the simple
graph with vertex set
V=\{g\in G\mid g\notin Z(G)\}

and vertices
u,v\in V
are adjacent if and only
if
u
and
v
commute in
G
.

Iranmanesh and Jafarzadeh 2008
proved that the commuting graph
of a finite symmetric or alternating group is either
disconnected, or has diameter at most five. They conjectured that the commuting graph of any finite non-abelian group is either disconnected,
or has diameter bounded above by a universal constant independent of the group.
This conjecture was disproved by
Giudici and Parker 2013
.

Nevertheless, the conjecture of
[
1
]
is true for some restricted
classes of groups.

Parker 2013
proved that the commuting graph
of a solvable group with trivial center is either disconnected or has diameter at most eight.

Morgan and Parker 2013
proved that if a group has trivial center, then every connected
component of its commuting graph has diameter at most 10.
The commuting graph of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})

is either disconnected or has diameter at most eight
[
5
]
.
Recently, Carleton and Lewis
[
6
]
considered
groups with abelian Sylow subgroups;
in particular, they prove that if such a group has derived length 2
and its commuting graph is connected, then the graph has diameter at most 4.

Here we consider the following groups.

Definition 1.1
.

A finite group
G
has a
cyclic-by-abelian factorization
if there exist a normal, cyclic subgroup
N\trianglelefteq G
and an abelian subgroup

A\leq G
such that
G=AN
.

This definition generalizes metacyclic groups. If
G
is metacyclic with
G/N=\langle aN\rangle

and
N
both cyclic, then
G
has the cyclic-by-abelian factorization
G=AN
, where
A:=\langle a\rangle
.

It is well-known that all groups of square-free order are metacyclic
[
7
, Chapter V,
§3
, Theorem 11]
.
Therefore, the class of groups that have a cyclic-by-abelian factorization includes
all groups of square-free order.

We prove the following theorem on the structure of the commuting graphs
of groups that have a cyclic-by-abelian factorization. It extends
the results of
[
8
]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G
be a finite, non-abelian group with cyclic-by-abelian factorization
G=AN
.
Let
\Gamma
be its commuting graph. Then the following statements hold.

1.

The graph
\Gamma
is connected if and only if there exists a non-central
g\in G
such that
C_{G}(g)
is non-abelian.

2.

If
\Gamma
is connected, then its diameter is at most four.

3.

If
\Gamma
is disconnected, then it is the disjoint union of one complete graph
on
(|G^{\prime}|-1)|Z(G)|
and
|G^{\prime}|
complete graphs,
each on
[G:G^{\prime}]-|Z(G)|
vertices.

We demonstrate that both
2
and
3
occur.

Example 1.3
.

Consider the dihedral group of order
4n
:

D_{4n}=\langle a,x\mid a^{2}=x^{2n}=1,x^{a}=x^{-1}\rangle.

It has the cyclic-by-abelian factorization

A\ltimes N
with
A=\langle a\rangle
and
N=\langle x\rangle
. Also,

|Z(D_{4n})|=2
and
|D_{4n}^{\prime}|=n
. Since
C_{D_{4n}}(u)=N
for every
non-central
u\in N
,
Lemma

2.3
and the theorem imply that the commuting graph of
D_{4n}

is the disjoint union of one complete graph on
2(n-1)
vertices and
n
complete graphs
on
2
vertices.

Example 1.4
.

Consider the group

G=\langle a,x\mid a^{m}=x^{n}=1,x^{a}=x^{2}\rangle

(1)

where
n\geq 9
is an odd multiple of
3
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
2^{m}\equiv 1\pmod{n}
.
Then
G=AN
where
A=\langle a\rangle
and
N=\langle x\rangle
.
It holds that
N\cap Z(G)=\{1\}
. Moreover, if
s=n/3
, then

(x^{s})^{a^{2}}=x^{4s}=x^{s}
and
C_{G}(x^{s})\not\leq\langle N,Z(G)\rangle
.
By
Lemma

2.3
and the theorem, the commuting graph of
G

is connected and has diameter at most four.

We used the computer algebra system
GAP

[
9
]
,
the
Small Groups
library
[
10
]
and the
GAP
package
GRAPE

[
11
]

to demonstrate that our results are sharp.

Various metacyclic groups have a commuting graph with diameter four, for example

\mathrm{SmallGroup}(60,7)
, which is the group
defined in (
1
) for
(n,m)=(15,4)
. Also,
\mathrm{SmallGroup}(96,118)

has a cyclic-by-abelian factorization, is
not
metacyclic and its commuting graph has diameter four.

If
G
has a cyclic-by-abelian factorization, then

G^{\prime}
is cyclic. However, this condition alone does not imply
Theorem

1.2
.
For example,
\mathrm{SmallGroup}(1040,93)
has a cyclic commutator
subgroup but its commuting graph has diameter five. Also,
\mathrm{SmallGroup}(48,15)

has a cyclic commutator subgroup and a disconnected commuting graph which
is not the disjoint union of complete graphs.

Following
[
12
]
, an AC-group is a group in which the centralizer
of every non-central element is abelian.
Theorem

1.2

asserts that a group that admits a cyclic-by-abelian factorization has a disconnected commuting
graph if and only if it is an AC-group. These were studied by
Schmidt 1971
; he refers to them as
\mathfrak{M}
-groups.

2
Preliminary results and notation

For

g,h\in G
we write
g^{h}:=h^{-1}gh
for conjugation and

[g,h]:=g^{-1}h^{-1}gh=g^{-1}g^{h}
for their commutator. The centralizer
of
g\in G
is denoted by
C_{G}(g)
, and the center of
G
by
Z(G)
.

We will use the following basic result frequently.

Remark 2.1
.

Let
G
have an abelian normal subgroup
N
. Then

[u,g]^{l}=[u^{l},g]
for every
u\in N
,
g\in G
and
l\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

[u,g]^{l}=(u^{-1}u^{g})^{l}=u^{-l}(u^{l})^{g}=[u^{l},g]
.
∎

The following lemma collects properties of groups that have a cyclic-by-abelian factorization.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
G
be a finite group with cyclic-by-abelian factorization
G=AN

and
N=\langle x\rangle
.

1.

Let
u,v\in N

and
g,h\in G
such that
g
and
h
commute. Then
gu
and
hv

commute if and only if
[u,h]=[v,g]
.

2.

N\cap Z(G)=\langle x^{n}\rangle
where
n:=|G^{\prime}|
. In particular,
|\langle N,Z(G)\rangle|=|G^{\prime}||Z(G)|
.

3.

If
[x,g]
generates
G^{\prime}
, then
|C_{G}(g)|=[G:G^{\prime}]
and
C_{G}(g)
is abelian.

Proof.

1
Note that

(gu)\cdot(hv)=(gh)(u^{h}v)\quad\text{ and }\quad(hv)\cdot(gu)=(hg)(v^{g}u).

As
gh=hg
, this implies that
gu
and
hv
commute
if and only if
u^{h}v=v^{g}u
. Since
N
is abelian,

[u,h]=u^{-1}u^{h}=v^{-1}v^{g}=[v,g]
.

2
Let
g\in G
. Then
1=[x,g]^{n}=[x^{n},g]
, so
x^{n}\in Z(G)\cap N
.
To show the other inclusion, suppose that
x^{s}\in N\cap Z(G)
. Note that
G^{\prime}

is the normal closure
of commutators of the form
[x,a]
for
a\in A
, so

G^{\prime}=\langle[x,a]\mid a\in A\rangle^{G}
.
For every
a\in A
, it holds that
[x,a]^{s}=[x^{s},a]=1
. Therefore,

G^{\prime}
is generated by elements of order dividing
s
. Moreover,
G/N
is abelian, so
G^{\prime}\leq N

is cyclic. From this, we conclude that
n=|G^{\prime}|
must also divide
s
, so
x^{s}\in\langle x^{n}\rangle
.

3
Let
B:=\langle A,Z(G)\rangle
and let
T:=\{1,x,\dots,x^{n-1}\}
.
Then
T
is a transversal of the right cosets of
N\cap Z(G)=B\cap N
in
G
,
and therefore also a transversal of the right cosets of
B
in
G=BN
. In particular, every
g\in G

can be uniquely written as
g=bx^{k}
with
b\in B
and
0\leq k<n
and
|G|=|B||G^{\prime}|
.

Now we determine
C_{G}(g)
. Write
g=ax^{k}
with
a\in B
and
0\leq k<n
. Fix
b\in B
.
We determine all integers
\ell=\ell(b)
such that
bx^{\ell}
commutes with
g
. Because
b
and
a
commute,
we know from
Lemma

2.2
that
g
and
bx^{\ell}
commute if and only if

[x^{k},b]=[x^{\ell},a]=[x,a]^{\ell}=[x,g]^{\ell}.

Since
[x,g]
generates
G^{\prime}
, there is exactly one integer
\ell
that satisfies this equation.

This establishes the existence of a mapping
\ell\colon B\to\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
such that

C_{G}(g)=\{bx^{\ell(b)}\mid b\in B\}.

These elements are distinct, so
|C_{G}(g)|=|B|
. As
|G|=|B||G^{\prime}|
,
it follows that
|C_{G}(g)|=[G:G^{\prime}]
.

We now prove that
C_{G}(g)
is abelian. Consider two elements
bx^{\ell(b)}

and
cx^{\ell(c)}
of
C_{G}(g)
.
It suffices to prove that
[x,c]^{\ell(b)}=[x,b]^{\ell(c)}
. As
both commute with
g

[x,a]^{\ell(b)}=[x^{k},b]\quad\text{ and }\quad[x,a]^{\ell(c)}=[x^{k},c].

Furthermore,
[x,a]=[x,g]
generates
G^{\prime}
, so there exist integers
r_{b}
and
r_{c}

such that
[x,a]^{r_{b}}=[x,b]
and
[x,a]^{r_{c}}=[x,c]
. We deduce that

[x,b]^{\ell(c)}=[x,a]^{r_{b}\ell(c)}=[x^{k},c]^{r_{b}}=[x,c]^{kr_{b}}=[x,a]^{kr_{b}r_{c}}.

Similarly,
[x,c]^{\ell(b)}=[x,a]^{kr_{b}r_{c}}
so

[x,c]^{\ell(b)}=[x,b]^{\ell(c)}
. We conclude that
bx^{\ell(b)}
and
cx^{\ell(c)}
commute
and that
C_{G}(g)
is abelian.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
G
be a finite, non-abelian group with cyclic-by-abelian factorization
G=AN
.
Then the following statements are equivalent.

1.

C_{G}(u)\leq\langle N,Z(G)\rangle
for every non-central
u\in N
.

2.

C_{G}(g)
is abelian for every non-central
g\in G
.

Moreover, if either of these statements hold, then for every non-central
g\in G

either
C_{G}(g)=\langle N,Z(G)\rangle
, or
|C_{G}(g)|=[G:G^{\prime}]
,
or both hold.

Proof.

\neg\ref{equiv:N}\implies\neg\ref{equiv:abelian}
: Let
h\in C_{G}(x)\setminus\langle N,Z(G)\rangle
with

x\in N
non-central. Write
h=ay
with
a\in A
and
y\in N
. Then
a
commutes with
x
. Also,
a

does not commute with every element of
N
. If it did, then
a
would be central and

h
would be in
\langle N,Z(G)\rangle
. Therefore,

\langle a,N\rangle
is a non-abelian subgroup of
C_{G}(x)
and
C_{G}(x)
is non-abelian.

1

\implies

2
: Let
g
be non-central in
G
. If

[x,g]
generates
G^{\prime}
, then
C_{G}(g)
is abelian and
|C_{G}(g)|=[G:G^{\prime}]
by
Lemma

2.2
.

Now, suppose that
[x,g]
does not generate
G^{\prime}
and has order
s<|G^{\prime}|
.
Then
[x^{s},g]=[x,g]^{s}=1
so
x^{s}
and
g
commute. Also,
x^{s}
is not central by
Lemma

2.2
.
Therefore,
g\in C_{G}(x^{s})=\langle N,Z(G)\rangle
. Write
g=yz
with
y\in N
and
z\in Z(G)
. Then

C_{G}(g)=C_{G}(y)=\langle N,Z(G)\rangle
. In particular,
C_{G}(g)
is abelian.
∎

3
Proof of
Theorem

1.2

.

Let
N=\langle x\rangle
and
n:=|G^{\prime}|
. We distinguish two cases.

Case
1
: There exists a non-central
g\in G
such that
C_{G}(g)
is non-abelian
.
Then there exists
u^{*}\in N
such that
C_{G}(u^{*})
is strictly larger than
\langle N,Z(G)\rangle
by
Lemma

2.3
. We show that
the vertex
u^{*}
has distance at most two to every other vertex of
\Gamma
.
That is, we prove that

C_{G}(g)\cap C_{G}(u^{*})\neq Z(G)\quad\text{ for all }\quad g\in G.

If
[x,g]
does not generate
G^{\prime}
, and has order
s<n
, then

1=[x,g]^{s}=[x^{s},g]

so
x^{s}
and
g
commute. By
Lemma

2.2
,
x^{s}
is not central, so
x^{s}

is a non-central element that commutes with both
g
and
u^{*}
.

Now suppose that
[x,g]
generates
G^{\prime}
. By assumption,
C_{G}(u^{*})

is strictly larger than
\langle N,Z(G)\rangle
. It follows from
Lemma

2.2
that

|C_{G}(u*)|>|G^{\prime}||Z(G)|
and
|C_{G}(g)|=[G:G^{\prime}]
. Using this, we see that

\displaystyle|C_{G}(g)\cap C_{G}(u^{*})|=\frac{|C_{G}(g)|[G:G^{\prime}]}{|C_{G}(u^{*})C_{G}(g)|}\geq\frac{C_{G}(u*)}{|G^{\prime}|}>\frac{|\langle N,Z(G)\rangle|}{|G^{\prime}|}=|Z(G)|

so
C_{G}(u^{*})\cap C_{G}(g)
contains at least one non-central element of
G
.

This proves that every vertex of
\Gamma
has distance at most two to
u^{*}
, so
\Gamma

is connected and has diameter at most four.

Case
2
: The centralizer of every non-central element of
G
is abelian
.
In this case, adjacency of two
vertices of
\Gamma
is a transitive relation. In other words,
\Gamma

is a disjoint union of complete graphs and, in particular, it is disconnected.

Let
g\in G
be non-central. If
g\in\langle N,Z(G)\rangle
, then
C_{G}(g)=\langle N,Z(G)\rangle
. Therefore,
the connected component of
g
in
\Gamma
is a complete graph on
|\langle N,Z(G)\rangle|-|Z(G)|=|Z(G)|(|G^{\prime}|-1)
vertices.
If
g\notin\langle N,Z(G)\rangle
, then
Lemma

2.3
asserts that
|C_{G}(g)|=[G:G^{\prime}]
, so
the connected component of
g
has
[G:G^{\prime}]-|Z(G)|
vertices.

We conclude that
\Gamma
is the disjoint union of one complete graph on
|Z(G)|(|G^{\prime}|-1)

vertices, and
r
complete graphs on
[G:G^{\prime}]-|Z(G)|
, where

r=\frac{|\Gamma|-|Z(G)|(|G^{\prime}|-1)}{[G:G^{\prime}]-|Z(G)|}=\frac{|G|-|Z(G)||G^{\prime}|}{\frac{|G|}{|G^{\prime}|}-|Z(G)|}=|G^{\prime}|.\qed
