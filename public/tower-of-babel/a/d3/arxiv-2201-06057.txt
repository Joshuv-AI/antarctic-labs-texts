A Cut Finite Element Method for two-phase flows with insoluble surfactants
Thomas Frachon, Sara Zahedi

Abstract
We propose a new unfitted finite element method for simulation of two-phase flows in presence of insoluble surfactant. The key features of the method are 1) discrete conservation of surfactant mass; 2) the possibility of having meshes that do not conform to the evolving interface separating the immiscible fluids; 3) accurate approximation of quantities with weak or strong discontinuities across evolving geometries such as the velocity field and the pressure. The new discretization of the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations coupled to the convection-diffusion equation modeling the surfactant transport on evolving surfaces is based on a space-time cut finite element formulation with quadrature in time and a stabilization term in the weak formulation that provides function extension. The proposed strategy utilize the same computational mesh for the discretization of the surface Partial Differential Equation (PDE) and the bulk PDEs and can be combined with different techniques for representing and evolving the interface, here the level set method is used. Numerical simulations in both two and three space dimensions are presented including simulations showing the role of surfactant in the interaction between two drops.



Introduction

Surfactants are present in many forms and play an important role in many applications, for example in drug delivery for their ability of solubilizing drugs and encapsulating microbubbles with a shell or in petroleum industries for promoting oil flow
[
1
]
. Computer simulation is an important tool in such applications and is used to study the effects of surfactants on drop deformation, breakup or coalescence in multiphase flow systems.

There are several challenges that computational methods need to handle both with respect to the representation and evolution of the interface separating the immiscible fluids and the approximation of quantities such as the surfactant concentration, the fluid velocity, and the pressure. Here we assume that we know the PDEs that model two-phase flow systems in the presence of insoluble surfactant and that we also have a numerical representation technique for representing and evolving the interface. Given that, we propose a strategy based on finite element methods to accurately discretize the given PDEs both in two and three space dimensions so that the total surfactant mass is conserved and with accurate approximation of discontinuities across evolving interfaces without conforming the mesh to the time dependent domains.

Many computational methods have been developed for simulations of two-phase flow systems with surfactants, see for example
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
, where discretizations based on finite differences are proposed,
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
for strategies based on boundary integral methods,
[
9
]
for a conservative discretization based on finite volume methods, and
[
10
,
11
]
for different strategies using finite element methods.

Our method falls into the class of so called unfitted finite element methods. We use a regular mesh that covers the computational domain but does not need to conform to outer or internal boundaries (in this case the interface). We refer to this mesh as the background mesh. On the background mesh we start with appropriate standard finite element spaces. We then define 1) active meshes corresponding to the subdomains occupied by the immiscible fluids and the interface; 2) active finite element spaces; 3) a weak formulation so that
the proposed method is accurate and robust independent of the interface position relative the background mesh.
Active spaces are defined as the restriction of the standard finite element spaces defined on the background mesh to the active mesh. From the active spaces we build pressure and velocity spaces with functions that are double valued on elements in a region around the interface. Hence functions in our function spaces can be discontinuous across interfaces separating the two fluids. Therefore, we don’t need to regularize discontinuities by introducing regularized delta functions to approximate the surface tension force as for example in
[
12
,
2
,
4
]
or/and regularized Heaviside functions to approximate densities and viscosities across the interface as for example in
[
13
]
. In the weak formulation of the Navier–Stokes equations coupled to the surfactant transport equation, we handle space and time similarly, physical interface conditions are imposed weakly, and we add stabilization terms in the weak form that are vital for the proposed method and its straightforward implementation.

This work is a further development of the space-time Cut Finite Element Method (CutFEM) proposed in
[
14
]
to two-phase Navier–Stokes flows where also surfactant is present. We note that a CutFEM for the two-phase Navier–Stokes equations without surfactant has also been proposed in
[
15
]
. However, the discretization in
[
15
]
is based on implicit Euler, linear elements for both velocity and pressure, and a first order approximation of the mean curvature proposed in
[
16
]
and is therefore limited to first order accuracy. For the discretization of the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations we here start with a regular mesh and the inf-sup stable Taylor-Hood element P2-P1 pair for velocity and pressure. See
[
17
]
and
[
18
]
for stability results. We also use an integration by part result in the presence of surfactant and avoid explicit computation of the mean curvature of the interface. The proposed space-time strategy for the surfactant transport equation is similar to
[
19
]
but here we rewrite the weak formulation so that the surfactant mass is discretely conserved without enforcing the mass with a Lagrange multiplier. As in
[
19
]
and
[
14
]
the space-time integrals in the weak formulation are approximated using quadrature rules, utilizing that the stabilization term defines the extension we need to have a robust and stable method. The proposed method decouples the representation and evolution of the interface from the discretization of the PDEs and although we in this work use the standard level set method
[
20
,
21
]
we emphasize that other interface representation techniques can also be used. See for example
[
22
]
where a space-time cut finite element discretization is used together with an explicit representation of the interface using cubic splines.

.

Recall the Sobolev spaces

L^{2}(U)=\left\{v\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\int_{U}|v|^{2}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\|v\|_{L^{2}(U)}^{2}<\infty\right\},

(3)

and

H^{1}(U)=\left\{v\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}v\in L^{2}(U),\nabla v\in L^{2}(U)\right\},

(4)

and is in this paper

,
\Omega_{i}(t)
, or
\Gamma(t)
for

. We will use the notation

for the

-inner product on

(similarly for inner products in

).
For

we write

and we mean

where
v_{i}=v|_{\Omega_{i}(t)}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{i}(t))
. We use the notation

\left(v\ ,\ w\right)_{\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(v_{i}\ ,\ w_{i}\right)_{\Omega_{i}}.

(5)

Given the time interval

we use the notation
\mathcal{G}_{I,\Gamma}
to denote the following space-time surface

\mathcal{G}_{I,\Gamma}=\bigcup_{t\in I}\{t\}\times\Gamma(t).

(6)

The Mathematical model

In this paper we consider two-phase flow systems with insoluble surfactants present. The subdomains
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
are occupied by incompressible and immiscible fluids with densities
\rho_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and viscosities
\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
i=1,2
.
Let
\mathbf{u}:I\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the fluid velocity,
p:I\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
denote the pressure, and
w:\mathcal{G}_{I,\Gamma}\rightarrow[0,w_{\infty})
denote the surfactant concentration where
w_{\infty}
is the highest surfactant concentration on
\Gamma
. We assume the dynamics is governed by the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations coupled to a time-dependent convection-diffusion equation on the interface
\Gamma(t)
that separates the two immiscible fluids:

\displaystyle\rho\left(\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla){\mathbf{u}}\right)-\nabla\cdot\left(2\mu\bm{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})-p\mathbf{I}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}

\displaystyle\quad\text{in}\ \Omega_{1}(t)\cup\Omega_{2}(t),\quad t\in(0,T]

(7)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{in}\ \Omega_{1}(t)\cup\Omega_{2}(t),

(8)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathbf{u}\rrbracket

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{on}\ \Gamma(t),

(9)

\displaystyle\llbracket(2\mu\bm{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})-p\mathbf{I})\mathbf{n}\rrbracket

\displaystyle=\sigma(w)\mathbf{H}+\nabla_{\Gamma}\sigma(w)

\displaystyle\quad\text{on}\ \Gamma(t),

(10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(t,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}(t,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\quad\text{on}\ \partial\Omega

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(0,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{0}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\quad\text{in}\ \Omega_{1}(0)\cup\Omega_{2}(0)

(12)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla w+(\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot\mathbf{u})w-\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot(D_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}w)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{on}\ \Gamma(t)

(13)

\displaystyle w(0,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=w^{0}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma(0)

(14)

Here,
\partial_{t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
,
\bm{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})=(\nabla\mathbf{u}+(\nabla\mathbf{u})^{T})/2
is the strain rate tensor,
\sigma
is the surface tension which depends on the surfactant concentration,
\mathbf{H}
is the mean curvature vector defined by
\mathbf{H}=\Delta_{\Gamma}\mathbf{x}_{\Gamma}
with
\mathbf{x}_{\Gamma}:\Gamma\ni\mathbf{x}\rightarrow\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
being the coordinate map or embedding of
\Gamma
into
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
D_{\Gamma}>0
is the diffusion coefficient and is assumed to be constant,
\mathbf{f}:I\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a given external force (e.g. the gravitational force), and
\mathbf{g}
is a given function such that
\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{n}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s=0
. We can also have other type of suitable boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
. The initial configuration, that is the interface
\Gamma(0)
and hence the subdomains
\Omega_{i}(0)
,
i=1,2
, the initial data
\mathbf{u}^{0}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
w^{0}:\Gamma(0)\rightarrow[0,w_{\infty})
are also given.
In this work we do not consider high Reynolds number flows.

The evolution of the interface
\Gamma(t)
is given by the following advection equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\phi=0\quad\textrm{in }\Omega,t\in I.

(15)

Here
\phi(t,\mathbf{x}):I\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the signed distance function with positive sign in
\Omega_{2}(t)
, the subdomain enclosed by the interface, and the zero level set of this function represents the interface
\Gamma(t)
, i.e.,

\Gamma(t)=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\phi(t,\mathbf{x})=0\right\}.

(16)

The initial condition
\phi(0,\mathbf{x})=\phi_{0}(\mathbf{x})
,
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
is given by the configuration of
\Gamma(0)
.

Finally, we use the following linear equation of state:

\sigma(w)=\sigma_{0}(1-\beta w),

(17)

where
\sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
are given parameters. Note that
\beta=0
corresponds to the surfactant-free (clean) interface with
\sigma=\sigma_{0}
being constant. Other models then this linear model describing the relation between the surface tension
\sigma:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and the surfactant concentration
w
can also be used, see
[
23
]
. The numerical method we will present is not restricted to this linear relation and we discuss this in Section
6.1
.

A weak formulation

Let

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}=

\displaystyle\left[H^{1}(\Omega_{1}(t)\cup\Omega_{2}(t))\right]^{d},

\displaystyle Q=

\displaystyle\left\{q\in L^{2}(\Omega_{1}(t)\cup\Omega_{2}(t))\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\left(\mu^{-1}q\ ,\ 1\right)_{\Omega_{1}(t)\cup\Omega_{2}(t)}=0\right\},

\displaystyle W=

\displaystyle H^{1}(\mathcal{G}_{I,\Gamma}).

A weak formulation of the two-phase flow model (

,

\displaystyle b(t,\mathbf{u},q)+\left(\sigma(w)\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{x}_{\Gamma}\ ,\ \nabla_{\Gamma}\langle\mathbf{v}\rangle\right)_{\Gamma(t)}

\displaystyle=l(t,v,q),\quad\forall v\in\mathbf{V},q\in Q,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t}\left(w\ ,\ r\right)_{\Gamma(t)}-\left(w\ ,\ \partial_{t}r+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla r\right)_{\Gamma(t)}+\left(D_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}w\ ,\ \nabla_{\Gamma}r\right)_{\Gamma(t)}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall r\in W,

(18)

and

\displaystyle a(t,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=\left(2\mu\varepsilon(\mathbf{u})\ ,\ \varepsilon(\mathbf{v})\right)_{\Omega_{1}(t)\cup\Omega_{2}(t)}

\displaystyle-\left(\{2\mu\varepsilon(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{n}\}\ ,\ \llbracket\mathbf{v}\rrbracket\right)_{\Gamma(t)}-\left(\llbracket\mathbf{u}\rrbracket\ ,\ \{2\mu\varepsilon(\mathbf{v})\mathbf{n}\}\right)_{\Gamma(t)}+\left(\lambda_{\Gamma}\llbracket\mathbf{u}\rrbracket\ ,\ \llbracket\mathbf{v}\rrbracket\right)_{\Gamma(t)}

(19)

\displaystyle b(t,\mathbf{v},q)

\displaystyle=\left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}\ ,\ q\right)_{\Omega_{1}(t)\cup\Omega_{2}(t)}-\left(\llbracket\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}\rrbracket\ ,\ \{q\}\right)_{\Gamma(t)}-\left(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}\ ,\ q\right)_{\partial\Omega},

(20)

\displaystyle l(t,\mathbf{v},q)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{f}\ ,\ \mathbf{v}\right)_{\Omega_{1}(t)\cup\Omega_{2}(t)}-\left(\mathbf{g}\ ,\ 2\mu\varepsilon(\mathbf{v})\mathbf{n}\right)_{\partial\Omega}+\left(\lambda_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{g}\ ,\ \mathbf{v}\right)_{\partial\Omega}-\left(\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{n}\ ,\ q\right)_{\partial\Omega},

where

(33)

where

are coefficients and

is the standard nodal basis function associated with mesh vertex

in the finite element space
\mathcal{V}_{h}^{1}

Before we formulate the finite element method for the two-phase flow model we propose and study a space-time cut finite element method for the surface PDE modeling surfactant transport.

The surfactant transport equation

In this section we assume that the velocity field
\mathbf{u}
is known and explicitly given and we consider the following convection-diffusion problem:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla w+(\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot\mathbf{u})w-\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot(D_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}w)

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }\Gamma(t),\quad t\in(0,T],

(38)

\displaystyle w(0,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=w^{0}

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma(0).

(39)

Following the derivation in Section
3.1
we have for
w
being a sufficiently smooth solution to the differential equation (
38
)-(
39
),
r\in W
, and
t\in I
that

\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t}\left(w\ ,\ r\right)_{\Gamma(t)}-\left(w\ ,\ \partial_{t}r+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla r\right)_{\Gamma(t)}+\left(D_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}w\ ,\ \nabla_{\Gamma}r\right)_{\Gamma(t)}=\left(f\ ,\ r\right)_{\Gamma(t)},

(40)

Assuming
w^{-}(t_{n},\mathbf{x})
is given, integrating in the time interval
I_{n}
, and using that
w^{+}(t_{n},\mathbf{x})=w^{-}(t_{n},\mathbf{x})
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{I_{n}}\left(f\ ,\ r\right)_{\Gamma(t)}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t=\int_{I_{n}}\left[\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t}\left(w\ ,\ r\right)_{\Gamma(t)}-\left(w\ ,\ \partial_{t}r+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla r\right)_{\Gamma(t)}+\left(D_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}w\ ,\ \nabla_{\Gamma}r\right)_{\Gamma(t)}\right]\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\left(w\ ,\ r\right)_{\Gamma(t_{n+1})}-\left(w^{-}(t_{n},\mathbf{x})\ ,\ r\right)_{\Gamma(t_{n})}+\int_{I_{n}}\left[-\left(w\ ,\ \partial_{t}r+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla r\right)_{\Gamma(t)}+\left(D_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}w\ ,\ \nabla_{\Gamma}r\right)_{\Gamma(t)}\right]\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t.

(41)

In particular, note that with
r=1
in (
5
) we get

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma(t_{n+1})}w(t_{n+1},\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s-\int_{\Gamma(t_{n})}w^{-}(t_{n},\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s-\int_{I_{n}}\int_{\Gamma(t)}f\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t=0.

(42)

The Finite element method

We are now ready to present a discretization of the convection-diffusion equation on the interface. Assume the active mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h,0}^{n}
and the solution
w_{h}^{-}(t_{n},\mathbf{x})
from the previous space-time slab are known: Find
w_{h}\in W_{h}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle A(\mathbf{u},w_{h},r_{h})+S(w_{h},r_{h})=\int_{I_{n}}\left(f\ ,\ r_{h}\right)_{\Gamma(t)}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t+\left(w_{h}^{-}\ ,\ r_{h}\right)_{\Gamma(t_{n})},\quad\forall r_{h}\in W_{h}^{n},

(43)

where

\displaystyle A(\mathbf{u},w_{h},r_{h})

\displaystyle=\left(w_{h}\ ,\ r_{h}\right)_{\Gamma(t_{n+1})}-\int_{I_{n}}\left(w_{h}\ ,\ \partial_{t}r_{h}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla r_{h}\right)_{\Gamma(t)}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t+\int_{I_{n}}\left(D_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}w_{h}\ ,\ \nabla_{\Gamma}r_{h}\right)_{\Gamma(t)}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t

(44)

and

\displaystyle S(w_{h},r_{h})=\int_{I_{n}}s_{w}(t,w_{h},r_{h})\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t.

(45)

The last term
S
is a stabilization term which is added to ensure that the method is stable and robust independent of how the interface is positioned relative the mesh. There are several choices for
s_{w}
, see e.g.
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
and references therein. In this work we use

\displaystyle s_{w}(t,w_{h},r_{h})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(c_{F,j}h^{2j-1}\left(\llbracket D^{j}_{\mathbf{n}_{F}}w_{h}\rrbracket_{F}\ ,\ \llbracket D^{j}_{\mathbf{n}_{F}}r_{h}\rrbracket_{F}\right)_{\mathcal{F}_{h,0}^{n}}+c_{\Gamma,j}h^{2j-1}\left(D^{j}_{\mathbf{n}_{{h}}}w_{h}\ ,\ D^{j}_{\mathbf{n}_{{h}}}r_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{h}(t)}\right),

(46)

where
c_{F,j}
, and
c_{\Gamma,j}
are positive constants. This stabilization was analyzed in connection with a CutFEM for surface PDEs on stationary surfaces in
[
27
]
. For the space-time CutFEM it is important to define the set
\mathcal{F}_{h,0}^{n}
, on which ghost penalty stabilization is applied, appropriately (see Figure
2
). In particular note that in a space-time slab the set
\mathcal{F}_{h,0}^{n}
does not depend on time. The proposed stabilization term yields an extension and therefore allows us to propose a cut finite element scheme based on directly approximating the space-time integrals in the weak formulation using quadrature rules, see the next section. The proposed stabilization can be used also when the polynomial degree
m>1
. For
m=1
an alternative is to extend the stabilization proposed in
[
26
]
to the space-time CutFEM, thus only having ghost penalty terms evaluated on the faces in
\mathcal{F}_{h,0}^{n}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle s_{w}(t,w_{h},r_{h})=c_{F,j}\left(\llbracket D^{j}_{\mathbf{n}_{F}}w_{h}\rrbracket_{F}\ ,\ \llbracket D^{j}_{\mathbf{n}_{F}}r_{h}\rrbracket_{F}\right)_{\mathcal{F}_{h,0}}.

If the problem is convection dominated other stabilization terms such as streamline diffusion stabilization can be added, see e.g.
[
28
,
29
]
.

5.1.1
Space-time CutFEM with quadrature in time and implementation

We propose a weak form where integrals in time are replaced with a
n_{q}
-point quadrature rule with weights
\alpha_{q}^{n}
and points
t_{q}^{n}
,
q=1,\cdots,n_{q}
. Thus, we propose the following weak formulation: Given
w_{h}^{-}(t_{n},\mathbf{x})
, find
w_{h}\in W_{h}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle A^{n}_{h}(\mathbf{u},w_{h},r_{h})+\sum_{q=1}^{n_{q}}\alpha_{q}^{n}s_{w}(t_{q}^{n},w_{h},r_{h})=\sum_{q=1}^{n_{q}}\alpha_{q}^{n}\left(f\ ,\ r_{h}\right)_{\Gamma(t_{q}^{n})}+\left(w_{h}^{-}\ ,\ r_{h}\right)_{\Gamma(t_{n})},\quad\forall r_{h}\in W_{h}^{n},

(47)

where

\displaystyle A_{h}^{n}(\mathbf{u},w_{h},r_{h})

\displaystyle=\left(w_{h}\ ,\ r_{h}\right)_{\Gamma(t_{n+1})}-\sum_{q=1}^{n_{q}}\alpha_{q}^{n}\left(w_{h}\ ,\ \partial_{t}r_{h}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla r_{h}\right)_{\Gamma(t_{q}^{n})}+\sum_{q=1}^{n_{q}}\alpha_{q}^{n}\left(D_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}w_{h}\ ,\ \nabla_{\Gamma}r_{h}\right)_{\Gamma(t_{q}^{n})}.

(48)

Recall the representation of functions in
W_{h}^{n}
, see (
32
), and note that for example for the second term in
A_{h}^{n}
we have

\sum_{q=1}^{n_{q}}\alpha_{q}^{n}(w_{h},\partial_{t}r_{h})_{\Gamma(t_{q}^{n})}=\sum_{q=1}^{n_{q}}\alpha_{q}^{n}\frac{1}{\Delta t_{n}}(w_{h,0},r_{h,1})_{\Gamma(t_{q}^{n})}+\sum_{q=1}^{n_{q}}\alpha_{q}^{n}\frac{t_{q}^{n}-t_{n}}{\Delta t_{n}^{2}}(w_{h,1},r_{h,1})_{\Gamma(t_{q}^{n})}.

(49)

Furthermore, since the set
\mathcal{F}_{h,0}^{n}
, see definition of
s_{w}
in (
46
), is on each space-time slab independent of time and the trial and the test functions, (
w_{h}
and
r_{h}
) which are time dependent are polynomials, the quadrature rule can be chosen so that the integration of the face stabilization term is exact, i.e.,

\displaystyle\int_{I_{n}}\left(\llbracket D^{j}_{\mathbf{n}_{F}}w_{h}(t,\mathbf{x})\rrbracket_{F}\ ,\ \llbracket D^{j}_{\mathbf{n}_{F}}r_{h}(t,\mathbf{x})\rrbracket_{F}\right)_{\mathcal{F}_{h,0}^{n}}dt=\sum_{q=1}^{n_{q}}\alpha_{q}^{n}\left(\llbracket D^{j}_{\mathbf{n}_{F}}w_{h}(t_{q}^{n},\mathbf{x})\rrbracket_{F}\ ,\ \llbracket D^{j}_{\mathbf{n}_{F}}r_{h}(t_{q}^{n},\mathbf{x})\rrbracket_{F}\right)_{\mathcal{F}_{h,0}^{n}}.

(50)

In the numerical examples we use Simpson’s quadrature rule. Hence the quadrature points are
t_{1}^{n}=t_{n}
,
t_{3}^{n}=t_{n+1}
,
t_{2}^{n}=\frac{t_{n}+t_{n+1}}{2}
, and the quadrature weights are
\alpha_{1}^{n}=\alpha_{3}^{n}=\frac{\Delta t_{n}}{6}
,
\alpha_{2}^{n}=\frac{4\Delta t_{n}}{6}
.

Note that taking
r_{h}=1
in the proposed weak formulation (
47
) yields the following conservation property

\displaystyle\left(w_{h}\ ,\ r_{h}\right)_{\Gamma(t_{n+1})}-\left(w_{h}^{-}\ ,\ r_{h}\right)_{\Gamma(t_{n})}-\sum_{q=1}^{n_{q}}\alpha_{q}^{n}\left(f\ ,\ r_{h}\right)_{\Gamma(t_{q}^{n})}=0.

(51)

To implement the proposed method one needs to know the position of the interface at the quadrature points,
\Gamma(t_{q}^{n})
, and also the set
\mathcal{F}_{h,0}^{n}
. Often we do not have the exact interface position. In this paper we use a piecewise planar approximation of the interface. Let
T^{n}=\{t_{n},\{t_{q}^{n}\},t_{n+1}\}
. For each
t\in T^{n}
, we either directly make an approximation of the level set function in the space
\mathcal{V}_{h}^{1}
if
\phi
is known explicitly at all time or we discretize the advection equation (
15
) using the Crank-Nicolson scheme in time and continuous FEM in space with linear Lagrange elements and streamline diffusion stabilization, following
[
14
]
. By looking for sign change in each element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, i.e., checking the sign of this approximated level set function,
\phi_{h}(t,\mathbf{x})
,
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
, we find those elements in the mesh that are cut by the interface and thus belong to the set
\mathcal{T}_{h,0}(t)
for each
t\in T^{n}
(see (
28
)). We compute a piecewise planar approximation
\Gamma_{h}(t)
at each time instance
t\in T^{n}
by computing the intersection of the zero level set of
\phi_{h}(t,\mathbf{x})
with each cut element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h,0}(t)
.

We also need the set of faces
\mathcal{F}_{h,0}^{n}
where the stabilization is active. This set consist of all interior faces (faces with two neighbors) in the active mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h,0}^{n}
. Hence we need to know if an element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
is in the active mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h,0}^{n}
or not. We say that an element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
is in the active mesh if it is cut by the interface at some time instance
t\in T^{n}
or if there are two time instances
t_{k}\in T^{n}
and
t_{l}\in T^{n}
where
K\cap\Omega_{1}(t_{k})\neq\emptyset
but
K\cap\Omega_{1}(t_{l})=\emptyset
(i.e.
K\cap\Omega_{2}(t_{l})\neq\emptyset
).
Using our approximation of the signed distance function,
\phi_{h}(t,\mathbf{x})
, we say that an element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
belongs to
\mathcal{T}_{h,0}^{n}
if it is in
\mathcal{T}_{h,0}(t)
at some time instance
t\in T^{n}
or the sign of
\phi_{h}(t_{k},K)
is different than
\phi_{h}(t_{l},K)
for two time instances
t_{k},t_{l}\in T^{n}
.

Remark 5.1

Note that if Reynolds’ transport theorem (
3.1
) holds, formulation (
40
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}w+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla w+w\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot\mathbf{u}\ ,\ r\right)_{\Gamma(t)}+\left(D_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}w\ ,\ \nabla_{\Gamma}r\right)_{\Gamma(t)}=\left(f\ ,\ r\right)_{\Gamma(t)},

(52)

and from (
5
) we have

\displaystyle\int_{I_{n}}\left[\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t}\left(w\ ,\ r\right)_{\Gamma(t)}-\left(w\ ,\ \partial_{t}r+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla r\right)_{\Gamma(t)}+\left(D_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}w\ ,\ \nabla_{\Gamma}r\right)_{\Gamma(t)}\right]\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t

(53)

\displaystyle=\left(\llbracket w\rrbracket_{t_{n}}\ ,\ r\right)_{\Gamma(t_{n})}+\int_{I_{n}}\left[\left(\partial_{t}w+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla w+w\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot\mathbf{u}\ ,\ r\right)_{\Gamma(t)}+\left(D_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}w\ ,\ \nabla_{\Gamma}r\right)_{\Gamma(t)}\right]\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t.

(54)

Hence, one may instead of the bilinear form
A_{h}^{n}
in (
48
) use

\displaystyle A_{h}^{n}

\displaystyle=\left(w_{h}\ ,\ r_{h}\right)_{\Gamma(t_{n})}+\sum_{q=1}^{n_{q}}\alpha_{q}^{n}\left[\left(\partial_{t}w_{h}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla w_{h}+w_{h}\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot\mathbf{u}\ ,\ r_{h}\right)_{\Gamma(t_{q}^{n})}+\left(D_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}w_{h}\ ,\ \nabla_{\Gamma}r_{h}\right)_{\Gamma(t_{q}^{n})}\right].

(55)

This form was used in
[
30
]
together with a Lagrange multiplier enforcing the correct surfactant mass. We will see in the numerical examples that the two forms (
48
) and (
55
) may not give equivalent discretizations since (
3.1
) does not hold in our discrete setting. However, the proposed discretization which is based on (
48
) results in discrete conservation of the surfactant mass without using a Lagrange multiplier, see equation (
51
).

Remark 5.2

For the proposed method and its implementation the stabilization term (
45
) is vital as it defines an extension of the approximate solution to the entire active space-time domain and hence allows to directly approximate the space-time integrals using quadrature rules. When the integrals in time are approximated by a quadrature rule, geometric computations such as the construction of the interface are done only at the quadrature points in time and space integrals are then computed at each quadrature point in time. We note that other space-time unfitted finite element methods have also been proposed see e.g.
[
31
,
32
,
33
]
. Those formulations are based on reformulating space-time integrals in the weak formulation to surface integrals over the space-time manifold in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
(when
\Gamma
is a surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
). In our formulation we never approximate surface integrals in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
. The implementation of the proposed space-time cut finite element method is straightforward starting from an implementation of CutFEM for stationary domains but relies on the stabilization terms and also on a method for representing and evolving the interface.

Numerical examples

We consider one example in two space dimensions and two examples in three space dimensions and validate that the proposed unfitted finite element method is accurate and conserves the surfactant mass at all time instances
t_{0},t_{1},\ \cdots,t_{N}
. In the examples in this section, the velocity field
\mathbf{u}(t,\mathbf{x})
is explicitly given, we always use a uniform time step size
\Delta t=T/N
, a uniform background mesh of the computational domain, and solve the resulting linear systems with a direct solver. Linear elements in both space and time are used. The proposed method uses the weak formulation (
47
) with
A_{h}^{n}(\mathbf{u},w_{h},r_{h})
as in (
48
) and is refered to as the conservative formulation. When

based on (
55

C_{F,1}
and
C_{\Gamma,1}
are in all examples equal to

(56)

The Finite element method for the two-phase flow system

We are now ready to propose a discretization of the PDEs in Section
3
describing a two-phase flow system with insoluble surfactants.
Starting from the initial condition
\mathbf{u}_{h}^{-}(t_{0},\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{u}^{0}(\mathbf{x})
in
\Omega_{1}(t_{0})\cup\Omega_{2}(t_{0})
and
w_{h}^{-}(t_{0},\mathbf{x})=w^{0}(\mathbf{x})
on
\Gamma(t_{0})
we solve the following variational formulation, where space and time are treated similarly and interface and boundary conditions are imposed weakly, one space-time slab at a time: for
I_{n}
,
n=0,1,\cdots,N-1
given
\mathbf{u}_{h}^{-}(t_{n},\mathbf{x})
and
w_{h}^{-}(t_{n},\mathbf{x})
find
\mathbf{u}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}^{n}_{h}
,
p_{h}\in Q_{h}^{n}
, and
w_{h}\in W_{h}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{u}_{h},p_{h},w_{h})

\displaystyle=B(\mathbf{u}_{h},p_{h},w_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h},q_{h},r_{h})+\int_{I_{n}}c(t,\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h})\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t+\int_{I_{n}}\mathbf{f}_{\Gamma}(t,\sigma(w_{h}),\mathbf{v}_{h})\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+A(\mathbf{u}_{h},w_{h},r_{h})+S(\mathbf{u}_{h},p_{h},w_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h},q_{h},r_{h})-L(\mathbf{v}_{h},q_{h},r_{h})=0,

(58)

for all
\mathbf{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}^{n}_{h}
,
q_{h}\in Q_{h}^{n}
, and
r_{h}\in W_{h}^{n}
.
Here the form
A(\mathbf{u}_{h},w_{h},r_{h})
is defined as in (
44
),

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{\Gamma}(t,\sigma(w_{h}),\mathbf{v}_{h})

\displaystyle=\left(\sigma(w_{h})\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{x}_{\Gamma}\ ,\ \nabla_{\Gamma}\langle\mathbf{v}_{h}\rangle\right)_{\Gamma(t)},

(59)

\displaystyle c(t,\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h},\mathbf{w}_{h})

\displaystyle=\left(\rho(\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{v}_{h})\ ,\ \mathbf{w}_{h}\right)_{\Omega_{1}(t)\cup\Omega_{2}(t)},

and

\displaystyle B(\mathbf{u}_{h},p_{h},w_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h},q_{h},r_{h})

\displaystyle=\int_{I_{n}}\left(\rho\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{h}\ ,\ \mathbf{v}_{h}\right)_{\Omega_{1}(t)\cup\Omega_{2}(t)}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t+\left(\rho\mathbf{u}_{h}\ ,\ \mathbf{v}_{h}\right)_{\Omega_{1}(t_{n})\cup\Omega_{2}(t_{n})}

\displaystyle+\int_{I_{n}}\left[a(t,\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h})-b(t,\mathbf{v}_{h},p_{h})+b(t,\mathbf{u}_{h},q_{h})\right]\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t,

(60)

where the forms
a(t,\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h})
and
b(t,\mathbf{v}_{h},p_{h})
are defined as in equation (
3.1
)-(
20
) with the penalty parameters
\lambda_{\Gamma}
,
\lambda_{\partial\Omega}
, and the weights
k_{1}
,
k_{2}
in the averaging operators (
22
) chosen as in
[
14
]
. Further,

\displaystyle L(v_{h},q_{h},r_{h})=\int_{I_{n}}l(t,\mathbf{v}_{h},q_{h},r_{h})\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t+\left(\rho\mathbf{u}_{h}^{-}\ ,\ \mathbf{v}_{h}\right)_{\Omega_{1}(t_{n})\cup\Omega_{2}(t_{n})}+\left(w^{-}_{h}\ ,\ r_{h}\right)_{\Gamma(t_{n})},

with
l(t,\mathbf{v}_{h},q_{h},r_{h})
defined as in (
21
) and we also have

\displaystyle S(\mathbf{u}_{h},p_{h},w_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h},q_{h},r_{h})=\int_{I_{n}}\left[s_{p}(p_{h},q_{h})+s_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h})+s_{w}(t,w_{h},r_{h})\right]\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t,

where the terms
s_{p}(p_{h},q_{h})
,
s_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h})
, and
s_{w}(t,w_{h},r_{h})
are appropriate stabilization terms. We choose
s_{w}(t,w_{h},r_{h})
as in (
46
) and

\displaystyle s_{p}(p_{h},q_{h})=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h,i}^{n}}C_{p}\mu_{i}^{-1}h^{3}\left(\llbracket\mathbf{n}_{F}\cdot\nabla p_{h,i}\rrbracket_{F}\ ,\ \llbracket\mathbf{n}_{F}\cdot\nabla q_{h,i}\rrbracket_{F}\right)_{F},

(61)

\displaystyle s_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h})=\sum_{l=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h,i}^{n}}\sum_{m=1}^{2}C_{\mathbf{u},m}(\mu_{i},\rho_{i})h^{2m-1}\left(\llbracket D^{m}_{\mathbf{n}_{F}}{\mathbf{u}_{h,i}^{l}}\rrbracket_{F}\ ,\ \llbracket D^{m}_{\mathbf{n}_{F}}{\mathbf{v}_{h,i}^{l}}\rrbracket_{F}\right)_{F}.

(62)

Here
C_{p}
and
C_{\mathbf{u},m}
are positive constants and
\llbracket v\rrbracket_{F}
denotes the jump of a function
v
at the face F and is defined as
\llbracket v\rrbracket_{F}=v^{+}-v^{-}
, where
v^{\pm}=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}v(t,\mathbf{x}\mp t\mathbf{n}_{F})
,
\mathbf{x}\in F
, and
\mathbf{n}_{F}
is a fixed unit normal to
F
. The stabilization terms
s_{p}
and
s_{\mathbf{u}}
are similar as in
[
35
]
but the sets
\mathcal{F}_{h,i}^{n}
are different in the space-time method. Note that for each space-time slab the sets
\mathcal{F}_{h,i}^{n}
,
i=0,1,2
do not depend on
t
, see Figure
2
but the trial and test functions do depend on time. These stabilization terms define an extension and therefore including them in the weak form allows us to formulate our space-time method based on quadrature in time and have a stable and robust discretization independent of how the interface cuts through the background mesh.

Note that from the weak formulation we find functions
p_{h}=(p_{h,1},p_{h,2})\in Q_{h}^{n}
and
\mathbf{u}_{h}=(\mathbf{u}_{h,1},\mathbf{u}_{h,2})\in\mathbf{V}_{h}^{n}
which are double valued in the interface region, see Section
4
. We use
(\mathbf{u}_{h},p_{h})
and define an approximate velocity field
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{h}
and pressure
\tilde{p}_{h}
by:

\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{h}(t,\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}\mathbf{u}_{h,1}&\text{for $\mathbf{x}$ in }\Omega_{1}(t)\\
\mathbf{u}_{h,2}&\text{for $\mathbf{x}$ in }\Omega_{2}(t)\end{cases},\quad\tilde{p}_{h}(t,\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}p_{h,1}&\text{for $\mathbf{x}$ in }\Omega_{1}(t)\\
p_{h,2}&\text{for $\mathbf{x}$ in }\Omega_{2}(t)\end{cases},\quad t\in I_{n}.

(63)

Implementation

The proposed discretization (
6
) leads to a non linear system which we solve using Newton’s method:

-

Choose initial starting guess
(\mathbf{u}_{h,0},p_{h,0},w_{h,0})\in\mathbf{V}^{n}_{h}\times Q_{h}^{n}\times W_{h}^{n}

-

While
\left\|(\delta\mathbf{u},\delta p,\delta w)\right\|>\varepsilon

i.

Solve:
DF(\mathbf{u}_{h,0},p_{h,0},w_{h,0})(\delta\mathbf{u},\delta p,\delta w)=F(\mathbf{u}_{h,0},p_{h,0},w_{h,0})

ii.

Update (
\mathbf{u}_{h,0},p_{h,0},w_{h,0}
) (new guess):
\mathbf{u}_{h,0}=\mathbf{u}_{h,0}-\delta\mathbf{u}
,
p_{h,0}=p_{h,0}-\delta p
and
w_{h,0}=w_{h,0}-\delta w
.

Here
DF(\mathbf{u}_{h},p_{h},w_{h})
is the differential of
F
at
(\mathbf{u}_{h},p_{h},w_{h})
,

\displaystyle DF(\mathbf{u}_{h},p_{h},w_{h})(\delta\mathbf{u},\delta p,\delta w)

\displaystyle=B(\delta\mathbf{u},\delta p,\delta w,\mathbf{v}_{h},q_{h},r_{h})+\int_{I_{n}}\left[c(t,\delta\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h})+c(t,\mathbf{u}_{h},\delta\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}_{h})\right]\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{I_{n}}\mathbf{f}_{\Gamma}(t,\delta w\sigma^{\prime}(w_{h}),\mathbf{v}_{h})\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t+A(\mathbf{u}_{h},\delta w,r_{h})-\int_{I_{n}}\left(w_{h}\ ,\ \delta\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla r_{h}\right)_{\Gamma(t)}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+S(\delta\mathbf{u},\delta p,\delta w,\mathbf{v}_{h},q_{h},r_{h}).

(64)

Since we use the linear equation of state (
17
), we have
\sigma^{\prime}(w_{h})=-\sigma_{0}\beta
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{\Gamma}(t,\delta w\sigma^{\prime}(w_{h}),\mathbf{v}_{h})

\displaystyle=-\sigma_{0}\beta\left(\delta w\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{x}_{\Gamma}\ ,\ \nabla_{\Gamma}\langle\mathbf{v}_{h}\rangle\right)_{\Gamma(t)}.

If for example the Langmuir model is used which is a nonlinear model that describe the relation between the surface tension and the surfactant concentration, i.e.,

\sigma(w_{h})=\sigma_{0}+\beta\ln(w_{\infty}-w),

(65)

where
\sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, and
w_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
are given parameters, we instead have
\sigma^{\prime}(w_{h})=\frac{-\beta}{w_{\infty}-w_{h}}
.

In the first step of the above algorithm we choose the initial guess
(\mathbf{u}_{h,0}(t),p_{h,0}(t),w_{h,0}(t))
to be equal to the solution from the previous space-time slab at time
t_{n}
, i.e.,
(\mathbf{u}_{h}^{-}(t_{n}),p_{h}^{-}(t_{n}),w_{h}^{-}(t_{n}))
. In the assembly of the linear system in step i. we approximate each space-time integral in
F
and
DF
by first using a quadrature rule in time and then at each quadrature point in time the integral in space is computed. We approximate all the integrals in time using Simpson’s quadrature rule. See also Section
5.1.1
.

Here, the exact level set function is not known explicitly except at time
t=0
. We find an approximation
\phi_{h}(t,\mathbf{x})
at each time instance
t\in T^{n}=\{t_{n},\{t_{q}^{n}\},t_{n+1}\}
by discretizing the level set equation (
15
) using the Crank-Nicolson scheme in time and continuous FEM with quadratic Lagrange elements in space and streamline diffusion stabilization following
[
14
]
. As in Section
5.1.1
we find a piecewise planar
\Gamma_{h}(t)
as the zero level set of
\phi_{h}(t,\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{V}_{h}^{1}
, for all
t\in T^{n}
. We then also have approximations
\Omega_{h,1}
and
\Omega_{h,2}
of the two subdomains
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
. The contribution from integration on
\Omega_{h,i}(t_{q}^{n})\cap K
is divided into contributions on one or several triangles in two dimensions and tetrahedra in three dimensions depending on how the interface cuts element
K
.

Conclusions

In this work our focus was on developing an accurate discretization for the surfactant transport equation coupled to the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations. The proposed discretization of the surface PDE and the bulk PDEs utilize the same computational mesh which does not need to conform to the evolving interfaces. We have presented a new space-time weak formulation for the surfactant transport equation which results in discrete conservation of surfactant mass without enforcing the mass with a Lagrange multiplier. In this paper we considered insoluble surfactants but in future work we will also consider soluble surfactants. In the presented scheme the computation of the mean curvature vector is avoided and this simplifies the computations. The presented space-time scheme is based on using quadrature rule in time to approximate the space-time integrals and has a straightforward implementation. We used linear elements in both space and time and a piecewise linear approximation of the interface from an approximate level set function and observed second order convergence. However, if better approximations of the interface is used the proposed space-time CutFEM method can be used with high order elements and result in higher order discretization than second order.

Our scheme relies on an accurate representation and evolution of the interface. We used the level set method which simplified the representation and evolution of the interface in three space dimensions and in the simulations when coalescence occured. However, we note that with our approximate level set function we do not conserve the total area of the drop and in future work we would like to improve on the representation of the interface.
