Toward Theoretical Guidance for Two Common Questions in Practical Cross-Validation based Hyperparameter Selection
\name Parikshit Ram \email p.ram@acm.org

Abstract
We show, to our knowledge, the first theoretical treatments of two common questions in cross-validation based hyperparameter selection: 1 After selecting the best hyperparameter using a held-out set, we train the final model using all of the training data – since this may or may not improve future generalization error, should one do this? 2 During optimization such as via SGD (stochastic gradient descent), we must set the optimization tolerance ρ \rho – since it trades off predictive accuracy with computation cost, how should one set it? Toward these problems, we introduce the hold-in risk (the error due to not using the whole training data), and the model class mis-specification risk (the error due to having chosen the wrong model class) in a theoretical view which is simple, general, and suggests heuristics that can be used when faced with a dataset instance. In proof-of-concept studies in synthetic data where theoretical quantities can be controlled, we show that these heuristics can, respectively, 1 always perform at least as well as always performing retraining or never performing retraining, 2 either improve performance or reduce computational overhead by 2 × 2\times with no loss in predictive performance.

Introduction

The learning process has various sources of errors. The first step in
(supervised) learning is the acquisition of (training) data. Given data, we
choose a model or function class
\mathcal{F}
which corresponds to not just a
method
(such as Support Vector Machines, Generalized Linear Models, Neural
Networks, Decision trees) but their specific configuration governed by their
respective
hyperparameters
(such as regularization forms and penalties,
trees depth) – these hyperparameters refer to anything that would affect the
predictive performance of the model learned from the training data. Given our
choice of the function class, the learning process searches for the function
that (approximately) minimizes the empirical risk (or some surrogate of it which
better represents the true risk or is easier to optimize). We currently have an
understanding of the factors
(
Vapnik 2006
;
Devroye et al. 2013
;
Bottou and Bousquet 2008
)
affecting the
excess risk

of this chosen function – (i) the choice of the function class and its capacity
to model the data generating process, (ii) the use of an empirical risk
estimate
instead of the true risk, and (iii) the approximation in the
empirical risk minimization (ERM).

However, in practice, the learning process is not limited to these steps. A
significant part of the whole exercise is the choice of the function class
\mathcal{F}

(method and its specifications). Usually, we consider a (possibly large) set of
function classes and select one of them based on the data-driven process of

model selection
or
hyperparameter selection
. This search can be
done via grid search (searching over a discretized grid of hyperparameter
values) or random search
(
Bergstra and Bengio 2012
)
. However, AutoML (automated
machine learning) has spurred a lot of research in the area of
hyperparameter optimization
or HPO
(
Hutter et al. 2011
;
Shahriari et al. 2016
;
Snoek et al. 2012
;
Bergstra et al. 2011
;
Bergstra et al. 2013
)
. The
automation allows us to look at even larger sets of function classes for
improved performance while being significantly more efficient than grid search
and more accurate than random search. The problem of HPO has been extended from
machine learning (ML) model configurations to the design of complete ML
pipelines known as the Combined Algorithm Selection and HPO (or CASH) problem,
with various schemes that handle (i) pipelines with fixed
architecture
(
Hutter et al. 2011
;
Bergstra et al. 2011
;
Rakotoarison et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2020
;
Kishimoto et al. 2022
)
, (ii) searching over the pipeline
architectures
(
Katz et al. 2020
;
Marinescu et al. 2021
)
,
(iii) deployment and fairness constraints
(
Liu et al. 2020
;
Ram et al. 2020
)
,
and (iv) operating in the decentralized setting
(
Zhou et al. 2021
;
Zhou et al. 2022
)
, leading to multiple open-source tools
(
Thornton et al. 2012
;
Kotthoff et al. 2017
;
Feurer et al. 2015
;
Feurer et al. 2020
;
Komer et al. 2014
;
Bergstra et al. 2015
;
Baudart et al. 2020
;
Baudart et al. 2021
;
Hirzel et al. 2022
)
.

There has also been a significant amount of theoretical work on development of
data-dependent penalties for penalty-based model selection, resulting in
guarantees in the form of “oracle inequalities” – the expected excess risk of
the selected model can be shown to be within a multiplicative and additive
factor of the best possible excess risk if an oracle provided us with the best
hyperparameter. This has been widely studied in (binary)
classification
(
Boucheron et al. 2005
)
, (bounded) regression and density
estimation
(
Massart 2007
;
Arlot et al. 2010
)
. However, in
practice, penalty-based model selection is not used for data-driven
hyperparameter selection, and we resort to some form of
cross-validation
1
1

1

“cross-validation” (see for example
(
Kearns 1996

Blum et al. 1999
)
)
and when there are multiple folds, it is specifically termed as “

(CV). These universally applicable CV

schemes at the cost of having less data for the learning since some amount of

(

. We focus on these

Our contributions.

While CV based model selection has been studied theoretically, there are various
questions in practical HPO, which have not been explored in literature. A common
practice is the learning of the final model on the selected hyperparameter with
all available data, reintroducing the held-out data in the training. This is
standard practice in many commercial ML tools since user data is too precious to
not include in the training of the deployed model. In this paper, we provide
answers for the following questions
2
2

2

We presented a preliminary version
of this work at the AutoML@ICML’21 workshop
(
Ram et al. 2021
)
.
:

[Q1a]

Theoretically, when does this practice
help in terms of excess risk and by how much?

[Q1b]

Is there a practical data-driven way of
deciding whether to do this or not?

A common practice is to approximate the ERM with a large tolerance during the
HPO and perform a more accurate ERM during the final model training on the
selected HP to reduce the overall computational costs. We study the following
related questions:

[Q2a]

How does this ERM approximation in
hyperparameter selection affect the excess risk?

[Q2b]

Is there a theoretically informed
practical way of setting these approximation levels during the hyperparameter
selection to better control the excess risk vs computation tradeoff?

Note that these answers can be utilized both by humans and by automated data
science systems.

Practical motivations.

In applications with large amount of data, the importance of question

Q1a
(and consequently
Q1b
)
might appear minimal (the computational aspect of questions

Q2a
and
Q2b
make them
critical with large data). However, we believe that even in such cases, the
important signal can still be quite small, and the relevant information in the
held-out set can have large impact (
positive or negative
) on the final
deployed model. For example, in various data science applications in finance,
the class/target of interest is usually very small. In the

Home Credit Default
Risk Kaggle Classification Challenge
, the training data has only around

8.5\%
of some
3\times 10^{5}
training examples from the positive class, and in
the
AllState Claim
Prediction Kaggle Regression Challenge
, the training set has less than
1\%

of some
13\times 10^{6}
examples with nonzero regression targets; rest are zero.
Furthermore, there are situations where the population has groups and the
underrepresented minority groups are significantly smaller than the majority
group(s). In this case, the presence or absence of the held-out data can have
significant (again positive or negative) impact on the fairness and accuracy of
the deployed model since many of the fairness metrics quantify the parity in the
group specific predictive performance.
Finally, “small” differences in predictive performance can have significant
impact depending on problems and applications – in data science leaderboards
such as Kaggle, minor changes in final performance can lead to significant
reordering of the leaderboard (although, relevance of such competitions and
results to practical problems remain an open question).
Such scenarios motivate us to study the question of
whether
the practice
of retraining after reintroducing the data held-out during HPO is helpful
(
Q1a
).

Empirical motivation.

As evidence of the lack of clarity on this
whether
question, we consider
HPO for LightGBM
(
Ke et al. 2017
)
on 40 OpenML
(
Vanschoren et al. 2013
)
data sets
(we detail the evaluation setup in §
2.2
). Of the 400
different HPO problems we solve, retraining has (i) no
significant
effect
(positive or negative) in 51/400 (12.75%) cases, (ii) has a positive effect
(improving test error) in 260/400 (65%) cases, and (iii) has a negative effect
in 89/400 (22.25%) cases. This highlights that
there is no single
universal correct answer to this question
.
Hence, we study this
whether
question rigorously and provide a
theoretically motivated data-driven heuristic as one answer.

Decomposing the excess risk

For a particular method (decision trees, linear models, neural networks), let

\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}
denote the function class for some
fixed
hyperparameter

\lambda\in\Lambda
(tree depth, number of trees for tree ensembles;
regularization parameter for linear and nonlinear models) in the space of valid
hyperparameters (HPs)
\Lambda
. For any model or function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
with
(x,y),x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\mathcal{Y}
generated from a
distribution
P
over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}

\ell:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}

E(f)
and the

with
n

\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim P^{n}

\begin{split}&E(f)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim P}\left[\ell(y,f(x))\right]=\int\ell(y,f(x))dP,\\
&E_{n}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\ell(y,f(x))\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\ell(y_{i},f(x_{i})).\end{split}

(1)

Bayes optimal model
as
f^{\star}

f^{\star}(x)=\argmin_{\hat{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(y,\hat{y})|x\right].

(2)

We denote with the following:

\bar{f}_{\lambda}=\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}}E(f),\qquad\qquad\hat{f}_{n,\lambda}=\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}}E_{n}(f),

(3)

as the
true risk minimizer
and the
empirical risk minimizer
(with

n

\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}
respectively.

When performing ERM over
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}
, the

incurred

\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt app}}}}(\lambda)=E(\bar{f}_{\lambda})-E(f^{\star})
, and
(ii) the

\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt est}}}}(n,\lambda)=E(\hat{f}_{n,\lambda})-E(\bar{f})

\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt app}}}}

Bottou & Bousquet

)
study the tradeoffs in a

\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt app}}}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt est}}}}
terms (as
above). They consider the large scale setting where the ERM needs to be
approximated given the computational budget and discuss the tradeoffs in the
excess risk of an approximate empirical risk minimizer
\tilde{f}_{n,\lambda}\in\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}
. In addition to
\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt app}}}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt est}}}}
, they introduce the

term
\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt opt}}}}
– the excess risk incurred due to approximate
ERM – and argue that, in compute-bound large-scale learning, approximate ERM on
all the samples
n
can achieve better generalization than high precision ERM on
a subsample of size
n^{\prime}\leq n
. Figure
1(a)
provides a visual
representation of this excess risk decomposition.

Given a set
\Lambda

L

\lambda\in\Lambda

\bar{\lambda}=\argmin_{\lambda\in\Lambda}E(\tilde{f}_{n,\lambda})
. However, in practice, with
n

iid

P
, we use cross-validation for model
selection and solve the following

\hat{\lambda}
:

\begin{split}&\hat{\lambda}=\argmin\nolimits_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\,E^{v}_{\mu}(\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\lambda})\qquad\qquad\quad\ \ \ \mbox{\tt(outer)}\\
&\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\lambda}\in\left\{\mathrm{g}:E_{m}(\mathrm{g})\leq E_{m}(\hat{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\lambda})+\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}\right\},\mbox{ \tt(inner)}\end{split}

(4)

where the

problem is an approximate ERM on
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}
for each

\lambda\in\Lambda
with
m<n
samples at an approximation tolerance of

\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}>0
producing
\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\lambda}
(we use
\hat{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\lambda}
to
denote the exact ERM solution in
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}
with
m
samples), and the
outer
problem considers an objective
E^{v}_{\mu}(\cdot)
which is evaluated
using
\mu<n
samples held-out from the ERM in the inner problem – while

E_{m}(\cdot)
and

might have the same form, the

\cdot^{v}
superscript highlights their difference.
Then, a final approximate ERM on
\mathcal{F}_{\hat{\lambda}}
with all
n

\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
tolerance produces

\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}\in\left\{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\hat{\lambda}}:E_{n}(f)\leq E_{n}(\hat{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}})+\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}\right\},

(5)

with
\hat{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}
denoting the exact ERM solution in

\mathcal{F}_{\hat{\lambda}}
.
This embodies the common practice of splitting the samples into a
training

and a

set (of sizes
m,\mu<n
respectively with
m+\mu\leq n
). In

-fold cross-validation
, the

k

\lambda
(on
k

m=n-\nicefrac{{n}}{{k}}

outer
optimization averages the
objectives from
k
held-out sets (of size
\mu=\nicefrac{{n}}{{k}}
) across the

k

training-validation split, and defer
k
-fold CV to future work.

At this point, multiple choices have to be made for computational and
statistical purposes:

\blacktriangleright

The number of samples
m
drives the computational cost of solving the

inner
problem for each
\lambda\in\Lambda
– larger
m
requires
larger compute budget.

\blacktriangleright

The approximation tolerance
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
in the
inner
ERM also drives
the computational cost – smaller
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
requires larger compute budget.

\blacktriangleright

The approximation tolerance
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
in the final ERM over

\mathcal{F}_{\hat{\lambda}}
drives the computational cost similar to
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
but to a
lesser extent since it is only over a single
\hat{\lambda}\in\Lambda
instead
of for each
\lambda\in\Lambda
. For this reason,
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
is usually
selected to be smaller
3
3

3

Often, for computational reasons,
m
might be
much less than
n
, and training the final
\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}
on all

n
samples to a tolerance of
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
might be computationally infeasible,
making
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}>\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
.
than
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
.

\blacktriangleright

The function
\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}
is selected over

\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}}
for statistical reasons since the former gets more

Many of these choices are often made ad hoc or via trial and error. To the best
of our knowledge, there is no mathematically grounded way of making some of
these practical choices. Moreover, it is not clear what is precisely gained by
selecting
\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}
over
\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}}
. Existing
theoretical guarantees for CV based model selection focus on the excess risk of

\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}}
, while in practical HPO,

\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}
is deployed, indicating a gap between theory and
practice. In this paper, we try to bridge this gap, and in the process, provide
a practical heuristic that allows us to select between

\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}}
and
\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}
in a data-driven
manner. Furthermore,
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
and
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
provide a way to control the
computation vs excess risk tradeoff, but it is not clear how to set them to
extract computational gains without significantly increasing the excess risk.
We explicitly highlight the role of
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
and
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
in the excess risk
and provide practical heuristics to select
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
and
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
in a
data-driven manner to better control this tradeoff.

2.1
Novel excess risk decomposition

We first present some intuitive decompositions of the excess risk to understand
the different sources of additional risk (and gains!). After the selection of

\hat{\lambda}
by solving problem (
4
), existing literature
focuses on the excess risk of
\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}}
, yet we are not aware
of any decomposition of its excess risk. We decompose this excess risk as:

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}&=E(\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}})-E(f^{\star})\\
&=\underbrace{E(\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}})-E(\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\bar{\lambda}})}_{\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt mcm}}}}}+\underbrace{E(\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\bar{\lambda}})-E(\hat{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\bar{\lambda}})}_{\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt opt}}}}}+\underbrace{E(\hat{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\bar{\lambda}})-E(\bar{f}_{\bar{\lambda}})}_{\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt est}}}}}+\underbrace{E(\bar{f}_{\bar{\lambda}})-E(f^{\star})}_{\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt app}}}}},\end{split}

(6)

where
\bar{f}_{\bar{\lambda}},\hat{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\bar{\lambda}}
and

\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\bar{\lambda}}
are the true risk minimizer, exact ERM
solution and approximate ERM solution respectively in the function class

\mathcal{F}_{\bar{\lambda}}
corresponding to the oracle HP

\bar{\lambda}
. We introduce a new term
\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt mcm}}}}=E(\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}})-E(\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\bar{\lambda}})
, the
model class mis-specification risk
. Figure
1(b)
visualizes this term
in the excess risk. This term incorporates the excess risk from selecting a
suboptimal HP (and corresponding model class).
However, there is a potential additional excess risk that is often ignored in
literature but is considered crucial in practice – the risk from learning the
model
\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}}
on
m<n
samples instead of all
n
samples,
or the
hold-in risk
, defined as
\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt hin}}}}=E(\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}})-E(\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}})
. This term is visualized in
Figure
1(c)
. This excess risk term does not appear explicitly in the
risk decomposition (
6
) for
\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}}
but
rather is implicitly incorporated in the estimation risk
\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt est}}}}
. However, when
studying the excess risk of
\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}
, the
\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt hin}}}}
does
explicitly appear in the decomposition:

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}&=E(\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}})-E(f^{\star})\\
&=\underbrace{E(\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}})-E(\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}})}_{-\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt hin}}}}}+\underbrace{E(\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}})-E(\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\bar{\lambda}})}_{\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt mcm}}}}}+\underbrace{E(\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\bar{\lambda}})-E(f^{\star})}_{\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt opt}}}}+\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt est}}}}+\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt app}}}}\text{ (see \eqref{eq:decomp-hpo-nmd})}}\end{split}

(7)

This excess risk decomposition for
\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}
is different from
previous decompositions in that the “
-\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt hin}}}}
” term in this excess risk
decomposition is potentially a
risk deficit
instead of an additional risk,
highlighting potential
risk we can recover
from this common practice of
training on all the data with the selected HP
\hat{\lambda}
.
These decompositions are intended to explicitly highlight the different sources
of risk (and gains) in the practical HPO process, providing some
intuition into the problem.

3
Bounding the excess risk

In this section, we bound the excess risks of
\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}}
and

\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}
and try to understand any improvement

\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}
might provide and the interaction with the ERM
approximation tolerances
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
and
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
based on our decompositions. We
have the following result for
\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}}
:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
P
be a distribution over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
and let
\ell:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}
,
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}
, be a
B
-bounded

\beta
-Lipschitz loss. Let
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}
be a class of functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
for any HP
\lambda\in\Lambda
.
Let
\hat{\lambda}
be the solution of (
4
) over the set of
L
HPs

\Lambda
with ERM on
m<n
samples to approximation tolerance
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}\geq 0
, a held-out set of size
\mu<n
, and
m+\mu\leq n
.
With probability at least
1-\delta
for
\delta\in(0,1)
, the excess risk

\mathcal{E}
of
\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}}
in (
6
) is bounded as:

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}&\leq\min\nolimits_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left\{8\,\beta\,\mathcal{R}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{\lambda})+\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt app}}}}(\lambda)\right\}+\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}+2B\,\sqrt{\log(\nicefrac{{2(L+1)}}{{\delta}})}\left(\nicefrac{{2}}{{\sqrt{2m}}}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{2\mu}}}\right),\end{split}

(8)

where
\mathcal{R}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{\lambda})
is the Radamacher complexity of
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}
.

The proof is provided in Appendix
A.1
. The above bound
(
8
) highlights how the different terms affect the
excess risk bounds – we get an excess risk bound within an additive factor of
the class that possesses the minimum combined (scaled) Radamacher complexity (a
proxy for estimation risk
\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt est}}}}

(
Bartlett and Mendelson 2002
)
) and
approximation risk
\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt app}}}}
. Note that the Radamacher complexity is with respect
to
m<n
samples, highlighting the statistical inefficiency introduced by the
held-out data for CV. The result also indicates that a larger held-out set
(larger
\mu
) is preferable. We study this excess risk bound to identify the
effect of the choices in HPO (such as
m,\mu,\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
); the best choice needs
to balance the terms in the bound – making one term, such as
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
much
smaller (say, by an order of magnitude) than the other terms will not improve
the excess risk significantly, but an order of magnitude larger
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
will
have significant ill-effects.
We have the following result for
\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}
:

4
4

4

With further structural assumptions (such as relationships between the
variance and expectations of the functions), we can improve the

\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{\mu}}}
dependence to
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\mu}}
. See for example,
Theorem
A.2
in Appendix
A
. We focus on
Theorem
3.1
since this will subsequently allow us to derive
practical heuristics that we cannot with the structural assumptions required
to get the tighter excess risk bounds.

Theorem 3.2
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
3.1
, let

\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}\in\mathcal{F}_{\hat{\lambda}}
be obtained via approximate ERM
with tolerance
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}\geq 0
over all
n
samples in (
5
). Let

\mathcal{I}_{n,m}^{\hat{\lambda}}(\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}},\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}):=E_{n}(\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}})-E_{n}(\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}})
be the
“empirical risk improvement” from performing the ERM over all
n
samples.
With probability at least
1-\delta
for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, the excess
risk
\mathcal{E}
of
\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}
in (
7
) is bounded
as:

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}\leq&\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left\{8\,\beta\,\mathcal{R}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{\lambda})+\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt app}}}}(\lambda)\right\}+8\,\beta\,\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F}_{\hat{\lambda}})+\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}+B^{\prime}\left(\nicefrac{{2}}{{\sqrt{2n}}}+\nicefrac{{2}}{{\sqrt{2m}}}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{2\mu}}}\right)-\bar{\mathcal{I}}\end{split}

(9)

where
B^{\prime}:=2B\sqrt{\log(\nicefrac{{2(L+2)}}{{\delta}})}
, and

\bar{\mathcal{I}}:=\max\left\{\mathcal{I}_{n,m}^{\hat{\lambda}}(\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}},\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}),\mathcal{I}_{n,m}^{\hat{\lambda}}(0,0)-\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}-(\nicefrac{{\mu}}{{n}})B\right\},

with
\mathcal{I}_{n,m}^{\hat{\lambda}}(0,0)=E_{n}(\hat{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}})-E_{n}(\hat{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}})
denoting the empirical risk improvement if
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}=\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}=0
.

The proof is provided in Appendix
A.2
. This result indicates
that we are only able to recover the hold-in risk
\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt hin}}}}
in terms of the excess
risk if the empirical risk improvement
\mathcal{I}_{n,m}^{\hat{\lambda}}(\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}},\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}})
is relatively significant. While

\mathcal{I}_{n,m}^{\hat{\lambda}}(0,0)
is always
\geq 0
by definition of the
ERM solution
\hat{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}
, the quantity

\mathcal{I}_{n,m}^{\hat{\lambda}}(\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}},\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}})
depends more closely to

\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
and, we should make
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
small enough to extract any gain from
retraining the final
\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}
on all the
n
samples –

\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
should not exceed a critical point where the

\mathcal{I}_{n,m}^{\hat{\lambda}}(\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}},\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}})
term is of the same order as
the
8\beta\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F}_{\hat{\lambda}})
and the

B^{\prime}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{\sqrt{2n}}})
terms. We cannot compute this critical point, but
we will discuss how we can select
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
in a data-driven way. Comparing
Theorems
3.2
to
3.1
allows us to provide a
theoretical answer to
Q1a
.
In HPO, a critical choice is the value of
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
, properly balancing the

and

highlight the roles of these ERM approximation

\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
and
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
, providing an answer for

3.1
Data-driven heuristics

In certain situations,
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
and
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
are specified outside our control
(for example, with models learned with techniques
other than
gradient
descent like decision tree), and hence it is not clear which learned model,

\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}}
or
\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}
, is better to
deploy. For this, we present a heuristic to make a data-driven choice between
the two. In practical scenarios, the approximation risk
\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt app}}}}
usually
dominates the excess risk. Hence, if we assume that
\min_{\lambda}\{8\beta\mathcal{R}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{\lambda})+\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt app}}}}(\lambda)\}
dominates
8\beta\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F}_{\hat{\lambda}})
(the latter does not have
\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt app}}}}
term and
m<n
) then we can compare the excess risk bounds of
\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}}

and
\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}
and utilize the following heuristic (note that

\log 2(L+1)\approx\log 2(L+2)
except from really small
L
), providing a
data-driven answer to
Q1b
:

Heuristic 3.1
.

Based on the quantities in Theorems
3.1
&

3.2

and
\delta>0
, we select
\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}
if

\mathcal{I}_{n,m}^{\hat{\lambda}}>2B\sqrt{2\log(\nicefrac{{2(L+2)}}{{\delta}})/n}
, else we select
\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}}
.

To answer
Q2b
, we need a way to make an informed
choice in terms of
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
and
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
. Intuitively, we can compare
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}

to the other
computable
terms in the bounds (
8
)
and (
9
)
– if
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
is of the order of these terms or larger, the
\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt opt}}}}
risk will
contribute significantly to the excess risk; if
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
is an order of
magnitude smaller that this term, then the
\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt mcm}}}}
risk will dominate the

\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt opt}}}}
and any further reduction in
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
is not beneficial. Based on this
observation, we propose another heuristic for HPO with approximate ERM
to facilitate the choice of
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
in the inner ERM of the bilevel
problem (
4
):

Heuristic 3.2
.

Based on the quantities in Theorem
3.1
and a scaling
parameter
\gamma>0
, we set

\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}=\gamma\,B\sqrt{2\log(\nicefrac{{2(L+1)}}{{\delta}})}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{\sqrt{m}}}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{\mu}}}).

(10)

A larger
\gamma
will imply a more computationally efficient HPO, while a
smaller
\gamma
will improve excess risk up until a point. As we will
demonstrate in our experiments,
\gamma\sim 0.1
is sufficiently
small
5
5

5

This
\gamma
implies that the optimization risk
\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt opt}}}}
is an
order of magnitude smaller than atleast some other term in the excess risk.

such that we still gain computational efficiency via the ERM approximation but
not see any adverse effect on the excess risk of
\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}}
.

While we have a very precise way of setting
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
given the theoretical
result, the choice of
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
is more involved. We present this in
Appendix
B
. Note that the choice of
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
does not play
a significant role in the computational cost of the HPO compared to

\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
since
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
is involved in the training for each
\lambda\in\Lambda
while
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
only influences the final training of
\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}
. For this reason,
if possible
,
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
is chosen to be
significantly smaller than
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
. Heuristics
3.2
and

B.1
(Appendix
B
) are our answers to

Q2b
.

4
Related work

While the use of smaller number of samples
m<n
to select the HP

\hat{\lambda}
is often recognized in practice, and leads to the final model
being learned via ERM on
\mathcal{F}_{\hat{\lambda}}
using all
n
samples,
no
theoretical guarantees exist for this process
. We explicitly study this
situation, introducing the hold-in risk
\mathcal{E}_{\text{{\footnotesize{\tt hin}}}}
, and provide a novel guarantee
for such a procedure in
Theorem
3.2
. Kearns
(
Kearns 1996
)
studies the
interaction between the approximation and estimation risks, and under certain
assumptions and restricted class of functions, proposes ways for selecting the
sizes of the training and held-out splits
m
and
\mu
in an informed
manner. To account for the fact that some of the training data is “wasted” as
the held-out set, Blum et al.
(
Blum et al. 1999
)
propose two different ways
of retaining the Hoeffding bounds of the error estimate on the held-out set
while still being able to utilize the full training data to train models
employed at test time. These techniques are ways of modifying the standard CV.

In addition to the above, there are various theoretical analyses focusing on
various aspects of the CV process such as obtaining tight variance estimates for
the
k
-fold CV score of any given HP
\lambda\in\Lambda

(
Nadeau and Bengio 1999
;
Nadeau and Bengio 2003
;
Rodriguez et al. 2009
;
Markatou et al. 2005
)
. These results are
complementary to ours and could be used to extend our current results (for the
single training/validation split based CV) to
k
-fold CV, with the variance
estimates for the
k
-fold CV metrics involved in the data-driven heuristics. We
will pursue this in future research. However, note that these existing results
do not directly help us obtain tighter excess risk bounds or allow comparison
between models
\tilde{\mathrm{g}}_{m,\hat{\lambda}}
(used during the HPO) and the final
deployed model
\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}
or provide any intuition regarding the
choices for
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
and
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
, which are the main questions
(
Q1a
,
Q1b
,

Q2a
,
Q2b
) we study.

Finally, as we discussed in §
1
, HPO has been widely studied over
the last decade. However, the questions we focus on are complementary to any
specific HPO scheme. We do not focus on how the HP was found (with any specific
HPO scheme such Bayesian Optimization
(
Shahriari et al. 2016
)
), but rather
on (i) the ERM involved in the evaluation of any HP during the HPO, and (ii) the
ERM involved in the final deployment after an HP is selected. Our
Heuristic
3.2
allows us to speed up
any
HPO scheme
without any additional excess risk, and our Heuristics
3.1
and

B.1
allow us to improve the predictive performance of the
deployed model for the HP
\hat{\lambda}
selected via
any
HPO
scheme. “Multi-fidelity” HPO schemes
(
Li et al. 2018
;
Jamieson and Talwalkar 2016
;
Sabharwal et al. 2016
;
Klein et al. 2017
;
Falkner et al. 2018
)
significantly improve
the computational efficiency by adaptively setting either the training set size

m<n
or the optimization approximation
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
on a per-HP basis instead of
using a single value of
m
or
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
. This is
quite different from our proposed Heuristic
3.2
and the
excess risk introduced by this adaptive strategy is not studied to the best of
our knowledge. We wish to extend our tradeoff analysis to multi-fidelity HPO in
future work. Meta-learning is another way of improving the efficiency of the HPO
process
(
Vanschoren 2018
)
, and has been used in some AutoML
toolkits
(
Feurer et al. 2015
;
Feurer et al. 2020
)
. Recently, some theoretical
guarantees have been established for such meta-learning based
HPO
(
Ram 2022
)
, and we also wish to extend our tradeoff analysis
to such meta-learning based HPO. Note that the proposed
Heuristics
3.1
and
B.1
is still beneficial
in both the above situations (multi-fidelity and meta-learning).

5
Conclusions

Our contributions focus on aspects of CV based HPO – we explore how to leverage
the different tradeoffs in the excess risk to make various practical decisions
in the HPO process in a data-driven manner. We use the novel excess risk
decomposition and theoretical analyses to answer the two questions in CV-based
HPO: (1) When is the process of training the model on all the data after the HPO
beneficial and can we choose between the two in a data driven manner?
(
Q1a
,
Q1b
) (2) At what level
should we set the tolerance of the optimization involved in model training
during HPO? (
Q2a
,
Q2b
).
The ideas can be utilized by data science practitioners as well as by automated
data science systems.

\Gamma(\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}):=\mathcal{I}_{n,m}^{\hat{\lambda}}(\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}},\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}})
,

\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}^{(0)}\leftarrow\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
, and
\kappa:=\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}+B\sqrt{2\log(\nicefrac{{2(L+2)}}{{\delta}})}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{\sqrt{n}}}+\nicefrac{{2}}{{\sqrt{m}}}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{\mu}}})
.

This heuristic leverages the fact that the empirical risk improvement

\mathcal{I}_{n,m}^{\hat{\lambda}}(\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}},\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}})
will increase as
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
is
reduced up until a point, and the excess risk of
\tilde{f}_{n,\hat{\lambda}}
is
closely tied to this empirical risk improvement – more improvement implies
better excess risk. Heuristic
B.1
tries to balance any
increase in this empirical risk improvement with the other (computable) terms,
denoted as
\kappa
, in the excess risk bound in (
9
)
– we stop reducing
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
when the increase in the empirical risk
improvement
\Gamma(\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}^{(T-1)})-\Gamma(\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}^{(T)})
is an order of
magnitude below
\kappa
, at which point, other terms dominate the excess
risk. Heuristics
3.2
and
B.1
are our
answers to
Q2b
.

Heuristic
B.1
just presents a way to identify when

\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
is sufficiently small in terms of statistical performance while being
able to gain computationally when we are able to approximate the ERM in an
iterative manner and progressively decrease
\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}
.

\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}=\nicefrac{{\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}}}{{10}}

\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}=\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}

(c)

\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}=2\cdot\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}

(d)

\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt out}}}=10\cdot\rho_{\text{{\footnotesize\tt in}}}
