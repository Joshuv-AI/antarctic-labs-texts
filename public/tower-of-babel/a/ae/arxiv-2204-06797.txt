Abstract
Integrated Nested Laplace Approximations (INLA) has been a successful approximate Bayesian inference framework since its proposal by [ 38 ] . The increased computational efficiency and accuracy when compared with sampling-based methods for Bayesian inference like MCMC methods, are some contributors to its success. Ongoing research in the INLA methodology and implementation thereof in the R package R-INLA , ensures continued relevance for practitioners and improved performance and applicability of INLA. The era of big data and some recent research developments, presents an opportunity to reformulate some aspects of the classic INLA formulation, to achieve even faster inference, improved numerical stability and scalability. The improvement is especially noticeable for data-rich models. We demonstrate the efficiency gains with various examples of data-rich models, like Cox’s proportional hazards model, an item-response theory model, a spatial model including prediction, and a 3-dimensional model for fMRI data.

Introduction

Bayesian inference for latent Gaussian models can be done using ’exact’ MCMC-based
or approximate methods. Integrated Nested Laplace Approximations
(INLA)
[
38
]
is an approximate method that deterministically
performs inference of latent Gaussian models. Latent Gaussian models
include various common and wieldy statistical models including, among
others, generalized linear mixed models (GLMM), generalized additive
mixed models (GAMM), smoothing spline models, spatial and
spatio-temporal models, survival and joint longitudinal-survival
models. The computational efficiency and accuracy of the inference
possible with INLA, act as catalysts for the uptake of the INLA
methodology in the applied sciences communities and motivates
continuous development and improvements of INLA as well as its
implementation through the
R
package
INLA
(R-INLA).

The classic formulation of the INLA methodology as presented by

[
38
]
includes the linear predictors as part of the latent
field. This formulation has various benefits such as automatic
inference of the linear predictors and conditional independence in the
likelihood contributions since each datapoint depends on only one
element in the latent field. Furthermore, it gives a unified framework
to go beyond Gaussian approximations, like using simplified Laplace
approximations which are much more accurate especially for the mean.
On the downside, the latent field gets artificially large with parts that are
almost singular. As an illustration, consider a simple regression model

y_{i}=a+bx_{i}+\epsilon_{i}
, with
n
observed pairs

(x_{i},y_{i})
, then the classic INLA formulation will represent this as
a graph of size
n+2
,
a
and
b
plus the
n
linear predictors,
whereas the modern formulation will use a graph of size
2
to
represent
a
and
b
, as the linear predictors are no longer an
explicit part of the latent field. In most cases, this
does not cause a serious issue, but can be leveraged to show an advantage in
comparisons
[
44
,
34
]
. But as applications are pushing the
boundaries more and more, we needed to reformulate the internal model
representation and how computations and approximations are done. Of
vital importance here, is the new Variational Bayes correction for the
mean as developed in
[
49
]
, that actually allows us to
gain higher accuracy in the mean, with essentially no added cost
in the new model representation. The new model formulation adds a
clear computational gain in data-rich scenarios, where the data is
much larger than the model itself and sometimes of higher dimension.
Additionally, the new formulation gives improved numerical stability
and a better framework for future
developments.

1.1
Latent Gaussian model

Suppose we have response data
\boldsymbol{y}_{n\times 1}
with density
function
\pi(y|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}},\boldsymbol{\theta})
and link function

h(.)
, that is linked to some covariates
\boldsymbol{Z}=\{\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{U}\}

through linear predictors

\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n\times 1}=\beta_{0}\boldsymbol{1}+\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{X}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}f^{k}(\boldsymbol{u}_{k})

(1)

such that
\{\boldsymbol{f}\}
are unknown functions of
\boldsymbol{U}
and

\boldsymbol{\beta}
is the coefficients for the linear effects of
\boldsymbol{X}
on

\boldsymbol{\eta}
. The inferential aim is to estimate

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{m\times 1}=\{\beta_{0},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{f}\}
,
which is the set of unobserved latent variables, or the latent field,
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, the
p
-variate vector of hyperparameters. In the case where a
Gaussian prior is assumed for
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}
, the hierarchical
statistical model in (
1
) is a latent Gaussian model (LGM).

Latent Gaussian models are a broad class of models such as Gaussian
processes for time series, spatial and spatio-temporal data or
clustered random effects models for data with a grouping structure.
The formulation given in (
1
) can be seen as any model
where each one of the
f^{k}(.)
terms can be written in matrix form
as
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\boldsymbol{u}_{k}
. But it is still not exhaustive as
volatility models can also be fitted with INLA, as in

[
28
]
and
[
4
]
.

The latent Gaussian model is the hierarchical Bayesian model defined
as follows,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\pi(y_{i}|\mathcal{X}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle N(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{Q}_{\pi}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}))

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}
contains the hyperparameters from the likelihood
(like shape or precision parameters) and the latent prior (like
precision or weight parameters), and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is allowed to assume
any (reasonable) prior. For inference we then need the following
marginal posteriors,

\pi(\theta_{j}|\boldsymbol{y})\quad\text{and}\quad\pi(\mathcal{X}_{j}|\boldsymbol{y})

1.2
Classic model formulation of INLA

We will now review the classic model formulation of
[
38
]
.
The linear predictors,
\boldsymbol{\eta}
, are now included in an augmented
latent field

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}=\{\boldsymbol{\eta},\beta_{0},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{f}\},

that includes the linear predictors as the first
n
elements of

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}
and the
m
model components as defined in
(
1
). This augmentation results in a singular covariance
matrix of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}
since
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is a deterministic
function of the other elements of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}
. To circumvent
the singularities, we redefine

\boldsymbol{\eta}=\beta_{0}\boldsymbol{1}+\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{X}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}f^{k}(\boldsymbol{u}_{k})+\boldsymbol{\epsilon},

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim N(\boldsymbol{0},\tau^{-1}\boldsymbol{I})
with large fixed

\tau
. We thus include a tiny noise term to the linear predictors to
move from a singular covariance matrix of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}
to a
non-singular covariance matrix. With this formulation, each datapoint
depends on only one element
of the latent field.

Now we can write the joint density of the latent field,
hyperparameters and the data as

\displaystyle\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})\propto\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\pi(y_{i}|\mathcal{X}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta})

(4)

From here the posterior of the hyperparameters can be approximated as

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})}{\pi_{G}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})}\right|_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})},

(5)

where
\pi_{G}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})
is a Gaussian
approximation to the true
\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})

using the Laplace method
[
47
]
by matching the mode
and the curvature around the mode of

\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})
. Next the posterior
marginals can be approximated by

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}(\mathcal{X}_{j}|\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\tilde{\pi}(\mathcal{X}_{j}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})d\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}(\theta_{j}|\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})d\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}.

The approximation
\tilde{\pi}(\mathcal{X}_{j}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})
can
be obtained in two ways, either from the Gaussian approximation

\pi_{G}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})
in
(
5
), or from another Laplace approximation step as
follows:

\tilde{\pi}(\mathcal{X}_{j}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})\propto\left.\frac{\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})}{\pi_{G}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{-j}|\mathcal{X}_{j},\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})}\right|_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{-j}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{-j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})},

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{-j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the mode of

\pi_{G}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{-j}|\mathcal{X}_{j},\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})
.
The Gaussian strategy is computationally cost effective but in the
case of non-Gaussian likelihoods can be quite inaccurate, whereas the
extra Laplace approximation step (the nested one) is computationally
more expensive but provides sufficiently accurate inference. The
unified treatment of model components and the linear predictors, allow
us to compute the simplified Laplace approximation (see
[
38
]
for details), say, for all
components, in a unified way.

1.3
Proposal

In many cases, the augmentation of the latent field by the linear
predictors does not increase the computational cost of the inference
by much, but for low-dimensional models with high-dimensional data,
the nested Laplace approximation gets more computationally expensive, mainly
due to this augmentation. As noted, initially the linear predictors
were included in the latent field definition to perform inference in a
unified way. The other options at the time were to not include them
and then calculate their posteriors, post-inference of the latent
field. In the case of the posterior Gaussian assumption for the latent
field and linear predictors this approach is cost effective but can be
quite inaccurate since the tiny errors compound in the post-inference
framework. Another option is to construct skew-normal marginals with a
Gaussian copula for the linear predictors from the inference of the
latent field. This option is computationally expensive but more
accurate and essentially negates the computational benefit of removing
the linear predictors from the latent field.

Recent developments by
[
49
]
enables us to define a new
framework for approximate Bayesian inference using INLA without the
linear predictor in the latent field formulation, defined as an extended latent
Gaussian model by
[
44
]
. The low-rank
Variational Bayes correction to the posterior means of a Gaussian
latent field proposed by
[
49
]
can now be used to
efficiently correct the posterior means of the latent field,
post-inference of the latent field, and then obtain the marginal
posteriors of the linear predictors from integrating the Gaussian
conditional marginals. Without this efficient correction, the results
might be inaccurate. We present the proposed methodology in
Section
2
and illustrate this new approach with
examples in Section
3
and Section
4
.

2
Methodology

In this section we propose the modern formulation of the INLA
methodology, and some details on the implementation thereof to attain
computational efficiency and sufficiently accurate inference.

2.1
Modern model definition and INLA

The main enhancement of the classic formulation is that we do not
augment the latent field with the “noisy" linear predictors. The
latent field of dimension
m
is defined as

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}=\{\beta_{0},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{f}\},

with Gaussian prior

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim N(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{Q}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))
,
and the
n
linear predictors are defined as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}},

(7)

with
\boldsymbol{A}
a sparse design matrix that links the linear predictors
to the latent field.

From this formulation the joint density of the latent field,
hyperparameters and the data is derived as

\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})\propto\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\pi(y_{i}|(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}})_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}).

(8)

With this formulation the first step is to find the mode and the
Hessian at the mode of
\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})
defined as
follows:

\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})=\left.\frac{\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})}{\pi_{G}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})}\right|_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}.

(9)

The Gaussian approximation

\pi_{G}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})
to

\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})
is calculated from a
second order expansion of the likelihood around the mode of

\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
as
follows

\displaystyle\log\left(\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})\right)

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(b_{i}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}})_{i}-\frac{1}{2}c_{i}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}})^{2}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{A}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}-\boldsymbol{b}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}

where
\boldsymbol{b}
is an
n
-dimensional vector with entries
\{b_{i}\}
and

\boldsymbol{D}
is a diagonal matrix with
n
entries
\{c_{i}\}
. Note that
both
\boldsymbol{b}
and
\boldsymbol{D}
depend on
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, so the Gaussian
approximation is for a fixed
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. The process is iterated to
find
\boldsymbol{b}
and
\boldsymbol{D}
that gives the Gaussian approximation
at the mode,
\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, so that

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y}\sim N\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\boldsymbol{Q}^{-1}_{\mathcal{X}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right).

(10)

Note that with the modern formulation, the graph of the Gaussian
approximation consists of two components,

1.

\mathcal{G}_{p}
: the graph obtained from the prior of the latent
field through
\boldsymbol{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

2.

\mathcal{G}_{d}
: the graph obtained from the data based on the
non-zero entries of
\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}

Next, the marginal conditional posteriors of the elements of

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}
is calculated from the joint Gaussian
approximation in (
9
) as

\mathcal{X}_{j}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y}\sim N\left(\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)_{j},\left(\boldsymbol{Q}^{-1}_{\mathcal{X}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)_{jj}\right).

(11)

To get the marginal posterior of
\mathcal{X}_{j}
we need to
(numerically) integrate
\boldsymbol{\theta}
out from (
11
)
using
K
integration points
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}
and area weights

\delta_{k}
defined by some numerical integration scheme

\tilde{\pi}(\mathcal{X}_{j}|\boldsymbol{y})=\int\pi_{G}(\mathcal{X}_{j}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})d\boldsymbol{\theta}\approx\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{G}(\mathcal{X}_{j}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k},\boldsymbol{y})\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}|\boldsymbol{y})\delta_{k}.

(12)

Even though we propose a Gaussian for the conditional marginal
posterior of
\mathcal{X}_{j}
in (
11
), the marginal
posterior of
\mathcal{X}_{j}
will be skewed due to the integration as
in (
12
).

2.2
Marginal posteriors of the linear predictors

\tilde{\pi}(\eta_{i}|\boldsymbol{y})

In the classic formulation, the marginal posteriors of the linear
predictors are automatically calculated since they constitute the
first
n
elements of the latent field. With the new formulation as
set out in Section
2.1
, we need to calculate the
marginal posteriors of the linear predictors after we calculated

\tilde{\pi}(\mathcal{X}_{j}|\boldsymbol{y})
and
\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})
,
and we need to do this in an efficient way if we want computational
benefit compared to the classic INLA framework.

In order to calculate
\tilde{\pi}(\eta_{i}|\boldsymbol{y})
, we first calculate

\tilde{\pi}(\eta_{i}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})
. We postulate a Gaussian density
for
\eta_{i}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y}
such that

\tilde{\pi}(\eta_{i}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})=\pi_{G}(\eta_{i}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})
, with mean

E(\boldsymbol{\eta}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})=\boldsymbol{A}E(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

and covariance matrix

\text{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\eta}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})=\boldsymbol{A}\text{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})\boldsymbol{A}^{\top},

(13)

from (
11
), and since

\boldsymbol{\eta}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}.

Note that
\boldsymbol{A}
is a sparse matrix so the computations are
efficient. However, in the Gaussian approximation to

\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})
we calculate the precision
matrix
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\mathcal{X}}
, but to use (
13
) we
need the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{Q}^{-1}_{\mathcal{X}}
, which is very
expensive to calculate and store. Conversely, for the marginal conditional
posterior of
\eta_{i}
, we only need the
i^{\text{th}}
diagonal element
of

\boldsymbol{A}\text{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}
.

Define the sparse selected inverse of a symmetric sparse matrix

\boldsymbol{Q}
with graph
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{X}}
as the symmetric matrix

\boldsymbol{C}
with elements

\{C_{il}:i\sim_{\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{X}}}l\text{ or }i=l\}
. The
elements of the sparse selected inverse is computed from the Cholesky
decomposition of
\boldsymbol{Q}=\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{L}^{\top}
as follows (see

[
46
]
and
[
38
]
for details)

C_{ij}=\frac{\delta_{ij}}{L_{ii}^{2}}-\frac{1}{L_{ii}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k>i\\
L_{ki}\neq 0\end{subarray}}L_{ki}C_{kj},

where
\delta_{ij}=1
for
i=j
and zero otherwise. This way we only
calculate the necessary elements of the inverse and using only the
non-zero elements of
\boldsymbol{L}
provided
\boldsymbol{Q}
has been properly
reordered. Now let
\boldsymbol{C}
be a sparse selected inverse of

\boldsymbol{Q}_{\mathcal{X}}=\boldsymbol{Q}+\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{A}
based on the
graph
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{X}}=\{\mathcal{G}_{p},\mathcal{G}_{d}\}
,
then

\text{Var}(\eta_{j}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y})=(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top})_{jj}=\sum_{il}\boldsymbol{A}_{ji}C_{il}(\boldsymbol{A}^{\top})_{lj}=\sum_{il}A_{ji}A_{jl}C_{il}.

(14)

Note that in
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\mathcal{X}}
we get elements from

\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{A}
whereas for the variance of the linear
predictors we need the diagonal of
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}
. Since

\boldsymbol{Q}_{\mathcal{X}}
is based on graph
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{X}}
we
know that for
i\sim_{\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{X}}}j
, we have

\left(\boldsymbol{Q}_{\mathcal{X}}\right)_{ij}=\sum_{l}(\boldsymbol{A}^{\top})_{ij}D_{il}A_{jl}=\sum_{l}A_{ji}D_{il}A_{jl}

with
A_{ji}A_{jl}\neq 0
for at least one
j
since

i\sim_{\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{X}}}l
based on
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{X}}
.
Also, in (
14
) we then know that if

i\sim_{\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{X}}}l
then we must have

A_{ji}A_{jl}\neq 0
. This ensures a sensible calculation of the
variances defined in (
14
).

Now that we can calculate the mean and the variance of the conditional
posterior of
\eta_{j}
we can calculate the marginal posterior of

\eta_{j}
using (
12
) as follows:

\displaystyle\eta_{j}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle N(\mu_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\sigma^{2}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))

\displaystyle\mu_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))_{j}

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{il}A_{ji}A_{jl}C_{il}

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}(\eta_{j}|\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{G}(\mathcal{\eta}_{j}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k},\boldsymbol{y})\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}|\boldsymbol{y})\delta_{k},

(15)

so that
\text{E}(\eta_{j}|\boldsymbol{y})=\mu_{j}
and

\text{Var}(\eta_{j}|\boldsymbol{y})=\sigma_{j}^{2}
.

2.3
Low-rank correction using Variational Bayes for increased
accuracy

The posterior means of
\boldsymbol{\eta}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}
might be inaccurate based on
the Gaussian assumption of the conditional posterior, especially in
the case of a non-Gaussian likelihood. The recent proposed Variational
Bayes correction to Gaussian means by
[
49
]
, can be used
to efficiently calculate an improved mean for the marginal posteriors
of the linear predictors, by improving the posterior means of the
latent field.

[
51
]
showed that Bayes’ rule is a
100\%
efficient information processing rule. Based on this work, we can solve for the posterior based on the following variational function,

E_{q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{y})}\left[-\log l(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{y})\right]+\text{KLD}\left[q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{y})||\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}})\right]

where
q(.)
is a member of the variational class,
\pi(.)
is the prior and
l(.)
is the likelihood function. Instead of using this method to calculate the posterior of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{\theta}
, we rather use the variational framework to calculate a posterior correction to the mean from the Gaussian approximation at the mode as in (
10
) and (
11
). There are other ways to find element-wise corrections to the elements of the Gaussian mean, but with element-wise changes there is no guarantee that the overall effects would be an improvement. With this variational formulation on the joint mean, we are sure of a joint improvement. Also, this formulation enables a low-rank correction where influential elements are corrected and the effect of these corrections are then propagated to the other elements such that the corrections imply an improved joint mean. We present the details of this approach next.

From Section
2.1
, (
10
) a mean,

\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\theta}
is
calculated in the Gaussian approximation at the mode of

\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})
. Using a Variational
framework we can improve on this mean as proposed by

[
49
]
. To this end, we formulate a low-rank variational
Bayes correction of size
p<m
to
\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, such that the
improved mean is

\boldsymbol{\mu}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{\lambda},

(16)

where
\boldsymbol{M}
is a matrix that propagates the correction made to
p

nodes to the rest of the latent field. The propagation matrix
\boldsymbol{M}

is derived from selected columns of

\boldsymbol{Q}_{\mathcal{X}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
(corresponding to the
p

nodes of explicit correction) as presented by
[
49
]
. The
explicit corrections
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, are solved for iteratively using
the variational function

\arg_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\min\left(E_{\mathcal{X}|\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim N(\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{Q}^{-1}_{\mathcal{X}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))}\left[-\log l(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{y})\right]+\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)\right),

where

\log l(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{y})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\pi(y_{i}|(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}})_{i})

The expected log-likelihood is calculated using
Gauss-Hermite quadrature with
n_{J}
nodes
\boldsymbol{x}
and
n_{J}

weights
\boldsymbol{w}
, as follows:

E_{\mathcal{X}|\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim N(\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{Q}^{-1}_{\mathcal{X}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))}\left[-\log l(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{y})\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{J}}w_{j}\log\pi[y_{i}|(\sigma_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})x_{j}+\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))],

where

\boldsymbol{Q}_{\mathcal{X}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{L}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
from a Cholesky decomposition,
\sigma_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is from (
15
) and

\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))_{i}
. Then a second-order
Taylor series expansion around
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\boldsymbol{0}
is calculated
and used to approximate

E_{\mathcal{X}|\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim N(\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{Q}^{-1}_{\mathcal{X}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))}\left[-\log l(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}|\boldsymbol{y})\right]

2.4
Other developments used in INLA

To find the mode of
\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})
we need to
perform unconstrained optimization. Based on the work of

[
1
]
we use the smart gradient approach to more
accurately and efficiently calculate the gradient, and hence find the
mode and the Hessian at the mode more accurately.

Numerical methods for estimating the gradient such as the forward
difference, backward or central finite difference methods
traditionally use the canonical basis. Instead of using the canonical
basis
\boldsymbol{e}
, we construct a new orthonormal basis using the
previous descent directions and the Modified Gram-Schmidt (MGS)
orthogonalization. Suppose
\tilde{\nabla}f
is the numerical gradient
of the gradient
\nabla f
of

f(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})
, and the dimension of

\boldsymbol{\theta}
is
q
. Now, at iteration
k
, we use the previous
q

descent directions and construct new directions as follows:

\boldsymbol{d}^{(k)}=\frac{\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}}{||\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}||}

Then we subtract those directions already included, through
projections, as to get a unique contribution for each direction. From
these projected directions we can construct an orthonormal basis using
MGS, contained in the columns of
\boldsymbol{G}^{(k)}
, that spans the same
space as the original direction vectors, and the estimated gradient at
iteration
k
can then be calculated as follows:

\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{d}}f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)})={\boldsymbol{G}^{(k)}}^{-\top}\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{e}}h^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\psi}),\quad h^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\psi})=f(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}+\boldsymbol{G}^{(k)}\boldsymbol{\psi}).

This smart gradient framework enables a more accurate and expedited
location of the mode of
\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})
.

For high performance computing environments, INLA has been parallelized using the
OpenMP
and
PARDISO
libraries.
PARDISO
is a state-of-the-art library containing parallelized versions of efficient sparse linear algebra solvers that is employed at all stages of the INLA algorithm. Another level of parallelism is introduced for calculating
\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})
at multiple
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, and finding the mode of
\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})
through a parallel line search algorithm. This multi-level parallelism has been shown to lead to speed-up factors of up to
10
for large models. This development allows the user to efficiently employ the INLA methodology in multi-core architectures. For more details see
[
13
]
.

5
Discussion

We present here a modern formulation of the INLA methodology, based on the reformulation of the latent field by removing the linear predictors from the model. This new framework stems from computational demands set by a certain class of models where the data is large and the model is moderate or small, termed data-rich models. The classic formulation of INLA has paved the way for the many developments within the methodology hitherto, but the need for the modern formulation became clear for the future development and continuing applicability of INLA. Applications of data-rich models using INLA has not been attainable with the classic formulation due to the synthetic enlargement of the latent model size by the linear predictors being included as part of the model components. Now, with the modern formulation, INLA can be used for full Bayesian inference of data-rich models such as spatio-temporal models for fMRI data.

The modern formulation, however, became possible only subsequent to the work of
[
49
]
. Since the linear predictors are removed from the model, the Bayesian inference thereof should now be done after the inference of the model is complete. This endeavor is not computationally efficient, unless a Gaussian posterior is assumed for the latent field model, conditional on the hyperparameters. But, the Gaussian assumption might lead to inaccuracies in the estimates which could compound to the linear predictors. A low-rank joint correction to the mean of the latent field, proposed by
[
49
]
, achieves accuracy on par with ’exact’ MCMC methods while being computationally efficient with essentially no additional cost. Without this efficient correction, the computational cost associated with the inference of the linear predictors would negate the benefit of removing them from the model. The modern formulation is currently
implemented in R-INLA by the command

Ψinla(..., inla.mode = "experimental")

and will be enabled by default in the near future.

The advantage of the proposed work is not only computational but it forms the basis of future developments in INLA, for efficient and accurate Bayesian inference of latent Gaussian models. The ideas presented can be used in other ways, like a joint variance and/or skewness correction for the Gaussian assumption, which are currently work in progress within the team. More efficient and accurate hyperparameter marginals are also of current and future interest within the modern framework. For the end user, we believe this work contributes to the toolbox of many
applied statisticians and scientists, who can now perform Bayesian
inference and stable prediction, also for data-rich models, in a computationally efficient and
accurate manner.
