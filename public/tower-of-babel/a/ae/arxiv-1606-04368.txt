Severi–Brauer varieties; a geometric treatment
János Kollár

Abstract
. We present a geometric treatment of Severi–Brauer varieties, without using any results from the theory of central simple algebras or Galois cohomology.



August 7, 2026

The story of Severi–Brauer varieties ties together three—seemingly unrelated—strands.

\bullet

Central simple algebras
are finite dimensional, simple algebras, whose center is a given field
k
. The first example is the quaternions, discovered by Hamilton in 1843. Wedderburn’s
contributions started in 1905, and the subject grew into its own around
1925 with the works of Albert, Brauer, Hasse and Noether.
See
[
vdW91
, Chap.13]
,
[
Jac80
, Chap.4]
,

[
Lan02
, Chap.XVII]
,
[
GSz06
]

or
[
Sta22
, Chap.073W]

for introductions.

\bullet

Severi–Brauer varieties
are varieties defined over a field
k
, that become isomorphic to projective space over the algebraic closure
\bar{k}
. Plane conics without
k
-points give the ‘baby examples,’ though they were usually viewed as part of the theory of quadratic forms.
Higher dimensional Severi–Brauer varieties
were first defined and studied by Severi
[
Sev1932
]
, and their theory came into full maturity starting with Châtelet
[
Châ44
]
, who used both Galois cohomology and central simple algebras.
For a variety
X
,
maps to non-trivial Severi–Brauer varieties give the best understood obstruction to having a
k
-point. Thus Severi–Brauer varieties appear especially frequently in arithmetic questions, see
[
CTS21
]
.

\bullet

H^{2}(k,{\mathbb{G}}_{m})
, the second Galois cohomology group of the multiplicative group
{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
. This is the most powerful viewpoint

[
GSz06
]
.

The aim of these notes is to present a geometric treatment of Severi–Brauer varieties, without using any results from the theory of central simple algebras or Galois cohomology. This reverses the
treatments prevalent since
[
Châ44
]
, which start either with
central simple algebras or with Galois cohomology, and proceed to derive geometric properties.

A series of
Asides
outline either
connections to the other approaches to Severi–Brauer varieties, or discuss connections with other topics.
These are not needed for the proofs, but provide motivation and hints for those who intend to go deeper into some of these topics.

Introductions to the algebraic and cohomological methods are in

[
GSz06
]
and
[
Mil80
, Chap.IV]
.
The arithmetic aspects are treated in
[
CTS21
]
.
Connections with algebraic geometry are discussed in
[
Gro68
,
AHVAV17
]
, while

[
KO81
,
Ver82
]
are more algebraic.

The main idea of our treatment is the following.

Assertion 1
.

The geometry of a Severi–Brauer variety
P
is best studied via the vector bundle

F(P)
which is obtained as the unique (up-to isomorphism) non-split extension

[formula]

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{P}\to F(P)\to T_{P}\to 0,

where
T_{P}
denotes the tangent bundle of
P
.

The vector bundle
F(P)
appears in Quillen’s work
[
Qui73
, §8.4]

(with a different construction), a similar bundle is used in
an unpublished preprint of Szabó

[
Sza06
]
(which also contains the first steps of a program to treat
Severi–Brauer varieties geometrically), and is a special case of the
absolutely split bundles studied by
Biswas–Nagaraj
[
BN07
]
and
Novaković
[
Nov24
]
.

: it is a direct sum of copies of
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
over

We show in Section

acts transitively on flags of twisted linear subvareties.

Twisted linear systems are studied in Section
6
, leading to a geometric definition of the Brauer group in Section
7
.
The index and the period of Severi–Brauer varieties is studied in Section
8
.

In Section
9
we construct
cyclic Severi–Brauer varieties from hypersurfaces
of degree
n+1
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}
and norm forms.

In Section

the sequence (

.1) for Severi–Brauer schemes.

1.
Geometrically split vector bundles

In this section we study abstract properties of the vector bundle
F(P)
defined in (
1
.1). Its key feature is that, while

F(P)
is usually indecomposable over the ground field
k
, it is a direct sum of several copies of a line bundle over
\bar{k}
.
Our main interest is in smooth projective varieties, but
the basic results in this section hold in the following setting.

Definition 2
.

Let
k
be a field, and
\bar{k}
a fixed algebraic closure of
k
.
Let
X
be a proper
k
-scheme that is geometrically reduced and geometrically connected. (That is,
X_{\bar{k}}
is reduced and connected.)

The basic results all work if
X
is not proper, as long as
H^{0}(X,{\mathcal{O}}_{X})\cong k
.

Given
a line bundle
{\mathcal{L}}
on
X_{\bar{k}}
,
let
T({\mathcal{L}})
denote the category of vector bundles
F
on
X
that

F_{\bar{k}}\cong\oplus_{i}{\mathcal{L}}
; a sum of copies of
{\mathcal{L}}
.

{\mathcal{L}}
is called a
twisted line bundle
on
X
if

T({\mathcal{L}})
contains a
nonzero vector bundle.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}_{i}
be twisted line bundles on
X
and

0\neq F_{i}\in T({\mathcal{L}}_{i})
.
Then
F_{1}\otimes F_{2}\in T({\mathcal{L}}_{1}\otimes{\mathcal{L}}_{2})
,
thus all twisted line bundles on
X
form a group

\operatorname{Pic}^{\rm tw}(X)
.

(
Aside.
We note in (
7
.4) that
\operatorname{Pic}^{\rm tw}(X)=\operatorname{\mathbf{Pic}}(X)(k)
, the
k
-points of the Picard scheme of
X
.)

A line bundle
L
on
X
is naturally identified with the
twisted line bundle
{\mathcal{L}}:=L_{\bar{k}}
.
We see in (
8
) that in this case

T({\mathcal{L}})
consists of sums of copies of
L
.
Conversely, if
T({\mathcal{L}})
contains a rank 1 bundle
L
, then

{\mathcal{L}}\cong L_{\bar{k}}
.
Thus there is a natural injection

[formula]

\operatorname{Pic}(X)\lhook\joinrel\to\operatorname{Pic}^{\rm tw}(X).

If
M
is a line bundle on
X
, then
T({\mathcal{L}}\otimes M_{\bar{k}})=T({\mathcal{L}})\otimes M
.
Thus twisted line bundles really study the quotient

\operatorname{Pic}^{\rm tw}(X)/\operatorname{Pic}(X)
.

Technically it is better to work with a separable algebraic closure
k^{s}
instead of an algebraic closure
\bar{k}
. (Note that
\bar{k}=k^{s}
if
\operatorname{char}k=0
, or if

\operatorname{char}k>0
and
k
is perfect.)
We prove in (
35
)
that the two versions are equivalent.

Our definition of twisted line bundles is related to, but quite different from,
the notion of twisted coherent sheaves studied in
[
Yos06
,
Lie07
]
.

The simplest example of a twisted line bundle is
{\mathcal{O}}_{C}(1)
of a plane conic.

Baby Example 3
.

Let
C
be a
k
-variety such that
C_{\bar{k}}\cong{\mathbb{P}}^{1}_{\bar{k}}
.
Then
{\mathcal{O}}_{C}(-K_{C})
is very ample and gives an embedding

C\lhook\joinrel\to{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{k}
whose image is a conic; see
[
KSC04
, Secs.1.2–3]
for details.

Assume that
C
has no
k
-points.
(For example, if
k\subset{\mathbb{R}}
then we can take

C=(x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=0)
.)

Then
C
has no degree 1 line bundles defined over
k
(since the zero set of any section would be a
k
-point), but we have

{\mathcal{O}}_{C}
and
{\mathcal{O}}_{C}(2)={\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(1)|_{C}
. Furthermore,

H^{1}\bigl(C,{\mathcal{O}}_{C}(-2)\bigr)\cong k
, thus we have a non-split extension

[formula]

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{C}\to F(C)\to{\mathcal{O}}_{C}(2)\to 0.

We know that, over
\bar{k}
,
F(C)
is isomorphic to

{\mathcal{O}}_{C_{\bar{k}}}(1)+{\mathcal{O}}_{C_{\bar{k}}}(1)
, but
F(C)
itself is an indecomposable vector bundle over
k
. Thus
F(C)\in T({\mathcal{L}})

for
{\mathcal{L}}:={\mathcal{O}}_{C_{\bar{k}}}(1)
.
We see next that it is better to think of (
3
.1) as

[formula]

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{C}\to F(C)\to T_{C}\to 0.

Definition 4

(Severi–Brauer variety)
.

A
Severi–Brauer variety
over a field
k
is a
variety
P
such that
P_{\bar{k}}\cong{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{\bar{k}}
, where
\bar{k}
is an algebraic closure of
k
, and
n=\dim P
.

We see in (
35
) that this is equivalent
to
P_{k^{s}}\cong{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k^{s}}
where
k^{s}
is a separable algebraic closure of
k
.

It is not easy to write down explicit Severi–Brauer varieties of dimension
n\geq 2
or even to show that they exist; see Sections
2
and
9
for some examples.

More generally,
let
K/k
be a field extension and
X
a
K
-scheme.
A
k
-scheme
Y
is called a
k
-form of
X
if
X\cong Y\times_{\operatorname{Spec}k}\operatorname{Spec}K
; we abbreviate this as
X\cong Y_{K}
.
See
[
Ser59
, Sec.V.20]
,

[
Ser88
, Sec.V.20]
or
[
GW10
, pp.456-463]

for general discussions.

Thus
n
-dimensional Severi–Brauer varieties over
k
are the
k
-forms of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{\bar{k}}
.

Main Example 5

(
{\mathcal{O}}_{P}(1)
of a Severi–Brauer variety)
.

Let
P
be a Severi–Brauer variety over a field
k
.

As in the 1-dimensional case,
{\mathcal{O}}_{P}(-K_{P})
is very ample and gives an embedding

P\lhook\joinrel\to{\mathbb{P}}^{N}_{k}
where
N=\binom{2n+1}{n}-1
, but this is very hard to use since the codimension of
P
is large.

The tangent bundle of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
can be presented using the Euler sequence

[formula]

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e}}{{\to}}\oplus_{i=0}^{n}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\partial}}{{\to}}T_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}\to 0;

see
[
EH16
, 3.2.4]
or

[
Har77
, II.8.13]
.

Note that the sequence (
5
.1) is determined by
its nonzero extension class

[formula]

\eta\in\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(T_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}})=H^{1}({{\mathbb{P}}^{n}},\Omega_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}^{1})\cong k.

Thus, for any Severi–Brauer variety
P
,

[formula]

\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(T_{P},{\mathcal{O}}_{P})=H^{1}(P,\Omega_{P}^{1})\cong k.

(It is not important for now, but in (
83
) we write down a
canonical isomorphism
H^{1}(P,\Omega_{P}^{1})\cong k
.)

We can thus define a vector bundle
F(P)
as the unique (up-to scaling) non-split extension

[formula]

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{P}\to F(P)\to T_{P}\to 0.

Note that
F(P)_{\bar{k}}\cong\oplus_{i=0}^{n}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(1)
, thus

F(P)\in T({\mathcal{L}})
where
{\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{\bar{k}}}(1)
.

The Picard group of

{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
is
{\mathbb{Z}}[{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(1)]\cong{\mathbb{Z}}
, but, as in the baby example,
there is usually no line bundle
{\mathcal{O}}_{P}(1)
.
In fact, we see during the first proof of (
14
) that
{\mathcal{O}}_{P}(1)
exists as a line bundle on
P
iff
P\cong{\mathbb{P}}^{\dim P}
.
(In most cases, the only line bundles on
P
are given by (multiples of)
the canonical class
K_{P}
, equivalently, by (powers of) the dualizing sheaf
\omega_{P}
. Both
{\mathcal{O}}_{P}(K_{P})
and
\omega_{P}

correspond to
{\mathcal{O}}_{P}(-\dim P-1)
.)

This gives us
the main examples of varieties with twisted line bundles:
X=P
,
{\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{O}}_{P_{\bar{k}}}(1)
.
We set

[formula]

T(P):=T\bigl({\mathcal{O}}_{P_{\bar{k}}}(1)\bigr)\quad\mbox{and let}\quad E(P):=E\bigl({\mathcal{O}}_{P_{\bar{k}}}(1)\bigr)

denote a minimal rank direct summand of
F(P)
.
(We see in (
8
) that
E(P)
is unique up to isomorphism.)

It follows from
(
12
) and (
49
.2) that the examples
\bigl(P,{\mathcal{L}}\bigr)
are universal: every other
twisted line bundle is obtained from some
\bigl(P,{\mathcal{L}}\bigr)
by pull-back and tensoring by a (non-twisted) line bundle.

Note.
We chose to write everyting in terms of the
twisted line bundle
{\mathcal{O}}_{P_{\bar{k}}}(1)
since the resulting vector bundle
F
fits in the exact sequence (
5
.4). One could also use its dual

{\mathcal{O}}_{P_{\bar{k}}}(-1)
. That would give
F^{*}
which is related to the cotangent bundle
\Omega_{P}^{1}
. More generally, the other twisted line bundles
{\mathcal{O}}_{P_{\bar{k}}}(m)
also lead to interesting vector bundles. If
(m,\dim P+1)=1
then

T\bigl({\mathcal{O}}_{P_{\bar{k}}}(m)\bigr)
is essentially equivalent to

T(P)
.

Variant Definitions 6
.

Although we will only deal with
T({\mathcal{L}})
as defined above,
the definition makes sense if
X
is normal and
{\mathcal{L}}
is a
rank 1 reflexive sheaf. All the properties in this note hold in that setting. This is sometimes convenient in studying the pull-back of a line bundle by a rational map
f:X\dasharrow Y
. If
Y
is proper and
X
is smooth then
f^{*}{\mathcal{L}}
naturally extends to a line bundle on
X_{\bar{k}}
, but if

X
is only normal then
f^{*}{\mathcal{L}}
usually extends only to a
rank 1 reflexive sheaf.

The definition and the basic properties also work when

{\mathcal{L}}
is replaced by a
simple
coherent sheaf
{\mathcal{G}}
on
X_{\bar{k}}
. (Simple means that
\operatorname{End}({\mathcal{G}})\cong\bar{k}
.)

It is also of interest to investigate bundles
F
such that

F_{\bar{k}}
is a direct sum of line bundles of different degrees.
The study of such bundles on Severi–Brauer varieties was started in
[
BN07
,
BN09
]
and completed in
[
Nov24
]
, where they are called
absolutely split
bundles. These bundles play a key role in understanding the bounded derived category of coherent sheaves on Severi–Brauer varieties; see
[
BSS11
]
and
[
Nov17
]
.

Aside
6
.1.
One can also consider those bundles
F
such that

F_{\bar{k}}
is an
extension
of copies of
{\mathcal{L}}
, or even of line bundles numerically equivalent to
{\mathcal{L}}
. This gives a better notion on Abelian varieties, though the resulting category is not semisimple;
see
[
Muk81
,
CH09
,
Bri13
]
for various characterizations and uses of these vector bundles.

Aside 7
.

Let
X
be any
k
-scheme,
F
a coherent sheaf on
X
, and
K/k
a Galois extension.
Then
F_{K}
is a coherent sheaf on
X_{K}
and

[formula]

F_{K}^{\sigma}\cong F_{K}\quad\mbox{for every}\quad\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(K/k).

Conversely, let
{\mathcal{F}}
be a coherent sheaf on
X_{K}
.
We would like to know whether
{\mathcal{F}}\cong F_{K}

for some coherent sheaf
F
on
X
. A clear necessary condition is

[formula]

{\mathcal{F}}^{\sigma}\cong{\mathcal{F}}\quad\mbox{for every}\quad\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(K/k).

These conditions are usually not sufficient, and Grothendieck’s descent theory
provides the full answer; see
[
BLR90
, Chap.6]
.

Another approach is to try to understand all coherent sheaves that
satisfy the naive descent conditions (
7
.2).
We claim that for line bundles, this corresponds exactly to the twisted ones.
(We prove in (
35
)
that we can go from
\bar{k}/k
to
k^{s}/k
in the definition of
twisted line bundles.)

Claim
7
.3.
Let
X
be a proper, geometrically reduced and geometrically connected
k
-scheme and
{\mathcal{L}}
a line bundle on
X_{k^{s}}
.
Then
{\mathcal{L}}
is a twisted line bundle on
X
iff

{\mathcal{L}}^{\sigma}\cong{\mathcal{L}}\quad\mbox{for every}\quad\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(k^{s}/k).

Proof. Pick
0\neq F\in T({\mathcal{L}})
.
For every

\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(k^{s}/k)
we have

\oplus_{i}{\mathcal{L}}\cong F_{k^{s}}\cong F_{k^{s}}^{\sigma}\cong\oplus_{i}{\mathcal{L}}^{\sigma}.

Thus there is a nonzero map
{\mathcal{L}}\to{\mathcal{L}}^{\sigma}
.
Applying powers of
\sigma
until we reach
\sigma^{m}=1
,
we get a sequence of nonzero maps

{\mathcal{L}}\to{\mathcal{L}}^{\sigma}\to{\mathcal{L}}^{\sigma^{2}}\to\cdots\to{\mathcal{L}}^{\sigma^{m}}\cong{\mathcal{L}}.

The composite is an isomorphism, hence all intermediate maps are isomorphisms.

Conversely, let
K/k
be a finite Galois extension.
Then
K\otimes_{k}K
is a direct sum of

|\operatorname{Gal}(K/k)|
copies of
K
; projections to the summands correspond to
the bilinear maps
(a,b)\mapsto a^{\sigma}\cdot b
where
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(K/k)
.
(See
[
ZS58
, Secs.III.14-15]

for a thorough discussion of tensor products of fields.)

With
K/k
as above, let
p:X_{K}\to X_{k}
be the natural morphism and
F
a coherent sheaf on
X_{K}
. We obtain that

p^{*}p_{*}F=(p_{*}F)\otimes_{k}K\cong F\otimes_{K}K\otimes_{k}K\cong\oplus_{\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(K/k)}F^{\sigma}.

This shows that if
{\mathcal{L}}^{\sigma}\cong{\mathcal{L}}

for every
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(k^{s}/k)
then

p_{*}{\mathcal{L}}\in T({\mathcal{L}})
. ∎

We can reformulate the above result in terms of the Picard
scheme

\operatorname{\mathbf{Pic}}(X)
of
X
. (See
[
Mum66
, Lect.19]
or
[
BLR90
, Chap.8]
for introductions to the Picard scheme. We will not use any of it in the sequel.)
We get that the class of
{\mathcal{L}}
in
\operatorname{\mathbf{Pic}}(X)
is
invariant under
\operatorname{Gal}(k^{s}/k)
.
Equivalently,
[{\mathcal{L}}]\in\operatorname{\mathbf{Pic}}(X)(k)
.
This shows that

[formula]

\operatorname{Pic}^{\rm tw}(X)=\operatorname{\mathbf{Pic}}(X)(k).

Next we prove some quite elementary but surprisingly useful results about the category
T({\mathcal{L}})
.

Lemma 8
.

Notation and assumptions are as in
Definition
2
.
Then all morphisms in
T({\mathcal{L}})
split and there is a unique vector bundle

E({\mathcal{L}})\in T({\mathcal{L}})
such that every other member of
T({\mathcal{L}})
is a sum of copies of

E({\mathcal{L}})
.

Proof. Let us start over
\bar{k}
. Since
\operatorname{Hom}({\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{L}})\cong\bar{k}
, morphisms
\phi:\oplus_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcal{L}}\to\oplus_{j=1}^{m}{\mathcal{L}}
are equivalent to an
m\times n
matrix
with entries in
\bar{k}
. Thus kernels and cokernels are also
sums of copies of
{\mathcal{L}}
and all morphisms split.

Let now
F_{1},F_{2}\in T({\mathcal{L}})
and
\phi:F_{1}\to F_{2}
a morphism.
Then
\ker\phi
is a coherent sheaf such that

(\ker\phi)_{\bar{k}}=\ker\bigl(\phi_{\bar{k}}\bigr)
is a sum of copies of
{\mathcal{L}}
. Thus

\ker\phi
is locally free and it is in
T({\mathcal{L}})
. Same for

\operatorname{coker}\phi
.

An exact sequence of sheaves
0\to F_{1}\to F_{2}\to F_{3}\to 0
in
T({\mathcal{L}})
is classified by an extension class

\eta\in\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(F_{3},F_{1})=H^{1}(X,F_{1}\otimes F_{3}^{*})
.
We already know that
\eta_{\bar{k}}
is trivial, hence so is
\eta
.
(Note that we do not claim that if
F_{1},F_{3}\in T({\mathcal{L}})
then

F_{2}\in T({\mathcal{L}})
, this is false if
H^{1}(X,{\mathcal{O}}_{X})\neq 0
.)

Finally let
E({\mathcal{L}})
be a vector bundle in
T({\mathcal{L}})
with smallest positive rank
and
F
any vector bundle in
T({\mathcal{L}})
. There is a
nonzero map
\phi:E({\mathcal{L}})\to F
since there is one over
\bar{k}
.
Since
\ker\phi\in T({\mathcal{L}})
,
\phi
is an injection and

F\cong E({\mathcal{L}})+F^{\prime}
for some
F^{\prime}\in T({\mathcal{L}})
. By induction on the rank we get that

F
is a sum of copies of

E({\mathcal{L}})
. ∎

Remark 9
.

Set
e=\operatorname{rank}E({\mathcal{L}})
. Then

\det E({\mathcal{L}})
is a line bundle on
X
such that

\bigl(\det E({\mathcal{L}})\bigr)_{\bar{k}}\cong\det\bigl(\oplus_{1}^{e}{\mathcal{L}}\bigr)\cong{\mathcal{L}}^{e}.

Thus
\det E({\mathcal{L}})
should be thought of as the

e
th tensor power of
{\mathcal{L}}
that is defined on
X
.
Thus, the twisted line bundle
{\mathcal{L}}

has 2 ‘shadows’ on
X
:

E({\mathcal{L}})\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\mathcal{L}}^{(e)}:=\det E({\mathcal{L}}).

Using these, we will be able to work as if
{\mathcal{L}}

were a line bundle on
X
.

Corollary 10
.

For every
F\in T({\mathcal{L}})
,

\dim_{k}\operatorname{End}(F)=(\operatorname{rank}F)^{2}
. Furthermore,

(10.1)

\operatorname{End}\bigl(E({\mathcal{L}})\bigr)
is a skew field,

(10.2)

\operatorname{End}\bigl(\oplus_{i=1}^{r}E({\mathcal{L}})\bigr)
is an
r\times r
matrix
algebra over
\operatorname{End}\bigl(E({\mathcal{L}})\bigr)
,

(10.3)

F=E({\mathcal{L}})
iff
\operatorname{End}(F)
is a skew field,

(10.4)

there is a natural correspondence between left ideals
I\subset\operatorname{End}(F)
and
direct summands
G\subset F
, given by

I\mapsto G(I):=\cap_{A\in I}\ker A,\quad\mbox{and}\quad G\mapsto I(G):=\{A:G\subset\ker A\}.

Proof. In order to compute dimensions, note that

\operatorname{End}(F)=H^{0}\bigl(X,{\mathchanc{End}}(F)\bigr)
, where
{\mathchanc{End}}(F)
denotes the sheaf of endomorphisms, thus

\dim_{k}\operatorname{End}(F)=h^{0}\bigl(X_{\bar{k}},{\mathchanc{End}}(F)_{\bar{k}}\bigr)=h^{0}\bigl(X_{\bar{k}},{\mathchanc{End}}(F_{\bar{k}})\bigr).

Since
F_{\bar{k}}
is a sum of
(\operatorname{rank}F)
copies of
{\mathcal{L}}
,

{\mathchanc{End}}(F_{\bar{k}})
is a sum of
(\operatorname{rank}F)^{2}
copies of
{\mathcal{O}}_{X_{\bar{k}}}
.
Thus
\dim_{k}\operatorname{End}(F)=(\operatorname{rank}F)^{2}
.

As we noted above, every endomorphism
\phi:E({\mathcal{L}})\to E({\mathcal{L}})

This is the first appearence of skew fields in our approach to

2.
First examples of Severi–Brauer varieties

So far the only examples of Severi–Brauer varieties we have are plane conics.
In this section we give 2 methods to construct new
Severi–Brauer varieties out of a given one, and then 2 constructions of Severi–Brauer varieties of dimensions 2 and 3.

16
Symmetric powers.

Given a variety
X
, let
\operatorname{Sym}^{n}(X)
denote its
n
th

symmetric power.
That is
\operatorname{Sym}^{n}(X)=X^{n}/S_{n}
is the quotient of the product of
n
copies on
X
by the symmetric group
S_{n}
permuting the factors.
We can also think of
\operatorname{Sym}^{n}(X)
as parametrizing unordered sets of
n
points

p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\in X
, where repetitions are allowed.

One can identify an unordered set of
n
points

p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
with the unique (up to a multiplicative constant) polynomial of degree
n
whose roots are
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
, thus

\operatorname{Sym}^{n}({\mathbb{P}}^{1})\cong{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.
Therefore, if
C
is a 1-dimensional
Severi–Brauer variety then
\operatorname{Sym}^{n}(C)
is an
n
-dimensional
Severi–Brauer variety.

If
n=2m
is even then

\operatorname{Sym}^{2m}(C)\cong|-mK_{C}|\cong{\mathbb{P}}^{2m}
. However, if
n
is odd
and
C(k)=\emptyset
then
\operatorname{Sym}^{n}(C)
also has no
k
-points.
Thus
we get our first examples of nontrivial Severi–Brauer varieties of dimension
n>1
.

Aside
16
.1.
Springer’s theorem says that if a quadric
hypersurface has a point in an odd degree field extension of
k

k
point. For 1-dimensional quadrics this is especially easy to prove:
if

is a point of degree

A symmetric power of a variety of dimension
\geq 2
is always singular, so the symmetric power of a Severi–Brauer variety of dimension at least 2 can not be a Severi–Brauer variety. (See, however, (
37
) and
[
Kol18
]
for
a birational study of such symmetric powers.)
Instead we have the following construction of larger dimensional
Severi–Brauer varieties from a smaller one.

Theorem 17
.

Let
P
be a Severi–Brauer variety, and

F\in T\bigl({\mathcal{O}}_{P_{\bar{k}}}(1)\bigr)
a vector bundle. Then

[formula]

C_{F}(P)\cong\operatorname{Proj}_{k}\textstyle{\sum}_{m,n=0}^{\infty}H^{0}\bigl(P,\operatorname{Sym}^{m}(F^{*})\otimes(\det F)^{\otimes n}\bigr)

is a Severi–Brauer variety of dimension
\dim P+\operatorname{rank}F
, which contains
P

as a subvariety.

Note that a summand in (
17
.1) is nonzero iff
m\leq n\cdot\operatorname{rank}F
.

A conceptual derivation of this formula is given in (
40
), which also proves that
C_{F}(P)
is the unique Severi–Brauer variety
(up to isomorphism) that contains
P
and has dimension
\dim P+\operatorname{rank}F
.

Proof. Set
r=\operatorname{rank}F
. We need to check that if
P\cong{\mathbb{P}}^{\dim P}
then

C_{F}(P)\cong{\mathbb{P}}^{\dim P+r}
.
In this case
F\cong\oplus^{r}{\mathcal{O}}_{P}(-1)
and
we can rewrite the right hand side of
(

.1) as

[formula]

\textstyle{\sum}_{n=0}^{\infty}\textstyle{\sum}_{m=0}^{nr}\operatorname{Sym}^{m}(k^{r})\otimes H^{0}\bigl(P,{\mathcal{O}}_{P}(nr-m)\bigr).

A degree
nr
homogeneous polynomial in the variables

x_{0},\dots,x_{s}
and
y_{1},\dots,y_{r}
can be uniquely written as

\textstyle{\sum}_{m=0}^{nr}\textstyle{\sum}_{|I|=m}{\mathbf{y}}^{I}f_{I}({\mathbf{x}})\quad\mbox{where}\quad\deg f_{I}=nr-m.

Thus the sum (
17
.2) can be naturally identified with

[formula]

\textstyle{\sum}_{n=0}^{\infty}H^{0}\bigl({\mathbb{P}}^{N},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{N}}(nr)\bigr)\subset\textstyle{\sum}_{d=0}^{\infty}H^{0}\bigl({\mathbb{P}}^{N},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{N}}(d)\bigr),

where

, and two sides of (

Thinking of

we see that

∎

If we blow up 6 points in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
, we get a cubic surface, and the same holds for Severi–Brauer surfaces.
One can reverse this construction by first writing down some
cubic surfaces and then proving that they are birational to Severi–Brauer surfaces over a field
k
.
The key step is to describe the lines
on a cubic surface, and the action of the absolute Galois group

\operatorname{Gal}(\bar{k}/k)
on them.
These tasks are easier for singular cubic surfaces: there are fewer lines and they are easier to locate.

Higher dimensional variants of these examples are discussed in Section

18
The cubic surface
S:=(xyz=t^{3})
.

The hyperplane section

If we resolve these singularities, the birational transforms of the lines

We can describe it by the dual graph

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}2&-&2&-\quad 1\quad-&2&-&2\\
|&&&&&&|\\
1&-&2&\rule[1.0pt]{40.0pt}{0.4pt}&2&-&1\end{array}

[formula]

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}1&-&1&-&1\\

Definition 19

The product is symmetric in the

[formula]

3.
Twisted linear subvarieties

In this section we study
Severi–Brauer varieties using their twisted linear subvarieties.
First we show in (
29
) that twisted linear subvarieties of a Severi–Brauer variety behave very much like linear subspaces of a vector space.
Then we show another way to associate a skew field to a
Severi–Brauer variety.

Definition 24
.

A subscheme
X\subset P
of a
Severi–Brauer variety is called
twisted linear
if

X_{\bar{k}}
is a linear subspace of
P_{\bar{k}}\cong{\mathbb{P}}^{\dim P}_{\bar{k}}
.

Let
k^{s}\subset\bar{k}
denote the separable closure. Then

P_{k^{s}}\cong{\mathbb{P}}^{\dim P}_{k^{s}}
by (
35
),
hence, if
X_{\bar{k}}
is a linear subspace, then so is

X_{k^{s}}
, since they are both given by the linear forms in the homogeneous ideal of
X_{k^{s}}
. Thus

X\subset P
is twisted linear iff

X_{k^{s}}
is a linear subspace of
P_{k^{s}}
.

The
twisted linear span
of a closed subscheme
Z\subset P

is denoted by
\langle Z\rangle
. Note that

\langle Z\rangle_{\bar{k}}=\langle Z_{\bar{k}}\rangle

which is equivalent to
\langle Z\rangle_{k^{s}}=\langle Z_{k^{s}}\rangle
.
Indeed,
\langle Z\rangle_{k^{s}}
is a linear subspace that contains

Z_{k^{s}}
, hence
\langle Z\rangle_{k^{s}}\supset\langle Z_{k^{s}}\rangle
.
Conversely, we need to show that
\langle Z_{k^{s}}\rangle
is
defined over
k
. It is clearly invariant under
\operatorname{Gal}(k^{s}/k)
,
thus

\langle Z_{k^{s}}\rangle
is defined over
k
by
(
25
).

All twisted linear subspaces form a
lattice

{\mathcal{L}}(P)
with intersection and span as the lattice operations.

A map
g:P\dasharrow Q
between Severi–Brauer varieties is called
twisted linear
if it is linear over
\bar{k}
. That is, the composite

{\mathbb{P}}_{\bar{k}}^{\dim P}\cong P_{\bar{k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g}}{{\dasharrow}}Q_{\bar{k}}\cong{\mathbb{P}}_{\bar{k}}^{\dim Q}\quad\mbox{is linear.}\quad

The key examples of twisted linear maps are projections.
Let
L\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
be a linear subspace. The projection of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}

with center
L
is the map
\pi_{L}
given by
those sections of
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(1)
that vanish along
L
.
If
L^{\perp}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
is a linear subspace complementary to
L
,
then we can think of
\pi_{L}
as a map

\pi_{L}:{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\dasharrow L^{\perp}
.

Next let
P
be a Severi–Brauer variety and

R\subset P
a twisted linear subvariety. Although

{\mathcal{O}}_{P}(1)
does not exist,
{\mathcal{O}}_{P}(\dim P+1)
does and
we can define the
projection

\pi_{R}:P\dasharrow R^{\perp}
of
P
with
center

R
as the map
given by
those sections of
{\mathcal{O}}_{P}(\dim P+1)
that vanish along
R
with multiplicity
\dim P+1
. It is clear that
R^{\perp}
is also a Severi–Brauer variety.

We say that two twisted linear subvarieties
Q
and
R

are
complementary
if
Q\cap R=\emptyset
and

\langle Q,R\rangle=P
. Equivalently, if
Q\cap R=\emptyset
and

\dim Q+\dim R=\dim P-1
.
Then
\pi_{R}
restricts to an isomorphism
Q\cong R^{\perp}
. I particular,
complements of
R
are all twisted linearly isomorphic to each other.
The projection
\pi_{R}:P\dasharrow R^{\perp}\cong Q
has a simple geometric description:
a point
p\in P\setminus R
is mapped to

\pi_{R}(p)=Q\cap\langle R,p\rangle
.
We see in (
27
) that complements exist.

We have used
Weil’s lemma on the field of definition of a subscheme;
see
[
Wei62
, I.7.Lem.2]
or
[
KSC04
, Sec.3.4]
for proofs.

Lemma 25
.

Let
X
be a
k
-scheme,

a Galois extension and
Z_{K}\subset X_{K}
a closed subscheme that is invariant under
\operatorname{Gal}(K/k)
. Then

is defined over

there is a unique closed subscheme

.∎

Theorem 26

(Châtelet correspondence)
.

Let

(26.2)

(26.3)

The map between the direct summands and the twisted linear subvarieties preserves the lattice structure, that is, intersections, linear spans and complements.

Proof. The equivalence between left ideals

and

, given by

[formula]

I\mapsto G(I):=\cap_{A\in I}\ker A,\quad\mbox{and}\quad G\mapsto I(G):=\{A:G\subset\ker A\},

was already discussed in (

).

To go from direct summands

to

{\mathcal{O}}_{P}\to F(P)

Aside on the Fundamental Theorem of Projective Geometry

Definition 30
.

An abstract
projective space

consists of a set

(called points) and a set

of subsets of

(30.1)

For any two distinct points

(30.2)

(30.3)

Every line has at least 3 points.

(
Comment.
The aim of the definition of abstract projective spaces is to axiomatize the properties of linear subspaces of projective spaces over fields. This leads to a long list of axioms but it turns out that everything can be derived from the above 3.)

A subset

Thus axiom (

It is clear that

iff any 4 points are coplanar.

5.

All Severi–Brauer varieties are built up from

The key to this is the following result, which shows how to

Proposition 39

Let

(39.1)

-bundle.

(39.2)

such that

(39.3)

is uniquely determined by
R
and

.

(39.4)

is birational to
Q\times{\mathbb{P}}^{c}
for

.

Proof. Let

be the exceptional divisor.
A geometric fiber of
\tilde{\pi}
is a linear space

of dimension
=\dim Q+1
, and

is identified with

Pushing forward the injection
{\mathcal{O}}_{B_{Q}P}\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{B_{Q}P}(E)

[formula]

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{R}\to\tilde{\pi}_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{B_{Q}P}(E)\to F^{*}\to 0,

where

is a vector bundle of rank
=\dim Q+1
on

Working over

shows that

.2) holds and (

Thus

determine

. There are many ways to see that they also determine

has Picard number 2, hence at most 2 nontrivial contractions. One of them is

Finally (

Theorem 41

(Flag transitivity)
.

Let
P_{1},P_{2}
be two Severi–Brauer varieties
such that

Let

If

Definition 50

(Products)
.

Here are 5 versions of the definition of products.
The first 2 variants recall known definitions from geometry and algebra.

(Polarized pair version

(Linear system version

.3) Following the previous examples, we set

Thinking of a Severi–Brauer variety as a twisted linear system

(Segre embedding version

Example 53

(Cremona transformations I)
.

The simplest birational transformation of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
is

[formula]

(x_{0}{:}\cdots{:}x_{n})\mapsto(x_{0}^{-1}{:}\cdots{:}x_{n}^{-1}).

After multiplying by
x_{0}\cdots x_{n}
, it is given by the linear system of degree
n
polynomials that vanish with multiplicity

at the coordinate vertices.

Thus if

is a Severi–Brauer variety of dimension

, and

a 0-dimensional, spanning subscheme of degree

, then

\bigl|{\mathcal{O}}_{P}(n)(-(n-1)Z)\bigr|
defines a birational map

P\stackrel{{\scriptstyle bir}}{{\sim}}P^{\vee}
.

Example 54

(Cremona transformations II)
.

A more precise version is the following.

Let
K/k
be an extension of degree
n+1
such that
P_{K}\cong{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{K}
.
Let
H_{0}\subset P_{K}
and
G_{0}\subset P_{K}^{\vee}
be hyperplanes whose conjugates

(resp.

and

Let
\pi,\pi^{\vee}
denote the coordinate projections of

. By (

is a line bundle,
and the

. The intersection of all these conjugates

is the graph of a Cremona transformation

\phi_{H,G}^{*}(G_{0})=H-H_{0}

Indeed, this claim can be checked over

,

, and then

is given by the equations

Let now

be another hyperplane. Then

(\phi_{H,G}\circ\phi_{G,H^{\prime}})^{*}(H^{\prime}_{0})=\phi_{H,G}^{*}(G-G_{0})=(n-1)H+H_{0}.

After canceling the common

that maps

to

.

The definition of

.

Let

The Brauer group

Theorem 56
.

The product defined in (
50
.4) makes the
Brauer equivalence classes of Severi–Brauer varieties into a commutative group. The inverse of
P
is
P^{\vee}
.

Proof. The product defines a commutative monoid structure on
the isomorphism classes of Severi–Brauer varieties.
If
P_{1}\dasharrow P_{2}
and
Q_{1}\dasharrow Q_{2}
are twisted linear maps, then so is

P_{1}\otimes Q_{1}\dasharrow P_{2}\otimes Q_{2}
, hence we get a
commutative monoid on the Brauer equivalence classes.

As we noted in (

),

{\mathcal{O}}_{P\times P^{\vee}}(1,1)
is a (non–twisted) line bundle, hence

|{\mathcal{O}}_{P\times P^{\vee}}(1,1)|
is a trivial Severi–Brauer variety.
This says that

P\otimes P^{\vee}\sim{\mathbb{P}}^{0}
. ∎

Definition 57

The group defined above is called the

, the second Galois cohomology group of

.
See

The order of

Since

is a (non-twisted) line bundle on

Proof. By (

|{\mathcal{O}}_{P}(m)|\sim|{\mathcal{O}}_{P}(1)|^{\otimes m}\sim(P^{\vee})^{\otimes m}\sim(P^{\otimes m})^{\vee}

.
∎

Let

Remark 61

Besides Cremona transformations as in (

Let

.

Let

-varieties. Assume that

Norm forms and Severi–Brauer varieties

We construct
Severi–Brauer varieties from degree
n
hypersurfaces in
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
that are defined using norm forms (
19
) of cyclic Galois extensions.
The main result is the following.

Theorem 74
.

Fix a cyclic Galois extension
K/k
of degree
n
.
Let
P
be a Severi–Brauer variety of dimension
n-1
over
k
that has a
K
-point.
Then
P
is birational to a hypersurface of the form

[formula]

X(K/k,e):=\bigl(\operatorname{norm}_{K/k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=et^{n}\bigr)\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\quad\mbox{for some}\quad e\in k^{\times},

and every such hypersurface is birational to at least one Severi–Brauer variety.

A more precise version is outlined in (
79
).

Aside 75
.

The elements of norm 1 form a
k
-subtorus

T^{n-1}\subset K^{\times}
. This torus acts on
X(K/k,e)
such that

X^{0}(K/k,e):=X(K/k,e)\setminus(t=0)
is a
torsor under
T^{n-1}
. We will see that the birational maps

X(K/k,e)\dasharrow P:=P(K/k,e,r)
are isomorphisms over
X^{0}(K/k,e)
,
and
the
P(K/k,e,r)
inherit this

T^{n-1}
-action.

First part of the proof.

Let
P
be a Severi–Brauer variety of dimension
n-1
over
k
that has a
K
-point. Pick a
K
-point
p\in P(K)
such that its conjugates

p_{1}=p,\dots,p_{n}
span
P
. By (
54
) two such points
p,p^{\prime}
differ by an automorphism of
P
, so this is not an additional choice.

Fix also a generator
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(K/k)
, this gives a cyclic order on the points
p_{i}
; we may assume that it is
\sigma(p_{i})=p_{i+1}
.
Let

H_{i}:=\langle p_{1},\dots,p_{i-1},\widehat{p_{i}},p_{i+1},\dots,p_{n}\rangle\subset P_{K}

denote the hyperplane that contains the points
\{p_{j}:j\neq i\}
and choose an equation
H_{i}=(m_{i}=0)
(also over
K
).
Consider the vectorspace of functions spanned by

1,\tfrac{m_{1}}{m_{n}},\tfrac{m_{2}}{m_{1}},\cdots,\tfrac{m_{n}}{m_{n-1}}.

Note that although
\sigma(H_{i})=H_{i+1}
, it is usually not possible to choose the
m_{i}
such that
\sigma^{*}(m_{i+1})=m_{i}
for every

. However, no matter how the
m_{i}
are chosen,
\sigma^{*}(m_{i+1})=\lambda_{i}m_{i}
for some
\lambda_{i}\in K^{\times}
, thus the induced linear system on
P
is defined over
k
.
This gives a rational map

[formula]

\phi:P\dasharrow X\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}.

This may be clearer if we think of
\phi
as given by the linear system spanned by the hypersurfaces

\begin{array}[]{l}H_{1}+\cdots+H_{n}\quad\mbox{and}\\
H_{1}+\cdots+H_{i-2}+2H_{i}+H_{i+1}+\cdots+H_{n}\quad\mbox{for}\quad i=1,\dots,n.\end{array}

The first of them is defined over
k
, and the others are permuted by
\operatorname{Gal}(K/k)
. So the linear system, and hence
\phi
, are defined over
k
.

We need to show that

is birational and

has the required equation.
Birationality of a map can be checked after a field extension.
Over
K
we can choose

as coordinates, and then the map
\phi_{K}:{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}_{\mathbf{u}}\cong P_{K}\dasharrow{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{\mathbf{x}}

is given by

(u_{1}{:}\dots{:}u_{n})\mapsto\bigl(1{:}\tfrac{u_{1}}{u_{n}}{:}\tfrac{u_{2}}{u_{1}}{:}\cdots{:}\tfrac{u_{n}}{u_{n-1}}\bigr),

whose image satisfies the obvious equation

x_{1}\cdots x_{n}=x_{0}^{n}
. Over
K
, the inverse is given by

(1{:}x_{1}{:}\cdots{:}x_{n})\mapsto\bigl(x_{1}{:}(x_{1}x_{2}){:}\cdots{:}(x_{1}\cdots x_{n})\bigr).

Thus, starting with a Severi–Brauer variety of dimension
n-1
over

, plus a cyclic

.

Next we check that the equation of

.

Let

be a field and

(x_{1}\cdots x_{n}=t^{n})

Severi–Brauer schemes

Definition 80
.

A
Severi–Brauer scheme
is a
smooth, proper morphism
p:P\to S
all of whose fibers are
Severi–Brauer varieties. Note that
\omega_{P/S}^{-1}
is
p
-ample, hence

p:P\to S
is projective.

We aim to show that the basic set-up of Assertion
1

generalizes to Severi–Brauer schemes. The vector bundle
F(P)
, or rather its dual, appears in
[
Qui73
, §8.4]
.

Theorem 81
.

Let
p:P\to S
be a Severi–Brauer scheme.

(81.1)

There is a unique non-split extension

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{P}\to F(P)\to T_{P/S}\to 0

that induces the extension given in (
5
.1) on all fibers.

(81.2)

p_{*}{\mathchanc{End}}\bigl(F(P)\bigr)
is a locally free sheaf of algebras
whose fiber over
s\in S
is the central simple algebra corresponding to
P_{s}
.

Note that
p_{*}{\mathchanc{End}}\bigl(F(P)\bigr)^{\rm opp}
is called the

Azumaya algebra
corresponding to
P
; taking the opposite is just a
convention.

Proof.
We aim to extend the method of Example
5
to
Severi–Brauer schemes.
A priori, the extensions in (
5
.1) tell us only about the fibers, so we should look for a non-split extension

[formula]

0\to p^{*}L\to F(P)\to T_{P/S}\to 0,

where
L
is some line bundle on
S
.
The extension class is

[formula]

\eta\in\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(T_{P/S},p^{*}L)=H^{1}(P,p^{*}L\otimes\Omega^{1}_{P/S}).

Note that
p_{*}\Omega^{1}_{P/S}=0
, hence

[formula]

H^{1}(P,p^{*}L\otimes\Omega^{1}_{P/S})=H^{0}\bigl(S,L\otimes R^{1}p_{*}\Omega^{1}_{P/S}\bigr).

Since
H^{1}(P_{s},\Omega^{1}_{P_{s}})=k
and
H^{i}(P_{s},\Omega^{1}_{P_{s}})=0
for
i>0
for every fiber,

R^{1}p_{*}\Omega^{1}_{P/S}
is a line bundle. Thus we need to take

[formula]

L:=\bigl(R^{1}p_{*}\Omega^{1}_{P/S}\bigr)^{*}.

We check in (
83
) that

R^{1}p_{*}\Omega^{1}_{P/S}\cong{\mathcal{O}}_{S}
, hence in fact we do have an extension as in
(
81
.1) and the formation of
F(P)
commutes with arbitrary base change.

Finally note that
{\mathchanc{End}}\bigl(F(P)\bigr)
is a locally free sheaf on

P
whose restriction to every geometric fiber is a sum of copies of
{\mathcal{O}}_{P_{s}}
.
Thus the formation of
p_{*}{\mathchanc{End}}\bigl(F(P)\bigr)
commutes with arbitrary base change.

Euler sequence, functorially.

For later purposes we work with

W

e^{*}:W\otimes\operatorname{Sym}_{S}(W^{*})[-1]\to\operatorname{Sym}_{S}(W^{*}),\quad\mbox{where}\quad e^{*}(w\otimes w^{*}):=w(w^{*})\in{\mathcal{O}}_{S},

0\to\Omega_{P/S}^{1}\to\pi^{*}W\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{P/S}(-1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e^{*}}}{{\to}}{\mathcal{O}}_{P}\to 0.

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{P}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e}}{{\to}}\pi^{*}W^{*}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{P/S}(1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\partial}}{{\to}}T_{P/S}\to 0,

Both are unchanged if we tensor
