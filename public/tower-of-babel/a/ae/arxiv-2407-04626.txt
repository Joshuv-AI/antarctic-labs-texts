Determination Problems for Orbit Closures and Matrix Groups
Rida Ait El Manssour, George Kenison, Mahsa Shirmohammadi, Anton Varonka, James Worrell

Abstract
. Computational problems concerning the orbit of a point under the action of a matrix group occur throughout computer science, including in program analysis, complexity theory, quantum computation, and automata theory. In many cases the focus extends beyond orbits proper to orbit closures under a suitable topology. Typically one starts from a group and a set of points and asks questions about the orbit closure of the set under the action of the group, e.g., whether two given orbit closures intersect. In this paper we consider a collection of what we call determination problems concerning matrix groups and orbit closures. These problems begin with a given variety and seek to understand whether and how it arises either as an algebraic matrix group or as an orbit closure. The how question asks whether the underlying group is s s -generated, meaning it is topologically generated by s s matrices for a given number s s . Among other applications, problems of this type have recently been studied in the context of synthesising loops subject to certain specified invariants on program variables. Our main result is a polynomial-space procedure that inputs a variety and a number s s and determines whether the given variety arises as an orbit closure of a point under an s s -generated commutative algebraic matrix group. The main tools in our approach are structural properties of commutative algebraic matrix groups and module theory. We leave open the question of determining whether a variety is an orbit closure of a point under an s s -generated algebraic matrix group (without the requirement of commutativity).

The general linear group

The computational study of orbits of subgroups of

For many applications it makes sense to study

Orbit closures can be seen as an instance of abstract semantics in program analysis, where one

In the case at hand, the abstract domain is the collection of

The algebraic subsets of

When

1.2.
Determination Problems

In this paper, we investigate a series of determination problems related to groups and their orbit closures. These problems start with a given algebraic set (commonly referred to as a variety) and examine whether it can be realized as a linear algebraic group or as an orbit closure, with the constraint that the underlying group be topologically
s
-generated.
Here we define an algebraic group
G\leq\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
to be

topologically
s
-generated
if there is a set
S\subseteq\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
of cardinality
s
such that

G=\overline{\langle S\rangle}
.
By
Proposition

2.1
,
algebraic groups are always topologically generated by a finite set.

In this context,
determining whether a variety
Z\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
arises as an orbit closure under the action of
G
is, in principle, straightforward.
Let
\mathrm{Sym}(Z)
denote the subgroup of
\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
consisting of all matrices that leave
Z
invariant, defined as
\mathrm{Sym}(Z)\coloneqq\{A\in\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}):A(Z)=Z\}

Any group
G
having

as an orbit-closure
is necessarily a subgroup of
\mathrm{Sym}(Z)
,
and so we
may assume without loss of generality that

is
\mathrm{Sym}(Z)

But

is
definable in first-order logic over
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
and hence the orbit closure of any
point of
Z
under
\mathrm{Sym}(Z)
is definable over the real closed field
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}\cap\mathbb{R}
.
3
3

3

The Zariski closure of every first-order definable

\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}\cap\mathbb{R}
after identifying
each element of
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
as the pair of its real and imaginary parts.

Hence the question of whether
Z
arises as the orbit closure of a point under

\mathrm{Sym}(Z)
reduces to the decision problem for the theory of real closed fields.
It further holds by

that

\mathrm{Sym}(Z)
has a finitely generated subgroup whose Zariski closure is

itself. Whence

is the orbit closure
of a point under some finitely generated matrix group if

\mathrm{Sym}(Z)
.

s
-generated group than simply determining whether it is an orbit closure.

1.3.
Application in Programming Languages

A research direction closely related to orbit-closure determination is the synthesis of linear loops.
While computing the Zariski closure of an orbit is used to compute polynomial invariants of loops,
the loop synthesis problem reverses this direction: given an input polynomial invariant, one seeks to construct update rules for loop variables that maintain the invariant.

Suppose a deterministic polynomial loop involves integer variables

where
*
denotes a wildcard that nondeterministically evaluates to false or true,
and the task is to determine assignments

and

is a loop invariant.
A valid solution in this case is to set

This kind of synthesis task is closely connected to the classical algebraic geometry problem of parametrising varieties defined by polynomial equations

Recent developments have focused on techniques for synthesising deterministic linear loops, often by encoding the synthesis task as a constraint-solving problem over algebraic structures

Geometrically, this amounts to finding an infinite orbit of a cyclic matrix group that remains entirely within the variety defined by the invariant. Some formulations restrict attention to infinite orbits in order to avoid trivial cases where the synthesis reduces to solving a finite system of polynomial equations

Several concrete approaches have been proposed to instantiate these synthesis procedures. One line of work develops constraint-solving methods that generate loops preserving a given polynomial invariant, based on templates supplied by the user

1.4.
Overview of the Main Result

We reduce our determination problems to satisfiability problems in fragments of the first-order theory of the algebraically closed or real-closed fields.
These theories are formulated in the first-order language of rings, which includes constant symbols
0
and
1
, as well as binary function symbols for addition and multiplication. The theory of algebraically closed field is the set of all sentences in this language that are true over
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Similarly, the theory of the real numbers consists of those sentences that are true over
\mathbb{R}
.
The following theorem gives a complexity bound on the decision problem for this theory.

Theorem 1.3 (

;
Basu et al., 1996
;

Consider a first-order sentence in the language of rings that mentions

\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
and
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}\cap\mathbb{R}
can both be decided in time
(mb)^{d^{2k+2}}
.

Following
Basu et al., 2006

the truth of first-order sentences, over both
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
and
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}\cap\mathbb{R}

with a fixed number of alternations can be decided in space
(d\log{b})^{O(1)}
.

The following theorem is our main contribution:

Theorem 1.4.

The orbit-closure determination problem for commutative matrices with parameters

can be decided in time

(mb)^{\mathrm{poly}(d)}

s
generators. Moreover, the generators of
\Lambda
have bit-size bounded polynomially in
the description of
Z
; hence
\Lambda
can also be guessed
in nondeterministic polynomial time.

(3)

The existence of an
s
-generated unipotent group
G_{u}
such that
Z=\overline{G_{u}\cdot G_{s}\cdot\bm{v}}

can be expressed in first-order logic given descriptions of
Z
,
\bm{v}
, and
\Lambda
.
This relies on the fact that for a unipotent matrix
U
, the matrix exponential
U^{n}
is a matrix of polynomials in
n
.

We write
\textrm{Id}_{d}
for the identity matrix of dimension
d
. A matrix
M\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d\times d}
is
unipotent
if
(M-\textrm{Id}_{d})^{d}
is the zero matrix, and

semisimple
if it is diagonalisable over
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Let
G
be a commutative algebraic group. By
Section

2
, the subset
G_{s}
of semisimple matrices in
G
forms an algebraic subgroup; similarly, the set
G_{u}
of unipotent matrices in
G
forms an algebraic subgroup.
Moreover, the decomposition
G=G_{u}\cdot G_{s}
holds in the commutative setting.

For a given instance of the orbit-closure determination problem, let
Z
be the zero locus of the input polynomials. If
Z
is
the orbit closure of a vector
\bm{v}

under the action of some group
G
, by the above, it can be written as

\overline{G_{u}\cdot G_{s}\cdot\bm{v}}
.
If both
G_{s}
and
G_{u}
are
s
-generated, then
by
Section

3
, the group
G
is also
s
-generated.

A key technical result,
Lemma

4.1
, states that for given a commutative matrix group
G
, generated by matrices
M_{1},\ldots,M_{s}
and a vector
\bm{v}
, we can choose a change of basis (given by an invertible matrix
P
) that brings both the group
G
and the vector
\bm{v}
into a simplified and canonical form. In this new basis:

•

the semisimple subgroup becomes diagonal;

•

the unipotent subgroup becomes upper unitriangular; and

•

\bm{v}
is mapped to a binary vector
T\bm{1}
, where
T\in\{0,1\}^{d\times k}
satisfies
T^{\mathsf{T}}T=\textrm{Id}_{k}
.

Our algorithm in
Theorem

1.4

guesses the matrix
T
.
The algorithm proceeds by assuming that
{P\bm{v}:=T\bm{1}}
.
Write

PG_{s}P^{-1}=\overline{\langle D_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle}\,.

In this new basis, the orbit-closure of
P\bm{v}
under

\langle D_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle
forms a linear algebraic group, and becomes
the zero set of a pure binomial ideal
I
.
Recall that a pure binomial ideal
in the variables
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})

is an ideal generated by polynomials of the from
\bm{x}^{\bm{\lambda}}-\bm{x}^{\bm{\lambda^{\prime}}}
, where

\bm{\lambda},\bm{\lambda^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
.
Associated with any such ideal is its exponent lattice
\Lambda
, a subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
collecting the vectors
\bm{\lambda}-\bm{\lambda^{\prime}}
.
Studying this lattice provides key structural insight into the underlying variety defined by
I
.

By the requirement on the number of generators, the orbit closure

\overline{\langle D_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle\cdot P\bm{v}}
seen as a linear algebraic group must also have
s
topological generators.
By
Proposition

2.4
,
the torsion subgroup of

\mathbb{Z}^{d}/\Lambda
has at most
s
generators.
A careful analysis in
Section

4.2
, combined with
Proposition

2.3
, shows that the degree bound
b
on the defining polynomials of
Z
also applies to the generators of the binomial ideal
I
.
The degree bound
b
allows the algorithm to guess a lattice
\Lambda
, generated by vectors with entries bounded in absolute value by
b
, and such that
\mathbb{Z}^{d}/\Lambda
has at most
s

generators, in line with
Proposition

2.4
.
For this lattice
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we define
H_{\Lambda}
as a subgroup of the
d
-dimensional multiplicative variety, where
\bm{a}\in H_{\Lambda}
if
\bm{a}^{\bm{\lambda}}=1
for all
\bm{\lambda}\in\Lambda
.

By
Section

3
, there exist unipotent matrices

U_{1},\ldots,U_{s}
that topologically generate
the unipotent subgroup
G_{u}
of
G
. Furthermore, by
Proposition

2.5

the following equality holds:

G_{u}=\biggl\{\exp\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{s}t_{i}\log U_{i}\biggl):t_{1},\ldots,t_{s}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}\biggr\}.

Since the power series of
\exp
has at most
d
terms in this case (see
Section

2
), it follows that
the group
G_{u}
is definable by a first-order formula with parameters
U_{1},\ldots,U_{s}
.

We are now in a position to encode the given instance of the problem as a first-order sentence in the theory of real-closed fields. An important observation enabling this reduction is that, for linear algebraic groups, the Zariski and Euclidean closures of an orbit coincide (see
Section

2
). In our encoding, we rely on this fact to describe the algebraic closure of a constructible set using Euclidean conditions:
by requiring that for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a group element constructed by our guesses mapping the image within
\varepsilon
of some vector in the input
Z
.

More in detail, the orbit-closure verification task is expressed as a first-order sentence with quantifier prefix of the form
\exists^{*}\forall^{*}\exists^{*}
,
that is, a block of existential quantifiers followed by a block of universal quantifiers and a final existential quantifier.
The outermost existential quantifiers encode the possible choices of the matrices
P
and
U_{1},\ldots,U_{s}
, while the
equality of
PZ
and

\overline{\langle U_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle\cdot TH_{\Lambda}}

is encoded by a
\forall^{*}\exists
-sentence with parameters
P
and
U_{1},\ldots,U_{s}
.
The algorithm returns "yes", meaning that
Z
is
an orbit closure of a vector
\bm{v}

under the action of the group
G
, if the above sentence is satisfiable over
\mathbb{R}
.
By
Theorem

1.3
, the truth of such a sentence can be decided in time
(mb)^{\mathrm{poly}(d)}
.
Then the overall complexity bound follows from the fact that the number of choices of the lattice
\Lambda
and vector
P\bm{v}
is at most
(2b)^{d^{2}+1}
.
This concludes our informal overview of the proof of
Theorem

1.4
;
the detailed proof can be found in
Section

4
.

Orbit-Closure vs. Group Determination.
En route to proving
Theorem

1.4
on orbit-closure determination, we consider a variant—namely group determination.

For group determination we first consider a simpler setting where the input polynomial ideal
I\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[\bm{x}]
is a pure binomial ideal.
Recall that we can associate a lattice

\Lambda=\{\bm{\lambda}-\bm{\lambda^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\bm{x}^{\bm{\lambda}}-\bm{x}^{\bm{\lambda^{\prime}}}\in I\}
with
I
.
It is well-known that, if the input ideal
I
defines a group
G
then it is necessarily topologically generated by diagonal matrices.
If the torsion subgroup of

\mathbb{Z}^{d}/\Lambda
is
s
-generated, then, by
Proposition

2.4
,
the minimal number of topological generators of
G
is either
s
or
s+1
(depending on the rank of
\Lambda
).
However, in the setting of orbit-closure determination, this lower bound on the number of generators may no longer hold, as shown by the following example.

Example 1.5.

Let
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{3}
be the associated lattice of the pure binomial ideal
I\coloneqq\langle x_{1}^{2}-x_{3}^{2},x_{2}^{2}-x_{3}^{2}\rangle
.
That is,
\Lambda=\{c_{1}(2,0,-2)^{\mathsf{T}}+c_{2}(0,2,-2)^{\mathsf{T}}:c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.
By the fundamental theory of finitely generated abelian groups, the quotient group
\mathbb{Z}^{3}/\Lambda
is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\times(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\times\mathbb{Z}
.
In particular, the torsion subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{3}/\Lambda
is
(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\times(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
, a product of two cyclic groups, which is
2
-generated.

Here and in the following, let
\Delta(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\GL_{3}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
denote a diagonal matrix with
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}
(in this order) on the diagonal.
Let

G\leq\GL_{3}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
be the algebraic subgroup of diagonal matrices induced by
I
, that is,

G=\{\Delta(\bm{a}):P(\bm{a})=0\text{ for all }P\in I\}.

Observe that
G
is isomorphic to
H_{\Lambda}
(recall that the elements of the latter are
\bm{a}
such that
P(\bm{a})=0
).
By
Proposition

2.4
,
the minimal number of topological generators for
G
is 2.
Indeed,
G
is topologically generated by the diagonal matrices
\Delta(2,-2,2)
and
\Delta(-2,2,2)
.

Changing our viewpoint, let us consider
I

as defining a subvariety
V
of
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{3}
rather than a set of diagonal matrices in
\GL_{3}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
.
The variety
V
can be written as the orbit closure of a vector
\bm{v}

under the action of the 1-generated (cyclic) group
\overline{\langle M\rangle}
, where

M=\begin{pmatrix}0&-2&0\\
2&\phantom{-}0&0\\
0&\phantom{-}0&2\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{v}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

In this section we introduce basic notions from algebraic geometry that are needed in the paper.

Ideals and Varieties.

Recall that an algebraic number is a complex number that is the root of a univariate polynomial with integer coefficients.
The collection of all algebraic numbers forms a sub-field of
\mathbb{C}
, which is denoted

\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
.
We write
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[\bm{x}]
for the
ring of polynomials in the variables
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
with coefficients in
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
.
A
polynomial ideal

I
is an additive subgroup of
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[\bm{x}]

that is closed under multiplication in
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[\bm{x}]
.
Given a set of polynomials
S\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[\bm{x}]
, we denote by
\langle S\rangle
the ideal
generated by
S
:

\langle S\rangle\coloneqq\left\{P_{1}Q_{1}+\cdots+P_{k}Q_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N},Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{k}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[\bm{x}],P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}\in S\right\}.

For example,
\langle x_{1}\rangle
is the ideal of all polynomials that are multiples of
x_{1}
.

An
algebraic set
(or
variety
) is the set of common zeroes of a finite collection of polynomials.
We may also refer to an algebraic set as the
zero locus
of these polynomials.
By Hilbert’s basis theorem every polynomial ideal
I\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[\bm{x}]
is finitely generated, that is, there exists a finite set
S\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[\bm{x}]
such that
I=\langle S\rangle
.
Thus the zero set of
I

V(I)\coloneqq\{\bm{a}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}:P(\bm{a})=0\text{ for all }P\in I\}

is a variety. As an example, consider an ideal
I
of the polynomial ring
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},x_{2}]
generated by polynomials
\{x_{1}^{2},x_{2}^{2}\}
.
The ideal

I=\langle x_{1}^{2},x_{2}^{2}\rangle=\{x_{1}^{2}Q_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})+x_{2}^{2}Q_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}):Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},x_{2}]\}

contains polynomials such as
x_{1}^{2}
,
x_{1}^{2}-\sqrt{2}x_{2}^{2}
, and
x_{1}^{3}+x_{1}^{2}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{2}^{2}-x_{2}^{3}
.
The zero set of
I

is the variety

V(I)\coloneqq\{(a_{1},a_{2})\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{2}:P(a_{1},a_{2})=0\text{ for all }P\in I\}=\{(a_{1},a_{2})\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{2}:a_{1}^{2}=0\wedge a_{2}^{2}=0\}.

Dually, the set of polynomials that vanish on a set
E\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
, denoted

I(E)\coloneqq\{P\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[\bm{x}]:P(a)=0\text{ for all }a\in E\},

is an ideal in
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[\bm{x}]
and is referred to as the

vanishing ideal
of
E
.
Resuming our example, we can consider
E=V(I)
for
I=\langle x_{1}^{2},x_{2}^{2}\rangle
. The vanishing ideal of this variety is

I(V(I))\coloneqq\{P\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},x_{2}]:P(a_{1},a_{2})=0\text{ for all }(a_{1},a_{2})\in V(I)\}\\
=\{P\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},x_{2}]:P(a_{1},a_{2})=0\text{ for all }(a_{1},a_{2})\text{ such that }a_{1}^{2}=a_{2}^{2}=0\}=\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle.

The ideal
I(V(I))
may, in general, be different from
I
.

Zariski Topology.

A
topology
on a set
X
is a collection
\tau
of subsets of
X
, called
closed sets
, that satisfy the following axioms:

(1)

\varnothing,X\in\tau
;

(2)

An arbitrary intersection of a collection of sets in
\tau
belongs to
\tau
;

(3)

Any finite union of sets in
\tau
belongs to
\tau
.

The
Zariski topology
on

\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
has as its closed sets
the varieties in
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
.
Given a set
E\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
, we denote by
\overline{E}
the
closure
of
E
in the Zariski topology, that is,
the smallest algebraic set that contains
E
.
We say that a set
E\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
is dense in an algebraic set
V
, if
\overline{E}=V
.

A closed set
A\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
is
irreducible
if it cannot be written as the union of two proper closed subsets. A maximal irreducible closed subset of
A
is called
an
irreducible component
of
A
.
By Hilbert’s basis theorem
every closed set
A
can be written as a finite union of its irreducible components.
For example, the set
A=\{\bm{a}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{2}:a_{1}a_{2}=0\}
has irreducible components

A_{1}=\{\bm{a}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{2}:a_{1}=0\}
and
A_{2}=\{\bm{a}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{2}:a_{2}=0\}
.
Given a closed set
E
, denote by
\dim E
the dimension of the variety
E
, that is,
the maximal length of a strictly decreasing chain of nonempty irreducible subvarieties of
E
. We define the dimension of an arbitrary set to be the dimension of
its closure.

Given an ideal
I\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[X]
over the variables
X=\{x_{i,j}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq d}
, and matrix
M\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d\times d}
, we write

M\cdot I
for the ideal
\{P(MX)\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[X]:P\in I\}
.
Clearly,
V(M\cdot I)=\{A\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d\times d}:MA\in V(I)\}
.

Linear Algebraic Groups.

A matrix
M\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d\times d}
is

nilpotent
if
M^{n}=0
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
It is

unipotent
if
M-\textrm{Id}_{d}
is nilpotent, and

semisimple
if it is diagonalisable over
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
.
The matrix
M\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d\times d}
is called

upper triangular
if all entries below the main diagonal are zero.
We use the term
upper unitriangular
to refer to an upper triangular matrix whose entries along the main diagonal are all ones.

Write
\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})

for the group of invertible
d\times d
matrices with entries in
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
.
We identify
\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
with the variety

\bigl\{(M,y)\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d\times d}\times\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}:\det(M)\cdot y=1\bigr\}\,.

Under this identification, matrix multiplication is a polynomial map
\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})\times\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})\rightarrow\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
, and, by Cramer’s rule, matrix inversion is also a polynomial map
\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})\rightarrow\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
.
A
linear algebraic group
(or algebraic matrix group)
G
is a Zariski-closed subgroup of
\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
.
In other words, a subgroup
G\leq\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
is algebraic if it can be defined by polynomial equalities.
As an example take the subgroup of diagonal matrices in
\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
, defined by

\{M=(m_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\in\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}):\;\;m_{ij}=0\text{ for all }i\neq j\}.

Denote by
G_{s}
the subset of semisimple matrices in
G
, and by
G_{u}
the subset of unipotent matrices. The following fact is well-known, see for example
Humphreys, 1975
, Chapter 6
.

{fact}

For a commutative algebraic group
G\leq\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
, the algebraic subgroups
G_{s}
and
G_{u}
form algebraic subgroups of
G
; moreover, we have the decomposition of
G
into
G_{u}\cdot G_{s}
.

The next fact follows from Chevalley’s Theorem; see
Borel, 1991
, Chapter AG, Corollary 10.2
and
Appendix

B
for details.

{fact}
[]

Let
G\leq\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
be an algebraic group and
\bm{v}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
a vector, the Zariski and Euclidean closures of the orbit
G\cdot\bm{v}
coincide.

We say that
G
is
topologically generated by

S\subseteq\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
if
G
is the smallest Zariski-closed subgroup of

\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
that contains
S
, that is,
G=\overline{\langle S\rangle}
.
If
G
is topologically generated by a set with
s
elements then we say that is

G
is
s
-generated
.

Proposition 2.1.

Let
G\leq\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
be an algebraic group, then
G
is topologically generated by a finite set of matrices.

The proof of
Proposition

2.1
is given in
Appendix

B
.

Lattices and the Multiplicative Group
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
.

The
rank
of an abelian group
\Lambda
is the size of a maximal linearly independent subset
Lang, 2002
.
A subgroup
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is called a
lattice
(and has rank at most
d
).
The set
\mathbb{Z}^{d}/\Lambda
contains all cosets of
\Lambda
in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, that is,
sets of the form
\bm{g}+\Lambda=\{\bm{g}+\bm{v}:\bm{v}\in\Lambda\}
where
\bm{g}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Together with an additive operation, the set of cosets defines the
quotient group

\mathbb{Z}^{d}/\Lambda
.
The
torsion subgroup
of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}/\Lambda
is the subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}/\Lambda
consisting of all its elements of finite order.

The
d
-dimensional multiplicative group
over
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
is defined as

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}=\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})\coloneqq\left\{\bm{a}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}:a_{1}\cdots a_{d}\neq 0\right\}.

Here the subscript
m
stands for
multiplicative
.
Evidently, this is a commutative group with respect to pointwise multiplication.
We identify
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
with the subgroup of diagonal matrices in
\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
via the map
\Delta

that sends
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\in\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}

to the diagonal matrix
\Delta(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\in\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})

which has
a_{1},\dots,a_{d}
(in this order) on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere.

We can now explain two important ingredients of our results.
We have so far identified the invertible diagonal matrices with the algebraic group
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
.
Now, we define the necessary vocabulary to discuss the correspondence of algebraic subgroups of
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
and lattices in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
This comes in handy when we need to decide whether a certain subgroup of diagonal invertible matrices is
s
-generated, in which we reduce it to a lattice problem.

Given a lattice
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, define

H_{\Lambda}\coloneqq\{\bm{a}\in\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}:\kern 5.0pt\forall\bm{v}\in\Lambda.\;a_{1}^{v_{1}}\cdots a_{d}^{v_{d}}=1\}.

The map
\Lambda\mapsto H_{\Lambda}
is an isomorphism
between lattices and algebraic subgroups
of
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
.
This implies that
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
is topologically generated by any
d
-tuple
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{d})
of multiplicatively
independent elements of
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
.

For variables
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
, write
\bm{x}^{\bm{\lambda}}
for the monomial
\prod_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{\lambda_{i}}
. A
pure binomial ideal
is
an ideal generated by polynomials of the form
\bm{x}^{\bm{\lambda}}-\bm{x}^{\bm{\lambda^{\prime}}}
, where
\bm{\lambda}
and
\bm{\lambda^{\prime}}
are non-negative integer vectors. More generally, a
binomial ideal
is one that is generated by polynomials of the form
\bm{x}^{\bm{\lambda}}-\theta\bm{x}^{\bm{\lambda^{\prime}}}
, where
\theta\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
.
It is known that
the vanishing ideal

I\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[\bm{x}]
of an algebraic subgroup of

\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
is a pure binomial ideal.

Example 2.2.

Let
\Lambda\coloneqq\{(2n,-n):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}=(2,-1)\cdot\mathbb{Z}
be a lattice in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
of rank 1.

A vector
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is equivalent to
(a+2b,0)
modulo
\Lambda
. Hence
the quotient group
\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\Lambda
comprises a set of cosets
\{(c,0)+\Lambda:c\in\mathbb{Z}\}
with group operation defined by componentwise addition.
Thus
\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\Lambda
is isomorphic to the additive group
\mathbb{Z}
, which has trivial torsion subgroup since
0
is the only element of finite order.

We have

\displaystyle H_{\Lambda}=

\displaystyle\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{G}_{m}^{2}:\kern 5.0pt\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}.\;x^{2n}y^{-n}=1\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{G}_{m}^{2}:\kern 5.0ptx^{2}=y\}.

The vanishing ideal of
H_{\Lambda}
is pure binomial, generated by
x^{2}-y
.

\blacktriangleleft

The following proposition shows how to recover the generators of the lattice
and hence the pure binomial ideal that vanishes on an algebraic subgroup of
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}

defined by its equations.

Proposition 2.3 (
Bombieri & Gubler, 2006
, Proposition 3.2.14
).

Let
G
be a subgroup of
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
defined by polynomial equations
\sum_{\bm{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}_{i}}a_{i,\bm{\lambda}}\bm{x}^{\bm{\lambda}}=0
for
i=1,\ldots,m
, where

\mathcal{L}_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Then
G=H_{\Lambda}
, where
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is generated by
vectors of the form
\bm{\lambda}_{i}-\bm{\lambda^{\prime}}_{i}

with
\bm{\lambda}_{i},\bm{\lambda^{\prime}}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}_{i}
, for
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
.

The next proposition follows from a standard result in Diophantine geometry concerning the number of generators of a subgroup of
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
(cf.
Bombieri & Gubler, 2006
, Chapter 3
);
we give a proof in
Appendix

B
.

Proposition 2.4.

Let
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be a lattice of rank
r
. Then

(1)

the torsion subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}/\Lambda
is
s_{0}
-generated, for some
s_{0}\leq r
, and

(2)

H_{\Lambda}
is
s
-generated, where
s\coloneqq s_{0}
if
\Lambda
has full rank and otherwise
s\coloneqq\max(s_{0},1)
.
Furthermore,

is the minimal number of topological generators for
H_{\Lambda}
.

Unipotent Matrices.

For a
d\times d
unipotent matrix
A
and a
d\times d
nilpotent matrix
B
, define

\log(A)\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}(-1)^{k+1}\frac{(A-\textrm{Id}_{d})^{k}}{k}\quad\text{and}\quad\exp(B)\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\frac{B^{k}}{k!}.

Let
G\subseteq\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
be a commutative subgroup of unipotent matrices.
The set
L\coloneqq\{\log(A):A\in G\}
is a linear subspace of

\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d^{2}}

consisting of nilpotent matrices
Borel, 1991
, Chapter II, Section 7.3
. Moreover,
\exp\colon L\to G
and
\log\colon G\to L
yield polynomial isomorphisms between
L
and
G
as algebraic groups, with additive and multiplicative group structures respectively.
Taken together, these observations lead to the following proposition.

Proposition 2.5.

Let
G
be a commutative group
of unipotent matrices and
L\coloneqq\{\log(A):A\in G\}
the associated linear subspace of nilpotent matrices.
Then
G
has a topological generator of cardinality
s
if and only if
L
is spanned by a set of
s
matrices
as a
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
-vector space.

Proof.

For all
A_{1},\ldots,A_{s}\in G
we have the following equivalences:

\displaystyle\,\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{s}\}\text{ topologically generates }G,

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\,

\displaystyle\,\{A_{1}^{n_{1}}A_{2}^{n_{2}}\cdots A_{s}^{n_{s}}:n_{1},\ldots,n_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}\}\text{ is dense in }G,

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\,

\displaystyle\,\{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{s}n_{i}\log(A_{i}):n_{1},\ldots,n_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}\}\text{ is dense in }L,

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\,

\displaystyle\,\{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{s}t_{i}\log(A_{i}):t_{1},\ldots,t_{s}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}\}=L,

as desired.
∎

Example 2.6.

We consider

where

. Since

.

,

3.
Commutative Group Determination

Recall that the group determination problem with parameters
(s,d,m,b)
asks, given
s\in\mathbb{N}
and a family
of
m
polynomials in
\mathbb{Q}[\{x_{i,j}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq d}]
, each
of total degree at most
b
,
to determine whether their zero locus
Z\subseteq\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
is an
s
-generated algebraic matrix group.
In this section, we first demonstrate a procedure for this problem subject to the
constraint that the underlying group is semisimple and commutative (
Proposition

3.1
).
Next, we generalise this result by lifting the requirement that the matrices are semisimple (
Proposition

3.2
).

Proposition 3.1.

The group determination problem for commutative semisimple matrices with parameters
(s,d,m,b)
can be decided
in time

(mb)^{\mathrm{poly}(d)}
, and in space bounded by
(d\log{b})^{O(1)}
.

Proof.

Recall that the input to the problem consists of
m
polynomials, each of total degree at most
b
, together with a natural number
s
. Let
Z
be the subvariety of
\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
defined by the input polynomials.
The task is to determine whether
Z
is a group that is topologically generated by at most
s
commutative semisimple matrices.

Suppose that the input is a positive instance of the problem, meaning that

Z
is an algebraic group that is topologically generated by
commutative semisimple matrices
M_{1},\ldots,M_{s}\in\GL_{d}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
.
Recall that commutative semisimple matrices are simultaneously diagonalisable
Horn & Johnson, 2012
, Theorem 1.3.21
.
Thus there exists
P\in\GL_{d}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})

such that
D_{i}\coloneqq P^{-1}M_{i}P
are diagonal.
Let
G
be the subgroup of

\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
defined by

G\coloneqq\{g\in\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}:\Delta(g)\in\overline{\langle D_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle}\}\,.

Since

Z=\overline{\langle M_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle}
, we have

P^{-1}ZP=\{\Delta(g):g\in G\}
.
Moreover, as

Z
is the zero set of polynomials of total degree at most
b
and conjugation by the linear transformation
P
does not increase the total degrees of the transformed polynomials, it holds that

P^{-1}ZP
is the zero set
of polynomials of degree at most
b
.
Hence,
by
Proposition

2.3
,
the group
G
has the form
H_{\Lambda}
for some lattice
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}

whose generators have norm at
most
b
and such that
H_{\Lambda}
is
s
-generated.

Conversely, for every such lattice
\Lambda
, the variety
P\{\Delta(g):g\in H_{\Lambda}\}P^{-1}
is topologically
s
-generated by commutative semisimple matrices.

The above reasoning shows the correctness of the following nondeterministic decision
procedure:

(1)

Guess a lattice
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
whose generators have norm at most
b
such that
H_{\Lambda}
is
s
-generated.

(2)

Output "yes" if there exists
P\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
such that

P^{-1}ZP=\{\Delta(g):g\in H_{\Lambda}\}
.

By
Proposition

2.4
, Step 1 amounts to guessing
\Lambda
subject to the condition that the torsion subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}/\Lambda
is generated by at most
s
elements.
We can understand this observation in terms of the number of non-unit elementary divisors of the desired lattice
\Lambda
; see
Appendix

B
for more details.

Step 2 amounts to checking the truth in
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
of the sentence

\exists P\in\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})\;\forall A=(a_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\in\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})\;\\
\left(P^{-1}AP\in Z\Leftrightarrow\textstyle\bigwedge_{i\neq j}a_{ij}=0\wedge(a_{11},a_{22},\ldots,a_{dd})\in H_{\Lambda}\right),

with respect to the theory of algebraically closed fields.
By
Theorem

1.3
, this can be done in time
(mb)^{\mathrm{poly}(d)}
.
The claimed running time for the overall procedure follows from
the fact that the number of possibilities for the lattice
\Lambda
is at most
(2b)^{d^{2}}
.
∎

By the following remark, a commutative algebraic group
G
is
s
-generated if its unipotent subgroup
G_{u}
and semisimple subgroup
G_{s}
are both
s
-generated.

Remark 3.1.

Let
U=\overline{\langle U_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle}
be a commutative unipotent
s
-generated algebraic group and
S=\overline{\langle S_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle}
a semisimple commutative
s
-generated algebraic group. Suppose moreover that the matrices
\{U_{i},S_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\}
are commutative.
Then we have

\overline{\langle U_{i},S_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle}=\overline{\langle S_{i}U_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle}
.
This relies on the fact that if

A\in\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
is semisimple and

B\in\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
is unipotent, then both
A
and
B
lie in the Zariski closure of the subgroup generated by their product
AB
; see
Humphreys, 1975
, Section 15.3
.

The next proposition generalises the procedure described in
Proposition

3.1
.
In
Proposition

3.2
we consider the group determination problem for
s
-generated commutative algebraic groups.
Key to our generalisation is the determination of a matrix
P\in\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
and properties associated with the semisimple and unipotent subgroups of the group
P^{-1}ZP
.

Proposition 3.2.

The group determination problem for commutative matrices with parameters
(s,d,m,b)
can be decided in time

(mb)^{\mathrm{poly}(d)}
, and in space bounded by
(d\log{b})^{O(1)}
.

Proof.

Recall that the input to the problem consists of
m
polynomials, each of total degree at most
b
, together with a natural number
s
. Let
Z
be the subvariety of
\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
defined by the input polynomials.
The task is to determine whether
Z
is a group that is topologically generated by at most
s
commutative matrices.

Suppose that the input is a positive instance of the problem, that is,

Z
is an algebraic group that is topologically generated by
commutative matrices
M_{1},\ldots,M_{s}\in\GL_{d}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
.
Recall that commutative matrices are simultaneously triangularisable
Horn & Johnson, 2012
, Theorem 2.3.3
.
Thus there exists
P\in\GL_{d}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})

such that
there exist diagonal matrices
D_{i}
and
upper unitriangular matrices
U_{i}
,

1\leq i\leq s
, where

P^{-1}M_{i}P=D_{i}U_{i}
, and moreover

D_{i}
and
U_{i}
commute.
Then we can recover
\overline{\langle D_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle}
as the set of diagonal matrices in
P^{-1}\,\overline{\langle M_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle}\,P
.
As in
Proposition

3.1
,
it follows that

\overline{\langle D_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle}
is the zero locus of a system of polynomials of degree at most
b
.

By
Proposition

2.3
,

\overline{\langle D_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle}=\{\Delta(g):g\in H_{\Lambda}\}

for some lattice
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
that is generated by vectors having supremum norm at most
b
and
H_{\Lambda}
is
s
-generated.
Note that

\overline{\langle U_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle}
is the set of upper unitriangular matrices in

P^{-1}\,\overline{\langle M_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle}\,P
.

The decision procedure is as follows:

(1)

Guess a lattice
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
whose generators have norm at most
b
and such that
H_{\Lambda}
is
s
-generated.

(2)

Output "yes" if there exists
P\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})

such that

(a)

G\coloneqq P^{-1}ZP
is a commutative group of upper triangular matrices;

(b)

\{A\in G:A\text{ diagonal}\}=\{\Delta(g):g\in H_{\Lambda}\}
; and

(c)

\{\log(A):A\in G,\;A\text{ unipotent}\}

is a linear variety of dimension at most
s
.

As in
Proposition

3.1
, guessing
\Lambda
that satisfies the condition of
Item

1

is via
Proposition

2.4
.
We claim that the procedure outputs "yes" if and only if the input is a positive instance of the problem.
Indeed,
Item

2a
checks that
G
is a commutative algebraic matrix group. In this case, by
Section

2
, both the set
G_{s}
of semisimple matrices in
G
and the set
G_{u}
of unipotent matrices in
G
form subgroups of
G
.
Next,
Items

2b
and
2c
respectively check that

G_{s}
and
G_{u}
are
s
-generated (relying on
Propositions

2.4
and
2.5
).
This in turn implies that
G
is itself
s
-generated, as noted in
Section

3
.

The existence of
P

satisfying
Items

2a
,
2b
and
2c
reduces to checking the truth in
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
of an
\exists^{*}\forall^{*}
-sentence in the language of fields. The existential quantifiers correspond to the possible choices of
P
, while the universal quantifiers range over entries of the group
G
defined in
Item

2a
. For a fixed choice of
\Lambda
, the truth of such a formula can be decided in time
(mb)^{\mathrm{poly}(d)}
by
Theorem

1.3
.
Given that the number of possible choices of the lattice
\Lambda
is at most
(2b)^{d^{2}}
the claimed complexity bound immediately follows.
∎

4.
Orbit-Closure Determination

Recall the aforementioned orbit-closure determination problem. The problem asks, given
s\in\mathbb{N}
and a family
of
m
polynomials in
\mathbb{Q}[\{x_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq d}]
, each of total degree at most
b
, to determine whether
their zero locus
Z\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
is
the orbit closure of some point
\bm{v}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
under the action of an
s
-generated algebraic matrix group, i.e.,
determine whether there exists an algebraic group topologically generated by matrices

M_{1},\ldots,M_{s}\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
and vector

\bm{v}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
such that

Z
is the
Zariski closure of the orbit

\{M_{1}^{\ell_{1}}\cdots M_{s}^{\ell_{s}}\bm{v}:\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.

Key to the main result in this section (
Theorem

1.4
) is
Lemma

4.1
.
Briefly, the lemma describes a change of basis on a given commutative matrix group
G
and vector
\bm{v}
such that in this new basis the elements of the semisimple subgroup
G_{s}\leq G
are diagonal, the elements of the unipotent subgroup
G_{u}\leq G
are upper unitriangular; and moreover
\bm{v}
is mapped to a binary vector with a prescribed structure.
In fact, the lemma permits us to focus on
the orbit closure of the vector
\bm{1}
(that is, the all-one vector).

Lemma 4.1.

Let
G=\overline{\langle M_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle}\leq\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}})
be a
commutative algebraic group and
\bm{v}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
. There exist
P\in\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}})
,
and
T\in\{0,1\}^{d\times k}
with the following properties:

(1)

the elements of
PGP^{-1}
consist of block diagonal matrices, where each block is a scalar multiple of a unitriangular matrix;

(2)

PG_{s}P^{-1}
consists of diagonal matrices and
PG_{u}P^{-1}
consists of upper unitriangular matrices,

(3)

T^{\mathsf{T}}T=\operatorname{Id}_{k}
,

(4)

P\bm{v}=T\bm{1}
is a
0
-
1
vector with at most one
1
per block.

Proof.

The proof of
Lemma

4.1
relies on

Theorem

4.2
and
Corollary

4.3
in Section
4.1
, immediately below.
These results show how to find
P
satisfying Item (1) such that

P\bm{v}=\bm{e}_{i_{1}}+\cdots+\bm{e}_{i_{k}}
is a sum of standard unit vectors of
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
.
Defining
T
to be the
d\times k
matrix with columns
\bm{e}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\bm{e}_{i_{k}}
,
it follows that
T^{\mathsf{T}}T=\mathrm{Id}_{k}
and
P\bm{v}=T\bm{1}
.

∎

4.1.
A Convenient Base Change for Commuting Matrices

This section contains technical details concerning the proof of
Lemma

4.1
.

Theorem 4.2.

Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
be pairwise commuting matrices in
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d\times d}
, and

\bm{v}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
a non-zero vector.
There exists
P\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
such that

•

PA_{i}P^{-1}
is block diagonal;

•

each block is a
scalar multiple of an upper unitriangular matrix;

•

P\bm{v}

is a 0-1 vector with at most one 1
per block.

Proof.

By
Newman, 1967
, Theorem 1
, there exists
P\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
such that
PA_{i}P^{-1}
is upper triangular for
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
.
We have the following easy refinement of this well-known fact.

Claim 4.2.

There exists
P\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
such that
PA_{i}P^{-1}
is upper triangular for
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
and
P\bm{v}=\bm{e}_{j}
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.

Proof of
Section

4.1
.

As mentioned above, by
Newman, 1967
, Theorem 1
, there exists
P\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
such that
PA_{i}P^{-1}
is upper triangular for
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
.
This yields a basis

\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{d}
of

\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
such that

A_{i}\bm{v}_{j}\in\mathrm{span}(\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{j})
for all
i
and
j
.
Let
\ell
be the minimum index such that

\bm{v}\in\mathrm{span}(\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{\ell})
. Then

\bm{v}=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}\bm{v}_{i}
, where

\alpha_{\ell}\neq 0
by minimality of
\ell
. Hence

\bm{v}_{\ell}\in\mathrm{span}(\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{\ell-1},\bm{v})
.
Consider a new basis in which
\bm{v}_{\ell}
is replaced by

\bm{v}
.
Note that
\mathrm{span}(\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{\ell})=\mathrm{span}(\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{\ell-1},\bm{v})
.

Taking
Q
to be the matrix with columns

\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{\ell-1},\bm{v},\bm{v}_{\ell+1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n}
we have that

Q\bm{e}_{\ell}=\bm{v}
. Let
P\coloneqq Q^{-1}
then we have that
PA_{i}P^{-1}
is upper
triangular for
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. This concludes the proof of the claim.
∎

By
Ouaknine et al., 2019
, Theorem 2.4

we can write
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{k}
such that
each subspace
V_{i}
is invariant under
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
and
for all
1\leq i\leq m
and
1\leq j\leq k
the restriction of
A_{i}

to
V_{j}
has a single eigenvalue.
Let
\bm{v}=\bm{v}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{v}_{k}
be the
corresponding decomposition of
\bm{v}
.

Fix a subspace
V_{j}
. We apply Claim
4.1

to the restrictions of
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
to
V_{j}
and the vector

\bm{v}_{j}
. We obtain a basis for
V_{j}
that includes
\bm{v}_{j}

and such that the restriction of
A_{i}
to
V_{j}
is upper triangular.
∎

The next corollary follows from
Theorem

4.2
.

Corollary 4.3.

Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
be matrices in
\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
and let

P\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})

be such that
PA_{i}P^{-1}
is block diagonal where each block is the
scalar multiple of an upper unitriangular matrix.
Given
M\in\langle A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}\rangle
,
M
is semi-simple if and
only if
PMP^{-1}
is diagonal and
M
is unipotent if and only if

PMP^{-1}
is upper unitriangular.

Proof.

Clearly
PMP^{-1}
is block diagonal. Thus

M
is semisimple if and only if it is
blockwise semisimple and it is unipotent if and only if it is
blockwise unipotent. Moreover, since each block is upper triangular
with constant diagonal, such a block is diagonalisable if and only if it is
already diagonal and is unipotent if and only if it is has all ones along the diagonal.
∎

4.2.
Decision Procedures for the Orbit-Closure Determination

The main contributions of this section are the procedures for certain cases of the orbit-closure determination problem (
Propositions

4.4
and
1.4
).
The procedure in

4.4

The procedure in

Proposition 4.4.

The orbit-closure determination problem for commutative semisimple matrices with parameters
(s,d,m,b)
can be decided in time

(mb)^{\mathrm{poly}(d)}
, and in space bounded by
(d\log{b})^{O(1)}
.

Proof.

Recall that the input to the problem consists of
m
polynomials, each of total degree at most
b
, together with a natural number
s
. Let
Z
be the subvariety of
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
defined by the input polynomials.
The task is to determine whether
Z
is an orbit-closure under a group that is generated by at most
s
commutative semisimple matrices.

Suppose that the input is a positive instance of the problem, that is,

Z=\overline{\langle M_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle\cdot\bm{v}}
for
semisimple commutative matrices
M_{1},\ldots,M_{s}\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
and a vector

\bm{v}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
.
By
Lemma

4.1
, there exist matrices

P\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
and

T\in\{0,1\}^{d\times k}

such that
D_{i}\coloneqq PM_{i}P^{-1}
are diagonal, and moreover

T^{\mathsf{T}}T=\mathrm{Id}_{k}

and
P\bm{v}=T\bm{1}
hold.

For all
i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
, denote by
D^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathrm{GL}_{k}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
the diagonal matrix uniquely defined by the requirement that
D_{i}T=TD^{\prime}_{i}
.
Write

G\coloneqq\left\{g\in\mathbb{G}_{m}^{k}:\Delta(g)\in\overline{\langle D^{\prime}_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle}\right\}
.
Then we have

PZ=\overline{\langle D_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle\cdot T\bm{1}}=\overline{T\langle D^{\prime}_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle\cdot\bm{1}}=\overline{\{Tg:g\in G\}}.

Note moreover that by the definition of

we have

G=\left\{g\in\mathbb{G}_{m}^{k}:Tg\in PZ\right\}

and so

is defined by polynomials of total degree at most
b
.
It follows from
Proposition

2.3
that
G=H_{\Lambda}
for some lattice
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}
that is generated by vectors whose entries have absolute value at most
b
.

Conversely, suppose that

PZ=\overline{\left\{Tg:g\in H_{\Lambda}\right\}}

for some matrices
P\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
and
T\in\{0,1\}^{d\times k}
such that
T^{\mathsf{T}}T=\mathrm{Id}_{k}
, and
lattice
\Lambda
as above. Then

Z=\overline{\langle M_{i}:1\leq i\leq s\rangle\cdot\bm{v}},

where
\bm{v}=P^{-1}T\bm{1}
and

M_{i}\coloneqq P^{-1}D_{i}P

with

D_{i}\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
being any diagonal matrices such that
D_{i}T=T\Delta(g_{i})
for some topological generators
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\}
of
H_{\Lambda}
.

In summary, the decision procedure is as follows:

(1)

Guess
k\subseteq\{0,\ldots,d\}
and
T\in\{0,1\}^{d\times k}
such that
T^{\mathsf{T}}T=\mathrm{Id}_{k}
.

(2)

Guess a lattice
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}
whose generators have norm at most
b
and such that
H_{\Lambda}
is
s
-generated (see
Proposition

2.4
).

(3)

Determine whether there exists
P\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
such that

PZ=\overline{\left\{Tg:g\in H_{\Lambda}\right\}}
.

Step 3 can be reduced in polynomial time to checking the truth of a
\exists^{*}\forall^{*}\exists^{*}
-sentence in the theory of
real closed fields. The outermost existential quantifiers correspond to the choice of the matrix
P
. Then
the right-to-left inclusion in the equation

PZ=\overline{\left\{Tg:g\in H_{\Lambda}\right\}}

is expressed by the formula

\forall g\in H_{\Lambda}\,\exists\bm{z}\in Z\,(P\bm{z}=Tg),

while, by
Section

2
,
the left-to-right inclusion
is expressed by the formula

\forall\bm{z}\in Z\,\forall\varepsilon>0\,\exists g\in H_{\Lambda}\,\left(\|P\bm{z}-Tg\|<\varepsilon\right).

By
Theorem

1.3
, the truth of such a sentence can be decided in time
(mb)^{\mathrm{poly}(d)}
.
The claimed overall complexity bound now follows from the fact that there are at most
(2b)^{d^{2}+1}
choices of the lattice

\Lambda
and matrix
U
.

5.
Algorithms to Compute Generators

In
Section

3
we gave algorithms
to determine whether a given variety is the Zariski closure of a commutative matrix group. These procedures
can also be used to find a set of generators of such a group, by quantifier elimination in the theory of algebraically closed fields.
In this section, we describe specialised algorithms that can be directly implemented using standard computer-algebra software. The cases we consider compute
a generator when the input variety is the Zariski closure of a cyclic group.
These algorithms rely on Gröbner-basis techniques.
The first algorithm finds a semisimple generator, if one exists, while the second algorithm finds a generator in the general case.

The ideal

is

f,g\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[\bm{x}]
implies that
f\in I
or
g^{n}\in I
for some
n\in\mathbb{N},

and it is
prime
if it satisfies the stronger condition that

fg\in I
only if
f\in I
or
g\in I
.
Recall that the radical of a primary ideal is necessarily prime.

A polynomial ideal
I
can be written as the intersection of primary ideals, giving the so-called
primary decomposition
of
I
. It is known that there exists a unique irredundant primary decomposition
I=\bigcap_{i=1}^{\ell}Q_{i}
, that is, a finite set
\{Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{\ell}\}
of primary ideals such that

•

the prime ideals
\sqrt{Q_{i}}
are all distinct; and

•

\bigcap_{i\neq j}Q_{i}\not\subseteq Q_{j}
holds for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
.

The prime ideals, the
\sqrt{Q_{i}}
’s, are called the
associated primes
of
I
.
An associated prime
\sqrt{Q}
of the ideal
I
is
called
minimal
if it does not contain any other
associated primes of
I
.

Both algorithms take as input a variety
Z
, given as the zero set of an ideal
I\subseteq\mathbb{Q}[X]
,
X=\{x_{i,j}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq d}
.
We will assume
a priori
that
Z
is a commutative subgroup of
\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
.
Verifying that
Z
is commutative entails first checking that
Z
is
closed under matrix multiplication (which implies closure under matrix inversion), which amounts to showing
that

F(XY)\in\sqrt{I(X)+I(Y)}

for all
polynomials
F
in
I
,
where
X=\{x_{i,j}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq d}
,
Y=\{y_{i,j}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq d}
.
Commutativity is captured by showing that

XY-YX\in\sqrt{I(X)+I(Y)}\,.

5.1.
Semisimple Generator

In the following we describe a procedure that, given an ideal

I\subseteq\mathbb{Q}[X]
,

X=\{x_{i,j}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq d}
,
determines whether there exists a
semisimple matrix

M\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})

such that
I
is the vanishing ideal of the
group
\langle M\rangle

and which moreover outputs such an
M
in case the answer is "yes".
We show that if such an
M
exists then it can be chosen such that its eigenvalues lie in the number field
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{q})
, where
q
is the number of minimal associated primes of
I
and
\zeta_{q}
is a primitive
q
th root of unity.

5.2.
General Generator

We employ the algorithm from the previous subsection to provide a procedure that, given an
algebraic set
Z\subseteq\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
determines whether
there exists a matrix

M\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})

such that
Z=\overline{\langle M\rangle}

and which moreover outputs such an
M
in the affirmative case.

Let the input ideal
I
be generated by a finite collection of polynomials
F_{1},\ldots,F_{k}\in\mathbb{Q}[X]
with
q
minimal associated primes. Write
Z\coloneqq V(I)
for the zero locus of
I
that is, by assumption, a commutative linear algebraic group.
Our algorithm first calls a (modified variant of) the procedure in
Figure

1
, with the input ideal
I
,
to check whether the subgroup
G_{s}
of all semisimple matrices in
Z

is one-generated.
The modification is as follows:
(1) the assertion in
Line 10
is omitted (as this assertion requires that
Z
is generated with a single semisimple matrix), and (2) the algorithm outputs
D_{q}
and the ideal
J_{1}
defining the locus point of a suitable
P
.

After the above subprocedure returns, our algorithm proceeds by verifying that the subgroup
G_{u}
of all unipotent matrices is one-generated. For this purpose, it checks

•

whether
V(I+\langle(X-\operatorname{Id}_{d})^{n}\rangle)
is a commutative linear algebraic group; and

•

whether
V(I+\langle(X-\operatorname{Id}_{d})^{n}\rangle)
is one-dimensional.

The algorithm returns "no" if either of the subgroups
G_{s}
or
G_{u}

is not one-generated. Otherwise, the procedure defines
the ideal
H\subseteq\mathbb{Q}[P,X,Q]
by

H\coloneqq\langle\,F_{1}(PXQ),\,\ldots,\,F_{k}(PXQ),\,PQ-\operatorname{Id}_{d},\,\{x_{i,j}\}_{j\neq i,i+1}\,\rangle

where

P=(p_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq d},\qquad X=\{x_{i,j}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\qquad\text{and }\qquad Q=(q_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\,.

It returns "yes" together with the matrix
\widetilde{P}D_{q}\widetilde{X}\widetilde{P}^{-1}
where
(\widetilde{P},\widetilde{X})\in J+H
.

Appendix A
Extended Background

Here we give an extended preliminary section, which collects together background material and definitions relevant to the paper.

A.1.
Ideals and Varieties

Let
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
denote the field of algebraic numbers and write
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
for the
ring of polynomials with coefficients in
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
over the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
.
A polynomial ideal
I
is an additive subgroup of
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]

that is closed under multiplication in
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
.
Given a finite collection of polynomials
S\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
, we denote by
\langle S\rangle
the ideal generated by
S
.

We briefly describe useful algebraic operations for ideals.
The
sum
of
I
and
J
, which we denote by
I+J
, is the ideal

I+J\coloneqq\{f+g:f\in I\text{ and }g\in J\}.

The sum
I+J
is the smallest ideal containing both
I
and
J
and, in addition, if
I=\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\rangle
and
J=\langle g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\rangle
, then
I+J=\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{r},g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\rangle
.
The
product
of
I
and
J
, which we denote by
IJ
, is the ideal given by

IJ=\left\{\textstyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}f_{\ell}g_{\ell}:f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\in I,g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\in J,r\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

The
intersection

I\cap J
is the set of polynomials common to both
I
and
J
.
Given an ideal
I\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[X]
, where
X=\{x_{i,j}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq d}
, and matrix
M\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d\times d}
, we write

M\cdot I
for the ideal
\{f(MX)\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[X]:f\in I\}
.

An
algebraic set
(or
variety
) is the set of common zeroes of a finite collection of polynomials.
By Hilbert’s basis theorem every polynomial ideal
I\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
is finitely generated.
Thus the set

V(I)\coloneqq\{\bm{a}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}:f(\bm{a})=0\text{ for all }f\in I\}

is a variety.
As an aside, the varieties
V(I+J)
and
V(IJ)
are easily shown to be equal to
V(I)\cap V(J)
and
V(I)\cup V(J)
, respectively.
Taking, as above,
M\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d\times d}
, we have

V(M\cdot I)=\{A\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d\times d}:MA\in V(I)\}.

Let
I\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
be an ideal and
denote by
\sqrt{I}
the
radical
of
I
, defined as

\sqrt{I}\coloneqq\{f\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]\mid f^{n}\in I\text{ for some }n\in\mathbb{N}\}.

By Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz, the ideal of all polynomials that vanish on
V(I)\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
is
\sqrt{I}
.

A polynomial ideal
I
is
principal
if there exists a polynomial
f
for which
\langle f\rangle=I
.
The ideal
I
is
primary
if for all
f,g\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
, if
fg\in I
then
f\in I
or
g^{n}\in I
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and it is
prime
if it satisfies the stronger condition that

fg\in I
only if
f\in I
or
g\in I
.
Recall that the radical of a primary ideal is necessarily prime.

A polynomial ideal
I
can be written as the intersection of primary ideals, giving the so-called
primary decomposition
of
I
. It is known that there exists a unique irredundant primary decomposition
I=\bigcap_{i=1}^{\ell}Q_{i}
, that is, a finite set
\{Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{\ell}\}
of primary ideals such that
(1) the prime ideals
\sqrt{Q_{i}}
are all distinct; and (2)
\bigcap_{i\neq j}Q_{i}\not\subseteq Q_{j}
holds for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
.
The prime ideals, the
\sqrt{Q_{i}}
’s, are called the
associated primes
of
I
.
An associated prime
\sqrt{Q}
of the ideal
I
is
called
minimal
if it does not contain any other
associated primes of
I
.

Let
k
be a field.
Consider a system of
m
equations in
d
variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
with coefficients in
k
:

\displaystyle a_{11}x_{1}+a_{12}x_{2}+\cdots+a_{1d}x_{d}

\displaystyle=b_{1}

\displaystyle a_{21}x_{1}+a_{22}x_{2}+\cdots+a_{2d}x_{d}

\displaystyle=b_{2}

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{{}={}}][c]{\vdots}

\displaystyle a_{m1}x_{1}+a_{m2}x_{2}+\cdots+a_{md}x_{d}

\displaystyle=b_{m}.

We call the solution set for such a system a
linear variety
.
The
dimension
of a non-empty linear variety is the number of independent equations in its definition.

A.2.
The Zariski Topology

The
Zariski topology
on

\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
has as its closed sets
the varieties in
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
.
Given a set
E\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
, we denote by
\overline{E}
the closure of
E
in the Zariski topology, i.e.,
the smallest algebraic set that contains
E
.
A closed set
A\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
is
irreducible
if it cannot be written as the union of two closed proper subsets. A maximal irreducible closed subset of
A
is called
an
irreducible component
of
A
.
By Hilbert’s basis theorem every closed set
A
can be written as a finite union of its irreducible components.

Given a closed set
E
, denote by
\dim E
the dimension of the variety
E
, that is,
the maximal length of a strictly decreasing chain of nonempty irreducible subvarieties of
E
. We define the dimension of an arbitrary set to be the dimension of
its closure.

A.3.
Linear Algebraic Groups

The general linear group
\GL_{d}(\mathbb{F})
is the group of all
d\times d
invertible matrices with entries in a given field
\mathbb{F}
.
The orbit closure of
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
, denoted by
\overline{G\cdot\bm{v}}
, is
the closure of
G\cdot\bm{v}
in the Zariski topology.
Recall that
the closed sets of the Zariski topology are algebraic sets–that is, sets of common zeros of a finite collection of polynomials.

Recall
that a matrix
M\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d\times d}
is

nilpotent
if
M^{n}=0
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
It is

unipotent
if
M-\textrm{Id}_{d}
is nilpotent, and

semisimple
if it is diagonalisable over
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
.
A matrix
M\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d\times d}
is called

upper triangular
if all entries below the main diagonal are zero.
We use the term
upper unitriangular
to refer to an upper triangular matrix whose entries along the main diagonal are all ones.

Write
\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})

for the group of
d\times d
invertible matrices with entries in
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
.
We identify
\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
with the variety

\bigl\{(M,y)\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d\times d}\times\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}:\det(M)\cdot y=1\bigr\}
.
Under this identification, matrix multiplication is a polynomial map
\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})\times\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})\rightarrow\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
, and, by Cramer’s rule, matrix inversion is also a polynomial map
\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})\rightarrow\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
.
A
linear algebraic group

G
is a Zariski-closed subgroup of
\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
.
Given a linear algebraic group
G
, it is well-known that it has a unique irreducible component that contains the identity matrix, which we denote by
G^{\circ}
.
The cosets of
G^{\circ}
are the irreducible components of
G
.

We say that
G
is
topologically generated by

S\subseteq\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
if
G
is the smallest Zariski closed subgroup of

\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
that contains
S
, that is,
G=\overline{\langle S\rangle}
.
If
G
is topologically generated by a set with
s
elements then we say that is

G
is
s
-generated
.
Denote by
G_{s}
the subset of semisimple matrices in
G
, and by
G_{u}
the subset of unipotent matrices.
If
G
is a commutative algebraic group then
G_{s}
and
G_{u}
form algebraic subgroups; moreover we have
G=G_{u}\cdot G_{s}
.

The
d
-dimensional multiplicative group
over
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}=\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})\coloneqq\left\{\bm{a}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}:a_{1}\cdots a_{d}\neq 0\right\}.

Here the subscript
m
stands for
multiplicative
.
Evidently this is a commutative group with respect to pointwise multiplication.

We identify
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
with the subgroup of diagonal matrices in
\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
via the map
\Delta

that sends
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\in\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}

to the diagonal matrix
\Delta(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\in\GL_{d}(\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\vphantom{\mathbb{Q}})
.

Given a subgroup
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, define

H_{\Lambda}\coloneqq\{\bm{a}\in\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}:\forall\bm{v}\in\Lambda\,(a_{1}^{v_{1}}\cdots a_{d}^{v_{d}}=1)\}.

The map

This implies that

A.4.
Lattices

The

of an abelian group

is the size of a maximal linearly independent subset

.
A subgroup

The

A.5.
Modules over Principal Ideal Domains

Let
R
be a commutative ring. A
module

M
is an additive abelian group together with an operation
R\times M\to M
such that for all
r,s\in R
and
x,y\in M
we have

(r+s)x=rx+sx\quad\text{and}\quad r(x+y)=rx+ry.

By definition of an operation, we have
1x=x
for all
x\in M
.
A module over
\mathbb{Z}
is abelian group and, vice versa, an abelian group is a module over
\mathbb{Z}
.

Let
M
be a module over a ring
R
and let
S\subseteq M
.
The set
S
is a
basis
of
M
if
S
is non-empty,
S
generates
M
, and the elements of
S
are linearly independent.
A
free module
is a module which admits a basis, or the zero module.
If
S
is a basis of a non-zero module
M
, then every element of
M
can be uniquely expressed as a linear combination of elements of
S
.

An
integral domain
is a non-zero commutative ring where the product of two non-zero elements is itself non-zero.
A
principal ideal domain
is an integral domain wherein every ideal is principal. Examples of principal ideal domains include
\mathbb{Z}
and the rings
\mathbb{F}[x]
, the univariate polynomials with coefficients in the field
\mathbb{F}
.

Broadly speaking, a finitely generated module over a principal ideal domain is given by a direct sum of cyclic modules (i.e., modules with a single generator).
One formulation of this structural result is the Elementary Divisors Theorem, see, for example,
Lang, 2002
.

Theorem A.1 (Elementary Divisors Theorem).

Let
F
be a free module over a principal ideal domain
R
and
M
a non-trivial finitely generated submodule.
Then there exists a basis of
F
, elements
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}
in this basis, and non-zero elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\in R
such that

(1)

a_{1}e_{1},\ldots,a_{m}e_{m}
form a basis of
M
over
R
, and

(2)

a_{i}\mid a_{i+1}
for
i=1,\ldots,m-1
.
