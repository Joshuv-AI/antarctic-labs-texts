Fay Identities of Pfaffian Type for Hyperelliptic Curves
Gaëtan BOROT a and Thomas BUC-D’ALCHÉ b

Abstract
We establish identities of Pfaffian type for the theta function associated with twice or half the period matrix of a hyperelliptic curve. They are implied by the large size asymptotic analysis of exact Pfaffian identities for expectation values of ratios of characteristic polynomials in ensembles of orthogonal or quaternionic self-dual random matrices. We show that they amount to identities for the theta function with the period matrix of a hyperelliptic curve, and in this form we reprove them by direct geometric methods.

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathring{S}\qquad U^{\prime}(x)=V^{\prime}(x)-W_{1}(x+{\rm i}0)-W_{1}(x-{\rm i}0)=0.

(2.6)

Remark 2.5
.

For the equilibrium measure in the unconstrained case, Theorem
2.1
says there exists a constant
c
such that

for all

. In view of (
2.6
), the latter property is equivalent to

\forall h\in[g]\qquad\int_{b_{h-1}}^{a_{h}}M(x)s(x)\,\differential x=0.

2.4
Determinantal and Pfaffian
formulae

Expectation values of ratios of
characteristic polynomials, also called kernels, are quantities of interest in random matrix theory. Let us introduce the notation

\left\langle{\cdot}\right\rangle^{V}_{N}
for the expectation value with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{N}^{V}
(the value of
\beta
will be specified in each case), and
\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\lambda})
. Given

x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in\mathbb{C}
with the condition
x_{j}\notin A
if

, the
m

\Biggl\langle\prod_{j=1}^{m}\det(x_{j} - \Lambda)^{c_{j}}\Biggr\rangle_{N}^{V}=\Biggl\langle\prod_{j=1}^{m}\prod_{i=1}^{N}(x_{j}-\lambda_{i})^{c_{j}}\Biggr\rangle_{N}^{V}.

In
[
7
]
, Borodin and Strahov derive formulae
to compute the kernels. In what follows, we will always consider the “balanced” case, that
is, when there are as many characteristic polynomials in the numerator
as in the denominator. Given two tuples of complex numbers
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m_{1}})
and

\bm{\tilde{x}}=(\tilde{x}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{x}_{m_{2}})
, we write

\Delta(\bm{x},\bm{\tilde{x}})=\prod_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\prod_{j=1}^{m_{2}}(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{j}).

3.1
Theta functions

Let us recall the definition and properties of the theta function.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\bm{\tau}
be a complex
g\times g
symmetric matrix such that
\imaginary\bm{\tau}
is positive definite. The
theta function with characteristics

\bm{\mu},\bm{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}^{g}
is the function defined by

\forall\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{g}\qquad\vartheta_{\bm{\mu},\bm{\nu}}(\bm{z}|\bm{\tau})=\sum_{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{g}}\exp({\rm i}\pi(\bm{n}+\bm{\mu})\cdot\bm{\tau}(\bm{n}+\bm{\mu}) + 2{\rm i}\pi(\bm{n}+\bm{\mu})\cdot(\bm{z} + \bm{\nu})).

We set
\theta:=\vartheta_{\bm{0},\bm{0}}
.

The condition
\imaginary\bm{\tau}>0
ensures that the function is well
defined. Let us define the period lattice associated to
\bm{\tau}
as
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{Z}^{g}\oplus\bm{\tau}(\mathbb{Z}^{g})
. The theta function is quasi-periodic: for
\bm{m},\bm{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{g}
we have

\bm{m}+\bm{\tau}(\bm{n})\in\mathbb{L}
, and for any
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{g}

\vartheta_{\bm{\mu},\bm{\nu}}(\bm{z}+\bm{m}+\bm{\tau}(\bm{n})|\bm{\tau})={\rm e}^{2{\rm i}\pi\bm{m}\cdot\bm{\mu}-{\rm i}\pi\bm{n}\cdot(\bm{\tau}(\bm{n})+2\bm{z}+2\bm{\nu})}\vartheta_{\bm{\mu},\bm{\nu}}(\bm{z}|\bm{\tau}).

Definition 3.2
.

An odd half-integer characteristic is
\bm{c}=\frac{1}{2}\bm{e}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\tau}(\bm{e}^{\prime})
, with
\bm{e},\bm{e}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{g}
such that
\bm{e}\cdot\bm{e}^{\prime}\in 2\mathbb{Z}+1
.

By direct computation, if
\bm{c}
is a odd half-integer characteristic, then
\theta(\bm{c}|\bm{\tau})=0
.

3.2
Geometry of Riemann surfaces

3.2.1
Basis of cycles and forms

Let
\widehat{C}
be a compact Riemann surface of genus
g
. The first
homology group
H_{1}(\widehat{C};\mathbb{Z})
has a basis
(\mathcal{A}_{h},\mathcal{B}_{h})_{h=1}^{g}
which can be chosen to have the following properties under the intersection pairing

\forall h,k\in[g]\qquad\mathcal{A}_{h}\cap\mathcal{A}_{k}=0,\qquad\mathcal{B}_{h}\cap\mathcal{B}_{k}=0,\qquad\mathcal{A}_{h}\cap\mathcal{B}_{k}=\delta_{h,k}.

Such a basis is called a symplectic basis of homology, and
\widehat{C}
equipped with such a basis is called a marked Riemann surface. We can for instance choose a point
p_{0}
and simple closed curves on
\widehat{C}
representing the
2g
classes
(\mathcal{A}_{h},\mathcal{B}_{h})_{h=1}^{g}

such that all the curves intersect each other at
p_{0}
only. For spectral curves of
\beta
-ensembles, we will later work with another set of representatives (Section
3.3.1
). We keep the same notation for homology classes and their representatives. The
surface
\widehat{C}^{0}=\widehat{C}\setminus\bigcup_{h=1}^{g}(\mathcal{A}_{h}\cup\mathcal{B}_{h})
is
then simply-connected. The
\mathcal{A}
-cycles
(\mathcal{A}_{h})_{h=1}^{g}
determine a dual basis of holomorphic 1-forms

(\differential u_{h})_{h=1}^{g}
, such that

\begin{split}\forall h,k\in[g]\qquad\oint_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}\differential u_{k}=\delta_{h,k}.\end{split}

The matrix of periods
\bm{\tau}
is then defined by

\forall h,k\in[g]\qquad\tau_{h,k}=\oint_{\mathcal{B}_{h}}\differential u_{k}.

(3.1)

It is symmetric and
\operatorname{Im}(\bm{\tau})
is definite positive, in particular we can consider the theta function with matrix
\frac{\beta}{2}\bm{\tau}
for any
\beta>0
. The theta function with matrix equal to (
3.1
) is called the Riemann theta function.

3.2.2
Abel map

With the 1-forms
(\differential u_{h})_{h=1}^{g}
defined in Section

3.2.1
, we can introduce the Abel map.

Definition 3.3
.

Choose a base point
p_{0}
in
\widehat{C}
. The Abel map

\bm{u}\colon\widehat{C}^{0}\to\mathbb{C}^{g}
is defined by

\begin{split}u_{i}(z)&=\int_{p_{0}}^{z}\differential u_{i},\end{split}

where the path of integration is in
\widehat{C}^{0}
.

The definition of the Abel map depends on a choice of base point
p_{0}
.
However, we will often consider differences
\bm{u}(z)-\bm{u}(w)
of Abel
maps, which are independent of
p_{0}
. Depending on the context, we may also consider the Abel map as a map
\bm{u}\colon\widetilde{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{g}
defined on the universal cover
\widetilde{C}
of
\widehat{C}
based at
p_{0}
. We say that
\bm{c}
is non-singular if
\theta(\bm{c}+\bm{u}(z)-\bm{u}(w)|\bm{\tau})
is not identically
0
when
z,w\in\widetilde{C}
. Non-singular odd half-integer characteristics exist, and in what follows we fix one.

3.2.3
Prime form

We introduce the holomorphic 1-form

\omega_{\bm{c}}=\sum_{h=1}^{g}\partial_{z_{h}}\theta(\bm{z}|\bm{\tau})\big|_{\bm{z}=\bm{c}}\,\differential u_{h}.

The prime form is

E(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{\theta(\bm{c}+\bm{u}(z_{1})-\bm{u}(z_{2})|\bm{\tau})}{\sqrt{\omega_{\bm{c}}(z_{1})}\sqrt{\omega_{\bm{c}}(z_{2})}}.

(3.2)

It is defined as a holomorphic bispinor on
\widetilde{C}\times\widetilde{C}
, i.e., a
\bigl(-\frac{1}{2}\bigr)\otimes\bigl(-\frac{1}{2}\bigr)
form. It has zeros only at
z_{1}=z_{2}
, and in local coordinates
\zeta
, we have

E(z_{1},z_{2})\mathop{\sim}_{z_{1}\rightarrow z_{2}}\frac{\zeta(z_{1})-\zeta(z_{2})}{\sqrt{\differential\zeta(z_{1})\differential\zeta(z_{2})}}.

The prime form on the Riemann sphere
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
reads

E_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{x_{1}-x_{2}}{\sqrt{\differential x_{1}\differential x_{2}}}.

Given a meromorphic function
X\colon\widehat{C}\rightarrow\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
, we can define the relative prime form

\tilde{E}(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{E(z_{1},z_{2})}{E_{0}(X(z_{1}),X(z_{2}))}.

We observe that
\tilde{E}(z_{1},z_{2})
is a function on
\widetilde{C}\times\widetilde{C}
, such that

\lim_{z_{2}\rightarrow z_{1}}\tilde{E}(z_{1},z_{2})=1.

(3.3)

3.2.4
Fundamental bidifferential

Definition 3.4
.

The fundamental bidifferential
B(z,w)
is the unique bidifferential
(
i.e., a
1\otimes 1
form on
\widehat{C}\times\widehat{C}
)
such that

1.

Symmetry:
B(z,w)=B(w,z)
.

2.

Normalisation:
\forall h\in[g]

\oint_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}B(\cdot,w)=0
.

3.

Singularities:
B(z,w)
is meromorphic with only a double pole at

, and if
\zeta
is a local coordinate, we have

B(z,w)\mathop{=}_{z\rightarrow w}\biggl(\frac{1}{(\zeta(z)-\zeta(w))^{2}}+S_{B,\zeta}(w)+\order{\zeta(z) - \zeta(w)}\biggr)\differential\zeta(z)\differential\zeta(w).

for some function
S_{B,\zeta}
locally defined on
\widehat{C}
.

The fundamental bidifferential can be expressed as

\begin{split}B(z,w)&=\differential_{z}\differential_{w}\ln\theta(\bm{c}+\bm{u}(z)-\bm{u}(w)),\end{split}

and this expression is independent on the choice of a non-singular odd half-integer characteristics
\bm{c}
. Given

and a choice of path
\gamma_{p,q}
from

\begin{split}\differential S_{p,q}(z)=\int_{\gamma_{p,q}}B(z,\cdot).\end{split}

It has two poles of order 1 in
p
and
q

-1
and
+1
. Equivalently, we can consider that it is specified by the choice of two points

and

\widetilde{C}

For

and

and

3.3
The spectral curve

We elaborate on Section
2.3
and construct the spectral curve associated to the equilibrium measure of
{\beta}
-ensembles, for the moment indifferently in the unconstrained case or the fixed filling fraction case. This prepares us for Section
4
where the asymptotics in the
\beta

3.3.1
Construction of the marked Riemann surface

The equation (
2.4
) satisfied by the Stieltjes transform of the equilibrium measure has two solutions:

\displaystyle F_{+}(x)=\frac{V^{\prime}(x)}{2}+y(x)=W_{1}(x),

\displaystyle F_{-}(x)=\frac{V^{\prime}(x)}{2}-y(x)=V^{\prime}(x)-W_{1}(x),

(3.5)

where
y(x)=-\frac{1}{2}M(x)s(x)
and
y(x)^{2}=\frac{1}{4}V^{\prime}(x)^{2}-P(x)
. After the birational transformation
(x,y)\mapsto\bigl(x,s=-\frac{2y}{M(x)}\bigr)
, we have the equation of an hyperelliptic curve

s^{2}=\sigma(x)=\prod_{h=0}^{g}(x-a_{h})(x-b_{h}),

where the Weierstraß points
a_{0},b_{0},\ldots,a_{g},b_{g}
are real. This curve is constructed from two sheets homeomorphic to

\mathbb{C}\setminus S
, which are glued together along
S
. The two sheets are embedded into the curve
by

\iota_{\pm}\colon\ \mathbb{C}\setminus S\longrightarrow C\subset\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C},\qquad x\longmapsto(x,\pm s(x)).

We denote the sheets by
C_{\pm}=\iota_{\pm}(\mathbb{C}\setminus S)
, and
\widehat{C}_{\pm}
are the sheets including their point at infinity. Adding these points at infinity
\infty_{\pm}
to
C
, we get a compact Riemann surface
\widehat{C}
. We define the projection map as the meromorphic function

X\colon\ \widehat{C}\longrightarrow\widehat{\mathbb{C}},\qquad(x,s)\longmapsto x.

This function has simple poles at
\infty_{\pm}
and it defines a degree
2
branched covering of the Riemann sphere
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
, whose branch points are the zeros of
\sigma
. It allows us to define local coordinates
\zeta_{p}
around any point
p\in\widehat{C}
, which is used in Section
3.2.5
to define a basis of meromorphic differentials:

•

If
p
is a ramification point, we take
\zeta_{p}(z)=\sqrt{X(z)-X(p)}
for some choice of sign for the squareroot.

•

If
p=\infty_{\pm}
, then
\zeta_{\infty_{\pm}}=X(z)^{-1}
.

•

In all the other cases,
\zeta_{p}(z)=X(z)-X(p)
.

We shall take
p_{0}=\infty_{+}
as reference point for the definition of the Abel map (Section
3.2.2
). For later use, we analyse the prime form over
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
near
\infty_{\pm}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

We have

E_{0}(z,\tilde{z})\sqrt{\differential\zeta_{\infty_{\pm}}(\tilde{z})}\Big|_{\tilde{z}=\infty_{\pm}}=\frac{-1}{\sqrt{-\differential X(z)}}.

Proof.

Since
\differential X(\tilde{z})=-X(\tilde{z})^{2}\differential\zeta_{\infty_{\pm}}(\tilde{z})
, we have

\lim_{\tilde{z}\rightarrow\infty_{\pm}}E_{0}(z,\tilde{z})\sqrt{\differential\zeta_{\infty_{\pm}}(\tilde{z})}=\lim_{\tilde{z}\rightarrow\infty_{\pm}}(X(z)-X(\tilde{z}))\frac{\sqrt{-X(\tilde{z})^{-2}\differential X(\tilde{z})}}{\sqrt{\differential X(z)\differential X(\tilde{z})}}=\frac{-1}{\sqrt{-\differential X(z)}}.

We choose representatives for a symplectic basis of homology on
\widehat{C}
like in Figure
1
. Namely, we take
\mathcal{A}_{h}

3.4

In Section

Definition 3.10
.

If

Proposition 3.11
.

For any
a_{0}<b_{0}<\cdots<a_{g}<b_{g}
, there exists a polynomial
V
of degree
(2g+2)
with top coefficient
\frac{t_{2g+2}}{2g+2}>0
and there exist for each

a segment

which is a neighborhood of

in
\mathbb{R}
, such that the unconstrained
\beta
-ensemble with potential
V
on
A=\bigsqcup_{h=0}^{g}A_{h}
admits an equilibrium measure with support
S=\bigsqcup_{h=0}^{g}[a_{h},b_{h}]
and in (

) we have
M(x)=t_{2g+2}\prod_{h=1}^{g}(x-z_{h})
having roots outside

and such that

for any

.

Proof.

Take
2g+2
real points
a_{0}<b_{0}<\cdots<a_{g}<b_{g}
and introduce polynomials
\sigma(x)=\prod_{h=0}^{g}(x-a_{h})(x-b_{h})
. We have seen in Section
2.3
that there exists a unique holomorphic function
s(x)
on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\bigsqcup_{h=0}^{g}[a_{h},b_{h}]
such that
s(x)^{2}=\sigma(x)
and
s(x)\sim x^{g+1}

as
x\rightarrow\infty
in the complex plane. Take
h\in[g]
and introduce the continuous function

\forall\bm{\lambda}\in[0,1]^{g}\qquad J_{h}(\bm{\lambda})=\int_{b_{h-1}}^{a_{h}}s(x)\prod_{k=1}^{g}(x-(\lambda_{k}b_{k-1}+(1-\lambda_{k})a_{k}))\,\differential x.

By continuity,
s(x)
has constant sign for
x\in(b_{h-1},a_{h})
. Besides, for
\lambda_{h}\in\{0,1\}
we have
\operatorname{sgn}(J_{h}(\bm{\lambda}))=(-1)^{g-h+1-\lambda_{h}}
. The Poincaré–Miranda theorem
[
30
]
then implies the existence of
\bm{\lambda}^{*}\in[0,1]^{g}
such that
J_{h}(\bm{\lambda}^{*})
vanishes for any
h\in[g]
. Since
J_{h}(\bm{\lambda})
does not vanish when
\lambda_{h}\in\{0,1\}
, this
\bm{\lambda}^{*}
must be in
(0,1)^{g}
. We let
z_{h}=\lambda_{h}^{*}b_{h-1}+\big(1-\lambda_{h}^{*}\big)a_{h}
for
h\in[g]
and introduce the polynomial
M(x)=t_{2g+2}\prod_{h=1}^{g}(x-z_{h})
, where the constant
t_{2g+2}
is chosen such that

\sum_{h=0}^{g}\int_{a_{h}}^{b_{h}}\frac{M(x)\operatorname{Im}(s(x+{\rm i}0))}{2\pi}=1.

(3.10)

The sign discussion for
s
in Section
2.3
reveals that all terms in (
3.10
) are positive, thus
t_{2g+2}>0
. Then,
V(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\mathcal{V}[M\cdot s](\xi)\differential\xi
is a polynomial of degree
2g+2
with top coefficient
\frac{t_{2g+2}}{2g+2}
, and

\mathop{\text{Res}}_{x=\infty}\frac{s(x)M(x)}{2}]\,\differential x=-\frac{1}{2{\rm i}\pi}\sum_{h=0}^{g}\oint_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}\frac{s(x)M(x)}{2}\,\differential x=\sum_{h=0}^{g}\int_{a_{h}}^{b_{h}}\frac{M(x)\operatorname{Im}(s(x+{\rm i}0))}{2\pi}=1,

where
\mathcal{A}_{h}
is a counterclockwise loop around
[a_{h},b_{h}]
. Therefore,

\frac{V^{\prime}(x)}{2}\mathop{\mathop{=}}_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{M(x)s(x)}{2}+\frac{1}{x}+\mathcal{O}\biggl(\frac{1}{x^{2}}\biggr).

This
V
defines the potential in a
\beta
-ensemble which we consider over the domain
A=\bigsqcup_{h=0}^{g}A_{h}
, where
A_{h}=\bigl[a_{h}^{\prime},b_{h}^{\prime}\bigr]
and
z_{h}<a_{h}^{\prime}<a_{h}
and
b_{h}<b_{h}^{\prime}<z_{h+1}
for any
h\in[0,g]
, with the conventions
z_{0}=-\infty
and
z_{g+1}=+\infty
. It remains to check that
W(x):=\frac{1}{2}(V^{\prime}(x)-M(x)s(x))
is the Stieltjes transform of the equilibrium measure of this (unconstrained)
\beta
-ensemble.

We define the measure
\mu
with support
S=\bigsqcup_{h=0}^{g}[a_{h},b_{h}]
and density

\frac{\differential\mu}{\differential x}=\frac{W(x-{\rm i}0)-W(x+{\rm i}0)}{2{\rm i}\pi}=\frac{M(x)s(x)}{2\pi}\mathds{1}_{S}(x).

By construction,
W
is the Stieltjes transform of
\mu
. Since
M
has a single zero between each components of the support,
\mu
is a positive measure, see the sign discussion in Section
2.3
. Define
U(x)=V(x)-2\int_{S}\ln|x-\xi|\differential\mu(\xi)
. We clearly have

\forall x\in S\qquad U^{\prime}(x)=V^{\prime}(x)-W_{1}(x+{\rm i}0)-W_{1}(x-{\rm i}0)=0.

Integrating this from
a_{h}
to
x\in(a_{h},b_{h})
, we find a constant
c_{h}
such that
U(x)=c_{h}
for any
x\in(a_{h},b_{h})
. Besides, for any
h\in[g]
we compute

c_{h}-c_{h-1}=\int_{b_{h-1}}^{a_{h}}U^{\prime}(x)\,\differential x=\int_{b_{h-1}}^{a_{h}}\bigl(V^{\prime}(x)-2W_{1}(x)\bigr)\differential x=\int_{b_{h-1}}^{a_{h}}M(x)s(x)\,\differential x.

Since we have chosen
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{g})
so that this integral vanishes,
c_{h}
is independent of
h
. As a result,
\mu
satisfies the characterisation of the equilibrium measure from Theorem
2.1
(unconstrained case). By uniqueness, this must be the equilibrium measure:
\mu=\mu_{{\rm eq}}
.
∎

Z^{V}_{N}\sim N^{\frac{\beta}{2}N+\varkappa}{\rm e}^{N^{2}\mathcal{E}[\mu_{{\rm eq}}]+N\mathcal{S}[\mu_{{\rm eq}}]+\mathcal{G}[\mu_{{\rm eq}}]}\,\vartheta_{-N\bm{\epsilon}^{*},\bm{0}}\bigl(\bm{v}_{{\rm eq}}\big|\tfrac{\beta}{2}\bm{\tau}\bigr).

Here

\mathcal{S}[\mu]=\biggl(1-\frac{\beta}{2}\biggr)({\rm Ent}[\mu]-\ln(\beta/2))+(\beta/2)\ln(2\pi/e)-\ln\Gamma(\beta/2),

where
{\rm Ent}[\mu]
is the von Neumann entropy of the probability measure
\mu
,
\varkappa
is a known universal constant depending only on
g
and
\beta
,
\mathcal{G}[\mu]
is a continuous functional whose expression is irrelevant for our purposes, and

\bm{v}_{{\rm eq}}=\frac{\nabla_{\epsilon}\mathcal{S}[\mu_{{\rm eq},\bm{\epsilon}}]}{2{\rm i}\pi}\bigg|_{\bm{\epsilon}=\bm{\epsilon}^{*}}.

(4.1)

In (
4.1
) one differentiates with respect to
\bm{\epsilon}=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{g})
,
keeping in mind that the filling fraction
\epsilon_{0}
, associated to the
component of the support
[a_{0},b_{0}]
, satisfies

\epsilon_{0}=1-(\epsilon_{1}+\cdots+\epsilon_{g})
and thus depends on

\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{g}
. The kernels appearing in the determinantal and
Pfaffian formulae of Borodin and Strahov (Theorems
2.6
–
2.8
) can be
estimated using the following generalised central limit theorem. We
use the notation
\oint_{S}
for a sum of contour integrals in the
positive direction around the connected components of the support
S

of the equilibrium measure.

Theorem 4.2

(
[
13
, Theorem 1.6]
)
.

Let
f
be a holomorphic function in a complex neighborhood of
A
. Under the same assumptions as Theorem
4.1
, we have as

\bigl\langle\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(\lambda_{i})}\bigr\rangle^{V}_{N}\sim{\rm e}^{N\mathcal{L}[f]+\mathcal{H}[f]+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{Q}[f,f]}\frac{\vartheta_{-N\bm{\epsilon}^{*},\bm{0}}\bigl(\bm{v}_{{\rm eq}}+\bm{\mathcal{U}}[f]\big|\frac{\beta}{2}\bm{\tau}\bigr)}{\vartheta_{-N\bm{\epsilon}^{*},\bm{0}}\bigl(\bm{v}_{{\rm eq}}\big|\frac{\beta}{2}\bm{\tau}\bigr)}.

Here

\mathcal{L}[f]=\oint_{S}W_{1}(\xi)\frac{f(\xi)\differential\xi}{2{\rm i}\pi},\qquad\mathcal{Q}[f,f]=\frac{2}{\beta}\oint_{S^{2}}W_{2}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\frac{f(\xi_{1})\differential\xi_{1}}{2{\rm i}\pi}\frac{f(\xi_{2})\differential\xi_{2}}{2{\rm i}\pi},

where
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are calculated in a model with fixed filling fraction tending to
\bm{\epsilon}^{*}
,

\mathcal{H}[f]
is a linear form whose expression is irrelevant, and

\bm{\mathcal{U}}[f]=\oint_{S}f(X(z))\frac{\differential\bm{u}(z)}{2{\rm i}\pi}.

4.2
Three explicit formulae

We will establish in Section
5.4
the following expression for the equilibrium energy in terms of the geometry of the spectral curve.

Proposition 4.3
.

The equilibrium energy is

-\mathcal{E}[\mu_{{\rm eq}}]=\frac{\beta}{2}\mathcal{L}[V]+\frac{\beta^{2}}{8}\mathcal{Q}[V,V]+{\rm i}\pi\beta\bm{\epsilon}^{*}\cdot(\bm{\tau}(\bm{\epsilon}^{*})+\bm{u}(\infty_{-})).

The right-hand side is proportional to
\frac{\beta}{2}
, since
W_{2}
contains a factor
\frac{2}{\beta}
, and so is the left-hand side. Accordingly this is an identity between
\beta
-independent quantities. There is a classical link between random matrix theory and the theory
of Frobenius manifolds: the free energy at leading order,
namely
\mathcal{E}[\mu_{{\rm eq}}]
coincides with the
prepotential of the Hurwitz–Frobenius manifold associated to the
spectral curve of the random matrix ensemble. A formula for this free energy was established in the more general context of the 2-matrix model in
[
4
]
, involving only the geometry of the spectral curve. It involves the same ingredient but does not have exactly the same form as Proposition
4.3
, which is the formula we need.

Before going further, we give two extra formulae. The first one evaluates the argument
\bm{v}_{{\rm eq}}
of the theta functions in Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
; it is not necessary for Section
5
, but we include it for completeness. The second one will be used in the proof of Lemma
4.8
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Assume
M
in (
2.5
) is of the form

M(x)=t_{2g+2}\prod_{h=1}^{g}(x-z_{h})
with roots

z_{h}\notin A
satisfying
b_{h-1}<z_{h}<a_{h}
for any

h\in[g]
, and denote by
(\bm{e}_{1},\ldots,\bm{e}_{g})
the
canonical basis of
\mathbb{C}^{g}
.

The function
\operatorname{Im}\bm{u}(z)=\int_{\infty_{+}}^{z}\differential\bm{u}
is a
single-valued function of
z\in\widehat{C}_{+}
, and we have

\bm{v}_{{\rm eq}}=2\pi\biggl(1-\frac{\beta}{2}\biggr)\Biggl[\frac{g+1}{2{\rm i}}\bm{u}(\infty_{-})+\sum_{k=1}^{g}\biggl(\operatorname{Im}\bm{u}(z_{k})+\frac{g+1-k}{2{\rm i}}\bm{\tau}(e_{k})\biggr)\Biggr].

Proof.

See Appendix
A
. As we explain in Section
5.4
,
\bm{u}(\infty_{-})
and
\bm{\tau}
are purely imaginary, so all terms in the right-hand side are real, as it should be. The domain
\widehat{C}_{+}
is homeomorphic to the non simply-connected domain
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus S
, so
\int_{\infty_{+}}^{z}\differential\bm{u}
is multi-valued. However, the ambiguities are
\mathcal{A}
-periods of
\differential\bm{u}
which are real, so
\operatorname{Im}\bm{u}(z)
is single-valued.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

If we remove the assumption on the roots of
M
in Lemma

4.4
, we can still compute
\bm{v}_{{\rm eq}}
and
obtain a formula of a similar form.

Lemma 4.6
.

\bm{\mathcal{U}}[V]=-\Res_{\infty_{+}}V\differential\bm{u}=\bm{\tau}(\bm{\epsilon}^{*})+\bm{u}(\infty_{-}).

Proof.

The first equality is obtained by moving the contour around
S
to
\infty_{+}
. We focus on the second equality. Since
\differential V=2(\phi-Y\differential X)
, we have

\Res_{\infty_{+}}V\differential\bm{u}=-\Res_{\infty_{+}}\bm{u}\differential V=-2\Res_{\infty_{+}}\bm{u}(\phi-Y\differential X).

Since
\infty_{+}
is the base point for the Abel map, we have

\bm{u}(\infty_{+})=0
and since
\phi
has only a simple pole at
\infty_{+}
,
the first term gives a vanishing residue. The hyperelliptic involution preserves
X
, sends
Y
and
\differential\bm{u}
to their opposite, and
\infty_{+}
to
\infty_{-}
. Hence, it sends
\bm{u}
to
\bm{u}(\infty_{-})-\bm{u}
, where
\infty_{-}\in\widehat{C}^{0}
. Using the involution as a change of variables, we get

\Res_{\infty_{+}}\bm{u}Y\differential X=\Res_{\infty_{-}}(\bm{u}-\bm{u}(\infty_{-}))Y\differential X=\Res_{\infty_{-}}\bm{u}Y\differential X-\bm{u}(\infty_{-}).

For the last equality, since the only poles of
Y\differential X
are
\infty_{\pm}
we could evaluate

\Res_{\infty_{-}}Y\differential X=-\Res_{\infty_{+}}Y\differential X=-\Res_{\infty_{+}}\phi=1.

We then write

\Res_{\infty_{+}}V\differential\bm{u}=(\Res_{\infty_{+}}+\Res_{\infty_{-}})Y\differential X-\bm{u}(\infty_{-}).

The first term can be computed with the Riemann bilinear identity
[
22
, equation (3.0.2)]

(\Res_{\infty_{+}}+\Res_{\infty_{-}})\bm{u}Y\differential X=\frac{1}{2{\rm i}\pi}\sum_{h=1}^{g}\biggl(\oint_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}\differential\bm{u}\cdot\oint_{\mathcal{B}_{h}}Y\differential X-\oint_{\mathcal{B}_{h}}\differential\bm{u}\cdot\oint_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}Y\,\differential X\biggr).

(4.2)

Taking into account that for any
h,k\in[g]
, we have
\oint_{\mathcal{B}_{h}}Y\differential X=0
(Remark
3.8
), and
\oint_{\mathcal{B}_{h}}\differential u_{k}=\tau_{h,k}
and
\oint_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}Y\differential X=2{\rm i}\pi\epsilon^{*}_{h}
, we find that (
4.2
) is equal to
-\bm{\tau}(\bm{\epsilon}^{*})
.
∎
