Roughening of two-dimensional interfaces in nonequilibrium phase-separated systems
John Toner

Abstract
I show that non-equilibrium two-dimensional interfaces between three dimensional phase separated fluids exhibit a peculiar ”sub-logarithmic” roughness. Specifically, an interface of lateral extent L L will fluctuate vertically (i.e., normal to the mean surface orientation) a typical RMS distance w ≡ ⟨ | h ⁡ ( 𝐫 , t ) | 2 ⟩ ∝ [ ln ⁡ ( L / a ) ] 1 / 3 w\equiv\sqrt{\langle|h(\mathbf{r},t)|^{2}\rangle}\propto[\ln{(L/a)}]^{1/3} (where a a is a microscopic length, and h ⁡ ( 𝐫 , t ) h(\mathbf{r},t) is the height of the interface at two dimensional position 𝐫 \mathbf{r} at time t t ). In contrast, the roughness of equilibrium two-dimensional interfaces between three dimensional fluids, obeys w ∝ [ ln ⁡ ( L / a ) ] 1 / 2 w\propto[\ln{(L/a)}]^{1/2} . The exponent 1 / 3 1/3 for the active case is exact. In addition, the characteristic time scales τ ⁡ ( L ) \tau(L) in the active case scale according to τ ⁡ ( L ) ∝ L 3 ​ [ ln ⁡ ( L / a ) ] 1 / 3 \tau(L)\propto L^{3}[\ln{(L/a)}]^{1/3} , in contrast to the simple τ ⁡ ( L ) ∝ L 3 \tau(L)\propto L^{3} scaling found in equilibrium systems with conserved densities and no fluid flow.



The KPZ equation
KPZ
is an excellent illustration of the novelty of non-equilibrium statistical mechanics. It describes moving interfaces (e.g., the surface of a crystal growing into a supersaturated vapor), and its solution reveals that the behavior of such non-equilibrium interfaces is very different from that of their equilibrium counterparts
EW
.

Recently, it was recognized
besse
that non-equilibrium interfaces in phase separated systems with a
conserved
density field belong to an entirely new universality class, which
besse
dubbed ”the
|\mathbf{q}|KPZ
equation”. They studied this universality class using the dynamical renormalization group (DRG), and estimated its scaling exponents in one dimension (1d) (that is, for a one-dimensional interface between a pair of two dimensional media) using a
2-\epsilon
expansion. Their results showed conclusively that the 1d problem was indeed a different universality class from the 1d KPZ equation.

In this paper, I show that this also holds in
d=2
(2d). More specifically, the
|\mathbf{q}|KPZ
equation also belongs to a new universality class, exhibiting scaling behavior different from
both
the 2d KPZ equation, and the equilibrium Edwards-Wilkinson model
EW
.

To be more quantitative, I find that relaxation times
\tau(L)
of interface fluctuations on a length scale
L
in the
|\mathbf{q}|KPZ
equation scale according to

\tau(L)\propto L^{3}[\ln{(L/a)}]^{1/3}\,,

(1)

in contrast to the simple
L^{3}
scaling found in equilibrium systems with a conserved density field.
This scaling is also very different from that found in the 2d KPZ equation
KPZ
.

In addition, the characteristic width of an interface of lateral extent
L
for the
|\mathbf{q}|KPZ
equation grows more slowly with
L
than in both the equilibrium case
EW
, and the 2d KPZ equation
KPZ
. I find for the
|\mathbf{q}|KPZ
equation:

w\equiv\sqrt{\langle|h(\mathbf{r},t)|^{2}\rangle}\propto[\ln{(L/a)}]^{1/3}\,,

(2)

where
a
is a microscopic length, and
h(\mathbf{r},t)
is the height of the interface at two dimensional position
\mathbf{r}
at time
t
.

Both the exponents
1/3
in the logarithms in (
1
) and (
2
) are
exact
, as is the power
3
on the
L^{3}
in (
1
).

I’ll now derive these results, starting with the model of

Model.—

In Fourier space, the equation of motion (EOM) for an active growing interface with between two regions of different densities with a conserved density field was derived in
fausti

besse
, and reads

\displaystyle-i\omega h(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sigma q^{3}h(\mathbf{q},t)+{\lambda\over 2}q\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{q},\omega}\left[|\nabla h(\mathbf{r},t)|^{2}\right]+f(\mathbf{q},t)\,.

Here
q\equiv|\mathbf{q}|
, the symbol
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{q},\omega}
represents the
{\mathbf{q},\omega}
’th Fourier component, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{q},\omega}\left[h(\mathbf{r},t)\right]\equiv{1\over(2\pi)^{d/2}}\int{\rm d}^{d}r\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}-\omega t)}h(\mathbf{r},t)\,,

and the Gaussian ”blue noise”

has zero mean and variance given by

\langle f(\mathbf{q},t)f(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=2Dq\delta^{d}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})\,.

(5)

The Brillouin zone of all Fourier transforms has an ultraviolet cutoff
\Lambda
; that is, wavevectors
\mathbf{q}
are restricted to the interior of a hypersphere:
|\mathbf{q}|<\Lambda

Linear theory
— The linearized version of the EOM (

\lambda

in terms of the random force

h(\mathbf{q},\omega)=G(\mathbf{q},\omega)f(\mathbf{q},\omega)\,,

(6)

where the “propagator”

G(\mathbf{q},\omega)\equiv{1\over-i\omega+\sigma q^{3}}\,.

(7)

The existence of a pole in this propagator at

\omega=-i\sigma q^{3}

(8)

shows that excitations of wavevector
\mathbf{q}
decay at a rate
-i\sigma q^{3}
, according to the linear theory. We will see later that this scaling changes when the
\lambda

Autocorrelating this with itself, and using the expression (
5

\displaystyle\langle h(\mathbf{q},\omega)h(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G(\mathbf{q},\omega)G(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\langle f(\mathbf{q},\omega)f(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\rangle=2DqG(\mathbf{q},\omega)G(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\delta^{d}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\delta^{d}(\omega+\omega^{\prime})

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2DqG(\mathbf{q},\omega)G(-\mathbf{q},-\omega)\delta^{d}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\delta^{d}(\omega+\omega^{\prime})\equiv C(\mathbf{q},\omega)\delta^{d}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\delta^{d}(\omega+\omega^{\prime})\,,

with

C(\mathbf{q},\omega)={2Dq\over(\omega^{2}+\sigma^{2}q^{6})}\,.

(10)

Integrating this expression over the frequencies
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
gives the
spatially

\displaystyle\langle h(\mathbf{q},t)h(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{\rm ET}(\mathbf{q})\delta^{d}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})

with

C_{\rm ET}(\mathbf{q})=\int{d\omega\over 2\pi}{2Dq\over(\omega^{2}+\sigma^{2}q^{6})}={D\over\sigma q^{2}}\,.

(12)

This correlation function diverges like

as

. This means that the surface will, according to the linearized theory, be algebraically “rough” in spatial dimensions

, logarithmically rough in

, and “smooth” in

. Specifically,

\langle(h(\mathbf{r},t))^{2}\rangle=\int{d^{d}q\over(2\pi)^{d}}\int{d^{d}q\over(2\pi)^{d}}\langle h(\mathbf{q},t)h(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},t)\rangle=\int{d^{d}q\over(2\pi)^{d}}C_{\rm ET}(\mathbf{q})={D\over\sigma(2\pi)^{d}}\int{d^{d}q\over q^{2}}\propto\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}L^{2-d},&d<2\,,\\
\ln\left({\Lambda L\over 2\pi}\right),&d=2\,,\\
{\rm finite}\,{\rm(independent}\,{\rm of}\,L),&d>2\,,\end{array}\,\right.

(13)

where the system size

acts as an infrared cutoff to the integral over
q

In

Nonlinear regime & DRG analysis.—

I now turn to the full, non-linear EOM of
h
(
). This was studied by
besse
using the dynamical renormalization group (DRG). They found the parameters in the renormalization group obeyed the recursion relations

\displaystyle{{\rm d}\ln\sigma\over{\rm d}\ell}

\displaystyle=z-3+g\,,

(14a)

\displaystyle{{\rm d}\ln{\lambda}\over{\rm d}\ell}

\displaystyle=z-3+\chi\,,

(14b)

\displaystyle{{\rm d}\ln D\over{\rm d}\ell}

\displaystyle=z-2\chi-d-1\,,

(14c)

where I’ve defined the dimensionless coupling

g\equiv{S_{d}D\lambda^{2}\over 8(2\pi)^{d}\sigma^{3}}\,,

(15)

with
S_{d}
being the surface area of a
d
-dimensional unit sphere.
diff g

In (
14a
)-(
14c
),
z
is the ”dynamical” exponent for the rescaling of time under the RG, and
\chi
is the ”roughness” exponent for the rescaling of the field
h(\mathbf{r},t)
in real space. I will later make specific convenient choices for these arbitrary exponents.

From (
14
), one can construct a closed recursion relation
for
\ln g
:

{{\rm d}\ln g\over{\rm d}\ell}=\epsilon-3g\,,

(16)

where
\epsilon\equiv 2-d
. This can obviously be turned into a closed recursion relation for
g
itself:

{{\rm d}g\over{\rm d}\ell}=\epsilon g-3g^{2}\,.

(17)

This recursion relation and its physical consequences were analyzed for
d<2
by
besse
. Here, I wish to focus on
d=2
, where
\epsilon=0
, and the recursion relation (
18
) becomes

{{\rm d}g\over{\rm d}\ell}=-3g^{2}\,.

(18)

The solution of this recursion relation is straightforward:

g(\ell)={g_{0}\over 1+3g_{0}\ell}\approx{1\over 3\ell}\,,

(19)

where
g_{0}\equiv g(\ell=0)
is the ”bare” value of the dimensionless coupling
g
, and the last, approximate equality holds at large RG time
\ell
.

This solution is, strictly speaking, only valid if the bare dimensionless coupling
g_{0}
is small, since only in that case will the recursion relation (
18
), which was derived assuming that
g(\ell)\ll 1
, hold.

However, one can make a stronger statement: as long as the bare
g(\ell)
lies in the basin of attraction of the RG fixed point at
g=0
,
g(\ell)
will eventually flow to sufficiently small values that (
18
)
does
hold. Once that happens,
g(\ell)
is guaranteed to flow to even smaller values, and, eventually, to exhibit the asymptotic behavior
g(\ell)\approx 1/(3\ell)
displayed in equation (
19
). Thus, for any such initial condition (which may, for all we know, include
all
initial conditions), the aforementioned asymptotic behavior will hold.

One might be mislead into thinking that, since the dimensionless coupling
g
that determines the importance of the nonlinearity
\lambda
vanishes upon renormalization, the effect of that non-linearity vanishes at long length and time scales. However, because the vanishing of
g(\ell)
is so slow (i.e., proportional to
1/\ell
, rather than exponential), this proves not to be the case. In fact, as I’ll show in a moment, the vanishing of
g
in two dimensions is
so
slow that it leads to
infinite
renormalization of the effective ”surface tension”
\sigma
, which in turns leads to ”anomalous hydrodynamics”: that is, different scaling laws for the long-distance, long-time dynamics of the system than those predicted by a purely linear theory which ignored the nonlinearity
\lambda
.

This situation is very analogous to that in equilibrium three dimensional smectic liquid crystals
pelc
, which also have a dimensionless coupling constant ( in that case associated with the anharmonic terms in the smectic elastic Hamiltonian) which vanishes like
1/\ell
as RG ”time”
\ell\to\infty
. In that case, this leads to ”anomalous elasticity”, in which both the elastic response of the smectic, and its fluctuations, scale differently at large length scales than predicted by a purely harmonic elastic theory.

To see this in our problem, I’ll begin by using the solution (
19

g(\ell)
in the recursion relation (
14a
) for the surface tension
\sigma
, and solving the resultant equation for the renormalized
\sigma(\ell)
. At large
\ell

\sigma

{{\rm d}\ln\sigma\over{\rm d}\ell}\approx z-3+{1\over 3\ell}\,.

(20)

Since the time rescaling exponent

is arbitrary, I will make the simplifying choice

\sigma(\ell)=\sigma_{0}\ell^{1/3}\,,

where

Note that
\sigma(\ell)
diverges as

To see this,

\displaystyle C(\mathbf{q},\omega;\sigma_{0},D_{0},\lambda_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{[2\chi+z+d]\ell}C(e^{\ell}\mathbf{q},e^{z\ell}\omega;\sigma(\ell),D(\ell),\lambda(\ell))\,.

This change in relaxation rates leads to a different scaling of the real space fluctuations as well.
With the result (

These results were all derived using the recursion relations (
14

besse

In conclusion, I have shown that non-equilibrium two dimensional interfaces in phase separated systems with a
conserved
density field belong to a new universality class, different from both equilibrium systems (whether with or without conserved densities) and the nonequilibrium KPZ equation. I’ve also shown that such interfaces have logarithmically faster relaxation than in equilibrium, and exhibit widths that diverge with system size as a smaller power of the logarithm of the system size than in equilibrium.
