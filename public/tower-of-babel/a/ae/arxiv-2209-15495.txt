Guoce Xin 1 , Chen Zhang 2 , Yue Zhou 3,∗ and Yueming Zhong 4

Abstract
. In this paper, we discover a new noncommutative algebra. We refer this algebra as the constant term algebra of type A A , which is generated by certain constant term operators. We characterize a structural result of this algebra by establishing an explicit basis in terms of certain forests. This algebra arises when we apply the method of the iterated Laurent series to investigate Beck and Pixton’s residue computation for the Ehrhart series of the Birkhoff polytope. This algebra seems to be the first structural result in the area of the constant term world since the discovery of the Dyson constant term identity in 1962.

: Primary 08A05; Secondary 47C05, 05A19.

Keywords
: constant term identities; ordered increasing forests; constant term algebra.

1.
Introduction

In the introduction, we briefly review the development of the area of constant term identities.
Then we introduce our main contribution in this paper: the construction of the constant term algebra of type
A
.

The study of constant term identities originates from the Dyson constant term identity
[
15
]
in 1962.
For nonnegative integers
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
,

(1.1)

\CT_{\boldsymbol{x}}\,\prod_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}(1-x_{i}/x_{j})^{a_{i}}=\binom{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}}{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}},

where
\displaystyle\CT_{\boldsymbol{x}}f
denotes taking the constant term of the Laurent polynomial (series)
f
with respect to
\boldsymbol{x}:=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
,
and

\binom{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}}{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}=\frac{(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n})!}{a_{1}!\cdots a_{n}!}

is the multinomial coefficient.
Dyson’s conjecture was soon proved by Gunson
[
19
]
and Wilson
[
32
]
.
Subsequently, using Lagrange interpolation Good
[
18
]
discovered an elegant one-page proof, and much later, Zeilberger obtained a combinatorial proof using tournaments
[
33
]
.

In 1975, Andrews
[
4
]
conjectured a
q
-analogue of the Dyson constant term identity.

(1.2)

\CT_{\boldsymbol{x}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(x_{i}/x_{j};q)_{a_{i}}(qx_{j}/x_{i};q)_{a_{j}}=\frac{(q;q)_{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}}}{(q;q)_{a_{1}}(q;q)_{a_{2}}\cdots(q;q)_{a_{n}}},

where

(z;q)_{k}:=(1-z)(1-zq)\cdots(1-zq^{k-1})
is a

q
-shifted factorial for a positive integer
k
and
(z;q)_{0}:=1
.
Up till now, there exist four essentially different proofs of the
q
-Dyson identity
[
34
,
17
,
24
,
10
]
.

The equal parameter case of the
q
-Dyson identity leads to two directions.
One is the famous Macdonald constant term conjecture
[
12
,
25
]
and the theory of Macdonald symmetric function
[
26
,
27
]
. Another direction is the
q
-Morris identity and its equivalent
q
-Selberg integral
[
5
,
28
,
21
,
20
]
. Since the discovery of the Selberg integral
[
30
]
, many generalizations were found, such as
[
1
,
2
,
6
,
22
,
23
,
31
]
. Forrester and Warnaar
[
16
]
gave a detailed review of the importance of the Selberg integral.

In the constant term world, many identities behave as bridges which connect different branches of mathematics, such as the Morris identity; or generate new theory, such as the Macdonald constant term conjecture; or just remain as items of the list. As far as we know, there exists no structural result yet. In this paper, we investigate a collection of certain constant term operators, and find that they form an algebra.

This work is inspired by the study of the Ehrhart polynomial
H_{n}(t)
of the Birkhoff polytope
[
8
,
9
]
. The problem is equivalent to counting the number of
n\times n
nonnegative integer matrices such that every row sum and every column sum equal to
t
.
The polynomial
H_{n}(t)
has been studied by many researchers, e.g.
[
8
,
11
,
14
,
29
,
3
,
13
,
7
]
. The record is kept by Beck and Pixton
[
8
]
, who obtained an explicit expression for
H_{9}(t)
using residue computation. When studying Beck and Pixton’s residue method using iterated Laurent series, we discovered an algebra of constant term operators. We introduce this algebra below.

It is known that
[
8
]

H_{n}(t)=\CT_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{-t}y_{i}^{-t}}{\prod_{1\leq i,j\leq n}(1-x_{i}y_{j})}.

By eliminating the
\boldsymbol{y}
variables using partial fraction decompositions (PFD as abbreviation), we obtain

(1.3)

H_{n}(t)=\CT_{\boldsymbol{x}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{-t}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i}^{t}}{\prod_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n}(1-x_{j}/x_{i})}\right)^{n}.

Expanding the sum in (
1.3
) by the multinomial theorem gives

(1.4)

H_{n}(t)=\sum\binom{n}{m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{n}}\CT_{\boldsymbol{x}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i}^{(m_{i}-1)t}}{\prod_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n}(1-x_{j}/x_{i})^{m_{i}}},

where the sum is over all the nonnegative integers
m_{i}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}=n
for a fixed positive integer
n
.
Here and what follows, the rational functions in
\boldsymbol{x}
are explained as their Laurent series expansions according to
1>x_{1}>\cdots>x_{n}>0
.

That writing
H_{n}(t)
as in (
1.4
) suggests us to consider the constant term of the next kind of rational functions of type
A

(1.5)

F=\frac{L(\boldsymbol{x})}{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(1-x_{j}/x_{i})^{q_{ij}}},

where
L(\boldsymbol{x})
is a homogeneous Laurent polynomial of degree 0 with coefficients in a field
\mathbb{F}
, the
q_{ij}
are nonnegative integers, and
F
is written in a reduced form. Denote by
\mathcal{A}_{n}=\mathcal{A}_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})
the set of all such rational functions. We focus on the case when
L(\boldsymbol{x})
is a monomial. The general case
follows by linearity.

To describe our algebra, we introduce its basic building blocks: the constant term operator
\CT\limits_{x_{i}=x_{j}}
and its slight extension
\CT\limits_{x_{i}=x_{j}^{*}}
.
For
u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
such that
u_{r}\neq i
,

c_{r}\in\mathbb{C}
and
q_{r}
positive integers, let

(1.6)

f(x_{i})=\frac{L_{0}(x_{i})}{\prod_{r=1}^{m}(1-c_{r}x_{i}/x_{u_{r}})^{q_{r}}},

where
L_{0}(x_{i})
is a Laurent polynomial in
x_{i}
, and
c_{r}\neq c_{s}
if
x_{u_{r}}=x_{u_{s}}
.
Assume the PFD of
f
with respect to
x_{i}
is given by

(1.7)

f(x_{i})=L_{1}(x_{i})+\sum_{r=1}^{m}\frac{A_{r}(x_{i})}{(1-c_{r}x_{i}/x_{u_{r}})^{q_{r}}},

where
L_{1}(x_{i})
is a Laurent polynomial, and
A_{r}(x_{i})
is a polynomial of degree less than
q_{r}
for each
r
.
Define

(1.8)

\CT_{x_{i}=c_{r}^{-1}x_{u_{r}}}f(x_{i})=A_{r}(0)\quad\text{and}\quad\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}^{*}}f(x_{i})=\sum_{u_{r}=j}A_{r}(0).

In fact, we can write

\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}^{*}}f(x_{i})=\sum_{u_{r}=j}\CT_{x_{i}=c_{r}^{-1}x_{u_{r}}}f(x_{i})

by the above definition.
In particular, if there is only one
r
such that
u_{r}=j
, then

\CT\limits_{x_{i}=x_{j}^{*}}f=\CT\limits_{x_{i}=c_{r}^{-1}x_{j}}f
.
Furthermore, if
c_{r}=1
then
\CT\limits_{x_{i}=x_{j}^{*}}f=\CT\limits_{x_{i}=x_{j}}f
.
We define the general operator
\CT\limits_{x_{i}=x_{j}^{*}}
in (
1.8
) for simplicity of some proofs in this paper and further development of this theory in the future.
We remark that when acting on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
, we only need the operator
\CT\limits_{x_{i}=x_{j}}
.

In this paper, we observe that such constant terms
\CT\limits_{x_{i}=x_{j}}
as operators on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
generate an algebra. We call it the constant term algebra of type
A
and denote by
\Xi
. This algebra characterizes the structure of certain constant term operators.
In this paper, we also discover a basis of
\Xi
, as stated in Theorem
3.22
below.
In the light of
\Xi
, we develop a new method to compute constant terms of type
A
rational functions.
In the next sequel, we will use
\Xi
to simplify the computation of
H_{n}(t)
. It is hopeful to obtain an explicit formula for
H_{10}(t)
.

The structure of this paper is as follows.
In Section 2, we introduce notation and basic facts about
\Xi
.
In Section 3, we establish the structure of
\Xi
by giving an explicit basis.

2.
Notation and basic facts

In this section, we give notations used throughout this paper and introduce several basic facts
of
\Xi
.

We obtain an explicit expression for the PFD of the rational function
f(x_{i})

in (
1.6
) using differential operators.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
f(x_{i})
and
A_{r}(x_{i})
be as in (
1.6
) and (
1.7
) respectively. For
r\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
, denote

(2.1)

g_{r}(x_{i})=f(x_{i})(1-c_{r}x_{i}/x_{u_{r}})^{q_{r}}=\frac{L_{0}(x_{i})}{\prod_{l=1,l\neq r}^{m}(1-c_{l}x_{i}/x_{u_{l}})^{q_{l}}}.

Then

(2.2)

A_{r}(x_{i})=\sum_{k=0}^{q_{r}-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}\big(D_{w}^{k}g_{r}(wc_{r}^{-1}x_{u_{r}})\big)\big|_{w=1}}{k!}\cdot(1-c_{r}x_{i}/x_{u_{r}})^{k},

where
D_{w}
is the differential operator with respect to
w
.
In particular,

(2.3)

\CT_{x_{i}=c_{r}^{-1}x_{u_{r}}}f(x_{i})=A_{r}(0)=\frac{(-1)^{q_{r}-1}}{(q_{r}-1)!}\Big(D_{w}^{q_{r}-1}\frac{g_{r}(wc_{r}^{-1}x_{u_{r}})}{w}\Big)\Big|_{w=1}.

Furthermore, if
q_{r}=1
then

(2.4)

A_{r}(0)=g_{r}(c_{r}^{-1}x_{u_{r}}).

Proof.

We only prove the
r=1
case. The other cases can be obtained by symmetry.

Observe that

\frac{(-1)^{q_{1}-1}}{(q_{1}-1)!}D_{w}^{q_{1}-1}\Big(\frac{1}{w-c_{1}x_{i}/x_{u_{1}}}\Big)=\frac{1}{(w-c_{1}x_{i}/x_{u_{1}})^{q_{1}}}.

Using this we can write

(2.5)

f(x_{i})=\frac{(-1)^{q_{1}-1}}{(q_{1}-1)!}\Big(D_{w}^{q_{1}-1}\frac{L_{0}(x_{i})}{(w-c_{1}x_{i}/x_{u_{1}})\prod_{r=2}^{m}(1-c_{r}x_{i}/x_{u_{r}})^{q_{r}}}\Big)\Big|_{w=1}.

The PFD of the next rational function with respect to
x_{i}
is given by

(2.6)

\frac{L_{0}(x_{i})}{(w-c_{1}x_{i}/x_{u_{1}})\prod_{r=2}^{m}(1-c_{r}x_{i}/x_{u_{r}})^{q_{r}}}=\overline{L}_{1}(x_{i},w)+\frac{\overline{A}_{1}(x_{i},w)}{(w-c_{1}x_{i}/x_{u_{1}})}+\sum_{r=2}^{m}\frac{\overline{A}_{r}(x_{i},w)}{(1-c_{r}x_{i}/x_{u_{r}})^{q_{r}}},

where

\overline{A}_{1}(x_{i},w)=\frac{L_{0}(x_{i})}{\prod_{r=2}^{m}(1-c_{r}x_{i}/x_{u_{r}})^{q_{r}}}\Big|_{x_{i}=wc_{1}^{-1}x_{u_{1}}}=g_{1}(wc_{1}^{-1}x_{u_{1}}),

and
\overline{L}_{1}(x_{i},w)
and
\overline{A}_{r}(x_{i},w)
are Laurent polynomial and polynomial in
x_{i}
but rational functions in
w
respectively.
Substituting (
2.6
) into (
2.5
) yields

f(x_{i})=\frac{(-1)^{q_{1}-1}}{(q_{1}-1)!}\Big(D_{w}^{q_{1}-1}\frac{g_{1}(wc_{1}^{-1}x_{u_{1}})}{w-c_{1}x_{i}/x_{u_{1}}}\Big)\Big|_{w=1}\\
+\frac{(-1)^{q_{1}-1}}{(q_{1}-1)!}\Big(D_{w}^{q_{1}-1}\overline{L}_{1}(x_{i},w)+D_{w}^{q_{1}-1}\sum_{r=2}^{m}\frac{\overline{A}_{r}(x_{i},w)}{(1-c_{r}x_{i}/x_{u_{r}})^{q_{r}}}\Big)\Big|_{w=1}.

By the uniqueness of the PFD of
f(x_{i})
,

(2.7)

\frac{A_{1}(x_{i})}{(1-c_{1}x_{i}/x_{u_{1}})^{q_{1}}}=\frac{(-1)^{q_{1}-1}}{(q_{1}-1)!}\Big(D_{w}^{q_{1}-1}\frac{g_{1}(wc_{1}^{-1}x_{u_{1}})}{w-c_{1}x_{i}/x_{u_{1}}}\Big)\Big|_{w=1}.

We can further write

\displaystyle D_{w}^{q_{1}-1}\frac{g_{1}(wc_{1}^{-1}x_{u_{1}})}{w-c_{1}x_{i}/x_{u_{1}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{q_{1}-1}\binom{q_{1}-1}{k}D_{w}^{k}g_{1}(wc_{1}^{-1}x_{u_{1}})\cdot D_{w}^{q_{1}-k-1}(w-c_{1}x_{i}/x_{u_{1}})^{-1}

(2.8)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{q_{1}-1}(-1)^{q_{1}-k-1}(q_{1}-k-1)!\binom{q_{1}-1}{k}\frac{D_{w}^{k}g_{1}(wc_{1}^{-1}x_{u_{1}})}{(w-c_{1}x_{i}/x_{u_{1}})^{q_{1}-k}}.

Substituting (
2.8
) into (
2.7
) yields (
2.2
) for the
r=1
case.

Taking
x_{i}=0
in (
2.7
) gives (
2.3
) for the
r=1
case. That is the formula for
A_{1}(0)
.

Taking
q_{r}=1
in (
2.3
) yields (
2.4
).
∎

Remark 2.2
.

The view of the operator
\CT\limits_{x_{i}=c_{r}^{-1}x_{u_{r}}}

using Laurent series may be helpful.

Expand
f(x_{i})
as a Laurent series at
x_{i}=c_{r}^{-1}x_{u_{r}}

f(x_{i})=\sum_{k=-q_{r}}^{\infty}h_{k}(c_{r})(1-c_{r}x_{i}/x_{u_{r}})^{k},

where the

are rational functions free of
x_{i}
. Comparing this with (
1.7

\frac{A_{r}(x_{i})}{(1-c_{r}x_{i}/x_{u_{r}})^{q_{r}}}=\sum_{k=-q_{r}}^{-1}h_{k}(c_{r})(1-c_{r}x_{i}/x_{u_{r}})^{k}.

It follows that

\CT_{x_{i}=c_{r}^{-1}x_{u_{r}}}f(x_{i})=A_{r}(0)=\sum_{k=-q_{r}}^{-1}h_{k}(c_{r}).

In particular,

\CT_{x_{i}=c_{r}^{-1}x_{u_{r}}}(1-c_{r}x_{i}/x_{u_{r}})^{k}=\begin{cases}1,\quad\text{if $k<0$;}\\
0,\quad\text{if $k\geq 0$.}\end{cases}

If all the
c_{r}=1
, the rational function
f(x_{i})
in (
1.6
) is a rational function of type
A
in (
1.5
), i.e.,

f(x_{i})=\frac{L_{0}(x_{i})}{\prod_{r=1}^{m}(1-x_{i}/x_{u_{r}})^{q_{r}}},

where the distinct integers
u_{r}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
are not equal to
i
.
By (
1.7
),

(2.9)

f(x_{i})=L_{1}(x_{i})+\sum_{r=1}^{m}\frac{A_{r}(x_{i})}{(1-x_{i}/x_{u_{r}})^{q_{r}}}.

We have an expression for
A_{r}(x_{i})
in (
2.2
).
But the explicit formula for
A_{r}(x_{i})
without the slack variable
w
is quite complicated.
Nevertheless, we can obtain its denominator. That is the next result.

Corollary 2.3
.

The denominator of
A_{r}(x_{i})
in (

) is
\prod_{l=1,\;l\neq r}^{m}(1-x_{u_{r}}/x_{u_{l}})^{q_{l}+q_{r}-1}

Note that
A_{r}(x_{i})
is only a polynomial in

, but it may be a rational function

\prod_{l=1,\;l\neq r}^{m}(1-x_{u_{r}}/x_{u_{l}})^{q_{l}+q_{r}-1}

Proof.

We only prove the

case. The other cases can be obtained by symmetry.

By Lemma
2.1
,

A_{1}(x_{i})=\sum_{k=0}^{q_{1}-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\Big(D_{w}^{k}\frac{L_{0}(wx_{u_{1}})}{\prod_{l=2}^{m}(1-wx_{u_{1}}/x_{u_{l}})^{q_{l}}}\Big)\Big|_{w=1}\cdot(1-x_{i}/x_{u_{1}})^{k}.

It is clear that
\prod_{l=2}^{m}(1-x_{u_{1}}/x_{u_{l}})^{q_{l}+k}

\big(D_{w}^{k}\frac{L_{0}(wx_{u_{1}})}{\prod_{l=2}^{m}(1-wx_{u_{1}}/x_{u_{l}})^{q_{l}}}\big)\big|_{w=1}

∎

We give the following simple result, which will be used in the next section.

Corollary 2.4
.

The PFD of the next rational function with respect to
x
is giving by

\frac{1}{(1-x/y)^{a}(1-x/z)^{b}(1-y/z)^{c}}=\frac{L_{1}(x,y,z)}{(1-x/y)^{a}(1-y/z)^{a+b+c-1}}+\frac{L_{2}(x,y,z)}{(1-x/z)^{b}(1-y/z)^{a+b+c-1}},

where
a,b,c
are nonnegative integers, and
L_{1}(x,y,z),L_{2}(x,y,z)
are Laurent polynomials in
x,y,z
.

Proof.

We can write

\frac{1}{(1-x/y)^{a}(1-x/z)^{b}(1-y/z)^{c}}=\frac{A_{1}(x)}{(1-x/y)^{a}}+\frac{A_{2}(x)}{(1-x/z)^{b}}

by the PFD with respect to
x
.
Here we have explicit formulas for
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
by Lemma
2.1
.
Using (
2.2
) in Lemma
2.1
, we have

A_{1}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{a-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\Big(D_{w}^{k}\frac{1}{(1-wy/z)^{b}(1-y/z)^{c}}\Big)\Big|_{w=1}\cdot(1-x/y)^{k}.

It is clear that
(1-y/z)^{b+c+k}
is the denominator of
\big(D_{w}^{k}\frac{1}{(1-wy/z)^{b}(1-y/z)^{c}}\big)\big|_{w=1}
.
Then
(1-y/z)^{a+b+c-1}
is the denominator of
A_{1}
. It follows that
\frac{A_{1}(x)}{(1-x/y)^{a}}
is of the form

\frac{L_{1}(x,y,z)}{(1-x/y)^{a}(1-y/z)^{a+b+c-1}}

in the corollary.
The formula for
A_{2}
is similar.
∎

Using (

) and (

) we can obtain the next result for computing

\CT\limits_{x_{i}=x_{j}^{*}}f(x_{i})
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
f(x_{i})
,
g_{r}(x_{i})
and
\CT\limits_{x_{i}=x_{j}^{*}}
be defined in (
1.6
),(
2.1
) and (
1.8
) respectively.
Then

(2.10)

\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}^{*}}f(x_{i})=\sum\frac{(-1)^{q_{r}-1}}{(q_{r}-1)!}\Big(D_{w}^{q_{r}-1}\frac{g_{r}(wc_{r}^{-1}x_{u_{r}})}{w}\Big)\Big|_{w=1},

where the sum here and in the next equation is over all
r
such that
u_{r}=j
.
In particular, if all the
q_{r}
in the above sum equal
1
, then

(2.11)

\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}^{*}}f(x_{i})=\sum g_{r}(c_{r}^{-1}x_{u_{r}}).

Recall the definition of the constant term operator
\CT\limits_{x_{i}=x_{j}^{*}}
in (
1.8
). It reduces to
\CT\limits_{x_{i}=x_{j}}
when acting on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
.
We denote
\CT\limits_{x_{i}=x_{j}}
by
[i,j]
for simplicity and refer it as a commutator. The constant term algebra
\Xi
of type
A
is generated by all such commutators. The algebra
\Xi
is a subalgebra of the algebra consisting of all linear transformations on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
.

An operator
\mathbf{L}\in\Xi
is
0
if and only if
\mathbf{L}\circ F=\mathbf{L}(F)=0
for all
F\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
.
The scalar multiplication, sum, and multiplication of operators are naturally defined
for
\mathbf{L}_{1},\mathbf{L}_{2}\in\Xi
and
F\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
as

(2.12a)

(k\cdot\mathbf{L}_{1})\circ F=k\big(\mathbf{L}_{1}(F)\big)\quad\text{for}\quad k\in\mathbb{C},

(2.12b)

(\mathbf{L}_{2}+\mathbf{L}_{1})\circ F=\mathbf{L}_{2}(F)+\mathbf{L}_{1}(F),

and

(2.12c)

(\mathbf{L}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{L}_{1})\circ F=\mathbf{L}_{2}\big(\mathbf{L}_{1}(F)\big)

respectively.

We simply write
\mathbf{L}(F)=\mathbf{L}F
if there is no ambiguity.
We say a monomial operator
L=[i_{s},j_{s}]\cdots[i_{1},j_{1}]
is of degree
s=\deg(L)
if it is nonzero. The identity operator
id
is of degree
0
. Clearly the product
L_{1}\cdot L_{2}
of two monomial operators is either
0
or of degree
\deg(L_{1})+\deg(L_{2})
.

The algebra
\Xi
of type
A
is a graded algebra with a natural decomposition

\Xi=\biguplus_{s=0}^{n-1}\Xi^{s},

where
\Xi^{s}
is the vector space spanned by all monomial operators of degree
s
. We will show that
this is in fact a direct sum decomposition and that
\Xi^{s}
is the null space when
s\geq n
.

The
n=1
case is worth mentioning. Type
A
rational functions in one variable are indeed complex numbers, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}_{1}=\mathbb{C}
:
by definition each
F\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
is a homogeneous Laurent polynomial in
x_{1}
of degree
0
, and hence a complex number. Consequently,
\Xi^{0}
is spanned by the identity operator
id
.

In what follows, a graph
G=(V,E)
is always a simple (vertex) labelled graph unless specified otherwise, where
V
and
E
are the vertex set and the edge set of
G
respectively.
If
(u,v)\in E
, let
G/{u\to v}
be the graph obtained from
G
by identifying
u
with
v
(or contracting
u
to
v
).
Define a subset of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
according to
H
(a subgraph of the complete graph
K_{n}
) as

\mathcal{A}_{n}(H)=\Big\{F:F=\frac{L(\boldsymbol{x})}{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(1-x_{j}/x_{i})^{q_{ij}}}\in\mathcal{A}_{n},q_{ij}=0\text{ if }(i,j)\notin E(H)\Big\}.

We call
x_{i}=x_{j}
(
x_{j}=x_{i}
) a nonzero pole of
F
with multiplicity
q_{ij}
if
q_{ij}>0
.
More generally, we say that
x_{i}=dx_{j}
(
x_{j}=d^{-1}x_{i}
) is a pole of a reduced rational function
R

if
1-x_{i}/(dx_{j})
is a factor of the denominator of
R
for
d\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
.
Then it is obvious to obtain the next simple result (by Corollary
2.3
).

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
F\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
,
[i,j]\in\Xi
, and
H
be a subgraph of
K_{n}
.

(1)

If
x_{i}=x_{j}
is not a pole of
F
, then
[i,j]F=0
.

(2)

The action of
[i,j]
on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is closed. That is,
[i,j]F\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
. Explicitly,

[i,j]
eliminates
x_{i}
and takes
\mathcal{A}_{n}(H)
to
\mathcal{A}_{n}(H/{i\to j})
.
Thus, if a nonzero monomial operator
L\in\Xi
contains
[i,j]
, then
i
can not appear to the left of
[i,j]
in
L
.

We will identify a monomial operator with its ordered digraph.

For a monomial operator
L=[i_{s},j_{s}]\cdots[i_{1},j_{1}]\in\Xi
, define an ordered digraph
D(L)
of
L
as: the vertex set of
D(L)
is
\{i_{1},j_{1},\dots,i_{s},j_{s}\}
, which is a subset of
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, and the edge set contains all the directed edges
\{i_{k}\rightarrow j_{k}:1\leq k\leq s\}
. Furthermore, for each inner vertex
j
, if several edges direct to
j
, then we put these edges from right to left as their corresponding order in
L
. That is, “ordered” means that the children of every inner vertex are arranged from right to left according to their order in
L
. See Figure
1
for an example.
If all the edges of a connected digraph direct towards a vertex
r
, then we say
r
is the root. Having the above definition, we give the next corollary of Lemma
2.6
.

D(L)
of
L=[6,2][5,6][1,2][7,2][3,7][8,7][4,2]
.

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
L=[i_{s},j_{s}]\cdots[i_{1},j_{1}]\in\Xi
. Then

(1)

The monomial operator
L
is nonzero only when
s\leq n-1
and all the
i
’s are distinct. Then,
\Xi^{s}
is the null space for
s\geq n
, and each vertex of
D(L)
has out degree at most
1
.

(2)

The operator
L
is nonzero only when its digraph
D(L)
is a directed rooted forest.

Proof.

(1) Assume two
i
’s in
L
are identical, say
i_{a}=i_{b}
for
1\leq a<b\leq s
. For any
F\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
, by the part (2) of Lemma
2.6
we have
L(F)=0
.

If
s>n
, then there exist two identical
i
’s in
L
since all the
i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. By the discussion above, we have
L(F)=0
.

If
s=n
and all the
i
’s are distinct, then
(i_{1},\dots,i_{n})
is a permutation of
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. For any
F\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
,
we have
E=[i_{n-1},j_{n-1}]\cdots[i_{1},j_{1}]F\in\mathbb{C}
since
F
is homogeneous in
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
of degree 0.
Applying
[i_{n},j_{n}]
to
E
yields 0.

If a vertex of
D(L)
has out degree at least two, then there exist two identical
i
’s in
L
. By the discussion above,
L=0
.

(2) By the result that out degree of every vertex is at most 1 in the part (1),
D(L)
must be rooted. Then, it suffices to show that
D(L)
contains no cycle.
Assume to the contrary that
D(L)
contains a cycle, then the cycle must be a directed cycle since
out degree of every vertex is at most 1 (again by the part (1)).
Denote the directed cycle of
D(L)
by

[i_{s^{\prime}},j_{s^{\prime}}],\dots,[i_{2^{\prime}},j_{2^{\prime}}],[i_{1^{\prime}},j_{1^{\prime}}]
with
1\leq 1^{\prime}<2^{\prime}<\cdots<s^{\prime}\leq s

and
\{i_{1^{\prime}},\dots,i_{s^{\prime}}\}=\{j_{1^{\prime}},\dots,j_{s^{\prime}}\}
.
That is, there exits an
l^{\prime}\in\{2^{\prime},\dots,s^{\prime}\}
such that
j_{l^{\prime}}=i_{1^{\prime}}
.
By the part (2) of Lemma
2.6
, we have
L=0
, a contradiction.
∎

3.
The structure of
\Xi

In this section, we obtain a structural result of the constant term algebra of type
A
.
That is, we characterize the basis of
\Xi
.

3.1.
The rules in
\Xi

In this subsection, we mainly introduce three important rules in
\Xi
for constructing its basis: the Commutativity Rule, the Exchange Rule and the V-Formula.
Equation (
2.10
) is our basic tool for proving these rules.

Let us begin with the next simple result, which will be used in constructing the basis of
\Xi^{n-1}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
F
be a rational function in two variables of the form

F=\frac{L(x_{i},x_{j})}{(1-x_{i}/x_{j})^{m}},

where
L(x_{i},x_{j})
is a homogeneous Laurent polynomial in
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
of degree 0 with coefficients in
\mathbb{C}
, and
m
is a nonnegative integer.
Then

(3.1)

[i,j]F=-[j,i]F\in\mathbb{C}.

Proof.

For
m=0
, both sides of (
3.1
) vanish by the part (1) of Lemma
2.6
.
Hence, we assume
m
to be a positive integer in the following proof.
Since
L(x_{i},x_{j})
is homogeneous in
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
of degree 0, we may assume
L=(x_{i}/x_{j})^{k}
for an integer
k
by linearity.
By (
2.10
),

[i,j]F=[i,j]\frac{(x_{i}/x_{j})^{k}}{(1-x_{i}/x_{j})^{m}}=\bigg(\frac{(-D_{w})^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}w^{k-1}\bigg)\bigg|_{w=1}=\frac{(-1)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}(k-1)(k-2)\cdots(k-m+1),

and

[j,i]F=[j,i]\frac{(-1)^{m}(x_{i}/x_{j})^{k-m}}{(1-x_{j}/x_{i})^{m}}=\bigg(\frac{(-D_{w})^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}(-1)^{m}w^{-k+m-1}\bigg)\bigg|_{w=1}\\
=\frac{(-1)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}(-1)^{m}(-k+m-1)\cdots(-k+2)(-k+1)=\frac{(-1)^{m}}{(m-1)!}(k-1)(k-2)\cdots(k-m+1).

Then (
3.1
) holds and the lemma follows.
∎

To find the relationship between the operators of
\Xi
, we need a formula for
[i^{\prime},j^{\prime}][i,j]F
.
Equation (
2.10
) provides an explicit formula for
[i,j]F
involving the slack variable
w
(set
w=1
at last) and the differential operator
D_{w}
.
It is not wise to remove
w
before applying
[i^{\prime},j^{\prime}]
to
[i,j]F
. Because the expression for
[i,j]F
after removing

w
is quite complicated.
Thus, it is necessary to work on parameterized type
A
rational functions.
Denote by
\mathcal{A}_{n}[u,u^{-1}]
the ring of Laurent polynomial of
u
with coefficients in
\mathcal{A}_{n}
.
It is natural to obtain the following switch rules.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
u
be a parameter in
\mathbb{C}
, and let
f,g
be rational functions of the form

f=\frac{E(u,x_{i},x_{j})}{(u-x_{i}/x_{j})^{q}},\qquad g=\frac{E(u,x_{i},x_{j})}{(1-ux_{i}/x_{j})^{q}},

where
q\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x_{i}=x_{j}
is not a pole of
E\in\mathcal{A}_{n}[u,u^{-1}]
. Then

(3.2)

\displaystyle D_{u}\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}^{*}}f

\displaystyle=\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}^{*}}D_{u}f,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}}(g|_{u=1})

\displaystyle=\Big(\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}^{*}}g\Big)\Big|_{u=1}.

Proof.

The case
q\leq 0
is trivial since both sides of (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) vanish.
So we assume
q>0
.

By Remark
2.2
,
we can write

f=\sum_{k=-q}^{\infty}h_{k}(u)\big(1-x_{i}/(ux_{j})\big)^{k}.

To prove (
3.2
), we compute directly:

D_{u}\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}^{*}}f=D_{u}\CT_{x_{i}=ux_{j}}f=D_{u}\sum_{k=-q}^{-1}h_{k}(u)=\sum_{k=-q}^{-1}h_{k}^{\prime}(u),

and

\displaystyle\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}^{*}}D_{u}f

\displaystyle=\CT_{x_{i}=ux_{j}}\sum_{k=-q}^{\infty}\Big(h_{k}^{\prime}(u)\big(1-x_{i}/(ux_{j})\big)^{k}+ku^{-2}h_{k}(u)(x_{i}/x_{j})\big(1-x_{i}/(ux_{j})\big)^{k-1}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-q}^{-1}h_{k}^{\prime}(u)+\sum_{k=-q}^{\infty}ku^{-2}h_{k}(u)\CT_{x_{i}=ux_{j}}(x_{i}/x_{j})\big(1-x_{i}/(ux_{j})\big)^{k-1}=\sum_{k=-q}^{-1}h_{k}^{\prime}(u).

Here the last equation holds since

\CT\limits_{x_{i}=ux_{j}}(x_{i}/x_{j})\big(1-x_{i}/(ux_{j})\big)^{k-1}=0

(
x_{i}=ux_{j}
is not a pole for
k>0
and using the definition (
1.8
) directly for
k<0
).

To prove (
3.3
), using Remark
2.2
again, we can write

g=\frac{E(u,x_{i},x_{j})}{(1-ux_{i}/x_{j})^{q}}=\sum_{k=-q}^{\infty}\tilde{h}_{k}(u)(1-ux_{i}/x_{j})^{k},

and

g|_{u=1}=\frac{E(1,x_{i},x_{j})}{(1-x_{i}/x_{j})^{q}}=\sum_{k=-q}^{\infty}\overline{h}_{k}(1-x_{i}/x_{j})^{k}.

Then
\tilde{h}_{k}(1)=\overline{h}_{k}
exists for each
k
, and we obtain

\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}}(g|_{u=1})=\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}}\sum_{k=-q}^{\infty}\tilde{h}_{k}(1)(1-x_{i}/x_{j})^{k}=\sum_{k=-q}^{-1}\tilde{h}_{k}(1),

and

\Big(\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}^{*}}g\Big)\Big|_{u=1}=\Big(\CT_{x_{i}=u^{-1}x_{j}}g\Big)\Big|_{u=1}=\Big(\sum_{k=-q}^{-1}\tilde{h}_{k}(u)\Big)\Big|_{u=1}=\sum_{k=-q}^{-1}\tilde{h}_{k}(1).

Note that this argument does not work if
E
has a pole at
x_{i}=x_{j}
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Equation (
3.3
)
also holds if
g
is multiplied by
(1-x_{i}/x_{j})^{-q^{\prime}}
for another integer
q^{\prime}
, but it seems hard to verify that
directly.

Now we introduce the Commutativity Rule.

Lemma 3.4

(Commutativity Rule)
.

Suppose
1\leq i,j,k,l\leq n
are distinct integers. Then

[k,l][i,j]=[i,j][k,l],\quad i.e.,\quad\CT_{x_{k}=x_{l}}\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}}F=\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}}\CT_{x_{k}=x_{l}}F\text{ for all }F\in\mathcal{A}_{n}.

Proof.

For any
F\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
, suppose
x_{i}=x_{j}
and
x_{k}=x_{l}
are poles of
F
with multiplicities
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
respectively. Then, we can write

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=\frac{E(x_{i},x_{j},x_{k},x_{l})}{(1-x_{i}/x_{j})^{m_{1}}(1-x_{k}/x_{l})^{m_{2}}},

where
E\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
may contain other variables but
x_{i}=x_{j}
and
x_{k}=x_{l}
are not poles of
E
.
By applying the constant term operator
[i,j]
to
F
and using (
2.10
),

\displaystyle[i,j]F

\displaystyle=\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\frac{E(ux_{j},x_{j},x_{k},x_{l})}{u(1-x_{k}/x_{l})^{m_{2}}}\Big|_{u=1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-x_{k}/x_{l})^{m_{2}}}\left(\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\frac{E(ux_{j},x_{j},x_{k},x_{l})}{u}\Big|_{u=1}\right).

Next applying
[k,l]
to both sides of the above and using (
2.10
) again, we have

\displaystyle[k,l][i,j]F

\displaystyle=[k,l]\frac{1}{(1-x_{k}/x_{l})^{m_{2}}}\left(\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\frac{E(ux_{j},x_{j},x_{k},x_{l})}{u}\Big|_{u=1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-D_{v})^{m_{2}-1}}{(m_{2}-1)!}\frac{1}{v}\left(\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\frac{E(ux_{j},x_{j},vx_{l},x_{l})}{u}\Big|_{u=1}\right)\Big|_{v=1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\frac{(-D_{v})^{m_{2}-1}}{(m_{2}-1)!}\frac{E(ux_{j},x_{j},vx_{l},x_{l})}{uv}\Big|_{u=v=1}.

The computation of
[i,j][k,l]F
is completely same, except that we shall use
v
for the first slack variable when applying
[k,l]
and
use
u
for the second slack variable.
We obtain

[i,j][k,l]F=\frac{(-D_{v})^{m_{2}-1}}{(m_{2}-1)!}\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\frac{E(ux_{j},x_{j},vx_{l},x_{l})}{uv}\Big|_{v=u=1}.

This completes the proof.
∎

By Lemma
3.4
, we can obtain the next corollary. Then we can identify
a monomial operator of
\Xi
as its corresponding ordered digraph.

Corollary 3.5
.

If two monomial operators
L,L^{\prime}\in\Xi
have the same ordered digraph, i.e.,

D(L)=D(L^{\prime})
, then
L=L^{\prime}
.

Proof.

We prove by induction on
s=\deg(L)
. The base cases
s\leq 1
is trivial.
Assume the corollary holds for
s-1
and less. We prove it holds for
s
.

If
D(L)
has at least two components, say
T_{1},T_{2},\dots,T_{k}
(
k\geq 2
).
For a given
1\leq i\leq k
, if there exist two monomial operators
L_{i},L_{i}^{\prime}\in\Xi
such that
D(L_{i})=T_{i}=D(L_{i}^{\prime})
, then
L_{i}=L_{i}^{\prime}
by the induction hypothesis.
The corollary follows by the commutativity of the
L_{i}
’s using Lemma
3.4
.

If
D(L)
has only one component, then by the part (2) of Corollary
2.7
,

D(L)
must be an ordered directed rooted tree with root
r
.
Assume the vertices
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}
directing to
r
are arranged from right to left.
For
i=1,\dots,k
, let
T_{i}
denote the ordered subtree rooted at
r_{i}
.
See Figure
2
below.
If there exist two monomial operators
L_{i},L_{i}^{\prime}\in\Xi
such that

D(L_{i})=T_{i}=D(L_{i}^{\prime})
for a given
1\leq i\leq k
, then
L_{i}=L_{i}^{\prime}
by the induction hypothesis. By the Commutativity Rule (Lemma
3.4
), we have

L=[r_{k},r]L_{k}[r_{k-1},r]L_{k-1}\cdots[r_{1},r]L_{1}=[r_{k},r]\cdots[r_{1},r]L_{k}\cdots L_{1}=L^{\prime}.

Figure 2.
T_{i}
: the ordered subtree rooted at
r_{i}
.

This completes the proof.
∎

The second basic rule in
\Xi
is the next Exchange Rule.

Lemma 3.6

(Exchange Rule)
.

For distinct
i,j,k\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and
F\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
,

[j,k][i,j]=-[i,k][j,i],\quad i.e.,\quad\CT_{x_{j}=x_{k}}\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}}F=-\CT_{x_{i}=x_{k}}\CT_{x_{j}=x_{i}}F.

Proof.

Assume
F\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
can be written as

(3.4)

F=\frac{E(x_{i},x_{j},x_{k})}{(1-x_{i}/x_{j})^{m_{1}}(1-x_{j}/x_{k})^{m_{2}}(1-x_{i}/x_{k})^{m_{3}}},

where
x_{i}=x_{j}
,
x_{j}=x_{k}
and
x_{i}=x_{k}
are not poles of
E
.

By Corollary
2.4
, we can write

(3.5)

F=F_{1}+F_{2}=\frac{E_{1}(x_{i},x_{j},x_{k})}{(1-x_{i}/x_{j})^{m_{1}}(1-x_{j}/x_{k})^{m^{\prime}_{2}}}+\frac{E_{2}(x_{i},x_{j},x_{k})}{(1-x_{j}/x_{k})^{m^{\prime}_{2}}(1-x_{i}/x_{k})^{m_{3}}},

where
E_{1},E_{2}
has no poles at
x_{i}=x_{j}
,
x_{j}=x_{k}
and
x_{i}=x_{k}
.
Since
x_{i}=x_{j}
is not a pole of
F_{2}
, we have
[i,j]F_{2}=[j,i]F_{2}=0
by the part (1) of Lemma
2.6
.
Consequently
[j,k][i,j]F_{2}=-[i,k][j,i]F_{2}=0
.

The lemma then holds by showing that
[j,k][i,j]F_{1}=-[i,k][j,i]F_{1}
.
Applying the constant term operator
[i,j]
to
F_{1}
and using (
2.10
) gives

(3.6)

[i,j]F_{1}=\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\frac{E_{1}(ux_{j},x_{j},x_{k})}{u(1-x_{j}/x_{k})^{m^{\prime}_{2}}}\bigg|_{u=1}=\frac{1}{(1-x_{j}/x_{k})^{m^{\prime}_{2}}}\cdot\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\frac{E_{1}(ux_{j},x_{j},x_{k})}{u}\bigg|_{u=1}.

Since
x_{j}=x_{k}
is not a pole of
E_{1}(ux_{j},x_{j},x_{k})
,
applying
[j,k]
and using (
2.10
) again give

\displaystyle[j,k][i,j]F_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-D_{v})^{m^{\prime}_{2}-1}}{(m^{\prime}_{2}-1)!}\frac{1}{v}\bigg(\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\frac{E_{1}(uvx_{k},vx_{k},x_{k})}{u}\bigg|_{u=1}\bigg)\bigg|_{v=1}

(3.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\frac{(-D_{v})^{m^{\prime}_{2}-1}}{(m^{\prime}_{2}-1)!}\frac{E_{1}(uvx_{k},vx_{k},x_{k})}{uv}\bigg|_{u=v=1}.

The situation of computing
[i,k][j,i]F_{1}
is a little different. Applying
[j,i]
to
F_{1}

gives

\displaystyle[j,i]F_{1}

\displaystyle=[j,i]\left(\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\frac{E_{1}(x_{i},x_{j},x_{k})}{(u-x_{i}/x_{j})(1-x_{j}/x_{k})^{m^{\prime}_{2}}}\Big|_{u=1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\CT_{x_{j}=x_{i}^{*}}\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\frac{E_{1}(x_{i},x_{j},x_{k})}{(1-ux_{j}/x_{i})(-x_{i}/x_{j})(1-x_{j}/x_{k})^{m^{\prime}_{2}}}\right)\bigg|_{u=1}

by

(
3.3
)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\CT_{x_{j}=x_{i}^{*}}\frac{E_{1}(x_{i},x_{j},x_{k})}{(1-ux_{j}/x_{i})(-x_{i}/x_{j})(1-x_{j}/x_{k})^{m^{\prime}_{2}}}\right)\bigg|_{u=1}

by

(
3.2
)

(3.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\frac{E_{1}(x_{i},x_{i}/u,x_{k})}{-u\big(1-x_{i}/(ux_{k})\big)^{m^{\prime}_{2}}}\bigg|_{u=1}.

by

(
2.10
)

The factor
\big(1-x_{i}/(ux_{k})\big)^{m^{\prime}_{2}}
in the denominator will contribute a pole
x_{i}=x_{k}
after setting
u=1
.

By applying
[i,k]
to both sides of (
3.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle[i,k][j,i]F_{1}

\displaystyle=[i,k]\left(\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\frac{E_{1}(x_{i},x_{i}/u,x_{k})}{-u\big(1-x_{i}/(ux_{k})\big)^{m^{\prime}_{2}}}\Big|_{u=1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\CT_{x_{i}=x_{k}^{*}}\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\frac{E_{1}(x_{i},x_{i}/u,x_{k})}{-u\big(1-x_{i}/(ux_{k})\big)^{m^{\prime}_{2}}}\right)\bigg|_{u=1}.

Here the last equation holds by (
3.3
).
Using (
3.2
) and (
2.10
) again, we obtain

\displaystyle[i,k][j,i]F_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\CT_{x_{i}=x_{k}^{*}}\frac{E_{1}(x_{i},x_{i}/u,x_{k})}{-u\big(1-x_{i}/(ux_{k})\big)^{m^{\prime}_{2}}}\right)\bigg|_{u=1}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\left(\frac{(-D_{v})^{m^{\prime}_{2}-1}}{(m^{\prime}_{2}-1)!}\frac{E_{1}(uvx_{k},vx_{k},x_{k})}{-uv}\Big|_{v=1}\right)\right)\bigg|_{u=1}

(3.9)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\frac{(-D_{v})^{m^{\prime}_{2}-1}}{(m^{\prime}_{2}-1)!}\frac{E_{1}(uvx_{k},vx_{k},x_{k})}{-uv}\bigg|_{u=v=1}.

Comparing (
3.1
) with (
3.1
) yields
[i,k][j,i]F_{1}=-[j,k][i,j]F_{1}
, as desired.
∎

The third rule is the next V-Formula.

Proposition 3.7

(V-Formula)
.

For distinct
i,j,k\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and
F\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
,

[j,k][i,k]=[i,k][j,k]+[i,k][j,i],\quad i.e.,\quad\CT_{x_{j}=x_{k}}\CT_{x_{i}=x_{k}}F=\CT_{x_{i}=x_{k}}\CT_{x_{j}=x_{k}}F+\CT_{x_{i}=x_{k}}\CT_{x_{j}=x_{i}}F.

Proof.

Let
F,F_{1},F_{2}
be as in (
3.4
) and (
3.5
). We can easily obtain
[j,k][i,k]F_{1}=0
and
[i,k][j,i]F_{2}=0
by the part (1) of Lemma
2.6
. Meanwhile,
we have figured out that

[i,k][j,i]F_{1}=\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\frac{(-D_{v})^{m^{\prime}_{2}-1}}{(m^{\prime}_{2}-1)!}\frac{E_{1}(uvx_{k},vx_{k},x_{k})}{-uv}\Big|_{u=v=1}

in the proof of Lemma
3.6
.
To prove the proposition, we compute
[i,k][j,k]F_{1},[j,k][i,k]F_{2}
and
[i,k][j,k]F_{2}
. For the sake of uniformity, we use
u
as the slack variable when applying
[i,k]
and use
v
when applying
[j,k]
.

To get
[i,k][j,k]F_{1}
, by applying
[j,k]
to
F_{1}
and using (
2.10
), we obtain

(3.10)

[j,k]F_{1}=\frac{(-D_{v})^{m^{\prime}_{2}-1}}{(m^{\prime}_{2}-1)!}\frac{E_{1}(x_{i},vx_{k},x_{k})}{\big(1-x_{i}/(vx_{k})\big)^{m_{1}}v}\Big|_{v=1}.

)

.
By applying

\displaystyle[i,k][j,k]F_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{(-D_{v})^{m^{\prime}_{2}-1}}{(m^{\prime}_{2}-1)!}\CT_{x_{i}=x_{k}^{*}}\frac{E_{1}(x_{i},vx_{k},x_{k})}{\big(1-x_{i}/(vx_{k})\big)^{m_{1}}v}\right)\bigg|_{v=1}

(

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{(-D_{v})^{m^{\prime}_{2}-1}}{(m^{\prime}_{2}-1)!}\left(\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\frac{E_{1}(uvx_{k},vx_{k},x_{k})}{uv}\Big|_{u=1}\right)\right)\bigg|_{v=1}

by

(

)

(3.11)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{1}-1}}{(m_{1}-1)!}\frac{(-D_{v})^{m^{\prime}_{2}-1}}{(m^{\prime}_{2}-1)!}\frac{E_{1}(uvx_{k},vx_{k},x_{k})}{uv}\bigg|_{u=v=1}.

To obtain

, by applying

[i,k]F_{2}=\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{3}-1}}{(m_{3}-1)!}\frac{E_{2}(ux_{k},x_{j},x_{k})}{(1-x_{j}/x_{k})^{m^{\prime}_{2}}u}\bigg|_{u=1}=\frac{1}{(1-x_{j}/x_{k})^{m^{\prime}_{2}}}\cdot\frac{(-D_{u})^{m_{3}-1}}{(m_{3}-1)!}\frac{E_{2}(ux_{k},x_{j},x_{k})}{u}\bigg|_{u=1}.

\displaystyle[j,k][i,k]F_{2}

Similar to

By (

) and (

), we have

From the Exchange Rule and the V-Formula, we can obtain the next general exchange property of the operators in
\Xi
.

Lemma 3.8
.

For any monomial operator
L\in\Xi
and distinct
i,j,k\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
,

(3.14)

[j,k]L[i,j]=-[i,k](L|_{j=i})[j,i].

Proof.

The operator on the right-hand side (without the sign) is indeed obtained from that on the left-hand side by exchanging
i
and
j
. Thus their corresponding digraphs are either both forests or both not forests. So we may assume that they are nonzero.

In general, assume

L=L_{s+1}[i_{s},j]L_{s}\cdots[i_{1},j]L_{1},

where all the appearances of
j
are displayed. Thus all the
L_{h}
’s
contain no
j
. Furthermore, no
i_{h}
appears to the left of
[i_{h},j]
, otherwise
this would make
L
to be zero by the part (2) of Lemma
2.6
.
It follows that

[j,k]L[i,j]=[j,k]L_{s+1}[i_{s},j]L_{s}\cdots[i_{1},j]L_{1}[i,j]=[j,k][i_{s},j]\cdots[i_{1},j][i,j]L_{s+1}\cdots L_{1}

by the Commutativity Rule (Lemma
3.4
).
Since the left-hand side of (
3.14
) is nonzero, the
L_{h}
’s contain no
i
also. Then by the same reasoning, we have

[i,k](L|_{j=i})[j,i]=[i,k]L_{s+1}[i_{s},i]L_{s}\cdots[i_{1},i]L_{1}[j,i]=[i,k][i_{s},i]\cdots[i_{1},i][j,i]L_{s+1}\cdots L_{1}.

We complete the proof by showing that

(3.15)

[j,k][i_{s},j]\cdots[i_{1},j][i,j]=-[i,k][i_{s},i]\cdots[i_{1},i][j,i].

We prove (
3.15
) by induction on
s
. The
s=0
case is the Exchange Rule (Lemma
3.6
). Assume (
3.15
) holds for
s-1
.
Denote the left-hand side of (
3.15
) by
H
.
Using the V-Formula, we have

[i_{1},j][i,j]=[i,j][i_{1},j]+[i,j][i_{1},i].

Substituting this into
H
yields

H=[j,k][i_{s},j]\cdots[i_{2},j][i,j][i_{1},j]+[j,k][i_{s},j]\cdots[i_{2},j][i,j][i_{1},i].

By the induction hypothesis, we have

[j,k][i_{s},j]\cdots[i_{2},j][i,j]=-[i,k][i_{s},i]\cdots[i_{2},i][j,i].

Then

H=-[i,k][i_{s},i]\cdots[i_{2},i]([j,i][i_{1},j]+[j,i][i_{1},i]).

Using the V-Formula again gives

H=-[i,k][i_{s},i]\cdots[i_{2},i][i_{1},i][j,i],

which is the right-hand side of (
3.15
).
∎

3.2.
The spanning elements of
\Xi

In this subsection, we find the spanning elements of
\Xi
.

Given an ordered digraph
D
, we call a monomial operator
L
with
D(L)=D
the realization of
D
(denoted by
L(D)
).
By the part (2) of Corollary
2.7
and the Commutativity Rule, to obtain the realization of a given ordered digraph
D
, it suffices to assume
that
D
is an ordered directed rooted tree
T
.
For a given
T
, we read out
L(T)
according to the direct edges of
T
level by level from up to down and left to right. That is, we first read out the highest and left-most edge
[i_{1},j_{1}]
, this is the left-most commutator in
L(T)
. Then, read out the next edge
[i_{2},j_{2}]
to the right and in the same level of
[i_{1},j_{1}]
. Continue this until the right-most edge
[i_{s_{1}},j_{s_{1}}]
in the highest level. Then read out the edges in the next level from left to right, say
[i_{s_{1}+1},j_{s_{1}+1}]\cdots[i_{s_{1}+s_{2}},j_{s_{1}+s_{2}}]
.
The reading ends at the lowest level and right-most edge
[i_{s},j_{s}]
of
T
.
Finally,

{L(T)}=[i_{1},j_{1}]\cdots[i_{s_{1}},j_{s_{1}}][i_{s_{1}+1},j_{s_{1}+1}]\cdots[i_{s_{1}+s_{2}},j_{s_{1}+s_{2}}]\cdots[i_{s},j_{s}].

For example, the realization of Figure
1
is:
[6,2][1,2][7,2][4,2][5,6][3,7][8,7]
.
Having Corollary
3.5
and the discussion above, we can identify a monomial operator
L\in\Xi
as an ordered directed rooted forest
D
, and vise versa.
In what follows, we always assume that a forest is ordered, directed and rooted, unless specified otherwise.

Note that the above way of reading is not unique. From the proof of Corollary
3.5
, we can give another way of reading according to subtrees (we omit the details).
But the realizations obtained by these two distinct ways of reading are equivalent by the Commutativity Rule.

To state our structural result, we need more notations.

Let
\mathbf{S}=\{S_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{\ell}\}
be a partition of
N=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
with
\ell=|\mathbf{S}|
parts. That is,
N
is the disjoint union of the nonempty sets
S_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,\ell
. For convenience, we arrange the
S_{i}
increasingly according to their minimal elements. Hence in particular
1\in S_{1}
.
Denote by
\mathrm{Par}(N,\ell)
the set of all partitions of
N
with
\ell
blocks, and let
\mathrm{Par}(N)=\cup_{\ell=1}^{n}\mathrm{Par}(N,\ell)
.
A tree
T
is said to be on
S
if the vertex set of
T
is
S
.
A forest
D
is said to be on
\mathbf{S}
if its connected components are trees on
S_{1}
, …,
S_{\ell}
. It is clear that
D
has exactly
n-|\mathbf{S}|
edges. Note that
|S_{i}|=1
gives the empty tree with the single-vertex set
S_{i}
.
Denote by
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{S}}
the set of all rooted forests on
\mathbf{S}
. Let
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{\ell})
be a sequence of integers. The sequence
\mathbf{r}
is
compatible
with
\mathbf{S}
if
r_{i}\in S_{i}
for every
i
. We use
\mathbf{r}\;\;\mathrm{compat}\;\;\mathbf{S}
to represent this.
Let
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}}
be the set of forests on
\mathbf{S}
with given roots
\mathbf{r}
. Denote by
\mathcal{T}_{S}
the set of all rooted trees on
S\subseteq N
,
and let
\mathcal{T}_{S,r}
be the set of all rooted trees on
S
with given root
r
.
We remark that the
\mathbf{S}
in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}}
is a partition of
N
. Hence, if
D\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}}
, then the vertex set
V(D)=N
.
But for
T\in\mathcal{T}_{S}
,
V(T)=S
may not be
N
. Isolated vertices are allowed in
D
, but not in
T
(except for single-vertex graphs). This inconsistency does not lead to misunderstanding in this paper.

A directed edge
i\to j
is
increasing
if
i>j
.
A forest
D
is
increasing
if its directed edges are all increasing.
It is
nearly increasing
if all the edges not end at the root are increasing. Let the superscript
{}^{\textrm{inc.}}
and
{}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}
stand for increasing and
nearly increasing respectively. For instance,
\mathcal{D}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}}
is the set of all nearly increasing forests on
\mathbf{S}
with given roots
\mathbf{r}
.
For a given integer
0\leq s\leq n-1
, we write

\mathcal{D}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{s}:=\bigcup_{\mathbf{S}\in\mathrm{Par}(N,n-s)}\mathcal{D}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{\mathbf{S}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{D}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{s}:=\bigcup_{\mathbf{S}\in\mathrm{Par}(N,n-s)}\mathcal{D}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{\mathbf{S}},

where

\mathcal{D}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{\mathbf{S}}:=\bigcup_{\mathbf{r}\;\;\mathrm{compat}\;\;\mathbf{S}}\mathcal{D}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{D}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{\mathbf{S}}:=\bigcup_{\mathbf{r}\;\;\mathrm{compat}\;\;\mathbf{S}}\mathcal{D}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}}.

If furthermore for each inner vertex
v
, the vertices directing to
v
have increasing labels
from left to right, then we say
D
is
augmented increasing
. Similarly, we can define an
augmented nearly increasing
forest.
Define
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}}
to be the set of
all augmented nearly increasing forests on
\mathbf{S}
with given roots
\mathbf{r}
, and similarly for
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}}
. Then similarly we define

\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{s}:=\bigcup_{\mathbf{S}\in\mathrm{Par}(N,n-s)}\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{\mathbf{S}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{s}:=\bigcup_{\mathbf{S}\in\mathrm{Par}(N,n-s)}\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{\mathbf{S}},

where

\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{\mathbf{S}}:=\bigcup_{\mathbf{r}\;\;\mathrm{compat}\;\;\mathbf{S}}\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{\mathbf{S}}:=\bigcup_{\mathbf{r}\;\;\mathrm{compat}\;\;\mathbf{S}}\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}}.

In particular,
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{n-1}=\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{\{N\}}
and
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{n-1}=\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{\{N\}}
.
We can change
forests
\mathcal{D}
to trees

\mathcal{T}
in the above notations.
For instance,

\overline{\mathcal{T}}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{S}
is the set of all augmented increasing (ordered directed rooted) trees on
S
(hence has exactly
|S|-1
edges).

The next result shows that any monomial operator
L
realizing a forest can be converted to be (nearly) increasing.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
\mathbf{S}
be a partition of
N
. Then for any
D\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{S}}
, there exists a
D^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{\mathbf{S}}
such that
L(D^{\prime})=\pm L(D)
.
Furthermore, if
\mathbf{S}=\{N\}
, then for any tree
T\in\mathcal{T}_{N}
,
there exists an increasing tree
T^{*}\in\mathcal{T}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{N}
such that
L(T^{*})=\pm L(T)
.

There may be a confusion in this proposition for the first-time reader. It is possible that different forests
D
and
D^{\prime}

have equivalent realizations
L(D)=L(D^{\prime})
in
\Xi
. For example,
[2,4][3,2][1,3]=[1,4][2,1][3,1]
, see their corresponding digraphs below (Figure
3
).
The equality can be obtained using the Exchange Rule twice. The digraph of the operator
[1,4][2,1][3,1]
is nearly increasing.

Figure 3.
The digraphs of
[2,4][3,2][1,3]
and
[1,4][2,1][3,1]
.

Proof.

To prove the first part, we begin with the proof of the case when
D
is a tree on
S
with a root
r
.
Assume the realization of
D
is

L=L(D)=[i_{s+l},j_{s+l}]\cdots[i_{s+1},j_{s+1}][i_{s},j_{s}]\cdots[i_{1},j_{1}],

where
i_{s+1},\dots,i_{s+l}
are all the vertices directing to the root
r
(so
j_{s+1}=\cdots=j_{s+l}=r
).
Define
\nu=\nu(L)
to be the integer such that

i_{k}>j_{k}
for
k=1,2,\dots,\nu-1
and
i_{\nu}<j_{\nu}
.
If
\nu\geq s+1
then
L
is nearly increasing.
We show that if
\nu\leq s
then we can find a monomial
L^{\prime}
such that

L^{\prime}=-L
and
\nu(L^{\prime})\geq\nu(L)+1
.

Since
j_{\nu}
is not the root, there exists
an
h>\nu
such that
[i_{h},j_{h}]=[j_{\nu},j_{h}]
appears in
L
(It is better to understand this by imaging the corresponding tree).
Notice that
j_{h}
may be the root
r
if
h>s
.
Denote by
\overline{L}=\cdots[i_{\nu},j_{\nu}]
the “subword” of
L
up to
[i_{\nu},j_{\nu}]
.
By Lemma
3.8
,
there exists
\overline{L}^{\prime}[j_{\nu},i_{\nu}]=-\overline{L}
. Thus,
let
L^{\prime}
be obtained from
L
by replacing
\overline{L}
by

\overline{L}^{\prime}[j_{\nu},i_{\nu}]
.
This operator
L^{\prime}
satisfies
L^{\prime}=-L
and
\nu(L^{\prime})\geq\nu(L)+1
.
Such operation can continue until all the
[i_{k},j_{k}]
for
1\leq k\leq s
are increasing,
i.e., all the
i_{k}>j_{k}
. Denote this operator by
L_{1}
. Then
D^{\prime}=D(L_{1})
is nearly increasing and satisfies
L_{1}=\pm L
.
The statement that
D^{\prime}
is also on
S
follows by the observation that all the operations we performed do not change the vertex set.

Suppose
D
has several components, say
T_{1},\dots,T_{k}
. Then by the Commutativity Rule

L(D)=L(T_{1})L(T_{2})\cdots L(T_{k})
, where
L(T_{i})
commutes with
L(T_{j})
for all
i,j
.
We have shown that for each
T_{i}
, there exists nearly increasing
T_{i}^{\prime}
such that
L(T_{i}^{\prime})=\pm L(T_{i})
.
The forest consisting of
T_{1}^{\prime},T_{2}^{\prime},\dots,T_{k}^{\prime}
is the desired
D^{\prime}
.

To prove the second part, we use the result in the first part to obtain a nearly increasing
T^{\prime}
on
N
with root
r
such that
L(T^{\prime})=\pm L(T)
.
By the Commutativity Rule, we may assume the realization of
T^{\prime}
is given by

L_{1}=L(T^{\prime})=[u_{l},r][u_{l-1},r]\cdots[u_{1},r][i_{s},j_{s}]\cdots[i_{1},j_{1}],

where
i_{m}>j_{m}
for
m=1,\dots,s
and
s+l=n-1
.
Define
\mu=\mu(L_{1})
to be the integer such that
u_{m}>r
for
m=1,\dots,\mu-1
,
but
u_{\mu}<r
. By Lemma
3.1
, we find that

L_{2}=[r,u_{l}][u_{l-1},r]\cdots[u_{\mu},r][u_{\mu-1},r]\cdots[u_{1},r][i_{s},j_{s}]\cdots[i_{1},j_{1}]=-L_{1}.

Note that if
L_{1}\notin\Xi^{n-1}
then we can not use Lemma
3.1
and the above equation does not hold in general.
Denote the subword
[r,u_{l}][u_{l-1},r]\cdots[u_{\mu},r]
in
L_{2}
by
L^{\prime\prime}
. By Lemma
3.8
,
we find

\overline{L}^{\prime\prime}=[u_{\mu},u_{l}][u_{l-1},u_{\mu}]\cdots[u_{\mu+1},u_{\mu}][r,u_{\mu}]=-L^{\prime\prime}.

Let
L_{3}
be obtained from
L_{2}
by replacing
L^{\prime\prime}
by
\overline{L}^{\prime\prime}
.
Then,
L_{3}
satisfies
L_{3}=L_{1}
and
\mu(L_{3})\geq\mu(L_{1})+1
.
Such operation can continue until we find an
L^{*}
such that
D(L^{*})
is increasing and
L^{*}=\pm L
.
∎

We find that the realization of any nearly increasing tree can be written as a linear combination of
the realizations of augmented nearly increasing trees.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
T^{(r)}\in\mathcal{T}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{S,r}
be a nearly increasing tree on
S
with the root
r
.
Then, its realization
L(T^{(r)})
can be written as a linear combination of the elements in

B=\{L(T):T\in\overline{\mathcal{T}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{S,r}\}
.

Proof.

We prove by induction on
|S|
. The base cases
|S|\leq 2
are trivial. Assume the lemma holds for
|S|<s
. We prove the lemma for the
|S|=s
case.

Let
u_{k},\dots,u_{1}
be all the children of the root
r
from left to right, and
T_{u_{i}}
be the subtree of
T^{(r)}
with the root
u_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,k
.
Then we can write

L(T)=[u_{k},r]\cdots[u_{1},r]L(T_{u_{k}})\cdots L(T_{u_{1}})=[u_{k},r]L(T_{u_{k}})\cdots[u_{1},r]L(T_{u_{1}}),

where the last equality can be obtained by the Commutativity Rule or the subtree reading of a digraph.
Note that since
T^{(r)}
is nearly increasing, every subtree
T_{u_{i}}
is increasing and the root
u_{i}
is the smallest vertex of
T_{u_{i}}
. By the induction hypothesis, for each
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}

L(T_{u_{i}})=\sum_{T^{\prime}_{j}}c_{j}L(T^{\prime}_{j}),

where
c_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
,
T^{\prime}_{j}
ranges over all augmented nearly increasing trees with the root
u_{i}
and the same vertex set of
T_{u_{i}}
. Since
u_{i}
is the smallest vertex of
T_{u_{i}}
, all these
T^{\prime}_{j}
are in fact augmented increasing.
Thus we may assume

T_{u_{i}}
itself to be augmented increasing.

Next we prove by another induction on the inversion number of
T^{(r)}
at the root
r
, defined by
\mathrm{inv}(T^{(r)};r)=\mathrm{inv}(u_{k}u_{k-1}\cdots u_{1})
.
If
\mathrm{inv}(T^{(r)};r)=0
, then
T^{(r)}
is augmented nearly increasing and
L(T^{(r)})
is itself an element of
B
.
Assume the lemma holds
for
\mathrm{inv}(T^{(r)};r)<t
. We prove the lemma for
\mathrm{inv}(T^{(r)};r)=t
.

Let
b=u_{i}>u_{i-1}=a
be the right-most adjacent decent of the sequence
u_{k}u_{k-1}\cdots u_{1}
.
That is, there does not exist a
j<i
such that
u_{j}>u_{j-1}
.
Then we can write

L(T^{(r)})=L_{4}[b,r]L_{3}[a,r]L_{2}L_{1},

where
L_{2}=L(T_{a})
and
L_{3}=L(T_{b})
are the realizations of the subtrees of
T^{(r)}
with roots
a
and
b
respectively. Notice that
L_{3}
is free of
r
and
a
, and hence commutes with
[a,r]
by the Commutativity Rule.
Together with the V-Formula, we have

L(T^{(r)})=L_{4}[b,r][a,r]L_{3}L_{2}L_{1}=L_{4}[a,r][b,r]L_{3}L_{2}L_{1}+L_{4}[a,r][b,a]L_{3}L_{2}L_{1}.

For a digraph
G
, if
L(G)
can be written as a linear combination of the elements in
B
, we say that
G
is
representable
.
Denote by
L^{\prime}:=L_{4}[a,r][b,r]L_{3}L_{2}L_{1}
and
L^{\prime\prime}:=L_{4}[a,r][b,a]L_{3}L_{2}L_{1}
. We will show that

L^{\prime}
and
L^{\prime\prime}
are both representable.

Since
L_{2}
commutes with
L_{3}
, and
L_{2}=L(T_{a})
commutes with
[b,r]
, we can write

L^{\prime}=L_{4}[a,r]L_{2}[b,r]L_{3}L_{1}.

Thus, its corresponding digraph
D(L^{\prime})
is just obtained from
T^{(r)}
by exchanging
T_{a}
and
T_{b}

(See Figure
4
), and hence has inversion number
t-1
. By the induction hypothesis on
\mathrm{inv}(T^{(r)};r)
,

L^{\prime}
is representable.

For
L^{\prime\prime}
, we can see that
its corresponding digraph
D^{\prime\prime}:=D(L^{\prime\prime})
is obtained from
T^{(r)}
by removing
b\to r
and adding

b\to a
as the left-most child of
a
, with
T_{b}
attached to
b
(See Figure
4
). Since
T_{b}
and
T_{a}
are increasing,
the subtree
T_{a}(L^{\prime\prime})
of
D(L^{\prime\prime})
with the root
a
is increasing, but need not be augmented increasing. By the induction hypothesis on
|S|
,
the realization of
T_{a}(L^{\prime\prime})
can be written as

L(T_{a}(L^{\prime\prime}))=[b,a]L_{3}L_{2}=\sum_{T}c(T)L(T),

where
T
ranges over all augmented nearly increasing rooted trees with the root
a
and the same vertex set of
T_{a}(L^{\prime\prime})
.
Since
a
is the smallest vertex of
T_{a}(L^{\prime\prime})
, these
T
’s are in fact augmented increasing.
Thus we may assume
T_{a}(L^{\prime\prime})
itself to be augmented increasing. Now
D^{\prime\prime}
has a smaller
\mathrm{inv}(D^{\prime\prime};r)
. By the induction hypothesis on
\mathrm{inv}(T^{(r)};r)
,
L^{\prime\prime}
is representable. This completes the proof.

Figure 4.
The illustration of obtaining
D(L^{\prime})
and
D(L^{\prime\prime})
from
T^{(r)}
.

∎

By Proposition
3.9
and Lemma
3.10
,
we obtain the spanning elements of
\Xi^{s}
.

Proposition 3.11
.

For
1\leq s\leq n-2
, the subspace
\Xi^{s}
is spanned by
B^{s}:=\{L(D):D\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{s}\}.

The subspace
\Xi^{n-1}
is spanned by
B^{n-1}:=\{L(D):D\in\overline{\mathcal{T}}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{n-1}\}.

Proof.

We show that any monomial operator
L=[i_{s},j_{s}]\cdots[i_{1},j_{1}]
can be written as a linear combination of the elements of
B^{s}
.

Let
D=D(L)
be the corresponding digraph of
L
. By Corollary
2.7
and Proposition
3.9
, we may assume that
D
is a nearly increasing
(ordered directed and rooted) forest for
1\leq s\leq n-2
, otherwise
L=0
.
Suppose
D
has
k=n-s
connected components, say
T_{r_{1}},\dots,T_{r_{k}}
,
where
r_{i}
is the root of the tree
T_{r_{i}}
for
i=1,\dots,k
.
Then, we can write

L=L(T_{r_{1}})\cdots L(T_{r_{k}}).

Here the
L(T_{r_{i}})
’s commute with each other by the Commutativity Rule.
By Lemma
3.10
, for each
i
,

L(T_{r_{i}})=\sum_{T^{(r_{i})}}c(T^{(r_{i})})L(T^{(r_{i})}),

where
T^{(r_{i})}
and
T^{(i)}
in the next equation range over all augmented nearly increasing trees with root
r_{i}
and the same vertex set of
T_{r_{i}}
for each
i
. It follows that
L
can be written in the form

L=\sum_{T^{(1)},\dots,T^{(k)}}c(T^{(1)})\cdots c(T^{(k)})L(T^{(1)})\cdots L(T^{(k)}).

This shows the first assertion.

For
s=n-1
, we may assume
D=D(L)
to be increasing by Proposition
3.9
. The proposition then follows by Lemma
3.10
and the similar argument as above.
∎

3.3.
The basis of
\Xi

In the previous subsection, we find the spanning elements of
\Xi^{s}
for each
s
.
In this subsection, we show that these spanning elements are linearly independent, and hence indeed form a basis of
\Xi^{s}
.

We find a simple result that can be used to construct an orthogonality of the monomial operators of
\Xi
.
The following rational function plays an important role in our construction. For an ordered directed rooted forest
D
, define

(3.16)

\epsilon(D)=\prod_{i\to j\in E(D)}\frac{1}{1-x_{i}/x_{j}}.

We have the next basic lemma.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
D
be an ordered directed rooted forest
and
P
be a Laurent polynomial. Then

\displaystyle\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}}P\epsilon(D)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}P|_{x_{i}=x_{j}}\epsilon(D/{i\to j}),&\mbox{ if }i\to j\in E(D);\\
-P|_{x_{i}=x_{j}}\epsilon(D/{i\to j}),&\mbox{ if }j\to i\in E(D);\\
0,&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Proof.

If
i\to j
is an edge of
D
, then
\epsilon(D)=\frac{1}{1-x_{i}/x_{j}}\epsilon(D^{\prime})
, where
D^{\prime}
is obtained from
D
by removing the edge
i\to j
.
By direct computation we have

\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}}P\epsilon(D)=\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}}\frac{1}{1-x_{i}/x_{j}}P\epsilon(D^{\prime})=P\epsilon(D^{\prime})\Big|_{x_{i}=x_{j}},

which is just
P\big|_{x_{i}=x_{j}}\epsilon(D/{i\to j})
.

When
j\to i
is an edge of
D
, similar computation yields

\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}}P\epsilon(D)=\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}}\frac{1}{1-x_{j}/x_{i}}P\epsilon(D^{\prime})=\CT_{x_{i}=x_{j}}\frac{-x_{i}/x_{j}}{1-x_{i}/x_{j}}P\epsilon(D^{\prime})=-P\epsilon(D^{\prime})\Big|_{x_{i}=x_{j}},

which is
-P|_{x_{i}=x_{j}}\epsilon(D/{i\to j})
.

Finally, if neither
i\to j
nor
j\to i
is an edge of
D
, then

x_{i}=x_{j}
is not a pole of
\epsilon(D)
. Thus
\CT\limits_{x_{i}=x_{j}}P\epsilon(D)=0
, as desired.
∎

Repeatedly use of Lemma
3.12
gives the next result.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
S,S^{\prime}
be subsets of
\{1,\dots,n\}
and
r\in S\cap S^{\prime}
.
Suppose
T\in\mathcal{T}_{S,r}
is a tree on
S
with the root
r
,
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{S^{\prime}}

and
P
is a Laurent polynomial. Then one of the following cases holds:

(1)

L(T)P\epsilon(T^{\prime})=0
. A sufficient condition is

S\not\subseteq S^{\prime}
.

(2)

L(T)P\epsilon(T^{\prime})
is of the form
\pm P^{\prime}\epsilon(T^{\prime\prime})
.
Here
P^{\prime}
is a Laurent polynomial obtained from
P
by substituting
x_{i}
by
x_{r}
for every
i\in S
, and
T^{\prime\prime}
is a tree on
(S^{\prime}\setminus S)\cup\{r\}
and is obtained from
T^{\prime}
through certain contractions.
In particular,
\epsilon(T^{\prime\prime})=1
if
S=S^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Suppose
L=L(T)=[i_{s},j_{s}]\cdots[i_{1},j_{1}]
is the realization of
T
.
Let
D_{1}=T^{\prime}
and recursively define
D_{k+1}=D_{k}/{i_{k}\to j_{k}}
for
k=1,2,\dots,s
if
i_{k}\to j_{k}
or
j_{k}\to i_{k}
is an edge of
D_{k}
. Observe that
D_{k}
is a tree on
S^{\prime}\setminus\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}\}
, and
D_{k+1}
is not defined if
i_{k}\not\in S^{\prime}
.
By Lemma
3.12
, if
D_{k+1}
is not defined for some
k
, this implies
[i_{k},j_{k}]\cdots[i_{1},j_{1}]P\epsilon(T^{\prime})=0
; otherwise we can obtain
D_{s+1}

and
S\subseteq S^{\prime}
. Moreover,
we know that
D_{s+1}
is a tree on
(S^{\prime}\setminus S)\cup\{r\}
and
LP\epsilon(T^{\prime})
is of the form
\pm P^{\prime}\epsilon(T^{\prime\prime})
. Here
T^{\prime\prime}=D_{s+1}

and
P^{\prime}
is obtained from
P
by successive replacements
x_{i_{1}}=x_{j_{1}}
, …,
x_{i_{s}}=x_{j_{s}}
. Since
T
is a tree on
S
, the
x_{i}
for all
i\in S
of
P

are replaced by
x_{r}
.
∎

Let
\mathbf{S}_{1},\mathbf{S}_{2}
be two partitions of
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. We say that
\mathbf{S}_{1}
is a
refinement
of
\mathbf{S}_{2}
if each block of
\mathbf{S}_{1}
is contained in one of the blocks of
\mathbf{S}_{2}
.
It is natural to generalize Lemma
3.13
to forests. Then, we have the next three consequences.

Corollary 3.14
.

Suppose
P
is a Laurent polynomial, and
D_{1},D_{2}
are two ordered directed rooted forests on
\mathbf{S}_{1}
and
\mathbf{S}_{2}
respectively.
If
L(D_{1})P\epsilon(D_{2})\neq 0
, then
\mathbf{S}_{1}
must be a refinement of
\mathbf{S}_{2}
. As a consequence,
|E(D_{2})|\geq|E(D_{1})|
.
Moreover,
L(D_{1})P\epsilon(D_{2})
is of the form
\pm P^{\prime}\epsilon(D_{3})
. Here
D_{3}
is a forest satisfying

|E(D_{3})|=|E(D_{2})|-|E(D_{1})|
,
and
P^{\prime}
is obtained from
P
by replacing each
x_{i}
by its corresponding
x_{r}
,
where
r
is the root of the component of
D_{1}
containing
i
.

Corollary 3.15
.

If
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are two forests with
|E(D_{2})|<|E(D_{1})|
, then
L(D_{1})\epsilon(D_{2})=0
.

Corollary 3.16
.

If two forests
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
have the same number of edges, then
L(D_{1})\epsilon(D_{2})\in\{-1,0,1\}
. In particular,
L(D_{1})\epsilon(D_{1})=1
.

Recall that
B^{s}=\{L(D):D\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{s}\}
for
s=1,\dots,n-2
and

B^{n-1}=\{L(D):D\in\overline{\mathcal{T}}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{n-1}\}
.
We find that for a fixed
s
the elements of
B^{s}
admit the next kind of orthogonality.

Lemma 3.17
.

For a fixed
s\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, let
D_{i},D_{j}
be two distinct forests of
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{s}
(trees of
\overline{\mathcal{T}}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{n-1}
if
s=n-1
)
with the same roots. Then

L(D_{i}):\mathcal{A}_{n}(D_{j})\mapsto\{0\}
. Consequently,

(3.20)

L(D_{i})\epsilon(D_{j})=\delta_{i,j},

where
\epsilon(D_{j})
is defined as in (
3.16
) and
\delta_{i,j}
is the Kronecker delta.

Proof.

We prove by induction on
n
, the number of variables. The lemma clearly holds for

n\leq 2
. Assume the lemma holds for
n-1
and less.

Let a leaf
u
of
D_{i}
be the right-most child of an inner vertex
v
. Since
D_{i}
is an augmented nearly increasing forest,
u
is also the largest child of
v
, and
u>v
if
v
is not a root. Then we can write
L(D_{i})=L(D_{i}/{u\to v})[u,v]
.

If
u\to v\not\in E(D_{j})
, then by the assumption that
D_{i}
and
D_{j}
have the same roots,
u
is not a root of
D_{j}
. We then discuss two cases: i) if
v
is not a root of
D_{i}
, then
u>v
so that
v\to u
is not increasing. Together with that neither
u
and
v
is a root of
D_{j}
gives
v\to u\notin E(D_{j})
; ii) if
v
is a root of
D_{i}
and
D_{j}
, then

v\to u
cannot be an edge of
D_{j}
. In both cases,

v\to u\notin E(D_{j})
.
Thus
[u,v]F=0
for any
F\in\mathcal{A}_{n}(D_{j})
.
It follows that
L(D_{i})(F)=L(D_{i}/{u\to v})[u,v](F)=0
.

On the other hand, assume that
u\to v
is an edge of
D_{j}
.
By the part (2) of Lemma
2.6
, we have

[u,v]F\in\mathcal{A}_{n}(D_{j}/{u\to v})
for
F\in\mathcal{A}_{n}(D_{j})
.
Then, if
D_{i}/{u\to v}\neq D_{j}/{u\to v}
, we can get

L(D_{i})(F)=L(D_{i}/{u\to v})[u,v](F)=L(D_{i}/{u\to v})(F^{\prime})=0.

Here
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}(D_{j}/{u\to v})
and the last equality holds by the induction hypothesis.
Hence, the lemma follows by showing that
D_{i}/{u\to v}\neq D_{j}/{u\to v}
.

We then consider two cases (recall that
u
is not a root of
D_{i}
and
D_{j}
):
i) If
u
is not a leaf of
D_{j}
, then it has at least one child, say
u^{\prime}>u
since
D_{j}
is nearly increasing. It follows that
u^{\prime}
becomes a child of
v
in
D_{j}/{u\to v}
, which can not be a child of
v
in
D_{i}/{u\to v}
by our choice of
u
.
ii) If
u
is a leaf in
D_{j}
, we assume to the contrary that
D_{i}/{u\to v}=D_{j}/{u\to v}
.
Then
u
is also the largest child of
v
in
D_{j}
. It follows that
D_{i}=D_{j}
, a contradiction.

Equation (
3.20
) follows from the proof above and Corollary
3.16
.
∎

We remark that the condition that
D_{i}
and
D_{j}
have the same roots cannot be dropped:
consider the case when
D_{i}
and
D_{j}
are both one-edge graphs consisting of
2\to 3

and
3\to 2
respectively.

Recall that
\mathrm{Par}(N,\ell)
is the set of all partitions of
N=\{1,\dots,n\}
with
\ell
blocks.

For
s=0,1,\dots,n-2
, let
\mathbf{S}\in\mathrm{Par}(N,n-s)
and the integer sequence
\mathbf{r}
be compatible with
\mathbf{S}
.
Denote by
B_{\mathbf{S}}:=\{L(D):D\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{\mathbf{S}}\}
and
B_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}}:=\{L(D):D\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}}\}
.
We have the next necessary and sufficient condition for the judgement of the zero operator in the spanning space of
B_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}}
, and obtain a basis of it.

Corollary 3.18
.

For any
\mathbf{L}\in\texttt{span}(B_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}})
,
\mathbf{L}
is the zero operator in
\Xi

if and only if
\mathbf{L}\epsilon(D)=0
for all
D\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}}
.
The elements of
B_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}}
form a basis of
\texttt{span}(B_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}})
.

Proof.

We only prove the sufficiency of the first claim, the necessity is trivial.

Since
\mathbf{L}\in\texttt{span}(B_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}})
, we can write

(3.21)

\mathbf{L}:=\sum_{D\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}}}c_{D}L(D)

for some complex numbers
c_{D}
. It suffices to show that
c_{D^{\prime}}=0
for each particular
D^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}}
.
Applying both sides of (
3.21
) to

\epsilon(D^{\prime})
and using Lemma
3.17
gives

\mathbf{L}\epsilon(D^{\prime})=c_{D^{\prime}}=0
.

Assume

\sum_{D\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}}}c_{D}L(D)=0.

From the proof above, we can obtain every
c_{D}=0
.
Then the elements of
B_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}}
are linearly independent and hence form a basis of

\texttt{span}(B_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}})
.
∎

From the space
\texttt{span}(B_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}})
, we can construct
\Xi
through direct sum. In other words,
the algebra
\Xi
is a direct sum of the spanning spaces
\texttt{span}(B_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}})
for certain
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{r}
.
We obtain this step by step in the next three decomposition lemmas.

Lemma 3.19
.

For fixed
s\in\{0,\dots,n-2\}
and
\mathbf{S}\in\mathrm{Par}(N,n-s)
,

(3.22)

\texttt{span}(B_{\mathbf{S}})=\bigoplus_{\mathbf{r}\;\;\mathrm{compat}\;\;\mathbf{S}}\texttt{span}\big(B_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}}\big).

Proof.

Assume

(3.23)

\sum_{\mathbf{r}}\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{r}}=0,

where
\mathbf{r}
is over all integer sequences compatible with
\mathbf{S}

and
\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{r}}\in\texttt{span}(B_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}})
. We need to show that every
\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{r}}
is in fact the zero operator.

Assume to the contrary that there exists a
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}=(r_{1}^{\prime},r_{2}^{\prime},\dots,r_{k}^{\prime})
such that
\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}
is nonzero. Note that
k=n-s\geq 2
.
By Corollary
3.18
, there exists a
D^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{\mathbf{S},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}
such that
\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}\epsilon(D^{\prime})=\gamma_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
.
By Corollary
3.16
we have
\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{r}}\epsilon(D^{\prime})=\gamma_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{C}.

Now applying both sides of (
3.23
) to
x_{r_{1}^{\prime}}^{1-k}x_{r_{2}^{\prime}}\cdots x_{r_{k}^{\prime}}\epsilon(D^{\prime})
and using Corollary
3.14
, we obtain

\sum_{\mathbf{r}}\gamma_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}x_{r_{1}}^{1-k}x_{r_{2}}\cdots x_{r_{k}}=0,

where
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{k})
is also over all integer sequences compatible with
\mathbf{S}
.
By the linear independency of the monomials, we have
\gamma_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}=0
for all
\mathbf{r}
. In particular
\gamma_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}=0
, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 3.20
.

(1) The set
B^{n-1}=\{L(D):D\in\overline{\mathcal{T}}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{n-1}\}

is a basis of the constant term algebra
\Xi^{n-1}
;

(2) For
s=0,1,\dots,n-2
,

(3.24)

\Xi^{s}=\bigoplus_{\mathbf{S}\in\mathrm{Par}(N,n-s)}\texttt{span}\big(B_{\mathbf{S}}\big)=\texttt{span}(B^{s}),

where
B^{s}=\{L(D):D\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{s}\}.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.11
, the algebra
\Xi^{n-1}
is spanned by
B^{n-1}
.
Thus, to prove (1), it suffices to prove that the elements of
B^{n-1}
are linearly independent. Assume

(3.25)

\sum_{T\in\overline{\mathcal{T}}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{n-1}}c_{T}L(T)=0,

where
c_{T}\in\mathbb{C}
for all
T
. Applying both sides of (
3.25
) to

\epsilon(T^{\prime})
for some
T^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathcal{T}}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{n-1}
and using Lemma
3.17
,
we get
c_{T^{\prime}}=0
. Let
T^{\prime}
range over all trees of
\overline{\mathcal{T}}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{n-1}
. This gives that all the coefficients
c_{T}
in (
3.25
) vanish. Then, the
L(T)
’s are linearly independent.

To prove (2), suppose

(3.26)

\sum_{\mathbf{S}\in\mathrm{Par}(N,n-s)}\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{S}}=0,

where
\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{S}}\in\texttt{span}\big(B_{\mathbf{S}}\big)
. We need to show
that
\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{S}}
is the zero operator for each
\mathbf{S}
.

Assume to the contrary that there exists an
\mathbf{S}^{\prime}
such that

\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{S}^{\prime}}\neq 0
. Then by Corollary
3.18
and Lemma
3.19
, there exists a
D^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{\mathbf{S}}
such that

\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{S}^{\prime}}\epsilon(D^{\prime})=\gamma\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
.
By Corollary
3.14
,
\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{S}}\epsilon(D^{\prime})=0
for all

\mathbf{S}\neq\mathbf{S}^{\prime}
. Therefore,
applying both sides of (
3.26
) to
\epsilon(D^{\prime})
gives

\gamma=0
, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 3.21
.

The constant term algebra
\Xi
has the following direct sum decomposition:

(3.27)

\Xi=\bigoplus_{s=0}^{n-1}\Xi^{s}=\bigoplus_{s=0}^{n-1}\texttt{span}(B^{s}).

Proof.

Suppose

(3.28)

\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}\mathbf{L}_{s}=0,

where
\mathbf{L}_{s}\in\Xi^{s}
.
We need to show that
\mathbf{L}_{s}
is the zero operator for each
s
.

Assume the contrary. Let
s^{\prime}
be the smallest integer such that
\mathbf{L}_{s^{\prime}}\neq 0
.
By Corollary
3.18
, Lemma
3.19
and Lemma
3.20
, there exists a
D^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\textrm{n.inc.}}_{s^{\prime}}
when
s^{\prime}\leq n-2
or
D^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathcal{T}}^{\textrm{inc.}}_{s^{\prime}}
when
s^{\prime}=n-1
,
such that

\mathbf{L}_{s^{\prime}}\epsilon(D^{\prime})=\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
.
By Corollary
3.15
, we have
\mathbf{L}_{s}\epsilon(D^{\prime})=0
for all
s>s^{\prime}
.

Now by applying both sides of (
3.28
) to
\epsilon(D^{\prime})
, we have

0=\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}\mathbf{L}_{s}\epsilon(D^{\prime})=\gamma^{\prime}.

This is a contradiction.
∎

Finally, we obtain our main theorem.

Theorem 3.22
.

The union
\cup_{s=0}^{n-1}B^{s}
forms a basis of
\Xi
.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of Lemmas
3.21
,
3.20
,
3.19
and Corollary
3.18
.
∎

4.
Concluding remark

We have established a structural result of the constant term algebra of type
A
. This algebra shall be useful in computing
constant terms of type
