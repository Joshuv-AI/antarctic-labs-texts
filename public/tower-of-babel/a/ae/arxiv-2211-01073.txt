Sectional nonassociativity of metrized algebras
Daniel J. F. Fox

Abstract
. The sectional nonassociativity of a metrized (not necessarily associative or unital) algebra is defined analogously to the sectional curvature of a pseudo-Riemannian metric, with the associator in place of the Levi-Civita covariant derivative. For commutative real algebras nonnegative sectional nonassociativity is usually called the Norton inequality, while a sharp upper bound on the sectional nonassociativity of the Jordan algebra of Hermitian matrices over a real Hurwitz algebra is closely related to the Böttcher-Wenzel-Chern-do Carmo-Kobayashi inequality. These and other basic examples are explained, and there are described some consequences of bounds on sectional nonassociativity for commutative algebras. A technical point of interest is that the results work over the octonions as well as the associative Hurwitz algebras.



1.
Introduction

For an algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ)
equipped with a metric, meaning a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form, that is invariant, meaning
h(x\circ y,z)=h(x,y\circ z)
for all
x,y,z\in\mathbb{A}
, the
sectional nonassociativity

\mathscr{K}(x,y)
of the
h
-nondegenerate subspace
\operatorname{Span}\{x,y\}\subset\mathbb{A}
is defined in
[
18
]
to be

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{K}(x,y)&=\frac{h(x\circ x,y\circ y)-h(x\circ y,y\circ x)}{h(x,x)h(y,y)-h(x,y)^{2}}.\end{split}

(Because
\circ
need not be commutative or anticommutative, the order of
x
and
y
in (
1.1
) matters.)
The sectional nonassociativity is analogous to the sectional curvature of the Levi-Civita connection of a pseudo-Riemannian metric, with the multiplication and its associator in place of the covariant derivative and its curvature. Section
3
describes in detail this analogy between the multiplication of an algebra an torsion-free connection on a tangent bundle. The notion itself, and its basic properties, are given in Section
4
.

Quantitative bounds on the sectional nonassociativity of a metrized real algebra encompass apparently unrelated inequalities such as the Böttcher-Wenzel-Chern-do Carmo-Kobayashi inequality bounding the norm of a matrix commutator and the Norton inequality asserting the nonnegativity of the quadratic expression
h(x\circ x,y\circ y)-h(x\circ y,x\circ y)
for certain metrized commutative algebras arising in the theory of vertex operator algebras whose most famous representative is the
196844
-dimensional Griess algebra on which the monster finite simple group acts by automorphisms.

It is apparent from (
1.1
) that, for a commutative algebra, the Norton inequality may be rephrased as
nonnegative sectional nonassociativity
. On the other hand, if
(\mathbb{A},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,],h)
is a metrized anticommutative algebra (for example, a Lie or Malcev algebra with
h
a multiple of its Killing form), (
1.1
) takes the form

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{h([x,y],[x,y])}{h(x,x)h(y,y)-h(x,y)^{2}}.

If
h
is positive definite, the right-hand side of (
1.2
) is bounded from above by
\frac{h([x,y],[x,y])}{h(x,x)h(y,y)}
and, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, this quantity is bounded from above. The Böttcher-Wenzel inequality asserts that for the algebra
\mathbb{M}(n,\mathbb{C})
of
n\times n
complex matrices metrized by the Frobenius norm this quantity is bounded from above by
2
(which is better than the constant obtained by applying Cauchy-Schwarz), and characterizes the case of equality. The Böttcher-Wenzel inequality can be interpreted as an upper bound on the sectional nonassociativity of the Lie algebra of
n\times n
matrices
\mathbb{M}(n,\mathbb{C})
with the usual matrix commutator.

For an anticommutative algebra, (
1.2
) is obviously nonnegative, with equality if and only if
[x,y]=0
, so that characterization of the equality case in the lower bound amounts to characterization of commuting pairs of elements.
On the other hand, for a commutative algebra, (
1.1
) can take both signs. These observations suggest looking for similar bounds for other metrized algebras, for example commutative algebras or other sorts of Lie algebras.

The extreme case of equal upper and lower bounds is that of constant sectional nonassociativity. This is closely related to the notion of projective associativity of commutative algebras studied in
[
18
]
. Although it is not solved here, the classification of metrized algebras with constant sectional nonassociativity appears an accessible problem. However, if anisotropy of the metric is not assumed, examples abound and it is still not clear what form the classification will take even in the simplest case of vanishing sectional nonassociativity. Essentially by definition, associative and alternative algebras have vanishing sectional nonassociativity. In Section
5
, it is shown that both a quadratic
2
-step nilpotent Lie algebra and a metrized antiflexible algebra have vanishing sectional nonassociativity, and nontrivial examples of such algebras are given. In particular, Example
15
exhibits a Euclidean metrized antiflexible algebra with vanishing sectional nonassociativity whose underlying Lie algebra is
\mathfrak{so}(3)
(which has constant sectional nonassociativity
1
when metrized by the negative of its Killing form) and whose underlying commutative algebra has constant negative sectional nonassociativity. (It gives a similar example with underlying Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
.)

Section
5
describes other examples of algebras having constant sectional nonassociativity. Example
16
explains that cross product algebras could be
defined
as antisymmetric metrized algebras having constant negative sectional nonassociativity.
Example
18
describes a one-parameter family of pairwise nonisomorphic
3
-dimensional metrized commutative algebras having constant sectional nonassociativities in
(-\infty,1/4]
.

Section
6
gives examples of algebras for which there can be established bounds on the sectional nonassociativities. Example
19
recalls a result of Miyamoto shows that the Griess algebras of OZ vertex operator algebras satisfy the Norton inequality. In this context it would be interesting to establish upper bounds. Example
20
shows that symmetric composition algebras such as para-Hurwitz and Okubo algebras have sectional nonassociativity bounded between
-1
and
1
.

The remainder of Section
6
is devoted to the proof of Theorem
6.1
, which gives sharp upper and lower bounds on the sectional nonassociativities of a simple Euclidean Jordan algebra of rank at least
3
together with characterization of the cases of equality in the bounds. These algebras are simply the Jordan algebras
\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h})
of Hermitian matrices over the real Hurwitz algebras
\mathbb{h}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C},\mathbb{H},\mathbb{O}\}
, where
n\geq 3
, and, in the case of the octonions,
\mathbb{O}
, attention is restricted to
n=3
. Theorem
6.1
states that the sectional nonassociativity,
\mathscr{K}(x,y)
, defined in Definition
4.1
, of the subspace spanned by linearly independent
x,y\in\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h})
satisfies

(1.3)

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathscr{K}(x,y)=\mathscr{K}_{h,\star}(x,y)\leq\tfrac{n}{2},

and characterizes the subspaces for equality is obtained in the upper and lower bound. The lower bound is the Norton inequality.
The sharp upper bound is deduced as a consequence of the Chern-do Carmo-Kobayashi inequality for the commutators of Hermitian matrices. This requires some argument to extend this inequality to the octonions that is formulated as Lemma
7.2
; this extension may be of independent interest.

These results are of interest from the point of view of applications of the Böttcher-Wenzel-Chern-do Carmo-Kobayashi inequality. This inequality is recalled in section
7
and it is shown that the Chern-do Carmo-Kobayashi inequality holds for
\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{H})
and partially for
\mathbb{Herm}(3,\mathbb{O})
.
These refinements, Theorem
7.1
and the variant Lemma
7.2
, valid over a real Hurwitz algebra, are used in the proof of Theorem
6.1
to estimate the sectional nonassociativity of the deunitalization of a simple Euclidean Jordan algebra. In a sense, this amounts to reinterpreting the Böttcher-Wenzel-Chern-do Carmo-Kobayashi inequalities as upper bounds on sectional nonassociativity.

The notion of sectional nonassociativity facilitates quantifications of nonassociativity both more refined and less exact than nonnegativity or nonpositivity, and these may in turn suggest refinements of the Böttcher-Wenzel inequality. More generally, the sectional nonassociativity is manifestly invariant under isometric algebra automorphisms and its relation with other similar quantities deserves to be explored more. See Remark
5
for further comments in this direction.

Not many examples of commutative algebras satisfying the Norton inequality are known and the description of such algebras is interesting and their classification, possibly with additional hypotheses, may be a possibly tractable problem. The main known examples are the Griess algebras of certain vertex operator algebras
[
43
]
and their subalgebras, as is explained next in Example
19
, and, by Theorem
6.1
, the simple Euclidean Jordan algebras, as is shown in Theorem
6.1
. (It is known that most of the simple Euclidean Jordan algebras can also be obtained as Griess algebras of certain vertex operator algebras
[
3
,
35
,
68
]
.) Further examples are the Majorana algebras introduced by A. A. Ivanov
[
28
,
29
]
which are defined by a list of axioms including the Norton inequality. See
[
65
]
for a family of examples depending on a parameter and further references.

More generally, the notion of sectional nonassociativity should help to organize the study of metrized algebras in the same manner that restriction on curvature quantities help to organize the study of Riemannian manifolds. Typically classes of algebras are defined by identities. From the point of view advocated here this is something like studying symmetric spaces (whose curvature tensors satisfy algebraic equalities).

Section
8
presents some general consequences for commutative algebras of bounds on sectional nonassociativity. Combined with the notion of sectional nonassociativity, results about the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the structure tensor can be used to relate the existence and description of idempotents and square-zero elements to conditions involving sectional nonassociativity. Since an automorphism of the algebra must permute its idempotents and square-zero elements, such results yield results about the size of the automorphism group. For example, Theorem
8.1
shows that a Euclidean metrized commutative algebra has finite automorphism group if its sectional nonassociativity is negative or if its sectional nonassociativity is nonpositive and the algebra contains no trivial subalgebra. In the other direction, Lemma
8.5
shows that nonnegative sectional nonassociativity precludes existence of
2
-nilpotents. The flavor of such results conforms broadly with the geometric analogy. For example, the automorphism group of a negatively curved Riemann surface is finite, while the (positively curved) sphere has a large continuous group of automorphisms.

The paper is structured as follows. Section
2
presents basic definitions related to metrized algebras. Section
3
shows that the analogy between multiplications and covariant derivatives has substance and presents results that help motivate the definition of sectional nonassociativity given in Section
4
. Section
4
also describes the most basic properties of the sectional nonassociativity. Section
5
presents examples having constant sectional nonassociativity; these include Hurwitz algebras, cross-product algebras, antiflexible algebras, and some novel three-dimensional algebras. Section
6
describes examples satisfying bounds on sectional nonassociativity generalizing the Norton inequality; these include symmetric composition algebras, Griess algebras of OZ vertez operator algebras, and the simple Euclidean Jordan algebras. The proof of the upper bound on the sectional nonassociativity of simple Euclidean Jordan algebras given in Theorem
6.1
requires the Böttcher-Wenzel-Chen-do Carmo-Kobayashi inequality for matrices over a Hurwitz algebra. Section
7
describes this and addresses what happens over the octonions. Finally, Section
8
presents some general consequences for commutative algebras of bounds on sectional nonassociativity.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{k}
be a field. Everywhere in the paper there is assumed
\operatorname{char}\mathbb{k}\notdivides 6
, although sometimes assumptions on
\operatorname{char}\mathbb{k}
are stated explicitly for clarity.

An algebra means a finite-dimensional vector space
\mathbb{A}
over
\mathbb{k}
equipped with a bilinear multiplication
\circ:\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{A}
. It need not be associative, commutative, or unital. The left and right multiplication endomorphisms
L_{\circ},R_{\circ}:\mathbb{A}\to\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})
are defined by
L_{\circ}(x)y=x\circ y=R_{\circ}(y)x
.

An algebra is
metrized
if it is equipped with a
metric
, meaning a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form,
h
, that is
invariant
(also called
associative
), meaning that
h(x\circ y,z)=h(x,y\circ z)
for all
x,y,z\in\mathbb{A}
.

A
Euclidean
metrized algebra is a real algebra metrized by a positive definite metric. (Essentially all results in the paper stated for Euclidean metrized algebras remain true with a real-closed base field in place of
\mathbb{R}
, but it would be distracting to state and prove them in this generality.) That an algebra be Euclidean metrized means that the adjoint of the left multiplication endomorphism
L_{\circ}(x)
equals the right multiplication endomorphism
R_{\circ}(x)
. Consequently, for a Euclidean metrized commutative algebra the multiplication endomorphisms
L_{\circ}(x)=R_{\circ}(x)
are self-adjoint with respect to a positive definite symmetric bilinear form.

Lemma 2.1

A two-dimensional metrized algebra

Proof.

If

Example 2
.

A commutative algebra is

Example 3
.

A

3.
The analogy between multiplications and connections

The following analogy between algebras and connections illuminates the point of view taken here. The multiplication
\circ
is analogous to a torsion-free connection on a tangent bundle, and the associator corresponds to (the negative of) the second covariant derivative. In fact, a torsion-free connection on a tangent bundle can be regarded as a Lie-admissible product on the space of vector fields, and in that setting the sectional nonassociativity defined in Section
4

is
simply the sectional curvature. However that setting differs from that considered here in several ways. First, the space of vector fields is infinite-dimensional. Second, the covariant derivative is a
C^{\infty}
-module map in its first argument. A general context encompassing both finite-dimensional algebras and connections on tangent bundles is algebroids, but this is not discussed here.

The notions suggested by the multiplication-connection analogy prove surprisingly robust and provide principles for organizing the zoo of general algebras different from those that arise from classical points of view. In particular, they offer possibilities for quantifying nonassociativity that turn out to be quite useful.

This idea has been discussed in similar terms in
[
26
]
in the context of Lie-admissible algebras and has roots in foundational articles on left-symmetric algebras
[
60
,
63
]
. In
[
8
]
there are considered algebras satisfying identities like those treated in this section from the more sophisticated point of view of Koszul duality of operads. The unrelated duality given by the adjoint multiplication considered here is motivated by considerations from affine differential geometry
[
19
]
. These ideas are expounded in the more limited context of metrized commutative algebras in
[
18
,
20
]
.

The analogy is implemented in practice in the following way. The space of connections on a bundle is an affine space. A reference connection is fixed and then any other connection is identified with a tensor. Any expression involving this connection involves some derivatives and some purely algebraic part expressible in terms of this tensor. The purely algebraic expression make sense also for the structure tensor of an algebra. For example, the covariant derivative of a metric
g
with respect to a torsion-free affine connection
\nabla
is
(\nabla_{X}g)(Y,Z)=X(g(Y,Z))-g(\nabla_{X}Y,Z)-g(Y,\nabla_{X}Z)
. The corresponding expression for a metric
g
on an algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ)
is
-g(x\circ y,z)-g(y,x\circ z)
. The vanishing of this expression is analogous to the metric tensor being parallel. This kind of invariance should probably be properly formulated as a cohomological condition, but it is not clear how to give sense to this last claim when working in the generality considered here.

In the analogy, the underlying bracket is regarded as background information of a topological rather than geometric nature, analogous to the Lie algebra of vector fields on a smooth manifold.

Applying the connection-algebra analogy to the curvature of an affine connection yields the following definitions.

Decomposing the product
\circ
by symmetries leads to two underlying algebras, from which
(\mathbb{A},\circ)
can be reconstructed (except when
\operatorname{char}\mathbb{k}=2
). The
underlying bracket
(or
commutator
) of the algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ)
is the commutator defined by
[x,y]=x\circ y-y\circ x
and
(\mathbb{A},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])
is the
underlying
antisymmetric algebra.
The
underlying commutative algebra
of an algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ)
is
\mathbb{A}
equipped with the product
x\odot y=x\circ y+y\circ x
.

The multiplication
\circ^{t}

adjoint
to
\circ
is that defined by
x\circ^{t}y=-y\circ x
. It is the negative of the opposite multiplication. By definition, that
L_{\circ^{t}}=-R_{\circ}
and
R_{\circ^{t}}=-L_{\circ}
. The sign in the definition of the adjoint multiplication
\circ^{t}
is chosen so that
\circ
and the adjoint product
\circ^{t}
have the same underlying bracket.

The
curvature

\mathsf{r}:\mathbb{A}\otimes\mathbb{A}\to\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})
and

\bar{\mathsf{r}}:\mathbb{A}\otimes\mathbb{A}\to\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})
of

are defined by

(3.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathsf{r}(x,y)z&=-[x,y,z]+[y,x,z]=\left([L_{\circ}(x),L_{\circ}(y)]-L_{\circ}([x,y])\right)z=(\mathscr{A}_{\circ}(y,x)-\mathscr{A}_{\circ}(x,y))z,\\
\bar{\mathsf{r}}(x,y)z&=-[z,x,y]+[z,y,x]=\left([R_{\circ}(x),R_{\circ}(y)]+R_{\circ}([x,y])\right)z=(\mathscr{A}_{\circ^{t}}(y,x)-\mathscr{A}_{\circ^{t}}(x,y))z.\end{split}

The adjoint curvature of
\circ
is the curvature of the adjoint multiplication
\circ^{t}
and vice-versa. For any tensorial object constructed from a multiplication
\circ
there is a corresponding tensorial object constructed in the same way from the adjoint multiplication
\circ^{t}
, and it is denoted by putting a
\bar{\,}
over the notation indicating the object associated with
\circ
. This convention is consistent with the notation
\mathsf{r}
and
\bar{\mathsf{r}}
.

The algebra has
self-adjoint curvature
if
\mathsf{r}=\bar{\mathsf{r}}
and
anti-self-adjoint curvature
if
\mathsf{r}=-\bar{\mathsf{r}}
.

Example 4
.

An algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ)
is

[x,y,x]=0
for all

. A commutative or anticommutative algebra is flexible. The symmetric composition algebras discussed in Example

It is evident from (

Example 5
.

An algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ)
is
antiflexible
if
[x,y,z]=[z,y,x]
for all
x,y,z\in\mathbb{A}
. It is evident from (

Example 6
.

An algebra is
left-symmetric
(also called

\mathscr{A}_{\circ}(x,y)=\mathscr{A}_{\circ}(y,x)
for all

Any left-symmetric algebra that is not right-symmetric gives an example of an algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ)
not isomorphic to its adjoint
(\mathbb{A},\circ^{t})

Example 7
.

An awkward aspect of the conventions here is that the underlying bracket of an anticommutative algebra is twice the original bracket, and this needs to be remembered when interpreting (

The analogue of the algebraic Bianchi identity for a curvature tensor is the vanishing of the complete antisymmetrization of
\mathsf{r}(x,y)z
. This need not occur in general for, as is the case for an affine connection, there is an obstruction related to compatibility with an underlying Lie bracket.

An algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ)

if the underlying bracket
[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]
satisfies the Jacobi identity, so that

is a Lie algebra. That

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathsf{t}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})&=\left(A_{\circ}([x_{1},x_{2}])-[A_{\circ}(x_{1}),A_{\circ}(x_{2})]\right)x_{3}=\mathsf{Cycle}_{1,2,3}[[x_{1},x_{2}],x_{3}]\\
&=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{3}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)[x_{\sigma(1)},x_{\sigma(2)},x_{\sigma(3)}]=-\mathsf{Cycle}_{1,2,3}\mathsf{r}(x_{1},x_{2})x_{3}=-\mathsf{Cycle}_{1,2,3}\bar{\mathsf{r}}(x_{1},x_{2})x_{3}.\end{split}

(The notation
\mathsf{Cycle}_{1,2,3}
indicates the sum over the cyclic permutations of the indicated indices.) The validity of the identity
-\mathsf{Cycle}_{1,2,3}\mathsf{r}(x_{1},x_{2})x_{3}=\mathsf{Cycle}\left([x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]-[x_{2},x_{1},x_{3}]\right)=\mathsf{Cycle}[[x_{1},x_{2}],x_{3}]

A straightforward computation shows

4.
Sectional nonassociativity

This section introduces quantitative notions of nonassociativity for metrized algebras. The sectional nonassociativity was introduced in
[
18
]
for metrized commutative algebras.

In the first part of this section the base field
\mathbb{k}
has characteristic not equal to
2
or
3
. Let
\mathbb{A}
be an
n
-dimensional
\mathbb{k}
-vector space with a metric
h
. Define

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} S^{2}\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}^{\ast}&=\left\{\begin{aligned} a&\in\otimes^{4}\mathbb{A}^{\ast}:\\
&a(x,y,z,w)=-a(y,x,z,w)=-a(x,y,w,z)=a(z,w,x,y)\,\,\text{for all}\,\,x,y,z,w\in\mathbb{A}\end{aligned}\right\},\\
\mathscr{MC}(\mathbb{A})&=\{a\in S^{2}\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}^{\ast}:a(x,y,z,w)+a(y,z,x,w)+a(z,x,y,w)=0\,\,\text{for all}\,\,x,y,z,w\in\mathbb{A}\}.\end{aligned}

The subspace
\mathscr{MC}(\mathbb{A})
comprises tensors of metric curvature tensor type.
An element
a\in S^{2}\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}^{\ast}
can be viewed as the bilinear form on
\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}
defined on decomposable elements by
a(x\wedge y,z\wedge w)=4a(x,y,z,w)
, so determines an associated quadratic form on
\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}
.
Endow
\otimes^{4}\mathbb{A}^{\ast}
and its subspaces with the metrics given by complete contraction with the metric
h
. If
\operatorname{char}\mathbb{k}\neq 3
,
S^{2}\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}^{\ast}
is isomorphic to the orthogonal direct sum
\mathscr{MC}(\mathbb{A})\oplus\mathsf{\Lambda}^{4}\mathbb{A}^{\ast}
, and the orthogonal projections onto the factors,
\mathcal{P}:S^{2}\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}^{\ast}\to\mathscr{MC}(\mathbb{A})
and
\mathcal{Q}:S^{2}\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}^{\ast}\to\mathsf{\Lambda}^{4}\mathbb{A}^{\ast}
are defined for
a\in S^{2}\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}^{\ast}
by

(4.2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{P}(a)(x,y,z,w)&=\tfrac{1}{3}\left(2a(x,y,z,w)-a(y,z,x,w)-a(z,x,y,w)\right),\\
\mathcal{Q}(a)(x,y,z,w)&=\tfrac{1}{3}\left(a(x,y,z,w)+a(y,z,x,w)+a(z,x,y,w)\right).\end{aligned}

The tensors
a
and
\mathcal{P}(a)
do not necessarily determine the same quadratic form on
\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}
, but the quadratic forms they determine agree on decomposable elements of
\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose
\operatorname{char}\mathbb{k}\notdivides 6
. Let
h
be a metric on the
\mathbb{k}
-vector space
\mathbb{A}
and let
\mathcal{P}:S^{2}\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}^{\ast}\to\mathscr{MC}(\mathbb{A})
be the orthogonal projection with respect to the inner products given by complete contraction with
h
. The quadratic forms determined on
\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}
by
a,b\in S^{2}\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}^{\ast}
coincide on decomposable elements of
\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}
if and only if
\mathcal{P}(a)=\mathcal{P}(b)
.

Proof.

For
\omega\in\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}
and
a\in S^{2}\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}^{\ast}
,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle a,\omega\otimes\omega\rangle&=\langle\mathcal{P}(a),\omega\otimes\omega\rangle+\langle\mathcal{Q}(a),\omega\otimes\omega\rangle=\langle\mathcal{P}(a),\omega\otimes\omega\rangle+\tfrac{1}{6}\langle\mathcal{Q}(a),\omega\wedge\omega\rangle,\end{split}

in which
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
denotes the pairing between dual vector spaces. Because
\omega\wedge\omega=0
if
\omega
is decomposable, this shows that the restrictions to decomposable
2
-forms of the quadratic forms determined by
a
and
\mathcal{P}(a)
coincide, and, moreover, that if
Q(a)=0
, then these quadratic forms are equal.

On the other hand, that the quadratic forms determined on
\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}
by
a,b\in S^{2}\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}^{\ast}
coincide on decomposable elements means that
c=a-b\in S^{2}\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}^{\ast}
satisfies
4c(x,y,x,y)=\langle c,(x\wedge y)\otimes(x\wedge y)\rangle=0
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{A}
. Linearizing in
x
and
y
and using the symmetries of
c
yields

(4.4)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=c(x,y,z,w)+c(z,y,x,w)+c(x,w,z,y)+c(z,w,x,y)=2c(x,y,z,w)-2c(y,z,x,w)

for all
x,y,z,w\in\mathbb{A}
. Hence
c(x,y,z,w)=c(y,z,x,w)=c(z,x,y,w)
, and in (
4.2
) this yields
\mathcal{P}(a)-\mathcal{P}(b)=\mathcal{P}(c)=0
.
∎

For a metrized algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
, it follows straightforwardly from the invariance of
h
that

(4.5)

\displaystyle h([x,y,z],w)=-h([y,z,w],x)=h([z,w,x],y)=-h([w,x,y],z),

\displaystyle\text{for all}\,\,x,y,z,w\in\mathbb{A}.

Define
\mathsf{r}^{\flat}(x,y,z,w)=h(\mathsf{r}(x,y)z,w)
and
\bar{\mathsf{r}}^{\flat}(x,y,z,w)=h(\bar{\mathsf{r}}(x,y)z,w)
.
From (
4.5
) and (
3.1
) it follows that

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mathsf{r}^{\flat}(x,y,z,w)=-\bar{\mathsf{r}}^{\flat}(x,y,w,z),

\displaystyle\text{for all}\,\,x,y,z,w\in\mathbb{A}.

By (
4.6
) and (
4.5
),

(4.7)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathsf{r}^{\flat}(x,y,z,w)+\bar{\mathsf{r}}^{\flat}(x,y,z,w)=\mathsf{r}^{\flat}(x,y,z,w)-\mathsf{r}^{\flat}(x,y,w,z)\\
&=\mathsf{r}^{\flat}(z,w,x,y)-\mathsf{r}^{\flat}(z,w,y,x)=\mathsf{r}^{\flat}(z,w,x,y)+\bar{\mathsf{r}}^{\flat}(z,w,x,y)\end{aligned}

so that
\mathsf{r}^{\flat}+\bar{\mathsf{r}}^{\flat}
is an element of
S^{2}\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}^{\ast}
(as it need not satisfy the algebraic Bianchi identity, it need not be in
\mathscr{MC}(\mathbb{A})
). This follows also from the alternative expression

(4.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathsf{r}^{\flat}(x,y,z,w)&+\bar{\mathsf{r}}^{\flat}(x,y,z,w)\\
&=-h([x,y],[z,w])+h(y\circ z,w\circ x)-h(x\circ z,w\circ y)+h(z\circ y,x\circ w)-h(z\circ x,y\circ w),\end{split}

which is evidently antisymmetric in
x
and
y
, and in
z
and
w
, and symmetric in the pairs
xy
and
zw
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose
\operatorname{char}\mathbb{k}\notdivides 6
. A metrized
\mathbb{k}
-algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
is Lie-admissible if and only if
\mathsf{r}^{\flat}+\bar{\mathsf{r}}^{\flat}\in\mathscr{MC}(\mathbb{A})
.

Proof.

By (
3.7
), the tensor
\mathsf{m}
defined by
\mathsf{m}^{\flat}=3\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{r}^{\flat}+\bar{\mathsf{r}}^{\flat})\in\mathscr{MC}(\mathbb{A})
satisfies

(4.9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathsf{m}(x,y)z&=-2(\mathsf{r}(x,y)z+\bar{\mathsf{r}}(x,y)z)+\mathsf{r}(y,z)x+\bar{\mathsf{r}}(y,z)x+\mathsf{r}(z,x)y+\bar{\mathsf{r}}(z,x)y)\\
&=-3(\mathsf{r}(x,y)z+\bar{\mathsf{r}}(x,y)z)-2\mathsf{t}(x,y,z),\end{split}

for all
x,y,z\in\mathbb{A}
.
The claim follows because
(\mathbb{A},\circ)
is Lie-admissible if and only if
\mathsf{t}
vanishes identically.
∎

Let
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
be a metrized algebra of dimension at least
2
. If

span a two-dimensional subspace
\mathbb{V}\subset\mathbb{A}
and
\bar{x}=ax+by
and
\bar{y}=cx+dy
span the same subspace

, then

(4.10)

\displaystyle h(\bar{x},\bar{x})h(\bar{y},\bar{y})-h(\bar{x},\bar{y})^{2}=(ad-bc)^{2}\left(h(x,x)h(y,y)-h(x,y)^{2}\right),

(4.11)

\displaystyle h(\bar{x}\circ\bar{x},\bar{y}\circ\bar{y})-h(\bar{x}\circ\bar{y},\bar{x}\circ\bar{y})=(ad-bc)^{2}\left(h(x\circ x,y\circ y)-h(x\circ y,y\circ x)\right),

from which it follows that (
4.11
) divided by (
4.10
) is well-defined and depends only the subspace
\mathbb{V}
, provided the denominator is non-zero, which is the case as long
\mathbb{V}
is
h
-nondegenerate. (A symmetric bilinear form
h
on a two-dimensional
\mathbb{k}
-vector space
\mathbb{V}
is nondegenerate if and only if
h(x,x)h(y,y)-h(x,y)^{2}\neq 0
for any basis
\{x,y\}
of
\mathbb{V}
; this is a special case of the statement that a symmetric bilinear form is nondegenerate if and only if the determinant of its Gram matrix with respect to any basis is nonvanishing
[
36
, Proposition I.
1.2
]
.) These observations show that Definition
4.1
is well made. By (
4.5
),

(4.12)

\displaystyle h([x,x,y],y)=-h([y,x,x],y),

\displaystyle\text{for all}\,\,x,y\in\mathbb{A}.

The second equality in (
4.13
) is (
4.12
).

Definition 4.1
.

Let
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
be a metrized
\mathbb{k}
-algebra of dimension at least
2
. Define the
sectional nonassociativity

\mathscr{K}(x,y)
of the
h
-nondegenerate subspace
\operatorname{Span}\{x,y\}\subset\mathbb{A}
to be

(4.13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{K}(x,y)&=\mathscr{K}_{\circ,h}(x,y)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\frac{h(\mathsf{r}(x,y)x,y)+h(\bar{\mathsf{r}}(x,y)x,y)}{h(x\circ x,y\circ y)-h(x,y)^{2}}\\
&=\frac{h([x,x,y],y)}{h(x,x)h(y,y)-h(x,y)^{2}}=\frac{-h([y,x,x],y)}{h(x,x)h(y,y)-h(x,y)^{2}}=\frac{h(x\circ x,y\circ y)-h(x\circ y,y\circ x)}{h(x,x)h(y,y)-h(x,y)^{2}}.\end{split}

The proviso that
\operatorname{Span}\{x,y\}
be
h
-nondegenerate is automatic when
h
is anisotropic.

By definition, the sectional nonassociativity is invariant under isometric algebra isomorphisms of metrized algebras.

The dependence of
\mathscr{K}=\mathscr{K}_{\circ,h}
on
\circ
and
h
is indicated with subscripts when helpful. For
r,s\in\dot{\mathbb{R}}
,
\mathscr{K}_{s\circ,rh}=s^{2}r^{-1}\mathscr{K}_{\circ,h}
, where
s\circ
means the multiplication
sx\circ y
.

Given an algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ)
, with underlying anticommutative and commutative products
[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]
and
\odot
, a straightforward computation shows that that
h
is invariant with respect to both of
[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]
and
\odot
, consequently the sectional nonassociativities
\mathscr{K}_{[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,],h}(x,y)
and
\mathscr{K}_{\odot,h}(x,y)
are defined. From the identity

(4.14)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {}_{\odot}&+[x,y,z]_{[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]}=[x,y,z]_{\odot}+[[x,y],z]+[[y,z],x]\\
&=[x,y,z]_{\odot}-[[z,x],y]+\mathsf{t}_{\circ}(x,y,z)=2[x,y,z]_{\circ}-2[z,y,x]_{\circ},\end{aligned}

(subscripts indicate the dependence on the different multiplications) together with (
4.12
) there follows

(4.15)

\displaystyle h([x,x,y]_{\odot},y)+h([x,x,y]_{[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]},y)=4h([x,x,y]_{\circ},y),

and with (
4.13
) this yields

(4.16)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 4\mathscr{K}_{\circ,h}(x,y)&=\mathscr{K}_{\odot,h}(x,y)+\mathscr{K}_{[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,],h}(x,y)\\
&=\frac{h(x\odot x,y\odot y)-h(x\odot y,y\odot x)}{h(x,x)h(y,y)-h(x,y)^{2}}+\frac{h([x,y],[x,y])}{h(x,x)h(y,y)-h(x,y)^{2}}.\end{aligned}

It follows that for a Euclidean metrized algebra,
4\mathscr{K}_{\circ,h}(x,y)\geq\mathscr{K}_{\odot,h}(x,y)
for all linearly independent

and
y
, with equality if and only if
x
and
y
commute.

For
h\in S^{2}\mathbb{A}^{\ast}
, define
h\owedge h\in\mathscr{MC}(\mathbb{A})
by

(4.17)

\displaystyle(h\owedge h)(x,y,z,w)=h(x,z)h(y,w)-h(x,w)h(y,z),

\displaystyle\text{for all}\,\,x,y,z,w\in\mathbb{A}.

By definition
(h\owedge h)(x,y,x,y)=|x|^{2}|y|^{2}-h(x,y)^{2}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathbb{k}
be an infinite field. A metrized
\mathbb{k}
-algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
of dimension
n\geq 2
has constant sectional nonassociativity
c\in\mathbb{k}
if and only if the quadratic forms determined on
\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}
by
-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathsf{r}^{\flat}+\bar{\mathsf{r}}^{\flat})
and
c(h\owedge h)
coincide on decomposable elements of
\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}
.

Proof.

That the coincidence of
-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathsf{r}^{\flat}+\bar{\mathsf{r}}^{\flat})
and
c(h\owedge h)
on decomposable elements of
\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}
implies constant sectional nonassociativity is immediate from the definition (
4.13
).

The Grassmannian
Gr(2,\mathbb{A})
of two-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{A}
is a projective variety via the Plücker embedding
Gr(2,\mathbb{A})\to\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A})
sending
\mathbb{k}\{x,y\}\in Gr(2,\mathbb{A})
to the span
\mathbb{k}\{x\wedge y\}
.
The homogeneous functions
H,K:\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{k}
defined by
H(x\wedge y)=h(x,x)h(y,y)-h(x,y)^{2}
and
K(x\wedge y)=-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathsf{r}^{\flat}+\bar{\mathsf{r}}^{\flat})(x,y,x,y)=h(x\circ x,y\circ y)-h(x\circ y,y\circ x)
are quadratic polynomials that restrict to the image of the Plücker embedding of the Grassmannian
Gr(2,\mathbb{A})
of two-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{A}
.
If
\mathbb{k}
is infinite, then the intersection of any two nonempty Zariski open subsets of
Gr(2,\mathbb{A})
is nonempty. That the sectional nonassociativity be constant equal to
c
is the same as assuming
cH-K
vanishes on
Gr(2,\mathbb{A})\setminus\{H=0\}
. As the complement
Gr(2,\mathbb{A})\setminus\{H=0\}
is infinite, if
cH-K
vanishes on
Gr(2,\mathbb{A})\setminus\{H=0\}
, it vanishes on
Gr(2,\mathbb{A})
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
be a metrized algebra over an infinite field of characteristic not equal to
2
or
3
.

(1)

(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
has constant sectional nonassociativity
c
if and only if
\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{r}^{\flat}+\bar{\mathsf{r}}^{\flat})=2ch\owedge h
.

(2)

If
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
is Lie-admissible, it has constant sectional nonassociativity
c
if and only if
\mathsf{r}^{\flat}+\bar{\mathsf{r}}^{\flat}=2ch\owedge h
.

(3)

If
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
is Lie-admissible and flexible, it has constant sectional nonassociativity
c
if and only if
\mathsf{r}=\bar{\mathsf{r}}=ch\owedge h
.

Proof.

By definition of
\mathscr{K}(x,y)
and Lemma
4.1
,

(4.18)

\displaystyle\begin{split}(h\owedge h)(x,y,x,y)\mathscr{K}(x,y)&=-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathsf{r}^{\flat}+\bar{\mathsf{r}}^{\flat})(x,y,x,y)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{r}^{\flat}+\bar{\mathsf{r}}^{\flat})(x,y,x,y).\end{split}

If there holds (
1
), then the last term of (
4.18
) equals
c(h\owedge h)(x,y,x,y)
, so (
4.18
) shows
\mathscr{K}(x,y)=c
.
On the other hand, by Lemma
4.3
, that
\mathscr{K}(x,y)=c
for all linearly independent
x,y\in\mathbb{A}^{\ast}
means that the quadratic forms determined on
\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}
by
-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathsf{r}^{\flat}+\bar{\mathsf{r}}^{\flat})
and
c(h\owedge h)
coincide on decomposable elements of
\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{A}
. By Lemma
4.1
this implies (
1
).
If
\circ
is Lie-admissible, then, by Lemma
4.2
,
\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{r}^{\flat}+\bar{\mathsf{r}}^{\flat})=\mathsf{r}^{\flat}+\bar{\mathsf{r}}^{\flat}
, so in this case (
1
) is equivalent to (
2
). If
\circ
is moreover flexible, then
\mathsf{r}=\bar{\mathsf{r}}
, so (
2
) is equivalent to (
3
).
∎

Example 13
.

Evidently the sectional nonassociativity of a metrized associative algebra is identically zero. More generally, the same is true for an alternative algebra. An algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ)
is
alternative
if
[x,x,y]=0
and
[x,y,y]=0
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{A}
. By (
4.13
), the sectional nonassociativity of a metrized alternative algebra is identically zero. This can also be seen as a consequence of the theorem of E. Artin (see
[
58
, p.
18
]
) that shows that the subalgebra generated by any two elements of an alternative algebra is associative.

For example, any Hurwitz algebra is alternative, so has vanishing sectional nonassociativity.

This observation illustrates the limitations of the analogy between the associator and the curvature tensor, for the octonions are alternative but not associative.

In the rest of this section it is supposed the base field is
\mathbb{R}
.

A Euclidean metrized algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
has positive, nonnegative, zero, etc. sectional nonassociativity if the given qualifier is valid for every two-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{A}
.

For a Euclidean metrized algebra, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the numbers

(4.19)

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)=\sup_{x,y\in\mathbb{A}:x\wedge y\neq 0}\mathscr{K}_{h}(x,y),

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)=\inf_{x,y\in\mathbb{A}:x\wedge y\neq 0}\mathscr{K}_{h}(x,y),

are finite and invariant under isometric automorphisms of
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
. Their estimation for any well delineated class of metrized algebras is a basic problem.

Such an estimate is most interesting when the invariant metric
h
is determined in some canonical manner by the algebra structure, as is the case for the Killing form
\tau_{\circ}(x,y)=\operatorname{tr}L_{\circ}(x)L_{\circ}(y)
of a Lie or Malcev algebra or the form
\operatorname{tr}L_{\circ}(x\circ y)
on a Jordan algebra.

Lemma
4.5
shows that taking direct sums of Euclidean metrized commutative algebras preserves conditions such as nonpositive and nonnegative sectional nonassociativity.

For readability, in the proofs of Lemmas
4.5
and
4.6
, there are written
|x|^{2}=h(x,x)
,
\langle x,y\rangle=h(x,y)
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
be a Euclidean metrized commutative algebra and suppose that
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{A}_{1}\oplus\mathbb{A}_{2}
is a decomposition into
h
-orthogonal ideals. There hold:

(4.20)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)\geq\min\{\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{A}_{1},\circ,h),\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{A}_{2},\circ,h),0\},

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)\leq\max\{\mathscr{U}(\mathbb{A}_{1},\circ,h),\mathscr{U}(\mathbb{A}_{2},\circ,h),0\}.

In particular, if
(\mathbb{A}_{1},\circ,h)
and
(\mathbb{A}_{2},\circ,h)
have nonpositive (resp. nonnegative) sectional nonassociativity, then
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
has nonpositive (resp. nonnegative) sectional nonassociativity.

Proof.

For any
x_{1},y_{1}\in\mathbb{A}_{2}
and
x_{2},y_{2}\in\mathbb{A}_{2}
, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

(4.21)

\displaystyle\begin{split}|x\wedge y|^{2}&-|x_{1}\wedge y_{1}|^{2}-|x_{2}\wedge y_{2}|^{2}=|x_{1}|^{2}|y_{2}|^{2}+|x_{2}|^{2}|y_{1}|^{2}-2\langle x_{1},y_{1}\rangle\langle x_{2},y_{2}\rangle=\\
&=\left(|x_{1}||y_{2}|-|x_{2}||y_{1}|\right)^{2}+2\left(|x_{1}||y_{1}||x_{2}||y_{2}|-\langle x_{1},y_{1}\rangle\langle x_{2},y_{2}\rangle\right)\geq 0,\end{split}

with equality if and only if
x=x_{1}+x_{2}
and
y=y_{1}+y_{2}
are linearly dependent.
Let
x_{i}\in\mathbb{A}_{i}
be the
h
-orthogonal projection of
x\in\mathbb{A}
.
By (
4.21
),
r_{i}=\tfrac{|x_{i}\wedge y_{i}|^{2}}{|x\wedge y|^{2}}
is contained in
[0,1]
, and, by the orthogonality of the ideals
\mathbb{A}_{1}
and
\mathbb{A}_{2}
, it follows that for linearly independent
x,y\in\mathbb{A}
,

(4.22)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} m_{1}r_{1}+m_{2}r_{2}\leq\mathscr{K}_{\mathbb{A},h}(x,y)=\mathscr{K}_{\mathbb{A}_{1},h}(x_{1},y_{1})r_{1}+\mathscr{K}_{\mathbb{A}_{2},h}(x_{2},y_{2})r_{2}\leq M_{1}r_{1}+M_{2}r_{2}.\end{aligned}

Applying to (
4.22
) the inequalities
\min\{a,b,c\}\leq ar_{1}+br_{2}+c(1-r_{1}-r_{2})\leq\max\{a,b,c\}
, valid for any
r_{1},r_{2}\in[0,1]
, with
(a,b,c)=(m_{1},m_{2},0)
or
(a,b,c)=(M_{1},M_{2},0)
yields (
4.20
).
∎

It is more difficult to relate the sectional nonassociativity of a tensor product to the sectional nonassociativities of its factors, but in some cases something can be said.

The tensor product of metrized algebras
(\mathbb{A}_{i},\circ_{i},h_{i})
,
i\in\{1,2\}
, is the vector space
\mathbb{A}_{1}\otimes\mathbb{A}_{2}
with the product defined by
(a_{1}\otimes b_{1})\circ(a_{2}\otimes b_{2})=(a_{1}\circ_{1}b_{1})\otimes(a_{1}\circ_{2}b_{2})
and extending bilinearly, and the metric defined by
h(a_{1}\otimes b_{1},a_{2}\otimes b_{2})=h_{1}(a_{1},b_{1})h_{2}(a_{1},b_{2})
and extending bilinearly.

Lemma 4.6
.

For
i\in\{1,2\}
let
(\mathbb{A}_{i},\circ_{i},h_{i})
be Euclidean metrized commutative algebras. For
a_{i},\bar{a}_{i}\in\mathbb{A}_{i}
, if
\mathscr{K}_{\mathbb{A}_{1},h_{1}}(a_{1},\bar{a}_{1})
and
\mathscr{K}_{\mathbb{A}_{2},h_{2}}(a_{2},\bar{a}_{2})
are both nonnegative, then
\mathscr{K}_{\mathbb{A}_{1}\otimes\mathbb{A}_{2},h_{1}\otimes h_{2}}(a_{1}\otimes a_{2},\bar{a}_{1}\otimes\bar{a}_{2})
is nonnegative.

Proof.

Straightforward computations show

(4.23)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} |(a_{1}\otimes a_{2})\wedge(\bar{a}_{1}\otimes\bar{a}_{2})|^{2}&=|a_{1}\wedge\bar{a}_{1}|^{2}|a_{2}\wedge\bar{a}_{2}|^{2}+|a_{2}\wedge\bar{a}_{2}|^{2}\langle a_{1},\bar{a}_{1}\rangle^{2}+|a_{1}\wedge\bar{a}_{1}|^{2}\langle a_{2},\bar{a}_{2}\rangle^{2},\\
\mathscr{K}_{\mathbb{A}_{1}\otimes\mathbb{A}_{2},h_{1}\otimes h_{2}}(a_{1}\otimes a_{2},\bar{a}_{1}\otimes\bar{a}_{2})&\tfrac{|(a_{1}\otimes a_{2})\wedge(\bar{a}_{1}\otimes\bar{a}_{2})|^{2}}{|a_{1}\wedge\bar{a}_{1}|^{2}|a_{2}\wedge\bar{a}_{2}|^{2}}=\mathscr{K}_{\mathbb{A}_{1},h_{1}}(a_{1},\bar{a}_{1})\mathscr{K}_{\mathbb{A}_{2},h_{2}}(a_{2},\bar{a}_{2})\\
&+\tfrac{|a_{1}\circ_{1}\bar{a}_{1}|^{2}}{|a_{1}\wedge\bar{a}_{1}|^{2}}\mathscr{K}_{\mathbb{A}_{2},h_{2}}(a_{2},\bar{a}_{2})+\tfrac{|a_{2}\circ_{2}\bar{a}_{2}|^{2}}{|a_{2}\wedge\bar{a}_{2}|^{2}}\mathscr{K}_{\mathbb{A}_{1},h_{1}}(a_{1},\bar{a}_{1}),\end{aligned}

from which the claim follows.
∎

5.
Examples of algebras with constant sectional nonassociativity

This section presents examples of metrized algebras having constant sectional nonassociativity.

Example 14
.

By Example
7
a
2
-step nilpotent Lie algebra satisfies
\mathsf{r}=\bar{\mathsf{r}}=0
. If such a Lie algebra is equipped with an invariant metric, then its sectional nonassociativity vanishes identically. In general a
2
-step nilpotent Lie algebra need not admit an invariant metric. For example, the Heisenberg Lie algebra of any odd dimension admits no invariant metric. However, there are
2
-step nilpotent Lie algebras that do admit invariant metrics. G. Ovando
[
52
]
has given some examples. For example the semidirect product of a Lie algebra with its dual carries a split signature invariant metric, and if the original Lie algebra is
2
-step nilpotent, its semidirect product with its dual is
2
-step nilpotent as well.

However, these examples obtained from
2
-step nilpotent real Lie algebras cannot be Euclidean - the invariance of the metric and the
2
-step nilpotence of
\mathfrak{g}
imply that
[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
is a nontrivial isotropic subspace.

Example 15
.

By Example
5
an antiflexible algebra has anti-self-adjoint-curvature, so a metrized antiflexible algebra has vanishing sectional nonassociativity. The following example of a three-dimensional antiflexible algebra that is not power-associative is a modification (by unimportant scalar factors) of one given by F. Kosier in
[
34
, p.
303
]
. Suppose
\operatorname{char}\mathbb{k}\neq 2
and equip
\mathbb{k}^{3}
with the product
x\circ y=(2x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{3},2x_{1}y_{2},2x_{3}y_{1})
where
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
and
y=(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})
. The two possible third powers of
(0,1,1)
are different, so the algebra is not power-associative.

On
(\mathbb{k}^{3},\circ)
, the metric
h
defined by
h(x,y)=x_{1}y_{1}+\tfrac{1}{2}(x_{2}y_{3}+x_{3}y_{2})
is invariant, for
h(x\circ y,z)=2x_{1}y_{1}z_{1}+x_{2}y_{3}z_{1}+x_{1}y_{2}z_{3}+x_{3}y_{1}z_{2}
is preserved by cyclic permutations of
x
,
y
, and
z
. A computation shows
[x,y,z]=(0,2x_{2}y_{3}z_{2},-2x_{3}y_{2}z_{3})
, which is evidently symmetric in
x
and
z
. It follows that
h([x,x,y],y)=0
, and
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
has vanishing sectional nonassociativity.

By Example
9
,
(\mathbb{A},\circ)
is Lie admissible as can also be checked using that its underlying bracket is
[x,y]=(x_{2}y_{3}-x_{3}y_{2},2(x_{1}y_{2}-x_{2}y_{1}),2(x_{3}y_{1}-x_{1}y_{3}))
. The underlying Lie algebra has Killing form equal to
8h
and is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{k})
for the elements
p=(1,0,0)
,
q=(0,1,0)
, and
r=(0,0,1)
are an
\mathfrak{sl}(2)
-triple satisfying
[p,q]=r
,
[r,p]=2p
, and
[r,q]=-2q
.

This example can be modified to yield a Euclidean example by taking
\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{k})
in place of
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{k})
. Define a product
\star
on
\mathbb{k}^{3}
by
x\star y=(x_{2}y_{3},x_{3}y_{1},x_{1}y_{2})
. The underlying anticommutative bracket is the usual vector cross product
x\times y=(x_{2}y_{3}-x_{3}y_{2},x_{3}y_{1}-x_{1}y_{3},x_{1}y_{2}-x_{2}y_{1})
on
\mathbb{k}^{3}
, while the underlying commutative product is its symmetric analogue
x\odot y=(x_{2}y_{3}+x_{3}y_{2},x_{3}y_{1}+x_{1}y_{3},x_{1}y_{2}+x_{2}y_{1})
(which is isomorphic to the
3
-dimensional case of the algebra of Example
17
). That the products
\star
,
\times
, and
\odot
are invariant with respect to the metric
g(x,y)=x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}+x_{3}y_{3}
follows from the cyclic symmetry of
g(x\star y,z)=x_{1}y_{2}z_{3}+x_{2}y_{3}z_{1}+x_{3}y_{1}z_{2}
in
x
,
y
, and
z
. The associators satisfy

(5.1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {}_{\star}&=((x_{3}z_{3}-x_{2}z_{2})y_{1},(x_{1}z_{1}-x_{3}z_{3})y_{2},(x_{2}z_{2}-x_{1}z_{1})y_{3}),\\
[x,y,z]_{\times}&=(x\times z)\times y=-[x,y,z]_{\odot},\end{aligned}

from which it is apparent that
\star
is antiflexible and it follows that
g([x,x,y]_{\star},y)=0
, so that the Euclidean metrized algebra
(\mathbb{k}^{3},\star,g)
has vanishing sectional nonassociativity, and
-g([x,x,y]_{\odot},y)=g([x,x,y]_{\times},y)=g(x,x)g(y,y)-g(x,y)^{2}
so that the Euclidean metrized algebras
(\mathbb{k}^{3},\odot,g)
and
(\mathbb{k}^{3},\times,g)
have constant sectional nonassociativities
-1
and
1
, respectively. The last statement is particular cases of Examples
16
and
17
.

Example 16
.

A metrized algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
is a (twofold)
cross product algebra
if it satisfies:

(1)

The multiplication
\circ
is antisymmetric.

(2)

The quantity
h(x\circ y,x\circ y)
equals the Gram determinant
h(x,x)h(y,y)-h(x,y)^{2}
.

It follows from (
1
) and the invariance of
h
that the product
x\circ y
is
h
-orthogonal to
x
and
y
,
h(x\circ y,x)=0
.
In
[
13
]
and
[
9
]
it is shown that a real cross product algebra exists only in dimensions
3
and
7
, in which case it is determined uniquely up to isomorphism by the signature of
h
, the only possibilities being the positive definite case, and the case with negative inertial index one greater than the positive inertial index.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathbb{k}
be an infinite field such that
\operatorname{char}\mathbb{k}\neq 2
. A metrized algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
with antisymmetric multiplication is a cross product algebra if and only if it has constant sectional nonassociativity
1
.

Proof.

The antisymmetry of
\circ
means that (
4.13
) takes the form (
1.2
) It follows that a cross product algebra has constant sectional nonassociativity
1
. On the other hand, if a metrized algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
with antisymmetric multiplication has constant sectional nonassociativity
1
then
h(x\circ y,x\circ y)=h(x,x)h(y,y)-h(x,y)^{2}
whenever
x
and
y
span an
h
-nondegenerate subspace. Because
\mathbb{k}
is infinite, arguing as in the proof of Lemma
4.3
, this holds if and only if
h(x\circ y,x\circ y)=h(x,x)h(y,y)-h(x,y)^{2}
for all
x
and
y
, so that
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
is a cross product algebra.

∎

Corollary 5.1
.

Suppose
\operatorname{char}\mathbb{k}\neq 2
. A metrized algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
with anisotropic
h
satisfies
h(x\circ y,x\circ y)=h(x,x)h(y,y)-h(x,y)^{2}
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{A}
if and only if
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
is a cross product algebra.

Proof.

To show the forward direction it suffices to show
\circ
is antisymmetric.
If
h(x\circ y,x\circ y)=h(x,x)h(y,y)-h(x,y)^{2}
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{A}
, then
h(x\circ x,x\circ x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{A}
. Because
h
is anisotropic, this implies
x\circ x=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{A}
. Because
\operatorname{char}\mathbb{k}\neq 2
, this implies
\circ
is antisymmetric. The reverse implication is immediate.

∎

Hence a cross product algebra can be
defined
as a metrized algebra with antisymmetric multiplication and having sectional nonassociativity
1
on every
h
-nondegenerate subspace. Moreover, it is not necessary to suppose antisymmetry if the metric is anisotropic and
\operatorname{char}\mathbb{k}\neq 2
.

Example 17
.

An example of a commutative algebra having constant negative sectional nonassociativity is the following. Consider
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the coordinatewise product
x\cdot y=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}
. Define
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by
\ell(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}
and define a modified product
x\circ y=\tfrac{n+1}{n-1}x\cdot y-\tfrac{1}{n-1}(\ell(x)y+\ell(y)x)
. Then
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\circ)
is a commutative algebra metrized by its Killing form

(5.2)

\displaystyle\tau_{\circ}(x,y)=\operatorname{tr}L_{\circ}(x)L_{\circ}(y)=\tfrac{n+1}{n-1}\ell(x\cdot y)-\tfrac{1}{n-1}\ell(x)\ell(y),

which is positive definite. With this metric, this algebra satisfies the reverse Norton inequality; in fact it has constant negative sectional nonassociativity. See
[
18
]
for details.

Example 18
.

This example describes a one-parameter family of pairwise nonisomorphic
3
-dimensional Euclidean metrized commutative algebras having constant sectional nonassociativity.

For
\epsilon\in[0,\infty)
, define a commutative algebra
(\mathbb{U}_{\epsilon},\circ)
as the
3
-dimensional
\mathbb{R}
-vector space
\mathbb{U}_{\epsilon}
with basis
\{f_{0},f_{1},f_{2}\}
and multiplication
\circ
given by

(5.3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &f_{0}\circ f_{0}=f_{0},&&f_{0}\circ f_{1}=(\tfrac{1}{2}-\epsilon)f_{1},&&f_{0}\circ f_{2}=(\tfrac{1}{2}+\epsilon)f_{2},&\\
&f_{1}\circ f_{1}=(\tfrac{1}{2}-\epsilon)f_{0},&&f_{1}\circ f_{2}=0,&&f_{2}\circ f_{2}=(\tfrac{1}{2}+\epsilon)f_{0}.\end{aligned}

For
x=x_{0}f_{0}+x_{1}f_{1}+x_{2}f_{2}
the matrix of

with respect to the ordered basis

is

(5.4)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x_{0}&(\tfrac{1}{2}-\epsilon)x_{1}&(\tfrac{1}{2}+\epsilon)x_{2}\\
(\tfrac{1}{2}-\epsilon)x_{1}&(\tfrac{1}{2}-\epsilon)x_{0}&0\\
(\tfrac{1}{2}+\epsilon)x_{2}&0&(\tfrac{1}{2}+\epsilon)x_{0}\end{pmatrix},

A calculation using

(5.5)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} x\circ y=&(x_{0}y_{0}+(\tfrac{1}{2}-\epsilon)x_{1}y_{1}+(\tfrac{1}{2}+\epsilon)x_{2}y_{2})f_{0}+(\tfrac{1}{2}-\epsilon)(x_{0}y_{1}+x_{1}y_{0})f_{1}+(\tfrac{1}{2}+\epsilon)(x_{0}y_{2}+x_{2}y_{0})f_{2},\end{aligned}

shows that the metric

satisfies

(5.6)

\displaystyle h(x\circ y,z)=x_{0}y_{0}z_{0}+(\tfrac{1}{2}-\epsilon)(x_{1}y_{1}z_{0}+x_{1}y_{0}z_{1}+x_{0}y_{1}z_{1})+(\tfrac{1}{2}+\epsilon)(x_{2}y_{2}z_{0}+x_{2}y_{0}z_{2}+x_{0}y_{2}z_{2}).

Because this is completely symmetric in
x
,
y
, and
z
it shows

is invariant with respect to
\circ
.
From (
5.4
) and (
5.5
) it follows that the matrix of
\mathscr{A}_{\circ}(x,y)=L_{\circ}(x\circ y)-L_{\circ}(x)L_{\circ}(y)

Corollary 5.2
.

For
\epsilon,\bar{\epsilon}\in[0,\infty)
,
(\mathbb{U}_{\epsilon},\circ)

are not isomorphic if
\epsilon\neq\bar{\epsilon}
.

Proof.

The algebra
(\mathbb{U}_{1/2},\circ)
is associative, isomorphic to a direct sum of a trivial ideal spanned by

and a two-dimensional ideal spanned by

and
f_{2}
that is isomorphic to the quadratic algebra
\mathbb{R}[t]/(t^{2}-1)
.
When

,

is not power associative. For example, for

An algebra isomorphism maps idempotents to idempotents and preserves the spectra of the corresponding left multiplication endomorphisms. By Lemma

, the algebra

has a unique nontrivial idempotent and only one one-dimensional subalgebra, while for all

A Euclidean metrized algebra

has nonnegative sectional nonassociativity if and only if

\displaystyle 0\leq h([x,x,y],y)=h(x\circ x,y\circ y)-h(x\circ y,y\circ x),

That is, the associator endomorphism

is nonnegative definite for all

For metrized commutative algebras (

because S. P. Norton showed that it holds for the Griess algebra of the monster finite simple group
[

Example 19

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
(\mathbb{A},\circ,q)
be a real symmetric composition algebra having associated bilinear form
h
. If
h
is positive definite, the sectional nonassociativity of
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
satisfies
|\mathscr{K}(x,y)|\leq 1
for all linearly independent
x,y\in\mathbb{A}
. There holds
\mathscr{K}(x,y)=1
if and only if
x\circ x
and
y\circ y
are linearly dependent, and there holds
\mathscr{K}(x,y)=-1
if and only if
x\circ y=y\circ x
.

Proof.

Linearizing the relation
q(x\circ y)=q(x)q(y)
shows that
h
satisfies

(6.2)

\displaystyle h(x\circ y,w\circ z)+h(w\circ y,x\circ z)=h(x,w)h(y,z)=h(y\circ x,z\circ w)+h(z\circ x,y\circ w).

Taking
z=x
and
w=y
in (
6.2
) yields

(6.3)

\displaystyle h(x\circ y,y\circ x)+h(x\circ x,y\circ y)=h(x,y)^{2},

while the multiplicativity of
q
yields
2|x\circ y|^{2}=|x|^{2}|y|^{2}=2|y\circ x|^{2}
. Together these yield

(6.4)

\displaystyle|x|^{2}|y|^{2}-h(x,y)^{2}=2|x\circ y|^{2}-h(x\circ y,y\circ x)-h(x\circ x,y\circ y).

There results

(6.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{K}(x,y)&=\tfrac{h(x\circ x,y\circ y)-h(x\circ y,y\circ x)}{|x|^{2}|y|^{2}-h(x,y)^{2}}=-1+\tfrac{2\left(|x\circ y|^{2}-h(x\circ y,y\circ x)\right)}{|x|^{2}|y|^{2}-h(x,y)^{2}}=-1+\tfrac{|x\circ y-y\circ x|^{2}}{|x|^{2}|y|^{2}-h(x,y)^{2}}\geq-1,\end{split}

where the second equality follows from
|x\circ y|=|y\circ x|
. Equality holds if and only if
x\circ y=y\circ x
. On the other hand, (
6.3
) yields

(6.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{K}(x,y)&=\tfrac{h(x\circ x,y\circ y)-h(x\circ y,y\circ x)}{|x|^{2}|y|^{2}-h(x,y)^{2}}=\tfrac{2h(x\circ x,y\circ y)-h(x,y)^{2}}{|x|^{2}|y|^{2}-h(x,y)^{2}}\leq\tfrac{2|x\circ x||y\circ y|-h(x,y)^{2}}{|x|^{2}|y|^{2}-h(x,y)^{2}}=1,\end{split}

the inequality by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and the last equality because

. Equality holds if and only if
x\circ x
and
y\circ y
are linearly dependent.

∎

The complex Okubo algebra is
\mathbb{B}=\mathfrak{sl}(3,\mathbb{C})
equipped with the multiplication

(6.7)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} x\star y&=\omega xy-\omega^{2}yx+\tfrac{\omega-\omega^{2}}{3}h(x,y)I=-\tfrac{1}{2}[x,y]+\tfrac{\omega-\omega^{2}}{2}\left(xy+yx+\tfrac{2}{3}h(x,y)I\right).\end{aligned}

where juxtaposition indicates the ordinary matrix product,
[x,y]=xy-yx
is the usual matrix commutator,

, and
\omega
is a nontrivial cube root of unity (so

[

[

From (

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}_{\mathbb{B},\star,h}(x,y)=\tfrac{h([x,x,y]_{\star},y)}{h(x,x)h(y,y)-h(x,y)^{2}}=\tfrac{h([x,y],[x,y])}{h(x,x)h(y,y)-h(x,y)^{2}}-1.

-dimensional Jordan algebra of a quadratic form often called a
spin factor
; this follows from the observation that the
\star
-square of a trace-free element is a multiple of the unit.

The matrix trace
\operatorname{tr}:\mathbb{M}(n,\mathbb{h})\to\mathbb{h}
is a
\mathbb{k}
-linear map satisfying
\operatorname{tr}(\bar{x}^{t})=\operatorname{tr}(\bar{x})=\overline{\operatorname{tr}(x)}
and so also
\mathsf{t}(\operatorname{tr}(x))=\operatorname{tr}(x+\bar{x})
. The
\mathbb{k}
-bilinear form
h
on
\mathbb{M}(n,\mathbb{h})
defined by
h(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{2n}\mathsf{t}(\operatorname{tr}(\bar{x}^{t}y))
is symmetric (even when
\mathbb{h}
is noncommutative).
Its restriction to
\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h})
satisfies
h(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{2n}\mathsf{t}(\operatorname{tr}(xy))=\tfrac{1}{n}\mathsf{t}(\operatorname{tr}(x\star y))
. The dimension dependent constant factor is a normalization.

The
nucleus

\mathscr{N}(\mathbb{A},\circ)
of an algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ)
is the set of elements
a\in\mathbb{A}
such that there vanish the associators
[a,x,y]
,
[x,a,y]
, and
[y,a,x]
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{A}
. It is straightforward to see that if
(\mathbb{A},\circ)
is commutative, then
\mathscr{N}(\mathbb{A},\circ)=\{a\in\mathbb{A}:[L_{\circ}(a),L_{\circ}(x)]=0\,\,\text{for all}\,\,x\in\mathbb{A}\}
.
If a unital Jordan algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ)
has nondegenerate trace form
\operatorname{tr}L_{\circ}(x\circ y)
and
\sigma\in S^{2}\mathbb{A}^{\ast}
is an invariant symmetric bilinear form, then there is
z\in\mathscr{N}(\mathbb{A},\circ)
such that
\sigma(x,y)=\operatorname{tr}L_{\circ}((z\circ x)\circ y)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{A}

[
33
, Chapter
3
, Theorem
10
]
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Suppose
\operatorname{char}\mathbb{k}\notdivides 2n
. Let
\mathbb{h}
be a
d
-dimensional Hurwitz
\mathbb{k}
-algebra and let
N=n+dn(n-1)/2
. The symmetric bilinear form
h
on
\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h})
defined by
h(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{2n}\mathsf{t}(\operatorname{tr}(xy))=\tfrac{1}{n}\mathsf{t}(\operatorname{tr}(x\star y))
is nondegenerate and invariant and satisfies
\operatorname{tr}L_{\star}(x,y)=Nh(x,y)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h})
.

Proof.

A consequence of the involutive invariance of the metric
h
on
\mathbb{h}
is that the linear map
\mathsf{t}:\mathbb{h}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
\mathsf{t}(x)=x+\bar{x}
satisfies

(6.9)

\displaystyle\mathsf{t}((xy)z)=h((xy)z,e)=h(xy,\bar{z})=h(x,\bar{z}\bar{y})=h(x(yz),e)=\mathsf{t}(x(yz)),

\displaystyle\text{for}\,\,x,y,z\in\mathbb{h}.

From (
6.9
) it follows that
\mathsf{t}(\operatorname{tr}((xy)z))=\operatorname{tr}((xy)z+\bar{z}(\bar{y}\bar{x}))=\operatorname{tr}(x(yz)+(\bar{z}\bar{y})\bar{x})=\mathsf{t}(\operatorname{tr}(x(yz))
for
x,y,z\in\mathbb{M}(n,\mathbb{h})
, and there follows

(6.10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}2nh(x\star y,z)&=2\mathsf{t}(\operatorname{tr}(x\star y)z)=\mathsf{t}(\operatorname{tr}((xy)z))+\mathsf{t}(\operatorname{tr}((yx)z))\\
&=\mathsf{t}(\operatorname{tr}(x(yz)))+\mathsf{t}(\operatorname{tr}(z(yx))=\mathsf{t}(\operatorname{tr}(x(yz)))+\mathsf{t}(\operatorname{tr}((zy)x))\\
&=\mathsf{t}(\operatorname{tr}(x(yz)))+\mathsf{t}(\operatorname{tr}(x(zy)))=2\mathsf{t}(\operatorname{tr}(x(y\star z))=2nh(x,y\star z),\end{split}

showing
h
is invariant on
\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h})
. Using a basis of
\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h})
as in
[
30
, pp.
125-126
]
is straightforward to check that
h
is nondegenerate.

From the invariance of
h
is follows that there is
z\in\mathscr{N}(\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h}),\star)
such that
h(x,y)=\operatorname{tr}L_{\star}((z\star x)\star y)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h})
. The
center
of a Jordan algebra is the subset of its nucleus comprising elements that commute with all other elements. By a theorem of A. A. Albert
[
1
]
, the nucleus of a simple Jordan
\mathbb{k}
-algebra (
\operatorname{char}\mathbb{k}\neq 2
) equals its center. Consequently, since
(\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h}),\star)
is simple, its nucleus equals its center. Because
(\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h}),\star)
is central simple, its center is its subalgebra, isomorphic to
\mathbb{k}
, spanned by its unit
[
30
, Chapter
5
, Section
7
]
. It follows that
z
is a multiple of the unit, so
h
is a multiple of
\operatorname{tr}L_{\star}(\,\cdot\,\star\,\cdot\,)
. Because
h(e,e)=1
and
\operatorname{tr}L_{\star}(e\star e)=\dim\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h})=N=n+dn(n-1)/2
where
d=\dim\mathbb{h}
, it follows that
\operatorname{tr}L_{\star}(\,\cdot\,\star\,\cdot\,)=Nh
.
∎

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\mathbb{h}
be a real Hurwitz algebra.
Equip

with the invariant metric

\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h})

satisfies

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathscr{K}(x,y)=\mathscr{K}_{h,\star}(x,y)\leq\tfrac{n}{2},

for all linearly independent
x,y\in\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h})
.

(1)

If
\mathbb{h}
is associative, equality holds in the lower bound of (
6.11
) if and only if
x
and
y
commute with respect to the matrix product; in particular, if

and

are linearly independent, then
\mathscr{K}(x,x\star x)=0
.

(2)

Equality holds in the upper bound of (
6.11
) if and only if
x
and
y
are simultaneously equivalent under
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h}),\star)
to scalar multiples of
e_{11}-e_{nn}
and
e_{1n}+e_{n1}
.

Proof.

For
x,y\in\mathbb{M}(n,\mathbb{h})
, let
[x,y]=xy-yx
. By (
4.14
), the associators
of
\star
and the matrix product
\cdot
satisfy

(6.12)

\displaystyle\begin{split}4[x,y,z]_{\star}&=2[x,y,z]_{\cdot}-2[z,y,x]_{\cdot}+[x,[y,z]]+[z,[x,y]].\end{split}

If
\mathbb{h}
is associative,
(\mathbb{M}(n,\mathbb{h}),\cdot)
is associative, so (
6.12
) becomes simply

.
Hence

(6.13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}h(x\star x,z\star z)&-h(x\star z,z\star x)=h([x,x,z]_{\star},z)=\tfrac{1}{4}h([x,[x,z]],z)=\tfrac{1}{4n}\operatorname{tr}([x,[x,z]]\star y)\\
&=\tfrac{1}{8n}\operatorname{tr}\left([x,[x,z]]z+z[x,[x,z]]\right)=-\tfrac{1}{4n}\operatorname{tr}[x,z][x,z]=\tfrac{1}{4n}\operatorname{tr}\overline{[x,z]}^{t}[x,z].\end{split}

When
\mathbb{h}
is associative, an automorphism of
(\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h}),\star)
preserves the ordinary matrix product and commutator bracket on
\mathbb{M}(n,\mathbb{h})
, but I do not know if this is true when
\mathbb{h}=\mathbb{O}

too).

Recall that the principal axis theorem states that every element of
\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h})
(where

if

) is equivalent via an element of

to a diagonal matrix. For

[
23
, Theorem

]

[

2.5
]

By the principal axis theorem there are

and diagonal

such that

6.12

\displaystyle 4[x,x,z]_{\star}=4g[\lambda,\lambda,y]_{\star}=g[\lambda,[\lambda,y]].

As in (

\displaystyle\begin{split}h&(x\star x,z\star z)-h(x\star z,z\star x)=h([x,x,z]_{\star},z)=\tfrac{1}{4}h(g[\lambda,[\lambda,y]],gy)=\tfrac{1}{4}h([\lambda,[\lambda,y]],y)\\
&=\tfrac{1}{4n}\operatorname{tr}([\lambda,[\lambda,y]]\star y)=\tfrac{1}{8n}\operatorname{tr}\left([\lambda,[\lambda,y]]y+y[\lambda,[\lambda,y]]\right)=-\tfrac{1}{4n}\operatorname{tr}[\lambda,y][\lambda,y]=\tfrac{1}{4n}\operatorname{tr}\overline{[\lambda,y]}^{t}[\lambda,y],\end{split}

where, in the case

[

By (

Since

If

The Chern-do Carmo-Kobayashi inequality over real Hurwitz algebras

Lemma 7.1

Let
\mathbb{h}
be a real Hurwitz algebra. Let

be metrized by

\displaystyle|[x,y]|^{2}\leq\mathscr{W}(|x|^{2}|y|^{2}-f(x,y)^{2})\leq\mathscr{W}|x|^{2}|y|^{2},

If

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies directly

is finite but is not optimal. The following argument from the proof of

shows that the apparently weaker inequality

or

equals

and

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}|[x,y]|^{2}=\tfrac{1}{4|x|^{2}|y|^{2}}|[p,q]|^{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{2|x|^{2}|y|^{2}}|p|^{2}|q|^{2}=2(|x|^{2}|y|^{2}-f(x,y)^{2}).\end{split}

If there is equality in the first inequality of (

(\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h}),\star)
is equivalent via an automorphism of
(\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h}),\star)
to a diagonal matrix.
Since an automorphism of
(\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h}),\star)
preserves the trace, it is isometric, and, when
\mathbb{h}
is associative it preserves the commutator of the matrix product on
\mathbb{M}(n,\mathbb{h})
restricted to
\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h})
, when
\mathbb{h}
is associative to prove
|[x,y]|^{2}\leq 2|x|^{2}|y|^{2}
in general it suffices to prove it when
x
is diagonal. By Lemma
7.1
, in either case
|[x,y]|^{2}\leq 2|x|^{2}|y|^{2}
implies (
7.5
).

The characterization of the equality case (
3
) goes through as in the proof of
[
11
, Lemma
1
]
. Precisely, if there is equality in (
7.6
), then

(7.7)

\displaystyle 0=4\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}|y_{ii}|^{2}+2\sum_{i\neq j}(x_{i}+x_{j})^{2}|y_{ij}|^{2}=2\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|y_{ii}|^{2}+2\sum_{i\neq j}(x_{i}+x_{j})^{2}|y_{ij}|^{2}.

If
x
is nonzero, this implies
y_{ii}=0
for
1\leq i\leq n
,
x_{i}+x_{j}=0
when
y_{ij}\neq 0
for
i\neq j
, and that at exactly two of
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
are nonzero. Suppose
i\neq j
are the indices such that
x_{i}=-x_{j}\neq 0
. It follows that
y_{kl}=0
if
\{k,l\}\neq\{i,j\}
. Hence
x
and
y
are scalar multiples of
e_{ii}-e_{jj}
and
e_{ij}+e_{ji}
, respectively. Since
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h}))
contains a subgroup acting as permutations on the diagonal subalgebra of
\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h})
, it can be assumed that
i=1
and
j=n
. The proof of the equality case (
4
) follows from (
3
) exactly as in the proof of (
2
) of Theorem
7.1
.
∎

Remark 2
.

Although the same difficulty related to diagonalization occurs in the proof of Theorem
6.1
as occurs in the proof of Lemma
7.2
, the end result in Theorem
6.1
is stated in terms manifestly invariant with respect to
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{h}))
, and as a result takes the same form whether or not
\mathbb{h}
is associative. This observation suggests that the upper bound on sectional nonassociativity, which is closely related to the commutator bound, is the more natural bound to consider, at least from the algebraic point of view, although it could also simply reflect a technical deficiency in the proof of Lemma
7.2
.

Remark 3
.

It would be useful to know whether (
7.5
) is true for
\mathbb{Herm}(3,\mathbb{O})
. This seems likely. More, generally, it would be interesting to know whether (
7.5
) is true for
\mathbb{Herm}(n,\mathbb{O})
for
n>3
. This would follow from a principal axis theorem and a characterization of the automorphism group, but it appears that neither is known. See
[
69
]
for what is known in this regard.

Remark 4
.

For a compact semisimple real Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
with Killing form
B_{\mathfrak{g}}
the number

(7.8)

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}(\mathfrak{g})=\mathscr{U}(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,],-B_{\mathfrak{g}})=\sup_{x,y\in\mathfrak{g}:x\wedge y\neq 0}\frac{-B_{\mathfrak{g}}([x,y],[x,y])}{B_{\mathfrak{g}}(x,x)B_{\mathfrak{g}}(y,y)-B_{g}(x,y)^{2}}

is positive and finite, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Its value is a basic automorphism invariant of
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])
. Its calculation for particular
\mathfrak{g}
can be viewed as a refinement of the Böttcher-Wenzel inequality. On the other hand, for some compact simple real Lie algebras
\mathscr{W}(\mathfrak{g})
can be estimated using the Böttcher-Wenzel inequality.
For
\mathfrak{su}(n)
and
\mathfrak{so}(n)
,
B_{\mathfrak{su}(n)}(x,y)=-2nf(x,y)
and
B_{\mathfrak{so}(n)}(x,y)=-(n-2)f(x,y)
where
f(x,y)=\operatorname{tr}\bar{x}^{t}y
, so in these cases it follows from the Böttcher-Wenzel inequality, Theorem
7.1
, that

(7.9)

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}(\mathfrak{so}(n))\leq\tfrac{2}{n-2},

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}(\mathfrak{su}(n))\leq\tfrac{1}{n}.

For the special case of
\mathfrak{so}(n)
, viewed as antisymmetric matrices, equipped with the Frobenius norm
f
, the essentially equivalent quantity
\sup_{x,y\in\mathfrak{g}:x\wedge y\neq 0}\tfrac{|[x,y]|_{f}}{|x|_{f}|y|_{f}}
was shown to equal
\sqrt{2}
if
n\geq 4
in
[
5
, Theorem
6
]
. By Lemma
7.3
, these estimates yield sharp numerical bounds on the sectional nonassociativites of the subspaces spanned by decomposable elements in algebras such as
\mathfrak{so}(m)\otimes\mathfrak{so}(n)
,
\mathfrak{so}(m)\otimes\mathfrak{su}(n)
, and
\mathfrak{su}(m)\otimes\mathfrak{su}(n)
.

Lemma
7.3
shows a partial lower bound on the sectional nonassociativities of the tensor product of Lie algebras.

Lemma 7.3
.

For compact semisimple real Lie algebras
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{h}
, let
\tau_{\circ}
be the Killing form of the tensor product algebra
(\mathfrak{g}\otimes\mathfrak{h},\circ)
, defined by
\tau_{\circ}(a,b)=\operatorname{tr}L_{\circ}(a)L_{\circ}(b)
for
a,b\in\mathfrak{g}\otimes\mathfrak{h}
. The sectional nonassociativity of the subspace of
(\mathfrak{g}\otimes\mathfrak{h},\circ,\tau_{\circ})
spanned by decomposable elements
a_{1}\otimes b_{1},a_{2}\otimes b_{2}\in\mathfrak{g}\otimes\mathfrak{h}
satisfies

(7.10)

\displaystyle 0\geq\mathscr{K}(a_{1}\otimes b_{1},a_{2}\otimes b_{2})\geq-\mathscr{W}(\mathfrak{g})\mathscr{W}(\mathfrak{h}).

Proof.

Because
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{h}
are compact, their Killing forms,
B_{\mathfrak{g}}
and
B_{\mathfrak{h}}
, satisfy
-B_{\mathfrak{g}}([a_{1},a_{2}],[a_{1},a_{2}])\geq 0
and
-B_{\mathfrak{h}}([b_{1},b_{2}],[b_{1},b_{2}])\geq 0
.
The upper bound in (
7.10
) follows from

(7.11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{K}(a_{1}\otimes b_{1},a_{2}\otimes b_{2})&=-\tfrac{B_{\mathfrak{g}}([a_{1},a_{2}],[a_{1},a_{2}])B_{\mathfrak{h}}([b_{1},b_{2}],[b_{1},b_{2}])}{B_{\mathfrak{g}}(a_{1},a_{1})B_{\mathfrak{g}}(a_{2},a_{2})B_{\mathfrak{h}}(b_{1},b_{1})B_{\mathfrak{h}}(b_{2},b_{2})-B_{\mathfrak{g}}(a_{1},a_{2})^{2}B_{\mathfrak{h}}(b_{1},b_{2})^{2}}\leq 0,\end{split}

where the positivity of the denominator follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. On the other hand, by definition of
\mathscr{W}(\mathfrak{g})
and
\mathscr{W}(\mathfrak{h})
,

(7.12)

\displaystyle\begin{split}-&\tfrac{\mathscr{K}(a_{1}\otimes b_{1},a_{2}\otimes b_{2})}{\mathscr{W}(\mathfrak{g})\mathscr{W}(\mathfrak{h})}=\tfrac{1}{\mathscr{W}(\mathfrak{g})\mathscr{W}(\mathfrak{h})}\tfrac{\left(-B_{\mathfrak{g}}([a_{1},a_{2}],[a_{1},a_{2}])\right)\left(-B_{\mathfrak{h}}([b_{1},b_{2}],[b_{1},b_{2}])\right)}{B_{\mathfrak{g}}(a_{1},a_{1})B_{\mathfrak{g}}(a_{2},a_{2})B_{\mathfrak{h}}(b_{1},b_{1})B_{\mathfrak{h}}(b_{2},b_{2})-B_{\mathfrak{g}}(a_{1},a_{2})^{2}B_{\mathfrak{h}}(b_{1},b_{2})^{2}}\\
&\leq\tfrac{\left(B_{\mathfrak{g}}(a_{1},a_{1})B_{\mathfrak{g}}(a_{2},a_{2})-B_{\mathfrak{g}}(a_{1},a_{2})^{2}\right)\left(B_{\mathfrak{h}}(b_{1},b_{1})B_{\mathfrak{h}}(b_{2},b_{2})-B_{\mathfrak{h}}(b_{1},b_{2})^{2}\right)}{B_{\mathfrak{g}}(a_{1},a_{1})B_{\mathfrak{g}}(a_{2},a_{2})B_{\mathfrak{h}}(b_{1},b_{1})B_{\mathfrak{h}}(b_{2},b_{2})-B_{\mathfrak{g}}(a_{1},a_{2})^{2}B_{\mathfrak{h}}(b_{1},b_{2})^{2}}\\
&=1-\tfrac{B_{\mathfrak{g}}(a_{1},a_{2})^{2}\left(B_{\mathfrak{h}}(b_{1},b_{1})B_{\mathfrak{h}}(b_{2},b_{2})-B_{\mathfrak{h}}(b_{1},b_{2})^{2}\right)+B_{\mathfrak{h}}(b_{1},b_{2})^{2}\left(B_{\mathfrak{g}}(a_{1},a_{1})B_{\mathfrak{g}}(a_{2},a_{2})-B_{\mathfrak{g}}(a_{1},a_{2})^{2}\right)}{B_{\mathfrak{g}}(a_{1},a_{1})B_{\mathfrak{g}}(a_{2},a_{2})B_{\mathfrak{h}}(b_{1},b_{1})B_{\mathfrak{h}}(b_{2},b_{2})-B_{\mathfrak{g}}(a_{1},a_{2})^{2}B_{\mathfrak{h}}(b_{1},b_{2})^{2}}\leq 1,\end{split}

which shows the lower bound in (
7.10
).
∎

Note that Lemma
7.3
does not imply that
\mathfrak{g}\otimes\mathfrak{h}
has nonpositive sectional nonassociativity. In fact, for any compact simple real Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
,
\mathfrak{so}(3)\otimes\mathfrak{g}
has sectional nonassociativities of both signs
[
18
]
.

Remark 5
.

Although this does not seem to be realized widely, for anti-Hermitian matrices the inequality (
7.4
) is closely related to Vinberg’s results on invariant norms on compact simple Lie algebras in
[
64
]
applied in the special case of
\mathfrak{su}(n)
. Precisely, Vinberg shows that, for an invariant norm,
||\,\cdot\,||
on a compact simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
the quantity
\theta(x)=\sup_{0\neq y\in\mathfrak{g}}\tfrac{||[x,y]||}{||y||}
does not depend on the choice of invariant norm, and equals the spectral norm of
\operatorname{ad}_{\mathfrak{g}}(x)
. Since, by definition,
\theta([x,y])\leq\theta(x)\theta(y)
, taking
\mathfrak{g}
to be a compact simple Lie algebra of matrices, e.g. antisymmetric or anti-Hermitian matrices, and taking
||\,\cdot\,||
to be the Frobenius norm there results
|[x,y]|_{f}\leq\theta(x)|y|_{f}
. It suffices then to check that the spectral norm of
\operatorname{ad}_{\mathfrak{g}}(x)
is no greater than
\sqrt{2}|x|_{f}
. In the case
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{su}(n)
this can be shown as follows. Since any anti-Hermitian matrix is unitarily conjugate to a diagonal matrix and the spectral norms of the adjoint representations of unitarily conjugate matrices are the same, it suffices to consider the case of diagonal
x\in\mathfrak{su}(n)
. In this case the nonzero eigenvalues of
\operatorname{ad}_{\mathfrak{su}(n)}(x)
have the form
\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}
where
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
are the diagonal elements of
x
. Since
|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}\leq 2|\lambda|^{2}\leq 2|x|^{2}_{f}
, where
\lambda
is the element of
x
with the greatest modulus, the spectral norm of
\operatorname{ad}_{\mathfrak{su}(n)}(x)
is no greater than
\sqrt{2}|x|_{f}
.

8.
Consequences for commutative algebras of conditions on sectional nonassociativity

This section describes some general structural consequences for commutative algebras of assumptions on the sectional nonassociativity.

Consequences of nonnegative sectional nonassociativity for commutative algebras can be found in
[
12
, section
17
]
and in
[
42
]
, where they are applied to the study of maximal associative subalgebras of the Griess algebra. By the analogy advocated here, associative subalgebras are analogous to flat submanifolds of a nonnegatively curved Riemannian manifold. Here such results are cast in a somewhat more general setting.

An element
e\in\mathbb{A}
is
idempotent
if
e\circ e=e
and
square-zero
if
e\circ e=0
.
Let
\mathbb{Idem}(\mathbb{A},\circ)=\{0\neq e\in\mathbb{A}:e\circ e=e\}
and
\mathbb{Nil}_{2}(\mathbb{A},\circ)=\{0\neq z\in\mathbb{A}:z\circ z=0\}
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
be a Euclidean metrized commutative algebra.

(1)

If
a+ib\in\mathbb{Nil}_{2}(\mathbb{A}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C},\circ)
and the
\mathbb{R}
-span of
a
and
b
is two-dimensional, then

(8.1)

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}(a,b)=\tfrac{|a\circ a|^{2}}{|a\wedge b|^{2}}\geq 0,

with equality if and only if
a
and
b
span a trivial subalgebra of
(\mathbb{A},\circ)
.

(2)

If
a+ib\in\mathbb{Idem}(\mathbb{A}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C},\circ)
and the
\mathbb{R}
-span of
a
and
b
is two-dimensional, then

(8.2)

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}(a,b)=\tfrac{1}{4}\tfrac{4|b\circ b|^{2}+|b|^{2}}{|a\wedge b|^{2}}>0.

Proof.

That
a+ib\in\mathbb{Nil}_{2}(\mathbb{A}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C},\circ)
is equivalent to the equations
a\circ a=b\circ b
and
a\circ b=0
, and in (
4.13
) these yield (
8.1
). If equality holds in (
8.1
), then
b\circ b=a\circ a=0
, so
a
and
b
generate a trivial subalgebra.
Suppose
a+ib\in\mathbb{Idem}(\mathbb{A}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C},\circ)
, so that
a\circ a-b\circ b=a
and
2a\circ b=b
. Then

(8.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}h(a\circ a,b\circ b)&-h(a\circ b,a\circ b)=h(b\circ b+a,b\circ b)=|b\circ b|^{2}+h(b,a\circ b)-\tfrac{1}{4}|b|^{2}=|b\circ b|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}|b|^{2},\end{split}

and in (
4.13
) this yields (
8.2
). In (
8.2
), equality cannot hold because it would imply
b=0
, contrary to hypothesis.
∎

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
be a Euclidean metrized commutative algebra.

(1)

If
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
has negative sectional nonassociativity, then
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
is finite.

(2)

If
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
has nonpositive sectional nonassociativity, either
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
is finite or
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
contains a trivial subalgebra of dimension
1
or
2
(the possibilities are not exclusive).

Proof.

For
k\geq 3
, let
\theta
be a multilinear
k
-form on a complex vector space
\mathbb{V}
. By a theorem of H. Suzuki
[
61
, Theorem B]
, either the group of linear automorphisms of
\theta
is finite or there is a nonzero vector
v\in\mathbb{V}
such that
\theta(v,\dots,v,w)=0
for all
w\in\mathbb{V}
. Regard
\mu(x,y,z)=h(x\circ y,z)
as a trilinear form on
\mathbb{A}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}
. An orthogonal automorphism of
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
preserves
\mu
, and extends to an automorphism of
\mathbb{A}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}
. By Suzuki’s theorem if
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
is not finite there exists
a+ib\in\mathbb{A}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}
such that
h((a+ib)\circ(a+ib),w)=0
for all
w\in\mathbb{A}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}
. By the nondegeneracy of
h
,
a+ib\in\mathbb{Nil}_{2}(\mathbb{A}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C},\circ)
. Note that it is not asserted that
a
and
b
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{R}
. The conclusion follows from (
1
) of Lemma
8.1
.
∎

For a Euclidean metrized commutative algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
, the endomorphism
L_{\circ}(e)
is
h
-self-adjoint for any
e\in\mathbb{A}
. If
e\in\mathbb{Idem}(\mathbb{A},\circ)
, then
L_{\circ}(e)
preserves
\langle e\rangle^{\perp}=\{y\in\mathbb{A}:h(e,y)=0\}
, for if
h(e,y)=0
then
h(L_{\circ}(e)y,e)=h(y,e\circ e)=h(y,e)=0
. Define the
orthogonal spectrum

(8.4)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Spec}^{\perp}(e)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{k}:\text{there is}\,\,x\in\mathbb{k}\{e\}^{\perp}\,\,\text{such that}\,\,L_{\circ}(e)x=\lambda x\},

so that
\operatorname{Spec}(e)=\operatorname{Spec}^{\perp}(e)\cup\{1\}
. Because
L_{\circ}(e)
preserves
\langle e\rangle^{\perp}
it has an eigenvector in
\langle e\rangle^{\perp}
. Because
h(e,e)>0
, this eigenvector is not a multiple of
e
, so in this case
\operatorname{Spec}^{\perp}(e)
is not empty.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
be a Euclidean metrized commutative algebra of dimension
n\geq 2
. If
0\neq e\in\mathbb{Idem}(\mathbb{A},\circ)
, then
4\mathscr{K}(e,x)\leq|e|^{-2}
for all
x\in\mathbb{A}
such that
x\wedge e\neq 0
, with equality if and only if
x-h(e,e)^{-1}h(e,x)e\in\ker(L_{\circ}(e)-\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Id}_{\mathbb{A}})
.

Proof.

Let
e\in\mathbb{Idem}(\mathbb{A},\circ)
.
Because
\mathscr{K}(e,x)
depends only on the subspace spanned by
e
and
x
, in computing
\mathscr{K}(e,x)
it may be supposed that
h(e,x)=0
.
Write
x=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}
where
x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}\in\langle e\rangle^{\perp}
are
h
-orthogonal eigenvectors of
L_{\circ}(e)
with (not necessarily distinct) eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n-1}
. There results

(8.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{K}(e,x)&=\frac{h(e\circ e,x\circ x)-h(e\circ x,e\circ x)}{|e|^{2}|x|^{2}}=\frac{h(e\circ x,x)-h(e\circ x,e\circ x)}{|e|^{2}|x|^{2}}=|e|^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{i}(1-\lambda_{i})\frac{|x_{i}|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}.\end{split}

Because
\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|x_{i}|^{2}=|x|^{2}
, (
8.5
) exhibits
\mathscr{K}(e,x)
as a convex combination, so implies

(8.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}|e|^{-2}\min\{\lambda_{i}(1-\lambda_{i}):1\leq i\leq n-1\}\leq\mathscr{K}(e,x)\leq|e|^{-2}\max\{\lambda_{i}(1-\lambda_{i}):1\leq i\leq n-1\}.\end{split}

As
\lambda(1-\lambda)\leq 1/4
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, (
8.6
) yields
4\mathscr{K}(e,x)\leq|e|^{-2}
. There holds equality if and only if
\lambda_{i}=1/2
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
, in which case
x=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}
is an eigenvector of
L_{\circ}(e)
with eigenvalue
1/2
.
∎

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
be a Euclidean metrized commutative algebra of dimension
n\geq 2
. Let
0\neq e\in\mathbb{Idem}(\mathbb{A},\circ)
.

(1)

If there is
b\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathscr{K}(\mathbb{U})\leq b
for all two-dimensional subspaces
\mathbb{U}\subset\mathbb{A}
containing
e
, then
\operatorname{Spec}^{\perp}(e)\subset(-\infty,\tfrac{1-\sqrt{B}}{2}]\cup[\tfrac{1+\sqrt{B}}{2},\infty)
, where
B=\max\{1-4b|e|^{2},0\}
. In particular, if
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
has nonpositive sectional nonassociativity, then
\operatorname{Spec}^{\perp}(e)\cap(0,1)=\emptyset
.

(2)

If there is
b\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathscr{K}(\mathbb{U})\geq b
for all two-dimensional subspaces
\mathbb{U}\subset\mathbb{A}
containing
e
, then
b\leq 1/(4|e|^{2})
and
\operatorname{Spec}^{\perp}(e)\subset[\tfrac{1-\sqrt{B}}{2},\tfrac{1+\sqrt{B}}{2}]
for
B=1-4b|e|^{2}\geq 0
. In particular, if
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
has nonnegative sectional nonassociativity, then
\operatorname{Spec}^{\perp}(e)\subset[0,1]
.

Proof.

In the setting of (
1
), if
0\neq y\in\mathbb{A}
is an eigenvector of
L_{\circ}(e)
with eigenvalue
\lambda
and orthogonal to
e
, then, by (
8.5
),
\lambda(1-\lambda)=|e|^{2}\mathscr{K}(e,y)\leq b|e|^{2}
, so
\lambda^{2}-\lambda+b|e|^{2}\geq 0
, which forces
\lambda
to be in the indicated range. In the setting of (
2
), the same argument shows that
\lambda^{2}-\lambda+b|e|^{2}\geq 0
, which forces
1-4b|e|^{2}\geq 0
and forces
\lambda
to be in the indicated range.
∎

Next there are given some results showing that nonnegative sectional nonassociativity precludes the existence of square-zero elements.

Lemma 8.4
.

If a nontrivial metrized algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
satisfies
\mathbb{A}\circ\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{A}
then its multiplication is faithful. In particular, the multiplication of a metrized semisimple commutative algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
is faithful.

Proof.

If
z\in\ker L_{\circ}
, then, by the invariance of
h
,
0=h(L_{\circ}(z)x,y)=h(z,x\circ y)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{A}
. This shows
\ker L
is contained in the orthocomplement
(\mathbb{A}\circ\mathbb{A})^{\perp}
of
\mathbb{A}\circ\mathbb{A}
. In particular, if
\mathbb{A}\circ\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{A}
, then
\ker L_{\circ}\subset(\mathbb{A}\circ\mathbb{A})^{\perp}=\mathbb{A}^{\perp}=\{0\}
, so
L_{\circ}
is injective.
If
\mathbb{A}=\oplus_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{A}_{i}
is a direct sum of simple ideals, then
\mathbb{A}_{i}\circ\mathbb{A}_{j}=\{0\}
if
i\neq j
, and, since
\mathbb{A}_{i}
is simple,
\mathbb{A}_{i}\circ\mathbb{A}_{i}=\mathbb{A}_{i}
, so
\mathbb{A}\circ\mathbb{A}=\sum_{i}\mathbb{A}_{i}\circ\mathbb{A}_{i}=\sum_{i}\mathbb{A}_{i}=\mathbb{A}
, and
L_{\circ}
is injective. By the invariance of
h
,
h(R_{\circ}(y)x,z)=h(x,L_{\circ}(y)z)
for all
x,y,z\in\mathbb{A}
, so were
R_{\circ}
not injective,
L_{\circ}
would not be injective.
∎

Lemma 8.5
.

Let
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
be a Euclidean metrized commutative algebra. Let
0\neq z\in\mathbb{Nil}_{2}(\mathbb{A},\circ)
.
For
0\neq y\in\mathbb{A}
not in the span of
z
,
\mathscr{K}(z,y)\leq 0
, with equality if and only if
z\circ y=0
. In particular,
\mathbb{Nil}_{2}(\mathbb{A},\circ)=\{0\}
if there holds either of the following conditions:

(1)

(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
has positive sectional nonassociativity.

(2)

(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
has nonnegative sectional nonassociativity and is semisimple.

Proof.

Let
0\neq z\in\mathbb{Nil}_{2}(\mathbb{A},\circ)
. For
y\in\mathbb{A}
linearly independent of
z
, there holds
\mathscr{K}(z,y)|z\wedge y|^{2}=-|L_{\circ}(z)y|^{2}\leq 0
, with equality if and only if
z\circ y=0
. In particular,
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
cannot have positive sectional nonassociativity, and if
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
has nonnegative sectional nonassociativity, then
L_{\circ}(z)y=0
for all
y\in\mathbb{A}
, so
L_{\circ}(z)=0
.
By Lemma
8.4
, if
L_{\circ}(z)=0
, then
(\mathbb{A},\circ)
is not semisimple.
∎

A commutative algebra is
exact
if
\operatorname{tr}L_{\circ}(x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{A}
. Exactness is a condition analogous to unimodularity of a Lie algebra. Evidently, an exact commutative algebra is not unital.

Lemma 8.6
.

A Euclidean metrized commutative algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
with nontrivial multiplication and nonnegative sectional nonassociativity is not exact.

Proof.

By
[
62
, Lemma
2.3
]
, there exists a nontrivial idempotent
e\in\mathbb{Idem}(\mathbb{A},\circ)
. Because
L_{\circ}(e)
is self-adjoint, there is an
h
-orthogonal basis
\{e,z_{1},\dots,z_{n-1}\}
of
\mathbb{A}
such that
z_{1},\dots,z_{n-1}
are eigenvectors of
L_{\circ}(e)
with (not necessarily distinct) eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n-1}\in\mathbb{R}
. By (
2
) of Lemma
8.3
, nonnegativity of the sectional nonassociativity implies
\lambda_{i}\in[0,1]
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
. It follows that
\operatorname{tr}L_{\circ}(e)=1+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{i}\geq 1
, so
(\mathbb{A},\circ)
is not exact.
∎

In a commutative
\mathbb{R}
-algebra
(\mathbb{A},\circ)
the set
\mathbb{Q}(\mathbb{A},\circ)=\{x\circ x:x\in\mathbb{A}\}
is a closed cone, the
cone of squares
. By definition,
\mathbb{Idem}(\mathbb{A},\circ)\subset\mathbb{Q}(\mathbb{A},\circ)
.

Lemma 8.7
.

Let
(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)
be a Euclidean metrized commutative algebra having nonnegative sectional nonassociativity. The set

(8.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)=\{x\in\mathbb{A}:L_{\circ}(x)\,\,\text{is nonnegative}\}

is a closed convex cone containing
\mathbb{Q}(\mathbb{A},\circ)
for all
x\in\mathbb{A}
and consequently containing
\mathbb{Idem}(\mathbb{A},\circ)
.

Proof.

That the sectional nonassociativity is nonnegative means that
L_{\circ}(x\circ x)\geq L_{\circ}(x)^{2}\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{A}
. This shows
\mathbb{Idem}(\mathbb{A},\circ)\subset\mathbb{Q}(\mathbb{A},\circ,h)\subset\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{A},\circ,g)
. In a Euclidean metrized commutative algebra every multiplication endomorphism
L_{\circ}(x)
is self-adjoint, so diagonalizable with real eigenvalues. If
x,y\in\mathbb{A}
and
t\in[0,1]
then
L_{\circ}(tx+(1-t)y)=tL_{\circ}(x)+(1-t)L_{\circ}(y)\geq 0
because the set of nonnegative definite self-adjoint endomorphisms of a Euclidean vector space is a closed convex cone.
∎

With the hypotheses of Lemma
8.7
, are the extreme rays of
\mathbb{Q}(\mathbb{A},\circ)
generated by elements of
\mathbb{Idem}(\mathbb{A},\circ)
? This is true for the cones of squares in the simple Euclidean Jordan algebras over
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
; these are the cones of nonnegative definite Hermitian matrices and their extremal rays are generated by the rank one idempotents. For the Griess algebras of OZ VOAs (Example
19
) it seems the question has not been studied.
