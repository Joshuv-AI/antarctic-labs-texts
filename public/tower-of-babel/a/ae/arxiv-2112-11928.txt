A Stochastic Bregman Primal-Dual Splitting Algorithm for Composite Optimization
Antonio Silveti-Falls, Cesare Molinari, Jalal Fadili

Abstract
We study a stochastic first order primal-dual method for solving convex-concave saddle point problems over real reflexive Banach spaces using Bregman divergences and relative smoothness assumptions, in which we allow for stochastic error in the computation of gradient terms within the algorithm. We show ergodic convergence in expectation of the Lagrangian optimality gap with a rate of O ⁡ ( 1 / k ) O\left(1/k\right) and that every almost sure weak cluster point of the ergodic sequence is a saddle point in expectation under mild assumptions. Under slightly stricter assumptions, we show almost sure weak convergence of the pointwise iterates to a saddle point. Under a relative strong convexity assumption on the objective functions and a total convexity assumption on the entropies of the Bregman divergences, we establish almost sure strong convergence of the pointwise iterates to a saddle point. Our framework is general and does not need strong convexity of the entropies inducing the Bregman divergences in the algorithm. Numerical applications are considered including entropically regularized Wasserstein barycenter problems and regularized inverse problems on the simplex.



Key words.
Bregman divergence; primal-dual splitting; noneuclidean splitting; saddle point problems; first order algorithms; convergence rates; relative smoothness; total convexity; Banach space.

Definition 2.4

Lemma 2.8

If

Total convexity is a sort of generalization of strict convexity to functions defined on Banach spaces. Indeed, for finite-dimensional spaces, strict convexity and total convexity are equivalent for functions with full domain

The expressions of

\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{d}

-\left\langle T\left(x-x^{\prime}\right),\mu-\mu^{\prime}\right\rangle.

Using Lemma

By Young’s inequality, for any

\displaystyle-\left\langle T\left(x-x^{\prime}\right),\mu-\mu^{\prime}\right\rangle\geq-\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\|T\left(x-x^{\prime}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\left\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\right\|_{2}^{2}

Combining the two, we find, for any

\begin{split}Bx=\begin{pmatrix}x_{2}-x_{1}\\

5.2
Variational problems with the entropic Wasserstein distance

Consider the optimal transport problem between two discrete measures,
\rho
and
\theta
, defined on two metric spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
. Let
C\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
be the ground cost on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. The cost
C
is typically application-dependent, and reflects some prior knowledge on the data to be processed. We regularize the optimal transport problem by subtracting in the objective the entropy of the transport plan
\pi
,

\begin{split}E\left(\pi\right)=-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{i,j}\log\left(\pi_{i,j}\right).\end{split}

The idea of regularizing the optimal transport problem by including the entropy of the transport plan
\pi
is not new. It was popularized by
[
21
]
and then explored, for example, in
[
22
]
for computing entropic Wasserstein barycenters, in
[
41
]
for approximating entropic Wasserstein gradient flows, in
[
23
]
for variational Wasserstein problems, in
[
24
]
, etc. For
\gamma>0
, the entropic regularization of the Kantorovich formulation of optimal transport can be written as the convex optimization problem

W_{\gamma}\left(\rho,\theta\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\inf_{\pi\in\Pi\left(\rho,\theta\right)}\left\{\left\langle C,\pi\right\rangle+\gamma\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{i,j}\log\left(\pi_{i,j}\right)=\gamma\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{i,j}\log\left(\frac{\pi_{i,j}}{\xi_{i,j}}\right)\right\},

(5.2)

where
\Pi\left(\rho,\theta\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\left\{\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}_{+}:\pi\mathds{1}=\rho,\pi^{T}\mathds{1}=\theta\right\}
is the so-called transportation polytope and
\xi_{i,j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\exp(\frac{-C_{i,j}}{\gamma})
is the Gibbs Kernel. When
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}
,
\gamma=0
and
C=d^{p}
, where
d
is a distance on
\mathcal{X}
, then
W_{0}^{1/p}
is the well-known
p
-Wasserstein distance.

We consider solving the following variational problem over discrete measures, i.e., vectors in the simplex
\Sigma^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\left\{x:x\geq 0,x^{T}\mathds{1}=1\right\}
,

\min_{\rho\in\Sigma^{n}}W_{\gamma}(F\rho,\theta)+J\circ B(\rho),

(5.3)

where
J\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
,
F:\Sigma^{n}\to\Sigma^{m}
and
B:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
are both linear operators. Seen as a matrix,
F
is typically column-stochastic while
\rho\in\Sigma^{n}
is a discrete measure over the metric space
\mathcal{X}
and
\theta\in\Sigma^{m}
is the fixed observed discrete measure over the metric space
\mathcal{Y}
.

Problem (
5.3
) is a natural way to solve inverse problems on discrete measures where one assumes that

\begin{split}\theta\approx F\rho_{0},\end{split}

where
\rho_{0}
is an unknown discrete measure over
\mathcal{Y}
to recover from the observed
\theta
. When
F=\mathrm{Id}
and
\gamma=0
, (
5.3
) is closely related to computing the Wasserstein gradient flow (aka JKO flow
[
31
]
) of
J\circ B
. The JKO flow was first studied in
[
31
]
as it relates to the Fokker-Planck equation before being generalized (cf.
[
2
]
,
[
50
]
). Entropic regularization, i.e., with
\gamma>0
, was studied in
[
41
]
to compute Wasserstein gradient flows over spaces of probability distributions with the topology induced by the Wasserstein metric.

Applying Fenchel-Rockafellar duality to (
5.2
) (see
[
42
, Proposition 2.4]
for the unregularized case and
[
22
, Section 5.1]
for the entropic case), it is straightforward to see that problem (
5.3
) reads also

\begin{split}\min_{\rho\in\Sigma^{n}}\sup_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\langle\tau,F\rho\right\rangle+\left\langle\eta,\theta\right\rangle-\gamma\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\exp\left({\frac{\tau_{i}+\eta_{j}-C_{i,j}}{\gamma}}\right)+J\circ B(\rho).\end{split}

(5.4)

Taking the supremum over
\eta
, one can easily show that (see also
[
27
, Proposition 2.1]
),

\begin{split}\min_{\rho\in\Sigma^{n}}\sup_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\langle\tau,F\rho\right\rangle-\gamma\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta_{j}\log\left({\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\exp\left({\frac{\tau_{i}-C_{i,j}}{\gamma}}\right)}\right)+J\circ B(\rho).\end{split}

(5.5)

Remark 5.5
.

Observe in
(
5.5
)
that the smooth term in
\tau
(excluding the inner product
\left\langle\tau,F\rho\right\rangle
) is actually a log-sum-exp smooth approximation of the
\max
function, which would appear naturally when marginalizing with respect to
\eta
in the case
\gamma=0
.

Now, dualizing on
J
, we finally get that (
5.3
) is equivalent to

\min_{\rho\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}}\sup_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\iota_{\left\{1\right\}}(\rho^{T}\mathds{1})+\left\langle\left(\tau,\zeta\right),\left(F\rho,B\rho\right)\right\rangle-\gamma\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta_{j}\log\left({\sum_{i=1}^{m}\exp\left({\frac{\tau_{i}-C_{i,j}}{\gamma}}\right)}\right)-J^{*}(\zeta).

(5.6)

The problem in (
5.6
) is a saddle point problem which can be solved with Algorithm
1
by taking

\begin{split}\mathcal{C}_{p}=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n},\quad\mathcal{C}_{d}=\mathbb{R}^{m+p},\quad T\rho=\left(F\rho,B\rho\right),\quad f\left(\rho\right)=0,\quad g\left(\rho\right)=\iota_{\left\{1\right\}}\left(\rho^{T}\mathds{1}\right),\\

The natural choice for the entropies is, again,

\begin{split}\phi_{p}\left(x\right)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\log\left(x_{i}\right)\enskip\text{and}\enskip\phi_{d}\left(\mu\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

Lemma 5.6
.

The function
h^{*}\left(\mu\right)
is
L_{d}
Lipschitz-smooth for
L_{d}\geq\gamma^{-1}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\theta_{j}=\gamma^{-1}

The log-sum-exp function (with temperature constant
\gamma
),

\begin{split}\mathrm{LSE}_{\gamma}\left(x\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\gamma\log\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(\frac{x_{i}}{\gamma}\right)\right),\end{split}

is

and convex on

(see
[

) and thus so is

. The gradient,
\nabla\mathrm{LSE}_{\gamma}\left(x\right)
, is given, component-wise, for each

by

\begin{split}\left(\sigma_{\gamma}\left(x\right)\right)^{\left(k\right)}=\frac{\exp\left(x_{k}/\gamma\right)}{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(x_{i}/\gamma\right)}.\end{split}

The function

is called the softmax function with temperature constant

and is Lipschitz-continuous in the euclidean norm with Lipschitz constant

(see

, Proposition 4]
). Thus, to see that the function

is Lipschitz-smooth, denote the

and notice

\begin{split}h^{*}\left(\mu\right)=h^{*}\left(\tau\right)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\theta_{j}\mathrm{LSE}_{\gamma}\left(\tau-C_{\cdot,j}\right)\implies\nabla h^{*}\left(\mu\right)=\nabla h^{*}\left(\tau\right)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\theta_{j}\sigma_{\gamma}\left(\tau-C_{\cdot,j}\right).\end{split}

With this we write,

\begin{split}\left\|\nabla h^{*}\left(\mu\right)-\nabla h^{*}\left(\mu^{\prime}\right)\right\|_{2}&=\left\|\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\theta_{j}\left(\sigma_{\gamma}\left(\tau-C_{\cdot,j}\right)-\sigma_{\gamma}\left(\tau^{\prime}-C_{\cdot,j}\right)\right)\right\|_{2}\\
&\leq\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\theta_{j}\right)\left\|\sigma_{\gamma}\left(\tau-C_{\cdot,j}\right)-\sigma_{\gamma}\left(\tau^{\prime}-C_{\cdot,j}\right)\right\|_{2}\\
&\leq\gamma^{-1}\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\theta_{j}\right)\left\|\tau-\tau^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\end{split}

and the desired claim follows.

Lemma A.1
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, for a fixed batch size
0<q<m
, let
B_{k}\subset\left\{1,\ldots,m\right\}
be the batch of
q
indices sampled at iteration
k
and define
B_{k}^{c}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\left\{1,\ldots,m\right\}\setminus B_{k}
. Consider the error term induced by the batch sampling:

\begin{split}\delta^{p}_{k}=-\nabla\left(\sum\limits_{i\in B_{k}^{c}}\left(Ax\right)_{i}\log\left(\frac{\left(Ax\right)_{i}}{b_{i}}\right)\right).\end{split}

If the entries of
A
are positive then
\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\delta^{p}_{k},x-x_{k+1}\right\rangle\right]\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded for all
x\in\Sigma^{n}
.

By Cauchy-Schwarz we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\delta^{p}_{k},x-x_{k+1}\right\rangle\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\delta^{p}_{k}\right\|\left\|x-x_{k+1}\right\|\right]\leq\mathrm{diam}_{\Sigma^{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\delta^{p}_{k}\right\|\right]\end{split}

and so it suffices to bound
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\delta^{p}_{k}\right\|\right]
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Rather than bound the expectation itself, we will provide a coarse bound which holds deterministically for an arbitrary batch. For any batch
B\subset\left\{1,\ldots,m\right\}
of size
0<q<m
, define
\tilde{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{(m-q)\times n}_{++}
to be the matrix composed of rows which were not sampled in the batch
B
and similarly for
\tilde{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m-q}_{++}
,

\begin{split}\tilde{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\begin{bmatrix}A_{i,\cdot}\end{bmatrix}_{i\in B^{c}}\quad\quad\mbox{and}\quad\quad\tilde{b}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\left(b_{i}\right)_{i\in B^{c}}.\end{split}

Using component wise
\log
and division we have, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\begin{split}\left\|\nabla\left(\sum\limits_{i\in B^{c}}\left(Ax_{k}\right)_{i}\log\left(\frac{\left(Ax_{k}\right)_{i}}{b_{i}}\right)\right)\right\|=\left\|\tilde{A}^{T}\log\left(\frac{\tilde{A}x_{k}}{\tilde{b}}\right)\right\|&\leq\left\|\tilde{A}\right\|\left\|\log\left(\frac{\tilde{A}x_{k}}{\tilde{b}}\right)\right\|\\
&\leq\left\|\tilde{A}\right\|\left(\left\|\log\left(\tilde{A}x_{k}\right)\right\|+\left\|\log\left(\tilde{b}\right)\right\|\right)\end{split}

As
A
and
b
are fixed from the problem data,
\left\|\tilde{A}\right\|
and
\left\|\log\left(\tilde{b}\right)\right\|
are bounded. All that remains is to bound
\left\|\log\left(\tilde{A}x_{k}\right)\right\|
, for which we first recall that
x_{k}\in\Sigma^{n}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
by design of the algorithm and the choice of
\phi_{p}
. Let
\underline{a}=\min\limits_{i,j}A_{i,j}>0
and
\overline{a}=\max\limits_{i,j}A_{i,j}
, then for each
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,m-q\right\}

\begin{split}\log\left(\left\langle\tilde{A}_{i,\cdot},x_{k}\right\rangle\right)\leq\log\left(\left\|\tilde{A}_{i,\cdot}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|x_{k}\right\|_{1}\right)=\log\left(\left\|\tilde{A}_{i,\cdot}\right\|_{\infty}\right)\leq\log\left(\overline{a}\right)\end{split}

as well as

\begin{split}\log\left(\left\langle\tilde{A}_{i,\cdot},x_{k}\right\rangle\right)\geq\log\left(\min\limits_{j}\tilde{A}_{i,j}\right)\geq\log\left(\underline{a}\right)\end{split}

so that the components of
\log\left(\tilde{A}x\right)
are contained in a ball of radius
\max\left\{\left|\log\left(\underline{a}\right)\right|,\left|\log\left(\overline{a}\right)\right|\right\}
, which is finite since the entries of
A
are positive and
A
is fixed. Thus
\left\|\log\left(\tilde{A}x\right)\right\|
is bounded and the proof is complete.
∎
