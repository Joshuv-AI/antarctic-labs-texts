Unification of topological invariants in Dirac models
Gero von Gersdorff, Shahram Panahiyan, Wei Chen

Abstract
Topological phases of materials are characterized by topological invariants that are conventionally calculated by different means according to the dimension and symmetry class of the system. For topological materials described by Dirac models, we introduce a wrapping number as a unified approach to obtain the topological invariants in arbitrary dimensions and symmetry classes. Given a unit vector that parametrizes the momentum-dependence of the Dirac model, the wrapping number describes the degree of the map from the Brillouin zone torus to the sphere formed by the unit vector that we call Dirac sphere. This method is gauge-invariant and originates from the intrinsic features of the Dirac model, and moreover places all known topological invariants, such as Chern number, winding number, Pfaffian, etc, on equal footing.



I
Introduction

The topological order in topological insulators (TIs) and superconductors (TSCs) is generally defined through the Bloch states according to the dimension and symmetry class of the system
Schnyder et al. 2008
;
Ryu et al. 2010
;
Kitaev 2009
;
Chiu et al. 2016
. Classification of these materials according to the time-reversal (TR), particle-hole (PH), and chiral symmetries renders a table of 10 symmetry classes in which the structure of the topological invariants repeats every 8 dimensions, often referred to as the periodic table of topological invariants. In a given symmetry class, the topological invariant (or charge) characterizes equivalence classes of different Hamiltonians that possess the same PH and TR symmetries. Hamiltonians with the same charge are equivalent under certain continuous deformations
Kitaev 2009
while those of unequal charges are not.
A commonly employed quantity to calculate these invariants is the gauge connection

\mathcal{A}_{\mu}^{ab}({\bf k})\equiv\braket{\psi^{a}_{-}({\bf k})|\partial_{\mu}|\psi^{b}_{-}({\bf k})}

(1)

where the states run over all the filled-band Bloch eigenstates, and
{\bf k}
is the momentum within the first Brillouin Zone (BZ). A unitary (
{\bf k}
-dependent) change of basis within the filled band eingenstates, generates a gauge transformation for the system.
The invariants for all known symmetry classes can then be formulated in terms of certain gauge-invariant functionals of
\mathcal{A}_{\mu}
(usually integrals over the BZ), such as Chern-number, winding-number, Chern-Simons number, etc.
Chiu et al. 2016
At times the functionals are defined only in certain classes of gauges governed by rather complicated constraints.
In general, the gauge dependence of the charges is not always manifest, and the additional constraints make some of them hard to compute in practice. A very useful overview of all the topological invariants in the gauge formalism can be found in Ref.
Chiu et al. 2016
.

The periodic table of topological invariants has the following structure. Of the ten symmetry classes, eight of them are the ”real” symmetry classes, which possess TR and/or PH symmetry, and the other two are the ”complex” ones, which do not.
In particular, in each dimension
D
there exists one
nontrivial class among the eight real symmetry classes, which is different for each
0\leq D\leq 7
and repeats periodically thereafter. This so-called ”primary series” is characterized by an integer topological invariant, which, according to whether
D
is even or odd, can be given in terms of either the Chern or winding number.
Another similar series, the so-called ”even series” is characterized by an even integer invariant.
The nontrivial complex classes have only periodicity two, they have also integer charges and we will refer to them as the complex series.
Starting from the primary series, one can obtain the remaining nontrivial real symmetry classes by considering the same Hamiltonians, but in one and two dimensions lower respectively.
Qi et al. 2008
;
Ryu et al. 2010
These are known as first and second descendants, and they are characterized by a binary invariant
\pm 1
. The binary charges can be computed in a more or less complicated construction that makes reference to the invariants of the parent (primary) class.

The goal of this paper is to provide a simple, practical, and unified characterization of the topological invariants in all nontrivial classes in any
D
that is manifestly gauge invariant.
We will focus our attention on the Dirac models that describe most of the topological materials, and in the context of the K-theory approach
Kitaev 2009
have been argued to provide a sufficiently general class of models. Our formalism is based on the fact that Dirac Hamiltonians in
D
dimensions can be considered as maps from the
D-
dimensional BZ torus
T^{D}
to a
D-
dimensional sphere
S^{D}
formed by the unit vector that parametrizes the Dirac Hamiltonian. For such maps, one can define the degree of the map which counts the number of times the BZ covers this sphere. We will call the degree of map the
wrapping number
. It can either be given as an integral or in terms of a rather simple algebraic formula, whose equivalence was shown by Kronecker over a century ago
Kronecker 1869
.
We will show that in the primary and complex series, the topological charge is always given by the wrapping number (for any
D
, even or odd), in the even series it is given by twice the wrapping number, and in the case of the descendants it is given by
-1
to the power of the wrapping number.
Moreover, using the algebraic formula, we will prove an extremely simple non-integral version of the binary charges for the case of Dirac Hamiltonians that also makes close connection to the known formulation of binary invariants in terms of Pfaffians.

II.2
The wrapping number

We will now define the central quantity of our paper that in differential geometry is usually called the degree of the map
{{\bf n}}({\bf k})

\displaystyle\deg[{{\bf n}}]

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V_{D}}\int_{BZ}\frac{1}{D!}\epsilon_{i_{0}\cdots i_{D}}n^{i_{0}}dn^{i_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge dn^{i_{D}},

(6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V_{D}}\int\epsilon_{i_{0}\cdots i_{D}}n^{i_{0}}\partial_{1}n^{i_{1}}\dots\partial_{D}n^{i_{D}}\,d^{D}k\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V_{D}}\int\epsilon_{i_{0}\cdots i_{D}}\frac{1}{|{\bf r}|^{D+1}}r^{i_{0}}\partial_{1}r^{i_{1}}\dots\partial_{D}r^{i_{D}}\,d^{D}k\,,\;\;\;\;

where
V_{D}
is the volume of the
D
dimensional unit sphere,

V_{D}=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{D+1}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{D+1}{2})}.

(7)

The quantity
\deg[{\bf n}]
is always an integer and counts how many times the domain manifold
T^{D}
wraps around the image manifold
S^{D}
under the map
{{\bf n}}(k)
. We will refer to
\deg[{\bf n}]
as the wrapping number.
Eq. (
6
) is a special case of a more general definition of the degree of a map between two arbitrary manifolds of the same dimension, which we review in App.
B
.
As we will see, in any dimension, all known topological invariants such as Chern numbers, winding numbers, Chern-Simons-invariants, Pfaffians, etc. can be expressed in terms of
\deg[{\bf n}]
. In Fig.
1
, a 1D and a 2D schematic example of wrapping number
\deg[{\bf n}]=+1
are given.

The degree of the map can be expressed in an alternative way which turns out to be extremely useful for the practical computation of topological invariants.
The method goes as follows: pick any point on the sphere
{\bf n}_{0}
, and find all points
{\bf k}_{i}
in the domain that map to this point,
{{\bf n}}({\bf k}_{i})={\bf n}_{0}
. If the point is chosen such that the ”Jacobians” at points
{\bf k}_{i}

J_{{\bf n}}({\bf k}_{i})\equiv\left.\epsilon_{i_{0}\cdots i_{D}}n^{i_{0}}\frac{\partial n^{i_{1}}}{\partial k^{1}}\cdots\frac{\partial n^{i_{D}}}{\partial k^{D}}\right|_{{\bf k}_{i}},

(8)

are all nonvanishing, then the set of these points is discrete and finite, and

\deg[{{\bf n}}]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf k}\ {\rm with}\\
{\bf n}({\bf k})={\bf n}_{0}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{sign}J_{{\bf n}}({\bf k}),

(9)

which is manifestly an integer. The equivalence of Eq. (
6
) and Eq. (
9
) was proven in a slightly different form by Kronecker
Kronecker 1869
and later generalized by Brouwer
Brouwer 1912
. Finally, since
J_{{\bf r}}=|{\bf r}|^{D+1}J_{{\bf n}}
according to Eq. (
6
), one can also compute
\operatorname{sign}J_{{\bf n}}=\operatorname{sign}J_{{\bf r}}
from the cyclic derivative of the
{\bf r}
vector, which is often much simpler.

In subsections
II.3
to
II.5
, we will show that the nontrivial Altland-Zirnbauer (AZ) classes
Altland and Zirnbauer 1997
represented by Dirac Hamiltonains can be classified according to the wrapping number, as summarized in Table
1
. It should also be emphasized that we do not set out to rederive the periodic table of topological materials from scratch, but rather construct our formalism based the existing knowledge, in particular Ref.
Ryu et al. 2010
and
Chiu et al. 2016
, and only focus on those classes and dimensions that are topologically nontrivial. We furthermore stress a very important property of the wrapping number, namely it is manifestly gauge-invariant, where the gauge means the
U(N/2)
freedom of choosing a basis for the filled-band eigenstates.
In fact, the wrapping number can be viewed as an intrinsic property of the Hamiltonian.

We also remark that the wrapping number is in accordance with a unified description of topological phase transitions (TPTs) based on the assumption that the topological invariants are generally momentum space integration of a certain curvature function, whose critical behavior gives rises to the statistical aspects of critical exponents, scaling laws, correlation functions
Chen et al. 2017
;
Chen and Sigrist 2019
;
Molignini et al. 2018
;
Molignini et al. 2020
;
Rufo et al. 2019
;
Abdulla et al. 2020
;
Kumar et al. 2020
, universality classes
Chen and Schnyder 2019
, fidelity susceptibilty
Panahiyan et al. 2020
;
Molignini et al.
, and renormalization group approach
Chen 2016
;
Chen et al. 2016
. Within this wrapping number formalism, the curvature function is simply the cyclic derivative of the
{\bf n}
-vector in Eq. (
6
). Precisely how these statistical aspects manifest within the context of the wrapping number is a fundamental issue that should be addressed elsewhere.

II.3
Winding number in terms of wrapping number

In odd dimensions, the systems
in the primary, complex and even series have chiral symmetry, and
the topological invariant can be defined by the so-called winding number
Chiu et al. 2016

\nu_{D}=\frac{(\frac{D-1}{2})!}{2D!}\left(\frac{i}{2\pi}\right)^{\frac{D+1}{2}}\int\operatorname{tr}S(QdQ)^{D},

(13)

where
S
is the generator of chiral symmetry (taken to be Hermitian) that satisfies
SH=-HS
,
Q
is the spectrally flattened Hamiltonian
Q={{\bf n}}\cdot\Gamma
in Eq. (
5
), and the power is to be understood as wedge products such that we obtain a
D
-form to integrate over.
1
1

1

In a basis where
S=\Gamma_{2n+1}
the Hamiltonian is off block diagonal and the winding number can be written in terms of the off-diagonal block matrices of dimension
N/2
.

Using the definition of the volume of the
D
-dimensional sphere Eq. (
7
) as well as
(QdQ)^{2}=-(dQ)^{2}
, this can be equivalently rewritten as

\nu_{D}=-\frac{1}{V_{D}D!}\left(-\frac{i}{2}\right)^{\frac{D+1}{2}}\int\operatorname{tr}[SQ(dQ)^{D}].

(14)

It is not hard to see that this is, in fact, proportional to the wrapping number in the cases of the primary, even, and complex series:

\nu_{D}=2^{-\frac{D+1}{2}}\operatorname{tr}\left[{\mathbbm{1}}\right]\,\deg[{{\bf n}}].

(15)

The proof of this formula uses the fact that

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left[SQ(dQ)^{D}\right]

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left[S\Gamma_{i_{0}}\cdots\Gamma_{i_{D}}\right]n^{i_{0}}dn^{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge dn^{i_{D}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left[S\Gamma_{0}\cdots\Gamma_{D}\right]\epsilon_{i_{0}\dots i_{D}}n^{i_{0}}dn^{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge dn^{i_{D}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i^{\frac{D+1}{2}}\operatorname{tr}[{\mathbbm{1}}]\epsilon_{i_{0}\dots i_{D}}n^{i_{0}}dn^{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge dn^{i_{D}},

where in the last line we used Eq. (
61
).
Using the value
n=\frac{D+1}{2}
for the primary and complex series one finds exactly

\nu_{D}=\deg[{{\bf n}}].

(17)

Notable examples for this case include 1D class BDI and AIII, in which
\nu_{D}
describes the quantized charge polarization of Wannier states
King-Smith and Vanderbilt 1993
;
Resta 1994
, the 3D winding numbers for class AIII
Schnyder et al. 2008
, and the 3D class DIII realized in TR-invariant TSCs
Fu and Berg 2010
. On the other hand, the value
n=\frac{D+3}{2}
for the even series yields

\nu_{D}=2\deg[{{\bf n}}],

(18)

and hence the winding number is twice the wrapping number. This situation corresponds to 1D class CII that is relevant to several theoretical models
Zhao and Wang 2014
;
Malard et al. 2020
, as well as 3D class CI.

II.4
Chern number in terms of wrapping number

In even dimensions, the systems in the primary, complex and even series lack chiral symmetry, and the winding number is not defined. Instead, the topological invariant for these systems can be defined by the Chern number
Chiu et al. 2016

{\rm Ch}_{\frac{D}{2}}=\frac{1}{V_{D}D!}\left(-\frac{i}{2}\right)^{\frac{D}{2}}\int\operatorname{tr}Q(dQ)^{D}.

(19)

Similar to the winding number, it is straightforward to express this as the wrapping number.
The calculation is very similar to the winding number. One has:

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left[Q(dQ)^{D}\right]

(20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left[\Gamma_{i_{0}}\cdots\Gamma_{i_{D}}\right]n^{i_{0}}dn^{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge dn^{i_{D}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left[\Gamma_{0}\cdots\Gamma_{D}\right]\epsilon_{i_{0}\dots i_{D}}n^{i_{0}}dn^{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge dn^{i_{D}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i^{\frac{D}{2}}\operatorname{tr}[{\mathbbm{1}}]\epsilon_{i_{0}\dots i_{D}}n^{i_{0}}dn^{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge dn^{i_{D}},

where Eq. (
60
) was used.
The result, again valid for the primary, complex and even series, reads

{\rm Ch}_{\frac{D}{2}}=2^{-\frac{D}{2}}\operatorname{tr}[{\mathbbm{1}}]\deg[{\bf n}],

(21)

In the primary and complex series, one has
n=\frac{D}{2}
and hence

{\rm Ch}_{\frac{D}{2}}=\deg[{\bf n}].

(22)

This situation includes 2D class A that is relevant to a variety of important physical phenomena such as quantum Hall effect (QHE)
Thouless et al. 1982
;
Niu et al. 1985
, quantum anomalous Hall effect (QAHE)
Haldane 1988
;
Liu et al. 2016
, Berry phase in TR-breaking systems
Xiao et al. 2005
;
Xiao et al. 2010
, and the orbital magnetization
Thonhauser et al. 2005
;
Ceresoli et al. 2006
;
Shi et al. 2007
;
Souza and Vanderbilt 2008
. Besides, this situation also covers the 2D class D realized by chiral
p
-wave superconductors
Read and Green 2000
. For the even series, one has
n=\frac{D+2}{2}
and hence

{\rm Ch}_{\frac{D}{2}}=2\deg[{\bf n}]\,,

(23)

and the Chern number is twice the wrapping number, which is realized in 2D class C.

We now comment on the peculiar case of class C. Notice that here we have implemented this class (which belongs to the even series) with
n=2
.
However, in this class in 2D one can in fact also choose
n=1
, with the following assignments

\Gamma_{1,2}=\sigma_{1,2},\qquad\Gamma_{0}=\sigma_{3},\qquad C=\sigma_{2}\,.

(24)

The difference to the
n=2
Dirac model is that all the parities are now positive, i.e.
{\bf r}(-{\bf k})={\bf r}({\bf k})
.
For this model one has that
{\rm Ch}_{1}=\deg[{\bf n}]
which naively can be even or odd.
However, we can apply our expression Eq. (
9
) to show that the wrapping number itself must be even in this case: For any point
{\bf k}
with
{{\bf n}}({\bf k})={{\bf n}}_{0}
, we have that also
{{\bf n}}(-{\bf k})={{\bf n}}_{0}
, and (as a consequence of the evenness of
D
) the Jacobians at these points are also equal. The only points where this doubling does not occur are the high symmetry points (
{\bf\overline{k}}=-{\bf\overline{k}}
modulo the dual lattice). However, it is also easy to see that at these points the Jacobian vanishes, and thus we must choose
{{\bf n}}_{0}\neq{\bf n}({\bf\overline{k}})
in order for Eq. (
9
) to be well defined in this particular example. As just argued, at such
{{\bf n}}_{0}
the source points come in pairs with equal Jacobians, and hence the wrapping number is even.

II.5
Descendants

Starting from each of the symmetry classes in the primary series, one can define so-called first and second descendants in the same symmetry class which live in one and two dimensions lower
Qi et al. 2008
;
Ryu et al. 2010
, and whose invariants are binary, that is, an element of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
instead of

For the remainder of this section, we denote the dimension of the parent by

\displaystyle:

) and Eq. (
45
) together with Eq. (
27
).
Then the maps
{\bf n}({\bf k})
are of the type

T^{D-1}\to S^{D-1}
(
T^{D-2}\to S^{D-2}
) for first (second) descendants.
In this case, the degree of the map is well defined and we will show that the binary invariants can be expressed in terms of them simply as

P_{(1,2)}[{\bf n}]=(-1)^{\deg[{\bf n}]}.

(28)

We will also show (see Eq. (

) and Eq. (

)) that by means of Eq. (

) one can derive an explicit and very simple formula for these invariants,

P_{(1,2)}[{\bf n}]=\prod_{\bf\overline{k}}n^{0}({\bf\overline{k}}),

(29)

where
{\bf\overline{k}}
are the high symmetry points (HSPs) in momentum space that satisfies
{\bf\overline{k}}=-{\bf\overline{k}}
up to a reciprocal lattice vector. Notice that because of parities,
n^{i}({\bf\overline{k}})=0
for
i\neq 0
, and hence
n^{0}({\bf\overline{k}})=\pm 1
. The construction of these invariants in our context follows closely the analysis of Refs.
Qi et al. 2008
;
Ryu et al. 2010
, and we will show it in details in the following two subsections.

II.6
First descendants

The spectrally flattened Hamiltonians are characterized by a map given in Eq. (
25
) with the constraint Eq. (
27
).
Let us consider two such Hamiltonians in a given symmetry class, parametrized by
{\bf n}_{1}({\bf k})
and
{\bf n}_{2}({\bf k})
and define a continuous path
{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}({\bf k},t)
that connect these two,

{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}({\bf k},0)={{\bf n}}_{1}({\bf k}),\qquad{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}({\bf k},\pi)={{\bf n}}_{2}({\bf k}).

(30)

We would like to interprete this path as a Hamiltonian in the parent class, in other words, we would like to view
t
as an additional component of momentum.
To comply with the relation
{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}(-{\bf k},-t)=P{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}({\bf k},t)
we need to extend the path to negative
t
by

{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}({\bf k},t)=P{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}(-{\bf k},-t),\qquad{\rm for\ }-\pi<t\leq 0\,.

(31)

In this way, the newly defined
{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}
is a member of the parent class (the same symmetry class in
D
dimensions).
3
3

3

It is maybe worth pointing out that for fixed times
t\neq 0,\pi
the system does not belong to the
D-1
dimensional symmetry class, so the interpolation is not to be regarded as a homotopy within the space of
D-1
dimensional Hamiltonians of that class.

Since the newly defined
{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}
is now a map form
T^{D}\to S^{D}
, one can compute the wrapping number of the parent class,

\deg[{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}]

which is an integer.
In principle, this integer can depend on the chosen path, however as we will show below, by choosing a different path it can only change by an even integer. Let us then define

\deg_{2}[{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}]\equiv\deg[{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}]\mod 2.

(32)

We thus declare two Hamiltonians topologically equal if
\deg_{2}[{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}]=0
and topologically different if
\deg_{2}[{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}]=1
. This is the strategy employed in Refs.
Qi et al. 2008
;
Ryu et al. 2010
,
schematically,

\mathbb{Z}_{2}{\rm\ invariant}={\rm deg}[{\bf n}]({\rm interpolation})\mod 2

(33)

where
{\rm deg}[{\bf n}]
here stands for Chern number
Qi et al. 2008
or winding number
Ryu et al. 2010
that is proved to be equal to the degree of the map in Secs.
II.3
and
II.4
, leading us to consider directly Eq. (
32
).

It remains to be shown that
\deg_{2}[{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}]
does not depend on the chosen path. We do this by explicit computation using Eq. (
9
), which actually simplifies considerably when taken modulo 2, as the sign of the Jacobian is always equal to
+1\mod 2
.
Let us pick as a reference point for instance the point
{\bf n}_{0}=(-1,0,0,\dots)
, in other words, minus the unit vector corresponding to the positive parity component (let’s call this the ”south pole” of
S^{D}
, with
-{\bf n}_{0}
being the ”north pole”).
Because of
{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}({\bf k},t)=P{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}(-{\bf k},-t)
, if
(k,t)
maps to the south pole, so does
(-k,-t)
, therefore the source points come in pairs unless they correspond to the
D
dimensional HSPs. Again because of parities, the HSPs must map to either the south or north pole.
Then Eq. (
9
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\deg_{2}[{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}({\bf k},t)\\
{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}({\bf k},t)={\bf n}_{0}\end{subarray}}\hskip-14.22636pt1\mod 2

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf\overline{k}},\bar{t}}\frac{1-n^{0}_{1\to 2}({\bf\overline{k}},\bar{t})}{2}\mod 2

(34)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf\overline{k}}}\frac{1-n^{0}_{1}({\bf\overline{k}})}{2}+\sum_{{\bf\overline{k}}}\frac{1-n^{0}_{2}({\bf\overline{k}})}{2}\mod 2.

In the second line we have discarded the non-HSPs in the summation (coming in pairs they contribute
0\mod 2
), and the summand evaluates to one (zero) for HSPs that map to the south pole (north pole), such that the sum can run over all HSPs. The two terms in the last line correspond to
\bar{t}=0
and
\bar{t}=\pi
.
Since the only information that enters on the final expression is contained in
{{\bf n}}_{1}
and
{{\bf n}}_{2}
, we conclude that

\deg_{2}[{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}]
is indeed independent of the path chosen and it constitutes a
well-defined relative invariant of the systems
{{\bf n}}_{1}
and
{{\bf n}}_{2}
.
It is often more convenient to define the parity invariant

P_{(1)}[{{\bf n}}_{1},{{\bf n}}_{2}]\equiv(-1)^{\deg_{2}[{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}]},

(35)

that takes values
+1
for equivalent and
-1
for inequivalent Hamiltonians
4
4

4

The subindex
(1)
refers to first descendant.
and is obviously in one to one correspondence with
\deg_{2}
.

Having defined a relative binary invariant between the systems
{{\bf n}}_{1}
and
{{\bf n}}_{2}
, we can consider the trivial constant Hamiltonian defined by
{{\bf n}}_{\rm ref}({\bf k})=(1,0,0,\dots)
as a reference and define the absolute
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
invariant

P_{(1)}[{{\bf n}}]\equiv P_{(1)}[{{\bf n}}_{\rm ref},{{\bf n}}]=\prod_{\bf\overline{k}}n^{0}({\bf\overline{k}}).

(36)

where the last equality follows from Eq. (
34
) and Eq. (
35
).

We now limit ourselves to the special case with one of the odd components of
{{\bf n}}({\bf k})
identically zero (taken, without loss of generality
n^{D}
) such that we have exactly
D
nonzero components
and
{\bf n}({\bf k})
becomes a map

{\bf n}({\bf k}):T^{D-1}\to S^{D-1}.

(37)

In this case we can define the quantity
\deg_{2}[{{\bf n}}]
via Eq. (
6
) and compute

\deg_{2}[{{\bf n}}]=\sum_{{\bf\overline{k}}}\frac{1-n^{0}({\bf\overline{k}})}{2}.

(38)

Comparing with Eq. (

) we see that

\deg_{2}[{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}]=\deg_{2}[{{\bf n}}_{1}]+\deg_{2}[{{\bf n}}_{2}],

(39)

and hence two Hamiltonians are topologically equivalent if and only if

\deg_{2}[{{\bf n}}_{1}]=\deg_{2}[{{\bf n}}_{2}].

(40)

Then, in this restricted class of Hamiltonians, we have

P_{(1)}[{\bf n}]=(-1)^{\deg_{2}[{{\bf n}}]},

(41)

and the topological invariant can be interpreted as wrapping number mod 2 of the map Eq. (
37
). This first descendant case covers the Majorana number in 1D class D
Kitaev 2001
, 2D class DIII realized by TR-invariant TSCs
Qi et al. 2009
;
Wang et al. 2014

;

II.7
Second descendants

For second descendants in
D-2
dimensions, we proceed in a similar way. Again, we would like to find a relative invariant between two Hamiltonians defined by
{{\bf n}}_{1}({\bf k})
and
{{\bf n}}_{2}({\bf k})
. Notice that these are now maps from
T^{D-2}\to S^{D}
.
We define a path
{{\bf n}}({\bf k},s)
with

{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}({\bf k},0)={{\bf n}}_{1}({\bf k}),\qquad{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}({\bf k},\pi)={{\bf n}}_{2}({\bf k}),

(42)

in which
{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}({\bf k},s)=P{{\bf n}}_{1\to 2}(-{\bf k},-s)
. This defines a map
T^{D-1}\to S^{D}
, i.e. a
D-1
dimensional Hamiltonian in the same symmetry class which can be thought of as a first descendant.
As just shown, we can define an invariant
P_{(1)}[{\bf n}_{1\to 2}]
for this first descendant.
Provided that this number is independent of the interpolation chosen, we can define

P_{(2)}[{\bf n}_{1},{\bf n}_{2}]\equiv P_{(1)}[{\bf n}_{1\to 2}]\,,

(43)

which constitutes now a relative invariant between the two second descendant Hamiltonians.
To see that
P_{(1)}[{\bf n}_{1\to 2}]
indeed only depends on
{\bf n}_{1}
and
{\bf n}_{2}
and not on the interpolation, we consider a second interpolation

{\bf n}^{\prime}_{1\to 2}
and compute the relative invariant

P_{(1)}[{\bf n}_{1\to 2},{\bf n}^{\prime}_{1\to 2}]
. To this end, one defines an ”interpolation of interpolations”

{\bf n}_{12\to 12^{\prime}}({\bf k},s,t)

that satisfies

\displaystyle{\bf n}_{12\to 12^{\prime}}({\bf k},s,0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf n}_{1\to 2}({\bf k},s),

\displaystyle{\bf n}_{12\to 12^{\prime}}({\bf k},s,\pi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf n}^{\prime}_{1\to 2}({\bf k},s),

(44)

as well as
n_{12\to 12^{\prime}}({\bf k},s,t)=Pn_{12\to 12^{\prime}}(-{\bf k},-s,-t)
. For this interpolation we can easily compute
\deg_{2}
.
The computation is just an iteration of Eq. (
II.6
). Again by using Eq. (
9
) one finds that this is always an even number and hence the two interpolations are equivalent and Eq. (
43
) is well defined.

Choosing again the trivial Hamiltonian defined by
{{\bf n}}_{\rm ref}=(1,0,0,\dots)
as a reference, we can define

P_{(2)}[{{\bf n}}]\equiv P_{(2)}[{{\bf n}}_{\rm ref},{{\bf n}}]=\prod_{\bf\overline{k}}n^{0}({\bf\overline{k}}).

(45)

Finally, by considering Hamiltonians with
n^{D-1}=n^{D}=0
, we can identify the right hand side of Eq. (
45
) with the
D-2
dimensional wrapping number,

P_{(2)}[{{\bf n}}]=(-1)^{\deg[{\bf n}]}\,,

(46)

of the map
{\bf n}({\bf k}):T^{D-2}\to S^{D-2}
. Examples of this second descendant case include 1D class DIII, the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
invariant in the quantum spin Hall effect (QSHE) of 2D class AII
Kane and Mele 2005a
;
Kane and Mele 2005b
;
Bernevig et al. 2006
;
Bernevig and Zhang 2006
;
Fu and Kane 2007
;
Moore and Balents 2007
, and 3D class CII.

III
Conclusions

In this paper we have shown that topological invariants for Dirac Hamiltonians in any dimensions and symmetry classes can be extracted from the wrapping number introduced in Eq. (
6
), which describes the degree of the map from the
D
dimensional BZ
T^{D}
to the
D
dimensional Dirac sphere
S^{D}
form by the
{\bf n}({\bf k})
-vector that parametrizes the Dirac Hamiltonian. Within the context of differential geometry, the wrapping number is simply the number of times the domain
T^{D}
covers (or wraps around) the image
S^{D}
under this map. By explicitly constructing the Dirac Hamiltonian for each nontrivial symmetry class in any dimension
Ryu et al. 2010
, we have shown that all known invariants can be expressed in terms of the wrapping number, as summarized in table
1
.
Moreover, in the case of all binary invariants we have derived a rather simple universal formula that only depend on the values of the Hamiltonian at the HSP points of the BZ, as given by Eq. (
29
).

Some of the results in the present paper have been previously obtained in certain special cases. In particular, the concept of the degree of the map has been recognized in Ref.
Wang et al. 2010
in order to show the equivalence of certain topological invariants. Our work significantly advances this type of approach by combining it with symmetry classification, resulting in a remarkable unification of all known topological invariants, which gives all the physical phenomena related to topological order such as QHE, QAHE, QSHE, charge polarization, Majorana fermions, chiral or helical TSCs, etc, a geometrical interpretation in terms of the Dirac sphere. Moreover, the algebraic formula given by Eq. (
9
) greatly simplifies the practical computation of
all
topological invariants, either integer or binary. In the latter case, it also directly leads us to the very simple explicit expression Eq. (
29
). Finally, we remark that for the most general Hamiltonians of dimension
2^{n}
beyond the Dirac Hamiltonian in Eq. (
4
), one requires the Dirac matrices with
\leq n
antisymmetric indices
Bernevig and Hughes 2013
to expand the Hamiltonian
H=\sum_{i}r^{i}\Gamma_{i}+\sum_{ij}r^{ij}\Gamma_{ij}+\dots
. Whether the wrapping number in Eq. (
6
) can be modified to describe the topology is an intriguing issue that awaits to be clarified. In addition, the cyclic derivative of the
{\bf n}
-vector that enters the integrand of Eq. (
6
) can be written in terms of single-particle Green’s function for several symmetry classes in low dimensions
Yakovenko 1990
;
Hlousek et al. 1990
;
Gurarie 2011
;
Santos et al. 2011
;
Chen 2018
. Whether such expression holds for all the topologically nontrivial cases on the periodic table remains to be explored.

Dirac Hamiltonians for all nontrivial symmetry classes in general dimension

The Clifford algebra of

\{\Gamma_{i},\Gamma_{j}\}=2\delta_{ij}\,,

2n+1

(49)

The sign of
\omega

\omega^{2}
is basis independent.
An important quantity is the matrix

B\equiv\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{3}\cdots\Gamma_{2n+1},

(50)

Appendix B
The degree of the map onto spheres

In this appendix we provide some basic information on the degree of the map.
Consider two manifolds,
N
and
M
of the same dimension
D
, and a map
f:N\to M
. Let
M
be orientable with a volume form
\omega
that is nowhere vanishing, and define

V_{M}\equiv\int_{M}\omega\,.

(63)

The integer-valued degree of the map
f
is defined as

\deg f=\frac{1}{V_{M}}\int_{N}f^{*}\omega,

(64)

where
f^{*}\omega
is the pullback of
\omega
over the map
f
, which is a
D
-form on
N
.
The equivalent algebraic definition of the degree reads

\deg f=\sum_{k\in f^{-1}(x_{0})}\operatorname{sign}J(k),

(65)

where
J
is the Jacobian of the map
f
,

J_{f}(k)=\det\frac{\partial f^{\mu}}{\partial k^{\nu}},

(66)

and
x_{0}
is some conveniently chosen base point on
M
that is non-singular, i.e.,
J(k)\neq 0
for all
k\in f^{-1}(x_{0})
.

We now proceed to derive the relevant expressions for the case
M=S^{D}
which are used in the main text.
Firstly, when
M
is Riemannian with metric
g
, we may choose

\omega=\sqrt{\det g(x)}\,d^{D}x\,,

(67)

where
x^{\mu}
are local coordinates on
M
, and we defined the usual shorthand
d^{D}x\equiv dx^{1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{D}
.
In terms of local coordinates
k^{\mu}
on
N
,

f^{*}\omega=\sqrt{\det g(f(k))}J_{f}(k)\,d^{D}k.

(68)

When
M=S^{D}
, we can embed it in
\mathbb{R}^{D+1}
as a unit vector
{\bf m}(x)^{2}=1
, and define the canonical metric as

g_{\mu\nu}(x)\equiv\frac{\partial{\bf m}}{\partial x^{\mu}}\cdot\frac{\partial{\bf m}}{\partial x^{\nu}}
. The volume form can be expressed as

\omega=\frac{1}{D!}\epsilon_{i_{0}i_{1}\cdots i_{D}}m^{i_{0}}dm^{i_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge m^{i_{D}},

(69)

that is, in local coordinates

\sqrt{\det g(x)}=\det\left({\bf m},\frac{\partial{\bf m}}{\partial x^{1}},\cdots,\frac{\partial{\bf m}}{\partial x^{D}}\right).

(70)

By defining

{\bf n}(k)\equiv{\bf m}(f(k)),

(71)

we can neatly express the pullback of
\omega
over
f

as

\displaystyle f^{*}\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D!}\epsilon_{i_{0}i_{1}\cdots i_{D}}n^{i_{0}}dn^{i_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge n^{i_{D}}

(72)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det\left({\bf n},\frac{\partial{\bf n}}{\partial k^{1}},\cdots,\frac{\partial{\bf n}}{\partial k^{D}}\right)d^{D}k.

Indeed,
since

\det\left({\bf n},\frac{\partial{\bf n}}{\partial k^{1}},\cdots,\frac{\partial{\bf n}}{\partial k^{D}}\right)\\
=J_{f}(k)\det\left({\bf m},\frac{\partial{\bf m}}{\partial x^{1}},\cdots,\frac{\partial{\bf m}}{\partial x^{D}}\right)_{x=f(k)},

(73)

Eq. (
72
) coincides with Eq. (
68
) by use of Eq. (
70
).

Moreover, we note that if
