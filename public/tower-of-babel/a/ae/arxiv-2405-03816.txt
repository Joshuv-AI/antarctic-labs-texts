Algorithmic complexity of β \beta -expansions and application to A/D conversion
Valentin Abadie, Helmut Boelcskei

Abstract




We establish diverse relationships between the algorithmic (Kolmogorov) complexity of the prefixes of any binary expansion and
\beta
-expansions. These relationships allow to develop intuitions on the complexity behavior of
\beta
-expansions, and raise problems related to compressibility of binary sequences generated in the context of A/D conversion relying on
\beta
-expansions. Our last contribution is to solve these problems.

1
Introduction

Measuring the complexity of real numbers is of major importance in computer science. Consider a non-computable real number
s
, i.e., a real number which cannot be stored on a computer
[
5
, Section 10]
. One can store only an approximation of
s
, for instance by considering a finite length bitstring
y
representing a prefix
x
of the binary expansion
{\bf x}
of
s
. For a fixed approximation error
\varepsilon>0
, the required length of
y
, as a function of
\varepsilon
, depends on the
algorithmic complexity
of the prefix
x
of the binary expansion achieving error
\varepsilon
. The
algorithmic complexity
of a binary sequence
x
, often referred to as
Kolmogorov complexity
, is the length of the smallest binary sequence
y
, for which there exists an algorithm, such that when presented with
y
as input, delivers
x
as output
[
1
, Definition 10.1]
[
6
, Section 14.2]
[
22
, Definition 2.1.2]
. The algorithmic complexity of the binary expansions of real numbers has been widely studied, but the algorithmic complexity of expansions in bases other than
2
remains poorly understood. Several papers have established an equivalence between the algorithmic complexity of the expansions in different bases
q\in\mathbb{N}

[
3
, Theorem 6.1]
[
17
, Theorem 5.1]
[
31
, Theorem 3]
. Here, we study the algorithmic complexity of expansions in noninteger bases, which display a much more sophisticated behavior. This type of expansions are often referred to as
\beta
-expansions
[
25
, (1)]
[
27
, Section 4]
.
\beta
-expansions display some redundancy properties that are used to design robust A/D converters
[
10
]
[
11
]
[
12
]
. However, we discover that the algorithmic complexity of
\beta
-expansions can be much larger than the algorithmic complexity of binary expansions of the same number. This engenders problems of compressibility of the sequences generated by the aforementioned A/D converters. Fortunately, we find a fast algorithm to fix this issue, converting any
\beta
-expansion of potentially large algorithmic complexity into another
\beta
-expansion which algorithmic complexity is equal to the algorithmic complexity of the binary expansions.

1.2
Binary expansions

Representing real numbers in terms of sequences of bits is of major interest in computer science and electrical engineering. The most common way to effect such a conversion is the binary expansion. Indeed, every real number
s\in[0,1]
can be written as a sum of negative powers of
2
. For example,
s=0.75
can be represented as

s=0.75=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}=2^{-1}+2^{-2}=1\times 2^{-1}+1\times 2^{-2}+0\times 2^{-3}+0\times 2^{-4}+\ldots

(2)

Here, the sequence
110^{\infty}
is called a binary expansion of
s=0.75
. We conspicuously say “a binary expansion” rather than “the binary expansion” as the expansion is not unique. Indeed, with

\frac{1}{4}=\frac{\frac{1}{8}}{\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{\frac{1}{8}}{1-\frac{1}{2}}=\sum_{i=3}^{\infty}2^{-i},

(3)

one has equivalently

s=0.75=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}=2^{-1}+\sum_{i=3}^{\infty}2^{-i}=1\times 2^{-1}+0\times 2^{-2}+\sum_{i=3}^{\infty}1\times 2^{-i},

(4)

so that
{\bf x}=101^{\infty}
constitutes an alternative binary representation of
s
. Generally speaking, for every given
s\in[0,1]
, there exist either one or two binary expansions. Following

[
31
, Section 1]
, we refer to it as 2-ambiguity of the binary expansion. The real numbers with precisely two binary expansions form a subset of the rational numbers called the dyadic numbers
[
28
, Section 1.5, Problem 44]
. Dyadic numbers are defined to be the real numbers that have a binary expansion ending with
0^{\infty}
. Dyadic numbers do not include, for instance,
s=1/3
, which indeed displays only one binary expansion. The definition of dyadic numbers immediately implies that they have two expansions: one that ends with
0^{\infty}
, and another one that ends with
1^{\infty}
. This can be seen as follows. Consider a dyadic number
s\neq 0
. By definition,
s
has a binary expansion that ends with
0^{\infty}
. Then, there exists a binary sequence
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
, of length

, such that
{\bf x}=x10^{\infty}
is a binary expansion of
s
, i.e.,

s=x_{1}\times 2^{-1}+\ldots+x_{n}\times 2^{-n}+1\times 2^{-(n+1)}+0\times 2^{-(n+2)}+0\times 2^{-(n+3)}+\ldots

(5)

As
1
satisfies the following algebraic equation

1=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}2^{-i},

(6)

one can replace the last digit
1
of the binary expansion of

according to

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=x_{1}\times 2^{-1}+\ldots+x_{n}\times 2^{-n}+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}2^{-i}\right)\times 2^{-(n+1)}

(7)

\displaystyle=x_{1}\times 2^{-1}+\ldots+x_{n}\times 2^{-n}+\sum_{i=n+2}^{\infty}2^{-i}

(8)

\displaystyle=x_{1}\times 2^{-1}+\ldots+x_{n}\times 2^{-n}+0\times 2^{-(n+1)}+1\times 2^{-(n+2)}+1\times 2^{-(n+3)}+\ldots

1.3
\beta
-expansions

Binary expansions are not the only way to represent real numbers as infinite sequences of bits. One can replace the base 2 in
s=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\bf x}_{i}2^{-i}
by
\beta\in(1,2]
to get so-called
\beta
-expansions, i.e., representations of real numbers as sums of negative powers of
\beta
, originally introduced in
[
27
, Section 4, Example 4]
. The set of real numbers that can be represented by
\beta
-expansions is larger: while every real number in
[0,1]
can be represented by a binary expansion, it turns out that for
\beta\in(1,2]
, every number in
I_{\beta}:=[0,(\beta-1)^{-1}]
can be represented by a
\beta
-expansion
[
8
, Section 1]
. Note that
I_{2}=[0,1]
, so this is consistent with the binary case.

\beta
-expansions exhibit a much richer structure than binary expansions, specifically, the 2-ambiguity can turn into an
\infty
-ambiguity. An example illustrating this aspect is the
G
-expansion of
s=1
, where
G
is the golden ratio, satisfying
G^{2}=G+1
. Indeed, it follows that

1=\frac{1}{G}+\frac{1}{G^{2}}=1\times\frac{1}{G}+1\times\frac{1}{G^{2}}+0\times\frac{1}{G^{3}}+0\times\frac{1}{G^{4}}+\cdots

(10)

and hence
{\bf x}=1100\ldots
is a
G
-expansion of
s=1
. An alternative representation of
s=1
in base
\beta=G
is obtained by noting that
\frac{1}{G^{2}}=\frac{1}{G^{3}}+\frac{1}{G^{4}}
, and hence

1=\frac{1}{G}+\frac{1}{G^{3}}+\frac{1}{G^{4}}=1\times\frac{1}{G}+0\times\frac{1}{G^{2}}+1\times\frac{1}{G^{3}}+1\times\frac{1}{G^{4}}+0\times\frac{1}{G^{5}}+\cdots

(11)

so that
{\bf x}^{\prime}=101100\ldots
is also a
G
-expansion of
s=1
. By iterating this heuristic, one can show that for every
n\geq 0
, the sequence
{\bf x}^{(n)}:=(10)^{n}110^{\infty}
is a
G
-expansion of
s=1
. There are hence infinitely many different
G
-expansions of
s=1
, all obtained by exploiting the algebraic equation

1=\frac{1}{G}+\frac{1}{G^{2}},

(12)

which is of the same flavor as the algebraic equation (
6
).

We can generalize the idea underlying the example
G=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
to all pairs
(s,\beta)
satisfying

(a)

\beta
satisfies an equation of the form

1=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta^{-n_{i}},

(13)

where
N,n_{1},\ldots,n_{N}\in\mathbb{N}
and the

are pairwise distinct.

(b)

there is a
\beta
-expansion of
s
ending with
0^{\infty}
.

Let
s,\beta
satisfying (a) and (b), and let
{\bf x}
be a
\beta
-expansion of
s
ending with
0^{\infty}
. Then, there exists

and

. Also, note that as there exists

such that

, there exists

, satisfying

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\bf x}_{i}\beta^{-i}=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}x_{i}\beta^{-i}+\beta^{-j}+\sum_{i=1}^{j}0\beta^{-i}

(14)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}x_{i}\beta^{-i}+\beta^{-j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}x_{i}\beta^{-i}+\beta^{-j}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{N}}y_{i}\beta^{-i}

(15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}x_{i}\beta^{-i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{N}}y_{i}\beta^{-i-j}.

(16)

\beta
-expansion of

1.4
Algorithmic complexity

We proceed to building up the definitions related to algorithmic complexity. We will remain on the surface of the topic, but we refer the interested reader to
[
22
]
for an in-depth study. An algorithm is a finite sequence of nonambiguous elementary mathematical operations. We consider a special class of algorithms, called
effective algorithms
, that are defined precisely by the formalism of Turing machines
[
32
]
. Effective algorithms are essentially algorithms that can be run on digital computers, assuming infinite memory. As a counter-example, Algorithm
1
is not an effective algorithm, as line
5
uses the comparison of the real variable “
r
” to the threshold
0.5
, which in full generality requires infinite precision, the latter being impossible to realize in practice, and a fortiori not possible on digital computers. Effective algorithms can manipulate finite sequences of nonnegative integers and perform operations such as shifts, copying and pasting which can then be used to define more complex operations on integers and rationals, such as addition, multiplication, divison and comparison. We can equip an effective algorithm
E
with an infinite binary sequence
{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. The effective algorithm can then use this sequence
{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
as a help to perform its computations. We write
E^{\bf x}
to denote that
E
is equipped with
{\bf x}
. In practice, such an infinite binary
{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is the mathematical model of a dataset.

Effective algorithms lead to the definition of a special class of functions. Let
X,Y
be two countable sets, such as
\{0,1\}^{\ast}
,
\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{Q}
, or a finite product of these sets. A function
\varphi:X\times\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\to Y
is called
computable
if there exists an effective algorithm
E_{\varphi}
that, when presented with input
x\in A
and equipped with
{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, delivers
\varphi(x,{\bf x})
as an output. A function
\psi:X\to Y
are said to be
computable relatively to

{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
if there exists a computable function
\varphi:X\times\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\to Y
such that
\psi(x)=\varphi(x,{\bf x})
, for all
x\in X
. In particular, we say that
\psi:X\to Y
is
computable
if
\psi
is computable relatively to
0^{\infty}
.

A universal computable function is a computable function
U:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\times\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\{0,1\}^{\ast}
such that for every given computable function
\varphi:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\times\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\{0,1\}^{\ast}
, there exists a
p\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
, called program for
\varphi
, so that
\varphi(\cdot,\cdot)=U(\langle p,\cdot\rangle,\cdot)
. The existence of universal computable functions was established in
[
32
]
. The concept of universal computable function is the base to the definition of algorithmic complexity, that we now introduce.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
U
be a universal computable function, and
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
. The algorithmic complexity of
x
with respect to
U
relatively to
{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is defined as the length of the shortest (finite) binary sequence
x^{\ast}
, called canonial sequence for
x
, which satisfies
U(x^{\ast},{\bf x})=x
. It is denoted
K_{U}[x|{\bf x}]=|x^{\ast}|
.

Note that the above definition of algorithmic complexity is given with respect to some universal computable function, and relatively to some sequence
{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Following
[
22
, Definition 2.1.2]
, we fix a universal computable function
\mathcal{U}_{\operatorname{ref}}
throughout the paper, and we define the
algorithmic complexity of
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
relatively to
{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
as

K[x|{\bf x}]:=K_{\mathcal{U}_{\operatorname{ref}}}[x|{\bf x}],\ \text{for all}\ x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast},\ {\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}.

(17)

Moreover, we can define the absolute measure of
algorithmic complexity of
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
by

K[x]:=K[x|0^{\infty}].

(18)

Algorithmic complexity reflects the structure (or the absence of structure) of a binary sequence
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
. High algorithmic complexity denotes the absence of structure, while low algorithmic complexity is an indicator of strong interdependencies between the bits of
x
. Moreover, two sequences that are structurally related display similar algorithmic complexities. More precisely, the following very simple standard lemma expresses that computable functions can only reduce algorithmic complexity. This lemma can be found as
[
22
, Exercise 2.1.6.a]
, and will turn out useful for the rest of the paper.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
\varphi:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\to\{0,1\}^{\ast}
be a computable function. Then,

K[\varphi(x)]\leq K[x]+\underset{|x|\to\infty}{\mathcal{O}}(1),\ \forall x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}.

(19)

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{U}_{\operatorname{ref}}
is the universal computable function fixed throughout the paper, and let
U:=\mathcal{U}_{\operatorname{ref}}(\cdot,0^{\infty})
. Let
\varphi:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\to\{0,1\}^{\ast}
be a computable function.
\varphi\circ U
is also a computable function (the composition of two computable functions is again a computable function
[
23
, Exercise 2.1.2]
). Further, as
U
is a universal computable function, there exists a program
p\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
such that
U(\langle p,x\rangle)=\varphi\circ U(x)
, for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
. Let now
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
, and
x^{\ast}\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
be such that
U(x^{\ast})=x
and
|x^{\ast}|=K[x]
. Then,
U(\langle p,x^{\ast}\rangle)=\varphi\circ U(x^{\ast})=\varphi(x)
. By definition, it immediately follows that
K[\varphi(x)]\leq|\langle p,x^{\ast}\rangle|=2|p|+|x^{\ast}|+1=K[x]+2|p|+1
.
∎

In this paper, we manipulate infinite binary sequences. Hopefully, algorithmic complexity naturally extends from finite to infinite sequences. For an infinite sequence
{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, we study the behavior of
K[{\bf x}_{1:n}]
in function of
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We also extend this concept to relative algorithmic complexity. Namely, for two infinite sequences
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, the algorithmic complexity of
{\bf x}
relatively to
{\bf y}
is understood through the study of the behavior of
K[{\bf x}_{1:n}|{\bf y}]
in function of
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

The difference of the algorithmic complexity between prefixes of the greedy and lazy binary expansions is well understood. For
s\in[0,1]
, the greedy binary expansion
{\bf x}
of
s
and the lazy binary expansion
{\bf x}^{\prime}
of
s
satisfy

|K[{\bf x}_{1:n}]-K[{\bf x}^{\prime}_{1:n}]|\leq c,\ \text{for all}\ n\in\mathbb{N},

(20)

where
c>0
is independant of
n
and
s
. This follows directly from the fact that one can generate the lazy binary expansion from the greedy binary expansion by following the procedure specified through (
5
)-(
9
). In particular, the operations (
5
)-(
9
) can be carried out by an effective algorithm.

To the best of our knowledge, there is no literature at all relating the algorithmic complexity of the
\beta
-expansions to the algorithmic complexity of the binary expansions, even though their rich structure suggests interesting relationships. In particular, as discussed in the previous section, for a given
s\in[0,1]
, its
\beta
-expansions could have a different algorithmic complexity than its binary expansions. We first make important remarks that will shape our study.

(a)

As mentionned before, the set of real numbers that can be represented by
\beta
-expansions is larger that the set of real numbers that can be represented by a binary expansion: while every real number in
[0,1]
can be represented by a binary expansion, every number in
I_{\beta}:=[0,(\beta-1)^{-1}]
can be represented by a
\beta
-expansion. In the sequel, we will consider only the real numbers in
[0,1]
. Therefore, the sequences that can be used as
\beta
-expansions are only those sequences
{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
that satisfy

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\bf x}_{i}\beta^{-i}\leq 1.

(21)

We denote by
\Omega_{\beta}
the set of such sequences.

(b)

\beta
-expansions do not approximate real numbers with the same rate as binary expansions. This has a consequence on how to interprete their relative algorithmic complexities. Indeed, for typical
s\in[0,1]
, the first
n
bits of the binary expansion approximate
s
with a rate of
\sim 2^{-n}
, while the first
n
bits of
\beta
-expansions approximate
s
with a rate of
\propto\beta^{-n}=2^{-n\log_{\beta}(2)}
. We need to compare the algorithmic complexities of binary expansions and
\beta
-expansions which approximate
s
with the same order of magnitude. Therefore, if
{\bf x}
is a
\beta
-expansion of
s
and
{\bf y}
is a binary expansion of
s
we need to compare
K[{\bf y}_{1:n}]
to
K[{\bf x}_{1:n(\beta)}]
, where
n(\beta):=\lceil n\log_{\beta}(2)\rceil
, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\beta\in(1,2]
.

(c)

By (
20
), the greedy and lazy binary expansions have similar algorithmic complexity. Therefore, without loss of generality, we will focus on comparing the algorithmic complexity of the
\beta
-expansions of
s
to the algorithmic complexity of the greedy binary expansion of
s
.

(d)

The base
\beta\in(1,2)
itself might contain a lot of information, which might be reflected in terms of algorithmic complexity. To avoid artifacts due to the information content of
\beta
, we evaluate the algorithmic complexity relative to
\beta
, that is defined based on the algorithmic complexity relative to infinite sequences. For
\beta\in(1,2)
, let
{\bf y}_{\beta}
be the greedy binary expansion of
\beta-1
. Then, we define

K[x|\beta]:=K[x|{\bf y}_{\beta}],\ \text{for all}\ x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}.

(22)

In what follows, we will hence consider
{\bf x}
a
\beta
-expansion of
s
, and the greedy binary expansion
{\bf y}
of
s
, and compare
K[{\bf y}_{1:n}|\beta]
to
K[{\bf x}_{1:n(\beta)}|\beta]
. This comparison is made formal through the evaluation of the following quantity.

Definition 1.2
.

For
{\bf x}\in\Omega_{\beta}
, we define

\underline{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{K[{\bf x}_{1:n(\beta)}|\beta]-K[{\bf y}_{1:n}|\beta]}{n},

(23)

and

\bar{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{K[{\bf x}_{1:n(\beta)}|\beta]-K[{\bf y}_{1:n}|\beta]}{n},

(24)

where
{\bf y}
is the greedy binary expansion of
s=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\bf x}_{i}\beta^{-i}
.

\bar{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})
(resp.
\underline{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})
) evaluates how more complex is the
\beta
-expansion
{\bf x}
of
s
as compared to the greedy binary expansion of
s
, in a worst-case (resp. best-case) fashion. Our work will be first dedicated to derive lower bounds on
\underline{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})
and upper bounds on
\bar{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})
. Then, we will focus on certain specific values of
\beta
, for which we will derive a fine-grained distribution of algorithmic complexity. Finally, we will show that our findings suggest that the robustness displayed by A/D conversion algorithms based on
\beta
-expansions is at the cost of generating more complex sequences, but we will give a concrete solution on how to fix this problem.

1.5
Main results

Our first fundamental result shows that
\beta
-expansions are at least as complex as the greedy binary expansion.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
. Then,

0\leq\underline{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})\leq\bar{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x}),

(25)

for all
{\bf x}\in\Omega_{\beta}
.

Our second result is a trivial upper bound, which is mere consequence of the definition of algorithmic complexity: the identity function
\operatorname{id}:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\to\{0,1\}^{\ast}
is computable, so by Definition of
\mathcal{U}_{\operatorname{ref}}
, there exists a word
p\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
so that
\mathcal{U}_{\operatorname{ref}}(\langle x,p\rangle,{\bf y}_{\beta})=\operatorname{id}(x)=x
for every
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
(recall that
{\bf y}_{\beta}
is the greedy binary expansion of
\beta-1
). Further, the Definition of relative algorithmic complexity implies that
K[x|\beta]\leq|\langle x,p\rangle|=|x|+2|p|
for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
. As a consequence, we get that

\underline{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})\leq\bar{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{K[{\bf x}_{1:n(\beta)}|\beta]}{n}\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{|{\bf x}_{1:n(\beta)}|+2|p|}{n}=\log_{\beta}(2).

(26)

This results in the following Lemma.

Lemma 1.3
.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
. Then,

0\leq\underline{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})\leq\bar{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})\leq\log_{\beta}(2),

(27)

for all
{\bf x}\in\Omega_{\beta}
.

We will identify classes of bases
\beta\in(1,2)
for which the upper bound can be improved. We will cover two results of different nature: a result that holds for almost all
\beta\in(1,2)
, and a result for
\beta
satisfying certain algebraic properties. The latter result will yield interesting corollaries. We now state the result on the improvement of the bound for almost all
\beta\in(1,2)
.

Theorem 1.4
.

For almost all
\beta\in(1,2)
,

0\leq\underline{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})\leq\bar{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})\leq\log_{\beta}\left(\frac{2}{\beta}\right),

(28)

for all
{\bf x}\in\Omega_{\beta}
.

We now move to the result that holds for
\beta\in(1,2)
satisfying some specific algebraic relationships. We first recall some facts about algebraic numbers. A real number
\beta
is called algebraic if there exists a polynomial
P=a_{d}X^{d}+\ldots+a_{1}X+a_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
such that
P(\beta)=0
. Note that there may be several such polynomials, we denote by
P_{\beta}
such a polynomial uniquely defined by

(a)

P_{\beta}
has minimal degree
d
, i.e. for every polynomial
Q
of degree less than
P
,
Q(\beta)\neq 0
.

(b)

the leading coefficient
a_{d}
of
P_{\beta}
is positive.

(c)

the leading coefficient
a_{d}
of
P_{\beta}
is minimal, i.e. for every other polynomial
Q
of degree
d
and positive leading coefficient
b_{d}>0
such that
Q(\beta)=0
,
b_{d}>a_{d}
.

P_{\beta}
is called the
minimal polynomial
of
\beta
. We denote by
L_{\beta}
the leading coefficient
a_{d}
of
P_{\beta}
and
T_{\beta}
the tail coefficient
a_{0}
of
P_{\beta}
. The roots of
P_{\beta}
, not including
\beta
, are called the
Galois conjugates of
\beta
(by root we mean any complex number
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
P_{\beta}(z)=0
). We denote by
G_{\beta}
the set of Galois conjugates of
\beta
. Following
[
15
, Lemma 1.51]
, we the set
G^{+}_{\beta}=\{z\in G_{\beta}:|z|>1\}
of Galois conjugates that lay outside of the unit circle.
G^{+}_{\beta}
will have a drastic impact of the algorithmic complexity of
\beta
-expansions.
The question of algorithmic complexity of
\beta
-expansions with
\beta
algebraic is slightly more complicated to handle, because of the following fact. When
\beta
is algebraic, there might be multiplicities of finite
\beta
-expansions, i.e., for a given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there might be different
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\beta^{-i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\beta^{-i}
. The existence of at least two such
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
only happens when
\beta
is algebraic. Indeed,

\displaystyle\exists x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}\ s.t.\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\beta^{-i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\beta^{-i}

(29)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\exists x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}\ s.t.\ \beta^{-n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}-y_{i})\beta^{n-i}=0

(30)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\exists x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}\ s.t.\ \sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(x_{n-i-1}-y_{n-i-1})\beta^{i}=0

(31)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\exists P=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(x_{n-i-1}-y_{n-i-1})X^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[X]\ s.t.\ P(\beta)=0.

(32)

These multiplicities naturally define an equivalence relationship on
\{0,1\}^{\ast}
, by
x\sim_{\beta}y\iff\left|x\right|=\left|y\right|=:n\ \text{and}\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\beta^{-i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\beta^{-i}
. We denote by
[x]_{\beta}
the equivalence class associated to
\sim_{\beta}
. To every
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
, we define
M_{\beta}(x)
to be the lexicographically maximal element of
[x]_{\beta}
. Moreover, given an infinite sequence
{\bf x}
, we define
M_{\beta}{\bf x}
as being the sequence such that
(M_{\beta}{\bf x})_{1:n}:=M_{\beta}(x_{1:n})
. The introduction of
M_{\beta}{\bf x}
allows to derive the main result on the algorithmic complexity of the
\beta
-expansions when
\beta
is algebraic.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
be an algebraic number. Then,

0\leq\underline{\Delta}_{\beta}(M_{\beta}{\bf x})\leq\bar{\Delta}_{\beta}(M_{\beta}{\bf x})\leq\log_{\beta}\left(L_{\beta}\prod_{z\in G^{\ast}_{\beta}}\left|z\right|\right),

(33)

for all
{\bf x}\in\Omega_{\beta}
.

The above theorem allows to derive several corollaries. First, we will establish a class of algebraic numbers for which there are no multiplicities, and hence for which
M_{\beta}{\bf x}
can be replaced by
{\bf x}
in the above result. This class is a generalization of the class of
Garsia numbers
introduced in
[
15
, Section 1.7]
. Second, we study another family of algebraic numbers, called
Pisot numbers
introduced in
[
26
]
, for which the above equation yields
\underline{\Delta}_{\beta}(M_{\beta}{\bf x})=\bar{\Delta}_{\beta}(M_{\beta}{\bf x})=0
. In the next section, we exploit this property to modify slightly the A/D converter of Daubechies in order to control the algorithmic complexity of the
\beta
-expansion it delivers.

We proceed to introduce a class of algebraic numbers, inspired and generalized from the Garsia numbers. First, we restrict the class of algebraic numbers as follows. A real number
\beta
is said to be an
algebraic integer
if
\beta
is an algebraic number, and the leading coefficient
L_{\beta}
of its minimal polynomial
P_{\beta}
satisfies
L_{\beta}=1
. Further, we define a subset
\mathscr{C}
of algebraic integers as follows. An algebraic integer
\beta
belongs to
\mathscr{C}
if and only if the tail coefficient
T_{\beta}
of its minimal polynomial
P_{\beta}
satisfies
T_{\beta}\geq 2
. The class
\mathscr{C}
yields a very important property: for
\beta\in\mathscr{C}
, there are no multiplicities, i.e.,
M_{\beta}{\bf x}={\bf x}
for all
{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. This results in the following corollary.

Corollary 1.6
.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
be an algebraic integer in
\mathscr{C}
. Then,

0\leq\underline{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})\leq\bar{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})\leq\sum_{z\in G^{+}_{\beta}}\log_{\beta}\left(\left|z\right|\right),

(34)

for all
{\bf x}\in\Omega_{\beta}
.

A simple example of such a number is
\sqrt{2}
. Indeed, the minimal polynomial of
\sqrt{2}
is
X^{2}-2
, hence
\sqrt{2}
belongs to
\mathscr{C}
. Moreover, the only Galois conjugate of
\sqrt{2}
is
-\sqrt{2}
, which yields

0\leq\underline{\Delta}_{\sqrt{2}}({\bf x})\leq\bar{\Delta}_{\sqrt{2}}({\bf x})\leq\log_{\sqrt{2}}\left(\sqrt{2}\right)=1,

(35)

for all
{\bf x}\in\Omega_{\beta}
. More generally, the
m
-th root of any integer
k\geq 2
belongs to
\mathscr{C}
, since its minimal polynomial is
P_{\sqrt[m]{k}}=X^{m}-k
. The Galois conjugates of
\sqrt[m]{k}
are
\sqrt[m]{k}\exp\left(i\frac{2\pi\ell}{m}\right)
,
\ell\in 1,\ldots,m-1
. This yields

\displaystyle 0\leq\underline{\Delta}_{\sqrt[m]{k}}({\bf x})\leq\bar{\Delta}_{\sqrt[m]{k}}({\bf x})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}}\log_{\sqrt[m]{k}}\left(\left|\sqrt[m]{k}\exp\left(i\frac{2\pi\ell}{m}\right)\right|\right)

(36)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}}\log_{\sqrt[m]{k}}\left(\sqrt[m]{k}\right)=m-1,

(37)

for all
{\bf x}\in\Omega_{\beta}
. On can appreciate the tradeoff between redundancy and complexity: larger
m
is, the closer
\sqrt[m]{k}
is to
1
, i.e., the more redundant the
\sqrt[m]{k}
-expansions are, the higher the upper bound
m-1
on their relative compressibility is.

We close this section with a note on Pisot numbers, that are defined as algebraic integers having all their Galois conjugates located strictly inside the unit disk of
\mathbb{C}
. Pisot numbers include notably the golden ratio
G=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
, since its only Galois conjugate is the other root of the polynomial
X^{2}-X-1
, which is
\frac{1-\sqrt{5}}{2}\simeq-0.61
, that is strictly inside of the unit disk. Note that if
\beta
is a Pisot number,
G^{+}_{\beta}=\emptyset
. This yields the following immediate corollary.

Corollary 1.7
.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
be a Pisot number. Then,

\underline{\Delta}_{\beta}(M_{\beta}{\bf x})=\bar{\Delta}_{\beta}(M_{\beta}{\bf x})=0,

(38)

for all
{\bf x}\in\Omega_{\beta}
.

Interestingly, this further has a consequence on the algorithmic complexity of the greedy
\beta
-expansion.

Corollary 1.8
.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
be a Pisot number,
s\in[0,1]
and
{\bf x}
be the greedy
\beta
-expansion of
s
. Then,

\underline{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})=\bar{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})=0.

(39)

In the next section, we exploit these considerations on Pisot numbers to control the complexity of the sequences delivered by the A/D conversion algorithm.

1.6
Application: a denoising algorithm for A/D conversion

The algorithm of A/D conversion presented before uses the redundant property of
\beta
-expansions to generate arbitrarily precise representations of the input, even in the presence of imperfect quantizers. However, as raised previously in this paper, the generated
\beta
-expansions could be more complex than the binary expansion of the same input, following a redundancy versus complexity tradeoff. The successive theorems presented above indeed suggest that the
\beta
-expansions generated could indeed be more complex, hence harder to store on a computer. However, we also have seen that when
\beta
is a Pisot number, we can identify a class of
\beta
-expansions that display the same complexity as the binary expansion. It turns out that Pisot
\beta
is a usual choice in A/D conversion, notably the golden ratio
\beta=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}

[
12
]
. Here, we first expose a statement that strengthens the idea that the
\beta
-expansions generated by the A/D conversion algorithm can be more complex than the binary expansions, and then we fix this problem by establishing an algorithm that converts any
\beta
-expansion into another
\beta
-expansion of minimal complexity, in linear time.

In the previous section, we have established that for all
\beta\in(1,2)
,

\underline{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})=0,\ \forall{\bf x}\in\Omega_{\beta}.

(40)

The proof of this statement actually yields an even stronger result, namely

K[{\bf y}_{1:n}|\beta]\leq K[{\bf x}_{1:n(\beta)}|\beta]+\underset{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{O}}(1),

(41)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and all
{\bf x}\in\Omega_{\beta}
, where
{\bf y}
is the greedy binary expansion of
s:=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\bf x}_{i}\beta^{-i}
. The question is now to estimate the distribution of
K[{\bf x}_{1:n(\beta)}|\beta]-K[{\bf y}_{1:n}|\beta]
, in order to understand if for typical
{\bf x}\in\Omega_{\beta}
,
K[{\bf x}_{1:n(\beta)}|\beta]
tends to be close or far from
K[{\bf y}_{1:n}|\beta]
. This results in the study of the following set

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\beta}[s,n,k]:=

\displaystyle\left\{{\bf x}_{1:n(\beta)}:{\bf x}\ \text{is a}\ \beta\text{-expansion of}\ s,\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 62.15224pt\left.K[{\bf x}_{1:n(\beta)}|\beta]\leq K[{\bf y}_{1:n}|\beta]+k\right\},

(42)

for
s\in[0,1]
,
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
. For
s\in[0,1]
,
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
, the set
\mathcal{K}_{\beta}[s,n,k]
contains the prefixes of length
n(\beta)
of those
\beta
-expansions of
s
that display an algorithmic complexity that is higher than the algorithmic complexity of the corresponding greedy binary expansion by at most
k
bits. We establish the following result.

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
be a Pisot number,
s\in[0,1]
and
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, there exists
M\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\#\mathcal{K}_{\beta}[s,n,k]\leq 2^{k+1}n^{M}.

(43)

It was established in
[
14
, Theorem 1]
that if
\beta
is a Pisot number, then for almost all
s\in[0,1]
, there exists a constant
\gamma>0
such that the set

\Sigma_{\beta}[s,n]=\left\{{\bf x}_{1:n(\beta)}:{\bf x}\ \text{is a}\ \beta\text{-expansion of}\ s\right\}

(44)

satisfies

\#\Sigma_{\beta}[s,n]\geq 2^{\gamma n},

(45)

for
n
large enough. This means that for
k<<\gamma n
, the set
\mathcal{K}_{\beta}[s,n,k]
contains a very small fraction of all the
\beta
-expansions of
s
. Concretely,

\frac{\#\mathcal{K}_{\beta}[s,n,\alpha n]}{\#\Sigma_{\beta}[s,n]}\leq 2^{k+1-\gamma n}n^{M},

(46)

for almost all
s\in[0,1]
, and
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
with
n
large enough. Then, for all
\alpha<\gamma
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\#\mathcal{K}_{\beta}[s,n,k]}{\#\Sigma_{\beta}[s,n]}=0,

(47)

for almost all
s\in[0,1]
. This means that asymptotically, the majority of the
\beta
-expansions of a typical
s\in[0,1]
are more complex than the binary expansions. This yields a tradeoff between the complexity and the precision of the A/D conversion algorithm.

However, we now show that we can break this tradeoff. By Corollary
1.7
, we have shown that if
\beta\in(1,2)
is a Pisot number, we have

\underline{\Delta}_{\beta}(M_{\beta}{\bf x})=\bar{\Delta}_{\beta}(M_{\beta}{\bf x})=0,

(48)

for all
{\bf x}\in\Omega_{\beta}
. We then break the tradeoff by designing an effective algorithm to compute
M_{\beta}
in linear time, i.e., there exists a constant
C>0
such that the algorithm delivers
M_{\beta}(x)
when presented with any input
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
in less than
Cn
computation steps. The property of the algorithm computing in linear time crucially relies on
\beta
being a Pisot number.

2
Multivalued functions and algorithmic complexity

In this section, we introduce multivalued functions, and we expose the pivotal result of this paper, that is seen to be a generalization of
[
31
, Theorem 2]
. We start by giving the definition of a multivalued function, and of a computable multivalued function.

Definition 2.1

(Multivalued function)
.

Let
X,Y
be two sets. We write
f:X\rightrightarrows Y
to denote the fact that
f
is a multivalued function from
X
to
Y
, i.e., for each
x\in X
,
f(x)
is a subset of
Y
.

Definition 2.2

(Computable multivalued function)
.

Let
f:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\rightrightarrows\{0,1\}^{\ast}
be a multivalued function. We say that
f
is computable if there exists a computable function
g:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\to\{0,1\}^{\ast}
such that

g(x)=\langle f(x)\rangle.

(49)

We now state the result on the algorithmic complexity of multivalued functions, which is a reformulation of classical results of nonprobabilistic statistics
[
22
, Section 5.5]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Computable multivalued functions and complexity)
.

Let
f:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\rightrightarrows\{0,1\}^{\ast}
be a computable multivalued function and
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
. For all
y\in f(x)
,

K[y]\leq K[x]+\log_{2}\#f(x)+2\log\log\#f(x)+\underset{\left|x\right|\to\infty}{\mathcal{O}}(1).

(50)

Proof.

Let
f:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\rightrightarrows\{0,1\}^{\ast}
be a computable multivalued function. Then, by Definition
2.2
, there exists
g:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\to\{0,1\}^{\ast}
computable, such that

g(x)=\langle f(x)\rangle.

(51)

For
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
, we let
y_{1}(x),y_{2}(x),\ldots,y_{\#f(x)}
be the list of all the elements of
f(x)
, such that

g(x)=\langle y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{\#f(x)}(x)\rangle.

(52)

We can then define a computable function
h:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\to\{0,1\}^{\ast}
such that

h(\langle x,\operatorname{bin}(i)\rangle)=y_{i}(x),

(53)

through the following effective algorithm. On input
\langle x,\operatorname{bin}(i)\rangle
, use the function
g
to generate the sequence

y=\langle y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{\#f(x)}(x)\rangle.

(54)

Then, use the sequence
y
to enumerate the elements of
f(x)
until reaching the
i
-th element, and output this element, which is
y_{i}(x)
. Hence,
h
is computable and one has

\displaystyle K[y_{i}(x)]=K[h(\langle x,\operatorname{bin}(i)\rangle)]

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\leq}\limits^{\vbox to5.425pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\begin{subarray}{c}\hskip 1.9919pt\text{\tiny Lem.}\\
\smash{\scalebox{0.6}{\text{ \ref{lem:computable functions decrease complexity}}}}\end{subarray}$}\vss}}}K[\langle x,\operatorname{bin}(i)\rangle]+\underset{\left|x\right|\to\infty}{\mathcal{O}}(1)

(55)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}K[x]+K[i]+2\log K[\operatorname{bin}(i)]+\underset{\left|x\right|\to\infty}{\mathcal{O}}(1),

(56)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}K[x]+\left|\operatorname{bin}\right|(i)+2\log\left|\operatorname{bin}\right|(i)+\underset{\left|x\right|\to\infty}{\mathcal{O}}(1)

(57)

\displaystyle\leq K[x]+\log i+2\log\log i+\underset{\left|x\right|\to\infty}{\mathcal{O}}(1),

(58)

\displaystyle\leq K[x]+\log\#f(x)+2\log\log\#f(x)+\underset{\left|x\right|\to\infty}{\mathcal{O}}(1),

(59)

for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
and
1\leq i\leq\#f(x)
, where (a) and (b) follow from
[
22
, Example 2.1.5]
and
[
22
, Theorem 2.1.2]
, respectively.
∎

As mentionned in previous section, we will consider algorithmic complexity relative to
\beta\in(1,2)
, defined in (
22
). Accordingly, we define functions that are computable relatively to
\beta\in(1,2)
as being the functions that are computable relatively to
{\bf y}_{\beta}
, where
{\bf y}_{\beta}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is the greedy binary expansion of
\beta-1
. We further study the notion multivalued functions that are computable relatively to
\beta\in(1,2)
.

Definition 2.3

(Relatively computable multivalued function)
.

Let
f:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\rightrightarrows\{0,1\}^{\ast}
be a multivalued function. We say that
f
is computable relatively to
{\bf y}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
(resp.
\beta\in(1,2)
) if there exists a function
g:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\to\{0,1\}^{\ast}
computable relatively to
{\bf y}
(resp.
\beta
) such that

g(x)=\langle f(x)\rangle.

(60)

We can finally derive a relation for relative algorithmic complexity of multivalued functions.

Theorem 2.2

(Relatively computable multivalued functions and complexity)
.

Let
f:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\rightrightarrows\{0,1\}^{\ast}
be a multivalued function computable relatively to
{\bf y}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
. Then,

K[y|{\bf y}]\leq K[x|{\bf y}]+\log_{2}\#f(x)+2\log\log\#f(x)+\underset{\left|x\right|\to\infty}{\mathcal{O}}(1),

(61)

for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
and
y\in f(x)
. Moreover, (
61
) also holds if we replace
{\bf y}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
by
\beta\in(1,2)
.

We omit the proof, as it is essentially the same as the proof of Theorem
2.1
, except that we need to relativize every equation with respect to
{\bf y}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

3
Lower bound on the complexity of
\beta
-expansions

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem 1.2. Let
\beta\in(1,2]
. For
s\in[0,1]
, we denote by
\Sigma_{\beta}(s)
the set of all
\beta
-expansions of
s
, i.e.,

\Sigma_{\beta}(s):=\left\{{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}:\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\bf x}_{i}\beta^{-i}=s\right\}.

(62)

Recall that, as explained in Sections
1.2
and
1.3
,
\#\Sigma_{2}(s)\leq 2
and
\#\Sigma_{\beta}(s)=2^{\aleph_{0}}
if
\beta<\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
, for all
s\in[0,1]
. Further, we define
\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)
to be the set of sequences of length
n
that are prefix of some
\beta
-expansion of
s
, i.e.,

\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n):=\{{\bf x}_{1:n}:{\bf x}\in\Sigma_{\beta}(s)\}.

(63)

In order to prove Theorem
1.2
, we will construct a multivalued function
f_{\beta\to 2}:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\rightrightarrows\{0,1\}^{\ast}
that is computable relatively to
\beta
, for
\beta\in(1,2]
. This function
f_{\beta\to 2}
will be designed so that when it is presented with an
n(\beta)
-prefix
x
of some
\beta
-expansion of a given real number
s\in[0,1]
, it outputs a set that contains all the
n
-prefixes of all the binary expansions of
s
. In mathematical symbols, this is expressed as

\Sigma_{2}(s,n)\subseteq f_{\beta\to 2}(x),\ \forall x\in\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n(\beta)),\ n\in\mathbb{N}.

(64)

The function
f_{\beta\to 2}
will be constructed so that
x\mapsto\#f_{\beta\to 2}(x)
is bounded, in order to use Theorem
2.2
.

3.1
Construction of a multivalued function to convert between bases
\beta
and
2

We now explain the heuristic that is at the origin of the contruction of the function
f_{\beta\to 2}
. This heuristic is inspired from the proof of
[
31
, Theorem 3]
. Let
\beta\in(1,2]
,
s\in[0,1]
and
x\in\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n(\beta))
. Since
x\in\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n(\beta))
, then there exists
{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
x{\bf x}
is a
\beta
-expansion of
s
, i.e.,

s=\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}x_{i}\beta^{-i}+\sum_{i=n(\beta)+1}^{\infty}{\bf x}_{i}\beta^{-i}.

(65)

Observe that

0\leq\sum_{i=n(\beta)+1}^{\infty}{\bf x}_{i}\beta^{-i}\leq\sum_{i=n(\beta)+1}^{\infty}\beta^{-i}\leq\frac{\beta^{-n(\beta)}}{\beta-1}\leq\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1}\cdot

(66)

Combining (
65
) and (
66
), we obtain

\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}x_{i}\beta^{-i}\leq s\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}x_{i}\beta^{-i}+\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1}\cdot

(67)

Therefore, from the knowledge of
x
only, we can deduce that
s
belongs to the interval
I(x)
defined as

I(x):=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}x_{i}\beta^{-i},\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}x_{i}\beta^{-i}+\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1}\right].

(68)

Now, by combining again (
65
) and (
66
) for the specific case of
\beta=2
, we get that

t-2^{-n}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}2^{-i}\leq t,

(69)

for all
t\in[0,1]
and
y\in\Sigma_{2}(t,n)
. This means that if we know exactly the value of
t
, we can deduce that all the
n
-prefixes of the binary expansions of
t
are exactly those sequences
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
that satisfy

\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}2^{-i}\in[t-2^{-n},t].

(70)

However, if we do not know exactly
t
, but rather we know that
t\in[a,b]\subseteq[0,1]
, then we can deduce that all the
n
-prefixes
y
of the binary expansions of
t
satisfy

\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}2^{-i}\in[a-2^{-n},b].

(71)

Now, in light of (
71
), (
68
) can be reinterpreted as follows. From the knowledge of
x
, we can deduce that all the
n
-prefixes
y
of the binary expansions of
s
satisfy

\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}2^{-i}\in\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}x_{i}\beta^{-i}-2^{-n},\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}x_{i}\beta^{-i}+\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1}\right]=:J(x).

(72)

The above heuristic results in the following formal definition of a multivalued function
\hat{f}_{\beta\to 2}
, that however is still not the multivalued function
f_{\beta\to 2}
we are looking for, for reasons we explain just next.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\hat{f}_{\beta\to 2}:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\rightrightarrows\{0,1\}^{\ast}
be the multivalued function defined as

\hat{f}_{\beta\to 2}(x):=\left\{y\in\{0,1\}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}2^{-i}\in J(x)\right\},

(73)

for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{n(\beta)}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\hat{f}_{\beta\to 2}(x)=\epsilon
for
x\in\{0,1\}^{m}
where
m\neq n(\beta)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

However, the function
\hat{f}_{\beta\to 2}
is clearly not computable (even relatively to
\beta
) since it makes use of comparisons between arbitrary real numbers to evaluate wether or not
\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}2^{-i}\in J(x)
, i.e., its output cannot be calculated from its input through an effective algorithm. The core of the problem is that the operation “
\in
” is not computable, as it relies on both the operations “
\leq
” and “
\geq
”. This well-known issue can be fixed by using an approximate version of “
\leq
”. Namely, following
[
2
, Section 4.1, p79-80]
, there exists a computable function
\varphi:\mathbb{N}\times\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\{0,1\}
such that

\varphi(n,\langle{\bf y},{\bf z}\rangle)=\begin{cases}0\ \text{if}\ s<t\\
0\ \text{or}\ 1\ \text{if}\ t\leq s\leq t+2^{-n},\\
1\ \text{if}\ t>s+2^{-n},\end{cases}

(74)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
s,t\in[0,1]
, and where
{\bf y}
and
{\bf z}
are the respective greedy binary expansions of
s
and
t
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the binary relation
\leq_{n}
by

s\leq_{n}t\iff\varphi(n,\langle{\bf y},{\bf z}\rangle),\ \forall s,t\in[0,1],

(75)

where
{\bf y},{\bf z}
are the respective greedy binary expansions of
s
and
t
. Based on this relation, we can construct an “approximate membership” relation for
n\in\mathbb{N}
by

s\in_{n}[a,b]\iff a\leq_{n}s-2^{-n}\ \text{and}\ a\leq_{n}b,\ \forall s,a,b\in[0,1].

(76)

Note that, in particular,

s\in_{n}[a,b]\Rightarrow s\in[a-2^{-n},b+2^{-n}],\ \ \forall s,a,b\in[0,1].

(77)

In consequence, for an interval
I:=[a,b]\subseteq[0,1]
, we define

I^{(n)}:=[a-2^{-n},b+2^{-n}],\ \text{for}\ n\in\mathbb{N},

(78)

and we get

s\in_{n}I\Rightarrow s\in I^{(n)},\ \ \forall s\in[0,1].

(79)

Now, the function
f_{\beta\to 2}
can simply be defined with the usage of this approxiamte membership relation.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
f_{\beta\to 2}:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\rightrightarrows\{0,1\}^{\ast}
be the multivalued function defined as

f_{\beta\to 2}(x):=\left\{y\in\{0,1\}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}2^{-i}\in_{n}J(x)\right\},

(80)

for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{n(\beta)}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
f_{\beta\to 2}(x)=\epsilon
for
x\in\{0,1\}^{m}
where
m\neq n(\beta)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

3.2
Consequences on algorithmic complexity

In order to use Theorem
2.2
, there remains to establish an upper bound on the cardinality of
f_{\beta\to 2}(x)
, for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
\beta\in(1,2]
and
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
,

\#f_{\beta\to 2}(x)\leq\frac{1}{\beta-1}+3,\ \forall x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}.

(81)

Proof.

Let
\beta\in(1,2]
and
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
.

(a)

Suppose that there is no
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\left|x\right|=n(\beta)
. Then,
f(x)=\epsilon
, so
\#f(x)=1\leq\frac{1}{\beta-1}+3
.

(b)

Suppose that there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\left|x\right|=n(\beta)
. By definition,

\displaystyle f_{\beta\to 2}(x)

\displaystyle=\left\{y\in\{0,1\}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}2^{-i}\in_{n}J(x)\right\}

(82)

\displaystyle\subseteq\left\{y\in\{0,1\}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}2^{-i}\in J(x)^{(n)}\right\}=:A(x)

(83)

We give an upper bound on the cardinality of
A(x)
, which turns out to be an upper bound on the cardinality of
f_{\beta\to 2}(x)
. Note that for
y,z\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, satisfying
y\neq z
, we have

\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}2^{-i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}2^{-i}\right|\geq 2^{-n}.

(84)

Therefore, for an interval
I=[a,b]
, there can be at most
2^{n}(b-a)
sequences
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
that satisfy
\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}2^{-i}\in I
.
By (
72
) and (
78
),

J(x)^{(n)}=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}x_{i}\beta^{-i}-2^{-n}-2^{-n},\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}x_{i}\beta^{-i}+\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1}+2^{-n}\right],

(85)

so

\displaystyle\#A(x)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}x_{i}\beta^{-i}+\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1}+2^{-n}-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}x_{i}\beta^{-i}-2^{-n}-2^{-n}\right)\right)

(86)

\displaystyle=2^{n}\left(2^{-n}\frac{1}{\beta-1}+3\cdot 2^{-n}\right)=\frac{1}{\beta-1}+3.

(87)

As
f_{\beta\to 2}(x)\subseteq A(x)
, we finally get

\#f_{\beta\to 2}(x)\leq\#A(x)\leq\frac{1}{\beta-1}+3.

(88)

∎

Finally, we prove Theorem
1.2
as a corollary of Theorem
2.2
and Lemma
3.1
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
. Then,

0\leq\underline{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})\leq\bar{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x}),

(89)

for all
{\bf x}\in\Omega_{\beta}
.

Proof.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
,
{\bf x}\in\Omega_{\beta}
, and
s:=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\bf x}_{i}\beta^{-i}
. By Lemma
3.1
,
f_{\beta\to 2}:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\rightrightarrows\{0,1\}^{\ast}
satisfies

\#f({\bf x}_{1:n(\beta)})\leq\frac{1}{\beta-1}+3.

(90)

Moreover, as
f_{\beta\to 2}
is computable relatively to
\beta
, Theorem
2.2
yields that

\displaystyle K[y|\beta]

\displaystyle\leq K[{\bf x}_{1:n(\beta)}|\beta]+\log\left(\frac{1}{\beta-1}+3\right)+\log\log\left(\frac{1}{\beta-1}+3\right)+\underset{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{O}}(1)

(91)

\displaystyle=K[{\bf x}_{1:n(\beta)}|\beta]+\underset{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{O}}(1),

(92)

for all
y\in f_{\beta\to 2}(x)\supseteq\Sigma_{2}(s,n)
. In particular, if
{\bf y}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is the greedy binary expansion of
s
, then

K[{\bf y}_{1:n}|\beta]\leq K[{\bf x}_{1:n(\beta)}|\beta]+\underset{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{O}}(1).

(93)

Finally, this yields

\bar{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})\geq\underline{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{K[{\bf x}_{1:n(\beta)}|\beta]-K[{\bf y}_{1:n}|\beta]}{n}\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\underset{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{O}}(n^{-1})=0.

(94)

∎

4
Upper bounds on the complexity of
\beta
-expansions

In this section, we establish the non-trivial upper bounds on
\underline{\Delta}_{\beta}
and
\bar{\Delta}_{\beta}
, namely we prove Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
.

The base of the incoming proofs is the construction of a multivalued function
f_{2\to\beta}
that is computable relatively to
\beta
, which is built to be a kind of inverse of the function
f_{\beta\to 2}
introduced in previous section. The heuristic guiding the construction of
f_{2\to\beta}
is exactly analoguous to the one we used for
f_{\beta\to 2}
, therefore we skip this part and directly give the definition of
f_{2\to\beta}
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
f_{2\to\beta}:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\rightrightarrows\{0,1\}^{\ast}
be the multivalued function defined as

f_{2\to\beta}(x):=\left\{y\in\{0,1\}^{n(\beta)}:\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}y_{i}\beta^{-i}\in_{n}J(x)\right\},

(95)

for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
, where
n:=|x|
and

J(x):=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}2^{-i}-2^{-n},\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}2^{-i}+2^{-n}\right].

(96)

By the same arguments that led to the definition of
f_{\beta\to 2}
, one can establish that

\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n(\beta))\subseteq f_{2\to\beta}({\bf x}_{1:n}),

(97)

for all
s\in[0,1]
and
{\bf x}\in\Sigma_{2}(s)
. The main difference with previous section, is that
\#f_{2\to\beta}(x)
is not necessarily bounded by a constant for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
. For example, it follows from
[
14
, Theorem 1.5]
that there exists
\alpha>0
such that

\#\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)\geq 2^{\alpha n},

(98)

for all
\beta\in\left(1,\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\right)
,
s\in[0,1]
and from some
n\in\mathbb{N}
onward. Therefore, it follows from (
97
) that

\#f_{2\to\beta}(x)\geq 2^{\alpha n(\beta)}

(99)

for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
\beta\in\left(1,\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\right)
and from some
n\in\mathbb{N}
onward. Also note that a trivial bound for
\#f(x)
is is
2^{n(\beta)}
, for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
with
n:=\left|x\right|
, since
f(x)
is a subset of
\{0,1\}^{n(\beta)}
. However, this trivial bound only allows to recover the trivial upper bound on
\underline{\Delta}_{\beta}
and
\bar{\Delta}_{\beta}
established in Lemma
1.3
. In the sequel, we show that we can improve this trivial upper bound in two cases: for almost all
\beta\in(1,2)
, and for algebraic
\beta\in(1,2)
.

4.1
Upper bound for almost all
\beta\in(1,2)

In this part, we consider
\mathbb{R}
as equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}
generated from the Euclidean topology. We denote by
\lambda
the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. Fix
\beta\in(1,2)
,
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
and let
n:=\left|x\right|
. We are interested at establishing an upper bound on
\#f_{2\to\beta}(x)
, with
f_{2\to\beta}(x)
being defined by (
95
). We can reformulate the definition of
f_{2\to\beta}(x)
in terms of an integral over a certain domain, with respect to a certain measure. For
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
\delta_{t}
the Dirac measure centered in
t
, formally defined as

\delta_{t}(A)=\begin{cases}1\text{ if }t\in A,\\
0\text{ if }t\notin A,\end{cases}

(100)

for all
A\in\mathcal{B}
. Hence, for all
y\in\{0,1\}^{n(\beta)}
, we have

\delta_{\sum_{i=1}^{\left|y\right|}y_{i}\beta^{-i}}(J(x)^{(n)})=\begin{cases}1\text{ if }\sum_{i=1}^{\left|y\right|}y_{i}\beta^{-i}\in J(x)^{(n)},\\
0\text{ if }\sum_{i=1}^{\left|y\right|}y_{i}\beta^{-i}\notin J(x)^{(n)},\end{cases}

(101)

By summing over all
y\in\{0,1\}^{n(\beta)}
, and by definition of
f_{\beta\to 2}(x)
in (
95
), we get

\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^{n(\beta)}}\delta_{\sum_{i=1}^{\left|y\right|}y_{i}\beta^{-i}}(J(x)^{(n)})\geq\#f(x).

(102)

This framework matches exactly that of the Bernoulli convolution, introduced in
[
18
, Section 6]
. Define
C(I_{\beta})
to be the set of continuous functions
f:I_{\beta}\to\mathbb{R}
, and
\mathcal{M}
to be the set of regular Borel measures on
I_{\beta}
(see Appendix
A
). Consider the sequence of regular Borel measures
(\nu_{\beta,m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
defined by

\nu_{\beta,m}:=\frac{1}{2^{m}}\sum_{u\in\{0,1\}^{m}}\delta_{\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}\beta^{-i}},\ \forall m\in\mathbb{N}.

(103)

Note that (
102
) can be reformulated as

2^{n(\beta)}\nu_{\beta,n(\beta)}(J(x))\geq\#f_{\beta\to 2}(x).

(104)

The Bernoulli convolution
\nu_{\beta}
is defined as being the weak limit in
\mathcal{M}
of the sequence
(\nu_{\beta,m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
, i.e., the unique regular Borel measure satisfies

\int_{I_{\beta}}fd\nu_{\beta}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_{I_{\beta}}fd\nu_{\beta,m},\ \forall f\in C(I_{\beta}).

(105)

The fact that there is indeed such a regular Borel measure
\mu
is a standard result of measure theory, see Appendix
A
for greater details. The Bernoulli convolution has been widely studied in the literature. In particular, it was shown in
[
30
]
that for
\lambda
-almost all
\beta\in(1,2)
,
\nu_{\beta}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure
\lambda
, i.e., for all
A\in\mathcal{B}
,
\lambda(A)=0\Rightarrow\nu_{\beta}(A)=0
. In particular, the Radon-Nykodym theorem
[
16
, Section 31, Theorem B]
states that if a measure
\nu
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure
\lambda
, there exists a function
h:I_{\beta}\to\mathbb{R}
, called
Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\nu
, such that

\nu\left([a,b]\right)=\int_{a}^{b}h(x)dx,

(106)

for all
a,b\in I_{\beta}
,
a<b
. We can exploit this to study the asymptotic behavior of
\#f_{2\to\beta}(x)
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
such that
\nu_{\beta}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\lambda
, and let
h_{\beta}:I_{\beta}\to\mathbb{R}
be the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\nu_{\beta}
. Then,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}2^{-n\log_{\beta}(2/\beta)}\#f_{2\to\beta}({\bf y}_{1:n})\leq 2\left(4+\frac{1}{\beta-1}\right)h_{\beta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\bf y}_{i}2^{-i}\right),

(107)

for all
{\bf y}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof.

The proof follows that of
[
19
, Lemma 3.4]
. Let
\beta\in(1,2)
such that
\nu_{\beta}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\lambda
, let
h_{\beta}:I_{\beta}\to\mathbb{R}
be the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\nu_{\beta}
, and fix
{\bf y}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
so that
J({\bf y}_{1:n})=[a_{n},b_{n}]
, according to (
96
). By definition,

\displaystyle f_{2\to\beta}({\bf y}_{1:n})

\displaystyle=\left\{y\in\{0,1\}^{n(\beta)}:\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}y_{i}\beta^{-i}\in_{n}J({\bf y}_{1:n})\right\}

(108)

\displaystyle\subseteq\left\{y\in\{0,1\}^{n(\beta)}:\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}y_{i}\beta^{-i}\in J({\bf y}_{1:n})^{(n)}\right\}=:A({\bf y}_{1:n}).

(109)

We study the cardinality of
A({\bf y}_{1:n})
, which turns out to be an upper bound to the cardinality of
f_{2\to\beta}({\bf y}_{1:n})
. Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and define, for all
m\geq n(\beta)
, then set

A_{m,n}:=\{z\in\{0,1\}^{m}:z_{1:n(\beta)}\in A({\bf y}_{1:n})\}.

(110)

We make the following two remarks:

(a)

For all
y\in A({\bf y}_{1:n})
, there are
2^{m-n(\beta)}
elements in
A_{m,n}
that are suffixes of
y
. This implies that

\#A_{m,n}\geq 2^{m-n(\beta)}\#A({\bf y}_{1:n}).

(111)

(b)

For every
z\in A_{m,n}
, since
z_{1:n(\beta)}\in A({\bf y}_{1:n})
, we have that

\displaystyle a_{n}-2^{-n}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}z_{i}\beta^{-i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}z_{i}\beta^{-i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}z_{i}\beta^{-i}

(112)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}z_{i}\beta^{-i}+\sum_{i=n(\beta)+1}^{\infty}z_{i}\beta^{-i}

(113)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}z_{i}\beta^{-i}+\sum_{i=n(\beta)+1}^{\infty}\beta^{-i}

(114)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}z_{i}\beta^{-i}+\frac{\beta^{-n(\beta)}}{\beta-1}

(115)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}b_{n}+2^{-n}+\frac{\beta^{-n(\beta)}}{\beta-1}

(116)

\displaystyle\leq b_{n}+2^{-n}+\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1},

(117)

where (a) and (b) follow from the definition of
A({\bf y}_{1:n})
and (
78
). Hence, for every
z\in A_{m,n}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{m}z_{i}\beta^{-i}\in\left[a_{n}-2^{-n},b_{n}+2^{-n}+\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1}\right],

(118)

which translates to

\#A_{m,n}\leq 2^{m}\nu_{\beta,m}\left(\left[a_{n}-2^{-n},b_{n}+2^{-n}+\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1}\right]\right).

(119)

Combining these two remarks, we get that

2^{-n(\beta)}\#A({\bf y}_{1:n})\leq\nu_{\beta,m}\left(\left[a_{n}-2^{-n},b_{n}+2^{-n}+\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1}\right]\right),

(120)

which then translates to

2^{-n(\beta)}\#f_{2\to\beta}({\bf y}_{1:n})\leq\nu_{\beta,m}\left(\left[a_{n}-2^{-n},b_{n}+2^{-n}+\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1}\right]\right),

(121)

since
f_{2\to\beta}({\bf y}_{1:n})\subseteq A({\bf y}_{1:n})
.
By taking the limit superior when
m\to\infty
, Lemma
A.2
delivers

2^{-n(\beta)}\#f_{2\to\beta}({\bf y}_{1:n})\leq\nu_{\beta}\left(\left[a_{n}-2^{-n},b_{n}+2^{-n}+\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1}\right]\right).

(122)

We assumed that
\nu_{\beta}
is absolutely continuous and of Radon-Nikodym derivative
h_{\beta}
. Hence, by the Radon-Nikodym theorem, we have

\nu_{\beta}\left(\left[a_{n}-2^{-n},b_{n}+2^{-n}+\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1}\right]\right)=\int_{a_{n}-2^{-n}}^{b_{n}+2^{-n}+\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1}}h_{\beta}(x)dx.

(123)

By definition of
J({\bf y}_{1:n})=[a_{n},b_{n}]
, one has
b_{n}-a_{n}=2\cdot 2^{-n}
, and
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}b_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\bf y}_{i}2^{-i}
. Incorporating this fact in (
123
) yields

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\nu_{\beta}\left(\left[a_{n}-2^{-n},b_{n}+2^{-n}+\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1}\right]\right)}{2^{-n}\left(4+\frac{1}{\beta-1}\right)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\nu_{\beta}\left(\left[a_{n}-2^{-n},b_{n}+2^{-n}+\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1}\right]\right)}{b_{n}+2^{-n}+\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1}-\left(a_{n}-2^{-n}\right)}

(124)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\int_{a_{n}-2^{-n}}^{b_{n}+2^{-n}+\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1}}h_{\beta}(x)dx}{b_{n}+2^{-n}+\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1}-a_{n}+2^{-n}}

(125)

\displaystyle=h_{\beta}\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}\right)

(126)

\displaystyle=h_{\beta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\bf y}_{i}2^{-i}\right).

(127)

Combining this result with (
122
) delivers

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}2^{-(n(\beta)-n)}\#f_{2\to\beta}({\bf y}_{1:n})

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\nu_{\beta}\left(\left[a_{n}-2^{-n},b_{n}+2^{-n}+\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1}\right]\right)}{2^{-n}}

(128)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\nu_{\beta}\left(\left[a_{n}-2^{-n},b_{n}+2^{-n}+\frac{2^{-n}}{\beta-1}\right]\right)}{2^{-n}}

(129)

\displaystyle=\left(4+\frac{1}{\beta-1}\right)h_{\beta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\bf y}_{i}\beta^{-i}\right).

(130)

We conclude the proof by noting that

2^{-(n(\beta)-n)}\geq 2^{-(n\log_{\beta}(2)+1-n)}=2\cdot 2^{-n\log_{\beta}(2/\beta)},\ \forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

(131)

∎

As a direct Corollary, we get Theorem
1.4
.

Corollary 4.2
.

For almost all
\beta\in(1,2)
,

0\leq\underline{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})\leq\bar{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})\leq\log_{\beta}\left(\frac{2}{\beta}\right),

(132)

for all
{\bf x}\in\Omega_{\beta}
.

Proof.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
such that
\nu_{\beta}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\lambda
, and let
h_{\beta}:I_{\beta}\to\mathbb{R}
be the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\nu_{\beta}
. Fix
{\bf x}\in\Omega_{\beta}
, define
s:=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\bf x}_{i}\beta^{-i}\in[0,1]
and let
{\bf y}
be the greedy binary expansion of
s
. By Theorem
4.1
, we have

\limsup_{n\to\infty}2^{-n\log_{\beta}(2/\beta)}\#f_{2\to\beta}{\bf y}_{1:n})\leq 2\left(4+\frac{1}{\beta-1}\right)h_{\beta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\bf y}_{i}2^{-i}\right).

(133)

Therefore, there exists
N>0
such that for all
n\geq N
,

2^{-n\log_{\beta}(2/\beta)}\#f_{2\to\beta}({\bf y}_{1:n})\leq 2\left(4+\frac{1}{\beta-1}\right)h_{\beta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\bf y}_{i}2^{-i}\right)+1=:C,

(134)

so

\#f_{2\to\beta}({\bf y}_{1:n})\leq C\cdot 2^{n\log_{\beta}(2/\beta)},

(135)

for large enough
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This results in

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\#(f_{2\to\beta}({\bf y}_{1:n}))}{n}\leq\log_{\beta}(2/\beta)

(136)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\log\#(f_{2\to\beta}({\bf y}_{1:n}))}{n}=0.

(137)

Finally, note that by definition of
f_{2\to\beta}
,
{\bf x}_{1:n(\beta)}\in f_{2\to\beta}({\bf y}_{1:n})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, by Theorem
2.1
, one has

\displaystyle K[{\bf x}_{1:n(\beta)}]

\displaystyle\leq K[{\bf y}_{1:n}]+\log\#(f_{2\to\beta}({\bf y}_{1:n}))+\log\log\#(f_{2\to\beta}({\bf y}_{1:n}))+\underset{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{O}}(1).

(138)

By incorporation of (
136
) and (
137
), we get

\bar{\Delta}_{\beta}({\bf x})=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{K[{\bf x}_{1:n(\beta)}]-K[{\bf y}_{1:n}]}{n}\leq\log_{\beta}(2/\beta).

(139)

∎

4.2
Upper bound when
\beta
is algebraic

In this section, we work with
\beta\in(1,2)
algebraic, and we establish Theorem
1.5
. Recall that for a fixed number
\beta\in(1,2)
, we have defined the equivalence relationship
\sim_{\beta}
over
\{0,1\}^{\ast}
by

x\sim_{\beta}y\iff n:=|x|=|y|\ \text{and}\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\beta^{-i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\beta^{-i},\ \forall x,y\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}.

(140)

Moreover, we have defined the function
M_{\beta}:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\to\{0,1\}^{\ast}
such that
M_{\beta}(x)
is the lexicographically maximal element of the equivalence class
[x]_{\beta}:=\{y\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}:x\sim_{\beta}y\}
, for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
. The pivotal result allowing to establish Theorem
1.5
is a mere generalization of the famous “separation lemma”
[
15
, Lemma 1.51]
, which establishes a lower bound on the distance between the numbers of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\beta^{-i}
,
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, if
\beta\in(1,2)
belongs to a certain class of algebraic numbers. We adapt the proof of this Lemma to get a similar result for
\beta\in(1,2)
being any algebraic number. For an algebraic number
\beta\in(1,2)
, we denote by
L_{\beta}
the leading coefficient of its minimal polynomial, and by
G_{\beta}
the set of its Galois conjugates. We further define

\displaystyle G_{\beta}^{+}

\displaystyle:=\{z\in G_{\beta}:|z|>1\},

(141)

\displaystyle G_{\beta}^{1}

\displaystyle:=\{z\in G_{\beta}:|z|=1\},

(142)

and

\Pi_{\beta}:=\prod_{z\in G_{\beta}}|1-|z||,\ \ \ \ \ \ \Pi_{\beta}^{+}:=\prod_{z\in G_{\beta}^{+}}|z|,\ \ \ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \ \ k_{\beta}:=\#G_{\beta}^{1}.

(143)

The result is expressed as follows.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
be an algebraic number,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
x\not\sim_{\beta}y
. Then,

\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\beta^{-i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\beta^{-i}\right|\geq\frac{L_{\beta}\Pi_{\beta}}{\displaystyle n^{k_{\beta}}\left(\beta L_{\beta}\Pi_{\beta}^{+}\right)^{n}}\cdot

(144)

As this result is tied to algebraic considerations that deviate a lot from the message of this paper, we postpone the proof to Appendix
B
. This Lemma has two important consequences, that are essential to establish
1.5
. First, this implies that we can lower bound the distance between numbers of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Mx)_{i}\beta^{-i}
,
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, and second, we can show that
M_{\beta}
is computable.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
be an algebraic number and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, for all
x,y\in M_{\beta}\left(\{0,1\}^{n}\right)
such that
x\neq y
,

\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\beta^{-i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\beta^{-i}\right|\geq\frac{L_{\beta}\Pi_{\beta}}{\displaystyle n^{k_{\beta}}\left(\beta L_{\beta}\Pi_{\beta}^{+}\right)^{n}}\cdot

(145)

Proof.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
be an algebraic number,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x,y\in M_{\beta}\left(\{0,1\}^{n}\right)
such that
x\neq y
. The result is established by showing that
x\not\sim_{\beta}y
. Indeed, if we prove that
x\not\sim_{\beta}y
, then we can immediately apply Lemma
4.3
to get (
145
).

We now proceed to prove that
x\not\sim_{\beta}y
. Since
x,y\in M_{\beta}(\{0,1\}^{n})
, there exists
u,v\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
x=M_{\beta}(u)
and
y=M_{\beta}(v)
. By definition of
M_{\beta}
,
x=M_{\beta}(u)\in[u]_{\beta}
and
y=M_{\beta}(v)\in[v]_{\beta}
, so
x\sim_{\beta}u
and
y\sim_{\beta}v
. We now show that
u\not\sim_{\beta}v
, yielding
x\not\sim_{\beta}y
. By means of contradiction, suppose that
u\sim_{\beta}v
. Then,
[u]_{\beta}=[v]_{\beta}
and hence
x=M_{\beta}(u)=M_{\beta}(v)=y
. This is in contradiction with
x\neq y
.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
be an algebraic number and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,

\#(M_{\beta}\circ f_{2\to\beta}({\bf y}_{1:n}))\leq\frac{4}{L_{\beta}\Pi_{\beta}}n(\beta)^{k_{\beta}}\left(L_{\beta}\Pi_{\beta}^{+}\right)^{n(\beta)}\cdot

(146)

Proof.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
be an algebraic number. Define
F:=M_{\beta}\circ f_{2\to\beta}
, and let
{\bf y}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We will proceed to find an upper bound for
\#F({\bf y}_{1:n})
.

(a)

Similary as in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we define a set
A({\bf y}_{1:n})\supseteq f_{2\to\beta}({\bf y}_{1:n})
by the observation that

\displaystyle f_{2\to\beta}({\bf y}_{1:n})

\displaystyle=\left\{y\in\{0,1\}^{n(\beta)}:\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}y_{i}\beta^{-i}\in_{n}J({\bf y}_{1:n})\right\}

(147)

\displaystyle\subseteq\left\{y\in\{0,1\}^{n(\beta)}:\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}y_{i}\beta^{-i}\in J({\bf y}_{1:n})^{(n)}\right\}=:A({\bf y}_{1:n}).

(148)

We define
\hat{F}({\bf y}_{1:n}):=M_{\beta}(A({\bf y}_{1:n}))
. Note that
F({\bf y}_{1:n})\subseteq\hat{F}({\bf y}_{1:n})
. We hence study the cardinality of
\hat{F}({\bf y}_{1:n})
, which will deliver an upper bound on the cardinality of
F({\bf y}_{1:n})
.

(b)

First note that
\hat{F}({\bf y}_{1:n})\subseteq A({\bf y}_{1:n})
. Indeed, let
x\in\hat{F}({\bf y}_{1:n})
. Then, there exists
u\in A({\bf y}_{1:n})
such that
M_{\beta}(u)=x
. Since
M_{\beta}^{-1}(\{x\})=[x]_{\beta}
, then we deduce that
u\sim_{\beta}x
, i.e.,

\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}x_{i}\beta^{-i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}u_{i}\beta^{-i}\overset{(a)}{\in}J({\bf y}_{1:n})^{(n)},

(149)

where (a) follows from the definition of
A({\bf y}_{1:n})
. We conclude that, indeed,
x\in A({\bf y}_{1:n})
, so
\hat{F}({\bf y}_{1:n})\subseteq A({\bf y}_{1:n})
.

(c)

As a direct consequence, remark that by denoting
m_{n}
to be the smallest distance between two different numbers of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{n(\beta)}x_{i}\beta^{-i}
,
x\in\hat{F}({\bf y}_{1:n})
, we have that

\#\hat{\Sigma}_{i}\leq\frac{\beta^{-i}}{\beta-1}\frac{i^{k_{\beta}}(\beta L_{\beta}\Pi_{\beta}^{+})^{i}}{L_{\beta}\Pi_{\beta}}=\frac{i^{k_{\beta}}(L_{\beta}\Pi_{\beta}^{+})^{i}}{(\beta-1)L_{\beta}\Pi_{\beta}}\cdot

(192)

Suppose that
\beta
is a Pisot number, then
L_{\beta}=\Pi_{\beta}^{+}=1
, and
k_{\beta}=0
, so

\#\hat{\Sigma}_{i}\leq\frac{1}{(\beta-1)\Pi_{\beta}}=:C_{\beta}\cdot

(193)

Therefore, the total number of steps required for the For loop defined in line
5
satisfies

\displaystyle t_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(2+11\#\hat{\Sigma}_{i-1}+8\#\hat{\Sigma}_{i-1}^{3}\right)

(194)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(2+11C_{\beta}+8C_{\beta}^{3}\right)

(195)

\displaystyle\leq\left(2+11C_{\beta}+8C_{\beta}^{3}\right)n.

(196)

∎

6
Distribution of the complexities of
\beta
-expansions

In Section
4
, we established an upper bound on the algorithmic complexity of the lexicographically largest elements of the equivalence classes generated by the equivalence relationship
\sim_{\beta}
that naturally appears when
\beta
is algebraic. In this section, we further study the algorithmic complexity of the remaining elements of those equivalence classes. When
\beta
is Pisot, this allows us to characterize precisely the distribution of the algorithmic complexities of all the
\beta
-expansions of a given real number
s\in[0,1]
, in function of the algorithmic complexity of its binary expansions. We start by building a multivalued function that is computable relatively to
\beta
, that generates the set of all
\beta
-expansions. Later, we will use this function to link the algorithmic complexity of a given
\beta
-expansion to the algorithmic complexity of the sequence of coin tosses that is effected in line 6 of Algorithm
3
.

6.1
A computable function to generate all the
\beta
-expansions from a fixed
\beta
-expansion

Recall that for a fixed number
\beta\in(1,2)
, we have defined the equivalence relationship
\sim_{\beta}
over
\{0,1\}^{\ast}
by

x\sim_{\beta}y\iff n:=\left|x\right|=|y|\ \text{and}\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\beta^{-i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\beta^{-i},\ \forall x,y\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}.

(197)

Moreover, we have defined the equivalence class
[x]_{\beta}:=\{y\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}:x\sim_{\beta}y\}
, for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
, which, seen as a multivalued function
[\cdot]_{\beta}:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\rightrightarrows\{0,1\}^{\ast}
, is proven to be computable in the proof of Corollary
4.6
.

We now construct a multivalued function
f_{\beta,1\to all}:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\rightrightarrows\{0,1\}^{\ast}
that is computable relatively to
\beta
, such that

\langle\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)\rangle\in f_{\beta,1\to all}\left(x\right),\ \forall x\in\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n),\ s\in I_{\beta},\ \text{and}\ n\in\mathbb{N}.

(198)

To build
f_{\beta,1\to all}
, we first follow the same intuitions that lead to the construction of
f_{\beta\to 2}
in Definition
3.2
and
f_{2\to\beta}
in Definition
4.1
. Namely, the knowledge of
x\in\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)
allows to infer that

s\in I(x):=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\beta^{-i},\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\beta^{-i}+\frac{\beta^{-n}}{\beta-1}\right],

(199)

and further deduce that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\beta^{-i}\in J(x):=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\beta^{-i}-\frac{\beta^{-n}}{\beta-1},\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\beta^{-i}+\frac{\beta^{-n}}{\beta-1}\right],

(200)

for all
y\in\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)
. This leads to the definition of the following function.

Definition 6.1
.

For
\beta\in(1,2)
, define
g_{\beta}:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\rightrightarrows\{0,1\}^{\ast}
as

g_{\beta}(x):=\left\{y\in\{0,1\}^{n}:n=\left|x\right|,\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\beta^{-i}\in_{n}J(x)\right\},

(201)

for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
.

By the preceding discussion, we have

\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)\subseteq g_{\beta}(x),\ \forall x\in\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n).

(202)

Now, define
\tilde{g}_{\beta}:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\to\{0,1\}^{\ast}/\sim_{\beta}
by

\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x):=g_{\beta}(x)/\sim_{\beta},\ \forall x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast},

(203)

and

\tilde{}\mathcal{N}_{g_{\beta}}(x):=\#\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x),\ \forall x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}.

(204)

Note that the function defined by

\begin{array}[]{rccc}\varphi_{\beta}:&\{0,1\}^{n}/\sim_{\beta}&\to&I_{\beta}\\
&[x]_{\beta}&\mapsto&\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\beta^{-i}\end{array}

(205)

is injective into
I_{\beta}
, since by definiton of
\sim_{\beta}
,
[x]_{\beta}\neq[y]_{\beta}\iff\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\beta^{-i}\neq\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\beta^{-i}
, for all
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. We define an ordering
\prec
on
\{0,1\}^{n}/\sim_{\beta}
by

u\prec v\iff\varphi_{\beta}(u)<\varphi_{\beta}(v),\ \forall u,v\in\{0,1\}^{n}/\sim_{\beta}.

(206)

For
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
, we enumerate the elements of
\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)
as follows. For
i\in\{1,\ldots,\tilde{}\mathcal{N}_{g_{\beta}}(x)\}
, we let
\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)|_{i}\in\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)
, such that

\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)|_{i}<\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)|_{i+1},\ \forall i\in\{1,\ldots,\tilde{}\mathcal{N}_{g_{\beta}}(x)-1\}.

(207)

We can then express
\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)
simply in function of the elements of
\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
,
s\in[0,1]
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)
. Then, there exists
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,\tilde{}\mathcal{N}_{g_{\beta}}(x)\}
,
i\leq j
, such that

\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)=\bigcup_{\ell=i}^{j}\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)|_{\ell}.

(208)

Proof.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
,
s\in[0,1]
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
x\in\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)
. The proof is in two parts: we first prove that
x\in\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)\Rightarrow[x]_{\beta}\subseteq\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)
, and further that
[x]_{\beta}\prec[y]_{\beta}\prec[z]_{\beta}
together with
x,z\in\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)
implies
y\in\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)
.

(a)

Let
x\in\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)
. By Lemma
5.1
,
[x]_{\beta}\in\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)
. Hence,
\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)
is expressed as

\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)=\bigcup_{u\in X}u,

(209)

for some subset
X\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}/\sim_{\beta}
. Since by (
202
),
\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)\subseteq g_{\beta}(x)
, we get that
u\subseteq g_{\beta}(x)\Rightarrow u\in g_{\beta}(x)/\sim_{\beta}\ =\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)
, so there exists
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,\tilde{}\mathcal{N}_{g_{\beta}}(x)\}
such that
u=\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)|_{\ell}
, for all
u\in X
. Therefore,

\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)=\bigcup_{\ell\in L}\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)|_{\ell},

(210)

for some
L\subseteq\{1,\ldots,\tilde{}\mathcal{N}_{g_{\beta}}(x)\}
.

(b)

We now end the proof by showing that
L
is made of consecutive elements. Let
i,j,k\in\{1,\ldots,\tilde{}\mathcal{N}_{g_{\beta}}(x)\}
, such that
i\leq j\leq k
and suppose that
\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)|_{i},\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)|_{k}\subseteq\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)
. Then, for all
y\in u_{j}(x)
, all
u\in\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)|_{i}
and all
v\in\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)|_{k}
, we have

\sum_{m=1}^{n}u_{m}\beta^{-m}\leq\sum_{m=1}^{n}y_{m}\beta^{-m}\leq\sum_{m=1}^{n}v_{m}\beta^{-m}.

(211)

Since
\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)|_{i},\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)|_{k}\subseteq\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)
, we further get

s-\frac{\beta^{-n}}{\beta-1}\leq\sum_{m=1}^{n}u_{m}\beta^{-m}\leq\sum_{m=1}^{n}y_{m}\beta^{-m}\leq\sum_{m=1}^{n}v_{m}\beta^{-m}\leq s.

(212)

By (
65
) and (
66
), this implies that
y\in\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)
, so
\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)|_{j}\subseteq\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)
. We have proven that
i,k\in L\Rightarrow j\in L
for all
i\leq j\leq k
, i.e.,
L
is made of consecutive elements.

∎

For every
M\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
\mathcal{C}(M)
to be the set of all subset of consecutive elements in
\{1,\ldots,M\}
. In mathematical symbols, this is expressed as

\mathcal{C}(M):=\{\{i,\ldots,j\}:i,j\in\{1,\ldots,M\},i\leq j\}.

(213)

We are ready to state the definition of the computable function
f_{\beta,1\to all}
.

Definition 6.2
.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
. Define
\iota_{\beta}(x)
to be the unique natural number such that

x\in\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)|_{\iota_{\beta}(x)}.

(214)

Let
f_{\beta,1\to all}:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\rightrightarrows\{0,1\}^{\ast}
be defined as

f_{\beta,1\to all}(x):=\left\{\left\langle\bigcup_{i\in A}\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)|_{i}\right\rangle:A\in\mathcal{C}\left(\tilde{}\mathcal{N}_{g_{\beta}}(x)\right),\iota_{\beta}(x)\in A\right\},

(215)

for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
,
s\in I_{\beta}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,

\langle\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)\rangle\in f_{\beta,1\to all}(x),\ \forall x\in\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n).

(216)

Proof.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
,
s\in I_{\beta}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)
. Then, by Lemma
6.1
, there exists
A\in\mathcal{C}(\tilde{}\mathcal{N}_{g_{\beta}}(x))
such that

\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)=\bigcup_{i\in A}\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)|_{i}.

(217)

The Lemma follows immediately by Definition
6.2
of
f_{\beta,1\to all}
.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
be an algebraic number and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,

\#f_{\beta,1\to all}(x)\leq\left(\frac{1}{\beta-1}+2\right)^{2}\frac{n^{2k_{\beta}}}{(L_{\beta}\Pi_{\beta})^{2}}\left(L_{\beta}\Pi_{\beta}^{+}\right)^{2n},

(218)

for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.

Proof.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
be an algebraic number,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. By Lemma
4.3
, we get that

|\varphi_{\beta}(u)-\varphi_{\beta}(v)|\geq\frac{L_{\beta}\Pi_{\beta}}{n^{k_{\beta}}\left(\beta L_{\beta}\Pi_{\beta}^{+}\right)^{n}}=:m_{n},

(219)

for all
u,v\in\{0,1\}^{n}/\sim_{\beta}
with
u\neq v
. Then,

\displaystyle\#\left(\varphi_{\beta}(\{0,1\}^{n}/\sim_{\beta})\cap J(x)^{(n)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|J(x)^{(n)}|}{m_{n}}

(220)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{m_{n}}\left(\frac{\beta^{-n}}{\beta-1}+2\cdot 2^{-n}\right)

(221)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\beta^{-n}}{m_{n}}\left(\frac{1}{\beta-1}+2\right)\cdot

(222)

By injectivity of
\varphi_{\beta}
,

\displaystyle\#\varphi_{\beta}^{-1}\left(\varphi_{\beta}(\{0,1\}^{n}/\sim_{\beta})\cap J(x)^{(n)}\right)

\displaystyle=\#\left(\varphi_{\beta}(\{0,1\}^{n}/\sim_{\beta})\cap J(x)^{(n)}\right)

(223)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\beta^{-n}}{m_{n}}\left(\frac{1}{\beta-1}+2\right)\cdot

(224)

Hence, since by Definition
6.1
,
\tilde{g}_{\beta}(x)\subseteq\varphi_{\beta}^{-1}\left(\varphi_{\beta}(\{0,1\}^{n}/\sim_{\beta})\cap J(x)^{(n)}\right)
for
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, we get that

\tilde{}\mathcal{N}_{g_{\beta}}(x)\leq\frac{2\beta^{-n}}{m_{n}}\left(\frac{1}{\beta-1}+2\right)=\left(\frac{1}{\beta-1}+2\right)\frac{2n^{k_{\beta}}}{L_{\beta}\Pi_{\beta}}\left(L_{\beta}\Pi_{\beta}^{+}\right)^{n}.

(225)

Now, there remains to establish a bound on
\#f_{\beta,1\to all}(x)=\#\{A:A\in\mathcal{C}(\tilde{}\mathcal{N}_{g_{\beta}}(x)),\iota_{\beta}(x)\in A\}
, for
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. Let
M\in\mathbb{N}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,i\}
, and define the set
\mathcal{D}(M,i):=\{A:A\in\mathcal{C}(M),i\in A\}
. We will establish a general bound on
\#\mathcal{D}(M,i)
, which as a corollary will give a bound on
\#\mathcal{D}(\tilde{}\mathcal{N}_{g_{\beta}}(x),\iota_{\beta}(x))
. By symmetry, we can restrict ourselves to
i\leq M/2
without loss of generality. Define the sets
\mathcal{D}_{k}(M,i)=\{A\in\mathcal{D}(M,i):\#A=k\}
, for
k\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
. Note that

\#\mathcal{D}_{k}(M,i)=\min\{k,i,M-k\}.

(226)

Then,

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{D}(M,i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\#\mathcal{D}_{k}(M,i)=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\min\{k,i,M-k\}

(227)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}k+\sum_{k=i}^{M-i}i+\sum_{k=M-i+1}^{M}(M-k)

(228)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}+(M-2i+1)i=i(i-1)+(M-2i+1)i

(229)

\displaystyle=Mi-i^{2}.

(230)

Note that
Mi-i^{2}
is maximal for
i=M/2
. Therefore,

\#\mathcal{D}(M,i)\leq\frac{1}{4}M^{2}.

(231)

We conclude that

\displaystyle\#f_{\beta,1\to all}(x)

\displaystyle=\#\mathcal{D}(\tilde{}\mathcal{N}_{g_{\beta}}(x),\iota_{\beta}(x))\leq\frac{1}{4}\tilde{}\mathcal{N}^{2}_{g_{\beta}}(x)

(232)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{\beta-1}+2\right)^{2}\frac{n^{2k_{\beta}}}{((\beta-1)L_{\beta}\Pi_{\beta})^{2}}\left(L_{\beta}\Pi_{\beta}^{+}\right)^{2n},

(233)

for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.

6.2
An algorithm to extract the coin tosses from a
\beta
-expansion

In this part, we show that every
\beta
-expansion of a given number
s\in I_{\beta}
can be associated with a sequence of coin tosses generated by Algorithm
3
. We will then study the algorithmic complexity of
\beta
-expansions through the study of this sequence of coin tosses. Following
[
9
, Section 1.2]
, we first establish the equations that govern Algorithm
3
, given fixed input
\beta\in(1,2)
,
s\in[0,1]
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. First remark that Algorithm
3
generates two sequences
(b_{i})_{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}
and
(r_{i})_{i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}}
, that are recursively defined according to the following pattern. By lines 1 and 9 in Algorithm
3
, we get that

r_{0}=s,\ \ r_{i}=\beta r_{i-1}-b_{i},\ \forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}.

(234)

Further, lines 4-6 deliver

b_{i}=\begin{cases}0\ \text{if}\ r_{i-1}\in[0,\beta^{-1})=:E_{\beta}^{(0)}\\
\text{Randomly}\ 0\text{ or }1\ \text{if}\ r_{i-1}\in[\beta^{-1},((\beta-1)\beta)^{-1}]=:S_{\beta}\\
1\ \text{if}\ r_{i-1}\in(((\beta-1)\beta)^{-1},(\beta-1)^{-1}]=:E_{\beta}^{(1)}\end{cases}=:T_{\beta}(r_{i-1}).

(235)

Note that the recursion generating
(r_{i})_{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}
can be simply expressed by

r_{i}=\beta r_{i-1}-T_{\beta}(r_{i-1})=:K_{\beta}(r_{i-1}),\ \forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}.

(236)

The output
\mathcal{A}_{\beta}(s,n)
of the algorithm is given in line 8 by

\mathcal{A}_{\beta}(s,n)=\coprod_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}=\coprod_{i=1}^{n}T_{\beta}(r_{i-1})=\coprod_{i=0}^{n-1}T_{\beta}(r_{i}).

(237)

In order to formalize better the problem, we can define
T_{\beta}(r)
formally as a random variable on
\{0,1\}
for
r\in I_{\beta}
, i.e., as a function
T_{\beta}(r):\{0,1\}\to\{0,1\}
satisfying

T_{\beta}(r)(x):=\begin{cases}0,\ \text{if}\ s\in E_{\beta}^{(0)},\\
x,\ \text{if}\ s\in S_{\beta},\\
1,\ \text{if}\ s\in E_{\beta}^{(1)},\end{cases}\ \forall x\in\{0,1\},\ r\in I_{\beta}.

(238)

Since the generation of the sequence
(r_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
might rely on an arbitrarily many realisations of the above random variable, we define an extension
\tilde{T}_{\beta}
of
T_{\beta}
, that will be useful to consider Algorithm
3
as a whole to be a random variable. Namely, we define
\tilde{T}_{\beta}(r):\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\{0,1\}
by

\tilde{T}_{\beta}(r)({\bf x}):=\begin{cases}0,\ \text{if}\ s\in E_{\beta}^{(0)},\\
{\bf x}_{1},\ \text{if}\ s\in S_{\beta},\\
1,\ \text{if}\ s\in E_{\beta}^{(1)},\end{cases}\ \forall{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}},\ r\in I_{\beta}.

(239)

Thanks to the formulation above, we can see Algorithm
3
as first generating an infinite sequence
{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
out of infinitely many Bernoulli experiments, and then reading successively the outcomes of this experiment, i.e., at each time step, the algorithm reads
{\bf x}_{1}
, and then discards it, such that the sequence
{\bf x}
is transformed into the sequence
{\bf x}^{\prime}:={\bf x}_{2}{\bf x}_{3}\ldots
. This is usually denoted by
{\bf x}^{\prime}=\sigma({\bf x})
, where
\sigma:\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is called the left-shift. Then, in the next time step, the algorithm will read
{\bf x}^{\prime}_{1}={\bf x}_{2}
to generate the next bit of the
\beta
-expansion of
s
. Note that when
r_{i}\notin S_{\beta}
, then
\tilde{T}_{\beta}(r_{i})({\bf x})
does not depend on
{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, as it is generated deterministically. Hence, the Algorithm
3
does not need to discard
{\bf x}_{1}
, and wait until the next time step
i
for which
r_{i}\in S_{\beta}
to discard
{\bf x}_{1}
. Accordingly, we define
\sigma_{r}:\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
by

\sigma_{r}({\bf x}):=\begin{cases}{\bf x}\ \text{if}\ r\notin S_{\beta},\\
\sigma({\bf x}),\ \text{if}\ r\in S_{\beta},\end{cases}\ \forall r\in I_{\beta},\ {\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}.

(240)

We are now ready to state formally the definition of the Algorithm
3
as a random variable. First, we model
K_{\beta}(r)
defined in (
236
) as a random variable on
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
for
r\in I_{\beta}
, i.e., as a function
K_{\beta}(r):\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\to I_{\beta}\times\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, defined by

K_{\beta}(r)({\bf x}):=(\beta r-\tilde{T}_{\beta}(r)({\bf x}_{1}),\sigma_{r}({\bf x})),\ \forall{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}.

(241)

Note that thanks to the formalism introduced, we can also consider the sequences
(b_{i})_{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}
and
(r_{i})_{i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}}
as random variables, i.e., each elements is a function from
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
to
\{0,1\}
and
I_{\beta}
, respectively. We denote their respective elements
b_{i}({\bf x})
and
r_{i}({\bf x})
, for
{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. In order to simplify the notations, we might consider
\tilde{T}_{\beta}
as a function from
I_{\beta}\times\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
into
\{0,1\}
and
K_{\beta}
as a function from
I_{\beta}\times\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
into itself, by making the confusion between
\tilde{T}_{\beta}(s)({\bf x})
and
\tilde{T}_{\beta}(s,{\bf x})
, and the confusion between
K_{\beta}(s)({\bf x})
and
K_{\beta}(s,{\bf x})
. Note that this way, we get a simple equation for
r_{i}({\bf x})
in function of
{\bf x}
:

r_{i}({\bf x})=\pi_{1}K^{i}_{\beta}(s,{\bf x}),\ \forall{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}},

(242)

where
\pi_{1}
is the projection onto the first coordinate. This simpler formalism allows to define the output of the Algorithm
3
as a random variable as well, i.e., as a function
\mathcal{A}_{\beta}(s,n):\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
, by

\mathcal{A}_{\beta}(s,n)({\bf x})=\coprod_{i=0}^{n-1}\tilde{T}_{\beta}\left(K_{\beta}^{i}(s,{\bf x})\right),\ \forall{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}.

(243)

We repeat that Algorithm
3
can generate different outputs of the same input
s\in[0,1]
.
[
7
, Theorem 1]
establishes that infact, every
\beta
-expansion of
s
can actually be generated by this Algorithm. This is formally expressed by

\mathcal{A}_{\beta}(s,n)\left(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\right)=\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n).

(244)

We let also
\mathcal{N}_{\beta}(s,n):=\#\left(\Sigma_{\beta}(s,n)\right)
.
We now leverage the formalism introduced above to express every
\beta
-expansion as a decomposition into a binary expansion and a sequence of coin tosses. We start by showing that for every
\beta
-expansion of
s
, we can associate a sequence of coin tosses that generated it. First, define

\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(s,{\bf x}):=\{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:\pi_{1}K_{\beta}^{i}(s,{\bf x})\in S_{\beta}\},

(245)

\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x}):=\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(s,{\bf x})\cap\{0,\ldots,n-1\},

(246)

and

h_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x}):=\#\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x})\leq n,

(247)

for all
{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. We first note a simple technical Lemma.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
,
s\in I_{\beta}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Then,

h_{\beta}(s,n+1,{\bf x})=h_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x})+1_{n\in\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(s,{\bf x})},

(248)

and therefore,

\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x})}\{h_{\beta}(s,i+1,{\bf x})\}=\{1,\ldots,h_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x})\}.

(249)

Proof.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
,
s\in I_{\beta}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Suppose that
n\notin S_{\beta}(s,{\bf x})
. Then,

\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(s,n+1,{\bf x})=\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(s,{\bf x})\cap\{0,\ldots,n\}=\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(s,{\bf x})\cap\{0,\ldots,n-1\}=\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x}),

(250)

therefore
h_{\beta}(s,n+1,{\bf x})=h_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x})
. Suppose now that
n\in S_{\beta}(s,{\bf x})
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(s,n+1,{\bf x})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(s,{\bf x})\cap\{0,\ldots,n\}

(251)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(s,{\bf x})\cap\{0,\ldots,n-1\}\right)\cup\{n\}

(252)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x})\cup\{n\}\,

(253)

therefore
h_{\beta}(s,n+1,{\bf x})=h_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x})+1
. This yields (
248
). Let
i_{1}<i_{2}<\ldots<i_{h_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x})}
be the elements of
\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x})
. For all

h_{\beta}(s,i_{1}+1,{\bf x})=h_{\beta}(s,i_{1},{\bf x})+1=1
. Moreover, for all

(254)

which by induction on

(255)

Then,

\displaystyle\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x})}\{h_{\beta}(s,i+1,{\bf x})\}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{k=1}^{h_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x})}\{h_{\beta}(s,i_{k}+1,{\bf x})\}

(256)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{k=1}^{h_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x})}\{k\}

(257)

\displaystyle=\{1,\ldots,h_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x})\}.

(258)

∎

h_{\beta}(s,n-1,{\bf x})
is exactly the number of bits of
{\bf x}
that Algorithm
3
had to read before delivering its output. Then, every bit of
{\bf x}
that is localed after the rank

is not read by the algorithm to produce its output. This is formally expressed in the following Lemma and the subsequent Corollary.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
,

,

. Then,

\begin{cases}\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x})=\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x}_{1:h_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x})}{\bf y}),\\

\displaystyle\pi_{2}K^{n}(s,{\bf x})=\sigma^{h_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x})}({\bf x}),\end{cases}

(259)

\pi_{1}
(resp.

\beta\in(1,2)

(262)

for all

.

\displaystyle\pi_{1}K^{n}_{\beta}(s,{\bf x})=\pi_{1}K^{n}_{\beta}\left(s,{\bf x}_{1:h_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x})}{\bf y}\right)=:r_{n},\\
\displaystyle\pi_{2}K^{n}(s,{\bf x})=\sigma^{h_{\beta}(s,n,{\bf x})}({\bf x})=\sigma^{h_{n}}({\bf x}),\end{cases}

(
H_{n}

where

, for all
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}

{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}

{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle K^{n+1}_{\beta}(s,{\bf x})

(267)

(H_{n})

(b)

Corollary 6.6

Lemma 6.7

Let
\beta\in(1,2)

Let
\beta\in(1,2)
,

,

(a)

(b)

{\bf v}

\displaystyle i-1\overset{(a)}{=}h_{\beta}(s,n_{i},{\bf u})

\displaystyle r_{i}:=\pi_{1}K_{\beta}^{n_{i}}(s,{\bf u})

where (a) follows by

Lemma 6.8

\beta\in(1,2)

Appendix A
Appendix on weak-star limits

In this part, we collect useful statements that help the understanding of weak limit measures. Let
X
be a locally compact Hausdorff topological space (see
[
21
, p31, p104]
). We denote by
\mathcal{B}
the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
X
, i.e., the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the open subsets of
X
(see
[
16
, Section 15]
). We recall the definition of a regular Borel measure.

Definition A.1
.

[
16
, Section 52]

A regular Borel measure
\mu:\mathcal{B}\to[0,\infty]
is a measure that is finite on compact sets, i.e.,
\mu(C)<\infty
for every compact set
C
, and that satisfies

\mu(A)=\inf\{\mu(U):A\subseteq U,U\text{ is open}\}=\mathop{\operatorname{sup}}\{\mu(U):U\subseteq A,U\text{ is compact}\}.

(400)

Regular Borel measures are a central element in the following Lemmata, that are key ingredients of the proof of Theorem
4.1
. Define the set of compactly supported continuous functions from
X
to
\mathbb{R}
as
[
16
, Section 55]

\mathcal{C}_{c}(X):=\left\{f:X\to\mathbb{R}:f\text{ is continuous, and }\overline{\{x:f(x)\neq 0\}}\text{ is compact}\right\}.

(401)

Note that if
X
is compact,
\mathcal{C}_{c}(X)
is simply the set of continuous functions.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
(\mu_{n}:\mathcal{B}\to[0,\infty])_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of regular Borel measures converging weakly, i.e.,
\left(\int_{X}fd\mu_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is converging in
\mathbb{R}
, for every
f\in\mathcal{C}_{c}(X)
. Then, there exists a regular Borel measure
\mu:\mathcal{B}\to[0,\infty]
such that

\int_{X}fd\mu=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{X}fd\mu_{n},\ \forall f\in\mathcal{C}_{c}(X).

(402)

Proof.

A map
\Lambda:\mathcal{C}_{c}(X)\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be positive if
\Lambda(f)\geq 0
when
f\geq 0
and linear if
\Lambda(\alpha f+g)=\alpha\Lambda(f)+\Lambda(g)
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
f,g\in\mathcal{C}_{c}(X)
. For every measure
\mu:\mathcal{B}\to[0,\infty]
, the functional defined by
\Lambda_{\mu}:f\mapsto\int_{X}fd\mu
is obviously positive and linear. Define the functional
\Lambda:\mathcal{C}_{c}(X)\to\mathbb{R}
as

\Lambda(f):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{X}fd\mu_{n},\ \forall f\in\mathcal{C}_{c}(X).

(403)

\Lambda
is well-defined since
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly. Moreover,
\Lambda
is obviously positive and linear, by positivity and linearity of the limit. Further, there is a standard result, known as Riesz representation theorem, that states that for every positive linear functional
\Lambda:\mathcal{C}_{c}(X)\to\mathbb{R}
, there exists a unique regular Borel measure
\mu
such that
\Lambda=\Lambda_{\mu}
(see
[
16
, Section 56, Theorems D and E]
). This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Suppose that
X
is compact. Let
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of regular Borel measures converging weakly to a Borel regular measure
\nu
, i.e.,

\int_{X}fd\mu=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{X}fd\mu_{n},\ \forall f\in\mathcal{C}_{c}(X).

(404)

Then, for every compact set
C
, we have

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(C)\leq\mu(C).

(405)

Proof.

Let
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of regular Borel measures converging weakly to the Borel regular measure
\mu
and
C
be a closed set. Let
U
be an open set, so that
C\subseteq U
. Since
U
is open, then
X\backslash U
is closed and disjoint from
C
. Since
X
is compact and Hausdorff, then it is normal (see
[
21
, p111]
and
[
21
, Exercise 4.78]
), so we can apply Urysohn’s Lemma
[
21
, Lemma 4.82]
: there exists a continuous function
f_{U}:X\to[0,1]
such that
f(C)=\{1\}
and
f(X\backslash U)=\{0\}
. Note that since

\chi_{C}(x)\leq f_{U}(x)\leq\chi_{U}(x),\ \forall x\in X,

(406)

where
\chi_{A}
denotes the characteristic function of
A\in\mathcal{B}
, then

\mu_{n}(C)=\int_{X}\chi_{C}d\mu_{n}\leq\int_{X}f_{U}d\mu_{n},

(407)

and therefore

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(C)\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{X}f_{U}d\mu_{n}=\int_{X}f_{U}d\mu\leq\int_{X}\chi_{U}d\mu=\mu(U).

(408)

Since the left-hand side of the inequality does not depend on
U
, we get

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(C)\leq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\text{ open},\\
U\supseteq C\end{subarray}}\mu(U)=\mu(C),

(409)

where the last equality follows by Borel regularity of
\mu
.
∎

Since
\mathbb{R}
is a locally compact Hausdorff topological space, and every closed interval
[a,b]
,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
, we can use the above Lemma to prove Theorem
4.1
.

In this section, we prove Lemma

Lemma B.1

Let

\mathbb{Q}[X]

We show the contraposition of the statement. Let

. First, note that since

is irreducible in
\mathbb{Q}[X]

\mathbb{Q}[X]
are either polynomials of the form
\alpha P
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
, or constant polynomials in
\mathbb{Q}[X]
. Then, the greatest common dividors of
P
and
Q

P

\alpha P
or constant polynomials. In particular, this means that either all the greatest common dividors of
P
and
Q
in
\mathbb{Q}[X]
are of degree
\deg P
, or they all are of degree
0
.

Now, assume that one root
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
of

Q
, then we know that the polynomial
X-\alpha
divides both
P
and
Q
in
\mathbb{C}[X]
, hence the greatest common dividors of
P
and
Q
in
\mathbb{C}[X]
are at least of degree
1
. There is a standard result in algebra that states that for any two fields
\mathbb{K},\mathbb{L}
satisfying
\mathbb{K}\subseteq\mathbb{L}
, the greatest common dividors of two polynomials in
\mathbb{K}[X]
are also greastest common dividors in
\mathbb{L}[X]

[
24
, Section III-8, Proposition 3]
. In particular, this means that if the greatest common dividors of

and

in
\mathbb{L}[X]
are of degree
d

P
and
Q
in
\mathbb{K}[X]
also are of degree
d
. Since
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{C}
are fields such that
\mathbb{Q}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, then by the discussion above the degree of the greatest common dividors of
P
and
Q
in
\mathbb{Q}[X]
are of degree at least
1
, which implies that they must be of the form
\alpha P
,

such that

of
P
, we have

(410)

i.e.,

is a root of

.
∎
