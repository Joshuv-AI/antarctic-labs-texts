Abstract
. The first aim of this article is to give information about the algebraic properties of alternate bases 𝜷 = ( β 0 , … , β p − 1 ) \boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{p-1}) determining sofic systems. We show that a necessary condition is that the product δ = ∏ i = 0 p − 1 β i \delta=\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\beta_{i} is an algebraic integer and all of the bases β 0 , … , β p − 1 \beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1} belong to the algebraic field ℚ ⁡ ( δ ) \mathbb{Q}(\delta) . On the other hand, we also give a sufficient condition: if δ \delta is a Pisot number and β 0 , … , β p − 1 ∈ ℚ ⁡ ( δ ) \beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta) , then the system associated with the alternate base 𝜷 = ( β 0 , … , β p − 1 ) \boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{p-1}) is sofic. The second aim of this paper is to provide an analogy of Frougny’s result concerning normalization of real bases representations. We show that given an alternate base 𝜷 = ( β 0 , … , β p − 1 ) \boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{p-1}) such that δ \delta is a Pisot number and β 0 , … , β p − 1 ∈ ℚ ⁡ ( δ ) \beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta) , the normalization function is computable by a finite Büchi automaton, and furthermore, we effectively construct such an automaton. An important tool in our study is the spectrum of numeration systems associated with alternate bases. The spectrum of a real number δ > 1 \delta>1 and an alphabet A ⊂ ℤ A\subset{\mathbb{Z}} was introduced by Erdős et al. For our purposes, we use a generalized concept with δ ∈ ℂ \delta\in{\mathbb{C}} and A ⊂ ℂ A\subset{\mathbb{C}} and study its topological properties.

1.
Introduction

Alternate bases are particular cases of Cantor real bases, which were introduced by the first two authors in
[
6
]
and then studied in
[
7
,
8
]
. A Cantor real base is a sequence
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of real numbers greater than
1
such that
\prod_{n=0}^{+\infty}\beta_{n}=+\infty
. A
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representation of a real number
x\in[0,1]
is an infinite sequence
a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots
of non-negative integers such that

x=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{\prod_{k=0}^{n}\beta_{k}}.

When choosing a constant sequence of bases
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta,\beta,\ldots)
, we obtain the well-known Rényi numeration system associated with one real base
\beta

[
19
]
. Moreover, when choosing a sequence of integer bases
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we obtain the so-called Cantor expansions of real numbers
[
5
]
. Thus, Cantor real bases generalize both expansions in a real base and Cantor expansions.

Two particular
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representations of
x\in[0,1]
can be obtained thanks to the greedy and lazy algorithms. In
[
6
]
, the classical combinatorial results of the greedy
\beta
-expansions were generalized to the framework of Cantor re al bases. In
[
8
]
, the lazy combinatorial properties of Cantor real bases were investigated. Moreover, in
[
7
]
, the classical dynamical results of greedy and lazy
\beta
-expansions were generalized while focusing on periodic Cantor real bases
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1},\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1},\ldots)
. Such Cantor real bases are called alternate bases and are simply denoted by
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1})
. Since one base numeration systems as defined by Rényi have been studied extensively from many aspects, a lot of other questions can be investigated in the framework of Cantor real bases and in particular, of alternate bases. In this paper, we study some algebraic properties of alternate base expansions.

Representations of real numbers involving more than one base simultaneously and independently aroused the interest of other mathematicians
[
4
,
14
,
16
,
23
]
.
But so far, most of the research was concentrated on the combinatorial properties of these representations as in
[
6
]
and not on their dynamical or algebraic properties which are studied respectively in
[
7
]
and in this paper.

The dynamical point of view of real base expansions was the focus of Bertrand-Mathis
[
3
]
who showed that the numeration system with base
\beta>1
defines a
\beta
-shift which is sofic, i.e., its factors form a language that is accepted by a finite automaton, if and only if the
\beta
-expansion of
1
is eventually periodic. Real bases
\beta
determining sofic
\beta
-shifts are called Parry numbers. An algebraic description of Parry numbers is not obvious. It is known that every Parry number is a Perron number, i.e., an algebraic integer
\beta>1
whose conjugates are in modulus smaller than
\beta
. On the other hand, the set of Parry numbers includes Pisot numbers, i.e., algebraic integers greater than
1
with conjugates inside the unit circle
[
2
]
. More detailed information on algebraic conjugates of a Parry number
\beta
was given by Solomyak
[
21
]
.

An analogue of the result of Bertrand-Mathis for alternate bases
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{p-1})
was proved in
[
6
]
. Namely, it is proved that the alternate base system defines a sofic
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-shift if and only if each of the
p
greedy
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}
-expansions of
1
is eventually periodic where
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}=(\beta_{i},\ldots,\beta_{p-1},\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{i-1})
. The first aim of this article is to give information about the algebraic properties of alternate bases
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{p-1})
determining sofic systems. In particular, in Theorem
14
we show a necessary condition, namely that the product
\delta=\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\beta_{i}
is an algebraic integer and all of the bases
\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1}
belong to the algebraic field
\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
. On the other hand, in Theorem
25
, we also give a sufficient condition: if
\delta
is a Pisot number and
\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
, then the
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-shift is sofic.

The importance of the class of Pisot bases in connection to automata was pointed out also by Frougny
[
11
]
who showed that normalization in a real base
\beta>1
which maps any
\beta
-representation of a real number in
[0,1)
to its greedy
\beta
-expansion is computable by a finite Büchi automaton if
\beta
is a Pisot number.
The second aim of this paper is to provide an analogy of Frougny’s result concerning normalization. In Theorem
32
, we show that given an alternate base
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{p-1})
such that
\delta=\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\beta_{i}
is a Pisot number and
\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
, the normalization function is computable by a finite Büchi automaton.

An important tool in our proofs is the spectrum of numeration systems associated with alternate bases. Its definition shows that one needs to consider the spectrum of
\delta=\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\beta_{i}
with a more general alphabet of non-integer digits. Hence, we first study the spectrum in the general framework of a complex base
\delta
such that
|\delta|>1
with an alphabet
A\subset\mathbb{C}
, which is defined as

X^{A}(\delta)=\{\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}\delta^{i}:n\in\mathbb{N},a_{i}\in A\}.

The notion of spectrum was originally introduced by Erdős, Joó and Komornik for a base
\delta\in(1,2)
and an alphabet of the form
A=\{0,1,\dots,m\}

[
9
]
. Topological properties of the spectrum determine many of the arithmetical aspects of the numeration system; see
[
12
]
. One of the main problems in the study of spectra is to describe bases which give spectra without accumulation points in dependence on the alphabet. For the case of real bases and symmetric integer alphabets, a complete characterization was given by Akiyama and Komornik
[
1
]
and Feng
[
10
]
.

In this paper, as an analogy to the results of
[
12
]
, we prove in Theorem
8
that the set of representations of zero in a complex base
\delta
such that
|\delta|>1
and an alphabet
A
of complex number is accepted by a finite Büchi automaton if and only if the spectrum
X^{A}(\delta)
has no accumulation point. Next, we deduce an analogue of Theorem
8
in the alternate base case, namely Theorem
13
. This result makes use of a Büchi automaton called the zero automaton which generalizes that defined by Frougny
[
11
]
and which is intimately linked with the Büchi automaton computing the normalization in alternate bases.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we first fix some notation and we provide the necessary background for a clear understanding of this work. Then, in Section
3
, we study the spectrum
X^{A}(\delta)
of a complex base
\delta
over an arbitrary alphabet
A
of complex numbers. In doing so, we also define and study the set
Z(\delta,A)
of
\delta
-representations of zero over
A
and an associated zero Büchi automaton
\mathcal{Z}(\delta,A)
. In Section
4
, we define the spectrum associated with an alternate base
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{p-1})
as a particular case of the complex spectra studied in the previous section. We then prove that the alternate base spectrum has no accumulation point if and only if the set of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representations of zero is accepted by a finite Büchi automaton, and furthermore, if and only if the alternate zero automaton is finite. Sections
5
and
6
are concerned with the algebraic properties of the alternate base
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{p-1})
determining sofic systems. In particular, Section
5
and Section
6
respectively give the necessary and the sufficient conditions stated above. In Section
8
, we show that if
\delta=\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\beta_{i}
is a Pisot number and each of the bases
\beta_{i}
belongs to the algebraic field
\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
then the normalization in the alternate base
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is computable by a finite Büchi automaton, and we effectively construct such an automaton. We end this paper with some open questions.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout this text, an interval of non-negative integers
\{i,\ldots,j\}
with
i\leq j
is denoted
[\![i,j]\!]
and
\left\lfloor\cdot\right\rfloor
and
\left\lceil\cdot\right\rceil
respectively denote the floor and ceiling functions.

2.1.
Numbers

An
algebraic number
is a complex number that is a root of a monic polynomial (a polynomial whose leading coefficient is 1) with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}
. The
minimal polynomial
of an algebraic number
\beta
is the monic polynomial of minimal degree that is annihilated by
\beta
. It is an irreducible polynomial over
\mathbb{Q}
and its degree is the
degree
of the algebraic number
\beta
. Roots of the same irreducible polynomial over
\mathbb{Q}
are distinct and are said to be
algebraically conjugate
.

An algebraic number is an
algebraic integer
if it is a root of a monic polynomial in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
. It can be shown that its minimal polynomial has also integer coefficients, and thus a rational number which is not an integer is never an algebraic integer. The set of all algebraic integers is closed under addition, subtraction and multiplication and therefore is a commutative subring of the complex numbers.

A
Pisot number
is an algebraic integer
\beta>1
whose Galois conjugates all have modulus less than
1
. In particular, every integer is a Pisot number.

The smallest subfield of the field
\mathbb{C}
of complex numbers containing a complex number
\beta
is denoted by
\mathbb{Q}(\beta)
. If
\beta
is an algebraic number of degree
d
then this field is of the form

\mathbb{Q}(\beta)=\{\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}a_{j}\beta^{j}:a_{j}\in\mathbb{Q}\}.

Let
\beta
be an algebraic number of degree
d
and let
\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{d}
be its Galois conjugates. We set
\beta_{1}=\beta
. Then for all
k\in[\![1,d]\!]
, the fields
\mathbb{Q}(\beta)
and
\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{k})
are isomorphic by the isomorphism

\psi_{k}\colon\mathbb{Q}(\beta)\to\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{k}),\ \sum_{j=0}^{d-1}a_{j}\beta^{j}\mapsto\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}a_{j}(\beta_{k})^{j}.

The norm
N_{\mathbb{Q}(\beta)/\mathbb{Q}}
on the algebraic field
\mathbb{Q}(\beta)
is defined by

N_{\mathbb{Q}(\beta)/\mathbb{Q}}\colon\mathbb{Q}(\beta)\to\mathbb{Q},\ x\mapsto\prod_{k=1}^{d}\psi_{k}(x).

Whenever
x
is an algebraic integer in
\mathbb{Q}(\beta)
, the rational
N_{\mathbb{Q}(\beta)/\mathbb{Q}}(x)
is actually an integer.

2.2.
Words

We make use of common notions in formal language theory. An
alphabet
is a finite set of symbols, called
letters
, or, in the context of numeration systems,
digits
. A finite (resp. infinite)
word
is a finite (resp. infinite) concatenation of letters. The length of a finite word is the number of its letters. The set of all finite words over an alphabet
A
equipped with the binary operation of concatenation and the empty word as the neutral element is the monoid
A^{*}
. A subset of
A^{*}
is called a
language
over
A
. The set of infinite words over
A
is denoted
A^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Throughout this text, the letters of a word
a
are denoted by
a_{n}
and are indexed from
0
, so that
a=a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots
if
a
is an infinite word and
a=a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{\ell-1}
if
a
is a finite word of length
\ell
. If a word
a\in A^{*}\cup A^{\mathbb{N}}
can be written as
a=uvw
for some
u,v\in A^{*}
,
w\in A^{*}\cup A^{\mathbb{N}}
then
v
is a
factor
and
u
is a
prefix
of the word
a
. The language of factors and prefixes of a given word
w
are denoted by
\mathrm{Fac}(w)
and
\mathrm{Pref}(w)
respectively.

A finite word
w
written as
k
-fold repetition of its factor
v
is denoted
w=v^{k}=v\cdots v
. Similarly, infinite repetition of the factor
v
is
v^{\omega}=vvv\cdots
. An infinite word
a
of the form
a=uv^{\omega}
for some
u,v\in A^{*}
is called
eventually periodic
. In the case where
u
is the empty word,
a
is said to be
purely periodic
.

2.3.
Automata

An
automaton
is a quintuplet
\mathcal{A}=(Q,I,F,A,E)
where
Q
is the set of
states
,
I\subseteq Q
is the set of
initial states
,
F\subseteq Q
is the set of
final states
,
A
is an alphabet, and
E\subseteq Q\times A\times Q
is the set of
transitions
. The automaton
\mathcal{A}
is said to be
deterministic
if the transition relation
E
is actually a function from
Q\times A
to
Q
, in which case we write
E\colon Q\times A\to Q
. The automaton
\mathcal{A}
is said to be
finite
if the set of states
Q
is finite (since an alphabet is always considered to be finite in this text). A finite word
a_{0}\cdots a_{\ell-1}
over the alphabet
A
is
accepted by
\mathcal{A}
if it is the label of a
successful path in
\mathcal{A}
, i.e., if there exists
q_{0},\ldots,q_{\ell}\in Q
such that
q_{0}\in I
,
q_{\ell}\in F
and for all
n\in[\![0,\ell-1]\!]
, we have
(q_{n},a_{n},q_{n+1})\in E
.

Büchi automata
are defined as classical automata except for the acceptance criterion which has to be adapted in order to deal with infinite words: an infinite word is accepted if it labels an initial path going infinitely many times through a final state. A major difference between Büchi and classical automata is that a set of infinite words accepted by a finite Büchi automaton is not necessarily accepted by a deterministic one. For more on Büchi automata, we refer the reader to
[
18
]
.

2.4.
Spectrum and set of
\beta
-representations of zero

For a real number
\beta>1
and
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
we let
Z(\beta,d)
denote the set of
\beta
-representations of zero over the alphabet
[\![-d,d]\!]
:

Z(\beta,d)=\{a\in[\![-d,d]\!]^{\mathbb{N}}:\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{\beta^{n+1}}=0\}.

The
d
-spectrum of
\beta
is the set

X^{d}(\beta)=\{\sum_{n=0}^{\ell-1}a_{n}\beta^{\ell-1-n}:\ell\in\mathbb{N},\ a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell-1}\in[\![-d,d]\!]\}.

The following theorem linking these two sets was proved in
[
12
]
.

Theorem 1

(Frougny and Pelantová
[
12
]
)
.

Let
\beta>1
and
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
Z(\beta,d)
is accepted by a finite Büchi automaton if and only if the spectrum
X^{d}(\beta)
has no accumulation point in
\mathbb{R}
.

The next result is due to Akiyama and Komornik
[
1
]
and Feng
[
10
]
.

Theorem 2

(Akiyama and Komornik
[
1
]
, Feng
[
10
]
)
.

Let
\beta>1
and
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
The spectrum
X^{d}(\beta)
has an accumulation point in
\mathbb{R}
if and only if
\beta-1<d
and
\beta
is not a Pisot number.

The following result is a direct consequence of Theorems
1
and
2
, as noticed in
[
12
]
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\beta>1
. The following assertions are equivalent.

(1)

The set
Z(\beta,d)
is accepted by a finite Büchi automaton for all
d\geq 0
.

(2)

The set
Z(\beta,d)
is accepted by a finite Büchi automaton for one
d\geq\left\lceil\beta\right\rceil-1
.

(3)

\beta
is a Pisot number.

2.5.
Alternate bases

Let
p
be a positive integer and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1})
be a
p
-tuple of real numbers greater than
1
. Such a
p
-tuple
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is called an
alternate base
and
p
is called its
length
. A
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representation
of a real number
x
is an infinite sequence
a=a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots
of integers such that

(2.1)

x=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\frac{a_{mp+i}}{(\beta_{0}\cdots\beta_{p-1})^{m}\beta_{0}\cdots\beta_{i}}.

We use the convention that for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\beta_{n}=\beta_{n\bmod p}
and

\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}=(\beta_{n},\ldots,\beta_{n+p-1}).

Therefore, the equality (
2.1
) can be rewritten as

x=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{\prod_{k=0}^{n}\beta_{k}}.

In order to simplify notation, we sometimes write
\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(a)
in order to designate the second term of the previous equality.

For
x\in[0,1]
, a distinguished
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representation
\varepsilon_{0}(x)\varepsilon_{1}(x)\varepsilon_{2}(x)\cdots
, called the
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion
of
x
, is obtained from the
greedy algorithm
:

•

\varepsilon_{0}(x)=\left\lfloor\beta_{0}x\right\rfloor
and
r_{0}(x)=\beta_{0}x-\varepsilon_{0}(x)

•

\varepsilon_{n}(x)=\left\lfloor\beta_{n}r_{n-1}(x)\right\rfloor
and
r_{n}=\beta_{n}r_{n-1}(x)-\varepsilon_{n}(x)
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
.

The
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of
x
is denoted
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x)
. Clearly, the
n
-th digit
\varepsilon_{n}(x)
takes value in
\{0,1,\dots,\lfloor\beta_{n}\rfloor\}
. The numbers
r_{n}(x)
computed by the greedy algorithm are called
remainders
. The
quasi-greedy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of
1
, denoted
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(1)
, is the
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representation of
1
given by
\lim_{x\to 1}d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x)
(where the limit is taken with respect to the product topology on infinite words).

The following theorem characterizing the admissible sequences is an analogue of Parry’s theorem
[
17
]
.

Theorem 4

(Charlier and Cisternino
[
6
]
)
.

An infinite word
a
over
\mathbb{N}
is the
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of some real number in the interval
[0,1)
if and only if
a_{n}a_{n+1}\cdots<_{\mathrm{lex}}d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(n)}}^{*}(1)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

The
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-shift associated with an alternate base
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is defined as the topological closure (with respect to the product topology of infinite words) of the union
\bigcup_{i=0}^{p-1}\{d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(i)}}(x)\colon x\in[0,1)\}
. Generalizing a celebrated result of Bertrand-Mathis for real bases
[
3
]
, Theorem
4
was used in
[
6
]
for showing that the
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-shift is sofic if and only if
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}^{*}(1)
is eventually periodic for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
. We refer to such bases as the
Parry alternate bases
.

3.
Spectrum and representations of zero in complex bases

In this section, we generalize Theorem
1
to the context of complex bases and general alphabets of complex digits. Here, a complex base is a complex number
\delta
such that
|\delta|>1
. For a complex base
\delta
and an alphabet
A
of complex numbers, we define the set of
\delta
-representations of zero over
A
by

Z(\delta,A)=\{a\in A^{\mathbb{N}}:\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{\delta^{n+1}}=0\}

and the
spectrum of
\delta
over the alphabet
A
by

(3.1)

X^{A}(\delta)=\{\sum_{n=0}^{\ell-1}a_{n}\delta^{\ell-1-n}:\ell\in\mathbb{N},\ a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell-1}\in A\}.

We say that a word
a_{0}\cdots a_{\ell-1}
over
A

corresponds
to the element
\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}a_{i}\delta^{\ell-1-i}
in the spectrum
X^{A}(\delta)
. For the remaining part of this section, we consider a fixed complex base
\delta
and a fixed alphabet
A\subseteq\mathbb{C}
.

We also define a relation
\sim_{\delta,A}
over
A^{*}
: for
a,b\in A^{*}
, we set
a\sim_{\delta,A}b
whenever for all
s\in A^{\mathbb{N}}
, we have
as\in Z(\delta,A)\iff bs\in Z(\delta,A)
. Obviously, this is a right congruence over
A^{*}
and
A^{*}\setminus\mathrm{Pref}(Z(\delta,A))
is one of its equivalence classes. In the context of real bases
\beta
and integer digits, this right congruence may be interpreted in terms of the remainders of the Euclidean division of polynomials in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
by
x-\beta
; see
[
11
]
. This interpretation is no longer possible in the present context of complex digits.

Lemma 5
.

Let
a,b\in\mathrm{Pref}(Z(\delta,A))
be such that
|a|=k
and
|b|=\ell
. We have
a\sim_{\delta,A}b
if and only if

\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}a_{n}\delta^{k-1-n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\ell-1}b_{n}\delta^{\ell-1-n},

that is, the words
a
and
b
correspond to the same element in the spectrum
X^{A}(\delta)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
a\sim_{\delta,A}b
. Since
a
and
b
belong to the set
\mathrm{Pref}(Z(\delta,A))
, there exists
s\in A^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
as,bs\in Z(\delta,A)
. We get

-\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{s_{n}}{\delta^{n+1}}=\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}a_{n}\delta^{k-1-n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\ell-1}b_{n}\delta^{\ell-1-n}.

Conversely, suppose that
a\not\sim_{\delta,A}b
. Without loss of generality we can suppose that
as\in Z(\delta,A)
and
bs\notin Z(\delta,A)
for some
s\in A^{\mathbb{N}}
. Then

-\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{s_{n}}{\delta^{n+1}}=\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}a_{n}\delta^{k-1-n}\neq\sum_{n=0}^{\ell-1}b_{n}\delta^{\ell-1-n}.

∎

Lemma 6
.

If the spectrum
X^{A}(\delta)
has an accumulation point in
\mathbb{C}
then there exists an infinite word in
Z(\delta,A)
with pairwise non equivalent prefixes with respect to the right congruence
\sim_{\delta,A}
. In particular, the right congruence
\sim_{\delta,A}
has infinitely many classes.

Proof.

Suppose that the spectrum
X^{A}(\delta)
has a complex accumulation point. Then there exists an injective sequence
(x^{(j)})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
in
X^{A}(\delta)
such that
\lim_{j\to+\infty}x^{(j)}
is finite. For each
j\in\mathbb{N}
we let
\rho(j)
denote the minimal exponent such that there exists a representation of
x^{(j)}
in the form
x^{(j)}_{0}\cdots x^{(j)}_{\rho(j)-1}\in A^{*}
, that is

x^{(j)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\rho(j)-1}x_{n}^{(j)}\delta^{\rho(j)-1-n}.

Obviously, the sequence
(\rho(j))_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is unbounded, and without loss of generality we can assume that
(\rho(j))_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is strictly increasing. Thus
\lim_{j\to+\infty}\rho(j)=+\infty
and we get

(3.2)

\lim_{j\to+\infty}\frac{x^{(j)}}{\delta^{\rho(j)}}=\lim_{j\to+\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\rho(j)-1}\frac{x_{n}^{(j)}}{\delta^{n+1}}=0.

With this, we will show the existence of the desired
\delta
-representation
a
of zero over
A
. Set
a_{0}
as a digit in
A
which occurs infinitely many times among
x_{0}^{(j)}
with
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
Inductively, for
n\geq 1
, set
a_{n}
as a digit in
A
which occurs infinitely many times among
x_{n}^{(j)}
, where
j\in\mathbb{N}
runs through the indices such that
x_{0}^{(j)}\cdots x_{n-1}^{(j)}=a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1}
. By (
3.2
), we get that

\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{\delta^{n+1}}=0,

that is, that
a=a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots
belongs to the set
Z(\delta,A)
.

We will show that no pair of distinct prefixes of the infinite word
a
belong to the same equivalence class. To show this by contradiction, we consider
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a_{0}\cdots a_{k-1}\sim_{\delta,A}a_{0}\cdots a_{\ell-1}
with
k>\ell
. By construction, there exists
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a_{0}\cdots a_{k-1}
is a prefix of
x^{(j)}_{0}\cdots x^{(j)}_{\rho(j)-1}
. Moreover, by Lemma
5
, we get

\displaystyle x^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}a_{n}\delta^{\rho(j)-1-n}+\sum_{n=k}^{\rho(j)-1}x^{(j)}_{n}\delta^{\rho(j)-1-n}

\displaystyle=\delta^{\rho(j)-k}\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}a_{n}\delta^{k-1-n}+\sum_{n=k}^{\rho(j)-1}x^{(j)}_{n}\delta^{\rho(j)-1-n}

\displaystyle=\delta^{\rho(j)-k}\sum_{n=0}^{\ell-1}a_{n}\delta^{\ell-1-n}+\sum_{n=k}^{\rho(j)-1}x^{(j)}_{n}\delta^{\rho(j)-1-n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\ell-1}a_{n}\delta^{\rho(j)-k+\ell-1-n}+\sum_{n=\ell}^{\rho(j)-k+\ell-1}x^{(j)}_{n+k-\ell}\delta^{\rho(j)-k+\ell-1-n}.

Thus, we have found a representation
a_{0}\cdots a_{\ell-1}x^{(j)}_{k}\cdots x^{(j)}_{\rho(j)-1}
of
x^{(j)}
which is shorter than
x^{(j)}_{0}\cdots x^{(j)}_{\rho(j)-1}
. This contradicts the definition of
\rho(j)
.
∎

Similarly as what is done in
[
11
]
, we define a Büchi automaton, which we call the
zero automaton
\mathcal{Z}(\delta,A)=(Q,0,Q,A,E)
. The set of states is
Q=X^{A}(\delta)\cap\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq\frac{M}{|\delta|-1}\}
where
M=\max\{|a|:a\in A\}
, and the transitions are given by the triplets
(z,a,z\delta+a)
in
Q\times A\times Q
.

Proposition 7
.

The zero automaton
\mathcal{Z}(\delta,A)
accepts the set
Z(\delta,A)
.

Proof.

Let
a
be an infinite word accepted by
\mathcal{Z}(\delta,A)
. For each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, the prefix
a_{0}\cdots a_{\ell-1}
labels a path in
\mathcal{Z}(\delta,A)
from the initial state
0
to the state
\sum_{n=0}^{\ell-1}a_{n}\delta^{\ell-1-n}
, i.e., its corresponding element in the spectrum
X^{A}(\delta)
. By definition of the set of states
Q
, we get that the sequence
(\sum_{n=0}^{\ell-1}a_{n}\delta^{\ell-1-n})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded. Hence, we obtain that

\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{\delta^{n+1}}=\lim_{\ell\to+\infty}\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{\ell-1}a_{n}\delta^{\ell-1-n}}{\delta^{\ell}}=0.

Conversely, consider an infinite word
a
over
A
that is not accepted by
\mathcal{Z}(\delta,A)
. Then there exists
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|\sum_{n=0}^{\ell-1}a_{n}\delta^{\ell-1-n}|>\frac{M}{|\delta|-1}
. Then

\left|\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{\delta^{n+1}}\right|\geq\left|\sum_{n=0}^{\ell-1}\frac{a_{n}}{\delta^{n+1}}\right|-\sum_{n=\ell}^{+\infty}\frac{M}{|\delta|^{n+1}}=\frac{\left|\sum_{n=0}^{\ell-1}a_{n}\delta^{\ell-n-1}\right|-\frac{M}{|\delta|-1}}{|\delta|^{\ell}}>0.

∎

We are now ready to state and prove the main theorem of this section, which is a generalization of Theorem
1
. This solves a problem that was left open in
[
12
]
.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\delta
be a complex number such that
|\delta|>1
and let
A
be an alphabet of complex numbers. Then the following assertions are equivalent.

(1)

The set
Z(\delta,A)
is accepted by a finite Büchi automaton.

(2)

The right congruence
\sim_{\delta,A}
has finitely many classes.

(3)

The spectrum
X^{A}(\delta)
has no accumulation point in
\mathbb{C}
.

(4)

The zero automaton
\mathcal{Z}(\delta,A)
is finite.

Proof.

Suppose that
Z(\delta,A)
is accepted by a finite Büchi automaton. Similarly as in
[
11
, Lemma 3.2]
), we get that the right congruence
\sim_{\delta,A}
has only finitely many classes. Hence
(1)\implies(2)
. The implication
(2)\implies(3)
is given by Lemma
6
. The implication
(3)\implies(4)
follows directly from the definition of the zero automaton. Finally, the implication
(4)\implies(1)
follows from Proposition

.
∎

Note that the zero automaton is deterministic. Therefore, the previous result shows in particular that if the set

is accepted by an arbitrary Büchi automaton, possibly non deterministic, then it must be also accepted by a deterministic one.

\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
satisfy the following set of equations

\frac{3}{\beta_{0}}=1,\quad\frac{1}{\beta_{1}}+\frac{1}{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}}=1,\quad\frac{1}{\beta_{2}}+\left(\frac{1}{\beta_{2}\beta_{0}}+\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\frac{\delta}{\delta-1}=1,

where
\delta=\beta_{0}\beta_{1}\beta_{2}
. Multiplying the first equation by
\delta
, the second one by
\beta_{1}\beta_{2}
and the third one by
(\delta-1)\beta_{2}
, we obtain identities

3\beta_{1}\beta_{2}-\delta=0,\quad-\beta_{1}\beta_{2}+\beta_{2}+1=0,\quad\beta_{1}\beta_{2}+(2-\delta)\beta_{2}+\delta-1=0.

In a matrix formalism, we have

(5.2)

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}3&0&-\delta\\
-1&1&1\\
1&2-\delta&\delta-1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\\
\beta_{2}\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right).

The existence of a non-zero vector
(\beta_{1}\beta_{2},\beta_{2},1)^{T}
as a solution of this equation forces that the determinant of the coefficient matrix is zero, that is, that
\delta^{2}-9\delta+9=0
. Hence we must have
\delta=\frac{9+3\sqrt{5}}{2}=3\varphi^{2}
where
\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
is the golden ratio. Solving (
5.2
) for this
\delta
, we obtain
\beta_{1}\beta_{2}=\frac{\delta}{3}=\varphi^{2}
and
\beta_{2}=\beta_{1}\beta_{2}-1=\varphi^{2}-1=\varphi
, and finally
\beta_{1}=1+\frac{1}{\varphi}=\varphi
. Consequently,
\beta_{0}=\frac{\delta}{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}}=3
. Indeed, the triple
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(3,\varphi,\varphi)
is an alternate base giving precisely (
5.1
) as the expansions of 1, as already observed in
[
6
]
.

For obtaining the values
\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
from the known eventually periodic expansions we have used the fact that
\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
and
\delta=\beta_{0}\beta_{1}\beta_{2}
are solutions of a system of polynomial equations in four unknowns
x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},y
, in our case

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}3x_{1}x_{2}-y&=&0\\
-x_{1}x_{2}+x_{2}+1&=&0\\
x_{1}x_{2}+(2-y)x_{2}+y-1&=&0\\
x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}&=&y.\end{array}\right.

The solution of the system yielded that
\delta
is a root of a monic polynomial with integer coefficient, i.e., is an algebraic integer. The same strategy can be applied to any alternate basis where all the expansions
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta^{(i)}}}(1)
are eventually periodic.

Recall that in case of classical Rényi expansion of
1
with a single basis, it is easy to show that if
d_{\beta}(1)
is eventually periodic (i.e., if
\beta
is a Parry number) then
\beta
is an algebraic integer. The monic polynomial with integer coefficient having
\beta
as a root is called the Parry polynomial of
\beta
.

In the proof of Theorem
14
, we will work with formal power series whose coefficients are given by eventually periodic sequences. Let us prepare explicit form of these sums.

For given
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
, we define
P_{m,k}
as the set of polynomials in
\mathbb{Z}[y]
of degree at most
m+k-1
of the form

(5.3)

\displaystyle(y^{k}-1)(\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}a_{n}y^{m-1-n})+\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}a_{m+n}y^{k-1-n}

where
a_{0},\ldots,a_{m+k-1}\in\mathbb{Z}
. We say that the polynomial (
5.3
) is
associated with the integers
a_{0},\ldots,a_{m+k-1}
. Note that this polynomial has maximal degree
m+k-1
if
a_{0}\neq 0
.

Lemma 16
.

Let
a
be an eventually periodic sequence of integers with preperiod
m\in\mathbb{N}
and period
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
, that is,
a=a_{0}a_{1}\cdots a_{m-1}(a_{m}a_{m+1}\cdots a_{m+k-1})^{\omega}
. Then

\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{y^{n+1}}=\frac{g}{y^{m}(y^{k}-1)}

where
g
is the polynomial in
P_{m,k}
associated with the integers
a_{0},\ldots,a_{m+k-1}
.

Proof.

This is a straightforward verification.
∎

Lemma 17
.

Suppose that
1
has an eventually periodic
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representation
a
of preperiod
mp
and period
kp
with
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
. Then

\delta^{m}(\delta^{k}-1)=\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}g_{j}(\delta)\beta_{j+1}\cdots\beta_{p-1}

where for each
j\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
,
g_{j}
is the polynomial in
P_{m,k}
associated with the integers
a_{j},a_{j+p}\ldots,a_{j+(m+k-1)p}
.

Proof.

Rewrite (
2.1
) as

1=\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{a_{np+j}}{\delta^{n+1}}\beta_{j+1}\cdots\beta_{p-1}.

Since for every
j\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
, the sequence
(a_{np+j})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is eventually periodic with preperiod
m
and period
k
, the result follows from Lemma
16
.
∎

Whenever all
p
expansions
d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(i)}}(1)
are eventually periodic, for
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
, we associate a system of polynomial equations, which we call the
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-polynomial system
by analogy to the
\beta
-polynomial
for real bases
\beta

[
17
]
. We do this as follows. Without loss of generality, we suppose that for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
, the expansion
d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(i)}}(1)
has a preperiod
m_{i}p
and a period
k_{i}p
with
m_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
and
k_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then, for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
, we let
g_{i,0},g_{i,1},\dots,g_{i,p-1}
be the associated polynomials in
P_{m_{i},k_{i}}
as in Lemma
17
, so that

\delta^{m_{i}}(\delta^{k_{i}}-1)=\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}g_{i,j}(\delta)\beta_{i+j+1}\cdots\beta_{i+p-1}.

For each
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
, since the first digit of
d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(i)}}(1)
is
\left\lfloor\beta_{i}\right\rfloor\geq 1
, the degree of
g_{i,0}
is
k_{i}+m_{i}-1
. The
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-polynomial system
is the system of
p+1
polynomial equations in
p+1
variables
x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{p-1},y
given by

(5.4)

\begin{cases}y^{m_{i}}(y^{k_{i}}-1)=\displaystyle{\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}}g_{i,j}x_{i+j+1}\cdots x_{i+p-1},&\text{for }i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]\\
y=\displaystyle{\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}}x_{i}\end{cases}

where, as usual, we use the convention
x_{n}=x_{n\bmod p}
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. By construction, the
p
-tuple
(\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1},\delta)
is a solution of this system.

Example 18
.

We resume Example
15
. By writing each of the expansions from (
5.1
) with a preperiod
3
and a period
3
, that is,

d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}}(1)=300(000)^{\omega},\quad d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(1)}}(1)=110(000)^{\omega}\quad\text{and}\quad d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(2)}}(1)=111(011)^{\omega},

we get
g_{0,0}=3(y-1)
,
g_{1,0}=g_{1,1}=g_{2,0}=y-1
,
g_{2,1}=g_{2,2}=y
and
g_{0,1}=g_{0,2}=g_{1,2}=0
. The associated
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-polynomial system is

\begin{cases}y(y-1)=3(y-1)x_{1}x_{2}\\
y(y-1)=(y-1)x_{2}x_{0}+(y-1)x_{0}\\
y(y-1)=(y-1)x_{0}x_{1}+yx_{1}+y\\
y=x_{0}x_{1}x_{2}.\end{cases}

By multiplying the second equation by
x_{1}x_{2}
and the third one by
x_{2}
and by substituting
x_{0}x_{1}x_{2}
by
y
, we get the three equations

\begin{cases}y(y-1)=3(y-1)x_{1}x_{2}\\
y(y-1)x_{1}x_{2}=y(y-1)x_{2}+y(y-1)\\
y(y-1)x_{2}=(y-1)y+yx_{1}x_{2}+yx_{2}.\end{cases}

Placing the first equation in the last line, this can be rewritten as

\begin{pmatrix}-y(y-1)&y(y-1)&y(y-1)\\
y&y-y(y-1)&y(y-1)\\
3(y-1)&0&-y(y-1)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}x_{2}\\
x_{2}\\
1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

The matrix of this system is equal to

M(y)-y(y-1)I_{3}
where

M(y)=\begin{pmatrix}g_{1,2}&yg_{1,0}&yg_{1,1}\\
g_{2,1}&g_{2,2}&yg_{2,0}\\
g_{0,0}&g_{0,1}&g_{0,2}\end{pmatrix}

and

is the identity matrix of size

.

Proof of Theorem
14
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
be such that the expansions
d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(i)}}(1)
all have preperiod
mp
and period
kp
, for
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
. Then we consider the associated polynomial system (
5.4
), where
m_{i}=m
and
k_{i}=k
for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
. We index the equations of this system from
0
to
p
. For each
i\in[\![1,p-1]\!]
, we multiply the
i
th equation by
\prod_{k=i}^{p-1}x_{k}
, which becomes

y^{m}(y^{k}-1)\prod_{k=i}^{p-1}x_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}(g_{i,j}\prod_{k=i+j+1}^{2p-1}x_{k}).

By substituting
x_{0}\cdots x_{p-1}
by
y
, the latter equation can be rewritten as

y^{m}(y^{k}-1)\prod_{k=i}^{p-1}x_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{p-i-1}(yg_{i,j}\prod_{k=i+j+1}^{p-1}x_{k})+\sum_{j=p-i}^{p-1}(g_{i,j}\prod_{k=i+j+1-p}^{p-1}x_{k}).

Now, the first
p
equations of the system can be written in the matrix form

(5.5)

(M(y)-y^{m}(y^{k}-1)I_{p})\overrightarrow{v}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{p-1})=\vec{0}

where
I_{p}
is the identity matrix of size
p
,
\vec{0}
is the zero column vector of size
p
,

\overrightarrow{v}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{p-1})=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{p-1}\\
x_{2}\cdots x_{p-1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{p-1}\\
1\end{pmatrix}

and

M(y)=\begin{pmatrix}g_{1,p-1}&yg_{1,0}&\cdots&yg_{1,p-3}&yg_{1,p-2}\\
g_{2,p-2}&g_{2,p-1}&\cdots&yg_{2,p-4}&yg_{2,p-3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
g_{p-1,1}&g_{p-1,2}&\cdots&g_{p-1,p-1}&yg_{p-1,0}\\
g_{0,0}&g_{0,1}&\cdots&g_{0,p-2}&g_{0,p-1}\end{pmatrix}

Since
(\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1},\delta)
is a non trivial solution of the original system, we get that
\delta
is a root of the polynomial
h=\det(M(y)-y^{m}(y^{k}-1)I_{p})
of
\mathbb{Z}[y]
. By construction, for every
i,j\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
, the polynomial
g_{i,j}
has degree at most
m+k-1
. Therefore, the highest degree of
h
is obtained from the product
\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}(g_{i,p-1}{-}y^{m}(y^{k}{-}1))
. This shows that
h
has leading coefficient
(-1)^{p}
. Since
\delta
is a root of
h
, we get that
\delta
is an algebraic integer.

It remains to prove that
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
. To that purpose, we will apply the famous Perron-Frobenius theorem. First, thanks to Lemma
16
, we know that the matrix
M(\delta)
has non-negative entries. Then, by Lemma
17
and since any
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion starts with a non-zero digit, the entries
\delta g_{1,0}(\delta)
,
\delta g_{2,0}(\delta),\ldots,\delta g_{p-1,0}(\delta)
,
g_{0,0}(\delta)
of
M(\delta)
in respective positions
(0,1)
,
(1,2),\ldots,(p-2,p-1)
,
(p-1,0)
are positive. Therefore, the matrix
M(\delta)
is irreducible. By the Perron-Frobenius theorem, the vector
\overrightarrow{v}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p-1})
is the unique positive eigenvector of
M(\delta)
having
1
as its last entry and the corresponding eigenvalue
\delta^{m}(\delta^{k}-1)
is the Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue of
M(\delta)
. Moreover, the rank of the matrix
M(\delta)-\delta^{m}(\delta^{k}-1)I
is
p-1
. Thus, the corresponding linear system in the unknowns
c_{1}=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{p-1}
,
c_{2}=x_{2}\cdots x_{p-1},\ldots,c_{p-1}=x_{p-1}
is equivalent to that obtained by deleting one its
p
equations. The obtained system has full rank
p-1
. Since all entries of
M(\delta)-\delta^{m}(\delta^{k}-1)I
belong to the field
\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
, any solution vector of the latter system has components
c_{i}
in
\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
. Hence, the products
\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\cdots\beta_{p-1},\beta_{2}\cdots\beta_{p-1},\ldots,\beta_{p-1}
all belong to
\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
. We obtain in turn that
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p-1}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
. Since moreover
\beta_{0}=\delta/(\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{p-1})
, we also get that
\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
.
∎

Let us emphasize that the greediness of the representations was not necessary in the proof of Theorem
14
. We only need that each
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}
-representation of
1
starts with a non-zero digit. Therefore, we have actually proved the following stronger result.

Theorem 19
.

If
1
has eventually periodic
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}
-representations for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
, then
\delta
is an algebraic integer. If moreover these
p
representations have non-negative digits and they all start with a non-zero digit, then
\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
.

From the proof of Theorem
14
, we deduce the following result about the uniqueness of the base.

Proposition 20
.

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{p-1})
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1})
are two alternate bases such that
\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\alpha_{i}=\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\beta_{i}
, and suppose that there exists
p
eventually periodic sequences
a^{(0)},\ldots,a^{(p-1)}
of non-negative integers such that
a_{0}^{(i)}\geq 1
and
\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(i)}}(a^{(i)})=\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}(a^{(i)})=1
for every
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
. Then
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{\beta}
.

Proof.

Using the same notation as in the proof of Theorem
14
, given the product
\delta=\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\beta_{i}
, the vector
\overrightarrow{v}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p-1})
is the unique positive eigenvector of
M(\delta)
having
1
as its last entry. Therefore, we must have
\overrightarrow{v}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{p-1})=\overrightarrow{v}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p-1})
, hence
\alpha_{i}=\beta_{i}
for all
i\in[\![1,p]\!]
. Moreover, we have
\alpha_{0}=\delta/(\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{p-1})=\delta/(\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{p-1})=\beta_{0}
.
∎

In particular, we get the following two corollaries.

Corollary 21
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{p-1})
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1})
be two alternate bases such that
\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\alpha_{i}=\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\beta_{i}
and suppose that for every
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
, the
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(i)}
-expansion of
1
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}
-expansions of
1
coincide and are eventually periodic. Then
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{\beta}
.

Corollary 22
.

If
d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(i)}}(1)=d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(1)
for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
and
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(1)
is eventually periodic, then
\beta_{i}=\beta_{0}
for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
.

Proof.

Apply Corollary
21
to
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1)}
.
∎

6.
Spectrum and periodicity of the expansions of
1
in alternate bases

In Section
5
, we have derived a necessary condition for the
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}
-expansions to be all eventually periodic, i.e., for
\boldsymbol{\beta}
to be a Parry alternate base. Namely that the product
\delta
of the bases is an algebraic integer and all
\beta_{j}
belong to the field
\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
. In this section, we give a sufficient condition.

We adopt the same notation and convention as in Section
4
: we fix an alternate base
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1})
, we set
\delta=\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\beta_{i}
, we consider a fixed
p
-tuple
\boldsymbol{D}=(D_{0},\ldots,D_{p-1})
where every
D_{i}
is an alphabet of integers containing
0
and we let
\mathcal{D}
be the corresponding alphabet or real numbers as defined in (
4.1
).

Proposition 23
.

If
D_{i}\supseteq[\![-\left\lfloor\beta_{i}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor\beta_{i}\right\rfloor]\!]
for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
and if the spectrum
X^{\mathcal{D}}(\delta)
has no accumulation point in
\mathbb{R}
, then
d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(i)}}(1)
is eventually periodic for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
.

Proof.

Suppose that
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(1)
is not eventually periodic. Then the sequence of remainders
(r_{\ell p-1}(1))_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
is injective. For all
x\in[0,1]
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(6.1)

r_{\ell p-1}(x)=\delta^{\ell}x-\sum_{n=0}^{\ell-1}d_{n}\delta^{\ell-1-n}

where
d_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\varepsilon_{np+i}(x)\beta_{i+1}\cdots\beta_{p-1}
. Since
D_{i}\supseteq[\![-\left\lfloor\beta_{i}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor\beta_{i}\right\rfloor]\!]
for each
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
, we get that for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, the remainder
r_{\ell p-1}(1)
is an element of
X^{\mathcal{D}}(\delta)
.
Since the remainders all belong to the interval
[0,1)
, the spectrum
X^{\mathcal{D}}(\delta)
has an accumulation point in
\mathbb{R}
. By Lemma
9
, either all the spectra
X(i)
based on the cyclic shifts
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}
of the alternate base and the corresponding shifted
p
-tuple of alphabets
\boldsymbol{D}^{(i)}
for
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
have an accumulation point or none of them has. The result follows.
∎

Proposition 24
.

If
\delta
is a Pisot number and
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
then the spectrum
X^{\mathcal{D}}(\delta)
has no accumulation point in
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Since the set
\mathcal{D}
is a finite subset of
\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
and
\delta
is an algebraic integer, there exist a positive integer
q
and a finite subset
A
of the ring of algebraic integers in
\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
such that

\mathcal{D}\cup(\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{D})=\frac{1}{q}A
.
Let
x,y\in X^{\mathcal{D}}(\delta)
such that
x\neq y
. There exists
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
a_{0},\ldots,a_{\ell-1}\in A
such that

x-y=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{n=0}^{\ell-1}a_{n}\delta^{n}.

We obtain that
q(x-y)
is an algebraic integer.
Let
d
denote the (algebraic) degree of
\delta
and let
\delta_{2},\ldots,\delta_{d}
be the Galois conjugates of
\delta
. Moreover, set
\delta_{1}=\delta
. Then

1\leq\big|N_{\mathbb{Q}(\delta)/\mathbb{Q}}(q(x-y))\big|=q|x-y|\prod_{k=2}^{d}|\psi_{k}(q(x-y))|.

Since
\delta
is a Pisot number, for all
k\in[\![2,d]\!]
, we have
|\delta_{k}|<1
and hence

|\psi_{k}(q(x-y))|\leq M\sum_{n=0}^{\ell-1}|\delta_{k}|^{n}\leq\frac{M}{1-|\delta_{k}|}

where
M=\max\{|\psi_{k}(a)|:k\in[\![2,d]\!],\,a\in A\}
. We get that

|x-y|\geq\frac{1}{q}\prod_{k=2}^{d}\frac{1-|\delta_{k}|}{M}.

The latter inequality states that the distance between distinct elements
x,y
of the spectrum
X^{\mathcal{D}}(\delta)
is bounded from below by a constant uniformly for all pairs
x,y
.
∎

As a consequence, we get the following theorem, which for the case
p=1
is a well-known result of Schmidt
[
20
]
.

Theorem 25
.

If
\delta
is a Pisot number and
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
then
d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(i)}}(1)
is eventually periodic for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
.

Proof.

First apply Proposition
24
with
\boldsymbol{D}=([\![-\left\lfloor\beta_{0}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor\beta_{0}\right\rfloor]\!],\ldots,[\![-\left\lfloor\beta_{p-1}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor\beta_{p-1}\right\rfloor]\!])
and then apply Proposition
23
.
∎

Let us make several remarks concerning the previous result. First, the condition of
\delta
being a Pisot number is neither sufficient nor necessary for
\boldsymbol{\beta}
to be a Parry alternate base, i.e., in order to have that
d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(i)}}(1)
is eventually periodic for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
. Indeed, it is not necessary even for
p=1
since there exist Parry numbers which are not Pisot. To see that it is not sufficient for
p\geq 2
, consider the alternate base
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\sqrt{\beta},\sqrt{\beta})
where
\beta
is the smallest Pisot number. The product
\delta
is the Pisot number
\beta
. However, the
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of
1
is equal to
d_{\sqrt{\beta}}(1)
, which is known to be aperiodic. This follows from the fact that the only Galois conjugate of
\sqrt{\beta}
is
-\sqrt{\beta}
, and thus
\sqrt{\beta}
is not a Perron number, hence not a Parry number either.

Furthermore, the bases
\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1}
need not be algebraic integers in order to have the property that
d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(i)}}(1)
is eventually periodic for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
. To see this, consider for instance the alternate base
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\frac{1+\sqrt{13}}{2},\frac{5+\sqrt{13}}{6})
from Example
12
. For this base, we have
d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}}(1)=2010^{\omega}
and
d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(1)}}(1)=110^{\omega}
. However,
\frac{5+\sqrt{13}}{6}
is not an algebraic integer.

As illustrated in the following example, for the same non Pisot algebraic integer
\delta
, there may exist two length-
p
alternate bases
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{0},\cdots,\alpha_{p-1})
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{0}\cdots\beta_{p-1})
such that
\alpha_{0}\cdots\alpha_{p-1}=\beta_{0}\cdots\beta_{p-1}=\delta
and for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
, the expansion
d_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(i)}}(1)
is eventually periodic whereas there exists
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
such that
d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(i)}}(1)
is not. The technique used for showing aperiodicity is inspired by the work
[
15
]
.

Example 26
.

Consider the real root
\delta>1
of the polynomial
x^{6}-x^{5}-1
. This number is an algebraic integer but it is not a Pisot number since two of its Galois conjugates have modulus greater than
1
. Consider the alternate base
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\frac{1+\delta^{7}}{\delta^{7}},\frac{\delta^{8}}{1+\delta^{7}})
. We can compute that
d_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(1)=10^{13}10^{\omega}
and
d_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(1)}}(1)=10^{18}10^{20}(10^{27})^{\omega}
. Now consider
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\frac{6}{5},\frac{5}{6}\delta)
. We prove that
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(1)
is not eventually periodic. Let
\gamma
be a Galois conjugate of
\delta
such that
|\gamma|>1
and let
\psi\colon\mathbb{Q}(\delta)\to\mathbb{Q}(\gamma)
be the corresponding field isomorphism induced by
\psi(\delta)=\gamma
. We prove that
(r_{12n-1}(1))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is not eventually periodic, where we set
r_{-1}(1)=1
. To do so, it is enough to prove that
\big(|\psi(r_{12n-1}(1))|\big)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is eventually strictly increasing. It can be computed that the word
10^{12}
is a prefix of
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(1)
and
d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(1)}}^{*}(1)
. Therefore, by Theorem
4
and Equality (
6.1
), for all
x\in[0,1]
, we get

r_{11}(x)\in\{\delta^{6}x\}\cup\{\delta^{6}x-\beta_{1}\delta^{k}:k\in[\![0,5]\!]\}\cup\{\delta^{6}x-\delta^{k}:k\in[\![0,5]\!]\}.

Hence, for all
x\in[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
, we have

\psi(r_{11}(x))\in\{\gamma^{6}\psi(x)\}\cup\{\gamma^{6}\psi(x)-\frac{5}{6}\gamma^{k+1}:k\in[\![0,5]\!]\}\cup\{\gamma^{6}\psi(x)-\gamma^{k}:k\in[\![0,5]\!]\}.

Since
|\gamma|\leq\frac{6}{5}
, we get

|\psi(r_{11}(x))|\geq|\gamma|^{6}|\psi(x)|-|\gamma|^{5}.

Thus, if we have

|\psi(x)|>\frac{|\gamma|^{5}}{|\gamma|^{6}-1}\simeq 5.49

then we obtain
|\psi(r_{11}(x))|>|\psi(x)|
.
It can be computed that
10^{13}10^{15}10^{13}10^{27}10^{11}
is the prefix of
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(1)
of length
84
. Hence, by using (
6.1
) again, we get

r_{83}(1)=\delta^{42}-\beta_{1}\delta^{41}-\beta_{1}\delta^{34}-\beta_{1}\delta^{26}-\beta_{1}\delta^{19}-\beta_{1}\delta^{5}.

This implies

\psi(r_{83}(1))=\gamma^{42}-\frac{5}{6}\gamma^{42}-\frac{5}{6}\gamma^{35}-\frac{5}{6}\gamma^{27}-\frac{5}{6}\gamma^{20}-\frac{5}{6}\gamma^{6}.

Now for
x=r_{83}(1)
, we have
|\psi(x)|\simeq 6.23>5.49
. We get
|\psi(r_{11}(x))|>|\psi(x)|
where
r_{11}(x)=r_{95}(1)
. Iterating the argument, we obtain that the sequence
(|\psi(r_{12n-1}(1))|)_{n\geq 7}
is strictly increasing.

7.
Alternate bases whose set of zero representations is accepted by a finite Büchi automaton

Once again, we use the notation introduced in Section
4
: namely we use fixed
\boldsymbol{\beta},\delta,\boldsymbol{D}
and then we work with the corresponding digit set
\mathcal{D}
, set of representations of zero
Z(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{D})
and spectrum
X^{\mathcal{D}}(\delta)
. We combine the previously established results in order to characterize for which alternate bases the set
Z(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{D})
is accepted by a finite Büchi automaton. In doing so, we generalize Theorem
3
to alternate bases. We need one more lemma.

Lemma 27
.

If the spectrum
X^{\mathcal{D}}(\delta)
has no accumulation point in
\mathbb{R}
and if there exists
j\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
such that
[\![-\left\lceil\delta\right\rceil+1,\left\lceil\delta\right\rceil-1]\!]\subseteq D_{j}
, then
\delta
is a Pisot number.

Proof.

Suppose that
X^{\mathcal{D}}(\delta)
has no accumulation point in
\mathbb{R}
and let
j
be an index as in the statement. Since
X^{\mathcal{D}}(\delta)=\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}X^{D_{i}}(\delta)\beta_{i+1}\cdots\beta_{p-1}
, the spectrum
X^{D_{j}}(\delta)
has no accumulation point in
\mathbb{R}
. By hypothesis on
j
, the spectrum
X^{\left\lceil\delta\right\rceil-1}(\delta)
has no accumulation point in
\mathbb{R}
either. By Theorem
2
, we get that
\delta
is a Pisot number.
∎

Theorem 28
.

The following assertions are equivalent.

(1)

The set
Z(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{D})
is accepted by a finite Büchi automaton for all
p
-tuple of alphabets of integers
\boldsymbol{D}=(D_{0},\ldots,D_{p-1})
.

(2)

The set
Z(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{D})
is accepted by a finite Büchi automaton for one
p
-tuple of alphabets of integers
\boldsymbol{D}=(D_{0},\ldots,D_{p-1})
such that
D_{i}\supseteq[\![-\left\lfloor\beta_{i}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor\beta_{i}\right\rfloor]\!]
for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
and
\left\lfloor\beta_{j}\right\rfloor\geq\left\lceil\delta\right\rceil-1
for some
j\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
.

(3)

\delta
is a Pisot number and
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
.

Proof.

The implication
(1)\implies(2)
is straightforward. Now, suppose that
(2)
holds. By Theorem
13
and Proposition
23
, the greedy expansions
d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(i)}}(1)
are eventually periodic for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
. Then, by Theorem
14
, we get that
\delta
is an algebraic integer and
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
. Moreover, since there exists
j\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
such that
\left\lfloor\beta_{j}\right\rfloor\geq\left\lceil\delta\right\rceil-1
, we obtain from Theorem
13
and Lemma
27
that
\delta
is a Pisot number. Hence, we have shown that
(2)\implies(3)
. Finally, the implication
(3)\implies(1)
is obtained by combining Proposition
24
and Theorem
13
.
∎

8.
Normalization in alternate base

In this section, we apply our results in order to show that the normalization in alternate base is computable by a finite Büchi automaton under certain hypotheses, in which case we construct such an automaton. The
normalization function

\nu_{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{D}}\colon(\cup_{i=0}^{p-1}D_{i})^{\mathbb{N}}\to(\cup_{i=0}^{p-1}[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{i}\right\rceil-1]\!])^{\mathbb{N}}
is the partial function mapping any
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representation
a\in\otimes_{n\in\mathbb{N}}D_{n}
of a real number
x\in[0,1)
to the
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of
x
. We say that
\nu_{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{D}}
is
computable by a finite Büchi automaton
if there exists a finite Büchi automaton accepting
the set

\{(u,v)\in\bigotimes_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(D_{n}\times[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!]):\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(u)=\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(v)\text{ and }\exists x\in[0,1),\ v=d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x)\}.

Following the same lines as in the real base case, we start by constructing a
converter
by using the zero automaton
\mathcal{Z}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{D})=(Q_{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{D}},(0,0),Q_{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{D}},\cup_{i=0}^{p-1}D_{i},E)
defined in Section
4
. Consider two
p
-tuples of alphabets
\boldsymbol{D}=(D_{0},\ldots,D_{p-1})
and
\boldsymbol{D^{\prime}}=(D^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,D^{\prime}_{p-1})
. We denote the
p
-tuple of alphabets
(D_{0}-D_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,D_{p-1}-D^{\prime}_{p-1})
by
\boldsymbol{D}-\boldsymbol{D^{\prime}}
. The
converter from
\boldsymbol{D}
to
\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}
is the Büchi automaton

\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{D^{\prime}})=(Q_{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{D}-\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}},(0,0),Q_{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{D}-\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}},\cup_{i=0}^{p-1}(D_{i}\times D_{i}^{\prime}),E^{\prime})

where
E^{\prime}
is the set of transitions defined as follows: for
(i,s),(j,t)\in Q_{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{D}-\boldsymbol{D^{\prime}}}
and for
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}a\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right]\in\cup_{i=0}^{p-1}(D_{i}\times D_{i}^{\prime})
, there is a transition

(i,s)\xrightarrow[\hskip 8.19447pt]{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}a\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right]}(j,t)

if and only if
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}a\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right]\in D_{i}\times D_{i}^{\prime}
and there is a transition
(i,s)\xrightarrow[\hskip 8.19447pt]{a-b}(j,t)
in
\mathcal{Z}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{D}-\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime})
.

Proposition 29
.

The converter
\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime})
accepts the set

\{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}u\\
v\end{smallmatrix}\right]\in\bigotimes_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(D_{n}\times D_{n}^{\prime}):\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(u)=\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(v)\}.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of Proposition
11
.
∎

Proposition 30
.

If
\delta
is a Pisot number and
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
, then the converter
\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{D^{\prime}}}
is finite.

Proof.

By Theorems
28
and
13
, the zero automaton
\mathcal{Z}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{D}-\boldsymbol{D^{\prime}})
is finite. Hence, so is the converter
\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{D^{\prime}}}
.
∎

In the case where
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is a Parry alternate base, i.e., where all
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}^{*}(1)
are eventually periodic, a deterministic finite automaton
\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
accepting
\mathrm{Fac}(\{d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x):x\in[0,1)\})
was built in
[
6
]
, thus showing that the corresponding
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-shift is sofic. Here, we consider a modification of this automaton in order to get a Büchi automaton accepting
\{d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x):x\in[0,1)\}
. Suppose that

d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}^{*}(1)=t_{0}^{(i)}\cdots t_{m_{i}-1}^{(i)}\big(t_{m_{i}}^{(i)}\cdots t_{m_{i}+n_{i}-1}^{(i)}\big)^{\omega}

for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
. Without loss of generality, we suppose that
d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(i)}}(1)
has a non-zero preperiod for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
, i.e., in the case of a purely periodic expansion
(t_{0}\cdots t_{n-1})^{\omega}
, we work with the writing
t_{0}(t_{1}\cdots t_{n-1}t_{0})^{\omega}
instead. Then we define the Büchi automaton

\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(Q,(0,0,0),F,[\![0,\max_{0\leq i<p}\left\lceil\beta_{i}\right\rceil-1]\!],E)

where the set of states is

Q=\{(i,j,k):i,j\in[\![0,p-1]\!],\ k\in[\![0,m_{i}+n_{i}-1]\!]\},

the set of final states is

F=\big\{(i,i,0):i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]\big\}

and the (partial) transition function
E\colon Q\times[\![0,\max_{0\leq i<p}\left\lceil\beta_{i}\right\rceil-1]\!]\to Q
is defined as follows: for each
i,j\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
and each
k\in[\![0,m_{i}+n_{i}-1]\!]
,
we have

E((i,j,k),t_{k}^{(i)})=\begin{cases}(i,(j+1)\bmod p,k+1),&\text{if }k\neq m_{i}+n_{i}-1;\\
(i,(j+1)\bmod p,m_{i}),&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

and for all
s\in[\![0,t_{k}^{(i)}-1]\!]
, we have

E((i,j,k),s)=((j+1)\bmod p,(j+1)\bmod p,0).

Proposition 31
.

If
d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(i)}}(1)
is eventually periodic for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
then the Büchi automaton
\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
accepts the set
\{d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x):x\in[0,1)\}
.

Proof.

For an alternate base
\boldsymbol{\beta}
of length
p
with
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(1)=t_{0}t_{1}t_{2}\cdots
and for
j\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
, we define the set

Y(\boldsymbol{\beta},j)=\{t_{0}\cdots t_{\ell-2}s:\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1},\ \ell\bmod p=j,\ t_{\ell-1}>0,\ s\in[\![0,t_{\ell-1}-1]\!]\}.

An infinite word is accepted by
\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
if and only if it can be factored as
u_{0}u_{1}u_{2}\cdots
where each factor
u_{n}
corresponds to a first return to a final state, i.e., for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a path labeled by
u_{n}
from a state of the form
(i,i,0)
to a state of the form
(j,j,0)
and
u_{n}
is the shortest next factor with this property. Since we have built
\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
by using a non-zero preperiod for each
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}^{*}(1)
, each such factor
u_{n}
must belong to the set
Y(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(|u_{0}|+\cdots+|u_{n-1}|)},|u_{n}|)
. Proposition 45 in
[
6
]
states that

\{d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x):x\in[0,1)\}=\bigcup_{h_{0}=0}^{p-1}Y(\boldsymbol{\beta},h_{0})\Bigg(\bigcup_{h_{1}=0}^{p-1}Y(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(h_{0})},h_{1})\Bigg(\bigcup_{h_{2}=0}^{p-1}Y(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(h_{0}+h_{1})},h_{2})\Bigg(\cdots\Bigg)\Bigg)\Bigg).

The conclusion follows.
∎

Theorem 32
.

If
\delta
is a Pisot number and
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
, then the normalization function
\nu_{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{D}}
is computable by a finite Büchi automaton.

Proof.

If
\delta
is a Pisot number and
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
, then by Theorem
25
, the greedy
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}
-expansions of
1
are eventually periodic for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
. By Proposition
31
, the finite Büchi automaton
\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
accepts the set
\{d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x):x\in[0,1)\}
. Thanks to this automaton, we construct a finite Büchi automaton accepting the set

\big\{(u,v)\in\bigotimes_{n=0}^{+\infty}(D_{n}\times[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!]):\exists x\in[0,1),\ v=d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x)\big\}.

By intersecting the latter Büchi automaton with the converter
\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime})
where

, which is finite by Proposition

, we get a finite Büchi automaton accepting the set

\{(u,v)\in\bigotimes_{n=0}^{+\infty}(D_{n}\times[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!]):\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(u)=\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(v)\text{ and }\exists x\in[0,1),\ v=d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x)\}.

∎

9.
Further work

We have shown that properties of alternate base numeration systems defined using a
p
-tuple of bases
(\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{p-1})
are related to the geometry of a generalized Erdős spectrum of the number
\delta=\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\beta_{i}
. For
p=1
, i.e., for classical numeration systems with one base
\beta
, the Erdős spectrum proved to be useful in different situations. Vávra in
[
22
]
used the spectrum to characterize complex bases
\beta
for which, with a suitably chosen digit set, every element of the field
\mathbb{Q}(\beta)
has an eventually periodic representation. In
[
13
]
the question whether a numeration system with a complex base
\beta
and a digit set
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{C}
allows a representation of any complex number is reformulated as the question whether the corresponding Erdős spectrum is relatively dense. Let us mention that, so far, the topological properties of the spectrum described in Theorem
2
are known only when the base
\beta
is a real number greater than
1
and the alphabet
\mathcal{D}
is a symmetric set of consecutive integers, i.e., of the form
[\![-d,d]\!]
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, in this case, we have a sharp bound on
d
for which the spectrum of a non-Pisot base
\beta>1
has an accumulation point: the spectrum
X^{d}(\beta)
has an accumulation point in
\mathbb{R}
if and only if
d<\beta-1
. Analogous results for real and complex bases
\beta
and arbitrary finite alphabets
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}
, or
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{C}
, would improve bounds on digit sets in several diverse problems, namely also in our Theorem
28
. For more details, see
[
12
]
.

One of the results of this paper is that if
\delta=\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\beta_{i}
is a Pisot number and
\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1}
belong to
\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
, the expansions
d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(i)}}(1)
are all eventually periodic. We have illustrated that
\delta
being Pisot is not a necessary condition. For
p=1
, bases
\beta
for which
1
has an eventually periodic greedy expansion are called Parry numbers. Solomyak obtained algebraic properties of Parry numbers
[
21
]
. It would be interesting to study the analogy of Parry numbers in the context of alternate bases. In particular, to find bounds on the algebraic conjugates of
\delta
.

In the case where
p=1
, for every sequence of non-negative digits
a=a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots
satisfying the lexicographic condition
a_{n}a_{n+1}a_{n+2}\cdots\leq_{\mathrm{lex}}a
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a unique
\beta>1
such that
d_{\beta}(1)=a

[
17
]
. It is not clear yet whether for
p
integer digit sequences
a^{(0)},\dots,a^{(p-1)}
satisfying analogous lexicographic conditions, there exists a unique
p
-tuple of bases
\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{p-1}
such that
d_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(i)}}(1)=a^{(i)}
for
i=0,1,\dots,p-1
. Corollary
21
represents a first step towards this direction.
