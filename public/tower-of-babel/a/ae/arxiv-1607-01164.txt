Abstract
. In this paper, we tailor-make new approximation operators inspired by rough set theory and specially suited for domain theory. Our approximation operators offer a fresh perspective to existing concepts and results in domain theory, but also reveal ways to establishing novel domain-theoretic results. For instance, (1) the well-known interpolation property of the way-below relation on a continuous poset is equivalent to the idempotence of a certain set-operator; (2) the continuity of a poset can be characterized by the coincidence of the Scott closure operator and the upper approximation operator induced by the way below relation; (3) meet-continuity can be established from a certain property of the topological closure operator. Additionally, we show how, to each approximating relation, an associated order-compatible topology can be defined in such a way that for the case of a continuous poset the topology associated to the way-below relation is exactly the Scott topology. A preliminary investigation is carried out on this new topology.

1.
Introduction

The invention of
domain theory
by Dana Scott in around the late 1960s was originally intended to give a denotational semantics for the
\lambda
-calculus
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
. Domain theory is a theory of approximation as it formalizes the notion of partial or incomplete information to represent computations that have not yet returned a result, and thus provides the mathematical foundation to explicate the phenomenon of approximation in computation. Another important but entirely independent theory of approximation that has far-reaching impact, particularly, in knowledge representation and reasoning as well as in data analysis is that of
rough set theory
. Introduced by Zdisław Pawlak in
[
4
]
as a mathematical tool for imperfect data analysis, rough set theory saw many applications in decision support occurring in various disciplines such as computer science, engineering, medicine, education and others.

Earlier works due to Guo-Qiang Zhang (
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
) already identified that domain theory, in the form of Scott information systems and formal concept analysis, has something to do with rough set theory because of its applications in knowledge representation and reasoning. But it was Yi Yu Yao (
[
12
,
13
]
) who first explicitly pointed out the role that domain theory plays in rough set theory relying on the notion of object-oriented concept. Following up on Yao’s work, Lei and Luo gave a rough concept representation of complete lattices and showed that rough approximable concepts represent algebraic lattices via two key representation theorems (Theorems 3.2 and 4.6,
[
7
]
).

While the aforementioned works demonstrate
in one direction
that domain theory manifests in rough set theory via formal concept analysis, it has never been shown
in the other direction
what rôle rough set theory plays in domain theory. It is for this very reason that we write this paper – we demonstrate how concepts and well-known results in domain theory can formulated and obtained via our rough set theoretic approach. Readers who are well-acquainted with rough set theory know very well that in Pawlak’s original formulation, the lower and upper approximation operators induced by an equivalence relation
R
on a universe
\mathbb{U}
of finite objects play a fundamental rôle. However, in domain theory, the way-below relation
\ll
on a poset (which is of central importance) is far from being reflexive, let alone an equivalence relation. For us, the most critical step in the adopting a rough set theoretic approach to domain theory is to get around this problem by defining a new set of approximation operators that is best suited for irreflexive binary relations on posets.

We first gather all the domain-theoretic preliminaries needed for our present development in Section
2
, and then study how the core concepts in domain theory can be formulated using ideas from rough set theory in Section
3
. This study is carried out over two subsections: (1) We begin by defining the new approximation operators induced by an auxiliary relation on a poset in Subsection
3.1
. Amongst the several order-theoretic properties that we prove concerning these operators, the highlight is a certain characterization of the interpolation property enjoyed by a given auxiliary relation in terms of the lower (respectively, upper) approximation operators. (2) We proceed to establish more domain-theoretic results in Subsection
3.2
by requiring further that the auxiliary relation is (pre-)approximating. To this achieve this, we introduce a new order-compatible topology induced by an (pre-)approximating relation. In the case of a continuous poset, we established that the topology induced by the way-below relation is
exactly
the Scott topology. This newly introduced topology is studied in greater detail in Section
4
.

In this paper, we characterize continuous posets to be precisely those for which the upper approximation operator induced by the way-below relation coincides with the Scott closure operator. Based on this new characterization of continuity, we obtain a novel characterization of Scott closed sets of a continuous poset–a matter which we continue to pursue further later in Section
5
. This further investigation reveals some link with an earlier result (unpublished) by Dongsheng Zhao
[
17
]
. We conclude this paper in Section
6
by pointing the reader to possible future directions and posing some open questions.

For references, we point the reader to
[
1
,
3
]
for domain theory, and
[
4
]
for rough set theory.

2.
Preliminaries

A
partially ordered set
(
poset
, for short) is a non-empty set
P
equipped with a reflexive, transitive and antisymmetric relation
\leq
. If
P
is a poset and
X\subseteq P
, we use the symbol
\bigvee X
(respectively,
\bigwedge X
) to denote the
least upper bound
or
supremum
(respectively, the
greatest lower bound
or
infimum
) of
X
, if it exists. We define the
upper closure
of
X
by

\mathord{\uparrow}X:=\{x\in P\mid\exists y\in X.~y\leq x\},

and dually,
\mathord{\downarrow}X
, the
lower closure
of
X
.
If
X=\mathord{\uparrow}X
(respectively,
X=\mathord{\downarrow}X
), then
X
is called an
upper
(respectively,
lower
) subset of
P
. It is well known that the collection of all upper (respectively, lower) subsets of a poset
P
ordered by inclusion is a complete lattice, and we denote it by
\mathcal{U}(P)

(respectively,
\mathcal{L}(P)
).

A subset
D
of
P
is
directed
(dually,
filtered
) provided it is non-empty and every finite subset of
D
has an upper (dually, lower) bound in
D
. A poset of which every directed subset has a supremum is called a
directed-complete partial order
(
dcpo
, for short).

In domain theory, the most important binary relation on a poset is the way-below relation, which we define as follows. Let
P
be a poset. We say that
x
is
way below

y
, denoted by
x\ll y
if for all directed subsets
D\subseteq P
for which
\bigvee D
exists, the relation
\bigvee D\geq y
always implies
x\in\mathord{\downarrow}D
. An element
x
satisfying the condition
x\ll x
is said to be
compact
. For each
x\in P
, we use the notations

\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadleftarrow$}}x:=\{y\in P\mid y\ll x\}\text{ and }\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadrightarrow$}}x:=\{y\in P\mid x\ll y\}.

It follows immediately from the definition of
\ll
that

\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadleftarrow$}}x
(respectively,
\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadrightarrow$}}x
) is a lower (respectively, upper) subset of
P
. A poset
P
is said to be
continuous
if for any
x\in P
, the set
\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadleftarrow$}}x
is directed and

x=\bigvee\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadleftarrow$}}x
.

The following proposition often comes in handy to prove that a poset is continuous.

Proposition 1
.

[
3
]

Let
P
be a poset. If for each
x\in P
there exists a directed set
D_{x}\subseteq\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadleftarrow$}}x
with
\bigvee D_{x}=x
, then

(1)

\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadleftarrow$}}x
is directed, and

(2)

\bigvee\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadleftarrow$}}x=x
.

In particular,
P
is continuous.

A binary relation
\prec
on a poset
P
is called an
auxiliary relation
if it satisfies the following conditions for any
u,~x,~y
and
z\in P
:

(1)

x\prec y
implies
x\leq y
;

(2)

u\leq x\prec y\leq z
implies
u\prec z
;

(3)

if the smallest element
\bot
of
P
exists, then
\bot\prec x
.

The set of all auxiliary relations on
P
is denoted by
\operatorname{Aux}(P)
.
Clearly, both
\leq
and
\ll
are auxiliary relations on
P
.

Let
{\prec}\in\operatorname{Aux}(P)
and
x\in P
.
We denote the set
\{y\in P\mid y\prec x\}
by
s_{\prec}(x)
.
From the definition of auxiliary relation, we have that
s_{\prec}(x)\in\mathcal{L}(P)
.

The set
\operatorname{Aux}(P)
is a complete lattice with respect to the containment of graphs as subsets of
P\times P
since it is closed under arbitrary intersections and unions in
P\times P
.

An auxiliary relation
\prec
on a poset
P
is called
pre-approximating
if for any
x\in P
,
s_{\prec}(x)
is directed. If in addition
x=\bigvee s_{\prec}(x)
holds for any
x\in P
, then we say that
\prec
is
approximating
. The set of all pre-approximating (respectively, approximating) auxiliary relations is denoted by
\operatorname{PApp}(P)
(respectively,
\operatorname{App}(P)
). For a poset
(P,\leq)
, the order relation
\leq
is trivially approximating, and the way below relation
\ll
is approximating if and only if
P
is continuous.

Proposition 2
.

[
3
]

In a poset
P
, the way below relation
\ll
is contained in all approximating auxiliary relations, i.e.,

\ll~\subseteq~\bigcap\{\prec\mid{\prec}\in\operatorname{App}(P)\}.

If
P
is a continuous poset, then

\ll~=~\bigcap\{\prec\mid{\prec}\in\operatorname{App}(P)\}.

Thus, the following are equivalent for a poset
P
:

(1)

P
is continuous.

(2)

The way-below relation
\ll
is the smallest approximating relation on
P
.

An auxiliary relation
\prec
on a poset
P
is said to satisfy the
Interpolation Property
(INT), provided that for all
x
and
z\in P
we have

(x\prec z)\implies(\exists y\in P.~x\prec y\prec z).

(INT)

In domain theory, much depends on the fact that
\ll
satisfies the interpolation property.

Proposition 3
.

[
3
]

Let
P
be a continuous poset. Then
\ll
satisfies (INT).

The most prominently featured topology in domain theory is the
Scott topology
. Let
P
be a poset and
U\subseteq P
. We say that
U
is
Scott open
if

(1)

U=\mathord{\uparrow}U
;

(2)

U
is inaccessible by directed suprema, i.e., whenever a directed subset
D
is such that
\sup D\in U
, there already exists
d\in D
such that
d\in U
.

A set is
Scott closed
if it is the complement of a Scott open set.
It follows immediately that a subset
A
of
P
is Scott closed if and only if it is lower and closed under the formation of directed suprema.
The collection of all Scott open subsets of
P
forms a topology on
P
called the
Scott topology
and is denoted by
\sigma(P)
.

As a direct consequence of Proposition
3
, we have:

Proposition 4
.

[
3
]

Let
P
be a continuous poset and
x\in P
.

(1)

The set
\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadrightarrow$}}x
is Scott open.

(2)

The collection
\mathcal{B}:=\{\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadrightarrow$}}x\mid x\in P\}
forms a base for the Scott topology on
P
.

(3)

The Scott interior of
\mathord{\uparrow}x
is precisely
\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadrightarrow$}}x
, i.e.,
\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(\mathord{\uparrow}x)=\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadrightarrow$}}x
.

3.
Approximation operators

We can define many kinds of operators for domain theory. In this paper, we aim to get ”approximation” operators. Here approximation operator means that for each subset of a poset, it is always captured between its lower and upper approximations.

3.1.
Auxiliary relation

In this subsection, we define a new set of approximation operators for domain theory.

{defi}
[Lower and upper approximations]
Let
P
be a poset and
{\prec}\in\operatorname{Aux}(P)
. For each subset
A
of
P
, the lower and upper approximations of
A
(with respect to
\prec
), denoted by
{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
and
{A}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}
respectively, are defined by

{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}:=\{x\in P\mid x\in A\ \&\ s_{\prec}(x)\cap A\neq\emptyset\}

and

{A}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}:=\{x\in P\mid s_{\prec}(x)\subseteq\mathord{\downarrow}A\}.

The following are immediate consequences arising from Definition
3.1
:

Proposition 5
.

Let
P
be a poset,
{\prec}\in\operatorname{Aux}(P)
,
A\subseteq P
and
a\in P
. Then the following properties hold:

(1)

{A}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}={(\mathord{\downarrow}A)}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}
.

(2)

A^{\mathord{\uparrow}\leq}=\mathord{\downarrow}A
and
A^{\mathord{\downarrow}\leq}=A
.

(3)

(\mathord{\uparrow}a)^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}=\{x\in P\mid a\prec x\}
.

(4)

For any set
A\subseteq P
,
x\in{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
if and only if there exists
y\in A
such that
y\prec x
.

(5)

{A}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}\in\mathcal{L}(P)
.

(6)

If
A\in\mathcal{U}(P)
, then
{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}\in\mathcal{U}(P)
.

Our choice of definition for the lower and upper approximations is justified in view of the following proposition:

Proposition 6
.

Let
P
be a poset. For each
A\subseteq P
and
{\prec}\in\operatorname{Aux}(P)
, we have

{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}\subseteq A\subseteq{A}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}.

Given a poset
P
and
{\prec}\in\operatorname{Aux}(P)
, we always have

{\emptyset}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}=\emptyset\text{ and }{P}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}=P.

It is very tempting to conclude that
{\emptyset}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}=\emptyset
. But this is not true in general.
For instance, if
P
has no bottom element, there may still be an element
x\in P
such that
s_{\prec}(x)=\emptyset
.
The following proposition tells us more about this situation.

Proposition 7
.

Let
P
be a poset and
{\prec}\in\operatorname{Aux}(P)
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

For any
x\in P
, the set
s_{\prec}(x)
is nonempty.

(2)

{\emptyset}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}=\emptyset
.

(3)

{P}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}=P
.

Proof 3.1
.

(1)
\implies
(2): It is obvious by Definition
3.1
.

(2)
\implies
(3): Suppose that
{P}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}\neq P
. Then there exists
x\in P
such that
x\not\in{P}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
.
This means that
s_{\prec}(x)\cap P=\emptyset
.
Thus,
s_{\prec}(x)=\emptyset
. This means that
s_{\prec}(x)\subseteq\mathord{\downarrow}\emptyset
, and hence
{\emptyset}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}\neq\emptyset
because
x\in{\emptyset}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
, and thus (2) cannot hold.

(3)
\implies
(1): Suppose for the sake of contradiction that there is
x\in P
such that
s_{\prec}(x)=\emptyset
.
Then we have
s_{\prec}(x)\cap P=\emptyset
.
This implies that there exists
x\in P
for which the condition
s_{\prec}(x)\cap P\neq\emptyset
fails to hold, i.e., there is
x\in P\backslash{P}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
, and thus (3) cannot hold.

We now turn the lower and upper approximations into set-valued operators.
Let
P
be a poset, and we denote the collection of upper (respectively, lower) subsets of
P
by
\mathcal{U}(P)
(respectively,
\mathcal{L}(P)
. Recall that
(\mathcal{U}(P),\subseteq)
and
(\mathcal{L}(P),\subseteq)
are complete lattices. In each case, infima are calculated as intersections and suprema as unions.

It is easy to establish the following:

Proposition 8
.

Let
P
be a poset and
{\prec}\in\operatorname{Aux}(P)
.
Then the following hold:

(1)

For any collection
\{A_{i}\mid i\in I\}
of lower subsets of
P
,

{\left(\bigcap_{i\in I}A_{i}\right)}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}=\bigcap_{i\in I}{A_{i}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}.

(2)

For any collection
\{B_{i}\mid i\in I\}
of upper subsets of
P
,

{\left(\bigcup_{i\in I}B_{i}\right)}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}=\bigcup_{i\in I}{B_{i}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}.

Considering the mappings
{(-)}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}:\mathcal{L}(P)\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}(P),~A\mapsto{A}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}
and
{(-)}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}:\mathcal{U}(P)\longrightarrow\mathcal{U}(P),~A\mapsto{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
, they are both very special monotone mappings in that they are part of certain adjunctions. At this juncture, the reader may like to recall that a pair of monotone maps
f:S\longrightarrow T
and
g:T\longrightarrow S
between posets
S
and
T
is called an
adjunction
if for each
s\in S
and
t\in T
,
f(s)\geq t
if and only if
s\geq g(t)
. In an adjunction
(f,g)
, the function
f
is called the upper adjoint and
g
the lower adjoint.

Lemma 9
.

[
3
]

(1)

Let
f:S\longrightarrow T
be a function between posets of which
S
is a complete lattice. Then
f
preserves infs iff
f
is monotone and has a lower adjoint.

(2)

Let
g:T\longrightarrow S
be a function between posets of which
T
is a complete lattice. Then
g
preserves sups iff
g
is monotone and has an upper adjoint.

Corollary 10
.

(1)

The monotone map

{(-)}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}:\mathcal{L}(P)\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}(P),~A\mapsto{A}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}

has a lower adjoint.

(2)

The monotone mapping

{(-)}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}:\mathcal{U}(P)\longrightarrow\mathcal{U}(P),~A\mapsto{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}

has an upper adjoint.

The upper and lower approximations are closely connected to each other.

Let
P
be a poset and
\prec
an auxiliary relation on
P
. For any
A\subseteq P
, we have:

\begin{split}&x\in{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}\\
\iff&(x\in A)\land(s_{\prec}(x)\cap A\neq\emptyset)\\
\iff&(x\in A)\land\neg(s_{\prec}(x)\cap A=\emptyset)\\
\iff&(x\in A)\land\neg(s_{\prec}(x)\subseteq P\backslash A).\end{split}

Observe that
s_{\prec}(x)\subseteq P\backslash A
always implies
x\in{(P\backslash A)}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}
because we always have
(P\backslash A)\subseteq\mathord{\downarrow}(P\backslash A)
.
Thus, contrapositivity forces that

\neg(x\in{(P\backslash A)}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec})\implies\neg(s_{\prec}(x)\subseteq P\backslash A)

which in turn entails that

(x\in A)\land\neg(x\in{(P\backslash A)}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec})\implies(x\in A)\land\neg(s_{\prec}(x)\subseteq P\backslash A),

i.e.,

A\cap(P\backslash{(P\backslash A)}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec})\subseteq{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}.

In the event that
A
is an upper set, then
P\backslash A
is lower so that
\mathord{\downarrow}(P\backslash A)=P\backslash A
.
Thus, we have

{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}=A\cap(P\backslash{(P\backslash A)}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec})

if
A
is an upper set.

The above discussion establishes the following partition property:

Theorem 11
.

Let
P
be a poset,
{\prec}\in\operatorname{Aux}(P)
and
A\subseteq P
.
Then the following hold:

(1)

{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}\cup{(P\backslash A)}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}=P
.

(2)

If in addition
A
is an upper set of
P
, then

{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}\cap{(P\backslash A)}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}=\emptyset.

That is,
{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
and
{(P\backslash A)}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}
define a partition on
P
.

One advantage of our operators is that it allows us to characterize the interpolating property of an auxiliary relation
\prec
. More precisely, an auxiliary relation is interpolating if and only if the upper (respectively, lower) approximation operator is a closure (respectively, kernel) operator on the lattice of lower (respectively, upper) sets. Here, a
closure
(respectively,
kernel
)
operator
refers to an idempotent, monotone self map
f
on a poset
L
with
1_{L}\leq f
(respectively,
f\leq 1_{L}
).

Theorem 12
.

Let P be a poset and
{\prec}\in\operatorname{Aux}(P)
.
Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\prec
satisfies (INT).

(2)

{({A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec})}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}={A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
for every upper set
A
of
P
.

(3)

The lower approximation operator is a kernel operator on the lattice,
{(\mathcal{U}(P),\subseteq)}
, of upper sets of
P
.

(4)

{({B}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec})}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}={B}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}
for every lower set
B
of
P
.

(5)

The upper approximation operator is a closure operator on the lattice,
(\mathcal{L}(P),\subseteq)
, of lower sets of
P
.

Proof 3.2
.

We only need to prove the equivalence of (1), (2) and (4).

(1)
\implies
(2): Let
A\in\mathcal{U}(P)
. By virtue of Proposition
6
, it suffices to show that
{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}\subseteq{({A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec})}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
. To this end, let
x\in{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
. Then there exists
y\in A
such that
y\prec x
.
By the (INT) property, there exists
z\in P
such that
y\prec z\prec x
. Since
A
is upper and
y\in A
, we have
z\in A
.
Hence
y\in{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
, and this implies
x\in{({A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec})}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
.

(2)
\implies
(1): Let
x
and
y
be elements of
P
such that
x\prec y
. By Proposition
5
(3), it follows that
y\in{(\mathord{\uparrow}x)}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
. Note that
\mathord{\uparrow}x\in\mathcal{U}(P)
so that by (2), we have that
{(\mathord{\uparrow}x)}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}={({(\mathord{\uparrow}x)}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec})}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
. So,
y\in{({(\mathord{\uparrow}x)}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec})}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
. Thus,
s_{\prec}(y)\cap{(\mathord{\uparrow}x)}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}\neq\emptyset
, and so there exists
z\prec y
with
z\in{(\mathord{\uparrow}x)}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
. Then there is
w\prec z
such that
w\in\mathord{\uparrow}x
. Consequently, we have
x\leq w\prec z\prec y
which implies that
x\prec z\prec y
for some
z\in P
. Thus,
\prec
satisfies the (INT) property.

(2)
\iff
(4): Immediate result of two applications of Theorem
11
.

On a poset
P
, the set
\operatorname{Aux}(P)
of auxiliary relations on
P
is a complete lattice relative to the containment of graphs as subsets of
P\times P
. We now develop certain basic properties of approximation operators arising from different auxiliary relations on
P
.

Proposition 13
.

Let
P
be a poset and
\prec_{1}\subseteq\prec_{2}
in
\operatorname{Aux}(P)
.
Then the following containments hold for any subset
A
of
P
:

A^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec_{1}}\subseteq A^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec_{2}}\text{ and }A^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec_{1}}\supseteq A^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec_{2}}.

Remark 3.3
.

(1)

In view of Proposition
6
that

{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}\subseteq A\subseteq{A}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec},

as
\prec
increases in
\operatorname{Aux}(P)
, the upper and lower approximations become closer to
A
, i.e., they give a better estimation of
A
.
In the extreme case when
\leq
, the largest element of
\operatorname{Aux}(P)
is taken, we have
A^{\mathord{\downarrow}\leq}=A
and
A^{\mathord{\uparrow}\leq}=\mathord{\downarrow}A
.

(2)

Let
A
be a fixed subset of
P
. Then we can talk about the aforementioned effect of auxiliary relations in
\operatorname{Aux}(P)
on the corresponding approximation operators by viewing this cause-and-effect phenomenon as monotone mappings. More precisely, the mappings defined below

u:(\operatorname{Aux}(P),\subseteq)\longrightarrow(\mathcal{P}(P),\supseteq),~\prec\mapsto{A}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}

and

l:(\operatorname{Aux}(P),\subseteq)\longrightarrow(\mathcal{P}(P),\subseteq),~\prec\mapsto{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}.

are monotone mappings between the complete lattices
(\operatorname{Aux}(P),\subseteq)
and
(\mathcal{P}(P),\supseteq)
(respectively,
(\mathcal{P}(P),\subseteq)
).

Indeed, the monotone mappings
u
and
l
, defined above in the remark, are lattice homomorphisms in the following sense:

Proposition 14
.

Let
P
be a poset,
A\subseteq P
and
\prec_{1},\prec_{2}\in\operatorname{Aux}(P)
. Then the following hold:

(1)

A^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec_{1}}\cap A^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec_{2}}=A^{\mathord{\uparrow}(\prec_{1}\cup\prec_{2})}
.

(2)

A^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec_{1}}\cup A^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec_{2}}=A^{\mathord{\downarrow}(\prec_{1}\cup\prec_{2})}
.

(3)

If
A
is filtered, then
A^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec_{1}}\cap A^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec_{2}}=A^{\mathord{\downarrow}(\prec_{1}\cap\prec_{2})}
.

Proof 3.4
.

Notice that for each
x\in P
, we always have:

s_{\prec_{1}\cup\prec_{2}}(x)=s_{\prec_{1}}(x)\cup s_{\prec_{2}}(x)

and

s_{\prec_{1}\cap\prec_{2}}(x)=s_{\prec_{1}}(x)\cap s_{\prec_{2}}(x).

Thus, (1) and (2) follow immediately.

For (3), if
x\in A^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec_{1}}\cap A^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec_{2}}
, then there are elements
y_{1},~y_{2}\in A
such that
y_{1}\prec_{1}x
and
y_{2}\prec_{2}x
. Since
A
is filtered, there exists
z\in A
such that
z\leq y_{1}
and
z\leq y_{2}
. Thus,
z\prec_{1}x
and
z\prec_{2}x
, and thus,
x\in A^{\mathord{\downarrow}(\prec_{1}\cap\prec_{2})}
.

3.2.
Approximating relations

In this subsection, we make use of the operators introduced in Definition
3.1
in the situation when the auxiliary relation is an approximating one. In particular, we study the relationships between the approximation operators derived from an approximating auxiliary relation and the Scott closure and interior operators. Crucially, we rely on a new topology defined from the given approximating auxiliary relation. Consequently, we obtain a novel characterization of the continuity of posets.

Let us begin with an interesting observation.

Proposition 15
.

Let
P
be a poset and
U\subseteq P
.
If
U
is upper and
U=U^{\mathord{\downarrow}\ll}
, then
U
is Scott open.

Proof 3.5
.

Since
U
is upper, it suffices to show that
U
is inaccessible by existing directed suprema. To this end, let
D
be a directed subset whose supremum,
\bigvee D
, exists and belongs to
U
. Because
U=U^{\mathord{\downarrow}\ll}
, there exists
x\in U
such that already
x\ll\bigvee D
.
This implies that there is
d\in D
such that
d\geq x
. Since
U
is upper,
d\in U
.

This observation leads us to consider, for the case of an arbitrary
{\prec}\in\operatorname{Aux}(P)
, those upper subsets
U
for which
U={U}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
. In general, these subsets do not form a topology on
P
but they do when
\prec
is pre-approximating.

{defi}

Let
P
be a poset and
{\prec}\in\operatorname{PApp}(P)

(1)

(2)

Theorem 16
.

Let
P
be a poset and
{\prec}\in\operatorname{PApp}(P)

Then the collection of
\prec

Proof 3.6
.

Recall that we have earlier remarked that
{\emptyset}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}=\emptyset

is directed and hence non-empty, by Proposition
7

{P}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}=P
. Thus,
\emptyset

are
\prec
-open sets.

Let

be any collection of
\prec

\bigcup_{i\in I}U_{i}
is
\prec
-open. For each

U_{i}={U_{i}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}

\bigcup_{i\in I}{U_{i}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
is
\prec
-open. But by Theorem

\bigcup_{i\in I}{U_{i}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}={\left(\bigcup_{i\in I}U_{i}\right)}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}

\bigcup_{i\in I}{U_{i}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}={\left(\bigcup_{i\in I}{U_{i}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}\right)}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
which completes the argument that
\bigcup_{i\in I}U_{i}
is
\prec
-open.

Let
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
be
\prec
-opens. We now prove that
U_{1}\cap U_{2}
is again
\prec
-open. It suffices to show that
{(U_{1}\cap U_{2})}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}=U_{1}\cap U_{2}
since we have
U_{1}={U_{1}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
and
U_{2}={U_{2}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
. To achieve this, we rely on Proposition

(4). Let

. This implies that
x\in U_{1}={U_{1}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
and
x\in U_{2}={U_{2}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
. Thus, by Proposition
5
(4), there exist
y_{1}\in U_{1}
and
y_{2}\in U_{2}
such that
y_{1}\prec x
and
y_{2}\prec x
.
Thus,
y_{1}
and
y_{2}\in s_{\prec}(x)
. Since
s_{\prec}(x)
is directed by assumption, there exists
y\in s_{\prec}(x)
such that
y_{1},~y_{2}\leq y
.
But
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
are upper, and thus,
y\in U_{1}\cap U_{2}
with

. By Proposition
5
(4), it follows that
{(U_{1}\cap U_{2})}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}=U_{1}\cap U_{2}
.

{defi}

[Topology generated by lower approximation]

We denote the collection of

or the

and the corresponding topological space,

[

.
. Whenever, we mention the

Proposition

already hints at a close relationship between the
\mu^{\prec}

Indeed, a
\prec
-open set bears some semblance to a Scott open set in the following sense:

Proposition 17
.

Let
P
be a poset,
U\subseteq P
and
{\prec}\in\operatorname{PApp}(P)
.
Consider the following statements:

(1)

U\in\mu^{\prec}(P)
.

(2)

is upper and inaccessible by existing suprema of directed sets of the form

Then (1)
\implies
(2).

If, in addition
{\prec}\in\operatorname{App}(P)
, then (2)
\implies
(1).

Proof 3.7
.

(1)
\implies
(2): Suppose
x\in P
is such that
\bigvee s_{\prec}(x)
exists and belongs to
U
. Since
U={U}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
, there exists
y\in U
such that
y\prec\bigvee s_{\prec}(x)\leq x
and so
y\prec x
.

Assume further that
{\prec}\in\operatorname{App}(P)
.
Then for each

,
s_{\prec}(x)
is directed and
\bigvee s_{\prec}(x)=x
. By (2), there exists
y\in s_{\prec}(x)
such that

, i.e.,
x\in{U}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
. So, (2)
\implies
(1).

The above immediately yields:

Corollary 18
.

Let
P
be a poset and
{\prec}\in\operatorname{App}(P)
.
Then the
\mu^{\prec}
-topology is finer than the Scott topology, i.e.,

\sigma(P)\subseteq\mu^{\prec}(P)
.

Proposition 19
.

Let
P
be a poset and
{\prec}\in\operatorname{App}(P)
.
Then the
\mu^{\prec}
-topology is order-compatible, i.e., its specialization order coincides with the underlying order.

Proof 3.8
.

Since the
\mu^{\prec}
-topology is finer than the Scott topology, its specialization order is contained in that of the Scott topology, which is the underlying order. The reverse containment holds by virtue of the fact that
\prec
-opens are upper sets.

Remark 3.9
.

The
\mu^{\ll}
-topology and Scott topology on a continuous poset coincide.

Proof 3.10
.

Immediate from Proposition
15
and
18
.

Lemma 20
.

Let
P
be a poset,
A\subseteq P
and
{\prec}\in\operatorname{App}(P)
. Then

\operatorname{int}_{\mu^{\prec}}(A)\subseteq{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}.

Proof 3.11
.

Follows from
\operatorname{int}_{\mu^{\prec}}(A)\subseteq A
and the monotonicity of the lower approximation operator on the power-set lattice.

Lemma 21
.

Let
P
be a poset,
A\subseteq P
and
{\prec}\in\operatorname{App}(P)
. Then

{A}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}\subseteq\operatorname{cl}_{\mu^{\prec}}(A).

Proof 3.12
.

We first prove that for each subset
B\subseteq P
, it is
\mu^{\prec}
-closed iff it is lower and closed under suprema of set of the form
s_{\prec}(a)
,
a\in P
. Suppose
B
is
\mu^{\prec}
-closed and there exists
a\in P
such that
s_{\prec}(a)\subseteq B
, then
a\in B
because
P\backslash B
is
\mu^{\prec}
-open.
The contrary is obvious.

For each
x\in{A}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}
,
s_{\prec}(x)\subseteq{\downarrow}A\subseteq\operatorname{cl}_{\mu^{\prec}}(A)
. Then
x\in\operatorname{cl}_{\mu^{\prec}}(A)
.

The following standard topological result comes in handy soon:

Lemma 22
.

For any topological space
(X,\tau)
and
A\subseteq X
, it holds that

X\backslash\operatorname{cl}_{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{int}_{\tau}(X\backslash A)

and hence
\operatorname{cl}_{\tau}(A)
and
\operatorname{int}_{\tau}(X\backslash A)
define a partition on

.

Theorem 23
.

Let
P
be a poset,
A\subseteq P
and
{\prec}\in\operatorname{App}(P)
. Then the following chain of set inclusions hold:

\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(A)\subseteq\operatorname{int}_{\mu^{\prec}}(A)\subseteq{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}\subseteq A\subseteq{A}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}\subseteq\operatorname{cl}_{\mu^{\prec}}{(A)}\subseteq\operatorname{cl}_{\sigma}(A).

Proof 3.13
.

That
\operatorname{int}_{\mu^{\prec}}(A)\subseteq{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}\subseteq A\subseteq{A}^{\mathord{\uparrow}\prec}

holds follows from Lemmas
20
,
21
and

6
. Notice that if we can establish that

\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(A)\subseteq\operatorname{int}_{\mu^{\prec}}(A)
holds for any subset
A
of
P
, then it follows from Lemma
22
and Theorem
11
that
\operatorname{cl}_{\mu^{\prec}}(A)\subseteq\operatorname{cl}_{\sigma}(A)
. Thus, it remains to prove that
\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(A)\subseteq\operatorname{int}_{\mu^{\prec}}(A)
. But this is immediate by Lemma
18
.

Lemma 24
.

If
P
is a continuous poset and
A\in\mathcal{U}(P)
, then

\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(A)=A^{\mathord{\downarrow}\ll}.

Proof 3.14
.

Since
P
is a continuous poset,
\ll
enjoys the (INT) property. Applying Theorem
12
for the upper set
A
, we have
(A^{\mathord{\downarrow}\ll})^{\mathord{\downarrow}\ll}=A^{\mathord{\downarrow}\ll}
. Thus,
A^{\mathord{\downarrow}\ll}
is a
\ll
-open set that is contained in
A
, and so
A^{\mathord{\downarrow}\ll}\subseteq\operatorname{int}_{\mu^{\ll}}(A)
. But Theorem
23
asserts that
\operatorname{int}_{\mu^{\ll}}(A)\subseteq A^{\mathord{\downarrow}\ll}
so that
\operatorname{int}_{\mu^{\ll}}(A)=A^{\mathord{\downarrow}\ll}
.
Finally,
P
is continuous and so Theorem
3.9
asserts that
\operatorname{int}_{\mu^{\ll}}(A)=\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(A)
. Thus, the proof is complete.

Lemma 25
.

If
P
is a poset such that every
A\in\mathcal{U}(P)
satisfies the equation

A^{\mathord{\downarrow}\ll}=\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(A),

then
P
is continuous.

Proof 3.15
.

Let
x\in P
be given. We need to show that

(1)

\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadleftarrow$}}x
is a directed set; and

(2)

\bigvee\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadleftarrow$}}x=x
.

Let
a,~b\in\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadleftarrow$}}x
be given.
Thus,
x\in\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadrightarrow$}}a
and
x\in\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadrightarrow$}}b
so that
x\in\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadrightarrow$}}a\cap\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadrightarrow$}}b
. By Proposition
5
(3),
(\mathord{\uparrow}a)^{\mathord{\downarrow}\ll}=\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadrightarrow$}}a
and

(\mathord{\uparrow}b)^{\mathord{\downarrow}\ll}=\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadrightarrow$}}b
.
By the given assumption,
\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadrightarrow$}}a=(\mathord{\uparrow}a)^{\mathord{\downarrow}\ll}=\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(\mathord{\uparrow}a)

and
\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadrightarrow$}}b=(\mathord{\uparrow}b)^{\mathord{\downarrow}\ll}=\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(\mathord{\uparrow}b)
. This implies that

x\in\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadrightarrow$}}a\cap\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadrightarrow$}}b=\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(\mathord{\uparrow}a)\cap\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(\mathord{\uparrow}b)=\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(\mathord{\uparrow}a\cap\mathord{\uparrow}b)=(\mathord{\uparrow}a\cap\mathord{\uparrow}b)^{\mathord{\downarrow}\ll}.

Thus there exists
c\in\mathord{\uparrow}a\cap\mathord{\uparrow}b
such that
c\ll x
.
This proves that
\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadleftarrow$}}x
is a directed set.

We now show that
\bigvee\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadleftarrow$}}x=x
. Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
\bigvee\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadleftarrow$}}x\neq x
. Because
x
is an upper bound of
\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadleftarrow$}}x
, this would mean that there is another upper bound
y
of
\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadleftarrow$}}x
such that
x\not\leq y
, i.e.,
x\in P\backslash\mathord{\downarrow}y
. Since
\mathord{\downarrow}y
is a Scott closed subset, the set
P\backslash\mathord{\downarrow}y
is Scott open, and hence equals to its Scott interior.
Thus,
x\in\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(P\backslash\mathord{\downarrow}y)=(P\backslash\mathord{\downarrow}y)^{\mathord{\downarrow}\ll}

by the assumption. Therefore there is an element
w\in P\backslash\mathord{\downarrow}y
such that
w\ll x
. This contradicts that
y
is an upper bound of the set
\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadleftarrow$}}x
since
w
belongs to
\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadleftarrow$}}x
and yet
w\not\leq y
. Then the poset
P
is continuous.

Lemma 26
.

If
P
is a poset with an approximating auxiliary relation
\prec
satisfying
{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}=\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(A)
for every
A\in\mathcal{U}(P)
, then the following hold:

(1)

\mu^{\prec}(P)=\sigma(P)
.

(2)

\prec=\ll
.

(3)

P
is continuous.

Proof 3.16
.

(1)

Note that
{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}=\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(A)
for every upper set
A
implies that

{({A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec})}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}=\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}A)=\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(A)={A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}.

Thus,
{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
is
\prec
-open, i.e.,
{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}=\operatorname{int}_{\mu^{\prec}}(A)
.
So, for any
\prec
-open set
U
, since it is upper by definition, we have that

U={U}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}=\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(U)
, which implies that every
\prec
-open set is Scott open. This proves (1).

(2)

Note that by Proposition
2
,
\ll\subseteq\prec
for any approximating auxiliary relation
\prec
. Let
x
and
y\in P
be such that
x\prec y
. We aim to show that
x\ll y
. Let
D
be a directed set whose supremum exists, and
\bigvee D\geq y
. Note that
\{w\in P\mid x\prec w\}={(\mathord{\uparrow}x)}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}=\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(\mathord{\uparrow}x)
is a Scott open set that contains

\bigvee D\geq y

\bigvee D\in\{w\in P\mid x\prec w\}

d\in D

x\prec d

x\leq d

Thus,
\prec=\ll

(3)

By the preceding part,
\prec=\ll

P
is continuous.

(2)

4.
Further properties of the
\mu^{\prec}
-topology

The
\mu^{\prec}
-topology induced by an approximating auxiliary relation on a
poset has very tight connections with the Scott topology as revealed in Section

{defi}

[c-space]
[
5
]

A
c-space
is a topological space
X
such that, for every
x\in X
, for every open neighbourhood
U
of
x
, there is a point
y\in U
such that
x\in\operatorname{int}(\mathord{\uparrow}y)
.

Lemma 29
.

Let
P
be a poset and
{\prec}\in\operatorname{PApp}(P)
.
If
P_{\mu^{\prec}}
is a

-space, then
\prec
is approximating.

Proof 4.1
.

Let
x\in P
be given. Since
\prec
is pre-approximating (i.e.,
s_{\prec}(x)
is directed), it suffices to prove that
\bigvee s_{\prec}(x)=x
. To do this, define a subset
D_{x}
of
P
as follows:

D_{x}:=\{y\in P\mid x\in\operatorname{int}_{\mu^{\prec}}(\mathord{\uparrow}y)\}.

Since
P_{\mu^{\prec}}
is a c-space, taking the open neighbourhood
U=P
of
x
, there exists
y\in P
such that
x\in\operatorname{int}_{\mu^{\prec}}(\mathord{\uparrow}y)
. Hence
D_{x}\neq\emptyset
.

We now prove that
D_{x}\subseteq s_{\prec}(x)
. For each
y\in D_{x}
, we have
x\in\operatorname{int}_{\mu^{\prec}}(\mathord{\uparrow}y)={\left(\operatorname{int}_{\mu^{\prec}}(\mathord{\uparrow}y)\right)}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}\subseteq{(\mathord{\uparrow}y)}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
. Hence

x\in{(\mathord{\uparrow}y)}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}
which then implies that there exists
p\in s_{\prec}(x)\cap\mathord{\uparrow}y
, i.e.,
y\leq p\prec x
. Since
\prec
is an auxiliary relation, it follows that
y\prec x
. Thus,
D_{x}\subseteq s_{\prec}(x)
.

Since
D_{x}\subseteq s_{\prec}(x)
, it is immediate that
x
is an upper bound of
D_{x}
.
We now show that
\bigvee D_{x}=x
. Let
z
be any upper bound of
D_{x}
. We show that
x\leq z
by showing that every open neighbourhood
U
of
x
contains
z
, and using the fact that
\leq
is the specialization order of
P_{\mu^{\prec}}
by virtue of Proposition
19
. Since
P_{\mu^{\prec}}
is a c-space,
x\in U
implies that there exists
y\in U
such that
x\in\operatorname{int}_{\mu^{\prec}}(\mathord{\uparrow}y)
, i.e.,
y\in D_{x}
. Since
z
is an upper bound of
D_{x}
, we have
y\leq z
, and since
U
is an upper set with respect to the specialization order
\leq
,
z\in U
. Because
s_{\prec}(x)
contains a non-empty subset, i.e.,
D_{x}
whose supremum equals to
x
, and
x
is an upper bound for
s_{\prec}(x)
, it follows that
\bigvee s_{\prec}(x)=x
. Thus, the proof that
\prec
is approximating is complete.

Lemma 30
.

Let
P
be a poset and
{\prec}\in\operatorname{PApp}(P)
which satisfies (INT).
Then the following hold:

(1)

For each
x\in P
,
s_{\succ}(x):=\{y\in P\mid x\prec y\}
is a
\prec
-open set.

(2)

The collection
\mathcal{B}:=\{s_{\succ}(x)\mid x\in P\}

\mu^{\prec}

Proof 4.2

(1)

Let
x\in P
be given. Clearly,
s_{\succ}(x)
is an upper set. Then
s_{\succ}(x):=\{y\in P\mid x\prec y\}

\prec
-open set from Theorem

(2)

Let
U
be a
\prec
-open set and
x\in U

U={U}^{\mathord{\downarrow}\prec}

y\in U

Proof 4.4

\implies

5.
Scott closure in a continuous poset

In this section, we showcase some domain-theoretic spin-offs that are a direct consequence of findings in the preceding section. In particular, we recall a characterization of Scott closed sets (c.f. Corollary
28
), i.e., the Scott closure of a subset
A
of a continuous poset comprises precisely those elements way-below which are all members of
\mathord{\downarrow}A
. We now proceed with a more in-depth study of the structure of
\operatorname{cl}_{\sigma}(A)
, following up this lead.

A subset
A
of a poset
P
is Scott closed if and only if it is a lower subset of
P
and closed under the formation of existing directed suprema.
One might claim that if one takes all the existing directed suprema of subsets of
\mathord{\downarrow}A
then one should obtain the Scott closure of
A
.

Example 5.1
.

The preceding claim can be easily refuted by the following counterexample
P
: (See Figure
1
.)

Figure 1
.
P

Let
A:=\{a_{ij},i,j\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Note that
1\in\operatorname{cl}_{\sigma}(A)=P
but there is no directed subset
D
in
{\downarrow}A
for which
\bigvee D=1
.
Thus, taking all the existing directed suprema of
A
in addition to those elements in
\mathord{\downarrow}A
does not, in general, form the Scott closure of
A
.

Let
P
be a poset and
A\subseteq P
, we define

A^{\prime}:=\{x\in P\mid\exists\text{ directed subset }D\text{ of }\mathord{\downarrow}A.~x=\bigvee D\}.

Proposition 34
.

Let
P
be a poset and
A\subseteq P
. Then, the following hold:

(1)

A\subseteq\mathord{\downarrow}A\subseteq A^{\prime}\subseteq\operatorname{cl}_{\sigma}(A)
.

(2)

A^{\prime}\subseteq A^{\uparrow\ll}
.

(3)

A^{\prime}=A
if and only if
A
is a Scott closed set.

Proof 5.2
.

(1)

Obvious.

(2)

Suppose
x\in A^{\prime}
. Then there is a directed subset
D
of
\mathord{\downarrow}A
such that
\bigvee D=x
. For each
y\ll x
, there is an element
d\in D
such that
y\leq d
.
Thus,
y\in\mathord{\downarrow}A
so that
\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadleftarrow$}}x\subseteq\mathord{\downarrow}A
. Hence
x\in A^{\uparrow\ll}
.

(3)

If
A^{\prime}=A
, then
A
is a lower set and closed for existing directed sups which implies that
A
is a Scott closed set. If
A
is a Scott closed set, then by (1),
we have
A\subseteq A^{\prime}\subseteq\operatorname{cl}_{\sigma}(A)=A
. Hence
A^{\prime}=A
.

{defi}

[One-step closure]
A poset
P
is said to have
one-step closure
if
A^{\prime}=\operatorname{cl}_{\sigma}(A)
for every subset
A\subseteq P
.

Remark 5.3
.

Let
P
be a poset and
A\subseteq P
. If
A^{\prime}=\operatorname{cl}_{\sigma}(A)
, then
A^{\prime}
is a Scott closed set. Conversely, if
A^{\prime}
is a Scott closed set, then
\operatorname{cl}_{\sigma}(A^{\prime})=A^{\prime}
.
By Proposition
34
(1),
A\subseteq A^{\prime}
always holds so that
\operatorname{cl}_{\sigma}(A)\subseteq\operatorname{cl}_{\sigma}(A^{\prime})=A^{\prime}
. Again since Proposition
34
(1) asserts that
A^{\prime}\subseteq\operatorname{cl}_{\sigma}(A)
, we must have
A^{\prime}=\operatorname{cl}_{\sigma}(A)
. Thus, a poset
P
has one-step closure if and only if
A^{\prime}
is Scott closed for every subset
A
of
P
.

Theorem 35
.

Every continuous poset has one-step closure.

Proof 5.4
.

Let
P
be a continuous poset and
A\subseteq P
. Then, on one hand,
A^{\prime}\subseteq\operatorname{cl}_{\sigma}(A)
holds by Proposition
34
(1). On the other hand, if
x\in\operatorname{cl}_{\sigma}(A)
, then
\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadleftarrow$}}x\subseteq\mathord{\downarrow}A
by virtue of Corollary
28
. But
P
is continuous so that

\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadleftarrow$}}x
is directed. These together imply that
x\in A^{\prime}
. Thus, we can conclude that
\operatorname{cl}_{\sigma}(A)=A^{\prime}
.

Remark 5.5
.

At the point of writing, it was brought to the attention of the authors that Theorem
35
had earlier been established by Dongsheng Zhao in his Ph.D. thesis (see
[
17
, Lemma 2.5]
) but the argument we use here has a very different motivation from Zhao’s.

What order-theoretic properties does a poset having one-step closure enjoy?
To answer this question, we need to recall the notion of meet-continuity of posets which was first introduced by
[
6
]
.

{defi}

A poset
P
is called
meet continuous
if for any
x\in P
and any directed subset
D
, whenever
\bigvee D
exists and
x\leq\bigvee D
, then

x\in\operatorname{cl}_{\sigma}(\mathord{\downarrow}D\cap\mathord{\downarrow}x)
.

Theorem 36
.

All posets having one-step closure are meet-continuous.

Proof 5.6
.

Suppose that there is a directed set
D\subseteq P
whose supremum exists and
x\in P
is such that
x\leq\bigvee D
. Then
\bigvee D\in D^{\prime}
. Since
P
has one-step closure, it follows that
D^{\prime}
is a Scott closed set which then implies that
x\in D^{\prime}
. So, there exists a directed set
D_{1}\subseteq\mathord{\downarrow}D
such that
\bigvee D_{1}=x
. It is clear that
D_{1}\subseteq\mathord{\downarrow}D\cap\mathord{\downarrow}x
and hence
x\in(\mathord{\downarrow}D\cap\mathord{\downarrow}x)^{\prime}=\operatorname{cl}_{\sigma}(\mathord{\downarrow}D\cap\mathord{\downarrow}x)
. This then completes the proof that
P
is meet continuous.

Theorem 37
.

Let
P
be a poset that has one-step closure.
Then
\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(\mathord{\uparrow}x)=\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadrightarrow$}}x
for each
x\in P
.

Proof 5.7
.

Since
\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(\mathord{\uparrow}x)\subseteq\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadrightarrow$}}x
always holds for any element
x\in P
, it remains to prove the reverse containment. For the sake of contradiction, suppose that
there is
y\in\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadrightarrow$}}x\setminus\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(\mathord{\uparrow}x)
. Then

y\in P\setminus\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(\mathord{\uparrow}x)=\operatorname{cl}_{\sigma}(P\setminus\mathord{\uparrow}x)=(P\setminus\mathord{\uparrow}x)^{\prime}
. So there is a directed subset

D\subseteq\mathord{\downarrow}(P\setminus\mathord{\uparrow}x)=P\setminus\mathord{\uparrow}x
such that

\bigvee D=y
. This will run contrary to
x\ll y
.
Hence
\mathord{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\twoheadrightarrow$}}x\subseteq\operatorname{int}_{\sigma}(\mathord{\uparrow}x)
.

Remark 5.8
.

Theorems
36
and
37
give us a more fine-grained means to explain the phenomena that continuous posets are always meet-continuous and possess one-step closure.

At the point of writing, we are not able to completely characterize those poset that have a one-step closure.

6.
Conclusion

In this paper, we develop the core of domain theory using suitably defined approximation operators.

In domain theory, the role played by auxiliary relations is indeed quite auxiliary – once the continuity of a dcpo
L
is characterized by the condition that the way-below relation
\ll
is the smallest approximating auxiliary relation on
L
, the auxiliary relations are almost never mentioned in the subsequent development of domain theory. For instance, auxiliary relations are involved in the study of the Scott topology (which is of central importance in domain theory). Our approximation operators fill in this gap by exploiting the lower and upper approximation operators induced by auxiliary relations. Many salient domain-theoretic concepts such as interpolation property (INT), Scott topology, etc., take on new meanings in the light of operator theory. Using our approximation operators, not only can several well-known results regarding the way-below relation for continuous posets be generalized to the context of approximating relations, new domain-theoretic properties regarding continuous posets have also been obtained.

While it is theoretically pleasing to see that the core of domain theory can be formulated and developed using operator theory, one can perhaps look for more practical applications derived from the matrimony of domain theory and operator theory. In real life situations, there may be opportunities to export the domain-theoretic considerations derived in this paper back into the world of imprecise knowledge.

We end this paper with some open problems:

Question 6.1
.

(1)

Characterize those posets having a one-step closure.

(2)

Quasicontinuous posets are a generalization of continuous posets and are gaining much research attention recently
[
2
]
. Can our operator theoretic approach in this paper be employed to study quasi-continuous posets?
