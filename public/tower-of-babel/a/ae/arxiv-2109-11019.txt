Engineering infinite-range SU( n n ) interactions with spin-orbit-coupled fermions in an optical lattice
Michael A. Perlin, Diego Barberena, Mikhail Mamaev, Bhuvanesh Sundar, Robert J. Lewis-Swan, Ana Maria Rey

Abstract
We study multilevel fermions in an optical lattice described by the Hubbard model with on site SU( n n )-symmetric interactions. We show that in an appropriate parameter regime this system can be mapped onto a spin model with all-to-all SU( n n )-symmetric couplings. Raman pulses that address internal spin states modify the atomic dispersion relation and induce spin-orbit coupling, which can act as a synthetic inhomogeneous magnetic field that competes with the SU( n n ) exchange interactions. We investigate the mean-field dynamical phase diagram of the resulting model as a function of n n and different initial configurations that are accessible with Raman pulses. Consistent with previous studies for n = 2 n=2 , we find that for some initial states the spin model exhibits two distinct dynamical phases that obey simple scaling relations with n n . Moreover, for n > 2 n>2 we find that dynamical behavior can be highly sensitive to initial intra-spin coherences. Our predictions are readily testable in current experiments with ultracold alkaline-earth(-like) atoms.

From lattice fermions to an SU(

) spin model

Here we derive a collective SU(

) spin model for a system of ultracold alkaline-earth(-like) atoms trapped in an optical lattice.
Without external driving fields, the evolution of such atoms in their electronic ground state is governed by the single-body kinetic and two-body interaction Hamiltonians

\displaystyle=-J\sum_{\braket{j,j^{\prime}},\mu}\hat{c}_{j\mu}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j^{\prime}\mu}+\text{h.c.},

(1)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{int}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{U}{2}\sum_{j,\mu,\nu}\hat{c}_{j\mu}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j\mu}\hat{c}_{j\nu}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j\nu},

The terms with

\hat{s}_{\mu\nu q}\equiv\hat{c}_{q\mu}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{q\nu}

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{kin}}

\displaystyle=-2J\sum_{q,\mu}\cos\left(q\right)\hat{s}_{\mu\mu q},

(5)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{int}}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{u}{2N}\sum_{p,q,\mu,\nu}\hat{s}_{\mu\nu p}\hat{s}_{\nu\mu q}.

(6)

In this case we can simply treat our system as

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{s}}_{q}\equiv\sum_{\mu,\nu}\hat{s}_{\mu\nu q}\outerproduct{\mu}{\nu},

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{A}}\cdot\bm{\hat{B}}\equiv\sum_{\mu,\nu}\hat{A}_{\mu\nu}^{\dagger}\hat{B}_{\mu\nu}.

We now discuss the spin Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\text{int}}
in Eq. (
9

The operator
\bm{\hat{s}}_{p}\cdot\bm{\hat{s}}_{q}
simply swaps the nuclear spin states of two atoms pinned to modes
p,q
.
The term
-\bm{\hat{s}}_{p}\cdot\bm{\hat{s}}_{q}
thereby assigns a definite energy of
-1
(
+1
) to a pair of spins that are symmetric (anti-symmetric) under exchange.
In this sense,
\bm{\hat{s}}_{p}\cdot\bm{\hat{s}}_{q}
is analogous to the enforcement of SU(2) spin alignment by ferromagnetic interactions, which similarly assigns different energies to the anti-symmetric spin-0 singlet
\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}-\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}
and the symmetric spin-1 triplets
\left\{\ket{\uparrow\uparrow},\ket{\downarrow\downarrow},\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}+\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}\right\}
.
By summing over all pair-wise exchange terms
\bm{\hat{s}}_{p}\cdot\bm{\hat{s}}_{q}
, the interaction Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\text{int}}
energetically enforces a permutational symmetry among all spins, opening an energy gap
u
between the manifold of all permutationally symmetric (PS) states and the orthogonal complement of excited (e.g. spin-wave) states that break permutational symmetry.
See Figure
1
for a summary of this section thus far.

In the case of SU(2), the PS manifold is precisely the Dicke manifold of collective states
\ket{m_{\text{z}}}
with total spin
S=\frac{N}{2}
and definite spin projection
m_{\text{z}}\in\left\{S,S-1,\cdots,-S\right\}
onto a fixed quantization axis.
Equivalently, Dicke states
\ket{m_{\text{z}}}=\ket{m_{\uparrow},m_{\downarrow}}
can be labeled by a definite number of spins
m_{\uparrow}=S+m_{\text{z}}
(
m_{\downarrow}=S-m_{\text{z}}
) pointing up (down) along the spin quantization axis, with
m_{\uparrow}+m_{\downarrow}=N
.
In the general case of SU(
n
), the PS manifold is similarly spanned by states
\ket{m_{s},m_{s-1},\cdots,m_{-s}}
with a definite number
m_{\mu}
of spins in state
\mu

\sum_{\mu}m_{\mu}=N
.
The dimension of the PS manifold is equal to the number of ways of assigning
N

n

{N+n-1\choose n-1}\sim N^{n-1}

External fields or additional interactions that respect permutational symmetry can induce nontrivial dynamics within the PS manifold.
Moreover, additional terms that explicitly break permutational symmetry can nevertheless lead to interesting dynamics that can be captured within the PS manifold perturbatively, as long as the coupling to non-PS states is weak compared
u
(see Appendix
B

This perturbative regime is thereby efficiently simulable, as the PS manifold has dimension
\sim N^{n-1}
(as compared to

for the entire spin Hilbert space).
Simulating dynamics within the PS manifold requires calculating matrix elements
\braket{\ell|\hat{\mathcal{O}}|m}
of spin operators
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
with respect to PS states
\ket{\ell},\ket{m}
; we discuss this calculation in Appendix
C

III
External control fields

We now consider the addition of external control fields to address atoms’ internal spin states, which will determine the observables we can access and initial states we can prepare.
Specifically, we consider off-resonantly addressing an electronic
\ket{\text{g}}\to\ket{\text{e}}
transition of the atoms, and then perturbatively eliminating electronic
\ket{\text{e}}
excitations to arrive at an effective ground-state Hamiltonian addressing nuclear spins.
For simplicity, we will assume that the total spin

However, the results of this section (namely the general form of effective nuclear spin Hamiltonians, as well as the corresponding set of accessible observables and initial states) are the same for transitions that take

Finally, we comment on the preparation of initial states.
Initial states are nominally prepared in the “lab frame”, and must be transformed according to the gauge transformation
\hat{c}_{j\mu}^{\dagger}\to e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\phi\mu j}\hat{c}_{j\mu}^{\dagger}
prior to evolution under the three-laser drive
\hat{H}_{\text{3LD}}
in Eq. (
11
), which is expressed in the “gauge frame”.
We assume the capability to prepare an initial state in which all spins are maximally polarized along the
z
axis, i.e.
\ket{\text{z}}^{\otimes N}=\ket{s}^{\otimes N}
, which is unaffected by the gauge transformation (up to a global phase).
The three-laser then allows us to rotate this state into one that is polarized along any spatial axis (in the gauge frame).
In addition, when
n>2
the three-laser drive allows us to prepare product states with nontrivial intra-spin correlations.
For example, when
n
is even we can prepare an
N

\displaystyle e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\pi}{2}\left(\hat{s}_{\text{y}}+\hat{s}_{\text{y}}^{2}\right)}\ket{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\,\text{even}}}{{\propto}}\ket{s}+\ket{-s}.

(14)

This state has a vanishing mean spin vector,
\braket{\hat{s}_{\text{x}}}=\braket{\hat{s}_{\text{y}}}=\braket{\hat{s}_{\text{z}}}=0
, but variances
\braket{\hat{s}_{\text{x}}^{2}}=\braket{\hat{s}_{\text{y}}^{2}}=s/2
and
\braket{\hat{s}_{\text{z}}^{2}}=s^{2}
.

IV
Spin-orbit coupling

We now consider the effect of spin-orbit coupling (SOC) induced by the control fields in Section
III
.
Before discussing SOC for
n
-level fermions, we briefly review the well-studied case of two-level SOC with a one-dimensional lattice
Wall et al. 2016
;
Kolkowitz et al. 2016
;
Bromley et al. 2018
;
He et al. 2019
.
In this case, SOC is induced by an external driving field that imprints a phase
e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\phi j}
on lattice site
j
, or equivalently imparts a momentum kick
q\to q+\phi
, upon the absorption of a photon
b
b

b

In order for the drive Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\text{drive}}^{(\phi)}
to be well-defined,
\phi
should be commensurate with the lattice, e.g.
\phi\in\mathbb{Z}\times 2\pi/L
on a one-dimensional lattice of
L
sites.
:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{drive}}^{(\phi)}=\dfrac{\Omega}{2}\sum_{q}\hat{c}_{q+\phi,\uparrow}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{q,\downarrow}+\text{h.c.}.

(15)

Identifying a numerical spin index
\mu=+\frac{1}{2}
(
-\frac{1}{2}
) with the state
\uparrow
(
\downarrow
), this drive Hamiltonian can be diagonalized in its momentum index
q
by the gauge transformation
\hat{c}_{q\mu}^{\dagger}\to\hat{c}_{q-\mu\phi,\mu}^{\dagger}
(equivalently
\hat{c}_{j\mu}^{\dagger}\to e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\phi\mu j}\hat{c}_{j\mu}^{\dagger}
), which takes

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{drive}}^{(\phi)}\to\hat{H}_{\text{drive}}\equiv\Omega\hat{S}_{\text{x}},

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{\text{x}}\equiv\sum_{q}\hat{s}_{\text{x},q},

(16)

where
\hat{s}_{\text{x},q}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{c}_{q,\uparrow}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{q,\downarrow}+\text{h.c.}
for two-level spins.

The two-level SOC drive in Eq. (
15
) has been implemented with an external laser that couples the two electronic states of nuclear-spin-polarized atoms, with
\downarrow
(
\uparrow
) indexing the ground (excited) electronic state
Wall et al. 2016
;
Livi et al. 2016
;
Kolkowitz et al. 2016
;
Bromley et al. 2018
;
He et al. 2019
.
In contrast, the drive we considered in Section
III
addresses electronic excitations off-resonantly, inducing an effective Hamiltonian in the ground-state hyperfine manifold with spin projections
\mu\in\left\{s,s-1,\cdots,-s\right\}
(a similar scheme was used to study SOC in a subspace of the ground-state manifold in Ref.
Mancini et al. 2015
).
Nonetheless, both the two-level drive in Eq. (
15
) and the
n
-level drive in Eq. (
11
) become homogeneous (i.e. independent of the spatial mode index
j
or
q
) and independent of the SOC angle
\phi
after the same spin-symmetric gauge transformation
c
c

c

The “asymmetric” gauge transformation
(\hat{c}_{j,\uparrow}^{\dagger},\hat{c}_{j,\downarrow}^{\dagger})\to(e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\phi j}\hat{c}_{j,\uparrow}^{\dagger},\hat{c}_{j,\downarrow}^{\dagger})
, sometimes performed in the two-state SOC literature, does not generalize as nicely to
n>2
.

\hat{c}_{j\mu}^{\dagger}\to e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\phi\mu j}\hat{c}_{j\mu}^{\dagger}
.

Of course, spin-orbit coupling cannot be “gauged away” entirely.
Making a gauge transformation to simplify the drive comes at the cost of making the kinetic energy in Eq. (
5
) spin-dependent, taking

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{kin}}\to\hat{H}_{\text{kin}}^{(\phi)}\equiv-2J\sum_{q}\cos\left(q+\mu\phi\right)\hat{s}_{\mu\mu q},

(17)

as visualized in Figure
4
.
To better interpret this Hamiltonian, we can write it in the form

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{kin}}^{(\phi)}=-2J\sum_{q}\left[\cos\left(q\right)\hat{w}_{+,q}^{(\phi)}-\sin\left(q\right)\hat{w}_{-,q}^{(\phi)}\right],

(18)

where

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{+,q}^{(\phi)}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\mu}\cos\left(\mu\phi\right)\hat{s}_{\mu\mu q},

(19)

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{-,q}^{(\phi)}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\mu}\sin\left(\mu\phi\right)\hat{s}_{\mu\mu q}.

(20)

For two-level spins with
\mu=\pm\frac{1}{2}
,
\hat{w}_{+,q}^{(\phi)}
is proportional to the identity operator and
\hat{w}_{-,q}^{(\phi)}=2\sin\left(\phi/2\right)\hat{s}_{\text{z},q}
, so the kinetic Hamiltonian in the gauge frame describes a (synthetic) inhomogeneous magnetic field:

\displaystyle\left.\hat{H}_{\text{kin}}^{(\phi)}\right|_{n=2}=4J\sin\left(\phi/2\right)\sum_{q}\sin\left(q\right)\hat{s}_{\text{z},q}.

(21)

When
n>2
, an inhomogeneous magnetic field is likewise recovered in the weak SOC limit
s\phi\ll 1
, in which case

\displaystyle\left.\hat{H}_{\text{kin}}^{(\phi)}\right|_{s\phi\ll 1}=2J\phi\sum_{q}\sin\left(q\right)\hat{s}_{\text{z},q}+O\left((s\phi)^{2}\right).

(22)

For larger
\phi
, this Hamiltonian acquires terms with higher powers of
\hat{s}_{\text{z},q}
, up to
\hat{s}_{\text{z},q}^{n-1}
.

Finally, the gauge transformation

also transforms the interaction Hamiltonian.
Applying this transformation to Eq. (

) and keeping only terms that respect coherences that can be imposed on initial states by the laser drive in Section

For sufficiently weak SOC (

The validity of this approximation has been previously benchmarked for SU(2)-symmetric interactions

To ensure that

Altogether, the interacting spin Hamiltonian in the gauge frame becomes

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{spin}}=-\dfrac{u}{2N}\bm{\hat{S}}\cdot\bm{\hat{S}}+2J\phi\sum_{q}\sin\left(q\right)\hat{s}_{\text{z},q},

consisting of a spin-locking

V
Mean-field theory and dynamical phases

We now study the dynamical behavior of the SOC spin Hamiltonian
H_{\text{spin}}
in Eq. (
23
), and henceforth work exclusively in the “gauge frame” of
\hat{H}_{\text{spin}}
and the three-laser drive
\hat{H}_{\text{3LD}}
in Eq. (
11
).
We use a Ramsey-like setup wherein we prepare an initial state with the three-laser drive (using fast pule sequences), then let the state evolve freely for some time under
\hat{H}_{\text{spin}}
, and finally apply again the three-laser drive to map observables of interest onto spin projection measurements (e.g. with spin qudit tomography
Newton and Young 1968
;
Perlin et al. 2020b
).
At the mean-field (MF) level, the undriven spin Hamiltonian (neglecting constant energy shifts) becomes

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{MF}}=u\sum_{q}\left[-\braket{\bar{\bm{s}}}\cdot\bm{\hat{s}}_{q}+h\sin\left(q\right)\hat{s}_{\text{z},q}\right].

(24)

where
\bar{\bm{s}}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{q}\bm{\hat{s}}_{q}
is the average spin matrix, and
h\equiv 2J\phi/u
is a dimensionless strength of the inhomogeneous magnetic field.
We assume that all momenta
q\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}\times 2\pi/N
are occupied.
Fixing the atom number
N
, the spin Hamiltonian has one free parameter,
h
, which determines the relative strength of the single-particle and interaction terms.
One should therefore expect distinct dynamical behaviors when
h\ll 1
, in which case strong spin-locking interactions should give rise to a long-range ordered phase, as opposed to
h\gg 1
, in which case long-range order should be destroyed by the strong inhomogeneous magnetic field
Smale et al. 2019
.

To investigate these behaviors quantitatively, we examine time-averaged observables of the form

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\hat{\mathcal{O}}\rangle\!\rangle_{\text{MF}}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\text{d}t\braket{\hat{\mathcal{O}}\left(t\right)}_{\text{MF}},

(25)

where
\braket{\hat{\mathcal{O}}\left(t\right)}_{\text{MF}}
is the mean-field value of observable
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
at time
t
.
Specifically, we consider the time-averaged magnetization

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{MF}}\equiv\absolutevalue{\bbk{\vec\sigma}_\MF},

\displaystyle\vec{\sigma}\equiv\dfrac{1}{Ns}\times\vec{S},

(26)

where
\vec{S}\equiv(\hat{S}_{\text{x}},\hat{S}_{\text{y}},\hat{S}_{\text{z}})
with
\hat{S}_{\alpha}\equiv\sum_{q}\hat{s}_{\alpha,q}
, and the time-averaged (dimensionless) interaction energy

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\bar{\bm{s}}\cdot\bar{\bm{s}}\rangle\!\rangle_{\text{MF}}=\dfrac{1}{N^{2}}\times\langle\!\langle\bm{\hat{S}}\cdot\bm{\hat{S}}\rangle\!\rangle_{\text{MF}}.

(27)

By design, these non-negative quantities are normalized to lie on the interval
[0,1]
, independent of the system size
N
or spin dimension
n
.
In the remainder of this section we will assume that
n
is even, both for the sake of experimental relevance (most relevant atomic nuclei are fermionic) and to avoid complications from parity effects.

Our numerical simulations of mean-field dynamics are performed with a Schwinger boson decomposition of spin operators:
\hat{s}_{\mu\nu q}=\hat{b}_{\mu q}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\nu q}
.
This decomposition requires no approximations, and reduces the number of variables to keep track of by a factor of
\sim n
.
See Appendices
E
and
F
for additional details about our numerical simulations and the Schwinger boson equations of motion.

The rescaling of magnetization and interaction energy can be understood by considering their limiting behavior as
h\to\infty
or
h\to 0
.

In the strong-field limit
h\to\infty
, we can ignore interactions and treat spins as though they simply precess at different rates.
The time-averaged transverse magnetization
\sigma_{\text{MF}}
then trivially vanishes as
h\to\infty
.
The interaction energy
\braket{\bar{\bm{s}}\cdot\bar{\bm{s}}}_{\text{MF}}=\braket{\bar{\bm{s}}}_{\text{MF}}\cdot\braket{\bar{\bm{s}}}_{\text{MF}}+O(1/N)
, meanwhile, has contributions from:

1.

the diagonal parts of the mean spin matrix
\braket{\bar{\bm{s}}}_{\text{MF}}
, which are conserved by inhomogeneous spin precession, and

2.

the off-diagonal parts of
\braket{\bar{\bm{s}}}_{\text{MF}}
, whose oscillations average to zero when evaluating the time average in
\langle\!\langle\bar{\bm{s}}\cdot\bar{\bm{s}}\rangle\!\rangle_{\text{MF}}
.

Altogether, the interaction energy
\langle\!\langle\bar{\bm{s}}\cdot\bar{\bm{s}}\rangle\!\rangle_{\text{MF}}
in the strong-field limit is determined by the time-independent diagonal part
\diag\braket{\bar{\bm{s}}}_{\text{MF}}=\diag\outerproduct{\x}{\x}
, namely

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to\infty}\langle\!\langle\bar{\bm{s}}\cdot\bar{\bm{s}}\rangle\!\rangle_{\text{MF}}=\Tr\left[\left(\diag\outerproduct{\x}{\x}\right)^{2}\right]=\gamma\left(n\right).

(31)

The same result can be obtained by computing the time-averaged interaction energy of two spins precessing at different rates.

In the weak-field limit
h\to 0
, the spin-locking
\bm{\hat{S}}\cdot\bm{\hat{S}}
interactions of the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\text{spin}}
energetically restrict dynamics to the permutationally symmetric (PS) manifold.
To first order in
h
, the effect of the inhomogeneous field can be acquired by projecting it onto the PS manifold, which takes
\hat{s}_{\text{z},q}\to\frac{1}{N}\hat{S}_{\text{z}}
.
The first order effect of the inhomogeneous field thus vanishes, as

\displaystyle\sum_{q}\sin\left(q\right)\hat{s}_{\text{z},q}\to\sum_{q}\sin\left(q\right)\times\frac{1}{N}\hat{S}_{\text{z}}=0.

(32)

At second order in
h
, the effective Hamiltonian within the PS manifold is related to the variance of the inhomogeneous field, rather than its (vanishing) average.
On a high level, the second-order effect of the inhomogeneous field within the PS manifold thus consists of permutation-symmetrized products of two spin-z operators,
\hat{s}_{\text{z},p}\hat{s}_{\text{z},q}
(with
p,q
possibly equal).
Altogether, the effective spin Hamiltonian at second order in
h
is (see Appendix
B
)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{spin}}^{\text{eff}}=\dfrac{h^{2}u}{2(N-1)}\times\left[\hat{S}_{\text{z}}^{2}-N\sum_{q}\hat{s}_{\text{z},q}^{2}\right],

(33)

which in the mean-field approximation becomes

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{MF}}^{\text{eff}}=-\frac{1}{2}h^{2}u\sum_{q}\hat{s}_{\text{z},q}^{2},

(34)

where we have used the fact that the axial magnetizations
\braket{\hat{s}_{\text{z},q}}=\frac{1}{N}\braket{\hat{S}_{\text{z}}}
within the PS manifold, and the initial value of
\braket{\hat{S}_{\text{z}}}=0
is conserved by
\hat{H}_{\text{spin}}
.

\langle\!\langle\bar{\bm{s}}\cdot\bar{\bm{s}}\rangle\!\rangle_{\text{MF}}\to 1

h\to 0
.
Moreover, the initial y-magnetization

\hat{H}_{\text{spin}}

\sigma_{\text{MF}}
is determined by the time-average of
\hat{s}_{\text{x}}
for a single (any) spin:

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\sigma_{\text{MF}}=\dfrac{1}{s}\lim_{T\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\text{d}\tau\absolutevalue{\lim_{T\to\infty} \f 1T \int_0^T \dd\tau\bk{\x|\s_\x\p{\tau}|\x}},

VI
Conclusions and future directions

Starting with an SU(
n
) Hubbard model describing ultracold fermionic alkaline-earth(-like) atoms on an optical lattice, we derived a momentum-space multilevel spin model with all-to-all SU(
n
)-symmetric interactions.
We then introduced external control fields, finding a simple three-laser drive that homogeneously addresses nuclear spins with a variety of spin Hamiltonians.
Taking a closer look at the effect of the spin-orbit coupling (SOC) induced by the driving lasers, we found that maintaining the validity of the spin model requires weak SOC, which in turn gives rise to a (synthetic) inhomogeneous magnetic field.
Finally, we examined dynamical behavior of the SU(
n
) spin model at the mean-field level, finding that long-time observables obey simple scaling relations with
n
, and that when
n>2
dynamical behavior can be highly sensitive to intra-spin coherences.

Our work makes important progress in understanding the SU(
n
) Fermi-Hubbard model in experimentally relevant parameter regimes, and we expect our findings to be readily testable in experiments with ultracold atoms.
Given the possibility for long-range SU(
n
) interactions, we hope our work stimulates further efforts into simulating SY and SYK-like models
Sachdev and Ye 1993
;
Bentsen et al. 2019
in cold atomic platforms.
In follow-up work, it would be interesting to study the relationship between initial states and dynamical phases of our SU(
n
) spin model more systematically, and to consider the effect of quantum corrections to mean-field behavior.
There is also room to improve on the three-laser drive introduced in this work, for which it is natural to ask what additional techniques or ingredients are necessary to implement universal control of individual nuclear spins.
Universal control would allow for an experimental study of
n
-dependence (including even/odd-
n
parity effects) in a single experimental platform, simply by controlling the occupation and coherence of internal spin states.
Finally, one can also study the SU(
n
) Hubbard model in the super-exchange regime that gives rise to a real-space (as opposed to momentum-space) spin model, where SOC gives rise to chiral multilevel spin interactions.
Unlike our present work, the super-exchange regime does not require weak SOC, and therefore has a larger parameter space in which to explore dynamical behavior.

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}\sum_{k\notin\left\{i,j\right\}}g_{ij}w_{k}\hat{\Pi}_{ij}\hat{X}_{k}\ket{\psi}=E_{0}\hat{\mathcal{O}}(w,\hat{X})\ket{\psi}-\sum_{k}g_{k}w_{k}\hat{X}_{k}\ket{\psi}.

(68)

The terms in Eq. (
63
) with

k\in\left\{i,j\right\}
, meanwhile, are

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\neq j\\
k\in\left\{i,j\right\}\end{subarray}}g_{ij}w_{k}\hat{\Pi}_{ij}\hat{X}_{k}\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}w_{j}\hat{X}_{i}\ket{\psi}.

(69)

so in total

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}\hat{\mathcal{O}}(w,\hat{X})\ket{\psi}=E_{0}\hat{\mathcal{O}}(w,\hat{X})\ket{\psi}+\sum_{k}\left[\sum_{j}g_{kj}w_{j}-g_{k}w_{k}\right]\hat{X}_{k}\ket{\psi}.

(70)

The action of the single-body perturbation
\hat{\mathcal{O}}(w,\hat{X})
on a permutationally symmetric state therefore generates an eigenstate of
\hat{H}_{0}
with interaction energy
E_{0}+\Delta
if the vector
w=\sum_{k}w_{k}\ket{k}
satisfies the eigenvalue equation

\displaystyle g^{(1)}\cdot w=\Delta w,

\displaystyle g^{(1)}\equiv g-\diag\vec{g},

(71)

where
g\equiv\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}\outerproduct{i}{j}
is a matrix of all couplings
g_{ij}
; the vector
\vec{g}\equiv\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}\ket{i}=\sum_{i}g_{i}\ket{i}
is the sum of all columns of
g
; and the matrix
\diag\vec{g}\equiv\sum_{i}g_{i}\outerproduct{i}{i}
has
\vec{g}
on the diagonal and zeroes everywhere else.

A similar calculation as above with arbitrary
M
yields an eigenvalue equation of the form

\displaystyle g^{(M)}\cdot w=\Delta w,

(72)

where we treat
w
as an
\absolutevalue{\D_N\p{M}}
-component vector, and
g^{(M)}
is a matrix with dimensions
\absolutevalue{\D_N\p{M}}\times\absolutevalue{\D_N\p{M}}
.
In the case of
M=2
, we have

\displaystyle g^{(2)}=\sum_{\left(k,\ell\right)\in\mathcal{D}_{N}\left(2\right)}\ket{k\ell}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}\\
i\notin\left\{k,\ell\right\}\end{subarray}}\left(g_{ik}\bra{i\ell}+g_{i\ell}\bra{ki}\right)+g_{k\ell}\bra{\ell k}-\left(g_{k}+g_{\ell}-g_{k\ell}\right)\bra{k\ell}\right],

(73)

and more generally

\displaystyle g^{(M)}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}_{N}\left(M\right)}\ket{k}\left[\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{M}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}\\
i\notin k\end{subarray}}g_{ik_{a}}\bra{k_{a:i}}+\sum_{\left\{a,b\right\}\in\mathcal{C}_{M}\left(2\right)}g_{k_{a}k_{b}}\bra{k_{a\leftrightarrow b}}-\tilde{g}_{k}\bra{k}\right],

(74)

where
k_{a}\in k=\left(k_{1},k_{2},\cdots,k_{M}\right)
;
k_{a:i}
a list that is equal to
k
except at the
a
-th position, where
k_{a}
replaced is by
i
, i.e.
k_{a:i}=\left(\cdots,k_{a-1},i,k_{a+1},\cdots\right)
;
\mathcal{C}_{L}\left(p\right)
is the set of all subsets (“choices”) of
p
elements from
\mathbb{Z}_{L}
;
k_{a\leftrightarrow b}
is equal to
k
except at the
a
-th and
b
-th positions, at which
k_{a}
and
k_{b}
are switched; and

\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{k}\equiv\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left\{i,j\right\}\in\mathcal{C}_{N}\left(2\right)\\
i\in k~\text{or}~j\in k\end{subarray}}g_{ij}=\sum_{i\in k}g_{i}-\sum_{\left\{a,b\right\}\in\mathcal{C}_{M}\left(2\right)}g_{k_{a}k_{b}}.

(75)

If the tensor
w
is permutationally symmetric, meaning that
w_{k}
is invariant under arbitrary permutations of
k
, then this symmetry is preserved by
g^{(M)}
.
In this case, we can replace sums over
k\in\mathcal{D}_{N}\left(M\right)
in Eqs. (
73
) and (
74
) by sums over
k\in\mathcal{C}_{N}\left(M\right)
, and replace vectors
\ket{k_{1},k_{2},\cdots,k_{M}}\to\ket{\left\{k_{1},k_{2},\cdots,k_{M}\right\}}
, such that e.g.
\ket{k_{a\leftrightarrow b}}=\ket{k}
.
These replacements reduce the size of
g^{(M)}
from
\absolutevalue{\D_N\p{M}}\times\absolutevalue{\D_N\p{M}}
to
\absolutevalue{\C_N\p{M}}\times\absolutevalue{\C_N\p{M}}
, where
\absolutevalue{\D_N\p{M}}=\prod_{j=0}^{M-1}\left(N-j\right)=M!\times{N\choose M}
and
\absolutevalue{\C_N\p{M}}={N\choose M}
.
Additional symmetries of
g
and
w
, such as translational invariance or lattice symmetries, can be used to further reduce the computational complexity of the eigenvalue problem in Eq. (
72
).

B.2
Recovering spin-wave theory

If the interaction Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{0}
is translationally invariant, then the single-body eigenvalue problem in Eq. (
71
) is solvable analytically.
In this case, the couplings
g_{ij}
depend only on the separation
\absolutevalue{i-j}
, so eigenvectors of
g
are plane waves of the form

\displaystyle w_{k}\equiv\sum_{d\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{D}}e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mud\cdot k}\ket{d},

(76)

where on a
D
-dimensional periodic lattice of
N=L^{D}
spins, lattice sites are indexed by vectors
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{D}
, and wavenumbers take on values
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{D}\times 2\pi/L
.
The eigenvalues of
g
can be determined by expanding

\displaystyle g\cdot w_{k}=\sum_{c,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{D}}g_{cd}e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mud\cdot k}\ket{c}=\sum_{c,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{D}}g_{c,c+d}e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\left(c+d\right)\cdot k}\ket{c}=\sum_{d\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{D}}g_{0,d}\cos\left(d\cdot k\right)w_{k},

(77)

where the imaginary contributions vanish in the sum over
d
because
g_{0,d}=g_{0,-d}
.
The remainder of Eq. (
71
) that we need to sort out is
\diag\vec{g}
, where all
g_{i}=\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}=\sum_{d}g_{0,d}
are equal, which implies that
\diag\vec{g}=\sum_{d}g_{0,d}
is a scalar.
We thus find that

\displaystyle g^{(1)}\cdot w_{k}=\Delta_{k}w_{k},

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}\equiv\sum_{d\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{D}}g_{0,d}\left[\cos\left(d\cdot k\right)-1\right],

(78)

in agreement with standard spin-wave theory.
Excitations generated by the action of
\hat{\mathcal{O}}\left(w_{k},X\right)
on PS states
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{E}_{0}
are known as spin-waves.
If
g_{ij}=-U/N
is constant, then the spin-wave excitation energies are
\Delta_{k}=U
independent of the wavenumber
k
.

Appendix C
Restricting spin operators to the permutationally symmetric manifold

Here we provide the restriction of a general
M
-body spin operator
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
to the permutationally symmetric (PS) manifold of
N
spins (each with
n
internal states).
Denoting the projector onto the PS manifold by
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{0}
, our task is essentially to find the coefficients of the expansion

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{0}\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{M}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{0}=\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{A}_{n}\left(N\right)}\braket{a|\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{M}|b}\outerproduct{a}{b},

(79)

where
\mathcal{A}_{n}\left(N\right)
is the set of all ways to assign
N
(identical) spins to
n
(distinct) states, such that for any
a\in\mathcal{A}_{n}\left(N\right)
the state
\ket{a}=\ket{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n}}
is labeled by the occupation number
a_{\mu}
of state
\mu
, with
\sum_{\mu}a_{\mu}=N
.
Written out explicitly,

\displaystyle\ket{a}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{C}\left(a\right)}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{distinct}\\
\text{permutations}\\
\hat{\Pi}\,\text{of}\,\tilde{a}\end{subarray}}\hat{\Pi}\ket{\tilde{a}},

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde{a}}\equiv\bigotimes_{\mu}\ket{\mu}^{\otimes a_{\mu}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\left(a\right)\equiv\dfrac{\left(\sum_{\mu}a_{\mu}\right)!}{\prod_{\nu}a_{\nu}!}.

(80)

Here
\mathcal{C}\left(a\right)
is a multinomial coefficient that counts the number of distinct ways to permute the tensor factors of the “standard-ordered” state
\ket{\tilde{a}}
, enforcing
\braket{a|a}=1
.
Using these states, with some combinatorics we can expand

\displaystyle\braket{a|\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{M}|b}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}_{n}\left(M\right)\\
\alpha\leq a,\beta\leq b\end{subarray}}\delta_{a-\alpha,b-\beta}\sqrt{\dfrac{\mathcal{C}\left(\alpha\right)\mathcal{C}\left(a-\alpha\right)\mathcal{C}\left(\beta\right)\mathcal{C}\left(b-\beta\right)}{\mathcal{C}\left(a\right)\mathcal{C}\left(b\right)}}\,\braket{\alpha|\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{M}|\beta},

(81)

where the restriction
\alpha\leq a
and the difference
a-\alpha
are evaluated element-wise, i.e.
\alpha\leq a\implies\alpha_{\mu}\leq a_{\mu}
and
\left(a-\alpha\right)_{\mu}=a_{\mu}-\alpha_{\mu}
for all
\mu
; and
\delta_{cd}=1
if
c=d
and zero otherwise.
We sum over both
\alpha
and
\beta
above merely to keep the expression symmetric with respect to transposition
\left(a,\alpha\right)\leftrightarrow\left(b,\beta\right)
; in practice, one can simply sum over
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{n}\left(M\right)
and set
\beta=b-a+\alpha
, throwing out terms with any
\beta_{\mu}<0
.
Note that, by slight abuse of notation, the operator
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{M}
on the left of Eq. (
81
) acts on an arbitrary choice of
M
spins (out of
N
), whereas the operator
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{M}
on the right of Eq. (
81
) is simply an
M
-spin operator, with matrix elements
\braket{\alpha|\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{M}|\beta}
evaluated with respect to the PS
M
-spin states
\ket{\alpha},\ket{\beta}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}\left(M\right)
.

Relaxing assumptions of the three-laser drive

In order to arrive at the drive Hamiltonian in Eq. (

1.

that the excited-state hyperfine manifold had the same total spin

2.

that all drive amplitudes are real (which enforces a phase-locking condition between the driving lasers).

To derive an effective drive Hamiltonian for the general case in which the excited-state hyperfine manifold has total spin
s+r

\sigma

\bm{v}

and
-1

along axis
\bm{v}

(83)

Here
\braket{j_{1}m_{1};j_{2}m_{2}|j_{3}m_{3}}

\hat{T}_{\pm}^{(r)}

\tr\left[\hat{T}_{\pm}^{(r)}{}^{\dagger}\hat{T}_{\pm}^{(r)}\right]=1
.
Still assuming real drive amplitudes, the corresponding effective drive Hamiltonian that replaces Eq. (
12

\absolutevalue{\Delta}\gg\absolutevalue{\Omega_{\v v\sigma}}

(84)

where
f_{r}^{(k)}

n

\displaystyle f_{0}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle f_{+1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=-s,

\displaystyle f_{-1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=s+1,

(85)

\displaystyle f_{0}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle f_{+1}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{s}{n+2},

\displaystyle f_{-1}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{s+1}{n-2},

(86)

\displaystyle f_{0}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle f_{+1}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{s(s+1)^{2}}{n+2},

\displaystyle f_{-1}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{s^{2}(s+1)}{n-2}.

(87)

\Omega_{m},\eta_{m}

\hat{H}_{\text{3LD}}^{\text{single}}=f_{r}^{(1)}\tilde{\Omega}_{+}\tilde{\Omega}_{-}\hat{s}_{\text{z}}+\tilde{\Omega}_{0}\sum_{\sigma\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}}\dfrac{\tilde{\Omega}_{+}+\sigma\tilde{\Omega}_{-}}{2}\left[f_{r}^{(1)}\sigma\hat{s}_{\tilde{\eta}_{\sigma},\text{x}}+f_{r}^{(2)}\left(\hat{s}_{\text{z}}\hat{s}_{\tilde{\eta}_{\sigma},\text{x}}+\hat{s}_{\tilde{\eta}_{\sigma},\text{x}}\hat{s}_{\text{z}}\right)\right]\\

(88)

\hat{s}_{\eta\alpha}\equiv e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\eta\hat{s}_{\text{z}}}\hat{s}_{\alpha}e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\eta\hat{s}_{\text{z}}}

\alpha
operator (e.g.
\hat{s}_{\pi/2,\text{x}}=\hat{s}_{\text{y}}

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}_{0}\equiv\dfrac{\eta_{+}-\eta_{-}}{2},

Appendix E
Mean-field theory

Here we describe the mean-field theory used to simulate the spin Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{spin}}=-\dfrac{u}{2N}\bm{\hat{S}}\cdot\bm{\hat{S}}+2J\phi\sum_{q}\sin\left(q\right)\hat{s}_{\text{z},q}

(90)

in Eq. (
23
) of the main text.
We begin by decomposing individual spin operators into Schwinger bosons as
\hat{s}_{\mu\nu q}=\hat{b}_{\mu q}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\nu q}
, such that the spin Hamiltonian becomes

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{spin}}\to\hat{H}_{\text{boson}}=-\dfrac{u}{2N}\sum_{p,q,\mu,\nu}\hat{b}_{\mu p}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\nu p}\hat{b}_{\nu q}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\mu q}+2J\phi\sum_{q,\mu}\sin\left(q\right)\mu\,\hat{b}_{\mu q}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\mu q}.

(91)

The Heisenberg equations of motion for the Schwinger boson operators are (see Appendix
F
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\partial_{t}\hat{b}_{\mu q}=-\dfrac{u}{N}\sum_{\nu,p}\hat{b}_{\nu p}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\mu p}\hat{b}_{\nu q}+2J\phi\sin\left(q\right)\mu\,\hat{b}_{\mu q}.

(92)

Our mean-field theory then treats all boson operators in these equations of motion as complex numbers,
\hat{b}_{\mu q}\to\braket{\hat{b}_{\mu q}}_{\text{MF}}
, with the initial value
\braket{\hat{b}_{\mu q}\left(t=0\right)}_{\text{MF}}
equal to the initial amplitude of spin
q
in state
\mu
.
Specifically, for an
N
-fold product state of the form
\ket{\psi}=\bigotimes_{q}\sum_{\mu}\psi_{\mu q}\ket{\mu}
we set
\braket{\hat{b}_{\mu q}\left(t=0\right)}_{\text{MF}}=\psi_{\mu q}
.
For pure initial product states, this mean-field treatment of the boson operators
\hat{b}_{\mu q}
is mathematically equivalent to a mean-field treatment of the spin operators
\hat{s}_{\mu\nu q}
, as in Eq. (
24
), but reduces the number of variables to keep track of by a factor of
\sim n
.

Appendix F
Schwinger boson equations of motion for quadratic spin Hamiltonians

Here we decompose a quadratic spin Hamiltonian into Schwinger bosons, and derive the equations of motion for the resulting boson operators.
We begin with a general spin Hamiltonian of the form

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu,\nu,\rho,\sigma\\
j<k\end{subarray}}g^{\mu\nu j}_{\rho\sigma k}\hat{s}_{\mu\nu j}\hat{s}_{\rho\sigma k}+\sum_{\mu,\nu,j}\epsilon_{\mu\nu j}\hat{s}_{\mu\nu j},

(93)

where
\mu,\nu
index orthogonal states of an
n
-level spin;
j,k
index one of
N
spins;
g^{\mu\nu j}_{\rho\sigma k}
and
\epsilon_{\mu\nu j}
are scalars; and
\hat{s}_{\mu\nu j}=\outerproduct{\mu}{\nu}_{j}
is a transition operator for spin
j
.
Strictly speaking, Eq. (
93
) only defines the couplings
g^{\mu\nu j}_{\rho\sigma k}
for
j<k
, so we enforce
g^{\mu\nu k}_{\rho\sigma j}=g^{\mu\nu j}_{\rho\sigma k}
and
g^{\mu\nu j}_{\rho\sigma j}=0
for completion.
Decomposing spin operators into Schwinger bosons as
\hat{s}_{\mu\nu j}=\hat{b}_{\mu j}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\nu j}
, where
\hat{b}_{\nu j}
a annihilates a boson of type
\nu
on site
j
, we can write this Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu,\nu,\rho,\sigma\\
j<k\end{subarray}}g^{\mu\nu j}_{\rho\sigma k}\hat{b}_{\mu j}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\nu j}\hat{b}_{\rho k}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\sigma k}+\sum_{\mu,\nu,j}\epsilon_{\mu\nu j}\hat{b}_{\mu j}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\nu j}.

(94)

The Heisenberg equations of motion for the boson operators are then

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\partial_{t}\hat{b}_{\alpha\ell}=\left[\hat{b}_{\alpha\ell},\hat{H}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu,\nu,\rho,\sigma\\
j<k\end{subarray}}g^{\mu\nu j}_{\rho\sigma k}\left[\hat{b}_{\alpha\ell},\hat{b}_{\mu j}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\nu j}\hat{b}_{\rho k}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\sigma k}\right]+\sum_{\mu,\nu,j}\epsilon_{\mu\nu j}\left[\hat{b}_{\alpha\ell},\hat{b}_{\mu j}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\nu j}\right]

(95)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu,\nu,\rho,\sigma,k}g^{\mu\nu\ell}_{\rho\sigma k}\left[\hat{b}_{\alpha\ell},\hat{b}_{\mu\ell}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\nu\ell}\right]\hat{b}_{\rho k}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\sigma k}+\sum_{\mu,\nu}\epsilon_{\mu\nu\ell}\left[\hat{b}_{\alpha\ell},\hat{b}_{\mu\ell}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\nu\ell}\right]

(96)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu,\nu}\left(\sum_{\rho,\sigma,k}g^{\mu\nu\ell}_{\rho\sigma k}\hat{b}_{\rho k}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\sigma k}+\epsilon_{\mu\nu\ell}\right)\left[\hat{b}_{\alpha\ell},\hat{b}_{\mu\ell}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\nu\ell}\right]

(97)

where

\displaystyle\left[\hat{b}_{\alpha\ell},\hat{b}_{\mu\ell}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\nu\ell}\right]=\delta_{\alpha\mu}\delta_{\alpha\nu}\hat{b}_{\alpha\ell}+\delta_{\alpha\mu}\left(1-\delta_{\alpha\nu}\right)\hat{b}_{\nu\ell}=\delta_{\alpha\mu}\hat{b}_{\nu\ell},

(98)

so

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\partial_{t}\hat{b}_{\alpha\ell}=\sum_{\nu}\left(\sum_{\rho,\sigma,k}g^{\alpha\nu\ell}_{\rho\sigma k}\hat{b}_{\rho k}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\sigma k}+\epsilon_{\alpha\nu\ell}\right)\hat{b}_{\nu\ell}.

(99)

In the case of uniform SU(
n
)-symmetric interactions of the form
\frac{g}{2}\bm{\hat{S}}\cdot\bm{\hat{S}}
and a diagonal external field, we have

\displaystyle g^{\alpha\nu\ell}_{\rho\sigma k}=g\times\delta_{\alpha\sigma}\delta_{\nu\rho},

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\alpha\nu\ell}=\epsilon_{\alpha\ell}\times\delta_{\alpha\nu}

(100)

so

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\partial_{t}\hat{b}_{\alpha\ell}=g\sum_{\nu,k}\hat{b}_{\nu k}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\alpha k}\hat{b}_{\nu\ell}+\epsilon_{\alpha\ell}\hat{b}_{\alpha\ell}.

(101)

Appendix G
Lax vector analysis

We start with the spin Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{spin}}=-\dfrac{u}{2N}\sum_{\mu,\nu}\hat{S}_{\mu\nu}\hat{S}_{\nu\mu}+2J\phi\sum_{q}\sin\left(q\right)\hat{s}_{\text{z},q},

(102)

where
\hat{S}_{\mu\nu}=\sum_{q}\hat{s}_{\mu\nu q}
.
The single-body operators that appear in this Hamiltonian have squared norms

\displaystyle\tr\left(\hat{s}_{\mu\nu q}^{\dagger}\hat{s}_{\mu\nu q}\right)=1

and

\displaystyle\tr\left(\hat{s}_{\text{z},q}^{\dagger}\hat{s}_{\text{z},q}\right)=\sum_{\mu}\mu^{2}=\dfrac{1}{12}(n+1)n(n-1)\equiv\xi^{2}.

(103)

The Lax formulation (following Refs.
Yuzbashyan et al. 2005
;
Yuzbashyan and Dzero 2006
;
Yuzbashyan et al. 2006
;
Yuzbashyan et al. 2015
;
Smale et al. 2019
) requires all single-body operators involved to have the same normalization, so we substitute
\hat{s}_{\tilde{\text{z}},q}\equiv\hat{s}_{\text{z},q}/\xi
to expand

\displaystyle\dfrac{\hat{H}_{\text{spin}}}{u}=-\dfrac{1}{2N}\sum_{\mu,\nu}\hat{S}_{\mu\nu}\hat{S}_{\nu\mu}+\xi h\sum_{q}\sin\left(q\right)\hat{s}_{\tilde{\text{z}},q},

where

\displaystyle h\equiv\dfrac{2J\phi}{u}.

(104)

The intensive, dimensionless,
(n^{2}-1)
-component Lax vector
\vec{\ell}\left(z\right)
associated with
\hat{H}_{\text{spin}}
, which is defined with an auxiliary complex parameter
z
, has components

\displaystyle\ell_{\alpha}\left(z\right)=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{q}\dfrac{\hat{s}_{\alpha,q}}{z-\sin q}+\delta_{\alpha,\tilde{\text{z}}}\,\xi h,

(105)

where
\alpha
indexes elements of a basis
\left\{\hat{s}_{\alpha}\right\}
of self-adjoint generators of SU(
n
), with normalization
\tr\left(\hat{s}_{\alpha}^{2}\right)=1
.
The squared magnitude
\vec{\ell}\left(z\right)^{2}=\sum_{\alpha}\ell_{\alpha}\left(z\right)^{2}
is a constant of motion (for any
z
), and its residues provide
N
mutually commuting quantities whose weighted sum recovers
\hat{H}_{\text{spin}}
.
When
n=2
, conservation of these residues provides sufficient dynamical constraints to make the spin system fully integrable.
In this case, dynamical behavior is governed by the roots of
\vec{\ell}\left(z\right)^{2}
, and the presence (or absence) of complex roots marks distinct dynamical phases of
\hat{H}_{\text{spin}}
.
However, the size of Hilbert space grows with
n
, while the number of conserved quantities provided by the Lax analysis (namely,
N
) does not.
When
n>2
, there is therefore no guarantee that the roots of
