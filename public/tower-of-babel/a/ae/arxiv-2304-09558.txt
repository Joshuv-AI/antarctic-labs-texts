Pseudo-differential calculi and entropy estimates with Orlicz modulation spaces
Anupam Gumber, Nimit Rana, Joachim Toft, Rüya Üster

Abstract
. We deduce continuity properties for pseudo-differential operators with symbols in Orlicz modulation spaces when acting on other Orlicz modulation spaces. In particular we extend well-known results in the literature. For example we generalize the classical result that if 1 p ′ + 1 q ′ ≤ 1 p 1 + 1 p 2 , 1 p ′ + 1 q ′ ≤ 1 q 1 + 1 q 2 , p j , q j ≤ q ′ , q ≤ p \frac{1}{p^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}\leq\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}},\quad\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}\leq\frac{1}{q_{1}}+\frac{1}{q_{2}},\quad p_{j},q_{j}\leq q^{\prime},\quad q\leq p and a ∈ M p , q a\in M^{p,q} , then the pseudo-differential operator Op ⁡ ( a ) \operatorname{Op}(a) is continuous from M p 1 , q 1 M^{p_{1},q_{1}} to M p 2 ′ , q 2 ′ M^{p_{2}^{\prime},q_{2}^{\prime}} . We also show that the entropy functional E ϕ E_{\phi} possess suitable continuity properties on a suitable Orlicz modulation space M Φ M^{\Phi} satisfying M p ⊆ M Φ ⊆ M 2 M^{p}\subseteq M^{\Phi}\subseteq M^{2} , though E ϕ E_{\phi} is discontinuous on M 2 = L 2 M^{2}=L^{2} .

Introduction

Pseudo-differential operators are important in several
fields of science and technology. In the theory of
partial differential equations, they are convenient
tools for handling various kinds of problems, e. g.
parametrix constructions, micro-local properties and
invertibility of (hypo-)elliptic operators. In
time-frequency analysis, pseudo-differential operators
appears when modelling non-stationary filters.

A pseudo-differential operator is a rule
in which for every function or distribution

a
(the symbol), defined
on the phase space (or time-frequency shift
space)
\mathbf{R}^{2d}
assigns a linear operator

\operatorname{Op}(a)
acting on functions or distributions
defined on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
. The assumptions on
the symbols, domains and ranks for
the pseudo-differential operators, usually
resemble on structures where they are
applied. Therefore, in the theory of
partial differential operators,
one usually assumes that the symbols
are smooth and that
differentiations of the symbols
lead to more restrictive
growth/decay properties at infinity.

When using pseudo-differential operators
for modelling time-dependent filters
in time-frequency analysis, any similar
assumptions on smoothness are usually
not relevant. Here it is more
relevant to assume that the involved
symbols, inputs and outputs (i. e.
the filter constants, ingoing signals
and outgoing signals) should fulfill
conditions on translation
and modulation invariance, as
well as
certain energy estimates of the
time-frequency content. This leads
to that the involved functions
and distributions should belong to
suitable
modulation spaces

a family of functions and distribution
spaces, introduced by Feichtinger in

[
8
]
. The theory of such spaces
was thereafter extended in several ways
(see e. g.

[
7
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
13
,
35
,
37
,
42
,
40
]
and the references
therein).

Recently, some investigations of
Orlicz modulation spaces have been performed in

[
45
,
46
,
34
]
. Such spaces are obtained by
imposing an Orlicz norm estimates on the
short time Fourier transforms of the involved
functions and distributions. By the definition it follows
that the family of Orlicz modulation spaces contain
all classical modulation spaces
M^{p,q}_{(\omega)}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
,
introduced by Feichtinger in
[
8
]
, which essentially
follows from the fact that the family of Orlicz spaces contains
all Lebesgue spaces. (See

[
21
]
and Section

On the other hand, the Orlicz modulation spaces becomes
a subfamily of broader classes of modulation spaces, given in
e. g.
[

30
,
31
]
.

A question which might appear is whether there are relevant
situations where it is fruitful to search among
Orlicz modulation spaces to deduce sharper estimates compared
to classical modulation spaces.
For example, consider the entropy functional
on short-time Fourier transforms

E(f)=E_{\phi}(f)\equiv-\iint_{\mathbf{R}^{2d}}|V_{\phi}f(x,\xi)|^{2}\log|V_{\phi}f(x,\xi)|^{2}\,dxd\xi+\|V_{\phi}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\log\|V_{\phi}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

We also give some examples on Young functions,

and

1.1.
Gelfand-Shilov spaces

For a real number
s>0
, the (standard Fourier invariant) Gelfand-Shilov
space
\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

(
\Sigma_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
) of Roumieu type (Beurling type)
consists of all

\|f\|_{\mathcal{S}_{s,h}}\equiv\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in\mathbf{N}^{d}\\

(1.1)

is finite for some
h>0
(for every
h>0
). We equip

\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
(
\Sigma_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
) by the canonical inductive limit
topology (projective limit topology) with respect to
h>0
, induced by
the semi-norms defined in (
1.1

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\Sigma_{t}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{t}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\hookrightarrow\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

(1.2)

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{t}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\hookrightarrow\Sigma_{t}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),\quad\frac{1}{2}\leq s<t,

with dense embeddings.
Here
A\hookrightarrow B
means that
the topological space
A
is continuously embedded in the
topological space
B

\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})=\Sigma_{t}(\mathbf{R}^{d})=\{0\},\qquad s<\frac{1}{2},\ t\leq\frac{1}{2}.

The
Gelfand-Shilov distribution spaces

and

and

(

),

(
\Sigma_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

\mathcal{S}_{s,h}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
with respect to
h>0
. It follows that

\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})=\bigcap_{h>0}\mathcal{S}_{s,h}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\quad\text{and}\quad\Sigma_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})=\bigcup_{h>0}\mathcal{S}_{s,h}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

(1.3)

for such choices of

\sigma

1.2.
Weight functions

A
weight
or
weight function
on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
is a
positive function
\omega\in L^{\infty}_{loc}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
such that
1/\omega\in L^{\infty}_{loc}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.
The weight
\omega
is called
moderate
,
if there is a positive weight
v
on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
such that

\omega(x+y)\lesssim\omega(x)v(y),\qquad x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}.

(1.6)

If
\omega
and

are weights on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
such that
(
1.6
) holds, then
\omega
is also called

v
-moderate
.
We note that (
1.6
)
implies that
\omega
fulfills
the estimates

v(-x)^{-1}\lesssim\omega(x)\lesssim v(x),\quad x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}.

(1.7)

We let
\mathscr{P}_{E}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
be the set of all moderate weights on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
.

It can be proved that if
\omega\in\mathscr{P}_{E}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, then

\omega
is
v
-moderate for some
v(x)=e^{r|x|}
, provided the
positive constant
r
is large enough (cf.
[
16
]
).
That is,
(
1.6
) implies

\omega(x+y)\lesssim\omega(x)e^{r|y|}

(1.8)

for some
r>0
. In particular, (
1.7
) shows that
for any
\omega\in\mathscr{P}_{E}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, there is a constant
r>0
such that

e^{-r|x|}\lesssim\omega(x)\lesssim e^{r|x|},\quad x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}.

(1.9)

We say that
v
is

submultiplicative
if
v
is even and
(
1.6
)
holds with
\omega=v
. In the sequel,

and

for

nothing else is stated.

We let
\mathscr{P}^{0}_{E}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
be the set of all
\omega\in\mathscr{P}_{E}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

such that (

) holds for every

. We also let

be the set of all
\omega\in\mathscr{P}_{E}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

Evidently,

1.3.
Orlicz Spaces

We recall that a function
\Phi:[0,\infty]\to[0,\infty]
is called

\Phi(s_{1}t_{1}+s_{2}t_{2})\leq s_{1}\Phi(t_{1})+s_{2}\Phi(t_{2}),

when

s_{j},t_{j}\in\mathbf{R}

satisfy
s_{j},t_{j}\geq 0
and

s_{1}+s_{2}=1,\ j=1,2

Definition 1.2
.

A function
\Phi_{0}
from
[0,\infty]
to

[0,\infty]

is called a
Young function
if
the following is true:

(1)

\Phi_{0}
is convex;

(2)

\Phi_{0}(0)=0
;

(3)

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\Phi_{0}(t)=\Phi_{0}(\infty)=\infty
.

A function
\Phi
from
[0,\infty]
to

[0,\infty]
is called a
quasi-Young function

(of order
p_{0}\in(0,1]
) if there is a Young function

\Phi_{0}
such that
\Phi(t)=\Phi_{0}(t^{p_{0}})
when

t\in[0,\infty]
.

We observe that
\Phi_{0}
and
\Phi

in Definition
1.2
might not
be continuous, because we permit

\infty
as function value. For example,

\Phi(t)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{when}\ t\leq a\\[4.30554pt]
\infty,&\text{when}\ t>a\end{cases}

Definition 1.5
.

Let

be a vector space. Then the functional

(3)

\|f+g\|_{\mathscr{B}}^{p_{0}}\leq\|f\|_{\mathscr{B}}^{p_{0}}+\|g\|_{\mathscr{B}}^{p_{0}}

.

The space
\mathscr{B}
is called a quasi-Banach space
(of order
p_{0}
) or an
p_{0}
-Banach space, if
\mathscr{B}
is complete
under the topology induced by the quasi-norm
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\mathscr{B}}
.

1.5.
Pseudo-differential operators

Next we recall some basic facts from
pseudo-differential calculus
(cf.
[
21
]
). Let
s\geq 1/2
,
a\in\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
, and let
A
belong to
\mathbf{M}(d,\mathbf{R})
,
the set of all
d\times d
-matrices with entries in
\mathbf{R}
.
Then the pseudo-differential operator
\operatorname{Op}_{A}(a)

defined by

\operatorname{Op}_{A}(a)f(x)=(2\pi)^{-d}\iint_{\mathbf{R}^{2d}}a(x-A(x-y),\xi)f(y)e^{i\langle x-y,\xi\rangle}\,dyd\xi

(1.17)

is a linear and continuous operator on
\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.
For
a\in\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
the
pseudo-differential operator
\operatorname{Op}_{A}(a)
is defined as
the continuous
operator from
\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
to
\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
with
distribution kernel given by

K_{a,A}(x,y)=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}(\mathscr{F}_{2}^{-1}a)(x-A(x-y),x-y).

(1.18)

Here
\mathscr{F}_{2}F
is the partial Fourier transform of
F(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
with respect to the variable
y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
. This
definition generalizes (
1.17
) and is well defined, since the mappings

\mathscr{F}_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad F(x,y)\mapsto F(x-A(x-y),y-x)

(1.19)

are homeomorphisms on
\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
.
The map
a\mapsto K_{a,A}
is hence a homeomorphism on

\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
.

If
A=0
, then
\operatorname{Op}_{A}(a)
is the standard or Kohn-Nirenberg representation

a(x,D)
. If instead
A=\frac{1}{2}I_{d}
, then
\operatorname{Op}_{A}(a)
agrees
with the Weyl operator or Weyl quantization
\operatorname{Op}^{w}(a)
. Here

I_{d}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix.

For any
K\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}})
, let
T_{K}
be the
linear and continuous mapping from
\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d_{1}})

to
\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d_{2}})
defined by

(T_{K}f,g)_{L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d_{2}})}=(K,g\otimes\overline{f})_{L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}})},\quad f\in\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d_{1}}),\quad g\in\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d_{2}}).

(1.20)

It is a well-known consequence of the Schwartz kernel
theorem that if
A\in\mathbf{M}(d,\mathbf{R})
, then

K\mapsto T_{K}
and
a\mapsto\operatorname{Op}_{A}(a)
are bijective mappings from

\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})

to the space of linear and continuous mappings from

\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
to

\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
(cf. e. g.
[
21
]
).

Likewise the maps
K\mapsto T_{K}

and
a\mapsto\operatorname{Op}_{A}(a)
are uniquely extendable to bijective
mappings from
\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
to the set of linear and
continuous mappings from
\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

to

A.1.
STFT Projections and
twisted convolutions

Let
s\geq\frac{1}{2}
.
If
\phi\in\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\setminus 0
,
then it follows from Fourier’s inversion
formula that

\operatorname{Id}=\operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}}=\left(\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}^{-2}\right)\cdot V_{\phi}^{*}\circ V_{\phi},

(A.1)

is the identity operator on

\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. The same and
following results hold
true with
\Sigma_{s}
and
\mathscr{S}

in place of
\mathcal{S}_{s}
at each occurrence.
The identity (
A.1
)
is equivalent to Moyal’s identity
(
3.1
). If we swap the
order of this composition we get certain types
of projections. More precisely, let

P_{\phi}\equiv\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}^{-2}\cdot V_{\phi}\circ V_{\phi}^{*}.

(A.2)

We observe that
P_{\phi}
is continuous on

\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
,

L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
and on
\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})

due to the mapping properties
for
V_{\phi}
and
V_{\phi}^{*}
.

It is clear that
P_{\phi}^{*}=P_{\phi}
, i. e.

P_{\phi}
is self-adjoint. Furthermore,

P_{\phi}^{2}=\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}^{-2}\cdot V_{\phi}\circ\Big(\underset{\text{The identity operator}}{\underbrace{\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}^{-2}\cdot V_{\phi}^{*}\circ V_{\phi}}}\Big)\circ V_{\phi}^{*}=\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}^{-2}\cdot V_{\phi}\circ V_{\phi}^{*}=P_{\phi},

giving that
P_{\phi}
is an orthonormal projection,
that is,

P_{\phi}^{*}=P_{\phi}\quad\text{and}\quad P_{\phi}^{2}=P_{\phi}.

(A.3)

The ranks of

are given by

\displaystyle P_{\phi}(\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{2d}))=V_{\phi}(\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))\quad\text{and}\quad P_{\phi}(\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d}))=V_{\phi}(\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})).

(A.4)

In fact, if

(A.5)

where

.
On the other hand, if

and

V_{\phi}(\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))

equals to an element in

P_{\phi}(\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d}))
,
i. e.
P_{\phi}(\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d}))=V_{\phi}(\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))
.
The same holds true
with
\mathcal{S}_{s}
in place of
\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}

at each occurrence, and
(
A.4
