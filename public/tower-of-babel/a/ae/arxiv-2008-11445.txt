Moufang sets generated by translations in unitals
Theo Grundhöfer, Markus J. Stroppel, Hendrik Van Maldeghem

Abstract
We consider unitals of order q q with two points which are centers of translation groups of order q q . The group G G generated by these translations induces a Moufang set on the block joining the two points. We show that G G is either SL ⁡ ( 2 , 𝔽 q ) \operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q}) (as in all classical unitals and also in some non-classical examples), or PSL ⁡ ( 2 , 𝔽 q ) \operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q}) , or a Suzuki or a Ree group. Moreover, G G is semi-regular outside the special block.

1
Unitals, translations and Moufang sets

A unital
\mathbb{U}=(U,\mathcal{B})
of order
q>1
is a

2
-
(q^{3}+1,q+1,1)
-design. In other words,
\mathbb{U}
is an incidence
structure such that any two points in
U
are joined by a unique block
in
\mathcal{B}
, there are
|U|=q^{3}+1
points, and every block has exactly

q+1
points. It follows that every point is on exactly
q^{2}
blocks.

1.1 Lemma
.

Let
\mathbb{V}
be a unital of order
q
, and let
\varphi\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{V})

be an automorphism of
\mathbb{V}
. If
\varphi
fixes more than

q^{2}+q-2
points then
\varphi
is trivial. In particular, if
\varphi
fixes a point
x
and a block
B
not
through
x
and all points on blocks joining
x
to a point on
B

then
\varphi
is trivial.

Proof.

Let
y
be a point that is moved by
\varphi
. Joining
y
with each
one of the fixed points yields a set of blocks through
y
. At most
one of those blocks can be a fixed block of
\varphi
, and a non-fixed
block contains at most one fixed point. If a fixed block through
y
exists then that block contains at most

q-1
fixed points. For the number
f
of fixed points we obtain

q^{2}-1\geq f-(q-1)
and
f\leq q^{2}+q-2
. If no block through
y
is
fixed then
f\leq q^{2}\leq q^{2}+q-2
.

The second assertion follows from the fact that the point set in
question contains
(q+1)q+1=q^{2}+q+1
points.
∎

An automorphism of
\mathbb{U}
is called a
translation
of

\mathbb{U}
with center
z
if it fixes each block through the
point
z
. The set of all translations with center
z
is denoted
by
\Gamma_{[z]}
.

A
Moufang set
is a set
X
together with a collection of groups

(R_{x})_{x\in X}
of permutations of
X
such that each
R_{x}
fixes
x

and acts regularly (i. e., sharply transitively) on

X\smallsetminus\{x\}
, and such that the collection

\{{R_{y}}\left|\vphantom{}\right.\,{y\in X}\}
is invariant under conjugation by the

little projective group

\langle{R_{x}}\,|\,{x\in X}\rangle
of
the Moufang set. The groups
R_{x}
are called
root groups
.

The finite Moufang sets are known explicitly:

1.2 Theorem
.

The little projective group of a finite Moufang set is either
sharply two-transitive, or it is permutation isomorphic to one of
the following two-transitive permutation groups of degree
q+1
:

\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q})
with a prime power
q>3
,
\operatorname{PSU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{f^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{f})
with
a prime power
q=f^{3}\geq 3^{3}
, a Suzuki group

\mathrm{Sz}(2^{s})={}^{2}B_{2}(2^{s})
with
q=2^{2s}\geq 2^{6}
, or a Ree group

\mathrm{Ree}(3^{r})={}^{2}G_{2}(3^{r})
with
q=3^{3r}
, where
r
and
s
are
positive odd integers.

This was proved (in the context of split BN-pairs of rank one) by
Suzuki
[
32
]
and Shult
[
28
]
for even
q
, and
by Hering, Kantor and Seitz
[
15
]
for odd
q
; these papers
rely on deep results on finite groups, but not on the classification
of all finite simple groups. See also Peterfalvi
[
25
]
. Note that
\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{2})\cong\operatorname{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{3})
,

\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{3})\cong\operatorname{A}_{4}\cong\operatorname{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{4})
,

\operatorname{PSU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{4}|\mathbb{F}_{2})\cong\operatorname{ASL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{3})
and
\mathrm{Sz}(2)\cong\operatorname{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{5})
are
sharply two-transitive. The smallest Ree group
\mathrm{Ree}(3)\cong\operatorname{P\Gamma L}(2,\mathbb{F}_{8})

is almost simple, but not simple.

Let
\mathbb{U}=(U,\mathcal{B})
be a unital of order
q
, and let

\Gamma=\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{U})
be its automorphism group. Throughout this paper, we assume that
\mathbb{U}
contains two
points
\infty
and
o
such that for
z\in\{\infty,o\}
the
translation group
\Gamma_{[z]}
has order
q
. Then
\Gamma_{[z]}
acts
transitively on
B\smallsetminus\{z\}
, for any block
B
through
z

(see
[
11
, 1.3]
). In particular,
\Gamma_{[x]}
has that
transitivity property for each point
x
on the block
B_{\infty}

joining
\infty
and
o
. The group
G
generated by

\Gamma_{[\infty]}\cup\Gamma_{[o]}
contains the translation group
\Gamma_{[x]}
for
each
x\in B_{\infty}
, and

(B_{\infty},(\Gamma_{[x]}|_{B_{\infty}})_{x\in B_{\infty}})
is a Moufang set,
with little projective group

G^{\dagger}\coloneqq G|_{B_{\infty}}\cong G/G_{[B_{\infty}]}
, where

G_{[B_{\infty}]}
is the kernel of the action on
B_{\infty}
. This
kernel coincides with the center
Z
of
G
,
see
[
11
, 3.1.2]
or
[
13
, 2.11]
. So
G
is a
central extension of the little projective group
G^{\dagger}
.

1.3 Corollary
.

The kernel
G_{[B_{\infty}]}=Z
acts semi-regularly on

U\smallsetminus B_{\infty}
.

Proof.

If
\varphi\in G_{[B_{\infty}]}=Z
fixes
x\in U\smallsetminus B_{\infty}
,
then it fixes also
x^{g}
for every
g\in G
, hence
all points on blocks joining
x
to a point on
B_{\infty}
.
Thus Lemma
1.1
implies that
\varphi
is trivial.
∎

1.4 Lemma
.

Let
(X,\{{\Delta_{x}}\left|\vphantom{}\right.\,{x\in X}\})
be a finite Moufang set.
If the little
projective group
\Phi=\langle\Delta_{x}\mid x\in X\rangle
is simple then

\Delta_{x}=[\Delta_{x},\Phi_{x}]
for every
x\in X
, where
\Phi_{x}

denotes the stabilizer of
x
in
\Phi
.

Proof.

By the classification of finite Moufang sets, see

1.2
, the simple group
\Phi
is isomorphic to

\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q})
,
\operatorname{PSU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{t^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{t})
,
\mathrm{Sz}(2^{s})
,
or
\mathrm{Ree}(3^{r})
, where
q>3
with
q+1=|X|
,
t>2
with
t^{3}+1=|X|
,

s>1
with
2^{2s}+1=|X|
, or
r>1
with
3^{3r}+1=|X|
,
respectively. We have
[\Delta_{x},\Phi_{x}]\leq\Delta_{x}
since
\Phi_{x}
normalizes

\Delta_{x}
; it remains to show that

\Delta_{x}\leq[\Delta_{x},\Phi_{x}]
. This inclusion is an ingredient
in simplicity proofs for
\Phi
that use the Iwasawa criterion:

For
\Phi=\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q})
the necessary commutators are computed
in the proof of
[
34
, Theorem 4.4, page 23]
. The case where

\Phi=\operatorname{PSU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{t^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{t})
is covered by
[
17
, Proof of II.10.13,
page 244]
. For
\Phi=\mathrm{Sz}(2^{s})
the assertion follows from
the commutator formula in
[
34
, page 205]
, and for

\Phi=\mathrm{Ree}(3^{r})
the three commutator
formulas in
[
6
, § 5, pages 36/37]
yield the assertion.
∎

1.6 Proposition
.

If
G^{\dagger}
is simple then
G
is a perfect central extension
of
G^{\dagger}
, i.e.
G
coincides with its commutator group
G^{\prime}
,
and
G_{[B_{\infty}]}
is isomorphic to a quotient of the Schur
multiplier of
G^{\dagger}
.

Proof.

If
z\in B_{\infty}
,
\tau\in\Gamma_{[z]}
and

\gamma\in G_{z}
, then
\gamma^{-1}\tau\gamma\in\Gamma_{[z]}
and

[\tau,\gamma]=\tau^{-1}\gamma^{-1}\tau\gamma\in\Gamma_{[z]}
.
Since
\Gamma_{[z]}
acts regularly on
B_{\infty}\smallsetminus\{z\}
, every
element of
\Gamma_{[z]}
is determined by its action
on
B_{\infty}
, i.e. by its image in
G^{\dagger}
.
By
1.4
every element of
\Gamma_{[z]}

is a product of elements in
\Gamma_{[z]}
that are commutators.
Hence
\Gamma_{[z]}\leq G^{\prime}
for every
z\in B_{\infty}
, and therefore

G^{\prime}=G
.

The kernel
G_{[B_{\infty}]}
of the action on
B_{\infty}
is the
center of
G
, so the perfect group
G
is a central extension of

G^{\dagger}=G/G_{[B_{\infty}]}
. Therefore
G_{[B_{\infty}]}
is
isomorphic to a quotient of the Schur multiplier of
G^{\dagger}
; see

[
20
, 2.1.7]
,
[
17
, V.23.3, page 629]
, or

[
4
, 33.8 (4) p. 169]
.
∎

2
Sharply two-transitive groups

2.1 Proposition
.

If
q\leq 3
then
\mathbb{U}
is the hermitian unital of order
q
.

Proof.

Every unital of order
2
is isomorphic to the hermitian one, see
e.g.
[
34
, 10.16]
.
Now let
q=3
. Since
G^{\dagger}\leq\operatorname{S}_{4}
is generated by
elements of order
3
, we have
G^{\dagger}=\operatorname{A}_{4}\cong\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{3})
; in
particular,
G^{\dagger}
is sharply two-transitive.
By
[
11
, 2.3]
the center
Z=G_{[B_{\infty}]}
has even order,
so there exists a central involution
\zeta

in
G
.

The product
G^{\prime}Z
induces the commutator group
(G^{\dagger})^{\prime}\cong\operatorname{C}\nolimits_{2}^{2}
.
Thus
G^{\prime}Z
has index
3
, and
G^{\prime}
acts transitively
on
B_{\infty}
. For
z\in B_{\infty}
, the translation group
\Gamma_{[z]}

is not contained in
G^{\prime}
. We obtain
G=G^{\prime}\Gamma_{[z]}G^{\prime}=G^{\prime}\Gamma_{[z]}
, and
G^{\prime}
has index
3
in
G
. This means that
Z\leq G^{\prime}
, and
G
is a
covering group of
\operatorname{A}_{4}
. Then
G\cong\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{3})
by
[
20
, 2.12.5]
.
This group acts regularly on
U\smallsetminus B_{\infty}
,
see
[
11
, 3.5]
.

We verify that
\mathbb{U}
is obtained by the construction described
in
[
12
, 2.1]
. The central involution
\zeta
does not fix any point apart from
those on
B_{\infty}
. Therefore, the point set
U\smallsetminus B_{\infty}

is partitioned by fixed blocks of
\zeta
; these are obtained as the
blocks joining
x\in U\smallsetminus B_{\infty}
with its image
under
\zeta
. The group
G
acts on this set of fixed blocks. There are
6
such
blocks, and at least one of them is fixed by a subgroup
S
of
order
4
in
G
. We pick a point
a
on that block and identify
the elements of
G
with the affine points via

\gamma\mapsto a^{\gamma}
. Then the block in question is
S
.

There are
4
blocks through
a
that join
a
to points
on
B_{\infty}
, their intersections with
U\smallsetminus B_{\infty}
are
identified with the Sylow
3
-subgroups (viz., the translation
groups) in
G
. Let
D
be any one of the remaining
4
blocks
through
a
. Then
D
is not stabilized by any translation, and not
stabilized by
\zeta
. As
\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{3})
contains only one
involution, we infer that the stabilizer of
D
in
G
is
trivial. Therefore, the set

\mathcal{D}\coloneqq\{{D\delta^{-1}}\left|\vphantom{}\right.\,{\delta\in D}\}

consists of
4
different blocks through
a
.

It has been proved in
[
12
, 3.3]
that the subgroup
S

and the set
\mathcal{D}
are unique, up to conjugation. The points at
infinity are the centers of translations. Therefore each such point
is incident with those blocks whose points outside
B_{\infty}
form an
orbit under the corresponding translation group.
This completes the proof that
\mathbb{U}
is isomorphic to the hermitian
unital
\mathbb{U}_{\mathcal{H}_{3}}
, see
[
12
, 3.3]
.
∎

Now we determine certain central extensions of finite sharply two-transitive
permutation groups; the following result is a variation of

[
14
, Lemma 1.1]
that is suitable for our purpose.

2.2 Theorem
.

Let
(G,X)
be a finite sharply two-transitive permutation group
with
|X|>1
and let
p
be the prime dividing
|X|
. If
E
is
a central extension of
G=E/Z
by a group
Z
of order
p
,
then
E
splits over
Z
(as a direct product
E=Y\times Z
with

Y\cong G
), or we have one of the following:

(a)

|X|=2=|G|
and
E
is cyclic of order
4
.

(b)

|X|=4
,
G=\operatorname{A}_{4}\cong\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{3})
and
E\cong\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{3})
.

(c)

|X|=p^{2}\in\{3^{2},5^{2},7^{2},11^{2}\}
and
E=P\rtimes H

where
P
is the Heisenberg group of order
p^{3}
and
H
is isomorphic
to
Q_{8}
,
\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{3})
,
2^{-}\operatorname{S}_{4}
or
\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{5})
, respectively.

We describe the groups in item (c).
The Heisenberg group of order
p^{3}
consists of all unipotent upper
triangular matrices in
\operatorname{GL}(3,\mathbb{F}_{p})
. By
Q_{8}
we denote the quaternion
group of order
8
, and
2^{-}\operatorname{S}_{4}
is the binary octahedral group,
i.e. the double cover of
\operatorname{S}_{4}
containing
just one involution, see
[
33
, 3.2.21, p. 301]
or

[
18
, XII.8.4]
; this double cover is isomorphic to the normalizer of

\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{3})
in
\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{9})
. The extension groups
E
in item (c) do not
split over
Z
since
P
is not abelian.

Proof of
2.2
.

It is well known that
|X|=p^{n}
is a power of a prime
p
and that
the Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
is an elementary abelian normal
subgroup of order
p^{n}
in
G
; see e.g.
[
27
, 7.3.1]
or

[
24
, 8.4]
or
[
18
, XII.9.1]
.

Let
P
be a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
E
. Then
|P|=p^{n+1}
and

Z\leq P
; moreover
P/Z
is the regular normal subgroup of
G
,
hence
P
is normal in
E
. Each point stabilizer (or Frobenius
complement)
G_{x}
has order
p^{n}-1
, and its pre-image
E_{x}\leq E

has order
(p^{n}-1)p
. The group
E_{x}
splits as a direct product

H\times Z
with
H\cong G_{x}
by the abelian (in fact, central) case
of the Schur–Zassenhaus theorem; see
[
27
, 9.1.2 or
11.4.12]
or
[
24
, 10.3]
or
[
17
, I.17.5,
page 122]
. Then

E=P\rtimes H

and
H
acts (by conjugation) sharply transitively on the set of
non-trivial elements of
P/Z
.

If
H
is trivial, then
|X|=2=|G|
, and
E
splits or is cyclic
as in item (a). From now on let
|H|>1
. Then
C_{P}(H)/Z
is a
proper
H
-invariant subgroup of
P/Z
, hence trivial. This means
that
C_{P}(H)=Z
. If
P
is abelian, then

P=C_{P}(H)\times[P,H]=Z\times[P,H]
, see

[
8
, 5.2.39]
or
[
27
, 10.1.6]
or

[
17
, III.13.4, p. 350]
, and then

splits over
Z
.

Now let
P
be non-abelian. Then
P^{\prime}
is a non-trivial subgroup of

Z
, as
P/Z
is (elementary) abelian, hence
P^{\prime}=Z
. The center of

yields a proper

-invariant subgroup of
P/Z
; this subgroup
is trivial, hence

is the center of
P
(and the Frattini
subgroup is
\Phi(P)=P^{p}P^{\prime}=Z
). Thus

is an extraspecial

p
-group.

The commutator map gives a non-zero symplectic form
f
on
P/Z

with values in the prime field

, and

is not degenerate,
hence
n=2m
is even; see
[
27
, p. 140]
or

[
17
, III.13.7, p. 353]
. The automorphism group
\mathop{\mathchoice{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\textstyle{H}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\textstyle{H}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\scriptstyle{H}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\scriptscriptstyle{H}\hfil$\crcr}}}}

on

has trivial intersection with the group of

and acts trivially on

, hence

by Winter
[

.

First we assume that the permutation group
(G,X)

2.3 Theorem
.

If
G^{\dagger}
is sharply two-transitive on
B_{\infty}
, then
q\leq 3
and
\mathbb{U}
is the hermitian unital of order
q
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.1
it suffices to show that
q\leq 3
. Thus
we assume that
q>3
and aim for a contradiction.

The degree
q+1
of
G^{\dagger}
is a power of some prime
r
, say

q+1=r^{n}
. By
[
11
, 3.1]
the kernel
G_{[B_{\infty}]}
is the center

Z
of
G
, and
r
divides
|Z|
by
[
11
, 2.3]
. Thus we
can choose a subgroup
U
of index
r
in
Z
; then
G/U
is a
central extension of
G^{\dagger}
by the group
Z/U
of order

r
. Such an extension

does not split: if

G/U=Z/U\times Y/U
then
Y
contains all Sylow
s
-subgroups of

G
with
s\neq r
, hence all translation groups
\Gamma_{[x]}
with

x\in B_{\infty}
; thus
Y=G
, which is a contradiction to

|Z/U|=r
.

Theorem
2.2
implies that
n=2\neq r
and that the Sylow

r
-subgroup of
G/U
is not abelian (and more, as in item (c), but
we do not need more). Let
R
be a Sylow
r
-subgroup of
G
and
let
H:=\Gamma_{[o]}
. Then
RZ/Z
is the regular normal subgroup
of
G^{\dagger}
, and
R
is characteristic in
RZ
, which is normal
in
G
; hence
R
is normal in
G
. The group
RHR=RH
contains
all conjugates of
H
in
G
, hence
G=RH=R\rtimes H
. Thus

Z=G_{[B_{\infty}]}=G_{o,\infty}=(R_{o}H)_{\infty}=(R\cap Z)H_{\infty}=R\cap Z
, which gives
Z\leq R
. The group
R
is not
abelian, but
R/Z
is abelian and has order
r^{2}
; thus
Z
is the
center of
R
. Now a (special case of a) result of Wiegold says that
|R^{\prime}|
divides

r
; see
[
40
, Theorem 2.1]
,
[
33
, p. 261]
,

[
20
, Lemma 3.1.1, p. 113]
or
[
17
, page 637]
. We claim that
R^{\prime}=Z
. Otherwise we can choose
U
as
above with
R^{\prime}\leq U<Z
, and then
R/U
is an abelian Sylow

r
-subgroup of
G/U
, contrary to Theorem
2.2
.

Thus
R^{\prime}=Z
has order
r
, and
R
is an extraspecial group of
order
r^{3}
. Since
r\neq 2
the group
H=\Gamma_{[o]}
contains an
involution
\alpha
inducing inversion on
R/R^{\prime}=R/Z
, hence
\alpha

fixes each subgroup between
Z
and
R
.

Each subgroup of order
r^{2}
is normal in
R
with abelian quotient,
and thus contains
R^{\prime}=Z
. As the group
H
acts transitively on the set of non-trivial
elements of
R/R^{\prime}
, it also acts transitively on the set of
subgroups of order
r^{2}
in
R
. If
S
is one of those subgroups
then
R
acts transitively on the set of non-central subgroups of
order
r
in
S
. There are
r
such subgroups, and the
involution
\alpha
(which leaves
S
invariant) fixes at least one
of them, say

.

The number of points not on
B_{\infty}
is

q^{3}+1-(q+1)=r^{2}(r^{2}-1)(r^{2}-2)
, and not divisible by
r^{3}=|R|
.
Therefore, there exists some subgroup of order
r
fixing at least
one point
x
not on
B_{\infty}
. That subgroup is not contained in the
center because the latter acts semi-regularly on

,
see
[
11
, 1.7]
. We have noted in the previous paragraph
that the non-central subgroups of order

form a single conjugacy

. Thus the group
T
fixes some affine point

. Then

fixes also

, and the point

where

meets the block joining

and

. This
contradicts the fact that

induces a subgroup of order
r
in the
regular normal subgroup on
B_{\infty}
.
∎

3.1 Lemma
.

Let

the following hold:

(a)

in
\operatorname{GL}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}})

is diagonalizable over
\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}
.

(b)

If

is an element of order
d
in
\operatorname{SU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})

then the characteristic polynomial of
A
is

X^{3}-t_{A}X^{2}+\mathop{\mathchoice{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\textstyle{t_{A}}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\textstyle{t_{A}}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\scriptstyle{t_{A}}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\scriptscriptstyle{t_{A}}\hfil$\crcr}}}}X-1
, where

is the trace of
A
.

(c)

Two elements of order

in
\operatorname{SU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})
are
conjugates under
\operatorname{SU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})
if, and only if, they
have the same trace.

(a) Let

. The minimal

then divides

, and every characteristic

\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}
because
r+1
divides the order of the multiplicative group of
\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}
.
As the minimal polynomial has only simple roots, the matrix
A
is
diagonalizable in
\operatorname{GL}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}})
.

(b) Now assume

. Let

. In the characteristic polynomial

equals

. The coefficient

equals

, where

is
the trace of

. Expanding the product of the linear factors, we

1.0pt\cr$\hfil\textstyle{\lambda_{0}}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern . Note
also that
F_{u,v}^{4}=\mathrm{id}
, and
F_{u,v}^{2}=\mathrm{id}
holds if
u=0
(then

v\in\mathbb{F}_{r}
). The product

JF_{u,v}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&u&v\\

.

Let

be the trace of the given matrix

and put

.
The norm map

N\colon\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}\to\mathbb{F}_{r}\colon x\mapsto x\mathop{\mathchoice{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\textstyle{x}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\textstyle{x}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\scriptstyle{x}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\scriptscriptstyle{x}\hfil$\crcr}}}}=x^{r+1}
is
surjective, hence we find
u\in\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}
such that

u\mathop{\mathchoice{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\textstyle{u}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\textstyle{u}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\scriptstyle{u}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\scriptscriptstyle{u}\hfil$\crcr}}}}=v+\mathop{\mathchoice{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\textstyle{v}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\textstyle{v}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\scriptstyle{v}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\scriptscriptstyle{v}\hfil$\crcr}}}}
.
We abbreviate
F\coloneqq F_{u,v}
and infer from
3.1

that
JF
and the diagonalizable matrix
A
have the same
characteristic polynomial, hence also the same set of eigenvalues.
If
JF
is diagonalizable, then
JF
has the same order as
A
, and

3.1
implies that
A
is conjugate to
JF
in

\operatorname{SU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})
. Now
A^{2}
is conjugate to
(JF)^{2}=JFJF=(JFJ^{-1})F
.

It remains to consider the case where
JF
is not diagonalizable. Then
the characteristic polynomial has a root
\lambda
with multiplicity
2

(not
3
since
A
is not central), and
A
is conjugate to the
diagonal matrix
\operatorname{diag}(\lambda,\lambda,\lambda^{-2})

where
N(\lambda)=\lambda^{r+1}=1\neq\lambda^{3}
.
Thus
JF
is similar (i.e. conjugate in
\operatorname{GL}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}})
) to its Jordan normal form

\left(\begin{matrix}\lambda&0&0\\
1&\lambda&0\\
0&0&\lambda^{-2}\end{matrix}\right),

hence
(JF)^{2}
is similar to

\operatorname{diag}(\lambda^{2},\lambda^{2},\lambda^{-4})
which is
similar to
A^{2}
. The matrix
(JF)^{2}=JFJF=(JFJ^{-1})F
is
conjugate to
A^{2}
in
\operatorname{SU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})
by
3.1
.
∎

3.3 Remark
.

The assumption that
A
is not central is needed
in
3.2
. Indeed, for any field
F
of characteristic two, non-trivial central
elements of
\operatorname{GL}(n,F)
are never products of two elements in Sylow

2
-subgroups. In fact, a non-trivial central element is of the form

u\,\mathrm{id}
with
u\in F
. The elements of Sylow
2
-subgroups are
unipotent (i.e. they have
1
as their only characteristic
root). If the product of unipotent elements
S,T
equals
u\,\mathrm{id}

then
S=uT^{-1}
is a unipotent element with characteristic
root
u
, so
u=1
and the product is trivial, indeed.

3.4 Theorem
.

The little projective group
G^{\dagger}
is not isomorphic to

\operatorname{PSU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})
, for any
r
.

Proof.

If
G^{\dagger}
is isomorphic to
\operatorname{PSU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})
then the
translation groups are the root subgroups, i.e. the (Sylow)
subgroups of order
r^{3}
in
\operatorname{PSU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})
. In particular,
we have
q=r^{3}
.
For
r=2
we have
q=8
, and
G^{\dagger}
is (isomorphic to) the sharply
two-transitive group

\operatorname{PSU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{4}|\mathbb{F}_{2})\cong\operatorname{Q}\nolimits_{8}\ltimes\mathbb{F}_{3}^{2}
; this is excluded
by
2.3
.

From now on, let
r>2
. The group
\operatorname{PSU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})
is
perfect, and
G
is a perfect central extension
of
\operatorname{PSU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})
, see
1.6
or
[
11
, 3.1]
.
For the case
at hand, we know that
\operatorname{SU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})
is the universal cover
of
\operatorname{PSU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})
, see
[
9
, Thm. 2]
. So we assume that
\operatorname{SU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})
acts (not necessarily
faithfully) on the unital
\mathbb{U}
such that the root subgroups induce transitive
groups of translations with center on
B_{\infty}
.

Assume first that
r
is odd, and let
2^{a}
be the highest power
of
2
dividing
|U\smallsetminus B_{\infty}|=(r^{3}+1)r^{3}(r^{3}-1)
. Then

2^{a}
divides
(r^{3}+1)(r-1)
and
2^{a+1}
divides

(r^{3}+1)r^{3}(r-1)(r+1)=|\operatorname{SU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})|
. So some
point in
U\smallsetminus B_{\infty}
is fixed by some involution
\gamma\in\operatorname{SU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})
.
We use coordinates such that the hermitian form defining

\operatorname{SU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}})
is given by

x_{0}\mathop{\mathchoice{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\textstyle{y_{2}}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\textstyle{y_{2}}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\scriptstyle{y_{2}}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\scriptscriptstyle{y_{2}}\hfil$\crcr}}}}+x_{1}\mathop{\mathchoice{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\textstyle{y_{1}}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\textstyle{y_{1}}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\scriptstyle{y_{1}}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\scriptscriptstyle{y_{1}}\hfil$\crcr}}}}+x_{2}\mathop{\mathchoice{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\textstyle{y_{0}}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\textstyle{y_{0}}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\scriptstyle{y_{0}}\hfil$\crcr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}\kern 0.0pt\crcr\kern 1.0pt\cr$\hfil\scriptscriptstyle{y_{0}}\hfil$\crcr}}}}
.
Then the matrices
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&0\\
1&1&0\\
-1/2&-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)

and
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-4&-8\\
0&1&4\\
0&0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
belong to root groups of

\operatorname{SU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})
, and their
product
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-4&-8\\
1&-3&-4\\
-1/2&1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
is an involution (and represents an
involution in
\operatorname{PSU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})
).
All involutions in
\operatorname{SU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})
are conjugate by
3.1
,
hence
\gamma
is a product of two root elements
and does not
fix any point outside
B_{\infty}
; this is a contradiction.

Therefore
r
is even. Let
p
be a prime dividing
r+1
, and let
m
be
the largest integer such that
p^{m}
divides
r+1
. Then
p
is odd
(because
r
is even), and
p^{2m}
divides

(r^{3}+1)r^{3}(r^{2}-1)=|\operatorname{SU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})|
.

If
p>3
then
p
does not divide
r^{2}-r+1
, and
p^{m+1}
does not
divide
|U\smallsetminus B_{\infty}|=({r^{3}+1})r^{3}({r^{2}+r+1})({r-1})
. So
there exists at least one orbit whose length is not divisible
by
p^{m+1}
, and there exists an element
\gamma
of order
p
in
the stabilizer of some point not in
B_{\infty}
. If
\gamma
is not
central in
\operatorname{SU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})
then
\gamma^{2}

is a product of two root elements (see
3.2
)
and does not fix any point outside
B_{\infty}
.
So
\gamma
is a central element of order
p>3
in

\operatorname{SU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})
, contradicting the fact
that the center of
\operatorname{SU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{r^{2}}|\mathbb{F}_{r})
has order
3
or is
trivial.

There remains the case where
p=3
is the only prime divisor
of
r+1
. Then
r+1=2^{d}+1=3^{m}
for positive integers
d

and
m
. We infer that
r=2^{d}\in\{2,8\}
, see e.g.
[
24
, Lemma
19.3]
; this is an old result of Levi ben Gerson from
1343, see
[
5
, § 4, pp. 169 ff]
. Since
r>2
we have

r=8
and
m=2
. Then
3^{3}=3^{m+1}
is the highest power of
3

dividing

|U\smallsetminus B_{\infty}|=({r^{3}+1})r^{3}({r^{2}+r+1})({r-1})=2^{9}\cdot 3^{3}\cdot 7\cdot 19\cdot 73
but
3^{5}=3^{2m+1}
divides

|\operatorname{SU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{64}|\mathbb{F}_{8})|=(r^{3}+1)r^{3}(r^{2}-1)=2^{9}\cdot 3^{5}\cdot 7\cdot 19
. We now find an element
\gamma
of order
3
in the stabilizer of a
point not in
B_{\infty}
. If
\gamma
is not central in
\operatorname{SU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{64}|\mathbb{F}_{8})
then

\gamma=\gamma^{-2}
is a product of two root elements
(see
3.2
) and does not fix any point
outside
B_{\infty}
. So
\gamma
is a central element of order
3
in

\operatorname{SU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{64}|\mathbb{F}_{8})
and fixes every point in
\mathbb{U}
,
see
1.3
. This means that
\operatorname{SU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{64}|\mathbb{F}_{8})
induces on
\mathbb{U}
a group
isomorphic to
\operatorname{PSU}(3,\mathbb{F}_{64}|\mathbb{F}_{8})
, of order

(r^{3}+1)r^{3}(r^{2}-1)/3=(8^{3}+1)8^{3}(8^{2}-1)/3=2^{9}\cdot 3^{4}\cdot 7\cdot 19
. Since
3^{4}
does not divide

|U\smallsetminus B_{\infty}|
we still find an element of order
3
in the
stabilizer of a point not on
B_{\infty}
, and reach a contradiction
using
3.2
again.
∎

4
Suzuki groups and Ree groups

4.1 Theorem
.

If
G^{\dagger}
is a Suzuki group then
q\geq 2^{6}
and
G=G^{\dagger}
, and
G

acts semi-regularly on
U\smallsetminus B_{\infty}
.

Proof.

We have
G^{\dagger}=\mathrm{Sz}(2^{s})
for some odd integer
s\geq 1
, and
the unital has order
q=2^{2s}
. The smallest Suzuki group
\mathrm{Sz}(2)\cong\operatorname{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{5})
is sharply
two-transitive, and excluded by
2.3
.

The Schur multiplier of
\mathrm{Sz}(2^{3})
is elementary abelian of
order
4
, see
[
3
]
, cf.
[
41
, 4.2.4]
and

[
20
, 7.4.2]
. If
\zeta
is a central involution in
G
then
\zeta
acts
trivially on
B_{\infty}
, and joining any point
x
with
x^{\zeta}

gives a block
B
fixed by
\zeta
. If that block does not
meet
B_{\infty}
then
\zeta
fixes at least one of the
q+1=65

points on
B
. This contradicts
1.3
. So
B
contains
a point
z
of
B_{\infty}
. Then there exists a translation
\tau

with center
z
such that
x^{\zeta}=x^{\tau}
. The translations have
order dividing
4
, hence
\tau\zeta
is an element of order
2

or
4
fixing
x
. If
\tau
has order
4
then
(\tau\zeta)^{2}=\tau^{2}
is non-trivial
translation fixing
x
. This is impossible, so
\tau
is an
involution. The automorphisms
\zeta\tau
and
\tau
induce the same action
on
B_{\infty}
. In particular, the involution
\zeta\tau
fixes no
point on
B_{\infty}
apart from
z
. For each point
y\in U\smallsetminus B_{\infty}
, the block joining
y

and
y^{\tau\zeta}
is fixed by
\tau\zeta
, and meets
B_{\infty}
in
a fixed point of
\tau\zeta
; that point has to be
z
. This means
that
\tau\zeta
fixes every block through
z
, and is a translation
with center
z
. Now
\zeta=\tau(\tau\zeta)\in\Gamma_{[z]}
is a
translation fixing every point on
B_{\infty}
. This contradicts the
fact that a non-trivial translation fixes only one point. So

G=G^{\dagger}
holds if
G^{\dagger}=\mathrm{Sz}(2^{3})
.

If
G^{\dagger}=\mathrm{Sz}(2^{s})
with
s>3
then
G=G^{\dagger}
because the
Schur multiplier is trivial; see
[
3
]
,
cf.
[
41
, 4.2.4]
and
[
20
, 7.4.2]
. Thus we have
G=G^{\dagger}=\mathrm{Sz}(2^{s})
for
s\geq 3
.
Consequently, each element of order
2
or
4
in
G
is a
translation.
Apart from the elements of order
4
, every element in
\mathrm{Sz}(2^{s})
is
strongly real, i.e. a product of two involutions; see e.g.
[
21
, 24.7,
24.6]
. In particular, every non-trivial element is the product of two
translations (viz., elements of order dividing
4
), and does not fix
any point in
U\smallsetminus B_{\infty}
. So the action
of
G
on
U\smallsetminus B_{\infty}
is semi-regular. ∎

The following result is contained in
[
14
, 2.6]
; we give a
more detailed proof.

4.2 Lemma
.

In the Ree group
\mathrm{Ree}(r)
with
r=3^{2e+1}\geq 3
, every element of prime
order is the product of two elements with orders dividing
9
.

Proof.

All involutions in
\mathrm{Ree}(r)
are conjugate (also for
r=3
), so each
of them is contained in a subgroup isomorphic to

\mathrm{Ree}(3)\cong\operatorname{P\Gamma L}(2,\mathbb{F}_{8})\cong\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{8})\rtimes\operatorname{C}\nolimits_{3}
. The
factorization
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&u+1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&u\end{smallmatrix}\right)
in
\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{8})
, where
u\in\mathbb{F}_{8}
satisfies
u^{3}+u+1=0
,
shows that every involution is the product of an element of order

3
with an element of order
9
(it is also the product of two
elements of order
9
, see
[
11
, Case (6), p. 429]
).

The root elements of
\mathrm{Ree}(r)
have orders dividing
9
; thus it
remains to consider elements with prime order
p>3
. We have

|\mathrm{Ree}(r)|=(r^{3}+1)r^{3}(r-1)=r^{3}(r^{2}-1)(r+\sqrt{3r}+1)(r-\sqrt{3r}+1),

and
\mathrm{Ree}(r)
contains subgroups isomorphic to
\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{r})
,
viz. subgroups of index
2
in centralizers of involutions, see

[
39
, page 62]
. If
p
divides
r^{2}-1
, then

\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{r})
contains a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
\mathrm{Ree}(r)
, and
every element of
\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{r})
is a product of two elements
(transvections) of order
3
by
[
11
, 3.4]
or

[
13
, 2.7]
.

It remains to consider primes
p>3
that divide
r\pm\sqrt{3r}+1
;
this includes the prime divisor
7
of
|\mathrm{Ree}(3)|
. The corresponding
Sylow
p
-subgroups are cyclic, hence all subgroups of order
p

are conjugate, and
\mathrm{Ree}(r)
contains the
Frobenius group
C_{p}\rtimes C_{3}
of order
3p
, see
[
39
, IV.3, page 83]
.
The inclusion
C_{p}\rtimes C_{3}\leq\operatorname{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p})
yields that
every element of order
p
in
C_{p}\rtimes C_{3}
is a
commutator, hence it is the product of two conjugate elements of order
3
.
∎

4.3 Theorem
.

If
G^{\dagger}
is a Ree group then
G=G^{\dagger}
, and the action of
G

on
U\smallsetminus B_{\infty}
is semi-regular.

Proof.

We have

G^{\dagger}=\mathrm{Ree}(r)
with
r=3^{2e+1}\geq 3
, and the unital has order
q=r^{3}
.

We first prove that
G=G^{\dagger}
if
r=3
; then

G^{\dagger}=\mathrm{Ree}(3)\cong\operatorname{P\Gamma L}(2,\mathbb{F}_{8})\cong\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{8})\rtimes C_{3}
.
As in
[
11
, Case (6), p. 429]
, we note that the final
term
D
of the commutator series of
G
is a cover of

\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{8})
, which has no proper cover
(see
[
17
, V.25.7]
or
[
31
]
), hence
D\cong\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{8})
. There exists a translation
\alpha\in G\smallsetminus D
of order
3

such that
\langle\alpha,D\rangle=\langle\alpha\rangle\ltimes D

induces
G^{\dagger}\cong G/G_{[B_{\infty}]}
on
B_{\infty}
, as in

[
11
, Case (6), p. 429]
.
Hence
G
is the direct product of
\langle\alpha\rangle\ltimes D
with
the center
G_{[B_{\infty}]}
of
G
.
Each Sylow
3
-subgroup of
\langle\alpha\rangle\ltimes D
has order
3^{3}

and acts faithfully on
B_{\infty}
, hence it is a full translation group
\Gamma_{[z]}
for
some
z\in B_{\infty}
. There exist at least two such Sylow
3
-subgroups
(as
D
is simple), and together they generate
G
. Hence

G=\langle\alpha\rangle\ltimes D
and
G_{[B_{\infty}]}
is trivial.

For
r>3
, the Ree group
\mathrm{Ree}(r)
is simple and has trivial Schur
multiplier; see
[
3
]
. So
G=G^{\dagger}
holds for
every
r\geq 3
, and the Sylow
3
-subgroups of
G
are the translation groups.
By
4.2
the stabilizer
G_{c}
of a point
c\in U\smallsetminus B_{\infty}

cannot contain any element of prime order, hence
G_{c}
is trivial.
∎

5
Proof of the Main Theorem

Let
\mathbb{U}
be a unital of order
q
with two points which are centers
of translation groups of order
q
. Then the group
G
generated by these two translation groups induces
a Moufang set (as defined in Section
1
) on the
block
B_{\infty}
containing the translation centers
(see
[
11
, 3.1]
, where our present group
G
is
called
\hat{G}
). We have listed the possibilities for the little projective
group
G^{\dagger}
in
1.2
. The group
G^{\dagger}
cannot be a unitary group, see
3.4
. If
G^{\dagger}
is sharply two-transitive then
q\in\{2,3\}
and
\mathbb{U}
is
the hermitian unital of order
q
, see
2.3
; thus

G\cong\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

Now assume that
G^{\dagger}
is isomorphic to
\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q})
but not
sharply two-transitive. Then
q>3
, the group
G^{\dagger}
is simple,
and
G
is a perfect central extension of
G^{\dagger}

by
1.6
. In most cases, the Schur multiplier
of
\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q})
is trivial. In those cases, we have

G\cong\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q})
or
G\cong\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q})
. The
Schur multiplier of
\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q})
is not trivial only if

q\in\{4,9\}
. In these cases, the arguments in
[
11
, p. 428,
(2)]
show that
G\cong\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{4})
if
q=4
and

G\cong\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{9})
or
G\cong\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{9})
if
q=9
. By
[
11
, 3.5]
, the action of
G
on
U\smallsetminus B_{\infty}

is semi-regular (this also applies if
q\leq 3
).

If
G^{\dagger}
is a Suzuki or Ree group then
G=G^{\dagger}
, and the action on

U\smallsetminus B_{\infty}
is semi-regular, see
4.1

and
4.3
. The smallest Suzuki group

\mathrm{Sz}(2)\cong\operatorname{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{5})
is sharply two-transitive, and excluded
by
2.3
.

In each one of the cases discussed above, the order
q
of the unital
turns out to be a prime power (thanks to the restriction
q\in\{2,3\}

in the sharply two-transitive case).

6
Simplifications of a previous paper

The present paper yields some simplifications of the classification
of the unitals admitting all translations in
[
11
]
, as we explain
now. The elimination of the sharply two-transitive groups in
2.3

leaves only Moufang sets which are determined uniquely by the isomorphism
type of their root groups, see
[
11
, 3.3]
. Thus the mapping

g\colon\mathcal{L}_{c}\to\mathbb{N}
considered in
[
11
, page 430]

is constant, and Proposition 4.2 in
[
11
]
is not needed
anymore; the proof of that proposition depends on the classification of the finite
simple groups. By
3.4
one can omit the consideration of
unitary groups.

The classification of the finite simple groups is still involved at
the very end of the proof in
[
11
, page 430]
, when we quote
a result of Kantor’s which uses the classification of finite doubly
transitive groups. If the order
q
of the unital is a power of
2
,
then the classification of finite simple groups can be avoided,
because the doubly transitive groups of degree
q^{3}+1
are classified
in
[
16
, Theorem 2]
; see also
[
29
]
.
