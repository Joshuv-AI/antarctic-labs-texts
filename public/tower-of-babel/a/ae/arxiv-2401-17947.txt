On the minimum spanning tree distribution in grids
Kristopher Tapp

Abstract
. We study the minimum spanning tree distribution on the space of spanning trees of the n n -by- n n grid for large n n . We establish bounds on the decay rates of the probability of the most and the least probable spanning trees as n → ∞ n\rightarrow\infty , and we develop general tools for studying the decay rates of spanning tree families.

Related work

There is a limited body of work relating the MST and UST (uniform spanning tree) distributions. The authors of
[
1
]
show that the expected diameter of a MST
of the complete graph on
n

n^{1/3}
, whereas the expected diameter of a UST is
n^{1/2}
. Chapter 10 of
[
12
]
studies the MST distribution in a setting generalized to infinite graphs. The authors of
[
13
]
derived an explicit formula for
\textnormal{Prob}_{\textnormal{MST}}(T)
. This formula is the basis of most of the results of our paper. Very recently, the authors of
[
3
]
derived a dual version of this formula. We will include both the formula and its dual version in Section 4. The authors of
[
3
]
also provide new tools for studying the MST distribution and generalizations in which the edge weights are not i.i.d but rather are drawn from separate distributions.

Similar to
[
3
]
, one of the motivations of our paper is the application to redistricting. The starting point of modern redistricting algorithms is a tiling of a state into precincts. The tiling’s dual graph
\mathcal{G}
has a vertex for each precinct and an edge between each pair of vertices that correspond to adjacent precincts. A key step in modern sampling algorithms is to draw a random spanning tree on a certain subgraph of
\mathcal{G}
. The methods in
[
15
]
and
[
6
]
use Wilson’s algorithm to draw a UST, whereas the method in
[
7
]
by default uses Kruskal’s algorithm to draw a MST. It was empirically demonstrated in
[
4
]
that MST-based methods result in plans with more compact districts (more specifically, with fewer cut edges). This empirical data has not been fully explained, and is one motivation for this paper.

There is a fairly large body of literature on a question that is perhaps more than tangentially related to ours. The seminal paper
[
2
]
defined the

where
\text{dist}_{T}(e)
denotes the path-length distance in
T
between the two endpoints

(which equals
1
if

). This paper and the follow-up literature focused on minimizing this measurement, with special attention to the case of grid graphs
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
,
[

]
. In fact, the fractal family from Figure

[

as an example of a family with low average stretch.

The relevance is that
\textnormal{Prob}_{\textnormal{MST}}
is negatively correlated with the average stretch of
T
; that is, a tree with lower average stretch tends to have a higher MST probability. Although we are not aware of any rigorous bounds along these lines, Section 4 will mention an analytic reason to expect this correlation, and Figure

For grid graphs, these problems seem harder. Identifying the spanning tree of

3.
The Bipartite Graph

In this section, we introduce a simple construction that naturally associates a bipartite graph to a spanning tree.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected simple undirected graph (not necessarily a grid). Let
T
be a spanning tree of
G
regarded as a subset of
E
. We’ll call the edges of
T

branches
and the edges of
T^{c}=E-T

chords
. For each chord
e\in T^{c}
, let
\cycle(e)
denote the unique cycle in the graph obtained by adding
e
to
T
. For each branch
e\in T
, let
\cut(e)
denote the cut set between the two components
T-\{e\}
; that is
\cut(e)
includes
e
and each chord that’s incident to both components.

We define a bipartite graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V}_{1},\mathcal{V}_{2},\mathcal{E})
associated to the spanning tree
T
as exemplified in Figure
2
. It contains a set
\mathcal{V}_{1}
of nodes correspond to the branches of
T
, and a set
\mathcal{V}_{2}
of nodes corresponding to the chords, with adjacency determined by whether the branch belongs to the cycle associated to the chord. Denote
M=|\mathcal{V}_{1}|=|T|
and
N=|\mathcal{V}_{2}|=|T^{c}|
. Notice that a branch
e\in\mathcal{V}_{1}
is adjacent to all of the chords in
\cut(e)
, while a chord
e\in\mathcal{V}_{2}
is adjacent to all of the branches in
\cycle(e)
. To avoid confusion, we’ll use the terms
vertices
and
edges
when referring to
G
, and the terms
nodes
and
links
when referring to
\mathcal{G}
.

Figure 2.
The bipartite graph
\mathcal{G}
associated to a spanning tree
T\subset G(3)

For
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
d_{i}
denote the average degree in
\mathcal{G}
of the nodes in
\mathcal{V}_{i}
. Notice that average stretch is a linear function of
d_{2}
because Equation
2.1
can be re-written as
\text{avg-stretch}(T)=\frac{M+N\cdot d_{2}}{M+N}
; thus, optimizing average stretch (among spanning trees of a fixed graph) is the same as optimizing
d_{2}
.

On the other hand,
d_{1}+1
equals the expected number of cut edges between the two components obtained by removing a random branch from
T
. This paper focuses on the case where
G=G(n)
for large
n
, in which case
2
2

2

More precisely, any spanning tree
T
of
G(n)
has
M=n^{2}-1
branches and
N=n^{2}-2n+1
chords.

M\approx N
so
d_{1}\approx d_{2}
. In this setting, a tree with small average stretch (small
d_{2}
) will on average form a bipartition with fewer cut edges when a random one of its branches is removed (because
d_{1}
is small).

In the literature on the mathematics of redistricting, it has been observed empirically that a spanning tree drawn from the MST distribution has exactly this advantage compared to a spanning tree drawn uniformly – it tends to form a more compact bipartition when one of its branches is removed; see
[
5
]
. This observation is partially accounted for by the above comments together with the previously mentioned observation that a spanning tree with higher MST probability tends to have a lower average stretch.

4.
An Explicit Formula for
\textnormal{Prob}_{\textnormal{MST}}(T)

In this section, we state and re-interpret the explicit formula for MST probability due to Lyons and Peres
[
13
]
, together with a dual version of the formula from
[
3
]
. We will re-frame both formulas in a way that makes it clear that
\textnormal{Prob}_{\textnormal{MST}}(T)
depends only on the bipartite graph
\mathcal{G}
associated to
T
. In fact, that is the framing under which the duality of the two formulas is best understood.

Exactly as in the previous section, let
G
be a connected graph (not necessarily a grid), let
T
be a spanning tree of
G
, let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V}_{1},\mathcal{V}_{2},\mathcal{E})
be the associated bipartite graph, and define
M=|\mathcal{V}_{1}|=|T|
and
N=|\mathcal{V}_{2}|=|T^{c}|
. Let
S(T)
denote the set of permutations (orders) of
T
.

Definition 4.1
.

For
\alpha=(e_{1},...,e_{M})\in S(T)
and
1\leq i\leq M
, define

P_{i}(\alpha)=|\mathcal{V}_{1}^{i}|+|\mathcal{V}_{2}^{i}|=i+|\mathcal{V}_{2}^{i}|,

where
\mathcal{V}_{1}^{i}=\{e_{1},...,e_{i}\}\subset\mathcal{V}_{1}
, and
\mathcal{V}_{2}^{i}\subset\mathcal{V}_{2}
denotes the set of nodes in
\mathcal{V}_{2}
that are connected by an arc in
\mathcal{G}
to at least one node in
\mathcal{V}_{1}^{i}
.

This definition of
P_{i}(\alpha)
can be illuminated via the following metaphor. The nodes of
\mathcal{V}_{2}
are unlit candles, while the nodes of
\mathcal{V}_{1}
are people who wish to light the candles using a single lighter that they must share. The people take turns in the order
\alpha
. On his/her turn, a person lights each not-yet-lit candle to which he/she is connected by a link in
\mathcal{G}
. Lighting one candle takes one unit of time, and passing the lighter to the next person also takes one unit of time. In this story,
P_{i}(\alpha)
equals the time at which the
i^{\text{th}}
pass of the lighter occurs. We will therefore refer to
(P_{1}(\alpha),...,P_{M}(\alpha))
as the sequence of
passing times
associated with
\alpha
.

Proposition 4.2

(
[
13
]
; see also
[
3
]
)
.

\textnormal{Prob}_{\textnormal{MST}}(T)=M!\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{M}P_{i}(\alpha)}\right)=\sum_{\alpha\in S(T)}\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{M}P_{i}(\alpha)},

where the
\mathbb{E}
denotes the expectation with respect to the uniform distribution on
S(T)
.

Sketch of Proof.

The proof in
[
13
]
(a more complete version of which is provided in
[
3
]
) involves noticing that for a fixed permutation
\alpha=(e_{1},e_{2},...,e_{M})
,

\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{M}P_{i}(\alpha)}=\frac{1}{P_{M}(\alpha)}\cdot\frac{1}{P_{M-1}(\alpha)}\cdots\frac{1}{P_{1}(\alpha)}

equals the probability that the algorithm described in the first paragraph of this paper will yield
T
in the
reverse
order
(e_{M},...,e_{1})
. This is because, if
\{e_{i+1},...,e_{M}\}
are the edges added to the tree so far, then
P_{i}(\alpha)=|\mathcal{V}_{1}^{i}|+|\mathcal{V}_{2}^{i}|
is the number of possibilities for the next edge that wouldn’t be rejected by the algorithm. This is because
|\mathcal{V}_{1}^{i}|
counts the not-yet-used branches, while
|\mathcal{V}_{2}^{i}|
counts the chords that wouldn’t be rejected.
∎

Imagine running Kruskal’s algorithm and recording the number of available choices at each step (edges that wouldn’t be rejected). This is a decreasing list of numbers that starts with
N+M
. The reversal of this list is the sequence of passing times.

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof.

Any spanning tree
T
of
G(n)
has
M=n^{2}-1
branches and
N=n^{2}-2n+1
chords. In particular,
N<M<n^{2}
.

Proposition
4.2
says that
\textnormal{Prob}_{\textnormal{MST}}(T)=M!\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{\prod P_{i}(\alpha)}\right)
, so it will suffice to find lower and upper bounds for
\frac{M!}{\prod P_{i}(\alpha)}
over all
T\in\mathcal{T}(G(n))
and all
\alpha\in S(T)
. For convenience, we’ll instead work with its reciprocal
\frac{\prod P_{i}(\alpha)}{M!}
.

For any
T\in\mathcal{T}(G(n))
and any
\alpha\in S(T)
, the passing times
\{P_{i}(\alpha)\}
form a sequence of
M
strictly increasing integers between
1
and
M+N
. We know that
P_{i}(\alpha)\leq N+i
because the upper extreme possibility for the sequence is
\{N+1,N+2,...,N+M\}
. So Stirling’s approximation gives:

(5.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\prod P_{i}(\alpha)}{M!}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(M+N)!}{N!M!}={M+N\choose M}\leq{2M\choose M}\approx\frac{4^{M}}{\sqrt{\pi M}}<4^{M}<4^{n^{2}},

from which it follows that
\textnormal{Prob}_{\textnormal{MST}}(T)\geq\sim\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{n^{2}}
as desired. Here and throughout this paper, we use “
\approx
” to mean the left side divided by the right side approaches
1
.

Next to prove the other bound, we wish to establish that
2i
is an approximate lower bound for
P_{i}(\alpha)
. For this, consider the forest (acyclic graph)
F_{i}
that has the same vertex set as
G(n)
and has the edge set
\{e_{i+1},e_{i+2},...,e_{M}\}
. This forest has
i+1
components (some of which might be individual vertices). Observe that
P_{i}(\alpha)
equals the number of cut edges of
F_{i}
. So we must prove that a forest with
i+1
components has at least approximately
2i
cut edges. For this, notice that
J=4n
equals the number of “exterior connectors,” which means edges that connect vertices of
G(n)
to vertices of its complement in the infinite lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Every component of
F_{i}
is incident to at least
4
“generalized cut edges,” which means cut edges or exterior connectors, namely at its top, bottom, left, and right-most vertices, so
4(i+1)\leq 2P_{i}(\alpha)+J
, or
P_{i}(\alpha)\geq 2(i+1)-\frac{1}{2}J
.

Since
\frac{J}{2}=2n
, we learn that
P_{i}(\alpha)\geq 2(i+1)-2n
for all
i\in\{1,2,...,M\}.

This gives no information about the indices
i\in\{1,2,...,n-1\}
, but applying the result to the remaining indices yields

\prod P_{i}(\alpha)\geq\prod_{i\geq n}P_{i}(\alpha)\geq 2\cdot 4\cdot 6\cdots(2M-2n).

We therefore have the following.

\frac{\prod P_{i}(\alpha)}{M!}\geq 2^{\left(M-n\right)}\cdot\frac{\left(M-n\right)!}{M!}\geq\frac{2^{\left(M-n\right)}}{M^{n}}.

It follows that

\textnormal{Prob}_{\textnormal{MST}}(T)=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{M!}{\prod P_{i}(\alpha)}\right)\leq\frac{M^{n}}{2^{(M-n)}}=\frac{\left(n^{2}-1\right)^{n}}{2^{(n^{2}-1-n)}}\sim\frac{1}{2^{n^{2}}},

so
\textnormal{Prob}_{\textnormal{MST}}(T)\leq\sim\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n^{2}}
.
∎

6.
Statement of main result

The next three sections will develop machinery that, among other things, will yield a lower bound on the decay base of certain types of families of spanning trees. In this section, we preview these upcoming sections by stating and roughly explaining this bound, which we already mentioned informally in Equation
1.1
. We begin by defining the additional hypotheses that will be needed for our spanning tree families.

Definition 6.1
.

The family
\mathcal{F}=\{T_{n}\subset G(n)\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is
bounded
if the average degree (among vertices in the bipartite graph associated to
T_{n}
) divided by
n^{2}
goes to zero as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

For example, the fractal family satisfies a much stronger hypothesis than bounded; it was proven in
[
2
]
that the average degree grows as
C\cdot\ln(n)
for this family. The centipede family is also bounded, but the double spiral family is not.

Definition 6.2
.

The
degree-mass
function of a spanning tree
T
is the function
p:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow[0,1]
defined so that for all
d\geq 1
,
p(d)
equals the portion of the nodes in
\mathcal{V}_{2}
that have degree
d
(in the bipartite graph associated with
T
).

For a grid graph, the support of the degree-mass function
p
includes only odd numbers that are
\geq 3
. This is because the length of every cycle of
G(n)
is an even number that is
\geq 4
.

Definition 6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{T_{n}\subset G(n)\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
be a family of spanning trees. Let
p_{n}
denote the degree-mass function for
T_{n}
. We call
\mathcal{F}

convergent
if for each
d
,
p_{\infty}(d)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}p_{n}(d)
exists. In this case, the
power series associated to

\mathcal{F}
is the function
f:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined as follows.

f(x)=1+x-\sum_{d\geq 3}p_{\infty}(d)\cdot(1-x)^{d}.

The centipede, double spiral and fractal families are all convergent. For each of these three families, we’ll compute (at least a truncation of) the associated associate power series in Section 10. An example of a non-convergent family can be obtained by alternating between members of two different families, like say the centipede and fractal families.

We will require one more hypothesis, which is technical but is also fairly weak.

Definition 6.4
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{T_{n}\subset G(n)\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
be a family of spanning trees. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, denote
\Delta_{n}=\mathcal{V}_{2}\times\mathcal{V}_{2}
(in the bipartite graph associated to
T_{n}
), and let
\tilde{\Delta}_{n}
be the set of pairs
(v,w)\in\Delta_{n}
that have at least one common neighbor in
\mathcal{V}_{1}
. The family
\mathcal{F}
is called
neighbor-connected
if either

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|\tilde{\Delta}_{n}|}{|\Delta_{n}|}=0,\text{ or }\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\text{avg}\left\{\text{max}\{d_{v},d_{w}\}\mid(v,w)\in\tilde{\Delta}_{n}\right\}=\infty,

where
d_{v}
and
d_{w}
denote the degrees of
v
and
w
.

We will show in Section 10 that the fractal, centipede and double spiral families are all neighbor-connected. The fractal family is neighbor-connected because
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|\tilde{\Delta}_{n}|}{|\Delta_{n}|}=0
, owing to the family’s small average stretch growth. On the other hand, for a family with
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|\tilde{\Delta}_{n}|}{|\Delta_{n}|}>0
, the second condition appears weak. According to results from
[
2
]
, the average degree in
\mathcal{V}_{2}
goes to infinity, so it is not much to ask that the same is true for the average degree among connected neighbor pairs.

One purposes of the next three sections is to prove the following.

Theorem 6.5
.

If
\mathcal{F}=\{T_{n}\subset G(n)\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a bounded, convergent, neighbor-connected family, and
f
is its associated power series, then its decay base is bounded as

Q(\mathcal{F})\geq\frac{1}{e\cdot\overline{f}},

where
\overline{f}=exp\left(\int_{0}^{1}\ln f(x)\,\text{dx}\right)
is the geometric mean of
f
.

The basic idea will be to show that
f
is the limit of the passing time sequences. More precisely, for each
n
one could uniformly choose
\alpha\in S(T_{n})
and then plot the sequence
\left\{\left(\frac{i}{M},\frac{P_{i}(\alpha)}{M}\right)\right\}
, where
M=n^{2}-1=|T_{n}|
. The reason for dividing by
M
is to scale the plot from
[1,M]\times[1,2M]
down to
[0,1]\times[0,2]
so that these plots can be compared across different values of
n
. In fact as
n\rightarrow\infty
, we’ll show essentially that these plots look more and more like the graph of
f
with higher and higher probability. This intuition makes Theorem
6.5
plausible, since Proposition
4.2
can be used to relate
Q(\mathcal{F})
to the geometric mean of the passing times.

7.
Approximate passing times

In this section, we let
T\subset\mathcal{T}(G(n))
and we derive an exact and an approximate formula for the expected value of an individual passing time. Most of the results will also hold for not-necessarily-grid graphs. Consider all running notation from Sections 3 and 4 to be defined with respect to
T
. In particular,
p
denotes the degree mass function of
T
.

Proposition 7.1
.

For each
i\in\{1,2,...,M\}
we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(P_{i}(\alpha))

\displaystyle=i+N\left(1-\sum_{d\geq 3}p(d)\cdot\frac{{M-d\choose i}}{{M\choose i}}\right),

where
\mathbb{E}
denotes the expectation with respect to a uniform choice of
\alpha\in S(T)
. Here the convention is that
{M-d\choose i}=0
when
i>M-d
.

Proof.

Fix
i\in\{1,2,...,M\}
. For each node
v\in\mathcal{V}_{2}
, let
d(v)
denote the degree of
v
, and let
\phi(v)
denote the probability (with respect to a uniform choice of
\alpha
) that
v\in\mathcal{V}_{2}^{i}
. Note that
\phi(v)
equals the probability that a random selection
without replacement
of
i
of the nodes of
\mathcal{V}_{1}
includes at least one of the
d(v)
nodes which are adjacent to
v
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(P_{i}(\alpha))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(i+|\mathcal{V}_{2}^{i}|\right)

\displaystyle=i+\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{2}}\phi(v)=i+\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{2}}\left(1-\frac{{M-d(v)\choose i}}{{M\choose i}}\right)

\displaystyle=i+\sum_{d\geq 3}p(d)\cdot N\cdot\left(1-\frac{{M-d\choose i}}{{M\choose i}}\right),

which simplifies to the claimed result.
∎

The formula in Proposition
7.1
is difficult to manage, so we next seek to approximate this formula, at least for
bounded
families. For this, we will define a sequence
\{\tilde{P}_{1},\tilde{P}_{2},...,\tilde{P}_{M}\}
, called the
approximate passing times
, such that
\tilde{P}_{i}
approximates the formula for
\mathbb{E}(P_{i}(\alpha))
in Proposition
7.1
. The formula comes essentially by using a selection
with
(rather than
without
) replacement in the previous proof.

Definition 7.2
.

For each
i\in\{1,2,...,M\}
, the
i^{\text{th}}
approximate passing time is

\tilde{P}_{i}=i+N\left(1-\sum_{d\geq 3}p(d)\left(\frac{M-i}{M}\right)^{d}\right).

For the next proposition, we will scale the actual and approximate passing times so they can be meaningfully compared across a family of spanning trees. For every
i
and every
\alpha
,
P_{i}(\alpha)\leq M+N<2M
, so the
scaled passing time

P_{i}(\alpha)/M
lies in
(0,2)
. Similarly, the
scaled approximate passing time

\tilde{P}_{i}/M
lies in
(0,2)
.

Proposition 7.3
.

For each
i\in\{1,2,...,M\}
,

\mathbb{E}(P_{i}(\alpha))\geq\tilde{P}_{i}.

Moreover,
if
\mathcal{F}=\{T_{n}\subset G(n)\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a bounded family of spanning trees, then for every
x\in(0,1)
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{P_{\lfloor xM_{n}\rfloor}(\alpha)}{M_{n}}\right)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{\tilde{P}_{\lfloor xM_{n}\rfloor}}{M_{n}}\right),

provided the limit on the right exists, where
M_{n}=n^{2}-1=|T_{n}|
.

Here
\lfloor\rfloor
denotes the floor function. If say
x=.15
, then the proposition considers, for each spanning tree in the family, the actual and approximate scaled passing time that is about
15\%
through the sequence.

Proof.

Let
x\in(0,1)
. Consider a fixed large value of
n
, and interpret all running notation with respect to the spanning tree
T=T_{n}
; in particular,
M_{n}
will be written simply as
M
, and
p
will denote the degree mass function of
T
. Set
i={\lfloor xM\rfloor}
.

Comparing the formulas from Proposition
7.1
and Definition
7.2
, we see that it will suffice to prove that the following “error” becomes arbitrarily small as
n\rightarrow\infty
:

(7.1)

E=\sum_{d\geq 3}p(d)\cdot\left|\left(\frac{M-i}{M}\right)^{d}-\frac{{M-d\choose i}}{{M\choose i}}\right|.

Let
\epsilon>0
. Let
\mu
denote the average degree in
\mathcal{V}_{2}
. Markov’s inequality gives that

\sum_{d\geq\frac{2\mu}{\epsilon}}p(d)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}
. Thus,

E\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\sum_{d<\frac{2\mu}{\epsilon}}p(d)\cdot\left|\left(\frac{M-i}{M}\right)^{d}-\frac{{M-d\choose i}}{{M\choose i}}\right|.

Since
\mathcal{F}
is bounded, we can assume that
n
is chosen so that
\mu
is arbitrarily small relative to
\min\{i,M-i\}
. In fact,
n
can be chosen so that each value of
d
for which
d\leq\frac{2}{\epsilon}\cdot\mu
is arbitrarily small relative to
\min\{i,M-i\}
.

But for values of
d
that are small relative to
\min\{i,M-i\}
, there is small error to the natural approximation of the probability, if an urn contains
M
balls of which
d
are red, that a selection of
i
of them includes no red balls:

\displaystyle\frac{{M-d\choose i}}{{M\choose i}}=\left(\frac{M-i}{M}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{M-i-1}{M-1}\right)\cdots\left(\frac{M-i-d}{M-d}\right)\approx\left(\frac{M-i}{M}\right)^{d}.

The previous equation would also remains true if the final “
\approx
” were replaced by “
\leq
”, which justifies the assertion that
\mathbb{E}(P_{i}(\alpha))\geq\tilde{P}_{i}.

∎

8.
The power series of a family of spanning trees

Proposition
7.3
allows us to approximate the expected values of individual passing times. The proposition ends with the caveat “provided the limit on the right exists.” In this section, we show that for a
convergent
family, this limit not only exists but is given by a simple formula, and we can also establish a bound on the
variance
of an individual passing time, denoted
\text{Var}(P_{i}(\alpha))
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is a convergent family and
f
is its associated power series (as in Definition
6.3
), it is straightforward to show that
\sum p_{\infty}(d)\leq 1
, but we’ll later see examples where this sum is strictly less than
1
, so
p_{\infty}
is not necessarily a valid probability function. Notice that
f^{\prime}(x)\geq 1
for all
x\in[0,1]
. Moreover,
f(0)\in[0,1]
and
f(1)=2
. If
p_{\infty}
is a valid probability function, then
f(0)=0
.

Proposition 8.1
.

If
\mathcal{F}=\{T_{n}\subset G(n)\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a convergent family and
f
is its associated power series, then for each
x\in(0,1]
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{\tilde{P}_{\lfloor xM_{n}\rfloor}}{M_{n}}\right)=f(x),

where
M_{n}=n^{2}-1=|T_{n}|
.

Proof.

Let
x\in(0,1]
. Consider a fixed large value of
n
, and interpret all running notation with respect to the spanning tree
T=T_{n}
. Set
i=\lfloor xM\rfloor
so that
\frac{M-i}{M}\approx 1-x
.

The tail
\sum_{d\geq D}p_{\infty}(d)
can be made arbitrarily small by choosing
D
large. Furthermore,
n
can be chosen large enough so that
p(d)
is arbitrarily close to
p_{\infty}(d)
for all
d\leq D
, where
p
denotes the degree mass function of
T=T_{n}
. In other words, the degree mass function of
T
becomes arbitrarily close to
p_{\infty}
where it matters. This observation and the fact that
M\approx N
give the following.

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{i}

\displaystyle\approx i+M\left(1-\sum_{d\geq 3}p_{\infty}(d)\left(\frac{M-i}{M}\right)^{d}\right)

\displaystyle=M\cdot\left(1+\frac{i}{M}-\sum_{d\geq 3}p_{\infty}(d)\left(\frac{M-i}{M}\right)^{d}\right)\approx M\cdot f\left(\frac{i}{M}\right)\approx M\cdot f(x).

∎

The proposition essentially says that the sequence
\{f(i/M)\mid i=1,2,...,M\}
approximates the scaled approximate passing times
\{\tilde{P}_{i}/M\mid i=1,2,...,M\}
.

Lemma 8.2
.

If
\mathcal{F}=\{T_{n}\subset G(n)\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a bounded, convergent, neighbor-connected family, then for every
x\in(0,1]
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{P_{\lfloor xM_{n}\rfloor}(\alpha)}{M_{n}}\right)=f(x),\text{ and }\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\text{Var}\left(\frac{P_{\lfloor xM_{n}\rfloor}(\alpha)}{M_{n}}\right)=0,

where
M_{n}=n^{2}-1=|T_{n}|
, and
f
is the power series associated to
\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

The first equation just combines Proposition
7.3
and Proposition
8.1
.

For the second equation, consider a fixed large value of
n
, an interpret all running notation with respect to the spanning tree
T=T_{n}
. Set
i=\lfloor xM\rfloor
.

If
\alpha\in S(T)
is uniformly selected, then
\mathcal{V}_{1}^{i}=\{e_{1},...,e_{i}\}\subset\mathcal{V}_{1}
is a random sample of
\mathcal{V}_{1}
. According to Equation
,
P_{i}(\alpha)=i+|\mathcal{V}_{2}^{i}|
, so it will suffice to bound the variance of
\frac{|\mathcal{V}_{2}^{i}|}{M}
. For this, we write

|\mathcal{V}_{2}^{i}|=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{2}}Y_{v},

where
Y_{v}
is the indicator random variable; that is,
Y_{v}\in\{0,1\}
depending on whether
v\in\mathcal{V}_{2}^{i}
.

Let
v,w\in\mathcal{V}_{2}
and let
d_{v},d_{w}
denote their degrees. Assume for the moment that these degrees are small relative to
M
. In this case,
Y_{v}
is well approximated by the Bernoulli random variable with success probability
1-x^{d_{v}}
, so
\mathbb{E}(Y_{v})\approx 1-x^{d_{v}}
and
\text{Var}(Y_{v})\approx x^{d_{v}}\left(1-x^{d_{v}}\right)
, and similarly for
Y_{w}
.

Let
d_{vw}
denote the number of neighbors in
\mathcal{V}_{1}
shared by
v
and
w
. The product
Y_{v}\cdot Y_{w}
is well approximated by the Bernoulli random variable with success probability
1-x^{d_{v}}-x^{d_{w}}+x^{d_{v}+d_{w}-d_{vw}}
. This gives

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(Y_{v},Y_{w})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(Y_{v}\cdot Y_{w})-\mathbb{E}(Y_{v})\cdot\mathbb{E}(Y_{w})

\displaystyle\approx x^{d_{v}+d_{w}-d_{vw}}-x^{d_{v}+d_{w}}

\displaystyle\leq x^{d_{v}+d_{w}-d_{vw}}\leq x^{\max\{d_{v},d_{w}\}}

because
d_{vw}\leq\min\{d_{v},d_{w}\}
. In particular, the second line of the above equation implies that
\text{Cov}(Y_{v},Y_{w})\approx 0
if
d_{vw}=0
; that is, if
v
and
w
have no common neighbors. This approximation assumes that the degrees are small relative to
M
so that the Bernoulli approximations are appropriate, but even without that assumption we have

\text{If }d_{vw}=0,\text{ then }\text{Cov}(Y_{v},Y_{w})\leq 0.

The knowledge that
Y_{v}=1
makes it slightly less likely that
Y_{w}=1
.

We’ll write
v\sim w
to mean that
d_{vw}>0
; that is,
v
and
w
have at least one common neighbor. We have

\displaystyle\text{Var}\left(|\mathcal{V}_{2}^{i}|\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v,w\in\mathcal{V}_{2}}\text{Cov}(Y_{v},Y_{w})\leq\sum_{v\sim w}\text{Cov}(Y_{v},Y_{w})\leq\approx\sum_{v\sim w}x^{\max\{d_{v},d_{w}\}}.

Define
\Delta=\mathcal{V}_{2}\times\mathcal{V}_{2}
and
\tilde{\Delta}=\{(v,w)\in\Delta\mid v\sim w\}
, as in Definition
6.4
.

\displaystyle\text{Var}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{V}_{2}^{i}|}{M}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{Var}\left(|\mathcal{V}_{2}^{i}|\right)}{M^{2}}\leq\approx\frac{\sum_{v\sim w}x^{\max\{d_{v},d_{w}\}}}{M^{2}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{|\tilde{\Delta}|}{|\Delta|}\cdot\text{avg}\{x^{\max\{d_{v},d_{w}\}}\mid v\sim w\},

which becomes arbitrarily small because
\mathcal{F}
is neighbor-connected.

All of the approximations above come from approximating the indicator variables by Bernoulli variables, which is only accurate for pairs
v,w\in\mathcal{V}_{2}
that have small degree relative to
M
. So to justify the seemingly unwarranted use of the symbol “
\approx
” in the above equations, we consider two cases. First, in the case that
\frac{|\tilde{\Delta}|}{|\Delta|}\rightarrow 0
, we get the desired conclusion using only that each covariance term is bounded above by
1
, without requiring these Bernoulli approximations.

Second, in the case that
\lim\frac{|\tilde{\Delta}|}{|\Delta|}>0
, we use the hypothesis that
\mathcal{F}
is bounded. We can choose
M
large enough that the average degree in
\mathcal{V}_{2}
is arbitrarily small relative to
M
. Since
\lim\frac{|\tilde{\Delta}|}{|\Delta|}>0
, this implies that the average value of
\max\{d_{v},d_{w}\}
among pairs for which
v\sim w
is arbitrarily small relative to
M
. Markov’s inequality, together with the fact that the covariance is always bounded by
1
, can be used to show that the terms for which the Bernoulli approximation isn’t justified make an arbitrarily small contribution to the above calculations.
∎

9.
The geometric mean of the scaled passing times

In this section, we define and study the following measurement, which is the geometric mean of the scaled passing times.

Definition 9.1
.

For
T\subset\mathcal{T}(G(n))
,

\mathcal{A}(T)=\frac{1}{M}\left(\prod P_{i}(\alpha)\right)^{\frac{1}{M}}=\left(\prod\frac{P_{i}(\alpha)}{M}\right)^{\frac{1}{M}},

which is a random variable with respect to a uniform choice of
\alpha\in S(T)
.

The support of
\mathcal{A}(T)
lies in
[0,2]
because
P_{i}(\alpha)/M\in[0,2]
for each
i
and each
\alpha
.

Proposition 9.2
.

If
\mathcal{F}=\{T_{n}\subset G(n)\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a family of spanning trees, then

Q(\mathcal{F})\geq\frac{1}{e\cdot\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(\mathcal{A}(T_{n})\right)\right)}.

In other words,

\textnormal{Prob}_{\textnormal{MST}}(T_{n})\geq\sim\left(e\cdot\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{A}(T_{n}))\right)^{-n^{2}}.

Proof.

Consider a fixed large value of
n
, an interpret all running notation with respect to the spanning tree
T=T_{n}
. Proposition
4.2
together with Stirling’s approximation and Jensen’s inequality yields:

\displaystyle\textnormal{Prob}_{\textnormal{MST}}(T)

\displaystyle=M!\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{\prod P_{i}(\alpha)}\right)\sim\left(\frac{M}{e}\right)^{M}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{\prod P_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-M}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{\prod\frac{P_{i}}{M}}\right)=e^{-M}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(\mathcal{A}(T)^{-M}\right)

\displaystyle\geq e^{-M}\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{A}(T))\right)^{-M}\sim\left(e\cdot\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{A}(T))\right)^{-n^{2}}.

∎

To interpret the following proposition, recall that the
geometric mean
of a function
f:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
is defined as

\overline{f}=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{1}\ln f(x)\,\text{dx}\right).

This is because a Riemann sum for
\int_{0}^{1}\ln f(x)\,\text{dx}
is the log of the geometric mean of the sample values.

Proposition 9.3
.

If
\mathcal{F}=\{T_{n}\subset G(n)\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a bounded convergent family, and
f
is its associated power series, then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\mathcal{A}(T_{n})\right)=\overline{f}.

Proof.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and each
\alpha\in S(T_{n})
, let
g_{n}^{\alpha}:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,2]
denote the piecewise linear function whose graph connects the following points:

\left\{\left(0,0\right),\left(\frac{1}{M},\frac{P_{1}(\alpha)}{M}\right),\left(\frac{2}{M},\frac{P_{2}(\alpha)}{M}\right),...,\left(1,\frac{P_{M}(\alpha)}{M}\right)\right\},

where
M=|T_{n}|
as usual.

Notice that for every
x\in[0,1]
,

(9.1)

g_{n}^{\alpha}(x)\geq x

We claim that for every
\epsilon>0
there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that if
n>n_{0}
then

(9.2)

\max\{|g_{n}^{\alpha}(x)-f(x)|\mid\epsilon\leq x\leq 1\}<\epsilon\text{ with probability }>1-\epsilon,

where “probability” means with respect to a uniform choice of
\alpha\in S(T_{n})
.
That is, with high probability, the graph of
g_{n}^{\alpha}
lies within small tubular neighborhood of the graph of
f
over most of its domain.

To prove this, let
\epsilon>0
. Define
C=\max\{f^{\prime}(x)\mid 0\leq x\leq 1\}
and choose
L\in\mathbb{N}
with
L>\frac{5C}{\epsilon}
. For each
j\in\{1,2,...,L\}
define
x_{j}=\frac{j}{L}
, so that the points
\{x_{j}\}
are equally spaced from
0
to
1
with gaps
<\frac{\epsilon}{5C}
.

For a fixed
j
, applying Lemma
8.2
to
x_{j}
establishes that there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n>n_{0}
,

(9.3)

|g_{n}^{\alpha}(x_{j})-f(x_{j})|<\frac{\epsilon}{5}\text{ with probability }>1-\epsilon.

In fact, it is straightforward to show that there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n>n_{0}
, Equation
9.3
is true
for all

j\in\{1,2,...,L\}
.

But if
|g_{n}^{\alpha}(x_{j})-f(x_{j})|<\frac{\epsilon}{5}
for all
j\in\{1,2,...,L\}
, then we claim that
|g_{n}^{\alpha}(x)-f(x)|<\epsilon
for all
x\in[\epsilon,1]
.

To see this, let
x\in[\epsilon,1]
. Since
x\geq\epsilon
, we know
x>x_{1}
, so it is possible to choose an index
j
such that
x_{j}<x<x_{j+1}
. By the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle|g_{n}^{\alpha}(x)-f(x)|

\displaystyle\leq|f(x)-f(x_{j})|+|f(x_{j})-g_{n}^{\alpha}(x_{j})|+|g_{n}^{\alpha}(x_{j})-g_{n}^{\alpha}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{5}+\frac{\epsilon}{5}+\frac{3\epsilon}{5}=\epsilon.

To justify the claim here that
|g_{n}^{\alpha}(x_{j})-g_{n}^{\alpha}(x)|<\frac{3\epsilon}{5}
, we use the fact that
g_{n}^{\alpha}
is increasing, so

\displaystyle|g_{n}^{\alpha}(x_{j})-g_{n}^{\alpha}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq|g_{n}^{\alpha}(x_{j})-g_{n}^{\alpha}(x_{j+1})|

\displaystyle\leq|g_{n}^{\alpha}(x_{j})-f(x_{j})|+|f(x_{j})-f(x_{j+1})|+|f(x_{j+1})-g_{n}^{\alpha}(x_{j+1})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{5}+\frac{\epsilon}{5}+\frac{\epsilon}{5}=\frac{3\epsilon}{5}.

This completes the proof of Equation
9.2
.

In summary, for any
\epsilon>0
, we can choose
n
sufficiently large so that the probability is
\geq 1-\epsilon
that the graph of
g_{n}^{\alpha}
lies within an
\epsilon
tubular neighborhood of the graph of
f
on
[\epsilon,1]
. If this event occurs then we have the following.

\displaystyle\ln\left(\mathcal{A}(T_{n})(\alpha)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum\ln\left(\frac{P_{i}(\alpha)}{M}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i<\epsilon M}\ln\left(\frac{P_{i}(\alpha)}{M}\right)+\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i\geq\epsilon M}\ln\left(\frac{P_{i}(\alpha)}{M}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\ln(2)+\int_{\epsilon}^{1}\ln(f(x)+\epsilon)\,\text{dx}

\displaystyle=\epsilon\ln(2)+\epsilon(1-\epsilon)+\int_{\epsilon}^{1}\ln(f(x))\,\text{dx}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\ln(2)+\epsilon(1-\epsilon)+\int_{0}^{1}\ln(f(x))\,\text{dx}-\int_{0}^{\epsilon}\ln(x)\,\text{dx}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\epsilon(\ln(2)-\ln(\epsilon))-\epsilon^{2}}_{\text{denoted }\psi(\epsilon)}+\int_{0}^{1}\ln(f(x))\,\text{dx},

The first inequality above uses the fact that each
\frac{P_{i}(\alpha)}{M}\leq 2
, while the second uses the fact that
f(x)\geq x
.

We therefore have the following.

\mathcal{A}(T_{n})(\alpha)\leq e^{\psi(\epsilon)}\cdot\overline{f}.

Since this occurs with probability
\geq(1-\epsilon)
, and since the support of
\mathcal{A}
is contained in
[0,2]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{A}(T_{n}))

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\cdot 2+(1-\epsilon)\cdot e^{\psi(\epsilon)}\cdot\overline{f}

Since
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\psi(\epsilon)=0
, this suffices to prove that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\mathcal{A}(T_{n})\right)\leq\overline{f}.

The corresponding upper bound is proven analogously, which requires Equation
9.1
and the fact that
f(x)\leq 2
.

∎

Combining Proposition
9.2
and Proposition
9.3
proves Theorem
6.5
, which we restate as follows.

Proposition 9.4
.

If
\mathcal{F}=\{T_{n}\subset G(n)\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a bounded, convergent, neighbor-connected family, and
f
is its associated power series, then

Q(\mathcal{F})\geq\frac{1}{e\cdot\overline{f}}.

In other words,

\textnormal{Prob}_{\textnormal{MST}}(T_{n})\geq\sim\left(e\cdot\overline{f}\right)^{-n^{2}}.

10.
Example families of spanning trees

In this section, we study some families of spanning trees, including the families pictured in Figure
1
.

Figure 4.
The bipartite graph of the centipede
T_{4}\subset G(4)
.

Example 10.1

(The Centipede Family)
.

We first study the centipede family
\mathcal{F}=\{T_{n}\subset G(n)\mid n\geq 3\}
. The bipartite graph of
T_{4}
is shown in Figure
4
. The degree-mass function
p_{n}
of the centipede
T_{n}
is particularly simple:

(10.1)

p_{n}(d)=\frac{1}{n-1}\text{ for each }d\in\{3,5,...,2n-1\}.

Therefore for each
d\geq 3
,
p_{\infty}(d)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}p_{n}(d)=0
, so
\mathcal{F}
is convergent and its power series is

f(x)=1+x.

This power series encodes the fact that as
n\rightarrow\infty
, the approximate passing times for
T_{n}
look more and more like the extreme case considered in the proof of the lower bound of Theorem
1.1
; namely
\{\tilde{P}_{i}\}\approx\{N,N+1,...,N+M\}
.

The centipede family is bounded because Equation
10.1
says that the maximum degree in
\mathcal{V}_{2}
equals
2n-1
, which become arbitrarily small relative to
n^{2}
. The centipede family is also neighbor-connected, as can be seen from the structure of the bipartite graph exemplified in Figure
4
. A pair of nodes of
\mathcal{V}_{2}
share a neighbor if and only if the corresponding chords are in the same or adjacent rows. Such pairs represent the full range of degrees in
\mathcal{V}_{2}
, so their degrees grow without bound.

Proposition
9.4
only provides the following old information.

Q(\mathcal{F})\geq\frac{1}{e\cdot\overline{f}}=\frac{1}{4}.

We do not know whether
Q(\mathcal{F})=\frac{1}{4}
.

Example 10.2

(The Double Spiral Family)
.

The double spiral family in Figure
1
is convergent and has the same power series as the centipede, namely
f(x)=1+x
. This family is not bounded because the average degree grows proportional to
M
. In fact, the family is designed to have high average degree growth, and hence be a good candidate for having the minimum possible decay base.

Example 10.3

(The Fractal Family)
.

We next study the fractal family
\mathcal{F}=\{F_{k}\subset G(2^{k})\mid k=1,2,...\}
. It is bounded because the average degree of nodes in the bipartite graph of
F_{k}\subset G(2^{k})
grows logarithmically in
n=2^{k}
, as shown in
[
2
]
.

Let
p_{k}
denote the degree-mass function for
F_{k}
. Unlike the mass functions for the centipede family, these mass functions converge to non-zero limits.

Lemma 10.4
.

For each
d\geq 3
, the sequence
\{p_{k}(d)\}
converges to a limit, which we denote as
p_{\infty}(d)
, and
\sum_{k\geq 3}p_{\infty}(k)=1
.

Proof.

Let
q_{k}(d)
denote the
number
of degree
d
nodes in
\mathcal{V}_{2}
, so

(10.2)

p_{k}(d)=\frac{q_{k}(d)}{|\mathcal{V}_{2}|}=\frac{q_{k}(d)}{2^{2k}-2^{k+1}+1}.

Fix a value
d\geq 3
. Assume it’s not the case that
q_{k}(d)=0
for all
k
. We claim there exists an index
k_{0}
beyond which
\{q_{k}(d)\}
satisfies a simple first order linear recurrence relation with base
4
; that is:

q_{k+1}(d)=4\cdot q_{k}(d)+C\,\,\,\text{ for all }k\geq k_{0}.

The solution is:

q_{k_{0}+t}=\left(A+\frac{C}{3}\right)4^{t}-\frac{C}{3}\,\,\,\text{ for all }t\geq 0,

where
A=q_{k_{0}}(d)
.
Therefore Equation
10.2
gives:

(10.3)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}p_{k}(d)=\frac{A+\frac{C}{3}}{4^{k_{0}}}.

To justify the recurrence relation, notice that each branch of
F_{k+1}
either belongs to one of the four copies of
F_{k}
out of which
F_{k+1}
is built, or is a “cut branch” that connects two of these quadrants. Thus,

q_{k+1}(d)=4\cdot q_{k}(d)+C_{k+1}(d),

where
C_{k+1}(d)
is the number of cut branches with degree
d
. We claim that for sufficiently large
k
,
C_{k+1}(d)
doesn’t depend on
k
. To see this, consider the list of degrees of nodes in the set
\mathcal{V}_{2}
from
F_{k+1}
corresponding to cut branches. This list is naturally partitioned into two halves of equal length. The inner half is exactly the list from
F_{k}
, whereas the minimal degree for the outer half goes to infinity as
k\rightarrow\infty
.

From the above calculations, one can prove that the convergence is uniform: for every
\epsilon>0
there exists
d_{0}
such that
\sum_{d\geq d_{0}}p_{k}(d)<\epsilon
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. From this it is straightforward to conclude that
\sum_{d\geq 1}p_{\infty}(d)=1
.
∎

Table
1
reports the value
p_{\infty}(d)
for all
3\leq d\leq 125
, which we computed using Equation
10.3
and a computer.

The Fractal family
\mathcal{F}
is neighbor-connected for the following reason. Define
\Delta_{k}=\mathcal{V}_{2}\times\mathcal{V}_{2}
(in the bipartite graph associated to
T_{k}
), and let
\tilde{\Delta}_{k}
be the set of pairs
(v,w)\in\Delta_{k}
that have at least one common neighbor in
\mathcal{V}_{1}
. Due to the recursive definition of the family,

\frac{|\tilde{\Delta}_{k}|}{|\Delta_{k}|}\approx\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{|\tilde{\Delta}_{k-1}|}{|\Delta_{k-1}|}.

This is because chords from different quadrants have no common neighbors, and the contribution of the cut edges between the quadrants becomes negligible as
k\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore
\frac{|\tilde{\Delta}_{k}|}{|\Delta_{k}|}\rightarrow 0
.

Example 10.5

(The Uniform Family)
.

Consider a family
\mathcal{F}=\{T_{n}\subset G(n)\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
where each spanning tree
T_{n}
is uniformly randomly chosen. We’ll call
\mathcal{F}
a
uniform family
. Let
p_{n}
denote the degree-mass function of
T_{n}
. Although the randomness means that
T_{n}
and
p_{n}
are not uniquely determined, the limit
p_{\infty}=\lim(p_{n})
is uniquely determined with probability
1
because it can be interpreted as the degree-mass function for a uniformly random spanning tree of the infinite lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Manna et al. studied this situation in
[
14
]
and proved that
p_{\infty}(3)\approx 0.29454
and
p_{\infty}(5)\approx 0.12409
(these are decimal approximations of the exact values that they calculated). The also provided strong numerical and theoretical evidence for their conjecture that
d\mapsto p_{\infty}(d)
decays as
d^{-8/5}
for large
d
.

This conjecture indicates that the following is a reasonable approximation of the power series
f
of
\mathcal{F}
:

f(x)\approx 2-x-(0.29454)x^{3}-(0.12409)x^{5}-C\cdot\sum_{d\geq 7\text{ odd }}d^{-8/5},

where
C
is chosen to make it a valid probability mass function. This yields:

(10.4)

e\cdot\overline{f}=3.433\cdots.

This is higher than the corresponding value for the fractal family, which is evidence that the fractal family has higher MST probability than does a uniform family, although we do not know whether a uniform family is necessarily bounded or neighbor-connected with probability one, or whether all uniform families necessarily have the same decay base.

Observe that a uniform family behaves qualitatively like fractals, not like centipedes, in the sense that
p_{\infty}
is a valid probability function.

11.
Questions and Conjectures

A natural open question is to identifying the optimal upper and lower bounds in Theorem
1.1
, which will require the construction a family of spanning trees that achieves each bound. A potential starting point for addressing the lower bound is to answer the following.

Question 11.1
.

Is the decay base of the double spiral family equal to
\frac{1}{4}
?

The fractal family
\mathcal{F}
might not achieve the optimal upper bound, but it is reasonable to expect it to come close. This family was originally believed to be optimal for the related problem of minimizing average stretch, but families were later constructed that were slightly better
[
9
]
,
[
11
]
. Proposition
9.4
only provided a lower bound for the decay base of the fractal family, but this bound isn’t sharp, so the following question remains.

Question 11.2
.

What is the decay base of the fractal family?

One way to address this is to quantify how close the inequality in Proposition
9.4
comes to being an equality. Here is a concise overview of the proof of Proposition
9.4
.

\displaystyle\textnormal{Prob}_{\textnormal{MST}}(T_{n})

\displaystyle\sim e^{-M}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(\mathcal{A}(T_{n})^{-M}\right)\geq e^{-M}\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{A}(T_{n}))\right)^{-M}

\displaystyle\sim e^{-M}\cdot\overline{f}^{-M}\sim\left(e\overline{f}\right)^{-n^{2}}

The only inequality here comes from Jensen’s Inequality, so it would be necessary to understand the Jensen Gap, which requires understanding how quickly the variance of
\mathcal{A}(T)
decays with
M
. The following conjecture quantifies this.

Conjecture 11.3
.

If
\mathcal{F}=\{T_{n}\subset G(n_{n})\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a bounded, convergent, neighbor-connected family, and
f
is its associated power series, then

Q(\mathcal{F})=\frac{\exp\left(\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{(n\cdot\sigma_{n})^{2}}{2}\right)}{e\cdot\overline{f}},

where
\sigma_{n}^{2}
denotes variance of the random variable
X_{n}=\ln(\mathcal{A}(T_{n}))
.

Sketch of potential proof idea.

Consider a fixed large value of
n
, an interpret all running notation with respect to the spanning tree
T=T_{n}
. The proof of Proposition
9.3
establishes that

\mathbb{E}(X)\approx\ln\overline{f}.

Let
\Psi
be the moment generating function of
X
. Stirling’s approximation gives:

\displaystyle\Psi(-M)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-MX}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\prod\frac{M}{P_{i}(\alpha)}\right)=M^{M}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(\prod\frac{1}{P_{i}(\alpha)}\right)

\displaystyle\approx e^{M}\cdot M!\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(\prod\frac{1}{P_{i}(\alpha)}\right)=e^{M}\cdot\textnormal{Prob}_{\textnormal{MST}}(T).

On the other hand, if we assume that
X
becomes normal as
n\rightarrow\infty
, then

\Psi(t)\approx\exp\left(\mathbb{E}(X)t+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}t^{2}\right),
so

\displaystyle\Psi(-M)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{e^{(\sigma\cdot M)^{2}/2}}{\overline{f}^{M}}

Combining the previous two equations yields

\displaystyle\textnormal{Prob}_{\textnormal{MST}}(T)\approx\frac{e^{(\sigma\cdot M)^{2}/2}}{e^{M}\cdot\overline{f}^{M}}=\left(\frac{e^{\sigma^{2}M/2}}{e\cdot\overline{f}}\right)^{M},

from which the result follows.
∎
