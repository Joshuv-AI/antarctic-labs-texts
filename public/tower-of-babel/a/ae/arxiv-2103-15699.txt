Lebesgue type decompositions and Radon-Nikodym derivatives for pairs of bounded linear operators
S. Hassi, H.S.V. de Snoo

Abstract
. For a pair of bounded linear Hilbert space operators A A and B B one considers the Lebesgue type decompositions of B B with respect to A A into an almost dominated part and a singular part, analogous to the Lebesgue decomposition for a pair of measures (in which case one speaks of an absolutely continuous and a singular part). A complete parametrization of all Lebesgue type decompositions will be given, and the uniqueness of such decompositions will be characterized. In addition, it will be shown that the almost dominated part of B B in a Lebesgue type decomposition has an abstract Radon-Nikodym derivative with respect to the operator A A .

47A05, 47A06, 47A65

1.
Introduction

Let
{\mathfrak{E}}
,
{\mathfrak{H}}
, and
{\mathfrak{K}}
be Hilbert spaces and

A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
be bounded
linear operators.
In the present paper it will be shown that there are so-called
Lebesgue type
decompositions
of the
operator
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
relative to the operator
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})

of the form

(1.1)

B=B_{1}+B_{2},\quad B_{1},B_{2}\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}}),

where
{\rm ran\,}B_{1}\perp{\rm ran\,}B_{2}
,
B_{1}
is almost dominated by
A
,
and
B_{2}
is singular with respect to
A
; the terminology will be explained below.
The collection of all Lebesgue type decompositions will be parametrized
and a criterion for the uniqueness of such decompositions will be established.
Furthermore, it will be shown that if
B
has the above Lebesgue type
decomposition (
1.1
) with respect to
A
,
then there exists a uniquely determined closed linear operator
C

from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
satisfying a certain minimality condition, that, in general,
is unbounded, such that

(1.2)

B_{1}=CA;

for details, see Theorem
7.4
below.
This operator
C
will be called the
Radon-Nikodym derivative

of
B_{1}
with respect to
A
.
The above results are the abstract analogs of the usual Lebesgue decomposition
of a finite measure into an absolutely continuous part and a singular part,
and of the corresponding Radon-Nikodym derivative for the absolutely continuous part.
There are, indeed, situations in measure theory where actually
there is more than one Lebesgue type decomposition.

The above results can be interpreted as special cases
of corresponding results for linear relations.
For any linear relation
T
from a Hilbert space
{\mathfrak{H}}
to a Hilbert space
{\mathfrak{K}}

there exist the so-called Lebesgue type decompositions of the form

T=T_{1}+T_{2}

such that
{\rm ran\,}T_{1}\perp{\rm ran\,}T_{2}
,
T_{1}
is a regular operator
(an operator whose closure in
{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{K}}
is an operator, i.e., a closable operator),
and
T_{2}
is a singular relation (a relation whose closure
is the product of closed linear subspaces in
{\mathfrak{H}}
and
{\mathfrak{K}}
, respectively).
A general treatment of Lebesgue type decompositions
of a linear relation
T
was carried out in the recent paper
[
7
]
.
For instance, in that paper an explicit parametrization of all
Lebesgue type decompositions of
T
was established
and the case where the Lebesgue type decomposition
of
T
is unique has been characterized therein.
In the setting of a pair of positive operators this type
of uniqueness result goes back to Ando
[
1
]
.

In the present paper Lebesgue type decompositions for
linear relations which are simultaneously operator ranges are studied (see
[
4
]
);
in what follows such linear relations are called shortly
operator range relations
.
Operator range relations coincide with the linear relations of the form

(1.3)

L(A,B)=\{\,\{Af,Bf\}:\,f\in{\mathfrak{E}}\,\},

where
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
.
The Lebesgue type decompositions of the relation
L(A,B)

correspond to the Lebesgue type decompositions
of the operator
B
with respect to the operator
A
in (
1.1
).
In particular,
B
is almost dominated by
A

precisely if the corresponding relation
L(A,B)
is regular, i.e.,

L(A,B)
is a closable operator;
moreover,
B
is singular with respect to
A
precisely if
L(A,B)
is singular.
If
L(A,B)
is regular,
then its closure is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
B

with respect to
A
mentioned in (
1.2
);
the precise meaning of this statement will be explained later.
The approach in the present paper makes it possible
to further develop the results established
in
[
7
]
, including uniqueness results, to the
setting of operator range relations;
see also
[
8
,
6
,
10
]
,

Here is a brief description of the contents of the paper.
The concept of operator ranges, including their natural topologies,
will be reviewed in Section

Operator range relations and a special normalized class

As an application, a construction of the operator range representation

The corresponding classification for pairs of

This involves the notions of domination and almost domination

7.7
.
Finally, all Lebesgue type decompositions for pairs of bounded linear operators are

[

2.
Linear relations admitting an operator range representation

In this section the special class of linear relations which, in addition,
are operator ranges, will be introduced.
In what follows, such linear relations are briefly called
operator range relations.

This class extends the class of closed linear relations and the operator range relations
have a number of useful properties.
The introduction will be facilitated by a brief treatment of operator ranges
in Hilbert spaces. The notions of operator ranges and operator range relations in the present sense
go back to
[
4
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
23
]
,
[
24
]
,
[
25
]
.
A brief survey is given in this section.
For the convenience of the reader, some proofs are included.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
{\mathfrak{X}}
be a Hilbert space with inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
.
A subspace
{\mathfrak{M}}
of
{\mathfrak{X}}
, together with an inner-product

(\cdot,\cdot)_{+}
on
{\mathfrak{M}}
, is said to be an operator range in
{\mathfrak{X}}
, if

(a)

{\mathfrak{M}}
is a Hilbert space when equipped with

(\cdot,\cdot)_{+}
;

(b)

\|u\|_{+}\geq c\|u\|_{\mathfrak{H}}
,
u\in{\mathfrak{M}}
, for some
c>0
.

In particular, a closed linear subspace is an operator range.

The terminology operator range is motivated the following lemma.
If
{\mathfrak{M}}\subset{\mathfrak{X}}
is the range of a bounded operator,
then there is a natural inner-product on
{\mathfrak{M}}
,
that makes
{\mathfrak{M}}
an operator range.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
{\mathfrak{X}}
and
{\mathfrak{Y}}
be Hilbert spaces and let
Z\in\mathbf{B}({\mathfrak{Y}},{\mathfrak{X}})
.
Then the linear space
{\mathfrak{M}}={\rm ran\,}Z
, equipped with the inner-product

(2.1)

(Zx,Zy)_{+}=(x,y),\quad x,y\in{\mathfrak{Y}}\ominus{{\rm ker\,}\,}Z,

is an operator range.

Proof.

Assume that
Z
is not the zero operator.
Note that
{\mathfrak{M}}={\rm ran\,}Z
with
(\cdot,\cdot)_{+}

in (
2.1
) is indeed an inner-product space.
To see that it is complete, let
(Zx_{n})
be a Cauchy sequence in
({\mathfrak{M}},(\cdot,\cdot)_{+})

with
x_{n}\in{\mathfrak{Y}}\ominus{{\rm ker\,}\,}Z
.
Then
(x_{n})
is a Cauchy sequence in
{\mathfrak{Y}}\ominus{{\rm ker\,}\,}Z
.
Thus
x_{n}\to x
for some
x\in{\mathfrak{Y}}\ominus{{\rm ker\,}\,}Z
and
Zx_{n}\to Zx
,
since
Z\in\mathbf{B}({\mathfrak{Y}},{\mathfrak{X}})
.
This shows (a) in Definition
2.1
.
By definition
\|Zx\|_{+}=\|x\|
,
x\in{\mathfrak{Y}}\ominus{{\rm ker\,}\,}Z
,
and
\|Zx\|\leq\|Z\|\|x\|
,
x\in{\mathfrak{Y}}
, lead to the inequality

\|Zx\|_{+}=\|x\|\geq\frac{1}{\|Z\|}\|Zx\|_{\mathfrak{X}},\quad x\in{\mathfrak{Y}}\ominus{{\rm ker\,}\,}Z,

which gives (b) in Definition
2.1
.
∎

There is also a converse to Lemma
2.2
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
({\mathfrak{M}},(\cdot,\cdot)_{+})
be an operator range in
{\mathfrak{X}}
.
Then there exists an operator
Z\in\mathbf{B}({\mathfrak{X}})

such that
{\mathfrak{M}}={\rm ran\,}Z
and

(2.2)

(Zx,Zy)_{+}=(x,y),\quad x,y\in{\mathfrak{X}}\ominus{{\rm ker\,}\,}Z.

The operator
Z
may be chosen to be nonnegative.

Proof.

Let
\imath:{\mathfrak{M}}\to{\mathfrak{X}}
be the identification map, where each space has its own topology.
Then it follows from (b) that
c\,\|\imath\,x\|\leq\|x\|_{+}
, so that
\imath
is bounded.
Its adjoint
\imath^{\times}
from
{\mathfrak{X}}
to
{\mathfrak{M}}

is a bounded mapping and one has the polar decomposition
(cf.
[
15
, Section VI 2.7]
)

\imath^{\times}=Z\,|\,\imath^{\times}|,

where
Z:{\mathfrak{X}}\to{\mathfrak{M}}

is the unique partial isometry with initial space
{\rm\overline{ran}\,}|\,\imath^{\times}|

and final space
{\rm\overline{ran}\,}\imath^{\times}
. Since the last space is the orthogonal complement
in
{\mathfrak{M}}
of
{{\rm ker\,}\,}\imath
, one sees that
{\rm\overline{ran}\,}\imath^{\times}={\mathfrak{M}}
. Consequently,
{\rm ran\,}Z={\mathfrak{M}}
,
and (
2.2
) holds as
Z
is a partial isometry from
{\mathfrak{X}}
to
{\mathfrak{M}}
.
Finally, it remains to observe that

c\|Zx\|\leq\|Zx\|_{+}\leq\|x\|,\quad x\in{\mathfrak{X}},

thanks to (b) and the fact that
Z
is a partial isometry. Thus
Z\in\mathbf{B}({\mathfrak{X}})
.

For the proof of the last statement, consider
Z\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{X}})
and its polar decomposition
Z=|Z^{*}|C
, where

C\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{X}})
is the unique partial isometry with initial space
{\rm\overline{ran}\,}Z^{*}
and final space
{\rm\overline{ran}\,}|Z^{*}|
.
Observe that, by the Douglas Lemma,
{\rm ran\,}|Z^{*}|={\rm ran\,}Z
, so that
{\rm ran\,}|Z^{*}|={\mathfrak{M}}
and, clearly,

(|Z^{*}|Cx,|Z^{*}|Cy)_{+}=(x,y)=(Cx,Cy),\quad x,y\in({{\rm ker\,}\,}Z)^{\perp}.

This implies that

(|Z^{*}|u,|Z^{*}|v)_{+}=(u,v),\quad u,v\in({{\rm ker\,}\,}|Z^{*}|)^{\perp},

where
|Z^{*}|\in\mathbf{B}({\mathfrak{X}})
is nonnegative.
∎

According to Lemma
2.2
and Lemma
2.3
,
an operator range
{\mathfrak{M}}

is naturally parametrized by a bounded operator
Z\in\mathbf{B}({\mathfrak{Y}},{\mathfrak{X}})
.
The subspace
{{\rm ker\,}\,}Z\subset{\mathfrak{Y}}
is called the
redundant part

of this parametrization, as it does not contribute to
{\rm ran\,}Z
.
The restriction
Z_{0}
of
Z
to
{\mathfrak{Y}}\ominus{{\rm ker\,}\,}Z
is called the

reduced part
or
reduction
of
Z
:

(2.3)

Z=(Z_{0}\,;\,O_{{{\rm ker\,}\,}Z}).

The topology of
{\mathfrak{M}}
is uniquely determined by the reduced part of the
representing operator in a sense to b explained below.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
{\mathfrak{M}}
be an operator range in
{\mathfrak{X}}
. Assume that
there exist Hilbert spaces
{\mathfrak{Y}}
and
{\mathfrak{Y}}_{1}
and operators

Z\in\mathbf{B}({\mathfrak{Y}},{\mathfrak{X}})
and
Z_{1}\in\mathbf{B}({\mathfrak{Y}}_{1},{\mathfrak{X}})
,
which each satisfy the conditions in Lemma
2.2
.
Then there is a bounded and boundedly invertible operator

W\in\mathbf{B}({\mathfrak{Y}}_{1},{\mathfrak{Y}})
such that

(2.4)

Z_{1}=ZW,\quad{{\rm ker\,}\,}W={{\rm ker\,}\,}Z_{1},\quad{\rm ran\,}W={\rm\overline{ran}\,}Z^{*}.

Consequently, the topologies induced on
{\mathfrak{M}}
by
Z

and by
Z_{1}
are equivalent.

Proof.

If
{\mathfrak{M}}={\rm ran\,}Z_{1}
with
Z_{1}\in\mathbf{B}({\mathfrak{Y}}_{1},{\mathfrak{X}})

with a Hilbert space
{\mathfrak{Y}}_{1}
, then
it is clear that the operators
Z_{1}
and
Z
have the same range.
Hence, by the Douglas lemma,
there is an operator

W\in\mathbf{B}({\mathfrak{Y}}_{1},{\mathfrak{Y}})
such that (
2.4
) holds.
Note that the operator
W
in Lemma
2.4
is a bounded bijective
mapping from
({{\rm ker\,}\,}Z_{1})^{\perp}
onto
({{\rm ker\,}\,}Z)^{\perp}
,
as follows from the closed graph theorem.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
{\mathfrak{M}}={\rm ran\,}Z
be an operator range as in Lemma
2.1

and let
Z_{0}
be the reduced part of
Z
.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

{\mathfrak{M}}
is an operator range in
{\mathfrak{X}}
that is closed;

(ii)

{\rm ran\,}Z
or, equivalently,
{\rm ran\,}Z_{0}
is closed;

(iii)

Z_{0}
is bounded and boundedly invertible.

Operator ranges in a Hilbert space
{\mathfrak{X}}
extend the notion of closed linear subspaces of
{\mathfrak{X}}
.
In particular, operator ranges form a lattice; cf.
[
4
]
.

The previous notion of operator range will now be extended
to subspaces of product spaces; see for instance
[
23
]
.

Definition 2.6
.

A linear relation
T
from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
is said to be an
operator range
relation
if its graph is an operator range in
{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{K}}
, thus

T={\rm ran\,}\Phi
for some
\Phi\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{K}})
,
where
{\mathfrak{E}}
is some Hilbert space.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
T
be a closed linear relation from the Hilbert space
{\mathfrak{H}}
to the Hilbert space
{\mathfrak{K}}
.
Then
T
is an operator range relation.

Proof.

Consider the orthogonal decomposition

{\mathfrak{E}}={\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{K}}=T\hoplus T^{\perp}
and let

\Phi=P_{T}
be the orthogonal projection from

{\mathfrak{E}}={\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{K}}
to
T
, so that
T={\rm ran\,}\Phi
.
∎

To characterize operator range relations
the following notions and notations are needed.
For a pair of operators
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and

B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})

the linear relation
L(A,B)
from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
is defined by

L(A,B)=\{\,\{Af,Bf\}:\,f\in{\mathfrak{E}}\,\}.

Let
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
be the orthogonal projections from

{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{K}}
onto the component subspaces

{\mathfrak{H}}\times\{0\}
and
\{0\}\times{\mathfrak{K}}
, respectively.
The mappings
\iota_{1}\{\varphi,0\}=\varphi
and

\iota_{2}\{0,\psi\}=\psi

identify
{\mathfrak{H}}\times\{0\}
with
{\mathfrak{H}}
and

\{0\}\times{\mathfrak{K}}
with
{\mathfrak{K}}
, respectively.
In the following theorem the operator range relations
are characterized; see
[
2
, Theorem 1.10.1]
.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
{\mathfrak{H}}
and
{\mathfrak{K}}
be Hilbert spaces
and let
T
be a linear relation from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
.
Then there are the following statements:

(a)

If
T={\rm ran\,}\Phi
, where
\Phi\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{K}})
,
then
T=L(A,B)
with

A=\iota_{1}P_{1}\Phi\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})\quad\mbox{and}\quad B=\iota_{2}P_{2}\Phi\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}}).

(b)

If
T=L(A,B)
, where
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and

B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
, then
T={\rm ran\,}\Phi
with

\Phi\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{K}})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Phi f=\{Af,Bf\},\quad f\in{\mathfrak{E}}.

Corollary 2.9
.

If
T
is an operator range relation from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
, then
{\rm dom\,}T
and
{{\rm ker\,}\,}T
are
operator ranges in
{\mathfrak{H}}
, while
{\rm ran\,}T
and
{\rm mul\,}T
are operator ranges in
{\mathfrak{K}}
.

The concept of an operator range relation extends in a certain way
the notion of a closed linear relation.
For instance, sums, and intersections, as well as Cartesian
products of operator range relations are again operator range relations.
In particular, operator range relations form a lattice; cf.
[
4
]
.
Notice also that if the relation
T
is itself a range of some closed linear relation

H
from a Hilbert space
{\mathfrak{E}}
to the Hilbert space

{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{K}}
, then
T
is still an operator range relation.

3.
Operator range relations and normalized pairs

Let
{\mathfrak{E}}
,
{\mathfrak{H}}
, and
{\mathfrak{K}}
be Hilbert spaces
and consider the pair of operators
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and

B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
. It will be convenient to recall from
[
5
,
26
]
, see also
[
4
]
,
the following auxiliary column and row operators.
The
column operator

c(A,B)
from
{\mathfrak{E}}
to
{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{K}}

is defined by

(3.1)

c(A,B)=\begin{pmatrix}A\\
B\end{pmatrix},\quad\mbox{i.e.,}\quad c(A,B)\varphi=\begin{pmatrix}A\varphi\\
B\varphi\end{pmatrix},\quad\varphi\in{\mathfrak{E}}.

Then
c(A,B)
belongs to
{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{K}})
.
Likewise, the
row operator

from
{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{K}}
to
{\mathfrak{E}}
is defined by

(3.2)

r(A,B)=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\end{pmatrix}\quad\mbox{i.e.,}\quad r(A,B)\begin{pmatrix}h\\
k\end{pmatrix}=Ah+Bk,\quad h\in{\mathfrak{H}},\,\,k\in{\mathfrak{K}}.

Then
r(A,B)
belongs to
{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{K}},{\mathfrak{E}})
and

(3.3)

{\rm ran\,}r(A,B)={\rm ran\,}A+{\rm ran\,}B.

Moreover, it is clear from (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) that

(3.4)

c(A,B)^{*}=r(A^{*},B^{*}).

Therefore, it follows from (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) that

(3.5)

c(A,B)^{*}c(A,B)=A^{*}A+B^{*}B,

and that

(3.6)

c(A,B)c(A,B)^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}AA^{*}&AB^{*}\\
BA^{*}&BB^{*}\end{pmatrix}.

For a pair of operators
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})

the linear relation
L(A,B)
from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
is defined by

(3.7)

L(A,B)=\{\,\{Af,Bf\}:\,f\in{\mathfrak{E}}\,\},

so that
L(A,B)
is an operator range relation
as in Theorem
2.8
, since in the present notation

(3.8)

L(A,B)={\rm ran\,}c(A,B).

The operator range relation
L(A,B)
is sometimes called a
quotient
,
as, indeed, in the sense of the product of
linear relations one can write
L(A,B)
as
BA^{-1}
; cf.
[
16
]
.
Note that the domain and the range of
L(A,B)

in (
3.7
) are given by

{\rm dom\,}L(A,B)={\rm ran\,}A,\quad{\rm ran\,}L(A,B)={\rm ran\,}B,

while the kernel and the multivalued part of

L(A,B)
are given by

(3.9)

{{\rm ker\,}\,}L(A,B)=A({{\rm ker\,}\,}B),\quad{\rm mul\,}L(A,B)=B({{\rm ker\,}\,}A).

Recall that for a linear relation
T
from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
the adjoint
T^{*}

is an automatically closed linear relation from
{\mathfrak{K}}
to
{\mathfrak{H}}
given by

(3.10)

T^{*}=JT^{\perp}=(JT)^{\perp},

where
J
stands the flip-flop operator
\{f,g\}\mapsto\{g,-f\}
; in other words

(3.11)

T^{*}=\{\,\{h,k\}\in{\mathfrak{K}}\times{\mathfrak{H}}:\,(g,h)=(f,k)\mbox{ for all }\{f,g\}\in T\,\}.

With
J
the definition of the adjoint can be expressed
in geometric terms as follows
T^{*}=(JT)^{\perp}
.
In particular,
T^{**}=T^{\perp\perp}
.

Now apply the definition (
3.10
) to the linear relation
L(A,B)
in (
3.7
).
Then, by (
3.8
), the adjoint of
L(A,B)
is given by
JL(A,B)^{\perp}=J({\rm ran\,}c(A,B))^{\perp}
,
i.e.

L(A,B)^{*}=J\,{{\rm ker\,}\,}c(A,B)^{*}=J\,{{\rm ker\,}\,}r(A^{*},B^{*}).

In other words, the adjoint of
L(A,B)
is given by

(3.12)

L(A,B)^{*}=\{\,\{k,h\}\in{\mathfrak{K}}\times{\mathfrak{H}}:\,B^{*}k=A^{*}h\,\},

cf. (
3.11
).
At this point, it is useful to introduce the following linear subspaces

{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)
and
{\mathfrak{R}}(A,B)
of
{\mathfrak{K}}
and
{\mathfrak{H}}
by

(3.13)

{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)=\{k\in{\mathfrak{K}}:\,B^{*}k\in{\rm ran\,}A^{*}\},\quad{\mathfrak{R}}(A,B)=\{h\in{\mathfrak{H}}:\,A^{*}h\in{\rm ran\,}B^{*}\},

respectively. In other words,

{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)
is the pre-image
(B^{*})^{-1}({\rm ran\,}A^{*})
and

{\mathfrak{R}}(A,B)
is the pre-image
(A^{*})^{-1}({\rm ran\,}B^{*})
.
Note that

{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)={\mathfrak{K}}\,\,\Leftrightarrow\,\,{\rm ran\,}B^{*}\subset{\rm ran\,}A^{*},\quad{\mathfrak{R}}(A,B)={\mathfrak{H}}\,\,\Leftrightarrow\,\,{\rm ran\,}A^{*}\subset{\rm ran\,}B^{*}.

From the expression (
3.12
) it is seen that
the domain and the range of
L(A,B)^{*}

are given by the sets in (
3.13
):

(3.14)

{\rm dom\,}L(A,B)^{*}={\mathfrak{D}}(A,B),\quad{\rm ran\,}L(A,B)^{*}={\mathfrak{R}}(A,B).

Moreover, the kernel and multivalued part of
L(A,B)^{*}
are given by

(3.15)

{{\rm ker\,}\,}L(A,B)^{*}={{\rm ker\,}\,}B^{*},\quad{\rm mul\,}L(A,B)^{*}={{\rm ker\,}\,}A^{*},

respectively. Observe that (
3.14
) implies that

(3.16)

{\rm mul\,}L(A,B)^{**}={\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)^{\perp},\quad{{\rm ker\,}\,}L(A,B)^{**}={\mathfrak{R}}(A,B)^{\perp}.

It is helpful to state some general properties for the operators
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and

B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
, in which case
c(A,B)\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{K}})
.
By means of the Douglas lemma, one sees, for instance, from (
3.5
) and (
3.4
), that

(3.17)

\begin{split}{\rm ran\,}(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)^{\frac{1}{2}}&={\rm ran\,}(c(A,B)^{*}c(A,B))^{\frac{1}{2}}={\rm ran\,}c(A,B)^{*}\\
&={\rm ran\,}r(A^{*},B^{*})={\rm ran\,}A^{*}+{\rm ran\,}B^{*},\end{split}

cf.
[
4
]
, and from (
3.6
) that

(3.18)

{\rm ran\,}\begin{pmatrix}AA^{*}&AB^{*}\\
BA^{*}&BB^{*}\end{pmatrix}^{\frac{1}{2}}={\rm ran\,}(c(A,B)c(A,B)^{*})^{\frac{1}{2}}={\rm ran\,}c(A,B).

Moreover, it is clear that
{\rm ran\,}c(A,B)
and
{\rm ran\,}c(A,B)^{*}
are simultaneously closed, and
therefore
the following spaces

(3.19)

{\rm ran\,}c(A,B),\quad{\rm ran\,}c(A,B)^{*}c(A,B),\quad{\rm ran\,}c(A,B)^{*},\quad{\rm ran\,}c(A,B)^{*}c(A,B),

are closed simultaneously; see e.g.
[
2
, Theorem 1.3.5]
.
The following characterization of closedness is a direct consequence of these considerations.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let the linear relation
L(A,B)
be given by (
3.7
).
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

L(A,B)
is closed;

(ii)

{\rm ran\,}(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)
is closed in
{\mathfrak{E}}
;

(iii)

{\rm ran\,}A^{*}+{\rm ran\,}B^{*}
is closed in
{\mathfrak{E}}
.

Proof.

Thanks to the equivalences in (
3.19
), the assertions in (i), (ii), and (iii)
follow from (
3.8
), (
3.5
), and (
3.4
) together with (
3.3
),
respectively.
∎

The next corollary shows that range space relations admit some specific properties
which are well known in the case of closed operators; cf. the closed graph theorem.
The following result goes back to Foias
[
4
]
, see also
[
23
]
; the present
proof seems to be new.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
T
be a range space relation from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
. Then the following implications hold:

(i)

If
\,{\rm mul\,}T=\{0\}
, then
{\rm dom\,}T
is closed implies that
T
is a bounded operator;

(ii)

If
\,{{\rm ker\,}\,}T=\{0\}
, then
{\rm ran\,}T
is closed implies that
T^{-1}
is a bounded operator.

Proof.

(i) By assumption one can write
T=L(A,B)
with some operators
A
and
B
as in (
3.7
).
Now the assumption
{\rm mul\,}T=\{0\}
means that

{{\rm ker\,}\,}A\subset{{\rm ker\,}\,}B\quad\mbox{or, equivalently,}\quad{\rm\overline{ran}\,}B^{*}\subset{\rm\overline{ran}\,}A^{*}.

If
{\rm dom\,}T={\rm ran\,}A
is closed, then also
{\rm ran\,}A^{*}
closed.
Hence, in fact,
{\rm ran\,}B^{*}\subset{\rm ran\,}A^{*}
and

{\rm ran\,}A^{*}+{\rm ran\,}B^{*}={\rm ran\,}A^{*}\quad\text{is closed}.

By Lemma
3.1

L(A,B)
a closed operator and the statement follows from the closed graph theorem.

(ii) This is obtained by applying (i) to the inverse
T^{-1}
.
∎

Let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
,
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
and
let the linear relation
L(A,B)
be given by (
3.7
).
In general, there will be some redundance
in the representation (
3.7
); cf. (
2.3
).
In fact, the redundant part is given by the closed linear subspace

{{\rm ker\,}\,}c(A,B)={{\rm ker\,}\,}A\cap{{\rm ker\,}\,}B.

Observe that it follows from the identities

({\rm ran\,}(A^{*}A+B^{*}B))^{\perp}={{\rm ker\,}\,}(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)={{\rm ker\,}\,}A\cap{{\rm ker\,}\,}B,

that the space
{\mathfrak{E}}
has
the following
orthogonal decomposition:

(3.20)

{\mathfrak{E}}={\rm\overline{ran}\,}(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)\oplus({{\rm ker\,}\,}A\cap{{\rm ker\,}\,}B).

Hence, one may reduce the representation in (
3.7
)
by introducing the restrictions

A_{0}
and
B_{0}
of
A
and
B
to
the closed linear subspace
{\mathfrak{E}}_{0}={\rm\overline{ran}\,}(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)

of
{\mathfrak{E}}
, cf. (
2.3
), so that

(3.21)

A=r(A_{0},O_{{{\rm ker\,}\,}A\,\cap\,{{\rm ker\,}\,}B}),\quad B=r(B_{0},O_{{{\rm ker\,}\,}A\,\cap\,{{\rm ker\,}\,}B}),

with respect to the decomposition (
3.20
).
It is clear that
{{\rm ker\,}\,}A_{0}\cap{{\rm ker\,}\,}B_{0}=\{0\}
, and thus

(3.22)

L(A,B)=\{\,\{A_{0}f,B_{0}\,f\}:\,f\in{\mathfrak{E}}_{0}\,\}

is a representation in terms of the reduced part
c(A_{0},B_{0})
of
c(A,B)
.
Recall that reduced representations as in (
3.22
)
are uniquely defined, up to everywhere defined operators
which are bounded and boundedly invertible; cf. Lemma
2.4
.
Furthermore, via (
3.6
),
one has from (
3.21
) that, with respect to the decomposition (
3.20
),

(3.23)

A^{*}A+B^{*}B=\begin{pmatrix}(A_{0})^{*}A_{0}+(B_{0})^{*}B_{0}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

It is clear that
{\rm\overline{ran}\,}((A_{0})^{*}A_{0}+(B_{0})^{*}B_{0})={\mathfrak{E}}_{0}
; hence one obtains the following lemma.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let the linear relation
L(A,B)
be given by (
3.7
)
and let
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
as in (
3.21
)
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

L(A,B)
is closed;

(ii)

{\rm ran\,}((A_{0})^{*}A_{0}+(B_{0})^{*}B_{0})={\mathfrak{E}}_{0}
.

In the rest of this section attention is paid to the case where the linear relation
L(A,B)
is closed.
In this case the operators
A
and
B
representing
L(A,B)
can be replaced by a normalized pair

A^{*}A+B^{*}B=I
, in which case certain formulas simplify. In fact, it is instructive to first consider the case
where
A^{*}A+B^{*}B
is an orthogonal projection.

Lemma 3.4
.

Assume that there exists an orthogonal projection
Q
in
{\mathfrak{E}}
such that

(3.24)

A^{*}A+B^{*}B=Q,

in which case the redundant part is given by
{{\rm ker\,}\,}A\cap{{\rm ker\,}\,}B={{\rm ker\,}\,}Q
.
Then the relation
L(A,B)
is closed and the orthogonal projection
from
{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{K}}
onto
L(A,B)
is given by

(3.25)

P_{L(A,B)}=\begin{pmatrix}AA^{*}&AB^{*}\\
BA^{*}&BB^{*}\end{pmatrix}.

Moreover, the orthogonal projection
from
{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{K}}
onto
L(A,B)^{*}
is given by

(3.26)

P_{L(A,B)^{*}}=\begin{pmatrix}I-BB^{*}&BA^{*}\\
AB^{*}&I-AA^{*}\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

Since
{\rm ran\,}Q
is closed, it follows from Lemma
2.8
that
L(A,B)
is closed.
Let
\Phi=c(A,B)
, so that
\Phi\in\mathbf{B}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{K}})
. Then (
3.24
)
means that
\Phi^{*}\Phi=Q
and
{\rm ran\,}\Phi^{*}={\rm ran\,}Q
. Observe that

(\Phi\Phi^{*})(\Phi\Phi^{*})=\Phi(\Phi^{*}\Phi)\Phi^{*}=\Phi Q\Phi^{*}=\Phi\Phi^{*},

so that
\Phi\Phi^{*}
is an orthogonal projection, mapping onto
{\rm ran\,}\Phi\Phi^{*}={\rm ran\,}\Phi
.
Thus the statement follows from (
3.6
); cf.
[
2
, Appendix D, p. 703]
.

Note that the condition (
3.24
) implies that

{{\rm ker\,}\,}A\cap{{\rm ker\,}\,}B={{\rm ker\,}\,}\Phi={{\rm ker\,}\,}Q
. Hence it follows that
{\rm ran\,}r(A^{*},B^{*})

is dense in
{\mathfrak{E}}_{0}
and thus
{\rm ran\,}r(A^{*},B^{*})={\mathfrak{E}}_{0}={\rm ran\,}Q

by Lemma
3.1
.
Note that therefore also
{\rm ran\,}r(B^{*},-A^{*})={\mathfrak{E}}_{0}
,
so that the selfadjoint mapping

\begin{pmatrix}B\\
-A\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}B\\
-A\end{pmatrix}^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}B\\
-A\end{pmatrix}(B^{*}\,\,-A^{*})

takes
{\mathfrak{K}}\times{\mathfrak{H}}
onto
JL(A,B)
. Moreover, this mapping is,
due to (
3.24
), idempotent. Thus the orthogonal projection
from
{\mathfrak{K}}\times{\mathfrak{H}}
onto
JL(A,B)
is given by

P_{JL(A,B)}=\begin{pmatrix}B\\
-A\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}B\\
-A\end{pmatrix}^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}BB^{*}&-BA^{*}\\
-AB^{*}&AA^{*}\end{pmatrix},

which is equivalento to (
3.25
).

Since
L(A,B)^{*}=(JL(A,B))^{\bot}
, the orthogonal projection
onto
L(A,B)^{*}
is given by

P_{L(A,B)^{*}}=I-P_{JL(A,B)}=\begin{pmatrix}I-BB^{*}&BA^{*}\\
AB^{*}&I-AA^{*}\end{pmatrix},

which gives (
3.26
).
∎

Recall that the adjoint relation
L(A,B)^{*}
has the representation (
3.12
).
As a closed linear relation from
{\mathfrak{K}}
to
{\mathfrak{H}}
it is an operator range relation.
Under the condition (
3.24
) such a parametrization can be made explicit via
Lemma
3.4
.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let the linear relation
L(A,B)
be given by (
3.7
)
and assume that the condition (
3.24
) holds.
Then the adjoint relation
L(A,B)^{*}
is given by

L(A,B)^{*}=\bigl\{\{(I-BB^{*})\varphi+BA^{*}\psi,\,AB^{*}\varphi+(I-AA^{*})\psi\}:\,\varphi\in{\mathfrak{K}},\,\psi\in{\mathfrak{H}}\bigr\}.

The pair of bounded linear operators
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and

B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})

in (
3.7
) is said to be
normalized
if

(3.27)

A^{*}A+B^{*}B=I.

In this case, the relation
L(A,B)
is closed by Lemma
3.4

and the representation of
L(A,B)
in (
3.7
)
is reduced.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let the linear relation
L(A,B)
be given by (
3.7
)
and assume that
L(A,B)
is closed. Then
L(A,B)

can be represented by a normalized pair.

Proof.

Since
L(A,B)
is closed,
it is known by Lemma
3.1

that
{\rm ran\,}(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)
is closed.
Thus for the reduced representation in (
3.22
) one now has

{\rm ran\,}((A_{0})^{*}A_{0}+(B_{0})^{*}B_{0})={\mathfrak{E}}_{0}

by Lemma
3.3
.
Replacing
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
by the equivalent pair

A_{0}^{\prime}=A_{0}((A_{0})^{*}A_{0}+(B_{0})^{*}B_{0})^{-{\frac{1}{2}}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad B_{0}^{\prime}=B_{0}((A_{0})^{*}A_{0}+(B_{0})^{*}B_{0})^{-{\frac{1}{2}}}

leads to
L(A,B)=L(A_{0}^{\prime},B_{0}^{\prime})
, where
A_{0}^{\prime}\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}}_{0},{\mathfrak{H}})
and

B_{0}^{\prime}\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}}_{0},{\mathfrak{K}})
form a normalized pair.
∎

If
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
are normalized as in (
3.27
),
then the orthogonal operator part of
L(A,B)=BA^{-1}

can be rewritten in terms of the
Moore-Penrose inverse

A^{(-1)}

of
A
, which is an operator from
{\rm ran\,}A\subset{\mathfrak{H}}
to
({{\rm ker\,}\,}A)^{\perp}\subset{\mathfrak{E}}
.
In fact, it is defined as follows: for
\psi\in{\rm ran\,}A
, there exists a unique
\varphi\in({\rm dom\,}A)^{\perp}

such that
A\varphi=\psi
, and
A^{(-1)}\psi:=\varphi
; cf.
[
2
]
.
Note that in the literature one often also extends
A^{(-1)}
by
A^{(-1)}\psi=0
for
\psi\in({\rm ran\,}A)^{\perp}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let the linear relation
L(A,B)
be given by (
3.7
)
and assume that the condition (
3.27
) holds.
Then the orthogonal operator part
L(A,B)_{\rm s}
is given by

(3.28)

L(A,B)_{\rm s}=\{\,\{A\varphi,B\varphi\}:\,\varphi\in({{\rm ker\,}\,}A)^{\perp}\},

and, consequently,

(3.29)

L(A,B)_{\rm s}=BA^{(-1)}.

Proof.

Consider the representation (
3.7
). Since
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
,
the orthogonal decomposition

{\mathfrak{E}}=({{\rm ker\,}\,}A)^{\perp}\oplus{{\rm ker\,}\,}A

leads to the alternative representation

(3.30)

L(A,B)=\{\,\{A\varphi,B\varphi+B\psi\}:\,\varphi\in({{\rm ker\,}\,}A)^{\perp},\,\psi\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}A\,\},

cf.
[
7
]
.
Note that for all
\varphi\in({{\rm ker\,}\,}A)^{\perp}
and
\psi\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}A
, one has by (
4.10
)

\begin{split}(B\varphi,B\psi)&=(B^{*}B\varphi,\psi)=((I-A^{*}A)\varphi,\psi)\\
&=(\varphi,\psi)-(A\varphi,A\psi)=0.\end{split}

Consequently, the range decomposition in the representation (
3.30
) is orthogonal.
Recall from (
3.9
) that
{\rm mul\,}L(A,B)=B({{\rm ker\,}\,}A)
.
Thus the orthogonal operator part of
L(A,B)
has the representation (
3.28
).
The representation (
3.29
) follows from the definition of
A^{(-1)}
.
∎

If
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
satisfy (
3.24
), then the statement in
Corollary
3.5
is a direct consequence of Lemma
3.4
.
Under the stronger condition (
3.27
),
a slightly different looking result can be obtained by invoking the polar decomposition of the operator
A
, i.e.,

A=V_{A}(A^{*}A)^{1/2},

where
V_{A}
is the unique partial isometry from
{\mathfrak{E}}
to
{\mathfrak{H}}
with initial space
{\rm\overline{ran}\,}A^{*}
and final space
{\rm\overline{ran}\,}A
.
Recall that
I-V_{A}(V_{A})^{*}=P_{\,{{\rm ker\,}\,}A^{*}}
.
By means of the polar decomposition of
A
one sees that
the normalization (

) leads to

\begin{split}I-AA^{*}&=I-V_{A}A^{*}A(V_{A})^{*}\\
&=I-V_{A}(V_{A})^{*}+V_{A}B^{*}B(V_{A})^{*}=P_{\,{{\rm ker\,}\,}A^{*}}+V_{A}B^{*}B(V_{A})^{*}.\end{split}

Moreover, by the well-known commutation relations

Therefore the orthogonal projection
from

onto

\begin{split}P_{L(A,B)^{*}}&=\begin{pmatrix}I-BB^{*}&(I-BB^{*})^{1/2}B(V_{A})^{*}\\

V_{A}B^{*}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}(I-BB^{*})^{1/2}\\
V_{A}B^{*}\end{pmatrix}^{*}+\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&P_{\,{{\rm ker\,}\,}A^{*}}\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

From this, the following result is now clear.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let the linear relation
L(A,B)
be given by (
3.7
)
and assume that the condition (
3.27
) holds. Then

L(A,B)^{*}=\{\,\{(I-BB^{*})^{1/2}k,V_{A}B^{*}k+P_{\,{{\rm ker\,}\,}A^{*}}h\}:\,h\in{\mathfrak{H}},\,k\in{\mathfrak{K}}\,\},

where the range decomposition is orthogonal.

Note that the results in Corollary
3.5
and Corollary
3.8
are of practical importance
in the description of boundary value problems (when one speaks of boundary values in parametrized from);
cf.
[
2
]
.

4.
Construction of the closure of operator range relations

Let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
.
Then the linear relation
L(A,B)
from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
in (
3.7
)
is an operator range relation.
By Lemma
2.7
the closure
L(A,B)^{**}

of
L(A,B)
is an operator range relation and
it will be shown how the closure
L(A,B)^{**}
can be represented
in terms of the original operators
A
and
B
.

First return to the treatment involving the pair of bounded operators
A
and
B
.
The following construction is inspired by arguments appearing in measure theory.
Due to the obvious inequalities

(4.1)

A^{*}A\leq A^{*}A+B^{*}B,\quad B^{*}B\leq A^{*}A+B^{*}B,

an application of the Douglas lemma
[
3
]
shows that there exists a pair of contractions

C_{A}\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and
C_{B}\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
,
such that

(4.2)

A=C_{A}\,(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)^{1/2},\quad B=C_{B}\,(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)^{1/2},

or, equivalently

(4.3)

A^{*}=(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)^{1/2}(C_{A})^{*},\quad B^{*}=(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)^{1/2}(C_{B})^{*}.

The contractions

C_{A}\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and
C_{B}\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})

are uniquely determined by the conditions that
C_{A}
and
C_{B}

vanish on
({\rm ran\,}(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)^{\frac{1}{2}})^{\perp}={{\rm ker\,}\,}A\cap{{\rm ker\,}\,}B

or, equivalently,

(4.4)

{\rm ran\,}(C_{A})^{*}\subset{\rm\overline{ran}\,}(A^{*}A+B^{*}B),\quad{\rm ran\,}(C_{B})^{*}\subset{\rm\overline{ran}\,}(A^{*}A+B^{*}B),

and with these conditions one also has as a consequence

{{\rm ker\,}\,}(C_{A})^{*}={{\rm ker\,}\,}A^{*},\quad{{\rm ker\,}\,}(C_{B})^{*}={{\rm ker\,}\,}B^{*}.

It follows from (
4.2
) that
L(A,B)

can be written as

(4.5)

L(A,B)=\{\,\{C_{A}\,(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)^{1/2}f,C_{B}\,(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)^{1/2}f\}:\,f\in{\mathfrak{E}}\,\}.

This representation will be called the

canonical representation
of
L(A,B)
.
Of course the pair
C_{A}
and
C_{B}
itself induces a linear
relation
L(C_{A},C_{B})
in
{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{K}}
, in other words,

(4.6)

L(C_{A},C_{B})=\{\,\{C_{A}f,C_{B}\,f\}:\,f\in{\mathfrak{E}}\,\}.

Analogous to (
3.1
) one may now introduce the column operator

c(C_{A},C_{B})
from
{\mathfrak{E}}
to
{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{K}}
by

(4.7)

c(C_{A},C_{B})=\begin{pmatrix}C_{A}\\
C_{B}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mbox{i.e.,}\quad c(C_{A},C_{B})\varphi=\begin{pmatrix}C_{A}\varphi\\
C_{B}\varphi\end{pmatrix},\quad\varphi\in{\mathfrak{E}}.

Then
c(C_{A},C_{B})
belongs to
{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{K}})
and

L(C_{A},C_{B})={\rm ran\,}c(C_{A},C_{B});

cf. (
3.8
). It follows from (
4.5
) that

(4.8)

L(A,B)={\rm ran\,}c(A,B)\subset{\rm ran\,}c(C_{A},C_{B})=L(C_{A},C_{B}).

Note that if
L(A,B)
is closed, then
L(C_{A},C_{B})=L(A,B)

by (
4.5
), (
3.17
), and Lemma
3.1
.

The next lemma contains a first step in establishing the connection between
the pairs
A,B
and
C_{A},C_{B}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
,
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
, and
let
C_{A}
,
C_{B}
be uniquely defined by
(
4.2
) and (
4.4
).
Then

(4.9)

{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A}\cap{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{B}={{\rm ker\,}\,}A\cap{{\rm ker\,}\,}B,

and

(4.10)

[(C_{A})^{*}C_{A}+(C_{B})^{*}C_{B}]h=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}h,&h\in{\rm\overline{ran}\,}(A^{*}A+B^{*}B),\\
0,&h\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}A\cap{{\rm ker\,}\,}B.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

It follows from (
4.2
), (
4.3
), and (
4.4
) that

(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)^{1/2}[(C_{A})^{*}C_{A}+(C_{B})^{*}C_{B}](A^{*}A+B^{*}B)^{1/2}=A^{*}A+B^{*}B.

Consequently, thanks to (
4.4
),

[(C_{A})^{*}C_{A}+(C_{B})^{*}C_{B}](A^{*}A+B^{*}B)^{1/2}=(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)^{1/2},

which by continuity of
(C_{A})^{*}C_{A}+(C_{B})^{*}C_{B}
implies that

(4.11)

[(C_{A})^{*}C_{A}+(C_{B})^{*}C_{B}]h=h,\quad h\in{\rm\overline{ran}\,}(A^{*}A+B^{*}B).

Hence the first part of (
4.10
) holds.

Keeping (
3.20
) in mind, it is clear that in the present context one also has

{\mathfrak{E}}={\rm\overline{ran}\,}((C_{A})^{*}C_{A}+(C_{B})^{*}C_{B})\oplus({{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A}\cap{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{B}).

Observe that it follows from (
4.4
) that

{\rm\overline{ran}\,}((C_{A})^{*}C_{A}+(C_{B})^{*}C_{B})\subset{\rm\overline{ran}\,}(A^{*}A+B^{*}B).

or, equivalently,

{{\rm ker\,}\,}A\cap{{\rm ker\,}\,}B={{\rm ker\,}\,}(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)\subset{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A}\cap{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{B}.

Assume that these inclusions are strict. Then there exists a nontrivial element

h\in{\rm\overline{ran}\,}(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)

with
h\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A}\cap{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{B}
, which contradicts (
4.11
). Thus

{\rm\overline{ran}\,}((C_{A})^{*}C_{A}+(C_{B})^{*}C_{B})={\rm\overline{ran}\,}(A^{*}A+B^{*}B),

and (
4.9
) follows. Thus also the second part of (
4.10
) holds.
∎

and

Lemma 4.2
.

Let

\begin{pmatrix}A\\
B\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}C_{A}\\
C_{B}\end{pmatrix}(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)^{1/2},

is the unique partial isometry with

{\rm\overline{ran}\,}(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)

Proof.

By Lemma
4.1

(C_{A})^{*}C_{A}+(C_{B})^{*}C_{B}

onto

Lemma 4.3
.

Let

,
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
,
let

,

be uniquely defined by

) and (

),

and

C_{A}

.

Proof.

With the restrictions
A_{0}
and
B_{0}

(4.14)

\begin{split}A&=C_{A}(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)^{\frac{1}{2}}=C_{A}\begin{pmatrix}((A_{0})^{*}A_{0}+(B_{0})^{*}B_{0})^{\frac{1}{2}}&0\\

Now let
{{\widetilde{C}}}_{A}\in\mathbf{B}({\mathfrak{E}}_{0},{\mathfrak{H}})

∎

The description of
L(A,B)^{**}
and its orthogonal operator part
follows easily from Lemma
4.2
and Lemma

.
The Moore-Penrose inverse
(C_{A})^{(-1)}
of

is an operator from

to
({{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A})^{\perp}\subset{\mathfrak{E}}

, and

; cf.
[
2
]
.
Note that in the literature one often also extends

A^{(-1)}\psi=0
for

Theorem 4.4
.

Let the linear relation
L(A,B)
be given by (
3.7
)
and let
C_{A}
and
C_{B}
be the uniquely defined operators
satisfying (
4.2
) and (
4.4
).
Then the linear relation
L(C_{A},C_{B})
is closed and

(4.15)

L(A,B)^{**}=L(C_{A},C_{B})=\{\,\{C_{A}\varphi,C_{B}\varphi\}:\,\varphi\in{\mathfrak{E}}\}=C_{B}(C_{A})^{-1}.

Consequently, the orthogonal operator part of

is given by

(4.16)

(L(A,B)^{**})_{\rm s}=\{\,\{C_{A}\varphi,C_{B}\varphi\}:\,\varphi\in({{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A})^{\perp}\}=C_{B}(C_{A})^{(-1)}.

Proof.

As shown in Lemma
4.2
one has
{\rm ran\,}c(C_{A},C_{B})={\rm\overline{ran}\,}c(A,B)
.
Hence, it follows from (
4.8
), after taking closures, that

and it is clear that
L(C_{A},C_{B})=C_{B}(C_{A})^{-1}
. Thus (
4.15
) has been shown.

In order to show (
4.16
), observe that
C_{A}\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
gives
the orthogonal decomposition

{\mathfrak{E}}=({{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A})^{\perp}\oplus{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A}.

Hence, (
4.15
) leads to the alternative representation

(4.17)

L(C_{A},C_{B})=\{\,\{C_{A}\varphi,C_{B}\varphi+C_{B}\psi\}:\,\varphi\in({{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A})^{\perp},\,\psi\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A}\,\}.

It follows from (

) that

({{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A})^{\perp}={\rm\overline{ran}\,}(C_{A})^{*}\subset{\rm\overline{ran}\,}(A^{*}A+B^{*}B).

Therefore, for all

and

\begin{split}(C_{B}\varphi,C_{B}\psi)&=((C_{B})^{*}C_{B}\varphi,\psi)=((I-(C_{A})^{*}C_{A})\varphi,\psi)\\
&=(\varphi,\psi)-(C_{A}\varphi,C_{A}\psi)=0.\end{split}

Consequently, the range decomposition in the representation (

) is orthogonal.
Furthermore, recall that

{\rm mul\,}L(C_{A},C_{B})=C_{B}({{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A}),

cf. (
3.9
), where
the right-hand side is closed as the left-hand side is closed.
Thus the orthogonal operator part of

L(C_{A},C_{B})_{\rm s}=\{\,\{C_{A}\varphi,C_{B}\varphi\}:\,\varphi\in({{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A})^{\perp}\},

which proves (
4.16
).
∎

5.
Regular and singular operator range relations

Let
{\mathfrak{E}}
,
{\mathfrak{H}}
, and
{\mathfrak{K}}
be Hilbert spaces
and consider the pair of bounded linear operators
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and

B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
.
Let the operator range relation
L(A,B)

be given by (
3.7
).
Criteria will be given for the regularity and singularity of
L(A,B)

in terms of
A~
and
B
, and in terms of
C_{A}
and
C_{B}
in (
4.2
).

First the regularity and the singularity of

L(A,B)
will be expressed in terms of the pair
A
and
B
; see
[
7
]

for some further equivalent statements which hold for general linear relations.
The regularity of
L(A,B)
is described by the following lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let the linear relation
L(A,B)
and
{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)
be given
by (
3.7
) and (
3.13
), respectively.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

L(A,B)
is regular;

(ii)

the set
{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)

is dense in
{\mathfrak{K}}
.

Moreover, the following statements are equivalent:

(iii)

L(A,B)
is a bounded operator;

(iv)

{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)={\mathfrak{K}}
, i.e.,
{\rm ran\,}B^{*}\subset{\rm ran\,}A^{*}
.

Finally, the following statements are equivalent:

(v)

L(A,B)\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{H}},{\mathfrak{K}})
.

(vi)

{\rm ran\,}B^{*}\subset{\rm ran\,}A^{*}
and
{\rm ran\,}A={\mathfrak{H}}
.

Proof.

(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii)
Recall that
L(A,B)
is regular if and only if

{\rm mul\,}L(A,B)^{**}=\{0\}
. It follows from (
3.16
)
that this is equivalent to
{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)
being dense in
{\mathfrak{K}}
; see (
3.14
).
The other description for
{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)
is obtained from (
3.13
).

(iii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iv)
The relation
L(A,B)
is a bounded operator precisely
if there exists
c\geq 0
such that

(5.1)

\|Bf\|\leq c\|Af\|,\quad f\in{\mathfrak{E}},

or, equivalently, if
B^{*}B\leq c^{2}A^{*}A
.
By the Douglas lemma
[
3
]
this is equivalent to
{\rm ran\,}B^{*}\subset{\rm ran\,}A^{*}
.
This last inclusion is the same as saying
{\rm dom\,}L(A,B)^{*}={\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)={\mathfrak{K}}
;
see (
3.13
) and (
3.14
).

(v)
\Leftrightarrow
(vi) This is now clear.
∎

Likewise, the singularity of
L(A,B)
can be expressed
in various equivalent useful ways.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let the linear relation
L(A,B)
be given by (
3.7
).
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

L(A,B)
is singular;

(ii)

L(A,B)^{**}={\rm\overline{ran}\,}A\times{\rm\overline{ran}\,}B
;

(iii)

{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)\subset{{\rm ker\,}\,}B^{*}
;

(iv)

{\mathfrak{R}}(A,B)\subset{{\rm ker\,}\,}A^{*}
;

(v)

{\rm ran\,}A^{*}\cap{\rm ran\,}B^{*}=\{0\}
;

(vi)

L(A,B)^{*}={{\rm ker\,}\,}B^{*}\times{{\rm ker\,}\,}A^{*}
.

Proof.

This is a straightforward application of
[
7
, Proposition 2.8]
,
together with (
3.14
) and (
3.15
).
It suffices to prove (iii)
\Leftrightarrow
(v).

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(v) Let
\ell\in{\rm ran\,}A^{*}\cap{\rm ran\,}B^{*}
.
Then
\ell\in{\rm ran\,}A^{*}
and
\ell=B^{*}h
for some
h\in{\mathfrak{H}}
.
Thus
h\in{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)
and by assumption one sees that
h\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}B^{*}
.
Thus it follows that
\ell=0
, and hence (v) holds.

(v)
\Rightarrow
(iii) This implication is trivial.
∎

Next the regularity and the singularity of

L(A,B)
will be expressed in terms of the pair

C_{A}
and
C_{B}
in (
4.2
).
Recall from Theorem
4.4
that
L(A,B)^{**}=L(C_{A},C_{B})
.
Thus the following characterization of the regularity of

is clear.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let the linear relation
L(A,B)
be given by (
3.7
)
and let
C_{A}
and
C_{B}
be the uniquely defined operators
satisfying (
4.2
) and (

).
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

is regular;

(ii)

is an operator;

(iii)

{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A}\subset{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{B}
.

Likewise, the singularity of
L(A,B)
can be expressed
as follows.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let the linear relation
L(A,B)
be given by (
3.7
)
and let
C_{A}
and
C_{B}
be the uniquely defined operators
satisfying (
4.2
) and (
4.4
).
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

L(A,B)
is singular;

(ii)

{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A}+{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{B}={\mathfrak{E}}
.

Proof.

Due to
L(A,B)^{**}=L(C_{A},C_{B})
(see Theorem
4.4
),
it follows from Lemma
5.2
that
L(A,B)
is singular
if and only if
L(C_{A},C_{B})={\rm ran\,}C_{A}\times{\rm ran\,}C_{B}
,
or, equivalently,
precisely when

(5.2)

{\rm ran\,}c(C_{A},C_{B})={\rm ran\,}C_{A}\times{\rm ran\,}C_{B}.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) Assume that
L(A,B)
is singular. Then, thanks to (
5.2
),
for every
e\in{\mathfrak{E}}
there exist
e_{1},e_{2}\in{\mathfrak{E}}
such that

c(C_{A},C_{B})e=\begin{pmatrix}h_{1}\\
h_{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad c(C_{A},C_{B})e_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}h_{1}\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad c(C_{A},C_{B})e_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
h_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Then, clearly,
e_{1}\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{B}
,
e_{2}\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A}
, and

e_{0}=e-e_{1}-e_{2}\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}c(C_{A},C_{B})={{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A}\cap{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{B}.

This shows that
e\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A}+{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{B}
. Therefore,
{\mathfrak{E}}={{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A}+{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{B}
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i)
Assume that
{\mathfrak{E}}={{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A}+{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{B}
. Let
a=a_{1}+a_{2}
and
b=b_{1}+b_{2}
with

a_{1},b_{1}\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{B}
and
a_{2},b_{2}\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}C_{A}
. Then the equality

\begin{pmatrix}C_{A}a\\
C_{B}b\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}C_{A}a_{1}\\
C_{B}b_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}C_{A}(a_{1}+b_{2})\\
C_{B}(a_{1}+b_{2})\end{pmatrix},

shows that
{\rm ran\,}C_{A}\times{\rm ran\,}C_{B}\subset{\rm ran\,}c(C_{A},C_{B})
.
Hence, (
5.2
) is satisfied.
∎

6.
Classification of pairs of bounded linear operators

Let
{\mathfrak{E}}
,
{\mathfrak{H}}
, and
{\mathfrak{K}}
be Hilbert spaces and let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})

and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})

The characterizations of the operator range relation

from (

)
will now be augmented by further characterizations in terms of

Definition 6.1
.

Let
{\mathfrak{E}}
,
{\mathfrak{H}}
, and
{\mathfrak{K}}
be Hilbert spaces and let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})

and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
. Then the operator
B
is said to be
dominated

by

B\prec A
, if there exists some
c>0
such that

\|Bf\|\leq c\|Af\|\quad\mbox{for all}\quad f\in{\mathfrak{E}}.

By the Douglas lemma this definition is equivalent to the factorization

B=CA
where
C\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{H}},{\mathfrak{K}})
;
the operator
C
is uniquely determined when
{\rm ran\,}C^{*}\subset{\rm\overline{ran}\,}A
, in
which case
{{\rm ker\,}\,}C^{*}={{\rm ker\,}\,}B^{*}
.
Note that in the present context Definition
6.1
agrees
with the definition of domination in
[
9
]
.
For the following, it is useful to recall from the Douglas lemma that
B
is dominated
by
A
if and only if
{\rm ran\,}B^{*}\subset{\rm ran\,}A^{*}
, i.e.,
{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)={\mathfrak{K}}
.

The following simple result is immediate from Lemma
5.1
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
and
let the relation
L(A,B)
be defined by (
3.7
).
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

B
is dominated by
A
;

(ii)

{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)={\mathfrak{K}}
;

(iii)

L(A,B)
is a bounded operator.

The notion of domination in Definition
6.1
is now extended.

Definition 6.3
.

Let
{\mathfrak{E}}
,
{\mathfrak{H}}
, and
{\mathfrak{K}}
be Hilbert spaces and let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})

and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
. Then the operator
B
is said to be

almost dominated
by
A
,
if there exists a sequence of bounded operators

B_{n}\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}}_{n})
, where
{\mathfrak{K}}_{n}
are Hilbert spaces,
and a sequence
c_{n}\geq 0
,
such that for all
f\in{\mathfrak{E}}
:

(a)

\|B_{n}f\|\leq c_{n}\|Af\|
;

(b)

\|B_{n}f\|\leq\|B_{n+1}f\|
;

(c)

\|B_{n}f\|\nearrow\|Bf\|
.

It is clear that if
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
is dominated by
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
, then
B

is automatically almost dominated by
A
by taking
B_{n}=B
and
c_{n}=c
.

The analog of Lemma
6.2
for the almost dominated case
is contained in the following theorem.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
and
let the relation
L(A,B)
be defined by (
3.7
).
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

B
is almost dominated by
A
;

(ii)

{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)

is dense in
{\mathfrak{K}}
;

(iii)

L(A,B)
is regular.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(iii)
Assume that
B
is almost dominated by
A
.
Then there exists a sequence of operators

B_{n}\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}}_{n})

as in Definition
6.3
.
Note that if
f\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}A
then
B_{n}f=0
due to (a),
and hence
\|Bf\|=\sup\|B_{n}f\|=0
due to (c).
One concludes that
{{\rm ker\,}\,}A\subset{{\rm ker\,}\,}B
,
so that

is an operator, see (

).
Define the sequence of linear relations
T_{n}

from
{\rm\overline{ran}\,}A
to
{\mathfrak{K}}_{n}
by

T_{n}={\rm clos\,}\{\,\{Af,B_{n}f\}:\,f\in{\mathfrak{E}}\,\}.

Due to (a) it follows
that each

is a closed bounded operator from
{\rm\overline{ran}\,}A
to
{\mathfrak{K}}_{n}
.
Furthermore, by (b) one sees that for
m\leq n

\|T_{m}Af\|=\|B_{m}f\|\leq\|B_{n}f\|=\|T_{n}Af\|,\quad f\in{\mathfrak{E}},

which implies that

(6.1)

\|T_{m}h\|\leq\|T_{n}h\|,\quad h\in{\rm\overline{ran}\,}A.

Moreover, if
h\in{\rm dom\,}L(A,B)
so that
h=Af
for some

,
then it follows from (c) that

(6.2)

\|T_{n}h\|=\|T_{n}Af\|=\|B_{n}f\|\nearrow\|Bf\|=\|T_{A,B}h\|.

Hence the sequence
T_{n}\in{\mathbf{B}}({\rm\overline{ran}\,}A,{\mathfrak{K}}_{n})

satisfies (
6.1
) and (
6.2
).
Thus
[
7
, Theorem 8.8]
implies that the operator

L(A,B)
is closable.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i) Assume that
L(A,B)
is regular,
so that
L(A,B)
is a closable operator from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
.
Then by
[
7
, Theorem 8.9]
there exists a sequence

T_{n}\in{\mathbf{B}}({\rm\overline{ran}\,}A,{\mathfrak{H}})
of bounded operators
with the property (
6.1
), such that

(6.3)

\|T_{n}h\|\nearrow\|T_{A,B}h\|,\quad h\in{\rm dom\,}L(A,B).

Define the operators
B_{n}=T_{n}A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
.
It will be shown that the conditions of Definition
6.3

are satisfied with
{\mathfrak{K}}_{n}={\mathfrak{H}}
.
First note that

\|B_{n}f\|\leq\|T_{n}\|\,\|Af\|,\quad f\in{\mathfrak{E}},

so that (a) is satisfied. Secondly, observe that
for all
m\leq n
it follows from (
6.1
) that

\|B_{m}f\|=\|T_{m}Af\|\leq\|T_{n}Af\|=\|B_{n}f\|,\quad f\in{\mathfrak{E}},

so that (b) is satisfied. Finally note that (

) implies that

\|B_{n}f\|=\|T_{n}Af\|\nearrow\|L(A,B)Af\|=\|Bf\|,\quad f\in{\mathfrak{E}},

so that (c) is satisfied.
Thus,
B
is almost dominated by
A
.

(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii) See Lemma
5.1
.
∎

The following definition finds its inspiration in a similar notion
which is current in measure theory.

Definition 6.5
.

Let
{\mathfrak{E}}
,
{\mathfrak{H}}
, and

It is straightforward to characterize the property

.

Let

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii)
Assume that

.

\Rightarrow
(i) Assume that

(ii)

.

7.
Almost domination and the Radon-Nikodym derivative

Let
{\mathfrak{E}}
,
{\mathfrak{H}}
, and
{\mathfrak{K}}
be Hilbert spaces and let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})

and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
.
Let
L(A,B)
be the corresponding operator range relation defined in (
3.7
).
If
B
is dominated or almost dominated by
A
,
then there is a factorization of
B
with respect to
A
, which gives
the notion of the Radon-Nikodym derivative
in the abstract setting of the operator range relation
L(A,B)
.

Observe the following straightforward remarks.
Let
L(A,B)
be an operator range relation as in (
3.7
) and
recall that
L(A,B)
is equal to the quotient
BA^{-1}
.
In the case that
L(A,B)
is an operator, one may write

(7.1)

Bf=L(A,B)Af\quad\mbox{for all}\quad f\in{\mathfrak{E}}.

If also
L(A,B)^{**}
is an operator, then it follows from

L(A,B)=\{\{Af,Bf\}:\,f\in{\mathfrak{E}}\}\subset L(A,B)^{**},

that one may write

(7.2)

Bf=L(A,B)^{**}Af\quad\mbox{for all}\quad f\in{\mathfrak{E}}.

Note that in this identity only the reduced part of the pair
A
and
B
plays a role.

First the case of domination will be considered.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

B
is dominated by
A
;

(ii)

B=CA
holds for some bounded linear operator
C
from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
;

(iii)

B=CA
holds for some closed bounded linear operator
C
from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
.

If one of these conditions is satisfied, then
L(A,B)
is a bounded linear operator
and
B=L(A,B)A
. Moreover,
if
B=CA
holds for some bounded linear operator
C
from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
,
then
L(A,B)\subset C
; and if
B=CA
holds for some closed bounded linear operator

C
from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
, then
L(A,B)^{**}\subset C
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) It follows from Lemma
6.2
that
L(A,B)

is a bounded linear operator. Hence it follows from (
7.1
) that (ii) holds.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii) To see this replace
C
in
B=CA
by its closure
C^{**}
.

(iii
\Rightarrow
(i) This is clear.

These equivalent statements imply that

L(A,B)
is a bounded linear operator and
it follows from (
3.7
) that
B=L(A,B)A
.
Therefore, if
B=CA
holds for some bounded linear operator

C
from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
, then
L(A,B)\subset C
and, if in addition
C
is closed,
it follows that
L(A,B)^{**}\subset C
.
∎

Notice that the Radon-Nikodym derivative in the following definition
satisfies the minimality property expressed in Lemma
7.1
.

Definition 7.2
.

Let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
and
assume that
B
is dominated by
A
.
Then the

R(A,B)

of

with respect to

is the bounded closed
operator
L(A,B)^{**}
from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
.

For an illustration of such a Radon-Nikodym derivative,
return to the inequalities
A^{*}A\leq A^{*}A+B^{*}B
and
B^{*}B\leq A^{*}A+B^{*}B
.
These inequalities imply that
there are
C_{A}\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and
C_{B}\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
,
such that the identities in (
4.2
)
hold, and they are unique when (
4.4
) is assumed.
It is clear from Definition
6.1
that
A
and
B
are dominated by
the operator
(A^{*}A+B^{*}B)^{\frac{1}{2}}
, so that each of the
following relations from
{\mathfrak{E}}
to
{\mathfrak{H}}
and from
{\mathfrak{E}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
, respectively,

L((A^{*}A+B^{*}B)^{{\frac{1}{2}}},A)\quad\mbox{and}\quad L((A^{*}A+B^{*}B)^{{\frac{1}{2}}},B)

is not only regular, but (the graph of) a bounded operator.
Recall that
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
are the reduction of the pair
A
and
B
;
see (

) and Lemma

.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
,
and let
C_{A}
and
C_{B}
be the uniquely defined operators
satisfying (

) and (

).
Then the Radon-Nikodym derivatives of

and

with respect to

\begin{array}[]{c}R((A^{*}A+B^{*}B)^{{\frac{1}{2}}},A)=C_{A_{0}},\quad R((A^{*}A+B^{*}B)^{{\frac{1}{2}}},B)=C_{B_{0}},\end{array}

Since the operators

and

satisfy the identities (

∎

Next, the notion of almost domination in the general case will be taken up again.

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})

(i)

(ii)

B=CA

C
from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
;

(iii)

B=CA

C
from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}

If one of these conditions is satisfied, then
L(A,B)
is a closable linear operator
such that
B=L(A,B)A
. Moreover,
if
B=CA
holds for some closable linear operator
C
from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
,
then
L(A,B)\subset C
and if
B=CA
holds for some closed linear operator

C
from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii)
Assume that

.
By Theorem

In particular, it follows from (

(ii)
\Rightarrow

It follows from

It will be helpful to compare the results in Lemma
7.3

and Theorem
7.7
, together
with Theorem
4.4
, with the following construction known
from measure theory.
Let
(\mu,\nu)
be a pair of finite positive measures.
Then
\mu
and
\nu
are absolutely continuous
with respect to the sum measure
\rho=\mu+\nu
:
\mu\ll\rho
,
\nu\ll\rho
.
This gives rise to the existence of the corresponding
Radon-Nikodym derivatives
f=\frac{d\mu}{d\rho}

and
g=\frac{d\nu}{d\rho}
and in this case

(7.5)

f+g=1\quad\rho\text{-a.e.}

If, in addition,
\nu\ll\mu
with the Radon-Nikodym derivative
h=\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}
,
then
\nu\ll\mu\ll\rho
implies that

g=\frac{d\nu}{d\rho}=\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}\,\frac{d\mu}{d\rho}=hf\quad\rho\text{-a.e.}

Since
\rho=\mu+\nu
and
\nu\ll\mu
, one has also
\rho\ll\mu
.
Consequently, one has
f>0

\rho
-a.e. (
\Leftrightarrow

\mu
-a.e.) and
thus, in fact, the Radon-Nikodym derivative
h
is given by

(7.6)

\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}=\frac{g}{f}\quad\mu\text{-a.e.}

Remark 7.8
.

The concept of Radon-Nikodym derivative given in Definitions
7.2
and
7.5

is applicable and uniquely determined for general operator range relations,
which are regular (i.e. closable operators).
Indeed, by Theorem
2.8
the operator
T
has the representation
T=L(A,B)
and
if
T={\rm ran\,}Z
for some other operator

,
then Lemma
2.4
shows that there exists a bounded and boundedly invertible
operator

such that

.
Then

L(A,B)={\rm ran\,}c(A,B)={\rm ran\,}ZW={\rm ran\,}Z

and taking closures leads to
{\rm\overline{ran}\,}Z=R(A,B)
.

8.
Lebesgue type decompositions for pairs of bounded linear operators

Let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
be bounded linear operators.
In this section it will be shown that there exist Lebesgue type decompositions

B=B_{1}+B_{2}
such that
B_{1}
is almost dominated by
A

and
B_{2}
is singular with respect to
A
. The main idea is to go back to the
corresponding operator range relation
L(A,B)
and to use the
Lebesgue type decompositions of
L(A,B)
; cf.
[
7
]
.

Definition 8.1
.

Let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and

B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
be bounded linear operators
and let
P
be the orthogonal projection onto
{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)^{\perp}
.
The regular part
B_{\rm{reg}}
and the singular part
B_{\rm{sing}}

are defined by

(8.1)

B_{\rm{reg}}=(I-P)B,\quad B_{\rm{sing}}=PB.

The corresponding decomposition

(8.2)

B=B_{\rm{reg}}+B_{\rm{sing}}.

is called the Lebesgue decomposition of
B
with respect to
A
.

Let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
be
as in Definition
8.1
.
Let
L(A,B)
from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
be defined as in (
3.7
)
and recall that

{\rm dom\,}L(A,B)^{*}={\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\rm mul\,}L(A,B)^{**}={\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)^{\perp};

cf. (
3.16
).
Then
the relation
L(A,B)
has the Lebesgue decomposition

(8.3)

L(A,B)=L(A,B)_{\rm{{reg}}}+L(A,B)_{\rm{{sing}}},

where the regular and singular components are given by

(8.4)

L(A,B)_{\rm{{reg}}}=(I-P)L(A,B),\quad L(A,B)_{\rm{{sing}}}=PL(A,B);

here
P
stands for the orthogonal projection from
{\mathfrak{K}}

onto
{\rm mul\,}L(A,B)^{**}={\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)^{\perp}
;
cf.
[
7
]
.
Via the decomposition (
8.3
)
one may now obtain the Lebesgue decomposition
of
B
with respect to
A
as in Definition
8.1
.

Theorem 8.2

(Lebesgue decomposition)
.

Let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and

B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
be bounded linear operators.
Then
B_{\rm{reg}}
is almost dominated by
A
,

B_{\rm{sing}}
is singular with respect to
A
, and

B
has the Lebesgue decomposition (
8.2
)
with respect to
A
.
The regular part
B_{\rm reg}
can be written as

(8.5)

B_{\rm reg}=R(A,B_{\rm reg})A,

where
R(A,B_{\rm reg})
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of

B_{\rm{reg}}
with respect to
A
:

(8.6)

R(A,B_{\rm reg})=L(A,B_{\rm reg})^{**}.

In fact, if
L(A,B)
is given by (
8.3
) and (
8.4
), then

(8.7)

L(A,B)_{\rm{reg}}=L(A,B_{\rm reg}),\quad L(A,B)_{\rm{sing}}=L(A,B_{\rm sing}).

Proof.

The decomposition (
8.2
) follows from (
8.1
).
It follows from (
8.4
)
that
L(A,B)_{\rm{{reg}}}
and
L(A,B)_{\rm{{sing}}}

have the representations

\begin{split}&L(A,B)_{\rm{reg}}=\{\,\{Af,(I-P)Bf\}:\,f\in{\mathfrak{E}}\,\}=\{\,\{Af,B_{\rm{reg}}f\}:\,f\in{\mathfrak{E}}\,\},\\
&L(A,B)_{\rm{sing}}=\{\,\{Af,PBf\}:\,f\in{\mathfrak{E}}\,\}=\{\,\{Af,B_{\rm{sing}}f\}:\,f\in{\mathfrak{E}}\,\},\end{split}

which give (
8.7
).
Since the relation
L(A,B)_{\rm{reg}}
is regular,
it follows from Theorem
6.4

that
B_{\rm{reg}}
is almost dominated by
A
.
Likewise,
since the relation
L(A,B)_{\rm{sing}}
is singular,
it follows from Theorem
6.6

that
B_{\rm{sing}}
is singular with respect to
A
.

The statements about the Radon-Nikodym derivative in (
8.5
)
and (
8.6
) follow from Theorem
7.4
.
∎

The Lebesgue decomposition in (
8.2
)
is an example of a so-called Lebesgue type decomposition.

Definition 8.3
.

Let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
be bounded operators.
The operator
B
is said to have a Lebesgue type
decomposition with respect to
A
,
if
B=B_{1}+B_{2}
where
B_{1},B_{2}\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
have the properties:

(a)

{\rm ran\,}B_{1}\perp{\rm ran\,}B_{2}
;

(b)

B_{1}
is almost dominated by
A
;

(c)

B_{2}
is singular with respect to
A
.

Let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})

as in Definition
8.3
.
Let the linear relation
L(A,B)
from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
be defined by (
3.7
).
According to
[
7
]
the Lebesgue type decompositions of
L(A,B)

are in one-to-one correspondence
with the closed linear subspaces
{\mathfrak{L}}\subset{\mathfrak{K}}
such that

(8.8)

{\mathfrak{L}}\subset{\rm\overline{dom}\,}L(A,B)^{*}\setminus{\rm dom\,}L(A,B)^{*},

which satisfy the condition

(8.9)

{\rm clos\,}({\mathfrak{L}}^{\perp}\cap{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B))={\mathfrak{L}}^{\perp}\cap{\rm clos\,}{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B).

Define the closed linear subspace
{\mathfrak{M}}
by

(8.10)

{\mathfrak{M}}={\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)^{\perp}\oplus{\mathfrak{L}},

and let
P_{\mathfrak{M}}
be the orthogonal projection from
{\mathfrak{K}}
onto
{\mathfrak{M}}
.
Then the corresponding Lebesgue type decomposition of
L(A,B)
is given by

(8.11)

L(A,B)=L(A,B)_{1}+L(A,B)_{2},

where the regular and singular components are given by

(8.12)

L(A,B)_{1}=(I-P_{\mathfrak{M}})L(A,B),\quad L(A,B)_{2}=P_{\mathfrak{M}}L(A,B),

respectively.
Via the decomposition (
8.11
) one may now obtain the
Lebesgue type decompositions of
B
with respect to
A
as in Definition
8.3
.

Theorem 8.4
.

Let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and

B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
be bounded linear operators.
Then the Lebesgue type decompositions of
B

with respect to
A
are in one-to-one correspondence
with the closed linear subspaces
{\mathfrak{L}}\subset{\mathfrak{K}}
in (
8.8
)
which satisfy the condition (
8.9
).
In particular, the corresponding Lebesgue type decomposition is given by

B=B_{1}+B_{2},\quad B_{1}=(I-P_{\mathfrak{M}})B,\quad B_{2}=P_{\mathfrak{M}}B,

where
{\mathfrak{M}}
is as in (
8.10
) and
P_{\mathfrak{M}}

is the orthogonal projection from
{\mathfrak{K}}
onto
{\mathfrak{M}}
, while

B_{1}
is almost dominated by
A
and

B_{2}
is singular with respect to
A
.
The regular part
B_{1}
can be written as

B_{1}=R(A,B_{1})A,

where
R(A,B_{1})
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of

B_{1}
with respect to
A
:

R(A,B_{1})=L(A,B_{1})^{**}.

In fact,
L(A,B)
is given by (
8.11
) and (
8.12
) precisely, when

L(A,B)_{\rm{1}}=L(A,B_{1}),\quad L(A,B)_{2}=L(A,B_{2}).

Proof.

Consider the linear relation
L(A,B)
from
{\mathfrak{H}}
to
{\mathfrak{K}}
defined by (
3.7
).

First it is shown that every Lebesgue type decomposition
of the linear relation
L(A,B)
(in the sense of
[
7
]
)
generates a Lebesgue type decomposition of
B

with respect to
A
as in Definition
8.3
.
To see this let
{\mathfrak{L}}\subset{\rm\overline{dom}\,}L(A,B)^{*}\setminus{\rm dom\,}L(A,B)

be a linear subspace
which satisfies (
8.9
) and let
{\mathfrak{M}}
be as defined
in (
8.10
) with the corresponding
orthogonal projection
P_{\mathfrak{M}}
onto
{\mathfrak{M}}
.
According to
[
7
, Theorem 5.4]
the formula

L(A,B)=(I-P_{\mathfrak{M}})L(A,B)+P_{\mathfrak{M}}L(A,B)

determines a Lebesgue type decomposition of
L(A,B)
,
where
(I-P_{\mathfrak{M}})L(A,B)
is the regular part and

P_{\mathfrak{M}}L(A,B)
is the singular part generated uniquely by the subspace
{\mathfrak{L}}
.
From the representation of the regular part

(I-P_{\mathfrak{M}})L(A,B)=\{\,\{Af,(I-P_{\mathfrak{M}})Bf\}:\,f\in{\mathfrak{E}}\,\}

and Theorem
6.4
it follows that
B_{1}=(I-P_{\mathfrak{M}})B

is almost dominated by
A
.
Likewise from the representation of the singular part

P_{\mathfrak{M}}L(A,B)=\{\,\{Af,P_{\mathfrak{M}}Bf\}:\,f\in{\mathfrak{E}}\,\}

and Theorem
6.6
it follows that
B_{2}=P_{\mathfrak{M}}B

is singular with respect to
A
.
Hence, the identity
B=B_{1}+B_{2}
is a Lebesgue type decomposition of
B
with respect to
A

in the sense of Definition
8.3
.

Conversely, assume that
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
,
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
,
and that
B
has a Lebesgue type decomposition as in Definition
8.3
.
Then it is clear that the corresponding relations satisfy

(8.13)

L(A,B)=L(A,B_{1})+L(A,B_{2}),

where, due to Theorem
6.4
and Theorem
6.6
,
the relation
L(A,B_{1})
is regular and the relation
L(A,B_{2})
is singular.
Hence (
8.13
) is a Lebesgue type decomposition for
L(A,B)
.
Again by
[
7
, Theorem 5.4]
there exists a linear subspace
{\mathfrak{L}}
, such that
(
8.8
) and (
8.9
) are satisfied, and a subspace
{\mathfrak{M}}
given by (
8.10
)
such that

(8.14)

\begin{split}L(A,B_{1})&=(I-P_{{\mathfrak{M}}})L(A,B)=L(A,(I-P_{{\mathfrak{M}}})B),\\
L(A,B_{2})&=P_{{\mathfrak{M}}}L(A,B)=L(A,P_{{\mathfrak{M}}}B).\end{split}

Thanks to the first identities in (
8.14
), for every
h\in{\mathfrak{E}}

there exists
f\in{\mathfrak{E}}
, such that

Ah=Af,\quad(I-P_{{\mathfrak{M}}})Bh=B_{1}f.

Thus
f=h+\varphi
for some
\varphi\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}A
. Since
L(A,B_{1})
is regular,
it is an operator and hence
{\rm mul\,}L(A,B_{1})=B_{1}({{\rm ker\,}\,}A)=0
; cf. (
3.9
).
This shows that
B_{1}\varphi=0
, and thus
(I-P_{{\mathfrak{M}}})Bh=B_{1}h
.
Therefore,
(I-P_{{\mathfrak{M}}})B=B_{1}
and, consequently,
P_{{\mathfrak{M}}}B=B_{2}
.
This proves the one-to-one correspondence between the Lebesgue type decompositions
of
B=B_{1}+B_{2}
in Definition (
8.3
) and of
L(A,B)
in (
8.11
) and (
8.12
).
The one-to-one correspondence between the closed subspaces
{\mathfrak{L}}
satisfying the conditions
(
8.8
) and (
8.9
) is obtained from
[
7
, Theorem 5.4]
.

The statement about the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
B_{1}
with respect to
A

follows from Theorem
6.4
.
∎

For any Lebesgue type decomposition of
B
with respect to
A
, the part
B_{1}
,
which is almost dominated by
A
, is, in fact, dominated by the regular part
B_{\rm reg}
.

Corollary 8.5
.

Let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})
and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
.
Let
B=B_{1}+B_{2}
be a Lebesgue type decomposition of
B
, then

\|B_{1}h\|\leq\|B_{\rm reg}h\|,\quad h\in{\mathfrak{E}}.

Proof.

Let
B=B_{1}+B_{2}
be a Lebesgue type decomposition of
B

with respect to
A
. Then as in the proof of Theorem
8.4

one finds that

B_{1}=(I-P_{{\mathfrak{M}}})B=(I-P_{\mathfrak{M}})(I-P)B=(I-P_{\mathfrak{M}})B_{\rm reg},

where it was used that
{\rm ran\,}P\subset{\rm ran\,}P_{{\mathfrak{M}}}
; cf. (
8.10
).
∎

The uniqueness of Lebesgue type decompositions of
B

with respect to
A
in Definition
8.3

can be characterized as follows.

Corollary 8.6
.

Let
A\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{H}})

and
B\in{\mathbf{B}}({\mathfrak{E}},{\mathfrak{K}})
.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

B
admits a unique Lebesgue type decomposition with respect to
A
;

(ii)

L(A,B)
admits a unique Lebesgue type decomposition;

(iii)

{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)
is closed;

(iv)

B_{\rm reg}
is dominated by
A
;

(v)

the Radon-Nikodym derivative
R(A,B_{\rm reg})
is a bounded operator.

In this case, all Lebesgue type decompositions of
B
with respect to
A

coincide with the Lebesgue decomposition of

B=B_{\rm reg}+B_{\rm sing}
in Theorem
8.2
.

Proof.

(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii)
The Lebesgue type decompositions of
B=B_{1}+B_{2}
with respect to
A

correspond to the Lebesgue type decompositions
of
L(A,B)=T_{1}+T_{2}
via Theorem
8.4
.
Hence
B
has a unique Lebesgue type decomposition with respect to
A

if and only
L(A,B)
has a unique Lebesgue type decomposition.

(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii)
By
[
7
, Theorem 6.1]

L(A,B)
has a unique Lebesgue type decomposition
if and only if
{\rm dom\,}L(A,B)^{*}
is closed, i.e.,
{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)
is closed; cf. (
3.14
).

(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iv) Again by
[
7
, Theorem 6.1]

L(A,B)
has a unique Lebesgue type decomposition if and only if

L(A,B)_{\rm{reg}}=L(A,B_{\rm reg})
is bounded; cf. Theorem
8.2
.
Now
L(A,B_{\rm reg})
is bounded if and only if

B_{\rm reg}
is dominated by
A
; cf Lemma
6.2
.

(iv)
\Leftrightarrow
(v) This follows from Corollary
7.6
.

The last statement is clear from (
8.8
), since if
{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)={\rm dom\,}L(A,B)^{*}
is closed,
then
{\mathfrak{L}}=\{0\}
and
{\mathfrak{M}}
in (
8.10
) coincides with
{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)^{\perp}
.
∎
