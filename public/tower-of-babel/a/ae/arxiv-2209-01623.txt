Computing Generalized Convolutions Faster Than Brute Force

Abstract
In this paper, we consider a general notion of convolution. Let D D be a finite domain and let D n D^{n} be the set of n n -length vectors (tuples) of D D . Let f : D × D → D f\colon D\times D\to D be a function and let ⊕ f \oplus_{f} be a coordinate-wise application of f f . The f f - Convolution of two functions g , h : D n → { − M , … , M } g,h\colon D^{n}\to\{-M,\ldots,M\} is ( g ⊛ f h ) ​ ( 𝐯 ) ≔ ∑ 𝐯 g , 𝐯 h ∈ D n s.t. 𝐯 = 𝐯 g ⊕ f 𝐯 h g ⁡ ( 𝐯 g ) ⋅ h ⁡ ( 𝐯 h ) (g\mathbin{\circledast_{f}}h)(\mathbf{v})\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v}_{g},\mathbf{v}_{h}\in D^{n}\\ \text{s.t. }\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}_{g}\oplus_{f}\mathbf{v}_{h}\end{subarray}}g(\mathbf{v}_{g})\cdot h(\mathbf{v}_{h}) for every 𝐯 ∈ D n \mathbf{v}\in D^{n} . This problem generalizes many fundamental convolutions such as Subset Convolution, XOR Product, Covering Product or Packing Product, etc. For arbitrary function f f and domain D D we can compute f f - Convolution via brute-force enumeration in 𝒪 ~ ​ ( | D | 2 ​ n ⋅ polylog ⁡ ( M ) ) \widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(|D|^{2n}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M)) time. Our main result is an improvement over this naive algorithm. We show that f f - Convolution can be computed exactly in 𝒪 ~ ​ ( ( c ⋅ | D | 2 ) n ⋅ polylog ⁡ ( M ) ) \widetilde{\mathcal{O}}((c\cdot|D|^{2})^{n}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M)) for constant c ≔ 3 / 4 c\coloneqq 3/4 when D D has even cardinality. Our main observation is that a cyclic partition of a function f : D × D → D f\colon D\times D\to D can be used to speed up the computation of f f - Convolution , and we show that an appropriate cyclic partition exists for every f f . Furthermore, we demonstrate that a single entry of the f f - Convolution can be computed more efficiently. In this variant, we are given two functions g , h : D n → { − M , … , M } g,h\colon D^{n}\to\{-M,\ldots,M\} alongside with a vector 𝐯 ∈ D n \mathbf{v}\in D^{n} and the task of the f f - Query problem is to compute integer ( g ⊛ f h ) ​ ( 𝐯 ) (g\mathbin{\circledast_{f}}h)(\mathbf{v}) . This is a generalization of the well-known Orthogonal Vectors problem. exact and parameterized algorithms. The most prominent example is a subset

[
22
,
36
]

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(2^{n}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M))

[

used by Björklund et al.

.

integers on the input.

Researchers considered a plethora of other variants of convolutions, such as:
Cover Product, XOR Product, Packing Product, Generalized Subset Convolution, or
Discriminantal Subset
Convolution
[

,
8
,

,

These subroutines are crucial ingredients in the design of efficient algorithms

or Bin

,

These convolutions are especially useful for dynamic programming algorithms on
tree decompositions and occur naturally during join operations (e.g.,

[

,
19
,
33
]
). Usually, in the process of algorithm
design, the researcher needs to design a different type of convolution from
scratch to solve each of these problems. Often this is a highly technical and
laborious task. Ideally, we would like to have a single tool that can be used as
a blackbox in all of these cases. This motivates the following ambitious goal in
this paper:

Goal:
Unify convolution procedures under one general umbrella.

Towards this goal, we consider the problem of computing
f
-
Generalized
Convolution
(
f
-
Convolution
) introduced by van Rooij
[
33
]
. Let
D
be a
finite domain and let

be the

D
. Let

f\colon D\times D\to D
be an arbitrary function and let
\oplus_{f}
be
a coordinate-wise application of the function

.

2

We provide a formal

\oplus_{f}
in Section

For two functions
g,h\colon D^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}

the
f
-

(g\mathbin{\circledast_{f}}h)\colon D^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}

(g\mathbin{\circledast_{f}}h)(\mathbf{v})\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v}_{g},\mathbf{v}_{h}\in D^{n}\\
\text{s.t. }\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}_{g}\oplus_{f}\mathbf{v}_{h}\end{subarray}}g(\mathbf{v}_{g})\cdot h(\mathbf{v}_{h}).

Here we consider a standard

In the

are given as an input

(g\mathbin{\circledast_{f}}h)

f
-

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(|D|^{n}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M))
. Such a result for arbitrary

So far, however, researchers have focused on
characterizing functions

-
Convolution

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(|D|^{n}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M))

In
[
33

r\in{\rm Nature}
the function

is defined as either (i) standard
addition:
f(x,y)\coloneqq x+y
, or (ii) addition with a maximum:
f(x,y)\coloneqq\min(x+y,r-1)
, or (iii) addition modulo
r
, or
(iv) maximum:
f(x,y)\coloneqq\max(x,y)
. Van Rooij
[
33
]
showed that for
these special cases the
f
-

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(|D|^{n}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M))

to differ between coordinates.
A recent result regarding generalized Discrete Fourier Transform
[

[

-

is a finite-group operation and
\omega
is the exponent of the
currently fastest matrix-multiplication algorithms.

3

3

This observation was brought to our attention by Jesper Nederlof
[

]
.

To the best of our knowledge

Nevertheless, for an arbitrary function

-

is a straightforward quadratic time

Question 1:

Is the naive
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(|D|^{2n}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M))
algorithm for
f
-
Convolution
optimal?

Similar questions were studied from the point of view of the Fine-Grained
Complexity. In that setting the focus is on convolutions with sparse
representations, where the input size is only the size of the support of the
functions
g
and
h
. It is conjectured that even subquadratic algorithms are
highly unlikely for these representations
[
18
,
24
]
.
However, these lower bounds do not answer Question
1
, because
they are highly dependent on the sparsity of the input.

1.1
Our Results

We provide a positive answer to Question
1

and show an exponential improvement (in
n
) over a naive

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(|D|^{2n}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M))
algorithm for every function
f
.

Theorem 1

(Generalized Convolution)
.

Let
D
be a finite set and
f\colon D\times D\to D
.
There is an algorithm for
f
-
Convolution
with the following running time

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\left(\frac{3}{4}\cdot|D|^{2}\right)^{n}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M)\right)
when
\lvert D\rvert
is even, or

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\left(\frac{3}{4}\cdot|D|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\cdot|D|\right)^{n}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M)\right)
when
\lvert D\rvert
is odd.

Observe that the running time obtained by Theorem
1
improves
upon the brute-force for every
|D|\geq 2
.
Our technique
works in a more general setting when
g\colon L^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}

and
h\colon R^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}
and
f\colon L\times R\to T
for arbitrary
domains
L,R
and
T
(see Section
2
for the exact
running time dependence).

Our Technique: Cyclic Partition.

Now, we briefly sketch the idea behind the proof of Theorem
1
.
We say that a function is
k
-cyclic if it can be represented as an addition
modulo
k
(after relabeling the entries of the domain and image). These
functions are somehow simple, because as observed in
[
33
,
32
]

f
-
Convolution
can be computed in
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k^{n}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M))
time if
f
is
k
-cyclic. In a nutshell, our idea
is to partition the function
f\colon D\times D\to D
into
cyclic functions and compute the convolution on these parts independently.

More formally, a cyclic minor
of the function
f\colon D\times D\to D
is a (combinatorial) rectangle

A\times B
with
A,B\subseteq D
and a number
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f
restricted to
A,B
is a
k
-cyclic function. The cost of the cyclic minor

(A,B,k)
is

\mathrm{cost}(A,B)\coloneqq k
. A cyclic
partition is a set
\{(A_{1},B_{1},k_{1}),\ldots,(A_{m},B_{m},k_{m})\}
of cyclic minors
such that for every
(a,b)\in D\times D
there exists a unique
i\in[m]
with
(a,b)\in A_{i}\times B_{i}
. The cost of the cyclic partition
\mathcal{P}=\{(A_{1},B_{1},k_{1}),\ldots,(A_{m},B_{m},k_{m})\}
is
\mathrm{cost}(\mathcal{P})\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{i}
. See Figure
1.1
for an example of a cyclic partition.

Figure 1.1:
Left figure illustrates exemplar function
f\colon D\times D\to D
over domain
D\coloneqq\{a,b,c,d\}
. We highlighted a cyclic
partition with red, blue, yellow and blue colors. Each color represents a
different minor of
f
. On the right figure we
demonstrate that the red-highlighted minor can be represented as addition modulo
3
(after
relabeling
a\mapsto 0
,
b\mapsto 1
and
c\mapsto 2
). Hence
the red minor has cost
3
. The reader can further verify that green and blue minors have
cost
2
and yellow minor has cost
1
, hence the cost of that particular
partition is
3+2+2+1=8
.

Our first technical contribution is an algorithm to compute
f
-
Convolution
when the cost of a cyclic partition is small.

Lemma 2

(Algorithm for
f
-
Convolution
)
.

Let
D
be an arbitrary finite set,
f\colon D\times D\to D
and let
\mathcal{P}
be the cyclic partition of
f
. Then there exists
an algorithm which given
g,h\colon D^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}
computes

(g\mathbin{\circledast_{f}}h)
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}((\mathrm{cost}(\mathcal{P})^{n}+|D|^{n})\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M))
time.

The idea behind the proof of Lemma
2
is as follows. Based on the partition

\mathcal{P}
, for any pair of vectors
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}\in D^{n}
,
we can define a type
{\overline{p}}\in[m]^{n}
such that
(\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{i})\in A_{{\overline{p}}_{i}}\times B_{{\overline{p}}_{i}}

for every
i\in[n]
. Our main idea is to go over each type
{\overline{p}}
and
compute the sum in the definition of
f
-
Convolution
only for pairs
(\mathbf{v}_{g},\mathbf{v}_{h})
that
have type
{\overline{p}}
. In order to do this, first we select the vectors
\mathbf{v}_{g}

and
\mathbf{v}_{h}
that are compatible with this type
{\overline{p}}
. For instance, consider
the example in Figure
1.1
. Whenever
{\overline{p}}_{i}
refers to,
say, the red-colored minor, then we consider
\mathbf{v}_{g}
only if its
i
-th coordinate is in
\{b,c,d\}

and consider
\mathbf{v}_{h}
only if its
i
-th coordinate is in
\{b,d\}
. After computing all
these vectors
\mathbf{v}_{g}
and
\mathbf{v}_{h}
, we can transform them according to the cyclic
minor at each coordinate. Continuing our example, as the red-colored minor is

3
-cyclic, we can represent the
i
-th coordinate of
\mathbf{v}_{g}
and
\mathbf{v}_{h}
as

\{0,1,2\}
and then the problem reduces to addition modulo
3
at that coordinate. Therefore, using
the algorithm of van Rooij
[
33
]
for cyclic convolution we can handle all
pairs of type
{\overline{p}}
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}((\prod_{i=1}^{n}k_{{\overline{p}}_{i}})\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M))
time.
As we go over all
m^{n}
types
{\overline{p}}
the sum of
m^{n}
terms is

\sum_{{\overline{p}}\in[m]^{n}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}k_{{\overline{p}}_{i}}\right)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{i}\right)^{n}=\mathrm{cost}(\mathcal{P})^{n}.

Hence, the overall running time is
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\mathrm{cost}(\mathcal{P})^{n}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M))
. This running time evaluation
ignores the generation of the vectors given as input for the cyclic convolution
algorithm. The efficient computation of these vectors is nontrivial and requires
further techniques that we explain in Section
3
.

It remains to provide the low-cost cyclic partition of an arbitrary function
f
.

Lemma 3
.

For any finite set
D
and any function
f\colon D\times D\to D
there is a cyclic partition
\mathcal{P}
of
f

such that
\mathrm{cost}(\mathcal{P})\leq\frac{3}{4}|D|^{2}
when
\lvert D\rvert
is even, or

\mathrm{cost}(\mathcal{P})\leq\frac{3}{4}|D|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}|D|
when
\lvert D\rvert
is
odd.

For the sake of presentation let us assume that
|D|
is even.
In order to show Lemma
3
, we partition
D
into pairs

A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
where
k\coloneqq|D|/2
and consider the
restrictions of
f
to
A_{j}\times D
one by one.
Intuitively, we partition the
D\times D
table describing
f
into pairs
of rows and give a bound on the cost of each pair.
This partition allows us to encode
f
on
A_{j}\times D
as a directed graph
G
with
|D|

edges and
|D|
vertices.
We observe that directed cycles and directed paths can be represented as
cyclic minors. Our goal is to partition graph
G
into such subgraphs in a way that the total cost of the resulting cyclic
partition is small.
Following this argument,
the proof of Lemma
3
becomes a graph-theoretic analysis.
The proof of Lemma
3
is
included in Section
4
.
We also give an example which suggests that the constant
\frac{3}{4}
in
\cref
lem:cost-bound cannot be improved further
while using the partition of

into arbitrary pairs
(see
\cref
lem:existenceOfMinor:tightness).

Our method applies for more
general functions
f\colon L\times R\to T
, where
domains
L,R,T
can be different and have arbitrary cardinality.
We note that a weaker variant of Lemma
3

in which the guarantee is
\mathrm{cost}(\mathcal{P}_{f})\leq\frac{7}{8}|D|^{2}
is easier to
attain (see Section
4
).

Efficient Algorithm for Convolution Query.

Our next contribution is an efficient algorithm to query a single value of

f
-
Convolution
. In the

Query
problem, the input is

and a single vector
\mathbf{v}\in D^{n}
. The task is to compute a value

(g\mathbin{\circledast_{f}}h)(\mathbf{v})
. Observe that this task generalizes
4
4

4

It is a special
case with

,
\mathbf{v}=0^{n}
and

the fundamental

.

Theorem 4

(Convolution Query)
.

For any finite set
D
and function
f\colon D\times D\to D
there is a

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(|D|^{\omega\cdot n/2}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M))
time algorithm for the

-
Query
problem.

Here

is the time needed to multiply two

{\{-M,\ldots,M\}}
and currently
\omega\in[2,2.372)

[
2
,
21
]
.
Note, that under the assumption that two matrices can be multiplied in the linear in the input
time (i.e.,
\omega=2
) then Theorem
4
runs in the nearly-optimal

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(|D|^{n}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M))
time. Theorem
4
is significantly faster
than Theorem
1
even when we plug-in the naive algorithm for matrix

\omega=3
). The proof of Theorem
4

is inspired by an interpretation of the

-

1.2
Related Work

Arguably, the problem of computing the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) is the
prime example of convolution-type problems in computer science. Cooley and
Tukey
[
17
]
proposed the fast algorithm to compute DFT. Later, Beth

[
4
]
and Clausen
[
16
]
initiated the study
of generalized DFTs whose goal has been to obtain a fast algorithm for
DFT where the underlying group is arbitrary.
After a long line of
works (see
[
30
]
for the survey), the currently best algorithm for
generalized DFT concerning group
G
runs in
\mathcal{O}(|G|^{\omega/2+\epsilon})

operations for every
\epsilon>0

[
31
]
.

A similar technique to ours was introduced by Björklund et
al.
[
9
]
. The paper gave a characterization of lattices that
admit a fast zeta transform and a fast Möbius transform. Their paper used the
notion of
covering pairs
, which is similar to cyclic partitions used in
this paper but with a completely different goal.

From the lower-bounds perspective to the best of our knowledge only
a naive
\Omega(|D|^{n})
lower bound is known for
f
-
Convolution
(as
this is the output size). We note that known lower bounds for different
convolution-type problems, such as

(\min,+)
-convolution
[
18
,
24
]
,

(\min,\max)
-convolution
[
13
]
, min-witness
convolution
[
25
]
, convolution-3SUM
[
14
]
or even skew-convolution
[
12
]

cannot be easily adapted to
f
-
Convolution
as the hardness of these problems comes
primarily from the ring operations.

The Orthogonal Vector problem is related to the
f
-
Query
problem. In the
Orthogonal Vector problem we are given two sets of
n
vectors
A,B\subseteq\{0,1\}^{d}
and the task is to decide if there is a pair
a\in A
,
b\in B

such that
a\cdot b=0
. In
[
37
]
it was shown that no
n^{2-\epsilon}\cdot 2^{o(d)}
algorithm for Orthogonal Vectors is possible for any
\epsilon>0

assuming SETH
[
35
]
. The currently best algorithm for Orthogonal
Vectors run in
n^{2-1/\mathcal{O}(\log(d)/\log(n))}

time
[
1
,
15
]
,
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot 2^{cd})
for
some constant
c<0.5

[
29
]
, or
\mathcal{O}(|{\downarrow}A|+|{\downarrow}B|)

[
7
]
(where
|{\downarrow}F|
is the total
number of vectors whose support is a subset of the support of input vectors).

Throughout the paper, we use Iverson bracket notation, where for the logic expression

Let

,

and

We extend the definition of such an arbitrary function

\lvert V_{\textup{{mid}}}(G)\rvert+\mathrm{Defi}(G)=\mathrm{LHS}(G_{m})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathrm{LHS}(G_{i})-\mathrm{LHS}(G_{i-1})\right)\leq m=\lvert E(G)\rvert.

From now, we focus on the proof of
\cref
eq:psi. For every
v\in V(G)
and
i\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
, let
\mathrm{defi}_{i}(v)
be the
deficiency of vertex
v
in graph
G_{i}
. Next, for every
v\in V(G)
and
i\in[m]
, we define

\Delta_{i}(v)\coloneqq\mathrm{defi}_{i}(v)-\mathrm{defi}_{i-1}(v)+\llbracket v\in V_{\textup{{mid}}}(G_{i})\setminus V_{\textup{{mid}}}(G_{i-1})\rrbracket

Consider a step
i\in[m]
. Let
e_{i}=(s,t)
be an
i
th edge that starts at
a vertex
s
and terminates at a vertex

. It holds that

\displaystyle\lvert V_{\textup{{mid}}}(G_{i})\rvert+\mathrm{Defi}(G_{i})=\lvert V_{\textup{{mid}}}(G_{i-1})\rvert+\mathrm{Defi}(G_{i-1})+\Delta_{i}(s)+\Delta_{i}(t).

Therefore
\mathrm{LHS}(G_{i})-\mathrm{LHS}(G_{i-1})=\Delta_{i}(s)+\Delta_{i}(t)
and to
establish
\cref
eq:psi it is enough to show that
\Delta_{i}(s)\leq 1
and
\Delta_{i}(t)\leq 0
.

Claim 28
.

It holds that
\Delta_{i}(s)\leq 1
.

{claimproof}

We consider two cases depending on whether
u
became a middle vertex.
If it happened that
s\in V_{\textup{{mid}}}(G_{i})\setminus V_{\textup{{mid}}}(G_{i-1})
, then

s\in V_{\textup{{snk}}}(G_{i-1})
which means that

s
has more incoming than outgoing edges in
G_{i-1}
. Hence

\mathrm{defi}_{i-1}(s)=\mathrm{defi}_{i}(s)=0
and we conclude that
\Delta_{i}(s)=1
.

Otherwise
s\notin V_{\textup{{mid}}}(G_{i})\setminus V_{\textup{{mid}}}(G_{i-1})
.
Because the edge
e_{i}
starts at
s
,
the deficiency of
s
can increase by at most
1
.
Hence, by
(\mathrm{defi}_{i}(s)-\mathrm{defi}_{i-1}(s))\leq 1

we conclude that
\Delta_{i}(s)\leq 1
.

\claimqedhere

Finally, we consider the end vertex
t
of the edge
e_{i}
.

Claim 29
.

It holds that
\Delta_{i}(t)\leq 0
.

{claimproof}

We again distinguish two cases
depending on whether
t
became a middle vertex.
If
t\in V_{\textup{{mid}}}(G_{i})\setminus V_{\textup{{mid}}}(G_{i-1})
, then
t\in V_{\textup{{src}}}(G_{i-1})

and moreover,
t
has no incoming edges and the positive number of outgoing edges
in
G_{i-1}
. Therefore
\mathrm{defi}_{i}(t)=\mathrm{defi}_{i-1}(t)-1
which means that

\Delta_{i}(t)\leq 0
.

It remains to analyse the case when

.
Since the edge
e_{i}
ends at
t
, the deficiency of
t
cannot increase and
\mathrm{defi}_{i}(v)\leq\mathrm{defi}_{i-1}(v)
. This means that
\Delta_{i}(t)\leq 0
.

\claimqedhere

By
\cref
clm:structProp:start,clm:structProp:end,
it follows that
\Delta_{i}(s)+\Delta_{i}(t)\leq 1
.
This establishes
\cref
eq:psi and concludes the proof.
∎

5
Querying a Generalized Convolution

In this section, we prove Theorem
4
.
The main idea is to represent the
f
-
Query
problem as a matrix multiplication problem,
inspired by a graph interpretation of
f
-
Query
.

Let
D
be an arbitrary set and
f\colon D\times D\to D
. We assume
D
and

f
are fixed throughout this section. Let
g,h\colon D^{n}\to{\{-M,\ldots,M\}}
and
\mathbf{v}\in D^{n}
be a
f
-
Query
instance.
We use
\mathbf{a}\|\mathbf{b}
to denote the concatenation of
\mathbf{a}\in D^{m}
and
\mathbf{b}\in D^{k}
.
That is

(\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{m})\|(\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{k})=(\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{m},\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{k})
.
If we assume that
n
is even,
then, for a vector
\mathbf{v}\in D^{n}
, let

\mathbf{v}^{(\mathrm{high})},\mathbf{v}^{{(\mathrm{low})}}\in D^{n/2}
be the unique vectors such that

\mathbf{v}^{{(\mathrm{high})}}\|\mathbf{v}^{{(\mathrm{low})}}=\mathbf{v}
.
Indeed, to achieve this assumption let
n
be odd,
fix an arbitrary
d\in D
, and
define
\widetilde{g},\widetilde{h}\colon D^{n+1}\to{\{-M,\ldots,M\}}
as

\widetilde{g}(\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots\mathbf{u}_{n+1})=\llbracket\mathbf{u}_{n+1}=d\rrbracket\cdot g(\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots\mathbf{u}_{n})

and
\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots\mathbf{u}_{n+1})=\llbracket\mathbf{u}_{n+1}=d\rrbracket\cdot h(\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots\mathbf{u}_{n})
for all
\mathbf{u}\in D^{n+1}
.
It can be easily verified that

(g\mathbin{\circledast_{f}}h)(\mathbf{v})=(\widetilde{g}\mathbin{\circledast_{f}}\widetilde{h})(\mathbf{v}\|(f(d,d)))
.
Thus, we can solve the
f
-
Query
instance
\widetilde{g}
,
\widetilde{h}
and
\mathbf{v}\|(f(d,d))

and obtain the correct result.

We first provide the intuition behind the algorithm
and then formally show the existence.

Intuition.

We define a directed multigraph
G

where the vertices are partitioned into four layers

\textnormal{L}^{{(\mathrm{high})}}
,
\textnormal{L}^{{(\mathrm{low})}}
,
\textnormal{R}^{{(\mathrm{low})}}
, and
\textnormal{R}^{{(\mathrm{high})}}
.
Each of these sets consists of
|D|^{n/2}
vertices
representing every vector in
D^{n/2}
.
For ease of notation, we use the vectors to denote the associated vertices; furthermore, the intuition assumes
g
and
h
are non-negative.
The multigraph
G
contains the following edges:

•

g(\mathbf{w}\|\mathbf{x})
parallel edges from
\mathbf{w}\in D^{n/2}
in
\textnormal{L}^{{(\mathrm{high})}}

to
\mathbf{x}\in D^{n/2}
in
\textnormal{L}^{{(\mathrm{low})}}
.

•

One edge from
\mathbf{x}\in D^{n/2}
in
\textnormal{L}^{{(\mathrm{low})}}

to
\mathbf{y}\in D^{n/2}
in
\textnormal{R}^{{(\mathrm{low})}}
if and only if
\mathbf{x}\oplus_{f}\mathbf{y}=v^{(\mathrm{low})}
.

•

h(\mathbf{z}\|\mathbf{y})
parallel edges from
\mathbf{y}\in D^{n/2}
in
\textnormal{R}^{{(\mathrm{low})}}

to
\mathbf{z}\in D^{n/2}
in
\textnormal{R}^{{(\mathrm{high})}}
.

•

One edge from
\mathbf{z}\in D^{n/2}
in
\textnormal{R}^{{(\mathrm{high})}}

to
\mathbf{w}\in D^{n/2}
in
\textnormal{L}^{{(\mathrm{high})}}
if and only if
\mathbf{w}\oplus_{f}\mathbf{z}=v^{(\mathrm{high})}
.

In the formal proof,
we denote the adjacency matrix between
\textnormal{L}^{{(\mathrm{high})}}
and
\textnormal{L}^{{(\mathrm{low})}}
by
W
,
between
\textnormal{L}^{{(\mathrm{low})}}
and
\textnormal{R}^{{(\mathrm{low})}}
by
X
,
between
\textnormal{R}^{{(\mathrm{low})}}

\textnormal{R}^{{(\mathrm{high})}}
by

,
and between
\textnormal{R}^{{(\mathrm{high})}}
and

.

We use the notation
\mathsf{Mat}_{\mathbb{Z}}(D^{n/2}\times D^{n/2})

to refer to a

where we use the values in

as indices.
The
transition matrices
of

,
h
and

are the matrices

W,X,Y,Z\in\mathsf{Mat}_{\mathbb{Z}}(D^{n/2}\times D^{n/2})
defined by

\displaystyle W_{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq g(\mathbf{w}\|\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\in D^{n/2}

\displaystyle X_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\llbracket\mathbf{x}\oplus_{f}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{v}^{(\mathrm{low})}\rrbracket

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in D^{n/2}

\displaystyle Y_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq h(\mathbf{z}\|\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in D^{n/2}

\displaystyle Z_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\llbracket\mathbf{w}\oplus_{f}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{v}^{(\mathrm{high})}\rrbracket

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}\in D^{n/2}

Recall that the

\textnormal{tr}(A)
of a matrix

is defined as
\textnormal{tr}(A)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i,i}
.
The next lemma formalizes the correctness of this construction.

Lemma 32
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be an even number,

g,h\colon D^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbf{v}\in D^{n}
.
Also, let
W,X,Y,Z\in\mathsf{Mat}_{\mathbb{Z}}(D^{n/2}\times D^{n/2})

be the transition matrices of
g
,
h
and
\mathbf{v}
.
Then,

(g\mathbin{\circledast_{f}}h)(\mathbf{v})=\textnormal{tr}(W\cdot X\cdot Y\cdot Z).

Proof.

For any
\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}\in D^{n/2}
it holds that,

(W\cdot X)_{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in D^{n/2}}W_{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}}\cdot X_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in D^{n/2}}\llbracket\mathbf{x}\oplus_{f}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{v}^{(\mathrm{low})}\rrbracket\cdot g(\mathbf{w}\|\mathbf{x}).

(5.1)

Similarly, for any
\mathbf{y},\mathbf{w}\in D^{n/2}
it holds that,

(Y\cdot Z)_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{w}}=\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in D^{n/2}}Y_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}}\cdot Z_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}}=\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in D^{n/2}}\llbracket\mathbf{w}\oplus_{f}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{v}^{(\mathrm{high})}\rrbracket\cdot h(\mathbf{z}\|\mathbf{y}).

(5.2)

Therefore, for any
\mathbf{w}\in D^{n/2}
,

\displaystyle(W\cdot X\cdot Y\cdot Z)_{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}}=\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in D^{n/2}}(W\cdot X)_{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}}\cdot(Y\cdot Z)_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in D^{n/2}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in D^{n/2}}\llbracket\mathbf{x}\oplus_{f}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{v}^{(\mathrm{low})}\rrbracket\cdot g(\mathbf{w}\|\mathbf{x})\right)\left(\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in D^{n/2}}\llbracket\mathbf{w}\oplus_{f}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{v}^{(\mathrm{high})}\rrbracket\cdot h(\mathbf{z}\|\mathbf{y})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in D^{n/2}}\llbracket\mathbf{x}\oplus_{f}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{v}^{(\mathrm{low})}\rrbracket\cdot\llbracket\mathbf{w}\oplus_{f}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{v}^{(\mathrm{high})}~\rrbracket\cdot g(\mathbf{w}\|\mathbf{x})\cdot h(\mathbf{z}\|\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in D^{n/2}}\llbracket(\mathbf{w}\|\mathbf{x})\oplus_{f}(\mathbf{z}\|\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{v}^{(\mathrm{high})}\|\mathbf{v}^{(\mathrm{low})}\rrbracket\cdot g(\mathbf{w}\|\mathbf{x})\cdot h(\mathbf{z}\|\mathbf{y}),

where the second equality follows by (
5.1
) and (
5.2
).
Thus,

\displaystyle\textnormal{tr}(W\cdot X~\cdot

\displaystyle~Y\cdot Z)=\sum_{\mathbf{w}\in D^{n/2}}(W\cdot X\cdot Y\cdot Z)_{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{w}\in D^{n/2}}\sum_{~\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in D^{n/2}}\llbracket(\mathbf{w}\|\mathbf{x})\oplus_{f}(\mathbf{z}\|\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{v}\rrbracket\cdot g(\mathbf{w}\|\mathbf{x})\cdot h(\mathbf{z}\|\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{t}\in D^{n}}\llbracket\mathbf{u}\oplus_{f}\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{v}\rrbracket\cdot g(\mathbf{u})\cdot h(\mathbf{t})

\displaystyle=(g\mathbin{\circledast_{f}}h)(\mathbf{v}).\qed

Now we have everything ready to give the algorithm for
f
-
Query
.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

The algorithm for solving
f
-
Query
works in two steps:

1.

Compute the transition matrices
W
,
X
,
Y
, and
Z

of
g
,
h
and
\mathbf{v}
as described above.

2.

Compute and return
\textnormal{tr}(W\cdot X\cdot Y\cdot Z)
.

By
\cref
lem:fquery this algorithm returns

(g\mathbin{\circledast_{f}}h)(\mathbf{v})
. Computing the transition matrices in
Step
1
requires
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(|D|^{n}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M))
time. Observe the maximal absolute values of an entry in the transition matrices is
M
. The computation of

W\cdot X\cdot Y\cdot Z
in Step
2
requires three matrix
multiplications of
|D|^{n/2}\times|D|^{n/2}
matrices, which can be done
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}((|D|^{n/2})^{\omega}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M))
time.
Thus, the overall running time of the

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(|D|^{\omega\cdot n/2}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M))
.
∎

Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we studied the
f
-
Convolution
problem and demonstrated
that the naive brute-force algorithm can be improved for every
f\colon D\times D\to D
. We achieve that by
introducing a
cyclic partition
of a function and showing that there always
exists a cyclic partition of bounded cost.
We give an
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}((c|D|^{2})^{n}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M))
time
algorithm that computes
f
-
Convolution
for
c\coloneqq 3/4
when
|D|
is even.

The cyclic partition is a very general tool and potentially it can be used to
achieve greater improvements for certain functions
f
. For example, in
multiple applications (e.g.,
[
19
,
33
,
23
,
28
]
) the function

f
has a cyclic partition with a single cyclic minor. Nevertheless, in our proof we
only use cyclic minors where one domain is of size is at most
2
.
We suspect that larger minors have to be considered to obtain better results.
Indeed, the lower bound from
\cref
lem:existenceOfMinor:tightness implies
that our technique of considering two
arbitrary
rows together
cannot give a faster algorithm than
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}((3/4\cdot\lvert D\rvert^{2})^{n}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M))
in general.
An improved algorithm would have to select these rows very carefully
or consider three or more rows at the same time.

We leave several open problems. Our algorithm offers an exponential (in
n
) improvement
over a naive algorithm for domains
D
of constant size. Can we hope for an

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(|D|^{(2-\epsilon)n}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M))
time algorithm for
f
-
Convolution
for some

\epsilon>0
? We are not aware of any lower bounds, so in principle even
an

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(|D|^{n}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M))
time algorithm is plausible.

Ideally, we would expect that the
f
-
Convolution
problem can be solved in

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}((|L|^{n}+|R|^{n}+|T|^{n})\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M))
for any function
f\colon L\times R\to T
.
In Figure
6.1
we

methods.

,

Theorem 33

(
[

There is an algorithm which given

\mathbf{r}_{j}
-th primitive root of unity

for every

and

in
\mathcal{O}(R\log(R))

Ideally, we would like to use the algorithm from
\Cref
thm:alg-convolution with
a sufficiently large prime

g\mathbin{\odot}h

The basic idea behind our approach is to compute
g\odot h
modulo

p_{i}

Theorem 34

(Chinese Remainder Theorem)
.

Let
p_{1},\dots,p_{m}
denote a sequence of integers that are pairwise coprime and define
P\coloneqq\prod_{i\in[m]}p_{i}
.
Also let
0\leq a_{i}<p_{i}
for all
i\in[m]
.
Then there is a unique number
0\leq s<P
such that

s\equiv a_{i}\mod{p_{i}}

for all
i\in[m]
.
Moreover, there is an algorithm that, given
p_{1},\dots,p_{m}
and

a_{1},\dots,a_{m}
, computes the number
s
in time
\mathcal{O}((\log P)^{2})
.

To find the small primes for the application of the Chinese Remainder Theorem,
we additionally use density properties of primes in arithmetic progression. Given
q\in\mathbb{N}
, we say
p\in\mathbb{N}
is a
q
-prime
if
p
is a prime
number and
p\equiv 1\mod q
.
We use
\prime_{q}(i)
to denote the
i
-th
q
-prime.
That is,
\prime_{q}(i)
is a
q
-prime
such that the number of
q
-primes smaller than
\prime_{q}(i)
is exactly
i-1
.
Also, for any
B,q\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

{\textsf{prime\_bound}}_{q}(B)\coloneqq\min\left\{m\in\mathbb{N}~\middle|~\prod_{i=1}^{m}\prime_{q}(i)\geq B\right\}

to be the minimal number
m
such that the product of the first
m

q
-primes is at least
B
. We use the following upper bound on
prime_bound
.

Lemma 35
.

Let
B,q\in\mathbb{N}
be integers such that
B,q\geq 3

and
m=\textnormal{{{prime\_bound}}}_{q}(B)
. Then
m\leq\ln(B)+1
and
\textnormal{\prime}_{q}(m)\leq\max\left\{\exp\left(8\cdot\sqrt{q}\cdot\ln^{3}(q)\right),~\exp(q),~2q\cdot\ln(B)\right\}
.

In the proof of
\Cref
lem:prime_bound we use a known result for the density of primes in arithmetic progressions taken from
[
3

. For any
x,q\in\mathbb{N}
, define

to be the sum of
\ln(p)
for all
q
-primes
p

p\leq x
. Formally, we define

\theta(x,q)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\llbracket\prime_{q}(i)\leq x\rrbracket\cdot\ln\left(\prime_{q}(i)\right).

With this definition,
we can now state the result about the density
of primes in arithmetic progressions.

Lemma 36

(
[
3
, Corollary 1.8]
)
.

Let
q
and
x
be integers with

and
x\geq\exp(8\cdot\sqrt{q}\cdot\ln^{3}q)

Then,

\theta(x,q)\geq\frac{x}{\varphi(q)}-\frac{1}{160}\cdot\frac{x}{\ln x}

where

Now we have everything ready to prove
\cref
lem:prime_bound.

Proof of
\Cref

We first prove the bound for

By the definition of

\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}\prime_{q}(i)<B

As

m-1<\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\ln(\prime_{q}(i))=\ln\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}\prime_{q}(i)\right)<\ln(B)

Now we prove the bound for

For this we set

By

\displaystyle\theta(x,q)

\displaystyle\theta(x,q)

\displaystyle\geq x\cdot\left(\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{160\cdot q}\right)

\displaystyle\geq x\cdot\frac{1}{2\cdot q}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\llbracket\prime_{q}(i)\leq x\rrbracket\cdot\ln(\prime_{q}(i))\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\theta(x,q)\right)\geq B,

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left((\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{r}_{i})\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M)\right)
algorithm for the

K
-
Cyclic Convolution Problem
.

Proof of
\Cref
thm:cyclic.

Fix a finite set
K=\{c_{1},\dots,c_{\ell}\}\subseteq\mathbb{N}

which is considered as a constant throughout this proof.
Let integers
k,M\in\mathbb{N}
, integer vector
\mathbf{r}\in K^{k}

and functions
g,h\colon Z\rightarrow{\{-M,\ldots,M\}}
where

Z=\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbf{r}_{1}}\times\dots\times\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbf{r}_{k}}

be an input for the
K
-
Cyclic Convolution Problem
.

For every
t\in[\ell]
, let
D_{t}
be the prime factors of
c_{t}
.
We define
R=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathbf{r}_{j}
and
observe that for any
\mathbf{v}\in Z
it holds that
\lvert(g\mathbin{\odot}h)(\mathbf{v})\rvert\leq R\cdot M^{2}
.
Further define
B\coloneqq 3\cdot R\cdot M^{2}
and
q=\prod_{c\in K}c=\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}c_{t}
.
Assume without loss of generality that
q\geq 3
and note that
q

depends only on the fixed finite set
K

and therefore, can be viewed as a constant.

With this notation we can formally state the algorithm.

1.

Iterate over the numbers of the form
q\cdot a+1

for
a\in\{1,2,\ldots\}

and test for each one if it is prime.
The process continues until the
product of the
q
-primes exceeds
B
.
Denote these numbers by
p_{1},\dots,p_{m}
.

2.

For every
i\in[m]
and
t\in[\ell]
,
iterate over all elements
x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{p_{i}}

and test whether
x^{c_{t}}\equiv 1\mod p_{i}

and
x^{{c_{t}}/{d}}\not\equiv 1\mod p_{i}

for every
d\in D_{t}
.
If so, then set
x
as the
c_{t}
-th root of unity in
{\mathbb{F}}_{p_{i}}
.

3.

For all
i\in[m]
,
use
\cref
thm:alg-convolution with the prime
p_{i}

and appropriate roots of unity
to compute the function
f^{(i)}\colon Z\to\mathbb{Z}_{p_{i}}

defined by

f^{(i)}(\mathbf{v})\coloneqq(g\odot h)(\mathbf{v})\mod{p_{i}}~~~~~~\forall\mathbf{v}\in Z.

4.

Define
P=\prod_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}
, we define a function
f_{P}\colon Z\to\mathbb{Z}_{P}
as follows.
For each
\mathbf{v}\in Z
,
use the Chinese Remainder Theorem (cf.
\cref
thm:chinese) to compute
the value
0\leq f_{P}(\mathbf{v})<P

such that
f_{P}(\mathbf{v})\equiv f^{(i)}(\mathbf{v})\mod p_{i}
for all
i\in[m]
.

5.

Finally,
compute the function
f\colon Z\to\mathbb{Z}
using the formula

f(\mathbf{v})=\begin{cases}f_{P}(\mathbf{v})&\textnormal{ if }f_{P}(\mathbf{v})<\frac{P}{2}\\
f_{P}(\mathbf{v})-P&\textnormal{ if }f_{P}(\mathbf{v})\geq\frac{P}{2}\end{cases}

for all
\mathbf{v}\in Z
and return
f
.

Before we move to proving the correctness,
we first argue that the algorithm is well-defined.
From the definition,
the first step computes the first
m={\textsf{prime\_bound}}_{q}(B)

q
-primes
such that
p_{1}=\prime_{q}(1),\ldots,p_{m}=\prime_{q}(m)
.
It remains to show that,
for every
i\in[m]
and
t\in[\ell]
,
the
c_{t}
-th primitive root of unity in
{\mathbb{F}}_{p_{i}}
exists.
Indeed, since
c_{t}
divides
p_{i}-1

(which is in turn true as
p_{i}\equiv 1\mod q
and
c_{t}
divides
q
),
such a root of unity exists.
Moreover, as
D_{t}
contains all prime factors of
c_{t}
,
one can easily show that it actually suffices to consider
only values of the form
x^{c_{t}/d}
for every
d\in D_{t}

to correctly decide if
x
is a primitive
c_{t}
-th root of unity in
{\mathbb{F}}_{p_{i}}
.
The application of
\cref
thm:alg-convolution in the second step is possible
as
\mathbf{r}_{j}\in K=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{\ell}\}
for every
j\in[n]

and the roots of unity are computed by the second step.

Now we argue about the correctness of the algorithm.

Claim 37
.

For all
\mathbf{v}\in Z
, we have
f(\mathbf{v})=(g\mathbin{\odot}h)(\mathbf{v})
.

{claimproof}

As the algorithm is well defined,
the third step computes,
the convolution of
g
and
h
modulo
p_{i}
for every
i\in[m]
.

Now fix some
\mathbf{v}\in Z
.
We define
b(\mathbf{v})=(h\mathbin{\odot}g)(\mathbf{v})\mod P

and observe
0\leq b(\mathbf{v})<P
.
Moreover, for every
i\in[m]
it holds that

b(\mathbf{v})\mod p_{i}=\left((h\mathbin{\odot}g)(\mathbf{v})\mod P\right)\mod p_{i}=(h\mathbin{\odot}g)(\mathbf{v})\mod p_{i}=f^{(i)}(\mathbf{v}).

Since
\cref
thm:chinese also guarantees the resulting number to be unique,
it follows that
f_{P}(\mathbf{v})=b(\mathbf{v})

which implies
f_{P}(\mathbf{v})=(g\mathbin{\odot}h)(\mathbf{v})\mod P
.

Now we focus on the last step.
By the definition of
m={\textsf{prime\_bound}}_{q}(B)
,
it holds that
P=\prod_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\geq B=3\cdot R\cdot M^{2}
.
Consider the following cases.

•

In case
(g\mathbin{\odot}h)(\mathbf{v})\geq 0
we have

(g\mathbin{\odot}h)(\mathbf{v})\leq R\cdot M^{2}<\frac{B}{2}\leq P.

This implies that
f_{P}(\mathbf{v})=(g\mathbin{\odot}h)(\mathbf{v})\mod P=(g\mathbin{\odot}h)(\mathbf{v})<\frac{B}{2}
.
Thus,
f(\mathbf{v})=f_{P}(\mathbf{v})=(g\mathbin{\odot}h)(\mathbf{v})
.

•

In case
(g\mathbin{\odot}h)(\mathbf{v})<0

it holds that

(g\mathbin{\odot}h)(\mathbf{v})\geq-R\cdot M^{2}>-P.

This now implies that

f_{P}(\mathbf{v})=(g\mathbin{\odot}h)(\mathbf{v})+P\geq P-R\cdot M^{2}>\frac{P}{2}.

Hence,
f(\mathbf{v})=f_{P}(\mathbf{v})-P=(g\mathbin{\odot}h)(\mathbf{v})+P-P=(g\mathbin{\odot}h)(\mathbf{v})
.

Hence,
f(\mathbf{v})=(g\mathbin{\odot}h)(\mathbf{v})
for all
\mathbf{v}\in Z
, which concludes the proof.

From
\cref
clm:cyclic:correctness we know that the algorithm is correct
and the function
f
returned by the algorithm is indeed
(g\mathbin{\odot}h)
.
It only remains to analyze the running time of the procedure.

Claim 38
.

The procedure terminates in time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(R\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M)\right)
.

{claimproof}

We consider each step on its own.

1.

Since prime testing can be
done in polynomial time (in the representation size of the number), we can find the sequence
p_{1},\dots,p_{m}
in time
\mathcal{O}(p_{m}\cdot\polylog p_{m})
. By
\Cref
lem:density_prob, and since
q
is a constant, it follows that

p_{m}\leq\max\left\{\exp\left(8\cdot\sqrt{q}\cdot\ln^{3}(q)\right),\,\exp(q),\,2q\cdot\ln(B)\right\}=\mathcal{O}(\ln(B))=\mathcal{O}(\log(R\cdot M))

and
m\leq\ln(B)+1=\ln(3RM^{2})+1
. Hence, the running time of this step is

\mathcal{O}(p_{m}\cdot\polylog p_{m})=\mathcal{O}(\polylog(R\cdot M))
.

2.

For each
i\in[m]
and
t\in[\ell]
, in Step
2
of the algorithm
we iterate over
p_{i}
values and check
\lvert D_{t}\rvert
values.
Since
D_{t}
are the prime factors of
c_{t}
(and hence
|D_{t}|
is a constant),
this takes time
\mathcal{O}(p_{i}\polylog p_{i})

which can be bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\polylog(R\cdot M))
.

Since
m\leq\log(3\cdot R\cdot M^{2})+1
and
\ell
is a constant,
the overall running time of the step is
\mathcal{O}(\polylog(R\cdot M))
.

3.

By
\cref
thm:alg-convolution, the number
of arithmetic operations required to compute
f^{(i)}
is

\mathcal{O}(R\cdot\log(R))
. Since each arithmetic operation is performed in

{\mathbb{F}}_{p_{i}}
, the total time spent to compute
f^{(i)}
is

\mathcal{O}(R\cdot\log(R)\cdot\log^{2}(p_{i}))=\mathcal{O}(R\cdot\log(R)\cdot\log^{2}(\log(R\cdot M)))=\mathcal{O}(R\cdot\polylog(R\cdot M)),

where the first equality holds
because
p_{i}\leq p_{m}=\mathcal{O}(\log(R\cdot M))
.

Finally, as
m=\mathcal{O}(\log(R\cdot M))
,
the overall computation time of this step is

m\cdot\mathcal{O}(R\cdot\polylog(R\cdot M)=\mathcal{O}(R\cdot\polylog(R\cdot M))
.

4.

As we iterate over all
R
values from

and by
\cref

this computation can be done in time
\mathcal{O}(R\cdot(\log P)^{2})
.
Since
\log P\leq m\cdot p_{m}\leq\mathcal{O}(\polylog(R\cdot M))

the overall running time of this step is
\mathcal{O}(R\cdot\polylog(R\cdot M))
.

5.

As we again iterate over all elements from

,
the computation time of this step is

\mathcal{O}(R\cdot\polylog P)=\mathcal{O}(R\cdot\polylog(R\cdot M))

where we use

.

As the running time of each step is at most
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(R\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M)\right)
,
the overall running time of the algorithm is
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(R\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(M)\right)
.

The proof now follows by
\cref
clm:cyclic:correctness,clm:cycle:runtime.
