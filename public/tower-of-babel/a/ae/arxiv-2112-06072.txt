A tight bound for the clique query problem in two rounds
Uriel Feige, Tom Ferster

Abstract
We consider a problem introduced by Feige, Gamarnik, Neeman, Rácz and Tetali [2020], that of finding a large clique in a random graph G ∼ G ⁡ ( n , 1 2 ) G\sim G(n,\frac{1}{2}) , where the graph G G is accessible by queries to entries of its adjacency matrix. The query model allows some limited adaptivity, with a constant number of rounds of queries, and n δ n^{\delta} queries in each round. With high probability, the maximum clique in G G is of size roughly 2 ​ log ⁡ n 2\log n , and the goal is to find cliques of size α ​ log ⁡ n \alpha\log n , for α \alpha as large as possible. We prove that no two-rounds algorithm is likely to find a clique larger than 4 3 ​ δ ​ log ⁡ n \frac{4}{3}\delta\log n , which is a tight upper bound when 1 ≤ δ ≤ 6 5 1\leq\delta\leq\frac{6}{5} . For other ranges of parameters, namely, two-rounds with 6 5 < δ < 2 \frac{6}{5}<\delta<2 , and three-rounds with 1 ≤ δ < 2 1\leq\delta<2 , we improve over the previously known upper bounds on α \alpha , but our upper bounds are not tight. If early rounds are restricted to have fewer queries than the last round, then for some such restrictions we do prove tight upper bounds.

Introduction

We consider a problem introduced by Feige, Gamarnik, Neeman, Rácz and Tetali
[
5
]
, that of finding a large clique in a random graph
G\sim G(n,\frac{1}{2})
, where the graph
G
is accessible by queries to entries of its adjacency matrix. The input to the problem is a random graph
G(V,E)
on
n
vertices, where for every pair
\{u,v\}
of distinct vertices,
(u,v)\in E
independently with probability
\frac{1}{2}
. To simplify some of the terminology, we sometimes refer to a pair
\{u,v\}
of distinct vertices as an edge, regardless of whether
(u,v)\in E
, but if
(u,v)\in E
we refer to it as a positive edge, and if
(u,v)\not\in E
we refer to it as a negative edge. The goal of the algorithm is to find in
G
a clique (a complete subgraph) that is as large as possible. The algorithm can access
G
via queries to the adjacency matrix of
G
, querying an edge
\{u,v\}
and getting in reply its status, whether it is positive or negative. We may think of these queries as queries to a random oracle, where for each new query (not previously asked), the oracle answers positively with probability
\frac{1}{2}
and negatively with probability
\frac{1}{2}
, independently of all previous answers. The limiting resource for the algorithm is the total number of queries
q
, whereas the running time of the algorithm may be unbounded (e.g., exponential in
n
). The sequence of queries asked by the algorithm may be adapative, in the sense that the choice of the next query depends on answers to previous queries.

The results of the
q
queries induce a graph
G^{\prime}(V,E^{\prime})
, where the edges of
G^{\prime}
are those edges that were queried and received a positive answer. As the algorithm is not bounded in its computation time, it can find the maximum clique in
G^{\prime}
. Hence the goal of the querying process is to produce a graph
G^{\prime}
in which the maximum clique is as large as possible. We denote this maximum clique by
K
and its size by
k
. Note that for any nontrivial algorithm (for which
k>1
),
K
(and likewise
k
) is a random variable that will depend on the input graph
G
. Hence for a given algorithm, we shall think of
k
as the median value of the size of the clique that the algorithm finds. For reasons that will become clear later, if
k
is even our results are somewhat simpler to express. Hence, we shall assume that
k
is even (this has negligible effect on the results, a difference of at most 1 in the size of the clique found).

We shall use
\delta
as a parameter indicating the allowed number of queries, where
q=n^{\delta}
. (Sometimes the interpretation of
\delta
will be relaxed to requiring
q=O(n^{\delta})
. This has negligible effect on the results.) Hence our goal is to maximize
k
(as a function of
n
and
\delta
), such that there is an algorithm making at most
n^{\delta}
queries, for which with probability at least
\frac{1}{2}
over the choice of
G\sim G(n,\frac{1}{2})
, the graph
G^{\prime}
contains a clique
K
of size
k
.

We consider only
\delta\geq 1
, as for smaller
\delta
the number of non-isolated vertices in
G^{\prime}
will be
n^{\prime}<n
, and we may solve a similar problem with
n^{\prime}
instead of
n
. For
\delta=2
, the whole graph may be queried. The clique of maximum size in a graph
G\sim G(n,\frac{1}{2})
is with high probability (w.h.p.) of size roughly (neglecting low order terms)
2\log n
(see
[
13
]
). Therefore, the size of the maximum clique in
G^{\prime}
satisfies w.h.p.
k\leq 2\log n+\Theta(1)
for every
1\leq\delta<2
. On the other hand, a clique of (expected) size
\log n
may be found using roughly
2n
queries in the following way. Initialize the sets
K=\emptyset
,
T=V(G)
. While
T
is not empty repeat the following:

1.

Choose arbitrarily
t\in T
, and update
K\leftarrow K\cup\{t\}
.

2.

Query all pairs
\{\{t,t^{\prime}\}\mid t^{\prime}\in T\setminus\{t\}\}
.

3.

Remove
t
and all its non-neighbors from
T
.

The size of
T
decreases by a factor of
2
in each iteration (in expectation), hence there are roughly
\log n
iterations, and the total expected number of queries is
2n
. After the last iteration, the set
K
is a clique of expected size
\log n
. Therefore, the values of
k
of interest are between
\log n
and
2\log n
. In all computations we will neglect terms of order
o(\log n)
in the size of
k
. Hence, we can state the problem we wish to solve in the following way: Maximize
\alpha
for which a clique of size
k\geq\alpha\log n-o(\log n)
may be found using a sequence of at most
n^{\delta}
queries. Note that
\alpha
is a function of
\delta
which satisfies
1\leq\alpha\leq 2
. We give a more formal definition of the value
\alpha
we wish to find.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
1\leq\delta<2
,
\alpha\leq 2
. A deterministic algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, which makes a sequence of at most
n^{\delta}
adaptive queries (based on oracle answers), will be called a
(\delta,\alpha)
-algorithm if for every sufficiently large
n
, with probability at least
\frac{1}{2}
, the graph
G^{\prime}
(induced on edges discovered by the algorithm) contains a clique of size
k\geq\alpha\log n-o(\log n)
.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
1\leq\delta<2
.
\alpha_{*}(\delta)
is defined as the supremum over
\alpha
such that there exists a
(\delta,\alpha)
-algorithm. Note that
1\leq\alpha_{*}(\delta)\leq 2
.

Remark 1.3
.

Since we do not limit the time complexity of algorithms, if
G^{\prime}
contains at least one clique of size
k
,
\mathcal{A}
can be assumed to find such a clique
K
(e.g., by using exhaustive search), and to return it.

A full solution to the stated problem is to find the exact value of
\alpha_{*}(\delta)
for every
1\leq\delta<2
. Lower bounds on
\alpha_{*}(\delta)
are proved by algorithms which find cliques of size
\alpha\log n
(for
\alpha>1
). Such algorithms were presented in
[
5
]
, and can be found in Section
4.1
. The presented algorithms are suitable also for a slightly different problem setting, in which the adaptiveness of the algorithm is restricted to a constant number of rounds. The upper bounds we will present in this paper will be for this restricted setting. In this setting, the algorithm may use a constant number
l
of rounds, where in each round it may query at most
n^{\delta}
edge queries simultaneously, and the oracle answers all of them. This means that the algorithm may choose queries on round
i
based on answers given in previous rounds, but not on those given for queries in round
i
. Denote by
q_{i}
the number of queries in round
i
. The pattern of queries in the first round gives us the queries graph
Q_{1}
with
n
vertices and
q_{1}
edges. Likewise, the pattern of queries in any other round
i
(that may depend on the answers to queries of previous rounds) gives us the queries graph
Q_{i}
with
n
vertices and
q_{i}
edges. Note that the edges of
Q_{i}
consist of all pairs queried in round
i
, and not only positive ones. We denote by
Q_{\leq i}
the queries graph which includes all queries up to round
i
, i.e.,
E(Q_{\leq i})=\bigcup_{j\leq i}E(Q_{j})
. (Note that we use the term ’up to’ in the meaning of ’up to and including’). We modify definitions
1.1
and
1.2
to comply with the restricted setting.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
l\in\mathbb{N}
,
1\leq\delta<2
,
\alpha\leq 2
. A deterministic algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, which makes
l
rounds of queries with at most
n^{\delta}
queries in each round, will be called a
(\delta,l,\alpha)
-algorithm if for every sufficiently large
n
, with probability at least
\frac{1}{2}
,
G^{\prime}
contains a clique of size
k\geq\alpha\log n-o(\log n)
.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
l\in\mathbb{N}
,
1\leq\delta<2
.
\alpha_{*}(\delta,l)
is defined as the supremum over
\alpha
such that there exists a
(\delta,l,\alpha)
-algorithm.

These definitions can be generalized to the case where the number of queries may not be the same in all rounds. In general, we may assume that in round
i
the algorithm is restricted to
q_{i}=n^{\delta_{i}}
queries for some
0<\delta_{i}<2
. This leads to similar definitions of a
((\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{l}),l,\alpha)
-algorithm and
\alpha_{*}((\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{l}),l)
(see definitions in Section
5.1.2
). Note that for every
l\in\mathbb{N}
, denoting
\delta\coloneqq\max_{i}\delta_{i}
we have:

\alpha_{*}((\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{l}),l)\leq\alpha_{*}(\delta,l)\leq\alpha_{*}(\delta)

1.1
Previous results

The problem of bounding
\alpha_{*}(\delta)
and
\alpha_{*}(\delta,l)
was studied by Feige, Gamarnik, Neeman, Rácz and Tetali
[
5
]
. They proved the following results regarding
\alpha_{*}(\delta,l)
:

1.

For every
1\leq\delta<2
,
\alpha_{*}(\delta,1)=\delta
.

2.

For every
1\leq\delta\leq\frac{6}{5}
,
\alpha_{*}(\delta,2)\geq\frac{4\delta}{3}
, and for every
\frac{6}{5}\leq\delta<2
,
\alpha_{*}(\delta,2)\geq 1+\frac{\delta}{2}
.

3.

For every
1\leq\delta<2
,
\alpha_{*}(\delta,3)\geq 1+\frac{\delta}{2}
.

4.

For
\delta=1
and every
l\in\mathbb{N}
,
\alpha_{*}(1,l)\leq 2^{1-\frac{1}{2^{l}-1}}
. This gives:
\alpha_{*}(1,2)\leq 1.588
,
\alpha_{*}(1,3)\leq 1.812
and so on.

5.

For every
1\leq\delta<2
and
l\in\mathbb{N}
,
\alpha_{*}(\delta,l)\leq 2-\delta\left(\frac{2-\delta}{2}\right)^{l}
.

An upper bound on
\alpha_{*}(\delta)
was proved by Alweiss, Ben Hamida, He, and Moreira
[
1
]
. They showed that for every
1\leq\delta<2
:

\alpha_{*}(\delta)\leq 1+\sqrt{1-\frac{(2-\delta)^{2}}{2}}

For example, for
\delta=1
this gives
\alpha_{*}(1)\leq 1+\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}}\sim 1.707
. Since
\alpha_{*}(\delta,l)\leq\alpha_{*}(\delta)
, this result also improves the upper bound on
\alpha_{*}(\delta,l)
for every
l\geq 3
.

We prove upper bounds on
\alpha_{*}(\delta,l)
and
\alpha_{*}((\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{l}),l)
for
l\in\{2,3\}
(see Figure
1
). Namely, we upper bound the size of clique that is likely to be found using two or three rounds algorithms. First, we prove the following upper bound for two-rounds algorithms:

Theorem 2.1
.

For every
1\leq\delta<2
:

\alpha_{*}(\delta,2)\leq\frac{4}{3}\delta

The upper bound in Theorem
2.1
is tight when
1\leq\delta\leq\frac{6}{5}
. When
\frac{6}{5}<\delta<2
we have the lower bound
\alpha_{*}(\delta,2)\geq 1+\frac{\delta}{2}
(see
[
5
]

4.1
), so there is a gap between the bounds. We improve the upper bound for this case by proving the following theorem:

Theorem 2.2
.

For every
\frac{6}{5}<\delta<2

\alpha_{*}(\delta,2)\leq 1+\sqrt{(\delta-1)(3-\delta)}

The lower bound
\alpha_{*}(\delta,2)\geq 1+\frac{\delta}{2}

\frac{6}{5}<\delta<2

queries in round 1. Therefore, we consider algorithms in which round 1 is restricted to query at most

\delta_{1}<\delta

Theorem 2.3
.

For every

\alpha_{*}\Big(\Big(\frac{3}{2}-\frac{\delta}{4},\delta\Big),2\Big)\leq 1+\frac{\delta}{2}

The upper bound in Theorem

is tight for every

3
Related Work

There is extensive research on the query complexity of deterministic and randomized algorithms. For a given graph property, the query complexity is the smallest number of edge queries needed to determine whether an input graph satisfies the property. For some properties, such as determining whether a graph contains an edge, or whether a graph is connected,
all edges must be queried. These are called evasive properties. The Aanderaa–Karp–Rosenberg conjecture claims that every nontrivial monotone graph property (i.e remains true when edges are added) for graphs on
n
vertices is evasive. While the AKR conjecture is still open, there are many works proving variations of it. Rivest and Vuillemin
[
16
]
showed that at least
\frac{n^{2}}{16}
queries are needed to test for any nontrivial monotone graph property. The constant was later improved by Scheidweiler and Triesch
[
17
]
to the current best result of
\frac{n^{2}}{3}-o(n^{2})
queries. Kahn, Saks and Sturtevant used a topological approach to show that the AKR conjecture is true when the number of vertices is a prime power
[
9
]
, and Yao showed it in the case of bipartite graphs
[
19
]
.

There has also been research on the query complexity of randomized algorithms. No nontrivial monotone property is known with randomized query complexity less than
\frac{n^{2}}{4}
, and a similar conjecture by Karp says that
\Omega(n^{2})
queries are required in this case. The first non-linear lower bound, of
\Omega(n\log^{1/12}n)
, was proved by Yao
[
18
]
, and later improved to the current best lower bound of
\Omega(n^{4/3}\log^{1/3}n)
by Chakrabarti and Khot
[
2
]
. Other lower bounds were given by Friedgut, Kahn and Wigderson
[
8
]
and O’Donnell, Saks, Schramm and Servedio
[
14
]
where the bound depends on a critical probability
p
such that a graph drawn from
G(n,p)
satisfies the property with probability
\frac{1}{2}
. The specific property of having a subgraph isomorphic to a given graph (which is similar to our case) was proved by Gröger to have a lower bound of
\Omega(n^{3/2})

[
4
]
. A summary on Evasiveness of Graph Properties can be found in lecture notes by Neal Young from a course taught by László Lovász
[
11
]
.

A setting more similar to ours, where the input graph is drawn randomly from
G(n,p)
, was introduced by Ferber, Krivelevich, Sudakov and Viera. They proved that when
p>\frac{\ln n+\ln\ln n+\omega(1)}{n}
a Hamiltonian cycle can be found using
(1+o(1))n
positive edge queries (which is optimal)
[
6
]
. However, when
p=\frac{1+\epsilon}{n}
, in order to find a path of length
l=\Omega(\frac{\log(\frac{1}{\epsilon})}{\epsilon})
at least
\Omega(\frac{l}{p\epsilon\log(\frac{1}{\epsilon})})
queries are required (and not only the trivial bound of
\Omega(\frac{l}{p})
)
[
7
]
. Colon, Fox, Grinshpun and He
[
3
]
, as part of work concerning the online Ramsey number, studied the problem of determining the number of queries needed to find in
G\sim G(n,p)
a copy of a target graph, in particular, a constant size clique.

The problem we study, of finding a large clique in
G\sim G(n,\frac{1}{2})
,
was introduced by Feige, Gamarnik, Neeman, Rácz and Tetali
[
5
]
,
and further studied by Alweiss, Ben Hamida, He, and Moreira
[
1
]
.
See Section
1.1
for results from these papers.
The latter paper also proves bounds on the number of queries to a random graph
G\sim G(\infty,p)
needed in order to find a copy of a graph
H
with degeneracy
d
.

A related problem, that of finding a planted clique of size
k\geq(2+\epsilon)\log n
in a
G(n,\frac{1}{2})
graph by adaptive probing, was studied by Ràcz and Schiffer
[
15
]
. They proved that
o(\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}}+n)
queries do not suffice in order to find the planted clique, and that
O(\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}}\log^{2}n+n\log n)
queries do suffice. Mardia, Asi and Chandrasekher
[
12
]
designed sub-linear time algorithms (
o(n^{2})
time complexity, and consequently also
o(n^{2})
queries) that find planted cliques of size
k=\omega(\sqrt{n\log\log n})
.

Preliminaries

4.1
Algorithms - lower bounds

The following algorithms, which lower bound
\alpha_{*}(\delta,l)
, were presented in
[
5
]
.

Algorithm 1 - One round

The following one round algorithm finds a clique of size
\delta\log n
:

Round 1:
Choose arbitrarily a set
S
of
n^{\delta/2}
vertices, and query all pairs within
S
(roughly
n^{\delta}
pairs). Find a clique
K
of size
\delta\log n
in the graph induced on
S
, and return
K
. Such a clique exists w.h.p. as this is the expected size of the maximum clique in
S
.

Algorithm 2 - Two rounds when
\delta\leq\frac{6}{5}

The following two rounds algorithm finds a clique of size
\frac{4}{3}\delta\log n
when
1\leq\delta\leq\frac{6}{5}
:

Round 1:
Choose arbitrarily a set
S
of
n^{\delta/6}
vertices and a set
T
of
n^{{5\delta}/6}
vertices not in
S
(
|T|\leq n
since
\delta\leq\frac{6}{5}
). Query all pairs within
S
and all pairs in
S\times T
(roughly
n^{\delta}
pairs). Find a clique
S^{\prime}
of size
\frac{\delta}{3}\log n
in the graph induced on
S
. Such a clique exists w.h.p. as this is the expected size of the maximum clique in
S
. Let
T^{\prime}\subset T
be the set of mutual neighbors of
S^{\prime}
. It is of expected size
|T|\cdot 2^{-\frac{\delta}{3}\log n}=n^{{\delta}/2}
.

Round 2:
Query all pairs within
T^{\prime}
(roughly
n^{\delta}
pairs). Find a clique
T^{\prime\prime}
of size
\delta\log n
in the graph induced on
T^{\prime}
(neglecting low order terms, such a clique exists w.h.p.). Return
K\coloneqq S^{\prime}\cup T^{\prime\prime}
, which is of size
\frac{4\delta}{3}\log n
.

We get that for every
1\leq\delta\leq\frac{6}{5}
:
\alpha_{*}(\delta,2)\geq\frac{4}{3}\delta
.

Algorithm 3 - Two rounds when
\delta\geq\frac{6}{5}

The following two rounds algorithm finds a clique of size
(1+\frac{\delta}{2})\log n
when
\frac{6}{5}\leq\delta<2
:

Round 1:
Choose arbitrarily a set
S
of
n^{(1-\delta/2)/2}
vertices, and let
T
be the set of all vertices not in
S
. Query all pairs within
S
(roughly
n^{1-\delta/2}<n^{\delta}
pairs), and all pairs in
S\times T
(roughly
n^{3/2-\delta/4}
pairs, which is less than
n^{\delta}
when
\delta\geq\frac{6}{5}
). Find a clique
S^{\prime}
of size
(1-\frac{\delta}{2})\log n
in the graph induced on
S
(exists w.h.p.). Let
T^{\prime}\subset T
be the set of mutual neighbors of
S^{\prime}
. It is of expected size
|T|\cdot 2^{-(1-\frac{\delta}{2})\log n}\simeq n^{{\delta}/2}
.

Round 2:
Query all pairs within
T^{\prime}
(roughly
n^{\delta}
pairs). Find a clique
T^{\prime\prime}
of size
\delta\log n
(neglecting low order terms) in the graph induced on
T^{\prime}
(exists w.h.p.). Return
K\coloneqq S^{\prime}\cup T^{\prime\prime}
, which is of size
(1+\frac{\delta}{2})\log n
.

We get that for every
\frac{6}{5}\leq\delta<2
:
\alpha_{*}(\delta,2)\geq 1+\frac{\delta}{2}
.

Algorithm 4 - Three rounds

The following three rounds algorithm finds a clique of size
(1+\frac{\delta}{2})\log n
.

Round 1:
Choose arbitrarily a set
S
of
n^{(1-\delta/2)/2}
vertices. Query all pairs within
S
(roughly
n^{1-\delta/2}<n^{\delta}
pairs). Find a clique
S^{\prime}
of size
(1-\frac{\delta}{2})\log n
in the graph induced on
S
(exists w.h.p.).

Round 2:
Let
T
be a set of
\frac{n}{|S^{\prime}|}
vertices not in
S
. Query all pairs in
S^{\prime}\times T
(roughly
n
pairs) and let
T^{\prime}\subset T
be the set of mutual neighbors of
S^{\prime}
. It is of expected size
|T|\cdot 2^{-(1-\frac{\delta}{2})\log n}\simeq\frac{n^{{\delta}/2}}{|S^{\prime}|}
.

Round 3:
Query all pairs within
T^{\prime}
(roughly
n^{\delta}
pairs). Find a clique
T^{\prime\prime}
of size
\delta\log n
(neglecting low order terms) in the graph induced on
T^{\prime}
(exists w.h.p.). Return
K\coloneqq S^{\prime}\cup T^{\prime\prime}
, which is of size
(1+\frac{\delta}{2})\log n
.

We get that for every
1\leq\delta<2
:
\alpha_{*}(\delta,3)\geq 1+\frac{\delta}{2}
.

Algorithms 2 and 3 have the same queries structure in general. Both query on round 1 all pairs within some set
S
, and between
S
and some set
T
, and on round 2 query all pairs within some
T^{\prime}\subset T
. The sizes of the sets are chosen such that the constraint on the number of queries is satisfied, and the returned clique size is maximized (in relation to similar algorithms). The major difference between algorithms 2 and 3 is that in Algorithm 2 the set of vertices participating in any query is of size smaller than
n
(when
\delta<\frac{6}{5}
). So the effective number of vertices in
G
in that case is smaller than
n
(as we may discard vertices that are never queried). When
\delta=\frac{6}{5}
both algorithms are identical and this is the smallest
\delta
for which the size of
T
(and therefore the effective size of the graph
G
) is of size roughly
n
. For larger
\delta
, the size of
T
cannot increase further, and the growth rate of
\alpha
as a function of
\delta
decreases (from
\frac{4}{3}\delta
to
1+\frac{\delta}{2}
).

In fact, Algorithm 3 resembles Algorithm 4 more than it resembles Algorithm 2. Both algorithms 3 and 4 find the same clique size, and the extra round in Algorithm 4 has no benefit to the size of the clique. The only difference is that round 1 of Algorithm 3 is split to rounds 1,2 in Algorithm 4, and this allows to leave out some unnecessary queries. This reduction in the number of queries allows using Algorithm 4 also when
\delta<\frac{6}{5}
, in which case the clique returned is larger than the one returned by Algorithm 2 (
\frac{4}{3}\delta<1+\frac{\delta}{2}
for
\delta<\frac{6}{5}
).

4.2
Constrained algorithms

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a
(\delta,l,\alpha)
-algorithm. Assuming the algorithm succeeds, we denote the clique it returns by
K
, where
|V(K)|=k=\alpha\log n
. After round
i
, each pair of vertices
u,v\in V
is of one of three types:

1.

The pair
u,v
was not queried up to round
i
.

2.

The pair
u,v
was queried up to round
i
and answered positively.

3.

The pair
u,v
was queried up to round
i
and answered negatively.

The state
K_{i}
, of the returned clique after round
i
, is the state of all pairs of vertices of
K
after round
i
. Since we assume the algorithm succeeds and
K
is indeed a clique, the last state
K_{l}
consists only of pairs of type
2
. Therefore, there are no pairs of type
3
in any of the states
K_{i}
, and we can look at
K_{i}
as a subgraph of
K_{l}
which contains only edges queried up to round
i
. Note that
K_{0}
is an independent set.

The only constraints we had on the algorithm so far were the number of query rounds it was allowed to make and the number of edge queries in each of them. Following the proof method introduced in
[
5
]
, the algorithms we will analyze will have some more constraints upon them. Those constraints will refer to properties of the states
K_{i}
during the algorithm’s runtime. Such properties may be for example - the size of the maximum matching in
K_{i}
, the number of edges in some subgraph, and etc. The idea behind this is that such algorithms will be easier to analyze because we may assume the intermediate states
K_{i}
satisfied several properties.

Each constraint will be associated with some parameter which receives values out of some finite domain. The (ordered) set of all parameters will be referred to as the template, and each proper assignment of the template will be referred to as a signature. Let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of all possible signatures (note that
\mathcal{S}
may be a subset of the Cartesian product of the parameters’ domains). We say that an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
had a signature
p\in\mathcal{S}
if the values of all parameters in the template were according to
p
during the runtime of the algorithm. For example, we may use two constraints - the size of the maximum matching in
K_{1}
, and the number of edges in
K_{1}
. The template will be
(m_{1},e_{1})
, and the set of signatures will be some subset of the following set (considering only pairs of integers):

\mathcal{S}\subseteq\Big[0,\frac{k}{2}\Big]\times\Big[0,{k\choose{2}}\Big]

For a signature
p\in\mathcal{S}
, and an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, we define the algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{p}
as follows.
\mathcal{A}_{p}
succeeds, and returns the clique
K
, if and only if
\mathcal{A}
returned
K
and had the signature
p
. Otherwise, the algorithm fails. In the example with template
(m_{1},e_{1})
, if the maximum matching of
K_{1}
when
\mathcal{A}
runs is
a^{*}
and the number of edges is
b^{*}
, then only
\mathcal{A}_{p}
with
p=(a^{*},b^{*})
returns
K
(assuming
\mathcal{A}
succeeds).
Note that if
\mathcal{A}
succeeds, there is exactly one
p\in\mathcal{S}
for which the algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{p}
succeeds. If
\mathcal{A}
fails, all
\mathcal{A}_{p}
’s fail too.

Due to the extra structure, it will be simpler to analyze the algorithms
\mathcal{A}_{p}
instead of
\mathcal{A}
. Given some upper bound on the probability
\mathcal{A}_{p}
succeeds for any
p
, we will derive a bound on the probability
\mathcal{A}
succeeds, and then derive a bound on
\alpha_{*}(\delta,l)
. This can be generalized to the case where we have several upper bounds (denote their amount by
B
). We will assume that the
B
bounds have the following form:

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{p}\text{ succeeds})\leq n^{f_{1}^{j}(p)k}\cdot 2^{-f_{2}^{j}(p){k\choose{2}}}

where
f_{1}^{j},f_{2}^{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for every
1\leq j\leq B
, and
f_{1}^{j}(p),f_{2}^{j}(p)
do not depend on
n
. We give a brief overview to the reason we consider bounds of this form, which will become more clear when we prove the actual bounds.

•

n^{f_{1}^{j}(p)k}
will be the number of candidate sets (sets of vertices of
G
) that satisfy the signature
p
, and possibly may be returned as the clique
K
.

•

2^{-f_{2}^{j}(p){k\choose{2}}}
will be the probability that some subset of the edges within a candidate set will be positive. If this event occurs, we refer to the candidate set as a feasible set. A clique
K
returned by the algorithm must be a feasible set.

•

The product
n^{f_{1}^{j}(p)k}\cdot 2^{-f_{2}^{j}(p){k\choose{2}}}
will be an upper bound on the expected number of feasible sets. Using this bound we will derive a bound on
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{p}\text{ succeeds})
(using Markov’s inequality).

In order to prove upper bounds on
\alpha_{*}(\delta,l)
using upper bounds on
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{p}\text{ succeeds})
, we prove the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
1\leq\delta<2
,
l\geq 1
, and let
\mathcal{S}
be a set of signatures such that
|\mathcal{S}|=n^{o(\log n)}
. Suppose we have several upper bounds (denote their amount by
B
) on the probability algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{p}
succeeds. Suppose the bounds are of the following form:
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{p}\text{ succeeds})\leq n^{f_{1}^{j}(p)k}\cdot 2^{-f_{2}^{j}(p){k\choose{2}}}
where
f_{1}^{j},f_{2}^{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for every
1\leq j\leq B
, and
f_{1}^{j}(p),f_{2}^{j}(p)
do not depend on
n
. Then:

\alpha_{*}(\delta,l)\leq\max_{p\in\mathcal{S}}\min_{j}\frac{2f_{1}^{j}(p)}{f_{2}^{j}(p)}

Proof.

Denote
\alpha=\alpha_{*}(\delta,l)
,
k=\alpha\log n
, and let
\mathcal{A}
be a
(\delta,l,\alpha)
-algorithm. Assume for contradiction
\alpha>\max_{p\in\mathcal{S}}\min_{j}\frac{2f_{1}^{j}(p)}{f_{2}^{j}(p)}
. Given the upper bounds, we have that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{p}\text{ succeeds})\leq\min_{j}n^{f_{1}^{j}(p)k}\cdot 2^{-f_{2}^{j}(p){k\choose{2}}}
. If
\mathcal{A}
succeeds, there must be some
p\in\mathcal{S}
for which
\mathcal{A}_{p}
succeeds (we define
\mathcal{S}
to include all possible outcomes). Hence, using union bound we get:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}\text{ succeeds})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{p}\text{ succeeds})

\displaystyle\leq\Big[\max_{p\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{p}\text{ succeeds})\Big]\cdot n^{o(\log n)}

\displaystyle\leq\Big[\max_{p\in\mathcal{S}}\min_{j}\Big(n^{f_{1}^{j}(p)k}\cdot 2^{-f_{2}^{j}(p){k\choose{2}}}\Big)\Big]\cdot n^{o(\log n)}

Taking
\log
of both sides we get:

\displaystyle\log\Big(\Big[\max_{p\in\mathcal{S}}\min_{j}\Big(n^{f_{1}^{j}(p)k}\cdot 2^{-f_{2}^{j}(p){k\choose{2}}}\Big)\Big]\cdot n^{o(\log n)}\Big)\geq

\displaystyle-1

\displaystyle\max_{p\in\mathcal{S}}\min_{j}\Big[{f_{1}^{j}(p)k}\log n-f_{2}^{j}(p){k\choose{2}}\Big]+o(\log^{2}n)\geq

\displaystyle-1

\displaystyle\max_{p\in\mathcal{S}}\min_{j}\Big[(2f_{1}^{j}(p)-\alpha f_{2}^{j}(p))\Big]\frac{1}{2}\alpha\log^{2}n+o(\log^{2}n)\geq

\displaystyle-1

For sufficiently large
n
, this expression is dominated by the term
\log^{2}n
. Since we assumed that
\alpha>\max_{p\in\mathcal{S}}\min_{j}\frac{2f_{1}^{j}(p)}{f_{2}^{j}(p)}
, we have that for every
p\in\mathcal{S}
there exists
j
such that:

\displaystyle\alpha>\frac{2f_{1}^{j}(p)}{f_{2}^{j}(p)}

\displaystyle\implies 2f_{1}^{j}(p)-\alpha f_{2}^{j}(p)<0

Therefore,
\max_{p\in\mathcal{S}}\min_{j}\Big[(2f_{1}^{j}(p)-\alpha f_{2}^{j}(p))\Big]<0
and as
n\rightarrow\infty
we have that:

\max_{p\in\mathcal{S}}\min_{j}\Big[(2f_{1}^{j}(p)-\alpha f_{2}^{j}(p))\Big]\frac{1}{2}\alpha\log^{2}n+o(\log^{2}n)\rightarrow-\infty

Which is a contradiction.
∎

Gallay-Edmonds decomposition (GED)

In order to prove upper bounds in the case of three rounds, we will use the Gallay-Edmonds Decomposition (see, e.g.,

a

The probability

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{p}\text{ succeeds})\leq{q_{1}\choose m_{1}k}\cdot 2^{-e_{1}{k\choose{2}}}\cdot{q_{2}\choose m_{2}k}\cdot 2^{-e_{2}{k\choose{2}}}

Given

we get:

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{p}\text{ succeeds})\leq n^{\frac{1}{2}\delta k}\cdot 2^{-(e_{1}+e_{2}){k\choose{2}}}

The edges in

1.

, queried in round 1.

2.

The following lemma bounds the number of free edges. Its proof appears after the end of the proof of the current theorem.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let

Applying Lemma

We apply Lemma

∎

5.2.1
Free edges and vertices

The concept of free edges, introduced in the proof of Theorem
5.1
, may be extended to any number of rounds
l
. Given a prefect matching
M
of the returned clique
K
, we partition its edges according to the round in which they were queried, i.e.,
M=\bigcup_{i\leq l}M_{i}
. We denote
|M_{i}|=m_{i}k
, and include the parameters
m_{1},\dots,m_{l}
in the template. We denote by
V_{M_{i}}
the set of vertices covered by
M_{i}
, and denote
V_{M}^{i}\coloneqq\bigcup_{j\leq i}V_{M_{j}}
. Intuitively, an edge
e
is free for the algorithm, if the algorithm chooses queries such that many candidates for
K
will contain
e
, based on previous information that
e
is positive. Since
V(K)=\bigcup_{i\leq l}V_{M_{i}}
, and the candidates for the set
V_{M_{i}}
are chosen in round
i
, if
e
has some endpoint
u\in V_{M_{i}}
and
e
was answered positively prior to round
i
, then the algorithm may choose to include
u
in
V_{M_{i}}
based on the information that
e
is positive. Therefore, the algorithm will not ’pay’ for the probability
e
is positive. This intuition suggests that the free edges are exactly the edges that were queried on some round
j
, and have an endpoint
u\in V_{M_{i}}
where
i>j
.

In order to prove strong upper bounds on
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{p}\text{ succeeds})
we want the probability factor of the bound to be small (i.e.,
2^{-f_{2}(p){k\choose{2}}}
, see Section
4.2
). The free edges will not be included in the
f_{2}(p){k\choose{2}}
edges which account for this probability, so we would like to reduce the number of free edges. One way of doing this is by a careful choice of the matching
M
(as will be done in the proof of Theorem
5.16
). Yet another way is as follows.
Consider the matching
M^{i^{*}}\subseteq M
which contains only matching edges queried up to round
i^{*}
for some
1\leq i^{*}\leq l
, i.e.,
M^{i^{*}}=\bigcup_{j\leq i^{*}}M_{j}
. This defines the set
V_{M}^{i^{*}}
of vertices covered by
M^{i^{*}}
. In order to reduce the number of free edges, we ignore the template parameters
m_{i^{*}+1},\dots,m_{l}
, and allow the rest of the vertices to be any set of vertices, regardless of the rounds in which they participate in queries (we used a similar approach in the proof of Lemma
4.3
). This means that the family of candidates for this set of vertices is chosen deterministicly before any answers are given, thus reducing the number of free edges. We denote this set of vertices by
V_{free}^{i^{*}}
, and refer to them as ’free’ vertices for the algorithm,
because they are not restricted to participate in any matching. Hence, the family of their candidates will be larger (we will choose single vertices out of
n
vertices, and not pairs of vertices out of
n^{\delta}
edges). Therefore, as we decrease the value of
i^{*}
, the number of free edges decreases and the number of free vertices increases, which is a trade-off that we may use in order to prove different upper bounds on
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{p}\text{ succeeds})
.

Determine some
1\leq i^{*}\leq l
. Given the partition
V(K)=V_{M}^{i^{*}}\cup V_{free}^{i^{*}}=\bigcup_{i\leq i^{*}}V_{M_{i}}\cup V_{free}^{i^{*}}
, for every
1\leq i\leq i^{*}
we denote by
e_{i}{k\choose{2}}
the number of edges queried on round
i
that have both endpoints in
V_{M}^{i}\cup V_{free}^{i^{*}}
. The parameters
e_{i}
, together with the parameters
m_{i}
, will be a part of the template. We denote the number of free vertices by
|V_{free}^{i^{*}}|=v_{free}^{i^{*}}k
. We have that:

v_{free}^{i^{*}}=2\sum_{i^{*}<i\leq l}m_{i}

Similar to the proof of Theorem
5.1
, we will define candidate and feasible sets for each round number
i\leq i^{*}
, and prove an upper bound on the expected number of feasible sets. The definition will be recursive since candidate sets contain feasible sets and vice versa.
We start with the definition of

Denote by

Lemma 5.11
.

For every

and
1\leq i\leq i^{*}
, assuming

, we have:

\E_{1,\dots,i}[F^{i}]\leq n^{\Big[v_{free}^{i^{*}}+\sum_{j\leq i}\delta_{j}m_{j}\Big]k}\cdot 2^{-\sum_{j\leq i}e_{j}{k\choose{2}}}

The subscript indicates the rounds over which the expectation is taken.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on the round number
i
. For
i=1
, the number of
0
-candidates is at most
{{n}\choose{v_{free}^{i^{*}}k}}\leq n^{v_{free}^{i^{*}}k}
, and the number of
1
-candidates is at most
n^{v_{free}^{i^{*}}k}\cdot{{n^{\delta}}\choose{m_{1}k}}\leq n^{(v_{free}^{i^{*}}+\delta_{1}m_{1})k}
. The family of
1
-candidates is determined deterministically by the algorithm before it receives answers. Hence, each candidate is
1
-feasible with probability

, so we get the desired bound:

\E_{1}[F^{1}]\leq n^{\Big[v_{free}^{i^{*}}+\delta_{1}m_{1}\Big]k}\cdot 2^{-e_{1}{k\choose{2}}}

Assuming the claim is correct for some
i-1
, we wish to prove it for
i

i
-candidate

is the union of two sets

where

is an

-feasible set, and

is a set with a perfect matching in

. After round

there are at most

such pairs, and the probability such a pair induces an

-feasible set is at most

independently of the answers up to round

(since this probability comes from round
i
answers). By linearity of expectation we get:

\displaystyle\E_{1,\dots,i}[F^{i}]

\displaystyle=\E_{1,\dots,i-1}[\E_{i}[F^{i}]]\leq\E_{1,\dots,i-1}[F^{i-1}\cdot n^{\delta_{i}m_{i}k}\cdot 2^{-e_{i}{k\choose{2}}}]=

\displaystyle=n^{\delta_{i}m_{i}k}\cdot 2^{-e_{i}{k\choose{2}}}\cdot\E_{1,\dots,i-1}[F^{i-1}]\leq n^{\Big[v_{free}^{i^{*}}+\sum_{j\leq i}\delta_{j}m_{j}\Big]k}\cdot 2^{-\sum_{j\leq i}e_{j}{k\choose{2}}}

∎

For

As we defined in the intuitive explanation, an edge

the number of free edges, we have that:

e_{free}^{i^{*}}=1-\sum_{1\leq i\leq i^{*}}e_{i}

Using Lemma

\alpha_{*}((\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{l}),l)\leq\max_{p\in\mathcal{S}}\min_{1\leq i^{*}\leq l}\frac{2\Big[v_{free}^{i^{*}}+\sum_{i\leq i^{*}}\delta_{i}m_{i}\Big]}{1-e_{free}^{i^{*}}}

Proof.

\displaystyle=\frac{2\big[(1-\frac{\delta}{2})m_{1}+m_{2}+\delta m_{3}\big]}{1-e_{free}^{3}}=

\displaystyle=\frac{2\big[(1-\frac{\delta}{2})m_{1}+m_{2}+\delta(\frac{1}{2}-m_{1}-m_{2})\big]}{1-e_{free}^{3}}=

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta-(3\delta-2)m_{1}-(2\delta-2)m_{2}}{1-e_{free}^{3}}\leq

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta-(3\delta-2)(s_{1}+d_{1})-(2\delta-2)(\tilde{s_{2}}+\tilde{d_{2}})}{1-2[2m_{3}(s_{1}+\tilde{s_{2}})+2(s_{1}+d_{1})(\tilde{s_{2}}+\tilde{d_{2}})+m_{1}^{\prime}(\tilde{s_{2}}+2\tilde{d_{2}})]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2-(2+\delta)(s_{1}+d_{1})-2(\tilde{s_{2}}+\tilde{d_{2}})}{1-2[2(s_{1}+d_{1})(\tilde{s_{2}}+\tilde{d_{2}})+m_{1}^{\prime}(\tilde{s_{2}}+2\tilde{d_{2}})]}

•

The bounds we proved on the gradients of

We partitioned the domain into

\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}

\epsilon

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert}\leq\sqrt{4\Big(\frac{\epsilon}{2}\Big)^{2}}=\epsilon

\gamma

We chose

-net of the domain includes roughly

\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}
are to
1+\frac{\delta}{2}
in that area. We start with a sparse
\epsilon_{0}
-net for some large
\epsilon_{0}

\epsilon_{i}

\epsilon_{0}

\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}

y

\alpha_{i}(y)+L_{i}\epsilon_{i}\leq 1+\frac{\delta}{2}

16

C.2
Phase 2 - sequential least squares programming

In Phase 2 we prove that in any remaining hypercube all points satisfy
\min\{\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}\}\leq 1+\frac{\delta}{2}
. For each hypercube we use a sequential least squares programming (SLSQP) algorithm which maximizes a non linear function under non linear constraints. Since SLSQP finds a local maximum, we do not use it over the whole domain, but rather on small hypercubes (small in relation to the second derivatives of the functions). The SLSQP algorithm works when the objective function and the constraints are twice continuously differentiable. Our objective function is not differentiable (minimum of two functions) so we modify the optimization problem before applying SLSQP. Any point
x\in\gamma
satisfies
\alpha_{2}(x)=\alpha_{3}(x)=1+\frac{\delta}{2}
. Therefore, in order to find
\max_{s_{1},\tilde{s_{2}},\tilde{d_{2}}}\min\{\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}\}
for every
\delta
, it is enough to limit the search only to points in which
\alpha_{2}\geq 1+\frac{\delta}{2}
and
\alpha_{3}\geq 1+\frac{\delta}{2}
(we used the same argument on
\alpha_{1}
). We get the following constraints:

\displaystyle 1+\frac{\delta}{2}\leq\frac{\delta-(3\delta-2)s_{1}-(2\delta-2)(\tilde{s_{2}}+\tilde{d_{2}})}{1-2[2(\frac{1}{2}-(s_{1}+\tilde{s_{2}}+\tilde{d_{2}}))(s_{1}+\tilde{s_{2}})+2s_{1}(\tilde{s_{2}}+\tilde{d_{2}})+\big[\frac{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}{2+\delta}-s_{1}\big](\tilde{s_{2}}+2\tilde{d_{2}})]}

\displaystyle 1+\frac{\delta}{2}\leq\frac{2-(2+\delta)s_{1}-2(\tilde{s_{2}}+\tilde{d_{2}})}{1-2[2s_{1}(\tilde{s_{2}}+\tilde{d_{2}})+\big[\frac{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}{2+\delta}-s_{1}\big](\tilde{s_{2}}+2\tilde{d_{2}})]}

We use SLSQP to show that the only points which satisfy all constraints are either the point
v_{\tilde{d_{2}}=\frac{1}{2}}
or they are on the path
\gamma
. This will prove Lemma
C.1
.
Given a remaining hypercube close to
\gamma
, and assuming a point which satisfies all constraints exists in it, we want to guarantee it will be found by the SLSQP algorithm, when it is initialized in the center of the hypercube. We create a maximization problem with the previous and new constraints, where the objective function is the square of the distance from the path
\gamma
:

\max_{s_{1},\tilde{s_{2}},\tilde{d_{2}},\delta}s_{1}^{2}+(\tilde{s_{2}}-\frac{2-\delta}{2+\delta})^{2}+\tilde{d_{2}}^{2}

If the algorithm returns a point on
\gamma
(i.e., with distance
0
from the path) this means that no point in the hypercube satisfies all constraints (unless the hypercube intersects the path). Equivalently, for remaining hypercubes close to
v_{\tilde{d_{2}}=\frac{1}{2}}
, the maximized objective function will be the square of the distance from
v_{\tilde{d_{2}}=\frac{1}{2}}
:

\max_{s_{1},\tilde{s_{2}},\tilde{d_{2}},\delta}s_{1}^{2}+\tilde{s_{2}}^{2}+(\frac{1}{2}-\tilde{d_{2}})^{2}+(1-\delta)^{2}

For each hypercube remaining after Phase 1 (roughly
2\cdot 10^{8}
hypercubes) we applied SLSQP initialized in its center. No run of the algorithm failed to find a maximizing point, and in all runs this point was on the path
\gamma
(or it was
v_{\tilde{d_{2}}=\frac{1}{2}}
for the hypercubes in its neighborhood). Therefore, there is no other point in any of the remaining hypercubes which satisfies all constraints, and Lemma
C.1
follows.

C.3

We solve the optimization problem described in Section
5.2.4
