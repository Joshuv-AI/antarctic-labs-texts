Rong-Hua Wang


Abstract




Rational reductions for holonomic sequences

Abstract.

Given a holonomic sequence
F(n)
, we characterize rational functions
r(n)
so that
r(n)F(n)
can be summable.
We provide upper and lower bounds on the degree of the numerator of
r(k)
and show the denominator of
r(n)
can be read from annihilators of
F(k)
.
This illustration provides the so-called rational reductions which can be used to generate new multi-sum equalities and congruences from known ones.

Keywords
: rational reduction; holonomic sequence; congruences.

Mathematics Subject Classification
: 11A07, 11B65

1
Introduction

In 1990s, Wilf and Zeilberger
[
27
,
28
]
developed the classical WZ theory which can prove and discover new combinatorial identities mechanically.
Zeilberger’s algorithm (also known as the method of creative telescoping), designed based on the celebrated Gosper’s algorithm
[
8
]
, is the core of the WZ theory.
The crucial step of algorithm is determining the
summability
of a given hypergeometric term
t_{n}
. That is determining whether
t_{n}
can be written as a difference of another hypergeometric term.
During the past three decades, Zeilberger’s algorithm has been intensively developed and applied to combinatorics, number theoy and mathematical physics.
At the same time, it is still a attracting subject of ongoing research.
One can consult
[
5
]
for a selection of open problems in this context.

The classical Ramanujan-type series for
\pi^{-1}
are of the form

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(bn+c)\frac{a(n)}{m^{n}}=\frac{\lambda\sqrt{\mu}}{\pi},

(1.1)

where
b,c,m
are integers with
bm\neq 0
,
\mu
is a positive and square free integer,
\lambda
is a nonzero number, and
a(n)
is a product of binomial coefficients in
\{\binom{2n}{n}^{3},\binom{2n}{n}^{2}\binom{3n}{n},\binom{2n}{n}^{2}\binom{4n}{2n},\binom{2n}{n}\binom{3n}{n}\binom{6n}{3n}\}.

Extensive work has been done around applications,
q
-ananlogue forms and
p
-adic analogue forms of Ramaujan-type series. One can consult
[
2
,
3
,
7
,
9
,
10
,
22
,
11
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
and references therein for more related results.

In 2020, Sun
[
18
]
deduced many new series, similar to the classical Ramanujan-type series, in the form

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r(n)\frac{a(n)}{m^{n}}=\frac{\lambda\sqrt{\mu}}{\pi}

(1.2)

by the method of telescoping.
Here
r(n)
are rational functions,
a(n)
and
m
are give as in (
1.1
).
It is straightforward to see that
\frac{a(n)}{m^{n}}
is hypergeometric.
Later, Hou and Li
[
12
]
gave upper and lower bounds on the degree of the numerator of
r(n)
such that
r(n)t_{n}
is summable for a given hypergeometric term
t_{n}
,
and showed that the denominator of
r(n)
can be read from the Gosper representation of
t_{n+1}/t_{n}
. Based on the obtained criteria, they presented a systematic method to construct series like (
1.2
).

There are also many series for
\pi^{-1}
involving other combinatorial sequences.
For instance, in 2004, Chan, Chan and Liu
[
4
]
and Rogers
[
15
]
derived

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{5n+1}{64^{n}}\mathrm{Domb}(n)=\frac{8}{\sqrt{3}\pi}\text{ and }\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(3n+1)\frac{\mathrm{Domb}(n)}{(-32)^{n}}=\frac{2}{\pi}

respectively. Here

\mathrm{Domb}(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}^{2}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2(n-k)}{n-k}
is the
Domb numbers
.
Later, Sun presented many interesting conjectures involving the generalized central coefficient
T_{n}(b,c)
which denotes the coefficient of
x^{n}
in the expansion of
(x^{2}+bx+c)^{n}
. For example, Sun
[
17
,
19
]
posed the following conjectural series

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1638n+277}{(-240)^{3n}}\binom{2n}{n}\binom{3n}{n}T_{3n}(62,1)=\frac{44\sqrt{105}}{\pi},

which was confirmed by J. Wan and W. Zudilin
[
24
]
.
In 2021, Guo and Lian
[
10
]
found two series involving
H_{n}^{(2)}
corresponding to some classical Ramanujan-type series.
These two identities were proved by Wei
[
26
]
.
Here
H_{n}^{(m)}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k^{m}}
is the harmonic number of order
m
.
Recently, more series with summands involving Harmonic numbers has been discussed in
[
21
,
13
]
. Especially, Sun
[
21
]
posed many interesting conjectures on the values of some series involving harmonic numbers of orders not exceeding three.

Let
{\mathbb{K}}
be a field of characteristic
0
. The
annihilator
of a sequence
F(n)
over
{\mathbb{K}}
, denoted by
{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n)
, is defined by

{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n):=\left\{L=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)\sigma^{i}\in{\mathbb{K}}[n][\sigma]\mid L(F(n))=0\right\},

(1.3)

where
\sigma
is the shift operator and
L(F(n))=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)F(n+i)
.
A sequence
F(n)
is called
holonomic
or
P-recursive
if and only if
{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n)\neq\{0\}
.
We call
J
in (
1.3
) the order of
L
.
The minimum order of all such recurrences is called the
order
of
F(n)
, denoted by
{\mathrm{ord\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n)
.

By Zeilberger’s algorithm, one can check that all afore mentioned sequences such as
\mathrm{Domb}(n)
,
T_{n}(b,c)
and
H_{n}^{(2)}
are holonomic.
A holonomic sequence
F(n)
of order
J
is call
summable
if

F(n)=\Delta\left(\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}u_{i}(n)F(n+i)\right)

for some rational functions
u_{i}(n)\in{\mathbb{K}}(n)
,
0\leq i\leq J-1
.
Here
\Delta=\sigma-1
is the difference operator.

It happens frequently that a holonomic sequence
F(n)
is not summable.
However, when multiplied with a suitable rational function
r(n)
, the product can be summable.
In 1999, Abramov and van Hoeij
[
1
]
provided an algorithm to determine the summaility of
r(n)F(n)
for any given rational function
r(n)
and holonomic function
F(n)
.
Suppose
L\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n)
is of order
{\mathrm{ord\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n)
, they proved that
r(n)F(n)
is summable if and only if the corresponding adjoint equation with respect to
L
has rational solutions.

In this paper, we will characterise rational functions
r(n)=a(n)/b(n)
such that
r(n)F(n)
is summable for any given holonomic sequence
F(n)
.
Here
a(n),b(n)
are polynomials over
{\mathbb{K}}
and
\gcd(a(n),b(n))=1
.
More precisely, we first provide an upper bound and a lower bound on the degree of
p(n)\in{\mathbb{K}}[n]
if
p(n)F(n)
is summable.
The upper bound given here can be better than the one provided in
[
12
]
for some hypergeometric terms.
Then we will show the denominator
b(n)
can be read from annihilators of
F(n)
.
By choosing appropriate
b(n)
, one can generate new multi-sum identities and congruences mechanically from known ones.

The paper is organized as follows.
Section 2 is devoted to providing upper and lower bounds on the degree of
p(n)\in{\mathbb{K}}[n]
such that
p(n)F(n)
is summable. In Section 3, we first characterize the denominator of rational functions
r(n)
with
r(n)F(n)
being summable, and then introduce two kinds of rational reductions. Examples of discovery and proof of new equalities and congruences automatically are provided in Section 4.

2
Summablity of
p(n)F(n)

Let
F(n)
be a holonomic sequence, in this section, we will provide upper and lower bounds on the degree of
p(n)\in{\mathbb{K}}[n]
such that
p(n)F(n)
is summable.

For any recurrence operator
L=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)\sigma^{i}
, the
adjoint
of
L
is defined by

L^{\ast}=\sum_{i=0}^{J}\sigma^{-i}a_{i}(n)
, and thus

L^{\ast}(x(n))=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n-i)x(n-i)

for any
x(n)\in{\mathbb{K}}[n].

When
L\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n)
, by
[
6
, eq (1.6)]
or
[
23
, Proposition 3.2]
we know

L^{\ast}(x(n))F(n)=\Delta\left(-\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}u_{i}(n)F(n+i)\right),

(2.1)

where

u_{i}(n)=\sum_{j=1}^{J-i}a_{i+j}(n-j)x(n-j).

(2.2)

That means
L^{\ast}(x(n))F(n)
is summable for any
x(n)\in{\mathbb{K}}[n]
.
From equality (
2.1
) we obtain

\sum_{n=\ell}^{k-1}L^{\ast}(x(n))F(n)=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}u_{i}(\ell)F(\ell+i)\right)-\left(\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}u_{i}(k)F(k+i)\right).

(2.3)

Given
L=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)\sigma^{i}\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n)
.

The degree
of
L
, written as
\deg L
, is given by

\deg L=\max_{0\leq k\leq J}\{\deg b_{k}(n)-k\},

(2.4)

where

b_{k}(n)=\sum_{j=k}^{J}\binom{j}{k}a_{J-j}(n+j-J),\quad 0\leq k\leq J.

(2.5)

Let
d=\deg L
and

f(s)=\sum_{k=0}^{J}[n^{d+k}](b_{k}(n))s^{\underline{k}},

(2.6)

where

[n^{d+k}](b_{k}(n))
denotes the coefficient of
n^{d+k}
in
b_{k}(n)
and
s^{\underline{k}}
denotes the falling factorial
s(s-1)\cdots(s-k+1)
.
Let

R_{L}=\{s\in{\mathbb{N}}\mid f(s)=0\}.

(2.7)

Then
L
is called
degenerated
if
R_{L}\neq\emptyset
and
nondegenerated
otherwise.

In 2023, Wang and Zhong
[
25
, Lemma 2.3]
proved that for any nonzero polynomial
x(n)\in{\mathbb{K}}[n]
, we have

\deg L^{\ast}(x(n))\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}<\deg L+\deg x(n),&\hbox{if $L$ is degenerated and $\deg x(n)\in R_{L}$,}\\
=\deg L+\deg x(n),&\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(2.8)

The following theorem shows that an upper bound on the degree of
p(n)
such that
p(n)F(n)
is summable can be given by the degree and continued zero index of
L
.
The
continued zero index
of
L
, denoted by
C_{L}
, is defined as follows:
if
L^{\ast}(1)\neq 0
, then
C_{L}=0
; if
L^{\ast}(1)=0
, then
C_{L}
is the positive integer such that
L^{\ast}(n^{C_{L}})\neq 0
and

L^{\ast}(n^{i})=0
if
0\leq i\leq C_{L}-1
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose
F(n)
is a holonomic sequence and
L=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)\sigma^{i}\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n)
.
Then there exists a nonzero polynomial
p(n)
such that
p(n)F(n)
is summable and

\deg p(n)\leq d+C_{L},

(2.9)

where
d=\deg L
and
C_{L}
is the continued zero index of
L
.

Proof.
By the definition of
C_{L}
, we have
L^{\ast}(n^{C_{L}})\neq 0
.
With the help of (
2.8
) and (
2.1
), one can derive that

\deg L^{\ast}(n^{C_{L}})\leq d+C_{L}

(2.10)

and
L^{\ast}(n^{C_{L}})F(n)
is summable.

The inequality (
2.9
) provides an upper bound.
It may happen that there exists a polynomial
p(n)
such that
p(n)F(n)
is summable and
\deg p(n)<d+C_{L}
.

Example 2.2
.

Let

F(n)=\frac{(-1)^{n}f_{n}}{n(n-1)},

where
f_{n}
denotes the Franel number
f_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}^{3}
.
By Zeilberger’s algorithm, one can see

L=8n(n^{2}-1)-n(7n^{2}+21n+16)\sigma-(n+2)^{3}\sigma^{2}\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n).

Then
J={\mathrm{ord\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n)=2
,
d=\deg L=2
,
C_{L}=0
, and thus the upper bound given in
(
2.9
) is
2
.
Take
p(n)=2-3n
, one can check that

(2-3n)F(n)=\Delta\left((8n^{3}-5n-2)F(n)+(n+1)^{3}F(n+1)\right).

Then we know
(2-3n)F(n)
is summable while
\deg(2-3n)=1<d
.

The following example shows that the continued zero index
C_{L}
in (
2.9
) is necessary.

Example 2.3
.

Let
H_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k}
denote the Harmonic number and

F(n)=\frac{H_{n}}{n(n+1)}.

By Zeilberger’s algorithm, one can see

L=n(n+1)^{2}-(n+1)(n+2)(2n+3)\sigma+(n+2)^{2}(n+3)\sigma^{2}\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n).

Then
J={\mathrm{ord\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n)=2
,
d=\deg L=1
and
C_{L}=1
.
Let
a_{0}(n)=n(n+1)^{2}
and
a_{2}(n)=(n+2)^{2}(n+3)
.
It is easy to check that
\gcd(a_{0}(n),a_{2}(n+h))=1
for any
h\in{\mathbb{N}}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
.
Note that

(n^{2}+n)F(n)=\Delta\left(n(n^{3}+2n^{2}-1)F(n)-(n-1)(n+1)^{2}(n+2)F(n+1)\right).

Then we know
(n^{2}+n)F(n)
is summable, here
\deg(n^{2}+n)=d+C_{L}
=2.
Since
C_{L}=1
, Theorem 2.4 and Lemma 2.5 in
[
25
]
lead to the fact that there exists no polynomial
p(n)
with
\deg p(n)\leq 1
such that
p(n)F(n)
is summable.

Example
2.3
also illustrates that for some holonomic sequences, the upper bound given in Theorem
2.1
can also be a lower bound.
Next we show this happens frequently.
To this aim, we first provide two lemmas on
C_{L}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
L=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)\sigma^{i}
. Then the continued zero index
C_{L}\leq J
.

Proof.
Note that
f(s)
given by (
2.6
) is a polynomial in
s
and
\deg f(s)\leq J
.
Thus the set
R_{L}
defined in (
2.7
) contains at most
J
elements. The proof then follows directly from (
2.8
) and the definition of
C_{L}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

If
L=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)\sigma^{i}
is nondegenerated. Then
C_{L}=0
.

Proof.
As
L
is nondegenerated, we know
\deg L^{\ast}(x(n))=d+\deg x(n)
by (
2.8
).
Here
d=\deg L
.
Then
\deg L^{\ast}(n^{C_{L}})=d+C_{L}\geq 0
as
L^{\ast}(n^{C_{L}})\neq 0
, and thus

d\geq-C_{L}\geq-J
by Lemma
2.4
.
Note that
\deg L^{\ast}(1)=d\geq-J
, one can see
L^{\ast}(1)\neq 0
.
Hence
C_{L}=0
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
L=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)\sigma^{i}\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n)
with
a_{0}(n)a_{J}(n)\neq 0
and
J>0
be the order of
F(n)
.
Suppose
\gcd(a_{0}(n),a_{J}(n+h))=1
for any
h\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
L
is nondegenerated.
If
p(n)
is a nonzero polynomial such that
p(n)F(n)
is summable, then

\deg p(n)\geq\deg L+C_{L}.

Proof.
Since
\gcd(a_{0}(n),a_{J}(n+h))=1
for any
h\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have
p(n)F(n)
is summable if and only if
p(n)=L^{\ast}(x(n))
for some
x(n)\in{\mathbb{K}}[n]
by
[
25
, Theorem 2.4]
.
As
L
is nondegenerated, we have

\deg p(n)=\deg L^{\ast}(x(n))=\deg L+\deg x(n)\geq\deg L+C_{L}

by Lemma
2.5

Since hypergeometric terms are all holonomic, Theorem
2.1
also provides an upper bound for hypergeometric terms. In general, the upper bound provided in
[
12
]
may be superior than (
2.9
) since Q.-H. Hou and G.-J. Li
[
12
]
considered the Gosper representation of
F(n)
.
The following example shows when
F(n)
contains no polynomial part, the upper bound given by Theorem
2.1
can be better.

Example 2.7
.

Let

F(n)=\frac{\binom{2n}{n}^{4}}{(2n-1)^{4}256^{n}}.

It is easy to check that

L=(2n-1)^{4}-16(n+1)^{4}\sigma\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n).

Then
J={\mathrm{ord\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n)=1
,
d=\deg L=3
and
C_{L}=0
.
Hence the upper bound given in (
2.9
) is
d=3
while the one presented in
[
12
]
is
4
.

3
Summablity of
r(n)F(n)

In this section, we will characterise the denominator of rational function
r(n)
such that
r(n)F(n)
is summable.
The criteria motivate the discovery of the so-called
rational reduction
, which can be used to generated new equalities from known ones.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
L=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)\sigma^{i}\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n)
with
a_{0}(n)a_{J}(n)\neq 0
and
J>0
be the order of
F(n)
.
Let
a(n),b(n)\in{\mathbb{K}}[n]
such that
\frac{a(n)}{b(n)}F(n)
is summable.
Suppose that for
\forall h\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\gcd(a_{0}(n),a_{J}(n+h))=\gcd(b(n),b(n+J+h))=1

(3.1)

and

\gcd(a_{0}(n),b(n+J+h))=\gcd(b(n),a_{J}(n+h))=1.

(3.2)

Then
b(n)\mid a(n)
.

Proof.
Let
G(n)=\frac{F(n)}{b(n)}
. Then
a(n)G(n)
is summable.
Since
L=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)\sigma^{i}\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n)
, one can check that

L_{1}=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)b(n+i)\sigma^{i}\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}G(n).

Take
b_{i}(n)=a_{i}(n)b(n+i)
,
0\leq i\leq J
.
When conditions in Theorem
3.1
are satisfied, we have

\gcd(b_{0}(n),b_{J}(n+h))=1
for any nonnegative integer
h
.
Thus by
[
25
, Theorem2.4]
we derive that

a(n)=L_{1}^{\ast}(x(n))=b(n)\sum\limits_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n-i)x(n-i)=b(n)L^{\ast}(x(n)),

for some
x(n)\in{\mathbb{K}}[n]
.
This completes the proof.

By Theorem
3.1
, if
\frac{a(n)}{b(n)}F(n)
is summable with
b(n)\nmid a(n)
and conditions (
3.1
) holds, then
b(n)
must contain factors from
a_{0}(n-J-h)
or
a_{J}(n+h)
for some nonnegative integer
h
.

To proceed, some notations are needed.
Suppose
A(n)\in{\mathbb{K}}[n]
and
I\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
The
shift product operator of order
I
, denoted by
SP_{I}
, is defined by

SP_{I}(A(n))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{I}A(n+j),&\hbox{when $I>0$,}\\[10.0pt]
1,&\hbox{when $I=0$,}\\[5.0pt]
\prod\limits_{j=1}^{-I}A(n-j),&\hbox{when $I<0$.}\end{array}\right.

(3.3)

Let
L=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)\sigma^{i}
,
a_{i}(n)\in{\mathbb{K}}[n]
. Define

d_{L}=\max_{0\leq i\leq J}\{\deg a_{i}(n)\}.

(3.4)

The following lemma shows the connection between
d_{L}
and
\deg L
is closely related to the degenerality of
L
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Given
L=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)\sigma^{i}
,
a_{i}(n)\in{\mathbb{K}}[n]
, and
d_{L}
be defined by
(
3.4
).
Then
\deg L\leq d_{L}
, and
L
is nondegenerated if
\deg L=d_{L}
.

Proof.
By the definition of
b_{k}(n)
in (
2.5
), it is easy to see
\deg b_{k}(n)\leq d_{L}
.
Then

\deg L=\max_{0\leq k\leq J}\{\deg b_{k}(n)-k\}\leq\max_{0\leq k\leq J}\{d_{L}-k\}=d_{L}.

When
\deg L=d_{L}
, we know
\deg b_{0}(n)=d_{L}
.
thus

f(s)=\sum_{k=0}^{J}[n^{\deg L+k}](b_{k}(n))s^{\underline{k}}=[n^{d_{L}}](b_{0}(n))

is a nonzero constant.
So
L
is nondegenerated.

When
\deg L=d_{L}
, the operator
L
is called
strongly nondegenerated
, as the condition
\deg L=d_{L}
is not necessary for
L
to be nondegenerated.

Example 3.3
.

Let
F(n)=\mathrm{Domb}(n)/16^{n}
, Zeilberger’s algorithm leads to

L=8(2+n)^{3}\sigma^{2}-(3+2n)(12+15n+5n^{2})\sigma+2(1+n)^{3}\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n).

One can check that

\deg L=2
and
L
is nondegenerated while
d_{L}=3>\deg L
.

Assume
L=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)\sigma^{i}\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n)
is nondegenerated.
For any polynomial
p(n)\in{\mathbb{K}}[n]
, by equality (
2.8
) we can decompose
p(n)
as

p(n)=q(n)+\tilde{p}(n),

such that
q(n),\tilde{p}(n)\in{\mathbb{K}}[n]
,
q(n)F(n)
is summable and
\deg\tilde{p}(n)<\deg L
, utilizing the division algorithm.
The above decomposition is called
polynomial reduction
of
p(n)
with
L
in
[
25
]
.

Next, we will introduce two kinds of rational reductions which can be used after polynomial reduction.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
F(n)
be a holonomic sequence,
L=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)\sigma^{i}\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n)
is strongly nondegenerated and

a_{0}(n)=A_{0}(n)\tilde{A}_{0}(n),

where
A_{0}(n),\tilde{A}_{0}(n)\in{\mathbb{K}}[n]
.
For any
p(n)\in{\mathbb{K}}[n]
, if positive integer
I\geq J
, we can find a polynomial
\tilde{p}(n)
and a holonomic sequence
T(n)
such that

p(n)F(n)=\frac{\tilde{p}(n)}{SP_{-I}(A_{0}(n)))}F(n)+\Delta(T(n)),

(3.5)

and
\deg\tilde{p}(n)<\deg L+(J-1)\deg A_{0}(n)
.

Proof.
Take
G(n)=\frac{F(n)}{SP_{-I}(A_{0}(n))}
.
Since
L=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)\sigma^{i}\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n)
, it is straightforward to check that

\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)SP_{-I}(A_{0}(n+i))\sigma^{i}\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}G(n).

Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)SP_{-I}(A_{0}(n+i))\sigma^{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{0}(n)\prod_{j=0}^{I-J}A_{0}(n-j)\prod_{j=I-J+1}^{I}A_{0}(n-j)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{J}a_{i}(n)\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}A_{0}(n+j)\prod_{j=0}^{I-J}A_{0}(n-j)\prod_{j=I-J+1}^{I-i}A_{0}(n-j)\sigma^{i}.

Then we have

L_{1}=\tilde{A}_{0}(n)\prod_{j=I-J+1}^{I}A_{0}(n-j)+\sum_{i=1}^{J}a_{i}(n)\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}A_{0}(n+j)\prod_{j=I-J+1}^{I-i}A_{0}(n-j)\sigma^{i}

is an annihilator of
G(n)
.
Note that
L_{1}
is strongly nondegenerated as
L
is strongly nondegenerated and the shift opeartor
\sigma^{j}
does not change the leading coefficient. Hence

\deg L_{1}=\deg L+(J-1)\deg A_{0}(n).

The polynomial reduction with
L_{1}
leads to the decompostition

p(n)SP_{-I}(A_{0}(n))=\tilde{p}(n)+q(n),

such that
\tilde{p}(n)<\deg L_{1}
and
q(n)G(n)=\Delta(T(n))
for some holonomic sequence
T(n)
.
Then

\displaystyle p(n)F(n)=

\displaystyle p(n)SP_{-I}(A_{0}(n))G(n)=(\tilde{p}(n)+q(n))G(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{p}(n)}{SP_{-I}(A_{0}(n))}F(n)+\Delta(T(n)).

This completes the proof.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
F(n)
be a holonomic sequence,
L=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)\sigma^{i}\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n)
is strongly nondegenerated and

a_{J}(n)=A_{J}(n)\tilde{A}_{J}(n),

where
A_{J}(n),\tilde{A}_{J}(n)\in{\mathbb{K}}[n]
.
For any
p(n)\in{\mathbb{K}}[n]
, if positive integer
I\geq J
, we can find a polynomial
\tilde{p}(n)
and a holonomic sequence
T(n)
such that

p(n)F(n)=\frac{\tilde{p}(n)}{SP_{I}(A_{J}(n-J)))}F(n)+\Delta(T(n)),

(3.6)

and
\deg\tilde{p}(n)<\deg L+(J-1)\deg A_{J}(n)
.

Proof.
Take
G(n)=\frac{F(n)}{SP_{I}(A_{J}(n-J))}
.
Note that

\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)SP_{I}(A_{J}(n-J+i))\sigma^{i}\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}G(n).

and that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(n)SP_{I}(A_{J}(n-J+i))\sigma^{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}a_{i}(n)\prod_{j=1}^{J-i-1}A_{J}(n-j)\prod_{j=0}^{I-J}A_{J}(n+j)\prod_{j=I-J+1}^{I-J+i}A_{J}(n+j)\sigma^{i}

\displaystyle+\tilde{A}_{J}(n)\prod_{j=0}^{I-J}A_{J}(n+j)\prod_{j=I-J+1}^{I}A_{J}(n+j)\sigma^{J}.

Thus one can see

\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}a_{i}(n)\prod_{j=1}^{J-i-1}A_{J}(n-j)\prod_{j=I-J+1}^{I-J+i}A_{J}(n+j)\sigma^{i}+\tilde{A}_{J}(n)\prod_{j=I-J+1}^{I}A_{J}(n+j)\sigma^{J}

falls into
{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}G(n)
.
The proof then is similar to that of Theorem
3.4
.

The decompositions (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) will be called the
rational reduction
to
p(n)
with
L
.
In Theorem
3.4
and Theorem
3.5
, the operator
L
is assumed to be strongly nondegenerated.
When this is not the case, all discussions in the proof go well except the determination of
\deg L_{1}
.
Since
\deg L_{1}
is free of
I
and

\deg(p(n)SP_{I}(A(n)))=\deg p(n)+I\cdot\deg A(n)
for any polynomial
A(n)
.
When
I
is large enough, one can still obtain equalities as (
3.5
) or (
3.6
).
Example
4.2
in the next subsection is a concrete illustration.

4
Applications of rational reductions

In this section, we will show how rational reduction provides an algorithmic way to generate new equalities and congruences from known ones.
We will take Domb numbers as examples to illustrate the process.

In 2009, Rogers
[
15
]
derived

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(3n+1)\frac{\mathrm{Domb}(n)}{(-32)^{n}}=\frac{2}{\pi}.

(4.1)

Sun
[
20
]
proposed series of conjectured identities with the summand in the form of
p(n)\frac{\mathrm{Domb}(n)}{(-32)^{n}}
, where
p(n)
is a polynomial and
\deg p(n)\geq 3
.
The conjectures are confirmed recently in
[
25
]
by the polynomial reduction.
Next, we provide several equalities involving
r(n)\frac{\mathrm{Domb}(n)}{(-32)^{n}}
with
r(n)
being rational using the rational reductions generated in Section 3.

Theorem 4.1
.

The following equalities holds:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{27+103n+141n^{2}+78n^{3}+15n^{4}}{(n+1)^{2}(n+2)^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{Domb}(n)}{(-32)^{n}}=\frac{18}{\pi}

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{239+807n+993n^{2}+582n^{3}+165n^{4}+18n^{5}}{(n+1)^{3}(n+2)^{3}}\frac{\mathrm{Domb}(n)}{(-32)^{n}}=80-\frac{162}{\pi}

(4.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2+5n-9n^{2}-21n^{3}+39n^{4}}{n^{2}(n-1)^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{Domb}(n)}{(-32)^{n}}=-9\left(\frac{2}{\pi}-\frac{11}{12}\right)

(4.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{-26-21n+105n^{2}-9n^{3}-147n^{4}+306n^{5}}{n^{3}(n-1)^{3}}\frac{\mathrm{Domb}(n)}{(-32)^{n}}=\frac{162}{\pi}-\frac{217}{8}

(4.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{5729+8701n+5895n^{2}+1879n^{3}+228n^{4}}{(n+1)^{2}(n+2)^{2}(n+3)^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{Domb}(n)}{(-32)^{n}}=\frac{486}{\pi}.

(4.6)

Proof.

Let
F(n)=\mathrm{Domb}(n)/(-32)^{n}
.
By Zeilberger’s algorithm, we find

L=(n+1)^{3}+(2n+3)(5n^{2}+15n+12)\sigma+16(n+2)^{3}\sigma^{2}\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n).

If we take
A_{2}(n)=(n+2)^{2}
which divides
(n+2)^{3}
and
I=2
.
Then

G(n)=\frac{F(n)}{SP_{2}(A_{2}(n-2))}=\frac{F(n)}{(n+1)^{2}(n+2)^{2}}.

By Zeilberger’s algorithm, we find that
L_{1}=\sum_{i=0}^{2}a_{i}(n)\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}G(n)
with

a_{2}(n)=16(2+n)(3+n)^{2}(4+n)^{2}
,
a_{1}(n)=(3+n)^{2}(3+2n)(12+15n+5n^{2})
, and
a_{0}(n)=(1+n)^{5}.

One can check that
L_{1}
is strongly nondegenerated and
\deg L_{1}=5
.
So
P_{s}(n)=L_{1}^{\ast}(n^{s})
is polynomial and
\deg P_{s}(n)=s+5
for any
s\geq 0
.
Note that
(3n+1)F(n)
=
q(n)G(n)
, where
q(n)=(3n+1)(n+1)^{2}(n+2)^{2}
.
Since
\deg q(n)=5=\deg L_{1}
, by polynomial reduction to
q(n)
with
L_{1}
, we have

q(n)=\frac{1}{9}(27+103n+141n^{2}+78n^{3}+15n^{4})-\frac{1}{9}P_{0}(n).

(4.7)

From equality (
2.1
), we know

P_{0}(n)G(n)=\Delta\left(-u_{0}(n)G(n)-u_{1}(n)G(n+1)\right)

with
u_{i}(n)=\sum_{j=1}^{2-i}a_{i+j}(n-j)
.
Multiplying both sides of (
4.7
) with
G(n)
and then summing over
n
from
0
to
\infty
derives equality (
4.2
) from (
4.1
).

If we take
A_{2}(n)=(n+2)^{3}
and
I=2
, similar discussions lead to equality
(
4.3
).
Taking
A_{0}(n)=(n+1)^{2}
or
A_{0}(n)=(n+1)^{3}
and
I=2
will arrive at equalities
(
4.4
)
and
(
4.5
)
respectively. Note that the summations have to start from
2
.
Equality (
4.6
) follows by taking
A_{2}(n)=(n+2)^{2}
and
I=3
.
We can obtain infinitely many new series by increasing
I
.

In 2013, Sun
[
16
]
conjectured that when
p
is a prime and
p\equiv 1\pmod{3}
,

\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}(3n+1)\frac{\mathrm{Domb}(n)}{16^{n}}\equiv 0\pmod{p^{2}}.

(4.8)

This conjecture was confirmed and generalized by Mao and Liu
[
14
]
recently.
By the rational reduction and congruence (
4.8
), we can discover new arithmetic properties of Domb numbers.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
p
be a prime and
p\equiv 1\pmod{3}
. Then

\sum_{n=2}^{p-1}\frac{(n+1)^{2}}{n(n-1)}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{Domb}(n)}{16^{n}}\equiv\frac{3}{2}\pmod{p^{2}}.

(4.9)

Proof.
Let
F(n)=\mathrm{Domb}(n)/16^{n}
, we know by Example
3.3
that

L=8(2+n)^{3}\sigma^{2}-(3+2n)(12+15n+5n^{2})\sigma+2(1+n)^{3}\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}F(n)

and that
L
is not strongly nondegenerated.
Taking
A_{0}(n)=n+1
and
I=2
, we have
G(n)=\frac{F(n)}{SP_{-I}(A_{0}(n))}=\frac{F(n)}{n(n-1)}
and

L_{1}=8(2+n)^{4}\sigma^{2}-n(3+2n)(12+15n+5n^{2})\sigma+2(-1+n)n(1+n)^{2}\in{\mathrm{ann\hskip 2.15277pt}}G(n).

One can check that
L_{1}
is nondegenerated and
\deg L_{1}=3
.
By polynomial reduction with respect to
L_{1}
, we have

n(n-1)(3n+1)=-L_{1}^{\ast}(n^{0})+2(n+1)^{2}.

(4.10)

Equality (
2.3
) leads to

\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{p-1}L_{1}^{\ast}(n^{0})G(n)=\

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{1}u_{i}(2)G(2+i)-\sum_{i=0}^{1}u_{i}(p)G(k+p)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle 5-2p^{2}(2-3p+p^{2})G(-1+p)+8p^{4}G(p)

\displaystyle\equiv\

\displaystyle 5\pmod{p^{2}},

where
u_{0}(n)=2+7n+6n^{2}-5n^{3}-2n^{4}
and

u_{1}(n)=8(1+n)^{4}
.
Multiplying both sides of (
4.10
) with
G(n)
and then summing over
n
from
2
to
p-1
arrives at

\sum_{n=2}^{p-1}\frac{(n+1)^{2}}{n(n-1)}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{Domb}(n)}{16^{n}}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(5+\sum_{n=2}^{p-1}(3n+1)\frac{\mathrm{Domb}(n)}{16^{n}}\right)\equiv\frac{3}{2}\pmod{p^{2}},

when
p\equiv 1\pmod{3}
.
