Abstract
. We prove that a polynomial mapping f : 𝕂 n ↦ 𝕂 p f:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\mapsto{\mathbb{K}^{p}} , where 𝕂 = ℝ \mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R} or ℂ \mathbb{C} , attains a Lipschitz trivial value 𝐜 {\bf c} if and only if there exist a polynomial mapping g : 𝕂 m ↦ 𝕂 p g:\mathbb{K}^{m}\mapsto{\mathbb{K}^{p}} , for which the value 𝐜 {\bf c} is a regular value of properness, and a linear surjective projection π : 𝕂 n → 𝕂 m \pi:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\to\mathbb{K}^{m} such that f = g ∘ π f=g\circ\pi . The integer m m is the 𝕂 \mathbb{K} -codimension of the accumulation set at infinity of the level f − 1 ​ ( 𝐜 ) f^{-1}({\bf c}) in the hyperplane at infinity. In the complex case, it is equivalent to require the mapping g g be generically finite and dominant. Last, we show this result cannot extend to rational mappings over 𝕂 n {\mathbb{K}^{n}} .



Introduction

Let
f:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
be a dominant polynomial mapping over
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
.
There exists a smallest subset
{\rm Bif}_{k}(f)
, contained in an algebraic
subset of
{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
of positive codimension, with the following property: the
mapping
f
induces a locally trivial
\mathcal{C}^{k}
fibre bundle structure over each
connected component of
{\mathbb{K}^{p}}\setminus{\rm Bif}_{k}(f)
. The notion of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}

triviality is generally finer than that of
\mathcal{C}^{0}
triviality and over the
last fifty years, following the seminal paper
[
10
]
, a
significant literature about
the
\mathcal{C}^{0}
and the
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
local triviality has been developed
(see for instance

[
11
,
3
,
9
,
6
,
5
]
).

This paper investigates an intermediate case: when and over which
subset of values the mapping induces a locally
bi-Lipschitz trivial fibre bundle structure. Our goal is to characterize
polynomial mappings admitting
Lipschitz trivial values, that is over
a neighbourhood of which there is a bi-Lipschitz trivialization, problem
recently raised in
[
1
]
. Our main result
Theorem
3.1
when combined with Proposition
2.8

implies the following

Theorem
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
be a polynomial mapping and let
n-1-m
be
the dimension of
the set of accumulation points at infinity of the fibre
f^{-1}({\bf c})
.
The mapping
f
attains
the Lipschitz trivial value
{\bf c}

if and only if

f=g\circ\pi,

for a linear surjective projection
\pi:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{m}}
and a polynomial mapping

g:{\mathbb{K}^{m}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
for which
{\bf c}
is a regular value of properness.

In the complex case, the statement equivalently requires that
m=p
and
the polynomial mapping
g\penalty\ :\penalty\ {\mathbb{C}^{p}}\penalty\ \to\penalty\ {\mathbb{C}^{p}}
be dominant and generically finite (see
Corollary
4.1
). Therefore, either almost all values of a
complex polynomial mapping are Lipschitz trivial or there are none.
In contrast with the complex case, there exist non-proper real polynomial
mappings admitting values of properness.

The main result completely describes the real and complex polynomial mappings
admitting Lipschitz trivial values, recovering the case of complex
polynomial functions of
[
1
]
. Our proof is very different, more concise,
and covers both real and complex cases. Moreover, we show that the
main theorem cannot extend without further hypotheses to a wider class of
rational mappings (even those which are regulous, see
[
2
]
).

The article is organized as follows.
Section
2
presents properties of Lipschitz trivial values
of differentiable mappings. The main result is proved in Section
3

while Section
4
describes the set of Lipschitz trivial
values in the real and complex cases. Section
5
deals with
the relation
between Lipschitz trivial values of a real mapping and that of its
complexification. In Section
6
we show that the main result
cannot extend to rational mappings.

1.
Preliminaries

Throughout the paper
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{R}
.
We use the conventions that
\dim\emptyset=-1
and empty mappings are
proper and continuous.

We embed the affine space
{\mathbb{K}^{n}}
in
{\mathbb{KP}^{n}}
as
{\bf x}\mapsto[{\bf x}:1]
. The
hyperplane at infinity
H_{\infty}:={\mathbb{KP}^{n}}\setminus{\mathbb{K}^{n}}
consists of the points
of
{\mathbb{KP}^{n}}
of the form
[{\bf v}:0]
.

Definition 1.1
.

The accumulation set at infinity
X^{\infty}
of a subset
X
of
{\mathbb{K}^{n}}
is
defined as

X^{\infty}:=\overline{X}^{{\mathbb{KP}^{n}}}\cap H_{\infty},

where
\overline{X}^{{\mathbb{KP}^{n}}}
is the closure
of
X
taken in
{\mathbb{KP}^{n}}
.

The following notions lie at the heart of our problem.

Definition 1.2
.

A mapping
\varphi:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
is proper at the value
{\bf c}\in{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
, if there exists a
neighbourhood
\mathcal{V}
of
{\bf c}
in
{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
such that the restriction mapping
of
\varphi
to
\varphi^{-1}(\mathcal{V})
is proper.
Let us denote by
J(\varphi)
the Jelonek set of values at which the mapping
\varphi

is not proper.

The Jelonek set of a real or complex polynomial mappings is always contained in
an algebraic set of dimension at most
n-1
, see
[
4
,
5
]
.

Definition 1.3
.

A mapping
\varphi:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
is topologically trivial at the value

{\bf c}\in{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
, if there exist a neighbourhood
\mathcal{V}
of
{\bf c}
in
{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
and a
trivializing homeomorphism

(1)

H:\varphi^{-1}({\bf c})\times\mathcal{V}\to\varphi^{-1}(\mathcal{V})

which satisfies
(\varphi\circ H)({\bf x},{\bf t})={\bf t}
.

When
H
is a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
diffeomorphism, the mapping
\varphi
is called

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
trivial at the value
{\bf c}
. The complement of the set of
values at which the mapping
\varphi
is
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
trivial is
the set of bifurcation values
{\rm Bif}(\varphi)
, see
[
10
]
.

Remark 1.4
.

In particular, the mapping
\varphi
is locally trivial at any value
of the open subset
{\mathbb{K}^{p}}\setminus{\rm clos}(Im(\varphi))
, the complement of
the closure of the image of
\varphi
.

Example 1.5
.

Note that the real polynomial function
x\mapsto x^{2021}
is topologically
trivial at each
c\in\mathbb{R}
, but not
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
trivial at
0
.

Let
K_{0}(\varphi):=\varphi(\text{crit}(\varphi))
be the set of critical values of

\varphi
. The mapping
\varphi
is said to be regular at
{\bf c}
once
{\bf c}\notin K_{0}(\varphi)
.
When a polynomial mapping
\varphi
is proper and regular at a value
{\bf c}
,
the mapping
\varphi
is
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
trivial at
{\bf c}
by Sard’s and Ehresmann’s
Theorems. Thus the set
{\rm Bif}(\varphi)\setminus K_{0}(\varphi)
contains only non-proper
values when it is non-empty. Moreover, by
[
5
]
, when
\varphi
is
polynomial and
n=p
we get

{\rm Bif}(\varphi)=K_{0}(\varphi)\cup J(\varphi)
.

2.
Lipschitz trivial values

In this section we present general properties of differentiable mappings with
Lipschitz trivial values.

Let again
\varphi:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
be a mapping.

Any subset
X
of
{\mathbb{K}^{n}}
inherits a metric structure from restricting the ambient
euclidean distance to
X
, this metric is called the outer metric
on
X
. On a product
X\times Y
of
metric spaces we will consider the product metric.

Definition 2.1
.

A Lipschitz trivial value
{\bf c}\in{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
of the mapping
\varphi
is a value
such that there exists a trivializing homeomorphism as in (
1
)
of Definition
1.3
which is bi-Lipschitz with respect to the outer
metrics.
Denote by
{\rm L}(\varphi)
the set of Lipschitz trivial values of
\varphi
.

Remark 2.2
.

In light of Definition
2.1
and Remark
1.4
, the
subset
{\rm L}(\varphi)
is open and contains
{\mathbb{K}^{p}}\setminus{\rm clos}(Im(\varphi))
.
In particular, the mapping
\varphi
attains a Lipschitz
trivial value only if its image is Zariski-dense in
{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
.

Example 2.3
.

The set of Lipschitz trivial values of the polynomial mapping
\mathbb{K}^{3}\to\mathbb{K}^{2}

defined as
(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,xy+xz)
is

\mathbb{K}^{2}\setminus 0\times\mathbb{K}
. There is a single critical value
(0,0)
, and none
of the
values
(0,b)
with
b\neq 0
is taken. Moreover,
each level
(a,b)
with
a\neq 0
is an affine line.

The next result emphasizes the rigid asymptotic behaviour of levels near a
Lipschitz trivial value.

Proposition 2.4
.

Assume that
{\bf c}
is a Lipschitz trivial value of
\varphi:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
. There exists a neighbourhood

\mathcal{V}
of
{\bf c}
such that the following properties hold:

(i)

the mapping
\varphi
is Lipschitz on

\varphi^{-1}(\mathcal{V})
;

(ii)

there exist
0<\delta<\epsilon
such that

T_{\delta}(\varphi^{-1}({\bf c}))\subset\varphi^{-1}(\mathcal{V})\subset T_{\epsilon}(\varphi^{-1}({\bf c})),

where the open tube
T_{r}(S)
of radius
r
around a subset
S
of
{\mathbb{K}^{n}}

is defined as

T_{r}(S):=\{{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{K}^{n}}:{\rm dist}({\bf x},S)<r\}.

Proof.

Denote by
d_{\bf c}
the outer metric on
\varphi^{-1}({\bf c})
and by

\mathcal{U}:=\varphi^{-1}(\mathcal{V})
.
Since
\varphi
has a Lipschitz trivial value at
{\bf c}
, there exist an open ball

\mathcal{V}=B^{p}({\bf c},r)
of
{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
and a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism

G=(\varphi,\psi):\mathcal{U}\mapsto\mathcal{V}\times\varphi^{-1}({\bf c}).

Therefore, there exists
L>1
such that for any
{\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}
we have

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\left\lVert{\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert G({\bf x})-G({\bf x}^{\prime})\right\rVert\leq L\left\lVert{\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}\right\rVert.

Point (i) follows from inequalities (
2
) since
for all
{\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}
we find

\|\varphi({\bf x})-\varphi({\bf x}^{\prime})\|\leq\|\varphi({\bf x})-\varphi({\bf x}^{\prime})\|+d_{\bf c}(\psi({\bf x}),\psi({\bf x}^{\prime}))\leq L\left\lVert{\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}\right\rVert.

To prove (ii), define the following radii

\delta:=\frac{r}{L}\;\;{\rm and}\;\;\epsilon:=Lr.

Point (i) yields the first inclusion
T_{\delta}(\varphi^{-1}({\bf c}))\subset\mathcal{U}
.
Note that for
{\bf c},{\bf t}\in\mathcal{V}
and
{\bf x}^{\prime}\in\varphi^{-1}({\bf t})
, there exists
{\bf x}\in\varphi^{-1}({\bf c})
such that
\psi({\bf x})=\psi({\bf x}^{\prime})
. Therefore,
estimates (
2
) provide

\dfrac{1}{L}\left\lVert{\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert G({\bf x})-G({\bf x}^{\prime})\right\rVert=\|{\bf c}-{\bf t}\|+d_{\bf c}(\psi({\bf x}),\psi({\bf x}^{\prime}))=\|{\bf c}-{\bf t}\|.

Thus we obtain
\mathcal{U}\subset T_{\epsilon}(\varphi^{-1}({\bf c}))
.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

Point (i) of Proposition
2.4
implies that each first order
partial derivative of each component of the mapping
\varphi
is bounded over

\varphi^{-1}(\mathcal{V})
.

Property 2.6
.

Let
\tau:{\mathbb{K}^{m}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
be a mapping Lipschitz trivial at the value
{\bf c}
and
\pi:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{m}}
be a linear surjective projection. Then the mapping

\tau\circ\pi:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
is Lipschitz trivial at the value
{\bf c}
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi:=\tau\circ\pi
. Up to a
\mathbb{K}
-linear change of coordinates in
{\mathbb{K}^{n}}
,
we can assume that for any subset
V
of
{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
the following holds true

\varphi^{-1}(V)=\tau^{-1}(V)\times\mathbb{K}^{n-m}.

Denote
({\bf u},{\bf v})\in{\mathbb{K}^{m}}\times\mathbb{K}^{n-m}={\mathbb{K}^{n}}
.
If the bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism

G:\tau^{-1}(\mathcal{V})\mapsto\mathcal{V}\times\tau^{-1}({\bf c})

provides trivialisation of
\tau
over a neighbourhood
\mathcal{V}
of
{\bf c}
, then
the mapping

H:\varphi^{-1}(\mathcal{V})\to\mathcal{V}\times\varphi^{-1}({\bf c}),\quad({\bf u},{\bf v})\to(G({\bf u}),{\bf v})

is a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism trivialising
\varphi
over
\mathcal{V}
.
∎

Property 2.7
.

Let
\varphi:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
be a smooth mapping with a nowhere dense set of critical
values. Any regular value of
\varphi
that is also a value of properness
is a Lipschitz trivial value.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a non-empty open subset of
{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
such that
\varphi
is proper
over
\mathcal{V}
.
We can further assume that
{\rm clos}(\mathcal{V})
does not intersect with
K_{0}(\varphi)
.
Therefore, the mapping
\varphi
is
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
locally trivial over
\mathcal{V}
by
Ehresmann’s Theorem.
The restriction of any
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
trivialisation of
\varphi
over
\mathcal{V}

to any open subset
\mathcal{U}
relatively compact in
\mathcal{V}
is necessarily
bi-Lipschitz over
\mathcal{U}
.
∎

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{R}^{p}}
be a

\mathcal{C}^{k}
mapping with
k\geq\max(n-p+1,1)
.
Any Lipschitz trivial value of
\varphi
is a regular value.

The proof will follow from the next result.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
f:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
mapping, where
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
are open
subsets of
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
and
{\mathbb{R}^{p}}
respectively. If there exists a bi-Lipschitz
homeomorphism

(f,\psi):\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}\times F

where
F
is a
C^{1}
sub-manifold of some
\mathbb{R}^{q}
,
then
f
has no critical points in

\mathcal{U}
.

Proof.

Let
H:\mathcal{V}\times F\to\mathcal{U}
be the inverse of the bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism

(f,\psi)
. Since
H
is bi-Lipschitz, it is differentiable almost everywhere.
At a point
{\bf y}_{0}
where
H
is differentiable, its differential
D_{{\bf y}_{0}}H
has
rank
n
.
Moreover, having
H
bi-Lipschitz implies that any limit of the form
D=\lim_{{\bf y}_{n}\to{\bf y}}D_{{\bf y}_{n}}H
at the
given point
{\bf y}
of
\mathcal{V}\times F
has also rank
n
.
Therefore the differential of
f
must have rank
p
at each point of
\mathcal{U}
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.8
.

By hypothesis
\varphi
satisfies Sard’s Theorem.
Let
{\bf c}
be a Lipschitz trivial value of
\varphi
.

If
{\bf c}
does not lie in
Im(\varphi)
, the image of
\varphi
, then it belongs to

{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\setminus{\rm clos}(Im(\varphi))
, thus is a regular value.

Assume
{\bf c}
is a value taken by
\varphi
. Let
\mathcal{V}
be an open neighbourhood of
{\bf c}

over which
\varphi
is Lipschitz trivial.
Thus, by Lemma
2.9
,
the mapping
\varphi
has no critical point in
\varphi^{-1}(\mathcal{V})
,
therefore
{\bf c}
is a regular value.
∎

Property 2.10
.

Let
\varphi:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
be a mapping locally Lipschitz trivial over the
connected open subset
\mathcal{V}\subset{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
.
Then the family
(\varphi^{-1}({\bf t})^{\infty})_{{\bf t}\in\mathcal{V}}
of accumulation
sets at infinity of the levels of
\varphi
is constant, i.e.

\varphi^{-1}({\bf t})^{\infty}=\varphi^{-1}(\mathcal{V})^{\infty},\;\;\forall{\bf t}\in\mathcal{V}.

Proof.

Consider two sequences
({\bf x}_{k})_{k}
and
({\bf x}_{k}^{\prime})_{k}
of
{\mathbb{K}^{n}}

satisfying
the following property: there exists a positive constant
A
such that

|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{k}^{\prime}|\;\leq\;A\;\,{\rm for}\;\,k\gg 1.

If furthermore
|{\bf x}_{k}|
goes to
\infty
and does so such that
[{\bf x}_{k}:1]\to[{\lambda}:0]\in H_{\infty}
as
k\to\infty
, then we deduce that
|{\bf x}_{k}^{\prime}|
goes
to
\infty
and
[{\bf x}_{k}^{\prime}:1]\to[{\lambda}:0]
as
k
goes to
\infty
.

Let
{\bf c}\in\mathcal{V}
be a Lipschitz trivial value of
\varphi
.
Up to taking a smaller
\mathcal{V}
containing
{\bf c}
,
point (ii) of Proposition
2.4
states that

\varphi^{-1}(\mathcal{V})
is contained in the open tube
T_{\varepsilon}(\varphi^{-1}({\bf c}))
for some
positive radius
\varepsilon
. The first part of the proof then gives the result.
∎

3.
Polynomial mappings with Lipschitz trivial values depend on fewer variables

Let
f:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
be a polynomial mapping.
The level
f^{-1}({\bf t})
is denoted by
F_{\bf t}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
be a polynomial mapping with
\dim F_{\bf c}^{\infty}=n-1-m

for a value
{\bf c}\in{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
. If the mapping
f
attains
{\bf c}
as a Lipschitz
trivial value, then there exist a polynomial mapping
g:{\mathbb{K}^{m}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{p}}

which is proper at
{\bf c}
and a linear surjective projection
\pi:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{m}}

such that

f=g\circ\pi.

We start with the following key result.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
be a polynomial mapping and assume that the point

[1:0:\dots:0]
lies in
F_{\bf c}^{\infty}
. If there exists a neighbourhood
\mathcal{V}

of
{\bf c}
such that
f
satisfies points (i) and (ii) of
Proposition
2.4
, then the mapping
f
does not depend on
the coordinate
x_{1}
.

Proof.

Since
[1:0:\dots:0]\in F_{\bf c}^{\infty}
,
there exists an arc
{\gamma}:I\to F_{\bf c}
parametrized as

{\gamma}(t)=(t^{d},p(t)+A_{0}(1/t))\in\mathbb{K}\times\mathbb{K}^{n-1},

where
I
is a connected component of the complement of an Euclidean ball
of
\mathbb{K}
, the
mapping
p\penalty\ :\penalty\ \mathbb{K}\penalty\ \to\penalty\ \mathbb{K}^{n-1}
is polynomial of degree
\leq d-1
, and
A_{0}

is a
\mathbb{K}
-analytic map germ
(\mathbb{K},0)\to(\mathbb{K}^{n-1},{\bf 0})
.

Consider the following dominant polynomial mapping

{\Gamma}:\mathbb{K}\times\mathbb{K}^{n-1}\to{\mathbb{K}^{n}},\;\;(t,\varepsilon)\mapsto(t^{d},p(t)+\varepsilon).

Let
{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{K}\to{\mathbb{K}^{n}}
be the polynomial arc

{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}:t\mapsto{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(t):={\Gamma}(t,\varepsilon).

Since
A_{0}(1/t)\to{\bf 0}
as
|t|\to\infty
, we conclude that

\|{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(t)-{\gamma}(t)\|=\left\|\varepsilon-A_{0}(1/t)\right\|\to\|\varepsilon\|.

Take a neighbourhood
\mathcal{V}
of
{\bf c}
in
{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
such that
f
satisfies
points (i) and (ii) of Proposition
2.4
.
Point (ii) of Proposition
2.4
and the definition of
{\Gamma}

guarantee the existence of constants
\delta>0
and
R>0
such that

(3)

\displaystyle{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(t)\in f^{-1}(\mathcal{V})

for any
\|\varepsilon\|<{\delta}
and
t\in I
such that
|t|>R
.
Since
f
is Lipschitz on
f^{-1}(\mathcal{V})
, we get

\|f({\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(t))-{\bf c}\|\leq L\cdot\|\varepsilon-A_{0}(1/t)\|\to L\cdot||\varepsilon\|

as
|t|\to\infty
. Therefore for each
\|\varepsilon\|<{\delta}
, the polynomial
mapping
t\to f\circ{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(t)
is bounded, thus constant.
Writing
{\bf x}=(x_{1},{\bf y})
, we deduce

{\bf 0}\equiv\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}(f\circ{\gamma}_{\varepsilon})(t)=d\cdot t^{d-1}\cdot{\partial}_{x_{1}}f({\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(t))+{\partial}_{{\bf y}}f({\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(t))\cdot p^{\prime}(t).

Since
p
has degree
\leq d-1

and the first order partial derivatives of
f
are bounded along

{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
by Remark
2.5
,
for
\|\varepsilon\|<{\delta}
we conclude that

({\partial}_{x_{1}}f)\circ{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}\equiv 0.

Since the subset
\mathbb{K}\times\{\varepsilon:\|\varepsilon\|<{\delta}\}\subset{\mathbb{K}^{n}}
is open and non-empty and the mapping
{\Gamma}
is dominant, we conclude
that the mapping
{\partial}_{x_{1}}f
is identically null.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Note that
f
satisfies the claim of Proposition
2.4
. If

\dim F_{\bf c}^{\infty}=-1
, then the fibre
F_{\bf c}
is compact and from
point (ii) of Proposition
2.4
the subset
f^{-1}(\mathcal{V})
is
compact for a small compact neighbourhood
\mathcal{V}
of
{\bf c}
. Thus
f
is proper
at
{\bf c}
and taking
\pi
as the identity mapping of
{\mathbb{K}^{n}}
yields the claim.

Assume
F_{\bf c}^{\infty}
is of dimension
n-1-m\geq 0
. Thus there exist
n-m

points
[{\bf v}_{1}:0],\ldots,[{\bf v}_{n-m}:0]
of
F_{\bf c}^{\infty}
such that the
vectors
{\bf v}_{1},\dots,{\bf v}_{n-m}
are
\mathbb{K}
-linearly independent in
{\mathbb{K}^{n}}
.
Take a
\mathbb{K}
-linear change of coordinates
\ell:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{n}}
such that
\ell({\bf v}_{j})={\bf e}_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,n-m
, where
\{{\bf e}_{1},\dots,{\bf e}_{n}\}
is the
standard orthonormal basis of
{\mathbb{K}^{n}}
. Applying Lemma
3.2

we conclude that the polynomial mapping
f\circ\ell
depends
only on
{\bf u}:=(x_{n-m+1},\dots,x_{n})
.
Let
g
be the polynomial mapping restriction of
f\circ\ell
to
{\mathbb{K}^{m}}
,
the subspace of
{\mathbb{K}^{n}}
generated by
{\bf e}_{n-m+1},\ldots,{\bf e}_{n}
.
Let
\pi_{0}:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{m}}
be the orthogonal projection of
{\mathbb{K}^{n}}
onto the
subspace
{\mathbb{K}^{m}}
. Therefore, we find

f=g\circ\pi_{0}\circ\ell^{-1}.

Note that
(f\circ\ell)^{-1}({\bf t})=\mathbb{K}^{n-m}\times g^{-1}({\bf t})
.
Since

n-1-m=\dim F_{\bf c}^{\infty}=n-m+\dim g^{-1}({\bf c})^{\infty},

we deduce that
g^{-1}({\bf c})
is compact. From Proposition
2.4

applied to the levels of
f\circ\ell
over
\mathcal{V}
we get that

g^{-1}(\mathcal{V})
is bounded. Thus
{\bf c}
is a value of properness of
g
.
∎

4.
On the set of Lipschitz trivial values of real and complex mappings

This section presents some consequences of Theorem
3.1
. In particular,
complex polynomial mappings admitting Lipschitz trivial values have a very
rigid structure, while the real setting allows for more variety.

Corollary 4.1
.

A complex polynomial mapping
f:{\mathbb{C}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{C}^{p}}
attains a Lipschitz trivial value
if and only if there exist a dominant polynomial mapping
g:{\mathbb{C}^{p}}\to{\mathbb{C}^{p}}

and a linear surjective projection
\pi:{\mathbb{C}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{C}^{p}}
such that

f=g\circ\pi.

In such a case we get

{\rm L}(f)=\mathbb{C}^{p}\setminus{\rm Bif}(g).

In particular, the set of regular Lipschitz trivial values is either empty or
the complement of an
algebraic hypersurface.

Proof.

Assume there exists a dominant polynomial mapping
g:{\mathbb{C}^{p}}\to{\mathbb{C}^{p}}
such that

f=g\circ\pi

for some linear surjective projection
\pi:{\mathbb{C}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{C}^{p}}
. Therefore
g
is
generically finite and by Properties
2.6

and
2.7
, the set
{\rm L}(f)
of Lipschitz trivial values
is not empty.
For the converse statement, note that
f
is dominant and
n-p=\dim F_{\bf c}=1+\dim F_{\bf c}^{\infty}
for a generic level
{\bf c}
of
f
, so Theorem
3.1
gives the claim.

For the second part of the assertion, observe that the subset

{\rm L}(g)\cap(J(g)\cap K_{0}(g))
is empty by Property
2.6
and
Proposition
2.8
,
since
g
is generically finite.
We recall that
J(g)\cup K_{0}(g)={\rm Bif}(g)
and if non-empty, it is an
algebraic hypersurface by
[
5
]
.
∎

As a consequence, and with a different proof, we recover the main result
of
[
1
]
.

Corollary 4.2

(
[
1
]
Theorem 10)
.

A complex polynomial function
f:{\mathbb{C}^{n}}\to\mathbb{C}
admits a Lipschitz trivial value if and
only if it depends on a single variable, i.e., there exist
(n-1)
linearly
independent vectors
{\bf v}_{2},\ldots,{\bf v}_{n},
of
{\mathbb{C}^{n}}
such that

{\partial}_{{\bf v}_{i}}f\equiv 0
for all
i\geq 2
.

The real case is more nuanced than the complex one and Lipschitz trivial values
admit a richer structure as we can see below.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{R}^{p}}
be a polynomial mapping admitting a Lipschitz trivial value.
There exists a polynomial mapping
g:{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
and linear surjective
projection
\pi:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{R}^{m}}
such that
f=g\circ\pi
and

{\rm L}(f)={\mathbb{R}^{p}}\setminus(J(g)\cup K_{0}(g)).

Moreover, the mapping
g
is unique up to linear changes of coordinates.

Proof.

The demonstration is similar to proof of Corollary
4.1
.
Moreover, up to a linear change of coordinates in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
,
we have
Df=Dg\oplus{\bf 0}:{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\to{\mathbb{R}^{p}}
.
Thus using Property
2.6
, Proposition
2.8

and Theorem
3.1
we get

{\rm L}(f)={\rm L}(g).

To show uniqueness take
g:{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\to{\mathbb{R}^{p}}
proper at
{\bf c}
such that
f=g\circ\pi
for a linear surjective mapping
\pi:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{R}^{m}}
. For any polynomial
mapping
h:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to{\mathbb{R}^{p}}
and a linear surjective projection
\sigma:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}

such that
f=h\circ\sigma
, we get, up to a linear change of coordinates

\ell:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
, that

(h\circ\ell)^{-1}({\bf t})=g^{-1}({\bf t})\times\mathbb{R}^{k-m}

for
{\bf t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, since at least the level
{\bf c}
of
g
is compact. Thus
either
m<k
and
h
does not attain a proper value, or
m=k
and

h\circ\ell=g
.
∎

Example 4.4
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{3}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be the suspension at infinity of the Motzkin polynomial
given by

f(x,y,z)=x^{4}y^{2}+x^{2}y^{4}-3x^{2}y^{2}+1.

We have
J(f)=[1,\infty)
and
K_{0}(f)=\{0,1\}
. Moreover,

{\rm L}(f)=(-\infty,1)\setminus\{0\}.

Indeed, the values of
[1,+\infty)
are not Lipschitz trivial values of
f
, since
f

does not satisfy the necessary condition (ii) of Proposition
2.4

(the distance between any two levels in
(1,+\infty)
is zero).

Example
4.4
illustrates that the set of Lipschitz trivial values of
a real polynomial mapping can be open and not dense in the image, whereas for
complex mappings Lipschitz trivial values
follow a local-global principle as stated in Corollary
4.1
.

For polynomial mappings Property
2.10
is
refined as the following necessary condition on the fibres.

Property 4.5
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{p}}
be a polynomial mapping. There exists a
\mathbb{K}
-linear subspace

A
of
{\mathbb{K}^{n}}
of positive codimension such that

\widehat{F_{\bf t}^{\infty}}=A,\quad\text{for all }{\bf t}\in{\rm L}(f)\cap Im(f),

where
\widehat{F_{\bf t}^{\infty}}
is the
\mathbb{K}
-cone of
{\mathbb{K}^{n}}
over
F_{\bf t}^{\infty}

with vertex at the origin, and where the cone over the empty set is defined as
the null subspace.

Example 4.6
.

The polynomial mapping
f:\mathbb{K}^{3}\to\mathbb{K}^{2}
, defined as
(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,xy+z)
,
is surjective and
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
trivial at each
{\bf c}\in\mathbb{K}^{2}
.
Each level
F_{\bf t}
is an affine line, like the mapping in Example

2.3
. Yet, the family of accumulation sets at infinity

(\widehat{F_{\bf t}^{\infty}})_{{\bf t}\in\mathbb{K}^{2}}
is nowhere locally constant. Therefore
Property
4.5
implies that this mapping cannot admit
any Lipschitz trivial value.

5.
Lipschitz trivial values of real mappings and their complexifications

Throughout this section let
f:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{R}^{p}}
be a real polynomial mapping and let

f_{\mathbb{C}}
be its complexification. We will show that the set of real Lipschitz trivial values of

f_{\mathbb{C}}
is either empty or equal to the set of Lipschitz trivial values of
f
, up
to a set of positive codimension.

Proposition 5.1
.

If
f_{\mathbb{C}}
admits a Lipschitz trivial value, then
{\rm L}(f)
is a semi-algebraic
dense open subset of
{\mathbb{R}^{p}}
.
More precisely,

{\rm L}(f_{\mathbb{C}})\cap{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\ \subset\ {\rm L}(f).

Proof.

Denote
F_{\mathbb{C},{\bf t}}:=f_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}({\bf t})
.
If
{\rm L}(f_{\mathbb{C}})\cap Im(f_{\mathbb{C}})
is not empty, then
f_{\mathbb{C}}
is dominant, and thus
the image of
f
is Zariski dense.
Corollary
4.1
and Property
2.6
imply
the existence of a
\mathbb{C}
-linear
subspace
A_{\mathbb{C}}
of
{\mathbb{C}^{n}}
of dimension
n-p
such that for each
{\bf t}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}\setminus{\rm Bif}(f_{\mathbb{C}})
,
the level
F_{\mathbb{C},{\bf t}}
is a disjoint union of finitely many affine subspaces
of
\mathbb{C}
-dimension
n-p
, parallel to
A_{\mathbb{C}}
. Since
f({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
is Zariski dense,
there exists an open set
\mathcal{V}
in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that the level
F_{{\bf t}}
is of
dimension
n-p
for
{\bf t}\in\mathcal{V}
. As the intersection of the complex fibre

F_{\mathbb{C},{\bf t}}
with
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the fibre
F_{{\bf t}}
is necessarily a disjoint union of
parallel real affine subspaces.
Therefore,
\dim f^{-1}(\mathcal{V})^{\infty}=n-1-p
. Note that
f
, as the
restriction of
f_{\mathbb{C}}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, satisfies assumptions (i) and (ii) of
Lemma
3.2
.
Using Theorem
3.1
we get that
f=g\circ\pi
for some real linear
surjective projection
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
and real polynomial mapping

g:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Thus
f_{\mathbb{C}}=g_{\mathbb{C}}\circ\pi_{\mathbb{C}}
and necessarily

g_{\mathbb{C}}
is generically finite.
Since Corollaries
4.1
and
4.3
yield

{\rm L}(f_{\mathbb{C}})=\mathbb{C}^{p}\setminus{\rm Bif}(g_{\mathbb{C}}),\quad\text{and}\quad{\rm L}(f)=\mathbb{R}^{p}\setminus{\rm Bif}(g).

We get the claim since
{\rm Bif}(g)=J(g)\cup K_{0}(g)
is semi-algebraic of
positive codimension by
[
5
]
.
∎

Proposition 5.2
.

The subset
B:={\rm Bif}(g_{\mathbb{C}})\cap{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\setminus{\rm Bif}(g)
is semi-algebraic
of dimension
\leq p-1
. Either

{\rm L}(f)\setminus B={\rm L}(f_{\mathbb{C}})\cap\mathbb{R}^{p}\;\;{\rm when}\;\dim F_{\bf c}^{\infty}=n-1-p\;\;\text{\rm for some }{\bf c}\in{\rm L}(f),

or
{\rm L}(f_{\mathbb{C}})=\emptyset
otherwise.

Proof.

In view of Proposition
5.1

we only need to show the inclusion

{\rm L}(f)\setminus B\subset{\rm L}(f_{\mathbb{C}}).

If
{\rm L}(f)\neq\emptyset
, then Property
4.5

holds true and let
m:=\dim A
. From Corollary
4.1
the
condition
m=n-p
is necessary to have
{\rm L}(f_{\mathbb{C}})\neq\emptyset
. When

m=n-p
, there exist a polynomial mapping
g:{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\to{\mathbb{R}^{p}}
with Zariski
dense image and a linear surjective projection
\pi:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{R}^{p}}

such that
f=g\circ\pi
. Thus
f_{\mathbb{C}}=g_{\mathbb{C}}\circ\pi_{\mathbb{C}}
, and
g_{\mathbb{C}}
is
dominant.
We have
{\rm Bif}(g)\cup B={\rm Bif}(g_{\mathbb{C}})\cap{\mathbb{R}^{p}}
and the claim follows from
Corollaries
4.1
and
4.3
.
∎

6.
Rational functions and Lipschitz trivial values

It is natural to ask whether we can extend the category of mappings that satisfy
the claim of our main result. We answer in the negative, as
Property
6.3
demonstrates that
Theorem
3.1
is sharp in the sense that it does not
hold for rational but non-polynomial mappings.

Let
f:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{K}
be a rational function,
n\geq 2
.
Its indeterminacy locus
I(f)
is the subset of
{\mathbb{K}^{n}}
where denominator and
numerator vanish simultaneously (for all representations of
f
as a fraction).
Let
\overline{\mathbb{K}}
be the compactification of
\mathbb{K}
defined as follows

\overline{\mathbb{C}}:=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}\;\;{\rm and}\;\;\overline{\mathbb{R}}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}.

Property 6.1
.

Assume that the rational function
f:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{K}
does not extend
continuously through the point
{\bf x}_{0}\in{\mathbb{K}^{n}}
, i.e., the subset
J:=\{\lim_{{\bf x}\to{\bf x}_{0}}f({\bf x})\}\subset\overline{\mathbb{K}}
of accumulation values
of
f
at
{\bf x}_{0}
does not reduce to a single value in
\mathbb{K}
. Then

{\rm L}(f)\cap J=\emptyset
.

Proof.

If
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
then
J=\overline{\mathbb{C}}
. When
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
, the set
J
is closed,
semi-algebraic and has non-empty interior.
Suppose
J\cap{\rm L}(f)
is non-empty, thus it is open. In such a case,
there exists an open subset
\mathcal{V}
of
J\cap{\rm L}(f)
such that
f
is Lipschitz
trivial over
\mathcal{V}
. Then any trivializing bi-Lipschitz
homeomorphism satisfies Estimates (
2
), contradicting
the fact that
{\bf x}_{0}
lies in the closure of any level
f^{-1}(t)
when
t\in\mathcal{V}
.
∎

Corollary 6.2
.

A complex rational function with Lipschitz trivial values has empty
indeterminacy locus.

On the other hand, the real setting is more flexible. Real rational
functions may extend continuously (or even smoothly) through their
indeterminacy locus onto
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
, in such a case they are called regulous.

Proposition 6.3
.

There exist rational functions
f:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{K}
with empty
indeterminacy locus that admit Lipschitz trivial values which are not
values of properness, and are never of the form
g\circ\pi
with

g:{\mathbb{K}^{m}}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{K}
a rational function and
\pi:{\mathbb{K}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{K}^{m}}
a
linear surjective projection with
n>m
.

Proof.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
be the non-constant
function
{\bf x}\mapsto h({\bf x}):=1+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}^{2}

and consider the rational
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
function
f:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\to\mathbb{R}
defined
as

f({\bf x},y)=y-\frac{1}{h({\bf x})}.

We have
I(f)=\emptyset
and
f
has no critical point.
Observe that the partial derivatives of
f

are uniformly bounded over
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
, thus
f
is a Lipschitz function over
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
.

For
c\in\mathbb{R}
define the following mapping

G:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\to\mathbb{R}\times f^{-1}(c),\;\;({\bf x},y)\mapsto\left(f({\bf x},y),\left({\bf x},c+\frac{1}{h({\bf x})}\right)\right).

It is a Lipschitz homeomorphism with inverse

G^{-1}\left(t,\left({\bf x},c+\frac{1}{h({\bf x})}\right)\right)=\left({\bf x},t+\frac{1}{h({\bf x})}\right).

The inverse
G^{-1}
is also Lipschitz, thus each value
c
of
\mathbb{R}
is
Lipschitz trivial for
f
. Since any level of
f
is a graph over

{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
, the function
f
cannot be proper at
c
. Last, there exists no vector

{\bf v}
of
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\setminus{\bf 0}
such that
{\partial}_{\bf v}f\equiv 0
.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

The function
f
defined in the proof of
Proposition
6.3

is regulous
[
8
,
7
,
2
]
.
