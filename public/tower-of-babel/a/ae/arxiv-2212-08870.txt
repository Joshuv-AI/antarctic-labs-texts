Cutoff for the Averaging process on the hypercube and complete bipartite graphs
Pietro Caputo, Matteo Quattropani, and Federico Sau

Abstract
. We consider the averaging process on a graph, that is the evolution of a mass distribution undergoing repeated averages along the edges of the graph at the arrival times of independent Poisson processes. We establish cutoff phenomena for both the L 1 L^{1} and L 2 L^{2} distance from stationarity when the graph is a discrete hypercube and when the graph is complete bipartite. Some general facts about the averaging process on arbitrary graphs are also discussed.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 60K35; secondary 82B20; 82C26.

1.
Introduction

The averaging process on a finite graph
G=(V,E)
, referred to as
{\rm Avg}(G)
below, is the stochastic process defined as follows. Start with some probability mass function
\eta
on
V
and consider independent Poisson clocks with rate
1
on each edge
xy\in E
; when
xy
rings, update both masses
\eta(x)
and
\eta(y)
to their average value
\frac{1}{2}(\eta(x)+\eta(y))
. As soon as the underlying graph is connected, whatever the initial distribution
\eta
, the dynamics converges to the uniform distribution
\pi\equiv 1/|V|
, and just as for the simple random walk on
G
, the convergence rate associated to this process is an important feature of the underlying graph.
Unlike the case of simple random walks however, the information available in the literature on convergence rates for
{\rm Avg}(G)
is rather limited. The mathematical analysis of the averaging process started about a decade ago with the work of Aldous and Lanoue
[
AL12
]
. More recently, for the special case of the complete graph a full account was given in
[
CDSZ22
]
, where the authors proved the existence of a cutoff phenomenon. On the contrary, in
[
QS21
]
the process was shown to have no cutoff on “finite-dimensional” graphs such as the
d
-dimensional grid, that is graphs satisfying a suitable Nash inequality which guarantees the existence of a well-defined limiting heat flow.
Very little is known for other families of graphs. We refer to the recent work
[
MSW22
]
for some partial results, and for some motivating connections to
opinion dynamics, random distributed algorithms, and quantum computing.

In this paper we study the convergence to stationarity of
{\rm Avg}(G)
when
G=\{0,1\}^{d}
is the hypercube graph with
n=2^{d}
vertices, and when
G=K_{m,n-m}
is the complete bipartite graph with the two parts of size
m
and
n-m
, respectively.
In both cases, we are ultimately interested in quantifying, as
n\to\infty
, the (mean)
L^{p}
-distance to equilibrium for
p=1,2
, that is

(1.1)

\displaystyle(t,\xi)\longmapsto\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{p}^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\ ,

where
\eta_{t}
denotes the
mass distribution of
{\rm Avg}(G)
at time
t\geq 0
, while
\mathds{E}_{\xi}
stands for the expectation over
the random updates when starting from the configuration
\eta_{0}=\xi
,
and we write
\|\cdot\|_{p}
for the
L^{p}(\pi)
-norm with respect to
\pi
, the uniform distribution on the vertex set
V
. For
p\geq 1
, convexity shows that for any fixed
\xi
the function above is monotone nonincreasing in
t\geq 0
. When
p=1
, (
1.1
) is twice the expected value of the usual total variation distance between
\eta_{t}
and
\pi
.
For worst-case initial conditions
\xi
, we analyze the distance to equilibrium at times of the form

(1.2)

\displaystyle T(a)=t_{\rm mix}+aw\,,\qquad a\in\mathbb{R}\,,

for some appropriate choices of
t_{\rm mix}=t_{\rm mix}(n)
and
w=w(n)
such that
t_{\rm mix}\gg w
, and prove
cutoff
results around the mixing time
t_{\rm mix}
, with
w
controling the size of the so-called
cutoff window
. We refer e.g. to
[
LP17
]
for background on the cutoff phenomenon for Markov chains.

For the hypercube we establish that both the
L^{1}
and
L^{2}
distances have a cutoff phenomenon with window
w=O(1)
, and mixing time
t_{\rm mix}=\frac{1}{2}\log d
, which coincides with the
L^{p}
-mixing time of the lazy random walk on
\{0,1\}^{d}
where the
d
coordinates flip independently at rate
\frac{1}{2}
; see, e.g.,
[
LP17
]
.
This settles a question that was mentioned as an open problem in
[
AL12
]
.

Theorem 1.1

(
G=\{0,1\}^{d}
: Cutoff in
L^{p}
,
p=1,2
)
.

Let
n=2^{d}
,
and

(1.3)

\displaystyle T(a):=\frac{1}{2}\log d+a\ ,\qquad a\in\mathbb{R}\ .

Then,

(1.4)

\lim_{a\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\xi}\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{T(a)}}{\pi}-1\right\|^{p}_{p}\right]=0\ ,\qquad p=1,2\ ,

and

(1.5)

\lim_{a\to-\infty}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\xi}\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{T(a)}}{\pi}-1\right\|^{p}_{p}\right]=\begin{cases}2&\text{if }p=1\\
+\infty&\text{if }p=2\ .\end{cases}

In the case of complete bipartite graphs
G=K_{m,n-m}
, we establish two cutoff results in
L^{1}
and
L^{2}
, with distinct locations and different sizes for the cutoff windows. Our results apply to all sizes
1\leq m\leq n/2
. The two special cases
m=1
(star graph) and
m=n/2
(regular case) were discussed as open problems in
[
MSW22
]
.

Theorem 1.2

(
G=K_{m,n-m}
: Cutoff in
L^{1}
)
.

Let, for all
1\leq m\leq n/2
,

(1.6)

\displaystyle T(a):=\frac{n}{2(n-m)}\frac{\log_{2}n}{m}+a\frac{\sqrt{\log n}}{m}\ ,\qquad a\in\mathbb{R}\ .

Then,

(1.7)

\lim_{a\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\xi}\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{T(a)}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{1}\right]=0\ ,

and

(1.8)

\lim_{a\to-\infty}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\xi}\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{T(a)}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{1}\right]=2\ .

Concerning the
L^{2}
-norm, besides determining the size of the cutoff window, we also identify the so-called
cutoff profile
.

Theorem 1.3

(
G=K_{m,n-m}
: Cutoff in
L^{2}
)
.

Let, for all
1\leq m\leq n/2
,

(1.9)

\displaystyle T(a):=\frac{\log n}{\theta m}+\frac{a}{\theta m}\ ,\qquad a\in\mathbb{R}\ ,

where

(1.10)

\displaystyle\theta=\theta_{m,n-m}:=\frac{3n}{8m}\left(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{32}{9}\frac{m}{n}\left(\frac{n-m}{n}\right)}\right)\in\left[\frac{1}{2},\frac{2}{3}\right]\,.

Then, assuming that
m/n\to b\in[0,1/2]
,

(1.11)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\xi}\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{T(a)}}{\pi}-1\right\|^{2}_{2}\right]=\left(1+D(b)\right)e^{-a}\ ,\qquad a\in\mathbb{R}\ ,

where

(1.12)

D(b):=\frac{3-4b-\sqrt{9-32b+32b^{2}}}{2\sqrt{9-32b+32b^{2}}}\in[0,\tfrac{\sqrt{6}-2}{4}]\ ,\qquad b\in[0,1/2]\ .

We refer the reader to Section
2.6
below for further discussions around these results and for a comparison with previously known facts. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we give formal definitions, recall and establish a few general facts that hold for
{\rm Avg}(G)
on any graph
G
. In Section
3
we prove Theorem
1.1
, in Section
4
we prove Theorem
1.2
, and finally in Section
5
we prove Theorem
1.3
.

2.
The Averaging process on arbitrary graphs: general facts

The goal of this section is to collect a number of preliminary facts about the Averaging process on an arbitrary (unweighted) graph. These general observations allow a first, not necessarily optimal, control on the mixing times. Some of them can be found in the literature only in a discrete-time setting (e.g.,
[
MSW22
]
), others were formulated for more general reference measures
\pi
(e.g.,
[
QS21
]
), or for weighted graphs (e.g.,
[
AL12
,
QS21
]
); the entropy inequalities from
[
BC21
]
were not originally related to the Averaging process. While not everything discussed in this section will be needed in the proof of our main results, we believe that a unified presentation is beneficial and could be of use as a reference for future research.

2.1.
Averaging process vs. (lazy) random walk

We start with a simple observation relating the expectation of
{\rm Avg}(G)
with the transition probability of a random walk on the same graph.

Given a finite connected graph
G=(V,E)
, with
|V|=n
, the Averaging process
{\rm Avg(G)}
is the continuous-time Markov process
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on the set
\Delta(V)
of probability measures on
V
with infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm Avg}
given by

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}f(\eta)=\sum_{xy\in E}\big(f(\eta^{xy})-f(\eta)\big)\ ,\qquad\eta\in\Delta(V)\ ,\ f:\Delta(V)\to\mathbb{R}\ ,

where
\eta^{xy}
is defined as

(2.2)

\displaystyle\eta^{xy}(z):=\begin{cases}\eta(z)&\text{if}\ z\neq x,y\\
\frac{\eta(x)+\eta(y)}{2}&\text{otherwise}\ .\end{cases}

When starting from a configuration
\xi\in\Delta(V)
,
\mathds{P}_{\xi}
and
\mathds{E}_{\xi}
denote the corresponding law and expectation, respectively.
The uniform measure
\pi\equiv 1/n
is a fixed point of the dynamics, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}f(\pi)=0
for all
f:\Delta(V)\to\mathbb{R}
.

The process
{\rm RW}(G)
is the continuous-time Markov chain
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, with state space
V
, with generator

(2.3)

L^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm RW}}\psi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y:\,xy\in E}\big(\psi(y)-\psi(x)\big)\ ,\qquad x\in V\ ,\ \psi:V\to\mathbb{R}\ .

That is,
X_{t}
is the simple random walk on
G
with jump rate
1/2
. We will refer to it as
lazy random walk
(or, simply,
random walk
) on
G
, and call
{\mathbf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm RW}}_{\xi}
the corresponding law when starting from a distribution
\xi\in\Delta(V)
; when
\xi={\bf 1}_{x}
, we simply write
{\mathbf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm RW}}_{x}
. Clearly,
\pi\equiv 1/n
is the unique equilibrium distribution for
{\rm RW}(G)
. Further, we let
\lambda_{\rm gap}
denote its
spectral gap
, and
t_{\rm rel}
its
relaxation time
:
\lambda_{\rm gap}>0
is the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of
-L^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm RW}}
, and

(2.4)

t_{\rm rel}=(\lambda_{\rm gap})^{-1}\ .

The first observation is that, for all
x\in V
,
t\geq 0
and
\xi\in\Delta(V)
,

(2.5)

\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\eta_{t}(x)\right]={\mathbf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm RW}}_{\xi}(X_{t}=x)=:\pi_{t}^{\xi}(x)\ ,

which equivalently rewrites, for some collection of random variables
Z_{t}^{\xi}=(Z_{t}^{\xi}(x))_{x\in V}
, as

(2.6)

\eta_{t}=\pi^{\xi}_{t}+Z_{t}^{\xi}\ ,\qquad\text{with}\ \eta_{0}=\xi\ ,\ \sum_{x\in V}Z_{t}^{\xi}(x)=0\ ,\ \mathds{E}_{\xi}[Z_{t}^{\xi}(x)]=0\ .

In other words, the probability mass function at time
t
is given by the deterministic random walk kernel
\pi^{\xi}_{t}
plus a mean-zero noise term
Z_{t}^{\xi}
. The identity in (
2.5
), as emphasized in
[
AL12
]
, is an instance of
duality
; see also
[
Lig05
]
. An intuitive explanation of (
2.5
) is that, under repeated averages on
G
, any given “infinitesimal chunk” of the initial mass performs a (lazy) random walk on
G
.
As a first immediate consequence of (
2.5
)–(
2.6
), one has the following general lower bound, stating that the presence of the noise term
Z_{t}^{\xi}
cannot speed up convergence to equilibrium.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
QS21
, Prop. 5.1]
)
.

For all
p\in[1,\infty)
,
t\geq 0
, and
\xi\in\Delta(V)
,

(2.7)

\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|^{p}_{p}\right]\geq\bigg\|\frac{\pi_{t}^{\xi}}{\pi}-1\bigg\|^{p}_{p}\ .

Proof.

By using the dual formulation of the
L^{p}
-distance, and calling
q
the conjugate exponent of
p
, we get

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\sup_{\|\psi\|_{q}=1}\sum_{x\in V}\left(\eta_{t}(x)-\pi(x)\right)\psi(x)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\|\psi\|_{q}=1}\sum_{x\in V}\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left(\eta_{t}(x)-\pi(x)\right)\psi(x)\right]

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|\psi\|_{q}=1}\sum_{x\in V}\left(\pi^{\xi}_{t}(x)-\pi(x)\right)\psi(x)=\bigg\|\frac{\pi_{t}^{\xi}}{\pi}-1\bigg\|_{p}\ ,

where for the second identity we used (
2.5
).
The result follows by Jensen inequality.
∎

Remark 2.2

(Sharpness of (
2.7
))
.

As we will comment more in detail in Section
2.6
below, for certain families of graphs the lower bound in (
2.7
) is asymptotically optimal (e.g., for the hypercube), for others it is sub-optimal (e.g., for the
L^{1}
-distance on the complete graph, see
[
CDSZ22
]
).

The lower bound in Proposition
2.1
is not the only relation that one can derive between the
L^{p}
-distance of
{\rm Avg}(G)
and that of
{\rm RW}(G)
. As first noticed in
[
AL12
]
, the infinitesimal mean contraction of the
L^{2}
-distance for
{\rm Avg}(G)
can be expressed in terms of the Dirichlet form of
{\rm RW}(G)
, giving rise to a version of Poincaré inequality for
{\rm Avg}(G)
. More precisely, recalling the definitions of infinitesimal generators
\mathcal{L}
and
L^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm RW}}
in (
2.1
) and (
2.3
), respectively,
and letting
\mathcal{E}^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm RW}
denote the Dirichlet form for
{\rm RW}(G)

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm RW}(\psi)=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{xy\in E}\big(\psi(x)-\psi(y)\big)^{2}\ ,\qquad\psi:V\to\mathbb{R}\ ,

a simple computation yields

(2.9)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\,\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\mathcal{L}\,\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=-\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\mathcal{E}^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm RW}\left(\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}\right)\right]\ .

As a consequence of this, the variational formulation of the spectral gap, Grönwall inequality, and the Markov property of
{\rm Avg}(G)
yield the following result.

Proposition 2.3

(
[
AL12
, Prop. 2]
)
.

Letting
t_{\rm rel}
be defined as in (
2.4
),

(2.10)

\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t+s}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq e^{-\frac{s}{t_{\rm rel}}}\,\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\ ,\qquad\xi\in\Delta(V)\ ,\ s,t\geq 0\ .

Remark 2.4

(Sharpness of (
2.10
))
.

Compared to the more common Poincaré inequality for
{\rm RW}(G)
, the inequality in Proposition
2.3
comes with a missing factor
2
in the exponential contraction rate. In general, such an inequality cannot be improved, with the equality being attained when, for instance,
G
is the complete graph (see
[
CDSZ22
]
).

2.2.
Second moments and coupled random walks

As already pointed out in
[
AL12
]
, also the second moments of
\eta_{t}
admit a formulation in the spirit of (
2.5
), at the cost of replacing
{\rm RW}(G)
by a
suitable coupling of two random walks.
For a given graph
G=(V,E)
, we call

{\rm CRW}(G)
the Markov process
(X_{t},Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined as follows. The state space is
V\times V
, interpreted as the set of positions of two labeled particles; as in
{\rm Avg}(G)
we have independent rate 1 Poisson clocks at the edges of
G
; when an edge
xy
rings, each particle on the endpoints of that edge is placed independently at
x
or at
y
with probability
1/2
.
We note that as long as
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
are at (graph) distance strictly larger than
1
, they evolve as two independent
{\rm RW}(G)
–processes, whereas while
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
sit on the endpoints of the same edge (on the same vertex or on opposite vertices), they can experience synchronous jumps.

An argument analogous to that used for (
2.5
), involving this time the evolution of two infinitesimal portions of masses of
{\rm Avg}(G)
, should convince the reader that the following identity holds: for all
x,y\in V
,
t\geq 0
, and
\xi\in\Delta(V)
,

(2.11)

\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\eta_{t}(x)\eta_{t}(y)\right]={\mathbf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm CRW}}_{\xi\otimes\xi}(X_{t}=x,Y_{t}=y)\ ,

with
{\mathbf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm CRW}}_{\xi\otimes\xi}
denoting the law of
{\rm CRW}(G)
when starting from
\xi\otimes\xi
(two independent samples from
\xi
). By a generalization of the above two-particle process that goes under the name of
Binomial splitting process
,
relations such as (
2.11
) extend to higher–order moments, see
[
QS21
]
.
As the next proposition shows, (
2.11
) yields two convenient alternative formulations of the
L^{2}
-distance to equilibrium of
{\rm Avg}(G)
in terms of the transition probabilities of
{\rm CRW}(G)
.

Proposition 2.5

(
[
AL12
,
QS21
]
)
.

For all
t\geq 0
and
\xi\in\Delta(V)
,

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=n\,{\mathbf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm CRW}}_{\xi\otimes\xi}(X_{t}=Y_{t})-1

and

(2.13)

\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\bigg\|\frac{\pi_{t}^{\xi}}{\pi}-1\bigg\|_{2}^{2}+\mathcal{N}^{\xi}_{t}\ ,

where

(2.14)

\mathcal{N}^{\xi}_{t}:=\frac{n}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{xy\in E}\left(\pi^{\xi}_{s}(x)-\pi^{\xi}_{s}(y)\right)^{2}\Phi_{t-s}(x,y)\,{\rm d}s\ ,

and

(2.15)

\Phi_{t}(x,y):=\frac{1}{2}\left({\mathbf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm CRW}}_{x,x}\left(X_{t}=Y_{t}\right)+{\mathbf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm CRW}}_{y,y}\left(X_{t}=Y_{t}\right)-2\,{\mathbf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm CRW}}_{x,y}\left(X_{t}=Y_{t}\right)\right)\in[0,1]\ .

The identity in (
2.12
) follows at once by expanding the
L^{2}
-norm and using (
2.11
). The identity in (
2.13
) is slightly more involved, and can be obtained by an explicit comparison of the generator of the
{\rm CRW}(G)
–process and that of two independent
{\rm RW}(G)
–processes; we refer to
[
QS21
, Prop. 5.5]
for the details.

2.3.
Worst–case initial condition

In the literature on mixing times of Markov chains, the distance to equilibrium at some time
t
is most often analyzed by starting the chain from a
worst–case initial condition
, namely one which maximizes the distance at time
t
. By a straightforward convexity argument, when the state space is finite, worst–case initial conditions
\xi\in\Delta(V)
are always Dirac distributions on some element of the chain’s state space. An analogous argument, based on the
linearity
of
{\rm Avg}(G)
, shows that the worst–case
L^{p}
distance for
{\rm Avg}(G)
is achieved at Dirac masses
{\bf 1}_{x}
,
x\in V
, for all
p\geq 1
.
A version of this fact already appeared in
[
MSW22
]
for the case of the
L^{1}
-distance in the discrete-time model. The same idea was also used in
[
CDSZ22
]
in the context of the complete graph, and in
[
QS21
]
to estimate the
L^{1}
-distance of the Binomial splitting process
on finite-dimensional graphs. The simple proof given here covers every graph and all
L^{p}
-distances with
p\geq 1
.

Proposition 2.6
.

For any graph
G
, for all
t\geq 0
and
p\in[1,\infty)
,

(2.16)

\sup_{\xi\in\Delta(V)}\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{p}^{p}\right]=\sup_{x\in V}\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{x}}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{p}^{p}\right]\ .

Proof.

The inequality ‘
\geq
’ is obvious. The proof of the inverse inequality follows by observing that, when the initial condition is
\xi\in\Delta(V)
, the probability mass function at time
t
can be represented as

(2.17)

\eta_{t}=\sum_{y\in V}\xi(y)\,\eta^{(y)}_{t}\ ,\qquad t\geq 0\ ,

where, for each
y\in V
,
\eta^{(y)}_{t}
is the averaging process on
G
with initial condition
{\bf 1}_{y}
, and the evolutions
(\eta^{(y)}_{t},\,t\geq 0)_{y\in V}

are coupled by the use of the same edge update sequence.
Therefore, letting
\mathds{E}
denote the expectation with respect to this coupling,
by Jensen inequality,

(2.18)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{p}^{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in V}\pi(x)\,\mathds{E}\left[\bigg|\sum_{y\in V}\xi(y)\bigg(\frac{\eta^{(y)}_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)}-1\bigg)\bigg|^{p}\right]

(2.19)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x\in V}\pi(x)\sum_{y\in V}\xi(y)\,\mathds{E}\left[\bigg|\frac{\eta^{(y)}_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)}-1\bigg|^{p}\right]=\sum_{y\in V}\xi(y)\,\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{y}}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{p}^{p}\right]\ .

This concludes the proof.
∎

2.4.
Relative entropy decay

Relative entropy is commonly used
to measure the distance from stationarity, and it is natural to relate the mixing properties of a system to its exponential decay rate.
In the usual Markov chain setup, this leads to the so-called Log-Sobolev and modified Log-Sobolev inequalities
[
DSC96
,
BT06
]
. These inequalities control the global entropy functional in terms of a local Dirichlet form. In the setting of the averaging process, we are led to a new set of inequalities where the global entropy functional is controlled by local versions of the entropy itself rather than by a Dirichlet form. Interestingly, these new inequalities were recently introduced and studied by
[
BC21
]
as a natural factorization statement for various random walk models, see also
[
CP21
]
for a spin system setting. However, the striking connection to the Averaging process that we point out here appears to be new. The latter can be formalized as follows.

Let

(2.20)

\displaystyle D(\eta||\pi):=\sum_{x\in V}\eta(x)\log\frac{\eta(x)}{\pi(x)}\ ,\qquad\eta\in\Delta(V)\ ,

denote the
relative entropy
(or
Kullback–Leibler divergence
) of
\eta\in\Delta(V)
with respect to
\pi\equiv 1/|V|
, and consider its expected value
\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[D(\eta_{t}||\pi)\right]
under the Averaging process
with initial condition
\xi\in\Delta(V)
.
Recalling that
n=|V|
, the
local entropy
of
\eta\in\Delta(V)
at the edge
xy\in E
is defined as

(2.21)

\displaystyle{\rm ent}_{xy}(\eta):=\frac{n}{2}\,\eta(x)\log\frac{\eta(x)}{\frac{1}{2}(\eta(x)+\eta(y))}+\frac{n}{2}\,\eta(y)\log\frac{\eta(y)}{\frac{1}{2}(\eta(x)+\eta(y))}\ .

Proposition 2.7
.

The largest constant
\kappa\geq 0
satisfying, for all
\xi\in\Delta(V)
and
t\geq 0
,

(2.22)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[D(\eta_{t}||\pi)\right]\leq e^{-\kappa\,t}D(\xi||\pi)\ ,

coincides with the largest constant
\kappa\geq 0
satisfying, for all
\eta\in\Delta(V)
,

(2.23)

\displaystyle\kappa\,D(\eta||\pi)\leq\frac{2}{n}\sum_{xy\in E}{\rm ent}_{xy}(\eta)\ .

Proof.

Recalling the notation (
2.2
), and observing that

(2.24)

\eta(x)\log\frac{\eta(x)}{\pi(x)}+\eta(y)\log\frac{\eta(y)}{\pi(y)}-\eta^{xy}(x)\log\frac{\eta^{xy}(x)}{\pi(x)}-\eta^{xy}(y)\log\frac{\eta^{xy}(y)}{\pi(y)}=\frac{2}{n}\,{\rm ent}_{xy}(\eta)\ ,

we see that, as a result of an update at edge
xy
, the relative entropy of
\eta
decreases from
D(\eta||\pi)
to
D(\eta^{xy}||\pi)=D(\eta||\pi)-\frac{2}{n}\,{\rm ent}_{xy}(\eta)
.
Therefore,

(2.25)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[D(\eta_{t}||\pi)\right]=\mathcal{L}\,\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[D(\eta_{t}||\pi)\right]=-\frac{2}{n}\,\sum_{xy\in E}\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[{\rm ent}_{xy}(\eta_{t})\right]\ .

By integrating over time, we see that (
2.23
) implies (
2.22
). On the other hand, if (
2.22
) holds for all
t>0
, then subtracting
D(\xi||\pi)
from both sides and taking the limit
t\to 0^{+}
one obtains (
2.23
).
∎

Following
[
BC21
]
, we call
\kappa=\kappa(G)
the
entropy constant
of the graph
G
. One can compute the value of
\kappa(G)
for certain simple graphs. For instance, consider the trivial case where
G
consists of a single edge
xy
. In this case
n=2
and
D(\eta||\pi)={\rm ent}_{xy}(\eta)
so that
\kappa=1
. Therefore, using the product structure one easily checks that the entropy constant of the hypercube
\{0,1\}^{d}
is again
\kappa=1
for all
d\geq 1
.
We refer to
[
BC21
]
for other examples including random walks on hypergraphs and processes with more than one particle such as the Binomial splitting process.

Proposition
2.7
is the entropic analogue of the
L^{2}
-statement in Proposition
2.3
.
A linearization argument shows that the constant
\kappa=\kappa(G)
in (
2.22
) satisfies
\kappa(G)\leq 1/t_{\rm rel}(G)
for any graph
G
.
Moreover, one can show (see
[
BC21
, Lem. 2.1]
) that
\kappa(G)\geq 2\log(2)\beta(G)
, for any
G
, where
\beta(G)
denotes the Log-Sobolev constant of
G
defined as the largest constant
\beta\geq 0
satisfying, for all
\eta\in\Delta(V)
,

(2.26)

\displaystyle\beta\,D(\eta||\pi)\leq\mathcal{E}^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm RW}(\sqrt{\eta/\pi})\ .

In particular, this implies that the entropic bound
\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[D(\eta_{t}||\pi)\right]\leq e^{-\beta\,t}D(\xi||\pi)
proposed in

[
AL12
, Prop. 7]
can be improved by a factor
2\log(2)
in the exponential decay rate.
Further relations of interest include upper bounds on
\kappa(G)
in terms of both
\beta(G)
and the Modified Log-Sobolev constant of
G
, see
[
BC21
]
for more details.

Finally, Proposition
2.7
and Pinsker’s inequality

(2.27)

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\eta}{\pi}-1\right\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{2D(\eta||\pi)}\ ,\qquad\eta\in\Delta(V)\ ,

provide the following upper bound on
L^{1}
-distance in terms of the entropy constant
\kappa
.

Corollary 2.8
.

For all
\xi\in\Delta(V)
, and for all
t\geq 0

(2.28)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{1}\right]\leq e^{-\kappa\,t/2}\sqrt{2\log n},

where
\kappa=\kappa(G)
denotes the entropy constant of
G
.

Proof.

From (
2.27
) and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, one has

(2.29)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{1}\right]\leq\sqrt{2\,\mathds{E}_{\xi}\,D(\eta_{t}||\pi)}\leq e^{-\kappa\,t/2}\sqrt{2D(\xi||\pi)}\ .

By convexity,
D(\xi||\pi)
is maximized when
\xi
is a Dirac mass at a vertex; therefore, since
\pi\equiv 1/n
,
D(\xi||\pi)\leq\log n
.
∎

2.5.
A lower bound using relative entropy

Concerning lower bounds, the following statement is the continuous-time version of a useful estimate established in the recent work
[
MSW22
]
.

Proposition 2.9

(
[
MSW22
, Th. 1]
)
.

Let

(2.30)

T_{\varepsilon}:=(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\log_{2}n}{\langle{\rm deg}\rangle}\,,\qquad\varepsilon\in(0,1)\,,

where
{\rm deg}(x):=
degree of
x\in V
in the graph
G
, and
\langle{\rm deg}\rangle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x}{\rm deg}(x)
.
Then,

(2.31)

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\xi\in\Delta(V)}\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{T_{\varepsilon}}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{1}\right]\geq\varepsilon\ .

Proof.

As in the proof of Proposition
2.7
, an update at edge
xy
at time
t
causes the relative entropy of
\eta_{t}
to decrease from
D(\eta_{t}||\pi)
to
D(\eta^{xy}_{t}||\pi)=D(\eta_{t}||\pi)-\frac{2}{n}\,{\rm ent}_{xy}(\eta_{t})
. Since
\frac{2}{n}\,{\rm ent}_{xy}(\eta_{t})\leq(\eta_{t}(x)+\eta_{t}(y))\log 2
, integrating and using (
2.5
), one has

(2.32)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[D(\eta_{t}||\pi)\right]&\geq D(\xi||\pi)-\log 2\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{xy\in E}\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\eta_{s}(x)+\eta_{s}(y)\right]{\rm d}s\\
&=D(\xi||\pi)-\log 2\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in V}\pi^{\xi}_{s}(x)\,{\rm deg}(x)\,{\rm d}s\,.\end{aligned}

Since
\langle{\rm deg}\rangle-{\rm deg}
has mean zero with respect to
\pi
, there exists

\psi:V\to\mathbb{R}
solving

(2.33)

L^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm RW}}\psi(x)=\log 2\>(\langle{\rm deg}\rangle-{\rm deg}(x))\ ,\qquad x\in V\ .

Moreover,
\psi
is defined up to constants, so that we are allowed to select a translation of
\psi
satisfying

(2.34)

\psi\geq 0\ ,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\psi(y)=0\quad\text{for some}\ y\in V\ .

Note that if
G
is regular (i.e.,
{\rm deg}\equiv\text{const.}
), then we can take
\psi\equiv 0
.
Introducing the function

(2.35)

F(\eta):=D(\eta||\pi)-\sum_{x\in V}\eta(x)\,\psi(x)\ ,\qquad\eta\in\Delta(V)\ ,

the two conditions in (
2.34
) ensure, respectively,

(2.36)

\displaystyle F(\eta_{t})\leq D(\eta_{t}||\pi)\ ,\qquad\mathds{P}_{{\bf 1}_{y}}\text{-a.s.}\ ,

and

(2.37)

\displaystyle F({\bf 1}_{y})=D({\bf 1}_{y}||\pi)=\log n\ .

By combining (
2.35
), (
2.5
), (
2.37
), (
2.32
), and writing
\pi_{t}^{y}=\pi_{t}^{{\bf 1}_{y}}
,

(2.38)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{y}}\left[F(\eta_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{y}}\left[D(\eta_{t}||\pi)\right]-\sum_{x\in V}\pi^{y}_{t}(x)\,\psi(x)

(2.39)

\displaystyle\geq\log n-\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in V}\left(\log 2\,\pi^{y}_{s}(x)\,{\rm deg}(x)+\big(L^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm RW}}\pi^{y}_{s}\big)(x)\,\psi(x)\right){\rm d}s

(2.40)

\displaystyle=\log n-\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in V}\pi^{y}_{s}(x)\left(\log 2\,{\rm deg}(x)+L^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm RW}}\psi(x)\right){\rm d}s\,

where for the last identity we used that
L^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm RW}}
is symmetric.
By (
2.33
), the expression inside the time integral above equals
\log 2\,\langle{\rm deg}\rangle
, thus, using (
2.36
),

(2.41)

\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{y}}\left[D(\eta_{t}||\pi)\right]\geq\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{y}}\left[F(\eta_{t})\right]\geq\log n-\langle{\rm deg}\rangle\,t\log 2\,,\qquad t\geq 0\,,

and choosing
t=T_{\varepsilon}
as in (
2.30
) one has
\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{y}}D(\eta_{t}||\pi)\geq\varepsilon\log n=\varepsilon D({\bf 1}_{y}||\pi)
.
To obtain the desired lower bound on the
L^{1}
-norm one uses the fact that, for
\pi
uniform, Fannes–Audenaert inequality (see, e.g.,
[
Aud07
]
)

(2.42)

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\eta}{\pi}-1\right\|_{1}\geq\frac{D(\eta||\pi)}{\log n}-\frac{1}{e\log n}\ ,\qquad\eta\in\Delta(V)\ ,

holds true. This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.10
.

The above proof shows that
\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{y}}D(\eta_{T_{\varepsilon}}||\pi)\geq\varepsilon D({\bf 1}_{y}||\pi)

\displaystyle\kappa(G)\leq\frac{\langle{\rm deg}\rangle}{\log_{2}n}\,.

). Moreover, the bound (

) is saturated also when

is the hypercube, in which case one has
\kappa(G)=1
.

Remark 2.11
.

For certain families of graphs such as the complete graphs, the lower bound in (

) predicts very sharply the order of the

-mixing time for
{\rm Avg}(G)
, see the discussion in Section

. However, due to the

-dependence
in (

2.6.1.
Hypercube

Here

On the other hand, the entropy contraction from

.

]
. Indeed, mixing for

3.
Mixing on the hypercube

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
. We let
G=(V,E)
be the
d
-hypercube
V=\{0,1\}^{d}
, whose edge set consists of unordered pairs
\{x,x^{i}\}
, with
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\{0,1\}^{d}
and

(3.1)

\displaystyle x^{i}_{j}=(x^{i})_{j}:=\begin{cases}x_{j}&\text{if}\ j\neq i\\
1-x_{i}&\text{otherwise}\ .\end{cases}

By Proposition
2.6
and transitivity of the underlying graph,
all
Dirac masses are worst-case initial conditions for
{\rm Avg}(G)
. Hence, throughout this section, we fix an arbitrary
x_{0}\in V
, and set
\xi={\bf 1}_{x_{0}}
as an initial condition.

As already discussed in Section
2.6.1
, the claim in Theorem
1.1
reduces to estimating from above the
L^{2}
-mixing time of
{\rm Avg}(G)
.
In view of (
2.12
), we have, for all
t\geq 0
,

(3.2)

\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{x_{0}}}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=2^{d}\>{\mathbf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm CRW}}_{x_{0},x_{0}}(X_{t}=Y_{t})-1\ .

In order to estimate the probability on the right-hand side of (
3.2
), we analyze the Hamming distance (cf. (
3.3
) below) between these two coupled walks. It is well-known that, in absence of coupling effects, i.e., for two independent copies of
{\rm RW}(G)
on
G=\{0,1\}^{d}
, the process induced by this projection corresponds to the Markov chain on
\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
known as Ehrenfest urn (see, e.g.,
[
LP17
, Sect. 2.3]
). The Hamming distance between the two coordinates of
{\rm CRW}(G)
still yields a birth-and-death chain, but this time with a “defect” due to the interaction.
For the purpose of proving Theorem
1.1
, we carry out an analysis of the
L^{2}
-mixing time of such a perturbation of the Ehrenfest urn.
Mixing with respect to
L^{1}
- and separation distances for perturbations of birth-and-death chains has been studied
in
[
DSC06
]
and
[
CSC15
]
, see, e.g.,
[
CSC13
, Th. 4.10]
. However, for the
L^{2}
-distance, the situation seems to be much less generally understood. For this we combine general results on birth-and-death chains with the recent characterization
[
HP18
]
of
L^{2}
-mixing times in the reversible context.

3.1.
A perturbation of the Ehrenfest urn

We start with some definitions. Consider the two birth-and-death chains obtained from both
{\rm RW}(G)\otimes{\rm RW}(G)
(i.e., two independent copies of
{\rm RW}(G)
) and
{\rm CRW}(G)
via the projection

(3.3)

(x,y)\in\{0,1\}^{d}\times\{0,1\}^{d}\longmapsto|x-y|:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}-y_{i}|\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\}\ .

Let
P_{t}(k,\ell)
and
S_{t}(k,\ell)
,
k,\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
,
denote the transition kernels of these two Markov chains, and let
\mathcal{L}^{P}
and
\mathcal{L}^{S}
denote their infinitesimal generators, respectively. Writing
p^{P,S}_{k}
, resp.
q^{P,S}_{k}
, for the associated birth, resp. death, rates when in state
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
, we have

(3.4)

p^{P}_{k}=d-k\ ,\qquad q^{P}_{k}=k\ ,

and

(3.5)

p^{S}_{k}={\bf 1}_{k\neq 0}\,p^{P}_{k}+{\bf 1}_{k=0}\,\frac{d}{2}\,,\qquad q^{S}_{k}={\bf 1}_{k\neq 1}\,q^{P}_{k}+1_{k=1}\,\frac{1}{2}\ ,

while the Binomial distribution
\nu
given by

(3.6)

\nu(k):=\binom{d}{k}\,2^{-d}\ ,\qquad k\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\}\ ,

is reversible for both chains.
Finally, we let
P_{t}
and
S_{t}
represent the Markov semigroups of the (continuous–time) Ehrenfest urn and its perturbation, respectively.

With these definitions, the right-hand side of (
3.2
) further reads as

(3.7)

\displaystyle 2^{d}S_{t}(0,0)-1

\displaystyle=\left\|S_{t}\left(\frac{{\bf 1}_{0}}{\nu}\right)-1\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\nu)}=\left\|S_{t/2}\left(\frac{{\bf 1}_{0}}{\nu}\right)-1\right\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}^{2}\,.

The desired upper bound on the
L^{2}
-mixing of
{\rm Avg}(G)
at times
\frac{1}{2}\log d+\Theta(1)
then follows by (
3.2
) as soon as the
S
-chain is well-mixed in
L^{2}
at times
\frac{1}{4}\log d+\Theta(1)
. This last claim is established in the next proposition, which is the main technical result of this section.

Proposition 3.1
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
c=c_{\varepsilon}>0
such that, for all
d
sufficiently large,

(3.8)

\left\|S_{t}\left(\frac{{\bf 1}_{0}}{\nu}\right)-1\right\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}^{2}\leq\varepsilon

holds for all
t>\tfrac{1}{4}\log d+c
.

The proof of this result is postponed to Section
3.2
below.

Remark 3.2
.

By well-known monotonicity properties of birth-and-death chains (see, e.g.,
[
DLP10
, Lem. 4.1]
), we have the following comparison inequalities:

(3.9)

P_{t}(0,0)\leq S_{t}(0,0)\leq P_{t/2}(0,0)\ ,\qquad t\geq 0\ .

Since the
P
-chain mixes abruptly at times
t\approx\frac{1}{4}\log d
, the bounds in (
3.9
) are insufficient to establish cutoff for the
S
-chain.
In this respect, Proposition
3.1
tells us that the first inequality in (
3.9
) is sharp for times
t\approx\tfrac{1}{4}\log d
. Therefore, the second inequality — which, roughly speaking, accounts for the early delay of the
S
-chain when exiting state
0
— needs to be improved for such times.

3.2.
Proof of Proposition
3.1

The idea of the proof Proposition
3.1
is based on the probabilistic approach to
L^{2}
-mixing recently introduced in
[
HP18
]
, which links
L^{2}
-mixing times to estimates of suitable hitting times. Building on such a machinery, the following lemma represents the main technical step to derive the claim in Proposition
3.1
. Its proof combines two ingredients: the knowledge of the distribution of hitting times for birth-and-death chains (
[
BS87
]
), and a discrete weighted Hardy’s inequality (
[
Mic99
]
). As a result, we obtain a comparison between the
P
-chain and the
S
-chain that is much finer than the one implied by (
3.9
). This, together with the results in
[
HP18
]
, provides a tight comparison between the mixing behavior of the two chains.

In what follows,
\mathds{P}^{P,S}_{k}
denotes the law of the
P
- or
S
-chain defined in Section
3.1
when starting from state
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
, while
\nu([0,M)):=\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\nu(k)
(cf. (
3.6
)).

Lemma 3.3
.

Define
\tau_{M}
to be the first hitting time of the state
M\in\{0,1,\dots,d\}
. Then, for every
\sigma>0
, there exists
b=b(\sigma)>0
satisfying, for all
d
large enough,

(3.10)

\mathds{P}^{S}_{0}\left(\tau_{M}>t+b\right)\leq\left(\nu([0,M))\right)^{\sigma}+\mathds{P}^{P}_{0}\left(\tau_{M}>t\right)\ ,\qquad t>0\ ,\ M\in\{1,\dots,d/2\}\ .

Proof.

As shown in
[
BS87
]
(see also, e.g.,
[
CSC13
]
), for any birth-and-death chain on
\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
with rate matrix
\mathcal{L}
started in
k=0
, the hitting time
\tau_{M}
of the target
M\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
is distributed as the sum of
M
independent exponential random variables with rates given by
(\lambda_{M,i})_{i=0}^{M-1}
, these being the distinct eigenvalues of
-\mathcal{L}|_{M}
, the generator of the sub-Markovian chain
\mathcal{L}
killed upon exiting
\{0,1,\ldots,M-1\}
.

Now, while the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the Ehrenfest urn
\mathcal{L}^{P}
defined in Section
3.1
are well-known (see, e.g.,
[
KM65
]
), neither those of
\mathcal{L}^{P}|_{M}
nor of
\mathcal{L}^{S}|_{M}
are explicit. Nevertheless, a comparison of eigenvalues is available. Letting
U:=\diag(\sqrt{\nu})
and
\tilde{}\mathcal{L}^{P,S}:=U\mathcal{L}^{P,S}U^{-1}
, we have

(3.11)

\displaystyle-\tilde{}\mathcal{L}^{S}=-\tilde{}\mathcal{L}^{P}+\mathcal{R}\ ,

with
\mathcal{R}=(\mathcal{R}_{ij})_{i,j=0}^{d}
being the following rank-one matrix:
\mathcal{R}_{ij}=0
if
i+j>1
, and

(3.12)

\mathcal{R}_{00}=-\frac{d}{2}\ ,\qquad\mathcal{R}_{01}=\mathcal{R}_{10}=\sqrt{\frac{d}{2}}\ ,\qquad\mathcal{R}_{11}=-\frac{1}{2}\ .

By removing, for every
M\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, the last
d-M+1
rows and columns of such matrices, we recover analogous relations for the sub-Markovian chains killed upon exiting
\{0,1,\ldots,M-1\}
; in particular, the symmetric matrix
-\tilde{}\mathcal{L}^{S}|_{M}
is a rank-one perturbation of
-\tilde{}\mathcal{L}^{P}|_{M}
. Therefore, Weyl theorem (see, e.g.,
[
HJ12
, Th. 4.3.1]
) yields

(3.13)

(\lambda_{M,i-1}^{P}\vee\lambda_{M,i-1}^{S})\leq\lambda_{M,i}^{S}\leq\lambda_{M,i}^{P}\ ,\qquad i=0,\ldots,M-1\ .

Moreover, the variational characterization of the spectral bounds
\lambda_{M,0}^{P}
and
\lambda_{M,0}^{S}
and the definitions of rates in (
3.4
)–(
3.5
) ensure that

(3.14)

\frac{1}{2}\,\lambda_{M,0}^{P}\leq\lambda_{M,0}^{S}\ .

Now, let
Z^{P,S}_{M,i},\tilde{Z}^{P,S}_{M,i}\sim{\rm Exp}(\lambda^{P,S}_{M,i})
be all mutually independent exponential r.v.’s, with
\mathds{P}
denoting their joint law; then, for all
s>0
and
M\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, (
3.13
)–(
3.14
) yield

(3.15)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}Z^{S}_{M,i}>s\right)\leq\mathds{P}\left(Z^{S}_{M,0}+\sum_{i=1}^{M-1}Z^{P}_{M,i-1}>s\right)\leq\mathds{P}\left(2\,\tilde{Z}^{P}_{M,0}+\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}Z^{P}_{M,i}>s\right)\ .

Setting
s=t+b>0
in the above inequality, by independence, we further get

(3.16)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}Z^{S}_{M,i}>t+b\right)\leq\mathds{P}\left(Z^{P}_{M,0}>b/2\right)+\mathds{P}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}Z^{P}_{M,i}>t\right)\ .

In view of
Z^{P}_{M,0}\sim{\rm Exp}(\lambda^{P}_{M,0})
and the aforementioned explicit distribution of
\tau_{M}
under
\mathds{P}_{0}^{P,S}
, the above inequality reads as

(3.17)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}^{S}_{0}\left(\tau_{M}>t+b\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{b}{2}\,\lambda^{P}_{M,0}\right)+\mathds{P}^{P}_{0}\left(\tau_{M}>t\right)\ .

We estimate the first term on the right-hand side above in Appendix
A
below, by proving

(3.18)

\displaystyle\limsup_{d\to\infty}\sup_{1\leq M\leq d/2}\left(\lambda^{P}_{M,0}\right)^{-1}\log\left(\frac{1}{\nu([0,M))}\right)<\infty\ ,

which, combined with (
3.17
), yields the assertion of the lemma.
∎

We are now in a good position to prove Proposition
3.1
.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

We start by recalling some of the results in
[
HP18
]
(namely, Prop. 3.8, and Thm.s 1.2 & 5.1), suitably adapted to the context of a generic birth-and-death chain on
\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
with transition matrix
Q_{t}
, reversible measure
\mu
, and starting from
k=0
. Concisely, the following chain of inequalities hold true in this context:

(3.19)

\displaystyle\rho^{Q}_{0}\leq T^{Q}_{2,0}\leq\bar{\rho}^{Q}_{0}+5\,t_{\rm rel}^{Q}\leq\rho_{0}^{Q}+8\,t^{Q}_{\rm LS}+10\,t^{Q}_{\rm rel}\ ,

where:

•

T^{Q}_{2,0}
is the
L^{2}
-mixing time of the
Q
-chain when starting from
k=0
, i.e.,

(3.20)

T^{Q}_{2,0}:=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\left\|Q_{t}\left(\frac{{\bf 1}_{0}}{\mu}\right)-1\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\leq\frac{1}{2}\right\}\ ;

•

t_{\rm rel}^{Q}:=(\lambda_{\rm gap}^{Q})^{-1}
is the relaxation time of the chain;

•

t_{\rm LS}^{Q}:=(\beta_{\rm LS}^{Q})^{-1}
is the inverse of the Log-Sobolev constant of the chain;

•

letting
\mu([0,M)):=\sum_{\ell=0}^{M-1}\mu(\ell)
and
\mu([M,d]):=\sum_{\ell=M}^{d}\mu(\ell)
,
M\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,

(3.21)

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}_{0}^{Q}\coloneqq\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\mathds{P}^{Q}_{0}\left(\tau_{M}>t\right)\leq\mu([0,M))^{3}\ \text{for all}\ M\in\{1,\ldots,d/2\}\right\}\ ;

•

\rho_{0}^{Q}
is defined analogously to
\bar{\rho}_{0}^{Q}
with
\mu([0,M))^{3}
replaced by

(3.22)

\mu([0,M))+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\mu([0,M))\,\mu([M,d])}\ .

We now specialize the inequalities in (
3.19
) to our setting of
P
- and
S
-chain. It is immediate to check by tensorization and comparison of Dirichlet forms that
t^{P}_{\rm rel},t^{P}_{\rm LS},t^{S}_{\rm rel},t^{S}_{\rm LS}=O(1)
.
Further, we know the
L^{2}
-mixing of the Ehrenfest urn, namely
T^{P}_{2,0}\leq\frac{1}{4}\log d+\Theta(1)
. These two observations yield

(3.23)

\displaystyle\rho_{0}^{P}\leq\bar{\rho}_{0}^{P}\leq\frac{1}{4}\log d+a\ ,

for some
a>0
large enough. In view of the definition of
\bar{\rho}_{0}^{P}
(see (
3.21
)), and Lemma
3.3
,
we get, for all
M\in\{1,\ldots,d/2\}
,

(3.24)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}^{S}_{0}\left(\tau_{M}>\frac{1}{4}\log d+a+b\right)

\displaystyle\leq\nu([0,M))^{3}+\mathds{P}^{P}_{0}\left(\tau_{M}>\frac{1}{4}\log d+a\right)

(3.25)

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\nu([0,M))^{3}\ ,

where
b=b(\sigma)>0
is the constant appearing in the statement of Lemma
3.3
for
\sigma=3
. Note that
2\,\nu([0,M))^{3}\leq\nu([0,M))
for all
M\in\{1,\ldots,d/2\}
. Therefore, the definition of
\rho_{0}^{S}
, the last inequality in (
3.19
) and
t_{\rm rel}^{S},t_{\rm LS}^{S}=O(1)
yield the desired upper bound on
T^{S}_{2,0}
, thus, concluding the proof of the proposition.
∎

4.
L^{1}
-mixing on complete bipartite graphs

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.2
. All throughout, we let
m\in\{1,\ldots,\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor\}
, and consider the complete bipartite graph with parts
|\mathcal{C}_{1}|=m
and
|\mathcal{C}_{2}|=n-m
. In this context,
\mathcal{L}
in (
2.1
) reads as follows:

(4.1)

\mathcal{L}f(\eta)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{C}_{2}}\left(f(\eta^{xy})-f(\eta)\right)\ ,\qquad\eta\in\Delta(V)\ ,\ f:\Delta(V)\to\mathbb{R}\ .

The proof of Theorem
1.2
is divided into several steps. Following
[
CDSZ22
]
, we use the discretized dynamics defined as follows.
Define

(4.2)

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lfloor\log_{2}n-\log(n)^{1/3}\rfloor\ .

We divide a unit mass into
2^{H}
chunks. To each chunk we assign a label
u\in\{1,\ldots,2^{H}\}
, a position in
V
, and a mass equal to
2^{-H}
.
We add a cemetery state
\{\dagger\}
and define a dynamics of these mass chunks as follows.
For every chunk
u=1,\ldots,2^{H}
, we let
e_{t}(u)\in V\cup\{\dagger\}
denote its position at time
t\geq 0
, and introduce two sequences
(\beta_{t}(u))_{t\geq 0}
and
(\alpha_{t}(u))_{t\geq 0}\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
, with
\beta_{0}(u)=\alpha_{0}(u)=0
.
We write
(w_{t}(x))_{x\in V\cup\{\dagger\}}
for the amount of mass at
x
at time
t
, that is

(4.3)

w_{t}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{u=1}^{2^{H}}2^{-H}{\bf 1}_{e_{t}(u)=x}\ ,\qquad x\in V\cup\{\dagger\}\ ,\ t\geq 0\,.

The dynamics is given by the following process:

(1)

all chunks are initially placed on the same site
x_{0}\in V
:
e_{0}(u)=x_{0},\;\forall u
;

(2)

the process follows the same edge updates of the averaging process: each edge rings independently with rate 1;

(3)

when edge
xy
rings at time
t
, two types of scenarios can occur:

(a)

good scenarios:

(i)

there are no chunks at the endpoints of
xy
, and nothing occurs, or

(ii)

exactly one of the endpoints, say
y
, of the edges is empty, and
x
contains
2^{i}
chunks with
i>0
; in this case half of the chunks on
x
are selected uniformly at random and moved to
y
; the other half remains at
x
; concurrently, for all chunks
u
sitting at either
x
or
y
, we set
\alpha_{t}(u)\coloneqq\alpha_{t^{-}}(u)+1
;

(b)

bad scenarios:

(i)

\beta
-event: if both
w_{t^{-}}(x)>0
and
w_{t^{-}}(y)>0
, then set

(4.4)

\beta_{t^{\prime}}(u)=1\ ,\qquad e_{t^{\prime}}(u)=\dagger\ ,

for all
t^{\prime}\geq t
, and all
u=1,\ldots,2^{H}
such that
e_{t^{-}}(u)\in\{x,y\}
. In particular,
w_{t}(x)=w_{t}(y)=0
, and
w_{t}(\dagger)=w_{t^{-}}(\dagger)+w_{t^{-}}(x)+w_{t^{-}}(y)
.

(ii)

\alpha
-event: if exactly one of the extremes, say
y
, of the edges is empty, and
x
contains exactly one chunk
u
, i.e.
\alpha_{t^{-}}(u)=H
, then set, for all
t^{\prime}\geq t
,

(4.5)

\alpha_{t^{\prime}}(u)=H+1\ ,\qquad e_{t^{\prime}}(u)=\dagger\ .

In words, the discretized mass splits exactly along an edge if the proposed edge is not occupied at both ends, while if both ends are occupied all mass chunks from that edge are sent to the cemetery. Moreover, if a single mass chunk sits at one of the endpoints of the proposed edge, then it is sent to the cemetery.
By construction, the following properties hold a.s.:

(1)

the total mass of the chunks is preserved by the dynamics, i.e.,

(4.6)

w_{t}(\dagger)+\sum_{x\in V}w_{t}(x)=1\ ,\qquad t\geq 0,

and the mass at
\dagger
increases when a bad scenario occurs.

(2)

if
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denotes the Averaging process with all the mass initially placed at
x_{0}\in V
, then

(4.7)

\eta_{t}(x)\geq w_{t}(x)\ ,\qquad x\in V\ ,\ t\geq 0\ ;

(3)

the process
(w_{t})_{t\geq 0}
eventually sends all chunks to
\dagger
, i.e.,

(4.8)

\lim_{t\to\infty}w_{t}(\dagger)=1\ .

(4)

for any chunk not in
\dagger
at time
t\geq 0
, the variable
\alpha
satisfies

(4.9)

w_{t}(e_{t}(u))=2^{-\alpha_{t}(u)}\ ,\qquad\forall u:\,\;e_{t}(u)\neq\dagger\ .

We start with a technical lemma, and all throughout adopt the following notation (cf. (
1.6
)):

(4.10)

\displaystyle t_{\rm mix}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\log_{2}n\ ,\qquad\gamma\coloneqq\frac{m(n-m)}{n}\ ,

(4.11)

\displaystyle t^{\lambda}_{\rm mix}\coloneqq t_{\rm mix}+\frac{\lambda}{\gamma}\sqrt{\log n}\ ,\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\ .

Lemma 4.1
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, there exist constants
\lambda=\lambda(\varepsilon)>0
and
b=b(\varepsilon)>0
such that for all
u=1,\ldots,2^{H}
,

(4.12)

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathds{P}\left(\log_{2}(n)-b\sqrt{\log n}\leq\alpha_{t^{-\lambda}_{\rm mix}}(u)\leq H\,,\,\beta_{t^{-\lambda}_{\rm mix}}(u)=0\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

The proof of Lemma
4.1
is postponed to the end of this section.
Next, we assume its validity and finish the proof of the theorem through the following two propositions.

Proposition 4.2

(Lower bound)
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\lambda=\lambda(\varepsilon)>0
such that for every
x_{0}\in V

(4.13)

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{x_{0}}}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t^{-\lambda}_{\rm mix}}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{1}\right]\geq 2-\varepsilon\ .

Proof.

In view of the definition and properties of the process
(w_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, setting
t=t^{-\lambda}_{\rm mix}
,

(4.14)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{x_{0}}}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in V}\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{x_{0}}}\left[\left(\eta_{t}(x)-\frac{1}{n}\right)_{+}\right]

(4.15)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{x\in V}\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{x_{0}}}\left[\left(\eta_{t}(x)-\frac{1}{n}\right)_{+}{\bf 1}_{\{w_{t}(x)\geq\frac{1}{n}\}}\right]

(4.16)

\displaystyle\ \geq\sum_{x\in V}\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{x_{0}}}\left[\left(w_{t}(x)-\frac{1}{n}\right){\bf 1}_{\{w_{t}(x)\geq\frac{1}{n}\}}\right]

(4.17)

\displaystyle=2^{-H}\sum_{u=1}^{2^{H}}\mathds{P}\left(e_{t}(u)\neq\dagger\right)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in V}\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{x_{0}}}\left[{\bf 1}_{\{w_{t}(x)\geq\frac{1}{n}\}}\right]

(4.18)

\displaystyle\geq\mathds{P}\left(\alpha_{t}(u_{0})\leq H,\beta_{t}(u_{0})=0\right)-\frac{2^{H}}{n}\ ,

where
u_{0}
is any fixed chunk label, and we use symmetry and the fact that at most
2^{H}
sites can be non empty at any given time. Finally, since
2^{H}=o(n)
, Lemma
4.1
concludes the proof. ∎

Proposition 4.3

(Upper bound)
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\lambda=\lambda(\varepsilon)>0

such that for every
x_{0}\in V

(4.19)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{x_{0}}}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t^{\lambda}_{\rm mix}}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{1}\right]\leq\varepsilon\ .

Proof.

Fix constants
c,b>0
to be specified later, and let
T^{-}:=t_{\rm mix}^{-c}
be defined as in (
4.10
). Given the processes
(\eta_{t})
and
(w_{t})
, we define a third process
(\tilde{\eta}_{t})
as follows:

(1)

for times
t<T^{-}
,
\tilde{\eta}_{t}=w_{t}
;

(2)

for times
t\geq T^{-}
, we let
(\tilde{\eta}_{t})
evolve as an Averaging process, following the same edge updates of
(\eta_{t})
, but starting at time
T^{-}
from the following configuration (possibly, with total mass strictly less than one): for every
x\in V
,

(4.20)

\tilde{\eta}_{T^{-}}(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}w_{T^{-}}(x)&\text{if}\ w_{T^{-}}(x)\leq\frac{2^{b\sqrt{\log n}}}{n}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\ ;\end{cases}

(3)

|\tilde{\eta}|:=\sum_{x\in V}\tilde{\eta}_{T^{-}}(x)\in[0,1]

In view of this construction, for all

,

(4.21)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{x_{0}}}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{1}\right]

(4.22)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x\in V}\mathds{E}\left[\left|\eta_{t}(x)-\frac{1}{n}-\left(\tilde{\eta}_{t}(x)-\frac{|\tilde{\eta}|}{n}\right)\right|\right]+\sum_{x\in V}\mathds{E}\left[\left|\tilde{\eta}_{t}(x)-\frac{|\tilde{\eta}|}{n}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq 1-\mathds{E}\left[|\tilde{\eta}|\right]+\sum_{x\in V}\mathds{E}\left[\eta_{t}(x)-\tilde{\eta}_{t}(x)\right]+\mathds{E}\left[|\tilde{\eta}|\left\|\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{t}}{|\tilde{\eta}|\pi}-1\right\|_{1}\right]\ ,

where we use the triangle inequality twice and the fact that
\eta_{t}(x)\geq\tilde{\eta}_{t}(x)

As a consequence,

(4.24)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{1}\right]\leq 2\left(1-\mathds{E}\left[|\tilde{\eta}|\right]\right)+\mathds{E}\left[|\tilde{\eta}|\left\|\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{t}}{|\tilde{\eta}|\pi}-1\right\|_{1}\right]\ .

The first term on the right-hand side of (

) is at most
2\varepsilon
if the constants
c=c(\varepsilon)
, where
c
is the constant appearing in
T^{-}=t_{\rm mix}^{-c}
, and

are large enough. Indeed,
by Lemma

,

(4.25)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[|\tilde{\eta}|\right]=2^{-H}\sum_{u=1}^{2^{H}}\mathds{P}\left(\log_{2}(n)-b\sqrt{\log n}\leq\alpha_{T^{-}}(u)\leq H\,,\,\beta_{T^{-}}(u)=0\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon\,.

As for the second term on the right-hand side of (
4.24
), letting
\mathcal{F}_{s}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the edge updates up to time

, the Markov property yields

(4.26)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[|\tilde{\eta}|\left\|\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{t}}{|\tilde{\eta}|\pi}-1\right\|_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left[|\tilde{\eta}|\mathds{E}\left[\left\|\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{t}}{|\tilde{\eta}|\pi}-1\right\|_{1}\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{T^{-}}\right]\right]

(4.27)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\left[|\tilde{\eta}|\sqrt{\mathds{E}\left[\left\|\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{t}}{|\tilde{\eta}|\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{T^{-}}\right]}\right]

(4.28)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\left[|\tilde{\eta}|\sqrt{e^{-(t-T^{-})/t_{\rm rel}}\left\|\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{T^{-}}}{|\tilde{\eta}|\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}}\right]\ .

Above we used Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Aldous-Lanoue’s

-upper bound from time
T^{-}
to

.
By definition of the process
(\tilde{\eta}_{t})
,

(4.29)

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{T^{-}}}{|\tilde{\eta}|\pi}-1\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{T^{-}}}{|\tilde{\eta}|\pi}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\tilde{\eta}_{T^{-}}\right\|_{\infty}\frac{n}{|\tilde{\eta}|}\leq\frac{2^{b\sqrt{\log n}}}{|\tilde{\eta}|}\,.

Finally, applying this with

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[|\tilde{\eta}|\left\|\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{t^{\lambda}_{\rm mix}}}{|\tilde{\eta}|\pi}-1\right\|_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{-(t^{\lambda}_{\rm mix}-T^{-})}{2t_{\rm rel}}\right)2^{b\sqrt{\log n}}\,\mathds{E}\left[\sqrt{|\tilde{\eta}|}\right]

(4.31)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{n}{n-m}\frac{c+\lambda}{4}\sqrt{\log n}\right)2^{b\sqrt{\log n}}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\left(b\log 2-\frac{c+\lambda}{4}\right)\sqrt{\log n}\right).

For all
\varepsilon>0
and

, by choosing
\lambda>0
sufficiently large, the right-hand side is bounded above by
\varepsilon
for all

large enough.
This concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Set
t:=t^{-c}_{\rm mix}
and fix
u\in\{1,\ldots,2^{H}\}
.
We start by proving that, for every
c>0
,

(4.33)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathds{P}(\beta_{t}(u)=0)=1\ .

Suppose the chunk
u
starts in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
and note that the hitting time
\tau_{1}
of the set
\mathcal{C}_{1}
for

is

. Once the chunk is in
\mathcal{C}_{1}

\mathcal{C}_{2}

at some time

Then, by neglecting the time spent in

each time, we can estimate

\mathds{P}(\tau_{j}<t)\leq\mathds{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j}X_{i}<t\right)\ ,\qquad j\in\mathbb{N}\ ,

where

are i.i.d.
{\rm Exp}(m/2)
. If instead

\mathcal{C}_{1}
then
\tau_{1}=0
and the

) holds with the sum on the right hand side going up to

j
.
Therefore, recalling that
t=O(m^{-1}\log n)
, taking e.g.

in (

) we have

(4.35)

\mathds{P}(u\ \text{hits $\mathcal{C}_{1}$ more than $\log^{2}(n)$ times before $t$})=o(1)\ .

Let us consider the event

that after the
i
-th hitting of
\mathcal{C}_{1}
,
u
experiences a
\beta
-event before moving to
\mathcal{C}_{2}
or to
\dagger
because of an
\alpha
-event. We are going to show that
uniformly in
i\geq 1
,

(4.36)

\log^{2}(n)\mathds{P}(E_{i})=o(1).

In view of (
4.35
), by a union bound this implies

(4.37)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\exists\,s\leq t\ \text{s.t.}\ e_{s^{-}}(u)\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\,,\,\beta_{s}(u)=1\right)=o(1).

To prove (
4.36
), observe that, while in
\mathcal{C}_{1}
,
u
experiences attempts to average with other vertices at a rate
n-m
, and that at any attempt one has the following options:

(1)

u
remains in
\mathcal{C}_{1}
with probability
q_{0}\leq 1/2
;

(2)

u
moves to
\dagger
because of a
\beta
-event with probability
q_{1}\leq 2^{H}/(n-m)\eqqcolon p=O(2^{-\log^{1/3}n})
, since at any given time there are at most
2^{H}
vertices with positive mass;

(3)

u
moves to
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, or to
\dagger
because of an
\alpha
-event, with probability
1-q_{0}-q_{1}\geq\frac{1}{3}
.

In particular, the overall number of attempts before moving to
\mathcal{C}_{2}
or to
\dagger
is dominated by a geometric random variable with parameter
1/2
.
Thus, a union bound shows that
\mathds{P}(E_{i})\leq 6p=O(2^{-\log^{1/3}n})
, which proves (
4.36
).

In order to prove (
4.33
), we are left with proving

(4.38)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\exists\,s\leq t\ \text{s.t.}\ e_{s^{-}}(u)\in\mathcal{C}_{2}\,,\,\beta_{s}(u)=1\right)=o(1).

To this end, observe that, while in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
,
u
experiences attempts to average with other vertices at a rate
m
, and thus by the argument in (
4.35
) we may assume that there are at most
\log^{2}(n)
such attempts up to time
t
. Thus, letting
\Xi=\{0<\xi_{1}<\xi_{2}<\dots\}
denote the set of random times at which these attempts occur, it suffices to show that uniformly in
s\leq t
,

(4.39)

\displaystyle\log^{2}(n)\,\mathds{P}\left(e_{s^{-}}(u)\in\mathcal{C}_{2}\,,\,\beta_{s}(u)=1\,|\,s\in\Xi\right)=o(1).

To prove this, suppose
s\in\Xi
, say
s=\xi_{i}
for some
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
The event
e_{s^{-}}(u)\in\mathcal{C}_{2}\,,\,\beta_{s}(u)=1
implies that the attempt involves
u
and a vertex
x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
with
w_{s}(x)>0
. Thus

(4.40)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(e_{s^{-}}(u)\in\mathcal{C}_{2}\,,\,\beta_{s}(u)=1\,|\,s\in\Xi\right)

(4.41)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}\mathds{P}\left(\text{edge }(e_{s^{-}}(u),x)\text{ rings at time $s$}\,,\;w_{s^{-}}(x)>0\,|\,s\in\Xi\right)

(4.42)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}\mathds{P}\left(w_{s^{-}}(x)>0\,|\,s\in\Xi\,,\;\text{edge }(e_{s^{-}}(u),x)\text{ rings at time $s$}\,\right).

For any
a>0
, the event
w_{s^{-}}(x)>0
implies that either
x
has received mass within the time interval
[s-a,s]
, or
x
has received no mass and experienced less than
H
updates within the time interval
[s-a,s]
. Therefore,

(4.43)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(e_{s^{-}}(u)\in\mathcal{C}_{2}\,,\,\beta_{s}(u)=1\,|\,s\in\Xi\right)

(4.44)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\max_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}\mathds{P}\left(w_{s^{-}}(x)>0\,|\,s\in\Xi\,,\;\text{edge }(e_{s^{-}}(u),x)\text{ rings at time $s$}\right)

(4.45)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\max_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}\mathds{P}\left(A_{x}\right)+\max_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}\mathds{P}\left(B_{x}\right)\,,

where
A_{x}
is the event that
x
received mass within the time interval
[s^{-}-a,s^{-}]
, and
B_{x}
is the event that
x
experienced less than
H
updates within the time interval
[s^{-}-a,s^{-}]
.
Recalling that there are at most
2^{H}
vertices with positive mass, one has

(4.46)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(A_{x}\right)\leq 2^{H}a\,.

On the other hand, the probability of less than
H
updates at
x
in

is bounded by the probability that a Poisson random variable with parameter
(n-m)a\geq na/2
is less or equal than
H
.
Thus,

(4.47)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(B_{x}\right)\leq\left(na/2\right)^{H+1}e^{-na/2}\,.

We can take

so that
\mathds{P}\left(A_{x}\right)=o(1)
and
\mathds{P}\left(B_{x}\right)\leq 2^{\log^{2}(n)}e^{-2^{\log^{1/4}(n)}}=o(1)
. This ends the proof of (
4.33
).

To conclude the proof of the lemma we are left with estimating
\alpha_{t}(u)
, that is the number of good-scenario splittings involving the chunk labeled
u
up to time
t
.
We suppose
e_{0}(u)\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
; the proof for the case
e_{0}(u)\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
is analogous.
We
proceed as follows:

(1)

let
(Z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be i.i.d.
{\rm Geom}(1/2)
; each
Z_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
indicates the number of attempts needed for
u

to succeed in moving to the other block;

(2)

given
(Z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, let
(Y_{i,j})_{j=1}^{Z_{i}}
be a family of independent Exponential r.v.’s, with

(4.48)

Y_{i,j}\sim\begin{cases}{\rm Exp}(n-m)&\text{if }i\text{ odd}\\
{\rm Exp}(m)&\text{if }i\text{ even}\ ;\end{cases}

the r.v.
\sum_{j=1}^{Z_{i}}Y_{i,j}
represents the time
u
spends during the
i
-th visit to a block before moving to the other block.

Note that the random variable

(4.49)

K_{t}\coloneqq\sup\left\{k\geq 1\>:\>\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{Z_{i}}Y_{i,j}\leq t\right\}\>

represents the number of switchings from a block to the other. Therefore, if
\beta_{t}(u)=0
,

(4.50)

(H+1)\wedge\sum_{i=1}^{K_{t}}Z_{i}\leq\alpha_{t}(u)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K_{t}+1}Z_{i},

where the
+1
term in the second summation bounds the number of splittings that
u
experiences in the time intercurring from its last change of block.

On the one hand, since for every
i\geq 1

\mathds{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{Z_{i}}Y_{i,j}+\sum_{j=1}^{Z_{i+1}}Y_{i,j}\right]=\frac{2}{\gamma}\ ,

by the CLT, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\bar{\zeta}=\bar{\zeta}(\varepsilon)>0
such that, for all
\zeta>\bar{\zeta}
and for all
n
large enough

(4.51)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\gamma t+\zeta\sqrt{\log n}}\sum_{j=1}^{Z_{i}}Y_{i,j}>t\,\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon\,.

Note that
\bar{\zeta}
can be chosen independently of the constant
c
in the definition of
t=t^{-c}_{\rm mix}
.
From (
4.51
) it follows that

(4.52)

\mathds{P}\left(K_{t}+1<\gamma t+\zeta\sqrt{\log n}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon\ .

Hence,

(4.53)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\alpha_{t}(u)\leq H\,,\,\beta_{t}(u)=0\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathds{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K_{t}+1}Z_{i}\leq H\,,\,\beta_{t}(u)=0\right)

(4.54)

\displaystyle\geq\mathds{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K_{t}+1}Z_{i}\leq H,\>K_{t}+1\leq\gamma t+\zeta\sqrt{\log n}\right)-\varepsilon\,.

To finish the proof, observe that

(4.55)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\gamma t+\zeta\sqrt{\log n}}Z_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\log_{2}n-2\left(c-\zeta\right)\sqrt{\log n}\,.

Thus,
by choosing
c=c(\zeta,\varepsilon)>0
large enough, another application of the CLT shows that (
4.53
) is at least
1-2\varepsilon
.

For what concerns the lower bound on
\alpha_{t}(u)
, we proceed in a similar fashion. By the CLT there exists some
\bar{\xi}(\varepsilon)>0
such that for all
\xi>\bar{\xi}(\varepsilon)

(4.56)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\gamma t-\xi\sqrt{\log n}}\sum_{j=1}^{Z_{i}}Y_{i,j}\leq t\,\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon\ ,\qquad\mathds{P}\left(K_{t}>\gamma t-\xi\sqrt{\log n}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon\ .

Therefore, for every
\varepsilon>0
and
\xi>\bar{\xi}(\varepsilon)>0
,

(4.57)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\alpha_{t}(u)>\log_{2}n-b\sqrt{\log n}\,,\,\beta_{t}(u)=0\right)

(4.58)

\displaystyle\geq\mathds{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K_{t}}Z_{i}>\log_{2}n-b\sqrt{\log n},\>\beta_{t}(u)=0\right)

(4.59)

\displaystyle\geq\mathds{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K_{t}}Z_{i}>\log_{2}n-b\sqrt{\log n},\>K_{t}>\gamma t-\xi\sqrt{\log n}\right)-\varepsilon\geq 1-2\varepsilon\ ,

where the last step follows by

(4.60)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\gamma t-\xi\sqrt{\log n}}Z_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\log_{2}n-2\left(\xi+c\right)\sqrt{\log n}\ ,

and the CLT by choosing
b=b(\varepsilon,c,\xi)>0
large enough.
This completes the proof of Lemma
4.1
.
∎

5.
L^{2}
-mixing on complete bipartite graphs

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.3
. We keep the same notation as
at the beginning of Section
4
; in particular,
1\leq m\leq n/2
, and
|\mathcal{C}_{1}|=m
,
|\mathcal{C}_{2}|=n-m
. Furthermore, we remark that, in this context,
L^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm RW}}
of
{\rm RW}(G)
reads as

(5.1)

L^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm RW}}\psi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y\in V}\big(\psi(y)-\psi(x)\big)\left({\bf 1}_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}{\bf 1}_{y\in\mathcal{C}_{2}}+{\bf 1}_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{2}}{\bf 1}_{y\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}\right)\ ,\qquad x\in V\ ,\ \psi:V\to\mathbb{R}\ ,

from which it is straightforward to check that

(5.2)

t_{\rm rel}=\frac{2}{m}\ .

Moreover, we remark that

(5.3)

\text{the worst-case initial condition is $\xi={\bf 1}_{x_{0}}$ with $x_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{2}$}\ .

Indeed, observe that:

•

starting with all the mass at a single site
x_{0}
is worst-case by Proposition
2.6
, and the dependence on
x_{0}
is only through the subset
\mathcal{C}_{i}
,
i=1,2
, such that
x_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{i}
;

•

starting from
\eta={\bf 1}_{x_{0}}
,
x\in V
, after the first effective update for
{\rm Avg}(G)
, the next configuration is
\frac{1}{2}{\bf 1}_{x_{0}}+\frac{1}{2}{\bf 1}_{y}
, for some
y\in V
in
\mathcal{C}_{i}\not\ni x_{0}
; further, from that time on, mixing will not depend on neither
x_{0}
nor
y
;

•

letting
\tau_{i}
denote the random time of the first effective update when
x_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{i}
,

(5.4)

\displaystyle\tau_{i}\sim\begin{cases}{\rm Exp}(n-m)&\text{if}\ i=1\\
{\rm Exp}(m)&\text{if}\ i=2\ ;\end{cases}

Hence, since
n-m\geq m
, such times can be coupled such that, a.s.,
\tau_{1}\leq\tau_{2}
.

Before entering the details of the proof of the theorem (Sect.
5.2
below), let us first discuss the particularly relevant case of the star graph, i.e.,
G=K_{m,n-m}
with
m=1
.

5.1.
A motivating example: the star graph

Set
m=1
, and let
x_{*}
denote the unique element in
\mathcal{C}_{1}
(i.e., the center of the star). As pointed out in Section
2.6.2
, the
L^{2}
-mixing must occur between times
\frac{t_{\rm rel}}{2}\log n
and
t_{\rm rel}\log n
. As shown in the following corollary, which specializes Theorem
1.3
to the setting
m=1
, the
L^{2}
-cutoff occurs strictly in between these two extremes, namely around times
\frac{3}{4}t_{\rm rel}\log n
.

Corollary 5.1

(
L^{2}
-cutoff on the star graph)
.

Let
T(a):=\frac{3}{4}t_{\rm rel}\left(\log n+a\right)
,
a\in\mathbb{R}
. Then,

(5.5)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\xi\in\Delta(V)}\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{T(a)}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=e^{-a}\ ,\qquad a\in\mathbb{R}\ .

Let us briefly comment over this result, with the aim of illustrating that the arguments used to derive Proposition
2.3
, while they provide a sharp
L^{2}
-estimate for the case of the complete graph
K_{n}
, they do not prove to be useful in the context of the star graph.

A simple computation shows that

(5.6)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\mathcal{E}^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm RW}\left(\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}\right)\right]

(5.7)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{t_{\rm rel}}\left(\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\mathds{E}_{\xi}\left[\left(\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}(x_{*})-1\right)^{2}\right]\right),

for all
\xi\in\Delta(V)
and
t\geq 0
.
By integrating over time and setting
\xi={\bf 1}_{x}
for some
x\neq x_{*}
,

(5.8)

\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{x}}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=e^{-\frac{t}{t_{\rm rel}}}\left\|\frac{{\bf 1}_{x}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}-e^{-\frac{t}{t_{\rm rel}}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{e^{\frac{s}{t_{\rm rel}}}}{t_{\rm rel}}\,\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{x}}\left[\left(\frac{\eta_{s}}{\pi}(x_{*})-1\right)^{2}\right]{\rm d}s\ .

Since
\left\|\frac{{\bf 1}_{x}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\approx n
, the first term on the right-hand side above is divergent for
t=(1-\delta)t_{\rm rel}\log n
,
\delta\in(0,1)
, and drops down to order
\Theta(1)
only if
t=t_{\rm rel}\left(\log n+a\right)
. Therefore,
L^{2}
-mixing of
{\rm Avg}(G)
at the strictly smaller time
T(a)=\frac{3}{4}t_{\rm rel}\left(\log n+a\right)
must be due to cancellations of diverging terms between first and second terms on the right-hand side of (
5.8
).
In particular, in view of the duality relation in (
2.11
), controlling this latter term boils down to estimating the transition probabilities of
{\rm CRW}(G)
in the time interval
[0,T(a)]
. This approach proves to be difficult and, instead of dealing with
{\rm CRW}(G)
, one might be tempted to bound the last term in (
5.8
) by relying on the duality with the simpler process
{\rm RW}(G)
. In order to do so, one must use Jensen inequality, obtaining

(5.9)

e^{-\frac{t}{t_{\rm rel}}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{e^{\frac{s}{t_{\rm rel}}}}{t_{\rm rel}}\,\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{x}}\left[\left(\frac{\eta_{s}}{\pi}(x_{*})-1\right)^{2}\right]{\rm d}s\geq e^{-\frac{t}{t_{\rm rel}}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{e^{\frac{s}{t_{\rm rel}}}}{t_{\rm rel}}\left(\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{x}}\left[\frac{\eta_{s}}{\pi}-1\right]\right)^{2}{\rm d}s\ .

Nonetheless, it turns out that the right-hand side of (
5.9
) is of order
O(1)
for all
t\geq 0
, implying that (
5.8
) combined with the lower bound in (
5.9
) is too weak to deduce the desired control.

In the proof of Theorem
1.3
we show how to handle the transition probabilities of
{\rm CRW}(G)
around times

to derive first order estimates on the

-distance without relying directly on (
5.8
).

Proof of Theorem
1.3

We now turn to the proof of Theorem

Remark 5.3

(

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Fix
x_{0}\in V
and
t>0
; then, by duality in (

),

(5.15)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{x_{0}}}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=n\sum_{x\in V}\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{x_{0}}}\left[\left(\eta_{t}(x)\right)^{2}\right]-1\\
&=n\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}{\mathbf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm CRW}}_{x_{0},x_{0}}\left(X_{t}=x,Y_{t}=x\right)+n\sum_{y\in\mathcal{C}_{2}}{\mathbf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm CRW}}_{x_{0},x_{0}}\left(X_{t}=y,Y_{t}=y\right)-1\ .\end{aligned}

In what follows, we analyze the above transition probabilities for
{\rm CRW}(G)
by lumping some states and considering the following auxiliary Markov chain with five states

(5.16)

\displaystyle 0_{1}

\displaystyle:=\{(x,x):x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\}

(5.17)

\displaystyle 0_{2}

\displaystyle:=\{(y,y):y\in\mathcal{C}_{2}\}

(5.18)

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle:=\{(x,y)\cup(y,x):x\in\mathcal{C}_{1},y\in\mathcal{C}_{2}\}

(5.19)

\displaystyle 2_{1}

\displaystyle:=\{(x,x^{\prime}):x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{1},x\neq x^{\prime}\}

(5.20)

\displaystyle 2_{2}

\displaystyle:=\{(y,y^{\prime}):y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{2},y\neq y^{\prime}\}\ ,

and corresponding
5\times 5
transition rate matrix

(5.21)

\displaystyle Q=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{3(n-m)}{4}&\frac{n-m}{4}&\frac{n-m}{2}&0&0\\
\frac{m}{4}&-\frac{3m}{4}&\frac{m}{2}&0&0\\
\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{4}&-\frac{n-1}{2}&\frac{m-1}{2}&\frac{n-m-1}{2}\\
0&0&n-m&-(n-m)&0\\
0&0&m&0&-m\end{pmatrix}\ .

and reversible measure
\mu:\{0_{1},0_{2},1,2_{1},2_{2}\}\to[0,1]
given by

(5.22)

\displaystyle\mu=n^{-2}\begin{pmatrix}m&n-m&2m(n-m)&m(m-1)&(n-m)(n-m-1)\end{pmatrix}\ .

This lumping allows us to rewrite (
5.15
) as follows:

(5.23)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{x_{0}}}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=n\,e^{tQ}(0_{i},0_{1})+n\,e^{tQ}(0_{i},0_{2})-1\ ,\qquad x_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{i}\ ,\ i\in\{1,2\}\ .

Let
\{(\rho_{j},\phi_{j})\}_{j=0}^{4}
denote the eigenvalue-eigenfunction pairs of
-Q
, with
(\rho_{0}=0,\phi_{0}\equiv 1)
, and
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{4}
orthonormal basis in

; hence, we have

(5.24)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathds{E}_{{\bf 1}_{x_{0}}}\left[\left\|\frac{\eta_{t}}{\pi}-1\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=n\,e^{tQ}(0_{i},0_{1})+n\,e^{tQ}(0_{i},0_{2})-1\\
&\qquad=n\,\mu(0_{1})\sum_{j=0}^{4}e^{-\rho_{j}t}\phi_{j}(0_{i})\,\phi_{j}(0_{1})+n\,\mu(0_{2})\sum_{j=0}^{4}e^{-\rho_{j}t}\phi_{j}(0_{i})\,\phi_{j}(0_{2})-1\\
&\qquad=\sum_{j=1}^{4}e^{-\rho_{j}t}\phi_{j}(0_{i})\left(\frac{m}{n}\phi_{j}(0_{1})+\frac{n-m}{n}\phi_{j}(0_{2})\right)\ ,\qquad x_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{i}\ ,\ i\in\{1,2\}\ .\end{aligned}

Furthermore, the following eigenpair
(\rho_{2},\phi_{2})
for
-Q
is explicit:

(5.25)

\displaystyle\rho_{2}=\frac{n}{2}\ ,\qquad\phi_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-m}}\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{n-m}{m}&1&\frac{n-2m}{2m}&-\frac{n-m}{m}&1\end{pmatrix}\ .

Although the remaining eigenvalues
\rho_{1},\rho_{3},\rho_{4}
and eigenfunctions
\phi_{1},\phi_{3},\phi_{4}
of
-Q
are, in general, not explicit, we now provide sharp asymptotic estimates which will yield the desired claim.

Recall from (

(2)

Proof of (

Recall that
\nu(j):=2^{-d}\binom{d}{j}
,
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
, and that we want to prove

(A.1)

\displaystyle\limsup_{d\to\infty}\sup_{1\leq M\leq d/2}\left(\lambda^{P}_{M,0}\right)^{-1}\log\left(\frac{1}{\nu([0,M))}\right)<\infty\ .

We divide the proof into two steps.

A.1.
Discrete weighted Hardy’s inequality

We start by estimating
\lambda_{M,0}^{P}
by means of a discrete weighted Hardy’s inequality (see, e.g.,
[
Mic99
]
).

Lemma A.1
.

For every
M\in\{1,\ldots,d/2\}
, define

(A.2)

\displaystyle C_{M}:=\max_{0\leq k\leq M-1}\nu([0,k])\sum_{j=k}^{M-1}\frac{1}{\nu(j)(d-j)}\ .

Then,

(A.3)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4C_{M}}\leq\lambda_{M,0}^{P}\leq\frac{1}{C_{M}}\ .

Proof.

Recall that
\lambda_{M,0}^{P}>0
is the largest value
\lambda>0
satisfying

(A.4)

\displaystyle\lambda\,\nu(f^{2})\leq\mathcal{E}_{P}(f)\ ,\qquad f\in\mathcal{F}_{M-1}:=\left\{f:\{0,1,\ldots,d\}\to\mathbb{R}:f=0\ \text{on}\ [M,d]\right\}\ .

We now apply the following version of weighted Hardy’s inequality (e.g.,
[
Mic99
]
or
[
CSC13
, Prop. A.3]
): for all positive measures
\pi,\mu
on
\{0,1,\ldots,M-1\}
, define

(A.5)

\displaystyle\varLambda=\varLambda_{\pi,\mu}:=\frac{1}{4}\left(\max_{0\leq k\leq M-1}\pi([0,k])\sum_{j=k}^{M-1}\frac{1}{\mu(j)}\right)^{-1}\ ;

then

(A.6)

\displaystyle\varLambda\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\left(\sum_{j=k}^{M-1}g(j)\right)^{2}\pi(k)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\left(g(k)\right)^{2}\mu(k)\ ,\qquad g:\{0,1,\ldots,M-1\}\to\mathbb{R}\ .

Applying this to
\pi(k):=\nu(k)
,
\mu(k):=\nu(k)(d-k)
, we get, setting, for every
f\in\mathcal{F}_{M-1}
,
g(k):=f(k)-f(k+1)
,

(A.7)

4\varLambda\geq\lambda_{M,0}^{P}\geq\varLambda\ .

This concludes the proof of the lemma.

In view of Lemma
