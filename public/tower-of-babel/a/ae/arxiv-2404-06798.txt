MedRG: Medical Report Grounding with Multi-modal Large Language Model
Ke Zou, Yang Bai, Zhihao Chen, Yang Zhou, Yidi Chen, Kai Ren, Meng Wang, Xuedong Yuan 🖂, Xiaojing Shen, Huazhu Fu 🖂

Abstract
Medical Report Grounding is pivotal in identifying the most relevant regions in medical images based on a given phrase query, a critical aspect in medical image analysis and radiological diagnosis. However, prevailing visual grounding approaches necessitate the manual extraction of key phrases from medical reports, imposing substantial burdens on both system efficiency and physicians. In this paper, we introduce a novel framework— Med ical R eport G rounding ( MedRG )—an end-to-end solution for utilizing a multi-modal Large Language Model to predict key phrase by incorporating a unique token, < < 𝙱𝙾𝚇 \mathtt{BOX} > > , into the vocabulary to serve as an embedding for unlocking detection capabilities. Subsequently, the vision encoder-decoder jointly decodes the hidden embedding and the input medical image, generating the corresponding grounding box. The experimental results validate the effectiveness of MedRG, surpassing the performance of the existing state-of-the-art medical phrase grounding methods. This study represents a pioneering exploration of the medical report grounding task, marking the first-ever endeavor in this domain.

Introduction

Medical report grounding aims to establish connections between medical reports and regions of interest (ROI) in medical images. This process plays a crucial role in enhancing the comprehension of semantic information within medical reports, enabling the identification of grounding boxes associated with phrases in medical images. Such a capability is pivotal for interpretable medical diagnosis and radiology analysis. In this paper, we present a pioneering approach: an end-to-end, large language model-empowered medical report grounding framework. Specifically, as shown in Fig.
1
, taking X-ray as an example, given an image and its corresponding medical report, our framework system will output the key phrases in the report and their corresponding grounding boxes. This innovative approach significantly boosts the efficiency of medical professionals, facilitating improved communication between patients and clinicians.

Method

2.1
Problem definition

Medical report grounding aims to extract crucial diagnostic information, resembling the expertise of an X-ray specialist, from an original medical report
{{\bf{x}}_{t}}
and an X-ray image
{{\bf{x}}_{i}}
. Specifically, the objective is to identify the medical phrase
{{\bf{y}}_{p}}
and its corresponding detection grounding box
{{\bf{y}}_{b}}
within the X-ray image. This process can be summarized as follows:

\left({{{\bf{y}}_{p}},{{\bf{y}}_{b}}}\right)=\mathcal{F}\left({{{\bf{x}}_{t}},{{\bf{x}}_{i}}}\right),

(1)

where
{\bf{y}}_{b}=(x,y,w,h)
, and
(x,y)
denote the coordinates of the bounding box in the image, while
(w,h)
correspond to its height and width, respectively. Furthermore,
\mathcal{F}\left(\cdot\right)
represents the mapping function that relates the input variables to the output. While Chen et al.
[
1
]
proposed medical report grounding, their approach necessitates manual preprocessing of input phrases. Leveraging the reasoning capabilities of the large vision-language model (VLM), our method automatically infers phrases from original medical reports and conducts the corresponding grounding box. This advancement significantly enhances the efficiency of medical personnel.

2.2
Architecture

1) Medical report to medical phrase.
Our primary objective is to generate medical phrases as input for predicting grounding boxes. However, being restricted solely to medical phrases may lead to unnecessarily complicating the framework as in
[
1
]
. The currently booming large-scale visual language models can predict text by taking images and text as output. Remarkably, building upon the insights from
[
9
]
, we leverage a large visual language model to generate phrases and utilize their embedding as inputs for predicting grounding boxes, thereby enhancing the elegance of the framework. Consequently, given an X-ray image
{{\bf{x}}_{i}}
and a medical report
{{\bf{x}}_{t}}
, we first obtain the objective function as follows:

\left\{{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{{{\bf{e}}_{box}}={\mathcal{F}_{m}}\left({{{\bf{x}}_{t}},{{\bf{x}}_{i}}}\right)}\\
{{{\bf{y}}_{p}}=f\left({{{\bf{e}}_{box}}}\right)}\end{array}}\right.,

(2)

where
{\mathcal{F}_{m}}
is the pretrained LLaVA model with a LORA adaptor.
f\left(\cdot\right)
represents the MLP projection layer to predict phrase
{{\bf{y}}_{p}}
. It is noteworthy that in Eq.
2
, we employ the embedding
{\bf{e_{box}}}
as a detection paradigm, integrating detection capabilities into the VLM. To facilitate this, we expand the vocabulary of the original language model by a new token, namely
<
\mathtt{BOX}
>
, which serves as a marker for requesting detection box outputs.

2) Grounding Box Decoder.
At the same time, to enhance the accuracy of the detected grounding box, we adopt the widely-used SAM framework
[
8
]
for prediction. Specifically, we employ the visual backbone
{\mathcal{F}_{enc}}
to extract visual feature embedding
{{\bf{z}}_{enc}}
. The embedded
{\bf{e_{box}}}
is then combined with the visual features and fed into the decoder
{\mathcal{F}_{dec}}
. Notably,
{\bf{e_{box}}}
serves as a contextual cue. The process can be described as follows:

\left\{{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{{{\bf{z}}_{enc}}={{\cal F}_{enc}}\left({{{\bf{x}}_{i}}}\right)}\\
{{{\bf{z}}_{dec}}={{\cal F}_{dec}}\left({{{\bf{z}}_{enc}},{{\bf{e}}_{box}}}\right)}\end{array}}\right..

(3)

3) Prediction with Box Decoder.
In the end, we obtain box-shaped predictions by modifying the last layer of the original SAM
[
8
]
. Specifically, we employ the MLP layer
f\left(\cdot\right)
to obtain grounding box prediction in a single forward. It can be further expressed as:

{{\bf{y}}_{b}}{\rm{=}}f\left({{{\bf{z}}_{dec}}}\right).

(4)

2.3
Training

The proposed model is designed to be trained end-to-end and consists of two key components: phrase generation and detection box prediction.

Phrase generation.
To enhance the ability of the visual language model to summarize medical phrases in medical reports effectively, we employ an auto-regressive cross-entropy loss. This loss function enables the model to learn the correlation between the predicted phrase and the actual phrase. Concretely, during the training process, given the ground truth of phrase
{{{\bf{\hat{y}}}}_{p}}
, the formulation of the training process is as follows:

{\mathcal{L}_{p}}{\rm{=}}{\mathcal{L}_{CE}}\left({{{{\bf{\hat{y}}}}_{p}},{{\bf{y}}_{p}}}\right),

(5)

Grounding box prediction.
Moreover, to generate accurate grounding boxes using the grounding box encoder-decoder, we utilize two loss functions: smooth L1 loss
[
5
]
and generalized intersection over union (GIoU) loss
[
17
]
. These loss functions serve as guidance for improved box prediction. Specifically, during the optimization process, given the ground truth of box
{{{\bf{\hat{y}}}}_{b}}
, the formulation can be expressed as follows:

{{\cal L}_{b}}{\rm{=}}{{\cal L}_{{l_{1}}}}\left({{{{\bf{\hat{y}}}}_{b}},{{\bf{y}}_{b}}}\right)+{{\cal L}_{giou}}\left({{{{\bf{\hat{y}}}}_{b}},{{{\bf{y}}}_{b}}}\right).

(6)

Total objectives.
Finally, the total target training function can be summarized as the summation of the aforementioned two components:

{{\cal L}_{all}}={{\cal L}_{p}}+{{\cal L}_{b}}.

(7)

3
Experiments

3.1
Experimental Settings

Benchmark.
Quantitative assessments and benchmarks are crucial for grounding tasks in inferential medical reporting. To ensure reliable evaluation, we curated a benchmark dataset by collecting corresponding original reports from the MIMIC-CXR dataset
[
7
]
. It comprises 1153 Image-Phrase-BBox triplet samples. To ensure that each sample consists of an X-ray image, medical report, phrase query, and bounding box quadruple, we performed preprocessing on the MS-CXR dataset. We named it as MRG-MS-CXR data, which encompassed diverse X-ray report diagnostic scenarios, as depicted in Fig.
1

b
. Its diagnostic phrases mainly include cardiomegaly, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, etc. The MRG-MS-CXR benchmark comprises a total of 835 pairs of reports, images, grounding boxes, and phrases from 867 patients. Following the
[
1
]
, we split the dataset into train-validation-test sets in a ratio of 7:1:2 based on the patients, comprising 585, 83, and 167 pairs.

Evaluation Metrics.
To evaluate the effectiveness of the MRG task, we employ two types of evaluation metrics to measure the accuracy of phrase prediction and bounding box estimation. We adopt three commonly used indicators from previous natural language prediction methods, namely BLEU, ROUGE_L, and CIDEr, to assess the quality of phrase predictions. Additionally, we utilize the widely-used metric mIoU (mean Intersection over Union) in bounding box prediction for a comprehensive comparison. Furthermore, we introduce the accuracy (Acc) of bounding box prediction as an evaluation metric. Specifically, we consider a predicted bounding box as a positive sample if its mIoU with the ground truth bounding box exceeds certain thresholds (0.1, 0.3, and 0.5). We represent these thresholds as AP10, AP30, and AP50, respectively.

Baselines.
Medical and general visual grounding methods, such as MedRPG
[
1
]
, TransVG
[
3
]
, RefTR
[
14
]
, VGTR
[
4
]
and SeqTR
[
22
]
are compared with our model. All the settings are shared the same with our model. Since these methods are fed with the preprocessed medical phrases as input, we use GPT-4
[
15
]
and LLaMA
[
19
]
for extracting medical phrases from the medical reports in the testing time. During training for a fair comparison, we employed their official implementations for all baselines. In addition, all comparison methods select the optimal model on the validation set and share the same experimental Settings.

Implementation Details.
We conducted the following experiments on the PyTorch platform with an NVIDIA 80G A100. The training scripts are based on deepspeed engine
[
16
]
. We trained our model by the AdamW
[
12
]
optimizer with a learning rate of 0.00005. Besides, the WarmupDecayLR strategy was employed for the learning rate scheduler and its iterations are set to 100. The gradient accumulation step and batch size are set to 10 and 2, respectively. During training, the best checkpoints on the validation dataset of baselines and our model were reported with different random seeds.

Experimental Results

Tab.

summarizes the experimental results of our proposed method MedRG and other methods on our pre-processed dataset MRG-MS-CXR. We will compare and analyze the results from two aspects:

Grounding Box Prediction.
The left half of Table 1 shows a comparison of ground box predictions. It should be noted that the existing visual grounding method is not suitable for the end-to-end medical report grounding task because it requires the extraction of key medical phrases. Therefore, we used different language models GPT-4 and LLaMA to extract phrases from medical reports for these methods. As shown in Tab.

\textgreater
0.3) is close to 80%, and the AP10 (mIOU
\textgreater
0.1) is more than 90%. Overall, our methods improved the AP50 and mIOU indexes by 33.53% and 24.33% compared with the above methods. This is mainly due to two aspects, on the one hand, the multi-modal LLM extracts the key phrases and embeds them as new tokens in the learning of the visual decoder. The visual decoder, on the other hand, extracts key positioning information to predict the grounding box corresponding to the phrase.

Phrase Extraction.
The right half of Tab.
1
shows the predictions for key phrases. As can be seen from Tab.
1
, the ability of our method to extract key phrases is superior to that of untrained GPT-4 and LLaMA. From the three indexes of BLEU, ROUGE_L, and CIDEr, it has obvious performance improvement. This is attributed to the introduction of an end-to-end multi-modal LLM phrase prediction framework. It fine-tuned LORA with a multi-modal LLM based on an existing dataset for key phrase prediction in medical reports.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our work introduces a new
Med
ical
R
eport
G
rounding (MedRG) framework for better medical image analysis and radiological diagnosis. It aims to extract phrases automatedly and localize the relevant regions in the medical image. By harnessing a multi-modal large language model, MedRG extends the vocabulary through the introduction of a
<
\mathtt{BOX}
>
token, advocating the embedding-as-detection paradigm to unlock detection capabilities. The experimental results show that our proposed MedRG method achieves significantly improved performance compared to current SOTA medical phrase grounding methods. This study not only contributes to advancing multi-modality medical image analysis but also marks a pivotal step in addressing the limitations of existing radiological diagnosis approaches.
