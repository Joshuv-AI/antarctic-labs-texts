Meromorphic Solution of the Degenerate Third Painlevé Equation Vanishing at the Origin
Alexander V. KITAEV

Abstract
We prove that there exists the unique odd meromorphic solution of dP3, u ⁡ ( τ ) u(\tau) such that u ⁡ ( 0 ) = 0 u(0)=0 , and study some of its properties, mainly: the coefficients of its Taylor expansion at the origin and asymptotic behaviour as τ → + ∞ \tau\to+\infty .

Introduction

The degenerate third Painlevé equation can be written in the following form,

\displaystyle u^{\prime\prime}(\tau)=\frac{(u^{\prime}(\tau))^{2}}{u(\tau)}-\frac{u^{\prime}(\tau)}{\tau}+\frac{1}{\tau}\big({-}8\epsilon u^{2}(\tau)+2ab\big)+\frac{b^{2}}{u(\tau)},

where
\epsilon,a,b\in\mathbb{C}

\epsilon b=0

Otherwise,

\epsilon
and

\mathbb{C}{\setminus}0

by rescaling variables
u
and
\tau

1.1

In our previous works with A.H. Vartanian (see
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
) we used both parameters

\epsilon
and
b
because asymptotic results crucially depend on
\arg\tau
, so that it is convenient to identify
suitable case of equation (
1.1
) without making the rescaling, because in many cases it changes
\arg\tau
.
Here, in Sections
2
–
6
I discuss explicit results and it is more
convenient, to simplify corresponding expressions, to put

\displaystyle\epsilon=+1.

(1.2)

Moreover, in these sections we keep “normalization” condition

1.1
)
(with
b\in\mathbb{R}{\setminus}0

\displaystyle u_{\epsilon=-1}(\tau)=iu_{\epsilon=+1}(i\tau).

Equation (

In this paper we consider a particular solution which is holomorphic and vanishing at

In Section

My attention to this solution was attracted by B.I. Suleimanov. In

\displaystyle\epsilon=\pm 1,\qquad a=\frac{i}{2},\qquad b=64/k^{3},

Suleimanov’s study has some physical motivation: self-focusing phenomenon for the one-dimensional
propagation of the light in unstable quasi gaseous media. He argues that under the assumption of small dispersion
in the case when the wave propagation can be described by the integrable nonlinear Schrödinger equation the
leading term of the intensity of a signal in the neighborhood of the focusing points under a number of scaling
transformations is described by a special case of

-function. This is a new application of the higher
Painlevé functions, so that it would be interesting to see a more detailed explanation of these ideas.

In Section

numerators of the Taylor coefficients. It is written in a fully conjectural manner. Due to the appearance of

architecturally, can be divided by segments. Each segment consists of two parts: the first one resembles all the
previously built fence and the second part is a newly constructed one. The rules of the construction have periodic
properties described in the section.

In this section we assume that both parameters
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
. We begin with the following lemma which looks
special however, in fact, resembles the general case.

Lemma 2.1
.

If there exists a holomorphic solution of equation (
1.1
) for
a=b
, then for
4a^{2}+(2k+1)^{2}\neq 0
,

k\in\mathbb{Z}
, it is an odd function of
\tau

4a^{2}+(2k+1)^{2}=0
, then this solution depends on one complex parameter. This parameter
can be chosen such that this solution is odd. Moreover, for
a^{2}+k^{2}\neq 0
the odd solution is unique.

Proof.

The reader may start with the general form of the Taylor expansion vanishing at
\tau=0
, substitute it into
equation (
1.1
), and, equating the coefficients for the leading terms in
\tau
, prove that it can
be presented in the following form

\displaystyle u(\tau)=-\frac{\tau}{2}\left(1+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}b_{k}\tau^{2k}+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}\tau^{2k+1}\right),

(2.1)

where the coefficients
b_{k}
and
c_{k}
depend only on
a
. Substantially, here we have determined the leading coefficient of the expansion, follows from the cancelation of
the last two terms in equation (
1.1
) (
a=b
!), and separated odd and even parts of the solution.
Now, substituting expansion (
2.1
) into equation (
1.1
), taking the common denominator,
and equating zero the coefficients of powers
\tau^{2k}
, and
\tau^{2k+1}

we find the recurrence equations for the coefficients:

\displaystyle c_{0}\big(4a^{2}+1\big)=0,\qquad b_{1}\big(a^{2}+1\big)=1,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\big(4a^{2}+(2k+1)^{2}\big)c_{k}=C_{k}(c_{0},\ldots,c_{k-1},b_{1},\ldots,b_{k-1}),

(2.3)

\displaystyle\big(4a^{2}+(2k)^{2}\big)b_{k}=B_{k}(c_{0},\ldots,c_{k-1},b_{1},\ldots,b_{k-1}),

(2.4)

where
C_{k}
and
B_{k}
are polynomials in variables indicated above. Polynomials,

have one important property,
which do no have polynomials

, namely, each term of these polynomials consists of odd number of factors

c_{l}
and some factors

,

, where we put

equation (
2.3
) is obtained by equating zero coefficients of even powers of
\tau
and equation (
2.4
)
of the odd, respectively. The coefficients of the even powers of
\tau
are obtained with the help of the odd ones
by multiplication by

which comes from the denominator. As a result such coefficients always have odd
number of factors of parameters

with junior subscripts

Now it is easy to finish the proof. Assume

for all integer

2.2
) and equations (
2.3
), (
2.4
) imply that all coefficients

Thus the solution is odd. If for some integer

is valid, then, obviously,

for

, and

is a complex parameter. The coefficients

determined. In case we put

we obtain the odd solution. Finally, if for the odd solution

for all integers

the construction is unique, because of equations (
2.4

2.3

∎

Proposition 9.1
.

The function
u(\tau)
with
a<0
is bounded for
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover,
u(\tau)
is an odd
function and
u(\tau)>0
for
\tau>0
.

As follows from expansion (
2.19
) the solution possesses the following symmetries:

\displaystyle u(\tau)=-u(-\tau),\qquad u(\tau,a)=-iu(i\tau,-a).

(9.1)

Proof.

It is enough to prove boundedness of our solution for
\tau>0
. Expansion (
2.19
) implies (recall
a<0
)
u(\tau)>0
for
0<\tau<\delta
at least for some
rather small
\delta>0
. Assume that
\tau_{z}>0
is a zero of
u(\tau)
, then substituting the Taylor expansion
of
u(\tau)
at
\tau_{z}
with the leading term
A(\tau-\tau_{z})^{n}
with
n>0
into equation (
1.1
) one
proves that
n=1
and
b^{2}/A^{2}=-1
. Therefore, real solutions of equation (
1.1
) have no zeroes
on the real line. It means that our solution is positive on the positive semiaxis. Analogously, substituting
the Laurent expansion of
u(\tau)
at pole
\tau_{p}
with the leading term
A_{1}/(\tau-\tau_{p})^{m}
with
m>0

one finds
m=2
and
2\tau_{p}=-8A_{1}
, If we assume
\tau_{p}>0
we find that

u(\tau)\underset{\tau\to\infty}{\sim}-\tfrac{\tau_{p}}{4(\tau-\tau_{p})^{2}}<0
. Thus, our positive solution
does not have poles on positive semiaxis.
∎

Lemma 9.2
.

If a solution of equation (
1.1
) has positive real part for
\tau>0
, then it does not have poles for
\tau>0
.

Proof.

The Laurent expansion of solutions of equation (
1.1
) at the pole
\tau_{p}
reads

\displaystyle-\frac{\tau_{p}}{4(\tau-\tau_{p})^{2}}+O(1),\qquad\tau\to\tau_{p}.

∎

Since
\tau
and
\tau_{p}
are assumed real, then the imaginary part of any solution does not have
poles on the real axis except possibly
\tau_{p}=0
. Moreover, if we assume that
\tau_{p}>0
, then real
part of any solution with a pole is negative in some its neighborhood, however we assume that the real part is positive.

Proposition 9.3
.

For
\operatorname{Re}a<0
there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that for
|\operatorname{Im}a|<\varepsilon
and
\tau>0

\operatorname{Re}u(\tau)>0
.

Assume there is a sequence

\operatorname{Re}a_{n}\equiv\beta<0

\operatorname{Im}a_{n}\to 0
, such that
the functions

. If the sequence
\tau_{n}
has a bounded
subsequence, then it leads to immediate contradiction with Proposition

, since in this case

u(\tau_{0})=u(\tau_{0},0)=0

\tau_{0}
is a finite limiting point of the sequence

The case when the sequence
\tau_{n}
is unbounded also cannot happen. It follows from the general property of local
uniformness of asymptotics of the Painlevé equations. This property means the following: assume that an asymptotic
formula is valid for some open subset in the space of the monodromy parameters, as it happens for
asymptotics (
8.4
)–(
8.7
), which is valid in the open strip,
a=\beta+i\alpha

with
\beta<0
and
|\alpha|<1
. We consider any compact subset of this strip, for our purposes

\beta=\beta_{0}<0
and a segment

Then there exist
\Upsilon=\Upsilon(\beta,\epsilon)
such that the error estimate in equation (

from the above compact subset. Thus, if we choose the error estimate in (

\Upsilon

\tau_{n}>\Upsilon
cannot exist because
u(\tau)
is close to the first term in asymptotics (

values of
\tau>\Upsilon
. The following conjecture is the extension of Proposition
9.3
.

If
\operatorname{Re}a\leq 0

\operatorname{Re}u(\tau)>0
for

Remark 9.5
.

Solutions for the parameter
a
from Conjecture
9.4
have neither zeroes nor poles on
the positive semiaxis. To prove the absence of poles one can surely apply Zhou’s vanishing lemma
[
13
]
.
In our case the Riemann–Hilbert problem corresponding to the monodromy data defined in Section
7

can be formulated on the positive real semiaxis and the circle centered at
0
. The jump matrices are

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&s_{0}\\
s_{0}&1+s_{0}^{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad{\rm e}^{2\pi(a-i/2)\sigma_{3}},\qquad{\rm and}\qquad G

inside, outside, and on the circle, respectively. There are singular points at
zero and infinity with the proper behavior, which we do not discuss here.
The vanishing lemma implies that the jump matrices on the real axis are positively defined;
we get the following condition for the trace of the diagonal matrix

\displaystyle\cosh(2\pi(a-i/2))+\cosh(2\pi(\bar{a}+i/2))>0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\cos(2\pi\operatorname{Im}a)<0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{1}{4}<\operatorname{Im}a<\frac{3}{4},

and arbitrary
\operatorname{Re}a
. The trace of the nondiagonal matrix is
2+s_{0}^{2}
its positiveness
exactly coincide with the above condition on
a
. There is one more condition that demand
the vanishing lemma,
G^{\dagger}=G^{-1}
. I recall that matrix
G
(see the second line of equations in
system (
7.12
)), contains one free complex parameter, which is
related with the constant of integration in equation (
7.1
), so that for a given
u(\tau)

it can be chosen arbitrarily. If we choose it to satisfy the condition on matrix
G
we get

\operatorname{Im}a=\pm 1/2
with arbitrary
\operatorname{Re}a
.

Thus, in particular, the Suleimanov solution is regular on the positive semiaxis,
in fact, on the coordinate cross, because of the symmetries (
9.1
). Unfortunately, this
remark does not shed any light on the proof of Conjecture
9.4
.
