Adjoints of sums of m-accretive operators and applications to non-autonomous evolutionary equations
Rainer Picard, Sascha Trostorff, and Marcus Waurick

Abstract
We provide certain compatibility conditions for m-accretive operators such that the adjoint of the sum is given by the closure of the sum of the respective adjoint. We revisit the proof of well-posedness of the abstract class of partial differential-algebraic equations known as evolutionary equations. We show that the general mechanism provided here can be applied to establish well-posedness for non-autonomous evolutionary equations with L ∞ L_{\infty} -coefficients thus not only generalising known results but opening up new directions other methods such as evolution families have a hard time to come by.



Keywords
Evolutionary Equations, Non-autonomous Equations,
m-accretive Operators

Preliminaries

Throughout this section, let
H_{0},H_{1},H_{2}
be Hilbert spaces.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
T\colon\operatorname{dom}(T)\subseteq H_{0}\to H_{1}

be a closed linear operator and
B\colon H_{1}\to H_{2}
bounded
and linear. Then, if
B
is one-to-one and has closed range,
BT

is closed.

Proof.

The closed graph theorem yields that the adstriction
\widetilde{B}\colon H_{1}\to\operatorname{ran}(B)

of
B
is a continuously invertible operator. Next, let
(x_{n})_{n}

in
\operatorname{dom}(BT)
such that
x_{n}\to x
and
BTx_{n}\to y
in
H_{0}

and
H_{2}
as
n\to\infty
for some
x\in H_{0}
and
y\in H_{2}
.
Then, by the continuity of
(\widetilde{B})^{-1}
, we infer
Tx_{n}=\left(\widetilde{B}\right)^{-1}BTx_{n}\to\left(\widetilde{B}\right)^{-1}y

in
H_{1}
as
n\to\infty
. By the closedness of
T
, we obtain

x\in\operatorname{dom}(T)\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(BT)
and
Tx=\left(\widetilde{B}\right)^{-1}y.

Applying
\widetilde{B}
to both sides of the latter equality, we infer

y=BTx
as desired.
∎

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
T\colon\operatorname{dom}(T)\subseteq H_{0}\to H_{1}

be closed,
B\colon H_{2}\to H_{0}
be a bounded linear operator.
Then, as an identity of relations, we have

(TB)^{*}=\overline{B^{*}T^{*}}.

Moreover,
TB
is densely defined if and only if
B^{*}T^{*}
is
a closable operator. If,
B^{*}
is one-to-one and has closed range
and
T
is densely defined, then
B^{*}T^{*}
is closed. In particular,
in this case, we have
TB
is densely defined and

(TB)^{*}=B^{*}T^{*}.

Proof.

The first statement is a consequence of
[
12
, Theorem 2.3.4]
.
The stated equivalence follows from
(TB)^{*}=\overline{B^{*}T^{*}}

in conjunction with
[
12
, Lemma 2.2.7]
. Finally, the statement
containing
B^{*}T^{*}
closed is a direct consequence of Lemma
2.1
.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

The assumptions on
B^{*}
are equivalent to
B

being onto. Indeed, by the closed range theorem, the closed range
of
B
is then inherited by
B^{*}
and the equation
H_{0}=\ker(B^{*})\oplus\overline{\operatorname{ran}}(B)

yields that (almost) surjectivity of
B
is equivalent to injectivity
of
B^{*}
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
T\colon\operatorname{dom}(T)\subseteq H_{0}\to H_{0}

be densely defined and closed,
B\colon H_{0}\to H_{0}

be bounded, linear operator mapping onto
H_{0}
. Then

\left(B^{*}TB\right)^{*}=B^{*}T^{*}B.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
and Remark
2.3
,
B^{*}T

is closed. It is – trivially – densely defined as so is
T
.
Hence, by Theorem
2.2
, we deduce

\left((B^{*}T)B\right)^{*}=\overline{B^{*}(B^{*}T)^{*}}.

Next, since
\left(B^{*}T\right)^{*}
is a closed linear operator
as the adjoint of a densely defined operator and as
B^{*}
is one-to-one
with closed range (see Remark
2.3
),
B^{*}(B^{*}T)^{*}

is closed by Lemma
2.1
; i.e.,
\overline{B^{*}(B^{*}T)^{*}}=B^{*}(B^{*}T)^{*}
.
Next, we compute
(B^{*}T)^{*}
. For this, using Theorem
2.2

again, we deduce

\left(T^{*}B\right)^{*}=\overline{B^{*}T^{**}}=\overline{B^{*}\overline{T}}=\overline{B^{*}T}=B^{*}T,

as
B^{*}T
is closed, again by Lemma
2.1
. Computing
adjoints on both sides, we infer

T^{*}B=\left(T^{*}B\right)^{**}=\left(B^{*}T\right)^{*},

where we used that
T^{*}B
is closed. As a consequence of the above,
we get

\left((B^{*}T)B\right)^{*}=B^{*}(B^{*}T)^{*}=B^{*}T^{*}B.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
T\colon\operatorname{dom}(T)\subseteq H_{0}\to H_{0}
be densley defined and
closed and
B\colon H_{0}\to H_{0}
be a topological isomorphism.
If
D\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(T)
is a core for
T
, then
B^{-1}[D]
is a
core for
B^{*}TB.

Proof.

B
being a topological isomorphism,
B^{-1}
maps dense sets onto
dense sets; thus
B^{-1}[D]
is dense in
H_{0}
. Also it is elementary
to see that
B^{-1}[D]\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(B^{*}TB)
. Finally, let
x\in\operatorname{dom}(B^{*}TB)=\operatorname{dom}(TB).

Then
Bx\in\operatorname{dom}(T).
By assumption, we find
(y_{n})_{n}
in

such that
y_{n}\to Bx
and

in

as

Defining

TBx_{n}=Ty_{n}\to TBx

The continuity of

∎

3
Adjoints of Sums of m-accretive Operators

This section is devoted to computing the adjoint of a sum of two (unbounded)
operators
T
and
S
both densely defined and closed on a Hilbert
space
H
. The aim is to provide conditions so that

(S+T)^{*}=\overline{{S^{*}+T^{*}}}.

We refer to
[
9
]
, where several conditions for this
equality were given. The main theorem, which in turn is relevant to
the applications we have in mind, reads as follows:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
T\colon\operatorname{dom}(T)\subseteq H_{0}\to H_{1}

and
S\colon\operatorname{dom}(S)\subseteq H_{0}\to H_{1}
be two densely defined
closed operators such that
\operatorname{dom}(S)\cap\operatorname{dom}(T)
is dense. Moreover,
we assume that there exist families
(L_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}

\left(K_{\varepsilon}\right)_{\varepsilon>0}
and
(\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}

in
L(H_{1})
and
\left(R_{\varepsilon}\right)_{\varepsilon>0}
,
in
L(H_{0})
such that
L_{\varepsilon}\to 1_{H_{1}},\,R_{\varepsilon}\to 1_{H_{0}}

and
K_{\varepsilon},\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon}\to 0
in the weak operator
topology as
\varepsilon\to 0.
Moreover, assume that

\displaystyle L_{\varepsilon}S

\displaystyle\subseteq SR_{\varepsilon}+K_{\varepsilon},

\displaystyle L_{\varepsilon}T

\displaystyle\subseteq TR_{\varepsilon}+\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon}

(2)

and

L_{\varepsilon}^{\ast}[\operatorname{dom}\left((S+T)^{\ast}\right)]\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(S^{\ast})\cap\operatorname{dom}(T^{\ast}).

(3)

Then

(S+T)^{\ast}=\overline{S^{\ast}+T^{\ast}}.

Proof.

Since in general
\overline{S^{\ast}+T^{\ast}}\subseteq(S+T)^{\ast}

(see
[
12
, Theoerem 2.3.2]
) it suffices to prove the remaining
inclusion. So, let
u\in\operatorname{dom}(S+T)^{\ast}
and set
u_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq L_{\varepsilon}^{\ast}u\in\operatorname{dom}(S^{\ast})\cap\operatorname{dom}(T^{\ast}).

Let
v\in\operatorname{dom}(S)\cap\operatorname{dom}(T).
We compute

\displaystyle\langle(S^{\ast}+T^{\ast})u_{\varepsilon},v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle L_{\varepsilon}^{\ast}u,(S+T)v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle u,L_{\varepsilon}(S+T)v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle u,(S+T)R_{\varepsilon}v+(K_{\varepsilon}+\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon})v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\left(R_{\varepsilon}^{\ast}(S+T)^{\ast}+(K_{\varepsilon}+\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon})^{\ast}\right)u,v\rangle

and since
\operatorname{dom}(S)\cap\operatorname{dom}(T)
is dense, we infer

(S^{\ast}+T^{\ast})u_{\varepsilon}=\left(R_{\varepsilon}^{\ast}(S+T)^{\ast}+(K_{\varepsilon}+\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon})^{\ast}\right)u\rightharpoonup(S+T)^{\ast}u,

where we have used
R_{\varepsilon}^{\ast}\to 1_{H_{0}}
and
(K_{\varepsilon}+\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon})^{\ast}\to 0

in the weak operator topology. Since also
u_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup u

(use again
L_{\varepsilon}^{\ast}\to 1_{H_{1}}
in the weak operator
topology), we infer that
u\in\operatorname{dom}\left(\overline{S^{\ast}+T^{\ast}}\right)

with

\left(\overline{S^{\ast}+T^{\ast}}\right)u=(S+T)^{\ast}u.

Remark 3.2
.

If
SR_{\varepsilon}
is bounded and
\operatorname{dom}(S)\cap\operatorname{dom}(T)

is a core for
T
in the above theorem, then (
3
)
holds true. Indeed, from (
2
) we infer

\left(SR_{\varepsilon}+K_{\varepsilon}\right)^{\ast}\subseteq\left(L_{\varepsilon}S\right)^{\ast}=S^{\ast}L_{\varepsilon}^{\ast}.

If now
SR_{\varepsilon}
is bounded, the operator on the left-hand
side in the above inclusion is bounded, and hence, the operator on
the right-hand side is defined on
H_{1}
, meaning that
\operatorname{ran}(L_{\varepsilon}^{\ast})\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(S^{\ast})
.
Hence, for
u\in\operatorname{dom}(T)\cap\operatorname{dom}(S)
and
v\in\operatorname{dom}((S+T)^{\ast})

we compute

\displaystyle\langle Tu,L_{\varepsilon}^{\ast}v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(S+T)u-Su,L_{\varepsilon}^{\ast}v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle L_{\varepsilon}(S+T)u,v\rangle-\langle u,S^{\ast}L_{\varepsilon}^{\ast}v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(S+T)R_{\varepsilon}u+(K_{\varepsilon}+\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon})u,v\rangle-\langle u,S^{\ast}L_{\varepsilon}^{\ast}v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle u,R_{\varepsilon}^{\ast}(S+T)^{\ast}v+(K_{\varepsilon}+\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon})^{\ast}v-S^{\ast}L_{\varepsilon}^{\ast}v\rangle

and since
\operatorname{dom}(T)\cap\operatorname{dom}(S)
is a core for
T
, we infer that
also
L_{\varepsilon}^{\ast}v\in\operatorname{dom}(T^{\ast}).

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
S\colon\operatorname{dom}(S)\subseteq H\to H
and

T\colon\operatorname{dom}(T)\subseteq H\to H
both m-accretive. If
(1+T)^{-1}(1+S)^{-1}=(1+S)^{-1}(1+T)^{-1}
,
then
\operatorname{dom}(S)\cap\operatorname{dom}(T)
is dense in
H
and

(S+T)^{*}=\overline{{S^{*}+T^{*}}}.

Before we prove this result, we draw some elementary consequences
of the commutator condition. A first consequence of this will be that

S+T
is densely defined.

Proposition 3.4
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
3.3
,
the following holds:

1.

For all
\varepsilon>0
, we have

(1+\varepsilon S)^{-1}T\subseteq T(1+\varepsilon S)^{-1}.

2.

\operatorname{dom}(S)\cap\operatorname{dom}(T)
is a core for
T
. In particular,
\operatorname{dom}(S)\cap\operatorname{dom}(T)

is dense in
H
.

Proof.

For the first statement, we observe that
(1+T)^{-1}(1+S)^{-1}=(1+S)^{-1}(1+T)^{-1}

yields

(1+S)^{-1}(1+T)\subseteq(T+1)(1+S)^{-1}.

As a consequence,

(1+S)^{-1}T\subseteq T(1+S)^{-1}.

By
[
12
, Lemma 9.3.3 (a)]
, we deduce for all
\varepsilon>0

that

(1+\varepsilon S)^{-1}T=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+S)^{-1}T\subseteq T\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+S)^{-1}=T(1+\varepsilon S)^{-1}.

The second statement is based on the observation that
(1+\varepsilon S)^{-1}\to 1

as
\varepsilon\to 0+
in the strong operator topology (this follows
from the strong convergence on
\operatorname{dom}(S)
and the uniform boundedness
of the resolvents). Let now
x\in\operatorname{dom}(T)
and define
x_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq(1+\varepsilon S)^{-1}x.

Then,
x_{\varepsilon}\to x
as
\varepsilon\to 0+
and by part 1
of the present proposition, we deduce
x_{\varepsilon}\in\operatorname{dom}(T)

and

Tx_{\varepsilon}=(1+\varepsilon S)^{-1}Tx\to Tx,

yielding that
\operatorname{dom}(T)\cap\operatorname{dom}(S)
is a core for
T
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

We apply Theorem
3.1
. By Proposition
3.4

we see that for
L_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq R_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq(1+\varepsilon S)^{-1}

and
K_{\varepsilon}=\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon}=0
the relations (
2
)
are satisfied. Furthermore, by Proposition
3.4
we have
that
\operatorname{dom}(S)\cap\operatorname{dom}(T)
is dense and a core for
T.
Since clearly

SR_{\varepsilon}
is bounded, Remark
3.2
gives
that also (
3
) is satisfied. Thus, the assertion follows
from Theorem
3.1
.
∎

Applications to Evolutionary Equations

This section is devoted to apply the previous findings to operator
equations in weighted, vector-valued
L_{2}
-type spaces. The general
setting can be found in
[
8
,
12
]
. Throughout, let
H

be a Hilbert space and for
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
we let

L_{2,\rho}(\mathbb{R};H)\coloneqq\{f\in L_{2,\textnormal{loc}}(\mathbb{R};H);\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|f(t)\|_{H}^{2}\exp(-2\rho t)dt<\infty\},

endowed with the obvious norm and corresponding scalar product. We
define

\partial_{0}\colon H_{\rho}^{1}(\mathbb{R};H)\subseteq L_{2,\rho}(\mathbb{R};H)\to L_{2,\rho}(\mathbb{R};H),\phi\mapsto\phi^{\prime}.

For
\rho>0
, it can be shown that
\partial_{0}
is m-accretive
with
\operatorname{Re}\partial_{0}=\rho
.

For a bounded, strongly measurable, operator-valued function
M\colon\mathbb{R}\to L(H)
,
we denote by

M(m_{0})\in L(L_{2,\rho}(\mathbb{R};H))

the associated multiplication operator of multiplying by
M
. In
applications,
M
will be induced by scalar-valued measurable functions;
that is,
M\in L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}).
We will work under the following
standing assumptions.

1.

Let
A\colon\operatorname{dom}(A)\subseteq H\to H
be
m
-accretive.

2.

Let
M_{0},M_{1}\colon\mathbb{R}\to L(H)
be strongly measurable and uniformly
bounded.

3.

M_{0}(m_{0})^{*}=M_{0}(m_{0})
.

Here we have employed the custom to re-use the notation
A
for the
(canonically) extended operator defined on
L_{2,\rho}(\mathbb{R};H)
with
domain
L_{2,\rho}(\mathbb{R};\operatorname{dom}(A))
. The aim is to study the well-posedness
of non-autonomous problems of the form

\left(\partial_{0}M_{0}(m_{0})+M_{1}(m_{0})+A\right)U=F

(4)

under suitable commutator conditions of
M_{0}(m_{0})
with
\partial_{0}

or with
A
.

Bounded Commutator with
\partial_{0}

We begin to study the case when
M_{0}(m_{0})
and
\partial_{0}

have a bounded commuator; that is, we assume there exists a strongly
measurable and uniformly bounded mapping
M^{\prime}_{0}\colon\mathbb{R}\to L(H)

such that

M_{0}(m_{0})\partial_{0}\subseteq\partial M_{0}(m_{0})-M_{0}^{\prime}(m_{0}).

Remark 4.1
.

In
[
10
]
it was shown that this assumption is equivalent
to the Lipschitz-continuity of
M_{0}
. In this case,
M_{0}
is
differentiable almost everywhere and
M_{0}^{\prime}
is just the so-defined
derivative of
M_{0}.

Moreover, we impose the following accretivity condition on
M_{0}(m_{0})

and
M_{1}(m_{0})
:

\exists c>0,\,\rho_{0}>0\,\forall\rho\geq\rho_{0}:\,\rho M_{0}(t)+\frac{1}{2}M_{0}^{\prime}(t)+M_{1}(t)\geq c\quad(t\in\mathbb{R}\text{ a.e.}).

(5)

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume (
5
).
Then for each
\rho\geq\rho_{0}
the operator

\partial_{0}M_{0}(m_{0})+M_{1}(m_{0})-c

is accretive. Moreover,
\operatorname{dom}(\partial_{0})
is a core for this operator.

Proof.

If
u\in\operatorname{dom}(\partial_{0})
we infer

\displaystyle 2\langle(\partial_{0}M_{0}(m_{0})+M_{1}(m_{0}))u,u\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\left(\partial_{0}M_{0}(m_{0})+M_{0}(m_{0})\partial_{0}\right)u,u\rangle+\langle M_{0}^{\prime}(m_{0})u,u\rangle+2\langle M_{1}(m_{0})u,u\rangle.

Moreover, with
\partial_{0}^{*}=-\partial_{0}+2\rho
(see
[
12
, Corollary 3.2.6]
)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{0}M_{0}(m_{0})u,u\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle u,M_{0}(m_{0})\partial_{0}^{\ast}u\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle u,M_{0}(m_{0})\partial_{0}u\rangle+2\rho\langle u,M_{0}(m_{0})u\rangle,

which gives

\langle\left(\partial_{0}M_{0}(m_{0})+M_{0}(m_{0})\partial_{0}\right)u,u\rangle=2\rho\langle u,M_{0}(m_{0})u\rangle.

Summarising, we obtain

\langle(\partial_{0}M_{0}(m_{0})+M_{1}(m_{0}))u,u\rangle=\langle\left(\rho M_{0}(m_{0})+\frac{1}{2}M_{0}^{\prime}(m_{0})+M_{1}(m_{0})\right)u,u\rangle\geq c\|u\|^{2}.

It remains to prove that
\operatorname{dom}(\partial_{0})
is a core for
\partial_{0}M_{0}(m_{0}).

This however follows from

(1+\varepsilon\partial_{0})^{-1}M_{0}(m_{0})=M_{0}(m_{0})(1+\varepsilon\partial_{0})^{-1}-\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon\partial_{0})^{-1}M_{0}^{\prime}(m_{0})(1+\varepsilon\partial_{0})^{-1},

which gives

(1+\varepsilon\partial_{0})^{-1}\partial_{0}M_{0}(m_{0})=\partial_{0}M_{0}(m_{0})(1+\varepsilon\partial_{0})^{-1}-\varepsilon\partial_{0}(1+\varepsilon\partial_{0})^{-1}M_{0}^{\prime}(m_{0})(1+\varepsilon\partial_{0})^{-1}.

If now
u\in\operatorname{dom}(\partial_{0}M_{0}(m_{0}))
we set
u_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq(1+\varepsilon\partial_{0})^{-1}u\in\operatorname{dom}(\partial_{0})

and since
(1+\varepsilon\partial_{0})^{-1}\to 1
strongly, the latter
equality proves that
u_{\varepsilon}\to u
with respect to the graph
norm of
\partial_{0}M_{0}(m_{0}).

∎

We obtain

Lemma 4.4
.

Under the standing assumptions together
with (

M_{0}(m_{0})A=AM_{0}(m_{0}).

Proof.

The inclusion

Remark 4.5
.

It is a consequence of the definition of the square root (see also

[
8

M_{0}(m_{0})A\subseteq AM_{0}(m_{0})

leads to

M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}A\subseteq AM_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2};

thus, by Lemma

M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}A=AM_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}.

In particular, we obtain

M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1/2}A=AM_{0}(m_{0})^{-1/2}.

For motivating the main result of this section, we consider the following

\left(\partial_{0}M_{0}(m_{0})+M_{1}(m_{0})+A\right)U=F.

After multiplication by
M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}
the latter can be rewritten

\displaystyle\left(M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}\partial_{0}M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}+M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}M_{1}(m_{0})M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1/2}+M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}AM_{0}(m_{0})^{-1/2}\right)M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}U

\displaystyle=M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}F.

Using the latter remark and (

If instead of the above equation, we consider

Theorem 4.7
.

Under the standing assumptions,

\displaystyle\left(M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}\partial_{0}M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}+M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}M_{1}(m_{0})M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1/2}+A\right)^{*}

\displaystyle=\overline{\left(M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}\partial_{0}^{*}M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}+M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1/2}M_{1}(m_{0})^{*}M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1/2}+A^{*}\right)}.

Proof.

First of all note that
M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}M_{1}(m_{0})M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1/2}

is a bounded linear operator and can, thus, be assumed to be
0

when computing the adjoint. Next,

\left(M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}\partial_{0}M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}\right)^{*}=M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}\partial_{0}^{*}M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}

by Theorem
2.4
applied to
T=\partial_{0}
and
B=M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}=B^{*}
.
In particular, note that it particularly follows that

\left(M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}\partial_{0}M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}\right)

is m-accretive. Thus, for proving the present theorem, it suffices
to apply Theorem
3.3
to
T=M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}\partial_{0}M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}

and
S=A
. What remains is to show the commutativity of the resolvents:

\displaystyle\left(1+T\right)^{-1}\left(1+S\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(1+M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}\partial_{0}M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2})^{-1}(1+A)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2}(M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1}+\partial_{0})M_{0}(m_{0})^{1/2})^{-1}(1+A)^{-1}

\displaystyle=M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1/2}(M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1}+\partial_{0})^{-1}M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1/2}(1+A)^{-1}

\displaystyle=M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1/2}(M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1}+\partial_{0})^{-1}(1+A)^{-1}M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1/2}\left((1+A)(M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1}+\partial_{0})\right)^{-1}M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1/2}\left((M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1}+\partial_{0})(1+A)\right)^{-1}M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\left(1+S\right)^{-1}\left(1+T\right)^{-1},

where we used
\left(1+A\right)\partial_{0}=\partial_{0}(1+A)
and
Lemma
4.4
for
M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1}(1+A)=(1+A)M_{0}(m_{0})^{-1}
.
Hence, Theorem
3.3
is applicable and the assertion
follows.

5
Conclusion

We provided applicable conditions for m-accretive operators so that
the adjoint of the sum can be represented as the closure of the sum
of adjoints of the individual operators. We applied this observation
to evolutionary equations and developed well-posedness criteria for
the same. The case of the operator
M_{0}
being
L_{\infty}
-in
time only and having non-trivial (possibly time-independent) kernel
remains open for easily applicable conditions establishing well-posedness.
Thus, to properly address the general situation of time-dependent
partial differential algebraic equations appears to be a challenge
for future research.
