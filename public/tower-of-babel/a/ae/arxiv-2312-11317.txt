Lyapunov Exponents of Linear Switched Systems
Andrei A. Agrachev, Michele Motta

Abstract
We explicitly compute the maximal Lyapunov exponent for a switched system on SL 2 ​ ( ℝ ) \mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}) . This computation is reduced to the characterization of optimal trajectories for an optimal control problem on the Lie group. Keywords. Control Theory, Optimal Control, Switched system, Dynamical Systems, Lyapunov Exponents

Introduction

Let

\dot{y}=A(t)y,\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(1.1)

be a linear system of ordinary differential equations; here
t\mapsto A(t),\ t\geq 0,
is a measurable bounded family of
n\times n
-matrices. Let
X(t)
be the fundamental matrix of system (1.1), the solutions of (1.1) have a form:

y(t)=X(t)y(0)
.

Principal Lyapunov exponent of system (1.1) is defined as follows:

\ell(A(\cdot))=\limsup\limits_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln\|X(t)\|.

(1.2)

This quantity does not depend on the choice of norm in the space of matrices and is a natural measure of instability of system (1.1).
Indeed, system (1.1) is asymptotically stable with an exponential convergence rate if and only if
\ell(A(\cdot))<0
.

Principal Lyapunov exponents play a key role in the general theory of Dynamical Systems, see
[
7
]
,
[
2
]
.

If
A(t)\equiv A
is a constant matrix, then
\ell(A)
is maximum of the real parts of the eigenvalues of
A
. Moreover, for any
A(\cdot)
and any
c\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
\ell(A(\cdot)+cI)=\ell(A(\cdot))+c
.

In what follows, we use standard notations:
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is Lie algebra of all real
n\times n
-matrices and

\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is Lie group of all nondegenerate real
n\times n
-matrices. Similarly
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbb{R})

is Lie algebra of all real
n\times n
-matrices with zero trace and
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is Lie group of all real
n\times n
-matrices whose determinant equals 1.

Let
S\subset\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
be a compact subset. A switched system induced by
S
is the set of systems of ordinary differential equations

\dot{y}=A(t)y,\quad A(t)\in S,\ 0\leq t<\infty,

where
t\mapsto A(t),\ t\geq 0,
is any measurable map with values in
S
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{S}\subset L_{\infty}([0,\infty);\mathrm{GL}_{n}\mathbb{R}))
be the set of all such maps. Principal Lyapunov
exponent of the switched system is defined as follows:

\ell(S)=\sup\limits_{A(\cdot)\in\mathcal{A}_{S}}\ell(A(\cdot))\,.

Clearly,
\ell(S)\geq\sup\limits_{A\in S}\ell(A)
.

We say that the switched system is exponentially stable if
\ell(S)<0
. Stability of switched dynamical systems is really important for applications and there is a big literature devoted to this subject, see for instance
[
1
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
5
]
,
and references there.

A more natural mathematical problem is to compute or at least to estimate principal Lyapunov exponent of any switched system; many results about stability can be naturally generalized in this direction.

In particular, paper
[
1
]
is about stability but if you analyse proofs of the main results you will find the characterization of Lie subalgebras

\mathfrak{g}\subset\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
such that

\ell(S)=\sup\limits_{A\in S}\ell(A),\quad\forall\,S\subset\mathfrak{g}\,.

(1.3)

Namely, a Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}\subset\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
has property (
1.3
) if and only if
\mathfrak{g}
does not contain
a subalgebra isomorphic to
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

In the current paper, we explicitly compute
\ell(\{A,B\})

In what follows we use shortened notation

\ell(A,B)\doteq\ell(\{A,B\})
for any pair of matrices
A,B
which generates Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Such a formula automatically provides us with an explicit expression of
\ell(A,B)
for matrices
A,B
of any size if
these matrices generate a Lie algebra isomorphic to
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Indeed, let
\Psi:\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to Lie(A,B)
be such isomorphism; then
\Psi
is a representation of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. The representation
\Psi
is a direct sum of irreducible representations,

\Psi=\bigoplus\limits_{i=1}^{k}\Psi_{i}
, where
\Psi_{i}:\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathfrak{sl}_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{R}),

i=1,\ldots,k,

n_{1}\geq\cdots\geq n_{k}\geq 2
. Then

\ell(A,B)=(n_{1}-1)\ell(\Psi^{-1}(A),\Psi^{-1}(B)).

We hope that our computation will serve as a building block for eventual estimates of the Lyapunov exponents in much more general cases.

Our final result can be stated as follows. To simplify the notation, set
a=\mathrm{tr}(A^{2}),b=\mathrm{tr}(B^{2}),c=\mathrm{tr}(AB)
.

Theorem 1.4

(Main Theorem)
.

Suppose that
A,B\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, with
a\geq b
.

1.

If
a\geq 0
and
c>a
or
a<0
and
c\geq\sqrt{ab}
, then

\ell(A,B)=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{c^{2}-ab}{c-\frac{a+b}{2}}};

2.

If
a\geq 0
and
c\leq a
, then

\ell(A,B)=\sqrt{\frac{a}{2}};

3.

If
a<0
and
c\leq-\sqrt{ab}
, then

\ell(A,B)=\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{-2}{a}}+3\sqrt{\frac{-2}{b}}}\mathrm{arcosh}\left(\frac{-c}{\sqrt{ab}}\right).

We point out that for every
A,B\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that
a,b\leq 0
, then
c^{2}\geq ab
(see Section 6 for more explanations).

In Figure
1
you can see a graphical representation of Main Theorem.

The whole paper is devoted to prove our Main Theorem and it is structured as follows:
in Section 2 we show how computing the Lyapunov exponents
\ell(A,B)
can be reduced to an optimal control problem and we will apply Pontryagin Maximum Principle (PMP) to this problem. We stated the precise version of PMP that we used in the Appendix.
In Section 3 we will deduce from PMP some general properties of Pontryagin extremals. In particular, we will divide them into two categories:
bang-bang
extremals and singular extremals.
In Section 4 and 5 we study
bang-bang
and singular extremals respectively.
In Section 6 we show which extremal is the optimal one, depending on
\mathrm{tr}(A^{2}),\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})
and
\mathrm{tr}(AB)
.

.

2
Reduction to OC Problem and application of PMP

As explained in the Introduction, we will study the switched system induced by two matrices
A,B
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{X}(t)=X(t)\big(u(t)A+(1-u(t))B\big),\\
X(0)=\mathrm{Id},\end{array}\right.

(2.1)

where

X:[0,T]\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})

,
T>0
,

A,B\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, such that
A,B,[A,B]
are linearly independent,

u\in\mathcal{U}:=\{v:[0,T]\to\{0,1\}\;|\;v\text{ measurable}\}
. Without loss of generality, we can suppose
\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})\geq\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})
.

Notice that here we write
\dot{X}=XA(t)
, while in the Introduction we had
\dot{X}=A(t)X
. Indeed, using the notation of the Introduction, system (
2.1
) should be written as
\dot{X}^{T}=X^{T}A(t)^{T}
, where the
T
superscript denotes the transpose matrix. To simplify the notation we will drop this superscript.

We prefer to solve the system with
A
and
X
in the reversed order because system (
2.1
) is left-invariant.

We want to find the principal Lyapunov exponent
\ell(A,B)
.
Fixed
u\in\mathcal{U}
, we will denote with
X(\cdot;u)
the solution of (
2.1
) with control equal to
u
.

A natural relaxation of our problem (
2.1
) consists in taking
u:[0,T]\to[0,1]
measurable instead of
u:[0,T]\to\{0,1\}
.
This way, we can restate the original problem into a control problem and so we can use all the classical results from Control Theory.

Given
X\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
with real eigenvalues, let us denote with
\lambda(X)
the eigenvalue of
X
bigger than 1. If
X
has complex eigenvalue, we denote with
\lambda(X)
the real part of these eigenvalues.

Another crucial observation is that if the norm in (
1.2
) is the norm induced by the Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, then its value coincides with the absolute value of
\lambda(X(T))
.
Hence,
if for every
T>0
we can compute
c(T)
such that

\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\lambda(X(T;u))=c(T),

then, we obtain that

\ell(A,B)=\limsup_{T\to+\infty}\frac{1}{T}\ln c(T).

To be precise, the previous equality should be a “lower or equal” a priori, because the optimal strategy to maximize
\lambda
up to time
T
can be change when
T
goes to infinity.
We prove however that the limit is realized by a fixed control
u(t)
, which is explicitly computed.
For those familiar with the terminology of Control Theory, the optimal strategy will be one of the following three possibilities:
the constant singular control, corresponding to case 1. in the Main Theorem,
the constant control equal to 1, corresponding to case 2.,
a bang-bang periodic control, corresponding to case 3.
We will define these concepts later.

Instead of finding

\sup_{u}\lambda(X(T;u))
, one can find

\sup_{u}f\big(\lambda(X(T;u))\big)
,
for a suitable
f
monotone in
\lambda
.
It turns out that a convenient choice of
f
is the trace
\mathrm{tr}(X(T;u))
: besides being monotone in
\lambda
, it is a linear function of
X
and it is invariant after a change of basis.

Thus, we can restate our original problem into the following Optimal Control problem:

Problem
.

Fix
A,B\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
T\geq 0
. Let
\mathcal{U}=\{u:[0,T]\to[0,1]\;|\;u\text{ measurable}\}
and for each
u\in\mathcal{U}
denote with
X(\cdot;u)
the solution of the following Cauchy problem on
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
:

\begin{cases}\dot{X}(t)=X(t)(u(t)A+(1-u(t))B),\\
X(0)=\mathrm{Id}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}),\end{cases}\quad t\in[0,T].

(2.2)

Define the functional

\Phi_{T}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\Phi_{T}(u)=\mathrm{tr}X(T;u)
.
We want to find

\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{U}

which maximizes
\Phi_{T}
:

\mathrm{tr}X(T;\tilde{u})=\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathrm{tr}X(T;u).

(2.3)

If we are able to solve this problem for each fixed
T
, then from the estimate of

\mathrm{tr}X(T;u)
we can deduce an estimate for
\|X(T;u)\|
which is uniform with respect to
u\in\mathcal{U}
and depends only on
T
.
Then, the desired value of the principal Lyapunov exponent follows.

Notice that if
[A,B]
is a linear combination of
A
and
B
, then the Lie algebra generated by
A,B
is two-dimensional, hence is solvable. In this case we already know from
[
1
]
the value of
\ell(A,B)
.

Existence of maximum in (
2.3
) is guaranteed by Filippov Theorem (see, for instance, Theorem 10.1 in
[
8
]
).

So, we can apply Pontryagin Maximum Principle (PMP) to our problem. For a general reference, see Theorem 12.3 in
[
8
]
. You can find in Appendix A (see Theorem
A.5
) the precise statement that we use here.

We define the Pontryagin left-invariant Hamiltonian function:

H(\eta,u)=\mathrm{tr}\big(\eta(uA+(1-u)B)\big)\quad\eta\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R}),u\in[0,1].

(2.4)

Here we identify tangent and cotangent space of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
through Killing form of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, which is non-degenerate since
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is simple (see Appendix A).
Recall also that Killing form
K
on
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
can be computed as

K(M_{1},M_{2})=4\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2}),\quad M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R}).

From Pontryagin Maximum Principle and the theory of left-invariant Hamiltonian systems on Lie Groups (see Appendix A)
we obtain that if
\tilde{u}
is a local maximum for
\Phi_{T}
, then there exists a Lipschitz function
\eta:[0,T]\to\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{\eta}(t)=\big[\eta(t),\>\tilde{u}(t)A+\big(1-\tilde{u}(t)\big)B\big],\\
\eta(0)=\eta_{0}.\end{array}\right.

(2.5)

for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
(see Proposition
A.10
and Equation
A.12
). We will call this ODE adjoint system and its solution
\eta
adjoint trajectory. Moreover, the two following conditions must hold:

\displaystyle H(\eta(t),\tilde{u}(t))

\displaystyle=\max_{u\in[0,1]}H(\eta(t),u),\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in[0,T],

(2.6)

\displaystyle\eta(T;\tilde{u})

\displaystyle=X(T;\tilde{u})-\frac{\mathrm{tr}(X(T;\tilde{u}))}{2}\mathrm{Id}.

(2.7)

The latter condition is known as
transversal condition
, see Lemma
A.13
.

We will call Pontryagin
extremal
a couple
(X(\cdot,u),\eta(\cdot;u))
satisfying all necessary conditions prescribed by PMP.

3
General properties of extremals

In the following, if it is not important to specify the control we will denote with
X
the solution of (
2.2
) instead of
X(\cdot;u)
.

The solution
\eta
to equation (
2.5
) can be expressed as

\eta(t)=X(t)^{-1}\eta_{0}X(t).

(3.1)

Indeed, differentiating the previous expression and recalling that

\frac{d}{dt}[X(t)^{-1}]=-X(t)^{-1}\dot{X}(t)X(t)^{-1},

one obtains equation (
2.5
).

Thus, from (
3.1
), we can see that the determinant along any solution of (
2.5
) is constant.
For
M\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we have the formula
2\det(M)=-\mathrm{tr}(M^{2})
. In what follows, it will be more convenient to use the expression
\mathrm{tr}(M^{2})
instead of
\det M
.

Notice that from (
2.7
), it follows that

\mathrm{tr}(\eta(T)^{2})=\frac{1}{2}\big(\mathrm{tr}X(T)\big)^{2}-2.

So, since

X(T)\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
then

\mathrm{tr}(X(T))^{2}\geq 4,

at least for large
T
,
and so

\mathrm{tr}(\eta(T)^{2})>0
.
Moreover, since
\mathrm{tr}(\eta^{2})
is constant, we can deduce that
\mathrm{tr}(\eta(t)^{2})>0
for all
t\in[0,T]
.
In particular,
\eta(t)
must have real eigenvalues for all
t\in[0,T]
.

3.1
Invariant Hyperboloid

Equation (
2.5
) is invariant after a dilation, in the sense that for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
, if
\eta(\cdot)
is a solution with initial value
\eta(0)=\eta_{0}
, then
\tilde{\eta}(t)=c\eta(t)
is a solution with initial value
\tilde{\eta}(0)=c\eta_{0}
.

So, it is not restrictive to suppose that
\mathrm{tr}(\eta(t)^{2})=\mathrm{tr}(\eta_{0}^{2})=1
. Hence, the trajectory
\eta
is contained in the set

H:=\{M\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\>|\>\mathrm{tr}(M^{2})=1\},

which is a (connected) hyperboloid in the three-dimensional vector space
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
To see this, just take

M=\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}&m_{2}\\
m_{3}&-m_{1}\end{pmatrix}.

Then

\mathrm{tr}(M^{2})=2(m_{1}^{2}+m_{2}m_{3})=2\left(m_{1}^{2}+\left(\frac{m_{2}+m_{3}}{2}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{m_{2}-m_{3}}{2}\right)^{2}\right).

Since
A,B,[A,B]
are linearly independent, we can write

M=\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma[A,B],

and the equation defining
H
reads

\alpha^{2}\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})+\beta^{2}\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})+\gamma^{2}\mathrm{tr}([A,B]^{2})+2\alpha\beta\mathrm{tr}(AB)=1.

To simplify the previous expression, we will use the following result.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
A,B\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Then

\mathrm{tr}([A,B]^{2})=2\mathrm{tr}(AB)^{2}-2\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})\mathrm{tr}(B^{2}).

(3.3)

Proof.

From the definition of bracket, we have

\mathrm{tr}([A,B]^{2})=2\mathrm{tr}((AB)^{2})-2\mathrm{tr}(A^{2}B^{2}).

Since
A,B\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
A^{2}
and
B^{2}
are scalar, that is
A^{2}=\lambda^{2}\mathrm{Id}
and
B^{2}=\mu^{2}\mathrm{Id}
. So

\mathrm{tr}(A^{2}B^{2})=2\lambda^{2}\mu^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})\mathrm{tr}(B^{2}).

On the other hand, the minimal polynomial of
AB
is

(AB)^{2}-\mathrm{tr}(AB)AB+\det(AB)\mathrm{Id}=0,

and computing the trace of the expression above leads to

\mathrm{tr}((AB)^{2})=\mathrm{tr}(AB)^{2}-2\det A\det B=\mathrm{tr}(AB)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})\mathrm{tr}(B^{2}).

and so

\mathrm{tr}([A,B]^{2})=2\mathrm{tr}((AB)^{2})-2\mathrm{tr}(A^{2}B^{2})=2\mathrm{tr}(AB)^{2}-2\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})\mathrm{tr}(B^{2}).

as we wanted to prove.
∎

Using the previous Lemma, we can rewrite the equation for
H
as

\alpha^{2}\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})+\beta^{2}\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})+2\gamma^{2}\big(\mathrm{tr}(AB)^{2}-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})\big)+2\alpha\beta\mathrm{tr}(AB)=1.

3.2
Switching function,
bang-bang
and singular extremals

Equation (
2.6
) can be rewritten more explicitly as

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(\eta(t)B)+\tilde{u}(t)\mathrm{tr}(\eta(t)(A-B))

\displaystyle=\max_{u\in[0,1]}\big[\mathrm{tr}(\eta(t)B)+u\mathrm{tr}(\eta(t)(A-B))\big]

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}(\eta(t)B)+\max_{u\in[0,1]}u\mathrm{tr}(\eta(t)(A-B)),

(3.5)

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
.
Hence, we are lead to define
\varphi:[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
,

\varphi(t)=\mathrm{tr}(\eta(t)(A-B)).

(3.6)

We will call
\varphi

switching function
. Notice that
\varphi
is at least Lipschitz.
If
\varphi
has only isolated zeros, then a control
u
, in order to satisfy Pontryagin Maximum Principle, must be of the form

u(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}1\quad\text{ if }\varphi(t)>0,\\
0\quad\text{ if }\varphi(t)<0,\end{array}\right.

(3.7)

that is, the isolated zeros of
\varphi
are exactly the times when there are a
switch
in the dynamics.

If
\varphi(t_{0})=0
, we will call
t_{0}
a
switching time
and the corresponding
\eta(t_{0})

switching point
.

If
\varphi
has any nonisolated zero, then Pontryagin Maximum Principle does not determine the control directly, but in our case it is still possible to bypass this obstacle and determine the control.

Definition 3.8

(
bang-bang
control, Singular control)
.

We will call
bang-bang
a control
u
and its corresponding extremal if
\varphi
has only isolated zeros.

If instead
\varphi\equiv 0
, then we will call the associated control
u
and its extremal
singular
.

A control
u
which takes only extremal values, i.e.
u(t)\in\{0,1\}
for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
, is called
bang-bang
.
We will first focus on
bang-bang
extremals and then we will discuss separately the singular case.

4
Bang-bang
extremals

4.1
Properties of
bang-bang
extremals

In order to determine the structure of Pontryagin
bang-bang
extremals, it will be useful to study the plane of switching points:

\Pi=\{M\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\;|\;\mathrm{tr}(MA)=\mathrm{tr}(MB)\}.

(4.1)

This is the set of matrices in
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
for which the function
\varphi
is zero.

Since the matrices
A,B,[A,B]
form a bases of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
by hypothesis, we can write the equation
\mathrm{tr}(MA)=\mathrm{tr}(MB)
as

\alpha\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})+\beta\mathrm{tr}(AB)+\gamma\mathrm{tr}([A,B]A)=\alpha\mathrm{tr}(AB)+\beta\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})+\gamma\mathrm{tr}([A,B]B),

that is

\beta=\frac{\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})}{\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}\alpha.

(4.2)

So, if we impose
\mathrm{tr}(M^{2})=1
, that is if we intersect the hyperboloid
H
with the plane
\Pi
we obtain the equation of a conic

\displaystyle\frac{\big(2\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})\big)\mathrm{tr}\big([A,B]^{2}\big)}{\big(\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})\big)^{2}}\alpha^{2}+\mathrm{tr}\big([A,B]^{2}\big)\gamma^{2}=1.

(4.3)

Figure 2:
Examples of switching curves. In both pictures you can see the orange hyperboloid
H
, the blue plane
\Pi
and their intersection, the switching curve
S
.

Definition 4.4

(Switching curve)
.

We will call
switching curve

S
the intersection curve
\Pi\cap H
, whose equation is (
4.3
).

So, the coefficient in front of
\alpha
is positive if and only if

\big(2\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})\big)\mathrm{tr}\big([A,B]^{2}\big)>0.

Hence, if

\mathrm{tr}\big([A,B]^{2}\big)>0
,
then the switching curve is an ellipse if

\mathrm{tr}(AB)>\frac{\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})+\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}{2}
,
and is an hyperbola if

\mathrm{tr}\big([A,B]^{2}\big)>0

and

\mathrm{tr}(AB)<\frac{\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})+\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}{2}
.

If instead

\mathrm{tr}\big([A,B]^{2}\big)<0

,
the switching curve is an hyperbola if

\mathrm{tr}(AB)<\frac{\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})+\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}{2}
.
Notice that

\mathrm{tr}(AB)>\frac{\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})+\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}{2}

implies

\mathrm{tr}\big([A,B]^{2}\big)>0
,
so we can neglet the case

\mathrm{tr}(AB)>\frac{\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})+\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}{2}

and

\mathrm{tr}\big([A,B]^{2}\big)<0

.

4.2
Hyperboloid foliation

Let us now fix an initial point on the hyperboloid:

\eta_{0}\in H.

Define the half spaces

\displaystyle\Pi^{+}

\displaystyle=\{M\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\;|\;\mathrm{tr}(MA)>\mathrm{tr}(MB)\},

\displaystyle\Pi^{-}

\displaystyle=\{M\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\;|\;\mathrm{tr}(MA)<\mathrm{tr}(MB)\},

and the half hyperboloids

\displaystyle H^{+}

4.3
Bang-bang

So far, we proved that there are two qualitatively different type of adjoint trajectories: with a finite number of switches or periodic trajectories with an infinite number of switches.

If the adjoint trajectory is periodic, then also the switching function (

However, not every

In this Section we are going to describe how to find

From (

We will proceed as follows: for every

The following result simplifies this task.

Proposition 4.10
.

Define the bang-bang control

u(\tau)=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if}\quad\tau\in[0,t],\\
0\quad\text{if}\quad\tau\in[t,T].\end{cases}

Let
X
be the corresponding solution of (
2.2

\eta
be the solution of (
2.5

Suppose that
\eta
satisfies the condition of transversality (
2.7

If

0
and
T

Proof.

The solution of equation (
2.5
) is

\eta(\tau)=X(\tau)^{-1}\eta_{0}X(\tau).

Since
\eta
must satisfy the transversal condition, we know by Proposition
4.7
that
\eta
is periodic of period
T
, so it must be

\eta_{0}=X(T)-\frac{\mathrm{tr}\big(X(T)\big)}{2}\mathrm{Id}.

So, putting together the last two equations with the formula for
X
, we obtain that

\eta(\tau)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}e^{(t-\tau)A}e^{sB}e^{\tau A}-\frac{\mathrm{tr}\left(X(T)\right)}{2}\mathrm{Id}\quad\text{ if }\tau\in[0,t]\\[4.0pt]
e^{(s-\tau+t)B}e^{tA}e^{\tau-tB}-\frac{\mathrm{tr}\left(X(T)\right)}{2}\mathrm{Id}\quad\text{ if }\tau\in[t,t+s]\end{array}\right.

We are assuming that
t
to be a switching time, so it satisfy

\mathrm{tr}(\eta(t)A)=\mathrm{tr}(\eta(t)B).

Using the formula for
\eta
that we have just obtained, we get

\mathrm{tr}(e^{sB}e^{tA}A)=\mathrm{tr}(e^{sB}e^{tA}B).

(4.11)

The left hand side can be rewritten as

\mathrm{tr}(e^{sB}e^{tA}A)=\mathrm{tr}(e^{sB}Ae^{tA})=\mathrm{tr}(e^{tA}e^{sB}A)=\mathrm{tr}(X(T)A).

Similarly, the right hand side of (

) is equal to

\mathrm{tr}(X(T)B)
.
Hence, it follows that also

\mathrm{tr}(X(T)A)=\mathrm{tr}(X(T)B),

(4.12)

which implies that
T
(and thus, since
\eta
is
T

) is a switching time.
∎

Concerning the value of the principal Lyapunov exponent, when the adjoint trajectory is periodic it can be computed as follows:

\displaystyle\ell_{\text{per}}(T)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to+\infty}\frac{1}{nT}\log\|X(nT)\|=

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to+\infty}\frac{1}{nT}\log\|X(T)^{n}\|=

Using the identity

\mathrm{arcosh}x=\log(x+\sqrt{x^{2}-1})

\ell_{\text{per}}(T)=\frac{\mathrm{arcosh}\left(\frac{\mathrm{tr}(X(T))}{2}\right)}{T}.

4.4

Now, we are now going to compute the upper bound for Principal Lyapunov exponent of periodic controls.
As we saw previously, in order to find the extremals of our problem we have to study the functional

Since equations (

Up to exchange the role of

Proposition 4.16

With this notations, the functional in (

\Phi(t,s)=2\cosh(\lambda t)\cosh(\mu s)+\frac{\mathrm{tr}(AB)}{\lambda\mu}\sinh(\lambda t)\sinh(\mu s).

This formula is a simple consequence of the following Lemma.

Lemma 4.18

If

Using these facts in the series expansion of

If

Remark 4.22

(4.31)

If instead

\mathrm{tr}\big([A,B]^{2}\big)<0,

then control with switches are not optimal.

Proof.

First, suppose that

\mathrm{tr}\big([A,B]^{2}\big)>0
.
One can check that this condition implies that the coefficient in (
4.26
) is smaller than 1:

\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\frac{\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})}{\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}<1.

So, for every
t>0
it makes sense to take the inverse hyperbolic tangent in (
4.26
) on both sides.
Moreover,
from point
(1)
of Lemma
4.29
, for any
t>0
we have

\ell\left(\frac{t}{2},\frac{s(t)}{2}\right)>\ell(t,s(t)).

In particular, we can find
t_{1}<t
such that
t_{1}+s(t_{1})=\frac{1}{2}(t+s(t))
, so that

\ell(t_{1},s(t_{1}))>\ell\left(\frac{t}{2},\frac{s(t)}{2}\right)>\ell(t,s(t)).

Moreover, we have that
\lim_{t\to 0}s(t)=0
, so

\ell(t,s(t))<\lim_{\tau\to 0}\ell(\tau,s(\tau)).

To evaluate the last limit, one can use Taylor expansions.
This leads to

\lim_{t\to 0}\ell(t,s(t))=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{tr}\big([A,B]^{2}\big)}{2\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}}.

It is possible to avoid to compute explicitly this limit (see next Subsection).

Suppose now that

\mathrm{tr}\big([A,B]^{2}\big)<0
.
Notice that this can happen only if
\mathrm{tr}(A^{2}),\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})>0
.
Similarly to the previous case, this inequality implies

\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\frac{\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})}{\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}>1.

So, we can solve equation (
4.26
) only for
t<\bar{t}
, where

\bar{t}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathrm{arctanh}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\mu}\frac{\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})}{\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}\right).

Moreover,

\lim_{t\to\bar{t}}s(t)=+\infty
.
Then, from point (2) of Lemma
4.29
, we have

\ell(2t,2s(t))>\ell(t,s(t)).

So, in particular we can find
t_{1}\in(t,\bar{t})
such that
t_{1}+s(t_{1})=2(t+s(t))
. So, this implies

\ell(t_{1},s(t_{1}))>\ell(2t,2s(t))>\ell(t,s(t)).

So, we obtain that

\ell(t,s(t))<\lim_{\tau\to\bar{t}}\ell(\tau,s(\tau)).

But as
t\to\bar{t}
, the trajectory spends more and more time with control equal to
0
. So, this strategy is for sure worse than spending all the time with control equal to 1, which is always admissible if

\mathrm{tr}\big([A,B]^{2}\big)<0

. Hence, in this case switches are not optimal.
∎

We now prove Lemma
4.29
.

Proof of Lemma
4.29
.

Recall that

l(t,s)=\frac{1}{t+s}\log\big(\alpha(t,s)\big).

Hence, we can write

\displaystyle\ell\left(\frac{t}{2},\frac{s}{2}\right)-\ell(t,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{t+s}\log\left(\alpha\left(\frac{t}{2},\frac{s}{2}\right)\right)-\frac{1}{t+s}\log\big(\alpha(t,s)\big)=

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t+s}\log\left(\frac{\alpha\left(\frac{t}{2},\frac{s}{2}\right)^{2}}{\alpha(t,s)}\right).

So, we can see that this difference is positive if and only if

\frac{\alpha\left(\frac{t}{2},\frac{s}{2}\right)^{2}}{\alpha(t,s)}>1.

Since
\alpha(t,s)>1
for any
t,s>0
, we have that

\alpha\left(\frac{t}{2},\frac{s}{2}\right)^{2}>\alpha(t,s)\quad\text{iff}\quad\alpha\left(\frac{t}{2},\frac{s}{2}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha\left(\frac{t}{2},\frac{s}{2}\right)^{2}}>\alpha(t,s)+\frac{1}{\alpha(t,s)},

which is the same as

\mathrm{tr}\left(X\left(\frac{t}{2},\frac{s}{2}\right)^{2}\right)>\mathrm{tr}\big(X(t,s)\big).

The term in the left hand side can be rewritten as

X\left(\frac{t}{2},\frac{s}{2}\right)^{2}=X(t,s)+e^{\frac{t}{2}A}\big[e^{\frac{s}{2}B},e^{\frac{t}{2}A}\big]e^{\frac{s}{2}B}.

So, in the end, it amounts just to compute

\mathrm{tr}\left(e^{\frac{t}{2}A}\big[e^{\frac{s}{2}B},e^{\frac{t}{2}A}\big]e^{\frac{s}{2}B}\right)
,
which can be done using formula (
4.19
). One obtains

\mathrm{tr}\left(e^{\frac{t}{2}A}\big[e^{\frac{s}{2}B},e^{\frac{t}{2}A}\big]e^{\frac{s}{2}B}\right)=\frac{\sinh(\lambda\frac{t}{2})^{2}\sinh(\mu\frac{s}{2})^{2}}{2\lambda^{2}\mu^{2}}\mathrm{tr}\big([A,B]^{2}\big).

So, if

\mathrm{tr}\big([A,B]^{2}\big)>0

,
then we obtained point (1) of the Lemma. If instead

\mathrm{tr}\big([A,B]^{2}\big)<0
,
then all inequalities are reversed and we obtain point (2).
∎

Remark 4.32
.

Notice that this is a completely general result about product of matrix exponentials. Indeed, it holds for a generic control
u_{t,s}
, even if it do not correspond to a Pontryagin extremal.

4.4.3
Limit trajectory as switching time tends to zero

In the previous Subsection we saw that if
\mathrm{tr}([A,B]^{2})>0
, then the upper bound for the principal Lyapunov is obtained as the switching time tends to zero.
Now, we want to discuss some more details about the limit trajectory as the switching time goes to zero. This will allow us to evaluate the upper bound without computing directly the limit.

Proposition 4.33
.

The limit trajectory as the switching time goes to zero is the singular trajectory.

Proof.

First, recall that from (
4.27
)

s(t)=\frac{\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})}{\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}t+o(t)\quad\text{ as }t\to 0.

So, our trajectory spends
t
time with control equal to 1, which corresponds to matrix
A
, and approximately

\frac{\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})}{\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}t

time with control equal to 0, corresponding to matrix
B
.
For simplicity, let us call

c=\frac{\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})}{\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})},

the coefficient appearing in
s(t)
.

So, by convex approximation (see Theorem 8.2 in
[
8
]
), we know that as the number of switches goes to infinity (i.e.,
t
goes to zero), the flow tends uniformly (in the
C^{\infty}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
-topology) to

T\mapsto\exp\big(T(vA+(1-v)B)\big),

where

v=\frac{1}{1+c}=\frac{\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}{2\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})},

which corresponds to the value of the singular control (see Section 5).
∎

So, since the convergence in the previous proof is uniform on compact time intervals and since in the case of periodic controls we can reduce to the finite time case (see
4.15
), we obtain the following Corollary.

Corollary 4.34
.

With the notation used in Subsection 4.4.2, we have

\lim_{\tau\to 0}\ell(\tau,s(\tau))=\lambda_{*}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{tr}\big([A,B]^{2}\big)}{2\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}},

where
\lambda_{*}
is the positive eigenvalue of
5.7
(see Section 5).

5
Singular extremals

In this Section we are going to deal with the case of singular extremals (see Definition
3.8
). Recall the definition of the (left-invariant) Hamiltonian function

H(\eta,u)=\mathrm{tr}\big(\eta(uA+(1-u)B)\big),

and the switching function

\varphi(t)=\mathrm{tr}\big(\eta(t)(A-B)\big).

(5.1)

Recall also that the singular extremals correspond to the case of
\varphi(t)=0
for
t\in[\tau_{1},\tau_{2}]
, and
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}>0
.
In this case, since
\varphi
is Lipschitz, we can derive it:

0=\dot{\varphi}(t)=\mathrm{tr}\big(\dot{\eta}(t)(A-B)\big)=\mathrm{tr}\Big(\big[\eta(t),uA+(1-u)B\big](A-B)\Big)=\mathrm{tr}\big(\eta(t)[A,B]\big),

where in the last equality we used the identity

\mathrm{tr}([M_{1},M_{2}]M_{3})=\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}[M_{2},M_{3}]).

Notice that
\dot{\varphi}
does not depend on
u
and in particular it is again a Lipschitz function.
So, if
\eta_{*}
is a point of a singular trajectory, it must satisfy the two following conditions:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathrm{tr}\big(\eta_{*}(A-B)\big)=0,\\
\mathrm{tr}\big(\eta_{*}[A,B]\big)=0\end{array}\right..

These two conditions are linear in
\eta_{*}
, so, since
A,B
and
[A,B]
are linearly independent, they determine a straight line in
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

If we write

\eta_{*}=\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma[A,B],

then the second equation implies
\gamma=0
. Hence, from (
4.9
), we see that if we take
c_{*}=\mathrm{tr}(\eta_{*}A)
, the line
r_{c_{*}}
is tangent to the hyperboloid
H
(see discussion in Subsection 4.2).

There are two intersection between the line (
5
) and the hyperboloid
H
described in Section 2 if and only if

\big(2\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})\big)\mathrm{tr}([A,B]^{2})>0.

(5.4)

If there are no intersection, then there are no singular extremals. So, from now on we will assume that this inequality hold.

We can compute explicitly these two intersection: let us call
\eta_{*}
one of the two intersection point. Then, from (
4.2
) and
\gamma=0
we obtain

\eta_{*}=\alpha_{*}A+\alpha_{*}\frac{\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})}{\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}B,

where
\alpha_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
is chosen such that
\mathrm{tr}(\eta_{*}^{2})=1
.
Since the intersection between (
5
) and
H
is a discrete set and since the solution
\eta_{*}
is Lipschitz, the only possibility for
\eta_{*}
is to be constant.
Hence

0=\dot{\eta}_{*}(t)=[\eta_{*},u_{*}A+(1-u_{*})B],

from which we can determine the value of the singular control
u_{*}

u_{*}=\frac{\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}{2\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}.

(5.5)

In order to be an admissible control, it must hold
u_{*}\in[0,1]
.
The inequality
u_{*}\geq 0
is equivalent to the condition

\left\{\mathrm{tr}(AB)>\frac{\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})+\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}{2}\right\}\cup\left\{\mathrm{tr}(AB)\leq\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})\right\},

while the other inequality
u_{*}\leq 1
is equivalent to

\left\{\mathrm{tr}(AB)\geq\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})\right\}\cup\left\{\mathrm{tr}(AB)<\frac{\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})+\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}{2}\right\},

and recalling that
\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})>\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})
, we obtain

Proposition 5.6
.

u_{*}
is an admissible control if and only if

\mathrm{tr}(AB)\geq\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})\quad\text{ or }\quad\mathrm{tr}(AB)\leq\mathrm{tr}(B^{2}).

Then, we obtain

u_{*}A+(1-u_{*})B=\frac{\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}{2\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}A+\frac{\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})}{2\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}B,

(5.7)

and

\mathrm{tr}\Big(\big(u_{*}A+(1-u_{*})B\big)^{2}\Big)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{tr}\big([A,B]^{2}\big)}{2\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}.

(5.8)

So, if we take the constant control equal to
u_{*}
, then the associated Lyapunov exponent is

l=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{tr}\big([A,B]^{2}\big)}{2\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}}.

(5.9)

A direct computation shows that

\mathrm{tr}\Big(\big(u_{*}A+(1-u_{*})B\big)^{2}\Big)\geq\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})\quad\text{iff}\quad\mathrm{tr}(AB)\geq\frac{\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})+\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}{2}.

(5.10)

So, taking into account that singular control is admissible if
\mathrm{tr}(AB)\geq\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})
, we obtain that singular constant control are better than constant control if
\mathrm{tr}(AB)\geq\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})
.
On the other hand, if

\mathrm{tr}(AB)\leq\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})

then constant control is better than constant singular control.

The following result ends the discussion about the singular case.

Proposition 5.11
.

There are no solution to the adjoint system (
2.5
) containing a
bang-bang
piece and a singular piece.

Proof.

We already noticed that for
\eta_{*}
we have
\gamma=0
in (
4.9
)
and the intersection line between the planes
\Pi_{A,c_{*}}

and
\Pi_{B,c_{*}}
is tangent to the hyperboloid
H
, where
c_{*}=\mathrm{tr}(A\eta_{*})
.
Recall from Section 4 that as the switching time in a
bang-bang
trajectory in
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
tends to zero, the resulting trajectory tends to a singular trajectory. The convergence in
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
implies that also the sequence of the corresponding adjoint trajectories tends to the singular adjoint trajectory, which is constant. Thus, in a neighborhood of
\eta_{*}
sufficiently small and contained in the hyperboloid
H
, the phase portrait is as in Figure
4
.

S
\eta_{*}

Figure 4:
Phase portrait on the hyperboloid
H
for the adjoint system (
2.5
) near
\eta_{*}
. The black line
S
is the switching curve,
\eta_{*}
is the singular point. Above and below
S
we have the two
bang
pieces, and as the switching time goes to zero the adjoint trajectory tends to
\eta_{*}
.

Thus, any possible trajectory starting from
\eta_{*}
and escaping the switching curve
S
would inevitably cross some of the periodic trajectories tending to
\eta_{*}
, contradicting the local uniqueness of the solution of an ODE.
∎

So, to resume, we found that if an extremal contains a singular piece, then the whole extremal is singular, and the corresponding Lyapunov exponent is

\ell_{\text{sing}}(A,B)=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{tr}\big([A,B]^{2}\big)}{2\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}},

(5.12)

and this is the optimal solution if
\mathrm{tr}(AB)\geq\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})
.

6
Discussion of cases

We end up with some final remarks about which is the optimal strategy depending on the values
\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})
,
\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})
,
\mathrm{tr}(AB)
. As before, we always assume
\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})\geq\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})
.

So far, we have obtained that all possible extremals satisfying PMP are in one of the following form:

•

constant control;

•

constant singular control;

•

if
\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})<0
,
bang-bang
periodic control as in Subsection 4.4.1.

As pointed out in Subsection 4.4.2, there is also this other kind of extremals accumulating at singular extremals. However, we saw that their associated principal Lyapunov exponent is at most strictly less then the one associated to singular extremal, so we can omit them from the discussion.

First, assume that
\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})\geq 0
. In this case we already saw in Section 5 that if
\mathrm{tr}(AB)\geq\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})
, then the value of the Principal Lyapunov exponent given by the constant singular control is bigger than the one given by the constant control. So, the first two point of the Main Theorem are proved.

We turn now to the
\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})<0
case. We have the following restriction on the possible values of
\mathrm{tr}(AB)
.

Lemma 6.1
.

If

\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})\leq\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})<0
,
then

|\mathrm{tr}(AB)|\geq\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}
.
In particular

\mathrm{tr}([A,B]^{2})\geq 0
.

Proof.

Up to a change of base, we can suppose

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\lambda\\
\lambda&0\end{pmatrix},\quad B=P\begin{pmatrix}0&-\mu\\
\mu&0\end{pmatrix}P^{-1},\quad P=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}),\quad\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}.

Since
A
in this form commutes with rotations, that is matrix of the form

R_{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},\quad\theta\in[0,2\pi],

we can use another change of base given by a rotation matrix to kill one element of
P
. That is, we can further assume that
P
is in the form

P=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
0&\frac{1}{\alpha}\end{pmatrix},\quad\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R},\quad\alpha\neq 0.

With
A,B
in this form, we can compute directly
\mathrm{tr}(AB):

\mathrm{tr}(AB)=-\lambda\mu\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}+\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}\right).

Since

\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}+\alpha^{2}\geq 2

,
we obtain

|\mathrm{tr}(AB)|\geq 2|\lambda\mu|=\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}.

∎

Remark 6.2
.

From the previous proof, we can give a geometric interpretation of the sign of
\mathrm{tr}(AB)
in the case
\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})\leq\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})<0
.
Define the cone of imaginary matrices in
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
:

\mathcal{C}=\{M\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\>|\>\mathrm{tr}(M^{2})<0\}.

To see that
\mathcal{C}
is a cone, one can reason as at the beginning of Subsection 3.1. Using the form for
A,B
introduced in the previous proof, if
\mathrm{tr}(AB)<0
, then
\lambda\mu>0
, that is
A,B
are in the same connected component. If instead
\mathrm{tr}(AB)>0
, then
\lambda\mu<0
, hence
A,B
are in two different connected component.

In particular, if
\mathrm{tr}(AB)<0
, then the whole segment of velocities

\{uA+(1-u)B\>|\>u\in[0,1]\}

is inside the cone
\mathcal{C}
. In this case, if the singular control is admissible, then it has imaginary eigenvalues, hence singular constant control cannot be optimal (the resulting trajectory in
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
would be periodic, hence bounded). So, in this case the best admissible strategy is the one given in Subsection 4.4.1, and we obtain the third point of Main Theorem.

If instead
\mathrm{tr}(AB)>0
, then part of the segment of velocities is outside the cone, and in particular singular control is admissible since
\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})<0
.

The following Lemma shows that in this case constant singular control are again optimal.

Lemma 6.3
.

If
\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})\leq\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})<0

and

\mathrm{tr}(AB)\geq\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}
,
then

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{-2}{\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})}}+\sqrt{\frac{-2}{\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}}}\mathrm{arcosh}\left(\frac{\mathrm{tr}(AB)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}}\right)\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{tr}(AB)^{2}-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}{2\mathrm{tr}(AB)-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}}.

Proof.

First, notice that the two quantities are homogeneus in
\mathrm{tr}(A^{2}),\mathrm{tr}(B^{2}),\mathrm{tr}(AB)
. So, left hand side can be rewritten as

\frac{2}{\pi}a\frac{\sqrt{b}}{1+\sqrt{b}}\mathrm{arcosh}\left(\frac{-c}{\sqrt{b}}\right),

where
a=\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})}{-2}}
,
b=\frac{\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})}{\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})}
,
c=\frac{\mathrm{tr}(AB)}{\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})}
. From the hypothesis of the Lemma, we deduce
a>0
,
b\geq 1
,
c\leq-\sqrt{b}\leq-1
. Using similar notation, the right hand side of the inequality in the thesis is

a\sqrt{\frac{c^{2}-b}{b+1-2c}}.

Define

\displaystyle f(b,c)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\sqrt{b}}{1+\sqrt{b}}\mathrm{arcosh}\left(\frac{-c}{\sqrt{b}}\right),

\displaystyle g(b,c)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{c^{2}-b}{b+1-2c}}.

Notice that for every
b
, if
c\to-\sqrt{b}
then both functions tend to zero, i.e.:

\forall b\geq 1\quad\lim_{c\to-\sqrt{b}}f(b,c)=0=\lim_{c\to-\sqrt{b}}g(b,c).

Hence, if for every
b\geq 1
and every
c\leq-\sqrt{b}
we have

\frac{\partial f}{\partial c}(b,c)\geq\frac{\partial g}{\partial c}(b,c),

(6.4)

then we obtain
f(b,c)\leq g(b,c)
for every
b\geq 1
and
c\leq-\sqrt{b}
.
These derivatives are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g}{\partial c}(b,c)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\sqrt{b}}{\sqrt{b}+1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{c^{2}-b}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g}{\partial c}(b,c)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{c^{2}-b}}\frac{(c-1)(b-c)}{(b+1-2c)^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

So (
6.4
) reduces to

\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\sqrt{b}}{\sqrt{b}+1}\leq\frac{(1-c)(b-c)}{(b+1-2c)^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

Since we want this inequality to hold for every
c\leq-\sqrt{b}
and since the right hand side is decreasing in
c
, it is sufficient to prove

\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\sqrt{b}}{\sqrt{b}+1}\leq\frac{(1+\sqrt{b})(b+\sqrt{b})}{(b+1+2\sqrt{b})^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

After a few simplifications, this reduces to

(\sqrt{b}+1)^{\frac{3}{2}}\geq\frac{2}{\pi},

which is true for every
b\geq 1
.
∎

This concludes the proof of the fourth case of Main Theorem.

Appendix A
Pontryagin Maximum Principle

In this paper we used a version of Pontryagin Maximum Principle (PMP) which is slightly different from the one that is usually written in books (see, for instance
[
8
]
or
[
4
]
).
So, for completeness, we recall here the precise statement of the PMP that we used in Section 2.

Let
M
be a smooth manifold of dimension
n
,

U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
(f_{u})_{u\in U}
, a family of smooth vector field on
M
. Suppose furthermore that

M\times\overline{U}\ni(q,u)\mapsto f_{u}(q)

and

M\times\overline{U}\ni(q,u)\mapsto\frac{\partial f_{u}}{\partial q}(q)

are continuous.

Fix
T>0
and define
\mathcal{U}=\{u:[0,T]\to U,u\in L_{\textit{loc}}^{\infty}([0,T],U)\}
the space of admissible controls. Fix
q_{0}\in M
and for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
denote with
q_{u}:[0,T]\to M
the solution to the Cauchy Problem

\begin{cases}\dot{q}_{u}(t)=f_{u(t)}(q_{u}(t))\\
q_{u}(0)=q_{0}\end{cases}.

(A.1)

Existence and uniqueness of solution to (
A.1
) are guaranteed by Carathéodory Theorem, see, for instance
[
3
]
.

Let
a:M\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function. We define the cost functional

J(u)=a(q_{u}(T)),\quad u\in\mathcal{U}.

Consider the following optimal control problem:

Problem

(Optimal control)
.

Maximize
J
among all admissible control
u
, i.e., find

\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{U}

such that

\begin{cases}\dot{q}_{\tilde{u}}(t)=f_{\tilde{u}(t)}(q_{\tilde{u}}(t)),\quad t\in[0,T]\\
q_{\tilde{u}}(0)=q_{0}\\
a(q_{\tilde{u}}(T))=\displaystyle\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}a(q_{u}(T))\end{cases}.

(A.2)

A solution
\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{U}
to this problem is called
optimal control
and the corresponding solution to (
A.1
) is called
optimal trajectory
.

Definition A.3

(Attainable set)
.

Fix
q_{0}\in M
. The set of attainable points from
q_{0}
of the control system (
A.1
) at time
T>0
from
q_{0}\in M
is

We recall now some results that we used in Section 2 about Hamiltonian systems on Lie groups. For more detailed reference see Chapter 18 in
[
8
]
and Section 7.6 in
[

.

Let
M\subset GL(N)
be a Lie Group and
\mathcal{M}
its Lie algebra.
Given
q\in M
, define
L_{q}(p)=qp
,
p\in M
, the multiplication on the left by
q
.

Take a Hamiltonian function
h\in C^{\infty}(T^{*}M)
and define

\mathcal{H}(\xi,q)=h(L_{q^{-1}}^{*}\xi,q),\quad\xi\in T_{q}^{*}M,q\in M.

We will call
\mathcal{H}
the trivialized Hamiltonian function of
h
.
We say that
h
is left-invariant if
\mathcal{H}
does not depend on
q
.
In this case, the function
\mathcal{H}
can be seen simply as a smooth function on
\mathcal{M}^{*}
,
hence its differential
\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}
can be seen as a function from
\mathcal{M}^{*}
in
(\mathcal{M}^{*})^{*}=\mathcal{M}
.

Recall the definition of the adjoint representation: given
v\in\mathcal{M}
,
\mathrm{ad}v(w)=[v,w]
, where
w\in\mathcal{M}
.

Proposition A.10
.

Let
M
be a Lie group and take
h\in C^{\infty}(T^{*}M)
. If
h
is left-invariant, then the associated Hamiltonian system can be rewritten as

\begin{cases}\dot{q}=L_{q*}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H},\\
\dot{\xi}=(\mathrm{ad}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H})^{*}\xi.\end{cases}

(A.11)

In our particular case, that is
M=\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathcal{M}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we have that the Killing bilinear form on
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is non-degenerate, so it provides an intrinsic identification between
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{*}
.
That is, for every
\xi\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
there is an
\eta\in\mathcal{M}
such that

K(\eta,v)=\langle\xi,v\rangle\quad\forall v\in\mathcal{M}.

So, one can compute

K(\dot{\eta},v)=\langle\dot{\xi},v\rangle=\langle(\mathrm{ad}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H})^{*}\xi,v\rangle=\langle\xi,(\mathrm{ad}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H})v\rangle=\\
=\langle\xi,[\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H},v]\rangle=K(\eta,[\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H},v])=K([\eta,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}],v),\quad\forall v\in T_{\mathbbm{1}}M.

So, the final form for the Hamiltonian system is

\begin{cases}\dot{q}=L_{q*}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H},\\
\dot{\eta}=[\eta,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}].\end{cases}

(A.12)

Notice that the second equation does not involve
q
.

), we have the following result.

Lemma A.13
.

Let
\mathrm{tr}:\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}
be the trace operator on
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Let us identify the solutions

\xi(\cdot)\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})^{*}

and

\eta(\cdot)\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})

via the Killing form of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, that is

\xi(t)=K(\eta(t),\cdot)
.
Then

\eta(T)=\frac{1}{4}\left(X_{\tilde{u}}(T)-\frac{\mathrm{tr}(X_{\tilde{u}}(T))}{2}I\right).

Proof.

Define
X=X_{\tilde{u}}(T)
and let
U\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Then

\displaystyle\langle\xi(T),XU\rangle=\langle\mathrm{d}_{X}\mathrm{tr},XU\rangle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\mathrm{tr}(Xe^{tU})\right)_{|t=0}=\mathrm{tr}(XU).

Moreover

\mathrm{tr}(XU)=\mathrm{tr}((X-\frac{\mathrm{tr}(X)}{2}I)U)

and

X-\frac{\mathrm{tr}(X)}{2}I\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Recalling that

K(U,V)=4\mathrm{tr}(VU)

for

U,V\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})

and that
K
is non degenerate bilinear form,
it follows that there exists a

Y\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that

K(Y,U)=\langle\mathrm{d}_{X}\mathrm{tr},XU\rangle

for all
U\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Then

4\mathrm{tr}(YU)=\mathrm{tr}\left(\left(X-\frac{\mathrm{tr}(X)}{2}I\right)U\right)\quad\text{for all }U\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R}).

Hence

Y=\frac{1}{4}\left(X-\frac{\mathrm{tr}(X)}{2}I\right)
,
as we wished.
∎
