Spin noise spectroscopy of an alignment-based atomic magnetometer
M. Koźbiał ID, L. Elson ID, L. M. Rushton ID, A. Akbar ID, A. Meraki ID, K. Jensen ID, J. Kołodyński ID

Abstract
Optically pumped magnetometers (OPMs) are revolutionising the task of magnetic-field sensing due to their extremely high sensitivity combined with technological improvements in miniaturisation which have led to compact and portable devices. OPMs can be based on spin-oriented or spin-aligned atomic ensembles which are spin-polarised through optical pumping with circular or linear polarised light, respectively. Characterisation of OPMs and the dynamical properties of their noise is important for applications in real-time sensing tasks. In our work, we experimentally perform spin noise spectroscopy of an alignment-based magnetometer. Moreover, we propose a stochastic model that predicts the noise power spectra exhibited by the device when, apart from the strong magnetic field responsible for the Larmor precession of the spin, white noise is applied in the perpendicular direction aligned with the pumping-probing beam. By varying the strength of the noise applied as well as the linear-polarisation angle of incoming light, we verify the model to accurately predict the heights of the Larmor-induced spectral peaks and their corresponding linewidths. Our work paves the way for alignment-based magnetometers to become operational in real-time sensing tasks.

In what follows, we firstly formalise the description of an atomic steady-state in the spherical-tensor representation, in order to then show how such a formalism allows us to compactly model the magnetometer dynamics, as well as predict the behaviour of the detected signal.

II.1
Spherical tensor representation of the atomic steady state

Any density matrix describing an atom of total angular momentum
F
can always be written in the eigenbasis of the angular-momentum operators
\hat{F}^{2}
and
\hat{F}_{z}
as
Happer 1972
:

\rho^{(F)}=\sum_{M,M^{\prime}=-F}^{F}\rho_{MM^{\prime}}^{(F)}\left|F,M\phantom{{}^{\prime}}\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle F,M^{\prime}\right|.

(1)

However, it is much more convenient to decompose the density matrix in the basis of
spherical tensor operators
of rank
\kappa=0,1,\dots,2F
, which transform independently in each
\kappa
-subspace under rotations, and hence magnetic fields, in a well-behaved manner. In particular, the atomic state of fixed
F
in Eq. (
1
) can be generally written as:

\rho^{(F)}=\sum_{\kappa=0}^{2F}\sum_{q=-\kappa}^{\kappa}m_{\kappa q}^{(F)}\,{{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\kappa)}_{q}\!(F),

(2)

where the density matrix is now decomposed into a sum of rank-
\kappa

components
, each constituting a sum (over
q
) of, in principle, non-Hermitian
tensor operators

{{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\kappa)}_{q}\!(F)
(see e.g.
Happer 1972
and App.
A
) multiplied by their corresponding complex
coefficients

m_{\kappa q}^{(F)}
. For a given fixed
F
,
{{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\kappa)}_{q}
form a basis and they formally satisfy
\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{{{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\kappa)}_{q}({{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\kappa^{\prime})}_{q^{\prime}})^{\dagger}\right\}=\delta_{\kappa\kappa^{\prime}}\delta_{qq^{\prime}}
, while it is convenient to also impose
{{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\kappa){\dagger}}_{q}=(-1)^{q}{{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\kappa)}_{-q}
, so that conditions
m_{\kappa q}=(-1)^{q}m^{*}_{\kappa,-q}
and
\mathrm{Im}\{m_{\kappa 0}\}=0
ensure then the density matrix (
2
) is Hermitian. The
m_{00}=1/\sqrt{2F+1}
coefficient is fixed by the
\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\rho\right\}=1
condition, while the corresponding (
\kappa=0
) tensor operator,
{{\mathcal{T}}}^{(0)}_{0}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2F+1}}\openone_{2F+1}
, is the only one with a non-zero trace, being invariant under any rotations.

In principle, the atomic steady state may involve more than one
F
-level, e.g.
F=3,4
in the case of the D1-line transition in caesium. However, if the laser field is tuned to a specific optical transition from a single
F
-level, and there is no coherent coupling between levels of different
F
, one can disregard coherences between these and most generally write the atomic steady state as:

\rho=\bigoplus_{F}p_{F}\;\rho^{(F)},

(3)

where
p_{F}
is the effective fraction of atoms having the total angular momentum
F
. Moreover, one may then focus on the dynamics of only one particular
\rho^{(F)}
for the
F
-level actually contributing to the light-atom interaction, with
\rho^{(F)}
being then decomposable just as in Eq. (
2
). In such a case, as done in what follows, the
(F)
-superscript can be dropped for simplicity.

As the case of a
linearly polarised
pump is of our interest, see Fig.
1
, from symmetry arguments it follows that, independently of the spin-number

, only even-
\kappa

. Moreover, as we show later, our model predicts no significant coupling between components of different

II.2
Magnetometer dynamics and measurement

In order to be able to predict the PSD in our experiment, we must move away from just the steady-state description. In particular, we must be able to model the stochastic dynamics of the atoms, so that the auto-correlation function of the detected signal can be computed, whose Fourier transform specifies the PSD.

II.2.1
Atomic stochastic dynamics

Dissipative non-unitary evolution.

A dissipative evolution of the atomic state (
1
) is generally described by the Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad equation
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}[\hat{H},\rho]+\Phi[\rho],

(9)

where
\hat{H}
is the system Hamiltonian, while the map

\Phi[\rho]=\sum_{i}\Gamma_{i}(\hat{L}_{i}\rho\hat{L}_{i}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{L}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{i},\rho\})

(10)

is responsible for the decoherence, with
\hat{L}_{i}
being the quantum jump (Lindblad) operators and
\Gamma_{i}\geq 0
the corresponding dissipation rates, which must be non-negative for a Markovian evolution
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
.

In the absence of the
\bm{B}_{\mathrm{noise}}
-field, the Hamiltonian incorporates only the interaction of the atom with the static field, i.e.
\hat{H}=\gamma_{\mathrm{gmr}}\bm{B}_{0}\cdot\hat{\bm{F}}=\Omega_{L}\hat{F}_{z}
. However, as the static field introduces anisotropicity in the system, we split the decoherence map as follows:

\Phi[\rho]\coloneqq\sum_{\alpha=x,y,z}\Phi_{\alpha}[\rho]+\Phi_{\mathrm{iso}}[\rho],

(11)

where

\displaystyle\Phi_{\alpha}[\rho]

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha}}{\hbar^{2}}(\hat{F}_{\alpha}\rho\hat{F}_{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{F}_{\alpha}^{2},\rho\})

(12)

can be interpreted as arising from magnetic-field fluctuation in each
\alpha=x,y,z
direction, whereas:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathrm{iso}}[\rho]

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Gamma_{\mathrm{iso}}\!\left(\sum_{M,M^{\prime}}\!\!\hat{L}_{M,M^{\prime}}\rho\hat{L}_{M,M^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\!-\!\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{L}_{M,M^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{M,M^{\prime}},\rho\}\right)

(13)

with
\hat{L}_{M,M^{\prime}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2F+1}}\left|F,M\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle F,M^{\prime}\right|
, represents isotropic dissipation that can be equivalently written as

\Phi_{\mathrm{iso}}[\rho]=\Lambda-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{\Gamma},\rho\},

(14)

where
\hat{\Gamma}\coloneqq\Gamma_{\mathrm{iso}}\openone_{2F+1}
and
\Lambda\coloneqq\frac{\Gamma_{\mathrm{iso}}}{2F+1}\,\openone_{2F+1}
are typically referred to as the re-population and depolarising terms, respectively
Auzinsh et al. 2014
. Although we assume in our model the rates
\Gamma_{\alpha}
and
\Gamma_{\mathrm{iso}}
to be phenomenological and account for various dissipation mechanisms, Eq. (
14
) can be naturally interpreted as the loss of polarised atoms that then reappear in the beam in a completely unpolarised state.

Impact of the stochastic
\bm{B}_{\mathrm{noise}}
-field.

In our experiment, see Fig.
1
(c), stochastic
\bm{B}_{\mathrm{noise}}
-field is applied in the light-propagation direction
x
, which leads to another term in the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{noise}}(t)=\gamma_{\mathrm{gmr}}\bm{B}_{\mathrm{noise}}(t)\cdot\hat{\bm{F}}=\gamma_{\mathrm{gmr}}B_{\mathrm{noise}}(t)\hat{F}_{x}
with

B_{\mathrm{noise}}(t)=\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{noise}}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{gmr}}}\,\xi(t)\;\approx\;\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{noise}}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{gmr}}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi\Delta f}}\frac{\mathrm{d}W_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t},

(15)

where
\Omega_{\mathrm{noise}}
is an effective magnetic noise amplitude given in the units of Larmor frequency, while
\xi(t)
represents the stochastic process for the noise we generate, see App.
C
for its further characteristics, which effectively exhibits a constant power spectrum in a frequency range
f\in[\delta\!f,f_{\textrm{cutoff}}]
(in Hz) with
\delta\!f
set close to zero to eliminate spurious low-frequency contributions, and some large cut-off
f_{\textrm{cutoff}}
imposed to prevent the effect of aliasing. Now, as we will deal with processes occurring at (angular) frequencies
\omega\approx\Omega_{L}
, such noise in Eq. (
15
) can be effectively described as white with the correct rescaling factor
\Delta f\coloneqq f_{\textrm{cutoff}}-\delta\!f\approx f_{\textrm{cutoff}}
—the white noise can be interpreted as the time-derivative,
\frac{\mathrm{d}W}{\mathrm{d}t}
, of the Wiener process,
W_{t}

Gardiner 1985
. As a result, we can write the (stochastic) time-increment induced by the noise involving the Hamiltonian as

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{noise}}(t)\mathrm{d}t=\sqrt{\omega_{\mathrm{noise}}}\,\hat{F}_{x}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(16)

where we define
\omega_{\mathrm{noise}}\coloneqq\Omega_{\mathrm{noise}}^{2}/(2\pi f_{\textrm{cutoff}})
as the effective
noise spectral density
, while
\mathrm{d}W_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{d}t)
is then normally distributed with variance
\mathrm{d}t
, i.e. the Wiener increment
Gardiner 1985
.

In order to correctly include the white noise in the deterministic dynamics (
9
), we must explicitly compute the time-increment of the density matrix,
\mathrm{d}\rho=\rho(t+\mathrm{d}t)-\rho(t)
, that is now stochastic. By adding the noise contribution to Eq. (
9
), we define the stochastic map

\mathcal{K}(t)[\rho]\coloneqq-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{\mathrm{noise}}(t)+\hat{H},\rho]+\Phi[\rho],

(17)

which allows us to write for small
\mathrm{d}t
:

\displaystyle\rho(t+\mathrm{d}t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{K}(t)\mathrm{d}t}\left[\rho(t)\right]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\mathcal{K}(t)\mathrm{d}t)^{n}[\rho(t)]}{n!}

\displaystyle=\rho(t)+\mathcal{K}[\rho(t)]\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{K}\mathrm{d}t)^{2}[\rho(t)]+O(\mathrm{d}t^{5/2})

\displaystyle=\rho(t)-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}[\hat{H},\rho(t)]\mathrm{d}t+\Phi[\rho(t)]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle-\frac{\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\omega_{\mathrm{noise}}}}{\hbar}[\hat{F}_{x},\rho(t)]\mathrm{d}W_{t}-\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{noise}}}{2\hbar^{2}}[\hat{F}_{x},[\hat{F}_{x},\rho(t)]]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+O(\mathrm{d}t^{3/2}),

(18)

where according to the Itô calculus implying
\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{2}=\mathrm{d}t
we obtain a dissipative term at the second (
n=2
) order, while all the other terms can be ignored with
\mathrm{d}W_{t}\mathrm{d}t=O(\mathrm{d}t^{3/2})
within the big-
O
notation
Gardiner 1985
.

As a consequence, we obtain the desired stochastic differential equation describing the atomic dynamics as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\rho

\displaystyle=(-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}[\hat{H},\rho]+\Phi_{\mathrm{iso}}[\rho])\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\sum_{\alpha=y,z}\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha}}{\hbar^{2}}(\hat{F}_{\alpha}\rho\hat{F}_{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{F}_{\alpha}^{2},\rho\})\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\frac{\Gamma_{x}+\omega_{\mathrm{noise}}}{\hbar^{2}}(\hat{F}_{x}\rho\hat{F}_{x}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{F}_{x}^{2},\rho\})\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle-\frac{\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\omega_{\mathrm{noise}}}}{\hbar}[\hat{F}_{x},\rho]\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(19)

which, apart from the expected term generating random rotations around the
\bm{B}_{\mathrm{noise}}
-field direction, accounts for the fact that (by the fluctuation-dissipation theorem) the white noise must also increase the dissipation rate in the
x
-direction from
\Gamma_{x}
to
\Gamma_{x}+\omega_{\mathrm{noise}}
.

II.2.2
Spherical-tensor representation

We have argued that when considering the relevant
F
-subspace of the atomic steady state described in Eq. (
2
), the nature and geometry of light-atom interactions allows us to reduce its form, so that it contains only the alignment component (
\kappa=2
) in Eq. (
4
). Consistently, as shown in App.
A
, the evolution determined by Eq. (
19
) does not couple spherical-tensor components of different rank
\kappa
. Hence, while incorporating optical pumping into the dynamics (
19
), the evolution of the atomic state must be completely described by the
m
-vector (
5
) of, now time-dependent, alignment coefficients, i.e.:

\textbf{m}_{t}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}m_{2,-2}(t)&m_{2,-1}(t)&m_{2,0}(t)&m_{2,1}(t)&m_{2,2}(t)\end{array}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}.

(20)

This evolves under the dynamics (
19
) according to the following stochastic differential equation, see App.
A
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\textbf{m}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\textbf{A}_{0}+\textbf{A}_{\Phi}+\frac{\textbf{A}_{\mathrm{noise}}^{2}}{2}\right)\textbf{m}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\textbf{A}_{\mathrm{noise}}\,\textbf{m}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(21)

where
\textbf{A}_{0}=-\mathrm{i}\Omega_{L}\textbf{J}^{(2)}_{z}
and
\textbf{A}_{\mathrm{noise}}=-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\omega_{\mathrm{noise}}}\textbf{J}^{(2)}_{x}
are
5\times 5
matrices that should be associated with the free evolution and stochastic noise, respectively. These are defined with help of the representation of angular momentum operators,
\textbf{J}^{(\kappa)}_{\alpha}
with
\alpha=x,y,z
, acting on the vector space of alignment—equivalent to ones acting on the state-space of a spin-2 particle, written in the
\{\left|2,-2\right\rangle,\dots,\left|2,2\right\rangle\}
basis to agree with Eq. (
20
).

In a similar fashion, the matrix-representation of the dissipative map
\Phi
in Eq. (
11
), see App.
A
, reads

\displaystyle\textbf{A}_{\Phi}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha}\Gamma_{\alpha}(\textbf{J}^{(2)}_{\alpha})^{2}-\Gamma_{\mathrm{iso}}\openone_{5}

\displaystyle\equiv-\textrm{diag}\{\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}\},

(22)

which we, however, force above to have a diagonal form postulated in Ref.
Weis et al. 2006
with three dissipation rates:

\bm{\Gamma}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma_{0}\\
\Gamma_{1}\\
\Gamma_{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}6\Gamma_{\perp}+\Gamma_{\mathrm{iso}}\\
\Gamma_{\parallel}+5\Gamma_{\perp}+\Gamma_{\mathrm{iso}}\\
4\Gamma_{\parallel}+2\Gamma_{\perp}+\Gamma_{\mathrm{iso}}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(23)

Formally, this corresponds to the assumption that in the absence of the induced magnetic-field noise one should differentiate only between dephasing rates along,
\Gamma_{\parallel}\coloneqq\Gamma_{z}
, and perpendicular to,
\Gamma_{\perp}\coloneqq\Gamma_{x}=\Gamma_{y}
, the static field, while keeping the isotropic depolarisation rate,
\Gamma_{\mathrm{iso}}
, as an independent parameter. The effective rates
\bm{\Gamma}
, see also App.
A
, are then defined by reparametrising the problem as in Eq. (
23
).

Finally, in order to include the impact of optical pumping in Eq. (
21
), we enforce that, in the absence of the noisy magnetic field, the
\textbf{m}_{t}
-vector (
20
) must converge with time to the steady state described in Sec.
II.1
. This way, we obtain the desired stochastic dynamical equation for the vector of alignment coefficients as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\textbf{m}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\textbf{A}_{0}+\frac{\textbf{A}_{\mathrm{noise}}^{2}}{2}\right)\textbf{m}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\textbf{A}_{\Phi}(\textbf{m}_{t}-\textbf{m}^{\mathrm{ss}})\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\textbf{A}_{\mathrm{noise}}\textbf{m}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(24)

where the steady state
\textbf{m}^{\mathrm{ss}}
is given by Eq. (
7
), being already averaged over the fast (Larmor) precession around
z
and, hence, satisfying
\textbf{A}_{0}\textbf{m}^{\mathrm{ss}}=\textbf{0}
.

II.2.3
Detected signal

We depict the phenomenon of polarisation rotation in Fig.
1
(c), i.e. the effect that the angle
\theta
of the linearly polarised incoming beam is changed to
\theta^{\prime}
upon leaving the atomic cell. Treating the atomic ensemble as an optically thin sample, the change of the angle,
\Delta\theta\coloneqq\theta^{\prime}-\theta
, obeys then
Auzinsh et al. 2014
;
Meraki et al. 2023
:

\Delta\theta\propto\mathrm{i}(\tilde{m}_{2,1}+\tilde{m}_{2,-1}),

(25)

with the proportionality constant depending on the optical depth, interaction strength, light power etc.

In the above,
\tilde{m}_{\kappa,q}
are the alignment coefficients defined with the quantisation axis along the direction of incoming light polarisation, i.e. tilted away by
\theta
from
z
in the
yz
-plane, see Fig.
1
(c). Hence, Eq. (
25
) can be re-expressed with help of the
\textbf{m}_{t}
-vector (
20
) (defined with
z
being the quantisation axis) by simply rotating
\textbf{m}_{t}
by an angle
\theta
around the light-propagation direction
x
, i.e.:

\Delta\theta\propto\textbf{h}^{\mathrm{T}}\,\textbf{D}^{(2)}_{\theta}\textbf{m}_{t},

(26)

where
\textbf{h}=(0,\mathrm{i},0,\mathrm{i},0)^{\mathrm{T}}
and
\textbf{D}^{(2)}_{\theta}=D^{(2)}_{m,m^{\prime}}(-\pi/2,\theta,\pi/2)
is the appropriate Wigner D-matrix, see App.
A
.

As a result, based on Eq. (
26
), we may write the detected signal of an alignment-based magnetometer as:

\displaystyle S(t)

\displaystyle=g_{D}\,\textbf{h}^{\mathrm{T}}\,\textbf{D}^{(2)}_{\theta}\textbf{m}_{t}+\zeta(t)

\displaystyle=g_{D}\,\tfrac{1}{2}[\sqrt{6}m_{2,0}(t)+m_{2,2}(t)+m_{2,-2}(t)]\sin(2\theta)

\displaystyle+g_{D}\,\mathrm{i}[m_{2,1}(t)+m_{2,-1}(t)]\cos(2\theta)+\zeta(t),

(27)

where
g_{D}
is the effective proportionality constant, whereas
\zeta(t)
denotes the detection noise, which is completely uncorrelated from the magnetic-field noise affecting the atom dynamics in Eq. (
24
). Moreover, as we assume here the impact of the measurement backaction exerted on the atomic state by the light to be ignorable
Deutsch and Jessen 2010
;
Troullinou et al. 2021
,
\zeta(t)
shall not exhibit any correlations with any noise exhibited by the atoms.

It becomes clear from the expression (
27
) that, as the signal depends on all the alignment coefficients with
q=0,\pm 1,\pm 2
, it must contain components that oscillate at frequencies
0
,
\Omega_{L}
and
2\Omega_{L}
, respectively. In other words, the signal contains information about different spherical-tensor components of the atomic state, in particular the ones illustrated in Fig.
1
(d), each of which should yield a peak in the PSD at the corresponding frequency.

III
Spin noise spectroscopy

Denoting the Fourier transform of any signal, here the measured current of the balanced photodetector
\delta S(t)
defined in Eq. (
28
), over a finite-time interval
[0,T]
as:

\delta S(\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\delta S(t),

(29)

its
power spectral density
(PSD) is defined as
Sinitsyn and Pershin 2016
:

\mathrm{PSD}(\omega)\coloneqq\left\langle|\delta S(\omega)|^{2}\right\rangle,

(30)

where by
\left\langle\dots\right\rangle
we denote throughout the article an average over stochastic trajectories.

Importantly, provided that
\delta S(t)
is stationary and ergodic, which can be assured by letting
T\gg\tau_{\mathrm{coh}}
in Eq. (
29
) where
\tau_{\mathrm{coh}}
is some effective coherence time of the noisy system under study, we may rewrite the power spectrum according to the Wiener-Khinchin theorem as
Sinitsyn and Pershin 2016
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{PSD}(\omega)\;

\displaystyle\underset{\textrm{ss}}{=}\;2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\cos(\omega t)\left\langle\delta S(t)\delta S(0)\right\rangle

(31a)

\displaystyle\underset{\textrm{ss}}{=}\;2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\cos(\omega t)\left\langle S(t),S(0)\right\rangle

(31b)

where by “ss”, as before, we emphasise the above to hold in the steady state. In particular, see Eq. (
31a
), the PSD (
30
) can be expressed in terms of the
auto-correlation function
of the (zero-mean) signal
\delta S(t)
; or equivalently, see Eq. (
31b
), the
auto-covariance function
of the actual detected signal,
S(t)
specified in Eq. (
27
), i.e.
\left\langle S(t_{1}),S(t_{2})\right\rangle\coloneqq\left\langle S(t_{1})S(t_{2})\right\rangle-\left\langle S(t_{1})\right\rangle\left\langle S(t_{2})\right\rangle

Gardiner 1985
.

Substituting explicitly the form of the detected signal (
27
) into Eq. (
31b
), we obtain the form of the PSD applicable to our problem as

\mathrm{PSD}(\omega)=g_{D}^{2}\,\textbf{h}^{\mathrm{T}}\,\textbf{D}^{(2)}_{\theta}\mathbf{\Xi}(\omega)\textbf{D}^{(2)\mathrm{T}}_{\theta}\textbf{h}+\left\langle|\zeta(\omega)|^{2}\right\rangle,

(32)

where the (5x5) matrix
\mathbf{\Xi}(\omega)
is defined as:

\Xi_{pq}(\omega)\coloneqq 2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\cos(\omega t)\langle m_{2,p}(t),m_{2,q}(0)\rangle,

(33)

with
p,q=-2,..,2
specifying the coefficients of the
\textbf{m}_{t}
-vector (
20
) evaluated in the steady state. As the detection noise
\zeta(\omega)
is uncorrelated from any other noise sources within our model, it leads to a noise floor in Eq. (
30

IV
Conclusions

We prepare a detailed dynamical model allowing us to predict spin-noise spectra of an alignment-based magnetometer, which we then verify experimentally. Its applicability relies on the presence of the excess white-noise being applied along the propagation direction of the light beam that is used to both pump and probe the atomic ensemble, which is perpendicular to the direction of the strong magnetic field responsible for Larmor precession.

On the one hand, the added noise amplifies the Larmor-induced peaks to be clearly visible within the power spectral density above the level of detection noise. As a result, our model describing the spin noise in the device can now be used by signal-processing tools interpret the detection data better—e.g., when operating the device as a scalar magnetometer, Bayesian inference methods such as the Kalman filter
Jiménez-Martínez et al. 2018
can be applied to track fast changes of the strong magnetic field in real time beyond the magnetometer bandwidth
Wilson et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2020
. On the other hand, the induced noise can be used to naturally perform sensing tasks in the so-called
covert
manner
Hao et al. 2022
, i.e. so that an adversary having access to the output of the magnetometer would not be able to recover the signal without possessing the pre-calibrated dynamical model that we propose. In this sense, we expect our model to be useful also for tracking time-varying signals encoded in oscillating RF-fields (amplitude/phase) directed perpendicularly to both the scalar field and the added noise.

It would be interesting to generalise our model to a device that operates at the quantum limit
Zapasskii et al. 2013
;
Glasenapp et al. 2014
;
Shah et al. 2010
, i.e. with detection noise being small enough, so that the predicted peaks in the spectrum arise without the need to artificially amplify them by applying the excess classical noise. Moreover, similarly to orientation-based magnetometers
Lucivero et al. 2016
;
Troullinou et al. 2021
;
Lucivero et al. 2017
, maybe such model could be also capable to incorporate the effect of pumping and probing the ensemble with squeezed light, so the detection noise can be even further reduced. We leave such open questions for the future.

Experimental data created during this research are openly available from the University of Nottingham data repository at
https://doi.org/10.17639/nott.7434
.

Appendix A
Evolution in the basis of spherical tensor operators

The evolution of the atomic ensemble without the effect of pumping, described by Eq. (
19
), is written in terms of the density matrix (
1
). In this appendix we show how to write it in terms of the vector of all spherical tensor components
\textbf{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{full}}
. In particular, since the right-hand side of the equation is linear in terms of
\rho
, we would like to find specific linear operators that act on
\textbf{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{full}}
that correspond to specific operations performed on
\rho
, and enable us to write the dynamical equation in the form of (
21
). We also show that, since the resulting operators do not couple spherical tensor components of different rank, we only need the rank-2 component for a full description of the problem under consideration.

A.1
Rotations induced by magnetic fields

Spherical-tensor operators,
{{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\kappa)}_{q}
, are defined by the decomposition of the density matrix (
1
) with respect to irreducible representations of the 3D-rotation group, i.e for each
\kappa=0,..,2F
, operators
{{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\kappa)}_{q}
with
q=-\kappa,...,\kappa
form a basis of a
(2\kappa+1)
-dimensional irreducible representation. These operators form a convenient basis for density matrices of a system with fixed angular momentum
F
:

{\rho}=\sum_{\kappa=0}^{2F}\sum_{q=-\kappa}^{\kappa}m_{\kappa q}{{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\kappa)}_{q},

(43)

with a scalar product
\mathrm{Tr}\Big[{{{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\kappa)}_{q}}^{\dagger}{{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\kappa^{\prime})}_{q^{\prime}}\Big]=\delta_{\kappa\kappa^{\prime}}\delta_{qq^{\prime}}
, and with well-defined behaviour under rotations generated by the angular-momentum operators, i.e. a vector
\hat{\bm{F}}:=(\hat{F}_{x},\hat{F}_{y},\hat{F}_{z})^{T}
. In particular, any
SO(3)
rotation can be parameterised either by the axis (represented by a normalised vector
\bm{n}
), and angle of rotation
\varphi
:

\hat{\mathcal{R}}(\bm{n},\varphi)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\varphi\bm{n}\cdot\hat{\bm{F}}},

(44)

or by the three Euler angles
\phi
,
\theta
,
\psi
that correspond to subsequent rotations about the
z
-,
y
- and again the
z
-axes, respectively:

\hat{\mathcal{R}}(\psi,\theta,\phi)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\psi\hat{F}_{z}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta\hat{F}_{y}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\phi\hat{F}_{z}}.

(45)

We now use the operator above to define the Wigner D-matrix:

\displaystyle D^{(F)}_{M,M^{\prime}}(\psi,\theta,\phi):=\langle F,M|\hat{\mathcal{R}}(\psi,\theta,\phi)|F,M^{\prime}\rangle.

(46)

The spherical tensor operators behave analogously to angular momentum eigenstates under rotations if we treat the rank
\kappa
as the total angular momentum, and the index
q
as the projection on the
z
axis:

\hat{\mathcal{R}}(\psi,\theta,\phi){{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\kappa)}_{q^{\prime}}\hat{\mathcal{R}}(\psi,\theta,\phi)^{\dagger}=\sum_{q=-\kappa}^{\kappa}D^{(\kappa)}_{q,q^{\prime}}(\psi,\theta,\phi){{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\kappa)}_{q}.

(47)

This means that for the spherical tensor component vector
\textbf{m}_{t}^{(\kappa)}
composed of
m_{\kappa,q}(t)
coefficients (
q=-\kappa,...,\kappa
), the rotation generators are
\textbf{J}^{(\kappa)}_{x}
,
\textbf{J}^{(\kappa)}_{y}
and
\textbf{J}^{(\kappa)}_{z}
, which are the matrix representations of angular momentum operators cut to the subspace of total angular momentum
\kappa
.

This simplifies the study of dynamics of
\rho
under rotations, but also facilitates the description of light-atom interactions.
Whenever one considers dipole-type interactions, which correspond to multiplying two (dipole) vectors, spherical-tensor components with
\kappa\leq 2
describing the atom are sufficient to find the output light state.
Happer 1972
;
Bevilacqua et al. 2014
.

We use these properties of the spherical tensor to find the expressions for the evolution of the atomic state described by Eq. (
19
) in the spherical tensor basis. Since the Hamiltonian resulting from the magnetic field generates the rotation of the atomic state about the magnetic field vector
\bm{B}
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\mathrm{i}[\gamma_{\mathrm{gmr}}\bm{B}\cdot\hat{\bm{F}},\rho],

(48)

the evolution in the spherical tensor basis will also be driven by respective rotation generators:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\textbf{m}_{t}^{(\kappa)}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\mathrm{i}\gamma_{\mathrm{gmr}}(\bm{B}\cdot\bm{J}^{(\kappa)})\textbf{m}_{t}^{(\kappa)},

(49)

where
\bm{J}^{(\kappa)}=\left(\textbf{J}^{(\kappa)}_{x},\textbf{J}^{(\kappa)}_{y},\textbf{J}^{(\kappa)}_{z}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}
.

This can be directly shown using the commutation relations for the spherical tensor operators and the angular momentum operators:

\displaystyle[\hat{F}_{z},{{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\kappa)}_{q}]

\displaystyle=\hbar q{{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\kappa)}_{q},

(50)

\displaystyle[\hat{F}_{\pm},{{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\kappa)}_{q}]

\displaystyle=\hbar\sqrt{\kappa(\kappa+1)-q(q\pm 1)}{{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\kappa)}_{\pm q},

(51)

that enable us to find the exact form of the matrix:

\displaystyle(\textbf{A}_{0})^{(\kappa),q}_{(\kappa^{\prime}),q^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{i}\Omega_{L}}{\hbar}\mathrm{Tr}\{{{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\kappa){\dagger}}_{q}[\hat{F}_{z},{{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\kappa^{\prime})}_{q^{\prime}}]\}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\Omega_{L}q\delta_{\kappa\kappa^{\prime}}\delta_{qq^{\prime}}=-\mathrm{i}\Omega_{L}(\textbf{J}^{(\kappa)}_{z})^{q}_{q^{\prime}}.

(52)

Using Eq. (
51
) we could analogously find that commuting the density matrix with
\hat{F}_{x}
and
\hat{F}_{y}
is equivalent to acting the operators
\textbf{J}^{(\kappa)}_{x}
and
\textbf{J}^{(\kappa)}_{y}
, respectively, on
\textbf{m}_{t}^{(\kappa)}
. We use this to also obtain:

\textbf{A}_{\mathrm{noise}}=-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\omega_{\mathrm{noise}}}\textbf{J}^{(2)}_{x}.

(53)

Let us now note that commuting density matrix twice with some operator:

[\hat{F}_{\alpha},[\hat{F}_{\alpha},\rho]]=2\hat{F}_{\alpha}\rho\hat{F}_{\alpha}-\{\hat{F}_{\alpha}^{2},\rho\}

(54)

is then equivalent to acting with the square of the respective operator
\textbf{J}^{(\kappa)}_{\alpha}
. This is responsible for the appearance of the
\textbf{A}_{\mathrm{noise}}^{2}/2
term in Eq. (
21
). In either case, the operation does not couple spherical tensor coefficients of different rank.

A.2
Decoherence

We would also like to find the correct description of decoherence using the spherical tensor coefficients. It is driven by non-unitary evolution, described by an operator:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}t}=\Phi[\rho]=\sum_{\alpha}\Phi_{\alpha}[\rho]+\Phi_{\mathrm{iso}}[\rho],

(55)

where
\Phi_{\alpha}[\rho]
is the part of dissipation that comes form the unknown fluctuations of the magnetic field:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\alpha}[\rho]

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\alpha}(\hat{F}_{\alpha}\rho\hat{F}_{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{F}_{\alpha}^{2},\rho\}),

(56)

and the isotropic part of the dissipation reads:

\Phi_{\mathrm{iso}}[\rho]=\Lambda-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{\Gamma},\rho\},

(57)

where
\hat{\Gamma}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{iso}}\openone_{2F+1}
and
\Lambda=\frac{\Gamma_{\mathrm{iso}}}{2F+1}\,\openone_{2F+1}
. It causes the decay of all matrix components at the same rate
\Gamma_{\mathrm{iso}}
(
-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{\Gamma},\rho\}
) and pumps the
m_{0,0}
components in the rate that balances the decay (
\Lambda
). This means that all of the
\kappa\neq 0
components of the spherical tensor decay at the same rate
\Gamma_{\mathrm{iso}}
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}m_{\kappa,q}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\Gamma_{\mathrm{iso}}m_{\kappa,q}.

(58)

The operator (
56
), on the other hand, is proportional to a double commutator of Eq. (
54
), therefore we obtain the following operator of decoherence in the spherical-tensor basis:

\displaystyle\textbf{A}_{\Phi}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha}\Gamma_{\alpha}(\textbf{J}^{(2)}_{\alpha})^{2}-\Gamma_{\mathrm{iso}}\openone_{5},

(59)

which for the case
\Gamma_{x}=\Gamma_{y}=\Gamma_{\perp}
,
\Gamma_{z}=\Gamma_{\parallel}
takes the form:

\textbf{A}_{\Phi}=-\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\Gamma_{2}&0&0&0&0\\
0&\Gamma_{1}&0&0&0\\
0&0&\Gamma_{0}&0&0\\
0&0&0&\Gamma_{1}&0\\
0&0&0&0&\Gamma_{2}\end{array}\right),

(60)

with:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}

\displaystyle=3\Gamma_{\perp}+\Gamma_{\mathrm{iso}},

(61)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\Gamma_{\parallel}+5\Gamma_{\perp})+\Gamma_{\mathrm{iso}},

(62)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}

\displaystyle=2\Gamma_{\parallel}+\Gamma_{\perp}+\Gamma_{\mathrm{iso}}.

(63)

We see again that spherical tensor coefficients of different rank do not couple, so we can limit our considerations to the rank-2 component, relevant to our physical system.

Appendix B
Noise spectrum resulting from an inhomogeneous linear SDE

For the purpose of predicting the spin-noise spectrum, we need to consider the following multiple-variable stochastic differential equation:

\mathrm{d}\textbf{m}_{t}=(\textbf{v}+\textbf{F}\textbf{m}_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\textbf{G}\textbf{m}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(64)

where
\textbf{m}_{t}
is an evolving vector, while operators
F
and
G
together with vector
v
parameterise the evolution of the system. The equation is linear and inhomogeneous. Equations like this, in general, are not analytically solvable if the operators
F
and
G
do not commute. However, we only need to calculate the Fourier transform of the time-autocovariance matrix:

\bm{\Xi}(t)=\langle\textbf{m}_{0},\textbf{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}=\langle\textbf{m}_{0}\textbf{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}-\langle\textbf{m}_{0}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}\langle\textbf{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}.

(65)

In order to obtain the steady-state mean value
\langle\,.\,\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}
, we average over the possible paths of the stochastic process
W_{t}
given particular value of the initial point
\textbf{m}_{0}
, and then separately over all values of the initial point. We indicate these subsequent stochastic averages explicitly by the distinct subscripts, i.e.:

\langle\textbf{m}_{0}\textbf{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}=\langle\textbf{m}_{0}\langle\textbf{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle_{W_{t}|\textbf{m}_{0}}\rangle_{\textbf{m}_{0}}.

(66)

Path-averaging of Eq. (
64
), which cancels the stochasic increment, leads to

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\textbf{m}_{t}\rangle_{W_{t}|\textbf{m}_{0}}=\textbf{v}+\textbf{F}\langle\textbf{m}_{t}\rangle_{W_{t}|\textbf{m}_{0}},

(67)

which yields the following evolution of the average value:

\langle\textbf{m}_{t}\rangle_{W_{t}}=-\textbf{F}^{-1}\textbf{v}+\mathrm{e}^{\textbf{F}t}\left(\textbf{m}_{0}+\textbf{F}^{-1}\textbf{v}\right).

(68)

The steady-state mean of
\textbf{m}_{t}
, which is only obtained if all eigenvalues of
F
have negative real parts, can be found by taking the limit
t\rightarrow+\infty
:

\langle\textbf{m}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}=-\textbf{F}^{-1}\textbf{v},

(69)

where we drop the subscript
t
in
m
, because by definition the steady-state mean value does not evolve.

Finally, by substituting the expressions (
68
) and (
69
) into Eq. (
65
) we obtain:

\langle\textbf{m}_{0},\textbf{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}=\left[\langle\textbf{m}\textbf{m}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}-\textbf{F}^{-1}\textbf{v}\textbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}(\textbf{F}^{-1})^{\mathrm{T}}\right]\mathrm{e}^{\textbf{F}^{\mathrm{T}}t},

(70)

which is true for
t\geq 0
.
An analogous result for
t<0
can be found by the use of the fact that for the steady state:

\langle\textbf{m}_{0},\textbf{m}_{-t}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}=\langle\textbf{m}_{t},\textbf{m}_{0}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}=\langle\textbf{m}_{0},\textbf{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle^{\mathrm{T}},

(71)

so that for any
t
we can finally write:

\displaystyle\langle\textbf{m}_{0},\textbf{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}=

(72)

\displaystyle\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left[\langle\textbf{m}\textbf{m}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}-\textbf{F}^{-1}\textbf{v}\textbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}(\textbf{F}^{-1})^{\mathrm{T}}\right]\mathrm{e}^{\textbf{F}^{\mathrm{T}}t}&\textrm{for }t<0,\\
\mathrm{e}^{-\textbf{F}^{\mathrm{T}}t}\left[\langle\textbf{m}\textbf{m}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}-\textbf{F}^{-1}\textbf{v}\textbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}(\textbf{F}^{-1})^{\mathrm{T}}\right]&\textrm{for }t\geq 0.\\
\end{array}\right.

In order to get the mean steady-state value of
\langle\textbf{m}\textbf{m}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle
, we need to find

\mathrm{d}(\textbf{m}_{t}\textbf{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}})=\mathrm{d}\textbf{m}_{t}\,\textbf{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}+\textbf{m}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}+\mathrm{d}\textbf{m}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}.

(75)

The
\mathrm{d}\textbf{m}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}
term is necessary to account for the property of the stochastic increment that
\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{2}=\mathrm{d}t
. We substitute
\mathrm{d}\textbf{m}_{t}
from Eq. (
64
), take the average, divide by
\mathrm{d}t
and obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\textbf{m}_{t}\textbf{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\textbf{v}\langle\textbf{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle+\langle\textbf{m}_{t}\rangle\textbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}+\textbf{F}\langle\textbf{m}_{t}\textbf{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle\textbf{m}_{t}\textbf{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle\textbf{F}^{\mathrm{T}}+\textbf{G}\langle\textbf{m}_{t}\textbf{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle\textbf{G}^{\mathrm{T}},

(76)

which equals 0 in the steady state. Hence, after substituting
\langle\textbf{m}_{t}\rangle=\langle\textbf{m}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}
from (
69
), we obtain:

\textbf{F}\,\bm{\sigma}+\bm{\sigma}\,\textbf{F}^{\mathrm{T}}+\textbf{G}\,\bm{\sigma}\,\textbf{G}^{\mathrm{T}}=\textbf{v}\,\textbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}\,(\textbf{F}^{\mathrm{T}})^{-1}+\textbf{F}^{-1}\,\textbf{v}\,\textbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}},

(77)

where
\bm{\sigma}=\langle\textbf{m}\,\textbf{m}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}
. Importantly, in case we are given a specific set of
v
,
F
and
G
, we can solve (
77
) as a system of linear equations to find
\bm{\sigma}
.

Possessing a particular form of
\bm{\sigma}
, we can explicitly calculate the Fourier transform of
\bm{\Xi}(t)
, i.e.:

\displaystyle\bm{\Xi}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{e}^{-\textbf{F}^{\mathrm{T}}t}\left[\langle\textbf{m}\textbf{m}^{\mathrm{T}}\rangle-\textbf{F}^{-1}\textbf{v}\textbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}(\textbf{F}^{-1})^{\mathrm{T}}\right]

(78)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{+\infty}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}t\left[\bm{\sigma}-\textbf{F}^{-1}\textbf{v}\textbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}(\textbf{F}^{\mathrm{T}})^{-1}\right]\exp\left[(-\mathrm{i}\omega+\textbf{F}^{\mathrm{T}})t\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}(\textbf{F}+\mathrm{i}\omega)^{-1}\left[\bm{\sigma}-\textbf{F}^{-1}\textbf{v}\textbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}(\textbf{F}^{\mathrm{T}})^{-1}\right]

(79)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\bm{\sigma}-\textbf{F}^{-1}\textbf{v}\textbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}(\textbf{F}^{\mathrm{T}})^{-1}\right](\textbf{F}^{\mathrm{T}}-\mathrm{i}\omega)^{-1}.

We can simplify this formula by using Eq. (
77
) and finding that:

(\textbf{F}+\mathrm{i}\omega)\bm{\Xi}(\omega)(\textbf{F}^{\mathrm{T}}-\mathrm{i}\omega)=\textbf{G}\,\bm{\sigma}\,\textbf{G}^{\mathrm{T}},

(80)

so we obtain:

\bm{\Xi}(\omega)=(\textbf{F}+\mathrm{i}\omega)^{-1}\textbf{G}\,\bm{\sigma}\,\textbf{G}^{\mathrm{T}}(\textbf{F}^{\mathrm{T}}-\mathrm{i}\omega)^{-1}.

(81)

If the signal is a linear combination of the
\textbf{m}_{t}
components, parameterised by a vector
k
of the same dimension as
\textbf{m}_{t}
:

S(t)=\textbf{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\,\textbf{x}_{t},

(82)

then the PSD is given by:

\displaystyle\mathrm{PSD}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\textbf{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\,\bm{\Xi}(\omega)\,\textbf{k}=

\displaystyle=\textbf{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\,(\textbf{F}+\mathrm{i}\omega)^{-1}\textbf{G}\,\bm{\sigma}\,\textbf{G}^{\mathrm{T}}(\textbf{F}^{\mathrm{T}}-\mathrm{i}\omega)^{-1}\,\textbf{k}.

(83)

B.1
Effective Lorentzian form

To find the functional form of
\mathrm{PSD}(\omega)
one can write Eq. (
83
) in the eigenbasis of the
F
matrix. If we write the eigenvalues of
F
as
\lambda_{\alpha}=-\gamma_{\alpha}+\mathrm{i}\omega_{\alpha}
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{PSD}(\omega)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\frac{k_{\alpha}k_{\beta}Q_{\alpha\beta}}{[-\gamma_{\alpha}+\mathrm{i}(\omega+\omega_{\alpha})][-\gamma_{\beta}-\mathrm{i}(\omega-\omega_{\beta})]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta}k_{\alpha}k_{\beta}Q_{\alpha\beta}\frac{\gamma_{\alpha}+\gamma_{\beta}+\mathrm{i}(\omega_{\alpha}+\omega_{\beta})}{(\gamma_{\alpha}+\gamma_{\beta})^{2}+(\omega_{\alpha}+\omega_{\beta})^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{\gamma_{\alpha}+\mathrm{i}(\omega+\omega_{\alpha})}{\gamma_{\alpha}^{2}+(\omega+\omega_{\alpha})^{2}}+\frac{\gamma_{\beta}-\mathrm{i}(\omega-\omega_{\beta})}{\gamma_{\beta}^{2}+(\omega-\omega_{\beta})^{2}}\right),

(84)

where
k_{\alpha}
,
k_{\beta}
are the components of the
k
vector and
Q_{\alpha\beta}
are the components of the matrix
\textbf{G}\,\bm{\sigma}\,\textbf{G}^{\mathrm{T}}
, in both cases written in the eigenbasis of
F
. As one can see, the resulting spectrum is a sum of symmetric (absorptive) and antisymmetric (dispersive) Lorentzian peaks, whose widths are the opposites of real parts (
\gamma_{\alpha}
), and central frequencies are the imaginary parts of the eigenvalues and their opposites (
\pm\omega_{\alpha}
):

\displaystyle\mathrm{PSD}(\omega)=\sum_{\alpha}\left(\frac{p_{\alpha}^{a}\gamma_{\alpha}^{2}}{\gamma_{\alpha}^{2}+(\omega-\omega_{\alpha})^{2}}+\frac{p_{\alpha}^{a}\gamma_{\alpha}^{2}}{\gamma_{\alpha}^{2}+(\omega+\omega_{\alpha})^{2}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{p_{\alpha}^{d}\gamma_{\alpha}(\omega-\omega_{\alpha})}{\gamma_{\alpha}^{2}+(\omega-\omega_{\alpha})^{2}}+\frac{p_{\alpha}^{d}\gamma_{\alpha}(\omega+\omega_{\alpha})}{\gamma_{\alpha}^{2}+(\omega+\omega_{\alpha})^{2}}\right),

(85)

which, in the case of a real signal
S(t)
we know to be real, positive, and symmetric around
\omega=0
.

Analytic prediction of peak amplitudes

Crucially, the atomic magnetometer under study, described by dynamics (

Appendix D
Amplitude calibration for the noise spectral density
\omega_{\mathrm{noise}}=2\pi f_{\mathrm{noise}}

In order to determine the value of the constant
c
relating the magnitude of the noise spectral density to the (RMS) amplitude of the voltage applied in the noise generator, i.e.
\omega_{\mathrm{noise}}=2\pi f_{\mathrm{noise}}=2\pi cV_{\mathrm{noise}}^{2}

The signal from the generator, however, is first transformed thorough a 1 MHz low-pass filter to prevent the effect of aliasing, as well as a 1 kHz high-pass filter to eliminate any spurious contributions at very low (DC-like) frequencies. We record 100 traces of 0.01 s duration of the current reaching the coil (sampled at 40 MHz), while varying the voltage amplitude in the noise generator. Based on these we compute the PSD (
30
), which is presented in Fig.
7
in the units of
\mathrm{Hz}^{2}/\mathrm{Hz}
, after performing adequate rescaling given the values of the coil calibration factor (10.1
\mathrm{nT/mV_{rms}}
) and the gyromagnetic ratio of caesium (3.5 Hz/nT).

From Fig.
7
we can determine
f_{\mathrm{noise}}
by seeing how the PSD varies with different white-noise amplitudes. We average areas of Fig.
7
in five different frequency ranges (specified in the label of Fig.
7
) to obtain the effective value of
f_{\mathrm{noise}}
as a function of the noise amplitude—five values of
V_{\mathrm{noise}}
(stated in
\mathrm{mV_{rms}}
) are used. For each of the averaged ranges, we then fit the quadratic dependence,

, what allow us to overall obtain

{}_{\mathrm{rms}}^{2}

.
