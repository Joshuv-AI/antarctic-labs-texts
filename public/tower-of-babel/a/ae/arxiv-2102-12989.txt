Distributed Optimization with Coupling Constraints
Xuyang Wu, He Wang, Jie Lu

Abstract
In this paper, we develop a novel distributed algorithm for addressing convex optimization with both nonlinear inequality and linear equality constraints, where the objective function can be a general nonsmooth convex function and all the constraints can be fully coupled. Specifically, we first separate the constraints into three groups, and design two primal-dual methods and utilize a virtual-queue-based method to handle each group of the constraints independently. Then, we integrate these three methods in a strategic way, leading to an integrated primal-dual proximal (IPLUX) algorithm, and enable the distributed implementation of IPLUX. We show that IPLUX achieves an O ⁡ ( 1 / k ) O(1/k) rate of convergence in terms of optimality and feasibility, which is stronger than the convergence results of the state-of-the-art distributed algorithms for convex optimization with coupling nonlinear constraints. Finally, IPLUX exhibits competitive practical performance in the simulations.



I
Introduction

This paper is intended to develop a distributed algorithm for the following convex composite optimization problem with coupling nonlinear inequality and linear equality constraints:

\begin{split}\underset{x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{n}}}{\operatorname{minimize}}~~&\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f_{i}(x_{i})+h_{i}(x_{i}))\\
\operatorname{subject~to}~~~~&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{g}_{i}^{\ell}(x_{i})\leq 0,\quad\forall\ell=1,\ldots,\bar{p},\\
&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{A}_{i}x_{i}=\bar{b}.\\
\end{split}

(1)

In problem (
1
), the decision variables
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}

\forall i=1,\ldots,n
are possibly of different dimensions. In the objective function, each
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a convex and differentiable function whose gradient is Lipschitz continuous, and each
h_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is a convex function that can be non-differentiable. Since
h_{i}
is allowed to take the value of
+\infty
, it may include an indicator function with respect to any closed convex set
X_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
, so that a set constraint
x_{i}\in X_{i}
can be implicitly imposed. Each of the
\bar{p}
nonlinear inequality constraints in (
1
) is described by the sum of
n
convex functions
\bar{g}_{i}^{\ell}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
\forall i=1,\ldots,n
, where each
\bar{g}_{i}^{\ell}
is associated with the variable
x_{i}
. The linear equality constraint is encoded by the matrices
\bar{A}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{m}\times d_{i}}

\forall i=1,\ldots,n
and the vector
\bar{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{m}}
.

I-A

Motivating Examples

Problem (
1
) arises in many real applications and is often of huge size. Typically, we distribute the problem data to a network of agents and employ the agents to cooperatively address the entire problem. Presented below are several widely-studied distributed optimization problems that are specialized from (
1
).

I-A
1
Consensus Optimization

Consensus optimization is prevalent in multi-agent systems
[
1
]
, which requires all the agents to reach a consensus that minimizes their total costs subject to their individual constraints. This problem is usually formulated as
\operatorname{minimize}_{z\in\cap_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{i}(z)
, where each
\varphi_{i}
is a convex function and each
X_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a closed convex set. It can also be rewritten as

\displaystyle\underset{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\operatorname{minimize}}~~~~

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\varphi_{i}(x_{i})+\mathcal{I}_{X_{i}}(x_{i}))

\displaystyle\operatorname{subject~to}~~~

\displaystyle x_{1}=x_{2}=\cdots=x_{n},

where
\mathcal{I}_{X_{i}}(x_{i})
is the indicator function with respect to
X_{i}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{I}_{X_{i}}(x_{i})=0
if
x_{i}\in X_{i}
and
+\infty
otherwise. Clearly, it is in the form of (
1
) with only the linear equality constraint.

I-A
2
Distributed Control

Suppose we conduct model predictive control (MPC) on a large-scale control system consisting of
n
interconnected subsystems. The subsystems jointly derive their control actions by iteratively solving convex optimization problems in the following form
[
2
]
:

\displaystyle\underset{y_{i},z_{i},\;\forall i=1,\ldots,n}{\operatorname{minimize}}~~

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}y_{i}^{T}H_{i}y_{i}+w_{i}^{T}y_{i}+\gamma\|z_{i}\|_{1}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\operatorname{subject~to}~~~

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{ji}y_{i}\leq q_{j},\quad\forall j=1,\ldots,n,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{ji}^{\prime}y_{i}=b_{j}^{\prime},\quad\forall j=1,\ldots,n,

\displaystyle\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{ji}^{\prime\prime}y_{i}\Bigr)-b_{j}^{\prime\prime}=z_{j},\quad\forall j=1,\ldots,n.

Here,
H_{i}
is a positive semidefinite matrix,
w_{i}
is a given vector, and
\gamma
is a nonnegative weight. Each
y_{i}
consists of the states and the control variables associated with subsystem
i
. In addition,
z_{i}

\forall i=1,\ldots,n
are auxiliary variables, which, together with the last group of constraints, are used for
\ell_{1}
-regularization. The first group of constraints describe the coupling relations among the subsystems, which are often sparsely connected, and the second group of constraints characterize the system model. Observe that such a distributed MPC problem is also an example of (
1
).

I-A
3
Resource Allocation

Many resource allocation problems can be cast in the form of (
1
). For example, the economic dispatch problem
[
3
]
requires
n
power generators to determine the minimum-cost power generations by solving

\displaystyle\underset{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}}{\operatorname{minimize}}~~~

\displaystyle~\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{i}(x_{i})

\displaystyle\operatorname{subject~to}~~

\displaystyle~\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=D,

\displaystyle~x_{i}^{\min}\leq x_{i}\leq x_{i}^{\max},\quad\forall i=1,\ldots,n.

Here,
\varphi_{i}(x_{i})
represents the power generation cost of generator

when its power generation is

,

is the global power demand across the entire power network, and

indicates the local generation limit of generator

1
), where the constraint

can be either viewed as inequality constraints or merged into the objective function via the indicator function with respect to the closed interval
[x_{i}^{\min},x_{i}^{\max}]
.

II
Problem Reformulation

In this section, we reformulate problem (
1

II-A

Constraint Separation

In many real-world problems, part of the constraints may have a sparse structure, i.e., some of the
\bar{g}_{i}^{\ell}
’s and some rows of the
\bar{A}_{i}
’s may be zero. In such cases, we separate the sparse constraints from the dense ones, and rewrite (
1
) as follows:

\begin{split}\underset{x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}},\;\forall i\in\mathcal{V}}{\operatorname{minimize}}~&\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}(f_{i}(x_{i})+h_{i}(x_{i}))\\
\operatorname{subject~to}~&\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}g_{i}(x_{i})\leq\mathbf{0}_{\tilde{p}},\;\;\,\quad\quad\qquad\qquad(\text{2a})\\
&\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}A_{i}x_{i}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}b_{i},\;\;\;\qquad\qquad\quad(\text{2b})\\
&\sum_{j\in S_{i}^{\operatorname{in}}}g_{ij}^{s}(x_{j})\leq\mathbf{0}_{p_{i}},~\forall i\in\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{in}},\quad(\text{2c})\\
&\sum_{j\in S_{i}^{\operatorname{eq}}}A_{ij}^{s}x_{j}=b_{i}^{s},~\forall i\in\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{eq}},\qquad\!(\text{2d})\end{split}

(2)

where
\mathcal{V}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. The constraints (2a) and (2b) represent the densely-coupled constraints with few zero entries, where each
g_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{p}}
is a vector-valued convex function (i.e., each of its entry is a convex function),
A_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{m}\times d_{i}}
, and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{m}}
. Note that
\tilde{p}
and
\tilde{m}
can be
0
, which means the absence of (2a) and (2b). The constraints (2c) and (2d) represent the sparse constraints with an abundance of zero entries. Here,
\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{in}}
and
\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{eq}}
are subsets of
\mathcal{V}
, which could be empty so that (2c) and (2d) are also allowed to be absent. In addition,
S_{i}^{\operatorname{in}}
and
S_{i}^{\operatorname{eq}}
are proper subsets of
\mathcal{V}
(which do not necessarily contain
i
) and often,
|S_{i}^{\operatorname{in}}|,|S_{i}^{\operatorname{eq}}|\ll n
. For convenience, we additionally define
S_{i}^{\operatorname{in}}=\emptyset

\forall i\in\mathcal{V}-\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{in}}
and
S_{i}^{\operatorname{eq}}=\emptyset

\forall i\in\mathcal{V}-\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{eq}}
. Moreover, each
g_{ij}^{s}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}}
is convex,
A_{ij}^{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}\times d_{j}}
, and
b_{i}^{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
. Note that
\tilde{p}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{in}}}p_{i}=\bar{p}
and

\bar{p}
and

are the dimensions of the inequality and equality constraints in problem (
1
), respectively.

Equivalent Problem

This subsection transforms (

We first introduce a set of auxiliary variables

\forall i\in\mathcal{V}
, and equivalently convert the constraint (2a) to

\displaystyle g_{i}(x_{i})\leq t_{i},\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{V},

(3)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}t_{i}=\mathbf{0}_{\tilde{p}}.

(4)

By doing so, we decouple the dense nonlinear inequality constraint (2a) into
n
independent nonlinear constraints in (
3
) at the cost of additionally imposing the linear equality constraint (
4
), which, intuitively, may be easier to tackle. Then, we attempt to rewrite (
2
) in terms of the new decision variables

\displaystyle y_{i}=[x_{i}^{T},t_{i}^{T}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}+\tilde{p}},\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{V}.

(5)

For the sake of compactness, let
\mathbf{y}=[y_{1}^{T},\ldots,y_{n}^{T}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where
N=n\tilde{p}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}d_{i}
. We thus express the objective function in (
2
) by
\Phi(\mathbf{y}):=f(\mathbf{y})+h(\mathbf{y})
, with

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}f_{i}(x_{i}),\quad h(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}h_{i}(x_{i}).

Next, we group together all the inequality constraints including (
3
) and the sparse inequality constraint (2c) by defining

G_{i}(\mathbf{y})=\begin{cases}\begin{bmatrix}g_{i}(x_{i})-t_{i}\\
\sum_{j\in S_{i}^{\operatorname{in}}}g_{ij}^{s}(x_{j})\end{bmatrix},&\text{if }i\in\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{in}},\\
g_{i}(x_{i})-t_{i},&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

for each
i\in\mathcal{V}
. Observe that

\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y}):=\begin{bmatrix}G_{1}(\mathbf{y})\\
\vdots\\
G_{n}(\mathbf{y})\end{bmatrix}\leq\mathbf{0}_{n\tilde{p}+\underset{i\in\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{in}}}{\sum}p_{i}}

is identical to (
3
) and (2c).

Subsequently, we express (
4
) and the dense equality constraint (2b) in a compact form. To do so, we introduce the following notations: For each
i\in\mathcal{V}
, let
B_{i}=\operatorname{diag}(A_{i},I_{\tilde{p}})\in\mathbb{R}^{(\tilde{m}+\tilde{p})\times(d_{i}+\tilde{p})}
and
c_{i}=[b_{i}^{T},\mathbf{0}_{\tilde{p}}^{T}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{m}+\tilde{p}}
. Then, let
\mathbf{B}=\operatorname{diag}(B_{1},\ldots,B_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n(\tilde{m}+\tilde{p})\times N}
and
\mathbf{c}=[c_{1}^{T},\ldots,c_{n}^{T}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n(\tilde{m}+\tilde{p})}
. Note that

\displaystyle(\mathbf{1}_{n}\!\otimes\!I_{\tilde{m}+\tilde{p}})^{T}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}\!-\!\mathbf{c})\!=\!\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}(B_{i}y_{i}\!-\!c_{i})\!=\!\begin{bmatrix}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{V}}(A_{i}x_{i}\!-\!b_{i})\\
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}t_{i}\end{bmatrix}.

Hence,
(\mathbf{1}_{n}\!\otimes\!I_{\tilde{m}+\tilde{p}})^{T}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}\!-\!\mathbf{c})\!=\!\mathbf{0}_{\tilde{m}+\tilde{p}}
is exactly (
4
) and (2b).

It remains to describe the sparse equality constraint (2d) in terms of
\mathbf{y}
. To this end, define
B_{ij}^{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}\times(d_{j}+\tilde{p})}

\forall i\in\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{eq}}

\forall j\in\mathcal{V}
as follows:

B_{ij}^{s}=\begin{cases}[A_{ij}^{s},\mathbf{O}_{m_{i}\times\tilde{p}}],&\text{if }j\in S_{i}^{\operatorname{eq}},\\
\mathbf{O}_{m_{i}\times(d_{j}\!+\tilde{p})},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then, let
B_{i}^{s}=\begin{bmatrix}B_{i1}^{s},\ldots,B_{in}^{s}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}\times N}

\forall i\in\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{eq}}
. Note that for each
i\in\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{eq}}
,
B_{ij}^{s}y_{j}
is equal to
A_{ij}^{s}x_{j}
if
j\in S_{i}^{\operatorname{eq}}
and is equal to
\mathbf{0}_{m_{i}}
otherwise. Hence,
B_{i}^{s}\mathbf{y}-b_{i}^{s}=(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}}B_{ij}^{s}y_{j})-b_{i}^{s}=(\sum_{j\in S_{i}^{\operatorname{eq}}}A_{ij}^{s}x_{j})-b_{i}^{s}
. It follows that (2d) can be written as
\mathbf{B}^{s}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{c}^{s}
, where
\mathbf{B}^{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{eq}}}m_{i})\times N}
and
\mathbf{c}^{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{eq}}}m_{i}}
are given by

\mathbf{B}^{s}=\begin{bmatrix}B_{\zeta_{1}}^{s}\\
\vdots\\
B_{\zeta_{a}}^{s}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{c}^{s}=\begin{bmatrix}b_{\zeta_{1}}^{s}\\
\vdots\\
b_{\zeta_{a}}^{s}\end{bmatrix},\quad\{\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{a}\}=\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{eq}}.

In other words,
\mathbf{B}^{s}
and
\mathbf{c}^{s}
are obtained by vertically stacking
B_{i}^{s}
and
b_{i}^{s}

\forall i\in\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{eq}}
, respectively.

Combining the above, we derive the following equivalent form of problem (
2
):

\begin{split}\underset{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\operatorname{minimize}}~~&~\Phi(\mathbf{y})=f(\mathbf{y})+h(\mathbf{y})\\
\operatorname{subject~to}~&~\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y})\leq\mathbf{0}_{n\tilde{p}+\underset{{i\in\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{in}}}}{\sum}p_{i}},\\
&~(\mathbf{1}_{n}\otimes I_{\tilde{m}+\tilde{p}})^{T}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c})=\mathbf{0}_{\tilde{m}+\tilde{p}},\\
&~\mathbf{B}^{s}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{c}^{s}.\end{split}

(6)

III
Algorithm

In this section, we propose a novel distributed algorithm for solving problem (
6
) that is equivalent to (
2
) and (
1
).

We adopt a separation principle in our algorithm design. Specifically, we develop two new primal-dual proximal methods and utilize a prior virtual-queue-based method to minimize
\Phi(\mathbf{y})
subject to each of the three constraints in (
6
), respectively. Then, we deliberately unite these three methods, so that (
6
) with all the three constraints can be solved.

III-A

Proximal Algorithm for Sparse Equality Constraints

We tentatively focus on the sparse linear equality constraint
\mathbf{B}^{s}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{c}^{s}
and consider the following special case of (
6
):

\begin{split}\underset{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\operatorname{minimize}}~~&~\Phi(\mathbf{y})\\
\operatorname{subject~to}~&~\mathbf{B}^{s}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{c}^{s}.\end{split}

(7)

We endeavor to solve problem (
7
) in a distributed fashion. To this end, we try to apply the Method of Multipliers
[
20
]
to (
7
), which has the following primal-dual dynamics:

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}(k+1)\in

\displaystyle\operatorname{\arg\;\min}_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Phi(\mathbf{y})+\langle\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(k),\mathbf{B}^{s}\mathbf{y}\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|\mathbf{B}^{s}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c}^{s}\|^{2},

(8)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(k+1)=

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(k)+\gamma(\mathbf{B}^{s}\mathbf{y}(k+1)-\mathbf{c}^{s}),\quad\forall k\geq 0.

(9)

Here,
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{eq}}}m_{i}}
is arbitrarily chosen and
\gamma>0
.

Although (
7
) has a sparse structure, the primal update (
8
) in the Method of Multipliers is still a
centralized
operation since
\|\mathbf{B}^{s}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c}^{s}\|^{2}
mingles the coordinates of the optimization variable
\mathbf{y}
. To decentralize the primal update, we add a proximal term
\frac{\gamma}{2}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}(k))^{T}(\lambda^{2}I_{N}-(\mathbf{B}^{s})^{T}\mathbf{B}^{s})(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}(k))
,
\lambda>0
to the minimized function on the right-hand side of (
8
), where
\mathbf{y}(0)
is arbitrarily given. It can be shown that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{B}^{s}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c}^{s}\|^{2}+(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}(k))^{T}(\lambda^{2}I_{N}-(\mathbf{B}^{s})^{T}\mathbf{B}^{s})(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}(k))

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}(k)+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}(\mathbf{B}^{s})^{T}(\mathbf{B}^{s}\mathbf{y}(k)-\mathbf{c}^{s})\|^{2}+C(k),

where
C(k)=\|\mathbf{B}^{s}\mathbf{y}(k)-\mathbf{c}^{s}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\|(\mathbf{B}^{s})^{T}(\mathbf{B}^{s}\mathbf{y}(k)-\mathbf{c}^{s})\|^{2}
is independent of
\mathbf{y}
. As a result, the primal update (
8
) is changed to the following:

\begin{split}&\mathbf{y}(k+1)=\operatorname{\arg\;\min}_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}~\Phi(\mathbf{y})+\langle\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(k),\mathbf{B}^{s}\mathbf{y}\rangle\\
&+\frac{\gamma\lambda^{2}}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}(k)+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}(\mathbf{B}^{s})^{T}(\mathbf{B}^{s}\mathbf{y}(k)-\mathbf{c}^{s})\|^{2}.\end{split}

(10)

To reduce the computational cost of (
10
) and (
9
), we additionally apply the linear transformation
\mathbf{v}(k)=(\mathbf{B}^{s})^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

\forall k\geq 0
, which results in

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}(k+1)=\operatorname{\arg\;\min}_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}~\Phi(\mathbf{y})+\Delta_{1}^{k}(\mathbf{y}),

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}(k+1)=\mathbf{v}(k)+\gamma(\mathbf{B}^{s})^{T}(\mathbf{B}^{s}\mathbf{y}(k+1)-\mathbf{c}^{s}),

(12)

where
\Delta_{1}^{k}(\mathbf{y}):=\langle\mathbf{v}(k),\mathbf{y}\rangle+\frac{\gamma\lambda^{2}}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}(k)+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}(\mathbf{B}^{s})^{T}(\mathbf{B}^{s}\mathbf{y}(k)-\mathbf{c}^{s})\|^{2}
. Note that the computations involving
\mathbf{B}^{s}
in (
10
) and (
9
) include calculating
(\mathbf{B}^{s})^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(k)
,
(\mathbf{B}^{s})^{T}(\mathbf{B}^{s}\mathbf{y}(k)-\mathbf{c}^{s})
, and
\mathbf{B}^{s}\mathbf{y}(k+1)
, yet (
11
) and (
12
) only need to calculate
(\mathbf{B}^{s})^{T}(\mathbf{B}^{s}\mathbf{y}(k+1)-\mathbf{c}^{s})
, as
(\mathbf{B}^{s})^{T}(\mathbf{B}^{s}\mathbf{y}(k)-\mathbf{c}^{s})
can be inherited from the last iteration.

Due to the above linear transformation, we require
\mathbf{v}(k)\in\operatorname{Range}((\mathbf{B}^{s})^{T})
for each
k\geq 0
. This can be guaranteed by choosing
\mathbf{v}(0)\in\operatorname{Range}((\mathbf{B}^{s})^{T})
, because (
12
) indicates that
\mathbf{v}(k+1)-\mathbf{v}(k)\in\operatorname{Range}((\mathbf{B}^{s})^{T})

\forall k\geq 0
.

The updates (
11
)–(
12
) as well as the initialization of arbitrary
\mathbf{y}(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\mathbf{v}(0)\in\operatorname{Range}((\mathbf{B}^{s})^{T})
constitute a new primal-dual proximal algorithm for solving problems with sparse linear equality constraints in the form of (
7
). It can be viewed as a proximal variation of the Method of Multipliers, and can be executed in a decentralized manner, which will be discussed in Section
III-E
.

III-B

Proximal Algorithm for Dense Equality Constraints

We switch to problem (
6
) with only the densely-coupled linear equality constraint, i.e.,

\begin{split}\underset{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\operatorname{minimize}}~~&~\Phi(\mathbf{y})\\
\operatorname{subject~to}~&~(\mathbf{1}_{n}\otimes I_{\tilde{m}+\tilde{p}})^{T}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c})=\mathbf{0}_{\tilde{m}+\tilde{p}}.\end{split}

(13)

Instead of directly addressing (
13
), we tackle the Lagrange dual problem of (
13
), which is given by

\underset{u\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{m}+\tilde{p}}}{\operatorname{maximize}}\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Phi(\mathbf{y})+\langle u,(\mathbf{1}_{n}\otimes I_{\tilde{m}+\tilde{p}})^{T}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c})\rangle.

(14)

For each
i\in\mathcal{V}
, let
u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{m}+\tilde{p}}
be a local copy of the dual variable
u\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{m}+\tilde{p}}
in (
14
), and let
\mathbf{u}=[u_{1}^{T},\ldots,u_{n}^{T}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n(\tilde{m}+\tilde{p})}
. Thus, the dual problem (
14
) can be rewritten as

\begin{split}\underset{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n(\tilde{m}+\tilde{p})}}{\operatorname{maximize}}~~&~D_{e}(\mathbf{u}):=\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Phi(\mathbf{y})+\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c}\rangle\\
\operatorname{subject~to}~&~u_{1}=\ldots=u_{n}.\end{split}

(15)

To solve the equivalent dual problem (
15
), we develop another proximal algorithm based on the P-EXTRA algorithm
[
7
]
. P-EXTRA is a distributed first-order method for nonsmooth convex consensus optimization. According to
[
19
]
, when we apply P-EXTRA to solve (
15
), it can be expressed in the following form:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(k+1)=

\displaystyle\operatorname{\arg\;\min}_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n(\tilde{m}+\tilde{p})}}-D_{e}(\mathbf{u})+\langle\mathbf{u},H^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}(k)\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\mathbf{u}-W\mathbf{u}(k)\|^{2},

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{z}}(k+1)=

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{z}}(k)+\rho H^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{u}(k+1).

Here,
\mathbf{u}(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n(\tilde{m}+\tilde{p})}
is arbitrarily selected and
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}(0)=\rho H^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{u}(0)
. In addition,
\rho>0
,
W=P^{W}\otimes I_{\tilde{m}+\tilde{p}}
, and
H=P^{H}\otimes I_{\tilde{m}+\tilde{p}}
, where
P^{W},P^{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
satisfy the assumption below.

Assumption 1
.

The matrices
P^{W}
,
P^{H}
satisfy the following:

(a)

[P^{W}]_{ij}=[P^{H}]_{ij}=0
,
\forall i\in\mathcal{V}
,
\forall j\notin\mathcal{N}_{i}\cup\{i\}
.

(b)

P^{W}
and
P^{H}
are symmetric and positive semidefinite.

(c)

P^{W}\mathbf{1}_{n}=\mathbf{1}_{n}
,
\operatorname{Null}(P^{H})=\operatorname{span}(\mathbf{1}_{n})
.

(d)

P^{W}+P^{H}\preceq I_{n}
.

Assumption
1
(a) is used to enable distributed implementation and Assumption
1
(b)–(d) are for ensuring the convergence of P-EXTRA. The connectivity of the graph
\mathcal{G}
makes Assumption
1
easily hold. For example, we may let
P^{W}=\frac{I_{n}+P^{\prime}}{2}
and
P^{H}=\frac{I_{n}-P^{\prime}}{2}
for some
P^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that
[P^{\prime}]_{ij}=[P^{\prime}]_{ji}>0

\forall\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{E}
,
[P^{\prime}]_{ii}=1-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}[P^{\prime}]_{ij}>0

\forall i\in\mathcal{V}
, and
[P^{\prime}]_{ij}=0
otherwise. Assumption
1
also yields the following properties of
W
and
H
:

\displaystyle W=W^{T}\succeq\mathbf{O},\;H=H^{T}\succeq\mathbf{O},

(16)

\displaystyle W(\mathbf{1}_{n}\otimes I_{\tilde{m}+\tilde{p}})=\mathbf{1}_{n}\otimes I_{\tilde{m}+\tilde{p}},

(17)

\displaystyle H(\mathbf{1}_{n}\otimes I_{\tilde{m}+\tilde{p}})=\mathbf{O},

(18)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Range}(H)\!=\!\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n(\tilde{m}+\tilde{p})}|(\mathbf{1}_{n}\otimes I_{\tilde{m}+\tilde{p}})^{T}\mathbf{y}\!=\!\mathbf{0}\},

(19)

\displaystyle W+H\preceq I_{n(\tilde{m}+\tilde{p})}.

(20)

For the purpose of distributed design later, we further set
\mathbf{z}(k)=H^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}(k)

\forall k\geq 0
, leading to

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(k+1)=

\displaystyle\operatorname{\arg\;\min}_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n(\tilde{m}+\tilde{p})}}-D_{e}(\mathbf{u})+\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{z}(k)\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\mathbf{u}-W\mathbf{u}(k)\|^{2},

(21)

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}(k+1)=

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}(k)+\rho H\mathbf{u}(k+1),

(22)

where
\mathbf{z}(0)=\rho H\mathbf{u}(0)
.

Nevertheless, neither P-EXTRA nor its equivalent form (
21
)–(
22
) can be applied to solve (
15
) because the dual function
D_{e}
generally cannot be expressed in closed form. To overcome this, we propose an implementable approximation of P-EXTRA for solving (
15
).

First of all, with the same initialization and parameter settings as P-EXTRA described above, for each
k\geq 0
, let

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}(k+1)\in

\displaystyle\operatorname{\arg\;\min}_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Phi(\mathbf{y})+\frac{1}{2\rho}\|\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\langle W\mathbf{u}(k)-\frac{1}{\rho}\mathbf{z}(k),\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c}\rangle,

(23)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(k+1)=

\displaystyle W\mathbf{u}(k)+\frac{1}{\rho}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}(k+1)-\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{z}(k)),

(24)

and
\mathbf{z}(k)
be updated as (
22
). In fact, the method described by (
23
), (
24
), and (
22
) is equivalent to P-EXTRA described by (
21
)–(
22
), provided that
\mathbf{y}(k+1)
in (
23
) is well-defined.

Below, we verify such an equivalent relation. To do so, we tentatively assume
\mathbf{y}(k+1)
in (
23
) exists, and will address this issue shortly. Note that (
23
) is identical to

-\mathbf{B}^{T}(W\mathbf{u}(k)+\frac{1}{\rho}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}(k+1)-\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{z}(k)))\in\partial\Phi(\mathbf{y}(k+1)).

Then, substituting (
24
) into the above gives

-\mathbf{B}^{T}\mathbf{u}(k+1)\in\partial\Phi(\mathbf{y}(k+1)),

which indicates

\mathbf{y}(k+1)\in\operatorname{\arg\;\min}_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Phi(\mathbf{y})+\langle\mathbf{u}(k+1),\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c}\rangle.

(25)

In addition, (
25
) implies
\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}(k+1)-\mathbf{c}\in\partial D_{e}(\mathbf{u}(k+1))

[
23
]
. On the other hand, (
24
) results in
\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}(k+1)-\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{z}(k)+\rho(\mathbf{u}(k+1)-W\mathbf{u}(k))
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}(k)+\rho(\mathbf{u}(k+1)-W\mathbf{u}(k))\in\partial D_{e}(\mathbf{u}(k+1)),

which is equivalent to (
21
). Therefore,
(\mathbf{z}(k),\mathbf{u}(k))
generated by (
23
), (
24
), and (
22
) is exactly the same as
(\mathbf{z}(k),\mathbf{u}(k))
generated by (
21
)–(
22
), yet (
23
), (
24
), and (
22
) do not rely on
D_{e}
and thus do not require that
D_{e}
be in closed form.

Apart from that, the additional variable
\mathbf{y}(k)
updated as in (
23
) is more than an auxiliary variable. It can also be used as an online approximate primal solution to problem (
13
), while (
21
)–(
22
) cannot provide such a real-time primal approximation. To see this, note that for any optimum
\mathbf{u}^{\star}
of (
15
),
\mathbf{y}^{\star}\in\operatorname{\arg\;\min}_{\mathbf{y}}\Phi(\mathbf{y})+\langle\mathbf{u}^{\star},\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c}\rangle
is an optimum of (
13
). Thus, from (
25
),
\mathbf{y}(k)
is able to converge to a primal optimum of (
13
), as long as
\mathbf{u}(k)
converges to a dual optimum. Since
\mathbf{u}(k)
results from P-EXTRA, it is guaranteed to converge to an optimum of (
15
) under proper conditions
[
7
]
.

It remains to address the issue that (
23
) may not be well-posed as the convex optimization problem on its right-hand side may not have an optimal solution. To this end, we modify (
23
) by adding a proximal term
\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}(k)\|^{2}
,
\alpha_{1}>0
to its right-hand side, which gives

\mathbf{y}(k+1)=\operatorname{\arg\;\min}_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Phi(\mathbf{y})+\Delta_{2}^{k}(\mathbf{y}),

(26)

where
\mathbf{y}(0)
can be arbitrary and
\Delta_{2}^{k}(\mathbf{y}):=\frac{1}{2\rho}\|\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c}\|^{2}+\langle W\mathbf{u}(k)-\frac{1}{\rho}\mathbf{z}(k),\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c}\rangle+\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}(k)\|^{2}
. This ensures the unique existence of
\mathbf{y}(k+1)
.

The above proximal algorithm originating from P-EXTRA for solving the dual problem of (
13
) can be summarized as follows: Given any
\mathbf{u}(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n(\tilde{m}+\tilde{p})}
and
\mathbf{y}(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we set
\mathbf{z}(0)=\rho H\mathbf{u}(0)
and then successively update
\mathbf{y}(k+1)
,
\mathbf{u}(k+1)
, and
\mathbf{z}(k+1)
for each
k\geq 0
according to (
26
), (
24
), and (
22
), respectively. The entire process can be executed in a fully distributed way over the graph
\mathcal{G}
, which will be discussed in Section
III-E
.

III-C

Virtual-Queue Algorithm for Sparse Inequality Constraints

Now we only keep the inequality constraint for problem (
6
):

\begin{split}\underset{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\operatorname{minimize}}~~&~\Phi(\mathbf{y})\\
\operatorname{subject~to}~&~\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y})\leq\mathbf{0}_{n\tilde{p}+\underset{{i\in\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{in}}}}{\sum}p_{i}}.\end{split}

(27)

We may utilize the virtual-queue-based algorithm
[
21
]
to solve (
27
). The virtual-queue-based algorithm can be viewed as a modification of the dual subgradient algorithm, with an additional proximal term in the primal update and an approximate dual update that resembles a queuing equation. It takes the following form when applied to problem (
27
): Arbitrarily pick
\mathbf{y}(0)\in\operatorname{dom}(h)
and set
\mathbf{q}(0)=\max\{-\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y}(0)),\mathbf{0}_{n\tilde{p}+\sum_{{i\in\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{in}}}}p_{i}}\}
. Then, for any
k\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\!\!\mathbf{y}(k\!+\!1)\!=

\displaystyle\!\operatorname{\arg\;\min}_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Phi(\mathbf{y})+\Delta_{3}^{k}(\mathbf{y}),

(28)

\displaystyle\!\!\mathbf{q}(k\!+\!1)\!=

\displaystyle\max\{-\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y}(k+1)),\mathbf{q}(k)\!+\!\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y}(k+1))\},

(29)

where
\Delta_{3}^{k}(\mathbf{y}):=\langle\mathbf{q}(k)+\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y}(k)),\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y})\rangle+\frac{\alpha_{2}}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}(k)\|^{2}
,
\alpha_{2}>0
.

Recall from Section
II-C
that the inequality constraint in (
27
) is sparse, which would enable the distributed implementation of the above updates (cf. Section
III-E
). Indeed, although the virtual-queue-based algorithm is targeted at inequality-constrained convex problems, it can also deal with equality constraints by means of transforming each equality constraint into two inequalities. However, this would significantly increase the number of constraints and thus the algorithm complexity. Moreover, the virtual-queue-based algorithm would rely on centralized coordination when it comes to the dense constraints in problem (
6
).

III-D

Integrated Primal-Dual Proximal Algorithm

The three constraints in problem (
6
) are considered separately so far—We have proposed two new proximal algorithms in Sections
III-A
and
III-B
, and presented the virtual-queue-based algorithm
[
21
]
in Section
III-C

VI
Conclusion

We have developed the integrated primal-dual proximal (IPLUX) algorithm to solve nonsmooth convex optimization problems with both nonlinear inequality and linear equality constraints in a distributed way. Moreover, we have shown that both the objective error and the constraint violation of IPLUX dissipate at
O(1/k)
rates. IPLUX is able to address more general problems than most prior distributed optimization algorithms, achieve stronger convergence results than the existing methods that also admit coupling nonlinear inequality constraints, and possess superior practical performance in solving constrained distributed convex optimization problems.
