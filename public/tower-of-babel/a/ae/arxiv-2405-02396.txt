Geometry, Dissipation, Cooling, and the Dynamical Evolution of Wind-Blown Bubbles
Lachlan Lancaster, Eve C. Ostriker, Chang-Goo Kim, Jeong-Gyu Kim, Greg L. Bryan

Abstract
Bubbles driven by energy and mass injection from small scales are ubiquitous in astrophysical fluid systems and essential to feedback across multiple scales. In particular, O stars in young clusters produce high velocity winds that create hot bubbles in the surrounding gas. We demonstrate that the dynamical evolution of these bubbles is critically dependent upon the geometry of their interfaces with their surroundings and the nature of heat transport across these interfaces. These factors together determine the amount of energy that can be lost from the interior through cooling at the interface, which in turn determines the ability of the bubble to do work on its surroundings. We further demonstrate that the scales relevant to physical dissipation across this interface are extremely difficult to resolve in global numerical simulations of bubbles for parameter values of interest. This means the dissipation driving evolution of these bubbles in numerical simulations is often of a numerical nature. We describe the physical and numerical principles that determine the level of dissipation in these simulations; we use this, along with a fractal model for the geometry of the interfaces, to explain differences in convergence behavior between hydrodynamical and magneto-hydrodynamical simulations presented here. We additionally derive an expression for momentum as a function of bubble radius expected when the relevant dissipative scales are resolved and show that it still results in efficiently-cooled solutions as postulated in previous work.

II.3
The Bubble-Shell Interface

In
Figure 1

In reality, the boundary between the wind-blown bubble and its shell may combine all of these features. Ignoring for a moment the diffusion of mass, there is a ‘contact surface’ that separates the injected mass from the surrounding mass. This surface is distorted and amplified in area by dynamical instabilities as well as the larger scale inhomogeneities in the background medium. ) and the rate at which energy is diffused across the surface (which determines

we show these scales as functions of both temperature (left panel) and pressure (right panel) for given choices of the parameters of the dissipative physics, defined and explained below.

II.4.1
Thermal Conduction

The first dissipation mechanism is thermal conduction mediated by individual particle collisions over a mean free path
[
127
,
126
]
, parameterized by the (temperature-dependent) thermal conductivity
\kappa(T)
, which produces a heat flux
-\kappa\nabla T
. In balancing heat transport through conduction against cooling in the energy equation, one can derive a characteristic length scale
\lambda_{F}
, which we will refer to as the ‘Field Length’
8
8

8

Note that this differs by order unity from the length at which thermal instability is suppressed by thermal conduction
[
23
]
.
[
23
,
151
,
64
,
132
]
:

\lambda_{F}(T)=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa(T)T}{n^{2}\Lambda(T)}}=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa(T)Tt_{\rm cool}(T)}{P}}\,.

(18)

Below we take
\kappa
as given by either

\frac{\kappa_{SP}(T)}{{\rm erg}/{\rm s}/{\rm cm}/{\rm K}}=\begin{cases}2.5\times 10^{3}\left(\frac{T}{\rm K}\right)^{1/2}&T<T_{\rm eq}\\
6\times 10^{-7}\left(\frac{T}{\rm K}\right)^{5/2}&T\geq T_{\rm eq}\end{cases}

(19)

which is a combination of Spitzer (high temperatures) and Parker
[
105
, low temperatures, ]
conductivity, where the point of switching between the two
T_{\rm eq}=6.45\times 10^{4}\,{\rm K}
is the point at which they are equal.

In the left panel of
Figure 2
, we show
\lambda_{F}
as a function of temperature
T
at
P/k_{B}=10^{5}{\rm K\ cm^{-3}}
. The cooling function used here,
\Lambda(T)
, is that from
Kim & Ostriker [57]
. Since
t_{\rm cool}\propto P^{-1}
, it is clear that
\lambda_{F}\propto P^{-1}
if one considers a fixed temperature. In the right panel of
Figure 2
, we show
\lambda_{F}
relative to the bubble radius
R_{\rm bub}
, selecting
\lambda_{F}(T_{\rm pk})
. The bubble radius is calculated as a function of
P_{\rm hot}
assuming the efficiently cooled solution (
Equation A4
with
R_{\rm f}\to R_{\rm bub,EC}
) with
\dot{p}_{w}=4.8\times 10^{4}M_{\odot}/{\rm km}/{\rm s}/{\rm Myr}
. Really, we should choose the value at which
\lambda_{F}
is minimized in the range of temperatures probed by the conductive interface, roughly
5000\,{\rm K}<T<10^{8}\,{\rm K}
, which is slightly different from
T_{\rm pk}
due to the temperature dependence of
\kappa(T)T
in the numerator of
Equation 18
. However, the dependence of
\lambda_{F}
on
T
is so strongly influenced by
\Lambda(T)
that this temperature is essentially
T_{\rm pk}
, so we will use the latter for simplicity.

Note that, in the presence of a magnetic field,
\mathbf{B}
, thermal conduction across field lines is expected to be suppressed, due to suppression of electron transport across field lines. In the context of WBBs expanding into a magnetized medium, the process of ‘magnetic draping’ of the field lines around the bubble will virtually guarantee that the magnetic field is mostly parallel to the bubble’s surface, suppressing conduction. Furthermore, recent plasma physics simulations and experiments have demonstrated that thermal conduction can be strongly suppressed due to plasma instabilities in high-
\beta
, weakly collisional and turbulent plasmas, as may be the case in some regimes of these mixing layers
[
110
,
69
,
16
,
91
]
. Additionally, as derived in
Cowie & McKee [8]
, there is a maximum heat flux that can be carried by electrons,
q_{\rm max}\sim(3/2)\rho c_{s,{\rm iso}}^{3}
(where
c_{s,{\rm iso}}
is the isothermal sound speed), which limits the flux when the temperature gradient scale length,
T/|\nabla T|
, is comparable to the mean free path of electrons. All of these effects can decrease
\kappa
by an order of magnitude, making
\lambda_{F}
a factor of 3 or more smaller.

We can derive a rough estimate of the value of the velocity that would be induced by resolved thermal conduction by assuming that all cooling occurs in a layer of thickness
\lambda_{F}(T_{\rm pk})
. We assume the cooling proceeds at a rate per unit area of
\lambda_{F}n^{2}\Lambda(T_{\rm pk})
and balance this against advection of enthalpy from the hot gas into the layer at a rate
5Pv_{\rm cond}/2
. This gives us

v_{\rm cond}\approx\frac{2}{5}\sqrt{\frac{\kappa(T_{\rm pk})\Lambda(T_{\rm pk})}{k_{B}^{2}T_{\rm pk}}}\approx\frac{\lambda_{F}}{t_{\rm cool}}\,,

(20)

which is independent of pressure.

We should note that there is another scale associated with the action of thermal conduction, which comes from the balancing of the advection term with the conductive heat transport term in a one-dimensional energy equation of a conductive cooling layer
[
132
,
131
, e.g.]
. This ‘evaporation length’ scale is given by

\lambda_{\rm evap}=\frac{2\kappa T}{5Pv}

(21)

where
v
is the gas velocity. Though the value of this length scale depends on the details of the evaporative mass loss
[
18
, which is subject to the energy lost in the cooling layer – see section 5.3 of]
, this scale is generally much larger than
\lambda_{F}
. It is therefore generally possible to resolve an evaporative mass flow from the surface even if the details of cooling in the surface are unresolved in a given simulation.

II.4.2
Turbulent Dissipation

The second dissipation mechanism is turbulent diffusivity, which has been explored extensively in recent literature
[
26
,
132
,
77
]
. In particular, we imagine a turbulent cascade in the hot gas with energy containing scale
L
and velocity normalization
v_{t}(L)
, following a spectrum

v_{t}(\ell)=v_{t}(L)\left(\frac{\ell}{L}\right)^{p}\,,

(22)

with
p=1/3
for subsonic, Kolmogorov turbulence. We define the eddy turn-over time at scale
\ell
as

t_{\rm eddy}(\ell)\equiv\frac{\ell}{v_{t}(\ell)}\,.

(23)

The mixing that leads to cooling is expected to take place on a scale
\ell_{\rm cool}
such that
t_{\rm eddy}(\ell_{\rm cool})=t_{\rm cool}
, where
Equation 16
gives
t_{\rm cool}
. Combining
Equation 22
and
Equation 23
with this definition we obtain the cooling length

\ell_{\rm cool}\equiv L\left(\frac{t_{\rm cool}}{t_{\rm eddy}(L)}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-p}}\,.

(24)

In this picture, eddies on scales larger than
\ell_{\rm cool}
mix too slowly to contribute significantly to energy losses. At scales
\lesssim\ell_{\rm cool}
, the surface area of the interface is rapidly enhanced on a time-scale shorter than
t_{\rm cool}
(since
t_{\rm eddy}(\ell)<t_{\rm cool}
for all
\ell<\ell_{\rm cool}
). In particular, this enhancement in area is so rapid that it does not matter what the micro-physical means of energy transport across the surface is; the area will always become large enough to carry the heat being mixed by the turbulence. In the language of the combustion literature
[
132
]
, we can think of mixing on the scale
\ell_{\rm cool}
as occurring in the ‘well-stirred reactor’ limit, at which point an effective turbulent diffusivity may be applied. Either way, this results in the
\ell_{\rm cool}
-scale eddy being ‘mixed’ to the intermediate temperatures where cooling is rapid.

The velocity scale associated with turbulent dissipation is built in:

v_{\rm turb}\approx v_{t}(\ell_{\rm cool})=v_{t}(L)\left(\frac{t_{\rm cool}}{t_{\rm eddy}(L)}\right)^{\frac{p}{1-p}}\,.

(25)

We can see that this is very similar to
Equation 20
if we remember that
v_{t}(\ell_{\rm cool})=\ell_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm cool}
.

In the presence of a magnetic field, turbulent heat diffusion is also altered due to the modification of instabilities which drive the turbulence
[
14
,
152
]
. Roughly speaking, instabilities will be suppressed if the velocity scale associated with the instability (by which we mean the length scale associated with the instability, divided by its linear theory growth time) is less than the Alfvèn speed in the medium
[
3
]
. This likely has drastic consequences for the WBB as, due to compression of the magnetic field, the shell of the WBB will be strongly magnetically dominated. This is likely the cause of the large differences in fractal structure (
Section IV.4
) and turbulent properties (
Section IV.7
) between our HD and MHD simulations.

In this section and the previous one we have defined two mechanisms of physical heat dissipation and the associated length scales relevant to resolving them. These length scales are functions of temperature, as shown in the left panel of
Figure 2
. Considering one dissipation mechanism alone, it is relevant to resolve the minimum such length scale in the relevant temperature range for the problem. This is essentially determined by where the cooling rate is maximized, due to the strong temperature dependence of
\Lambda(T)
. However, considering a realistic case where both dissipation mechanisms are acting, it is only important to resolve the larger of the two length scales, as the associated dissipation mechanism will dominate the heat transport. For example, in the right panel of
Figure 2
we can see that, for
v_{t}(L)=300\,{\rm km/s}
, the turbulent dissipation scale,
\ell_{\rm cool}
, is always greater than length scale associated with thermal conduction,
\lambda_{F}
. In this case, it is only necessary to resolve
\ell_{\rm cool}
, which is can be orders of magnitude larger than
\lambda_{F}
at lower pressures (later in the bubble evolution). If instead we were simulating a bubble where
\ell_{\rm cool}<\lambda_{F}
, as is the case for the
v_{t}(L)=75\,{\rm km/s}
case in
Figure 2
, it would then be necessary to resolve
\lambda_{F}
, though we will see that this is often not the case.

II.4.3
Numerical Dissipation

When the appropriate dissipative scales are resolved (the maximum of
\ell_{\rm cool}
or
\lambda_{F}
for a given condition of the gas) physical dissipation controls the pace at which energy can be transported across the interface and therefore sets
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
. As we can see from comparing
\Delta x\approx 0.1\,{\rm pc}
at our highest resolution to the relevant dissipative scales in the right panel of
Figure 2
, we are only just near resolving the turbulent dissipation at the latest times in our simulations for relevant choices of the turbulent dissipative parameters (see
Section IV.7
). The question then arises: how is
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
set when the relevant physical dissipative scales are not properly resolved?

In keeping with the previous two subsections, let us first suppose that
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
is set by dissipation, this time of a numerical nature. In numerical dissipation the relevant dissipative velocity scale associated with dissipation across contact discontinuities (as is relevant here) is roughly the local gas velocity relative to the grid. For example, consider the advection equation in one-dimension,
\partial_{t}q+a\partial_{x}q=0
with
q
some scalar field and
a
the advection speed. If we solve this equation on a fixed grid with spatial discretization
\Delta x
, using a first-order upwind scheme one can analytically show that the actual equation being solved (to second order accuracy) is
\partial_{t}q+a\partial_{x}q=C_{\rm num}\partial_{x}^{2}q
with numerical diffusivity
C_{\rm num}=\Delta xa(1-|c|)/2
with
c
the Courant number (see e.g. Chapter 5 of
Toro [135]
or Chapter 20 of
Press et al. [109]
). From
C_{\rm num}
we can read off the relevant spatial and velocity scale for the dissipation:
\Delta x
and
a
, the resolution and the advection velocity. While it is much more difficult to derive an exact numerical diffusivity for a higher-order method, let alone a system as complicated as the MHD equations, the same basic principle still holds. That is, for diffusion across contact discontinuities, the numerical diffusive velocity scale,
v_{\rm num}
, is comparable to the local velocity of the gas relative to the grid:

v_{\rm num}\approx v_{\rm gas}\,,

(26)

in analogy to
Equation 20
and
Equation 25
.

In the previous sections we posited that the local gas velocity is set by the relevant diffusive velocity at the interface
v_{\rm gas}\approx v_{\rm diff}
with
v_{\rm diff}=v_{\rm cond},\,v_{\rm turb}
. However, here, this provides no constraint, as the relevant diffusive velocity is the local gas velocity. We will see in the next section that a consistency condition on cooling at the wind bubble’s interface will lead to a meaningful estimate of the numerical diffusive velocity as

v_{\rm num}\approx\frac{\Delta x}{t_{\rm cool}}\,.

(27)

II.5
Relation Between
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
and Cooling

As we discuss in
Appendix A
, the assumption of an entirely ‘steady’ flow in the bubble interior necessitates that most of the mass and energy in the bubble is advected out of it. As discussed in
Section II.4
, this transfer can be accomplished by the diffusive flux associated with a turbulent boundary layer in the physical case; numerical diffusion can alternatively play this role if the turbulence is not sufficiently resolved. Whether physically or numerically driven, mixing of the energy from the interior with mass from the shell can produce temperatures where cooling is very strong, so the energy mixed into the shell can be largely radiated away. This loss of energy is essentially a consistency condition on the assumption of a ‘steady’ interior: if energy were not lost, there would be no movement of energy out of the hot gas bubble, as is required by the steady flow assumption.

If we assume for simplicity that this cooling is dominated by gas at
T_{\rm pk}
, given in
Equation 17
, and that the volume of gas at this temperature is
V_{\rm pk}
then the cooling rate of this gas is

\dot{E}_{\rm cool,pk}=\left(\frac{P(T_{\rm pk})}{k_{B}T_{\rm pk}}\right)^{2}\Lambda(T_{\rm pk})V_{\rm pk}=\frac{P(T_{\rm pk})V_{\rm pk}}{t_{\rm cool}(T_{\rm pk})}

(28)

where
\Lambda(T)
is the cooling function (we have ignored heating here),
P(T_{\rm pk})
is the pressure of the gas in
V_{\rm pk}
, and
t_{\rm cool}
is the cooling time (
Equation 16
). If we assume that the cooling layer is in pressure equilibrium with the hot gas, as seems reasonable from resolved simulations
[
18
,
26
]
, then we have
P(T_{\rm pk})=P_{\rm hot}
.

We can define the thickness of the cooling region as

w_{\rm cool}\equiv\frac{V_{\rm pk}}{A_{\rm bub}}\,.

(29)

Then, with
\dot{E}_{\rm cool,pk}\approx L_{w}
from the assumption of strong cooling, and using
Equation 11
and
Equation 28
, we have

\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle\approx\frac{3}{8}\frac{\sqrt{\Lambda(T_{\rm pk})V_{\rm pk}L_{w}}}{A_{\rm bub}k_{B}T_{\rm pk}}\propto\left(\frac{w_{\rm cool}}{A_{\rm bub}}\right)^{1/2}\,.

(30)

The last proportionality has ignored factors having to do with the exact properties of the cooling (as well as the source luminosity), assuming them to be roughly constant.

It is worth discussing what the above relation is telling us about both the bubble and boundary layer dynamics and thermodynamics. Suppose that, at a given pressure,
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
were too high for
Equation 30
to hold. This incongruence can be seen as a failure from either the assumption of cooling matching the energy flux being provided (
\dot{E}_{\rm cool,pk}\approx L_{w}
), or in the context of a steady state bubble (
Equation 11
). From the perspective of the bubble,
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
being too large means that the bubble is losing energy at its outer surface faster than it gains energy through the wind shock, which will lead to pressure loss. If we take
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
as given, steady-state can be re-established when de-pressurization reaches the point at which
Equation 11
is again satisfied (which would also involve an increase in
R_{\rm f}
from
Equation A4
). In our picture, this decrease in pressure also affects the rate of cooling in the interface layer, with the new lower pressure driving down cooling from
Equation 28
. From the perspective of the cooling layer,
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
being too large means that it is being provided energy faster than it can cool it away.
If we enforce, in this thought experiment, fixed
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
and isobaric conditions, the build up of energy resulting from insufficient cooling would cause an expansion of the cooling layer (an increase in
w_{\rm cool}
). This would continue until the cooling layer was once again in equilibrium according to
Equation 30
.

We emphasize that in the resolved case,
Equation 30
is not a
prediction
of the value of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
but rather a
consistency condition
on the relationship between
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
, the properties of the cooling, and the geometry of the cooling layer (
w_{\rm cool}
&
A_{\rm bub}
). In fact, if we replace
P(T_{\rm pk})
in
Equation 28
with
Equation 11
and assume
\dot{E}_{\rm cool,pk}=L_{w}
as above we have

\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle=\frac{3}{8}\frac{w_{\rm cool}}{t_{\rm cool}}\sim\frac{w_{\rm cool}}{t_{\rm cool}}\,.

(31)

Comparing this with
Equation 20
and
Equation 25
we see that, with the appropriate choices of
w_{\rm cool}=\lambda_{F}
and
\ell_{\rm cool}
respectively, this condition approximately holds.

In the unresolved case,
Equation 30
must still hold, and therefore acts as a condition on
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
that effectively contributes to setting its value if
w_{\rm cool}
is numerically rather than physically controlled. In fact, if we assume
w_{\rm cool}\approx\Delta x
in
Equation 31
, we get a similar relation to the resolved cases:
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle\sim\Delta x/t_{\rm cool}
, as foreshadowed by
Equation 27
.

In the present simulations, the enhanced cooling in the shell can also lead to a pressure dip if
w_{\rm cool}
is not fully resolved. We will return to the measured scaling behavior of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
in the context of our simulations in
Section IV.5
.

II.6
Momentum Evolution with Turbulent Dissipation

We now wish to give a model for how the bubble should evolve in the situation that dissipative physics is controlled by turbulent mixing at small scales, as in
Section II.4.2
, rather than numerics. We specify here to the case of turbulent mixing since, as demonstrated by the right panel of
Figure 2
, we expect turbulent heat transport to dominate over conduction in most real WBBs. To this end, we can assume
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
is equal to a resolved, turbulent dissipation velocity
v_{t}(\ell_{\rm cool})
(see
Equation 25
). We can also replace
A_{\rm bub}
with a fractal model for its evolution, given by

A_{\rm bub}(\ell)=4\pi R_{\rm bub}^{2}\left(\frac{R_{\rm bub}}{\ell}\right)^{d}\,,

(32)

where
d
is the so-called ‘excess fractal dimension.’ This is discussed in depth in
Fielding et al. [26]
,
Lancaster et al. [77]
and we return to it in
Section IV.4
.

Substituting for
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
and using
A_{\rm bub}(\ell_{\rm cool})
in
Equation 11
, we arrive at

P_{\rm hot}=\frac{3L_{w}}{32\pi R_{\rm bub}^{2}v_{t}(L)}\left(\frac{R_{\rm bub}}{L}\right)^{-d}\left(\frac{t_{\rm cool}}{t_{\rm eddy}(L)}\right)^{\frac{d-p}{1-p}}\,.

(33)

Since
t_{\rm cool}
depends on
P_{\rm hot}
(assuming the cooling layer is isobaric; we will return to this below), we can rearrange the above to solve for the dependence of
P_{\rm hot}
on the other parameters. Doing this, and setting
\alpha_{p}=4\pi R_{\rm bub}^{2}P_{\rm hot}/\dot{p}_{w}
we can derive

\alpha_{p}=\frac{3V_{w}}{16v_{t}(L)}\left[\frac{3L_{w}\Lambda(T_{\rm pk})}{32\pi Lv_{t}^{2}(L)(k_{B}T_{\rm pk})^{2}}\right]^{\frac{p-d}{1-2p+d}}\\
\times\left(\frac{R_{\rm bub}}{L}\right)^{\frac{d-2p+dp}{1-2p+d}}\,.

(34)

It should be noted that the above relation puts no restrictions on physically allowable values of
\alpha_{p}
or the pressure. In reality,
\alpha_{p}\geq 1
and has an upper limit imposed by energy conservation from the
Weaver et al. [145]
solution.

If we choose
p=1/3
for Kolmogorov turbulence and
d=1/2
as seems reasonable in our hydrodynamical simulations (see
Section IV.4
), we have

\alpha_{p}=\frac{3}{16}\frac{V_{w}}{v_{t}(L)}\left[\frac{3L_{w}\Lambda(T_{\rm pk})}{32\pi Lv_{t}^{2}(L)(k_{B}T_{\rm pk})^{2}}\right]^{-1/5}\,;

(35)

i.e.
\alpha_{p}
is independent of the bubble size
R_{\rm bub}
, and also (based on the
-1/5
power) insensitive to the details of the cooling function. As we can see in the right panel of
Figure 3
, the physically motivated choice of
p
and
d
that results in a
\alpha_{p}
which is constant in time is somewhat serendipitous: large changes from
d=1/2
result in large changes in the exponent of
\alpha_{p}
with
R_{\rm bub}
. Fortunately, for reasonable values for the HD case, near
d=1/2
, this exponent does not change drastically.

We can re-write
Equation 35
in a more physically motivated way as

\alpha_{p}=\frac{3}{16}\frac{V_{w}}{v_{t}(L)}\left[\frac{32\pi}{3}\frac{P_{\rm hot}L^{3}(t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm eddy}(L))}{L_{w}t_{\rm eddy}(L)}\right]^{1/5}\,.

(36)

Consider the last fraction in the square bracket term. The numerator is a measure of the amount of thermal energy that is able to be stored in the shocked gas:
P_{\rm hot}L^{3}
is the thermal energy in a turbulent eddy and
t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm eddy}(L)
is a measure of the fraction of energy that can be retained before being lost to dissipation and cooling. The denominator is a measure of how much energy is being provided to the bubble. If the energy that the bubble can retain (numerator) is large compared to the rate at which energy is provided to the shocked wind (denominator), then the momentum enhancement is higher.

If we take quantities relevant to the high-resolution HD simulations that we present below (
L=4\,{\rm pc}
,
v_{t}(L)=600\,{\rm km/s}
,
T_{\rm pk}=1.59\times 10^{4}\,{\rm K}
,
\Lambda(T_{\rm pk})=2\times 10^{-22}\,{\rm erg/s}\,{\rm cm}^{3}
,
L_{w}=4.875\times 10^{37}\,{\rm erg/s}
,
V_{w}=3230\,{\rm km/s}
,
\overline{n}_{\rm H}=100\,{\rm cm}^{-3}
) the quantity in square brackets above is roughly
1/5
and the prefactor in front is roughly unity, implying
\alpha_{p}\approx 0.2
and constant in time. The fact that
\alpha_{p}<1
is simply because we have not explicitly enforced momentum conservation in the derivation given above. Physically, however,
\alpha_{p}\geq 1
and is bounded from above by the corresponding energy-driven solution from
Weaver et al. [145]
. These bounds are shown as grey regions in the left-panel of
Figure 3
for the
L_{w}
and
V_{w}
as quoted above and an assumed mean background density that is identical to our simulations.

The fact that the derived
\alpha_{p}<1
for the parameters quoted above simply means that turbulent mixing over a fractal surface is likely more than capable of dispersing enough energy for the bubble’s evolution to approach the momentum-conserving solution (
\alpha_{p}=1
) as posited in
Lancaster et al. [77]
. Motivated by the fact that turbulence is likely reduced in the, more realistic, MHD case we instead plot the
\alpha_{p}
evolution with
R_{\rm bub}
according to
Equation 34
for an assumed
v_{t}(L)=10\,\,{\rm km}\,{\rm s}^{-1}
, slightly above the observed Alfvén speeds in our MHD simulations. Though the turbulence in the hot gas in our MHD simulations lies far above this value (see
Figure 15
), this choice is likely more representative of turbulence in resolved MHD mixing layers. This choice results in moderate
\alpha_{p}
enhancement above unity. Interestingly, in this formalism, fixing other parameters and reducing the fractal dimension of the bubble results in a higher
\alpha_{p}
, but one that decreases with time (
R_{\rm bub}
).

III
Methods

To provide a test of the theory of WBB evolution discussed above, we run three-dimensional (magneto)hydrodynamic simulations, detailed below.

III.1
Numerical Methods

We use the
Athena
code
[
129
]
. The numerical methods employed in these simulations are nearly identical to those used in
Lancaster et al. [78]
; in particular we use the same ‘hybrid/subcell method’ for injecting wind mass (tracked as a passive scalar field
\rho_{\rm wind}
) and energy onto the grid and the same basic setup, which we will detail further below. However, unlike
Lancaster et al. [78]
, we employ the chemistry and radiative cooling module of
Kim et al. [63]
, which tracks the non-equilibrium photochemistry of molecular, atomic, and ionized hydrogen as well as the equilibrium chemistry of carbon- and oxygen-bearing species (
{\rm C}
,
{\rm C}^{+}
,
{\rm CO}
,
{\rm O}
, and
{\rm O}^{+}
), and their effects on the thermal heating and cooling of the medium. For the heating and ionization of neutral gas, we adopt uniform FUV background radiation of
Draine [15]
and cosmic ionization rate per hydrogen
\xi_{\rm cr}=2\times 10^{-16}\,{\rm s}^{-1}

[
51
]
. For cooling by helium and metals at high temperature (
T>2\times 10^{4}\,{\rm K}
), we use the tabulated collisional ionization equilibrium (CIE) cooling function from
Gnat & Ferland [38]
. Our hydrogen cooling is fully dynamical, dependent on the local radiation field and ionization state of the gas and therefore not assumed to be CIE.

Our simulations are all run using the linearized Roe Riemann solver for flux calculations, the piecewise linear method (PLM) for second-order spatial reconstruction, and the unsplit second-order van Leer integrator for temporal evolution
[
129
, and references therein]
. We also include the ‘H-correction’ of
Sanders et al. [117]
to prevent carbuncle instability when the difference in signal speeds in different grid-aligned directions is greater than
20~\,{\rm km}\,{\rm s}^{-1}
. We do not include gravity in these simulations, and we use open/outflow boundary conditions.

We run simulations of both hydrodynamics (HD) and magneto-hydrodynamics (MHD). In our MHD simulations, we employ Athena’s default constrained transport (CT) method for evolving the magnetic field, which we will refer to as the
CT-Contact
scheme

. However, we found that this method is subject to numerical instabilities in regions of our simulation with strong source terms such as the bubble’s edge (where cooling is strong) and the wind injection region. In order to suppress these numerical instabilities we implement the upwind constrained transport (UCT) HLL scheme of
Londrillo & Del Zanna [82]
as described in
Mignone & Del Zanna [93]
. As the
UCT-HLL
method is more diffusive

III.2
Simulation Description

In all of the simulations detailed here, we first set up a turbulent, multi-phase background before initializing a constant mechanical luminosity,
L_{w}
, and mass loss rate,
\dot{M}_{w}
, wind at the center of the domain. Here we choose parameters of our background medium to be representative of a GMC with a total mass of
M_{\rm cl}=10^{5}\,M_{\odot}
and a radius of
R_{\rm cl}=20\,{\rm pc}
. In particular, we initialize our simulations within a box of side length
L_{\rm box}=2R_{\rm cl}=40\,{\rm pc}
with a given number of resolving elements per grid-aligned direction
N_{\rm res}\equiv L_{\rm box}/\Delta x
. We set the density everywhere to
n_{{\rm H},0}=86.25\,\,{\rm cm}^{-3}
and initialize a Gaussian, random-phase turbulent velocity field with power spectrum
|v_{k}|^{2}\propto k^{-4}
for wavenumbers in the range
2\leq kL_{\rm box}/(2\pi)\leq 64
. The normalization of the velocity field is chosen so that the total kinetic energy in the domain is twice the binding energy,
2W_{\rm cl}=(6/5)GM_{\rm cl}^{2}/R_{\rm cl}
, of a uniform-density spherical cloud of mass
M_{\rm cl}
and radius
R_{\rm cl}
. The initial one-dimensional velocity dispersion is therefore
\sim 3\,\,{\rm km}\,{\rm s}^{-1}
. In our simulations with magnetic fields we additionally set a uniform magnetic field everywhere of
\mathbf{B}_{0}=B_{0}\mathbf{\hat{z}}
with
B_{0}=13.5\,\mu{\rm G}
. This value was chosen based on setting the dimensionless mass-to-flux ratio equal to 2 as in
Kim et al. [67]
.

We then allow the simulation to evolve, without any further turbulent driving, until the total kinetic energy has become equal to
W_{\rm cl}
. The thermal pressure is initially set everywhere to
P/k_{B}=6000\,{\rm K}\,\,{\rm cm}^{-3}
. While this is above the equilibrium pressure at these densities, the simulation domain quickly relaxes to its equilibrium pressure during the initial turbulent evolution.

After the turbulent evolution, which lasts approximately
0.7\,{\rm Myr}
, the wind is turned on. For all simulations, we assume a stellar mass of
5000\,M_{\odot}
with stellar wind luminosity per unit stellar mass of
L_{w}/M_{*}=9.75\times 10^{33}\,{\rm erg}\,{\rm s}^{-1}\,M_{\odot}^{-1}
. We consider two different values of the mass-loss rate per unit stellar mass:
\dot{M}_{w}/M_{*}=10^{-2}\,{\rm Myr}^{-1}
and
2.965\times 10^{-3}\,{\rm Myr}^{-1}
. The first corresponds to values used in the simulations of
Lancaster et al. [78]
while the latter is more representative of the value derived from Starburst99 for a solar metallicity population with a Kroupa IMF
[
81
,
80
]
. These choices of mass loss rate correspond to wind-velocities of
V_{w}=1759
and
3230\,\,{\rm km}\,{\rm s}^{-1}
and the momentum input rate of
\dot{p}_{w}=8.795\times 10^{4}
and
4.788\times 10^{4}M_{\odot}\,{\rm km}\,{\rm s}^{-1}\,{\rm Myr}^{-1}
, respectively, for the given value of
L_{w}/M_{*}
. The higher wind velocity is our fiducial value. The simulations with
\dot{M}_{w}/M_{*}=10^{-2}\,{\rm Myr}^{-1}
and
V_{w}=1759\,\,{\rm km}\,{\rm s}^{-1}
will be referred to as our ‘mass loss rate modified’ simulations, using the addendum
Mwmod
. The full parameter suite of the simulations is detailed in
Table 1
.

For all simulations we inject mass, momentum, and energy using the hybrid feedback method detailed in
Lancaster et al. [78]
. Our effective injection radius,
r_{\rm fb}/\Delta x=3.2,\,3.2,
and
6.4
for the simulations with
N_{\rm res}=128,\,256,
and
512
respectively.
Pittard et al. [108]
demonstrated in one-dimensional simulations that it was necessary for
r_{\rm fb}
to be less than a maximum value,
r_{\rm inj,max}=\sqrt{\dot{p}_{w}/4\pi P_{\rm amb}}
, in order for a WBB to be successfully inflated, with
P_{\rm amb}
the ambient pressure in the medium. For our default parameter values
r_{\rm inj,max}=55\,{\rm pc}
, which is larger than the domain size of our simulations. Thus, all of our simulations lie in the regime
r_{\rm fb}\ll r_{\rm inj,max}/10
recommended by
Pittard et al. [108]
in order to properly capture energy-driven bubbles.

III.3
Measurement Details

Throughout the following section we will refer to various definitions of thermal phases of the gas in our simulations. These are generally similar to those outlined in
Lancaster et al. [78]
, with the exception that we re-label ‘Ionized Gas’ as ‘Warm-Hot Ionized Medium’ (WHIM) and make a few small changes to the temperature ranges given our new cooling module; these are given in
Table 2
. The wind phases are as in
Lancaster et al. [78]
where the shocked wind is defined as all gas hotter than
10^{6}\,{\rm K}
and with a radial velocity less than half the wind velocity. The free wind is defined as all gas with a radial velocity that is greater than half
V_{w}
as well as all gas contained within the feedback region.

In
Figure 4
and
Figure 5
we provide a rendering of our highest resolution fiducial simulation:
N512_hydro
and
N512_mhd
respectively. It is clear that the bubble’s geometry is very complicated and highly ‘folded’. It is also clear from the top-right panel that the
T=10^{6}\,{\rm K}
iso-temperature surface encloses nearly all of the WBB’s volume.

We are particularly concerned in this work with properties of the gas near the bubble’s surface. In order to precisely locate this surface within our simulations we follow
Fielding et al. [26]
and
Lancaster et al. [78]
by using the
marching_cubes
algorithm from
scikit-image

[
139
]
. Specifically, we measure iso-temperature surfaces at
\log_{10}\left(T/{\rm K}\right)=3.75-6
in logarithmic steps of
0.25
(10 temperatures) and using step sizes of
\ell/\Delta x=1,\,2,\,4,\,8,\,16,\,32,\,\&\,64
. We can only perform such measurements as post-processing on simulation snapshots, which we output from the simulations every
\sim 0.01\,{\rm Myr}
(100 per simulation). A three-dimensional rendering of such an iso-temperature surface is given in the left-panel of
Figure 4
for
T=10^{6}\,{\rm K}
and a spacing of
\ell/\Delta x=1
for the
N_{\rm res}=512
simulation.

After using the
marching_cubes
algorithm to define the iso-temperature surfaces we calculate the total area of the surface
A_{\rm bub}(T,\ell)
and interpolate the fluid variables to each facet of the surface that is returned by the algorithm. We then calculate the component of the fluid velocity normal to each facet of the surface,
v_{\rm out}
, and the dot product of the facet normal and the radial vector,
\mathbf{\hat{n}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{r}}
. The quantities
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle\mathbf{\hat{r}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\right\rangle
, presented below, correspond to the area-weighted average over all the faces of the iso-temperature surfaces. Unless it is stated otherwise, we use the iso-temperature surfaces defined by
T=10^{6}\,{\rm K}
and measured on the resolution scale
\ell=\Delta x
for all analyses detailed below.

We will also be concerned with the distribution of gas in the simulation as a function of temperature. To investigate this in detail we take each snapshot of the simulations and compute the volume-weighted, pressure-weighted, and cooling-weighted histograms in temperature in 200 logarithmically spaced temperature bins between
10^{1.5}\,{\rm K}<T<10^{8.5}\,{\rm K}
. For these measurement we restrict to gas that has a wind mass fraction
f_{\rm wind}=\rho_{\rm wind}/\rho>10^{-4}
and a radial velocity
v_{r}<V_{w}/2
to avoid contamination from the background and free wind respectively. However, the former constraint does somewhat bias measurement in our MHD simulations, where the momentum from the forward-shock of the wind bubble is carried away from wind-polluted material by MHD waves.

Further details on measurements of specific physical quantities are presented as needed in the remainder of the paper.

IV.1
Radial Momentum

One of the most important aspects of WBBs is the dynamical effect they have on their surroundings, exemplified by the bulk momentum that they inject
[
54
]
, so it is natural to first investigate this aspect of our simulations. Here we measure the total radial momentum carried by the wind bubble by computing evolution of the total radial momentum within the simulation domain, and then subtracting out the evolution of the radial momentum from a simulation that is identical except it does not include wind feedback. This comparison to the no-feedback simulation is necessary since, due to our outflow boundary conditions and the turbulent conditions, the total radial momentum in the simulation is non-zero even when there is no feedback. We use this method instead of the method of
Lancaster et al. [78]
, which measured total radial momentum in gas that included some fraction of wind material, as we found that in our MHD simulations momentum is able to be carried out of wind-polluted material by MHD waves, which has been observed previously
[
98
, e.g.]
.

In
Lancaster et al. [77]
we posited that turbulent mixing in WBBs followed by radiative cooling was strong enough to reduce the bubble evolution to nearly the ‘momentum conserving’ regime. That is, the momentum communicated to the surroundings by the bubble is very close to that put in by the wind,
p_{r}\sim\dot{p}_{w}t
or
\alpha_{p}\sim 1
. The simulations of
Lancaster et al. [78]
showed measured values of
\alpha_{p}
between 1 and 4 (see their Fig. 8). Additionally, these simulations showed that the measured energy retention fraction was consistent with the prediction for the measured values of
\alpha_{p}
. In
Lancaster et al. [77]
,
Lancaster et al. [78]
we further argued that in the case of a radial flow in the bubble, the speed at the edge of the bubble,
\sim(3/16)V_{w}/\alpha_{p}
, should match the effective diffusion velocity that carries material into the shell. Here, from
Equation 15
, we have taken a further step to see that the value of
\alpha_{p}
is actually determined by the interplay of the bubble’s geometry and dissipation at its surface in time. The quasi-steady state assumption imposes the global constraint
\alpha_{p}\approx(R_{\rm bub}/R_{\rm f})^{2}
, which has a lower limit of unity, but there is no intrinsic reason why the ratio should be exactly 1 or why it should be constant in time.

As we can see upon inspection of
Figure 6
, this is indeed not the case. Here we show the total radial momentum evolution in our default HD simulations with two different input wind momenta,
\dot{p}_{w}
. As described in
Section III
, this difference is mediated by a difference in
\dot{M}_{w}
, while both simulations have the same mechanical wind luminosity,
L_{w}
. Though the input momenta,
\dot{p}_{w}
, are different by about a factor of 2 between these simulations, the momentum they impart to background are the same to within 10%. That is,
\alpha_{p}
is a factor
\sim 2
larger in the simulation with lower momentum injection rate (having lower
\dot{M}_{w}
, higher
V_{w}
). As we will discuss further in
Section IV.3
, the agreement in
\dot{p}_{r}
is largely due to the fact that momentum input to the shell is governed by dissipation processes at the bubble’s surface (which set
P_{\rm hot}
through through
Equation 11
), and these dissipation processes remain largely unchanged between these two simulations. For fixed
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle A_{\rm bub}/R_{\rm bub}^{2}
,
Equation 13
says that
\dot{p}_{r}\propto L_{w}
, i.e. the same value for the two models shown, consistent with the simulation result of
Figure 6
. At the same time,
Equation 15
says that
\alpha_{p}\propto V_{w}
; this implies a larger value for
\alpha_{p}
in the fiducial simulation, which has higher
V_{w}
and lower
\dot{p}_{w}
.

The second interesting issue we would like to introduce in
Figure 7
is the
opposite
resolution dependence of the total radial momentum in our HD and MHD simulations. While the HD simulations have decreasing momentum output with increased resolution, the MHD simulations have the opposite behaviour. As we will discuss below, and should be clear from
Equation 15
, this must be solely due to differences in the relative scaling of dissipation and geometry between the two types of simulation.

The Interface

In
Figure 10
we show key properties of the bubble’s interior and interface as a function of its sphere-equivalent area,

. In the left hand panels of
Figure 10
we can see the difference in HD (top) vs. MHD (bottom) bubble dynamics that we saw in
Figure 7
: the opposite trend with resolution of the bubble’s pressure (and hence radial force through
Equation 9
; see
Figure 8
).

Since
Figure 9
shows that
Equation 11
is an accurate predictor of the bubble’s interior pressure, we can attribute the difference in resolution dependence of
P_{\rm hot}
to a difference in resolution dependence of the quantities
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
and
A_{\rm bub}
defined at the interface, which we show in the middle and right panels of
Figure 10
. In particular, since
P_{\rm hot}\propto(\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle A_{\rm bub})^{-1}
and consistently decreases at higher resolution in the HD simulations, the increase of
A_{\rm bub}
must be steeper than the decrease of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
at smaller
\Delta x
. In the HD simulations, we have found dependence on resolution varying roughly as
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle\propto(\Delta x)^{1/4}
and
A_{\rm bub}\propto(\Delta x)^{-1/2}
: thus, increased surface area wins over decreased outflow velocity normal to the surface, which in these simulations ultimately is due to numerical dissipation.
In contrast, the relative scalings with resolution in the MHD simulations
nearly
compensate for one another:
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle\propto(\Delta x)^{1/4}
and
A_{\rm bub}\propto(\Delta x)^{-1/4}
. In the end, however, the decreased dissipation marginally wins over the increased bubble surface area, resulting in the increased pressure and therefore increased momentum input to the surroundings.

The question remains as to the origin of this dependence on numerical resolution,
\Delta x
. In addition, we would like to understand why
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
decreases and
A_{\rm bub}/(4\pi R_{\rm bub}^{2})
increases for larger bubble radius (a proxy for later time in
Figure 10
). We investigate the scalings of
A_{\rm bub}
in
Section IV.4
and
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
in
Section IV.5
, below.

IV.4
Scaling of
A_{\rm bub}

As has been discussed extensively in the literature, dynamically unstable interfaces can lead to fractal, self-similar structures
[
26
,
132
,
77
,
78
]
. We will show in this section that the scaling of
A_{\rm bub}
with time and resolution can be entirely explained through a fractal model for the geometry of these WBBs. We will leave discussion as to the origin of this fractal structure for
Section V
.

There are several ways to think about fractal structure. Traditionally in mathematics
[
86
,
148
]
and in the astrophysical literature
[
92
,
122
,
22
]
, describing something as a fractal means parameterizing how some aspect of its structure, say
f
, varies as a function of scale, say
\ell
, as a power law in that scale,
f(\ell)\propto\ell^{a}
for some
a
. For example, how the measurement of the size of the boundary of some region varies as a function of the physical scale on which you measure it: like measuring the coastline of England with a mile long ruler (not able to describe the structure of small bays and inlets) versus a foot long ruler (maybe not able to describe every rock). For our current purposes, and generally in the mixing-layer literature, we are interested in the area of the WBB interface and how this varies with scale. We can describe this scaling as
[
78
,
26
, see e.g.]

d_{\ell}\equiv-\frac{d\log A_{\rm bub}(\ell)}{d\log\ell}\,.

(39)

We see that
d_{\ell}=0
corresponds to an interface with no finer-scale structure (area does not get larger when measured at smaller scales) and
d_{\ell}>0
corresponds to the formation of smaller scale structure (area increases as scale on which it is measured decreases).

In mathematics, fractal structures can be infinitely self-similar, continuously forming structure on smaller and smaller scales. Physical structures, in contrast, have to be regulated and eventually become smooth on some scale. This implies that
d_{\ell}
is not constant as a function of
\ell
but must vary, as it must return to
0
at the scale on which structure becomes smooth. In the context of real WBBs this is
at least
the mean free path of individual particles, at which point the idea of a boundary between fluid elements becomes meaningless, and is more likely to have to do with dissipative physics. In our ideal (M)HD simulations, however, this scale is the resolution scale
10
10

10

It is interesting to note that, in the mathematical world of ideal fluid dynamics, there is no regulatory scale, and one
can
form infinitely self-similar structures.
.

IV.5
Value and Scaling of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle

In
Section II.4
we discussed the dissipative scales that need to be resolved in order for
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
to be set by physical processes. When these processes are not well resolved and cooling is strong enough so that the local value of
v_{\rm out}
(the velocity of the fluid into the surface, relative to the surface) is large compared to the velocity of the surface,
W_{\rm bub}
, then the local numerical dissipation is set by the local grid velocity,
v_{\rm out}
. But, as we motivated in
Section II.4
, it is the dissipation which should be setting
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
in the first place.

In
Section II.5
we described how cooling provides a constraint on the value of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
. In particular, through its role in setting
P_{\rm hot}
(through
Equation 11
),
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
sets the pressure in the cooling layer (assuming the cooling layer is iso-baric with the bubble interior) and hence sets the cooling rate. Requiring a given cooling rate then requires a specific value of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
.

For our numerical simulations, in fact the cooling layer is not perfectly iso-baric with the hot bubble interior, but instead has a pressure dip:

f_{\rm dip}\equiv\frac{P(T_{\rm pk})}{P_{\rm hot}}\,.

(42)

Accounting for this in
Equation 30
gives us

\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle_{\rm pred}=\frac{3\sqrt{\Lambda(T_{\rm pk})V_{\rm pk}L_{w}}}{8A_{\rm bub}k_{B}T_{\rm pk}}f_{\rm dip}\,,

(43)

where we have included the subscript for clarity of comparison in
Figure 13
. If we remove the factors that are explicitly dependent on the details of cooling we have

\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle_{\rm pred}\propto f_{\rm dip}\sqrt{\frac{w_{\rm cool}}{A_{\rm bub}}}

(44)

where we have used
V_{\rm pk}=w_{\rm cool}A_{\rm bub}
with
w_{\rm cool}
the thickness of the cooling layer.

In the left hand panel of
Figure 13
we show
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
measured from the simulations against the prediction of
Equation 43
, where we take
V_{\rm pk}
as the volume of gas with temperatures
10^{4}<T/{\rm K}<4.8\times 10^{4}
. This is chosen to match the rough temperature range over which the highest rate of cooling occurs (see
Appendix D
). We measure
T_{\rm pk}
and
P(T_{\rm pk})
using the cooling and pressure weighted temperature histograms as detailed in
Section III.3
, and
\Lambda(T_{\rm pk})
measured by interpolating the collisional ionization equilibrium (CIE) cooling curve of
Kim & Ostriker [57]
.

Using this CIE
\Lambda(T)
is not strictly faithful to the non-equilibrium cooling that we are employing in these simulations. While we could employ the equilibrium cooling curve from
Kim et al. [63]
for the non-photoionized case (e.g. top panel of Figure 3 there), this is negligibly different than
Kim & Ostriker [57]
for the temperature ranges of interest here. Additionally, the true cooling in our simulations will differ from the equilibrium curve of
Kim et al. [63]
due to the combination of turbulent and numerical mixing of the electron fraction,
x_{e}
, across the cooling layer. The CIE cooling curve is therefore a useful simplification. Using a single value of
T_{\rm pk}
is of course a great simplification itself, since there is a wide range of temperatures where cooling occurs in these simulations. It is less obvious, but using a single value of
P(T_{\rm pk})
is also strictly inaccurate since, in these simulations, there are large pressure fluctuations in the cooling layer.

With these caveats in mind, the correspondence seen in the left panel of
Figure 13
acts as a test of
Equation 44
, that is, the
scaling
of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
and its dependence on the width of the cooling layer
w_{\rm cool}
, the area of the bubble’s surface,
A_{\rm bub}
, and the pressure dip between the hot gas bubble and the cooling layer,
f_{\rm dip}
. In particular, this shows that the resolution and time dependence of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
can be explained through the resolution and time scaling of these quantities.

We investigate the resolution scaling of
w_{\rm cool}
(which is independent of time in all of our simulations) in the middle panel of
Figure 13
. This width appears to scale roughly linearly with
\Delta x
. This is not unexpected; in the strong-cooling limit
[
87
,
26
,
132
]
the cooling region will always occupy roughly a one-cell thickness volume over the interface unless the dissipative scales are resolved. The fact that
w_{\rm cool}<\Delta x
illustrates how quickly this transition occurs. Indeed, for a wider definition of the “cooling region” that includes hotter, not as rapidly cooling gas we would find
w_{\rm cool}>\Delta x
and scaling linearly with it. Together this means that the edge of the bubble always has approximately one cell at intermediate temperatures, but this is not always at the most rapidly cooling temperatures.

Finally, we’d like to investigate a relationship between the pressure at the cooling interface and
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
or equivalently
P_{\rm hot}
. In the right panel of
Figure 13
we show the fractional dip in pressure from the wind-bubble interior to the peak-cooling gas,
P(T_{\rm pk})/P_{\rm hot}
, as a function of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
. While
f_{\rm dip}
is strongly dependent on resolution itself, it is clear from this panel that
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
and
f_{\rm dip}
follow a relation to one another that is roughly independent of resolution but somewhat dependent on whether the medium is magnetized; thus, there may in principle be dependence on the numerical solver (MHD vs. HD).
As we discuss in
Appendix C
, this difference likely has to do with the details of the Riemann problem being solved at the interface and how these differ between the HD and MHD cases. With a fully resolved turbulent layer, this pressure dip should tend to unity
[
26
, i.e. disappear, see ]
. In any case, this relationship follows the regulatory pattern we set out at the beginning of this section: if
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
increases not only does
P_{\rm hot}
decrease (through the bubble structure relationship laid out in
Appendix A
) but the pressure dip also gets more extreme, leading to an
even smaller

P(T_{\rm pk})
and thus much decreased cooling. The opposite is true for decreased values of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
.

If we accept this final relation as empirical, then it closes the loop on our causality argument for the dynamics of the bubble:
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
adjusts so that the pressure in the cooling gas produces a fixed
\dot{E}_{\rm cool,pk}
given the volume of cooling gas
V_{\rm pk}=w_{\rm cool}A_{\rm bub}
. This adjustment of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
is not only dependent on how
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
relates to
P_{\rm hot}
but also how it relates to
f_{\rm dip}
. This means the detailed value of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
has a non-trivial dependence on the details of the Riemann solver and cooling, which determine
f_{\rm dip}
. With
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
determined,
P_{\rm hot}
then sets the dynamics of the bubble. In particular, the fact that the whole dynamics problem comes down to a question of energy regulation through cooling explains why the resulting momentum is independent of
\dot{p}_{w}
but has the same value as long as
L_{w}
is the same, as we saw in
Figure 6
. That is, after
P_{\rm hot}
adjusts such that the cooling rate matches the input energy rate in
Equation 28
, the same
P_{\rm hot}
sets the expansion rate through
Equation 9
(see
Figure 8
).

Given the complexity of the picture laid out above, and the fact that in the end it is dependent upon an empirical relationship between
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
and
f_{\rm dip}
, it is still quite possible that physical turbulent dissipation plays some role in the setting of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
as well. We will return to this in
Section IV.7
.

Our detailed analysis has produced significant insight into bubble dynamics and the reasons for resolution dependence in numerical simulations, but a number of mysteries remain. In particular, the relationship between the exact fraction of energy lost in the cooling layer, the fractional dip in the pressure,
f_{\rm dip}
, and
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
remains uncertain. These issues are likely explained by the detailed nature of numerical dissipation at the WBB interfaces and how this numerical dissipation is driven or modified by the presence of strong cooling and a strong mean velocity. As these details are not dependent upon the three-dimensional, dynamical nature of the simulations at hand, they are more likely to be made clear through the investigation of much simpler, one-dimensional simulations, where the parameters of the problem can be more easily tuned by hand. We leave this detailed investigation for future work.

Finally, we have found an empirical relation for
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
based solely on
R_{\rm bub}
and
\Delta x
as

\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle\propto R_{\rm bub}^{-1/2}\Delta x^{1/4}\,.

(45)

\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle

Combining the arguments from

and

along with
Equation 45

\alpha_{p}\propto V_{w}R_{\rm bub}^{1/2-d}\Delta x^{d-1/4}\,.

(48)

\alpha_{p,{\rm MHD}}\propto V_{w}R_{\rm bub}^{1/4}\,.

If we instead apply

IV.7
Relation Between
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
and Turbulence

In the TRML literature it has often been shown that the amount of cooling in the interface layers is straightforwardly dependent upon the amount of turbulence in the mixing layer
[
26
,
132
,
14
]
. Indeed, in
Lancaster et al. [77]
and
Lancaster et al. [78]
the amount of turbulence in the hot, shocked wind gas is causally tied to the amount of mixing, and therefore dissipation, at the wind bubble’s interface. While we will discuss nuances in this connection in
Section V.1
, it is worth reporting the relationship between turbulence and dissipation in the simulations at hand.

We quantify turbulence in our simulations by measuring the second-order structure function of the non-radial velocity field in the hot, shocked wind. We do this in exactly the same manner as described in Section 4.5 of
Lancaster et al. [78]
except that we restrict solely to the
T>10^{6}\,{\rm K}
gas
11
11

11

In
Lancaster et al. [78]
all gas with
v_{r}<V_{w}/2
and
T>2\times 10^{4}\,{\rm K}
was used.
. To give a brief description of the process here: we randomly choose 400 points in the shocked wind volume and calculate the average norm of the difference in the non-radial velocity field as a function of distance,
\ell
, away from the chosen point. We then use all 400 samplings of the square deviation as a function of distance to compute the mean over the samples and then take the square-root. This gives us
v_{t}(\ell)
, the second-order structure function as a function of scale.

In the left panel of
Figure 15
we show
v_{t}(4\,{\rm pc})
, which we found to be approximately the energy containing scale for the turbulence across all of the simulations, as a function of
R_{\rm bub}
. We see that, while the turbulent velocity is relatively well converged in the MHD simulations, it steadily increases with resolution in the HD simulations
12
12

12

We note that the turbulent velocity measured here is larger than in
Lancaster et al. [78]
likely due to both (i) our measurement being restricted to higher temperature gas and (ii)
V_{w}
being a factor of
\sim 1.8
larger in these simulations.
. This is likely due, at least in part, to decreased numerical viscosity at higher resolution, allowing for a better resolved dynamic range in the HD simulations. In contrast, small-scale turbulence is likely suppressed in the MHD simulations due to the stabilization of instabilities from the magnetic field. If this small-scale suppression is well-resolved in even the lowest resolution simulations, then we would not expect a better resolved dynamic range, and therefore increased turbulence, at higher resolution.

There is an additional effect that can have a significant impact on the resolution dependence of turbulence in the HD simulations. As we can see in
Figure 7
, in the HD simulations we have lower
\alpha_{p}
at higher resolution. Through
Equation 10
, this implies a smaller ratio of
R_{\rm bub}/R_{\rm f}
, which in turn implies more direct contact between the free-wind and dense clumps in the background cold gas. This process is directly observable in
Figure 4
. This direct contact between the dense gas and the free wind creates oblique bow shocks which seed vorticity, and hence turbulence, in the shocked wind material. Indeed, such bow-shocks were posited as a main source of turbulence in the hot gas in
Lancaster et al. [78]
. However, we still expect turbulent mixing at the interface, seeded by instabilities, even in the absence of this effect.

It is also interesting to compare
the turbulence and dissipation at the bubble’s surface, quantified by
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
. To this end we take the energy-containing scale turbulence, which we believe to be well resolved, and extrapolate it to the expected turbulent velocity at the resolution scale using
v_{t}(\Delta x)=v_{t}(L)(\Delta x/L)^{1/3}
, the expected scaling for Kolmogorov turbulence. We can see from the right panel of
Figure 15
, where we compare
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
with this scaled turbulent velocity, that they have a close to linear relation with one another, as one would expect from recent work in TRMLs
[
26
,
77
,
132
,
14
]
. It is encouraging that this relationship appears more linear in the higher resolution simulations. Thus, while our current simulations are not fully resolved, this result suggests that dissipation mediated by turbulence could be achieved with an enhancement in resolution. The right panel of Figure 2 also indicates that at least for the pressures present at late time, we are close to being able to resolve
\ell_{\rm cool}

V
Discussion

V.1
Relation to Past Work

A key thesis of this work, laid out in
Section II
and
Section IV
, is that the dynamics of WBBs are intimately dependent upon the movement of energy across their bubble-shell interfaces. In particular, we have emphasized how this depends both on the multi-scale
geometry
of these interfaces and the small-scale
dissipation
processes that act across them. These two effects acting together make for a particularly difficult physical problem.

If we ignore geometry, considering one-dimensional problems, the role of dissipation across such interfaces has long been solved under the further assumptions of time-steady, isobaric flows
[
151
,
8
,
52
,
64
]
. This is unfortunately not the picture that applies to WBBs. The full complications of the problem were circumvented in
Weaver et al. [145]
by ignoring the contribution of cooling at the interface, deriving a solution that required all conductive heat flux to be balanced by evaporation of mass from the shell into the hot, wind bubble.

El-Badry et al. [18]
ameliorated this picture by running spherically symmetric, radiative, one-dimensional simulations of super-bubble evolution with both explicit Spitzer conductivity (
\propto T^{5/2}
) and a constant diffusivity at low temperatures,
\kappa_{\rm mix}
(meant to account approximately for turbulent mixing) and showed that a predictable, constant fraction of the wind energy is lost to cooling within the interface layer,
\dot{E}_{\mathrm{cool}}\propto\kappa_{\rm mix}^{1/2}

[
18
, see Eq. 36 of]
. This essentially solves the problem from a ‘purely diffusive’, one-dimensional picture, albeit by appealing, in an effective way, to a mechanism of diffusion that is inherently three-dimensional: turbulence. We note that the problem, of theoretical interest no doubt, that appeals to only Spitzer-like heat dissipation in the one-dimensional context but includes cooling in the dynamical WBB picture has not been fully resolved
[
18
, in the numerical sense, see Appendix A of]
.

Turbulence spans both sides of the divide we have drawn here between dissipation and geometry, and it is worth emphasizing which aspects of turbulence lie on either side in the context of these interfaces. One can treat turbulence purely as a micro-physical dissipation, as it is treated in
El-Badry et al. [18]
and countless other astrophysical applications
[
123
,
114
, e.g. ]
. However, the
root
of this ‘effective’ micro-physical dissipation is itself geometry: turbulence is
stretching-enhanced diffusion
, it only acts to increase the surface area over which true micro-physical dissipation may act. As we discussed in
Section II.4.2
, the scale on which turbulence effectively becomes micro-physical for these interfaces, is the cooling length,
\ell_{\rm cool}
, at which point cooling effectively ‘stands still’ as the turbulent cascade efficiently mixes material. Above this scale, larger scale turbulence acts to significantly enhance the surface area over which energy transport takes place, while being too slow at mixing to be of actual importance in mediating that energy transport. This is the geometric role that it serves. The distinction between these two regimes is essentially about the ratio of cooling time to the eddy-turnover time on the largest scales (quantified by
\xi
in
Fielding et al. [26]
and the Damköhler number,
{\rm Da}
in
Tan et al. [132]
).

While
El-Badry et al. [18]
simply treated
\kappa_{\rm mix}
as a parameter,
Lancaster et al. [77]
linked its expected value to predictions from the theory of
Fielding et al. [26]
discussed in
Section II.4.2
. Folded into this theory, and the model for diffusivity given in
Lancaster et al. [77]
, is the geometric role that turbulence plays, accounted for using an additional factor for the increase in fractal area as a bubble expands. While, in this case, the energy lost to cooling would no longer necessarily be a constant fraction of the energy input rate, modifying the WBB expansion solution, the evaporative flow and temperature of the hot medium could still be computed following
El-Badry et al. [18]
.

The true complexity of this problem is realized when we allow resolved dissipation to act in concert with complicated geometry. Here the geometry changes the temperature and velocity structure of the flow in three dimensions, changing the nature of the conductive heat transport. In particular, while either condensation or evaporation is allowed exclusively in one-dimensional problems, in this complicated three-dimensional picture, both could occur simultaneously.

The largest further complication to this picture is the presence of magnetic fields, which alter the turbulent cascade
[
119
,
39
]
, reduce the strength of physical conduction processes
[
127
,
126
,
91
]
, and alter the geometric enhancement of surfaces, as we have demonstrated here. While the MHD picture of TRMLs has begun to be investigated in recent numerical works
[
14
,
152
]
, the picture of the roles of physical versus numerical dissipation in these simulations still appears unclear.

In the present work, we have emphasized that the resolution of the relevant micro-physical diffusive scales (outlined in
Section II.4
) is important to accurately capture the dynamics of WBBs. We have presented a detailed study of the origin of the numerical diffusivity in these simulations, as well as why it has a weak scaling with resolution and leads to momentum-driven-like behavior (summarized in
Section IV.6
). In particular, the decrease in dissipation at higher resolution is accomplished by a near equal increase in surface area, making the net result the same in our MHD simulations. At the same time, from the results of
Section IV.7
, it is likely that turbulent dissipation may play some role in the setting of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
in our simulations.

These results may also provide an explanation for the lack of resolution dependence in simulations of Kelvin-Helmholz mixing with cooling, even when
\ell_{\rm cool}
is not resolved. In particular, for the Kelvin-Helmholz problem in which there is not a strong mean velocity field flowing into the interface (unlike the present situation), we would expect the numerical dissipation velocity to scale as
\Delta x^{1/2}
, as was demonstrated for one-dimensional problems in
Tan et al. [132]
. Similarly, as is demonstrated in
Fielding et al. [26]
and here, we would expect the area of the fractal interface to scale as
\Delta x^{-1/2}
. These effects perfectly balance one another in this case. We emphasize that the Kelvin-Helmholz problem is quite different from the present case, where there
is
a strong flow into the mixing layer, with a comparable effective velocity of dissipation.

Finally, what does the numerical dependence demonstrated here tell us about our ability to use numerical simulations to predict the evolution of WBBs? In
Lancaster et al. [77]
,
Lancaster et al. [78]
we described the root of the “momentum-driven”-like behaviour present in both
Lancaster et al. [78]
and the simulations presented here as due to extreme cooling, with
\alpha_{p}
approaching its lower bound of unity. We argued that the strong cooling was enabled by turbulence-driven mixing of hot gas into denser material at the interface with the shell.

What we have demonstrated in this work is that the turbulence, while likely playing a role in the enhancement of the fractal structures in our simulations, is not the only, or necessarily dominant, source of diffusivity. This is clearly evidenced by the resolution dependence of the total momentum carried by the bubble, as laid out in
Figure 6
, as well as the resolution dependence of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
(laid out in
Section IV.5
), which plays a major role in setting the bubble’s interior pressure.

While numerical dissipation is playing a role in setting
\dot{p}_{r}
in the current simulations, given the available resolution, this does not change the overall physical picture for WBB evolution outlined in
Lancaster et al. [77]
. As discussed in
Section II.4
and above, in the real world turbulence should play a major role in both enhancing the surface area of the interface and in the transport that leads to intermingling of hot and cool gas at small scales. The main difference between
Lancaster et al. [77]
and the picture suggested by this work is that this evolution need not be “momentum-driven” in the
\alpha_{p}=1
or even
\alpha_{p}=const.
sense, but rather could have
\alpha_{p}
that varies with time, as suggested by
Equation 15
.

However, as we showed in
Section II.6
, we can use
Equation 15
to derive the momentum scaling that we would expect in WBBs where dissipation at the interface is mediated through turbulent mixing (described in
Equation 25
), and the overall bubble geometry is given by a fractal geometry. If we apply a Kolmogorov scaling for the turbulence and an excess fractal dimension of
d=1/2
(as seems relevant for our hydro simulations) this results in an
\alpha_{p}
which is constant in time (“momentum-driven”) with an order unity value for the parameters relevant to the simulations described here. This validates and lends further meaning to the physical picture laid out in
Lancaster et al. [77]
.

V.2
Prospects for Future Work

While the argument laid out in
Section II.6
resolves the issue of the evolution of WBBs whose interiors can be thought of as quasi-steady (details in
Appendix A
) and whose turbulent properties and fractal geometry are known, several open questions remain. First among these is when these conditions may apply. Since the relevant speeds involved in setting the interior and boundary conditions – the sound speed and flow speeds within the bubble and the turbulent velocities in the mixing layer – are large compared to the bubble expansion velocity, a quasi-steady state should be easy to establish.

The question of the nature of energy transport and geometry at turbulent interfaces has some solid theoretical basis
[
26
,
132
]
. However, some open questions remain as to the details of the resolution (in)dependence of the results obtained from these simulations and the interpretation of the scaling of the total cooling with the relevant physical parameters (
t_{\rm cool}
,
v_{t}(L)
, etc.). Understanding the nature of the resolution dependence of these simulations is, in our view, essential to a confident theoretical model for dissipation in these systems. To settle this issue it is essential that future numerical work spatially resolves the dynamics of these interfaces while including explicit, physically motivated, diffusive properties
and
demonstrating that physical diffusion is dominant over numerical diffusion.

This problem is made even harder by the ubiquitous presence of magnetic fields in astrophysical fluids, which these interfaces are likely not exempt from. While this problem has begun to be explored numerically
[
14
,
152
]
, questions of numerical dependence still remain. The magnetic field not only changes the geometry of these interfaces, as we have demonstrated here, but also reduces the strength of thermal conduction
[
110
,
69
,
16
,
91
]
. If magnetic fields act to significantly dampen the turbulent diffusivity, through altering the cascade at these interfaces by stabilizing small-scale instabilities, as seems likely from our study as well as
Zhao & Bai [152]
and
Das & Gronke [14]
, this makes the resolution requirements even stricter. Numerical studies that resolve the dynamics and dissipative processes, especially in the presence of magnetic fields, will likely be quite costly but are absolutely necessary.

Even if we have an understanding of the small-scale diffusive processes mentioned above, we still need a way of accurately accounting for them in larger-scale simulations where we have little hope of resolving the appropriate scales. While one way forward is to potentially include a modified diffusivity that accounts for this unresolved physics
[
18
,
77
]
, one still needs to ensure that the numerical diffusivity is less than this new, modified diffusivity. One promising avenue for ensuring this is to explicitly separate the high and low entropy components of the fluid in a subgrid manner
[
124
,
147
,
2
]
. The validity of this approach is subject to the details of the coupling between these subgrid components, which must be tuned to match the understanding gained from resolved interface simulations.

VI
Conclusions

We end with a summary of our main conclusions:

1.

We demonstrate that the evolution of a WBB is governed by boundary conditions at its surface (see
Section II.2
and
Appendix A
). These boundary conditions are a combination of
geometry
, embodied by the area of the bubble’s surface,
A_{\rm bub}
, and
dissipation
, embodied by the interface’s ability to absorb high-entropy gas,
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
, as seen in
Equation 15
. Increased
A_{\rm bub}
gives more surface area over which energy can be dissipated, reducing the degree to which momentum is enhanced above that input by the wind,
\alpha_{p}
. At the same time decreased
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
limits losses at each point on the surface, increasing
\alpha_{p}
.

2.

We demonstrate that solutions with constant
\alpha_{p}
are not necessarily “momentum-dirven” (see
Figure 6
). Our past work suggested, with evidence from numerical simulations, that turbulent mixing at the bubble’s interface leads to efficient enough cooling that these WBBs always enter a
\alpha_{p}\approx 1
“momentum-driven” limit,
Figure 6
demonstrates that this is not actually a “momentum-driven” evolution, as is also apparent from a fair interpretation of
Equation 15
. In
Section IV
we showed how this apparent “momentum-driven”-like behaviour is simply determined by the relative scaling of
A_{\rm bub}
and
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
both in time and with resolution,
\Delta x
. In particular, differences in these scaling relations between the HD and MHD simulations, due mostly to differences in
A_{\rm bub}
from differing fractal structures, can explain the opposite convergence behaviour apparent in these simulations, as seen in
Figure 7
. This is summarized in
Section IV.6
.

3.

In analyzing the scaling of
A_{\rm bub}
,
Section IV.4
demonstrated, using three different measurement techniques, how the surfaces of the simulated WBBs are well described as fractals with an excess fractal dimension of
d=1/2,\,1/4
for the HD and MHD simulations respectively. The fractal dimension of the MHD WBBs is also clearly time-dependent and this is likely related to the growth of magnetic pressure in the shell of the WBB as the bubble evolves. This strong magnetic pressure both halts dynamical instabilities such as Rayleigh-Taylor and Kelvin-Helmholtz but also creates a ‘thick’ magnetized shell which also suppresses ‘thin-shell’ Vishniac instabilities
[
141
]
.

4.

In analyzing the scaling of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
,
Section IV.5
demonstrated how the resolution and time dependence of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
is likely set by a consistency condition (discussed in
Section II.5
) on the cooling occurring in the interface for ‘steady’ wind bubbles. The value of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
is then determined by the volume of gas that is cooling rapidly,
V_{\rm pk}=A_{\rm bub}w_{\rm cool}
, and the pressure of the rapidly cooling gas,
P(T_{\rm pk})
, which, for isobaric layers where
P(T_{\rm pk})=P_{\rm hot}
, is also dependent on the value of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
through how
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
acts as a boundary condition on the bubble’s interior structure. The straightforward relationship outlined in
Equation 30
is somewhat complicated in the simulations due to a dip in the pressure at the peak cooling gas, leading to a prediction following
Equation 44
. If the turbulence were fully resolved,
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
would be independent of numerical details. However, at current resolutions,
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
and the dip in pressure,
f_{\rm dip}
, seem to be related through the details of how the Riemann problem is solved at the interface (see
Figure 13
and
Appendix C
) this does not lead to a straightforward prediction for the scaling of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
. To circumvent this we give an empirical relation for the dependence of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
on the bubble’s size,
R_{\rm bub}
, and the physical resolution,
\Delta x
presented in
Equation 45
and
Figure 14
.

5.

The explicit dependence on numerical resolution in our simulations, detailed above, is only due to the fact that the relevant dissipative scales (
Section II.4
) are not fully resolved in our simulations (see
Figure 2
). While running a simulation that that resolves the length scales of interest here is prohibitively expensive, in
Section II.6
we used our understanding of the momentum scaling of bubbles derived in
Appendix A
along with our understanding of the turbulent dissipative physics discussed in
Section II.4.2
to derive the momentum evolution we would expect if our simulations did resolve the relevant dissipative scales. The result, given in
Equation 35
, suggests that resolved simulations are expected to exhibit a “momentum-like” scaling with
\alpha_{p}
of order unity for the parameters relevant to the problem explored here. This further validates the physical picture drawn in
Lancaster et al. [77]
.

In
Section V
we provide an in-depth discussion of the consequences of these results in the context of past work. In particular, in
Section V.1
we emphasize one key point: numerical dissipation is not physical dissipation and this can lead to important differences when the relevant dissipative scales are not resolved. A corollary of this discussion is our speculation that the lack of resolution dependence seen in simulations of the Kelvin-Helmholz mixing layers with cooling
[
26
,
132
]
– even when dissipative scales are not resolved – is due to a conspiracy between the relative scaling of the fractal structure of the layer (
A_{\rm bub}\propto\Delta x^{-1/2}
for
d=1/2
) and the numerical dissipation (
\propto\Delta x^{1/2}
see
Tan et al. [132]
Section 4). Confirmation of this suggestion remains to be shown clearly in the mixing layer context.

We further emphasize that future work should investigate the effects of temperature dependent, Spitzer-like conductivity in 3D numerical simulations, which can potentially lead to mass-loading of the bubble’s interior (evaporative flows) combined with net cooling in the interface layer, as is suggested by the 1D simulations of
El-Badry et al. [18]
. While the bubble expansion dynamics may be unchanged, the thermal state has important observational consequences for these systems. In particular, X-ray emission from these bubbles
[
138
,
137
,
83
,
84
,
115
,
118
,
146
]
is likely dominated by the intermediate temperature gas that exists as a result of such evaporative flows
[
6
,
106
,
133
]
.

Appendix A
Fluid Structure of The Bubble Interior

In this appendix we focus on the structure of the fluid in the wind bubble interior – i.e. the free and shocked winds. This generalizes the analysis given in Appendix A of
Lancaster et al. [77]
and provides more justification and explanation of the assumptions.

The free-wind structure can be thought of as the limit of the
Chevalier & Clegg [4]
wind solution far outside the “injection region” of mass and energy. This region is steady and super-sonic at constant velocity
V_{w}
, with negligible thermal energy. The only relevant parameter of the fluid that varies is its density, which is determined by mass conservation as

\rho(r)=\frac{\dot{M}_{w}}{4\pi r^{2}V_{w}}\,.

(A1)

The free-wind ends at the shock surface where the Rankine-Hugoniot jump conditions tell us the post-shock fluid variables as

\rho_{\rm ps}=\rho_{\rm hot}=4\rho_{\rm free}(R_{\rm f})=\frac{\dot{M}_{w}}{\pi R_{\rm f}^{2}V_{w}}\,,

(A2)

v_{\rm ps}=\frac{V_{w}}{4}\,,

(A3)

and

P_{\rm ps}=P_{\rm hot}=3\rho_{\rm ps}v_{\rm ps}^{2}=\frac{3\dot{p}_{w}}{16\pi R_{\rm f}^{2}}\,.

(A4)

In
Equation A2
–
Equation A4
we have treated the free wind as a spherical volume of radius
R_{\rm f}
bounded by a perpendicular shock. More generally, one may allow for oblique wind shocks with
\mu_{\mathrm{f}}\equiv\left\langle\mathbf{\hat{r}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\right\rangle_{\rm free}
the mean obliquity of the free wind’s surface, in which case
Equation A2
–
Equation A4
respectively have additional factors
\left(4-3\mu_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}\right)^{-1}
,
\left(4-3\mu_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}\right)
, and
\mu_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}
on the right-hand side of each equation. With weighting by solid angle, it is straightforward to show that
1/4\leq\mu_{\mathrm{f}}\leq 1
.

With the above strong-shock jump conditions the shocked wind will be subsonic with Mach number
\mathcal{M}\approx 1/\sqrt{5}
, with
\mathcal{M}
decreasing as we go outward in to the shocked wind (as we will see below). Having restricted to the case of adiabatic shocked winds, we can make progress by assuming that this region is isobaric.
To justify the isobaric assumption we need to show that the sound-crossing time for the shocked wind,
t_{\rm sc,hot}\approx R_{\rm bub}/c_{s,{\rm hot}}
is much smaller than the expansion time of the bubble
t_{\rm exp}\equiv R_{\rm bub}/\dot{R}_{\rm bub}
, or equivalently
c_{s,{\rm hot}}\gg\dot{R}_{\rm bub}
. Since
c_{s,{\rm hot}}\approx 0.6V_{w}
, this is equivalent to assuming that
\dot{R}_{\rm bub}\ll V_{w}
, which is equivalent to assuming that we’ve left the ‘free-expansion’ stage, which we assume in the main text.

Given the assumed lack of spatial symmetry of the post-shock flow in the bubble interior, to make progress we must adopt a further simplifying assumption. We shall assume that the shocked wind flow is steady, with all partial time derivatives vanishing from Equations
1
,
2
, and
3
. This is of course not strictly true: since the bubble is expanding, the structure has to evolve with time. Our steady assumption is then that the time-scale on which the fluid structure changes (or is non steady)
t_{\rm ns}\equiv\rho/|\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}|
is long compared to the time-scale for structure in the flow to be established, which is again the sound-crossing time. We will justify
a posteriori
that
t_{\rm ns}\approx t_{\rm exp}
and thus,
t_{\rm ns}\gg t_{\rm sc,hot}
holds.

The assumption of a (quasi-)steady flow is more restrictive than is readily apparent. In particular, for the time rate of change of the fluid variables to be fractionally small throughout the shocked wind, nearly all mass, energy, and momentum that enters through the source terms must be carried out of the bubble. Otherwise, material would build up within the bubble interior, changing the local fluid variables. This means that, under these assumptions, we cannot think of the edge of the bubble as a contact discontinuity, across which mass/density cannot pass. Instead, the steady assumption necessitates the presence of strong diffusive transport across the bubble’s surface.

In the general case there would be small-scale turbulence within the shocked wind, which would break the steady assumption as the eddy-turnover time is small compared to the evolution time of the bubble. However, this turbulence will likely only be dominant at the surface of the bubble. Indeed, this turbulence should be one way in which the diffusion mentioned above is mediated. Also, while individual turbulent eddies would evolve more rapidly than the bubble expands, the statistical properties of the turbulence may be assumed to evolve more slowly, consistent with the quasi-steady assumption. Thus, our
ansatz
is that there will be a turbulent boundary layer with quasi-steady properties that effectively impose boundary conditions on the interior of the WBB.

The structure of the shocked wind will then be determined by the evolution of an adiabatic, steady, subsonic flow
[
77
, e.g. Appendix A of]
. The characteristics of such a flow along with the matching of boundary conditions at its inner and outer boundary surfaces will determine the extent of the volume. These boundary conditions will be the Rankine-Hugoniot relations at the interior shock and the matching of the velocity and pressure at the bubble’s outer edges.

Figure 16:
An illustration of gas streamlines in the shocked wind in a general, non-spherical geometry. Consider the blue and red material surfaces that enter the shocked wind at its boundary with the free wind, each covering roughly similar areas and corresponding to five streamlines as shown. Following the streamlines through the shocked wind, we can see how each material surface is deformed as it approaches the interface between the shocked wind and the shell. In particular the red material surface is clearly much more deformed and has reached a much larger surface area than the blue surface. The dilation factor,
\varphi
, accounts for this deformation along with the ‘normal’ increase in area of a material surface that one would get in a spherical geometry so that
\varphi
is much larger for the ‘red’ streamlines than it is for the ‘blue’ streamlines.

We now generalize the arguments from Appendix A of
Lancaster et al. [77]
to remove the assumption of a radial outflow. Given this lack of symmetry, we consider properties of the gas on streamlines as a parcel of wind material moves from the shock front towards the contact discontinuity. Our assumptions allow us to give an adiabatic equation of state

\frac{P}{\rho}=\frac{P_{\rm ps}}{\rho_{\rm ps}}\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\rm ps}}\right)^{2/3}

(A5)

and a constant Bernoulli parameter along the flow line (steady, assuming no diffuisve transport across streamlines)

\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+\frac{5}{2}\frac{P}{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}v_{\rm ps}^{2}+\frac{5}{2}\frac{P_{\rm ps}}{\rho_{\rm ps}}\,.

(A6)

The final relevant equation comes from steady flow along with conservation of mass (
Equation 1
), which we will write here for any individual streamline as

\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\rm ps}}=\frac{v_{\rm ps}}{v\varphi}

(A7)

where
\varphi
is the ‘area dilation factor’ proportional to the area normal to a given flux tube, and
v
is the magnitude of the flow velocity. For a radial flow we would have
\varphi=(r/R_{\rm f})^{2}
as in
Lancaster et al. [77]
, but for the more general case this accounts for how the area over which mass flux is spread can be much larger than the
\propto r^{2}
dependence if we allow for large deformations, as illustrated in
Figure 16
.

As in
Lancaster et al. [77]
we can put together
Equation A5
,
Equation A6
, and
Equation A7
along with the jump conditions to get

\frac{v}{v_{\rm ps}}=\varphi^{-1}\left(\frac{15}{16-(v/v_{\rm ps})^{2}}\right)^{3/2}\,.

(A8)

This technically has an analytic solution, but if we consider flow far into the shocked wind region,
v\ll v_{\rm ps}
, we can simplify the above to

\frac{v}{v_{\rm ps}}=\left(\frac{15}{16}\right)^{3/2}\varphi^{-1}\,.

(A9)

Our assumption that we are ‘far’ into the shocked wind
v\ll v_{\rm ps}
is then equivalent to assuming
\varphi\gg 1
, which is even more true in the aspherical case.

It should be noted that a similar conclusion would have been drawn from assuming that the shocked wind is isobaric. In this case, the Bernoulli equation is not used, but the adiabatic assumption implies constant density and therefore (with
Equation A7
)

\frac{v}{v_{\rm ps}}=\varphi^{-1}\,.

(A10)

As we justified above, it is very reasonable to assume the shocked wind is isobaric, so we will use this even simpler version of the velocity profile.

If
\mathbf{\hat{s}}=\mathbf{v}/v
is the direction of a given streamline and
\mathbf{\hat{n}}
is the normal to the bubble surface with local area
dA_{\mathrm{bub}}
, the area element perpendicular to the streamline is
\mathbf{\hat{s}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\,dA_{\mathrm{bub}}=dA_{\perp}=\varphi d\Omega_{\mathrm{f}}R_{\rm f}^{2}
, for
d\Omega_{\mathrm{f}}
the corresponding solid angle element of the streamline at the free wind surface. We then have
\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\,dA_{\mathrm{bub}}=v\,dA_{\perp}=v_{\rm ps}\,d\Omega_{\mathrm{f}}R_{\rm f}^{2}
, where we have used
Equation A10
. Integrating over area for all streamlines in the fluid at the edge of the bubble, we arrive at

4\pi v_{\rm ps}R_{\rm f}^{2}=\left\langle\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\right\rangle\approx\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle A_{\rm bub}\,,

(A11)

where

\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle\equiv A_{\rm bub}^{-1}\int_{A_{\rm bub}}\left(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{bub}}\right)\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}dA

(A12)

is the velocity normal to the bubble’s surface, the frame co-moving with the surface (
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{bub}}
is the local velocity of surface in the lab frame) averaged over that surface.
Here, in defining the area
A_{\rm bub}
of the bubble surface (and the corresponding
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
), we must choose a ‘ruler’ scale
\ell
in the case that the surface is fractal. We will discuss this further in
Appendix B
. If
d
is the ‘excess dimension’ of the fractal, we will have
A_{\rm bub}=4\pi R_{\rm bub}^{2}(R_{\rm bub}/\ell)^{d}
.

If we take
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
and
A_{\rm bub}
as given at any time, we can see that the free wind radius,
R_{\rm f}
, is then determined through
Equation A11
. We can then use the jump conditions (Eqs.
A2
,
A3
, and
A4
) to infer the post-shock pressure based on
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
and
A_{\rm bub}
. Since the shocked wind is assumed isobaric, this provides the pressure of all of the hot, shocked wind as

P_{\rm hot}=\frac{3}{8}\frac{L_{w}}{\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle A_{\rm bub}}\,.

(A13)

We note that, if we had simply assumed that the enthalpy flux into the shell were all of
L_{w}
the prefactor on the right-hand side of the above equation would be
2/5
instead of
3/8
, which are the same within
\sim 6\%
. This difference arises from our matching of the outward enthalpy flux from the shocked wind (
5P_{\rm hot}\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle A_{\rm bub}/2
) to the enthalpy flux through the interior wind shock (
15L_{w}/16
), instead of
L_{w}
itself. The former is more consistent with the assumption of steady mass flow combined with assumptions that the flow is and isobaric, so we use that instead here.

It is worth emphasizing what
Equation A13
is telling us and how exactly this condition is enforced within the dynamical picture of the bubble. Suppose, at a given point in the bubble’s evolution with a fixed surface area,
A_{\rm bub}
, that the velocity
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
(as set by the boundary condition in the mixing layer) is high enough that the flux through the surface,
P_{\rm hot}\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
, is larger than the steady-state value as given in
Equation A13
, such that there is a greater rate of energy leaving the shocked wind than is being provided through the interior shock. This would lower the energy density and pressure within the bubble, and this de-pressurization would cause the interior wind shock to move outward. With larger
R_{\rm f}
, a new equilibrium at lower pressure as set by
Equation A4
could be established, which for the given
A_{\rm bub}
and
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
would be compatible with a balance between energy flow into and out of the bubble. Conversely, if
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
(as limited by turbulent mixing in the physical case) is low enough that the energy outflow from the bubble to the boundary layer is smaller than the energy inflow at the free wind surface, energy buildup and the resulting pressurization of the bubble interior will move
R_{\rm f}
inward. Once a new steady state in the bubble interior is established, with pressure consistent with
Equation A4
everywhere, the specific energy of the flow into the boundary layer will be high enough that the total losses can match the total gains. In this way, under the assumption of a steady bubble,
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
and
P_{\rm hot}
are necessarily inversely proportional to one another.

Using the projection of the radial component of the momentum flux on the bounding surface of the bubble, we may compute the rate of radial momentum leaving the bubble as

\dot{p}_{r}=A_{\rm bub}\left(P_{\rm hot}\left\langle\mathbf{\hat{r}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\right\rangle+\rho_{\rm hot}\left\langle v_{r}(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}-W_{\mathrm{bub}})\right\rangle\right)\,,

(A14)

where
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{bub}}=W_{\rm bub}\mathbf{\hat{n}}
is the velocity of the bubble’s surface, which, by the steady assumption, is much less than the gas velocity, so that the second term in the above is essentially
\left\langle v_{r}v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
. Additionally, following
Lancaster et al. [78]
, we define
\left\langle\mathbf{\hat{r}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\right\rangle
as the “foldedness” of the bubble: the average value of the dot product of the radial unit vector
\mathbf{\hat{r}}
with the unit vector normal to the bubble’s surface
\mathbf{\hat{n}}
.

Continuing from the above assuming
P_{\rm hot}=P_{\rm ps}
,
\rho_{\rm hot}=\rho_{\rm ps}
and using the shock jump conditions we have

\dot{p}_{r}=\frac{\dot{p}_{w}}{4}\frac{A_{\rm bub}}{4\pi R_{\rm f}^{2}}\left(3\left\langle\mathbf{\hat{r}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\right\rangle+\frac{\left\langle v_{r}v_{\rm out}\right\rangle}{v_{\rm ps}^{2}}\right)

(A15)

where the factor

\frac{A_{\rm bub}}{4\pi R_{\rm f}^{2}}=\frac{\varphi_{\rm bub}}{\langle\mathbf{\hat{s}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\rangle}

(A16)

represents the ratio of the surface area of the bubble to the surface area of the interior shock surface (assumed spherical), and
\varphi_{\rm bub}
is the ‘dilation factor’ of the flow averaged over all streamlines at the outer limit of the WBB.
From
Equation A10
we can write
\langle v_{r}v_{\rm out}\rangle/v_{\rm ps}^{2}=\langle\mathbf{\hat{r}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{s}}\,\mathbf{\hat{s}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}/\varphi^{2}\rangle
for the Reynolds stress term in
Equation A15
.

As quantified in
Lancaster et al. [78]
(see Figure 16), it seems generally true for the case of WBBs that
A_{\rm bub}\left\langle\mathbf{\hat{r}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\right\rangle\approx 4\pi R_{\rm bub}^{2}
; that is, the product of the total area and its average foldedness is approximately equal to is sphere-equivalent surface area. This implies that

\left\langle\mathbf{\hat{r}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\right\rangle\approx\frac{4\pi R_{\rm bub}^{2}}{A_{\rm bub}}\,.

(A17)

Using
Equation A16
and
Equation A17
in
Equation A15
, we obtain

\dot{p}_{r}=\frac{\dot{p}_{w}}{4}\left[3\left(\frac{R_{\rm bub}}{R_{\rm f}}\right)^{2}+\varphi_{\rm bub}\frac{\langle\mathbf{\hat{r}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{s}}\,\mathbf{\hat{s}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}/\varphi^{2}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{\hat{s}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\rangle}\right]

(A18)

We note that in the case of a purely radial post-shock flow,
\langle\mathbf{\hat{r}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{s}}\,\mathbf{\hat{s}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\rangle/\langle\mathbf{\hat{s}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\rangle=1
and
\varphi=\varphi_{\rm bub}=(R_{\rm bub}/R_{\rm f})^{2}
for the second term in
Equation A18
. In this case, the square bracket is equal to
3(R_{\rm bub}/R_{\rm f})^{2}+(R_{\rm f}/R_{\rm bub})^{2}
, which recovers Equation A11 of
Lancaster et al. [77]
. More generally, the term representing Reynolds stresses depends on the orientation of streamlines and the bubble surface. If
\mathbf{\hat{n}}
and
\mathbf{\hat{r}}
are aligned (i.e. the surface is spherical), while
\mathbf{\hat{s}}
is randomly oriented (but still outward) with
\varphi
independent of
\mathbf{\hat{s}}
,
\langle\mathbf{\hat{r}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{s}}\,\mathbf{\hat{s}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\rangle/\langle\mathbf{\hat{s}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}\mu^{2}d\mu/\int_{0}^{1}\mu d\mu=2/3
. If the bubble surface is highly folded so that
\mathbf{\hat{n}}
and
\mathbf{\hat{r}}
are independent, and
\varphi
is independent of
\mathbf{\hat{s}}
, then
\langle\mathbf{\hat{r}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{s}}\,\mathbf{\hat{s}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\rangle/\langle\mathbf{\hat{s}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{\hat{r}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{s}}\rangle
, which has an upper limit of unity. Thus, for a range of circumstances, the second term in square brackets is
\varphi_{\rm bub}^{-1}
times a factor no larger than 1. If, furthermore, the surface is so folded that
\mathbf{\hat{n}}
has no preferred orientation, then
\langle\mathbf{\hat{s}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\rangle\sim\left\langle\mathbf{\hat{r}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\right\rangle
and from
Equation A16
and
Equation A17
we have
\varphi_{\rm bub}\approx(R_{\rm bub}/R_{\rm f})^{2}
.

In practice, we are frequently in the
\varphi_{\rm bub}\gg 1
limit (and hence, implicitly,
R_{\rm bub}^{2}\gg R_{\rm f}^{2}
), so that the first term in parentheses in
Equation A18
(associated with the pressure force) is much greater than the second term (associated with Reynolds stresses). In this case, we can disregard the Reynolds stress term to arrive at

\dot{p}_{r}=\frac{3}{4}\dot{p}_{w}\left(\frac{R_{\rm bub}}{R_{\rm f}}\right)^{2}=\frac{3}{4}\dot{p}_{w}\varphi_{\rm bub}\frac{\left\langle\mathbf{\hat{r}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\right\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{\hat{s}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\rangle}\,.

(A19)

We note, however,that
Equation A19
does not apply in the extreme cooling limit. For this case, the post-shock flow is radial and it immediately enters the shell. Thus,
R_{\rm bub}/R_{\rm f}=1
, the square bracket in
Equation A18
is equal to 4, and
\dot{p}_{r}\to\dot{p}_{w}
. This corresponds to the “Efficiently Cooled” limit where
\alpha_{p}=1
in
Lancaster et al. [77]
.

In the case where the thermal pressure term dominates, we can recast the radial stress communicated out of the bubble (
Equation A19
) as

\dot{p}_{r}\approx 4\pi R_{\rm bub}^{2}P_{\rm hot}=\frac{3}{4}\dot{p}_{w}\frac{V_{w}/4}{\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle}\frac{4\pi R_{\rm bub}^{2}}{A_{\rm bub}}\,.

(A20)

The first equality is from
Equation A19
with
Equation A4
, while in the last equality we have applied
Equation A13
again. This tells us that the ‘normal’ force term,
4\pi R_{\rm bub}^{2}P_{\rm hot}
still applies in the completely aspherical, messy picture, as long as
Equation A17
applies.

It is worthwhile to remark on the cause and effect of how this structure is set up within the shocked wind gas. Considering
A_{\rm bub}
to be set at a fixed time
t
by the past evolution of the bubble, and that the flow in the shocked wind is subsonic, we see from
Equation A11
that
R_{\rm f}
is set by the velocity at which gas is moving out of the ‘steady’ wind region. As is explicit in all of the derivations of this section, this condition is maintained on timescales that are short compared with the expansion time of the bubble (over which
A_{\rm bub}
may vary). It is then
R_{\rm f}
, through
Equation A4
, that determines
P_{\rm hot}
, which determines the instantaneous radial momentum evolution of the bubble in
Equation A20
.

Causality requires that the dynamics are determined by the properties of the flow at the bubble’s surface. This can be seen by dividing
Equation A20
by
\dot{p}_{w}
to obtain an explicit prediction for the momentum enhancement factor as

\alpha_{p}=\frac{3}{4}\frac{V_{w}/4}{\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle}\frac{4\pi R_{\rm bub}^{2}}{A_{\rm bub}}\,.

(A21)

We then see that the momentum enhancement is determined solely by the average outward velocity at the surface,
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
which we will show is governed by dissipation, and the geometry of its surface, given by
A_{\rm bub}
.

Appendix B
Fractal Description of the Surface

The quantities
A_{\rm bub}
and
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
respectively quantify the area and mean outflow velocity at the bubble’s surface. These are used in computing mass and thermal energy flows through the surface, respectively proportional to
\rho_{\rm hot}
and
P_{\rm hot}
, and the momentum flow given in
Equation A20
. Special care is required when the surface has a ‘fractal’ structure. In reality this fractal structure will only be present over some range of scales, and in particular it will be regulated or smoothed out by dissipative physics on the smallest scales. Despite that, we can think of measuring both
A_{\rm bub}
and
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
as a function of scale. For
A_{\rm bub}
, this is the usual measurement for fractals where we only allow for surface elements of a given linear dimension,
\ell
. We can also think of this as insisting that the surface describing the interface has some minimum radius of curvature,
\ell
. This is shown schematically in the left panel of
Figure 17
.

The outflow velocity,
v_{\rm out}
, also has a fractal structure, and therefore
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
is dependent on the measurement scale,
\ell
. We depict this schematically for two given scales,
\ell_{\rm hi}<\ell_{\rm lo}
in the right panel of
Figure 17
, where
\ell_{\rm hi}
represents the high resolution picture, at small scale, and
\ell_{\rm lo}
represents the low resolution picture, at large scale. In particular, imagine the light blue surface in
Figure 17
, measured on scale
\ell_{\rm hi}
, is the ‘true’ interface between hot and cold gas and we can define a normal component of the outflow velocity
v_{\rm out,hi}=\mathbf{v}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\rm hi}
at each facet of this interface. We would then define
\langle v_{\rm out}(\ell_{\rm hi})\rangle
as the average of this quantity over the facets. We can then ascribe a value
v_{\rm out,lo}
to a given facet defined on the larger scale
\ell_{\rm lo}
by associating each smaller scale facet with a larger scale one and setting
v_{\rm out,lo}=N^{-1}\sum_{i}^{N}v_{{\rm hi},i}\left(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{{\rm hi},i}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\rm lo}\right)
, where the sum is over all smaller facets associated with the larger facet (and we have implicitly assumed that all facets at a given scale have the same area). We note that provided
P_{\rm hot}
and
\rho_{\rm hot}
are effectively spatially uniform, there is no need to apply additional weighting in the average.

With the above definition of the fractal nature of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
we can then note that there is a natural relationship between the fractal scaling of
A_{\rm bub}
and
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
at fixed time provided by the mass continuity condition. Through
Equation A11
, the product
A_{\rm bub}\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
must be independent of
\ell
at a fixed
R_{\rm bub}
or time. In particular, if we measure both
A_{\rm bub}
and
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
at the same
\ell
, and we assume
A_{\rm bub}=4\pi R_{\rm bub}^{2}(R_{\rm bub}/\ell)^{d}
then we have

\langle v_{\rm out}\rangle\frac{A_{\rm bub}}{4\pi R_{\rm bub}^{2}}=\langle v_{\rm out}\rangle\left(\frac{R_{\rm bub}}{\ell}\right)^{d}\,.

(B1)

Thus, in order for
Equation A20
to be well-defined for
\ell\rightarrow 0
, we must have
\langle v_{\rm out}\rangle\propto\ell^{d}
. That is, the mean outflow velocity through the bubble surface must decrease as the ‘ruler’ scale shrinks.

We can see how foldedness leads to a decrease of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
at smaller
\ell
by considering the case of a purely radial flow with constant speed (relative to bubble’s surface)
v_{r}
in the bubble interior, for which (using
Equation A17
)

\langle v_{\rm out}\rangle=v_{r}A_{\rm bub}^{-1}\int_{A_{\rm bub}}\mathbf{\hat{r}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\,dA\approx v_{r}\frac{4\pi R_{\rm bub}^{2}}{A_{\rm bub}}=v_{r}\left(\frac{\ell}{R_{\rm bub}}\right)^{d}\,.

(B2)

Furthermore, if the fractal structure has no preferred orientation, the above scaling would be true if the velocity in the hot gas has any direction that is uncorrelated with the interface structure, not just a purely-radial direction, since
\langle\mathbf{\hat{s}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\rangle\sim\left\langle\mathbf{\hat{r}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\right\rangle
. We caution, however, that while this limiting case illustrates the effect of the bubble’s fractal geometry on the scaling of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
with the length of the ruler
\ell
, it does not provide information about the overall normalization of
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
. In actuality, the velocity in the bubble interior will not be purely radial, and the magnitude of the outflow velocity through the surface must depend not just on properties in the bubble interior, but also on dissipative processes and cooling within the interaction region of the shell near the interface (see
Section II.4
and
Section II.5
).

Appendix C
Effect of the Riemann Solver

The results of the main text indicate that the evolution of simulated WBBs, at the resolutions probed here, is dependent upon the effects of numerical dissipation. It is therefore informative to investigate simulations that change the nature of that dissipation. To that end, we run a suite of 6 hydrodynamical simulations corresponding to two choices of resolution
N_{\rm res}=128,\,256
and three choices of Riemann solver: the Roe Solver with H-Correction (as in the main text)
[
111
,
117
]
, the Harten-Lax-van Leer (HLL) method
[
46
]
with the Einfeldt estimate for wave-speeds
[
17
]
(HLLE), and finally the HLLE method with the contact-wave restored (HLLC)
[
136
]
. These methods are reviewed pedagogically in
Toro [135]
and their implementation in the
Athena
code is explained in
Stone et al. [129]
. We run these simulations without the non-equilibrium cooling and chemistry module of
Kim et al. [63]
and instead use the collisional-ionization equilibrium cooling used in
Lancaster et al. [78]
and originally described in
Kim & Ostriker [57]
. As we will see, these different choices in solvers lead to small quantitative differences but do not make a difference to the overall dynamics of these bubbles.

In
Figure 18
we present the results of these simulations. This figure portrays the key quantities of the WBB’s evolution that are highlighted in
Figure 10
and
Figure 13
. Other aspects of the WBB’s that are shown in the main text remain broadly consistent here: (i)
Equation 9
and
Equation 10
are good descriptions of the force exerted by the bubbles, (ii)
Equation 11
provides a good prediction for the bubble’s interior pressure, illustrating that the interiors are nearly steady, and (iii) the convergence properties of the total momenta carried by these bubbles are similar to the hydrodynamical simulations shown in
Figure 7
.

Two factors indicate that the HLLC Riemann solver is much less diffusive for the present application than the other two Riemann solvers:

1.

As shown in the top middle panel of
Figure 18
,
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
is much smaller with the HLLC solver at fixed bubble properties, indicating a decreased aptitude to allow diffusion across the bubble’s interface.

2.

The thickness of the cooling layer,
w_{\rm cool}
, is much smaller when using the HLLC solver, by a factor
\approx 2
compared to the HLLE solver, and slightly more compared to Roe. This indicates that the interface is less ‘smeared-out’ by numerical diffusion.

This is not surprising as the HLLC solver was explicitly developed to help better resolve contact discontinuities like the bubble’s surface that we are investigating here. Notably, the more expensive Roe solver with the H-correction seems to be as diffusive as the simple HLLE solver for these types of problems. This may not be as surprising when one considers that the H-correction, which effectively adds diffusion, is only applied when differences in wave-speeds are large in different grid-aligned directions, as they almost always are at the interface. While it is still discouraging that this makes the method essentially as diffusive as the HLLE solver, this likely only applies at the WBB interface; other regions of the solution are still less diffusive (at least from visual inspection) with the Roe + H-correction solver as with the HLLE solver.

We also see from the top right panel of
Figure 18
that the surface area of the bubble is noticeably larger with the HLLC solver, especially at late times. If one considers that the HLLC solver is less diffusive and therefore less likely to smooth out small scale structure at the bubble’s surface, this development is not particularly surprising. What is very intriguing is that the competition between geometry (increased
A_{\rm bub}
) and diffusion (decreased
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
) here seems to be nearly completely balanced as we see in the top left panel: the resulting interior pressure in the HLLC solution is nearly exactly the same at given
R_{\rm bub}
, which means that
\dot{p}_{r}
is nearly independent of the solver. It remains to be seen whether this is a coincidence or points to a more fundamental dynamical relationship that is as yet undiscovered here.

Turning our attention to the bottom left panel of
Figure 18
, we see that the relationship between the measured
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
and that predicted by
Equation 43
remains consistent between the various Riemann solvers, as in the results in the main section of the paper. As we explained in
Equation 30
, this consistency condition should always roughly hold, even if it varies slightly due to the assumption that all gas is cooling at a single temperature, which cannot be the case.

One clear indication of strong differences between the Riemann solvers is given in the final, bottom-right panel of
Figure 18
. Here we see that the relationship between the pressure dip and the average velocity at the interface is different amongst all the Riemann solvers: a clear indication that this is a numerical artifact, as has been reported elsewhere
[
53
,
26
]
. The higher value of
P(T_{\rm pk})/P_{\rm hot}
in the HLLC solver is yet another indication of its lower diffusivity. It is interesting that these relationships do seem to remain resolution independent even with the different Riemann solvers.

The exact origin of the relationships shown here for different Riemann solvers may be more fully explicated with targeted, simplified, one-dimensional simulations.

Appendix D
Cooling Function and the Effects of its Variation

Finally, we present an additional series of simulations where we have modified the cooling function by hand. All other parameters of the simulations are as in the main text. We modify the cooling function by beginning with the CIE cooling function of
Kim & Ostriker [57]
(as in
Appendix C
), which we will denote
\Lambda_{T}(T)
here. We then modify it by adopting

\Lambda(T)=\begin{cases}\Lambda_{T}(T)&T<T_{\rm red}\\
\Lambda_{T}(T)10^{-\frac{T-T_{\rm red}}{\Delta T}}&T_{\rm red}<T<1.1T_{\rm red}\\
\Lambda_{0}\Lambda_{T}(T)&T>1.1T_{\rm red}\end{cases}

(D1)

where
T_{\rm red}=7000\,{\rm K}
is the temperature above which we reduce the cooling function,
\Lambda_{0}
is the factor by which we reduce it and

\displaystyle\Delta T=-\frac{T_{\rm red}}{10\log_{10}\left(\Lambda_{0}\right)}

(D2)

is a factor to account for a smooth transition between the two regimes (the middle term in
Equation D1
). The modified cooling functions and the changes they impose to the cooling time of the gas are presented in
Figure 19
. We note that, for
\Lambda_{0}=1
we simply use
\Lambda_{T}(T)
(these results are the same simulations as the “Roe + H-Corection” simulations of
Appendix C
).

The main summary results of these simulations are presented in
Figure 20
in an exactly analogous way to that presented in
Figure 18
. The main takeaway from the results presented here is that the formalism presented in the main body of the text still applies in this cooling-modified picture. In particular, the pressure in the hot gas is still straightforwardly predicted from
Equation 11
with the area of the bubble,
A_{\rm bub}
, being described by a fractal and the small scale dissipative velocity,
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
, being determined by numerical dissipation as given by
Equation 43
. In particular, we see here that
A_{\rm bub}
is independent of the strength of the cooling in the interface layer (top right of
Figure 20
) indicating that this surface is simply determined by the dynamical evolution of the bubble, surface hydrodynamical instabilities, and the structure of the background gas into which the bubble expands.

In the bottom row of
Figure 20
we can see that, while
Equation 43
still accurately predicts the velocity into the surface at all levels of cooling, the surface itself is becoming better resolved as cooling becomes weaker. This is indicated by an increase in the effective width of the cooling layer,
w_{\rm cool}
(middle bottom panel), and an increase in the pressure as measured by
f_{\rm dip}
closer to 1 (right bottom panel), as cooling becomes weaker. It seems that cooling does not have a large effect on
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
, as shown in the top middle panel. In particular, reduction of
\Lambda
by a factor of 10 does not decrease
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
by a factor
1/\sqrt{10}
, as
Equation 30
would imply if the cooling volume
V_{\rm pk}
were fixed. This is because, at a lower cooling rate, the correct cooling length is better resolved, leading to
V_{\rm pk}
larger and
f_{\rm dip}
closer to 1, such that
Equation 43
predicts only a modest decrease in
\left\langle v_{\rm out}\right\rangle
.
