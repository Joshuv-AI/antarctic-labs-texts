Algebraic fundamental groups of fake projective planes
Matthew Stover

Abstract
Fundamental groups of fake projective planes fall into fifty distinct isomorphism classes, one for each complex conjugate pair. We prove that this is not the case for their algebraic fundamental groups: there are only forty-six isomorphism classes. We show that there are four pairs of complex conjugate pairs of fake projective planes that are Aut ⁡ ( ℂ ) \Aut(\mathbb{C}) -equivalent and hence have mutually isomorphic algebraic fundamental groups. All other pairs of algebraic fundamental groups are shown to be distinct through explicit finite étale covers. As a by-product, this provides the first examples of commensurable but nonisomorphic lattices in a rank one semisimple Lie group that have isomorphic profinite completions.

Introduction

If
X
is a smooth complex projective surface, then
\pi_{1}(X)
will denote its topological fundamental group and
\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{alg}}(X)
its algebraic fundamental group. Then
\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{alg}}(X)
is the profinite completion of
\pi_{1}(X)
. This paper classifies the isomorphism classes of algebraic fundamental groups of fake projective planes, proving that there are exactly forty-six classes. It is known that there are fifty isomorphism classes of topological fundamental groups. Indeed, Cartwright and Steger completed the classification of fake projective planes
[
16
]
, proving that there are one-hundred isomorphism classes that divide into fifty distinct complex conjugate pairs. Each complex conjugate pair determines a lattice in the Lie group
\PU(2,1)
, and Mostow rigidity implies that distinct complex conjugate pairs have nonisomorphic fundamental groups. The main result of this paper is:

Theorem 1.1
.

There are exactly forty-six isomorphism classes of algebraic fundamental groups of fake projective planes. Specifically, suppose that
X
and
Y
are fake projective planes and that
\pi_{1}(X)=\Gamma_{j},\pi_{1}(Y)=\Gamma_{k}
for
j<k
under the numbering given in the Appendix. Then
\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{alg}}(X)
is isomorphic to
\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{alg}}(Y)
if and only if
\{j,k\}
is one of the pairs

\{34,35\}\,,\,\{43,44\}\,,\,\{47,49\}\,,\,\{48,50\}.

To prove that certain fake projective planes have nonisomorphic algebraic fundamental groups, we exhibit explicit finite quotients of their topological fundamental groups that preclude the existence of an isomorphism of algebraic fundamental groups. Said in terms of étale covers, we produce explicit connected, étale, Galois covers that distinguish between the two algebraic fundamental groups. This part of the proof is the content of §
4
. In fact, the proof is effective. If two fake projective planes have nonisomorphic algebraic fundamental groups, we show that this can be detected by an étale cover of degree at most
248
; see Corollary
4.13
. See Remark
4.14
for an alternate argument found by a referee that, while ineffective and hence not sufficient to prove Corollary
4.13
, cuts a much faster path to reducing the proof of Theorem
1.1
to proving that the four given pairs have isomorphic algebraic fundamental groups.

The more technical and difficult part of the paper, completed in §
7
, is proving that the remaining pairs have isomorphic algebraic fundamental groups. This is done following Serre’s observation that
\Aut(\mathbb{C})
equivalent complex projective varieties have the same algebraic fundamental group
[
31
]
. Here, we use conjugation of Shimura varieties to prove that four pairs of fake projective planes are
\Aut(\mathbb{C})
equivalent. Specifically, we use the close relationship between fake projective planes and Shimura varieties, which have canonical models defined over a number field, to define the action of
\Aut(\mathbb{C})
on fake projective planes and then apply work of Shih on conjugation of Shimura varieties
[
32
]
; this approach has the added feature of working directly with connected components, avoiding a minor but annoying technicality that would arise using the adelic definition. This requires careful analysis of the arithmetic lattices associated with these fake projective planes. One case should also be covered by work of Borisov and Fatighenti
[
4
]
on explicit equations for fake projective planes, but this would require verification that we did not pursue (see Remark
7.8
). The proof of Theorem
1.1
therefore allows us to give the complete
\Aut(\mathbb{C})
classification of fake projective planes.

Corollary 1.2
.

There are exactly forty-six
\Aut(\mathbb{C})
equivalence classes of fake projective planes.

Using
\Aut(\mathbb{C})
conjugation to find locally symmetric varieties with nonisomorphic topological fundamental groups but isomorphic algebraic fundamental groups has some history. Examples are found in Milne and Suh
[
25
]
, Bauer, Catanese, and Grunewald
[
3
]
, and Rajan
[
29
]
. Stated in the language of discrete subgroups of Lie groups, one constructs nonisomorphic lattices in (possibly distinct) Lie groups with isomorphic profinite completions. See for example
[
2
,
22
]
for more on higher rank lattices with the same profinite completion.

The rank one case has recently been of particular interest, especially in light of the remarkable breakthrough by Bridson, McReynolds, Reid, and Spitler
[
6
]
, who found hyperbolic
3
-manifold groups
\Gamma
that are
profinitely rigid
, i.e., so that if
\Lambda
is any other finitely generated, residually finite group with profinite completion
\widehat{\Lambda}\cong\widehat{\Gamma}
, then
\Lambda\cong\Gamma
. The first examples of nonisomorphic real rank one lattices with isomorphic profinite completions were given in
[
34
]
, where we proved using
\Aut(\mathbb{C})
conjugation that, for all
n\geq 2
, there are torsion-free cocompact lattices in
\PU(n,1)
that are not isomorphic but have isomorphic profinite completions. A distinct feature of the examples in
[
34
]
is that the lattices are not commensurable (the higher-rank lattices in
[
29
]
are commensurable, but those in
[
25
]
are not). In this paper, we provide the first commensurable examples in rank one, confirming in this case the suspicion in
[
25
, Rem. 2.7]
echoed in
[
34
, Rem. 2.3]
.

Corollary 1.3
.

There are commensurable lattices in
\PU(2,1)
that are not isomorphic but have isomorphic profinite completions.

We note, however, that it is still an open problem whether or not there are distinct nonuniform lattices in
\PU(n,1)
with isomorphic profinite completions. Some specific examples where certain methods used in this paper could be of use are the following:

Question 1.4
.

Consider neat nonuniform lattices
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
in
\PU(n,1)
so that the associated ball quotients admit biholomorphic smooth toroidal compactifications; see for instance
[

18

20

and references therein for many (often commensurable) examples. Is it ever the case that the profinite completions

It is noted in the Appendix to

Question 1.5

Suppose that
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}<\PU(n,1)
are neat nonuniform lattices with isomorphic profinite completions. Are the smooth toroidal compactifications of the ball quotients
\Gamma_{j}\backslash\mathbb{B}^{n}

Remark 1.6
.

There are many smooth projective surfaces not birational to a fake projective plane with (topological, hence algebraic) fundamental group isomorphic to the fundamental group of a fake projective plane. See
[

2
The action of
\Aut(\mathbb{C})

This section follows
[
3
, §4]
. To start, an element
\tau\in\Aut(\mathbb{C})
acts on an element
f(x)=\sum\alpha_{I}x^{I}
of the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{N}]
by

\tau(f)(x)=\sum_{I=(i_{0},\dots,i_{N})}\tau(\alpha_{I})x^{I},

where we use the usual multi-index notation for polynomials. Given a projective variety
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{N}
cut out by homogeneous polynomials
\{f_{1}(x),\dots,f_{r}(x)\}
, the coordinate action of
\tau\in\Aut(\mathbb{C})
on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
maps
X
to a new variety

X^{\tau}=\{x\in\mathbb{P}^{N}\ :\ \tau(f_{j})(x)=0\textrm{ for }1\leq j\leq r\}.

Indeed,
f_{j}(x)=0
if and only if
\tau(f_{j})(\tau(x))=0
.

Applying the above to the graph of an element
\psi\in\Aut(X)
, we obtain an element
\psi^{\tau}\in\Aut(X^{\tau})
. A
G
-marked variety
is then a triple
(X,G,\eta)
, where
X
is a projective variety and
\eta:G\to\Aut(X)
is an injective homomorphism. For each
G
-marking
(X,G,\eta)
of
X
, we obtain the
G
-marking

(X,G,\eta)^{\tau}=(X^{\tau},G,\tau\circ\eta\circ\tau^{-1})

(1)

of
X^{\tau}
. Set
\eta^{\tau}=\tau\circ\eta\circ\tau^{-1}
. We record some essential properties of the
\Aut(\mathbb{C})
action on projective varieties.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{N}
be a projective variety. For
\tau\in\Aut(\mathbb{C})
and a marking
(X,G,\eta)
, the following hold.

1.

If
\eta(g)
has a fixed point for some
g\in G
, then
\eta^{\tau}(g)
has a fixed point on
X^{\tau}
.

2.

One has
(X/\eta(G))^{\tau}=X^{\tau}/\eta^{\tau}(G)
.

3.

The variety
X
is smooth if and only if
X^{\tau}
is smooth.

Proof.

The first property follows from the definition of
\eta^{\tau}
and the fact that
X\to X^{\tau}
is a bijection on points. The second property is
[
3
, Rem. 4.4(4)]
. The third follows from the fact that smoothness is defined by the rank of the Jacobian matrix for the system of polynomials, which is invariant under the action of
\Aut(\mathbb{C})
.
∎

We will use the following corollary in §
7
.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
G
be a finite group and
(Y,G,\eta)
be a smooth projective surface such that
\eta(G)
has no fixed points (i.e.,
Y\to X
is finite étale) and
X=Y/\eta(G)
is a fake projective plane. Then
X^{\tau}
is a fake projective plane.

Proof.

Lemma
2.1
implies that
X^{\tau}=Y^{\tau}/\eta^{\tau}(G)
is also a smooth projective surface. Betti numbers are invariant under
\tau
by Serre’s GAGA principle, so
X^{\tau}
also has the same complex cohomology as
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. We must rule out the possibility that
X^{\tau}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. Since
X
is a ball quotient, so is
X^{\tau}
by a theorem of Kazhdan
[
21
]
. In particular,
X^{\tau}
has infinite fundamental group, and therefore it is a fake projective plane.
∎

3
Profinite completions of discrete groups

Let
X
be a smooth complex projective variety. Throughout this paper,
\pi_{1}(X)
will denote the topological fundamental group, considered as a finitely generated discrete group, and
\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{alg}}(X)
the algebraic fundamental group. Then
\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{alg}}(X)
is isomorphic to the profinite completion of
\pi_{1}(X)
. As explained in the introduction, there are now many known examples of pairs

so that
\pi_{1}(X)
is not isomorphic to
\pi_{1}(Y)
but
\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{alg}}(X)\cong\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{alg}}(Y)
. Here and throughout, the profinite completion of a group
\Gamma
is denoted
\widehat{\Gamma}
.

This section is concerned with tools to prove that
\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{alg}}(X)
is not isomorphic to
\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{alg}}(Y)
through finite quotients of
\pi_{1}(X)
and
\pi_{1}(Y)
. These tools come from the study of profinite completions of arbitrary finitely generated discrete groups. See
[
30
]
for a thorough survey; we will only need the following two fundamental facts found there:

Lemma 3.1

(Prop. 3.2
[
30
]
)
.

If
\Gamma
and
\Delta
are discrete groups with
\widehat{\Gamma}\cong\widehat{\Delta}
, then
\Gamma
and
\Delta
have isomorphic abelianizations.

Theorem 3.2

(Cor. 4.2
[
30
]
)
.

If
\Gamma,\Delta
are finitely generated groups with
\widehat{\Gamma}\cong\widehat{\Delta}
, then one has equalities

\displaystyle\left|\Hom(\Gamma,Q)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\Hom(\Delta,Q)\right|

\displaystyle\left|\mathrm{Epi}(\Gamma,Q)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathrm{Epi}(\Delta,Q)\right|

for all finite groups
Q
, where
\mathrm{Epi}(\Lambda,Q)
is the set of epimorphisms of a group
\Lambda
onto
Q
.

In the language of smooth complex projective varieties, Lemma
3.1
says that if
\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{alg}}(X)\cong\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{alg}}(Y)
, then
H_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})\cong H_{1}(Y,\mathbb{Z})
. Theorem
3.2
says that if
X
and
Y
admit a different number of isomorphism classes of connected étale coverings with Galois group some fixed finite group

, then they have nonisomorphic algebraic fundamental groups. Note that Theorem
3.2
is slightly stronger than the more elementary statement that if
X
admits a connected étale cover with group
Q
and
Y
does not, then their algebraic fundamental groups are not isomorphic. (Here we call étale covers isomorphic if they are associated with the same conjugacy class of subgroups of the fundamental group.)

6
Buildings and their vertices

This section continues with the notation established in §
5
. Our proof that certain fake projective plane groups have isomorphic profinite completions requires very specific information about the maximal compact subgroups in the arithmetic definition of each group. This section briefly introduces the building
X_{\nu}
associated with the groups
H_{F_{\nu}}
over the nonarchimedean completions
F_{\nu}
of
F
and the open compact subgroups of
H_{F_{\nu}}
stabilizing vertices of
X_{\nu}
.

The algebraic groups
H_{F_{\nu}}
we will need to understand are locally isomorphic to either
\SL_{3}(F_{\nu})
or the special unitary group
\SU(h,E_{\mu})
of a hermitian form
h
with respect to a quadratic extension
E_{\mu}/F_{\nu}
. We handle each case individually.

6.1
The building for
\SL_{3}(F_{\nu})

This case is quite classical, and we refer to either
[
37
, §2.8]
or
[
15
, §2]
for details. The vertices of
X_{\nu}
are homothety classes of lattices
\mathcal{L}\subset F_{\nu}^{3}
. We denote the homothety class of
\mathcal{L}
by
[\mathcal{L}]
. Here, a lattice means an
\mathcal{O}_{\nu}
submodule of rank three, where
\mathcal{O}_{\nu}
is the valuation ring of
F_{\nu}
. Choosing a uniformizer
\pi
for
\mathcal{O}_{\nu}
, distinct vertices
[\mathcal{L}_{1}]
and
[\mathcal{L}_{2}]
are adjacent if there are representatives
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
for the homothety classes so that
\pi\mathcal{L}_{1}\subset\mathcal{L}_{2}\subset\mathcal{L}_{1}
. Chambers of
X_{\nu}
consist of triples of mutually adjacent vertices.

Then
\PGL_{3}(F_{\nu})
acts transitively on the vertices of
X_{\nu}
. This implies that all vertices are
hyperspecial
, and the stabilizer
P_{\mathcal{L}}<\SL_{3}(F_{\nu})
of
[\mathcal{L}]
is a maximal open compact subgroup. We call
P_{\mathcal{L}}
a
hyperspecial parahoric subgroup
, and note that all maximal parahoric subgroups are conjugate in
\GL_{3}(F_{\nu})
. For consistency with the unitary case, we say that all vertices of the building are
Type 1
vertices.

6.2
The building for
\SU(h,E_{\mu})

See
[
15
, §2]
for an excellent detailed construction of the building from our point of view. In this case, the building
X_{\nu}
is a tree. As with
\SL_{3}(F_{\nu})
, the vertices are related to homothety classes of lattices. Given a lattice
\mathcal{L}
, let
\mathcal{L}^{*}
denote its dual with respect to the form
h
. It ends up that either
[\mathcal{L}^{*}]=[\mathcal{L}]
or the two homothety classes define adjacent vertices in the building for
\SL_{3}(E_{\mu})
. The vertices of
X_{\nu}
fall into two classes:

Type 1:
Classes
[\mathcal{L}]
for which
[\mathcal{L}]=[\mathcal{L}^{*}]
.

Type 2:
Unordered pairs
([\mathcal{L}],[\mathcal{L}^{*}])
of neighbors in the building for
\SL_{3}(E_{\mu})
.

A Type 1 vertex
[\mathcal{L}^{\prime}]
is adjacent to a Type 2 vertex
([\mathcal{L}],[\mathcal{L}^{*}])
if and only if the triple forms a chamber in the building for
\SL_{3}(E_{\mu})
. A vertex is never adjacent to another of the same type. Type 1 vertices are hyperspecial and the Type 2 vertices are special, but not hyperspecial. The action of
\SU(h,E_{\mu})
preserves the type of a vertex, and the action is transitive on vertices of a given type. The maximal open compact subgroups of
\SU(h,E_{\mu})
are vertex stabilizers, hence there are two conjugacy classes.

7.3.9
The isomorphism of profinite completions for
j=47,49

Proposition 7.11
.

Let
X_{47},X_{49}
be fake projective planes with fundamental groups
\Gamma_{47},\Gamma_{49}
and choose a representative
\tau\in\Aut(\mathbb{C})
for the generator of
\Gal(F/\mathbb{Q})
. Then, up to complex conjugation,
X_{47}^{\tau}
is isomorphic to
X_{49}
. Therefore
\widehat{\Gamma}_{47}\cong\widehat{\Gamma}_{49}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.2
, the algebraic group associated with
X_{47}^{\tau}
is the algebraic group with hermitian form
\tau(h)
, which is again
H
. By Theorem
5.1
, we must describe the open compact subgroup at each localization under the isomorphism of algebraic groups over the finite adeles of
F
. In particular, we must show that the self-isomorphism of
H(\mathbb{A}_{F}^{\infty})
induced by
\tau
takes
K_{47}
to
K_{49}
. By Proposition
5.2
, we only need to understand the
\tau
-conjugate of the local lattice used to defined each open compact subgroup
P_{\nu}
of
H_{F_{\nu}}
.

For
|\nu|\neq 2,3,7
,
P_{\nu}
is defined to be the stabilizer in
H_{F_{\nu}}
of the standard
E_{\mu}
lattice for
\mu
dividing
\nu
. Since
\det(h)
is a unit of
F
, the standard lattice is self-dual for
h
,
P_{\nu}
is the stabilizer of a Type
1
vertex of the building for
H_{F_{\nu}}
. Then
\tau(h)
also has determinant a unit, so the stabilizer of the standard lattice is again
P_{\nu}
. Thus, up to conjugacy in
H_{F_{\tau(\nu)}}
we send
P_{\nu}
to
P_{\nu}\cong P_{\tau(\nu)}
under the isomorphism. For
\mathfrak{p}_{7}
, the determinant of the conjugate
h_{c}
defined in Equation (
11
) is again a unit of
F
, so the same argument implies that
P_{7}
is mapped to a conjugate of
P_{7}
. For
\mathfrak{p}_{2}
, the open compact subgroup is defined by the unique maximal order of
D_{2+}\cong D_{2-}
, so it is again
P_{2}
under the isomorphism induced by
\tau
.

It remains to consider
|\nu|=3
. Here, we have that

\nu_{3\pm}(\det(\tau(h)))=1-\nu_{3\pm}(\det(h)).

This means that replacing
h
with
\tau(h)
swaps whether or not the standard lattice is self-dual with respect to the conjugate form described in the definition of the open compact subgroups for each of the two
3
-adic places in §
7.3.7
. It follows from Proposition
5.2
that
P_{3}^{\prime}\times P_{3}
is mapped to
P_{3}\times P_{3}^{\prime}
by swapping factors under the Galois action of
\tau
. This combines with the assertions for the other
P_{\nu}
to prove that the isomorphism of algebraic groups over
\mathbb{A}_{F}^{\infty}
takes
K_{47}
to
K_{49}
, which completes the proof of the proposition.
∎
