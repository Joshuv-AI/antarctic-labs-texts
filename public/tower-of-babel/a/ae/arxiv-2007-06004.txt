1.
Introduction

1.1.
Min-max theories for minimal submanifolds

The study of minimal surfaces, namely surfaces which are critical for the area functional,
has always been a central topic in geometric analysis and stimulated huge developments in the calculus of variations, geometric measure theory, partial differential equations and differential geometry.

Among the most important questions in the calculus of variations is Plateau’s problem,
which was actually posed by Lagrange in 1760, asking to find a surface of least area for any assigned (smooth, closed) one-dimensional boundary
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
This question was famously resolved, independently, by Douglas and Radó in 1930–1931
[
11
,
35
]
, for a connected
\Gamma
and with
\Sigma
minimizing the area among (branched) immersed disks.
The method is based on the fact that any immersion of the disk can be reparametrized to be conformal, so that the area becomes the Dirichlet energy; this functional has much better analytic properties and admits only a finite dimensional invariance group, whereas the area is invariant for the whole group of diffeomorphisms of the domain.

In order to construct higher dimensional minimal submanifolds, an approach involving parametrizations does not seem to be available.
On the other hand, bringing together the theories of rectifiable sets and De Rham’s currents, Federer and Fleming
[
13
]
developed the modern
theory of currents
, which allows to have an intrinsic weak notion of submanifold,
together with notions of boundary and area (the
mass
). Most importantly, there exists a natural topology which makes
integral currents
a compact space—for instance with assigned boundary and an upper bound for the mass—and the mass a lower semicontinuous functional.
These properties make it straightforward to apply the direct method of calculus of variations, in order to find a mass-minimizing object
\Sigma^{k}
with an assigned boundary
\partial\Sigma^{k}=\Gamma^{k-1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, provided
\Gamma
is itself a cycle and
1<k\leq m
.

The higher dimensional version of Plateau’s problem thus reduced to the problem of reaching a satisfactory regularity theory for the minimizer
\Sigma
.
This task was accomplished in codimension
1
, namely
k=m-1
, away from the boundary
\Gamma
—a crowning achievement which came from the work of De Giorgi, Fleming, Almgren, Simons and Federer.
The theory of currents is flexible enough to produce mass-minimizing cycles in a given homology class of a closed Riemannian manifold.

The obvious next question is to be able to produce general critical points for the area, either in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with a boundary constraint or in a closed curved ambient. The following conjecture by Yau, whose statement echoes the same question for geodesics, attracted a lot of attention in the last decades.

Conjecture

(Yau, 1982)
.

Does every closed three-dimensional Riemannian manifold
(\mathcal{M}^{3},g)
admit infinitely many closed immersed minimal surfaces?

A very robust method to produce general, possibly unstable critical points is by means of min-max problems, and goes back to the work of Birkhoff on the existence of closed geodesics on the 2-sphere with an arbitrary metric: see
[
4
]
for an introduction and a large collection of examples implementing this idea.

The main issue is then how to implement a min-max construction in the setting of minimal surfaces.
A successful theory was proposed by Almgren and Pitts
[
3
,
34
]
: within the theory of currents, using cycles mod
2
they produce an
almost minimizing varifold
in the limit. The notion of
varifold
, for which the reader may consult
[
41
, Chapters 4 and 8]
,
differs from the one of current in that, while also retaining good compactness properties, the mass becomes continuous under weak convergence: this property is essential to guarantee that the limit object attains the min-max value—on the other hand, lower semicontinuity of the mass for currents is just good enough for minimization problems.

While the regularity theory for general integer rectifiable stationary varifolds is wide open, with notable exceptions provided by the small-excess (and multiplicity-one) regularity theorem by Allard
[
1
]
and the deep structure result in the stable, codimension one case by Wickramasekera
[
45
]
, the technical requirement of
almost minimality
enabled Pitts—together with later work by Schoen and Simon
[
40
]
—to recover the full regularity for the limit varifold, proving that the singular set is empty in ambient dimension
3\leq m\leq 7
.

The use of this min-max framework led to the solution of several long-standing problems, including the Willmore conjecture, by Marques and Neves
[
27
]
, and the Yau conjecture, by Marques, Neves, Song and others
[
29
,
21
,
26
,
42
]
.

This theory was used also to construct
free boundary
minimal hypersurfaces:
given an ambient
\mathcal{M}^{m}
and a submanifold
\mathcal{N}
—usually
\mathcal{M}
has no boundary, or
\mathcal{N}
is precisely
\partial\mathcal{M}
—they are hypersurfaces
\Sigma^{m-1}
with boundary, embedded or immersed in
\mathcal{M}
, which are critical for the
(m-1)
-area with the constraint
\partial\Sigma\subseteq\mathcal{N}
.
This is equivalent to the fact that
\Sigma
is minimal and meets
\mathcal{N}
orthogonally along
\partial\Sigma
.

The most studied case is
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N})=(\overline{B}^{3},S^{2})
with the Euclidean metric. In
[
22
]
, using an equivariant version of the Simon–Smith theory—which is itself a less technical and more effective relative of Almgren–Pitts for surfaces, allowing for instance to control the genus of the resulting min-max surface—Ketover built free boundary minimal surfaces in the ball with arbitrarily big genus and three boundary components. In the same spirit, a very recent work by Carlotto, Franz and Schulz
[
7
]
constructs surfaces with connected boundary and arbitrary genus.

In
[
25
,
16
]
the Almgren–Pitts theory for hypersurfaces in arbitrary dimension is adapted to the free boundary case.
We also mention
[
10
]
for a similar min-max theory in the free boundary case, avoiding discretized constructions.

Several other techniques are used to construct free boundary minimal submanifolds, including notably desingularization methods and the study of extremal eigenvalue problems; for a survey of recent results, we invite the reader to consult
[
24
]
.

Recently, in the closed case, another approach using the Allen–Cahn functional was proposed by Guaraco
[
17
]
.
This theory, which started with the work of Modica
[
30
]
for minimizers and Hutchinson–Tonegawa
[
19
]
for general critical points, interprets a minimal hypersurface as a limit interface of a phase transition, hence as a sort of limit level set of functions which are critical for rescalings of the Allen–Cahn energy, which should be seen as a relaxation of the area for the level sets.
This approach seems to be at least as powerful as Almgren–Pitts; the additional structure given by having a sequence of smooth
critical
functions converging to the limit already allowed to obtain finer results: see, e.g., the recent works by Chodosh–Mantoulidis
[
9
]
and Bellettini
[
5
]
.

In codimension two, interesting attempts have been made using the Ginzburg–Landau energy for complex valued maps, by Cheng
[
8
]
and Stern
[
43
]
. This functional, which appears formally identical to Allen–Cahn—the latter being just Ginzburg–Landau for real valued maps—exhibits a totally different behavior in terms of energy concentration, due to the dominance of the angular part of the map in the Dirichlet term.
This component forces the asymptotic analysis to take place on infinitely many scales, making the study very challenging.
A different attempt, based on rescalings of the Yang–Mills–Higgs energy for sections and connections of a Hermitian line bundle,
was proposed by the author and Stern
[
33
]
. In this last framework, the asymptotic analysis becomes much simpler and quite similar to the Allen–Cahn setting, although a regularity theory still lacks.

Yet another framework, which is the one whose study is continued in this paper, was introduced by Rivière
[
37
]
.
It concerns minimal surfaces, but works in arbitrary codimension. As in the classical works
[
11
,
39
]
, it uses parametrizations
\Phi:\Sigma^{2}\to(\mathcal{M}^{m},g)
. On the other hand, the area is not immediately relaxed with the Dirichlet energy; rather, it uses the functional

\displaystyle E_{\sigma}^{\prime}(\Phi):=\operatorname{area}(\Phi)+\sigma^{2}\int_{\Sigma}(1+|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}|^{2})^{p}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi}

for
\sigma>0
and a fixed exponent
p>1
, where the norm of the second fundamental form
\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}
and the area element
\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi}
are with respect to the metric
\Phi^{*}g
induced by
\Phi
. By studying critical points for
E_{\sigma}^{\prime}
, one hopes to get a limit minimal immersion regardless of the topology of the closed surface
\Sigma
, while in the work by Sacks–Uhlenbeck
[
39
]
—which relaxes the Dirichlet energy—one can just reach a harmonic map, whose minimality is not guaranteed unless
\Sigma
is a sphere.
As for the free boundary case, minimality holds automatically only if
\Sigma
is a disk—a fact already exploited to solve Plateau’s problem;
in fact, we mention that the same approach developed in
[
39
]
was used to build free boundary minimal disks in
[
44
]
.
Note that
E_{\sigma}^{\prime}
is invariant under diffeomorphisms, whereas the Dirichlet energy is only conformally invariant.

The main outcome of
[
37
]
is that, once a sequence of maps
\Phi_{k}
critical for
E_{\sigma_{k}}
is carefully chosen,
the induced varifolds converge to a kind of limit object called
parametrized stationary varifold
.
This notion defines a special class of varifolds which are induced by a parametrization
\Phi
—possibly with a new domain
\widetilde{\Sigma}
due to bubbling phenomena—and a Borel multiplicity function
N
defined on
\widetilde{\Sigma}
. A crucial feature is that stationarity can be localized with respect to the domain. This allows to obtain the full regularity for the limit object: the regularity theory was started in
[
38
]
, where
N\equiv 1
is assumed, and carried out in full generality in
[
31
]
, where parametrized stationary varifolds are axiomatically studied.

Later, in
[
32
]
, the authors show that actually
N\equiv 1
in the variational setting, by exploiting the results from
[
37
,
31
]
.
This fact allows to obtain an upper bound on the Morse index of the limit minimal immersion in terms of the number of min-max parameters.
In this sense it is the correct analogue of the
multiplicity one conjecture
by Marques–Neves
[
28
]
,
which has been confirmed very recently in the generic, codimension one case for the Almgren–Pitts framework
[
46
]
.

1.2.
Main results

In this paper we study a similar energy for surfaces with boundary; namely, choosing
p=2
and replacing
\sigma
with
\sigma^{2}
for conveniency, we work with the energies

\displaystyle E_{\sigma}(\Phi):=\operatorname{area}(\Phi)+\sigma\operatorname{length}(\Phi|_{\partial\Sigma})+\sigma^{4}\int_{\Sigma}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}|^{4}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi},

where
\Sigma
is a fixed compact oriented surface with (possibly nonempty) boundary, and
\Phi:\Sigma\to\mathcal{M}^{m}
is a smooth immersion with the constraint
\Phi(\partial\Sigma)\subseteq\mathcal{N}
.
The parameter
\sigma
should be thought dimensionally as a length.

In this work we fully exploit the invariance of
E_{\sigma}
under diffeomorphisms of the domain, namely the principle that every diffeomorphism invariant quantity should depend only on the shape of the immersed surface.
In computing the first variation we will see that, using infinitesimal variations of the form
w=X(\Phi)
,
all second-order terms involving
w
are expressible just in terms of the second fundamental form of
\Phi
, as expected.
A natural consequence of this is that the first variation of the relaxing terms
\sigma\operatorname{length}(\Phi|_{\partial\Sigma})

and
\sigma^{4}\int_{\Sigma}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}|^{4}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi}
, for such special ambient deformations, can be bounded in terms of these quantities themselves (and the ambient vector field
X
).

Also, working on a Finsler manifold
\mathfrak{M}
of
W^{2,4}
immersions, equipped with a norm on
T_{\Phi}\mathfrak{M}
involving the induced metric
g_{\Phi}:=\Phi^{*}g
, we observe that also
\|X(\Phi)\|_{\Phi}
is bounded in terms of
E_{\sigma}(\Phi)
,
X
and
\sigma
.
Since in the asymptotic analysis we will use only this particular kind of variations, we do not need to construct critical points of
E_{\sigma}
: it suffices to have
\|dE_{\sigma}(\Phi)\|_{\Phi}
very small in terms of
\sigma
.
Since such almost critical maps are easy to construct using pseudo-gradient flows and can be assumed, without loss of generality, to be smooth, this makes the paper self-contained—except for the regularity theory in
Section

7
.

These observations, detailed in Sections
2
and
3
, represent a major simplification over the original work
[
37
]
, which appeals to
[
6
]
for the Palais–Smale property of
E_{\sigma}^{\prime}
and the regularity of critical maps.
The formulas obtained in this paper are quite simple, independently of the ambient: differently from
[
37
]
—where
\mathcal{M}
is assumed to be the round sphere
S^{3}
in order to simplify the presentation—we can deal immediately with general closed manifolds
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
.

As in the closed case, the main difficulty is to prove a lower bound for the area of the immersed surface
\Phi
in suitable balls
B_{r}(p)
in the ambient. This is accomplished by studying how the ratio
\frac{\mu(B_{s}(p))}{s^{2}}
behaves as
s
varies, with
\mu
denoting the area measure of
\Phi
on
\mathcal{M}
. While for
s<\sigma
the boundedness of the quantity
\sigma^{4}\int_{\Sigma}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}|^{4}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi}
is enough—in that, heuristically, magnifying by a factor
s^{-1}
we get an
L^{4}
-bound on the second fundamental form and we can apply directly the monotonicity formula—for
s>\sigma
we have to use the almost criticality of
\Phi
.

Namely, we use the same vector fields used to show the (approximate) monotonicity of
\frac{\mu(B_{s}(p))}{s^{2}}
for free boundary minimal surfaces, in order to understand the growth rate of this ratio for our immersed surface.
Oversimplifying, the quantity
\frac{\sigma^{4}}{s}\int_{\Sigma}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}|^{4}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi}
appears among the error terms: since this has to be integrated between
\sigma
and
r
, this produces an error
\sigma^{4}\log(\sigma^{-1})\int_{\Sigma}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}|^{4}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi}
.
As in
[
37
]
, this can be assumed to be infinitesimal with a careful selection of
\sigma
and
\Phi
, based on Struwe’s monotonicity trick for relaxed energies. In reality, the argument also requires a
maximal
bound

\displaystyle\sigma^{4}\int_{\Phi^{-1}(B_{s}(p))}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}|^{4}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi}\leq\delta\mu(B_{s}(p))\quad\text{for all }s>0.

We add the additional term
\sigma\operatorname{length}(\Phi|_{\partial\Sigma})
in
E_{\sigma}
in order to deal with the additional challenge of having a nontrivial boundary
\Phi|_{\partial\Sigma}
. Due to this, we cannot use the monotonicity formula on a ball
B_{s}(p)
(with
s<\sigma
) whose preimage intersects
\partial\Sigma
. In principle, one can impose a strong control of the boundary by adding a term involving the geodesic curvature of
\Phi|_{\partial\Sigma}
; however, this would still require to understand the topology of
\Phi^{-1}(B_{s}(p))
.

Rather, using a covering argument, we show that the set of points with distance less than
\sigma
from
\Phi(\partial\Sigma)
has an area (i.e., the measure
\mu
) controlled by
\sigma\operatorname{length}(\Phi|_{\partial\Sigma})
; this term is again infinitesimal as
\sigma\to 0
, so that this set can be ignored in the asymptotic analysis.

The rest of the paper adapts the remaining arguments from
[
37
]
and
[
38
]
to the free boundary case—again with some important simplifications.
In
Section

6
we study carefully what happens when the conformal structure induced by
\Phi
degenerates as
\sigma\to 0
, which is more delicate and less well known for surfaces with boundary.

The following is the main result of this work.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{M}^{m},g)
be a closed Riemannian manifold,
\mathcal{N}^{n}\subset\mathcal{M}
a closed embedded submanifold (with
1\leq n<m
),
and let
\Sigma
be a compact oriented surface, possibly with boundary.
Given a sequence
\Phi_{k}
of immersions which are
\sigma_{k}^{5}
-critical for
E_{\sigma_{k}}
, have bounded area and satisfy the condition

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}\log\sigma_{k}^{-1}\operatorname{length}(\Phi_{k}|_{\partial\Sigma})+\sigma_{k}^{4}\log\sigma_{k}^{-1}\int_{\Sigma}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi_{k}}|^{4}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi_{k}}\to 0,

there exists a subsequence such that the induced varifolds converge to a
parametrized free boundary stationary varifold
for the couple
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N})
. Moreover, the connected components
\Sigma_{i}
of its domain have

\chi(\Sigma_{i})\geq\chi(\Sigma)
and
g(\Sigma_{i})\leq g(\Sigma)
.

In this statement
\chi(\cdot)
is the Euler characteristic and
g(\cdot)
is the genus. The last part of the statement follows from the analysis carried out in
Section

6
.
We refer to
Definition

5.9
for the precise description of this notion of parametrized varifold; the fact that one can localize the stationarity with respect to the domain stems from the fact that one can use variations
w=X(\Phi)
also just on a domain
\omega\subset\Sigma
, extending
w
to vanish on the complement, provided
X
is supported far from
\Phi(\partial\omega)
.

As in the closed case, we can actually assert that the multiplicity function in the parametrized varifold is everywhere equal to
1
: see
7.1
.

Remark 1.2
.

This result applies also to a compact ambient manifold
\mathcal{M}
with boundary
\mathcal{N}
, such as the flat unit ball
\overline{B}^{3}
; note that the (almost) criticality should be understood formally, for infinitesimal variations
w
which are sections of
\Phi^{*}T\mathcal{M}
, with
w(\partial\Sigma)\subseteq T\mathcal{N}
.
Indeed, we can smoothly extend
\mathcal{M}
to a closed Riemannian manifold and reduce to the previous statement.

Remark 1.3
.

It also applies to the case
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
, with
\mathcal{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
a closed embedded submanifold:
the lower bounds obtained in
Section

4
(see also the proof of
Proposition

5.1
)
show that the varifolds induced by
\Phi_{k}
form a tight sequence, and the result then follows with the same proofs.

As for the regularity of the limit, we have the following.

Theorem 1.4
.

For a parametrized free boundary stationary varifold
(\widetilde{\Sigma},\Phi,N)
, the map
\Phi
is smooth up to the boundary
\partial\Sigma
, where
\partial_{\nu}\Phi\perp T\mathcal{N}
. Also, on the components of
\widetilde{\Sigma}
where
\Phi
is not (a.e.) constant, the multiplicity
N
is constant and
\Phi
is a branched minimal immersion outside
\partial\Sigma
.

Remark 1.5
.

We stress that the limit (branched) immersion
\Phi
is free boundary minimal in the sense that it meets the constraint
\mathcal{N}
orthogonally along
\partial\widetilde{\Sigma}
.
However, there could be points
x
in the interior
\operatorname{int}(\Sigma)=\Sigma\setminus\partial\Sigma
with

\Phi(x)\in\mathcal{N}
—a possibility which cannot happen, e.g., for
(\overline{B}^{3},S^{2})
(on the components where
\Phi
is not constant); unlike the main result of
[
25
]
, at such points the orthogonality is not guaranteed.

A simple corollary is, for instance, the following. Note that other min-max situations can be dealt with in the same way.

Corollary 1.6
.

Given any collection
\mathcal{F}
of compact subsets of the space of smooth immersions
(\Sigma,\partial\Sigma)\to(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N})
, assuming
\mathcal{F}
to be stable for isotopies of this space, the min-max value

\displaystyle\beta:=\inf_{A\in\mathcal{F}}\max_{\Phi\in A}\operatorname{area}(\Phi)

is the sum of the areas of finitely many free boundary minimal (branched) immersions
\Phi_{(i)}:\Sigma_{(i)}\to\mathcal{M}
, whose domains are connected and have
\chi(\Sigma_{(i)})\geq\chi(\Sigma)
and
g(\Sigma_{(i)})\leq g(\Sigma)
.

Remark 1.7
.

While we deal only with surfaces
\Sigma
, the proofs in the next two sections generalize immediately to closed
k
-dimensional domains, with the energy

\displaystyle\Phi\mapsto\text{$k$-area}(\Phi)+\int_{\Sigma}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}|^{p}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi}

for
W^{2,p}
immersions
\Phi:\Sigma\to\mathcal{M}
, with
p>k
.
We get a stationary varifold in the limit, which is rectifiable since its density is bounded below, by
Proposition

4.2
,
Corollary

4.6
and the arguments from
Proposition

5.1
(which carry over with obvious changes).

However, what seems so far out of reach (when
k>2
) is how to retain a
parametrized
structure for the limit varifold.

1.3.
Organization of the paper

We conclude the introduction with a very brief description of the structure of the paper.

•

In

we show how to deduce
Corollary

1.6
from

, by introducing a Finsler manifold of maps and checking that it satisfies the conditions guaranteeing that Struwe’s monotonicity trick applies;

•

for special variations

,

\Phi_{k}

, under the assumption that

.

2.
Almost critical points for
E_{\sigma}

Let
(\mathcal{M}^{m},g)
be a closed Riemannian manifold and
\mathcal{N}^{n}\subset\mathcal{M}
a closed embedded submanifold, with
1\leq n<m
.
For simplicity, we will assume without loss of generality that
\mathcal{M}
is isometrically embedded in some Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{Q}
,
although the proofs could be easily modified so as to avoid the Nash embedding theorem.

Also, let
\Sigma
be a compact surface, possibly with boundary
\partial\Sigma
.
In this paper we will study the following relaxation of the area functional: given an immersion
\Phi:\Sigma\to\mathcal{M}
, we let

(2.1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} E_{\sigma}(\Phi)&:=\operatorname{area}(\Phi)+\sigma\operatorname{length}(\Phi|_{\partial\Sigma})+\sigma^{4}\int_{\Sigma}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}|^{4}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi}\\
&\phantom{:}=\int_{\Sigma}\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi}+\sigma\int_{\partial\Sigma}\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi|_{\partial\Sigma}}+\sigma^{4}\int_{\Sigma}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}|^{4}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi}.\end{aligned}

Here
\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi}
and
\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi|_{\partial\Sigma}}
are the (two- and one-dimensional) volume forms of the induced metric
\Phi^{*}g
on
\Sigma
and
\partial\Sigma
, which we will often identify with the corresponding measures.
In the last term,
\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}
denotes the second fundamental form of
\Phi
.

In order to construct almost critical maps for
E_{\sigma}
, with the constraint
\Phi(\partial\Sigma)\subseteq\mathcal{N}
, we introduce the topological space

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}:=\{\Phi\in W^{2,4}(\Sigma,\mathcal{M}):\Phi\text{ is an immersion and }\Phi(\partial\Sigma)\subseteq\mathcal{N}\},

with the topology induced from
W^{2,4}(\Sigma,\mathcal{M})
, in turn induced from
W^{2,4}(\Sigma,\mathbb{R}^{Q})
.
Recall that
W^{2,4}(\Sigma,\mathbb{R}^{Q})
embeds into
C^{1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{R}^{Q})
, so that the definition makes sense and
\mathfrak{M}
is canonically a Banach manifold.

For each
\Phi\in\mathfrak{M}
, the tangent space
T_{\Phi}\mathfrak{M}
identifies with the Banach space of
W^{2,4}
sections
s:\Sigma\to T\mathcal{M}
of the pullback bundle
\Phi^{*}T\mathcal{M}
, with
s\in T\mathcal{N}
along
\partial\Sigma
.

Given
\Phi\in\mathfrak{M}
, we call
g_{\Phi}:=\Phi^{*}g
the metric that
\Phi
induces on
\Sigma
.
We endow
T_{\Phi}\mathfrak{M}
with the following norm: we let

\displaystyle\|s\|_{\Phi}:=\|s\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla s\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla^{2}s\|_{L^{4}},

where
\nabla
is the pullback connection on
\Phi^{*}T\mathcal{M}

and the norms are with respect to the metrics
g
on
T\mathcal{M}
and
g_{\Phi}
on
T^{*}\Sigma
.
It is straightforward to check that this choice satisfies the requirements to be a Finsler structure on
\mathfrak{M}
(see
[
14
, p. 54]
for the definition).

Proposition 2.1
.

The Finsler manifold
\mathfrak{M}
is complete.

Recall that the distance between two elements
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}\in\mathfrak{M}
(in the same connected component) is defined to be the infimum of

\int_{0}^{1}\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|_{\gamma(t)}\,dt
, as
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathfrak{M}
ranges among all piecewise
C^{1}
curves from
\Phi_{1}
to
\Phi_{2}
. It is a consequence of the Finsler structure axioms that it induces the original topology on
\mathfrak{M}
.

Proof.

Let
(\Phi_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be a Cauchy sequence. Up to subsequences, we can assume that
\sum_{k}\operatorname{dist}(\Phi_{k},\Phi_{k+1})<\infty
.
Hence, by definition we can find a piecewise
C^{1}
curve
\Phi:[0,\infty)\to\mathfrak{M}
of finite length, with
\Phi(k)=\Phi_{k}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We will use the notation
\Phi_{t}
in place of
\Phi(t)
. It suffices to show that
\Phi_{t}
converges in
W^{2,4}
as
t\to\infty
. With a perturbation argument, we can assume that
\Phi_{t}(x)
is smooth in the couple
(x,t)
.

Let
w_{t}:=\frac{d\Phi_{t}}{dt}
. Since
w_{t}
is bounded pointwise by the summable (in
t
) quantity
\|w_{t}\|_{\Phi_{t}}
, we know that
\Phi_{t}
converges in
C^{0}
to a limit
\Phi_{\infty}
.

Let
g_{t}:=g_{\Phi_{t}}
be the metric induced by the immersion
\Phi_{t}
on
\Sigma
. For a fixed
v\in T\Sigma
we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}g_{t}(v,v)=\frac{d}{dt}|d\Phi_{t}[v]|^{2}=2\left\langle d\Phi_{t}[v],\nabla_{v}w_{t}\right\rangle

and, since
|\nabla_{v}w_{t}|\leq\|w_{t}\|_{\Phi_{t}}|v|_{g_{t}}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{d}{dt}g_{t}(v,v)\Big|\leq 2g_{t}(v,v)\|w_{t}\|_{\Phi_{t}}.

Hence, for
v\neq 0
, the time derivative of
\log g_{t}(v,v)
is bounded in
L^{1}
on
[0,\infty)
. Thus there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle C^{-2}g_{0}(v,v)\leq g_{t}(v,v)\leq C^{2}g_{0}(v,v)

for all
t\geq 0
and all
v\in T\Sigma
. As a consequence, for any
x\in\Sigma
and any
v\in T_{x}\Sigma

\displaystyle|\nabla_{\partial_{t}}(d\Phi_{t}[v])|\leq|\nabla w_{t}|_{g_{t}}|v|_{g_{t}}\leq C\|w_{t}\|_{\Phi_{t}}|v|_{g_{0}},

with
\nabla_{\partial_{t}}
being the covariant derivative along the curve
\Phi_{t}(x)
. Together with the
C^{0}
convergence
\Phi_{t}\to\Phi_{\infty}
, this implies that actually
\Phi_{t}\to\Phi_{\infty}
in
C^{1}
. Finally, given smooth vector fields
X,Y
on
\Sigma
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\partial_{t}}\nabla_{X}(d\Phi_{t}[Y])=\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}w_{t}+\operatorname{Rm}(d\Phi_{t}[X],w_{t})(d\Phi_{t}[Y])

where
\riem(V,W)Z=\nabla^{2}_{W,V}Z-\nabla^{2}_{V,W}Z
is the Riemann tensor of
\mathcal{M}
. Again, thanks to the comparability between
g_{0}
and
g_{t}
, the right-hand side is bounded in
L^{4}
by
\|w_{t}\|_{\Phi_{t}}
, up to a multiplicative constant depending only on
X,Y
. This implies the convergence
\Phi_{t}\to\Phi_{\infty}
in
W^{2,4}
.
∎

The following variational result, essentially due to Struwe, is proved in
[
36
]
. Before stating it, we give a notion of
admissible family
.

Definition 2.2
.

Given a Banach manifold
\mathfrak{M}
, a nonempty family
\mathcal{F}
of subsets of
\mathfrak{M}
is said to be
admissible

if, for any continuous deformation
F:[0,1]\times\mathfrak{M}\to\mathfrak{M}
with
F_{0}=\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{M}}
and
F_{t}
a homeomorphism for all
0\leq t\leq 1
, we have
F_{1}(A)\in\mathcal{F}
for all
A\in\mathcal{F}
(where
F_{t}:=F(t,\cdot)
).

Proposition 2.3
.

Assume
(E_{\sigma})_{\sigma\geq 0}
is a family of
C^{1}
functionals on a complete Finsler manifold
\mathfrak{M}
, with
E_{\sigma}(x)
differentiable in
\sigma
and
\sigma\mapsto E_{\sigma}(x)
,
\sigma\mapsto\frac{d}{d\sigma}E_{\sigma}(x)
both increasing in
\sigma
, for every
x\in\mathfrak{M}
. Assume also that

(2.2)

\displaystyle\|dE_{\sigma_{j}}(x_{j})-dE_{\sigma}(x_{j})\|\to 0

whenever

1\geq\sigma_{j}\geq\sigma>0
,
\sigma_{j}\to\sigma
and
\limsup_{j\to\infty}E_{\sigma}(x_{j})<\infty
.

Then, for any admissible family
\mathcal{F}
, defining the min-max values

\displaystyle\beta(\sigma):=\inf_{A\in\mathcal{F}}\sup_{x\in A}E_{\sigma}(x),

there exist sequences
(\sigma_{k})\subseteq(0,1)
and
(x_{k})\subseteq\mathfrak{M}
, with
\sigma_{k}\to 0
, such that

\displaystyle E_{\sigma_{k}}(x_{k})-\beta(\sigma_{k})\to 0,\quad\|dE_{\sigma_{k}}(x_{k})\|<f(\sigma_{k}),\quad\sigma_{k}\log(1/\sigma_{k})\frac{d}{d\sigma}E_{\sigma}(x_{k})\Big|_{\sigma_{k}}\to 0,

where
f:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is any function fixed in advance.

This statement is quite robust and can be adapted to other kinds of min-max problems, where one replaces admissible families with other notions.

Remark 2.4
.

Actually, in
[
36
]
the functional
E_{\sigma}
is assumed to be Palais–Smale, and the second conclusion becomes
dE_{\sigma_{k}}(x_{k})=0
. Without this hypothesis, we can still find almost critical points
x_{k}
for
E_{\sigma_{k}}
,
in the sense that we can require
\|dE_{\sigma_{k}}(x_{k})\|
to be as small as we want, with the same proof.

Proposition 2.5
.

The functionals
(E_{\sigma})_{\sigma\geq 0}
previously defined satisfy the assumptions of
Proposition

2.3
.

Before proving this fact, we make an important observation.

Proposition 2.6
.

For
X,Y
vector fields on
\Sigma
we have
(\nabla d\Phi)(X,Y)=\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}(\Phi_{*}X,\Phi_{*}Y)
.

Proof.

The left-hand side equals
\nabla_{X}(\Phi_{*}Y)-\Phi_{*}\nabla_{X}Y
;
since
\Phi
is an isometry from
(\Sigma,g_{\Phi})
to the immersed surface
\Phi
,
the term
\Phi_{*}\nabla_{X}Y
equals (locally) the Levi-Civita connection
\nabla_{\Phi_{*}X}\Phi_{*}Y

on this surface; the latter equals the orthogonal projection of
\nabla_{X}(\Phi_{*}Y)
onto the tangent plane, since
\nabla
is the pullback of the Levi-Civita connection from
\mathcal{M}
.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

2.5
.

We only need to check that
Eq.

2.2
holds. We first show how to obtain an upper bound for
|dE_{\sigma^{\prime}}(\Phi)[w]-dE_{\sigma}(\Phi)[w]|
, when
1\geq{\sigma^{\prime}}\geq\sigma>0
.

If
\Phi\in\mathfrak{M}
is a smooth map and
(\Phi_{t})
is a smooth variation (with
\Phi_{0}=\Phi
), we compute

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Sigma}\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi_{t}}\Big|_{t=0}=\int_{\Sigma}\left\langle d\Phi,\nabla w\right\rangle\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi},

where
w:=\frac{d}{dt}\Phi_{t}\Big|_{t=0}
belongs to
T_{\Phi}\mathfrak{M}
,
and the scalar product (with respect to
g_{\Phi}
) in the integral is bounded by
2\|\nabla w\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 2\|w\|_{\Phi}
.

With a similar computation for the length of
\Phi|_{\partial\Sigma}
, we get

(2.3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}E_{\sigma}(\Phi)\Big|_{t=0}&=\int_{\Sigma}(1+\sigma^{4}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}|^{4})\left\langle d\Phi,\nabla w\right\rangle\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi}+\sigma\int_{\partial\Sigma}\left\langle d\Phi[\tau],\nabla_{\tau}w\right\rangle\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi|_{\partial\Sigma}}\\
&\quad+\sigma^{4}\int_{\Sigma}\frac{d}{dt}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi_{t}}|^{4}\Big|_{t=0}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi},\end{aligned}

where
\tau
is the unit vector (with respect to
g_{\Phi}
) orienting
\partial\Sigma
.

Given a local orthonormal frame
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
, oriented as
\Sigma
, define
n_{t}:=d\Phi_{t}[e_{1}]\wedge d\Phi_{t}[e_{2}]
.
We have
|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi_{t}}|=|\nabla n_{t}|
and

\displaystyle\nabla_{\partial_{t}}\nabla_{X}n_{t}=\nabla_{X}\nabla_{\partial_{t}}n_{t}+\riem\Big(d\Phi[X],\frac{d\Phi}{dt}\Big)n_{t},

where
\riem(a,b)(c\wedge d):=(\riem(a,b)c)\wedge d+c\wedge(\riem(a,b)d)
for vectors in
T\mathcal{M}
. At
t=0
the above equals
\nabla_{X}\omega+R(d\Phi[X],w)n
, where
n:=n_{0}
and

\displaystyle\omega:=\nabla_{e_{1}}w\wedge d\Phi[e_{2}]+d\Phi[e_{1}]\wedge\nabla_{e_{2}}w-\left\langle d\Phi[e_{i}],\nabla_{e_{i}}w\right\rangle n.

Using
Proposition

2.6
we see that
|\nabla_{X}\omega|\leq C|X|(|\nabla^{2}w|+|\nabla w||\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}|)
.

Finally, the contribution of the metric

for the time derivative of
|\nabla n_{t}|^{4}
is just
-4|\nabla n|^{2}\left\langle d\Phi\otimes\nabla w,\nabla n\otimes\nabla n\right\rangle
. Combining this fact with the preceding computations, we deduce that the time derivative of
|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi_{t}}|^{4}
at

is bounded by

(2.4)

\displaystyle|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}|^{3}|\nabla^{2}w|+|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}|^{4}|\nabla w|+|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}|^{3}|w|

up to a multiplicative constant depending on
\mathcal{M}
.

Thus, using
Eq.

2.3
,
Eq.

2.4
, Hölder’s inequality and Young’s inequality, we see that

\displaystyle|dE_{\sigma^{\prime}}(\Phi)[w]-dE_{\sigma}(\Phi)[w]|\leq C\frac{{\sigma^{\prime}}-\sigma}{\sigma}E_{\sigma}(\Phi)\|w\|_{\Phi}+C({\sigma^{\prime}}-\sigma)E_{\sigma}(\Phi)^{3/4}\|w\|_{\Phi}

for
0<\sigma\leq{\sigma^{\prime}}\leq 2\sigma
.
Since
E_{\sigma}
and
E_{\sigma^{\prime}}
are
C^{1}
functionals, this bound holds for general
\Phi\in\mathfrak{M}
and
w\in T_{\Phi}\mathfrak{M}
.
Starting from this estimate, it is immediate to check that
Eq.

2.2
is satisfied.
∎

Thanks to

, letting

we can then find sequences of numbers

and maps

.
In particular,

\displaystyle\|dE_{\sigma_{k}}(\Phi_{k})\|_{\Phi_{k}}<\sigma_{k}^{5}

and

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}\log(1/\sigma_{k})\operatorname{length}(\Phi_{k}|_{\partial\Sigma})+\sigma_{k}^{4}\log(1/\sigma_{k})\int_{\Sigma}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi_{k}}|^{4}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi_{k}}\to 0.

Since smooth functions are dense in
\mathfrak{M}
, we can assume that the maps

are smooth.

and the varifolds

induced by

. Note that the weight measure

equals

.

For any

, by compactness of

3.
First variation

In this section we will derive a particularly useful formula for the first variation of
E_{\sigma}
at
\Phi\in\mathfrak{M}
, for infinitesimal variations
w\in T_{\Phi}\mathfrak{M}
of the form
X(\Phi)
, with
X
a smooth vector field on
\mathcal{M}
.

Let
\Phi\in\mathfrak{M}
be a smooth map and
w\in T_{\Phi}\mathfrak{M}
a smooth section of
\Phi^{*}T\mathcal{M}
, with
w\in T\mathcal{N}
on
\partial\Sigma
.
In the sequel,
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
will be an oriented orthonormal basis at an arbitrary point of
\Sigma
, with respect to the induced metric
g_{\Phi}
.
The
(1,1)
-tensor
J:T\Sigma\to T\Sigma
, given by
Je_{1}:=e_{2}
and
Je_{2}:=-e_{1}
, is parallel for this metric.

As in the proof of
Proposition

2.5
, we use the notation
n:=\Phi_{*}e_{1}\wedge\Phi_{*}e_{2}
and we set
f:=|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}|^{2}=|\nabla n|^{2}
.
We also define the sections
\widehat{I}
and
\widehat{J}
of
\Phi^{*}T\mathcal{M}\otimes T^{*}\Sigma
, as well as the section

\widehat{\mathrm{I\!I}}
of
\Phi^{*}T\mathcal{M}\otimes T^{*}\Sigma\otimes T^{*}\Sigma
, by

\displaystyle\widehat{I}(v):=\Phi_{*}v,\ \widehat{J}(v):=\Phi_{*}(Jv),\ \widehat{\mathrm{I\!I}}(v,v^{\prime}):=\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}(\Phi_{*}v,\Phi_{*}v^{\prime}),\quad\text{for }v,v^{\prime}\in T\Sigma.

Recall the following formula, which was computed in that proof:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} dE_{\sigma}(\Phi)[w]&=\int_{\Sigma}(1+\sigma^{4}f^{2})\big\langle\widehat{I},\nabla w\big\rangle+\sigma\int_{\partial\Sigma}\left\langle\Phi_{*}\tau,\nabla_{\tau}w\right\rangle\\
&\quad+4\sigma^{4}\int_{\Sigma}f\left\langle\nabla n,\nabla\omega+\riem(d\Phi,w)n\right\rangle-4\sigma^{4}\int_{\Sigma}f\big\langle\widehat{I}\otimes\nabla w,\nabla n\otimes\nabla n\big\rangle,\end{aligned}

where we omit the volume forms and
\omega
denotes the infinitesimal variation of
n
, namely

\displaystyle\omega=\nabla_{e_{i}}w\wedge\widehat{J}(e_{i})-\big\langle\nabla w,\widehat{I}\big\rangle n.

When the variation
w
has the form
w=X(\Phi)
, using
Proposition

2.6
we get

(3.2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \nabla w&=\nabla X(\Phi)[\Phi_{*}\cdot]=\nabla X\circ\widehat{I},\\
\nabla_{e_{i},e_{j}}^{2}w&=\nabla^{2}X(\Phi)[\Phi_{*}e_{i},\Phi_{*}e_{j}]+\nabla X(\Phi)[\widehat{\mathrm{I\!I}}(e_{i},e_{j})].\end{aligned}

For such special variations,
Eq.

3.1
becomes

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} dE_{\sigma}(\Phi)[w]&=\int_{\Sigma}(1+\sigma^{4}f^{2})\left\langle\widehat{I},\nabla X\circ\widehat{I}\right\rangle+\sigma\int_{\partial\Sigma}\left\langle\Phi_{*}\tau,\nabla X[\Phi_{*}\tau]\right\rangle\\
&\quad+4\sigma^{4}\int_{\Sigma}f(\left\langle\nabla n,\nabla\omega\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla n,\riem(d\Phi,X(\Phi))n\right\rangle)\\
&\quad-4\sigma^{4}\int_{\Sigma}f\big\langle\widehat{I}\otimes(\nabla X\circ\widehat{I}),\nabla n\otimes\nabla n\big\rangle.\end{aligned}

We now write the term
\left\langle\nabla n,\nabla\omega\right\rangle
in a way which will prove useful for our later work. Since
\left\langle\nabla_{e_{i}}n,n\right\rangle=0
, we compute

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla n,\nabla\omega\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle\nabla n,\nabla(\nabla_{e_{i}}w\wedge\widehat{J}(e_{i}))\big\rangle-\big\langle\nabla w,\widehat{I}\big\rangle|\nabla n|^{2}

and the first term equals
\big\langle\nabla_{e_{j}}n,\nabla^{2}_{e_{j},e_{k}}w\wedge\widehat{J}(e_{k})+\nabla_{e_{k}}w\wedge\widehat{\mathrm{I\!I}}(e_{j},Je_{k})\big\rangle
.
Substituting the above formulas for
\nabla w
and
\nabla^{2}w
, we get

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla n,\nabla\omega\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla_{e_{j}}n,\nabla^{2}X[\Phi_{*}e_{j},\Phi_{*}e_{k}]\wedge\widehat{J}(e_{k})+\nabla X[\widehat{\mathrm{I\!I}}(e_{j},e_{k})]\wedge\widehat{J}(e_{k})

\displaystyle\quad+\nabla X[\Phi_{*}e_{k}]\wedge\widehat{\mathrm{I\!I}}(e_{j},Je_{k})\rangle-\big\langle\nabla X,\widehat{I}\big\rangle|\nabla n|^{2}.

Thus,

(3.4)

\displaystyle f|\left\langle\nabla n,\nabla\omega\right\rangle|\leq C(\mathcal{M})(\|\nabla X\|_{L^{\infty}}f^{2}+\|\nabla^{2}X\|_{L^{\infty}}f^{3/2}).

We are now ready to state an initial consequence of this bound.

Definition 3.1
.

A
k
-varifold
\mathbf{v}
on
\mathcal{M}
is a
free boundary stationary varifold
for the couple
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N})
if it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|(F_{t})_{*}\mathbf{v}\|(\mathcal{M})\Big|_{t=0}=0

whenever
(F_{t})_{-\varepsilon<t<\varepsilon}
is a family of diffeomorphisms of
\mathcal{M}
with
F_{t}(\mathcal{N})=\mathcal{N}
,
F_{0}=\operatorname{id}

and
F_{t}(x)
smooth in the couple
(t,x)
. We say that
\mathbf{v}
is free boundary stationary
outside
a closed set
K\subseteq\mathcal{M}
if the same holds for isotopies
(F_{t})
such that
F_{t}|_{U}=\operatorname{id}
for some neighborhood
U\supseteq K
.

Definition 3.2
.

We denote
\mathcal{X}_{fb}
the linear space of smooth vector fields
X
on
\mathcal{M}
which are tangent to
\mathcal{N}
,
namely such that
X(p)\in T_{p}\mathcal{N}
for all
p\in\mathcal{N}
.

Remark 3.3
.

With
X:=\frac{d}{dt}F_{t}\Big|_{t=0}
, we have
X\in\mathcal{X}_{fb}
and

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|(F_{t})_{*}\mathbf{v}\|(\mathcal{M})\Big|_{t=0}=\int_{(p,\Pi)\in\operatorname{Gr}_{k}(\mathcal{M})}\operatorname{div}_{\Pi}X\,d\mathbf{v}(p,\Pi),

where
\operatorname{Gr}_{k}(\mathcal{M})
is the Grassmannian bundle made of couples
(p,\Pi)
with
p\in\mathcal{M}
and
\Pi\subseteq T_{p}\mathcal{M}
a
k
-plane. Conversely, given
X
tangent to
\mathcal{N}
, we can take
F_{t}
to be its flow. Hence,

\mathbf{v}
is a free boundary stationary varifold if and only if

\displaystyle\int_{(p,\Pi)\in\operatorname{Gr}_{k}(\mathcal{M})}\operatorname{div}_{\Pi}X\,d\mathbf{v}(p,\Pi)=0\quad\text{for all }X\in\mathcal{X}_{fb}.

Similarly,
\mathbf{v}
is free boundary stationary outside
K
if and only if the same holds for all
X\in\mathcal{X}_{fb}\cap C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathcal{M}\setminus K)
.

Given a sequence
(\Phi_{k})
as in
Section

2
, the following holds.

Theorem 3.4
.

The varifolds
\mathbf{v}_{k}
induced by
\Phi_{k}
converge, up to subsequences, to a free boundary stationary varifold
\mathbf{v}_{\infty}
.

A priori it is not clear whether
\mathbf{v}_{\infty}
is integer rectifiable. This, together with a structure theorem for
\mathbf{v}_{\infty}
, will be proved later on.

Proof.

Fix any
(F_{t})_{-\varepsilon<t<\varepsilon}
as above and consider the variation
(F_{t}\circ\Phi_{k})\subseteq\mathfrak{M}
.
The corresponding infinitesimal variation
w_{k}\in T_{\Phi_{k}}\mathfrak{M}
is just
w_{k}=X\circ\Phi_{k}
. Hence,
Eq.

3.3
and
Eq.

2.5
give

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\Sigma}(1+\sigma_{k}^{4}f_{k}^{2})\left\langle\widehat{I}_{k},\nabla X\circ\widehat{I}_{k}\right\rangle+\sigma_{k}\int_{\partial\Sigma}\left\langle(\Phi_{k})_{*}\tau,\nabla X[(\Phi_{k})_{*}\tau]\right\rangle\\
&+4\sigma_{k}^{4}\int_{\Sigma}f_{k}(\left\langle\nabla n_{k},\nabla\omega_{k}\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla n_{k},\riem(d\Phi_{k},X(\Phi_{k}))n_{k}\right\rangle)\\
&-4\sigma_{k}^{4}\int_{\Sigma}f_{k}\big\langle\widehat{I}_{k}\otimes(\nabla X\circ\widehat{I}_{k}),\nabla n_{k}\otimes\nabla n_{k}\big\rangle\\
&=o(\sigma_{k}^{4}\|w_{k}\|_{\Phi_{k}}).\end{aligned}

We now show that all terms where
\sigma_{k}
appears are infinitesimal as
k\to\infty
.
Note that
\big|\big\langle\widehat{I}_{k},\nabla X\circ\widehat{I}_{k}\big\rangle\big|\leq 2\|\nabla X\|_{L^{\infty}}
, since the scalar product is with respect to the induced metric
g_{\Phi_{k}}
.
Hence, by
Eq.

2.6
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}^{4}\int_{\Sigma}f_{k}^{2}\big\langle\widehat{I}_{k},\nabla X\circ\widehat{I}_{k}\big\rangle\to 0

and similarly the boundary term is also infinitesimal.
Thanks to the boundedness of the area of
\Phi_{k}
, the pointwise bound
Eq.

3.4
and Hölder’s inequality,
we deduce that also the remaining terms in the left-hand side of
Eq.

3.5
are infinitesimal,
except for the first one.

We now estimate
\|w_{k}\|_{\Phi_{k}}
. Note first that
|w_{k}|\leq\|X\|_{L^{\infty}}
and
|\nabla w_{k}|\leq\|\nabla X\|_{L^{\infty}}
. Also, from
Eq.

3.2
we get

\displaystyle|\nabla^{2}w_{k}|\leq\|\nabla^{2}X\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla X\|_{L^{\infty}}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi_{k}}|.

We deduce that
\sigma_{k}\|w_{k}\|_{\Phi_{k}}\to 0
.

Finally,
\big\langle\widehat{I}_{k},\nabla X\circ\widehat{I}_{k}\big\rangle(x)=\operatorname{div}_{(\Phi_{k})_{*}[T_{x}\Sigma]}X
, so that

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\big\langle\widehat{I}_{k},\nabla X\circ\widehat{I}_{k}\big\rangle=\int_{(p,\Pi)\in\operatorname{Gr}_{2}(\mathcal{M})}\operatorname{div}_{\Pi}X\,d\mathbf{v}_{k}(p,\Pi)

and, taking any subsequential limit
\mathbf{v}_{\infty}
, the claim follows.
∎

In order to obtain more information for the asymptotic behavior of the measures

introduced at the end of
Section

2
, we first obtain (various versions of) a lower bound on the mass
\frac{|\mathbf{v}_{k}|(B_{r}(p))}{r^{2}}
. The main idea will be to mimick the proof of the monotonicity formula for stationary varifolds; since that proof uses vector fields in the ambient
\mathcal{M}
, we will be able to use formula
Eq.

3.3
, involving variations of the form
X(\Phi)
.

The statements contained in this section make it essential to require the decays
\sigma_{k}^{4}\log\sigma_{k}^{-1}\int_{\Sigma}f_{k}^{2}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi_{k}}\to 0
,
as well as
\sigma_{k}\log\sigma_{k}^{-1}\operatorname{length}(\Phi_{k}|_{\partial\Sigma})\to 0
,
guaranteed by
Proposition

2.3
.

Rather than dealing with the sequence
(\Phi_{k})
, in this section all the statements concern a general smooth map
\Phi\in\mathfrak{M}
, with a fixed value of
\sigma
. Of course, in order for the results to be useful in the asymptotic analysis, the constants appearing in their statements will depend neither on
\Phi
nor on
\sigma
.

Definition 4.1
.

In the following statements, we say that a smooth map
\Phi\in\mathfrak{M}
is
\varepsilon
-critical
for
E_{\sigma}
if
\|dE_{\sigma}(\Phi)\|_{\Phi}\leq\varepsilon
, meaning that
|dE_{\sigma}(\Phi)[w]|\leq\varepsilon\|w\|_{\Phi}
for all
w\in T_{\Phi}\mathfrak{M}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\Phi
be
\sigma^{5}
-critical for
E_{\sigma}
,
x\in\Sigma
, and denote
p:=\Phi(x)
.
Assume
U\subseteq\Sigma
is an open neighborhood of
x
.
Defining the measures
\mu:=(\Phi|_{U})_{*}(\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi})
and
\lambda:=(\Phi|_{\partial\Sigma\cap U})_{*}(\sigma\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi|_{\partial\Sigma}})+(\Phi|_{U})_{*}(\sigma^{4}f^{2}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi})
on
\mathcal{M}
, assume also that

\displaystyle\lambda(B_{s}(p))\leq\delta\mu(B_{5s}(p))\quad\text{for all radii }s>0,

for some
0<\delta<1
.
Given
r>s\geq\sigma
, if
B_{r}(p)\cap\Phi(\partial U)=\emptyset
then we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(B_{r}(p))}{r^{2}}\geq(c-C\delta\log(r/s))\frac{\mu(B_{s}(p))}{s^{2}}-C\sigma^{2},

for some constants
c,C>0
depending on
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
.

Note that
\partial U
is the topological boundary of
U
in
\Sigma
and therefore does not include
\partial\Sigma\cap U
.
Recall that
f=|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}|^{2}
.

Before delving into the proof, we state without proof an immediate but useful fact.

Proposition 4.3
.

There exists a constant
c_{F}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N})
such that, for every
p\in\mathcal{M}
, there are coordinates

\displaystyle\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{m}):B_{c_{F}}(p)\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

depending on the center
p
, satisfying

(4.1)

\displaystyle g_{ij}(0)=\delta_{ij},\quad\|g_{ij}\|_{C^{2}}\leq C(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N}),\quad\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,p)\leq|\xi|\leq 2\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,p)

for the Euclidean metric
|\cdot|
.
When
p\in\mathcal{N}
we also ask that the coordinates are adapted to
\mathcal{N}
, in the sense that
B_{c_{F}}(p)\cap\mathcal{N}
corresponds to
\{\xi_{n+1}=\cdots=\xi_{m}=0\}
.

Proof of
Proposition

4.2
.

Without loss of generality, we can assume
r,\frac{\sigma}{s}\leq c^{\prime}
for a constant
c^{\prime}<c_{F}
to be chosen later. Once we get the desired estimate with these constraints, the statement follows in general with possibly different values of
c
and
C
.

We will imitate the proof of the monotonicity formula, using now our equation
Eq.

3.3
. Assume first
B_{r}(p)\cap\mathcal{N}=\emptyset
.
In this case we can find coordinates
\xi:B_{r}(p)\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
as in
Proposition

4.3
.
Given a decreasing cut-off function
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{c}([0,\infty))
, with
\chi=1
on
[0,1/4]
and
\chi=0
on
[1/2,\infty)
, for
0<\tau<r
we set

\chi_{\tau}:=\chi(|\xi|/\tau)
and
X_{\tau}:=\chi_{\tau}\xi_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{i}}
.

Note that, by
Eq.

4.1
, we have
|\nabla X_{\tau}|\leq C
,
|\nabla^{2}X_{\tau}|\leq C\tau^{-1}
and

(4.2)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}_{\Pi}(X_{\tau})\geq(2-C\tau)\chi_{\tau}+(1+C\tau)\chi^{\prime}(|\xi|/\tau)\frac{|\xi|}{\tau}

for any
p\in B_{\tau}(q)
and any 2-plane
\Pi\subseteq T_{p}\mathcal{M}
(recall that
\chi^{\prime}\leq 0
).

We now want to apply
Eq.

3.3
with the infinitesimal variation
w:=X_{\tau}(\Phi)\bm{1}_{U}
, which is admissible since

X_{\tau}
vanishes near
\Phi(\partial U)
.
By
Eq.

3.4
we have

\displaystyle\sigma^{4}f\left\langle\nabla n,\nabla\omega\right\rangle\leq C\sigma^{4}(f^{2}+\tau^{-1}f^{3/2})\bm{1}_{\spt(w)};

hence, the corresponding term in the first variation is bounded by

\displaystyle C\lambda(B_{\tau}(p))+C\sigma\tau^{-1}\lambda(B_{\tau}(p))^{3/4}\mu(B_{\tau}(p))^{1/4}\leq C(\delta+\sigma\tau^{-1})\mu(B_{5\tau}(p)).

Similarly, the curvature term in
Eq.

3.3
is bounded by
C\sigma\mu(B_{5\tau}(p))
,
while the last term is again bounded by
C\delta\mu(B_{5\tau}(p))
.
Also, the boundary term vanishes since the support of
w
does not intersect
\partial\Sigma
.

Finally, as in the proof of
3.4
, we have

\displaystyle\|w\|_{\Phi}

\displaystyle\leq\|X_{\tau}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla X_{\tau}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\Big(\int_{\operatorname{spt}(w)}(\|\nabla^{2}X_{\tau}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla X_{\tau}\|_{L^{\infty}}f^{1/2})^{4}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi}\Big)^{1/4}

\displaystyle\leq C+C\tau^{-1}\mu(B_{\tau}(p))^{1/4}+\sigma^{-1}\lambda(B_{\tau}(p))^{1/4}

\displaystyle\leq C+C(\tau^{-4}+\sigma^{-4})\mu(B_{5\tau}(p)),

so that from
Eq.

2.5
we get
|dE_{\sigma}(\Phi)[w]|\leq C\sigma^{5}+C\sigma\mu(B_{5\tau}(p))
for
\tau\geq\sigma
.

Hence, defining
h(\tau):=\tau^{-2}\int_{U}\chi_{\tau}(\Phi)\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi}
,
Eq.

3.3
and a straightforward computation give

(4.3)

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(\tau)\geq-C(\delta+\sigma\tau^{-1})\tau^{-3}\mu(B_{5\tau}(p))-C\tau^{-2}\mu(B_{5\tau}(p))-C\sigma^{2}

for
\tau\geq\sigma
. Call
\overline{r}
the biggest radius in
[s,r]
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(B_{\overline{r}}(p))}{\overline{r}^{2}}\geq\frac{\mu(B_{s}(p))}{s^{2}}.

For
\frac{r}{5}\geq\tau\geq\overline{r}\geq\sigma
,
Eq.

4.3
becomes

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(\tau)\geq-C(\delta\tau^{-1}+\sigma\tau^{-2}+1)\frac{\mu(B_{s}(p))}{s^{2}}-C\sigma^{2}.

Integrating this inequality between
8\overline{r}
and
\frac{r}{5}
we get

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(B_{r/5}(p))}{(r/5)^{2}}\geq h(r/5)\geq h(8\overline{r})-C(\delta\log(r/s)+\sigma s^{-1}+r)\frac{\mu(B_{s}(p))}{s^{2}}-C\sigma^{2}r,

unless
r<40\overline{r}
, in which case the statement follows trivially. Since
r
and
\frac{\sigma}{s}
are both bounded by
c^{\prime}
,
observing that
h(8\overline{r})\geq\frac{\mu(B_{\overline{r}}(p))}{64\overline{r}^{2}}\geq\frac{\mu(B_{s}(p))}{64s^{2}}
we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(B_{r/5}(p))}{(r/5)^{2}}\geq\Big(\frac{1}{64}-C\delta\log(r/s)-2Cc^{\prime}\Big)\frac{\mu(B_{s}(p))}{s^{2}}-C\sigma^{2}r,

and the statement follows in this case, once we impose
2Cc^{\prime}<\frac{1}{64}
.

If
r^{\prime}:=\operatorname{dist}(p,\mathcal{N})<r
, we let
q
be a nearest point to
p
in
\mathcal{N}
(hence,
q=p
when
r^{\prime}=0
).
If
r^{\prime}\geq s
, we know that the claim holds with
r^{\prime}
replacing
r
;
so it follows also for
r
if either
s\geq r/8
or
r^{\prime}\geq r/8
, with possibly different constants. Assume in the sequel that
r^{\prime},s<r/8
.
For
\tau>2r^{\prime}+s
we have

\displaystyle\lambda(B_{\tau}(q))\leq\lambda(B_{2\tau}(p))\leq\delta\mu(B_{10\tau}(p))\leq\delta\mu(B_{20\tau}(q)).

So, using now coordinates centered at
q
and adapted to
\mathcal{N}
and defining
h
as before, we get

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(\tau)\geq-C(\delta\tau^{-1}+\sigma\tau^{-2}+1)\frac{\mu(B_{20\tau}(p))}{\tau^{2}}-C\sigma^{2}

for
\tau\geq 2r^{\prime}+s\geq\sigma
;
note that now also the boundary term in
Eq.

3.3
is taken into account, giving again a contribution
bounded by
C\tau^{-3}\lambda(B_{\tau}(q))\leq C\delta\tau^{-3}\mu(B_{20\tau}(q))
in the previous right-hand side.
Similarly to the above,
assume
2r^{\prime}+s\leq\overline{r}^{\prime}\leq r/2
to be the smallest radius in this interval such that

\frac{\mu(B_{\tau}(q))}{\tau^{2}}\leq\frac{\mu(B_{s}(p))}{s^{2}}
for
\tau\in[\overline{r}^{\prime},r/2]
;
if such radius does not exist, then we have
\frac{\mu(B_{r/2}(q))}{(r/2)^{2}}\geq\frac{\mu(B_{s}(p))}{s^{2}}

and we are done thanks to the inclusion
B_{r}(p)\supseteq B_{r/2}(q)
.
Integrating from
8\overline{r}^{\prime}
to
r/40
(again, we can assume
\overline{r}^{\prime}\leq\frac{r}{320}
), we conclude that either

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(B_{r/40}(q))}{(r/40)^{2}}\geq\Big(\frac{1}{64}-C\delta\log(r/s)-2Cc^{\prime}\Big)\frac{\mu(B_{s}(p))}{s^{2}}-C\sigma^{2},

in which case we are done since
\mu(B_{r}(p))\geq\mu(B_{r/40}(q))
, or

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(B_{r/40}(q))}{(r/40)^{2}}\geq\frac{\mu(B_{2r^{\prime}+s}(q))}{64(2r^{\prime}+s)^{2}}-(C\delta\log(r/s)+2Cc^{\prime})\frac{\mu(B_{s}(p))}{s^{2}}-C\sigma^{2}.

In this second case, if
r^{\prime}<s
then we use the inequality
\frac{\mu(B_{2r^{\prime}+s}(q))}{(2r^{\prime}+s)^{2}}\geq\frac{\mu(B_{s}(p))}{(3s)^{2}}
and we are done.
Otherwise, if
r^{\prime}\geq s
we use the inequality
\frac{\mu(B_{2r^{\prime}+s}(q))}{(2r^{\prime}+s)^{2}}\geq\frac{\mu(B_{r^{\prime}}(p))}{(3r^{\prime})^{2}}

and we conclude using the already obtained lower bound for this last ratio.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

A similar choice of test vector fields gives the following monotonicity for general free boundary stationary varifolds
\mathbf{v}
: given
p
in
\mathcal{M}
, one has

(4.4)

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathbf{v}|(B_{r}(p))}{r^{2}}\geq(1+C(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N})\sqrt{r})^{-1}\frac{|\mathbf{v}|(B_{s}(p))}{s^{2}}

for
0<s<r<\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{M})
if
p\in\mathcal{N}
, and for
0<s<r<\operatorname{dist}(p,\mathcal{N})
otherwise.
Indeed, it suffices to establish
Eq.

4.4
assuming
r
small, and also
s\geq\frac{r}{2}
, since for
s<\frac{r}{2}
we can then compare dyadic radii
r,\frac{r}{2},\dots,2^{-k}r
until
2^{-k-1}r\leq s
.
Pick coordinates as in
Proposition

4.3
, with
|\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,p)-|\xi||\leq C\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,p)^{2}
, and take now
\chi
such that
\chi=1
on
[0,1-2\sqrt{r}]
,
\chi=0
on
[1-\sqrt{r},\infty)
and
|\chi^{\prime}|\leq Cr^{-1/2}
, so that
\chi_{\tau}
is supported in
B_{r}(p)
for
\tau\leq r
. Setting
h(\tau):=\tau^{-2}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\chi_{\tau}\,d|\mathbf{v}|
, the stationarity of
\mathbf{v}
and
Eq.

4.2
then give

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(\tau)\geq-Cr^{-5/2}|\mathbf{v}|(B_{r}(p)),

which, integrating from
s
to
r
, implies

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathbf{v}|(B_{r}(p))}{r^{2}}-\frac{|\mathbf{v}|(B_{(1-C\sqrt{r})s}(p))}{s^{2}}\geq\int_{s}^{r}h^{\prime}(\tau)\,d\tau\geq-Cr^{-3/2}|\mathbf{v}|(B_{r}(p))

and
Eq.

4.4
follows easily. Hence, the density

\displaystyle\theta(\mathbf{v},p):=\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{|\mathbf{v}|(B_{r}(p))}{\pi r^{2}}

exists at any
p\in\mathcal{M}
. It also follows that

(4.5)

\displaystyle|\mathbf{v}|(B_{r}(p))\leq C(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N})|\mathbf{v}|(\mathcal{M})r^{2}

for all
r>0
: this is clear if
p\in\mathcal{N}
, while for
p\not\in\mathcal{N}
and
r\geq\operatorname{dist}(p,\mathcal{N})
we have

B_{r}(p)\subseteq B_{2r}(q)
for some
q\in\mathcal{N}
,
so that
|\mathbf{v}|(B_{r}(p))\leq C|\mathbf{v}|(\mathcal{M})(2r)^{2}
, and
Eq.

4.5
follows also for
r<\operatorname{dist}(p,\mathcal{N})
thanks to
Eq.

4.4
again. In the same way, using the inclusions
B_{s}(p)\supseteq B_{s-d}(q)
and
B_{2d}(q)\supseteq B_{d}(p)
, with
d:=\operatorname{dist}(p,\mathcal{N})
and
q\in\mathcal{N}
a nearest point to
p
, we deduce that
|\mathbf{v}|(B_{s}(p))\geq cs^{2}\theta(\mathbf{v},p)
holds even for
3d<s<\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{M})
.
Thus,

(4.6)

\displaystyle|\mathbf{v}|(B_{r}(p))\geq c(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N})\theta(\mathbf{v},p)r^{2}

for all
p\in\mathcal{M}
and all
0<r<\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{M})
.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
\Phi
be a
\sigma^{5}
-critical point for
E_{\sigma}
, let
\delta>0
,
and let
U\subseteq\Sigma
be an open set which intersects
\partial\Sigma
but does not contain entirely any boundary component of
\Sigma
.
Denote
S_{\delta}
the set of points
p\in\mathcal{M}\setminus\Phi(\partial U)
satisfying the maximal bound

\displaystyle\lambda(B_{s}(p))\leq\delta\mu(B_{5s}(p))\quad\text{for all radii }s>0.

Let
T
be a Borel set of points having distance less than
\sigma
from
\Phi(\partial\Sigma\cap U)
, and such that their distance from
\Phi(\partial U)
is at least
5\sigma
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\mu(S_{\delta}\cap T)\leq C\sigma\operatorname{length}(\Phi|_{\partial\Sigma\cap U})\frac{\mu(\mathcal{M})}{\operatorname{dist}(T,\Phi(\partial U))^{2}}+C\sigma^{3}\operatorname{length}(\Phi|_{\partial\Sigma\cap U}),

for some
C
depending on
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
, provided
\delta\log(1/\sigma)
is small enough.

Proof.

Let
L:=\operatorname{length}(\Phi|_{\partial\Sigma\cap U})
. We first note that the set of points
T^{\prime}
in
\Phi(\partial\Sigma\cap U)
with distance less than
\sigma
from
T
can be covered with at most
\sigma^{-1}L
balls
B_{\sigma}(p_{j})
, with
\operatorname{dist}(p_{j},\Phi(\partial U))\geq 4\sigma
. Indeed, note first that
\operatorname{dist}(T^{\prime},\Phi(\partial U))\geq 4\sigma
; we can discard the components of
\partial\Sigma\cap U
producing an arc of length less than
2\sigma
, since this arc is disjoint from
T^{\prime}
;
we are left with finitely many components, corresponding to curves
\gamma_{i}:I_{i}\to\mathcal{N}
with endpoints in
\Phi(\partial U)
, where
I_{i}=(0,|I_{i}|)
is an open interval;
assuming each of them to be parametrized by arclength,
we then subdivide
[\sigma,|I_{i}|-\sigma]
into at most
\sigma^{-1}|I_{i}|
intervals
I_{i\ell}
of size less than
\sigma

and we pick a point
p_{i\ell}
in
\gamma_{i}(I_{i\ell})\cap T^{\prime}
, discarding the intervals for which this intersection is empty.
The resulting collection of balls
\{B_{\sigma}(p_{i\ell})\}
is the desired one.

Hence,
T
is covered by a collection of balls
\{B_{2\sigma}(p_{j})\mid j\in J\}
, with
|J|\leq\sigma^{-1}L

and
\operatorname{dist}(p_{j},\Phi(\partial U))\geq 4\sigma
.

Now let
J^{\prime}\subseteq J
denote the set of indices
j
such that
B_{2\sigma}(p_{j})
intersects
S_{\delta}
and, for
j\in J^{\prime}
, choose a point
q_{j}\in S_{\delta}\cap B_{2\sigma}(p_{j})
. Then we have

\displaystyle T\cap S_{\delta}\subseteq\bigcup_{j\in J^{\prime}}B_{4\sigma}(q_{j}).

Note that
\operatorname{dist}(q_{j},\Phi(\partial U))\geq\operatorname{dist}(T,\Phi(\partial U))-3\sigma
,
which is comparable with
\operatorname{dist}(T,\Phi(\partial U))
,
so that
Proposition

4.2
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(\mathcal{M})}{\operatorname{dist}(T,\Phi(\partial U))^{2}}\geq(c-C\delta\log(1/\sigma))\frac{\mu(B_{4\sigma}(q_{j}))}{(4\sigma)^{2}}-C\sigma^{2}

for constants
c,C
depending solely on
\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N}
.
Summing over
j\in J^{\prime}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mu(T)\leq\sum_{j\in J^{\prime}}\mu(B_{4\sigma}(q_{j}))\leq\sigma^{-1}L\frac{C}{1-C\delta\log(1/\sigma)}\Big(\sigma^{2}\frac{\mu(\mathcal{M})}{\operatorname{dist}(T,\Phi(\partial U))^{2}}+\sigma^{4}\Big)

and the statement follows.
∎

Corollary 4.6
.

Under the same assumptions as in
Proposition

4.2
, if
B_{\sigma}(p)\cap\mathcal{N}=\emptyset
then

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(B_{r}(p))}{r^{2}}\geq c-C\delta\log(r/\sigma)-C\sigma^{2},

provided
\delta
and
\sigma
are small enough.

Proof.

We first claim that

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mu(B_{\sigma}(p))>c^{\prime}\sigma^{2}

for some universal
c^{\prime}>0
.

The second fundamental form of the immersed surface
\Phi
in
\mathbb{R}^{Q}
is bounded by
|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi}|+C(\mathcal{M})
, so the monotonicity formula in the ball
\widetilde{B}_{t}(p):=B_{t}^{\mathbb{R}^{Q}}(p)
(see, e.g.,
[
41
, eq. (17.4)]
, whose proof carries over to the setting of immersed surfaces) and Hölder’s inequality give

(4.8)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\mu(\widetilde{B}_{t}(p))}{t^{2}}-\frac{\mu(\widetilde{B}_{t/2}(p))}{(t/2)^{2}}&\geq-Ct^{-1}(\sigma^{-1}\lambda(\widetilde{B}_{t}(p))^{1/4}\mu(\widetilde{B}_{t}(p))^{3/4}+\mu(\widetilde{B}_{t}(p)))\\
&\geq-Ct^{-1}(\sigma^{-1}\delta^{1/4}+1)\mu(\widetilde{B}_{20t}(p))\end{aligned}

for
t\leq\sigma
small enough.
Let
\overline{t}\leq\frac{\sigma}{2}
be the biggest radius such that
\mu(\widetilde{B}_{\overline{t}}(p))\geq\frac{\pi}{2}\overline{t}^{2}
;
note that
\overline{t}
exists since
\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\mu(\widetilde{B}_{t}(p))}{\pi t^{2}}\geq 1
.
If
\overline{t}\geq\frac{\sigma}{80}
then we are done, thanks to the inclusion
B_{2\overline{t}}(p)\supseteq\mathcal{M}\cap\widetilde{B}_{\overline{t}}(p)
. Otherwise,
Eq.

4.8
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(\widetilde{B}_{t}(p))}{t^{2}}-\frac{\mu(\widetilde{B}_{t/2}(p))}{(t/2)^{2}}\geq-Ct(\sigma^{-1}\delta^{1/4}+1)

for
\overline{t}\leq t\leq\frac{\sigma}{40}
. Setting
t:=2^{-k}(\sigma/40)
in the last inequality and summing on
k=0,\dots,k_{0}-1
,
where
k_{0}
is the biggest integer such that
t\geq\overline{t}
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(\widetilde{B}_{\sigma/40}(p))}{(\sigma/40)^{2}}\geq\frac{\mu(\widetilde{B}_{\overline{t}}(p))}{4\overline{t}^{2}}-C\delta^{1/4}-C\sigma

and claim
Eq.

4.7
follows again, for
\delta
and
\sigma
small enough.

The statement now follows by applying
Proposition

4.2
with
s:=\sigma
.
∎

5.
Asymptotic behavior of the area, in
\Sigma
and in
\mathcal{M}

We now investigate the asymptotic behavior of the maps
\Phi_{k}
introduced in
Section

2
.
Recall that
\nu_{k}
is the area measure of
\Phi_{k}
on
\Sigma
, meaning that
\nu_{k}(U)
is the area of the immersion
\Phi_{k}|_{U}
for any open set
U\subseteq\Sigma
. Also, let
\mu_{k}:=(\Phi_{k})_{*}\nu_{k}
be the corresponding measure on
\mathcal{M}
, and recall that
\mathbf{v}_{k}
is the
2
-varifold induced by
\Phi_{k}
, namely
\mathbf{v}_{k}:=(\Phi_{k})_{*}(\Sigma)
, the varifold pushforward of the canonical multiplicity one
2
-varifold on
\Sigma

Up to subsequences, we can assume that
\mu_{k}
,
\nu_{k}
and
\mathbf{v}_{k}
converge weakly to limits
\mu_{\infty}
,
\nu_{\infty}
and
\mathbf{v}_{\infty}

In this section we show structure theorems for the limit measures
\nu_{\infty}
,
\mu_{\infty}
and for the limit varifold
\mathbf{v}_{\infty}
, namely
5.2
,
5.3
and
5.11
. The regularity of
\mathbf{v}_{\infty}
will be studied in
Section

7
.

We will assume for simplicity that the maps
\Phi_{k}
induce the same conformal structure on
\Sigma
; we will discuss the general case later, in
Section

6
.

Given a reference metric
g_{0}
(on
\Sigma
) compatible with this structure,
\operatorname{vol}_{g_{0}}
will denote either the corresponding volume form or the associated measure.

Note that
\nu_{k}=\frac{1}{2}|d\Phi_{k}|_{g_{0}}^{2}\,\operatorname{vol}_{g_{0}}
. Hence, viewing
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{Q}
, the maps
\Phi_{k}
are bounded in
W^{1,2}(\Sigma,\mathbb{R}^{Q})
and, up to subsequences, we can extract a weak limit
\Phi_{\infty}
. Note that we have the strong convergence in
L^{2}
for the maps
\Phi_{k}\to\Phi_{\infty}
and the traces
\Phi_{k}|_{\partial\Sigma}\to\Phi_{\infty}|_{\partial\Sigma}
; hence,
\Phi_{\infty}
and its trace
\Phi_{\infty}|_{\partial\Sigma}
take values into
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
, respectively.

Proposition 5.1
.

Given
x\in\Sigma
, fix a local conformal chart centered at
x
such that
the chart domain corresponds to
U^{\prime}:=B_{1}^{2}
if
x\not\in\partial\Sigma
, or to
U^{\prime}:=B_{1}^{2}\cap\{\Im(z)\geq 0\}

if
x\in\partial\Sigma

Theorem 5.2

\displaystyle|(\nabla^{\mathcal{M}_{k}})^{2}Y_{k}|\leq C\|\rho_{\tau_{k}}*X\|_{C^{2}}\leq C\tau_{k}^{-1}\|dX\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Note that
\Psi_{k}

with the same formula

\tau_{k}^{5}
-critical for
E_{\tau_{k}}
for the manifold
\mathcal{M}_{k}
and the corresponding Finsler manifold

:
indeed, identifying
T_{\Phi_{k}}\mathfrak{M}
and
T_{\Psi_{k}}\mathfrak{M}_{k}
with subsets of

, for all

\displaystyle|dE_{\tau_{k}}(\Psi_{k})[w]|

\displaystyle=r_{k}^{-2}|dE_{\sigma_{k}}(\Phi_{k})[r_{k}w]|\leq r_{k}^{-1}\sigma_{k}^{5}\|w\|_{\Phi_{k}}\leq r_{k}^{2}\tau_{k}^{5}\|w\|_{\Phi_{k}}

and it is immediate to check that

(assuming

For the vector field
Y_{k}
, recalling
Eq.

3.4
, the term

in

3.3

is bounded by

\displaystyle C|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Psi_{k}}|^{4}\|\nabla^{\mathcal{M}_{k}}Y_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}}+C|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Psi_{k}}|^{3}\|(\nabla^{\mathcal{M}_{k}})^{2}Y_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq C(|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Psi_{k}}|^{4}+\tau_{k}^{-1}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Psi_{k}}|^{3})\|dX\|_{L^{\infty}}.

We now use the almost criticality of
\Psi_{k}
with the infinitesimal variation
Y_{k}(\Psi_{k})
, or more precisely

Y_{k}(\Psi_{k})\bm{1}_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}}
for the extension
r_{k}^{-1}(\Phi_{k}-p)\in\mathfrak{M}_{k}
of
\Psi_{k}
.
For
k
large enough,

does not intersect

,
where

is supported, and hence this is an admissible variation.
As in the proof of

, since the immersions

have bounded area we obtain

\displaystyle\|Y_{k}(\Psi_{k})\|_{\Psi_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|dX\|_{L^{\infty}}+C\|(\nabla^{\mathcal{M}_{k}})^{2}Y_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|dX\|\Big(\int_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Psi_{k}}|^{4}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Psi_{k}}\Big)^{1/4}

\displaystyle\leq C\tau_{k}^{-1}\|dX\|_{L^{\infty}}\Big(1+\Big(\int_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}}\tau_{k}^{4}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Psi_{k}}|^{4}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Psi_{k}}\Big)^{1/4}\Big)

for some

independent of
X
.
Hence,

3.3
and the

-criticality of

, together with Young’s inequality, give

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}}\left\langle\partial_{i}\Psi_{k},\nabla Y_{k}(\Psi_{k})[\partial_{i}\Psi_{k}]\right\rangle\Big|

\displaystyle\leq C\|dX\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}}(\tau_{k}^{4}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Psi_{k}}|^{4}+\tau_{k}^{3}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Psi_{k}}|^{3})\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Psi_{k}}

\displaystyle\quad+C\tau_{k}^{4}\|dX\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Since

, Hölder’s inequality gives the upper bound

\displaystyle C\Big(r_{k}^{-2}\sigma_{k}^{4}\int_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi_{k}}|^{4}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi_{k}}+\Big(r_{k}^{-2}\sigma_{k}^{4}\int_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Phi_{k}}|^{4}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi_{k}}\Big)^{3/4}+\tau_{k}^{4}\Big)\|dX\|_{L^{\infty}}

for the previous right-hand side. In view of

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}}\left\langle\partial_{i}\Psi_{k},\nabla Y_{k}(\Psi_{k})[\partial_{i}\Psi_{k}]\right\rangle\Big|\leq\delta_{k}^{\prime}\|dX\|_{L^{\infty}}

for some

independent of

.
Also, replacing

with

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}}\left\langle\partial_{i}\Psi_{k},\nabla Y_{k}(\Psi_{k})[\partial_{i}\Psi_{k}]\right\rangle-\int_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}}\left\langle\partial_{i}\Psi_{k},\nabla X_{k}(\Psi_{k})[\partial_{i}\Psi_{k}]\right\rangle\Big|

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}}|H^{\Psi_{k}}||\rho_{\tau_{k}}*X-X|\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Psi_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq C\tau_{k}\|dX\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}}|\mathrm{I\!I}^{\Psi_{k}}|\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Psi_{k}}

is infinitesimal with respect to
\|dX\|_{L^{\infty}}
.
Choose now

.
Writing

, in view of

5.8

replacing

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}}(\partial_{1}\varphi(\Psi_{k})\partial_{i}\Psi_{k}^{1}\partial_{i}\Psi_{k}^{1}+\partial_{2}\varphi(\Psi_{k})\partial_{i}\Psi_{k}^{1}\partial_{i}\Psi_{k}^{2}),

up to another infinitesimal error.

denote the Jacobian of the composition of
\Psi

Using

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}}J_{k}\partial_{1}\varphi(\Psi_{k})\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Psi_{k}}

plus an error which is infinitesimal with respect to

(see also
[
31
, Lemma A.6]
).
Hence, by the area formula, the projection
\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\prime\prime}
of
\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\prime}
onto

has an integer multiplicity

satisfying

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{B_{\alpha}^{2}}N_{k}\partial_{1}\varphi\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\Big|\leq\delta_{k}^{\prime\prime}\|dX\|_{L^{\infty}}\quad\text{with }\delta_{k}^{\prime\prime}\to 0

and, using the vector field

with

So, by Allard’s strong constancy lemma
[

,
it follows that

Finally, since

converges to

we have

\displaystyle\pi(\alpha/2)^{2}N=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{B_{\alpha/2}^{2}}N_{k}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\pi(\alpha/2)^{2}\overline{N}_{k}

Proposition 5.6
.

Given an open subset
\omega\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset\widetilde{\Sigma}
with
\nu_{\infty}(\partial\omega)=0
, the immersions
\Phi_{k}|_{\omega}
converge to the varifold

Proof.

By splitting
\omega
into finitely many pieces with
\nu_{\infty}
-negligible boundary, we can reduce to the case that
\omega
is contained in a local chart; in the sequel, we identify
\omega
with a subset of

Let
\mathbf{v}_{k,\omega}:=(\Phi_{k})_{*}(\omega)
be the varifold induced by
\Phi_{k}|_{\omega}
. Viewing
\mathbf{v}_{k,\omega}
(for
k\leq\infty
) as a varifold in
\mathbb{R}^{Q}
, by the area formula and
5.3
it suffices to show that

(5.9)

\displaystyle\int_{\omega}\varphi(\Phi_{k}(x),d\Phi_{k}(x)[T_{x}\Sigma])\,d\nu_{k}(x)\to\int_{\omega}\varphi(\Phi_{\infty}(x),d\Phi_{\infty}(x)[T_{x}\Sigma])\,d\nu_{\infty}(x)

for any
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}(\operatorname{Gr}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{Q}))
.
The last integrand is meant to be zero at points where

does not have full rank.
In order to simplify the notation, we indicate
the plane

\Pi_{k}(x)

Let
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}^{\prime}
be the subset of
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
consisting of the points

\int_{B_{r}^{2}(x)}|d\Phi_{\infty}-d\Phi_{\infty}(x)|^{2}=o(r^{2})

For any point

,
pick a sequence of radii

\displaystyle|\Phi_{\infty}(x+ry)-\Phi_{\infty}(x)-d\Phi_{\infty}(x)[ry]|=o(r)\quad\text{for }|y|=1.

Given any
\varepsilon>0
, we claim that

(5.10)

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\int_{B_{r}^{2}(x)}|\varphi(\Phi_{k},\Pi_{k})-\varphi(\Phi_{\infty}(x),\Pi_{\infty}(x))|\,d\nu_{k}\leq\varepsilon r^{2}

If this is not true, then using a diagonal argument as in the proof of
5.3

(5.11)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}(x)}|\varphi(\Phi_{k},\Pi_{k})-\varphi(\Phi_{\infty}(x),\Pi_{\infty}(x))|\,d\nu_{k}\geq\varepsilon r_{k}^{2}

as well as

\displaystyle r_{k}^{-2}|\nu_{k}(B_{r_{k}}^{2}(x))-\nu_{\infty}(B_{r_{k}}^{2}(x))|\to 0,

and such that the varifolds induced by
\Psi_{k}:=r_{k}^{-1}(\Phi_{k}|_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}(x)}-\Phi_{\infty}(x))

converge tightly to a rectifiable varifold
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}
supported in a bounded subset of
\Pi_{\infty}(x)
.
In particular, the
\nu_{k}
-measure of the set of points in

is

.
Since
\Phi_{k}=\Phi_{\infty}(x)+r_{k}\Psi_{k}
, we deduce that

(5.12)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}(x)}|\varphi(\Phi_{k},\Pi_{k})-\varphi(\Phi_{\infty}(x),\Pi_{k})|\,d\nu_{k}\leq r_{k}^{1/2}\|d\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\nu_{k}(B_{r_{k}}^{2}(x))+o(r_{k}^{2})=o(r_{k}^{2})

(5.13)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}(x)}|\varphi(\Phi_{\infty}(x),\Pi_{k})-\varphi(\Phi_{\infty}(x),\Pi_{\infty}(x))|\,d\nu_{k}=o(r_{k}^{2}).

Combining

5.12

5.11

By the Besicovitch covering lemma, we can then cover any fixed compact set

\displaystyle\int_{B_{j}}|\varphi(\Phi_{\infty},\Pi_{\infty})-\varphi(\Phi_{\infty}(x_{j}),\Pi_{\infty}(x_{j}))|\,d\nu_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon r_{j}^{2},

Theorem 5.11
.

There exists a compact Riemann surface
\Sigma^{\prime}
and a quasiconformal homeomorphism
\varphi:\Sigma^{\prime}\to\Sigma

such that
(\Sigma^{\prime},\Phi_{\infty}\circ\varphi,N\circ\varphi)
is a free boundary parametrized stationary varifold for
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N})
.

We refer the reader to
[
20

for basic properties of quasiconformal homeomorphisms.

Proof.

For a.e.
\omega\subseteq\Sigma
,
\Phi_{k}|_{\partial\omega}
converges in

to
\Phi_{\infty}|_{\partial\omega}
(up to subsequences) and

, with

the finite set of atoms for
\nu_{\infty}
.

With respect to the fixed metric

on
\Sigma
,
we can find an arbitrarily small radius

we have

to

(up to subsequences).
Let

Repeating the proof of
3.4

with vector fields in
\mathcal{X}_{fb}
supported outside
\Phi_{\infty}(\partial\widetilde{\omega})
, we deduce that the varifold limit of

is free boundary stationary outside this set;
by
Proposition

5.6
, this limit is
\mathbf{v}_{\widetilde{\omega}}
.
Since the images
\Phi_{\infty}(\partial B_{r}(x))
, for

, have arbitrarily small diameter (see, e.g.,
[
31
, Lemma A.3]
), we deduce that

\mathbf{v}_{\omega}
is free boundary stationary outside (
\Phi_{\infty}(\partial\omega)
and) a finite set

also converges to the free boundary stationary varifold

,

4.5
we get
|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}|(B_{s}(p))\leq Cs^{2}

.
Hence, given

\Phi_{\infty}(\partial\omega)
, we can multiply it by the product

of cut-off functions

, with

on

,

outside

, as

.
The proof that
\mathbf{v}_{\omega}
is stationary for a.e.
\omega\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset\operatorname{int}(\Sigma)
is analogous.

Finally, we show how to obtain a weakly conformal reparametrization. Note that, by
5.3
,

\displaystyle N|\partial_{1}\Phi_{\infty}\wedge\partial_{2}\Phi_{\infty}|\geq\frac{1}{2}|d\Phi_{\infty}|^{2}

a.e. in a local conformal chart

(with

), since the left-hand side is the density of
\nu_{\infty}
(in
U^{\prime}
) and thus, for any open set

,

\displaystyle\int_{V}\frac{1}{2}|d\Phi_{\infty}|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\nu_{k}(V)\leq\nu_{\infty}(\overline{V})=\int_{\overline{V}}N|\partial_{1}\Phi_{\infty}\wedge\partial_{2}\Phi_{\infty}|\,d\mathcal{L}^{2},

from which the previous claim follows. Call
\overline{C}
an upper bound for

and assume that the image

of the chart is either the ball or the half-ball. Arguing as in the first part of the proof of
[
31
, Theorem 4.1]
, we can find a
\frac{\overline{C}^{2}-1}{\overline{C}^{2}+1}
-quasiconformal homeomorphism
\psi:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}

such that
\Phi_{\infty}\circ h^{-1}\circ\psi^{-1}
is weakly conformal on
\psi(U^{\prime})
;
using the Riemann mapping theorem and Carathéodory’s theorem, by composing
\psi
with a conformal map, we can replace
\psi
with a homeomorphism
U^{\prime}\to U^{\prime}
, with the additional property that it preserves
U^{\prime}\cap\{\Im(z)=0\}
(as a set) in the half-ball case.
Recall that
\psi^{-1}
is quasiconformal, as well (see, e.g.,
[
20
, Theorem 4.10 and Proposition 4.2]
).

Set
\theta:=h^{-1}\circ\psi^{-1}:U^{\prime}\to U
. Note that, given two overlapping charts
U_{1},U_{2}
,
the corresponding homeomorphisms
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
differ by a conformal map, namely

\theta_{1}^{-1}\circ\theta_{2}
is conformal on
\theta_{2}^{-1}(U_{1}\cap U_{2})
. This holds since a.e. the differential

either vanishes or has rank
2
and, by construction,
\theta_{i}
is weakly conformal at a.e.
x_{i}
such that

; on the other hand, the two maps

,
with
x_{1}:=\theta_{1}^{-1}\circ\theta_{2}(x_{2})
, are both nontrivial linear conformal maps for a.e.
x_{2}
such that

, so that

is conformal at these points.

In this argument we used the facts that a quasiconformal homeomorphism carries negligible sets to negligible sets
[
20
, Lemma 4.12]
and satisfies the chain rule
[
23
, Lemma III.6.4]
.

Note that the Cauchy–Riemann equations satisfied by
\theta_{1}^{-1}\circ\theta_{2}
give its smoothness away from the boundary and, by the Schwarz reflection principle, this map is smooth up to
\theta_{2}^{-1}(\partial\Sigma\cap U_{1}\cap U_{2})
. Thus, the maps
\theta^{-1}
define an atlas for a new smooth and conformal structure on
\Sigma
;
calling
\Sigma^{\prime}
a copy of
\Sigma
with this structure, we can just take
\varphi
to be the identity
\Sigma^{\prime}\to\Sigma
.

Finally, as explained in
Proposition

7.3
(whose proof does not use that
\Phi
is weakly conformal), the stationarity property holds on
\Sigma
for all domains; the same then holds for
\Sigma^{\prime}
.
∎

6.
Degeneration of the conformal structure and bubbling

In this section we describe how to recover all the area in the limit as a sum of masses of parametrized (free boundary) stationary varifolds, without the assumption that the maps
\Phi_{k}
induce a fixed conformal structure on
\Sigma
.

Namely, denoting
\mathbf{v}_{k}

Before dealing with possible concentration points, we focus on how to remove the assumption of the fixed conformal structure.

First of all, recall that on the oriented surface

We also recall that

6.1.
Flat case

We now treat the torus case in detail, deferring the other cases to a later discussion.

If
\Sigma_{k}=\mathbb{C}/(\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\lambda_{k})
, setting
\ell_{k}:=|\lambda_{k}|\geq 1
, we can also assume that
\ell_{k}\to\ell_{\infty}\in[1,\infty]
up to a subsequence.
If
\ell_{\infty}<\infty
, assuming
\lambda_{k}\to\lambda_{\infty}
and defining
\Sigma_{\infty}:=\mathbb{C}/(\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\lambda_{\infty})
, we can find diffeomorphisms
\varphi_{k}:\Sigma_{\infty}\to\Sigma
such that
the pullback of the conformal structure
[g_{\Phi_{k}}]
converges smoothly to the flat one.

Since the area of
\Phi_{k}
is bounded,
the sequence
\Phi_{k}\circ\varphi_{k}
is then bounded in
W^{1,2}(\Sigma_{\infty})
and we can extract a subsequence converging to a weak limit
\Phi_{\infty}
. Defining the area measure
\nu_{k}
on
\Sigma_{\infty}
as in the previous section, note that again their limit in the sense of Radon measures (up to subsequences) is also equal to the limit of
\frac{1}{2}|d\Phi_{k}|^{2}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma_{\infty}}
.

All the proofs in
Section

5
carry over, just replacing
\Phi_{k}
with
\Phi_{k}\circ\varphi_{k}
and
(\Sigma,g_{0})
with
(\Sigma_{\infty},g_{\Sigma_{\infty}})
. Assume in the sequel
\ell_{k}\to\ell_{\infty}=\infty
.

Remark 6.2
.

Actually, in the proof of
5.3
we used the conformality of the maps
\Phi_{k}
; since the proof was local in
\operatorname{int}(\Sigma)
, we can precompose
\Phi_{k}
with a conformal map
h_{k}:B_{1}^{2}\to(\Sigma,g_{k})
which is a diffeomorphism with the image and converges smoothly to the inverse of a conformal chart for
\Sigma=\Sigma_{\infty}
.
The statement for the sequence
(\Phi_{k})
then follows from its validity for the conformal maps
\Phi_{k}\circ h_{k}
.

Note that, since
|\Re(\lambda_{k})|\leq\frac{1}{2}
, we can use instead
S^{1}\times\ell_{k}S^{1}
as a domain for
\Phi_{k}
,
with the induced conformal structure becoming asymptotically the flat one.
Given a big parameter

, we can subdivide the circle
\ell_{k}S^{1}
into

with

gives

\displaystyle\int_{S^{1}\times\ell_{k}S^{1}}|d\Phi_{k}|^{2}\leq C

for some constant

independent of

Hence, for each

, there is only a bounded amount of indices

such that
\frac{1}{2}\int_{S^{1}\times I_{k,j}}|d\Phi_{k}|^{2}\geq\frac{c_{Q}}{8}
, for the constant
c_{Q}
from
Proposition

6.1
. Up to subsequences, we can then find a nonempty collection of arcs
J_{k,1},\dots,J_{k,h}

which are unions of the previous intervals, in such a way that

\displaystyle L<\lim_{k\to\infty}|J_{k,j}|<\infty,\quad\operatorname{dist}(J_{k,j},J_{k,j^{\prime}})\to\infty\text{ for }j\neq j^{\prime}

and
\frac{1}{2}\int_{S^{1}\times I_{k,j}}|d\Phi_{k}|^{2}<\frac{c_{Q}}{8}
whenever
I_{k,j}
is not included in one of the arcs
J_{k,1},\dots,J_{k,h}
. We now claim that

(6.1)

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\int_{S^{1}\times(\ell_{k}S^{1}\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{h}RJ_{k,j})}|d\Phi_{k}|^{2}\to 0\quad\text{as }R\to\infty,

provided
L
was chosen big enough. Here

is the arc dilated by a factor
R
, with the same center.

Once this is proved, we can fix
j\in\{1,\dots,h\}
and, shifting
J_{k,j}
to be centered at
0
, we obtain
a (local) weak limit
\Phi_{\infty,j}:S^{1}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{M}
of the maps
\Phi_{k}
, viewing these as maps defined on bigger and bigger subsets of
S^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
. We can again repeat the analysis which was done in the previous section.

Note that in the limit we get a map with domain
S^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
. Since this cylinder is conformally the same as the sphere minus two points, we can see the domain as the sphere: note that replacing the cylinder with the sphere preserves stationarity, by the same argument used in the proof of
5.11
to remove the set of atoms.

By
Eq.

6.1
, the sum of the masses of the limit varifolds for

is equal to the limit of the area of
\Phi_{k}
, up to the contribution of concentration points in the
h
copies of
S^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
. We will discuss later how to recover the area which gets concentrated at these points.

In order to prove
Eq.

6.1
, fix
k
and
j
, and let
I_{k,s},\dots,I_{k,s+t}
be the intervals lying between two consecutive arcs
J_{k,j}
and
J_{k,j+1}
(with indices modulo
N_{k}
and modulo

).
We claim that eventually we cannot have
\sum_{i=2}^{t^{\prime}}\int_{S^{1}\times I_{k,s+i}}\frac{1}{2}|d\Phi_{k}|^{2}\geq\frac{c_{Q}}{2}

for any
1<t^{\prime}<t
. If
t^{\prime}
is the minimum such index, since the energy carried by each
S^{1}\times I_{k,s+i}
is at most
\frac{c_{Q}}{8}
we deduce that the sum is less than
\frac{5}{8}c_{0}
.

Since
|I_{k,i}|\geq L
, we can select
a\in I_{k,s+1}
and

such that
\int_{S^{1}\times\{a,b\}}|d\Phi_{k}|\leq CL^{-1/2}
;
we can apply
Proposition

6.1
with
U_{k}:=S^{1}\times[a,b]
and deduce that eventually
\int_{S^{1}\times[a,b]}\frac{1}{2}|d\Phi_{k}|^{2}
is either at least
\frac{7}{8}c_{Q}
or at most
2\delta(CL^{-1/2},C)
. Since the first possibility cannot happen, we are in the second case.
Hence, we get
\frac{c_{Q}}{2}\leq 2\delta(CL^{-1/2},C)
, which is a contradiction for
L
big enough, since

\delta(CL^{-1/2},C)\to 0
as
L\to\infty
.

But then we can repeat the argument selecting
a^{\prime}
in the part of
RJ_{k,j}\setminus(R/2)J_{k,j}
following
J_{k,j}

and
b^{\prime}
in the part of
RJ_{k,j+1}\setminus(R/2)J_{k,j+1}
preceding
J_{k,j+1}
, with

\int_{S^{1}\times\{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\}}|d\Phi_{k}|\leq CR^{-1/2}
. We already know that the area carried by the region

is eventually less than

, so we deduce that it is bounded by

, and

6.1
follows.

In the case of the annulus, namely
\Sigma_{k}=S^{1}\times[0,\ell_{k}]
, up to subsequences either we are in the easy case that
\ell_{k}
has a limit in
(0,\infty)
,
or
\ell_{k}\to\infty
, or
\ell_{k}\to 0
.
The second case can be dealt with in the same way as before, by subdividing the interval
[0,\ell_{k}]
and making sure that
I_{k,1}\subseteq J_{k,1}
and
I_{k,N_{k}}\subseteq J_{k,h}
.
In this case,

produces again infinite cylinders, or equivalently spheres, in the limit for

On the other hand,

and

In the last case

In the limit we get maps with domain

, which is conformally a disk (minus two boundary points which can be ignored).

6.2.
Hyperbolic case

Finally, we explain how to deal with the hyperbolic case
\chi(\Sigma)<0
. In this case there is no straightforward description of all the possible conformal classes of surfaces.
In case
\Sigma
has no boundary, by Bers’ theorem we can decompose
(\Sigma,g_{k})
into hyperbolic pairs of pants, with lengths of their boundaries bounded above in terms of the topology of
\Sigma
: see
[
18
, Theorem IV.3.7]
for a self-contained proof.
We call
\{\beta_{i}\}
the collection of closed geodesics, depending on
k
but with fixed cardinality, which bound the pairs of pants.
Up to subsequences, we can assume that the combinatorial configuration of the decomposition does not depend on
k
, with a consistent labeling for the curves
\beta_{i}
, and that the length of
\beta_{i}
converges to a finite number as
k\to\infty
.

Then we can apply
[
18
, Proposition IV.5.1]
to the connected components of the surface
(\Sigma,g_{k})
, cut open along those geodesics
\{\beta_{i}\}_{i\in I}
whose length converges to
0
. We get a possibly disconnected limit surface
\Sigma_{\infty}
, which equals a closed Riemann surface minus finitely many points (two for each degenerating
\beta_{i}
), and diffeomorphisms
\psi_{k}:\Sigma_{\infty}\to\Sigma\setminus\bigcup_{i\in I}\beta_{i}

such that the pullback metric
\psi_{k}^{*}g_{k}
converges locally to the metric of
\Sigma_{\infty}
.
Then we repeat the analysis with the maps
\Phi_{k}\circ\psi_{k}
and obtain a limit parametrized varifold, whose domain
\Sigma_{\infty}
can be replaced with a (possibly disconnected) closed surface. Apart from concentration points, part of the area of
\Phi_{k}
could be concentrating in collar neighborhoods of the geodesics
\beta_{i}
, for
i\in I
. These neighborhoods can be conformally identified with cylinders
S^{1}\times[0,L_{k,i}]
, with
L_{k,i}\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
, and one can recover the missing part of the area as in the degenerating cylinder case; note that the pieces
S^{1}\times J_{k,1}
and
S^{1}\times J_{k,h}
from that analysis have to be discarded, since their contribution is already given by
\Sigma_{\infty}
, while all the other pieces produce varifolds parametrized by spheres.

If
\partial\Sigma\neq\emptyset
, let us call
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{b}
the boundary components of
\Sigma
.
We cannot directly decompose
\Sigma
into pairs of pants whose boundary curves have bounded length, since the length of some
\gamma_{i}
with respect to
g_{k}
could fail to stay bounded as
k\to\infty
.

Instead, we first glue two copies of
(\Sigma,g_{k})
along the geodesic boundary
\partial\Sigma=\bigcup_{i=1}^{b}\gamma_{i}
, obtaining a hyperbolic surface
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{k}
. This surface comes equipped with a canonical involution
i_{k}
, which flips the two glued copies.

For a decomposition for
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{k}
as in the closed case, we can assume that all the simple closed geodesics of length less than
2\sinh^{-1}(1)
appear in the collection
\{\beta_{i}\}
: see
[
18
, Lemma IV.4.1]
and the proof of Bers’ theorem.

The thin part
T_{k}:=\{x\in\widetilde{\Sigma}_{k}:\operatorname{inj}(x)\leq\lambda\}
is invariant under
i_{k}
, since
i_{k}
is an isometry.
For
\lambda
small enough, it consists of finitely many disjoint annuli containing a (simple closed) geodesic of length at most
2\lambda
, which is then in
\{\beta_{i}\}
: see the proof of
[
18
, Proposition IV.4.2]
, which also shows that the curves
\beta_{j}
with length bigger than
2\lambda
are disjoint from
T_{k}
. Hence, choosing
\lambda
small enough, we can assume
\beta_{j}\cap T_{k}=\emptyset
for the indices
j\not\in I
corresponding to non-degenerating curves.

The boundary of
T_{k}
has a constant geodesic curvature
\kappa=\kappa(\lambda)
.
Let
S_{k}:=\widetilde{\Sigma}_{k}\setminus\operatorname{int}(T_{k})
.
Taking a limit
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty}
as in the previous discussion, the proof of
[
18
, Proposition IV.5.1]
shows that we can assume
\psi_{k}^{-1}(S_{k})
to be a constant domain
S_{\infty}
, whose complement is the union of finitely many cusps
\{C_{j}\}_{j\in J}
. Namely, each
\overline{C}_{j}
is isometric to the quotient of
\{\Im(z)\geq\Lambda\}\subset\mathbb{H}
by the standard parabolic isometry
z\mapsto z+1
, for some
\Lambda>0
depending on
\lambda
.

The maps
\psi_{k}^{-1}\circ i_{k}\circ\psi_{k}
converge locally smoothly to an isometry
i_{\infty}:\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty}\to\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty}
, since
i_{k}
is an isometry for
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{k}
.
The components of
\partial T_{k}
meeting
\partial\Sigma\subset\widetilde{\Sigma}_{k}
are necessarily invariant sets for
i_{k}
, so that
\partial\Sigma
meets
\partial T_{k}
orthogonally on
\partial\Sigma\cap\partial T_{k}
. Also, we have a lower bound on the injectivity radius on
S_{k}
; this implies that a shortest path
\alpha
joining a point in
S_{k}\cap\gamma_{i}
to another curve
\gamma_{i^{\prime}}
has length bounded below by
\lambda
, since the geodesic
i_{k}\circ\alpha
has the same endpoints; similarly, a shortest path between two close points in
S_{k}\cap\gamma_{i}
must be
\gamma_{i}
itself. Also, the length of a geodesic
\gamma_{i}
intersecting
S_{k}
cannot be smaller than
2\lambda
.
These remarks imply that on
S_{\infty}
the one-dimensional submanifold
\psi_{k}^{-1}(\partial\Sigma)
converges graphically to a limit
\Gamma_{\infty}\subseteq\{x\in S_{\infty}:i_{\infty}(x)=x\}
, which meets
\partial S_{\infty}=\partial T_{\infty}
orthogonally.

Thus, the domains
\psi_{k}^{-1}(\Sigma)
converge graphically on
S_{\infty}
to a domain
S_{\infty}^{\prime}
bounded by
\Gamma_{\infty}
.
If
C
is an
i_{k}
-invariant component of
T_{k}
, either
i_{k}
interchanges the two circles in
\partial C
or it preserves them (as sets).
In the former case, the core geodesic of
C
appears in both collections
\{\gamma_{i}\}
and
\{\beta_{j}\}
, and equals
\partial\Sigma\cap C
.
In the latter case, there are just two diametrically opposite fixed points of
i_{k}
on each circle, so
\partial\Sigma
splits
C
into two isometric pieces; we can thus assume that
\psi_{k}^{-1}(\Sigma\cap C)
equals two half-cusps in this case.

Hence,
T_{\infty}^{\prime}:=\psi_{k}^{-1}(\Sigma\cap T_{k})
is a constant union of cusps and half-cusps. The union
S_{\infty}^{\prime}\cup T_{\infty}^{\prime}
is the desired limit surface, which is a compact Riemann surface
\Sigma_{\infty}
minus finitely many points (in the interior or on the boundary).
The area contribution which gets lost because of degenerating geodesics can be recovered as in the case of degenerating tori or annuli.

Note that
\Sigma_{\infty}
has at least
b(\Sigma)-|I|
boundary components.
Also, the Euler characteristic of its double is

\displaystyle 2(2-2g(\Sigma_{\infty})-b(\Sigma_{\infty}))

\displaystyle=2\chi(\Sigma_{\infty})=\chi(\widetilde{\Sigma}_{j})+2|I|=2\chi(\Sigma)+2|I|

\displaystyle=2(2-2g(\Sigma)-b(\Sigma))+2|I|

and we deduce
\chi(\Sigma_{\infty})\geq\chi(\Sigma)
,
g(\Sigma_{\infty})\leq g(\Sigma)
.

Note, however, that the number of boundary components could increase in principle: for instance, if
\Sigma
has genus one and one boundary component,
(\Sigma,g_{k})
could degenerate conformally into an annulus.

6.3.
Concentration points

We finally deal with concentration points for the area, or equivalently for the Dirichlet energy.
The problem is local; since there can be only finitely many concentration points, we can deal with just a single one.
Let
U^{\prime}
denote the ball or the half-ball. Up to precomposing the maps
\Phi_{k}
with suitable diffeomorphisms
U^{\prime}\to U\subset\Sigma
,
we can assume that the induced conformal classes converge smoothly to the standard one, and that we have the tight convergence

\displaystyle\nu_{k}^{\prime}:=\frac{1}{2}|d\Phi_{k}|^{2}\,\mathcal{L}^{2}\rightharpoonup m\,\mathcal{L}^{2}+\alpha\delta_{0}

of measures on
U^{\prime}
.
Looking at a sufficiently small neighborhood of the concentration point, we can assume that
\int_{U^{\prime}}m<\frac{c_{Q}}{2}
, while from
5.2
we have the lower bound
\alpha\geq c_{Q}
.
Let
B_{r_{k}}^{2}(x_{k})
be a ball of minimal radius such that
\int_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}(x_{k})\cap U^{\prime}}\frac{1}{2}|d\Phi_{k}|^{2}\geq\alpha-\frac{c_{Q}}{2}
, so that the integral is exactly
\alpha-\frac{c_{Q}}{2}
and necessarily
r_{k}\to 0
,
x_{k}\to 0
. It suffices to show that

(6.2)

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\nu_{k}^{\prime}((B_{R^{-1}}^{2}(x_{k})\setminus B_{Rr_{k}}^{2}(x_{k}))\cap U^{\prime})\to 0\quad\text{as }R\to\infty.

Once this is done, we deduce that the area (or Dirichlet energy) measures of
\Psi_{k}:=\Phi_{k}(x_{k}+r_{k}\cdot)
converge subsequentially to a measure
\nu
(on the plane or a upper half-plane) of total mass
\alpha
. There could be further concentration points for this new sequence of maps, but their masses are at most
\alpha-\frac{c_{Q}}{2}
: this is obvious if there are at least two such points;
if there is only one point
\overline{x}
of mass bigger than
\alpha-\frac{c_{Q}}{2}
, then eventually

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r_{k}/2}(x_{k}+r_{k}\overline{x})\cap U^{\prime}}\frac{1}{2}|d\Phi_{k}|^{2}=\int_{B_{1/2}^{2}(\overline{x})\cap U_{k}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{2}|d\Psi_{k}|^{2}>\alpha-\frac{c_{Q}}{2},

where
U_{k}^{\prime}:=r_{k}^{-1}(U^{\prime}-x_{k})
, contradicting the minimality of
r_{k}
. Thus, this blow-up process has to be iterated only a finite amount of times.

The proof of
Eq.

6.2
is similar to the one of
Eq.

6.1
.
Select radii
R^{1/2}r_{k}<a<Rr_{k}
and
R^{-1}<b<R^{-1/2}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{a}^{2}(x_{k})\cap U^{\prime}}|d\Phi_{k}|^{2}\leq\frac{2C}{a\log R},\quad\int_{\partial B_{b}^{2}(x_{k})\cap U^{\prime}}|d\Phi_{k}|^{2}\leq\frac{2C}{b\log R},

where
C
is an upper bound for
\int_{U^{\prime}}|d\Phi_{k}|^{2}
;
this can be done since the right-hand sides integrate to
C
on the two intervals.
Now the length of
\Phi_{k}|_{\partial B_{a}^{2}\cap U^{\prime}}
and
\Phi_{k}|_{\partial B_{b}^{2}\cap U^{\prime}}
is bounded by

\frac{C}{\sqrt{\log R}}
, for a different constant
C
. Since the area of
\Phi_{k}
between the two radii is bounded by
\alpha+\int_{U^{\prime}}m-(\alpha-\frac{c_{Q}}{2})+o(1)
, whose limit is less than
c_{Q}
, for
R
big enough we can apply
Proposition

6.1

and deduce that

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\nu_{k}^{\prime}((B_{b}^{2}(x_{k})\setminus B_{a}^{2}(x_{k}))\cap U^{\prime})\leq\delta\Big(\frac{C}{\sqrt{\log R}},C\Big).

Since
B_{R^{-1}}^{2}(x_{k})\setminus B_{Rr_{k}}^{2}(x_{k})\subseteq B_{b}^{2}(x_{k})\setminus B_{a}^{2}(x_{k})
, this proves
Eq.

6.2
.

The limit maps produced by concentration points have domains which are the plane or a half-plane, hence conformally the sphere or the disk (minus one point).

Proof of
1.1
.

Thanks to the arguments from this section and the previous one, we obtain disjoint domains
U_{k,1},\dots,U_{k,N}\subseteq\Sigma
such that
the varifold induced by
\Phi_{k}|_{U_{k,i}}
converges to a parametrized free boundary stationary varifold,
as
k\to\infty
, and
\int_{\Sigma\setminus\bigcup_{i}U_{k,i}}\operatorname{vol}_{\Phi_{k}}\to 0
.

Since we can merge the domains of these parametrized varifolds into a (possibly disconnected)
compact Riemann surface, the statement follows.
∎

7.
Regularity

From the previous section we know that the limit varifold
\mathbf{v}_{\infty}
is a parametrized free boundary stationary varifold

(\Sigma^{\prime},\Phi,N^{\prime})
, for some weakly conformal map
\Phi:\Sigma^{\prime}\to\mathcal{M}
with
\Phi(\partial\Sigma^{\prime})\subseteq\mathcal{N}

and
N^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}(\Sigma^{\prime},\{1,2,\dots\})
.
This parametrized varifold gives rise, in local charts for
\Sigma^{\prime}
, to a local parametrized stationary varifold, as defined in
[
31
, Definition 2.9]
(see also
[
31
, Remark 2.3]
).
The main result of that work, namely
[
31
, Theorem 5.7]
, tells us that
N^{\prime}
is locally constant and
\Phi
is a branched minimal immersion, on (the interior of) the components of
\Sigma^{\prime}
where
\Phi
is not (a.e.) constant.

Hence, in order to study the regularity of
\Phi
, we can discard these trivial components and replace
N^{\prime}
with
1
, without affecting the stationarity property enjoyed by the parametrized varifold (recall
Definition

5.9
).

Remark 7.1
.

Actually, the main result of
[
32
]
still applies in this setting, so that
we have
N=1
automatically for
\Phi
arising as a limit of the maps
\Phi_{k}
.
Indeed,
[
32
, Theorem 3.2]
(with
\tau_{k}^{4}
in place of
\tau_{k}^{2}
) still holds for smooth maps
\Psi_{k}:\overline{B}_{R}^{2}\to\mathcal{M}_{p_{k},\ell_{k}}
,
with
\mathcal{M}_{p_{k},\ell_{k}}:=\ell_{k}^{-1}(\mathcal{M}-p_{k})
(
p_{k}\in\mathcal{M}
), which are
\tau_{k}^{5}
-critical on the interior for
E_{\tau_{k}}
(where the term
\tau_{k}\operatorname{length}(\Phi|_{\partial\Sigma})
can be now ignored): see also
1.3
. The other property needed for that paper is that if
\Psi
, with values into
\mathcal{M}_{p,\ell}
, is
\tau^{5}
-critical for
E_{\tau}
, then
\lambda^{-1}(\Psi-q)
is
(\tau/\lambda)^{5}
-critical for
E_{\tau/\lambda}
(with the manifold
\mathcal{M}_{p+\ell q,\lambda\ell}
), whenever
\lambda\leq 1
:
this also holds and was obtained along the proof of
5.3
.

Under the assumptions of
Section

5
, this gives
\frac{1}{2}|d\Phi_{j}|^{2}\,\mathcal{L}^{2}\rightharpoonup|\partial_{1}\Phi_{\infty}\wedge\partial_{2}\Phi_{\infty}|\,\mathcal{L}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}|d\Phi_{\infty}|^{2}\,\mathcal{L}^{2}
in local charts, far from the concentration points.

This implies that
\Phi_{j}\to\Phi_{\infty}
in
W^{1,2}_{loc}
here, so that
\Phi_{\infty}
is still weakly conformal; hence, there was actually no need to reparametrize it.
These remarks, however, are not needed in the present section, which establishes the regularity of general parametrized free boundary stationary varifolds.

For simplicity, since we will not need to refer back to the original setting, we will write
\Sigma
in place of
\Sigma^{\prime}
in the rest of this section.
In order to prove
1.4
, we wish to show the following result. The fact that
\Phi|_{\Sigma\setminus\partial\Sigma}
is a branched minimal immersion then follows as discussed in the last step of the proof of
[
31
, Theorem 5.7]
.

Theorem 7.2
.

The map
\Phi:\Sigma\to\mathcal{M}
is
C^{\infty}
-smooth up to the boundary
\partial\Sigma
and has
\partial_{\nu}\Phi\perp T\mathcal{N}
at
\partial\Sigma
.

As already mentioned, the interior regularity was already established in
[
31
]
. Here we show again how it can be obtained when

—a fact proved in
[
38
]
and used in
[
31
]
—presenting a slightly simplified proof which covers also the boundary regularity.

We first show a simple strenghtening of
5.11
.
In the sequel, given
\omega\subseteq\Sigma
open, we let
\mathbf{v}_{\omega}:=\Phi_{*}(\omega)
.

Proposition 7.3
.

The map
\Phi
is continuous and the stationarity (respectively, free boundary stationarity) of
\mathbf{v}_{\omega}
in
Definition

5.9

holds for any domain
\omega\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset\Sigma\setminus\partial\Sigma
(respectively,
\omega\subseteq\Sigma
).

Proof.

The continuity of
\Phi
can be obtained by the same arguments used in the proof of
5.2
.

As for the second statement, given
\omega\subseteq\Sigma
and a vector field
X\in\mathcal{X}_{fb}
supported outside
\Phi(\partial\omega)
,
we can find a nonnegative smooth function
\rho\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\omega)
such that
\rho=1
near the compact set
\Phi^{-1}(\operatorname{spt}(X))\cap\omega
.
The stationarity of
\mathbf{v}_{\omega}
against the vector field
X
then follows from the same property for the varifolds
\mathbf{v}_{\{\rho>\lambda\}}
, for
0<\lambda<1
, each of which agrees with
\mathbf{v}_{\omega}
near
\operatorname{spt}(X)
.
The proof in the case
\omega\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset\Sigma\setminus\partial\Sigma
is analogous.
∎

Let us fix a metric on
\Sigma
, compatible with the conformal structure.
As in
[
38
]
, we first show that

(7.1)

\displaystyle\Phi\text{ is smooth near }\mathcal{G}^{\prime},

with
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\subseteq\Sigma\setminus\partial\Sigma

defined to be the set of points

such that

has rank
2
and, in a chart centered at
x
,
\int_{B_{r}^{2}}|d\Phi-d\Phi(0)|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}=o(r^{2})
.

Before proving this, let us set
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}:=\Sigma\setminus\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{B}:=\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(\mathcal{B}^{\prime}))
and
\mathcal{G}:=\Sigma\setminus\mathcal{B}
.

Remark 7.4
.

Note that
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{G^{\prime}}

are both
\Phi
-saturated: this means that whenever
\Phi(x)=\Phi(y)
and

, the same holds for

\mathcal{G}

Arguing as in the proof of

\displaystyle|\mathbf{v}_{\Sigma}|(\Phi(\mathcal{B}^{\prime}))\leq C\int_{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}|d\Phi|^{2}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma}=0.

Hence, as
|\mathbf{v}_{\Sigma}|=\Phi_{*}(\frac{1}{2}|d\Phi|^{2}\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma})
, we get

a.e. on
\mathcal{B}
.

Proof of
Eq.

.

Given

, we can choose a conformal chart centered at

, mapping a neighborhood

to

.
Viewing

(see, e.g.,
[

Moreover,
\int_{B_{r}^{2}}\frac{1}{2}|d\Phi|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}=\pi s^{2}+o(r^{2})

[

on the ball

where the varifold

has generalized mean curvature bounded in

, small excess (for

\pi(1-\delta)^{2}s^{2}+O(\delta)s^{2}
.

We deduce that on some ball
B_{\theta}^{Q}(\Phi(0))
the varifold
\mathbf{v}_{B_{r}^{2}}
agrees with the graph
S
of a smooth function

, with multiplicity one, up to rotating the coordinates.
2
2

2

The smoothness of

can be assumed by standard Schauder theory, since

satisfies an elliptic equation on a small ball.

Selecting a new radius

,
such that
\Phi(r^{\prime}y)=\Phi(0)+d\Phi(0)[r^{\prime}y]+o(r^{\prime})

. Hence, viewing

By the chain rule, any point

For any

As

But then, recalling

Remark 7.5
.

We implicitly ask that the chosen representative of

.

It follows that

Remark 7.6
.

Given

. Hence, using

\displaystyle\int_{U}\left\langle\nabla w,d\Phi\right\rangle\,\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma}=0,

solves the harmonic map equation

on
\mathcal{G}
.

In order to show

, let

,

\displaystyle\int_{U^{\prime}}\left\langle\nabla(fe_{k}),d\Phi\right\rangle\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}=0

and all nonnegative

Indeed, once this is done, if

\displaystyle-\partial_{i}(g_{jk}(\Phi)\partial_{i}\Phi^{j})+\Gamma_{pk}^{j}(\Phi)g_{jq}(\Phi)\partial_{i}\Phi^{p}\partial_{i}\Phi^{q},

The smoothness of

If instead

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\partial_{i}(g_{jk}(\Phi)\partial_{i}\Phi^{j})+\Gamma_{pk}^{j}(\Phi)g_{jq}(\Phi)\partial_{i}\Phi^{p}\partial_{i}\Phi^{q}=0,\\

.

By the coarea formula,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg(-\int_{\partial\{f>\lambda\}}\left\langle e_{k}(\Phi),\partial_{\nu}\Phi\right\rangle+\int_{\{f>\lambda\}}\left\langle\nabla(e_{k}\circ\Phi),d\Phi\right\rangle\bigg)\,d\lambda=0.

\lambda
.

Proposition 7.7
.

For almost every value of
\lambda>0
, for

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial\omega}\left\langle e_{k}(\Phi),\partial_{\nu}\Phi\right\rangle+\int_{\omega}\left\langle\nabla(e_{k}\circ\Phi),d\Phi\right\rangle=0.

Fix
\lambda

\omega

\partial U^{\prime}
if

,
and such that the trace

is

.
For all

\varepsilon

.

Take a smooth function

. Hence,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-\int_{\partial\omega}\rho_{\varepsilon}(\Phi)\left\langle e_{k}(\Phi),\partial_{\nu}\Phi\right\rangle+\int_{\omega}\left\langle e_{k}(\Phi)\,d(\rho_{\varepsilon}\circ\Phi),d\Phi\right\rangle+\int_{\omega}\rho_{\varepsilon}(\Phi)\left\langle\nabla e_{k}(\Phi)[d\Phi],d\Phi\right\rangle

\displaystyle\to-\int_{\partial\omega}\left\langle e_{k}(\Phi),\partial_{\nu}\Phi\right\rangle+\int_{\omega}\left\langle\nabla e_{k}(\Phi)[d\Phi],d\Phi\right\rangle,

as desired.
∎

Proposition A.1
.

A continuous,
W^{1,2}
map
u:B_{1}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
solving a linear system of the form

\displaystyle-\partial_{i}(g_{jk}\partial_{i}u^{j})+b_{kpq}\partial_{i}u^{p}\partial_{i}u^{q}=0,

with
g\geq\lambda>0
symmetric and continuous and
b
bounded,
is
W^{1,r}_{loc}
for all
r<\infty
.

The same holds for
u
defined on the half-ball
U^{\prime}:=B_{1}^{2}\cap\{\Im(z)\geq 0\}
,
if in addition we have

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}u^{k}=0\text{ for }k\leq n,\quad u^{k}=0\text{ for }k>n,

as well as
g_{ij}=0
for
i\leq n
,
j>n
, on the boundary
\partial U^{\prime}
, for some
0\leq n\leq m
.

Remark A.2
.

The condition
\partial_{\nu}u^{k}=0
could be written more faithfully as
g_{jk}\partial_{\nu}\Phi^{j}=0
and is of course meant in a weak sense, coupled with the equation: namely,
we require
\int_{U^{\prime}}(g_{jk}\partial_{i}f\partial_{i}u^{j}+b_{kpq}f\partial_{i}u^{p}\partial_{i}u^{q})=0

for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(U^{\prime})
and
k\leq n
, allowing
f
to be nonzero on
\partial U^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Assume
u
is a solution on the unit ball. Then, for any ball
B_{2r}^{2}(x)\subseteq B_{1}^{2}
,
we can integrate the equation against
\eta^{2}(u-(u)_{B_{2r}^{2}(x)})
, where
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{2r}^{2}(x))
is a cut-off function satisfying
\eta=1
on
B_{r}^{2}(x)
and
|d\eta|\leq\frac{2}{r}
. Recall that the notation
(u)_{S}
indicates the average of
u
on a set
S
. This gives

\displaystyle\lambda\int\eta^{2}|du|^{2}\leq C\int\eta|du|\,|d\eta|\,|u-(u)_{B_{2r}^{2}(x)}|+C\int\eta^{2}|du|^{2}\operatorname{osc}(u,B_{2r}^{2}(x))

and, applying Young’s inequality, it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}^{2}(x)}|du|^{2}\leq Cr^{-2}\int_{B_{2r}^{2}(x)}|u-(u)_{B_{2r}^{2}(x)}|^{2}\leq Cr^{-2}\Big(\int_{B_{2r}^{2}(x)}|du|\Big)^{2}

whenever
\operatorname{osc}(u,B_{2r}^{2}(x))
is small enough.
The classical Gehring’s lemma (see, e.g.,
[
15
, Theorem V.1.2]
) then implies
that
du\in L^{r}(B)
for some
r>2
and any fixed ball
B\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset B_{1}^{2}
(with
r
depending on
B
). Then the nonlinear term
b_{kpq}\partial_{i}u^{p}\partial_{i}u^{q}
is
L^{r/2}(B)

and standard elliptic regularity theory gives
du\in L^{s}_{loc}(B)
, with
\frac{1}{s}=\frac{2}{r}-\frac{1}{2}
, so that
s>r
; iterating, we get
du\in L^{t}_{loc}
for any
t<\infty
.

If we are in the half-ball case, then we can reduce to the previous case by reflection.
We extend
g
and
u
to
\widetilde{g}
and
\widetilde{u}
on the ball
B_{1}^{2}
, by means of the formula

\displaystyle g(s,-t):=Ug(s,t)U,\quad\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{u}^{1}\\
\vdots\\
\widetilde{u}^{m}\end{pmatrix}(s,-t):=U\begin{pmatrix}u^{1}\\
\vdots\\
u^{m}\end{pmatrix}(s,t)

for
(s,-t)
in the lower half-ball, with
U:=\begin{pmatrix}I_{n}&\\
&-I_{m-n}\end{pmatrix}
. Note that, by our hypotheses on
g
,
\widetilde{g}
is still continuous. Also, it is straightforward to check that
\widetilde{u}
solves

\displaystyle-\partial_{i}(\widetilde{g}_{jk}\partial_{i}\widetilde{u}^{j})+\widetilde{b}_{kpq}\partial_{i}\widetilde{u}^{p}\partial_{i}\widetilde{u}^{q}=0,

with
\widetilde{b}_{kpq}
extending
b_{kpq}
according to the following rule:
if
k\leq n
then
\widetilde{b}_{kpq}(s,-t):=b_{kpq}(s,t)
if
p
and
q
belong to the same set in the partition
\{\{1,\dots,n\},\{n+1,\dots,m\}\}
, and
\widetilde{b}_{kpq}(s,-t):=-b_{kpq}(s,t)
otherwise;
if
k>n
then the opposite holds.
Then from the case of the full ball we deduce
d\widetilde{u}\in L^{t}_{loc}
for any
t<\infty
.
∎

Remark A.3
.

If the coefficients are smooth functions of
u
, then
u
is smooth.
To check this, note that in the full ball case
u
is
C^{0,\alpha}_{loc}
for any
\alpha<1
.
The same is then true for the coefficients
g_{jk}(u)
.
Since the nonlinearity
b_{kpq}\partial_{i}u^{p}\partial_{i}u^{q}
belongs to
L^{r}_{loc}
for all
r<\infty
,
classical Schauder theory then gives
du\in C^{0,\alpha}_{loc}
for all
\alpha<1

and bootstrapping we reach
u\in C^{\infty}
.

In the half-ball case, we can still argue in the same way that
d\widetilde{u}\in C^{0,\alpha}_{loc}
for all
\alpha<1
.
So
\widetilde{g}
is locally Lipschitz and we deduce
\widetilde{u}\in W^{2,r}_{loc}
for all
r<\infty
.
Differentiating the original equation in the first variable preserves the boundary conditions and leads to an equation of the form

\displaystyle\partial_{i}(g_{jk}\partial_{i}(\partial_{1}u^{j}))+f_{k}=0

with
f_{k}\in L^{r}_{loc}
for all
r<\infty
, and the same reflection trick (applied to
w:=\partial_{1}u
)
gives
\partial_{1}u\in W^{2,r}_{loc}
for all
r<\infty
. Iterating we get the same for all derivatives
\partial_{1}^{k}u
.
Now the equation allows to deduce inductively that
u\in W^{k,r}_{loc}
for all
k
,
since
g_{jk}(u)\Delta u^{j}=-\partial_{i}(g_{jk}(u))\partial_{i}u^{j}+b_{kpq}(u)\partial_{i}u^{p}\partial_{i}u^{q}
;
this expresses
\partial_{22}u
in terms of
\partial_{11}u
and lower order derivatives and
hence, for any multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
with
\alpha_{2}\geq 2
,
we deduce that
\partial^{\alpha}u=\partial_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\partial_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}u\in L^{r}_{loc}
for all
r<\infty
from the same property enjoyed by
\partial_{1}^{\alpha_{1}+2}\partial_{2}^{\alpha_{2}-2}u
and lower order derivatives of
u
.

The following statements deal with general varifolds. It is clear that we can assume the smallness constant
c_{V}
appearing in all of them to be always the same.

Lemma A.4
.

There exists
c_{V}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N})>0
with the following property.
Given
p\in\mathcal{N}
and
0<s<c_{V}
, for any
2
-varifold
\mathbf{v}
on
\mathcal{M}
which is free boundary stationary outside
\overline{B}_{s}(p)
and has density
\theta\geq\overline{\theta}
on
\operatorname{spt}(|\mathbf{v}|)\setminus\overline{B}_{s}(p)
, either
\operatorname{spt}(|\mathbf{v}|)\subseteq B_{2s}(p)
or

|\mathbf{v}|(\mathcal{M}\setminus\overline{B}_{s}(p))\geq c_{V}\overline{\theta}
.

Proof.

Pick
\gamma>0
small, to be fixed along the proof; we will choose
c_{V}\leq\gamma
,
so that the varifold is free boundary stationary outside
\overline{B}_{\gamma}(p)

Possibly multiplying
\mathbf{v}
by
\overline{\theta}^{-1}
, we can assume
\overline{\theta}=1
.
Note that if
q\in\operatorname{spt}(|\mathbf{v}|)\setminus B_{2\gamma}(p)
then by
Eq.

4.6
we have

(A.1)

\displaystyle|\mathbf{v}|(B_{\gamma}(q))\geq c(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N})\gamma^{2}\theta(|\mathbf{v}|,q)\geq c(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N})\gamma^{2}.

Otherwise,
|\mathbf{v}|
is supported in
B_{2\gamma}(p)
.
Assume we are in this second case and pick a set of coordinates
(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}):B_{5\gamma}(p)\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
centered at
p
,
with
\mathcal{N}
corresponding to
\{x_{n+1}=\dots=x_{m}=0\}
. We can impose that
\|g_{ij}-\delta_{ij}\|_{C^{1}}\leq\gamma

(in coordinates),
for
\gamma

p\in\mathcal{N}
.

On this ball, we define the vector field

to be

Assuming

For
\gamma

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}_{\Pi}X\geq 0

Lemma A.6
.

There exist
c_{V}>0
and
\delta:(0,\infty)^{2}\to(0,\infty)
, with
\lim_{s\to 0}\delta(s,t)=0
for every
t

Given two points

and a radius
s>0

;
if a

-varifold
\mathbf{v}
on
\mathcal{M}
is free boundary stationary outside
B
, has density
\theta\geq\overline{\theta}
on
\operatorname{spt}(|\mathbf{v}|)\setminus B

\displaystyle|\mathbf{v}|(B_{r}(q))\leq c^{\prime}r^{2}\quad\text{for all }q\in\mathcal{M},\ r>0,

c_{V}
and the function

\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
.

Proof.

We can assume
\overline{\theta}=1
. From
Eq.

4.6

with density at least

\operatorname{spt}(|\mathbf{v}|)
has
|\mathbf{v}^{\prime}|(\mathcal{M})\geq\lambda(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N})
.
Let
\delta(s,c^{\prime})
be the supremum of all possible masses
|\mathbf{v}|(\mathcal{M})
which are smaller than

, for
\mathbf{v}
as in the statement, with

to be specified below. Take a sequence
s_{k}\to 0
of positive numbers and a sequence
