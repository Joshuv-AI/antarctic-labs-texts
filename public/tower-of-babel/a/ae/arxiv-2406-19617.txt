Stochastic Zeroth-Order Optimization under Strongly Convexity and Lipschitz Hessian: Minimax Sample Complexity
Qian Yu, Yining Wang, Baihe Huang, Qi Lei, Jason D. Lee

Abstract
Optimization of convex functions under stochastic zeroth-order feedback has been a major and challenging question in online learning. In this work, we consider the problem of optimizing second-order smooth and strongly convex functions where the algorithm is only accessible to noisy evaluations of the objective function it queries. We provide the first tight characterization for the rate of the minimax simple regret by developing matching upper and lower bounds. We propose an algorithm that features a combination of a bootstrapping stage and a mirror-descent stage. Our main technical innovation consists of a sharp characterization for the spherical-sampling gradient estimator under higher-order smoothness conditions, which allows the algorithm to optimally balance the bias-variance tradeoff, and a new iterative method for the bootstrapping stage, which maintains the performance for unbounded Hessian.

Introduction

Stochastic optimization of an unknown function with access to only noisy function evaluations is a fundamental problem in operations research, optimization, simulation and bandit optimization research, commonly known as
zeroth-order optimization

(
Chen et al., 2017
)
,
derivative-free optimization

(
Conn et al., 2009
;
Rios & Sahinidis, 2013
)
or
bandit optimization

(
Bubeck et al., 2021
)
. In this problem, an optimization algorithm interacts sequentially with an oracle and obtains noisy function evaluations at queried points every time. The algorithm produces an approximately optimal solution after
T
such evaluations, with its performance evaluated by the expected difference between the function values at the approximate optimal solution produced and the optimal solution. A more rigorous formulation of the problem is given in Sec.
2
below.

Existing works and results on stochastic zeroth-order optimization could be broadly categorized into two classes:

1.

Convex functions
. In the first thread of research, the unknown objective function to be optimized is assumed to be
concave
(for maximization problems) or
convex
(for minimization problems). For these problems, with minimal smoothness (e.g. objective function being Lipschitz continuous) it is possible to achieve a sample complexity of
\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
for an expected optimization error or
\varepsilon
, which is also a polynomial function of domain dimension
d
; see for example the works of
Agarwal et al. (2013)
;
Lattimore & Gyorgy (2021)
;
Bubeck et al. (2021)
;

2.

Smooth functions
. In the second thread of research, the unknown objective function to be optimized is assumed to be highly
smooth
, but not necessary concave/convex.
Typical results assume the objective function is Hölder smooth of order
k\geq 1
, meaning that the
(k-1)
-th derivative of the objective function is Lipschitz continuous.
Without additional conditions, the optimal sample complexity with such smoothness assumptions is
\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-(2+d/k)})

(
Wang et al., 2019
)
, which scales exponentially with the domain dimension
d
.

In this paper, we study the optimal sample complexity of stochastic zeroth-order optimization when the objective function exhibits both (strong) convexity and a high degree of smoothness.
As we have remarked in the first bullet point above,
with convexity and Hölder smoothness of order
k=1
(equivalent to the objective function being Lipschitz continuous), the works of
Agarwal et al. (2013)
;
Lattimore & Gyorgy (2021)
;
Bubeck et al. (2021)
established an
\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
upper bound.
With higher order of Hölder smoothness, i.e.,
k=2
(equivalent to the gradient of the objective being Lipschitz continuous), it is shown that simpler algorithms exist but the sample complexity remains
\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})

(
Besbes et al., 2015
;
Agarwal et al., 2010
;
Hazan & Levy, 2014
)
, which seemingly suggests the relatively smaller role smoothness plays in the presence of convexity.
In this paper we show that with even higher order of Hölder smoothness, i.e.,
k=3
(specifically, the Hessian of the objective being Lipschitz continuous), the optimal sample complexity is improved to
O(\varepsilon^{-1.5})
, which is significantly smaller than the sample complexity of the convex-without-smoothness setting
\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
, or the smooth-without-convexity setting
\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-(2+d/3)})
. More importantly, when the Lipschitzness of Hessian is defined in Frobenius norm (see condition A1), we propose an algorithm that also achieves the optimal dimension dependency, which fully characterizes the optimal sample complexity.

Summary of technical contributions.

We developed several important techniques in this paper to achieve the optimal sample complexity when the objective function is strongly convex and has Lipschitz Hessian. First, we show that when estimating the gradient under a stochastic environment, even with an unbounded action space, it could be beneficial to sample with non-isotropic distributions (as opposed to conventional standard Gaussian, or uniform distributions on hyperspheres).
Second, we present a new approach to analyze the bias and variance of the hyperellipsoid-sampling-based gradient estimators, which enables obtaining sharp bounds with tight constants and strengthens the best-known results in the higher-order smoothness case. Third, we present a two-stage bootstrap-type framework for the algorithmic design, which extends the perturbative analysis in the final stage to the full regime. This extension relies on a non-trivial modification of Newton’s method, and we proved its robustness under stochastic observation.
We complete the characterization of the minimax regret by deriving a lower bound using the KL-divergence-based approach.

Related works on gradient estimators.

Gradient estimation serves as a key building block for stochastic zeroth-order optimization algorithms. For instance, a classical one-point estimator was proposed as early as in

Flaxman et al. (2005)
;
Blair (1985)
, where the gradient
\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})
is estimated based on empirical measures of
f(\boldsymbol{x}+r\boldsymbol{u})
for some fixed
r
and i.i.d. uniformly random
\boldsymbol{u}
on the unit hypersphere. This was later refined to be two-point estimators, and the sampling distribution of
\boldsymbol{u}
was generalized to isotropic distributions such as standard Gaussian (e.g., see
Agarwal et al. (2010)
;
Bach & Perchet (2016)
;
Zhang et al. (2020)
). A majority of prior work focused on the analysis for such estimators under the Lipschitz gradient assumption, where the best guaranteed bound for the bias is at the order of
\Theta(r)
, with a polynomial factor dependent on
d
. The line of works by
Bach & Perchet (2016)
;
Akhavan et al. (2020)
;
Novitskii & Gasnikov (2021)
also adopted isotropic sampling, and it was shown that with higher-order smoothness of
k=3
, this bound can be improved to
\Theta(r^{2})
.
The improvement of sample complexity in our work is mainly due to the tight characterization of our gradient estimator, which covers the special case of isotropic sampling and provides a bound of
\frac{r^{2}\rho\sqrt{d}}{2(d+2)}
in the estimation bias. This strengthens or improves the bounds presented in prior works, and a detailed comparison can be found in Appendix
A
.

On the other hand, non-isotropic sampling was used as early as in
Abernethy et al. (2008)
, then extended in
Saha & Tewari (2011)
;
Hazan & Levy (2014)
. Primarily, they were used to ensure that the sampling points are contained within a bounded action set.

(
Suggala et al., 2021
)
showed the necessity of non-isotropic sampling over quadratic loss function in the adversarial setting.
In this work, we essentially demonstrated that non-isotropic sampling can be used to refine a preliminary algorithm by adding a mirror-descent-like final stage.
More recently, non-isotropic sampling was also adopted in
Lattimore & György (2023)
to optimize convex and global Lipschitz functions.

2
Problem Formulation

We consider the stochastic optimization problem under the class of functions that are strongly convex and have Lipschitz Hessian. The goal in this setting is to design learning algorithms to achieve approximately the global minimum of an unknown objective function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

A learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
can interact with the function by adaptively sampling their value for
T
times, and receive noisy observations. At each time
t\in[T]
, the algorithm selects
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and receives the following observation,

\displaystyle y_{t}=f(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})+w_{t},

(1)

where
\{w_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
are independent random variables with zero mean and bounded variance. Formally, the algorithm can be described by a list of conditional distributions where each
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
is selected based on all historical data
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{\tau},y_{\tau}\}_{\tau<t}
and the corresponding distribution. Then for any
t
, we assume that
\mathbb{E}[w_{t}|\{\boldsymbol{x}_{\tau},y_{\tau}\}_{\tau<t},\boldsymbol{x}_{t}]=0
and
\textup{Var}[w_{t}|\{\boldsymbol{x}_{\tau},y_{\tau}\}_{\tau<t},\boldsymbol{x}_{t}]\leq 1
for any
t
.
2
2

2

If the variances of
w_{t}
’s are bounded by a different constant, all our results can be reproduced by normalizing the values of
f
.
For simplicity, we also adopt a common assumption that the additive noises are subgaussian, particularly,
\mathbb{P}[|w_{t}|>s|\{\boldsymbol{x}_{\tau},y_{\tau}\}_{\tau<t},\boldsymbol{x}_{t}]\leq 2e^{-{s^{2}}}
for all
s>0
and
t\in[T]
. However, the subgaussian assumption can be removed by adopting more sophisticated mean-estimation methods (e.g., see
Nemirovskii & Yuom (1983)
;
Jerrum et al. (1986)
;
Alon et al. (1999)
;
Lee & Valiant (2022)
;
Yu et al. (2023a)
).

We assume that the objective function
f
is second-order differentiable. Furthermore, we impose the following conditions.

(A1)

(Lipschitz Hessian). There exist a constant
\rho\in(0,+\infty)
such that for all
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that
\|\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x})-\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\|_{\textup{F}}\leq\rho\|\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\textup{F}}
denotes the Frobenius norm;

(A2)

(Strong Convexity). There exists a constant
M\in(0,+\infty)
such that for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the minimum eigenvalue of the Hessian
\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x})
is greater than
M
.

(A3)

(Bounded Distance from Initialization to Optimum Point). There exists a constant
R\in(0,+\infty)
such that the infimum of
f(\boldsymbol{x})
within the hyperball
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}\leq R
is identical to the infimum of
f(\boldsymbol{x})
over the entire
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

In the rest of this paper, we let
\mathcal{F}(\rho,M,R)
denote the set of all second-order differentiable functions that satisfy the above conditions, with corresponding constants given by
\rho,M
, and
R
.
We aim to find algorithms to achieve asymptotically the following minimax simple regret, which measures the expected difference of the objective function on
x_{T}
and the optimum.

\mathfrak{R}(T;\rho,M,R):=\inf_{\mathcal{A}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}(\rho,M,R)}\mathbb{E}\left[f(\boldsymbol{x}_{T})-f(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})\right],

where
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
denotes the global minimum point of
f
.

.

For any dimension

4
Proof Ideas for Theorem
3.1

The proposed algorithm operates in two stages (see Algorithm
4
).
In the first stage, the algorithm uses a small fraction of samples to obtain a rough estimation of the global minimum point. We ensure that the estimation in the first stage is sufficiently accurate with high probability, so that in the following final stage, the objective function can be approximated by a quadratic function and the resulting approximation error can be bounded using tensor analysis.

5
Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we achieve the first minimax simple regret for bandit optimization of second-order smooth and strongly convex functions. We derived the matching upper and lower bounds and proposed an algorithm that integrates a bootstrapping stage with a mirror-descent stage. Our key technical innovations include a sharp characterization of the spherical-sampling gradient estimator under higher-order smoothness conditions and a novel iterative method for the bootstrapping stage that remains effective with unbounded Hessians.

While these advancements settle the fundamental problem of optimizing second-order smooth and strongly convex functions with zeroth-order feedback, the techniques and insights presented in this paper also pave the way for further research in this domain. One interesting follow-up direction is to generalize our analysis to the online setting for the average regret metric. Additionally, investigating the fundamental tradeoff between simple regret and average regret could yield valuable insights for task-specific algorithmic designs.

. Recall the definition of uniform sampling error in Definition
B.1
. We have essentially proved that
P_{4\epsilon}\geq\frac{1}{2}
. According to earlier discussions, this implies that the minimax simple regret is lower bounded by
\epsilon=\Omega\left(\frac{\rho^{\frac{2}{3}}T^{-\frac{2}{3}}}{M}\right)

B.2
Proof for the General Case

The generalization of the earlier 1D lower bound is obtained by constructing a set of hard-instance functions where the optimization problem over this subset consists of
d
binary hypothesis estimation problems, each identical to a 1D construction. Formally, for any
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},s_{2},...,s_{d})\in\{1,2\}^{d}
and any input
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{(1)},x_{(2)},...,x_{(d)})
, we let

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}f_{s_{j}}(x_{(j)}).

One can verify that
f_{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathcal{F}(\rho,M,R)
for all
\boldsymbol{s}
for sufficiently large
T
.

Note that the simple regret for the above function class can be written as the sum of
d
individual terms
\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(f_{s_{j}}(x_{(j)})-\inf_{x}f_{s_{j}}(x)\right)
. As proved earlier, the expectation of each term associated with any index
j
is at least
\Omega\left(\frac{\rho^{\frac{2}{3}}T^{-\frac{2}{3}}}{M}\right)
even if all entries of
\boldsymbol{s}
except
s_{j}
is known. Therefore, the total expected regret is lower bounded by
\Omega\left(\frac{d\rho^{\frac{2}{3}}T^{-\frac{2}{3}}}{M}\right)
.

Proof of Theorem

We use the following elementary facts, which are versions of well-known properties of subgaussian and subexponential distributions in
Vershynin (2018)
, but with explicit and possibly improved constant factors. For completeness, we provide their proofs in Appendix

Proposition C.1
.

For any real-valued zero-mean independent random variables
z_{1},...,z_{k}
, if

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[|z_{j}|\geq K]\leq 2\exp\left({-\frac{K^{2}}{\sigma_{j}^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\forall\,j\in[k],K\in[0,+\infty),

(15)

for some
\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{k}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k}z_{j}\right|\geq K\right]\leq 2\exp\left({-\frac{K^{2}}{4\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sigma_{j}^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\forall\,K\in[0,+\infty).

(16)

Proposition C.2
.

For any real-valued independent random variables
z_{1},...,z_{k}
, if

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[|z_{j}|\geq K]\leq 2\exp\left({-\frac{K}{\sigma_{j}}}\right)

\displaystyle\forall\,j\in[k],K\in[0,+\infty),

(17)

for some positive
\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{k}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k}z_{j}\right|\geq K\right]\leq 2\exp\left({-\frac{K}{3\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sigma_{j}}}\right)

\displaystyle\forall\,K\in[0,+\infty).

(18)

Proof of equation (
4
).

We first prove the bound entry-vise. Consider any
m_{k}
, which contains a summation of
2n
independent subgaussian variables. By Prop.
C.1
we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[|m_{k}-\mathbb{E}\left[m_{k}\right]|\geq K\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left({-\frac{K^{2}}{2/nr^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\forall\,K\in[0,+\infty).

Then, by the Lipschitz Hessian condition, the bias for each entry is bounded as follows.

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[m_{k}\right]-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\right|\leq\frac{1}{6}\rho r^{2},

(20)

where
x_{k}
denotes the
k
th entry of
\boldsymbol{x}
.
Hence, each

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|m_{k}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\right|^{2}\geq K^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left({-\frac{K^{2}}{\frac{\rho^{2}r^{4}}{4}+\frac{4}{nr^{2}}}}\right).

(21)

By the independence of

(22)

∎

\displaystyle\ \sqrt{\frac{3M}{2\rho||\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\textup{B}}||_{2}}}\left(\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textup{B}})-\frac{M}{4}||\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\textup{B}}||_{2}^{2}\right)

(37)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{3M}{2\rho||\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\textup{B}}||_{2}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textup{B}})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ \frac{3^{\frac{2}{3}}M}{4\rho^{\frac{2}{3}}}\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textup{B}})^{\frac{2}{3}}.

To summarize, the following inequality holds in both cases.

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textup{B}})\leq\max\left\{2(f(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textup{B}})-f(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})),\frac{8\rho}{3M^{\frac{3}{2}}}(f(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textup{B}})-f(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}))^{\frac{3}{2}}\right\}.

(38)

To apply inequality (
36
), we use the following implications.

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textup{B}})\leq 2(f(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textup{B}})-f(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}))

\displaystyle+\frac{8\rho}{3M^{\frac{3}{2}}}(f(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textup{B}})-f(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}))^{\frac{3}{2}},

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}\left(\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textup{B}})\geq\frac{M^{3}}{8\rho^{2}}\right)\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textup{B}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8\rho}{M^{\frac{3}{2}}}(f(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textup{B}})-f(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}))^{\frac{3}{2}}.

Then, the derived inequality can be simplified using our assumptions on
n_{\textup{g}}
and
n_{\textup{H}}
.

Appendix E
Remaining details for Theorem
3.1

To complete the proof, we essentially need to prove inequality (
6
). To illustrate the main ideas, we start with an analysis in a simplified setting where estimation errors for the BootstrapingEst and HessianEst functions are zero. Then, we show how the proof steps can be modified to have the errors and uncertainties incorporated.

E.1
Analysis for the zero-error case

We prove that when the estimation errors are set to zero,
the first stage of Algorithm
4
reduces

\nabla f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})
to a vector of bounded length in boundedly many iterations. This is summarized in the following proposition.

Proposition E.1
.

For any fixed parameter values
\rho,M
,
R
, let
\left\{\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\right\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}
be sequences defined for any
f\in\mathcal{F}(\rho,M
,
R)
, such that
\boldsymbol{z}_{1}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\left(\boldsymbol{z}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\right)
equals
-\widetilde{H}_{t}^{-1}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})
, where
\widetilde{H}_{t}
is a matrix that has the same eigenvectors of
\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})
, with each eigenvalue
\lambda
replaced by
\max\{\lambda,m_{t}\}
, and
m_{t}
being the smallest value for
||\widetilde{H}_{t}^{-1}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})||_{2}\leq\frac{M}{\rho}
. There exists an explicit function
T(\rho,M,R)\leq 5R^{2}\rho^{2}/M^{2}+1
such that

\nabla f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})\leq\frac{M^{2}}{2\rho}
holds for any
f
and any
t\geq T(\rho,M,R)
.

Proof.

For convenience, let
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{r}}_{t}\triangleq-(\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}))^{-1}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})
and
{\boldsymbol{r}}_{t}\triangleq-\widetilde{H}_{t}^{-1}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})
. To investigate the evolution of gradients,
we integrate the Lipschitz Hessian condition and obtain that

\displaystyle||\nabla f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t+1})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})-\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})\,{\boldsymbol{r}_{t}}||_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\rho||{\boldsymbol{r}}_{t}||_{2}^{2}.

(39)

From the definition of
{\boldsymbol{r}_{t}}
, if
||\widetilde{\boldsymbol{r}}_{t}||_{2}\leq M/\rho
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t+1})\right|\right|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\rho||\widetilde{\boldsymbol{r}}_{t}||_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{M^{2}}{2\rho}.

(40)

Note that by strong convexity, when the above bound holds,

\displaystyle||\widetilde{\boldsymbol{r}}_{t+1}||_{2}\leq\frac{\left|\left|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t+1})\right|\right|_{2}}{M}\leq\frac{M}{2\rho}\leq\frac{M}{\rho}.

(41)

Hence, once
||\widetilde{\boldsymbol{r}}_{t}||_{2}
reaches below
M/\rho
for some
t_{0}
, our desired bound on
\left|\left|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t+1})\right|\right|_{2}
remain hold for any
t>t_{0}
. Therefore, for the purpose of our proof, we can focus on the case where
||\widetilde{\boldsymbol{r}}_{t}||_{2}>M/\rho
and show that this condition can only hold for boundedly many iterations.

Consider any fixed function
f\in\mathcal{F}(\rho,M,R)
, let
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
denote its global minimum point. A crucial step in our proof is to show that

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t})\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{t}\geq 0.6||\boldsymbol{r}_{t}||_{2}^{2}.

(42)

For brevity, let
\beta
denote the minimum eigenvalue of
\widetilde{H}_{t}
, and
\widetilde{f}
denote the following quadratic approximation.

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\triangleq f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})+(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t})\nabla f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})+\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t})(\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}))(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t}).

We apply the strong convexity condition at point

\boldsymbol{y}\triangleq\boldsymbol{z}_{t+1}-0.4\beta\widetilde{H}_{t}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{r}}_{t}
. Recall that
f
is minimized at
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
, we have

\displaystyle 0\geq f(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})-f(\boldsymbol{y})\geq

\displaystyle\,\nabla f(\boldsymbol{y})\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y})+\frac{M}{2}||\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y}||_{2}^{2}.

(43)

By integrating the Lipschitz Hessian, similar to inequality (
39
),
\nabla f(\boldsymbol{y})
can be approximated with
\nabla f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})+\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t})=\nabla\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{y})
. Formally,

\displaystyle||\nabla f(\boldsymbol{y})-\nabla\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{y})||_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\rho||\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t}||_{2}^{2}.

(44)

Hence, inequality (
43
) implies that

\displaystyle 0\geq

\displaystyle\,\nabla\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{y})\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y})+\frac{M}{2}||\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y}||_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\rho||\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t}||_{2}^{2}||\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y}||_{2}.

(45)

To characterize the terms in the above inequality, we first note that

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t}||_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=||\boldsymbol{r}_{t}||_{2}^{2}-0.8\boldsymbol{r}_{t}\cdot\widetilde{H}_{t}^{-1}\beta\boldsymbol{r}_{t}+0.16||\widetilde{H}_{t}^{-1}\beta\boldsymbol{r}_{t}||_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq||\boldsymbol{r}_{t}||_{2}^{2}-0.64||\widetilde{H}_{t}^{-1}\beta\boldsymbol{r}_{t}||_{2}^{2}.

For convenience, we denote
c\triangleq||\widetilde{H}_{t}^{-1}\beta\boldsymbol{r}_{t}||_{2}/||\boldsymbol{r}_{t}||_{2}
. We have that
c\in(0,1]
and

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t}||_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-0.64c^{2}\right)||\boldsymbol{r}_{t}||_{2}^{2}.

(46)

We also consider the following vector,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}

\displaystyle\triangleq(\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}))^{-1}\left(\nabla\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{y})+(\beta+0.36cM)\boldsymbol{r}_{t}-0.6cM(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t})\right)

\displaystyle=0.6\widetilde{H}_{t}^{-1}\left(\beta\boldsymbol{r}_{t}+0.4cM\left(\widetilde{H}_{t}^{-1}\beta\boldsymbol{r}_{t}-\boldsymbol{r}_{t}\right)\right),

of which the L2 norm is no greater than
0.6c||\boldsymbol{r}_{t}||_{2}\leq{0.6cM}/{\rho}
, which can be proved in the eigenbasis of
\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})
.
By Cauchy’s inequality, we have that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})

\displaystyle\geq-||\boldsymbol{q}||_{2}||\nabla\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})||_{2}

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{0.6cM}{\rho}||\nabla\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})||_{2}.

(47)

Note that

\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y}=(\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}))^{-1}\left(\nabla\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})-\nabla\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{y})\right)
. The LHS of the above inequality can be written as
(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y})\cdot(\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}))\cdot\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{y})
, where the first term contains
\nabla\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{y})\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y})
, and the second term is bounded as follows.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{y})=

\displaystyle\,-0.6\widetilde{H}_{t}^{-1}\left(\widetilde{H}_{t}^{-1}\beta^{2}\boldsymbol{r}_{t}+(\beta-0.4cM)(\boldsymbol{r}_{t}-\widetilde{H}_{t}^{-1}\beta\boldsymbol{r}_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\,\cdot\left(0.4\beta\boldsymbol{r}_{t}+0.6\left(\widetilde{H}_{t}-\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})\right)\boldsymbol{r}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,-0.6\widetilde{H}_{t}^{-1}\cdot\widetilde{H}_{t}^{-1}\beta^{2}\boldsymbol{r}_{t}\cdot 0.4\beta\boldsymbol{r}_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,-{0.24c^{2}\beta||\boldsymbol{r}_{t}||_{2}^{2}}.

(48)

On the other hand,
observe that inequality (
44
) holds for any generic
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The RHS of inequality (
47
) can be characterized as follows.

\displaystyle||\nabla\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})||_{2}

\displaystyle=||\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})-\nabla\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})||_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\rho||\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t}||_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\rho||\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y}||_{2}^{2}+\rho(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y})\cdot(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\rho||\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t}||_{2}^{2}.

(49)

Therefore, by combining inequalities (
45
), (
47
), and
(
49
), we have that

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y})\cdot\left(\beta+0.36cM\right)\boldsymbol{r}_{t}\geq

\displaystyle\,-\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{y})+(0.5-0.3c)M||\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y}||_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle-0.5\rho||\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t}||_{2}^{2}\cdot||\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y}||_{2}-{0.3cM}||\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t}||_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\,-\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{y})-\frac{\rho^{2}||\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t}||_{2}^{4}}{(8-2.4c)M}-{0.3cM}||\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t}||_{2}^{2},

where the second line is obtained by taking the infimum w.r.t.
||\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y}||_{2}
.
Then, we apply inequalities (
46
), (
48
), and
||\boldsymbol{r}_{t}||_{2}\leq M/\rho

to obtain the following bound.

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y})\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{t}\geq

\displaystyle\,\frac{{0.24c^{2}}\beta-\frac{M\left(1-0.64c^{2}\right)^{2}}{(8-2.4c)}-{0.3cM}\left(1-0.64c^{2}\right)}{\beta+0.36cM}\cdot||\boldsymbol{r}_{t}||_{2}^{2}.

Note that the above bound is non-decreasing w.r.t.
\beta
, and our construction implies
\beta\geq M
. We can substitute
\beta
in the above inequality with
M
. Further, note that

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t})\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{t}=

\displaystyle\,||\boldsymbol{r}_{t}||_{2}^{2}-\boldsymbol{r}_{t}\cdot 0.4\beta\widetilde{H}_{t}^{-1}\boldsymbol{r}_{t}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\,\left(1-0.4{c}\right)||\boldsymbol{r}_{t}||_{2}.

We have obtained a lower bound of
(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t})\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{t}
as a function of
c
. This dependency is removed by taking the infimum, i.e.,

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t})\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{t}\geq

\displaystyle\,\inf_{c\in(0,1]}\left(\frac{{0.24c^{2}}-\frac{\left(1-0.64c^{2}\right)^{2}}{(8-2.4c)}-{0.3c}\left(1-0.64c^{2}\right)}{1+0.36c}+1-0.4{c}\right)\cdot||\boldsymbol{r}_{t}||_{2},

then inequality (
42
) is obtained.

We use this key inequality to obtain the following recursion rule.

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t}||_{2}^{2}-||\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t+1}||_{2}^{2}=

\displaystyle\,2\boldsymbol{r}_{t}\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t})-||\boldsymbol{r}_{t}||_{2}^{2}\geq 0.2||\boldsymbol{r}_{t}||_{2}^{2}.

Recall that for
f\in\mathcal{F}(\rho,M,R)
, we assumed that
||\boldsymbol{x}^{*}||_{2}\leq R
. Therefore, for
\boldsymbol{z}_{1}=\boldsymbol{0}
, the above recursion implies that the inequality
||\widetilde{\boldsymbol{r}}_{t}||_{2}>M/\rho
can hold for no greater than
5R^{2}\rho^{2}/M^{2}
iterations as
||\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{z}_{t}||_{2}^{2}
has to be non-negative for any
t
. Hence, based on the earlier discussion, we have proved that either
||\widetilde{\boldsymbol{r}}_{t}||_{2}\leq M/\rho
or
\nabla f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t+1})=\boldsymbol{0}
for all
t\geq 5R^{2}\rho^{2}/M^{2}
and all
f\in\mathcal{F}(\rho,M,R)
.
Recall inequality (
40
), this implies that
||\nabla f(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})||_{2}\leq\frac{M^{2}}{2\rho}
for all
t\geq 5R^{2}\rho^{2}/M^{2}+1
.
∎

.

Recall the recursion provided by inequality (

E.2
Generalization to the noisy case

Now we prove that, given bounded many iterations, the bootstrapping stage in Algorithm
4
provides an
\boldsymbol{x}_{\textup{B}}
that is sufficiently close to
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
with high probability even in the presence of noise. For convenience, let
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})}
denote the realization of vector
\boldsymbol{x}
at the end of the
k
th iteration in the bootstrapping stage and
N\triangleq\lfloor T^{0.1}\rfloor
denote the number of iterations. Therefore, we have
\boldsymbol{x}_{\textup{B}}=\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{(\textup{B})}
.
Further, we define
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{(\textup{B})}\triangleq\boldsymbol{0}
. We let
\boldsymbol{m}_{k},H_{k}
denote the realization of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{m}},H_{m^{*}}
in the
(k+1)
th iteration of the bootstrapping stage. Therefore, we have
\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})}=\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})}-H_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}_{k}
.
As a Benchmark for our analysis, we use
\widetilde{H}_{k}
to denote the value of
H_{m^{*}}
in the zero-error case, i.e., they denotes the value of
H_{m^{*}}
under the special case of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{m}}=\nabla f\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})}\right)
and
\widehat{H}=\nabla^{2}f\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})}\right)
. Hence, the update in the zero-error case can be denoted as
\boldsymbol{r}_{k}\triangleq-\widetilde{H}_{k}^{-1}\nabla f\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})}\right)
.

We let
E_{k}
be the indicator function of the event where there exists an
j<k
such that

\big|\big|\boldsymbol{x}_{j+1}^{(\textup{B})}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{(\textup{B})}-\boldsymbol{r}_{j}\big|\big|_{2}\geq{MT^{-0.2}}/{\rho}
. Intuitively,
E_{k}=0
describes the event that the optimization steps can be characterized similar to the zero-error case.
Notice that
E_{k}
is non-decreasing. We have either
E_{N}=0
, or
E_{k_{0}}=1
for some
k_{0}\in\{1,2,...,N\}
.
We provide the analysis separately for these two cases.

For the first case, i.e., when
E_{N}=0
, we can follow the earlier arguments and prove the following proposition (see Appendix
F.3
for details).

Proposition E.3
.

For any function
f\in\mathcal{F}(\rho,M,R)
and any sequence
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{(\textup{B})},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{(\textup{B})},...,\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}^{(\textup{B})}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that satisfies
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{(\textup{B})}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
E_{N-1}=0
, we have
||\boldsymbol{r}_{N-1}||_{2}\leq{2MT^{-0.2}}/{\rho}
when
T
is sufficiently large.

Recall the definition of
E_{N}=0
. The above proposition immediately implies that

\displaystyle\left|\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{(\textup{B})}-\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}^{(\textup{B})}\right|\right|_{2}\leq{3MT^{-0.2}}/{\rho}<M/\rho

when
T
is large. Hence, in such cases,
\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{(\textup{B})}
is obtained by the Newton update. Formally, if
\widehat{H}
denotes the estimator returned by the HessianEst function in the
N
th iteration, we have

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{(\textup{B})}-\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}^{(\textup{B})})\cdot\widehat{H}=-\boldsymbol{m}_{N-1},

(50)

Therefore, by applying the above results to the Lipschitz Hessian condition, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{(\textup{B})})\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}^{(\textup{B})})+(\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{(\textup{B})}-\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}^{(\textup{B})})\cdot\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}^{(\textup{B})})\right|\right|_{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}||\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{(\textup{B})}-\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}^{(\textup{B})}||_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}^{(\textup{B})})-\boldsymbol{m}_{N-1}+(\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{(\textup{B})}-\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}^{(\textup{B})})\cdot\left(\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}^{(\textup{B})})-\widehat{H}\right)\right|\right|_{\textup{F}}+\frac{9M^{2}}{2\rho T^{0.4}}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}^{(\textup{B})})-\boldsymbol{m}_{N-1}\right|\right|_{2}+\frac{3M}{\rho T^{0.2}}\cdot\left|\left|\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}^{(\textup{B})})-\widehat{H}\right|\right|_{\textup{F}}+\frac{9M^{2}}{2\rho T^{0.4}}.

(51)

Hence, by direct integration of the tail bounds in Theorem
4.3
, we can conclude that

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{N}^{(\textup{B})})\right|\right|_{2}^{3}\cdot\mathbbm{1}(E_{N}=0)\cdot T^{\frac{2}{3}}\right]=0.

(52)

Now we consider the second case, i.e., when
E_{N}=1
. By its definition, we must have the event of
E_{k}=0
to
E_{k+1}=1
for a unique
k\in\{0,1,...,N-1\}
, which implies that
\big|\big|\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})}-\boldsymbol{r}_{k}\big|\big|_{2}\geq{MT^{-0.2}}/{\rho}
. We prove that conditioned on any of these events, the random variable
||\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})})||_{2}
has a super-polynomial tail, which contributes vanishingly to their moments in the asymptotic sense.
Formally, let

\displaystyle M_{k}

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left[||\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})})||_{2}^{3}\cdot\mathbbm{1}(E_{k+1}=1,E_{k}=0)\right],

We aim to prove that

\displaystyle\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\max_{k\in\{0,1,...,N-1\}}M_{k}\cdot NT^{\frac{2}{3}}=0.

(53)

Consider any fixed
k\in\{0,1,...,N-1\}
and conditioned on any realization of
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})}
, we
characterize the distribution of
\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})}

by providing the following proposition, which is proved in Appendix
F.4
.

Proposition E.4
.

Consider any vectors
\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, any positive definite matrices
H,H^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with all eigenvalues lower bounded by
M
, and any fixed parameter
R_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. Let
H_{m^{*}}
be the symmetric matrix sharing the same eigenbasis of
H
but with each eigenvalue
\lambda
replaced with
\max\{\lambda,m^{*}\}
, where
m^{*}
is chosen to be the smallest value such that
||H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}||_{2}\leq R_{0}
. Let
H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime}
be defined correspondingly for
\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}
and
H^{\prime}
. We have that

\displaystyle\left|\left|H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right|\right|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{2R_{0}}{M}\cdot\left({\left|\left|\boldsymbol{m}-\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right|\right|_{2}}+{R_{0}}\cdot{\left|\left|H-H^{\prime}\right|\right|_{\textup{F}}}\right).

(54)

Furthermore, when
\left|\left|H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right|\right|_{2}>0
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left|H^{\prime}_{m^{\prime*}}\left(H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right)\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \leq\left(3+\frac{2R_{0}}{||H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}||_{2}}\right)\left(\left|\left|\boldsymbol{m}-\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right|\right|_{2}+R_{0}\left|\left|H-H^{\prime}\right|\right|_{\textup{F}}\right).

(55)

By choosing
R_{0}=M/\rho
,
H_{m^{*}}=H_{N-1}
,
H^{\prime}_{m^{\prime*}}=\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}^{(\textup{B})})
,
\boldsymbol{m}=\boldsymbol{m}_{N-1}
, and
\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}=\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}^{(\textup{B})})
for Proposition
E.4
, the condition of
E_{k+1}
can be characterized by the estimation errors of the gradient and Hessian.
For brevity, we define

\displaystyle\Psi\triangleq\left|\left|\boldsymbol{m}-\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right|\right|_{2}+R_{0}\left|\left|H-H^{\prime}\right|\right|_{\textup{F}}.

We also let
\beta
denote the minimum eigenvalue of
{H}_{k}
. The condition of
E_{k+1}=1
and
E_{k}=0
implies that
||H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}||_{2}\geq R_{0}T^{-0.2}
, which implies that
\Psi\geq\frac{\beta R_{0}T^{-0.2}}{3+2T^{0.2}}
according to inequality (
55
). Hence,
M_{k}
can be bounded as follows.

\displaystyle M_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[||\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})})||_{2}^{3}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\left(\Psi\geq\frac{\beta R_{0}T^{-0.2}}{3+2T^{0.2}}\right)\right],

On the other hand, by generalizing inequality (
51
), we have

\displaystyle\left|\left|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})})\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})})+(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})})\cdot\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})})\right|\right|_{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}||\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})}||_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})})-\boldsymbol{m}_{k}\right|\right|_{2}+\left|\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})}\right|\right|_{2}\cdot\left|\left|\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})})-\widehat{H}\right|\right|_{\textup{F}}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ +\left|\left|\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})}\right)\left(\widehat{H}-H_{k}\right)\right|\right|_{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}||\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})}||_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Psi+R_{0}\beta+\frac{R_{0}M}{2}.

(56)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}M_{k}\cdot NT^{\frac{2}{3}}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\left(\Psi+R_{0}\beta+\frac{R_{0}M}{2}\right)^{3}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\left(\Psi\geq\frac{\beta R_{0}T^{-0.2}}{3+2T^{0.2}}\right)\cdot NT^{\frac{2}{3}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=0.

(57)

Since the above bounds are uniform over the index
k
, equation (
53
) is implied. The above arguments also show that

\displaystyle\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}[E_{N}=1]\cdot N^{3}T^{\frac{2}{3}}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}N\cdot\max_{k}\mathbb{P}[E_{k+1}=1,E_{k}=0]\cdot N^{3}T^{\frac{2}{3}}=0.

(58)

So far, we have proved that the moments of the gradient norm
\left|\left|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})})\right|\right|_{2}
is bounded after entering the
E_{k}=1
phase. We proceed to bound their contribution to the
N
th iteration. To that end, we denote

\displaystyle G_{k}

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left[||\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})})||_{2}^{3}\cdot\mathbbm{1}(E_{k}=1)\right].

This sequence is initialized with
G_{0}=0
by definition.
We establish the following recursion for sufficiently large
T
.

\displaystyle G_{k+1}

\displaystyle\leq G_{k}\left(1+\frac{1}{N}\right)+6N^{2}(\rho R_{0}^{2})^{3}\cdot\mathbb{P}[E_{N}=1]+M_{k}.

We note that conditioned on any fixed
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})}
the gradient norm function
||\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})})||_{2}
can be approximated with its linear expansion. Formally, let
\widetilde{g}(\boldsymbol{x})\triangleq\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})})+(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})})\cdot\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})}),
we have

\displaystyle\left|\left|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})})\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left|\widetilde{g}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})})\right|\right|_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\rho\left|\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})}\right|\right|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left|\widetilde{g}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})})\right|\right|_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\rho R_{0}^{2}.

Then, in the eigenbasis of
\widehat{H}
, it is clear that

\displaystyle\left|\left|\widetilde{g}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})})\right|\right|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\,\left|\left|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})})+(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})})\cdot\widehat{H}\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle+\left|\left|(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})})\cdot\left(\widehat{H}-\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})})\right)\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\left|\left|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})})\right|\right|_{2}+\left|\left|\boldsymbol{m}_{k}-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})})\right|\right|_{2}+R_{0}\left|\left|\widehat{H}-\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})})\right|\right|_{\textup{F}}.

Recall that by Theorem
4.3
, when
T
is sufficiently large, the moments of
\left|\left|\boldsymbol{m}_{k}-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})})\right|\right|_{2}+R_{0}\left|\left|\widehat{H}-\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})})\right|\right|_{\textup{F}}
is upper bounded by any fixed quantity.
Therefore, as a rough estimate, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[||\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}^{(\textup{B})})||_{2}^{3}\cdot\mathbbm{1}(E_{k}=1)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[||\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{(\textup{B})})+\rho R_{0}^{2}||_{2}^{3}\cdot\mathbbm{1}(E_{k}=1)\right]

\displaystyle\leq G_{k}\left(1+\frac{1}{N}\right)+6N^{2}(\rho R_{0}^{2})^{3}\cdot\mathbb{P}[E_{k}=1]

when
T
is sufficiently large. Consequently, our needed recursion is implied by the monotonicity of
E_{k}
, and we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}G_{N}\cdot T^{\frac{2}{3}}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\left(\max_{k}M_{k}+6N^{2}(\rho R_{0}^{2})^{3}\cdot\mathbb{P}[E_{k}=1]\right)\cdot N\left(1+\frac{1}{N}\right)^{N}T^{\frac{2}{3}}

\displaystyle=0.

(59)

Then, inequality (
6
) is obtained by combining strong convexity, the above bound, and inequality
(
52
).

Remark E.5
.

Note that compared to the simple regret guarantee stated in inequality (
6
),
we have essentially proved a stronger statement that the moments of the gradient at the outcome of the bootstrapping stage follow similar power decay laws. Therefore, while we presented a final stage algorithm that uses non-isotropic sampling to be compatible with general bootstrapping stages, our specific bootstrapping stage actually allows for the use of isotropic (hyperspherical) sampling for gradient estimation in the final stage.

F.1
Proof of Proposition
C.1

Proof.

Recall that all
z_{j}
’s have zero expectations.
By subgaussianity, we have that all even moments of
z_{j}
are bounded as follows.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[z_{j}^{2\ell}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{K=0}^{{+\infty}}2\ell K^{2\ell-1}\mathbb{P}\left[\left|z_{j}\right|\geq K\right]\textup{d}K

\displaystyle\leq\int_{K=0}^{{+\infty}}2\ell K^{2\ell-1}\min\left\{2\exp\left({-\frac{K^{2}}{\sigma_{j}^{2}}}\right),1\right\}\textup{d}K

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}(1+\ln 2)\,\sigma_{j}^{2}&\text{if $\ell=1$,}\\
(2+2\ln 2+\ln^{2}2)\,\sigma_{j}^{4}\ \ \ \ &\text{if $\ell=2$,}\\
2\cdot\ell!\,\sigma_{j}^{2\ell}&\text{if $\ell>2$.}\end{cases}

(60)

Using AM-GM inequality, the odd moments of
z_{j}
can then be bounded using the even moments. Specifically,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[z_{j}^{2\ell+1}\right]\leq\frac{1}{2s}\mathbb{E}\left[z_{j}^{2\ell}\right]+\frac{s}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[z_{j}^{2\ell+2}\right].

Therefore, we have obtained the following upper bounds for the moment-generating function.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(sz_{j})\right]

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\frac{s^{m}}{m!}\mathbb{E}\left[z_{j}^{m}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{7s^{2}}{12}\mathbb{E}\left[z_{j}^{2}\right]+\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\,\left(2\ell+2+\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\right)\frac{s^{2\ell}}{(2\ell)!\cdot 2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[z_{j}^{2\ell}\right].

Applying inequality (
60
), the expression above can be bounded with a series of
(s\sigma_{j})^{2}
. The coefficient of each
(s\sigma_{j})^{2\ell}
is no greater than
\frac{1}{\ell!}
, which can be verified numerically for
\ell\leq 2
and inductively for
\ell\geq 3
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(sz_{j})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\,\frac{(s\sigma_{j})^{2\ell}}{\ell!}=e^{(s\sigma_{j})^{2}}.

(61)

Because
z_{j}
’s are independent,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(s\sum_{j}z_{j}\right)\right]=\prod_{j}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(sz_{j})\right]\leq\exp\left(s^{2}\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}^{2}\right).

Inequality (
16
) is implied by Markov’s bound. Specifically, for any
K\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{k}z_{j}\geq K\right]

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{s\geq 0}{\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(s\sum_{j}z_{j}\right)\right]}\cdot{\exp\left(-sK\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{s\geq 0}{\exp\left(s^{2}\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}^{2}-sK\right)}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{K^{2}}{4\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}^{2}}\right).

For the same reason, we also have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{k}z_{j}\leq-K\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{K^{2}}{4\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}^{2}}\right).

Hence, by union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k}z_{j}\right|\geq K\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{k}z_{j}\geq K\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{k}z_{j}\leq-K\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{K^{2}}{4\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}^{2}}\right).

(62)

∎

Proof of Proposition
C.2

Proof.

By subexponentiality, the moment-generating function of each
|z_{j}|
is bounded as follows for any
s<\frac{1}{\sigma_{j}}
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(s|z_{j}|)]

\displaystyle=1+\int_{K=0}^{{+\infty}}s\exp({sK})\cdot\mathbb{P}\left[\left|z_{j}\right|\geq K\right]\textup{d}K

\displaystyle\leq 1+\int_{K=0}^{{+\infty}}s\exp({sK})\cdot\min\left\{2\exp\left({-\frac{K}{\sigma_{j}}}\right),1\right\}dK

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{s\sigma_{j}}}{1-s{\sigma_{j}}}.

(63)

Because
z_{j}
’s are independent,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(s\left|\sum_{j}z_{j}\right|\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(s\sum_{j}\left|z_{j}\right|\right)\right]=\prod_{j}\mathbb{E}[\exp(s|z_{j}|)]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{s\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}}}{\prod_{j}(1-s\sigma_{j})}.

(64)

We choose
s=1/(3\sum_{j}\sigma_{j})
, note that
s\sigma_{j}\leq 1/3
, we have
(1-s\sigma_{j})\geq\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{3s\sigma_{j}}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(s\left|\sum_{j}z_{j}\right|\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{(\ln 2-3\ln\frac{2}{3})(s\sum_{j}\sigma_{j})}=3/2^{\frac{2}{3}}<2.

Then, inequality (
18
) is implied by Markov’s bound, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k}z_{j}\right|\geq K\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(s\left|\sum_{j}z_{j}\right|\right)\right]\cdot{\exp\left(-sK\right)}

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{K}{3\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}}\right).

∎

\left|\left|H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right|\right|_{2}\leq 2R_{0}

\max\left\{||H^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}||_{2},||H^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}||_{2}\right\}>R_{0}

m^{*}\geq m^{\prime*}

if the

R_{0}

m^{\prime*}

\displaystyle\left|\left|H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\triangleq H_{m^{*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime},

Firstly, if
m^{*}=m^{\prime*}
, we follow similar arguments from equation (
69
) to inequality (
70
). I.e., in this case, we have

\displaystyle H^{\prime}_{m^{\prime*}}\left(H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{m}-\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime})+(H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime}-H_{m^{*}})H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}.

(74)

Hence, by triangle inequality and inequality (
71
),

\displaystyle\left|\left|H^{\prime}_{m^{\prime*}}\left(H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right)\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq||\boldsymbol{m}-\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}||_{2}+\left|\left|H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime}-H_{m^{*}}\right|\right|_{\textup{F}}\left|\left|H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq||\boldsymbol{m}-\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}||_{2}+||H^{\prime}-H||_{\textup{F}}\cdot R_{0}.

(75)

Then, for
m^{*}>m^{\prime*}
, we define
H^{\prime}_{m^{*}}
and
\boldsymbol{a}
,
\boldsymbol{b}
,
\boldsymbol{c}
as in the earlier proof steps.
We first prove the following key inequality.

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}\cdot||\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}\leq 2||\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{b}||_{2}\cdot||\boldsymbol{a}||_{2}.

(76)

Recall the assumption in this case implies that
||\boldsymbol{a}||_{2}=R_{0}
. By taking the squares on both sides, the inequality above is equivalent to the following linear inequality of vector
\boldsymbol{a}
.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\left(8R_{0}^{2}\,\boldsymbol{b}-2||\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}\,\boldsymbol{c}\right)\leq 4R_{0}^{2}\cdot\left(R_{0}^{2}+||\boldsymbol{b}||_{2}^{2}\right)-||\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}\cdot\left(R_{0}^{2}+||\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}\right).

(77)

By Cauchy’s inequality, the LHS of inequality (
77
) is upper bounded by
||\boldsymbol{a}||_{2}\cdot||8R_{0}^{2}\,\boldsymbol{b}-2||\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}\,\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}
.
The coefficient of
||\boldsymbol{a}||_{2}
in this expression can be further characterized as follows.

\displaystyle\left|\left|8R_{0}^{2}\,\boldsymbol{b}-2||\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}\,\boldsymbol{c}\right|\right|_{2}^{2}=

\displaystyle 4\cdot\left(4R_{0}^{2}-||\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}\right)\left(4R_{0}^{2}||\boldsymbol{b}||_{2}^{2}-||\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}||\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+16R_{0}^{2}\cdot||\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R_{0}^{2}}\left(4R_{0}^{2}\cdot\left(R_{0}^{2}+||\boldsymbol{b}||_{2}^{2}\right)-||\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}\cdot\left(R_{0}^{2}+||\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{R_{0}^{2}}\left(4R_{0}^{2}\cdot\left(R_{0}^{2}-||\boldsymbol{b}||_{2}^{2}\right)-||\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}\cdot\left(R_{0}^{2}-||\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+16R_{0}^{2}\cdot||\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{4}.

(78)

We prove that the contribution from the second term and the third term in the above expression is non-positive.
To that end, note that the definition of
H^{\prime}_{m^{*}}
,
H^{\prime}_{m^{\prime*}}
and the assumption of
m^{*}>m^{\prime*}
imply that
(\boldsymbol{c}-\boldsymbol{b})\cdot\boldsymbol{b}\geq 0
. We have the following inequalities.

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}+||\boldsymbol{b}||_{2}^{2}\leq||\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}\leq R_{0}^{2}.

(79)

Therefore,
0\leq 4R_{0}^{2}\cdot\left(R_{0}^{2}-||\boldsymbol{b}||_{2}^{2}\right)-||\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}\cdot\left(R_{0}^{2}-||\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}\right)\leq 4R_{0}^{2}\cdot||\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}
, and equation (
78
) implies that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\left(8R_{0}^{2}\,\boldsymbol{b}-2||\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}\,\boldsymbol{c}\right)\leq\left|4R_{0}^{2}\cdot\left(R_{0}^{2}+||\boldsymbol{b}||_{2}^{2}\right)-||\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}\cdot\left(R_{0}^{2}+||\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}\right)\right|.

By utilizing the above bound, inequality (
77
) is proved by noting that its RHS is non-negative, which can be proved using inequality (
79
). As mentioned earlier, this implies inequality (
76
).

To proceed further, we note that
\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}
lies in the eigenspace of
H_{m^{*}}^{\prime}
associated with eigenvalue
m^{*}
. Hence,

\displaystyle H_{m^{*}}^{\prime}\left(\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{c}\right)=H_{m^{*}}^{\prime}\left(\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{b}\right)+m^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}\right).

Therefore, by triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left|H^{\prime}_{m^{*}}\left(H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right)\right|\right|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\left|\left|H_{m^{*}}^{\prime}\left(\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{b}\right)\right|\right|_{2}+m^{*}\left|\left|\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}\right|\right|_{2}.

(80)

Note that by inequality (
76
) and the fact that all eigenvalues of
H_{m^{*}}^{\prime}
are lower bounded by
m^{*}
, we have

\displaystyle m^{*}\left|\left|\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}\right|\right|_{2}\leq\frac{2R_{0}}{||\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}}\left|\left|H_{m^{*}}^{\prime}\left(\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{b}\right)\right|\right|_{2}.

Therefore, it remains to upper bound the
\ell_{2}
norm of
H_{m^{*}}^{\prime}\left(\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{b}\right)
.

By the definition of vectors
\boldsymbol{a}
,
\boldsymbol{b}
,

\displaystyle H_{m^{*}}^{\prime}\left(\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{b}\right)

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{m}-\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime})+(H_{m^{*}}^{\prime}-H_{m^{*}})\,\boldsymbol{a}.

(81)

The triangle inequality implies that

\displaystyle\left|\left|H_{m^{*}}^{\prime}\left(\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{b}\right)\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left|\boldsymbol{m}-\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right|\right|_{2}+R_{0}\left|\left|H_{m^{*}}^{\prime}-H_{m^{*}}\right|\right|_{\textup{F}}.

(82)

Hence,

\displaystyle\left|\left|H^{\prime}_{m^{*}}\left(H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right)\right|\right|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{2R_{0}}{||\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\left|\left|\boldsymbol{m}-\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right|\right|_{2}+R_{0}\left|\left|H_{m^{*}}^{\prime}-H_{m^{*}}\right|\right|_{\textup{F}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{2R_{0}}{||H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}||_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\left|\left|\boldsymbol{m}-\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right|\right|_{2}+R_{0}\left|\left|H-H^{\prime}\right|\right|_{\textup{F}}\right),

where the last step is due to inequality (
71
). Thus, inequality (
55
) is implied by the semi-positive-definiteness of
H^{\prime}_{m^{*}}-H^{\prime}_{m^{\prime*}}
.

Finally, when
m^{*}<m^{\prime*}
,
we let
H_{m^{\prime*}}
denote the symmetric matrix sharing the same eigenbasis of
H
, but with each eigenvalue
\lambda
replaced by
\max\{\lambda,m^{\prime*}\}
. Due to the equivalence of
H
and
H^{\prime}
, our earlier proof steps imply that

\displaystyle\left|\left|H_{m*}\left(H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right)\right|\right|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{2R_{0}}{||H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}||_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\left|\left|\boldsymbol{m}-\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right|\right|_{2}+R_{0}\left|\left|H-H^{\prime}\right|\right|_{\textup{F}}\right).

Hence, by triangle inequality, we can use the above bound as follows.

\displaystyle\left|\left|H^{\prime}_{m^{\prime*}}\left(H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right)\right|\right|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\left|\left|\left(H_{m^{\prime*}}-H^{\prime}_{m^{\prime*}}\right)\left(H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right)\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle+\left|\left|H_{m^{\prime*}}\left(H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right)\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\left|H_{m^{\prime*}}-H^{\prime}_{m^{\prime*}}\right|\right|_{\textup{F}}\left|\left|H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle+\left|\left|H_{m^{\prime*}}\left(H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right)\right|\right|_{2}.

Note that
\left|\left|H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right|\right|_{2}\leq 2R_{0}
.
By inequality (
71
), it is clear that

\displaystyle\left|\left|H^{\prime}_{m^{\prime*}}\left(H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right)\right|\right|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\left(3+\frac{2R_{0}}{||H_{m^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}-H_{m^{\prime*}}^{\prime-1}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}||_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\left|\left|\boldsymbol{m}-\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\right|\right|_{2}+R_{0}\left|\left|H-H^{\prime}\right|\right|_{\textup{F}}\right).

∎
