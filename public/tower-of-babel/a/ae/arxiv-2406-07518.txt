On constant mean curvature 1-immersions of surfaces into hyperbolic 3-manifolds.
Gabriella Tarantello, Stefano Trapani

Abstract
The work of Bryant [ 7 ] revealed striking analogies between constant mean curvature (CMC) 1 1 -immersions of surfaces into the hyperbolic space ℍ 3 \mathbb{H}^{3} (Bryant surfaces) and minimal immersions into the euclidean space 𝔼 3 . \mathbb{E}^{3}. Ever since, the role of (CMC) 1 1 -immersions in hyperbolic geometry has been widely explored, see e.g. [ 44 ] and references therein. In account of [ 53 ] , [ 16 ] and after [ 48 ] , for a given surface S S (closed, orientable and of genus 𝔤 ≥ 2 \mathfrak{g}\geq 2 ) here we pursue the existence and uniqueness of (CMC) 1 1 -immersions of S S into hyperbolic 3-manifolds. It has been shown in [ 22 ] that, for | c | < 1 |c|<1 , the moduli space of (CMC) c c -immersions of S S into hyperbolic 3 3 -manifolds can be parametrised by elements of the tangent bundle of the Teichmüller space of the surface S . S. In turn in [ 48 ] it was pointed out that (CMC) 1 1 -immersions enter as ”critical” objects, in the sense that they can be attained only as limits of (CMC) c c -immersions, as | c | → 1 − . |c|\to 1^{-}. However, the passage to the limit can be prevented by possible blow-up phenomena, and at the limit ( | c | → 1 − |c|\to 1^{-} ) we could end up at best with an immersed surface having conical singularities supported at finitely many points (the blow-up points). If the genus 𝔤 = 2 \mathfrak{g}=2 then blow up can occur at a single point, and in [ 48 ] it was shown how it could be prevented and the passage to the limit ensured in terms of the image Z Z of Kodaira map given in ( 2.36 ). In this note we show that actually blow-up can occur only at one of the six Weierstrass points of the surface. Thus, in Theorem 1 and Theorem 4 we establish existence and uniqueness results under a sufficient ”compactness” condition, which in fact turns out to be also necessary, as shown in [ 51 ] . In addition we analyze the case of higher genus, where multiple (up to 𝔤 − 1 \mathfrak{g}-1 ) blow-up points can occur. In this case, for any 1 ≤ ν ≤ 𝔤 − 1 , 1\leq\nu\leq\mathfrak{g}-1, we identify in the ν \nu -secant variety of Z Z the appropriate replacement of Z Z (relative to ν = 1 \nu=1 ), see Proposition 2.1 . Moreover, in Theorem 3 we improve in a substantial way the asymptotic analysis of [ 48 ] , which concerns only the case of ”blow-up” with minimal mass. As a consequence, we cover the case of genus 𝔤 = 3 \mathfrak{g}=3 (see Theorem 5 ), and provide relevant contributions for arbitrary genus.

We prove:

If

)

As in

In the framework of Hitchin ’s self-duality theory (

Thus, for the unique solution pair:

we need to analyze what happens, when

The new difficulty here is that blow-up can occur at an accumulation point of different zeroes of the holomorphic quadratic differentials

Thus in this situation, when

The very aim of our blow-up analysis will be to show that, if blow-up occurs and we let

\displaystyle\int_{X}\beta\wedge\alpha=0\quad\forall\alpha\in Q(D)\ \mbox{with}\,\,D\,\mbox{ the blow up divisor}.

Unfortunately (by mere technical reasons), at present we can show (

In this way, we shall be able to handle the case of genus

As we shall see in Section

However, on the basis of the analysis we have contributed here, it should be possible to elaborate additional scalings together with inductive arguments to remove such restriction, a task we hope to carry out in future work.

Let
X\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathfrak{g}}(S)
be a given Riemann surface with (unique) hyperbolic metric
g_{X}
and induced scalar product

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, norm
\|\cdot\|
and volume

.

For any given point
x_{0}\in X
,
we can introduce holomorphic
\{z\}
-coordinates around
x_{0}
centred at the origin (namely
x_{0}
is mapped to
0
) and for small

we denote by :

\begin{array}[]{l}B(x_{0};r),\,\,\mbox{the geodesic ball centred at $x_{0}$ with radius r},\\
\,\,\Omega_{r}\,\,\mbox{the image of $B(x_{0};r)$ in $\mathbb{C}$, with }\,0\in\Omega_{r},\\
B_{r}\mbox{ the disc in $\mathbb{C}$ of center the origin and radius $r$.}\end{array}

(2.1)

Hence, for
\delta>0
sufficiently small we have:
B_{\delta}\subseteq\Omega_{r}.

Moreover, for
z=x+iy\in\Omega_{r}
(
r>0
small) we have the following local expression of the conformal and Riemannian structure of
X
:

\begin{array}[]{l}\partial=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-i\frac{\partial}{\partial y})\;\text{ and }\;\bar{\partial}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+i\frac{\partial}{\partial y})\\
dz=dx+idy,\;d\bar{z}=dx-idy,\\
g_{X}=e^{2u_{X}}dzd\bar{z}\quad u_{X}\;\text{smooth,}\\
dA=\frac{i}{2}e^{2u_{X}}dz\wedge d\bar{z},\end{array}

(2.2)

with the wedge
\wedge
product defined as usual on complex valued forms.

Furthermore, without loss of generality, we can consider the so called ”normal” coordinates at
x_{0},
by assuming further that
u_{X}
satisfies:

u_{X}(0)=|\nabla u_{X}(0)|=0.

(2.3)

In addition, in such local coordinate the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta
on
(X,g_{X})
can be expressed (locally) as follows:

\Delta=4e^{-2u_{X}}\partial\bar{\partial}

and in particular we have:

4\partial\bar{\partial}u_{X}=e^{2u_{X}}
in
\Omega_{r}
.

Actually (with abuse of notation) in the sequel we also denote
the
flat
Laplacian by

\Delta=4\partial\bar{\partial}
,
unless confusion arises.

Throughout this paper, we let:

\displaystyle E=T_{X}^{1,0}\text{ the holomorphic tangent bundle of $X$}

(2.4)

with dual:

E^{*}=(T_{X}^{1,0})^{*}=K_{X}\text{ the canonical bundle of $X.$}

The holomorphic line bundles
E
and
E^{*}
will be equipped with the complex structure induced by
X
, and with an hermitian product induced by a metric
g
(conformal to the metric
g_{X}
) defined in
X
.
Thus, on sections and forms valued on
E
(or
E^{*}
), we have a well defined d-bar operator denoted by:
\bar{\partial}
and a fiber-wise hermitian product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{g}
and norm
\|\cdot\|_{g}.

In the sequel, unless confusion arises, we shall drop the subscript
g_{X}
in the hermitian product and norm induced by

We let:

\begin{array}[]{l}A^{0}(E)=\{\text{smooth sections of $X$ valued on $E$}\},\\
A^{0,1}(X,E)=\{\text{$(0,1)$-forms valued on $E$ }\},\end{array}

and recall that the elements in
A^{0,1}(X,E)=A^{0,1}(X,\mathbb{C})\otimes E
are also known as Beltrami differentials.
By using the operator:

\bar{\partial}=\bar{\partial}_{E}:A^{0}(E)\longrightarrow A^{0,1}(X,E).

we can define the
(0,1)
-Dolbeault cohomology group as follows:

\mathcal{H}^{0,1}(X,E)=A^{0,1}(X,E)/\overline{\partial}(A^{0}(E)),

(2.5)

so that, for any Beltrami differential
\beta\in A^{0,1}(X,E),
there correspond the cohomology class:

[\beta]=\{\beta+\bar{\partial}\eta,\;\forall\;\eta\in A^{0}(E)\}\in\mathcal{H}^{0,1}(X,E).

Similarly, we let:

\displaystyle A^{1,0}(X,E^{*})=\{\text{$(1,0)$-forms valued on $E^{*}$ }\}=A^{1,0}(X,\mathbb{C})\otimes E^{*}.

We can consider the
wedge product
to act on:
A^{0,1}(X,E)\times A^{1,0}(X,E^{*}),
so for
\beta\in A^{0,1}(X,E)
and
\alpha\in A^{1,0}(X,E^{*}),
we have:
\beta\wedge\alpha\in A^{1,1}(X,\mathbb{C})

satisfying the well-known properties of the wedge product, see
[
17
]
Chapter 2 Section 2.

Consequently, we obtain the
bilinear form:

\begin{split}A^{1,0}(X,E^{*})\times A^{0,1}(X,E)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\;:\;(\alpha,\beta)\longrightarrow\int_{X}\beta\wedge\alpha,\end{split}

(2.6)

which, by Serre duality (see
[
55
]
), is non-degenerate and induces the isomorphism:

A^{1,0}(X,E^{*})\simeq(A^{0,1}(X,E))^{*}.

(2.7)

For
x\in X,
we consider the anti-linear Hodge star operator:

*_{x}:
\,[T_{x}(X)^{*}]^{0,1}\to[T_{x}(X)^{*}]^{1,0}
defined in the usual way, see
[
59
]
,

and we recall that a map
L:V\to W
between complex vector spaces is called anti-linear, if it is
\mathbb{R}
linear and
L(iv)=-iL(v)\ \forall v\in V.

Also for
\varphi\in[T_{x}(X)^{*}]^{0,1}\,\,\ \mbox{ and }e,f\in E_{x}

\sharp_{x}:E_{x}\to E^{*}_{x}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad*_{x}:\,[T_{x}(X)^{*}]^{0,1}\otimes E_{x}\to[T_{x}(X)^{*}]^{1,0}\otimes E^{*}_{x}

\sharp_{x}(e)(f)=<f,e>_{x},
and
*_{x}(\varphi\otimes e)=*_{x}(\varphi)\otimes\sharp_{x}(e),
for every
x\in X.

In this way, we obtain the Hodge star operator defined on forms:

*_{E}:A^{0,1}(X,E)\longrightarrow A^{1,0}(X,E^{*}),

(2.8)

where we see that, for given
\beta\in A^{0,1}(X,E),
the form

*_{E}\beta\in A^{1,0}(X,E^{*})
is identified by the condition:

\xi\wedge*_{E}\beta=\langle\xi,\beta\rangle\,dA,\quad\forall\;\xi\in A^{0,1}(X,E).

We have:

<*(\beta_{1}),*(\beta_{2})>=\overline{<\beta_{1},\beta_{2}>}=<\beta_{2},\beta_{1}>\,\,\,\mbox{ and }*_{E}(i\beta)=-i*_{E}(\beta).

(2.9)

Hence, the Hodge operator
*_{E}
depends on the metric
g_{X}
and it
defines an invertible, norm preserving operator, with inverse
*_{E}^{-1}.
In fact, (

between

In local holomorphic coordinates, for
*=*_{E}

\beta=\beta(z)(d\bar{z}\otimes\frac{\partial}{\partial z})\quad\mbox{ then }\,\,\,\|\beta\|=|\beta(z)|,\;\;\beta\in A^{0,1}(X,E);\\
\alpha=h(z)(dz)^{2}\,\,\mbox{ then }\|\alpha\|=2|h|e^{-2u_{X}},\,\,\,\alpha\in A^{1,0}(X,E^{*}).\end{split}

As already mentioned, from Theorem

as

For this purpose we let:

u_{t}=w_{t}+d_{t},\;\text{ with }\;\int_{X}w_{t}dA=0\;\text{ and }\;d_{t}=\fint_{X}u_{t}dA

\beta_{t}=\beta_{0}+\bar{\partial}\eta_{t}\in A^{0,1}(X,E)\;\text{ and }\;\alpha_{t}=e^{u_{t}}*_{E}\beta_{t}\in C_{2}(X)

and recall that,

The following easy bounds where derived in

]
:

Lemma 4.1
.

\begin{array}[]{l}\;\forall\;q\in[1,2)\;\;\exists\;C_{q}>0\;:\;\|w_{t}\|_{W^{1,q}(X)}\leq C_{q},\\
w_{t}\leq C\;\text{ in }\;X\text{ and }\,\;te^{d_{t}}\leq 1,\\
\int_{X}e^{-u_{t}}dA\geq C\fint_{X}\|\beta_{0}\|^{2}dA,\\
s_{t}\leq d_{t}+C,\\
\end{array}

(4.1)

for a suitable constant
C>0.

Proof.

See Lemma 3.7 and Remark 3.1 of
[
48
]
.
∎

It was observed in
[
48
]
that the map:

\displaystyle t\to 4\int_{X}e^{u_{t}}\|\beta_{0}+\bar{\partial}\eta_{t}\|^{2}dA=4\pi(\mathfrak{g}-1)-t\int_{X}e^{u_{t}}dA

is decreasing in
(0,+\infty)
(see Lemma 3.6 of
[

]
), and so it is well defined the value:

\displaystyle\rho([\beta])=\rho([\beta_{0}]):=4\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int_{X}e^{u_{t}}\|\beta_{0}+\bar{\partial}\eta_{t}\|^{2}dA=4\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int_{X}e^{\xi_{t}}\|\widehat{\alpha}_{t}\|^{2}dA.

(4.2)

Notice in particular that,

\displaystyle\rho([\beta])\in[0,4\pi(\mathfrak{g}-1)]\text{ and }\rho([\beta])=0\Longleftrightarrow[\beta]=0.

(4.3)

Furthermore, it was shown in
[
48
]
that in case

is bounded from below then necessarily:

\displaystyle\rho([\beta])=4\pi(\mathfrak{g}-1)\;\text{ i.e. }\,\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}t\int_{X}e^{u_{t}}=0.

(4.4)

In view of the estimates in (
4.1
), along a (positive) sequence

,
for

d_{k}:=d_{t_{k}},\,\;u_{k}=u_{t_{k}},\,\;w_{k}:=w_{t_{k}},

we may assume that,

w_{k}\longrightarrow w_{0}\;\text{ and }\;e^{w_{k}}\longrightarrow e^{w_{0}}\;\text{ pointwise and in }\;L^{p}(X),\;

t_{k}e^{d_{k}}\longrightarrow\mu\geq 0\;\text{ and so }\;t_{k}e^{u_{k}}\longrightarrow\mu e^{w_{0}}\;\text{ pointwise and in }\;L^{p}(X),

(4.5)

for any

and as
k\longrightarrow+\infty
.

In addition, it follows from the discussion in Section 1.1, that
for suitable
1\leq N\leq 4(\mathfrak{g}-1)
and
k
large, we may assume that the zero divisor of
\widehat{\alpha}_{k}:=\widehat{\alpha}_{t_{k}}\in C_{2}(X)\setminus\{0\}

\displaystyle\text{div}(\widehat{\alpha}_{k})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}n_{j}z_{j,k}\;\,\text{and}\,\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}n_{j}=4(\mathfrak{g}-1).

with
z_{j,k}\neq z_{l,k}\;\mbox{ for $j\neq l\in\{1,\ldots,N\}$}
the (distinct) zeroes of
\widehat{\alpha}_{k}
and
n_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
the multiplicity relative to
z_{j,k},\;j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

Moreover,
\text{ as }k\to+\infty,
we may let,

\widehat{\alpha}_{k}\to\widehat{\alpha}_{0},\;\;z_{j,k}\longrightarrow z_{j},\;\text{ with }\;\widehat{\alpha}_{0}(z_{j})=0,\;\,j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}.

Since the total multiplicity of each

adds up to
the value:
4(\mathfrak{g}-1)
,
we see that
\widehat{\alpha}_{0}
cannot vanish anywhere else.
However, while the points in
Z^{(k)}:=\{z_{1,k},\ldots,z_{N,k}\},
are distinct (and correspond to the distinct zeroes of
\widehat{\alpha}_{k}
,) their limit points
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}\}
may
not
be distinct.

Therefore, we let:
D_{0}=div(\widehat{\alpha}_{0}),
be the zero divisor of
\widehat{\alpha}_{0}
and define its support:

which collects the distinct points in
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}\}
. To be more precise we set,

D_{0}=\text{div}(\widehat{\alpha}_{0})=\sum_{p\in Z^{(0)}}n_{p}p\,\ \text{with}\sum_{p\in Z^{(0)}}n_{p}=4(\mathfrak{g}-1)

Hence,
we can identify the set
Z_{0}
of points in
Z^{(0)}
(possibly empty) which correspond to the limit of ”collapsing” zeroes in
Z^{(k)}
as given by:

\displaystyle Z_{0}:=\{p\in Z^{(0)}:n_{p}>n_{j}\text{ with }p=z_{j}\text{ for some }1\leq j\leq N\},

which is indeed well defined (independently on the choice of the index

) since if

for

, then

.

\xi_{k}=-(u_{t_{k}}-s_{t_{k}})

so that

. Moreover, we have:

Also notice that,

R_{k}(z)=8\prod_{j=1}^{N}(d_{g_{X}}(z,z_{j,k}))^{2n_{j}}G_{k}(z),\;z\in X,

where

.
Hence (by taking a subsequence if necessary) we may assume that,

the sequence we started with. In particular the origin must be a zero of

in (

Since we have
|q_{s}|=1

has decreased by at least one unit, and so we can proceed with an induction argument. To this purpose we note that for suitable

and
\delta_{1}>0
sufficiently small, we have

\displaystyle-\Delta\varphi_{k}=\prod_{j=1}^{s_{1}}|z-q_{j,k}|^{2n_{j}}h_{k}^{(1)}(z)e^{\varphi_{k}}-g_{1,k}(z)\text{ in }\overline{B}_{\delta_{1}},

where

\displaystyle 0\leq|q_{1,k}|\leq\cdots\leq|q_{s_{1},k}|=\tau_{k}^{(1)}\to 0,\mbox{ \ while $q_{j,k}\to q_{j}\neq 0,\,\text{ for }j=s_{1}+1,\ldots s,$}

\displaystyle h_{k}^{(1)}(z)=\prod_{j=s_{1}+1}^{s}|z-q_{j,k}|^{2n_{j}}h_{1,k}(z)\to h_{0}^{(1)}(z)\quad\text{uniformly in }\,\,\overline{B}_{\delta_{1}},

in
\overline{B}_{\delta_{1}}.

Furthermore for

More importantly, by (

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{k\to+\infty}\int_{B_{\delta}}W_{1,k}(z)e^{\varphi_{k}}\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}=

\displaystyle\psi_{1,k}(z):=\prod_{j=s_{1}+1}^{s}(z-q_{j,k})^{n_{j}}\psi_{k}(\tau_{k}z)\to\prod_{j=s_{1}+1}^{s}(z-q_{j})^{n_{j}}\psi_{0}(0):=\psi_{0}^{(1)}(z),

\overline{B}_{\delta_{1}}
as

In case

Similarly if

The proof of Theorem

, Theorem

and Theorem

To prove Theorem
4

we recall that a Riemann surface of genus
\mathfrak{g}=2
is
hyperelliptic
, i.e.
it admits a unique biholomorphic involution:

\displaystyle j:X\to X

(cf.
[
39
]
,
[
17
]
), with
exactly
six
distinct fixed points, i.e.

\{q\in X:j(q)=q\}.

We recall from
[
48
]
that, for genus
\mathfrak{g}=2
and
[\beta]\neq 0
, the functional
F_{t}
is always bounded from below, but possibly not coercive.

Moreover, the infimum of
F_{t}
is
\underline{not}
attained iff
\xi_{t_{k}}
blows-up, for any given sequence
t_{k}\to 0^{+}
, and it admits exactly one blow-up point and the ”concentration” property holds. Thus, the blow-up divisor takes the form:
D=q,
for suitable
q\in X,
and by Theorem
D
we have that:
\tau(q)=[\beta]_{\mathbb{P}}.

By using the equivariance of
F_{t}
as establish in Appendix 2, we shall show that the blow-up point
q
must be one of the fixed points of
j,

i.e.
j(q)=q
, and moreover it is uniquely identified, independently on the choice of the given sequence
t_{k}\to 0^{+}
.

To this purpose, we consider the pullback map:

\displaystyle j^{*}:A^{0,1}(X,E)\to A^{0,1}(X,E),

defined in Appendix 2 and used in Proposition
6.2
to show the following:

if
\beta_{0}\in A^{0,1}(X,E)
is harmonic (with respect to
g_{X}
) then
j^{*}(\beta_{0})=\beta_{0}
.

Consequently, by using this fact together with the invariance of the Donaldson functional, as established in Proposition
6.1
of the Appendix 2,
we deduce that,

\displaystyle F_{t}(u_{t},\eta_{t})=F_{t}(u_{t}\circ j,j^{*}(\eta_{t})).

In other words,
(u_{t}\circ j,j^{*}(\eta_{t}))
is also a minimizer for
F_{t}
, and due to the uniqueness of the minimizer we conclude that,

\displaystyle u_{t}=u_{t}\circ j\text{ and }\eta_{t}=j^{*}(\eta_{t}).

Consequently,

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}=e^{u_{t}}*_{E}(\beta_{0}+\bar{\partial}\eta_{t})=e^{u_{t}\circ j}*_{E}(j^{*}(\beta_{0})+\bar{\partial}j^{*}(\eta_{t}))\quad\text{ and }\quad\xi_{t}=\xi_{t}\circ j.

Therefore, if along a sequence
t_{k}\to 0^{+}
, we have that
\xi_{t_{k}}
blows-up at the point
q
, then by the above identity, we see that
\xi_{t_{k}}
blows-up also at
j(q),
and by the uniqueness of the blow-up point, we conclude that
j(q)=q,
as claimed.
On the other hand, for genus
\mathfrak{g}=2
the Kodaira map is two-to-one (see
[
48
]
) in the sense that,
\tau(q_{1})=\tau(q_{2})

iff
q_{1}=q_{2}
or
q_{1}=j(q_{2}).
Therefore
\tau^{-1}([\beta]_{\mathbb{P}})=\{q\},
hence the blow-up point is uniquely identified independently on the chosen sequence
t_{k}\to 0^{+}.

With this information, part (i) of Theorem
4
follow immediately. While part (ii) is a direct consequence of Theorem
E
.

At this point we focus on the proof of Theorem
3
and Theorem
5
.

Thus, from now on we assume that
\xi_{k}
blows up with corresponding blow-up divisor:

6.1
Appendix 1

Throughout this section we use complex coordinates.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\psi:=\psi(z)
satisfy:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}-\Delta\psi=|z^{2}-1|^{2}e^{\psi}~~~\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\\
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|z^{2}-1|^{2}e^{\psi}\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}=16\pi.\end{array}\right.

then,

\displaystyle\psi(z)=\ln\frac{8|A|^{2}}{(|z|^{2}+|Az^{2}+Bz+A|^{2})^{2}},

(6.4)

for any
A,B\in\mathbb{C}
with
A\neq 0
. In particular,

\displaystyle\psi(\frac{1}{z})=\psi(z)+8\ln|z|.

(6.5)

Proof.

We notice that the function
\varphi(z):=\psi(z)+2\ln|z^{2}-1|
satisfies:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}-\Delta\varphi=e^{\varphi}-4\pi\delta_{p=1}-4\pi\delta_{p=-1}~~~\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\\
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{\varphi}\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}=16\pi.\end{array}\right.

Hence, by Liouville formula
[
32
]
, we know that

\displaystyle\varphi(z)=\ln\frac{8|f^{\prime}(z)|^{2}}{(1+|f(z)|^{2})^{2}}

(6.6)

with
f
a rational function on
\mathbb{C}
with critical points exactly
\pm 1
of multiplicity
1
. Moreover, the integral condition in (
6.1
) implies that
f
must have degree 2. Moreover, recall that (
6.6
) is invariant if we replace
f
with the function:
\frac{\gamma f+e}{-\overline{e}f+\overline{\gamma}}\,\,
for given
\gamma,e\in\mathbb{C}
satisfying:
|\gamma|^{2}+|e|^{2}=1.
Therefore, without loss of generality we can take:
f(z)=\frac{az^{2}+bz+c}{\alpha z+\beta},
with
a\neq 0
and
\alpha\neq 0
.

By direct calculations, we see that
f^{\prime}
admits simple zeroes at
\pm 1
, if and only if
\beta=0
and
a=c
. Namely, by setting
A:=\frac{a}{\alpha}\neq 0
,
B:=\frac{b}{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}
. We find:
f(z)=A(z+\frac{1}{z})+B
. Clearly,
f(z)=f(\frac{1}{z})
, which should not be surprising, since all the critical points of
f
lie on the unit circle, and so (up to equivalence)
f
must be symmetric with respect to such circle.

Consequently,
f^{\prime}(z)=A\left(1-\frac{1}{z^{2}}\right)
and therefore:

\displaystyle\psi(z)+2\ln|1-z^{2}|=\varphi(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{8|A|^{2}|z^{2}-1|^{2}}{|z|^{4}(1+|A(z+\frac{1}{z})+B|^{2})^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{8|A|^{2}|z^{2}-1|^{2}}{|z|^{4}(1+|A(z+\frac{1}{z})+B|^{2})^{2}}\right),

and we deduce that
\psi
is given by (
6.4
), from which (
6.5
) can be easily checked.

∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\psi
satisfy (
6.1
), then

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{\psi(z)}(z^{2}-1)\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}=-8\pi.

Proof.

Let us prove first that the given integral is real, in the sense that it admits a vanishing imaginary part.

By virtue of (
6.5
), we use the change of variable
z=\frac{1}{w}
to find:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{\psi(z)}z^{2}\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{\psi(\frac{1}{w})}\frac{1}{w^{2}}\frac{1}{|w|^{4}}\frac{i}{2}dw\wedge d\bar{w}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{\psi(w)}\frac{1}{w^{2}}|w|^{4}\frac{i}{2}dw\wedge d\bar{w}=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{\psi(w)}\bar{w}^{2}\frac{i}{2}dw\wedge d\bar{w}.

That is,
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{\psi(z)}z^{2}\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}
coincides with its complex conjugate, and so,

\displaystyle\text{Im}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{\psi(z)}z^{2}\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}\right)=0.

As a consequence we also deduce that:

\text{Im}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{\psi(z)}(z^{2}-1)\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}\right)=0.

Next, by setting
z:=x+iy
, in cartesian coordinate we find:

\displaystyle\text{Re}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{\psi(z)}(z^{2}-1)\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{\psi}(x^{2}-1-y^{2})dxdy.

So by setting:
R(x,y)=|(x+iy)^{2}-1|^{2}=(x^{2}-y^{2}-1)^{2}+(2xy)^{2}
and
\beta:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}R(x,y)e^{\psi}dxdy
, we obtain that :

\displaystyle-\Delta\psi=R(x,y)e^{\psi}\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\text{ and }\beta=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}R(x,y)e^{\psi}dxdy=8.

Therefore, we can apply Theorem 2 of
[
8
]
and obtain the following useful identity:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(x\frac{\partial R}{\partial x}+y\frac{\partial R}{\partial y}\right)e^{\psi}dxdy=\pi\beta(\beta-4).

By straightforward calculations we find:

\displaystyle x\frac{\partial R}{\partial x}+y\frac{\partial R}{\partial y}=4(R(x,y)+x^{2}-y^{2}-1)=4(R(x,y)+\text{ Re}(z^{2}-1)),

and consequently,

\displaystyle\pi\beta(\beta-4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(x\frac{\partial R}{\partial x}+y\frac{\partial R}{\partial y}\right)e^{\psi}=4\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}R(x,y)e^{\psi}+4\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{\psi}\text{Re}(z^{2}-1)\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 8\pi\beta+4\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{\psi}\text{Re}(z^{2}-1)\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}.

Thus, we obtain:

4\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{\psi}\text{Re}(z^{2}-1)\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}=\pi\beta(\beta-4)-8\pi\beta=\pi\beta(\beta-12),

with
\beta=8
. In other words,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{\psi(z)}\text{Re}(z^{2}-1)dzd\bar{z}=-8\pi

as claimed.

∎

6.2
Appendix 2: Invariance of Donaldson functional

Let
X
and
Y
be two Riemann surfaces with genus
\mathfrak{g}\geq 2
, and suppose there exists:

f:X\to Y\text{ a biholomorphic map.}

We denote by
g_{X}
and
g_{Y}
the unique hyperbolic metric (compatible with the complex structure) in
X
and
Y
respectively.
Hence

\displaystyle f:(X,g_{X})\to(Y,g_{Y})

defines an orientation-preserving isometry.

In holomorphic
z
-coordinates on
X
and
w
-coordinates on
Y
as in (
2.3
), we have:

\displaystyle g_{X}=e^{2u_{X}}|dz|^{2}\text{ and }g_{Y}=e^{2u_{Y}}|dw|^{2},

with
u_{X}
and
u_{Y}
smooth functions on
X
and
Y
respectively. So that the isometry condition states that,

\displaystyle e^{2u_{X}(z)}=e^{2u_{Y}(f(z))}|f^{\prime}(z)|^{2},

(6.7)

and the following holds for the area elements:

\displaystyle dA_{X}(z)=\frac{i}{2}e^{2u_{X}(z)}dz\wedge d\bar{z}=\frac{i}{2}e^{2u_{Y}(f(z))}|f^{\prime}(z)|^{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}

\displaystyle dA_{Y}(w)=\frac{i}{2}e^{2u_{Y}(w)}dw\wedge d\bar{w}.

Also, we denoted by
E_{X}=T_{X}^{1,0}
and
E_{Y}=T_{Y}^{1,0}
the
holomorphic tangent bundles relative to
X
and
Y
respectively,
with hermitian products

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{E_{X}},

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{E_{Y}}
and norms
\|\cdot\|_{E_{X}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{E_{X}}
induced by
g_{X},
and
g_{Y}
respectively.

Next, we consider the pullback
f^{*}
of the map
f
on the space of Beltrami differentials:

\displaystyle f^{*}:A^{0,1}(Y,E_{Y})\to A^{0,1}(X,E_{X})

defined as follows: for
\beta\in A^{0,1}(Y,E_{Y})
and for
x\in X
, we take
v\in(T^{0,1}_{X})_{x}
, then

\displaystyle(f^{*}(\beta))_{x}[v]:=(df^{-1})_{y}(\beta_{y}(\overline{(df)_{x}[\bar{v}]})),\text{ with }y=f(x).

Claim 1:

\displaystyle\|f^{*}(\beta)\|_{E_{X}}=\|\beta\|_{E_{Y}},~~\forall\beta\in A^{0,1}(Y,E_{Y}).

(6.8)

To establish (
6.8
), for any
x_{0}\in X
and
y_{0}=f(x_{0})\in Y
, we use holomorphic
z
-coordinates around
x_{0}
and holomorphic
w
-coordinates around
y_{0},
as in (
2.3
).

Therefore we can write:
\beta=\beta(w)d\bar{w}\frac{\partial}{\partial w}
in a small ball around the origin. Hence, in the
z
-coordinates around the origin, we have:

\displaystyle(f^{*}(\beta))_{z}=(\beta(f(z))\bar{f^{\prime}}(z)(f^{-1})^{\prime}(f(z))d\bar{z}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}=\beta(f(z))\frac{\bar{f^{\prime}}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}d\bar{z}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}.

(6.9)

Consequently, by (
2.12
) we have:

\displaystyle\|\beta_{y_{0}}\|_{E_{Y}}=|\beta(0)|\text{ and }\|f^{*}(\beta)_{x_{0}}\|_{E_{X}}=|\beta(f(0))|=|\beta(0)|=\|\beta_{y_{0}}\|_{E_{Y}}

and (
6.8
) is established.

Claim 2:
If
\beta\in A^{0,1}(Y,E_{Y})
is harmonic with respect to
g_{Y}
then
f^{*}(\beta)\in A^{0,1}(X,E_{X})
is harmonic with respect to
g_{X}
.

To establish Claim 2, we denote still by
f^{*}
the following pullback by the map f :

\displaystyle f^{*}:A^{0}(E_{Y}^{*})\to A^{0}(E_{X}^{*})

defined locally by setting:
f^{*}(b(w)dw)=b(f(z))f^{\prime}(z)dz.

When
f
is biholomorphic we see that the tensor product of the above map
f^{*}
, namely:

\displaystyle f^{*}\otimes f^{*}:A^{0}(E_{Y}^{*}\otimes E_{Y}^{*})\to A^{0}(E_{X}^{*}\otimes E_{X}^{*}),

maps the space
C_{2}(Y)
into
C_{2}(X).
At this point Claim 2 follows esaily by the commutation relation :

\displaystyle(f^{*}\otimes f^{*})(*_{y})=*_{x}\circ f^{*}.

(6.10)

To check (
6.10
), for a Beltrami differential
\beta
on
Y
we use the local holomorphic coordinates above and set:
\beta=\beta(w)d\bar{w}\frac{\partial}{\partial w},
then by formula (
2.11
) we have:

\displaystyle*_{y}\left(\beta(w)d\bar{w}\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\right)=-\frac{i}{2}\overline{\beta}(w)e^{2u_{Y}(w)}(dw)^{2},

clearly a similar local formula holds for the local expression
*_{x}
applied to a Beltrami differential on
X.

Therefore, by using (
6.7
) and (
6.9
), locally we derive:

\displaystyle(f^{*}\otimes f^{*})(*_{y}\beta)=-\frac{i}{2}\bar{\beta}(f(z))e^{2u_{Y}(f(z))}(f^{\prime}(z))^{2}(dz)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2}\bar{\beta}(f(z))\frac{(f^{\prime}(z))^{2}}{|f^{\prime}(z)|^{2}}e^{2u_{Y}(f(z))}|f^{\prime}(z)|^{2}(dz)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2}\overline{\left(\beta(f(z))\frac{\bar{f^{\prime}}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}\right)}e^{2u_{X}(z)}(dz)^{2}=*_{x}(f^{*}(\beta)).

Next, still by
f^{*}
we denote the pullback by the map f acting on sections, namely:

\displaystyle f^{*}:A^{0}(E_{Y})\to A^{0}(E_{X})

defined as follows:

\text{if }\eta\in A^{0}(E_{Y})\text{ then }(f^{*}(\eta))_{x}=(df^{-1})_{y}(\eta_{y})\text{ for }x\in X\text{ and }y=f(x).

Claim 3:

\forall\eta\in A^{0}(E_{Y})
, we have:

\displaystyle\bar{\partial}(f^{*}(\eta))=f^{*}(\bar{\partial}\eta)

(6.11)

To establish (
6.11
) we use again the local expression:
\eta=a(w)\frac{\partial}{\partial w}
and compute:

\displaystyle(f^{*}(\eta))_{z}=(df^{-1})(f(z))a(f(z))\frac{\partial}{\partial z}=\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(z)}a(f(z))\frac{\partial}{\partial z}.

Since
f
is biholomorphic and in view of (
6.9
), we find:

\displaystyle\bar{\partial}(f^{*}(\eta))_{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(z)}\frac{\partial a}{\partial\bar{w}}(f(z))\frac{\partial\bar{f}}{\partial\bar{z}}\right)d\bar{z}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial a}{\partial\bar{w}}(f(z))\frac{\bar{f^{\prime}}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}d\bar{z}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}=f^{*}(\bar{\partial}\eta).

Claim 4:

\forall u\in C^{\infty}(Y)
and
\forall x\in X
, we have:

|\nabla(u\circ f)|_{g_{X}}(x)=|\nabla(u)|_{g_{Y}}(f(x)).

(6.12)

To establish (
6.12
) we use the definition of gradient together with the fact that
f
is an isometry with respect to the hyperbolic metrics,
g_{X}
and
g_{Y}
respectively. Therefore,
\forall x\in X
and
\forall v\in E_{x},
we have:

\begin{array}[]{l}<df_{x}(\nabla(u\circ f)(x)),df_{x}(v)>_{g_{Y}}=<\nabla(u\circ f)(x)),v>_{g_{X}}=\\
d(u\circ f)_{x}(v)=du_{f(x)}(df_{x}(v))=<\nabla(u)(f(x)),df_{x}(v)>_{g_{Y}}.\end{array}

Hence
df_{x}(\nabla(u\circ f)(x))=\nabla(u)(f(x))
and consequently:
|\nabla(u)|_{g_{Y}}(f(x))=|df_{x}(\nabla(u\circ f)(x))|_{g_{Y}}=|\nabla(u\circ f)|_{g_{X}}(x)

and the claim is established.

At this point, we are ready to establish the invariance of the Donaldson functional defined in (
3.2
).

For harmonic
\beta_{0}\in A^{0,1}(Y,E_{Y})\setminus\{0\}
, consider the Donaldson functional relative to the pair
(Y,[\beta_{0}])
:

\displaystyle F_{t}^{\beta_{0}}[Y](u,\eta)=\int_{Y}\left(\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}_{g_{Y}}}{4}-u+te^{u}+4e^{u}\|\beta_{0}+\bar{\partial}\eta\|^{2}\right)dA_{Y},

with
(u,\eta)\in H^{1}(Y)\times W^{1,2}(Y,E_{Y})
.

Thus, by Claim 2, it is well defined the Donaldson functional relative to the pair
(X,f^{*}(\beta_{0}))
as follows:

\displaystyle F_{t}^{f^{*}(\beta_{0})}[X](v,\xi)=\int_{X}\left(\frac{|\nabla v|^{2}_{g_{X}}}{4}-v+te^{v}+4e^{v}\|f^{*}(\beta_{0})+\bar{\partial}\xi\|^{2}_{E_{X}}\right)dA_{X},

with
(v,\xi)\in H^{1}(X)\times W^{1,2}(X,E_{X})
. The following holds:

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
f:X\to Y
biholomorphic then

\displaystyle F_{t}^{\beta_{0}}[Y](u,\eta)=F_{t}^{f^{*}(\beta_{0})}[X](u\circ f,f^{*}(\eta)),

\forall(u,\eta)\in H^{1}(Y)\times W^{1,2}(Y,E_{Y}).

Proof.

In view of (
6.7
), (
6.8
), Claim 2, claim 4 and (
6.11
), we derive:

\displaystyle\int_{X}\left(\frac{|\nabla(u\circ f)|^{2}_{g_{X}}}{4}-(u\circ f)+te^{u\circ f}+4e^{u\circ f}\|f^{*}(\beta_{0})+\bar{\partial}f^{*}(\eta)\|^{2}\right)dA_{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{X}\left(\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}_{g_{Y}}(f(x))}{4}-u(f(x)+te^{u(f(x))}+4e^{u(f(x))}\|f^{*}(\beta_{0})+\bar{\partial}f^{*}(\eta)\|^{2}_{E_{X}}\right)dA_{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{Y}\left(\frac{|\nabla u(y)|^{2}_{g_{Y}}}{4}-u(y)+te^{u(y)}+4e^{u(y)}\|\beta_{0}+\bar{\partial}\eta\|^{2}_{E_{Y}}\right)dA_{Y}=F_{t}^{\beta_{0}}[Y](u,\eta),

\forall(u,\eta)\in H^{1}(X)\times W^{1,2}(Y,E_{Y})
.
∎

The invariance property of Proposition
6.1
is crucial to establish Theorem
4
.
It will be applied to the Riemann surface
X
of genus
2,
where the biholomorphism is given by the unique involution:

\displaystyle j:X\to X

(cf.
[
39
]
,
[
17
]
). Moreover in the proof of Theorem
4
also we need the following:

Proposition 6.2
.

If
\beta_{0}\in A^{0,1}(X,E)\setminus\{0\}
is harmonic with respect to
g_{X}
then

\displaystyle j^{*}(\beta_{0})=\beta_{0}.

Proof.

Recall that
j
admits exactly six fixed points, say:
\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{6}\}
. Furthermore, since
dj_{q}
has order
2
then
j^{\prime}(q)=\pm 1
.

Therefore, by means of (
6.9
), we see that

\displaystyle(j^{*}(\beta_{0}))_{q}=(\beta_{0})_{q},\forall q\in\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{6}\}.

As a consequence, the harmonic Beltrami differential

vanishes at those six points.

Thus also the holomorphic quadratic differential:

\displaystyle\alpha^{0}=*_{E}(j^{*}(\beta_{0})-\beta_{0})\in H^{0}(X,E^{*}\otimes E^{*})=C_{2}(X)

vanishes at those six points as well. But we have shown in (

) that

admits degree

(for

) and so

must be identically zero. That implies that,

as claimed.
∎

6.3
Appendix 3: Secant varieties.

Our goal here is to establish Proposition
2.1
.

Since the statement of Proposition
2.1
is well known for
\mathfrak{g}=2
(i.e.
\nu=1
), from now on we suppose that
X
admits genus
\mathfrak{g}\geq 3.
As a consequence, the corresponding Kodaira map
\tau
is one to one, moreover
\tau(X)
is a non degenerate complex curve.

For this purpose let,

N=3(\mathfrak{g}-1)\text{ and }V:=C_{2}(X).

For fixed
\nu\in\{1,\ldots,2\mathfrak{g}-3\},
let
G_{N-\nu}(V)
be the Grassmannian of
N-\nu
dimensional complex subspaces of
V
and define the map:

\Psi:X^{(\nu)}\to G_{N-\nu}(V)\,\,\text{ with }\Psi(D)=Q(D).

(6.13)

It is well known that
\Psi
is holomorphic, but for the reader convenience we will give a direct proof of this fact.
Recall that, to give a structure of complex manifold on
G_{N-\nu}(V)
, we choose a basis
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}
of
V
and identify
G_{N-\nu}(V)
with
G_{N-\nu}(\mathbb{C}^{N})
. The structure on
G_{N-\nu}(\mathbb{C}^{N})
is the quotient of
\Omega
modulo the group
GL(\nu)
acting by left multiplication, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{\nu\times N}
is the open set of
\nu\times N
complex matrices of maximal rank. The holomorphic quotient map
q:\Omega\to G_{N-\nu}(\mathbb{C}^{N})
is defined by setting:
A\to\text{Ker}(A)
. Given
A\in\mathbb{C}^{\nu\times N}
a
\nu\times N
complex matrix, we denote by
A^{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{\nu}
the
j
-th column of
A
, for
j=1,\ldots,N
. Fix a set
I
of
\nu
positive integers, namely:
I=\{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\cdots<i_{\nu}\leq N\},
let
A_{I}
the
\nu\times\nu
matrix formed with the columns
A^{i_{1}},A^{i_{2}},\ldots,A^{i_{\nu}}
and define:
\Omega_{I}:=\{A\in\Omega:\det(A_{I})\neq 0\}
. Then
q(A)=q((A_{I})^{-1}A)
for every
A\in\Omega_{I}
. Consider the subset
\Lambda_{I}\subset\Omega_{I}
given as follows:
\Lambda_{I}=\{A\in\Omega_{I}:A_{I}=Id_{\nu}\}
with
Id_{\nu}
the identity metrix, then the restriction of
q
on
\Lambda_{I}
gives a local holomorphic chart of
G_{N-\nu}(\mathbb{C}^{N})
.

Now let
p_{0}=(x_{1}^{0},\ldots,x_{\nu}^{0})\in X^{(\nu)},
by (
2
) we find
\alpha^{0}\in H^{0}(X,\otimes^{2}(K_{X}))
which does not vanish at
x_{j}^{0}
for all
1\leq j\leq\nu.

Hence, we can choose a small open neighborhood
\textbf{W}=W_{1}\times W_{2}\cdots\times W_{\nu}
of
p_{0},
such that
\alpha^{0}
is nowhere zero on each
W_{j}
, for
1\leq j\leq\nu.

We consider the map
\tilde{\Psi}:\textbf{W}\to\Omega
so that, for given indices
1\leq j\leq\nu
and
1\leq h\leq N
and for
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\nu})
, we set:

\displaystyle[\tilde{\Psi}(x)]_{j,h}=\left(\frac{\alpha_{h}}{\alpha^{0}}\right)^{0}(x_{j}),\ldots,\left(\frac{\alpha_{h}}{\alpha^{0}}\right)^{\nu_{j}-1}(x_{j}),

\displaystyle\text{ \ if the point \ }x_{j}\text{ appears }\nu_{j}\text{ times in }x;

where the upper index denotes the holomorphic derivative and
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}\}
is the fixed basis in
V=C_{2}(X)
. Observe that with the given local holomorphic charts above, we have
\Psi|_{\textbf{W}}=q\circ\tilde{\Psi}.
In order to show that the map
\Psi
is holomorphic, we need the following:

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
U:D(0,\varepsilon)\to\mathbb{C}^{N}
be a holomorphic map where
D(0,\varepsilon)\subset\mathbb{C}
is the open disk with center
0
and radius
\varepsilon
. Let
z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{k}
be distinct points in
D(0,\varepsilon)
, and define inductively the following vectors:

\begin{array}[]{l}F_{1}(z_{1}):=U(z_{1}),\\
F_{j+1}(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{j-1},z_{j},z_{j+1}):=\frac{F_{j}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j-1},z_{j+1})-F_{j}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j-1},z_{j})}{z_{j+1}-z_{j}}.\end{array}

We have:

1)

\text{span}\{F_{1}(z_{1}),F_{2}(z_{1},z_{2}),\ldots,F_{k}(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{k})\}=\text{span}\{U(z_{1}),U(z_{2}),\ldots,U(z_{k})\}
,

2)

\displaystyle\lim_{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j})\to(0,\ldots,0)}F_{j}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j})=\frac{U^{j-1}(0)}{(j-1)!},

for all
1\leq j\leq k
.

Proof.

First, we show the statement 1). For each
j\geq 1
, we claim that we can find suitable functions
c_{h},1\leq h\leq j
such that

\displaystyle F_{j}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j})=c_{1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j})U(z_{1})+\;\ldots\;+c_{j}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j})U(z_{j}),

with
c_{j}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j})\neq 0
. Indeed, while this is clear for
j=1
, if by induction we assume it is true for
j
, then by using the definition, for
j+1
we find that,

\displaystyle c_{j+1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j+1})=\frac{c_{j}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j-1},z_{j+1})}{z_{j+1}-z_{j}}\neq 0.

Hence, each
F_{j}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j})
is in the
\text{span}(U(z_{1}),U(z_{2}),\ldots,U(z_{j})\}
. To show the vice-versa, again by induction, it suffices to show that, for
1\leq j\leq k
then
U(z_{j+1})
is in the
\text{span}\{F_{1}(z_{1}),F_{2}(z_{1},z_{2}),\ldots,F_{j+1}(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{j+1})\}.

But, using again the definition, we have:

\displaystyle F_{j+1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j-1},z_{j},z_{j+1}):=\frac{F_{j}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j-1},z_{j+1})-F_{j}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j-1},z_{j})}{z_{j+1}-z_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{z_{j+1}-z_{j}}\big(c_{1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j-1},z_{j+1})U(z_{1})+c_{2}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j-1},z_{j+1})U(z_{2})

\displaystyle+\cdots c_{j-1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j-1},z_{j+1})U(z_{j-1})\big)+\left(\frac{c_{j}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j-1},z_{j+1})}{z_{j+1}-z_{j}}\right)U(z_{j+1})

-\frac{F_{j}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j-1},z_{j})}{z_{j+1}-z_{j}},

and by the previous argument, the term:

\displaystyle\frac{F_{j}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j-1},z_{j})}{z_{j+1}-z_{j}}\in\text{span}\{U(z_{1}),U(z_{2}),\ldots,U(z_{j})\},

with

\left(\frac{c_{j}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j-1},z_{j+1})}{z_{j+1}-z_{j}}\right)\neq 0.

Since,
by the induction assumption we know that,

\text{span}\{U(z_{1}),U(z_{2}),\ldots,U(z_{j})\}\subset\text{span}\{F_{1}(z_{1}),F_{2}(z_{1},z_{2}),\ldots,F_{j+1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j+1})\}
, by means of the identity above, we conclude that,

U(z_{j+1})\in\text{span}\{F_{1}(z_{1}),F_{2}(z_{1},z_{2}),\ldots,F_{j+1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j+1})\}
as claimed, and the proof of statement 1) is completed.

To prove 2), we will show that, for given
2\leq j\leq k
and
\mathbf{t}:=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{j-1})
there exist polynomials
\lambda_{s}^{j}(\mathbf{t})
with
1\leq s\leq k
and a polynomial
Q_{j}(\mathbf{t})
such that
\lambda_{s}^{j}(\mathbf{t})

and
Q(\mathbf{t})
are strictly positive on the open cube
(0,1)^{j-1}
, they do not dependent on
U
and:

\displaystyle F_{j}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j})=\int_{[0,1]^{j-1}}U^{j-1}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{j}\lambda_{s}^{j}(\mathbf{t})z_{s}\right)Q_{j}(\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{t},

(6.14)

with
d\mathbf{t}:=dt_{1}\cdots dt_{j-1}
. Since each derivative of
U
is bounded on compact sets of
D(0,\varepsilon)
then statement 2) follows from the above formula and from the dominated convergence theorem, as we shall see also that:
\frac{1}{(j-1)!}=:\int_{[0,1]^{j-1}}Q(\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{t}
.

To show (
6.14
), we proceed by induction as we have:

F_{2}(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{U(z_{2})-U(z_{1})}{z_{2}-z_{1}}=\frac{\int_{0}^{1}U(\gamma(t_{1}))\gamma^{\prime}(t_{1})dt_{1}}{z_{2}-z_{1}}

with
\gamma(t)=tz_{2}+(1-t)z_{1}
. However
\gamma^{\prime}(t)=z_{2}-z_{1}
, and therefore, if we let
\lambda_{1}^{2}(t_{1})=1-t_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}^{2}(t_{1})=t_{1}
, and
Q_{2}(t_{1})\equiv 1,
we get the formula above for
j=2
. Next, assume that the formula is valid for
j
. We let
Z^{\prime}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j-1})
,
\lambda^{\mathbf{j}}(\mathbf{t}):=(\lambda_{1}^{j}(\mathbf{t}),\ldots,\lambda_{j-1}^{j}(\mathbf{t}))
, and
\langle\lambda^{j},Z^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{s=1}^{j-1}\lambda_{s}^{j}(\mathbf{t})z_{s}
, with
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{j-1})
. We find:

\displaystyle F_{j+1}(Z^{\prime},z_{j},z_{j+1})=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\int_{[0,1]^{j-1}}\left(U^{j-1}\left(\langle\lambda^{j},Z^{\prime}\rangle+\lambda_{j}^{j}(\mathbf{t})z_{j+1}\right)-U^{j-1}\left(\langle\lambda^{j},Z^{\prime}\rangle+\lambda^{j}_{j}(\mathbf{t})z_{j}\right)\right)Q_{j}(\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{t}\right)}{z_{j+1}-z_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{j-1}}\frac{U^{j-1}\left(\langle\lambda^{j},Z^{\prime}\rangle+\lambda_{j}^{j}(\mathbf{t})z_{j+1}\right)-U^{j-1}\left(\langle\lambda^{j},Z^{\prime}\rangle+\lambda^{j}_{j}(\mathbf{t})z_{j}\right)}{\lambda_{j}^{j}(\mathbf{t})(z_{j+1}-z_{j})}(\lambda_{j}^{j}Q_{j})(\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{j-1}}\int_{[0,1]}U^{j}(\gamma(\tau))(\lambda_{j}^{j}Q_{j})(\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{t}d\tau,

with

\displaystyle\gamma(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau(\langle\lambda^{j},Z^{\prime}\rangle+\lambda_{j}^{j}(\mathbf{t})z_{j+1})+(1-\tau)(\langle\lambda^{j},Z^{\prime}\rangle+\lambda_{j}^{j}(\mathbf{t})z_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\lambda^{j},Z^{\prime}\rangle+\tau\lambda_{j}^{j}(\mathbf{t})z_{j+1}+(1-\tau)\lambda_{j}^{j}(\mathbf{t})z_{j}.

Consequently, we obtain the formula above also for
2\leq j\leq k
, by setting:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}\lambda_{s}^{j+1}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{j-1},t_{j})=\lambda_{s}^{j}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{j-1}),\text{for }1\leq s\leq j-1,\\
\lambda_{j}^{j+1}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{j})=(1-t_{j})\lambda_{j}^{j}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{j-1}),\\
\lambda_{j+1}^{j+1}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{j-1},t_{j})=t_{j}\lambda_{j}^{j}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{j-1}).\\
Q_{j+1}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{j})=\lambda_{j}^{j}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{j-1})Q_{j}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{j-1}),\\
Q_{2}(t_{1})\equiv 1\\
\lambda_{1}^{2}(t_{1})=1-t_{1},\lambda_{2}^{2}(t_{1})=t_{1},\end{array}\right.

So by using induction, we find that,

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}^{j}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{j-1})=t_{1}t_{2}\cdots t_{j-1},

and

\displaystyle Q_{j}(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{j-1})=t_{1}^{j-2}t_{2}^{j-3}t_{3}^{j-4}\cdots t_{j-2}.

In particular, we obtain:
\int_{[0,1]^{j-1}}Q_{j}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{j-1})dt_{1}\cdots dt_{j-1}=\frac{1}{(j-1)!}
.

∎

Lemma 6.4
.

If
1\leq\nu\leq g-1
the sub-variety
\tilde{\Sigma}_{\nu}
in (

) coincides with the
\nu
-secant variety

of

Proof.

Let
D\in X^{(}{\nu})
be an effective divisor in
X

\nu

1\leq\nu\leq\mathfrak{g}-1.
We set:

Q(D)^{\perp}=\{\beta\in V^{*}:\,\,\int_{X}\beta\wedge\alpha=0\,\,\ \forall\alpha\in Q(D)\},

so that
\tilde{\Sigma}_{\nu}
is a closed subvariety (in Zariski topology) formed by union of all the projective subspaces
\pi(Q(D)^{\perp}),
for any
D\in X^{(\nu)},
and it is

On the other hand, if
D=\sum_{j=1,...,\nu}p_{j}\in X^{(}{\nu})
then

Q(D)\subseteq Q(p_{j})\ \mbox{so}\ Q(p_{j})^{\perp}\subseteq Q(D)^{\perp}\,\,\forall\,j\in\{1,\ldots,\nu\},

and therefore,
\tau(p_{j})\in\pi(Q(D)^{\perp}),\,\,\forall\,j\in\{1,\ldots,\nu\}.

As a consequence,
\pi(span(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{\nu}))\subseteq\pi(Q(D)^{\perp}),

)=
\nu-1

) (recall (
2
)) we conclude that,

\pi(span(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{\nu}))=\pi(Q(D)^{\perp}).

It is easy to show that, if
