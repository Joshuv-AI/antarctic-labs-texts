Impurities in graphene and their influence on the Casimir interaction
N. Khusnutdinov, D. Vassilevich

Abstract
We study the influence of impurities in graphene described by a scattering rate Γ \Gamma on the Casimir interaction between graphene and an ideal conductor or between two identical sheets of graphene at zero temperature and chemical potential. To this end, we compute the polarization tensor of quasiparticles in graphene and corresponding conductivities for TE and TM channels. The Casimir energy density is evaluated with the help of the Lifshitz formula. We find that depending on the value of mass gap parameter the presence of Γ \Gamma may lead to a slight damping or to a considerable enhancement of the Casimir interaction.



I
Introduction

The Casimir effect
Bordag et al. 2009a
;
Milton 2004
is an interaction of neutral bodies due to quantum vacuum fluctuations. The study of Casimir interaction between advanced materials is a new and promising area of research
Woods et al. 2016
. On one hand, the unusual electronic properties of these materials lead to interesting effects on the Casimir force. On the other hand, the improved quality of Casimir experiments makes them a useful tool for exploration of the materials themselves.

The Dirac materials (where the quasiparticles obey a quasirelativistic Dirac-type equation at sufficiently low energies) provide us with an example of interplay between Quantum Field Theory and Condensed Matter. Graphene is a prominent representative of this family
Castro Neto et al. 2009
;
Das Sarma et al. 2011
. Dealing with the Dirac materials it is natural to describe the interaction with electromagnetic field by the polarization tensor of quasiparticles and use this tensor to calculate the Casimir interaction. In the case of graphene, such an approach was used in
Bordag et al. 2009b
and in
Fialkovsky et al. 2011
at zero and non-zero temperature, respectively. Remarkably, the polarization tensor approach to Casimir interaction of graphene was the only one which was confirmed at experiments
Banishev et al. 2013
;
Klimchitskaya et al. 2014
;
Liu et al. 2021a
;
Liu et al. 2021b
.

All real materials contain impurities. Particular form of impurities can vary. Impurities refer to a general form for breaking the cleanliness of pristine materials. A classification of impurities and defects in graphene-like materials may be found in the reviews
Banhart et al. 2010
;
Zhang et al. 2010
;
Araujo et al. 2012
;
Fritz and Vojta 2013
. The two-dimensional nature of graphene decreases the number of possible types of defects and impurities. The point is that it is energetically favourable for adatoms or substitutional impurities to reside outside of the graphene surface. They may be charged
Ando 2006
;
Cheianov and Fal’ko 2006
;
Chen et al. 2008
, magnetic
Fritz and Vojta 2013
, isotopic
Fan et al. 2003
;
Simon et al. 2005
, topological such as pentagons and heptagons
An et al. 2001
;
Zhang et al. 2010
, or be a consequence of imperfections and growth-induced defects like point
Kotakoski et al. 2006
and cluster defects
Banhart et al. 2010
. Intentional impurity is usually called a dopant while the impurity itself can be both intentional and unintentional (accidental). Doping is used to change the physical or chemical properties of a material. Impurities in graphene
Altanhan and Kozal 2012
;
Viola et al. 2018
may transform a linear dispersion near Dirac points to a quadratic one which signifies the appearance of a mass gap induced by impurities. There are different approaches to describing impurities and their impact on the physical properties of materials. The common is a tight-binding model with a shot- or long-range potential
Zhang et al. 2010
, and scattering approach
Ando and Nakanishi 1998
;
Ando et al. 1998
.

With this large variety of the types of impurities in graphene we need a good model which captures the universal properties of impurities while being sufficiently simple for being used in calculation of the polarization tensor. A successful way of describing impurities consists in adding to the propagator of quasiparticles a parameter
\Gamma
which describes the impurity scattering rate. In other words,
\Gamma
is an imaginary part of the fermion self-energy. Such a description has been applied to
graphene mostly in the presence of an external magnetic field in
Gorbar et al. 2002
;
Gusynin and Sharapov 2006
;
Gusynin et al. 2006a
;
Gusynin et al. 2006b
;
Fialkovsky and Vassilevich 2012
. The computations of
Fialkovsky and Vassilevich 2012
are in a very good agreement with the measurements
Crassee et al. 2010
of giant Faraday rotation in graphene. In principle
\Gamma
can depend on the frequency though keeping it constant appeared to be a good approximation. In this work, we neglect the other role of impurities which is their ability to create a non-zero chemical potential
\mu
.

A particular form of impurities which were atoms (mostly sodium) on the surface of graphene and their influence on the Casimir force were considered in
Liu et al. 2021b
;
Liu et al. 2021a
. According to these papers such impurities lead to a mass gap and a non-zero chemical potential of graphene but not to the appearance of impurity scattering described by scattering rate
\Gamma
.

The primary goal of this paper is to study the influence of impurity scattering rate
\Gamma
on the Casimir interaction between graphene and an ideal metal and between two graphene sheets. We restrict ourselves to the case of a zero temperature and vanishing chemical potential. This is a somewhat simplified setting. Therefore, we will not try to compare our results to any existing experiment (they were done at room temperature). Rather, we will clarify some generic features of the Casimir interaction of graphene. Our main conclusions is that turning on
\Gamma
leads to a slight decrease of the Casimir energy density for small masses and to a considerable enhancement thereof for large masses. Thus, taking impurities into account may be essential for analysing precision Casimir measurements.

This paper is organized as follows. In the next Section, we calculate the polarization tensor of quasiparticles in graphene in the Pauli–Villars subtraction scheme. In Section
III
, we analyse the conductivities following from this polarization tensor and discuss their properties which are relevant for the Casimir interaction. The Casimir interaction itself is studied in Section
IV
. Section
V
contains some concluding remarks. We put long formulas for the conductivities in the Appendix. We use the units
\hbar=c=1
.

II
Polarization tensor

To fix our conventions, we start with the Dirac operator

\not{D}=\mathrm{i}\tilde{\gamma}^{i}(\partial_{i}+\mathrm{i}eA_{i})-m,

(1)

which describes the propagation of quasiparticles in graphene. The Latin letters form the middle of Alphabet will are used to denote coordinates of
2+1
dimensional vectors,
i,j,k=0,1,2
. The letters from the beginning of Alphabet will denote spatial components,
a,b,c=1,2
. A twiddle above a vector means that the spatial components are rescaled with the Fermi velocity,

\tilde{\gamma}^{0}=\gamma^{0},\qquad\tilde{\gamma}^{a}=v_{F}\gamma^{a}.

(2)

The Dirac
\gamma
matrices are
8
-dimensional (which corresponds to four generations of fermions in graphene). They satisfy the condition
\gamma^{i}\gamma^{j}+\gamma^{j}\gamma^{i}=2g^{ij}
with the flat metric
g^{ij}=(+,-,-)
. Besides,
\mathrm{tr}\,(\gamma^{i}\gamma^{j})=8g^{ij}
and
\mathrm{tr}\,(\gamma^{i}\gamma^{j}\gamma^{l}\gamma^{k})=8(g^{ij}g^{lk}-g^{il}g^{jk}+g^{ik}g^{jl})
. We will use boldface to denote spatial components of the momenta 3-vectors,
k=(k_{0},\mathbf{k})
, so that
p\cdot k=p_{0}k_{0}-\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{k}
with
\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{k}=k_{a}p_{a}\delta^{ab}
with
\delta^{ab}
being the Kronecker symbol.

The propagator
S(x)
is the kernel of an inverse of a free Dirac operator
\not{D}_{0}=\not{D}_{A=0}
, i.e.
\not{D}^{x}_{0}S(x-y)=\delta^{(3)}(x-y)
. After a Fourier transform

S(x)=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{\mathrm{i}kx}S(k),

(3)

one arrives at the following expression

S(k)=-\frac{k_{0}\gamma^{0}+v_{F}k_{a}\gamma^{a}+m}{k_{0}^{2}-v_{F}^{2}k^{2}-m^{2}}.

(4)

To introduce impurities, characterized by the scattering rate
\Gamma
, and chemical potential
\mu
one shifts the temporal component of the momentum
k
in the propagator (
4
) as
k_{0}\to\widehat{k}_{0}=k_{0}+\mathrm{i}\Gamma\mathrm{sgn}\,(k_{0})+\mu
, (see
Gorbar et al. 2002
;
Gusynin and Sharapov 2006
;
Gusynin et al. 2006b
;
Gusynin et al. 2006a
;
Tkachov 2013
) For the rest of this work we set
\mu=0
. We denote
\widehat{k}=(\widehat{k}_{0},\mathbf{k})
.

The introduction of
\Gamma
into the Quantum Field Theory approach leads to certain difficulties with the gauge invariance. The modification of fermionic propagator can be understood as a consequence of an additional term in the Dirac action containing
\psi^{\dagger}\Gamma\mathrm{sgn}\,(-\mathrm{i}\partial_{0})\psi
. To maintain gauge invariance, each partial derivative has to be accompanied by a gauge field. That is, one should consider the expression
\psi^{\dagger}\Gamma\mathrm{sgn}\,(-\mathrm{i}(\partial_{0}+\mathrm{i}eA_{0}))\psi
. Although it is not clear how one should deal with the sign function of a differential operator, new (and rather complicated) vertexes involving
\psi^{\dagger}
,
\psi
, and
A_{0}
seem to be inevitable. To overcome this difficulty we proceed as in
Holanda et al. 2023
. Namely, we consider only the diagrams without
A_{0}
lines which are thus independent of whichever vertices involving
A_{0}
. I.e., we consider only the spatial components of polarization tensor,

\Pi^{ab}(p)=\mathrm{i}e^{2}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\mathrm{tr}\,\left(S(\widehat{k})\tilde{\gamma}^{a}S(\widehat{k-p})\tilde{\gamma}^{b}\right).

(5)

The full tensor may be recovered, if needed, with the help of the transversality condition.

In the expression (
5
), one can change the integration variable
k\to\tilde{k}
. The Jacobian factor
v_{F}^{-2}
cancels
v_{F}^{2}
coming from
\tilde{\gamma}^{a}
and
\tilde{\gamma}^{b}
. As a result, the whole dependence on (
5
)
v_{F}
remains in the external momentum only, so that

\Pi^{ab}(p)=\Pi^{ab}_{v_{F}=1}(\tilde{p}).

(6)

In other words, to compute the spatial components of polarization tensor it is sufficient to do the computations for
v_{F}=1
and then replace
p
by
\tilde{p}
. Till the end of this section, all computations will be done for
v_{F}=1
. Thus,

\Pi^{ab}(p)=\frac{\mathrm{i}e^{2}}{\pi^{3}}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}k\frac{-\delta^{ab}(m^{2}-\widehat{k}\cdot(\widehat{k-p}))+2k^{a}k^{b}-k^{a}p^{b}-k^{b}p^{a}}{(\widehat{k}^{2}-m^{2})((\widehat{k-p})^{2}-m^{2})}.

(7)

By using the Feynman parametrization this integral can be rewritten as

\Pi^{ab}(p)=\frac{\mathrm{i}e^{2}}{\pi^{3}}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}k\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,\frac{-\delta^{ab}(m^{2}-\widehat{k}\cdot(\widehat{k-p}))+2k^{a}k^{b}-k^{a}p^{b}-k^{b}p^{a}}{(x[\widehat{k}^{2}-m^{2}]+(1-x)[(\widehat{k-p})^{2}-m^{2}])^{2}}.

(8)

The denominator of the integrand reads

\displaystyle(x[\widehat{k}^{2}-m^{2}]+(1-x)[(\widehat{k-p})^{2}-m^{2}])^{2}=(\mathbf{q}^{2}-R)^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{k}-(1-x)\mathbf{p},

(9)

\displaystyle R=x\widehat{k}_{0}^{2}+(1-x)(\widehat{k-p})_{0}^{2}-x(1-x)\mathbf{p}^{2}-m^{2}.

We change the integration variable
\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}\to\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{q}
. Note, that the terms in numerator of (
8
) which are linear in
\mathbf{q}
are integrated to zero. Also, under the integral one may replace
\mathbf{q}^{a}\mathbf{q}^{b}
by
\tfrac{1}{2}\delta^{ab}\mathbf{q}^{2}
. After the integration over
\mathbf{q}
one arrives at the expression

\Pi^{ab}(p)=\frac{\mathrm{i}e^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\int\mathrm{d}k_{0}\frac{\delta^{ab}(m^{2}-\widehat{k}_{0}(\widehat{k-p})_{0}-x(1-x)\mathbf{p}^{2})+2x(1-x)p^{a}p^{b}}{R}.

(10)

To integrate over
k_{0}
one has to split the integration region into a union of intervals where the signs of
k_{0}
and
k_{0}-p_{0}
are constant. If, for simplicity, we restrict ourselves to the case
p_{0}>0
these are the intervals
(-\infty,0)
,
(0,p_{0})
, and
(p_{0},\infty)
. One can easily show that the integrals over the first and the last intervals coincide. We have,

\displaystyle\Pi^{ab}(p)=\frac{\mathrm{i}e^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\left[2\int_{-\infty}^{0}\mathrm{d}k_{0}\,\frac{\delta^{ab}(m^{2}-({k}_{0}-\mathrm{i}\Gamma)({k_{0}-p_{0}}-\mathrm{i}\Gamma)-x(1-x)\mathbf{p}^{2})+2x(1-x)p^{a}p^{b}}{x({k}_{0}-\mathrm{i}\Gamma)^{2}+(1-x)(k_{0}-p_{0}-\mathrm{i}\Gamma)^{2}-x(1-x)\mathbf{p}^{2}-m^{2}}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\left.\int_{0}^{p_{0}}\mathrm{d}k_{0}\,\frac{\delta^{ab}(m^{2}-({k}_{0}+\mathrm{i}\Gamma)({k_{0}-p_{0}}-\mathrm{i}\Gamma)-x(1-x)\mathbf{p}^{2})+2x(1-x)p^{a}p^{b}}{x({k}_{0}+\mathrm{i}\Gamma)^{2}+(1-x)(k_{0}-p_{0}-\mathrm{i}\Gamma)^{2}-x(1-x)\mathbf{p}^{2}-m^{2}}\right].

(11)

The integral over
k_{0}
is divergent, so that we need a regularization and renormalization procedure for which we choose the Pauli–Villars subtraction. We subtract from the integrand in (
11
) the same expression due to a regulator field with a mass
M
, compute the integrals and then send
M\to\infty
. Symbolically,

\Pi^{ab}_{\mathrm{PV}}(p)=\lim_{M\to\infty}(\Pi^{ab}(p,m)-\Pi^{ab}(p,M)).

(12)

We represent the polarization tensor through two form factors,
\alpha
and
\beta
, as

\displaystyle\Pi^{ab}_{\mathrm{PV}}(p)=\alpha(p)\delta^{ab}+\beta(p)\,\frac{p^{a}p^{b}}{\mathbf{p}^{2}}.

(13)

These form factors read

\displaystyle\alpha(p)=\frac{2\mathrm{i}e^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\left\{\frac{-\mathrm{i}\pi p^{2}(1-x)x}{\sqrt{-q}}-\frac{2p^{2}(1-x)x}{\sqrt{q}}\arctan\left(\frac{p_{0}x+\mathrm{i}\Gamma}{\sqrt{q}}\right)+\frac{2P^{2}(1-x)x}{\sqrt{Q}}\arctan\left(\frac{P_{0}x-\mathrm{i}\Gamma}{\sqrt{Q}}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left.\frac{1}{2}p_{0}(1-2x)\ln\left((p_{0}x+\mathrm{i}\Gamma)^{2}+q\right)-\frac{1}{2}P_{0}(1-2x)\ln\left((P_{0}x-\mathrm{i}\Gamma)^{2}+Q\right)\right\},

(14)

\displaystyle\beta(p)=-\frac{4\mathrm{i}e^{2}\mathbf{p}^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,x(1-x)\left\{\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{2\sqrt{-q}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\arctan\left(\frac{p_{0}x+\mathrm{i}\Gamma}{\sqrt{q}}\right)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{Q}}\arctan\left(\frac{P_{0}x-\mathrm{i}\Gamma}{\sqrt{Q}}\right)\right\},

where
q=p^{2}(1-x)x-m^{2}
,
Q=P^{2}(1-x)x-m^{2}
,
P^{2}=P_{0}^{2}-\mathbf{p}^{2}
, and
P_{0}=p_{0}+2\mathrm{i}\Gamma
. Note that the integrals over
x
in (
14
) are convergent.

In the limit
\Gamma\to 0
our results agree with previous calculations,

\Pi^{ab}_{\mathrm{PV}}(\Gamma=0)=-\frac{e^{2}}{\pi}\left(\delta^{ab}+\frac{p^{a}p^{b}}{p^{2}}\right)\frac{2m|p|-(p^{2}+4m^{2})\mathrm{arctanh}(|p|/2m)}{2|p|},

(15)

see
Appelquist et al. 1986
and also
Bordag et al. 2009b
.

III
Conductivities

From now on we restore the dependence of polarization tensor on the Fermi velocity according to Eq. (
6
).

In this Section, we consider the conductivity tensor defined as

\sigma^{ab}=\frac{\Pi^{ab}_{\mathrm{PV}}}{\mathrm{i}p_{0}}.

(16)

For vanishing spatial momenta,
p_{a}=0
, only a scalar conductivity
\sigma(p_{0})
entering the tensor conductivity as

\delta^{ab}\sigma(p_{0})=\sigma^{ab}|_{p_{a}=0}

(17)

is important.
It is convenient to measure
\sigma(p_{0})
in the units of universal conductivity of graphene
\sigma_{\mathrm{gr}}=e^{2}/4
which is nothing else than the conductivity of a pristine graphene with
\Gamma\to+0
at
m\to 0
. In terms of the form factors from the previous Section,
\sigma(p_{0})=\alpha(p_{0},0)/(\mathrm{i}p_{0})
. For arbitrary values of the parameters
\sigma(p_{0})
can only be evaluated numerically. However, an expansion in
p_{0}
for
p_{0}\ll\Gamma
and
p_{0}\ll m
can be done analytically,

\frac{\sigma}{\sigma_{\mathrm{gr}}}=\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}\left\{\frac{2\Gamma^{2}}{\Gamma^{2}+m^{2}}+\frac{\mathrm{i}p_{0}}{3m}\left(\frac{\Gamma m\left(\Gamma^{2}-5m^{2}\right)}{\left(\Gamma^{2}+m^{2}\right)^{2}}+2\arctan\left(\frac{\Gamma}{m}\right)-\pi\right)+\mathcal{O}(p_{0}^{2})\right\}.

(18)

At
p_{0}=0
this equation coincides with a relation obtained in
Gorbar et al. 2002
.

Let us check whether at small frequencies
p_{0}
the conductivity can be approximated by the Drude formula

\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{Dr}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{gr}}}=\frac{s\mathfrak{g}}{\mathrm{i}p_{0}+\mathfrak{g}}=\frac{s\mathfrak{g}^{2}}{p_{0}^{2}+\mathfrak{g}^{2}}-\mathrm{i}\frac{sp_{0}\mathfrak{g}}{p_{0}^{2}+\mathfrak{g}^{2}},

(19)

where
\mathfrak{g}
has the meaning of inverse scattering rate (similarly to
\Gamma
) while
s
defines the Drude weight. This is a phenomenological formula valid mostly for metals. There is no profound reason why it should describe the conductivity of graphene. However, this is an interesting check.

By identifying the leading terms in the small
p_{0}
expansion of (
19
) with (
18
) we obtain

s=\frac{8\Gamma^{2}}{\pi^{2}(\Gamma^{2}+m^{2})},\qquad\mathfrak{g}=-\frac{6\Gamma^{2}m}{(\Gamma^{2}+m^{2})\left(\frac{\Gamma m\left(\Gamma^{2}-5m^{2}\right)}{\left(\Gamma^{2}+m^{2}\right)^{2}}+2\arctan\left(\frac{\Gamma}{m}\right)-\pi\right)}.

(20)

This quantities are plotted at Fig.
1
while the real and imaginary parts of
\sigma
are depicted at Fig.
2
in comparison with the Drude formula.

Figure 1:
The plots of the parameters of Drude model given by Eq. (
20
)

Somewhat surprisingly, the Drude scattering rate
\mathfrak{g}
may be considerably larger or considerably smaller than the scattering rate
\Gamma
of the quasiparticles. By looking at Fig.
2
, we see that the agreement between imaginary parts of
\sigma
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{Dr}}
is fairly good at low frequencies though the deviations become larger at higher frequencies. The real parts show qualitatively different behaviour.
\Re\sigma
increases at low frequencies while
\Re\sigma_{\mathrm{Dr}}
decreases. Besides,
\Re\sigma_{\mathrm{Dr}}
does not have a jump at the threshold of pair creation
p_{0}=2m
which is a characteristic feature of the conductivity of graphene. We conclude that the Drude formula fails to give a reasonable approximation to the conductivity of graphene with impurities in the range of frequencies which are relevant for the Casimir effect. The quality of approximation may be improved by using the Drude–Lorentz formulas for conductivities
Drosdoff and Woods 2010
.

) with parameters (

). The dashed lines are the conductivity derived from the polarization tensor with
\mathbf{p}=0

For
\mathbf{p}\neq\mathbf{0}
, it is convenient to use two different conductivities,

\sigma_{\mathrm{te}}=\frac{\alpha}{\mathrm{i}p_{0}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sigma_{\mathrm{tm}}=\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\mathrm{i}p_{0}}.

(21)

As we will see in the next Section, these conductivities describe the reflection of TE and TM waves, respectively, on the surface of graphene. We will also see that TM modes give a dominant contribution to the Casimir interaction.

We would like to discuss a property of the conductivities which will be important for the analysis of Casimir effect. When
\Gamma=0
and the
\tilde{p}
is much smaller than
m
, the imaginary parts conductivities are small while the real part vanishes, see (
15
). The smallness is a direct consequence of the Pauli–Villars subtraction which ensures that the polarization tensor vanished at
m\to\infty
. Since
\Gamma
influences conductivities, switching on
\Gamma
at
\tilde{p}<2m
should lead to an increase of
\Re\sigma_{\mathrm{tm}}
. One should only find whether this increase is large or small. Our numerical results are presented on Fig.
3
where
\Re\sigma_{\mathrm{tm}}(\Gamma)
divided by the absolute values of
\sigma_{\mathrm{tm}}
at
\Gamma=0
is depicted as a function of
\Gamma
for three different frequencies
p_{0}
and a fixed
m=0.01
eV. For
p_{0}
much smaller than
2m
there is a strong enhancement of the conductivity. This enhancement becomes weaker and disappears as
p_{0}
approaches
2m
.

as a function of

for

eV,
\mathbf{p}=0
, and three different values of frequency
p_{0}
.

IV
The Casimir energy

Let us consider two parallel infinite planes in the vacuum separated by a distance
a
. One plane will be always occupied by graphene, while the other plane will be either graphene or an ideal conductor. The Casimir energy per unit surface for two interacting plane surfaces
I
and
II
is given by the Lifshitz formula (see, for example,
Bordag et al. 2009a
)

\mathcal{E}^{I,II}=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\xi\left[\ln\left(1-e^{-2ap_{E}}r^{(I)}_{\mathrm{te}}r^{(II)}_{\mathrm{te}}\right)+\ln\left(1-e^{-2ap_{E}}r^{(I)}_{\mathrm{tm}}r^{(II)}_{\mathrm{tm}}\right)\right],

(22)

where the integrations are done over the spatial momentum
\mathbf{p}
parallel to the plates and over the imaginary frequency
\xi
,
p_{0}=\mathrm{i}\xi
, and
p_{E}=\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\mathbf{p}^{2}}
. In (
22
),
r_{\mathrm{te},\mathrm{tm}}^{I,II}
denote the reflection coefficient for TE and TM waves on the first and second surfaces, respectively. These coefficients can be expressed through the polarization tensor as
Fialkovsky et al. 2011

r_{\mathrm{te}}=-\left(1+\frac{2p_{E}}{\xi\sigma_{\mathrm{te}}}\right)^{-1},\ r_{\mathrm{tm}}=\left(1+\frac{2\xi}{p_{E}\sigma_{\mathrm{tm}}}\right)^{-1},

(23)

where
\sigma_{\mathrm{te},\mathrm{tm}}
have been defined above in Eq. (
21
). After continuation to the imaginary frequencies they read

\sigma_{\mathrm{te}}=-{\alpha}/{\xi}
,
\sigma_{\mathrm{tm}}=-{(\alpha+\beta)}/{\xi}
.

For an ideal conductor
r_{\mathrm{tm}}=-r_{\mathrm{te}}=1
which can be obtained from (
23
) by taking the limits
\sigma_{\mathrm{te},\mathrm{tm}}\to\infty
. The Casimir energy density for two ideal conductors reads

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{id}}=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{720a^{3}}.

(24)

We like to mention that Eq. (
22
) is valid only if the reflection matrix is diagonal in the TE-TM basis. Otherwise, the Lifshitz formula is a bit more complicated, see e.g.,
Fialkovsky et al. 2018
.

Since the reflection coefficients depend on
|\mathbf{p}|
rather than on individual components of
\mathbf{p}
we can integrate over the angular coordinates in the Lifshitz formula (
22
) which boils down to the replacement
\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{p}\to(2\pi)|\mathbf{p}|\mathrm{d}|\mathbf{p}|
. Next, we introduce new variables,
y
and
z
, by the formulas
\mathbf{p}^{2}=p_{E}^{2}(1-y^{2})
,
\xi=p_{E}y
, and
p_{E}\to z/a
. In these new variables,

\mathcal{E}^{I,II}=\frac{1}{a^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2}\mathrm{d}z}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}y\left[\ln\left(1-\frac{e^{-2z}}{\left(1+\frac{2}{y\sigma_{\mathrm{te}}^{I}}\right)\left(1+\frac{2}{y\sigma_{\mathrm{te}}^{II}}\right)}\right)+\ln\left(1-\frac{e^{-2z}}{\left(1+\frac{2y}{\sigma_{\mathrm{tm}}^{I}}\right)\left(1+\frac{2y}{\sigma_{\mathrm{tm}}^{II}}\right)}\right)\right],

(25)

One can notice that apart from an overall factor of
1/a^{3}
the whole dependence of Casimir energy (
25
) on the distance resides in the conductivities where it appears in the combinations

\tilde{m}=\frac{am}{z},\qquad\tilde{\Gamma}=\frac{a\Gamma}{z}.

(26)

Explicit formulas for
\sigma_{\mathrm{te}}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{tm}}
are quite long and deferred to the Appendix
A
, see (
33
). The limit
a\to 0
is equivalent to taking
m\to 0
and
\Gamma\to 0
so that we have the case gapless pristine graphene studied in
Bordag et al. 2009b
with
\mathcal{E}\sim 1/a^{3}
.

To analyze the opposite limit of large distance we have to take
\tilde{m}\to\infty
and
\tilde{\Gamma}\to\infty
while keeping the fraction
\tilde{\Gamma}/\tilde{m}=\Gamma/m
fixed. In this limit,
\sigma_{\mathrm{te}}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{tm}}
behave identically,

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{te}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{gr}}},\ \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{tm}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{gr}}}\to

\displaystyle\frac{8\tilde{\Gamma}^{2}}{\pi^{2}\left(\tilde{\Gamma}^{2}+\tilde{m}^{2}\right)}=\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}G,

(27)

where

G\equiv\frac{\Gamma^{2}}{m^{2}+\Gamma^{2}}\leq 1.

(28)

Thus, we have the case of constant conductivities analysed in
Khusnutdinov et al. 2014
. The Casimir energy decays as
1/a^{3}
and

V
Conclusions

In this paper, we studied the Casimir interaction of graphene containing impurities described by a scattering rate
\Gamma
. We evaluated the polarization tensor of quasiparticles in the Pauli–Villars subtraction scheme and computed the corresponding conductivities. Next, we used the conductivities in the Lifshitz formula to calculate the Casimir energy density for various values of parameters. Our main message is that the presence of impurities can considerably enhance the Casimir interaction for large values of the mass (about
m=0.1
eV) and somewhat damp the Casimir interaction for near zero mass. Thus, the non-zero impurity scattering rate
\Gamma
should be taken into account in the analysis of precision Casimir experiments with graphene. To do this however one should also take into consideration the chemical potential and temperature since both influence in an essential way the Casimir force
Fialkovsky et al. 2011
;
Bordag et al. 2016
;
Liu et al. 2021b
. This will be our next task which we are going to take up in the near future.

An additional motivation for considering the influence of impurities jointly with the temperature comes from the old discussion on the Nernst theorem in the Casimir physics
Bezerra et al. 2004
;
Brevik et al. 2008
;
Bezerra et al. 2006
, see
Mostepanenko 2021
for a recent review. The violation of Nernst theorem was blamed on the relaxation rate parameter present in the Drude formula. It is interesting to check what happens if this rate appears in a different model.

In
Holanda et al. 2023
an unconventional version of the Pauli–Villars subtraction scheme in the presence of impurities was suggested. It would be interesting to study the influence of this scheme on Casimir interaction.

Finally, we would like to mention an approach to the Casimir interaction of graphene based on the QFT effective action
Bordag et al. 2009b
;
Farias et al. 2017
. This approach is equivalent to a fine structure constant expansion of the Lifshitz formula.
