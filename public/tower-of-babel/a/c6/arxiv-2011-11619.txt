Neural collapse with unconstrained features
Dustin G. Mixon, Hans Parshall, Jianzong Pi

Abstract
Neural collapse is an emergent phenomenon in deep learning that was recently discovered by Papyan, Han and Donoho. We propose a simple unconstrained features model in which neural collapse also emerges empirically. By studying this model, we provide some explanation for the emergence of neural collapse in terms of the landscape of empirical risk.



1
Introduction

Consider the task of learning a function
\mathcal{X}\to[C]
, where
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents a space of signals and
[C]:=\{1,\ldots,C\}
represents a set of
C\in\mathbb{N}
labels.
Given a labeled training set
\{(x_{i},c_{i})\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
in
\mathcal{X}\times[C]
, one might seek both a feature map
h\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
and a linear classifier
z\mapsto\arg\max(Wz+b)
with
W\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times p}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
for which empirical risk is small:

R_{e}(h,W,b):=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{L}(Wh(x_{i})+b,y_{i}).

Here,
\mathcal{L}\colon\mathbb{R}^{C}\times\mathbb{R}^{C}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
denotes some loss function and
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
denotes the one-hot vector representation
e_{c_{i}}
of the label
c_{i}
.
To accomplish this, it is common to take
h
to reside in some parameterized family
\mathcal{H}=\{h_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
, such as a family of neural networks, and then locally minimize empirical risk over
(\theta,W,b)
.
Such methods have revolutionized classification in various domains, notably image domains
[
8
]
, but this level of performance is largely under-explained by existing theory.

Papyan, Han and Donoho
[
11
]
recently observed several emergent phenomena in the
terminal phase of training
, that is, when the above empirical risk is minimized even after the classifier
x\mapsto\arg\max(Wh(x)+b)
interpolates the training set.
They focused on the balanced case in which there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for each
c\in[C]
, it holds that

|\{i\in\mathcal{I}:c_{i}=c\}|=N,

i.e.,
|\mathcal{I}|=CN
.
For several common families
\mathcal{H}
of neural networks, locally minimizing empirical risk results in
(h,W,b)
that satisfies several properties, which are collectively referred to as
neural collapse
:

(NC1) Variability collapse.

For each
c\in[C]
, there exists
\mu_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that for every
i\in\mathcal{I}
with
y_{i}=e_{c}
, it holds that
h(x_{i})=\mu_{c}
.

(NC2) Simplex equiangular tight frame structure.

Put
\mu_{G}:=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mu_{c}
.
Then

\|\mu_{c}-\mu_{G}\|_{2}=\|\mu_{c^{\prime}}-\mu_{G}\|_{2}

for every
c,c^{\prime}
.
Furthermore, let the
c
th column of
M\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times C}
be

\tilde{\mu}_{c}:=\frac{\mu_{c}-\mu_{G}}{\|\mu_{c}-\mu_{G}\|_{2}}
.
Then

M^{\top}M=\frac{C}{C-1}I_{C}-\frac{1}{C-1}1_{C}1_{C}^{\top}.

(NC3) Self-duality.

\frac{W^{\top}}{\|W\|_{F}}=\frac{M}{\|M\|_{F}}
.

(NC4) Equivalence to nearest class center.

\arg\max_{c}(Wz+b)_{c}=\arg\min_{c}\|z-\mu_{c}\|_{2}
.

In general, an equiangular tight frame (ETF) is any tuple
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of unit vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
for which there exist
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}v_{i}^{*}=\alpha I_{d},\qquad|\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle|^{2}=\beta\qquad\forall i,j\in[n],~i\neq j.

It is natural to identify each
v_{i}v_{i}^{*}
with the corresponding point in complex projective space.
ETFs were introduced in
[
15
]
as convenient maximizers of minimum pairwise distance in this space.
As optimal projective codes, ETFs find applications in multiple description coding
[
15
]
, digital fingerprinting
[
9
]
, compressed sensing
[
2
]
,
and quantum state tomography
[
13
]
.
These applications have motivated a flurry of recent work to discover various infinite families of ETFs; see
[
6
]
for a living survey.
The simplex ETF arises from one of the simplest constructions: take
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
to be the
n=d+1
vertices of an origin-centered regular simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We note that negating any vector in an ETF produces another ETF (as ETFs are fundamentally a projective objects), whereas property (NC2) is not invariant to such an operation.
The regular simplex also emerges as an optimal spherical code by virtue of achieving equality in Rankin’s simplex bound
[
12
]
.
In addition,
C
vertices of a regular simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
induce Voronoi cells that partition
\mathbb{R}^{p}
into
C
isometric cones that are highly symmetric, and this geometry plays a fundamental role in (NC4).

The rich geometric structure of neural collapse has some advantages in the context of machine learning.
In particular, if the feature map
h
generalizes, then neural collapse helps the classifier
x\mapsto\arg\max(Wh(x)+b)
to also generalize.
Indeed, consider a member
x
of the test set corresponding to class
c
.
If the corresponding point
h(x)
in the feature domain is a perturbation of
\mu_{c}
, then
h(x)
is most likely to reside in the Voronoi cell containing
\mu_{c}
if the class means form the vertices of a regular simplex.
Papyan, Han and Donoho
[
11
]
provide some theoretical justification along these lines.

While the notion of neural collapse appears useful in both theory and practice, it remains unexplained why neural collapse emerges from empirical risk minimization.
This paper provides some explanation for this emergence.
In the next section, we propose a simple
unconstrained features model
in which a strong notion of neural collapse empirically emerges.
For this model, Section 3 identifies an invariant subspace of the gradient descent dynamical system that encourages convergence to this strong notion of neural collapse.
We conclude in Section 4 with a brief discussion.

2
Unconstrained features and strong neural collapse

Empirically, neural collapse is a phenomenon that occurs in the terminal phase of training, in which the trained classifier interpolates the training set.
This behavior is only feasible when the feature maps in
\mathcal{H}
restricted to the training set
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
form a high-dimensional subset of
(\mathbb{R}^{p})^{\mathcal{I}}
.
Indeed, the observations by Papyan, Han and Donoho
[
11
]
were made for over-parameterized families of neural networks.
In this paper, we take this to an extreme of sorts by considering the following
unconstrained features model
:

\mathcal{H}:=\{h\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}\},\qquad\mathcal{X}:=\{x_{i}:i\in\mathcal{I}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Under the reasonable assumption that
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
whenever
i\neq j
, then we may represent
\mathcal{H}
by the matrix space
\mathbb{R}^{p\times CN}
; explicitly, we identify
\mathcal{I}
with
[CN]
, and the
i
th column of
H\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times CN}
is given by
h(x_{i})
.
Recalling that
\{c_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
consists of
N
copies of each member of
[C]
, we may similarly represent
\{y_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
as the columns of the matrix
I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top}
.
Note that feature maps in the unconstrained features model are only defined over the training set, while feature maps that are typically trained in practice are defined over all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Of course, such a choice of
\mathcal{H}
has no hope of generalizing to a test set, but we will find that it facilitates the study of neural collapse.
For simplicity, we consider the loss defined by
\mathcal{L}(u,v):=\frac{1}{2}\|u-v\|_{2}^{2}
, in which case empirical risk reduces to

R_{e}(H,W,b)=\frac{1}{2}\|WH+b1_{CN}^{\top}-I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top}\|_{F}^{2},\qquad H\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times CN},~W\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times p},~b\in\mathbb{R}^{C}.

Following common practice in deep learning
[
10
]
, we initialize gradient descent close to the origin.
As illustrated in Figure
1
, the following phenomena emerges from this local optimization, which we collectively refer to as
strong neural collapse
:

\displaystyle WW^{\top}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{N}(I_{C}-\frac{1}{C}1_{C}1_{C}^{\top}),

(SNC1)

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(W\otimes 1_{N})^{\top},

(SNC2)

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C}1_{C}.

(SNC3)

The following lemma establishes that the points exhibiting strong neural collapse form a subset of the global minimizers of empirical risk.
In the next section, we show how the emergence of strong neural collapse is an artifact of the optimization landscape.

Run gradient decent to minimize

for
C=N=3
and

, initializing at a random choice of
H_{0}
and

with
\|H_{0}\|_{F}=\|W_{0}\|_{F}=\varepsilon
and

.

Lemma 1
.

(a)

If
(H,W,b)
exhibits strong neural collapse, then
R_{e}(H,W,b)=0
.

(b)

Strong neural collapse implies neural collapse.

Proof.

For (a), we have

\displaystyle WH+b1_{CN}^{\top}-I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top}

\displaystyle=W(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(W\otimes 1_{N})^{\top})+\frac{1}{C}1_{C}1_{CN}^{\top}-I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top}

\displaystyle=(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}WW^{\top}+\frac{1}{C}1_{C}1_{C}^{\top}-I_{C})\otimes 1_{N}^{\top},

which vanishes by (
SNC1
).
Next, we consider (b).
Letting
w_{c}^{\top}
denote the
c
th row of
W
, then (NC1) follows from (
SNC2
) by taking
\mu_{c}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}w_{c}
.
For (NC2), observe that (
SNC1
) implies
\|W^{\top}1_{C}\|_{2}^{2}=1_{C}^{\top}WW^{\top}1_{C}=0
, and so

\mu_{G}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mu_{c}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}w_{c}=\frac{1}{C\sqrt{N}}W^{\top}1_{C}=0.

Then for every
c\in[C]
, it holds that

\|\mu_{c}-\mu_{G}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\mu_{c}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}w_{c}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}(WW^{\top})_{cc}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(1-\frac{1}{C}).

Furthermore,
\tilde{\mu}_{c}=\frac{\mu_{c}}{\|\mu_{c}\|_{2}}=\frac{w_{c}}{\|w_{c}\|_{2}}
, and so

(M^{\top}M)_{cc^{\prime}}=\langle\tilde{\mu}_{c},\tilde{\mu}_{c^{\prime}}\rangle=\langle\frac{w_{c}}{\|w_{c}\|_{2}},\frac{w_{c^{\prime}}}{\|w_{c^{\prime}}\|_{2}}\rangle=\frac{(WW^{\top})_{cc^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{N}(1-\frac{1}{C})}=(\frac{C}{C-1}I_{C}-\frac{1}{C-1}1_{C}1_{C}^{\top})_{cc^{\prime}}.

Since
\|w_{c}\|_{2}^{2}=\sqrt{N}(1-\frac{1}{C})
is constant over
c\in[C]
, we also have
M\propto W^{\top}
, from which (NC3) follows.
It remains to verify (NC4).
For this, we first apply (
SNC3
) and the fact that
\mu_{c}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}w_{c}
to obtain

\arg\max_{c}(Wz+b)_{c}=\arg\max_{c}(Wz)_{c}=\arg\max_{c}\langle z,w_{c}\rangle=\arg\max_{c}\langle z,\mu_{c}\rangle.

Finally, (
SNC1
) implies
\|\mu_{c}\|_{2}=\|\mu_{c^{\prime}}\|_{2}
for every
c,c^{\prime}
, and so

\arg\max_{c}\langle z,\mu_{c}\rangle=\arg\min_{c}(\|z\|_{2}^{2}-2\langle z,\mu_{c}\rangle+\|\mu_{c}\|_{2}^{2})=\arg\min_{c}\|z-\mu_{c}\|_{2}^{2}.\qed

3
The effect of empirical risk minimization

Write
Z:=(H,W,b)
and consider the
gradient flow
ordinary differential equation

Z^{\prime}(t)=-\nabla R_{e}(Z(t)).

This serves as a model for gradient descent with small learning rate that has been used to analyze the training of neural networks
[
1
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
14
]
.
To analyze gradient flow, we start by computing the gradient:

\nabla_{H}R_{e}=W^{\top}A,\quad\nabla_{W}R_{e}=AH^{\top},\quad\nabla_{b}R_{e}=A1_{CN},\quad A:=WH+b1_{CN}^{\top}-I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top}.

Notably, the resulting gradient flow differential equation is nonlinear.
In order to analyze the initial behavior of our trajectory, we consider a modification in which
A
is replaced by
\tilde{A}:=b1_{CN}^{\top}-I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top}
.
By partially decoupling
(H,W)
and
b
, this modification facilitates analysis, while serving as an good approximation in the regime where
(H,W)
is small.

Theorem 2
.

The solution to the ordinary differential equation

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=-W(t)^{\top}\tilde{A}(t),\qquad W^{\prime}(t)=-\tilde{A}(t)H(t)^{\top},

\displaystyle b^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=-\tilde{A}(t)1_{CN},\qquad\tilde{A}(t):=b(t)1_{CN}^{\top}-I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top}

with initial condition
H(0)=H_{0}
,
W(0)=W_{0}
,
b(0)=0
satisfies

\|(H(t),W(t))-e^{\sqrt{N}t}\cdot\Pi_{T}(H_{0},W_{0})\|_{E}\leq e^{1/(C\sqrt{N})}\cdot\|\Pi_{T^{\perp}}(H_{0},W_{0})\|_{E},\quad b(t)=(\frac{1-e^{-CNt}}{C})1_{C}

for all
t\geq 0
, where
\|(H,W)\|_{E}^{2}:=\|H\|_{F}^{2}+\|W\|_{F}^{2}
and
\Pi_{T}
denotes orthogonal projection onto the subspace

T:=\{(H,W):H=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(W\otimes 1_{N})^{\top},~1_{C}^{\top}W=0\}.

Proof.

We start by solving for
b(\cdot)
, which is governed by the differential equation

b^{\prime}(t)=-\tilde{A}(t)1_{CN}=(I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top}-b(t)1_{CN}^{\top})1_{CN}=N(1_{C}-Cb(t)).

Since
b(0)=0
, we may write
b(t)=\beta(t)1_{C}
, in which case
\beta^{\prime}(t)=N(1-C\beta(t))
, and so
\beta(t)=\frac{1-e^{-CNt}}{C}
, as claimed.
Next, writing
U=(H,W)
, then the unsolved portion of our system is given by

U^{\prime}(t)=L_{t}(U(t)),\quad L_{t}(H,W):=(W^{\top}M_{t},M_{t}H^{\top}),\quad M_{t}:=(I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})-\beta(t)1_{C}1_{CN}^{\top}.

First, we observe that each
L_{t}
is self-adjoint:

\displaystyle\langle L_{t}(H,W),(\tilde{H},\tilde{W})\rangle_{E}

\displaystyle=\langle(W^{\top}M_{t},M_{t}H^{\top}),(\tilde{H},\tilde{W})\rangle_{E}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}((W^{\top}M_{t})^{\top}\tilde{H})+\operatorname{tr}((M_{t}H^{\top})^{\top}\tilde{W})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(M_{t}^{\top}W\tilde{H})+\operatorname{tr}(HM_{t}^{\top}\tilde{W})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(\tilde{H}M_{t}^{\top}W)+\operatorname{tr}(M_{t}^{\top}\tilde{W}H)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}((M_{t}\tilde{H}^{\top})^{\top}W)+\operatorname{tr}((\tilde{W}^{\top}M_{t})^{\top}H)

\displaystyle=\langle(\tilde{W}^{\top}M_{t},M_{t}\tilde{H}^{\top}),(H,W)\rangle_{E}=\langle(H,W),L_{t}(\tilde{H},\tilde{W})\rangle_{E}.

Next, we claim that
\{L_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
are simultaneously diagonalizable over five eigenspaces:

\displaystyle E_{1}^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle:=\{(H,W):H=\epsilon\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(W\otimes 1_{N})^{\top},~1_{C}^{\top}W=0\},

\displaystyle E_{2}^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle:=\{(H,W):H=\epsilon\cdot z1_{CN}^{\top},~W=\sqrt{N}1_{C}z^{\top},~z\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\},

\displaystyle E_{3}

\displaystyle:=\{(H,W):(I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})H^{\top}=0,~W=0\},

where
\epsilon\in\{\pm\}
.
To see this, first note that

\operatorname{dim}E_{1}^{\epsilon}=p(C-1),\qquad\operatorname{dim}E_{2}^{\epsilon}=p,\qquad\operatorname{dim}E_{3}=pC(N-1).

Since these dimensions sum to
pC(N+1)=\operatorname{dim}(\mathbb{R}^{p\times CN}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{C\times p})
, it suffices to show that each nonzero member of each claimed eigenspace is an eigenvector, and that the claimed eigenspaces have distinct eigenvalues.
First, suppose
(H,W)\in E_{1}^{\epsilon}
.
Then

\displaystyle W^{\top}M_{t}

\displaystyle=W^{\top}((I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})-\beta(t)1_{C}1_{CN}^{\top})=W^{\top}(I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})=W^{\top}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top}=\epsilon\sqrt{N}\cdot H,

\displaystyle M_{t}H^{\top}

\displaystyle=((I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})-\beta(t)1_{C}1_{CN}^{\top})(\epsilon\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(W\otimes 1_{N})^{\top})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\epsilon\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\cdot((I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})-\beta(t)1_{C}1_{C}^{\top}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})(W\otimes 1_{N})=\epsilon\sqrt{N}\cdot W,

i.e.,
(H,W)
is an eigenvector of
L_{t}
with eigenvalue
\epsilon\sqrt{N}
.
Next, suppose
(H,W)\in E_{2}^{\epsilon}
.
Then

\displaystyle W^{\top}M_{t}

\displaystyle=(\sqrt{N}1_{C}z^{\top})^{\top}((I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})-\beta(t)1_{C}1_{CN}^{\top})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{N}z1_{C}^{\top}((I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})-\beta(t)1_{C}1_{CN}^{\top})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{N}z(1_{C}^{\top}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top}-C\beta(t)1_{CN}^{\top})=\sqrt{N}(1-C\beta(t))\cdot z1_{CN}^{\top}=\epsilon\sqrt{N}(1-C\beta(t))\cdot H,

\displaystyle M_{t}H^{\top}

\displaystyle=((I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})-\beta(t)1_{C}1_{CN}^{\top})(\epsilon\cdot z1_{CN}^{\top})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\epsilon\cdot((I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})-\beta(t)1_{C}1_{CN}^{\top})1_{CN}z^{\top}

\displaystyle=\epsilon\cdot((I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})(1_{C}\otimes 1_{N})-\beta(t)1_{C}1_{CN}^{\top}1_{CN})z^{\top}

\displaystyle=\epsilon\cdot(N1_{C}-CN\beta(t)1_{C})z^{\top}=\epsilon N(1-C\beta(t))\cdot 1_{C}z^{\top}=\epsilon\sqrt{N}(1-C\beta(t))\cdot W,

i.e.,
(H,W)
is an eigenvector of
L_{t}
with eigenvalue
\epsilon\sqrt{N}(1-C\beta(t))
.
Finally, suppose
(H,W)\in E_{3}
.
Then
W^{\top}M_{t}=0
and

M_{t}H^{\top}=((I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})-\beta(t)1_{C}1_{CN}^{\top})H^{\top}=-\beta(t)1_{C}1_{CN}^{\top}H^{\top}=-\beta(t)1_{C}1_{C}^{\top}(I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})H^{\top}=0,

i.e.,
(H,W)
is an eigenvector of
L_{t}
with eigenvalue
0
.
Overall, letting
\Pi_{i}^{\epsilon}
denote orthogonal projection onto
E_{i}^{\epsilon}
, we have the spectral decomposition

L_{t}=\sqrt{N}\Big(\Pi_{1}^{+}-\Pi_{1}^{-}+(1-C\beta(t))\Pi_{2}^{+}-(1-C\beta(t))\Pi_{2}^{-}\Big).

Finally, we solve the differential equation
U^{\prime}(t)=L_{t}(U(t))
by finding the orthogonal projection of
U(t)
onto each eigenspace of
L_{t}
.
First,
\Pi_{1}^{\epsilon}U^{\prime}(t)=\epsilon\sqrt{N}\Pi_{1}^{\epsilon}U(t)
, and so

\Pi_{1}^{\epsilon}U(t)=e^{\epsilon\sqrt{N}t}\Pi_{1}^{\epsilon}U(0).

Next,
\Pi_{2}^{\epsilon}U^{\prime}(t)=\epsilon\sqrt{N}(1-C\beta(t))\Pi_{2}^{\epsilon}U(t)
, and so

\Pi_{2}^{\epsilon}U(t)=f_{\epsilon}(t)\Pi_{2}^{\epsilon}U(0),\qquad f_{\epsilon}(t):=\operatorname{exp}(\frac{\epsilon}{C\sqrt{N}}(1-e^{-CNt})).

An application of the Pythagorean theorem then gives

\displaystyle\|U(t)-e^{\sqrt{N}t}\Pi_{1}^{+}U(0)\|_{E}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|e^{-\sqrt{N}t}\Pi_{1}^{-}U(0)+f_{+}(t)\Pi_{2}^{+}U(0)+f_{-}(t)\Pi_{2}^{-}U(0)\|_{E}^{2}

\displaystyle=e^{-2\sqrt{N}t}\|\Pi_{1}^{-}U(0)\|_{E}^{2}+f_{+}(t)^{2}\|\Pi_{2}^{+}U(0)\|_{E}^{2}+f_{-}(t)^{2}\|\Pi_{2}^{-}U(0)\|_{E}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Pi_{1}^{-}U(0)\|_{E}^{2}+e^{2/(C\sqrt{N})}\|\Pi_{2}^{+}U(0)\|_{E}^{2}+\|\Pi_{2}^{-}U(0)\|_{E}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{2/(C\sqrt{N})}\|(I-\Pi_{1}^{+})U(0)\|_{E}^{2}.

The result then follows by observing that
T=E_{1}^{+}
.
∎

Theorem 3
.

Select
Z_{0}:=(H_{0},W_{0},b_{0})\in S
and consider
Z(\cdot)
such that

Z^{\prime}(t)=-\nabla R_{e}(Z(t)),\qquad Z(0)=Z_{0}.

(a)

Z(t)\in S
for every
t\geq 0
.

(b)

Select
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that
b_{0}=\frac{1-\alpha}{C}1_{C}
.
Then
(H(\cdot),W(\cdot),b(\cdot))=Z(\cdot)
is given by

\displaystyle W^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{N}W(t)-W(t)W(t)^{\top}W(t),\qquad W(0)=W_{0},

\displaystyle H(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(W(t)\otimes 1_{N})^{\top},\qquad b(t)=\frac{1-\alpha e^{-CNt}}{C}1_{C}.

(c)

Let
\Pi
denote orthogonal projection onto
\operatorname{ker}W_{0}^{\top}
.
Then

\lim_{t\to\infty}W(t)W(t)^{\top}=\sqrt{N}(I-\Pi).

In particular, if
\operatorname{rank}W_{0}\geq C-1
, then
\lim_{t\to\infty}Z(t)
exhibits strong neural collapse.

Proof.

We start by verifying (a).
Since
S
is a subspace, it suffices to show that
Z:=(H,W,b)\in S
implies
\nabla R_{e}(Z)\in S
.
First, the constraint
1_{C}^{\top}W=0
implies

H1_{CN}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(W\otimes 1_{N})^{\top}(1_{C}\otimes 1_{N})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(W^{\top}1_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top}1_{N})=0.

This then implies

\displaystyle\nabla_{b}R_{e}(Z)

\displaystyle=(WH+b1_{CN}^{\top}-I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})1_{CN}

\displaystyle=CNb-(I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})(1_{C}\otimes 1_{N})=CNb-N1_{C}\in\operatorname{span}\{1_{C}\}.

(1)

In addition, we have

\displaystyle 1_{C}^{\top}\nabla_{W}R_{e}(Z)

\displaystyle=1_{C}^{\top}(WH+b1_{CN}^{\top}-I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})H^{\top}

\displaystyle=-(1_{C}^{\top}\otimes 1)(I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})H^{\top}=-1_{CN}^{\top}H^{\top}=0.

It remains to verify that
\nabla_{H}R_{e}(Z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(\nabla_{W}R_{e}(Z)\otimes 1_{N})^{\top}
.
To this end, writing
b=\beta 1_{C}
with
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
gives
W^{\top}b=\beta W^{\top}1_{C}=0
.
As such, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{H}R_{e}(Z)

\displaystyle=W^{\top}(WH+b1_{CN}^{\top}-I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})

\displaystyle=W^{\top}WH-W^{\top}(I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})

\displaystyle=W^{\top}W\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(W\otimes 1_{N})^{\top}-W^{\top}(I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})=W^{\top}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}WW^{\top}-I_{C})\otimes 1_{N}^{\top}.

Next,

\displaystyle\nabla_{W}R_{e}(Z)

\displaystyle=(WH+b1_{CN}^{\top}-I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})H^{\top}

\displaystyle=WHH^{\top}-(I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})H^{\top}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}W(W\otimes 1_{N})^{\top}(W\otimes 1_{N})-\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top})(W\otimes 1_{N})

\displaystyle=WW^{\top}W-\sqrt{N}W,

(2)

and so
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(\nabla_{W}R_{e}(Z)\otimes 1_{N})^{\top}=\nabla_{H}R_{e}(Z)
, as desired.

For (b), we see from (
1
) that

b^{\prime}(t)=-\nabla_{b}R_{e}(Z(t))=N(1_{C}-Cb(t)).

Writing
b(t)=\beta(t)1_{C}
, then
\beta^{\prime}(t)=N(1-C\beta(t))
, and so
\beta(t)=\frac{1-\alpha e^{-CNt}}{C}
, as claimed.
Also, we see from (
2
) that

W^{\prime}(t)=-\nabla_{W}R_{e}(Z(t))=\sqrt{N}W(t)-W(t)W(t)^{\top}W(t).

The expression for
H(t)
follows from the fact that
Z(t)\in S
.

For (c), consider
G(t):=W(t)W(t)^{\top}
.
Then

\displaystyle G^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=W^{\prime}(t)W(t)^{\top}+W(t)W^{\prime}(t)^{\top}

\displaystyle=(\sqrt{N}W(t)-W(t)W(t)^{\top}W(t))W(t)^{\top}+W(t)(\sqrt{N}W(t)-W(t)W(t)^{\top}W(t))^{\top}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{N}W(t)W(t)^{\top}-2W(t)W(t)^{\top}W(t)W(t)^{\top}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{N}G(t)-2G(t)^{2}.

Since
G^{\prime}(t)
and
G(t)
are simultaneously diagonalizable, it follows that
G(\cdot)
takes the form
G(t)=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}(t)\Pi_{i}
, where
\{\Pi_{i}\}
denote orthogonal projections onto the eigenspaces of
G(0)
.
Furthermore, we have

\lambda_{i}^{\prime}(t)=2\lambda_{i}(t)(\sqrt{N}-\lambda_{i}(t)).

Since
G(0)
is positive semidefinite, we have
\lambda_{i}(0)\geq 0
for every
i
.
If
\lambda_{i}(0)=0
, then
\lambda_{i}(t)=0
for all
t\geq 0
.
If
\lambda_{i}(0)>0
, then
\lim_{t\to\infty}\lambda_{i}(t)=\sqrt{N}
.
It follows that

\lim_{t\to\infty}G(t)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}(t)\Pi_{i}=\sum_{\lambda_{i}(0)>0}\sqrt{N}\Pi_{i}=\sqrt{N}(I-\Pi).

For the last claim, we first show that
\lim_{t\to\infty}Z(t)
exists.
Since
\lim_{t\to\infty}b(t)
exists and
H(t)
is determined by
W(t)
, it suffices to show that
\lim_{t\to\infty}W(t)
exists.
Observe that the singular vectors of
W(t)
are also singular vectors of
W^{\prime}(t)
, and so
W(t)=\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}(t)u_{j}v_{j}^{\top}
, where
\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}(0)u_{j}v_{j}^{\top}
denotes any singular value decomposition of
W_{0}
.
For each
j
, select
i(j)
such that
\Pi_{i(j)}u_{j}=u_{j}
.
Considering the above analysis of
\lambda_{i(j)}(t)=\sigma_{j}(t)^{2}
, it follows that
\lim_{t\to\infty}W(t)=N^{1/4}\sum_{\sigma_{i}(0)>0}u_{i}v_{i}^{\top}
.
Next, note that
\operatorname{ker}(W_{0}^{\top})\supseteq\operatorname{span}\{1_{C}\}
since
(H_{0},W_{0},b_{0})\in S
.
Thus,
\operatorname{rank}W_{0}\geq C-1
implies
\operatorname{ker}(W_{0}^{\top})=\operatorname{span}\{1_{C}\}
, and so
\Pi=\frac{1}{C}1_{C}1_{C}^{\top}
.
As such,
\lim_{t\to\infty}G(t)
satisfies (
SNC1
), while (
SNC2
) and (
SNC3
) follow from taking limits of
H(t)
and
b(t)
, respectively.
∎

At this point, there appears to be an “unreasonable effectiveness” of gradient descent in which locally minimizing empirical risk implicitly encourages strong neural collapse.
We conclude with the following result, which explains why this behavior is actually reasonable:

Lemma 4
.

For every
(H,W,b)\in S
, it holds that

R_{e}(H,W,b)=\frac{1}{2}\|WW^{\top}-\sqrt{N}(I-\frac{1}{C}1_{C}1_{C}^{\top})\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{CN}{2}\|b-\frac{1}{C}1_{C}\|_{2}^{2}.

(3)

In particular, the global minimizers of

\text{minimize}\qquad R_{e}(H,W,b)\qquad\text{subject to}\qquad(H,W,b)\in S

are precisely the
(H,W,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times CN}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{C\times p}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{C}
which exhibit strong neural collapse.

Proof.

Suppose
(H,W,b)\in S
.
The implied form of
H
then gives

\displaystyle WH+b1_{CN}^{\top}-I_{C}\otimes 1_{N}^{\top}

\displaystyle=W(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(W\otimes 1_{N})^{\top})+b1_{CN}^{\top}-(I_{C}-\frac{1}{C}1_{C}1_{C}^{\top}+\frac{1}{C}1_{C}1_{C}^{\top})\otimes 1_{N}^{\top}

\displaystyle=(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}WW^{\top}-(I_{C}-\frac{1}{C}1_{C}1_{C}^{\top}))\otimes 1_{N}^{\top}+(b-\frac{1}{C}1_{C})1_{CN}^{\top}.

Since
1_{C}^{\top}W=0
and
b\in\operatorname{span}\{1_{C}\}
, it follows that the two terms on the right-hand side are orthogonal to each other.
The Pythagorean theorem then gives

\displaystyle R_{e}(H,W,b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}WW^{\top}-(I_{C}-\frac{1}{C}1_{C}1_{C}^{\top}))\otimes 1_{N}^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|(b-\frac{1}{C}1_{C})1_{CN}^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|WW^{\top}-\sqrt{N}(I-\frac{1}{C}1_{C}1_{C}^{\top})\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{CN}{2}\|b-\frac{1}{C}1_{C}\|_{2}^{2}.

For the second part of the result, first observe that the constraint
(H,W,b)\in S
implies (
SNC2
).
Subject to this constraint, (
3
) gives that equality in
R_{e}(H,W,b)\geq 0
holds precisely when (
SNC1
) and (
SNC3
) both hold.
In addition,
(H,W,b)
exhibits strong neural collapse only if
(H,W,b)\in S
.
The result follows.
∎

4
Discussion

In this paper, we introduced the
unconstrained features model
that captures the neural collapse phenomena observed by Papyan, Han and Donoho
[
11
]
, and then we identified an invariant subspace of the gradient descent dynamical system that encourages neural collapse.
While we have explained much about the emergence of neural collapse in the unconstrained features model, there are several directions for future investigation.
First, it would be nice to fully characterize the dynamics of gradient flow in the unconstrained features model.
How does the distance from
S
behave over the full gradient flow trajectory?
To what extent does strong neural collapse hold for the limit point of this trajectory when the initialization is at most
\epsilon>0
away from the origin?
What behaviors emerge from other popular local optimization methods such as Adaptive Moment Estimation
[
7
]
?
What alternatives to the unconstrained features model are amenable to theoretical treatment?
