Global well-posedness for the one-phase Muskat problem Thanks: MSC Classification: .
Hongjie Dong, Francisco Gancedo, Huy Q. Nguyen

Abstract
. The free boundary problem for a two-dimensional fluid filtered in porous media is studied. This is known as the one-phase Muskat problem and is mathematically equivalent to the vertical Hele-Shaw problem driven by gravity force. We prove that if the initial free boundary is the graph of a periodic Lipschitz function, then there exists a global-in-time Lipschitz solution in the strong L t ∞ ​ L x 2 L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x} sense and it is the unique viscosity solution. The proof requires quantitative estimates for layer potentials and pointwise elliptic regularity in Lipschitz domains. This is the first construction of unique global strong solutions for the Muskat problem with initial data of arbitrary size.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we study the dynamics of a two dimensional incompressible fluid permeating a homogeneous porous medium. This physical phenomena is modeled by the classical Darcy law
[
37
]

(1.1)

\mu u(x,y,t)=-\nabla_{x,y}p(x,y,t)-\rho(0,1),\quad\nabla_{x,y}\cdot u(x,y,t)=0,\quad(x,y)\in\Omega_{t}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},~t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Here
u
is the fluid velocity,
p
is the fluid pressure, and the positive constants
\mu
and
\rho
are respectively the dynamic viscosity and fluid density. The permeability of the media and the gravitational acceleration have been normalized for the sake of notational simplicity. This problem is known as the one-phase Muskat problem
[
63
]
and, interestingly, is mathematically equivalent to the vertical Hele-Shaw problem driven by gravity. It is a fundamental parabolic free boundary problem in fluid mechanics. We should point out that the horizontal Hele-Shaw problem driven by fluid injection or suction has a different nature and has been studied extensively.

The fluid occupies a time-dependent domain
\Omega_{t}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
whose boundary
\Sigma_{t}
moves with the fluid

(1.2)

\mathcal{V}(\Sigma_{t})=u\cdot n\quad\text{on }\Sigma_{t}=\partial\Omega_{t},

where
n
is the outward pointing unit normal to
\Sigma_{t}
. Neglecting surface tension, the pressure is continuous across the free boundary

(1.3)

p|_{\Sigma_{t}}=0.

One is interested in the
geometry
and
regularity
of the free boundary
\Sigma_{t}
as time evolves. Regarding the geometry, there are two distinguished cases: graph and non-graph boundary. For both kinds of geometry, the existence and uniqueness of strong solution for a
short time
have been established even for much more general settings, including multi-phase, with rigid boundaries, with surface tension, nonconstant permeability. See e.g.
[
42
,
20
,
45
]
and more recent developments in
[
30
,
6
,
28
,
29
,
7
,
21
,
61
,
2
,
3
,
65
,
64
,
47
,
5
,
4
]
.

In this paper we are concerned with long-term dynamics of the Muskat problem. Global existence and uniqueness of solutions have been obtained either when the initial free boundary is the graph of a small function (in certain function spaces)
[
66
,
30
,
44
,
21
,
27
]
or is close to a circle
[
20
,
24
,
50
]
. There are many recent developments for the former in the direction of low regularity solutions. These include “medium data” in the Wiener algebra
[
26
,
25
,
49
,
51
]
, “medium data” in the Lipschitz norm
[
12
,
13
]
, small data in critical Sobolev spaces allowing for arbitrarily large slopes
[
33
,
52
,
5
]
and even infinite slope
[
4
]
. We note that these norms are scaling invariant for the setting considered.

The geometry has an interesting interplay with the regularity. It is known that for the two-phase problem (two fluids separated by a free interface), some initial graph interfaces with large slopes can turn over, passing from a stable regime to an unstable one
[
18
]
. Subsequently the solutions lose regularity in finite time
[
16
]
. On the other hand, there exist solutions that shift stability regime from stable to unstable and then return back to stable
[
32
]
. Particles on the free boundary cannot collide along a smooth curve (splat singularity) for both the two-phase
[
31
]
and one-phase problems
[
34
]
. However, the one-phase problem is more singular in the sense that it can develop splash singularity
[
17
]
from some
non-graph
initial boundary, while the two-phase problem cannot
[
53
]
. Here, splash singularity occurs when two particles collide at a single point on the free boundary while the boundary remains regular. The remaining less well-understood scenario is the one-phase problem starting from
graph initial boundaries
. Then, it is known that in stark contrast to its two-phase counterpart, the free boundary of the one-phase problem cannot turn over. Two fundamental questions for this scenario are: 1) Does there exist a unique global solution? 2) If yes, what is its long-term regularity?

In this paper we affirmatively answer the first question. More precisely, we prove that if the initial free boundary is the graph of a periodic Lipschitz function, then there exists a global Lipschitz solution in the strong
L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}
sense, and hence almost everywhere. Moreover, it is the unique viscosity solution
[
36
]
. In order to establish this result, we first show that the graph free boundary of the one-phase Muskat problem obeys a nonlinear integro-differential equation.

Proposition 1.1
.

Assume that the fluid domain is given by

\Omega_{t}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad y<f(x,t)\}

for some function
f(x,t):\mathbb{R}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
that is
2\pi
periodic in
x
. Then,
f
obeys the equation

(1.4)

\partial_{t}f=-\kappa G(f)f,\quad\kappa=\frac{\rho}{\mu}.

Here, for
f,g:\mathbb{T}\equiv\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
the Dirichlet-Neumann operator
G(f)g
is given by

(1.5)

G(f)g=\frac{1}{4\pi}p.v.\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\sin(x\!-\!x^{\prime})\!+\!\sinh(f(x)\!-\!f(x^{\prime}))\partial_{x}f(x)}{\cosh(f(x)\!-\!f(x^{\prime}))\!-\!\cos(x\!-\!x^{\prime})}\theta(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

where
\theta:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies

(1.6)

\frac{1}{2}\theta(x)+\frac{1}{2\pi}p.v.\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\sinh(f(x)\!-\!f(x^{\prime}))\!-\!\sin(x\!-\!x^{\prime})\partial_{x}f(x)}{\cosh(f(x)\!-\!f(x^{\prime}))\!-\!\cos(x\!-\!x^{\prime})}\theta(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}=\partial_{x}g(x).

We shall prove using deep results from layer potential theory for
C^{1}
and Lipschitz domains
[
46
,
68
]
that
G(f)g
is well-defined in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
with a quantitative bound, provided that
f\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T})
and
g\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T})
. Our main result is stated as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

For all
f_{0}\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T})
, there exists

f\in C(\mathbb{T}\times[0,\infty))\cap L^{\infty}([0,\infty);W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T})),\quad\partial_{t}f\in L^{\infty}([0,\infty);L^{2}(\mathbb{T}))

such that
f|_{t=0}=f_{0}
,
f
satisfies (
1.4
) in
L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}
, and

\|f(t)\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\|f_{0}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\quad\text{a.e. }t>0.

Moreover,
f
is the unique viscosity solution of (
1.4
) and is stable in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
.

Remark 1.3
.

We shall prove in Theorem
6.3
that viscosity solutions of (
1.4
) obey the comparison principle. Consequently, every modulus of continuity of
f_{0}
is preserved by
f(t)
for all
t>0
.

It turns out that sufficiently smooth solutions of the one-phase Muskat problem obey the comparison principle. See Proposition
5.1
and
[
3
]
. As a consequence, so long as the free boundary remains to be a graph, its slope is bounded by the initial slope. Thus starting from a Lipschitz graph, any sufficiently smooth solution must be a graph.

The comparison principle has been discovered for other free boundary problems including the Stefan problem
[
8
,
58
,
59
]
and the horizontal Hele-Shaw problem
[
11
,
58
,
19
]
. Motivated by the fact that these models may develop singularities in finite time, the notion of viscosity solutions, introduced by Crandall and Lions
[
36
]
, has been employed to construct global solutions past singularities. In the aforementioned works, viscosity solutions for the unknown scalars are merely continuous and so are their zero level sets–the free boundary. Consequently, the dynamics of the free boundary is not satisfied in the classical sense (pointwise). This is also the case for variational weak solutions constructed in
[
43
,
57
]
. Higher regularity of weak (viscosity or variational) solutions to these problems has been studied in
[
8
,
22
,
23
]
. In particular, for the horizontal one-phase Hele-Shaw problem, the authors in
[
22
]
proved that if the initial Lipschitz norm of the free boundary is small, then for a short time the unique viscosity solution satisfies the equations pointwise. We also mention that by using the methodology of convex integration, non-unique weak solutions have been constructed in the purely unstable
[
48
,
14
,
62
]
and partially unstable scenarios
[
15
]
.

As far as the Muskat problem is concerned, to the best of our knowledge there has not been any global well-posedness result for initial data of arbitrary size, either for weak or strong solutions. We should point out that for the two-phase problem, the authors in
[
39
]
proved the existence (without uniqueness) of global weak solutions that are monotone in
\mathbb{R}
. In order to obtain viscosity solutions that satisfy the equation almost everywhere, one cannot appeal to the Perron method as in the aforementioned works. Instead, we construct solutions by the vanishing viscosity approach. More precisely, for small
\varepsilon>0
we consider the approximate equation

(1.7)

\partial_{t}f^{\varepsilon}=-\kappa G(f^{\varepsilon})f^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\partial_{x}^{2}f^{\varepsilon}.

The added viscous term
\varepsilon\partial_{x}^{2}f^{\varepsilon}
retains the comparison principle for smooth solutions. The first difficulty is to establish global regularity for (
1.7
) with large data. This is done in Proposition
5.3
via the layer potential representation (
1.5
)-(
1.6
) of the Dirichlet-Neumann operator
G(f)g
and a
careful decomposition of its singular and non-singular parts. An important ingredient in its proof is the
L^{2}
bound

(1.8)

\|G(f)g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C(1+\|f\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T})})^{2}\|\partial_{x}g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})},

where the slope
\|f\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T})}
is controlled for all time. The proof of (
1.8
) relies on a quantitative bound for the inverse
(\frac{1}{2}I-K^{*}[f])^{-1}
, where
K[f]
is the boundary double layer potential for the fluid domain
\{y<f(x)\}
. This is established in Section
4
. Under the
C^{2,\alpha}
regularity condition for the boundary, a similar bound was obtained
[
28
]
. The quantitative bound is also crucial to the uniform
L^{2}
bound for the solution
\theta^{\varepsilon}
of (
1.6
) with
f=f^{\varepsilon}
. Finally, from the uniform Lipschitz bound for
f^{\varepsilon}
and the uniform
L^{2}
bound for
\theta^{\varepsilon}
, we are able to pass to the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
in equations (
1.4
)-(
1.5
) and (
1.6
) using a decomposition into small and large scales inspired by
[
26
,
25
]
. We thus obtain a global Lipschitz solution in the strong
L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}
sense.

An advantage of the viscosity regularization (
1.7
) is that one can show, in a direct manner, that the constructed solution
f
is also a viscosity solution (see Definition
6.1
). The proof of this makes use of the contraction estimate for the Dirichlet-Neumann operator associated to two different domains
[
65
]
. In order to obtain the uniqueness of viscosity solutions we prove that they obey the comparison principle. By the sup and inf-convolution technique, this reduces to proving the consistency of viscosity solutions. That is, if a viscosity solution is smooth (
C^{1,1}
) at a point
(x_{0},t_{0})
then it satisfies equation (
1.4
) classically at the same point. It is known that the harmonic extension
\phi
of
f
to the domain
\{y<f(x)\}
is
C^{1}
at the boundary point
(x_{0},f(x_{0}))
in all nontangential directions (see, for instance,
[
9
, Lemma 11.17]
), so that
G(f)f
is classically well-defined at
x_{0}
. However, without min-max formulas available at hand as in
[
10
,
67
,
19
]
, the
C^{1}
regularity seems insufficient. Here, we prove that in two dimensions, the harmonic extension
\phi
is in fact
C^{1,\alpha}
at
(x_{0},f(x_{0}))
with quantitative estimates (see Theorem
2.12
and Corollary
2.14
). This pointwise
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity for harmonic functions in Lipschitz domains is of independent interest. It allows us to reduce to the Dirichlet-Neumann operator for an interior disk tangent to
\{y=f(x)\}
at
(x_{0},f(x_{0}))
, so that an explicit integral formula can be used (see Proposition
B.1
) to conclude the consistency of viscosity solutions.

2.
The Dirichlet-Neumann operator

Notation 2.1
.

For any set
\mathcal{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote

\Lip(\mathcal{O})=\Big\{g:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}:\|g\|_{\Lip(\mathcal{O})}:=\sup_{x,y\in\mathcal{O},x\neq y}\frac{|g(x)-g(y)|}{|x-y|}<\infty\Big\}.

For
\alpha\in(0,1]
we say that
g
is
C^{1,\alpha}
at
x_{0}\in\overline{\mathcal{O}}
if there exists
v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and positive numbers
M
and
\gamma
such that

(2.1)

|g(x)-g(x_{0})-v\cdot(x-x_{0})|\leq M|x-x_{0}|^{1+\alpha}\quad\forall x\in\Omega,~|x-x_{0}|<\gamma.

If
x_{0}
is an interior point of
\mathcal{O}
then (
2.1
) implies that
\nabla g(x)
exists and equals
v
.

Notation 2.2
.

For
f:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
we denote

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Omega_{f}=\{(x,f(x)):x\in\mathbb{T}^{d},~y<f(x)\},\quad\Sigma=\{(x,f(x)):x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\},

(2.3)

\displaystyle N(x)=(-\partial_{x}f(x),1),\quad n(x)=\frac{N(x)}{|N(x)|}.

For
\phi:\Omega_{f}\to\mathbb{R}
and

we denote by

(2.4)

\lim_{z\to_{N}z_{0}}\phi(z)

the limit of
\phi
when
\Omega_{f}\ni z\to z_{0}
in the direction of
N
.

Notation 2.3
.

For
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

2.1.
Definition and global properties

Definition 2.4
.

For
f:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, the Dirichlet-Neumann operator
G(f)
is defined by

(2.5)

\big(G(f)g\big)(x)=\partial_{N}\phi(x,f(x)):=\lim_{h\to 0^{-}}\frac{1}{h}\big[\phi\big((x,f(x))+hN(x)\big)-\phi(x,f(x))\big],

where
\phi(x,y)
solves the elliptic problem

(2.6)

\begin{cases}\Delta_{x,y}\phi=0\quad\text{in}~\Omega_{f},\\
\phi(x,f(x))=g(x),\quad\nabla_{x,y}\phi\in L^{2}(\Omega_{f}).\end{cases}

When
f
and
g
are time-dependent, we write

(2.7)

\big(G(f)g\big)(x,t)\equiv\big(G\big(f(t)\big)g(t)\big)(x).

The one-phase Muskat problem has a compact reformulation in terms of the Dirichlet-Neumann operator.

Proposition 2.5
.

If the fluid domain is given by
\Omega_{f}
for some
f(x,t):\mathbb{R}\times(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}
, then
f
satisfies

(2.8)

\partial_{t}f=-\kappa G(f)f\quad\text{on }(0,T),

where
\kappa={\rho}/{\mu}
.

This reformulation has been exploited in
[
3
,
65
,
64
,
47
]
. We recall its proof for completeness.

Proof.

Let
q=p+\rho y
denote the ‘hydraulic head’. From Darcy’s law (
1.1
) we have
\mu u=-\nabla_{x,y}q
and
\Delta_{x,y}q=0
in
\Omega_{f}
. Moreover, (
1.3
) implies
q(x,f(x))=\rho f(x)
. By the definition of the Dirichlet-Neumann operator, at any fixed time we have

\rho\big(G(f)f\big)(x)=\big(G(f)(\rho f)\big)(x)=N(x)\cdot\nabla_{x,y}q(x,f(x))=-\mu N(x)\cdot u(x,f(x)).

For graph boundary, (
1.2
) yields

\partial_{t}f(x,t)=N(x,t)\cdot u(f(x,t),t)=-\frac{\rho}{\mu}\big(G(f)f\big)(x,t),

which finishes the proof of (
2.8
).
∎

In order to define
G(f)g
we first study the well-posedness of the elliptic problem (
2.6
). It was proved in Proposition 3.6
[
65
]
that (
2.6
) has a unique variational solution when
f\in\Lip(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
g\in\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Of course, this is only nonstandard when the domain is unbounded. Adaption to horizontally periodic domains is straightforward. To fix notation for later purposes, we prove

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
f\in\Lip(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and
g\in\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Then there exists a unique variational solution
\phi\in\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f})
to (
2.6
),
where

(2.9)

\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f})=\{u\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega_{f}):\nabla_{x,y}u\in L^{2}(\Omega_{f})\}/\mathbb{R}

endowed with the norm
\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f})}=\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{f})}
. Moreover,
\phi
satisfies

(2.10)

\|\phi\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f})}\leq C(1+\|f\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T}^{d})})\|g\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

We shall refer to the solution
\phi
of (
2.6
) as the harmonic extension of
g
to
\Omega_{f}
.

Proof.

According to Theorem
A.2
, there exists
\underline{g}\in\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f})
such that
\Tr(\underline{g})=g
and

(2.11)

\|\underline{g}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f})}\leq C(1+\|f\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T}^{d})})\|g\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

Denote

(2.12)

\dot{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{f})=\{u\in\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f}):\Tr(u)=0\},

where the trace operator
\Tr:\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f})\to\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T})
is given in Theorem
A.1
. We then define
\phi
solution to (
2.6
) to be

(2.13)

\phi=u+\underline{g},

where

is the unique variational solution in
\dot{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{f})
of the equation
-\Delta_{x,y}u=\Delta_{x,y}\underline{g}
. That is,
u\in\dot{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{f})
satisfies

(2.14)

\int_{\Omega_{f}}\nabla_{x,y}u\cdot\nabla_{x,y}\varphi dxdy=-\int_{\Omega_{f}}\nabla_{x,y}\underline{g}\cdot\nabla_{x,y}\varphi dxdy\quad\forall\varphi\in\dot{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{f}).

Since
\dot{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{f})
is a Hilbert space, the existence and uniqueness of

is guaranteed by the Lax-Milgram theorem. From (
2.14
) we have

(2.15)

\int_{\Omega_{f}}\nabla_{x,y}\phi\cdot\nabla_{x,y}\varphi dxdy=0\quad\forall\varphi\in\dot{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{f}).

Moreover, inserting
\varphi=u
in (
2.14
) we obtain (
2.10
) from (
2.11
). We note that the solution constructed by (
2.13
) and (
2.14
) is independent of the choice of
\underline{g}
.

2.2.
Pointwise properties in two dimensions

2.2.1.
Pointwise
C^{1,\alpha}
estimate for harmonic functions

Suppose that
U
is a Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
r>0
, we denote
U_{r}(x_{0},y_{0})=B_{r}(x_{0},y_{0})\cap U
and
U_{r}=U_{r}(0)
. We also define the half ball as

B^{+}_{r}(x_{0},y_{0})=\{(x,y)\in B_{r}(x_{0},y_{0}):y>y_{0}\}.

We assume that
0\in\partial U
. Suppose that there exists some
r_{0}>0
such that in a coordinate system,
\partial U\cap B_{2r_{0}}
can be represented by a Lipschitz graph with Lipschitz constant
L>0
.

Let
u
be a harmonic function in
U
, which vanishes on
\partial U
.

Lemma 2.10
.

Under the conditions above, there exist
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(L)>0
and
M_{1}=M_{1}(L)>0
such that
u\in C^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon_{0}}(U_{r_{0}})
and

(2.18)

\|u\|_{C^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon_{0}}(U_{r_{0}})}\leq M_{1}r_{0}^{-\frac{3}{2}-\varepsilon_{0}}\|u\|_{L^{2}(U_{2r_{0}})}.

Proof.

By scaling, we may assume that
r_{0}=1
and
\|u\|_{L^{2}(U_{2})}=1
. By the boundary De Giorgi-Nash-Moser estimate, we know that

(2.19)

\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(U_{3/2})}\leq M(L).

Now we fix a point
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\partial U\cap B_{1}
. Since
\partial U\cap B_{2}
can be represented by a Lipschitz graph with Lipschitz constant
L>0
, we may assume that

(2.20)

U_{1/2}(x_{0},y_{0})\subset\big\{(x,y)\in B_{1/2}(x_{0},y_{0}):y-y_{0}>-L|x-x_{0}|\big\}.

Let
\beta=\tan^{-1}(1/L)\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
,
\gamma=\pi/(2\pi-\beta)\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
, and define

2.2.2.
Pointwise comparison principle for Dirichlet-Neumann

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
f_{1},\,f_{2}\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T})
be
C^{1,1}
at
x_{0}
. If
f_{1}(x)\leq f_{2}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{T}
and
f_{1}(x_{0})=f_{2}(x_{0})
, then
G(f_{j})f_{j}
are classically well-defined at
x_{0}
and

(2.37)

\big(G(f_{1})f_{1}\big)(x_{0})\geq\big(G(f_{2})f_{2}\big)(x_{0}).

Proof.

Let
\phi_{j}
be the harmonic extension of
f_{j}
to
\Omega_{j}\equiv\Omega_{f_{j}}
as given by Proposition
2.6
. Then
p_{j}:=\phi_{j}-y
are harmonic in
\Omega_{j}
and identically vanish on
\partial\Omega_{j}=\{(x,y):y=f_{j}(x)\}
. Since
x_{0}
is a
C^{1,1}
boundary point, Theorem
2.12
implies that the normal derivatives
\partial_{N_{j}}p_{j}
, where
N_{j}(x)=(-\partial_{x}f_{j}(x),1)
, are classically well-defined at
x_{0}
. Consequently, in view of the definition (
2.5
), we have

(2.38)

\displaystyle\big(G(f_{j})f_{j}\big)(x_{0})=\partial_{N_{j}(x_{0})}(p_{j}+y)(x_{0},f_{j}(x_{0}))=\partial_{N_{j}(x_{0})}p_{j}(x_{0},f_{j}(x_{0}))+1

are classically well-defined at
(x_{0},f(x_{0}))
. We first claim that

(2.39)

p_{j}(x,y)\geq 0\quad a.e.~\Omega_{j}.

If
\phi_{j}
are smooth then so do
p_{j}
and (
2.39
) is a consequence of the maximum principle for harmonic functions. Here, following Proposition 4.3
[
65
]
we prove (
2.39
) for variational solutions. Indeed, recall from (
2.15
) that

(2.40)

\int_{\Omega_{j}}\nabla_{x,y}\phi\cdot\nabla_{x,y}\varphi dxdy=0\quad\forall\varphi\in\dot{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{j}).

Inserting
\varphi=\min\{\phi-\inf_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f_{j},0\}\in\dot{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{j})
into (
2.40
) gives

(2.41)

\inf_{\Omega_{j}}\phi_{j}\geq\inf_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f_{j}.

On the other hand, by choosing
\varphi=\max\{\phi-\sup_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f_{j},0\}
we obtain

(2.42)

\sup_{\Omega_{j}}\phi_{j}\leq\sup_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f_{j}.

It follows from (
2.41
) that
p_{j}\geq 0
for
y\leq\inf_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f_{j}
.
Hence,
p_{j}^{-}=\min\{p_{j},0\}
identically vanishes in
\{(x,y):y\leq\inf_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f_{j}\}
and
p_{j}^{-}\in\dot{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{j})
. Thus inserting
\varphi=p_{j}^{-}
into (
2.40
) and replacing
\phi_{j}=p_{j}+y
we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{j}}\nabla_{x,y}p_{j}\cdot\nabla_{x,y}p_{j}^{-}dxdy

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega_{j}}\partial_{y}p_{j}^{-}dxdy

\displaystyle=-\int_{\inf_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f_{j}<y<f_{j}(x)}\partial_{y}p_{j}^{-}dxdy=p^{-}_{j}(x,\inf_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f_{j})=0.

This implies
\int_{\Omega_{j}}|\nabla_{x,y}p_{j}^{-}|^{2}=0
and thus
p_{j}\geq 0
a.e.
\Omega_{j}
. We obtain the claim (
2.39
).

Since
f_{1}\leq f_{2}
, we have
\Omega_{1}\subset\Omega_{2}
and
p:=p_{1}-p_{2}
satisfies
p(x,f_{1}(x))=-p_{2}(x,f_{1}(x))\leq 0
by virtue of (
2.39
). In addition, noticing
p=\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}
we find from (
2.40
) that

\int_{\Omega_{1}}\nabla_{x,y}p\cdot\nabla_{x,y}\varphi dxdy=0\quad\forall\varphi\in\dot{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{1}).

Then as for (
2.42
), upon choosing
\varphi=\max\big\{p-\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}(p(x,f_{1}(x))),0\big\}
we obtain

\sup_{\Omega_{1}}p\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}(p(x,f_{1}(x)))\leq 0.

Therefore,
p
attains the zero maximum at the boundary point
(x_{0},f_{1}(x_{0}))
. In addition, we have
N_{1}(x_{0})=N_{2}(x_{0})
since
\partial_{x}f_{1}(x_{0})=\partial_{x}f_{2}(x_{0})
at the maximum point
x_{0}
of
f_{1}-f_{2}
. We deduce that

0\leq\partial_{N_{1}(x_{0})}p(x_{0},f_{1}(x_{0}))=\partial_{N_{1}(x_{0})}p_{1}(x_{0},f_{1}(x_{0}))-\partial_{N_{2}(x_{0})}p_{2}(x_{0},f_{2}(x_{0})).

Then (
2.37
) follows from this and (
2.38
).∎

3.
Contour dynamics

In this section, we use potential theory to express the Dirichlet-Neumann operator, thereby obtaining a contour dynamics reformulation of the Muskat problem.

The Newtonian kernel for
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
is

(3.1)

\mathcal{N}(z)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\ln\big(\cosh y-\cos x\big),\quad z=(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}.

We shall identify functions defined on
\mathbb{T}
to functions defined on the graph
\Sigma=\{(x,f(x)):x\in\mathbb{T}\}
. Precisely,
h:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
is identified to
\widetilde{h}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
\widetilde{h}(x,f(x))=h(x)
. The double layer potential associated to
\Omega_{f}
for a function
h:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
is

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}[f]h(z)

\displaystyle:=-\int_{\Sigma}(\partial_{n(x^{\prime})}\mathcal{N})(z-z^{\prime})\widetilde{h}(z^{\prime})dz^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\sin(x-x^{\prime})\partial_{x}f(x^{\prime})-\sinh(y-f(x^{\prime}))}{\cosh(y-f(x^{\prime}))-\cos(x-x^{\prime})}h(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

where
z=(x,y)\in(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})\setminus\Sigma
and
z^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},f(x^{\prime}))\in\Sigma
. Clearly,
\mathcal{K}[f]h(z)
is harmonic on
(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})\setminus\Sigma
. A direct calculation using (hyperbolic) trigonometric identities gives

\partial_{x^{\prime}}\Big(\arctan\frac{\tanh(\frac{y-f(x^{\prime})}{2})}{\tan(\frac{x-x^{\prime}}{2})}\Big)=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sin(x-x^{\prime})\partial_{x}f(x^{\prime})-\sinh(y-f(x^{\prime}))}{\cosh(y-f(x^{\prime}))-\cos(x-x^{\prime})}.

Hence,

(3.3)

\mathcal{K}[f]h(z)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\partial_{x^{\prime}}\Big(\arctan\frac{\tanh(\frac{y-f(x^{\prime})}{2})}{\tan(\frac{x-x^{\prime}}{2})}\Big)h(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}.

The single layer potential is

(3.4)

\displaystyle S[f]h(x,y)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\ln\big(\cosh(y-f(x^{\prime}))-\cos(x-x^{\prime})\big)h(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},\quad(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}.

Note that the preceding single layer potential differs from the one in
[
46
,
68
]
by the arc length element
\sqrt{1+|\partial_{x}f(x^{\prime})|^{2}}
.

The boundary double layer potential is

(3.5)

\displaystyle K[f]h(x)

\displaystyle=:p.v.\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\sin(x-x^{\prime})\partial_{x}f(x^{\prime})-\sinh(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))}{\cosh(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))-\cos(x-x^{\prime})}h(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-p.v.\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\partial_{x^{\prime}}\Big(\arctan\frac{\tanh(\frac{f(x)-f(x^{\prime})}{2})}{\tan(\frac{x-x^{\prime}}{2})}\Big)h(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

where
z=(x,f(x))
and
z^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},f(x^{\prime}))
.
The adjoint of
K[f]
is

(3.6)

\displaystyle K^{*}[f]h(x)

\displaystyle=-p.v.\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\partial_{x}\Big(\arctan\frac{\tanh(\frac{f(x)-f(x^{\prime})}{2})}{\tan(\frac{x-x^{\prime}}{2})}\Big)h(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=p.v.\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\sinh(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))-\sin(x-x^{\prime})\partial_{x}f(x)}{\cosh(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))-\cos(x-x^{\prime})}h(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}.

If
f\in\Lip(\mathbb{T})
and
h\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T})
for some
p\in(1,\infty)
then the nontangential limit

(3.7)

\lim_{\Omega\ni z^{\prime}\to z}\mathcal{K}h(z^{\prime})=(\frac{1}{2}I+K)h(z)

holds a.e.
z\in\mathbb{T}
(see Theorem 1.10
[
68
]
). Consequently, the unique solution
\phi
of the Dirichlet problem (
2.6
)
is given by

(3.8)

\phi=\mathcal{K}(\frac{1}{2}I+K)^{-1}g.

It was proved in Section 4
[
68
]
that for Lipschitz domains, the mappings

(3.9)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}I+K:L^{p}(\mathbb{T})\to L^{p}(\mathbb{T}),\quad p\in[2,\infty),

(3.10)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}I+K:W^{1,p}(\mathbb{T})\to W^{1,p}(\mathbb{T}),\quad p\in(1,2],

(3.11)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}I-K^{*}:L^{p}_{0}(\mathbb{T})\to L^{p}_{0}(\mathbb{T}),\quad p\in(1,2]

are invertible. Here,
L^{p}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
denotes the space of zero mean functions in
L^{p}(\mathbb{T})
.

Proposition 3.1
.

For
f\in\Lip(\mathbb{T})
and
g\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T})
, we have for a.e.
x\in\mathbb{T}
that

(3.12)

\displaystyle(G(f)g)(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}p.v.\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\sin(x-x^{\prime})+\sinh(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))\partial_{x}f(x)}{\cosh(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))-\cos(x-x^{\prime})}\theta(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}

(3.13)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}p.v.\int_{\mathbb{T}}\partial_{x}\ln\Big(\cosh(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))-\cos(x-x^{\prime})\Big)\theta(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

where

(3.14)

\theta=(\frac{1}{2}I-K^{*})^{-1}(\partial_{x}g).

Proof.

Applying (
3.8
) we have that the unique solution
\phi
to (
2.6
) is
\phi=\mathcal{K}\Theta
with
\Theta=(\frac{1}{2}I+K)^{-1}g
. Note that
\Theta\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T})
in view of (
3.10
). Setting
\theta=\partial_{x}\Theta\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
we deduce from (
3.3
) that

\phi(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\arctan\frac{\tanh(\frac{y-f(x^{\prime})}{2})}{\tan(\frac{x-x^{\prime}}{2})}\theta(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},\quad(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}.

It follows from (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) that

\displaystyle\partial_{x}K[f]\Theta(x)

\displaystyle=p.v.\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\partial_{x}\Big(\arctan\frac{\tanh(\frac{f(x)-f(x^{\prime})}{2})}{\tan(\frac{x-x^{\prime}}{2})}\Big)\partial_{x}\Theta(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}=-K^{*}[f]\theta(x),

whence
\theta=\partial_{x}(\frac{1}{2}I+K)^{-1}g=(\frac{1}{2}I-K^{*})^{-1}\partial_{x}g
.

For
(x,y)\in(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})\setminus\Sigma
, we compute

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\phi(x,y)=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\sinh(y-f(x^{\prime}))}{\cosh(y-f(x^{\prime}))-\cos(x-x^{\prime})}\theta(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

\displaystyle\partial_{y}\phi(x,y)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\sin(x-x^{\prime})}{\cosh(y-f(x^{\prime}))-\cos(x-x^{\prime})}\theta(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}.

Set
T(x)=(1,\partial_{x}f(x))
to be the tangent to
\Sigma
. For
\Omega_{f}\ni(\widetilde{x},y)\to_{N}(x,f(x))
, by the definition (
2.5
) and the mean-value theorem, we have

\displaystyle(G(f)g)(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{(\widetilde{x},y)\to_{N}(x,f(x))}N(x)\cdot(\nabla_{x,y}\phi)(\widetilde{x},y)

\displaystyle=\lim_{(\widetilde{x},y)\to_{N}(x,f(x))}\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\sinh(y-f(x^{\prime}))\partial_{x}f(x)+\sin(\widetilde{x}-x^{\prime})}{\cosh(y-f(x^{\prime}))-\cos(\widetilde{x}-x^{\prime})}\theta(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\lim_{(\widetilde{x},y)\to_{N}(x,f(x))}T(x)\cdot\nabla S[f]\theta(\widetilde{x},y).

Since
f\in\Lip(\mathbb{T})
and
\theta\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
, Theorem 1.6
[
68
]
asserts that the tangential derivative of
S[f]\theta
is continuous up to the boundary. That is,

\lim_{(\widetilde{x},y)\to_{N}(x,f(x))}T(x)\cdot\nabla S[f]\theta(\widetilde{x},y)=T(x)\cdot\nabla S[f]\theta(x,f(x))

for a.e.
x\in\mathbb{T}
. Therefore, for a.e.
x\in\mathbb{T}
we obtain

(3.15)

\displaystyle(G(f)g)(x)

\displaystyle=T(x)\cdot\nabla S[f]\theta(x,f(x))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}p.v.\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\sinh(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))\partial_{x}f(x)+\sin(x-x^{\prime})}{\cosh(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))-\cos(x-x^{\prime})}\theta(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

proving (
3.12
). Finally, since

(3.16)

\frac{\sin(x-x^{\prime})+\sinh(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))\partial_{x}f(x)}{\cosh(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))-\cos(x-x^{\prime})}=\partial_{x}\ln\Big(\cosh(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))-\cos(x-x^{\prime})\Big),

we obtain (
3.13
).
∎

Proposition
1.1
follows at once from Propositions
2.5
and
3.1
.

4.
Quantitative bound for inverse of double layer potential

According to (
3.11
),
\frac{1}{2}I-K^{*}:L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T})\to L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
in invertible provided that the boundary
f
of
\Omega_{f}
is Lipschitz. This is sufficient to deduce the unique solvability of the Neumann problem in Lipschitz domain with
L^{2}
data
[
68
]
. However, in order to obtain uniform bounds for approximate solutions of the Muskat problem we shall need a
quantitative bound
for the inverse of
\frac{1}{2}I-K^{*}
. This is the content of the next proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that

(4.1)

\|(\frac{1}{2}I\pm K^{*})^{-1}\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T})\to L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C(1+\|f\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T})})^{\frac{5}{2}}.

Proof.

In order to get this result, we will show that there exists a constant
M=\bar{C}(1+\|\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})})^{5/2}
with
\bar{C}>1
such that

(4.2)

\frac{1}{M}\leq\frac{\|(\frac{1}{2}I-K^{*}[f])(\eta)\|_{L_{0}^{2}(\mathbb{T})}}{\|(\frac{1}{2}I+K^{*}[f])(\eta)\|_{L_{0}^{2}(\mathbb{T})}}\leq M\quad\forall\eta\in L_{0}^{2}(\mathbb{T}).

We claim that (
4.2
) yields

(4.3)

\|\eta\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T})}\leq 2M\|(\frac{1}{2}I-K^{*}[f])(\eta)\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T})}\quad\forall\eta\in L_{0}^{2}(\mathbb{T}).

This in turn implies the desired bound (
4.1
). Indeed, if (
4.3
) is false then there exists
\eta_{1}\in L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
with
\|\eta_{1}\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T})}=1
such that

\|(\frac{1}{2}I-K^{*}[f])(\eta_{1})\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T})}<\frac{1}{2M}.

Then

\|(\frac{1}{2}I+K^{*}[f])(\eta_{1})\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T})}\geq\|\eta_{1}\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T})}-\|(\frac{1}{2}I-K^{*}[f])(\eta_{1})\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T})}>1-\frac{1}{2M}>\frac{1}{2},

but using (
4.2
) again we find the opposite:

\|(\frac{1}{2}I+K^{*}[f])(\eta_{1})\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T})}\leq M\|(\frac{1}{2}I-K^{*}[f])(\eta_{1})\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T})}<\frac{1}{2}.

This finishes the proof of (
4.3
).
Now we prove that

(4.4)

\|(\frac{1}{2}I-K^{*}[f])(\eta)\|_{L_{0}^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\leq M\|(\frac{1}{2}I+K^{*}[f])(\eta)\|_{L_{0}^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\quad\forall\eta\in L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T}).

Recall that the single layer potential given by

(4.5)

S[f](\eta)(x,y)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\ln\Big(\cosh(y-f(x^{\prime}))-\cos(x-x^{\prime})\Big)\eta(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}=H(x,y).

With
\Omega=\{(x,y):x\in\mathbb{T},y<f(x)\}
,
H
satisfies (see
[
68
]
)

(4.6)

\begin{cases}\Delta_{x,y}H(x,y)=0,\quad(x,y)\in\Omega\cup(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}),\\
\partial_{N}H(x,f(x))=(-\frac{1}{2}I+K^{*}[f])(\eta)(x)\quad\text{a.e. }x\in\mathbb{T},\\
\partial^{c}_{N}H(x,f(x))=(\frac{1}{2}I+K^{*}[f])(\eta)(x)\quad\text{a.e. }x\in\mathbb{T},\end{cases}

where
\partial_{N}
is the normal derivative from inside the domain
\Omega
and
\partial_{N}^{c}
is to the normal derivative from inside the complementary set
\Omega^{c}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
. By Theorem 1.6
[
68
]
, the tangential derivative
\partial_{\tau}H
is continuous across the
\partial\Omega
. Thus we shall not distinguish the tangential derivative of
H
when approaching
\partial\Omega
from either side. Since
\int_{\mathbb{T}}\eta dx=0
we can write

\displaystyle H(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\ln\Big(\frac{\cosh(y-f(x^{\prime}))-\cos(x-x^{\prime})}{\cosh y-\cos x}\Big)\eta(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\ln\Big(\frac{\sinh^{2}(\frac{y-f(x^{\prime})}{2})+\sin^{2}(\frac{x-x^{\prime}}{2})}{\sinh^{2}\frac{y}{2}+\sin^{2}\frac{x}{2}}\Big)\eta(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}

and deduce that as
|y|\to\infty
,
H(x,y)
is bounded and
\nabla H
decay uniformly in
x\in\mathbb{T}
.

We consider a compactly supported vector field
V(x,y)
given by

(4.7)

V(x,y)=(\Gamma_{\delta}*G)(x,y),

where the function
G(x,y)
is given by

(4.8)

G(x,y)=\begin{cases}n(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{T},\quad|y|\leq 2\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}+2,\\
0\quad\mbox{elsewhere},\end{cases}

and
\Gamma_{\delta}(x,y)
is a smooth approximation of the identity with compact support. The parameter
\delta>0
is taken small enough so that

(4.9)

V(x,f(x))\cdot n(x)\geq\frac{1}{2}\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{T}.

One could check that

(4.10)

\|V\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|\nabla V\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C=O\big(\delta^{-1}\big),

where
C
is independent of
f
.

Let us recall the Rellich identity for
V
and
H
:

(4.11)

\int_{\partial\Omega}V\cdot n|\nabla H|^{2}d\sigma=\int_{\partial\Omega}2\partial_{n}H(V\cdot\nabla H)d\sigma+\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\cdot V)|\nabla H|^{2}dX-\int_{\Omega}2(\nabla V\nabla H)\cdot\nabla HdX,

where
\nabla V
is a
2\times 2
matrix acting on
\nabla H
. Using this together with the normal and tangential decomposition for
\nabla H=(\partial_{n}H)n+(\partial_{\tau}H)\tau
gives

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}V\cdot n|\partial_{n}H|^{2}d\sigma=

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial\Omega}2\partial_{n}H\partial_{\tau}HV\cdot\tau d\sigma+\int_{\partial\Omega}V\cdot n|\partial_{\tau}H|^{2}d\sigma

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\cdot V)|\nabla H|^{2}dX+\int_{\Omega}2(\nabla V\nabla H)\cdot\nabla HdX.

Condition (
4.9
), Cauchy-Schwarz and Young’s inequalities yield

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\int_{\partial\Omega}|\partial_{n}H|^{2}d\sigma\leq

\displaystyle(1+8\|V\|_{L^{\infty}})\|V\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{\partial\Omega}|\partial_{\tau}H|^{2}d\sigma+3\|\nabla V\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla H|^{2}dX.

Plugging in the bounds for
V
in (
4.10
) we find that

(4.12)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}|\partial_{n}H|^{2}d\sigma\leq

\displaystyle 36\int_{\partial\Omega}|\partial_{\tau}H|^{2}d\sigma+12C\int_{\Omega}|\nabla H|^{2}dX.

Using the boundedness of
H
and the decay of
\nabla H
as
|y|\to\infty
, we can integrate by parts to obtain

(4.13)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla H|^{2}dX=\int_{\Omega}\nabla H\cdot\nabla[H-H(-\pi,f(-\pi))]dX=\int_{\partial\Omega}[H-H(-\pi,f(-\pi))]\partial_{n}Hd\sigma.

By the fundamental theorem of calculus and Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality,

\displaystyle\left|H(x,f(x))-H(-\pi,f(-\pi))\right|=\left|\int_{-\pi}^{x}\partial_{\tau}H(x^{\prime},f(x^{\prime}))\sqrt{1+|\partial_{x}f(x^{\prime})|^{2}}dx^{\prime}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\int_{-\pi}^{x}|\partial_{\tau}H(x^{\prime},f(x^{\prime}))|^{2}\sqrt{1+|\partial_{x}f(x^{\prime})|^{2}}dx^{\prime}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\int_{-\pi}^{x}\sqrt{1+|\partial_{x}f(x^{\prime})|^{2}}dx^{\prime}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\pi\|N\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}}\Big(\int_{\partial\Omega}|\partial_{\tau}H|^{2}d\sigma\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

It follows from this and Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality for (
4.13
) that

(4.14)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla H|^{2}dX\leq 2\pi\|N\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\Big(\int_{\partial\Omega}|\partial_{\tau}H|^{2}d\sigma\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\int_{\partial\Omega}|\partial_{n}H|^{2}d\sigma\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Plugging above inequality into (
4.12
) and using Young’s inequality we get

(4.15)

\int_{\partial\Omega}|\partial_{n}H|^{2}d\sigma\leq 2(12C\pi)^{2}\|N\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\int_{\partial\Omega}|\partial_{\tau}H|^{2}d\sigma.

Next, we employ the Rellich identity (
4.11
) for
V
and
H
in
\Omega^{c}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
to get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}|\nabla H|^{2}d\sigma

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\partial\Omega}2\partial^{c}_{n}H(V\cdot\nabla H)d\sigma+\int_{\Omega^{c}}(\nabla\cdot V)|\nabla H|^{2}dX-\int_{\Omega^{c}}2(\nabla V\nabla H)\cdot\nabla HdX

\displaystyle\leq 2\Big(\int_{\partial\Omega}|\partial_{n}^{c}H|^{2}d\sigma\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\int_{\partial\Omega}|\nabla H|^{2}d\sigma\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}+3C\int_{\Omega^{c}}|\nabla H|^{2}dX.

As before, using Young’s inequality and (
4.14
) for
\Omega^{c}
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\int_{\partial\Omega}|\nabla H|^{2}d\sigma

\displaystyle\leq 4\int_{\partial\Omega}|\partial_{n}^{c}H|^{2}d\sigma+6C\pi\|N\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\Big(\int_{\partial\Omega}|\partial_{\tau}H|^{2}d\sigma\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\int_{\partial\Omega}|\partial^{c}_{n}H|^{2}d\sigma\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The obvious bound
|\partial_{\tau}H|^{2}\leq|\nabla H|^{2}
combined with Young’s inequality yields

(4.16)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}|\partial_{\tau}H|^{2}d\sigma

\displaystyle\leq 2(12C\pi)^{2}\|N\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\int_{\partial\Omega}|\partial^{c}_{n}H|^{2}d\sigma.

The above argument when applied to
\Omega
gives

(4.17)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}|\partial_{\tau}H|^{2}d\sigma

\displaystyle\leq 2(12C\pi)^{2}\|N\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\int_{\partial\Omega}|\partial_{n}H|^{2}d\sigma.

Combining (
4.15
) and (
4.16
) allows us to relate both normal derivatives due to the continuity of the tangential derivative:

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}|\partial_{n}H|^{2}d\sigma

\displaystyle\leq 4(12C\pi)^{4}\|N\|^{4}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\int_{\partial\Omega}|\partial^{c}_{n}H|^{2}d\sigma,\quad C=O(\delta^{-1}).

Thus we obtain (
4.4
) with
M=2(12C\pi)^{2}(1+\|f\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T})})^{5/2}
. The opposite inequality

\|(\frac{1}{2}I+K^{*}[f])(\eta)\|_{L_{0}^{2}}\leq M\|(\frac{1}{2}I-K^{*}[f])(\eta)\|_{L_{0}^{2}}

follows by the same argument, completing the proof.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The invertibility of
(\frac{1}{2}I-K^{*})
on
L^{2}_{0}
was proved in
[
68
]
using the inequalities

(4.18)

\displaystyle\|(\frac{1}{2}I\pm K^{*})(\eta)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C\|(\frac{1}{2}I\mp K^{*})(\eta)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}+C\Big|\int_{\mathbb{T}}S[f](\eta)dx\Big|.

The inequalities in (
4.2
) dispense with the second term on the right-hand of (
4.18
) and hence allowed us to obtain the quantitative bound (
4.1
).

As an interesting corollary of the proof of Proposition
4.1
, we deduce a quantitative bound for the tangential derivative of the single layer potential in terms of the double layer potential. This in turn yields the continuity of
G(g):\dot{H}^{1}\to L^{2}
assuming only that the boundary is Lipschitz.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
f\in\Lip(\mathbb{T})
. There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that for all
\eta\in L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
, we have

(4.19)

\|\nabla S[f](\eta)\cdot(1,\partial_{x}f)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C(1+\|f\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T})})^{2}\|(\frac{1}{2}I-K^{*}[f])(\eta)\|_{L^{2}}.

Consequently,

(4.20)

\|G(f)g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C(1+\|f\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T})})^{2}\|\partial_{x}g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}

for all
g\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T})
.

Proof.

Denote
H(x,y)=S[f](\eta)(x,y)
the single lay potential as given by (
4.5
). Now (
4.17
) allows us to bound
\partial_{\tau}H
by
\partial_{n}H
as

\displaystyle\|\nabla S[f](\eta)\cdot(1,\partial_{x}f)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\|f\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T})})\|\partial_{\tau}H\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\|f\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T})})^{2}\|\partial_{n}H\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})},

where we recall that
\tau
and
n
are respectively the unit tangent and normal to the surface
\{y=f(x)\}
. On the other hand, it follows from the second equation in (
4.6
) that

\|\partial_{n}H\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\|\partial_{N}H\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}=\|(-\frac{1}{2}I+K^{*}[f])(\eta)\|_{L^{2}},

from which (
4.19
) follows.

By the density of
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T})
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{T})
, it suffices to prove (
4.20
) for
g\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T})
. Set
\theta=(\frac{1}{2}I-K^{*}[f])^{-1}(\partial_{x}g)
. Recall from (
3.15
) that
G(f)g
is a tangential derivative of
S[f]\theta
:

(G(f)g)(x)=\nabla S[f](\theta)(x,f(x))\cdot(1,\partial_{x}f(x)).

Thus (
4.20
) follows at once from (
4.19
) and the fact that
(\frac{1}{2}I-K^{*}[f])\theta=\partial_{x}g
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

The inequality (
4.20
) upgrades the continuity (
2.16
) by half a derivative without any additional regularity assumption on the surface
f
. By interpolating (
2.16
) and (
4.20
), we obtain

(4.21)

\|G(f)g\|_{H^{\sigma-1}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C(1+\|f\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T})})^{2}\|g\|_{\dot{H}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T})}

for all
\sigma\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
. For the same range of
\sigma
, this is an improvement over (
2.17
) which requires in addition that
f\in H^{s_{0}}(\mathbb{T})
for some
s_{0}>\frac{3}{2}
.

Remark 4.5
.

Proposition
4.1
and Corollary
4.3
are valid for general bounded domains of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
with Lipschitz boundary.

5.
Viscosity regularization

We consider the viscosity regularization of the Muskat problem:

(5.1)

\partial_{t}f^{\varepsilon}=-\kappa G(f^{\varepsilon})f^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\partial_{x}^{2}f^{\varepsilon},\quad\varepsilon\geq 0.

5.1.
Comparison principle for smooth solutions

Proposition 5.1

(
Comparison principle).

Assume that
f_{j}\in C([0,T];C^{2}(\mathbb{T}))
,
j=1,2
are smooth solutions of (
5.1
) with
\varepsilon\geq 0
. If
f_{1}(\cdot,0)\leq f_{2}(\cdot,0)
then

(5.2)

f_{1}(x,t)\leq f_{2}(x,t)\quad\forall(x,t)\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,T].

Proof.

Assume by contradiction that
M_{0}=\max_{\mathbb{T}\times[0,T]}(f_{1}-f_{2})=(f_{1}-f_{2})(x_{0},t_{0})>0
. We have
t_{0}>0
since
f_{1}(\cdot,0)\leq f_{2}(\cdot,0)
. Choose
\eta>0
sufficiently small so that
(f_{1}-f_{2}-\eta t)(x_{0},t_{0})=M_{0}-\eta t_{0}>0
, and thus
M_{*}=\max_{\mathbb{T}\times[0,T]}(f_{1}-f_{2}-\eta t)>0
. Moreover,
M_{*}
is attained at some point
(x_{*},t_{*})
with
t_{*}>0
because
(f_{1}-f_{2}-\eta t)|_{t=0}\leq 0
. Then

\max_{\mathbb{T}\times[0,T]}(f_{1}-f_{2}-\eta t-M_{*})=0

and is attained at
(x_{*},t_{*})
. Applying Proposition
2.15
gives

\big(G(f_{1})f_{1}\big)(x_{*},t_{*})\geq\big(G(f_{2}+\eta t+M_{*})(f_{2}+\eta t+M_{*})\big)(x_{*},t_{*}).

In addition, at the maximum point
(x_{*},t_{*})
with
t_{*}>0
we have

\partial_{t}f_{1}(x_{*},t_{*})\geq\partial_{t}(f_{2}+\eta t+M_{*})(x_{*},t_{*}),\quad\partial_{x}^{2}f_{1}(x_{*},t_{*})\leq\partial_{x}^{2}(f_{2}+\eta t+M_{*})(x_{*},t_{*}).

It follows that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(f_{2}+\eta t+M_{*})\leq\partial_{t}f_{1}

\displaystyle=-\kappa G(f_{1})f_{1}+\varepsilon\partial_{x}^{2}f_{1}

\displaystyle\leq-\kappa G(f_{2}+\eta t+M_{*})(f_{2}+\eta t+M_{*})+\varepsilon\partial_{x}^{2}f_{2}

\displaystyle=-\kappa G(f_{2})(f_{2})+\varepsilon\partial_{x}^{2}f_{2}=\partial_{t}f_{2}

at
(x_{*},t_{*})
. But this implies
\eta\leq 0
which is contradictory.
∎

Owing to the translation invariance of (
5.1
), comparison principle implies maximum principles for the amplitude and the slope of solutions.

Corollary 5.2

(
Maximum principles).

Let
f\in C([0,T];C^{2}(\mathbb{T}))
be a smooth solution of (
5.1
) with
\varepsilon\geq 0
. If
f(\cdot,0)
has the modulus of continuity
\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
then so does
f(\cdot,t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
. That is,

(5.3)

|f(x,t)-f(y,t)|\leq\gamma(|x-y|)\quad\forall(x,t)\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,T].

In particular, for
\gamma(z)=z\|f^{\varepsilon}(\cdot,0)\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T})}
we have

(5.4)

\|f(\cdot,t)\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T})}\leq\|f(\cdot,0)\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T})}\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

If
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are smooth solutions in
C([0,T];C^{2}(\mathbb{T}))
of (
5.1
) with
\varepsilon\geq 0
, then

(5.5)

\|f_{1}(\cdot,t)-f_{2}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\|f_{1}(\cdot,0)-f_{2}(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

Proof.

The modulus of continuity
\gamma
of
f(\cdot,0)
is equivalent to
f(x+y,0)\leq f(x,0)+\gamma(|y|)
for all
x,\,y\in\mathbb{T}
. For every fixed
y
, the function
f(x+y,t)+\gamma(|y|)
is a smooth solution of (
5.1
) with initial data
f(x+y,0)+\gamma(|y|)
. The comparison principle in Proposition
5.1
implies that

f(x,t)\leq f(x+y,t)+\gamma(|y|)
for all
x,\,y\in\mathbb{T}
and
t\in[0,T]
. Therefore,
|f(x,t)-f(x+y,t)|\leq\gamma(|y|)
, proving (
5.3
).

We turn to prove (
5.5
). We have
f_{1}(\cdot,0)\leq f_{2}(\cdot,0)+\|f_{1}(\cdot,0)-f_{2}(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}
and
f_{2}(\cdot,t)+\|f_{1}(\cdot,0)-f_{2}(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}
is a smooth solution of (
5.1
). By the comparison principle in Proposition
5.1
,

f_{1}(x,t)\leq f_{2}(x,t)+\|f_{1}(\cdot,0)-f_{2}(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\quad\forall(x,t)\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,T].

Thus we can interchange the role of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
to obtain (
5.5
).
∎

5.2.
Global well-posedness of smooth solutions

By virtue of Proposition
3.1
, in the class of Lipschitz solutions, (
5.1
) is equivalent to the system

(5.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}f^{\varepsilon}(x)&=\frac{1}{4\pi}p.v.\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\sin x^{\prime}+\sinh(f^{\varepsilon}(x)-f^{\varepsilon}(x-x^{\prime}))\partial_{x}f^{\varepsilon}(x)}{\cosh(f^{\varepsilon}(x)-f^{\varepsilon}(x-x^{\prime}))-\cos x^{\prime}}\theta^{\varepsilon}(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}+\varepsilon\partial_{x}^{2}f^{\varepsilon}(x),\\
\frac{1}{2}\theta^{\varepsilon}(x)&-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\sinh(f^{\varepsilon}(x)-f^{\varepsilon}(x-x^{\prime}))-\sin x^{\prime}\partial_{x}f^{\varepsilon}(x)}{\cosh(f^{\varepsilon}(x)-f^{\varepsilon}(x-x^{\prime}))-\cos x^{\prime}}\theta^{\varepsilon}(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}=-\kappa\partial_{x}f^{\varepsilon}(x).\end{split}

We prove that (
5.6
) is globally well-posed for any smooth initial data in Sobolev spaces.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
s\geq 2
. For each
f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T})
, there exists a unique solution
f^{\varepsilon}\in C([0,T];H^{s}(\mathbb{T}))\cap L^{2}([0,T];H^{s+1}(\mathbb{T}))
a solution to (
5.1
) for any time
T>0
with initial data
f^{\varepsilon}_{0}
. In particular,
f^{\varepsilon}
obeys the maximum principles

(5.7)

\|f^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\|f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})},\quad\|f^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T})}\leq\|f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T})}\quad\forall t\geq 0.

Proof.

We first note that the dissipative term
\varepsilon\partial_{x}^{2}f^{\varepsilon}
makes the regularized Muskat problem (
5.1
) semilinear. Then by the contraction mapping method, it was proved in Section 4.1.5
[
65
]
that for any
s>\frac{3}{2}
, (
5.1
) has a unique solution
f^{\varepsilon}\in C([0,T_{\varepsilon});H^{s})\cap L^{2}_{\text{loc}}([0,T_{\varepsilon});H^{s+1})
where
T_{\varepsilon}
is the maximal time. Moreover, if
T_{\varepsilon}<\infty
then

\lim\sup_{t\to T^{\varepsilon}}\|f^{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{s}}=\infty.

Therefore, to conclude that
f^{\varepsilon}
is global in time, it suffices to prove that the
H^{s}
norm of
f^{\varepsilon}
is bounded on
[0,T_{\varepsilon})
. To this end, we shall prove that for any
T<T_{\varepsilon}
, the
C([0,T];H^{s})
norm of
f^{\varepsilon}
can be controlled by
\|f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\|_{H^{s}}
and the
L^{\infty}([0,T];W^{1,\infty})
norm of
f^{\varepsilon}
, where the latter is uniformly bounded in time owing to the maximum principles (
5.4
) and (
5.5
).

For notational simplicity we shall write
f^{\varepsilon}=f
. In what follows,
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
denotes continuous nondecreasing functions that may change from line to line but only depend on
\kappa
.

L^{2}
estimate
. Using
(G(f)f,f)\geq 0
and
(\partial_{x}^{2}f,f)\leq 0
, we find at once that

(5.8)

\|f(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|f_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\quad\forall t>0.

\dot{H}^{1}
estimate
. Multiplying (
5.1
) by
-\partial_{x}^{2}f
, then integrating by parts and using the estimate (
4.20
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}\|G(f)f\|_{L^{2}}-\varepsilon\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}\|\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}-\varepsilon\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{-1}\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

By a Grönwall argument we find that

(5.9)

\|\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}(t)+\varepsilon\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}\leq\|\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}(0)\exp\Big(\varepsilon^{-1}\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})t\Big).

\dot{H}^{2}
estimate
. We differentiate (
5.1
) in
x
, then multiply by
-\partial_{x}^{3}f
and integrate by parts to obtain

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\kappa(\partial_{x}[G(f)f],\partial_{x}^{3}f)_{L^{2},L^{2}}-\varepsilon\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

where by Cauchy-Schwarz and Young’s inequalities

\kappa(\partial_{x}G(f)f,\partial_{x}^{3}f)_{L^{2},L^{2}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\varepsilon}\|\partial_{x}[G(f)f]\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

We claim that for any
\nu\in(0,1)
,

(5.10)

\|\partial_{x}[G(f)f]\|_{L^{2}}\leq\nu\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{\nu}\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})(\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}).

Taking (
5.10
) for granted, we choose
\nu=\frac{\varepsilon}{4\kappa}
so that the above estimates yield

(5.11)

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{-3}\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})(1+\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}})\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Then applying Grönwall’s lemma and (
5.9
) to bound

\int_{0}^{t}(1+\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}(t^{\prime}))dt^{\prime}\leq t+\varepsilon^{-1}\|\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}(0)\exp\Big(\varepsilon^{-1}\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})t\Big),

we arrive at the
\dot{H}^{2}
estimate

(5.12)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}(t)\!+\!\varepsilon\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}(s)ds\leq C(\|f_{0}\|_{H^{2}},\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}},t,\varepsilon)\end{split}.

The proof of (
5.10
) requires a careful decomposition of the singular integral representation (
3.12
)-(
3.14
) of
G(f)f
and is postponed to Lemma
5.4
below.

Higher regularity
. In this step we prove that
H^{s}
regularity with
s>2
can be propagated. To this end, we use Sobolev estimates for the Dirichlet-Neumann operator with the control norm in
L^{\infty}_{t}H^{\frac{3}{2}+}_{x}
provided by (
5.12
). Indeed, fixing
s_{0}\in(\frac{3}{2},2)
and applying (
2.17
) with
g=f
and
\sigma=s
, we have

(5.13)

\|G(f)f\|_{H^{s-1}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{H^{s_{0}}})\|f\|_{H^{s}}.

Then an
H^{s}
energy estimate for (
5.1
) gives

(5.14)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|f\|_{H^{s}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa(G(f)f,f)_{H^{s-1},H^{s+1}}-\varepsilon\|\partial_{x}f\|_{H^{s}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa\mathcal{F}(\|f(t)\|_{H^{s_{0}}})\|f(t)\|_{H^{s}}\|f(t)\|_{H^{s+1}}-\varepsilon\|f(t)\|_{H^{s+1}}^{2}+\varepsilon\|f(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\mathcal{F}^{2}(\|f(t)\|_{H^{s_{0}}})\|f(t)\|^{2}_{H^{s}}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|f(t)\|_{H^{s+1}}^{2}+\varepsilon\|f_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

where in the last inequality we used Young’s inequality and the maximum principle for the
L^{2}
norm of
f
. By Grönwall’s lemma, we obtain

(5.15)

\|f(t)\|_{H^{s}}^{2}+\varepsilon\|f\|_{L^{2}([0,t];H^{s+1})}^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon t)\|f_{0}\|_{H^{s}}^{2}\exp\Big(t\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{F}^{2}\big(\|f\|_{L^{\infty}([0,t];H^{s_{0}})}\big)\Big).

Then using the
\dot{H}^{2}
estimate (
5.12
) together with (
5.8
), (
5.5
) and (
5.4
), we arrive at

(5.16)

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T];H^{s})}\leq C(\|f_{0}\|_{H^{s}},T,\varepsilon)\quad\forall T<T_{\varepsilon}.

This concludes the proof of global regularity for (
5.1
).
∎

We now prove the claim (
5.10
).

Lemma 5.4
.

There exists
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
nondecreasing such that for any
\nu\in(0,1)
,

(5.17)

\|\partial_{x}[G(f^{\varepsilon})f^{\varepsilon}]\|_{L^{2}}\leq\nu\|\partial_{x}^{3}f^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}+\frac{1}{\nu}\mathcal{F}(\|f^{\varepsilon}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T})})(\|\partial_{x}^{2}f^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial_{x}^{2}f^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}^{2}).

Proof.

We shall write
f^{\varepsilon}=f
,
\theta^{\varepsilon}=\theta
, and
\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x)=f(x)-f(x-x^{\prime})
. Recall that
G(f)f
can be written as on the right-hand side of the first equation in (
5.6
) with
\theta=(\frac{1}{2}I-K^{*})^{-1}(-\partial_{x}f)
. Moreover, by virtue of Proposition
4.1
,

(5.18)

\|\theta\|_{L^{2}}\leq C(1+\|\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{\infty}})^{\frac{5}{2}}\|\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}\leq\sqrt{2\pi}C(1+\|\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{\infty}})^{\frac{7}{2}}.

We decompose
4\pi\partial_{x}[G(f)f](x)=\sum_{j=1}^{4}G_{j}(x)
where

G_{3}(x)=-\int\frac{[\sin x^{\prime}+\sinh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))\partial_{x}f(x)]\sinh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))\delta_{x^{\prime}}(\partial_{x}f)(x)}{(\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime})^{2}}\theta(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

and

Here and throughout this proof we write
\int=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}
.

Control of
G_{1}
. We split
G_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{5}G_{1,j}
where, for

to be chosen,

G_{1,4}(x)=\partial_{x}f(x)\partial_{x}^{2}f(x)\int_{|x^{\prime}|<\delta}\Big(\frac{1}{\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime}}-\frac{2}{(1+(\partial_{x}f(x))^{2})(x^{\prime})^{2}}\Big)x^{\prime}\theta(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

and

G_{1,5}(x)=\frac{2\partial_{x}f(x)\partial_{x}^{2}f(x)}{1+(\partial_{x}f(x))^{2}}\int_{|x^{\prime}|<\delta}\frac{\theta(x-x^{\prime})}{x^{\prime}}dx^{\prime}.

We shall use frequently the facts that

(5.19)

\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime}=2\sinh^{2}\big(\frac{\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x)}{2}\big)+2\sin^{2}\big(\frac{x^{\prime}}{2}\big)

(5.20)

\frac{|x^{\prime}|}{2}\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\big|\sin\frac{x^{\prime}}{2}\big|\quad\forall x^{\prime}\in[-\pi,\pi].

C

\displaystyle\|G_{1,2}\|_{L^{2}}

where we bounded the

term in the estimates for
G_{1,1}
and
G_{1,4}
by
C\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

For any
\nu\in(0,1)

with

(5.21)

\|G_{1}\|_{L^{2}}\leq 2^{-10}\nu\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{\nu}\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}(1+\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}})^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Control of
G_{2}
. We decompose

where

The integral kernel in

\|G_{2,1}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}\Big\|\int|\theta(\cdot-x^{\prime})|dx^{\prime}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}.

\|G_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\big(\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}\big)

\nu\in(0,1)
, we have

(5.23)

\|G_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\leq 2^{-10}\nu\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{\nu}\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})(\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}).

Control of
G_{3}
. We split
G_{3}=\sum_{j=1}^{7}G_{3,j}

where

\displaystyle G_{3,1}(x)=

\displaystyle\int\frac{(\sinh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))\partial_{x}f(x)\sinh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))\delta_{x^{\prime}}(\partial_{x}f)(x)}{(\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime})^{2}}\theta(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\int\frac{\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x)\partial_{x}f(x)(\sinh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))\delta_{x^{\prime}}(\partial_{x}f)(x)}{(\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime})^{2}}\theta(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

G_{3,2}(x)=\int_{|x^{\prime}|>\delta}\frac{(\sin x^{\prime}+\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x)\partial_{x}f(x))\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x)\delta_{x^{\prime}}(\partial_{x}f)(x)}{(\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime})^{2}}\theta(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

\displaystyle G_{3,3}(x)=\int_{|x^{\prime}|<\delta}\frac{(\sin x^{\prime}+\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x)\partial_{x}f(x))(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x)-\partial_{x}f(x)x^{\prime})\delta_{x^{\prime}}(\partial_{x}f)(x)}{(\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime})^{2}}\theta(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

G_{3,4}(x)=\partial_{x}f(x)\int_{|x^{\prime}|<\delta}\frac{(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x)-\partial_{x}f(x)x^{\prime})x^{\prime}\delta_{x^{\prime}}(\partial_{x}f)(x)}{(\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime})^{2}}\theta(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

G_{3,5}(x)=\partial_{x}f(x)\int_{|x^{\prime}|<\delta}\frac{(\sin x^{\prime}+(\partial_{x}f(x))^{2}x^{\prime})x^{\prime}(\delta_{x^{\prime}}(\partial_{x}f)(x)-\partial_{x}^{2}f(x)x^{\prime})}{(\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime})^{2}}\theta(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

\displaystyle G_{3,6}(x)=

\displaystyle\partial_{x}f(x)\partial_{x}^{2}f(x)\int_{|x^{\prime}|<\delta}(\sin x^{\prime}+(\partial_{x}f(x))^{2}x^{\prime})(x^{\prime})^{2}A(x,x^{\prime})\theta(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}

with

\displaystyle A(x,x^{\prime})=\Big(\frac{1}{(\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime})^{2}}-\frac{4}{(1+(\partial_{x}f(x))^{2})^{2}(x^{\prime})^{4}}\Big),

and

\displaystyle G_{3,7}(x)=

\displaystyle\frac{4\partial_{x}f(x)\partial_{x}^{2}f(x)}{(1+(\partial_{x}f(x))^{2})^{2}}\int_{|x^{\prime}|<\delta}\frac{\sin x^{\prime}+(\partial_{x}f(x))^{2}x^{\prime}}{(x^{\prime})^{2}}\theta(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}.

The integral kernel in
G_{3,1}
is smooth enough so that

\|G_{3,1}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\|\theta\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}.

By brutal force we have

\|G_{3,2}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}f\|_{C^{\frac{1}{2}}}\Big\|\int_{|x^{\prime}|>\delta}\frac{|\theta(\cdot-x^{\prime})|}{|x^{\prime}|^{\frac{3}{2}}}dx^{\prime}\Big\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\frac{\|\partial^{2}_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}}{\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

For the next two terms, Gagliardo-Nirenberg’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|G_{3,3}\|_{L^{2}}+\|G_{3,4}\|_{L^{2}}\leq&\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\Big\|\int_{|x^{\prime}|<\delta}|\theta(\cdot-x^{\prime})|dx^{\prime}\Big\|_{L^{2}}\\
\leq&\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}\delta.\end{split}

The term
G_{3,5}
is bounded by

\|G_{3,5}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{\dot{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\Big\|\int_{|x^{\prime}|<\delta}\frac{|\theta(\cdot-x^{\prime})|}{|x^{\prime}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}dx^{\prime}\Big\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}.

As for
G_{1,4}
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|G_{3,6}\|_{L^{2}}\leq&\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\Big\|\int_{|x^{\prime}|<\delta}|\theta(\cdot-x^{\prime})|dx^{\prime}\Big\|_{L^{2}}\\
\leq&\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}\delta.\end{split}

Finally, the kernels in
G_{3,7}
are of Calderón-Zygmund type in such a way that

\|G_{3,7}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Gathering the above estimates we find that

\|G_{3}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\big(\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}\delta+\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}+\|\partial^{2}_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}\delta^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big).

For any
\nu\in(0,1)
, using Young’s inequality for the first term and choosing

\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{\nu}{2^{11}\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})(1+\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}})},

we obtain

(5.24)

\|G_{3}\|_{L^{2}}\leq 2^{-10}\nu\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{\nu}\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}.

Control of
G_{4}
. We first note that

G_{4}(x)=4\pi\nabla(S[f](\partial_{x}\theta))(x,f(x))\cdot(1,\partial_{x}f(x)).

In view of the bound (
4.19
) for tangential derivative of
S[f]
, we have

\|G_{4}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|(\frac{1}{2}I-K^{*})(\partial_{x}\theta)\|_{L^{2}}.

To proceed, we differentiate the second equation in (
5.6
) with respect to
x
to have

(5.25)

(\frac{1}{2}I-K^{*})(\partial_{x}\theta)(x)=-\kappa\partial_{x}^{2}f(x)+G_{4,1}+G_{4,2}

where

G_{4,1}(x)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\frac{\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))\delta_{x^{\prime}}(\partial_{x}f)(x)-\sin x^{\prime}\partial_{x}^{2}f(x)}{\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime}}\theta(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

and

(5.26)

G_{4,2}(x)=\frac{-1}{4\pi}\int\frac{(\sinh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\sin x^{\prime}\partial_{x}f(x))\sinh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))\delta_{x^{\prime}}(\partial_{x}f)(x)}{(\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime})^{2}}\theta(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}.

Therefore,

(5.27)

\|G_{4}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})(\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}+\|G_{4,1}\|_{L^{2}}+\|G_{4,2}\|_{L^{2}})

and it remains to bound
G_{4,1}
and
G_{4,2}
. A further splitting gives
G_{4,1}=G_{4,1}^{1}+G_{4,1}^{2}+G_{4,1}^{3}+G_{4,1}^{4}
where

G_{4,1}^{1}(x)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\frac{(\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime}+\cos x^{\prime}-1)\delta_{x^{\prime}}(\partial_{x}f)(x)}{\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime}}\theta(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

G_{4,1}^{2}(x)=\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}f(x)}{4\pi}\int\frac{x^{\prime}-\sin x^{\prime}}{\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime}}\theta(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

G_{4,1}^{3}(x)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{|x^{\prime}|>\delta}\frac{\delta_{x^{\prime}}(\partial_{x}f)(x)-x^{\prime}\partial_{x}^{2}f(x)}{\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime}}\theta(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

and

G_{4,1}^{4}(x)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{|x^{\prime}|<\delta}\frac{\delta_{x^{\prime}}(\partial_{x}f)(x)-x^{\prime}\partial_{x}^{2}f(x)}{\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime}}\theta(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}.

Since the kernel integral in
G_{4,1}^{1}
is bounded by
C\|\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{\infty}}
, we have

\|G_{4,1}^{1}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\theta\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}.

Similarly, the kernel integral in
G_{4,2}^{2}
is bounded by an absolute constant, yielding

\|G_{4,1}^{2}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}\|\theta\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}.

Using convolution properties, we bound

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|G_{4,1}^{3}\|_{L^{2}}&\leq C\|\partial_{x}f\|_{\dot{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\Big\|\int_{|x^{\prime}|>\delta}\frac{|\theta(\cdot-x^{\prime})|}{|x^{\prime}|^{\frac{3}{2}}}dx^{\prime}\Big\|_{L^{2}}+C\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}\Big\|\int_{|x^{\prime}|>\delta}\frac{|\theta(\cdot-x^{\prime})|}{|x^{\prime}|}dx^{\prime}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}\\
&\leq C\frac{\|\partial_{x}f\|_{\dot{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\|\theta\|_{L^{2}}}{\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}}+C\frac{\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}\|\theta\|_{L^{2}}}{\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}}\\
&\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\frac{\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}}{\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}}.\end{split}

Similarly,
G_{4,1}^{4}
can be controlled as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|G_{4,1}^{4}\|_{L^{2}}\leq&\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{\dot{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\Big\|\int_{|x^{\prime}|<\delta}\frac{|\theta(\cdot-x^{\prime})|}{|x^{\prime}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}dx^{\prime}\Big\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

We have proved that

\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|G_{4,1}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}+\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}\big(\delta^{-\frac{1}{2}}+1\big).

For any
\nu\in(0,1)
, choosing

\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{\nu}{2^{10}\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})(1+\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}})},

we obtain

(5.28)

\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|G_{4,1}\|_{L^{2}}\leq 2^{-10}\nu\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{\nu}\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\big(\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\big).

As for
G_{4,2}
, we decompose
G_{4,2}=\sum_{j=1}^{4}G_{4,2}^{j}
where

G_{4,2}^{1}(x)=\frac{-1}{4\pi}\int\frac{(\sinh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))\sinh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))\delta_{x^{\prime}}(\partial_{x}f)(x)}{(\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime})^{2}}\theta(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

G_{4,2}^{2}(x)=\frac{-\partial_{x}f(x)}{4\pi}\int\frac{(x^{\prime}-\sin x^{\prime})\sinh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))\delta_{x^{\prime}}(\partial_{x}f)(x)}{(\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime})^{2}}\theta(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

G_{4,2}^{3}(x)=\frac{-1}{4\pi}\int_{|x^{\prime}|>\delta}\frac{(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x)-x^{\prime}\partial_{x}f(x))\sinh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))\delta_{x^{\prime}}(\partial_{x}f)(x)}{(\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime})^{2}}\theta(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

and

G_{4,2}^{4}(x)=\frac{-1}{4\pi}\int_{|x^{\prime}|<\delta}\frac{(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x)-x^{\prime}\partial_{x}f(x))\sinh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))\delta_{x^{\prime}}(\partial_{x}f)(x)}{(\cosh(\delta_{x^{\prime}}f(x))-\cos x^{\prime})^{2}}\theta(x-x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}.

Since the integral kernel in
G_{4,2}^{1}
is bounded by
\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})
, it follows that

\|G_{4,2}^{1}\|_{L^{2}}+\|G_{4,2}^{2}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\theta\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}.

Next we estimate
G_{4,2}^{3}
and
G_{4,2}^{4}
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|G_{4,2}^{3}\|_{L^{2}}\leq&\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\Big\|\int_{|x^{\prime}|>\delta}\frac{|\theta(\cdot-x^{\prime})|}{|x^{\prime}|^{2}}dx^{\prime}\Big\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\frac{\|\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}}{\delta}\end{split}

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|G_{4,2}^{4}\|_{L^{2}}\leq&\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\Big\|\int_{|x^{\prime}|<\delta}|\theta(\cdot-x^{\prime})|dx^{\prime}\Big\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}\delta.\end{split}

We thus obtain

\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|G_{4,2}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}\delta+\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}(\delta^{-1}+1).

For any
\nu\in(0,1)
, choosing

\delta=\frac{\nu}{2^{10}\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})(1+\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}})}

gives

(5.29)

\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|G_{4,2}\|_{L^{2}}\leq 2^{-10}\nu\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{\nu}\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}.

Then plugging (
5.28
) and (
5.29
) into (
5.27
) we obtain

(5.30)

\|G_{4}\|_{L^{2}}\leq 2^{-9}\nu\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{\nu}\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}.

Finally, combining (
5.21
), (
5.23
), (
5.24
) and (
5.30
) we conclude that

\|\partial_{x}[G(f)f]\|_{L^{2}}\leq\nu\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{\nu}\mathcal{F}(\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}})(\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}),

which finishes the proof.
∎

6.
Viscosity solutions and comparison principle

By virtue of the comparison principle for the Dirichlet-Neumann operator given by Proposition
2.15
and the fact that
G(f+C)(f+C)=G(f)f
for any constant
C
, we have the following natural definition of viscosity solutions for the Muskat problem (
2.8
).

Definition 6.1

(
Viscosity solutions).

A function
f:\mathbb{T}\times[0,T]
is called a viscosity subsolution (resp. supersolution) of (
2.8
) on
(0,T)
provided that

(i)
f
is upper semicontinuous (resp. lower semicontinuous) on
\mathbb{T}\times[0,T]
, and

(ii) for every
\psi:\mathbb{T}\times(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}
with
\partial_{t}\psi\in C(\mathbb{T}\times(0,T))
and
\psi\in C((0,T);C^{1,1}(\mathbb{T}))
, if
f-\psi
attains a global maximum (resp. minimum) over
\mathbb{T}\times[t_{0}-r,t_{0}]
at
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{T}\times(0,T)
for some
r>0
, then

(6.1)

\partial_{t}\psi(x_{0},t_{0})\leq-\kappa\big(G(\psi)\psi\big)(x_{0},t_{0})\quad(\text{resp. }\geq).

A viscosity solution is both a viscosity subsolution and viscosity supersolution.

The next proposition shows that if a viscosity solution is regular (
C^{1,1}
) at a point
(x_{0},t_{0})
then it satisfies the equation classically at the same point.

Proposition 6.2

(
Consistency).

Let
f
be a viscosity subsolution of (
2.8
) on
(0,T)
. Assume that
f\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,T))
and
f
is
C^{1,1}
at
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{T}\times(0,T)
. Then
G(f)f
is classically well-defined at
(x_{0},t_{0})
and

(6.2)

\partial_{t}f(x_{0},t_{0})\leq-\kappa\big(G(f)f\big)(x_{0},t_{0}).

The corresponding statement for viscosity supersolutions holds true.

Proof.

According to Proposition
2.15
,
G(f)f
is classically well-defined at
(x_{0},t_{0})
. We shall write
X=(x,t)
and
X_{0}=(x_{0},t_{0})
. Since
f
is
C^{1,1}
at
X_{0}
, for some
r_{0}\in(0,\frac{1}{10}\min\{\pi,t_{0},T-t_{0}\})
and
C>0
, we have

(6.3)

f(X)\leq f(X_{0})+\nabla_{X}f(X_{0})\cdot(X-X_{0})+\frac{C}{2}|X-X_{0}|^{2}:=\overline{\psi}(X),\quad|X-X_{0}|<r_{0}.

Let
\psi
be the tangent parabola with double opening:

(6.4)

\psi(X)=f(X_{0})+\nabla_{X}f(X_{0})\cdot(X-X_{0})+C|X-X_{0}|^{2}.

We can find a family of functions
\psi_{r}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})
,
r\in(0,r_{0})
satisfying

(6.5)

\displaystyle\psi_{r}\leq\psi\quad\text{in }B_{r_{0}}(X_{0}),

\displaystyle\psi_{r}\geq f\quad\text{on }\mathbb{T}\times[0,T],

\displaystyle\forall\delta\in(0,r_{0}^{2}),\quad\psi_{r}\to f\quad\text{in}\,\,C(\mathbb{T}\times[\delta,T-\delta]),

\displaystyle\forall\delta\in(0,r_{0}^{2}),\quad\sup_{r\in(0,\sqrt{\delta})}\|\psi_{r}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T}\times[\delta,T-\delta])}<\infty.

The construction of
\psi_{r}
is postponed to Appendix
C
. The properties in (
6.5
) imply that
\psi_{r}(t_{0})
’s are uniformly
C^{1,1}
at
x_{0}
, i.e. there exists

M_{0}>0
independent of
r\in(0,r_{0})
such that

(6.6)

|\psi_{r}(x_{0}+x,t_{0})+\psi_{r}(x_{0}-x,t_{0})-2\psi_{r}(x_{0},t_{0})|\leq M|x|^{2}\quad\forall|x|<r_{0}.

Since each
\psi_{r}
is a valid test function for the viscosity subsolution
f
, we have

(6.7)

\partial_{t}\psi_{r}(X_{0})\leq-\kappa\big(G(\psi_{r})\psi_{r}\big)(X_{0}).

At the maximum point
X_{0}
of
f-\psi_{r}
, we have
\partial_{t}f=\partial_{t}\psi_{r}
. Thus (
6.2
) will be a consequence of (
6.7
) and

(6.8)

\lim_{r\to 0}\big(G(\psi_{r})\psi_{r}\big)(X_{0})=\big(G(f)f)(X_{0}).

We shall skip the time variable in the remainder of the proof because
t=t_{0}
is fixed in (
6.8
). The proof of (
6.8
) proceeds in two steps.

Step 1.
Let
\phi_{r}
(reps.
\phi
) be the harmonic extension of
\psi_{r}
to
\Omega_{r}\equiv\Omega_{\psi_{r}}
(resp.
\Omega_{f}
).
We claim that

(6.9)

\lim_{r\to 0}\phi_{r}(x,y)=\phi(x,y)\quad\forall(x,y)\in\Omega_{f},

where we note that
\Omega_{f}\subset\Omega_{r}
for all
r
. From (
2.41
), (
2.42
) and the uniform boundedness of
\|\psi_{r}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T})}
, we have

(6.10)

\|\phi_{r}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{r})}\leq\|\psi_{r}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq M.

Fix a sequence of bounded subsets
\Omega^{j}\Subset\Omega_{f}
satisfying
\Omega^{j}\subset\Omega^{j+1}
and
\cup_{j\geq 1}\Omega^{j}=\Omega_{f}
. Let
r_{n}
be an arbitrary sequence of positive numbers converging to
0
. We relabel
\phi_{n}=\phi_{r_{n}}
and
\Omega_{n}\equiv\Omega_{r_{n}}
. The uniform bound (
6.10
) implies that every subsequence of
\phi_{n}
has a subsequence converging weakly-* in
L^{\infty}(\Omega_{f})
. For notational simplicity, we write
\phi_{n}\to\phi_{\infty}
weakly-* in
L^{\infty}(\Omega_{f})
. We have the gradient estimate for the harmonic functions
\phi_{j}

\|\nabla_{x,y}\phi_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{j})}\leq C_{j}\|\phi_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{f})}\leq C_{j}M.

By virtue of the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem, for every
j
there exists a subsequence of
\phi_{n}
converging to
\phi_{\infty}
in
C(\overline{\Omega^{j}})
. Using the Cantor diagonal argument, we can find a subsequence
\phi_{n_{k}}\to\phi_{\infty}
in
C(\overline{\Omega^{j}})
for all
j
. Thus (
6.9
) will follow once we can prove that

(6.11)

\phi_{\infty}=\phi.

This can be achieved by using the variational characterization of
\phi_{n_{k}}
and
\phi
. Indeed, we recall from (
2.13
) and (
2.14
) that
\phi_{n}=u_{n}+\underline{\psi_{n}}
, where
\underline{\psi_{n}}\in\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})
with
\underline{\psi_{n}}(x,\psi_{n}(x))=\psi_{n}(x)
(in the trace sense)
and
u_{n}\in\dot{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{n})
satisfies

(6.12)

\int_{\Omega_{n}}\nabla_{x,y}u_{n}\cdot\nabla_{x,y}\varphi dxdy=-\int_{\Omega_{n}}\nabla_{x,y}\underline{\psi_{n}}\cdot\nabla_{x,y}\varphi dxdy\quad\forall\varphi\in\dot{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{n}).

In particular, the uniform bounds

\displaystyle\|u_{n}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}\leq\|\underline{\psi_{n}}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}\leq C(1+\|\psi_{n}\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T}^{d})})\|\psi_{n}\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq\widetilde{C},

\displaystyle\|\phi_{n}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}\leq 2\|\underline{\psi_{n}}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}\leq 2\widetilde{C}

hold. Since
\Omega_{f}\subset\Omega_{n}
, upon passing to a subsequence of
n_{k}
, we have

(6.13)

u_{n_{k}}\rightharpoonup u_{\infty}\quad\text{in }\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f}),\quad\phi_{n_{k}}\rightharpoonup\phi_{\infty}\quad\text{in }\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f}).

As for the convergence of
\underline{\psi_{n}}
, we recall from Theorem
A.2
that we can choose

Theorem 6.3

(
Comparison principle).

Assume that

are respectively a bounded viscosity subsolution and supersolution of (

) on

Proof.

For classical sub and supersolutions, one can follow the proof of Proposition
5.1
. Here the source difficulty comes from the low regularity of

and
g

and

be respectively the sup-convolution and inf-convolution of

and
g

\displaystyle f^{\delta}(x,t)=\sup_{(y,s)\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,T]}f(y,t)-\frac{1}{2\delta}\big(|x-y|^{2}+|t-s|^{2}\big),

\displaystyle g_{\delta}(x,t)=\inf_{(y,s)\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,T]}g(y,t)+\frac{1}{2\delta}\big(|x-y|^{2}+|t-s|^{2}\big)

for
(x,t)\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,T]

(reps.
g

and

[
35

(i)

.

(ii)

f^{\delta}
(reps.
g_{\delta})
is semiconvex (reps. semiconcave) in the senses that each point has a tangent paraboloid from below (reps. above) with opening
\delta^{-1}
.

(iii)

The half-relaxed limits

(6.17)

\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta\to 0\\
(y,s)\to(x,t)\end{subarray}}f^{\delta}(y,s)=f(x,t),\quad\liminf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta\to 0\\
(y,s)\to(x,t)\end{subarray}}g_{\delta}(y,s)=g(x,t)

hold for all
(x,t)\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,T]
.

The boundedness of

and

is only used in (i).

Next we prove that each

(reps.

) is a viscosity subsolution (reps. supersolution) of (

). Indeed, let

\displaystyle f^{\delta}(x_{0},t_{0})=f(y_{0},s_{0})-\frac{1}{2\delta}\big(|x_{0}-y_{0}|^{2}+|t_{0}-s_{0}|^{2}\big),

\displaystyle f^{\delta}(x,t)\geq f(x-x_{0}+y_{0},t-t_{0}+s_{0})-\frac{1}{2\delta}\big(|x_{0}-y_{0}|^{2}+|t_{0}-s_{0}|^{2}\big).

Therefore, the function

Appendix A
Traces for homogeneous Sobolev spaces

Let
f\in\Lip(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and recall the notation
\Omega_{f}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}:y<f(x)\}
. Let
\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f})
denote the homogeneous Sobolev space defined by (
2.9
). We prove the trace and lifting theorems for functions in
\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f})
.

Theorem A.1
.

There exists a unique bounded linear trace operator
\Tr:\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f})\to\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
such that the following hold.

(1)

\Tr(u)(x)=u(x,f(x))
for all
u\in\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f})\cap C(\overline{\Omega_{f}})
.

(2)

For some constant
C=C(d)
,

(A.1)

\|\Tr(u)\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq C(1+\|f\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T}^{d})})\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f})}.

Proof.

By the density of
C^{\infty}_{c}(\overline{\Omega_{f}})
in
\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f})
, it suffices to prove the estimate (
A.1
) for
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\overline{\Omega_{f}})
.

Step 1:

f=0
, i.e.
\Omega_{f}=\Omega_{0}=\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{-}
. We extend
u
to
\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
by even reflection. That is,
u(x,y)=u(x,-y)
for
y>0
. Clearly,
\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R})}\leq 2\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{-})}
. We have

\Tr(u)(x)=u(x,0)
and

\widehat{\Tr(u)}(k)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{u}(k,\xi)d\xi,\quad\widehat{u}(k,\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}u(x,y)e^{-ixk-iy\xi}dydx.

By the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
,

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{u}(k,\xi)d\xi\right|^{2}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}(k^{2}+\xi^{2})|\widehat{u}(k,\xi)|^{2}d\xi\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{k^{2}+\xi^{2}}d\xi\leq\frac{\pi}{|k|}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(k^{2}+\xi^{2})|\widehat{u}(k,\xi)|^{2}d\xi,

so

\displaystyle\|\Tr(u)\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}|k||\widehat{\Tr(u)}(k)|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(k^{2}+\xi^{2})|\widehat{u}(k,\xi)|^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle=C(d)\|\nabla_{x,y}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R})}^{2}\leq 2C(d)\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{-})}^{2}.

Step 2:

f\in\Lip(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Setting
v(x,z)=u(x,z+f(x))
for
(x,z)\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{-}
, we have
\Tr(u)(x)=v(x,0)
and
v\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{-})
with

\|v\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{-})}\leq(1+\|f\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T}^{d})})\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f})}.

Using Step 1 we deduce that

\|\Tr(u)\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=\|v(\cdot,0)\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq C(d)\|v\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{-})}\leq C(d)(1+\|f\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T}^{d})})\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f})},

thereby finishing the proof.
∎

Theorem A.2
.

For every
g\in\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, there exists
u\in\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f})
such that
\Tr(u)=g
and for some universal constant
C=C(d)
,

(A.2)

\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f})}\leq C(1+\|f\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T}^{d})})\|g\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

Moreover, we can choose
u(x,y)=u_{\sharp}(x,y-f(x))
, where
u_{\sharp}(x,Y)=e^{Y|D_{x}|}g(x)
for
(x,Y)\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{-}
.

Proof.

Clearly, with the given function
u
we have
u(x,f(x))=u_{\sharp}(x,0)=g(x)
.
Since
\partial_{x_{j}}u_{\sharp}(x,Y)=e^{Y|D_{x}|}\partial_{x_{j}}g(x)
and
\partial_{Y}u_{\sharp}(x,Y)=e^{Y|D_{x}|}|D_{x}|g(x)
, using the Plancherel Theorem for the
x
variable one can easily prove that

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\|\partial_{x_{j}}u_{\sharp}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{-})}=C_{1}(d)\|g\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2},\quad\|\partial_{Y}u_{\sharp}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{-})}=C_{2}(d)\|g\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

Consequently,

\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega_{f})}\leq 2(1+\|f\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T}^{d})})\|u_{\sharp}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{-})}\leq C(d)(1+\|f\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T}^{d})})\|g\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}

which finishes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
The Dirichlet-Neumann operator for the disk

Proposition B.1
.

Let
g\in C(\mathbb{T})
be
C^{1,\alpha}
at
x\in\mathbb{T}
. Let
u\in C(\overline{B_{1}})
be the unique solution of the Dirichlet problem

\Delta u=0\quad\text{in~}B_{1}(0),\quad u|_{\partial B_{1}(0)}=g,

where
e^{ix}\equiv(\cos x,\sin x)
. Then the normal derivative

\partial_{n}u(e^{ix}):=\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}e^{ix}\cdot\nabla u(re^{ix})

is well-defined and

(B.1)

\partial_{n}u(e^{ix})=-\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{g(x+x^{\prime})+g(x-x^{\prime})-2g(x)}{\sin^{2}(\frac{x^{\prime}}{2})}dx^{\prime}.

Proof.

Since
g
is
C^{1,\alpha}
at
x
, there exist
M>0
and
\gamma>0
such that

(B.2)

|g(x+x^{\prime})-g(x)-\partial g(x)x^{\prime}|\leq M|x^{\prime}|^{1+\alpha}\quad\forall|x^{\prime}|<\gamma.

Since
g\in C(\mathbb{T})
,

is given by the Poisson formula

u(z)=\int_{\partial B_{1}(0)}K(z,z^{\prime})g(z^{\prime})dS(z^{\prime}),\quad K(z,z^{\prime})=\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{2\pi}\frac{1}{|z-z^{\prime}|^{2}}

. Consequently,

(B.3)

z\cdot\nabla u(z)=\int_{\partial B_{1}(0)}z\cdot\nabla_{z}K(z,z^{\prime})g(z^{\prime})dS(z^{\prime}),

(B.4)

z\cdot\nabla_{z}K(z,z^{\prime})=-\frac{|z|^{2}}{\pi|z-z^{\prime}|^{2}}-(1-|z|^{2})\frac{z\cdot(z-z^{\prime})}{\pi|z-z^{\prime}|^{4}}.

When

we have

and thus (
B.3
) yields

(B.5)

\int_{\partial B_{1}(0)}z\cdot\nabla_{z}K(z,z^{\prime})dS(z^{\prime})=0,\quad z\in B_{1}(0).

Denoting

and using (
B.5

(B.6)

\displaystyle z\cdot\nabla u(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{1}(0)}z\cdot\nabla_{z}K(z,z^{\prime})[g(z^{\prime})-g(z_{*})]dS(z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{|z|^{2}}{\pi}\int_{\partial B_{1}(0)}\frac{1}{|z-z^{\prime}|^{2}}[g(z_{*})-g(z^{\prime})]dS(z^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{\pi}\int_{\partial B_{1}(0)}\frac{z\cdot(z-z^{\prime})}{|z-z^{\prime}|^{4}}[g(z_{*})-g(z^{\prime})]dS(z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=J_{1}+J_{2},\quad z\in B_{1}(0).

\|h^{(r)}-h\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}\leq r\|h\|_{\Lip(U+B_{r}(0))}.

For
r\in(0,r_{0})
, let
g_{r}=f^{(r^{2})}+(M+2)r^{2}
, so that
g_{r}-f\geq 2r^{2}
. By increasing
C
if necessary, we can assume
C>4(M+2)
. Then for
|X-X_{0}|\in[r,r_{0})
, we have

(C.1)

\displaystyle\psi(x)-g_{r}(x)

\displaystyle\geq\nabla_{X}f(X_{0})(X-X_{0})+C|X-X_{0}|^{2}-\big[f(X)+Mr^{2}+(M+2)r^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\geq\overline{\psi}(X)-f(X)+\frac{C}{2}r^{2}-2(M+1)r^{2}\geq 2r^{2}.

Let
F(a,b)=\min\{a,b\}
. Note that
\|F\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=1
and
F^{(\delta)}(a,b)=a
if
a<b-\delta
. In addition,
F^{(\delta)}(a,b)\leq a^{(\delta)}=a
and likewise
F^{(\delta)}(a,b)\leq b
.

Define
\psi_{r}=F^{(r^{2})}(g_{r},\psi)
in
B_{r}(X_{0})
and
\psi_{r}=g_{r}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{r}(X_{0})
. In fact, (
C.1
) shows that
\psi(X)-g_{r}(X)\geq r^{2}
in an open neighborhood of
\partial B_{r}(X_{0})
, so that
\psi_{r}=g_{r}
in the same neighborhood. It follows that
\psi_{r}
is a smooth function on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We have
\psi_{r}\leq\psi
in
B_{r}(X_{0})
and in view of (
C.1
),
\psi_{r}(X)\leq\psi(X)
for
|X-X_{0}|\in[r,r_{0})
. Consequently,
\psi_{r}\leq\psi
in
B_{r_{0}}(X_{0})
. In
B_{r}(X_{0})
, when
\psi_{r}(X)<\psi(X)
, we must have
\psi(X)\geq g_{r}(X)-r^{2}
, whence

\displaystyle\psi_{r}(X)\geq\min\{g_{r}(X),\psi(X)\}-r^{2}\geq g_{r}(X)-2r^{2}\geq f(X).

Therefore,
\psi_{r}\geq f
in
B_{r}(X_{0})
.

Let
\delta\in(0,r_{0}^{2})
be arbitrarily small. Clearly,

\lim_{r\to 0}f^{(r^{2})}=f\quad\text{in}\quad C(\mathbb{T}\times[\delta,T-\delta]),\quad\sup_{r^{2}\in(0,\delta)}\|f^{(r^{2})}\|_{\Lip(\mathbb{T}\times[\delta,T-\delta])}\leq M.

This together with the definition of
\psi_{r}
yields

\lim_{r\to 0}\psi_{r}=f\quad\text{in}\quad C(I\times[\delta,T-\delta]),\quad\sup_{r^{2}\in(0,\delta)}\|\psi_{r}\|_{\Lip(I\times[\delta,T-\delta])}<\infty,

where
I=[x_{0}-\pi,x_{0}+\pi]
and
X_{0}=(x_{0},t_{0})
.

We have proved that
\psi_{r}
satisfies all the properties in (
6.5
) but with
\mathbb{T}
replaced by
I
. To finish the proof, we note that
x_{0}
is the midpoint of
I
and
\psi_{r}=g_{r}
for
|X-X_{0}|>r
. Therefore, since
g_{r}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})
and
I
has length
2\pi
, we can periodize
\psi_{r}
as

\psi_{r}(x+k2\pi,t)=\psi_{r}(x,t)\quad\forall(x,t)\in I\times\mathbb{R},\,\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}

and obtain
\psi_{r}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})
.
