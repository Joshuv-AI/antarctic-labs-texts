Sard properties for polynomial maps in infinite dimension
Antonio Lerario, Luca Rizzi, Daniele Tiberio

Abstract
. Sard’s theorem asserts that the set of critical values of a smooth map from one Euclidean space to another one has measure zero. A version of this result for infinite-dimensional Banach manifolds was proven by Smale for maps with Fredholm differential. It is well–known, however, that when the domain is infinite dimensional and the range is finite dimensional, the result is not true – even under the assumption that the map is “polynomial” – and a general theory is still lacking. Addressing this issue, in this paper, we provide sharp quantitative criteria for the validity of Sard’s theorem in this setting. Our motivation comes from sub–Riemannian geometry and, as an application of our results, we prove the sub–Riemannian Sard conjecture for the restriction of the Endpoint map of Carnot groups to the set of piece–wise real–analytic controls with large enough radius of convergence, and the strong Sard conjecture for the restriction to the set of piece–wise entire controls.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The Sard property for polynomial maps on infinite-dimensional spaces

The classical Sard theorem
[
34
,
39
]
states that the set of critical values of a smooth map
f:N\to M
, where
N
and
M
are finite dimensional manifolds, has measure zero in
M
. Smale
[
40
]
proved a version of this result in the case both
M
and
N
are infinite dimensional Banach manifolds and the map
f
is Fredholm: in this case the conclusion is that the set of regular values of
f
is residual in
N
.

When
N
is infinite-dimensional and
M
is finite dimensional,
f
cannot be Fredholm and Smale’s result cannot be applied. In fact there are smooth
surjective maps without regular points from any infinite-dimensional Banach space to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, see
[
11
]
. Even under the hypothesis that
f
is a
polynomial map
, the set of its critical values can be of positive measure: Kupka
[
28
]
constructed an example of a smooth map
f:\ell^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, whose restriction to each finite dimensional space is a polynomial of degree
3
, and with the property that the set of its critical values is the segment
[0,1]
. On the other hand, using a quantitative version of the classical Sard theorem
[
45
]
, Yomdin has shown that if a map
f:\ell^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
can be approximated well-enough with finite dimensional polynomials, still the set of its critical values has measure zero, see
[
46
]
. (Kupka’s map does not have this property.) In Yomdin’s proof, the fact that the codomain is one-dimensional is essential and new technical difficulties arise for maps with values in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
m>1
.

In this paper we investigate the Sard property for polynomial maps defined on Hilbert spaces and with values in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, for general
m\geq 1
.

Definition 1
.

Given a Hilbert space
H
and
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the class of maps

(2)

\mathscr{P}_{d}^{m}(H):=\bigg\{f:H\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\,\bigg|\,\dim(E)<\infty\implies f|_{E}\textrm{ is a polynomial map of degree $d$}\bigg\},

where by polynomial map we mean that each component of
f|_{E}
is a polynomial of degree
d
. We require that each element of
\mathscr{P}_{d}^{m}(H)
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}
(in the Fréchet sense), and that its differential
Df:H\to\mathcal{L}(H,\mathbb{R}^{m})
is weakly continuous and locally Lipschitz.

The map constructed by Kupka belongs to this family, as well as the Endpoint maps for horizontal path spaces on Carnot groups (see
Section

1.3
).

We need to introduce a notion of
quantitative compactness
for subsets of Hilbert spaces.
More precisely, given
K\subset H
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\Omega_{n}(K,H)
its
Kolmogorov
n
–width
:

(3)

\Omega_{n}(K,H):=\inf_{\dim(E)=n}\sup_{u\in K}\inf_{v\in E}\|v-u\|,

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the norm of the Hilbert space
H
, and the infimum ranges over all vector subspaces
E\subset H
of dimension
n
(see
Definition

30
).
A set
K\subset H
is compact if and only if it is bounded and its
n
–width goes to zero as
n
goes to infinity.

Our results are stated in terms of
entropy dimension
of the set of
\nu
-critical values
of a map
f:H\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, namely
f(\crit_{\nu}(f))
, where
\crit_{\nu}(f)
is the set of points where
Df
has rank at most
\nu\leq m-1
. The classical set of critical values corresponds to setting
\nu=m-1
, and it is denoted by
f(\crit(f))
, where
\crit(f)
is the set of critical points. We refer to
Definition

27
of entropy dimension (denoted by
\dim_{e}
, and also known as Minkowski dimension), noting that it is larger than the Hausdorff one so that in the forthcoming estimates one can replace the former with the latter for simplicity.

Our first theorem is a sufficient condition for the validity of the Sard property (see
Theorem

35
). We denote by
\mu
the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Theorem A

(Sard under
n
–width assumptions)
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space and
K\subset H
be a compact set such that, for some
q>1
, it holds

(4)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}(K,H)^{1/n}\leq q^{-1}.

Let
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
. There exists
\beta_{0}=\beta_{0}(d,m)>1
such that for all
f\in\mathscr{P}_{d}^{m}(H)
and
\nu\leq m-1
we have

(5)

\dim_{e}\bigg(f\big(\crit_{\nu}(f)\cap K\big)\bigg)\leq\nu+\frac{\ln\beta_{0}}{\ln q}.

In particular, if
q>\beta_{0}
, then the Sard property holds on
K
:

(6)

\mu\bigg(f\big(\crit(f)\cap K\big)\bigg)=0.

Remark 2
.

The constant
\beta_{0}(d,m)
of
Theorem

A
comes from semialgebraic geometry, and its origin is discussed in
Section

1.2
. We sometimes call it the
semialgebraic constant
.

By building upon Kupka’s counterexamples
[
28
]
, we prove that the quantitative assumption
q>\beta_{0}
in
Theorem

A
cannot be dispensed of. The following result corresponds to
Theorem

39
. We denote by
B_{X}(r)
the closed ball of radius
r
in a Banach space
X
.

Theorem B

(Counterexamples to Sard)
.

Let
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
, with
d\geq 3
, and let
1<q<(d-1)^{1/d}
. There exist a Hilbert space
H
, and
f\in\mathscr{P}_{d}^{m}(H)
such that
K=\crit(f)\cap B_{H}(r)
is compact for all
r>0
, with

(7)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}(K,H)^{1/n}\leq q^{-1},

and
f:H\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
does not verify the Sard property, namely
\mu(f(\crit(f)\cap K))>0
. Therefore, the semialgebraic constant
\beta_{0}(d,m)
of
Theorem

A
satisfies

(8)

\beta_{0}(d,m)\geq(d-1)^{1/d},\qquad\forall\,m\in\mathbb{N},\,d\geq 3.

Remark 3
.

The case
d=2
, which is left out from
Theorem

B
, is special. This is related to the fact that the complexity of semialgebraic sets defined by quadratic equations has a different behaviour compared to the case
d\geq 3
. We currently expect that
any

f\in\mathscr{P}^{m}_{2}(H)
satisfies the Sard property globally on
H
. This will be the subject of a future work.

Replacing the entropy dimension with the Hausdorff one (which is smaller than the former, but countably stable) we strengthen
Theorem

A
as follows (see
Theorem

42
).

Theorem C

(Sard on linear subspaces)
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space and
K\subset H
be a compact set such that, for some
q>1
, it holds

(9)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}(K,H)^{1/n}\leq q^{-1}.

Let
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\beta_{0}=\beta_{0}(d,m)>1
be the same constant of
Theorem

A
. Consider the (possibly non-closed) linear subspace

(10)

V:=\mathrm{span}(K).

Then for all
f\in\mathscr{P}_{d}^{m}(H)
and
\nu\leq m-1
the restriction
f|_{V}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfies

(11)

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\bigg(f\big(\mathrm{Crit}_{\nu}(f|_{V})\big)\bigg)\leq\nu+\frac{\log\beta_{0}}{\log q}.

In particular, if
q>\beta_{0}
, then the restriction
f|_{V}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfies the Sard property

(12)

\mu\bigg(f\big(\mathrm{Crit}(f|_{V})\big)\bigg)=0.

In all previous results,
f\big(\mathrm{Crit}_{\nu}(f|_{V})\big)
can be replaced with the smaller set
f(\mathrm{Crit}_{\nu}(f)\cap V)
.

Remark 4
.

The restriction
f|_{V}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
map in the Fréchet sense from the normed vector space
(V,\|\cdot\|)
to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, with
D_{u}(f|_{V})=(D_{u}f)|_{V}
for all
u\in V
.

Example 5
.

Let
q\in(1,\infty)
with
q>\beta_{0}(d,m)
, where
\beta_{0}(d,m)>1
is the number in
Theorems

A
and
C
, for
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
H=\ell^{2}
with the usual norm. Consider the subset
K\subset B_{H}(1)
given by

(13)

K=\left\{u\in\ell^{2}\,\middle|\,\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|u_{j}|^{2}q^{2j}\leq 1\right\}.

One can easily verify that
K
is compact. Furthermore, the linear space
V=\mathrm{span}(K)
is dense in
H
. We can estimate the
n
–width of
K
as follows. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
consider the
n
-dimensional subspaces
E_{n}=\{x\in\ell^{2}\mid x_{j}=0,\,\forall\,j\geq n+1\}\subset H
. From (
3
), we obtain

(14)

\displaystyle\Omega_{n}(K,H)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{u\in K}\inf_{v\in E_{n}}\|u-v\|^{2}

(15)

\displaystyle=\sup_{u\in K}\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}|u_{j}|^{2}

(16)

\displaystyle\leq q^{-2(n+1)}\sup_{u\in K}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|u_{j}|^{2}q^{2j}\leq q^{-2(n+1)}.

Hence
\limsup_{n}\Omega_{n}(K,H)^{1/n}\leq q^{-1}
, with
q>\beta_{0}(d,m)
. Thus, by
Theorem

C
it holds (in particular)

(17)

\mu\bigg(f\big(\mathrm{Crit}(f|_{V})\big)\bigg)=\mu\bigg(f\big(\mathrm{Crit}(f)\cap V\big)\bigg)=0,\qquad\forall\,f\in\mathscr{P}_{d}^{m}(H).

Note that even if
V\subset H
is dense, the unrestricted map
f:H\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
may not have the Sard property (see e.g. the Kupka counterexamples in
Section

4.3
).

A
,
F
and
C
are deduced from a more general result for maps that are “well-approximated” by polynomials. This can be regarded as our main result concerning the Sard property, and does not make use of the concept of
n
–width. We report here the statement (see
Theorem

28
).

Theorem D

(Sard criterion for well-approximated maps)
.

Let
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
. There exists a constant
\beta_{0}=\beta_{0}(d,m)>1
such that the following holds. Let
H
be a Hilbert space, and let
f:H\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
map such that its differential
Df:H\to\mathcal{L}(H,\mathbb{R}^{m})
is weakly continuous. Let
B\subset H
be a bounded set with this approximation property: there exist a sequence
E_{n}\subset H
of linear subspaces,
\dim(E_{n})=n
, and polynomial maps
f_{n}:E_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
with uniformly bounded degree:

(18)

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\deg f_{n}\leq d<\infty,

such that for some
q>1
,
c\geq 0
, and all large enough
n
it holds

(19)

\sup_{x\in B}\bigg(\|f(x)-f_{n}\circ\pi_{E_{n}}(x)\|+\|\left(D_{x}f\right)|_{E_{n}}-D_{\pi_{E_{n}}(x)}f_{n}\|_{\op}\bigg)\leq cq^{-n}.

Then for all
\nu\leq m-1
it holds

(20)

\dim_{e}\bigg(f\big(\crit_{\nu}(f)\cap B\big)\bigg)\leq\nu+\frac{\ln\beta_{0}}{\ln q}.

In particular, if
q>\beta_{0}
, then
f
satisfies the Sard property on
B
:

(21)

\mu\bigg(f(\crit(f)\cap B)\bigg)=0.

By means of the general
Theorem

D
we single out a class of maps satisfying the Sard property on the whole domain of definition. This is the content of
Theorem

46
, that we report here. The case
m=1
corresponds to
[
47
, Thm. 10.12]
. In the statement, we fix a Hilbert basis
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset H
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we set
E_{k}:=\mathrm{span}\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}
and we denote by
\pi_{k}:H\to E_{k}
the corresponding orthogonal projection, that is if
x=\sum_{i}x_{i}e_{i}
, then
\pi_{k}(x)=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
.

Theorem E

(Global Sard for special maps)
.

Let
H
be a separable Hilbert space. For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
p_{k}:E_{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be polynomial maps with

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\deg p_{k}\leq d
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
, and such that

(22)

\sup_{x\in B_{E_{k}}(1)}\|p_{k}(x)\|\leq q^{-k},\qquad\forall\,k\in\mathbb{N},

for some
q>1
. Then the map
f:H\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined by

(23)

f(x):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}),\qquad\forall\,x\in H,

is well–defined,
f\in\mathscr{P}_{d}^{m}(H)
(see
Definition

1
), and for all
\nu\leq m-1
and
r>0
it holds

(24)

\dim_{e}\bigg(f\left(\crit_{\nu}(f)\cap B_{H}(r)\right)\bigg)\leq\nu+\frac{\ln\beta_{0}}{\ln q},

where
\beta_{0}=\beta_{0}(d,m)>1
is the same constant given by
Theorem

D
. In particular, if
q>\beta_{0}
, then
f
satisfies the Sard property globally on
H
:

(25)

\mu\bigg(f\big(\crit(f)\big)\bigg)=0.

As a consequence of
Theorems

A
and
B
, we see that there is a threshold on the
n
–width for the validity of the Sard property. Motivated by this fact, for a compact set
K\subset H
we define the quantity:

(26)

\omega(K,H):=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}(K,H)^{1/n}\in[0,1].

Roughly speaking, smaller
\omega(K,H)
means that
K
is “more compact” in
H
, and better approximated by finite-dimensional subspaces.
We obtain the following statement (see
Theorem

47
).

Theorem F

(Sard threshold on compacts)
.

For all
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
\omega_{0}(d,m)\in(0,1]
such that

(1)

for every
f\in\mathscr{P}_{d}^{m}(H)
and for every compact set
K\subset H
with
\omega(K,H)<\omega_{0}(d,m)
,

(27)

\mu\bigg(f\left(\mathrm{crit}(f)\cap K\right)\bigg)=0;

(2)

for every
\omega>\omega_{0}(d,m)
, with
\omega\in(0,1]
, there exist
f\in\mathscr{P}_{d}^{m}(H)
and a compact set
K\subset H
with
\omega(K,H)=\omega
and such that

(28)

\mu\bigg(f\left(\mathrm{crit}(f)\cap K\right)\bigg)>0.

Remark 6
.

By construction, and
Theorem

A
,
\omega_{0}\geq\beta_{0}^{-1}
. Furthermore, if
d\geq 3
,
Theorem

B
yields
\omega_{0}<1
, so that
Item

2
is non-vacuous in these cases.

1.2.
Quantitative semialgebraic geometry

The proof of
Theorem

D
needs some fine properties of semialgebraic sets. The strategy of the proof is conceptually similar to Yomdin’s
[
46
]
, and uses the theory of
variations
, introduced by Vitushkin
[
43
,
44
]
and developed in
[
47
]
.

Let us denote by
V_{i}(S)
the
i
-th variation of a semialgebraic set, which is a sort of
i
-dimensional volume, (see
Definition

15
). Furthermore, recall that for a
C^{1}
map
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\Lambda=(\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
the set of almost-critical values of
F
is defined by

(29)

C^{\Lambda}(F):=\bigg\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\bigg|\,\sigma_{i}(D_{x}F)\leq\Lambda_{i},\quad\forall\,i=1,\dots,m\bigg\},

where
\sigma_{1}(D_{x}F)\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{m}(D_{x}F)
are the singular values of
D_{x}F
, see
Definition

17
(here we assume
n\geq m
). In
[
47
, Cor. 7.4]
a quantitative estimate on the variations of the almost-critical values of polynomial maps has been obtained.
In that estimate the dimensional parameter
n
does not appear explicitly. We obtain
Theorem

G
below, which makes this dependence explicit (see
Theorem

26
).

Theorem G

(Quantitative variations estimates)
.

Let
n\geq m
, and
p:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a polynomial map with components of degree at most
d
. For
i=0,\dots,m
,
\Lambda=(\Lambda_{1},\dots,\Lambda_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
and
r>0
, we have

(30)

V_{i}(p(C^{\Lambda}(p)\cap B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(r)))\leq\mathrm{cst}(m,r)n^{m}\beta_{0}^{n}\Lambda_{0}\cdots\Lambda_{i},

where
\beta_{0}=\beta_{0}(d,m)>1
depends only on
d
and
m
,
\mathrm{cst}(m,r)
depends only on
m,r
, and we set
\Lambda_{0}=1
.

A key technical step in the proof of
Theorem

G
is a quantitative substitute for the
definable choice
theorem in semialgebraic geometry, which we prove in
[
31
]
(see
Theorem

14
).

1.3.
Applications to Endpoint maps of Carnot groups

We discuss now the implications of
Theorem

F
and
Theorem

C
in the context of sub-Riemannian geometry.

Recall that an
m
-dimensional
Carnot group
of step
s\in\mathbb{N}
is a connected and simply connected Lie group
(\mathbb{G},\cdot)
of dimension
m
, whose Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
admits a stratification of step
s
, that is

(31)

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{s},

where

\mathfrak{g}_{i}\neq\{0\}
,

\mathfrak{g}_{i+1}=[\mathfrak{g}_{1},\mathfrak{g}_{i}]

for all
i=1,\dots,s-1
and
[\mathfrak{g}_{1},\mathfrak{g}_{s}]=\{0\}
. The group exponential map yields an identification
\mathbb{G}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and the first stratum
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
of the Lie algebra defines a smooth, totally non-holonomic distribution
\Delta\subseteq T\mathbb{R}^{m}
of rank
k:=\dim\mathfrak{g}_{1}
.

Fix a global trivialization of
\Delta
:

(32)

\Delta=\mathrm{span}\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}\},

where each
X_{i}
is a left-invariant vector field. Let
I:=[0,1]
be the unit interval and
H:=L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
. We say that a curve
\gamma:I\to\mathbb{G}
is
horizontal
if it is absolutely continuous and there exist
u\in H
, called
control
such that for a.e.
t\in I
it holds

(33)

\dot{\gamma}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}(t)X_{i}(\gamma(t)),

Furthermore, for any given
u\in H
there exists a unique
\gamma_{u}:I\to\mathbb{G}
satisfying (
33
) and such that
\gamma_{u}(0)=e
(the identity
e\in\mathbb{G}
, identified with
0\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
). Note that the class of horizontal curves does not depend on the choice of the global trivialization of
\Delta
.

The
Endpoint map
is the map that sends a control
u
to the the corresponding final point
\gamma_{u}(1)
:

(34)

\mathcal{E}:L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})\to\mathbb{G}.

The
Sard conjecture
is the conjecture that Endpoint map has the Sard property:

Sard conjecture:
the set
\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{Crit}(\mathcal{E}))
has zero measure.

The conjecture was introduced by Zhitomirskii and Montgomery, see
[
33
, Sec. 10.2]
for general sub-Riemannian structures, where the Endpoint map can be defined in a similar way, on a suitable domain of horizontal paths, which has the structure of a Hilbert manifold. For the specific case of Carnot groups, it is known to be true for step
\leq 2
, see
[
4
,
30
]
. Furthermore, the conjecture has been verified in
[
19
]
for filiform Carnot groups, in
[
20
]
for Carnot groups of rank 2 and step 4, and rank 3 and step 3, and for a handful of other specific examples described in
[
30
]
.

Assume that
\Delta
is also endowed with a left-invariant norm. This choice induces a length structure on the space of horizontal curves, and correspondingly, a distance, which gives
\mathbb{G}
the structure of
sub-Finsler Carnot group
(or
sub-Riemannian Carnot group
if the norm is induced by a scalar product). In this case, within the set of critical points of
\mathcal{E}
(also called singular horizontal paths), the energy-minimizing ones play a significant role. At the corresponding critical values, the distance loses regularity: it is never smooth and it can even lose local semiconcavity
[
9
, Sec. 4.2]
. For this reason, the understanding of the “abundance” of such critical values is crucial. Denoting by
H_{\min}\subseteq L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
the set of minimizing horizontal paths starting from the identity, the
minimizing Sard conjecture
can be formulated as follows:

Minimizing Sard conjecture:
the set
\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{Crit}(\mathcal{E})\cap H_{\min})
has zero measure.

The most general result to date is that the above set is a closed nowhere dense set
[
7
,
37
]
, which of course does not imply the minimizing Sard conjecture. The above conjecture is one of the main open problems in sub-Riemannian geometry
[
8
, Prob. 3]
,
[
37
, Conj. 1]
. In general, the problem is settled in the following cases:

-

Thanks to the work of Agrachev-Sarychev
[
6
]
and Agrachev-Lee
[
5
]
, in absence of so-called Goh singular minimizing paths, all sub-Riemannian minimizing paths (resp. sub-Finsler, under suitable smoothness assumptions on the norm) are solution of a finite-dimensional Hamiltonian flow so that the corresponding set of critical values has zero measure by the finite-dimensional Sard theorem. Recently, Rifford proved that, more generally, the minimizing Sard conjecture holds for structures where all non-trivial Goh paths have Goh-rank
\leq 1
almost everywhere, see
[
38
]
. For Carnot groups, these results can be applied for example when the distribution is
pre-medium fat
, see
[
38
, (1.2)]
. In passing, we remark that for generic (so, typically not Carnot) sub-Riemannian structures with distribution of rank
\geq 3
, there are no non-trivial singular Goh minimizing paths, see
[
22
, Cor. 2.5]
and
[
3
, Thm. 8]
. As a consequence the minimizing Sard conjecture holds true. Thanks to
[
38
, Cor. 1.4]
, the latter result extends the case of rank
\geq 2
.

-

The minimizing Sard conjecture is true also for Carnot groups of step
\leq 3
, see
[
30
]
. In this case, the problem is reduced (in a non-trivial way) to a finite-dimensional one. First, by noting that singular minimizing curves are non-singular in some proper subgroup, and then exploiting the fact that Carnot subgroups are parametrized by a finite-dimension manifold.

Finally, we refer to
[
48
,
17
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
35
,
37
]
for further works on various forms of the Sard conjecture on general sub-Riemannian structures.

The following result (
Proposition

52
) connects this framework to the previous sections.

Proposition H

(Polynomial properties of the Endpoint map)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a Carnot group of topological dimension
m
, step
s
, and rank
k
. Then the Endpoint map
\mathcal{E}\in\mathscr{P}_{s}^{m}(H)
, for
H=L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})

As a consequence of
Proposition

H
, we can apply
Theorem

C
to the study of Endpoint maps of Carnot groups. We record here a first immediate corollary.

Corollary I

(Sard criterion for Endpoint maps)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a Carnot group of topological dimension
m
, step
s
, and rank
k
. Let
K\subset L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
be a compact subset and let
q>1
be such that

(35)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}(K,L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k}))^{1/n}\leq q^{-1}.

Then, letting
V:=\mathrm{span}(K)
, the restriction
\mathcal{E}|_{V}:V\to\mathbb{G}
satisfies

(36)

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\bigg(\mathcal{E}\big(\mathrm{Crit}(\mathcal{E}|_{V})\big)\bigg)\leq m-1+\frac{\log\beta_{0}}{\log q},

where
\beta_{0}=\beta_{0}(s,m)>1
is the same constant of
Theorem

A
. In particular, it holds:

(i)

if
q>\beta_{0}
, the restriction
\mathcal{E}|_{V}:V\to\mathbb{G}
has the Sard property, namely

(37)

\mu\bigg(\mathcal{E}\big(\mathrm{Crit}(\mathcal{E}|_{V})\big)\bigg)=0.

(ii)

if
q=\infty
, the restriction
\mathcal{E}|_{V}:V\to\mathbb{G}
has the so-called strong Sard property, namely

(38)

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\bigg(\mathcal{E}\big(\mathrm{Crit}(\mathcal{E}|_{V})\big)\bigg)\leq m-1.

In all previous results,
\mathcal{E}\big(\mathrm{Crit}(\mathcal{E}|_{V})\big)
can be replaced with the smaller set
\mathcal{E}\big(\mathrm{Crit}(\mathcal{E})\cap V\big)
.

We can apply
Corollary

I
to find large sets on which the Sard property holds for the Endpoint map of Carnot groups. This is a more functional-analytic approach to the Sard conjecture that, unlike previous ones, does not resort to reduction to finite-dimensional cases.

An interesting case in which the assumptions of
Corollary

I
can be effectively checked is given by sets of controls that are “sufficiently regular”. We introduce the notation to state our result, see
Section

5.2
for additional details. Given
r>0
, and a closed interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, consider the set:

(39)

\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r):=\bigg\{u:I\to\mathbb{R}^{k}\,\bigg|\,\textrm{$u$ is real-analytic with radius of convergence}>r\bigg\},

endowed with the sup norm over the
r
-neighbourhood of
I
in
\mathbb{C}
. More generally, let
\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell)
be the set of piecewise analytic controls:

(40)

\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell)=\bigg\{u:I\to\mathbb{R}^{k}\,\bigg|\,u|_{I_{j}}\in\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I_{j},\mathbb{R}^{k};r),\,\text{ for all $j=1,\dots,\ell$}\bigg\},

where
I_{j}=\inf I+\left[\frac{(j-1)|I|}{\ell},\frac{j|I|}{\ell}\right]
, for all
j=1,\dots,\ell
, which we equip with a suitable norm (see
Section

5.2
). In
Proposition

53
we prove that the unit ball
K
of
\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell)
, with
I=[0,1]
, is compact in
H
and its
n
–width satisfies:

(41)

\Omega_{n}(K,H)\leq\frac{(k\ell)^{1/2}}{\ln r}\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{k\ell}\right\rfloor},\qquad\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N},\quad r>1.

By applying
Corollary

I
we can therefore deduce the following result (see
Theorem

54
).

Theorem J

(Sard property on piecewise real–analytic controls)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a Carnot group of topological dimension
m
, step
s
, and rank
k
. Let
I=[0,1]
. For
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
r=r(m,s,k,\ell)>1
such that

(42)

\mu\big(\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{Crit}(\mathcal{E})\cap\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell))\big)=\mu\big(\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{Crit}(\mathcal{E}|_{\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell)})\big)=0.

Namely, the Sard property holds on the space of piecewise real–analytic controls with radius of convergence
>r
and with
\ell
pieces.
Furthermore it holds

(43)

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\big(\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{Crit}(\mathcal{E})\cap\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};\infty,\ell))\big)\leq\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\big(\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{Crit}(\mathcal{E}|_{\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};\infty,\ell)})\big)\leq m-1.

Namely, the strong Sard property holds on the space of piecewise entire controls with
\ell
pieces.

Remark 7

(Stronger versions)
.

By inspecting the proof of
Theorem

J
, one actually obtains more general estimates for the Hausdorff dimension of the sets of critical values as in
Corollary

I
. Furthermore, all these results hold in a stronger form, replacing
\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell)
with the larger space

(44)

\mathrm{span}\left(\overline{\{u\in\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell)\mid\|u\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell)}\leq 1\}}\right),\qquad r\in(0,\infty],

where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell)}
, defined in (

), is a sort of Hardy
\infty
-norm, while the closure is w.r.t.
L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
.

Remark 8

(Regularity and Sard)
.

Sussmann established that for real–analytic sub-Riemannian structures (such as Carnot groups), minimizing horizontal paths are real–analytic on an open and dense set of their interval of definition
[
41
]
. In light of that result,
Theorem

J
hints at an unexpected link between the regularity problem of sub-Riemannian geodesics and the minimizing Sard conjecture.

Remark 9

(Surjectivity of the Endpoint map)
.

Of course a subset of controls
V\subset L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
must satisfy
\mathcal{E}(V)=\mathbb{G}
to be relevant for the theory. By the Rashevskii-Chow theorem,
\mathcal{E}:L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is surjective. We observe that
\mathcal{E}
is surjective also on
\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell)
if
\ell\geq 4m
, since from the proof of the Rashevskii-Chow theorem any pair of points can be joined by a concatenation of
4m
horizontal paths with constant controls, see
[
2
, Sec. 3.2]
. Actually, the Endpoint map is surjective when restricted on any set that is dense in
L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
, see e.g.
[
12
, Sec. 2.5]
. It follows that
\mathcal{E}
is surjective on the sets of
Theorem

J
since in any case they contain all (restrictions on
I
of) polynomial controls.

Finally, we prove in
Proposition

58
that the Endpoint map on Carnot groups is surjective already when restricted to polynomial controls of large enough degree (depending on
\mathbb{G}
). This is a particular case of the following more general result (see
Proposition

59
).

Proposition K

(Quantitative surjectivity)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a Carnot group with topological dimension
m
and rank
k
. Let
S\subset L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
be a dense set. Then there exist
u_{0},u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\in S
such that

(45)

\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{span}\{u_{0},u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\})=\mathbb{G}.

In other words, one can find an
(m+1)
-dimensional vector subspace
E\subset L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
of controls (depending on
\mathbb{G}
) such that the restriction of the Endpoint map to
E
is surjective. Furthermore,
E
can be assumed to have generators in any prescribed dense set
S\subset L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
.
Proposition

K
can be seen as a more refined version (for Carnot groups) of the well–known fact that the Endpoint map is surjective when restricted on any set of controls that is dense in
L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
, see e.g.
[
12
, Sec. 2.5]
.

1.4.
Closing thoughts on the Sard conjecture

We do not venture in a guess in favor of Sard conjecture on the whole
H=L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
, instead we propose a direction of future investigation that can be approached with our techniques. Recall that
H_{\mathrm{min}}\subset H
is the set of energy-minimizing controls. It is known that
H_{\min}
is boundedly compact in
H
i.e., the intersection
H_{\min}\cap B_{H}(r)
is compact for all
r>0
, see
[
2
, Thm. 8.66]
,
[
1
]
. In fact, from the very recent result by Lokutsievskiy and Zelikin
[
32
, Cor. 1
(\mathfrak{G})
]
, one can deduce the following estimate for the
n
–width on Carnot groups

(46)

\Omega_{2n+1}(H_{\min}\cap B_{H}(1),H)\leq Cn^{-\frac{1}{2s}},\qquad\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N},

for some
C>0
depending on
\mathbb{G}
. Estimate (
46
) can be understood as a quantitative compactness property for
H_{\min}
. Unfortunately, the polynomial decay (w.r.t.
n
) of (
46
) is too weak to apply
Theorem

A
, since the latter requires an exponential one.

If one could prove that, for some
q^{-1}<\omega_{0}(s,m)
, it holds

(47)

\Omega_{n}(H_{\min}\cap B_{H}(1),H)\leq q^{-n},\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty,

then
Theorem

F
would settle the minimizing Sard conjecture on Carnot groups.

We also note that it would be sufficient to prove (
47
) for the set of the so-called
strictly abnormal
energy-minimizing controls
H_{\min}^{\mathrm{str.abn}}\subsetneq H_{\min}
. Unfortunately, we were not able to produce a direct estimate of the
n
–width of (bounded subsets of)
H_{\min}
or
H_{\min}^{\mathrm{str.abn}}
, so that we have no further evidence to support this idea towards a proof of the minimizing Sard conjecture.

In the opposite direction, and to conclude, our results can be used to identify spaces where the (general) Sard conjecture can be violated. If, for a given Carnot group
\mathbb{G}
, there is a linear subset of controls
V\subset H
such that
\mu(\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{Crit}(\mathcal{E})\cap V))>0
, then the following necessary condition must hold:

(48)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}(V\cap B_{H}(r),H)^{1/n}\geq\omega_{0}(s,m).

3.1.
Semialgebraic sets and maps

The goal of this section is to prove
Theorem

G
.
We begin by recalling some basic notions from semialgebraic geometry.

Definition 10

(Semialgebraic sets and maps)
.

We say that a set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
semialgebraic
if

(49)

S=\bigcup_{i=1}^{a}\bigcap_{j=1}^{b_{i}}\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\mathrm{sign}(p_{ij}(x))=\sigma_{ij}\right\},

for some finite set of polynomials
p_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
and
\sigma_{ij}\in\{0,+1,-1\}
, where

(50)

\mathrm{sign}(r):=\begin{cases}+1&r>0,\\
-1&r<0,\\
0&r=0.\end{cases}

We call the description (
49
) a
representation
of
S
.

A map
f:A\to B
between semialgebraic sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},B\subset\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
is said to be
semialgebraic
if its graph is a semialgebraic set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
.

The representation of a semialgebraic set
S
as in (
49
) is not unique. However, a representation is useful to quantify the complexity of
S
, using the following notion.

Definition 11

(Diagram of a semialgebraic set)
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a semialgebraic set represented as in (
49
). We will say that the triple

(51)

\left(n,a\cdot\max_{i}\{b_{i}\},\max_{i,j}\{\deg(p_{ij})\}\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{3}

is a
diagram
for
S
. Below, the equation “
D(S)=(m,c,d)
” will mean that there exists a representation of
S
as in (
49
) with
n\leq m
,
a\cdot\max_{i}\{{b_{i}}\}\leq c
and
\max_{i,j}\{\deg(p_{ij})\}\leq d
.

Remark 12
.

There are several ways of quantifying the complexity of a semialgebraic set, depending on the way it is presented. Essentially, these notions should contain three pieces of information: the number of variables, the “combinatorics” of the presentation, and the degrees of the defining polynomials. For instance, every semialgebraic set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be presented as

(52)

S=\bigcup_{i=1}^{a}\bigcap_{j=1}^{b_{i}}E_{ij},

where each
E_{ij}
has the form
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid p_{ij}(x)\leq 0\}
or
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid p_{ij}(x)<0\}
,
where
p_{ij}
is a polynomial with
\deg(p_{ij})\leq d
for all
i,j
.
Given a presentation of this type, it is immediate to see that
S
also admits a diagram
D(S)=(n,c,d)
(i.e. it can be presented as in (
49
) with
c=c(a,b_{1},\dots,b_{a})
.

3.1.1.
Dimension and stratifications

Every semialgebraic subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be written as a finite union of semialgebraic sets, each of them semialgebraically homeomorphic to an open cube
(0,1)^{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, for some
m\leq n
(
[
21
, Thm. 2.3.6]
). This allows to define the
dimension
of a semialgebraic set as the maximum of the dimensions
m
of these cubes. Every semialgebraic set can be written as a finite union of smooth, semialgebraic disjoint manifolds called
strata
(
[
21
, Prop. 9.1.8]
).

Notice that the dimension of a semialgebraic set is preserved by semialgebraic homeomorphisms and behaves naturally under product structures:

(53)

\dim(A\times B)=\dim(A)+\dim(B).

Moreover, if
f:A\to B
is a continuous semialgebraic map, then

(54)

\dim(f(A))\leq\dim(A).

3.1.2.
Semialgebraic triviality

Continuous semialgebraic maps

are “piecewise” trivial fibrations: there exists a partition of
B
into finitely many semialgebraic sets

(55)

B=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{b}B_{j}

and, for every
j=1,\ldots,b
there exist fibers
F_{j}:=f^{-1}(b_{j})
, with
b_{j}\in B_{j}
and a semialgebraic homeomorphism
\psi_{j}:f^{-1}(B_{j})\to B_{j}\times F_{j}
that make the following diagram commutative:

(56)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B_{j}\times F_{j}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{-1}(B_{j})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B_{j}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi_{j}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}

This result is called
semialgebraic triviality
, see
[
10
, Thm. 5.46]
.

3.2.
Definable choice

We recall from
[
31
]
the following
quantitative approximate definable choice
result, which will be a key tool in the proof of
Theorem

G
.

Let us explain the name and the context of this result. Given a polynomial map
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
and a compact semialgebraic set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for every
y\in F(A)
one would like to choose a point
x(y)\in F^{-1}(y)\cap A
so that the resulting set
C:=\{x(y)\}_{y\in F(A)}
is semialgebraic and
with controlled dimension
. In particular, the “choice” of such
x(y)
has to be “definable”. In this way
C
would be of dimension
\ell
. The fact that this can be done follows from semialgebraic triviality (see
Section

3.1.2
). However, in this way there is no control on the geometry of
C
(in particular, the number of its connected components) in terms of the data defining
A
(namely, its diagram, see
Definition

11
).

For many geometric applications (as in this paper) one does not really need that
C
is a choice over
A
, but it is enough that it is close to it. More precisely, given
\varepsilon>0
, denote by
\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(A)
the Euclidean
\varepsilon
–neighbourhood of
A
. Then one can relax the requirements for the definable choice and ask, given
\varepsilon>0
, for a set
C_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(A)
, of dimension
\leq\ell
, whose image under
F
is
\varepsilon
-close to
F(A)
(in the Hausdorff metric, denoted by
\mathrm{dist}_{H}
), and with a control the geometry of
C_{\varepsilon}
.

Theorem 14

(Quantitative approximate definable choice
[
31
]
)
.

For every
c,d,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
\beta_{\mathrm{dc}}>1
satisfying the following statement. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a closed semialgebraic set contained in the ball

with diagram

(62)

D(A)=(n,c,d).

Let also
F=(F_{1},\ldots,F_{\ell}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
be a polynomial map with components of degree bounded by
d
. Then for every
\varepsilon\in(0,r)
there exists a closed semialgebraic set
C_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

(1)

\dim(C_{\varepsilon})\leq\ell
;

(2)

C_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(A)
;

(3)

\mathrm{dist}_{H}(F(C_{\varepsilon}),F(A))\leq L(F,r)\cdot\varepsilon
, where

L(F,r):=2+\mathrm{Lip}(F,B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(2r))
;

(4)

for every
e=1,\ldots,n
and every affine space
\mathbb{R}^{e}\simeq E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the number of connected components of

is bounded by

(63)

b_{0}(E\cap C_{\varepsilon})\leq\beta_{\mathrm{dc}}^{e}.

3.3.
Variations and their behaviour under polynomial maps

We recall the definition of

and developed in the semialgebraic context by Comte and Yomdin

The variations encode the size of a set, through an integral-geometric approach.
Given a polynomial map

and a semialgebraic set

Definition 17

Let

In finite dimension, the following Weyl inequality holds for the singular values of the difference of two matrices (see

|\sigma_{k}(L_{1})-\sigma_{k}(L_{2})|\leq\|L_{1}-L_{2}\|_{\op},\qquad\forall\,k=1,\dots,m.

The Weyl inequality generalizes to infinite-dimensional spaces. Since we are not able to find a reference, we provide below a self-contained statement and its proof in the form that we will need later.

Lemma 18
.

If
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}(H,\mathbb{R}^{m})
, then

|\sigma_{k}(L_{1})-\sigma_{k}(L_{2})|\leq\|L_{1}-L_{2}\|_{\op}

Proof.

Let

be linear and continuous. Let

a finite dimensional subspace such that

, and denote by

to

where

is the inclusion.
We now prove that for all

\sigma_{k}(L)=\sigma_{k}(L_{W}).

Indeed, since the adjoint

coincides with the orthogonal projection

(75)

\lambda_{k}(L_{W}\circ L_{W}^{\top})=\lambda_{k}(L\circ i_{W}\circ i_{W}^{\top}\circ L^{\top})=\lambda_{k}(L\circ\pi_{W}\circ L^{\top})=\lambda_{k}(L\circ L^{\top}).

In the last equality, we used the fact that

,
indeed

L(v)=L(\pi_{W}v+\pi_{W^{\perp}}v)=L(\pi_{W}v)

since

Now given

, which is a finite dimensional linear subspace of

By (

concluding the proof.

3.4.
Variations of almost-critical values of polynomial maps

In this section we recall the notion of almost-critical points
C^{\Lambda}(p)
of
a polynomial map
p:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
The set
p(C^{\Lambda}(p))
is the set of its
almost-critical values.
The main result of this section is
Theorem

26
, which provides
a version of

[
47
, Cor. 7.4]
where the dependence of the parameters with respect to
n
is explicit.
This result is based on
Theorem

19
and on the specific semialgebraic structure of almost-critical points of polynomial maps, that we prove in
Proposition

25
.

Definition 22
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a
C^{1}
map with
n\geq m
.
Given
\Lambda=(\Lambda_{1},\dots,\Lambda_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
, the set of
almost-critical points
of
f
is defined as

(105)

C^{\Lambda}(f):=\bigg\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\bigg|\sigma_{i}(D_{x}f)\leq\Lambda_{i},\,\forall\,i=1,\dots,m\bigg\}.

The following lemma is fundamental to study the semialgebraic structure of the set of almost-critical points of a polynomial map.

Lemma 23
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
be a semialgebraic set and let

f:A\to\mathbb{R}
be a semialgebraic function.
Then, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the sublevel set

\{x\in A\mid f(x)\leq t\}
admits a semialgebraic description whose
diagram does not depend on
t
.

Proof.

The graph of
f
is semialgebraic, hence we can write it as

(106)

\graf(f)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{a}\bigcap_{j=1}^{b_{i}}E_{ij},

where
E_{ij}
is of the form

\{p_{ij}<0\}
or

\{p_{ij}\leq 0\}

and

p_{ij}:\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a polynomial. Denoting by
\pi_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{s}
the projection, we have

(107)

\{x\in A\mid f(x)\leq t\}=\pi_{1}\Big(\graf(f)\cap\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}\mid y-t\leq 0\}\Big).

The set
\graf(f)\cap\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}\mid y-t\leq 0\}
is semialgebraic, with a description given by

(108)

\graf(f)\cap\{(x,y)\mid y-t\leq 0\}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{a}\bigcap_{j=1}^{b_{i}+1}E_{i,j},

where

E_{i,b_{i}+1}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}\mid y-t\leq 0\}
.
In particular, this set admits a diagram that does not depend on
t
, since the degree of the polynomial
y-t
is equal to
1
.
As explained in
Section

3.1.3
, the projection of a semialgebraic set is semialgebraic and its diagram is controlled explicitly by the diagram of the original set only. In particular, the diagram of the projection (
107
) admits a description with a diagram
(s,I,J)
, that does not depend on
t
.
∎

Let

denote the set of positive semidefinite matrices of size

Lemma 24
.

The functions
\lambda_{i}:{\sym}_{m}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
are continuous and semialgebraic.

In the following proposition we provide an estimate on the diagram of a description of the (semialgebraic) set of almost-critical points of polynomial maps.

Proposition 25

Let
d,m\in\mathbb{N}

and
d^{\prime}=d^{\prime}(d,m)

with components of degrees at most
d

\Lambda=(\Lambda_{1},\dots,\Lambda_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}

is closed, semialgebraic, with

(109)

D(C^{\Lambda}(p))=(n,c^{\prime},d^{\prime}).

(In particular, the diagram does not depend on

Proof.

By
Lemma

24
, for every

\lambda_{k}:{\sym}_{m}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
is semialgebraic, hence from

23

and the semialgebraic functions

for any

we can write

(110)

\{M\in\sym_{m}^{+}\mid\lambda_{k}(M)\leq t^{2}\}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{a}\bigcap_{j=1}^{b_{i}}\{M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}\mid P_{k,i,j,t}(M)\leq 0\}

for some polynomials

P_{k,i,j,t}:\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}\to\mathbb{R}
,
a,b_{1},\dots,b_{a}\in\mathbb{N}
, and with

a\max_{i}b_{i}

and

\max_{i,j}\deg(P_{k,i,j,t})
depending only on
m
and on
k
(and not on
t
).
We can assume that
a
does not depend on
k
, and all the
b_{i}
’s do not depend on
a,k
, simply by adding empty or trivial sets.

Now, for any
k=1,\dots,m

(111)

\{N\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\mid\sigma_{k}(N)\leq t\}=\{N\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\mid\lambda_{k}(NN^{\top})\leq t^{2}\},

therefore we can write

(112)

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\sigma_{k}(D_{x}p)\leq t\}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{a}\bigcap_{j=1}^{b_{i}}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid P_{k,i,j,t}(D_{x}pD_{x}p^{\top})\leq 0\}.

Observe that
N\to NN^{\top}
is polynomial with components of degree
2
.
Therefore, the map

q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},

given by
q(x):=D_{x}pD_{x}p^{\top}
is polynomial, with components of degree at most
2d
.
Hence, the polynomial

(113)

P_{k,i,j,t}\circ q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

has degree at most

2d\max_{i,j}\deg P_{k,i,j,t}
, and in particular its degree has a bound that depends on only
d,m,k
(and not on
t
).
The set of almost-critical points of
p
can be described as

(114)

C^{\Lambda}(p)=\bigcap_{k=1}^{m}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\sigma_{k}(D_{x}p)\leq\Lambda_{k}\}=\bigcap_{k=1}^{m}\bigcup_{i=1}^{a}\bigcup_{j=1}^{b_{i}}\{x\mid P_{k,i,j,\Lambda_{k}}(D_{x}pD_{x}p^{\top})\leq 0\}.

It is clear that
C^{\Lambda}(p)
is closed. Note that

d^{\prime}:=\max_{k,i,j}\deg(P_{k,i,j,\Lambda_{k}}\circ q)
depends only on
d,m
. Using the distributivity of intersection, we can rewrite
C^{\Lambda}(p)
in the form (
49
), where the number of unions and intersections depends only on
m
, and the maximum degree of the polynomials is
c^{\prime}=c^{\prime}(d,m)
, concluding the proof.
∎

Now we state and prove the main result of this section (
Theorem

G
in the Introduction).

Theorem 26
.

Let
n\geq m
, and
p:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a polynomial map with components of degree at most
d
. For
i=0,\dots,m
,
\Lambda=(\Lambda_{1},\dots,\Lambda_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
and
r>0
, we have

(115)

V_{i}(p(C^{\Lambda}(p)\cap B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(r)))\leq\mathrm{cst}(m,r)n^{m}\beta_{0}^{n}\Lambda_{0}\cdots\Lambda_{i},

where
\beta_{0}=\beta_{0}(d,m)>1
depends only on
d
and
m
,
\mathrm{cst}(m,r)
depends only on
m,r
, and we set
\Lambda_{0}=1
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

25
the set
A=C^{\Lambda}(p)\cap B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(r)
is a closed and bounded semialgebraic set whose diagram is
D(A)=(n,c^{\prime},d^{\prime})
where
c^{\prime}=c^{\prime}(d,m)
,
d^{\prime}=d^{\prime}(d,m)
(which in particular do not depend on
n
,
\Lambda
). Furthermore, for the polynomial map
p:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
it holds, by construction

(116)

\overline{\sigma}_{i}:=\sup_{x\in A}\Big({\sigma}_{1}(D_{x}p)\cdots{\sigma}_{i}(D_{x}p)\Big)\leq\Lambda_{0}\cdots\Lambda_{i}.

We can then apply
Theorem

19
, yielding (
115
) with
\beta_{0}=\beta_{\mathrm{yc}}(c^{\prime},\max\{d,d^{\prime}\},m)>1
.
∎

4.
Sard properties for infinite-dimensional maps

Let
H
be a Hilbert space. We consider a map
f:U\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
where
U
is an open subset of
H
. If
f
is
C^{1}
we denote by
\crit(f)
the set of its critical points. For a fixed
\nu\in\mathbb{N}
, we also consider the set

(117)

\crit_{\nu}(f)=\{x\in U\mid\mathrm{rank}(D_{x}f)\leq\nu\}.

Given a relatively compact
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, for any
\varepsilon>0
we denote by

M(\varepsilon,S)
the
\varepsilon
-entropy
of
S
, which is the minimum number of closed balls of radius
\varepsilon
that we need to cover
S
.

Definition 27

(Entropy dimension)
.

The
entropy dimension
of
S
is defined as

(118)

\dim_{e}(S)=\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\ln M(\varepsilon,S)}{\ln(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})}.

In
[
47
, Ch. 2]
, it is proved that

(119)

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(S)\leq\dim_{e}(S).

The inequality can be strict, since there are sequences of real numbers with positive entropy dimension. We also note that the entropy dimension is stable under
finite
unions of sets, namely

(120)

\dim_{e}\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{N}S_{n}\right)=\max_{n\leq N}\dim_{e}(S_{n}),

while it is not even sub-additive with respect to
countable
unions. In fact, the entropy dimension of a point in
\mathbb{R}
is equal to zero, while the entropy dimension of
\mathbb{Q}\subset\mathbb{R}
is equal to one. (This is a major difference with respect to the Hausdorff dimension which is countably stable.)

For Banach spaces
X
,
Y
and linear continuous maps
L\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
, we denote by

(121)

\|L\|_{\op}:=\sup_{\|x\|_{X}\leq 1}\|Lx\|_{Y}

the operator norm. With some abuse of notation the same symbol
\|L\|_{\op}
is used for different
X
and
Y
, but there should be no confusion as the domain and codomain of
L
are clear from the context. We also use without risk of confusion the symbol
\|\cdot\|
to denote the usual Euclidean norm of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

4.1.
Sard-type theorem for well approximated maps

The purpose of this section is to prove the following theorem, which is our main Sard-type result (
Theorem

D
in the Introduction).

Theorem 28
.

Let
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
. There exists a constant
\beta_{0}=\beta_{0}(d,m)>1
such that the following holds. Let
H
be a Hilbert space, and let
f:H\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
map such that its differential
Df:H\to\mathcal{L}(H,\mathbb{R}^{m})
is weakly continuous. Let
B\subset H
be a bounded set with this approximation property: there exist a sequence
E_{n}\subset H
of linear subspaces,
\dim(E_{n})=n
, and polynomial maps
f_{n}:E_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
with uniformly bounded degree:

(122)

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\deg f_{n}\leq d<\infty,

such that for some
q>1
,
c\geq 0
, and all large enough
n
it holds

(123)

\sup_{x\in B}\bigg(\|f(x)-f_{n}\circ\pi_{E_{n}}(x)\|+\|\left(D_{x}f\right)|_{E_{n}}-D_{\pi_{E_{n}}(x)}f_{n}\|_{\op}\bigg)\leq cq^{-n}.

Then for all
\nu\leq m-1
it holds

(124)

\dim_{e}\bigg(f\big(\crit_{\nu}(f)\cap B\big)\bigg)\leq\nu+\frac{\ln\beta_{0}}{\ln q}.

In particular, if
q>\beta_{0}
, then
f
satisfies the Sard property on
B
:

(125)

\mu\bigg(f(\crit(f)\cap B)\bigg)=0.

Remark 29
.

As it will be clear from the proof, the upper bound
cq^{-n}
in the r.h.s. of (
123
) can be replaced by any
C_{n}\geq 0
, such that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}C_{n}^{1/n}=q^{-1}
, yielding the same results.

For the benefit of the reader we provide a general outline of the strategy of the proof. Our goal is to estimate the “size” of the set

f(\crit_{\nu}(f)\cap B)
.
More precisely, we bound the
\varepsilon
-entropy
of

f(\crit_{\nu}(f)\cap B)

for any

\varepsilon>0
, and then pass to the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
.

We observe that the
\varepsilon
-entropy of

f(\crit_{\nu}(f)\cap B)
is controlled with the one of the set of
almost-critical values
f(C^{\Lambda}(f)\cap B)
, see
Definition

17
. In turn, this is controlled by the
\varepsilon
-entropy of suitable almost-critical values of the approximating maps
f_{n}
, namely
f(C^{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}(f_{n})\cap B)
, see (
136
).

To proceed, we use the relation between the
\varepsilon
-entropy of sets in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and their Vitushkin variations (
Definition

15
), provided by

[
47
, Thm. 3.5]
.

Finally, we estimate the Vitushkin variations of
f_{n}(C^{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}(f_{n})\cap B)
applying
Theorem

G
, exploiting the hypothesis that the maps
f_{n}
are polynomial. This is the connection with quantitative semialgebraic geometry from
Section

3
. The crucial point of
Theorem

G
is that the bound is quantitative with respect to parameters
\Lambda=\Lambda_{\varepsilon}
and
n
. This allows to chose a large enough
n=n_{\varepsilon}
, see (
150
), to ensure convergence when passing to the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Proof.

Step 1
. We show that for any

\nu=0,\dots,m-1

the set
\crit_{\nu}(f)\cap B
is contained in a suitable set of almost-critical points of
f
. By assumption,
B
is weakly pre-compact. Since
Df:H\to\mathcal{L}(H,\mathbb{R}^{m})
is weakly continuous, and
by
Lemma

18
the function

\sigma_{i}:\mathcal{L}(H,\mathbb{R}^{m})\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous for every
i=1,\dots,m
. Therefore we have

(126)

\Sigma_{i}:=\sup_{B}\sigma_{i}(D_{x}f)<\infty.

Hence, defining
\Lambda:=(\Sigma_{1},\dots,\Sigma_{\nu},0\dots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
we get

(127)

\crit_{\nu}(f)\cap B\subset C^{\Lambda}(f)\cap B.

Step 2
. We now relate critical points of
f
to almost-critical points of the approximating polynomials
f_{n}
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Recall that, by
Lemma

18
, the singular values are
1
-Lipschitz with respect to the operator norm. Then for any sufficiently large

n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

cq^{-n}\leq\varepsilon
and for all
x\in B
we have

(128)

|\sigma_{i}\left(D_{x}f|_{E_{n}}\right)-\sigma_{i}(D_{\pi_{E_{n}}(x)}f_{n})|\leq\|\left(D_{x}f\right)|_{E_{n}}-D_{\pi_{E_{n}}(x)}f_{n}\|_{\op}\leq\varepsilon.

Hence, for all
x\in B\cap C^{\Lambda}(f)
we have the following estimate for all
i=1,\dots,m

(129)

\sigma_{i}\left(D_{\pi_{E_{n}}(x)}f_{n}\right)\leq\varepsilon+\sup_{y\in C^{\Lambda}(f)\cap B}\sigma_{i}\left(D_{y}f|_{E_{n}}\right)\leq\varepsilon+\sup_{y\in C^{\Lambda}(f)\cap B}\sigma_{i}\left(D_{y}f\right)\leq\varepsilon+\Lambda_{i},

where, in the second inequality, we used that singular values are monotone with respect to restriction to subspaces. Hence, defining

(130)

\Lambda^{\varepsilon}:=(\Sigma_{1}+\varepsilon,\dots,\Sigma_{\nu}+\varepsilon,\varepsilon,\dots,\varepsilon),

by (
129
) we get

(131)

\pi_{E_{n}}\left(C^{\Lambda}(f)\cap B\right)\subset C^{\Lambda^{\varepsilon}}(f_{n})\cap\pi_{E_{n}}(B)\subset C^{\Lambda^{\varepsilon}}(f_{n})\cap B_{E_{n}}(r),

where
r>0
is such that
B\subseteq B_{H}(r)
, and thus
B_{E_{n}}(r)=B_{H}(r)\cap E_{n}
denotes the ball in
E_{n}
defined by the restriction of the Hilbert norm.

Step 3
.
We use the inclusion (
131
) to estimate the entropy dimension of

f(\crit_{\nu}(f)\cap B)

with the one of almost-critical values of
f_{n}
. By (
123
), for sufficiently large
n
, we have

(132)

\sup_{x\in B}\|f(x)-f_{n}\circ\pi_{E_{n}}(x)\|\leq\varepsilon.

Finally, we get

(133)

\displaystyle M(2\varepsilon,f(\crit_{\nu}(f)\cap B)

\displaystyle\leq M(2\varepsilon,f(C^{\Lambda}(f)\cap B))

by (
127
)

(134)

\displaystyle\leq M\left(2\varepsilon,\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}\left(f_{n}\circ\pi_{E_{n}}\left(C^{\Lambda}(f)\cap B\right)\right)\right)

by (
132
)

(135)

\displaystyle\leq M\left(\varepsilon,f_{n}\circ\pi_{E_{n}}\left(C^{\Lambda}(f)\cap B\right)\right)

by definition of
\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}

(136)

\displaystyle\leq M(\varepsilon,f_{n}(C^{\Lambda^{\varepsilon}}(f_{n})\cap B_{E_{n}}(r))),

by (
131
)

where we recall that
M(\varepsilon,A)
is the minimal number of closed balls of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
of radius
\varepsilon
needed to cover
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(A)
denotes the
\varepsilon
neighbourhood of
A
.
This concludes the proof of step 3.

The rest of the proof consists in estimating (
136
). We will use the results from
Section

3.4
, for this reason in the next step we discuss how to bring the problem to the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Step 4
.
We fix a basis for
E_{n}
which is orthonormal with respect to the inner product of
H
. This yields a linear isometry of Hilbert spaces
\ell_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to E_{n}
. In particular, for the transpose, it holds
\ell_{n}^{\top}=\ell_{n}^{-1}
. Consider then the polynomial map
\tilde{f}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined by
\tilde{f}_{n}:=f_{n}\circ\ell_{n}
,
n\geq m
. We have

(137)

\sigma_{i}(D_{v}\tilde{f}_{n})=\lambda_{i}(D_{\ell_{n}(v)}f_{n}\circ\ell_{n}\circ\ell_{n}^{\top}\circ D_{\ell_{n}(v)}f_{n}^{\top})^{1/2}=\sigma_{i}(D_{\ell_{n}(v)}f_{n}),\qquad\forall\,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

It follows that, for sufficiently large
n
,

(138)

\ell_{n}(C^{\Lambda^{\varepsilon}}(\tilde{f}_{n}))=C^{\Lambda^{\varepsilon}}(f_{n}).

Hence,
we have

(139)

f_{n}(C^{\Lambda^{\varepsilon}}(f_{n})\cap B_{E_{n}}(r))=f_{n}(\ell_{n}(C^{\Lambda^{\varepsilon}}(\tilde{f}_{n}))\cap B_{E_{n}}(r))=\tilde{f}_{n}(C^{\Lambda^{\varepsilon}}(\tilde{f}_{n})\cap B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(r)).

By
[
47
, Thm. 3.5]
we have

(140)

M(\varepsilon,\tilde{f}_{n}(C^{\Lambda^{\varepsilon}}(\tilde{f}_{n})\cap B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(r)))\leq C(m)\sum_{i=0}^{m}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{i}}V_{i}\bigg(\tilde{f}_{n}(C^{\Lambda^{\varepsilon}}(\tilde{f}_{n})\cap B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(r))\bigg),

where
C(m)>0
is a constant that depends only on
m
. We now apply the results from
Section

3.4
in order to estimate the right-hand side of the last inequality.

Step 5
. Take
n
sufficiently large so that it also satisfies
n\geq m
. Then, by
Theorem

G
, for every
i=0,\dots,m
we have

(141)

V_{i}\bigg(\tilde{f}_{n}(C^{\Lambda^{\varepsilon}}(\tilde{f}_{n})\cap B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(r))\bigg)\leq\mathrm{cst}(m,r)n^{m}\beta_{0}^{n}\Lambda_{0}^{\varepsilon}\cdots\Lambda_{i}^{\varepsilon},

where we recall
\beta_{0}=\beta_{0}(d,m)
and
\Lambda_{0}^{\varepsilon}=1
by definition. Now, we have for all
i=1,\dots,m

(142)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\varepsilon}_{1}\cdots\Lambda^{\varepsilon}_{i}

\displaystyle=(\Lambda_{1}+\varepsilon)\cdots(\Lambda_{i}+\varepsilon)

(143)

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=0}^{i}\varepsilon^{i-h}\sum_{0=j_{0}<j_{1}<\dots<j_{h}\leq i}\Lambda_{j_{0}}\cdots\Lambda_{j_{h}}

(144)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{h=0}^{i}\varepsilon^{i-h}\sum_{0=j_{0}<j_{1}<\cdots<j_{h}\leq i}\Lambda_{0}\cdots\Lambda_{h}

(145)

\displaystyle=\binom{i}{h}\sum_{h=0}^{i}\varepsilon^{i-h}\Lambda_{0}\cdots\Lambda_{h}

(146)

\displaystyle\leq i!\sum_{h=0}^{i}\varepsilon^{i-h}\Lambda_{0}\cdots\Lambda_{h}.

Therefore, by (
140
), (
141
) and (
146
), for large
n
we have

(147)

\displaystyle M(\varepsilon,\tilde{f}_{n}(C^{\Lambda^{\varepsilon}}(\tilde{f}_{n})\cap B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(r)))

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{cst}(m,r)C(m)n^{m}\beta_{0}^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{m}\frac{i!}{\varepsilon^{i}}\sum_{h=0}^{i}\varepsilon^{i-h}\Lambda_{0}\cdots\Lambda_{h}

(148)

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{\mathrm{cst}}(m,r)n^{m}\beta_{0}^{n}\sum_{h=0}^{\nu}\frac{\Lambda_{0}\cdots\Lambda_{h}}{\varepsilon^{h}},

where we note that, by definition,
\Lambda_{h}=0
for all
h>\nu
, and
\widetilde{\mathrm{cst}}(m,r)
denotes a constant that depends only on
m,r
.
From this we deduce that

(149)

\ln M(\varepsilon,\tilde{f}_{n}(C^{\Lambda^{\varepsilon}}(\tilde{f}_{n})\cap B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(r)))\leq\ln(\widetilde{\mathrm{cst}}(m,r))+n\ln\beta_{0}+m\ln n+\ln\left(\sum_{h=0}^{\nu}\frac{\Lambda_{0}\cdots\Lambda_{h}}{\varepsilon^{h}}\right),

for all
\varepsilon>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
cq^{-n}\leq\varepsilon
and
n\geq m
.

Step 6
. We show how (
149
) yields the estimate on
\dim_{e}(f(\crit_{\nu}(f)\cap B))
. For
\varepsilon>0
we choose
n=n_{\varepsilon}
where

(150)

n_{\varepsilon}:=\left\lceil\log_{q}\frac{c}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil,

in such a way that (
136
), (
139
) and (
149
) hold when
\varepsilon\to 0
. By (
136
) and (
139
) we have

(151)

\dim_{e}f(\crit_{\nu}(f)\cap B)\leq\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\ln M(\varepsilon,\tilde{f}_{n_{\varepsilon}}(C^{\Lambda^{\varepsilon}}(\tilde{f}_{n_{\varepsilon}})\cap B_{\mathbb{R}^{{n_{\varepsilon}}}}(r)))}{\ln\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}}.

Hence now we estimate the right-hand side through (
149
).
As
\varepsilon
goes to zero we have

(152)

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{n_{\varepsilon}\ln\beta_{0}}{\ln\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\left\lceil\log_{q}\frac{c}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil\ln\beta_{0}}{\ln\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}}=\frac{\ln\beta_{0}}{\ln q}.

We also have, taking into account (
150
), that

(153)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{m\ln n_{\varepsilon}}{\ln(\frac{1}{2\varepsilon})}=0.

Furthermore

(154)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\ln(\frac{1}{2\varepsilon})}\ln\left(\sum_{h=0}^{\nu}\frac{\Lambda_{0}\cdots\Lambda_{h}}{\varepsilon^{h}}\right)=\nu.

Therefore,

(155)

\dim_{e}f(\crit_{\nu}(f)\cap B)\leq\nu+\frac{\ln\beta_{0}}{\ln q},

concluding the proof.
∎

4.2.
Sard-type theorems and Kolmogorov
n
-width

In this section, we recall the definition of Kolmogorov
n
-width, and we show its role in
Sard-type theorems.

Definition 30

(Kolmogorov
n
-width)
.

Let
X
be a normed space and
S\subset X
be a subset. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the
Kolmogorov
n
-width
of
S
in
X
is

(156)

\Omega_{n}(S,X)=\inf_{\dim E=n}\sup_{u\in S}\inf_{y\in E}\|u-y\|,

where the infimum is taken over all
n
-dimensional linear subspaces
E
of
X
.

The asymptotics of
n
-width measures in a quantitative way the compactness of a set, in fact the following holds (see
[
36
, Prop. 1.2]
).

Proposition 31
.

S
is precompact if and only if

S
is bounded and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}(S)=0
.

For various properties of the
n
-width we refer the reader to
[
36
]
;
we recall here those that we will need in the sequel, see
[
36
, Thm. 1.1]
.

Proposition 32
.

Let
X
be a normed space and
S\subset X
. Then for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

(1)

\Omega_{n}(S,X)=\Omega_{n}(\bar{S},X)
, where
\bar{S}
is the closure of
S
;

(2)

For every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

(157)

\Omega_{n}(\alpha S,X)=|\alpha|\Omega_{n}(S,X);

(3)

Let
\mathrm{co}(S)
be the convex hull of
S
. Then

(158)

\Omega_{n}(\mathrm{co}(S),X)=\Omega_{n}(S,X);

(4)

Let
\mathrm{b}(S)=\{\alpha x\mid x\in S,\,|\alpha|\leq 1\}
be the balanced hull of
S
. Then

(159)

\Omega_{n}(\mathrm{b}(S),X)=\Omega_{n}(S,X).

Remark 33
.

As a consequence, in all our results, up to enlarging
S
, one can assume without loss of generality that
S
is convex and centrally symmetric, without changing its
n
-width.

We establish now a connection between
n
-width of compact sets and locally Lipschitz functions.

Lemma 34
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space,
(Y,\|\cdot\|_{Y})
a Banach space, and let
f:H\to Y
be locally Lipschitz. Let
K\subset H
be a compact subset. Then there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
there exist a linear subspace
E_{n}\subset H

of dimension
n

such that

(160)

\sup_{x\in K}\|f(x)-f(\pi_{E_{n}}(x))\|_{Y}\leq c(f,K)\Omega_{n}(K,H).

Proof.

Let
\{B_{H}(x_{i},r/2)\}_{i\in I}
a finite cover of
K
by balls, with centers
x_{i}\in K
and radii
r/2>0
. We can assume that
f
is
L
-Lipschitz on each
B_{H}(x_{i},r)
, for some
L>0
. Since
K
is compact
\Omega_{n}(K,H)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, hence there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
we have
\Omega_{n}(K,H)<r/4
. By definition of
n
-width, for all
n\geq n_{0}
there exists a
n
-dimensional subspace
E_{n}\subset H
such that

(161)

\sup_{x\in K}\|x-\pi_{E_{n}}(x)\|<2\Omega_{n}(K,H)<\frac{r}{2}.

Let
x\in K
. Then by construction
x\in B_{H}(x_{i},r/2)
for some
i\in I
. By (
161
) we have
\pi_{E_{n}}(x)\in B_{H}(x_{i},r)
. Since
f
is
L
-Lipschitz on every
B_{H}(x_{i},r)
we have

(162)

\sup_{x\in K}\|f(x)-f(\pi_{E_{n}}(x))\|_{Y}\leq L\sup_{x\in K}\|x-\pi_{E_{n}}(x)\|\leq 2L\Omega_{n}(K,H),

where we used again (
161
). This proves the result, with
c(f,K)=2L
.
∎

We can now prove the following Sard-type theorem, corresponding to
Theorem

A
in the Introduction.

Theorem 35
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space and
K\subset H
be a compact set such that, for some
q>1
, it holds

(163)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}(K,H)^{1/n}\leq q^{-1}.

Let
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
. There exists
\beta_{0}=\beta_{0}(d,m)>1
such that for all
f\in\mathscr{P}_{d}^{m}(H)
and
\nu\leq m-1
we have

(164)

\dim_{e}\bigg(f\big(\crit_{\nu}(f)\cap K\big)\bigg)\leq\nu+\frac{\ln\beta_{0}}{\ln q}.

In particular, if
q>\beta_{0}
, then the Sard property holds on
K
:

(165)

\mu\bigg(f\big(\crit(f)\cap K\big)\bigg)=0.

Proof.

Let
q_{\varepsilon}\in(0,q)
. By assumption,
\Omega_{n}(K,H)\leq q^{-n}_{\varepsilon}
for sufficiently large
n
.
We prove that
f
satisfies the hypothesis of
Theorem

D
. Since
f\in\mathscr{P}^{m}_{d}(H)
(see
Definition

1
), the map

(166)

(f,Df):H\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathcal{L}(H,\mathbb{R}^{m})

is locally Lipschitz. The codomain
Y=\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathcal{L}(H,\mathbb{R}^{m})
is a Banach space equipped with the norm
\|(v,A)\|_{Y}=\|v\|+\|A\|_{\op}
for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
A\in\mathcal{L}(H,\mathbb{R}^{m})
. Hence we can apply
Lemma

34
to
get
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
there exists a
n
-dimensional linear subspace

E_{n}\subset H
such that

(167)

\sup_{x\in K}\|f(x)-f(\pi_{E_{n}}(x))\|+\|D_{x}f-D_{{\pi_{E_{n}(x)}}}f\|_{\op}\leq c(f,K)q^{-n}_{\varepsilon},

where
\pi_{E_{n}}:H\to E_{n}
denotes the orthogonal projection. Let
f_{n}:E_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined by the restriction
f_{n}:=f|_{E_{n}}
, which are polynomial maps with degree uniformly bounded above by
d
. It holds

(168)

\|(D_{x}f)|_{E_{n}}-D_{\pi_{E_{n}}(x)}f_{n}\|_{\op}=\|(D_{x}f)|_{E_{n}}-(D_{\pi_{E_{n}}(x)}f)|_{E_{n}}\|_{\op}\leq\|D_{x}f-D_{\pi_{E_{n}}(x)}f\|_{\op}.

As a consequence of the above two estimates, assumption (
19
) of
Theorem

D
holds, yielding

(169)

\dim_{e}\bigg(f\big(\crit_{\nu}(f)\cap K\big)\bigg)\leq\nu+\frac{\ln\beta_{0}}{\ln q_{\varepsilon}}.

Letting
q_{\varepsilon}\uparrow q
we obtain the thesis.
∎

4.3.
Counterexamples to the Sard theorem in infinite dimension

In this section we provide examples of maps
f\in\mathscr{P}^{m}_{d}(H)
as in
Theorem

A

for which there exists a compact set
K\subset H

such that the set

f(\crit(f)\cap K)
has not measure zero, however it holds

\Omega_{n}(K,H)\leq cq^{-n}
. This shows the necessity of the quantitative assumption
q>\beta_{0}(m,d)
.
As a consequence, we get lower bounds on the semialgebraic constant
\beta_{0}
.

We start with the following example, which is a minor modification of Kupka’s one
[
28
]
.

Example 36

(Kupka revisited)
.

Let
q>1
. Let
f:\ell^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

(170)

f(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\phi(q^{k-1}x_{k}),

where
\phi
is the Kupka polynomial as in

[
47
, Sec. 10.2.3]
. Namely
\phi
has degree
3
, with
\phi(0)=\phi^{\prime}(0)=\phi(1)-1=\phi^{\prime}(1)=0
. If we assume

q^{3}/2<1

then
f
is
C^{\infty}
. All critical points have the form

(171)

\crit(f)=\left\{\left(x_{1},\frac{x_{2}}{q},\dots,\frac{x_{k}}{q^{k-1}},\dots\right)\,\middle|\,x_{i}\in\{0,1\}\right\},

and the set of critical values is
[0,1]
.
Furthermore, given

y=\left(x_{1},\frac{x_{2}}{q},\dots,\frac{x_{k}}{q^{k-1}},\dots\right)\in\crit(f)
,
we consider its projection
y_{n}
on

\mathrm{span}\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
, that is

(172)

y_{n}=\left(x_{1},\frac{x_{2}}{q},\dots,\frac{x_{n}}{q^{n-1}},0,0,0,\dots\right).

We have

(173)

\left\lVert y-y_{n}\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}}^{2}\leq\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q^{2(k-1)}}\leq\int_{n}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q^{2t}}=\frac{q^{-2n}}{2\ln q}.

Hence,

(174)

\Omega_{n}(\crit(f),\ell^{2})\leq\sup_{y\in\crit(f)}\left\lVert y-y_{n}\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}}\leq\frac{q^{-n}}{\sqrt{2\ln q}}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}(\crit(f),\ell^{2})\leq q^{-1}.

Since
f
and
\crit(f)
satisfy all the hypothesis of
Theorem

A
(provided that
q^{3}/2<1
), we get a consequence that

(175)

\beta_{0}(1,3)\geq 2^{1/3}.

We now get the lower bound on
\beta_{0}(1,d)
for
d\geq 3
by improving on the above construction.

Example 37

(Kupka revisited, degree
d\geq 3
)
.

We need the following classical fact, which follows from
[
27
]
: for any
d\geq 2
there exists a polynomial
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
of degree
d
such that

(176)

\psi(\crit(\psi))=\{0,\dots,d-2\}.

(See also
[
26
, Thm. 1]
which gives a version of this result taking into account also possible multiplicities.)
We fix
d\geq 3
and we consider a polynomial
\psi
of degree
d
such that (
176
) holds. For
q>1
we consider the map
f_{d}:\ell^{2}\to\mathbb{R}

(177)

f_{d}(x):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(d-1)^{k}}\psi(q^{k-1}x_{k}).

If
q\in(1,(d-1)^{1/d})
, then the map
f_{d}
is
C^{\infty}
. The set of critical points of
f_{d}
is

(178)

\crit(f_{d})=\left\{\left(y_{1},\frac{y_{2}}{q},\dots,\frac{y_{k}}{q^{k-1}},\dots\right)\in\ell^{2}\,\middle|\,\psi^{\prime}(y_{k})=0,\,\forall\,k\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

Hence we have

(179)

f_{d}(\crit(f_{d}))=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(d-1)^{k}}c_{k}\,\middle|\,c_{k}\in\{0,\dots,d-2\}\right\}=[0,1].

Let
\zeta=\max\{|z|\mid\psi^{\prime}(z)=0\}
. Exactly as in
Example

36
we get for every
n\in\mathbb{N}

(180)

\Omega_{n}(\crit(f_{d}),\ell^{2})\leq\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{2\ln q}}q^{-n}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}(\crit(f_{d}),\ell^{2})\leq q^{-1}.

For any
q\in(1,(d-1)^{1/d})
the function
f_{d}
and the set
K=\crit(f_{d})
satisfy the hypothesis of
Theorem

35
, but
f_{d}(\crit(f_{d}))=[0,1]
. It follows that

(181)

\beta_{0}(1,d)\geq(d-1)^{1/d},\qquad\forall\,d\geq 3.

In the next example we extend the construction to arbitrary dimension of the codomain.

Example 38

(Kupka revisited,
d\geq 3
, arbitrary codomain)
.

Let
f_{d}
be as in
Example

37
, and consider

g_{d}:\mathbb{R}^{m-1}\times\ell^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined as

(182)

g_{d}(x,y):=(x,f_{d}(y)).

We have

(183)

\crit(g_{d})=\mathbb{R}^{m-1}\times\crit(f_{d}).

Taking
E_{n+m-1}=\mathrm{span}\{(e_{i},0),\,(0,e_{j})\mid i=1,\dots,m-1,\,j=1,\dots,n\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m-1}\times\ell^{2}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we can estimate the
n
-width of
\crit(g_{d})
as

(184)

\Omega_{n+m-1}(\crit(g_{d}),\mathbb{R}^{m-1}\times\ell^{2})\leq\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{2\ln q}}q^{-n}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}(\crit(g_{d}),\mathbb{R}^{m-1}\times\ell^{2})\leq q^{-1}.

We observe, provided that
q\in(1,(d-1)^{1/d})
, the function
g_{d}
satisfies all the hypothesis of
Theorem

35
, but the set

g_{d}(\crit(g_{d}))=\mathbb{R}^{m-1}\times[0,1]
has not measure zero. Hence

(185)

\beta_{0}(d,m)\geq(d-1)^{1/d},\qquad\forall\,m\in\mathbb{N},\,d\geq 3.

We collect the content of
Examples

36
,
37
and
38
in a unified statement (see
Theorem

B
).

Theorem 39
.

Let
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
, with
d\geq 3
, and let
1<q<(d-1)^{1/d}
. There exist a Hilbert space
H
, and
f\in\mathscr{P}_{d}^{m}(H)
such that
K=\crit(f)\cap B_{H}(r)
is compact for all
r>0
, with

(186)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}(K,H)^{1/n}\leq q^{-1},

and
f:H\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
does not verify the Sard property, namely
\mu(f(\crit(f)\cap K))>0
. Therefore, the semialgebraic constant
\beta_{0}(d,m)
of
Theorem

A
satisfies

(187)

\beta_{0}(d,m)\geq(d-1)^{1/d},\qquad\forall\,m\in\mathbb{N},\,d\geq 3.

In all the examples above we have taken
K
to be set of all critical points of a given function in a ball.
We now show a different construction, where the set
K
is strictly contained in the set of critical points, more precisely is the set of points where the differential has rank zero.

Example 40

(Rank zero counterexample to Sard)
.

We consider
f_{d}:\ell^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
as in
Example

37
, and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. We define

h:(\ell^{2})^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
as

(188)

h(x^{1},\dots,x^{m}):=(f_{d}(x^{1}),\dots,f_{d}(x^{m})).

For any
x=(x^{1},\dots,x^{m})\in(\ell^{2})^{m}

we have

(189)

\im(D_{x}h)=\im(D_{x^{1}}f_{d})\times\dots\times\im(D_{x^{m}}f_{d}).

Hence
x\in\crit_{\nu}(h)
(the set of points where the rank of the differential is
\leq\nu
) if and only if
at least
m-\nu
components of
x
are critical points of
f_{d}
.
In particular, we consider the compact set
K
of all critical points of
h
of rank zero, namely

(190)

K:=\crit_{0}(h)=\crit(f_{d})^{m}.

We have
K\subsetneq\crit(h)
, and

(191)

h(\crit(h)\cap K)=h(\crit_{0}(h))=[0,1]^{m}.

As in the previous examples,
K
is compact with exponential
n
-width. By taking finite-dimensional spaces
E_{nm}=\mathrm{span}\{\underbrace{(0,\dots,e_{i},\dots,0)}_{j\text{-th component}}\in(\ell^{2})^{m}\mid i=1,\dots,n,\,j=1,\dots,m\}
, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(192)

\Omega_{nm}(K,(\ell^{2})^{m})\leq m\Omega_{n}(\crit(f_{d}),\ell^{2})\leq\frac{m\zeta}{\sqrt{2\ln q}}q^{-n}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}(K,(\ell^{2})^{m})\leq q^{-1/m},

where
\zeta
is the same constant appearing in
Example

37
.

4.4.
Sard on linear subspaces

Next, we prove
Theorem

C
. We will need the following lemma.

Lemma 41

(Critical points under restrictions)
.

Let
(X,\|\cdot\|)
be a normed space and let
f:X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
map. For any linear subspace
Y\subset X
, consider the restriction
f|_{Y}:Y\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Note that the latter is a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
map in the Fréchet sense from the normed vector space
(Y,\|\cdot\|)
to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, with
D_{u}(f|_{Y})=(D_{u}f)|_{Y}
for all
u\in Y
. Then, for all
\nu=0,\dots,m-1
it holds

(193)

\mathrm{Crit}_{\nu}(f|_{Y})\supseteq\mathrm{Crit}_{\nu}(f)\cap Y,

with equality if
Y
is dense in
X
.

Proof.

The inclusion is proven by noting that, for all
u\in Y
, it holds

(194)

\mathrm{rank}\left(D_{u}(f|_{Y}):Y\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\right)\leq\mathrm{rank}\left(D_{u}f:X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\right).

Assume that
Y
is dense in
X
. Thus if
\lambda\in(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{*}\setminus\{0\}
is such that

0\equiv\lambda\circ D_{u}(f|_{Y})=(\lambda\circ D_{u}f)|_{Y}
, since
Y
is dense in
X
, it follows that

\lambda\circ D_{u}f\equiv 0
. This means that for all
u\in Y

(195)

\mathrm{rank}\left(D_{u}f:X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\right)\leq\mathrm{rank}\left(D_{u}(f|_{Y}):Y\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\right).

It follows that
\mathrm{Crit}_{\nu}(f|_{Y})\subseteq\mathrm{Crit}_{\nu}(f)\cap Y
, proving equality of the two sets.
∎

We can now prove
Theorem

C
, of which we recall the statement.

Theorem 42
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space and
K\subset H
be a compact set such that, for some
q>1
, it holds

(196)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}(K,H)^{1/n}\leq q^{-1}.

Let
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\beta_{0}=\beta_{0}(d,m)>1
be the same constant of
Theorem

A
. Consider the (possibly non-closed) linear subspace

(197)

V:=\mathrm{span}(K).

Then for all
f\in\mathscr{P}_{d}^{m}(H)
and
\nu\leq m-1
the restriction
f|_{V}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfies

(198)

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\bigg(f\big(\mathrm{Crit}_{\nu}(f|_{V})\big)\bigg)\leq\nu+\frac{\log\beta_{0}}{\log q}.

In particular, if
q>\beta_{0}
, then the restriction
f|_{V}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfies the Sard property

(199)

\mu\bigg(f\big(\mathrm{Crit}(f|_{V})\big)\bigg)=0.

In all previous results,
f\big(\mathrm{Crit}_{\nu}(f|_{V})\big)
can be replaced with the smaller set
f(\mathrm{Crit}_{\nu}(f)\cap V)
.

Proof.

Let
X
denote the closure of
V
in
H
. Observe first that

(200)

\Omega_{n}(K,H)=\Omega_{n}(K,X).

The inequality
\Omega_{n}(K,H)\leq\Omega_{n}(K,X)
is clear since every
n
-dimensional subspace of
X
is also a subspace of
H
. For the other inequality,
since
X
is closed in
H
, we can write
H=X\oplus X^{\perp}
and denote by
\pi:H\to X
the orthogonal projection. Then, using that
K\subset V\subset X
, we obtain

(201)

\displaystyle\Omega_{n}(K,H)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\subset H\\
\dim Y=n\end{subarray}}\sup_{u\in K}\inf_{y\in Y}\|u-y\|\geq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\subset H\\
\dim Y=n\end{subarray}}\sup_{u\in K}\inf_{y\in Y}\|u-\pi(y)\|

(202)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\subset H\\
\dim Y=n\end{subarray}}\sup_{u\in K}\inf_{z\in\pi(Y)}\|u-z\|

(203)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\subset X\\
\dim Z\leq n\end{subarray}}\sup_{u\in K}\inf_{z\in Z}\|u-z\|

(204)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\subset X\\
\dim Z=n\end{subarray}}\sup_{u\in K}\inf_{z\in Z}\|u-z\|=\Omega_{n}(K,X).

We can apply
Theorem

A
to the map
\tilde{f}\in\mathscr{P}_{d}^{m}(X)
, defined by
\tilde{f}:=f|_{X}
, with
f\in\mathscr{P}_{d}^{m}(H)
, and the compact set
K\subset X
obtaining for all
\nu\leq m-1

(205)

\dim_{e}\bigg(\tilde{f}\big(\crit_{\nu}(\tilde{f})\cap K\big)\bigg)\leq\nu+\frac{\ln\beta_{0}}{\ln q}.

By applying
Lemma

41
(with
Y=V
) and noting that, on
K\subset V
, we have
\tilde{f}=f|_{V}
, we obtain:

(206)

\dim_{e}\bigg(f\big(\crit_{\nu}(f|_{V})\cap K\big)\bigg)=\dim_{e}\bigg(\tilde{f}\big(\crit_{\nu}(\tilde{f})\cap K\big)\bigg)\leq\nu+\frac{\ln\beta_{0}}{\ln q}.

Let
K^{\prime}
be the balanced and convex hull of
K
. It holds:

(207)

V=\mathrm{span}(K)=\mathrm{span}(K^{\prime})=\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}jK^{\prime}.

By
Proposition

32
the
n
-width of the compact, convex, centrally symmetric sets
jK^{\prime}
satisfy

(208)

\Omega_{n}(jK^{\prime},H)=j\Omega_{n}(K^{\prime},H)=j\Omega_{n}(K,H).

Therefore we can repeat the above argument, obtaining (
206
) for
jK^{\prime}
in place of
K
, for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Furthermore, we can replace the entropy dimension with the (smaller) Hausdorff dimension, which has the advantage of being countably stable. Taking the supremum over
j\in\mathbb{N}
we obtain (
198
).

To conclude, we observe that by
Lemma

41
we have
f(\mathrm{Crit}_{\nu}(f|_{V}))\supseteq f(\mathrm{Crit}_{\nu}(f)\cap V)
, which yields the last claim of the statement of the theorem.
∎

4.5.
A class of maps with the global Sard property

Using
Theorem

D
, we study a class of maps from a Hilbert space to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
for which the Sard property holds true. The case
m=1
is not new, and it was proved in
[
46
]
, see also
[
47
]
.
We need the following preliminary facts.

Lemma 43

(Markov inequality)
.

Let
p:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a polynomial map with components of degree at most
d
. Then for every
r>0
it holds

(209)

\sup_{x\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(r)}\|D_{x}p\|_{\op}\leq\frac{\sqrt{m}d^{2}}{r}\sup_{x\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(r)}\|p(x)\|.

Proof.

From
[
25
, Thm. VI]
we get the thesis for
r=1
. Then, we conclude considering the rescaled polynomial

x\to p(rx)
.
∎

Lemma 44

(Estimates on balls at different radii)
.

Let
p:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a polynomial map with components of degree at most
d
. Then for all
r>0
it holds

(210)

\sup_{x\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(r)}\|p(x)\|\leq\alpha(d,m)n^{d}(1+r)^{d}\sup_{x\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1)}\|p(x)\|,

where
\alpha(d,m)=m^{d/2}d^{2d}(d+1)
is a constant depending only on
d
and
m
.

Proof.

We can write the polynomial map as

(211)

p(x)=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq d}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha},

where
c_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and the sum is over the multi-indices
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
with
|\alpha|=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\leq d
. Since
|\alpha|!c_{\alpha}=\partial_{x^{\alpha}}p|_{x=0}
, we can iterate Markov inequality (for
r=1
, see
Lemma

43
) and obtain

(212)

\|c_{\alpha}\|\leq\frac{1}{|\alpha|!}m^{|\alpha|/2}d^{2|\alpha|}\sup_{x\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1)}\|p(x)\|\leq m^{|\alpha|/2}d^{2|\alpha|}\sup_{x\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1)}\|p(x)\|.

Therefore, for
r>1
, we have

(213)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(r)}\|p(x)\|

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1)}\|p(rx)\|

(214)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1)}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{d}r^{i}\left(\sum_{|\alpha|=i}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}\right)\right\|

(215)

\displaystyle\leq(1+r)^{d}\left(\sum_{|\alpha|\leq d}m^{|\alpha|/2}d^{2|\alpha|}\right)\sup_{x\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1)}\|p(x)\|

(216)

\displaystyle\leq(1+r)^{d}m^{d/2}d^{2d}(d+1)n^{d}\sup_{x\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1)}\|p(x)\|,

where we used the fact that the number of multi-indices
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
with
|\alpha|\leq d
is equal to
(1+n+n^{2}+\dots+n^{d})\leq(d+1)n^{d}

∎

For the rest of this section, we assume that the Hilbert space
H
is separable, and we fix a Hilbert basis
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset H
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we set
E_{k}:=\mathrm{span}\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}
and we denote by
\pi_{k}:H\to E_{k}
the corresponding orthogonal projection, that is if
x=\sum_{i}x_{i}e_{i}
, then
\pi_{k}(x)=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
. We now define a special class of maps in this setting.

Proposition 45

(Construction of special maps)
.

Let
H
be a separable Hilbert space. For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
p_{k}:E_{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be polynomial maps with

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\deg p_{k}\leq d
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
and such that

(217)

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sup_{x\in B_{E_{k}}(r)}\|p_{k}(x)\|<\infty,\qquad\forall\,r>0.

Then the map
f:H\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
given by

(218)

f(x):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}),\qquad\forall\,x\in H,

is well–defined,
f\in\mathscr{P}_{d}^{m}(H)
(see
Definition

1
) and its differential is given by

(219)

D_{x}f=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}D_{x}(p_{k}\circ\pi_{k}).

Proof.

For a Banach spaces
(X,\|\cdot\|_{X})
,
(Y,\|\cdot\|_{Y})
, and a closed set
U\subseteq X
, we denote by

\mathcal{B}(U,Y)

the space of all continuous and bounded functions
f:U\to Y
. It is a Banach space when endowed with the norm
\sup_{x\in U}\left\lVert f(x)\right\rVert_{Y}
.

We first note that the assumption (
217
) implies that the sequence of maps
f_{n}:H\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
given by
f_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}\circ\pi_{k}
is Cauchy in
\mathcal{B}(B_{H}(r),\mathbb{R}^{m})
for all
r>0
. In particular the right hand side of (
218
) converges locally uniformly. For any
n\in N
we have

(220)

D_{x}f_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}D_{x}(p_{k}\circ\pi_{k}).

Furthermore,

x\mapsto D_{x}f_{n}\in\mathcal{B}(B_{H}(r);\mathcal{L}(H,\mathbb{R}^{m}))
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
r>0
. We now prove that it is a Cauchy sequence.
Indeed, using
Lemma

43
, for any

n_{2}\geq n_{1}

(221)

\sup_{x\in B_{H}(r)}\left\lVert D_{x}f_{n_{2}}-D_{x}f_{n_{1}}\right\rVert_{\op}\leq\frac{\sqrt{m}d^{2}}{r}\sum_{k>n_{1}}\sup_{x\in B_{E_{k}}(r)}\|p_{k}(x)\|\to 0,

as
n_{2},n_{1}\to\infty
. Therefore,
\{Df_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has a limit in
\mathcal{B}(B_{H}(r),\mathcal{L}(H,\mathbb{R}^{m}))
, for all
r>0
. It follows that
f
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
and (
219
) holds, see e.g.
[
29
, Thm. 9.1]
.

Thanks to (
219
), we prove that
Df:H\to\mathcal{L}(H,\mathbb{R}^{m})
is weakly continuous.

5.
Applications to the Endpoint maps of Carnot groups

We apply in this last section the results from the previous sections to the study of the Sard property for Endpoint maps for Carnot groups.

Definition 48

(Carnot groups)
.

An
m
-dimensional Carnot group of step
s\in\mathbb{N}
is a connected and simply connected Lie group
(\mathbb{G},\cdot)
of dimension
m
, whose Lie algebra of left-invariant vector fields
\mathfrak{g}
admits a stratification of step
s
, that is

(260)

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{s},

where

\mathfrak{g}_{i}\neq\{0\}
,

\mathfrak{g}_{i+1}=[\mathfrak{g}_{1},\mathfrak{g}_{i}]

for all
i=1,\dots,s-1
and
[\mathfrak{g}_{1},\mathfrak{g}_{s}]=\{0\}
. We set
k_{i}=\dim\mathfrak{g}_{i}
,
a_{i}=k_{1}+\dots+k_{i}
, and
k=k_{1}
, which is called the
rank
of the Carnot group.

We identify elements of
\mathfrak{g}
with vectors of
T_{e}\mathbb{G}
. Since
\mathfrak{g}
is nilpotent and
\mathbb{G}
is simply connected, the group exponential map
\exp_{\mathbb{G}}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{G}
is a smooth diffeomorphism.

The stratification (
260
) allows the definition of a family of automorphisms

\delta_{\lambda}:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{G}
, for
\lambda>0
, called
dilations
such that

(261)

\delta_{\lambda}(\exp_{\mathbb{G}}(v))=\exp_{\mathbb{G}}(\lambda^{i}v),\qquad\forall\,v\in\mathfrak{g}_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,s.

Fix an
adapted basis
of
\mathfrak{g}
, namely a set of vectors
v_{1},\dots,v_{m}\in\mathfrak{g}
such that for all
i=1,\dots,s
the list of vectors
v_{k_{i-1}+1},\dots,v_{k_{i}}
is a basis of
\mathfrak{g}_{i}
, with the convention
k_{0}=0
.
For any
j=1,\dots,m
the
weight
of
v_{j}
is the unique

w_{j}\in\{1,\dots,s\}
such that
v_{j}\in\mathfrak{g}_{w_{j}}
. We define a diffeomorphism

\varphi_{\mathbb{G}}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{G}
, by

(262)

\varphi_{\mathbb{G}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}):=\exp_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}v_{i}\right).

The map
\varphi_{\mathbb{G}}
defines global coordinates on
\mathbb{G}
, called
exponential coordinates
(of first kind).
For any
x\in\mathbb{G}
we denote by

\tau_{x}:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{G}
the left translation on
\mathbb{G}
, that is
\tau_{x}(y)=x\cdot y
. We denote by
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
the left-invariant vector fields on
\mathbb{G}
corresponding to the adapted basis. They are given by

X_{j}(x)=D_{e}\tau_{x}(v_{j})

for any
x\in\mathbb{G}
,
where
D_{e}\tau_{x}
denotes the differential
of
\tau_{x}

at the unit element.

We recall the definition of (homogeneous) polynomials on Carnot groups. We say that
P:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{R}
is a polynomial if for some (and then any) set of exponential coordinates
\varphi_{\mathbb{G}}
, the map
P\circ\varphi_{\mathbb{G}}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is a polynomial. Any polynomial
P:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{R}
can be written as

(263)

P(\varphi_{\mathbb{G}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}))=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{m}}c_{\alpha}x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{m}^{\alpha_{m}},\qquad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

for some
c_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}
, which are non-zero only for a finite set of multi-indices. For
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
, we denote by

(264)

|\alpha|_{\mathbb{G}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\alpha_{i},

the weighted degree of the multi-index
\alpha
. The weighted degree of a polynomial
P
is

(265)

\deg_{\mathbb{G}}(P):=\max\left\{|\alpha|_{\mathbb{G}}\,\middle|\,c_{\alpha}\neq 0\right\}.

A polynomial
P
is homogeneous of weighted degree
w
if
P(\delta_{\lambda}x)=\lambda^{w}P(x)
for any
x\in\mathbb{G}
and
\lambda>0
.

Remark 49
.

In other words,
P
is homogeneous of weighted degree
w
if and only if

(266)

P(\varphi_{\mathbb{G}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{m}\\
|\alpha|_{\mathbb{G}}=w\end{subarray}}c_{\alpha}x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{m}^{\alpha_{m}}.

In particular any such polynomial depends only on the variables
x_{i}
with
w_{i}\leq w
, namely
x_{1},\dots,x_{k_{w}}
.

It is easy to see that the concepts we just introduced do not depend on the choice of exponential coordinates. For this reason, in the following, we fix such a choice, and we omit
\varphi_{\mathbb{G}}
from the notation, identifying
\mathbb{G}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Note that
e\in\mathbb{G}
is identified with
0\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

We recall from
[
12
, Prop. 5.18]
the following result on the structure of the vector fields
X_{j}
.

Proposition 50
.

In exponential coordinates
(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the vector fields
X_{j}
, for
j=1,\dots,k
have the following form:

(267)

X_{j}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=\partial_{j}+\sum_{h>k}Q_{jh}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k_{w_{h}-1}})\partial_{h},

where
Q_{jh}:\mathbb{G}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is a homogeneous polynomial of weighted degree

.

5.1.
Endpoint maps

In this section we introduce the Endpoint map associated to a Carnot group. We work with the separable Hilbert space

(268)

H:=L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k}),

, and where

\mathbb{G}

is determined by

(273)

\gamma_{u,i}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\int_{0}^{t}u_{j}(s)Q_{ji}(\gamma_{u,1}(s),\dots,\gamma_{u,k_{w_{i}-1}}(s))\,ds.

Recall that
Q_{ji}
is a homogeneous polynomial of weighted degree
w_{i}-1
and as already highlighted in the notation, depends only on the functions
\gamma_{u,\ell}(s)
with
w_{\ell}\leq w_{i}-1=\theta
. By the induction assumption, all the functions
E\ni u\mapsto\gamma_{u,\ell}(s)
are polynomials of degree
w_{\ell}\leq\theta
, depending continuously on
s
. By using the definition of weighted degree (see
Remark

49
), it follows that the function

(274)

E\ni u\mapsto Q_{ji}(\gamma_{u,1}(s),\dots,\gamma_{u,k_{w_{i}-1}}(s)),

is a polynomial of degree
w_{i}-1
, with coefficients depending continuously on
s\in I
. Then, the integrand in (
273
), namely the function

(275)

E\ni u\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{k}u_{j}(s)Q_{ji}(\gamma_{u,1}(s),\dots,\gamma_{u,k_{w_{i}-1}}(s)),

is a polynomial of degree
w_{i}=\theta+1
, with coefficients depending continuously on
s\in I
. We conclude easily using (
273
).
∎

Thanks to
Proposition

H
we can apply the results of
Section

4
to Endpoint maps of Carnot groups, obtaining
Corollary

I
in the Introduction.

5.2.
Sard property for real–analytic controls

We specialize the result in
Corollary

I
to some concrete set of controls
K
, estimating its
n
-width.

Fix a closed interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
and let

\mathcal{D}(r,I):=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid d(z,I)\leq r\}
.
The radius of convergence of a real–analytic function
u:I\to\mathbb{R}
is the largest

r\in(0,\infty]
such that
u
extends to a (unique) holomorphic function on
\mathcal{D}(r,I)
.
For fixed
r>0
we denote by
\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r)\subset L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
the set of real–analytic controls whose components have radius of convergence strictly greater than
r
:

(276)

\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r):=\bigg\{u\in L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})\,\bigg|\,\textrm{$u$ is real--analytic with radius of convergence
strictly greater than }r\bigg\},

which we endow with the supremum norm:

(277)

\|u\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r)}:=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{D}(r,I)}\|u(z)\|.

Similarly, we consider finite concatenations of real–analytic controls as above. More precisely, for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
divide the interval
I
in equispaced sub-intervals:

(278)

I=I_{1}\cup\dots\cup I_{\ell},\qquad I_{a}=\inf I+\left[\frac{(a-1)|I|}{\ell},\frac{a|I|}{\ell}\right],

and we let
\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell)\subset L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
be the set of piecewise analytic controls
u:I\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
that are real–analytic on each set
I_{1},\dots,I_{\ell}
, with radius of convergence strictly greater than
r
, namely

(279)

\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell)=\bigg\{u\in L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})\,\bigg|\,u|_{I_{a}}\in\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I_{a},\mathbb{R}^{k};r)\subset L^{2}(I_{a},\mathbb{R}^{k}),\,\text{ for all $a=1,\dots,\ell$}\bigg\},

endowed with the norm

(280)

\|u\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell)}=\max_{a=1,\dots,\ell}\|u\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I_{a},\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell)}.

Proposition 53
.

Fix
I=[0,1]
,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
r>1
. Let
Y=\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell)
. Then the set
B_{Y}:=\{u\in V\mid\|u\|_{Y}\leq 1\}
is precompact in
L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
, and for its
n
-width it holds:

(281)

\Omega_{n}(B_{Y},L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k}))\leq\frac{(k\ell)^{1/2}}{\ln r}\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{k\ell}\right\rfloor}.

Proof.

We start by proving the case
\ell=1
. Fix
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})\in B_{Y}
. Since
r>1
, any
u_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,k
has an expansion in power series on the complex unit ball centered at the origin, denoted by
B_{\mathbb{C}}(1)
. In particular, we have for any
t\in I

(282)

u_{j}(t)=\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{(h)}_{j}(0)}{h!}t^{h}.

For any
N\geq 1
we consider the truncated sum, given for any
t\in I
as

(283)

u_{j,N}(t)=\sum_{h=0}^{N-1}\frac{u^{(h)}_{j}(0)}{h!}t^{h}.

By the Cauchy integral formula, and since
r>1
, we have

(284)

u_{j}^{(h)}(0)=\frac{h!}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial B_{\mathbb{C}}(r)}\frac{u_{j}(z)}{z^{h+1}}\,dz,

where
B_{\mathbb{C}}(r)
is the complex unit ball of radius one centred at zero. Therefore,

(285)

|u_{j}^{(h)}(0)|\leq\frac{h!}{r^{h}}.

Hence we have

(286)

\|u_{j}-u_{j,N}\|_{L^{2}(I)}\leq\left\lVert u_{j}-u_{j,N}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(I)}\leq\sum_{h=N}^{\infty}\frac{|u_{j}^{(h)}(0)|}{h!}\leq\sum_{h=N}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{h}}\leq\int_{N}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{x}}\,dx=\frac{r^{-N}}{\ln r}.

It follows that for all
u\in B_{Y}
it holds

(287)

\|u-u_{N}\|_{L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})}\leq\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|u_{j}-u_{j,N}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(I)}\right)^{1/2}\leq\frac{k^{1/2}r^{-N}}{\ln r}.

Thanks to this result we can find an approximating subspace. Consider the standard basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{k}
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
we
denote by
f_{h}\in L^{2}(I)
the function
f_{h}(t)=t^{h}
. We have

(288)

u_{N}=\sum_{h=0}^{N-1}\frac{u^{(h)}(0)}{h!}f_{h}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{h=0}^{N-1}\frac{u^{(h)}_{j}(0)}{h!}f_{h}\otimes e_{j}.

In particular,

u_{N}\in V_{N}:=\mathrm{span}\{f_{h}\otimes e_{j}\mid j=1,\dots,k,\;h=0,\dots,N-1\}
and
\dim(V_{N})=kN
.
Hence from (
287
) it follows that for any
N\in\mathbb{N}

(289)

\Omega_{kN}(B_{Y},L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k}))\leq\frac{k^{1/2}}{\ln r}\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)^{N}.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
we consider
N=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{k}\right\rfloor
.
By
Definition

30
, the
n
-width is non-increasing as a function of
n\in\mathbb{N}
, hence by (
289
) we have

(290)

\Omega_{n}(B_{Y},L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k}))\leq\Omega_{kN}(B_{Y},L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k}))\leq\frac{k^{1/2}}{\ln r}\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{k}\right\rfloor}.

This proves the estimate on the
n
-width for
\ell=1
.

We sketch the argument for general
\ell
. In this case, for any
u\in\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell)
we can write

(291)

u=\sum_{a=1}^{\ell}u|_{I_{a}}\mathbbm{1}_{I_{a}},

where
I_{a}
are the intervals of the decomposition (
278
), and
\mathbbm{1}_{I_{a}}
are the corresponding characteristic functions. By definition, each
u|_{I_{a}}
is the restriction to
I_{a}
of a real–analytic function with radius of convergence
r_{a}\geq r>1
. We can expand each
u|_{I_{a}}
in Taylor series centered at the lower bound of
I_{a}
. Then, in order to obtain the finite-dimensional approximation, we truncate the series as in the previous case, repeating analogous estimates for the remainder (taking into account the length of the intervals
|I_{a}|=1/\ell
). This concludes the estimate on the
n
-width for general
\ell
.

To prove the compactness assertion, observe that there is a linear immersion (given by the inclusion)
\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
and it holds
\|u\|_{L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})}\leq\|u\|_{C^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell)}
for all
u\in C^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell)
. In particular
B_{Y}
is bounded in
L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
and since its
n
-width tends to zero as
n\to\infty
it is also precompact by
Proposition

31
.
∎

We can now prove
Theorem

J
in the Introduction, of which we recall the statement.

Theorem 54
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a Carnot group of topological dimension
m
, step
s
, and rank
k
. Let
I=[0,1]
. For
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
r=r(m,s,k,\ell)>1
such that

(292)

\mu\big(\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{Crit}(\mathcal{E})\cap\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell))\big)=\mu\big(\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{Crit}(\mathcal{E}|_{\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell)})\big)=0.

Namely, the Sard property holds on the space of piecewise real–analytic controls with radius of convergence
>r
and with
\ell
pieces.
Furthermore it holds

(293)

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\big(\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{Crit}(\mathcal{E})\cap\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};\infty,\ell))\big)\leq\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\big(\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{Crit}(\mathcal{E}|_{\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};\infty,\ell)})\big)\leq m-1.

Namely, the strong Sard property holds on the space of piecewise entire controls with
\ell
pieces.

Proof.

Observe that
\mathcal{E}\in\mathscr{P}^{m}_{s}(L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k}))
by
Proposition

H
. Let
Y:=\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};r,\ell)
. Let
B_{Y}
be the unit ball of
Y
with respect to the norm (
280
), namely
B_{Y}=\{u\in Y\mid\|u\|_{Y}\leq 1\}
. By
Propositions

53
and
31
, for any
r>1
we have that
K:=\overline{B_{Y}}
is compact (we stress that the closure is taken in the
L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
topology, and not w.r.t. the norm
\|\cdot\|_{Y}
), and it holds

(294)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}(K,L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k}))^{1/n}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}(B_{Y},L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k}))^{1/n}\leq r^{-\tfrac{1}{k\ell}},

where we used
Proposition

32
for the first equality.
We can apply
Item

(i)
of
Corollary

I
: for
r>r(m,s,k,\ell):=\beta_{0}(s,m)^{k\ell}
it holds

(295)

\mu\big(\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{Crit}(\mathcal{E})\cap\mathrm{span}(K))\big)=\mu\big(\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{Crit}(\mathcal{E}|_{\mathrm{span}(K)})\big)=0.

By
Lemma

41
, the above equalities remain valid replacing
X:=\mathrm{span}(K)
with any linear subspace of the latter, dense in it w.r.t. the
L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
topology. In particular, this is the case for
Y
, observing that
Y=\mathrm{span}(B_{Y})
and thus
\bar{Y}=\overline{\mathrm{span}(B_{Y})}\supseteq\mathrm{span}(\overline{B_{Y}})=\mathrm{span}(K)
.

The same argument via
Item

(ii)
of

yields the statement for the space
\mathcal{C}^{\omega}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k};\infty,\ell)
.
∎

5.3.
Surjectivity of the Endpoint map on finite-dimensional spaces of controls

The Endpoint maps of sub-Riemannian manifolds are surjective when restricted to piecewise constant controls: this follows from the proof of the Rashevskii-Chow theorem in
[
2
]
.
In this section, we prove that in Carnot groups the Endpoint maps are surjective also when they are restricted to the space of controls
which consists in the set of polynomial maps of some large enough fixed degree (which depends on the Carnot group). The proof is obtained by using a quantitative version of the inverse function theorem, as it can be found in
[
23
]
. For completeness we present and prove in our setting the statements we need.

Given
M\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{m})
we denote by
\sigma(M)
the smallest singular value of
M
, namely

(296)

\sigma(M):=\inf_{\left\lVert v\right\rVert=1}\left\lVert Mv\right\rVert.

From the definition it follows that for all
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{m})
it holds

(297)

|\sigma(M_{1})-\sigma(M_{2})|\leq\left\lVert M_{1}-M_{2}\right\rVert_{\op}.

From
[
23
, Lemma 3]
we obtain the following technical lemma.

Lemma 55
.

Let
M_{0}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{m})
be invertible. Then, for every
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
\|v\|=1
there exists
w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
\|w\|=1
such that

(298)

\langle w,Mv\rangle\geq\frac{\sigma(M_{0})}{2},

for all
M
such that
\|M-M_{0}\|_{\op}\leq\tfrac{\sigma(M_{0})}{2}
.

Proof.

Given
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
\|v\|=1
, the set
C_{v}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}

(299)

C_{v}:=\left\{Mv\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\,\middle|\,\|M-M_{0}\|_{\op}\leq\frac{\sigma(M_{0})}{2}\right\},

is convex. We now prove that its distance from
0
is at least
\frac{\sigma(M_{0})}{2}
.
Indeed, by (
297
), for any
M
with
\|M-M_{0}\|_{\op}\leq\tfrac{\sigma(M_{0})}{2}
we obtain

\sigma(M)>\frac{\sigma(M_{0})}{2}
. Therefore,

(300)

\|Mv\|\geq\inf_{\|v\|=1}\|Mv\|=\sigma(M)>\frac{\sigma(M_{0})}{2}.

Hence, we have proved that
C_{v}
is separated from the ball
B_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left(\frac{\sigma(M_{0})}{2}\right)
.
We conclude the proof thanks to
the usual separation theorem for convex sets. Indeed, it directly provides

w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
\|w\|=1
such that for all
M
with
\|M-M_{0}\|_{\op}\leq\tfrac{\sigma(M_{0})}{2}
we have

(301)

\langle w,Mv\rangle\geq\frac{\sigma(M_{0})}{2},

concluding the proof.
∎

From
[
23
, Lemma 4]
we obtain the following “quantitative injectivity” lemma.

Lemma 56
.

Let
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be an open set, and
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(W,\mathbb{R}^{m})
. Assume
x_{0}\in W
is such that
D_{x_{0}}f
is invertible. Set
\sigma_{f,x_{0}}:=\sigma(D_{x_{0}}f)>0
.
There exists
r_{f,x_{0}}>0
such that for any
g\in C^{1}(W,\mathbb{R}^{m})
with

(302)

\sup_{z\in W}\left\lVert D_{z}f-D_{z}g\right\rVert_{\op}<\frac{\sigma_{f,x_{0}}}{4},

and all
x,y\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(x_{0},r_{f,x_{0}})
it holds

(303)

\|g(x)-g(y)\|\geq\frac{\sigma_{f,x_{0}}}{2}\|x-y\|.

Proof.

Since
f
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
there exists
r=r_{f,x_{0}}>0
such that
B_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(x_{0},r)\subset W
and

(304)

\sup_{z\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(x_{0},r)}\left\lVert D_{z}f-D_{x_{0}}f\right\rVert_{\op}<\frac{\sigma_{f,x_{0}}}{4}.

Let
g\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(W,\mathbb{R}^{m})
as in the statement. By (
302
) we get

(305)

\left\lVert D_{z}g-D_{x_{0}}f\right\rVert_{\op}<\frac{\sigma_{f,x_{0}}}{2},

for all
z\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(x_{0},r)
.
Now we fix
x\neq y
in
B_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(x_{0},r)
, and we have

(306)

g(x)-g(y)=\|x-y\|\int_{0}^{1}D_{(1-t)y+tx}g\cdot\frac{x-y}{\|x-y\|}\,dt.

We define
v_{x,y}:=\frac{x-y}{\|x-y\|}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and we apply
Lemma

55
to
v=v_{x,y}
and

M_{0}=D_{x_{0}}f
, which is invertible by hypothesis. We denote by
w_{x,y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
the provided unit vector, such that

(307)

\left\langle w_{x,y},D_{z}g\cdot\frac{x-y}{\|x-y\|}\right\rangle\geq\frac{\sigma_{f,x_{0}}}{2},

for all
z\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(x_{0},r)
. We conclude the proof by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, which gives

(308)

\|g(x)-g(y)\|\geq\|x-y\|\left\langle w_{x,y},\int_{0}^{1}D_{(1-t)y+tx}g\cdot\frac{x-y}{\|x-y\|}\,dt\right\rangle\geq\frac{\sigma_{f,x_{0}}}{2}\|x-y\|,

concluding the proof.
∎

We conclude with the following inclusion, analogous to
[
23
, Lemma 5]
, providing the “quantitative surjectivity” counterpart of the previous statement. We report the proof for completeness.

Lemma 57
.

In the same setting of
Lemma

56
, the following inclusion holds

(309)

g(B_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(x_{0},r_{f,x_{0}}))\supset B_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left(g(x_{0}),\frac{r_{f,x_{0}}\sigma_{f,x_{0}}}{8}\right).

Proof.

Set
r=r_{f,x_{0}}
and
\sigma=\sigma_{f,x_{0}}
. Let
y\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left(g(x_{0}),\frac{r\sigma}{8}\right)
. Let
x
be a minimum of
\|y-g(\cdot)\|^{2}
on
B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(x_{0},r)
. We claim that
x\in\mathrm{int}B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(x_{0},r)
. Otherwise, using
Lemma

56
, we have

(310)

\displaystyle\frac{r\sigma}{8}

\displaystyle\geq\|y-g(x_{0})\|

(311)

\displaystyle\geq\|g(x)-g(x_{0})\|-\|y-g(x)\|

(312)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sigma}{2}\|x-x_{0}\|-\|y-g(x)\|

(313)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sigma r}{2}-\|y-g(x_{0})\|

(314)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sigma r}{2}-\frac{\sigma r}{8}=\frac{3r\sigma}{8},

which is a contradiction. Thus
x
yields a local minimum for the function
\|y-g(\cdot)\|^{2}
, and consequently

\nabla_{x}\|y-g(\cdot)\|^{2}=-2D_{x}g\cdot(y-g(x))=0
. Note that by (
305
) and the fact that the singular values are
1
-Lipschitz (see
Lemma

18
) it follows that
\sigma(D_{x}g)>\tfrac{\sigma}{2}
, hence
D_{x}g
is invertible. Hence
y=g(x)
.
∎

We can now state the surjectivity property of the Endpoint maps of Carnot groups.

Proposition 58
.

For every Carnot group
\mathbb{G}
there exists
d_{\mathbb{G}}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(315)

\mathcal{E}\left(\{u\in L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})\mid u\text{ is a polynomial map with components of degrees}\leq d_{\mathbb{G}}\}\right)=\mathbb{G}.

Proof.

The classical proof of the Rashevskii-Chow theorem provides
u_{0}\in L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
such that
D_{u_{0}}\mathcal{E}
is surjective and
\mathcal{E}(u_{0})=0
(see e.g.
[
2
, Sec. 3.2]
).
In particular there exist

w_{1},\dots,w_{m}\in\ker(D_{u_{0}}\mathcal{E})^{\perp}

such that the vectors

D_{u_{0}}\mathcal{E}(w_{1}),\dots,D_{u_{0}}\mathcal{E}(w_{m})
are linearly independent.
Now we consider the map
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{G}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined as

(316)

f(s_{1},\dots,s_{m})=\mathcal{E}\left(u_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}s_{i}w_{i}\right).

The differential
D_{0}f
is invertible by construction, in particular
f
covers a neighbourhood of
f(0)=0
. The idea of the proof is to construct a perturbation
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{G}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{m}
of the form

(317)

g(s_{1},\dots,s_{m})=\mathcal{E}\left(q_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}s_{i}p_{i}\right),

where
q_{0},p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\in L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
are suitably chosen polynomial maps, in such a way that the image of
g
stills contains a neighbourhood of
0
. This will be done by applying
Lemma

57
, as we now explain.

Given

\varepsilon_{1},\eta_{1}>0

by the density of polynomial maps in
L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
and by the continuity of
\mathcal{E}
and
D\mathcal{E}
,
there exists a polynomial map

q_{0}\in L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})
such that

(318)

\left\lVert\mathcal{E}(q_{0})\right\rVert<\varepsilon_{1}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\left\lVert D_{u_{0}}\mathcal{E}-D_{q_{0}}\mathcal{E}\right\rVert_{\op}<\eta_{1}.

Now we prove that we can find polynomial maps

p_{1},\dots,p_{m}

and a neighbourhood
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
of
0
such that, setting
\sigma_{f,0}=\sigma(D_{x_{0}}f)
, it holds

(319)

\sup_{W}\left\lVert D_{s}f-D_{s}g\right\rVert_{\op}\leq\frac{\sigma_{f,0}}{4}.

By the density of polynomial maps, given
\eta_{2}>0

we consider
p_{1},\dots,p_{m}

such that for any
i=1,\dots,m

(320)

\left\lVert w_{i}-p_{i}\right\rVert<\eta_{2}.

We get the following estimate

(321)

\displaystyle\left\lVert D_{0}f-D_{0}g\right\rVert_{\op}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|D_{u_{0}}\mathcal{E}(w_{i})-D_{q_{0}}\mathcal{E}(p_{i})\|

(322)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|D_{u_{0}}\mathcal{E}(w_{i})-D_{q_{0}}\mathcal{E}(w_{i})\|+\|D_{q_{0}}\mathcal{E}(w_{i})-D_{q_{0}}\mathcal{E}(p_{i})\|

(323)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert D_{u_{0}}\mathcal{E}-D_{q_{0}}\mathcal{E}\right\rVert_{\op}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lVert w_{i}\right\rVert+\left\lVert D_{q_{0}}\mathcal{E}\right\rVert_{\op}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lVert w_{i}-p_{i}\right\rVert

(324)

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lVert w_{i}\right\rVert+\left\lVert D_{q_{0}}\mathcal{E}\right\rVert_{\op}m\eta_{2}.

Given
\eta_{3}>0
, by the continuity of
Df
and
Dg
there exists a neighbourhood
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
of
0
such that

(325)

\sup_{s\in W}\left\lVert D_{s}f-D_{0}f\right\rVert_{\op}+\left\lVert D_{s}g-D_{0}g\right\rVert_{\op}<\eta_{3}.

Hence, by the triangle inequality we get the following estimate

(326)

\sup_{s\in W}\left\lVert D_{s}f-D_{s}g\right\rVert_{\op}\leq\eta_{3}+\eta_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lVert w_{i}\right\rVert+\left\lVert D_{q_{0}}\mathcal{E}\right\rVert_{\op}m\eta_{2}.

To obtain (
319
) it is enough to choose
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\eta_{3}
small enough.
We have thus found
g
in such a way
\|f(0)-g(0)\|<\varepsilon_{1}
and
g
satisfies the hypothesis of
Lemma

57
. By the latter, we find
r_{f,0}>0
such that

(327)

g(B_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(r_{f,0}))\supset B_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left(g(0),\frac{r_{f,0}\sigma_{f,0}}{8}\right).

Since
\|g(0)\|<\varepsilon_{1}
by construction, taking
\varepsilon_{1}=\frac{r_{f,0}\sigma_{f,0}}{16}

(328)

g(B_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(r_{f,0}))\supset B_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left(\frac{r_{f,0}\sigma_{f,0}}{16}\right).

Let then
d_{\mathbb{G}}
be the maximum degree of the polynomial maps

q_{0},\dots,p_{m}
.
We have proved that

(329)

B_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left(\frac{r_{f,0}\sigma_{f,0}}{16}\right)\subset\mathcal{E}\left(\{u\in L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k})\mid u\text{ is a polynomial map of degree at most }d_{\mathbb{G}}\}\right).

In other words, the Endpoint map is surjective on a small ball when restricted to polynomial controls of degree
\leq d_{\mathbb{G}}
. Now recall that, for Carnot groups, dilations have the following property:

(330)

\delta_{\lambda}(\mathcal{E}(u))=\mathcal{E}(\lambda u),\qquad\forall\,\lambda>0,\quad u\in L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{k}).

From (
329
) and (
330
) it follows that
\mathcal{E}
, when restricted to polynomial controls of degree
\leq d_{\mathbb{G}}
is surjective on the whole
\mathbb{G}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
