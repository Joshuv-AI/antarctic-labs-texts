Introduction

A proper (vertex)
q
-coloring of a graph
G=(V,E)
is an assignment of each vertex to one of
q
colors so that the colors of adjacent vertices are distinct. Let
\Delta
be the maximum degree of
G
. It has been widely conjectured that the single-site Glauber dynamics for uniformly sampling proper colorings on
G
mixes rapidly as long as the number of colors
q
is at least
\Delta+2
. Since the seminal work of Jerrum
[
Jer95
]
and Salas and Sokal
[
SS97
]
where Glauber dynamics was shown to be rapidly mixing when
q\geq 2\Delta
, a number of works devoted to resolving the conjecture and the current best bound requires
q\geq\left(\frac{11}{6}-\epsilon_{0}\right)\Delta
for some
\epsilon_{0}\approx 10^{-5}

[
CDM+19
]
, which is still far from desired.

Another line of work to approach the conjecture is by considering special graph families, with the most successful cases being those graphs with large girths (e.g.,
[
DF03
,
Mol04
,
DFHV13
,
CGŠV21
,
FGYZ20
]
). Notably in a very recent work of Chen, Liu, Mani and Moitra
[
CLMM23
]
, it was proven that for any
\Delta\geq 3
, there exists a
\mathrm{g}>0
such that for any graph
G
with the maximum degree at most
\Delta
and the girth at least
\mathrm{g}
, the single-site Glauber dynamics for sampling proper
q
-colorings on
G
mixes rapidly as long as
q\geq\Delta+3
. This result almost solves the aforementioned conjecture regarding the case of large girth graphs.

A closely related problem is
sampling proper edge colorings
, where one assigns colors to the edges instead of vertices so that adjacent edges have distinct colors. It is clear that a proper edge coloring of
G
naturally corresponds to a vertex coloring of the line graph of
G
in which the vertices are the edges of
G
and two vertices are adjacent in the line graph if and only if the corresponding edges are incident in
G
. As a result, studying sampling proper colorings on line graphs is of particular interest. Unlike graphs with a large girth which are “locally sparse”, line graphs are “locally dense”. Specifically, a line graph is formed by gluing together several cliques with each vertex belonging to exactly two cliques. Previous methods such as coupling can hardly take advantage of this special structure and therefore the rapid mixing condition for line graphs remains the same as that for general graphs for a long time.

In a recent breakthrough, Abdolazimi, Liu and Oveis Gharan
[
ALG21
]
developed a new technique to establish rapid mixing results for Glauber dynamics, namely the matrix trickle-down theorem, which can well-utilize the structural information of the underlying graphs. As a result, it was shown in
[
ALG21
]
the Glauber dynamics for sampling colorings on line graphs mixes rapidly as long as
q>\frac{10}{6}\Delta
. The technique, incorporating with recent revolutionary development of the “local-to-global” scheme for high-dimensional expanders
[
AL20
,
CLV21
]
, yields optimal mixing time for certain Markov chains. Specifically, the matrix trickle-down theorem is a generalization of the trickle-down theorem in
[
Opp18
]
which established connections between the spectral gaps of the local walks on links of the underlying simplicial complexes. Instead of solely looking at the spectral gap, the matrix trickle-down theorem takes into account the local walks themselves, and establishes more general connections between (the transition matrix of) local walks on links.

To apply the matrix trickle-down, one needs to design appropriate matrix upper bounds for local walks while keeping their spectrum bounded. Moreover, these matrices must satisfy certain inductive inequality constraints. The construction of these matrix upper bounds is the main technical challenge since one has to have precise control over the magnitudes of their eigenvalues. In this work, we systematically study the constraints arising in the matrix trickle-down theorem and provide an almost optimal construction of matrix upper bounds for sampling proper colorings on line graphs. Our construction has several advantages compared to the one in
[
ALG21
]
:

•

Our construction of the matrix upper bounds for each local walk is explicit.

•

We directly relate the spectrum of each matrix upper bound to that of the adjacent matrix of the line graph, which is a clique locally and is therefore well-understood. Therefore, we can obtain desired spectrum bound in an improved regime.

•

We reduce the existence of matrix upper bounds to the feasibility of a system of inequalities, for which we give a complete characterization.

As a result, we obtain an

2.1
Simplicial Complexes

Let
U
be a universe. A
simplicial complex

\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{U}
is a collection of subsets of
U
that is closed under taking subsets. That is, if
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}
and
\sigma^{\prime}\subseteq\sigma
, then
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
. Every
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}
is called a
face
, and a face that is not a proper subset of any other face is called a
maximal face
or a
facet
. The dimension of a face
\sigma
is
\mathtt{dim}(\sigma):=\absolutevalue{\sigma}
, namely the number of elements in
\sigma
. For every
k\geq 0
, we use
\mathcal{C}_{k}:=\left\{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}\,\colon\,\absolutevalue{\sigma}=k\right\}
to denote the set of faces of dimension
k
. Specifically,
\mathcal{C}_{0}=\left\{\varnothing\right\}
. The dimension of
\mathcal{C}
is the maximum dimension of faces in
\mathcal{C}
. We say
\mathcal{C}
is a
pure

n
-dimensional simplicial complex if all maximal faces in
\mathcal{C}
are of dimension
n
. In the following, we assume
\mathcal{C}
is a pure
n
-dimensional simplicial complex. For every face
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}
, we define its co-dimension
\mathtt{codim}(\sigma):=n-\mathtt{dim}(\sigma)
.

Let
\pi_{n}
be a distribution over the maximal faces
\mathcal{C}_{n}
. We use the pair
(\mathcal{C},\pi_{n})
to denote a
weighted simplicial complex
where for each
1\leq k<n
, the distribution
\pi_{n}
induces a distribution
\pi_{k}
over
\mathcal{C}_{k}
. Formally, for every
1\leq k<n
and every
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}
,

\displaystyle\pi_{k}(\sigma^{\prime}):=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}_{n}\,\colon\,\sigma^{\prime}\subset\sigma}\pi_{n}(\sigma).

One can easily verify that
\pi_{k}
is a distribution on
\mathcal{C}_{k}
. Combined with
\pi_{n}
itself, for each
1\leq k\leq n
, the distribution over
\mathcal{C}_{k}
is defined. Sometimes, we omit the subscript when
j=1
, i.e., we write
\pi
for
\pi_{1}
.

For a face
\tau\in\mathcal{C}
of dimension
k
, we define its
link
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\tau}=\left\{\sigma\setminus\tau\,\colon\,\sigma\in\mathcal{C}\land\tau\subseteq\sigma\right\}.

Clearly,
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
is a pure
(n-k)
-dimensional simplicial complex. Similarly, for every
1\leq j\leq n-k
, we use
\mathcal{C}_{\tau,j}
to denote the set of faces in
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
of dimension
j
. We also use
\pi_{\tau,j}
to denote the
marginal distribution
on
\mathcal{C}_{\tau,j}
. Formally, for every
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau,j}
,

\displaystyle\pi_{\tau,j}(\sigma):=\mathbf{Pr}_{\alpha\sim\pi_{k+j}}\left[\alpha=\tau\cup\sigma\,\colon\,\tau\subseteq\alpha\right]=\frac{\pi_{k+j}(\tau\cup\sigma)}{\binom{k+j}{k}\cdot\pi_{k}(\tau)}.

We drop the subscript when
j=1
, i.e., we write
\pi_{\tau}
for
\pi_{\tau,1}
.
Note that the marginal distributions
\pi_{\tau,j}
are the same as the distributions over
C_{\tau,j}
induced from the weighted simplicial complex
\left(\mathcal{C}_{\tau},\pi_{\tau,m-k}\right)
, so there is no ambiguity about this notation.

Let
(\mathcal{C}^{(1)},\pi^{(1)})
,
(\mathcal{C}^{(2)},\pi^{(2)})
be two pure weighted simplicial complexes of dimension
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
respectively. We can define another pure weighted simplicial complex
(\mathcal{C},\pi)
of dimension
d_{1}+d_{2}
whose maximal faces are the disjoint union of maximal faces of
\mathcal{C}^{(1)}
and
\mathcal{C}^{(2)}
. Moreover,
\pi
is the product measure
\pi^{(1)}\times\pi^{(2)}
.
(\mathcal{C},\pi)
is also called the
product
of
(\mathcal{C}^{(1)},\pi^{(1)})
and
(\mathcal{C}^{(2)},\pi^{(2)})
. This definition can be naturally generalized to the products of more than two weighted simplicial complexes.

Then we define notations for matrices related to
\pi_{\tau}
. Define
\Pi_{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\mathcal{C}_{1}}
as
\Pi_{\tau}\triangleq\operatorname{diag}(\pi_{\tau})
supported on
\mathcal{C}_{\tau,1}\times\mathcal{C}_{\tau,1}
. For convenience, define the pseudo inverse
\Pi_{\tau}^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\mathcal{C}_{1}}
of
\Pi_{\tau}
as
\Pi_{\tau}^{-1}(x,x)=\pi_{\tau}(x)^{-1}
for
x\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau,1}
and
0
otherwise. Similarly, the pseudo inverse square root
\Pi_{\tau}^{-1/2}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\mathcal{C}_{1}}
is defined as
\Pi_{\tau}^{-1/2}(x,x)=\pi_{\tau}(x)^{-1/2}
for
x\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau,1}
and
0
otherwise.

2.2
Vertex Coloring

Fix a color set
[q]=\left\{1,2,\dots,q\right\}
where
q\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph and
\mathcal{L}=\left\{L_{v}\subseteq[q]\,\colon\,v\in V\right\}
be a collection of color lists associated with each vertex in
V
. For every
v\in V
, we use
\ell_{v}:=\absolutevalue{L_v}
to denote the size of
L_{v}
. The pair
(G,\mathcal{L})
is an instance of list-coloring. If there exists an integer
\beta
such that
|L_{v}|>\Delta(v)+\beta
for any
v\in V
where
\Delta(v)
is the degree of
v
, we call
(G,\mathcal{L})
a
\beta
-extra list-coloring instance.

We say
\sigma:V\rightarrow[q]
is a proper coloring if
\sigma(v)\in L_{v}
for any
v\in V
and
\sigma(u)\neq\sigma(v)
for any
\left\{u,v\right\}\in E
. We also regard
\sigma
as a set of pairs of vertex and color, namely
\left\{(v,c)\in V\times[q]\,\colon\,\sigma(v)=c\right\}
. Let
\Omega
denote the set of all proper colorings and
\mu
be the uniform distribution on
\Omega
. Let
\Lambda\subseteq V
and
\tau\in[q]^{\Lambda}
. We say
\tau
is a proper partial coloring on
\Lambda
if it is a proper coloring on
(G[\Lambda],\mathcal{L}|_{\Lambda})
where
G[\Lambda]
is the subgraph of
G
induced by
\Lambda
and
\mathcal{L}|_{\Lambda}=\left\{L_{v}\in\mathcal{L}\,\colon\,v\in\Lambda\right\}
. We also define
\mu^{\tau}
on
\Omega
as
\mu^{\tau}(\cdot)=\mathbf{Pr}_{\sigma\sim\mu}\left[\cdot\subset\sigma\;\middle|\;\tau\subset\sigma\right]
.
For a subset
S\subseteq V\setminus\Lambda
and a partial coloring
\omega
on
S
, define
\mu^{\tau}_{S}(\omega)=\mathbf{Pr}_{\sigma\sim\mu}\left[\omega\subset\sigma\;\middle|\;\tau\subset\sigma\right]
.

Assume
\absolutevalue{V}=n
. The list-coloring instance
(G,\mathcal{L})
can be naturally represented as a weighted simplicial complex
(\mathcal{C},\pi_{n})
where
\mathcal{C}
consists of all proper partial colorings and
\pi_{n}=\mu
.

The following identity is useful throughout the paper:

Proposition 3
.

Let
\Lambda\subseteq V
. For every partial coloring
\tau
on
\Lambda
, every
S\subseteq V\setminus\Lambda
and partial coloring
\omega\in[q]^{S}
, it holds that

\mu^{\tau}_{S}(\omega)=\binom{\mathtt{codim}(\tau)}{|S|}\cdot\pi_{\tau,|S|}(\omega).

2.3
Markov Chains and Mixing Time

Let
\Omega
be a finite discrete state space. Let
P
be the
transition matrix
of a Markov chain with the stationary distribution
\pi
. We say
P
is
reversible
with respect to the stationary distribution
\pi
if it satisfies the detailed balance condition, i.e., for every
x,y\in\Omega
, it holds that

\displaystyle\pi(x)P(x,y)=\pi(y)P(y,x).

Only reversible chains are considered in this paper.

For a weighted simplicial complex
\left(\mathcal{C},\pi_{n}\right)
, the
single-site Glauber dynamics
on
\left(\mathcal{C},\pi_{n}\right)
is a Markov chain on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
with the transition matrix

P_{\mathtt{GL}}\left(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\right):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in\sigma}\pi_{\sigma\setminus\left\{x\right\},1}\left(\left\{x\right\}\right)&\text{if }\sigma=\sigma^{\prime},\\
\frac{1}{n}\pi_{\sigma\cap\sigma^{\prime},1}\left(\sigma^{\prime}\setminus\sigma\right)&\text{if }\sigma\cap\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{n-1},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

From an operational view, each transition of the Glauber dynamics, with the current state being
\sigma
, consists of two steps:

1.

Uniformly select a random
x\in\sigma
.

2.

Select a
y\in\mathcal{C}_{\sigma\setminus\left\{x\right\},1}
following the distribution
\pi_{\sigma\setminus\left\{x\right\},1}
and transfer to the state
\sigma\setminus\left\{x\right\}\cup y
.

One can easily verify that the Glauber dynamics on
\left(\mathcal{C},\pi_{n}\right)
is reversible, with
\pi_{n}
as the stationary distribution.

We are concerned with the convergence rate of Markov chains, which is described by the mixing time.
The mixing time is defined as the duration required
for the total variation distance between
\mu_{t}:=(P^{t})^{\top}\mu_{0}

and the stationary distribution
\pi
to become smaller than
\varepsilon
, starting from any initial distribution
\mu_{0}
. Formally,

\displaystyle t_{\mathtt{mix}}(\varepsilon):=\min_{t>0}\max_{\mu_{0}}D_{\mathtt{TV}}(\mu_{t},\pi)\leq\varepsilon,

where
D_{\mathtt{TV}}(\mu,\nu):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\absolutevalue{\mu(x)-\nu(x)}
is the total variation distance.

For a reversible Markov chain
P
on a discrete space
\Omega
, since
P
is self-adjoint with respect to the inner product induced by
\pi
, all eigenvalues of
P
are real.
So we can define the
spectral gap
of
P
as
\lambda(P):=1-\lambda_{2}(P)
, where
\lambda_{2}(P)
denotes the second largest eigenvalue of
P
.
And the
absolute spectral gap
of
P
as
\lambda_{\star}:=1-\max\left\{|\lambda|\,\colon\,\lambda\text{ is an eigenvalue of }P\text{ and }\lambda\neq 1\right\}
.
The following lemma arises to bound the mixing time of a reversible Markov chain by its absolute spectral gap.

Lemma 4

(Theorem 12.4 in
[
LPW17
]
)
.

For an irreducible reversible Markov chain
P
on a discrete space,

\displaystyle t_{\mathtt{mix}}(P,\varepsilon)\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\star}(P)}\left(\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{\pi_{\min}}+\log\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\right),

(1)

where
\pi_{\min}=\min_{x\in\Omega}\pi(x)
.

Notice that when all eigenvalues of
P
are non-negative, the absolute spectral gap in the above lemma equals the spectral gap. This is the case of Glauber dynamics, as in
Proposition

5
.

2.4
Local-to-Global Scheme

The
local random walk

P_{\tau}
on
\mathcal{C}_{\tau,1}
is defined as

\displaystyle P_{\tau}(x,y)=\frac{\pi_{\tau,2}(\{x,y\})}{2\pi_{\tau,1}(x)},

for all
x,y\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau,1}
. An operational view of the local chain is as follows: when the current state is at
x\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau,1}
, move to
y\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau,1}
with probability proportional to
\pi_{\tau,2}(\left\{x,y\right\})
. Note that we will treat
P_{\tau}
as a matrix in
[0,1]^{\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\mathcal{C}_{1}}
such that the undefined entries are
0
. It is obvious that
P_{\tau}
is reversible with respect to
\pi_{\tau}
. Specifically, we denote the local random walk on
\mathcal{C}_{1}
by
P
, i.e.,

\displaystyle P(x,y)=\frac{\pi_{2}(\{x,y\})}{2\pi_{1}(x)}.

P
is also reversible with respect to
\pi
.
We say a weighted simplicial complex
(\mathcal{C},\pi_{n})
is
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n-2})
-
local spectral expander
if for any
0\leq k\leq n-2
and
\tau\in\mathcal{C}_{k}
,
\lambda_{2}(P_{\tau})\leq\gamma_{k}
.

We focus on the spectral gaps of local walks in this paper. As studied earlier by
[
AL20
]
, the local spectral expansion implies bounds for Glauber dynamics.

Proposition 5

(
[
AL20
]
)
.

Let
(\mathcal{C},\pi_{n})
be a weighted simplicial complex where
\pi_{n}
is a uniform distribution over proper list-colorings over a graph
G=(V,E)
with
|V|=n
and maximum degree
\Delta
. If
\left(\mathcal{C},\pi_{n}\right)
is a
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n-2})
-local spectral expander, then

1.

all eigenvalues of the Glauber dynamics are real and non-negative;

2.

the second largest eigenvalue of the Glauber dynamics is at most
1-\frac{1}{n}\prod_{i=0}^{n-2}(1-\gamma_{i})
.

The mixing time in terms of the local spectral expansion then follows from the proposition and
Lemma

4
. To obtain a tighter mixing time bound, we employ the following proposition concerning the local spectral expansion and the modified log-Sobolev constant.

Proposition 6

(
[
CLV21
]
)
.

Let
(\mathcal{C},\pi_{n})
be a weighted simplicial complex where
\pi_{n}
is a uniform distribution over proper list-colorings of a graph
G=(V,E)
with
|V|=n
and maximum degree
\Delta
. If
(\mathcal{C},\pi_{n})
is a
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n-2})
-local spectral expander with
\gamma_{k}\leq\frac{\gamma}{n-k}
for all
k
, then the modified log-Sobolev constant is at least
\Omega_{\gamma,\Delta}(1/n)
, and the mixing time of the Glauber dynamics is at most
O_{\gamma,\Delta}(n\log n)
.

2.5
Trickle-Down Theorems

The trickle-down theorem of Oppenheim states that the spectral gaps of local walks in a certain dimension imply spectral gaps of local walks in larger dimensions.

Proposition 7

(Trickle-Down Theorem in
[
Opp18
]
)
.

Given a weighted simplicial complex
(\mathcal{C},\pi_{d})
, suppose the following holds:

•

\lambda_{2}(P)<1
, i.e., the local walk
P
is irreducible;

•

There exists some
0\leq\lambda\leq 1/2
such that
\lambda_{2}(P_{x})\leq\lambda
for all
x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
.

Then the local walk
P
satisfies the spectral bound
\lambda_{2}(P)\leq\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}
.

A more general version of the trickle-down theorem was established in
[
ALG21
]
. Instead of bounding the second largest eigenvalues of local walks, it bounds (the transition matrix of) local walks directly.

A symmetric matrix
A
is
positive semi-definite
, written as
A\succeq 0
if and only if all its eigenvalues are nonnegative. For two symmetric matrices
A
and
B
of the same dimension, we write
A\succeq B
, or equivalently
B\preceq A
if and only if
A-B\succeq 0
. As a result, the binary relation
\preceq
defines an order between matrices called
Loewner Order
. For brevity, we write
A\preceq_{\pi}B
if
\Pi A\preceq\Pi B
for any
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\mathcal{C}_{1}}
in the following statement.

Proposition 8

(Theorem 3.2 in
[
ALG21
]
)
.

Given a
d
-dimensional weighted simplicial complex
(\mathcal{C},\pi_{d})
, suppose the following conditions hold:

•

\lambda_{2}(P)<1
where
P
is the local walk on
\mathcal{C}_{1}
;

•

For a family of matrices
\left\{N_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\mathcal{C}_{1}}\right\}
and a constant
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{2}
,

P_{x}-\alpha\mathbf{1}\pi_{x}^{\top}\preceq_{\pi_{x}}N_{x}\preceq_{\pi_{x}}\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}I,

(2)

where
\pi_{x}
is the stationary distribution of
P_{x}
.

Then for any matrix
N\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\mathcal{C}_{1}}
satisfying
N\preceq_{\pi}\frac{1}{2\alpha}I
and
\Pi^{-1}\mathbf{E}_{x\sim\pi}\left[\Pi_{x}N_{x}\right]\preceq_{\pi}N-\alpha N^{2}
, it holds that

P-\left(2-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\cdot\mathbf{1}\pi^{\top}\preceq_{\pi}N.

In particular,
\lambda_{2}(P)\leq\lambda_{1}(N)
.

We include a proof of
Proposition

8

Appendix

(\mathcal{C}_{\tau},\pi_{\tau,k})

of dimension
n_{1},\dots,n_{M}
respectively and

M_{\tau}=\sum_{i\in[M]\colon n_{i}\geq 2}\frac{n_{i}(n_{i}-1)}{k(k-1)}\cdot M_{\tau\cup\eta_{-i}}

where
\eta_{-i}=\eta\setminus\mathcal{C}^{(i)}_{1}
for an arbitrary
\eta\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau,k}
.

Then for every face
\tau\in\mathcal{C}_{d-k}
with
k>2
, it holds that

\Pi_{\tau}P_{\tau}-\frac{k}{k-1}\pi_{\tau}\pi_{\tau}^{\top}\preceq M_{\tau}\preceq\frac{k-1}{3k-1}\Pi_{\tau}.

In particular,
\lambda_{2}(P_{\tau})\leq\lambda_{1}(\Pi_{\tau}^{-1}M_{\tau})
.

2.6
Properties of Loewner Order

We collect some useful results on the properties of the Loewner order below.

Lemma 10
.

Let
A,B
be two matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. For any constant
\varepsilon>0
, we have

AB^{\top}+BA^{\top}\preceq\varepsilon AA^{\top}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}BB^{\top}
. Moreover,
(A+B)(A+B)^{\top}\preceq(1+\varepsilon)AA^{\top}+\left(1+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)BB^{\top}
and
(A-B)(A-B)^{\top}\succeq(1-\varepsilon)AA^{\top}+\left(1-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)BB^{\top}
.

Proof.

Obviously

\varepsilon AA^{\top}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}BB^{\top}-AB^{\top}-BA^{\top}=\left(\sqrt{\varepsilon}A-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}B\right)\left(\sqrt{\varepsilon}A-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}B\right)^{\top}\succeq 0.

The second and third inequalities then follow from the first one by expanding the respective LHS.
∎

We use
\mathtt{supp}\,A
to denote the support of a matrix
A
, namely the collection of coordinates with nonzero value.

Lemma 11
.

Let
U
be a finite set and
\left\{U_{1},\dots,U_{n}\right\}
be a collection of subsets of
U
. Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{U\times U}
be a matrix. Assume
A=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}
where each
A_{i}
is a matrix satisfying
\mathtt{supp}\,A_{i}\subseteq U_{i}\times U_{i}
. For every
v\in U
, let
T(v):=\left\{i\in[n]\,\colon\,v\in U_{i}\right\}
. For every
i\in[n]
, let
m_{i}:=\max_{v\in U_{i}}\absolutevalue{T(v)}
. Then

AA^{\top}\preceq\sum_{i\in[n]}m_{i}\cdot A_{i}A_{i}^{\top}.

Proof.

Let
A(v)
denote the row in
A
indexed by
v\in U
. For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{U}
,

x^{\top}A^{\top}Ax=\|Ax\|^{2}=\sum_{v\in U}((Ax)_{v})^{2}=\sum_{v\in U}\langle A(v),x\rangle^{2}.

(3)

We write
A(v)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}(v)=\sum_{i\in T(v)}A_{i}(v)
. Then

\mathtt{RHS}\mbox{ of \eqref{eqn:mle-square-sum-1}}=\sum_{v\in U}\langle\sum_{i\in T(v)}A_{i}(v),x\rangle^{2}=\sum_{v\in U}\left(\sum_{i\in T(v)}\langle A_{i}(v),x\rangle\right)^{2}

(4)

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\left(\sum_{i\in T(v)}\langle A_{i}(v),x\rangle\right)^{2}\leq|T(v)|\sum_{i\in T(v)}\langle A_{i}(v),x\rangle^{2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathtt{RHS}\mbox{ of \eqref{eqn:mle-square-sum-2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in U}|T(v)|\sum_{i\in T(v)}\langle A_{i}(v),x\rangle^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{v:i\in T(v)}|T(v)|\langle A_{i}(v),x\rangle^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\cdot x^{\top}A_{i}^{\top}A_{i}x.

∎

Corollary 12
.

Let
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}
be a collection of symmetric matrices and
\Pi\succeq 0
. Then

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\right)\Pi\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\right)\preceq n\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\Pi A_{i}.

Proof.

It follows from
Lemma

11
directly by substituting
A_{i}
by
A_{i}\Pi^{1/2}
and noting that
m_{i}\leq n
.
∎

Lemma 13
.

Let
A
be a matrix and
B,B^{\prime}
be two symmetric matrices such that
B\preceq B^{\prime}
. Then

A^{\top}BA\preceq A^{\top}B^{\prime}A.

Proof.

For any
\mathbf{x}
, we have

\mathbf{x}^{\top}\left(A^{\top}B^{\prime}A-A^{\top}BA\right)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{\top}A^{\top}(B^{\prime}-B)A\mathbf{x}\geq 0.

∎

Lemma 14
.

Let
A,B,\Pi
be matrices where
B
and
\Pi
are diagonal. Assume
\Pi\succeq 0
. Then

A\preceq\Pi B\iff\Pi^{-\frac{1}{2}}A\Pi^{-\frac{1}{2}}\preceq B.

Proof.

Since
B,\Pi
are diagonal,
\Pi B=\Pi^{1/2}B\Pi^{1/2}
. This lemma is then implied by
Lemma

13
.
∎

Appendix A
The Matrix Trickle-Down Theorem

Let
(\mathcal{C},\pi_{n})
be a weighted pure
n
-dimensional simplicial complex.

Proposition 27

(
[
Opp18
]
)
.

The following identities hold.

1.

\mathbf{E}_{x\sim\pi_{1}}\left[\Pi_{x}\right]=\Pi
.

2.

\mathbf{E}_{x\sim\pi_{1}}\left[\Pi_{x}P_{x}\right]=\Pi P
.

3.

\mathbf{E}_{x\sim\pi_{1}}\left[\pi_{x}\pi_{x}^{\top}\right]=\Pi P^{2}
.

Proof.

1.

For any
y\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x\sim\pi_{1}}\left[\Pi_{x}\right](y,y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\pi_{1}(x)\pi_{x}(y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{\sigma\sim\pi_{n}}\left[x\in\sigma\right]}{n}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{\sigma\sim\pi_{n}}\left[y\in\sigma\;\middle|\;x\in\sigma\right]}{n-1}.

This can be simplified to

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{\sigma\sim\pi_{n}}\left[x,y\in\sigma\right]}{n(n-1)}=\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{\sigma\sim\pi_{n}}\left[y\in\sigma\right]}{n}=\Pi(y,y).

2.

For any
y,z\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
, if
y=z
,
\mathbf{E}_{x\sim\pi_{1}}\left[\Pi_{x}P_{x}\right](y,z)=\Pi P(y,z)=0
. Otherwise, direct calculation gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x\sim\pi_{1}}\left[\Pi_{x}P_{x}\right](y,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\\
x\neq y,z\end{subarray}}\pi_{1}(x)\pi_{x}(y)P_{x}(y,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\\
x\neq y,z\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{\sigma\sim\pi_{n}}\left[x\in\sigma\right]}{n}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{\sigma\sim\pi_{n}}\left[y\in\sigma\;\middle|\;x\in\sigma\right]}{n-1}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{\sigma\sim\pi_{n}}\left[y,z\in\sigma\;\middle|\;x\in\sigma\right]}{(n-2)\cdot\mathbf{Pr}_{\sigma\sim\pi_{n}}\left[y\in\sigma\;\middle|\;x\in\sigma\right]}.

This can be simplified to

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\\
x\neq y,z\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{\sigma\sim\pi_{n}}\left[x,y,z\in\sigma\right]}{n(n-1)(n-2)}=\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{\sigma\sim\pi_{n}}\left[y,z\in\sigma\right]}{n(n-1)}.

On the other hand, we have

\Pi P(y,z)=\pi_{1}(y)\cdot\frac{\pi_{2}(y,z)}{2\pi_{1}(y)}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{\sigma\sim\pi_{n}}\left[y,z\in\sigma\right]}{\binom{n}{2}}=\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{\sigma\sim\pi_{n}}\left[y,z\in\sigma\right]}{n(n-1)}.

3.

For every
y,z\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x\sim\pi_{1}}\left[\pi_{x}\pi_{x}^{\top}\right](y,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\\
x\neq y,z\end{subarray}}\pi_{1}(x)\pi_{x}(y)\pi_{x}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\\
x\neq y,z\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{\sigma\sim\pi_{n}}\left[x\in\sigma\right]}{n}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{\sigma\sim\pi_{n}}\left[y\in\sigma\;\middle|\;x\in\sigma\right]}{n-1}\cdot\pi_{x}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\\
x\neq y,z\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{\sigma\sim\pi_{n}}\left[x,y\in\sigma\right]}{n(n-1)}\cdot\pi_{x}(z).

On the other hand, note that for every
x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
, the row of
P
indexed by
x
is
\pi_{x}
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\Pi P^{2}\right)(y,z)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}(\Pi P)(y,x)\cdot P(x,z)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}\pi_{1}(y)\pi_{y}(x)\cdot\pi_{x}(z).

This can be written as

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\\
x\neq y,z\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{\sigma\sim\pi_{n}}\left[y\in\sigma\right]}{n}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{\sigma\sim\pi_{n}}\left[x\in\sigma\;\middle|\;y\in\sigma\right]}{n-1}\cdot\pi_{x}(z)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\\
x\neq y,z\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{\sigma\sim\pi_{n}}\left[x,y\in\sigma\right]}{n(n-1)}\cdot\pi_{x}(z).

∎
