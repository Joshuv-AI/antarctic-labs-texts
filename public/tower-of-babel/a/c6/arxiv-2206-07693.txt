Splitting quasireductive supergroups and volumes of supergrassmannians
Vera Serganova, Alexander Sherman

Abstract
. We introduce the notion of splitting subgroups of quasireducitve supergroups, and explain their significance. For G ​ L ​ ( m | n ) GL(m|n) , Q ⁡ ( n ) Q(n) , and defect one basic classical supergroups, we give explicit splitting subgroups. We further prove they are minimal up to conjugacy, except in the G ​ L ​ ( m | n ) GL(m|n) case where it remains a conjecture. A key tool in the proof is the computation of the volumes of complex supergrassmannians, which is of interest in its own right.

1.
Introduction

In this paper we deal with representations of quasireductive supergroups
G
over
\mathbb{C}
, meaning that
G_{0}
, the underlying group of
G
, is reductive. Many important supergroups (including
GL(m|n)
,
SOSp(m|2n)
,
Q(n)
,..) are quasireductive, and although their representation theory is similar to that of reductive groups, semisimplicity of
\operatorname{Rep}G
is very rare. On the other hand, the property of being quasireductive is equivalent to
\operatorname{Rep}G
being Frobenius, a property shared by representations of finite groups in positive characteristic.

We say a quasireductive subgroup
K\subseteq G
is
splitting
if the trivial
G
-submodule of
\mathbb{C}[G/K]
spanned by the constant function 1 splits off. In other words, there is a
G
-submodule
V\subseteq\mathbb{C}[G/K]
such that we have an isomorphism of
G
-modules
\mathbb{C}[G/K]\cong\mathbb{C}\langle 1\rangle\oplus V
.
Here
\mathbb{C}[G/K]
denotes the superalgebra of polynomial functions on
G/K
. In modular representation theory, the analogous notion is very natural. Indeed, if
G
is a finite group and
\Bbbk
is a field of characteristic
p>0
, then the
G
-submodule of constant functions in
\Bbbk[G/K]
splits off if and only if
|G|/|K|
is prime to
p
. In particular, the minimal ‘splitting’ subgroups in the modular setting would be the Sylow
p
-subgroups of
G
, which are all conjugate to one another. In modular representation theory in characteristic
p
, one may often reduce questions about
G
-modules to analogous questions about modules over a (normalizer of a) Sylow
p
-subgroup (see
[
B
]
for more on modular representation theory).

Heuristically speaking, a splitting subgroup
K
of
G
has representation theory that is at least as complicated as that of
G
, despite being a smaller group. For example, if the trivial subgroup of
G
is splitting, then
G
has semisimple representation theory. Further, projectivity of a
G
-module can always be checked by restricting to a splitting subgroup.

1.1.
Main theorem

In this paper we initiate a study of minimal splitting subgroups
K
of distinguished quasireductive supergroups
G
; or if not minimal, then at least conjecturally so. The supergroups we consider are
GL(m|n),Q(n),
and all other supergroups
G
such that
\operatorname{Lie}G
is either
\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(n|n)
or simple basic classical of defect 1. In particular we show the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

The following subgroups are splitting.

(1)

SL(1|1)^{\min(m,n)}\subseteq GL(m|n),\ \ \ P\left(SL(1|1)^{n}\right)\subseteq PSL(n|n);

(2)

Q(2)^{n}\subseteq Q(2n),\ \ \ \ Q(2)^{n}\times Q(1)\subseteq Q(2n+1);

(3)

for any
G
with
\operatorname{Lie}G
a defect 1 basic classical Lie superalgebra:

SL(1|1)\subseteq G.

Further, in cases (2) and (3), these subgroups are minimal splitting subgroups, and are unique up to conjugacy with this property.

In the above, the subgroups
SL(1|1)^{\operatorname{def}\mathfrak{g}}
, where
\operatorname{def}\mathfrak{g}
is the defect of
\mathfrak{g}
(see Section
3.2
), are those that arise from the root subgroup of
(\operatorname{def}\mathfrak{g})
-many isotropic, mutually orthogonal odd roots of
\mathfrak{g}
. In the case of
Q(n)
we take the natural root subgroup of the specified type (in fact it is a Levi subgroup).

The proof of (3) can be done using straightforward techniques in the algebraic category, in particular by studying the space of functions on supersymmetric spaces for the defect one supergroups. However for the infinite series
GL(m|n)
and
Q(n)
we need techniques from differential geometry.

1.2.
Connections to support varieties

In
[
BoKN1
]
,
[
BoKN2
]
, and
[
GGNW
]
, cohomological support varieties are studied for quasireductive Lie superalgebras
\mathfrak{g}
. There, they prove that for basic Lie superalgebras and
\mathfrak{q}(n)
, there are detecting subalgebras
\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{f}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
on which one can compute the support variety of a given
\mathfrak{g}
-module
M
after restricting to
\mathfrak{e}
or
\mathfrak{f}
. In the basic classical cases, their subalgebras
\mathfrak{f}
agree with the Lie superalgebras of our splitting subgroups above. For
\mathfrak{q}(n)
, they have
\mathfrak{e}=\mathfrak{f}=\mathfrak{q}(1)^{n}
, so their detecting subalgebras are smaller.

In a forthcoming work, we will study rank varieties determined by the Duflo-Serganova functor for homological elements, i.e.
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{hom}
(see Section
2.6
). Our splitting subgroups will play an important role in these rank varieties; it will be interesting to understand the connections of these rank varieties to the ones defined in
[
BoKN1
]
.

1.3.
Volumes of supergrassmannians

In order to prove parts (1) and (2) of Theorem
1.1
, we use a unitary trick. First of all, by Lemma
2.11
, we may reduce (1) to showing that
GL(1|1)^{\min(m,n)}\subseteq GL(m|n)
is splitting. Also, the case of
PSL(n|n)
may be reduced to that of
GL(n|n)
, so we put this case aside in this introductory discussion.

Now both
GL(m|n)
and
Q(n)
admit real compact forms
U(m|n)
and
UQ(n|n)
. If we consider the natural embeddings

U(1|1)^{\min(m,n)}\subseteq U(m|n),\ \ \ \ UQ(2)^{n}(\times UQ(1))\subseteq UQ(2n(+1)),

then their complexifications are the subgroups we are interested in showing are splitting. On the other hand, their quotients are compact supermanifolds admitting invariant volume forms; thus if we can show their volumes are nonzero, we may obtain our desired splitting via the integration map.

Instead of computing these volumes directly, we answer a separate question, which is sufficient for our purposes via an inductive argument for splitting subgroups. Consider the supergrassmannian
Gr(r|s,m|n)
or
(r|s)
-planes in
\mathbb{C}^{m|n}
. This is a homogeneous space for
U(m|n)
with stabilizer of a point given by
U(r|s)\times U(m-r|n-s)
. The study of supergrassmannians was initiated in the early 80s by Manin and his students, see
[
M
]
and
[
PS
]
.

We prove that:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\omega
be an invariant volume form on
\mathcal{M}:=Gr(r|s,m|n)
; then

\int_{\mathcal{M}}\omega\neq 0

if and only if
\operatorname{sdim}\mathcal{M}\geq 0
.

An explicit formula for this volume is given in Corollary
5.18
. We note that while our formula does not agree with conjecture given in
[
V
]
, the two formulas are consistent on when the volume is nonzero.

Now using the unitary trick, Theorem
1.2
implies that
GL(r|s)\times GL(m-r|n-s)\subseteq GL(m|n)
is splitting if and only if
(r-s)((m-r)-(n-s))\geq 0
. With the help of the transitivity of splitting subgroups (see Corollary
2.7
), one can obtain part (1) of Theorem
1.1
.

For the
Q(n)
case, we take
\mathbb{C}^{n|n}
with a fixed odd linear automorphism
F:\mathbb{C}^{n|n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n|n}
, and consider the space
QGr(r,n)
, which is the supergrassmannian of
(r|r)
-dimensional,
F
-stable subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{n|n}
. This is a homogeneous space for
UQ(n)
with stabilizer
UQ(r)\times UQ(n-r)
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\omega
be an invariant volume form on
\mathcal{M}:=QGr(r,n)
; then

\int_{\mathcal{M}}\omega\neq 0

if and only if
r(n-r)
is even.

From this we can again use the unitary trick and transitivity to prove part (2) of Theorem
1.1
.

1.4.
Localization of integrals

In order to prove Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
, we use the results of
[
SZ
]
, where a localization theorem was proven for integrals on supermanifolds. We use the following version of it.

Let
\mathcal{M}=Gr(r|r,n|n)
or
QGr(r,n)
, and
\mathcal{U}=U(n|n)
or
UQ(n)
in the respective cases; then there exists an element
Q\in(\operatorname{Lie}\mathcal{U})_{\overline{1}}
such that the zeroes of
Q
are isolated and nondegenerate; in other words if
p
is a zero of
Q
, then the natural endomorphism
Q:T_{p}\mathcal{M}\to T_{p}\mathcal{M}
is an isomorphism.

In this case, the result of
[
SZ
]
implies that the integral
\int_{\mathcal{M}}\omega
localizes to
Z(Q)
, the zero set of
Q
. In particular we have that

\int_{\mathcal{M}}\omega=(2\pi)^{\operatorname{dim}\mathcal{M}_{0}}\sum\limits_{p\in Z(Q)}\alpha(T_{p}\mathcal{M},\omega_{p}),

where
\alpha(T_{p}\mathcal{M},\omega_{p})\in\mathbb{C}
can be computed in terms of the form
\omega_{p}
and the action of
Q
on
T_{p}\mathcal{M}
(see Section
5.1
for the precise definition). In our situation we are able to explicitly compute
\alpha(T_{p}\mathcal{M},\omega_{p})
to obtain the values of our integrals and show they are nonzero. To deal with the general case of
Gr(r|s,m|n)
, either the localization theorem applies similarly to the above, or we can apply a trick of realizing the two step flag variety
Fl(r_{1}|s_{1},r_{2}|s_{2},m|n)
as a fiber bundle in two differ ways, allowing us to obtain relationships between volumes of different Grassmannians.

1.5.
Summary of sections

Section 2 introduces splitting subgroups and proves general properties they satisfy. Section 3 defines contragredient supergroups and their defect subgroups, which are the natural candidates for minimal splitting subgroups. We prove they are minimal in the defect one case, and also discuss what happens for
G=Q(n)
. In Section 4 we prove defect subgroups are splitting for all defect one contragredient supergroups via transitivity and the use of symmetric spaces. Section 5 computes the volume of the supergrassmannians for
GL(m|n)
and
Q(n)
via the localization technique of
[
SZ
]
. Finally Section 6 applies the volume computations to proving Theorem
1.1
.

2.
Splitting subgroups

2.1.
Preliminaries and notation

We work throughout over the field
\mathbb{C}
of complex numbers. For a super vector space
V
, we write
V=V_{\overline{0}}\oplus V_{\overline{1}}
for its
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-gradation. We will write
\mathcal{M}
for a supervariety, and let
\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{M}]
denote the algebra of global functions on
\mathcal{M}
. If
\mathcal{M}
is smooth, we set
\operatorname{dim}\mathcal{M}_{1}
to be the odd dimension of
\mathcal{M}
.

2.2.
Quasireductive supergroups

Recall that a linear algebraic supergroup
G
is called quasireductive if the underlying algebraic group
G_{0}
is reductive. Let
\mathfrak{g}:=\operatorname{Lie}G
denote the Lie superalgebra of
G
. Then by definition, a representation of
G
(or a
G
-module) is a
\mathfrak{g}
-module
V
such that the action of
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
on
V
integrates to an action of
G_{0}
. We write
\operatorname{Rep}G
for the category of all representations of
G
, and
\mathcal{F}(G)
for the subcategory of finite-dimensional
G
-modules.

For a quasireductive supergroup
G
, the category
\mathcal{F}(G)
is Frobenius i.e., there are enough projective objects and
every projective object is injective, see
[
S1
]
. Since
\operatorname{Rep}G
is the coinductive completion of
\mathcal{F}(G)
, one may check that the class of projectives and the class of injectives coincide in
\operatorname{Rep}G
, and that they consist exactly of arbitrary direct sums of finite-dimensional indecomposable projectives (or equivalently injectives).

2.3.
Homogeneous spaces and induction

Let
K
be a quasireductive subgroup of
G
. We may form the homogeneous supervariety
G/K
, and we
write
\mathbb{C}[G/K]
for its space of functions. Because
K
and
G
are quasireductive,
G/K
in fact will be affine. See
[
MaT
]
for more on the constructions of quotients, and proof of the affinity of
G/K
. Recall that Frobenius reciprocity tells us that
\operatorname{Res}_{K}^{G}
is left adjoint to
\operatorname{Ind}_{K}^{G}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
K\subset G
are quasireductive supergroups then the induction functor
\operatorname{Ind}^{G}_{K}:\operatorname{Rep}K\to\operatorname{Rep}G
is exact and takes injectives to injectives.

Proof.

By the geometric definition of induction,
\operatorname{Ind}^{G}_{K}M
is isomorphic to the functor of global sections of the
G
-equivariant vector bundle

G\times_{K}M
. Exactness now follows from the affinity of
G/K
. By Frobenius reciprocity, injectivity of a
K
-module
M
implies injectivity of
\operatorname{Ind}^{G}_{K}M
.
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

If
K\subset G
are quasireductive supergroups, then the restriction functor
\operatorname{Res}_{K}:\operatorname{Rep}G\to\operatorname{Rep}K
takes projectives to projectives.

Proof.

By Frobenius reciprocity,
\operatorname{Res}_{K}^{G}
is left adjoint to
\operatorname{Ind}_{K}^{G}
.
∎

We need the following analogue of Shapiro’s lemma for quasireductive supergroups.

Lemma 2.3

(Frobenius Reciprocity)
.

Let
K\subset G
be quasireductive supergroups and
N
be a
K
-module.
For a
G
-module
M
, and for all
i
, we have natural isomorphisms

\operatorname{Ext}^{i}_{G}(M,\operatorname{Ind}^{G}_{K}N)\cong\operatorname{Ext}^{i}_{K}(\operatorname{Res}_{K}^{G}M,N).

Proof.

If
I^{\bullet}
is an injective resolution of
N
then
\operatorname{Ind}^{G}_{K}I^{\bullet}
is an injective resolution of
\operatorname{Ind}^{G}_{K}N
. The statement
now follows from Frobenius reciprocity:

\operatorname{Hom}_{G}(M,\operatorname{Ind}^{G}_{K}I^{s})\simeq\operatorname{Hom}_{K}(\operatorname{Res}_{K}^{G}M,I^{s}).

∎

2.4.
Splitting subgroups

The
G
-module
\mathbb{C}[G/K]
contains a trivial submodule
\mathbb{C}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[G/K]
spanned by the constant function
1
. In what follows, if
V
is a
G
-module and
W\subseteq V
is a
G
-submodule, we say
W
splits off
V
if
W
is a direct summand of
V
as a
G
-module.

Proposition 2.4
.

Assume that
G
is quasireductive and
K
is a quasireductive closed subgroup.
The following are equivalent:

(1)

The trivial
G
-submodule
\mathbb{C}
splits off
\mathbb{C}[G/K]
as a
G
-module;

(2)

for any
G
-module
M^{\prime}
the natural injective morphism
M^{\prime}\to\operatorname{Ind}^{G}_{K}M^{\prime}
splits;

(3)

for any pair of
G
-modules
M,M^{\prime}
, the restriction morphism

\operatorname{Ext}^{i}_{G}(M,M^{\prime})\to\operatorname{Ext}^{i}_{K}(M,M^{\prime})

is injective for all
i
.

Definition 2.5
.

We say that a subgroup
K\subseteq G
is a splitting subgroup in
G
if it is quasireductive and satisfies the above equivalent conditions.

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2) follows from a natural isomorphism

\tau:\operatorname{Ind}^{G}_{K}M^{\prime}\to(\operatorname{Ind}^{G}_{K}\mathbb{C})\otimes M^{\prime}.

Indeed, if
\pi:\operatorname{Ind}^{G}_{K}\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is a
splitting homomorphism then
(\pi\otimes 1_{M^{\prime}})\tau\varphi=1_{M^{\prime}}
.

Let us show that (2)
\Rightarrow
(3). Note that by (2) the natural homomorphism

\varphi:\text{Ext}_{G}^{i}(M,M^{\prime})\to\text{Ext}^{i}_{G}(M,\operatorname{Ind}^{G}_{K}M^{\prime})

is an embedding. Using Lemma
2.3
we have an isomorphism

\psi:\text{Ext}^{i}_{G}(M,\operatorname{Ind}^{G}_{K}M^{\prime})\to\text{Ext}^{i}_{K}(M,M^{\prime}).

The composition
\psi\varphi
is the restriction homomorphism.

For
(3)\Rightarrow(1)
, we have a short exact sequence of
G
-modules

0\to\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}[G/K]\to\mathbb{C}[G/K]/\mathbb{C}\to 0.

The evaluation morphism at
eK
provides a splitting as
K
-modules, and thus by (3) the sequence must also split over
G
.
∎

Corollary 2.6
.

If
K
is a splitting subgroup of
G
, then a
G
-module
M
is projective if and only if its restriction to
K
is projective.

2.5.
Various sufficient criteria for splitting

In what follows we always assume that
G
,
H
, and
K
are quasireductive. Our first result follows immediately from the definition of splitting.

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
K\subseteq H\subseteq G
.

(1)

If
K
is a splitting subgroup in
G
, then
H
is also a splitting subgroup in
G
.

(2)

If
K
is a splitting subgroup of
H
, and
H
is a splitting subgroup of
G
, then
K
is a splitting subgroup of
G
.

Remark 2.8
.

By Corollary
2.7
, we may work inductively to prove that a given subgroup of
G
is splitting, in the following sense: if
K\subseteq G
is a subgroup then it is splitting if and only there exists a chain of inclusions
K=H_{0}\subseteq H_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq H_{k}=G
such that
H_{i}
is a splitting subgroup of
H_{i+1}
for all
i
.

Lemma 2.9
.

Suppose that
R
is a normal subgroup of
G
such that
R^{\circ}
is reductive, and let
H=G/R
. Write
\pi:G\to H
for the quotient map. Let
K\subseteq G
be a subgroup, and let

\overline{K}:=\pi(K)=K/K\cap R\subseteq H.

If
\overline{K}
is a splitting subgroup of
H
, then
K
is a splitting subgroup of
G
.

Proof.

Write
\varphi:\mathbb{C}[H/\overline{K}]\to\mathbb{C}
for the
H
-equivariant, and thus
G
-equivariant splitting (
H
being a quotient of
G
). Because
R
has semisimple representation theory, we have a
G
-equivariant projection
\mathbb{C}[G/K]\to\mathbb{C}[G/K]^{R}=\mathbb{C}[H/\overline{K}]
. Taking the composition with
\varphi
gives our desired splitting.
∎

Lemma 2.10
.

Suppose that
N\subseteq G
is a normal subgroup of
G
, and write
H=G/N
. Suppose that
K\subseteq G
is a splitting subgroup. Then
\overline{K}
is a splitting subgroup of
H
, where
\overline{K}=K/K\cap N
.

Proof.

We have a natural
G
-equivariant map
G/K\to H/\overline{K}
. Thus we have an inclusion
\mathbb{C}[H/\overline{K}]\to\mathbb{C}[G/K]
, so composing with the splitting
\mathbb{C}[G/K]\to\mathbb{C}
gives the result.
∎

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
K\subset G
be a subgroup such that
(\operatorname{Lie}G)_{\overline{1}}=(\operatorname{Lie}K)_{\overline{1}}
. Then
K
is splitting in
G
.

Proof.

We have
G/K=G_{0}/K_{0}
. Hence the statement.
∎

For the proof of the following, see Section 6 of
[
Sh2
]
.

Lemma 2.12
.

Suppose that
G
is quasireductive,
K
is a splitting subgroup of
G
, and
\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
is an odd abelian ideal. Then
\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{k}
.

Lemma 2.13
.

Suppose that
K\subseteq H\subseteq G
are quasireductive supergroups such that
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\rtimes\mathfrak{l}
for a quasireductive Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{l}
. Suppose further that
\mathfrak{l}
stabilizes
\mathfrak{k}
. If
K\subseteq G
is splitting, then
K\subseteq H
is splitting as well.

Proof.

Applying Lemma
2.10
, we may pass to a finite cover of
G
so that
G_{0}\cong H_{0}\times L_{0}
. Then by the theory of Harish-Chandra pairs for supergroups, we obtain a decomposition
G=H\rtimes L
. Since
\mathfrak{l}
stabilizes
\mathfrak{k}
,
L
must similarly stabilize
K
, and thus
K\rtimes L\subseteq G
is a subgroup, and since it contains
K
it is splitting. Consider the natural
H
-equivariant map

H/K\to(H\rtimes L)/(K\rtimes L).

It is a surjective closed embedding, and since the supervarieties are smooth of the same dimension, it must be an isomorphism. Since the trivial
G
-submodule splits off functions on the RHS as an
H
-module, it must do the same on the LHS, and we are done.
∎

2.6.
Geometric criterion

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\operatorname{Lie}G
. An element
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
is called homological if
x^{2}:=\frac{1}{2}[x,x]
is a semisimple element in
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
. For example, if
[x,x]=0
then
x
is homological. We set

\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{hom}:=\{x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}:x\text{ is homological}\}.

Observe that
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{hom}
is
G_{0}
-stable; however it need not be closed or open in
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
.

Let
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{hom}
. Then if
M
is a
G
-module,
[x,x]
acts semisimply on
M
. One can define a tensor functor

DS_{x}:\operatorname{Rep}G\to\operatorname{SVect}
defined by:

DS_{x}M:=\operatorname{ker}x/(xM\cap\operatorname{ker}x)=H(x,M^{[x,x]}).

Assume that
\mathcal{M}
is a affine algebraic supervariety with a vector field
Q
such that
Q^{2}
acts semisimply on
\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{M}]
. We say that a point
p\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
is a zero of
Q
if
Q(\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{M}])\subset I_{p}
, where
I_{p}
is the maximal ideal determined by
p
. Let
Z(Q)
denote zero locus of
Q
, which is a Zariski-closed subset of
\mathcal{M}_{0}
.

Lemma 2.14
.

If
Z(Q)=\emptyset
then
DS_{Q}\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{M}]=0
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{M})
denote the ideal generated by odd functions on
\mathcal{M}
and consider the
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{M})
-adic filtration
on
\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{M}]
.
Consider the corresponding graded
\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{M}_{0}]
-module
\operatorname{gr}\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{M}]
. Note that it has a compatible structure of a

Q^{2}
-module since
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{M})
is
Q^{2}
-stable. The operator
Q:\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{M}]\to\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{M}]
induces a vector field
\operatorname{gr}Q

in
\operatorname{gr}\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{M}]
of degree
-1
. In particular,
\operatorname{gr}Q
defines a morphism
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{M})/\mathcal{J}^{2}(\mathcal{M})\to\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{M}]/\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{M})
, which defines a section
\gamma
of the normal bundle
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}
.

The condition
Z(Q)=\emptyset
implies that
\gamma
has no zeros on
\mathcal{M}_{0}
.
Therefore
\gamma
defines an injective morphism
\gamma:\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}
of a trivial line bundle
\mathcal{L}
.
The dual morphism
\mathcal{N}^{*}_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}\to\mathcal{L}^{*}
splits by affinity of
\mathcal{M}_{0}
. This implies the existence
of
Q^{2}
-invariant
f\in\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{M})
such that
\operatorname{gr}Q(\operatorname{gr}f)=1
.

We have thus shown that

Q(f)=1+\eta

where
\eta\in\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{M})
is even, nilpotent, and
Q
-invariant. Thus if we set
g=f/(1+\eta)
we see that
Q(g)=1
, and we are done.
∎

Theorem 2.15
.

Let
G
be a quasireducitve supergroup and
K\subset G
be a splitting subgroup. Then for any homological
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
the orbit
G_{0}x
has non-trivial intersection with
\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}}
; equivalently,
G_{0}\cdot\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}}^{hom}=\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{hom}
.

Proof.

Assume that there exists
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
such that
G_{0}x\cap\mathfrak{k}=\emptyset
. Let
Q
be the odd vector field on
G/K
corresponding to
x
.
Then the Lie algebra of the stabilizer of any
p\in(G/K)_{0}
does not contain
x
and hence
Z(Q)=\emptyset
. By Lemma
2.14
we have

DS_{Q}\mathbb{C}[G/K]=0
; but if
\mathbb{C}
splits as a direct summand we must have
\mathbb{C}\subset DS_{Q}\mathbb{C}[G/K]
, giving a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 2.16
.

Suppose that
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{hom}
and
\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}}^{hom}
contain dense open subsets of
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
and
\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}}
, respectively. Then if
K
is splitting in
G
we must have
\operatorname{sdim}G/K\geq 0
.

Proof.

Since
K
is splitting in
G
, by Theorem
2.15
,
G_{0}\cdot\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}}^{hom}=\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{hom}
. By our assumptions, this implies that the image of the map
G_{0}\times\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}}\to\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
contains an open subset of
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
. Since we work over
\mathbb{C}
, it follows that for some
(g,x)\in G_{0}\times\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}}
, the map of tangent spaces

T_{(g,x)}(G_{0}\times\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}})\to T_{g\cdot x}\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}

is surjective. We may act by
g^{-1}
so that WLOG
g=e
. Then we have

T_{(e,x)}(G_{0}\times\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}})=\mathfrak{g}_{0}\times\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}}\to\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}},

given by

(u,x)\mapsto[u,x]+x.

Since
\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}
preserves
\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}}
, if we choose a splitting of vector spaces
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}=\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}\oplus\mathfrak{m}
, we obtain that the restriction of the above map to:

\mathfrak{m}\times\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}}\to\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}

remains surjective. This implies that

\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}-\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}+\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}}\geq\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}.

From this we obtain
\operatorname{sdim}G/K\geq 0
.
∎

3.
Defect subgroups

3.1.
Contragredient superalgebras

We call a finite-dimensional Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
contragredient if
\mathfrak{g}\simeq\mathfrak{g}(A)
is
a finite-dimensional Kac-Moody superalgebra
with symmetrizable Cartan matrix. Then
\mathfrak{g}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{s}\mathfrak{g}_{i}
is a direct sum of Kac-Moody superalgebras
with indecomposable Cartan matrix. Each
\mathfrak{g}_{i}
is either simple (basic classical or exceptional) or isomorphic to
\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(n|n)
for

n\geq 1
. We call
\mathfrak{g}
indecomposable if
s=1
. Furthermore,
\mathfrak{g}
satisfies the following properties:

(1)

\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
is reductive and
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
is a semisimple
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
-module;

(2)

the centralizer of the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
in
\mathfrak{g}
coincides with
\mathfrak{h}
;

(3)

in the root decomposition

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Delta}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},

all roots spaces
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
have dimension
1|0
or
0|1
;

(4)

\mathfrak{g}
admits a nondegenerate, invariant supersymmetric form
(-,-)
, which we fix.

We say that
\alpha\in\Delta
is either even or odd if the corresponding root space is of that parity. In this section we denote by
G

a connected
quasireductive algebraic supergroup with Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
h\in\mathfrak{h}
the centralizer
\mathfrak{g}^{h}
is the direct product of a contragredient Lie superalgebras and a
subalgebra of
\mathfrak{h}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
\alpha(h)\in\mathbb{Q}
for all roots
\alpha
.
We have the decomposition

\mathfrak{g}^{h}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Delta(\mathfrak{g}^{h})}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\quad\Delta(\mathfrak{g}^{h})=\{\alpha\in\Delta\mid\alpha(h)=0\}.

Let
\varepsilon
denote the minimum of
\alpha(h)
for all roots
\alpha
such that
\alpha(h)>0
. Let
h_{0}\in\mathfrak{h}
be some
regular element such that
|\alpha(h_{0})|<1
for
\alpha\in\Delta
. Set
h^{\prime}=h+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}h_{0}
and let
\Delta^{+}
be the set
of positive roots defined by
h^{\prime}
. If
B
is the corresponding set of simple roots, then
B^{\prime}=B\cap\Delta(\mathfrak{g}^{h})
is the base
of
\mathfrak{g}^{h}
. By construction
B^{\prime}
is linearly independent. That implies
\mathfrak{g}^{h}=\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
where
\mathfrak{a}\subset Z(\mathfrak{g}^{h})
is the subspace
complementary to
[\mathfrak{g}^{h},\mathfrak{g}^{h}]\cap Z(\mathfrak{g}^{h})
. Then
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
is a quotient of the Kac-Moody superalgebra by
some central ideal
\mathfrak{c}
. Since the restriction of the invariant form on
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
is nondegenerate,
\mathfrak{c}=0
.

∎

3.2.
Defect subgroups

For an odd root
\alpha\in\Delta
, we say that
\alpha
is isotropic if
(\alpha,\alpha)=0
, and otherwise we say
\alpha
is non-isotropic.

The defect of
\mathfrak{g}
is the maximal number
d
of mutually orthogonal linearly independent isotropic roots of
\mathfrak{g}
.

For an odd isotropic root
\alpha
, choose non-zero elements
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
and
y\in\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}
so that
h:=[x,y]\neq 0
. Then we obtain a subalgebra

\mathfrak{s}=\mathbb{C}\langle h,x,y\rangle\cong\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(1|1).

We write
S
for the corresponding subgroup of
G
; note that either
S\cong SL(1|1)
, or if
\alpha
lies in a factor of
\mathfrak{g}
isomorphic to
\operatorname{Lie}D(2,1;a)
, then for irrational
a
,
h
generates a two-dimensional torus in
G
.

Definition 3.2
.

A quasireductive subgroup
D\subset G
is called a defect subgroup if its Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{d}
satisfies the following:

(1)

the
G_{0}
-orbit of any homological element of
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
has a non-trivial intersection with
\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{1}}
;

(2)

\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{1}}=2d
;

(3)

[\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{1}},\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{1}}]=\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{0}}
,
[\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{1}},\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{0}}]=0
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d}\}
be a maximal set of mutually orthogonal linearly independent isotropic roots in
\Delta
. Then the supergoup
D
with Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{d}
generated by
\{\mathfrak{g}_{\pm\alpha_{i}}\mid i=1,\dots d\}
is a defect subgroup, and any defect subgroup is conjugate to
D
.

Proof.

Let us prove the first assertion. Let
x
be a homological odd element such that

[x,x]=h
. Without loss of generality we may assume that
h
lies in the fixed Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
of
\mathfrak{g}
. Note that

x\in\mathfrak{g}^{h}
and that
\mathfrak{g}^{h}

is a direct sum of a contragredient superalgebra and a purely even center by Lemma
3.1
.
Thus, if
h
is not central, then we can reduce the statement to the contragredient superalgebra of
smaller dimension and central
h
. Hence we can assume that
h
is central and
\mathfrak{g}
is indecomposable.
If the center of
\mathfrak{g}
is trivial, then
[x,x]=0
and the statement follows from the description of the self-commuting cone in
[
GHSS
]
. If
h\neq 0
then we must have
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(n|n)
, and one can check by direct computation that

x
is conjugate to the matrix proportional to
\left(\begin{matrix}0&1_{n}\\
1_{n}&0\end{matrix}\right)
which immediately
implies the statement.

Now we deal with the second assertion.
It is possible to choose
x_{i}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_{i}},y_{i}\in\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha_{i}}
such that if we set
x=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(x_{i}+y_{i})

then the Lie algebra of the minimal torus
containing
h=[x,x]
coincides with
\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{0}}
. Since
x
is homological,
using the action of
G_{0}

we can assume without loss of generality
that the Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{d}^{\prime}
of a defect subgroup
D^{\prime}
contains
x
and hence
\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{0}}
.
Furthermore,
by direct inspection of root systems of all exceptional and basic classical superalgebras we learn that
the centralizer of any generic element of
\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{0}}

is isomorphic to

\mathfrak{d}\oplus\mathfrak{l}
where
\mathfrak{l}
is a reductive Lie superalgebra. Therefore
\mathfrak{l}_{\overline{1}}
does not contain non-zero homological
elements. Hence

\mathfrak{d}^{\prime}_{\overline{1}}\subset\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{1}}
and by dimension count
\mathfrak{d}=\mathfrak{d}^{\prime}
.
∎

The main theorem of this text is:

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
G
be a quasireductive supergroup with contragredient Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
such that every
direct summand of
\mathfrak{g}
has defect
\leq 1
or is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{sl}(m|n)
with
m\neq n
, or
\mathfrak{gl}(n|n)
.
Then a defect subgroup is a splitting subgroup.

Proof.

If
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are splitting in
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
respectively, then

K_{1}\times K_{2}
is splitting in
G_{1}\times G_{2}
. Therefore it suffices to prove the statement for all simple Lie algebras of defect
1
and for
GL(m|n)
. The former is done in Section 4 and the latter in Section 6.
∎

(2)

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
K\subseteq Q(n)

K\cap SQ(n)

(2)

the image of

3.3.
Density of
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{hom}

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a contragredient Lie superalgebra.

(1)

For any
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
we have
\operatorname{dim}G_{0}\cdot x=\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}-\operatorname{dim}(\mathfrak{g}^{x})_{\overline{1}}
where
\mathfrak{g}^{x}
denotes the centralizer of
x
in
\mathfrak{g}
.

(2)

There exists a Zariski open set set
U\subset\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{1}}
such that for all
x\in U

\operatorname{codim}G_{0}\cdot x=\operatorname{dim}(DS_{x}{\mathfrak{g}})_{\overline{1}}+\operatorname{dim}(\mathfrak{d}^{x})_{\overline{1}}.

Proof.

(1) follows from the fact that
\operatorname{sdim}G\cdot x=0
since
G_{0}\cdot x
has an odd symplectic form (see
[
GHSS
]
).
(2) For
x\in\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{1}}
set
y=[x,x]
. There exists a Zariski open dense set
U
such that
\mathfrak{g}^{y}=\mathfrak{h}+\mathfrak{d}+DS_{x}(\mathfrak{g})

for any
x\in U
. Then
DS_{x}(\mathfrak{g})\subset\mathfrak{g}^{x}\subset\mathfrak{g}^{y}
implies

(\mathfrak{g}^{x})_{\overline{1}}=(DS_{x}{\mathfrak{g}})_{\overline{1}}\oplus(\mathfrak{d}^{x})_{\overline{1}}
.
∎

The following statement is proven, in different terms and perspective, in Theorem 3.3.1 of
[
BoKN1
]
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a contragredient Lie superalgebra.
Then
\mathfrak{g}^{hom}_{\overline{1}}
contains a Zariski open subset in
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
if and only if
DS_{x}(\mathfrak{g})_{\overline{1}}=0
for some
x\in\mathfrak{g}^{hom}_{\overline{1}}
.

Proof.

Consider the action map
\varphi:G_{0}\times\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{1}}\to\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
defined by
(g,x)\mapsto gx
.
We have to check when its image contains a dense
open subset. It suffices to check that

D\varphi_{(e,x)}:\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}\oplus\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{1}}\to\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}

is surjective for some

x\in\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{1}}
. Moreover, we my assume that
x\in U
as defined in (2) of Lemma
3.8
.

Let

\tilde{\mathfrak{d}}:=\mathfrak{h}+\mathfrak{d}
.
Then the restriction of the form to
\tilde{\mathfrak{d}}
is nondegenerate. Let us choose
x\in\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{1}}
as in the proof of
Proposition
3.3
. Then

[\mathfrak{h},x]=[\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}},x]\cap\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{1}}.

Furthermore, the restriction of the form on
[\mathfrak{h},x]
is non-degenerate and the orthogonal complement

[\mathfrak{h},x]^{\perp}\cap\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{1}}
coincides with
(\mathfrak{d}^{x})_{\overline{1}}
.
Therefore we obtain

\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{1}}=([\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}},x]\cap{\mathfrak{d}}_{\overline{1}})\oplus(\mathfrak{d}^{x})_{\overline{1}}.

By (2) of Lemma
3.8
,

\operatorname{dim}[\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}},x]=\operatorname{dim}G_{0}\cdot x=\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}-\operatorname{dim}(\mathfrak{d}^{x})_{\overline{1}}-\operatorname{dim}(DS_{x}\mathfrak{g})_{\overline{1}}.

The differential
D\varphi_{(e,x)}
is given by the formula

D\varphi_{(e,x)}(u,y)=y+[u,x]

for all
u\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
,
y\in\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{1}}
. Therefore

\operatorname{Im}D\varphi_{(e,x)}=[\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}},x]+\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{1}}

and

\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Im}D\varphi_{(e,x)}=\operatorname{dim}[\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}},x]+\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{1}}-\operatorname{dim}([\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}},x]\cap\mathfrak{d}_{\overline{1}})=\operatorname{dim}[\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}},x]+\operatorname{dim}(\mathfrak{d}^{x})_{\overline{1}}.

We have

\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Im}D\varphi_{(e,x)}=\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}-\operatorname{dim}(DS_{x}{\mathfrak{g}})_{\overline{1}}.

Thus, we obtain that
D\varphi_{(e,x)}
is surjective if and only if
(DS_{x}\mathfrak{g})
is an even Lie algebra.
∎

3.4.
Minimal splitting subgroups

By part (1) of Corollary
2.7
, the condition of being a splitting subgroup is closed under taking supersets (i.e. sets containing a given one) in the poset of quasireductive subgroups of
G
. Clearly this poset is stable under conjugation.

Conjecture 3.11
.

If
G
is a quasireductive supergroup with
\operatorname{Lie}G
contragredient, then up to conjugacy a defect subgroup
D
of
G
is the unique minimal splitting subgroup.

Proposition 3.12
.

Conjecture
3.11
holds for all
G
of defect 1.

Proof.

Let
K\subseteq G
be a splitting subgroup; then necessarily
\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}}
contains elements
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{hom}
such that
h:=x^{2}
and
h^{\prime}:=(x^{\prime})^{2}
are semisimple, nonzero, and non-conjugate. We may conjugate so that there exists an odd isotropic root
\alpha
such that
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}
; this is by Proposition
3.3
and the fact that
G
is defect one.

Choose a maximal torus
\mathfrak{t}
of
\mathfrak{g}
containing
h
, and conjugate within
K
(using reductivity of
K_{0}
) so that
h^{\prime}
also lies in
\mathfrak{t}
. Now the centralizer of
\mathbb{C}\langle x,h\rangle
in
\mathfrak{g}
is known to be
\mathbb{C}\langle h,y\rangle\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{x}
, where
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
is a reductive Lie superalgebra (i.e. has
(\mathfrak{g}_{x})_{\overline{1}}^{hom}=0
) and
y\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}
has
[y,x]=0
(see
[
GHSS
]
). If
\alpha(h^{\prime})\neq 0
, then we obtain
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}
inside
\mathfrak{k}
, forcing the corresponding defect subgroup of
\alpha
to lie in
K
, and we would be done. Thus suppose instead that
\alpha(h^{\prime})=0
, which implies that
h^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{g}_{x}\oplus\mathbb{C}\langle h\rangle
.

By the Jacobi identity we have
[x^{\prime},[x,x^{\prime}]]=0
, which implies that
[x,x^{\prime}]\in\mathbb{C}\langle h^{\prime}\rangle\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{x^{\prime}}
. However since
\mathfrak{g}_{x^{\prime}}
splits off
\mathfrak{g}
as a trivial module under
\langle x^{\prime},h^{\prime}\rangle,
we must in fact have
[x,x^{\prime}]=rh^{\prime}
for some
r\in\mathbb{C}
. If
r=0
, then
[x,x^{\prime}]=0
which implies that
x^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{C}\langle h,y\rangle\times\mathfrak{g}_{x})_{\overline{1}}^{hom}=\mathbb{C}\langle y\rangle
. It follows that
x
is a nonzero multiple of
y
, and since
\langle x,y^{\prime}\rangle=\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}
we are done.

Therefore suppose instead that
r\neq 0
; then since
h^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{g}_{x}\oplus\mathbb{C}\langle h\rangle
and
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
splits off
\mathfrak{g}
as a trivial module under
\operatorname{ad}(x)
, we must have
h^{\prime}=sh
for some
s\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
.

From here, if we decompose
x^{\prime}=\sum\limits x_{\beta}^{\prime}
into a sum of root vectors, for all
\beta\notin\{\pm\alpha\}
we must have
\beta(h^{\prime})\neq 0
; on the other hand
[h^{\prime},x^{\prime}]=0
, so it follows that
x^{\prime}=x_{\alpha}^{\prime}+x_{-\alpha}^{\prime}
. From here it is easy to see that
x,x^{\prime}
generate the defect subgroup determined by
\alpha
, and since this will lie in
K
we are done.
∎

For
Q(n)
we have the following clean result:

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
G=Q(n)
and
K
a splitting subgroup of
G
. Then
K
contains a subgroup conjugate to
Q(2)^{d}
for
n=2d
and to
Q(2)^{d}\times Q(1)
for
n=2d+1
.

Proof.

In the Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}(n)
, the odd component is an adjoint
G_{0}=GL(n)
-module. Theorem
2.15
implies that without loss of generality we may assume that

\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}}
contains a diagonal matrix
u
with distinct eigenvalues

\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
such that
\lambda^{2}_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}^{2}
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
. Since
K_{0}\subset G_{0}
is a reductive subgroup and
[u,u]\in\mathfrak{k}_{0}
,
this implies that
K_{0}
contains a maximal torus of
G_{0}
.
Hence
\mathfrak{k}
contains an even part
\mathfrak{h}_{0}
of the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathfrak{g}
. Theorem
2.15
implies that
\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}}
contains, up to conjugacy, any
v\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
of full rank with eigenvalues
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}
.
Then
[v,v]
is conjugate to some element
t\in\mathfrak{h}_{0}
which is also of full rank. Therefore there exists an odd element
t^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{k}
of full rank such that
[t^{\prime},t^{\prime}]=t
. If eigenvalues of
t
are distinct then the
condition
[t^{\prime},t]=0
implies
t^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{h}_{\overline{1}}
. That means
\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}}\cap\mathfrak{h}_{\overline{1}}
contains a Zariski dense subset of
\mathfrak{h}_{\overline{1}}
, hence
\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{k}
. Now we have that

\mathfrak{k}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Delta_{\mathfrak{k}}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}

for some subset
\Delta_{\mathfrak{k}}
of roots. Since
\mathfrak{k}_{0}
is a reductive root subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}_{0}=\mathfrak{gl}(n)
, we get
\mathfrak{k}_{0}=\mathfrak{gl}(n_{1})\oplus\dots\mathfrak{gl}(n_{p})
for some
n_{1}+\dots+n_{p}=n
. Hence

\mathfrak{k}=\mathfrak{q}(n_{1})\oplus\dots\oplus\mathfrak{q}(n_{p})
. The maximal rank of any
u\in k_{\overline{1}}
such that
[u,u]=0
equals

2\sum\lfloor n_{i}/2\rfloor
. But the maximal rank of a self-commuting odd element in
\mathfrak{q}(n)
is
2\lfloor n/2\rfloor
. Since
\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}}
must contain a self-commuting element of maximal rank we obtain that for even
n

all
n_{i}
are even and
for odd
n
at most one
n_{i}
is odd. Therefore
K
contains
Q(2)^{d}
for
n=2d
and
Q(2)^{d}\times Q(1)
for
n=2d+1
.
∎

For cases (3), (5) and (6), we will first prove that:

Proof.

See Section

∎

Proposition 4.2
.

For

\mathbb{C}
splits off

Proof.

Write

for the summand of

in the principal block. Then by Lemma

4.1.
Action of the Casimir

For cases (3), (5), and (6) we will show that the action of the Casimir is enough for proving that for
\lambda\neq 0
as in Lemma
4.1
,
\lambda
does not lie in the principal block, thus proving Lemma
4.1
.

Let
G/K
be a supersymmetric space and write
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
for the corresponding supersymmetric pair from an involution
\theta
of
\mathfrak{g}
. We have a decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
, where
\mathfrak{p}
is the
(-1)
-eigenspace of
\theta
. Choose a Cartan subspace
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
. We may decompose
\mathfrak{g}
into the weight spaces of the action of
\mathfrak{a}
, and we write
\overline{\Delta}
for the nonzero weights of this action, which are exactly the restrictions of roots from
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
to
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
for a
\theta
-stable Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
containing
\mathfrak{a}
. We refer to
\overline{\Delta}
as the set of restricted roots for this supersymmetric pair.

If we choose a positive set of restricted roots
\overline{\Delta}^{+}
, we may define

\mathfrak{n}:=\bigoplus\limits_{\alpha\in\overline{\Delta}^{+}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}.

We say
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
admits an Iwasawa decomposition if
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{n}
for some choice of
\mathfrak{n}
as above. In this case we may choose a Borel subalgebra
\mathfrak{b}
containing
\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{n}
. We call such a Borel subalgebra an Iwasawa Borel subalgebra.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose that
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
admits an Iwasawa decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{n}
, and let
\mathfrak{b}\supseteq\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{n}
be an Iwasawa Borel subalgebra.

(1)

The space of
\mathfrak{b}
-eigenfunctions in
\mathbb{C}[G/K]
of a given weight is at most one-dimensional, and non-zero
\mathfrak{b}
-eigenfunction must be non-vanishing at
eK
.

(2)

If there is a
\mathfrak{b}
-eigenfunction of weight
\lambda
appearing in
\mathbb{C}[G/K]
, then
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
.

Proof.

Because
\mathfrak{b}+\mathfrak{k}=\mathfrak{g}
,
B
has an open orbit on
G/K
, implying the first statement. For the second statement, if
f\in\mathbb{C}[G/K]
is a
\mathfrak{b}
-eigenfunction of weight
\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
, then for
t\in\mathfrak{h}\cap\mathfrak{k}
we have
0=(tf)(eK)=\lambda(t)f(eK)
. On the other hand we have
\mathfrak{h}=\tilde{\oplus}\mathfrak{a}
, so this forces
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume that
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
admits an Iwasawa decomposition such that

(1)

The restricted root system
\overline{\Delta}
is an indecomposable even root system;

(2)

Invariant form is definite on the root lattice
\mathbb{Z}\overline{\Delta}\subset\mathfrak{a}^{*}
;

(3)

If
m_{\alpha}=\operatorname{sdim}(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})
,
\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{k}\in\overline{\Delta}^{+}
are simple roots,
\rho=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\in\overline{\Delta}^{+}}m_{\alpha}\alpha
, then
\rho=\sum c_{i}\alpha_{i}
with
c_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
;

(4)

For every simple root
\alpha_{i}
the root space
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_{i}}
has a non-trivial even part.

Then the Casimir element
C
acts with positive eigenvalue on any
\mathfrak{b}
-eigenfunction
f_{\lambda}
of weight
\lambda\neq 0
.

Proof.

One can compute that
Cf_{\lambda}=(\lambda+2\rho,\lambda)f_{\lambda}
. The condition (4) implies the existence of a root
\mathfrak{sl}(2)
subalgebra associated with every simple
root
\alpha_{i}
. Therefore the dominance condition on
\lambda
implies
(\lambda,\alpha_{i})\geq 0
. Therefore we have

(\lambda+2\rho,\lambda)=(\lambda,\lambda)+2\sum c_{i}(\lambda,\alpha_{i})>0.

∎

We now use Lemma
4.4
to prove Lemma
4.1
.

•

(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2m|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2m|2n-2)\times\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2))
,
n>m
: here
\mathfrak{a}
is one-dimensional and our restricted root system is

. If we write
\alpha
for the positive long root, we obtain that

which is a non-negative multiple of

by assumption.

•

(\mathfrak{g}(1|2),D(1,2;3))

\mathfrak{a}
is given by the Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}_{2}

\mathfrak{a}^{*}
. If we write
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
for the simple roots of
\mathfrak{g}_{2}
where

\frac{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1})}{(\alpha_{2},\alpha_{2})}=3,

we obtain that

\rho=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}.

•

(F(3|1),D(1,2;2)\times\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(2))
: Here the Cartan subspace is given by the Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{o}(7)
, and thus the form is definite on

. If we write

for the simple roots of the restricted root system where

\frac{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1})}{(\alpha_{2},\alpha_{2})}=3/8,\ \ \ \frac{(\alpha_{2},\alpha_{2})}{(\alpha_{3},\alpha_{3})}=2,

we obtain that

4.2.
SOSp(2|2)\times SO(2)\subseteq D(2,1;\alpha)

For this subgroup we will need to study the supersymmetric pair
(D(2,1;\alpha),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2|2)\times\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{o}(2))
more closely. Present the root system of

with basis
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\epsilon_{3}

\pm 2\epsilon_{i}

\pm\epsilon_{1}\pm\epsilon_{2}\pm\epsilon_{3}

\lambda_{0}
and

[
Ger
]
, the extension graph of the principal block is

\textstyle{\lambda_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\lambda_{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\lambda_{3}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

In particular we have

5.
Volumes of supergrassmannians

5.1.
Invariants of representations of
\mathfrak{q}(1)

In this section the ground field is
\mathbb{R}
.
Consider a Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{q}
, defined over
\mathbb{R}
, with basis
\{Q,Q^{2}\}
where
Q
is an odd element.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
V
be a
\mathfrak{q}
-module such that
Q:V\to V
is an isomorphism and
Q^{2}:V\to V
is a compact linear operator (the closure of the subgroup generated by
\operatorname{exp}Q^{2}
is compact in
GL(V_{\overline{0}})\times GL(V_{\overline{1}})
). Then the following hold:

(1)

\operatorname{dim}V_{\overline{0}}=\operatorname{dim}V_{\overline{1}}=2n
for some positive integer
n
;

(2)

Every
Q^{2}
-invariant nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form
B_{0}
on
V_{\overline{0}}
extends uniquely to a bilinear supersymmetric
\mathfrak{q}
-invariant form
B
on
V
.

Proof.

(1) follows from the fact that
Q^{2}
defines a compact isomorphism on both
V_{\overline{0}}
and
V_{\overline{1}}
. For (2), observe that given a
Q^{2}
-invariant nondenegerate supersymmetric bilinear form
B_{0}
on
V_{\overline{0}}
, for
v,w\in V_{\overline{0}}
we must have
B(Qv,Qw)=B(Q^{2}v,w)
. This indeed defines a form with the desired properties, and uniqueness also follows.
∎

Let us fix a volume form
\omega
on
V
and orientation
\omega_{\overline{0}}
on
V_{\overline{0}}
. Let
B
be a
\mathfrak{q}
-invariant even symmetric bilinear form.
Choose bases
e_{1},\dots,e_{2n}\in V_{\overline{0}}
and
f_{1},\dots,f_{2n}\in V_{\overline{1}}
such that

\omega(e_{1},\dots,e_{2n},f_{1},\dots,f_{2n})=1,\omega_{\overline{0}}(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})>0.

Set

\alpha(V,B,\omega):=(-\mathbf{i})^{n}\exp\left(\frac{\pi\mathbf{i}(\operatorname{dim}V_{\overline{0}}^{+}-\operatorname{dim}V_{\overline{0}}^{-})}{4}\right)\frac{\operatorname{Pf}(B_{1})}{\sqrt{|\det B_{0}|}},

where
\operatorname{dim}V_{\overline{0}}^{+}-\operatorname{dim}V_{\overline{0}}^{-}
is the signature of
B_{0}
, and
\operatorname{Pf}(B_{1})
is computed with respect to our chosen basis.

Lemma 5.2
.

If
V
satisfies the conditions of Lemma
5.1
then
\alpha(V,B,\omega)
does not depend on a choice of
B
.

Proof.

Let
B
be some invariant form such that
B_{0}
is positive definite and let
B^{\prime}
be some other invariant form. Then there is a
B_{0}
-orthonormal basis
v_{1},\dots,v_{2n}\in V_{\overline{0}}
which is also orthogonal with respect to
B^{\prime}_{0}
.
Since
B^{\prime}-tB
is
\mathfrak{g}
-invariant for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
there is a decomposition
V=V^{1}\oplus\dots\oplus V^{s}
orthogonal with respect to both forms
B
and
B^{\prime}
, where
V^{i}=\operatorname{ker}(B^{\prime}-t_{i}B)
where
t_{1},\dots,t_{s}
are eigenvalues of

B^{\prime}_{0}
. Since each
V^{i}
satisfies the assumption of Lemma
5.1
we have
\operatorname{dim}V^{i}=(2n_{i}|2n_{i})
with
n_{1}+\dots+n_{s}=n
. Let
B^{i}
denote the restriction of
B
to
V^{i}
and
\omega^{i}
a volume form on
V^{i}
such that

\omega=\prod_{i=1}^{s}\omega^{i}
. Then by direct inspection
\alpha(V^{i},B^{i},\omega^{i})=\alpha(V^{i},t_{i}B^{i},\omega^{i})
and therefore

\alpha(V,B,\omega)=\prod_{i=1}^{s}\alpha(V^{i},B^{i},\omega^{i})=\prod_{i=1}^{s}\alpha(V^{i},t_{i}B^{i},\omega^{i})=\alpha(V,B^{\prime},\omega).

∎

Since the coefficient
\alpha(V,B,\omega)
does not depend on the choice of
B
we just write
\alpha(V,\omega)
. The following lemma gives a practical way to compute
\alpha(V,\omega)
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
V
satisfy the conditions of Lemma
5.1
. Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{2n}\in V_{\overline{0}}
be a positively oriented basis such that the matrix of
Q_{0}^{2}
is skew-symmetric in this basis.
(Such basis exists by compactness of
Q^{2}
.)
For any basis
f_{1},\dots,f_{2n}
of
V_{\overline{1}}
such that
\omega(e_{1},\dots,e_{2n},f_{1},\dots,f_{2n})=1
, the matrix
\left(\begin{matrix}0&Q_{01}\\
Q_{10}&0\end{matrix}\right)
of
Q
satisfies the property

Q^{t}_{01}Q_{10}
is skew-symmetric and

\alpha(V,\omega)=\operatorname{Pf}(Q^{t}_{01}Q_{10}^{-1})

Proof.

Choose an invariant form
B
on
V
such that
e_{1},\dots,e_{2n}
is an orthonormal basis with respect to
B_{0}
; then by skew symmetry of
Q_{0}^{2}
,
B_{0}
will be
Q^{2}
-invariant, so may be extended uniquely to an invariant form
B
on
V
using Lemma
5.1
. For any
v,w\in V_{\overline{1}}
we have

B(v,w)=B(v,QQ^{-1}w)=B(Qv,Q^{-1}w).

Therefore
B_{1}=Q^{t}_{01}Q_{10}^{-1}
. That implies that
B_{1}
is skew-symmetric. Using the definition of
\alpha(V,B,\omega)
and noticing that
B_{0}
is the identity matrix we get the desired result.
∎

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
V=\mathbb{C}^{n|n}
with standard complex basis
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\in V_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in V_{1}
, and real volume form

\omega=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{dz_{i}d\bar{z}_{i}}{d\zeta_{i}d\bar{\zeta}_{i}}.

If the action of
Q
in the standard basis is given by

Qu_{i}=(1+\mathbf{i})d_{i}v_{i},\quad Qv_{i}=(1+\mathbf{i})c_{i}v_{i}

for
c_{i},d_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, then
\alpha(V,\omega)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{c_{i}}{d_{i}}
.

Proof.

With respect to our complex basis,

\overline{Q}^{t}_{01}Q_{10}^{-1}=-\operatorname{diag}(\frac{c_{1}}{d_{1}}\mathbf{i},\dots,\frac{c_{n}}{d_{n}}\mathbf{i}).

Here we recall that the transpose of a
n\times n
complex matrix
A
viewed as a real
2n\times 2n
-matrix is
\overline{A}^{t}
. From here one easily checks that the Pfaffian of the corresponding real matrix is as desired.
∎

5.2.
Schwarz–Zaboronsky formula

This section will deal with Berezinian integration on real supermanifolds. We refer to
[
M
]
for details.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a compact real supermanifold with underlying manifold
\mathcal{M}_{0}
and a volume form
\omega
. We fix an orientation on the underlying manifold
\mathcal{M}_{0}
. Let
Q
be a vector field on
\mathcal{M}
.
A point
p\in\mathcal{M}
is a zero of
Q
if for any smooth function
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}\mathcal{M}
we have
Q(f)\subset I_{p}
where
I_{p}
is the vanishing ideal of
p
. By
Z(Q)
we denote the set of all zeros of
Q
.
If
p\in Z(Q)
then
Q
induces a linear operator on
T^{*}_{p}\mathcal{M}=I_{p}/I_{p}^{2}
.
We say that
p\in Z(Q)
is isolated if
Q:T^{*}_{p}\mathcal{M}\to T^{*}_{p}\mathcal{M}
is an isomorphism. Let us consider an odd vector field
Q
on
\mathcal{M}
satisfying the following properties:

(1)

Z(Q)
is finite and consists of isolated zeros;

(2)

Q\omega=0
;

(3)

Q^{2}
is a compact vector field, i.e.,there exists a compact Lie group
\mathcal{K}
acting on
\mathcal{M}
such that
Q\in\operatorname{Lie}\mathcal{K}
.

Let us note that by Lemma
5.1

\operatorname{dim}\mathcal{M}=(2n|2n)
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. From
[
SZ
]
we have:

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a supermanifold of dimension
(2n|2n)
with volume form
\omega
and odd vector field
Q
satisfy the above assumptions. Then there exists an odd function
\sigma
such that

(1)

Q^{2}\sigma=0
;

(2)

Q\sigma\notin I_{p}
for any
p\notin Z(Q)
; and

(3)

the Hessian of
Q\sigma
at each point
p\in Z(Q)
is nondegenerate.

Furthermore,

\int_{\mathcal{M}}\omega=(2\pi)^{n}\sum_{p\in Z(Q)}\alpha(T_{p}\mathcal{M},\operatorname{Hess}_{p}(Q\sigma),\omega_{p}),

where
\operatorname{Hess}_{p}(Q\sigma)
is the Hessian of
Q\sigma
at
p
, which will be an even symmetric
Q
-invariant form on
T_{p}\mathcal{M}
.

It follows from Lemma
5.2
that the function
\sigma
can be removed from the Schwarz–Zaboronsky formula:

Corollary 5.6
.

\int_{\mathcal{M}}\omega=(2\pi)^{n}\sum_{p\in Z(Q)}\alpha(T_{p}\mathcal{M},\omega_{p}).

Corollary 5.7
.

If
Z(Q)=\emptyset
then
\int_{\mathcal{M}}\omega=0
.

5.3.
Supergrassmannian

The supergrassmannian
Gr(r|s,m|n)
is the superscheme representing the functor of
(r|s)
-dimensional subspaces in
\mathbb{C}^{m|n}
. We refer to
[
M
]
and
[
PS
]
for its construction and properties.

If
G=GL(m|n)
,
K=GL(r|s)\times GL(m-r|n-s)
and
P\subset G
a maximal parabolic subgroup of
G
containing
K
then the homogeneous space
G/P
is isomorphic to
Gr(r|s,m|n)
. The underlying algebraic variety is a product of two classical Grassmannians

Gr(r|m)\times Gr(s|n)
. If
p\in Gr(r|s,m|n)
is the point with stabilizer
P
then
the tangent space
T_{p}Gr(r|s,m|n)
can be identified with
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p}
. This immediately implies the formulas for dimension and superdimension of
Gr(r|s,m|n)
:

(5.1)

\operatorname{dim}Gr(r|s,m|n)=(r(m-r)+s(n-s)|r(n-s)+s(m-r)),

(5.2)

\operatorname{sdim}Gr(r|s,m|n)=(r-s)((m-r)-(n-s)).

Recall that
G
has a compact real form
\mathcal{U}=U(m|n)
, the unitary supergroup. The Lie algebra

\mathfrak{u}(m|n)
is the set of fixed points of antilinear involution
\theta
defined by

\theta\left(\begin{matrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}-\bar{A}^{t}&\mathbf{i}\bar{C}^{t}\\
\mathbf{i}\bar{B}^{t}&-\bar{D}^{t}\end{matrix}\right).

Let
\mathcal{K}:=\mathcal{U}\cap K=\mathcal{U}\cap P\simeq U(r|s)\times U(m-r|n-s)
.

Proposition 5.8
.

The unitary supergroup
\mathcal{U}
acts transitively on the supergrassmannian
Gr(r|s,m|n)
and we have an isomorphism of real supermanifolds

Gr(r|s,m|n)\simeq\mathcal{U}/\mathcal{K}.

Furthermore,
Gr(r|s,m|n)
has a unique up to normalization
\mathcal{U}
-invariant volume form.

Proof.

The first assertion follows from the corresponding classical fact for the underlying manifolds

Gr(r|m)\times Gr(s|n)\simeq(U(m)/(U(r)\times U(m-r))\times(U(n)/(U(s)\times U(n-s))

and comparison of odd dimensions of
\mathcal{U}/\mathcal{K}
and
G/P
.

To prove the second assertion we consider the point
p\in Gr(r|s,m|n)
which is stabilized by
\mathcal{K}
. The tangent space
T_{p}Gr(r|s,m|n)
is a
\mathcal{K}
-module. One can identify it
with the space
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}^{r|s},\mathbb{C}^{m-r|n-s})
. In this way a tangent vector is a
(r|s)\times(m-r|n-s)
-matrix
y
. The real part of the Hermitian form

H(y_{1},y_{2})=\operatorname{str}\theta(y_{1})y_{2}
defines a non-degenerate supersymmetric bilinear form
B
on
T_{p}Gr(r|s,m|n)
considered as a supervector space over
\mathbb{R}
. Since
\mathcal{K}
is connected it is clear
that
\mathcal{K}\subset SL(T_{p}Gr(r|s,m|n))
and hence preserves a volume form
\omega_{p}
on
T_{p}Gr(r|s,m|n)
. Then using a standard translation argument,
\omega_{p}
induces a unique invariant form on
Gr(r|s,m|n)

∎

We choose an orientation
Gr(r|s,m|n)_{0}
via its complex structure, i.e. so that for
z=x+iy
the basis
dx,dy
is positively oriented; similarly choose an orientation on
T_{p}Gr(r|s,m|n)_{\overline{1}}
according to its complex structure.

If we let
\operatorname{dim}_{\mathbb{R}}Gr(r|s,m|n)=(d_{1}|d_{2})
, then choosing local, positive oriented coordinates
u_{1},\dots,u_{d_{1}},\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{d_{2}}
, we normalize
\omega
so that in these local coordinates

\omega=\sqrt{|\operatorname{Ber}B|}\frac{dx_{1}\cdots dx_{d_{1}}}{d\xi_{1}\cdots d\xi_{d_{2}}},

where
B
denotes the matrix form of the metric in our chosen local coordinate system.

Definition 5.9
.

With the above chosen volume form
\omega
on
Gr(r|s,m|n)
, we set

V(r|s,m|n)=\int\limits_{Gr(r|s,m|n)}\omega.

Observe that we have
V(r|s,m|n)=V(s|r,n|m)
.

5.4.
Orientation subtlety and volume relationship

Over the real supergroup
\mathcal{U}(m|n)
there is an obvious identification of homogeneous supervarieties
Gr(r|s,m|n)\cong Gr(m-r|n-s,m|n)
; however over
GL(m|n)
these spaces are
not
isomorphic. Indeed the corresponding parabolics will not be conjugate in general. However, the antilinear involution
\theta
takes one parabolic to the other, inducing an isomorphism of real supervarieties

\Theta:Gr(r|s,m|n)\cong Gr(m-r|n-s,m|n)

which is
\mathcal{U}(m|n)
-equivariant.

This subtle point is important because the orientation chosen on
Gr(r|s,m|n)
comes from the complex structure induced as a
GL(m|n)
homogeneous space. Thus
\Theta
will not in general take the chosen orientation on
Gr(r|s,m|n)
to the chosen orientation on
Gr(m-r|n-s,m|n)
. Since
\theta
is acting by complex conjugate on coordinates, the orientation on the even part will be reversed
\operatorname{dim}_{\mathbb{C}}Gr(r|s,m|n)_{0}
times. On the other hand the invariant form
\omega
will change by a sign of
(-1)^{\operatorname{dim}_{\mathbb{C}}Gr(r|s,m|n)_{0}+\operatorname{dim}_{\mathbb{C}}Gr(r|s,m|n)_{1}}
. Therefore the volume will change by
(-1)^{\operatorname{dim}_{\mathbb{C}}Gr(r|s,m|n)_{1}}
, giving:

Lemma 5.10
.

V(r|s,m|n)=(-1)^{r(n-s)+s(m-r)}V(m-r|n-s,m|n).

5.5.
Computation of volumes

In this section we seek to show that:

Theorem 5.11
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=Gr(r|s,m|n)
. Then the integral

\int_{\mathcal{M}}\omega\neq 0
if and only if
\operatorname{sdim}\mathcal{M}\geq 0
.

Remark 5.12
.

Note that in the case
\operatorname{sdim}\mathcal{M}=0
, the normalization of
\omega
is independent of the choice of invariant form
B
that we made.

Proposition 5.13
.

Assume that
\operatorname{sdim}\mathcal{M}<0
. Then
\int_{\mathcal{M}}\omega=0
.

Proof.

By Corollary
5.7
it suffices to construct an odd vector field
Q
satisfying conditions (2) and (3) with
Z(Q)=\emptyset
.
However here we have
\mathfrak{u}_{\overline{1}}^{hom}=\mathfrak{u}_{\overline{1}}
and
\mathfrak{v}_{\overline{1}}^{hom}=\mathfrak{v}_{\overline{1}}
, where
\mathfrak{v}=\operatorname{Lie}\mathcal{K}
. The argument of Corollary
2.16
still works in our situation, and thus we find that
\mathcal{U}_{0}\cdot\mathfrak{v}_{\overline{1}}\neq\mathfrak{u}_{\overline{1}}^{hom}
, and we are done.
∎

Next we prove

Proposition 5.14
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=Gr(r|r,n|n)
. Then
\int_{\mathcal{M}}\omega=(2\pi)^{2(n-r)r}\binom{n}{r}
.

Proof.

Consider the standard basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{n},f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
in
\mathbb{C}^{n|n}
. Let
p\in\mathcal{M}
which corresponds to the subspace spanned by
e_{1},\dots,e_{r},f_{1},\dots,f_{r}
.
Choose
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
such that
a_{i}\pm a_{j}\neq 0
for any
i\neq j
. Let

Q=\left(\begin{matrix}0&A\\
A&0\end{matrix}\right),\quad A=(1+\mathbf{i})\operatorname{diag}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}).

Then
Q\in\operatorname{Lie}(\mathcal{K})
. Moreover, it is clear that
Q:T_{p}\mathcal{M}\to T_{p}\mathcal{M}
is an isomorphism.

Let us compute
\alpha(T_{p}(\mathcal{M}),\omega_{p})
. We use the identification

T_{p}\mathcal{M}\simeq\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}^{r|r},\mathbb{C}^{n-r|n-r})
and the
Q
-invariant decomposition

T_{p}\mathcal{M}=\bigoplus_{1\leq i\leq r,r<j\leq n}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}e_{i}\oplus\mathbb{C}f_{i},\mathbb{C}e_{j}\oplus\mathbb{C}f_{j}).

Define a basis
u_{ij}^{\pm},v_{ij}^{\pm}
in
T_{p}\mathcal{M}
by

u_{ij}^{\pm}=e_{i}\otimes e_{j}^{*}\pm f_{i}\otimes f_{j}^{*},\quad v_{ij}^{\pm}=f_{i}\otimes e_{j}^{*}\pm e_{i}\otimes f_{j}^{*}.

Then we have

Qu_{ij}^{\pm}=(1+i)(a_{i}\mp a_{j})v_{ji}^{\pm},\quad Qv_{ij}^{\pm}=(1+i)(a_{i}\pm a_{j})u_{ji}^{\pm}.

Further
\theta u_{ij}^{\pm}=-u_{ji}^{\pm}
,
\theta v_{ij}^{\pm}=\pm iv_{ji}^{\pm}
. Thus we have

\langle u_{ij}^{\pm},u_{ij}^{\mp}\rangle=-2,\ \ \ \langle iu_{ij}^{\pm},iu_{ij}^{\mp}\rangle=2.

\langle iv_{ij}^{\mp},v_{ij}^{\pm}\rangle=2.

Let
z_{ij}^{\pm}=x_{ij}^{\pm}\pm iy_{ij}^{\pm}
be the complex coordinate dual to
u_{ij}^{\pm}
, and let
\zeta_{ij}^{\pm}=\xi_{ij}^{\pm}+i\eta_{ij}^{\pm}
be the complex coordinate dual to
v_{ij}^{\pm}
. Then the bilinear form (as an element of
S^{2}(\mathcal{T}^{*}\mathcal{M}_{p})
) at this points looks as follows:

2\sum\limits_{i,j}\left(-dx_{ij}^{+}dx_{ij}^{-}+dy_{ij}^{+}dy_{ij}^{-}\right)-2\sum\limits_{i,j}\left(d\xi_{ij}^{+}d\eta_{ij}^{-}+d\xi_{ij}^{-}d\eta_{ij}^{+}\right).

Thus the volume form at this point is given by:

\frac{\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq r<j\leq n}dx_{ij}^{+}dy_{ij}^{+}dx_{ij}^{-}dy_{ij}^{-}}{\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq r<j\leq n}d\xi_{ij}^{+}d\eta_{ij}^{-}d\xi_{ij}^{+}d\eta_{ij}^{-}}=\frac{\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq r<j\leq n}dz_{ij}^{+}d\overline{z_{ij}^{+}}dz_{ij}^{-}d\overline{z_{ij}^{-}}}{\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq r<j\leq n}d\zeta_{ij}^{+}d\overline{\zeta_{ij}^{+}}d\zeta_{ij}^{-}d\overline{\zeta_{ij}^{-}}}

Therefore by Lemma
5.4
we find that:

\alpha(T_{p}\mathcal{M},\omega_{p})=\prod_{i\leq r,j>r}\frac{a_{i}+a_{j}}{a_{i}-a_{j}}\frac{a_{i}-a_{j}}{a_{i}+a_{j}}=1.

In particular we see that
\alpha(T_{p}\mathcal{M},\omega_{p})
does not depend on a choice of
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
.

Now consider
Q
as a vector field on
\mathcal{M}
and let us compute
Z(Q)
. As in the proof of Proposition
5.13

we should describe

S:=\{g\in\mathcal{U}_{0}\mid\operatorname{Ad}_{g}^{-1}(Q)\in\operatorname{Lie}\mathcal{K}\}.

Then
Z(Q)
is in bijection with
S/\mathcal{K}
. Note that the projection of
Q^{2}
to either factor of
GL(n)\times GL(n)=GL(n|n)_{0}
is a generic element of the Cartan subalgebra, so we can reduce the situation to
computation elements
w\in W/W_{K}
such that

(5.3)

w(Q)\in\operatorname{Lie}\mathcal{K},

where
W
is the Weyl group of
\mathcal{U}
and
W_{K}
is the Weyl group of
\mathcal{K}
.
Note that
W\simeq S_{n}\times S_{n}
. Let
w=(\sigma,\tau)
for some
\sigma,\tau\in S_{n}
. Then
w
satisfies (
5.3
) iff for each
i
either
\sigma(i),\tau(i)\in\{1,\dots,r\}
or

\sigma(i),\tau(i)\in\{r+1,\dots,n\}
. Therefore
w=(\sigma,\sigma)w^{\prime}
for some
w^{\prime}\in W_{K}
. Thus,
|Z(Q)|=\binom{n}{r}
. Moreover,
Z(Q)
is on the orbit of the diagonal
S_{n}
in
W
.
Furthermore,

\alpha(T_{wp}\mathcal{M},\omega_{wp})=\alpha(T_{p}\mathcal{M},\omega_{p})=1,

since
w^{-1}Q
has the same form as
Q
with permuted
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
.
Thus, Theorem
5.5
gives
\int_{\mathcal{M}}\omega=(2\pi)^{2r(n-r)}\binom{n}{r}
.
∎

Proposition 5.15
.

Let
m>n
. Then we have

V(m-n|0,m|n))=(-1)^{n(m-n)}V(n|n,m|n)=(2\pi)^{n(m-n)}.

Proof.

We show that
V(m-n|0,m|n)=(2\pi)^{n(m-n)}
, with the second equality following from Lemma
5.10
.

Choose
Q\in\operatorname{Lie}U(n|n)\subset\operatorname{Lie}{\mathcal{K}}
in the same way as in the proof of Lemma
5.14
. One can easily see
Q
has exactly one zero point
p
with stabilizer

\mathcal{K}
; it remains to compute
\alpha(T_{p}Gr(m-n|0,m|n),\omega_{p})
. In the first case,
T_{p}Gr(m-n|0,m|n)=\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{C}^{m-n|0},\mathbb{C}^{n|n})
, and we may take as basis

u_{ij}=e_{i}\otimes e_{j}^{*},\ \ \ \ v_{ij}=f_{i}\otimes e_{j}^{*},\ \ \ 1\leq i\leq n,\ n<j\leq m.

We see that

Qu_{ij}=(1+i)a_{i}v_{ij},\ \ \ \ Qv_{ij}=(1+i)a_{i}u_{ij}.

Thus following the same reasoning as in Lemma
5.14
, we find that

\alpha(T_{p}\mathcal{M},\omega_{p})=\prod\limits_{i,j}\frac{a_{i}}{a_{i}}=1.

∎

In what follows, we set
V(a,b):=V(a|0,b|0)=V(0|a,0|b)
for
0\leq a\leq b
, and use the notation
I(\mathcal{M},\omega):=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\omega
.

Corollary 5.16
.

Let
a\leq b\leq c
. Then

V(b|a,c|a)=\frac{V(b-a,c-a)V(a|a,c|a)}{V(a|a,b|a)}.

Proof.

Consider the partial flag variety
Fl(a|a,b|a,c|a)
with invariant form
\tilde{\omega}
. We use the double bundle

Gr(b|a,c|a)\xleftarrow{Gr(a|a,b|a)}Fl(a|a,b|a,c|a)\xrightarrow{Gr(b-a,c-a)}Gr(a|a,c|a).

The fibers are marked on the arrows. By
\mathcal{U}
-equivariance we can choose an invariant volume form
\tilde{\omega}
on

Fl(a|a,b|a,c|a)
such that

\tilde{\omega}=\omega_{f}\omega_{b}=\omega^{\prime}_{f}\omega^{\prime}_{b},

where
\omega_{f}
( respectively,
\omega^{\prime}_{f}
) are is a volume form on the fiber of the left (respectively, right) bundle and
\omega_{b}
(respectively,
\omega^{\prime}_{b}
) is a volume form on the base of the left
(respectively, right) bundle.

Using the right bundle we get

I({Fl(a|a,b|a,c|a)},\tilde{\omega})=I(Gr(b-a,c-a),\omega^{\prime}_{f})I(Gr(a|a,c|a),\omega^{\prime}_{b}).

Applying now the left bundle we obtain

I({Fl(a|a,b|a,c|a)},\tilde{\omega})=I({Gr(a|a,b|a)},\omega_{f})I({Gr(b|a,c|a)},\omega_{b}).

That gives the desired formula.
∎

Proposition 5.17
.

Assume that
\operatorname{sdim}Gr(r|s,m|n)\geq 0
and
r\geq s
then

V(r|s,m|n)=(-1)^{(r-s)(n-s)}\frac{V(s|s,n|n)V(n|n,m|n)V(r-s,m-n)}{V(s|s,r|s)V(n-s|n-s,m-r|n-s)}.

Proof.

We start with the case
r=s
and use again the same method as in the proof of Corollary
5.16
and we use the same notations.
Note that
m>n
. Consider the partial flag variety
Fl(s|s,n|n,m|n)
and the double bundle

Gr(s|s,m|n)\xleftarrow{Gr(n-s|n-s,m-s|n-s)}Fl(s|s,n|n,m|n)\xrightarrow{Gr(s|s,n|n)}Gr(n|n,m|n).

Then by Proposition
5.14
and Proposition
5.15
we obtain

I({Fl(s|s,n|n,m|n)},\tilde{\omega})=V(s|s,n|n)V(n|n,m|n)\neq 0.

From the left bundle we get

I({Fl(s|s,n|n,m|n)},\tilde{\omega})=V(n-s|n-s,m-s|n-s)V(s|s,m|n).

That gives

V(s|s,m|n)=\frac{V(s|s,n|n)V(n|n,m|n)}{V(n-s|n-s,m-s|n-s)}.

For the general statement consider the double bundle

Gr(r|s,m|n)\xleftarrow{Gr(s|s,r|s)}Fl(s|s,r|s,m|n)\xrightarrow{Gr(r-s|0,m-s|n-s)}Gr(s|s,m|n).

Then

I({Fl(s|s,r|s,m|n)},\tilde{\omega})=V(r-s|0,m-s|n-s)V(s|s,m|n).

On the other hand, from the left bundle

I({Fl(s|s,r|s,m|n)},\tilde{\omega})=V(s|s,r|s)V(r|s,m|n),

and using Lemma
5.10
we get

V(r|s,m|n)=(-1)^{(r-s)(n-s)}\frac{V(s|s,n|n)V(n|n,m|n)V(m-r|n-s,m-s|n-s)}{V(s|s,r|s)V(n-s|n-s,m-s|n-s)}.

Using Corollary
5.16
for
a=n-s,b=m-r,c=m-s
we have

V(m-r|n-s,m-s|n-s)=\frac{V(r-s,m-n)V(n-s|n-s,m-s|n-s)}{V(n-s|n-s,m-r|n-s)},

and substitution gives the desired formula.
∎

Corollary 5.18
.

Assume that
r\geq s
and
\operatorname{sdim}Gr(r|s,m|n)\geq 0
. Then

\int_{Gr(r|s,m|n)}\omega=(-1)^{s(m+n+r+s)}\binom{n}{s}(2\pi)^{A}\int_{Gr(r-s,m-n)}\omega,

where
A=(m-r)s+(n-s)r=\operatorname{dim}Gr(r|s,m|n)_{1}
.

Now Corollary
5.18
and Proposition
5.13
imply Theorem
5.11
.

5.6.
The volume of
Q
-Grassmannian

In this subsection we assume that
G=Q(n)
and
P
is the parabolic subgroup with the Levi subgroup
K=Q(r)\times Q(n-r)
. The homogeneous space
QGr(r,n)=G/P
is called a
Q
-Grassmannian. It represents the functor
of all
r|r
-dimensional subspaces in
\mathbb{C}^{n|n}
invariant under the fixed odd linear operator
F:\mathbb{C}^{n|n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n|n}
.
As in the case of
GL
the complex quasireductive group
Q(n)
has a compact form
\mathcal{U}=UQ(n)
and the Lie algebra of
\mathcal{U}
is the set of fixed of the involution
\theta
restricted to
\mathfrak{q}(n)
defined in the previous section.
It is well defined as
\mathfrak{q}(n)\subset\mathfrak{gl}(n|n)
is
\theta
-stable. We set
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{U}\cap K\simeq UQ(r)\times UQ(n-r)
. Here is an analogue of Proposition
5.8
.

Proposition 5.19
.

There is an isomorphism
QGr(r,n)\simeq G/P\simeq\mathcal{U}/\mathcal{K}
. Furthermore, there exists an unique up to normalization
\mathcal{U}
-invariant volume form
\omega
on
\mathcal{M}:=QGr(r,n)
.

Proof.

The first assertion can be proven in the exact same way as Proposition
5.8
. To prove the existence of invariant form let us consider a point
p\in\mathcal{M}
with stabilizer
\mathcal{K}
. There is an
obvious identification of the tangent space
T_{p}\mathcal{M}
with
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}[F]}(\mathbb{C}^{r|r},\mathbb{C}^{n-r|n-r})
. Note that in contrast with the case of undecorated supergrassmannians the
\mathcal{K}
-module

T_{p}\mathcal{M}
is not self-dual. However, since
\mathcal{K}
admits no nontrivial characters, we have that
\mathcal{K}\subset SL(T_{p}\mathcal{M})
. It will be useful to choose a precise coordinate system in
T_{p}\mathcal{M}
. If we represent a tangent vector by a matrix of the form

\left(\begin{matrix}Z&W\\
W&Z\end{matrix}\right)
where
Z,W
are
r\times(n-r)
-matrices, then

\omega_{p}=\prod_{i\leq r,j>r}\frac{dz_{ij}d\bar{z}_{ij}}{dw_{ij}d\bar{w}_{ij}}.

∎

Proposition 5.20
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=QGr(n,r)
. Then
\int_{\mathcal{M}}\omega=(2\pi)^{2r(n-r)}C(r,n)
, where

C(r,n)=\begin{cases}\binom{m}{l}\ \text{if}\ n=2m,r=2l,n=2m+1,r=2l+1,2l\\
0\ \ \ \ \text{if}\ n=2m,r=2l+1\end{cases}.

In particular,
\int_{\mathcal{M}}\omega\neq 0
if and only if
r(n-r)
is even.

Proof.

The idea of the proof is to use again the Schwarz–Zaboronsky formula for a suitably chosen odd vector field. We observe that
Q
constructed in the proof of proposition

5.14
is an element of
\operatorname{Lie}UQ(n)
via the embedding
UQ(n)\subset U(n|n)
. It is straightforward to check that
Z(Q)
has
\binom{n}{r}
points and all these points are obtained from the point

p
by the action of the Weyl group
S_{n}
. Every such point represents the coordinate subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{n|n}
with basis
u_{i_{1}},\dots,u_{i_{r}},v_{i_{1}},\dots,v_{i_{r}}
if
u_{1},\dots,u_{n},v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
is the standard basis.
Thus, we enumerate the points of
Z(Q)
by
r
element subsets
S
of
\{1,\dots,n\}
and use the notation
p_{S}
. Choosing exactly half of the basis
u_{ij}^{+},v_{ij}^{+}
defined in the proof Proposition
5.14
we obtain

\alpha(S):=\alpha(T_{p_{S}}\mathcal{M})=\prod_{i\in S,j\notin S}\frac{a_{i}+a_{j}}{a_{i}-a_{j}}.

Thus, we have reduced the proof of the theorem to computation of the expression

C(r,n):=\sum_{|S|=r}\alpha(S).

First, note that
C(r,n)
is a symmetric rational function in
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
of homogeneous degree
0
with denominator given by the Vandermonde determinant. That actually means that it is a constant which does not depend on

a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
as it should be by Theorem
5.5
.
Next, the substitution
a_{n}=0
gives the recursions:

C(r,n)=C(r,n-1)+(-1)^{n-r}C(r-1,n-1),\quad C(r,n)=(-1)^{r(n-r)}C(n-r,n),\quad C(1,2)=0.

From this one may show that
C(r,n)
has the desired values.
∎

6.
Splitting subgroups for
GL(m|n)
and
Q(n)

Theorem 6.1
.

(a) Let
G=GL(m|n)
,
K=GL(r|s)\times GL(m-r|n-s)
. Then
K
is a splitting subgroup of
G
if and only if
\operatorname{sdim}Gr(r|s,m|n)\geq 0
.

(b) Let
G=Q(n)
,
K=Q(r)\times Q(n-r)
. Then
K
is a splitting subgroup of
G
if and only if
r(n-r)
is even.

Proof.

If
\operatorname{sdim}Gr(r|s,m|n)<0
or
r(n-r)
is odd, the necessary condition for
K
does not hold. Therefore it remains to show that if
\operatorname{sdim}Gr(r|s,m|n)\geq 0
or
r(n-r)
is even the trivial
G
-submodule splits in

\mathbb{C}[G/K]
. We use unitary trick.
Consider the embedding
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{U}/\mathcal{K}\to G/K
as the closed
\mathcal{U}
-orbit of
p\in G/K
with stabilizer
K
. Define the map
I:\mathbb{C}[G/K]\to\mathbb{C}
by

I(f):=\int_{\mathcal{M}}f\omega.

Note that
I
is
\mathcal{U}
-invariant and hence
G
-invariant.
Theorem
5.11
and Theorem
5.20
imply
I(1)\neq 0
. Therefore
\mathbb{C}
splits.
∎

Corollary 6.2
.

(a) Let
G=GL(m|n)
with defect
d=\min(m,n)
, then the defect subgroup
D\simeq SL(1|1)^{d}
is splitting in
G
and hence any subgroup of
G
containing
D
is splitting.

(b) Let
G=Q(2d)
(respectively,
Q(2d+1)
) then the subgroup
Q(2)^{d}
(respectively,
Q(2)^{d}\times Q(1)
) is splitting.

Proof.

For (b) we just use Theorem
6.1
and transitivity Corollary
2.7
(2). For (a) assume that
d=n
, then by the same argument
GL(1|1)^{d}\times GL(m-n)
is splitting.
Furthermore,
H:=GL(1|1)^{d}
is splitting in
GL(1|1)^{d}\times GL(m-n)
. Therefore
H
is splitting in
G
. Finally
D
is splitting in
H
by Lemma
2.11
.
∎
