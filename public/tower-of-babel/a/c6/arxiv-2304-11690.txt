On algebras of double cosets of symmetric groups
with respect to Young subgroups

Abstract




We consider the subalgebra

\Delta
in the group algebra of the symmetric group

G=S_{n_{1}+\dots+n_{\nu}}
consisting of all functions invariant
with respect to left and right shifts by elements of the Young subgroup

H:=S_{n_{1}}\times\dots\times S_{n_{\nu}}
.
We discuss structure constants of the algebra

\Delta
and construct an algebra with continuous parameters
n_{1}

extrapolating algebras
\Delta
, it can be also can be rewritten as
an asymptotic algebra as
n_{j}\to\infty
(for fixed
\nu
).
We show that there is a natural map from the Lie algebra of the group of pure braids
to

\Delta

(and therefore this Lie algebra acts in spaces of multiplicities
of the quasiregular representation of the group
G
in functions
on
G/H
).

1. Introduction.

Let

G
be a locally compact group,
K\subset G
its compact subgroup.
Consider the corresponding Hecke–Iwahori algebra,
i.e, the convolution algebra

\mathcal{M}(G,K)
of complex-valued compactly supported measures on
G
, which are
invariant with respect to left and right shifts by elements of
K
(see,
e.g.,
[
1
]
). We can regard such measures as measures on the space
of double cosets

K\backslash G/K
.

It is easy to see that for any unitary representation of the group

G
the algebra

\mathcal{M}(G,K)
acts in the space of all

K
-invariant vectors. For this reason, Hecke-Iwahori algebras are a tool for
investigation of
representations of groups. They are used in numerous contexts
by different reasons starting work by Gelfand
[
2
]
, who introduced the notion of spherical subgroups and showed that the phenomenon
of sphericity is related to commutativity of an algebra

\mathcal{M}(G,K)
. Such algebras can be interesting objects by themselves and
can live by their own lives as the Hecke algebra, the affine Hecke algebra
(both objects were introduced by Iwahori, see

[
3
]
), the Yokonuma algebra
[
4
]
.

On the other hand, such algebras can be complicated objects
in cases that a priori seem simple. For instance, if

G=\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
,
K=\mathrm{SO}(2)
(or, more generally
G
is a real simple Lie group
of rank 1 and
K
is the maximal compact subgroup),
then the product in
\mathcal{M}(G,K)
is determined
by a hypergeometric kernel of type
{}_{2}F_{1}
, see, e.g.,
[
5
]
, Sect. 7.
In this case

\mathcal{M}(G,K)
is an interesting useful object, see Flensted-Jensen, Koornwinder

[
6
]
, but a generalization of the mentioned hypergeometric formula to classical groups of rank
>1
does not seem to be known
(on some continuations of this topic, see

[
7
]
,
[
8
]
). An example of a complicated object admitting nontrivial
descriptions is the algebra of conjugacy classes of the symmetric group

S_{n}
, see Ivanov, Kerov

[
9
]
; on the algebra of conjugacy classes of
\operatorname{GL}
over a finite field,
see Méliot
[
10
]
.

Olshanski
[
11
]
discovered that often a pass to an infinite limit
leads to degenerations of algebras
\mathcal{M}(G_{n},K_{n})
(as
\mathcal{M}(\operatorname{U}(n),\mathrm{O}(n))
or

\mathcal{M}(S_{2n},S_{n}\times S_{n})
);
on the set
K_{\infty}\backslash G_{\infty}/K_{\infty}
itself a
natural multiplication can arise. This, in its turn, creates a tool
for investigation of representations of infinite-dimensional groups.
The author (e.g.,
[
12
]
) observed that such multiplications
are a mass phenomenon and admit explicit descriptions in a wide generality
(including descriptions of spaces
K_{\infty}\backslash G_{\infty}/K_{\infty}
).
The present note together with
[
13
]
is a kind of an experiment –
an attempt of a descent from a relatively understandable infinite-dimensional
limit to prelimit objects. Clearly, our object is very complicated, we choose it
since in this case a limit picture seems the most transparent, see

[
15
]
,
[
12
]
, §8.

2. Algebra of double cosets with respect to a Young subgroup.

By
S_{N}
we denote the group of all permutations of a set
\Omega
with
N

elements. Let

N=n_{1}+n_{2}+\dots+n_{\nu}.

Split
\Omega
into a disjoint union
\Omega=\coprod P_{j}
,
where a number
\#P_{j}
of elements of a set
P_{j}
is
n_{j}
.
Denote by

Y\{n_{j}\}
the corresponding
Young subgroup
, i.e., the subgroup
in
S_{N}
consisting of permutations sending each set
P_{j}
to itself.
Obviously,
Y\{n_{j}\}\simeq\prod_{j}S_{n_{j}}
. To each double coset
Y\{n_{j}\}\cdot g\cdot Y\{n_{j}\}
we assign
an integer matrix

a_{ij}=\#\bigl(g(P_{i})\cap P_{j}\bigr).

It is easy to show (see, e.g.,
[
14
]
, Sect. 1.3), that the matrix
\{a_{ij}\}

satisfies conditions

(1)

\sum\nolimits_{i}a_{ij}=n_{j},\quad\sum\nolimits_{j}a_{ij}=n_{i},\quad a_{ij}\geqslant 0,

and a double coset is uniquely determined by such matrix.
We will use three notations for this double coset:

(1)
\xi\{a_{ij}\}
;

(2)
\xi\bigl(\sum a_{ij}E_{ij}\bigr)
(where
E_{ij}

is a matrix with unit at
ij
-th place,
and all other matrix elements are 0);

(3)
\xi[A]
, where
A
is an explicitly written matrix.

Obviously, the number of elements of a double coset

\xi\{a_{ij}\}
is

(2)

\mu\{a_{ij}\}=\frac{\prod(n_{j}!)^{2}}{\prod a_{ij}!}.

Remark.

The number of double cosets (i.e., the number of integer matrices

\{a_{ij}\}
satisfying
(
1
)) is a complicated combinatorial function,
it was examined, in particular, in

[
16
]
,
[
17
]
.

\boxtimes

Consider the group algebra

{\mathbb{C}}[S_{N}]
and its element

\Pi:=\frac{1}{\prod n_{j}!}\sum_{h\in Y\{n_{j}\}}h.

Clearly,
\Pi^{2}=\Pi
, and the subspace

\Delta\{n_{j}\}:=\Pi\,{\mathbb{C}}[S_{N}]\,\Pi\subset\Pi[S_{N}]

coincides with the algebra of functions on
S_{N}
invariant with respect to left and right shifts by elements of

Y\{n_{j}\}
. A natural basis in this algebra consists of elements

\Xi\{a_{ij}\}=\frac{1}{\mu\{a_{ij}\}}\sum_{g\in\xi\{a_{ij}\}}g.

For basis elements

\Xi
we use three types of notations
similarly to notations (1)–(3)
for

\xi
.

Proposition 1
.

A product of basis elements of the algebra

\Delta\{n_{j}\}

is

(3)

\Xi\{a_{ij}\}\cdot\Xi\{b_{jk}\}=\\
=\sum_{\{c_{ik}\}}\frac{\prod a_{ij}!\prod b_{jk}!}{\prod n_{j}!}\Biggl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{t_{ijk}\}:\,t_{ijk}\geqslant 0,\\
\sum\limits_{i}t_{ijk}=b_{jk},\,\sum\limits_{j}t_{ijk}=c_{ik},\,\sum\limits_{k}t_{ijk}=a_{ij}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\prod_{ijk}t_{ijk}!}\Biggr)\,\Xi\{c_{ik}\}.

Proof.
Let us evaluate the number of pairs

g\in\xi\{a_{ij}\}
,
h\in\xi\{b_{jk}\}
such that

hg\in\xi\{c_{ik}\}
. Set

T_{ijk}:=g(P_{i})\cap P_{j}\cap h^{-1}(P_{k}),\qquad t_{ijk}:=\#T_{ijk}.

Fix numbers

t_{ijk}
. They satisfy relations

(4)

\displaystyle a_{ij}=\#\bigl(g(P_{i})\cap P_{j}\bigr)=\#\bigl(\coprod_{k}T_{ijk}\bigr)=\sum_{k}t_{ijk};

(5)

\displaystyle b_{jk}=\#\bigl(h(P_{j})\cap P_{k}\bigr)=\#\bigl(\coprod_{i}T_{ijk}\bigr)=\sum_{i}t_{ijk};

(6)

\displaystyle c_{ik}=\#\bigl(hg(P_{i})\cap P_{k}\bigr)=\#\bigl(\coprod_{j}T_{ijk}\bigr)=\sum_{j}t_{ijk}.

By the construction,

P_{j}=\coprod_{ik}T_{ijk}
, a collection of such partitions
can be chosen by

(7)

\prod_{j}\frac{n_{j}!}{\prod_{ik}t_{ijk}!}

ways. On the other hand,

P_{i}=\coprod_{jk}g^{-1}T_{ijk}
, a collection of such partitions
can be chosen by

\prod_{i}\frac{n_{i}!}{\prod_{jk}t_{ijk}!},

ways, this number coincides with
(
7
). A collection of bijections

g:g^{-1}T_{ijk}\to T_{ijk}
can be chosen by
\prod_{ijk}t_{ijk}!

ways. Similarly, we choose sets

hT_{ijk}
and bijections

h:T_{ijk}\to T_{ijk}
. Thus, the desired number of pairs

g
,
h
with fixed collection
t_{ijk}

is

\Bigl(\frac{\prod_{j}n_{j}!}{\prod_{ijk}t_{ijk}!}\Bigr)^{3}\prod_{ijk}(t_{ijk}!)^{2}.

To get a coefficient
(
3
), we must sum these numbers over all collections

t_{ijk}
satisfying
(
4
)–(
6
) and divide by a normalization
factor
\mu\{a_{ij}\}\,\mu\{b_{jk}\}
, see (
2
).

\square
.

Example:
\nu=2
.
Consider the case
\nu=2
,
N=n_{1}+n_{2}
.
It is known that in this case the algebra

\Delta(n_{1},n_{2})
is commutative and the pair
S_{N}\supset S_{n_{1}}\times S_{n_{2}}
is spherical, see, e.g.,

[
18
]
, §6.2, spherical functions are expressed in terms
of Hahn hypergeometric orthogonal polynomials of type
{}_{3}F_{2}(1)
.
The basis of
\Delta(n_{1},n_{2})
consists of functions

\varphi_{a}=\Xi\begin{bmatrix}n_{1}-a&a\\
a&n_{2}-a\end{bmatrix},\qquad\text{where $a=0$, $1$, \ldots, $\min(n_{1},n_{2})$.}

Products have a form

\varphi_{a}\varphi_{b}=\sum s_{ab}^{c}\varphi_{c}
, where
the structure constants are given by the formula

s_{ab}^{c}=\frac{(a!\,b!)^{2}(n_{1}-a)!\,(n_{2}-a)!\,(n_{1}-b)!\,(n_{2}-b)!}{n_{1}!\,n_{2}!}\times\\
\times\!\!\!\!\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma,\tau:\\
0\leqslant\sigma,\tau\leqslant\min(a,b),\\
\sigma+\tau=a+b-c,\\
n_{1}-a-b+\tau\geqslant 0,\\
n_{2}-a-b+\sigma\geqslant 0\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\frac{1}{\sigma!\,\tau!\,(a-\sigma)!(b-\sigma)!\,(a-\tau)!\,(b-\tau)!\,(n_{1}-a-b+\tau)!(n_{2}-a-b+\sigma)!},

in (
3
) we denote
t_{121}=\sigma
,
t_{212}=\tau
.
Inequalities for

\sigma
,
\tau
can be omitted if to assume that a factorial of a negative
integer is
\infty
. Substituting
\tau=a+b-c-\sigma

we come to summation in
\sigma
. The summands take the form

\frac{1}{\sigma!(a\!+\!b\!-\!c\!-\!\sigma)!(a\!-\!\sigma)!(b\!-\!\sigma)!(c\!-\!b\!+\!\sigma)!(c\!-\!a\!+\!\sigma)!\,(n_{1}-\!c\!-\!\sigma)!(n_{2}-\!a\!-\!b\!+\!\sigma)!}.

We transform factorials as

(p+\sigma)!=p!(p+1)_{\sigma}
,

(q-\sigma)!=(-1)^{\sigma}q!/(-q)_{\sigma}
. If we perform this operation
for all factorials in the denominator (this is possible if

a+b\leqslant n_{2}
), then we come to

s_{a,b}^{c}=\frac{a!\,b!\,(n_{1}-a)!\,(n_{2}-a)!\,(n_{1}-b)!\,(n_{2}-b)!}{n_{1}!\,n_{2}!\,(n_{1}-c)!\,(n_{2}-a-b)!\,(a+b-c)!\,(c-a)!\,(c-b)!}\times\\
\times{}_{4}F_{3}\left[\begin{matrix}-a,-b,c-a-b,c-n_{1}\\
c-b+1,c-a+1,n_{2}-a-b+1\end{matrix};\,1\right],

where
{}_{4}F_{3}
is a generalized hypergeometric function
(in fact the sum is finite, notice that all parameters of the upper row
of
{}_{4}F_{3}
are integers
\leqslant 0
).
If
a+b>n_{2}
, then we pass to a summation in
\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma-a-b+n_{2}

and again come to an expression of the form

{}_{4}F_{3}(1)
.

In spite of simplicity of this case, the identity

\sum_{\gamma}s^{\gamma}_{ab}s^{\delta}_{\gamma c}=\sum_{\gamma}s^{\delta}_{a\gamma}s^{\gamma}_{bc}
for structure constants providing the associativity
of the algebra
\Delta(n_{1},n_{2})
is an identity of an unusual type for functions

{}_{4}F_{3}(1)
.

3. The homomorphism of the Lie algebra of braid group.

Recall that according Maltsev

[
19
]
any finitely generated nilpotent group without torsion
can be canonically embedded to a nilpotent Lie group as a cocompact lattice,
and therefore any such discrete group
determines a Lie algebra. The construction admits an extension
to residually nilpotent groups

[
20
]
, in particular it is valid for the group
of pure braids. The corresponding Lie algebra
\mathrm{Br}_{\nu}

was constructed by Kohno
[
21
]
(see another way of construction
in
[
22
]
), it has generators

L_{ij}
, where
i,j=1
, …,
\nu
and
i\neq j
, such that
L_{ij}=L_{ji}
;
relations (’infinitesimal braid relations’) are

\displaystyle[L_{ij},L_{jk}+L_{ki}]=0,\qquad\text{where $i$, $j$, $k$ are pairwise distinct;}

\displaystyle[L_{ij},L_{kl}]=0,\qquad\text{where $i$, $j$, $k$, $l$ are pairwise distinct.}

Note (see, e.g.,
[
22
]
) that elements
L_{\nu 1}
,
L_{\nu 2}
, …,
L_{\nu(\nu-1)}

generate a free Lie algebra
\mathrm{Free}_{\nu-1}
,
it is an ideal in

\mathrm{Br}_{\nu}
.
The algebra
\mathrm{Br}_{\nu}
is a semi-direct product
of the subalgebra

\mathrm{Br}_{\nu-1}
generated by
L_{ij}
with
i,j<\nu
,
and the ideal
\mathrm{Free}_{\nu-1}
.

Kohno
[
23
]
,
[
24
]
also showed that if the algebra

\mathrm{Br}_{\nu}
acts in a finite dimensional linear space,
then we have in this space a representation of the group of pure braids,
this representation
arises from the Knizhnik–Zamolodchikov connection.
The algebra
\mathrm{Br}_{\nu}
is graded by positive degrees 1, 2, 3, …(degrees of generators are 1), this gives a possibility to define
the corresponding infinite-dimensional Lie group as a certain
’submanifold’ in a completed universal enveloping algebra of
\mathrm{Br}_{\nu}
;
there are different ways to define such completions.
In particular, there is a completion, which acts in all finite-dimensional
representations of
\mathrm{Br}_{\nu}
and contains the group of pure braides,
[
25
]
.

Many nontrivial actions of the Kohno algebra are known, see, e.g.,

[
26
]
–
[
28
]
,
[
25
]
, in particular it acts in various
spaces of multiplicities of representations. By the Frobenius duality,
the next theorem shows that

\mathrm{Br}_{\nu}
and the corresponding ’Lie group’ act in a natural way in spaces of multiplicities of the quasiregular representation of
S_{N}
in functions on
S_{N}/Y\{n_{i}\}
.

Let
i\neq j
. Consider the transposition
r_{ij}
,
exchanging an element of
P_{i}

and an element of
P_{j}
. Set

\mathfrak{r}_{ij}:=\Pi\,r_{ij}\,\Pi=\Xi\Bigl(\sum_{k:\,k\neq i,j}n_{k}E_{kk}+(n_{i}-1)E_{ii}+(n_{j}-1)E_{jj}+E_{ij}+E_{ji}\Bigr).

Theorem 2
.

Elements
\widetilde{\mathfrak{r}}_{ij}:=n_{i}n_{j}\,\mathfrak{r}_{ij}

satisfy relations

(8)

\displaystyle[\widetilde{\mathfrak{r}}_{ij},\widetilde{\mathfrak{r}}_{jk}+\widetilde{\mathfrak{r}}_{ik}]=0;

(9)

\displaystyle[\widetilde{\mathfrak{r}}_{ij},\widetilde{\mathfrak{r}}_{kl}]=0,\quad\text{if $i$, $j$, $k$, $l$
are pairwise distinct.}

Proof.
To verify (
8
) we without loss of generality
can assume

ijk=123
and
\nu=3
,

\mathfrak{r}_{23}\mathfrak{r}_{12}=\Xi\begin{bmatrix}n_{1}&0&0\\
0&n_{2}-1&1\\
0&1&n_{3}-1\end{bmatrix}\,\Xi\begin{bmatrix}n_{1}-1&1&0\\
1&n_{2}-1&0\\
0&0&n_{3}-1\end{bmatrix}=\\
=\frac{n_{2}-1}{n_{2}}\cdot\Xi\begin{bmatrix}n_{1}-1&1&0\\
1&n_{2}-2&1\\
0&1&n_{3}-1\end{bmatrix}+\frac{1}{n_{2}}\cdot\Xi\begin{bmatrix}n_{1}-1&0&1\\
1&n_{2}-1&0\\
0&1&n_{3}-1\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore,

[\mathfrak{r}_{23},\mathfrak{r}_{12}]=\frac{1}{n_{2}}\cdot\Xi\begin{bmatrix}n_{1}-1&0&1\\
1&n_{2}-1&0\\
0&1&n_{3}-1\end{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{n_{2}}\cdot\Xi\begin{bmatrix}n_{1}-1&1&0\\
0&n_{2}-1&1\\
1&0&n_{3}-1\end{bmatrix},

and this implies
(
8
).

To establish (
9
) it is sufficient to verify the identity for

ijkl=1234
and
\nu=4
. We have

\qquad\qquad\mathfrak{r}_{12}\mathfrak{r}_{34}=\Xi\begin{bmatrix}n_{1}-1&1&0&0\\
1&n_{2}-1&0&0\\
0&0&n_{3}-1&1\\
0&0&1&n_{4}-1\end{bmatrix}=\mathfrak{r}_{34}\mathfrak{r}_{12}.\qquad\qquad\square

4. Universalization of algebras
\Delta\{n_{j}\}
.

Fix
\nu
. Denote by
\mathcal{B}
the set of all integer
collections

\{a_{ij}\}
, where
i
,
j\leqslant\nu
and
i\neq j
,
satisfying conditions

a_{ij}\geqslant 0,\qquad\sum_{i:\,i\neq j}a_{ij}=\sum_{i:\,i\neq j}a_{ji}.

We define numbers

a^{*}_{jj}:=\sum_{i:\,i\neq j}a_{ji}=\sum_{i:\,i\neq j}a_{ij}.

We also introduce formal variables

\varepsilon_{1}
, …,
\varepsilon_{\nu}
.
Denote

(\!(p,q;\varepsilon_{j})\!):=\prod_{m=p}^{q-1}(1-m\varepsilon_{j}),\qquad\text{in particular,}\quad(\!(p,p;\varepsilon_{j})\!):=1.

For each collection

\{a_{ij}\}\in\mathcal{B}
we assign a formal basis element

{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{a_{ij}\}
.
Fix three such elements

{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{a_{ij}\}
,
{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{b_{jk}\}
,
{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{c_{ik}\}
.
Consider the set

\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}(\{a_{ij}\},\{b_{jk}\},\{c_{ik}\})

of all collections
\{t_{ijk}\}
of non-negative
integers, such that not all subscripts
i
,
j
,
k
coincide,
satisfying the conditions

(10)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{i}t_{ijk}

\displaystyle=b_{jk}\,\,\text{if}\,\,j\neq k;

(11)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{j}t_{ijk}

\displaystyle=c_{ik}\,\,\text{if}\,\,i\neq k;

(12)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{k}t_{ijk}

\displaystyle=a_{ij}\,\,\text{if}\,\,i\neq j,

and also

(13)

a_{jj}^{*}+\sum_{k:k\neq j}t_{jjk}=b_{jj}^{*}+\sum_{i:i\neq j}t_{ijj}=c_{jj}^{*}+\sum_{m:m\neq j}t_{jmj}=:t_{jjj}^{*}

(we denote three equal values (
13
) by
t_{jjj}^{*}
).
By these equalities,

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k:\,(j,k)\neq(i,i)}t_{ijk}=t^{*}_{iii};\,\sum_{i,k:\,(i,k)\neq(j,)}t_{ijk}=t^{*}_{jjj};\,\sum_{i,j:\,(i,j)\neq(k,k)}t_{ijk}=t^{*}_{kkk}.

Note that sets

\mathcal{T}(\{a_{ij}\},\{b_{jk}\},\{c_{ik}\})

are bounded in virtue of (
10
)–(
12
) and
t_{ijk}\geqslant 0
,
therefore they are finite.
Moreover, for fixed

\{a_{ij}\}
,
\{b_{jk}\}
the set

(14)

\bigcup\limits_{\{c_{ik}\}\in\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{T}(\{a_{ij}\},\{b_{jk}\},\{c_{ik}\}),

is finite.
Indeed, equalities
(
10
), (
12
) are sufficient for boundedness.
Therefore for fixed
\{a_{ij}\}
,
\{b_{jk}\}

only finite number of sets

\mathcal{T}(\{a_{ij}\},\{b_{jk}\},\{c_{ik}\})
is non-empty.

Let us define the following values (
structure constants
):

\mathfrak{s}_{\{a_{ij}\},\{b_{jk}\}}^{\{c_{ik}\}}=\\
=\sum_{\mathcal{T}}\frac{\prod\limits_{i,j:i\neq j}a_{ij}!\prod\limits_{j,k:j\neq k}b_{jk}!}{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j,k:\\
\text{$i\neq j$ or $i\neq k$, or $j\neq k$}\end{subarray}}t_{ijk}!}\prod_{j}\varepsilon_{j}^{a_{jj}^{*}+b_{jj}^{*}-t_{jjj}^{*}}\frac{(\!(a_{jj}^{*},t_{jjj}^{*};\varepsilon_{j})\!)(\!(b_{jj}^{*},t_{jjj}^{*};\varepsilon_{j})\!)}{(\!(0,t_{jjj}^{*};\varepsilon_{j})\!)}.

According the following lemma this expression is a well-defined
power series in

\varepsilon_{1}
, …,
\varepsilon_{\nu}
.

Lemma 3
.

a)

For any point
\in\mathcal{T}(\{a_{ij}\},\{b_{jk}\},\{c_{ik}\})
we have

a_{jj}^{*}+b_{jj}^{*}-t_{jjj}^{*}\geqslant 0
.

b)
If
a_{jj}^{*}+b_{jj}^{*}-t_{jjj}^{*}=0

for all
j
, then
a_{ij}+b_{ij}=c_{ij}
for all pairs with
i\neq j
.

Proof.

a^{*}_{jj}=\sum_{i:i\neq j}a_{ij}=\sum_{i:i\neq j}\sum_{k}t_{ijk}=t^{*}_{jjj}-\sum_{k:k\neq j}t_{jjk},\qquad b^{*}_{jj}=t^{*}_{jjj}-\sum_{i:i\neq j}t_{ijj}.

Therefore,

a_{jj}^{*}+b_{jj}^{*}-t_{jjj}^{*}=t^{*}_{jjj}-\sum_{k:k\neq j}t_{jjk}-\sum_{i:i\neq j}t_{ijj}=\sum_{i,k:i\neq j,k\neq j}t_{ijk}.

All summands in the right hand side are non-negative, therefore the sum is non-negative. This proves the statement a).

If for all
j
expressions

a_{jj}^{*}+b_{jj}^{*}-t_{jjj}^{*}
are zero,
then only nonzero
t_{\dots}

can be
t_{ijj}
or
t_{jjk}
. But in this case,
a_{ij}=\sum_{k}t_{ijk}=t_{ijj}
,

b_{ij}=\sum_{m}t_{mij}=t_{iij}
,

c_{ij}=\sum_{l}t_{ilj}=t_{ijj}+t_{iij}
, and this proves
the statement b).

\square

a)

b)

Set

Then

\mathfrak{s}_{\{a_{ij}\},\{b_{jk}\}}^{\{c_{ik}\}}(\{n_{j}^{-1}\})=0,\qquad\text{if
some
$c_{jj}^{*}>n_{j}$}.

Moreover, the algebra

\Delta^{\circ}\{n_{j}\}
with a basis
{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}^{\circ}\{a_{ij}\}
,
where
a_{jj}^{*}\leqslant n_{j}
and relations

{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}^{\circ}\{a_{ij}\}\,{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}^{\circ}\{b_{jk}\}=\sum_{\{c_{ik}\}}\mathfrak{s}_{\{a_{ij}\},\{b_{jk}\}}^{\{c_{ik}\}}(\{n_{j}^{-1}\})\,{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}^{\circ}\{c_{ik}\}

coincides with
\Delta\{n_{j}\}
.

Proof.
We write structure constants in formula
(
3
) as

\prod_{i,j:i\neq j}a_{ij}!\,\prod_{j,k:j\neq k}b_{jk}!\,\sum\biggl(\frac{1}{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j,k:\\
\text{$i\neq j$ or $i\neq k$, or $j\neq k$}\end{subarray}}t_{ijk}!}\cdot\prod_{j}\frac{a_{jj}!\,b_{jj}!}{n_{j}!\,t_{jjj}!}\biggr).

Next, we set
n_{j}-a_{jj}=:a_{jj}^{*}
,
1/n_{j}=\varepsilon_{j}

and transform the factor

\prod_{j}
as

\prod_{j}\frac{(n_{j}-a_{jj}^{*})!\,(n_{j}-b_{jj}^{*})!}{n_{j}!\,(n_{j}-t_{jjj}^{*})!}.

Further,

n_{j}!=n_{j}^{n_{j}}\cdot 1\cdot(1-\tfrac{1}{n_{j}})\cdot(1-\tfrac{2}{n_{j}})\dots\cdot(1-\tfrac{n_{j}-1}{n_{j}})=\\
=\varepsilon_{j}^{-n_{j}}1\cdot(1-\varepsilon_{j})(1-2\varepsilon_{j})\dots(1-(n_{j}-1)\varepsilon_{j})=\varepsilon_{j}^{-n_{j}}(\!(0,t_{jjj}^{*};\varepsilon_{j})\!)(\!(t_{jjj}^{*},n_{j};\varepsilon_{j})\!).

Similarly,

\displaystyle(n_{j}-t_{jjj}^{*})!

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{j}^{-n_{j}+t_{jjj}^{*}}(\!(t_{jjj}^{*},n_{j};\varepsilon_{j})\!);

\displaystyle(n_{j}-a_{jj}^{*})!

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{j}^{-n_{j}+a_{jjj}^{*}}(\!(a_{jj}^{*},t_{jjj}^{*};\varepsilon_{j})\!)(\!(t_{jjj}^{*},n_{j};\varepsilon_{j})\!).

Cancelling the fraction by
(\!(t_{jjj}^{*},n_{j};\varepsilon_{j})\!)^{2}
,
we come to the expression for
\mathfrak{s}_{\dots}^{\dots}
.

Next, let all

a_{ll}^{*}
,
b_{ll}^{*}\leqslant n_{l}
and some
t_{jjj}^{*}>n_{j}
.
Then each of three factors of the expression

\frac{(\!(a_{jj}^{*},t_{jjj}^{*};\varepsilon_{j})\!)(\!(b_{jj}^{*},t_{jjj}^{*};\varepsilon_{j})\!)}{(\!(0,t_{jjj}^{*};\varepsilon_{j})\!)}

contains
(1-n_{j}\varepsilon_{j})
, therefore the substitution
\varepsilon_{j}=1/n_{j}

gives 0. On the other hand, if some
t_{kkk}^{*}\leqslant n_{k}
,
then the factor

(1-n_{k}\varepsilon_{k})
is absent in the numerator and in the denominator,
therefore we have no pole at

\varepsilon_{k}=1/n_{k}
. Therefore, any summand, for which

t_{jjj}^{*}>n_{j}
for some
j
, vanishes.

If for the same

a_{ll}^{*}
,
b_{ll}^{*}
some
c_{jj}^{*}>n_{j}
, then for all summands of

\mathfrak{s}_{\dots}^{\dots}
we have
t_{jjj}^{*}\geqslant c_{jj}^{*}>n_{j}
, and therefore
all summands are 0.

Thus we proved the statement
b). Next, consider the algebra over the ring

{\mathbb{Q}}\bigl[\{\varepsilon_{j}\},\{(1-m\varepsilon_{j})^{-1}\}\bigr]

defined in the statement
a). Since the sets (
14
) are finite,
both sides of the desired identity (associativity)

({\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{a_{ij}\}\,{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{a_{jk}^{\prime}\})\,{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{a_{kl}^{\prime\prime}\}={\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{a_{ij}\}\,({\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{a_{jk}^{\prime}\}\,{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{a_{kl}^{\prime\prime}\})

expand in finite sums with rational coefficients
by the basis
{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{\cdot\}
. Substituting
\varepsilon_{j}=1/n_{j}
with
sufficiently large

n_{j}
we get an identity in the algebra
\Delta\{n_{j}\}
. Therefore,
the desired identity for coefficients

\in{\mathbb{Q}}\bigl[\{\varepsilon_{j}\},\{(1-m\varepsilon_{j})^{-1}\}\bigr]
also is fulfilled.
Therefore, expansions in series in
\varepsilon_{j}
also coincide.

\square

Remark.
Substituting
\varepsilon_{j}\mapsto 1/n_{j}

we get poles in some structure constants
\mathfrak{s}_{\dots}^{\dots}
(as shows the example below).
Therefore we do not
get a well-defined
infinite-dimensional

(at least for our normalization of basis elements).
But these poles do not appear under conditions (
15
) of
Theorem 4.b. Note also that after a substitution

,

\alpha_{j}\neq 1
,
\frac{1}{2}

5. The commutative limit and Poisson algebra.
Consider
the algebra

\Delta
as an algebra over
{\mathbb{Q}}[[\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{\nu}]]
.
Our algebra has a decreasing filtration in powers
of

\varepsilon
, …,
\varepsilon_{\nu}
. By Lemma

3
, the corresponding graded algebra

\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits(\Delta)
is commutative,
the product is given by the formula

{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{a_{ij}\}\,{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{b_{ij}\}={\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{a_{ij}+b_{ij}\}.

I.e., we get a commutative algebra over

{\mathbb{Q}}
with a basis
{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{a_{ij}\}
.
In fact this limit participates in considerations
of
[
15
]
,
[
12
]
, §8.

It is easy to write the next term of expansions in powers
of
\varepsilon_{j}
,

{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{a_{ij}\}\,{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{b_{ij}\}={\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{a_{ij}+b_{ij}\}+\\
+\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}\sum_{\alpha,\gamma}a_{\alpha j}b_{j\beta}\,{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\bigl(\sum_{i,k:i\neq k}(a_{ik}+b_{ik})E_{ik}+E_{\alpha\gamma}-E_{\alpha j}-E_{j\gamma}\bigr)+\sum_{j}O(\varepsilon_{j}),

where
\sum_{j}O(\varepsilon_{j})
denotes a formal series
in monomials

\prod_{j}\varepsilon_{j}^{m_{j}}
with
\sum m_{j}\geqslant 2
.

Therefore,

{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{a_{ij}\}\,{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{b_{ij}\}-{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{b_{ij}\}\,{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{a_{ij}\}=\\
=\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}\sum_{\alpha,\gamma}\bigl(a_{\alpha j}b_{j\beta}-a_{\beta j}b_{j\alpha}\bigr)\,{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\bigl(\sum_{i,k:i\neq k}(a_{ik}+b_{ik})E_{ik}+E_{\alpha\gamma}-E_{\alpha j}-E_{j\gamma}\bigr)+\sum_{j}O(\varepsilon_{j}).

In particular, assuming

\varepsilon_{1}=\dots=\varepsilon_{\nu}=\varepsilon
, we get a Poisson bracket
on the algebra
\mathop{\mathrm{gr}}\nolimits(\Delta)
:

\Bigl\{{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{a_{ij}\},{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{b_{ij}\}\Bigr\}:=\biggl(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Bigl({\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{a_{ij}\}\,{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{b_{ij}\}-{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{b_{ij}\}\,{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\{a_{ij}\}\Bigr)\biggr)\biggr|_{\varepsilon=0},

its explicit form is clear from the previous formula.
