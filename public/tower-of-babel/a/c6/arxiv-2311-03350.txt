Differentiable Cutting-plane Layers for Mixed-integer Linear Optimization
Gabriele Dragotto ID Stefan Clarke ID Jaime Fernández Fisac ID, Bartolomeo Stellato ID

Abstract
We consider the problem of solving a family of parametric mixed-integer linear optimization problems where some entries in the input data change. We introduce the concept of cutting-plane layer (CPL), i.e. , a differentiable cutting-plane generator mapping the problem data and previous iterates to cutting planes. We propose a CPL implementation to generate split cuts, and by combining several CPLs, we devise a differentiable cutting-plane algorithm that exploits the repeated nature of parametric instances. In an offline phase, we train our algorithm by updating the internal parameters controlling the CPLs, thus altering cut generation. Once trained, our algorithm computes, with predictable execution times and a fixed number of cuts, solutions with low integrality gaps. Preliminary computational tests show that our algorithm generalizes on unseen instances and captures underlying parametric structures.

Introduction

We consider the problem of optimizing a
parametric family
of
mixed-integer optimization problems (MIPs)
of the form

z^{\star}(\theta)=\begin{array}[t]{ll}\text{minimize}&c(\theta)^{T}x\\
\text{subject to}&A(\theta)x\leq b(\theta),\quad x\geq 0,\\
&x_{i}\in\mathbf{Z},\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{I},\end{array}

(1)

with respect to the decision vector
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
, where the
\mathcal{I}
is the set of indices of integer-constrained variables, and the rational problem data
A(\theta)\in\mathbf{R}^{m\times n}
,
b(\theta)\in\mathbf{R}^{m}
, and
c(\theta)\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
depend on the parameters
\theta\in\Theta
. We denote the feasible region of problem (
1
) as
X(\theta)
, and we assume that
A(\theta)
,
b(\theta)
contain the upper bounds on
x
.
For any given
\theta\in\Theta
, problem
eq.

1
represents a single instance, which we assume to be feasible and bounded.

In the past decades, we have witnessed dramatic theoretical and computational developments in solving single
MIPs
instances, in particular, linear ones
[
17
]
.
Researchers and practitioners can nowadays choose among several commercial
[
43
,
34
,
47
]
and open-source
[
16
,
46
]
mixed-integer solvers based on branch-and-cut
[
59
]
, an expert blend of the branch-and-bound
[
51
]
algorithm, cutting planes
[
41
,
48
]
, and heuristics.
While the above methodologies are tremendously effective for solving single
MIPs
from scratch, little is known, in theory and practice, on how to optimize a family of similar
MIPs

[
40
,
55
]
.
In many real-world settings, users repeatedly solve similar
parametric
instances where only some critical entries of the input data change,
e.g.
, prices, demands, or capacities. For instance, an energy company may generate the optimal power dispatch by repeatedly solving a unit commitment problem where only the predictions for energy demand vary across time.
Similarly, a hybrid (
e.g.
, mixed-integer) controller may solve an optimal control problem to update the system inputs (
e.g.
, actuator torques) as the sensor signals (
e.g.
, system state) and goals (
e.g.
, desired trajectory) vary over time.
Whenever the variables are all continuous, linear-programming duality enables us to understand how the parametric variations in
\theta
affect solutions; in principle, duality extends to the discrete case as
subadditive duality
, yet the approach “does not integrate well with current computational practice”
[
44
]
.
Therefore, the majority of the limited literature on parametric
MIPs
and reoptimization focuses on reusing the information of the branch-and-cut tree (
e.g.
,
[
38
,
60
]
); it comes as no surprise that exploiting this information poses significant unsolved mathematical and computational challenges in mixed-integer optimization
[
40
]
and beyond
[
6
]
.

Our contributions.

This work opens a novel research direction by introducing a differentiable cutting-plane algorithm to optimize parametric families of
MIPs
.
We summarize our contributions as follows:

(i.)

We introduce a differentiable cutting-plane algorithm designed to exploit the repeated structure of parametric instances (
section

2
).
During an offline phase, we train our algorithm to generate effective cutting planes, precisely split cuts, by
dynamically adjusting
the
cut-generating parameters
,
i.e.
, internal parameters controlling how cuts are generated (
e.g.
, disjunction and normalization).
Our algorithm interprets cutting-plane generation as a
sequential decision problem
where the
control inputs
are the cut-generating parameters, and the
state
is the accumulated information across cutting-plane rounds,
e.g.
, the generated cuts and the primal bounds.
Once trained, our algorithm can tackle unseen instances by computing, in a fixed number of cutting plane rounds, solutions with low integrality gaps.

(ii.)

We introduce
cutting-plane layers (CPLs)
,
i.e.
, differentiable cutting-plane generators with tunable weights (
section

3
).

CPLs
are the basic building blocks of our algorithm, and support a forward pass,
i.e.
, the generation of cutting planes performed by solving a convex optimization problem, and a backward pass,
i.e.
, differentiation with respect to the layers’ weights.
We propose an implementation of
CPLs
to generate split cuts, and we establish a theoretical correspondence between the split cuts and the generalized subadditive Gomory cuts from Chételat and Lodi
[
24
]
.

(iii.)

Finally, we conduct preliminary testing of our algorithm on two small families of parametric 2-matching and hybrid control instances where either the objective or right-hand side changes (
section

4
). Our algorithm generalizes well on unseen instances and provides up to a
3
-fold improvement over a standard cutting-plane algorithm.

1.1
A concise literature review

Learning for mixed-integer optimization.

A nurtured stream of research leverages machine learning to enhance mixed-integer optimization
[
12
,
29
]
, focusing mainly on data-driven heuristics for branch-and-cut algorithms (
e.g.
,
[
49
,
4
,
53
,
39
,
44
,
14
,
15
,
23
]
). Several works
[
45
,
63
,
61
,
13
,
64
,
65
,
32
]
addressed the problem improving cutting plane algorithms, primarily focusing on
cutting plane selection
,
i.e.
, the problem of selecting when and which cuts to add; see
[
32
]
for a detailed survey.
While these heuristics offer appealing computational advantages, they are limited to selecting from a finite set of commonly used cuts.
In addition, they rely on a complete branch-and-cut algorithm, whose overhead can be prohibitively large for some applications,
e.g.
, real-time embedded optimization.
In this work, we propose a fixed-length cutting-plane algorithm that dynamically tailors cut generation to a specific family of parametric problems.
In sharp opposition to the previous works, we do not perform cutting plane selection but
generation
,
i.e.
, we control the generation of the cuts and their coefficients.
To the best of our knowledge, the only exception is Chételat and Lodi
[
24
]
, where the authors propose a continuous cutting-plane algorithm based on a specific class of subadditive functions.
While this approach shares several characteristics with ours (see
section

3.1
), it focuses on solving a single problem and is not designed for parametric families.

Generating good cuts.

The classes, number, and general properties of the cuts significantly affect the efficiency and convergence of cutting-plane algorithms
[
59
,
31
,
27
]
.
Although popular metrics for cut effectiveness include cut violation, parallelism, or sparsity
[
18
,
31
,
66
,
5
]
, they primarily focus on the effect of the cuts at the current round, which may not be a direct metric for the overall algorithm behavior.
Our algorithm will, instead, learn to construct cuts by maximizing the bound improvement they induce across the entire sequence of cutting plane rounds (
e.g.
, similarly to Padberg’s
optimal separator

[
58
, Ch.9]
).
In addition, we choose to focus on disjunctive cuts, whose popularity soared in correspondence with their implementation inside branch-and-cut frameworks
[
10
,
8
,
9
,
35
]
and their conic extensions (
e.g.
,
[
50
,
52
]
).
Specifically, we consider
split cuts

[
26
]
because, first, they include many other cuts as special cases (
e.g.
, Gomory mixed-integer and mixed-integer rounding cuts); and second, because their closure can tightly approximate the convex-hull of
X(\theta)

[
36
,
11
,
30
]
. As reported by Cornuéjols
[
27
]
, “the split closure is surprisingly tight, and integer programming solvers should probably do a better job of approximating it”.

Learning sequential decision-making.

We model our cutting plane algorithm as a sequential decision problem where the cut-generating parameters represent the
input actions
, and the continuous relaxation represents a
dynamical system
(
e.g.
, an environment), both interacting in a closed-loop fashion. When we cannot model the system or its goal with a mathematical model, reinforcement learning techniques,
e.g.
, policy gradient methods, can improve the closed-loop behavior
[
62
,
68
,
33
]
.
However, in our setting, the environment and the goal (
e.g.
, increasing relaxation’s bound) are well-defined, and structure-exploiting approaches offer more efficient alternatives in terms of data and computation
[
56
]
.
In a sense, our
CPLs
act as convex-optimization control policies
[
3
]
that can be efficiently trained and evaluated by solving small optimization problems.

2
Differentiable cutting plane algorithm

Consider a single instance from the parametric family of
MIPs
in
eq.

1
by fixing
\theta
. We denote its (an) optimal solution as
x^{\star}(\theta)
with objective
z^{\star}(\theta)
, and we refer to
\operatorname{\bf conv}(X(\theta))
as the convex hull of its feasible solutions.
We can solve the instance via a cutting-plane algorithm, an iterative method that progressively tightens a relaxation of problem
eq.

1
by adding extra inequalities.
Specifically, in each round (iteration)
r
, a standard cutting plane algorithm solves the
relaxed
problem

z(\theta)=\begin{array}[t]{ll}\text{minimize}&c(\theta)^{T}x\\
\text{subject to}&A(\theta)x\leq b(\theta),\quad G(\theta)x\leq h(\theta),\quad x\geq 0,\end{array}

(2)

where we denote the feasible region as
\tilde{X}(\theta)
.
In this linear optimization problem, we drop the integrality requirements, and we add a set of inequalities
G(\theta)x\leq h(\theta)
, where
G(\theta)\in\mathbf{R}^{K\times n}
and
h(\theta)\in\mathbf{R}^{K}
.
Such inequalities, called
cutting planes
or
cuts
, guarantee that at each round, the previous fractional solution is cut off while retaining any integer-feasible point in
\operatorname{\bf conv}(X(\theta))
.
More precisely, given a solution
x^{r}(\theta)
to problem
eq.

2
, we say that an inequality
g^{T}x\leq h
, with
g\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
h\in\mathbf{R}
, is

1.

a valid inequality if it is satisfied by any point of (
1
),
i.e.
,
g^{T}x\leq h
for any
x\in\operatorname{\bf conv}(X(\theta))
, and

2.

a cut for
x^{r}(\theta)
if it is valid for
\operatorname{\bf conv}(X(\theta))
and violated by
x^{r}(\theta)
,
i.e.
,
g^{T}x^{r}(\theta)>h
.

Since
z(\theta)
is a lower bound for
z^{\star}(\theta)
, cutting plane algorithms guarantee monotonic bound improvements.

2.1
Our algorithm

Our cutting-plane algorithm should satisfy two foremost properties: it should provide
high-quality solutions
for the parametric family of
MIPs
in
eq.

1
, and it should be
differentiable
.
On the one hand, when we say provide high-quality solutions, we mean that the algorithm should dynamically adapt the cut generation (
i.e.
, separation) procedure to exploit the structure of the parametric family;
for instance, by identifying which constraints or variables should play a more active role in cut generation.
On the other hand, to be
differentiable
, our algorithm should implement both a forward pass,
i.e.
, unrolling a given number of iterations of a cutting plane method, and a backward pass,
i.e.
, computing gradients with respect to its key parameters.
Similarly to
[
24
]
, differentiability plays a pivotal role in adjusting the parameters that control cutting-plane generation; we call these
cut-generating parameters

\sigma
.

Overview of our differentiable cutting-plane algorithm.

Cutting plane algorithm.

Our algorithm, outlined in
fig.

1
, has a fixed size, meaning it performs a finite number of cutting plane rounds
R
where, in each round, it adds
K
cutting planes.
As an input, the algorithm receives a vector
\theta
defining a particular instance of problem (
1
) and produces, as an output, a solution
x^{R}(\theta)
.
During each iteration
r=0,\dots,R
,
we represent the state of our algorithm as
s^{r}(\theta)=(z^{r}(\theta),x^{r}(\theta),G^{r}(\theta),h^{r}(\theta)),

i.e.
, the current objective value, candidate solution, and cutting plane coefficients.
The first step, the
initialization block
, initializes
s^{0}(\theta)
by solving the initial continuous relaxation without cutting planes. Given this state, our algorithm generates
K
cuts and solves the resulting continuous relaxation, obtaining a new state
s^{r+1}(\theta)
.

Policy.

The idea of using a policy accounting for the
state
of the cutting-plane algorithm stems from the observation that cut generation is, indeed, an optimal control problem
[
5
]
; as previously generated cuts affect the generation in later iterations, the cut generator should exploit the state of the system to produce better cuts.
Here, a
policy
generates the cut coefficient (
i.e.
, control inputs) using two components. First, an
RNN
produces cut parameters
(\sigma^{1},\dots,\sigma^{K})
from the history of past
M
states
(s^{r}(\theta),\dots,s^{r-M+1}(\theta))
.
Second, we feed the output of the
RNN
into
K

CPLs
to generate the cuts
(G^{r+1}(\theta),h^{r+1}(\theta)),
as detailed in
section

3
.
Our algorithm then solves the continuous relaxation to produce a new candidate solution
x^{r+1}(\theta)
with objective
z^{r+1}(\theta)
, and an updated state
s^{r+1}(\theta)
.

Loss function.

To optimize the performance of our architecture for the considered family of parametric instances defined in problem (
1
), we need to train the policy such that it generates high-quality cutting plane coefficients, by tuning the
RNN
weights, denoted as
\rho
.
Specifically, our goal is to minimize the
loss function
,

\displaystyle L(\rho)={\bf E}_{\theta}\left(-\sum_{r=1}^{R}\gamma^{r}c(\theta)^{T}\left(x^{r}(\theta)-x^{r-1}(\theta)\right)\right),

(3)

which is the expectation over
\theta
of the negative sum of primal bound improvements, discounted by factor
\gamma\in(0,1)
.
This loss function is
unsupervised
, as it does not include the true optimal solution but only the bound induced by the cuts.
Intuitively, a higher primal bound in early iterations correlates with improved policy performance, leading to a reduced loss.
Furthermore, the
RNN
will likely produce cut parameters, and thus cuts, inducing greater improvements in the primal bound over
R
rather than, for example, improvements in the violation with respect to the previous candidate solution.

Training.

We assume to observe a set of the realization of the parameters
\theta
, which we will call the
training set

\mathcal{T}=\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
To train our policy, we use a stochastic gradient method
[
20
]
of the form
\rho^{\ell+1}=\rho^{\ell}-\mu\nabla_{\rho}\hat{L}(\rho^{\ell}),
where
\nabla_{\rho}\hat{L}(\rho^{\ell})
is an unbiased stochastic gradient of
L(\rho)
with respect to the
RNN
weights
\rho
, and
\mu
is the learning rate.
This process computes the gradients using the chain rule (
i.e.
, backpropagation through time
[
67
]
), which also requires differentiating through optimization problems (
e.g.
, the continuous relaxation and the
CPLs
); the latter differentiation is equivalent to the implicit differentiation of each optimization problem’s optimality conditions
[
2
,
1
]
.

3
Cut generation and cutting plane layers

We consider split cuts,
i.e.
, disjunctive cuts from two-sided disjunctions, which are generated by exploiting a Farkas argument on the original simplex tableau or via the so-called
cut-generating linear program (CGLP)

[
7
,
26
]
. We focus on the latter, as it enables us to explore a larger space of cutting planes,
e.g.
, via expressive normalizations (see
section

3.2
).
Since this problem is convex, we can differentiate its optimal solution with respect to its parameters
[
2
,
1
]
.

The CGP.

We slightly generalize the
CGLP
to a parametrized convex optimization problem in the cut parameters
\sigma
, which we denote as the
cut-generating program (CGP)
. Compared to
CGLP
, the
CGP
features a convex normalization constraint, but the separation procedure is analogous to that of
CGLP
. Specifically, we generate cuts by considering a
valid
two-sided
split
disjunction for
\operatorname{\bf conv}(X(\theta))
in the form of
(\pi^{T}x\leq\eta)\lor(\pi^{T}x\geq\eta+1)
with
\pi\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
\eta\in\mathbf{R}
. When we say the disjunction is valid, we mean that any feasible solution in
X(\theta)
belongs to one side of the disjunction. At a given iteration
r
, we can determine if there exists a cut
g^{T}x\leq h
separating a fractional solution
\bar{x}
from

\operatorname{\bf conv}(\{x\in\tilde{X}(\theta):\pi^{T}x\leq\eta\}\cup\{x\in\tilde{X}(\theta):\pi^{T}x\geq\eta+1\})

by solving the
CGP
formulated as

\displaystyle\underset{{g,h,u,v}}{\text{maximize}\;}\quad

\displaystyle g^{T}\bar{x}-h

(4a)

subject to

\displaystyle g\leq\begin{bmatrix}A(\theta)^{T}&\pi\end{bmatrix}u,

(4b)

\displaystyle g\leq\begin{bmatrix}A(\theta)^{T}&-\pi\end{bmatrix}v,

(4c)

\displaystyle h\geq\begin{bmatrix}b(\theta)^{T}&\eta\end{bmatrix}u,

(4d)

\displaystyle h\geq\begin{bmatrix}b(\theta)^{T}&-(\eta+1)\end{bmatrix}v,

(4e)

\displaystyle\left\lVert D\begin{bmatrix}u\\
v\end{bmatrix}\right\lVert_{p}\leq 1,

(4f)

\displaystyle u\geq 0,v\geq 0

(4g)

where
u\in\mathbf{R}^{m+1}
,
v\in\mathbf{R}^{m+1}
,
g\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
,
h\in\mathbf{R}
,
D\in\mathbf{R}^{(m+1)\times(m+1)}
, and
p
is the norm type.
The cut parameters are
\sigma=(\pi,\eta,D)
.
The
CGP
is a linear problem (the usual
CGLP
) if
p=1
or
\infty
, and a second-order cone problem if
p=2
.
By construction, any feasible solution
(g,h,u,v)
to problem
eq.

4
such that
g^{T}\bar{x}-h>0
corresponds to a violated split cut.
Without
eq.

4f
, the feasible set of
eq.

4
is a cone, and if there exists at least one solution such that
g^{T}\bar{x}-h>0
, the problem is unbounded. Therefore, we truncate the cone via the normalization constraint
eq.

4f
to recover a cut.
Traditionally, the normalization constraint involves a
0/1
linear combination of
u
and
v
(or
g,h
), and has the role of selecting which cut to generate by pushing
CGLP
to select one of the extreme rays. For instance, if
p=1
and
D
is a zero matrix except
D_{m+1,m+1}=D_{2(m+1),2(m+1)}=1
,
we recover the so-called
trivial
normalization.
Instead, in
eq.

4f
, we consider a generalized normalization constraint involving the
p
-norm of a linear transformation of
u
and
v
via
D
.
One natural question is whether generalizing the
CGLP
via a 2-norm brings any benefits.
When
p=1
, cuts derived from the extreme rays of
CGLP
dominate those in the interior
[
35
]
.
Empirically, as we show in
section

4
,
dominated cuts
may lead to better cuts in later iterations.
Furthermore, as we motivate in the next section,
p=2
enables us to represent a large family of (subadditive) cuts.

3.1
Cut representation and range

Subadditive valid inequalities.

For brevity, we limit our exposition to the pure-integer case, although our results extend to the mixed-integer one, and we focus on a single realization of
\theta
. Let the fractional part of a vector

be
\{y\}=y-\lfloor y\rfloor
.
Chételat and Lodi
[

devise a cut generation scheme via a

and

\displaystyle\Phi_{w,t}(y)

\displaystyle=\min\Big(\{w^{T}y\},\frac{t}{1-t}(1-\{w^{T}y\})\Big)+\max\Big(-w,\frac{t}{1-t}w\Big)^{T}y.

The resulting inequality
\Phi_{w,t}(-A(\theta))x\geq\Phi_{w,t}(-b(\theta))
is valid for
\operatorname{\bf conv}(X(\theta))
for any
w\in\mathbf{R}^{m}
and

.
In

[

]
are feasible solutions to problem

4

In the following, we write

to represent the

We also write the sum of the function
\Phi_{w,t}
over the

\Phi_{w,t}(A(\theta))=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Phi_{w,t}(A(\theta)_{j})

.

Let

and

{w_{+}}^{T}w_{-}=0

\displaystyle\pi_{j}

Finally, let

(g,h,u,v)

(ii.)

(iii.)

In view of these results, the subadditive inequalities from

can be interpreted as an

For implicit, we mean that cuts are the solutions to the optimization problem rather than the result of a closed-form expression. Naturally, there are advantages and disadvantages in either approach. The subadditive inequalities are

cuts corresponding to feasible, yet not necessarily optimal, solutions of

That is why the approach in

requires an over parametrization of their generating parameters,

Conversely, our explicit

Cut range.

Since our approach generates arbitrary split cuts, it can also generate Gomory mixed-integer, lift-and-project, and mixed-integer rounding cuts
[

,
57
,
28
]
.
Hence,

.

Corollary 3.1.1
.

Let
\bar{x}
be a fractional point. If

, then the set of cuts generated by varying

and solving

.

The previous corollary implies that the closure of the cuts generated by
eq.

4

contains the closure of the subadditive Gomory cuts

\Phi_{w,t}(-A(\theta))x\geq\Phi_{w,t}(-b(\theta))
over

.

3.2
Why cut-generating parameters matter

We briefly explain the motivations, intuitions, and implications of dynamically adjusting
\sigma
.

Disjunctions.

First, the policy can select the variables participating in the split disjunction via
\pi
and
\eta
, and thus in the generation of the cut.
The problem of selecting how to form the disjunctions in
\pi
is generally
\mathcal{NP}
-hard

[
54
,
22
]
. Balas and Sexena
[
11
]

Normalization.

Different normalizations

in
eq.

4

5
Concluding remarks

We presented a differentiable cutting-plane algorithm designed to exploit the repetitive structure of parametric mixed-integer optimization.
Although our computational tests are still preliminary and performed on relatively small sets of instances, we are optimistic about this new research direction.
We believe our work has three areas of potential impact. First, our work can lay the foundations for embedding mixed-integer solvers
as a layer
in differentiable architectures. Second, our algorithm can inform researchers in our community about the structural properties of cutting planes on parametric instances and, perhaps, propel methodological advances in reoptimization. Finally, our approach can potentially enable the solution of small-to-medium sized
MIPs
in embedded devices, such as microcontrollers.

-\Phi_{w,t}(-A(\theta))x\leq-\Phi_{w,t}(-b(\theta))

,

\pi^{T}\bar{x}\end{bmatrix},\quad q_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}A(\theta)\bar{x}\\

We can rewrite the

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{{u,v}}{\text{maximize}}&\min\big(q_{1}^{T}u+q_{3}^{T}u,q_{2}^{T}v+q_{4}^{T}v,q_{1}^{T}u+q_{4}^{T}v,q_{2}^{T}u+q_{3}^{T}v\big)\\[5.0pt]
\text{subject to}&\quad\left\lVert D\begin{bmatrix}u\\
v\end{bmatrix}\right\lVert_{2}\leq 1,\\
&u\geq 0,v\geq 0.\end{array}

(5)

Without loss of generality, we assume

attains the minimum in

q_{1}^{T}u^{\star}+q_{3}^{T}u^{\star}>0

We need to choose

are optimal in

5

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{{u,v}}{\text{maximize}}&q_{1}^{T}u+q_{3}^{T}u\\
\text{subject to}&\left\lVert D\begin{bmatrix}u\\
v\end{bmatrix}\right\lVert_{2}^{2}\leq 1\\

A.3
Optimal control problem formulation

We aim to control the energy balance between a supercapacitor and a fuel cell to match a given power demand. The goal is to minimize energy losses while maintaining the device within
acceptable operating limits to prevent lifespan degradation. For a detailed description of the problem, we refer the reader to
[
15
,
37
]
.
The optimization problem reads as

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{P_{\tau},\zeta_{\tau},\xi_{\tau},\psi_{\tau}}{\text{minimize}\;}&\sum_{\tau=0}^{H-1}f(P_{\tau},\zeta_{\tau})\\
\text{subject to}&E_{\tau+1}=E_{\tau}+\upsilon(P_{\tau}-P^{\text{load}}_{\tau})\\
&E^{\text{min}}\leq E_{\tau}\leq E^{\text{max}}\\
&0\leq P_{\tau}\leq\zeta_{\tau}P^{\text{max}}\\
&\zeta_{\tau+1}=\zeta_{\tau}+\psi_{\tau}\\[-3.0pt]
&k_{\tau+1}=k_{\tau}+\xi_{\tau}-\xi_{\tau-H}\\
&k_{\tau}\leq N^{\text{sw}}\\
&G(\psi_{\tau},\zeta_{\tau},\xi_{\tau})\leq h\\
&E_{0}=E^{\text{init}},\quad\zeta_{0}=\zeta^{\text{init}},\quad k_{0}=s^{\text{init}},\\
&\zeta\in\{0,1\}^{H},\quad\xi\in\{0,1\}^{H},\quad\psi\in[-1,1]^{H}.\end{array}

Here,
\tau
> 0 is the sampling time,
H
is the horizon length,
E_{\tau}\in[E^{\text{min}},E^{\text{max}}]
is the energy stored,
P_{\tau}\in[0,P^{\text{max}}]
is the power provided by the fuel cell,
P^{\text{load}}_{\tau}
is the desired power load.
The variable
\zeta_{\tau}\in\{0,1\}
is the on-off state of the fuel cell, whereas

\xi_{\tau}\in\{0,1\}
is a binary variable which is
1
iff the control
\zeta_{\tau}
changes at time
\tau

and
0
otherwise.
The variable
\psi_{\tau}
represents the amount of change brought by

\xi_{\tau}
in the battery state

\zeta_{\tau}
, while
k_{\tau}
is the number of times the control switched between
\tau-H
and
\tau
. We set a limit
N^{\text{sw}}
to the maximum allowed number of switches between times
\tau-H
and
\tau
. The problem parameters are
\theta=(E^{\text{init}},\zeta^{\text{init}},s^{\text{init}},\xi^{\text{past}},P^{\text{load}})
where
\xi^{\text{past}}=(\xi_{-H},\dots,\xi_{-1})
and
P^{\text{load}}=(P^{\text{load}}_{0},\dots P^{\text{load}}_{H-1})
. The objective term

f
is a function representing the affine stage power cost,
i.e.
,
f(P,\zeta)=\omega P+\delta\zeta
for fixed parameters
\omega,\delta\in\mathbf{R}
.
