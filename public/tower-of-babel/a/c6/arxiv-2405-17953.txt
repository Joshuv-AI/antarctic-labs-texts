Graph Threading with Turn Costs
Erik D. Demaine, Yael Kirkpatrick, Rebecca Lin

Abstract
How should we thread a single string through a set of tubes so that pulling the string taut self-assembles the tubes into a desired graph? While prior work [ITCS 2024] solves this problem with the goal of minimizing the length of string, we study here the objective of minimizing the total turn cost. The frictional force required to pull the string through the tubes grows exponentially with the total absolute turn angles (by the Capstan equation), so this metric often dominates the friction in real-world applications such as deployable structures. We show that minimum-turn threading is NP-hard, even for graphs of maximum degree 4 4 , and even when restricted to some special cases of threading. On the other hand, we show that these special cases can in fact be solved efficiently for graphs of maximum degree 3 3 , thereby fully characterizing their dependence on maximum degree. We further provide polynomial-time exact and approximation algorithms for variants of turn-cost threading: restricting to threading each edge exactly twice, and on rectangular grid graphs.

1
Introduction

Deployable structures are dynamic systems capable of transitioning
between multiple geometric configurations,
with applications to compact storage, ease of transportation, and versatility.
One intriguing approach to building deployable structures is to thread a
collection of tubes with string so that different configurations
result from adjustments to string tension.
For example, this idea is at the heart of the “push puppet” family of toys.
Interest in this approach is growing within various applied fields,
including fabrication
[
15
]
and robotics
[
6
]
,
with the goal of combining the advantages in adaptability and safety of
soft materials with the precision and integrity of rigid
structures
[
5
,
17
]
.

Threading was formalized into theoretical computer science
in recent work by Demaine, Kirkpatrick, and Lin
[
9
]
.
Specifically, a
threading
of a graph is a closed walk that,
at every vertex, induces a connected graph on the incident edges.
(See Section
2
for precise definitions.)
Thus routing a single string along this walk, and pulling the string taut,
self-assembles the tubes (edges) to mimic the connectivity of the given graph.
Past work
[
9
]

gave a polynomial-time algorithm for finding the

minimum-length
threading of a given graph.

In this paper, we analyze threading according to a
turn
metric,
motivated by challenges that arise when constructing threadings in
practice
[
15
]
.
While minimizing length makes sense to save on material cost and
manufacturing time, the feasibility of deployable structures is
limited by the force required to pull the string, which is usually
dominated by the amount of friction in the system.
According to the Capstan equation, friction increases
exponentially

with the sum of the absolute values of turn angles in the threading
route
[
14
]
.
This motivates us to find threadings of minimum total “turn cost”,
where we allow each vertex to specify the (symmetric) cost of turning
between every pair of incident edges.
(Again see Section
2
for precise definitions.)

1.1
Our Results

In this paper, we analyze the
Threading
problem under a turn metric, where the goal is to find the threading of a graph with minimum total turn cost. We present several new results:

•

In Section
3
, we prove
NP
-completeness of

Threading
under a turn metric in various settings.
These results provide a stark contrast to the length metric where
Threading
can be solved in polynomial time
[
9
]
.

–

We give a simple proof that
Threading
is
NP
-complete, even if the objective is simply to minimize the number of turns, that is, the turn costs are either
0
or
1
.
This reduction shows how just one linear-degree vertex can
effectively simulate an arbitrary Hamiltonian Cycle instance,
motivating the restriction to bounded degree.

–

We prove that
Threading
is
NP
-complete even for graphs of
maximum degree
4
, via a reduction from
1-in-3 SAT
.

–

Perfect Threading
is a special case where we require using
the smallest possible turn cost when restricted to every vertex.
Such a threading is not always possible, and we prove that it is

NP
-complete to decide even for graphs of maximum degree
4
.
The analogous problem for the length metric (visiting each vertex the
fewest possible times) is of course polynomial, and was a helpful
stepping stone toward the full polynomial-time algorithm

[
9
]
.

–

It follows from our reductions that both
Threading
and
Perfect Threading

are
NP
-complete even when we further constrain the threading to
at most two visits per edge, a problem we call
Double Threading
.

•

In Section
4
, we provide polynomial-time algorithms for
Perfect Threading
and
Double Threading

in graphs of maximum degree
\leq 3
.
As we show these problems
NP
-hard for graphs of maximum degree
4
, our results give a complete characterization of the complexity of these two problems.
(It remains open whether the more general
Threading
problem is hard
in graphs of maximum degree
\leq 3
.)

•

In Section
5
, we further analyze interesting special cases of

Threading
under our turn metric, in particular developing several more
polynomial-time algorithms:

–

When each edge must be traversed
exactly
twice,
Exactly-Double Threading
, we show that the problem is equivalent to solving a sequence of
Traveling Salesman
problems, one at each vertex. We use this fact to prove the intractability of
Exactly-Double Threading
for unbounded-degree graphs, to develop polynomial-time approximation algorithms, and to develop exact algorithms for graphs of logarithmic degree or for minimizing the number of turns (turn costs
\in\{0,1\}
).

–

We present a strategy for threading on rectangular grid graphs.

–

If the ratio between the maximum and minimum turn costs is
r
,
we provide a
2r
-approximation algorithm.

Minimizing turn cost is a generalization of minimizing length
(e.g., define the
e\to f
turn cost to be
\frac{|e|+|f|}{2}
),
but minimum-turn threadings can have arbitrarily longer-than-optimal lengths
(see
Figure 2
).

1.2
Related Work

Turn costs have been studied for several other problems.
In the angular-metric traveling salesman problem
[
2
]
,
the goal is to minimize the total absolute turn angle of the tour.
In minimum-turn milling
[
3
]
, the goal is to cover a region
with a moving tool while minimizing the total turn cost or number of turns,
motivated by machinery such as mills which operate more efficiently
moving in straight lines rather than turning.
In minimum-turn Eulerian tour
[
10
]
, the goal is to minimize the
total turn cost; in surprising contrast to regular Eulerian tours,
this problem is NP-hard even for minimizing the number of turns
(turn costs
\in\{0,1\}
) in graphs of bounded degree.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we discuss the necessary background for
Threading
and introduce several special variants of the problem. We assume all graphs in question to be connected with a minimum degree of at least
2
.

Definition 1

(Threading
[
9
]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with
n=|V|
vertices and
m=|E|
edges,
where each edge
e\in E(G)
represents a tube and each vertex
v\in V(G)
represents the junction where the tubes incident to
v
meet. A
threading

T
of
G
is a closed walk through
G
that visits every edge at least once, induces connected “junction graphs” at every vertex, and has no “U-turns”, meaning that the walk cannot traverse the same edge twice in sequence. The
junction graph

J(v)
of a vertex
v
induced by a closed walk has a vertex for each tube incident to
v
, and has an edge between two of these vertices every time the walk visits
v
immediately in between traversing the corresponding tubes.

Figure 1:

Two distinct threadings of a tetrahedron, where each junction graph is either a tree (a) or a cycle (b) (
[
9
, Figure 2]
) Their threading graphs are shown in the corners.

While prior work
[
9
]
focused on minimum-
length
threading, we shift our attention to minimum-
turn
threading. Let us first define turn costs formally:

Definition 2

(Turn Cost)
.

A
turn
at a vertex
v
is an unordered pair
(uv,vu)
of distinct edges both incident to
v
.
Let
T(G)
denote the set of all turns in
G
.

Turn costs
for
G
are defined by a function
\alpha:T(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, specifying a nonnegative real number for each turn in
G
.
For a closed walk
W
in
G
traversing a sequence of vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell}
, the
turn cost
of
W
is the sum of the turn costs for all consecutive pairs of edges (turns) along
W
:

\alpha(W)=\sum_{i=2}^{\ell-1}\alpha(v_{i-1}v_{i},v_{i}v_{i+1})+\alpha(v_{\ell-1}v_{\ell},v_{\ell}v_{1})+\alpha(v_{\ell}v_{1},v_{1}v_{2}).

Now we can state the problem of finding a minimum-turn threading as:

Problem 2.3

(
Threading
)
.

Given
(G,\alpha)
, find a threading
T
of
G
that minimizes
\alpha(T)
.

The decision version of
Threading
is as follows:

Problem 2.4

(
Decision Threading
)
.

Given
(G,\alpha)
and a nonnegative real number
c
, does there exist a threading
T
of
G
such that
\alpha(T)\leq c
?

Naïve Double-Threading Solution.

We can naïvely compute a threading. First, assign each junction graph to be a cycle; see the example in Figure
1
b. Then, compute an Euler tour in
O(m)
time through the union of these junction graphs (Figure
1
) that prohibits U-turns
[
9
, Section 2.2]
. This tour is a threading that “double-threads” each edge.

Definition 2.5

(Minimum-Turn Perfect Threading)
.

A

Definition 1
.

perfect threading is a threading where every junction graph is a tree. Not all graphs have perfect threadings
[
9
]
.
A

Definition 2
.

minimum-turn perfect threading is a threading where each junction graph is a minimum spanning tree.

By definition, each junction graph induced by a threading is connected, and so contains a spanning tree. Hence, the cost incurred by the threading at each junction must be at least that of a minimum spanning tree at that junction. Therefore, if a minimum-turn perfect threading exists, it is a minimum-turn threading. It follows that finding a minimum-turn perfect threading is a subproblem of
Threading
.

Problem 2.6

(
Perfect Threading
)
.

Given
(G,\alpha)
,
find a minimum-turn perfect threading of
G
, if it exists.

At times, it may be pragmatic to limit the number of traversals through a tube, which motivates our final problem definition:

Problem 2.7

(
Double Threading
)
.

Given
(G,\alpha)
, find a minimum-turn threading of
G
that traverses each edge at most twice.

We prove our first result:
Decision Threading
is in
NP
. It is straightforward to verify that the same conclusion holds for the decision versions of
Perfect Threading
and
Double Threading
.

Theorem 2.8
.

Decision Threading
is in
NP
.

Proof 2.9
.

A purported solution
T
to
Decision Threading
can be verified by checking that
T
is indeed a threading of
G
and that
\alpha(T)\leq c
. Each of these checks can be performed in
O(|T|)
time.

We now prove that, for any threading
T
, there exists a threading
T^{\prime}
with size polynomial in
m
such that
\alpha(T^{\prime})\leq\alpha(T)
, thereby showing that a polynomial-size witness for the solution exists. We can construct
T^{\prime}
by repeatedly deleting cycles in
T
that do not disconnect any junction graphs until no such cycles remain. We next show that
T^{\prime}
traverses each edge at most
O(m^{2})
times.

Assume for a contradiction that
T^{\prime}
traverses an edge
uv\in E(G)
more than
|T(G)|+1
times, where
T(G)
denotes the set of all turns in
G
. Then
T^{\prime}
contains at least
|T(G)|+1
cycles, each formed via a revisit to
uv
. By the pigeonhole principle, there exists at least one cycle that fails to traverse a turn not already covered by the other cycles. This redundant cycle may be removed to produce a shorter walk for which junction graphs are still connected. This contradicts our construction of
T^{\prime}
. Hence, it follows that
uv
is traversed
\leq|T(G)|+1
times. Since
|T(G)|=\sum_{v\in V(G)}d(v)^{2}\in O(m^{2})

[
8
]
, our conclusion follows.

While the proof of Theorem
2.8
demonstrates that an edge is traversed
O(m^{2})
times, Figure
2
shows an example where an edge is traversed
\Omega(m)
times.

Figure 2
:

A tree-like graph with degree-3 junctions as marked by the circles. A green line connecting an edge pair indicates a turn cost of
0
, whereas a red line indicates a turn cost of
1
. All other turns have zero cost. The minimum-turn threading of this graph deviates far from a perfect threading — it traverses the “root” triangle as many times as there are “leaf” triangles.

3

Intractability of
Threading

In Section
3.1
, we show that
Threading
is
NP
-complete, even with turn costs
\in\left\{0,1\right\}
, via a simple reduction from the
Hamiltonian Cycle
problem. The reduction uses the turn costs at a single linear-degree vertex to encode the connectivity of an arbitrary graph. We later utilize this technique to give positive results in Section
5.1
. In Section
3.2
, we show that
Threading
remains NP-complete for graphs of maximum degree
4
by simulating
1-in-3 SAT
with vertices of degree
\leq 4
.

3.1

General Graphs

Theorem 3.10
.

Threading
is
NP
-complete, even if only minimizing the number of turns.

We prove the hardness of
Threading
by reducing from
Hamiltonian Cycle
, which is
NP
-hard
[
12
]
. The
Hamiltonian Cycle
problem asks whether a graph
G
contains a Hamiltonian cycle, a cycle that visits every vertex exactly once.

Given an instance
G
of
Hamiltonian Cycle
with
n
vertices and
m
edges, we construct an instance
(H,\alpha)
of
Threading
as follows (Figure
3
): First, create
n
copies of the cycle graph
C_{3}
, one per vertex in
G
, and connect each cycle via a bridge to a central vertex
c
. We refer to each cycle and its incident bridge as an “arm” and denote the arm corresponding to vertex
v_{i}
as
a_{i}
. Next, at vertex
c
, assign each pair of incident edges from distinct arms
a_{i}
and
a_{j}
a turn cost of
0
if
v_{i}v_{j}\in E(G)
. Assign all remaining turns a cost of
1
. By design, traversing each arm incurs a cost of
4
. The time to construct
(H,\alpha)
is
O(m+n)
.

Figure 3
:

Constructing an instance
H
of
Threading
from an instance
G
of
Hamiltonian Cycle
. The turn costs at the central vertex in
H
are depicted inside the right-most circle, where a green line connecting a pair of edges indicates a turn cost of
0
and a red line indicates a turn cost of
1
. Turn costs for unlabeled turns are assumed to be
1
. The edges of the junction graph induced by the threading in orange are in bold.

Lemma 3.11
.

Graph
G
contains a Hamiltonian cycle if and only if its corresponding instance
(H,\alpha)
to
Threading
has a threading of turn cost
4n
.

Proof 3.12
.

Suppose
G
has a Hamiltonian cycle
C
. Label the vertices of
G
such that
C
is given by the sequence of vertices
v_{1},v_{2},...,v_{n},v_{1}
. Construct a walk
T
through
H
that traverses the arms the same order:
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n},a_{1}
. Here,
a_{i}a_{j}a_{k}
indicates the walk entering arm
a_{j}
directly from arm
a_{i}
, traversing all edges of
a_{j}
in either a clockwise or counter-clockwise fashion, and then exiting
a_{j}
directly to arm
a_{k}
. This walk creates a connected junction graph at each vertex. Most notably, at the central vertex
c
, the junction graph is given by the Hamiltonian cycle
C
where each vertex
v_{i}
maps to the bridge from arm
a_{i}
. It follows that
T
is a threading of
H
. This threading incurs a turn cost of
4n
: Each arm of cost
4
is traversed exactly once because
C
is a Hamiltonian cycle, and moving between consecutive arms
a_{i}
and
a_{j}
in
H
incurs zero cost because
v_{i}v_{j}
is in the Hamiltonian cycle and is hence in
E(G)
.

Now assume there exists a threading
T
of
H
of turn cost
4n
. By definition,
T
visits each arm at least once, and upon entering an arm, it fully traverses the arm because U-turns are prohibited. Given that each arm incurs a cost of
4
, turn cost
\alpha(T)=4n
implies two facts: (1)
T
traverses each arm exactly once and (2) incurs zero turn cost at vertex
c
. Labeling the sequence of arms traversed by
T
as
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}
, we map
T
to vertices
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n},v_{1}
in
G
. Consecutive vertices in this sequence are adjacent by (2), hence this sequence forms a cycle. Furthermore, each vertex is visited once per (1), so this cycle is Hamiltonian.

It follows from Theorem
2.8
and Lemma
3.11
that
Threading
is
NP
-complete, even with turn costs
\in\left\{0,1\right\}
.

3.2

Maximum-Degree-4 Graphs

Next we consider bounded degree graphs and show that even the special case of
Perfect Threading
is
NP
-complete in this setting.

Theorem 3.13
.

Perfect Threading
in graphs of maximum degree
4
is
NP
-complete.

Since
Perfect Threading
is a special case of
Threading
(Section
2
), we conclude:

Corollary 3.14
.

Threading
in graphs of maximum degree
4
is
NP
-complete.

We prove Theorem
3.13
via a reduction from

NP

[

.

Definition 3.15

(
1-in-3 SAT
)
.

Given
m
clauses consisting of three literals each, over a set of
n
boolean variables, the
1-in-3 SAT
problem asks to determine whether there exists a truth assignment to the variables such that each clause contains exactly one
true
literal.

For example, the formula

\varphi=\mathbbm{1}(w,x,\lnot y)\wedge\mathbbm{1}(\lnot w,x,\lnot z)\wedge\mathbbm{1}(x,y,z),

(1)

where
\mathbbm{1}(x,y,z)
represents that exactly one of

has a satisfying assignment of
x=\textsc{false},y=\textsc{true},z=\textsc{false}

Given an arbitrary instance
\varphi
of

Perfect Threading
. This graph comprises several gadgets to simulate

Variable Gadget.

For each variable
x

3

x
: If the bottom left edge is double-threaded, then
x
is assigned
true
; conversely, if the bottom right edge is double-threaded, then
x
is assigned

\lnot x
is assigned

(
Figure 4

To simulate a clause

.

Lemma 3.17
.

Proof 3.18

4

Maximum-Degree-3 Graphs

Here we present polynomial-time algorithms for solving
Perfect Threading
and
Double Threading
on graphs of maximum degree
3
, thereby fully characterizing the complexity of these two problems.

4.1

Perfect Threading

Theorem 4.20
.

Perfect Threading
in graphs of maximum degree
3
is in
P
.

Let us consider an instance
(G,\alpha)
of
Perfect Threading
with graph
G
of maximum degree
3
. We begin our proof of the theorem by observing that a spanning tree at a degree-
3
junction
v\in V(G)
is a path of length
2
. Hence, one of the edges incident to
v
must be double-threaded while the remaining two edges are single-threaded. From this observation, we deduce that finding a perfect threading in
G
is a matching problem: Selecting one incident edge per degree-
3
vertex to double-thread is equivalent to finding a set of edges such that each degree-
3
vertex is incident to one edge from this set.

However, not every spanning tree qualifies as a
minimum
spanning tree (MST). Hence, to achieve a minimum-turn perfect threading, we must double-thread only those edges that could induce MSTs. That is, we seek matchings that select for edges
uv
corresponding to degree-
2
vertices in MSTs for both junction graphs
J(u)
and
J(v)
. See our proposed algorithm below:

Algorithm 2

Perfect Threading
on maximum-degree-3 graphs.

1.

Delete all degree-
2
vertices in
G
and their incident edges.

2.

For every remaining vertex
v\in V
, consider all MSTs for
J(v)
and delete any edge incident to
v
of degree
1
in all MSTs.

3.

Compute a perfect matching of the remaining graph
G^{\prime}
. Return the matching if it exists.

Correctness.

The correctness the algorithm follows from the claim below:

Claim 4
.

Graph
G
has a perfect threading if and only if the graph
G^{\prime}
produced by Algorithm
2
has a perfect matching.

Proof 4.21
.

Suppose
G
has a minimum-turn perfect threading
T
. Consider the set
M
of edges that are double-threaded by
T
. Since
T
induces an MST at every junction, none of the edges in
M
could be removed in Step
2
of Algorithm
2
. Hence, every edge of
M
exists in
G^{\prime}
. Furthermore, since every vertex in
G^{\prime}
corresponds to a degree-
3
vertex in
G
, it must have exactly one incident edge that is double-threaded by
T
, implying one incident edge in
M
. Therefore,
M
is a perfect matching in
G^{\prime}
.

Conversely, given a perfect matching
M
of
G^{\prime}
, we can construct junction graphs that result in a minimum-turn perfect threading: For every degree-2 vertex, we take the trivial junction graph comprising a single edge. For every degree-
3
vertex
v
with its matching
u
in
M
, we set
J(v)
to be the path of length
2
, with the central vertex corresponding to the edge
uv
. This path forms an MST by the construction of
G^{\prime}
(Step 2).

Running-Time.

Step
1
runs in
O(n)
time. Step
2
also runs in
O(n)
time given that each degree-
3
vertex has at most three MSTs. Finally, since
|G^{\prime}|\in O(n)
, Step
3
runs in
O(n^{2}\log{n})
time via the perfect matching algorithm by Galil, Micali, and Gabow
[
11
]
.

4.2

Double Threading

While perfect threadings may not exist for some graphs, double-threadings are always possible — refer to our naïve algorithm in Section
2
for details. Here we present an efficient solution to
Double Threading
in maximum-degree-
3
graphs.

Theorem 4.22
.

Double Threading
in graphs of maximum degree 3 can be solved in time
O(m^{2}\log m)
.

{proofsketch}

Suppose
G
is a graph of maximum degree
3
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of vertex-disjoint simple cycles in
G
. Prior work
[
9
, Lemma 9]
achieves a threading
T
that single-threads every edge in
\mathcal{C}
and double-threads every other edge.
Because
G
has maximum degree
3
, the turn cost of this threading is:

\alpha(T)=\underbrace{\sum_{(uv,vw)\in T(G)}\alpha(uv,vw)}_{\text{total cost of all turns in $G$}}-\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\alpha(C).

Therefore, solving
Double Threading
on
G
is equivalent to finding a collection of vertex-disjoint simple cycles in
G
whose total turn cost is maximized. We give an algorithm to solve this problem; see Algorithm
3
below.

Algorithm 3

Computing maximum-turn vertex-disjoint simple cycles.

1.

Construct a weighted graph
G^{\prime}
from
(G,\alpha)

(Figure
9
) as follows:

(a)

For each edge
uv
, create a zero-weight path of length
3
comprising vertices
uv^{+}
,
uv^{-}
,
vu^{-}
, and
vu^{+}
.

(b)

For each turn
(uv,vw)
in
T(G)
, add an edge of weight
\alpha(uv,vw)
connecting the vertices
vu^{+}
and
vw^{+}
.

2.

Compute a maximum weight perfect matching
M
in
G^{\prime}
.

3.

Return the set of edges
S=\left\{uv\in E(G):(uv^{-},vu^{-})\in M\right\}
.

:

G^{\prime}
from

Correctness.

We briefly explain why the matching

in

corresponds to a collection of vertex-disjoint simple cycles in
G
. Consider two possible cases for each vertex
v\in V(G)
:

1.

Suppose

contains a weighted edge

created in Step
1b

given that the set of vertices
\{v{x}^{+}\}_{x\in N(v)}

\{v{x}^{+}\}_{x\in N(v)\setminus\{u,w\}}

through

2.

Otherwise, all vertices in

are matched to vertices in
\{v{x}^{-}\}_{x\in N(v)}

for Step

Finding a double-threading of

[

.

5

Other Special Cases

In this section, we give polynomial-time algorithms for several additional variants of
Threading
.
We show that
Exactly-Double Threading
is equivalent to the
Traveling Salesperson
problem. This result paves the way for several insights: a fixed-parameter tractable algorithm contingent on the maximum degree of the graph, approximation algorithms, and a polynomial-time algorithm if the goal is simply to minimize the number of turns. Next, we give a polynomial-time algorithm to thread rectangular grids. Finally, we conclude our paper with a polynomial-time approximation for
Threading
given bounded positive turn costs.

5.1

Exactly-Double Threading

Consider
n
cities, with distance
d(i,j)
between cities
i
and
j
. The
Traveling Salesperson
problem (TSP) asks if there exists a tour of length at most some constant
C\geq 0
that visits each city exactly once.

Theorem 5.23
.

Exactly-Double Threading
is equivalent to
Traveling Salesperson
.

{proofsketch}

First, we sketch a reduction from TSP to
Exactly-Double Threading
. Given an instance of TSP, we construct an instance
(G,\alpha)
of
Exactly-Double Threading
: Create
n
distinct arms incident to a central vertex
c
as in the proof of Lemma
3.11
. Set the turn costs between any two arms
a_{i}
and
a_{j}
about vertex
c
to
d(i,j)
. Set all other turn costs to
1
. It follows that there exists a tour of length at most
C
that visits each city exactly once if and only if there exists a threading of
G
of cost at most
C+4n
.

Let’s now consider the reverse direction. We reduce
Exactly-Double Threading
to TSP: Leveraging the key observation that each junction graph must be precisely a cycle, we find the minimum-cost cycle at each junction
v
by solving TSP on
v
— we assign a city to each edge incident to
v
, with distance
\alpha(uv,vw)
between cities
uv
and
vw
. Subsequently, we apply the polynomial-time procedure by Demaine, Kirkpatrick, and Lin
[
9
]
to construct a threading, using these minimum-cost cycles as junction graphs.

A fixed-parameter tractable algorithm for
Exactly-Double Threading
follows directly from the proof of Theorem
5.23
:

Corollary 5.24
.

Exactly-Double Threading
can be solved in polynomial time if the maximum vertex degree
\Delta
of the graph is in
O(\log n)
.

{proofsketch}

Solving TSP on each junction can be achieved in
O(d(v)^{2}+2^{d(v)})
time via the Bellman-Held algorithm
[
4
,
13
]
, so the runtime is polynomial as long as
d(v)\leq\Delta
is logarithmic in
n
.

We can further inherit results from TSP: If the turn costs at each junction form a metric space, that is, if they obey the triangle inequality, then we can approximate
Exactly-Double Threading
in polynomial time by solving metric TSP approximately at each junction. For instance, if all turn costs are either
1
or
2
, then we can utilize the approximation algorithm for (1, 2)-TSP by Adamaszek, Mnich, and Paluch
[
1
]
to obtain an
8/7
-approximation scheme that runs in
O(\sum_{v\in V(G)}d(v)^{3}+m)
time. More generally, we can apply the Christofides-Serdyukov algorithm
[
7
]
to obtain a
3/2
-approximation scheme that achieves the same time complexity.

Corollary 5.25
.

Exactly-Double Threading
is in
P
if turn costs are in
\{0,1\}
.

{proofsketch}

This result follows from the fact that we can solve TSP in a
complete
graph
G
with costs in
\{0,1\}
in polynomial time.

The hardness of
Double Threading
given turn costs in
\left\{0,1\right\}
remains open.

5.2

Rectangular Grid Graphs

A

Definition 5
.

rectangular grid graph
G
is a
w\times h
lattice graph consisting of
w
rows and
h
columns of vertices, referred to as

Definition 6
.

vertex rows and

Definition 7
.

vertex columns, respectively. Each 90-degree turn at a vertex incurs a cost of
1
while moving in a straight line incurs no cost. Below, we introduce a strategy for efficiently computing a threading
G
with few turns. We first consider the following observations:

Observation 5.26
.

Every vertex requires at least one turn to connect its junction graph.

Proof 5.27
.

The junction graph of vertex
v
is connected only if the threading traverses
v
uniquely at least
d(v)-1
times. Let’s consider the implication of this requirement on the three types of vertices in
G
: (1) A degree-
2
vertex, being a corner, necessitates a turn. (2) A degree-
3
vertex permits one straight traversal. If this traversal is taken, then any different traversal must be a turn. (3) A degree-
4
vertex permits two separate straight traversals, but an additional distinct traversal is required, thereby requiring a turn.

Observation 5.28
.

Each vertex column contains at least
2\lceil h/2\rceil
turns, and each vertex row contains at least
2\lceil w/2\rceil
turns.

Proof 5.29
.

Our subsequent argument for vertex columns applies symmetrically to vertex rows. By Observation
5.26
, it suffices to argue that an even number of turns is required for each vertex column. When a thread enters a vertex column, it must exit the column either by returning the way it entered, inducing two turns, or leaving in the opposite direction, which results in either no turns if going straight or two turns if forming a staircase.

It follows that:

Lemma 5.30
.

A threading of
G
with
w
vertex columns and
h
vertex rows has at least
2\lceil w/2\rceil\cdot 2\lceil h/2\rceil
turns if one of
w
or
h
is even, or both.

Proof 5.31
.

If both
w
and
h
are even, then the threading has at least
w\cdot h=2\lceil w/2\rceil\cdot 2\lceil h/2\rceil
turns by Observation
5.26
, as desired. If
w
is odd and
h
is even, then each vertex row necessitates at least
w+1
turns by Observation
5.26
and
5.28
, so the threading once again has a total of at least
(w+1)\cdot h=2\lceil w/2\rceil\cdot 2\lceil h/2\rceil
turns. The same analysis applies to even
w
and odd
h
.

Theorem 5.32
.

In
O(m)
time, we can compute a threading of
G
with
2\lceil w/2\rceil\cdot 2\lceil h/2\rceil
if at least one of
w
and
h
are even, which is optimal by Lemma
5.30
, or
2\lceil w/2\rceil\cdot 2\lceil h/2\rceil-4
turns if both
w
and
h
are odd.

To prove Theorem
5.32
, we leverage the following lemma:

Lemma 5.33
.

Given an edge cycle cover
\mathcal{C}
of
G
, where each cycle contains no U-turns and collectively traverses each
v\in V(G)
uniquely at least
d(v)-1
times, we can construct a threading of
G
with cost
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\alpha(C)
.

{proofsketch}

The cycles in
\mathcal{C}
traverse each edge of
G

d(v)-1
times without U-turns, they connect all junction graphs. Therefore, we can construct a threading of equal turn cost as
\mathcal{C}
from the junction graphs induced by
\mathcal{C}
.

We present Algorithm

5.3

Approximation for Bounded Turn Costs

Theorem 5.34
.

Given that all turn costs are positive, we can attain a
2r
-approximation for
Threading
, with
r
representing the ratio between the maximum and minimum turn costs. This approximation can be computed in
O(m)

Proof 5.35
.

Apply the naïve double-threading algorithm described in Section
2

\alpha(T)

1

(i.e., the maximum cost of a cycle at
v

\frac{\alpha(T)}{\alpha(\textsf{OPT})}\leq\frac{r\cdot\sum_{v\in V}d(v)}{\sum_{v\in V}d(v)-1}=\frac{2rm}{2m-n}\leq\frac{2rm}{m}=2r

as desired.
