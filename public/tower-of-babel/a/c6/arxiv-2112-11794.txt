1.1
Preliminaries

We consider a Toeplitz matrix

\left[a_{i-j}\right]_{i,j=1}^{n}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{0}&a_{-1}&a_{-2}&\cdots&\cdots&a_{-(n-1)}\\
a_{1}&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
a_{2}&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&a_{-2}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&a_{-1}\\
a_{n-1}&\cdots&\cdots&a_{2}&a_{1}&a_{0}\end{bmatrix},

which is characterized from the fact to show constant entries along each diagonal. When taking a function
f\colon[-\pi,\pi]\to\mathbb{C}

\hat{f}_{k}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(\theta)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k\theta}\dif\theta,\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

In order to fix the terminology, we refer to
\{T_{n}(f)\}_{n}
as the Toeplitz sequence generated by
f
, which in turn is called the generating function or the symbol of
\{T_{n}(f)\}_{n}
: we remind that the notion of symbol is a different notion recalled in Definition
1.1
and the related theorem for Toeplitz matrix-sequences is reported in Theorem
1.1
. If the generating function
f
is real-valued, then all the matrices

are Hermitian and their spectral properties are known in detail, from the localization of the eigenvalues to the asymptotic spectral distribution in the Weyl sense; see Definition
1.1
,
Theorem
1.1
,
[

23
]

In the current work we focus on the case where

so that

. We say that a cosine expansion function

is monotone if it is either increasing or decreasing over the interval
[0,\pi]

In

Here we are interested to extend the machinery both theoretically and computationally to the more involved case of linear combinations of matrix-order depending symbols (see (

•

proving formally the asymptotic expansions;

•

1.2
The Problem and the Literature

The eigenvalues of Toeplitz matrices
T_{n}(f)
with a real-valued symbol
f
, satisfying some conditions and tracing out a simple loop over the interval
[-\pi,\pi]
, are known to admit an asymptotic expansion with the form

\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f))=f(d_{j,n})+c_{1}(d_{j,n})h+c_{2}(d_{j,n})h^{2}+O(h^{3}),

where
h=\frac{1}{n+1}
,
d_{j,n}=\pi jh
, and
c_{k}
are some bounded coefficients depending only on
f
. The numerical results presented in the literature suggests that the effective conditions for the expansion to hold are weaker and reduce to an even character of
f
and monotonicity over
[0,\pi]
.

As already mentioned in Subsection
1.1
, we investigate the superposition caused over this expansion, when considering a linear combination of symbols, that is

\lambda_{j}\big(T_{n}(f_{0}+\beta_{n}^{(1)}f_{1}+\beta_{n}^{(2)}f_{2}+\cdots)\big),

(1.4)

where
\beta_{n}^{(t)}=o(\beta_{n}^{(s)})
if
t>s
and the symbols
f_{j}
are either simple loop or satisfy the weaker conditions mentioned before.
We prove that the asymptotic expansion holds also in this setting under mild assumptions and we show numerically that there is much more to investigate, opening the door to linear in time algorithms for the computation of eigenvalues of large matrices of this type.

The problem is of application interest in particular in the case where the coefficients of the linear combination are given functions of
h
, considering spectral features of matrices stemming from the numerical approximation of standard differential operators and distributed order fractional differential equations
[
6
,
16
,
28
]
. In particular, for standard differential operators, our approach could be very promising for any local approximation technique of integro-differential operators, giving rise to
\glt
matrix-sequences (see
[
2
,
3
,
23
,
24
]
and references therein), with special attention to the case of Finite Elements
[
12
]
and Isogeometric analysis both with maximal regularity and intermediate regularity
[
13
]
.

Furthermore, from a theoretical viewpoint, it is worth stressing that in the current work it is the first time that an eigenvalue expansion is theoretically obtained for a Toeplitz matrix-sequence with a symbol depending on
n
.

In the next steps we introduce the essential of the
\glt
theory (see
[
2
,
3
,
23
,
24
]
and references therein) and of the new concept of
\glt
momentary symbols
[
8
]
, related to matrix structures as those appearing in (
1.4
).

1.2.1
\glt

\glt

In this technical part we give the essentials of the
\glt

\glt
matrix-sequences and we spend few words on the new concept of
\glt

Definition 1.1
.

Let
f\colon D\to\mathbb{C}
be a measurable function defined on the Lebesgue measurable set
D
of positive and finite measure. Assume that
\{A_{n}\}_{n}
is a sequence of matrices such that
\dim(A_{n})=d_{n}\rightarrow\infty
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
and with eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}(A_{n})
and singular values
\sigma_{j}(A_{n})
,
j=1,\ldots,d_{n}
.

•

We say that
\{A_{n}\}_{n}
is distributed as
f
over
D
in the sense of the eigenvalues, and we write
\{A_{n}\}_{n}\sim_{\lambda}(f,D),
if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{d_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{n}}F(\lambda_{j}(A_{n}))=\frac{1}{\mu(D)}\int_{D}F(f(t))\dif t,

(1.5)

for every continuous function
F
with compact support. In this case, we say that
f
is the
spectral symbol
of
\{A_{n}\}_{n}
.

•

We say that
\{A_{n}\}_{n}
is distributed as
f
over
D
in the sense of the singular values, and we write
\{A_{n}\}_{p}\sim_{\sigma}(f,D),
if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{d_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{n}}F(\sigma_{j}(A_{n}))=\frac{1}{\mu(D)}\int_{D}F(|f(t)|)\dif t,

(1.6)

for every continuous function
F
with compact support. In this case, we say that
f
is the
symbol
of
\{A_{n}\}_{n}
in the sense of the singular values.

Throughout the paper, when the domain can be easily inferred from the context, we replace the notation
\{A_{n}\}_{n}\sim_{\lambda,\sigma}(f,D)
with
\{A_{n}\}_{n}\sim_{\lambda,\sigma}f
. A noteworthy result due to Tilli
[
36
]
and Tyrtyshnikov and Zamarashkin
[
39
]
is the following

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
f\in L^{1}([-\pi,\pi])
, then
\{T_{n}(f)\}_{{n}}\sim_{\sigma}(f,[-\pi,\pi]).
If
f
is a real-valued function almost everywhere,
then
\{T_{n}(f)\}_{{n}}\sim_{\lambda}(f,[-\pi,\pi]).

In the sequel, we introduce the
\glt
class, a
\ast
-algebra of matrix-sequences containing Toeplitz matrix-sequences. The formal definition of
\glt

3

23
,
24

, respectively. The original construction is involved and needs a whole coherent set of definitions and mathematical objects; see

35

\glt

\glt

[

]

Throughout, we use the following notation

\{A_{n}\}_{n}\glteq{\kappa(x,\theta)},\qquad\kappa\colon[0,1]\times[-\pi,\pi]\rightarrow\mathbb{C},

to say that the sequence

is
\glt

\glt

\kappa(x,\theta)

Here we list four main features of
\glt

GLT 1

Let
\{A_{n}\}_{n}\glteq\kappa
with a function
\kappa\colon G\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
,
G=[0,1]\times[-\pi,\pi]
, then
\{A_{n}\}_{n}\sim_{\sigma}(\kappa,G)

are Hermitian, then it also holds that
\{A_{n}\}_{n}\sim_{\lambda}(\kappa,G)

GLT 2

The set of
\glt
sequences forms a
\ast

\{A_{n}\}_{n}\glteq\kappa_{1}
and
\{B_{n}\}_{n}\glteq\kappa_{2}

\bullet

\{\alpha A_{n}+\beta B_{n}\}_{n}\glteq\alpha\kappa_{1}+\beta\kappa_{2},\quad\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C};

\bullet

\{A_{n}B_{n}\}_{n}\glteq\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2};

\bullet

\{A_{n}^{*}\}_{n}\glteq{\kappa^{*}_{1}};

\bullet

\{A^{-1}_{n}\}_{n}\glteq\kappa_{1}^{-1}
provided that
\kappa_{1}
is invertible a.e.

GLT 3

Any sequence of Toeplitz matrices
\{T_{n}(f)\}_{n}
generated by

is a
\glt
matrix-sequence with symbol
\kappa(x,\theta)=f(\theta)

\glt

\kappa(x,\theta)=a(x)

[D_{n}(a)]_{j,j}=a(\frac{j}{n})
,

.

GLT 4

Let
\{A_{n}\}_{n}\sim_{\sigma}0
. We say that
\{A_{n}\}_{n}
is a
zero-distributed matrix-sequence
. Every zero-distributed matrix-sequence is a
\glt

0
and viceversa, i.e.,
\{A_{n}\}_{n}\sim_{\sigma}0

\iff

\{A_{n}\}_{n}\glteq 0
.

If

2
Main results

For a constant
\alpha\geqslant 0
, the well-known weighted Wiener algebra
W^{\alpha}
is the collection of all functions
f\colon[0,2\pi]\to\mathbb{C}
whose Fourier coefficients
\hat{f}_{j}
satisfy

\|f\|_{\alpha}\coloneqq\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}|\hat{f}_{j}|(|j|+1)^{\alpha}<\infty.

We address real-valued symbols
f
in
W^{\alpha}
, tracing out a simple loop and satisfying the following conditions:

1.

The range of
f
is a segment
[0,\mu]
with
\mu>0
.

2.

f(0)=f(2\pi)=0
,
f^{\prime}(0)=f^{\prime}(2\pi)=0
, and
f^{\prime\prime}(0)=f^{\prime\prime}(2\pi)>0
.

3.

There is a
\sigma_{0}\in(0,2\pi)
such that
f(\sigma_{0})=\mu
,
f^{\prime}(\sigma)>0
for
0<\sigma<\sigma_{0}
,
f^{\prime}(\sigma)<0
for
\sigma_{0}<\sigma<2\pi
,
f^{\prime}(\sigma_{0})=0
, and
f^{\prime\prime}(\sigma_{0})<0
.

The collection of all these symbols is called the
simple loop class
and is denoted by
\SL^{\alpha}
.
In this note we consider symmetric symbols in
\SL^{\alpha}
, that is
\sigma_{0}=\pi
and
f(s)=f(2\pi-s)
for each
s\in[0,\pi]
. Obviously, it is enough to study such a symbols in the interval
[0,\pi]
.

For every
\lambda\in[0,\mu]
there exists a unique
s\in[0,\pi]
satisfying
f(s)=\lambda
, and the symbol
f-\lambda
has 2 zeros:
\pm s
, implying that the Toeplitz operator
T(f-\lambda)
is not invertible. According to the simple-loop method (see
[
5
]
), by considering

\displaystyle b_{f}(\sigma,s)

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{f(\sigma)-f(s)}{4\sin\big(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\si-s}{2}$}}\big)\sin\big(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\si+s}{2}$}}\big)}

(2.1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f(\sigma)-f(s)}{2(\cos(s)-\cos(\sigma))}\qquad(\sigma\in[0,2\pi],\ s\in[0,\pi]),

we obtain a real and continuous function, which is also bounded away from zero. The resulting operator
T(b_{f}(\cdot,s))
is invertible and therefore, since the finite section method can be applied (see
[
11
]
for example), the related finite Toeplitz matrices
T_{n}(b_{f}(\cdot,s))
are also invertible. Note that

can be thought of as the quotient between
f-\lambda
and

, which is similar to the preconditioning process of the ill-conditioned matrix
T_{n}(f-\lambda)
used for example in
[

,
30
]
; for a general account on preconditioning in a Toeplitz setting see
[
29
,
23
]
and references therein.

For a function
u
with a singularity at some point in the interval
I
, let
\,\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\nmid$}}\kern-5.05557pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-3.4017pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.98824pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.83476pt}}\!\int_{I}u(x)\dif x
be the Cauchy principal value of the singular integral
\int_{I}u(x)\dif x
. The function
b_{f}
admits the so called Wiener–Hopf factorization
b_{f}=[b_{f}]_{-}[b_{f}]_{+}
(index zero) with

[b_{f}]_{\pm}(t,s)\coloneqq\exp\Big\{\frac{1}{2}\log b_{f}(t,s)\pm\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\,\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\nmid$}}\kern-5.05557pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-3.4017pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.98824pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.83476pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\log b_{f}(\tau,s)}{\tau-t}\dif\tau\Big\},

where

and
\sigma\in[0,2\pi]
.
We recall that the Wiener–Hopf factorization (also called method or decomposition) was introduced by N. Wiener and E. Hopf in 1931. What we call Wiener–Hopf factorization has its origin in the work of Gakhov
[
21
]
, but Mark Krein
[
27
]
was the first to understand the operator theoretic essence and its algebraic background, and to present it in a clear way; for a nice and modern explanation see
[
9
, §1.4]
.

As in
[
4
,
5
]
, we define the function
\eta_{f}\colon[0,\pi]\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle\eta_{f}(s)

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\nmid$}}\kern-5.05557pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-3.4017pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.98824pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.83476pt}}\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\log b_{f}(\sigma,s)}{\tan\big(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\si-s}{2}$}}\big)}\dif\sigma-\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\nmid$}}\kern-5.05557pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-3.4017pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.98824pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.83476pt}}\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\log b_{f}(\sigma,s)}{\tan\big(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\si+s}{2}$}}\big)}\dif\sigma

(2.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sin(s)}{2\pi}\,\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\nmid$}}\kern-5.05557pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-3.4017pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.98824pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.83476pt}}\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\log b_{f}(\sigma,s)}{\cos(s)-\cos(\sigma)}\dif\sigma.

Now
[
5
, Theorem 2.3]
tells us, in particular, that the eigenvalues of
T_{n}(f)
are given by

\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f))=f(d_{j,n})+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}c_{\ell}(d_{j,n})\,h^{\ell}+E_{j,n,\alpha},

(2.3)

where

•

the eigenvalues of
T_{n}(f)
are arranged in nondecreasing order;

•

h\coloneqq\frac{1}{n+1}
and

;

•

the coefficients

depend only on

and can be found explicitly, for example

(2.4)

•

E_{j,n,\alpha}=O(h^{\alpha})
is the remainder (error) term, which satisfies the bounding
|E_{j,n,\alpha}|\leqslant\kappa_{\alpha}h^{\alpha}
for some constant
\kappa_{\alpha}
depending only on
\alpha
and
f
.

For

and a constant

, we investigate the relationship between the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f))
,
\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(g))
, and
\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f+\beta g))

\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f+\beta g))=f(d_{j,n})+\beta g(d_{j,n})+O(h).

Indeed, as a challenge in the field, we are looking for a more detailed result involving a complete expansion and a real constant

depending on

.
The symbol

depends on
n

is

or

We start with the symbol

, that is

. Consider the function

\psi(s)\coloneqq\frac{\sin(s)}{2\pi}\,\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\nmid$}}\kern-5.05557pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-3.4017pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.98824pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.83476pt}}\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{b_{g}(\sigma,s)}{b_{f}(\sigma,s)(\cos(s)-\cos(\sigma))}\dif\sigma.

.

Let
f
and
g
be two symmetric symbols in
\SL^{\alpha}
with

. Then

\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f+gh))=f(d_{j,n})+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}\Psi_{\ell}(d_{j,n})h^{\ell}+E_{j,n,\alpha},

are bounded functions from

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g-f^{\prime}\eta_{f},

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}\eta_{f}^{2}+f^{\prime}\eta_{f}\eta_{f}^{\prime}-f^{\prime}\psi-g^{\prime}\eta_{f}.

The remainder

satisfies the bounding

for some constant

, and

For the next result we will study the eigenvalues corresponding to the symbol

, that is

\varphi(s)\coloneqq\frac{\sin(s)}{2\pi}\,\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\nmid$}}\kern-5.05557pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-3.4017pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.98824pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.83476pt}}\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{b_{g}(\sigma,s)}{(b_{f}(\sigma,s)+b_{g}(\sigma,s))(\cos(s)-\cos(\sigma))}\dif\sigma.

.

Let
f
and

be two symmetric symbols in

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1,1}

\displaystyle g,

\displaystyle-\eta_{f+g}(f^{\prime}+g^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2,2}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2,0}

3
Proof of the main results

Our specific aim is to recreate the so called simple-loop method working with the symbol
f+\beta_{n}g
for two particular values of
\beta_{n}
, i.e.
h
and
h^{h}
. As we will show, in both cases we arrive at the important equation

\frac{s}{h}+\eta_{f+\beta_{n}g}(s)=\pi j+\Delta_{j,n,\alpha},

(3.1)

where
\Delta_{j,n,\alpha}
satisfies some smoothness and bounding conditions (the previous relation (
3.1
) is the key for the calculation of asymptotic expansions for the eigenvalues of several Toeplitz matrix-sequences, see for example
[
4
, Eq. 4.15]
and
[
5
, Eq. 5.7]
). At this point we decided to expand
\eta_{f+\beta_{n}g}
into factors with coefficients not involving
n
, then we made a technical work claiming the function on the left side of (
3.1
) is a contraction and henceforth, we can use the Banach fixed point theorem in order to solve it for
s
. Thus, we start with the following technical result.

Lemma 3.1
.

As
n\to\infty
, we have

(i)

\eta_{f+gh}(s)=\eta_{f}(s)+\psi(s)h+O(h^{2})
,

(ii)

\eta_{f+gh^{h}}(s)=\eta_{f+g}(s)+\varphi(s)h\log(h)+O(h^{2}\log^{2}(h))
,

where
\psi,\varphi
are
\lfloor\alpha\rfloor-1
times continuously differentiable functions, given by

\displaystyle\psi(s)

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{\sin(s)}{2\pi}\,\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\nmid$}}\kern-5.05557pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-3.4017pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.98824pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.83476pt}}\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{b_{g}(\sigma,s)}{b_{f}(\sigma,s)(\cos(s)-\cos(\sigma))}\dif\sigma,

\displaystyle\varphi(s)

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{\sin(s)}{2\pi}\,\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\nmid$}}\kern-5.05557pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-3.4017pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.98824pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.83476pt}}\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{b_{g}(\sigma,s)}{(b_{f}(\sigma,s)-b_{g}(\sigma,s))(\cos(s)-\cos(\sigma))}\dif\sigma,

Proof.

By
[
5
, Lemma 4.4]
we know that
s\mapsto b_{f}(\cdot,s)
and
s\mapsto b_{g}(\cdot,s)
act continuously from
[0,\pi]
to
W^{\alpha-1}
, which combined with the fact that the singular integral operator is continuous over the weighted Wiener algebras, claims that
\psi,\varphi
are
\lfloor\alpha\rfloor-1
times continuously differentiable functions. For the part (i), we use the expansion

\displaystyle\log(b_{f}+b_{g}h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log(b_{f})+\log^{\prime}(b_{f})b_{g}h+\frac{1}{2}\log^{\prime\prime}(b_{f})b_{g}^{2}h^{2}+O(h^{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log(b_{f})+\frac{b_{g}}{b_{f}}h-\frac{b_{g}^{2}}{2b_{f}^{2}}h^{2}+O(h^{3}).

From the definition of
\eta
(
2.2
) we get

\displaystyle\eta_{f+gh}(s)-\eta_{f}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sin(s)}{2\pi}\,\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\nmid$}}\kern-5.05557pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-3.4017pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.98824pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.83476pt}}\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\log(b_{f}(\sigma,s)+b_{g}h(\sigma,s))-\log b_{f}(\sigma,s)}{\cos(s)-\cos(\sigma)}\dif\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi(s)h+O(h^{2}),

proving the first part.

For the part (ii), we use the expansion

h^{h}=\mathrm{e}^{h\log(h)}=1+h\log(h)+\frac{1}{2}h^{2}\log^{2}(h)+O(h^{3}|\log^{3}(h)|)

and we easily arrive at

b_{f}+b_{g}h^{h}=b_{f}+b_{g}+b_{g}h\log(h)+\frac{b_{g}}{2}h^{2}\log^{2}(h)+O(h^{3}|\log^{3}(h)|).

Note that for every
n
the symbols
b_{f}+b_{g}h^{h}
and
b_{f}+b_{g}
are positive, bounded, and bounded away from zero. Thus we can apply the logarithm to the former and expand around the latter so that

\log(b_{f}+b_{g}h^{h})=\log(b_{f}+b_{g})+\frac{b_{g}}{b_{f}+b_{g}}h\log(h)+\frac{b_{f}b_{g}}{2(b_{f}+b_{g})}h^{2}\log^{2}(h)+O(h^{3}|\log^{3}(h)|).

Finally, combining this expansion with (
2.2
), the second part is proven.
∎

The following lemma can be derived directly from the expansion (
2.3
). It shows how the eigenvalues of the sum of two Toeplitz matrices are related with the individual ones, which is highly nontrivial given the inherent nonlinearity of the eigenvalues with respect to the entries of the considered matrix.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
f
and
g
be two symmetric symbols in
\SL^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\geqslant 2
. Then we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f+g))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f))+\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(g))+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}Q_{\ell}(d_{j,n})h^{\ell}+E_{j,n,\alpha},

where
Q_{\ell}
are bounded functions from
[0,\pi]
to
\mathbb{R}
depending only on
f,g
, that can be expressed explicitly. For example

\displaystyle Q_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\eta_{f}-\eta_{f+g})+g^{\prime}(\eta_{g}-\eta_{f+g}),

\displaystyle Q_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{2}(\eta_{f+g}^{2}-\eta_{f}^{2})+\frac{g^{\prime\prime}}{2}(\eta_{f+g}^{2}-\eta_{g}^{2})

\displaystyle+f^{\prime}(\eta_{f+g}\eta_{f+g}^{\prime}-\eta_{f}\eta_{f}^{\prime})+g^{\prime}(\eta_{f+g}\eta_{f+g}^{\prime}-\eta_{g}\eta_{g}^{\prime}).

The remainder
E_{j,n,\alpha}=O(h^{\alpha})
satisfies the bounding
|E_{j,n,\alpha}|\leqslant\kappa_{\alpha}h^{\alpha}
for some constant
\kappa_{\alpha}
depending only on
\alpha
,
f
, and
g
.

Proof.

Let
c_{k}^{f}

(k=1,2)
be the function involved in the expansion (
2.3
) associated with the symbol
f
. We recall that
\eta_{f+g}
is associated with
f+g
. It is easy to show that
b=b_{f}+b_{g}
. The situation with
\eta_{f+g}
is more delicate. Consider the function
\rho\coloneqq\eta_{f+g}-\eta_{f}-\eta_{g}
. From (
2.4
) we obtain

\displaystyle c_{1}^{f+g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-f^{\prime}\eta_{f+g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(f^{\prime}+g^{\prime})(\eta_{f}+\eta_{g}+\rho)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{1}^{f}+c_{1}^{g}+Q_{1},

where
Q_{1}\coloneqq f^{\prime}(\eta_{f}-\eta_{f+g})+g^{\prime}(\eta_{g}-\eta_{f+g})
. Similarly

\displaystyle c_{2}^{f+g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}\eta_{f+g}^{2}+f^{\prime}\eta_{f+g}\eta_{f+g}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(f^{\prime\prime}+g^{\prime\prime})(\eta_{f}+\eta_{g}+\rho)^{2}+(f^{\prime}+g^{\prime})(\eta_{f}+\eta_{g}+\rho)(\eta_{f}^{\prime}+\eta_{g}^{\prime}+\rho^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{2}^{f}+c_{2}^{g}+Q_{2},

where

\displaystyle Q_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(\eta_{f+g}^{2}-\eta_{f}^{2})+\frac{1}{2}g^{\prime\prime}(\eta_{f+g}^{2}-\eta_{g}^{2})

\displaystyle+f^{\prime}(\eta_{f+g}\eta_{f+g}^{\prime}-\eta_{f}\eta_{f}^{\prime})+g^{\prime}(\eta_{f+g}\eta_{f+g}^{\prime}-\eta_{g}\eta_{g}^{\prime}).

According to the simple-loop method, all the involved functions in the terms

are bounded (see
[
4
, §3]
), thus they are also bounded. Finally, putting together the previous expressions for
c_{1}^{f+g}
and
c_{2}^{f+g}
in (
2.3
), the proof is over.
∎

For the proofs of the main theorems we need to mimic the complete calculation of the simple-loop method, but since most of the modifications are very simple, we decided to mention only the most relevant. From (
2.1
) it is clear that
b_{f+\beta_{n}g}=b_{f}+\beta_{n}b_{g}
. Following
[
5
]
we can see that all the results in Section 3 can be applied to the symbol
f+\beta_{n}g
for any real and positive
\beta_{n}
bounded uniformly for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and that all the results in Section 4 can be applied to
b_{f}
and
b_{g}
, separately. One of the key ingredients of Section 5 is the function
\Theta_{k}(t,s)\coloneqq[T_{k}^{-1}(b(\cdot,s))\chi_{0}](t)
, where
\chi_{0}(t)\coloneqq 1
, who is a polynomial satisfying certain properties. After the adjustments, in
[
5
, Lemma 5.1]
we obtained

\Theta_{k}(t,s)=[\{T_{k}(b_{f}(\cdot,s))+\beta_{n}T_{k}(b_{g}(\cdot,s))\}^{-1}\chi_{0}](t),

which is still a polynomial with the same properties, but its coefficients now depend on
n
.

After the implementation of the following minor changes, we was able to replicate the result in Lemma 5.4, even with the same remaining function
R_{1}^{(n)}
.

•

To find a bound for the operator
\|T_{n}^{-1}(b_{f+\beta_{n}g}(\cdot,s))\|
in the Lebesgue space
\ell_{1}
, we proceed as follows, since the function
b_{f+\beta_{n}g}=b_{f}+\beta_{n}b_{g}
is positive, bounded, and bounded away from zero, the Toeplitz matrix
T_{n}(b_{f+\beta_{n}g})
is positive definite and invertible, hence its operator norm equals its largest eigenvalue (which must be real and positive), and which is naturally bounded from below by
\inf\{b_{f+\beta_{n}g}(s)\colon 0\leqslant s\leqslant\pi\}
. Then

\|T_{n}^{-1}(b_{f+\beta_{n}g}(\cdot,s))\|\leqslant\inf\{b_{f}(s)+\beta_{n}b_{g}(s)\colon 0\leqslant s\leqslant\pi\}^{-1}.

If the constant
\beta_{n}
takes the value
h
, the last infimum is greater than
\inf\{b_{f}(s)\colon 0\leqslant s\leqslant\pi\}
, while for
\beta_{n}=h^{h}
it is greater than

\inf\{b_{f}(s)\colon 0\leqslant s\leqslant\pi\}+\inf\{b_{g}(s)\colon 0\leqslant s\leqslant\pi\}.

In both cases the provided bound is positive, finite, and does not depend on
n
.

•

For the two values of
\beta_{n}
considered, the function
b_{f+\beta_{n}g}
is continuous, bounded, and bounded away from zero uniformly in
n
. Thus, the boundness of the Wiener–Hopf factor
[b_{f+\beta_{n}g}]_{+}^{-1}
is a direct consequence of the fact that the singular integral operator is bounded over the space of continuous functions.

Lemma
3.3
is a plain and direct consequence, which for the reader convenience we present here, but without a proof.

Lemma 3.3
.

For every sufficiently large natural number
n
there exists a real-valued function
R_{2}^{(n)}\in C[0,\pi]
with the following properties:

(i)

a number
\lambda=f(s)+\beta_{n}g(s)
is an eigenvalue of

if and only if there is a

such that

\frac{s}{h}+\eta_{f+\beta_{n}g}(s)-R_{2}^{(n)}(s)=j\pi;

(ii)

R_{2}^{(n)}(0)=R_{2}^{(n)}(\pi)=0
;

(iii)

\|R_{2}^{(n)}\|_{L_{\infty}}=o(h^{\alpha-1})
as
n\to\infty
.

.

Assume that

and let
\beta_{n}=h
and for simplicity write

instead of

. Combining Lemmas

and

G_{n}(s)=j\pi,

(3.2)

where

. The function

is continuous on the interval

with
G_{n}(0)=0
and
G_{n}(\pi)=\frac{\pi}{h}
, hence by

we know that

(i)

the eigenvalues of

are all distinct:

\lambda_{1}(T_{n}(f+gh))<\cdots<\lambda_{n}(T_{n}(f+gh));

(ii)

the numbers
s_{j,n}\coloneqq\big[f+gh\big]_{[0,\pi]}^{-1}(\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f+gh))

(j=1,\ldots,n)
satisfy the relation (
3.2
) with

(iii)

for every sufficiently large

, (

) has exactly one solution

for each

.

Let
F_{n}(s)\coloneqq\frac{s}{h}+\eta_{f}(s)+\psi(s)h

Write (
3.2
) as
s=\Phi_{j,n}(s)+\Delta_{n,\alpha}(s)
where
\Delta_{n,\alpha}(s)=o(h^{\alpha})+O(h^{3})
as
n
grows to infinity. We will iterate over the relation
s=\Phi_{j,n}(s)
. We have
\hat{s}_{j,n}^{(0)}=d
and
\hat{s}_{j,n}^{(1)}=\Phi_{j,n}(\hat{s}_{j,n}^{(0)})=d-\eta_{f}(d)h-\psi(d)h^{2}
. To evaluate
\hat{s}_{j,n}^{(2)}
first note that

\displaystyle\eta_{f}(\hat{s}_{j,n}^{(1)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{f}(d)-\eta_{f}^{\prime}(d)\{\eta_{f}(d)h+\psi(d)h^{2}\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\eta_{f}^{\prime\prime}(d)\{\eta_{f}^{2}(d)+\psi^{2}(d)h^{2}+2\eta_{f}(d)\psi(d)h\}h^{2}+O(h^{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{f}(d)-\eta_{f}(d)\eta_{f}^{\prime}(d)h+\big\{\frac{1}{2}\eta_{f}^{2}(d)\eta_{f}^{\prime\prime}(d)-\eta_{f}^{\prime}(d)\psi(d)\big\}h^{2}+O(h^{3})

and that

\displaystyle\psi(\hat{s}_{j,n}^{(1)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi(d)-\psi^{\prime}(d)\{\eta_{f}(d)h+\psi(d)h^{2}\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}(d)\{\eta_{f}^{2}(d)+\psi^{2}(d)h^{2}+2\eta_{f}(d)\psi(d)h\}h^{2}+O(h^{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi(d)-\psi^{\prime}(d)\eta_{f}(d)h+O(h^{2}).

Now by the Taylor theorem we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{s}_{j,n}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d-\eta_{f}(d-\eta_{f}(d)h-\psi(d)h^{2})h-\psi(d-\eta_{f}(d)h-\psi(d)h)h^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d-\eta_{f}(d)h+\{\eta_{f}(d)\eta_{f}^{\prime}(d)-\psi(d)\}h^{2}+O(h^{3}),

which combined with
|s_{j,n}-\hat{s}_{j,n}^{(2)}|=o(h^{\alpha})+O(h^{3})
leads to prove the theorem for the case
2\leqslant\alpha<3
. The remaining cases can be readily proved, essentially in the same manner.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

This proof is quite similar to the previous one, thus to avoid unnecessary repetition, we will only remark the main differences. Taking
\beta_{n}=h^{h}
we obtain

\displaystyle G_{n}(s)

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{s}{h}+\eta_{f+g}(s)+\varphi(s)h\log(h),

\displaystyle F_{n}(s)

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{s}{h}+\eta_{f+g}(s)+\varphi(s)h\log(h),

\displaystyle\Phi_{j,n}(s)

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle d-\eta_{f+g}(s)h-\varphi(s)h^{2}\log(h).

The remaining function
\Delta_{n,\alpha}
now have order
o(h^{\alpha})+O(h^{3}\log^{2}(h))
as
n\to\infty
, and
\hat{s}_{j,n}^{(0)}=d
and
\hat{s}_{j,n}^{(1)}=\Phi_{j,n}(\hat{s}_{j,n}^{(0)})=d-\eta_{f+g}(d)h-\varphi(d)h^{2}\log(h)
. To evaluate
\hat{s}_{j,n}^{(2)}
first note that

\displaystyle\eta_{f+g}(\hat{s}_{j,n}^{(1)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{f+g}(d)-\eta_{f+g}^{\prime}(d)\{\eta_{f+g}(d)h+\varphi(d)h^{2}\log(h)\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\eta_{f+g}^{\prime\prime}(d)\{\eta_{f+g}^{2}(d)+\varphi^{2}(d)h^{2}\log^{2}(h)

\displaystyle+2\eta_{f+g}(d)\varphi(d)h\log(h)\}h^{2}+O(h^{3}|\log^{3}(h)|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{f+g}(d)-\eta_{f+g}(d)\eta_{f+g}^{\prime}(d)h

\displaystyle+\big\{\frac{1}{2}\eta_{f+g}^{2}(d)\eta_{f+g}^{\prime\prime}(d)-\eta_{f+g}^{\prime}(d)\varphi(d)\log(h)\big\}h^{2}+O(h^{3}|\log^{3}(h)|).

and that

\displaystyle\varphi(\hat{s}_{j,n}^{(1)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(d)-\varphi^{\prime}(d)\{\eta_{f+g}(d)h+\varphi(d)h^{2}\log(h)\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(d)\{\eta_{f+g}^{2}(d)+\varphi^{2}(d)h^{2}\log^{2}(h)+2\eta_{f+g}(d)\varphi(d)h\log(h)\}h^{2}+O(h^{3}|\log^{3}(h)|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(d)-\varphi^{\prime}(d)\eta_{f+g}(d)h+O(h^{2}|\log(h)|).

Now by the Taylor theorem we obtain

\hat{s}_{j,n}^{(2)}=d-\eta_{f+g}(d)h+\{\eta_{f+g}(d)\eta_{f+g}^{\prime}(d)-\varphi(d)\log(h)\}h^{2}+O(h^{3}|\log^{3}(h)|),

which combined with

give us the theorem for the case

. The remaining cases can be readily proved.
∎

4
Numerical tests and an algorithm proposal

The purpose of our main results is to provide asymptotic expansions revealing the fine structure of the eigenvalue behavior in a matrix-less fashion, that is, without the need of any matrix inversion, multiplication by vectors, or even the storage of its entries. Such an expansion has proved to be useful in several applications. In
[
19
,
20
]
the authors exploited those expansions and provided a clever and fully numerical algorithm, that calculates the eigenvalues of arbitrarily large matrices within machine precision. More specifically they devised an extrapolation algorithm for computing the eigenvalues of banded symmetric Toeplitz matrices with a high level of accuracy and low computation cost, starting from the computation of the eigenvalues of small size matrices, in the same spirit of the classical extrapolation procedures for the summation of smooth functions.

As in previous works, the purpose of this section is to check if we calculate the involved coefficients in all of our results correctly, and we want to prove that our asymptotic expressions deliver good approximations even for values of
n
in the early hundreds. The numerical calculation of a singular integral can be very difficult, we therefore use a standard regularization trick (see
[
4
, §7]
) which works as follows. For an integral of the form

\mathcal{I}(v)=\,\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\nmid$}}\kern-5.05557pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-3.4017pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.98824pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.83476pt}}\!\int_{\gamma}^{\delta}\frac{f(u,v)}{h(u,v)}\dif u,

where for some
u_{0}\in[\gamma,\delta]
, we have
h(u_{0},v)=0
and
f(u_{0},v)\neq 0
, we can write

\mathcal{I}(v)=\,\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\nmid$}}\kern-5.05557pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-3.4017pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.98824pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.83476pt}}\!\int_{\gamma}^{\delta}\frac{f(u,v)-f(u_{0},v)}{h(u,v)}\dif u+f(u_{0},v)\,\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\nmid$}}\kern-5.05557pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-3.4017pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.98824pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.83476pt}}\!\int_{\gamma}^{\delta}\frac{\dif u}{h(u,v)},

and in most cases, the above integrals are easier to tackle than
\mathcal{I}
itself. For the case of
\eta
, the value of
\,\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\nmid$}}\kern-5.05557pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-3.4017pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.98824pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.83476pt}}\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cot\big(\frac{\sigma-s}{2}\big)\dif\sigma=0
, and hence we obtain

\eta(s)=\frac{\sin(s)}{2\pi}\,\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\nmid$}}\kern-5.05557pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-3.4017pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.98824pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\nmid$}}\kern-2.83476pt}}\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\log b(\sigma,s)-\log b(s,s)}{\cos(s)-\cos(\sigma)}\dif\sigma,

where
b(s,s)
can be calculated with the L’Hôpital rule as

b(s,s)=\frac{f^{\prime}(s)}{2\sin(s)},\quad b(0,0)=\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(0)}{2},\quad\mbox{and}\quad b(\pi,\pi)=-\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(0)}{2}.

Consider the simple-loop symbol given by

f(\sigma)\coloneqq\frac{(1+\rho)^{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{1-\cos(\sigma)}{1-2\rho\cos(\sigma)+\rho^{2}}\qquad(0\leqslant\sigma\leqslant 2\pi),

for a constant

4.1
A substantial improvement of the algorithm in
[
19
]

For
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
let
f_{k}(\theta)\coloneqq(2-2\cos(\theta))^{k}
and
\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
. Consider the symbol

F_{n}(\theta)\coloneqq f_{2}(\theta)+\alpha_{1}f_{1}(\theta)h^{2}+\alpha_{0}f_{0}(\theta)h^{4}.

(4.1)

The related Toeplitz matrices
T_{n}(F_{n})
appear when discretizing differential equations with the Finite Differences method.

Figure 6:
The symbol
F_{n}
and its first two derivatives.

The functions
f_{k}
with
k\neq 2
, do not belong to
\SL^{\alpha}
for any
\alpha
, thus
F_{n}
do not fully satisfy our hypothesis and we can not apply our theoretical results, but in
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
there is numerical evidence suggesting that, nevertheless we can expect an eigenvalue expansion of the form

\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(F_{n}))\sim f_{2}(d_{j,n})+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}c_{\ell}(d_{j,n})h^{\ell},

(4.2)

where
\sim
means asymptotic expansion. We now proceed to determine the continuous functions
c_{\ell}
following the algorithm proposed in
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
. This algorithm is a clever interplay between extrapolation and interpolation. For given natural numbers
k
and
n_{0}
, the extrapolation step, gives us a numerical approximation of
c_{\ell}

(\ell=1,\ldots,k-1)
at the regular mesh
\pi jh_{0}

(j=1,\ldots,n_{0})
of the interval
[0,\pi]
. Then, in the interpolation step, we use certain polynomial interpolation to obtain the value of
c_{\ell}
at any point in the interval
[0,\pi]
.

We noticed that this algorithm can produce large errors when evaluating
c_{\ell}
close to the extreme points
\{0,\pi\}
, i.e.
10^{3}
bigger that the rest. As we can see in Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
, the functions
c_{\ell}
are a sum of products of
\eta
, the symbol, and its derivatives, and we must have
\eta(0)=\eta(\pi)=0
(see (
2.2
)). Therefore, considering the nature of the symbol (
4.1
) we have

c_{2}(0)=\alpha_{1}f_{1}(0)=0
,

c_{2}(\pi)=\alpha_{1}f_{1}(\pi)=4\alpha_{1}
,

c_{4}(0)=\alpha_{0}f_{0}(0)=0
,

c_{4}(\pi)=\alpha_{0}f_{0}(\pi)=\alpha_{0}
,

while
c_{\ell}(0)=c_{\ell}(\pi)=0
for
\ell\neq 2,4
. Hence we propose here to include those values in the interpolation step. Our numerical results reveal that the errors in the extreme points almost disappear and that the approximation of the functions
c_{\ell}
improves in such a way that the respective errors behave better when
\ell
increases (see Tables
4
and
5
).
We emphasize that this contribution represents a substantial improvement to the algorithm presented in
[
19
]
and it is a general idea to be used in various contexts.

5
Conclusions, Perspectives, and Open Problems

The eigenvalues of Toeplitz matrices
T_{n}(f)
with a real-valued symbol
f
, satisfying some conditions and tracing out a simple loop over the interval
[-\pi,\pi]
, are known to admit an asymptotic expansion with the form

\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f))=f(d_{j,n})+c_{1}(d_{j,n})h+c_{2}(d_{j,n})h^{2}+O(h^{3}),

where
h=\frac{1}{n+1}
,
d_{j,n}=\pi jh
, and
c_{k}
are some bounded coefficients depending only on
f
.
In practice, the latter expansion is numerically observed under the only condition of monotonicity and even character of the generating function
[
20
]
.

In this note we have investigated the superposition caused over this expansion, when considering a linear combination of symbols and, from a theoretical point of view, we stress that this is the first time that an eigenvalue expansion is theoretically obtained for a Toeplitz matrix-sequence with a symbol depending on
n
. As a further relevant contribution we have improved the precision of the algorithm in
[
19
]
, by using analytic information obtained in our theoretical findings.

The problem has noteworthy applications in the differential setting, when the coefficients of the linear combination are given functions of
h
:
In particular, by using the new expansions, we can give matrix-less eigensolvers for large matrices stemming from the numerical approximation of standard differential operators and distributed order fractional differential equations.

\glt
momentary symbols, a quite new research line discussed in
[
7
,
8
]
