Complete Positivity and Thermal Relaxation in Quadratic Quantum Master Equations
F. Nicacio, T. Koide

Abstract
The ultimate goal of this project is to develop a systematic method for deriving quantum master equations that satisfy the requirements of a completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) map, further describing thermal relaxation processes. In this paper, we assume that the quantum master equation is obtained through the canonical quantization of the generalized Brownian motion proposed in [ Physics Letters A, 494 , 129277 (2024) ]. At least classically, this dynamics describes the thermal relaxation process regardless of the choice of the system Hamiltonian. The remaining task is to identify the parameters ensuring that the quantum master equation meets complete positivity. We limit our discussion to many-body quadratic Hamiltonians and establish a CPTP criterion for our quantum master equation. This criterion is useful for applying our quantum master equation to models with interaction such as a network model, which has been used to investigate how quantum effects modify heat conduction.



I
Introduction

In recent years, there has been considerable discussion about how thermodynamical
descriptions can be extended to small fluctuating systems.
For this, there are at least two approaches:
stochastic thermodynamics
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
and
quantum thermodynamics
[
4
,
5
]
.
The former considers classical systems and assumes stochastic dynamics
like Brownian motion and the latter considers
open quantum systems.

In classical systems, it is possible to introduce a model of Brownian motion
applicable to any particle Hamiltonian, allowing for the definitions of heat, work,
and entropy in a manner consistent with thermodynamics
[
7
]
.
In open quantum systems, the time evolution is often assumed to be given by
a completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP)
map
with the Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad (GKSL)
equation
exemplifying such dynamics.
Admittedly heat, work, and entropy can be defined in line with thermodynamics
through the GKSL equation, at least when the system’s Hamiltonian is given
by a harmonic oscillator, but its applicability
to general Hamiltonians remains unclear.
It is thus still worthwhile to study the systematic derivation of a master equation
applicable to describe thermal relaxation processes.

Recently, the present authors introduced the generalized model of Brownian motion
and considered its canonical quantization
[
7
]
.
Compared to the
GKSL
equation, this approach has at least two advantages.
One is its simplicity:
our master equation is determined only by the system Hamiltonian and
a few parameters (coupling constants and temperatures).
The other is that, regardless of the choice of the system Hamiltonian,
the classical limit of the quantum master equation always describes
the thermal relaxation process, as we will show.
Noteworthy, besides the asymptotic approaching towards an equilibrium state,
thermal relaxation here also implies the appropriate inequality
involving heat and entropy change during the process,
as dictated by the second law of thermodynamics.
Nevertheless, the obtained evolution is generally non-
CPTP
.
Interestingly, however,
for a harmonic oscillator a tuning of
the coupling parameters ensures the conformity with a
CPTP
map
[
7
]
.
Therefore, it is important to investigate whether
our quantum master equation satisfies the
CPTP
requirement
for arbitrary Hamiltonians.

In this paper, we assume that the quantum dynamics is governed
by a master equation obtained through
the canonical quantization of generalized Brownian motion
[
7
]
,
and apply it to a general many-body quadratic Hamiltonian.
We then establish a
CPTP
map in our quantum master equation
providing a necessary and sufficient criterion,
which represents quantum constraints for physically admissible parameters
of a genuine quantum dynamics, like an uncertainty principle.
Although the discussion is limited only to quadratic Hamiltonians,
this simplified situation remains of interest for discussing heat conduction,
where the system Hamiltonian is represented by linearly interacting harmonic oscillators,
known as the network model.

This paper is organized as follows.
The derivation of the new quantum master equation proposed in Ref.
[
7
]

is briefly summarized in Sec.
II

and its classical limit is derived in Sec.
III

for a general Hamiltonian.
This quantum master equation is mapped to a general quadratic form,
which is called the quadratic time-convolutionless master equation
and then the criterion to obtain a
CPTP

evolution is discussed in Sec.
IV
.
The relationship between our equation and the
Bloch-Redfield equation is examined in Sec.
V
.
The derived criterion is applied to one degree
of freedom systems in Sec.
VI
,
while in Sec.
VII
, it is applied to the network model.
Section
VIII
is devoted to concluding remarks.

II
Canonical quantization of generalized Brownian motion

In this section,
we give a brief summary of the derivation of the quantum master equation
developed in Ref.
[
7
]
.

Let us consider a general many-body system which is characterized by
the canonical pairs of position and momentum in Cartesian coordinates,

\{q_{i},p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
In the absence of interaction with the environment, the dynamics of this system
is described by a general Hamiltonian
H
which can be non-linear.
Upon introducing this interaction,
the phase space distribution
\rho(\{q_{i},p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},t)

is determined by the following
Fokker-Planck-Kramers (FPK
) equation:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho=\{H,\rho\}_{\text{PB}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{D}_{\text{FPK}}^{(i)}[\rho]\,,

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\text{FPK}}^{(i)}[\rho]:=\beta_{i}^{-1}\gamma_{p_{i}}\{{\rm e}^{-\beta_{i}H}\{{\rm e}^{\beta_{i}H}\rho,q_{i}\}_{\text{PB}},\,q_{i}\}_{\text{PB}}\,\,+

\displaystyle\beta_{i}^{-1}\gamma_{q_{i}}\{{\rm e}^{-\beta_{i}H}\{{\rm e}^{\beta_{i}H}\rho,p_{i}\}_{\text{PB}},\,p_{i}\}_{\text{PB}}\,,

where
\{\bullet,\bullet\}_{\text{PB}}
denotes the Poisson bracket and,

\gamma_{p_{i}}
and
\gamma_{q_{i}}
are real parameters.
In this equation,
the
i
-th degree of freedom, described by the pair
(q_{i},p_{i})
,
interacts with a heat bath with
\beta_{i}=(k_{B}T_{i})^{-1}
,
where
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant and
T_{i}
is temperature.
When all baths have the same temperature
\beta_{1}=...=\beta_{n}=\beta
,
the stationary state is given by the thermal equilibrium state
\sim{\rm e}^{-\beta H}

for any Hamiltonian
H
bounded from below,
including the relativistic Hamiltonian
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.
Independently of the choice of
\gamma_{q_{i}}
and
\gamma_{p_{i}}
,
heat, work, and entropy are introduced in such a way that the laws analogous
to the first and second laws of thermodynamics are satisfied
[
7
]
.

As shown in Ref.
[
7
]
,
the canonical quantization of Eq. (
1
)
leads to the quantum master equation

\displaystyle\tfrac{d}{dt}\hat{\rho}(t)=\tfrac{\mathsf{i}}{\hbar}\ [\hat{\rho}(t),\hat{H}]+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{D}^{(i)}_{\text{Q}}[\hat{\rho}]\,,

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{(i)}_{\text{Q}}[\hat{\rho}]:=-\frac{\gamma_{p_{i}}}{\beta_{i}\hbar^{2}}[{\rm e}^{-\tfrac{\beta_{i}}{2}\!\hat{H}}[{\rm e}^{\tfrac{\beta_{i}}{2}\!\hat{H}}\,\hat{\rho}\,{\rm e}^{\tfrac{\beta_{i}}{2}\!\hat{H}},\hat{q}_{i}]{\rm e}^{-\tfrac{\beta_{i}}{2}\!\hat{H}},\hat{q}_{i}]

\displaystyle\hskip 45.52458pt-\frac{\gamma_{q_{i}}}{\beta_{i}\hbar^{2}}[{\rm e}^{-\tfrac{\beta_{i}}{2}\!\hat{H}}[{\rm e}^{\tfrac{\beta_{i}}{2}\!\hat{H}}\,\hat{\rho}\,{\rm e}^{\tfrac{\beta_{i}}{2}\!\hat{H}},\hat{p}_{i}]{\rm e}^{-\tfrac{\beta_{i}}{2}\!\hat{H}},\hat{p}_{i}]\,,

see also Eq.(
36
).
When all particles interact with the same heat bath,
or all the baths have the same temperature,
again the thermal equilibrium state
\sim{\rm e}^{-\beta\hat{H}}

is a fixed point of this equation.
Moreover, effortlessly, one can show that this
master equation ensures trace preservation,
\tfrac{d}{dt}{\rm Tr}(\hat{\rho})=0
.
In the case of harmonic oscillators,

\hat{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\hat{p}^{2}_{i}}{2m_{i}}+\frac{m_{i}\omega^{2}_{i}}{2}\hat{q}^{2}_{i}\right)\,,

with
m_{i}
being masses and
\omega_{i}
angular frequencies,
we have shown that this evolution is
CPTP
,
by choosing

\gamma_{p_{i}}=(m_{i}\omega_{i})^{2}\gamma_{q_{i}}
,

i.e.
, it reduces to a
GKSL
form,
thus ensuring complete positivity
[
7
]
.
This condition is reproduced later in Sec.
VI

with a systematic procedure.

For the sake of later convenience, let us reexpress the
FPK
equation.
A point in a
2n
-dimensional phase space is denoted as

x=(q_{1},...\,,q_{n},p_{1},...\,,p_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}\,,

(3)

which is the column vector composed by
n
Cartesian coordinates together
with
n
canonical conjugate momenta.
The
2n\times 2n
symplectic matrix is introduced by

{\sf J}:=\left(\!\!\begin{array}[]{rc}{\bf 0}_{n}&\mathsf{I}_{n}\\
-\mathsf{I}_{n}&{\bf 0}_{n}\end{array}\!\!\right),\,\,\,{\sf J}^{-1}={\sf J}^{\top}=-{\sf J}\,,

where
\mathsf{I}_{n}
and
{\bf 0}_{n}
are, respectively,
the
n\times n
block identity and block zero matrices.

By opening the Poisson bracket in Eq. (
1
)
and employing the notation from Eq. (
3
),
the
FPK
equation can be succinctly written as the continuity equation.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho(x,t)+\partial_{x}\!\cdot\!\mathcal{J}_{\text{K}}=0,

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\text{K}}:=({\sf I}_{2n}+{\bf C}{\sf J})({\sf J}\partial_{x}H)\rho-{\bf D}\,\partial_{x}\rho,

where
\partial_{x}:=(\partial_{q_{1}},...,\partial_{q_{n}},\partial_{p_{1}},...,\partial_{p_{n}})^{\top}

and

\displaystyle{\bf C}

\displaystyle:={\rm Diag}(\gamma_{q_{1}},...\,,\gamma_{q_{n}},\gamma_{p_{1}},...\,,\gamma_{p_{n}})\,,

(5)

\displaystyle{\bf D}

\displaystyle:={\rm Diag}\left(\frac{\gamma_{q_{1}}}{\beta_{1}},...\,,\frac{\gamma_{q_{n}}}{\beta_{n}},\frac{\gamma_{p_{1}}}{\beta_{1}},...\,,\frac{\gamma_{p_{n}}}{\beta_{n}}\right)\,.

A general quadratic Hamiltonian is expressed as

H=\frac{1}{2}(x-\xi)\cdot{\bf H}(x-\xi)+\varphi\,,

(6)

where
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is a column vector,

\varphi\in\mathbb{R}
is a constant,
and the Hessian of the Hamiltonian,
{\bf H}_{ij}=\partial^{2}_{x_{i}x_{j}}H(x)
,
is a
2n\times 2n
symmetric real matrix.
Using that
\partial_{x}H={\bf H}(x-\xi)
under this setup ,
Eq. (
4
) becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\partial_{t}\rho(x,t)+\partial_{x}\!\cdot\!\mathcal{J}_{\text{K}}=0,\\
&\mathcal{J}_{\text{K}}=({\sf I}_{2n}+{\bf C}{\sf J}){\sf J}{\bf H}(x-\xi)\rho-{\bf D}\,\partial_{x}\rho\,.\end{split}

III
Classical Limit of the Quantum Evolution

Before discussing complete positivity,
let us first confirm the classical limit
of our quantum master equation (
2
)
for a general Hamiltonian.

Canonical quantization promotes the column vector (
3
)
to the following column vector of operators:

\hat{x}:=(\hat{q}_{1},...\,,\hat{q}_{n},\hat{p}_{1},...\hat{p}_{n})^{\top}\,,

(7)

and each of its
2n
components, generically called
\hat{x}_{j}
, is Hermitian.
In this notation, the canonical commutation relations
are compactly written as

[\hat{x}_{j},\hat{x}_{k}]=\mathsf{i}\hbar\,\mathsf{J}_{jk}\,.

(8)

Expanding the exponentials in Eq. (
2
),
retaining the terms up to first order in
\beta_{i}
,
and employing the column vector in Eq. (
7
),
we find the quantum master equation in high temperature limit,

\displaystyle\tfrac{d}{dt}\hat{\rho}(t)=\tfrac{\mathsf{i}}{\hbar}\ [\hat{\rho}(t),\hat{H}]+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{D}^{(i)}_{0}[\hat{\rho}]+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{D}^{(i)}_{1}[\hat{\rho}]\,,

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{(i)}_{0}[\hat{\rho}]:=-\frac{1}{{\hbar}}\sum_{j=1}^{2n}{\bf K}_{ij}\left[\{\hat{\rho},\hat{x}_{i}\hat{x}_{j}\}-2\hat{x}_{i}\hat{\rho}\hat{x}_{j}\right]\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{(i)}_{1}[\hat{\rho}]:=\frac{\beta_{i}}{2\hbar}\sum_{j=1}^{2n}{\bf K}_{ij}\left[\{\hat{\rho},[\hat{x}_{i},\hat{H}]\},\hat{x}_{j}\right]\,,

where
\{\bullet,\bullet\}
denotes the anti-commutator and

{\bf K}=\frac{1}{\hbar}{\sf J}{\bf D}{\sf J}^{\top}\!={\rm Diag}\!\left(\frac{\gamma_{p_{1}}}{\hbar\beta_{1}},...,\frac{\gamma_{p_{n}}}{\hbar\beta_{n}},\frac{\gamma_{q_{1}}}{\hbar\beta_{1}},...,\frac{\gamma_{q_{n}}}{\hbar\beta_{n}}\right).

(10)

Noteworthy,
the zeroth order term
\mathcal{D}^{(i)}_{0}[\hat{\rho}]

is a generator of a
GKSL
equation for the Lindblad operator

\hat{L}_{i}=\sqrt{2{\bf K}_{ii}}\,\hat{x}_{i}
.

Considering the Wigner function
W(x,t)
for the density operator
\hat{\rho}(t)
,
the Wigner symbol of each term in Eq. (
9
)
may be determined using the recipe in Appendix
A
.
Note that the Wigner symbol of the operator
\hat{x}

is the column vector
x
in Eq. (
3
)
[
11
]

and
H(x)
is the symbol of the Hamiltonian operator
\hat{H}
.
Then the products of operators in Eq. (
9
) are
evaluated using the Groenewold formula as

\displaystyle\tfrac{d}{dt}\hat{\rho}(t)\rightarrow\partial_{t}W(x,t)\,,

\displaystyle\tfrac{\mathsf{i}}{\hbar}\ [\hat{\rho}(t),\hat{H}]\rightarrow-\{W(x,t),H(x)\}_{\text{PB}}+O(\hbar^{2})

\displaystyle\{\hat{\rho},[\hat{H},\hat{x}_{i}]\}\hat{x}_{j}\rightarrow-{2i\hbar}W(x,t)\dot{x}_{i}x_{j}+\hbar^{2}({\sf J}\partial_{x_{j}}W)\dot{x}_{i}

\displaystyle+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2}W(x,t)[{\sf J}{\bf H}(x){\sf J}^{\top}]_{ij}+O(\hbar^{2})\,,

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{i}\{\hat{\rho},[\hat{H},\hat{x}_{j}]\}\rightarrow-2i\hbar W(x,t)x_{i}\dot{x}_{j}-\hbar^{2}({\sf J}\partial_{x_{i}}W)\dot{x}_{j}

\displaystyle-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2}W(x,t)[{\sf J}{\bf H}(x){\sf J}^{\top}]_{ji}+O(\hbar^{2})\,,

\displaystyle\{\hat{\rho},\hat{x}_{i}\hat{x}_{j}\}-2\hat{x}_{i}\hat{\rho}\hat{x}_{j}\rightarrow\hbar({\sf J}\partial_{x})_{i}({\sf J}\partial_{x})_{j}W(x,t)\,,

where
\{W,H\}_{\text{PB}}:=\partial_{x}W\cdot{\sf J}\partial_{x}H

[
11
]

and

{\bf H}(x):=\partial_{xx}H(x)
is the Hessian of the symbol
H(x)
,
a classical Hamiltonian.
See the discussion Appendix
A
for details.
The first line in the above list represents an exact expression,
as it directly corresponds to the mapping of the density operator
to its corresponding Wigner function
W(x,t)
.
The last line is also an exact representation, but for a different reason:
in this case, terms of order
\hbar^{2}
and higher vanish
in the Groenewold product formula.
All other lines are semiclassical expansions,
through the Groenewold formula,
keeping terms of
O(\hbar)
.

In the end, by collecting all the symbols mentioned above and
using
{\bf K}
from Eq. (
10
),
the Wigner transformation of Eq. (
9
),
while discarding
O(\hbar^{2})
terms, becomes

\displaystyle\partial_{t}W(x,t)+\partial_{x}\!\cdot\!{\mathcal{J}}_{W}=0\,,

(11)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathcal{J}}_{W}:=({\sf I}_{2n}+{\bf C}{\sf J})[{\sf J}\partial_{x}H(x)]\,W-{\bf D}\,\partial_{x}W\,.

One can see that the above equation for the Wigner function matches
the FPK equation (
4
).
When all the temperatures of the baths are the same,
the FPK equation (
1
) describes thermal relaxation processes,
as shown in Ref.
[
7
]
.
Due to this equivalence, the same thermal relaxation
is observed in Eq. (
11
).
In other words, the classical limit of
the quantum master equation (
2
) describes
the same thermal relaxation process as
the FPK equation (
1
),
despite the issue that the Wigner function
does not satisfy the positive semidefinite property
of true probability densities.

In the discussion so far, we have assumed that the matrix
{\bf C}

in Eq. (
5
) does not depend on the bath temperatures.
However it is also possible to consider temperature
dependence as discussed in Sec.
VI.1
.
In the context of this section, the additional temperature dependences,
which come from Eq. (
25
),
affect only the higher order terms beyond
O(\hbar^{2})

and thus the above conclusion is not affected.

IV
CPTP Criterion

A general quadratic Hamiltonian operator corresponding
to Eq. (
6
) is given by

\hat{H}=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{x}-\xi)\cdot{\bf H}(\hat{x}-\xi)+\varphi\hat{1}\,.

(12)

See the related discussion in Appendix
B
.
With the quantum master equation (
2
) in mind for such Hamiltonian,
we will consider the
quadratic time-convolutionless (QTCL)
master equation,

\displaystyle\tfrac{d}{dt}\hat{\rho}(t)=\tfrac{\mathsf{i}}{\hbar}\ [\hat{\rho}(t),\hat{H}]+\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}]\,,

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}]=-\frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_{j,k=1}^{2n}{\bf\Xi}_{jk}^{\top}\hat{\rho}(\hat{x}_{j}-\eta_{j})(\hat{x}_{k}-\eta_{k})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_{j,k=1}^{2n}({\bf\Xi}+{\bf\Xi}^{\dagger})_{jk}(\hat{x}_{j}-\eta_{j})\hat{\rho}(\hat{x}_{k}-\eta_{k})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_{j,k=1}^{2n}{\bf\Xi}_{jk}^{\ast}(\hat{x}_{j}-\eta_{j})(\hat{x}_{k}-\eta_{k})\hat{\rho}\,,

where
{\bf\Xi}_{jk}
are the elements of a
2n\times 2n

complex matrix
\bf\Xi
and
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
,
which can be time-dependent due to the time dependences
of the parameters in the Hamiltonian (
12
).
See Appendix
C
for details.
Our quantum master equation (
2
)
is reproduced by choosing this matrix and column vector appropriately.
For the moment, however, we will continue the discussion without their specifications.

Performing the decomposition

{\bf\Xi}=\tfrac{1}{2}({\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}+{\bf\Xi}_{\text{A}})\,,

(14)

where

\displaystyle{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}:={\bf\Xi}+{\bf\Xi}^{{\dagger}}\,,

\displaystyle{\bf\Xi}_{\text{A}}:={\bf\Xi}-{\bf\Xi}^{{\dagger}}\,,

the
QTCL
equation is written as

\displaystyle\tfrac{d}{dt}\hat{\rho}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\mathsf{i}}{\hbar}[\hat{\rho}\,,\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}]-\tfrac{1}{2\hbar}\!\left\{\hat{\rho}\,,(\hat{x}-\eta)\cdot{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}^{\ast}(\hat{x}-\eta)\right\}

(15)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{\hbar}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}({\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}})_{ij}(\hat{x}_{i}-\eta_{i})\hat{\rho}(\hat{x}_{j}-\eta_{j})\,,

where

\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}:=\hat{H}-\tfrac{\mathsf{i}}{2}(\hat{x}-\eta)\cdot{\bf\Xi}_{\text{A}}^{\ast}(\hat{x}-\eta)\,.

(16)

Note that
\mathsf{i}\,{\bf\Xi}_{\text{A}}^{\ast}
is Hermitian and that
such shift of the system Hamiltonian,
induced by the interaction with the environment,
is commonly observed in various different approaches
[
6
,
12
]
.

Due to the Hermiticity of
{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}
,
one can introduce the eigenvectors
{v}_{\mu}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n}
as

{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}{v}_{\mu}=a_{\mu}{v}_{\mu}\,,\,\,\,\mu=1,...,2n\,,

where
a_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}
are the respective eigenvalues
and the matrix
{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}
can be represented by

{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{2n}a_{\mu}{v}_{\mu}{v}_{\mu}^{\dagger}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{2n}\lambda_{\mu}g_{\mu\nu}\lambda_{\nu}^{\dagger}\,,

(17)

where
g_{\mu\nu}:=\text{sgn}(a_{\mu})\delta_{\mu\nu}
.
In short, the master equation in Eq. (
15
)
takes the form of a general time-convolutionless
(GTCL)
equation
[
13
]
:

\displaystyle\tfrac{d}{dt}\hat{\rho}=\tfrac{\mathsf{i}}{\hbar}[\hat{\rho},\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}]-\!\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^{2n}\frac{g_{\mu\nu}}{2\hbar}\left(\{\hat{\rho},\hat{L}_{\mu}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{\nu}\}-2\hat{L}_{\mu}\hat{\rho}\hat{L}_{\nu}^{\dagger}\right)\,,

(18)

where

\hat{L}_{\mu}=\lambda_{\mu}\cdot(\hat{x}-\eta)

are the Lindblad operators representing the action of the
environment in the system evolution.

Now we are in a position to examine
the criterion on the parameters in the
QTCL
equation (
15
)
[or equivalently, Eq. (
18
)] to ensure that
the resulting time evolution is a
CPTP
map.
As well-known
[
13
,
14
]
,
a
GKSL
equation is given by a
GTCL
equation (
18
)
for
g_{\mu\nu}=\delta_{\mu\nu}
.
To satisfy this,
we should require that
a_{\mu}\geq 0,\forall\mu
in Eq. (
17
).
Consequently, the time evolution of the
QTCL
equation
is a
CPTP
map if and only if

{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}\geq 0\,,

(19)

which means that its eigenvalues are non-negative.

For a given evolution of
\hat{\rho}
,
the definitions of
\hat{H}
and
{\cal D}[\hat{\rho}]
in Eq. (
13
),
or equivalently the definitions of
\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}

and
\hat{L}_{\mu}
in Eq. (
18
),
are known to be non-unique
[
15
]
.
Consequently, the definition of
{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}
may be ambiguous.
However, as we show in Appendix
D
,
the matrix
{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}

remains invariant despite the aforementioned nonuniqueness,
thereby ensuring the validity of the derived condition for a general
QTCL
equation.

When the
GTCL
equation is given by our quantum master equation (
2
)
with the Hamiltonian (
12
),
we find
\eta=\xi
and the matrix
{\bf\Xi}
defined by

\displaystyle{\bf\Xi}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2n}{\bf K}_{ik}({\sf S}_{\!\beta_{k}})_{kj}={\bf K}_{ii}({\sf S}_{\!\beta_{i}})_{ij}\,,\,\,\,(\beta_{i+n}=\beta_{n})\,,

(20)

where
\bf K
is defined in Eq. (
10
) and

\displaystyle{\sf S}_{\!\beta_{k}}

\displaystyle:=\exp[{\mathsf{i}\hbar\beta_{k}}{\sf J}{\bf H}/2]\,;

see Appendix
B
.
Note that the different rows of
{\bf\Xi}
depend on different values
of temperature,
e.g.
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\bf\Xi}_{12}&={\bf K}_{11}({\sf S}_{\!\beta_{1}})_{12}=\frac{\gamma_{p_{1}}}{\hbar\beta_{1}}({\sf S}_{\!\beta_{1}})_{12}\,,\\
{\bf\Xi}_{21}&={\bf K}_{22}({\sf S}_{\!\beta_{2}})_{12}=\frac{\gamma_{p_{2}}}{\hbar\beta_{2}}({\sf S}_{\!\beta_{2}})_{21}\,,\end{split}

and

\displaystyle{\bf\Xi}^{\dagger}_{ij}={\bf\Xi}^{\ast}_{ji}

\displaystyle={\bf K}_{jj}({\sf S}_{\!\beta_{j}}^{\ast})_{ji}={\bf K}_{jj}({\exp}[\mathsf{i}\hbar\beta_{j}{{\sf J}{\bf H}}/2])_{ji}

\displaystyle={\bf K}_{jj}({\exp}[-\mathsf{i}\hbar\beta_{j}{{\bf H}{\sf J}}/2])_{ij}\,.

The criterion (
19
) imposes a constraint on
the system-bath interaction parameters, as detailed in Eq. (
10
).
We can assert that the bath-momentum couplings
\gamma_{p_{i}}

are intrinsically linked to the position-bath couplings
\gamma_{q_{i}}
,
as anticipated in Sec.
II
for the harmonic oscillator.
In the end,
the criterion determines the permissible values for a genuine physical evolution
represented by a
CPTP
map.

To study the meaning of above criterion in the corresponding classical theory,
let us remember the discussion in Sec.
III
.
As discussed in Ref.
[
7
]
, the
FPK
equation permits any value of

\gamma_{q_{i}}
and
\gamma_{p_{i}}
, differently from its quantum counterpart.
Therefore, the criterion (
19
) will be automatically satisfied
in the classical limit of the matrix
{\bf\Xi}
.
Expanding the matrix
{\mathsf{S}}_{\!\beta_{k}}
in Eq. (
20
),

{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}
is given by

{\bf\Xi}_{\rm H}={\bf K}>0\,,

where we used

{\bf\Xi}\approx{\bf K}-\tfrac{\mathsf{i}}{2}{\sf J}{\bf C}{\bf H}\,.

It is easy to see that the criterion (
19
)
is satisfied in the classical limit.
Again, we can employ the analogy with the uncertainty principle
that does not provide constraints to classical variables.

Our master equation (
2
)
drives the system towards thermal equilibrium
when
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=...=\beta_{n}=\beta
,
at least in its classical limit.
However, an additional condition is required to ensure complete positivity.
In standard approaches, the situation is inverted:
one assumes a
CPTP
equation and imposes additional constrains,
called quantum detailed balance conditions
[
16
]
,
to describe a thermal relaxation process.
In Appendix
E
, as a consequence of detailed balance,
we show that a
CPTP
evolution driven by our quantum master equation
for the quadratic Hamiltonian (
12
)
will attain thermal equilibrium if this Hamiltonian has
a positive definite Hessian,
{\bf H}>0
,
which includes the cases of harmonic oscillators
and linearly coupled oscillators called the network model.

V
Bloch-Redfield Equation

The Bloch-Redfield equation is derived from a microscopic system-bath Hamiltonian
using Born-Markov approximations
[
12
]
,
assuming that the bath degrees of freedom are thermally equilibrated.
Further approximations often lead to a
CPTP
equation.
For a standard discussion and various improvements, see Refs.

[
12
,
18
,
17
,
23
,
24
,
19
,
22
,
21
,
20
]
.

In contrast,
the derivation of our quantum master equation (
2
)
is more phenomenological, since it is obtained by applying a canonical quantization
to the classical model of a generalized Brownian motion (
1
),
which results from the coarse-graining of classical degrees of freedom.

Considering Hermitian system operators
\hat{A}_{i}
(
i=1,2,\cdots
),
the Bloch-Redfield equation is defined by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}(t)=\frac{\mathsf{i}}{\hbar}[\hat{\rho}(t),\hat{H}]+{\cal R}[\hat{\rho}]\,,

(21)

\displaystyle\cal{\displaystyle R}[\hat{\rho}]=-\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\int^{\infty}_{0}\!\!d\tau\,{\bf\Gamma}_{ij}(\tau)[\hat{A}_{i},\tilde{A}_{j}(t)\hat{\rho}]+\text{h.c.}\,,

where the functions

{\bf\Gamma}_{ij}(s)
are characterized by the temporal correlation functions
of the bath degrees of freedom and

\tilde{A}_{j}(t):={\rm e}^{\frac{\mathsf{i}}{\hbar}\hat{H}t}\hat{A}_{j}{\rm e}^{-\frac{\mathsf{i}}{\hbar}\hat{H}t}
.
In this form,
the Markov approximation has already been employed, considering that
the time scale of the system evolution is much slower than the one of
the bath degrees of freedom.

The dissipative terms in Eq. (
2
) can be rewritten as

{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{Q}}^{(i)}[\hat{\rho}]=-\frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_{j=1}^{2n}{\bf K}_{ij}[\hat{x}_{i},{\rm e}^{-\frac{\beta_{j}}{2}\hat{H}}\hat{x}_{j}{\rm e}^{\frac{\beta_{j}}{2}\hat{H}}\hat{\rho}]+\text{h.c.}\,.

In Appendix
C
,
it is shown that the quantum master equation (
2
) and
the Bloch-Redfield equation have a very similar structure
if the system operators in the Bloch-Redfield equation are chosen to be
linear operators in
\hat{x}
,
i.e.
,

\hat{A}_{i}=a_{i}\hat{x}_{i}
, for a real vector
a\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.
However, it is important to note that

{\cal D}^{(i)}_{\text{Q}}[\hat{\rho}]

does not exhibit any oscillatory behavior induced by
\hat{H}
.
In the standard approach
[
12
]
,
a
CPTP
evolution is achieved from the Bloch-Redfield equation by applying
the rotating wave approximation, which eliminates non-secular terms associated
with oscillations caused by
{\rm e}^{\pm\frac{\mathsf{i}}{\hbar}\hat{H}t}
.

Let us apply our
CPTP
criterion (
19
)
to the Bloch-Redfield equation (
21
).
For a quadratic Hamiltonian and linear operators
\hat{A}_{i}
,
it is possible to write
{\cal R}[\hat{\rho}]
above as
{\cal D}[\hat{\rho}]

in
QTCL
(
13
) for the matrix
{\bf\Xi}
with elements

{\bf\Xi}_{ij}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_{k=1}^{2n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!d\tau\,a_{i}{\bf\Gamma}_{ik}^{\ast}(\tau)a_{k}\,({\sf S}_{t})_{kj}\,,

where
\tilde{A}_{j}(t)
and
{\sf S}_{t}
are represented using
Eqs. (
32
) and (
31
).
Consequently, to satisfy the criterion (
19
),
the column vector
a
should be chosen appropriately for a given matrix
{\bf\Gamma}
.

Although no one has yet succeeded in finding such parameters,
there have been several attempts to minimize the violation of the
CPTP
map
by selecting appropriate parameters.
The generator
{\cal D}[\hat{\rho}]
in Eq. (
13
)
can be expanded using variables differently from
(\hat{x}_{i}-\eta_{i})
.
In Ref.
[
17
]
,
the expansion in terms of
\hat{A}_{i}+\alpha_{i}(t)\tilde{A}_{i}

is considered and
a non-
CPTP
time-convolutionless master equation is obtained.
A
CPTP
equation is derived from this by
truncating the terms that violate positivity using
the parameters
\alpha_{i}(t)
which minimize the contributions from
these non-positivity terms.
A similar approach is considered in Ref.
[
18
]
,
which uses a different expansion and discusses the relation
to the result in Ref.
[
17
]
.
Other related approaches and decompositions are performed in
Refs.
[
20
,
23
,
24
,
19
,
22
,
21
]
.

VI
Applications to
n=1

Let us apply our criterion to a quadratic Hamiltonian of a system with
one-degree of freedom (
n=1
). We then find

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathsf{S}}_{\beta}&=\cosh(\Theta){\sf I}_{2}-\mathsf{i}\frac{\sinh\Theta}{\Theta}{\sf J}{\bf H}\,,\\
{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}&=2\cosh(\Theta)\,{\bf K}-\mathsf{i}(\gamma_{q}H_{11}+\gamma_{p}H_{22})\frac{\sinh\Theta}{2\Theta}{\sf J}\,,\\
{\bf\Xi}_{\text{A}}&=\mathsf{i}\,\frac{\sinh\Theta}{2\Theta}\begin{pmatrix}-2\gamma_{p}H_{12}&\gamma_{q}H_{11}-\gamma_{p}H_{22}\\
\gamma_{q}H_{11}-\gamma_{p}H_{22}&2\gamma_{q}H_{12}\end{pmatrix}\,,\end{split}

where
H_{ij}
denote the elements of the Hessian
\bf H
and

\Theta=\frac{\hbar\beta}{2}\sqrt{\det{\bf H}}\,.

(22)

The indeces
i=1
in
\beta
,
\gamma_{p}
and
\gamma_{q}
are omitted for simplicity.
In this calculation, we used
({\sf J}{\bf H})^{2}=-(\det{\bf H}){\sf I}_{2}

for a
2\times 2
matrix
\bf H
.

VI.1
Elliptic case:
\det{\bf H}>0

We first consider the case of
\det{\bf H}>0
.
The eigenvalues of
{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}
are given by

\displaystyle\psi_{\pm}({\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}})=\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}\pm\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{({\rm Tr}{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}})^{2}-4\det{{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}}}\,,

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\rm Tr}{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}&=\frac{2\cosh\Theta}{\hbar\beta}\,{\rm Tr}{\bf C}\geq 0\,,\\
\det{{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}}&=\frac{4\cosh^{2}\!\Theta}{\hbar^{2}\beta^{2}}\det{\bf C}-\left[\frac{\sinh\Theta}{2\Theta}\,{\rm Tr}({\bf C}{\bf H})\right]^{2}\,,\end{split}

where
{\bf C}
is defined by Eq. (
5
).

Note that
{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}
is Hermitian and thus its eigenvalues are real,

({\rm Tr}{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}})^{2}\geq 4\det{{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}}
.
Therefore, to satisfy our criterion (
19
), we require

\det{{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}}\geq 0\Longleftrightarrow\frac{1}{4}\frac{({\rm Tr}{{\bf CH}})^{2}}{\det{{\bf CH}}}\tanh^{2}\Theta\leq 1\,,

which for an arbitrary temperature becomes

\frac{1}{4}\frac{({\rm Tr}{{\bf CH}})^{2}}{\det{{\bf CH}}}\leq 1\,,

(23)

since
\tanh\Theta\leq 1
.

For example, when the Hamiltonian satisfies
H_{12}=0
,
noting that
H_{21}=H_{12}
,
Eq. (
23
) leads to

\frac{\gamma_{q}H_{11}}{\gamma_{p}H_{22}}+\frac{\gamma_{p}H_{22}}{\gamma_{q}H_{11}}\leq 2\,.

Therefore,
the quantum master equation (
2
) becomes a
CPTP
evolution for any temperature of heat bath
when
\gamma_{q}H_{11}=\gamma_{p}H_{22}
.
For a harmonic oscillator,
where
H_{11}=m\omega^{2}
and
H_{22}=1/m
,
this condition reproduces the result in Ref.
[
7
]
,

\gamma_{p}=(m\omega)^{2}\gamma_{q}\,.

(24)

Even after employing the condition (
24
),
there still remains some freedom for the choice of the parameters
which can be utilized to introduce further temperature dependence.
For example, let us assume

\gamma_{p}=\frac{m\beta\hbar\omega}{2\sinh(\beta\hbar\omega/2)}\tilde{\gamma}\,,

(25)

where
\tilde{\gamma}
is a non-negative constant.
Using Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
),
the quantum master equation (
2
)
for a harmonic oscillator becomes

\displaystyle\tfrac{d}{dt}\hat{\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathsf{i}}{\hbar}[\hat{\rho},\hat{H}]-\mathsf{i}\frac{\tilde{\gamma}}{4\hbar}\left([\hat{q},\{\hat{p},\hat{\rho}\}]-[\hat{p},\{\hat{q},\hat{\rho}\}]\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{\tilde{\gamma}}{4\hbar}\coth(\tfrac{1}{2}\beta\hbar\omega)\left(m\omega[\hat{q},[\hat{q},\hat{\rho}]]+\frac{1}{m\omega}[\hat{p},[\hat{p},\hat{\rho}]]\right)\,,

(26)

which is essentially the same as the master equation for a harmonic oscillator
interacting with thermal radiation through
the Born-Markov approximations
[
12
,
25
]
.
Indeed, this is rewritten as Eq. (
18
)
by choosing
g_{\mu\nu}=\delta_{\mu\nu}
,
\eta=0
, and

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{L}_{1}&=\sqrt{\hbar\tilde{\gamma}(\bar{n}+1)}\,\hat{a}\,,\\
\hat{L}_{2}&=\sqrt{\hbar\tilde{\gamma}\bar{n}}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}\end{split}

(27)

with

\bar{n}=[\exp(\hbar\beta\omega)-1]^{-1}
and

\hat{a}=\sqrt{m\omega/(2\hbar)}\hat{q}+\mathsf{i}\hat{p}/\sqrt{2\hbar m\omega}
.
Noteworthy,

\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\gamma_{p}=m\tilde{\gamma}

and thus the classical limit of the master equation (
26
) works
as well as the discussion in Sec.
IV
.

VI.2
Hyperbolic and parabolic cases:
\det{\bf H}\leq 0

For the hyperbolic case,
\det{\bf H}<0
,
we replace
\sqrt{\det{\bf H}}
by
\mathsf{i}\sqrt{|\det{\bf H}|}

in Eq. (
22
),
such that
\Theta=\frac{\hbar\beta}{2}\sqrt{|\det{\bf H}|}
.
Differently from the elliptic case, note that

{\rm Tr}{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}=2\cos(\Theta){\rm Tr}{\bf C}/(\hbar\beta)

can take negative values,
which would imply
{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}\ngeq 0
.
Thus, to satisfy the criterion (
19
), we should have

\displaystyle 0\leq\Theta\leq\tfrac{\pi}{2}\,\,\,\text{or}\,\,\,\tfrac{\pi}{2}+(2N+1)\pi\leq\Theta\leq\tfrac{\pi}{2}+2(N+1)\pi

for
N=0,1,2...
, and

\displaystyle\left|\frac{{\rm Tr}({\bf CH})}{2\sqrt{\det({\bf CH})}}\tan\Theta\right|\leq 1\,.

As an example,
scattering through a parabolic barrier is
described by the Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=\tfrac{1}{2}H_{22}p^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}|H_{11}|q^{2}

with
H_{11}>0
,
H_{22}<0
, and
H_{12}=0
.
Although the
CPTP
criterion is applicable,
such a (quantum or classical) system will not reach thermal equilibrium
due to the Hamiltonian not being bounded from below,
as discussed in Sec.
II
.

For the parabolic case,
\det{{\bf H}}=0
, we find that

{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}=2{\bf K}-\tfrac{\mathsf{i}}{2}{\rm Tr}({\bf CH}){\sf J}
,
and the
CPTP
condition becomes

\frac{1}{4}\frac{[\hbar\beta\,{\rm Tr}{({\bf CH}})]^{2}}{\det{{\bf C}}}\leq 1\,;

the simplest example of such a system is the free particle Hamiltonian.

VI.3
Caldeira-Leggett Model

Our criterion is still applicable to a
GTCL
equation
which is not cast into the form of Eq. (
2
).
Let us consider the Caldeira-Leggett equation in a high-temperature environment
[
26
]
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{\rho},\hat{H}_{\text{o}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma_{\text{o}}\{\hat{q},\hat{p}\}]-\frac{\zeta}{\hbar^{2}\beta}[\hat{q},[\hat{q},\hat{\rho}]]

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}\gamma_{\text{o}}[\hat{q},\hat{\rho}\hat{p}]+\frac{i}{\hbar}\gamma_{\text{o}}[\hat{p},\hat{\rho}\hat{q}]\,,

where
\hat{H}_{\text{o}}
is a “renormalized” version of the original
system Hamiltonian,

\gamma_{\text{o}}
is a relaxation constant and
\zeta
is a damping coefficient.

This equation is not expressed in the form of
our quantum master equation (
2
),
but can be mapped to the form of the
GTCL
for
the following identifications:

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}&=\frac{2\zeta}{\hbar\beta}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right)-\mathsf{i}{\gamma_{\text{o}}}{\mathsf{J}}\,,\\
{\bf\Xi}_{\text{A}}&=-\mathsf{i}\gamma_{\text{o}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right)\,;\end{split}

note that
\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}=\hat{H}_{\text{o}}+\frac{\gamma_{\text{o}}}{2}\{\hat{q},\hat{p}\}
.
One can see that one of the eigenvalues of
{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}

takes a negative value,

\psi_{\pm}({\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}})=\frac{\zeta}{\hbar\beta}\pm\sqrt{\frac{\zeta^{2}}{\hbar^{2}\beta^{2}}+\gamma_{\text{o}}^{2}}\gtrless 0\,,

showing that the Caldeira-Leggett equation is not a
GKSL

equation from Eq. (
19
),
as expected
[
12
]
.

Now we study the conditions for our master equation (
2
)
to be reduced to this Caldeira-Leggett equation.
To this end, we need to find matrices
\bf K
and
{\mathsf{S}}_{\!\beta}

satisfying Eq. (
20
).
From Eq. (
14
), one obtains the
matrix
\bf\Xi
of the Caldeira-Leggett model using the above matrices

{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}
and
{\bf\Xi}_{\text{A}}
.
Thus the quantities satisfying the relation

{\bf\Xi}={\bf K}{\mathsf{S}}_{\!\beta}
are defined
if and only if

\displaystyle\begin{split}\gamma_{p}&=\zeta\,{\rm sech}\Theta\,,\\
\gamma_{\text{o}}&=\zeta H_{22}\Theta^{-1}\tanh\Theta\,,\end{split}

together with
H_{12}=0
and
\gamma_{q}=0
.
Since
\gamma_{q}=0
, the Caldeira-Leggett equation is regarded
as a special case of our quantum master equation (
2
),
where the interactions with the heat bath do not modify the particle momenta
— this is the common perspective in standard Brownian motion
[
27
]
.
The nullity of
H_{12}
means that the above identification works,
for instance, for the harmonic oscillator and for the free-particle cases,
where there are no contributions proportional to
\hat{q}\hat{p}
or
\hat{p}\hat{q}
.
As discussed in Sec.
VI.1
,
\gamma_{p}
is temperature-dependent.

VI.4
Ordinary Kramers Equation

The ordinary Kramers equation is obtained from the generalized Kramers
equation in Eq. (
1
) by setting
\gamma_{q_{i}}=0,\forall i
.
When Hamiltonians is bounded from below,
its evolution by Eq. (
1
) drives the system towards
thermal equilibrium
[
27
]
.
To show the quantization problem in the ordinary Kramers equation,
it is sufficient to consider
n=1
.

Setting
\gamma_{q}=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\gamma_{p}}{\hbar\beta}\cosh\Theta\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}-\mathsf{i}\gamma_{p}H_{22}\frac{\sinh\Theta}{2\Theta}{\sf J}\,,

\displaystyle{\bf\Xi}_{\text{A}}

\displaystyle=-\mathsf{i}\gamma_{p}\frac{\sinh\Theta}{2\Theta}\begin{pmatrix}2H_{12}&H_{22}\\
H_{22}&0\end{pmatrix},

from which we can calculate

\det({\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}})=-[2H_{22}\gamma_{p}{\Theta}^{-1}\sinh(\Theta)]^{2}\,.

Consequently, the criterion (
19
) is fulfilled only
for a Hamiltonian with
H_{22}=0
.
This corresponds to the hyperbolic case where
\det{\bf H}=-H_{12}^{2}

and
\Theta=\tfrac{\hbar\beta}{2}\sqrt{|\det{\bf H}|}
.
Since the hyperbolic Hamiltonian is unbounded,
the system does not relax to equilibrium.

VII
Application to network model for
n=2

Let us now consider two oscillators with equal masses and frequencies,
which interact linearly with each other. Each oscillator,
indexed by
i
, interacts with a heat bath at temperature
\beta_{i}
.
This model, commonly referred to as the network model, has been extensively
used to study heat conduction in stochastic and quantum thermodynamics

[
1
,
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
28
]
.
To simplify the notation, we redefine the position and momentum variables
in such a way that both of them have the same dimension,
[q_{i}]=[p_{j}]
.
Then the Hamiltonian operator is defined by

\hat{H}=\tfrac{\omega}{2}(\hat{p}_{1}^{2}+\hat{p}_{2}^{2}+\hat{q}_{1}^{2}+\hat{q}_{2}^{2})+\tfrac{\kappa}{2}(\hat{q}_{1}-\hat{q}_{2})^{2}\,,

where
\kappa>0
characterizes the interaction between the oscillators
and the dimensions of the parameters are such that

[\omega]=[\kappa]=[(\gamma_{p_{i}})^{-1}]=[(\gamma_{q_{j}})^{-1}]
.

The matrix
{\bf\Xi}
in (
20
) is calculated as

\displaystyle{\bf\Xi}=\!\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\gamma_{p_{1}}}{\hbar\beta_{1}}({\sf S}_{\beta_{1}})_{11}&\frac{\gamma_{p_{1}}}{\hbar\beta_{1}}({\sf S}_{\beta_{1}})_{12}&\frac{\gamma_{p_{1}}}{\hbar\beta_{1}}({\sf S}_{\beta_{1}})_{13}&\frac{\gamma_{p_{1}}}{\hbar\beta_{1}}({\sf S}_{\beta_{1}})_{14}\\
\frac{\gamma_{p_{2}}}{\hbar\beta_{2}}({\sf S}_{\beta_{2}})_{21}&\frac{\gamma_{p_{2}}}{\hbar\beta_{2}}({\sf S}_{\beta_{2}})_{22}&\frac{\gamma_{p_{2}}}{\hbar\beta_{2}}({\sf S}_{\beta_{2}})_{23}&\frac{\gamma_{p_{2}}}{\hbar\beta_{2}}({\sf S}_{\beta_{2}})_{24}\\
\frac{\gamma_{q_{1}}}{\hbar\beta_{1}}({\sf S}_{\beta_{1}})_{31}&\frac{\gamma_{q_{1}}}{\hbar\beta_{1}}({\sf S}_{\beta_{1}})_{32}&\frac{\gamma_{q_{1}}}{\hbar\beta_{1}}({\sf S}_{\beta_{1}})_{33}&\frac{\gamma_{q_{1}}}{\hbar\beta_{1}}({\sf S}_{\beta_{1}})_{34}\\
\frac{\gamma_{q_{2}}}{\hbar\beta_{2}}({\sf S}_{\beta_{2}})_{41}&\frac{\gamma_{q_{2}}}{\hbar\beta_{2}}({\sf S}_{\beta_{2}})_{42}&\frac{\gamma_{q_{2}}}{\hbar\beta_{2}}({\sf S}_{\beta_{2}})_{43}&\frac{\gamma_{q_{2}}}{\hbar\beta_{2}}({\sf S}_{\beta_{2}})_{44}\end{pmatrix},

where

\displaystyle({\sf S}_{\!\beta})_{ii}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\cosh(\tfrac{\hbar\beta\omega}{2})+\tfrac{1}{2}\cosh(\tfrac{\hbar\beta\vartheta}{2})\,,

\displaystyle({\sf S}_{\!\beta})_{12}

\displaystyle=({\sf S}_{\!\beta})_{21}=\tfrac{1}{2}\cosh(\tfrac{\hbar\beta\omega}{2})-\tfrac{1}{2}\cosh(\tfrac{\hbar\beta\vartheta}{2})\,,

\displaystyle({\sf S}_{\!\beta})_{13}

\displaystyle=({\sf S}_{\!\beta})_{24}=-\tfrac{\mathsf{i}}{2}\sinh(\tfrac{\hbar\beta\omega}{2})-\tfrac{\mathsf{i}\omega}{2\vartheta}\sinh(\tfrac{\hbar\beta\vartheta}{2})\,,

\displaystyle({\sf S}_{\!\beta})_{14}

\displaystyle=({\sf S}_{\!\beta})_{23}=-\tfrac{\mathsf{i}}{2}\sinh(\tfrac{\hbar\beta\omega}{2})+\tfrac{\mathsf{i}\omega}{2\vartheta}\sinh(\tfrac{\hbar\beta\vartheta}{2})\,,

\displaystyle({\sf S}_{\!\beta})_{34}

\displaystyle=({\sf S}_{\!\beta})_{43}=\tfrac{1}{2}\cosh(\tfrac{\hbar\beta\omega}{2})-\tfrac{1}{2}\cosh(\tfrac{\hbar\beta\vartheta}{2})\,,

\displaystyle({\sf S}_{\!\beta})_{31}

\displaystyle=({\sf S}_{\!\beta})_{42}=\tfrac{\mathsf{i}}{2}\sinh(\tfrac{\hbar\beta\omega}{2})+\tfrac{\mathsf{i}\vartheta}{2\omega}\sinh(\tfrac{\hbar\beta\vartheta}{2})\,,

\displaystyle({\sf S}_{\!\beta})_{32}

\displaystyle=({\sf S}_{\!\beta})_{41}=\tfrac{\mathsf{i}}{2}\sinh(\tfrac{\hbar\beta\omega}{2})-\tfrac{\mathsf{i}\vartheta}{2\omega}\sinh(\tfrac{\hbar\beta\vartheta}{2})\,,

and
\vartheta:=\sqrt{\omega(\omega+2\kappa)}
.

The eigenvalues of
{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}
are numerically determined
and their behaviors are shown in Fig.
1
.
In the top panels,
all eigenvalues in the shaded regions of the
\beta_{1}\times\beta_{2}
plane
are non-negative, showing that the time evolution of
the quantum master equation (
2
) is a
CPTP
map
for the corresponding system parameters,
according to the criterion (
19
).
In the bottom panels, all four eigenvalues of
{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}

are shown as functions of
\beta_{1}
for
\beta_{2}=\beta_{1}
.
These results suggest that it is possible to find appropriate parameters
for which the quantum master equation (
2
) conforms to
a
CPTP
map for any temperature difference.

Before concluding the discussion in this section,
let us examine the classical limit of the matrix
{\bf\Xi}
,

\displaystyle{\bf\Xi}\approx\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\gamma_{p_{1}}}{\hbar\beta_{1}}&0&-\tfrac{\mathsf{i}}{2}\gamma_{p_{1}}\omega&0\\
0&\frac{\gamma_{p_{2}}}{\hbar\beta_{2}}&0&-\tfrac{\mathsf{i}}{2}\gamma_{p_{2}}\omega\\
\tfrac{\mathsf{i}}{2}\gamma_{q_{1}}(\kappa+\omega)&-\tfrac{\mathsf{i}}{2}\gamma_{q_{1}}\kappa&\frac{\gamma_{q_{1}}}{\hbar\beta_{1}}&0\\
-\tfrac{\mathsf{i}}{2}\gamma_{q_{2}}\kappa&\tfrac{\mathsf{i}}{2}\gamma_{q_{2}}(\kappa+\omega)&0&\frac{\gamma_{q_{2}}}{\hbar\beta_{2}}\end{pmatrix}\,.

It is then easy to see that the criterion is satisfied,

{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}={\bf K}>0
,
which is the same behavior in the classical
limit found in Sec.
VI
.

VIII
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, assuming that quantum master equations are derived through the canonical
quantization of generalized Brownian motions
[
7
]
,
we established a criterion to ensure the complete positivity of the quantum master
equation (
2
).
The scope of this paper is confined to general quadratic Hamiltonians,
where the quantum master equation retains a quadratic form.
We demonstrated that our quantum master equation can
be transformed into the general form defined by Eq. (
13
) by appropriately
selecting the matrix
{\bf\Xi}
.
We then explored the criterion for this general form to be represented by
a
CPTP
map, as given by Eq. (
19
).

The criterion (
19
)
is developed from the
GKSL
“theorem”
[
13
,
14
]
,
and hence it is state-independent,
relying solely on the system Hamiltonian and interaction parameters.
When the criterion is applied to the entire set of
one-particle quadratic Hamiltonians,
we determine the parameters satisfying the requirements for a

CPTP
evolution.
Alternatively, considering a different perspective,
bosonic Gaussian channels represent
CPTP
operations
defined over the set of bosonic Gaussian states.
A mapping between two Gaussian states will be a Gaussian channel
if it satisfies the Heisenberg uncertainty principle
[
33
]
,
which corresponds to, in the context of the present paper, a
CPTP
criterion.
In fact, the evolution governed by the
QTCL
equation (
13
),
when constrained by the
CPTP
criterion (
19
),
generates a one-parameter Gaussian channel
[
34
]
.
Consequently, the criterion (
19
)
is equivalent to the Heisenberg uncertainty principle as mentioned.
However, in our case, it applies without requiring knowledge
about the state of the environment.

While this outcome aligns with that derived in Ref.
[
7
]

in the application to non-interacting harmonic oscillators,
the methodology presented here is systematic and
significantly simpler than the approach employed in the referenced work.
Currently, our focus lies predominantly on the application to the network model,
where the system is represented as an ensemble of harmonic oscillators
interacting linearly with each other.
The non-unitary part
{\cal D}^{(i)}_{\text{Q}}[\hat{\rho}]
in Eq. (
2
)
for such a system is influenced by the interaction terms in the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
.
This influence results in our quantum master equation for
the network being a global master equation.
While the Lindblad operators in standard global equation of a network model
are typically assumed to be proportional to
the ladder operators that diagonalize
\hat{H}
,
the non-unitary part of Eq. (
2
) does not exhibit such
a simple structure.
Consequently, numerical calculations are required to
determine the parameters that satisfy the CPTP criterion,
differently from the standard global equation in the network model.
The results are summarized in Fig.
1
,
where the region of parameters that meet the CPTP map requirement is highlighted.
Exactly speaking, however, the existence of a parameter set to ensure
that our evolution forms a
CPTP
map for any combination
of two temperatures,
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
, has not yet been demonstrated.
This is left as a future task.

Recently, the debates have focused on the desired thermodynamical attributes
and properties of a general quantum master equation.
Various criteria and approaches have been discussed in
Refs.
[
35
,
36
,
37
]
.
In this context, new derivations and modifications
have been proposed
[
17
,
18
,
31
,
38
]
.
The advantage of our theory lies in its ability to derive equations that satisfy the
CPTP condition while remaining consistent with the results of stochastic
thermodynamics.

Our findings suggest that a unified framework for stochastic
and quantum thermodynamics can be formulated, even for interacting models.
For instance, given the system Hamiltonian defined by the network model,
our quantum master equation offers an alternative framework for
describing heat conduction. In studying this issue, the structure of
local and global quantum master equations
has been extensively discussed
[
29
,
30
,
31
]

to clarify their applicable domains, thermodynamic consistency,
complete positivity,
and so on
[
17
,
18
,
31
,
38
,
32
]
).
Our master equation is also global for a network of oscillators,
but unlike the standard global master equation, it reduces to the
corresponding generalized Brownian motion in the classical limit,
enabling the study of Fourier’s law of heat conduction with quantum effects.

In contrast to the microscopic derivation of, for instance,
a Bloch-Redfield equation,
it should be noted that our approach is phenomenological,
since our quantum master equation is derived by applying canonical quantization
to a generalized model of Brownian motion, which is already a coarse-grained theory.
Just as viscous coefficients in hydrodynamics are determined by external inputs,
such as the Green-Kubo-Nakano formula,
the coefficients
\gamma_{q_{i}}
and
\gamma_{p_{i}}
in Eq. (
2
),
and also in Eq. (
1
),
should be determined from microscopic theories.
It might be possible to apply the projection operator technique
to derive the Green-Kubo-Nakano formula for these parameters,
as demonstrated in Refs.
[
39
,
40
]
, for instance.

Certain limitations on the applicability of our quantum master equation
emerge when its derivation from a Brownian motion model becomes important.
Specifically, Eq. (
1
) is derived under the assumption of
Markov processes,
which presumes that the relaxation time scale
of the bath degrees of freedom is significantly faster and
well separated from the relaxation scale of the system.
Out of these conditions, which could arise in extremely low temperature regions,
the validity of our quantum master equation may be compromised.

Our theory is currently applied only to particle systems,
but it should be applicable to quantum fields, as well.
In quantum field theory, it is relatively easy to handle processes
where the number of particles changes
[
41
]
,
making such an extension important for studying the effects of
chemical potential in quantum thermodynamics. These studies are future challenges.

Appendix A
Wigner-Wyel Calculus

The action of the parity operator
\hat{R}_{0}=\hat{R}_{0}^{\dagger}

on the column vector (
7
),

\hat{R}_{0}\hat{x}\hat{R}_{0}=-\hat{x}
,
is a reflection over the origin
[
42
]
.
Using the Weyl operator in Eq. (
30
),
we define the displaced parity operator,
or the reflection operator
[
11
,
43
]
,
as
\hat{R}_{x}:=\hat{T}_{x}\hat{R}_{0}\hat{T}_{x}^{\dagger}
.
This operator is Hermitian (
\hat{R}_{x}=\hat{R}_{x}^{\dagger}
)
and unitary, making it an involutory operator (
\hat{R}_{x}^{2}=\hat{1}
), satisfying

\hat{R}_{x}\hat{x}\hat{R}_{x}^{\dagger}=-\hat{x}+2x\,.

An arbitrary operator
\hat{O}
acting on the Hilbert space of the
continuous-variable system of
n
degrees of freedom
can be uniquely expanded as a linear combination of
the reflection operators
[
11
]
:

\displaystyle\hat{O}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2n}x}{(\pi\hbar)^{n}}\,O(x)\,{\hat{R}}_{x}.

Mathematically speaking,
the set
\{\hat{R}_{x}\,|\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\}

is a basis in the vector space of the operators.
The coefficient
O(x)
of the expansion
is the Wigner symbol of the operator
\hat{O}
and given by

O(x)=2^{n}{\rm Tr}\,(\hat{O}{\hat{R}}_{x}),

(28)

by virtue of the following property
[
11
]
:

{\rm Tr}\,({\hat{R}}_{x}{\hat{R}}_{x^{\prime}})=(\pi\hbar)^{n}\delta(x^{\prime}-x)\,.

The trace in Eq. (
28
) on a coordinate basis
gives us the Wigner symbols
[
43
,
11
]
:

O(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\!\!\!\!\!\!d^{n}\!q^{\prime}\,\langle q+\textstyle\frac{1}{2}q^{\prime}|\,\hat{O}\,|q-\textstyle\frac{1}{2}q^{\prime}\rangle\,{\rm e}^{-\frac{\mathsf{i}}{\hbar}p\cdot q^{\prime}}\,.

The Wigner function
W(x)
of a system is
(a normalized version of) the Wigner symbol of the
corresponding density operator
\hat{\rho}

[
43
,
11
]
,

W(x):=\frac{1}{(\pi\hbar)^{n}}{\rm Tr}\left[\hat{\rho}{\hat{R}}_{x}\right].

The Wigner representation is a suitable platform to perform
semiclassical approximations derived in Ref.
[
11
]
by
composing and expanding the symbols (
28
).
The Wigner symbol for the product of operators,
say
\hat{O}_{12}=\hat{O}_{1}\hat{O}_{2}
,
is obtained by the Groenewold rule
[
11
]
:

O_{12}(x)={\rm e}^{\tfrac{\mathsf{i}\hbar}{2}\partial_{x^{\prime}}\cdot{\sf J}\partial_{x^{\prime\prime}}}\,O_{1}(x^{\prime})|_{x^{\prime}=x}O_{2}(x^{\prime\prime})|_{x^{\prime\prime}=x}\,.

Note that, for quadratic polynomials in
x
,
which is the case of a quadratic Hamiltonian in Eq. (
6
)
taken as the Wigner symbol of Eq. (
12
),
the expansion of the above exponential ends in second order
[
11
]
.

Appendix B
Quadratic Hamiltonians

Let us consider the evolution described by the unitary operator

\hat{U}_{t}=\hat{T}_{\xi}\,\hat{M}_{{\sf S}_{t}}\hat{T}_{\xi}^{\dagger}\,{\rm e}^{-\tfrac{\mathsf{i}t}{\hbar}\varphi}\,,

(29)

where the Weyl operator,
a displacement operator in quantum optics
[
25
]
,
and the metaplectic operator are, respectively,
defined by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{T}_{\xi}&:={\exp}\!\left[\frac{\mathsf{i}}{\hbar}\hat{x}\cdot{\sf J}\xi\right]\,,\,\,\,\\
\hat{M}_{{\sf S}_{t}}&:={\exp}\!\left[-\frac{\mathsf{i}t}{2\hbar}\hat{x}\cdot{\bf H}\hat{x}\right]\,.\end{split}

(30)

Their actions in the operator
\hat{x}
are

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{T}_{\xi}^{\dagger}\hat{x}\hat{T}_{\xi}&=\hat{x}+\xi\,,\\
\hat{M}_{{\sf S}_{t}}^{\dagger}\hat{x}\hat{M}_{{\sf S}_{t}}&={\sf S}_{t}\hat{x}\,,\end{split}

where
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and the symplectic matrix
{\sf S}_{t}
is

{\sf S}_{t}:=\exp[{\mathsf{J}{\bf H}t}]\,.

(31)

See
[
44
,
45
,
46
]
,
for instance.
From these covariance rules,
we obtain the Heisenberg evolution of the operator
\hat{x}
as

\hat{U}_{t}^{\dagger}\hat{x}\hat{U}_{t}={\sf S}_{t}(\hat{x}-\xi)+\xi\,.

(32)

Note that for the real column vector

\xi:=(\xi_{1},...\,,\xi_{2n})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
,
the sum
\hat{x}^{\prime}=\hat{x}+\xi

should be interpreted as a column vector of operators
with components
\hat{x}^{\prime}_{j}=\hat{x}_{j}+\xi_{j}\hat{1}_{j}
,
where
\hat{1}_{j}
is the identity operator acting
on the Hilbert space associated to the

(j\,{\rm mod}\,n)^{\text{th}}
degree of freedom.

The unitary operator in Eq. (
29
) is a member of
the inhomogeneous metaplectic group
[
44
,
45
]
,
a unitary subgroup generated by quantum quadratic Hamiltonians:

\hat{H}:=\mathsf{i}\hbar\hat{U}_{t}^{-1}\frac{d\hat{U}_{t}}{dt}=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{x}-\xi)\cdot{\bf H}(\hat{x}-\xi)+\varphi\hat{1}\,,

which is the symmetric quantization of the Hamiltonian
in Eq. (
6
) and is the quantum operator in Eq. (
12
).

Performing a Wick rotation
t\longmapsto-{\mathsf{i}}\hbar\beta_{k}/2
with

\beta_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}
in the unitary evolution (
29
),
one obtains
\hat{U}_{t}\,\,\longmapsto\,\,\exp[\tfrac{1}{2}\beta_{k}\hat{H}]
.
Consequently, from (
32
),

\hat{U}_{t}^{\dagger}\hat{x}\hat{U}_{t}\longmapsto\hat{y}:={\sf S}_{\!\beta_{k}}(\hat{x}-\xi)+\xi,

(33)

where, from Eq. (
31
),

{\sf S}_{t}\longmapsto{\sf S}_{\!\beta_{k}}:={\exp}[-\mathsf{i}\hbar\beta_{k}{{\sf J}{\bf H}}/2]\,,

(34)

which is a complex symplectic matrix belonging to the
Wick rotated symplectic group
[
46
]
,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\sf{\displaystyle S}_{\!\beta_{k}}^{\top}{\sf J}{\sf S}_{\!\beta_{k}}={\sf J}\,,

(35)

\displaystyle\sf{\displaystyle S}_{\!\beta_{k}}^{-1}={\sf S}_{-\beta_{k}}={\sf S}_{\!\beta_{k}}^{\ast}\,.

Note that
\hat{y}^{\dagger}={\sf S}_{\!\beta_{k}}^{\ast}(\hat{x}-\xi)+\xi
,
for
\hat{y}
in Eq. (
33
).

Appendix C
Derivation of the QTCL Equation

The most general linear form of
{\cal D}[\hat{\rho}]

for a quadratic dynamics is given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}[\hat{\rho}]=

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}({\bf L}_{ij}\hat{x}_{i}\hat{x}_{j}\hat{\rho}+{\bf M}_{ij}\hat{x}_{i}\hat{\rho}\hat{x}_{j}+{\bf N}_{ij}\hat{x}_{i}\hat{x}_{j}\hat{\rho})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}(\alpha_{i}\hat{x}_{i}\hat{\rho}+\beta_{i}\hat{\rho}\hat{x}_{i})+c\hat{\rho}\,,

where
{\bf L}
,
{\bf M}
, and
{\bf N}

are arbitrary
2n\times 2n
complex matrices,

\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}
are arbitrary complex vectors,
and
c\in{\mathbb{C}}
is an arbitrary complex number.

To ensure the trace preservation,

{\rm Tr}{\mathcal{D}}[\hat{\rho}]=0
, one finds

{\bf L}+{\bf M}^{\top}+{\bf N}=0\,,\,\,\,\alpha+\beta=0\,,\,\,\,c=0\,,

while Hermiticity,

{\mathcal{D}}^{\dagger}={\mathcal{D}}
,
requires

{\bf L}={\bf N}^{\dagger}\,,\,\,\,{\bf M}={\bf M}^{\dagger}\,,\,\,\,\alpha=\beta^{\ast}\,.

These constrains are solved by a complex

2n\times 2n
matrix
\bf\Xi
and a
real
vector
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.
Indeed, one can write

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\bf L}&={\bf\Xi}^{\top}\,,\\
{\bf M}&=-({\bf\Xi}+{\bf\Xi}^{\dagger})\,,\\
{\bf N}&={\bf\Xi}^{\ast}\,,\\
\alpha&=\beta^{\ast}=2\mathsf{i}{\rm Im}({\bf\Xi})\eta\,.\end{split}

After expanding all the parenthesis in Eq. (
13
),
it is easy to confirm that the latter is equivalent to Eq. (
13
).
The above discussion is still applicable when matrices and vectors are time-dependent.

We further discuss the reexpression of
our quantum master equation (
2
)
in the form of the
QTCL
equation.
Using the notation in Eq. (
7
),
the dissipative part of Eq. (
2
) may be reorganized as

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal{D}^{(k)}_{\text{Q}}[\hat{\rho}]=

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\hbar}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}{\bf K}_{ij}(\hat{\rho}\,\hat{y}_{i}\hat{x}_{j}+\hat{x}_{i}\hat{y}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho})

(36)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\hbar}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}{\bf K}_{ij}(\hat{x}_{i}\hat{\rho}\hat{y}_{j}+\hat{y}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}\hat{x}_{j})\,.

for a generic Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
.
In the above equation,
{\bf K}
is defined in Eq. (
10
) and

\hat{y}_{i}:={\rm e}^{\tfrac{1}{2}\beta_{i}\hat{H}}\hat{x}_{i}\,{\rm e}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta_{i}\hat{H}}

(37)

for
i=1,...,2n
and with
\beta_{j+n}=\beta_{j}
for
j=1,...,n
.

For the quadratic Hamiltonian (
12
),
the operator vector in Eq. (
37
) is given in Eq. (
33
)
and

\hat{y}_{i}^{\dagger}={\rm e}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta_{i}\hat{H}}\hat{x}_{i}\,{\rm e}^{\tfrac{1}{2}\beta_{i}\hat{H}}={\sf S}_{\!\beta_{i}}^{\ast}(\hat{x}-\xi)+\xi\,.

Inserting these in Eq. (
36
),
we found a
QTCL
master equation (
13
),
where the matrix
\bf\Xi
is given by Eq. (
20
) and
\eta=\xi
.

Appendix D
Uniqueness of the Matrix
{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}

Normally, a quantum master equation is expressed as
the sum of unitary and non-unitary generators of the evolution,
this decomposition is however not unique.
As discussed in Refs.
[
15
,
47
]
,
the
GTCL
equation (
18
) for general Lindblad operators
is invariant under the following transformation:

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\mu}^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\nu=1}^{2n}{U}_{\mu\nu}\hat{L}_{\nu}+\varsigma_{\mu}\qquad(\mu=1,...,2n),

(38)

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\prime}_{\rm eff}

\displaystyle:=\hat{H}_{\rm eff}+\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^{2n}\frac{{g}_{\mu\nu}}{2\mathsf{i}}(\varsigma_{\mu}^{\ast}{U}_{\nu\kappa}\hat{L}_{\kappa}-\varsigma_{\mu}{U}_{\nu\kappa}^{\ast}\hat{L}_{\kappa}^{\dagger}),

where
U
is a complex
2n\times 2n
unitary matrix satisfying
UgU^{\dagger}=g
,
and
\varsigma_{\mu}
are complex numbers.
Reexpressing Eq. (
18
) using the transformed operators,

\hat{L}_{\mu}^{\prime}
and
\hat{H}^{\prime}_{\rm eff}
,
changes the unitary and non-unitary parts of the
GTCL
equation.
Choosing
g_{\mu\nu}=\delta_{\mu\nu}
,
the
GTCL
equation is reduced to the
GKSL
equation.
This nonuniqueness for the
GKSL
equation is already pointed out
in the seminal
GKSL
works
[
48
]
and similar transformations
are reported in other descriptions of open systems.
For instance, although the Bloch-Redfield equations (and generalizations)
are considered in Ref.
[
23
]
,
the prescription follows the same structure of above transformation:
a change in the Lindblad operators is compensated
by a modification of the Hamiltonian, or
vice-versa
.

As shown in Sec.
IV
, the
QTCL
equation (
13
)
is reduced to the
GTCL
equation (
18
)
with
\hat{L}_{\mu}=\lambda_{\mu}\cdot(\hat{x}-\eta)
.
Applying Eq. (
38
), these Lindblad operators become

\hat{L}^{\prime}_{\mu}={U}_{\mu\nu}\lambda_{\nu}\cdot(\hat{x}-\eta)+\varsigma_{\mu}\,,

but the matrix
{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}
defined by Eq. (
17
) is invariant:

{\bf\Xi}^{\prime}_{\text{H}}={U}{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}{U}^{\dagger}={\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}\,.

Noteworthy,
neither the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\rm eff}

nor the matrices
{\bf\Xi}
and
{\bf\Xi}_{\text{A}}
in Eq. (
14
)
are invariant for the same transformation (
38
).

Appendix E
Quantum Detailed Balance Conditions

For a
GKSL
equation written as Eq. (
18
),
the quantum detailed balance conditions
[
16
]

are expressed, for
\mu=1,...,n
, as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&{\text{\it(i)}}\,\,\,[\hat{H},\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}]=0;\\
&{\text{\it(ii)}}\,\,\,\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{\mu}\hat{U}={\rm e}^{-i\omega_{\mu}t}\hat{L}_{\mu}\,,\,\,\,\hat{U}:={\rm e}^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}t}\,,\quad\\
&{\text{\it(iii)}}\,\,\,\hat{L}_{\mu+n}={\rm e}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta\hbar\omega_{\mu}}\hat{L}_{\mu}^{\dagger}\,.\end{split}

These necessary and sufficient conditions relate the Bohr
frequencies
\omega_{\mu}
of the system Hamiltonian
\hat{H}

with the Lindblad operators in order to asymptotically drive
the system to the equilibrium state.
The
GKSL
equation in Eq. (
26
) is one example:
the operators in Eq. (
27
) and
a harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian are detailed balanced.
In this particular case
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}
,
and the asymptotic state of Eq. (
26
) is
a thermal Gibbs state
\sim\exp[-\beta\hat{H}]

[
12
]
.
These conditions for
GKSL
equation,
written as the
GTCL
equation (
18
),
for a quadratic Hamiltonian and linear Lindblad operators are thoroughly
studied in Ref.
[
49
]
.

For the quadratic Hamiltonian in Eq. (
16
),
using
\hat{H}
in Eq. (
12
) and the canonical commutation relation
in Eq. (
8
), we determine
[
44
,
45
,
46
]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathsf{i}}{4}[(\hat{x}-\xi)\cdot{\bf H}(\hat{x}-\xi),(\hat{x}-\xi)\cdot{\bf\Xi}^{\ast}_{\text{A}}(\hat{x}-\xi)]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\hbar}{2}(\hat{x}-\xi)\cdot({\bf H}{\sf J}{\bf\Xi}^{\ast}_{\text{A}}-{\bf\Xi}^{\ast}_{\text{A}}{\sf J}{\bf H})(\hat{x}-\xi)\,,

where
\xi=\eta
.
Consequently, the condition (
i
) can be written as

(-{\sf J}{\bf H})^{\top}{\bf\Xi}_{\text{A}}={\bf\Xi}_{\text{A}}{\sf J}{\bf H}
,
which is equivalent to

{\sf S}_{t}^{\top}{\bf\Xi}_{\text{A}}{\sf S}_{t}={\bf\Xi}_{\text{A}}\,,\,\,\,{\sf S}_{t}={\rm e}^{{\sf J}{\bf H}t}\,.

For a linear Lindblad operator
\hat{L}_{\mu}=\lambda_{\mu}\cdot(\hat{x}-\xi)
,
using Eqs. (
31
) and (
32
),
the condition (
ii
) is thus expressed as an eigenvalue equation:

{\sf S}_{t}^{\top}\lambda_{\mu}={\rm e}^{-i\omega_{\mu}t}\lambda_{\mu}\,,

(39)

because

\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{\mu}\hat{U}=\lambda_{\mu}\cdot(\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{x}\hat{U}-\xi)={\sf S}_{t}^{\top}\lambda_{\mu}\cdot(\hat{x}-\xi)\,.

The condition (
iii
) immediately reads

\lambda_{\mu+n}={\rm e}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta\hbar\omega_{\mu}}\lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}\,.

(40)

From Eq. (
40
), the matrix in Eq. (
17
) for

g_{\mu\nu}=\delta_{\mu\nu}
becomes

{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{2n}\lambda_{\mu}\lambda_{\mu}^{\dagger}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\left(\lambda_{\mu}\lambda_{\mu}^{\dagger}+{\rm e}^{-\beta\hbar\omega_{\mu}}\lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}\lambda_{\mu}^{\top}\right)\,.

Using Eq. (
39
), it is then straightforward to show that

{\sf S}_{t}^{\top}{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}{\sf S}_{t}={\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}
.
Finally, from the definition in Eq. (
14
),
we find a necessary condition for quantum detailed balance of
a quadratic
GKSL
equation (
18
)

{\sf S}_{t}^{\top}{\bf\Xi}{\sf S}_{t}={\bf\Xi}\,.

However, it will be more useful for our purposes
to perform the Wick rotation (
34
) in above relations to find

{\sf S}_{\beta}^{\top}{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}{\sf S}_{\beta}={\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}\,,\,\,\,{\sf S}_{\beta}^{\top}{\bf\Xi}_{\text{A}}{\sf S}_{\beta}={\bf\Xi}_{\text{A}}\,,\,\,\,{\sf S}_{\beta}^{\top}{\bf\Xi}{\sf S}_{\beta}={\bf\Xi}\,.

(41)

To require our master equation to describe thermal relaxation,
the matrix in Eq. (
20
) becomes

{\bf\Xi}={\bf K}{\sf S}_{\beta}
, since all the temperatures are equal.
Applying relations in Eq. (
41
) for this matrix
{\bf\Xi}

and using the properties of
{\sf S}_{\beta}
in Eq. (
35
),
we find

{\bf\Xi}_{\text{A}}=0\,,\,\,\,{\bf\Xi}_{\text{H}}=2{\bf\Xi}=2{\bf K}{\sf S}_{\beta}\,,

which are necessary conditions for our master equation describe
a thermal relaxation process.
From our condition (
19
),
the matrix
{\sf S}_{\beta}=\exp[-i\hbar\beta{\sf J}{\bf H}/2]

must be positive semidefinite and, then,
the Hamiltonian matrix
{\sf J}{\bf H}
must have purely imaginary eigenvalues.
Such Hamiltonian matrices are called elliptic
[
46
]

and include harmonic oscillators and linearly coupled oscillators,
both with
{\bf H}>0
.
