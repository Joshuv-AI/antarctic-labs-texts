Approximation of topological singularities through free discontinuity functionals: the critical and super-critical regimes
V. Crismale, L. De Luca, R. Scala

Abstract
. We further investigate the properties of an approach to topological singularities through free discontinuity functionals of Mumford-Shah type proposed in [ 28 ] . We prove the variational equivalence between such energies, Ginzburg-Landau, and Core-Radius for anti-plane screw dislocations energies in dimension two, in the relevant energetic regimes | log ⁡ ε | a |\log\varepsilon|^{a} , a ≥ 1 a\geq 1 , where ε \varepsilon denotes the linear size of the process zone near the defects. Further, we remove the a priori restrictive assumptions that the approximating order parameters have compact jump set. This is obtained by proving a new density result for 𝕊 1 \mathbb{S}^{1} -valued S ​ B ​ V p SBV^{p} functions, approximated through functions with essentially closed jump set, in the strong B ​ V BV norm. Keywords : Functions of Bounded Variation; Strict Convergence; Jacobian determinant; Topological Singularities; Γ \Gamma -convergence; Ginzburg-Landau Model; Core-Radius Approach. Introduction

This paper concerns the analysis of topological singularities, which is a central topic in models arising in Physics and Materials Science. Vortices in superconductivity and superfluidity and (screw end edge) dislocations in single crystal plasticity are the main examples of such phenomenon
[
4
,
41
,
43
,
49
,
50
,
51
]
.

In the last decades several models have been introduced to describe the emergency of these objects. Among them,
the most celebrated is the Ginzburg-Landau (GL) model, mainly studied in the context of superconductivity.
In such a model, the order parameter is a function
u\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
and the energy functional (in its simplest form) reads as

(0.1)

\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{GL}}_{\varepsilon}(u):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\big(1-|u|^{2}\big)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x,

where the parameter
\varepsilon>0
is referred to as
coherence length
.
Here and below
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a bounded open set with Lipschitz continuous boundary.
A topological singularity is nothing but a point around which
u
has non-trivial winding number and hence the main object to look at is the Jacobian determinant (of
u
)
Ju:=\det\nabla u
.
Denoting by
\mathbb{S}^{1}
the set of unitary vectors in the plane, we notice that close to a topological singularity,
u
cannot be
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-valued
(a singularity can be seen somehow as a zero of the order parameter); therefore, the parameter
\varepsilon
can be interpreted as the size of the region where

u
fails to take values in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
and hence as the core-radius of the topological singularity.

The variational analysis of the (GL) functional has been first systematized in the monography
[
6
]
(see also
[
59
]
and the references therein for the asymptotic analysis in terms of
\Gamma
-convergence), where the (GL) model is compared with (and shown somehow to be “equivalent” to) the so-called core-radius (CR) approach, in antiplane elasticity. Within this framework, the main variable is represented by the distribution of topological singularities
\mu=\sum\alpha_{i}\delta_{\xi_{i}}
(with integer weights
\alpha_{i}
) but the energy functional depends both on
\mu
- which here plays the role of the Jacobian in (GL) - and on a map
u\in H^{1}(\Omega_{\varepsilon}(\mu);\mathbb{S}^{1})
“compatible with
\mu
”. Here,
\varepsilon
is the core-radius,
\Omega_{\varepsilon}(\mu):=\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{i}\overline{B}_{\varepsilon}(\xi_{i})
and the notion of compatibility is given by the fact that
\deg(u,\partial B_{\varepsilon}(\xi_{i}))=\alpha_{i}
(assuming that the balls
B_{\varepsilon}(\xi_{i})
are pairwise disjoint). The energy of the system thus writes

(0.2)

\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{CR}}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,u):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}(\mu)}|\nabla u|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+|\mu|(\Omega).

Here, the quantity
|\mu|(\Omega)
plays the same role of the potential term in
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{CR}}_{\varepsilon}
, namely, avoids that the cores do not cover the whole domain; in other words, it serves only to guarantee compactness and does not provide any energy contribution in the asymptotics as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

The (CR) approach is mostly used to model screw dislocations in semi-discrete theories. Loosely speaking, in pure (anti-plane) elasticity the bulk energy is determined by the Hooke’s law, and
reads as
\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x
, where the displacement
w
lies in
H^{1}(\Omega)
.
In presence of a finite distribution
\mu=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
of (scalar) defects, the material has a purely plastic behavior in the cores
B_{\varepsilon}(\xi_{i})
and, oversimplifying, such a plastic contribution can be expressed by
|\mu|(\Omega)
.
Moreover, along a closed circuit enclosing the singularity
\xi_{i}
, a displacement
w
compatible with
\mu
should have a jump
[w]
equal to
\alpha_{i}
. Therefore, the displacement
w
is only in
SBV^{2}(\Omega_{\varepsilon}(\mu))
with
[w]\in\mathbb{Z}
and its elastic energy should be given by
\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}(\mu)}|\nabla w|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x
, where
\nabla w
is the absolutely continuous part of
\mathrm{D}w
. Setting

(0.3)

u=e^{2\pi\imath w},

one obtains that the total energy associated to the pair
(\mu,u)
is given by
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{CR}}_{\varepsilon}
.

In this paper, we adopt a different viewpoint, following the approach proposed in
[
28
]
.
The main feature is that the order parameter is now an
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-valued map, as in the (CR) approach, defined on the whole
\Omega
, as in the (GL) approach. Clearly, in presence of topological singularities, such a map cannot be in
H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{1})
. But, instead of removing small disks around the singularities (as in (CR)) or to weaken the
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-constraint (as in (GL)), the map
u
is now allowed to jump. More precisely,
u
is a
special function of bounded variation
with square-integrable approximate gradient (i.e.,
u\in SBV^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{1})
).
The energy functional we consider is

(0.4)

\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(u):=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{H}^{1}(S_{u}),

where
\varepsilon>0
is a small parameter determining the size of the jump set
S_{u}
of
u
. Here and throughout the paper
\mathcal{H}^{1}
denotes the (one-dimensional) Hausdorff measure.

Formally, the functional
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}

has the structure of the Mumford-Shah functional
[
56
]
, but the
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-constraint makes the analysis completely different.
Indeed, having in mind the identity (
0.3
) and the (CR) approach for screw dislocations, jumps of the map
u
correspond to non-integer jumps of the displacement
w
and should pay energy.
In other words, the (amplitude of the) jump
[w]
of the displacement exhibits a transition between integers in a little portion of
S_{w}
.
The transition is assumed to have length of the order of
\varepsilon
, and corresponds to the presence of singularities.
In this respect,

\mathcal{H}^{1}(S_{u})
is the analogue of the potential term in (GL) and of the plastic term in (CR) and
the parameter
\varepsilon
can be understood also in this case as the core-radius of the singularity.

We highlight that for
SBV
maps the definition of topological degree as well as that of Jacobian determinant are not so standard so that the notion of topological singularity is not so clear as in (GL) and in (CR). Nevertheless, in
[
28
]
, using the
minimal lifting
in
[
45
]
, a notion of Jacobian determinant is provided also for
SBV
functions; we recall such a definition in Section
1
(see
[
53
]
where in a more restrictive setting this notion was first introduce, and see also
[
9
]
for a different definition of Jacobian determinant in dimension 2).
In a nutshell, given a map
u\in SBV(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{1})
, the Jacobian
Ju
of
u
is defined as the boundary of the
1
-current
T_{u}

defined by

(0.5)

\displaystyle T_{u}:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(-u^{1}\partial_{x_{2}}u^{2}+u^{2}\partial_{x_{2}}u^{1};u^{1}\partial_{x_{1}}u^{2}-u^{2}\partial_{x_{1}}u^{1})+\frac{1}{2}(u^{+}\wedge u^{-})\bm{\tau}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits S_{u}

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle T_{u}^{D}+T_{u}^{S}\,.

We stress that dealing with the energy considered in
[
28
]
, that is

(0.6)

\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(u):=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\overline{S}_{u}),

rules out, for instance,
SBV^{2}
functions with jump set dense in the whole
\Omega
; such a fact is crucial to prove compactness, rendering the analysis more simpler.

In
[
28
, Theorem 3.1]
the
\Gamma
-convergence analysis of the functional
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}
at the energy regime
|\log\varepsilon|
has been developed.
As one may expect, such an analysis reveals that the functional
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}
shares the same compactness and
\Gamma
-convergence properties of the functionals
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{CR}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{GL}}
.
Specifically, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, the Jacobian determinant tends to concentrate around a finite number of effective singularities and the
\Gamma
-limit of
the functionals
\frac{\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}}{|\log\varepsilon|}
is given (up to multiplicative constants) by the total variation of
the limiting measure of the Jacobians.
Since, in view of the possibile presence of short dipoles, a uniform bound on the total variation of the dislocations’ distributions is not available, the natural setting for such an asymptotic analysis is the (strong) flat convergence for Jacobian determinants, the flat topology being the strong topology in the dual of Lipschitz continuous functions with compact support in
\Omega
.

In the present paper we generalize the analysis done in
[
28
]
along two directions.
On the one hand, we show that the penalization term can be “weakened” considering only the length of the jump set instead of its closure, i.e., working with the functional
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
rather than with
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}
. Therefore it is not needed to assume
a priori
that the jump set is compact.
On the other hand, we show that the functional
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
shares the same asymptotic behavior of the functionals
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{CR}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{GL}}
also in other energy regimes.

The first improvement is obtained by means of a density result in
SBV^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{1})
,
p>1
, with respect to energies
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
for fixed
\varepsilon>0
, through functions in
SBV^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{1})
with (essentially) closed jump set, converging in the strong
BV
norm and such that also the two unilateral traces of the approximants along the jump set converge; in particular, by using the characterization of
Ju
as the boundary of
T_{u}
in (
0.5
), the strong convergence of Jacobian determinants with respect to flat norm follows.

Our result hinges on tools developed in a slightly different setting, that is when only the symmetric part of the diffuse gradient is controlled in some
L^{p}
, for
p>1
, rather than the whole diffuse gradient. Mechanically, this corresponds to consider fracture models for general linearized elasticity without the anti-plane assumption, described by the Griffith functional
[
40
]
instead of the Mumford-Shah one.

In fact, the main tool for density results developed in the context of Mumford-Shah functional is an approximated Poincaré-Wirtinger inequality for
SBV^{p}
functions with small (
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}
-measure of the) jump set, due to De Giorgi-Carriero-Leaci (
[
25
]
): given
u\in SBV^{p}
there exists a truncation in
W^{1,p}
such that
u
differs from
w
on an exceptional set
\omega
whose volume is controlled by
(\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(S_{u}))^{1^{*}}
,
d\geq 2
being the space dimension
and
1^{*}:=d/d{-}1
. In the same paper, this result has been used to prove that the jump set of Mumford-Shah minimizers is essentially closed, namely the
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}
measure of the jump set equals that of its closure. After short time a generalization for
SBV^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{k-1})
maps has been proven in
[
16
]
; by combining such a generalization with an argument in
[
8
]
(cf. Lemma 5.2 therein), one can show that the Mumford-Shah functional in
SBV^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{1})
can be approximated through
SBV^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{1})
functions having essentially closed jump set and converging pointwise.
Unfortunately, pointwise convergence of a sequence of functions does not guarantee convergence of the corresponding Jacobians; since we need convergence in the flat norm such an approach is not satisfactory for our purposes. For functions with finite Griffith energy (with exponent
p
), that is in the space
GSBD^{p}
(
[
23
]
), a fundamental tool is the approximated Poincaré-Korn inequality in
[
10
]
, stating that for any
u\in GSBD^{p}
with small jump set there exists an infinitesimal rigid motion
a
(i.e. an affine function with null symmetrized gradient) such that the
L^{\frac{dp}{d-1}}
-norm of
u-a
is estimated by the
L^{p}
norm of
e(u)
, the symmetrized diffuse gradient of
u
, outside an exceptional set
\omega
whose volume is controlled by
(\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(J_{u}))^{{1^{*}}}
. Moreover, a convolution of
u\chi_{\omega^{c}}+a\chi_{\omega}
at the same scale of the domain provides a function with
L^{p}
-norm of the symmetrized diffuse gradient controlled
by those of
u
.

This result, on which other contribution in this direction rest (see, e.g.,the approximation in
GSBD^{p}
through functions with essentially closed jump set
[
19
,
11
]
and the analogue of
[
25
]
for the Griffith functional
[
20
,
17
,
12
]
), has been generalized by
[
18
]
and
[
13
]
: here, any
u\in GSBD^{p}
is approximated, in terms of the Griffith energy, by functions
W^{1,p}
on a slightly smaller domain, with essentially closed jump set, which differ from
u
on a set
\omega
whose boundary is controlled by
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(J_{u})
; further, in
[
18
]
it is shown that in dimension two it is possible to guarantee that the approximants coincide with
u
in the boundary neighborhood where they are not in
W^{1,p}
. In
[
18
]
such an approximation is used to prove an integral representation result, while in
[
13
]
the main result is the approximation of any
u\in GSBD^{p}
, with respect to the Griffith energy, through functions with essentially closed jump set differing from
u
on sets of vanishing perimeter.

Moreover, Friedrich
[
32
]
proved a
piecewise Korn inequality
in dimension two, showing that up to subtracting piecewise rigid functions (finite sums of infinitesimal rigid motions multiplied by characteristic functions), any
u
in
GSBD^{p}
can be approximated by functions in
SBV^{q}\cap L^{\infty}
, for
q<p
, in particular the diffuse gradient of the approximants is estimated on the whole domain by
e(u)
; this is a very powerful tool allowing to overcome the lack of a Coarea Formula in
GSBD
and then to show, e.g., existence of quasi-static evolutions for Brittle Fracture models (see, for instance,
[
34
]
). In the same spirit, in
[
33
]
a similar result has been shown in the Mumford Shah setting, namely dealing only with full diffuse gradients.

Eventually, we refer to
[
29
]
for the two dimensional analogue of
[
16
]
for maps in
SBV^{p(\cdot)}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{k-1})
, (with
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
) whose approximate gradient is integrable with respect to the variable exponent
p(\cdot)
over
\Omega
and whose jump set has finite
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-measure (see also
[
48
]
for the variable exponents analogue of
[
25
]
), obtained under the assumption that the function
p(\cdot)
is regular enough and takes values in
(1,2)
. This uses the analogue of the approximation of
[
18
]
, proven by employing
retractions
\mathcal{P}\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\setminus\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{S}^{k-1}
with locally
q
-integrable gradient for
q\in[1,2)
, where
\mathcal{X}
is a smooth complex of codimension two (cf. e.g.
[
14
]
).

We then compare our main density result
Corollary
2.2
with
[
13
, Theorem 5.1]
: we are in two dimensions and consider the full diffuse gradient instead of its symmetrized part, however we keep in the approximation the constraint of being
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-valued. We notice that also a version with symmetrized diffuse gradient is readily shown with essentially the same proof, see Theorem
2.6
.

Since in our application the case
p=2
is the relevant one, we cannot follow a strategy based on retractions. Moreover, the proof of
[
18
]
,
[
13
]
, and
[
33
]
is not compatible with a non-convex target space such as
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

Our approach is based on the existence of a
lifting

\varphi\in SBV^{p}(\Omega)
(i.e., such that
u=e^{2\pi\imath\varphi}
) with
\pi\|\varphi\|_{BV}\leq\|u\|_{BV}
(
[
24
]
), for which we provide a suitable approximation (Theorem
2.1
) and then compose the approximants with
e^{2\pi\imath\cdot}
. We observe that, since in
[
13
, Theorem 5.1]
the set on which the traces of the approximants differ from those of the given function has only finite
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}
measure and then could be dense in the original jump set, after an application of
[
13
, Theorem 5.1]
to
\varphi
and the composition with
e^{2\pi\imath\cdot}
, one could obtain approximating functions with jump set dense in the integer jump set of
\varphi
, whose
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-measure could be of order
\|u\|_{BV}
.

Therefore we need a more refined density result, which allows to approximate integer jumps with integer jumps as well. The strategy is to work locally near points with integer jump at a scale for which the jump is almost flat and assumes a constant integer value. Then locally most of the jump set of
\varphi
can be transferred (up to a small error) into a flat segment
S
on which the jump has the same value; such
S
can be chosen in such a way that the remaining small jump set of
\varphi
, on any square with arbitrarily small sidelength with a side contained in
S
, is small compared to the sidelength: this follows by a two dimensional argument drawn from
[
18
]
.

In this way, the approximation for functions with small jump set of
[
18
]
may be applied at every scale; therefore a Whitney-type argument combined with the fact that the approximants coincide with the original function on the boundary of any square ensures that the traces are the same on both sides of
S
, so the new jump is still integer.
This strategy may be replicated for different target manifolds, provided a lifting with good
BV
bounds as in
[
24
]
exists (in this respect see e.g.
[
15
]
), but limiting to two dimensional domains. We notice that in the present context several hard issues arise when considering space dimension
d\geq 3
, such as the lack of the analogue of the Ball Construction.

The density result described above allows to develop the
\Gamma
-convergence analysis for the functional
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
also in different energetic regimes (as it applies for fixed
\varepsilon>0
), thus generalizing the setting of
[
28
]
.

First, we develop the
\Gamma
-convergence analysis in the so-called critical regime, that is
|\log\varepsilon|^{2}
. Loosely speaking, since
|\log\varepsilon|
is the energy cost of an isolated singularity, the fact that
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\sim|\log\varepsilon|^{2}
implies that, for
\varepsilon>0
, the number of singularities of the Jacobians
Ju_{\varepsilon}
is of order
|\log\varepsilon|
; therefore, the Jacobians
Ju_{\varepsilon}
, once rescaled by
|\log\varepsilon|
, should converge (in the flat norm) to a measure
\mu
that is not anymore atomic but diffuse. Furthermore, we prove that such a measure
\mu
lies also in
H^{-1}(\Omega)
.
Indeed,
by standard compactness results in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, also the fields
T^{\mathrm{D}}_{u_{\varepsilon}}
, once scaled by
|\log\varepsilon|
, should converge (weakly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
) to a field
T^{\mathrm{D}}
, whose distributional divergence is shown to be given by
-\pi\mu
. As one may expect, the
\Gamma
-limit accounts both for the plastic contribution of
\mu
as well as for the elastic energy of
T^{\mathrm{D}}
. That is the reason why the
|\log\varepsilon|^{2}
regime is called critical, since in such a case the elastic and plastic effects are of the same order.
The
\Gamma
-convergence analysis for the functional
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
is provided in Theorems
3.3
and
3.4
which are proved in Section
5
. Second, the proofs of the compactness and of the lower bound are obtained combining the corresponding results for the core-radius approach together with the refined ball construction machinery introduced in
[
28
]
to analyze the
|\log\varepsilon|
regime.

Finally, adopting the same strategy, in Theorem
3.4
we analyze also the super-critical regimes
|\log\varepsilon|^{2}\ll N_{\varepsilon}\ll\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
. In such a case, the interaction elastic energy is larger and larger than the core energy, so that (unless scaling differently the two quantities
Ju_{\varepsilon}
and
T^{\mathrm{D}}_{u_{\varepsilon}}
) one keeps track of the only
T^{\mathrm{D}}
and the Jacobian determinants do not play any role when computing the effective energy.

We highlight that the
\Gamma
-convergence analysis for the functional
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{GL}}
in the regime
|\log\varepsilon|^{2}
has been developed in
[
47
,
60
,
61
]
, where the authors consider also the case with magnetic field. The analysis for
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{CR}}
is provided in Section
4
and is somehow a short self-contained resume of the results above, along the lines of
[
2
]
.

However, a similar result in the context of edge dislocations within the (CR) approach is proven in
[
35
]
under the well-separation assumption for the singularities (see also
[
55
]
for such an analysis in the nonlinear elasticity framework); such an assumption has been removed in
[
39
]
.
In view of the asymptotic equivalence result
[
1
]
between the Ginzburg-Landau model and the purely discrete models of
XY
spin systems and screw dislocations, we have that the analysis in the (GL) context extends also to such discrete models.

1.
Preliminary results

In this section we collect some preliminary notions on the flat norm of measures and currents, as well as some properties of
BV
functions that will be used throughout the paper.

Flat norm of Radon measures

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer and let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded and open set. We denote by
\mathcal{M}_{b}(U)
the space of Radon measures on
U
with finite total variation. If
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{b}(U)
, we denote by
|\mu|(U)
the total variation of
\mu
. We recall that a sequence
\mu_{k}\in\mathcal{M}_{b}(U)
converges tightly to
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{b}(U)
if
\mu_{k}
converges to
\mu
weakly* as measure, and
|\mu_{k}|(U)\rightarrow|\mu|(U)
.
We also introduce the concept of flat norm of a measure
\mu
, denoted by
\|\mu\|_{\mathrm{flat}}
, as

(1.1)

\displaystyle{\|\mu\|_{\mathrm{flat}}:=\sup_{{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{\ffi\in\Cc^{0,1}(U)}{\|\ffi\|_{C^{0,1}(U)}\le 1}}}\int_{U}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mu\,.}

Here and below, the Lipschitz norm
\|\varphi\|_{C^{0,1}(U)}
is defined by

\|\varphi\|_{C^{0,1}(U)}:=\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}+\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{x,y\in U}{x\neq y}}\frac{|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|}{|x-y|}\,.

By a density argument we easily see that the supremum in (
1.1
) can be equivalently computed among smooth and compactly supported (in
U
) functions
\varphi
with
\|\varphi\|_{C^{0,1}(U)}\leq 1
.

Flat norm of
k
-currents.

Let
n\geq 2
be an integer and let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set. For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
0\leq k\leq n
, we denote by
\mathcal{D}^{k}(U)
the topological vector space of smooth and compactly supported
k
-forms on
U
, and by
\mathcal{D}_{k}(U)
its dual, i.e., the space of
k
-currents on
U
.

The mass
|T|
of a current
T\in\mathcal{D}_{k}(U)
is defined as

|T|=\sup\{\langle T,\omega\rangle:\;\omega\in\mathcal{D}^{k}(U),\|\omega\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1\}\,.

As done in (
1.1
) for measures, we define the
flat norm
of a current
T\in\mathcal{D}_{k}(U)
in
U
by

(1.2)

\|T\|_{\mathrm{flat},U}:=\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{\omega\in\mathcal{D}^k(U)}{\|\omega\|_{F,U}\le 1}}\langle T,\omega\rangle,

where

\|\omega\|_{F,U}:=\|\omega\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}+\|\mathrm{d}\omega\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}\,.

In the special case that
T
is a
0
-current and has finite mass, then it can be standardly identified with a measure, and the flat norm of
T
coincides with the flat norm of the measure
T
defined in (
1.1
).

Jacobian for
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-valued Sobolev maps

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded and open set.
Given a map
u\in W^{1,1}(U;\mathbb{S}^{1})
we recall that the distributional Jacobian
Ju=\textrm{Det}(\nabla u)
of
u
is defined by

(1.3)

\langle Ju,\varphi\rangle_{U}:=\int_{U}\nabla\varphi\cdot\lambda_{u}\,\;\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\qquad\textrm{for every }\varphi\in C_{\mathrm{c}}^{\infty}(U),

where

(1.4)

\displaystyle\lambda_{u}:=\frac{1}{2}\Big(-u^{1}\frac{\partial u^{2}}{\partial x_{2}}+u^{2}\frac{\partial u^{1}}{\partial x_{2}};u^{1}\frac{\partial u^{2}}{\partial x_{1}}-u^{2}\frac{\partial u^{1}}{\partial x_{1}}\Big)\,;

notice that

\lambda_{u}\in L^{1}(U;\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Moreover, denoting by
j(u)\in L^{1}(U;\mathbb{R}^{2})
the
current
associated to
u
, i.e.,

(1.5)

j(u):=\frac{1}{2}(u^{1}\nabla u^{2}-u^{2}\nabla u^{1})\,,

one has
j^{\perp}(u)=\lambda_{u}
and
j(u)=\pi\nabla w
, where
w
is a generic lifting of
u
, i.e., a map in
SBV^{2}(U)
satisfying (
0.3
)
and

\nabla w
is the approximate gradient of
w
. Furthermore, it is easy to check that

Ju=-\mathrm{Div}\lambda_{u}=\mathrm{curl}\,j(u)=\pi\mathrm{curl}(\nabla w),

holds in the sense of distributions.

In the sequel we will use the fact that a function
u\in H^{1}(U;\mathbb{S}^{1})
satisfies
\textrm{Det}(\nabla u)=0
in the sense of distributions.
Moreover, if
u\in H^{1}(U\setminus\overline{B};\mathbb{S}^{1})
, where
B\subset U
is a ball, then, integrating by parts,

\int_{U\setminus\overline{B}}\lambda_{u}\cdot\nabla\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\partial B}\lambda_{u}\cdot{\bm{\nu}}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}=\int_{\partial B}j(u)\cdot{\bm{\tau}}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1},\qquad\textrm{for every }\varphi\in C_{\mathrm{c}}^{\infty}(U),

where
{\bm{\nu}}
is the inner normal vector to
\partial B
,
{\bm{\tau}}=-{\bm{\nu}}^{\perp}
is the counter-clockwise tangent vector to
\partial B
. Notice that
j(u)\cdot{\bm{\tau}}=\frac{1}{2}(u^{1}\frac{\partial u^{2}}{\partial{\bm{\tau}}}-u^{2}\frac{\partial u^{1}}{\partial{\bm{\tau}}})
on
\partial B
.

We recall that
\deg(u,\partial B)\in\mathbb{Z}
is defined as

(1.6)

\displaystyle\deg(u,\partial B):=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\partial B}j(u)\cdot{\bm{\tau}}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\partial B}\lambda_{u}\cdot{\bm{\nu}}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}\,,

whenever
u\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial B;\mathbb{S}^{1})
.

Jacobian for
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-valued
SBV
maps

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded and open set.
For any
p\in[1,+\infty)
the symbol
SBV^{p}(U;\mathbb{R}^{2})
denotes the space of functions
u\in BV(A;\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that the Cantor part
\mathrm{D}^{c}u\equiv 0
, and
\nabla u\in L^{p}(A;\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2})
, where
\nabla u
is the density of
\mathrm{D}^{a}u
, i.e.,
\mathrm{D}^{a}u:=\nabla u\mathcal{L}^{2}
.
The space
SBV^{p}(U;\mathbb{S}^{1})
denotes the set of the functions
u\in SBV^{p}(U;\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that
|u|=1
a.e. in
U
.

The following result, proven in
[
28
, Corollary 2.1]
, is specialized here to maps taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
u\in SBV(U;\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{\infty}(U;\mathbb{R}^{2})
; then there exists a unique measure
\nu_{u}\in\mathcal{M}_{b}(U;\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2\times 2})
such that, whenever
\{v_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{1}(U;\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap W^{1,1}(U;\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{\infty}(U;\mathbb{R}^{2})

satisfies
\|v_{k}\|_{{L^{\infty}(U;\mathbb{R}^{2})}}\leq C<+\infty
for all
k\geq 1
and

v_{k}\overset{\mathrm{strict}}{\rightharpoonup}u
in
BV(U;\mathbb{R}^{2})
, then
v_{k}\otimes\nabla v_{k}\rightarrow\nu_{u}
, where
(\nu_{u})_{j}^{i,h}
is defined (for all
\varphi\in C_{\mathrm{c}}(U)
)
by

\displaystyle\int_{U}\varphi(x)\;\mathrm{d}(\nu_{u})^{i,h}_{j}=

\displaystyle\int_{U\setminus S_{u}}\phi(x)u^{h}(x)\partial_{x_{j}}u^{i}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{S_{u}}\phi(x)(u^{h,+}(x)+u^{h,-}(x))(u^{i,+}(x)-u^{i,-}(x))\bm{\nu}_{j}(x)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(x)\,,

for every
i,j,h\in\{1,2\}
.
Finally, if
\{u_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset SBV(U;\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{\infty}(U;\mathbb{R}^{2})
with

(1.7)

\|u_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(U;\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C,

for some constant
C>0
, and
u_{k}\overset{\mathrm{strict}}{\rightharpoonup}u
in
BV(U;\mathbb{R}^{2})
, then

(1.8)

\nu_{u_{k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\nu_{u}\qquad\textrm{in }\mathcal{M}_{b}(U;\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2\times 2})\,.

In the following, for every map
u\in SBV(U;\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{\infty}(U;\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we set

[u^{h}\mathrm{D}_{j}u^{i}]:=(\nu_{u})^{i,h}_{j},\;\;\;\;i,j,h\in\{1,2\}\,,

For any map
u\in SBV(U;\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{\infty}(U;\mathbb{R}^{2})
we introduce the
1
-current
T_{u}
defined by

(1.9)

\displaystyle T_{u}:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(-[u^{1}\mathrm{D}_{2}u^{2}]+[u^{2}\mathrm{D}_{2}u^{1}];[u^{1}\mathrm{D}_{1}u^{2}]-[u^{2}\mathrm{D}_{1}u^{1}])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(-u^{1}\partial_{x_{2}}u^{2}+u^{2}\partial_{x_{2}}u^{1};u^{1}\partial_{x_{1}}u^{2}-u^{2}\partial_{x_{1}}u^{1})+\frac{1}{2}(u^{+}\wedge u^{-})\bm{\tau}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits S_{u}

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle T_{u}^{D}+T_{u}^{S}\,.

where we have noted
\alpha\wedge\beta=-\alpha\cdot\beta^{\perp}=\det(\alpha,\beta).

Notice that
T_{u}^{D}\in L^{1}(U;\mathbb{R}^{2})
and that, if
u\in W^{1,1}(U;\mathbb{S}^{1})
, then
T_{u}=T_{u}^{D}=\lambda_{u}
with
\lambda_{u}
defined in (
1.4
).
Finally, we highlight that if
u\in SBV(U;\mathbb{S}^{1})
, for any lifting

of
u
, i.e., satisfying (
0.3
), it holds that

(1.10)

T^{D}_{u}:=\pi\nabla^{\perp}w;\qquad\qquad T^{S}_{u}:=\frac{1}{2}\sin(2\pi(w^{-}-w^{+}))\bm{\tau}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits S_{u}\,.

The distributional Jacobian
Ju\in\mathcal{D}_{0}(U)
of

is defined as the boundary of
T_{u}
, namely

(1.11)

\displaystyle Ju:=\partial T_{u}\qquad\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{D}_{0}(U).

Essentially by definition, it easily follows that

\|Ju\|_{\text{{\rm flat}},U}\leq C\|u\|_{BV},

for all
u\in SBV(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{1})
, for a universal constant
C>0
.

Remark 1.2
.

We point out that in general

is not a Radon measure. This notion of Jacobian determinant was first introduced in
[
53
]
under some special hypotheses on

. Under these hypotheses it turns out that

is also a Radon measure.

2.
Density results in
SBV^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{1})

In this section we prove that any function
u\in SBV^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{1})
can be approximated - in the strong
BV
norm - by (sequence of) functions in
SBV^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{1})
with closed jump set. As a consequence (see Corollary
2.3
), we deduce that the corresponding currents can be approximated in the flat norm.

In what follows for every function
\varphi\in SBV^{p}(\Omega)
(with
p\geq 1
) we denote by
S_{\varphi}^{\mathrm{frac}}
the
fractional jump set
of
\varphi
, i.e.,
S^{\mathrm{frac}}_{\varphi}:=\{x\in S_{\varphi}\colon[\varphi]\notin\mathbb{Z}\}
, and by
S_{\varphi}^{\mathrm{int}}
the

, namely,
S_{\varphi}^{\mathrm{int}}:=S_{\varphi}\setminus S_{\varphi}^{\mathrm{frac}}
.
The main result of this section is the following.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded open set with finite perimeter,
p\in(1,+\infty)
, and
\varepsilon>0
. Then for every
\varphi\in SBV^{p}(\Omega)
there exist:

•

closed sets
\Gamma^{\mathrm{int}}=\Gamma^{\mathrm{int}}_{\varepsilon}
,
\Gamma^{\mathrm{frac}}=\Gamma^{\mathrm{frac}}_{\varepsilon}
, finite unions of disjoint
C^{1}
curves;

•

a set
\widetilde{\omega}=\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}
, finite union of cubes;

•

a set of finite perimeter
\widehat{\omega}=\widehat{\omega}_{\varepsilon}
;

•

a function
\theta=\theta_{\varepsilon}\in SBV^{p}(\Omega)\cap W^{1,p}(\Omega\setminus({\Gamma^{\mathrm{int}}\cup\Gamma^{\mathrm{frac}}}\cup\overline{\widetilde{\omega}}))

;

such that

(2.1)

\{\nabla\varphi\neq\nabla\theta\}\subset\widetilde{\omega}\cup\widehat{\omega},\quad\mathcal{L}^{2}(\{\varphi\neq\theta\})<\varepsilon,\quad\theta=0\textrm{ in }\widetilde{\omega},

[\theta](x)\in\mathbb{Z}
for
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
x\in\Gamma^{\mathrm{int}}
,

(2.2)

|\mathcal{H}^{1}(S^{\mathrm{int}}_{\varphi})-\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma^{\mathrm{int}})|+\mathcal{H}^{1}(S^{\mathrm{frac}}_{\varphi}\triangle\Gamma^{\mathrm{frac}})+\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial\widetilde{\omega})+\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial^{*}\widehat{\omega})\leq\varepsilon,

and

(2.3)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\theta|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x\leq(1+\varepsilon)\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x.

Moreover,
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma^{\mathrm{frac}}\cap\{\theta^{+}\neq\varphi^{+}\})+\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma^{\mathrm{frac}}\cap\{\theta^{-}\neq\varphi^{-}\})\leq\varepsilon
, where
\theta^{\pm}
and
\varphi^{\pm}
denote the traces of
\theta
and
\varphi
on the two sides of
\Gamma
.

Before providing the proof of Theorem
2.1

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded open set of finite perimeter,
p\in(1,+\infty)
, and
\varepsilon>0
. Then for every
u\in SBV^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{1})

•

a closed set
\Gamma=\Gamma_{\varepsilon}

•

a set
\widetilde{\omega}=\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}

•

(2.4)

Moreover,
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma\cap\{v^{+}\neq u^{+}\})+\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma\cap\{v^{-}\neq u^{-}\})\leq\varepsilon
, where
v^{\pm}
and
u^{\pm}
denote the traces of

and

on the two sides of
\Gamma
.

Furthermore, since

∎

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\log\mathcal{R}ad(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon})|}\left|\sum_{B\in{\mathscr{C}}_{\varepsilon}(t^{p}_{\varepsilon})}\int_{B}\Big(\varphi_{\varepsilon}-\fint_{B}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}x\Big)\;\mathrm{d}(\widetilde{\mu}_{\varepsilon}-\nu^{p}_{\varepsilon})\right|

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{|\log\mathcal{R}ad(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon})|}\left|\sum_{B\in\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon}(t^{p}_{\varepsilon})\setminus{\mathscr{C}}_{\varepsilon}(t^{p}_{\varepsilon})}\int_{B\cap V}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}(\widetilde{\mu}_{\varepsilon}-\nu^{p}_{\varepsilon})\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\log\mathcal{R}ad(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon})|}\sum_{B\in\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon}(t^{p}_{\varepsilon})}\int_{B}\big(\max_{B}\varphi_{\varepsilon}-\min_{B}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\big)\big(|\widetilde{\mu}_{\varepsilon}|(B)+|\nu^{p}_{\varepsilon}|(B)\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\log\mathcal{R}ad(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon})|}\sum_{B\in\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon}(t^{p}_{\varepsilon})}\mathrm{diam}(B)\big(|\widetilde{\mu}_{\varepsilon}|(V)+|\nu^{p}_{\varepsilon}|(V)\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\mathcal{R}ad^{p}(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon})|\log\mathcal{R}ad(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon})|\,,

whence (
4.10
) follows.

Moreover, by the very definition of
\lambda_{u_{\varepsilon}}
in (
1.4
) and by the energy bound (
4.4
), we immediately have that

(4.11)

\frac{1}{2}\int_{V(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon})}|2\lambda_{u_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\int_{V(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon})}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\leq C|\log\mathcal{R}ad(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon})|^{2}\,,

thus, up to extracting a further subsequence, there exists
\lambda\in L^{2}(V;\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that (
4.6
) holds.
Notice that, for
p\in(0,1)
fixed, since
|U(t^{p}_{\varepsilon})|\rightarrow 0
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, we also deduce

(4.12)

\frac{\lambda_{u_{\varepsilon}}}{|\log\mathcal{R}ad(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon})|}\chi_{V(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon}(t^{p}_{\varepsilon}))}\rightharpoonup\lambda\qquad\text{ weakly in }L^{2}(V;\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Now we prove (ii).
To this end, let
p\in(0,1)
be fixed;
by (
4.9
) and by
Proposition
4.2
(4), we get

(4.13)

\displaystyle\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{2|\log\mathcal{R}ad(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon})|^{2}}\int_{(U(t_{\varepsilon}^{p})\setminus U(0))\cap V}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\geq

\displaystyle\pi{(1-p)}\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{|\nu_{\varepsilon}^{p}|(V)}{|\log\mathcal{R}ad(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon})|}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\pi{(1-p)}|\mu|(V)\,.

Furthermore, by (
4.12
), we have that

\displaystyle\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{2|\log\mathcal{R}ad(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon})|^{2}}\int_{V(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{p}))}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x=

\displaystyle\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{2}\int_{V}\Big|\frac{2\lambda_{u_{\varepsilon}}}{|\log\mathcal{R}ad(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon})|}\Big|^{2}\chi_{V(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{p}))}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2\int_{V}|\lambda|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\,,

which, together with (
4.13
) (letting

), yields (ii).
∎

5.1.
Proof of compactness and lower bound

By the energy bound (

), together with Corollary

, we have that

(5.1)

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\overline{S}_{u_{\varepsilon}})\leq C\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|^{2}\,,

\overline{S}_{u_{\varepsilon}}

of open balls (in

and

) that
\mathcal{R}ad(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon})\geq\varepsilon
, so that, from now on

(5.2)

\varepsilon\leq\mathcal{R}ad(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon})\leq C\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|^{2}\,,

for some

. Moreover, by construction,

(5.3)

u_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}(\Omega(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon});\mathbb{S}^{1})\,,

where we recall that

5.2

\varepsilon
small enough,

\bigcup_{B\in\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon}}\overline{B}\subset\Omega\,.

Up to applying a merging procedure (as described in Definition
4.1
) to the balls in
\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon}
, we can assume without loss of generality that these balls are mutually (essentially) disjoint, and still satisfy (
5.2
) and (
5.4
).
For
\varepsilon>0
small enough we set

(5.5)

\mu_{\varepsilon}:=\sum_{B\in\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon}}\deg(u_{\varepsilon},\partial B)\delta_{x(B)}\,.

By (
3.11
), (
5.2
), and (
5.3
), for
\varepsilon
small enough it holds

(5.6)

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon})}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,\;\mathrm{d}x\leq\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{2}\leq C|\log\mathcal{R}ad(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon})|^{2}\,.

Therefore we can apply Theorem
4.3
to the family
\{(\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon};\mu_{\varepsilon})\}_{\varepsilon}
.
Notice that, in view of the very definition of
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}
, we have that also the family
\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon}(1)
satisfies (
5.4
) (for
\varepsilon
small enough), so that
{\mathscr{C}}_{\varepsilon}(1)\equiv\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon}(1)

Setting

\widetilde{\mu}_{\varepsilon}:=\sum_{B\in\mathscr{B}_{\varepsilon}(1)}\mu_{\varepsilon}(B)\delta_{x(B)}\,,

by Theorem
4.3
(i) (more precisely, by (
4.5

(5.7)

\frac{\widetilde{\mu}_{\varepsilon}}{|\log\varepsilon|}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{flat}}}{{\rightarrow}}\mu\,,

for some

. By construction,
\mathrm{supp}\,\mu\subseteq\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}
.
Moreover, by arguing verbatim as in the proof of
[
28

one can prove that

which, combined with (

).
Furthermore, by (

) and by (

), we have that

\frac{1}{|\log\varepsilon|^{2}}\int_{\Omega}|2T^{D}_{u_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{|\log\varepsilon|^{2}}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\leq C\,,

so that, up to a further subsequence,

(5.8)

\frac{T^{D}_{u_{\varepsilon}}}{|\log\varepsilon|}\rightharpoonup T^{D}\qquad\textrm{in }L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})\,,

for some field

It remains to show that

, which will imply also that
\mu\in H^{-1}(\Omega)

To this end, let

(5.9)

\langle Ju_{\varepsilon},\varphi\rangle=\langle T_{u_{\varepsilon}},\nabla\varphi\rangle=\langle T^{D}_{u_{\varepsilon}},\nabla\varphi\rangle+\langle T^{S}_{u_{\varepsilon}},\nabla\varphi\rangle\,,

By, (

5.9
), (
5.10
), and (

\langle\pi\mu,\varphi\rangle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{|\log\varepsilon|}\langle Ju_{\varepsilon},\varphi\rangle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{|\log\varepsilon|}\langle T^{D}_{u_{\varepsilon}},\nabla\varphi\rangle=\langle T^{D},\nabla\varphi\rangle=\langle-\mathrm{Div}T^{D},\phi\rangle\,,

5.2.
Proof of the upper bound

In order to construct the recovery sequence, we first introduce some notation.

and for every

Lemma 5.1

with

\displaystyle 4\pi^{2}+4\pi\varepsilon\frac{N^{\bar{l}}_{\varepsilon}}{r^{\bar{l}}_{\varepsilon}}+\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{l=1}{\newatop{l\neq\bar l}{m^l\neq 0}}}^{L}N^{l}_{\varepsilon}\frac{4\pi\varepsilon}{r^{l}_{\varepsilon}}\leq 4\pi^{2}+4\pi\varepsilon\sum_{l=1}^{L}|m^{l}||\omega^{l}|N_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{3}{2}}\leq C\,,

where in the last but one inequality we have used (
5.14
) together with the fact that

\inf_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{n=1,\ldots,N_\ep^{\bar l}}{n\neq\bar n}}|x_{\varepsilon}^{\bar{l},n}-x_{\varepsilon}^{\bar{l},\bar{n}}|\geq r_{\varepsilon}^{\bar{l}},\qquad\qquad\inf_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{l=1,\ldots,L}{l\neq\bar l}}\inf_{n=1,\ldots,N_{\varepsilon}^{l}}|x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}-x_{\varepsilon}^{\bar{l},\bar{n}}|\geq r^{\bar{l}}_{\varepsilon},

so that (for
\varepsilon
small enough)
\textrm{dist}(\partial B_{\rho_{\varepsilon}^{n}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{\bar{l},\bar{n}}),x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n})\geq r^{\bar{l}}_{\varepsilon}
.
It follows that

(5.47)

\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{l=1}{m^l\neq 0}}^{L}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\varepsilon}^{l}}\int_{\partial B_{\!\!\rho^{l,n}_{\varepsilon}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n})}|T_{v_{\varepsilon}}|\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}\leq CN_{\varepsilon}=C|\log\varepsilon|\,.

Analogously, by (
5.46
), for every
\bar{l}=1,\ldots,L
with
m^{\bar{l}}\neq 0
, for every
\bar{n}=1,\ldots,N^{\bar{l}}_{\varepsilon}
and for
\varepsilon
small enough we have

\displaystyle|T_{v_{\varepsilon}}|(B_{\!\rho^{\bar{l},\bar{n}}_{\varepsilon}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{\bar{l},\bar{n}}))\leq

\displaystyle 2\pi\int_{B_{\!\!\rho^{\bar{l},\bar{n}}_{\varepsilon}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{\bar{l},\bar{n}})}\frac{1}{|x-x_{\varepsilon}^{\bar{l},\bar{n}}|}\;\mathrm{d}x+2\pi\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{n=1}{n\neq\bar n}}^{N_{\varepsilon}^{\bar{l}}}\int_{B_{\!\!\rho^{\bar{l},\bar{n}}_{\varepsilon}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{\bar{l},\bar{n}})}\frac{1}{|x-x_{\varepsilon}^{\bar{l},n}|}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+2\pi\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{l=1}{\newatop{l\neq\bar l}{m^l\neq 0}}}^{L}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\varepsilon}^{l}}\int_{B_{\!\!\rho^{\bar{l},\bar{n}}_{\varepsilon}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{\bar{l},\bar{n}})}\frac{1}{|x-x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}|}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\varepsilon+CN^{\bar{l}}_{\varepsilon}\frac{\varepsilon}{r^{\bar{l}}_{\varepsilon}}+C\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{l=1}{\newatop{l\neq\bar l}{m^l\neq 0}}}^{L}N^{l}_{\varepsilon}\frac{\varepsilon}{r^{l}_{\varepsilon}}\leq CN_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{3}{2}}\varepsilon\,,

whence we deduce that

(5.48)

\frac{1}{|\log\varepsilon|}{|T_{v_{\varepsilon}}|}\Big(\bigcup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{l=1}{m^l\neq 0}}^{L}\bigcup_{n=1}^{N^{l}_{\varepsilon}}B_{\!\rho_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n})\Big)\to 0\qquad\textrm{as }\varepsilon\to 0\,.

Let finally
\varphi\in C_{\mathrm{c}}^{1}(\Omega)
be such that
\|\varphi\|_{C^{0,1}_{\mathrm{c}}(\Omega)}\leq 1
. By the very definition of distributional Jacobian,
integrating by parts and using that

Ju_{\varepsilon}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\Big(\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{l=1}{m^l\neq 0}}^{L}\bigcup_{n=1}^{N^{l}_{\varepsilon}}B_{\!\rho_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n})\Big)=Jv_{\varepsilon}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\Big(\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{l=1}{m^l\neq 0}}^{L}\bigcup_{n=1}^{N_{\varepsilon}^{l}}B_{\!\rho_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n})\Big)=0,

we obtain

\displaystyle\langle Ju_{\varepsilon},\varphi\rangle_{\Omega}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{l=1}{m^l\neq 0}}^{L}\sum_{n=1}^{N^{l}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{B_{\!\!\rho_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n})}\nabla\varphi\cdot\;\mathrm{d}T_{u_{\varepsilon}}+\int_{\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{l=1}{m^l\neq 0}}^{L}\bigcup_{n=1}^{N^{l}_{\varepsilon}}B_{\!\!\rho_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n})}\nabla\varphi\cdot\;\mathrm{d}T_{u_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{l=1}{m^l\neq 0}}^{L}\sum_{n=1}^{N^{l}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{B_{\!\!\rho_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n})}\nabla\varphi\cdot\;\mathrm{d}T_{u_{\varepsilon}}-\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{l=1}{m^l\neq 0}}^{L}\sum_{n=1}^{N^{l}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\partial B_{\!\!\rho_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n})}\varphi T_{u_{\varepsilon}}\cdot\bm{\nu}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}\,,

and

\displaystyle\langle Jv_{\varepsilon},\varphi\rangle_{\Omega}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{l=1}{m^l\neq 0}}^{L}\sum_{n=1}^{N^{l}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{B_{\!\!\rho_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n})}\nabla\varphi\cdot\;\mathrm{d}T_{v_{\varepsilon}}+\int_{\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{l=1}{m^l\neq 0}}^{L}\bigcup_{n=1}^{N^{l}_{\varepsilon}}B_{\!\!\rho_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n})}\nabla\varphi\cdot\;\mathrm{d}T_{v_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{l=1}{m^l\neq 0}}^{L}\sum_{n=1}^{N^{l}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{B_{\!\!\rho_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n})}\nabla\varphi\cdot\;\mathrm{d}T_{v_{\varepsilon}}-\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{l=1}{m^l\neq 0}}^{L}\sum_{n=1}^{N^{l}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\partial B_{\!\!\rho_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n})}\varphi T_{v_{\varepsilon}}\cdot\bm{\nu}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}\,.

Therefore, by (
5.45
)-(
5.48
), and using that

Ju_{\varepsilon}(B_{\!\rho_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}))=Jv_{\varepsilon}(B_{\!\rho_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n})),\qquad\textrm{for every }l=1,\ldots,L\textrm{ with }m^{l}\neq 0,\,\,n=1,\dots,N_{\varepsilon}^{l},

we obtain

\displaystyle\big|\langle Ju_{\varepsilon}-\pi\mu_{\varepsilon},\varphi\rangle_{\Omega}\big|\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{l=1}{m^l\neq 0}}^{L}\sum_{n=1}^{N^{l}_{\varepsilon}}\Big|\int_{\partial B_{\!\!\rho_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n})}\varphi(T_{u_{\varepsilon}}-T_{v_{\varepsilon}})\cdot\bm{\nu}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}\Big|

\displaystyle+|T_{u_{\varepsilon}}|\Big(\bigcup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{l=1}{m^l\neq 0}}^{L}\bigcup_{n=1}^{N^{l}_{\varepsilon}}B_{\!\rho_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n})\Big)+|T_{v_{\varepsilon}}|\Big(\bigcup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{l=1}{m^l\neq 0}}^{L}\bigcup_{n=1}^{N^{l}_{\varepsilon}}B_{\!\rho_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n})\Big)\,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{l=1}{m^l\neq 0}}^{L}\sum_{n=1}^{N^{l}_{\varepsilon}}\mathrm{osc}_{B_{\!\!\rho_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n})}(\varphi)\int_{\partial B_{\!\!\rho^{l,n}_{\varepsilon}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n})}\big(|T_{u_{\varepsilon}}|+|T_{v_{\varepsilon}}|\big)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}

\displaystyle+|\log\varepsilon|\mathrm{o}(1)\,.

Since
\mathrm{osc}_{B_{\!\!\rho_{\varepsilon}^{l,n}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{l,n})}(\varphi)\leq C\varepsilon
, it follows that

\frac{1}{|\log\varepsilon|}\left\|{Ju_{\varepsilon}}-\pi\mu_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{\mathrm{flat},\Omega}\to 0\qquad\textrm{as }\varepsilon\to 0\,,

whence (
FJ
) follows by Lemma
5.1
(b).

Case 2: General case.

We argue by density, namely we show that for every
(\mu;T^{D})\in(\mathcal{M}(\Omega)\cap H^{-1}(\Omega))\times L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
with
\mathrm{supp}\,\mu\subset\subset\Omega
and

-\mathrm{Div}\,T^{D}=\pi\mu
there exists a sequence
\{(\mu_{k};T^{D}_{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(\mathcal{M}(\Omega)\cap H^{-1}(\Omega))\times L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
with
\mathrm{supp}\,\mu_{k}\subset\subset\Omega
and
-\mathrm{Div}T^{D}_{k}=\pi\mu_{k}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mu_{k}
is locally constant for every
k
(and takes the form as in Step 1), and

(5.49)

\mu_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\mu\,,\qquad|\mu_{k}|(\Omega)\to|\mu|(\Omega)\,,\qquad T^{D}_{k}\to T^{D}\quad\textrm{in }L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})\qquad\textrm{as }k\to+\infty\,.

First, let
\{\rho_{h}\}_{h>0}
be a sequence of standard mollifiers. We define

f_{h}:=\mu\ast\rho_{h}\,,\qquad\mu_{h}:=f_{h}\;\mathrm{d}x\,,\qquad T^{D}_{h}:=(T^{D}\ast\rho_{h})\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\Omega

By construction,
-\mathrm{Div}\,T^{D}_{h}=2\pi\mu_{h}
for every
h>0
and, for
h
small enough,
|\mu_{h}|(\partial\Omega)=0
. Moreover,

\mu_{h}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\mu\,,\qquad|\mu_{h}|(\Omega)\to|\mu|(\Omega)\,,\qquad T^{D}_{h}\to T^{D}\textrm{ in }L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})\,.

Furthermore, since
\{f_{h}\}_{h>0}\subset C^{\infty}(\Omega)
, for every
h>0
, there exists a sequence
\{f_{h}^{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
with
f^{j}_{h}
locally constant as in Step 1, such that

\|f^{j}_{h}-f_{h}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\to 0\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\int_{\Omega}(f^{j}_{h}-f_{h})\;\mathrm{d}x=0\,.

For every
h>0
and
j\in\mathbb{N}
, let
w_{h}^{j}
be the solution to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta w=f^{j}_{h}-f_{h}&\textrm{in }\Omega\\
w=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega\,.\end{array}\right.

By standard elliptic estimates, we have

\|\nabla w^{j}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C\|f^{j}_{h}-f_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})}\,.

Finally, for every
h>0
and for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we set

(T^{D})_{h}^{j}:=T^{D}_{h}+2\pi\nabla w^{j}_{h}
, so that
-\mathrm{Div}\,(T^{D})_{h}^{j}=2\pi\mu_{h}^{j}
, and, for every
h>0
,

(T^{D})_{h}^{j}\to T^{D}_{h}\qquad\textrm{in }L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})\qquad\textrm{(as $j\to+\infty$)}\,.

Using a standard diagonal argument one can find a sequence
\{(\mu_{k};T^{D}_{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying (
5.49
).
Finally, by arguing as in the second step of Case 1, we can construct the recovery sequence for the functional
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}
.
∎

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

The compactness statement follows immediately by (
3.14
) and (
1.10
).

Analogously, the lower bound (
3.15
) is a consequence of (
1.10
) and of the lower semicontinuity of the

norm with respect to the weak convergence.

Therefore, it remains to prove only the upper bound.
The proof is fully analogous to that of Theorem
3.3
(iii) in Subsection
5.2
.
We briefly sketch it.
Let
\beta\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
be such that
\beta^{\perp}=T^{D}
and we set
\mu:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathrm{curl}\;\beta=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathrm{Div}T^{D}
. Then
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)\cap H^{-1}(\Omega)
. Moreover, by construction,
\mathrm{supp}\,\mu\subset\subset\Omega
.

We show how to prove the claim only in the case
\mu=\chi_{\omega}\;\mathrm{d}x
for some
\omega\subset\Omega
with Lipschitz continuous boundary, since the other cases follow by the former by arguing verbatim as in the proof of Theorem
3.3
(iii).
Let

(6.1)

\mu_{\varepsilon}:=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\varepsilon}^{\omega}}\delta_{x_{\varepsilon}^{n}}

be the measure provided by Lemma
5.1
and let
\overline{\beta}_{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
be the field defined in (
5.29
).
By arguing verbatim as in Case 1 (first step) of the proof of Theorem
3.3
(iii), and using that here
N_{\varepsilon}\gg|\log\varepsilon|
, we have that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{N^{2}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Omega}|\widehat{K}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{N_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\varepsilon}}\int_{A_{\varepsilon,r_{\varepsilon}}(x_{\varepsilon}^{n})}|\widehat{K}_{\varepsilon}|^{n}\;\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\log r_{\varepsilon}+|\log\varepsilon|}{N_{\varepsilon}}=0\,;

analogously, by arguing as in (

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{N^{2}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Omega}|\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{N^{2}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x=0\,.

Therefore,

(6.2)

\frac{\overline{\beta}_{\varepsilon}}{N_{\varepsilon}}\to 0\qquad\textrm{strongly in }L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})\,.

Finally, defining the sequence

as in (

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{N_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\beta|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|T^{D}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\,,
