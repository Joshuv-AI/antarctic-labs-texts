Hardy decomposition of higher order Lipschitz classes by polymonogenic functions
Lianet De la Cruz Toranzo, Ricardo Abreu Blaya, Swanhild Bernstein

Abstract
In this paper we find a decomposition of higher order Lipschitz functions into the traces of a polymonogenic function and solve a related Riemann-Hilbert problem. Our approach lies in using a cliffordian Cauchy-type operator, which behaves as an involution operator on higher order Lipschitz spaces. The result obtained is a multidimensional sharpened version of the Hardy decomposition of Hölder continuous functions on a simple closed curve in the complex plane.

Introduction

The space of Hölder functions of exponent
0<\alpha\leq 1
defined on a set of the complex plane, has numerous properties and plays an important role in the theory of analytic functions. For instance, it makes sense to speak of a singular version of the Cauchy transform. Actually, by the Plemelj-Sokhotski’s formulae, the boundary value problem on finding a sectionally analytic function vanishing at infinity and with jump on a closed smooth curve equals to a given Hölder function
f
, is solved by the Cauchy transform of
f

[
13
]
. This result leads to the Hardy decomposition of the Hölder class as the sum of two disjoint Hölder spaces, which are characterized for the limiting values of the Cauchy transform as approaching the curve from the interior and the exterior domain.
When
\alpha=1
, the Hölder condition becomes the well-known Lipschitz condition, which has been widely used in the theory of differential equations, whereas when
\alpha>1
the class of Hölder is constituted by constants only. However, a non-trivial space emerges under that condition if we consider the higher order Lipschitz functions. In
[
2
]
the authors found a decomposition of the Lipschitz class in the context of polyanalytic functions
[
4
]
, that is, null solutions of the iterated Cauchy-Riemann equation. When looked at from a more general point of view, the null solutions to the iterated Dirac operator in Clifford analysis (see for instance,
[
5
]
and
[
9
]
), leads to polymonogenic functions and therefore a natural question to ask is whether such a decomposition continues to hold in this multidimensional setting. Very recently in
[
8
]
, while attempting to prove it, the authors obtained a decomposition of the first order Lipschitz class into traces of bimonogenic functions, which are nothing more than Clifford algebra-valued harmonic functions as the Dirac operator factorize the Laplacian. In this paper we bring together the methods carried out in the above mentioned works and prove a general decomposition of higher order Lipschitz classes into Clifford algebra-valued polymonogenic functions.
The organization of the paper is the following. In Section
2
we present some preliminaries about real and Clifford analysis. In Section
3
we summarize without proofs the relevant material on the Cauchy type integral operator and its singular version related to higher order Lipschitz functions and we extend Theorem 3 of
[
1
]
to the general order with the aid of a multi-index formula for the iterated Dirac operator. In Section
4
we state and prove our main results. Finally, we also solve a Riemann Hilbert problem for polymonogenic functions with given Lipschitz data.

Definitions and related results

An
m
-tuple
j=(j_{1},\dots,j_{m})
with non-negative integer components

for every

is called multi-index. Given a multi-index

we recall:
x^{j}=x_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots x_{m}^{j_{m}}
,
j!=j_{1}!\cdots j_{m}!
,
|j|=j_{1}+\cdots+j_{m}
and
\partial^{j}=\partial^{j_{1}}_{x_{1}}\dots\partial^{j_{m}}_{x_{m}}.
The reader is cautioned that, throughout this work, the letters
j
,
l
,
p
and
q
will be reserved for multi-indexes, while the letters
i
,
s
,
u
and
v
are reserved for indexes of summation.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
E
be a closed subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
k
a non-negative integer and
0<\alpha\leq 1
. We shall say that a real valued function
f
, defined on
E
, belongs to the higher order Lipschitz class
{\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha,\textbf{E})
if there exist real valued functions
f^{(j)}
,
0<|j|\leq k
, defined on
E
, with
f^{(0)}=f
, which together with

R_{j}(x,y)=f^{(j)}(x)-\sum_{|j+l|\leq k}\frac{f^{(j+l)}(y)}{l!}(x-y)^{l},\quad x,y\in\textbf{E}

(1)

satisfy

|f^{(j)}(x)|\leq M,\quad|R_{j}(x,y)|\leq M|x-y|^{k+\alpha-|j|},\quad x,y\in\textbf{E},|j|\leq k,

(2)

where here and subsequently
M
is a positive constant not necessary the same at different occurrences.

Remark 2.1
.

We observe that the function
f^{(0)}
does not necessarily determine the elements
f^{(j)}
(see
(
15
, p. 176)
). Therefore, an element of
{\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha,\textbf{E})
should be interpreted as a collection
f=\{f^{(j)}:\textbf{E}\mapsto\mathbb{R},\quad|j|\leq k\}
. If no confusion can arise, we write
f
for both the collection and the element
f^{(0)}
. We use superscripts to distinguish between individual elements and sub-collections. In this way, for all
|j|\leq k
, the data
f_{(j)}:=\{f^{(j+l)},\quad 0\leq|l|\leq k-|j|\}
belongs to
{\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha-|j|,\textbf{E})
. In particular,
f_{(j)}=\{f^{(j)},\quad|j|=k\}
belongs to the Hölder class
\textbf{C}^{\alpha}(\textbf{E})
. When
\textbf{E}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
, however, the functions
f^{(j)}
are uniquely determined by
f
and
{\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha,\mathbb{R}^{m})
actually consists of continuous and bounded functions
f
with continuous and bounded partial derivatives
\partial^{j}f=f^{(j)}
up to the order
k
. In what follows, we shall concern ourselves only with case
0<\alpha<1.

The higher order Lipschitz class introduced by E. Stein in
[
15
]
appears to be the most appropriate class of functions to describe properties in terms of Banach spaces of certain linear extension operators related to Whitney-type problems on extensions of real functions defined on closed sets of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. For the convenience of the reader, we include the Whitney extension theorem
[
16
]
as it will be a key point to define our multidimensional singular integral operator later.

Theorem 2.2
.

(Whitney’s theorem)
Let
f\in{\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha,\textbf{E})
. Then, there exists a function
\tilde{f}\in{\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha,\mathbb{R}^{m})
satisfying

(i)

\tilde{f}|_{\textbf{E}}=f^{(0)},\quad\partial^{j}\tilde{f}|_{\textbf{E}}=f^{(j)}
,
0<|j|\leq k
,

(ii)

\tilde{f}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\textbf{E})
,

(iii)

|\partial^{j}\tilde{f}(x)|\leqslant M\quad\mbox{dist}(x,\textbf{E})^{\alpha-1}
, for
|j|=k+1
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\textbf{E}
.

Actually, the linear extension operator

{\mathcal{E}}_{k}(f^{(j)})(x)=\begin{cases}f^{(0)}(x),&x\in\textbf{E}\\
{\sum\limits_{i}}^{\prime}P(x,p_{i})\varphi_{i}^{*}(x),&x\in\textbf{E}^{c},\end{cases}

where
\sum_{i}\varphi_{i}^{*}=1
and
P(x,y)
denotes the Taylor expansion of
f
about
y
, maps
{\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha,\textbf{E})
continuously into
{\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha,\mathbb{R}^{m})
and the norm is independent of the closed set
E
. For a deeper discussion we refer the reader to
(
15
, Ch. VI)
.

Although the preceding definition involves real-valued functions, we now introduce the algebras we shall be concerned with.

Definition 2.2
.

Let us consider an orthonormal basis
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
governed by the multiplication rules:
e_{i}^{2}=-1
,
e_{i}e_{j}=-e_{j}e_{i},i\neq j
for every
i,j=1,\dots,m.
The real Clifford algebra
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
is that generated by
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
over
\mathbb{R}
.

An element
a\in\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
may be written as
a=\sum_{A}a_{A}e_{A},
where
a_{A}\in\mathbb{R}
and
A
runs over all the possible ordered sets
A=\{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq m\}
or
A=\emptyset
and
e_{A}:=e_{i_{1}}e_{i_{2}}\cdots e_{i_{k}},\quad e_{0}=e_{\emptyset}=1.
It is clear that
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is embedded in
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
, provided we identify
\underline{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
by
\underline{x}=x_{1}e_{1}+\dots+x_{m}e_{m};\quad x_{i}\in\mathbb{R},i=\overline{1,m}
. A norm for
a\in\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
may be defined by
\|a\|^{2}=\sum_{A}|a_{A}|^{2}.
In particular,
\|\underline{x}\|=|\underline{x}|
for
\underline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
|\cdot|
stands for the Euclidean norm.

We shall consider Clifford algebra-valued functions defined on subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, namely
f=\sum_{A}f_{A}e_{A}
,
f_{A}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
. Conditions as continuity, differentiability, Lipschitz and so on, are ascribed to an
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
-valued function by doing so to each of its real components
f_{A}
. From now on,
\Omega
stands for a Jordan domain, i.e. a bounded oriented connected open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
whose boundary
\Gamma
is a compact topological surface. For simplicity, we assume
\Gamma
to be sufficiently smooth, e.g. Lyapunov surface. In addition, we denote the interior of
\Omega
by
\Omega_{+}
and the exterior by
\Omega_{-}
.

Definition 2.3
.

An
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
-valued function
f
in
\textbf{C}(\Omega)
is called left (resp. right) monogenic if
{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{x}}\,f=0
(resp.
f\,{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{x}}=0
) in
\Omega
, where
{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{x}}
is the Dirac operator

{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{x}}={\partial_{x_{1}}}e_{1}+{\partial_{x_{2}}}e_{2}+\cdots+{\partial_{x_{m}}}e_{m}.

The so-called Clifford-Cauchy kernel

E_{0}(\underline{x})=-\frac{1}{\sigma_{m}}\frac{\underline{x}}{|\underline{x}|^{m}}\quad(\underline{x}\neq 0),

is a two-sided monogenic function. Here
\sigma_{m}
stands for the surface area of the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Definition 2.4
.

An
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
-valued function
f
in
\textbf{C}^{k}(\Omega)
is called (left) polymonogenic of order
k
or simply
k
-monogenic if
{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{x}}^{k}\,f=0
in
\Omega
, where
{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{x}}^{k}
is the iterated Dirac operator.

Every polymonogenic function can be represented in terms of its values and those of its successive derivatives on the boundary
(
14
, Thm 7)
. Namely, if
f
is
k
-monogenic in
\Omega
, then

f(\underline{x})=\sum_{u=0}^{k-1}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{u}E_{u}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{u}f(\underline{y})d\underline{y},x\in\Omega,

(3)

where the
E_{u}
’s satisfy
\partial_{\underline{x}}E_{u+1}(\underline{x})=E_{u}(\underline{x})
for all
\underline{x}\not=0
and
u=0,\ldots,k-1
.

3
Cauchy-type and singular integral operators

Formula (
3
) and Whitney extension theorem (component-wise applied to
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
-valued functions) can be combined to give the following operators, which have been shown to be well-defined
[
7
]
:

Definition 3.1
.

The Cauchy transform related to
f\in{\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha,\Gamma)
and its singular version are given respectively by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{(0)}f=[{\mathcal{C}}_{k}f]^{(0)}(\underline{x})=\sum\limits_{s=0}^{k}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{s}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{s}\tilde{f}(\underline{y})d\underline{y},\quad\underline{x}\in\Omega\setminus\Gamma,

(4)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{k}^{(0)}f=[{\mathcal{S}}_{k}f]^{(0)}(\underline{z})=2\sum\limits_{s=0}^{k}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{s}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{s}\tilde{f}(\underline{y})d\underline{y},\quad\underline{z}\in\Gamma,

(5)

where
\tilde{f}
denotes the
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
-valued Whitney extension of
f\in{\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha,\Gamma)
.

We see at once that our definition coincides with the classical one related to a Hölder function
f\in\textbf{C}^{\alpha}(\Gamma)
. Namely,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}_{0}f(\underline{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})f(\underline{y})d\underline{y},\quad\underline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\Gamma,

(6)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{0}f(\underline{z})

\displaystyle=2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})f(\underline{y})d\underline{y},\quad\underline{z}\in\Gamma.

(7)

The Cauchy transform (
6
) represents a monogenic function in
\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\Gamma
with continuous limiting values as approaching the contour
\Gamma
. Actually, the Plemelj-Sokhotski formula
[
11
]
gives an even more precise information:

[{\mathcal{C}}_{0}f]^{+}=\frac{1}{2}[I+{\mathcal{S}}_{0}]f\quad\mbox{and }\quad[{\mathcal{C}}_{0}f]^{-}=\frac{1}{2}[-I+{\mathcal{S}}_{0}]f,

(8)

where
I
is the identity operator and
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\pm}f(\underline{z})=\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\underline{x}\to\underline{z}\\
\underline{x}\in\Omega_{\pm}\end{subarray}}{\mathcal{C}}_{0}f(\underline{x}).

When looked at from a general point of view, the preceding fact suggests that identical conclusions hold with respect to
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{(0)}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{k}^{(0)}
. In fact, as it was pointed out in
[
7
]
, the function

is polymonogenic of order

\begin{cases}[{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{(0)}f]^{+}(\underline{z})-[{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{(0)}f]^{-}(\underline{z})=\tilde{f}\mid_{\Gamma}=f^{(0)}(\underline{z}),&\quad\underline{z}\in\Gamma,\\

or, equivalently,

[{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{(0)}f]^{+}=\frac{1}{2}[I^{(0)}+{\mathcal{S}}_{k}^{(0)}]f\quad\mbox{and }\quad[{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{(0)}f]^{-}=\frac{1}{2}[-I^{(0)}+{\mathcal{S}}_{k}^{(0)}]f.

(9)

Here
I^{(0)}f=f^{(0)}
and, as usual,
[{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{(0)}f]^{\pm}(\underline{z})=\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\underline{x}\to\underline{z}\\
\underline{x}\in\Omega_{\pm}\end{subarray}}{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{(0)}f(\underline{x}).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{x}}^{s}=c_{s}\begin{cases}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j|=s\\

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j+l|=s\\
j_{i}-even\\
|l|=1\end{subarray}}e_{(l)}\partial^{(j+l)}_{\underline{x}},&\quad s\quad\emph{odd},\end{cases}

(10)

where

if

if

Proof.

The proof is by induction on

. For

,
{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{x}}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}e_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}=\sum\limits_{|l|=1}e_{(l)}\partial_{\underline{x}}^{(l)}
.
Setting

Assuming (

) to hold for

, we will prove it for
s+1
. If
s+1
is odd,

is even and then, by the induction hypothesis,
{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{x}}^{s}=c_{s}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j|=s\\
j_{i}-even\end{subarray}}\partial^{(j)}_{\underline{x}}
being
s=4p
or

. After applying the Dirac operator one more time, we get

{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{x}}({\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{x}}^{s})={\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{x}}^{s+1}=\sum\limits_{|l|=1}e_{(l)}\partial^{(l)}_{\underline{x}}\Big(c_{s}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j|=s\\
j_{i}-even\end{subarray}}\partial^{(j)}_{\underline{x}}\Big)=c_{s}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j+l|=s+1\\
j_{i}-even\\
|l|=1\end{subarray}}e_{(l)}\partial^{(j+l)}_{\underline{x}}.

Since

when

is even, (
10
) holds for
s+1

We now suppose that
s+1
is even, so that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{x}}^{s+1}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{|q|=1}e_{(q)}\partial^{(q)}_{\underline{x}}\Big(c_{s}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j+l|=s\\
j_{i}-even\\
|l|=1\end{subarray}}e_{(l)}\partial^{(j+l)}_{\underline{x}}\Big)=c_{s}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j+l+q|=s+1\\
j_{i}-even\\
|l|=1,|q|=1\end{subarray}}e_{(q)}e_{(l)}\partial^{(j+l+q)}_{\underline{x}}

\displaystyle=-c_{s}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j+l+l|=s+1\\
j_{i}-even\\

the last equality being a consequence of the fact that

Since

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j+l|=s\\

for all
|l|\leq k
. To complete the proof, similar considerations apply to the functions
G_{i}=f^{(0,\ldots,1,\ldots,0)}
, where the multi-index has all entries zero except the
i
th which is one. Consequently,
f^{(j)}=0
for all
|j|\leq k+1.

∎

For completeness of exposition, we conclude this section with the relevant material from
[
7
]
. Let us first introduce the notation
E_{u}^{(j)}(\underline{x})=\partial_{\underline{x}}^{j}E_{u}(\underline{x}),\quad\underline{x}\not=0
. We define

{\mathcal{S}}_{k}^{(j)}f(\underline{z})=2\sum\limits_{u=0}^{k}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{u}E_{u}^{(j)}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{u}R(\underline{y},\underline{z})d\underline{y}+f^{(j)}(\underline{z})

to be the associated elements of the collection
{\mathcal{S}}_{k}f
. Although the definition may seem artificial, it is actually very much in the spirit of the classical case. Indeed, when
k=0
it reduces to

{\mathcal{S}}_{0}f(\underline{z})=2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})[f(\underline{y})-f(\underline{z})]d\underline{y}+f(\underline{z}),

which is nothing more than a reformulation of (
7
).

The Plemelj-Privalov theorem in this framework asserts that, for
0<\alpha<1
the inclusion
{\mathcal{S}}_{k}({\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha,\Gamma))\subset{\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha,\Gamma)
holds, and thus, makes it legitimate to ask whether the operator is an involution. Whereas for a single function
f\in\textbf{C}^{\alpha}(\Gamma)
the equality
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}^{2}=I
makes sense, it is somewhat misleading for a Lipschitz data. The involution, to be discussed in Section
4
, must thus be approached by considering every element, i.e.
[{\mathcal{S}}_{k}^{2}f]^{(j)}=f^{(j)}
for all
|j|\leq k
.

4
Involution property and consequences

This section is devoted to establishing and proving our main result, but first we need a key lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

For
s
even (resp. odd), the sum

c_{s}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j|=s\geq 2\\
j_{i}-even\end{subarray}}[{\mathcal{S}}_{k}f]^{(j)}(\underline{y})\Big(\mbox{resp.}c_{s}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j+l|=s\\
j_{i}-even,|l|=1\end{subarray}}e_{(l)}[{\mathcal{S}}_{k}f]^{(j+l)}(\underline{y})\Big)

is equal to

2\sum\limits_{u=1}^{k}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{u}{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{s}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{u}\tilde{f}(\underline{\zeta})d\underline{\zeta}

(14)

or equivalently, to

2\sum\limits_{u=0}^{k-s}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{u}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{u+s}\tilde{f}(\underline{\zeta})d\underline{\zeta}.

(15)

Proof.

Indeed, if
s
is even

\displaystyle c_{s}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j|=s\geq 2\\
j_{i}-even\end{subarray}}[{\mathcal{S}}_{k}f]^{(j)}(\underline{y})

\displaystyle=c_{s}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j|=s\geq 2\\
j_{i}-even\end{subarray}}\Bigl\{2\sum\limits_{u=0}^{k}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{u}E_{u}^{(j)}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{u}R(\underline{\zeta},\underline{y})d\underline{\zeta}+f^{(j)}(\underline{y})\Bigr\}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{u=1}^{k}2\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{u}{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{s}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{u}R(\underline{\zeta},\underline{y})d\underline{\zeta}+c_{s}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j|=s\geq 2\\
j_{i}-even\end{subarray}}f^{(j)}(\underline{y}).

(16)

With Theorem
2.2
in mind, we get

\tilde{f}(\underline{\zeta})=\tilde{f}(\underline{y})+\sum\limits_{0<|l|\leq k}\frac{\partial_{\underline{y}}^{(l)}\tilde{f}(\underline{y})}{l!}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})^{l}+R(\underline{\zeta},\underline{y}),\quad\underline{y},\underline{\zeta}\in\Gamma.

Therefore,

{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{u}R(\underline{\zeta},\underline{y})={\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{u}\tilde{f}(\underline{\zeta})-{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{u}\tilde{f}(\underline{y})-P_{u}[f](\underline{\zeta},\underline{y}),\quad 0\leq u\leq k,

(17)

where

\displaystyle P_{u}[f](\underline{\zeta},\underline{y})=

\displaystyle c_{u}\begin{cases}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|p|=u\\
p_{i}-even\end{subarray}}\Bigl\{\sum\limits_{|p|<|l|\leq k}\dfrac{\partial_{\underline{y}}^{(l)}\tilde{f}(\underline{y})}{(l-p)!}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})^{l-p}\Bigl\},&u\,even,\\
\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|p+q|=u\\
p_{i}-even,|q|=1\end{subarray}}e_{(q)}\Bigl\{\sum\limits_{|p+q|<|l|\leq k}\dfrac{\partial_{\underline{y}}^{(l)}\tilde{f}(\underline{y})}{(l-(p+q))!}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})^{l-(p+q)}\Bigl\},&u\,odd.\end{cases}

(18)

Let us note that, by definition,
P_{k}[f](\underline{\zeta},\underline{y})=0
. In this way, (
17
) becomes

{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{k}R(\underline{\zeta},\underline{y})={\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{k}\tilde{f}(\underline{\zeta})-{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{k}\tilde{f}(\underline{y}),u=k.

On the other hand, it is a matter of straightforward computation to show that

{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{u}P_{s}[f](\underline{\zeta},\underline{y})=P_{u+s}[f](\underline{\zeta},\underline{y})+{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{u+s}\tilde{f}(\underline{y}),\quad\forall u=1,\ldots,k-s.

(19)

Note that, for
u=k-s
, (
19
) becomes

{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{k-s}P_{s}[f](\underline{\zeta},\underline{y})={\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{k}\tilde{f}(\underline{y}).

(20)

Now, substituting (
17
) into (
16
) gives

\displaystyle c_{s}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j|=s\geq 2\\
j_{i}-even\end{subarray}}[{\mathcal{S}}_{k}f]^{(j)}(\underline{y})

\displaystyle=2\sum\limits_{u=1}^{k}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{u}{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{s}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{u}\tilde{f}(\underline{\zeta})d\underline{\zeta}

\displaystyle-2\sum\limits_{u=1}^{k}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{u}{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{s}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{u}\tilde{f}(\underline{y})d\underline{\zeta}

\displaystyle-2\sum\limits_{u=1}^{k-1}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{u}{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{s}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta})P_{u}[f](\underline{\zeta},\underline{y})d\underline{\zeta}

\displaystyle+c_{s}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j|=s\geq 2\\
j_{i}-even\end{subarray}}f^{(j)}(\underline{y}).

(21)

Consequently, it suffices to show that the sum, say
``K"
, of the last three terms in (
21
) vanishes.

By the kernel’s rules

\displaystyle K(\underline{y})

\displaystyle=-2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{s}\tilde{f}(\underline{y})d\underline{\zeta}

\displaystyle-2\sum\limits_{u=1}^{k-s}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{u}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta})\Big[{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{u+s}\tilde{f}(\underline{y})+P_{u+s}[f](\underline{\zeta},\underline{y})\Big]d\underline{\zeta}

\displaystyle-2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta})P_{s}[f](\underline{\zeta},\underline{y})d\underline{\zeta}

\displaystyle+c_{s}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j|=s\geq 2\\
j_{i}-even\end{subarray}}f^{(j)}(\underline{y}).

By (
19
), (
20
), Theorem
2.2
and the fact that
\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta})d\underline{\zeta}=\frac{1}{2}
, we get

K(\underline{y})=-2\sum\limits_{u=0}^{k-s}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{u}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{u}P_{s}[f](\underline{\zeta},\underline{y})d\underline{\zeta}.

Set
F(\underline{\zeta})=P_{s}[f](\underline{\zeta},\underline{y})
and for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small let
B_{\epsilon}(\underline{y})
be the open ball with center in
\underline{y}\in\Gamma
and radius
\epsilon
,
C_{\epsilon}(\underline{y})
its boundary and
\Gamma_{\epsilon}:=\Gamma\setminus\Gamma\cap B_{\epsilon}(\underline{y})
. We thus get

p.v.\int_{\Gamma}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{u}F(\underline{\zeta})d\underline{\zeta}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{u}F(\underline{\zeta})d\underline{\zeta}.

Denote by
\Omega_{*}
the interior of
\Gamma^{*}:=\Gamma_{\epsilon}\cup C_{\epsilon}^{*}(\underline{y})
, where
C_{\epsilon}^{*}=C_{\epsilon}\cap\Omega_{+}
. Then
F(\underline{\zeta})
is
(k-s+1)
-monogenic in
\Omega_{*}
and continuous on
\Gamma^{*}\cup\overline{\Omega}_{*}
so that the Cauchy formula (applied in
\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{*}
) yields

\sum\limits_{u=0}^{k-s}\int_{\Gamma^{*}}(-1)^{u}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{u}P_{s}[f](\underline{\zeta},\underline{y})d\underline{\zeta}=0.

We are left with the task of proving

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}\Bigg\{\sum\limits_{u=0}^{k-s}\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{y})}(-1)^{u}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{u}F(\underline{\zeta})d\underline{\zeta}\Bigg\}=0.

By (
18
),
|P_{s}[f](\underline{\zeta},\underline{y})|\leq M\sum\limits_{0<|j|\leq k-s}|\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y}|^{|j|}
. Since
|j|\geq 1
, it holds for
|\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y}|
sufficiently small that
|\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y}|^{|j|}\leq|\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y}|
. Therefore, for
u=0

\Big|\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{y})}E_{0}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta})P_{s}[f](\underline{\zeta},\underline{y})d\underline{\zeta}\Big|\leq M\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{y})}\frac{|\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y}|}{|\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y}|^{m-1}}d\underline{\zeta}\leq M\epsilon\to 0\quad(\epsilon\to 0).

In a similar way, for
u\geq 1

\Big|\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{y})}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta})P_{u+s}[f](\underline{\zeta})d\underline{\zeta}\Big|\leq M\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{y})}\frac{|\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y}|}{|\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y}|^{m-u-1}}d\underline{\zeta}\leq M\epsilon^{u+1}

and

\Big|\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{y})}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{u+s}\tilde{f}(\underline{y})d\underline{\zeta}\Big|\leq M\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{y})}\frac{d\underline{\zeta}}{|\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y}|^{m-u-1}}\leq M\epsilon^{u}

tend to zero as
\epsilon
tends to
0
.

Then for
1\leq u\leq k-s
we have by (
19
) that

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{y})}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{u}F(\underline{\zeta})d\underline{\zeta}\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{y})}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta})[P_{u+s}[f](\underline{\zeta})+{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{u+s}\tilde{f}(\underline{y})]d\underline{\zeta}\Big|\to 0\quad(\epsilon\to 0).

Hence
K=0
as claimed. In the same manner we can see that (
14
) holds for
s
odd.
∎

We are now in a position to state our main result, which ensures that the singular integral operator behaves as an involution on the higher order Lipschitz class.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
f\in{\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha,\Gamma)
. Then
{\mathcal{S}}_{k}^{2}=I
, where
I
is the identity operator. That is,

[{\mathcal{S}}_{k}^{2}f]^{(j)}=f^{(j)}\quad\forall\quad|j|\leq k.

(22)

Proof.

Before going to the proof, we briefly outline our strategy. We first show (
22
) for
j=0
and then we take advantage of Theorem
3.2
to show (
22
) for all
|j|\leq k.

Let
j=0
, then

\displaystyle[{\mathcal{S}}_{k}^{2}f]^{(0)}(\underline{z})

\displaystyle=2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\widetilde{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}f}(\underline{y})d\underline{y}

\displaystyle+2\sum\limits_{2\leq s-even}\int_{\Gamma}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})c_{s}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j|=s\\
j_{i}-even\end{subarray}}\partial^{(j)}_{\underline{y}}\widetilde{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}f}(\underline{y})d\underline{y}+

\displaystyle+2\sum\limits_{s-odd}\int_{\Gamma}-E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})c_{s}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j+l|=s\\
j_{i}-even\\
|l|=1\end{subarray}}e_{(l)}\partial^{(j+l)}_{\underline{y}}\widetilde{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}f}(\underline{y})d\underline{y}.

Combining the fact that
\partial_{\underline{y}}^{(j)}\widetilde{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}f}\mid_{\Gamma}=[{\mathcal{S}}_{k}f]^{(j)}
with Lemma
4.1
we get

\displaystyle[{\mathcal{S}}_{k}^{2}f]^{(0)}(\underline{z})=2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\Big[2\sum\limits_{u=0}^{k}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{u}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{u}\tilde{f}(\underline{\zeta})d\underline{\zeta}\Big]d\underline{y}

\displaystyle+2\sum\limits_{s=1}^{k}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{s}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\Bigg\{2\sum\limits_{u=1}^{k}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{u}{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{s}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{u}\tilde{f}(\underline{\zeta})d\underline{\zeta}\Bigg\}d\underline{y}.

The involution property for the classical singular operator yields

2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\Big[2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta})\tilde{f}(\underline{\zeta})d\underline{\zeta}\Big]d\underline{y}=f^{(0)}(\underline{z}).

Therefore, by (
10
) and Theorem
2.2
, we are reduce to proving that

2\sum\limits_{s=0}^{k}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{s}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\Big[2\sum\limits_{u=1}^{k}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{u}{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{s}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{u}\tilde{f}(\underline{\zeta})d\underline{\zeta}\Big]d\underline{y}=0.

By Fubini’s theorem this equation can be rewritten in the form:

4\sum\limits_{s=0}^{k}(-1)^{s}\sum\limits_{u=1}^{k}(-1)^{u}\int_{\Gamma}\Big[\int_{\Gamma}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{s}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})d\underline{y}\Big]n(\underline{\zeta}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{\zeta}}^{u}\tilde{f}(\underline{\zeta})d\underline{\zeta}=0.

(23)

It will thus be sufficient to show that

Q(\underline{z}):=\sum\limits_{s=0}^{k}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{s}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{s}F(\underline{y})d\underline{y}=0,

(24)

where
F(\underline{y}):=\sum\limits_{u=1}^{k}(-1)^{u}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})
.

The function
Q
can be handled in much the same way as
K
in the proof of Lemma
4.1
, the main difference being in the definition of
\Gamma^{*}
and, hence, the estimations. We choose
\epsilon>0
so small that the balls
B_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})
and
B_{\epsilon}(\underline{\zeta})
are disjoint. Set
\Gamma_{\epsilon}:=\Gamma\setminus\Gamma\cap\big(B_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})\cup B_{\epsilon}(\underline{\zeta})\big)
so that

p.v.\int_{\Gamma}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{s}F(\underline{y})d\underline{y}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{s}F(\underline{y})d\underline{y}.

Denote by
\Omega_{*}
the interior of
\Gamma^{*}:=\Gamma_{\epsilon}\cup C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})\cup C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{\zeta})
. The last two sets being the intersections of
\Omega
and the boundaries of those above-mentioned balls respectively. For abbreviation, we set
C_{\epsilon}^{*}=C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})\cup C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{\zeta})
. Then
F(\underline{y})
is
(k+1)
-monogenic in
\Omega_{*}
and continuous on
\Gamma^{*}\cup\overline{\Omega}_{*}
. By the Cauchy formula (in
\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{*}
) it will be sufficient to prove that

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}\Bigg\{\sum\limits_{s=0}^{k}\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}}(-1)^{s}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{s}F(\underline{y})d\underline{y}\Bigg\}=0.

(25)

Since
{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{s}F(\underline{y})=\sum\limits_{u=s}^{k}(-1)^{u+s}E_{u-s}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})=\sum\limits_{u=0}^{k-s}(-1)^{u}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})
for
s\geq 1
, the sum of integrals in (
25
) becomes

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{s=0}^{k}\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})\cup C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{\zeta})}(-1)^{s}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{s}F(\underline{y})d\underline{y}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{s=1}^{k}(-1)^{s}\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})E_{s}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})d\underline{y}

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{s=1}^{k}(-1)^{s}\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})E_{0}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})d\underline{y}

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{s=1}^{k-1}(-1)^{s}\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\sum\limits_{u=1}^{k-s}(-1)^{u}E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})d\underline{y}.

(26)

First, we note that the last term of (
26
) splits into the pair of sums

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{s=1}^{k-1}\sum\limits_{u=1}^{k-s}\int a_{su}=\sum\limits_{u=1}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}\int a_{uu}+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}s,u=1\\
s\neq u\end{subarray}}^{k-1}\int(a_{su}+a_{us}),k>1.

(27)

We divide the second sum in (
27
) into three cases depending on the parity: when both
s
and
u
are even (
s,u\geq 2
), when both
s
and
u
are odd and, finally, when
u
is even (
u\geq 2
) and
s
is odd. We only show the first one since similar arguments apply to the others.

\displaystyle\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}}\Big[E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})E_{u}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})+E_{u}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})E_{s}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})\Big]d\underline{y}

\displaystyle=M\left\{\frac{1}{\epsilon^{m-s-1}}\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})}\frac{\underline{y}-\underline{\zeta}}{|\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y}|^{m-u}}d\underline{y}+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{m-u-1}}\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{\zeta})}\frac{\underline{y}-\underline{z}}{|\underline{y}-\underline{z}|^{m-s}}d\underline{y}\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{1}{\epsilon^{m-u-1}}\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})}\frac{\underline{y}-\underline{\zeta}}{|\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y}|^{m-s}}d\underline{y}+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{m-s-1}}\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{\zeta})}\frac{\underline{y}-\underline{z}}{|\underline{y}-\underline{z}|^{m-u}}d\underline{y}\right\},

(28)

where
M
is a constant dependent on
s,u
and
m
throughout
[
14
]
. After the change of variable
\underline{y}=-\underline{t}+\underline{z}+\underline{\zeta}
, we get that the first and fourth integral in (
28
) are equal, and thus we are reduced to proving that its limit is zero as
\epsilon\to 0
. This observation applies to the second and third integral as well. It is clear that
h(\underline{y}):=\frac{\underline{y}-\underline{\zeta}}{|\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y}|^{m-u}}
is continuous on
C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})
and thus bounded, which yields

\Big|\frac{1}{\epsilon^{m-s-1}}\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})}\frac{\underline{y}-\underline{\zeta}}{|\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y}|^{m-u}}d\underline{y}\Big|\leq\frac{N}{\epsilon^{m-s-1}}\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})}d\underline{y}=N^{\prime}\epsilon^{s}\to 0\quad(\epsilon\to 0).

Hence the second sum in (
27
) tends to zero as
\epsilon\to 0
, and so does the first one.

Finally, we note that the estimations of those integrals in the first two sums of (
26
) whenever
s
is odd and
u=0
hold from the third case above-mentioned when we drop the assumption
u\geq 2
. It remains the case when
s
is even (and
u=0
). That is,

\displaystyle\int_{C_{\epsilon}^{*}}\Big[E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})E_{s}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})+E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})E_{0}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})\Big]d\underline{y}

\displaystyle=\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})E_{s}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})d\underline{y}+\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{\zeta})}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})E_{s}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})d\underline{y}

\displaystyle+\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})E_{0}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})d\underline{y}+\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{\zeta})}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})E_{0}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})d\underline{y}.

The first and fourth integral above are the left and right Cauchy transform in
\underline{y}=\underline{z}
and
\underline{y}=\underline{\zeta}
respectively of the functions
E_{s}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})
and
-E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})
, which are continuous in
B^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})
and
B^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{\zeta})
respectively. Letting

yields

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}\Bigg\{\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})E_{s}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})d\underline{y}+\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{\zeta})}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})E_{0}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})d\underline{y}\Bigg\}=0.

For the second and third integral, we proceed as in the first case and so
Q=0
as desired.

We are now ready to proceed to the final stage of our proof. From Theorem
3.2
, we only need to show that

(A)

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j|=s\\
j_{i}-even\end{subarray}}[{\mathcal{S}}_{k}^{2}f]^{(j)}-\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j|=s\\
j_{i}-even\end{subarray}}f^{(j)}=0
if
s
is even.

(B)

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j+l|=s\\
j_{i}-even\\
|l|=1\end{subarray}}e_{(l)}[{\mathcal{S}}_{k}^{2}f]^{(j+l)}-\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j+l|=s\\
j_{i}-even\\
|l|=1\end{subarray}}e_{(l)}f^{(j+l)}=0
if
s
is odd.

Let us first prove
A
. After applying Lemma
4.1
twice and having in mind Lemma
3.1
and Theorem
2.2

\displaystyle c_{s}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|j|=s\\

For a characterization of the space

Assume

-monogenic in
\Omega_{-}
,
f(\infty)

for every

. Consider the ball
B_{R}(\underline{x})
with center in
\underline{x}\in\Omega_{-}
and radius

sufficiently large such that

. Then, if (
3

Since

\displaystyle\int_{C_{R}(\underline{x})}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})f(\underline{y})d\underline{y}=\int_{C_{R}(\underline{x})}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})[f(\underline{y})-f(\infty)]d\underline{y}

\displaystyle+\Big(\int_{C_{R}(\underline{x})}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})d\underline{y}\Big)f(\infty),

implies that

\int_{C_{R}(\underline{x})}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})f(\underline{y})d\underline{y}\to f(\infty),\quad\mbox{as}\quad\mathbb{R}\to\infty.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle|\underline{y}-\underline{x}|^{u}\lVert{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{u}f(\underline{y})\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{i=0}^{u}\binom{u}{i}|\underline{y}|^{i}|\underline{x}|^{u-i}\lVert{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{u}f(\underline{y})\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{i=0}^{u}\binom{u}{i}|\underline{x}|^{u-i}|\underline{y}|^{u}\lVert{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{u}f(\underline{y})\rVert\quad\mbox{as}\quad|\underline{y}|\to\infty.

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\sum_{u=1}^{k-1}\int_{C_{R}(\underline{x})}(-1)^{u}E_{u}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{u}f(\underline{y})d\underline{y}\Big\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{u=1}^{k-1}\int_{C_{R}(\underline{x})}|E_{u}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})|\lVert{\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{u}f(\underline{y})\rVert d\underline{y}

\displaystyle f(\underline{x})=-\sum_{u=1}^{k-1}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{u}E_{u}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y}){\mathcal{D}}_{\underline{y}}^{u}f(\underline{y})d\underline{y}+f(\infty),\quad\underline{x}\in\Omega_{-}.
