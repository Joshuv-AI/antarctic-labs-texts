Device Activity Detection in mMTC with Low-Resolution ADC: A New Protocol
Zhaorui Wang, Ya-Feng Liu, Ziyue Wang, Liang Liu, Haoyuan Pan, Shuguang Cui

Abstract
This paper investigates the effect of low-resolution analog-to-digital converters (ADCs) on device activity detection in massive machine-type communications (mMTC). The low-resolution ADCs induce two challenges on the device activity detection compared with the traditional setup with the assumption of infinite ADC resolution. First, the codebook design for signal quantization by the low-resolution ADC is particularly important since a good design of the codebook can lead to small quantization error on the received signal, which in turn has significant influence on the activity detector performance. To this end, prior information about the received signal power is needed, which depends on the number of active devices K K . This is sharply different from the activity detection problem in traditional setups, in which the knowledge of K K is not required by the BS as a prerequisite. Second, the covariance-based approach achieves good activity detection performance in traditional setups while it is not clear if it can still achieve good performance in this paper. To solve the above challenges, we propose a communication protocol that consists of an estimator for K K and a detector for active device identities: 1) For the estimator, the technical difficulty is that the design of the ADC quantizer and the estimation of K K are closely intertwined and doing one needs the information/execution from the other. We propose a progressive estimator which iteratively performs the estimation of K K and the design of the ADC quantizer; 2) For the activity detector, we propose a custom-designed stochastic gradient descent algorithm to estimate the active device identities. Numerical results demonstrate the effectiveness of the communication protocol.

I-B

Contributions

In this paper, we consider the activity detection problem in mMTC with low-resolution ADC and propose efficient approaches to solving the corresponding detection problem, which thus address the above two challenges. The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows.

First, we put forth a communication protocol to solve the detection problem where the antennas at the BS have low-resolution ADCs. The overall communication is divided into two phases. In phase I, we design an estimator for the number of active devices
K
. Based on the estimated
K
, the BS designs the ADC quantizer accordingly; In phase II, the BS detects the active device identities based on the received signals quantized by low-resolution ADCs.

Second, we design a detector for active devices in Phase II of the communication protocol, assuming that the BS already has an estimation of
K
. In the traditional setup with infinite-resolution ADC, the activity detection problem can be efficiently solved by the traditional gradient descent algorithm. However, the gradient in this paper is hard to compute due to the low-resolution ADC sampling. We solve this problem through a custom-designed stochastic gradient descent (SGD) algorithm
[
28
]
by exploiting the structure of the problem judiciously. We evaluate the performance of the designed detector. We test the detector under correlated Rayleigh fading channels. The numerical results show that, when
K
is perfectly estimated by the BS, by only applying a 4-bit ADC quantizer, our detector can achieve nearly the same performance as that with infinite-resolution ADC quantizer. We also evaluate the performance of the detector when
K
is imperfectly estimated by the BS. The numerical results show that the imperfect knowledge of
K
can have a significant penalty on the performance of the detector.

Third, we propose an estimator for
K
in Phase I of the communication protocol. In Phase I, the received signals to estimate
K
still need to go through the ADC quantizer, while the design of which still need the information of
K
. Thus, the technical difficulty here is that the design of the ADC quantizer and the estimation of
K
are closely intertwined and doing one needs the information/execution from the other. We propose a progressive estimator, which iteratively performs the estimation of the number of the active devices
K
and the design of the quantizer. The numerical results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed estimator.

II-B
) and (
20
) (by replacing
K
by
\hat{K}
therein). We show how to estimate
\gamma
in (
5
) based on the received-quantized signal
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}^{Q}
in (
19
). Once
\gamma
is estimated, we can determine the active devices based on the estimated
\gamma
.

III-A

Problem Formulation

Denote
p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}^{Q}|\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})
the probability density function (PDF) of
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}^{Q}
given
\gamma
and
S
. The detection problem is formulated as

\displaystyle\min_{\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}}

\displaystyle-p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}^{Q}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})

(22)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}\geq 0.

The PDF
p(\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}^{Q}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})
in (
22
) is expressed as

\displaystyle p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}^{Q}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})=\int_{\mathcal{I}_{f(\bar{y}_{1}^{Q})}}\int_{\mathcal{I}_{f(\bar{y}_{2}^{Q})}}\dots\int_{\mathcal{I}_{f(\bar{y}_{2L_{\rm I}M}^{Q})}}p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})d\bar{y}_{1}d\bar{y}_{2}\dots d\bar{y}_{2L_{\rm I}M},

(23)

where
f(x)
maps
x
to the index of the quantization interval where it falls in, i.e.,

\displaystyle f(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}1,~~~~{\rm if}~x\in\left(-\infty,(1-2^{(B-1)})\Delta\right),\\
q,~~~~{\rm if}~x\in\left[(q-1-2^{(B-1)})\Delta,(q-2^{(B-1)})\Delta\right),~q\in[2,2^{B}-1],\\
2^{B},~~{\rm if}~x\in\left[(2^{(B-1)}-1)\Delta,+\infty\right),\end{array}\right.

and
p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})
denotes the PDF of
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}
given
\gamma
and
S
, i.e.,

\displaystyle p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{ML}|\mbox{\boldmath{$\Sigma$}}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\exp{\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath{$\Sigma$}}^{-1}\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}\right)}.

(27)

The expression of
\Sigma
is shown in (
9
).

III-B

Computational Challenge of Problem (
22
) and Motivation of SGD Algorithm

In the rest of this section, we show how to solve the estimation problem in (
22
). Specifically, if the ADC resolution is infinite, then the objective function
p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}^{Q}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})
in (
22
) reduces to

\displaystyle p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}^{Q}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})=p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}}).

(28)

In this case, the problem in (
22
) could be solved efficiently by the traditional gradient descent algorithm
[
33
,
16
]
, where the gradient of
p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}^{Q}|\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})
with respect to
\gamma
can be computed with
{\cal O}(NL_{\rm I}^{2})
complexity. However, in this paper when the ADC resolution is finite, the objective function
p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}^{Q}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})
is an integral of
p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})
over the space

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}\left(\bar{y}_{1}^{Q},\bar{y}_{2}^{Q},\dots,\bar{y}_{2L_{\rm I}M}^{Q}\right)\overset{\Delta}{=}\mathcal{I}_{f(\bar{y}_{1}^{Q})}\times\mathcal{I}_{f(\bar{y}_{2}^{Q})}\times\dots\times\mathcal{I}_{f(\bar{y}_{2L_{\rm I}M}^{Q})},

(29)

and its gradient is also an integral of the gradient of
p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})
over the space
\mathcal{I}\left(\bar{y}_{1}^{Q},\dots,\bar{y}_{2L_{\rm I}M}^{Q}\right)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\nabla p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}^{Q}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})=\int_{\mathcal{I}_{f(\bar{y}_{1}^{Q})}}\int_{\mathcal{I}_{f(\bar{y}_{2}^{Q})}}\dots\int_{\mathcal{I}_{f(\bar{y}_{2L_{\rm I}M}^{Q})}}\nabla p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})d\bar{y}_{1}d\bar{y}_{2}\dots d\bar{y}_{2L_{\rm I}M},

(30)

where
\nabla p(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})
denotes the gradient of
p(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})
.

It is challenging to compute the gradient in (
30
) and apply the traditional gradient descent algorithm to solve problem (
22
). More specifically, although we can get a closed-form of
\nabla p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}|\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})
in (
30
), it is hard to get a closed-form of
\nabla p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}^{Q}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})
due to the high-dimensional integration (especially in the massive MIMO system where
M
is usually large). As an alternative, one can numerically compute the gradient in (
30
). However, the computational complexity is quite high, which further prevents us from applying the traditional gradient descent algorithm to solve the problem (
22
). Specifically, if we sample
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}
uniformly, the gradient
\nabla p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}^{Q}|\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})
in (
30
) can be approximated as

\displaystyle\nabla p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}^{Q}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})\approx\mu\sum_{i_{1}}\sum_{i_{2}}\dots\sum_{i_{2L_{\rm I}M}}\nabla p\left(\left[\bar{y}_{1}^{(i_{1})},\bar{y}_{2}^{(i_{2})},\dots,\bar{y}_{2L_{\rm I}M}^{(i_{2L_{\rm I}M})}\right]^{T}\Big|\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}}\right),

(31)

where
\mu
is a constant, and
\bar{y}_{\ell}^{(i_{\ell})}
is the
i_{\ell}
-th sample of
\bar{y}_{\ell}
in the sample space
\mathcal{I}_{f(\bar{y}_{{\ell}}^{Q})}
. Suppose the number of samples in each dimension is
I
. Then from (
31
), we know that the computational complexity is in the order of
{\cal O}(I^{2L_{\rm I}M})
, which would be prohibitively high when
M
and
L_{\rm I}
are large.

To solve the aforementioned computational challenge, we propose to apply a stochastic gradient descent (SGD) algorithm
[
28
]
to solve the problem (
22
). Specifically, at each iteration, the SGD algorithm computes a stochastic gradient of
p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})
by sampling only one point in each dimension of
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}
, instead of sampling many points in each dimension of
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}
to approximately compute the true gradient like that in (
31
). The per-iteration computational complexity is thus reduced significantly. Moreover,
[
28
]
has proven the convergence of the SGD algorithm.

In the next two subsections, we tailor the SGD algorithm to solve our interested problem (
22
), which essentially requires two basic steps. The first step is to rewrite the problem (
22
) into a favorable expectation form required by SGD. The second step is to compute the stochastic gradient, which is still a nontrivial task.

III-C

Rewriting (
22
) as a Form of Expected-Value Objective Function

The SGD algorithm is well suited for solving problems with an expected-value objective function
[
28
]
, e.g., in this paper the objective function should have a form like

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}\left(g\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}\right)\right),

(32)

where
g\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}\right)
is a function consisting of a random vector
x
and a deterministic vector
\gamma
that needs to be estimated in this paper. In this case, we should rewrite our objective function in (
22
) as an expected-value objective function like that in (
32
).

First, from (
7
) we know that each dimension of
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}
is Gaussian distributed with zero mean. In particular, for
\bar{y}_{\ell}
,
\ell=1,\dots,2NL_{\rm I}
, although the range of its PDF extends to infinity on both sides,
95\%
of the realizations of
\bar{y}_{\ell}
fall in the interval
[-2\sqrt{\lambda},2\sqrt{\lambda}]
, and
99.7\%
of the realizations fall in the interval
[-3\sqrt{\lambda},3\sqrt{\lambda}]
, where
\sqrt{\lambda}
denotes the standard deviation of
\bar{y}_{\ell}
that has been defined in (
11
). In this case, the objective function
p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}^{Q}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})
in (
22
) can be well approximated if the integration in (
23
) does not take account the values of
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}
that are far from its mean
0
, i.e.,

\displaystyle p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}^{Q}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})

\displaystyle\approx\int_{\mathcal{J}_{f(\bar{y}_{1}^{Q})}}\int_{\mathcal{J}_{f(\bar{y}_{2}^{Q})}}\dots\int_{\mathcal{J}_{f(\bar{y}_{2L_{\rm I}M}^{Q})}}p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})d\bar{y}_{1}d\bar{y}_{2}\dots d\bar{y}_{2L_{\rm I}M}

\displaystyle\overset{\Delta}{=}v\left(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}^{Q}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}}\right),

(33)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{q}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\left(-2^{(B-1)}\Delta,(1-2^{(B-1)})\Delta\right),~{\rm if}~q=1,\\
\left[(2^{(B-1)}-1)\Delta,2^{(B-1)}\Delta\right),~~~{\rm if}~q=2^{B},\\
\mathcal{I}_{q},~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~{\rm if}~q=2,\dots,2^{B}-1,\end{array}\right.

and
f(x)
has been defined in (
III-A
).

Second, in (
33
) the equality still holds when replacing the variable
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}
by another variable
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}=[x_{1},\dots,x_{2L_{\rm I}M}]^{T}
inside the integral, i.e.,

\displaystyle v\left(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}^{Q}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{J}_{f(\bar{y}_{1}^{Q})}}\int_{\mathcal{J}_{f(\bar{y}_{2}^{Q})}}\dots\int_{\mathcal{J}_{f(\bar{y}_{2L_{\rm I}M}^{Q})}}p(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})dx_{1}dx_{2}\dots dx_{2L_{\rm I}M}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{J}_{f(\bar{y}_{1}^{Q})}}\int_{\mathcal{J}_{f(\bar{y}_{2}^{Q})}}\dots\int_{\mathcal{J}_{f(\bar{y}_{2L_{\rm I}M}^{Q})}}p(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})\frac{1}{p(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})}p(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})dx_{1}dx_{2}\dots dx_{2L_{\rm I}M},

(37)

where
p(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})
in (
37
) is defined as the PDF of
x
. Last, in (
37
) denote

\displaystyle g\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}\right)=p(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}})\frac{1}{p(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})}.

(38)

Then,
v\left(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}^{Q}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}}\right)
in (
37
) is expressed as

\displaystyle v\left(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}}^{Q}\mid\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{J}_{f(\bar{y}_{1}^{Q})}}\int_{\mathcal{J}_{f(\bar{y}_{2}^{Q})}}\dots\int_{\mathcal{J}_{f(\bar{y}_{2L_{\rm I}M}^{Q})}}g\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}\right)p(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})dx_{1}dx_{2}\dots dx_{2L_{\rm I}M}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}\left(g\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}\right)\right).

(39)

In particular, in this paper, to ease the algorithm execution and analysis, we define the PDF of
x
as

\displaystyle p(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{J}_{f(\bar{y}_{1}^{Q})}||\mathcal{J}_{f(\bar{y}_{2}^{Q})}|\dots|\mathcal{J}_{f(\bar{y}_{2L_{\rm I}M}^{Q})}|},

(40)

i.e.,
x
is uniformly distributed in the space

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left(\bar{y}_{1}^{Q},\dots,\bar{y}_{2L_{\rm I}M}^{Q}\right)\overset{\Delta}{=}\mathcal{J}_{f(\bar{y}_{1}^{Q})}\times\mathcal{J}_{f(\bar{y}_{2}^{Q})}\times\dots\times\mathcal{J}_{f(\bar{y}_{2L_{\rm I}M}^{Q})}.

(41)

The optimization problem in (
22
) is then reformulated as

\displaystyle\min_{\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}}

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}\left(g\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}\right)\right)

(42)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}\geq 0,

where
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\in\mathcal{J}\left(\bar{y}_{1}^{Q},\dots,\bar{y}_{2L_{\rm I}M}^{Q}\right)
, and
g\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}\right)
is expressed in
(
38
)
.

III-D

Tailored Stochastic Gradient Descent Algorithm

The SGD algorithm to solve problem (
42
) is carried out as follows. At the
i
-th iteration, the algorithm uniformly generates a vector
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)}
from the sample space
\mathcal{J}\left(\bar{y}_{1}^{Q},\bar{y}_{2}^{Q},\dots,\bar{y}_{2L_{\rm I}M}^{Q}\right)
according to its PDF defined in (
40
). Then the variable
\gamma
is updated as follows:

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i)}=\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}-\theta_{i}\nabla g\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right),

(43)

where
\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i)}
is the value of
\gamma
at the
i
-th iteration,
\theta_{i}
is a step size parameter that controls the convergence speed of the SGD algorithm, and
\nabla g\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right)
is the stochastic gradient at the
i
-th iteration. The algorithm is terminated when

\displaystyle\frac{||\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i)}-\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}||_{1}}{N}<\epsilon

(44)

where
\epsilon>0
is a threshold that controls the termination of the algorithm.

In this paper, it is nontrivial to compute the stochastic gradient
\nabla g\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right)
in (
43
) in order to execute the SGD algorithm. Specifically,
\nabla g\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right)
at the
i
-th iteration is expressed as

\displaystyle\nabla g\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right)=\exp{\left(\bar{g}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right)\right)}\nabla\bar{g}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right),

(45)

where

\displaystyle\bar{g}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\log{\left(g\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right)\right)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)}\right)^{T}\mbox{\boldmath{$\Sigma$}}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)}-\frac{1}{2}\log{\left(|\mbox{\boldmath{$\Sigma$}}|\right)}-ML_{\rm I}\log{\left(2\pi\right)}-\log{\left(p(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)})\right)}.

(46)

Note that
\Sigma
in (
9
) is a function of
\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}
. In (
45
), the computation of
\nabla\bar{g}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right)
is simple. However, the other term
\exp{\left(\bar{g}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right)\right)}
in (
45
) often takes either a value which is very close to zero or a pretty large value due to the exponential operator. This problem further leads to the vanishing and exploding gradient problem, which makes the SGD algorithm fragile to be used to solve problem (34). To overcome the above instability issue, we propose to normalize the overall gradient
\nabla g\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right)
at each iteration, i.e., the
\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i)}
updated in (
43
) is revised as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i)}=\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}-\theta_{i}\frac{\nabla g\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right)}{||\nabla g\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right)||_{2}}.

(47)

In the following, we explain the intuition of the above normalization. Specifically, in (
45
), denote the gradient direction by

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$a$}}^{(i)}=\frac{\nabla g\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right)}{||\nabla g\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right)||_{2}}=\frac{\nabla\bar{g}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right)}{||\nabla\bar{g}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right)||_{2}},

(48)

and the gradient length by

\displaystyle b^{(i)}=\exp{\left(\bar{g}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right)\right)}||\nabla\bar{g}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right)||_{2}.

(49)

The gradient direction
\mbox{\boldmath{$a$}}^{(i)}
determines the direction along which
\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i)}
moves at the
i
-th iteration. The term
b^{(i)}
determines the step length with which
\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i)}
moves along the corresponding direction at the
i
-th iteration. Note that, here we only study the step length arising from the gradient itself, not the additional parameter
\theta_{i}
in (
43
). With the step length information
b^{(i)}
, the algorithm might converge quickly. Without the step length information
b^{(i)}
, e.g., the step length is set to be
b^{(i)}=1
, a good performance of the algorithm can still be expected as long as the gradient direction information
\mbox{\boldmath{$a$}}^{(i)}
is provided, and a sufficient number of iterations is performed. In our case, since the term
\exp{\left(\bar{g}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}\right)\right)}
causes significant disturbance of the overall gradient, we only use the gradient direction information
\mbox{\boldmath{$a$}}^{(i)}
as that in (
47
). Note that, compared with the original SGD update in (
43
), the above normalization may lead to a bias term (i.e.,
b^{(i)}
) on the update in (
47
). We will show in Section
IV-A
that, this bias term does not affect the convergence of the SGD algorithm. We name this algorithm as the Normalized SGD (NSGD) algorithm. The detailed procedures of the NSGD algorithm is shown in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Proposed NSGD Algorithm for Solving Problem (
42
)

1:

Initialization:
i=0
,
\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(0)}=\mbox{\boldmath{$0$}}
,
\eta=1
,
\epsilon>0
.

2:

while

\eta>\epsilon

do

3:

i\leftarrow i+1
;

4:

Generate
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{J}\left(\bar{y}_{1}^{Q},\dots,\bar{y}_{2L_{\rm I}M}^{Q}\right)
in a uniform manner;

5:

Update
\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i)}
according to (
47
);

6:

Compute
\eta=\frac{||\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i)}-\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}||_{1}}{N}
;

7:

end

while

8:

Output
\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i)}
.

The above NSGD algorithm can be viewed as a process that solves a series of optimization problems, each of which is inexactly solved by the traditional gradient descent algorithm with only one gradient descent iteration. That is, instead of solving a problem with objective function being
\mathbb{E}_{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}\left(g\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}\right)\right)
in (
42
), Algorithm
1
solves a series of problems with objective function being
g(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}})
. Specifically, at the
i
-th iteration the problem being solved is

\displaystyle\min_{\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}}

\displaystyle-g\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}\right)

(50)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}\geq 0.

Performing a normalized gradient descent step on the above problem at the current point
\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}^{(i-1)}
yields the update in (
47
).

V
Number of Active device Estimator in Phase I

In this section, we propose an estimator to estimate the number of active devices
K
in Phase I of the communication protocol. The received signals to estimate
K
still need to go through the ADC quantizer, while the design of which still need the information of
K
. Thus, the technical difficulty here is that the design of the ADC quantizer and the estimation of
K
are closely intertwined and doing one needs the information/execution from the other. This seems like a chicken-and-egg problem. This section aims to propose an efficient algorithm to solve this dilemma.

V-A

Problem Formulation

In this section, we study the estimator for
K
under i.i.d. Rayleigh fading channels. The i.i.d. Rayleigh fading model is widely assumed in the current literature, e.g.,

\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}_{n}=\mbox{\boldmath{$I$}}

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$h$}}_{n}=\ddot{\mbox{\boldmath{$h$}}}_{n},~n=1,2,\dots,N.

In Section

Since in Phase I we only need to estimate

\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{1}=\dots=\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{N}=\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}\in\mathbb{C}^{L_{\rm N}\times 1}

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$Y$}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\alpha_{n}\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{n}\sqrt{\beta}\mbox{\boldmath{$h$}}^{T}_{n}+\mbox{\boldmath{$Z$}}=\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}\sqrt{\beta}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\alpha_{n}\mbox{\boldmath{$h$}}^{T}_{n}+\mbox{\boldmath{$Z$}}.

V-C

VI
Conclusion

We investigated a device activity detection problem in mMTC, where the BS is equipped with a massive MIMO and low-resolution ADCs. This setup causes additional challenges in the activity detection problem. First, the BS should have the knowledge of the number of active devices
K
in order to do better ADC quantization. Second, it is not clear if the covariance-based approach can still achieve good performance. To solve the above problems, we proposed a communication protocol that consists of an estimator for
K
and a detector for active device identities. For the estimator for
K
, we proposed a progressive estimator PEA which iteratively performs the estimation of
K
and the design of the quantizer. For the detector for active device identities, we proposed a custom-designed NSGD algorithm to solve the problem whose objective involves high-dimensional integration. Numerical results demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed communication protocol.
