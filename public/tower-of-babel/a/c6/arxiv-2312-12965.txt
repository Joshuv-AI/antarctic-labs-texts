Ceresa cycles of bielliptic Picard curves
Jef Laga, Ari Shnidman

Abstract
. We prove that the Chow class κ ∞ ​ ( C t ) \kappa_{\infty}(C_{t}) of the Ceresa cycle of the genus three curve C t : y 3 = x 4 + 2 ​ t ​ x 2 + 1 C_{t}\colon y^{3}=x^{4}+2tx^{2}+1 is torsion if and only if Q t = ( t 2 − 1 3 , t ) Q_{t}=(\sqrt[3]{t^{2}-1},t) is a torsion point on the elliptic curve y 2 = x 3 + 1 y^{2}=x^{3}+1 . In particular, there are infinitely many plane quartic curves over ℂ {\rm\mathbb{C}} with torsion Ceresa cycle. Over ℚ ¯ \overline{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}} , we show that the Beilinson–Bloch height of κ ∞ ​ ( C t ) \kappa_{\infty}(C_{t}) is proportional to the Néron–Tate height of Q t Q_{t} . 1.
Introduction

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field and
C
a smooth, projective, and connected curve over
k
of genus
g\geq 2
with Jacobian variety
J
.
Let
e
be a degree-
1
divisor on
C
and let
\iota_{e}\colon C\hookrightarrow J
be the Abel-Jacobi map based at
e
.
We study the torsion behaviour of the Ceresa cycle

(1.1)

\displaystyle\kappa_{e}(C):=[\iota_{e}(C)]-(-1)^{*}[\iota_{e}(C)]\in{\rm CH}_{1}(J)

in the Chow group modulo rational equivalence.
If
\kappa_{e}(C)
is torsion, then
(2g-2)e=K_{C}
in
{\rm CH}_{0}(C)\otimes{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
, where
K_{C}
is the canonical divisor class.
Moreover if
(2g-2)e=K_{C}
in
{\rm CH}_{0}(C)\otimes{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
, then the image of
\kappa_{e}(C)
in
{\rm CH}_{1}(J)\otimes{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
is independent of
e
and we denote it by
\kappa(C)
; see §
2.1
for these claims.
The class
\kappa(C)
vanishes if and only if
\kappa_{e}(C)
is torsion for some degree-
1
divisor
e
.

While
\kappa_{e}(C)
is always homologically trivial, it need not be trivial in
{\rm CH}_{1}(J)
.
Indeed, if
k={\rm\mathbb{C}}
then Ceresa famously showed
[
12
]
that for a very general curve of genus
g\geq 3
,
\kappa_{e}(C)
is of infinite order for every choice of
e
, even modulo algebraic equivalence.
On the other hand, if
C
is hyperelliptic and
e
is a Weierstrass point, then
\kappa_{e}(C)=0
.
Beyond this, little is known about the set of genus-
g
curves with a vanishing Ceresa cycle, though see §
1.4
for some recent results and examples.

In this paper, we study the Ceresa cycles of bielliptic Picard curves over fields of characteristic
\neq 2,3

[
32
]
. As
k
is algebraically closed, such a curve
C
has an affine model of the form

(1.2)

\displaystyle y^{3}=x^{4}+2tx^{2}+1,

for some
t\in k\setminus\{\pm 1\}
, unique up to replacing
t
with
-t
.
The unique point
\infty\in C(k)
at infinity has the property that
(2g-2)\infty=4\infty
is canonical.
Our main result shows that the Ceresa cycles of these curves are governed by points on the single elliptic curve
\widehat{E}\colon y^{2}=x^{3}+1
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
C_{t}
be the smooth projective curve with model (
1.2
), and let
Q\in\widehat{E}(k)
be a point with
y
-coordinate equal to
t
.
Then
\kappa_{\infty}(C_{t})\in{\rm CH}_{1}(J_{t})
is torsion if and only if
Q\in\widehat{E}(k)
is torsion.

In other words,
\kappa_{\infty}(C_{t})
is torsion if and only if
(\sqrt[3]{t^{2}-1},t)\in\widehat{E}(k)
is torsion, for any choice of cube root.
See Theorem
5.17
for a slightly different formulation of this theorem which also relates the torsion orders.
Theorem
1.1
(which is proved in §
5.5
) appears to be the first characterization of torsion Ceresa cycles in a positive-dimensional family of curves, aside from families where
\kappa(C)
is identically zero.
We deduce from this result the following corollary (see §
5.6
).

Corollary 1.2
.

There are infinitely many
{\rm\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}
-isomorphism classes of genus
3
curves
C
with
\kappa(C)\neq 0
that can be defined over
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
.
In fact,
\kappa_{\infty}(C_{t})
has infinite order for all
t\in{\rm\mathbb{Q}}\setminus\{0,\pm 1,\pm 3\}
.

The first line of Corollary
1.2
is not surprising given Ceresa’s result, but it does not follow from it. Robin de Jong has given a different proof of this infinitude, using a different family of curves
[
13
]
.

Since
\widehat{E}
has infinitely many torsion points, we also deduce from Theorem
1.1
that there are infinitely many plane quartic curves over
{\rm\mathbb{C}}
with
torsion
Ceresa cycle. As we explain below, our proof shows that there is an algebraic correspondence from
J_{t}
to
\widehat{E}
that sends
\kappa(C_{t})
to a multiple of
Q
. This allows us to deduce the following stronger statement:

Theorem 1.3
.

There exist infinitely many
t\in{\rm\mathbb{C}}
such that
\kappa_{\infty}(C_{t})
is torsion.
Each such
t
lies in
{\rm\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}
.
Moreover, the finite torsion orders of the classes
\kappa_{\infty}(C_{t})
are unbounded.

Note that it was only recently discovered that there exists
any
plane quartic curve with torsion Ceresa cycle: Qiu–Zhang
[
42
, §4.1]
showed that
C_{0}\colon y^{3}=x^{4}+1
has this property.

For
t\in{\rm\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\setminus\{\pm 1\}
, we write
h(\kappa(C_{t}))
for the Beilinson–Bloch height
\langle\kappa(C_{t}),\kappa(C_{t})\rangle

[
6
,
31
]
of
\kappa(C_{t})
.
We show that
h(\kappa(C_{t}))
is proportional to the Néron–Tate height of
Q
(Theorem
5.20
), which implies the following nondegeneracy and Northcott properties.

Theorem 1.4
.

We have
h(\kappa(C_{t}))=0
if and only if
\kappa_{\infty}(C_{t})
is torsion. Moreover, for any
X\in{\rm\mathbb{R}}
and
d\in{\rm\mathbb{N}}
, the set

\left\{t\in{\rm\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\setminus\{\pm 1\}\colon h(\kappa(C_{t}))\leq X\hskip 8.53581pt\mbox{and}\hskip 8.53581pt[{\rm\mathbb{Q}}(t):{\rm\mathbb{Q}}]\leq d\right\}

is finite.

Since the field of definition of
C_{t}
is
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}(t^{2})
(Lemma
3.2
), this implies that for every fixed
d\in\mathbb{N}
, there are finitely many
{\rm\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}
-isomorphism classes of curves
C_{t}
with torsion Ceresa cycle that can be defined over a number field of degree
\leq d
.
In particular, there are finitely many curves
C_{t}
with
\kappa_{\infty}(C_{t})
torsion that can be defined over
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
. In fact, there are three such curves up to
{\rm\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}
-isomorphism:
C_{0}
,
C_{\pm 3}
and
C_{\pm\sqrt{-3}}
(Proposition
5.19
). Note that
C_{\sqrt{-3}}
has
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
-model
y^{3}=x^{4}+6x^{2}-3
.

Finally, we show that for all
t
, the image of
\kappa_{\infty}(C_{t})
in the Griffiths group of homologically trivial cycles modulo algebraic equivalence is torsion, in the following strong sense:

Theorem 1.5
.

There exists an integer
N\geq 1
such that for every bielliptic Picard curve
C
over a field of characteristic not
2
nor
3
, the cycle
N\cdot\kappa_{\infty}(C)\in{\rm CH}_{1}(J)
is algebraically trivial.

Theorems
1.3
-
1.5
are quick corollaries of our proof of Theorem
1.1
, which we discuss below.

1.1.
Relation with modified diagonal cycles

All results above hold verbatim for the canonical modified diagonal cycle
\Delta_{GS,\infty}(C_{t})\in{\rm CH}_{1}(C_{t}^{3})
defined by Gross and Schoen
[
24
]
; see §
2
for the definition of
\Delta_{GS,e}(C)
and the class
\Delta_{GS}(C)\in{\rm CH}^{3}(C)\otimes{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
. Indeed,
\kappa(C)
vanishes if and only if
\Delta_{GS}(C)
does
[
49
, Theorem 1.5.5]
, and the relation
h(\Delta_{GS}(C))=\frac{3}{2}h(\kappa(C))

[
14
, (1.1)]
gives an analogue of Theorem
1.4
as well.

S. Zhang has shown a similar Northcott property for the height of
\Delta_{GS}(C)
in families of curves over smooth projective bases
[
49
, Theorem 1.3.5]
, i.e. compact subvarieties inside
\mathcal{M}_{g}
. As the family
C_{t}
does not extend to a compact curve inside
\mathcal{M}_{3}
, Theorem
1.4
suggests that Zhang’s Northcott property might very well extend to an open dense subset
U_{g}
of
\mathcal{M}_{g}
. Indeed, several months after the first version of this paper was posted, Gao and Zhang proved that such a set
U_{g}
exists
[
22
]
. Interestingly, in another preprint we have shown that the locus of Picard curves must be contained in the complement of
U_{3}

[
33
, §1.3]
, so that Theorem
1.4
does not follow from
[
22
]
.

1.2.
Summary of proof

A curve
C
of the form (
1.2
) has an order
6
automorphism (or more canonically, a
\mu_{6}
-action), and the class
\kappa_{\infty}(C)
is fixed by the induced
\mu_{6}
-action on
J
. On the other hand, over
{\rm\mathbb{C}}
, the
\mu_{6}
-invariant part of the Hodge structure
\HH^{3}(J({\rm\mathbb{C}}),{\rm\mathbb{Q}})(1)
is two-dimensional and of type
(1,0)+(0,1)
. This suggests that there should exist an algebraic correspondence from
J
to an elliptic curve that sends
\kappa_{\infty}(C)
to a point on this elliptic curve.
Instead of attempting to construct this correspondence explicitly, we prove this by maneuvering within the flexible category of (pure) Chow motives.

Namely, we show that there is an isomorphism of Chow motives

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}^{3}(J)^{\mu_{6}}\simeq\mathfrak{h}^{1}(E^{\Delta})(-1),

where
J
is the Jacobian of any curve of the form
C\colon y^{3}=x^{4}+ax^{2}+b
,
E^{\Delta}
is a certain twist of
\widehat{E}
depending on
a
and
b
, and the remainder of the notation will be explained later.
It is crucial for our proof that we find a universal such isomorphism, in the category of relative Chow motives over the parameter space
S={\rm\mathbb{A}}^{2}_{{\rm\mathbb{Z}}[1/6]}\setminus\{\Delta_{a,b}=0\}
. The elliptic scheme
\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}\to S
can then be thought of as the universal
j
-invariant
0
elliptic curve with a nonzero marked point
\mathcal{Q}
. The main ingredients in the construction of this universal isomorphism are the Chow–Künneth decomposition for motives of abelian schemes (via Beauville’s Fourier transform)
[
3
,
15
]
, Moonen’s refinement of this decomposition in the presence of extra endomorphisms
[
37
]
, and our study of the Galois action on the endomorphism algebra of the universal Prym surface
\mathcal{P}\to S

[
32
]
. The latter endomorphism algebra is a nonsplit quaternion algebra over
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
.

The Ceresa cycle of
C
naturally lives in
{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathfrak{h}^{3}(J)^{\mu_{6}})
(Lemma
5.12
), so the question becomes: what is the corresponding element of
{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathfrak{h}^{1}(E^{\Delta}(-1))=E^{\Delta}(k)\otimes{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
, under the isomorphism (
1.3
)? We show that it corresponds to a
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}^{\times}
-multiple of the marked point
\mathcal{Q}
by showing it universally over
S
, using two soft inputs.
First, the elliptic scheme
\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}\rightarrow S
(which after
{\rm\mathbb{G}}_{m}
-scaling, is essentially an elliptic surface) has Mordell–Weil group of rank 1, generated by
\mathcal{Q}
.
Second, we show by explicit computation that the universal Ceresa cycle
\kappa_{\infty}(\mathcal{C})
has at least one infinite order specialization, using reduction mod
p
arguments based on the methods of
[
8
,
11
,
17
]
, see §
2
.
Therefore
\kappa_{\infty}(\mathcal{C})
corresponds to a nonzero section of
\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}
, and hence a multiple of
\mathcal{Q}
.
Specializing at geometric points, we recover Theorem
1.1
. The construction allows us to quickly deduce the remaining results in the introduction as well.

The “only if” direction of Theorem
1.1
could be proved without the machinery of Chow motives, by implementing the above argument at the level of cohomology (via complex Abel-Jacobi maps and Hodge theory). However, this is not enough for the “if” direction since the injectivity of higher Abel-Jacobi maps for varieties over
{\rm\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}
is very much open. The motivic approach therefore seems crucial for an unconditional result and is in any case more direct.

1.3.
Geometric interpretation via bigonal duality

C_{t}\colon y^{3}=x^{4}+2tx^{2}+1
is a double cover of the elliptic curve
E_{t}\colon y^{3}=x^{2}+2tx+1
. Associated to any such double cover is the
bigonal dual cover

\widehat{C}_{t}\to\widehat{E}_{t}
constructed by Pantazis and Donagi
[
41
,
16
]
, whose Prym is dual to the Prym of
C_{t}\to E_{t}
; see
[
32
, §2.7]
for a construction in the situation considered here. In our case, the curve
\widehat{C}_{t}
has model
y^{3}=(x^{2}+t)^{2}-1
so that
\widehat{E}_{t}
is isomorphic to
\widehat{E}\colon y^{2}=x^{3}+1
, for all
t
. The branch locus of
\widehat{C}_{t}\to\widehat{E}_{t}
consists of
\infty
and the three points
(\sqrt[3]{t^{2}-1},t)
. Theorem
1.1
therefore says that the Ceresa cycle
\kappa_{\infty}(C_{t})
is torsion if and only if
\widehat{C}_{t}
is branched along torsion points of
\widehat{E}_{t}
. While this formulation is intriguing, our proof does not use the bigonal dual construction in a direct way.

One might ask if this is a more general phenomenon: if
C\rightarrow E
is a double cover of a genus
1
curve by a genus
3
curve, whose ramification locus contains a hyperflex point
\infty\in C(k)
, are the torsion properties of
\kappa_{\infty}(C)
related to the torsion properties of the branch locus of the dual cover
\widehat{C}\rightarrow\widehat{E}
?
Adam Logan has verified for us, using
[
17
]
, that out of a sampling of 99 such bielliptic curves whose dual cover is branched along
7
-torsion points, all of them have infinite order Ceresa cycle. Thus, the answer seems to be ‘no’ in general. It would be interesting to test this hypothesis on other families of genus
3
curves lying in the intersection (within
\mathcal{M}_{3}
) of the bielliptic locus and a Picard modular surface associated to an imaginary quadratic field other than
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-3})
, but these families are non-trivial to write down.
In the aforementioned
[
33
]
, we have generalized Theorem
1.1
to all genus three Picard curves
y^{3}=f(x)
using different methods. This suggests that the bielliptic structure which we exploit in this paper, while a very useful tool to understand and “compute” the Ceresa cycle, does not tell the whole story.

1.4.
Previous results

Several examples of nonhyperelliptic curves with torsion Ceresa cycle were recently found, conditional on Beilinson–Bloch type conjectures
[
7
,
23
,
4
,
35
]
.
Qiu and W. Zhang then gave some unconditional examples
[
42
]
, including one in genus 3 and one-dimensional families in genus
4
and
5
.
Laterveer also found a two-dimensional family in genus
5

[
34
]
.
Subsequently, Qiu–Zhang found examples of plane quartic curves (dominated by Shimura curves) with trivial automorphism group and torsion Ceresa cycle
[
43
]
.

The proofs of
[
42
]
and
[
34
]
rely on the vanishing of the Chow motive
(\mathfrak{h}^{1}(C)^{\otimes 3})^{{\rm Aut}(C)}
, and hence of
\mathfrak{h}^{3}(J)^{{\rm Aut}(C)}
, for certain curves
C
, generalizing the observation that hyperelliptic curves have trivial Ceresa cycle.
For our curves
C=C_{t}
with
t\neq 0,\pm 1
, the motive
\mathfrak{h}^{3}(J)^{{\rm Aut}(C)}=\mathfrak{h}^{3}(J)^{\mu_{6}}
does not vanish, but (
1.3
) shows that it is a direct summand of the motive of a curve, hence is still reasonably accessible. It would be interesting to study other families of curves where
\mathfrak{h}^{3}(J)^{{\rm Aut}(C)}
is “small” in a suitable sense. More generally, one can try to study the nonzero isotypic components of
\kappa(C)
with respect to the action of
{\rm End}(J)
on
\mathfrak{h}^{3}(J)
. For example, even though some of the examples in
[
43
]
have trivial automorphism group, they all satisfy
{\rm End}(J)\neq{\rm\mathbb{Z}}
.

Aside from the aforementioned
[
13
]
, Eskandari and Murty have shown that infinitely many Fermat curves have infinite order Ceresa cycle
[
18
]
, proving a weak version of Corollary
1.2
(where the genus is allowed to grow). If we fix
g
but allow for curves defined over
{\rm\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}
(and not necessarily over
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
), then the analogue of Corollary
1.2
holds for every genus
g\geq 3
by
[
49
, Theorem 1.3.5]
.

Prior to
[
42
]
, Beauville and Schoen showed that the Ceresa cycle of the curve
y^{3}z=x^{4}+xz^{3}
is torsion in the Griffiths group
[
5
]
. It was observed in
[
35
]
that their proof applies to the bielliptic Picard curve
C_{0}
as well. Theorem
1.5
gives the first example of a
family
of plane quartic curves with this property.

1.5.
Further questions

Let

After the current paper was posted, several papers have appeared that study this torsion locus and its variants; see

2.
Ceresa and modified diagonal cycles

We recall the definitions of Ceresa and modified diagonal cycles, and show, by means of an example, a way to verify that
\kappa_{e}(C)
is infinite order, for genus 3 curves over
{\rm\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}
. The latter is based on the methods of
[
11
]
and
[
17
]
, which are in turn based on ideas of Bloch
[
8
]
. In this section, and for the rest of the paper, we work over a general (not necessarily algebraically closed) field
k
of characteristic
\neq 2,3
.

2.1.
Ceresa and modified diagonal cycles

Let
C/k
be a curve of genus
g\geq 2
with Jacobian
J
.
Let
e
be a degree-
1
divisor on
C
and let
\iota_{e}\colon C\rightarrow J
be the Abel–Jacobi map based at
e
.
We define the Ceresa cycle based at
e
to be

\displaystyle\kappa_{e}(C):=[\iota_{e}(C)]-(-1)_{*}[\iota_{e}(C)]\in{\rm CH}_{1}(J).

The modified diagonal cycle of Gross and Schoen
[
24
]
is a close relative of the Ceresa cycle. For
e\in C(k)
, the
modified diagonal cycle

\Delta_{GS,e}=\Delta_{GS,e}(C)
based at
e
is

\Delta_{GS,e}:=\Delta_{123}-\Delta_{12}-\Delta_{13}-\Delta_{23}+\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+\Delta_{3}\in{\rm CH}^{2}(C^{3}),

where
\Delta_{123}=\{(c,c,c)\colon c\in C\}
,
\Delta_{12}=\{(c,c,e)\colon c\in C\}
, and so on.
For a general degree
1
divisor
e=\sum_{i}n_{i}[p_{i}]
on
C
, we define
\Delta_{GS,e}(C)=\sum_{i}n_{i}\Delta_{GS,p_{i}}\in{\rm CH}^{2}(C^{3})
.

We collect some useful facts about the Ceresa and modified diagonal cycles.

Proposition 2.1
.

The cycle classes
\kappa_{e}(C)
and
\Delta_{GS,e}(C)
are homologically trivial.

Proof.

For
\kappa_{e}(C)
, this is immediate from the fact that
(-1)^{*}
acts trivially on even dimensional cohomology. For
\Delta_{GS,e}(C)
, this is
[
24
, Proposition 3.1]
.
∎

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose that either
\kappa_{e}(C)\in{\rm CH}_{1}(J)
or
\Delta_{GS,e}(C)\in{\rm CH}^{2}(C^{3})
is torsion.
Then
(2g-2)e-K_{C}
is torsion in
{\rm CH}_{0}(C)
, where
K_{C}
is the canonical divisor class.

Proof.

If
\kappa_{e}(C)
is torsion, then this follows from
[
33
, Lemma 2.10]
, but we give an alternative proof here.
If

Assume now that

Proposition 2.5

Let

[

We now show by example how Proposition

Lemma 2.6
.

.

in Proposition

and show that

Let

be the symmetric cube of

Lemma 2.7
.

V\simeq\HH^{3}(J_{\overline{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}},{\rm\mathbb{Z}}_{7}(2))\oplus\HH^{1}(C_{\overline{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}},{\rm\mathbb{Z}}_{7}(1))

Lemma 2.8
.

The class

has infinite order.

Proof.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that

3.
Bielliptic Picard curves

We recall some results on bielliptic Picard curves from
[
32
]
; we refer to that paper for more details.
In particular, we collect the results we will need on the endomorphism algebra of the Prym variety and its monodromy in §

.

3.1.
Definitions

A
bielliptic Picard curve

C
over a field
k
(of characteristic
\neq 2,3
) is a smooth projective plane quartic curve over
k
with affine model of the form

(3.1)

\displaystyle y^{3}=x^{4}+ax^{2}+b,

for some
a,b\in k
. We often write
C=C_{a,b}
. Smoothness of
C
implies that

\displaystyle\Delta_{a,b}:=16b(a^{2}-4b)\neq 0,

and any plane quartic of the form (
3.1
) with
\Delta_{a,b}\neq 0
is smooth.
These curves admit a
\mu_{6}
-action given by
\zeta\cdot(x,y)=(\zeta^{3}x,\zeta^{4}y)
for every
\zeta\in\mu_{6}
.
The unique fixed point of this action is the unique point
\infty
at infinity.
Since
\infty
intersects the line at infinity with multiplicity
4
, the divisor
4\infty
is canonical.

Define the
j
-invariant
of
C_{a,b}
to be
j(C_{a,b}):=(4b-a^{2})/4b\in k^{\times}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

[
32
, §2.2-3]

Let
C_{a,b}
and
C_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}
be bielliptic Picard curves over
k
.

(1)

C_{a,b}\simeq C_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}
if and only if there exists
\lambda\in k^{\times}
such
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=(\lambda^{6}a,\lambda^{12}b)
.

(2)

C_{a,b}
is isomorphic to
C_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}
over
\overline{k}
if and only if
j(C_{a,b})=j(C_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}})
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
C_{a,b}
be a bielliptic Picard curve over
k^{\mathrm{sep}}
. Then
C
is defined over
k
(in other words, there exists a curve
X
over
k
such that
X_{k^{\mathrm{sep}}}\simeq C_{a,b}
) if and only if
j(C_{a,b})\in k
.

Proof.

Suppose that
j=j(C_{a,b})\in k
.
If
j=1
, then
a=0
and
C_{a,b}
is
k^{\mathrm{sep}}
-isomorphic to
C_{0,1}
and defined over
k
.
If
j\neq 1
, then a computation using Lemma
3.1
(1) shows that
C_{a,b}
is
k^{\mathrm{sep}}
-isomorphic to
C_{2,(1-j)^{-1}}
and hence also defined over
k
.
Conversely, suppose that
C_{a,b}
is defined over
k
.
Then
C_{a,b}\simeq\sigma^{*}(C_{a,b})=C_{\sigma(a),\sigma(b)}
for all
\sigma\in{\rm Gal}_{k}
.
By Lemma
3.1
(2),
j(C_{a,b})=\sigma(j(C_{a,b}))
for all
\sigma\in{\rm Gal}_{k}
. In other words,

.
∎

3.2.

The curve

admits an involution

Let

\displaystyle P:=\ker(1+\tau^{*}\colon J\rightarrow J)

associated to the double cover

.

Lemma 3.3
.

[
32
, §2.5]

P
is an abelian surface and

.

3.3.

The main ingredient needed from

We will need a universal version of this story over the parameter space of all bielliptic Picard curves, so let

Our proof of Theorem

The most important sections are §

We call a scheme

For the remainder of this section fix such a scheme

4.1.
Correspondences

We denote by
\mathsf{SmProj}(S)
the category of smooth projective
S

For

\mathsf{SmProj}(S)

for the relative dimension of

.
If

we define the

-vector space of

by
{\rm Corr}(X,Y):={\rm CH}^{*}(X\times_{S}Y;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})

We say a correspondence has

{\rm Corr}^{r}(X,Y):=\bigoplus_{i}{\rm CH}^{r+d(X_{i}/S)}(X_{i}\times_{S}Y;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})

where
X=\sqcup_{i}X_{i}
is a decomposition such that

is equidimensional.
If
p\in{\rm Corr}^{r}(X,Y)
and
q\in{\rm Corr}^{s}(Y,Z)
are correspondences, denote by
q\circ p=\pi_{XZ,*}(\pi_{XY}^{*}p\cdot\pi_{YZ}^{*}q)\in{\rm Corr}^{r+s}(X,Z)
their composition.
For an
S
-morphism

, let
\Gamma_{f}\subset X\times_{S}Y
be its graph and let
{}^{t}\Gamma_{f}\subset Y\times_{S}X
be the transpose.
Write
\Delta_{X/S}
for the graph of the identity

.

4.2.
Relative Chow motives

We denote by
\mathsf{Mot}(S)
the category of relative Chow motives with respect to graded correspondences over

[
15
, §1]
for more details.
The results of
[
15
]
are stated under the standing assumption that

[

We recall that an object of
\mathsf{Mot}(S)
is a triple
(X/S,p,m)
, where
X/S
is a smooth projective
S
-scheme,

is an idempotent correspondence and

Morphisms are given by

{\rm Hom}((X/S,p,m),(Y/S,q,n)):=q\circ{\rm Corr}^{m-n}(X,Y)\circ p.

Given a motive

4.4.
Descending direct summands of motives

Let
\pi\colon\tilde{S}\rightarrow S
be a finite étale morphism of adequate schemes.
Suppose additionally that it is Galois and write
G
for the group of Deck transformations, so we have an isomorphism of
S
-schemes
g\colon\tilde{S}\rightarrow\tilde{S}
for every
g\in G
.
Using the pullback functor from §
4.3
, we obtain functors
g^{*}\colon\mathsf{Mot}(\tilde{S})\rightarrow\mathsf{Mot}(\tilde{S})
.
Let
M,N
be objects of
\mathsf{Mot}(S)
and write
\tilde{M}=\pi^{*}M
,
\tilde{N}=\pi^{*}N
for the corresponding objects of
\mathsf{Mot}(\tilde{S})
.
Then we have canonical identifications
\tilde{M}=g^{*}\tilde{M}
and
\tilde{N}=g^{*}\tilde{N}
for all
g\in G
.
It follows by functoriality that
{\rm Hom}(\tilde{M},\tilde{N})
inherits a
G
-action.

Lemma 4.1
.

In the above notation, the natural map
{\rm Hom}(M,N)\rightarrow{\rm Hom}(\tilde{M},\tilde{N})^{G}
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

This is well known when
S={\rm Spec}(k)

[
44
, Lemma 1.17]
; we sketch the generalization of the classical proof to the relative setting.
Write
M=(X/S,p,m)
and
N=(Y/S,q,n)
.
Then an element of
{\rm Hom}(\tilde{M},\tilde{N})^{G}
is the same as a
G
-invariant element
\alpha
of
q\circ{\rm CH}^{d(X/S)+m-n}(X\times_{S}Y;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})\circ p
.
Choose a cycle
Z
on
X_{\tilde{S}}\times_{\tilde{S}}Y_{\tilde{S}}
representing
\alpha
.
Then
\sum_{g\in G}g^{*}Z
descends to a unique cycle
W
of
X\times_{S}Y
.
Moreover the rational equivalence class
[W]
is independent of the choice of
Z
representing
\alpha
.
It follows that
\alpha\mapsto\frac{1}{|G|}[W]
is a well-defined inverse to the base change map above.
∎

This implies that Galois-invariant summands also satisfy descent:

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
M
be an object of
\mathsf{Mot}(S)
and
\tilde{U}\oplus\tilde{V}
a direct sum decomposition of
\tilde{M}=\pi^{*}M
such that we have an equality of direct summands
g^{*}\tilde{U}=\tilde{U}
and
g^{*}\tilde{V}=\tilde{V}
in
g^{*}\tilde{M}=\tilde{M}
for all
g\in G
.
Then there exists a unique direct sum decomposition

such that
\pi^{*}U=\tilde{U}
and
\pi^{*}V=\tilde{V}
.

Proof.

Since direct sum decompositions correspond bijectively to a pair of orthogonal idempotents in the endomorphism ring, this follows immediately from Lemma
4.1
.
∎

4.5.
Group actions on motives

Let
M
be a motive in
\mathsf{Mot}(S)
and
G
a finite group acting on
M
.
Then every idempotent in the group algebra
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}[G]
gives rise to an idempotent in
M
.
For example, if
\rho
is an irreducible (but not necessarily absolutely irreducible) representation defined over
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
and
e_{\rho}
is the corresponding central idempotent, we get a direct summand
M_{\rho}
of
M
, called the
\rho
-isotypic component
.
Applying this to
\rho=
the trivial representation, we have
e_{\rho}=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}g
and we see that the
motive of
G
-fixed points

M^{G}=M_{\rho}
is well-defined and a direct summand of
M
.

We may also consider actions by nonconstant finite group schemes, using Lemma
4.1
.
For example, if
n
is invertible in
S
, a
\mu_{n}
-action on a motive
M
in
\mathsf{Mot}(S)
is an
{\rm Aut}(\tilde{S}/S)
-equivariant map
\mu_{n}(\tilde{S})\rightarrow{\rm Aut}(f^{*}M)
, where
f\colon\tilde{S}=S\times_{{\rm\mathbb{Z}}[1/n]}{\rm\mathbb{Z}}[\zeta_{n}][1/n]\rightarrow S
.
The
{\rm Aut}(\tilde{S}/S)
-equivariance implies that the
\mu_{n}
-fixed points of
f^{*}M
descend to a well-defined direct summand
M^{\mu_{n}}
of
M
.

4.6.
Chow groups of motives

The (covariant)
Chow groups
of a motive
M
in
\mathsf{Mot}(S)
are by definition the
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
-vector spaces
{\rm CH}^{p}(M):={\rm Hom}(\mathbb{L}^{\otimes p},M)
for
p\in{\rm\mathbb{Z}}
, where we recall from §
4.2
that
\mathbb{L}
denotes the Lefchetz motive.
For example,
{\rm CH}^{p}(\mathfrak{h}(X/S))={\rm CH}^{p}(X;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
for all
X/S
in
\mathsf{SmProj}(S)
.

Suppose a finite group
G
acts on
M
.
This induces an action of
G
on
{\rm CH}^{p}(M)
for every
p
.
If
e
is an idempotent in
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}[G]
corresponding to direct summand
e\cdot M
of
M
, then
{\rm CH}^{p}(e\cdot M)=e\cdot{\rm CH}^{p}(M)
.
In particular,
{\rm CH}^{p}(M^{G})={\rm CH}^{p}(M)^{G}
.

4.7.
Relative Artin motives

Assume that
S
is connected.
An
Artin motive
in
\mathsf{Mot}(S)
is a direct summand of a motive of the form
\mathfrak{h}(X/S)
, where
X\rightarrow S
in
\mathsf{SmProj}(S)
has relative dimension zero or equivalently, is finite étale.
When
S
is the spectrum of a field, Artin motives are the same as Galois representations on finite-dimensional
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
-vector spaces; we give a similar (presumably well known) description in the relative setting.
Let
f\colon\tilde{S}\rightarrow S
be a connected finite étale cover with Galois group
G={\rm Aut}(\tilde{S}/S)
.
We say an Artin motive
M
in
\mathsf{Mot}(S)
is
trivialized by
f
if
f^{*}M\simeq\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{S}}^{\oplus n}
for some
n\in{\rm\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
.
In that case the
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
-vector space
{\rm Hom}(\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{S}},f^{*}M)
is
n
-dimensional (since
\tilde{S}
is connected) and has a natural
G
-action, by the same reasoning as §
4.4
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Every Artin motive in
\mathsf{Mot}(S)
is trivialized by some connected finite étale Galois cover
f\colon\tilde{S}\rightarrow S
.
Fixing such an
f
, the assignment
M\mapsto V_{M}:={\rm Hom}(\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{S}},f^{*}M)
induces an equivalence between the full subcategory of Artin motives in
\mathsf{Mot}(S)
trivialized by
f
and the category of
{\rm Aut}(\tilde{S}/S)
-representations on finite-dimensional
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
-vector spaces.

Proof.

(Sketch)
If
M
is a direct summand of
\mathfrak{h}(X/S)
, then
M
is trivialized by the compositum of the Galois closures of the connected components of
X
, justifying the first sentence.
Fully faithfulness of
M\mapsto V_{M}
follows from Lemma
4.1
and the fact that
{\rm Hom}(f^{*}M,f^{*}N)\rightarrow{\rm Hom}(V_{M},V_{N})
is an isomorphism when
\tilde{S}
is connected.
Moreover, again since
\tilde{S}
is connected
V_{\mathfrak{h}(\tilde{S}/S)}
is isomorphic to the regular representation of
G
.
Therefore essential surjectivity follows from the fact that every
G
-representation is a direct summand of copies of the regular representation.
∎

5.
Proof of main theorem

This section is the technical heart of the paper.

({\rm NS}_{\mathcal{P}}\otimes\mathfrak{h}^{1}(\mathcal{E}/S))^{\mu_{3}}\simeq\mathfrak{h}^{1}(\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}/S)

5.2.
Chow groups

Our next goal is to translate the isomorphism of Theorem
5.2
into a concrete statement about Chow groups.
We will compute
{\rm CH}^{2}
of
\mathfrak{h}^{3}(\mathcal{J}/S)^{\mu_{6}}
and
\mathfrak{h}^{1}(\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}/S)(-1)
(in the sense of §
4.6
) below.
To this end we will use the Beauville decomposition of the Chow groups of an abelian variety
[
3
]
, extended by Deninger–Murre to the relative setting
[
15
, Theorem 2.19]
.

Proposition 5.9

(Beauville decomposition)
.

Let
A/S
be an abelian scheme and
p\in{\rm\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
.
For
i\in{\rm\mathbb{Z}}
let

{\rm CH}^{p}_{(i)}(A;{\rm\mathbb{Q}}):=\{\alpha\in{\rm CH}^{p}(A;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})\mid(n)^{*}\alpha=n^{2p-i}\alpha\text{ for all }n\in{\rm\mathbb{Z}}\}.

Then
{\rm CH}^{p}_{(i)}(A;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
is nonzero for only finitely many
i
and we have a direct sum decomposition

(5.5)

\displaystyle{\rm CH}^{p}(A;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})=\bigoplus_{i\in{\rm\mathbb{Z}}}{\rm CH}^{p}_{(i)}(A;{\rm\mathbb{Q}}).

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
T
be an adequate scheme and
T\rightarrow S
a morphism.
Then there are isomorphisms:

(1)

{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathfrak{h}^{1}(\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}_{T}/T)(-1))=\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}(T)\otimes{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
.

(2)

{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathfrak{h}^{3}(\mathcal{J}_{T}/T)^{\mu_{6}})={\rm CH}^{2}_{(1)}(\mathcal{J}_{T};{\rm\mathbb{Q}})^{\mu_{6}}
.

Moreover if
T^{\prime}\rightarrow S
is another such morphism and if
f\colon T^{\prime}\rightarrow T
is an
S
-morphism that admits pullbacks (see §
4.3
) then the above isomorphisms are compatible with base change along
f
.

Proof.

(1)

We have
{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathfrak{h}^{1}(\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}_{T}/T)(-1))={\rm CH}^{1}(\mathfrak{h}^{1}(\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}_{T}/T))
. We will prove the stronger statement that if
X\rightarrow T
is an abelian scheme of relative dimension
1
then
{\rm CH}^{1}(\mathfrak{h}^{1}(X/T))=X(T)\otimes{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
.
This is the relative version of a classical curve computation
[
39
, Theorem 2.7.2]
; we briefly sketch the details.
Write
\Gamma_{e}\subset X\times_{T}X
for the graph of the zero section
e\colon T\rightarrow X
.
Then
\mathfrak{h}^{1}(X/T)=(X/T,[\Delta_{X/T}]-[\Gamma_{e}]-[^{t}\Gamma_{e}],0)
.
Using the equivalence between Cartier divisors and line bundles and unwinding the definition of
{\rm CH}^{1}(\mathfrak{h}^{1}(X/T))
, we see that it is exactly the
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
-tensor product of the set of isomorphism classes of line bundles on
X
, fiberwise of degree zero and rigidified along the zero section.
In other words,
{\rm CH}^{1}(\mathfrak{h}^{1}(X/T))=\mathbf{Pic}^{0}_{X/T}(T)\otimes{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
, where
\mathbf{Pic}^{0}_{X/T}
denotes the identity component of the Picard functor
[
10
, §8.1, Proposition 4]
.
Since
X
is a relative elliptic curve, the map
P\mapsto[P]-[e]
induces an isomorphism
X\simeq\mathbf{Pic}^{0}_{X/T}
.

(2)

By definition of the Chow–Künneth decomposition of Theorem
4.4
and the Beauville decomposition (
5.5
),
{\rm CH}^{p}(\mathfrak{h}^{i}(A/S))={\rm CH}^{p}_{(2p-i)}(A)
for every abelian scheme
A/S
and
i,p\in{\rm\mathbb{Z}}
.
Therefore
{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathfrak{h}^{3}(\mathcal{J}_{T}/T))={\rm CH}^{2}_{(1)}(\mathcal{J}_{T};{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
.
Since
{\rm CH}^{p}(M^{G})={\rm CH}^{p}(M)^{G}
for every finite group
G
acting on a motive
M
(see §
4.6
), the result follows from taking
\mu_{6}
-fixed points.

The statement about compatibility with pullbacks follows from the construction of the isomorphisms.
∎

.

Let
T

Then there exists an isomorphism of

5.3.
The image of the Ceresa cycle

The defining equation (
3.1
) of
\mathcal{C}
shows there exists a unique section
\infty\colon S\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
at infinity.
We view
\mathcal{C}
as a closed subscheme of
\mathcal{J}
using the Abel–Jacobi map based at
\infty
.
We define the
universal Ceresa cycle
to be the cycle

\kappa_{\infty}(\mathcal{C}):=[\mathcal{C}]-(-1)^{*}[\mathcal{C}]\in{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathcal{J}).

For every field-valued point
s\colon{\rm Spec}(k)\rightarrow S
, the pullback
s^{*}(\kappa_{\infty}(\mathcal{C}))\in{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathcal{J}_{s})
equals the Ceresa cycle
\kappa_{\infty}(\mathcal{C}_{s})
of the curve
\mathcal{C}_{s}
over
k
with respect to the basepoint at infinity defined in §
2.1
.
We will work with
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
-coefficients, so let
\kappa(\mathcal{C})
be the image of
\kappa_{\infty}(\mathcal{C})
in
{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathcal{J};{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
, and for each
s\in S(k)
let
\kappa(\mathcal{C}_{s})
be the image of
\kappa_{\infty}(\mathcal{C}_{s})
in
{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathcal{J}_{s};{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
.
The next lemma shows that, at least fiberwise,
{\rm CH}^{2}
of
\mathfrak{h}^{3}(\mathcal{J}/S)^{\mu_{6}}
naturally contains the Ceresa cycle.

Lemma 5.12
.

For every field
k
and
s\in S(k)
, we have
\kappa(\mathcal{C}_{s})\in{\rm CH}^{2}_{(1)}(\mathcal{J}_{s};{\rm\mathbb{Q}})^{\mu_{6}}={\rm CH}^{2}(\mathfrak{h}^{3}(\mathcal{J}_{s})^{\mu_{6}})
.

Proof.

Write
C=\mathcal{C}_{s}
and
J=\mathcal{J}_{s}
.
Since
J
is defined over a field, Beauville has shown
[
3
, Théorème and Proposition 3(a)]
that
{\rm CH}^{2}_{(1)}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
is zero unless
i\in\{0,1,2\}
.
So using (
5.5
) we may write
[C]=[C]_{0}+[C]_{1}+[C]_{2}
in
{\rm CH}^{2}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
, where
(n)^{*}
acts on
[C]_{i}
as
n^{4-i}
.
Therefore
\kappa(C)=[C]-(-1)^{*}[C]=([C]_{0}+[C]_{1}+[C]_{2})-([C]_{0}-[C]_{1}+[C]_{2})=2[C]_{1}\in{\rm CH}^{2}_{(1)}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
.
Since
\kappa(C)
is also
\mu_{6}
-invariant, we conclude the lemma.
∎

Let
\kappa(\mathcal{C})_{(1)}
be the component in
{\rm CH}^{2}_{(1)}(\mathcal{J})
of
\kappa(\mathcal{C})
in the Beauville decomposition (
5.5
) of
{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathcal{J};{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
.
(It might very well be that
\kappa(\mathcal{C})=\kappa(\mathcal{C})_{(1)}
, but this is not needed in our analysis.)
Since
\kappa(\mathcal{C})
is
\mu_{6}
-invariant,
\kappa(\mathcal{C})_{(1)}\in{\rm CH}^{2}_{(1)}(\mathcal{J})^{\mu_{6}}={\rm CH}^{2}(\mathfrak{h}^{3}(\mathcal{J}/S)^{\mu_{6}})
.

Choose for the remainder of the paper a collection of isomorphisms
\Phi_{T}
satisfying the conclusions of Corollary
5.11
.
Define

\sigma_{0}:=\Phi_{S}(\kappa(\mathcal{C})_{(1)})\in\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}(S)\otimes{\rm\mathbb{Q}}.

Let
\text{den}(\sigma_{0})
be the smallest positive integer
d
such that
d\cdot\sigma_{0}\in\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}(S)
.
Let
\sigma=\text{den}(\sigma_{0})\cdot\sigma_{0}
, a section of the abelian scheme
\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}\rightarrow S
.

Corollary 5.13
.

For every field
k
and
s\in S(k)
,
\sigma(s)\in\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}_{s}(k)
is torsion if and only if
\kappa_{\infty}(\mathcal{C}_{s})\in{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathcal{J}_{s})
is torsion.

Proof.

Follows from Corollary
5.11
and Lemma
5.12
.
∎

For technical reasons when relating the torsion orders of
\kappa_{\infty}(\mathcal{C}_{s})
and
\sigma(s)
, it will be useful to have an integral lift of a multiple of
\kappa(\mathcal{C})_{(1)}
, in the following sense:

Lemma 5.14
.

There exists a class
\kappa_{{\rm\mathbb{Z}}}\in{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathcal{J})
and an integer
N\geq 1
such that the image of
\kappa_{{\rm\mathbb{Z}}}
in
{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathcal{J};{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
is
N\cdot\kappa(\mathcal{C})_{(1)}
and such that
s^{*}(\kappa_{{\rm\mathbb{Z}}})=N\cdot\kappa_{\infty}(\mathcal{C}_{s})
for every field-valued point
s\colon{\rm Spec}(k)\rightarrow S
.

Proof.

Write
\kappa(\mathcal{C})=\kappa(\mathcal{C})_{(1)}+\kappa^{\prime}
in
{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathcal{J};{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
, where
\kappa^{\prime}
is the sum of the other components in the Beauville decomposition (
5.5
).
There exist an integer
N_{1}\geq 1
and classes
\alpha,\beta\in{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathcal{J})
whose images in
{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathcal{J};{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
are
N_{1}\kappa(\mathcal{C})_{(1)}
and

respectively.
Since the class
N_{1}\kappa(\mathcal{C})-\alpha-\beta
is zero in
{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathcal{J};{\rm\mathbb{Q}})

such that
N_{1}N_{2}\kappa(\mathcal{C})=N_{2}\alpha+N_{2}\beta

such that

for every field-valued point

.

.

such that

∎

5.4.
Identifying
\sigma

Our next goal is to explicitly identify the section
\sigma
of
\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}\rightarrow S
, at least up to integer multiples.
The equation
y^{2}=x^{3}+4b(a^{2}-4b)^{2}
allows us to define
{\rm\mathbb{G}}_{m}
-actions on
\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}
and

by the formulas
\lambda\cdot(x,y)=(\lambda^{2}x,\lambda^{3}y)
and
\lambda\cdot(a,b)=(\lambda a,\lambda^{2}b)
.
With these actions, the morphism
\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}\rightarrow S
is
{\rm\mathbb{G}}_{m}
-equivariant.
Write
\mathrm{MW}^{{\rm\mathbb{G}}_{m}}(\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}/S)
for the set of
{\rm\mathbb{G}}_{m}
-equivariant sections of
\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}\rightarrow S
.
It is an abelian group, the
{\rm\mathbb{G}}_{m}
-equivariant Mordell–Weil group of
\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}/S
.

Lemma 5.15
.

\mathrm{MW}^{{\rm\mathbb{G}}_{m}}(\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}/S)
is free of rank
1
, generated by the section
\mathcal{Q}:=((a^{2}-4b),a(a^{2}-4b))
.

Proof.

Let
T
be the closed subscheme of
S
given by setting
a=1
.
Restricting sections to
T
induces an injection
\mathrm{MW}^{{\rm\mathbb{G}}_{m}}(\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}/S)\hookrightarrow\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}(T)
, so it suffices to prove that
\mathcal{Q}|_{T}
generates the Mordell–Weil group of the elliptic surface
\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}|_{T}\rightarrow T
.

Let
\pi\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow{\rm\mathbb{P}}^{1}_{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
be the minimal regular model of
\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}_{T}\rightarrow T={\rm\mathbb{P}}^{1}_{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}\setminus\{0,1/4,\infty\}
.
Using Tate’s algorithm, we see that the three singular fibers above

have Kodaira type
\text{\rm II},\text{\rm IV}
and
I
{}_{0}^{*}
respectively.
By
[
45
, Lemma 7.8]
, there are no nonzero torsion sections of
\pi
.
By the Shioda–Tate formula
[
45
, Theorem 6.3, Proposition 6.6 and §8.8]
, the Mordell–Weil group of
\mathcal{X}_{\bar{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}}\rightarrow{\rm\mathbb{P}}^{1}_{\bar{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}}
is free of rank
2
.
The
\mu_{3}
-action on
\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}
shows that it is also a
{\rm\mathbb{Z}}[\omega]
-module, necessarily of rank
1
.
Taking Galois invariants, it follows that the Mordell–Weil group of
\mathcal{X}\rightarrow{\rm\mathbb{P}}^{1}_{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
is free of rank
1
over
{\rm\mathbb{Z}}
.
To show that it is generated by (the closure of)
\mathcal{Q}|_{T}
, it suffices to find a single
b\in T({\rm\mathbb{Q}})
for which
\mathcal{Q}_{b}
is primitive in
\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}_{b}({\rm\mathbb{Q}})
, i.e. not divisible by
n
for every

.
One may
check
that this holds for

.
∎

Theorem 5.16
.

There exists an integer
N\geq 1
such that

.

Proof.

The section
\sigma\in\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}(S)
is
{\rm\mathbb{G}}_{m}
-equivariant.
Moreover by our concrete computation with

We conclude using Lemma

.
∎

5.5.
Proof of the main theorem

Putting everything together, we prove the following theorem which implies Theorem
1.1
of the introduction.

Theorem 5.17
.

Let
k
be a field of characteristic
\neq 2,3
and let
C/k
be a smooth projective curve with equation
y^{3}=x^{4}+ax^{2}+b
and Jacobian
J
.
Then the Ceresa cycle
\kappa_{\infty}(C)\in{\rm CH}^{2}(J)
is torsion if and only if
\mathcal{Q}_{(a,b)}=((a^{2}-4b),a(a^{2}-4b))
is a torsion point of the elliptic curve

.

Moreover, the quotient of the torsion orders
\ord(\kappa_{\infty}(C))/\ord(\mathcal{Q}_{a,b})
, whenever it is defined, takes finitely many values when varying over all
k
and
C
as above.

Proof of Theorem
5.17
.

The curve
C
is isomorphic to the pullback
\mathcal{C}_{s}
of
\mathcal{C}\rightarrow S
along the
k
-point
s=(a,b)\colon{\rm Spec}(k)\rightarrow S
.
By Corollary
5.13
,
\kappa_{\infty}(C)
is torsion if and only if
\sigma(s)\in\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}_{s}(k)
is torsion.
By Theorem
5.16
,
\sigma(s)
is a multiple of
\mathcal{Q}_{s}=\mathcal{Q}_{(a,b)}
, so
\sigma(s)
is torsion if and only if
\mathcal{Q}_{s}=(a^{2}-4b,a(a^{2}-4b))
is torsion.
Tracing through these equivalences and using Lemma

6.
Algebraic triviality and the Griffiths group

Let
C
be a curve over a field
k
and
J
be its Jacobian. The Griffiths group of
1
-cycles on
J
is the group
{\rm Gr}_{1}(J)={\rm Gr}^{g-1}(J)={\rm CH}_{1}(J)_{0}/A_{1}(J)
, where
A_{1}(J)
is the subgroup of algebraically trivial
1
-cycles. The very general Ceresa cycle (of a genus
g\geq 3
curve over
{\rm\mathbb{C}}
) is known to be of infinite order in
{\rm Gr}_{1}(J)

[
40
]
. In particular, the Ceresa cycle of a very general curve is not algebraically trivial, nor is any multiple of it. In this section we show that this fails for bielliptic Picard curves. We also discuss the Beilinson–Bloch conjectures in this context, and show that they imply that
{\rm Gr}^{2}(J)
is finite for certain bielliptic Picard curves.

6.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.5

We keep the notation of §
5.1
. Theorem
1.5
follows from the following proposition.
It essentially follows from the fact that homological and algebraic equivalence on a curve coincide.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\kappa_{{\rm\mathbb{Z}}}\in{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathcal{J})
be a class and
N_{1}\geq 1
an integer satisfying the conclusions of Lemma
5.14
.
Then there exists an integer
N\geq 1
, a smooth projective relative curve
\mathcal{X}\rightarrow S
with two sections
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
and a cycle
\mathcal{Z}
on
{\rm CH}^{1}(\mathcal{J}\times_{S}X)
, with the property that
\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma_{1}}=N\cdot\kappa_{{\rm\mathbb{Z}}}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma_{2}}=0
.

This proposition implies Theorem
1.5
, since for every
k
-point
s\colon{\rm Spec}(k)\rightarrow S
, corresponding to the bielliptic Picard curve
\mathcal{C}_{s}=C
with
X=\mathcal{X}_{s}
,
p_{i}=\sigma_{i}(s)
and
Z=\mathcal{Z}_{s}
, the cycle
Z\in{\rm CH}^{1}(J\times_{k}X)
has the property that
Z_{p_{1}}=s^{*}(\kappa_{{\rm\mathbb{Z}}})=NN_{1}\cdot\kappa_{\infty}(C)
and
Z_{p_{2}}=0
.
By definition, this means that
(NN_{1})\cdot\kappa_{\infty}(C)
is algebraically trivial.

Proof.

We will use the isomorphisms
\Phi
from Corollary
5.11
.
Recall from §
5.3
that there exists an integer
N_{2}\geq 1
such that
\Phi_{S}(N_{2}\cdot\kappa(\mathcal{C})_{(1)})=\sigma\in\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}(S)
.
We can deform the section
\sigma
of
\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}
to the identity section
\infty\colon S\rightarrow\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}
.
More precisely, there exists a smooth projective relative curve
\mathcal{X}\rightarrow S
, a section
\mathcal{V}\in\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}(\mathcal{X})
and sections
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
of
\mathcal{X}\rightarrow S
, such that
\mathcal{V}_{\sigma_{1}}=\sigma
and
\mathcal{V}_{\sigma_{2}}=\infty
.
In fact,
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}
with
\sigma_{1}=\sigma
,
\sigma_{2}=\infty
and with
\mathcal{V}=\Delta_{\mathcal{X}/S}\subset\mathcal{X}\times_{S}\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{E}^{\Delta}\times_{S}\mathcal{X}
will do.
There exists an

such that
\Phi_{\mathcal{X}}^{-1}(N_{3}\cdot\mathcal{V})\in{\rm CH}^{2}_{(1)}(\mathcal{J}\times_{S}\mathcal{X};{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
is the image of an integral cycle

6.2.
Beilinson–Bloch conjectures

We briefly describe some consequences of the Beilinson–Bloch conjecture
[
6
,
8
]
in our setting. Let
X
be a smooth projective geometrically integral variety over a number field
k
.
Fix a prime
\ell
and denote the
\ell
-adic cohomology
\HH^{i}(X_{\bar{k}},{\rm\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})
simply by
\HH^{i}(X)
.
The conjecture predicts that for each
i\in{\rm\mathbb{Z}}
, the group of homologically trivial cycles
{\rm CH}^{i}(X)_{\mathrm{hom}}
is finitely generated, the
L
-function
L(\mathfrak{h}^{2i-1}(X),s)
has analytic continuation and functional equation, and

\mathrm{rk}\,({\rm CH}^{i}(X)_{\mathrm{hom}})=\ord_{s=i}L(\mathfrak{h}^{2i-1}(X),s).

Similarly, if
\epsilon\in{\rm CH}^{\dim X}(X\times X)
is an idempotent cutting out a direct summand of the Chow motive
\mathfrak{h}(X)
, then

\mathrm{rk}\,(\epsilon\circ{\rm CH}^{i}(X)_{\mathrm{hom}})=\ord_{s=i}L(\epsilon\cdot\mathfrak{h}^{2i-1}(X),s).

Let
C=C_{a,b}
be a bielliptic Picard curve over
k
with Jacobian
J
. By Theorem
5.1
and Proposition
5.10
, the Beilinson–Bloch conjecture for the motive
\mathfrak{h}^{3}(J)^{\mu_{6}}
reads:

Conjecture 6.2
.

\mathrm{rk}\,{\rm CH}^{2}_{(1)}(J)^{\mu_{6}}=\ord_{s=1}L(\mathfrak{h}^{1}(E^{\Delta}),s).

The (weak) Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture states that
\ord_{s=1}L(\mathfrak{h}^{1}(E^{\Delta}),s)={\rm rk}\,E^{\Delta}(k)
.
Therefore the Beilinson–Bloch conjecture for
\mathfrak{h}^{3}(J)^{\mu_{6}}
is equivalent to the weak Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture for
E^{\Delta}
(by Corollary
5.11
).

6.3.
The Griffiths group

As detailed in
[
9
, 1.3]
, the Beilinson–Bloch conjecture should also be compatible with the coniveau filtrations on
{\rm CH}^{i}(X)
and
\HH^{2i-1}(X)(i)
. In our setting, this allows us to predict the rank of the Griffiths group
{\rm Gr}^{2}(J)
, at least in some cases; we briefly sketch the details below.
To formulate it, we use the decomposition
\mathfrak{h}^{2}(P)=\mathfrak{h}_{\mathrm{alg}}^{2}(P)+\mathfrak{h}_{\mathrm{tr}}^{2}(P)
from
[
26
]
which has the property that the
\ell
-adic realization of
\mathfrak{h}^{2}_{{\mathrm{tr}}}(P)
is

V:=({\rm NS}(P_{\bar{k}})(-1)\otimes{\rm\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})^{\perp}\subset\HH^{2}(P),

the transcendental part of
\HH^{2}(P)
.

Remark 6.3
.

If
P
does not have CM then
V
is three dimensional. If
P
is moreover of
{\rm GL}_{2}
-type, then
V
is (up to twist) the symmetric square of the associated 2-dimensional
{\rm Gal}_{k}
-representation. If
P
has CM by an imaginary quadratic field
K
, then
V
is
2
-dimensional and the
L
-function
L(V,s)
is a product of Hecke
L
-functions attached to the field
K
.

Using the isogeny decomposition
J\sim P\times E
and the Künneth formula, one checks that the first graded piece
\mathrm{gr}^{0}\HH^{3}(J)(2)
in the coniveau filtration is a nonzero quotient of
V\otimes\HH^{1}(E)(2)
. Define

\mathfrak{h}_{\mathrm{Gr}}^{3}(J):=\mathfrak{h}_{{\mathrm{tr}}}^{2}(P)\otimes\mathfrak{h}^{1}(E).

The following prediction then follows from
[
9
, 1.3]
.

Conjecture 6.4
.

Let
C
be a bielliptic Picard curve over a number field
k
. Then

\mathrm{rk}\,{\rm Gr}^{2}(J)\leq\ord_{s=2}L(\mathfrak{h}_{\mathrm{Gr}}^{3}(J),s),

with equality if
V\otimes\HH^{1}(E)
is an irreducible
{\rm Gal}_{k}
-representation.

Assuming this conjecture, we exhibit below some new examples of nonhyperelliptic Jacobians over a number fields with finite Griffiths group. First we describe some known examples.

Example 6.5
.

Following up on
[
7
]
, Gross found two nonhyperelliptic Jacobians with finite Griffiths group (assuming the Beilinson–Bloch conjecture): the genus
7
Fricke-Macbeath curve and its genus
3
quotient
[
23
]
, both defined over
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
.

The other previously known example is a certain “special” bielliptic Picard curve. To put it in context, note that if
P
is defined over
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
and has CM (over
{\rm\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}
) by an imaginary quadratic field
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{d})\neq{\rm\mathbb{Q}}(\omega)
, then
L(\mathfrak{h}_{\mathrm{Gr}}^{3}(J),s)
is a Hecke
L
-function for the biquadratic CM field
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}(\omega,\sqrt{d})
.

Example 6.6
.

The curve
C_{0,1}\colon y^{3}=x^{4}+1
has CM by
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}(i)

[
32
, Example 6.15]
. In
[
35
, Theorem 1.3]
, Lilienfeldt and the second author show that the corresponding Hecke
L
-function has nonvanishing central value, and hence
{\rm Gr}^{2}(J_{0,1})
is conjecturally finite.

Using Theorem
3.4
and mod
p
point counts of
E
and
J
, one can compute the good Euler factors for the
L
-function
L(\mathfrak{h}^{3}_{\mathrm{Gr}}(J),s)
, even for non-CM bielliptic Picard curves
C_{a,b}
. These
L
-functions are not known to have analytic continuation in general but we can still compute their analytic rank in Magma assuming that they do.

Example 6.7
.

Using
[
2
,
47
]
, we have verified (conditional on the analytic continuation of the
L
-function) that for both
C_{6,-3}
and
C_{6,1}
, the central value
L(\mathfrak{h}^{3}_{\mathrm{Gr}}(J),2)
is nonzero. Hence
{\rm Gr}^{2}(J_{6,-3})
and
{\rm Gr}^{2}(J_{6,1})
are conjecturally finite groups.

Theorem
1.5
suggests that the behavior in Examples
6.6
and
6.7
is not so special, and we expect that many bielliptic Picard Jacobians over
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
have finite Griffiths group.
