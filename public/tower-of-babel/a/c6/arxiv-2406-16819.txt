Entropy as Noether charge for quasistatic gradient flow
Aaron Beyen, Christian Maes

Abstract
Entropy increase is fundamentally related to the breaking of time-reversal symmetry. By adding the ‘extra dimension’ associated with thermodynamic forces, we extend that discrete symmetry to a continuous symmetry for the dynamical fluctuations around (nonlinear) gradient flow. The latter connects macroscopic equilibrium conditions upon introducing a quasistatic protocol of control parameters. The entropy state function becomes the Noether charge. As a result, and following ideas expressed by Shin-ichi Sasa and co-workers, the adiabatic invariance of the entropy, part of the Clausius heat theorem, gets connected with the Noether theorem.



I
Introduction

Traditionally, within the context of macroscopic physics, gradient flow describes the relaxation to equilibrium. It appears in a wide variety of contexts, ranging from kinetic theory and hydrodynamics, over Landau theory to machine learning. Generally speaking, gradient flow is a dynamics of steepest descent where a cost function (
e.g.
in the form of a thermodynamic potential) is decreased in updating a physical condition represented as a point on a differential manifold.
Quasistatic
gradient flow connects a sequence of equilibrium conditions by slow variation of external parameters (
e.g.
pressure or temperature) in the cost function. Associated with such a reversible evolution appears a state function, the entropy or, more generally, a free energy.

II
Gradient flow

Gradient flow refers to a certain structure in the dynamics of relaxation to equilibrium,
[
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
. It is a dynamics of steepest descent where a cost function (or, free energy) is extremized. Gradient flow systems are numerous in physics, ranging from the Master equation for Markov jump processes to the spatially-homogeneous Boltzmann equation on the space of probability measures,
[
30
]
. We start with a couple of simple examples to illustrate the structure.

II.1
Examples

II.1.1
Linear flow: type-B dynamics

A standard example of (linear) gradient flow for a scalar density
\rho
in a regular volume
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},
with fixed conditions
\rho=\bar{\rho}
on the boundary
\partial\Lambda
, is given by

\dot{\rho}=\nabla\cdot\left(\chi(\rho)\nabla\left(\frac{\delta{\mathcal{F}}[\rho]}{\delta\rho}\right)\right)

(1)

in which the gradient structure is already explicit. It can be written as
a continuity equation of the form

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}=-\nabla\cdot j_{\rho},\ \text{ where }

(2)

\displaystyle j_{\rho}=\chi\left(\rho\right)\,F,\quad F=-\nabla\,\mu,\quad\mu=\frac{\delta{\mathcal{F}}[\rho]}{\delta\rho}

(3)

We say that this example is “linear” because the current is proportional to the thermodynamic force
F
, for a local chemical potential
\mu
which is the variational derivative of a given free energy functional
{\mathcal{F}}[\rho]
.

That example includes the (even more linear) Smoluchowski equation when considering independent particles, each with position
x
subject to the overdamped dynamics

\dot{x}=-\gamma\,\nabla U(x)+\sqrt{2\gamma\,T}\,\xi

with confining potential
U(x)
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and for standard white noise
\xi
.
Their density
\rho
(in the limit of
N
identical independent copies) satisfies the Smoluchowski equation

\dot{\rho}=-\nabla\cdot j_{\rho},\quad j_{\rho}=-\gamma\nabla U\rho-\gamma T\,\nabla\rho

(4)

for
\chi(\rho)=\gamma\,\rho
and
{\mathcal{F}}(\rho)=\int\textrm{d}V\ \rho\ U+T\int\textrm{d}V\ \rho\log\rho
on some fixed connected volume.

Equations (
1
) and (
4
) are of the form

\dot{z}=M\,\textrm{d}S,\qquad\;\;M=D\,X\,D^{\dagger}

(5)

for a symmetric positive semi-definite operator
X
, with
D=-\nabla\cdot
representing minus the divergence,
D^{\dagger}=\nabla
the gradient and
M=-\nabla\cdot\chi\nabla
for a density-dependent mobility
\chi
(being a 3 × 3 symmetric non-negative matrix). The state function
S(z)
is an entropy for a closed and isolated system, but it becomes some other thermodynamic potential depending on the context for open systems. In (
1
), the state function
S=-{\mathcal{F}}
is minus the free energy functional
\mathcal{F}
.

In other examples, the operator
D
can be the identity operator (for a non-conserved variable), or a stoichiometry matrix (for chemical reactive systems) and its adjoint
D^{\dagger}
satisfies the relation
a\cdot Db=b\cdot D^{\dagger}a
.

In (
5
),
\textrm{d}S
denotes the derivative with respect to
z
. It is increasing in time along a trajectory
z_{t}
:

\dot{S}(z_{t})=\textrm{d}S(z_{t})\cdot\dot{z}_{t}=\textrm{d}S(z_{t})\cdot M\textrm{d}S(z_{t})\geq 0

where we used that
M
is positive semi-definite. The equilibrium state
z^{*}
is then defined as the one that maximizes
S
and pays a central role in the rest of the paper.
Mathematically it makes sense to think of
z
as a point of a Riemannian manifold, moving
by steepest descent of the functional
-S
. The descent is measured in a metric provided by
the operator
X
, which is in general related to the physical mobility.

This paper uses an extension of gradient flow (
5
) to nonlinear evolutions
[
31
,
25
,
32
,
27
]
, which appear
e.g.
in the context of Markov jump processes where the relation between force and current is indeed nonlinear. The next example of the Curie-Weiss (mean-field Ising) model is of that type.

II.1.2
Nonlinear flow: Curie-Weiss dynamics

A Curie-Weiss relaxational dynamics for the magnetization
m\in[-1,1]
is given by the evolution

\displaystyle\dot{m}=2\nu\sqrt{1-m^{2}}\,\sinh\left(-\beta{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}(m)\right),\qquad\beta{\mathcal{F}}(m)=-\beta\phi(m)-s(m)

(6)

for some rate
\nu>0
, inverse temperature
\beta
, energy
-\phi(m)
, and mixing entropy

s(m)=-\left[\frac{1+m}{2}\log\left(\frac{1+m}{2}\right)+\frac{1-m}{2}\log\left(\frac{1-m}{2}\right)\right]

(7)

The prefactor
\chi(m)=2\nu\sqrt{1-m^{2}}\geq 0
decides the rate at which the Landau free energy function
{\mathcal{F}}
is decreasing in time along a curve
m_{t}
solving (
6
) :

\frac{\textrm{d}{\mathcal{F}}}{\textrm{d}t}(m_{t})={\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}(m_{t})\dot{m}_{t}=\nu\,{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}(m_{t})\chi(m_{t})\sinh\left(-\beta{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}(m_{t})\right)\leq 0

That example stands for a larger class of mean-field systems; see
e.g.
,
[
33
,
34
]
. We come back to it in Appendix
A
. The point here is that (
6
) is an example of a nonlinear gradient flow, announcing the general structure introduced in the next section.

II.2
General Setup

The notion of thermal equilibrium requires a physical coarse-graining in terms of a (set of physically well-chosen) macroscopic variable(s). Similar to (
5
) for the linear gradient flow, we denote them by
z
(matter densities
\rho
in
II.1.1
, magnetization
m
in Example
II.1.2
,
etc
.) and represent them as points on a smooth manifold which we do not need to make explicit. We assume that
z
has even parity under time-reversal, such that the corresponding current (time-change of
z
or flux of particle or energy density) is odd.

To describe gradient flow, we need a state function
S_{\lambda}(z)
depending on a set of control parameters
\lambda
. Here,
S_{\lambda}
might be an entropy, minus a free energy or some other thermodynamic potential depending on the context,
etc
. We write
\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}
for the (possibly functional) derivative of
S_{\lambda}
with respect to
z
.
The corresponding (nonlinear) gradient flow for
z
is

\dot{z}=Dj_{z,\lambda},\qquad j_{z,\lambda}~=\partial_{f}\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}(D^{\dagger}\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}/2;z)

(8)

with
D,D^{\dagger}
as before, and for a convex functional
f\mapsto\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}(f;z)
where
f
stands for a thermodynamic force (cotangent vector). Its (Fréchet) derivative with respect to
f
is denoted as
\partial_{f}\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}
and gives the current
j_{z,\lambda}
. We refer to
[
27
,
35
,
36
,
28
,
31
,
26
]
for more details.

In Example
II.1.2
, the operators
D=D^{\dagger}
are the identity, the states
z
are the values of the magnetization
m\in[-1,+1]
and the evolution (
6
) can be written as

\displaystyle\dot{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{f}\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}(S_{\lambda}^{\prime}(m)/2;m)\quad\text{ for }S_{\lambda}(m)=-\beta{\mathcal{F}}(m)\;\text{ and }

\displaystyle\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}(f,m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu\sqrt{1-m^{2}}\left(\cosh(2f)-1\right)

We note that the linear version (
5
) (and thus also Example
II.1.1
) can be rewritten with
\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}(f;z)=f\cdot X_{\lambda}f
such that
\partial_{f}\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}(f;z)=2X_{\lambda}f
for a symmetric positive semi-definite operator
X_{\lambda}
.

In general, the functional
\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}
should satisfy the following conditions to yield a meaningful gradient flow,
[
27
,
28
]
:

•

min
f

\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}(f;z)=\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}(0;z)=0

•

\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}(f;z)
is differentiable and
strictly
convex in its first argument,
i.e.
,

\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}(tf+(1-t)g;z)<t\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}(f)+(1-t)\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}(g)\quad\forall f,g,\quad t\in(0,1)

•

\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}
is symmetric in
f
,
i.e.

\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}(-f;z)=\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}(f;z)

It then follows that

\displaystyle\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}(f;z)\geq

\displaystyle\ \psi^{\star}_{\lambda}(0;z)=0\quad\forall f,\qquad\text{ and }

(9)

\displaystyle\partial_{f}\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}(f;z)

\displaystyle=0\iff f=0

(10)

i.e.
,
f=0
is the
unique
minimizer of
\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}(f;z)
. Moreover, as a consequence of (
9
), under a curve
z_{t}
satisfying the dynamics (
8
),

\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t}S_{\lambda}(z_{t})=\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}(z_{t})\cdot\dot{z}_{t}=2~D^{\dagger}\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}(z_{t})/2\cdot\partial_{f}\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}(D^{\dagger}\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}(z_{t})/2;z_{t})\geq 0,

(11)

which implies the monotonicity of
S_{\lambda}(z)
in time.
The (unique) maximum
z^{*}_{\lambda}
of
S_{\lambda}
(for a given
\lambda
) has
j_{z^{*}_{\lambda}}=0
and represents the unique
equilibrium state
,

\frac{\partial S_{\lambda}}{\partial z}(z^{*}_{\lambda})=0,\qquad S_{\lambda}(z)\leq S_{\lambda}(z^{*}_{\lambda}),\qquad\frac{\partial S_{\lambda}}{\partial\lambda}(z_{\lambda}^{*})=0

(12)

The last condition indicates that the thermodynamic force conjugate to
\lambda
needs to vanish in equilibrium; a condition on the
\lambda-
dependence.

The original references for the above-sketched framework are

,

That requires time-dependent control parameters

III
Onsager example

The present section wishes to introduce the main concepts and steps
within the simplest possible Onsager and Onsager-Machlup setups,
[
35
,
36
,
37
]
. It starts from a stochastic dynamics that describes the fluctuations around a macroscopic evolution.

We have one macroscopic variable
z\in{\mathbb{R}}
that is subject to a fluctuating relaxational dynamics for times
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
,

\dot{z}=\gamma\frac{\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}}{\textrm{d}z}(z)+\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{N}}\xi

(13)

with “entropy”
S_{\lambda}(z)
, Onsager coefficient
\gamma>0
, standard white noise
\xi
and a large parameter
N
such as the number of components or the size of the system in terms of a typical intercomponent distance. The fact that we call
S_{\lambda}
an entropy may refer to the case where
z
denotes a time-dependent volume with thermally isolated walls subject to a certain imposed pressure
\lambda
. That is then basically the example of
[
22
]
. Other scenarios are possible where
S_{\lambda}
would for instance stand for minus a free energy instead.

Equation (
13
) is not (yet) a gradient flow due to the noise term, but becomes one in the macroscopic limit
N\uparrow\infty

\dot{z}=\gamma\frac{\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}}{\textrm{d}z}=D\ \partial_{f}\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}\left(D^{\dagger}\frac{\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}}{\textrm{d}z}\Big/2;z\right),\qquad\text{ with }\ D=1,\qquad\psi^{\star}_{\lambda}(f;z)=\gamma f^{2}

(14)

III.1
Action, Lagrangian and Hamiltonian

Using the fact that
\xi
in (
13
) has Gaussian statistics with unit variance, one easily obtains that the
probability
\mathbb{P}[\Gamma]\propto e^{-N{\mathcal{A}}(\Gamma)}
of a trajectory
\Gamma=((z_{t},\dot{z}_{t}),t\in[t_{1},t_{2}])
is governed by the
path-space action

\mathcal{A}[\Gamma]=-S_{\lambda}(z_{t_{1}})+\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\textrm{d}t\,L_{\lambda}(\dot{z}_{t};z_{t})

(15)

with “Lagrangian,”

L_{\lambda}(\dot{z};z)=\frac{1}{4\gamma}\left(\dot{z}-\gamma\frac{\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}}{\textrm{d}z}(z)\right)^{2}\geq 0

(16)

In (
15
), we assume that the initial value
z_{t_{1}}
gets sampled from the equilibrium distribution
\propto e^{NS_{\lambda}(z_{t_{1}})}
, while the Lagrangian characterizes the subsequent dynamics.

In this framework, the macroscopic equation of motion (
14
) corresponds to the trajectory that dominates the path probability
\mathbb{P}\propto e^{-N\mathcal{A}}
in the limit
N\uparrow\infty
,
i.e.
, it corresponds to solving
L(\dot{z},z)=0
for
\dot{z}
(
zero-cost flow
). Since the Lagrangian (
16
) is positive and strictly convex in
\dot{z}
, that also amounts to minimizing the Lagrangian (
16
) at fixed
z
,

0=\frac{\partial L_{\lambda}}{\partial\dot{z}}=\frac{1}{2\gamma}\left(\dot{z}-\gamma\frac{\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}}{\textrm{d}z}\right)

(17)

We observe that if (
14
) is satisfied, then also the Euler-Lagrange equation for (
16
) is verified

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{z}L_{\lambda}-\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t}\left(\partial_{\dot{z}}L_{\lambda}\right)\iff\ddot{z}=\gamma^{2}\,\frac{\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}}{\textrm{d}z}(z)\,\frac{\textrm{d}^{2}S_{\lambda}}{\textrm{d}z^{2}}(z)

(18)

but not
vice versa
. For what follows, Eq. (
18
) is not essential, but its importance in other settings such as for using Hamilton-Jacobi theory is clear,
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
, partially motivating the terminology of Lagrangian for the integrand (
16
).

For completeness, we mention here also that the minimization or variational principle for determining the typical and correct evolution can also be obtained from entropy and dissipation principles, at least for quadratic Lagrangians (Gaussian-type processes) such as (
13
). That is done via the Onsager dissipation functions or the Rayleighian and it amounts to a form of minimum or maximum entropy production principle; see e.g.
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
.

As explained in
[
44
,
45
]
, the Rayleighian corresponding to the dynamics (
13
) equals

R_{\lambda}(z,\dot{z})=\frac{1}{2\gamma}\dot{z}^{2}-\frac{\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}}{\textrm{d}z}\dot{z}=\Phi(\dot{z})-\dot{S}_{\lambda},\qquad\Phi(\dot{z})=\frac{1}{2\gamma}\dot{z}^{2}

Following Onsager’s principle, this function is minimized with respect to
\dot{z}
at fixed
z
,
i.e.
,
\frac{\delta R_{\lambda}}{\delta\dot{z}}=0
, to obtain the equations of motion. Here,
\Phi(\dot{z})
is called the dissipation function since it equals half of the work done to the surroundings per unit time. The Lagrangian
L
has a similar form to the Rayleighian
R

\displaystyle L_{\lambda}(\dot{z};z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\gamma}\left(\dot{z}-\gamma\frac{\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}}{\textrm{d}z}(z)\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{4\gamma}\dot{z}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}}{\textrm{d}z}(z)\dot{z}+\frac{\gamma}{4}\left(\frac{\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}}{\textrm{d}z}(z)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{L}(\dot{z})-\dot{S}_{\lambda}+\frac{\gamma}{4}\left(\frac{\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}}{\textrm{d}z}(z)\right)^{2}

The last term
\frac{\gamma}{4}\left(\frac{\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}}{\textrm{d}z}\right)^{2}
is not present in the Rayleighian, but since we minimise both at fixed
z
, they yield the same gradient flow equation. Consequently, the Lagrangian in the large deviation setup is similar, but not equivalent, to the Rayleighian in Onsager’s principle and both lead to the same equations of motion.

However, we want to emphasize that dynamical large deviation theory is broader and can also be applied to
e.g.
discrete Markov jump processes
[
46
]
and nonquadratic Lagrangians (see Section
IV
), which do not fall under Onsager’s principle. Moreover, the path-space action is not only used to derive the equations of motion but also allows calculating stationary probability densities
[
38
,
39
,
40
,
47
]
,
etc
.

The Legendre transform of
L_{\lambda}(z,\dot{z})
is the “Hamiltonian,”

H_{\lambda}(f;z)=\sup_{\dot{z}}\left\{f\dot{z}-L_{\lambda}(\dot{z};z)\right\}=\gamma\,f^{2}+\gamma f\,\frac{\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}}{\textrm{d}z}=\gamma\,f\,\left(f+\frac{\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}}{\textrm{d}z}\right)

The variable
f
is dual to the current
\dot{z}
, and represents a
thermodynamic force
. As (
14
) corresponds to the condition
L(\dot{z};z)=0
, it follows from the Legendre transform (see Section
IV
) that the
Hamiltonian zero-cost flow
becomes

\dot{z}=\partial_{f}H_{\lambda}(f=0;z)=\gamma\,\frac{\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}}{\textrm{d}z}(z)

(19)

which indeed gives the correct result. If this equation is satisfied, then also the more general Hamilton’s equations of motion are verified

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H_{\lambda}}{\partial f}(f;z)=\dot{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\gamma f+\gamma\frac{\textrm{d}S_{\lambda}}{\textrm{d}z}(z)

(20)

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial H_{\lambda}}{\partial z}(f;z)=\dot{f}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma f\,\frac{\textrm{d}^{2}S_{\lambda}}{\textrm{d}z^{2}}(z)

(21)

for
f\equiv 0
, but not
vice versa
.

III.2
Quasistatic analysis

We introduce a
slow time-dependence
in the control parameter
\lambda\to\lambda(\varepsilon t)=\lambda_{0}\,\cos(\varepsilon t)
for a small rate
\varepsilon>0
. For simplicity of illustration, we put
t_{1}=0,t_{2}=\tau/\varepsilon
in (
15
) , and choose as ‘entropy’ function

S_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)}(z)=-\frac{1}{2}\big(z-\lambda_{0}\cos(\varepsilon t)\big)^{2}

(22)

where the instantaneous equilibrium state follows

\,z_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)}^{*}=\lambda(\varepsilon t)=\lambda_{0}\cos(\varepsilon t)

The choice of entropy (
22
) looks arbitrary (as it is an example) but in fact, it represents the simplest setup, close-to-equilibrium, that allows deforming the equilibrium state slowly and doing an exact computation.

We solve the macroscopic equation

\dot{z}=\gamma\frac{\textrm{d}S_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)}}{\textrm{d}z}(z)=-\gamma\big(z-\lambda_{0}\cos(\varepsilon t)\big),\quad t\in[0,\tau/\varepsilon]

(23)

which we start from equilibrium
z_{0}=\lambda_{0}
with maximum entropy
S_{\lambda_{0}}(z_{0})=0
, after which the solution of (
23
) slowly deviates from it. An exact calculation yields

\displaystyle z^{(\varepsilon)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{0}\ e^{-\gamma t}+\lambda_{0}\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\gamma^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\cos(\varepsilon t)-e^{-\gamma t}\right)+\varepsilon\ \lambda_{0}\frac{\gamma}{\gamma^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}\sin(\varepsilon t)

\displaystyle\dot{z}^{(\varepsilon)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{0}\frac{\gamma\ \varepsilon^{2}}{\gamma^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\cos(\varepsilon t)-e^{-\gamma t}\right)-\varepsilon\ \lambda_{0}\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\gamma^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}\sin(\varepsilon t)

and it solves

L_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)}(\dot{z};z)=\frac{1}{4\gamma}\big[\dot{z}+\gamma\big(z-\lambda_{0}\cos(\varepsilon t)\big)\big]^{2}=0

which expresses the quasistatic motion (
23
) as the zero-cost flow for the Lagrangian
L_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)}(\dot{z};z)
of the path-space action as introduced in (
15
)–(
16
). Up to order
O(\varepsilon^{2}
), the solution can be written in the general form

\displaystyle z^{(\varepsilon)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\,z_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)}^{*}+\varepsilon\Delta z_{\varepsilon t}+O(\varepsilon^{2})=\lambda_{0}\cos(\varepsilon t)+\varepsilon\frac{\ \lambda_{0}}{\gamma}\,\sin(\varepsilon t)+O(\varepsilon^{2})

\displaystyle\dot{z}^{(\varepsilon)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\partial_{\lambda}z^{*}_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)}\dot{\lambda}(\varepsilon t)+O(\varepsilon^{2})=-\varepsilon\ \lambda_{0}\sin(\varepsilon t)+O(\varepsilon^{2})

Similarly, the “quasistatic Hamiltonian” equals
H_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)}(f,z)=\gamma\,f^{2}-\gamma\,f\,\big(z-\lambda_{0}\cos(\varepsilon t)\big)
, and (
23
) combined with
f=0
express the zero-cost flow in the corresponding Hamilton equation (
20
).

From substituting the zero-cost flow in (
22
), it immediately follows that

S_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)}(z^{(\varepsilon)}_{t})=S_{\lambda_{0}}(z_{0})-\varepsilon^{2}\frac{\lambda_{0}^{2}}{2\gamma}\sin^{2}(\varepsilon t)+O(\varepsilon^{3})

Therefore, trivially here, the
entropy
S_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)}
is conserved uniformly in time
t\in[0,\tau/\varepsilon]

We start in the Hamiltonian formalism where we consider a shift in the thermodynamic force

IV.1
Lagrangian and Hamiltonian

We summarize the properties we suppose about the Lagrangian in (
30

[
27
,
28
]
:

•

L_{\lambda}(j;z)\geq 0

•

L_{\lambda}(j;z)\text{ is {strictly} convex in its first argument, {\it i.e.},}

L_{\lambda}(c\,j+(1-c)\,j^{\prime};z)<c\,L_{\lambda}(j;z)+(1-c)\,L_{\lambda}(j;z),\quad\forall j,j^{\prime},\quad c\in(0,1)

(31)

•

L\text{ induces an evolution equation for }z\text{ by requiring zero-cost}:

\qquad\qquad L_{\lambda}(j_{z,\lambda};z)=0\iff\dot{z}=Dj_{z,\lambda},

(32)

and we refer to calculations as “on shell” when

\text{ and }\dot{z}\text{ are related in that way}
.

Therefore, for every

, the Lagrangian has a unique minimum

at

, inducing the gradient flow
\dot{z}=Dj_{z,\lambda}
. That (first-order) evolution is a distinguished subclass of the Lagrangian flow described by the (second-order) Euler-Lagrange equation

\partial_{z}L_{\lambda}(z_{t},j_{z,\lambda}(t))-\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t}\Big(\partial_{j}L_{\lambda}(z_{t},j_{z,\lambda}(t))\Big)=0,

(33)

if the initial conditions match.
To see this, note that when for all

is identically zero on shell), then

0=\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}z}\Big(L_{\lambda}(z,j_{z,\lambda})\Big)=\partial_{z}L_{\lambda}(z,j_{z,\lambda})+\partial_{j}L_{\lambda}(z,j_{z,\lambda})\ \partial_{z}j_{z,\lambda}

Since the Lagrangian is minimal at
j=j_{z,\lambda}
, one has
\partial_{j}L_{\lambda}(z,j_{z,\lambda})=0
such that (
34
) gives
\partial_{z}L_{\lambda}(z,j_{z,\lambda})=0
on shell. As both terms are zero, it immediately follows that also the Euler-Lagrange equation (
33
) is satisfied. Hence, a solution of the gradient flow
\dot{z}=Dj_{z,\lambda}
solves the Euler-Lagrange equation as well. Even though we do not need the second-order dynamics (
33
) in what follows, that result is still interesting and an important check since,
a priori
, one expects the Noether charge to only be conserved when the full Euler-Lagrange equations are satisfied.

The Lagrangian
L_{\lambda}
is related to the Hamiltonian
H_{\lambda}
through the usual Legendre
transform,

H_{\lambda}(f;z)=\sup_{j}\Big\{j\cdot f-L_{\lambda}(j;z)\Big\},\qquad L_{\lambda}(j;z)=\sup_{f}\Big\{j\cdot f-H_{\lambda}(f;z)\Big\}

(35)

where

, the variable dual to the current

, represents a thermodynamic force.

The properties (
31
)–(
32
) of the Lagrangian translate into

\displaystyle\hbox to0.0pt{\hss\textbullet\hskip 10.0pt}\text{$L_{\lambda}=0$ at its minimum}\Longrightarrow H_{\lambda}(0;z)=\sup_{j}\Big\{-L_{\lambda}(j;z)\Big\}=-\inf_{j}\Big\{L_{\lambda}(j;z)\Big\}=0

(36)

\displaystyle\hbox to0.0pt{\hss\textbullet\hskip 10.0pt}H_{\lambda}(f;z)\text{ is {strictly} convex in its first argument}

(37)

\displaystyle\hbox to0.0pt{\hss\textbullet\hskip 10.0pt}L_{\lambda}(j_{z,\lambda};z)=0\Longrightarrow\sup_{f}\Big\{j_{z,\lambda}\cdot f-H_{\lambda}(f;z)\Big\}=0

(38)

The last property can be further simplified by noting from (

is strictly concave in

and thus has at most one maximum. Since
j_{z,\lambda}\cdot 0-H_{\lambda}(0;z)=0
due to (

), the supremum (

only. Remembering that

also satisfies

, it follows that the zero-cost flow becomes

\dot{z}=Dj_{z,\lambda}\iff L_{\lambda}(j_{z,\lambda};z)=0\iff j_{z,\lambda}=\partial_{f}H_{\lambda}(f;z)\text{ and }f=0

(39)

The equation
j_{z,\lambda}=\partial_{f}H_{\lambda}(f;z)
is the analogue of the first Hamilton equation;
the condition
f=0
is a subclass of the second Hamilton equation

\dot{f}=-\partial_{z}H_{\lambda}(f;z)

since
\partial_{z}H_{\lambda}(0;z)=0
as follows from (
36
).
Summarizing, similar to how

satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equations, the relations

VI
Entropy as a Noether charge

VI.1
Hamiltonian formalism

We start with the expression of the path-space action in terms of the Hamiltonian,

\mathcal{A}=-S_{\lambda_{1}}(z_{t_{1}})+\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\textrm{d}t\ \big[f_{t}\cdot j(f_{t};z_{t})-H_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)}(f_{t};z_{t})\big]

VII
Conclusion

We have extended the relation between the entropy change
\Delta S_{\lambda}
and the (discrete) time-reversal transformation to a continuous symmetry for quasistatic gradient dynamics. That generalizes ideas and results first expressed and obtained by Sasa
et al.
, connecting the first part of the Clausius heat theorem with the Noether theorem.

The novelty of our approach is in considering the general large deviation structure for gradient flows, including the case of jump processes (leading to generalized gradient flow). That allows us to extend the results to pure relaxational processes including spin-flip or (chemical) reaction dynamics. We have illustrated that with Curie-Weiss and urn models.

The large deviation approach also sheds new light on the continuous symmetry: in the Hamiltonian version of the Noether argument, it is the shift in thermodynamic force that realizes the symmetry. That suggests the picture where the probability of macroscopic trajectories is parametrized by the time-symmetric and the time-antisymmetric part in the action. That antisymmetric part is governed by a dissipation (or Onsager) functional and basically multiplies currents (fluxes) with (thermodynamic) forces. Time reversal is implemented by a change in the force while keeping the time-symmetric part unchanged. We suggest seeing it as the analogue of a rotation in the two-dimensional plane to implement reflection (on the one-dimensional axis).

We believe that the results may prove interesting for
unifying
classical and quantum mechanical, stochastic, and general relativistic arguments for the existence of entropy as exact differential in reversible transformation, invariant under adiabatic (
i.e.
, no heat) conditions. That has no relation however with the Second Law (non-decreasing entropy) which here is assumed from the outset in the very framework of gradient flow.
