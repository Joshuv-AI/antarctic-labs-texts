Trisecting the 9 9 -vertex complex projective plane
Richard Evan Schwartz

Abstract
In this paper we will give a short and direct proof that Wolfgang Kühnel’s 9 9 -vertex simplicial complex 𝑪 𝑷 9 2 \mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}_{9} is homeomorphic to 𝑪 𝑷 2 \mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2} , the complex projective plane. The idea of our proof is to recall the trisection of 𝑪 𝑷 2 \mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2} into 3 3 bi-disks and then to see this trisection inside a symmetry-breaking subdivision of 𝑪 𝑷 9 2 \mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}_{9} . After giving the proof we will elaborate on the construction and sketch an explicit homeomorphism.



1
Introduction

A
k
-
simplex
is a
k
-dimensional convex polytope with

k+1
vertices. For
k=0,1,2,3
respectively, a
k
-simplex is usually called a

vertex
,
edge
,
triangle
,
tetrahedron
. When
k
is not important, a
k
-simplex is
just called a
simplex
.

A
simplicial complex
is
a finite collection
\cal C
of simplices, all in an ambient
Euclidean space, such that

•

If
S\in\cal C
and
S^{\prime}
is a sub-simplex of
S
then
S^{\prime}\in\cal C
.

•

If
S,T\in\cal C
then
S\cap T
is
either empty or in
\cal C
.

Informally, the simplices in a simplicial
complex fit together cleanly, without crashing through each other.
The
support

|{\cal C\/}|
of
\cal C
is the union
of all the simplices in
\cal C
. Often we blur the
distinction between
\cal C
and
|{\cal C\/}|
and think of
a simplicial complex as a union of simplices.

A simplicial complex may be described with no
mention of the ambient space containing it,
but there is always the understanding that
in principle one can find an isomorphic complex in some
Euclidean space. To give a pertinent example, let

\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}_{6}
be the quotient of the
regular icosahedron by the antipodal map. This simplicial complex has

6
vertices,
15
edges, and
10
faces.
One can reconstruct
\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}_{6}
in
\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}^{5}
by fixing some

5
-simplex
\Sigma\subset\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}^{5}
, the
convex hull of vertices
v_{1},...,v_{6}
, then
mapping
vertex
k
of
\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}_{6}
to
v_{k}
and extending linearly.

Figure 1:
\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}_{6}
, the
6
-vertex triangulation of
\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}
.

Figure 1 shows another incarnation of

\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}_{6}
. In this picture, the outer edges of the hexagon are
supposed
to be identified according to the labels.
The complex
\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}_{6}
is called a
6
-
vertex triangulation

of the real projective plane
\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}
because its support
is homeomorphic to
\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}
. This triangulation has
the fewest number of vertices amongst triangulations of
\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}
, so it is called a

of
\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}
. It is in fact the unique

\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}

-sphere.

The subject of this paper is
\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}_{2}^{9}
.

\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}_{9}^{2}

\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}
. Since [

work done trying to understand
\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}_{9}^{2}

\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}
. See the article by

[

] for a survey of these proofs.

].

The purpose of this paper is to give a new and very
nice proof that
\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}_{9}^{2}\cong\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}
. The basic idea of the proof
here is to recall
the trisection of
\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}
into
3
bi-disks, and then to
see this trisection inside a symmetry-breaking subdivision of

\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}_{9}^{2}
. The construction is perfectly compatible with an
easier version that works for
\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}_{6}
, so I will explain that as
well.

The picture developed here is related to the

10
-vertex triangulation
\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}_{10}
of
\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}
that
in [
BK
] is constructed by
building outward from
T_{7}^{2}
. Indeed Denis
Gorodkov, in a private communication, explained to me how
one can find a “path” from

\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}_{9}
to
\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}_{10}
using the subdivision idea and then something
akin to bi-stellar flips. (I’ll let Denis tell this story elsewhere
if he wants to, but see the end of §2 for a hint.)

My proof also has a close
kinship with the “red-white-blue discussion” in
§1.3 of the M.P.I.M. preprint by
Morin and Yoshida that is the precursor to
[
MY
] (and has the same title).
This discussion is, in turn, related to Figure 8
in [
KB
]. Morin and Yoshida describe the red-white-blue
discussion as a “topological insight” but they don’t
really push it forward into a proof. I think that my
picture is very similar, but clarified by the special subdivision.

The approach here possibly could shed light on
Gorodkov’s result that

\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}_{15}\cong\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}
. The same subdivision and
trisection ideas go through for
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}_{15}
almost

verbatim
, and I can see computationally that each of the
3

sub-complexes
is shellable and therefore PL homeomorphic to an
8
-ball.
However, the high dimensional topology involved in analyzing

\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}_{15}^{2}
makes a direct topological analysis of the whole complex
formidable. For instance, the sub-complex that plays the role of

T^{2}_{7}
has
288

6
-simplices. A key step in extending the proof
here to
\mbox{\boldmath{$H$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}_{15}
would be showing that this
288
-monster is homeomorphic to

(S^{3}\times S^{3}\times S^{3})/S^{3}
in a
3
-fold symmetric way.

2
The Real Case

\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}
is the
space of scale equivalence classes of nonzero
vectors in
\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}^{3}
.
We denote the equivalence class
of
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}^{3}

by
[x_{1}:x_{2}:x_{3}]\in\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}
.

We have the
trisection

\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}=\beta_{1}\cup\beta_{2}\cup\beta_{3}
,
where
\beta_{j}
is the
set where

\max(|x_{1}|,|x_{2}|,|x_{3}|)=|x_{j}|
.
Points in
\beta_{1}
may be written
uniquely in the form
[1:x_{2}:x_{3}]
, with

|x_{2}|,|x_{3}|\leq 1
. Thus

\beta_{1}
is a square. So are
\beta_{2}
and
\beta_{3}
.
Each intersection
\beta_{i}\cap\beta_{j}
is a pair of
opposite edges, and the triple intersection
is a union of the
4
points
[\pm 1:\pm 1:\pm 1]
.
If we interpret
\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}
as the
quotient of a cube by the antipodal map,
then the
3
quotient faces are
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}
.

The trisection has
3
-fold symmetry. The map

\Sigma:(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\to(x_{2},x_{3},x_{1})

permutes the sets
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}
.
In terms of the cube,
\Sigma

rotates around the appropriate long diagonal.

\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}_{6}^{2}
has a very similar

3
-fold symmetry: The permutation
S=(123)(456)
acts as
a rotational symmetry of
\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}_{6}^{2}
.

We add a new vertex
[123]
at the center of the triangle
(1,2,3)
,
and also new vertices
[12],[13],[23]
at the centers of the
corresponding edges.

Figure 2: A subdivision of
\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}_{6}
into
18
triangles.

Using the new vertices, we divide the central triangle of

\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}_{6}
into
6
triangles and we subdivide
each of the adjacent triangles in half.
The subdivision has
18=3\times 6
triangles, with each having
exactly one vertex from the set
\{1,2,3\}
.
For
j=1,2,3
we let
B_{k}
be the subset of
6
new
triangles having
k
for a vertex. The sets

B_{1},B_{2},B_{3}
are colored green, red, blue in
Figure 2.

This is now the trisection,
and there is a clear homeomorphism from
this subdivided complex to
\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}
which
maps
B_{j}
to
\beta_{j}
and conjugates
S
to
\Sigma
.

Incidentally, a related approach would be to add only
[123]
and
then to replace the sides
(1,2)
,
(2,3)
,
(3,1)
with the sides

([123],6)
,
([123],5)
and
([123],4)
. Gorodkov’s “path” from

\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}_{9}
to
\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}_{2}^{10}
is a more elaborate complex-number analogue of this.

The Smooth Trisection: Complex Case

The complex projective plane
\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}
is defined
just as
\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}
but with respect to the field
C

of complex numbers.
We denote points in
\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}

by
[z_{1}:z_{4}:z_{7}]
. The variable names
will line up with the notation for
\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}_{9}^{2}
.
We have the trisection

\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}=\beta_{1}\cup\beta_{4}\cup\beta_{7}
,
where
\beta_{j}
is defined just as in the real case,
using the complex norm in place of the absolute value.
This time,
\beta_{j}
is the product of
2
unit disks.
The bi-disks
\beta_{1},\beta_{4},\beta_{7}

have disjoint interiors and are permuted by the same
map
\Sigma
as defined in the real case.

The boundary
\partial\beta_{1}
is a
3
-sphere, and
it decomposes into the solid tori

\beta_{14}
and
\beta_{17}
. Here

\beta_{ij}=\beta_{i}\cap\beta_{j}
.
To see that
\beta_{14}
is a solid torus,
note that
\beta_{14}
consists
of points of the form
[1:u:z]
with

|z|\leq|u|=1
and is therefore the
product of the unit disk and the unit circle.
The
central torus

\beta_{147}=\beta_{14}\cap\beta_{17}=\beta_{1}\cap\beta_{4}\cap\beta_{7}
consists of points where

|z_{1}|=|z_{4}|=|z_{7}|
. We discuss
\beta_{147}
in more detail, with a
view towards seeing it inside
\mbox{\boldmath{$C$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}_{9}^{2}
.

Hexagonal Structure:
Let

\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}^{3}_{0}\subset\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}^{3}
denote the plane of points whose coordinates sum to
0
.
Let
H=\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}^{3}_{0}\cap[-1,1]^{3}
. The vertices of this regular hexagon
are the permutations of
(1,-1,0)
.
Let
\overline{H}
be the
flat torus obtained by identifying the opposite sides
of
H
by translations. The translation vectors are
the cyclic permutations of
\pm(1,1,-2)
.
The map

(x_{1},x_{4},x_{7})\to[x^{*}_{1}:x^{*}_{4}:x^{*}_{7}],\hskip 30.0ptx^{*}=e^{\frac{2\pi ix}{3}}

induces a homeomorphism
\overline{H}\to\beta_{147}
.
The main point behind this fact
is that
[1^{*}:1^{*}:(-2)^{*}]=[0^{*}:0^{*}:0^{*}]
, etc.
We equip
\beta_{147}
with the
metric which makes
\overline{H}\to\beta_{147}
an isometry.

Symmetries:

The
3
fixed points of
\Sigma
lie in

\beta_{147}
and correspond to the points
on
\overline{H}
represented by the center and vertices of
H
.
The fixed point set of coordinatewise complex conjugation, which
we call
\cal T
, is
\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}
. Note that

\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$P$}}^{2}\cap\beta_{147}=\{[\pm 1:\pm 1:\pm 1]\}
.
These points correspond to the center of
H
and to the
centers of the edges of
H
.

A Contractible Loop:

The line in
\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}_{0}^{3}
where
x_{1}=x_{4}
bisects
H
and contains
the midpoints of a pair of opposite sides. This line gives
rise to a geodesic loop in
\overline{H}
. See the loop

in Figure 4 below. The corresponding loop

is given by

.
The loop
\alpha_{14}
is
contractible in
\beta_{14}

\beta_{14}

consisting of points
[1:1:z]
with
|z|\leq 1
.
