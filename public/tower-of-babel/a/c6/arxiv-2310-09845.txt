Introduction

This article makes no claim to originality, other than, perhaps, the simple statement here called the
Abstract Maximum Principle
. Actually, the whole contents are strongly based on H. Sussmann’s and coauthors’ papers (in particular, on
[
3
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
4
]
), in which, in a much more general context, the set-separation approach is regarded as foundational for necessary conditions for minima.
So, rather than being the exposition of original material, this paper has mainly a pedagogical purpose. From the Abstract Maximum Principle it is possible to deduce several necessary conditions for both finite dimensional minimum problems and for optimal control problems. More in general, this Principle seems apt to capture some consequences of the geometric and topological idea of (possibly vector-valued) minimization in a parametrized problem.

Approximating cones

If
X
is a real vector space, a subset
C\subseteq X
is a
cone

if
c\in C\iff rc\in C
for every non-negative real number
r
. A
convex cone

C

is a cone
C
that is also a convex subset. Clearly, a subset
C
is a convex cone if and only if it is invariant by non-negative linear combinations, namely, if and only if, for every positive integer
q
and for all
q
-tuples
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{q})\in[0,+\infty)^{q}
,

(c_{1},\ldots,c_{q})\in C^{q}
, one has
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{q}\alpha_{1}c_{i}\in C
.

Definition

(Directional differentiability).
Let
n
,
m
be positive integers, and consider a point
\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and a real number
\delta>0
. Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a convex cone, and let
F:\hat{x}+C\cap B_{m}(\delta)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a continuous function. We say that
F
is differentiable at
\hat{x}
in the direction of
C
, with differential
L:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
if

F(\hat{x}+c)=F(\hat{x})+Lc+o(c),\quad\forall c\in C\cap B_{m}(\delta),

(1)

where
B_{m}(\delta)
is the closed ball of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with center 0 and radius
\delta
, and
o(\cdot):C\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes any function such that
\lim_{c\to 0}\frac{|o(c)|}{|c|}=0
.

Transversality of cones

Let
X
be a finite-dimensional, real vector space.

Definition 3.1
.

Let

{\mathbf{K}}_{1}
,
{\mathbf{K}}_{2}\subseteq X
be convex cones.

1.

We say that
{\mathbf{K}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{K}}_{2}
are transversal if

{\mathbf{K}}_{1}-{\mathbf{K}}_{2}:=\big\{k_{1}-k_{2}\ \ |\ (k_{1},k_{2})\in{\mathbf{K}}_{1}\times{\mathbf{K}}_{2}\big\}=X;

2.

we say that
{\mathbf{K}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{K}}_{2}
are strongly transversal,
if they are transversal and
{\mathbf{K}}_{1}\cap{\mathbf{K}}_{2}\neq\{0\}
.

Some examples in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

•

In
\mathbb{R}
, the only nontrivial cones are
[0,+\infty[
,
]-\infty,0]
, and
\mathbb{R}
. The cones
[0,+\infty[
and
]-\infty,0]
are not transversal, while all the other pairs of cones (
\neq\{0\}
) are strongly transversal.

•

In
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the cones
{\mathbf{K}}_{1}:=[0,+\infty[^{2}
and
{\mathbf{K}}_{2}:=]-\infty,0]\times[0,+\infty[
are not transversal: indeed,
{\mathbf{K}}_{1}-{\mathbf{K}}_{2}=[0,+\infty[\times\mathbb{R}
.

•

The cones
{\mathbf{K}}_{1}={\mathbf{K}}_{2}:=[0,+\infty[^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
are strongly transversal. Notice incidentally that, in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the cones
\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{1}:={\mathbf{K}}_{1}\times\{0\},\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{2}:={\mathbf{K}}_{2}\times\{0\}
are not transversal.

•

The cones
\hat{\mathbf{K}}_{1}=\hat{\mathbf{K}}_{2}:=\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
are not transversal.

•

In
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the cones
{\mathbf{K}}_{1}:=\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}
,
{\mathbf{K}}_{2}:=\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}
are transversal but they
are not
strongly transversal.
Notice that they are subspaces: actually this is the only case where transversality can differ from strong transversality, as shown by Proposition
3.1
below.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{K}}_{1}
,
{\mathbf{K}}_{2}\subseteq X
be convex cones. Then conditions i) and ii) below are equivalent:

i)

{\mathbf{K}}_{1},{\mathbf{K}}_{2}
are transversal;

ii)

either
{\mathbf{K}}_{1},{\mathbf{K}}_{2}
are strongly transversal or both
{\mathbf{K}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{K}}_{2}

are linear subspaces and
{\mathbf{K}}_{1}\oplus{\mathbf{K}}_{2}=X
.

Proof.

Condition i) follows from ii) by definition. To prove that i) implies ii), let us assume that
{\mathbf{K}}_{1},{\mathbf{K}}_{2}
are transversal but not strongly transversal, so

{\mathbf{K}}_{1}\cap{\mathbf{K}}_{2}=\{0\}
. Let us prove that
{\mathbf{K}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{K}}_{2}
are linear subspaces. Let
k\in{\mathbf{K}}_{1}
. By the transversality assumption we get the existence of

k_{1}\in{\mathbf{K}}_{1}
and
k_{2}\in{\mathbf{K}}_{2}
such that
k=k_{2}-k_{1}
, so that
k_{1}+k=k_{2}\in{\mathbf{K}}_{2}
and

k_{1}+k\in{\mathbf{K}}_{1}
. Since
{\mathbf{K}}_{1}\cap{\mathbf{K}}_{2}=\{0\}
it follows that
k_{1}+k=0
, so

-k=k_{1}\in{\mathbf{K}}_{1}
. Hence, for every
k\in{\mathbf{K}}_{1}
one has
-k\in{\mathbf{K}}_{1}
. Since
{\mathbf{K}}_{1}
is a convex cone, we deduce that
{\mathbf{K}}_{1}
is a linear subspace.
Of course, the same conclusion holds for
{\mathbf{K}}_{2}
as well. Therefore, since
{\mathbf{K}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{K}}_{2}
are linear subspaces, one has
{\mathbf{K}}_{1}+{\mathbf{K}}_{2}={\mathbf{K}}_{1}-{\mathbf{K}}_{2}=X
,
{\mathbf{K}}_{1}\cap{\mathbf{K}}_{2}=\{0\}
, that is
{\mathbf{K}}_{1}\oplus\mathbf{K}_{2}=X
.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

Let us anticipate that Proposition
3.1
will be crucial in the proof of the Abstract Maximum Principle (see Th.
5.1
), where, by construction, one of the two involved cones is not a subspace. Hence, by having proved that these two cones are not strongly transversal, one gets that they are not transversal (which eventually gives the Maximum Principle).

.

Proposition 3.2
.

If

(4)

(5)

Let us prove (

The only cones in

Definition 3.3
.

Two cones

\mathbf{K}_{2}\subseteq X

.

Let
{\mathbf{K}}_{1},{\mathbf{K}}_{2}\subseteq X
be convex cones. The following condititions are equivalent:

•

{\mathbf{K}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{K}}_{2}

{\mathbf{K}}_{1}

Assume that
{\mathbf{K}}_{1}

.

1

1

4.1
Directional Open Mapping results

Roughly speaking an
Open Mapping
result consists in the possibility of deducing that, given a map
F:X\to Y
, the local image
F(U)
of a neighbourhood
U
of
x\in X
of is enough ‘thick’, meaning that it contains an open subset of
Y
.
Actually, in Theorem
4.2
we will deal with a
directional
generalization of the classical Open Mapping theorem.

From the classical Inverse Map Theorem one obtains the following Open Mapping result:

Theorem 4.1

(
Open Mapping).

Let
n,m
be positive integers,
n\leq m
, and consider a point
\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and a real number
\delta>0
. Let
F:\hat{x}+B_{m}(\delta)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
C^{0}
function, differentiable at
\hat{x}
.

If the differential
dF(\hat{x})

is surjective,
then there exists
r>0
such that

F(\hat{x}+B_{m}(\delta))\supseteq{F(\hat{x})+B_{n}(r).}

In this case, one says that
F
is open at
\hat{x}
.

Proof.

Up to re-ordering the components of
x=(x^{1},\dots,x^{m})
and writing
{\mathbf{x}}^{1}
and
{\mathbf{x}}^{2}
in place of
(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})
and
(x^{n+1},\dots,x^{m})
, respectively, we can assume that
the matrix
\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial{{\mathbf{x}^{1}}}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{1},\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{2})
is non singular.
Therefore, by the Inverse Function Theorem, there exists
\eta\in]0,\delta]
such that the map

{\mathbf{x}^{1}}\mapsto F({\mathbf{x}^{1}},\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{2})
is a diffeomorphism from
\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{1}+B_{n}(\eta)
onto the open set
F\big(\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{1}+B_{n}(\eta),\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{2}\big)
.
In particular,
F\big(\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{1}+B_{n}(\eta),\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{2}\big)
is a neighbourhood of
F(\hat{x})
, so
we can choose
r>0
verifying
F\Big(\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{1}+B_{n}(\eta),\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{2}\Big)\supseteq F(\hat{x})+B_{n}(r)
. Hence

F(\hat{x}+B_{m}(\delta))\supseteq F\Big(\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{1}+B_{n}(\bar{\eta})\big),\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{2}\Big)\supseteq F(\hat{x})+B_{n}(r),

so the proof is concluded.

∎

This result is generalized in Theorem

where the surjectivity of the differential is replaced by the assumption that the image of a conic domain via a directional differential has nonempty interior.

.

(Directional Open Mapping )
If

\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

\delta>0

in the direction of

L:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

2

.

Let us point out that Theorem

Let us prepose a technical lemma to the proof of Theorem

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. Let
\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
R>\rho>0
, and let
\phi:\hat{x}+B_{n}(R)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a continuous map
\rho

|\phi(x)-x|\leq\rho,\quad\forall x\in\hat{x}+B_{n}(R).

Then,

\phi(\hat{x}+B_{n}(R)\big)\supset\hat{x}+B_{n}\big(R-\rho).

(6)

Proof.

Up to the translation
x\to x-\hat{x}
it is not restrictive to assume that
\hat{x}=0
.
Let us fix a point
y\in B_{n}(R-\rho)
and let us define a map

by letting

f_{y}(x):=x-\phi(x)+y\quad\forall x\in B_{n}(R).

By

\begin{array}[]{c}|f_{y}(x)|=|x-\phi(x)+y|\leq|x-\phi(x)|+|y|\\
\leq\rho+R-\rho=R\end{array}

hence

. By the arbitrariness of

∎
