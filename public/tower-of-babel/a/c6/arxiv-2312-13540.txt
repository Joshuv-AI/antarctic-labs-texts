Considering a Superposition of Classical Reference Frames Preprint: AIP/123-QED
Elliott Tammaro, H. Angle, E. Mbadu

Abstract
A ubiquitous feature of quantum mechanical theories is the existence of states of superposition. This is expected to be no different for a quantum gravity theory. Guided by this consideration and others we consider a framework in which classical reference frames may be in superposition relative to one another. Mirroring standard quantum mechanics we introduce a complex-valued wavefunctional, which takes as input the transformations between the coordinates, Ψ ⁡ [ x ⁡ ( x ′ ) ] \Psi[x(x^{\prime})] , with the interpretation that an interaction between the reference frames may select a particular transformation with probability distribution given by the Born rule — P ⁡ [ x ⁡ ( x ′ ) ] = probability distribution functional ≡ | Ψ ⁡ [ x ⁡ ( x ′ ) ] | 2 P[x(x^{\prime})]=\text{probability distribution functional}\equiv|\Psi[x(x^{\prime})]|^{2} . The cases of two and three reference frames in superposition are considered explicitly. It is shown that the set of transformations is closed. A rule for transforming wavefunctions from one system to another system in superposition is proposed and consistency with the Schrodinger equation is demonstrated.

I
Introduction

Quantum theories, by virtue of their linearity, include states of superposition. Superpositions are states that consist of a sum over (typically classically distinct) states, such as position, momentum, energy, et cetera. It is expected that a quantum gravity theory must also include superpositions. In the case of a quantum gravity theory that resembles classical general relativity so that the gravitational field remains a manifestation of spacetime curvature, states corresponding to superpositions of spacetime metrics must be considered. Interpreting the meaning of such states, which is an end goal, is an open question, which likely cannot be resolved without recourse to a particular quantum gravity theory

The reference frame is itself an extended system, which permits study of the investigated system from its platform. The reference frame has often been treated as a large (perhaps lab-sized) system, and accordingly, is strictly classical. However, the size of systems that exhibit quantum behavior has been steadily growing with the improvement of experimental technique. It is prudent to inquire about the possibility for a system under investigation to serve as a frame of reference in its own right. A treatment of the problem of a quantum observer making observations of an observer has also been considered in several approaches, including notably Everett’s relative state interpretation and relational quantum mechanics

To this end, we work in position space.

II
Two Classical Reference Frames in Superposition

Let O and O’ be two frames of reference. Classically, each frame has a set of coordinates (measuring rods) for establishing positions and clocks for recording times. The coordinates will be denoted in the usual way
x_{\alpha}=(x,y,z,t)
and
x^{\prime}_{\beta}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime},t^{\prime})
. Greek letter indices will run from 1 to 4, while Roman letter indices will run from 1 to 3. Unless otherwise stated, summation notation is in use. When there is no risk of confusion indices may not be written explicitly. There is a transformation between the
O
and
O^{\prime}
frames such that
x_{\alpha}=x_{\alpha}(x^{\prime})
. The transformation must, of course, be invertible so that
x^{\prime}_{\beta}=x^{\prime}_{\beta}(x)
.

We wish to consider that these two reference frames are in superposition with respect to one another. What should this mean? A system is in a superposition of “classical states” (states with well-defined properties such as position, momentum, energy, et cetera) when a wavefunction is introduced, which assigns a complex number (amplitude) to a set of classical states. For example, a particle is in a superposition of two classical position states,
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and
\mathbf{x}_{1}
, when its wavefunction,
\psi
, is such that

\psi\propto\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0})+\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{1}).

(1)

To mirror this notion of superposition while extending it to include superposed reference frames, the transformation between the reference frames should not be fixed, but instead replaced with a complex-valued function(al) on the space of transformations. We introduce just such an amplitude
\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}
and allow

\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}=\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}\left[x_{\alpha}(x^{\prime})\right].

(2)

Equation
2
is defined on the “forward" transformations
x(x^{\prime})
(we ignore coordinate indices here for clarity). As will be demonstrated it will take quantities from frame
O^{\prime}
to
O
. We expect that there should be a wavefunctional defined on the “reverse" transformation
x^{\prime}(x)
taking quantities from
O
to
O^{\prime}
. That is, there must be a wavefunctional
\Psi_{O^{\prime}O}\left[x^{\prime}_{\alpha}(x)\right]
, which is “inverse" to
\Psi[x_{\alpha}(x)]
. The precise sense in which these two wavefunctional can be said to be inverse to one another will be made clear in the next section.

This wavefunctional is, by its nature, a relational quantity. We cannot speak of a reference frame in superposition in any absolute sense. We may only state that a reference frame is in superposition relative to another frame. We give a physical interpretation that again mirrors the case of standard quantum mechanics. Two reference frames are in superposition when the the transformation rule between them is completely described by a wavefunctional on the space of transformations
\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}[x_{\alpha}(x^{\prime})]
. If the reference frames were to interact so as to ascertain a specific transformation then the physical interaction would drive the wavefunctional toward a delta functional, which selects one particular transformation. That is to say that if two reference frames were in superposition then a mutual interaction, or coupling, would, in the phrasing of standard quantum mechanics, collapse the superposition. There is an issue with this if the wavefunctional is to be seen as a functional of
x_{\alpha}(x^{\prime})
, because
x_{\alpha}(x^{\prime})
contains time as a coordinate and includes the time component of the transformation. If the interaction that led to collapse-like behavior occurred at a time
t_{1}
then the transformation function would supposedly be known for all time. If the coordinate systems are, however, again isolated then it is expected that they, by some dynamics, may again evolve to be superposition. This is inconsistent with the claim that the transformation was established at time
t_{1}
and remains valid for all times. We remedy this situation in this present work by considering superpositions of time-independent transformations. Then we have,

\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}\left[x_{\alpha}(x^{\prime}_{\beta})\right]\xrightarrow{}\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}\left[x_{i}(x^{\prime}_{j})\right],

(3)

where again Roman indices are restricted to
1,2,\text{and }3
.

\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}[x_{i}(x^{\prime}_{j})]\xrightarrow{interaction}\delta[x_{i}(x^{\prime}_{j})-x^{0}_{i}(x^{\prime}_{j})].

(4)

The probability density for the interaction to select
x_{i}(x^{\prime}_{j})
is, in accord with the Born rule,

\mathcal{P}\left[x_{i}(x^{\prime})\right]=\Psi^{*}_{OO^{\prime}}\left[x_{i}(x^{\prime}_{j})\right]\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}\left[x_{i}(x^{\prime}_{j})\right].

(5)

The path integral of
\mathcal{P}\left[x_{i}(x^{\prime})\right]
over a subset
S
of all possible transformations
x_{i}(x^{\prime})
would give the probability of the transformation, upon interaction, to be an element of
S
, in accord with the Born rule
Feynman, Hibbs, and Styer 2010
. That is,

\displaystyle\int_{S}\mathcal{D}x_{1}(x^{\prime})\mathcal{D}x_{2}(x^{\prime})\mathcal{D}x_{3}(x^{\prime})\mathcal{P}[x_{i}(x^{\prime})]=

\displaystyle\text{Probability }O\text{ and }O^{\prime\prime}\text{are related by a transformation in }S.

For clarity it is worthwhile to consider an explicit example. For simplicity assume the transformations between reference frames
O
and
O^{\prime}
are restricted to rotations, so that

x_{i}(x^{\prime}_{j})=R_{ij}x^{\prime j}.

(6)

Upon substituting equation
6
into equation
2
,
\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}\left[R_{ij}x^{\prime}_{j}\right]=\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}\left(R_{ij}\right)
. The wavefunctional is reduced to a function of the parameters of the rotation matrix
R_{ij}
.
In analogy to the state defined in
1
, consider a superposition of two possible rotations
R_{ij}(+\theta,\mathbb{\hat{n}})
and
R_{ij}(-\theta,\mathbb{\hat{n}})

\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}[x(x^{\prime})]=\delta\left(R_{ij}-R_{ij}(+\theta,\mathbf{\hat{n}})\right)+\delta\left(R_{ij}-R_{ij}(-\theta,\mathbf{\hat{n}})\right).

(7)

Where delta functions of rotation matrices vanish unless the argument vanishes, thereby enforcing
R_{ij}=R_{ij}(+\theta,\mathbb{\hat{n}})
or
R_{ij}=R_{ij}(-\theta,\mathbb{\hat{n}})
. Note that normalization of the state is ignored here for elegance.
Upon interaction between the reference frames the transformation between them will be
x_{i}=R_{ij}(+\theta,\mathbb{\hat{n}})x^{\prime}_{j}
with 50% probability and will be
R_{ij}(-\theta,\mathbb{\hat{n}})x^{\prime}_{j}
the remainder. This example will be further explored in the next section.

III
Three Observers in Superposition

When a third reference frame,
O^{\prime\prime}
, is introduced and the transformation from
O
to
O^{\prime}
as well as the transformation from
O^{\prime}
to
O^{\prime\prime}
is specified, then the transformation from
O
to
O^{\prime\prime}
is fixed by the action of composition. If
x^{\prime}(x)
takes coordinates from
O
to
O^{\prime}
and
x^{\prime\prime}(x^{\prime})
takes coordinates from
O^{\prime}
to
O^{\prime\prime}
, then the composition
x^{\prime\prime}(x^{\prime}(x))
takes coordinates from
O
to
O^{\prime\prime}
.
It is expected that if
O
and
O^{\prime}
are in superposition, and the wavefunctional
\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}[x(x^{\prime})]
is known, and that
O^{\prime}
and
O^{\prime\prime}
are also in superposition with a known wavefunctional
\Psi_{O^{\prime}O^{\prime\prime}}[x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})]
, then
O
and
O^{\prime\prime}
are also in superposition and that the wavefunctional
\Psi_{OO^{\prime\prime}}[x(x^{\prime\prime})]
may be calculated from
\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}[x(x^{\prime})]
and
\Psi_{O^{\prime}O^{\prime\prime}}[x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})]
. That is to say, there must be a rule for composing transformations when the systems are in mutual superposition. We do not seek a derivation from first principles but instead, as will be demonstrated, there seems to be only one natural choice.

Imagine that both
\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}[x(x^{\prime})]
and
\Psi_{O^{\prime}O^{\prime\prime}}[x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})]
are proportional to delta functionals that enforce particular transformations between
O
and
O^{\prime}
and
O^{\prime}
and
O^{\prime\prime}
. That is,

\displaystyle\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}[x(x^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta\left[x(x^{\prime})-x_{0}(x^{\prime})\right]

(8)

\displaystyle\Psi_{O^{\prime}O^{\prime\prime}}[x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta\left[x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})-x^{\prime}_{0}(x^{\prime\prime})\right]

(9)

The delta functionals enforce
x(x^{\prime})=x_{0}(x^{\prime})
and
x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})=x^{\prime}_{0}(x^{\prime\prime})
, where
x_{0}(x^{\prime})
and
x^{\prime}_{0}(x^{\prime\prime})
, are particular transformation functions. Note that the indices for the coordinates have been suppressed. These wavefunctionals do not represent a superposition. The transformations are fixed, and thus the transformation from
O
to
O^{\prime\prime}
is also fixed. Namely, the transformation from
O
to
O^{\prime\prime}
is formed from composition:
x(x^{\prime\prime})=x(x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime}))=x_{0}(x^{\prime}_{0}(x^{\prime\prime}))
. As a result it must be that

\Psi_{OO^{\prime\prime}}[x(x^{\prime\prime})]=\delta\left[x(x^{\prime\prime})-x_{0}(x^{\prime}_{0}(x^{\prime\prime}))\right]

(10)

Equation
10
enforces the known transformation from
O
to
O^{\prime\prime}
. There is seemingly only one way that equation
10
can be expressed in terms of equations
8
and
9
. Multiply equations
8
and
9
. The product is

\delta\left[x(x^{\prime})-x_{0}(x^{\prime})\right]\delta\left[x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})-x^{\prime}_{0}(x^{\prime\prime})\right].

(11)

This defines a functional of both
x(x^{\prime})
and
x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})
. It should not be worrisome to see the coordinates
x^{\prime}
treated as both independent and dependent quantities in the same expression since equation
11
being a functional expression depends only on the distinct functions
x(x^{\prime})
and
x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})
, while making the physical operation clearer.

We need to construct a functional of a
single
function
x(x^{\prime\prime})
. To do so, functionally integrate equation
11
over
x(x^{\prime})
and
x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})
with the restriction that
x(x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime}))=x(x^{\prime\prime})
. That is, compute

\iint_{\mathcal{S}_{x(x^{\prime\prime})}}Dx(x^{\prime})Dx^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}[x(x^{\prime})]\Psi_{O^{\prime}O^{\prime\prime}}[x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})],

(12)

where
\mathcal{S}_{x(x^{\prime\prime})}
is the set of paired functions
\left(x(x^{\prime}),x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})\right)
, such that the composition of
x(x^{\prime})
and
x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})
yields
x(x^{\prime\prime})
, that is so that
x(x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime}))=x(x^{\prime\prime})
. i.e.

\mathcal{S}_{x(x^{\prime\prime})}=\{\left(x(x^{\prime}),x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})\right)|x(x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime}))=x(x^{\prime\prime})\}.

(13)

The restricted path integral in equation
12
yields a functional of a single function
x(x^{\prime\prime})
, as a valid transformation composition rule must do.

Expression
12
for wavefunctionals
8
and
9
is

\iint_{S_{x(x^{\prime\prime})}}Dx(x^{\prime})Dx^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})\delta\left[x(x^{\prime})-x_{0}(x^{\prime})\right]\delta\left[x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})-x^{\prime}_{0}(x^{\prime\prime})\right].

(14)

Evaluating functional integrals is generally very difficult. Here we will argue for the result of the integral, and in appendix
A
a more detailed discussion and conjecture is provided. We claim that equation
14
evaluates to

\delta[x(x^{\prime\prime})-x_{0}(x^{\prime}_{0}(x^{\prime\prime}))],

(15)

which it must if it is to reproduce our expectation.
Equation
14
consists of an integral over
S_{x(x^{\prime\prime})}
. The integral sums over a range of functions
x(x^{\prime})
and
x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})
. Without restricting the integral to
S_{x(x^{\prime\prime})}
, there would be no consistent function
x(x^{\prime\prime})
formed by the composition
x(x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime}))
). Thus, this restriction ensures that the function
x(x^{\prime\prime})
is well-defined and that
14
may be regarded as a functional of
x(x^{\prime\prime})
. The integrand in equation
14
consists of a product of delta functionals. The first delta functional, and therefore the whole expression, will vanish if
x(x^{\prime})\neq x_{0}(x^{\prime})
. The second functional, and therefore the expression, will vanish if
x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})\neq x_{0}(x^{\prime}_{0}(x^{\prime\prime}))
. We conclude that expression
14
will vanish unless
x(x^{\prime\prime})=x_{0}(x^{\prime}_{0}(x^{\prime\prime}))
. Now consider

\int Dx(x^{\prime\prime})\left(\iint_{S_{x(x^{\prime\prime})}}Dx(x^{\prime})Dx^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})\delta\left[x(x^{\prime})-x_{0}(x^{\prime})\right]\delta\left[x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})-x^{\prime}_{0}(x^{\prime\prime})\right]\right).

(16)

The double integrals inside the parenthesis are restricted to set
S_{x(x^{\prime\prime})}
, while the exterior integral integrates over the restriction. Together, the whole function space is integrated over, and the result is unity.
\iint_{S_{x(x^{\prime\prime})}}Dx(x^{\prime})Dx^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})\delta\left[x(x^{\prime})-x_{0}(x^{\prime})\right]\delta\left[x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})-x^{\prime}_{0}(x^{\prime\prime})\right]
vanishes if
x(x^{\prime\prime})\neq x_{0}(x^{\prime}_{0}(x^{\prime\prime}))
and gives unity when integrated with respect to
x(x^{\prime\prime})
. These are the two defining properties of a delta functional in
x(x^{\prime\prime})
, which is the claim.

For delta functional wavefunctionals equation
12
evaluates to equation
15
, which is the expected form of the wavefunctional since it enforces the same transformation as equation
10
. We generalize from this result and posit that the composition rule for wavefunctionals is as follows—

\Psi_{OO^{\prime\prime}}[x(x^{\prime\prime})]=\iint_{S_{x(x^{\prime\prime})}}Dx(x^{\prime})Dx^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}[x(x^{\prime})]\Psi_{O^{\prime}O^{\prime\prime}}[x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})].

(17)

To see how equation
17
works in the case of a superposition it is worthwhile to return to the case of two rotations in superposition begun in Section
II
. Take
\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}
to be

\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}(R_{ij})=A_{OO^{\prime}}\delta\left(R_{ij}-R_{ij}(+\theta,\mathbb{\hat{n}})\right)+B_{OO^{\prime}}\delta\left(R_{ij}-R_{ij}(-\theta,\mathbb{\hat{n}})\right).

(18)

Where
A_{OO^{\prime}}
and
B_{OO^{\prime}}
are (possibly complex) coefficients. Similarly, take
\Psi_{O^{\prime}O^{\prime\prime}}
to be

\Psi_{O^{\prime}O^{\prime\prime}}(R^{\prime}_{ij})=A_{O^{\prime}O^{\prime\prime}}\delta\left(R^{\prime}_{ij}-R_{ij}(+\gamma,\mathbb{\hat{n^{\prime}}})\right)+B_{O^{\prime}O^{\prime\prime}}\delta\left(R^{\prime}_{ij}-R_{ij}(-\gamma,\mathbb{\hat{n^{\prime}}})\right).

(19)

The two terms in equation
18
indicate that there are two possible transformations in superposition,
x=R(+\theta,\mathbb{\hat{n}})x^{\prime}
or
x=R(-\theta,\mathbb{\hat{n}})x^{\prime}
. Likewise from equation
19
there are also two transformations,
x^{\prime}=R(+\gamma,\mathbb{\hat{n^{\prime}}})x^{\prime\prime}
or
x^{\prime}=R(-\gamma,\mathbb{\hat{n^{\prime}}})x^{\prime\prime}
. As a result, there are four transformations between
x
and
x^{\prime\prime}
that are in superposition. They are,
x=R(+\theta,\mathbb{\hat{n}})R(+\gamma,\mathbb{\hat{n^{\prime}}})x^{\prime\prime}
,
x=R(+\theta,\mathbb{\hat{n}})R(-\gamma,\mathbb{\hat{n^{\prime}}})x^{\prime\prime}
,
x=R(-\theta,\mathbb{\hat{n}})R(+\gamma,\mathbb{\hat{n^{\prime}}})x^{\prime\prime}
, or

. That is,
\Psi_{OO^{\prime\prime}}
should have four delta function terms enforcing the transformations. Explicitly,

\displaystyle\Psi_{OO^{\prime\prime}}(R^{\prime\prime}_{ij})=C\delta\left(R^{\prime\prime}_{ij}-R_{im}(+\theta,\mathbb{\hat{n}})R_{mj}(+\gamma,\mathbb{\hat{n^{\prime}}})\right)

\displaystyle+D\delta\left(R^{\prime\prime}_{ij}-R_{im}(+\theta,\mathbb{\hat{n}})R_{mj}(-\gamma,\mathbb{\hat{n^{\prime}}})\right)

\displaystyle+E\delta\left(R^{\prime\prime}_{ij}-R_{im}(-\theta,\mathbb{\hat{n}})R_{mj}(+\gamma,\mathbb{\hat{n^{\prime}}})\right)

\displaystyle+F\delta\left(R^{\prime\prime}_{ij}-R_{im}(-\theta,\mathbb{\hat{n}})R_{mj}(-\gamma,\mathbb{\hat{n^{\prime}}})\right).

(20)

Now apply equation

. The product of
\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}
and
\Psi_{O^{\prime}O^{\prime\prime}}
is

\displaystyle\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}\Psi_{O^{\prime}O^{\prime\prime}}=

(21)

\displaystyle A_{OO^{\prime}}A_{O^{\prime}O^{\prime\prime}}\delta\left(R_{ij}-R_{ij}(+\theta,\mathbb{\hat{n}})\right)\delta\left(R^{\prime}_{ij}-R_{ij}(+\gamma,\mathbb{\hat{n^{\prime}}})\right)+A_{OO^{\prime}}B_{O^{\prime}O^{\prime\prime}}\delta\left(R_{ij}-R_{ij}(+\theta,\mathbb{\hat{n}})\right)\delta\left(R^{\prime}_{ij}-R_{ij}(-\gamma,\mathbb{\hat{n^{\prime}}})\right)

+A_{OO^{\prime}}B_{O^{\prime}O^{\prime\prime}}\delta\left(R^{\prime\prime}_{ij}-R_{im}(+\theta,\mathbb{\hat{n}})R_{mj}(-\gamma,\mathbb{\hat{n^{\prime}}})\right)\\

we have

1=\iint_{S_{1}}Dx(x^{\prime})Dx^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})\Psi_{OO^{\prime}}[x(x^{\prime})]\Psi_{O^{\prime}O}[(x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime}))],

(23)

where
S_{1}
, not to be confused with the unit circle, is the set of transformation functions that compose to give the identity. That is to say, the integral is restricted to a set of functions
x(x^{\prime})
and
x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})
such that
x(x^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime}))=1
. Equation
23
makes precise the notion of an inverse wavefunctional. The similarity between equations
17
and
23
and matrix multiplication and matrix inverses should be apparent.

IV
Consistency with the Schrödinger equation

The Schrödinger equation is invariant under rigid Euclidean motions (rotations and translations). Let us prove that the Schrödinger equation is invariant under any transformation to a superposition of rotated or translated frames. That is, the present formalism is consistent with Schrödinger equation. Assume that relative to a frame
O
there is a system that is studied, which has a wavefunction
\psi(x,t)
. Now consider a second frame
O^{\prime}
in superposition relative
O
. There is a wavefunctional
\Psi[x(x^{\prime})]
which governs the transformation between the two superposed frames. How are we to transform the known wavefunction
\psi(x,t)
in the
O
frame to the wavefunction that would be associated to it in the
O^{\prime}
frame? We hypothesize the following transformation rule:

\psi^{\prime}(x^{\prime},t)=\int_{S}\mathcal{D}x(x^{\prime})\Psi[x(x^{\prime})]\psi(x(x^{\prime}),t).

(24)

where
S
is the set of functions
x(x^{\prime})
. The transformation in equation
24
is natural. There are only a few distinct rules that can transform a function
\psi(x,t)
to a function of variables
x^{\prime}
, and it ensures that if the system described by
\psi(x,t)
happened to be in position eigenstate relative to
O
then
O^{\prime}
, assumed to be in a superposition relative to
O
, would associate a wavefunction
\psi^{\prime}(x,t)
to the system that corresponds to a superposition. This is ensured because eq.
24
obviously superposes
\psi(x(x^{\prime}),t)
for each function
x(x^{\prime})
for which
\Psi[x(x^{\prime})]
does not vanish.
We can extend equation
24
to wavefunctions of multi-particle systems as follows. Let
\psi(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n},t)
be the wavefunction of a multi-particle system in the
O
frame. A generic transformation from the frame
O^{\prime}
to
O
will consist of map
X:O\rightarrow O^{\prime}
that takes as input the coordinates in
O^{\prime}
and outputs the coordinates in
O
. Let
x^{\prime}_{1}
map to
x_{1}
under
X
, and let
x^{\prime}_{2}
map to
x_{2}
, and so on. Then the multi-particle extension of equation
24
takes the form —

\displaystyle\psi

{}^{\prime}(x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2},...,x^{\prime}_{n},t)=

(25)

\displaystyle\int_{S}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}X(x^{\prime})\Psi[X(x^{\prime})]\psi(X(x^{\prime}_{1}),X(x^{\prime}_{2}),...,X(x^{\prime}_{n}),t).

Note that the wavefunctional,
\Psi
, and the path integral measure,
\mathcal{D}X
depend only on the form of the function
X
and not on the value of the
X
for any particular input
x^{\prime}
, despite writing
\Psi[X(x^{\prime})]
and
\mathcal{D}X(x^{\prime})
in equation
25
.

Using equations
24
and
25
, we can now transform derivatives as well, such as
\frac{\partial\psi^{\prime}(x^{\prime},t)}{\partial x^{{}^{\prime}i}}
. Take the derivative of eq.
24
with respect to
x^{{}^{\prime}i}
—

\frac{\partial\psi^{\prime}(x^{\prime},t)}{\partial x^{{}^{\prime}i}}=\int_{S}\mathcal{D}x(x^{\prime})\Psi[x(x^{\prime})]\frac{\partial\psi(x,t)}{\partial x^{j}}\frac{\partial x^{j}}{\partial x^{{}^{\prime}i}}.

(26)

To extend equation
26
to multiple particles we adopt the following notation,
x_{I}^{i}
is the
i^{th}
spatial component of the
I^{th}
particle. Consequently,

\frac{\partial\psi^{\prime}}{\partial x_{I}^{{}^{\prime}i}}=\int_{S}\mathcal{D}X(x^{\prime})\Psi[X(x^{\prime})]\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial X_{I}^{j}}\frac{\partial X^{j}}{\partial x^{{}^{\prime}i}},

(27)

where
\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial X_{I}^{j}}
means differentiate
\psi
with respect to the
j^{th}
spatial component of the
I^{th}
particle, while
\frac{\partial X^{j}}{\partial x^{{}^{\prime}i}}
does not require an
I
index because
X(x^{\prime})
is the same function for every particle.
The Schrodinger equation is invariant under Euclidean transformations and so we restrict our superposition of coordinate systems to a superposition of Euclidean transformations. As a result,
\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial X_{I}^{j}}\frac{\partial X^{j}}{\partial x^{{}^{\prime}i}}
would be a rotation, and we can readily compute the transformation of the Laplacian from equation
25
.

\frac{\partial\psi^{\prime}}{\partial x_{I}^{{}^{\prime}j}\partial x_{I}^{{}^{\prime}i}}=\int_{S}\mathcal{D}X(x^{\prime})\Psi[X(x^{\prime})]\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial X_{I}^{n}X_{I}^{m}}\frac{\partial X^{n}}{\partial x^{{}^{\prime}j}}\frac{\partial X^{m}}{\partial x^{{}^{\prime}i}}.

(28)

Letting
i=j
, summing over the repeated indices, and noting that
\frac{\partial X^{n}}{\partial x^{{}^{\prime}j}}\frac{\partial X^{m}}{\partial x^{{}^{\prime}i}}=\delta^{nm}
yields the desired transformation —

\frac{\partial\psi^{\prime}}{\partial x_{I}^{{}^{\prime}i}\partial x_{I}^{{}^{\prime}i}}=\nabla_{I}^{2}\psi^{\prime}=\int_{S}\mathcal{D}X(x^{\prime})\Psi[X(x^{\prime})]\nabla_{I}^{2}\psi.

(29)

It is easy to see that

\frac{\partial\psi^{\prime}}{\partial t}=\int_{S}\mathcal{D}X(x^{\prime})\Psi[X(x^{\prime})]\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}.

(30)

The Schrodinger equation for
N

-\sum_{I=1}^{N}\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m_{I}}\nabla_{I}^{2}\psi+V(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{3N})\psi=i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t},

(31)

where each of the particles may have a different mass

and

We may now transform the Schrodinger equation

\Psi[X(x^{\prime})]

\displaystyle\int_{S}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}X(x^{\prime})\Psi[X(s^{\prime})]V(X(x_{1}^{\prime}),X(x_{2}^{\prime}),...,X(x_{3N}^{\prime}))\psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\hbar\int_{S}\mathcal{D}X(x^{\prime})\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}

Using equations

(33)

where it should be apparent that
\nabla_{I}^{2}
acting on
\psi^{\prime}
is
\sum_{i}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{\prime}_{i}\partial x^{\prime}_{i}}
.
The potential energy must be invariant with respect to any Euclidean transformations (generally by being a function of the Euclidean distance of the differences of the coordinates). Therefore,

V(X(x_{1}^{\prime}),X(x_{2}^{\prime}),...,X(x_{3N}^{\prime}))=V(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},...,x_{3N}^{\prime}),

(34)

and

\int_{S}\mathcal{D}X(x^{\prime})\Psi[X(x^{\prime})]V(X(x_{1}^{\prime}),X(x_{2}^{\prime}),...,X(x_{3N}^{\prime}))\psi=\\

In equation

\displaystyle V(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},...,x_{3N}^{\prime})\int_{S}\mathcal{D}X(x^{\prime})\Psi[X(x^{\prime})]\psi

in the last equality. Substituting equation

, and so demonstrates that under a superposition of transformations

V
Discussion and Future Work

This framework demonstrates the feasibility for coordinate systems to be considered in superposition relative to one another via a (complex-valued) wavefunctional
\Psi[x(x^{\prime})]
, which takes as input transformation functions. It is posited that interaction between the frames will introduce collapse-like behavior and select a particular transformation amongst the superposed transformations with a probability given by the Born rule. A rule for composing wavefunctionals is provided. It is shown that with this composition rule, equation
17
, they are closed. The wavefunctional, as introduced here, permits the transformation of wavefunctions “relative" to one frame to be expressed relative to another. This transformation of wavefunctions is consistent with the standard Schrodinger equation.

It is expected that frames may evolve to form superpositions so that after interaction between the frames, which may act to select a particular transformation
x(x^{\prime})
by destroying superposition, is removed, they again will evolve into superposition. That is to say that this framework suggests, but does not single out, a kind of frame dynamics. Because of this possibility only the superposition of time-independent transformations is considered in this present work. The extension of this framework to time-dependent transformations, such as the Galilean transformations or Lorentz transformations and the form of the frame dynamics is a matter of future work.

Integral of Delta Functions over Measure Zero Sets

Consider the integral

\int_{L}dx\delta(x-x_{0})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&x_{0}\notin L\\
1&x_{0}\in L\\
\end{array}\right.

(38)

Consequently,

\frac{1}{L}\int_{L}dx\delta(x-x_{0})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&x_{0}\notin L\\
\frac{1}{L}&x_{0}\in L\\
\end{array}\right.

(39)

If the limit as
L
approaches zero is taken, while
L
is centered at
X
, then

\lim_{L\xrightarrow{}0}\int_{L}dx\delta\left(x-x_{0}\right)=\delta(X-x_{0})

(40)

L
is an interval in the limit whose length approaches zero. After the limit, it is a measure zero set, namely that of a single point.

Consider the integral in two dimensions.

\int_{A}d^{2}x\delta(x-x_{0})\delta(y-y_{0})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&(x_{0},y_{0})\notin A\\
1&(x_{0},y_{0})\in A\\
\end{array}\right.

(41)

Or for infinitesimal area
A
centered at
(X,Y)

\lim_{A\xrightarrow{}0}\frac{1}{A}\int_{A}d^{2}x\delta(x-x_{0})\delta(y-y_{0})=\delta(X-x_{0})\delta(Y-y_{0})

(42)

The area, however, may be made infinitesimal in many ways. For example, the area may be decreased by choosing points that form a curve. More precisely, let

be a curve, and let
S
be a set of points that form the curve defined by
F
and collection of points that lie infinitesimally around it so that
S
has area
A
. We conjecture,

\lim_{A\xrightarrow{}0}\frac{1}{A}\int_{S}d^{2}x\delta(x-x_{0})\delta(y-y_{0})=k\delta(F(x_{0},y_{0})).

(43)

Where the limit that

approaches zero is taken such that the remaining elements of
S
are those points that satisfy

. We have inserted a possible normalization constant
k
, whose exact value would be computed in a proof. More generally, let
S
be a measure zero set, defined by a membership function
F
, which returns
0
for elements in the set. That is,

S=\{(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{N})|F(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{N})=0\}.

(44)

\lim_{V\xrightarrow{}0}\frac{1}{V}\int_{S}d^{N}x\delta(x_{1}-x^{0}_{1})\delta(x_{2}-x^{0}_{2})\cdots\delta(x_{N}-x^{0}_{N})\\
