Standpoint Linear Temporal Logic
Nicola Gigante, Lucía Gómez Álvarez, Tim S. Lyon

Abstract
Many complex scenarios require the coordination of agents possessing unique points of view and distinct semantic commitments. In response, standpoint logic ( SL \mathrm{SL} ) was introduced in the context of knowledge integration, allowing one to reason with diverse and potentially conflicting viewpoints by means of indexed modalities. Another multi-modal logic of import is linear temporal logic ( LTL \mathrm{LTL} )—a formalism used to express temporal properties of systems and processes, having prominence in formal methods and fields related to artificial intelligence. In this paper, we present standpoint linear temporal logic ( SLTL \mathrm{SLTL} ), a new logic that combines the temporal features of LTL \mathrm{LTL} with the multi-perspective modelling capacity of SL \mathrm{SL} . We define the logic SLTL \mathrm{SLTL} , its syntax, its semantics, establish its decidability and complexity, and provide a terminating tableau calculus to automate SLTL \mathrm{SLTL} reasoning. Conveniently, this offers a clear path to extend existing LTL \mathrm{LTL} reasoners with practical reasoning support for temporal reasoning in multi-perspective settings.

Standpoint Linear Temporal Logic

Example.

Medical devices require testing and certification prior to marketing and use by medical professionals. Albeit, regulations differ from country to country, giving rise to potentially conflicting standards and safety qualifications. For instance, Germany (
\mathsf{DE}
) and Italy (
\mathsf{IT}
) may agree that a medical device
\mathtt{X}
has been deemed
safe according to testing
(
\mathtt{X\_TestSafe}
), so long as it has been found that it never
malfunctions
(
\mathtt{Malf}
). This judgement can be expressed by the
\mathrm{SLTL}
formula shown below, where
\ast
is the universal standpoint which encodes that the formula is unequivocal from all perspectives (i.e. from the perspective of both
\mathsf{DE}
and
\mathsf{IT}
).

\mathop{\square}\nolimits_{\ast}(\mathtt{X\_TestSafe}\rightarrow\mathsf{G}\neg\mathtt{Malf})

(1)

Yet, despite the agreement on what makes
\mathtt{X}
safe according to testing, each country may differ in how it considers
\mathtt{X}
to be
safe overall
(
\mathtt{X\_Safe}
). It could be that Italy deems a device safe so long as it has been deemed safe according to testing or has been found
safe by comparison
(
\mathtt{X\_SafeComp}
). We could formalise this perspective in our language as:

\mathop{\square}\nolimits_{\mathsf{IT}}(\mathtt{X\_Safe}\rightarrow\mathtt{X\_SafeComp}\mathrel{\vee}\mathtt{X\_TestSafe})

(2)

The notion of ‘safe by comparison’ relies on
\mathtt{X}
’s relation to other devices within its domain of application. If a
comparable
device
\mathtt{Y}
exists in terms of architecture, materials used, applicability, etc. (
\mathtt{X\_Comp\_Y}
), and this device has been deemed safe by testing (
\mathtt{Y\_TestSafe}
), then it can be argued that
\mathtt{X}
is safe by comparison. We can express this as the formula shown below, where
\mathrm{Devices}
consists of the set of devices within
\mathtt{X}
’s domain of application (of which there are only finitely many).

\mathop{\square}\nolimits_{\mathsf{IT}}(\mathtt{X\_SafeComp}\rightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\bigvee_{\mathtt{Y}\in\mathrm{Devices}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\mathtt{X\_Comp\_Y}\mathrel{\wedge}\mathtt{Y\_TestSafe})

(3)

In contrast to Italy’s perspective, Germany may qualify
\mathtt{X}
as safe, so long as it has been tested safe, that is:

\mathop{\square}\nolimits_{\mathsf{DE}}(\mathtt{X\_Safe}\rightarrow\mathtt{X\_TestSafe})

(4)

We can see that Germany’s standpoint on what counts as safe is more stringent than, or subsumed by, Italy’s standpoint, a fact which may be encoded as
\mathsf{DE}\preceq\mathsf{IT}
. As each nation takes a different stance on what it deems safe, it is clear that propositions may be inconsistent with one perspective as opposed to the other. For example, the proposition ‘it is conceivable that
\mathtt{X}
is safe overall though not safe according to testing’, i.e. the formula

\mathop{\lozenge}\nolimits_{\mathsf{\ast}}(\mathtt{X\_Safe}\mathrel{\wedge}\neg\mathtt{X\_TestSafe})
is consistent with formulae (1)-(4) above. This is due to Italy’s standpoint, which allows for
\mathtt{X}
to be safe by comparison. Still, the formula
\mathop{\square}\nolimits_{\mathsf{\ast}}(\mathtt{X\_Safe}\mathrel{\wedge}\neg\mathtt{X\_TestSafe})
is inconsistent with formulae (1)-(4) since Germany regards
\mathtt{X}
as not safe overall if it has not been determined safe by testing.

As demonstrated above, the use of standpoint modalities permits the modelling of distinct, conflicting perspectives. Without the use of modalities to ‘wrap’ reasoning, we would achieve undesirable inconsistencies that are unrepresentative of the actual (consistent) scenario we aim to model. Thus, the use of standpoint modalities with LTL improves our capacity to represent knowledge more faithfully, and as shown in Sect.
, this does not come at a cost in terms of complexity. Indeed, an attractive feature of standpoint logic is its low complexity in relation to other knowledge integration approaches, being
\mathrm{NP}
-complete
[
gomez2021standpoint
,
LyoGom22
]
.

Semantics

The models used for

Definition 3

(Temporal Standpoint Structure)
.

The tableau introduces support for standpoint reasoning into the structure of a tableau calculus for

\Delta(c_{i})=\Delta_{i}
, for
c_{i}=\langle\epsilon_{i},\ell_{i},\Delta_{i}\rangle
. Finally, we use
\langle c\rangle
to denote to a constraint set such that
\ell(c)\neq\textbf{f}
(referred to as a
labelled or diamond set
) and
[c]
to denote a constraint set such that
\ell(c)=\textbf{f}
(referred to as an
unlabelled or box set
).

Definition 8

(Node Constraint Set for
\Phi
)
.

Let
u,u^{\prime},u^{\prime\prime},\dots
be the nodes of a tableau checking the satisfiability of a set of formulas
\Phi
. The constraint set of a node
u
, denoted
\Gamma(u)
, is a set
\Gamma(u):=\{c_{1},\ \hbox to8.00003pt{.\hss.\hss.},\ c_{n}\}
of constraint sets such that

(a)

For each
\hbox{$\mathsf{s}$}\in\mathcal{S}
, there is exactly one constraint set
[c]\in\Gamma(u)
such that
\epsilon(c)=\mathrm{enc}(\hbox{$\mathsf{s}$})
(i.e., there is one unlabelled set per standpoint);

(b)

For each
\langle c\rangle\in\Gamma(u)
, there is no
c^{\prime}\in\Gamma(u)
with
\ell(c)=\ell(c^{\prime})
(i.e., labels are unique).

We say that
\Gamma(u)=\Gamma(u^{\prime})
if for each
c\in\Gamma(u)
there is some
c^{\prime}\in\Gamma(u^{\prime})
such that
\epsilon(c)=\epsilon(c^{\prime})
and
\Delta(c)=\Delta(c^{\prime})
and
vice versa
.

Intuitively,
\Gamma(u)
represents the structure of standpoints at a certain
time point. Later, we will show how to construct a model
\mathfrak{M}
from a tableau
branch. In doing that, each labelled set
c
will correspond to a point in a sequence belonging to the standpoint determined by

\epsilon(c)
. Unlabelled sets encode ‘standpoint
types’ and are necessary to ensure that full precisification sequences can be reconstructed during model construction.

Let us define some useful functions. We will use
\iota(\Gamma,\epsilon,\ell)=\Delta(c)
, if there is some
c\in\Gamma
such that
\epsilon(c)=\epsilon
and
\ell(c)=\ell
; otherwise,
\iota(\Gamma,\epsilon,\ell)=\emptyset
.
The
union
and
set difference
operations are defined as follows (respectively) for sets of constraint sets:

•

\Gamma\cup\Gamma^{\prime}=\{\ \langle\epsilon,\ell,\Delta\cup\Delta^{\prime}\rangle\ |\ \langle\epsilon,\ell,\Delta\rangle\in\Gamma,\iota(\Gamma^{\prime},\epsilon,\ell)=\Delta^{\prime}\}
;

•

\Gamma\setminus\Gamma^{\prime}=\{\ \langle\epsilon,\ell,\Delta\setminus\Delta^{\prime}\rangle\ |\ \langle\epsilon,\ell,\Delta\rangle\in\Gamma,\iota(\Gamma^{\prime},\epsilon,\ell)=\Delta^{\prime}\}
.

Branches within a tableau will either become
ticked
(
✓
) or
crossed
(
✗
) throughout the processing and expansion of the tableau. Once all branches are ticked or crossed, the tableau is deemed
complete
, and a model can be extracted from each ticked branch. A counter-model can be extracted if all branches are crossed.

An
\mathsf{X}
-eventuality
is defined to be a formula of the form
\mathsf{X}(\varphi\,\mathcal{U}\,\psi)
, and if such a formula occurs in the constraint set of a node, this implies that a pending request still needs to be fulfilled at a future moment. A
poised branch
is defined to be a branch
\overline{u}=\langle u_{0},\ldots,u_{n}\rangle
such that
u_{n}
contains only constraint sets with formulae of the form
p
and
\neg p
with
p\in\mathcal{P}
and
\mathsf{X}\alpha
with
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{V}}
, and such that no expansion rules are applicable. Let us now describe how our tableaux are initialised and how rules are applied to them.

Initialisation:
Tableaux are generated by taking
r
as the root node with
\Gamma(r)=\{\langle\{*\},\ell_{0},\Phi\rangle\}\cup\{\langle\mathrm{enc}(\hbox{$\mathsf{s}$}),\textbf{f},\emptyset\rangle\mid\hbox{$\mathsf{s}$}\in\mathcal{S}\}

as input.

Expansion:
We expand a tableau by repeatedly applying the rules in Fig.
1
as well as the rules defined below.

The
expansion rules
, shown in Fig.
1
, work as follows: each rule looks for constraint set
\langle\epsilon,\ell,\Delta\rangle\in\Gamma(u)
and a formula
\varphi\in\Delta
, where
u
is the current node. By applying the rules, one or two children
u^{\prime}
and
u^{\prime\prime}
are created with
\Gamma(u^{\prime})=\Gamma(u)\setminus\{\langle\epsilon,\ell,\{\varphi\}\rangle\}\cup\Gamma_{1}(\varphi)
and
\Gamma(u^{\prime\prime})=\Gamma(u)\setminus\{\langle\epsilon,\ell,\{\varphi\}\rangle\}\cup\Gamma_{2}(\varphi)
, respectively. In addition, we have the following rules:

\mathsf{BOX}^{1}

Given a node constraint set
\Gamma(u)
and a constraint set
c\in\Gamma(u)
with
\mathop{\square}\nolimits_{\mathsf{\hbox{$\mathsf{s}$}}}\alpha\in\Delta(c)
and
\bot\notin\Delta(c)
, a child node
u^{\prime}
is created with

\Gamma(u^{\prime})=\Gamma(u^{\prime})\setminus\{\langle\epsilon,\ell,\{\mathop{\square}\nolimits_{\mathsf{\hbox{$\mathsf{s}$}}}\alpha\}\rangle\}\cup\{\langle\mathrm{enc}(\hbox{$\mathsf{s}$}),\textbf{f},\{\alpha\}\rangle\}
, and if
\ell(c)=\textbf{f}
, then a second child node is created with
\Gamma(u^{\prime})=\Gamma(u^{\prime})\setminus\{\langle\epsilon,\ell,\{\mathop{\square}\nolimits_{\mathsf{\hbox{$\mathsf{s}$}}}\alpha\}\rangle\}\cup\{\langle\epsilon,\textbf{f},\{\bot\}\rangle\}
.

\mathsf{BOX}^{2}

Given a node constraint set
\Gamma(u)
and two constraint sets
[c],c^{\prime}\in\Gamma(u)
with
\epsilon(c^{\prime})\supseteq\epsilon(c)
,
\alpha\in\Delta(c)
, and
\alpha\notin\Delta(c^{\prime})
, then a child node
u^{\prime}
is created with

\Gamma(u^{\prime})=\Gamma(u^{\prime})\cup\{\langle\epsilon(c^{\prime}),\ell(c^{\prime}),\{\alpha\}\rangle\}
.

\mathsf{DIA}^{1}

Given a node constraint set
\Gamma(u)
and an indexed set
\langle\epsilon,\ell,\Delta\rangle\in\Gamma(u)
with
\mathop{\lozenge}\nolimits_{\mathsf{s}}\alpha\in\Delta
, then a child node
u^{\prime}
is created such that
\Gamma(u^{\prime})=\Gamma(u)\setminus\{\langle\epsilon,\ell,\{\mathop{\lozenge}\nolimits_{\mathsf{s}}\alpha\}\rangle\}\cup\{\langle\mathrm{enc}(\hbox{$\mathsf{s}$}),\ell_{i},\{\alpha\}\rangle\}
, with
\ell_{i}=\ell^{\prime}
if there is
\langle\mathrm{enc}(\hbox{$\mathsf{s}$}),\ell^{\prime},\Delta^{\prime}\rangle\in\Gamma(u)
with
\alpha\in\Delta^{\prime}
and otherwise
\ell_{i}
is a fresh label.

\mathsf{DIA}^{2}

Given a node constraint set
\Gamma(u)
and two indexed sets
\langle\epsilon,\ell,\Delta\rangle,\langle\epsilon,\ell^{\prime},\Delta^{\prime}\rangle\in\Gamma(u)
with
\Delta^{\prime}\subseteq\Delta
and
\ell^{\prime}\neq\textbf{f}
, then a child node
u^{\prime}
is created with
\Gamma(u^{\prime})=\Gamma(u)\setminus\{\langle\epsilon,\ell^{\prime},\Delta^{\prime}\rangle\}
.

\mathsf{STEP}

Given a poised branch
\overline{u}=\langle u_{0},\ldots,u_{n}\rangle
, a child
u^{\prime}
is added to
\overline{u}
with
\Gamma(u^{\prime})=\{\langle\epsilon,\textbf{f},\{\alpha\mid\mathsf{X}\alpha\in\Delta\}\rangle\mid\langle\epsilon,\textbf{f},\Delta\rangle\in\Gamma(u)\}\cup\{\langle\epsilon,\ell,\{\alpha\mid\mathsf{X}\alpha\in\Delta\}\rangle\mid\langle\epsilon,\ell,\Delta\rangle\in\Gamma(u),\Delta\neq\emptyset\}

\mathsf{CONTRADICTION}

If
c\in\Gamma(u_{n})
and
\bot\in\Delta(c)
, then
u_{n}
is
crossed
.

2

\bot
is a shortcut for
\{p,\neg p\}
for some
p\in\mathcal{P}
.

\mathsf{EMPTY}

Given a branch
\overline{u}=\langle u_{0},\ldots,u_{n}\rangle
, if for all
c\in\Gamma(u_{n})
we have
\Delta(c)=\emptyset
, then
u_{n}
is
ticked
.

\mathsf{LOOP}

If there exists a poised node
u_{i}
such that
u_{i}<u_{n}
,
\Gamma(u_{i})=\Gamma(u_{n})
, and all
\mathsf{X}
-eventualities requested in
u_{i}
are fulfilled in
\overline{u}_{[i+1\ldots n]}
, then
u_{n}
is
ticked
.

\mathsf{PRUNE}

If two positions
i
and
j
exist such that
i<j\leq n
,
\Gamma(u_{i})=\Gamma(u_{j})=\Gamma(u_{n})
, and among the
\mathsf{X}
-eventualities requested in these nodes, all those fulfilled in
\overline{u}_{[j+1\ldots n]}
are fulfilled in
\overline{u}_{[i+1\ldots j]}
as well, then
u_{n}
is
crossed
.
