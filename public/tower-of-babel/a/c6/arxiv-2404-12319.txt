Marginal Analysis of Count Time Series in the Presence of Missing Observations
Simon Nik

Abstract
Time series in real-world applications often have missing observations, making typical analytical methods unsuitable. One method for dealing with missing data is the concept of amplitude modulation. While this principle works with any data, here, missing data for unbounded and bounded count time series are investigated, where tailor-made dispersion and skewness statistics are used for model diagnostics. General closed-form asymptotic formulas are derived for such statistics with only weak assumptions on the underlying process. Moreover, closed-form formulas are derived for the popular special cases of Poisson and binomial autoregressive processes, always under the assumption that missingness occurs. The finite-sample performances of the considered asymptotic approximations are analyzed with simulations. The practical application of the corresponding dispersion and skewness tests under missing data is demonstrated with three real-data examples. Key words: amplitude modulation; dispersion index; skewness index; missing data; Poisson autoregessive model; binomial autoregessive model



1
Introduction

Count time series have a discrete sample space, i. e., the time series consists of non-negative integers (counts) from
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1,\ldots\}
. These counts could either be unbounded, i. e., the range is given by the full set
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, or the range could be the finite subset
\{0,\ldots,n\}
with a given upper bound
n\in\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
. Many models for count time series have been proposed during the last decades, see
Weiß (2018)
for an overview. For example, for unbounded counts, the first-order integer-valued autoregressive (INAR(1)) model by
McKenzie (1985)
is quite popular, which is defined by the model recursion

\displaystyle X_{t}=\rho\circ X_{t-1}+\epsilon_{t}

(1.1)

with
\rho\in[0,1)
. Here, the multiplication of the ordinary AR(1) model’s recursion is replaced by “
\circ
”, which denotes the binomial thinning operation
(
Steutel & van Harn, 1979
)
, and
(\epsilon_{t})_{\mathbb{Z}=\{\ldots,-1,0,1,\ldots\}}
represents an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) count process of innovations. The binomial thinning is defined by requiring that
\rho\circ X|X\sim\textup{Bin}(X,\rho)
, and we assume that these thinnings are executed independently of other thinnings and innovations. The most popular instance of the INAR(1) family is the Poisson INAR(1) model, abbreviated as Poi-INAR(1). This model has Poisson-distributed innovations, which lead to Poisson-distributed observations, i. e.,
\epsilon_{t}\sim\textup{Poi}(\lambda)
and
X_{t}\sim\textup{Poi}(\mu)
where
\lambda>0
and
\mu=\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]=\lambda/(1-\rho)
. A detailed survey about the properties of the INAR(1) model can be found in
Weiß (2018, Chapter 2.1)
.

Together with the INAR(1) model,
McKenzie (1985)
also introduced the binomial AR(1) model, abbreviated as BAR(1). This model is used if one is concerned with count data that has a fixed upper limit
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In this case, the INAR(1)’s innovation term is replaced by a further thinning,
\beta\circ(n-X_{t-1})
, such that this term cannot be larger than
n-X_{t-1}
. Thus, for a fixed upper bound
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the BAR(1) model is defined by the recursion

\displaystyle X_{t}=\alpha\circ X_{t-1}+\beta\circ(n-X_{t-1}),

(1.2)

for
\pi\in(0,1)
,
\rho\in\big(\max\bigl\{\tfrac{-\pi}{1-\pi},\tfrac{1-\pi}{-\pi}\bigr\};\ 1\big)
and
\beta\coloneqq\pi(1-\rho)
,
\alpha\coloneqq\beta+\rho
. Here, all thinnings are performed independently of each other, and the thinnings at time
t
are independent of
(X_{s})_{s<t}
. In addition, the condition on
\rho
guarantees that the thinning parameters
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1)
. For more details about the properties of the BAR(1) model, we refer to
Weiß (2018, Chapter 3.3)
.

It is known that the Poi-INAR(1) and BAR(1) processes establish a stationary and ergodic Markov chain with marginal distribution Poi
(\lambda)
and Bin
(n,\pi)
, respectively. Furthermore, the autocorrelation function (ACF) is

\rho(h)\coloneqq\mathbb{C}orr[X_{t},X_{t-h}]=\rho^{h}
for
h\geq 0
. Moreover, the Poi-INAR(1) process as well as the BAR(1) process are
\alpha
-mixing with exponentially decreasing weights
(
Weiß, 2018
, p.24 & p.60)
. Hence, the requirements for the central limit theorem (CLT) according to
Ibragimov (1962)
are satisfied and will be used later on. For further properties, the reader is referred to
Alzaid & Al-Osh (1988)
;
Weiß (2018)
.

A wide variety of models and techniques for time series analysis can be found in
(
Box et al., 2015
;
Weiß, 2018
)
. However, when faced with missing data in real-world applications – stemming from issues such as measuring device malfunctions
(
Neave, 1970
)
, outlier removal in statistical process monitoring
(
Weiß & Testik, 2015
)
, or sporadic failures
(
Scheinok, 1965
;
Bloomfield, 1970
)
– many existing models and tools often cannot be used. The challenge of incomplete data in count time series has already been acknowledged in literature
(
Andersson & Karlis, 2010
;
Jia et al., 2014
;
Yan & Wang, 2022
;
Zhang & Zhang, 2023
)
. But while these existing works predominantly focus on parameter estimation for model fitting using imputation methods, our emphasis lies in exploring common types of diagnostic statistics. Model diagnostics are crucial within the classical Box–Jenkins program, where they are used for model identification and checks of model adequacy. This perspective has not been explored for count time series so far.

Existing methods adapting standard analytical tools for real-valued time series to missing data, such as the sample ACF or spectral estimators
(
Parzen, 1963
;
Scheinok, 1965
;
Bloomfield, 1970
;
Dunsmuir & Robinson, 1981
;
Yajima & Nishino, 1999
)
, are influenced by the work of
Parzen (1963)
. They treat real-valued time series with missing observations as a special case of amplitude-modulated time series, where the amplitude-modulating process is binary and independent of the actual process. Subsequently, tools for time series are applied to the amplitude-modulated time series, with variations in their asymptotic behaviors based on the type of missing data. Here,
Scheinok (1965)
assumed missing data to be i.i.d., while
Bloomfield (1970)
allowed for serial dependence.

In this article, we derive the general asymptotic formulas for unbounded and bounded dispersion and skewness indices in the context of missing data. Moreover, we are able to derive closed-form formulas for popular special cases of Poisson and binomial autoregressive processes, and use these expressions for model diagnostics. This article is organized as follows. Section
2
provides a brief background on common univariate and bivariate count models. Section
3
introduces the concept of amplitude modulation, setting the foundation for our subsequent derivations in Sections
4
–
6
. These sections present crucial results on the asymptotics of various indices, with full derivations available in the Appendix. Section
7
investigates the finite-sample performance of our asymptotic approximations through simulations. In Section
8
, we apply our asymptotic results to three real-data sets. Finally, Section
9
draws conclusions and outlines potential future research directions.

2
On univariate and bivariate count models

We consider two types of distribution for a univariate count random variable
X
: the Poisson distribution, abbreviated as
\textup{Poi}(\mu)
with
\mu\in(0,\,\infty)
if we are concerned with unbounded counts, and the binomial distribution, abbreviated as
\textup{Bin}(n,\pi)
with
\pi\in(0,1)
, if we are concerned with a given upper bound
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The Poisson distribution’s mean and variance coincide and equal
\mu
, whereas the binomial distribution’s mean and variance are
\mu=n\pi
and
\sigma^{2}=n\pi(1-\pi)
, see
Johnson et al. (2005, Chapter 3–4)
for more details on these distributions. We pay particular attention to the factorial moments of both distributions and therefore make use of the following definition.

Definition 2.1

Let
(X_{t})
be a stationary count process with existing moments. We
define the
k
th factorial moment as
\mu_{(k)}=\mathbb{E}[(X_{t})_{(k)}]
with
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\mu_{(1)}=\mu
, where
x_{(k)}\coloneqq x\cdots(x-k+1)
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
x_{(0)}=1
denote the falling factorial. Furthermore, we define the mixed factorial moments as

\displaystyle\mu_{(k,l)}(h)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[(X_{t})_{{}_{(k)}}\cdot(X_{t-h})_{{}_{(l)}}],

(2.1)

with
k,l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
h\in\mathbb{Z}
. Note that

(i)

\mu_{(0,0)}(h)=1,

(ii)

\mu_{(k,0)}(h)=\mu_{(k)},

(iii)

\mu_{(0,l)}(h)=\mu_{(l)},

(iv)

\mu_{(k,l)}(h)=\mu_{(l,k)}(-h).

For count data, factorial moments often take simple expressions, such as
\mu_{(k)}=\mu^{k}
for the Poisson distribution and
\mu_{(k)}=n_{(k)}\pi^{k}
for the binomial distribution. When it comes to mixed factorial moments, we make use of two specific bivariate distributions: If we consider the pair
(X_{t},X_{t-h})
from a Poi-INAR(1) process, with lag
h\in\mathbb{N}
, then this pair is bivariate Poisson distributed, see
Alzaid & Al-Osh (1988)
, i. e.,

\displaystyle(X_{t},X_{t-h})\sim\text{BPoi}\Big((1-\rho^{h})\mu,\ (1-\rho^{h})\mu,\ \rho^{h}\mu\Big).

(2.2)

When considering the pair
(X_{t},X_{t-h})
from a BAR(1) process, this pair is bivariate binomial distributed, see
Aleksandrov et al. (2022)
, i. e.,

\displaystyle(X_{t},X_{t-h})\sim\text{BBin}(n,\pi,\pi,\rho^{h}).

(2.3)

Kocherlakota & Kocherlakota (2014, Section 3.2 and 4)
provide a comprehensive overview of bivariate Poisson and binomial distributions. Closed-form expressions for general mixed factorial moments can be found there. In particular, we obtain for (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) the following propositions.

Proposition 2.2

For the BPoi-distributed pair
(X_{t},X_{t-h})
from a Poi-INAR(1) process, we have a closed-form expression for the joint factorial moments of lag
h\in\mathbb{N}
, that is,

\displaystyle\mu_{(k,s)}(h)=\mu_{(k)}\mu_{(s)}\sum_{i=0}^{\min{\{k,s\}}}\binom{k}{i}\binom{s}{i}i!\Bigg(\frac{\rho^{h}}{\mu}\Bigg)^{i}.

Proposition 2.3

For the BBin-distributed pair
(X_{t},X_{t-h})
from a BAR(1) process, we have a closed-form expression for the joint factorial moments of lag
h\in\mathbb{N}
, that is,

\displaystyle\mu_{(k,s)}(h)=n_{(k)}n_{(s)}\pi^{k+s}\sum_{i=0}^{\min{\{k,s\}}}\frac{\binom{k}{i}\binom{n-k}{s-i}}{\binom{n}{s}}\Big(1+\tfrac{1-\pi}{\pi}\rho^{h}\Big)^{i}.

We have a symmetric parameterization in both propositions, hence
\mu_{(k,s)}(h)=\mu_{(s,k)}(h)
.

3
Count time series with missing data

We adopt the idea of amplitude modulation introduced by
Parzen (1963)
to handle missing data in count time series. For a count process
(X_{t})
, we define the amplitude-modulation process
(O_{t})
as

\displaystyle O_{t}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }X_{t}\text{ is observed},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

The concept of amplitude modulation can be applied to any data, but we shall focus on unbounded and bounded counts.
Rubin (1976)
concludes that the type of missingness, whether it is Missing Completely at Random (MCAR), Missing at Random (MAR), or Missing Not at Random (MNAR), may affect statistical inference. We follow
Bloomfield (1970)
;
Dunsmuir & Robinson (1981)
;
Yajima & Nishino (1999)
by assuming that
(O_{t})
is independent of
(X_{t})
, which corresponds to an MCAR-type of missingness.
Weiß (2021, Example 2)
illustrates the challenges that arise if
(O_{t})
is not independent of
(X_{t})
. The derivation of the subsequent asymptotics under different types of missingness should be further investigated in future research.

We shall consider diagnostic procedures for marginal distributions that use factorial moment-based statistics. Thus, for
(X_{t})
, we consider the vector-valued process
(\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{t})
given by

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{t}\coloneqq(X_{t,1},\ldots,X_{t,m})^{\top}=((X_{t})_{{}_{(1)}},(X_{t})_{{}_{(2)}},\ldots,(X_{t})_{{}_{(m)}})^{\top},

(3.1)

which comprises the first
m
falling factorials. Furthermore, let us define the amplitude modulation of
(\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{t})
as
(O_{t}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{t})
, and
\overline{O\,\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}O_{t}\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{t}
. Thus, the mean of
\overline{O\,\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\overline{O\,\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}]=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[O_{t}]\mathbb{E}[\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{t}]=\Big(\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[O_{t}]\Big)\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}=\mathbb{E}[\overline{O}]\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$},

(3.2)

with
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}\coloneqq(\mu_{(1)},\ldots,\mu_{(m)})^{\top}=(\mathbb{E}[(X_{t})_{{}_{(1)}}],\ldots,\mathbb{E}[(X_{t})_{{}_{(m)}}])^{\top}
. This, however, implies to estimate
\mu
by

\displaystyle\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}\coloneqq\frac{\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}O_{t}\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{t}}{\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}O_{t}}\eqqcolon\frac{\overline{O\,\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}}{\overline{O}},

(3.3)

analogous to
Weiß (2021)
. From now on, we assume that
(O_{t})
is stationary with
\mathbb{E}[O_{t}]=\uptau
and autocovariance function
\gamma_{O}(h)\coloneqq CoV[O_{h},O_{0}]
, so
\uptau(h)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[O_{h}O_{0}]=\gamma_{O}(h)+\uptau^{2}
. Let us define the vector
(\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{t}^{\ast})
as

\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{t}^{\ast}\coloneqq(X_{t,0}^{\ast},\ldots,X_{t,m}^{\ast})^{\top}=O_{t}(1,(X_{t})_{{}_{(1)}},(X_{t})_{{}_{(2)}},\ldots,(X_{t})_{{}_{(m)}})^{\top}.

(3.4)

For the mean of
(\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{t}^{\ast})
, we obtain

\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}^{\ast}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{t}^{\ast}]=\uptau(1,\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}^{\top})^{\top}.

(3.5)

Additionally, let us assume that appropriate mixing assumptions on
(X_{t})
and
(O_{t})
hold, i. e., such that
Ibragimov (1962, Theorem 1.7)
is applicable:

(A)

moments
\mathbb{E}|X_{t}|^{2m+\delta}<\infty
of order
2m+\delta
with some
\delta>0
exist, and the process is
\alpha
-mixing with exponentially decreasing weights.

Here, the stationary process
(X_{t})
with probability space
(\Omega,\mathfrak{A},\mathbb{P})
is said to be
\alpha
-mixing with exponentially decreasing weights if there exists a non-negative sequence
(\alpha_{k})_{\mathbb{N}}
of weights such that
\alpha_{k}\leq e^{-\vartheta k}
for some
\vartheta>0
and each
k\in\mathbb{N}
and such that for each
t\in\mathbb{Z}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
and all events
E_{1}\in\mathfrak{A}\big(X_{t},X_{t-1},\ldots\big)
,
E_{2}\in\mathfrak{A}\big(X_{t+k},X_{t+k+1},\ldots\big)
the following holds:

\displaystyle|\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\cap E_{2})-\mathbb{P}(E_{1})\mathbb{P}(E_{2})|\leq e^{-\vartheta k}.

Condition (
A
) is satisfied for both the Poi-INAR(1) and the BAR(1) process, see
Schweer & Weiß (2014)
;
Weiß (2018)
. The main task here is to derive a CLT for
(\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{t}^{\ast}})
(see Appendix
A.1
) and then apply the Delta method to it, resulting in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1

Under the aforementioned assumption (
A
), it holds that

\displaystyle\sqrt{T}\Big(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}\Big)\ \xrightarrow{\text{d}}\ \mathcal{N}\Big(\mathbb{0},\mathbb{\Sigma}\Big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{\Sigma}=(\sigma_{ij})_{i,j=1,\ldots,m},

where
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{0},\mathbb{\Sigma})
denotes the multivariate normal distribution, and where

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}=\tfrac{1}{\uptau}(\mu_{(i,j)}(0)-\mu_{(i)}\mu_{(j)})+\tfrac{1}{\uptau^{2}}\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\uptau(h)\Big(\mu_{(j,i)}(h)+\mu_{(i,j)}(h)-2\mu_{(i)}\mu_{(j)}\Big).

The bias satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}]=0+\mbox{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}}$}(T^{-1})
.

Proof.

See Appendix
A.1
.
∎

A.8.1

For the Poisson model (

\displaystyle(i)\hskip 10.00002pt\sigma_{ij}^{\text{Poi}}

\displaystyle(ii)\hskip 10.00002pt\sigma_{ij}^{\text{Bin}}

4
Poisson index of dispersion

One of the most well-known diagnostic statistic for unbounded counts is the index of dispersion, defined as
I^{\textup{Poi}}=\sigma^{2}/\mu
. The Poisson distribution has equidispersion since mean and variance always coincide, i. e.,
I^{\textup{Poi}}=1
. The sample counterpart to the index of dispersion is given by
\hat{I}^{\textup{Poi}}=\hat{\mu}_{(2)}/\hat{\mu}-\hat{\mu}+1
. Starting from Theorem
3.1
, we derive the asymptotics of
\hat{I}^{\textup{Poi}}
, and define the function
g
by

\displaystyle g(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}}-x_{1}+1.

Note that
g(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}})=(g\circ\mbox{\boldmath$f$})(\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}^{\ast}})
and
g(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$})=(g\circ\mbox{\boldmath$f$})(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}^{\ast})
with
\mbox{\boldmath$f$}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})=(\frac{x_{1}}{x_{0}},\frac{x_{2}}{x_{0}})^{\top}
. Then,
g
has the partial derivatives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}g=-\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}^{2}}-1,\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}g=\frac{1}{x_{1}},\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{1}^{2}}g=\frac{2x_{2}}{x_{1}^{3}},\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{2}^{2}}g=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{2}\partial x_{1}}g=-\frac{1}{x_{1}^{2}}.

So, we get the Jacobian
D
and the Hessian
H
by evaluating the partial derivatives in
\mu
, which leads to

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$D$}=\Big(-\frac{\mu_{(2)}}{\mu^{2}}-1,\frac{1}{\mu}\Big),\hskip 20.00003pt\mbox{\boldmath$H$}=\frac{1}{\mu^{3}}\begin{pmatrix}2\mu_{(2)}&-\mu\\
-\mu&0\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(4.1)

This implies the Taylor approximation
\hat{I}^{\textup{Poi}}\approx I^{\textup{Poi}}+\mbox{\boldmath$D$}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$})+\tfrac{1}{2}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$H$}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$})
, which can be used to conclude the asymptotic variance and bias of
\hat{I}^{\textup{Poi}}
, that is

\displaystyle\sigma_{\hat{I}^{\textup{Poi}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{T}\Big(d_{1}^{2}\sigma_{11}+d_{2}^{2}\sigma_{22}+2d_{1}d_{2}\sigma_{12}\Big),

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{\hat{I}^{\textup{Poi}}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{T}\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}h_{11}\sigma_{11}+h_{12}\sigma_{12}\Big).

(4.3)

Without making specific assumptions about
\uptau(h)
, let us derive general expressions for the asymptotic variance and bias.

Theorem 4.1

For any count process
(X_{t})
and amplitude-modulating process
(O_{t})
that satisfy assumption (
A
), the asymptotic variance and bias of
\hat{I}^{\textup{Poi}}
are given by

\displaystyle(i)\hskip 10.00002pt\sigma_{\hat{I}^{\textup{Poi}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{T\uptau\mu^{2}}\Bigg[\big(\tfrac{\mu_{(2)}}{\mu}+\mu\big)^{2}(\mu_{(2)}+\mu)-2\big(\tfrac{\mu_{(2)}}{\mu}+\mu\big)(\mu_{(3)}+2\mu_{(2)})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\mu_{(4)}+4\mu_{(3)}+2\mu_{(2)}-\mu^{4}+\tfrac{2}{\uptau}\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\uptau(h)\Big(\big(\tfrac{\mu_{(2)}}{\mu}+\mu\big)^{2}\mu_{(1,1)}(h)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-\big(\tfrac{\mu_{(2)}}{\mu}+\mu\big)\big(\mu_{(2,1)}(h)+\mu_{(1,2)}(h)\big)+\mu_{(2,2)}(h)-\mu^{4}\Big)\Bigg],

\displaystyle(ii)\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{B}_{\hat{I}^{\textup{Poi}}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{T\uptau\mu^{3}}\Bigg[\mu_{(2)}^{2}-\mu\big(\mu_{(2)}+\mu_{(3)}\big)+\tfrac{2}{\uptau}\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\uptau(h)\Big(\mu_{(2)}\mu_{(1,1)}(h)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-\tfrac{\mu}{2}\big(\mu_{(2,1)}(h)+\mu_{(1,2)}(h)\big)\Big)\Bigg].

The proof of Theorem
4.1
can be found in Appendix
A.3.1

A.8.2

Ristíc et al. (2013)
;
Borges et al. (2016)

Zhang et al. (2017)

Therefore, we selected the Poi-INAR(1) process as an illustrative example in the following corollary to specify the asymptotic variance and bias.

where

A.3.2

5
Binomial index of dispersion

If we would choose the (Poisson) index of dispersion for bounded counts, then the binomial distribution, which has a finite range, would result in underdispersion, i. e.,
I^{\textup{Poi}}<1
. This phenomenon is caused by the fact the
I^{\textup{Poi}}
does not account for the boundedness of the range. Thus, the binomial index of dispersion, defined as
I^{\textup{Bin}}=\sigma^{2}/(\mu(1-\frac{\mu}{n}))
, is the more appropriate choice, which results in equidispersion if being concerned with binomial counts. The binomial index of dispersion’s sample counterpart is given by
\hat{I}^{\textup{Bin}}=(\hat{\mu}_{(2)}+\hat{\mu}-\hat{\mu}^{2})/(\hat{\mu}(1-\frac{\hat{\mu}}{n}))
. Starting from Theorem
3.1
, we derive the asymptotics of
\hat{I}^{\textup{Bin}}

\displaystyle g(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{n(x_{2}+x_{1}-x_{1}^{2})}{x_{1}(n-x_{1})}.

Conclusions

In this article, we considered unbounded and bounded count time series with missing data. We were able to derive general formulas for the asymptotics of relevant statistics, namely types of dispersion and skewness index. We derived closed-form expressions for the special cases of Poisson and binomial autoregressive processes. Thus, the considered indices can be used to test the null of a Poisson or binomial distribution, respectively, in the presence of missing data. The developed approaches were applied to two real-world data examples, and simulations were used to analyze the finite-sample performance of the resulting asymptotic approximations. It became evident through our simulation study that our asymptotic derivations are well-suited for approximating the true finite-sample distributions. Therefore, simply omitting the missing observations may lead to misleading and false conclusions. As a result, when examining count time series, missing observations should be carefully taken into account.

A task for further research is to investigate the derivation of our asymptotics under different types of missingness. Additionally, expanding the scope of the derived asymptotics to higher-order autoregressive processes such as the Poi-INAR(2) process, or to a different class of processes, such as the integer-valued moving average (INMA) process could be another research direction. The parameter estimation in the presence of missing observations, also recall the existing approach by
Andersson & Karlis (2010)
, might be another field of research, which could be investigated in the future.

. For

\sigma_{00}

\displaystyle\sigma_{00}^{\ast}

. Next, we obtain

\displaystyle CoV[O_{t}(X_{t})_{(i)},O_{s}(X_{s})_{(j)}]

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=CoV[O_{0}(X_{0})_{(i)},O_{0}(X_{0})_{(j)}]+\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\Big(CoV[O_{0}(X_{0})_{(i)},O_{h}(X_{h})_{(j)}]

A.6
Autocorrelation and Missing Data

In this section, we briefly describe our method for estimating the autocorrelation function for cloud from data with missing values. We use the method provided by
Dunsmuir & Robinson (1981)
. Let us recall that the amplitude modulation of
(\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{t})
is
(O_{t}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{t})
, implying that we estimate
\mu
by

\displaystyle\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}=\frac{\overline{O\,\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}}{\overline{O}}.

In
Dunsmuir & Robinson (1981, p. 260f)
, they first consider a known mean of zero. When the mean is unknown a mean correction is necessary, see
Dunsmuir & Robinson (1981, p. 277f)
.
As a result, we can estimate the autocorrelation function as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{DR}(l)=\frac{\hat{C}_{DR}(l)}{\hat{C}_{DR}(0)},\hskip 18.49988pt0\leq l<T,

(A.10)

where
\hat{C}_{DR}(l)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T-l}O_{t}O_{t+l}(X_{t}-\hat{\mu})(X_{t+l}-\hat{\mu})
. If we now assume that we have i.i.d. data, the asymptotic variance, according to
(
Dunsmuir & Robinson, 1981
, p. 274)
, is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\uptau(l)},\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for }l=0,\ldots,T-l,

where
\uptau(l)=\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T-l}\mathbb{E}[O_{t}O_{t+l}]=\gamma_{O}(l)+\uptau^{2}
.
If using
\hat{\rho}_{DR}(l)
to test the null hypothesis of serial independence at lag
l
on level
\alpha
, then the critical value is
\pm z_{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}/\sqrt{\uptau(l)}
, and is therefore dependent on the lag
l
.

A.8
Raw moments

In this section, we present alternative results for the CLT and the Poisson index of dispersion if we are concerned with raw moments. Therefore, let us consider the vector
(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t})
which is given by

\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t}\coloneqq(Y_{t,1},\ldots,Y_{t,m})^{\top}=(X_{t},X_{t}^{2},\ldots,X_{t}^{m})^{\top}.

(A.11)

Furthermore, we define the amplitude modulation of
(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t})
as
(O_{t}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t})
. Let us define
\overline{O\,\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}}\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}O_{t}\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t}
. Then, the mean of
\overline{O\,\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\overline{O\,\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}}]=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[O_{t}]\mathbb{E}[\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t}]=\Big(\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[O_{t}]\Big)\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$},

(A.12)

with
\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}\coloneqq(\mu,\ldots,\mu_{m})^{\top}=(\mathbb{E}[X_{t}],\ldots,\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{m}])^{\top}
. This implies to estimate
\nu
by

\displaystyle\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}}\coloneqq\frac{\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}O_{t}\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t}}{\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}O_{t}}\eqqcolon\frac{\overline{O\,\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}}}{\overline{O}}.

(A.13)

Definition A.7

Let us denote the mixed raw moment as

\displaystyle\mu_{k,l}(h)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{k}\cdot X_{t-h}^{l}],

(A.14)

respectively with
0\leq k\leq l
,
k,l\in\mathbb{N}
and
h\in\mathbb{Z}
. In addition, we declare

(i)

\mu_{0,0}(h)=1,

(iii)

\mu_{0,l}(h)=\mu_{l},

(ii)

\mu_{k,0}(h)=\mu_{k},

(iv)

\mu_{k,l}(h)=\mu_{l,k}(-h)
.

Note that for
h=0
, one can simplify
\mu_{k,l}(0)=\mu_{k+l}
.

A.8.1
Central Limit Theorem

The following proposition can be seen as the analogon to Theorem
3.1
.

Proposition A.8

Let
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}^{\ast}}=(\overline{O},\overline{O\,\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}}^{\top})^{\top}
, and define the function
\mbox{\boldmath$f$}:[0,1]\times[0,\infty)^{m}\rightarrow[0,\infty)^{m}
. Then,
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}}=\mbox{\boldmath$f$}(\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}^{\ast}})
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}=\mbox{\boldmath$f$}(\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}^{\ast})
. Thus, the Delta-Method implies

\sqrt{T}\Big(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}\Big)\ \xrightarrow{\text{d}}\ \mathcal{N}\Big(\mathbf{0},\mathbb{\Sigma}\Big)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{with}\hskip 9.24994pt\mathbb{\Sigma}=(\sigma_{ij})_{i,j=1,\ldots,m},

(A.15)

where

\sigma_{ij}=\tfrac{1}{\uptau}(\mu_{i+j}-\mu_{i}\mu_{j})+\tfrac{1}{\uptau^{2}}\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\uptau(h)\Big(\mu_{j,i}(h)+\mu_{i,j}(h)-2\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\Big).

(A.16)

Here, the bias satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}]=0+\mbox{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}}$}(T^{-1})
.

Proof.

For the raw moments, the CLT is derived analogously to the case of the factorial moments. Hence, let us define the vector
(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t}^{\ast})
as

\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t}^{\ast}\coloneqq(Y_{t,0}^{\ast},\ldots,Y_{t,m}^{\ast})^{\top}=O_{t}(1,X_{t},X_{t}^{2};\ldots,X_{t}^{m})^{\top}.

(A.17)

For the mean of
(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t}^{\ast})
, we obtain

\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}^{\ast}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t}^{\ast}]=\uptau(1,\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}^{\top})^{\top}.

(A.18)

Now, we assume that appropriate mixing assumptions on
(X_{t})
and
(O_{t})
hold according to (
A
), i. e.,
\alpha
-mixing with exponentially decreasing weights, which then hand over to
(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t}^{\ast})
. Then, we have the CLT

\sqrt{T}\Big(\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}^{\ast}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}^{\ast}\Big)\ \xrightarrow{\text{d}}\ \mathcal{N}\Big(\mathbf{0},\mathbb{\Sigma}^{\ast}\Big)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{with}\hskip 9.24994pt\mathbf{\Sigma^{\ast}}=(\sigma_{ij}^{\ast})_{i,j=0,\ldots,m},

(A.19)

where

\sigma_{ij}^{\ast}=CoV[Y_{0,i}^{\ast},Y_{0,j}^{\ast}]+\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\Big(CoV[Y_{0,i}^{\ast},Y_{h,j}^{\ast}]+CoV[Y_{h,i}^{\ast},Y_{0,j}^{\ast}]\Big).

(A.20)

These covariances compute as

\sigma_{ij}^{\ast}=\begin{cases}\uptau(1-\uptau)+2\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{O}(h)&\text{if }i=j=0,\\
\sigma_{00}^{\ast}\mu_{j}&\text{if }i=0,\ j>0,\\
\uptau(\mu_{i+j}-\mu_{i}\mu_{j})+\sigma_{00}^{\ast}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}&\text{if }i,j>0.\\
\hskip 9.24994pt+\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\uptau(h)\Big(\mu_{j,i}(h)+\mu_{i,j}(h)-2\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\Big)\\
\end{cases}

(A.21)

Here,
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}^{\ast}}=(\overline{O},\overline{O\,\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}}^{\top})^{\top}
are the required components for the calculation of
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}}
. The derivations of the covariances follow the same steps as for the factorial moments. Thus, we use the same function
f
as defined in Section
A.1
. Then,
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}}=\mbox{\boldmath$f$}(\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}^{\ast}})
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}=\mbox{\boldmath$f$}(\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}^{\ast})
, and the
Jacobian of
f
evaluated in
\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}^{\ast}
equals

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$D$}=\frac{1}{\uptau}\begin{pmatrix}-\mu&1&0&\cdots&0\\
-\mu_{2}&0&1&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
-\mu_{m}&0&\cdots&0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}.

So the linear Taylor approximation
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}}\approx\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}+\mbox{\boldmath$D$}(\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}^{\ast}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}^{\ast})
together with the CLT implies

\displaystyle\sqrt{T}\Big(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}\Big)\ \xrightarrow{\text{d}}\ \mathcal{N}\Big(\mathbb{0},\mathbb{\Sigma}\Big)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{with}\hskip 9.24994pt\mathbb{\Sigma}=\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\mathbb{\Sigma}^{\ast}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{\top}.

We can compute the entries
(\sigma_{ij})_{i,j=1,\ldots,m}
as

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l=0}^{m}d_{ik}d_{jl}\sigma_{kl}^{\ast}=d_{i0}d_{j0}\sigma_{00}^{\ast}+d_{i0}d_{jj}\sigma_{0j}^{\ast}+d_{ii}d_{j0}\sigma_{i0}^{\ast}+d_{ii}d_{jj}\sigma_{ij}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\uptau^{2}}\Big(\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\sigma_{00}^{\ast}-\mu_{i}\sigma_{0j}^{\ast}-\mu_{j}\sigma_{i0}^{\ast}+\sigma_{ij}^{\ast}\Big)=\tfrac{1}{\uptau^{2}}\Big(\sigma_{ij}^{\ast}-\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\sigma_{00}^{\ast}\Big)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\uptau}(\mu_{i+j}-\mu_{i}\mu_{j})+\tfrac{1}{\uptau^{2}}\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\uptau(h)\Big(\mu_{j,i}(h)+\mu_{i,j}(h)-2\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\Big),

where in the last step, we utilized that
\sigma_{0j}^{\ast}=\sigma_{00}^{\ast}\cdot\mu_{j}
. Analogously to the factorial moments, the bias for the raw moments is also negligible for practice. This is due to the quadratic Taylor approximation and follows the same steps as for the factorial moments.
∎

A.8.2
Poisson Index of Dispersion

Similarly to Section
4
, we start from (
A.15
) and define a function
g
by

\displaystyle g(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}}-x_{1}.

Since the difference between
g
and the function chosen in Section
4

is only a constant 1, the partial derivatives coincide. Therefore, we get the Jacobian
D
and the Hessian
H
by evaluating the partial derivatives in
\nu
, which leads to

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$D$}=\Big(-\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu^{2}}-1,\frac{1}{\mu}\Big),\hskip 18.49988pt\mbox{\boldmath$H$}=\frac{1}{\mu^{3}}\begin{pmatrix}2\mu_{2}&-\mu\\
-\mu&0\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Hence, the asymptotic variance and bias of
\hat{I}^{\textup{Poi}}
is

\displaystyle\sigma_{\hat{I}^{\textup{Poi}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{T}\Big(d_{1}^{2}\sigma_{11}+d_{2}^{2}\sigma_{22}+2d_{1}d_{2}\sigma_{12}\Big),

(A.22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{\hat{I}^{\textup{Poi}}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{T}\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}h_{11}\sigma_{11}+h_{12}\sigma_{12}\Big).

(A.23)

Proposition A.9

The asymptotic variance of
\hat{I}^{\textup{Poi}}
for any Poisson model is given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{\hat{I}^{\textup{Poi}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{T\uptau}\Bigg[\big(\tfrac{\mu_{2}}{\mu^{2}}+1\big)^{2}(\mu_{2}-\mu^{2})-\tfrac{2}{\mu}\big(\tfrac{\mu_{2}}{\mu^{2}}+1\big)(\mu_{3}-\mu\mu_{2})+\tfrac{1}{\mu^{2}}(\mu_{4}-\mu_{2}^{2})

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\tfrac{2}{\uptau}\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\uptau(h)\Big(\big(\tfrac{\mu_{2}}{\mu^{2}}+1\big)^{2}\big(\mu_{1,1}(h)-\mu^{2}\big)+\tfrac{1}{\mu^{2}}\big(\mu_{2,2}(h)-\mu_{2}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt-\tfrac{1}{\mu}\big(\tfrac{\mu_{2}}{\mu^{2}}+1\big)\big(\mu_{2,1}(h)+\mu_{1,2}(h)-2\mu\mu_{2}\big)\Big)\Bigg].

Proof.

Let us start by applying (
A.16
) to (
A.22
). Then, we get

\displaystyle\sigma_{\hat{I}^{\textup{Poi}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{T}\Bigg[\big(-\tfrac{\mu_{2}}{\mu^{2}}-1\big)^{2}\Big(\tfrac{1}{\uptau}(\mu_{2}-\mu^{2})+\tfrac{2}{\uptau^{2}}\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\uptau(h)\big(\mu_{1,1}(h)-\mu^{2}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\tfrac{2}{\mu}\big(-\tfrac{\mu_{2}}{\mu^{2}}-1\big)\Big(\tfrac{1}{\uptau}(\mu_{3}-\mu\mu_{2})+\tfrac{1}{\uptau^{2}}\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\uptau(h)\big(\mu_{2,1}(h)+\mu_{1,2}(h)-2\mu\mu_{2}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\tfrac{1}{\mu^{2}}\Big(\tfrac{1}{\uptau}(\mu_{4}-\mu_{2}^{2})+\tfrac{2}{\uptau^{2}}\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\uptau(h)\big(\mu_{2,2}(h)-\mu_{2}^{2}\big)\Big)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{T\uptau}\Bigg[\big(\tfrac{\mu_{2}}{\mu^{2}}+1\big)^{2}(\mu_{2}-\mu^{2})-\tfrac{2}{\mu}\big(\tfrac{\mu_{2}}{\mu^{2}}+1\big)(\mu_{3}-\mu\mu_{2})+\tfrac{1}{\mu^{2}}(\mu_{4}-\mu_{2}^{2})

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\tfrac{2}{\uptau}\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\uptau(h)\Big(\big(\tfrac{\mu_{2}}{\mu^{2}}+1\big)^{2}\big(\mu_{1,1}(h)-\mu^{2}\big)+\tfrac{1}{\mu^{2}}\big(\mu_{2,2}(h)-\mu_{2}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt-\tfrac{1}{\mu}\big(\tfrac{\mu_{2}}{\mu^{2}}+1\big)\big(\mu_{2,1}(h)+\mu_{1,2}(h)-2\mu\mu_{2}\big)\Big)\Bigg].

∎

Proposition A.10

The asymptotic bias of
\hat{I}^{\textup{Poi}}
for any Poisson model is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{\hat{I}^{\textup{Poi}}}=\tfrac{1}{T\uptau\mu^{3}}\Bigg[\mu_{2}^{2}-\mu_{3}\mu+\tfrac{2}{\uptau}\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\uptau(h)\Big(\mu_{2}\mu_{1,1}(h)-\tfrac{\mu}{2}\big(\mu_{2,1}(h)+\mu_{1,2}(h)\big)\Big)\Bigg].

Proof.

The asymptotic bias becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{\hat{I}^{\textup{Poi}}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{T}\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}h_{11}\sigma_{11}+h_{12}\sigma_{12}\Big)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{T}\Bigg[\tfrac{\mu_{2}}{\mu^{3}}\Big(\tfrac{1}{\uptau}(\mu_{2}-\mu^{2})+\tfrac{2}{\uptau^{2}}\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\uptau(h)\big(\mu_{1,1}(h)-\mu^{2}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt-\tfrac{1}{\mu^{2}}\Big(\tfrac{1}{\uptau}(\mu_{3}-\mu\mu_{2})+\tfrac{1}{\uptau^{2}}\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\uptau(h)\big(\mu_{2,1}(h)+\mu_{1,2}(h)-2\mu\mu_{2}\big)\Big)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{T\uptau\mu^{2}}\Bigg[\tfrac{\mu_{2}}{\mu}(\mu_{2}-\mu^{2})-(\mu_{3}-\mu\mu_{2})

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\tfrac{1}{\uptau}\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\uptau(h)\Big(\tfrac{2\mu_{2}}{\mu}\big(\mu_{1,1}(h)-\mu^{2}\big)-\big(\mu_{2,1}(h)+\mu_{1,2}(h)-2\mu\mu_{2}\big)\Big)\Bigg].

Further simplification leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{\hat{I}^{\textup{Poi}}}=\tfrac{1}{T\uptau\mu^{3}}\Bigg[\mu_{2}^{2}-\mu_{3}\mu+\tfrac{2}{\uptau}\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\uptau(h)\Big(\mu_{2}\cdot\mu_{1,1}(h)-\tfrac{\mu}{2}\big(\mu_{2,1}(h)+\mu_{1,2}(h)\big)\Big)\Bigg].

∎
