Ain’t No Stoppin’ Us Monitoring Now
Luca Ciccone, Francesco Dagnino, Angelo Ferrando

Abstract
Not all properties are monitorable. This is a well-known fact, and it means there exist properties that cannot be fully verified at runtime. However, given a non-monitorable property, a monitor can still be synthesised, but it could end up in a state where no verdict will ever be concluded on the satisfaction (resp., violation) of the property. For this reason, non-monitorable properties are usually discarded. In this paper, we carry out an in-depth analysis on monitorability, and how non-monitorable properties can still be partially verified. We present our theoretical results at a semantic level, without focusing on a specific formalism. Then, we show how our theory can be applied to achieve partial runtime verification of Linear Temporal Logic (LTL).

Introduction

Runtime Verification (RV)
[
8
,
9
,
21
]
is a well-established approach, whose aim is to
achieve the formal verification of software (resp., hardware) systems at runtime.
This means that,
differently from other formal verification techniques, such as Model Checking
[
10
]
,
RV focuses on the analysis of actual system executions, rather than on a model (
i.e.
abstraction) of it.
More precisely,
given a formal property, which can be expressed in various formalisms
[
25
,
17
,
5
,
20
,
22
]
, a
monitor
is synthesised and used
to verify the property against system executions.
A monitor can be seen abstractly as a function that, given a
finite observation
of a system execution
(
e.g.
a finite sequence, a.k.a. trace, of events generated by the system),
returns a verdict stating whether it satisfies or violates the property.
Intuitively, what the monitor does is checking whether the current observation of the system execution carries enough information to conclude the satisfaction (resp., violation) of the property under analysis, that is,
it does not need to observe more to determine the verdict.

Unfortunately, for some properties, the monitor could end up in scenarios where, no matter what it observes, it would never be able to conclude a verdict.
Such cases are problematic since the monitor keeps on observing the system, thus consuming resources, even though it has no hope to determine the satisfaction (resp., violation) of the property.
This has lead to the notion of
monitorability

[
19
,
2
,
8
,
27
]
.
Roughly, a property is said to be
monitorable

if we can synthesise a monitor which has always the possibility of eventually verifying it, that is,
the above situation cannot happen.
Actually, this is just one out of many different flavours of monitorability that exist in the literature
[
19
,
2
,
1
,
3
]
. Nevertheless, we focus on this one as it does not only require the existence of a correct monitor, but the latter to be well behaved as well,
i.e.
it has not to run uselessly.

Since monitors of non-monitorable properties may eventually be unable to conclude anything about the system, RV approaches usually rule such properties out, restricting themselves to monitorable ones.
However, even though a non-monitorable property is in some cases ill-behaved, it may still be relevant to analyse, especially when there are fragments of it where a verdict can be reached.

In this paper, we take seriously this idea, providing first steps towards
partial
runtime verification.
Specifically, we present an abstract semantic approach to partially monitor any given property, which makes
our framework independent from specific formalisms used to describe properties and monitors.
We build on the abstract setting for monitorability proposed in
[
28
]
.
First of all, we observe that any property admits best safety and cosafety approximations, which turn out to be monitorable, like all safety and cosafety properties, under mild assumptions
1
1

1

In the standard linear time setting, safety and cosafety properties are known to be always monitorable
[
4
,
27
]
, but, as we will observe, this is no longer true in our abstract setting which covers also branching time properties.
.
Then, we show how to combine the verdicts of the (standard) monitors of these approximations to build a generalised monitor, which is able to partially verify the initial property, even though it may be non-monitorable in general.
Roughly, a generalised monitor is not only able to determine the satisfaction or violation of the property, but also when to safely give up on the verification, since it will never be able to conclude anything.
In a sense, we move also the monitorability check at runtime with the usual advantages:
we do not discard a property only because we statically detect that in some cases it may not be verified,
but rather we synthesise a monitor that, when such cases happen, stops running and, otherwise, keeps on verifying the property.
We dub this approach partial RV, since the monitor may determine neither the satisfaction nor the violation of the property, but we are guaranteed that it keeps on running only if it has the possibility of concluding something on the property.

To test the effectiveness of the proposed framework,
we instantiate it describing two approaches to achieve partial RV of properties in the linear time setting.
In the first one, we focus on properties expressed in Linear Temporal Logic (LTL)
[
25
]
.
In particular, we show how to extend the standard synthesis procedure of monitors for LTL formulas, to build a generalised monitor.
In the second one, instead, we focus on the Linear Time
\nu
-calculus (a purely coinductive fragment of the Linear Time
\mu
-calculus
[
6
,
30
]
).
This calculus is much less expressive then other formalisms, such as LTL or the full Linear Time
\mu
-calculus,
since, as we show, it can express only safety, thus monitorable, properties.
We briefly discuss how one can directly use an
LT\nu
-term as a monitor, thus avoiding the synthesis procedure which is usually very computationally expensive, and then
we show how to combine two
LT\nu
-terms to specify a generalised monitor.
Finally, we prove that in this way we can describe generalised monitors of all LTL formulas.
For both approaches we provide prototype implementations with some empirical evaluations of their performances.

2
Preliminaries on monitorability

In this section, we recall well-known notions about monitorability, phrasing them in an abstract, purely semantic setting, following
[
28
]
.
We need this abstract setting to develop our results independently from specific choices of system models and specification languages.

Assume a set
\mathcal{B}
of
(system) behaviours

\alpha,\beta,\gamma
.
These can be whatever one needs, e.g., traces of events, trees, states, etc.
Then, a
property
is a subset of behaviours
P\subseteq\mathcal{B}
.
Intuitively, one cannot directly access and manipulate system behaviours as a whole, because they may have an infinite nature;
instead we can analyse the system through
observations
, which are intuitively finite approximations of system behaviours.

Definition 1
(Observation system)

An
observation structure
over
\mathcal{B}
consists of a preordered set
\langle\mathcal{O},\triangleleft\rangle
of
observations
together with a binary relation

\triangleleft_{b}\subseteq\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{B}
such that the following properties hold:

•

o\triangleleft p
and
p\triangleleft_{b}\alpha
implies
o\triangleleft_{b}\alpha

•

for all
o\in\mathcal{O}
there is
\alpha\in\mathcal{B}
such that
o\triangleleft_{b}\alpha

Let us set
\mathcal{B}(o)=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{B}\mid o\triangleleft_{b}\alpha\}
and

\mathcal{O}(\alpha)=\{o\in\mathcal{O}\mid o\triangleleft_{b}\alpha\}
.
Intuitively, the preorder on observations models a refinement between them:
o\triangleleft p
means that
p
is a finer observation w.r.t.
o
.
The relation
\triangleleft_{b}
relates observations to behaviours:
o\triangleleft_{b}\alpha
means that
o
approximates the behaviour
\alpha
.

Example 1
(Linear Time)

Assume a set of events
\mathcal{E}
.
In the standard setting of runtime verification,
we are interested in monitoring properties of single executions of a system.
Hence, in this setting,
we can take as set of behaviours either the set
\mathcal{E}^{\omega}
of infinite traces or the set
\mathcal{E}^{\infty}
of finite or infinite traces.
Then, the set of observations can be the set
\mathcal{E}^{\star}
of finite traces and both relations
\triangleleft
and
\triangleleft_{b}
are given by prefixing.

Example 2
(Hyperproperties)

Assume again a set of events
\mathcal{E}
.
In some cases, we may be interested in monitoring properties on multiple executions of a system.
In this case, we can consider as set of behaviours the set
\wp(\mathcal{E}^{\omega})
(resp.
\wp(\mathcal{E}^{\infty})
) of sets of infinite (resp. finite or infinite) traces of events.
Here, the set of observation can be
\wp_{F}(\mathcal{E}^{\star})
consisting of finite sets of finite traces of events, where the refinement order is given by

X\triangleleft Y
iff for all
u\in X
there is
v\in Y
such that
u
is a prefix of
v
.
Finally, the relation
\triangleleft_{b}
relating observations and behaviours will be given by

X\triangleleft_{b}T
iff for all
u\in X
there is
\sigma\in T
such that
u
is a prefix of
\sigma
.
Note that, a property in this setting is what is usually called
hyperproperty

[
11
]
.

Example 3
(Branching Time)

Assume again a set of events
\mathcal{E}
.
In a branching time setting, we can take as set of behaviours the set
\mathsf{Tr}^{\infty}(\mathcal{E})
of finite and infinite trees with edges labelled by events in
\mathcal{E}
.
We illustrate two possible observation structures.
A first choice for observations can be the set of finite traces
\mathcal{E}^{\star}
with the prefixing ordering.
The approximation relation is given by
u\triangleleft_{b}t
iff

t
contains a path starting from the root labelled by
u
.
Another choice for observations can be the set
\wp_{F}(\mathcal{E}^{\star})
with the structure of
Example

2
.
The approximation relation is given by
X\triangleleft_{b}t
iff
for all
u\in X
there is a path in
t
starting from the root labelled by
u
.

In the following, let us fix an observation structure
\langle\mathcal{O},\triangleleft,\triangleleft_{b}\rangle
over
\mathcal{B}
.
Monitoring a property means trying to establish whether the system satisfies or violates it just relying on a (finite) observation of it.
Formally, a monitor is a function
M:\mathcal{O}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}_{3}
where
\mathcal{V}_{3}
is the set of
verdicts

\{\mathsf{yes},\mathsf{no},\mathsf{?}\}
.
This means that a monitor, given an observation, can either accept it (verdict
\mathsf{yes}
), or reject it (verdict
\mathsf{no}
), or be inconclusive (verdict
\mathsf{?}
).
On
\mathcal{V}_{3}
it is clearly defined an
information order

\preceq
given by

\mathsf{?}\preceq\mathsf{yes}
and
\mathsf{?}\preceq\mathsf{no}
.
A monitor
M
is
impartial

[
14
]
if it is monotone, that is,

o\triangleleft p
implies
M(o)\preceq M(p)
, for all
op\in\mathcal{O}
.
This means that it cannot retract conclusive verdicts when considering finer observations.

Given a property
P
, we define the
abstract monitor
of
P
to be a function

\mathcal{M}_{P}:\mathcal{O}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}_{3}
given as follows:

\mathcal{M}_{P}(o)=\begin{cases}\mathsf{yes}&\mathcal{B}(o)\subseteq P\\
\mathsf{no}&\mathcal{B}(o)\cap P=\emptyset\\
\mathsf{?}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

In other words,

\mathcal{M}_{P}(o)=\mathsf{yes}
when all behaviours approximated by
o

satisfy

P
, in which case we say that
o

positively determines
P
,

\mathcal{M}_{P}(u)=\mathsf{no}
when all behaviours approximated by
o

violate

P
, in which case we say that
o

negatively determines
P
, and

\mathcal{M}_{P}(o)=\mathsf{?}
when no conclusive verdict can be emitted yet.
We say that
o

determines
P
when
\mathcal{M}_{P}(o)\neq\mathsf{?}
.
It is easy to check that the abstract monitor is impartial.

Proposition 1

Let
P
be a property on
\mathcal{B}
.
Then, for all
o,p\in\mathcal{O}
,

o\triangleleft p
implies

\mathcal{M}_{P}(o)\preceq\mathcal{M}_{P}(p)
.

We say that an impartial monitor
M
is
sound
for a property
P
if, for all
o\in\mathcal{O}
,

M(o)\preceq\mathcal{M}_{P}(o)
, that is, it cannot contraddict the abstract monitor of
P
.
Moreover, we say that
M
is
almost complete
if, for all
o\in\mathcal{O}
, there exists
p\in\mathcal{O}
such that

o\triangleleft p
and
M(p)=\mathcal{M}_{P}(o)
.
In other words,
M
is almost complete when it can always eventually meet the verdict of the abstract monitor of
P
.

The following lemmas relate the abstract monitor with set inclusion and complementation; where the following holds:

\overline{\mathsf{yes}}=\mathsf{no}
,
\overline{\mathsf{no}}=\mathsf{yes}
and
\overline{\mathsf{?}}=\mathsf{?}
.

Lemma 1

Let
P,Q
be properties on
\mathcal{B}
such that
P\subseteq Q
and
o\in\mathcal{O}
be an observation.
Then,

\mathcal{M}_{P}(o)=\mathsf{yes}
implies
\mathcal{M}_{Q}(o)=\mathsf{yes}
and

\mathcal{M}_{Q}(o)=\mathsf{no}
implies
\mathcal{M}_{P}(o)=\mathsf{no}
.

Lemma 2

Let
P
be a property on
\mathcal{B}
and
o\in\mathcal{O}
be an observation.
Then,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}\setminus P}(o)=\overline{\mathcal{M}_{P}(o)}
.

Using the abstract monitor, we define when a property
P
is
monitorable
(w.r.t. the notion of
\forall
-monitorable
[
27
]
).
This happens when the abstract monitor has always the possibility of emitting a positive or negative verdict.

Definition 2

A property
P
on
\mathcal{B}
is
monitorable
if,
for every observation
o\in\mathcal{O}
, there exists
p\in\mathcal{O}
such that

o\triangleleft p
and

\mathcal{M}_{P}(p)\neq\mathsf{?}
.

Thus, every observation has a refinement that determines the property.
Another feature of monitorable properties is that they are closed under complementation.

Proposition 2

Let
P
be a monitorable property on
\mathcal{B}
.
Then,
\mathcal{B}\setminus P
is monitorable.

We write

Let

3
Partial monitoring via (co)safety completions

When we want to monitor a property
P
,
usually we first check whether it is monitorable, to ensure that the monitor will not run forever even when it will never reach any conclusive verdict.
In this section, we propose a different approach, describing its semantic foundations:
instead of ruling out non-monitorable properties, we show it is always possible to find monitorable (over/under) approximations preserving either negative or positive verdicts.
Moreover, combining such approximations, we define a generalised abstract monitor which detects when it will not be able to provide any conclusive verdict.

To develop our approximations, we rely on standard lattice-theoretic notions for which we refer to
[
12
]
.
In particular, the set of all properties over
\mathcal{B}
, that is, the powerset
\wp(\mathcal{B})
, is a complete lattice w.r.t. subset inclusion.
Then, the key idea is to look for approximations of properties in the classes of safety and cosafety properties, which are well-behaved subsets of
\wp(\mathcal{B})
.

Let us start with safety properties.
Informally, a safety property is a property for which we can always finitely determine whether a behaviour violates it.

Definition 3
(Safety property)

A property
P
on
\mathcal{B}
is a
safety property
if
for every
\alpha\notin P
there is an observation
o\triangleleft_{b}\alpha
that negatively determines it.

Proposition 3

P
is a safety property on
\mathcal{B}
if and only if
for every
\alpha\in\mathcal{B}
,
\mathcal{B}(o)\cap P\neq\emptyset
for each observation
o\triangleleft_{b}\alpha
implies
\alpha\in P
.

This notion is pretty standard
[
27
]
and usually safety properties come with a crucial feature: they are always monitorable.
However, in our abstract setting, this is not true in general.
For instance,
Example

4
(
2
) shows a safety property which is not monitorable.
The issue with this example is that observations do not have enough structure to support monitorability of safety properties.
Indeed, if we consider the same property in the second observation structure of
Example

3
, it is monitorable.
Intuitively, the problem is that, if we use finite traces to monitor branching time properties, the monitor can fall into a branch where there is no hope to reach a conclusive verdict.
Hence, to recover monitorability of safety properties, we need to assume a richer structure on observations (see below).

Definition 4

An observation structure
\langle\mathcal{O},\triangleleft,\triangleleft_{b}\rangle
over
\mathcal{B}
is
directed
if,
for all
o,p\in\mathcal{O}
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{B}
,
o\triangleleft_{b}\alpha
and
p\triangleleft_{b}\alpha
imply that there is
q\in\mathcal{O}
such that

q\triangleleft_{b}\alpha
and
o\triangleleft q
and
p\triangleleft q
.

In other words, the set
\mathcal{O}(\alpha)
is a directed set w.r.t.
\triangleleft
for every
\alpha\in\mathcal{B}
.
Note that, all the examples of observation structures we have seen are directed except for the first one in
Example

3
.
Now, for directed observation structures, we can prove that safety properties are monitorable.

Proposition 4

Let
\langle\mathcal{O},\triangleleft,\triangleleft_{b}\rangle
be a directed observation structure on
\mathcal{B}
.
Then, every safety property is monitorable.

From now on let us assume a directed observation structure
\langle\mathcal{O},\triangleleft,\triangleleft_{b}\rangle
over
\mathcal{B}
.
Denote by
\mathbb{S}
the set of all safety properties over
\mathcal{B}
.
Then, the set
\mathbb{S}
is a suborder of
\wp(\mathcal{B})
and the following lemma shows it is a closure system, that is, it is closed under arbitrary intersections.

Lemma 3

\mathbb{S}
is a closure system.

Like any closure system,
\mathbb{S}
induces a closure operator on
\wp(\mathcal{B})
:

\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}}(P)=\bigcap\{Q\in\mathbb{S}\mid P\subseteq Q\}

Being a closure operator,
\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}}
is a monotone function on
\wp(\mathcal{B})
w.r.t. subset inclusion and has the following two properties for any
P\subseteq\mathcal{B}
:

P\subseteq\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}}(P)
and
\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}}(\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}}(P))\subseteq\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}}(P)
.
In other words,
\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}}(P)
is
the best safety (over)approximation of
P
and we call it the
safety completion of
P
.

We now give an alternative characterisation of
\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}}
.
Let
P
be a property on
\mathcal{B}
.
Define the set
\mathsf{NR}(P)
of
P
-unrefutable
behaviours as follows:

\mathsf{NR}(P)=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{B}\mid\mathcal{B}(o)\cap P\neq\emptyset\text{ for all }o\triangleleft_{b}\alpha\}

Proposition 5

\mathsf{NR}
is a closure operator.

Note that, by
Proposition

3
,
P
is a safety property if and only if
\mathsf{NR}(P)\subseteq P
if and only if
P
is a fixpoint of
\mathsf{NR}
, as
\mathsf{NR}
is a closure operator.
Then, we get the following result.

Corollary 1

\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}}(P)=\mathsf{NR}(P)
, for all
P\subseteq\mathcal{B}
.

The key property of the safety completion of
P
is that it exactly preserves all negative verdicts of
P
as proved by the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1

Let
P
be a property on
\mathcal{B}
and
o\in\mathcal{O}
.
Then,
\mathcal{M}_{P}(o)=\mathsf{no}
if and only if
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}}(P)}(o)=\mathsf{no}
.

Therefore, if we are interested in detecting just violations of
P
, we can monitor its safety completion, which is necessarily monitorable and emits all and only negative verdicts of
P
.
Moreover, when
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}}(P)}(o)=\mathsf{yes}
, we are sure that
\mathcal{M}_{P}(o)\neq\mathsf{no}
and, since an abstract monitor is impartial, we can safely stop the execution, as we will never receive a negative verdict.

We now dualise everything considering cosafety properties.
Informally, a cosafety property is a property for which we can always finitely determine whether a behaviour satisfies it.

Definition 5
(Cosafety property)

A property
P
on
\mathcal{B}
is a
cosafety property
if
for every
\alpha\in P
, there exists an observation
o\triangleleft_{b}\alpha
positively determining
P
.

Proposition 6

P
is a cosafety property if and only if
\mathcal{B}\setminus P
is a safety property.

This proposition allows us to dualise all results obtained for safety properties.
Denote by
{\mathsf{co}\mathbb{S}}
the set of all cosafety properties on
\mathcal{B}
.
From
Propositions

2
and
4
we get that cosafety properties are monitorable and from
Lemma

3
we get that
{\mathsf{co}\mathbb{S}}
, as a suborder of
\wp(\mathcal{B})
, is an interior system, that is, it is closed under arbitrary unions.
Therefore, we get an interior operator
on
\wp(\mathcal{B})
:

\Delta_{{\mathsf{co}\mathbb{S}}}(P)=\bigcup\{Q\in{\mathsf{co}\mathbb{S}}\mid Q\subseteq P\}

Being an interior operator,
\Delta_{{\mathsf{co}\mathbb{S}}}
is a monotone function on
\wp(\mathcal{B})
with respect to set inclusion and has the following two properties for any
P\subseteq\mathcal{B}

\Delta_{{\mathsf{co}\mathbb{S}}}(P)\subseteq P
and
\Delta_{{\mathsf{co}\mathbb{S}}}(P)\subseteq\Delta_{{\mathsf{co}\mathbb{S}}}(\Delta_{{\mathsf{co}\mathbb{S}}}(P))
.
In other words,
\Delta_{{\mathsf{co}\mathbb{S}}}(P)
is
the best cosafety (under)approximation of
P
and we call it the
cosafety completion of
P
.

It is easy to see that

\mathcal{B}\setminus\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}}(P)=\Delta_{{\mathsf{co}\mathbb{S}}}(\mathcal{B}\setminus P)
.
Hence, the cosafety completion of
P
exactly preserves positive verdicts of
P
, as proved below.

Corollary 2

Let
P
be a property on
\mathcal{B}
and
o\in\mathcal{O}
.
Then,
\mathcal{M}_{P}(o)=\mathsf{yes}
if and only if
\mathcal{M}_{\Delta_{{\mathsf{co}\mathbb{S}}}(P)}(o)=\mathsf{yes}
.

Therefore, if we are interested in detecting just satisfaction of
P
, we can monitor its cosafety completion, which is necessarily monitorable and emits all and only positive verdicts of
P
.
Moreover, when
\mathcal{M}_{\Delta_{{\mathsf{co}\mathbb{S}}}(P)}(u)=\mathsf{no}
, we are sure that
\mathcal{M}_{P}(u)\neq\mathsf{yes}
and, since an abstract monitor is impartial, we can safely stop the execution as we will never receive a positive verdict.

In summary, for any property
P
on
\mathcal{B}
,
there are two monitorable properties

\Delta_{{\mathsf{co}\mathbb{S}}}(P)\subseteq P\subseteq\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}}(P)
,
where the former captures exactly positive verdicts of
P
and the latter captures exactly negative verdicts of
P
.
However, combining the two completions, we get even more information: we can characterise the so called
ugly
observations, that is, those observations after which it is not possible to emit any conclusive verdict.
These are precisely those observations that the monitorability constraint rules out, but, if they can be detected, there is no need to restrict ourselves to monitorable properties.

Proposition 7

Let
P
be a property on
\mathcal{B}
and
o\in\mathcal{O}
an observation.
Then,
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}}(P)}(o)=\mathsf{yes}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\Delta_{{\mathsf{co}\mathbb{S}}}(P)}(o)=\mathsf{no}

if and only if,
for every
p\in\mathcal{O}
such that
o\triangleleft p
,
\mathcal{M}_{P}(p)=\mathsf{?}
.

Relying on these results, we define a notion of generalised monitor as a function
\widehat{M}:\mathcal{O}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}_{6}

\mathcal{V}_{6}
is a set of verdicts consisting of six elements:

\mathsf{yes}
,
\mathsf{no}
and
\mathsf{?}
as before and
\mathsf{?}_{\mathsf{yes}}
, when no negative verdict can be reached,

\mathsf{?}_{\mathsf{no}}
, when no positive verdict can be reached, and

\mathsf{\chi}

5
Related Work

The present work can be seen as a foundational counterpart of
[
16
]
, where the authors present the idea of detecting ugly prefixes of LTL properties at runtime from a practical perspective.
Indeed, they focus only on implementation aspects and
no study on the theory behind it is done whatsoever.
To provide such a foundation in a very general setting, covering both linear and branching time, we build on the framework in
[
28
]
.
They consider also gray-box monitoring and a concrete instantiation on hyperproperties, which we do not address, but they are both interesting directions for further work.

In this paper, we consider the notion of monitorability introduced by Pnueli and Zaks
[
27
]
and Peled and Havelund
[
24
]
, which generalises the first proposal
[
19
]
.
This is one of the most commonly adopted in the literature and it has a more operational nature.
That is, it focuses on the fact that a monitor should not run uselessly
rather than on the verdicts it can produce.

Other notions of monitorability are considered, for instance, by Aceto et al.
[
1
]
.
Their completely monitorable properties corresponds to properties that are both safety and cosafety, while partially monitorable properties correspond to either safety or cosafety properties, depending on whether one focuses on satisfaction or violation.
Hence, in the linear-time these notions are stronger than the one we consider, while in branching-time they are incomparable, since, as we have noticed,
depending on the observation structure, (co)safety properties may not be monitorable in our setting.

Aceto et al.
[
3
]
introduce the notion of
best monitorable consequence
, which can be seen as an instance of our best safety approximation of a property, at least when no additional condition on monitors is imposed. They also provide a syntactic construction to compute the best monitorable consequence of
recHML
formulas, which could be a good starting point to obtain an instance of generalised monitors in a branching-time setting.
5
5

5

The observation structure they use is not directed (
Definition

4
), hence we will need to adjust it, for instance considering hyperproperties.

On a more general side, different works can be found on the monitorability of LTL properties.
In
[
29
]
, an extension of LTL called robust LTL (rLTL) is presented. With respect to LTL, rLTL semantics allows for distinguishing various “degrees” to which a
trace violates a property. This is obtained through five different verdicts (instead of the standard three), which quantitatively denote how much the property has been violated. It is important to note that in rLTL, all properties become monitorable
[
23
]
; simply because the resulting monitor does not look for the complete satisfaction (resp., violation) of the property, but it settles for a certain degree of satisfaction (resp., violation) of the latter.

In
[
7
]
, a two-step approach to address monitorability of LTL properties is proposed. Specifically, the satisfaction (resp., violation) of the LTL property is given by taking into consideration a prediction over the future of the analysed trace. Differently from other works
[
31
]
, such prediction is only based upon past experience,
i.e.
, by only looking at how the trace behaved in the past. According to such prediction, the LTL property is evaluated considering how far the trace is supposed to be from satisfying or violating the property. Moreover, such distance is evaluated considering the safety and cosafety aspects of the property.

In
[
18
]
, an approach to extend the set of monitorable LTL properties through assumptions over the system is presented. Thanks to the additional information on the system, not all possible future continuations, given a prefix, are considered, but only a certain subset of them. Consequently, properties that are considered non-monitorable in general, can be monitored when such subset of traces reduces to only satisfying (resp., violating) traces.

With respect to the previously listed works, our contribution moves forward towards the concept of partial RV. Instead of focusing on extending LTL semantics, or presenting a predictive version of the latter, we present a general-purpose approach to partially monitor formal properties. Since we tackle this problem at a semantic level, our theoretical contribution is not limited to LTL; even though we present a possible instantiation exploiting LTL. Moreover, works in literature are more focused on estimating fully non-monitorable properties
6
6

6

Where with “fully” non-monitorable, we mean properties for which there are no prefixes that determine them neither positively, nor negatively.
, while we care for properties that are indeed non-monitorable in general, but can be monitored in some scenarios (
i.e.
some prefixes can still determine the property at runtime). In fact, we present a solution that can be used both for monitoring the monitorable parts of a given property, and at the same time, to safely recognise when to stop the verification; since no final verdict will ever be concluded.

6
Conclusions and Future Work

This paper introduces partial RV, studying its semantic foundations.
We show how to build monitors capable to recognise when they will not be able to reach any conclusive verdict, and then to stop their execution.
In this way, we can
partially
monitor any property, avoiding issues related to non-monitorability, that is, having monitors running forever even though they will never be able to reach any conclusive verdict.
We show that this can be achieved by combining safety and cosafety completions, which are well-behaved monitorable over/under approximations of any property.
These results are developed independently from specific setups, covering both branching and linear time settings.
Then, we present concrete instantiations of the framework for linear time properties, using LTL and Linear Time
\nu
-calculus as concrete languages.
In both cases, we developed prototypes, available as supplementary material, with an empirical evaluation of their performances.

As future work, we are planning to further test our prototypes on a set of well-known properties (a starting point could be the ones listed in
[
9
]
).
On a more theoretical perspective, we are considering to further explore the branching time scenario, and its implications in our monitoring setting.

Definition 10
(Rank of a term)

Let
\mathsf{t}
be a LT
\nu

\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{t})
computes the

\mathsf{t}

\begin{array}[]{lcllcl}\mathcal{R}(\top)&=&0&\qquad\mathcal{R}(\bot)&=&0\\
\mathcal{R}(p)&=&0&\qquad\mathcal{R}(p^{\bot})&=&0\\
\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{t}\land\mathsf{s})&=&1+\max\{\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{t}),\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{s})\}&\qquad\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{t}\lor\mathsf{s})&=&1+\max\{\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{t}),\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{s})\}\\

\mathop{\circ}\mathsf{s}
.

0.A.6
Linear Temporal Logic

The semantics of LTL formulae is specified by a satisfation relation
w,i\mathrel{\models}_{L}\phi
(see
Definition

14
),
stating that an infinite trace
w
satisfies the formula
\phi
starting from the index
i\in\mathbb{N}
. We write
w\mathrel{\models}_{L}\phi
iff
w,0\mathrel{\models}_{L}\phi
.
The semantics of a formula
\phi
is then defined as
\llbracket\phi\rrbracket=\{w\in\mathcal{E}^{\omega}\mid w\mathrel{\models}_{L}\phi\}
.

Definition 13
(LTL Syntax)

The set of
negation free
LTL formulae is inductively defined by the grammar

\phi,\psi\Coloneqq\top\mid\bot\mid p\mid p^{\bot}\mid\phi\land\psi\mid\phi\lor\psi\mid\phi\mathrel{\mathcal{U}}\psi\mid\phi\mathrel{\mathcal{R}}\psi\mid\mathop{\circ}\phi

We use the following abbreviations

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathop{\Diamond}\phi&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny\sf def}}}{{=}}&\top\mathrel{\mathcal{U}}\phi\\
\mathop{\square}\phi&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny\sf def}}}{{=}}&\neg\mathop{\Diamond}\neg\phi\end{array}

(LTL Semantics)
