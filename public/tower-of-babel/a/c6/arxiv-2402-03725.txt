Charge correlator expansion for free fermion negativity Preprint: APS/123-QED
Yang-Yang Tang

Abstract
Logarithmic negativity is a widely used entanglement measure in quantum information theories, which can also be efficiently computed in quantum many-body systems by replica trick or by relating to correlation matrices. In this paper, we demonstrate that in free-fermion systems with conserved charge, Rényi and logarithmic negativity can be expanded by connected charge correlators, analogous to the case for entanglement entropy in the context of full counting statistics (FCS). We confirm the rapid convergence of this expansion in random all-connected Hamiltonian through numerical verification, especially for systems with only local hopping. We find that the replica trick that get logarithmic negativity from the limit of Rényi negativity is valid in this method only for translational invariant systems. Using this expansion, we analyze the scaling behavior of negativity in extensive free-fermion systems. In particular, in 1+1 dimensional free-fermion systems, we observe that the scaling behavior of negativity from our expansion is consistent with known results from the method with Toeplitz matrix. These findings provide insights into the entanglement properties of free-fermion systems, and demonstrate the efficacy of the expansion approach in studying entanglement measures.



I
Introduction

Entanglement, which refers to the non-separability of
multipartite quantum states

Werner 1989
, is widely regarded as the most
non-classical phenomenon in quantum mechanics

Horodecki et al. 2009
. Due to the significant utility of
entangled states in quantum information theories and experiments,
various entanglement detections and measures have been
proposed to detect and quantify the entanglement of quantum
states
Gühne and Tóth 2009
;
Plbnio and Virmani 2007
. For pure
states, the well-known von Neumann entropy uniquely captures
their entanglement
Plbnio and Virmani 2007
. However, for
mixed states, the condition is complicated, as entanglement entropy
contains both classical and quantum correlations. It is widely
believed that there is no unique entanglement measure for mixed
states, and therefore various entanglement measures are defined
for different purposes
Plbnio and Virmani 2007
.

Among the various entanglement measures, the (logarithmic) negativity,
also known as partial transpose (PT) negativity

Vidal and Werner 2002
, is the most
commonly employed one in the study of quantum many-body systems.
This measure is particularly useful as it can be computed efficiently
compared to other measures that rely on variational expressions

Plbnio and Virmani 2007
.
The negativity measure is inspired by the positive partial
transpose (PPT) criterion

Peres 1996
;
Horodecki et al. 1996
, which claims that for
separable (non-entangled) states
\rho
, the partial transposition
of the density matrix
\rho^{T_{1}}
have no negative eigenvalues. Negativity quantifies
how negative the eigenvalues of
\rho^{T_{1}}
are, and has been
shown to bound the utility of the state
\rho
in quantum teleportation

Vidal and Werner 2002
.
Moreover, the logarithmic negativity is additive for product states
and thus is extensively used in quantum many-body systems
Calabrese et al. 2012a
;
Calabrese et al. 2013
.
It has also been
proven to bound the entanglement that can be asymptotically distilled
from
\rho

Vidal and Werner 2002
.

Fermionic systems possess an alternative definition for negativity,
known as partial time-reversal (TR) negativity, which exhibits
superior properties compared to the fermionic PT negativity based
on the corresponding bosonic density matrix

Shapourian et al. 2017a
;
Shapourian et al. 2017b
. The key advantage
of partial TR negativity is that the partial TR of a fermionic
Gaussian state remains Gaussian. This feature is particularly
useful for free-fermion systems, as the partial TR negativity for
the ground state or thermal equilibrium state at finite temperature
can then be efficiently computed in terms of two-point correlator
matrices
Shapourian et al. 2017a
;
Shapourian and Ryu 2019
. Therefore,
partial TR negativity offers a more efficient and accurate
method for studying negativity in fermionic systems.

Negativity can also be computed by replica trick, where the logarithmic
negativity
\mathcal{E}
is the replica limit

negativity

;
Calabrese et al. 2013
;
Shapourian et al. 2017a
;
Shapourian and Ryu 2019
,
analogous to the case for
von Neumann entropy and Rényi entropy. For certain geometry and dimension,
both bosonic

;
Calabrese et al. 2013
and fermionic

;
Shapourian and Ryu 2019

negativity

can be computed by CFT methods,
and therefore the scaling behavior of logarithmic
negativity

can also be analyzed.

II
Correlator expansion for fermion negativity

II.1
Review: Charge cumulant expansion for fermion entropy

Charge cumulant expansion for entropy was first derived in the context
of FCS
Klich and Levitov 2009
. Here we briefly review the alternative
derivation by replica method
Tam et al. 2022
. The von Neumann entropy
and
n
-th Rényi
entropy of region
A
in a bipartite system
A\cup B
is defined as

S_{A}=-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}\log\rho_{A})

(13)

and

S_{A}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{1-n}\log\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}^{n}),

(14)

where
\rho_{A}=\mathrm{Tr}_{B}(\rho_{AB})
is the
reduced density matrix on subsystem
A
.
In replica methods we calculate
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}^{n})
, and to get von Neumann entropy we take the limit

S_{A}=\lim_{n\rightarrow 1}S_{A}^{(n)}=\lim_{n\rightarrow 1}\frac{1}{1-n}\log\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}^{n})

(15)

or

S_{A}=-\left.\frac{\partial\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}^{n})}{\partial n}\right|_{n=1}=-\left.\frac{\partial\log\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}^{n})}{\partial n}\right|_{n=1}.

(16)

\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}^{n})
can be written in the form of the expectation
value of a twist operator
by inserting the overcomplete bases of coherent states.
For a single-fermion system,

\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)=\sum_{n}\braket{n|\rho|n}=\int d\bar{\eta}d\eta e^{-\bar{\eta}\eta}\braket{-\bar{\eta}|\rho|\eta}

(17)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^{n})=

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{i}}\braket{n_{1}|\rho|n_{2}}\cdots\braket{n_{n}|\rho|n_{1}}

(18)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d\bar{\eta}_{1}d\eta_{1}\cdots d\bar{\eta}_{n}d\eta_{n}e^{-\sum_{i}\bar{\eta}_{i}\eta_{i}}

\displaystyle\times\braket{-\bar{\eta}_{1}|\rho|\eta_{2}}\braket{\bar{\eta}_{2}|\rho|\eta_{3}}\cdots\braket{\bar{\eta}_{n}|\rho|\eta_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d\bar{\eta}_{1}d\eta_{1}\cdots d\bar{\eta}_{n}d\eta_{n}e^{-\sum_{i}\bar{\eta}_{i}\eta_{i}}

\displaystyle\times\braket{-\bar{\eta}_{1}|\rho|-\eta_{2}}\braket{-\bar{\eta}_{2}|\rho|-\eta_{3}}\cdots\braket{-\bar{\eta}_{n}|\rho|\eta_{1}},

where
\ket{\eta}
and

are independent coherent
states of fermion satisfying
\psi\ket{\eta}=\eta\ket{\eta}

The minus sign comes from the anticommutation relation of
fermion operator

Therefore for a bipartite system, we can view
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}^{n})
as

n

\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}^{n})=\braket{T_{n}},

(19)

where the operation of

T_{n}(A)\ket{\eta_{1},\cdots,\eta_{n-1},\eta_{n}}=\ket{-\eta_{2},\cdots,-\eta_{n},\eta_{1}}

(20)

or equivalently for the fermion operators

\begin{aligned} T_{n}(A)^{\dagger}\psi_{a}T_{n}(A)=\begin{cases}-\psi_{a+1}\ &a<n\\
\psi_{1}\ &a=n\end{cases}\end{aligned}.

(21)

We can also write
T_{n}
in the matrix form as

\begin{aligned} T_{n}(A)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
\ &0&-1\\
\ &\ &\ddots&\ddots\\
\ &\ &\ &0&-1\\
1&\ &\ &\ &0\end{pmatrix}\end{aligned}.

(22)

In region

the operation is the
identity matrix

\begin{aligned} T_{n}(B)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\ &1\\
\ &\ &\ddots\\
\ &\ &\ &1\\
\ &\ &\ &\ &1\end{pmatrix}\end{aligned}.

(23)

To calculate
\braket{T_{n}}
, we diagonalize
T_{n}(A)

by introducing Fourier transformation in replica space

\tilde{\psi}_{p}(\bm{r})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{a=1}^{n}e^{-\frac{2\pi ip}{n}a}\psi_{a}(\bm{r}),

(24)

where

(25)

Here we have the freedom to adjust the choice of the range of
p

by adding integer multiplies of

(

-(n-1)/2
to
(n+1)/2

In the ”momentum” replica space

T_{n}^{\dagger}\tilde{\psi}_{p}(\bm{r})T_{n}=\tilde{\psi}_{p}(\bm{r})\begin{cases}-e^{\frac{2\pi ip}{n}}&\bm{r}\in A\\
1&\bm{r}\in B.\end{cases}

(26)

and through the anticommutation relation of fermion operators
we can get

T_{n}=\prod_{p=-\frac{n-1}{2}}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}e^{(\frac{2\pi ip}{n}+\pi i)Q_{A,p}},

(27)

where

Q_{A,p}=\int_{\bm{r}\in A}d^{D}\bm{r}\tilde{\psi}_{p}^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\tilde{\psi}_{p}(\bm{r})

(28)

is the total charge operator in
A

In some texts there is another form of

\begin{aligned} T_{n}(A)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
\ &0&1\\
\ &\ &\ddots&\ddots\\
\ &\ &\ &0&1\\
(-1)^{n+1}&\ &\ &\ &0\end{pmatrix}\end{aligned},

(29)

which will result in

T_{n}=\prod_{p=-\frac{n-1}{2}}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}e^{\frac{2\pi ip}{n}Q_{A,p}}.

(30)

For free-fermion systems with conserved

), (

) and (

Thus all

, and to get (

\displaystyle=\sum_{M=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{p}p^{M}\right)\frac{1}{M!}\left(\frac{2\pi i}{n}\right)^{M}\braket{Q_{A}^{M}}_{c}.

For the range of

(34)

Note that this is the only natural choice to make

smallest

(

\mathrm{even}\ M\end{subarray}}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{M!}\left(\frac{2\pi i}{n}\right)^{M}\braket{Q_{A}^{M}}_{c}

and it is usually assumed that by taking limit
n_{e}\rightarrow 1

while keeping
n_{e}
even we would get the negativity
\mathcal{E}
.
In
Shapourian et al. 2017a
it is proved that Rényi negativity
can also be written as the expectation value of a twist operator

\mathcal{E}_{n}=\braket{T_{n}},

(45)

where the operation of the twist operator on the three regions is

\begin{aligned} T_{n}(A)=\begin{pmatrix}0&\ &\ &\ &(-1)^{n-1}\\
1&0\\
\ &1&0\\
\ &\ &\ddots&\ddots\\
&\ &\ &1&0\end{pmatrix}\end{aligned},

(46)

\begin{aligned} T_{n}(B)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
\ &0&-1\\
\ &\ &\ddots&\ddots\\
\ &\ &\ &0&-1\\
1&\ &\ &\ &0\end{pmatrix}\end{aligned},

(47)

and

\begin{aligned} T_{n}(C)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\ &1\\
\ &\ &\ddots\\
\ &\ &\ &1\\
\ &\ &\ &\ &1\end{pmatrix}\end{aligned}.

(48)

The matrices (
46
) and (
47
) can be
simultaneously diagonalized only when
n
is even.
Similarly with
(
27
), for even
n_{e}
it is direct to get

T_{n_{e}}=\prod_{p=-\frac{n_{e}-1}{2}}^{\frac{n_{e}-1}{2}}e^{-\frac{2\pi ip}{n_{e}}Q_{A,p}+(\frac{2\pi ip}{n_{e}}+\pi i)Q_{B,p}},

(49)

which is equivalent to (57) of
Shapourian et al. 2017a

for free-fermion systems with conserved
U(1)
charge,
since they differ with only a shift of the range of
p
.
Similarly with the case for Rényi entropy,
for free-fermion systems with conserved charge
the expectation value of
T_{n}
decouples into each momentum replica space

\braket{T_{n_{e}}}=\prod_{p=-\frac{n_{e}-1}{2}}^{\frac{n_{e}-1}{2}}\braket{e^{-\frac{2\pi ip}{n_{e}}Q_{A,p}+(\frac{2\pi ip}{n_{e}}+\pi i)Q_{B,p}}}.

(50)

Now in order to get the cumulant expansion,
we shift the phase before
Q_{B,p}
by
2\pi i
for the positive
half of
p
, so

\displaystyle\braket{T_{n_{e}}}=

\displaystyle\prod_{p=-\frac{n_{e}-1}{2}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\braket{e^{-\frac{2\pi ip}{n_{e}}Q_{A,p}+(\frac{2\pi ip}{n_{e}}+\pi i)Q_{B,p}}}

(51)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{p=\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{n_{e}-1}{2}}\braket{e^{-\frac{2\pi ip}{n_{e}}Q_{A,p}+(\frac{2\pi ip}{n_{e}}-\pi i)Q_{B,p}}}.

Then we apply the cumulant expansion and get

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n_{e}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{M=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{M!}(\pi i)^{M}

(52)

\displaystyle\times\left[\sum_{p=-\frac{n_{e}-1}{2}}^{-1/2}\left<{\left(-\frac{2p}{n_{e}}Q_{A}+\left(\frac{2p}{n_{e}}+1\right)Q_{B}\right)^{M}}\right>_{c}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{p=\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{n_{e}-1}{2}}\left<{\left(-\frac{2p}{n_{e}}Q_{A}+\left(\frac{2p}{n_{e}}-1\right)Q_{B}\right)^{M}}\right>_{c}\right].

Here the range of
p
is chosen for a similar reason
as (
33
). This is also the only way to make
the coefficient
smallest for even
M
and 0 for odd
M
,
which is the key point for the convergence of the expansion.
Written the expansion by orders of
M
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n_{e}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M=2\\
\mathrm{even}\ M\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}_{n_{e}}^{(M)},

(53)

Summing over
p
we get the first two terms

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n_{e}}^{(2)}=-\pi^{2}

\displaystyle\left[\frac{n_{e}^{2}-1}{6n_{e}}(\braket{Q_{A}^{2}}_{c}+\braket{Q_{B}^{2}}_{c})\right.

(54)

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{n_{e}^{2}+2}{6n_{e}}\braket{Q_{A}Q_{B}}_{c}\right],

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n_{e}}^{(4)}=\pi^{4}

\displaystyle\left[\frac{3n_{e}^{4}-10n_{e}^{2}+7}{360n_{e}^{3}}(\braket{Q_{A}^{4}}_{c}+\braket{Q_{B}^{4}}_{c})\right.

(55)

\displaystyle+\frac{3n_{e}^{4}+10n_{e}^{2}-28}{360n_{e}^{3}}(\braket{Q_{A}^{3}Q_{B}}_{c}+\braket{Q_{A}Q_{B}^{3}}_{c})

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{n_{e}^{4}+14}{120n_{e}^{3}}\braket{Q_{A}^{2}Q_{B}^{2}}_{c}\right].

It is also direct for higher orders. We have numerically
checked that this expansion converges rapidly for thermal equilibrium
states of randomly
generated all-connected free Hamiltonian, see Sec.
III
.
For Hamiltonian with local random hopping, the expansion is extremely
accurate at low temperature.

Taking the limit
n_{e}\rightarrow 1
we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n_{e}\rightarrow 1}^{(2)}=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}\braket{Q_{A}Q_{B}}_{c}

(56)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n_{e}\rightarrow 1}^{(4)}=\pi^{4}

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{1}{24}(\braket{Q_{A}^{3}Q_{B}}_{c}+\braket{Q_{A}Q_{B}^{3}}_{c})\right.

(57)

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{1}{8}\braket{Q_{A}^{2}Q_{B}^{2}}_{c}\right].

The analytic continuation is ambiguous
mathematically, and it is questionable whether
\mathcal{E}_{n_{e}\rightarrow 1}=\mathcal{E}
. In Sec.
III
we find that
the correspondence of the expansion for
\mathcal{E}_{n_{e}\rightarrow 1}
with
\mathcal{E}
requires translational
invariance in the Hamiltonian.

It is well known that Rényi and von Neumann entropy of Gaussian states can be computed
efficiently by relating to the two-point correlator matrices
Chung and Peschel 2001
;
Peschel 2003
;
Cheong and Henley 2004
.
Both Rényi and logarithmic negativity of Gaussian states
can also be related to correlator matrices, and so do
the connected charge correlators.
The correlator matrix of a bipartite system
A\cup B
can be partitioned to block
matrices as

C=\begin{pmatrix}C^{11}&C^{12}\\
C^{21}&C^{22}\end{pmatrix},

(58)

where the matrix elements are
C_{ij}=\braket{c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}}
.
C^{11}
and

C^{22}
are the reduced correlator matrices on the subsystems
A
and
B
, and

C^{12}
and
C^{21}
contain correlation between two regions.
Using Wick’s theorem, the connected charge correlators in the expansion can be reduced to the
trace of the product of the block matrices as

\braket{Q_{A}^{2}}_{c}=\mathrm{Tr}C^{11}-\mathrm{Tr}(C^{11})^{2},

(59)

\braket{Q_{A}Q_{B}}_{c}=-\mathrm{Tr}(C^{12}C^{21}),

(60)

\braket{Q_{B}^{2}}_{c}=\mathrm{Tr}C^{22}-\mathrm{Tr}(C^{22})^{2},

(61)

\braket{Q_{A}^{4}}_{c}=\mathrm{Tr}C^{11}-7\mathrm{Tr}(C^{11})^{2}+12\mathrm{Tr}(C^{11})^{3}-6\mathrm{Tr}(C^{11})^{4},

(62)

\displaystyle\braket{Q_{A}^{3}Q_{B}}_{c}=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Tr}(C^{12}C^{21})+6\mathrm{Tr}(C^{11}C^{12}C^{21})

(63)

\displaystyle-6\mathrm{Tr}(C^{11}C^{11}C^{12}C^{21}),

\displaystyle\braket{Q_{A}^{2}Q_{B}^{2}}_{c}=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Tr}(C^{12}C^{21})+2\mathrm{Tr}(C^{11}C^{12}C^{21})

(64)

\displaystyle+2\mathrm{Tr}(C^{22}C^{21}C^{12})-4\mathrm{Tr}(C^{11}C^{12}C^{21}C^{12})

\displaystyle-2\mathrm{Tr}(C^{12}C^{21})^{2},

\displaystyle\braket{Q_{A}Q_{B}^{3}}_{c}=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Tr}(C^{12}C^{21})+6\mathrm{Tr}(C^{22}C^{21}C^{12})

(65)

\displaystyle-6\mathrm{Tr}(C^{22}C^{22}C^{21}C^{12}),

\braket{Q_{B}^{4}}_{c}=\mathrm{Tr}C^{22}-7\mathrm{Tr}(C^{22})^{2}+12\mathrm{Tr}(C^{22})^{3}-6\mathrm{Tr}(C^{22})^{4}.

(66)

To calculate the negativity of Gaussian states, we follow the method developed in

Shapourian et al. 2017a
and
Shapourian and Ryu 2019
. Define the covariance matrices

\Gamma=\mathbb{I}-2C=\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma^{11}&\Gamma^{12}\\
\Gamma^{21}&\Gamma^{22}\end{pmatrix}

(67)

(where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity matrix), the corresponding transformed matrices

\Gamma_{\pm}=\begin{pmatrix}-\Gamma^{11}&\pm i\Gamma^{12}\\
\pm i\Gamma^{21}&\Gamma^{22}\end{pmatrix},

(68)

and a new single particle correlation function

C_{\Xi}=\frac{1}{2}[\mathbb{I}-(\mathbb{I}+\Gamma_{+}\Gamma_{-})^{-1}(\Gamma_{+}+\Gamma_{-})].

(69)

Denoting
\zeta_{i}
and
\xi_{i}
as the eigenvalues of
C
and
C_{\Xi}
respectively,
the logarithmic and Rényi negativity for free system with conserved charge is

\mathcal{E}=\sum_{j}\log[\xi_{j}^{1/2}+(1-\xi_{j})^{1/2}]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\log[\zeta_{j}^{2}+(1-\zeta_{j})^{2}]

(70)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n_{e}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\log[\xi_{j}^{n_{e}/2}+(1-\xi_{j})^{n_{e}/2}]

(71)

\displaystyle+\frac{n_{e}}{2}\sum_{j}\log[\zeta_{j}^{2}+(1-\zeta_{j})^{2}].

Using (
59
) to (
66
), we calculate the
first two orders of the charge correlator expansion (
54
)
and (
55
)
and compare it
with the exact
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{E}_{n_{e}}
calculated by
(
70
) and (
71
), see
Fig.
1
. From Fig.
1
(a)(b) we see that the convergence of Rényi negativity
is generally fast even for all-connected Hamiltonian. When there are only local hopping terms
that decays exponentially with the distance,
which is the normal case for extensive systems, the convergence is extremely accurate
at low temperature.
For high temperature the convergence becomes slow,
particularly for higher
n_{e}
.
Note that these phenomena are all similar to the case of the expansion for Rényi and
von Neumann entropy (
36
) and (
37
), where the convergence
is better for systems with low temperature and local hopping terms.

However, the correspondence of the expansion at replica limit
n_{e}\rightarrow 1
with logarithmic negativity
requires more constraint.
Fig.
1
(b) shows the bad convergence even for local random Hamiltonian,
from Fig.
1
(c) we see that the convergence
is valid for translational invariant systems. With translational symmetry, the convergence is also
better in systems with local hopping terms.

Therefore, from numerical verification we conclude that the charge correlator
expansion can be used to analyze the scaling behavior of Rényi negativity for local free
Hamiltonian with conserved charge, while for logarithmic negativity it further requires
translational invariance. In
Shapourian et al. 2017a
it is numerically verified that
the scaling behavior of negativity in Kitaev chain
and SSH model (both with translational invariant Hamiltonian)
is consistent with the CFT result from replica trick.

IV
Applications

IV.1
(1+1)-d free fermion

(1+1)-d free-fermion systems with translational symmetry at zero temperature, with the Euler characteristic of the Fermi sea being
\chi_{F}
(equals the number of segments of the Fermi sea), is also a CFT with central charge

c=\chi_{F}

Tam et al. 2022
. For (1+1)-d
free-fermion systems,
the
M=2
term is related to
\chi_{F}

and (
4
) becomes the universal second-order density correlator

\int\frac{dq^{\prime}}{2\pi}\braket{\rho_{q}\rho_{q^{\prime}}}_{c}=\chi_{F}|q|,

(72)

for small
q
. where
\rho_{q}
is the charge density in momentum space

\rho_{q}=\int{dx}\rho(x)e^{-iqx}=\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}c_{k}^{\dagger}c_{k+q}.

(73)

After Fourier transformation we get density correlator
\braket{\rho(x_{1})\rho(x_{2})}_{c}
in real space.
Here we have to apply the invariance of correlator under translation in systems with translational symmetry by

\displaystyle\braket{\rho(x_{1})\rho(x_{2})}_{c}

\displaystyle=\braket{\rho(x_{1}-x_{2})\rho(0)}_{c}

(74)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{dq}{2\pi}\frac{dq^{\prime}}{2\pi}\braket{\rho_{q}\rho_{q^{\prime}}}_{c}e^{iq(x_{1}-x_{2})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\chi_{F}}{2\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}}.

(
74
) applies for large
x_{1}-x_{2}\gg\varepsilon
,
where the short-distance cutoff
\varepsilon
is about
the inverse of the curvature of Fermi surface
Tam et al. 2022
(in
1d the length of the shortest segment or interval of Fermi sea).

To get charge correlator of regions we integrate (
74
) in real space.
Now suppose we take the region
A:\ -l_{1}\leq x\leq 0
,

B:\ d\leq x\leq d+l_{2}
and
C
being the rest parts.
For
\braket{Q_{A}^{2}}_{c}
we encounter the case for short distance
with small
x_{1}-x_{2}
. To solve this problem we consider
a simplified model, where for
|x_{1}-x_{2}|>\varepsilon

\braket{\rho(x_{1})\rho(x_{2})}_{c}=-\frac{\chi_{F}}{2\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}},

(75)

and for
|x_{1}-x_{2}|\leq\varepsilon
it is a constant

\braket{\rho(x_{1})\rho(x_{2})}_{c}=-\frac{\chi_{F}}{2\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

(76)

We consider the case that the regions
A,B
are much larger
than the short-distance cutoff,

i.e.

l_{1},l_{2}\gg\varepsilon
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\braket{Q_{A}^{2}}_{c}

\displaystyle=\int_{-l_{1}}^{0}dx_{1}\int_{-l_{1}}^{0}dx_{2}\braket{\rho(x_{1})\rho(x_{2})}_{c}

(77)

\displaystyle=\frac{\chi_{F}}{2\pi^{2}}\left[2\log\frac{l_{1}}{\varepsilon}+O\left(\frac{l_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right].

Similarly for

\braket{Q_{B}^{2}}_{c}
we have

\displaystyle\braket{Q_{B}^{2}}_{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{\chi_{F}}{2\pi^{2}}\left[2\log\frac{l_{2}}{\varepsilon}+O\left(\frac{l_{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right].

(78)

For
\braket{Q_{A}Q_{B}}_{c}
, at
d\geq\varepsilon
the integration get

\displaystyle\braket{Q_{A}Q_{B}}_{c}

\displaystyle=\int_{-l_{1}}^{0}dx_{1}\int_{d}^{d+l_{2}}dx_{2}\braket{\rho(x_{1})\rho(x_{2})}_{c}

(79)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\chi_{F}}{2\pi^{2}}\log\frac{(l_{1}+d)(l_{2}+d)}{d(l_{1}+l_{2}+d)}

and for
d<\varepsilon\ll l_{1},l_{2}
,

\displaystyle\braket{Q_{A}Q_{B}}_{c}

\displaystyle=\int_{-l_{1}}^{0}dx_{1}\int_{d}^{d+l_{2}}dx_{2}\braket{\rho(x_{1})\rho(x_{2})}_{c}

(80)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\chi_{F}}{2\pi^{2}}\left[\log\frac{(l_{1}+d)(l_{2}+d)}{\varepsilon(l_{1}+l_{2}+d)}+O\left(\frac{d}{\varepsilon}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\chi_{F}}{2\pi^{2}}\log\frac{l_{1}l_{2}}{\varepsilon(l_{1}+l_{2})}.

In the last step we ignore the small
O(d/\varepsilon)

terms.
With the above results for charge correlators,
for
d\gg\varepsilon

(
54
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n_{e}}^{(2)}=

\displaystyle\chi_{F}\left[-\frac{n_{e}^{2}-1}{6n_{e}}\left(\log\frac{l_{1}l_{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+O\left(\frac{l}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)\right.

(81)

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{n_{e}^{2}+2}{12n_{e}}\log\frac{(l_{1}+d)(l_{2}+d)}{d(l_{1}+l_{2}+d)}\right].

And for
d\rightarrow 0
it becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n_{e}}^{(2)}=

\displaystyle\chi_{F}\left[-\frac{n_{e}^{2}-1}{6n_{e}}\left(\log\frac{l_{1}l_{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+O\left(\frac{l}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)\right.

(82)

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{n_{e}^{2}+2}{12n_{e}}\log\frac{l_{1}l_{2}}{\varepsilon(l_{1}+l_{2})}\right].

First we consider the case
d\rightarrow 0
,

i.e.
regions
A
and
B
are adjacent,
(
82
) can also be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n_{e}}^{(2)}=\chi_{F}

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{n_{e}^{2}-4}{12n_{e}}\log\frac{l_{1}l_{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{n_{e}^{2}+2}{12n_{e}}\log\frac{l_{1}+l_{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right.

(83)

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{n_{e}^{2}-1}{6n_{e}}O\left(\frac{l}{\varepsilon}\right)\right],

which is identical to (85) in
Shapourian et al. 2017a

at even
n_{e}
except for the short distance
cutoff (note that the term for short distance
cutoff

O\left(l/{\varepsilon}\right)

is generally large and cannot be neglected). Taking the limit
n_{e}\rightarrow 1

we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n_{e}\rightarrow 1}^{(2)}=\frac{\chi_{F}}{4}\log\frac{l_{1}l_{2}}{\varepsilon(l_{1}+l_{2})}.

(84)

Then the first term related to
divergent short-distance cutoff
is eliminated, which is consistent with what we expect
for negativity.
Numerical checks in Sec.
III
have confirmed that for system with translational symmetry,
the expansion of
\mathcal{E}_{n_{e}\rightarrow 1}
converges to
\mathcal{E}
,
so (
84
) captures the scaling property of
\mathcal{E}
.

We can also consider the cases that region
A
and
B
are far apart,

i.e.

d\gg l_{1},l_{2}
. Then (
81
) can be
expanded to the second order of
l_{i}/d
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n_{e}}^{(2)}=\chi_{F}

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{n_{e}^{2}-1}{6n_{e}}\left(\log\frac{l_{1}l_{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+O\left(\frac{l}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)\right.

(85)

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{n_{e}^{2}+2}{12n_{e}}\frac{l_{1}l_{2}}{d^{2}}\right].

Taking the limit
n_{e}\rightarrow 1
the first term vanishes
and we also get rid of the short-distance cutoff

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n_{e}\rightarrow 1}^{(2)}=\frac{\chi_{F}}{4}\frac{l_{1}l_{2}}{d^{2}}.

(86)

Therefore it is proportional to the length of the two regions
and decays with the distance by a power law.

IV.2
free fermion in higher dimension

Similar to the 1+1-dimensional case, we consider free-fermion
system with conserved charge in
(D+1)
-dimension.
Here we consider region
A
and
B
to be two balls with
radius
R_{1},R_{2}
and the distance between their centers
d\gg R_{1},R_{2}
.
In higher dimensions the
M
-th order connected
charge correlator satisfies

\int\frac{d\bm{q}_{M}}{2\pi}\braket{\rho_{\bm{q}_{1}}\cdots\rho_{\bm{q}_{M}}}_{c}\sim k_{F}^{D+1-M}q^{M-1}

(87)

for small
\bm{q}

Tam et al. 2022
,
where
q
is the typical length of
\bm{q}_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots M-1
.
In (
54
) the second order correlator
is

\int\frac{d\bm{q}^{\prime}}{2\pi}\braket{\rho_{\bm{q}}\rho_{\bm{q}^{\prime}}}_{c}\sim k_{F}^{D-1}|\bm{q}|.

(88)

by Fourier transformation it gives the real space correlator

\braket{\rho(\bm{r}_{1})\rho(\bm{r}_{2})}_{c}\sim\frac{k_{F}^{D-1}}{|\bm{r}_{1}-\bm{r}_{2}|^{D+1}},

(89)

for
|\bm{r}_{1}-\bm{r}_{2}|\gg\varepsilon
.
Taking the similar model for short distance cutoff
as the 1+1-d case, the integral in real space gives

\displaystyle\braket{Q_{A}^{2}}_{c}

\displaystyle\sim(k_{F}R_{1})^{D-1}\left(1+O\left(\frac{R_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right),

(90)

\displaystyle\braket{Q_{B}^{2}}_{c}

\displaystyle\sim(k_{F}R_{2})^{D-1}\left(1+O\left(\frac{R_{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right),

(91)

and

\displaystyle\braket{Q_{A}Q_{B}}_{c}

\displaystyle\sim k_{F}^{D-1}\frac{R_{1}^{D}R_{2}^{D}}{d^{D+1}}.

(92)

Therefore (
54
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n_{e}}^{(2)}\sim

\displaystyle k_{F}^{D-1}\left[c_{1}\frac{n_{e}^{2}-1}{6n_{e}}\left(R_{1}^{D-1}+R_{2}^{D-1}+O\left(\frac{R}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)\right.

(93)

\displaystyle\left.+c_{2}\frac{n_{e}^{2}+2}{6n_{e}}\frac{R_{1}^{D}R_{2}^{D}}{d^{D+1}}\right],

where
c_{1},c_{2}
are some finite coefficient.
We see that when regions
A
and
B
are far apart, the terms from

\braket{Q_{A}^{2}}_{c}
and
\braket{Q_{B}^{2}}_{c}

contain a constant term with short-distance cutoff that do not depend on the distance between

A
and
B
, and is much larger than the terms from
\braket{Q_{A}Q_{B}}_{c}
. This incidates that Rényi
negativity is not a good entanglement measure.
However, at replica limit
n_{e}\rightarrow 1
, the first term with short
distance cutoff
is also eliminated, and we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n_{e}\rightarrow 1}^{(2)}\sim

\displaystyle k_{F}^{D-1}\frac{R_{1}^{D}R_{2}^{D}}{d^{D+1}}.

(94)

This is consistent with our expectation for logarithmic negativity as an
entanglement measure.
The negativity is proportional to the area of two regions
and decays also by a power law with the distance between them.

V
Conclusion and discussion

In this paper, we derive the connected charge correlator expansion
for Rényi and logarithmic negativity in free-fermion systems
with conserved
U(1)
charge. We numerically check the validity of
the expansion, and find that the convergence at replica limit requires
translational symmetry. Using this expansion, we analyze the
scaling behavior of negativity in extensive free-fermion systems.
From the expansion, it is direct to note that there are
terms that diverge for short distance cutoff in the expansion for
Rényi negativity, but they vanish at the limit
n_{e}\rightarrow 1
.
This is consistent with our expectation for logarithmic negativity rather than
Rényi negativity as an
entanglement measure.

Our research in charge correlator expansion is generally
limited to free-fermion systems. One can also consider
canonical bosonic systems where the charge operator is
similarly defined, but will encounter difficulty in the
range of
p
(
34
). For bosonic systems,
the range of
p
is given
by
p=0,1,\cdots,n-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)
. There
seems to be no natural choice of the range to make the
summation
\sum_{p}p^{M}
smallest for even
M
and
0 for odd
M
, as required for the convergence in fermionic systems. As a
consequence, we cannot obtain the correct coefficient in
the expansion after the terms with higher
M
are truncated.
Spin systems may also be considered, but the charge operator
is hard to define, and free-spin systems (without spin-spin
interaction) may not be intriguing for research.

The charge expansion may also be applied to other computable
entanglement measures
besides PT negativity,
e.g.
the computable cross norm or
realignment (CCNR) negativity

Rudolph 2003
;
Rudolph 2005
;
Chen and Wu 2003
.
Recently, it has been shown that Rényi and logarithmic CCNR negativity
of two intervals that are not adjacent
has a universal expression
Yin and Liu 2023
in (1+1)-d CFT.
The twist operators for CCNR negativity cannot be simultaneously
diagonalized, but they can be simultaneously block
diagonalized in the form of
2\times 2
matrices.
Therefore CCNR negativity can be in free systems can be related to
the expectation value of
2\times 2

operators instead of
U(1)
charge operators.
We suppose that the charge expansion for these computable entanglement
measures can provide a deeper understanding of the
entanglement properties of quantum systems.

Formula (
4
) shows that the universal
(D+1)
-th
order charge correlator in
D
-dimensional free-fermion systems
is proportional to the Euler characteristic of the Fermi sea.
The leading term in the expansion for Rényi and logarithmic negativity
(
54
) and (
56
) contains second-order charge
correlators, and therefore in (1+1)-d system, it extracts the
Euler characteristic, as shown in Sec.
IV.1
. However, for
higher dimensions, the divergent terms always
exist in the leading term. It is an interesting question whether there are
ways to subtract the divergent terms and extract topological terms in the expansion for negativity,
like the case for topological entanglement entropy
Tam et al. 2022
.

Appendix A
Cumulant
expansion for Rényi and von Neumann entropy

From (
33
)

\displaystyle\log\braket{T_{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{M=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{p}p^{M}\right)\frac{1}{M!}\left(\frac{2\pi i}{n}\right)^{M}\braket{Q_{A}^{M}}_{c},

(95)

the summation
of
p
is

C_{n,M}=\sum_{p=-\frac{n-1}{2}}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}p^{M}

(96)

It can be written in the form
of generalized harmonic number
Choi and Srivastava 2011

H_{n}^{(s)}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(j+z)^{s}}\ \ \ \ (n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},s\in\mathbb{C},z\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\mathbb{Z}^{-}).

(97)

First consider the case of even
n
, the result is

\displaystyle\sum_{p=-\frac{n-1}{2}}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}p^{M}

\displaystyle=\left[1+(-1)^{M}\right]\times\left[\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{M}+\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{M}+\cdots+\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)^{M}\right]

(98)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}2H_{n/2}^{(-M)}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\ &\mathrm{even}\ M\\
0\ &\mathrm{odd}\ M.\end{cases}

For the range of arguments we consider here, the
generalized harmonic number is related to the
generalized Riemann zeta function (this is generally
not correct).

\zeta(n,a)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(k+a)^{n}}

(99)

by

H_{n}^{(s)}(z)=\zeta(s,z+1)-\zeta(s,z+n+1)

(100)

and
\zeta(n)=\zeta(n,1)
. Thus

\displaystyle\log\braket{T_{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M=2\\
\mathrm{even}\ M\end{subarray}}^{\infty}2H_{n/2}^{(-M)}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{1}{M!}\left(\frac{2\pi i}{n}\right)^{M}\braket{Q_{A}^{M}}_{c}

(101)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M=2\\
\mathrm{even}\ M\end{subarray}}^{\infty}2\left[\zeta\left(-M,\frac{1}{2}\right)-\zeta\left(-M,\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\right]\frac{1}{M!}\left(\frac{2\pi i}{n}\right)^{M}\braket{Q_{A}^{M}}_{c}.

For even
M\geq 0
,
\zeta\left(-M,1/2\right)=0
,
we get

\displaystyle\log\braket{T_{n}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M=2\\
\mathrm{even}\ M\end{subarray}}^{\infty}2\zeta\left(-M,\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\frac{1}{M!}\left(\frac{2\pi i}{n}\right)^{M}\braket{Q_{A}^{M}}_{c}.

(102)

For odd
n
, it is easy to check that we get

\displaystyle\log\braket{T_{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M=2\\
\mathrm{even}\ M\end{subarray}}^{\infty}2H_{(n-1)/2}^{(-M)}\left(0\right)\frac{1}{M!}\left(\frac{2\pi i}{n}\right)^{M}\braket{Q_{A}^{M}}_{c}

(103)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M=2\\
\mathrm{even}\ M\end{subarray}}^{\infty}2\left[\zeta\left(-M,1\right)-\zeta\left(-M,\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\right]\frac{1}{M!}\left(\frac{2\pi i}{n}\right)^{M}\braket{Q_{A}^{M}}_{c}.

For even
M\geq 0
,
\zeta\left(-M,1\right)=0
,
thus we get the same result

\displaystyle\log\braket{T_{n}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M=2\\
\mathrm{even}\ M\end{subarray}}^{\infty}2\zeta\left(-M,\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\frac{1}{M!}\left(\frac{2\pi i}{n}\right)^{M}\braket{Q_{A}^{M}}_{c}.

(104)

This gives the cumulant expansion for Rényi entropy (
36
).

Since the right hand side of (
104
) is real
for all positive integer
n
, we
now extend it from
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
to
n\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

entropy by taking the

The analytic continuation can differ with
periodic functions and affect the result, but we assume

\frac{d}{dn}\zeta(k,n)=-k\zeta(k+1,n),

(105)

we get

\displaystyle S_{A}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow 1}\frac{\log\braket{T_{n}}}{1-n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M=2\\
\mathrm{even}\ M\end{subarray}}^{\infty}2\left.\frac{d}{dn}\zeta\left(-M,\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\right|_{n=1}\frac{1}{M!}({2\pi i})^{M}\braket{Q_{A}^{M}}_{c}

(106)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M=2\\
\mathrm{even}\ M\end{subarray}}^{\infty}M\left.\zeta\left(1-M,\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\right|_{n=1}\frac{1}{M!}({2\pi i})^{M}\braket{Q_{A}^{M}}_{c}.

And by using the relation

\zeta(1-M)=2(2\pi)^{-M}\cos(\pi M/2)(M-1)!\zeta(M),

\displaystyle S_{A}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M=2\\
\mathrm{even}\ M\end{subarray}}^{\infty}M\zeta\left(1-M,1\right)\frac{1}{M!}({2\pi i})^{M}\braket{Q_{A}^{M}}_{c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M=2\\
\mathrm{even}\ M\end{subarray}}^{\infty}2\zeta(M)\braket{Q_{A}^{M}}_{c}.
