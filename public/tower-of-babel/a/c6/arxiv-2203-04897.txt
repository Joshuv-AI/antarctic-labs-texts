CTRW approximations for fractional equations with variable order
V. N. Kolokoltsov

Abstract
The standard diffusion processes are known to be obtained as the limits of appropriate random walks. These prelimiting random walks can be quite different however. The diffusion coefficient can be made responsible for the size of jumps or for the intensity of jumps. The ”rough” diffusion limit does not feel the difference. The situation changes, if we model jump-type approximations via CTRW with non-exponential waiting times. If we make the diffusion coefficient responsible for the size of jumps and take waiting times from the domain of attraction of an α \alpha -stable law with a constant intensity α \alpha , then the standard scaling would lead in the limit of small jumps and large intensities to the most standard fractional diffusion equation. However, if we choose the CTRW approximations with fixed jump sizes and use the diffusion coefficient to distinguish intensities at different points, then we obtain in the limit the equations with variable position-dependent fractional derivatives. In this paper we build rigorously these approximations and prove their convergence to the corresponding fractional equations for the cases of multidimensional diffusions and more general Feller processes.



Key words:
variable order fractional equations,
continuous time random walks (CTRW), subordinated Markov processes.

1
Introduction

The standard diffusion equation

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}a(x)\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(t,x),

(1)

with a positive continuous function
a(x)
,
and the corresponding diffusion process are known to be obtained as the limit of appropriate
random walks. These prelimiting random walks can be quite different however. For example,
approximating random walks in continuous time can be chosen as jump type processes with the generators

L_{h}f(x)=\frac{1}{2h^{2}}[f(x+h\sqrt{a(x)})+f(x-h\sqrt{a(x)})-2f(x)],

or as jump type processes with the generators

\tilde{L}_{h}f(x)=\frac{a(x)}{2h^{2}}[f(x+h)+f(x-h)-2f(x)],

since both
L_{h}
and
\tilde{L}_{h}
tend to
(1/2)a(x)(d^{2}/dx^{2})
, as
h\to 0
.
In the first approximation, the diffusion coefficient
a(x)
is responsible for the size of jumps (with constant intensity),
and in the second approximation, it is responsible for the intensity of jumps (with constant sizes).
The ”rough” diffusion limit does not feel the difference.
The situation changes, if we model jump-type approximations via CTRW with non-exponential waiting times.
If we make the diffusion coefficient responsible for the size of jumps and take
waiting times from the domain of attraction of an
\alpha
-stable law with a constant intensity
\alpha
,
then the standard scaling would lead (in the limit
of small jumps and large intensities) to the most standard fractional diffusion equation

D^{\alpha}_{0+*}u(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}a(x)\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(t,x),

(2)

where
D^{\alpha}_{0+*}
is the so-called Caputo-Dzerbashyan fractional derivative.

However, if we will use the CTRW approximations with fixed jump sizes and use
a(x)

to distinguish intensities at different points, then (as will be shown) we get in
the limit the equation with a variable position-dependent fractional derivative:

D^{\alpha a(x)}_{0+*}u(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(t,x).

(3)

Of course, for any decomposition
a(x)=b(x)c(x)
with positive
b(x),c(x)
, one can use

b(x)
as a function controlling the intensity of jumps and
c(x)
as a function controlling
the spread of the jumps. In this scenario we get in the limit the equation

D^{\alpha b(x)}_{0+*}u(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}c(x)\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(t,x).

(4)

From this point of view, all equations (
4
) are equally
legitimate fractional extensions of the diffusion equation (
1
).

Similarly (though technically more cumbersome), for a time dependent diffusion equation

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}a(t,x)\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(t,x),

(5)

factorising
a(t,x)=b(t,x)c(t,x)
we get by the CTRW approximation the fractional equation with variable order,
which is time- and position-dependent:

D^{\alpha b(t,x)}_{0+*}u(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}c(t,x)\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(t,x).

(6)

In this paper we aim to develop these CTRW approximations in a general multidimensional case.
Namely, let us look at the CTRW approximations and possible corresponding fractional limits
for the diffusion processes generated by the operator

L_{dif}f(x)=\frac{1}{2}{\rm{tr}}\left(G(x)\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x^{2}}(x)\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}G_{ij}(x)\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(x),\quad x\in\mathbf{R}^{d},

(7)

a positive

and stable-like processes generated by the operator

2
CTRW approximation

We are looking for the CTRW approximations to the equations (
9
).

For the operator
L_{st}^{\beta}
, we choose standard random walk approximations

L_{st,\tau}^{\beta}f(x)=\frac{1}{\tau}\int(f(x+\tau^{1/\beta}z)-f(x))p(x,dz),

(11)

with
p(x,dz)
a symmetric probability kernel in
\mathbf{R}^{d}

(i.e.
\int f(z)p(x,dz)=\int f(-z)p(x,dz)
), so that

\sup_{x}|(L_{st}^{\beta}-L_{st,\tau}^{\beta})f(x)|\to 0,\quad\text{as}\quad\tau\to 0,

(12)

uniformly for
f
from any bounded subset of
C^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.

The operator
L_{st,\tau}^{\beta}
generates a jump-type Markov process with the intensity
\tau^{-1}

and the distribution of jumps given by
p(x,dz)
.

Remark 1
.

Of course, such approximations are not unique. The existence is however more or less obvious. For the diffusion operator
(
7
) the measures
p(x,dz)
can be (and usually are) taken as discrete, for instance,
of the type

\sum_{i}(\delta_{a_{i}e_{i}}+\delta_{-a_{i}e_{i}})+\sum_{i>j}(\delta_{b_{ij}(e_{i}+e_{j})}+\delta_{-b_{ij}(e_{i}+e_{j})}),

with some numbers
a_{i},b_{ij}
, where
\{e_{i}\}
is the standard basis in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
. For the stable generator
one can take as
p(x,dz)
a probability measure with an appropriate power tail,
see e.g.
[
13
]
or Section 8.3 from
[
8
]
.

For a generator
L
of an arbitrary Feller process with a core
C^{k}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, where
k
equals one or two,
on can use an arbitrary approximation
L_{\tau}
of the type

L_{\tau}f(x)=\frac{1}{\tau}\int(f(x+z)-f(x))p_{\tau}(x,dz),

(13)

with

a family of probability kernels in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
such that

\sup_{x}|(L-L_{\tau})f(x)|\to 0,\quad\text{as}\quad\tau\to 0,

(14)

uniformly for
f
from any bounded subset of
C^{k}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.

We shall now construct a process with waiting times having power tails. For exponential waiting times,
the intensity
a(s,x)
governs the tail distribution
\mathbf{P}(T>t)=e^{-ta(s,x)\tau}
with a scaling parameter
\tau
.
Hence, for the power tail the analogous dependence will be the power law

\mathbf{P}(T>t)\sim\frac{1}{a(s,x)\alpha}t^{-a(s,x)\alpha},\quad t\to\infty.

(15)

To simplify presentation we shall make (
15
) more precise. Namely, we assume
that these distributions have continuous densities
Q_{s,x}(r)
such that

Q_{s,x}(r)=r^{-1-a(s,x)\alpha}\,\,\text{for}\,\,r\geq B,\,\,\text{and}\,\,Q_{s,x}(r)\leq 1\,\,\text{for all}\,\,r,

(16)

with some
B>0
uniformly for all
s,x
.

Remark 2
.

(i) The coefficients at the power of
t
in (
15
) are chosen for convenience.
If not used here, then it would appear in (
16
) and
in the limiting equation. (ii) Simplification arising from (
16

is not essential and can be dispensed with.

As usual for CTRW modelling, we start by building an auxiliary scaled enhanced Markov chain
with spatial jumps distributed according to
p(x,dz)
and with an additional
coordinate
r
specifying the total waiting times. In the usual CTRW scaling
(see e.g.
[
13
]
and
[
8

\tau

\tau^{1/(\alpha a(s,x))}

(X^{\tau}_{x,s},S^{\tau}_{x,s})(k\tau)

,

(X^{\tau}_{x,s},S^{\tau}_{x,s})(0)=(x,s)

and

distributed according to

We obtain the Markov chain with transition operators in time

U^{\tau}F(x,s)=\int_{\mathbf{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}F(x+\tau^{1/\beta}y,s+\tau^{1/(\alpha a(s,x))}r)Q_{s,x}(r)\,dr\,p(x,dy).

(17)

When working with an arbitrary generator

(rather than more concrete

U^{\tau}F(x,s)=\int_{\mathbf{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}F(x+y,s+\tau^{1/(\alpha a(s,x))}r)Q_{s,x}(r)\,dr\,p_{\tau}(x,dy).

We are interested in the value of the first coordinate

We define the
scaled CTRW approximation for the process with the intensity of jumps governed by

We are going to identify the weak limit of the process

and to show that the limiting processes are governed by the equations extending

As an auxiliary step, we shall identify the weak limit of the Markov chain

that is, the weak limit of the chains with transitions

It is known (see e.g. Theorem 19.28 of

that if such chain converges to a Feller process, then the generator of this limiting process

\Lambda F=\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{1}{\tau}(U^{\tau}F-F).

(20)

4
A general representation for a subordination

In this section we derive a representation for Markov processes subordinated by its monotone coordinate.
It supplies the proof for the part (ii) of Theorem
3.1

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
Y_{s}
be an adapted process on a stochastic basis
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}_{t},P)
and
\Sigma
a stopping time.
Let the pairs
(Y_{s},\Sigma)
have joint densities
g_{s}(y,\sigma)
for
s>0
such that, for
s
from any bounded interval
separated from zero,
g_{s}(y,\sigma)
is bounded and right continuous in
s
.
Then, for any
K>0
and a continuous bounded function
F(y)
,

\mathbf{E}[F(Y_{\Sigma})\mathbf{1}(\Sigma\in[1/K,K])]=\int dy\int_{1/K}^{K}d\sigma\,g_{\sigma}(y,\sigma)F(y).

(31)

Proof.

Let us choose the discrete approximations

\Sigma_{\tau}=\tau[\Sigma/\tau+1]
(square brackets denoting the integer part), so that
\Sigma_{\tau}=k\tau

when
(k-1)\tau\leq\Sigma<k\tau
. Thus
\Sigma_{\tau}
are right continuous stopping times such that

\Sigma_{\tau}\geq\Sigma
for all
\tau
, they depend monotonically on
\tau
and
\Sigma_{\tau}\to\Sigma
, as
\tau\to 0
.
Then we can write

\mathbf{E}[F(Y_{\Sigma})\mathbf{1}(\Sigma\in[1/K,K])]=\lim_{\tau\to 0}\mathbf{E}[F(Y_{\Sigma_{\tau}})\mathbf{1}(\Sigma\in[1/K,K])]

=\lim_{\tau\to 0}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}[F(Y_{\Sigma_{\tau}})\mathbf{1}(\Sigma\in[1/K,K])\mathbf{1}(\Sigma_{\tau}=k\tau)]

=\lim_{\tau\to 0}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}\left[F(Y_{k\tau})\mathbf{1}(\Sigma\in[1/K,K])\mathbf{1}(\Sigma\in[(k-1)\tau,k\tau))\right]

=\lim_{\tau\to 0}\int dy\int_{1/K}^{K}d\sigma\,g_{\sigma_{\tau}}(y,\sigma)F(y).

And therefore

\mathbf{E}[F(Y_{\Sigma})\mathbf{1}(\Sigma\in[1/K,K])]=\int dy\int_{1/K}^{K}d\sigma\,g_{\sigma}(y,\sigma)F(y)

+\lim_{\tau\to 0}\int dy\int_{1/K}^{K}d\sigma\,(g_{\sigma_{\tau}}-g_{\sigma})(y,\sigma)F(y).

Since the last term tends to zero (by the right continuity of
g
and dominated convergence),
we obtain (
31
).

Proposition 5.1

Let

for any continuous

and some constant

\left|h^{-\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(hy)p(y)dy-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{f(y)dy}{y^{1+\alpha}}\right|\leq C_{B}Lh^{1-\alpha},

(40)

with

Remark 8

If, instead of assuming Lipschitz at zero, one assumes Hölder continuity at zero,
that is

for

and some

(

\left(\frac{B^{\beta-\alpha}}{\beta-\alpha}+\int_{0}^{B}y^{\beta}p(y)dy\right)Lh^{\beta-\alpha}.

Proof.

Let

with

. Then

h^{-\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(hy)p(y)dy=h^{-\alpha}\int_{C/h}^{\infty}p(y)dy

Hence by linearity and the density argument it follows that

for any bounded measurable
f
having support on
[Bh,\infty)
.
Next let

have support on

\left|h^{-\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(hy)p(dy)\right|=\left|h^{-\alpha}\int_{0}^{B}f(hy)p(y)dy\right|\leq h^{1-\alpha}L\int_{0}^{B}yp(y)dy,

and

\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{f(y)dy}{y^{1+\alpha}}\right|=\left|\int_{0}^{Bh}\frac{f(y)dy}{y^{1+\alpha}}\right|\leq L\int_{0}^{Bh}\frac{dy}{y^{\alpha}}=\frac{B^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}Lh^{1-\alpha},
