Finite groups with large Chermak-Delgado lattices
Georgiana Fasolă, Marius Tărnăuceanu

Abstract
Given a finite group G G , we denote by L ⁡ ( G ) L(G) the subgroup lattice of G G and by 𝒞𝒟 ⁡ ( G ) {\cal CD}(G) the Chermak-Delgado lattice of G G . In this note, we determine the finite groups G G such that | 𝒞𝒟 ⁡ ( G ) | = | L ⁡ ( G ) | − k |{\cal CD}(G)|=|L(G)|-k , k = 1 , 2 k=1,2 .



MSC2020 :
Primary 20D30; Secondary 20D60, 20D99.

Key words :
Chermak-Delgado measure, Chermak-Delgado lattice, subgroup lattice, generalized quaternion
2
-group.

1
Introduction

Let
G
be a finite group and
L(G)
be the subgroup lattice of
G
. The
Chermak-Delgado measure
of a subgroup
H
of
G
is defined by

m_{G}(H)=|H||C_{G}(H)|.

Let

m^{*}(G)={\rm max}\{m_{G}(H)\mid H\leq G\}\mbox{ and }{\cal CD}(G)=\{H\leq G\mid m_{G}(H)=m^{*}(G)\}.

Then the set
{\cal CD}(G)
forms a modular, self-dual sublattice of
L(G)
, which is called the
Chermak-Delgado lattice
of
G
. It was first introduced by Chermak and Delgado
[
4
]
, and revisited by Isaacs
[
5
]
. In the last years there has been a growing interest in understanding this lattice (see e.g.
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
). We recall several important properties of the Chermak-Delgado measure:

\bullet

if
H\leq G
then
m_{G}(H)\leq m_{G}(C_{G}(H))
, and if the measures are equal then
C_{G}(C_{G}(H))=H
;

\bullet

if
H\in{\cal CD}(G)
then
C_{G}(H)\in{\cal CD}(G)
and
C_{G}(C_{G}(H))=H
;

\bullet

the maximal member
M
of
{\cal CD}(G)
is characteristic and satisfies
{\cal CD}(M)={\cal CD}(G)
;

\bullet

the minimal member
M(G)
of
{\cal CD}(G)
(called the
Chermak-Delgado subgroup
of
G
) is characteristic, abelian and contains
Z(G)
.

In
[
12
]
, the Chermak-Delgado measure of
G
has been seen as a function

m_{G}:L(G)\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}^{*},\,H\mapsto m_{G}(H),\,\forall\,H\in L(G).

If
G
is non-trivial, then
m_{G}
has at least two distinct values, or equivalently
{\cal CD}(G)\neq L(G)
(see Corollary 3 of
[
11
]
). This leads to the following natural question:

How large can the lattice
{\cal CD}(G)
be
?

Note that the dual problem of finding finite groups with small Chermak-Delgado lattices has been studied in
[
6
,
7
]
.

Our main result is stated as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group. Then

a)

|{\cal CD}(G)|=|L(G)|-1
if and only if
G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p}
or
G\cong Q_{8}
;

b)

|{\cal CD}(G)|=|L(G)|-2
if and only if
G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}
.

For the proof of the above theorem, we need the following well-known result (see e.g. (4.4) of
[
10
]
, II).

Theorem A
.

A finite
p
-group has a unique subgroup of order
p
if and only if it is either cyclic or a generalized quaternion
2
-group.

We recall that a
generalized quaternion
2
-group
is a group of order
2^{n}
for some positive integer
n\geq 3
, defined by

Q_{2^{n}}=\langle a,b\mid a^{2^{n-2}}=b^{2},a^{2^{n-1}}=1,b^{-1}ab=a^{-1}\rangle.

We also need the following theorem taken from
[
12
]
.

Theorem B
.

Let
G
be a finite group. For each prime
p
dividing the order of
G
and
P\in{\rm Syl}_{p}(G)
, let
|Z(P)|=p^{n_{p}}
. Then

|{\rm Im}(m_{G})|\geq 1+\sum_{p}n_{p}\,.

(1)

Finally, we indicate a natural open problem concerning the above study.

Open problem.
Determine the finite groups
G
such that
|{\cal CD}(G)|=|L(G)|-k
, where
k\geq 3
.

Most of our notation is standard and will usually not be repeated here. Elementary notions and results on groups can be found in
[
5
]
.
For subgroup lattice concepts we refer the reader to
[
9
]
.

2
Proof of the main result

First of all, we solve the problem for generalized quaternion
2
-groups.

Lemma 2.1
.

Under the above notation, we have:

a)

|{\cal CD}(Q_{2^{n}})|=|L(Q_{2^{n}})|-1
if and only if
n=3
, i.e.
G\cong Q_{8}
;

b)

|{\cal CD}(Q_{2^{n}})|\neq|L(Q_{2^{n}})|-2
for all
n\geq 3
.

Proof.

We easily obtain

m^{*}(Q_{2^{n}})=2^{2n-2},\,\forall\,n\geq 3

and

{\cal CD}(Q_{2^{n}})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\{Q_{8},\langle a\rangle,\langle b\rangle,\langle ab\rangle,\langle a^{2}\rangle\},&n=3\\
\{\langle a\rangle\},&n\geq 4.\end{array}\right.

These lead immediately to the desired conclusions.
∎

We are now able to prove our main result.

Proof of Theorem 1.1.

a)
Since
|{\cal CD}(G)|=|L(G)|-1
, we have
{\cal CD}(G)=L(G)\setminus\{H_{0}\}
, where
H_{0}\leq G
. We infer that
|{\rm Im}(m_{G})|=2
and so
G
is a
p
-group with
|Z(G)|=p
by Theorem B. Then
m_{G}(1)<m_{G}(Z(G))
, implying that

H_{0}=1\mbox{ and }m^{*}(G)=m_{G}(Z(G))=p^{n+1},

where
|G|=p^{n}
.

Assume that there exists
H\leq G
with
|H|=p
and
H\neq Z(G)
. Then
H\notin{\cal CD}(G)
, which shows that
H=1
, a contradiction. Thus
G
has a unique subgroup of order
p
and Theorem A leads to

G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p^{n}}\mbox{ or }G\cong Q_{2^{n}}\mbox{ for some }n\geq 3.

(2)

In the first case we easily get
n=1
, i.e.
G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, while in the second one we get
G\cong Q_{8}
by item a) in Lemma 2.1.

b)
The condition
|{\cal CD}(G)|=|L(G)|-2
means
{\cal CD}(G)=L(G)\setminus\{H_{1},H_{2}\}
, where
H_{1},H_{2}\leq G
. Then
|{\rm Im}(m_{G})|\leq 3
. Recall that we cannot have
|{\rm Im}(m_{G})|=1
.

If
|{\rm Im}(m_{G})|=2
, then

m_{G}(H_{1})=m_{G}(H_{2})\neq m^{*}(G)

and again
G
is a
p
-group with
|Z(G)|=p
. It is clear that one of the two subgroups
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
must be trivial, say
H_{1}=1
. Then
G
has at most two subgroups of order
p
, namely
Z(G)
and possibly
H_{2}
. This implies that it has exactly one subgroup of order
p
because the number of subgroups of order
p
in a finite
p
-group is congruent to
1

({\rm mod}\,p)
. Consequently, one obtains again (2). For
G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p^{n}}
we easily get
n=2
, i.e.
G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}
, while for
G\cong Q_{2^{n}}
we get no solution by item b) in Lemma 2.1.

If
|{\rm Im}(m_{G})|=3
, then
m_{G}(H_{1})
,
m_{G}(H_{2})
and
m^{*}(G)
are distinct. Also, the inequality (1) becomes

3\geq 1+\sum_{p}n_{p}\,.

Since
n_{p}\geq 1
for all
p
, we have the following two possibilities:

Case 1.

|G|=p^{n}
and
|Z(G)|\in\{p,p^{2}\}

Obviously, if
G
is abelian, we get
G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}
. Assume that
G
is not abelian. Since
m_{G}(1)<m_{G}(Z(G))=m_{G}(G)
, we infer that one of the two subgroups
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
is trivial, and that

m^{*}(G)=m_{G}(Z(G))=m_{G}(G).

If
|Z(G)|=p
, then
G
has a unique subgroup of order
p
and so it is a generalized quaternion
2
-group, contradicting item b) in Lemma 2.1. The same thing can be also said when
|Z(G)|=p^{2}
because all subgroups of order
p
of
G
are outside of
{\cal CD}(G)
.

Case 2.

|G|=p^{n}q^{m}
and the Sylow
p
-subgroups and
q
-subgroups of
G
have centers of orders
p
and
q
, respectively

Let
P
be a Sylow
p
-subgroup and
Q
be a Sylow
q
-subgroup of
G
. Since
P\subseteq C_{G}(Z(P))
, we have

m_{G}(Z(P))=p\,|C_{G}(Z(P))|=p^{n+1}q^{x}\mbox{ for some }0\leq x\leq m,

and similarly

m_{G}(Z(Q))=p^{y}q^{m+1}\mbox{ for some }0\leq y\leq n.

Also, we have

m_{G}(1)=p^{n}q^{m}\mbox{ and }m_{G}(G)=p^{n}q^{m}|Z(G)|.

We observe that the measures
m_{G}(Z(P))
,
m_{G}(Z(Q))
and
m_{G}(1)
are distinct, and consequently they are all possible measures of the subgroups of
G
. We distinguish the following two subcases:

Subcase 2.1.

Z(G)=1

Then
m^{*}(G)=m_{G}(1)=m_{G}(G)
. Indeed, if
m^{*}(G)=m_{G}(Z(P))
, then
1
,
G
and
Z(Q)
will be outside of
{\cal CD}(G)
, a contradiction. In the same way, we cannot have
m^{*}(G)=m_{G}(Z(Q))
. Since
m_{G}(P)
is divisibly by
p^{n+1}
and
m_{G}(Q)
is divisibly by
q^{m+1}
, we infer that
m_{G}(P)=m_{G}(Z(P))
and
m_{G}(Q)=m_{G}(Z(Q))
. Thus

P,Z(P),Q,Z(Q)\notin{\cal CD}(G)

and our hypothesis implies that
P=Z(P)
and
Q=Z(Q)
, i.e.
G
is a non-abelian group of order
pq
. Assume that
p<q
. Then
{\cal CD}(G)
consists of the unique subgroup of order
q
of
G
and therefore we obtain
|L(G)|=3
, a contradiction.

Subcase 2.2.

Z(G)\neq 1

Then
m_{G}(1)<m_{G}(G)
, which shows that
m_{G}(G)
equals either
m_{G}(Z(P))
or
m_{G}(Z(Q))
. Assume that
m_{G}(G)=m_{G}(Z(P))
. Then
x=m
and
|Z(G)|=p
, implying that

Z(G)=Z(P).

(3)

Note that we cannot have
m^{*}(G)=m_{G}(Z(Q))
because in this case
1
,
Z(G)
and
G
will be outside of
{\cal CD}(G)
, a contradiction. Consequently, we have

m^{*}(G)=m_{G}(Z(P))=m_{G}(P)=m_{G}(Z(G))=m_{G}(G).

It follows that
1
,
Z(Q)
and
Q
are not contained in
{\cal CD}(G)
, which leads to
Q=Z(Q)
. In other words, we have
{\cal CD}(G)=L(G)\setminus\{1,Q\}
. Thus
{\cal CD}(G)
is the lattice interval

[G/Z(G)]=\{H\in L(G)\mid Z(G)\leq H\leq G\}

and Corollary 2 of
[
11
]
shows that
G
is nilpotent. Then
G=P\times Q
and so
Z(G)=Z(P)\times Q
, contradicting (3).

This completes the proof.∎
