Using Restart Sequences to Determine Beneficial First Passage Under Restart Preprint: APS/123-QED
Jason M. Flynn, Sergei S. Pilyugin

Abstract
First Passage (FP) processes are utilized widely to model phenomena in many areas of mathematical applications, from biology to computer science. Introducing a mechanism to restart the parent process can alter the first passage characteristics, and the properties of the resulting First Passage Under Restart (FPUR) process have been subject to much recent investigation. Here we seek to more fully characterize whether a discrete FP process can have its mean hitting time reduced through the implementation of restart by analyzing a construct we call the restart sequence, which is determined solely by the distribution of the FP process.



I
The initial framework

From
Flynn and Pilyugin 2021
, we recall that for a given First Passage (FP) process with hitting time denoted by
\mathcal{U}
, we are interested in whether introducing a particular restart mechanism can produce a First Passage under Restart (FPUR) process with a reduced mean FP time (we call this case
beneficial restart
). We define the FPUR restart process’s hitting time,
\mathcal{T}
, recursively by

\mathcal{T}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathcal{U}&\text{if }\mathcal{R}>\mathcal{U}\\
\mathcal{R}+\mathcal{T}^{*}&\text{if }\mathcal{R}\leq\mathcal{U}\end{cases},

(1)

where
\mathcal{T}^{*}
is an iid copy of
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{R}
represents our ‘restart time.’ In general,
\mathcal{R}
is a random variable that can be drawn from an arbitrary distribution. We must be aware, however, of the quantity
p_{r}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}\leq\mathcal{U})
, which denotes the probability that, on an individual foray, the FPUR process restarts before the underlying FP process finishes. When
p_{r}=1
we see that the FPUR process will, with probability 1, restart before reaching its terminal state, meaning that
\mathcal{T}
is not finite and that the FPUR process will never resolve. We call this
preemptive restart
, and it is clearly a property of the interplay between the distributions of
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{R}
, rather than a property solely of
\mathcal{R}
.

There are multiple approaches to deriving formulas for
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}]
as seen in
Flynn and Pilyugin 2021
, but perhaps the simplest can be derived directly from (
1
) as follows.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}\mid\mathcal{U}<\mathcal{R}]\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{U}<\mathcal{R})

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{R}+\mathcal{T}^{*}\mid\mathcal{R}\leq\mathcal{U}]\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}\leq\mathcal{U})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}\mid\mathcal{U}<\mathcal{R}](1-p_{r})+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{R}\mid\mathcal{R}\leq\mathcal{U}]p_{r}+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}^{*}]p_{r}

Recalling that
\mathcal{T}^{*}
is iid to
\mathcal{T}
, we can simply write

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}\wedge\mathcal{R}]}{1-p_{r}}.

(2)

This formulation lends itself to some immediate observations. First, the limit as
p_{r}\to 1
sends
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}]\to\infty
as previously mentioned (except in the case where
\mathcal{R}=0
). Second, if at least one of
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]
and
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{R}]
is finite, then
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}]<\infty
. That is, if we assume
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]=\infty
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}]<\infty
for any non-preemptive restart with
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{R}]<\infty
, and thus trivially beneficial restart. We therefore limit our attention to cases where
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]<\infty
when looking for beneficial restart.

II
Introducing the restart sequence,
\{S_{n}\}

We observe that restart is beneficial precisely when
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]<0
, or equivalently by (
2
) as

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle>\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}\wedge\mathcal{R}]}{1-p_{r}}-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-p_{r}}\left(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}\wedge\mathcal{R}]-(1-p_{r})\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{n\geq 0}\left(1-U(n)-u(n)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]\right)\left(1-R(n)\right)}{1-p_{r}}.

Since we assume restart is non-preemptive,
1-p_{r}
is clearly greater than 0, and we concern ourselves with the numerator.

For a given FP time,
\mathcal{U}
, with probability mass function (PMF),
u(n)
, and corresponding cumulative mass function (CMF),
U(n)
, we then define the “restart sequence of
\mathcal{U}
” by

S_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(1-U(k)-u(k)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]\right),

(3)

often abbreviated to simply
S_{n}
when the FP process is unambiguous. Analysis of this sequence can provide substantial insight into the behavior of the FPUR process. We begin with the observation that for any distribution,
\mathcal{U}
, with
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]<\infty
, we have that
S_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
as seen below. Since
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]=\sum_{n\geq 0}(1-U(n))
, we can write

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}S_{n}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(1-U(k)-u(k)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(1-U(k)\right)-\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]U(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\left(1-U(k)\right)-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]\lim_{n\to\infty}U(n)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]=0.

II.1
Determining whether beneficial restart is possible

Lemma 1
.

Let
\{a_{n}\}
be some sequence such that
\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}<\infty
and define
A_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}
. Then the following are equivalent.

•

A_{n}\geq 0
for all
n\geq 0
.

•

\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}b_{n}\geq 0
for any sequence,
\{b_{n}\}
, decreasing monotonically to 0.

Theorem 1
.

\mathcal{U}
permits beneficial restart iff there exists some
n\geq 0
such that
S_{n}<0
.

Proof.

We note that the quantity
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]
has the same sign as
\sum_{n\geq 0}\left(1-U(n)-u(n)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]\right)\left(1-R(n)\right)
. Clearly
b_{n}\coloneqq 1-R(n)
is monotonically decreasing to 0, so by our Lemma, restart is non-beneficial iff
\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(1-U(k)-u(k)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]\right)\geq 0
.
∎

Corollary 1
.

If there exists a beneficial restart, then there exists a beneficial sharp restart. Furthermore, sharp restart will minimize the value of
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}]
across all restart distributions.

Proof.

Suppose there there exists some restart distribution,
R(n)
, for which
\mathcal{U}
permits a beneficial restart. Then we know there exists some
n^{*}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
S_{n^{*}}<0
. Let
\tilde{R}(n)
be the CMF of
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}=n^{*}+1
, that is,
\tilde{R}(n)=\mathbb{1}_{[n^{*}+1,\infty)}(n)
. Looking at the proof of Theorem
1
, we can immediately see that this distribution will also provide a beneficial restart, and thus sharp restart at
N=n^{*}+1
is beneficial.

For the second part of the claim, we first make an observation. Let
x=\{x_{n}\}
be an element of
X
, the set of nonnegative summable sequences, and consider
F(x)\coloneqq\frac{\sum_{n}\alpha_{n}x_{n}}{\sum_{n}\beta_{n}x_{n}}
where
\beta_{n}>0
, with the intent to minimize
F(x)
over
X
. It’s clear that for any number
\lambda
such that
\alpha\geq\lambda\beta
, we have

F(x)=\frac{\sum_{n}\alpha_{n}x_{n}}{\sum_{n}\beta_{n}x_{n}}\geq\frac{\sum_{n}\lambda\beta_{n}x_{n}}{\sum_{n}\beta_{n}x_{n}}=\lambda.

Now, supposing there exists a limit
L\coloneqq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\beta_{n}}
, we have two possibilities:

•

if
\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\beta_{n}}>L
for all
n
, then
F(x)>L
for all
x
and
\inf_{x}F(x)=L
, but the minimum is never attained, or

•

if
\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\beta_{n}}\leq L
for some
n
, then there exists
m
such that
\min_{n}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\beta_{n}}=\frac{\alpha_{m}}{\beta_{m}}
. Furthermore, for
e_{m}\coloneqq\{\delta_{m,n}\}_{n\geq 0}
, we have that
\min_{x}F(x)=F(e_{m})=\frac{\alpha_{m}}{\beta_{m}}
, and thus
F
attains its minimum value.

We now apply this observation to equation (
2
) where

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}\wedge\mathcal{R}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}(1-U(n))(1-R(n))

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}(1-U(n))\sum_{k>n}r(k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k>0}r(k)\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}(1-U(n))

and

\displaystyle 1-p_{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}u(n)(1-R(n))=\sum_{n\geq 0}u(n)\sum_{k>n}r(k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k>0}r(k)\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}u(n)=\sum_{k>0}r(k)U(k-1).

Letting
\alpha_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}(1-U(n))
and
\beta_{k}\coloneqq U(k-1)
, we have
\beta_{k}>0
. We can then rearrange

S_{k-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}(1-U(n))-U(k-1)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]=\alpha_{k}-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]\beta_{k}

to get

\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\beta_{k}}=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]+\frac{S_{k-1}}{\beta_{k}}=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]+\frac{S_{k-1}}{U(k-1)}.

Since
S_{k-1}\to 0
and
U(k-1)\to 1
, we have
\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\beta_{k}}=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]
. For a sequence
r=\{r(n)\}_{n\geq 0}
, we have that

F(r)=\frac{\sum_{n}\alpha_{n}r(n)}{\sum_{n}\beta_{n}r(n)}=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}],

which gives us two possibilities:

•

if
S_{k}>0
for all
k
, then
\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\beta_{k}}>\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]
and thus
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}]=F(r)>\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]
for all summable sequences
r
, and no restart is beneficial, or

•

if
S_{k}\leq 0
for some
k
, then there exists
m\geq 1
such that

\min_{k}\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\beta_{k}}=\frac{\alpha_{m}}{\beta_{m}}=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]+\frac{S_{m-1}}{U(m-1)}\leq\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}].

In this case, sharp restart at
m
, that is,
r=e_{m}
will minimize
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}]=F(e_{m})\leq\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]
. In particular, if
S_{k}
is ever negative, then
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}]=F(e_{m})<\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]
, and there exists a most beneficial restart, which is sharp.

∎

To illustrate an immediate application of this theorem, we compute the sequence
\{S_{n}\}
for some simple distributions below to check for beneficial restart.

II.1.1
Example:
\mathcal{U}=N

For the deterministic FP time, we have
u(n)=\delta_{n,N}
,
U(n)=\mathbb{1}_{[N,\infty)}(n)
and
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]=N
. Thus we can directly compute the sequence,
\{S_{n}\}
.

\displaystyle S_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(1-U(k)-u(k)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(1-\mathbb{1}_{[N,\infty)}(k)-\delta_{k,N}N\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\mathbb{1}_{[0,N)}(k)-\delta_{k,N}N\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}n+1&\text{if }0\leq n<N\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Note that
S_{n}\geq 0
for all
n
, and thus a deterministic FP time cannot be reduced by adding restart.

II.1.2
Example:
\mathcal{U}\sim\text{Geom}(\rho)

For the geometrically distributed FP time, we have that
u(n)=\rho(1-\rho)^{n}
,
U(n)=1-(1-\rho)^{n+1}
and
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]=\frac{1-\rho}{\rho}
. Thus we can directly compute the sequence,
\{S_{n}\}
.

\displaystyle S_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(1-U(k)-u(k)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(1-(1-(1-\rho)^{k+1})-\rho(1-\rho)^{k}\cdot\frac{1-\rho}{\rho}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left((1-\rho)^{k+1}-(1-\rho)^{k+1}\right)

\displaystyle=0

Note that since
S_{n}\equiv 0
, it is trivially greater than or equal to
0
and
\mathcal{U}
does not permit beneficial restart. In fact, the geometric distribution is, in some sense, the threshold of beneficial restart, as we shall see throughout the paper. Furthermore, for any non-preemptive restart,
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]
; that is, introducing restart does not change the mean hitting time of a geometrically distributed FP process.

II.1.3
Example:
\mathcal{U}
as the mixture of two point masses

For weights
w_{1},w_{2}\in(0,1)
and
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
with
w_{1}+w_{2}=1
and
M_{2}>M_{1}
, we define the PMF
u(n)=w_{1}\delta_{n,M_{1}}+w_{2}\delta_{n,M_{2}}
. We can easily compute the cumulative mass function and
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]
, so we can write

S_{n}=\begin{cases}n+1&n<M_{1}\\
w_{2}(n+1)-w_{1}w_{2}(M_{2}-M_{1})&M_{1}\leq n<M_{2}\\
0&M_{2}\leq n\end{cases}.

It’s clear that
S_{n}
will be nonnegative outside of
[M_{1},M_{2})
, but
S_{M_{1}}<0
exactly when
M_{2}>\frac{(w_{1}+1)M_{1}+1}{w_{1}}
. That is, when the two point masses are sufficiently far apart, sharp restart at
M_{1}+1
will be beneficial.

III
Properties and techniques

Since the behavior of the restart sequence completely determines which distributions permit beneficial restart, it’s helpful to understand some of its properties and to develop some tools to help examine it more closely. To that end, we introduce some notation and terminology below.

III.1
First Step Analysis (FSA)

We begin simply, by defining
a\coloneqq\min\{n\mid u(n)>0\}
and
b\coloneqq\max\{n\mid u(n)>0\}
, if such a maximum exists. Note that
a\geq 0
, and
b\geq a
exists only when the distribution of
\mathcal{U}
is finitely supported. Furthermore,
a>0
has an interesting and immediate result: since
u(n)
and
U(n)
are 0 for all
n<a
, we can see that
S_{n}=n+1
for all
n<a
. Thus, the first possibility for
S_{n}<0
(and therefore beneficial restart) is at
n=a
. Thus, we can check
S_{a}<0
, which if true indicates that a beneficial restart exists. The converse, however, is not true. In the other case, when
S_{a}\geq 0
,
S_{n}<0
could be true for some larger
n
and thus beneficial restart might still be possible. Ultimately
S_{a}<0
is sufficient, but not necessary, to show the existence of a beneficial restart and, crucially, usually easy to check.

Proposition 1
.

If
\frac{a+1-u(a)}{u(a)}<\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]
, then
\mathcal{U}
admits beneficial restart.

We call checking the inequality from this proposition the “First Step Analysis,” or FSA.

Proof.

We know that
S_{a-1}=a
for
a>0
, hence

\displaystyle S_{a}

\displaystyle=S_{a-1}+1-U(a)-u(a)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]

\displaystyle=a+1-u(a)-u(a)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]).

This quantity is negative precisely when

u(a)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]>a+1-u(a),\text{ or}

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]>\frac{a+1-u(a)}{u(a)}.

(4)

Clearly, the same holds for
a=0
.
∎

III.2
Finitely supported distributions

When
b<\infty
, we can say even more. Since the support of
\mathcal{U}
is contained in
\{a,\ldots,b\}
, we know that
U(n)=1
for
n\geq b
and
u(n)=0
for
n>b
. Thus, for any
n>b
, we can see that

\displaystyle S_{n}

\displaystyle=S_{b}+\sum_{k=b+1}^{n}(1-U(k)-u(k)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}])

\displaystyle=S_{b}+\sum_{k=b+1}^{n}(1-1-0)=S_{b},

and thus the restart sequence becomes constant after
b
. Notice, however, that
S_{b}=\sum_{k=0}^{b}(1-U(k))-U(b)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]
which is exactly 0. Therefore, we can see that
S_{n}=0
for all
n\geq b
. In fact, the sequence will always descend to 0 at
b
, as we show next.

Proposition 2
.

For a finitely supported distribution,
\mathcal{U}
, the last non-zero step of the sequence
S_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}
will be down. That is,
S_{b-1}^{\mathcal{U}}>0
.

Proof.

Let’s examine the difference
S_{b}-S_{b-1}
. We can clearly see that

\displaystyle S_{b}-S_{b-1}

\displaystyle=S_{b-1}+(1-U(b)-u(b)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}])-S_{b-1}

\displaystyle=1-1-u(b)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]

\displaystyle=-u(b)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}].

Since
u(b)>0
by the definition of
b
and
S_{b}=0
, we can immediately see that
S_{b-1}=u(b)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]>0
.
∎

III.3
Forcing a gap in the support of
\mathcal{U}

It’s natural to ask how various perturbations to the distribution of
\mathcal{U}
will affect
S_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}
, and the first we consider is the introduction of a gap into the support, in which a distribution is cut at a certain point and all subsequent probabilistic mass is shifted back. That is, let
g,m
be chosen from
\mathbb{N}
. Then define
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
with the following distribution:

\tilde{u}(n)=\begin{cases}u(n)&\text{ if }n<m\\
0&\text{ if }m\leq n<m+g\\
u(n-g)&\text{ if }m+g\leq n\end{cases}.

Clearly,
\tilde{u}(n)
is still a probability distribution, and we can compute its mean to be
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathcal{U}}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]+g(1-U(m-1))
for
m>0
(simply
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]+g
for
m=0
). The cumulative mass function for
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
can similarly be written in terms of
\mathcal{U}
.

\tilde{U}(n)=\begin{cases}U(n)&\text{ if }n<m\\
U(m-1)&\text{ if }m\leq n<m+g\\
U(n-g)&\text{ if }m+g\leq n\end{cases}

Now, we may compute
S_{n}^{\tilde{\mathcal{U}}}
. We consider three regions, just as with the distribution above.

S_{n}^{\tilde{\mathcal{U}}}=\begin{cases}S_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}-U(n)(1-U(m-1))g\\
\quad\quad\quad\text{ if }n<m\\
S_{m-1}^{\mathcal{U}}+(1-U(m-1))(1-U(m-1)g+n-m)\\
\quad\quad\quad\text{ if }m\leq n<m+g\\
S_{n-g}^{\mathcal{U}}+(1-U(m-1))(1-U(n-g))g\\
\quad\quad\quad\text{ if }m+g\leq n\end{cases}

•

Pre-Gap:
If
n<m
, it is clear that
S_{n}^{\tilde{\mathcal{U}}}\leq S_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}
with equality only if the support hasn’t started by, or finishes before, the gap. Thus, if
\mathcal{U}
has a beneficial restart before the gap, then so will
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
. If
\mathcal{U}
does not permit beneficial restart, then
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
will for
g>\frac{S_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}}{U(n)(1-U(m-1))}
. This is true provided
U(m-1)<1
, which is always the case if
\mathcal{U}
has unbounded support (like the geometric). This makes sense considering that
\lim_{g\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathcal{U}}]=\infty
, and we know that non-preemptive restart is beneficial in that case.

•

Within-Gap:
For
m\leq n<m+g
, we can see that
S_{n}^{\tilde{\mathcal{U}}}
is monotonically increasing. On this interval, therefore, we have that

1-gU(m-1)\leq\frac{S_{n}^{\tilde{\mathcal{U}}}-S_{m-1}^{\mathcal{U}}}{1-U(m-1)}\leq(1-U(m-1))g.

So long as
g\leq\frac{1}{U(m-1)}
then
S_{n}^{\tilde{\mathcal{U}}}\geq S_{m-1}^{\mathcal{U}}
, and a lack of beneficial restart at
m
means that
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
won’t permit beneficial restart within the gap. If, however,
g\geq\frac{1}{U(m-1)}
or
S_{m-1}^{\mathcal{U}}<0
, then it’s not immediately clear.

•

Post-Gap:
In the last case, where
n\geq m+g
, we see that
S_{n}^{\tilde{\mathcal{U}}}\geq S_{n-g}^{\mathcal{U}}
. Thus
S_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}\geq 0
implies that restart won’t be beneficial after the support of
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
resumes. On the other hand, if restart is beneficial for
\mathcal{U}
at
N+1
, then it will be for
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
at
N+1+g
iff
g<\frac{|S_{N}^{\mathcal{U}}|}{(1-U(m-1))(1-U(N))}
.

An interesting subcase is the one in which the entire distribution is pushed back, which we consider now.

III.3.1
Delaying a FP time

If
m=0
, then the previous trichotomy reduces to

S_{n}^{\tilde{\mathcal{U}}}=\begin{cases}n+1&\text{ if }0\leq n<g\\
S_{n-g}^{\mathcal{U}}+(1-U(n-g))g&\text{ if }g\leq n\end{cases}.

Thus, we can say that
S_{n}^{\tilde{\mathcal{U}}}\geq 0
for
n<g
and
S_{n}^{\tilde{\mathcal{U}}}\geq S_{n-g}^{\mathcal{U}}
for
n\geq g
. Thus, if
\mathcal{U}
does not permit beneficial restart, neither will
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
. This sheds more light on our previous example with the shifted geometric distribution; delaying by 1 only increased the values of
S_{n}
.

If, on the other hand, sharp restart at
N+1
is beneficial for
\mathcal{U}
, we can see that restart at
N+1+g
will be beneficial for
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
iff
g<\frac{|S_{N}^{\mathcal{U}}|}{1-U(N)}
. Equivalently, we can say if the gap is too large, then
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
will not permit beneficial restart, even if
\mathcal{U}
does.

III.4
Uniqueness of the distribution

It is clear that each distribution,
\mathcal{U}
, will generate a unique restart sequence, so we are immediately prompted to ask whether each restart sequence is associated with a single distribution. Suppose that
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
are distinct distributions, but that
\{S_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}\}
and
\{S_{n}^{\mathcal{V}}\}
are the same sequence.

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=S_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}-S_{n}^{\mathcal{V}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(1-U(k)-u(k)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}])-\sum_{k=0}^{n}(1-V(k)-v(k)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}])

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(V(k)-U(k)+v(k)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}]-u(k)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}])

Since this must be true for all
n\geq 0
, we have

0=V(k)+v(k)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}]-U(k)-u(k)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]

for all
k\geq 0
. When
k=0
, this equates to
0=v(0)(1+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}])-u(0)(1+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}])
. If we suppose further that
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
have the same mean, then it’s clear that
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
must have the same distribution. Without this last supposition, however, we can expect no such result.

In general, define
p\coloneqq\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]+1}
and
q\coloneqq 1-p
. We know that
U(0)=\frac{1-S_{0}}{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]+1}
, and since
S_{n}=S_{n-1}+1-U(n)+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}](U(n)-U(n-1))
for
n\geq 1
, we can write

U(n)=p(1+S_{n-1}-S_{n})+qU(n-1).

Defining
F_{n}=1+S_{n-1}-S_{n}
and
S_{-1}=0
, we have

U(n)=p\sum_{k=0}^{n}F_{n-k}q^{k}.

(5)

That is, since
F_{n}
is the same for
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
, and
p,q
depend only on the mean of
\mathcal{U}
or
\mathcal{V}
, we offer the following proposition.

Proposition 3
.

If a given a restart sequence,
\{S_{n}\}
, can be associated with a distribution,
\mathcal{U}
, such distribution is determined uniquely up to
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]
.

III.5
Convexity analysis on the restart sequence

We begin by defining convexity of the restart sequence in a standard way: let
C_{n}\coloneqq\frac{S_{n-1}-2S_{n}+S_{n+1}}{2}
and say that the sequence is convex at
n
if
C_{n}>0
, concave at
n
if
C_{n}<0
or linear at
n
if
C_{n}=0
. In fact, there is a quick way to check this inequality.

Proposition 4
.

Define
K\coloneqq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]}{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]+1}
.

•

\{S_{n}\}
is convex at
n
if
u(n+1)<Ku(n)
,

•

\{S_{n}\}
is linear at
n
if
u(n+1)=Ku(n)
, and

•

\{S_{n}\}
is concave at
n
if
u(n+1)>Ku(n)
.

Proof.

\displaystyle C_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{n-1}-2S_{n}+S_{n+1}}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(S_{n-1}-2(S_{n-1}+1-U(n)-u(n)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}])\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad+(S_{n-1}+1-U(n)-u(n)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]+1

\displaystyle\quad\quad\left.-U(n+1)-u(n+1)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}])\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(U(n)-U(n+1)+(u(n)-u(n+1))\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(u(n)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]-u(n+1)(1+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}])\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]+1}{2}\left(u(n)K-u(n+1)\right)

∎

As a note, it can be useful to consider the ratio
r_{n}\coloneqq\frac{u(n+1)}{u(n)}
when appropriate. In this case, we need only compare
r_{n}
and
K
to determine convexity at
n
.

III.5.1
Bounded distributions

For distributions with finite support that produce restart sequences with constant convexity, a lot can be said. Since
\{S_{n}\}
decreases to 0 at
b
by Proposition
2
, we can immediately make the following proposition.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
have a distribution with finite support. Then

•

\{S_{n}\}
convex or linear on its support means
S_{n}\geq 0
for all
n
, and

•

\{S_{n}\}
concave on its support means that
S_{a}<0
iff
S_{n}<0
, and we need only perform a FSA by checking inequality (
4
).

We note here that any that any non-decreasing PMF will produce a concave
\{S_{n}\}
, since
K<1
. For example, we can then immediately confirm that a constant distribution does not permit beneficial restart. Since
u(n)=\frac{1}{b-a+1}
for all
a\leq n\leq b
, we have
\frac{u(n+1)}{u(n)}=1>K
and
\{S_{n}\}
is concave. We then check inequality (
4
) and see

\displaystyle\frac{a+1-u(a)}{u(a)}

\displaystyle=\frac{a+1-\frac{1}{b-a+1}}{\frac{1}{b-a+1}}

\displaystyle=(b-a+1)(a+1)-1

\displaystyle=ab-a^{2}+a+b-a+1-1

\displaystyle=a(b-a)+b\geq b\geq\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}].

Thus
\mathcal{U}\sim\text{Unif}(a,b)
does not permit beneficial restart.

III.6
Convex or concave tails

In cases where
\mathcal{U}
has infinite support, there are additional considerations. Rather than considering the entire support, we often just consider the tail of
\{S_{n}\}
. Since
S_{n}\to 0
, we can make the following observation.

Proposition 6
.

Suppose there exists some
M\in\mathbb{N}
such that the sign of
d_{n}\coloneqq u(n+1)-Ku(n)
is constant for all
n\geq M
. Then the tail of
\{S_{n}\}
is either convex (
d_{n}<0
), concave (
d_{n}>0
), or linear (
d_{n}=0
) after
M
. In the case of a convex or linear tail,
S_{n}\geq 0
for all
n\geq M
and sharp restart after
M
won’t be beneficial. For a concave tail, sharp restart at
any
time after
M
will be beneficial.

III.6.1
Example of a convex tail: the shifted geometric

We’ve already seen the so-called “shifted geometric,” or the geometric distribution on
\mathbb{Z}^{+}
with rate parameter
\rho
. This clearly satisfies
u(n+1)-Ku(n)<0
for
M=1
, since
K=\frac{1}{1+\rho}
. As previously determined, this distribution does not allow for beneficial restart.

III.6.2
Examples of a linear tail

The only linear subsequence that converges to zero is the one with all zeros, and that’s what we see in the tails below.

•

The standard geometric distribution on
\mathbb{N}
produces the restart sequence with
S_{n}=0
for all
n
.

•

Any finitely supported distribution has
S_{n}=0
for all
n\geq b
.

•

As discussed in Section
III.4
, the above case need not arise only from a finitely supported distribution. The sequence
\{S_{n}\}=\{1,0,0,\ldots\}
can clearly arise as a result of
\mathcal{U}=1
when
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]=1
. But more generally, we can produce a distribution associated with this restart sequence using formula (
5
) for other values of
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]
. The sequence
\{F_{n}\}
becomes
\{0,2,1,1,\ldots\}
, which allows for
U(n)=p\sum_{k=0}^{n}F_{n-k}q^{k}
with
p=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]+1}
and
q=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]}{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]+1}=K
, and thus
u(0)=0
,
u(1)=2p
and
u(n)=(1-2p)pq^{n-2}
for
n\geq 2
with
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]\geq 1
. Since
q=K
from our earlier definition, it’s clear that, for
n\geq 2
, we have
\frac{u(n+1)}{u(n)}=K
, and thus the tail of
\{S_{n}\}
is linear (all zeros).

III.6.3
Example of a concave tail: the zeta distribution on
\mathbb{N}

For
p\in(1,\infty)
, define
u(n)=(\zeta(p)(n+1)^{p})^{-1}
for
n\geq 0
, which has mean
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]=\frac{\zeta(p-1)}{\zeta(p)}-1
. Then, for
p>2
,
K<1
is fixed and
r_{n}=\frac{(n+1)^{p}}{(n+2)^{p}}
is monotonically increasing to 1. Thus there exists some
M\in\mathbb{N}
such that
r_{n}>K
for all
n>M
, and sharp restart is beneficial after this point (and possibly before).

III.7
Log-concave distributions

We begin by recalling that a non-negative sequence,
\{a_{n}\},
is called
logarithmically-concave
or just
log-concave
if
a_{n}^{2}\geq a_{n-1}a_{n+1}
and the sequence has no internal 0s. We now consider FP times that have log-concave PMF.

On the support of
\mathcal{U}
, it is useful to define
r_{n}=\begin{cases}\frac{u(n+1)}{u(n)}&a\leq n\leq b\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}
, which is a weakly decreasing sequence in
n
for
u(n)
log-concave.

Proposition 7
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a FP process with a log-concave PMF. Then
\mathcal{U}
does not permit beneficial restart.

Proof.

Suppose by way of contradiction that there exists some
n^{*}
such that
S_{n^{*}}<0
and beneficial restart is possible. Since
\{S_{n}\}
converges to
0
, there exists some
s\coloneqq\min_{n}\{S_{n}\}
, and we define
m\coloneqq\min\{n\mid S_{n}=s\}
. We have immediately that
S_{m}\leq S_{m+1}
, and then there are two cases to address.

•

m>0

In this case, we also have that
S_{m}<S_{m-1}
, and thus we can write

\displaystyle S_{m}-S_{m-1}

\displaystyle<0\leq S_{m+1}-S_{m}

\displaystyle 1-U(m)-u(m)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]

\displaystyle<1-U(m)-u(m+1)\left(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]+1\right)

K=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]}{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]+1}>\frac{u(m+1)}{u(m)}\geq\frac{u(n+1)}{u(n)},

for all
n\geq m
.

•

m=0

In this case, we have instead that
S_{m}<0
, and thus we can write

\displaystyle S_{m}

\displaystyle<0\leq S_{m+1}-S_{m}

\displaystyle 1-U(0)-u(0)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]

\displaystyle<1-U(0)-u(1)\left(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]+1\right)

K=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]}{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{U}]+1}>\frac{u(1)}{u(0)}\geq\frac{u(n+1)}{u(n)},

for all
n\geq m
.

In either case, the restart sequence in convex after
m
. Whether the support is finite or infinite, this is not possible, and thus
\mathcal{U}
does not permit beneficial restart.
∎

III.7.1
Example: Binomial

For
N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
p\in[0,1]
, we define
u(n)=\binom{N}{n}p^{n}(1-p)^{N-n}
. We can then compute
r_{n}=\frac{p(N-n)}{(1-p)(n+1)}
, which is decreasing in
n
over its support and therefore
\mathcal{U}
has a log-concave PMF and does not permit beneficial restart.

III.7.2
Example: Poisson

For
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
, we define
u(n)=\frac{\lambda^{n}e^{-\lambda}}{n!}
, which gives us
r_{n}=\frac{\lambda}{n}
. Since
r_{n}
is clearly decreasing in
n
, we have that the PMF of
\mathcal{U}
is log-concave, and
\mathcal{U}
does not permit beneficial restart.

III.8
Log-convex distributions

Having identified such a definitive result for FP procesess with log-concave PMF, it is reasonable to ask what can be said about those with log-convex PMF, that is, those that satisfy the inequality
u(n)^{2}\leq u(n-1)u(n+1)
. These distributions have a different nature than those that are log-concave, and aren’t as amenable to interpretations with finite support. For those with infinite support, however, we offer the following proposition.

Proposition 8
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a FP process with a log-convex PMF. Then either
\mathcal{U}
permits beneficial restart or
\mathcal{U}\sim\text{Geom}(\rho)
.

Proof.

The proof follows the same form as that of Proposition
7
. Simply assuming that there exists some
n^{*}
such that
S_{n^{*}}\geq 0
and reversing all the inequalities (paying special attention to which inequalities are strict) leads to the following conclusion: either
S_{n}=0
for all
n\geq 0
(with
r_{n}
constant) or we have a contradiction. Thus, we have the result. As a special note, if
\mathcal{U}
is not geometrically distributed, then
S_{n}<0
for all
n\geq 0
, and all non-preemptive sharp restarts are beneficial.
∎

III.8.1
Example: Zeta distribution supported on
\mathbb{N}

If we consider our previous example again, we can see that the zeta distribution on
\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
is log-convex. Since
r_{n}
is non-constant, we have
S_{n}<0
for all
n\geq 0
and all non-preemptive sharp restarts are beneficial.

III.8.2
Example: Negative Binomial

For
r\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
p\in[0,1]
, we define
u(n)=\binom{n+r-1}{n}p^{n}(1-p)^{r}
. We can then compute
r_{n}=\frac{p(n+r)}{(n+1)}
, which is weakly decreasing in
n
over its support and therefore
\mathcal{U}
has a log-concave PMF and doesn’t permit beneficial restart. In particular, if
r=1
, then
u(n)
reduces to
(1-p)p^{n}
, the geometric distribution.

While the standard model with
r\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
has a very natural interpretation, of some interest is the case where we relax that condition and instead let
r
take values in
(0,\infty)
. It’s not precisely clear what this process is modeling, but it is still a well-defined probability distribution. And, as soon as
r
passes below 1, we see that
r_{n}
becomes monotonically increasing and thus
u(n)
is log-convex.

In summary, for
r\geq 1
, beneficial restart is impossible, but all non-preemptive sharp restarts become beneficial with
r<1
.

IV
Mixture Distributions

Considering the restart sequence generated by a mixture distribution can allow us to take a different angle for approaching some distributions that are more easily understood as the convex linear combination of possibly simpler distributions, and also to explore perturbations of known distributions.

Let’s consider a case when distribution of our FP process is the convex linear combination of two other distributions; that is, we have FP time
\mathcal{Y}
with PMF given by
y(n)=w_{1}x_{1}(n)+w_{2}x_{2}(n)
with
w_{1}+w_{2}=1
and
x_{1}(n),x_{2}(n)
the PMF of two independent FP times
\mathcal{X}_{1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{2}
. We can immediately see that the cumulative distributions have the same pattern,
Y(n)=w_{1}X_{1}(n)+w_{2}X_{2}(n)
, and as a result, we have that
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Y}]=w_{1}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{1}]+w_{2}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{2}]
. Notice that we are not saying
\mathcal{Y}=w_{1}\mathcal{X}_{1}+w_{2}\mathcal{X}_{2}
.
\mathcal{Y}
is not the blending of two random variables, but rather a random variable drawn from a blended distribution.

Our interest is now in determining whether this new distribution will permit beneficial restart. To this end we compute the partial sums,
S_{n}^{\mathcal{Y}}
, given the sums
S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}_{1}}
and
S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}_{2}}
. We then consider the circumstances (if any) under which
S_{n}^{\mathcal{Y}}<0
.

\displaystyle S_{n}^{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left[1-Y(k)-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Y}]y(k)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left[(w_{1}+w_{2})-(w_{1}X_{1}(k)+w_{2}X_{2}(k))\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad-(w_{1}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{1}]x_{1}(k)+w_{2}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{2}]x_{2}(k))

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+(w_{1}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{1}]x_{1}(k)+w_{2}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{2}]x_{2}(k))

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad-\left.(w_{1}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{1}]+w_{2}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{2}])(w_{1}x_{1}(k)+w_{2}x_{2}(k))\right]

\displaystyle=w_{1}S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}_{1}}+w_{2}S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}_{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+w_{1}w_{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{1}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{2}])(x_{1}(k)-x_{2}(k))

Computing the last sum gives us the formula we desire:

\scriptsize{S_{n}^{\mathcal{Y}}=w_{1}S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}_{1}}+w_{2}S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}_{2}}+w_{1}w_{2}(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{1}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{2}])(X_{1}(n)-X_{2}(n)).}

(6)

From (
6
) we can make several observations. Among them we have:

•

Suppose
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{1}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{2}]
. Then
S_{n}^{\mathcal{Y}}
reduces to
w_{1}S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}_{1}}+w_{2}S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}_{2}}
. In the case where
S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}_{1}},S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}_{2}}\geq 0
, then so is
S_{n}^{\mathcal{Y}}
, and
\mathcal{Y}
does not permit beneficial restart. In the opposite case, where
S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}_{1}},S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}_{2}}<0
, again
S_{n}^{\mathcal{Y}}
follows and beneficial restart is possible.

•

Without loss of generality, suppose that
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{1}]>\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{2}]
. Equivalently,

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}(1-X_{1}(n))-(1-X_{2}(n))

\displaystyle>0

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}(X_{2}(n)-X_{1}(n))>0.

Thus we know
X_{1}(n)-X_{2}(n)<0
for at least some
n\geq 0
, which implies that the last term in (
6
) must be negative for some
n\geq 0
.

•

The derivation of (
6
) assumed that the linear combination was convex, but it need not be. Provided that
w_{1}+w_{2}=1
and
y(n)
is still a valid probability mass function, then we can allow for
w_{1},w_{2}<0
. This is useful for analyzing perturbations of distributions, but also for constructing certain complicated distributions.

IV.1
Some Examples

IV.1.1
Example: Mixture of two point masses

Let
x_{1}(n)=\delta_{M_{1},n}
and
x_{2}(n)=\delta_{M_{2},n}
for
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
X_{1}(n)=\mathbb{1}_{[M_{1},\infty)}(n)
and
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{1}]=M_{1}
, and similar for
\mathcal{X}_{2}
. Furthermore, we know that these purely deterministic FP distributions do not permit beneficial restart, which means that
S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}_{1}}\geq 0
for all
n\geq 0
, and we can confirm that by computing

\displaystyle S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}1-X_{1}(k)-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{1}]x_{1}(k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbb{1}_{[0,M_{1})}(n)-M_{1}\delta_{M_{1},n}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}n+1&\text{ if }0\leq n<M_{1}\\
0&\text{ if }M_{1}\leq n\end{cases}\geq 0\text{ for all }n\geq 0.

Now, let’s suppose that
M_{2}>M_{1}
, and define
y(n)\coloneqq w_{1}x_{1}(n)+w_{2}x_{2}(n)
for some appropriate
w_{1},w_{2}
. By (
6
), we have

S_{n}^{\mathcal{Y}}=w_{1}S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}_{1}}+w_{2}S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}_{2}}+w_{1}w_{2}(M_{2}-M_{1})(X_{2}(n)-X_{1}(n)).

The difference in cumulative partial sums reduces to

X_{2}(n)-X_{1}(n)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ for }0\leq n<M_{1}\\
-1&\text{ for }M_{1}\leq n<M_{2}\\
0&\text{ for }M_{2}\leq n\end{cases}.

Thus the partial sums collapse to

S_{n}^{\mathcal{Y}}=\begin{cases}n+1&\text{ for }0\leq n<M_{1}\\
w_{2}\left(n+1-w_{1}(M_{2}-M_{1})\right)&\text{ for }M_{1}\leq n<M_{2}\\
0&\text{ for }M_{2}\leq n\end{cases}.

The partial sums are clearly non-negative for
n
smaller than
M_{1}
and greater than or equal to
M_{2}
, so the only place we need look for beneficial restart is for
M_{1}\leq n<M_{2}
. In this region,
S_{n}^{\mathcal{Y}}<0
iff

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle>w_{2}\left(n+1-w_{1}(M_{2}-M_{1})\right)

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle<w_{1}(M_{2}-M_{1})-1,

which is only possible when
M_{1}<w_{1}(M_{2}-M_{1})-1
, or equivalently
M_{2}>\frac{(1+w_{1})M_{1}+1}{w_{1}}
, which eliminates the possibility of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
adjacent. This result clearly coincides with the one in Section
II.1.3
.

IV.1.2
Example: Mixture of two geometric distributions on
\mathbb{N}

Let
x_{1}(n)=\rho(1-\rho)^{n}
and
x_{2}(n)=\gamma(1-\gamma)^{n}
with
\gamma\geq\rho
so
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{1}]\geq\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{2}]
. We note that
X_{1}(n)=1-\left(1-\rho\right)^{n+1}
and
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{1}]=\frac{1-\rho}{\rho}
with similar results for
\mathcal{X}_{2}
. Computing the partial sums yields

S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}_{1}}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}1-\left(1-(1-\rho)^{n+1}\right)-\frac{1-\rho}{\rho}\cdot\rho(1-\rho)^{n}=0.

Just as previously noted, the geometric distribution starting at 0 is the threshold distribution, with its partial sums equal to 0 for all
n\geq 0
. This simplifies (
6
) to

\displaystyle S_{n}^{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle=w_{1}w_{2}(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{1}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{2}])(X_{1}(n)-X_{2}(n))

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{1}w_{2}}{\rho\gamma}(\gamma-\rho)((1-\gamma)^{n+1}-(1-\rho)^{n+1}).

From the above, it is clear that
S_{n}^{\mathcal{Y}}\leq 0
for all
n\geq 0
with equality only when
\gamma=\rho
(and in that case, clearly
\mathcal{Y}
is simply geometric and we know its partial sums to be
0
). Thus, for
\gamma\neq\rho
, we have that beneficial restart is possible – in particular, sharp restart is beneficial for all
N\geq 1
.

IV.1.3
Example: Perturbation by geometric distribution

Let FP time
\mathcal{X}_{1}
have some arbitrary PMF
x_{1}(n)
, and
\mathcal{X}_{2}
be distributed geometrically on
\mathbb{N}
with rate parameter
\gamma
. Since the restart sequence for
\mathcal{X}_{2}
is identically
0
, we can easily compute the partial sums for
\mathcal{Y}
.

S_{n}^{\mathcal{Y}}=w_{1}S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}_{1}}+w_{1}w_{2}(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{1}]-\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma})(X_{1}(n)+(1-\gamma)^{n+1}-1)

In the case where
\gamma
is selected so that
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{1}]
, the second term is clearly 0, and we have simply
S_{n}^{\mathcal{Y}}=w_{1}S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}_{1}}
. In other words, for any FP
\mathcal{X}_{1}
with finite mean, there exists
\gamma\in(0,1)
such that a perturbation by the geometric distribution with rate
\gamma
only scales the restart sequence by
w_{1}
, and thus does not affect whether restart is beneficial at all.

V
Conclusion

Using the construct of the restart sequence allows us to understand the nature of FPUR processes. In particular, it allows us to see that any FP process with a log-concave PMF will not permit beneficial restart, and any process with a log-convex PMF will, except one which is geometrically distributed, which isn’t affected by restart at all. Additionally, if the tail of the PMF decays more quickly than geometrically, late restarts won’t be beneficial. On the other hand, if the PMF has a heavy tail, then all late restarts will be beneficial.
Much investigation remains to be done. Can this tool be extended to the continuous time case? Can the restart sequence provide useful information about FP processes with highly fragmented supports? Additionally, exploring the kinds of perturbations to FP time distributions that can be analyzed using the restart sequence can be explored moving forward.
