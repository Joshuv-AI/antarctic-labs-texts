Homogeneous multigrid for hybrid discretizations: application to HHO methods
Daniele A. Di Pietro, Zhaonan Dong, Guido Kanschat, Pierre Matalon, Andreas Rupp

Abstract
. We prove the uniform convergence of the geometric multigrid V-cycle for hybrid high-order (HHO) and other discontinuous skeletal methods. Our results generalize previously established results for HDG methods, and our multigrid method uses standard smoothers and local solvers that are bounded, convergent, and consistent. We use a weak version of elliptic regularity in our proofs. Numerical experiments confirm our theoretical results. Keywords. Geometric multigrid, homogeneous multigrid, hybrid high-order methods, skeleton methods.

65F10, 65N30, 65N50

1.
Introduction

In the context of fast solvers for linear systems arising from hybrid discretizations of second-order elliptic equations, we propose in this work a generalized framework for the convergence analysis of geometric multigrid methods
[
BVEM00
,
TOS01
]
.

Hybrid discretization methods have been part of the numerical analyst’s toolbox to solve partial differential equations since the seventies, starting with hybridized versions of mixed methods such as the Raviart-Thomas (RT-H)
[
RT77
]
and the Brezzi-Douglas-Marini (BDM-H)
[
BDM85
]
methods. They have gained growing interest in recent years with the outbreak of modern schemes such as the unifying framework of Hybridizable Discontinuous Galerkin (HDG) methods
[
CGL09
]
and Hybrid High-Order (HHO) methods
[
DPEL14
,
DPE15
,
DPD20
]
. The list also includes, but is not limited to, Compatible Discrete Operators (CDO)
[
BE14
]
, Mixed and Hybrid Finite Volumes (MHFV)
[
DE06
,
EGH10
,
DEGH10
]
, Mimetic Finite Differences (MFD)
[
BdVLM14
]
, weak Galerkin
[
WY13
]
, local discontinuous Galerkin-hybridizable (LDG-H)
[
CDG08
]
and discontinuous Petrov–Galerkin (DPG)
[
DG11
]
methods. Hybrid discretization methods are characterized by the location of their degrees of freedom (DOFs), placed both within the mesh cells and on the faces. In this configuration, the discrete scheme is built so that the cell DOFs are only locally coupled, leaving the face DOFs in charge of the global coupling. Algebraically, this feature enables the local elimination of the cell unknowns from the arising linear system, resulting in a Schur complement of reduced size, where only the face unknowns remain. The mechanical engineering terminology refers to this elimination process as
static condensation
, and the resulting system is often called the (statically)
condensed
system. The terminology
trace
or
skeleton
system is also employed.
For an extended introduction to hybrid methods and hybridization, we refer to the preface of
[
DPD20
]
and the first pages of
[
CGL09
]
.

This paper addresses the fast solution of the condensed systems arising from hybrid discretizations of second-order elliptic equations, focusing on multigrid methods.
Various multigrid solvers and preconditioners have been designed over the last decade, focusing primarily on HDG
[
CDGT14
,
SA17
,
WMBT19
,
MBTS20
,
LRK21
,
LRK22a
]
and HHO
[
DPHM+21a
,
DPHM+21b
,
BDP21
,
DPMMR23
]
, but also covering specifically DPG
[
RC17
,
PD21
]
and RT-H/BDM-H
[
GT09
]
.
The above references focus on geometric multigrid methods, while a fully algebraic multigrid method has been considered in
[
DPHM+23
]
.

The main difficulty in designing a geometric multigrid algorithm for a trace system resides in the fact that the DOFs are supported by the mesh skeleton, which makes classical intergrid transfer operators designed for element-based DOFs unsuitable. While earlier approaches
[
CDGT14
,
GT09
,
KW18
]
recast trace functions into bulk functions in order to make use of a known efficient solver (typically, a standard piecewise linear continuous Finite Element multigrid solver), the more recent developments seem to converge toward the so-called notion of
homogeneous
multigrid, where the hybrid discretization is conserved at every level of the mesh hierarchy and “interskeleton” transfer operators are designed to communicate trace functions from one mesh skeleton to the other. To this end, multiple skeletal injection operators have already been proposed
[
LRK22a
,
DPHM+21a
]
.

In this paper, we build upon the work of
[
LRK21
,
LRK22a
,
LRK22b
,
LWKR23
]
on HDG methods to propose a generalized demonstration framework for the uniform convergence of
homogeneous
multigrid methods with V-cycle. The main motivation behind this generalization is to include the HHO methods in its application scope. Indeed, if recent multigrid solvers for HHO methods
[
DPHM+21a
,
DPHM+21b
,
DPMMR23
]
have experimentally shown their optimal behavior, the supporting theory is still missing. In the second part of this paper, we then prove that the classical HHO method fulfills the assumptions of this new framework.

The present theory does not assume full elliptic regularity of the problem, thus allowing complex domains with re-entrant corners. However, although modern hybrid methods natively handle polyhedral elements, it is restricted to simplicial meshes. The multigrid method is built in a standard fashion from an abstract injection operator: standard smoothers are used, and the restriction operator is chosen as the adjoint of the injection operator. A classical, symmetric V-cycle is used if the problem exhibits full elliptic regularity. If not, then a variable V-cycle is employed, in which the number of (symmetric) smoothing steps increases as the level decreases in the hierarchy.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce notations and the model problem. Section
3
describes the abstract framework: (i) the hybrid method is described abstractly, in the form of approximation spaces, local linear operators, stabilization term and bilinear form; (ii) the injection operators used in the multigrid method and its analysis are introduced; (iii) the properties assumed from the hybrid method ((
HM1
)-(
HM8
)) and the injection operator ((
IA1
)-(
IA2
)) are listed.
Section
4
describes the multigrid method and asserts the associated convergence results, while Section
5
carries out the convergence analysis.
In Section
6
, we verify our framework’s assumptions for the standard HHO method, and analyze various injection operators in Section
7
.
Finally, Section
8
presents the numerical experiments supporting our theoretical results, realized with the HHO method on two and three-dimensional test cases.

2.
Problem formulation and notation

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a polygonally bounded Lipschitz domain with boundary
\partial\Omega
. We approximate the unknown
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
satisfying

(2.1)

-\Delta u=f\qquad\text{ in }\Omega

for some right-hand side
f
. We assume the problem to be regular in the sense that

(2.2)

\|u\|_{1+\alpha}\leq c\|f\|_{\alpha-1}\qquad\text{ for some }\alpha\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1].

Here,
\|\cdot\|_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
denotes the induced norm of the Sobolev–Slobodeckij space
H^{\alpha}(\Omega)
. By
\|\cdot\|_{-\alpha}
and
H^{-\alpha}(\Omega)
we denote the dual norm and the dual space of
H^{\alpha}_{0}(\Omega)
with respect to the extension of
L^{2}
duality, respectively. We denote by
\|\cdot\|_{X}
and
(\cdot,\cdot)_{X}
the
L^{2}
-norm and
L^{2}
-scalar product with respect to the set
X\subset\Omega
, respectively.
The same notation is also used for the inner product
of
[L^{2}(X)]^{d}
(the exact meaning can be inferred from the context) such that for all
\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}\in[L^{2}(X)]^{d}
,
(p,q)_{X}\coloneq\int_{X}\boldsymbol{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}
, where
\cdot
denotes the dot product. Without loss of generality, we assume that
\Omega
has diameter 1.

Remark 2.1

(More general boundary conditions)
.

The following approaches directly transfer to non-homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions since those only influence the right-hand sides of the numerical schemes. However, this is not true (without some further arguments) for locally changing the type of the boundary condition.

3.
Discontinuous skeletal methods

We will render a general framework for all discontinuous skeletal methods covering HDG and HHO methods. To this end, we start with a family
(\mathcal{T}_{\ell})_{\ell=0,\dots,L}
of successively refined simplicial meshes and their corresponding face sets
(\mathcal{F}_{\ell})_{\ell=0,\dots,L}
.
For all level
\ell
, we define
h_{\ell}=\max_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{\ell}}h_{T}
, where
h_{T}
denotes the diameter of
T
.
The family
(\mathcal{T}_{\ell})_{\ell=0,\dots,L}
is regular (see
[
EG21
, Def. 11.2]
for a precise definition), which implies that mesh elements do not deteriorate, and all of its elements are geometrically conforming, which excludes hanging nodes. Additionally, we assume that either
F\subset\partial\Omega
, or
F\cap\partial\Omega
comprises at most one point, and that refinement does not progress too fast, i.e., there is
c_{\text{ref}}>0
such that

(3.1)

h_{\ell}\geq c_{\text{ref}}h_{\ell-1}\qquad\forall\ell\in\{1,\dots,L\}.

Finally, we assume that our mesh is quasi-uniform implying that there is a constant
c_{\textup{uni}}
such that
h_{\ell}\leq c_{\textup{uni}}\min_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{\ell}}h_{T}
.

Next, we choose a finite-dimensional space
M_{F}\subset L^{2}(F)
of functions living on face
F
for all
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
. In the numerical scheme, this space will be the test and trial spaces for the skeletal variable
m_{\ell}
approximating the trace of the unknown
u
on the skeleton, which we denote by
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
. Moreover, finite-dimensional approximation spaces
V_{T}\subset L^{2}(T)
and
\boldsymbol{W}_{T}\subset L^{2}(T)^{d}
are defined element-wise. The space
V_{T}
is the local test and trial space for the primary unknown
u
, while the space
\boldsymbol{W}_{T}
is the local test and trial space for the dual unknown
\boldsymbol{q}=-\nabla u
. Global spaces are defined by concatenation via

\displaystyle M_{\ell}

\displaystyle\coloneq\left\{m\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{\ell})\;\middle|\;\begin{array}[]{r@{\,}c@{\,}ll}m_{|F}&\in&\mathcal{P}_{p}&\forall F\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell}\\
m_{|F}&=&0&\forall F\subset\partial\Omega\end{array}\right\},

(3.4)

\displaystyle V_{\ell}

\displaystyle\coloneq\bigl\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega)\big|\;v_{|T}\in V_{T},\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{\ell}\bigr\},

(3.5)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}

\displaystyle\coloneq\bigl\{\boldsymbol{q}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\big|\;\boldsymbol{q}_{|T}\in\boldsymbol{W}_{T},\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{\ell}\bigr\}.

We define element-wise, abstract linear operators

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{T}\colon M_{\ell}|_{\partial T}\to V_{T},

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{T}\colon L^{2}(T)\to V_{T},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{T}\colon M_{\ell}|_{\partial T}\to\boldsymbol{W}_{T}.

The choice of these operators, called local solvers, influences the numerical schemes. Global linear operators are constructed by concatenation of their element-local analogous:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\ell}\colon M_{\ell}\to V_{\ell},

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\ell}\colon L^{2}(\Omega)\to V_{\ell},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell}\colon M_{\ell}\to\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}.

One can show that
m_{\ell}
approximates the trace of
u
on the skeleton and that

u\approx\mathcal{U}_{\ell}m_{\ell}+\mathcal{V}_{\ell}f

in the bulk of
\Omega
if
m_{\ell}
satisfies

(3.6)

a_{\ell}(m_{\ell},\mu)=\int_{\Omega}f\,\mathcal{U}_{\ell}\mu\qquad\text{ for all }\mu\in M_{\ell},

where the elliptic and continuous bilinear form
a_{\ell}
has the form

(3.7)

a_{\ell}(m,\mu)=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell}m\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell}\mu+s_{\ell}(m,\mu).

The symmetric positive semi-definite
s_{\ell}
is usually referred to as (condensed)
penalty
or
stabilizing term
.

The choices of the spaces
M_{F}
,
V_{T}
,
\boldsymbol{W}_{T}
, the local solvers
\mathcal{U}_{\ell}
,
\mathcal{V}_{\ell}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell}
and the stabilizing term
s_{\ell}
completely define a discontinuous skeletal method.

Remark 3.1

(Possible choices of hybrid methods)
.

•

For the
LDG-H
method, we set
M_{F}=\mathcal{P}_{p}(F)
,
V_{T}=\mathcal{P}_{p}(T)
, and
\boldsymbol{W}_{T}=\mathcal{P}^{d}_{p}(T)
.
The operators
\mathcal{U}_{T}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{T}
map
m_{\partial T}
to the element-wise solutions
u_{T}\in V_{T}
and
\boldsymbol{q}_{T}\in\boldsymbol{W}_{T}
of

(3.8a)

\displaystyle\int_{T}\boldsymbol{q}_{T}\cdot\boldsymbol{p}_{T}-\int_{T}u_{T}\nabla\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{p}_{T}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\partial T}m\boldsymbol{p}_{T}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}

(3.8b)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial T}(\boldsymbol{q}_{T}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}+\tau_{\ell}u_{T})v_{T}-\int_{T}\boldsymbol{q}_{T}\cdot\nabla v_{T}

\displaystyle=\tau_{\ell}\int_{\partial T}mv_{T}

with test functions
v_{T}\in V_{T}
and
\boldsymbol{p}_{T}\in\boldsymbol{W}_{T}
, and
\boldsymbol{\nu}
the outward normal vector to
\partial T
. In this sense
\mathcal{U}_{T}\colon m_{\partial T}\mapsto u_{T}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{T}:m_{\partial T}\mapsto\boldsymbol{q}_{T}
for all
T\in\mathcal{T}_{\ell}
.

Finally, the bilinear form
a_{\ell}
is defined as

a_{\ell}(m_{\ell},\mu)=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell}m_{\ell}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell}\mu+\underbrace{\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{\ell}}\tau_{\ell}\int_{\partial T}(\mathcal{U}_{\ell}m_{\ell}-m_{\ell})(\mathcal{U}_{\ell}\mu-\mu)}_{=s_{\ell}(m_{\ell},\mu)}

for some parameter
\tau_{\ell}>0
.

•

For the
RT-H
and
BDM-H
methods, one uses the framework of the LDG-H method replacing the local bulk spaces by the RT-H and BDM-H spaces with
\tau_{\ell}=0
.

•

For the
HHO
method, we set
M_{F}=\mathcal{P}_{p}(F)
,
V_{T}=\mathcal{P}_{p}(T)
, and
\boldsymbol{W}_{T}=\nabla\mathcal{P}_{p+1}(T)
.
For
(u_{T},m_{\partial T})\in V_{T}\times M_{\partial T}
, we define
\boldsymbol{q}_{T}(u_{T},m_{\partial T})
as the element-wise solution of

(3.9)

\int_{T}\boldsymbol{q}_{T}(u_{T},m_{\partial T})\cdot\boldsymbol{p}_{T}-\int_{T}u_{T}\nabla\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{p}_{T}=-\int_{\partial T}m_{\partial T}\boldsymbol{p}_{T}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}

for all
\boldsymbol{p}_{T}\in\boldsymbol{W}_{T}
.
Note that this relation is the same as (
3.8a
). In HHO terminology,
\boldsymbol{q}_{T}(u_{T},m_{\partial T})
corresponds to
-\nabla\theta_{T}^{p+1}(u_{T},m_{\partial T})
, where
\theta_{T}^{p+1}
denotes the so-called local higher-order reconstruction operator, defined by (
3.9
) and the closure condition

(3.10)

\int_{T}\theta_{T}^{p+1}(u_{T},m_{\partial T})=\int_{\partial T}u_{T}.

In a second step, given a local bilinear stabilizer
\underline{s}_{T}((u_{T},m_{\partial T}),(v_{T},\mu))
, we introduce the local bilinear form

(3.11)

\underline{a}_{T}((u_{T},m_{\partial T}),(v_{T},\mu))\\
=\int_{T}\boldsymbol{q}_{T}(u_{T},m_{\partial T})\cdot\boldsymbol{q}_{T}(v_{T},\mu)+\underline{s}_{T}((u_{T},m_{\partial T}),(v_{T},\mu)).

Consider the following local problems:

(i) For
m_{\partial T}\in M_{\partial T}
, find
u_{T}^{1}\in V_{T}
such that

(3.12)

\underline{a}_{T}((u_{T}^{1},0),(v_{T},0))=-\underline{a}_{T}((0,m_{\partial T}),(v_{T},0))\qquad\forall v_{T}\in V_{T}.

(ii) For
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, find
u_{T}^{2}\in V_{T}
such that

(3.13)

\underline{a}_{T}((u_{T}^{2},0),(v_{T},0))=\int_{T}fv_{T}\qquad\forall v_{T}\in V_{T}.

We define
\mathcal{U}_{T}\colon m_{\partial T}\mapsto u_{T}^{1}
solution of (
3.12
),
\mathcal{V}_{T}\colon f\mapsto u_{T}^{2}
solution of (
3.13
), and we set element-by-element

(3.14)

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{T}\colon m_{\partial T}\mapsto\boldsymbol{q}_{T}(\mathcal{U}_{T}m_{\partial T},m_{\partial T})

Finally, the global bilinear form is defined as

(3.15)

\displaystyle a_{\ell}(m_{\ell},\mu)=:

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{\ell}}\underline{a}_{T}((\mathcal{U}_{T}m_{\partial T},m_{\partial T}),(\mathcal{U}_{T}\mu,\mu))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell}m_{\ell}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell}\mu+\underbrace{\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{\ell}}\underline{s}_{T}((\mathcal{U}_{\ell}m_{\ell},m_{\ell}),(\mathcal{U}_{\ell}\mu,\mu))}_{=s_{\ell}(m_{\ell},\mu)}.

3.1.
Operators for the multigrid method and analysis

For functions
\rho,\mu\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{\ell})
, we set

\langle\rho,\mu\rangle_{\ell}=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\frac{|T|}{|\partial T|}\int_{\partial T}\rho\mu.

Importantly,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\ell}
defines a scalar product on
M_{\ell}
, and the norm of this scalar product

\|\cdot\|_{\ell}\coloneq\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\ell}^{\frac{1}{2}}

scales like the
L^{2}
-norm in the bulk of the domain. Moreover, the scalar product can readily be applied to a combination of bulk and skeleton functions: For example,
\langle\mu,v\rangle_{\ell}
can readily be evaluated for
\mu\in M_{\ell}
and
v\in V_{\ell}
with the understanding that the trace of
v
on
\partial T
is used inside the integral. We relate the bilinear forms
a_{\ell}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\ell}
using the operator
A_{\ell}
, which is defined via

(3.16)

\langle A_{\ell}\rho,\mu\rangle_{\ell}=a_{\ell}(\rho,\mu)\qquad\forall\rho,\mu\in M_{\ell}.

The injection operator
I_{\ell}\colon M_{\ell-1}\to M_{\ell}
remains abstract at this level. Its properties securing our analytical findings are listed in Section
3.3
. Possible realizations of injection operators can be found in
[
LRK22a
]
. The multigrid operator
B_{\ell}\colon M_{\ell}\to M_{\ell}
for preconditioning
A_{\ell}
will be defined in Section
4.2
.

The operators
P^{\langle\cdot\rangle}_{\ell-1}
and
P^{a}_{\ell-1}
, which are defined via

(3.17)

\displaystyle P^{\langle\cdot\rangle}_{\ell-1}

\displaystyle\colon M_{\ell}\to M_{\ell-1},

\displaystyle\langle P^{\langle\cdot\rangle}_{\ell-1}\rho,\mu\rangle_{\ell-1}

\displaystyle=\langle\rho,I_{\ell}\mu\rangle_{\ell}

\displaystyle\forall\mu\in M_{\ell-1},

(3.18)

\displaystyle P^{a}_{\ell-1}

\displaystyle\colon M_{\ell}\to M_{\ell-1},

\displaystyle a_{\ell-1}(P^{a}_{\ell-1}\rho,\mu)

\displaystyle=a_{\ell}(\rho,I_{\ell}\mu)

\displaystyle\forall\mu\in M_{\ell-1},

replace the
L^{2}
and the Ritz projections of conforming methods, respectively. While the former (or a discrete variation of it) is relevant for implementing multigrid methods, the latter is key to the analysis. We also introduce the
L^{2}
-projections

\displaystyle\Pi^{\partial}_{\ell}\colon

\displaystyle H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to M_{\ell},

\displaystyle\langle\Pi^{\partial}_{\ell}v,\mu\rangle_{\ell}=\langle v,\mu\rangle_{\ell}

\displaystyle\forall\mu\in M_{\ell},

\displaystyle\Pi^{\textup{d}}_{\ell}\colon

\displaystyle H^{1}(\Omega)\to V_{\ell},

\displaystyle(\Pi^{\textup{d}}_{\ell}v,w)_{\Omega}=(v,w)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\forall w\in V_{\ell}.

3.2.
Assumptions on discontinuous skeletal methods

Here and in the following,
\lesssim
means smaller than or equal to, up to a constant independent of the mesh size
h_{\ell}
and the multigrid level
\ell
.
We also write
A\simeq B
as a shortcut for “
A\lesssim B
and
B\lesssim A
”.
We assume that the numerical hybrid method, characterized in Section
3
, satisfies the following conditions for any
\mu\in M_{\ell}
:

•

The trace of the bulk unknown approximates the skeletal unknown:

(HM1)

\|\mathcal{U}_{\ell}\mu-\mu\|_{\ell}\lesssim h_{\ell}\|\mu\|_{a_{\ell}},

where
\|\cdot\|_{a_{\ell}}
denotes the norm induced by
a_{\ell}
on
M_{\ell}
, i.e.,

(3.19)

\|\cdot\|_{a_{\ell}}\coloneq a_{\ell}(\cdot,\cdot)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

•

The operators
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell}\mu
and
\mathcal{U}_{\ell}\mu
are continuous:

(HM2)

\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell}\mu\|_{\Omega}\lesssim h^{-1}_{\ell}\|\mu\|_{\ell}\quad\text{ and }\quad\|\mathcal{U}_{\ell}\mu\|_{\Omega}\lesssim\|\mu\|_{\ell}.

•

The dual approximation
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell}\mu\sim-\nabla_{\ell}\mathcal{U}_{\ell}\mu
, where
\nabla_{\ell}
denotes the broken gradient.
That is,

(HM3)

\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell}\mu+\nabla_{\ell}\mathcal{U}_{\ell}\mu\|_{\Omega}\lesssim h_{\ell}^{-1}\|\mathcal{U}_{\ell}\mu-\mu\|_{\ell}.

•

Consistency with the standard linear finite element method: If
w\in\overline{V}^{\textup{c}}_{\ell}
, we have

(HM4)

\mathcal{U}_{\ell}\gamma_{\ell}w=w\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell}\gamma_{\ell}w=-\nabla w,

where
\gamma_{\ell}
is the trace operator to the skeleton
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
and

\overline{V}^{\textup{c}}_{\ell}\coloneq\bigl\{v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\;\big|\;v_{|T}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(T)\;\;\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{\ell}\bigr\}.

•

Convergence of the skeletal unknown to the traces of the analytical solution. That is, if
m_{\ell}
is the skeletal function of the hybrid approximation to
u\in H^{1+\alpha}(\Omega)
, we have

(HM5)

\|m_{\ell}-\Pi^{\partial}_{\ell}u\|_{a_{\ell}}\lesssim h_{\ell}^{\alpha}\|u\|_{\alpha+1}.

•

The usual bounds on the eigenvalues of the condensed discretization matrix hold:

(HM6)

\|\mu\|^{2}_{\ell}\lesssim a_{\ell}(\mu,\mu)\lesssim h^{-2}_{\ell}\|\mu\|^{2}_{\ell}.

•

If
\rho=\gamma_{\ell}w
for
w\in\overline{V}^{\textup{c}}_{\ell}
, the stabilization satisfies

(HM7)

s_{\ell}(\rho,\mu)=0.

•

The global bilinear form is bounded by an HDG-type norm:

(HM8)

a_{\ell}(\mu,\mu)\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}\mu\|^{2}_{\Omega}+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{\ell}}\tfrac{1}{h_{T}}\|\mathcal{U}\mu-\mu\|_{\partial T}^{2}.

Remark 3.2

(HDG methods)
.

For the LDG-H (with
\tau_{\ell}h_{\ell}\lesssim 1
), the RT-H, and the BDM-H methods, (
HM7
) follows directly from (
HM4
), and (
HM8
) follows directly from the definition of the bilinear form
a_{h}
. They use almost identical assumptions to prove multigrid convergence for the HDG, BDM-H, and RT-H methods. (

)–(

) have been used to prove the convergence of multigrid methods for HDG, while (

), (

), and (

3.3.
Assumptions on injection operators

Our analytical findings will rely on the following conditions to hold on all mesh levels
\ell
:

(1)

Boundedness:

(IA1)

\|I_{\ell}\rho\|_{\ell}\lesssim\|\rho\|_{\ell-1}\qquad\forall\rho\in M_{\ell-1}.

(2)

Conformity with linear finite elements:

(IA2)

I_{\ell}\gamma_{\ell-1}w=\gamma_{\ell}w\qquad\forall w\in\overline{V}^{\textup{c}}_{\ell-1}.

This way, we do not restrict ourselves to one specific injection operator. All injection operators in
[
LRK22a
]
satisfy the desired properties. An immediate consequence of (
HM4
) and (
IA2
) is

Lemma 3.3

(Quasi-orthogonality)
.

Let (
HM4
), (
HM7
), and (
IA2
) hold, then we have for all
\mu\in M_{\ell}
and
w\in\overline{V}^{\textup{c}}_{\ell-1}
that

(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell}\mu-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell-1}P^{a}_{\ell-1}\mu,\nabla w)_{\Omega}=0.

Proof.

For
\rho=\gamma_{\ell-1}w
, assumptions (
IA2
) and (
HM4
), we obtain using the embedding
\overline{V}^{\textup{c}}_{\ell-1}\subset\overline{V}^{\textup{c}}_{\ell}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell-1}\rho=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell-1}\gamma_{\ell-1}w=-\nabla w=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell}\gamma_{\ell}w=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell}I_{\ell}\gamma_{\ell-1}w=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell}I_{\ell}\rho.

The definitions of
a_{\ell}
,
a_{\ell-1}
, and (
HM7
) yield

\displaystyle a_{\ell-1}(P^{a}_{\ell-1}\mu,\rho)

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell-1}P^{a}_{\ell-1}\mu,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{\ell-1}\rho)_{\Omega},

\displaystyle a_{\ell}(\mu,I_{\ell}\rho)

4.
Multigrid algorithm and abstract convergence results

If (
2.1
) has full elliptic regularity, which means that (
2.2
) holds for
\alpha=1
, we use a standard (symmetric) V-cycle multigrid method.
Otherwise, we use a variable V-cycle multigrid method to solve the system of linear equations arising from (
3.6
).

We follow the lines of
[
LRK22a
]
and avoid a discussion about good smoothing operators. That is, we allow our smoother in smoothing step
i

to fit the criteria of
[
BP92
]
, which allow pointwise Jacobi and Gauss-Seidel methods.
Next, we present the multigrid method as in
[
BPX91
]
. Afterwards, we present the abstract convergence results of
[
DGTZ07
]
.

4.1.
Multigrid algorithm

We recursively define the multigrid operator of the refinement level
\ell

B_{\ell}\colon M_{\ell}\ni\mu\mapsto B_{\ell}\mu\in M_{\ell}

with
n_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
smoothing steps on level
\ell
: Let
B_{0}=A^{-1}_{0}
be the exact inverse of
A_{\ell}
on the coarsest level. For
\ell>0
, set
x^{0}=0\in M_{\ell}
.

(1)

Perform
n_{\ell}
smoothing steps

x^{i}=x^{i-1}+R_{\ell}^{i}(\mu-A_{\ell}x^{i-1}).

(2)

Perform recursive multigrid step

q=B_{\ell-1}P^{\langle\cdot\rangle}_{\ell-1}(\mu-A_{\ell}x^{n_{\ell}}),

and set
y^{0}=x^{n_{\ell}}+I_{\ell}q
.

(3)

Perform
n_{\ell}
smoothing steps

y^{i}=y^{i-1}+R^{i+n_{\ell}}_{\ell}(\mu-A_{\ell}y^{i-1}).

(4)

Set
B_{\ell}\mu=y^{n_{\ell}}
.

If
n_{\ell}=n
independent of the level, we obtain the standard V-cycle. The variable V-cycle is characterized by

(4.1)

\displaystyle\rho_{1}n_{\ell}\leq n_{\ell-1}\leq\rho_{2}n_{\ell},

which needs to hold for all
\ell\leq L
and uniform constants
1<\rho_{1}\leq\rho_{2}
.

4.2.
Main convergence results

We use the results obtained in
[
BPX91
,
DGTZ07
]
. These publications show convergence under three abstract assumptions, which are assumed to hold for all level
\ell
. In order to illustrate these assumptions, let
\lambda^{A}_{\ell}
be the largest eigenvalue of
A_{\ell}
, and

\displaystyle K_{\ell}\coloneq\bigl(\mathds{1}-(\mathds{1}-R_{\ell}A_{\ell})(\mathds{1}-R^{\prime}_{\ell}A_{\ell})\bigr)A^{-1}_{\ell},

where
\mathds{1}
is the identity matrix and
R^{\prime}_{\ell}
is the transpose of
R_{\ell}
. The aforementioned, abstract assumptions claim existence of constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}>0
independent of the mesh level
\ell
and of the function
\mu\in M_{\ell}
satisfying:

•

Regularity of approximation:

(A1)

|a_{\ell}(\mu-I_{\ell}P^{a}_{\ell-1}\mu,\mu)|\leq C_{1}\left(\frac{\|A_{\ell}\mu\|^{2}_{\ell}}{\lambda^{A}_{\ell}}\right)^{\alpha}a_{\ell}(\mu,\mu)^{1-\alpha}.

If
\alpha=1
in (
2.2
), (
A1
) simplifies to

(A1’)

|a_{\ell}(\mu-I_{\ell}P^{a}_{\ell-1}\mu,\mu)|\leq C_{1}\frac{\|A_{\ell}\mu\|^{2}_{\ell}}{\lambda^{A}_{\ell}}.

•

Boundedness of the composition
I_{\ell}\circ P^{a}_{\ell-1}\colon M_{\ell}\to M_{\ell}
of the injection and Ritz quasi-projection operators:

(A2)

\|\mu-I_{\ell}P^{a}_{\ell-1}\mu\|_{a_{\ell}}\leq C_{2}\|\mu\|_{a_{\ell}}.

•

Smoothing hypothesis:

(A3)

\frac{\|\mu\|^{2}_{\ell}}{\lambda^{A}_{\ell}}\leq C_{3}\langle K_{\ell}\mu,\mu\rangle_{\ell}.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let (
2.2
) hold with
\alpha=1
, as well as (

), (
A2
), and (

).
Then, for the standard V-cycle, for all
\ell\geq 0
, and for all

where

\delta=\frac{C_{1}C_{3}}{n-C_{1}C_{3}}\qquad\text{with}\qquad n>2C_{1}C_{3}.

Proof.

This result is an immediate consequence of
[
DGTZ07
, Thm. 3.1]
.
∎

Theorem 4.2

2.2
) hold for

A2
), and (

7.
Injection operators for HHO

Consider two successive levels
\ell
(fine) and
\ell-1
(coarse).
Given the coarse faces
\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1}
, the mesh nestedness allows us to decompose
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
as the disjoint union
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\ell}\cup\mathring{\mathcal{F}}_{\ell}
, where

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\ell}\coloneq\{F\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell}\mid\exists F_{\ell-1}\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1}\textrm{ s.t. }F\subset F_{\ell-1}\},\qquad\mathring{\mathcal{F}}_{\ell}\coloneq\mathcal{F}_{\ell}\setminus\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\ell}.

In the following, we introduce three injection operators used in
[
DPHM+21a
]
, denoted by
I_{\ell}^{i}
,
i\in\{1,2,3\}
.
The first one is defined as

(7.1)

(I_{\ell}^{1}\mu)_{|F}\coloneq\begin{cases}\mu_{|F}&\text{ if }F\in\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\ell},\\
(\mathcal{U}_{\ell-1}\mu)_{|F}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

This first operator exploits the nestedness of the meshes to straightforwardly transfer values from coarse faces to their embedded fine faces. Regarding the fine faces that are
not
geometrically included in the coarse skeleton (i.e.
\mathring{\mathcal{F}}_{\ell}
), we make use of the local solver:
\mathcal{U}_{\ell-1}
builds a bulk function in the coarse cells, whose traces provide admissible approximations on the fine faces.

Instead of the straight injection for the embedded faces, an alternative is to also use the bulk functions reconstructed from the local solver. As
\mathcal{U}_{\ell-1}
yields a discontinuous polynomial, and the faces of
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\ell}
are located at the interface of two coarse cells, we propose to take the average of the respective traces, i.e.

(7.2)

(I_{\ell}^{2}\mu)_{|F}\coloneq\{\!\{\mathcal{U}_{\ell-1}\mu\}\!\}_{F}.

Remark that this formula also holds for
F\in\mathring{\mathcal{F}}_{\ell}
, as the average trace of a continuous bulk function on both sides of a face reduces to its regular trace.
This injection operator comes with advantages.
First, it allows the mesh nestedness condition to be relaxed, paving the way to generalized multigrid methods on non-nested meshes, as developed in
[
DPHM+21b
]
.
Additionally, a more complex formula can handle difficulties occurring at cell interfaces.
Typically, using an adequate weighted average formula can yield robust convergence in the presence of large jumps in the diffusion coefficient; see
[
DPHM+21a
]
.

Finally, the third injection operator leverages the higher-order reconstruction operator, a salient feature of the HHO methods:

(7.3)

(I_{\ell}^{3}\mu)_{|F}\coloneq\pi_{\ell}^{p}\{\!\{\theta_{\ell-1}^{p+1}(\mathcal{U}_{\ell-1}\mu,\mu)\}\!\}_{F},

where
\pi_{\ell}^{p}\colon L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{\ell})\to M_{\ell}
denotes the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projector onto the skeletal polynomial space of degree

L^{2}
-orthogonal projector onto the lower-order space.

8.
Numerical experiments

8.1.
Experimental setup

The numerical tests reported in this section are performed on the Poisson problem (
2.1
), on two- and three-dimensional domains. Namely, the fully elliptic problem shall be tested on the unit square and cube, and the low-regularity problem shall be tested on the L-shape domain. The problem is discretized using the standard HHO method as described in Section
3
, with the classical stabilizing term
[
DPD20
, Ex. 2.7 (2.22)]
. Tests will span the polynomial degrees
p=1,2,3
, leaving the special case of
p=0
out of the scope, as it does not verify the hypotheses of our framework; see Remark
8.1
. Modal polynomial bases are used in cells and on faces to assemble the method (specifically, we use
L^{2}
-orthogonal Legendre bases).

Remark 8.1
.

(The lowest order case) The HHO method with
p=0
does not verify the error estimates (
6.9
) and (
6.10
). Consequently, (
HM5
) does not hold and the multigrid method might not be uniformly convergent. It is worth noting that this limitation is not just theoretical. It has been observed in practice in
[
DPHM+21a
,
DPHM+21b
]
, where suboptimal convergence is reported for
p=0
while the method exhibits optimal convergence for the higher orders, with no variation in any other parameter.

The multigrid method is constructed as described in Section
4.1
. Section
7
describes the injection operators we consider. The V-cycle is carried out using pointwise Gauss–Seidel smoothing iterations, arranged so that both the smoothing step and the multigrid iteration remain symmetric. Namely, for V(1,1), the pre-smoothing iteration is performed in the forward order and the post-smoothing iteration in the backward order.
For V(2,2), the pre- and post-smoothing procedures involve a single forward iteration followed by a single backward iteration. The grid hierarchy is built by successive refinements of an initial simplicial mesh, and the condensed problem is assembled at every level. The coarsest system is solved with the Cholesky factorization. The stopping criterion relies on the backward error
\|\mathbf{r}\|_{2}/\|\mathbf{b}\|_{2}
, where
\mathbf{r}
denotes the residual of the algebraic system,
\mathbf{b}
the right-hand side, and
\|\cdot\|_{2}
the standard Euclidean norm applied to the vector space of coordinates. In all experiments, convergence is considered to be reached once
\|\mathbf{r}\|_{2}/\|\mathbf{b}\|_{2}<10^{-6}
is satisfied.

8.3.
Conclusions

We have constructed and rigorously analyzed a homogeneous multigrid method for HHO methods, which does not internally change the discretization scheme. To this end, we generalized the homogeneous multigrid framework for HDG methods and verified the resulting, more general assumptions for HHO methods. We verified our analytical findings numerically using several injection operators.
