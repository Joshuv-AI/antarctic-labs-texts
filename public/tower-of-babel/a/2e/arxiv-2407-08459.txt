Genus expansion for non–linear random matrix ensembles with applications to neural networks
Nicola Muça Cirone, Jad Hamdan, Cristopher Salvi

Abstract
. We present a unified approach to studying certain non-linear random matrix ensembles and associated random neural networks at initialization. This begins with a novel series expansion for neural networks which generalizes Faà di Bruno’s formula to an arbitrary number of compositions. The role of monomials is played by random multilinear maps indexed by directed graphs, whose edges correspond to random matrices. Crucially, this expansion linearises the effect of the activation functions, allowing for the direct application of Wick’s principle and the genus expansion technique. As an application, we prove several results about neural networks with random weights. We first give a new proof of the fact that they converge to Gaussian processes as their width tends to infinity. Secondly, we quantify the rate of convergence of the Neural Tangent Kernel [ 21 ] to its deterministic limit in Frobenius norm. Finally, we compute moments of the limiting spectral distribution of the Jacobian (only the first two of which were previously known), expressing them as sums over non-crossing partitions. All of these results are then generalized to the case of neural networks with sparse and non-Gaussian weights, under moment assumptions.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Randomly initialised neural networks and non-linear random matrix theory

Deep neural networks (NNs) whose weights’ and biases’ entries are initialised as appropriately rescaled, independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) Gaussian random variables converge to Gaussian processes (GPs) as their width tends to infinity. This well-known fact was originally observed by
Neal [28]
for shallow feedforward networks and more recently by
Matthews et al. [25]
for multi-layer feedforward networks, by
Novak et al. [30]
and
[
13
]
for deep convolutional networks, and by
Yang [43]
for more general architectures, including recurrent and attention-based networks. The study of such random initializations is of practical interest, as a better understanding of their properties can help make design choices that improve training. For instance, the concentration of the spectrum of the network’s input-output Jacobian can help prevent vanishing and exploding gradients
[
14
,
40
,
35
]
. Other relevant objects which have been studied include the Gram
[
33
]
and Fisher information
[
34
]
matrices, as well as the
neural tangent kernel
(NTK)
[
21
]
. From a theoretical viewpoint, these offer natural non-linear generalizations of the classical questions of random matrix theory (RMT) and free probability, and as such have attracted the attention of both communities
[
31
,
32
,
1
,
42
,
11
]
.

Albeit of a similar nature, these results have been derived using a wide array of tools which do not always offer a clear path towards universality (meaning initialisations with non-Gaussian random weights). The goal of this paper is to propose a unifying framework that is naturally suited to this task, and is built on the
genus expansion
technique.

This technique has its roots in the connection between matrix integrals and the enumeration of (combinatorial) maps, which was first discovered in the context of quantum field theory (see
[
12
,
41
,
4
]
, as well as
[
44
]
for an accessible introduction to the subject). The link to random matrix theory was later established by Harer and Zagier
[
19
]
in a seminal work investigating moduli spaces of curves, and has since been used to study various matrix ensembles and their asymptotic first and second-order freeness (e.g.
[
27
,
29
,
37
,
38
]
, as well as
[
10
]
and the references therein).
Roughly speaking, the approach consists of expanding traces of random matrix products and evaluating their expectation using Wick’s principle. This yields a sum whose terms are in bijection with a set of graphs, and one determines which terms are of leading order by embedding their corresponding graphs into surfaces and computing their Euler characteristic.

In a recent work, Dubach and Peled
[
10
]
used this approach to study products of non-Hermitian random matrices, and in particular showed that it can be adapted to non-Gaussian and sparse matrices with relative ease. This would, at least in principle, make it ideal for the study of multilayer neural networks, but the presence of non-linear activations in that setting prohibits one from being able to apply Wick’s formula.

We circumvent this problem by developing a graphical language to express a large class of matrix/vector products, and deriving an expansion for neural networks in terms of this language. This linearises the effect of the activation functions, allowing for the use of Wick’s formula and the connection to the enumeration of maps to be made. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first use of the genus expansion technique in deep learning and non-linear RMT. A high-level overview of our approach is given in section
1.3
below.

1.3.
Overview of our method.

1.3.1.
A graphical language for neural network computations.

The idea of using a graphical language to simplify computations involving multilinear maps is not new, dating back to at least the 1970s with the introduction of Penrose diagrams
[
36
]
,
which have more recently been applied in the context of machine learning (see
[
2
,
5
]
).
As discussed earlier, graphs have also been used to evaluate expectations of products of Gaussian variables, and this forms the basis of the genus expansion technique.

The graphs that we introduce are novel and accomplish both of these tasks at once. On the one hand, they can be used to express deterministic products and operations involving multilinear maps. On the other, when dealing with tensors with Gaussian entries, the expectation of these operations can once again be expressed in terms of graphs (in the sense of equation (
10
)). We explain this briefly below, deferring to Section
2
for more details.

In what we call a
product
graph
G=(V,E)
, edges will correspond to matrices and vertices to vectors, which we call the inputs of their respective edge/vertex. The graph’s structure then dictates a well-defined product involving these inputs, the result of which we call the
value
of the graph and denote by
\mathbf{W}_{G}
. For instance, a path of length
k
can be used to express an (ordinary) product of
k
matrices, while trees can be used to express Hadamard (entrywise) products (this is depicted in Figures
1
and
2
).

If we omit inputs for some vertices and edges of the graph and view them as variables, then the resulting graph can be associated to a (multi)linear map and we call it an
operator graph
. Differentiation, composition and other operations involving these maps then turn out to be expressible using simple manipulations of their corresponding graphs (composition, for instance, reduces to attaching graphs by a vertex), as explained in Section
2.1
and the figures therein.

Remark
.

After completing this work, we were made aware of the theory of traffic probability
[
24
]
, with which the graphical formalism developed here overlaps significantly. We leave the task of reconciling both theories to future work, but point out similarities between the two across Section
2
.

1.3.2.
Graph expansions of neural networks.

The connection to neural networks is made by expanding their output at a given input
\mathbf{x}
as a linear combination of product graphs

(9)

\Phi(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{G\in\mathcal{F}}c(G)\mathbf{W}_{G}

for some family of graphs
\mathcal{F}
and combinatorial factors
c(G)
.

This is achieved in Theorem
4
, which generalizes Faà di Bruno’s formula (see
[
7
,
22
]
) to the case of an arbitrary number of compositions. In similar tasks, trees have been shown to be a natural combinatorial tool to keep track of terms (see the literature on Butcher series
[
26
,
16
]
, and, more generally, on Runge-Kutta methods for ordinary differential equations
[
17
]
), and this is reflected here in the fact that
\mathcal{F}
(in Eq. (
9
)) turns out to be a set of rooted trees.

By applying our previously mentioned graphical rules to each term in this sum, we derive similar expansions for various related quantities, namely the
k
-th coordinate of
\Phi(\mathbf{x})
, the neural tangent kernel, and the trace of the input-output Jacobian of
\Phi
times its transpose, raised to an arbitrary power. The moment method then reduces the study of the asymptotic distribution of these quantities to computing
\mathbb{E}\mathbf{W}_{G}
for various graphs
G
.

1.3.3.
Wick’s principle and genus expansion.

When
G
is a product graph whose edge inputs have Gaussian entries, our main tool to evaluate
\mathbb{E}\mathbf{W}_{G}
is Wick’s principle, which reduces the expectation of products of Gaussian variables to the sum of their pairwise covariances. Applied to
\mathbf{W}_{G}
, it yields the following simple identity

(10)

\mathbb{E}{\mathbf{W}_{G}}=\sum_{\phi}\mathbf{W}_{G_{\phi}},

(see Theorem
6
), where the sum is taken over
admissible
pairings
\phi
of the edges of
G
(see Def.
8
), and
G_{\phi}
is the graph obtained from
G
after identifying edges paired by
\phi
(meaning that we consider such edges to be the same edge in
G_{\phi}
). Under additional assumptions on
G
(c.f. Assumption
2
), we find that
\mathbf{W}_{G_{\phi}}=\sigma_{G}N^{|V(G_{\phi})|}
for every
\phi
, where
|V(G_{\phi})|
is the number of vertices in
G_{\phi}
and
\sigma_{G}
is a variance parameter. The leading order of
\mathbf{W}_{G}
is thus determined by the pairings for which
|V(G_{\phi})|
is maximised.

Instead of counting this quantity directly, it turns out to be much simpler to embed the graph onto a surface
S_{\phi}
(as defined in Equation
38
) and to then compute
|V(G_{\phi})|
using the
Euler characteristic formula

|V(G_{\phi})|-|E(G_{\phi})|+f(G_{\phi}:S_{\phi})=2-2g(S_{\phi}),

where
|E(G_{\phi})|
is the number of edges of
G_{\phi}
,
f(G_{\phi}:S_{\phi})
the number of faces of
G_{\phi}
in
S_{\phi}
and
g(S_{\phi})
the genus of
S_{\phi}
. This formula allows us to identify which
\phi
give rise to leading and sub-leading order terms in Eq. (
10
), which we call
atomic
and
bi-atomic
pairings, respectively, following
[
10
]
. We use this to give a more explicit version of equation (
10
), and to extend it to centred mixed moments
\mathbb{E}\{\prod_{G}(\mathbf{W}_{G}-\mathbb{E}\mathbf{W}_{G})\}
as well (see Lemma
1
). Lastly, we combine these results to obtain a limit theorem for the joint moments of product graphs (Theorem

), reminiscent of a celebrated result of Diaconis and Shahshahani
[
8
]
for traces of powers of random unitary matrices and its recent extension generalisation in
[
10
]
(Theorem 1.2).

When the edge inputs in
G
are complex, non-Gaussian or sparse matrices (or any combination of the three), we show that the expressions for
\mathbb{E}\mathbf{W}_{G}
and
\mathbb{E}\{\prod_{G}(\mathbf{W}_{G}-\mathbb{E}\mathbf{W}_{G})\}
hold up to an

error term (see Sections
6.3
,
6.1
,
6.2
, respectively). This allows us to extend all of our results to NNs with such weight matrices.

1.4.
Possible generalizations

We imposed two simplifying assumptions in order to better illustrate our method ( polynomial activations and the absence of biases in (
1
)). We briefly discuss how these can be lifted below.

1.4.1.
Random biases

Typically, one defines
\Phi_{L}
as

\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{x})=W_{0}\mathbf{x},\quad\Phi_{\ell+1}(\mathbf{x})=W_{\ell+1}\varphi_{\ell+1}(\Phi_{\ell}(\mathbf{x}))+b_{\ell+1}

for some vectors
\{b_{\ell}\}_{\ell}
(the
biases
) which can also be initialized at random.
The method presented here can be adapted to this setting, albeit at the cost of additional combinatorial bookkeeping (namely a more complicated set of graphs
\mathcal{F}
in
9
). When applying Wick’s theorem to compute the resulting
\mathbb{E}\mathbf{W}_{G}
, one would also have to pair vertices first, before being able to pair edges as explained in
1.3.3
.

1.4.2.
Non-polynomial activations

Restricting to polynomial activations allowed us to ignore issues of convergence in Theorem
4
. That said, the expansion in the latter formally holds for any (reasonably well-behaved) function, and extending the results here to more commonly used activations (e.g. Sigmoid, ReLU) should thus be feasible by an approximation argument as was done by Benigni and Peché in
[
1
]
. We present some numerical evidence that Theorem
3
holds with ReLU activations in Section
9
.

Graphical descriptions of analytic operations

In this section we explain how various matrix–vector products can be expressed by means of directed graphs.

Consider a directed graph

. We associate a vector
\mathbf{X}_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}(v)}

\mathbf{X}_{e}
and
\mathbf{X}_{v}
the
inputs
of the edge

and vertex
v
respectively. Here,

is a positive integer which call the
dimension
of the vertex
v
, and we extend
\mathfrak{d}
to edges by using the shorthand
\mathfrak{d}(e)=(\mathfrak{d}(u),\mathfrak{d}(v))

As we are often going to talk about vertices and edges simultaneously, it will be useful to have a single term to refer to both types of objects. For this purpose we will use the term

Definition 1

•

•

2.1.
Operator graphs and their associated linear map

So far, we have associated a vector/matrix to each cell in our graphs.
By
freeing
some of these cells–meaning that we consider their inputs as variables–we can use graphs to define more general linear maps between tensors.

Consider for example the product graph
(G,\mathfrak{d},\mathfrak{C})

\mathbf{x}_{a}

In this particular case, the linear map in question would be

More generally, let
G
be a directed graph with dimensions assigned by
\mathfrak{d}

\mathcal{F}
be an (ordered) sequence of free cells of
G
(meaning vertices and edges which are considered as variables), assuming that the remaining cells

linear in these inputs.

Any map defined this way will be scalar-valued, but we can generalize to vector-valued maps through partial evaluation. Namely, given a partition of
\mathcal{F}
into two subsequences
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{in}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{out}}

(13)

(\mathbf{X}_{c})_{c\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{in}}}\mapsto\big(\otimes_{c\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{out}}}\mathbf{X}_{c}\mapsto\mathbf{W}_{G}\big).

This is equivalent to considering the map

(14)

(\mathbf{X}_{c})_{c\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{in}}}\mapsto\mathbf{Y},\quad\langle\mathbf{Y},\otimes_{c\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{out}}}\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{c}}\rangle=\mathbf{W}_{G}\,\text{ for any }(\mathbf{X}_{c})_{c\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{out}}}

where
\mathbf{Y}\in\bigotimes_{c\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{out}}}\mathbb{R}^{{\mathfrak{d}}(c)}
is the Riesz representation of
\big(\otimes_{c\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{out}}}\mathbf{X}_{c}\mapsto\mathbf{W}_{G}\big)
. Note that the input and output dimensions of this map depend on the partition of
\mathcal{F}
that is being taken. We refer to it as the
operator associated to

G
, which we define below. In what follows, we abuse notation slightly and write

to mean

Definition 5
.

Fix

,
\mathbb{T}_{L}
is the set of operator graphs defined inductively by

\mathbb{T}_{0}=\{[\bullet_{\mathbf{e}_{1}}]_{0},\cdots,[\bullet_{\mathbf{e}_{N_{0}}}]_{0}\},\quad\mathbb{T}_{\ell+1}:=\{[\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{M}]_{\ell+1}~|~M\geq 0,~\forall i\in[M]~\tau_{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{\ell}\}.

Remark
.

Note that for any
\tau\in\mathbb{T}_{\ell}
, there is a unique dimension function

that is compatible with its inputs:
if an edge

is labeled by

By definition of the bracket operation

\mathbf{W}_{[\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{M}]_{\ell+1}}=W_{\ell+1}(\odot_{i=1}^{M}\mathbf{W}_{\tau_{i}})

Note that by construction, the trees in

are

of

, and define it recursively as

s([\bullet_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}]_{0})=1,\quad s([(\tau_{1})^{k_{1}}\cdots(\tau_{m})^{k_{m}}]_{\ell})=\prod_{i}(k_{i}!)s(\tau_{i})^{k_{i}},

where
\tau_{1},...,\tau_{m}
are assumed to be distinct and
\tau_{1}^{k_{1}}
denotes the repetition of
\tau_{1}
,
k_{1}
times. Lastly, we introduce the following bit of notation: for any vector
\mathbf{x}
, we let

\mathbf{x}_{[\bullet_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}]_{0}}=[\mathbf{x}]_{i},\quad\mathbf{x}_{[\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{M}]_{\ell}}=\prod_{i=1}^{M}\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{i}}\in\mathbb{R},

and for any activation function
\varphi
,

(19)

\varphi_{[{\bullet_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}}]_{0}}=1,\quad\varphi_{[\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{M}]_{\ell+1}}=\varphi^{(M)}_{\ell+1}(0)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{M}\varphi_{\tau_{i}}\in\mathbb{R},

where
\varphi^{(M)}
is the
M
-th derivative of
\varphi
.

Definition 6
.

Given
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}}
we define the sets
\mathbb{T}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})
of operator graphs obtained from the trees in
\mathbb{T}_{\ell}
by changing all the leaves’ inputs from
\bullet_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}
to
\mathbf{x}
. We will henceforth denote this operation by
\bullet_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}\mapsto\bullet_{\mathbf{x}}
.

If we extend
s(\eta)
and
\varphi_{\eta}
to
\mathbb{T}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})
setting the base cases as
s([\bullet_{\mathbf{x}}]_{0})=\varphi_{[\bullet_{\mathbf{x}}]_{0}}=1
, then clearly
\varphi_{\tau}=\varphi_{\tau(\mathbf{x})}
for all
\tau\in\mathbb{T}_{\ell}
since the definition of
\varphi
only depends on the out-degree of the vertices, which is left unchanged.
While it is not generally true that
s(\tau)=s(\tau(\mathbf{x}))
, we nonetheless have the useful equality

(20)

\frac{1}{s(\eta)}\mathbf{W}_{\eta}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{T}_{\ell}:\tau(\mathbf{x})=\eta}\frac{\mathbf{x}_{\tau}}{s(\tau)}\mathbf{W}_{\tau}.

This follows from the fact that
s(\tau(\mathbf{x}))/s(\tau)
counts the number of ways in which the non-plane tree
\tau
is obtained from

by applying the operation

.

Example
.

Consider the trees

.
Then

, but

while
s(\tau)=s([\bullet_{1}]_{0})s([\bullet_{2}]_{0})=1
.
Notice that
\tau
can be obtained both by
[[\bullet_{\mathbf{x}}]_{0}~[\bullet_{\mathbf{x}}]_{0}]_{1}\mapsto[[\bullet_{1}]_{0}~[\bullet_{2}]_{0}]_{1}
and

ways.

We can now state the main result of this section.

Theorem 4

(Tree expansion)
.

Let
\Phi_{L}
be as defined in (
16
). Then for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}}
,
\Phi_{L}(\mathbf{x})
admits the following expansion over
\mathbb{T}_{\ell}
:

(21)

\Phi_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{T}_{\ell}}\frac{\varphi_{\tau}}{s(\tau)}\mathbf{x}_{\tau}\mathbf{W}_{\tau}=\sum_{\eta\in\mathbb{T}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})}\frac{\varphi_{\eta}}{s(\eta)}\mathbf{W}_{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\ell+1}}.

Remark
.

For
\ell=1
this expansion reduces to the classical (vector valued) Taylor expansion since
s([[\bullet_{\mathbf{e}_{1}}]_{0}^{k_{1}}\cdots[\bullet_{\mathbf{e}_{d}}]_{0}^{k_{d}}]_{1})=\prod_{i}k_{i}!
.

Remark
.

Despite the space
\mathbb{T}_{\ell}
being infinite, this sum is always well-defined since only finitely many
\varphi_{\tau}
are non-zero (having assumed that the
\varphi
are polynomials).

Proof.

We proceed by induction on depth
\ell
for the first equality.
If
\ell=0
then
\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{x})=W_{0}\mathbf{x}
and

\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{T}_{0}}\frac{\varphi_{\tau}\mathbf{x}_{\tau}}{s(\tau)}\mathbf{W}_{\tau}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{0}}\frac{1\cdot[\mathbf{x}]_{i}}{1}W_{0}\mathbf{e}_{i}=W_{0}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{0}}[\mathbf{x}]_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}\right)=W_{0}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}}

as needed.

Now assume then the claim holds for
\ell
. For
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\ell}}
, we write
\varphi_{\ell+1}(\mathbf{y})
to mean
\varphi_{\ell+1}
applied componentwise to
\mathbf{y}
. Then by Taylor expansion around the origin,

\varphi_{\ell+1}(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{M=0}^{\infty}\frac{\varphi_{\ell+1}^{(M)}(0)}{M!}\mathbf{y}^{\odot M}

where
\odot
denotes the Hadamard product. Note that this sum is actually finite, since
\varphi_{\ell+1}
is assumed to be a polynomial.

If
y=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{T}_{\ell}}\frac{\varphi_{\tau}}{s(\tau)}\mathbf{W}_{\tau}\mathbf{x}_{\tau}
then Proposition
14
yields

y^{\odot M}=\sum_{\bar{\tau}=\llbracket\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{M}\rrbracket\in{\mathbb{X}^{M}_{\mathbb{T}_{\ell}}}}\frac{M!}{{\mathfrak{s}(\bar{\tau})}}\odot_{i=1}^{M}\left(\frac{\varphi_{\tau_{i}}}{s(\tau_{i})}\mathbf{W}_{\tau_{i}}\mathbf{x}_{\tau}\right).

Here,
\mathbb{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{\ell}}^{M}
denotes the set of cardinality-
M
multisets of elements of
\mathbb{T}_{\ell}
, and the
\mathfrak{s}(\bar{\tau})
are symmetry factors defined as

\mathfrak{s}(\llbracket\rrbracket)=1,\quad\mathfrak{s}(\llbracket(\eta_{1})^{k_{1}}\cdots(\eta_{m})^{k_{m}}\rrbracket):=\prod_{i=1}^{m}(k_{i}).

(
\llbracket(\eta_{1})^{k_{1}}\cdots(\eta_{m})^{k_{m}}\rrbracket
is the multiset containing
\eta_{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{\ell}
with multiplicity
k_{i}
.) Letting
\mathbb{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{\ell}}=\cup_{M\geq 0}\mathbb{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{\ell}}^{M}
, we have a natural bijection between
\mathbb{T}_{\ell+1}
and
\mathbb{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{\ell}}
given by

\tau:=[\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{m}]_{\ell+1}\mapsto\bar{\tau}:=\llbracket\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{m}\rrbracket.

Put simply, we identify a rooted non-plane tree in
\mathbb{T}_{\ell+1}
with the multiset of subtrees (
\in\mathbb{T}_{\ell}
) rooted at its depth-
1
vertices. Under this identification,
s(\tau)={\mathfrak{s}(\bar{\tau})}\prod_{i}s(\tau_{i})
and we conclude that

\displaystyle W_{\ell+1}\varphi_{\ell+1}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=W_{\ell+1}\sum_{M=0}^{\infty}\frac{\varphi^{(M)}(0)}{M!}\mathbf{y}^{\odot M}

\displaystyle=\sum_{M=0}^{\infty}\varphi^{(M)}(0)\sum_{\bar{\tau}=\llbracket\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{M}\rrbracket\in\mathbb{X}^{M}_{\mathbb{T}_{\ell}}}\frac{1}{\mathfrak{s}(\bar{\tau})}W_{\ell+1}\left[\odot_{i=1}^{M}\left(\frac{\varphi_{\tau_{i}}}{s(\tau_{i})}\mathbf{W}_{\tau_{i}}\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{i}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{M=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\bar{\tau}=\llbracket\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{M}\rrbracket\in\mathbb{X}^{M}_{\mathbb{T}_{\ell}}}\frac{\varphi^{(M)}(0)\prod_{i}\varphi_{\tau_{i}}}{\mathfrak{s}(\bar{\tau})\prod_{i}s(\tau_{i})}\left(W_{\ell+1}\left[\odot_{i=1}^{M}\mathbf{W}_{\tau_{i}}\right]\right)\left(\prod_{i}\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{T}_{\ell+1}}\frac{\varphi_{\tau}\mathbf{x}_{\tau}}{s(\tau)}\mathbf{W}_{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\ell+1}}.

The second equality in the theorem statement readily follows from

\displaystyle\sum_{\eta\in\mathbb{T}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})}\frac{\varphi_{\eta}}{s(\eta)}\mathbf{W}_{\eta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\eta\in\mathbb{T}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{T}_{\ell}:\tau(\mathbf{x})=\eta}\frac{\varphi_{\tau}}{s(\tau)}\mathbf{W}_{\tau}\mathbf{x}_{\tau}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{T}_{\ell}}\frac{\varphi_{\tau}}{s(\tau)}\mathbf{W}_{\tau}\mathbf{x}_{\tau}.

∎

For any set

Theorem 5

(Wick’s theorem)
.

Let
(Z_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be a real centered Gaussian vector.
Then for any sequence of integers

\mathbb{E}\Big\{\prod_{j=1}^{k}Z_{i_{j}}\Big\}=\sum_{\phi}\prod_{j=1}^{k/2}\mathbb{E}\big\{Z_{i_{\phi_{j,1}}}Z_{i_{\phi_{j,2}}}\big\}.

where the sum is taken over all pairings of
\{1,...,k\}
.

The arguments below are inspired by those of Dubach and Peled

Theorem 6

(Wick expansion)
.

Proof.

Let

.
Recall that for each

.
Then for any
\mathbf{i}_{V}\in\mathcal{I}^{G}

\mathbb{E}\Big\{\prod_{c\in C}[\mathbf{X}_{c}]_{i_{c}}\Big\}=\prod_{c\in C\setminus E_{\mathcal{W}}}[\mathbf{X}_{c}]_{i_{c}}\mathbb{E}\Big\{\prod_{e\in E_{\mathcal{W}}}[\mathbf{X}_{e}]_{i_{e}}\Big\}.

By Wick’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big\{\prod_{e\in E_{\mathcal{W}}}[\mathbf{X}_{e}]_{i_{e}}\Big\}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\phi}\prod_{\{e,e^{\prime}\}\in\phi}\mathbb{E}\Big\{[\mathbf{X}_{e}]_{i_{e}}[\mathbf{X}_{e^{\prime}}]_{i_{e^{\prime}}}\Big\}

where the sum is over all pairings
\phi
of

. Note that if

then

\prod_{\{e,e^{\prime}\}\in\phi}\mathbb{E}\Big\{[\mathbf{X}_{e}]_{i_{e}}[\mathbf{X}_{e^{\prime}}]_{i_{e^{\prime}}}\Big\}=0

and we can therefore write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{W}_{G}\}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{i}_{V}}\bigg(\prod_{c\in C\setminus E_{\mathcal{W}}}[\mathbf{X}_{c}]_{i_{c}}\bigg)\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{P}(G)}\prod_{\{e,e^{\prime}\}\in\phi}\mathbb{E}\Big\{[\mathbf{X}_{e}]_{i_{e}}[\mathbf{X}_{e^{\prime}}]_{i_{e^{\prime}}}\Big\}

(34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{P}(G)}\sum_{\mathbf{i}_{V}}\bigg(\prod_{c\in C\setminus E_{\mathcal{W}}}[\mathbf{X}_{c}]_{i_{c}}\bigg)\prod_{\{e,e^{\prime}\}\in\phi}\sigma_{\ell(e)}^{2}\delta_{i_{e}i_{e^{\prime}}},

For any vertices

\varphi(\varepsilon)\in\phi

\nu\in V_{\mathcal{W}}/\sim_{\phi}

Then for any

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{G_{\phi}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{I}^{G_{\phi}}}\prod_{c\in E_{\mathcal{W}}^{c}\cup V_{\mathcal{W}}^{c}}[\mathbf{X}_{c}]_{i_{c}}\bigg(\prod_{\varepsilon\in(E_{\mathcal{W}}/\sim_{\phi})}\sigma_{\ell(\varepsilon)}^{2}\bigg)\bigg(\prod_{\nu\in(V_{\mathcal{W}}/\sim_{\phi})}\prod_{u\in\varphi(\nu)}[\mathbf{X}_{u}]_{i_{\nu}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{I}^{G}}\prod_{c\in E_{\mathcal{W}}^{c}\cup V_{\mathcal{W}}^{c}}[\mathbf{X}_{c}]_{i_{c}}\bigg(\prod_{\{e,e^{\prime}\}\in\varphi(\epsilon)}\sigma_{\ell(e)}^{2}\delta_{i_{e}i_{e^{\prime}}}\bigg)\bigg(\prod_{\nu\in(V_{\mathcal{W}}/\sim_{\phi})}\delta_{\mathbf{i}|_{\nu}}\prod_{u\in\varphi(\nu)}[\mathbf{X}_{u}]_{i_{u}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg\{\prod_{i}

\displaystyle\Big(\mathbf{W}_{G_{i}}-\sum_{\psi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(G_{i})}\mathbf{W}_{(G_{i})_{\psi}}\Big)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\subseteq[c(G)]}(-1)^{|T^{c}|}\mathbb{E}\left\{\prod_{i\in T}\mathbf{W}_{G_{i}}\right\}\left(\prod_{i\notin T}\sum_{\psi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(G_{i})}\mathbf{W}_{(G_{i})_{\psi}}\right)

We now study a particular setting in which it will be possible to explicitly express

\mathbf{W}_{G_{\phi}}=\sigma_{G}N^{|V(G_{\phi})|},\quad\sigma_{G}=\prod_{e\in E_{\mathcal{W}}}\sigma_{\ell(e)},

given that all the edges of

are labeled by
\mathbf{I}_{N\times N}

The order of

Instead of counting the number of vertices

directly, it will be simpler to associate a surface to

To use it, we will need some basic notions from topological graph theory (see
[
15
]
for a more in-depth introduction). Any connected graph

can be viewed as a topological space, by viewing vertices as distinct points and edges as subspaces homeomorphic to
[0,1]
joining their ends, such that edges only meet at their endpoints.
An
embedding

\rho
of
G
on some orientable surface
S
is then defined as a homeomorphism from
G
(viewed as a space) to
S
, and is said to be
cellular
if the resulting faces are all homeomorphic to the open disk. For our purposes, all embeddings are assumed to be cellular.

The connected components of
S\setminus\rho(G)

are referred to as the
faces
of the embedding, and we denote the total number of faces by
f(G:S)
. Lastly, we denote the genus of
S
is denoted by
g(S)
. For any cellular embedding, we have the following formula.

Proposition 9

(Euler Characteristic formula)
.

Let
\rho
be a (cellular) embedding of a connected
G=(V,E)
on an orientable surface
S
. Then we have

|V|-|E|+f(G:S)=2-2g(S).

For instance, if
G
is a planar graph with
F
faces, then its drawing on the plane is a cellular embedding and this reduces to the famous identity
|V|-|E|+F=2
.

Further, it is well known that all orientable surfaces of genus
g
are equivalent up to homeomorphism (as a representative of their equivalence class, one can for instance take the surface obtained by adding
g
“handles” to a
2
-sphere, an idea going back to
[
3
]
). For any
g\geq 0
, it thus makes sense to talk about
the
surface of genus
g
, which we henceforth denote by
S_{g}
.

Lastly, we can define embeddings of disconnected graphs by considering each connected component separately. Letting
c(G)
denote the number of components and
G=\sqcup_{i=1}^{c(G)}G_{i}
, an embedding of
G
is given by embedding each
G_{i}
on an oriented surface
S_{i}
. We can also extend the definitions of
f
and
g
by writing
S=\sqcup_{i=1}^{c(G)}S_{i}
,

g(S):=\sum_{i=1}^{c(G)}g(S_{i}),\quad\text{ and }f(G:S):=\sum_{i=1}^{c(G)}f(G_{i}:S_{i}),

and we note that the Euler characteristic formula still holds with these definitions.

Going back the setting of Assumption
2
, let
G
be such a product graph and
\phi\in\mathcal{P}(G)
be an admissible pairing of its edges. Recalling that
G_{\phi}
is the graph obtained from
G
after identifying edges paired by
\phi
, we let

(38)

S_{\phi}:=\min_{g\geq 0}\{S_{g}:\ \text{there exists an embedding }\rho:G_{\phi}\to S_{g}\}.

if
G
is connected. Otherwise, let
(S_{i})_{\phi}
be the surface obtained this way from each connected component
G_{i}
of
G
, and define
S_{\phi}=\sqcup_{i=1}^{c(G)}(S_{i})_{\phi}
.

Remark
.

This minimum in (
38
) is always attained, and the value of
g
for which
S_{g}=S_{\phi}
is often referred to as the minimum orientable genus of
G
in the literature (see
[
15
]
).

We do not dwell on the details of these concepts and definitions, as we essentially only care about the case when
G_{\phi}
is a tree in our arguments. In that case,
g(S_{\phi})=0
and we concern ourselves with
f(G_{\phi}:S_{\phi})
and
c(G_{\phi})
instead.

Note also that we obtained
S_{\phi}
from
G_{\phi}
in a different way than in
[
10
]
: in their case,
G
is always a cycle, which naturally gives rise to a closed surface after identifying edges. No such construction seems to exist in our case.

For any
G
satisfying Assumption
2
and
\phi\in\mathcal{P}(G)
we note that
G_{\phi}
always has the same number of edges, which we denote by

(39)

\check{e}(G):=|E(G_{\phi})|=|E_{\mathcal{W}}(G)|/2+|E\setminus E_{\mathcal{W}}(G)|,

since the random edges are paired up by
\phi
and the rest are left untouched. Theorem
6
then yields the following.

Corollary

(Genus expansion)
.

Let
G
be a product graph satisfying Assumptions
1
and
2
.
Then for any
\phi\in\mathcal{P}(G)
,

(40)

\mathbf{W}_{G_{\phi}}=\sigma_{G}N^{2(c(G_{\phi})-g(S_{\phi}))-f(G_{\phi}:S_{\phi})+\check{e}(G)},

and it follows that

(41)

\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathbf{W}_{G}\right\}=\sigma_{G}N^{\check{e}(G)}\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{P}(G)}N^{2(c(G_{\phi})-g(S_{\phi}))-f(G_{\phi}:S_{\phi})}.

Proof.

This follows directly from Equation (
37
) and the Euler characteristic formula, which gives

(42)

|V(G_{\phi})|=2(c(G_{\phi})-g(S_{\phi}))+|E(G_{\phi})|-f(G_{\phi}:S_{\phi}).

∎

In this specific setting, we can also refine our result on centered mixed moments.

Lemma 1
.

Let
G
satisfy Assumptions
1
and
2
. Then for every
\phi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{AF}}(G)
, we have

2(c(G_{\phi})-g(S_{\phi}))-f(G_{\phi}:S_{\phi})\leq\frac{c(G)}{2}.

Further, equality holds if and only if
\phi
is bi-atomic, giving

\mathbb{E}\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{c(G)}\left(\mathbf{W}_{G_{i}}-\sigma_{G_{i}}N^{\check{e}(G_{i})+1}|\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(G_{i})|\right)\right\}=\sigma_{G}N^{\check{e}(G)+\frac{c(G)}{2}}\left(|\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{B}}(G)|+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\right).

Proof.

Assume that
c(G_{\phi})\leq{c(G)}/{2}
.
Then it suffices to prove that for each connected component
G_{i}
of
G
and its corresponding surface
S_{i}
,

2(1-g((S_{i})_{\phi}))-f\big((G_{i})_{\phi}:(S_{i})_{\phi}\big)\leq 1

and that the equality holds if and only if
(G_{i})_{\phi}
is a tree.

Since
f\big((G_{i})_{\phi}:(S_{i})_{\phi}\big)\geq 1
and
g\big((S_{i})_{\phi}\big)\geq 0
, we have

2(1-g\big((S_{i})_{\phi})\big)-f\big((G_{i})_{\phi}:(S_{i})_{\phi}\big)\leq 2(1-0)-1=1

with equality if and only if
f\big((G_{i})_{\phi}:(S_{i})_{\phi}\big)=1
and
g\big((S_{i})_{\phi}\big)=0
(which is equivalent to
(G_{i})_{\phi}
being a tree).
Now if
c(G_{\phi})>{c(G)}/{2}
instead, then there must be at least

2\bigg(c(G_{\phi})-\frac{c(G)}{2}\bigg)

components of
G_{\phi}
of the form
(G_{j})\big|_{\phi}
for some
1\leq j\leq c(G)
. Letting
k
be the number of components obtained this way, then we must have that
k\geq 2(c(G_{\phi})-{c(G)}/{2})
: assuming otherwise, the inequality

c(G)\geq k+2(c(G_{\phi})-k)=2\bigg(c(G_{\phi})-\frac{k}{2}\bigg)

quickly gives a contradiction. Since
\phi
is atom-free,

f((G_{i})_{\phi}:(S_{i})_{\phi})=1\implies g((S_{i})_{\phi})>0

for these components, and it follows that

2(1-g\big((S_{i})_{\phi}\big)-f\big((G_{i})_{\phi}:(S_{i})_{\phi}\big)\leq 0.

As for the remaining components (of which there are strictly less than
c(G)/2
) this quantity is
\leq 1
as proved above, and the lemma follows.
∎

Corollary
.

Let
G
be a product graph satisfying Assumptions
1
and
2
, and
\lambda_{G}:=({\sigma_{G}N^{\check{e}(G)+1}})^{-1}

(43)

\mathbb{E}\left\{\left(\lambda_{G}\mathbf{W}_{G}-|\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(G)|\right)^{2}\right\}=\frac{1}{N}\left(|\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{B}}(G)|+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\right).

Lastly, we note that by combining the previous two results, we can derive the following limit theorem for the joint distribution of product graphs.

Theorem 7
.

Let
(G_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be a sequence of connected product graphs satisfying Assumptions
1
and
2
. Then for any
m\geq 1
,

\big((\sigma_{G_{i}}N^{\check{e}(G_{i})+\frac{1}{2}}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{G_{i}}-|\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(G_{i})|\sqrt{N})_{i=1}^{m}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathbf{Z}=\big(Z_{1},...,Z_{m}\big)^{\top}

where the limit is a centered Gaussian vector with covariance function
C:=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}]

having entries
[C]_{i,j}=|\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{B}}(G_{i}\sqcup G_{j})|
.

Proof.

We use the method of moments. Setting

\tilde{Z}_{i}:=(\sigma_{G_{i}}N^{\check{e}(G_{i})+\frac{1}{2}})^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{G_{i}}-|\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(G_{i})|\sqrt{N}

we want to compute the expectations

\mathbb{E}\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{m}(\tilde{Z}_{i})^{k_{i}}\right\}

for non-negative integers
k_{i}
.
We are in the setting of Lemma
1
and the leading term of the expectation is the number of bi-atomic pairings of the induced graph.
Given a possible pairing of its connected components, for each of its pairs
(\tilde{G},\tilde{G}^{\prime})
this contributes a factor
|\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(\tilde{G}\sqcup\tilde{G}^{\prime})|
to the total sum.
But this is exactly the result of computing

\mathbb{E}\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{m}(Z_{i})^{k_{i}}\right\}

by applying Wick’s theorem.
∎

Applications to feed-forward networks

Recall that a neural network
\Phi_{L}
is defined by

(44)

\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{x})=W_{0}\mathbf{x},\quad\Phi_{\ell+1}(\mathbf{x})=W_{\ell+1}\varphi_{\ell+1}(\Phi_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})),

(\varphi_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 1}
, layer dimensions
(N_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}
and weight matrices
(W_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}
(see Equation (
16
)).
In this section, the weight matrices
W_{\ell}
are assumed to have i.i.d. Gaussian entries with standard deviation
\sigma_{\ell}
. Each of the results will depend on a different choice of
(N_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}
and
(\sigma_{\ell})_{\ell}
. Extensions to non-Gaussian/sparse matrices are handled in Sections
6.2
and
6.1
.

Note that the proofs of some auxiliary lemmas stated here are deferred to Section
7
to help with readability.

5.1.
Gaussian process limit

We begin by proving a special case of the Gaussian process limit (Theorem
1
), for
\Phi
with Gaussian weights.

Assumption 3
.

The input dimension
N_{0}
of
\Phi_{L}
is fixed, and
N_{\ell}=N
for all other layers
\ell>0
. Assume also that
\sigma_{0}=1
and
\sigma_{\ell}=N_{\ell}^{-1/2}
for
\ell\geq 1
,

Theorem 8
.

Under Assumption
3
, for any
M,L\geq 1
we have

(45)

([\Phi_{L}]_{1},...,[\Phi_{L}]_{M})\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{d}\mathcal{GP}(0,K_{L}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{M})

where the right hand side is a Gaussian Process indexed on
\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}}
, with diagonal covariance function defined by

(46)

\displaystyle K_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}}},~K_{\ell+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi_{\ell+1}(X_{\ell})\varphi_{\ell+1}(Y_{\ell})\right]

(47)

\displaystyle(X_{\ell},Y_{\ell})\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\begin{bmatrix}K_{\ell}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})&K_{\ell}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\\
K_{\ell}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})&K_{\ell}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y})\end{bmatrix}\right).

To prove this, we first consider the coordinate-wise tree expansion of
\Phi_{L}
given in Equation (
22
):

[\Phi_{L}(\mathbf{x})]_{k}=\sum_{\eta\in\mathbb{T}_{L,k}(\mathbf{x})}\frac{\varphi_{\eta}}{s(\eta)}\mathbf{W}_{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}.

Finding a distributional limit of
\Phi_{L}
using this expansion thus amounts to finding the limiting distribution of

\left(\mathbf{W}_{\eta}~|~\eta\in\cup_{i=1}^{M}\mathbb{T}_{L,k_{i}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\right)

as
N\to\infty
, for any fixed sequences
(\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}}~|~i\in[M])
and
(k_{i}\in[N_{L+1}]~|~i\in[M])
.

The mixed moments of the
\mathbf{W}_{\eta}
can be computed using Proposition
7
, which yields

(48)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg\{\prod_{i=1}^{m}\big(\mathbf{W}_{\eta_{i}}-\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(\eta_{i})}\mathbf{W}_{(\eta_{i})_{\phi}}\big)\bigg\}=\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{AF}}(\sqcup_{i}\eta_{i})}\mathbf{W}_{(\sqcup_{i}\eta_{i})_{\phi}}.

To identify the leading order terms on the right-hand side, we must therefore identify the pairings
\phi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{AF}}(\sqcup_{i}\eta_{i})
which maximize
\mathbf{W}_{(\sqcup_{i}\eta_{i})_{\phi}}
.
This is done in the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

Under Assumption
3
, for any
L\geq 0
and fixed sequences
(\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}}~|~i\in[M])
,
(k_{i}\in[N_{L+1}]~|~i\in[M])
and

\forall i\in[M],\quad\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(\eta_{i})=\emptyset\text{, and thus}\sum_{\psi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(\eta_{i})}\mathbf{W}_{(\eta_{i})_{\psi}}=0,

where
\mathcal{L}_{0}(\sqcup_{i}\eta_{i})
denotes the set of leaves of
\sqcup_{i}\eta_{i}
which are endpoints of

\alpha(\sqcup_{i}\eta_{i}):=\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{B}}(\sqcup_{i}\eta_{i})}\delta_{\phi}\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle_{\phi}

Proof.

See Section
7.2

∎

Here,
\alpha(\sqcup_{i}\eta_{i})
is essentially a weighted version of the cardinality of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{B}}(\sqcup_{i}\eta_{i})

Applying this lemma to (

(51)

\mathbb{E}\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{W}_{\eta_{i}}\right\}=\sigma_{\sqcup_{i}\eta_{i}}N^{\tfrac{1}{2}({|E(\sqcup_{i}\eta_{i})|-|\mathcal{L}_{0}(\sqcup_{i}\eta_{i})|})}\left(\alpha(\sqcup_{i}\eta_{i})+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{N}\Big)\right).

5.2.
Convergence of the Neural Tangent Kernel

Recall that
\Theta_{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{L+1}\times N_{L+1}}
is the matrix representation of

(55)

\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\lambda_{\ell}^{2}(\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}(\mathbf{x}))_{W_{\ell}}\circ(\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}(\mathbf{y}))_{W_{\ell}}^{\top},

for
(\lambda_{\ell}^{2})_{\ell\geq 0}
. We prove convergence of
\Theta_{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
to its limit under the following conditions.

Assumption 4
.

Assume that the input and output dimensions
N_{0}
and
N_{L+1}
of
\Phi_{L}
are fixed, and that
N_{\ell}=N
for all other layers
0<\ell\leq L
.
Assume also that
\sigma_{0}=1
and
\sigma_{\ell}=N^{-\frac{1}{2}}
for
\ell>0
(the “GP limit” initialization).

Theorem 9
.

Under Assumption
4
and if
\lambda_{\ell}^{2}=\sigma_{\ell}^{2}\mathbf{1}(\ell>0)+\mathbf{1}(\ell=0)
, we have

(56)

\mathbb{E}\bigg\{\Big\|\Theta_{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\Theta_{L}^{\infty}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{N_{L+1}}\Big\|^{2}\bigg\}=\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{N_{L+1}^{2}}{N}\bigg),

where

(57)

\Theta_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}}},\quad\Theta_{L}^{\infty}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=K_{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+\dot{K}_{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\Theta_{L-1}^{\infty}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

and
\dot{K}_{\ell}
is defined in the same way as
K_{\ell}
but substituting
\varphi_{\ell}
for
\varphi^{\prime}_{\ell}
in (
46
).

Recall that the
i,j
-th entry of the NTK can be expressed by the following graph expansion (see Equation (
32
))

(58)

\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\lambda_{\ell}^{2}[(\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}(\mathbf{x}))_{W_{\ell}}\circ(\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}(\mathbf{y}))_{W_{\ell}}^{\top}]_{ij}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\in\partial_{\ell}\mathbb{T}_{L,i}(\mathbf{x})\\
\eta\in\partial_{\ell}\mathbb{T}_{L,j}(\mathbf{y})\end{subarray}}\frac{\varphi_{\tau}\varphi_{\eta}}{s(\tau)s(\eta)}\lambda_{\ell}^{2}\mathbf{W}_{\tau\circ\eta^{\top}}.

We once again use Proposition
7
to study the joint distribution of the product graphs on the right-hand side, from which the following lemma follows.

Lemma 4
.

Under Assumption
4
, for any
\tau\in\partial_{\ell}\mathbb{T}_{L,i_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
and
\eta\in\partial_{\ell}\mathbb{T}_{L,i_{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})
, we have

(59)

\mathbb{E}\bigg\{\Big(\sigma_{\ell}^{2}\mathbf{W}_{\tau\circ\eta^{\top}}-a(\tau\circ\eta^{\top})\Big)^{2}\bigg\}=\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{N}\Big),

where

(60)

a(\tau\circ\eta^{\top}):=\sum_{\psi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(\tau\circ\eta^{\top})}\delta_{i_{1}i_{2}}\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle_{\psi}.

Proof.

See Section
7.3
.
∎

Remark
.

This implies that we need to take
\lambda_{\ell}^{2}=\sigma_{\ell}^{2}
to get a well-defined limit, as assumed in Theorem
9
. This is known as the “NTK parametrization” (see
[
21
]
).

As with Lemma
3
in the previous section, we will need a combinatorial decomposition of the limiting covariances
a(\tau\circ\eta^{\top})
in order to prove Theorem
9
by induction.

Lemma 5
.

For
L\geq 1
, there is a canonical bijection

\partial_{\ell}\mathbb{T}_{L,i}(\mathbf{x})\to\mathbb{T}_{L,i}(\mathbf{x})\times\partial_{\ell}\mathbb{T}_{L-1,1}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\eta\mapsto(\eta^{+},\eta^{-})

under which

a(\tau\circ\eta^{\top})=\alpha(\tau^{+}\sqcup\eta^{+})a(\tau^{-}\circ{\eta^{-}}^{\top}),\quad{s(\tau^{+})s(\tau^{-})=s(\tau),\quad\varphi_{\tau}=\dot{\varphi}_{\tau^{+}}\varphi_{\tau^{-}}}

where
\dot{\varphi}_{[\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{n}]_{L}}:=\varphi_{L}^{(n+1)}\prod_{i}\varphi_{\tau_{i}}
and
a
is defined as in the statement of Lemma
4
.
Further, if
\ell=L
, we have
\partial_{\ell}\mathbb{T}_{L-1,1}(\mathbf{x}):=\{\bullet\}
so that
\partial_{\ell}\mathbb{T}_{L,i}(\mathbf{x})\simeq\mathbb{T}_{L,i}(\mathbf{x})
and
a(\tau\circ\eta^{\top})=\alpha(\tau^{+}\sqcup\eta^{+})
.

Proof.

See Section
7.3
.
∎

We can now derive the rate of convergence of the NTK to its limit.

Proof of Theorem
9
.

Let
i,j\in\{1,...,N_{L+1}\}
be arbitrary, and recall from Equation (
58
) that

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell}^{2}[(\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}(\mathbf{x}))_{W_{\ell}}\circ(\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}(\mathbf{y}))_{W_{\ell}}^{\top}]_{ij}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau\in\partial_{\ell}\mathbb{T}_{L,i}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\eta\in\partial_{\ell}\mathbb{T}_{L,j}(\mathbf{y})}\frac{\varphi_{\tau}\varphi_{\eta}}{s(\tau)s(\eta)}\lambda_{\ell}^{2}\mathbf{W}_{\tau\circ\eta^{\top}}

for any
0\leq\ell\leq L
. Note that the number of terms in this sum is finite, and crucially does not depend on
N
nor
N_{0},N_{L+1}
. The same is true for the factors
\varphi_{\tau},\varphi_{\eta}
and
s(\tau),s(\eta)
. Lemma
4
and repeated applications of the elementary inequality
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
thus yield

(61)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg\{\Big(\sum_{\tau,\eta}\frac{\varphi_{\tau}\varphi_{\eta}}{s(\tau)s(\eta)}a(\tau\circ\eta^{\top})-\sum_{\tau,\eta}\frac{\varphi_{\tau}\varphi_{\eta}}{s(\tau)s(\eta)}\lambda_{\ell}^{2}\mathbf{W}_{\tau\circ\eta^{\top}}\Big)^{2}\bigg\}=\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\bigg),

where the implicit constant depends on
L
.

For
\ell=L
, Lemma
5
then gives

\sum_{\tau,\eta}\frac{\varphi_{\tau}\varphi_{\eta}}{s(\tau)s(\eta)}a(\tau\circ\eta^{\top})=\sum_{\tau^{+},\eta^{+}}\frac{\varphi_{\tau^{+}}\varphi_{\eta^{+}}}{s(\tau^{+})s(\eta^{+})}\alpha(\tau^{+}\sqcup\eta^{+})

which is in turn equal to
\delta_{ij}K_{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
by the proof of Theorem
8
.
Otherwise, noting that
s(\tau)=s(\tau^{+})s(\tau^{-})
and
\varphi_{\tau}=\dot{\varphi}_{\tau^{+}}\varphi_{\tau^{-}}
, we instead have

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau,\eta}\frac{\varphi_{\tau}\varphi_{\eta}}{s(\tau)s(\eta)}a(\tau\circ\eta^{\top})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau^{+},\eta^{+}}\sum_{\tau^{-},\tilde{\eta}}\frac{\dot{\varphi}_{\tau^{+}}\dot{\varphi}_{\eta^{+}}}{s(\tau^{+})s(\eta^{+})}\frac{\varphi_{\tau^{-}}\varphi_{\eta^{-}}}{s(\tau^{-})s(\eta^{-})}\alpha(\tau^{+}\sqcup\eta^{+})a(\tau^{-}\circ{\eta^{-}}^{\top})

which is equal to

\delta_{ij}\dot{K}_{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\lim_{N\to\infty}{\sigma_{\ell}^{2}}[(\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L-1}(\mathbf{x}))_{W_{\ell}}(\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L-1}(\mathbf{y}))_{W_{\ell}}^{\top}]_{1,1},

(once again by the proof of Theorem
8
).

It follows that
[\Theta_{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]_{ij}
converges in
L^{2}
to
[\Theta_{L}^{\infty}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{N_{L+1}}]_{ij}
at a rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
for each
i,j\in\{1,...,N_{L+1}\}
. The theorem then follows since
N_{L+1}
is finite.
∎

5.3.
Distribution of the squared singular values of the Jacobian

Consider the input-output Jacobian
\mathbf{J}_{L,\mathbf{x}}
, which we defined in Equation (
23
) as the matrix representation of

(62)

\mathrm{d}(\varphi_{L}\circ\Phi_{L-1})_{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{L}\times N_{0}}.

Letting
\{\xi_{1},...,\xi_{N_{L}}\}
be the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{J}_{L,\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{J}_{L,\mathbf{x}}^{\top}
, recall that the empirical spectral distribution (ESD) of this matrix is defined as the random measure

(63)

\rho_{L}:=\frac{1}{N_{L}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{L}}\delta_{\xi_{i}}

where
\delta_{\xi_{i}}
denotes a Dirac mass at
\xi_{i}
. Fix

and let
m_{k,L}^{(N)}(x)
denote the
k
-th moment of
\rho_{L}

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G(\boldsymbol{\eta})\in\mathrm{DC}(k,L)\end{subarray}}\frac{\dot{\varphi}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}{s(\boldsymbol{\eta})}\sum_{\pi\in\mathrm{NC}_{k}}\left(\prod_{B=\{j_{i}\}_{i=1}^{l}\in\pi}a({\wedge_{i=1}^{l}(\eta^{(j_{i})}_{1,L}\wedge\eta^{(j_{i})}_{2,L})})\right)\left(\prod_{\tilde{B}\in\pi^{*}_{k}}a({G(\boldsymbol{\eta}|_{\tilde{B}})})\right).

The next step will be to interchange the order of summation.

To that end, we must first distribute the factor
\frac{\dot{\varphi}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}{s(\boldsymbol{\eta})}
. The same arguments of Proposition
15
used to prove Theorem
8
show that

\mu_{k,L}=\sum_{(\eta^{(j)}_{i,L})_{j\in[k],i\in[2]}\in((\mathbb{T}_{L}(\mathbf{x}))^{*})^{2k}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\dot{\varphi}_{\eta^{(j)}_{2,L}}\dot{\varphi}_{\eta^{(j)}_{1,L}}}{s(\eta^{(j)}_{1,L})s(\eta^{(j)}_{2,L})}\right)a({\wedge_{j=1}^{k}(\eta^{(j)}_{1,L}\wedge\eta^{(j)}_{2,L})}).

Notice that the sum over
\mathrm{NC}_{k}
does not depend on a specific
G(\boldsymbol{\eta})
.
Splitting the sum over
G(\eta)
into two sums, the first over
(\eta^{(j)}_{i,L})_{j\in[k],i\in[2]}\in((\mathbb{T}_{L}(\mathbf{x}))^{*})^{2k}
and the second over the remaining trees in
\boldsymbol{\eta}
, interchanging the sums then gives

\sum_{\pi\in\mathrm{NC}_{k}}\bigg(\prod_{B\in\pi}\mu_{|B|,L}\bigg)\bigg(\prod_{\tilde{B}\in\pi^{*}}\sum_{G(\boldsymbol{\eta}|_{\tilde{B}})}\frac{\dot{\varphi}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}|_{\tilde{B}}}}{s(\boldsymbol{\eta}|_{\tilde{B}})}a(G(\boldsymbol{\eta}|_{\tilde{B}}))\bigg).

Lastly, we note that

\sum_{G(\boldsymbol{\eta}|_{\tilde{B}})}\frac{\dot{\varphi}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}|_{\tilde{B}}}}{s(\boldsymbol{\eta}|_{\tilde{B}})}a(G(\boldsymbol{\eta}|_{\tilde{B}}))=\sum_{G^{\prime}\in\mathrm{DC}(|\tilde{B}|,L-1)}\frac{\dot{\varphi}_{G^{\prime}}}{s(G^{\prime})}a(G^{\prime})=\tilde{m}_{|\tilde{B}|,L-1}.

It follows that

\tilde{m}_{k,L}=\sum_{\pi\in\mathrm{NC}_{k}}\mu_{\pi,L}\tilde{m}_{\pi^{*},L-1},

and we conclude that
\tilde{m}_{k,L}=m_{k,L}
for every
k,L
, where
m_{k,L}
is as in the Proposition’s statement.

∎

6.1.
Non-Gaussian weights

All of the results obtained in the previous section can be extended to neural networks
\Phi
with non-Gaussian random weights, under moment assumptions. Consider the following more general sequence
\mathcal{W}
.

Definition 12
.

Let
N>0
and
\mathcal{W}:=(W_{i}~|~i\in\mathbb{N})
, where the
W_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
are independent matrices with entries
\sim\sigma_{i}Z_{i}
, where
\{Z_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
is a family of i.i.d. random variables satisfying

\mathbb{E}\{Z_{i}^{2}\}=1,\quad\mathbb{E}\{Z_{i}^{2k+1}\}=0,\quad\mathbb{E}\{Z_{i}^{2k}\}<\infty\quad\forall k\geq 0.

Note that we take the matrices in this sequence to be square for simplicity only, the results in this section carrying over to rectangular matrices as in Section
4
by straightforward modifications. The graphs considered in this section are all assumed to satisfy the following condition.

Assumption 6
.

G
is a product graph with inputs which are either elements of
\mathcal{W}
, or deterministic vector/matrices with entries uniformly bounded in
N
.

We define
E_{\mathcal{W}}
and the set of admissible pairings of
E_{\mathcal{W}}
in exactly the same way as in the Gaussian case.

Recall also that for any
G
,
\check{e}(G):=|E(G_{\phi_{0}})|
for any fixed admissible pairing
\phi_{0}
of its edges (the choice of
\phi_{0}
is irrelevant, as the value of
|E(G_{\phi_{0}})|
is constant over pairings).

Theorem 11

(Approximate Wick expansion)
.

For any (possibly disconnected) product graph
G

(73)

\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathbf{W}_{G}\right\}=\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{P}(G)}\mathbf{W}_{G_{\phi}}+\sigma_{G}\mathcal{O}(N^{\check{e}(G)+c(G)-1})

where
\phi
runs over all of
G
’s
admissible
pairings.

Proof.

Consider the set of indexations
\mathcal{I}^{G}:=\prod_{v\in V(G)}\{1,...,N_{v}\}
. Recall that for each
e=(u,v)\in E
,
i_{e}=i_{u}i_{v}
.
Then

\mathbf{W}_{G}=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{I}^{G}}\prod_{c\in C}[\mathbf{X}_{c}]_{i_{c}},\quad\mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{W}_{G}\}=\sum_{\mathbf{i}}\left(\prod_{c\in C\setminus E_{\mathcal{W}}}[\mathbf{X}_{c}]_{i_{c}}\right)\mathbb{E}\left\{\prod_{e\in E_{\mathcal{W}}}[\mathbf{X}_{e}]_{i_{e}}\right\}

Following
[
10
]
we write
\mathbf{i}\rightsquigarrow\phi
whenever

\forall\{e,e^{\prime}\}\in\phi,~i_{e}=i_{e^{\prime}}

and say that an indexation
\mathbf{i}
is
balanced
whenever there exists a
\phi\in\mathcal{P}(G)
for which
\mathbf{i}\rightsquigarrow\phi
.

Note how for
unbalanced

\mathbf{i}
one has

\mathbb{E}\left\{\prod_{e\in E_{\mathcal{W}}}[\mathbf{X}_{e}]_{i_{e}}\right\}=0
so that we can write the expectation as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{W}_{G}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{P}(G)}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\rightsquigarrow\phi}\frac{1}{|\{\phi\in\mathcal{P}(G)~|~\mathbf{i}\rightsquigarrow\phi\}|}\left(\prod_{c\in C\setminus E_{\mathcal{W}}}[\mathbf{X}_{c}]_{i_{c}}\right)\mathbb{E}\left\{\prod_{e\in E_{\mathcal{W}}}[\mathbf{X}_{e}]_{i_{e}}\right\}

For simplicity write

\Gamma_{\phi}:=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\rightsquigarrow\phi}\frac{1}{|\{\phi\in\mathcal{P}(G)~|~\mathbf{i}\rightsquigarrow\phi\}|}\left(\prod_{c\in C\setminus E_{\mathcal{W}}}[\mathbf{X}_{c}]_{i_{c}}\right)\mathbb{E}\left\{\prod_{e\in E_{\mathcal{W}}}[\mathbf{X}_{e}]_{i_{e}}\right\}

so that

\mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{W}_{G}\}=\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{P}(G)}\Gamma_{\phi}.

Note that for all
\phi\in\mathcal{P}(G)
it holds, for constants depending on
G
but not on
N
, that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi}=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\rightsquigarrow\phi}\sigma_{G}\mathcal{O}(1)=\sigma_{G}\mathcal{O}(|\{\mathbf{i}~|~\mathbf{i}\rightsquigarrow\phi\}|)=\sigma_{G}\mathcal{O}(N^{|V(G_{\phi})|})

Notice that the maximum value of
|V(G_{\phi})|
is reached only when
\phi
is fully atomic, in which case one has

|V(G_{\phi})|=\check{e}(G)+c(G)

where we recall that
\check{e}=|E(G_{\phi})|

(otherwise, this is a strict inequality).

Furthermore, for such
\phi
, we have

(74)

\Gamma_{\phi}=\sum_{\mathcal{I}^{G}_{(1)}\ni\mathbf{i}\rightsquigarrow\phi}\left(\prod_{c\in C\setminus E_{\mathcal{W}}}[\mathbf{X}_{c}]_{i_{c}}\right)\mathbb{E}\left\{\prod_{e\in E_{\mathcal{W}}}[\mathbf{X}_{e}]_{i_{e}}\right\}+\sigma_{G}\mathcal{O}(N^{\check{e}(G)+c(G)-1})

where

\mathcal{I}^{G}_{(1)}:=\{\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{I}^{G}~|~\forall e,e^{\prime}\in E_{\mathcal{W}},i_{e}=i_{e^{\prime}}\implies\mathbf{X}_{e}=\mathbf{X}_{e^{\prime}}\text{ and }i_{e}\neq i_{e_{0}}\hskip 3.0pt\forall e_{0}\in E_{\mathcal{W}}\setminus\{e,e^{\prime}\}\},

noting that if
\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{I}_{(1)}^{G}
, there exists exactly one
\phi\in\mathcal{P}(G)
for which
\mathbf{i}\rightsquigarrow\phi
.

But in this case, we also have

\mathbf{W}_{G_{\phi}}=\sum_{\mathcal{I}^{G}_{(1)}\ni\mathbf{i}\rightsquigarrow\phi}\left(\prod_{c\in C\setminus E_{\mathcal{W}}}[\mathbf{X}_{c}]_{i_{c}}\right)\mathbb{E}\left\{\prod_{e\in E_{\mathcal{W}}}[\mathbf{X}_{e}]_{i_{e}}\right\}+\sigma_{G}\mathcal{O}(N^{\check{e}(G)+c(G)-1}),

so that we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{W}_{G}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(G)}\Gamma_{\phi}+\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{P}(G)\setminus\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(G)}\Gamma_{\phi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(G)}\Gamma_{\phi}+\sigma_{G}\mathcal{O}(N^{\check{e}(G)+c(G)-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(G)}\left(\mathbf{W}_{G_{\phi}}+\sigma_{G}\mathcal{O}(N^{\check{e}(G)+c(G)-1})\right)+\sigma_{G}\mathcal{O}(N^{\check{e}(G)+c(G)-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(G)}\mathbf{W}_{G_{\phi}}+\sigma_{G}\mathcal{O}(N^{\check{e}(G)+c(G)-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{P}(G)}\mathbf{W}_{G_{\phi}}+\sigma_{G}\mathcal{O}(N^{\check{e}(G)+c(G)-1})

where in the last equality we have used the fact that

\forall\phi\in\mathcal{P}(G)\setminus\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(G),~\mathbf{W}_{G_{\phi}}=\sigma_{G}\mathcal{O}(N^{\check{e}(G)+c(G)-1}).

∎

Proposition 12
.

Let
G=\sqcup_{i=1}^{c(G)}G_{i}
be a product graph with disjoint connected components
G_{i}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg\{\prod_{i=1}^{c(G)}\Big(

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{G_{i}}-\sum_{\psi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(G_{i})}\mathbf{W}_{(G_{i})_{\psi}}+\sigma_{G_{i}}\mathcal{O}(N^{\check{e}(G_{i})})\Big)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{B}}(G)}\mathbf{W}_{G_{\phi}}+\sigma_{G}\mathcal{O}(N^{\check{e}(G)+{c(G)}/{2}-1}).

Proof.

For any
\phi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(G)
, let
\Gamma_{\phi}=\mathbf{W}_{G_{\phi}}+\sigma_{G}\mathcal{O}(N^{\check{e}(G)})
and define
\Gamma_{\phi_{i}}
similarly for each
\phi_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(G_{i})
. Proceeding as in Proposition
7
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg\{\prod_{i=1}^{c(G)}\Big(\mathbf{W}_{G_{i}}-\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{P}(G_{i})}\Gamma_{\phi}\Big)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\subseteq[c(G)]}(-1)^{|T^{c}|}\sum_{\phi_{T}\times_{i\in T^{c}}\phi_{i}}\Big(\Gamma_{\phi_{T}}\prod_{i\in T^{c}}\Gamma_{\phi_{i}}\Big)

where the last sum is over
\mathcal{P}(G_{T})\times(\prod_{i\in T^{c}}\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{A}}(G_{i}))
.

Arguing as in the proof of the previous lemma, we find that

(75)

\Gamma_{\phi_{T}}\prod_{i\in T^{c}}\Gamma_{\phi_{i}}=\Gamma_{\phi_{T}\times_{i\in T^{c}}\phi_{i}}+\mathcal{O}(N^{|V(G_{\phi_{T}\times_{i\in T^{c}}\phi_{i}})|-1})

where the implicit constants do not depend from
N
.

But then using inclusion exclusion, we obtain on the one hand

\sum_{T\subseteq[c(G)]}(-1)^{|T^{c}|}\sum_{\phi_{T}\times_{i\in T^{c}}\phi_{i}}\Gamma_{\phi_{T}\times_{i\in T^{c}}\phi_{i}}=\sum_{\phi\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(G)}\Gamma_{\phi}

where
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(G)\subseteq\mathcal{P}(G)
is the set of pairings of
G
such that none of its connected components are paired within themselves, and on the other hand,

(76)

\sum_{T\subseteq[c(G)]}(-1)^{|T^{c}|}\sum_{\phi_{T}\times_{i\in T^{c}}\phi_{i}}\mathcal{O}(N^{|V(G_{\phi_{T}\times_{i\in T^{c}}\phi_{i}})|})=\mathcal{O}\Big(\sum_{\phi\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(G)}N^{|V(G_{\phi})|-1}\Big).

We conclude by arguing as before that the maximal order of the
\Gamma_{\phi}
is reached when the partition is bi-atomic, which is the only case when
V(G_{\phi})=\check{e}(G)+\frac{c(G)}{2}
and when

\Gamma_{\phi}=\mathbf{W}_{\phi}+\mathcal{O}\Big(N^{\check{e}(G)+{c(G)}/{2}-1}\Big).

∎

Reiterating the arguments in Section
5
then yields the following.

Corollary

Let

Proposition 13

Let

Consider the set of indexations

Definition 13
.

\mathcal{W}_{\mathbb{C}}:=(W_{i}~|~i\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\})

.
If
M=0
then the result holds by convention.
For the inductive case assume the lemma true up to
M
, then

\displaystyle\otimes_{i=1}^{M+1}\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}}v_{a_{i}}=

\displaystyle\left(\otimes_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}}v_{a_{i}}\right)\otimes\left(\sum_{a_{M+1}\in\mathcal{A}_{M+1}}v_{a_{M+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}_{M}\in\times_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{A}_{i}}v_{\boldsymbol{a}_{M}}\right)\otimes\left(\sum_{a_{M+1}\in\mathcal{A}_{M+1}}v_{a_{M+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a_{M+1}\in\mathcal{A}_{M+1}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}_{M}\in\times_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{A}_{i}}v_{\boldsymbol{a}_{M}}\otimes v_{a_{M+1}}

Letting
\boldsymbol{a}_{M}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{M})
, we have

v_{\boldsymbol{a}_{M}}\otimes v_{a}=v_{(a_{1},\dots,a_{M+1})}

and the claim follows.
∎

The next step will be to prove an analogous result with Hadamard products
\odot
instead of tensor products
\otimes
.
Given that the Hadamard product is commutative, it will be convenient to write the results in terms of multisets (meaning sets where elements can appear more than once) since they are unordered.

Given a set
\mathcal{A}
, let
\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{A}}^{M}
be the set of multisets of elements of
\mathcal{A}
of cardinality
M
, and
\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{A}}=\bigcup_{M=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{A}}^{M}
.
For any multiset
\tau=\llbracket(a_{1})^{k_{1}}\cdots(a_{N})^{k_{N}}\rrbracket\in\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{A}}
, where
a_{1},\dots,a_{N}
are distinct elements of
\mathcal{A}
, its
symmetry factor
is defined recursively as

(85)

\mathfrak{s}(\llbracket~\rrbracket)=1,\quad\mathfrak{s}(\tau):=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(k_{i})!.

Using these definitions, we can express Hadamard products of sums of vectors in the following compact form.

Proposition 14
.

Consider a set
\mathcal{A}
and a summable sequence
(v_{a})_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
indexed by
\mathcal{A}
. We have for any
M\in\mathbb{N}

(86)

(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes M}\ni\left(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}v_{a}\right)^{\odot M}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{A}}^{M}}\frac{M!}{\mathfrak{s}(\tau)}v_{\tau}

where for
\tau:=\llbracket a_{1}\cdots a_{M}\rrbracket
we define
v_{\tau}:=v_{a_{1}}\odot\cdots\odot v_{a_{M}}
.

Proof.

Considering
M
copies of
\mathcal{A}
as different sets and using Lemma
10
, we have on the one hand that

\otimes_{i=1}^{M}\left(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}v_{a}\right)=\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\times_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{A}}\otimes_{i=1}^{M}(v_{a_{i}}).

On the other hand if we define linearly

\bigodot:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes M}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{M}\mapsto v_{1}\odot\cdots\odot v_{M}

we have

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}v_{a}\right)^{\odot M}=

\displaystyle\bigodot\left(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}v_{a}\right)^{\otimes M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigodot\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}^{M}}\otimes_{i=1}^{M}(v_{a_{i}})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}^{M}}\odot_{i=1}^{M}(v_{a_{i}})

by definition.
Note that for any
\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{M}),\boldsymbol{a^{\prime}}=(a_{1}^{\prime},\dots,a_{M}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}^{M}
,

\odot_{i=1}^{M}(v_{a_{i}})=\odot_{i=1}^{M}(v_{a^{\prime}_{i}})

whenever

(a_{1},\dots,a_{M})=(a_{\sigma(1)}^{\prime},\dots,a_{\sigma(M)}^{\prime}).

for some
\sigma\in S_{M}

i.e.

\odot
is invariant to the action of
S_{M}
.
Therefore we can write

\left(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}v_{a}\right)^{\odot M}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{A}}^{M}}\frac{M!}{\mathfrak{s}(\tau)}v_{\tau}.

since the cardinality of orbit
\tau
is exactly
\frac{M!}{\mathfrak{s}(\tau)}
.
∎

We are now equipped to prove Proposition
15
, which we do using the previous proposition and Wick’s theorem. This proposition gives an expansion for covariances of type
\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)\psi(Y)]
, where
\varphi,\psi
are polynomials and
(X,Y)
is a Gaussian vector with covariance of the form (
87
).

Proposition 15
.

Let
\varphi,\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be polynomial functions.
Fix index sets
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2}
and
\lambda:(\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\mathcal{A}_{2})\times(\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\mathcal{A}_{2})\to\mathbb{R}
a symmetric and
summable
function.
Then if

(87)

(X,Y)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\begin{bmatrix}~\sum_{(a,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{1}}\lambda(a,a^{\prime})&\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2}}\lambda(a,b)\\
\sum_{(b,a)\in\mathcal{A}_{2}\times\mathcal{A}_{1}}\lambda(b,a)&\sum_{(b,b^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}_{2}\times\mathcal{A}_{2}}\lambda(b,b^{\prime})\end{bmatrix}\right)

the following expansion holds

(88)

\mathbb{E}\{\varphi(X)\psi(Y)\}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}}\sum_{\eta\in\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{A}_{2}}}\frac{\varphi_{\tau}\psi_{\eta}}{\mathfrak{s}(\tau)\mathfrak{s}(\eta)}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}(\tau\sqcup\eta)}\prod_{\{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\}\in\pi}\lambda(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})

where
\varphi_{\llbracket\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{m}\rrbracket}:=\varphi^{(m)}(0)
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}(\tau\sqcup\eta)
is the set of pairings of the entries of
\tau
and
\eta
.

Proof.

Using Wick’s theorem we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{\varphi(X)\psi(Y)\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{M,N=0}^{\infty}\frac{\varphi^{(M)}(0)\psi^{(N)}(0)}{M!N!}\mathbb{E}\{X^{M}Y^{N}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{M,N=0}^{\infty}\frac{\varphi^{(M)}(0)\psi^{(N)}(0)}{M!N!}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}([M]\sqcup[N])}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\}\in\pi\\
\pi_{1}<\pi_{2}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\{Z_{\pi_{1}}Z_{\pi_{2}}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{M,N=0}^{\infty}\frac{\varphi^{(M)}(0)\psi^{(N)}(0)}{M!N!}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}([M]\sqcup[N])}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\}\in\pi\\
\pi_{1}<\pi_{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{2}}\lambda_{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}).

Here
[M]\sqcup[N]:=((1,1),\dots,(1,M),(2,1),\dots,(2,N))
is ordered lexicographically, where with a slight abuse of notation
\mathcal{P}([M]\sqcup[N])
is the set of pairings of elements in
[M]\sqcup[N]
and

\lambda_{(i,*),(j,*)}(c,c^{\prime})=\lambda(c,c^{\prime})\mathbf{1}(c\in\mathcal{A}_{i})\mathbf{1}(c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{j}).

The claim would thus follow from the equality

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M!N!}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}([M]\sqcup[N])}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\}\in\pi\\
\pi_{1}<\pi_{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{2}}\lambda_{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}^{M}}\sum_{\eta\in\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{A}_{2}}^{N}}\frac{1}{\mathfrak{s}(\tau)\mathfrak{s}(\eta)}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}(\tau\sqcup\eta)}\prod_{\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\}\in\pi}\lambda(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}),

which we now prove.
Begin by noticing that

(89)

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}([M]\sqcup[N])}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\}\in\pi\\
\pi_{1}<\pi_{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{2}}\lambda_{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})

(90)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\Gamma_{1}:[M]\to\mathcal{A}_{1}}\sum_{\Gamma_{2}:[M]\to\mathcal{A}_{2}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}([M]\sqcup[N])}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\}\in\pi\\
\pi_{1}<\pi_{2}\end{subarray}}\lambda(\Gamma(\pi_{1}),\Gamma(\pi_{2}))

where
\Gamma((i,k))=\Gamma_{i}(k)
.
We are simply double counting: the left-hand side in (
89
) is obtained by labeling the elements of
[M]\sqcup[N]
after having paired them, while the right-hand side is obtained by reversing the order of the operations with the functions
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
specifying the labels.

Then for any fixed
\tau\in\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}^{M}
and
\eta\in\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{A}_{2}}^{N}
, we have

(91)

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}(\tau\sqcup\eta)}\prod_{\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\}\in\pi}\lambda(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}([M]\sqcup[N])}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\}\in\pi\\
\pi_{1}<\pi_{2}\end{subarray}}\lambda(\Gamma(\pi_{1}),\Gamma(\pi_{2}))

whenever
\Gamma_{1}:[M]\to\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}:[M]\to\mathcal{A}_{2}
satisfy

\tau=\llbracket\Gamma_{1}(1)\cdots\Gamma_{1}(M)\rrbracket\text{ and }\eta=\llbracket\Gamma_{2}(1)\cdots\Gamma_{2}(N)\rrbracket.

Letting
\Omega_{\tau,\eta}
denote the set of all such pairs
(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2})
, we note that

|\Omega_{\tau,\eta}|=\frac{M!}{\mathfrak{s}(\tau)}\frac{N!}{\mathfrak{s}(\eta)}

and, in turn, that

(92)

\bigsqcup_{\tau\in\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}^{M}}\bigsqcup_{\eta\in\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{A}_{2}}^{N}}\Omega_{\tau,\eta}=\{\Gamma_{1}:[M]\to\mathcal{A}_{1}\}\times\{\Gamma_{2}:[N]\to\mathcal{A}_{2}\}.

This allows us to conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\in\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}^{M}\\
\end{subarray}}\sum_{\eta\in\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{A}_{2}}^{N}}\frac{1}{\mathfrak{s}(\tau)\mathfrak{s}(\eta)}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\tau\sqcup\eta)}\prod_{\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\}\in\pi}\lambda(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}^{M}}\sum_{\eta\in\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{A}_{2}}^{N}}\frac{1}{\mathfrak{s}(\tau)\mathfrak{s}(\eta)}\frac{1}{|\Omega_{\tau,\eta}|}\sum_{(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2})\in\Omega_{\tau,\eta}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}([M]\sqcup[N])}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\}\in\pi\\
\pi_{1}<\pi_{2}\end{subarray}}\lambda(\Gamma(\pi_{1}),\Gamma(\pi_{2}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M!N!}\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}^{M}}\sum_{\eta\in\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{A}_{2}}^{N}}\sum_{(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2})\in\Omega_{\tau,\eta}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}([M]\sqcup[N])}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\}\in\pi\\
\pi_{1}<\pi_{2}\end{subarray}}\lambda(\Gamma(\pi_{1}),\Gamma(\pi_{2}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M!N!}\sum_{\Gamma_{1}:[M]\to\mathcal{A}_{1}}\sum_{\Gamma_{2}:[M]\to\mathcal{A}_{2}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}([M]\sqcup[N])}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\}\in\pi\\
\pi_{1}<\pi_{2}\end{subarray}}\lambda(\Gamma(\pi_{1}),\Gamma(\pi_{2}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M!N!}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}([M]\sqcup[N])}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\}\in\pi\\
\pi_{1}<\pi_{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{2}}\lambda_{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})

where the first equality follows from (
91
), the second from
|\Omega_{\tau,\eta}|=\frac{M!}{\mathfrak{s}(\tau)}\frac{N!}{\mathfrak{s}(\eta)}
, the third is (
92
) and the last (
89
).
∎
